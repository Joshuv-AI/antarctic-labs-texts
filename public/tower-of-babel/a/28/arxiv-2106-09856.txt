Theory of Seebeck ratchet in noncentrosymmetric electron-phonon coupled system
Yugo Onishi, Hiroki Isobe, Naoto Nagaosa

Abstract
A thermoelectric effect of the noncentrosymmetric electron-phonon coupled system is proposed, in which a temperature difference between electrons and phonons, instead of a spatial temperature gradient, induces an electric current. This is a realization of the Seebeck ratchet in solids with the asymmetric energy dispersion of electrons, and the dynamical phonons act as the fluctuation of the scalar potential and induce the dc current. Possible realizations of this mechanism are also discussed.



I
Introduction

The thermoelectric effect is the effect of conversion between heat and electricity. Since they are promising for applications such as reuse of exhaust heat and energy harvesting, thermoelectric materials with high efficiency have been actively explored
Dresselhaus et al. 2007
;
Mizuguchi and Nakatsuji 2019
;
Gutiérrez Moreno et al. 2020
. The performances of thermoelectric materials are characterized by the conductivity
\sigma
, the thermal conductivity
\kappa
, and the Seebeck coefficient
S
, and the efficiency of the material is often measured by the dimensionless figure of merit
ZT=\sigma S^{2}T/\kappa
with the temperature
T
.

The thermoelectric effects most frequently discussed are the ones in metals or semiconductors, where electrons or holes serve as carriers of charge and heat. These systems are well described by the semiclassical Boltzmann theory and the quantum linear response theory
Behnia 2015
;
Luttinger 1964
;
Kubo 1957
;
Ogata and Fukuyama 2019
. It is also well known that there exist several universal relations between thermoelectric coefficients, such as the Wiedemann-Franz law and the Mott formula. These relations impose some constraints on the thermoelectric effects of electronic origins in searching for efficient thermoelectric materials. In fact, the Wiedemann-Franz law shows that the conductivity and the thermal conductivity due to electrons are not independent, which makes it difficult to improve
ZT
. Under these constraints, various strategies to design efficient thermoelectric materials have been proposed, such as band engineering and low-dimensional materials
Pei et al. 2012
;
Chen et al. 2003
.

While only the electrons are the carriers of charge in materials, other degrees of freedom such as phonons can be carriers of heat. Since phonons contribute to the heat current and the thermal conductivity, it is preferable to reduce the thermal conductivity due to phonons in order to improve
ZT
. Therefore, the most basic design strategy for thermoelectric materials was “phonon-glass, electron-crystal”
Chen et al. 2003
. However, degrees of freedom such as phonons sometimes work positively for the thermoelectric effects. For example, in the phonon drag effect, phonons contribute to the Seebeck effect because electrons are dragged by the heat current of phonons. The phonon drag effect can be also described by the Boltzmann theory and the linear response theory. This phonon drag effect can lead to a large Seebeck effect at low temperatures in, e.g., doped semiconductors
Herring 1954
;
Mahan et al. 2014
and, was proposed recently as an origin of the large Seebeck effect at low temperature in FeSb
2

Matsuura et al. 2019
. Since thermoelectric effects using degrees of freedom other than electrons can avoid restrictions such as Wiedemann-Franz law, they are one of the promising ways to achieve high-performance thermoelectric effects. Therefore, how degrees of freedom other than electrons can contribute to thermoelectric effects is an important issue in terms of applications.

From this viewpoint, we explore the possible role of lower symmetry of the electron-phonon coupled system as a mechanism of the thermoelectric effect. Especially, nonreciprocal charge transport in noncentrosymmetric systems is a subject of recent intensive interest
Tokura and Nagaosa 2018
. In addition to the broken inversion symmetry
\cal{P}
, the time-reversal symmetry
\cal{T}
is often required to be broken due to the magnetic ordering or external magnetic field for the directional transport. This can be understood from the energy dispersion of the band structure, which satisfies
\varepsilon_{\uparrow}(\mathbf{k})=\varepsilon_{\downarrow}(-\mathbf{k})
when
\cal{T}
is preserved.
Hence the nonreciprocal charge transport is represented by the formula
Tokura and Nagaosa 2018

J=\sigma_{2}E^{2}

(1)

where the nonlinear conductivity
\sigma_{2}
is linear in the magnetic field
B
for small
B
.
This indicates that the diode effect is switchable by the magnetic field, although the rectification effect is still very small.

In the presence of electron-phonon interaction, the phonons act as the fluctuation of the scalar potential and the consequent internal electric field
E^{\mathrm{ph}}
. When the external electric field
E
in Eq.(
1
) is naively replaced by
E^{\mathrm{ph}}
, a dc current
J
appears in equilibrium. Of course, this is wrong since there is no dc current with dissipation in thermal equilibrium, and a nonequilibrium steady state with energy flow is required to support the dc current. This situation is analogous to a Brownian ratchet, where the Brownian motion with different temperatures between two systems induces the rotational motion in one direction
Reimann 2002
.

In this work, we propose a thermoelectric effect with the asymmetric electron dispersion driven by the phonons. Assuming that the electron temperature
T_{\mathrm{e}}
and that of phonons
T_{\mathrm{p}}
are different, we find that the correct formula is

J\propto\expectationvalue{(E^{\mathrm{ph}})^2}_{T_{\mathrm{p}}}-\expectationvalue{(E^{\mathrm{ph}})^2}_{T_{\mathrm{e}}}.

(2)

where the angle brackets
\expectationvalue{\dots}_{T}
represent the thermal average with temperature
T
.
While the heat current flows spatially in the conventional thermoelectric effects, the heat current flows between phonons and electrons in the effect proposed here. This is the crucial difference from previous studies. For example, in
Würger 2021
a mechanism similar to the ratchet is also proposed, but the mechanism is based on the spatial gradient of temperature in contrast to our proposal.

Our mechanism is similar to the one proposed for the magnon-driven spin-Seebeck effect
Xiao et al. 2010
. In the magnon-driven spin-Seebeck effect, the temperature difference between electrons and magnons induces the spin current. The results of the present work show that a similar mechanism induces a finite electric current in an electron-phonon coupled system.
The mechanism proposed by Bosisio
et al.

Bosisio et al. 2016
is closely related to the present work. While they discussed the behavior of the localized electrons in the presence of the phonons when there is a temperature difference between electrons and phonons, we consider itinerant electrons and thus do analysis in
k
-space.
It should be also noted that the inversion symmetry breaking (or, equivalently, reflection symmetry breaking in one-dimensional systems) is essential to realize the thermoelectric effects induced by the temperature difference between electrons and phonons. While in the model proposed by Bosisio
et al.
the asymmetric contacts between the system and the electrodes break the inversion symmetry, the dispersion of the electrons breaks the symmetry in our model. In that sense, the effect proposed in the present work is a bulk effect in contrast to the proposal by Bosisio
et al.
Another closely related work is done by Budkin
et al.

Budkin and Tarasenko 2020
. They proposed that the shift of the position of electrons is induced by phonons when the effective temperatures for electrons and phonons are different. The shift is described by the Berry connection as in the case of shift current
von Baltz and Kraut 1981
;
Morimoto and Nagaosa 2016
. However, this effect is also different from our proposal because the effect proposed in
Budkin and Tarasenko 2020
is an interband effect while the effect proposed in the present work is an intraband effect.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we present a model for an electron-phonon coupled system considered in the present work. In Sec.
III
, we solve the model perturbatively and calculate an electric current and heat current induced by the temperature difference between electrons and phonons. We also calculate quantities which correspond to the power factor and the figure of merit in conventional thermoelectric materials. Sec.
IV
is devoted to discussions and conclusions. We also discuss the possible realization of the proposed mechanism such as excitation by light and a metal-insulator junction.

II
Model

Let us consider a model which consists of spinless electrons with an asymmetric dispersion and phonons in one dimension (Fig.
1
). We assume that electrons have dispersion
\varepsilon_{k}
and phonons have dispersion
\omega_{q}
. The behavior of electrons can be described with the Boltzmann equation:

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f}{t}+\dot{r}\partialderivative{f}{r}+\dot{k}\partialderivative{f}{k}=\quantity(\pdv{f}{t})_{\mathrm{scatt}}

(3)

Here,
f(r,k,t)
is the distribution function of electrons, and the right-hand side describes the scattering of electrons. We assume that the scattering term is given by two terms: one corresponds to the scattering due to the electron-phonon interaction and the other corresponds to the other sources of scattering such as impurities. They can be expressed as follows.

\displaystyle\quantity(\pdv{f_k}{t})_{\mathrm{scatt}}=-\frac{f_{k}-f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k}}{\tau}+\quantity(\pdv{f_k}{t})_{\mathrm{phonon}}

(4)

\displaystyle\quantity(\pdv{f_k}{t})_{\mathrm{phonon}}=\sum_{k^{\prime}}\quantity(\Wtot_{k'\to k} - \Wtot_{k\to k'})

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k+q\to k}[f_{k},n_{q}]=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\frac{\absolutevalue{g_q}^{2}}{V}f_{k+q}\quantity(1-f_k)

\displaystyle\times\quantity[ n_q \delta(\ene_{k+q} - \ene_k+\hbar\omega_q) + \qty(n_q+1)\delta(\ene_{k+q}-\ene_k-\hbar\omega_q) ]

(6)

where
f_{k}
is shorthand for
f(r,k,t)
, and since we are interested only in the spatially uniform steady state, we omit
r
and
t
dependencies of
f(r,k,t)
.
f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k}=(1+e^{\beta_{e}(\varepsilon_{k}-\mu)})^{-1}
is the equilibrium distribution function of electron with temperature
T_{\mathrm{e}}=1/k_{\mathrm{B}}\beta_{\mathrm{e}}
and chemical potential
\mu
.
In Eq.(
4
), we use the relaxation time approximation, where
\tau
accounts for scatterings other than the electron-phonon interaction.
Here we assume that
\tau
is independent of
k
for simplicity.
The expression of electron-phonon scattering term is given in Eq.(
5
).
\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}
is the transition rate of an electron scattered from state
k^{\prime}
to
k
, and is given as Eq.(
6
) by the Fermi’s golden rule.
g_{q}
is the coupling constant between phonons and electrons,
n_{q}=n(r,q,t)
is phonon distribution function, and
V=La^{2}
is the volume of the system with length
L
and cross-section
a^{2}
(Fig.
1
). We do not consider
r
and
t
dependencies of
n(r,q,t)
as in the case of
f(r,k,t)
.

If electrons and phonon are in equilibrium with temperature
T=1/k_{\mathrm{B}}\beta
and chemical potential
\mu
, i.e.,

\displaystyle f(r,k,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon_{k}-\mu)}+1}

(7)

\displaystyle n(r,q,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{q}}-1}

(8)

then we can confirm
\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}=\mathcal{W}_{k\to k^{\prime}}
. This equality comes from the following identity which holds only in equilibrium:

\displaystyle f_{k^{\prime}}(1-f_{k})n_{q}\delta(\varepsilon_{k^{\prime}}-\varepsilon_{k}+\omega_{q})

\displaystyle=f_{k}(1-f_{k^{\prime}})(1+n_{q})\delta(\varepsilon_{k^{\prime}}-\varepsilon_{k}+\omega_{q})

(9)

Therefore, in equilibrium, electron-phonon scattering term, Eq.(
5
), is zero.

In this paper, we discuss an unconventional thermoelectric effect of the system described above.
Conventionally, the source of the thermoelectric effects is a spatial gradient of temperature.
However, we consider another type of thermoelectric effect. Instead of a spatial temperature gradient, we consider a temperature difference between electrons and phonons as schematically shown in Fig.
2
(For the realization of such a system, see Sec.
IV
). As we confirmed above, if electrons and phonons feel the same temperature, the system relaxes into the equilibrium state and no currents flow. However, we show that electric currents can occur when electrons and phonons feel different temperatures.

One-dimensional system considered in the present work. The length of the system is
L
and the cross-section is
a\times a
. The electric current flows along the wire, while the heat current flows between phonons with temperature
T_{\mathrm{p}}
and electrons with temperature
T_{\mathrm{e}}
.

III
Calculation and Results

III.1
Solution of the Boltzmann equation

In this section, we solve the Boltzmann equation Eq.(
3
) up to
\order{\abs{g_q}^2}
, when electrons and phonons feel different temperatures,
T_{\mathrm{e}}
for electrons and
T_{\mathrm{p}}
for phonons.

Before proceeding to detailed calculations, we remark on the phonon scattering term
\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}
. What we are interested in here is the effect due to temperature difference
T_{\mathrm{p}}-T_{\mathrm{e}}
. In other words, we are interested in the effect by
\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}=n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}(T_{\mathrm{p}})-n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}(T_{\mathrm{e}})
, where
n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}(T)
is the equilibrium distribution function at temperature
T
for phonon with wavevector
q
. However,
\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}
includes contribution from the deviation of
f_{k}
from the equilibrium distribution at
T_{\mathrm{e}}
,
\delta f_{k}=f_{k}-f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k}
.
To clearly distinguish these two contributions, we rewrite
\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}
by expanding
\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}
with respect to the deviation from the equilibrium distribution,
\delta f_{k}
and
\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}[f_{k},n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}(T_{\mathrm{p}})]

\displaystyle=W^{(0)}_{k^{\prime}\to k}+\tilde{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}[\delta f_{k}]+W_{k^{\prime}\to k}[\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}]

(10)

\displaystyle W^{(0)}_{k^{\prime}\to k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}[f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k}(T_{\mathrm{e}}),n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}(T_{\mathrm{e}})]

(11)

Here we neglect higher order terms such as
\delta f_{k}\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}
. To justify this expansion,
\delta f_{k}
and
\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}
need to be sufficiently small. This condition is discussed at the end of this section.
The first term is
\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}
when
f_{k}=f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k}(T_{\mathrm{e}})
and
n_{q}=n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}(T_{\mathrm{e}})
. Here, we explicitly write
T_{\mathrm{e}}
dependence of
f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k}
to emphasize that
f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k}
is the equilibrium distribution for
T=T_{\mathrm{e}}
. Since
W^{(0)}_{k^{\prime}\to k}=W^{(0)}_{k\to k^{\prime}}
,
W^{(0)}_{k^{\prime}\to k}
does not affect the Boltzmann equation, as mentioned at the end of the last section. The second term is the contribution from
\delta f_{k}
and the third is the contribution from
\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}
. The second term can be incorporated into the relaxation time
\tau
in Eq.(
4
).
The third term
W_{k^{\prime}\to k}
can be obtained by expanding
\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}
with respect to
\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}
.

\displaystyle W_{k+q\to k}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\frac{\absolutevalue{g_q}^{2}}{V}f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k+q}\quantity(1-\feq_k)

\displaystyle\times\quantity[\delta(\ene_{k+q} - \ene_k+\hbar\omega_q) + \delta(\ene_{k+q}-\ene_k-\hbar\omega_q) ]\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}_{q}

(12)

Let us now turn to solving the Boltzmann equation. We assume that there is no spatial variation of temperature and that there is no electric field. In this case, the Boltzmann equation for steady state is

\displaystyle-\frac{f_{k}-f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k}}{\tau}+\sum_{k^{\prime}}\quantity(W_{k'\to k} - W_{k\to k'})=0

(13)

Here,
f_{k}=f(r,k,t)
does not depend on
r
or
t
. Therefore, we consider only
k
-dependence of the distribution function
f_{k}
. This equation can be solved as

\displaystyle f_{k}

\displaystyle=f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k}+\tau\sum_{k^{\prime}}\quantity(W_{k'\to k} - W_{k\to k'})

(14)

\mathrm{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\beta_{e}\xi}+1}

(22a)

k\in L

\displaystyle\sum_{k^{\prime}}\quantity(W_{k'\to k} - W_{k\to k'})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\quantity(\abs{\gF}^2\NL(\xi_k-\hbar\omega) + \abs{\gB}^2\NR(\xi_k-\hbar\omega))\quantity[F(\xi_k-\hbar\omega,\xi_k)-F(\xi_k,\xi_k-\hbar\omega)]\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\tau\sum_{k'}(W_{k'\to k}-W_{k\to k'})}\ll 1

III.2
Calculation of the electric current

The electric current
J
is given by

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{L}\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\hbar}\partialderivative{\xi_k}{k}f_{k}=\frac{e\tau}{\hbar L}\sum_{k}\partialderivative{\xi_k}{k}\sum_{k^{\prime}}\quantity(W_{k'\to k} - W_{k\to k'})

(28)

where
e(<0)
is the charge of electrons.
Here we used the fact that in equilibrium
J=e/(\hbar L)\sum_{k}\partialderivative*{\xi_k}{k}f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k}=0
.

We can take
k
-summation in Eq.(
28
) explicitly.
After some algebra, we obtain

\displaystyle J=\frac{e\tau}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\xi}}{\hbar}

\displaystyle(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}\quantity(\abs{\gF}^2-\abs{\gB}^2)\quantity((\NL(\xi-\hbar\omega)-\NL(\xi)) - (\NR(\xi-\hbar\omega)-\NR(\xi)))

\displaystyle\times\quantity[F(\xi-\hbar\omega,\xi)-F(\xi,\xi-\hbar\omega)]\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)

(29)

where the factor
(-1)^{\mathrm{R}/\mathrm{L}}
is defined as
(-1)^{\mathrm{R}/\mathrm{L}}=\mathrm{sgn}\quantity(\eval{\pdv{\xi}{k}}_{\RR/\LL})
, and we have used the assumption
(-1)^{\mathrm{L}}=-(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}
.

We can further simplify this expression as

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}e\tau\omega^{2}\quantity(\abs{\gF}^2-\abs{\gB}^2)\quantity(\NR'(0)-\NL'(0))\quantity[\nneq(\omega, \Tp)-\nneq(\omega, \Te)]

(30)

Here,
N_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}(\xi)
is the
\xi
-derivative of
N_{\mathrm{R}}(\xi)
and similar for
N_{\mathrm{L}}^{\prime}(\xi)
.
For details of the derivation of Eqs.(
29
),(
30
), see Appendix
B
.
From Eq.(
30
), we conclude that a temperature difference between phonons and electrons can induce electric currents if
\absolutevalue{\gF}^{2}\neq\absolutevalue{\gB}^{2}
and
N_{\mathrm{L}}^{\prime}(0)\neq N_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}(0)
.

III.3
Calculation of the heat current

We can also calculate heat current
J_{Q}
from phonons to electrons as follows.
J_{Q}
can be defined as

\displaystyle J_{Q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\xi_{k}\quantity(\pdv{f_k}{t})_{\mathrm{phonon}}=\sum_{k}\xi_{k}\sum_{k^{\prime}}(\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}-\mathcal{W}_{k\to k^{\prime}})

(31)

This expression means that the change of energy of electrons due to phonon scattering is equal to the heat current from phonons to electrons. Note that we use here
\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}
instead of
W_{k^{\prime}\to k}
.

Up to
\order{\abs{g_q}^2}
,
\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}-\mathcal{W}_{k\to k^{\prime}}=W_{k^{\prime}\to k}-W_{k\to k^{\prime}}
in the absence of an electric field. By using Eqs.(
22
),(
31
), we obtain

\displaystyle J_{Q}

\displaystyle=2\pi V\hbar\omega^{2}\quantity[\abs{\gF}^2(\NL(0)^2+\NR(0)^2) + 2\abs{\gB}^2 \NR(0)\NL(0)]\quantity[\nneq(\omega,\Tp)-\nneq(\omega, \Te)]

(32)

where we have used an approximation
N_{\mathrm{R}}(\xi)\simeq N_{\mathrm{R}}(0)
and
N_{\mathrm{L}}(\xi)\simeq N_{\mathrm{L}}(0)
(see Appendix
B
for details). Since
J_{Q}
represents the heat current between electrons and phonons, it is proportional to the volume
V=La^{2}
.
In particular, the ratio between
J
and
J_{Q}
is

\displaystyle\frac{J}{J_{Q}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}\frac{e\tau}{2\pi V}\frac{\quantity(\abs{\gF}^2-\abs{\gB}^2)\quantity(\NR'(0)-\NL'(0))}{\absolutevalue{\gF}^{2}(N_{\mathrm{L}}(0)^{2}+N_{\mathrm{R}}(0)^{2})+2\absolutevalue{\gB}^{2}N_{\mathrm{R}}(0)N_{\mathrm{L}}(0)}

(33)

This ratio is independent of temperature.

III.4
Linear response coefficients

We can now calculate linear response coefficients. We define
2\times 2
matrix

as:

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle=L_{11}E+L_{12}\quantity(-\frac{\Delta T}{T})

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=L_{21}E+L_{22}\quantity(-\frac{\Delta T}{T})

\displaystyle L_{12}

\displaystyle L_{11}

\displaystyle L_{21}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}\frac{e\tau\hbar\omega^{3}}{a^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T}(\absolutevalue{\gF}^{2}-\absolutevalue{\gB}^{2})(N_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}(0)-N_{\mathrm{L}}^{\prime}(0))n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{e}})(n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{e}})+1)=L_{12}

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle:=L_{11}=\frac{e^{2}\tau}{4\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}a^{4}}\quantity(\frac{1}{\NR(0)}+\frac{1}{\NL(0)})

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle:=\quantity(\frac{q}{\Delta T})_{j=0}=\frac{L_{11}L_{22}-L_{12}L_{21}}{TL_{11}}\simeq\frac{L_{22}}{T}

IV
Discussion

What we calculated in the previous section is the electric current induced by the nonequilibrium distribution of phonons. As a similar phenomenon, the phonon drag effect on the Seebeck coefficient is well known and extensively investigated
Herring 1954
;
Zhou et al. 2015
;
Matsuura et al. 2019
. Although these two effects might look quite similar, their origins are qualitatively different. In phonon drag, the distribution of phonons which affects the electric current has nonzero net velocity since phonons carry the heat current. These phonons with finite velocity “drag” an electric current. On the other hand, in our calculation, we assume dispersionless phonons. Therefore, their group velocity is zero, resulting in no net velocity.
Instead, the phonon system excites electron-hole pairs with finite velocity.
This picture become clearer when Eq.(
29
) is transformed into the following form.

\displaystyle J=\frac{e\tau}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\xi}}{\hbar}(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}\quantity(\abs{\gF}^2-\abs{\gB}^2)

\displaystyle\times\quantity((\NL(\xi-\hbar\omega)-\NL(\xi)) - (\NR(\xi-\hbar\omega)-\NR(\xi)))

\displaystyle\times\quantity[F(\xi-\hbar\omega,\xi) \nneq(\omega,\Tp)-F(\xi,\xi-\hbar\omega)\qty(\nneq(\omega,\Tp)+1)]

(41)

This form can also be derived directly by using
\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}
instead of
W_{k^{\prime}\to k}
. The function
F(\xi-\hbar\omega,\xi)
in Eq.(
41
) is nothing but the probability for electron-hole pair, i.e., an electron with energy
\xi-\hbar\omega
and a hole with energy
-\xi
, to exist. Therefore, Eq.(
41
) can be understood as an electron-hole pair excitation (relaxation) by a phonon absorption (emission).
In equilibrium, the excitations and relaxations of electron-hole pairs balance and therefore no net current flows, but if the phonons and the electrons have different temperatures, this balance breaks and an electric current flows.

Another way to interpret this effect is to view the fluctuation of phonons as an effective electric field fluctuation. The electron-phonon interaction term we consider originally comes from the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{e-ph}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{\vec{k}}g_{\vec{q}}\hat{\rho}_{\vec{q}}(\hat{a}_{\vec{q}}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{-\vec{q}})

(42)

where
\hat{\rho}_{\vec{q}}
is the Fourier component of electron density, and
\hat{a}_{\vec{q}}
(
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\vec{q}}
) is the annihilation(creation) operator of phonon with wavevector
\vec{q}
. Therefore,
g_{\vec{q}}(\hat{a}_{\vec{q}}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{-\vec{q}})
can be interpreted as an effective electric field
E_{\vec{q}}^{\mathrm{ph}}
. On the other hand, the electric current
J
under electric field
E
can be expanded with respect to
E
as

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}E+\sigma_{2}E^{2}+\dots

(43)

The second term represents the second order response.
The expectation value
\expectationvalue{E_{\vec{q}}^{\mathrm{ph}}}
is always zero and thus no current flows due to linear response. However, its fluctuation,
\expectationvalue{(E_{\vec{q}}^{\mathrm{ph}})^2}\sim\absolutevalue{g_q}^{2}\expectationvalue{\cre{a}_{\vec{q}}\ani{a}_{\vec{q}}}
, is nonzero and can induce an electric current through the second order response. The corresponding term in Eq.(
30
) is the term proportional to
n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{p}})
. Eq.(
30
) also shows that this current is cancelled by the current proportional to
n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{e}})
, as in Eq.(
2
). Therefore, no net current flows in equilibrium, but if the phonons and electrons have different temperatures, the net current can be finite.

As we mentioned in Sec.
III.2
, our calculation shows that it is necessary to have
\absolutevalue{\gF}^{2}\neq\absolutevalue{\gB}^{2}
and
N_{\mathrm{L}}^{\prime}(0)\neq N_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}(0)
to induce finite a thermoelectric current. The first condition requires
q
-dependence of electron-phonon interaction parameter,
g_{q}
. The second condition requires that an asymmetric band structure of electrons is necessary.
To realize an asymmetric band structure in one-dimensional systems, both time reversal symmetry and spatial inversion symmetry need to be broken. Otherwise, wavevector
k
and
-k
can be related to each other through the symmetries and the two Fermi points
\mathrm{L}
and
\mathrm{R}
become equivalent, resulting in
N_{\mathrm{L}}^{\prime}(0)=N_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}(0)
. More physically, these symmetry breakings allow us to set a spatial direction of the materials. When there is a temperature difference between electrons and phonons, an electric current can flow in the direction. Because we now consider setting the spatial direction through the band dispersion of the electron, we need to break time reversal symmetry, but if the inversion symmetry is broken in other ways, e.g., asymmetric contacts with leads
Bosisio et al. 2016
, the time reversal symmetry breaking is not necessary.

From Eq.(
30
) and Eq.(
32
), the order of magnitude estimates of the current densities
j=J/a^{2}
and the heat current per unit volume
q=J_{Q}/V
are given by

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle\sim\frac{e\tau\omega^{2}}{a^{2}}(\Delta\absolutevalue{g}^{2})(\Delta N^{\prime}(0))(n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{p}})-n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{e}}))

(44)

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\sim\hbar\omega^{2}\absolutevalue{g}^{2}N(0)^{2}(n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{p}})-n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{e}}))

(45)

where
\Delta\absolutevalue{g}^{2}=\absolutevalue{\gF}^{2}-\absolutevalue{\gB}^{2}
,
\Delta N^{\prime}(0)=N_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}(0)-N_{\mathrm{L}}^{\prime}(0),g\sim g_{\mathrm{F/B}}
,
N(0)\sim N_{\mathrm{R/L}}
.

To estimate the order of currents, we consider GeTe doped with ferromagnetic Mn impurities. GeTe is a noncentrosymmetric semiconductor and nonreciprocal charge transport under magnetic field is experimentally observed at room temperature
Li et al. 2021
. By doping Mn, it also breaks time reversal symmetry. Therefore, it is a promising material to realize the proposed effect. We assume here that a temperature difference between electrons and phonons in this system is made by, for example, laser heating (for the realization of the temperature difference, see later discussions). Although Mn-doped GeTe is not a one-dimensional system, we roughly estimate the order of magnitude from our one-dimensional calculation.

We estimate the parameters for Mn-doped GeTe by the ones for GeTe
Askarpour and Maassen 2019
;
Li et al. 2021
:
\hbar\omega\sim$10\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}$
,
\tau\sim$1\text{\times}{10}^{-13}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
,
N(0)\sim\varepsilon_{F}^{-1}a^{-3}
,
N^{\prime}(0)\sim\varepsilon_{F}^{-2}a^{-3}
, Fermi energy
\varepsilon_{F}\sim$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}$
, lattice constant
a\sim$5\text{\times}{10}^{-10}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
,
\absolutevalue{g}^{2}\sim$1\text{\times}{10}^{-31}\text{\,}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}^{2}\mathrm{m}^{3}$
. By using lattice constant for parameter
a
, it is equivalent to consider a 1D wire. The value for
\tau\sim$1\text{\times}{10}^{-13}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
is calculated in
Askarpour and Maassen 2019
when considering only the scattering due to electron-phonon coupling. Note that
\tau
we consider in the present work represents all the contributions to the scattering of electrons including electron-phonon interactions as mentioned in Sec.
II
. Therefore, we roughly estimate the order of
\tau
as the one in
Askarpour and Maassen 2019
. The value for
\absolutevalue{g}^{2}\sim$1\text{\times}{10}^{-31}\text{\,}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}^{2}\mathrm{m}^{3}$
is determined so that it does not contradict to the results in
Askarpour and Maassen 2019
. Since the relaxation time for electron-phonon scattering can be evaluated with
\absolutevalue{g}^{2}
as
\tau_{\mathrm{el-ph}}\sim\hbar/(\absolutevalue{g}^{2}N(0)(n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)+1))\sim$1\text{\times}{10}^{-13}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
at
T=$300\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
, the value of
\absolutevalue{g}^{2}
is consistent with the value of
\tau
. We estimate
\Delta N^{\prime}(0)
as
\Delta\varepsilon_{F}/\varepsilon_{F}^{3}a^{3}
and
\Delta\varepsilon_{F}\sim$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}$
, which is of the order of the Zeeman gap due to the doped Mn impurities
Krempaský et al. 2016
. Although the coupling to the magnetic moment of Mn induces band splitting and does not induce the asymmetry of the band according to
Krempaský et al. 2016
, if one can tilt the magnetization by applying a magnetic field, the coupling induces the asymmetry of the band,
\Delta\varepsilon_{F}
.
\Delta\absolutevalue{g}^{2}
is estimated as
\Delta\absolutevalue{g}^{2}/\absolutevalue{g}^{2}\sim 0.01
.

Then

\displaystyle j\sim(n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{p}})-n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{e}}))\times$1\text{\times}{10}^{10}\text{\,}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}^{2}$

(46)

\displaystyle q\sim(n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{p}})-n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{e}}))\times$1\text{\times}{10}^{19}\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}^{3}$

(47)

At
T_{\mathrm{e}}=$300\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
and
\Delta T=T_{\mathrm{p}}-T_{\mathrm{e}}=$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
,
n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{p}})-n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{e}})\sim 0.001
, and
j\sim$1\text{\times}{10}^{7}\text{\,}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}^{2}$,q\sim$1\text{\times}{10}^{16}\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\mathrm{m}^{-3}$
. This value of the electric current density is experimentally observable. As we discuss later, we believe that
\Delta T=$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
is also experimentally realizable. Note that the inequality (
27
) becomes
\Delta T/T\ll 1
for the above parameters.

Using the same parameters, we can also estimate the quantities defined in the previous section,

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle\sim\quantity(\frac{\hbar\omega}{\kB T})^{2}n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)(n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)+1)\times$1\text{\times}{10}^{17}\text{\,}\mathrm{W}{K}^{-1}\mathrm{m}^{-3}$

(48a)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\sim\quantity(\frac{\hbar\omega}{\kB T})^{4}\quantity(\nneq(\omega)(\nneq(\omega)+1))^{2}\times$1\text{\times}{10}^{9}\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\mathrm{K}^{-2}\mathrm{m}^{-3}$

(48b)

\displaystyle ZT

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)(n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)+1)\times 10^{-5}

(48c)

At
T=$300\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
,
\lambda
is of the order of
1\text{\times}{10}^{17}\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\mathrm{K}^{-1}\mathrm{m}^{-3}
. This corresponds to the heat current between electrons and phonons, not to the heat current flowing spatially. A similar quantity is considered in, for example, a model called 2T-model. 2T-model describes a relaxation process of an electron-phonon coupled system after laser heating
Chen et al. 2006
. In this model, a parameter for the heat current between electrons and phonons is set to
\sim$1\text{\times}{10}^{16}\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\mathrm{K}^{-1}\mathrm{m}^{-3}$
for gold.

If one can heat either electrons or phonons and make temperature difference
\Delta T\sim$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
, the corresponding power output per volume will be

at
T=$300\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
. This is quite large because the value corresponds to power factor
\sim$1\text{\times}{10}^{3}\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\mathrm{K}^{-2}\mathrm{m}^{-1}$
for conventional thermoelectric materials when
\nabla T\sim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{K}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}$
.
In contrast,
ZT\sim$1\text{\times}{10}^{-5}\text{\,}$
are very small compared to conventional thermoelectric materials. This is because the relaxation through electron-phonon interaction is so fast that the heat current between electrons and phonons is much larger than the heat current flowing spatially in the conventional thermoelectric effects. The large heat current of the proposed effect results in low efficiency, but the power output is still quite large. According to Eq.(
40d
),
ZT
increases monotonically as temperature increases at high temperatures if
\tau
is independent of temperature. However, it should be noted that we approximate the band structure near the chemical potential in the derivation of these expressions, Eq.(
40d
) is valid only when the temperature is much smaller than the chemical potential,
k_{\mathrm{B}}T\ll\varepsilon_{F}
. If
\tau
is due to the electron-phonon interaction,
\tau
also depends on the temperature, and it decreases as the temperature increases (Eq.(
26
)). In that case

can be estimated by using Eq.(
26
) as

\displaystyle ZT\sim\quantity(\frac{\Delta\abs{g}^2}{\abs{g}^2})^{2}\quantity(\frac{\hbar\omega\Delta N'(0)}{N(0)})^{2}\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)

(49)

and at high temperature
\hbar\omega\ll k_{\mathrm{B}}T\ll\varepsilon_{F}
where the expression is still valid,
ZT
is independent of the temperature.

To realize our model experimentally, it is necessary to make the temperature difference between the phonon system and the electron system. One way to make such a situation is to excite only phonons (or only electrons) in a material with light or laser. By appropriately choosing the polarization and frequency of light, it would be possible to selectively excite either phonons or electrons. It is discussed in literature
Chen et al. 2006
how the temperatures of electrons and phonons change when the system is heated by ultrashort-pulsed lasers. In this case, only electrons are excited by the laser pulse. Although the duration of the temperature difference between electrons and phonons is quite short (
\sim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{s}$
), the maximum of the temperature difference reaches of the order of
1\text{\times}{10}^{3}\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
according to
Chen et al. 2006
.
Another idea is to use both an insulator and a metal. For example, a junction of insulator and metal with different temperatures may be one possible realization. A similar situation is discussed in
Schreier et al. 2013
, and it suggests that the temperature difference between electrons and phonons can be
\sim$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
at steady state near an interface between a metal and a magnetic insulator.
It may be also possible to use a topological insulator such as Bi
2
Te
3
, instead of a metal. In that case, the corresponding electron system is a surface state of the topological insulator. However, as discussed above, we have to break time reversal symmetry and inversion symmetry to set the spatial direction through the band structure. Therefore, in the case of Bi
2
Te
3
, we also have to apply a magnetic field. Although the surface state is not guaranteed in the presence of the magnetic field, if the magnetic field is small so that the splitting of the surface state bands due to the magnetic field is much smaller than the thermal energy, the proposed effect is expected to be realized. The condition on the field strength
B
is given by
\sqrt{\hbar v^{2}eB}\ll k_{\mathrm{B}}T
, where
v
is the velocity of the Dirac electron. In the case of Bi
2
Te
3
,
v\sim$1\text{\times}{10}^{5}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$
and thus the condition is
B\ll$100\text{\,}\mathrm{T}$
at
T=$300\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
. It should be noted that, from the point of view of symmetry, thermoelectric effects similar to the proposed effect may be realized even without a magnetic field if the inversion symmetry is broken. For example, in the case of Bi
2
Te
3
, the inversion symmetry is broken on the surface, so there is a possibility that a thermoelectric effect due to the temperature difference between electrons and phonons can be realized without a magnetic field by a mechanism different from the proposed mechanism.

So far, we have discussed the case where phonons and electrons feel different temperatures and show that the electric current can flow.
Similarly, if there are degrees of freedom which feel different temperatures from that of electrons, then similar physics could occur. For example, one can replace phonons with another electron system. In that case, an electron density fluctuation in one system induces electric currents in the other electronic system mediated by electron-electron interactions. Another candidate is spin degrees of freedom. In the case of the magnon-driven spin Seebeck effect
Xiao et al. 2010
, the temperature difference between electrons and magnons induces a spin current. It is also possible to induce electric current by the temperature difference between the electron system and the spin system if there is a certain interaction between them, e.g., spin-orbit interaction. In any of these systems, it should break the inversion symmetry to induce finite thermoelectric effects.

In conclusion, we propose a new thermoelectric effect induced by the temperature difference between phonons and electrons. This effect can be realized in metal-insulator junctions. Similar effects are expected in noncentrosymmetric systems if one can make a temperature difference between electrons and other degrees of freedom such as spins.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k\in\mathrm{R}}\partialderivative{\xi_k}{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{R}}\frac{\differential{k}}{2\pi}\partialderivative{\xi_k}{k}=\int_{\mathrm{R}}\frac{\differential{\xi}}{2\pi}\absolutevalue{\pdv{k}{\xi_k}}\partialderivative{\xi_k}{k}=\int_{\mathrm{R}}\frac{\differential{\xi}}{2\pi}(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}

(58)

Since the integrand is appreciable only when
\xi\simeq 0
, we can extend the range of integration to
[-\infty,\infty]
. Then we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k\in\mathrm{R}}\partialderivative{\xi_k}{k}\quantity(W_{k'\to k}-W_{k\to k'})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\xi}}{\hbar}(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}\left\{\quantity(\abs{\gB}^2\NL(\xi-\hbar\omega) + \abs{\gF}^2\NR(\xi-\hbar\omega))\quantity[F(\xi-\hbar\omega,\xi)-F(\xi,\xi-\hbar\omega)]\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\quantity(\abs{\gB}^2\NL(\xi) + \abs{\gF}^2\NR(\xi))\quantity[F(\xi,\xi-\hbar\omega)-F(\xi-\hbar\omega,\xi)]\right\}\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\xi}}{\hbar}(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}\quantity(\abs{\gB}^2(\NL(\xi-\hbar\omega)-\NL(\xi)) + \abs{\gF}^2(\NR(\xi-\hbar\omega)-\NR(\xi)))\quantity[F(\xi-\hbar\omega,\xi) \nneq(\omega)-F(\xi,\xi-\hbar\omega)\qty(\nneq(\omega)+1)]

(59)

and similar for summation over
k\in\mathrm{L}
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\partialderivative{\xi_k}{k}\quantity(W_{k'\to k}-W_{k\to k'})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\xi}}{\hbar}(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}\quantity(\abs{\gF}^2(\NL(\xi-\hbar\omega)-\NL(\xi)) + \abs{\gB}^2(\NR(\xi-\hbar\omega)-\NR(\xi)))\quantity[F(\xi-\hbar\omega,\xi) \nneq(\omega)-F(\xi,\xi-\hbar\omega)\qty(\nneq(\omega)+1)]

\displaystyle+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\xi}}{\hbar}(-1)^{\mathrm{L}}\quantity(\abs{\gB}^2(\NL(\xi-\hbar\omega)-\NL(\xi)) + \abs{\gF}^2(\NR(\xi-\hbar\omega)-\NR(\xi)))\quantity[F(\xi-\hbar\omega,\xi) \nneq(\omega)-F(\xi,\xi-\hbar\omega)\qty(\nneq(\omega)+1)]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\xi}}{\hbar}(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}\quantity(\abs{\gF}^2-\abs{\gB}^2)\quantity((\NL(\xi-\hbar\omega)-\NL(\xi)) - (\NR(\xi-\hbar\omega)-\NR(\xi)))\quantity[F(\xi-\hbar\omega,\xi)-F(\xi,\xi-\hbar\omega)]\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)

(60)

where we assumed that
(-1)^{\mathrm{L}}=-(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}
. From Eq.(
60
), we obtain Eq.(
29
) in the main text. The following formulas and approximations are useful.

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential{\xi}F(\xi,\xi-\hbar\omega)

\displaystyle=\hbar\omega n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{e}})

(61)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential{\xi}F(\xi-\hbar\omega,\xi)

\displaystyle=\hbar\omega(n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{e}})+1)

(62)

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{R}}(\xi-\hbar\omega)-N_{\mathrm{R}}(\xi)

\displaystyle\simeq-\hbar\omega N_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}(0)

(63)

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{L}}(\xi-\hbar\omega)-N_{\mathrm{L}}(\xi)

\displaystyle\simeq-\hbar\omega N_{\mathrm{L}}^{\prime}(0)

(64)

where
n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega,T_{\mathrm{e}})
is the Bose distribution function with temperature
T_{\mathrm{e}}
. By making use of these formulas and approximations, we can simplify Eq.(
29
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\partialderivative{\xi_k}{k}\quantity(W_{k'\to k}-W_{k\to k'})=\frac{(-1)^{\mathrm{R}}}{\hbar}\quantity(\abs{\gF}^2-\abs{\gB}^2)(-\hbar^{2}\omega^{2})\quantity(\NL'(0)-\NR'(0))\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)

(65)

Therefore, from Eq.(
28
), we obtain the result Eq.(
30
).

Similarly, we can obtain the expression for the heat current by using Eqs.(
22a
),(
22b
),(
31
).

\displaystyle J_{Q}

\displaystyle=V\int\differential{\xi}\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\xi\quantity[\NR(\xi)\qty(\abs{\gB}^2\NL(\xi-\hbar\omega) + \abs{\gF}^2\NR(\xi-\hbar\omega))+ \NL(\xi)\qty(\abs{\gF}^2\NL(\xi-\hbar\omega) + \abs{\gB}^2\NR(\xi-\hbar\omega))]

\displaystyle\times\quantity[F(\xi-\hbar\omega,\xi)-F(\xi,\xi-\hbar\omega)]\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)

\displaystyle+V\int\differential{\xi}\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\xi\quantity[\NR(\xi)\qty(\abs{\gB}^2\NL(\xi+\hbar\omega) + \abs{\gF}^2\NR(\xi+\hbar\omega))+ \NL(\xi)\qty(\abs{\gF}^2\NL(\xi+\hbar\omega) + \abs{\gB}^2\NR(\xi+\hbar\omega))]

\displaystyle\times\quantity[F(\xi+\hbar\omega,\xi)-F(\xi,\xi+\hbar\omega)]\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\simeq 2\pi V\omega\quantity[\NR(0)\qty(\abs{\gB}^2\NL(0) + \abs{\gF}^2\NR(0))+ \NL(0)\qty(\abs{\gF}^2\NL(0) + \abs{\gB}^2\NR(0))]

\displaystyle\quad\times\int\differential{\xi}\quantity[F(\xi-\hbar\omega,\xi)-F(\xi,\xi-\hbar\omega)]\delta n^{\mathrm{eq}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=2\pi V\hbar\omega^{2}\quantity[\abs{\gF}^2(\NL(0)^2+\NR(0)^2) + 2\abs{\gB}^2 \NR(0)\NL(0)]\quantity[\nneq(\omega,\Tp)-\nneq(\omega, \Te)]

(66)

where we have used
\sum_{k}=V\int\differential{\xi}N(\xi)
with the volume
V
, and an approximation
N_{\mathrm{R/L}}(\xi)\simeq N_{\mathrm{R/L}}(0)
. The last expression is Eq.(
32
) in the main text.

Appendix C
Interpretation of the definition of the heat current

We can transform the definition of the heat current Eq.(
31
) into the following form.

\displaystyle J_{Q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}(\xi_{k}-\xi_{k^{\prime}})\mathcal{W}_{k^{\prime}\to k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\frac{\absolutevalue{g_{k'-k}}^{2}}{V}f_{k^{\prime}}\quantity(1-f_k)\hbar\omega_{k^{\prime}-k}\quantity[n_{k'-k} \delta(\ene_{k'} - \ene_k+\hbar\omega_{k'-k}) - \qty(n_{k'-k}+1)\delta(\ene_{k'}-\ene_k-\hbar\omega_{k'-k})]

(67)

Here, we have used
\mathcal{W}_{k\to k^{\prime}}
instead of
W_{k\to k^{\prime}}
. The form of Eq.(
67
) might be easier to intuitively understand than Eq.(
31
). The first term in the summation in Eq.(
67
) represents the probability for electrons to absorb phonon multiplied by the absorbed energy
\hbar\omega_{k^{\prime}-k}
. Similarly, the second term corresponds to the phonon-emitting process.
