Delta family approach for the stochastic control problems of utility maximization
Jingtang Ma, Zhengyang Lu, Zhenyu Cui

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a new approach for stochastic control problems arising from utility maximization. The main idea is to directly start from the dynamical programming equation and compute the conditional expectation using a novel representation of the conditional density function through the Dirac Delta function and the corresponding series representation. We obtain an explicit series representation of the value function, whose coefficients are expressed through integration of the value function at a later time point against a chosen basis function. Thus we are able to set up a recursive integration time-stepping scheme to compute the optimal value function given the known terminal condition, e.g. utility function. Due to tensor decomposition property of the Dirac Delta function in high dimensions, it is straightforward to extend our approach to solving high-dimensional stochastic control problems. The backward recursive nature of the method also allows for solving stochastic control and stopping problems, i.e. mixed control problems. We illustrate the method through solving some two-dimensional stochastic control (and stopping) problems, including the case under the classical and rough Heston stochastic volatility models, and stochastic local volatility models such as the stochastic alpha beta rho (SABR) model.



Keywords:
stochastic control, Dirac Delta function, Delta sequence, HJB equation

Introduction

Stochastic control generally refers to the study of dynamical systems subject to random perturbations and depending on the unknown control, and seeks to find the optimal control that maximizes or minimizes certain performance functions. It has applications in many areas in science, including applied mathematics, engineering, economics, and finance. In finance, it is usually associated with the maximization of expected utility function evaluated at the terminal wealth. There is a vast literature on expected utility maximization or optimal portfolio choice starting from the seminal work of
Merton 1969
. There are two fundamental approaches to solving stochastic control problems: first is the Bellman’s dynamical programming principle (DPP), which leads to the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) partial differential equation (PDE) to characterize the optimal value function; second is the Pontryagin’s maximum principle. In recent years there have been considerable developments within both approaches, in particular inspired by problems arising from mathematical finance. The introduction of viscosity solution allows for going beyond the classical verification Bellman approach, and sets a rigorous and suitable framework for studying stochastic control using the DPP approach. On the other hand, the Pontryagin’s maximum principle finds a suitable presentation with the backward stochastic differential equations (BSDEs). The viscosity solution and BSDE are related through the PDE representation.

There are various methods for solving stochastic control problems arising from utility maximizations in the literature. One approach is of analytical nature and focuses on numerically solving the related HJB equation, which is a nonlinear PDE, see
Forsyth and Labahn 2007
;
White and Reisinger 2011
. An alternative approach is of probabilistic nature and utilizes the martingale duality method, see
Karatzas et al. 1991
;
Cvitanic and Karatzas 1992
. A recent account of the current literature is provided in
Pham 2009
and references therein. An extension to the finite-maturity utility maximization problem is to allow the investor to “exit” before the maturity in order to achieve the overall maximization of the expected utility. This leads to a mixed stochastic control problem which involves both optimal control and optimal stopping, see
Karatzas and Wang 2000
. Although there is extensive literature on the study of one-dimensional stochastic control problems, in contrast, the literature on high-dimensional stochastic control is relatively thin, see
Belomestny et al. 2010
for a Monte Carlo regression approach, and
Han and E 2016
,
Huré et al. 2021
for deep learning approach. Note that there are also further extensions of the utility maximization problem to allow for (fixed or proportional) transaction costs
(
Guasoni and Muhle-Karbe 2013
)
or taxes
(
Tahar 2010
)
. We focus on the high-dimensional finite-maturity optimal control and mixed control problems in this paper, and leave the consideration of these further extensions to future research.

The motivation behind our proposed approach stems from the following observation: the dynamical programming equation involves the computation of the associated conditional expectation whose payoff function is the optimal value function solved from the previous step, and in the first step the payoff function will be the given utility function. The traditional approach to derive the HJB equation involves rewriting this conditional expectation as the solution to a corresponding parabolic PDE through the Feynman Kac theorem, whose coefficients depend on the unknown control. Then we “maximize” the PDE with respect to the unknown control, and substitute the solution back to the PDE to arrive at the nonlinear HJB PDE. We take an alternative route and express the conditional expectation using the conditional density function, for which we find a representation using property of the Dirac Delta function and its series representation.

The contributions of the paper are three-fold. First, we propose a novel time-stepping scheme to solve HJB equations involving recursive integrations at each step. The method is straightforwardly extended to the high-dimensional stochastic control problems. Second, the method can be applied to mixed control problems involving optimal stopping. Third, we demonstrate the accurateness and efficiency of the method in two-dimensional stochastic control problems arising from applications, involving Heston, rough Heston and representative stochastic local volatility models, e.g. the stochastic alpha beta rho (SABR) model.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section
2
introduces the delta family method for both one-dimensional and high-dimensional stochastic control problems. Section
3
illustrates the applications of the method to solving the optimal investment problem under the Heston model, the optimal reinsurance and investment problem under the Heston and rough Heston model, and also a mixed stochastic control problem under general stochastic local volatility models. Section
4
concludes the paper.

Lemma 2.2

We have the following representation of the

{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf x}}\left[\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta(X_{t+h}^{(i)}-y_{i})\right]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta(u_{i}-y_{i})\cdot f({\bf u}\mid{\bf x})d{\bf u}=f({\bf y}\mid{\bf x}),

\quad\Box

2.2
Delta family algorithm for the stochastic control problems

Consider a general 1-D stochastic control problem with its value function characterized by

\displaystyle V(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}[U(X_{T})\mid X_{t}=x],

where
U
represents the utility function that is continuous on
[0,+\infty
) and satisfies
U(x)=-\infty
for
x<0
, and
U(x)\leq{C}(1+x^{\varrho})
for some constants
C>0
and
0<\varrho<1
.
The process
X_{t}
depends on the unknown control
\pi
.
From Bellman’s dynamical programming principle, we have the following characterization of the value function:

\displaystyle V(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}[V(t+h,X_{t+h})\mid X_{t}=x].

(2.6)

Based on (
2.6
), the traditional approach proceeds as follows:

1.

Assume
V(t,x)\in C^{1,2}
, and then apply the Ito’s lemma to rewrite the right hand side of (
2.6
) into a differential form.

2.

Then take the limit
h\rightarrow 0
to characterize the local behavior of the value function. This is achieved through obtaining the HJB PDE, which is a highly nonlinear PDE and challenging to solve.

We shall take an alternative approach, and the idea is to rewrite the conditional expectation in (
2.6
) using the conditional transition density function, and then use our previous representation in (
2.3
) to represent the density function. Note that the unknown control is only associated with the conditional density function.
We have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[V(t+h,X_{t+h})\mid X_{t}=x]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}V(t+h,y)f(y\mid x)dy

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}V(t+h,y)g_{k}\left(y\right)dy\right){\mathbb{E}}_{x}[g_{k}(X_{t+h})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}V(t+h,y)g_{k}\left(y\right)dy\right)\left(\int_{t}^{t+h}{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi}g_{k}(X_{u})]du+g_{k}(x)\right),

(2.7)

where
{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi}
is the infinitesimal generator of the Markov process
X
, and note that it contains the unknown control.
Plugging (
2.7
) into (
2.6
), we have

\displaystyle V(t,x)=\sup\limits_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}[V(t+h,X_{t+h})\mid X_{t}=x]

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}V(t+h,y)g_{k}\left(y\right)dy\right)\left(\int_{t}^{t+h}{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi}g_{k}(X_{u})]du+g_{k}(x)\right).

(2.8)

Assume that
h\rightarrow 0
is very small, from the mean value theorem, we have the following approximation:
\pi_{u}\rightarrow\pi_{t}=\pi
, which is an unknown constant, and
X_{u}\rightarrow X_{t}=x
. To summarize

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{t+h}{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi}g_{k}(X_{u})]du

\displaystyle\approx h\cdot{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi}g_{k}(x).

(2.9)

Combining (
2.2
) and (
2.9
), we have

\displaystyle V(t,x)\approx\sup\limits_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}V(t+h,y)g_{k}\left(y\right)dy\right)\left(h\cdot{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi}g_{k}(x)+g_{k}(x)\right).

(2.10)

The terminal condition is given by

V(T,x)=U(x),

where
U(\cdot)
is the known utility function.

Let
\pi^{*}
be the solution of

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}V(t+h,y)g_{k}\left(y\right)dy\right)\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi}g_{k}(x)}{\partial\pi}=0.

(2.11)

Then inserting
\pi=\pi^{*}
into (
2.10
) gives that

\displaystyle V(t,x)\approx\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}V(t+h,y)g_{k}\left(y\right)dy\right)\left(h\cdot{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi^{*}}g_{k}(x)+g_{k}(x)\right).

(2.12)

Taking inner product to (
2.12
) with
g_{j}(x),\;j=0,1,\ldots
and denoting

V_{j}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}V(t,x)g_{j}(x)dx,\quad j=0,1,\ldots,

we obtain that

\displaystyle V_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\approx V_{j}(t+h)+h\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}V_{k}(t+h)\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}g_{j}(x){\mathcal{L}}^{\pi^{*}}g_{k}(x)dx,\quad j=0,1,\ldots,

(2.13)

with
\pi^{*}
being the solution of (
2.11
) which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}V_{k}(t+h)\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi}g_{k}(x)}{\partial\pi}=0.

(2.14)

Using the orthogonality of the basis functions
g_{j}(x)

V(t,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}V_{j}(t)g_{j}(x).

(2.15)

Truncating the sums in (

), (
2.14

(Delta family algorithm for 1-D stochastic control problems)

1:

We shall divide the interval

\displaystyle V^{n}_{j}

For the high-dimensional Markov process, consider the following generic stochastic control problem:
Consider a general stochastic control problem with its value function characterized by

\displaystyle V(t,{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{{\pi}\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}[U({\bf X}_{T})\mid{\bf X_{t}=x}].

From Bellman’s dynamical programming principle, we have the following characterization of the value function:

\displaystyle V(t,{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{{\pi}\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}[V(t+h,{\bf X}_{t+h})\mid{\bf X_{t}=x}].

(2.18)

2.20
) which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k_{1}=0}^{M_{1}}\cdots\sum\limits_{k_{n}=0}^{M_{n}}V_{{\bf k}}^{n+1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\pi}{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi}\left(\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(x_{i})\right)=0.

The terminal condition

V^{N}_{{\bf m}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}}U({\bf x})\prod\limits_{j=1}^{d}g_{m_{j}}^{(j)}(x_{j})d{\bf x}.

(2.25)

The integrals in (
2.24
) and (
2.25
) can be evaluated by quadrature rules.

3:

Finally from (
2.23
), we obtain the approximation of the value functions

V(t_{n},{\bf x})\approx\sum\limits_{m_{1}=0}^{M_{1}}\cdots\sum\limits_{m_{n}=0}^{M_{n}}V_{{\bf m}}^{n}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{d}g_{m_{j}}^{(j)}(x_{j}),

and the optimal strategies

\displaystyle\pi^{*}(t_{n},x)

\displaystyle\approx\pi^{*}_{n}(x),\quad n=0,1,\ldots,N.

3
Applications and examples

In this section, we apply the delta family algorithms to solve the optimal investment problems under the Heston model, the optimal reinsurance and investment problems under respectively the Heston model and the rough Heston model, and optimal stopping investment problems under general stochastic local volatility (SLV) models

3.1
Optimal investment under the Heston model

Consider the Heston model:

\displaystyle\frac{dS_{t}}{S_{t}}

\displaystyle=(r+\lambda\mathcal{V}_{t})dt+\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{t}}dW_{t}^{(1)},

(3.1)

\displaystyle d\mathcal{V}_{t}

\displaystyle=\kappa(\theta-\mathcal{V}_{t})dt+\sigma\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{t}}dW_{t}^{(2)},

(3.2)

where
E[dW_{t}^{(1)}dW_{t}^{(2)}]=\rho dt
.
By definition, the wealth process is given by

\displaystyle\frac{dX_{t}}{X_{t}}

\displaystyle=\pi_{t}\frac{dS_{t}}{S_{t}}+(1-\pi_{t})rdt=\lambda\pi_{t}\mathcal{V}_{t}dt+\pi_{t}\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{t}}dW_{t}^{(1)},

(3.3)

where
\mathcal{V}
is given by (
3.2
).
The infinitesimal generator of (
3.3
) is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi}f(x,v)=\lambda\pi vx\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+\kappa(\theta-v)\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}+\frac{\pi^{2}vx^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}v}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial v^{2}}+\rho\pi\sigma xv\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x\partial v}.

(3.4)

A key observation is that the above infinitesimal generator is still a quadratic function in
\pi
. This is very convenient in working out the explicit optimizers, as seen in the one-dimensional case. To use the Legendre functions and the Gauss-Legendre quadrature rules, we set
(x,v)\in[x_{\min},x_{\max}]\times[v_{\min},v_{\max}]
and transform it into
(y_{1},y_{2})\in\Omega^{2}:=[-1,1]^{2}
using the following transformation

\displaystyle\hat{x}(y_{1})

\displaystyle=(x_{\max}-x_{\min})(y_{1}+1)/2+x_{\min},

(3.5)

\displaystyle\hat{v}(y_{2})

\displaystyle=(v_{\max}-v_{\min})(y_{2}+1)/2+v_{\min},

(3.6)

\displaystyle\tilde{x}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(x-x_{\min})}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}-1,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\tilde{v}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(v-v_{\min})}{v_{\max}-v_{\min}}-1.

(3.8)

By combining (
2.19
) and (
3.4
), we obtain that

\displaystyle V(t,x,v)\approx\sup\limits_{{\pi}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\Omega^{2}}V(t+h,\hat{x}(y_{1}),\hat{v}(y_{2}))g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(y_{1})g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(y_{2})d{\bf y}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))+h\cdot{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi}\left(g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))\right)\right),

(3.9)

where
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2})
, and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi}\left(g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\pi vx\frac{dg_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))}{dx}g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))+\kappa(\theta-v)g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))\frac{dg_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))}{dv}+\frac{\pi^{2}vx^{2}}{2}\frac{d^{2}g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))}{dx^{2}}g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}v}{2}g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))\frac{d^{2}g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))}{dv^{2}}+\rho\pi\sigma xv\frac{dg_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))}{dx}\frac{dg_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))}{dv}.

The first order condition gives that

\displaystyle\pi^{*}=

(3.10)

\displaystyle\frac{-\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\Omega^{2}}V(t+h,\hat{x}(y_{1}),\hat{v}(y_{2}))g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(y_{1})g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(y_{2})d{\bf y}\right)\left(\lambda g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))+\rho\sigma\frac{dg_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))}{dv}\right)\frac{dg_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))}{dx}}{x\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\Omega^{2}}V(t+h,\hat{x}(y_{1}),\hat{v}(y_{2}))g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(y_{1})g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(y_{2})d{\bf y}\right)\frac{d^{2}g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))}{dx^{2}}g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))}.

Inserting (
3.10
) into (
3.1
) gives that

\displaystyle V(t,x,v)\approx\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\Omega^{2}}V(t+h,\hat{x}(y_{1}),\hat{v}(y_{2}))g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(y_{1})g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(y_{2})d{\bf y}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))+h\cdot{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi^{\ast}}\left(g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))\right)\right).

Denote

V_{m_{1}m_{2}}(t):=\int_{\Omega^{2}}V(t,\hat{x}(y_{1}),\hat{x}(y_{2}))g_{m_{1}}^{(1)}(y_{1})g_{m_{2}}^{(2)}(y_{2})d\bf{y}.

Then the time-stepping algorithm as the corollary of Algorithm
3
is given as follows.

1:

(3.11)

where

(3.12)

The integrals in (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) are evaluated by Gauss-Legendre quadrature rules.

3:

Finally we obtain the approximation of the value functions

V(t_{n},x,v)\approx\sum\limits_{m_{1}=0}^{M_{1}}\sum\limits_{m_{2}=0}^{M_{2}}V_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{n}g_{m_{1}}^{(1)}(\hat{x}(x))g_{m_{2}}^{(2)}(\hat{v}(v)),

for
m_{1}=0,1,\ldots,M_{1};\,m_{2}=0,1,\ldots,M_{2};\,n=0,1,\ldots,N

and the optimal strategies

\displaystyle\pi^{*}(t_{n},x,v)

\displaystyle\approx\pi^{*}_{n}(x,v).

3.2
Optimal reinsurance-investment under (rough) Heston models

Assume that the surplus process of the insurer satisfies (
Bäuerle 2005
)

dR_{t}=c\left[\eta-\vartheta(1-\widehat{q}_{t})\right]dt+b\widehat{q}_{t}dW_{t},

where
c,\,b,\,\eta
are positive constants representing the claim rate, volatility and safety loading of the insurer, respectively, and
W_{t}
is the standard Brownian motion representing the uncertainty of the insurance market. The insurer chooses to participate in the reinsurance market to reduce the underlying risks or take an extra insurance business.
\widehat{q}_{t}
is the proportion of reinsurance at time
t
, which means that the insurer undertakes
100\widehat{q}_{t}\%
of the claims while the reinsurer undertakes the rest. Meanwhile, the insurer has to pay a premium at the rate of
c\vartheta(1-\widehat{q}_{t})
to the reinsurer due to the reinsurance business, where
\vartheta
denotes the safety loading of reinsurer and
\vartheta>\eta
is satisfied to exclude the arbitrage.
Further, the insurer has an option to invest in the continuous-time financial market that consists of one risky asset and one risk-free asset with interest
r>0
to get more benefits. The risky asset price
S
satisfies process (
3.1
) and the variance
\mathcal{V}
follows Heston model (
3.2
) or rough Heston model

\mathcal{V}_{t}=\mathcal{V}_{0}+\int^{t}_{0}K(t-s)\Big(\kappa(\theta-\mathcal{V}_{s})ds+\sigma\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{s}}dW_{s}^{2}\Big),

where
K(t)=\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}
and
0<\alpha<1
.
The standard Brownian motion
W_{t}^{1}
for the risky asset price
S
and
W_{t}^{2}
for the process of variance
\mathcal{V}
are correlated with correlation coefficient
-1\leq\rho\leq 1
and they are both independent of
W_{t}
for the surplus process.

We denote the wealth process of the insurer at time
t
by
\widehat{X}_{t}
. Assume that the amount of wealth (investment strategy) invested in the risky asset
S
and the risk-free asset
B
is
\widehat{\pi}_{t}
and
\widehat{X}_{t}-\widehat{\pi}_{t}
, respectively. Consequently, the dynamic equation of the wealth process
\widehat{X}_{t}
evolves according to

\displaystyle d\widehat{X}_{t}=r\widehat{X}_{t}dt+\lambda\widehat{\pi}_{t}\mathcal{V}_{t}dt+\widehat{\pi}_{t}\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{t}}dW_{t}^{1}+c(\eta-\vartheta)dt+c\vartheta\widehat{q}_{t}dt+b\widehat{q}_{t}dW_{t},

(3.13)

with
\widehat{X}_{t}=\widehat{x}
. Here
(\widehat{q}_{t},\widehat{\pi}_{t})
denotes admissible reinsurance-investment strategy. A reinsurance-investment strategy
\widehat{u}_{t}
,
{t\in[0,T]}
is said to be admissible if it satisfies that
(\widehat{q}_{t},\widehat{\pi}_{t})
is progressively measurable in regards to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
which satisfies
E\Big[\int_{0}^{T}b^{2}\widehat{q}^{2}_{t}dt\Big]<\infty
and
E\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\widehat{\pi}^{2}_{t}\mathcal{V}_{t}dt\Big]<\infty
, and there exists a unique strong solution to (
3.13
). The set of all admissible strategies is denoted by
\widehat{\Pi}
.

The aim of the insurer is to find the best strategy to maximize the expected utility of the terminal wealth with minimum guaranteed threshold
L\geq 0
, that is

\sup_{\widehat{u}\in{\widehat{\Pi}}}E\big[U(\widehat{X}_{T}-L)\big],

(3.14)

where
\widehat{X}_{t}
is subject to (
3.13
) and
\widehat{X}_{T}\geq{L}
.
To solve utility maximization (
3.14
), using

D(t):=c(\eta-\vartheta)\int^{T}_{t}e^{-r(s-t)}ds
,
X_{t}:=\widehat{X}_{t}+D(t)
,
\pi_{t}:=\widehat{\pi}_{t}/X_{t}
and
q_{t}:=\widehat{q}_{t}/X_{t}
, the wealth process (
3.13
) is rewritten as

dX_{t}=X_{t}\Big[rdt+\lambda\pi_{t}\mathcal{V}_{t}dt+\pi_{t}\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{t}}dW_{t}^{1}+c\vartheta{q}_{t}dt+b{q}_{t}dW_{t}\Big].

(3.15)

Obviously, the reinsurance-investment strategy
(q_{t},\pi_{t})
in (
3.15
) is also the admissible strategy and its admissible set is denoted by
\Pi
. Note that
X_{T}=\widehat{X}_{T}
. Thus, the equivalent form of utility maximization problem (
3.14
) is

\sup_{(q,\pi)\in{{\Pi}}}E\big[U({X}_{T}-L)\big],

(3.16)

For the Heston model, we define the value function as

V(t,x,v)=\sup_{(q,\pi)\in{{\Pi}}}E\big[U\big(X_{T}-L\big)|X_{t}=x,\mathcal{V}_{t}=v\big],

where
X_{t}
satisfies (
3.15
),
X_{T}\geq L
and
x=\widehat{x}+c(\eta-\vartheta){\displaystyle\int^{T}_{t}}e^{-rs}ds
.
The terminal condition is

V(T,x,v)=U\big(x-L\big).

Now we apply Algorithm
2
to solve the problem.
Denote the infinitesimal generator of (
3.15
) by
{\mathcal{L}}^{q,\pi}
. Then we derive that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{q,\pi}\left(g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))\right)

\displaystyle=(\lambda\pi v+c\vartheta q)x\frac{dg_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))}{dx}g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))+\kappa(\theta-v)g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))\frac{dg_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))}{dv}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}v}{2}g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))\frac{d^{2}g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))}{dv^{2}}+\frac{(\pi^{2}v+b^{2}q^{2})x^{2}}{2}\frac{d^{2}g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))}{dx^{2}}g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))

\displaystyle\quad+\rho\pi\sigma xv\frac{dg_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))}{dx}\frac{dg_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))}{dv},

where
\hat{x},\,\hat{v},\,\tilde{x},\,\tilde{v}
are given by (
3.5
) - (
3.8
).
The strategies
\pi^{\ast}
is given by (
3.10
) and
q^{\ast}

\displaystyle q^{*}=-\frac{c\vartheta\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\Omega^{2}}V(t+h,\hat{x}(y_{1}),\hat{v}(y_{2}))g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(y_{1})g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(y_{2})d{\bf y}\right)\frac{dg_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))}{dx}g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))}{b^{2}x\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\Omega^{2}}V(t+h,\hat{x}(y_{1}),\hat{v}(y_{2}))g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(y_{1})g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(y_{2})d{\bf y}\right)\frac{d^{2}g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{x}(x))}{dx^{2}}g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}(v))}.

Then the rest steps are similar to Algorithm
3
.

For rough Heston model, the fractional kernel can be expressed as the Laplace transform of a positive measure
\zeta

K(t)=\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\gamma t}\zeta(d\gamma),\quad\zeta(d\gamma)=\frac{\gamma^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}d\gamma.

Then
\zeta
is approximated by a finite sum of Dirac measures
\zeta^{\ell}=\sum^{\ell}_{i}c^{\ell}_{i}\delta_{\gamma^{\ell}_{i}}
with positive weights
(c^{\ell}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq{\ell}}
and mean reversions
(\gamma^{\ell}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq{\ell}}
for
{\ell}\geq 1
. This in turn yields an approximation
of the fractional kernel by a sequence of smoothed kernels
(K^{\ell})_{{\ell}\geq 1}
given by

K^{\ell}(t)=\sum^{\ell}_{i=1}c^{\ell}_{i}e^{-\gamma^{\ell}_{i}t},\quad{\ell}\geq 1.

Therefore, the stock price process can be well approximated by the multi-factor stochastic volatility model (see
Jaber and El Euch 2019
,
Jaber 2019
) as follows

\frac{dS^{\ell}_{t}}{S^{\ell}_{t}}=(r+\lambda\mathcal{V}^{\ell}_{t})dt+\sqrt{\mathcal{V}^{\ell}_{t}}dW_{t}^{1},

and the variance process
\mathcal{V}^{\ell}_{t}
is given by a fractional
square-root process

\mathcal{V}^{\ell}_{t}=\xi(t)+\sum^{{\ell}}_{i=1}c^{\ell}_{i}\mathcal{V}^{{\ell},i}_{t},

and

d\mathcal{V}^{{\ell},i}_{t}=(-\gamma^{\ell}_{i}\mathcal{V}^{{\ell},i}_{t}-\kappa\mathcal{V}^{\ell}_{t})dt+\sigma\sqrt{\mathcal{V}^{\ell}_{t}}dW_{t}^{2},

where
\xi(t)=\mathcal{V}_{0}+\kappa\theta{\displaystyle\int^{t}_{0}K(t-s)ds}
. These inspire us to approximate the transformed wealth process as follows

dX^{\ell}_{t}=X^{\ell}_{t}\Big[(r+\lambda\pi_{t}\mathcal{V}^{\ell}_{t})dt+\pi_{t}\sqrt{\mathcal{V}^{\ell}_{t}}dW_{t}^{1}+c\vartheta{q}_{t}dt+b{q}_{t}dW_{t}\Big].

(3.17)

The utility maximization problem (
3.16
) is approximated by

\sup_{(q,\pi)\in\Pi^{\ell}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[U\big(X^{\ell}_{T}-L\big)\right],

(3.18)

where
X^{\ell}_{t}
is subject to (
3.17
). Therefore, the utility maximization problem (
3.18
) can be solved by principle of dynamic programming.
Define the value function as follows

V(t,x,v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell})=\sup_{(q,\pi)\in\Pi^{\ell}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[U\big(X^{\ell}_{T}-L\big)|X_{t}=x,\mathcal{V}^{\ell,1}_{t}=v_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{V}^{\ell,\ell}_{t}=v_{\ell}\right],

with terminal condition

V(T,x,v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell})=U(x-L).

Then the infinitesimal generator of (
3.17
) is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{q,\pi}f(x,v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\left[r+\lambda\pi\left(\xi(t)+\sum^{\ell}_{l=1}c^{\ell}_{l}v_{l}\right)+c\vartheta q\right]xf_{x}+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\left[\pi^{2}\left(\xi(t)+\sum^{\ell}_{l=1}c^{\ell}_{l}v_{l}\right)+b^{2}q^{2}\right]f_{xx}

\displaystyle-\sum^{\ell}_{i=1}\left(\gamma^{\ell}_{i}v_{i}+\kappa\xi(t)+\kappa\sum^{\ell}_{l=1}c^{\ell}_{l}v_{l}\right)f_{v_{i}}+\,\frac{1}{2}\sum^{\ell}_{i,j=1}\sigma^{2}\left(\xi(t)+\sum^{\ell}_{l=1}c^{n}_{l}v_{l}\right)f_{v_{i}v_{j}}

\displaystyle+\sum^{\ell}_{i=1}\rho\sigma x\pi\left(\xi(t)+\sum^{\ell}_{l=1}c^{\ell}_{l}v_{l}\right)f_{xv_{i}}.

For ease of exposition, we denote
v_{0}:=x
and

{\bf v}:=(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell})
and for the computation we set
v_{l}\in[v_{\min}^{l},v_{\max}^{l}]\,l=0,1,\ldots,\ell
. Using the following transformation

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{v}_{0}(y_{0}),\hat{v}_{1}(y_{1}),\ldots,\hat{v}_{\ell}(y_{\ell})\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\left(\tilde{v}_{0}(v_{0}),\tilde{v}_{1}(v_{1}),\ldots,\tilde{v}_{\ell}(v_{\ell})\right),

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{l}(y_{l})

\displaystyle=(v_{\max}^{l}-v_{\min}^{l})(y_{l}+1)/2+v_{\min}^{l},

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{l}(v_{l})

\displaystyle=\frac{2(v_{l}-v_{\min}^{l})}{v_{\max}^{l}-v_{\min}^{l}}-1,

where
\mathbf{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell})\in\Omega^{\ell+1}:=[-1,1]^{\ell+1}
, similarly to the derivation in Section
2.2
, we obtain that

\displaystyle V(t,{\bf v})\approx\sup\limits_{(q,\pi)\in\Pi}\sum\limits_{k_{0},k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\Omega^{\ell+1}}V(t+h,\hat{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{y}))\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\ell}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(y_{i})d{\bf y}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\ell}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(\tilde{v}_{i}(v_{i}))+h{\mathcal{L}}^{q,\pi}\left(\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\ell}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(\tilde{v}_{i}(v_{i}))\right)\right).

Denote

V_{{\bf k}}(t):=\int_{\Omega^{\ell+1}}V(t,\hat{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{y}))\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\ell}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(y_{i})d{\bf y},\quad k_{j}=0,1,\ldots;\,j=0,1,\ldots,\ell.

The first order condition gives that

\displaystyle\pi^{*}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\sum\limits_{k_{0},k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}=0}^{\infty}V_{{\bf k}}(t)\left(\lambda\left(\frac{dg_{k_{0}}^{(0)}(\tilde{v}_{0}(x))}{dx}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}g_{k_{j}}^{(j)}(\tilde{v}_{j}(v_{j}))\right)\right)}{x\sum\limits_{k_{0},k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}=0}^{\infty}V_{{\bf k}}(t)\frac{d^{2}g_{k_{0}}^{(0)}(\tilde{v}_{0}(x))}{dx^{2}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(\tilde{v}_{i}(v_{i}))}

\displaystyle-\frac{\sum\limits_{k_{0},k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}=0}^{\infty}V_{{\bf k}}(t)\left(\rho\sigma\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(\frac{dg_{k_{0}}^{(0)}(\tilde{v}_{0}(x))}{dx}\frac{dg_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(\tilde{v}_{i}(v_{i}))}{dv_{i}}\prod\limits_{j=1,j\neq i}^{\ell}g_{k_{j}}^{(j)}(\tilde{v}_{j}(v_{j}))\right)\right)}{x\sum\limits_{k_{0},k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}=0}^{\infty}V_{{\bf k}}(t)\frac{d^{2}g_{k_{0}}^{(0)}(\tilde{v}_{0}(x))}{dx^{2}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(\tilde{v}_{i}(v_{i}))},

\displaystyle q^{*}=

\displaystyle-\frac{c\vartheta\sum\limits_{k_{0},k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}=0}^{\infty}V_{{\bf k}}(t)\left(\frac{dg_{k_{0}}^{(0)}(\tilde{v}_{0}(x))}{dx}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}g_{k_{j}}^{(j)}(\tilde{v}_{j}(v_{j}))\right)}{b^{2}x\sum\limits_{k_{0},k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}=0}^{\infty}V_{{\bf k}}(t)\frac{d^{2}g_{k_{0}}^{(0)}(\tilde{v}_{0}(x))}{dx^{2}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(\tilde{v}_{i}(v_{i}))}\vee 0.

Then Algorithm
2
is reduced to the following specialized Algorithm
4
for the optimal reinsurance-investment under the rough Heston model.

3.3
Optimal control stopping problem under SLV models

In this section, we solve the optimal control stopping problems under SLV models using the delta family methods combined with operator splitting technique.
Consider a fixed time horizon
[0,T]
. Let the stochastic processes and random variables be defined on a filtered probability space
\left(\Omega,F,\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{0\leq{t}\leq{T}},P\right)
, where
P
is the probability measure, and
F=\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{0\leq{t}\leq{T}}
is the filtration generated
by two standard Brownian motions
W_{t}^{1}
and
W_{t}^{2}
with
d\langle W_{t}^{1},W_{t}^{2}\rangle=\rho{dt}
and
-1\leq\rho\leq 1
. In addition, we assume that the markets do not have transaction costs or taxes, and the trading happens continuously.

We assume that the financial market consists of one risk-free asset with interest rate zero and one risky asset. The price of the risky asset
S_{t}
is subject to the SLV model as follows

\frac{dS_{t}}{S_{t}}=\omega(S_{t},\mathcal{V}_{t})dt+\eta(\mathcal{V}_{t})\Gamma(S_{t})dW_{t}^{1},

and the variance process
\mathcal{V}_{t}
is given by a fractional
square-root process

d\mathcal{V}_{t}=\beta(\mathcal{V}_{t})dt+\zeta(\mathcal{V}_{t})dW_{s}^{2}.

Remark 3.1
.

Some typical SLV models are listed as follows:

(i)

Heston model

\displaystyle\omega(S_{t},\mathcal{V}_{t})=(r+\lambda\mathcal{V}_{t})S_{t},~~\eta(\mathcal{V}_{t})=\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{t}},~~\Gamma(S_{t})=S_{t},

\displaystyle\beta(\mathcal{V}_{t})=\kappa(\theta-\mathcal{V}_{t}),~~\zeta(\mathcal{V}_{t})=\sigma\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{t}}.

(3.21)

(ii)

4/2 model

\displaystyle\omega(S_{t},\mathcal{V}_{t})=(r+\lambda\mathcal{V}_{t})S_{t},~~\eta(\mathcal{V}_{t})=a\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{t}}+b/\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{t}},~~\Gamma(S_{t})=S_{t},

\displaystyle\beta(\mathcal{V}_{t})=\kappa(\theta-\mathcal{V}_{t}),~~\zeta(\mathcal{V}_{t})=\sigma\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{t}}.

(3.22)

(iii)

\alpha
-Hyper model

\displaystyle\omega(S_{t},\mathcal{V}_{t})=(r+\lambda\mathcal{V}_{t})S_{t},~~\eta(\mathcal{V}_{t})=\exp({\mathcal{V}_{t}}),~~\Gamma(S_{t})=S_{t},

\displaystyle\beta(\mathcal{V}_{t})=\kappa(\theta-\exp(a\mathcal{V}_{t})),~~\zeta(\mathcal{V}_{t})=\sigma\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{t}}.

(3.23)

We study the following problem with its value function characterized by

V(t,x,s,v)=\sup_{\tau\in{[t,T]},\,\pi\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-\gamma(\tau-t)}U(X_{\tau}-L)\mid X_{t}=x,\mathcal{V}_{t}=v,S_{t}=s],

where
L>0
is the minimum wealth threshold value and
\gamma
is utility discount factor.
From
Guan et al. 2017
, the value function
V
satisfies the following HJB variational inequality

\min\Big\{-\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}-\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{L}^{\pi}[V],\,V-G(x)\Big\},

(3.24)

where
G(x)=U(x-L)
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\pi}[f(t,x,s,v)]=

\displaystyle-\gamma f+\Big[r+\Big(\frac{\omega(s,v)}{s}-r\Big)\pi\Big]xf_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\eta^{2}(v)\Gamma^{2}(s)}{s^{2}}\pi^{2}x^{2}f_{xx}

\displaystyle+\omega(s,v)f_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}(v)\Gamma^{2}(s)f_{ss}+\beta(v)f_{v}+\frac{1}{2}\zeta(v)f_{vv}

\displaystyle+\eta^{2}(v)\Gamma^{2}(s)\frac{\pi x}{s}f_{xs}+\zeta(v)\eta(v)\Gamma(s)\rho\Big(\frac{\pi x}{s}f_{xv}+f_{sv}\Big),

with terminal condition

V(T,x,s,v)=G(x),\quad x\in[0,\infty).

The HJB variational inequality (
3.24
) can be transformed as a nonlinear complementarity problem

\displaystyle-V_{t}\geq\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{L}^{\pi}[V],

(3.25)

\displaystyle V\geq G,

(3.26)

\displaystyle(V_{t}-\mathcal{L}V)(V-G)=0.

(3.27)

Adding the slack function
\psi(t,x,s,v)
, we rewrite (
3.25
) - (
3.27
) as

\displaystyle-V_{t}=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{L}^{\pi}[V]+\psi,

(3.28)

\displaystyle V\geq G,

(3.29)

\displaystyle\psi\geq 0,

(3.30)

\displaystyle\psi(V-G)=0.

(3.31)

Let
V^{n}(x,s,v)\approx V(t_{n},x,s,v)
and
\psi^{n}(x,s,v)\approx\psi(t_{n},x,s,v)
. Then
Using the operator splitting technique (see
Ikonen 2004
and
Chen and Shen 2010
), (
3.28
) - (
3.31
) are discretized in time by

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}^{n}=V^{n+1}+\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}}h\Big(\mathcal{L}^{\pi}[V^{n+1}]+\psi^{n+1}\Big),

(3.32)

\displaystyle\frac{V^{n}-\widetilde{V}^{n}}{h}=\psi^{n}-\psi^{n+1},

(3.33)

\displaystyle\psi^{n}\geq 0,\quad V^{n}\geq 0,\quad\psi^{n}(V^{n}-G)=0.

(3.34)

Furthermore (
3.33
) and (
3.34
) give explicitly that

(\psi^{n},V^{n})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Big(0,\widetilde{V}^{n}-h\psi^{n+1}\Big),&\text{if}\quad\widetilde{V}^{n}-h\psi^{n+1}\geq G,\\
\Big(\frac{G-\widetilde{V}^{n}}{h}+\psi^{n+1},G\Big),&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(3.35)

Now we derive the delta family methods to discretize (
3.32
) and (
3.35
) in space. Denote
v_{0}=x
,
v_{1}=s
,
v_{2}=v
and

\displaystyle{\bf v}

\displaystyle:=(v_{0},v_{1},v_{2}),

We set
v_{l}\in[v_{\min}^{l},v_{\max}^{l}]
,
l=0,1,2
and use the following transformation

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{v}_{0}(y_{0}),\hat{v}_{1}(y_{1}),\hat{v}_{2}(y_{2})\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\left(\tilde{v}_{0}(v_{0}),\tilde{v}_{1}(v_{1}),\tilde{v}_{2}(v_{2})\right),

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{l}(y_{l})

\displaystyle=(v_{\max}^{l}-v_{\min}^{l})(y_{l}+1)/2+v_{\min}^{l},

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{l}(v_{l})

\displaystyle=\frac{2(v_{l}-v_{\min}^{l})}{v_{\max}^{l}-v_{\min}^{l}}-1,

where
\mathbf{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},y_{2})\in\Omega^{3}:=[-1,1]^{3}
, to give that

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}^{n}({\bf v})

\displaystyle\approx\sum\limits_{k_{0},k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\Omega^{3}}V^{n+1}(\hat{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{y}))\prod\limits_{i=0}^{2}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(y_{i})d{\bf y}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left((1-\gamma h)\prod\limits_{i=0}^{2}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(\tilde{v}_{i}(v_{i}))+h\cdot{\mathcal{L}}^{\pi^{*}_{n+1}}\left(\prod\limits_{i=0}^{2}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(\tilde{v}_{i}(v_{i}))\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{k_{0},k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\Omega^{3}}\psi^{n+1}(\hat{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{y}))\prod\limits_{i=0}^{2}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(y_{i})d{\bf y}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{2}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(\tilde{v}_{i}(v_{i}))\right).

Denote

\displaystyle V^{n}_{{\bf k}}:=\int_{\Omega^{3}}V^{n}(\hat{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{y}))\prod\limits_{i=0}^{2}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(y_{i})d{\bf y},

\displaystyle\psi^{n}_{\bf k}:=\int_{\Omega^{3}}\psi^{n}(\hat{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{y}))\prod\limits_{i=0}^{2}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(y_{i})d{\bf y},

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}^{n}_{{\bf k}}:=\int_{\Omega^{3}}\widetilde{V}^{n}(\hat{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{y}))\prod\limits_{i=0}^{2}g_{k_{i}}^{(i)}(y_{i})d{\bf y},

for
{\bf k}=(k_{1},\,k_{2},\,k_{3})
;
k_{i}=0,1,\ldots,M_{i}
;
i=1,2,3
;
n=0,1,\ldots,N
.
Then
\pi^{*}_{n}({\bf v})
is given by

\displaystyle\pi^{*}_{n}({\bf v})=

\displaystyle\frac{(\omega(v_{1},v_{2})-rv_{1})v_{1}\sum\limits_{k_{0}=0}^{M_{0}}\sum\limits_{k_{1}=0}^{M_{1}}\sum\limits_{k_{2}=0}^{M_{2}}V^{n}_{{\bf k}}\left(\frac{dg_{k_{0}}^{(0)}(\tilde{v}_{0}(v_{0}))}{dv_{0}}g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{v}_{1}(v_{1}))g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}_{2}(v_{2}))\right)}{\eta^{2}(v_{2})\Gamma^{2}(v_{1})v_{0}\sum\limits_{k_{0}=0}^{M_{0}}\sum\limits_{k_{1}=0}^{M_{1}}\sum\limits_{k_{2}=0}^{M_{2}}V^{n}_{{\bf k}}\left(\frac{d^{2}g_{k_{0}}^{(0)}(\tilde{v}_{0}(v_{0}))}{dv_{0}^{2}}g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{v}_{1}(v_{1}))g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}_{2}(v_{2}))\right)}

\displaystyle+\frac{\zeta(v_{2})\rho v_{1}\sum\limits_{k_{0}=0}^{M_{0}}\sum\limits_{k_{1}=0}^{M_{1}}\sum\limits_{k_{2}=0}^{M_{2}}V^{n}_{{\bf k}}\left(\frac{dg_{k_{0}}^{(0)}(\tilde{v}_{0}(v_{0}))}{dv_{0}}\frac{dg_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{v}_{1}(v_{1}))}{dv_{1}}g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}_{2}(v_{2}))\right)}{\eta(v_{2})\Gamma(v_{1})v_{0}\sum\limits_{k_{0}=0}^{M_{0}}\sum\limits_{k_{1}=0}^{M_{1}}\sum\limits_{k_{2}=0}^{M_{2}}V^{n}_{{\bf k}}\left(\frac{d^{2}g_{k_{0}}^{(0)}(\tilde{v}_{0}(v_{0}))}{dv_{0}^{2}}g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{v}_{1}(v_{1}))g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}_{2}(v_{2}))\right)}

\displaystyle+\frac{v_{1}\sum\limits_{k_{0}=0}^{M_{0}}\sum\limits_{k_{1}=0}^{M_{1}}\sum\limits_{k_{2}=0}^{M_{2}}V^{n}_{{\bf k}}\left(\frac{dg_{k_{0}}^{(0)}(\tilde{v}_{0}(v_{0}))}{dv_{0}}g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{v}_{1}(v_{1}))\frac{dg_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}_{2}(v_{2}))}{dv_{2}}\right)}{v_{0}\sum\limits_{k_{0}=0}^{M_{0}}\sum\limits_{k_{1}=0}^{M_{1}}\sum\limits_{k_{2}=0}^{M_{2}}V^{n}_{{\bf k}}\left(\frac{d^{2}g_{k_{0}}^{(0)}(\tilde{v}_{0}(v_{0}))}{dv_{0}^{2}}g_{k_{1}}^{(1)}(\tilde{v}_{1}(v_{1}))g_{k_{2}}^{(2)}(\tilde{v}_{2}(v_{2}))\right)}.

(3.36)

Therefore Algorithm
2
is reduced to the following specialized Algorithm
5
for the optimal control stopping problem under SLV models.

4
Conclusions

In this paper, we propose a new approach to solve possibly high-dimensional stochastic control (and stopping) problems arising from utility maximization. We provide several algorithms and numerical results to verify the accurateness and efficiency of the proposed approach.
For future research, we shall consider more complex stochastic control problems arising in optimal investment with transaction cost, continuous-time principal-agent problems and mean-field games etc.
