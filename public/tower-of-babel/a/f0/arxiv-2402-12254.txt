Stability of the coronal magnetic field around large confined and eruptive solar flares
Manu Gupta, J. K. Thalmann, A. M. Veronig

Abstract
Context. The coronal magnetic field, which overlies the current-carrying field of solar active regions, straps the magnetic configuration below. The characteristics of this overlying field are crucial in determining if a flare will be eruptive and accompanied by a coronal mass ejection (CME), or if it will remain confined without a CME. Aims. In order to improve our understanding on the pre-requisites of eruptive solar flares, we study and compare different measures that characterize the eruptive potential of solar active regions — the critical height for torus instability as a local measure and the helicity ratio as a global measure — with the structural properties of the underlying magnetic field, namely the altitude of the center of the current-carrying magnetic structure. Methods. Using time series of 3D optimization-based nonlinear force-free magnetic field models for 10 different active regions (ARs) around the time of large solar flares, we determine the altitudes of the current-weighted centers of the non-potential model structures. Based on the potential magnetic field, we inspect the decay index, n n , in multiple vertical planes oriented along of or perpendicular to the flare-relevant polarity inversion line, and estimate the critical height ( h crit h_{\mathrm{crit}} ) for torus instability (TI) using different thresholds of n n . The critical heights are interpreted with respect to the altitudes of the current-weighted centers of the associated non-potential structures, as well as the eruptive character of the associated flares, and the eruptive potential of the host AR, as characterized by the helicity ratio. Results. Our most important findings are that (i) h crit h_{\mathrm{crit}} is more segregated in terms of flare type than the helicity ratio, and that (ii) coronal field configurations with a higher eruptive potential (in terms of the helicity ratio) also appear to be more prone to TI. Furthermore, we find no pronounced differences in the altitudes of the non-potential structures prior to confined and eruptive flares. An aspect which requires further investigation is that, quite generally, the modeled non-potential structures hardly reside in a torus-instable regime, requiring further assessment regarding the applicability of the chosen NLFF modeling approach when targeted at the structural properties of the coronal magnetic field. Conclusions.



1
Introduction

Solar flares are a sudden release of a vast amount of energy stored in the coronal magnetic fields by the process of magnetic reconnection
(
Priest & Forbes 2002
;
Fletcher et al. 2011
;
Shibata & Magara 2011
;
Benz 2017
, see reviews by)
. A coronal mass ejection (CME) is also observed with a flare when plasma and embedded magnetic field are able to successfully escape into the heliosphere
(
Forbes 2000
;
Chen 2011
, for a review see, e.g.,)
. Then the flare is called an eruptive flare. Conversely, when a flare is detected without a CME, it is referred to as a confined flare. It is a common understanding that solar eruptions such as flares and coronal mass ejections (CMEs) are different manifestations of the same physical processes causing a reconfiguration of coronal fields.

A number of possible mechanisms of storage and release type that can trigger and drive solar eruptions are known
(
Green et al. 2018
, see, e.g., Table 1 in)
. While driving mechanisms are capable of producing an eruption, trigger mechanisms can only act as an ignitor. For the latter, a driving mechanism has to take over in order to complete the eruption. Based on these physical mechanisms, the pre-eruption magnetic configuration exist primarily in two class of models: sheared arcade or twisted flux rope
(
Mackay et al. 2010
;
Gibson 2018
, for a review see, e.g.,)
. A sheared arcade (SA) is formed by the field lines extending along the polarity inversion line (PIL) and winding less than a full turn around the central axis. A flux rope is formed by magnetic fluxes that wrap around a central axis above the PIL and wind with at least one turn
(
Gibson & Fan 2006
, see, e.g.,)
.

For a curved current channel such as magnetic flux rope (MFR), an extra force–also known as “hoop” force–arises as a consequence of the interaction between the toroidal current and the self-generated internal poloidal field
(
Bateman 1978
;
Myers et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. The hoop force acts radially outwards and can be balanced by the strapping force of an external magnetic field. However, the MFR rope may expand and eventually become unstable if the strapping force decreases sufficiently faster than the hoop force in the direction of major radius of the curved channel.
Kliem & Török 2006
referred to this ideal MHD instability as torus instability (TI).

Green et al. 2018
considered TI as one of the only two driving mechanisms, the other being the flare reconnection.
Aulanier 2013
suggested breakout model
(
Antiochos et al. 1999
, see, e.g.,)
and TI as the only two possible mechanisms to initiate and drive solar eruptions, and concluded all other mechanisms to be leading to, or strengthening these two mechanisms.

Kliem & Török 2006
investigated the stability of a partial or complete toroidal current ring by including only the hoop force and the strapping force. Using the variation of the external field perpendicular to the direction of concentrated electric current as a measure, the decay index can be quantified as a function of height. At heights where a ”critical” value is exceeded, the TI instability may in principle act as a driver for an eruption.
Recently it was suggested that an additional tension force which was neglected by
Kliem & Török 2006
may play an important role in constraining the MFR eruption, thereby resulting in a failed torus instability
(
Myers et al. 2015
;
Zhong et al. 2021
, see, e.g.,)
. Both the hoop and tension forces in an MFR are dependent on its geometry, and therefore a range rather than a single value may be deduced for the critical decay index (0.8 – 1.6) based on different theoretical
(
Kliem & Török 2006
;
Olmedo & Zhang 2010
;
Démoulin & Aulanier 2010
;
Zuccarello et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
and observation-based works
(
Wang et al. 2017
;
Baumgartner et al. 2018
;
Jing et al. 2018
;
Duan et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
, as well as an experimental setup in a laboratory
(
Myers et al. 2015
;
Myers et al. 2017
;
Alt et al. 2021
)
.
Analyzing all major flares (GOES class M5 and larger) of solar cycle 24,
Duan et al. 2019

found 90% of the events to possess pre-flare MFRs much more complex than theoretical models on which TI-related thresholds are traditionally based. They suggested a lower limit of 1.3 for the critical decay index, based on the finding that all the events above this critical value exhibited an eruption. In a subsequent work,
Duan et al. 2021
showed that the average values of decay index decrease from before to after flares, with the value averaged over all considered flares decreasing from 0.88 to 0.63, indicative of MFRs to decrease in height through flares.

In recent years, TI was analyzed in a number of statistical studies of observed flares.

Wang et al. 2017
estimated the decay index for 60 two-ribbon flares (larger than or equal to GOES class M1.0), above a segment of the PIL located in between the two ribbons. They found a bimodal distribution of critical height (
h_{\mathrm{crit}}
) with many of the confined flares exhibiting significantly higher critical heights than the eruptive ones. Also, the decay index was found to be monotonously increasing with height for
84-86\%
of the flares and exhibits a saddle-like profile for about
14-16\%
of the flares. Saddle-like profiles may facilitate an eruption, nevertheless preventing the escape at upper heights, resulting in a failed eruption
(
Luo & Liu 2022
)
.
Baumgartner et al. 2018
calculated the decay index for 44 flares of GOES class M5.0 or larger within vertical planes based on a linear fit to the flaring PIL. For 42 out of 44 events, they found
h_{\mathrm{crit}}
larger (smaller) than 40 Mm for confined (eruptive) flares. The reason for a clear distinction among the flares in comparison to
Wang et al. 2017
was suspected to be different flares sizes in the two samples, with approximately
75\%
of the events below GOES class M5.0 in
Wang et al. 2017
whereas all of the flares are ¿ M5.0 in
Baumgartner et al. 2018
. Recently,
James et al. 2022
analyzed critical heights of 42 ARs at their central PIL. It was observed that CME rates were
63\%
higher during phases when critical height is increasing than when it is decreasing.

These stability analyses suggest a relationship between the critical height for TI and whether a large flare is associated with a CME or not. To get a full picture, however, one needs to inspect whether or not the coronal magnetic structure actually extends into the regime where TI might facilitate an eruption. Therefore it is necessary to locate the coronal altitude of the associated non-potential magnetic system (a flux rope or sheared arcade).
Zhou et al. 2019
studied the 3D magnetic field configuration of 16 filament eruptions that failed to be ejected and become coronal mass ejections. They found that in seven cases, the decay index at the filament apex exceed the theoretical threshold (
n_{\mathrm{crit}}
= 1.5) for TI and that in all those cases a strong rotation of the filament apex was observed during the eruptions.

In contrast to the critical height for TI —a local measure deduced from the coronal magnetic field structure— also more global measures exist which have been found tightly related to the eruptive potential of ARs. On scales characteristic for solar ARs, one may use the helicity ratio
|H_{\mathrm{J}}|/|H_{V}|
—the ratio of the helicity of the predominantly concentrated in strong electric current channels like a magnetic flux rope (MFR) or sheared arcade (SA), to the total one in the coronal volume—for details see
Pariat et al. 2017
. The helicity ratio stems from the known form of the relative magnetic helicity,
H_{V}

(
Berger 1984
)
, which can be decomposed into two components,
H_{\mathrm{J}}
and
H_{\mathrm{PJ}}
, the latter representing the helicity associated with the volume-threading field
(
Berger 2003
)
.

The information provided by the helicity ratio has shown promise in determining the likelihood of the CME productivity of an AR in a more general way (since computed from volume-integrated quantities), that is, without making assumptions about the specific underlying coronal magnetic field configuration (e.g. a MFR vs a SA). From simulation-based
(
Pariat et al. 2017
;
Zuccarello et al. 2018
;
Linan et al. 2018
;
Price et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
and observation-based
(
James et al. 2018
;
Moraitis et al. 2019
;
Thalmann et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
applications it became clear that the helicity ratio exhibits typically larger values for magnetic configurations/ARs that produce CMEs (compared to configurations showing none or confined activity).

In a first multi-event analysis,
Gupta et al. 2021
demonstrated that not only the helicity ratio, but also other AR-integrated quantities (the ratio of free to total magnetic energy and the ratio of
H_{\mathrm{J}}
to the unsigned AR flux) simultaneously exceed certain thresholds in ARs that produce eruptive flares. This finding is partially supported by the recent study of
Duan et al. 2023
, based on a much more extended sample of flares of GOES class
\geq
M3.9 that occurred from 2011 January to 2017 December within
45^{\circ}
in longitude of the disk center, only disagreeing in the potential distinctive power of the helicity ratio, which they did not find distinctly different for ARs producing different types of flares.

The time variation of the helicity ratio has been studied in detail by
Green et al. 2022
regarding its potential to indicate when key transitions occur in the coronal field before an eruption. They concluded, however, that further dedicated studies are required to enable a more comprehensive understanding of the (drivers of the) evolution of the coronal magnetic field and its impact on the helicity ratio.

In this paper, we aim to provide such a combined study of a local measure for the stability of the coronal magnetic field (critical height for TI) and a global measure for the eruptive potential of an AR (relative magnetic helicity ratio) and to inspect their relationships to the flare type (confined or eruptive). To do so, we combine the coronal magnetic field modeling and estimates of the helicity ratios for ten solar flares by
Gupta et al. 2021
(see
Sect.
2
paragraph 1 for a short review) with the results from a newly developed (refined) approach to compute the critical height for TI (see
Sect.
2.3
for details).

2
Data and methods

As a
global
(in the sense of a characteristic active-region scale) measure for the eruptive potential of an AR, we use the helicity ratios estimated for the pre-flare corona of ten flares of GOES class M1.0 or larger that occurred within 45° of the central meridian
(
Gupta et al. 2021
, see Table 1 in)
. These have been based on nonlinear force-free (NLFF) coronal magnetic field time-series modeling (see Sect. 2.2 in their work for details) of selected ARs (NOAAs 12673, 11158, 11429, 12297, 11890, 12192, 12268, 11302, 11339, 11166). The time-series NLFF modeling was employed in the form of two time series for each AR, one time series based on standard model parameter settings and one time series based on a setting which results in model magnetic fields with a lower residual solenoidality
(i.e., of enhanced reliability regarding the helicity computations; see
Thalmann et al. 2019
, for a dedicated study)
. From the subsequent computation of magnetic helicities (the total helicity,
H_{V}
, as well as the helicity of the current-carrying part of the field,
H_{\mathrm{J}}
) for each of the two NLFF models at each time instant, mean values were deduced and used for further analysis. The final values for the helicity ratios as listed in Table 3 of
Gupta et al. 2021
then resulted from another time-averaging of pre-flare mean values.

In order to derive a
local
measure for the stability of the coronal magnetic field, we apply a refined approach to compute the critical height for TI.

We note here that the TI as a trigger may only be relevant for a subset of our analyzed confined events, namely those that involve a failed eruption that becomes confined at larger coronal heights by a strong strapping field
(
Li et al. 2019
, “type II”, following the classification of)
. In our sample, type II confined events are the M2.0 flare in AR 12268, the M4.0 flare in AR 11302, and the M1.8 flare in AR 11339 (see Table 1). The X-class flares in our sample (hosted by AR 12192 or 11166) are type I events, that is, do not involve a significant eruption, and may serve as true negatives when probing their coronal configuration regarding proneness to TI.

This approach involves several steps, including the identification of the flare-relevant PIL and relevant information in the vertical direction in its surrounding (see
Sect.
2.1
), as well as the subsequent extraction of geometry-related (
Sect.
2.2
) and TI-related (
Sect.
2.3
) measures.

2.1
Flaring polarity inversion line and the corresponding vertical planes

In order to detect the major polarity inversion line (PIL) of an AR we use the vertical magnetic field component of the hmi.sharp_cea_720s data, constructed from polarization measurements of the Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager
(
Scherrer et al. 2012
, HMI;)
on board the Solar Dynamics Observatory
(
Pesnell et al. 2012
, SDO;)
, and used as an input to NLFF modeling in
Gupta et al. 2021
. To identify locations of flare (ribbon) pixels, we use the SDO Atmospheric Imaging Assembly
(
Lemen et al. 2012
, AIA;)
CEA-remapped 1600 Å images and binned to the same plate scale as the vector magnetic field data (720 km).

We apply the method of
Cai et al. 2020
to detect PILs on a gaussian smoothed vertical magnetic field (
\mathit{B_{z}}
) map. Secondly, to identify ribbon pixels (displayed in pink in
Fig.
1
(a)
), we apply the method of
Thalmann et al. 2015
, using sequences of 24-second time cadence 1600 Å images covering the flare impulsive phase. We generate a binary map using the 99th percentile of the intensity of the entire series of images as a threshold. Blooming effects are taken care of by requiring that a pixel is detected as a flaring pixel in at least five consecutive images. In a third step, the overlapping area of all detected PILs and the flare-pixel mask is determined, and the flare-relevant PIL (FPIL) is identified as that one with the largest overlap (indicated by yellow color in
Fig.
1
(a)
for AR 12673 and in Appendix
Fig.
4
for all of the ARs under study).

We proceed by defining different spatial regimes in order to perform the envisaged stability analysis. In particular, we define a single vertical plane along of the FPIL as one regime (”FPIL-aligned”, hereafter). Traditionally, a similar approach was used in a number of studies to locate the heights of critical decay index
(
Wang et al. 2017
;
Baumgartner et al. 2018
;
Jing et al. 2018
, see, e.g.,)
. The only difference to previous studies using a similar approach is that we restrict our analysis (extent of the vertical plane) to the central part (red curve in
Fig.
1
(a)
) of the full detected FPIL (yellow curve), assuming to spot the relevant central body of the possibly unstable coronal MFR (SA) configuration. As a second spatial regime to consider, we use vertical planes, distributed equidistantly along of the FPIL, each of being oriented along the local FPIL-normal direction (”FPIL-normal” hereafter; their footprint being displayed by cyan color lines in
Fig.
1
(a)
). This is motivated by the fact that the geometry of MFRs and SAs, though often approximated in such an idealized manner, is not symmetric about the FPIL. In other words, the central axis (cross symbol in
Fig
1
(c)
) of the involved twisted or sheared field is not necessarily located vertically above of the FPIL (see vertical red dashed line in
Fig.
1
(c)
and left column of
Fig.
7
and

8
). Thus, considerations of geometrical and magnetical properties in a FPIL-aligned plane may not reflect the actual conditions at those characteristic locations within MFRs and SAs.
These FPIL-normal planes enable us to capture the projections of an MFR. In the next
Sect.
2.2
we describe the method used to locate the current-weighted center of the MFR (SA).

For completeness we note that in cases where the eruption propagates in an oblique direction, the analysis of the decay index in a direction oblique to both, the FPIL and vertical direction, might be of advantage
(
Duan et al. 2019
, see, e.g.,)
. Alternatively, some authors used all pixels located at coordinates of an extended photospheric FPIL mask to study the decay index as a function of height
(
James et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. Exploring the differences arising from such different treatments is beyond the scope of this study, however.

2.2
Geometry-related measures

The definition of multiple FPIL-normal planes along of the FPIL (see
Fig.
1
(a)
for an example of such planes distributed along of the FPIL in AR 12673) represent a fast and easy way to investigate the distribution of magnetic field and electric currents as a function of height (see
Fig.
1
(c)
for a selected plane). To do so, we compute the components of the magnetic field and electric current normal and tangential to the FPIL-normal planes and interpolate them onto a grid with a resolution of 72 km.

In order to identify the center of the MFR (SA) projected into each of the FPIL-normal planes, we assume the central region to be represented in the form of a channel of strongest concentrated electric current. We use the 95th percentile of the unsigned electric current density in each of the FPIL-normal planes as selection criterion. Hereafter, we use a map of the Gaussian-smoothed total current density to find the corresponding current-weighted center. We show an exemplary result for the NLFF model of AR 12673 at 2017-Sep-06 08:59 UT in
Fig.
1
(c)
where the detected center of the strong current concentration present is marked by the black cross.
The corresponding height (altitude in the model corona) is referred to as
h_{\mathrm{center}}
hereafter. For further analysis, we compute the mean value
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}
and standard deviation from the range of values deduced from all of the FPIL-normal planes for each time step. We remark here that the applied averaging lowers the final measure for the altitude of the non-potential structure (
h_{\mathrm{center}}
), in comparison to the altitude of the apex of the non-potential structure determined from a single plane which is used in many studies in the literature (see the dedicated discussion in
Sect.
4
). We note here that in contrast to
Gupta et al. 2021
, we employ only one NLFF model with the standard model parameter settings, as both NLFF models from
Gupta et al. 2021
generate similar estimates of
h_{\mathrm{center}}
of the current channel.

2.3
Stability analysis

Following
Kliem & Török 2006
, the decay index is defined as,

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{d(ln(B_{\mathrm{hor}}))}{d(ln(h))},

(1)

where
B_{\mathrm{{hor}}}
represents the strapping field approximated by the horizontal component of the potential magnetic field and
h
is the height in the corona. A larger value of

indicates a potential field to more rapidly decay with height. The TI may occur in regions where
B_{\mathrm{{hor}}}
declines with height at a sufficiently steep rate.

The potential field is computed by applying a Green’s function method to the map of

used for PIL detection. We compute the decay index from the potential field projected into each of the FPIL-normal planes (see

1
(d)
for a selected plane).

We restrict the computation of the decay index to the portion of the FPIL-normal planes that cover the relevant non-potential core field, as indicated exemplary in

(c)
by the orange vertical lines.
This specific section is determined by the width between the center and the edge of the current density contour (e.g., black contour in
Fig.
1
(c)
), which is smaller on one side, and then reciprocated on the opposite side.
From

1

NOAA AR 12673

AR 12673 hosted two X-class flares on 2017 September 6. The first X2.2 flare was observed as a confined flare and 2.6 hours later X9.3 flare was observed along with a fast CME.
Zou et al. 2020
analyzed the evolution of the MFR around the time of these events by combining EUV observations with the magnetic field extrapolation. 4
Discussion

In this study, we calculated the critical height for torus instability and estimated the approximate coronal altitude of the current-weighted center of the MFR (SA).
To do so, we identified the flare-relevant PIL (FPIL) constraint by the location of flare pixels. Thereafter, planes at approximately equidistant locations along of the FPIL and oriented normally with respect to the local direction of the FPIL were defined,
in order to derive the altitude of the current-weighted center (
h_{\mathrm{center}}
) of the MFR/SA. Based on FPIL-normal planes and in addition —as traditionally done— based on an FPIL-aligned plane we estimated the critical height for torus instability (
h_{\mathrm{crit}}
) using the method described in
Sect.
2.3
.

From the time series (
Fig.
2
), we computed time-averaged pre-flare values of
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{crit}}}^{(n=1.5)}
over a 5-hour interval prior to the start of flares (last column in
Table
1
).
In addition, we estimated time-averaged pre-flare values of
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}
(fourth column in
Table
1
).
From
Fig.
2
(and the pre-flare time-averaged values in
Table
1
) it is evident that the structural characteristics of MFRs (SAs), as probed by our
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}
estimates, are not systematically different prior to confined versus eruptive flaring. From
Fig.
2
, however, it seems that
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}
is decreasing during the eruptive flares. This finding supports that the optimization-based modeling used here grasps the flare-related transformation of MFRs (SAs) to some extent.

Noteworthy, our estimates of
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}
are found restricted to low altitudes in the model volumes (
\approx
5 Mm), far below the critical height for TI (
\gtrsim
13 Mm for
n_{\mathrm{crit}}
= 1.5, the critical value most often used in literature). This would imply that none of the analyzed flares, including the obviously eruptive ones, were prone to TI. This being rather unlikely (and contrasting the conclusions drawn in a number of individual studies on the target ARs in our sample), hints at a different possibility. Using
n_{\mathrm{crit}}
= 1.5 as a threshold as commonly done must be regarded as very restrictive, however, especially given the underlying assumption of a toroidally symmetric, large-aspect-ratio flux rope in
Kliem & Török 2006
, and given that any departure from such an idealized structure significantly effects the forces acting on a MFR
(
Myers et al. 2016
)
. Correspondingly, from the observation-based modeling survey of
Duan et al. 2019
, the authors suggested
n_{\mathrm{crit}}
= 1.3 as a more realistic threshold (with the possible impact of particular methods used to model the coronal magnetic field unknown to date). Ultimately, there might be a range of values at which coronal structures become unstable, down to
n_{\mathrm{crit}}
= 0.4 and up to
n_{\mathrm{crit}}

\sim
2.5
(
Zou et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. In order to address this aspect, we inspect
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}
with respect to
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{crit}}}^{(n=0.8)}

(
Myers et al. 2016
;
Jing et al. 2018
, based on)
assuming to be representative for a lower corresponding limit. From
Table
1
, it appears that using this lower threshold value for
\mathbf{n_{\mathrm{crit}}}
yields a distribution of
{\langle\overline{h}_{\mathrm{crit}}\rangle}^{(n=0.8)}
that also to some extent segregates confined from eruptive flares (mean of
\approx
32 Mm and
\approx
8 Mm, respectively, see third column in the Table) and that
\langle{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}\rangle
reaches on average to that height regime prior to eruptive flares (
\Delta_{h}

\lesssim
4 Mm, on average, fifth column of the table). This gap (
\Delta_{h}
) between
\langle{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}\rangle
and
{\langle\overline{h}_{\mathrm{crit}}\rangle}^{(n=0.8)}
is exacerbated by the averaging done over the FPIL-normal planes which results in slightly lower values of the current-weighted center and higher
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{crit}}}^{(n=0.8)}
than the
h_{\mathrm{crit}}^{(n=0.8)}
values at the position of the current-weighted center of the non-potential structure (black cross in
Figs.
1
,

7
and

8
).

Following, for example,
Myers et al. 2015
and
Zhong et al. 2019
, there might be even cases where torus-unstable MFRs just fail to erupt fully. In order to possibly spot such cases, we interpret our findings also in context with the type of the confined flares — involving a reconfiguration without significant eruption or a failed eruption confined at larger heights
(
Li et al. 2019
, type I and II, respectively, see)
). For confined events of type I (the X3.1 flare in AR 12192 and the X1.5 flare in AR 11166) for which TI can be excluded as a flare trigger, we indeed find
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{crit}}}>>{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}
, irrespective of what threshold is used (
n_{\mathrm{crit}}=0.8
or
n_{\mathrm{crit}}
= 1.5; see
Figs.
2
(b) and
2
(j), respectively), serving as examples of “true negatives”. For the confined events of type II (the analyzed M-class flares hosted by AR 12268, 11302, and 11339) also,
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{crit}}}>>{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}
, as expected (see
Fig.
2
(d),
2
(f), and
2
(h)
, respectively, and
Table
1
).

Yet another possible implication of our finding of
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}
being considerably lower than
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{crit}}}^{(n=1.5)}
is a rather discouraging one, namely that the optimization-based NLFF modeling is not suited for TI-related analysis. In other words, the non-potential structures obtained based on optimization-based NLFF modeling succeed to picture the geometrical properties of coronal MFR (SA) structures only partially. Indeed, the
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}
values presented here are similar to the central axis height of the MFR recovered by optimization-based NLFF modeling of AR 12891 as examined in
Thalmann et al. 2023
. In that study the coronal altitudes of the center/arcade height of the model MFR were compared to the stereoscopically reconstructed height of the simultaneously observed active-region filament/prominence. Noteworthy, the NLFF-based MFR center height was found to be located several Mm lower than the stereoscopic estimate of the filament/prominence height, while the estimated (apex) height of the confining magnetic arcade was found at a height similar to the stereoscopic one. Thus, the vertical extent of magnetic structures in optimization-based NLFF models should possibly be regarded as representing an under-estimation (lower limit) of the true one.

This is supported also by the comparison to the results based on alternative reconstruction techniques which may yield different model MFR configurations, including a different estimate of
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}
. For instance, the data-driven approach applied in
Zou et al. 2020
to model AR 12673, suggests a pre-X9-flare value of the model MFR center of
\approx
19 Mm, with the authors taking the apex height of the magnetic field line with the strongest twist as a reference. From their Fig. 3(i) one might get the impression that this represents an extreme value (an upper limit), with the respective field line being located on the outskirts/envelope of the MFR body. Based on that figure, we hypothesize here that a corresponding twist-weighted estimate of the MFR center would possibly be found a few Mm lower (above
\approx
10 Mm), yet still at a distinctly larger altitude than our
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{center}}}\approx 5
Mm estimate based on our optimization-based NLFF model. Additional comparison to the set of events analyzed in
Duan et al. 2019
, who used a data-driven scheme to model the coronal magnetic field above all major-flare productive ARs of solar cycle 24, we may safely assume that our optimization-based MFR structures are residing at model heights lower by a factor of roughly two
(
Duan et al. 2019
, see right column of Figs. 3 – 9 in)
.

Based on the above considerations, we are now able to inspect the interplay of local and global stability measures. The helicity ratio, being based on the volume-integrated magnetic helicity, represents a global measure for the eruptive potential of a solar AR (global meaning length scales typical for solar ARs). As discussed in
Gupta et al. 2021
, a helicity ratio exceeding a certain value (0.1) does not unambiguously hint at an eruptive flare to happen, but merely expresses that the considered AR posses the potential to produce one
(
Duan et al. 2023
, see also the recent work by)
. To better understand this, also in relation to the more local impact of the strapping field, we interpret in the following the pre-flare time-averaged values of the helicity ratio and the critical height for TI.

In contrast to their study, however, we do not find any characteristic trends in time profiles of the critical height prior to eruptive or confined flares, likely because of the short duration of the time series analyzed here.

As discussed in
Baumgartner et al. 2018

Four of the five eruptive events studied in our restricted sample fall into the latter category. Correspondingly,

5
Summary and conclusions

We aimed to better understand the role of the strapping field in the occurrence of confined versus eruptive large flares by the combined analysis of the geometry of the underlying magnetic structure (a magnetic flux rope or sheared arcade, depending on the AR considered) and the critical height for TI. To do so, we analyzed the model magnetic field and electric current density projected into several vertical planes, either aligned with or normal to the flare-relevant PIL, based on NLFF magnetic field modeling of ten active regions. An important new aspect in our analysis is that we address the interplay of local (PIL-associated as sensed by
h_{\mathrm{crit}}
) and global (active-region) eruptive potential of the ARs, the latter characterized by the helicity ratio.

Similar to earlier dedicated works, we find a clear segregation between confined and eruptive flares in terms of
h_{\mathrm{crit}}
for TI, rather than regarding a characteristically different pre-flare magnetic field (in terms of the altitude of the current-weighted center of the pre-flare core field). In particular, we find the torus-unstable regime to reside lower down in the model atmosphere, closer to the altitudes occupied by the non-potential core field prior to eruptive flares. Importantly, in general it appears that magnetic field configurations which adhere to a generally larger eruptive potential (as sensed by the helicity ratio) are more prone to TI.

This most important finding awaits confirmation from the examination of a larger sample of events, and also in respect to the investigation of effects complicating the analysis. This includes the impact of, for instance, the selected approach to approximate the flare-relevant PIL and corona above of it, the method used to detect geometry-related measures (e.g., altitude of central magnetic axis or strongest electric currents, etc.) of the non-potential core field (a flux rope or sheared arcade), and also clarification of whether the coronal magnetic field modeling used (optimization-based NLFF modeling in this study) qualifies for such an analysis. The latter is possibly to be accomplished only by case-to-case verification of the model-deduced core magnetic field geometry by, for example, stereoscopic means.
