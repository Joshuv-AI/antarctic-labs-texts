New approximate stochastic dominance approaches for Enhanced Indexation models ⋆
Francesco Cesarone, Justo Puerto

Abstract
In this paper, we discuss portfolio selection strategies for Enhanced Indexation (EI), which are based on stochastic dominance relations. The goal is to select portfolios that stochastically dominate a given benchmark but that, at the same time, must generate some excess return with respect to a benchmark index. To achieve this goal, we propose a new methodology that selects portfolios using the ordered weighted average (OWA) operator, which generalizes previous approaches based on minimax selection rules and still leads to solving linear programming models. We also introduce a new type of approximate stochastic dominance rule and show that it implies the almost Second-order Stochastic Dominance (SSD) criterion proposed by Lizyayev and Ruszczyński 2012 . We prove that our EI model based on OWA selects portfolios that dominate a given benchmark through this new form of stochastic dominance criterion. We test the performance of the obtained portfolios in an extensive empirical analysis based on real-world datasets. The computational results show that our proposed approach outperforms several SSD-based strategies widely used in the literature, as well as the global minimum variance portfolio. Keywords : Portfolio selection; Stochastic dominance; Portfolio optimization; Expected shortfall; Ordered weighted average operator.

Introduction

It is well-known among academics and practitioners that active portfolio management is unlikely to generate extra returns over their benchmarks
(
Bufalo et al. 2022
)
.
For this reason, passive asset management has attracted great interest in recent decades, offering returns close to benchmarks at a lower cost.
Index tracking (IT) is a popular problem for dealing with the construction of passive management portfolios and has received large attention in the literature.
IT basically consists of a constrained optimization problem, in which one minimizes the distance between a benchmark and the tracking portfolio by typically using a fixed number of assets less than those available in the investment universe.
Comprehensive surveys on this problem can be found in

Beasley et al. 2003
,
Canakgoz and Beasley 2009
,
Guastaroba and Speranza 2012
,
Bruni et al. 2012
;
Bruni et al. 2013
;
Bruni et al. 2015
;
Bruni et al. 2017
,

Scozzari et al. 2013
,

Cesarone et al. 2019
,
and, more recently, in
Shu et al. 2020
,
Sant’Anna et al. 2022
.

A step towards a more sophisticated strategy, which has attracted increasing attention over the past decade, is the Enhanced Indexation (EI) problem, where the aim is not only to track a market index but simultaneously to generate an excess return over the benchmark.
An extensive review of the literature on EI is provided in
Canakgoz and Beasley 2009
,
Guastaroba et al. 2016
, and more recently in
Guastaroba et al. 2020
,
Beraldi and Bruni 2022
.

This paper focuses on portfolio selection strategies for EI based on Stochastic Dominance (SD) relations. Indeed, one relatively recent and promising approach for Enhanced Indexation consists of selecting portfolios that stochastically dominate a given benchmark.
SD provides a (partial) order in the space of random returns, has connections with Expected Utility Theory, and takes into account the full information regarding the distribution of the portfolio random return
(
Whitmore and Findlay 1978
;
Levy 1992
;
Shaked and Shanthikumar 1994
;
Müller and Stoyan 2002
;
Levy 2015
, see)
.

Roman et al. 2013
develop a Second-order Stochastic Dominance (SSD) approach, which consists of a multi-objective optimization problem to construct portfolios whose returns dominate those of a benchmark
(
Roman et al. 2006
;
Fábián et al. 2011a
, see also)
.
Among the infinite Pareto-optimal solutions,
the authors scalarize their multi-objective model as a Minimax problem, which is linear and is solved efficiently by cutting plane techniques.
Several SSD-based approaches are tested in
Hodder et al. 2015
, where the authors implement the exact SSD methods developed by
Kuosmanen 2004
and
Kopa and Post 2015
.

Alternative portfolio selection approaches for EI, based on relaxing SD rules, i.e., using some forms of approximate SD, can be found in
Bruni et al. 2012
,

Bruni et al. 2015
,

Bruni et al. 2017
,
and
Sharma et al. 2017
.
In fact, as discussed by
Leshno and Levy 2002
,
in some economic situations, the relaxation of SD conditions could provide advantages w.r.t. the exact ones.

Lizyayev and Ruszczyński 2012
propose different relaxations of SD for both First and Second-order rules, named
\varepsilon
-Almost-FSD (
\varepsilon
-AFSD) and
\varepsilon
-Almost-SSD (
\varepsilon
-ASSD), respectively.
They also describe the optimization models for
potential applications of
\varepsilon
-AFSD and
\varepsilon
-ASSD to portfolio selection, but without providing empirical tests on real datasets.
Furthermore, as discussed in the conclusions of
Post and Kopa 2017
, “the current literature offers little guidance for the specification of “epsilon”, or the admissible violation area.”
We point out that our approach does not require to explicitly specify “epsilon” since it aims to obtain solutions that satisfy the SD conditions with minimum violations.

Further developments and generalizations on Almost Stochastic Dominance (ASD) conditions can be found in
Levy et al. 2010
,
Tzeng et al. 2013
,
Post and Kopa 2013
,
Guo et al. 2014
,
Denuit et al. 2014
, and
Tsetlin et al. 2015
.
However, there appear to be no available applications of ASD to portfolio selection.

According to our literature review, the reader may realize that there is room for improvement in the portfolio selection methods based on SD rules.
Our goal is to elaborate in this direction by proposing a new methodology and to shed some light on this field, showing some of their connections.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows.
First, we address the multi-objective representation of the EI problem proposed by
Roman et al. 2013
by an alternative approach based on the
Ordered Weighted Average (OWA) operator

(
Yager 1988
)
.
Its advantage is that it allows us to consider a set of the worst realized objectives in the problem, whereas the Minimax operator, used in
Roman et al. 2013
, pays attention only to the maximum of them.
Furthermore, the convenience of this type of scalarization is that it allows highlighting a broader family of Pareto-optimal portfolios, improving the properties of the Minimax ones.
The application of the OWA operator for scalarizing multi-objective optimization problems has been widely studied in the literature
(
Fernández et al. 2014
;
Fernández et al. 2017
, see, e.g.,)
.
Indeed, OWA is a flexible operator that generalizes several criteria, including the Minimax one
(
Ogryczak 2000
;
Nickel and Puerto 2006
, see, e.g.,)
.
As a consequence, we can provide a more general method for highlighting a novel subset of Pareto-optimal solutions of the multi-objective problem in
Roman et al. 2013
.
Second, we propose a new methodology to select a portfolio that dominates a given benchmark index in terms of SD.
This methodology uses the OWA representation developed in
Marín et al. 2020
and leads to solving linear programming problems.
Furthermore, it is more robust and contains as special cases some known SD-based approaches to asset allocation
(
Lizyayev and Ruszczyński 2012
;
Roman et al. 2013
, see)
.
Third, with the purpose of providing a common framework to understand and explain approximate dominance rules,
we introduce a new rule that we call Cumulative Second-order
\mathcal{E}
-Stochastic Dominance (CS
\mathcal{E}
SD), and
we show that it implies the family of
\varepsilon
-ASSD rules proposed by
Lizyayev and Ruszczyński 2012
.
Exploiting some results in multi-criteria optimization
(
Ehrgott 2005
)
,
we prove that the optimal solution of our EI model based on OWA selects portfolios that dominate the benchmark index in terms of CS
\mathcal{E}
SD.
Fourth, to test and validate the performance of the portfolios obtained by our proposed strategies, we provide extensive empirical analysis based on the FTSE100, NASDAQ100, SP500, and Fama & French 49 Industry portfolios datasets, comparing their out-of-sample behavior with that of the portfolios constructed by
several SD-based approaches proposed in the literature
and with that of the long-only, long-short, 1-norm, and 2-norm minimum variance portfolios.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we recall the most commonly used exact stochastic dominance rules and present new approximate stochastic dominance criteria.
In Section
3
, we introduce the multi-objective optimization model of
Roman et al. 2013
, and then we provide a general method for finding a class of Pareto-optimal solutions, which SSD-dominate or almost SSD-dominate a given benchmark index.
In Section
4
, we discuss some theoretical features of our proposed approach, providing some statistical and economic interpretations and some illustrative examples.
Section
5
presents the results of an extensive empirical analysis based on real-world data, comparing the out-of-sample performance of our proposed approach with other concurrent strategies.
Conclusions and further research projects are presented in Section
6
.

2
Preliminary concepts on some exact and approximate SD criteria

One of the reasons that has made the use of stochastic dominance criteria for portfolio selection particularly attractive is that these criteria are able to provide a partial order in the space of random variables by avoiding the specification of a particular utility function, which represents the preferences of an investor.
Indeed, from Expected Utility Theory
(
Morgenstern and Von Neumann 1953
, EUT, see, e.g.,)
, given a utility function, a decision-maker prefers a random variable to another if it provides a larger value in terms of its expected utility, i.e., it determines a greater satisfaction.
However, the specification of a utility function is a strictly subjective issue, although it is a key element of EUT.

For the sake of readability, we recall here the most commonly used exact stochastic dominance criteria.
Let
A
and
B
be two random variables with cumulative distribution functions

F_{A}(\alpha)=\mathbf{Pr}(A\leq\alpha)
and
F_{B}(\alpha)=\mathbf{Pr}(B\leq\alpha)

for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 1

(Zero-order Stochastic Dominance - ZSD)
.

A
is preferred to
B
w.r.t. ZSD iff

F_{A-B}(0)=\mathbf{Pr}(A-B\leq 0)=0

(1)

The ZSD relation represents behavior of a decision maker who prefers a random variable over another only when the first gives better outcomes than the second in (almost) all states of the world.

Definition 2

(First-order Stochastic Dominance - FSD)
.

A
is preferred to
B
w.r.t. FSD iff

F_{A}(\alpha)\leq F_{B}(\alpha)\ \ \ \ \forall\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

Definition 3

(Second-order Stochastic Dominance - SSD)
.

A
is preferred to
B
w.r.t. SSD iff

\int_{-\infty}^{\alpha}F_{A}(\tau)d\tau\ \leq\int_{-\infty}^{\alpha}F_{B}(\tau)d\tau\ \ \ \ \forall\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.

(3)

These two last definitions require that each inequality is strict in at least one case.

As discussed, e.g., in
Levy 1992
, these relations can be linked to EUT in terms of different classes of utility functions.
Indeed,
A
is preferred to
B
w.r.t. FSD if and only if
E[u(A)]\geq E[u(B)]
for all non-decreasing utility functions
u
;

A
is preferred to
B
w.r.t. SSD if and only if the same holds for all non-decreasing and concave utility functions.
The SSD relation is less demanding than that of ZSD and of FSD.
More precisely, given an SD criterion of order
v
, when increasing the order,
the
v+1
-SD condition becomes less restrictive.
Furthermore,
v
-SD implies
(v+1)
-SD,
while the opposite is not necessarily true

(
Levy 2015
, see, e.g.,)
.
In this context,
Post and Kopa 2017
remark that Third-order Stochastic Dominance (TSD), although very appealing from a theoretical perspective, is highly demanding to test.
Therefore, these authors propose a method of selecting investment portfolios that dominate a given benchmark in terms of TSD, which is less demanding.
More precisely, their strategy is based on a refinement of the “SuperConvex” TSD (SCTSD) approach of
Bawa et al. 1985
that leads to a portfolio selection model formulated as a convex quadratic constrained programming.
As stated by the authors, SCTSD can be seen as an approximation of TSD although in any case results in a difficult quadratic program.
For the reasons mentioned above, we have restricted ourselves from considering TSD in our experiments. We want to remain within the framework of LP models, avoiding the more complex quadratic or even superconvex mathematical programs.
We also observe that fractional-order SD rules extending the classical integer-order SD have recently been proposed in the literature
(
Müller et al. 2017
;
Huang et al. 2020
, see)
.
However, there currently are no portfolio selection applications in this framework.

In this paper, we essentially focus on the SSD condition and its relaxations, characterizing our approach as an LP.
An example of an approximate (relaxed) version of the SSD criterion is given in

Lizyayev and Ruszczyński 2012
.

Definition 4

(Almost Second-order Stochastic Dominance - ASSD)
.

Given a tolerance
\vartheta>0,\,A
is preferred to
B
w.r.t. ASSD if

\int_{-\infty}^{\alpha}\left(F_{A}(\tau)-F_{B}(\tau)\right)d\tau\leq\vartheta\quad\forall\alpha\in\left[\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}\right],

where
\left[\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}\right]
is the combined range of outcomes of
A
and
B
.

Another example of relaxation of SD conditions can be found in
Bruni et al. 2017
, where the authors introduce
the Zero-order
\varepsilon
-Stochastic Dominance (
\mathrm{Z}\varepsilon\mathrm{SD}
) relation,
which is a relaxation (approximation) of the standard ZSD (see Definition
1
).
Although in their work only the ZSD relaxation is addressed,
this idea could also be extended to higher-order stochastic dominance rules.

Remark 1

(
\mathrm{Z}\varepsilon\mathrm{SD}
vs. ASSD)
.

We observe that the extension of the
\mathrm{Z}\varepsilon\mathrm{SD}
approach to SSD, i.e., the Second-order
\varepsilon
-Stochastic Dominance (
\mathrm{S}\varepsilon\mathrm{SD}
), is equivalent to ASSD, by simply considering
\varepsilon=\vartheta
.

Based on Definitions
3
and
4
, the SSD and ASSD relations can be expressed by the following equivalent criteria.

•

E[u(A)]-E[u(B)]\geq-\tau
holds for all non-decreasing and concave utility functions
u
;

•

E[[\eta-A]_{+}]-E[[\eta-B]_{+}]\leq\tau
holds for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
;

•

Tail_{\beta}(A)-Tail_{\beta}(B)\geq-\tau
holds for all
\beta\in(0,1]
,
where
Tail_{\beta}(A)
is the unconditional expectation of the worst
\beta
100% outcomes of
A
.

Clearly, these conditions are equivalent to SSD for
\tau=0
and to ASSD for
\tau>0
.

In the remaining part of the paper, we assume that the asset returns are discrete random variables defined on a discrete state space,
and that there are
T
states of nature, each with probability
\pi_{t}
with
t=1,...,T
.
We use a look-back approach, where the realizations of the discrete random returns correspond to the historical scenarios, and the investment decision is performed on an in-sample window of
T
historical outcomes, each with probability
\pi_{t}=1/T
, as is typically done in portfolio optimization
(see, e.g.,
Carleo et al. 2017
;
Cesarone 2020
;
Bellini et al. 2021
, and references therein)
.

Remark 2

(Equally likely states and Shannon entropy)
.

We observe that given a discrete random variable with probability distribution
\pi_{t}
(where
t=1,...,T
) the associated Shannon entropy value
S(\pi)=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{t}\log_{2}(\pi_{t})
is a measure for the degree of uncertainty or ignorance about the probability distribution.
Its value is zero when the probability of an event is 1 (i.e.,
\pi_{t}=1
for a
t=1,...,T
) or a certainty, while it is maximum when all the states of nature are equally likely (
\pi_{t}=1/T
), namely the case of maximum uncertainty
(
Myung et al. 1996
, see, e.g.,)
.
As for instance in
Post and Kopa 2017
, our experiments consider the case of maximum ignorance about the probability distribution
\pi_{t}
.

We denote by
R^{I}
the return of a given benchmark index and by
R
the return of a portfolio of assets.
Furthermore, we indicate by
(R_{(j)})_{1\leq j\leq T}
the ordered realizations such that
R_{(1)}\leq\ldots\leq R_{(T)}
, and, reversing the order, by
(R^{(j)})_{1\leq j\leq T}
the ordered realizations such that
R^{(1)}\geq\ldots\geq R^{(T)}
.
Let us assume w.l.o.g. that the scenarios of the portfolio return
R_{t}
and the index
R^{I}_{t}
occur with probabilities
\pi_{t}
for
t=1,...,T
.

In a discrete world, Definitions
2
and
3
can be then expressed as follows

•

R
FSD-dominates
R^{I}
iff
\displaystyle\sum_{R_{t}\leq\alpha}\pi_{t}\leq\sum_{R^{I}_{t}\leq\alpha}\pi_{t},\;\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.

•

R
SSD-dominates
R^{I}
iff

\eta\sum_{R_{t}\leq\eta}\pi_{t}-\sum_{R_{t}\leq\eta}R_{t}\pi_{t}\leq\eta\sum_{R_{t}^{I}\leq\eta}\pi_{t}-\sum_{R_{t}^{I}\leq\eta}R_{t}^{I}\pi_{t},\ \forall\eta\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that for a given threshold
\beta=\sum_{R_{t}\leq\eta}\pi_{t}
, we have that
Tail_{\beta}(R)=\displaystyle\sum_{R_{t}\leq\eta}\pi_{t}R_{t}
.
Then, since

CVaR_{\beta}(R)=-\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{R_{t}\leq\eta}\pi_{t}R_{t},

(4)

we have that

Tail_{\beta}(R)=-\displaystyle\beta CVaR_{\beta}(R)
.
Thus, requiring
Tail_{\beta}(R)\geq Tail_{\beta}(R_{I})
is equivalent to
consider
CVaR_{\beta}(R)\leq CVaR_{\beta}(R_{I})
for all
\beta\in(0,1]
.

In the particularly interesting case, that is usual in portfolio optimization (see also Remark
2
), where
\pi_{t}=1/T
for all
t
, the above criteria simplify resulting in:

•

R
FSD-dominates
R_{I}
iff
R_{(j)}\geq R^{I}_{(j)}
for all
j=1,\ldots,T
.

•

R
SSD-dominates
R_{I}
iff
\sum_{t=1}^{j}R_{(t)}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{j}R^{I}_{(t)}
for all
j=1,\ldots,T
.

Also in this case,
Tail_{\frac{j}{T}}(R)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{j}R_{(t)}
.
Next, since

CVaR_{\frac{j}{T}}(R)=-\frac{1}{j}\sum_{t=1}^{j}R_{(t)},

(5)

we have that

Tail_{\frac{j}{T}}(R)=-\displaystyle\frac{j}{T}CVaR_{\frac{j}{T}}(R)
.
Thus, requiring
Tail_{\frac{j}{T}}(R)\geq Tail_{\frac{j}{T}}(R_{I})
is equivalent to
consider
CVaR_{\frac{j}{T}}(R)\leq CVaR_{\frac{j}{T}}(R_{I})
for all
j=1,\ldots,T
.

When discrete random variables are considered,
we can introduce a
cumulative
version of
\mathrm{S}\varepsilon\mathrm{SD}
, named here
\mathrm{CS}\varepsilon\mathrm{SD}
,
similar to the
\mathrm{CZ}\varepsilon\mathrm{SD}
relation developed in
Bruni et al. 2017
.
This last approximate stochastic dominance relation was thought as a criterion for bounding the cumulative losses for Enhanced Indexation purposes
and, among other properties,
Bruni et al. 2017
show that
\mathrm{CZ}\varepsilon\mathrm{SD}
implies Z
\varepsilon
SD.
Therefore, similarly to how
\mathrm{Z}\varepsilon\mathrm{SD}
can be extended to
\mathrm{S}\varepsilon\mathrm{SD}
,
\mathrm{CZ}\varepsilon\mathrm{SD}
can also be generalized to SSD.
This generalization leads to the following definition and the fact that this new criterion implies
\mathrm{S}\varepsilon\mathrm{SD}
, or, equivalently, ASSD (see Section
4
, Proposition
2
, point 1).

Definition 5

(Cumulative Second-order
\varepsilon
-Stochastic Dominance -
CS\varepsilon SD
)
.

Given a tolerance
\varepsilon>0
,
A
is preferred to
B
w.r.t.
CS\varepsilon SD
if

\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}CVaR_{\frac{t}{T}}(A)-\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}CVaR_{\frac{t}{T}}(B)\leq\varepsilon\quad\forall\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T},

(6)

where
\mathcal{T}=\{1,\ldots,T\}
.

Definition
5
requires to check an exponential number of conditions to verify
CS\varepsilon SD
.
However, the next result shows that the condition can be verified in polynomial time.

For this purpose, we need to introduce some additional notation.
Let us denote by
\sigma
the permutation that provides the sorting of
\left(CVaR_{\frac{t}{T}}(A)-CVaR_{\frac{t}{T}}(B)\right)_{1\leq t\leq T}
as
CVaR_{\frac{\sigma(1)}{T}}(A)-CVaR_{\frac{\sigma(1)}{T}}(B)\geq\ldots\geq CVaR_{\frac{\sigma(T)}{T}}(A)-CVaR_{\frac{\sigma(T)}{T}}(B)
.
Next, for the ease of presentation, and whenever the framework is clear, we denote

\sum_{j=1}^{t}\left(CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(A)-CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(B)\right)^{(j)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{t}CVaR_{\frac{\sigma(j)}{T}}(A)-CVaR_{\frac{\sigma(j)}{T}}(B)

Proposition 1
.

Given a tolerance
\varepsilon>0,A
is preferred to
B
w.r.t.
CS\varepsilon SD
iff

\sum_{j=1}^{t}\left(CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(A)-CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(B)\right)^{(j)}\leq\varepsilon\quad\forall t\in\mathcal{T}.

Proof.

We observe that Conditions (
6
) are equivalent to require

\max_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}:|\mathcal{S}|=t}\left\{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}CVaR_{\frac{t}{T}}(A)-\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}CVaR_{\frac{t}{T}}(B)\right\}\leq\varepsilon\quad\forall t\in\mathcal{T}.

Furthermore, the maximum in the left-hand-side is attained on the
t
largest values, so that

\max_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}:|\mathcal{S}|=t}\left\{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}CVaR_{\frac{t}{T}}(A)-\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}CVaR_{\frac{t}{T}}(B)\right\}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\left(CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(A)-CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(B)\right)^{(j)}.

This proves the result.
∎

We point out that in Proposition
1
we have a single threshold
\varepsilon
,
\forall t
.
However, it can be generalized so that we can impose different tolerances
\varepsilon_{t}
for each
t\in\mathcal{T}
, as described in the following definition.

Definition 6

(Cumulative Second-order
\mathcal{E}
-Stochastic Dominance -
CS\mathcal{E}SD
)
.

Given a tolerance vector
\mathcal{E}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{T})\in\mathbb{R}^{T}_{>0}
,
A
is preferred to
B
w.r.t.
CS\mathcal{E}SD
if

\sum_{j=1}^{t}\left(CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(A)-CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(B)\right)^{(j)}\leq\varepsilon_{t}\quad\forall t\in\mathcal{T}.

In the next section, we introduce the Enhanced Indexation model of
Roman et al. 2013
, which properly generalized is closely connected to
CS\mathcal{E}SD
, as shown in Section
4.1
.

3
Enhanced Indexation based on stochastic dominance

Here, we first report the multi-objective approach of
Roman et al. 2013
for finding portfolios that SSD-dominate or almost SSD-dominate a given benchmark index.
Then, we provide a general method for finding a novel subset of Pareto-optimal solutions of such a multi-objective model.
More precisely, we generalize the existing minimax selection rule for the multi-objective approach of
Roman et al. 2013
, which, as shown in Section
3.1
, is strictly linked to the ASSD conditions of
Lizyayev and Ruszczyński 2012
(see Definition
4
, Remarks
1
and
3
).
Thus still keeping the model as an LP, in Section
3.2
we propose more flexible choices of Pareto-optimal solutions of such a multi-objective model, which, as discussed in Section
4
, are related to the
CS\mathcal{E}SD
rules and as we shall see, imply those of
Lizyayev and Ruszczyński 2012
.

We consider a setting where
n
assets are available in the investment universe with rates of return described by
the random variables
R_{1},\ldots,R_{n}
.
A portfolio is identified with a vector
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
, where
x_{k}

denotes the fraction of capital invested in the
k^{th}
asset.
Hence, the (linear) portfolio return is given by

R(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}R_{k}
.

As mentioned above,
Roman et al. 2013
propose the following multi-objective model whose Pareto-optimal solutions SSD-dominate or almost SSD-dominate a given benchmark index
(
Roman et al. 2006
;
Fábián et al. 2011b
;
Fábián et al. 2011a
;
Valle et al. 2017
;
Cesarone et al. 2022a
, see also)

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{x}&\left[(CVaR_{\frac{1}{T}}(R(x))-CVaR_{\frac{1}{T}}(R_{I})),\ldots,(CVaR_{\frac{T}{T}}(R(x))-CVaR_{\frac{T}{T}}(R_{I}))\right]\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in C\end{array}

(7)

where
C
is a polyhedron.
Alternatively, they find the SSD efficient portfolios by solving a slightly different
multi-objective optimization problem based on centered Tails

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\max_{x}&\left[(Tail_{\frac{1}{T}}(R(x))-Tail_{\frac{1}{T}}(R_{I})),\ldots,(Tail_{\frac{T}{T}}(R(x))-Tail_{\frac{T}{T}}(R_{I}))\right]\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in C\end{array}

(8)

In the following, we will focus on Problem (
7
) and then briefly discuss how the results obtained from this problem are also valid for Problem (
8
).

3.1
Minimax scalarization

Among the infinite Pareto-optimal points belonging to the efficient
T
-dimensional hypersurface generated by Problem (
7
),
Roman et al. 2013
consider the one obtained by solving the following Minimax problem

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{x}&\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq T}(CVaR_{\frac{j}{T}}(R(x))-CVaR_{\frac{j}{T}}(R_{I}))\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in C\end{array}

(9)

This model can be expressed as an LP problem,
using the CVaR reformulation of
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2000
;
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2002
.
However, due to the high
number of variables and constraints (more than
T^{2}
),
Problem (
9
) is solved by implementing cutting
plane techniques, as proposed by
Fábián et al. 2011a
.

We note that the above problem is equivalent to

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{x}&\displaystyle\left(CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(R(x))-CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(R_{I})\right)^{(1)}\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in C\end{array}

(10)

Remark 3

(Minimax scalarization vs. ASSD conditions)
.

From Problem (
10
), one can directly observe the link between Model (
9
) in the paper by
Roman et al. 2013
,
SSD
(Definition
3
) and ASSD (Definition
4
) conditions.
Indeed, denoting by
x^{\star}
the optimal solution of Problem (
10
), if the corresponding optimal value function

\left(CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(R(x^{\star}))-CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(R_{I})\right)^{(1)}
is nonpositive (positive), then the corresponding optimal portfolio SSD-dominates (ASSD-dominates) the benchmark index.

3.2
Ordered weighted average scalarization

To address the multi-objective optimization problems (
7
) and (
8
), we propose here an approach based on the Ordered Weighted Average (OWA) operator.
As shown below, using the OWA scalarization, we are able to provide a more general methodology to highlight a new subset of Pareto-optimal solutions of such multi-objective problems based on centered CVaRs (see Section
3.2.1
) and centered Tails (see Section
3.2.2
).

3.2.1
Maximizing OWA of the centered CVaRs

An alternative scalarization of Problem (
7
) consists of the following simple idea:
rather than minimizing the maximum centered CVaR, namely Model (
9
),
one can investigate the performance of the Pareto-optimal portfolios obtained by minimizing the sum of the
k
maximal CVaRs for a fixed
k=1,\ldots,T
.
It is well-known that this form of scalarization always results in Pareto-optimal solutions since the ordered median function is an isotone function provided than the lambda weights are non-negative
(
Nickel and Puerto 2006
, see Lemma 1.1 in)
.
Obviously, when
k=1
, one recovers the Minimax approach of
Roman et al. 2013
.
More specifically, we deal with Problem (
7
) by scalarizing the
T
goals in the following way.
To simplify the presentation, we introduce some notation.
Let us denote by

g_{t}(x):=CVaR_{t/T}(R(x))-CVaR_{t/T}(R_{I})
with
t\in\mathcal{T}
, and following our notation on Section
2
, by
(g^{(j)}(x))_{1\leq t\leq T}
the elements of a vector such that
g^{(1)}(x)\geq g^{(2)}(x)\geq\ldots\geq g^{(T)}(x)
.
We then consider the following problem

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{x}&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\lambda^{j}g^{(j)}(x)\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in C\end{array}

(11)

where
(\lambda^{j})_{1\leq j\leq T}
is a vector such that
\lambda^{1}\geq\lambda^{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda^{T}\geq 0
.
We can observe that our approach is less conservative (i.e., risk-averse) than that of
Roman et al. 2013
, since Problem (
11
) takes into account all differences between CVaRs at different confidence levels and not just the worst one.
However, our approach still models conservative behavior, since larger weights are assigned to the worst differences.

Thus, our goal is to obtain a tractable representation for the problem that minimizes the sum of an ordered weighted average (OWA) of the centered CVaRs,
(CVaR_{\cdot/T}(R(x))-CVaR_{\cdot/T}(R_{I}))_{1\leq j\leq T}^{(j)}
, with weights
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{T}\geq 0
.
Note that if
\lambda=(1,0,\ldots,0)
we obtain the Minimax portfolio
(
Roman et al. 2013
, as in)
, whereas if
\lambda=(1,1,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(k)}}{{\ldots}},1,0,\ldots,0)
we get the portfolio minimizing the sum of the
k
-worse centered CVaRs.

To achieve a more tractable formulation of Problem (
11
), we denote by
f_{t}(x)=-g_{t}(x)=CVaR_{t/T}(R_{I})-CVaR_{t/T}(R(x))

\forall t\in\mathcal{T}
, and by
(f_{(j)}(x))_{1\leq t\leq T}
the elements of a vector such that
f_{(1)}(x)\leq f_{(2)}(x)\leq\ldots\leq f_{(T)}(x)
.
Hence,
f_{(j)}(x)=-g^{(j)}(x)

\forall j\in\mathcal{T}
.
We then obtain the following maximization problem which is equivalent to (
11
)

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\max_{x}&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\lambda^{j}f_{(j)}(x)\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in C\end{array}

(12)

where, again,
(\lambda^{j})_{1\leq j\leq T}
is a vector such that
\lambda^{1}\geq\lambda^{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda^{T}\geq 0
.
Furthermore, using the approach described in
Nickel and Puerto 2006
(Theorem 1.3, pag. 13) and
Marín et al. 2020
, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\lambda^{j}f_{(j)}(x)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{z}\;\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\lambda^{j}f_{t}(x)z_{jt}

(13a)

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t.}\hskip 2.84544pt\sum_{j=1}^{T}z_{jt}=1,\;t=1,\ldots,T,

(13b)

\displaystyle\hskip 17.07182pt\sum_{t=1}^{T}z_{jt}=1,\;j=1,\ldots,T,

(13c)

\displaystyle\hskip 17.07182ptz_{jt}\in\{0,1\},

(13d)

Since the above problem is an assignment problem, and the constraint matrix (
13b
)-(
13c
) is totally unimodular, we can
relax the integrality constraints (
13d
) into
z_{jt}\geq 0
, thus obtaining an LP.
Then, Problem (
13a
)-(
13b
)-(
13c
) with
z_{jt}\geq 0
has an exact dual so that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\lambda^{j}f_{(j)}(x)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\max_{u,v}\;\sum_{t=1}^{T}u_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{T}v_{j}

(14)

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t.}\hskip 2.84544ptu_{t}+v_{j}\leq\lambda^{j}f_{t}(x),\;\forall j,t=1,\ldots,T,

\displaystyle\hskip 17.07182ptu_{t},v_{j}\mbox{ free}\qquad\forall j,t=1,\ldots,T

Therefore, if we wish to maximize the OWA of the centered CVaRs, then we can proceed by solving the following problem

\begin{array}[]{llll}\displaystyle\max_{x}&\sum_{j=1}^{T}\lambda^{j}f_{(j)}(x)=&\displaystyle\max_{x,u,v}&\sum_{t=1}^{T}u_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{T}v_{j}\\
&&\mbox{s.t.}&u_{t}+v_{j}\leq\lambda^{j}(CVaR_{t/T}(R_{I})-CVaR_{t/T}(R(x))),\;\forall j,t\\
&&&u_{t},v_{j}\mbox{ free},\;x\in C\end{array}

(15)

where
f_{(j)}(x)=(CVaR_{\cdot/T}(R_{I})-CVaR_{\cdot/T}(R(x)))_{(j)}
and
C
is a polyhedron.
This representation still needs some further refinement to become linear since the CVaR function requires an appropriate expansion.
Knowing that for a fixed portfolio
x\in C

\displaystyle CVaR_{t/T}(R(x)):=

\displaystyle\min_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R},d}\;\zeta+\frac{T}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\pi_{j}d_{j}

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t.}\hskip 2.84544ptd_{j}\geq-\zeta-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}r_{\ell j}x_{\ell},\quad j=1,\ldots,T,

\displaystyle\hskip 17.07182ptd_{j}\geq 0,\;\forall j\,,

we can express
f_{t}(x)=CVaR_{t/T}(R_{I})-CVaR_{t/T}(R(x))
using the following linear program

\begin{array}[]{lll}f_{t}(x):=&\displaystyle\max_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R},d}\;CVaR_{t/T}(R_{I})-\zeta-\frac{T}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\pi_{j}d_{j}&\\
&\mbox{s.t.}\hskip 2.84544ptd_{j}\geq-\zeta-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}r_{\ell j}x_{\ell},\quad j=1,\ldots,T,\\
&\hskip 17.07182ptd_{j}\geq 0,\;\forall j.\end{array}

(16)

Now, the largest feasible region for the inequalities
u_{t}+v_{j}+\lambda^{j}(\zeta_{t}+\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{T}d_{tk})\leq\lambda^{j}CVaR_{t/T}(R_{I}),\;\forall j,t
subject to the constraints of the above problem, is attained when
\lambda^{j}(\zeta_{t}+\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{T}d_{tk})
is minimized.
Thus, we obtain an alternative valid formulation for (
15
) substituting
CVaR_{t/T}(R(x))
by
\lambda^{j}(\zeta_{t}+\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{T}d_{tk})
plus the constraints in (
16
). Hence, Problem (
15
) becomes equivalent to the following one:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\max_{x,u,v,d,\zeta}&\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}u_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{T}v_{j}\\
\quad\mbox{s.t.}&\displaystyle u_{t}+v_{j}\leq\lambda^{j}(CVaR_{t/T}(R_{I})-\zeta_{t}-\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{T}d_{tk}),\;\forall j,t\\
&\displaystyle d_{tk}\geq-\zeta_{t}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}r_{\ell k}x_{\ell},\;t,k=1,\ldots,T,\\
&\zeta_{t},u_{t},v_{j}\mbox{ free, }d_{tk}\geq 0,\\
&x\in C\,,\end{array}

(17)

where, again,
C
is a polyhedron.
This last formulation is a Linear Program (LP) with
n+T+T+T^{2}+T
(namely,
x,u,v,d,\zeta
) variables, and
2T^{2}
constraints plus those defining the polyhedron
C
.
This LP problem returns the portfolio
x
that maximizes the ordered weighted average (OWA) of the centered CVaRs.
The reader may think that these figures are large, but still, the model solves as a linear program, which simplifies a lot due to nowadays efficiency of current solvers.

4.1

) scalarized by the OWA operator and the CS

4.1
OWA maximization and CS
\mathcal{E}
SD

We provide here the theoretical relationship between Model (
12
), which consists of maximizing the OWA of the centered CVaRs, and the CS
\mathcal{E}
SD criterion, described in Definition
6
.

Let
g^{(t)}(x):=\left(CVaR_{t/T}(R(x))-CVaR_{t/T}(R_{I})\right)^{(t)}

and
G^{(t)}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{t}g^{(j)}(x)
for each
t\in\mathcal{T}
.
Note that the efficient portfolios, whose returns dominate the return of a given benchmark index
R_{I}
w.r.t. CS
\mathcal{E}
SD, are those obtained by solving the following problems

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{x}&\displaystyle G^{(j)}(x)\\
\mbox{s.t.}&G^{(t)}(x)\leq\varepsilon_{t}\quad t=1,\ldots,T\quad t\neq j\\
&x\in C\end{array}

(23)

for each
j\in\mathcal{T}
.
In the following we show that Problem (
23
) is equivalent to Problem (
12
).
For the sake of readability, we recall below some well-known concepts in multi-criteria optimization.
Consider the following multi-objective optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left(h_{1}(x),h_{2}(x),\ldots h_{p}(x)\right)\,.

(24)

A standard technique to scalarize Problem (
24
)
is the
\varepsilon
-constraint method, namely

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{x}&\displaystyle h_{j}(x)\\
\mbox{s.t.}&h_{k}(x)\leq\varepsilon_{k}\quad k=1,\ldots,p\quad k\neq j\\
&x\in\mathcal{X}\end{array}

(25)

An alternative is the following weighted sum
problem

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\gamma_{k}h_{k}(x)

(26)

We first recall a theorem that links these two methods.

Theorem 7

(Theorem 4.6 of
Ehrgott 2005
)
.

1.

Suppose
\hat{x}
is an optimal solution of (
26
). If
\gamma_{j}>0
there exists
\hat{\varepsilon}
such that
\hat{x}
is an optimal solution of (
25
), too.

2.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is a convex set and
h_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are convex functions. If
\hat{x}
is an optimal solution of (
25
) for some
j
, there exists
\hat{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq}^{p}
such that
\hat{x}
is optimal for
\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\hat{\gamma}_{k}h_{k}(x)
.

The following result is instrumental to get the announced equivalence between models.

Lemma 8
.

Assume that
C
is a convex set. Then,
G^{(t)}
is a convex function for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

Let
t\in\mathcal{T}
. For
x,y\in C
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
we have that

G^{(t)}(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)=\sum_{j=1}^{t}g^{(j)}(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y).

Let
k_{1},\ldots,k_{t}
be indices such that

\sum_{j=1}^{t}g^{(j)}(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)=\sum_{j=1}^{t}g_{k_{j}}(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y).

Thus, since CVaR function is convex,

\sum_{j=1}^{t}g_{k_{j}}(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\leq\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{t}g_{k_{j}}(x)+(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{t}g_{k_{j}}(y).

Finally, note that

\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{t}g_{k_{j}}(x)+(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{t}g_{k_{j}}(y)\leq\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{t}g^{(j)}(x)+(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{t}g^{(j)}(y)=\alpha G^{(t)}(x)+(1-\alpha)G^{(t)}(y).

∎

Exploiting Theorem
7
and Lemma
8
, we now provide the theoretical link between Problems (
23
) and (
11
).

Theorem 9
.

1.

Suppose
\hat{x}
is an optimal solution of Problem (
11
).
If
\lambda^{j}-\lambda^{j+1}>0
, then there exists
\underline{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq}^{T}
such that
\hat{x}
is an optimal solution of Problem (
23
), too.

2.

If
\hat{x}
is an optimal solution of Problem (
23
) for some
j
, there exists
\hat{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq}^{T}
with
\hat{\lambda}_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\hat{\lambda}_{T}
such that
\hat{x}
is optimal for Problem (
11
).

Proof.

1.

We note that the objective function of Problem (
11
) can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\lambda^{j}g^{(j)}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{t=j}^{T}\left(\lambda^{t}-\lambda^{t+1}\right)g^{(j)}(x)\quad\mbox{or, equivalently,}

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{t=1}^{j}\left(\lambda^{j}-\lambda^{j+1}\right)g^{(t)}(x)

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\left(\lambda^{j}-\lambda^{j+1}\right)G^{(j)}(x)

where
G^{(j)}:=\sum_{t=1}^{j}g^{(t)}
for each
j\in\mathcal{T}
and we assume that
\lambda^{T+1}=0
.
Thus, we can reformulate Problem (
11
) as

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{x}&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\left(\lambda^{j}-\lambda^{j+1}\right)G^{(j)}(x)\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in C\,.\end{array}

(29)

Knowing that, by construction,
\lambda^{1}\geq\lambda^{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda^{T}\geq 0
and by hypothesis of item 1,
\lambda^{j}-\lambda^{j+1}>0
for some
j\in\mathcal{T}
,
the result follows by Theorem
7
.

2.

Assuming that
\hat{x}
is an optimal solution of Problem (
23
) and using point 2 of Theorem
7
, there must exist some
\hat{\gamma}=\left(\hat{\gamma}^{1},\hat{\gamma}^{2},\ldots,\hat{\gamma}^{T}\right)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq}^{T}
such that

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\gamma}^{t}G^{(t)}(x)\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\gamma}^{t}G^{(t)}(\hat{x})

for all
x\in C
. Equivalently,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\gamma}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}g^{(j)}(x)\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\gamma}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}g^{(j)}(\hat{x})

Exploiting relations (
27
) and (
28
) and denoting by
\hat{\gamma}^{t}=\hat{\lambda}^{t}-\hat{\lambda}^{t+1}

\forall t\in\mathcal{T}
, but
with
\hat{\lambda}^{T+1}=0
,
we have

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{j=t}^{T}\left(\hat{\lambda}^{j}-\hat{\lambda}^{j+1}\right)g^{(t)}(x)\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{j=t}^{T}\left(\hat{\lambda}^{j}-\hat{\lambda}^{j+1}\right)g^{(t)}(\hat{x})\,,

namely,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\lambda}^{t}g^{(t)}(x)\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\lambda}^{t}g^{(t)}(\hat{x})\,,

for all
x\in C
, which completes the proof.

∎

4.2
Statistical and economic interpretations of CS
\mathcal{E}
SD

In the previous section, we have shown (see Theorem
9
) the equivalence between the maximization of the OWA of the centered CVaRs, and the CS
\mathcal{E}
SD rule.

Next, we note that the OWA of the centered CVaRs, for
\lambda=(1,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t}}{{\ldots}},1,0,\ldots,0)
can be interpreted as the
t/T
-CVaR of the centered CVaRs.
Therefore, from a statistical viewpoint, according to
Bertsimas and Sim 2004
, it is more robust to maximize the
t/T
-CVaR of the centered CVaRs than the minimax model (
1/T
-CVaR) proposed by
Roman et al. 2013
.
It has been well-known since the early ’70s that minimax models provide robust solutions against uncertain data, although they may be too conservative.
An alternative is to consider the minimization of
t
-sums.
In this regard, the reader may note that our proposal, namely the minimization of the
t
largest losses of a sequence, provides more robust solutions than considering only the largest loss (
t=1
).
The interested reader is referred to the papers by
Bertsimas and Sim 2004
,
Puerto et al. 2017
, and the references therein for further insights on
k
-sum optimization.

On the other hand, based on the equivalence provided by Theorem
9
, we can also give a insightful interpretation to the CS
\mathcal{E}
SD rule. Indeed, any CS
\mathcal{E}
SD portfolio comes from an optimal solution of an OWA solution of centered CVaRs for particular choices of lambda weights.
In other words, the CS
\mathcal{E}
SD portfolios optimize weighted sums of centered CVaRs; each choice of the lambda parameters reflects the importance given by the decision-maker to the ordinal sequence of centered CVaRs.

Finally, we discuss on the advantages of the
CS\varepsilon SD
concept.
We prove that it is a refinement of the concept of ASSD in
Lizyayev and Ruszczyński 2012
and, moreover, that the expected utility of a
CS\varepsilon SD
portfolio does not exceed the expected utility of the dominating one by more than
\varepsilon
for a large class of reasonable utility functions.

Proposition 2
.

Let
A
and
B
be two random variables.

1.

If
A

CS\varepsilon SD
-dominates
B
then
A
ASSD-dominates
B
(
CS\varepsilon SD\Rightarrow
ASSD).

2.

If A
CS\varepsilon SD
-dominates B, then
E[u(A)]+\varepsilon\geq E[u(B)]
for any nondecreasing concave utility function
u
with first derivative
u^{\prime}\leq 1
.

Proof.

From Proposition
1
it follows that
CS\varepsilon SD
implies ASSD, or equivalently
S\varepsilon SD
, which requires
\left(CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(A)-CVaR_{\frac{\cdot}{T}}(B)\right)^{(1)}\leq\varepsilon
. This proves assertion (1.).

Next, to prove assertion (2.) we use Theorem 2 in
Lizyayev and Ruszczyński 2012
and the result in assertion (1.).
∎

5.1
Datasets and Methodology

In this section, we provide some details about the four real-world datasets on which we test all the portfolio selection strategies described in the previous section:

1.

FTSE100 (Financial Times Stock Exchange, UK), containing 80 assets and 3715 observations (Oct 2006-Dec 2020);

2.

NASDAQ 100 (National Association of Securities Dealers Automated Quotation, USA), containing 54 assets and 3715 observations (Oct 2006-Dec 2020);

3.

SP500 (Standard & Poor’s, USA), containing 336 assets and 3715 observations (Oct 2006-Dec 2020);

4.

FF49 (Fama & French 49 Industry portfolios, USA), containing 49 portfolios considered as assets and using the subsample where all the returns of the 49 industries are available, namely from July 1969 to June 2023.

The first three datasets are publicly available on the website
https://www.francescocesarone.com/data-sets

and consist of daily prices, adjusted for dividends and stock splits and obtained from Refinitiv
(
Cesarone et al. 2022b
, see, for more details,)
.
For the last dataset, we use daily returns downloaded from

https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html#Research
. In this case, we consider as a benchmark the Equally-Weighted portfolio.

Our aim is to examine the performance of all models described above, using a rolling time windows
scheme of evaluation.
More precisely, we consider an in-sample time window of 6 months (i.e., 125 financial days).
We then evaluate the portfolio out-of-sample performance over the next month (i.e., 20 financial days). Next, we shift the in-sample window by 1 month to cover the out-of-sample period, recalculate the optimal portfolio relative to the new in-sample window, and repeat the operation.

For each portfolio strategy, we calculate some standard performance measures recalled below
(
Cesarone et al. 2015
;
Cesarone et al. 2016
;
Cesarone et al. 2020
;
Bruni et al. 2017
;
Cesarone and Colucci 2017
, see, e.g.,)
.

•

The average
\hat{\mu}^{out}
(
ExpRet
) and the standard deviation
\hat{\sigma}^{out}
(
Vol
) of the out-of-sample portfolio returns.

•

The
Sharpe
ratio defined as
\hat{\mu}^{out}/\hat{\sigma}^{out}

(
Sharpe 1966
;
Sharpe 1994
)
.

•

The Maximum Drawdown
(
MDD
, see, e.g.,
Chekhlov et al. 2005
, and references therein)
.
Denoting by
R_{\tau}^{out}
the out-of-sample portfolio returns
for each portfolio strategy, we first consider the cumulative out-of-sample portfolio returns, which correspond to the values of wealth after
\tau

periods
W_{\tau}=W_{\tau-1}(1+R_{\tau}^{out})
, where
\tau=M+1,\ldots,\bar{T}
,
M=125
,
M
is the length of the in-sample window,
\bar{T}
is the number of observations available for a single dataset, and the initial wealth
W_{M}=1
.
We define the drawdowns as

dd_{\tau}=\frac{W_{\tau}-\max_{M+1\leq s\leq\tau}(W_{s})}{\max_{M+1\leq s\leq\tau}(W_{s})}.

Note that the drawdowns
dd_{\tau}
are obviously negative.
The Maximum Drawdown
Mdd
corresponds to the worst drawdown over the entire
out-of-sample period, namely
Mdd=\min_{M+1\leq\tau\leq T}(dd_{\tau})
.

•

The
Ulcer
Index, which is defined as the square root
of the mean of the squared percentage drawdowns
dd_{\tau}
with
\tau=M+1,\ldots,T

UI=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{\tau=M+1}^{T}dd_{\tau}^{2}}{T-M}}.

(30)

It evaluates the depth and the duration of drawdowns
in prices over the out-of-sample period
(
Martin and McCann 1989
, see, e.g.,)
.

•

The
Sortino
ratio
(
Sortino et al. 2001
)
, defined as the ratio between the
average of
R^{out}
and its downside deviation

\frac{E[R^{out}-r_{f}]}{\sigma(\min\{R^{out}-r_{f},\,0\})}\,.

•

The
Rachev
ratio with confidence levels of
5\%

(
Biglova et al. 2004
)
, defined as the ratio between the average of the best
5\%

values of
R^{out}
(with the opposite sign) and that of the worst
5\%
values of
R^{out}

\frac{C\!V\!a\!R_{5\%}(-R^{out})}{C\!V\!a\!R_{5\%}(R^{out})}\,.

•

The
Turnover

(
DeMiguel et al. 2009
, see, e.g.,)
evaluates the amount of trading required to perform in practice the portfolio strategy, and is defined as

Turnover=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mid x_{q,k}-x_{q-1,k}\mid,

where
Q
represents the number of rebalances,
x_{q,k}
is the portfolio weight of asset
k
after rebalancing, and
x_{q-1,k}
is the portfolio weight before rebalancing at time
q
. Lower turnover values indicate better portfolio performance. We point out that this definition of portfolio turnover is a proxy of the effective one, since it evaluates only the amount
of trading generated by the models at each rebalance,
without considering the trades due to changes in asset
prices between one rebalance and the next. Thus, by definition,
the turnover of the EW portfolio is zero.

•

The Jensen’s Alpha
(
Jensen 1968
,
AlphaJ
,)
, defined as the intercept of the line given by the linear regression of
R^{out}
on
R_{I}^{out}
, namely

\alpha=E[R^{out}]-\beta E[R_{I}^{out}]
,
where
\beta=Cov(R^{out},R_{I}^{out})/\sigma^{2}(R_{I}^{out})
.

•

The Value-at-Risk of the out-of-sample portfolio returns
R_{\tau}^{out}
with a confidence level equal to 1% (
VaR1
).

•

The
Appraisal
ratio
(
Treynor and Black 1973
)
, defined as the Jensen’s Alpha divided by the standard deviation of the residual return
\epsilon_{r}
, namely:

\frac{\alpha}{\sigma(\epsilon_{r})}\,.

where
\epsilon_{r}=R^{out}-\alpha-\beta E[R_{I}^{out}]
.
The larger is its value, the better is the portfolio performance.
This measure is particularly used by practitioners for evaluating the asset selection ability of a model.
It grasps the idea that a portfolio manager to generate
\alpha
has to deviate from the benchmark, i.e., take residual risk
\sigma(\epsilon_{r})
.

•

The Information ratio
(
Goodwin 1998
,
Info
,)
, defined as the expected value of the difference between the out-of-sample portfolio return and that of the benchmark index divided by the standard deviation of such difference, namely

\frac{E[R^{out}-R_{I}^{out}]}{\sigma(R^{out}-R_{I}^{out})}\,.

The larger is its value, the better is the portfolio performance.
This measure is equivalent to the Appraisal ratio with
\beta=1
.

•

The
Omega
ratio, defined as

\displaystyle\frac{E[\max(0,R^{out}-\eta)]}{E[\min(0,R^{out}-\eta)]}\,,

where
\eta
is fixed to 0.

•

The average number of selected assets (
ave #
) and running
time
in seconds.

•

The Return On Investment (
ROI
), namely the time-by-time return generated by each portfolio strategy over a specified time horizon
\Delta H
.
More precisely, ROI is defined as follows

ROI_{\tau}=\frac{W_{\tau}-W_{\tau-\Delta H}}{W_{\tau-\Delta H}}\qquad\tau=M+\Delta H+1,\ldots,\bar{T}

(31)

where
W_{\tau}=W_{\tau-\Delta H}\prod_{t=\tau-\Delta H+1}^{\tau}(1+R_{t}^{out})
is the portfolio wealth generated by investing at the beginning of the time horizon
the amount of capital
W_{\tau-\Delta H}
.

We also test the statistical significance of our results. To this end, the significance of the difference between the Sharpe ratios of the selected portfolios and that of the benchmark is tested both using the bootstrapping and HAC methodologies as presented in
Ledoit and Wolf 2008
.
Moreover, to test the statistical significance of the difference between the variances of the selected portfolios and that of the benchmark,
we use the bootstrapping method as proposed in
Ledoit and Wolf 2011
.
For both the bootstrap tests, we choose
M=3000
bootstrap repetitions and
block sizes
b=1,10
.
Furthermore, we adopt a
t
-statistic as in
Goodwin 1998
to test the statistical significance of the positivity of the Information ratio.

p

Conclusions

This paper has analyzed portfolio selection strategies for EI that are based on SD relations.
We have introduced a new type of approximate stochastic dominance rule, named
CS\mathcal{E}SD
, and we have proved that it implies the almost SSD proposed by
Lizyayev and Ruszczyński 2012
.
We have proposed a new methodology that select portfolios using the OWA operator which generalizes previous approaches based on minimax selection rules, and still leads to solving linear programming models.
Next, we have proved that our enhanced indexation model based on OWA selects portfolios that dominate the benchmarks in terms of this new form of stochastic dominance rule, and it highlights encouraging out-of-sample performances with respect to a benchmark index.
Finally, we have tested and validated the performance of our new proposal reporting an extensive empirical analysis based on three real-world datasets belonging to major stock markets across the world (FTSE100, NASDAQ100 and SP500) and on the Fama and French 49 industry portfolios.

This paper has also opened new avenues of research into the EI field. In a follow-up paper, we propose to extend the analysis to portfolio selection strategies based on approximate stochastic dominance conditions by considering an appropriate reshaping of the original benchmark index, as suggested by
Valle et al. 2017
and
Cesarone et al. 2022a
.
