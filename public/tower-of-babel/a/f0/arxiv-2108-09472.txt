The β \beta -Delaunay tessellation IV: Mixing properties and central limit theorems
Anna Gusakova, Zakhar Kabluchko, Christoph Thäle

Abstract
Various mixing properties of β \beta -, β ′ \beta^{\prime} - and Gaussian Delaunay tessellations in ℝ d − 1 \mathbb{R}^{d-1} are studied. It is shown that these tessellation models are absolutely regular, or β \beta -mixing. In the β \beta - and the Gaussian case exponential bounds for the absolute regularity coefficients are found. In the β ′ \beta^{\prime} -case these coefficients show a polynomial decay only. In the background are new and strong concentration bounds on the radius of stabilization of the underlying construction. Using a general device for absolutely regular stationary random tessellations, central limit theorems for a number of geometric parameters of β \beta - and Gaussian Delaunay tessellations are established. This includes the number of k k -dimensional faces and the k k -volume of the k k -skeleton for k ∈ { 0 , 1 , … , d − 1 } k\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\} . Keywords: Absolute regularity, beta-Delaunay tessellation, beta’-Delaunay tessellation, central limit theorem, Gaussian-Delaunay tessellation, mixing properties, radius of stabilization, stochastic geometry, tail triviality MSC: 52A22, 52B11, 53C65, 60D05, 60F05.

-Delaunay tessellation of

We would like to emphasize that the
\beta

\beta^{\prime}
- and Gaussian-Delaunay tessellations are of interest in the study of the asymptotic geometry of random convex hulls. For example, the
\beta
-Delaunay tessellation reflects the local asymptotic structure of so-called random

-polytopes in the

The purpose of the present paper is the study of several mixing properties for all the random tessellation models mentioned so far, that is, the
\beta
-, the
\beta^{\prime}
- and the Gaussian-Delaunay tessellation. More precisely, we study the following hierarchy of concepts:

•

ergodicity,

•

mixing of any order, also known as ergodic mixing,

•

tail triviality, also known as Kolmogorov’s K-property,

•

\alpha
-mixing, also known as strong mixing,

•

absolute regularity, also known as
\beta
-mixing (a terminology we do not follow to avoid confusion with the model parameter
\beta

Our main result is an explicit exponential bound on the absolute regularity coefficient of a
\beta
- and Gaussian-Delaunay tessellation as well as a polynomial bound in case of the
\beta^{\prime}
-Delaunay tessellation. The proof relies on strong estimates for the so-called radius of stabilization of our construction, which considerably refines previous results from part II of this paper
[
6
]
and are of independent interest. While for the purposes in part II much less explicit bounds were sufficient, in order to handle the absolute regularity coefficient in the way we need, much finer control on the radius of stabilization is required. In a sense, this is the technical heart of our paper.

As an application of our bounds on the absolute regularity coefficients we develop a central limit theory for combinatorial and also for geometric parameters associated with
\beta
- and Gaussian-Delaunay tessellations, using a rather general central limit theorem of Heinrich
[
8

9
]
for absolutely regular stationary random random fields. More precisely, we prove that the number of

k
-skeleton, which is the union of all
k
-dimensional faces of all tessellation cells.
This complements the limit theorems studied in part III
[
7
]
of this series of papers, which focus on a high-dimensional setting.

2.2
Description of the tessellation models

In this section we briefly recall the construction of the
\beta
-Delaunay, the
\beta^{\prime}
-Delaunay and the Gaussian-Delaunay tessellation we introduced in part I
[
5
]
and II
[
6
]
of this series of papers. We also refer the reader to these texts for further background material, details and proofs.

We start by introducing the three Poisson point processes on which the construction of our tessellations is based. For
\beta>-1
let
\eta_{\beta}
be a Poisson point process on the product space
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times[0,\infty)
whose intensity measure has density

(v,h)\mapsto c_{d,\beta}h^{\beta},\qquad c_{d,\beta}:={\Gamma({d\over 2}+\beta+1)\over\pi^{d\over 2}\Gamma(\beta+1)},

(1)

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times[0,\infty)
. Next, for
\beta>(d+1)/2
let
\eta_{\beta}^{\prime}
be a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times(-\infty,0]
whose intensity measure has density

(v,h)\mapsto c_{d,\beta}^{\prime}(-h)^{(-\beta)},\qquad c^{\prime}_{d,\beta}:={\Gamma(\beta)\over\pi^{d\over 2}\Gamma(\beta-{d\over 2})},

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times(-\infty,0]
. Finally, we denote by
\zeta
a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
whose intensity measure has density

(v,h)\mapsto{1\over(2\pi)^{d/2}}e^{h/2}

(2)

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on that space. Whenever possible, we will treat these three cases in parallel and write
\xi
in what follows for one of the Poisson processes
\eta_{\beta}
,
\eta_{\beta}^{\prime}
or
\zeta
.

We shall now briefly describe the two different ways to construct the
\beta^{(^{\prime})}
- and Gaussian Delaunay tessellation. For the first approach, let
\Pi_{\pm}
be the standard upward (
+
) or downward (
-
) paraboloid
\Pi_{\pm}:=\{(v,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}:h=\pm\|v\|^{2}\}
and
\Pi_{\pm,x}
be the shifted standard paraboloid with apex at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
we let
A^{\uparrow}:=\{(v,h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}\colon(v,h)\in A\text{ for some }h\leq h^{\prime}\}
and
A^{\downarrow}:=\{(v,h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}\colon(v,h)\in A\text{ for some }h\geq h^{\prime}\}
. The paraboloid growth process
\Psi(\xi)
based on
\xi
is now defined as

\Psi(\xi):=\bigcup_{x\in\xi}\Pi_{+,x}^{\uparrow}.

In other words,
\Psi(\xi)
is the Boolean model with paraboloid grains based on
\xi
. A point
x\in\xi
is called extreme in
\Psi(\xi)
if and only if its associated paraboloid is not fully covered by the paraboloids associated with other points of
\xi
. The collection of such extreme points is denoted by
\operatorname{ext}(\Psi(\xi))
.

Next, we introduce the paraboloid hull process, which can be regarded as dual to the paraboloid growth process. For any collection
x_{1}:=(v_{1},h_{1}),\ldots,x_{k}:=(v_{k},h_{k})
of
1\leq k\leq d
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
with affinely independent spatial coordinates
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, we define
\Pi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
to be the intersection of
\operatorname{aff}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\times\mathbb{R}
with any translate of
\Pi_{-}
containing all the points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
(although such translates of
\Pi_{-}
are not unique for
k<d
, their intersections with
\operatorname{aff}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\times\mathbb{R}
all coincide so that
\Pi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
is well defined). Further, we define the parabolic face
\Pi[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
as

\Pi[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]:=\Pi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\cap\left(\conv(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\times\mathbb{R}\right).

The paraboloid hull
\Phi(\xi)
of
\xi
is given by

\Phi(\xi)=\bigcup\limits_{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\xi_{\neq}^{d}}\left(\Pi[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]\right)^{\uparrow},

where
\xi_{\neq}^{d}
is the collection of all
d
-tuples of distinct points of
\xi
. In this context, the points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
are called vertices of the paraboloid face
\Pi[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
and we let
{\rm vert}(\Phi(\xi))
be the collection of vertices of all such paraboloid faces. The link between
\Psi(\xi)
and
\Phi(\xi)
arises from the fact that
\operatorname{ext}(\Psi(\xi))={\rm vert}(\Phi(\xi))
.

Based on the paraboloid hull processes
\Phi(\xi)
we are now in the position to define a random tessellation of
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
as follows. For any paraboloid face of
\Phi(\xi)
with vertices
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in\xi
we consider a polytope
\conv(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})
and let
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}(\xi)
be a collection of all such polytopes. With probability one
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}(\xi)
is a stationary random tessellation of
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
only having simplicial cells. In this case the construction above can be alternatively described as follows: for any collection
x_{1}=(v_{1},h_{1}),\ldots,x_{d}=(v_{d},h_{d})
of pairwise distinct points from
\xi
we say that the simplex
\conv(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})
belongs to
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}(\xi)
if and only if
\operatorname{int}\Pi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\cap\eta_{\beta}=\emptyset
.

Another alternative description of
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}(\xi)
can be made in terms of the dual of a Laguerre tessellation, as already mentioned in the introduction. To formally introduce this approach, for a point
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
and
(v,h)\in\xi
define the power function
\operatorname{pow}(w,(v,h)):=\|v-w\|^{2}+h

and the Laguerre cell

C((v,h),\xi):=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\colon\operatorname{pow}(w,(v,h))\leq\operatorname{pow}(w,(v^{\prime},h^{\prime}))\text{ for all }(v^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\xi\}.

We emphasize that it is not necessarily the case that a Laguerre cell is non-empty or that it contains interior points. The collection of all Laguerre cells of
\xi
with non-vanishing interior is called the Laguerre tessellation of
\xi
and we write

\mathcal{L}(\xi):=\{C((v,h),\xi)\colon(v,h)\in\xi,\;{\rm int}(C((v,h),\xi))\neq\varnothing\}.

Let
\mathcal{L}^{*}(\xi)
be the dual tessellation of
\mathcal{L}(\xi)
. This tessellation arises from
\mathcal{L}(\xi)
by including for distinct points
x_{1}=(v_{1},h_{1}),\ldots,x_{d}=(v_{d},h_{d})
of
\xi
the simplex
\conv(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})
in
\mathcal{L}^{*}(\xi)
if and only if the Laguerre cells corresponding to
(v_{1},h_{1}),\ldots,(v_{d},h_{d})
all have non-empty interior and share a common point. In our case
\mathcal{L}^{*}(\xi)
is almost surely a stationary random simplicial tessellation and coincides with
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}(\xi)
as introduced earlier.

Finally, we introduce the notation
\operatorname{skel}(\mathcal{L}^{*}(\xi))=\operatorname{skel}(\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}(\xi))
for the random set in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
arising from the union of all cell boundaries of cells from
\mathcal{L}^{*}(\xi)
or
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}(\xi)
.

3
Mixing concepts for stationary random closed sets

In this section we recall the definition of various concepts of mixing properties for stationary random closed sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. To this end we denote by
\mathcal{F}^{d-1}:=\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})
the set of closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
and by
\mathfrak{F}^{d-1}:=\mathfrak{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})
the Borel
\sigma
-field on
\mathcal{F}^{d-1}
generated by the usual Fell topology, see
[
16
, Chapter 2]
. For
\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{F}^{d-1}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
we define
T_{v}A:=\{a+v:a\in A\}
,
A\in\mathcal{A}
, and put
T_{v}\mathcal{A}:=\{T_{v}A:A\in\mathcal{A}\}
. From now on
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
will denote a stationary random closed set. We call
\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{F}^{d-1}
invariant if
\mathbb{P}(\{Z\in\mathcal{A}\}\Delta\{Z\in T_{v}\mathcal{A}\})=0
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, where
\Delta
denotes the symmetric difference. The stationary random closed set
Z
is called
ergodic
if
\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A})\in\{0,1\}
for any
\mathcal{A}
from the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the class of invariant sets. This is known to be equivalent to require that

\lim_{n\to\infty}{1\over(2n)^{d-1}}\int_{[-n,n]^{d-1}}\mathbb{P}(\{Z\in\mathcal{A}_{0}\}\cap\{Z\in T_{v}\mathcal{A}_{1}\})\,\mathrm{d}v=\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A}_{0})\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A}_{1})

for all
\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{A}_{1}\in\mathfrak{F}^{d-1}
. The random closed set
Z
is called (ergodic)
mixing
if

\lim_{\|v\|\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\{Z\in\mathcal{A}_{0}\}\cap\{Z\in T_{v}\mathcal{A}_{1}\})=\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A}_{0})\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A}_{1})

for all
\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{A}_{1}\in\mathfrak{F}^{d-1}
, where
\|v\|\to\infty
indicates that
v
runs through an arbitrary sequence
(v_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
satisfying
\|v_{n}\|\to\infty
, as
n\to\infty
. More generally, for integers
\ell\geq 1
one calls
Z
(ergodic)
mixing of order
\ell
if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\{Z\in\mathcal{A}_{0}\}\cap\{Z\in T_{v_{n}^{(1)}}\mathcal{A}_{1}\}\cap\ldots\cap\{Z\in T_{v_{n}^{(\ell)}}\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\})=\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A}_{0})\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A}_{1})\cdots\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell})

for all
\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\in\mathfrak{F}^{d-1}
, where the limit runs through arbitrary sequences
(v_{n}^{(i)})_{n\geq 1}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
, satisfying
\|v_{n}^{(i)}\|\to\infty
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
and
\|v_{n}^{(i)}-v_{n}^{(j)}\|\to\infty
for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
with
i\neq j
, as
n\to\infty
. Note that saying that
Z
is mixing of order
1
is the same as that
Z
is just mixing.

Next, we define for
n\geq 1
the following sub-
\sigma
-algebras of
\mathfrak{F}^{d-1}
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{n}^{d-1}

\displaystyle:=\sigma\{\{F\in\mathcal{F}^{d-1}:F\cap C=\varnothing\}:C\subset B_{n}\text{ compact}\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{-n}^{d-1}

\displaystyle:=\sigma\{\{F\in\mathcal{F}^{d-1}:F\cap C=\varnothing\}:C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\setminus B_{n}\text{ compact}\},

as well as the so-called
tail
\sigma
-algebra

\mathfrak{F}_{-\infty}^{d-1}:=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{F}_{-n}.

The stationary random closet set
Z
is called
tail-trivial
, provided that
\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A})\in\{0,1\}
for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{F}_{-\infty}^{d-1}
.

There are also two stronger types of mixing conditions we consider here, namely
\alpha
-mixing and absolute regularity (also known as
\beta
-mixing, but we do not follow this notion here to avoid confusion with our model parameter
\beta
). Given
0<a<b
we define the two functions

\mathscr{A}(a,b):=\sup\limits_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{F}_{a}^{d-1}\atop\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{F}_{-b}^{d-1}}|\mathbb{P}(\{Z\in\mathcal{A}\}\cap\{Z\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\})-\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A})\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime})|,

and

\mathscr{B}(a,b):={1\over 2}\sup\sum\limits_{i=1}^{I}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}|\mathbb{P}(\{Z\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\}\cap\{Z\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{j}\})-\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A}_{i})\mathbb{P}(Z\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{j})|,

where the supremum in the definition of
\mathscr{B}(a,b)
is taken over all pairs of finite partitions of
\mathcal{F}^{d-1}
:
\bar{\mathcal{A}}:=\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\in\mathfrak{F}_{a}^{d-1}\colon i=1,\ldots,I\}
,
\bar{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}:=\{\mathcal{A}_{j}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{F}_{-b}^{d-1}\colon j=1,\ldots,J\}
. The stationary random closed set
Z
is called
\alpha
-mixing
if for any
a>0
,
\lim\limits_{b\to\infty}\mathscr{A}(a,b)=0
and
Z
is called
absolutely regular
if for any
a>0
,
\lim\limits_{b\to\infty}\mathscr{B}(a,b)=0
.

The next lemma describes the relations between these different concepts.

Lemma 1
.

Let
Z
be a stationary random closet set in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.

(i)

If
Z
is mixing then
Z
is ergodic.

(ii)

If
Z
is mixing of some order
\ell\geq 1
then
Z
is mixing.

(iii)

If
Z
is tail-trivial then
Z
is mixing of any order
\ell\geq 1
.

(iv)

If
Z
is
\alpha
-mixing then
Z
is tail-trivial.

(v)

If
Z
is absolutely regular then
Z
is
\alpha
-mixing.

Proof.

That the mixing property of
Z
implies ergodicity is well known , see
[
16
, p. 408]
. Also, from the definition it directly follows that if
Z
is mixing of some order
\ell\geq 1
then
Z
is just mixing. Next, we need to prove that if
Z
is tail-trivial then
Z
is mixing of any order
\ell\geq 1
. For stationary random measures this is the content of
[
10
, Theorem 6.3.6]
and the proof carries over literally to stationary random closed sets (see also
[
4
, Exercise 12.3.7]
). That
\alpha
-mixing implies tail-triviality can be found in
[
1
, Section 2.5, p. 116]
. The final assertion is a consequence of the inequality
\mathscr{A}(a,b)\leq{1\over 2}\mathscr{B}(a,b)
for which we refer to
[
1
, Equation (1.11)]
.
∎

In particular, the last lemma shows that once absolute regularity of a stationary random closed set is established, all other (weaker) mixing notions follow automatically. This is the reason why from now on we focus on absolute regularity only. In order to establish the absolute regularity property we will rely on the following simple lemma we took from
[
12
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
0<a<b
and
\varepsilon>0
. If for some
\mathcal{B}\in\mathfrak{F}_{a}^{d-1}
it holds that for any
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{F}^{d-1}_{-b}
and for any
\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{F}^{d-1}_{a}
with
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
,

6
Central limit theorems

The bounds on the absolute regularity coefficients established in the previous section can be used to deduce central limit theorems for combinatorial and geometric parameters of
\beta
- and Gaussian Delaunay tessellations; for the
\beta^{\prime}
-Delaunay tessellation see Remark
11
below. We start with the number of
k
-dimensional faces. To be formal, let
c(\,\cdot\,)
be a so-called centre function which associates with a (potentially lower-dimensional) simplex
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
a point
c(S)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, the centre of
S
, in such a way that
c(S+v)=c(S)
for all

. There are many choices for such centre functions, for example
c(S)
could be the centre of gravity of
S
or the centre of the smallest ball containing
S
. For us it will turn out to be convenient to take for
c(S)
the lexicographically smallest vertex of
S
. In what follows we understand
c(S)
in exactly this way. Now, for a stationary random tessellation
\mathcal{T}
(identified with its skeleton, that is, the random closed set generated by the union of all cell boundaries) in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
denote by

the stationary point process formed by all centres (that is, lexicographically smallest vertices) of
k
-dimensional faces of
\mathcal{T}
, which are almost surely simplices of dimension
k
. In particular, for sets
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
,
X_{\mathcal{T},0}(B)
counts the number of vertices of
\mathcal{T}
in
B
,
X_{\mathcal{T},1}(B)
is the number of lexicographically smallest edge endpoints in
B
of edges of
\mathcal{T}
and
X_{\mathcal{T},d-1}(B)
is the same as the number of cells of
\mathcal{T}
whose lexicographically smallest vertex is located in
B
.

In what follows we shall use the notation
I_{n}:=[-n,n]^{d-1}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\lambda_{\mathcal{T},k}:=\mathbb{E}X_{\mathcal{T},k}([0,1]^{d-1})
be the intensity of the stationary point process

. We note that
\mathbb{E}X_{\mathcal{T},k}(I_{n})=(2n)^{d-1}\lambda_{\mathcal{T},k}
. For the
\beta
- (and
\beta^{\prime}
-) Delaunay tessellation the explicit values of
\lambda_{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\beta},k}
(and
\lambda_{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\beta}^{\prime},k}
) were determined in
[
5
, Theorem 6.4]
and for the Gaussian-Delaunay tessellation see
[
6
, Corollary 5.9]
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be one of the tessellations

for

or

(19)
