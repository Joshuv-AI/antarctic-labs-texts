Fermi-Dirac Integrals in Degenerate Regimes: A Novel Asymptotic Expansion
Jeremiah Birrell, Martin Formanek, Andrew Steinmetz, Cheng Tao Yang, Johann Rafelski

Abstract
We characterize in a novel manner the physical properties of the low temperature Fermi gas in the degenerate domain as a function of temperature and chemical potential. For the first time we obtain low temperature T T results in the domain where several fermions are found within a de Broglie spatial cell. In this regime, the usual high degeneracy Sommerfeld expansion fails. The other known semi-classical Boltzmann domain applies when fewer than one particle is found in the de Broglie cell. We also improve on the understanding of the Sommerfeld expansion in the regime where the chemical potential is close to the mass and also in the high temperature regime. In these calculcations we use a novel characterization of the Fermi distribution allowing the separation of the finite and zero temperature phenomena. The relative errors of the three approximate methods (Boltzmann limit, Sommerfeld expansion, and the new domain of several particles in the de Broglie cell) are quantified.

When investigating the low temperature limit of a Fermi gas, a systematic approach to approximating the physical integral involves employs the Sommerfeld expansion. It is worth noting that the Sommerfeld expansion, as described in Ref.

After several in-depth reviews of literature, we conclude that a full understanding of the domain of validity of the Sommerfeld expansion in the
(T,\Delta\mu)
-plane

\Delta\mu\equiv\mu-m\,,

(2)

is lacking. The Sommerfeld expansion fails for regimes where the corrections to the
T\rightarrow 0
limit cannot be taken to be small. This makes it difficult to search for alternative expansions that are valid in other regions of the
(T,\Delta\mu)
-plane when dealing with multiple scales. One generally requires specialized asymptotic expansions that are adapted to specific regimes under consideration. This is particularly relevant when
\Delta\mu
is small, a regime that the traditional Sommerfeld expansion was not designed for and which we consider here. Specifically, expanding the Sommerfeld result around the small chemical potential may lead to encountering singularities which underscore the necessity for a better tool to study this scenario.

In this work we explore the different domains of low temperature FD gas, characterize the domain of validity of Sommerfeld expansion in physical terms, and obtain a novel approximation applicable to domains of statistical parameters where the “expansion fails”. As a side-line result, we also provide a novel form of FD distribution that manifestly separates the zero and finite temperature contributions so that the finite temperature correction can be studied on its own in more complex environments than considered in our current method-oriented presentation.

Our own interest and motivations to explore FD systems originated in cosmological computations involving both high and low temperature physics, such as comic neutrino background

2
Sommerfeld-Type Asymptotic Characterization of Thermal Properties

2.1
A new FD distribution decomposition

In this section we will explore the Sommerfeld-type asymptotic expansions of thermal averages in various physics-relevant chemical potential and temperature regimes. As a convenient tool, we will employ the following novel way to write FD distribution in the zero temperature limit
T\to 0
.

At
T=0
the FD distribution reduces to a step function where a state
E_{i}
is either filled or empty. For given chemical potential
\mu(T)
, we have

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{FD}}[E_{i},\mu(T),T]=\frac{1}{\displaystyle e^{\left(\frac{E_{i}-\mu}{T}\right)}+1}\,,\qquad\lim_{T\to 0}f_{\mathrm{FD}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}1,\quad\mathrm{for}\quad{E_{i}}<{E_{F}}\\
0,\quad\mathrm{for}\quad{E_{i}}>{E_{F}}\end{array}\right.\,.

At
T=0
the energy of the last filled state is the Fermi energy
{E_{\mathrm{F}}=\mu(T\to 0)}
, while at finite temperature one speaks of chemical potential instead.

For the calculations in this section we find it useful to employ a novel reformulation which allows exact separation of the finite temperature behavior from the singular zero temperature limit, replacing the smooth FD distribution by the sum of three singular functions,

\frac{1}{e^{x}+1}=\Theta(-x)+\frac{1}{2}e^{-|x|}\left[\sgn(x)+\tanh(x/2)\right]\,,

(5)

the first term (Heaviside function) being the
T=0
limit and the other two describing the finite temperature residual.

In this form the zero temperature part is explicitly separated from the finite temperature contributions.

Lemma 1
.

The Fermi-Dirac distribution can be decomposed into three components:

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{FD}}(x)=\Theta(-x)+f_{\mathrm{T\neq 0}}(x)+\widetilde{f}_{\mathrm{T\neq 0}}(x)

(6)

where the temperature functions are defined as

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{T\neq 0}}=\frac{1}{2}e^{-|x|}\mathrm{sgn}\left(x\right),\qquad\widetilde{f}_{\mathrm{T\neq 0}}=\frac{1}{2}e^{-|x|}\tanh\left(\frac{x}{2}\right),\qquad x=\frac{E-\mu}{T}

(7)

and

\displaystyle\Theta(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r}1,\quad\mathrm{for}\quad{x}>0\\
0,\quad\mathrm{for}\quad{x}<0\end{array}\right.\,,\qquad\sgn(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r}+1,\quad\mathrm{for}\quad{x}>0\\
-1,\quad\mathrm{for}\quad{x}<0\\
\end{array}\right.\,,

where
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function and
\sgn(x)
is the sign function and the equality is in the sense of distributions. In particular the values at
x=0
are convention-dependent and do not affect our results.

Remark 1
.

To show the equivalency between the two distinct forms of the FD, we look at the three relevant regions of
x>0
, the origin
x=0
, and
x<0
. For
x>0
, Eq. (
6
) evaluates as

f_{\mathrm{FD}}(x>0)=0+\frac{1}{2}e^{-x}[1+\tanh(x/2)]=(e^{x}+1)^{-1}\,.

(12)

This can be seen by using the hyperbolic formula
\tanh(x/2)=(e^{x}-1)/(e^{x}+1)
. In a similar manner, the
x<0
region evaluates as

f_{\mathrm{FD}}(x<0)=1+\frac{1}{2}e^{x}[-1+\tanh(x/2)]=(e^{x}+1)^{-1}\,.

(13)

As we are considering the expressions in the sense of distributions, this completes the proof. The values at
x=0
are convention-dependent and irrelevant for our purposes, as the point
x=0
is a set of measure zero with respect to integration.

the solid line shows the FD distribution as a function of energy for chemical potential

, and electron mass
m=0.511\text{ MeV}
, in the top frame for temperature

, and in bottom frame for

limit. As the temperature increases, the distribution becomes broader as a wide range of states become thermally populated. As

decreases the
f_{\mathrm{T\neq 0}}
(blue dotted) and
\widetilde{f}_{\mathrm{T\neq 0}}
(red dash-dotted) components, see Eq. (

), which characterize finite
T
contributions become progressively more singular.

To illustrate how this novel form of distribution can be used to address the integrals common in statistical physics especially when temperature
T\to 0
, we examine the thermal average of any given function
G(p)
with smoothness and polynomial growth bounds as
p\to\infty
. The thermal average physical quantity, denoted as
\langle G\rangle_{T}
, is determined by integrating over the D-dimensional phase space

\displaystyle\langle G\rangle_{T}

\displaystyle\equiv\int^{\infty}_{0}\!\!\frac{d^{D}p}{(2\pi)^{D}}\,G(p)\,f_{FD}(p)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D}}\frac{2\pi^{D/2}}{\Gamma(D/2)}\int^{\infty}_{0}\!\!dp\,p^{D-1}\,G(p)\,f_{FD}(p).

(14)

By substituting our novel form of FD distribution, the physical quantity
\langle G\rangle_{T}
can be expressed as the sum of two distinct components:

\displaystyle\langle G\rangle_{T}=\langle G\rangle_{\Theta}+\langle G\rangle_{\Delta T}

(15)

where the
\langle G\rangle_{\Theta}
corresponds to the zero temperature limit and
\langle G\rangle_{\Delta T}
represents the finite temperature contribution. We have

\displaystyle\langle G\rangle_{\Theta}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D}}\frac{2\pi^{D/2}}{\Gamma(D/2)}\int^{\infty}_{0}\!\!dp\,p^{D-1}G(p)\Theta\left(\frac{-E+\mu}{T}\right),\qquad E=\sqrt{p^{2}+m^{2}},

(16)

\displaystyle\langle G\rangle_{\Delta T}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D}}\frac{2\pi^{D/2}}{\Gamma(D/2)}\int^{\infty}_{0}\!\!dp\,p^{D-1}\,G(p)\,\left[f_{\mathrm{T\neq 0}}(p)+\widetilde{f}_{\mathrm{T\neq 0}}(p)\right].

(17)

By employing our FD decomposition, in the following sections we obtain asymptotic expansions of the finite temperature contribution
\langle G\rangle_{\Delta T}
in different physical regimes of interest. Our novel decomposition guides the asymptotic analysis of
\langle G\rangle_{\Delta T}
by naturally reorganizing the integrand into a difference of integrals with exponentially decaying integrands. This allows us to utilize asymptotic analysis techniques to extract leading and subleading order behavior as
T\to 0
and to analytically account for the exact cancellation of certain terms, which otherwise cause problems in numerical computations.

2.2
Traditional Sommerfeld Regime

We now show that the decomposition Eq. (
6
) naturally motivates certain steps in the derivation of the traditional Sommerfeld expansion, see, e.g., Eq. (58.1) on page 170 of
Landau and
Lifshitz (1980)
, an asymptotic expansion for the difference between a
D
-dimensional thermal average at finite and zero temperature as
T\to 0
in the regime where
\mu>m
. In this derivation we will be particularly attentive to the location of singularities as well as precisely bounding error terms in the asymptotic expansion. While these details do not play a critical role in obtaining the correct form of the traditional Sommerfeld expansion, they will be important in our new results later in this work and so we wish to review these methods in a more familiar setting first.

Here we will assume that
\mu>m
,
k>1
is odd, and the density of states
H(E)
is polynomially bounded on
[m,\infty)
and is
C^{k}
on a neighborhood of
\mu
. We will obtain an asymptotic expansion of

\displaystyle I\equiv\int_{m}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)f_{FD}(E)

(18)

as
T\to 0
. First we note that, starting from
G(p)
in Eq. (
17
), the corresponding
H(E)
is given by

\displaystyle H(E)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D}}\frac{2\pi^{D/2}}{\Gamma(D/2)}E(E^{2}-m^{2})^{(D-2)/2}G(\sqrt{E^{2}-m^{2}})

(19)

and this
H
has the required properties if
D\geq 2
and
G
is polynomially bounded on
[0,\infty)
and is
C^{k}
on a neighborhood of
\sqrt{\mu^{2}-m^{2}}
(recall that we are assuming
\mu>m
, which allows us to avoid the singularity at
E=m
).

We start by using the decomposition Eq. (
6
) to break the integral into zero and nonzero temperature contributions

\displaystyle I=\int_{m}^{\mu}dE\,H(E)+\int_{m}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)\left[f_{T\neq 0}(E)+\widetilde{f}_{T\neq 0}(E)\right]\,.

(20)

Focusing on the non-zero temperature contribution in Eq. (
20
), we break the second integral into two terms as motivated by the discontinuity of Eq. (
7
) at
E=\mu
and then simplify the integrands using Eq. (
12
) and Eq. (
13
) as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)\left[f_{T\neq 0}(E)+\widetilde{f}_{T\neq 0}(E)\right]

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mu}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)\left[f_{T\neq 0}(E)+\widetilde{f}_{T\neq 0}(E)\right]+\int_{m}^{\mu}dE\,H(E)\left[f_{T\neq 0}(E)+\widetilde{f}_{T\neq 0}(E)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mu}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)\frac{1}{1+e^{(E-\mu)/T}}-\int_{m}^{\mu}dE\,H(E)\frac{1}{1+e^{-(E-\mu)/T}}\,.

(22)

The exponentially decaying factors in the above integrands imply that reducing the domains of integration to the intervals
[\mu,\mu+\delta]
and
[\mu-\delta,\mu]
respectively for any choice of
\delta>0
incurs an
\mathcal{O}(e^{-c/T})
error term for some
c>0
; the assumptions on
H
imply we can choose
\delta
so that
H
is
C^{k}
on
[\mu-\delta,\mu+\delta]
. To see that the error does in fact have the claimed behavior, use the polynomial boundedness
|H(E)|\leq CE^{q}
for some
C,q>0
and change variables to
z=(E-\mu)/T
to compute

\begin{split}&\left|\int_{\mu+\delta}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)\frac{1}{1+e^{(E-\mu)/T}}\right|\\
&\leq C\int_{\mu+\delta}^{\infty}dE\,E^{q}e^{-(E-\mu)/T}=CT\int_{\delta/T}^{\infty}dz\,(Tz+\mu)^{q}e^{-z}\\
&\leq 2^{q}CT\left(T^{q}\int_{\delta/T}^{\infty}dz\,z^{q}e^{-z}+\mu^{q}\int_{\delta/T}^{\infty}dz\,e^{-z}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-c/T}\right)\end{split}

(23)

for some
c>0
. The second integral in Eq. (
22
) is handled similarly. The
\mathcal{O}(e^{-c/T})
error is dominated by
\mathcal{O}(T^{r})
for any
r
and so the exact value of
c
will be seen to be irrelevant for our purposes.

The previous analysis then implies

\displaystyle Eq.\,(\ref{eq:traditional_sommerfeld_2_integral})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mu}^{\mu+\delta}\!dE\,H(E)\frac{1}{1+e^{(E-\mu)/T}}-\int_{\mu-\delta}^{\mu}\!dE\,H(E)\frac{1}{1+e^{-(E-\mu)/T}}+\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-c/T}\right)\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T\int_{0}^{\delta/T}dz\,\left[H(\mu+Tz)-H(\mu-Tz)\right]\frac{1}{1+e^{z}}+\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-c/T}\right)\,,

(24)

where to obtain the last line we changed variables to
z=(E-\mu)/T
in the first integral and
z=-(E-\mu)/T
in the second. The domain of integration is now such that
H
is
C^{k}
at
\mu\pm Tz
for all
z\in[0,\delta/T]
and therefore we can compute a Taylor expansion up to order
k
with remainder,

\displaystyle H(\mu+Tz)-H(\mu-Tz)=\sum_{n=1,n\text{ odd}}^{k-2}2H^{(n)}(\mu)\frac{(Tz)^{n}}{n!}+R_{k}(z)\,,

(25)

\displaystyle R_{k}(z)\equiv(Tz)^{k}\int_{0}^{1}ds\,\frac{(1-s)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\left[H^{(k)}(\mu+sTz)+H^{(k)}(\mu-sTz)\right]\,,

(26)

and
|H^{(k)}(\mu\pm sTz)|\leq C_{k}\equiv\sup_{y\in[\mu-\delta,\mu+\delta]}|H^{(k)}(y)|<\infty
for all
z\in[0,\delta/T]
,
s\in[0,1]
(it is finite due to continuity of
H^{(k)}
on the the compact interval
[\mu-\delta,\mu+\delta]
). Therefore the integral of the remainder term can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\left|T\int_{0}^{\delta/T}R_{k}(z)\frac{1}{1+e^{z}}\right|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2C_{k}}{k!}T^{k+1}\int_{0}^{\delta/T}dz\,z^{k}e^{-z}=\mathcal{O}\left(T^{k+1}\right)\,.

(27)

Combining this with Eq. (
24
) we obtain

\displaystyle Eq.\,(\ref{eq:traditional_sommerfeld_2_integral})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1,n\text{ odd}}^{k-2}2\frac{T^{n+1}}{n!}H^{(n)}(\mu)\int_{0}^{\delta/T}dz\,\frac{z^{n}}{1+e^{z}}+\mathcal{O}\left(T^{k+1}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-c/T}\right)\,.

(28)

Similarly to the case Eq. (
23
), the exponential decay factor in the integrand implies that we can change the domains of integration to
[0,\infty)
at the cost of an
\mathcal{O}(e^{-c/T})
error term. Using this and then the integral formula

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\nu-1}}{e^{x}+1}dx=\begin{cases}\ln(2)\,,&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\nu=1\\
(1-2^{1-\nu})\Gamma(\nu)\zeta(\nu)\,,&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\nu>1\end{cases}

(29)

(see 3.411.3 on page 353 of
Gradshteyn and
Ryzhik (2007)
) we obtain

\displaystyle Eq.\,(\ref{eq:traditional_sommerfeld_2_integral})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1,n\text{ odd}}^{k-2}2(1-2^{-n})\zeta(n+1)T^{n+1}H^{(n)}(\mu)+\mathcal{O}\left(T^{k+1}\right)\,.

(30)

Substituting this into Eq. (
18
) we obtain the traditional Sommerfeld expansion

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)f_{FD}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{\mu}dE\,H(E)+\sum_{n=1,n\text{ odd}}^{k-2}2(1-2^{-n})\zeta(n+1)T^{n+1}H^{(n)}(\mu)+\mathcal{O}(T^{k+1})\,.

(31)

We emphasize that the expansion Eq. (
31
) considers
\mu
to be fixed and so the implied constant in the
\mathcal{O}(T^{k+1})
error term has nontrivial
\mu
-dependence. This fact causes the failure of the traditional Sommerfeld expansion in certain cases where one is considering simultaneous
\mu
and
T
limits. This is not simply a mathematical consideration, as simultaneous limits are required to maintain appropriate physical parameters as
T\to 0
, e.g., the moderate density regime shown in Figure
1
. In the remainder of this work we derive several novel asymptotic expansions of FD integrals by precisely considering different
\mu
,
T
limits. The expansions in Sections
2.3
and
2.4
can still be considered to be of Sommerfeld type, while the regime considered in Section
3
leads to a wholly novel expansion.

2.3
Sommerfeld Expansion in the Nonrelativistic Degenerate Fermi Gas Regime
T\ll\mu-m\ll m

In this section we use the decomposition Eq. (
6
) to further investigate the Sommerfeld expansion in the limit where
T\ll\mu-m\ll m
. While in essence the traditional Sommerfeld expansion applies to this regime, a naive use of Eq. (
31
) often results in one encountering numerical difficulties due to singular behavior of various coefficients in the limit
\mu\to m
. In this section we derive a formula that precisely accounts for these singularities; this formula makes it straightforward to compute the coefficients in the expansion up to any desired order via a computer algebra system.

More specifically, in this section we will assume
D\geq 2
and

\displaystyle\mu=m+\Delta\mu\,,\,\Delta\mu>0\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\widetilde{T}=d\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{1+\gamma}\,,\,\,d,\gamma>0\,,

(32)

where
\widetilde{T}\equiv T/m
and
\Delta\widetilde{\mu}\equiv\Delta\mu/m
; these scalings imply that
T\ll\mu-m\ll m
as
\Delta\widetilde{\mu}\to 0
, matching the stated goal here. We let
G(p)
,
p\in[0,\infty)
, be a
C^{k}
function whose zeroth through
k
’th derivatives are polynomially bounded and will study
\langle G\rangle_{\Delta T}
in the limit
\Delta\widetilde{\mu}\to 0^{+}
. The final result is found in Theorem
2
below. To proceed with the derivation, first change variables in Eq. (
17
) to
E=\sqrt{p^{2}+m^{2}}
and then use Eq. (
12
) and Eq. (
13
):

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,p^{D-1}G(p)(f_{T\neq 0}+\widetilde{f}_{T\neq 0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{\infty}dE\,E(E^{2}-m^{2})^{(D-2)/2}G\left(\sqrt{E^{2}-m^{2}}\right)(f_{T\neq 0}+\widetilde{f}_{T\neq 0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mu}^{\infty}dE\,E(E^{2}-m^{2})^{(D-2)/2}G\left(\sqrt{E^{2}-m^{2}}\right)\frac{1}{1+e^{(E-\mu)/T}}

(33)

\displaystyle-\int_{m}^{\mu}dE\,E(E^{2}-m^{2})^{(D-2)/2}G\left(\sqrt{E^{2}-m^{2}}\right)\frac{1}{1+e^{(\mu-E)/T}}\,.

Next consider the first integral in Eq. (
33
). In general, its integrand will have a singularity at
E=m
. As the lower limit of integration approaches
m
in the regime under consideration, it is necessary to carefully resolve that singularity. We have chosen to begin with the integral expressed in terms of
p
because it simplifies this analysis, due to
E=\sqrt{p^{2}+m^{2}}
being is a smooth function of
p
but not vice versa. Thus if one begins with a smooth density of states
H(E)
, the corresponding
G(p)
will also be smooth but the reverse is not true. We note that if one is interested in
G(p)
that is not smooth at
p=0
then the following derivation can be easily modified to account for a power-law singularity of
G
at
0
.

To derive an expansion that correctly handles the singular behavior of the integrands in Eq. (
33
) at
E=m
, we first change variables to
z=(E-m)/m
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mu}^{\infty}dE\,E(E^{2}-m^{2})^{(D-2)/2}G\left(\sqrt{E^{2}-m^{2}}\right)\frac{1}{1+e^{(E-\mu)/T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{D}\int_{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}}^{\infty}dz\,(z+1)[(z+1)^{2}-1]^{(D-2)/2}G\left(m\sqrt{(z+1)^{2}-1}\right)\frac{1}{1+e^{(z-\Delta\widetilde{\mu})/\widetilde{T}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{D}\int_{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}}^{\infty}dz\,(z+1)(z+2)^{(D-2)/2}G\left(m\sqrt{z}\sqrt{z+2}\right)\frac{z^{(D-2)/2}}{1+e^{(z-\Delta\widetilde{\mu})/\widetilde{T}}}\,.

(34)

We have separated out the (possible) singularity coming from
z^{(D-2)/2}
but there still remains a potential singularity at
z=0
coming from the argument of
G
. The term involving
G
does not always have a Taylor expansion, but it does have a more general asymptotic expansion which we can obtain as follows. Define

\displaystyle F(y,m)\equiv(y^{2}+1)(y^{2}+2)^{(D-2)/2}G\left(my\sqrt{y^{2}+2}\right)\,.

(35)

We have assumed that
G
is
C^{k}
on
[0,\infty)
with polynomially bounded zeroth through
k
’th derivatives and therefore
y\mapsto F(y,m)
also has these properties. Hence we can use Taylor’s theorem with remainder to write

\displaystyle F(y,m)=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}(m)y^{n}+R_{k}(y,m)\,,\,\,\,a_{n}(m)=\frac{1}{n!}\partial_{y}^{n}F(0,m)\,,

(36)

where the remainder term is given by

\displaystyle R_{k}(y,m)=y^{k}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-s)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\partial_{y}^{k}F(sy,m)ds

(37)

and can be bounded via

\displaystyle|R_{k}(y,m)|\leq y^{k}[\alpha_{k}(m)+\beta_{k}(m)y^{q_{k}}]

(38)

for some coefficients
\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}\geq 0
and power
q_{k}\geq 0
due to the polynomial boundedness of
\partial_{y}^{k}F
.

With this we have

\displaystyle Eq.\,(\ref{eq:T_faster_int_1_change_vars})=

\displaystyle m^{D}\!\int_{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}}^{\infty}\!dz\,F(\sqrt{z},m)\frac{z^{(D-2)/2}}{1+e^{(z-\Delta\widetilde{\mu})/\widetilde{T}}}

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}(m)m^{D}\!\!\int_{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}}^{\infty}\!dz\,\frac{z^{(n+D-2)/2}}{1+e^{(z-\Delta\widetilde{\mu})/\widetilde{T}}}+m^{D}\!\!\int_{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}}^{\infty}\!dz\,R_{k}(\sqrt{z},m)\frac{z^{(D-2)/2}}{1+e^{(z-\Delta\widetilde{\mu})/\widetilde{T}}}\,.

The integral of the remainder term can be bounded by using Eq. (
38
) as follows

\displaystyle\left|m^{D}\int_{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}}^{\infty}dz\,R_{k}(\sqrt{z},m)\frac{z^{(D-2)/2}}{1+e^{(z-\Delta\widetilde{\mu})/\widetilde{T}}}\right|

(40)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m^{D}\int_{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}}^{\infty}dz\,z^{k/2}[\alpha_{k}(m)+\beta_{k}(m)z^{q_{k}/2}]z^{(D-2)/2}e^{-(z-\Delta\widetilde{\mu})/\widetilde{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\alpha_{k}(m)m^{D}\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(k+D)/2+\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,(1+d\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{\gamma}x)^{(k+D-2)/2}e^{-x}

\displaystyle+d\beta_{k}(m)m^{D}\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(k+q_{k}+D)/2+\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,(1+d\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{\gamma}x)^{(k+q_{k}+D-2)/2}e^{-x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(k+D)/2+\gamma}\right)\quad\text{as}\quad\Delta\widetilde{\mu}\to 0^{+}\,,

(41)

where we changed variables to
x=(z-\Delta\widetilde{\mu})/\widetilde{T}
and used Eq. (
32
). Making this same change of variables in the remaining terms, we therefore obtain

\displaystyle Eq.\,(\ref{eq:T_faster_int_1_change_vars})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}da_{n}(m)m^{D}\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(n+D)/2+\gamma}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dx\,\frac{(1+d\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{\gamma}x)^{(n+D-2)/2}}{1+e^{x}}

(42)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(k+D)/2+\gamma}\right)\,.

These remaining integrals are smooth as functions of
d\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{\gamma}
and can be Taylor expanded to order
k-1
by differentiating under the integral, with remainder
\mathcal{R}_{k}(C,x)
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{(1+Cx)^{\nu}}{1+e^{x}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{j!}\left.\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial C^{j}}\right|_{C=0}\!\!\!\frac{(1+Cx)^{\nu}}{1+e^{x}}C^{j}+\mathcal{R}_{k}(C,x)\right)

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{j!}\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}(\nu-\ell)\right)C^{j}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\frac{x^{j}}{1+e^{x}}

\displaystyle+C^{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\frac{x^{k}}{1+e^{x}}\int_{0}^{1}ds\frac{(1-s)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}(\nu-\ell)\right)(1+sCx)^{\nu-k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln(2)+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{j!}\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}(\nu-\ell)\right)C^{j}(1-2^{-j})\Gamma(j+1)\zeta(j+1)+\mathcal{O}(C^{k})\,,

where we used the integral formula Eq. (
29
). Now we apply this to the terms in Eq. (
42
) with
C=d\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{\gamma}
and
\nu=(n+D-2)/2
; note that for each
n
we must expand Eq. (
43
) up to order
k_{n}\equiv\lceil(k-n)/(2\gamma)\rceil
so that the the remainder term in Eq. (
43
) is of the same or higher order than the remainder in Eq. (
41
) for each
n
. Doing this yields

\displaystyle Eq.\,(\ref{eq:T_faster_int_1_change_vars})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}da_{n}(m)m^{D}\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(n+D)/2+\gamma}\bigg[\ln(2)

(44)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}-1}d^{j}\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{j\gamma}\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}[(n+D-2)/2-\ell]\right)(1-2^{-j})\zeta(j+1)\bigg]

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(k+D)/2+\gamma}\right)\,.

This completes the asymptotic expansion of the first integral in Eq. (
33
).

Now consider the second integral in Eq. (
33
). Again we change variables to
z=(E-m)/m
and make use of Eq. (
35
) and Eq. (
36
):

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{\mu}dE\,E(E^{2}-m^{2})^{(D-2)/2}G\left(\sqrt{E^{2}-m^{2}}\right)\frac{1}{1+e^{(\mu-E)/T}}

(45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{D}\int_{0}^{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}}dz\,F(\sqrt{z},m)\frac{z^{(D-2)/2}}{1+e^{(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}-z)/\widetilde{T}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}(m)m^{D}\int_{0}^{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}}dz\,\frac{z^{(n+D-2)/2}}{1+e^{(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}-z)/\widetilde{T}}}+m^{D}\int_{0}^{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}}dz\,R_{k}(\sqrt{z},m)\frac{z^{(D-2)/2}}{1+e^{(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}-z)/\widetilde{T}}}\,.

We bound the integral of the remainder by again using Eq. (
38
)

\displaystyle\left|m^{D}\int_{0}^{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}}dz\,R_{k}(\sqrt{z},m)\frac{z^{(D-2)/2}}{1+e^{(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}-z)/\widetilde{T}}}\right|

(46)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m^{D}\int_{0}^{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}}dz\,\left[\alpha_{k}(m)+\beta_{k}(m)z^{q_{k}/2}\right]z^{(k+D-2)/2}e^{-(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}-z)/\widetilde{T}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m^{D}\widetilde{T}\left[\alpha_{k}(m)\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(k+D-2)/2}+\beta_{k}(m)\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(k+q_{k}+D-2)/2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(k+D)/2+\gamma}\right)\,.

Therefore

\displaystyle Eq.\,(\ref{eq:T_faster_int_2_change_vars})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}da_{n}(m)m^{D}\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(n+D)/2+\gamma}\int^{\Delta\widetilde{\mu}/\widetilde{T}}_{0}du\,\frac{(1-\widetilde{T}u/\Delta\widetilde{\mu})^{(n+D-2)/2}}{1+e^{u}}

(47)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(k+D)/2+\gamma})\,,

where we changed variables to
u=(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}-z)/\widetilde{T}
. The remaining integrals can be expanded using the following computation for
C>0
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1/C}du\,\frac{(1-Cu)^{\nu}}{1+e^{u}}=\int_{0}^{1/C}du\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{j!}\left.\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial C^{j}}\right|_{C=0}!\!\!\frac{(1-Cu)^{\nu}}{1+e^{u}}C^{j}+\mathcal{R}_{k}(C,u)\right)

(48)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{C^{j}}{j!}(-1)^{j}\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}(\nu-\ell)\right)\int_{0}^{1/C}du\,\frac{u^{j}}{1+e^{u}}

\displaystyle+C^{k}(-1)^{k}\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}(\nu-\ell)\right)\int_{0}^{1/C}du\frac{1}{1+e^{u}}u^{k}\int_{0}^{1}ds\,\frac{(1-s)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}(1-Csu)^{\nu-k}\,.

When
\nu\geq k
we have
(1-Csu)^{\nu-k}\leq 1
and so it is straightforward to see that the integral of the remainder
\mathcal{R}_{k}(C,u)
is
\mathcal{O}(C^{k})
as
C\to 0
. When
\nu<k
we have
(1-Csu)^{\nu-k}\leq(1-s)^{\nu-k}
and so the remainder can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\left|C^{k}(-1)^{k}\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}(\nu-\ell)\right)\int_{0}^{1/C}du\frac{1}{1+e^{u}}u^{k}\int_{0}^{1}ds\,\frac{(1-s)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}(1-Csu)^{\nu-k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{k}\left|\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}(\nu-\ell)\right)\right|\int_{0}^{1/C}du\frac{1}{1+e^{u}}u^{k}\int_{0}^{1}ds\,\frac{(1-s)^{\nu-1}}{(k-1)!}=\mathcal{O}(C^{k})

(49)

for all
\nu>0
. Therefore we have shown

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1/C}du\,\frac{(1-Cu)^{\nu}}{1+e^{u}}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{C^{j}}{j!}(-1)^{j}\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}(\nu-\ell)\right)\int_{0}^{1/C}du\,\frac{u^{j}}{1+e^{u}}+\mathcal{O}(C^{k})

(50)

(note that this trivially holds for
\nu=0
as well). Also note that the exponential decay of the integrand implies

\displaystyle\int_{1/C}^{\infty}du\,\frac{u^{j}}{1+e^{u}}=\mathcal{O}(C^{k})

(51)

for any
k
and hence we are free to change the upper limits of integration to
\infty
without changing the order of the error, thereby yielding:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1/C}du\,\frac{(1-Cu)^{\nu}}{1+e^{u}}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{C^{j}}{j!}(-1)^{j}\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}(\nu-\ell)\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\frac{u^{j}}{1+e^{u}}+\mathcal{O}(C^{k})\,.

(52)

For each
n
we use this expansion up to order
k_{n}=\lceil(k-n)/(2\gamma)\rceil
with
C\equiv\widetilde{T}/\Delta\widetilde{\mu}=d\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{\gamma}
and
\nu=(n+D-2)/2
with
n\geq 0
,
D\geq 2
, along with the integral formulas
Eq. (
29
). Substituting these into Eq. (
47
) gives

\displaystyle Eq.\,(\ref{eq:T_faster_int_2_change_vars})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}da_{n}(m)m^{D}\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(n+D)/2+\gamma}\bigg[\ln(2)

(53)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}-1}(-1)^{j}d^{j}\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{j\gamma}\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}[(n+D-2)/2-\ell]\right)(1-2^{-j})\zeta(j+1)\bigg]

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(k+D)/2+\gamma}\right)\,,

This completes the asymptotic expansion of the second integral in Eq. (
33
).

Finally, subtracting Eq. (
53
) from Eq. (
44
) and canceling the shared terms (in particular, they cancel at leading order) we obtain the following asymptotic expansion.

Theorem 2
.

Let
D\geq 2
,
\mu=m+\Delta\mu
,
\Delta\mu>0
and
\widetilde{T}=d\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{1+\gamma}

with

d,\gamma>0
. Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and suppose
G(p)
is a
C^{k}
function on
p\in[0,\infty)
whose zeroth through
k
’th derivatives are polynomially bounded. Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,p^{D-1}G(p)(f_{T\neq 0}+\widetilde{f}_{T\neq 0})

(54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}da_{n}(m)m^{D}\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(n+D)/2+\gamma}

(55)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{j=1,\mathrm{odd}}^{\lceil(k-n)/(2\gamma)\rceil-1}2d^{j}\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{j\gamma}\left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}[(n+D-2)/2-\ell]\right)(1-2^{-j})\zeta(j+1)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(k+D)/2+\gamma}\right)

as
\Delta\widetilde{\mu}\to 0^{+}
, where
\Delta\widetilde{\mu}=(\mu-m)/m
,
\widetilde{T}=T/m
, and

\displaystyle a_{n}(m)=\frac{1}{n!}\partial_{y}^{n}|_{y=0}\left[(y^{2}+1)(y^{2}+2)^{(D-2)/2}G\left(my\sqrt{y^{2}+2}\right)\right]\,.

(56)

We provide explicit formulas for the first few
a_{n}
’s below:

\displaystyle a_{0}(m)=2^{(D-2)/2}G(0)\,,

(57)

\displaystyle a_{1}(m)=2^{(D-1)/2}mG^{\prime}(0)\,,

\displaystyle a_{2}(m)=2^{(D-6)/2}\left[(D+2)G(0)+4m^{2}G^{\prime\prime}(0)\right]\,,

\displaystyle a_{3}(m)=2^{(D-5)/2}\left((D+3)mG^{\prime}(0)+\frac{4}{3}m^{3}G^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\right)\,,

where the primes denote derivatives of
G(p)
with respect to
p
. In dimension
D=3
the leading order term in Eq. (
55
) will be determined by the first
n
for which
a_{n}(m)\neq 0
, which results in a term that scales with
\Delta\widetilde{\mu}^{(n+3)/2+2\gamma}
. In Figure
5
we show a comparison between the expansion Eq. (
55
) and numerical integration of Eq. (
54
), demonstrating the effectiveness of the expansion as well as error scaling that matches the result Eq. (
55
).

Figure 5
:

\langle G\rangle_{\Delta T}
(left) using the expansion Eq. (
55
) for
\gamma=1/2
,
d=1
, and
G(p)=E
, with the result obtained via numerical integration of Eq. (
54
). The right plot shows the difference between the numerical result and the expansion Eq. (

) corresponding to
k=3
and
k=4
. The right plot shows the difference between the numerical result and the two expansions. On a log-log plot the slopes of the error can be measured to be approximately
4
(for
k=4
) and
7/2
(for
k=3
), correctly matching the order of the expansion error in
\Delta\widetilde{\mu}
as stated in Eq. (
55
).

2.4
Sommerfeld Expansion in the Relativistic, Dense Regime
\mu\gg T\gg m

Interestingly, there is also a high temperature regime (
\mu\gg T\gg m
) where the traditional Sommerfeld expansion provides a good approximation to thermal averages.

Theorem 3

(High-Temperature Sommerfeld Expansion)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
be odd and suppose that
H(E)
is continuous on
[m,\infty)
, is smooth on a neighborhood of
\infty
, and

\displaystyle H^{(k)}(E)=a_{k}E^{q_{k}}+\mathcal{O}(E^{q_{k}-1})

(58)

as
E\to\infty
for some
a_{k}\neq 0
,
q_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mu=m[1+B(T/m)^{1+\gamma}]
,
\gamma,B>0
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)f_{FD}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{\mu}dE\,H(E)+\sum_{n=1,n\text{ odd}}^{k-2}2(1-2^{-n})\zeta(n+1)T^{n+1}H^{(n)}(\mu)+\mathcal{O}(\mu^{q_{k}}T^{k+1})

(59)

as
T\to\infty
.

To prove Eq. (
59
), parameterize
\mu
as a function of
T
as follows

\displaystyle\mu=m[1+B(T/m)^{1+\gamma}]\,,\,\,\gamma,B>0\,.

(60)

and assume that the density of states
H(E)
has the properties stated in Theorem
3
.
By integrating Eq. (
58
) and combining it with the assumption that
H
is continuous we can conclude that
H
is also polynomially bounded on
[m,\infty)
. We note that, by definition, the asymptotic behavior Eq. (
58
) implies that there exists
E_{0}
and
C
such that
H
is smooth on
[E_{0},\infty)
and
|H^{(k)}(E)-a_{k}E^{q_{k}}|\leq CE^{q_{k}-1}
for all
E\in[E_{0},\infty)
. For the remainder of this derivation we assume that
T
is large enough to ensure
\mu\geq E_{0}
.

Now consider the nonzero temperature component of the decomposition Eq. (
6
) and use Eq. (
12
) and Eq. (
13
) to write

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)(f_{T\neq 0}+\widetilde{f}_{T\neq 0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mu}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)\frac{1}{1+e^{(E-\mu)/T}}-\int_{m}^{\mu}dE\,H(E)\frac{1}{1+e^{(\mu-E)/T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,H(\mu+Tz)\frac{1}{1+e^{z}}-T\int^{(\mu-m)/T}_{0}dz\,H(\mu-Tz)\frac{1}{1+e^{z}}\,,

(61)

where we changed variables to
z=(E-\mu)/T
in the first integral and
z=(\mu-E)/T
in the second. Due to the exponentially decaying factors in the integrands in Eq. (
61
) along with polynomial boundedness of
H
, we can shrink the upper limits of integration to
(\mu-E_{0})/T
, thereby ensuring that
\mu-Tz\geq E_{0}
on the domain of integration, with the error incurred by this change being
\mathcal{O}(T^{-r})
as
T\to\infty
for any choice of
r>0
. A similar bound was derived in the traditional Sommerfeld case, see Section
2.2
for details, though here this error term also accounts for the dependence on
\mu
in accordance with Eq. (
60
). As we will see, the
\mathcal{O}(T^{-r})
error term will be negligible compared to the other asymptotic approximations made below. In light of this we can write

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)(f_{T\neq 0}+\widetilde{f}_{T\neq 0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T\int_{0}^{\frac{\mu-E_{0}}{T}}dz\,\frac{H(\mu+Tz)-H(\mu-Tz)}{1+e^{z}}+\mathcal{O}(T^{-r})\,.

(62)

As
\mu\pm Tz\geq E_{0}
for all
z
in the domain of integration, we can compute a Taylor expansion with remainder up to order
k
on this domain as follows:

\displaystyle H(\mu+Tz)-H(\mu-Tz)=\sum_{n=1,n\text{ odd}}^{k-2}2H^{(n)}(\mu)\frac{(Tz)^{n}}{n!}

(63)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+(Tz)^{k}\int_{0}^{1}ds\,\frac{(1-s)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\left[H^{(k)}(\mu+sTz)+H^{(k)}(\mu-sTz)\right]\,.

We emphasize that the reason for restricting the domain of integration in Eq. (
62
) (with the difference being accounted for in the error term) is that we are only assuming
H
is
C^{k}
on a neighborhood of
\infty
, and so the Taylor expansion argument is only valid on the restricted domain.

Substituting Eq. (
63
) this into Eq. (
62
) we can compute

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)(f_{T\neq 0}+\widetilde{f}_{T\neq 0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1,n\text{ odd}}^{k-2}\frac{2T^{n+1}}{n!}H^{(n)}(\mu)\int_{0}^{\frac{\mu-E_{0}}{T}}dz\,\frac{z^{n}}{1+e^{z}}+R_{k}+\mathcal{O}(T^{-r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1,n\text{ odd}}^{k-2}2(1-2^{-n})\zeta(n+1)T^{n+1}H^{(n)}(\mu)+R_{k}+\mathcal{O}(T^{-r})\,,

(64)

where to obtain the last line we again made use of the freedom to change the limits of integration while only incurring
\mathcal{O}(T^{-r})
error and then used the integral formula Eq. (
29
). The integrated remainder term is given by

\displaystyle R_{k}\equiv T^{k+1}\int_{0}^{\frac{\mu-E_{0}}{T}}dz\,\frac{z^{k}}{1+e^{z}}\int_{0}^{1}ds\,\frac{(1-s)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\left[H^{(k)}(\mu+sTz)+H^{(k)}(\mu-sTz)\right]\,.

(65)

Eq. (
58
) implies that
|H^{(k)}(E)|\leq CE^{q_{k}}
on
[E_{0},\infty)
for some
C>0
. Using this we can bound the integral of the remainder as follows:

\displaystyle|R_{k}|\leq

\displaystyle CT^{k+1}\int_{0}^{\frac{\mu-E_{0}}{T}}dz\,\frac{z^{k}}{1+e^{z}}\int_{0}^{1}ds\,\frac{(1-s)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\left(|\mu+sTz|^{q_{z}}+|\mu-sTz|^{q_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{q_{k}+1}CT^{k+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,\frac{z^{k}}{1+e^{z}}\int_{0}^{1}ds\,\frac{(1-s)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\left[\mu^{q_{k}}+(sTz)^{q_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\mu^{q_{k}}T^{k+1})

(66)

as
T\to\infty
. Note that due to the relation Eq. (
60
) between
\mu
and
T
we can equivalently write the error term as
\mathcal{O}(T^{(1+\gamma)q_{k}+k+1})
and so the
\mathcal{O}(T^{-r})
error term is negligible in comparison. Combining the bound Eq. (
66
) with Eq. (
64
) we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{\infty}dE\,H(E)f_{FD}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{\mu}dE\,H(E)+\sum_{n=1,n\text{ odd}}^{k-2}2(1-2^{-n})\zeta(n+1)T^{n+1}H^{(n)}(\mu)+\mathcal{O}(\mu^{q_{k}}T^{k+1})

(67)

as
T\to\infty
. This completes the proof of Theorem
3
.

Unless
q_{k}
is sufficiently negative, the absolute error in Eq. (
59
) grows as
T\to\infty
, however the relative error decays as we now show. First assume
q_{k}>0
and integrate Eq. (
58
) twice at the Fermi energy
E=\mu
to get

\displaystyle H^{(k-2)}(\mu)=\frac{a_{k}}{(q_{k}+1)(q_{k}+2)}\mu^{q_{k}+2}+\mathcal{O}(\mu^{q_{k}+1})

(68)

as
\mu\to\infty
. Therefore, the ratio of the error to the last included term in the expansion behaves as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{O}(\mu^{q_{k}}T^{k+1})}{T^{k-1}H^{(k-2)}(\mu)}=\frac{T^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\frac{\mathcal{O}(1)}{\frac{a_{k}}{(q_{k}+1)(q_{k}+2)}+\mathcal{O}(\mu^{-1})}=\mathcal{O}(T^{-2\gamma})\,,

(69)

which approaches
0
as
T\to\infty
. To obtain Eq. (
69
) we used Eq. (
60
) and also that
a_{k}\neq 0
. Continuing to integrate Eq. (
68
), one sees that earlier terms in the sum dominate the error term to a greater degree. Therefore the expansion Eq. (
67
) properly captures leading and sub-leading behavior, with a subdominant error term and a decaying relative error when
q_{k}>0
. When
q_{k}\leq 0
, integrating Eq. (
58
) becomes more subtle due to the need to handle potential logarithm and constant terms that arise. However, if instead we make the additional assumptions

\displaystyle H^{(k-j)}(E)=a_{k-j}E^{q_{k}+j}+\mathcal{O}(E^{q_{k}+j-1})

(70)

for
j=2,...,k-1
with
a_{k-j}\neq 0
then a similar computation to Eq. (
69
) shows

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{O}(\mu^{q_{k}}T^{k+1})}{T^{n+1}H^{(n)}(\mu)}=\mathcal{O}(T^{-(k-n)\gamma})

(71)

for all
n=1,...,k-2
and therefore the error term in Eq. (
67
) is again seen to be dominated by all other terms in the summation as
T\to\infty
.

3
Non-Sommerfeld Few Particle Regime

3.1
Asymptotic Expansion in the Regime
T\to 0
with
\mu-m\ll T

In this section we study thermal averages when
\mu-m\ll T\ll m
, a regime where we demonstrate a complete failure of the Sommerfeld formula. The techniques we use to study this regime are similar to those employed in Section
2.3
, but this time they lead to a wholly distinct and novel asymptotic expansion. In Figure
7
we preview this new result, which is stated in Theorem
5
below. In this figure we have parameterized
\mu-m=\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2})
and so
\mu-m
approaches zero faster than
T
. Note that here the Sommerfeld expansion Eq. (
31
) (red dot-dashed lines) fails at low temperature while our Theorem
5
(green dashed lines) provides an accurate approximation. One can see from the figure that the traditional Sommerfeld expansion is in fact a high temperature approximation under this relation between
\mu
and
T
; this corresponds to the behavior proven in Theorem
3
above.

Figure 7
:
Left: Comparison of asymptotic expansions of
\langle G\rangle_{T}
,
G(p)=1
, via the Sommerfeld expansion Eq. (
31
) (red dot-dashed line) and the new expansion Eq. (
88
) (green dashed line) where
\mu=m(1+\widetilde{T}^{3/2})
. Under this relationship between
\mu
and
T
, Theorem
3
and Theorem
5
respectively prove that Eq. (
59
) is valid at high temperature and Eq. (
88
) is valid at low temperature; we see this reflected in the figure. Right: The relative error of these asymptotic expansions, also compared with that of the Boltzmann approximation.

We now proceed with a detailed derivation of the new expansion. To make our assumptions explicit, here we consider a
D
-dimensional thermal average (
D\geq 2
) of a function
G(p)
,
p\in[0,\infty)
that is
C^{k}
and whose zeroth through
k
’th derivatives are polynomially bounded, and will assume that
\mu=m+bT
,
b\in\mathbb{R}
. To begin the derivation, first change variables in Eq. (
14
) to
z=(E-m)/m
so that
p=m\sqrt{z}\sqrt{z+2}
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,p^{D-1}G(p)f_{FD}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,(1+z)z^{(D-2)/2}(z+2)^{(D-2)/2}G\left(m\sqrt{z}\sqrt{z+2}\right)\frac{1}{1+e^{z/\widetilde{T}-b}}\,,

(72)

where
\widetilde{T}\equiv T/m
. Note that the integrand can have a square root singularity at
z=0
and so we cannot simply Taylor expand in
z
; this is a key difference from the derivation of the traditional Sommerfeld expansion. However we can employ a more general asymptotic expansion by again making use of the function
F(y,m)
, defined in Eq. (
35
), and its expansion Eq. (
36
), to rewrite the integral as follows:

\displaystyle Eq.\,(\ref{eq:fd_int_var_z})=

\displaystyle m^{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,F(\sqrt{z},m)\frac{z^{(D-2)/2}}{1+e^{z/\widetilde{T}-b}}

(73)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}(m)m^{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,\frac{z^{(n+D-2)/2}}{1+e^{z/\widetilde{T}-b}}+m^{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,R_{k}(\sqrt{z},m)\frac{z^{(D-2)/2}}{1+e^{z/\widetilde{T}-b}}\,.

When
b\geq 0
the integral of the remainder term can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\left|m^{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,R_{k}(\sqrt{z},m)\frac{z^{(D-2)/2}}{1+e^{z/\widetilde{T}-b}}\right|

(74)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m^{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,[\alpha_{k}(m)+\beta_{k}(m)z^{q_{k}/2}]\frac{z^{(k+D-2)/2}}{1+e^{z/\widetilde{T}-b}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m^{D}\int_{b\widetilde{T}}^{\infty}dz\,[\alpha_{k}(m)+\beta_{k}(m)z^{q_{k}/2}]z^{(k+D-2)/2}e^{-z/\widetilde{T}+b}

\displaystyle+m^{D}\int_{0}^{{b\widetilde{T}}}dz\,[\alpha_{k}(m)+\beta_{k}(m)z^{q_{k}/2}]z^{(k+D-2)/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{D}\alpha_{k}(m)\widetilde{T}^{(k+D)/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,(u+b)^{(k+D-2)/2}e^{-u}

\displaystyle+m^{D}\beta_{k}(m)\widetilde{T}^{(k+D+q_{k})/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,(u+b)^{(k+D-2+q_{k})/2}e^{-u}

\displaystyle+\alpha_{k}(m)m^{D}\frac{2}{k+D}(b\widetilde{T})^{(k+D)/2}+\beta_{k}(m)m^{D}\frac{2}{k+D+q_{k}}(b\widetilde{T})^{(k+D+q_{k})/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{T}^{(k+D)/2})

(75)

as
\widetilde{T}\to 0
. When
b<0
the integral of the remainder can be bounded more simply as follows

\displaystyle\left|m^{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,R_{k}(\sqrt{z},m)\frac{z^{(D-2)/2}}{1+e^{z/\widetilde{T}-b}}\right|

(76)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m^{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,(\alpha_{k}(m)+\beta_{k}(m)z^{q_{k}/2})z^{(k+D-2)/2}e^{-z/\widetilde{T}+b}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}(m)m^{D}e^{b}\widetilde{T}^{(k+D)/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,u^{(k+D-2)/2}e^{-u}

\displaystyle+\beta_{k}(m)m^{D}e^{b}\widetilde{T}^{(q_{k}+k+D)/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,u^{(q_{k}+k+D-2)/2}e^{-u}=\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{T}^{(k+D)/2})

(77)

as
\widetilde{T}\to 0
.

Remark 2
.

We emphasize that the implied constant in the bounds Eq. (
75
) and Eq. (
77
) depends on
m
and
b
, though if one restricts
b
to a set that is bounded above then the constants can be chosen independent of
b
.

Changing variables to
x=z/\widetilde{T}
, recalling the integral formula for the polylogarithm function
\mathrm{Li}_{s}(y)
, see 3.411.3 in
Gradshteyn and
Ryzhik (2007)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{x^{n/2}}{1+e^{x-b}}=-\Gamma(1+n/2)\mathrm{Li}_{1+n/2}(-e^{b})\,,

(78)

and using the remainder bounds Eq. (
75
) and Eq. (
77
) we obtain the following:

Theorem 4
.

Let
D\geq 2
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, and
G(p)
be a
C^{k}
function on
[0,\infty)
whose zeroth through
k
’th derivatives are polynomially bounded. Let
\mu=m+bT
for
b\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,p^{D-1}G(p)f_{FD}(p)

\displaystyle=-

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}(m)m^{D}\widetilde{T}^{(n+D)/2}\Gamma[(n+D)/2]\mathrm{Li}_{(n+D)/2}(-e^{b})+\mathcal{O}\left(\widetilde{T}^{(k+D)/2}\right)\,,

(79)

as
\widetilde{T}\to 0
, where
\widetilde{T}=T/m
,
a_{n}
is defined in Eq. (
56
) (explicit formulas for the first few
a_{n}
’s can be found in Eq. (
57
)), and the implied constant in the error term depends on
m
but is uniform in
b
when restricted to a fixed set that is bounded above.

3.2
Asymptotic Expansion in the Regime
T\ll m
,
\mu=m

When
b=0
(i.e.,
\mu=m
) one can use the integral formula Eq. (
29
) to further simplify the expansion Eq. (
79
). In dimension
D>2
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,p^{D-1}G(p)f_{FD}(p)

(80)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}(m)m^{D}\widetilde{T}^{(n+D)/2}\left(1-2^{-(n+D-2)/2}\right)\Gamma[(n+D)/2]\zeta[(n+D)/2]

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\widetilde{T}^{(k+D)/2}\right)

as
\widetilde{T}\to 0
, while for
D=2
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,pG(p)f_{FD}(p)

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{0}(m)m^{2}\ln(2)\widetilde{T}+\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}a_{n}(m)m^{2}\widetilde{T}^{(n+2)/2}\left(1-2^{-n/2}\right)\Gamma[(n+2)/2]\zeta[(n+D)/2]

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\widetilde{T}^{(k+2)/2}\right)

as
\widetilde{T}\to 0
. In particular, note that when
D=3
and
G(0)\neq 0
the leading order term scales with
\widetilde{T}^{3/2}
. This is in contrast to the Sommerfeld expansion Eq. (
31
), which applies to the case where
\mu-m
is bounded away from zero and whose leading order term scales with
\widetilde{T}^{2}
.

3.3
Asymptotic Expansion in the Regime
|\mu-m|\ll T\ll m

One can also use the expansion Eq. (
79
) to study the regime where
\mu
approaches
m
faster than
T
approaches zero. Mathematically we parameterize this as

\displaystyle\mu=m(1+B\widetilde{T}^{1+\gamma})\,,\,\,\,B\in\mathbb{R}\,,\,\,\gamma>0\,.

(82)

To proceed, first Taylor expand Eq. (
78
) to first order in
b
by differentiating under the integral to obtain

\displaystyle-\Gamma(1+n/2)\mathrm{Li}_{1+n/2}(-e^{b})=\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\frac{u^{n/2}}{1+e^{u-b}}

(83)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\frac{u^{n/2}}{e^{u}+1}+b\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\frac{u^{n/2}}{(e^{u/2}+e^{-u/2})^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-2^{-n/2})\Gamma(1+n/2)\zeta(1+n/2)+b\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\frac{u^{n/2}}{(e^{u/2}+e^{-u/2})^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2})\,.

(84)

See
Dingle (1957)
and
Garoni et al. (2001)
for in-depth discussion of the computation of Eq. (
83
). For
n=0
the leading order term in Eq. (
84
) has the indeterminate form
0\cdot\infty
(this case will only be relevant to us when
D=2)
; there one should instead use

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}du\frac{1}{1+e^{u-b}}=\ln(1+e^{b})=\ln(2)+\frac{b}{2}+\mathcal{O}(b^{2})\,.

(85)

The integral in Eq. (
84
) can be evaluated via integration by parts:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\frac{u^{n/2}}{(e^{u/2}+e^{-u/2})^{2}}=

\displaystyle-u^{n/2}/(e^{u}+1)|_{0}^{\infty}+\frac{n}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\frac{u^{n/2-1}}{e^{u}+1}

(86)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}\begin{cases}(1-2^{1-n/2})\Gamma(n/2)\zeta(n/2)&n>0,\,n\neq 2\\
\log(2)&n=2\end{cases}\,.

Now substitute Eq. (
83
) into Eq. (
79
) and let
b=B\widetilde{T}^{\gamma}
. We note that the remainder term in Eq. (
79
) is still
\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{T}^{1+(k+D-2)/2})
in this case because
b
remains bounded above (in fact, it remains bounded) as
\widetilde{T}\to 0
; see Remark
2
. The expansion Eq. (
79
) with
k=2
then simplifies to give the following result.

Theorem 5
.

Let
G(p)
be a
C^{2}
function on
[0,\infty)
whose zeroth through
2
nd derivatives are polynomially bounded. Let
\mu=m(1+B\widetilde{T}^{1+\gamma})
where
B\in\mathbb{R}
,
\gamma>0
,
\widetilde{T}=T/m
.

1.

For
D>2
and with
a_{n}
defined by Eq. (
56
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,p^{D-1}G(p)f_{FD}(p)

(87)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{0}(m)m^{D}(1-2^{-(D-2)/2})\Gamma(D/2)\zeta(D/2)\widetilde{T}^{D/2}

\displaystyle+a_{0}(m)m^{D}B\widetilde{T}^{D/2+\gamma}\frac{D-2}{2}\begin{cases}(1-2^{2-D/2})\Gamma[(D-2)/2]\zeta[(D-2)/2]&D\neq 4\\
\log(2)&D=4\end{cases}

\displaystyle+a_{1}(m)m^{D}(1-2^{-(D-1)/2})\Gamma[(D+1)/2]\zeta[(D+1)/2]\widetilde{T}^{(D+1)/2}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\widetilde{T}^{(2+D)/2}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{T}^{D/2+2\gamma})\,.

(88)

2.

For
D=2
and with
a_{n}
defined by Eq. (
56
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,pG(p)f_{FD}(p)=a_{0}(m)m^{2}\ln(2)\widetilde{T}+\frac{1}{2}a_{0}(m)m^{2}B\widetilde{T}^{1+\gamma}

\displaystyle+a_{1}(m)m^{2}(1-2^{-1/2})\frac{\pi^{1/2}}{2}\zeta(3/2)\widetilde{T}^{3/2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\widetilde{T}^{1+2\gamma}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\widetilde{T}^{2}\right)\,\,\,\,\text{as}\,\,\,\widetilde{T}\to 0\,.

(89)

Remark 3
.

When referring to the number of terms in the expansions in Theorem
5
and related results, e.g., in Figure
7
, we mean the index
k
that was used in Eq. (
79
) to obtain the expansion.

Remark 4
.

Even when the physical dimension
D
is fixed, the general result Eq. (
87
) for arbitrary

can be used to simplify calculations in cases where

has the form

,

, by replacing

with

) and Eq. (

. To obtain higher order terms in these expansions one simply needs to continue the expansion Eq. (

) to the appropriate higher order before substituting it into Eq. (

4
Discussion of Results

We resolved a century long oversight, the understanding of the dilute Fermi-Dirac (FD) quantum domain in which the traditional Sommerfeld expansion fails. We recognize the particle number per de Broglie volume as a critical physical quantity which separates the different FD regimes, i.e., the regions in the
(T/m,(\mu-m)/m)
-plane where different asymptotic expansions apply. We obtain a novel asymptotic expansion that is valid when the particle number per de Broglie volume is moderate (2 to 3 particles), as compared to the low density semiclassical Boltzmann limit (1 particle or less) and the traditional highly degenerate Sommerfeld limit (5 or more particles); see Figure
1
. This intermediate density regime was unrecognized in the literature, where often one finds a mere acknowledgement that “the Sommerfeld expansion fails”. In addition, we add to the understanding of the Sommerfeld expansion in the high temperature regime and when
\mu
is close to
m
