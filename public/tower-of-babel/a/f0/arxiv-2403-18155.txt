An inexact infeasible arc-search interior-point method for linear optimization
Einosuke Iida, Makoto Yamashita

Abstract
Arc-search interior-point methods (IPMs) are a class of IPMs that utilize an ellipsoidal arc to approximate the central path. On the other hand, inexact IPMs solve the linear equation system for the search direction inexactly at each iteration. In this paper, we propose an inexact infeasible arc-search interior-point method. We establish that the proposed method is a polynomial-time algorithm and we show that its iteration complexity is lower than an inexact infeasible line-search IPM. We conducted numerical experiments with benchmark problems from NETLIB. The numerical results demonstrate that the proposed method can reduce the number of iterations and the computation time compared to an existing inexact line-search IPM.



Keywords:

interior-point method, arc-search, inexact IPM, infeasible IPM, linear programming.

1
Introduction

Linear programming problems (LPs) have had an important role in both theoretical analysis and practical applications, and
many methods have been studied for solving LPs efficiently.
The first interior-point method (IPM) was proposed by Dikin
[
9
]

and the first polynomial-time IPM by Karmarkar
[
18
]
.
The use of IPMs have been extended to other optimization problems,
for example, second-order cone programming and semidefinite programming.

Various types of IPMs have been studied.
The IPM with a feasible inithial point, called feasible IPM,
was proved to be a polynomial algorithm
[
15
,
20
,
26
,
35
]

and their best iteration complexity is
\order{\sqrt{n} L}
,
where
n
is the number of variables and
L
is the binary length of the input data.
The IPM with the infeasible initial point, called infeasible IPM,
was also proved to have global convergence
[
21
]
and polynomial time complexity
[
49
]
.
Predictor-corrector algorithms were studied in
[
24
,
23
]
, and
two-dimensional search IPM was recently proposed by
Vitor and Easton 2022
.

Inexact IPMs are one of such variants.
They inexactly solve a linear equation system (LES) for the search direction at each iteration.
An infeasible inexact IPM (II-IPM) was first proposed for solving a constrained system of equations by
Bellavia 1998

and it has been extended for LPs
[
12
,
21
,
25
]
.

Several studies
[
12
,
25
,
22
]
proved the convergence of II-IPMs.
In particular, an II-IPM of
Mizuno and Jarre 1999
have
\order{n^2 L}
iteration complexity.
In
[
1
,
30
]
,
preconditioned conjugate gradient methods were used
as the inexact linear system solver.
The inexact IPMs have recently gained much attention due to their relevance to quantum computing.
Quantum linear system algorithms (QLSAs) have the potential to solve LESs fast.
Inexact IPMs using the QLSA, called quantum interior-point methods, are proposed in several papers
[
19
,
39
,
28
,
29
]
.

On the other hand,
studies to reduce the number of iterations in IPMs have also contributed to improving the numerical performance.
Higher-order IPMs using second-order or higher derivatives have been studied
[
31
,
24
,
13
,
23
,
11
]
,
but these sometimes have a worse polynomial bound,
or the analysis of computational complexity is not simple.
An arc-search IPM proposed by Yang
[
42
]
is one of the higher-order IPMs.
(IPMs that find the next iteration point on a straight line are called line-search IPMs in this paper.)
Arc-search IPMs employ an ellipsoidal arc to find the next iteration point.
Since the central path toward an optimal solution is generally a smooth curve,
the ellipsoidal arc can approximate the central path better than the straight line,
and a reduction in the number of iterations can be expected.

Yang and Yamashita 2018
showed that
their arc-search IPM has the iteration complexity of
\order{n L}
,
the same as that of the line-search IPM
[
38
]
.
In addition,
Yang
[
44
]
improved it to
\order{\sqrt{n} L}
under the assumption that the variables
x^{k}
and
s^{k}
are bounded below and away from zeros for all iterations until a stopping criterion is satisfied.
Numerical experiments in
[
43
,
47
]
demonstrated that the number of iterations LP was reduced compared to existing methods.
The arc-search IPMs have been applied to various optimization problems,
including second-order cone optimization
[
41
]
, SDP
[
48
]
, convex
[
46
]
, and nonlinear problems
[
40
]
.
In Iida et al.
[
17
]
,
an arc-search IPM with Nesterov’s restarting strategy further reduced the number of iterations.

Arc-search IPMs solve two linear equation systems (LESs) at each iteration to obtain the search direction, while line-search IPMs solve one LES.
Therefore,
if we save the computation time for LESs,
we could get a higher effect with arc-search than with line-search.
A possible way to reduce the computation time for LESs is to solve them inexactly.
For example,
the arc-search IPMs for nonlinear optimization problems
[
40
]
and convex optimization problems
[
46
]
omitted
the third-order derivatives in their LESs;
however
their arc-search IPMs reduced the computation time
without compromising the quality of solutions.
This result suggests that the arc-search IPMs can find
an optimal solution even with a degree of inexactness
in the LESs.

In this paper, we propose a novel inexact infeasible arc-search interior-point method (II-arc).
To the best of author’s knowledge,
this paper is the first paper that discusses an inexact IPM with the arc-search framework.
From the convergence analysis,
we reveal that the proposed II-arc method achieves a better iteration complexity than
existing inexact infeasible line-search IPMs (II-lines)
[
25
,
28
]
.

We conducted that numerical experiments using the conjugate gradient (CG) method as an inexact linear equation solver. The numerical results are consistent
with the convegence analysis, and the proposed
method (II-arc) reduced the number of iterations by almost half for many instances.

This paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces the standard form of LP problems and the formulas necessary for II-arc.
In Section
3
,
we describe the proposed method,
and in Section
4
,
we discuss the convergence and the polynomial iteration complexity.
Section
5
provides the results of the numerical experiments and the discussion.
Finally,
Section
6
gives conclusions of this paper and discusses future directions.

2
Preliminaries

In this paper, we consider an LP of the standard form:

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}c^{\top}x,\quad\text{s.t. }Ax=b,\quad x\geq 0,

(1)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
m\leq n
,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

are input data.
The associated dual problem of (
1
) is

\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m},s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}b^{\top}y,\quad\text{s.t. }A^{\top}y+s=c,\quad s\geq 0,

(2)

where
y
and
s
are the dual variable vector and the dual slack vector, respectively.
Let
\mathcal{S}^{*}
be the set of the optimal solutions to (
1
) and (
2
).
When
(x^{*},y^{*},s^{*})\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
,
it is well-known that
(x^{*},y^{*},s^{*})
satisfies
the KKT conditions:

\displaystyle Ax^{*}

\displaystyle=b

(3a)

\displaystyle A^{\top}y^{*}+s^{*}

\displaystyle=c

(3b)

\displaystyle(x^{*},s^{*})

\displaystyle\geq 0

(3c)

\displaystyle x_{i}^{*}s_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=0,\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

(3d)

We denote the primal and dual residuals in (
1
) and (
2
) as

\displaystyle r_{b}(x)

\displaystyle=Ax-b

(4a)

\displaystyle r_{c}(y,s)

\displaystyle=A^{\top}y+s-c,

(4b)

and define the duality measure as

\mu=\frac{x^{\top}s}{n}.

(5)

Letting
\zeta\geq 0
,
we define the set of
\zeta
-optimal solutions as

\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\zeta}=\left\{\left(x,y,s\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+m}\mid(x,s)\geq 0,\,\mu\leq\zeta,\,\norm{(r_b(x), r_c(y,s))}\leq\zeta\right\}.

(6)

From the KKT conditions (
3
),
we know
\mathcal{S}^{*}\subset\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\zeta}
.

In this paper,
we make the following assumptions for the primal-dual pair (
1
) and (
2
).
These assumptions are common ones in the context of IPMs
and are used in many papers
(for example, see
[
38
,
45
]
).

Assumption 2.1
.

There exists a constant
\omega\geq 1
such that

\omega\geq\max\left\{\norm{(x^*, s^*)}_{\infty}\mid(x^{*},y^{*},s^{*})\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\right\}.

(7)

Assumption 2.2
.

A
is a full-row rank matrix, i.e.,
\rank(A)=m
.

IPMs are iterative methods,
so we denote the
k
th iteration by
(x^{k},y^{k},s^{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
the initial point by
(x^{0},y^{0},s^{0})
.
We denote the duality measure of
k
th iteration as
\mu_{k}=(x^{k})^{\top}s^{k}/n
.

Similarly to Wright
[
38
, Chapter 6, p112]
,
we choose the initial point as follows to prove the polynomial convergence:

(x^{0},y^{0},s^{0})=\omega(e,0,e),

(8)

where
\omega
is a scalar satisfying (
7
).

Given a strictly positive iteration
(x^{k},y^{k},s^{k})
such that
(x^{k},s^{k})>0
,
the strategy of an infeasible IPM is to trace a smooth curve called an approximate central path:

\mathcal{C}^{k}=\left\{(x(t),y(t),s(t))\mid t\in(0,1],(x(1),y(1),s(1))=(x^{k},y^{k},s^{k})\right\},

(9)

where
(x(t),y(t),s(t))
is the unique solution of the following system

\displaystyle Ax(t)-b=t\ r_{b}(x^{k}),

(10a)

\displaystyle A^{\top}y(t)+s(t)-c=t\ r_{c}(y^{k},s^{k}),

(10b)

\displaystyle x(t)\circ s(t)=t(x^{k}\circ s^{k}),

(10c)

\displaystyle(x(t),s(t))>0.

(10d)

As
t\rightarrow 0
,

(x(t),y(t),s(t))
converges to an optimal solution
(x^{*},y^{*},s^{*})\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

Since (
10
) does not admit an analytical solution,
arc-search IPMs
[
45
]
use an ellipse
\mathcal{E}^{k}
to approximate the curve
\mathcal{C}^{k}
,
where

\mathcal{E}^{k}=\left\{(x(\alpha),y(\alpha),s(\alpha)):\begin{aligned} &(x(\alpha),y(\alpha),s(\alpha))=\overrightarrow{a}\cos(\alpha)+\overrightarrow{b}\sin(\alpha)+\overrightarrow{c},\\
&(x(0),y(0),s(0))=(x^{k},y^{k},s^{k})\\
\end{aligned}\right\},

\overrightarrow{a},\overrightarrow{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+m}
are the axes of the ellipse
and
\overrightarrow{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+m}
is the center of it.
The values of
(x(\alpha),y(\alpha),s(\alpha))\in\mathcal{E}^{k}

for an angle
\alpha\in[0,\pi/2]
is obtained by the following relation
[
45
, Theorem 5.1]
:

\displaystyle x(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\ x^{k}-\dot{x}\sin(\alpha)+\ddot{x}(1-\cos(\alpha)),

\displaystyle y(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\ y^{k}-\dot{y}\sin(\alpha)+\ddot{y}(1-\cos(\alpha)),

\displaystyle s(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\ s^{k}-\dot{s}\sin(\alpha)+\ddot{s}(1-\cos(\alpha)).

In arc-saerch frameworks,
we update the iteration points as
(x^{k+1},y^{k+1},s^{k+1})=(x(\alpha),y(\alpha),s(\alpha))
.
Here,

(\dot{x},\dot{y},\dot{s})
and
(\ddot{x},\ddot{y},\ddot{s})
are
the first and second derivatives

of (
10
) with respect to
t
, respectively.
Therefore,
they can be obtained as the solutions of the following LESs.

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}A&0&0\\
0&A^{\top}&I\\
S^{k}&0&X^{k}\end{bmatrix}\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{x}\\
\dot{y}\\
\dot{s}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r_{b}(x^{k})\\
r_{c}(y^{k},s^{k})\\
x^{k}\circ s^{k}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}A&0&0\\
0&A^{\top}&I\\
S^{k}&0&X^{k}\end{bmatrix}\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\ddot{x}\\
\ddot{y}\\
\ddot{s}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\\
-2\dot{x}\circ\dot{s}\end{array}\right].

Lastly,
we define a neighborhood of the approximate central
path
[
38
, Chapter 6]
:

\mathcal{N}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):=\left\{(x,y,s)\mid\begin{aligned} &(x,s)>0,\,x_{i}s_{i}\geq\gamma_{1}\mu\text{ for }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\\
&\norm{(r_b(x), r_c(y, s))}\leq[\norm{(r_b(x^0), r_c(y^0, s^0))}/\mu_{0}]\gamma_{2}\mu\\
\end{aligned}\right\},

(24)

where
\gamma_{1}\in(0,1)
and
\gamma_{2}\geq 1
are given parameters,
and
\norm{(r_b(x), r_c(y, s))}
is the norm of the vertical
concatenation of
r_{b}(x)
and
r_{c}(y,s)
.

Note that the initial point
(x^{0},y^{0},s^{0})
defined by (
8
) always satisfies
(x^{0},y^{0},s^{0})\in\mathcal{N}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
.
This neighborhood will be used in the convergence analysis.

3
The proposed method

In this section,
we propose an II-arc method.

To generate the sequence in the positive orthant
(x,s)>0
,
we introduce a perturbation into (
2
)
based on
Kojima et al. 1993

as follows:

\begin{bmatrix}A&0&0\\
0&A^{\top}&I\\
S^{k}&0&X^{k}\end{bmatrix}\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{x}\\
\dot{y}\\
\dot{s}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r_{b}(x^{k})\\
r_{c}(y^{k},s^{k})\\
x^{k}\circ s^{k}-\sigma\mu_{k}e\end{array}\right],

(25)

where
\sigma\in(0,1]
is the constant called centering parameter.
In the subsequent discussion,

(\dot{x},\dot{y},\dot{s})
denotes the solution of (
25
).
The proposed method solves (
25
) and (
2
) inexactly
at each iteration to obtain the ellipsoidal approximation.

Several approaches can be considered for solving the Newton system (
25
),
such as the augmented system and the Newton equation system (also known as the normal equation system, NES)
[
4
]
.
The NES formula of (
25
) is

M^{k}\dot{y}=\rho_{1}^{k},

(26)

where

\displaystyle M^{k}

\displaystyle=A(D^{k})^{2}A^{\top},

(27a)

\displaystyle D^{k}

\displaystyle=(X^{k})^{\frac{1}{2}}(S^{k})^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(27b)

\displaystyle\rho_{1}^{k}

\displaystyle=A(D^{k})^{2}r_{c}(y^{k},s^{k})+r_{b}(x^{k})-A(S^{k})^{-1}(x^{k}\circ s^{k}-\sigma\mu_{k}e)

\displaystyle=A(D^{k})^{2}A^{\top}y^{k}-A(D^{k})^{2}c+\sigma\mu_{k}A(S^{k})^{-1}e+Ax^{k}-b.

(27c)

When we solve the LES (
26
) exactly and obtain
\dot{y}
,
we can compute the other components
\dot{x}
and
\dot{s}
of the solution in (
25
).

Let
\tilde{\dot{y}}
be an inexact solution of
\dot{y}
.
We denote the error of
\tilde{\dot{y}}
by
r_{1}^{k}:=M^{k}\tilde{\dot{y}}-\rho_{1}^{k}=M^{k}\left(\tilde{\dot{y}}-\dot{y}\right)
.
Then, the inexact solution
\tilde{\dot{y}}
satisfies

M^{k}\tilde{\dot{y}}=\rho_{1}^{k}+r_{1}^{k},

(28)

We can compute the other inexact components
\tilde{\dot{x}}
and
\tilde{\dot{s}}
from
\tilde{\dot{y}}
,
and these inexact components satisfy the following conditions
[
28
]
:

\displaystyle A\tilde{\dot{x}}

\displaystyle=r_{b}(x^{k})+r_{1}^{k},

(29a)

\displaystyle A^{\top}\tilde{\dot{y}}+\tilde{\dot{s}}

\displaystyle=r_{c}(y^{k},s^{k}),

(29b)

\displaystyle S^{k}\tilde{\dot{x}}+X^{k}\tilde{\dot{s}}

\displaystyle=X^{k}s^{k}-\sigma\mu_{k}e.

(29c)

Bellavia and Pieraccini 2004
pointed out the residual must decrease at least
\order{\sqrt{n} \log n}
times faster than the duality measure.

Oliveira and Sorensen 2005

proposed a modification to NES which
enabled to relax the condition for the residual norm.
This modified NES was named as MNES
[
28
]
.
This paper employs the MNES formulation for
the analysis on the iteration complexity.

Since
A
is full row rank from Assumption
2.2
,
we can choose an arbitrary basis
\hat{B}\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}
such that
\absolutevalue*{\hat{B}}=m
and
A_{\hat{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is nonsingular.
Such a
\hat{B}
can be obtained with, for example, the maximum weight basis
algorithm
[
30
]
,
which requires
\order{n^2 m}
computational costs.
Now we can adapt (
26
) to

\hat{M}^{k}\dot{z}=\hat{\rho}_{1}^{k},

(30)

where

\displaystyle\hat{M}^{k}

\displaystyle=(D_{\hat{B}}^{k})^{-1}A_{\hat{B}}^{-1}M^{k}((D_{\hat{B}}^{k})^{-1}A_{\hat{B}}^{-1})^{\top},

(31a)

\displaystyle D_{\hat{B}}^{k}

\displaystyle=(X^{k}_{\hat{B}})^{\frac{1}{2}}(S_{\hat{B}}^{k})^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(31b)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{1}^{k}

\displaystyle=(D_{\hat{B}}^{k})^{-1}A_{\hat{B}}^{-1}\rho_{1}^{k}.

(31c)

The inexact solution
\tilde{\dot{z}}
of (
30
) satisfies

\hat{M}^{k}\tilde{\dot{z}}=\hat{\rho}_{1}^{k}+\hat{r}^{k}_{1},

(32)

where
\hat{r}_{1}^{k}
is the error of
\tilde{\dot{z}}
defined as

\hat{r}_{1}^{k}:=\hat{M}^{k}\tilde{\dot{z}}-\hat{\rho}_{1}^{k}=\hat{M}_{k}\left(\tilde{\dot{z}}-\dot{z}\right).

We discuss the range that the error
\hat{r}_{1}^{k}
should satisfy for the convergence analysis below.

Then,
we can obtain the first derivative
(\tilde{\dot{x}},\tilde{\dot{y}},\tilde{\dot{s}})

from the inexact solution in (
32
)
and the steps below:

\displaystyle\tilde{\dot{y}}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(D_{\hat{B}}^{k}\right)^{-1}A_{\hat{B}}^{-1}\right)^{\top}\tilde{\dot{z}}

(33a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\dot{s}}

\displaystyle=r_{c}(y^{k},s^{k})-A^{T}\tilde{\dot{y}}

(33b)

\displaystyle v_{1}^{k}

\displaystyle=\left(v_{\hat{B}}^{k},v_{\hat{N}}^{k}\right)=\left(D_{\hat{B}}^{k}\hat{r}^{k}_{1},0\right)

(33c)

\displaystyle\tilde{\dot{x}}

\displaystyle=x^{k}-(D^{k})^{2}\tilde{\dot{s}}-\sigma\mu_{k}(S^{k})^{-1}e-v_{1}^{k}.

(33d)

We also apply the MNES formulation to the second derivative (
2
).
Letting

\displaystyle\rho_{2}^{k}=2A(S^{k})^{-1}\tilde{\dot{x}}\circ\tilde{\dot{s}},\qquad\hat{\rho}_{2}^{k}=(D_{\hat{B}}^{k})^{-1}A_{\hat{B}}^{-1}\rho_{2}^{k},

we have

\hat{M}^{k}\ddot{z}=\hat{\rho}_{2}^{k}

(34)

with the same definition of
\hat{M}^{k}
as in (
31a
).
We use
\tilde{\ddot{z}}
to denote the inexact solution of (
34
),
then we have

\hat{M}^{k}\tilde{\ddot{z}}=\hat{\rho}_{2}^{k}+\hat{r}_{2}^{k},

(35)

where
\hat{r}_{2}^{k}
is defined as
\hat{r}_{2}^{k}:=\hat{M}_{k}\left(\tilde{\ddot{z}}-\ddot{z}\right)
.
Similarly to (
33
),
we compute
the inexact second derivative
(\tilde{\ddot{x}},\tilde{\ddot{y}},\tilde{\ddot{s}})

as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{\ddot{y}}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(D_{\hat{B}}^{k}\right)^{-1}A_{\hat{B}}^{-1}\right)^{\top}\tilde{\ddot{z}},

\displaystyle\tilde{\ddot{s}}

\displaystyle=-A^{T}\tilde{\ddot{y}},

\displaystyle v_{2}^{k}

\displaystyle=\left(v_{\hat{B}}^{k},v_{\hat{N}}^{k}\right)=\left(D_{\hat{B}}^{k}\hat{r}_{2}^{k},0\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\ddot{x}}

\displaystyle=-(D^{k})^{2}\tilde{\ddot{s}}-2(S^{k})^{-1}\tilde{\dot{x}}\circ\tilde{\dot{s}}-v_{2}^{k}.

Using the inexact solutions obtained above,
the next iteration point on the ellipsoidal arc
\mathcal{E}^{k}
is computed with the following formula:

\displaystyle x^{k}(\alpha)=

\displaystyle\ x^{k}-\tilde{\dot{x}}\sin(\alpha)+\tilde{\ddot{x}}(1-\cos(\alpha)),

(37a)

\displaystyle y^{k}(\alpha)=

\displaystyle\ y^{k}-\tilde{\dot{y}}\sin(\alpha)+\tilde{\ddot{y}}(1-\cos(\alpha)),

(37b)

\displaystyle s^{k}(\alpha)=

\displaystyle\ s^{k}-\tilde{\dot{s}}\sin(\alpha)+\tilde{\ddot{s}}(1-\cos(\alpha)).

(37c)

To give the framework of the proposed method,
we prepare the following functions:

\displaystyle G_{i}^{k}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=x_{i}^{k}(\alpha)s_{i}^{k}(\alpha)-\gamma_{1}\mu_{k}(\alpha)\text{ for }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},

\displaystyle g^{k}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=x^{k}(\alpha)^{\top}s^{k}(\alpha)-(1-\sin(\alpha))(x^{k})^{\top}s^{k},

\displaystyle h^{k}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\left(1-(1-\beta)\sin(\alpha)\right)(x^{k})^{\top}s^{k}-x^{k}(\alpha)^{\top}s^{k}(\alpha).

Here,
h^{k}(\alpha)\geq 0
corresponds to the Armijo condition with respect to the duality gap
\mu
.
In Section
4
,
we will show that the sequence
\{(x^{k},y^{k},s^{k})\}
converges to an optimal solution by selecting a step size
\alpha
satisfying:

G_{i}^{k}(\alpha)\geq 0\text{ for }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\quad g^{k}(\alpha)\geq 0,\quad h^{k}(\alpha)\geq 0.

(38)

Under this condition,
the next lemma ensures that a next iteration point
(x^{k}(\alpha),y^{k}(\alpha),s^{k}(\alpha))
is in the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume a step length
\alpha\in(0,\pi/2]
satisfies
G_{i}^{k}(\alpha)\geq 0
and
g^{k}(\alpha)\geq 0

5.1.2
Solving LESs

To solve the LESs inexactly,
we employ the conjugate gradient (CG) method in Scipy.

There are many other iterative solvers for solving LESs,

The preliminary experiments showed that CG was the fastest inexact solver in Scipy for II-arc and II-line.

The proposed method uses the MNES formulation in Section

but preliminary experiments showed that MNES lacks numerical stability.
Specifically,
CG did not converge to a certain accuracy even when a preconditioner was employed.
The search direction also did not satisfy (

A possible cause is that the condition number of the coefficient matrix

for MNES is extremely worse than that for NES; it is known that the condition number of MNES can grow up to the square of that of NES
[
32

Therefore,

[

and choose the NES formulations (

As for the solution accuracy,

Conclusion

In this work,
we proposed an inexact infeasible arc-search interior-point method
(II-arc) for solving LPs.
In particular,
we showed that the proposed method achieves a smaller polynomial iteration complexity than II-line by a factor of
n^{0.5}
.
In the numerical experiments,
II-arc was demanded less iterations than II-line,
since the ellipsoidal arc in II-arc can approximate
the central path more adequetly than the straight line used in II-line.
In addition, this iteration reduction enabled
II-arc to reduce the computational time.

Since the primary objective of this paper was to propose
the inexact infeasible arc-search IPM and to discuss its convergence,
there remain rooms for performance improvements.
For example, the method can be implemented with
other programming languages (such as C and Julia).
In addition, inexact linear system solvers,
including
[
2
]
,
and their preconditioners should be investigated.
The sensitivity of the inexact linear system solvers
can also be discussed, in particular,
in the case of ill-conditioned input matrix
A
.
From the viewpoint of the reduction in the number of iterations,
the combination with Nesterov’s restarting strategy
[
17
]
or
feasible IPMs
[
29
,
27
]
can be a future topic.
