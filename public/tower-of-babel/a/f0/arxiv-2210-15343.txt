Change of measure in a Heston-Hawkes stochastic volatility model
David R. Baños, Salvador Ortiz-Latorre, Oriol Zamora Font

Abstract
We consider the stochastic volatility model obtained by adding a compound Hawkes process to the volatility of the well-known Heston model. A Hawkes process is a self-exciting counting process with many applications in mathematical finance, insurance, epidemiology, seismology and other fields. We prove a general result on the existence of a family of equivalent (local) martingale measures. We apply this result to a particular example where the sizes of the jumps are exponentially distributed. Keywords: stochastic volatility, change of measure, risk neutral measure, existence of equivalent martingale measure, non-Markovian model, Hawkes process, volatility with jumps. AMS classification MSC2020: 60G55, 60J76, 91G15, 60H10.



1
Introduction

Valuation of assets and financial derivatives constitutes one of the core subjects of modern financial mathematics. There have been several approaches to asset pricing all of which can be classified in two larger groups: equilibrium pricing and rational pricing. The latter gives rise to the commonly used methodology of pricing financial instruments by ruling out arbitrage opportunities. As it is well-known, the absence of arbitrage is closely related to the existence of a probability measure, so-called risk neutral measure, hereby denoted by
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Prices of derivatives are expectations under such measure. This connection is known as the fundamental theorem of asset pricing
[
10
]
.

In contrast, markets evolve in time and they do it under the so-called real world measure, hereby denoted by
\mathbb{P}
. While we can attempt to model market movements under
\mathbb{P}
, we also need the dynamics under
{\mathbb{Q}}
for pricing purposes. If we are only interested in pricing, then modelling under
{\mathbb{Q}}
and calibrating is possible by matching market prices to theoretical ones. However, for asset liability management, investors need often to assess their positions under
\mathbb{P}
. A common risk management practice is to compute the risk exposure as a way to set economic and regulatory capital levels.
[
27
]
shows that exposures computed under the risk neutral measure are essentially arbitrary. They depend on the choice of numéraire and can be manipulated by choosing a different numéraire. Even when choosing commonly used numéraires, these exposures can differ by a factor of two or more. Furthermore, a crucial feature when assessing risk exposures is their distribution. While models under
{\mathbb{Q}}
may have well-known tractable distributional properties, this need not be the case when doing the passage to the
\mathbb{P}
-world and vice versa. For instance, the Vasicek model for interest rates is invariant under a restricted family of measure changes. This is a sought property of the Vasicek model, but it does not need to hold true in other models.

It is a common practice in the literature to assume that such measure change exists and set up a model under the risk neutral measure. Nonetheless, such assumption is not innocuous and nonsensical results can occur if it is in fact not satisfied, see e.g. the discussion in
[
28
]
and the references therein. For instance,
[
6
]
and
[
26
]
show examples of models where no equivalent local martingale measure exist. See also
[
25
]
for conditions to check existence of equivalent martingale measures under Markovian models without jumps.

From the modelling perspective, we adopt a stochastic volatility model in a non-Markovian setting with jumps. The reason for this is twofold. The non-Markovianity can be justified by the clustering of volatility, see e.g.
[
9
]
. This is a well-known stylized fact of volatility that cannot be captured under Markovianity. Literature on non-Markovian stochastic volatility models is vast. Here we mention some works on fractional volatility models, which appear when using the fractional Brownian motion as a driving noise for the volatility. The main characteristics of such noise are the possibility to model short and long-range dependence due to the fractional decay of its auto-correlation function and the ability to generate trajectories which are Hölder continuous of index different from
1/2
, corresponding to the case of Brownian motion. Fractional models with long range dependence were first studied in
[
8
]
. Then, in
[
2
]
, Malliavin calculus is introduced to study the asymptotics of the implied volatility in general stochastic volatility models, including fractional models with long and short term dependence. The popularity of rough models (short term dependence models) started with the findings of
[
16
]
. Their name stemming from the roughness of the underlying noise in the stochastic evolution of volatility. Several works in this direction have been conducted, see e.g.
[
16
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
to mention a few. None of the aforementioned works discusses change of measure. In fact, it is not clear whether the volatility process in the rough Heston model (see e.g.
[
14
]
and
[
13
]
) is strictly positive, which is a necessary condition for a proper change of measure to be possible.

The rough Heston model extends the classical Heston model to a model with fractional noise. It is moreover proven in
[
12
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
20
]
that the rough Heston model can be obtained as a limit of Heston models where the noise is a pure jump process known as Hawkes process. The latter, justifies again our choice of model with a self-exciting Hawkes factor.

The inclusion of jumps in the volatility may not be so clear at first glance. Often, researchers include jumps in the stock process in order to capture sudden changes of asset returns, see for instance the works of
[
3
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
15
]
. Classical stochastic volatility models without jumps, such as the Heston model, have the property that returns conditional on volatility are normally distributed. This property fails to explain many features of asset price behaviour. By adding jumps in the returns one can model sudden changes such as economic crashes as well as having more freedom in the modelling of distributions of asset returns. Nevertheless, jumps in returns are transient, in other words, a jump in returns today has no impact on the future distribution of returns
[
15
]
. Hence, Markov models for jumps in the asset dynamics such as Poisson process are often used. On the other hand, volatility is highly persistent. If the dynamics is driven by a Brownian motion then volatility can only increase gradually by a sequence of small normally distributed increments. Self-exciting jumps in the volatility provide a rapidly moving persistent factor. This justifies our use of the Hawkes component in the volatility.

Several works support the presence of jumps in the diffusive volatility.
[
5
]
,
[
11
]
and
[
23
]
provide evidence for the presence of positive jumps in volatility. For example, the authors in
[
23
, Section 5.4]
conduct an empirical study on the higher moments of the volatility process seeking evidence of jumps in volatility, as firstly conjectured by
[
5
]
. Their findings indicate the possibility of jumps (with positive mean jump size) in the stochastic-volatility process or at least fatter tailed innovations in the volatility process. This justifies our choice of positive self-exciting jumps in our volatility, which we model by compounding a Hawkes process with independent jump sizes. The positivity of jumps also allows us to keep volatility from hitting zero with probability one but, on the other hand, the self-exciting property of the Hawkes process may cause the process to explode. For this reason we assume a stability property to prevent explosion, see
[
19
, Section 3.1.1]
.

In this work we look at a Heston-type stochastic volatility model with correlated Brownian motions and add a jump part to the volatility process, namely, a compound Hawkes process. We show that a passage from
\mathbb{P}
to
{\mathbb{Q}}
and vice versa is possible for a rich enough family of probability measures. It is worth noting that our model has three non-tradable noises and one tradable asset, being thus incomplete. This gives rise to non-unique choices of measure change. The proof of existence of equivalent martingale measures for the classical Heston model is conducted in
[
28
]
.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we present our stochastic volatility model with correlated Brownian noises and a compound Hawkes component in the volatility. We also present in this section some useful properties on the existence and positivity of the volatility process. In Section
3
we prove the main results in this paper. First, we study the integrability of the exponential of the integrated variance and then we prove the existence of (local) martingale measures in our model. Finally, in Appendix
A
, we prove some technical lemmas on the existence of solutions of ODEs that appear in the proof of the main results.

\displaystyle\frac{dS_{t}}{S_{t}}

\displaystyle dv_{t}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\left(v_{t}-\bar{v}\right)dt+\sigma\sqrt{v_{t}}dW_{t}+\eta dL_{t},

where

[

Proposition 2.1
.

Equation (

3
Risk neutral probability measures

To prove the existence of a family of risk neutral probability measures we follow the classical approach where we employ Girsanov’s theorem in connection with the Novikov’s condition. Thus, we need to study the integrability of the exponential of the integrated variance, that is, what values
c>0
, if any, satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(c\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du\right)\right]<\infty.

By Corollary
2.3
,
v
is strictly positive and the previous expectation is finite for
c\leqslant 0
but for our applications is essential that
c
can be strictly positive.
Looking at the proof of
[
28
, Lemma 3.1]
one can see that for
c\leqslant\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}
the following holds

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(c\int_{0}^{T}\widetilde{v}_{u}du\right)\right]<\infty,

where
\widetilde{v}
is the standard Heston volatility given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{v}_{t}=v_{0}-\kappa\int_{0}^{t}\left(\widetilde{v}_{s}-\bar{v}\right)ds+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\widetilde{v}_{s}}dW_{s}.

The procedure to prove that (
3.1
) holds is to show that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(c\int_{0}^{T}\widetilde{v}_{u}du\right)\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-(\kappa\bar{v})\Phi(0)-v_{0}\psi(0)\right)<\infty,

where
\Phi
and
\psi
satisfy the following Riccati ODE

(3.3)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\psi^{2}(t)+\kappa\psi(t)+c

\displaystyle-\Phi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\psi(t)

\displaystyle\psi(T)

\displaystyle=\Phi(T)=0.

Due to the jump contribution of the compound Hawkes process, this procedure is more delicate in our model. However we can obtain a similar bound as in (
3.2
) with an additional function that will be the solution of an ODE where the moment generating function of
J_{1}
and the Hawkes parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
are involved.

We start making an assumption on the moment generating function of
J_{1}
. We write
M_{J}
for the moment generating function of
J_{1}
, that is,
M_{J}(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(tJ_{1}\right)\right]
. Since
J_{1}
is strictly positive,
M_{J}
is well-defined at least on the interval
(-\infty,0]
.

Assumption 1
.

There exists
\epsilon_{J}>0
such that
M_{J}
is well defined on
(-\infty,\epsilon_{J})
and it is the maximal domain in the sense that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\epsilon_{J}^{-}}M_{J}(t)=\infty.

Since
\epsilon_{J}>0
, all positive moments of
J_{1}
are finite.

Note that this is the case of the gamma distribution, chi-squared distribution, uniform distribution and others.

We start studying the functions that will appear in a bound like (
3.2
) for our variance. To find the ODEs that define those functions, one can consider the process
M=\{M(t),t\in[0,T]\}
defined by

\displaystyle M(t)=\exp\left(F(t)+G(t)v_{t}+H(t)\lambda_{t}+c\int_{0}^{t}v_{u}du\right),

for some unknown functions
F,G,H\colon[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying
F(T)=G(T)=H(T)=0
. Note that

\displaystyle M(T)=\exp\left(c\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du\right).

Hence,
\mathbb{E}[M(T)]
is exactly the expectation that we want to study. Now, if we can find
F,G
and
H
such that
M
is a local martingale, since it is non-negative, it will be a supermartingale and then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(c\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}[M(T)]\leqslant M(0)=\exp\left(F(0)+G(0)v_{0}+H(0)\lambda_{0}\right),

where the last expression will be finite as long as
F,G
and
H
exist and are well defined on
[0,T]
.

To make
M
a local martingale one can apply Itô’s formula to the process
M
and equate all the drift terms to
0
. By this procedure one will obtain the ODEs for
F,G
and
H

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{m}:=\left\{\mathbb{Q}(a)\in\mathcal{E}:|a|<\min\left\{\frac{\sqrt{2c_{l}}}{2},\sqrt{c_{l}-\rho^{2}}\right\}\right\}

is a set of equivalent martingale measures.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{Q}(a)\in\mathcal{E}_{m}\subset\mathcal{E}
, by Theorem
3.6

\widehat{S}
is a
(\mathcal{F},\mathbb{Q}(a))
-local martingale with the following dynamics

\displaystyle\frac{d\widehat{S}_{t}}{\widehat{S}_{t}}=\sqrt{v_{t}}\left(\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}dB_{t}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}+\rho dW_{t}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{t}=S_{0}\exp\left(\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{v_{s}}dB_{s}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}+\rho\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{v_{s}}dW_{s}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}v_{s}ds\right).

Since
\widehat{S}
is a positive
\left(\mathcal{F},\mathbb{Q}(a)\right)
-local martingale with
\widehat{S}_{0}=S_{0}
, it is a
\left(\mathcal{F},\mathbb{Q}(a)\right)
-supermartingale and it is a
\left(\mathcal{F},\mathbb{Q}(a)\right)
-martingale if and only if

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}[\widehat{S}_{T}]=S_{0}.

Similarly as we did in Theorem

, we define the processes

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}:

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{v_{s}}dB_{s}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}-\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}v_{s}ds\right)

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}:

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\rho\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{v_{s}}dW_{s}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}v_{s}ds\right).

Thus,
\widehat{S}_{t}=S_{0}Y_{t}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}Z_{t}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}
. Using that
Z_{T}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}
is
\mathcal{F}_{T}^{W^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}}\vee\mathcal{F}_{T}^{L}
-measurable we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}[\widehat{S}_{T}]

\displaystyle=S_{0}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}\left[Y_{T}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}Z_{T}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}\right]=S_{0}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}\left[\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}\left[Y_{T}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}Z_{T}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}|\mathcal{F}_{T}^{W^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}}\vee\mathcal{F}_{T}^{L}\right]\right]

(3.17)

\displaystyle=S_{0}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}\left[Z_{T}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}\left[Y_{T}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}|\mathcal{F}_{T}^{W^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}}\vee\mathcal{F}_{T}^{L}\right]\right].

Since
\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du<\infty,{\mathbb{Q}(a)}
-a.s. and
v
is
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}}\vee\mathcal{F}_{t}^{L}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
-adapted,

\displaystyle Y_{T}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}|\mathcal{F}_{T}^{W^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}}\vee\mathcal{F}_{T}^{L}\sim\text{Lognormal}\left(-\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{s}ds,(1-\rho^{2})\int_{0}^{T}v_{s}ds\right)

and we have that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}[Y_{T}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}|\mathcal{F}_{T}^{W^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}}\vee\mathcal{F}_{T}^{L}]=1.
Replacing the last expression in (
3
) we obtain that

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}[\widehat{S}_{T}]=S_{0}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}[Z_{T}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}],
and, hence, we only need to check
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}[Z_{T}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}]=1
. We will prove that Novikov’s condition holds, that is,

(3.18)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du\right)\right]<\infty.

Differently from the proof of
[
28
, Theorem 3.6]
, we can not directly apply Proposition
3.5
with the volatility parameters
\kappa^{(a)}
and
\bar{v}^{(a)}
given in (
3.8
) to prove that the previous expectation is finite. One reason is that under
{\mathbb{Q}(a)}
the Brownian motion
W^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}
and the compound Hawkes process
L
are not longer independent because

\displaystyle dW_{t}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}=dW_{t}+a\sqrt{v_{t}}dt,

and, therefore, the dynamics of
v
is different under
{\mathbb{Q}(a)}
. In order to check
(\ref{condQ})
we note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du\right)\frac{d\mathbb{Q}(a)}{d\mathbb{P}}\right],

where

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbb{Q}(a)}{d\mathbb{P}}=Y_{T}^{(a)}Z_{T}^{(a)},

and
Y_{T}^{(a)}
and
Z_{T}^{(a)}
are given in the statement of Theorem
3.6
.

Repeating the same argument as in Theorem
3.6
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du\right)Y_{T}^{(a)}Z_{T}^{(a)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du\right)Y_{T}^{(a)}Z_{T}^{(a)}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{T}^{W}\vee\mathcal{F}_{T}^{L}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du\right)Z_{T}^{(a)}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{T}^{(a)}|\mathcal{F}_{T}^{W}\vee\mathcal{F}_{T}^{L}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du\right)Z_{T}^{(a)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-a\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{v_{s}}dW_{s}-\frac{1}{2}(a^{2}-\rho^{2})\int_{0}^{T}v_{s}ds\right)\right],

Then, adding and subtracting
a^{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{s}ds
in the exponential and applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-a\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{v_{s}}dW_{s}-\frac{1}{2}(a^{2}-\rho^{2})\int_{0}^{T}v_{s}ds\right)\right]=

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-a\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{v_{s}}dW_{s}-a^{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{s}ds\right)\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}(a^{2}+\rho^{2})\int_{0}^{t}v_{s}ds\right)\right]

(3.19)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-2a\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{v_{s}}dW_{s}-2a^{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{s}ds\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left((a^{2}+\rho^{2})\int_{0}^{t}v_{s}ds\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Note that the first factor in (
3
) is the expectation of a Doléans-Dade exponential. Since
|a|<\frac{\sqrt{2c_{l}}}{2}
(recall the choice of
a
in (

)),
2a^{2}<c_{l}
and by Proposition
3.5
, Novikov’s condition holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(2a^{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{s}ds\right)\right]<\infty.

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-2a\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{v_{s}}dW_{s}-2a^{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{s}ds\right)\right]=1.

For the second term in (

3.5
. We need

which is true because

. Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left((a^{2}+\rho^{2})\int_{0}^{t}v_{s}ds\right)\right]<\infty,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}v_{u}du\right)\right]<\infty.

We conclude that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}[Z_{T}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}]=1
,
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}[\widehat{S}_{T}]=S_{0}
and
\widehat{S}
is a
(\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{Q}(a)})
-martingale. Therefore,
\mathcal{E}_{m}
is a set of equivalent martingale measures.
∎

Lemma A.1
.

For

, define

,
\Lambda(c):=\frac{2\eta c\left(e^{D(c)T}-1\right)}{D(c)-\kappa+\left(D(c)+\kappa\right)e^{D(c)T}}

and

\displaystyle c_{l}:=\sup\left\{c\leqslant\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}:\Lambda(c)<\epsilon_{J}\hskip 8.5359pt\text{and}\hskip 8.5359ptM_{J}\left(\Lambda(c)\right)\leqslant\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)\right\}.

Then,

and for

,

(i)

The ODE

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}G^{2}(t)+\kappa G(t)-c

\displaystyle G(T)

\displaystyle=0

has a unique solution in the interval

(iv)

The ODE

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\beta H(t)-M_{J}\left(\eta G(t)\right)\exp\left(\alpha H(t)\right)+1

\displaystyle H(T)

\displaystyle=0

has a unique solution in
[0,T]
.

Proof.

We first check that
c_{l}>0
. Since

\displaystyle\lim_{c\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Lambda(c)=0,

there exist positive values of

satisfying the inequality
\Lambda(c)<\epsilon_{J}
. Using that
\alpha<\beta
, one can check that
\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)>1
. Since the limit in (

M_{J}(0)=1

is a continuous function there exist positive values of

Therefore,

. From now on, let

(i) To find the solution we can transform equation (

. To see that

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(t)=\frac{-4cD(c)^{2}e^{D(c)(T-t)}}{\left(D(c)-\kappa+\left(D(c)+\kappa\right)e^{D(c)(T-t)}\right)^{2}}<0.

\displaystyle U=M_{J}(\eta G(0))>M_{J}(\eta G(T))=M_{J}(0)=1.

Moreover, by definition of

\displaystyle U=M_{J}(\eta G(0))=M_{J}\left(\frac{2\eta c(e^{D(c)T}-1)}{D(c)-\kappa+(D(c)+\kappa)e^{D(c)T}}\right)=M_{J}(\Lambda(c))\leqslant\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right).

(iv) Let us make the change of variables

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=f(t,h(t))=M_{J}\left(\eta G(T-t)\right)\exp\left(\alpha h(t)\right)-\beta h(t)-1

\displaystyle h(0)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle f_{m}(x):=-\beta x-1\leqslant f(t,x)\leqslant U\exp\left(\alpha x\right)-\beta x-1=:f_{M}(x).

(A.6)

\displaystyle h_{M}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=f_{M}(h_{M}(t))=U\exp\left(\alpha h_{M}(t)\right)-\beta h_{M}(t)-1

\displaystyle=0

Now we focus on the ODE

\displaystyle=f_{m}(h_{m}(t))=-\beta h_{m}(t)-1

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\leqslant U|\exp\left(\alpha x_{1}\right)-\exp\left(\alpha x_{2}\right)|+\beta|x_{1}-x_{2}|

for some constant

. Then, by the Picard-Lindelöf theorem (see
[

, Chapter II, Theorem 1.1]
) there is a unique solution

. Moreover, by
[

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\epsilon^{-}}h(t)\in\left\{-\frac{1}{\beta}-1,x_{p}+1\right\}.

Corollary A.2
.

Define
c_{s}
by

(A.9)

\displaystyle c_{s}:=\min\left\{\frac{\kappa\epsilon_{J}}{2\eta},\frac{\kappa}{2\eta}M_{J}^{-1}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)\right),\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

. The function

\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)>1
and

M_{J}^{-1}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)\right)>0,
and we can conclude that

.

Recall that
0<c_{l}\leqslant\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}
and its definition

(A.10)

\displaystyle c_{l}=\sup\left\{c\leqslant\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}:\Lambda(c)<\epsilon_{J}\hskip 8.5359pt\text{and}\hskip 8.5359ptM_{J}\left(\Lambda(c)\right)\leqslant\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)\right\},

D(c)=\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}
and
\Lambda(c)=\frac{2\eta c\left(e^{D(c)T}-1\right)}{D(c)-\kappa+\left(D(c)+\kappa\right)e^{D(c)T}}
.

and

. The following inequality holds

(A.11)

\displaystyle\Lambda(c)=\frac{2\eta c\left(e^{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}T}-1\right)}{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}-\kappa+\left(\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}+\kappa\right)e^{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}T}}<\frac{2\eta c}{\kappa}.

\displaystyle\Lambda(c)=\frac{2\eta c\left(e^{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}T}-1\right)}{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}-\kappa+\left(\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}+\kappa\right)e^{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}T}}<\frac{2\eta c}{\kappa}

\displaystyle\iff\kappa\left(e^{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}T}-1\right)<\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}-\kappa+\left(\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}+\kappa\right)e^{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\sigma^{2}c}T}

and

. Note that the inequality in

∎

Proof.

(i) The moment generating function is given by
M_{J}(t)=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-t},t<\lambda.
Hence, with the notation of Assumption
1
,
\epsilon_{J}=\lambda
and the inverse of the
M_{J}
is given by
M_{J}^{-1}(t)=\lambda\left(1-\frac{1}{t}\right),t>0
. Then, applying Corollary
A.2
we have the following expression of
c_{s}

\displaystyle c_{s}=\min\left\{\frac{\kappa\lambda}{2\eta},\frac{\kappa\lambda}{2\eta}\left(1-\frac{1}{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)}\right),\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

Note that since
\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)>1
, we have

\displaystyle 0<1-\frac{1}{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)}<1.

We conclude that

\displaystyle c_{s}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\frac{\kappa\lambda}{2\eta},\frac{\kappa\lambda}{2\eta}\left(1-\frac{1}{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)}\right),\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\frac{\kappa\lambda}{2\eta}\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\exp\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right),\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

(ii) The moment generating function is given by
M_{J}(t)=\left(1-\frac{t}{\lambda}\right)^{-\mu},t<\lambda.
Thus,
\epsilon_{J}=\lambda
and the inverse of
M_{J}
is given by
M_{J}^{-1}(t)=\lambda\left(1-t^{-1/\mu}\right),t>0.
Then, applying Corollary
A.2
we have the following expression of
c_{s}

\displaystyle c_{s}=\min\left\{\frac{\kappa\lambda}{2\eta},\frac{\kappa\lambda}{2\eta}\left(1-\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)\right)^{-1/\mu}\right),\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

Note that since
\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)>1
, we have

\displaystyle 0<1-\frac{1}{\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)\right)^{1/\mu}}<1.

We conclude that

\displaystyle c_{s}=\min\left\{\frac{\kappa\lambda}{2\eta}\left(1-\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)\right)^{-1/\mu}\right),\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

(iii) The moment generating function is given by
M_{J}(t)=e^{tj}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus,
\epsilon_{J}=\infty
and the inverse of
M_{J}
is given by
M_{J}^{-1}(t)=\ln(t)/j,t>0
. Then, applying Corollary
A.2
we conclude that

\displaystyle c_{s}=\min\left\{\frac{\kappa}{2\eta j}\left(\ln\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right),\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

∎
