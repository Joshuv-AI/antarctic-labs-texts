The determining role of covariances in large networks of stochastic neurons
Vincent Painchaud, Patrick Desrosiers, Nicolas Doyon

Abstract
Biological neural networks are notoriously hard to model due to their stochastic behavior and high dimensionality. We tackle this problem by constructing a dynamical model of both the expectations and covariances of the fractions of active and refractory neurons in the network’s populations. We do so by describing the evolution of the states of individual neurons with a continuous-time Markov chain, from which we formally derive a low-dimensional dynamical system. This is done by solving a moment closure problem in a way that is compatible with the nonlinearity and boundedness of the activation function. Our dynamical system captures the behavior of the high-dimensional stochastic model even in cases where the mean-field approximation fails to do so. Taking into account the second-order moments modifies the solutions that would be obtained with the mean-field approximation, and can lead to the appearance or disappearance of fixed points and limit cycles. We moreover perform numerical experiments where the mean-field approximation leads to periodically oscillating solutions, while the solutions of the second-order model can be interpreted as an average taken over many realizations of the stochastic model. Altogether, our results highlight the importance of including higher moments when studying stochastic networks and deepen our understanding of correlated neuronal activity.



1  Introduction

Neuronal activity is intrinsically stochastic. Sources of randomness have been identified at different scales, ranging from spontaneous neurotransmitters release to noise amplification at the network level, and have been shown to contribute to cellular and behavioral trial-to-trial variability
(
Calvin & Stevens 1968
;
White et al. 2000
;
Faisal et al. 2008
)
. Although stochasticity has often been treated as a nuisance, recent studies have highlighted its determining role in neural population coding
(
Azeredo & Rieke 2021
)
and in many brain functions, such as awareness
(
Zerlaut & Destexhe 2017
)
, decision making
(
Lebovich et al. 2019
)
, and working memory
(
Schneegans et al. 2020
)
. Moreover, biologically realistic numerical simulations have demonstrated that stochastic networks can support spike-time coding with millisecond precision
(
Nolte et al. 2019
)
, while abstract models have revealed the computational advantage of stochastic spiking neurons over deterministic ones
(
Maass 2014
)
.

From a mathematical point of view, incorporating stochasticity into neural network models gives rise to major difficulties, especially when trying to make interpretable predictions about their large-scale dynamical behavior. To address this, a common practice is to perform mean-field approximations
(
Brunel 2000
;
Liley et al. 2001
;
Ermentrout & Terman 2010
;
Huang 2021
)
, which have long been used in statistical mechanics when studying phase transitions in spin systems
(
Domb 1960
;
LeBowitz 1968
)
. Perhaps the most important example of a mean-field model is the one developed by
Wilson & Cowan 1972
, building on the work of
Beurle 1956
and
Griffith 1963
;
Griffith 1965
. This model achieved immediate success due to its ability to represent hysteresis phenomena as well as oscillations in biological networks of excitatory and inhibitory neurons. It is still widely used today, sometimes as a firing-rate model
(
Ermentrout & Terman 2010
;
Gerstner 1995
;
Vogels et al. 2005
;
Keeley et al. 2019
)
, and has been the starting point of several extensions
(
Destexhe & Sejnowski 2009
;
Bressloff et al. 2016
;
Cowan et al. 2016
;
Chow & Karimipanah 2020
;
Wilson & Cowan 2021
)
.

As it is based on a mean-field approximation, the Wilson–Cowan model is restricted to modeling only the average behavior of neural networks, and cannot represent correlations between the states of different neurons. However, correlations have been found to be important in brain activity
(
Salinas & Sejnowski 2001
;
Schneidman et al. 2006
;
Averbeck et al. 2006
;
Panzeri et al. 2022
;
Azeredo & Rieke 2021
)
, and there has been ongoing effort to generalize the Wilson–Cowan model to take them into account
(
Chow & Karimipanah 2020
)
. An important example of such work is that started by
Buice & Cowan 2007
;
Buice & Cowan 2009
, later joined by Chow
(
Buice et al. 2010
;
Buice & Chow 2013
)
and followed by
Bressloff 2009
;
Bressloff 2015
and by
Touboul & Ermentrout 2011
. These authors used path integral methods to derive a master equation that generalizes the Wilson–Cowan model, and then exploited van Kampen system-size expansions to obtain second-order corrections. This approach has been applied to study the relationship between structure and activity in neural networks
(
Ocker et al. 2017
)
.

Although these methods have led to many results, it is not clear that when including refractoriness explicitly in the model, the resulting systems would be appropriate to represent a biological neural network’s activity. Indeed,
Painchaud 2021
used a different method to obtain a dynamical system analogous to others obtained by
Buice et al. 2010
and by
Touboul & Ermentrout 2011
, but which describes the evolution of both active and refractory fractions of neural populations, yet this system can have unbounded solutions which cannot have a biological interpretation. As there is experimental
(
Berry & Meister 1998
;
Avissar et al. 2013
)
and theoretical
(
Rule et al. 2019
;
Weistuch et al. 2021
)
evidence that refractoriness plays an important role in neuronal activity, it is relevant to retain this aspect in a model that also incorporates correlations between neuronal states.

Here, we propose a new way to generalize the Wilson--Cowan model to explicitly include refractory fractions of neural populations as well as covariances between their activities (including the variance of each activity).

Throughout the text, unless specified otherwise, we will use the term “covariances” to refer both to variances—since a variance is the covariance of a random variable with itself—and to covariances of distinct random variables.
Our main goal is to improve the quality of the mean-field model’s prediction of the network’s activity while keeping the model relatively simple. To construct our model, the idea is to first build a stochastic process to model the dynamics from the microscopic point of view of individual neurons, and then to use this process to obtain a nonautonomous differential equation that models the same dynamics, but from the macroscopic point of view of neural populations. This nonautonomous differential equation poses a moment closure problem (see
(
Kuehn 2016
)
for an extensive discussion), which can be solved while still considering both first- and second-order moments.

The moment closure framework has not been very popular in computational neuroscience so far, but it has been extensively used in other areas of mathematical biology during the last decades. In particular, it has been used to include covariances in compartmental models in epidemiology
(
Levin & Durrett 1996
;
Keeling 1999
;
Joo & Lebowitz 2004
;
Sharkey 2008
;
Cator & Van 2012
;
Kiss et al. 2015
)
as well as in population dynamics models in ecology
(
Matsuda et al. 1992
;
Satō et al. 1994
)
. One of the main advantages of this method is that it reveals precisely where covariances can have an effect on the dynamics. Moreover, it allows a more systematic treatment of the neurons’ refractory period, as it has been done by
Painchaud et al. 2022
.

The paper is organized as follows. First, we construct a continuous-time Markov chain that describes the evolution of the state of each node in a large network, the goal being to mimic the behavior of biological neurons. This Markov chain is similar to one already proposed (but not extensively studied) by
Cowan 1990
and is reminiscent of a process recently studied by
Zarepour et al. 2019
. Then, we split the network into a small set of large populations and derive a nonautonomous system of differential equations that describes the evolution of the expected state of each population. Using a new approximation of the expectation of a sigmoid function, we find a solution to the moment closure problem that involves covariances between population state variables. Finally, we present three examples that demonstrate the impacts of covariances. The first one illustrates how covariances can increase the accuracy when predicting the macroscopic behavior of the Markov chain. In the other two examples, the impact of including covariances in the dynamical system goes beyond error reduction: the second-order system reaches a steady state where covariances are nonzero, implying that this steady state is inaccessible to the mean-field model. The paper is complemented by three appendices in which we provide the details of several computations left out of the main text as well as additional figures.

2  The model

We seek to describe the dynamics of a large biological neural network from a macroscopic point of view in such a way that the resulting model generalizes Wilson–Cowan’s, but also includes correlations between states of neurons. We are also looking for a description that would depend on statistical properties of neuron parameters rather than on their precise values. To do so, we construct a Markov chain to describe the states of all neurons of the network. Then, we split the network into populations and we derive a dynamical system that describes the evolution of the states of populations. The model constructed in this section is also presented under slightly different assumptions in
(
Painchaud et al. 2022
)
, and it is a special case of a more general model presented in
(
Painchaud 2021
, Chapter 2)
.

2.1  A modelization of a biological neural network

We consider a network of

An entry

To study the network’s dynamics from a macroscopic point of view, we suppose that the neurons are split into a small number

2.3  A macroscopic approximation of the Markov chain’s behavior

The above Markov chain completely describes the dynamics of the network from a microscopic point of view. Now, we want to find an approximation of this Markov chain to describe the macroscopic behavior of the network. To make this goal more precise, we introduce for each population
J\in{P}
the processes

A_{t}^{J}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j},\quad R_{t}^{J}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{j},\quad S_{t}^{J}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr),

(2.2)

which are the fractions of neurons in population
J
that are in the active, refractory and sensitive state respectively. Our goal is to find a dynamical system to describe the evolution of

\mathcal{A}_{J}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[A_{t}^{J}],\qquad\mathcal{R}_{J}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[R_{t}^{J}],\qquad\mathcal{S}_{J}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[S_{t}^{J}],

(2.3)

where
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}
denotes the expectation on
(\Omega,{F},\mathbb{P}^{\eta})
.

A first step towards an understanding of the evolution of these expected fractions of populations is a description of the evolution of the probabilities

\displaystyle p_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}^{j}=1]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}\big],

\displaystyle r_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}^{j}=i]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{j}\big],

\displaystyle q_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}^{j}=0]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\big].

By considering these probabilities at a time
t+\Delta t
and conditioning over all events
\{X_{t}=x\}

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\beta_{j}(\eta)p_{j}(t)+\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)\big],

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)\big]+\gamma_{j}(\eta)r_{j}(t),

(2.4b)

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{j}(\eta)r_{j}(t)+\beta_{j}(\eta)p_{j}(t).

(2.4c)

The details are given in Appendix

. We use these differential equations as the starting point to describe the evolution of the expected fractions of populations defined in

2.3
. Remark that this microscopic model has essentially the same form as models based on firing rates, except for the presence of the factor

To obtain expressions for the derivatives of
\mathcal{A}_{J}
,
\mathcal{R}_{J}
and
\mathcal{S}_{J}
, the idea is to average the derivatives given in

2.4
over

using the linearity of expectation and derivatives. To avoid overcomplicating the discussion, we give here only the main ideas of the arguments that lead us to the differential equations. The complete details, including the limits of the approximations that are made, are given in Appendix
A
. To obtain an expression for
\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}
, we start from the average

\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}(t)=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\bigg[\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\Bigl(-\beta_{j}(\eta)\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}+a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)\Bigr)\bigg].

Assuming that each population is large, the law of large numbers now motivates to approximate the averages of transition rates over populations by their expectations. This yields the approximation

\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{j}(\eta)\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}\approx\beta_{J}A_{t}^{J}

where
\beta_{J}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\beta_{j}]
for
j\in J
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}
being the expectation on
(H,{H},\mu)
. The activation rates are handled in the same way, but since these rates are proportional to step functions of the difference between neuronal inputs and thresholds, their expectations are proportional to the cumulative distribution functions
F_{\theta_{J}}
of the thresholds in population
J
evaluated at a population-averaged input:

\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)\approx\alpha_{J}F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}

where
\alpha_{J}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\alpha_{j}]
and

B_{t}^{J}\vcentcolon=\sum_{K\in{P}}c_{JK}A_{t}^{K}+Q_{J}\quad\text{with}\quad c_{JK}\vcentcolon=\lvert K\rvert\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[W_{jk}]

for
j\in J
and
k\in K
.

This method results in approximate expressions for the derivatives of
\mathcal{A}_{J}
,
\mathcal{R}_{J}
and
\mathcal{S}_{J}
when populations are large. It ultimately leads to model the macroscopic dynamics of the network by the differential equations

\displaystyle\SwapAboveDisplaySkip\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}(t)

\displaystyle=-\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}(t)+\alpha_{J}\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}],

(2.5a)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{J}(t)

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{J}\mathcal{R}_{J}(t)+\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}(t),

(2.5b)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{J}(t)

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{J}\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}]+\gamma_{J}\mathcal{R}_{J}(t),

(2.5c)

where
\gamma_{J}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\gamma_{j}]
for
j\in J
. For each population, one of these three equations is redundant since
A_{t}^{J}+R_{t}^{J}+S_{t}^{J}\equiv 1
for all

. Thus, if the network has
n

eq.

2.5
corresponds to
2n

A crucial aspect of
eq.

2.5
is that it is not autonomous: there is an explicit time dependence in the expectation
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}]
, which is additionally an unknown function. Further assumptions or approximations are required to obtain an autonomous system—this corresponds to a moment closure problem
(
Kuehn 2016
)
. The simplest way to close the system would be to use the mean-field approximation
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}]\approx F_{\theta_{J}}\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{J}(t)\bigr)\mathcal{S}_{J}(t)
, where
\mathcal{B}_{J}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[B_{t}^{J}]
. This results in the dynamical system

\displaystyle\SwapAboveDisplaySkip\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}+\alpha_{J}F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J},

(2.6a)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{J}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{J}\mathcal{R}_{J}+\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J},

(2.6b)

which is closed since for each population
J
,
\mathcal{S}_{J}
and
\mathcal{B}_{J}
are functions of expectations of active and refractory fractions. Since the approximation used to close the system does not depend on the specific choice of parameters
\eta\in H
, any explicit dependence on
\eta
disappeared. Thus, the macroscopic dynamics predicted by the model only depend on the averages of parameters over populations. This will be the case for any macroscopic model obtained from a closure of the system given in
eq.

2.5
, as long as the approximations used to close the system do not depend explicitly on
\eta
.

The mean-field system given in
eq.

2.6
has been studied in detail by
Painchaud et al. 2022
where it is shown to be closely related to Wilson–Cowan’s equations
(
Wilson & Cowan 1972
)
: if the refractory fractions are forced to their equilibrium solutions
\mathcal{R}_{J}=\frac{\beta_{J}}{\gamma_{J}}\mathcal{A}_{J}
, the resulting system is equivalent to Wilson–Cowan’s. In the next section, we go further and present a solution to the moment closure problem posed by
eq.

2.5
that includes second-order moments, with the goal of improving the mean-field model’s predictions of the expectations of the active, refractory and sensitive fractions of each population.

3  A second-order solution to the moment closure problem

We are looking for a solution to the moment closure problem posed by
eq.

2.5

\mathrm{C}_{YZ}^{JK}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathrm{Cov}[Y_{t}^{J},Z_{t}^{K}]=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[Y_{t}^{J}Z_{t}^{k}]-\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[Y_{t}^{J}]\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[Z_{t}^{K}],

where

and
Z
stand for either
A
,
R
,
S
or
B
, and

. Then, with the method we used above to find

, it can be shown (and this is done in Appendix

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{C}}_{AA}^{JK}(t)

\displaystyle=-(\beta_{J}+\beta_{K})\mathrm{C}_{AA}^{JK}(t)+\alpha_{K}\mathrm{Cov}[A_{t}^{J},F_{\theta_{K}}(B_{t}^{K})S_{t}^{K}]+\alpha_{J}\mathrm{Cov}[A_{t}^{K},F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}],

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{C}}_{RR}^{JK}(t)

\displaystyle=-(\gamma_{J}+\gamma_{K})\mathrm{C}_{RR}^{JK}(t)+\beta_{K}\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{KJ}(t)+\beta_{J}\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{JK}(t),

(3.1b)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{C}}_{AR}^{JK}(t)

\displaystyle=-(\beta_{J}+\gamma_{K})\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{JK}(t)+\beta_{K}\mathrm{C}_{AA}^{JK}(t)+\alpha_{J}\mathrm{Cov}[R_{t}^{K},F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}].

(3.1c)

Thus, to obtain a closed dynamical system involving only first and second moments of active and refractory fractions, we need to find approximations to expectations of the forms
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}]
,
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}A_{t}^{K}]
and
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})S_{t}^{J}R_{t}^{K}]
in terms of expectations and covariances of active and refractory fractions of populations. Remark that, since sensitive fractions and inputs are expressed in terms of active and refractory fractions by linear relations, the bilinearity of covariance implies that any covariance of the form
\mathrm{C}_{YZ}^{JK}
with
Y
and
Z
being
A
,
R
,
S
or
B
can be expressed using active and refractory fractions only.

In order to lighten notation, the time dependence will be kept implicit in the remainder of the section. For instance, we will write
B^{J}
instead of
B_{t}^{J}
.

3.1  The naive approach and the need to go further

The simplest solution to this moment closure problem is to assume that
F_{\theta_{J}}
is smooth enough and to use local approximations given by Taylor expansions to write the problematic expectations in terms of central moments of the dynamical variables. Then, all central moments of order higher than 2 can be neglected to close the system. Indeed, expanding
F_{\theta_{J}}
around the expectation
\mathcal{B}_{J}
of
B^{J}
and assuming that
F_{\theta_{J}}
is regular enough so that the expectation can be distributed in the series,

\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}F_{\theta_{J}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J}\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[(B^{J}-\mathcal{B}_{J})^{k}\big]+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}F_{\theta_{J}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[(S^{J}-\mathcal{S}_{J})(B^{J}-\mathcal{B}_{J})^{k}\big].

Now, all terms with
k\geq 3
in the first series or with
k\geq 2
in the second series are proportional to central moments of order at least 3. Neglecting these terms and keeping only the nonzero remaining ones, we find the approximation

\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}]\approx F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J}+\frac{1}{2}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J}\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}+F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{JJ}.

(3.2)

As
B^{J}
and
S^{J}
are related to active and refractory fractions of populations by linear relations, using this approximation in
eq.

2.5a
allows to write the derivative
\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}
in terms of
\mathcal{A}_{J}
’s and
\mathcal{R}_{J}
’s only.

The same method can be used on the covariances in the differential equations (
3.1
), and leads to

\mathrm{Cov}[A^{J},F_{\theta_{K}}(B^{K})S^{K}]\approx F_{\theta_{K}}(\mathcal{B}_{K})\mathrm{C}_{AS}^{JK}+F_{\theta_{K}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{K})\mathrm{C}_{AB}^{JK}\mathcal{S}_{K}

(3.3)

as well as a similar one in which
A
is replaced with
R
.

Using the approximations given in
eqs.

3.2
and
3.3
in the system given by
eqs.

2.5
and
3.1
leads to the system

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}+\alpha_{J}F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J}+\alpha_{J}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{JJ}+\frac{\alpha_{J}}{2}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J}\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ},

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{J}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{J}\mathcal{R}_{J}+\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J},

(3.4b)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\mathrm{C}}_{AA}^{JK}&=-(\beta_{J}+\beta_{K})\mathrm{C}_{AA}^{JK}+\alpha_{K}F_{\theta_{K}}(\mathcal{B}_{K})\mathrm{C}_{AS}^{JK}+\alpha_{K}F_{\theta_{K}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{K})\mathcal{S}_{K}\mathrm{C}_{AB}^{JK}\\
&\hskip 125.19212pt+\alpha_{J}F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathrm{C}_{AS}^{KJ}+\alpha_{J}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J}\mathrm{C}_{AB}^{KJ},\end{split}

(3.4c)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{C}}_{RR}^{JK}

\displaystyle=-(\gamma_{J}+\gamma_{K})\mathrm{C}_{RR}^{JK}+\beta_{K}\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{KJ}+\beta_{J}\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{JK},

(3.4d)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{C}}_{AR}^{JK}

\displaystyle=-(\beta_{J}+\gamma_{K})\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{JK}+\beta_{K}\mathrm{C}_{AA}^{JK}+\alpha_{J}F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathrm{C}_{RS}^{KJ}+\alpha_{J}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J}\mathrm{C}_{RB}^{KJ},

(3.4e)

which is a closed dynamical system since the linear relations
S^{J}=1-A^{J}-R^{J}
and
B^{J}=\sum_{K\in{P}}c_{JK}A^{K}
allow to write all covariances in terms of covariances between active and refractory fractions of populations.

This system has already been studied in detail by
Painchaud 2021
, and is reminiscent of other ones that appear in the literature. In particular, neglecting the refractory fractions as well as the factors corresponding to sensitive fractions in the activation terms, the system becomes the infinite-size system of
Touboul & Ermentrout 2011
, whose one-population case had previously been derived by
Buice et al. 2010
.

Figure 2:
Solution of the dynamical system from
eq.

3.4
with exponentially diverging variances, an inadmissible behavior. The parameters and initial state were chosen as in the example of Section
4.2
.

Nevertheless, this solution to the moment closure problem does not seem to be the most appropriate one, at least when refractory fractions of populations are to be included. Indeed,
Painchaud 2021
found examples in which the solutions of
eq.

3.4
have components that blow up. An example is given on Figure
2
, which depicts a solution whose components corresponding to variances diverge exponentially. In such cases, it is clear that the model cannot be interpreted biologically. This failure could be explained by the fact that the Taylor approximation from which the system is derived should only hold locally, when covariances are small. As there is no guarantee that the dynamical system will keep small covariances small, one could expect this to be a problem, especially since the right-hand sides of
eqs.

3.2
and
3.3
are unbounded (with respect to covariances), contrary to the functions they seek to approximate. It might be possible to solve this problem by including higher order terms in the dynamical system, in order to improve the accuracy of the approximations made in
eqs.

3.2
and
3.3
, but this would also have the effect to enlarge the system’s dimension. With the goal of improving the approximation without enlarging the dimension, this leads us to propose a different solution to the moment closure problem, which we build around a function that approximates the expectation of a sigmoid function of a random variable in terms of its first and second moments.

3.2  Approximation of a sigmoid function’s expectation

The difficulty in approximating the expectations
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}]
or
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}A^{K}]
comes, to a large extent, from the cumulative distribution function
F_{\theta_{J}}
. As a first step, we construct an approximation of the expectation of
F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})
only. To do so, we assume that in each population, the thresholds follow a symmetric, unimodal distribution, so that
F_{\theta_{J}}
has a sigmoid shape with inflection point at the mean
\theta_{J}
.

To construct our approximation, we ask a simple question: if the random variable
B^{J}
follows a symmetric distribution with mean
\mathcal{B}_{J}
and a small variance
\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}
, how should the expectation of
F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})
compare to
F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})
? It is not hard to find a qualitative answer. Indeed, when
\mathcal{B}_{J}<\theta_{J}
, the fact that
F_{\theta_{J}}
increases faster to the right of
\mathcal{B}_{J}
than it decreases to its left has the effect that
F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})
must underestimate the actual expectation of
F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})
. This effect is reversed when
\mathcal{B}_{J}>\theta_{J}
. This is sketched on Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
Example of comparison between
F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})
, the actual expectation
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})]
, and our approximation
G_{J}(\mathcal{B}_{J},\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ})
for a fixed variance
\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}
, assuming the thresholds follow a logistic distribution with mean
\theta_{J}
and scaling factor
s_{\theta_{J}}
. To compute
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})]
, we assumed as a heuristic device that
B^{J}
follows a logistic distribution with mean
\mathcal{B}_{J}
and scaling factor
s_{\theta_{J}}
as well, but this choice was made for illustration purposes only.

Observe from Figure
3
that the deformation of
F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})
into
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})]
resembles an increase in the standard deviation of the distribution of which
F_{\theta_{J}}
is the cumulative distribution function. If
\sigma_{\theta_{J}}
denotes the standard deviation of a threshold
\theta_{j}
of population
J
, then increasing the standard deviation of
\theta_{j}
by a factor
1+a
for some
a>0
corresponds to changing
\theta_{j}
to
\theta_{j}(1+a)-\theta_{J}a
. The cumulative distribution functions of the two cases can be related as

\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[\theta_{j}(1+a)-\theta_{J}a\leq x]=\mathbb{P}^{\eta}\Big[\theta_{j}\leq\frac{x+\theta_{J}a}{1+a}\Big].

Therefore, our previous observations suggest to look for an approximation of the form

\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})]\approx G_{J}\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{J},\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}\bigr)

(3.5)

with

G_{J}(b,v)\vcentcolon=F_{\theta_{J}}\Bigl(\frac{b+\theta_{J}g_{J}(b,v)}{1+g_{J}(b,v)}\Bigr),

(3.6)

for some
g_{J}\colon\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
to be determined.

We can find a candidate for
g_{J}
based on what
G_{J}
should be for small variances. Indeed, if the variance
\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}
of
B^{J}
is small, then a second-order Taylor approximation of
F_{\theta_{J}}
should provide a reasonable approximation of
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})]
, so that

\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})]\approx F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})+\frac{1}{2}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}.

(3.7)

For the approximation given by
eq.

3.5
to be consistent with this, it must be that

G_{J}(b,0)=F_{\theta_{J}}(b)\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{2}G_{J}(b,0)=\frac{1}{2}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(b)

for any
b\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\partial_{2}G_{J}
denotes the partial derivative of
G_{J}
with respect to its second argument. Computing this derivative and using the two conditions yields

\frac{1}{2}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(b)=F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(b)(\theta_{J}-b)\partial_{2}g_{J}(b,0),

leading us to define

g_{J}(b,v)\vcentcolon=\frac{v}{2(\theta_{J}-b)}\frac{F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(b)}{F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(b)}

(3.8)

for
b\neq\theta_{J}
, assuming that
F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}>0
. As long as the distribution of the thresholds in population
J
is unimodal and symmetric,
F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(b)
always has the same sign as
\theta_{J}-b
, and in particular
F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{J})=0
. Hence,
g_{J}
can be continuously extended to
b=\theta_{J}
by definition of the derivative (provided
F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime\prime}(\theta_{J})
exists), and it is nonnegative on
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
.

For the approximation given by
eq.

3.5
with
g_{J}
defined as above to make sense, we must ensure that
G_{J}
has several properties. First,
G_{J}\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{J},\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}\bigr)
should still be consistent with
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})]
in extreme cases: if
B^{J}
has zero variance, then it should be possible to replace it with its mean, while if it has infinite variance then
F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})
should be
0
or
1
with
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
–
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
probabilities. Additionally, if the expected input
\mathcal{B}_{J}
goes to
+\infty
or
-\infty
, then
F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})
should go to
1
or
0
regardless of the variance. Moreover, since
F_{\theta_{J}}
is increasing,
G_{J}\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{J},\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}\bigr)
should always increase with
\mathcal{B}_{J}
if the variance is fixed.

Adding some assumptions on the thresholds’ distribution allows to prove that
G_{J}
indeed has all of the properties enumerated above, in addition to being consistent with the Taylor approximation of
F_{\theta_{J}}
given in
eq.

3.7
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that the thresholds in population
J
follow a unimodal and symmetric distribution with mean
\theta_{J}
and cumulative distribution function
F_{\theta_{J}}
. Let
g_{J}
and
G_{J}
be defined by
eqs.

3.8
and
3.6
respectively. Suppose that

1
)

F_{\theta_{J}}
is
{C}^{4}
on
\mathbb{R}
;

2
)

F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}>0
;

3
)

g_{J}(\cdot,1)
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
;

4
)

\forall b\in\mathbb{R}
,
g_{J}(b,1)+(\theta_{J}-b)\partial_{1}g_{J}(b,1)\geq 0
.

Then
G_{J}
satisfies the following conditions.

i
)

G_{J}
is
{C}^{1}
on
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
;

ii
)

G_{J}(\cdot,0)=F_{\theta_{J}}
and
\forall b\in\mathbb{R},G_{J}(b,v)\to\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
as
v\to\infty
;

iii
)

\forall v\geq 0,G_{J}(b,v)\to 0
as
b\to-\infty
and
G_{J}(b,v)\to 1
as
b\to\infty
;

iv
)

\forall v\geq 0
,
G_{J}(\cdot,v)
is increasing;

v
)

\partial_{2}G_{J}(\cdot,0)=\frac{1}{2}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}
.

The proof is given in Appendix
B
. Assumption
4)
does not have a simple interpretation, but we use it to prove that the maps
G_{J}(\cdot,v)
are increasing, and it is fairly easy to verify for given distributions. In particular, we show in Appendix
B
that if the thresholds follow either a normal or a logistic distribution, then all assumptions hold. An example of the function
G_{J}(\cdot,v)
for a fixed variance
v
is shown on Figure
3
.

3.3  The moment closure

Approximations to expectations of the form
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}]
and
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}A^{K}]
can be constructed using the function
G_{J}
from the previous section. We start by giving an approximation of
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}]
in terms of the expectations
\mathcal{S}_{J}
and
\mathcal{B}_{J}
as well as the covariance
\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{JJ}
and the variance
\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}
. As in the simpler case of the expectation of
F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})
, the approximation of
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}]
must meet some requirements. First, the difference between the approximation and
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})]\mathcal{S}_{J}
should approximate the covariance between
F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})
and
S^{J}
, so its sign should always follow that of the covariance
\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{JJ}
because
F_{\theta_{J}}
is increasing. Then, if
\mathcal{B}_{J}
goes to
+\infty
or
-\infty
,
F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})
should go to either
1
or
0
, so
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}]
should go to either
\mathcal{S}_{J}
or
0
. Finally, the approximation we obtained in
eq.

3.2
from a Taylor approximation of
F_{\theta_{J}}
,

\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}]\approx F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J}+\frac{1}{2}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathcal{S}_{J}\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}+F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{JJ},

should be valid when covariances are small. This gives conditions on the value of the approximation and of its partial derivatives when
\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{JJ}=\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}=0
.

Now, the function
G_{J}
defined in
eq.

3.6
can be used to construct the desired approximation. The following result follows from Theorem
1
and is proven in Appendix
B
.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose that all assumptions of Theorem
1
hold. Then
f\colon(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to(0,\infty)
defined by

f(s,b,c,v)\vcentcolon=sG_{J}\Bigl(b+\frac{c}{s},v\Bigr)

satisfies the following conditions for any
s>0
,
b,c\in\mathbb{R}
and
v\geq 0
.

i
)

f
is
{C}^{1}
on
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
;

ii
)

f(s,b,c,v)\gtreqless sG_{J}(b,v)
when
c\gtreqless 0
, and in particular
f(s,b,0,v)=sG_{J}(b,v)
and
f(s,b,0,0)=sF_{\theta_{J}}(b)
;

iii
)

f(s,b,c,v)\to 0
as
b\to-\infty
and
f(s,b,c,v)\to s
as
b\to\infty
;

iv
)

\partial_{3}f(s,b,0,0)=F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(b)
;

v
)

\partial_{4}f(s,b,0,0)=\frac{1}{2}sF_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(b)
.

These results motivate the approximation

\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}]\approx\mathcal{S}_{J}G_{J}\Bigl(\mathcal{B}_{J}+\frac{\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{JJ}}{\mathcal{S}_{J}},\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}\Bigr).

(3.9)

Approximations to
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}A^{K}]
and
\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}R^{K}]
can be constructed in a similar way. Now, the only obvious conditions that the approximation should satisfy are those related to the Taylor approximation of
F_{\theta_{J}}
. Indeed, neglecting third central moments as before,

\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}A^{K}]\approx\bigl(\mathcal{A}_{K}\mathcal{S}_{J}+\mathrm{C}_{AS}^{KJ}\bigr)F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\\
+\bigl(\mathcal{A}_{K}\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{JJ}+\mathcal{S}_{J}\mathrm{C}_{AB}^{KJ}\bigr)F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{J})+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}_{K}\mathcal{S}_{J}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{J})\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ},

which should hold when covariances are small. The appropriate approximation is defined in the following result, which follows from Theorem
1
and is proven in Appendix
B
.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose that all assumptions of Theorem
1
hold. Then
f\colon(0,\infty)^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{5}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f(x,s,b,c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},v)\vcentcolon=(xs+c_{1})G_{J}\Bigl(b+\frac{c_{2}}{x}+\frac{c_{3}}{s},v\Bigr)

satisfies the following conditions for any
x,s>0
,
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
v\geq 0
.

i
)

f
is
{C}^{1}
on
(0,\infty)^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{5}
;

ii
)

f(x,s,b,0,0,0,v)=xsG_{J}(b,v)
, and in particular
f(x,s,b,0,0,0,0)=xsF_{\theta_{J}}(b)
;

iii
)

\partial_{4}f(x,s,b,0,0,0,0)=F_{\theta_{J}}(b)
;

iv
)

\partial_{5}f(x,s,b,0,0,0,0)=sF_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(b)
;

v
)

\partial_{6}f(x,s,b,0,0,0,0)=xF_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(b)
;

vi
)

\partial_{7}f(x,s,b,0,0,0,0)=\frac{1}{2}xsF_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(b)
.

These results motivate the approximation

\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[F_{\theta_{J}}(B^{J})S^{J}A^{K}]\approx\bigl(\mathcal{A}_{K}\mathcal{S}_{J}+\mathrm{C}_{AS}^{KJ}\bigr)G_{J}\Bigl(\mathcal{B}_{J}+\frac{\mathrm{C}_{AB}^{KJ}}{\mathcal{A}_{K}}+\frac{\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{JJ}}{\mathcal{S}_{J}},\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}\Bigr)

(3.10)

as well as a similar one where
A
is replaced with
R
.

3.4  The dynamical system

The last approximation together with
eq.

3.9
yield a closed dynamical system:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}+\alpha_{J}\mathcal{S}_{J}G_{J}\Bigl(\mathcal{B}_{J}+\frac{\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{JJ}}{\mathcal{S}_{J}},\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}\Bigr),

(3.11a)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{R}}_{J}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{J}\mathcal{R}_{J}+\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J},

(3.11b)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\mathrm{C}}_{AA}^{JK}&=-(\beta_{J}+\beta_{K})\mathrm{C}_{AA}^{JK}+\alpha_{K}H_{K}(\mathcal{A}_{J},\mathcal{S}_{K},\mathcal{B}_{K},\mathrm{C}_{AS}^{JK},\mathrm{C}_{AB}^{JK},\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{KK},\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{KK})\\
&\hskip 125.19212pt+\alpha_{J}H_{J}(\mathcal{A}_{K},\mathcal{S}_{J},\mathcal{B}_{J},\mathrm{C}_{AS}^{KJ},\mathrm{C}_{AB}^{KJ},\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{JJ},\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}),\end{split}

(3.11c)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{C}}_{RR}^{JK}

\displaystyle=-(\gamma_{J}+\gamma_{K})\mathrm{C}_{RR}^{JK}+\beta_{K}\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{KJ}+\beta_{J}\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{JK},

(3.11d)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{C}}_{AR}^{JK}

\displaystyle=-(\beta_{J}+\gamma_{K})\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{JK}+\beta_{K}\mathrm{C}_{AA}^{JK}+\alpha_{J}H_{J}(\mathcal{R}_{K},\mathcal{S}_{J},\mathcal{B}_{J},\mathrm{C}_{RS}^{KJ},\mathrm{C}_{RB}^{KJ},\mathrm{C}_{SB}^{JJ},\mathrm{C}_{BB}^{JJ}),

(3.11e)

where

H_{J}(x,s,b,c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},v)\vcentcolon=(xs+c_{1})G_{J}\Bigl(b+\frac{c_{2}}{x}+\frac{c_{3}}{s},v\Bigr)-xsG_{J}\Bigl(b+\frac{c_{3}}{s},v\Bigr).

For a network of
n
populations, there are
2n
equations for expectations of active and refractory fractions,
n(n+1)
equations for covariances between two active or two refractory fractions (including variances), and
n^{2}
equations for covariances between an active and a refractory fraction. Hence, the whole system has
n(2n+3)
dimensions.

This dynamical system can be interpreted from the definition of the function
G_{J}
. In particular,
eq.

3.11a
has the same form as the corresponding equation in the mean-field system, but the activation function is stretched horizontally by the variance of the input and shifted by the covariance between the input and the sensitive fraction. The derivatives of covariances have similar interpretations.

Corollaries to Theorem
1
guarantee that the functions
G_{J}
and
H_{J}
are always
{C}^{1}
on the relevant domains. Therefore, the vector field corresponding to
eq.

3.11
is
{C}^{1}
on
(0,1)^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n(2n+1)}
. The Picard–Lindelöf theorem then shows the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the differential equation for any initial condition in
(0,1)^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n(2n+1)}
. However, not every initial condition in this domain can be interpreted in terms of the underlying Markov chain, since the boundedness of the fractions of populations implies various bounds on the covariances, for example through the Bhatia–Davis inequality
(
Bhatia & Davis 2000
)
or the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Given the various conditions that relate all covariances together, it is not obvious to usefully characterize the domain where solutions certainly make sense from the point of view of the underlying Markov chain, but we do not expect this to be a problem as long as covariances remain small.

It is worth noting the following result about the case where all covariances are zero.

Proposition 1
.

The domain
{D}\vcentcolon=(0,1)^{2n}\times\{0\}^{n(2n+1)}
, where
\mathrm{C}_{AA}^{JK}=\mathrm{C}_{RR}^{JK}=\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{JK}=0
for all
J,K\in{P}
, is invariant under the flow of the dynamical system given in
eq.

3.11
.

Proof.

If
\mathrm{C}_{AA}^{JK}=\mathrm{C}_{RR}^{JK}=\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{JK}=0
for all
J,K\in{P}
, then all other covariances are zero as well. It is then clear from the definition of the functions
H_{J}
and from
eq.

3.11
that
\dot{\mathrm{C}}_{AA}^{JK}=\dot{\mathrm{C}}_{RR}^{JK}=\dot{\mathrm{C}}_{AR}^{JK}=0
. Hence, on the domain
{D}
, the vector field corresponding to the dynamical system is parallel to
{D}
, which implies its invariance.
∎

Proposition
1
has an important consequence, since by Theorem
1
,
G_{J}(\mathcal{B}_{J},0)=F_{\theta_{J}}(\mathcal{B}_{J})
. Indeed, it follows that if all covariances are forced to zero, the system reduces to the mean-field system given in
eq.

2.6
. This shows that we can see the mean-field system as a subsystem of that given in
eq.

3.11
. In particular, any steady state of the mean-field system is also a steady state of the second-order model in which all covariances are zero. Moreover, since the mean-field system can be seen as an extension of Wilson–Cowan’s system
(
Painchaud et al. 2022
)
, this means that our second-order system is also an extension of Wilson–Cowan’s.

4  Examples

We now illustrate how adding covariances can lead to a better prediction of the underlying Markov chain’s macroscopic behavior. We first give an example where the second-order model correctly predicts the steady state to which trajectories of the underlying Markov chain converge, whereas the mean-field model does not. Then, we present two examples in which the second-order model converges to a steady state with nonzero covariances that cannot be described by the mean-field model. In both cases, while the mean-field model is able to predict some aspects of the Markov chain’s macroscopic dynamics, the second-order model provides more information, leading to a better prediction of the Markov chain’s behavior.

4.1  A word on the methodology

In all examples, we compare the solutions of the mean-field and second-order models with trajectories of the underlying Markov chain obtained using the Doob–Gillespie algorithm
(
Gillespie 1976
;
Gillespie 1977
)
. To produce a meaningful comparison between the results of macroscopic and microscopic models, it is crucial to ensure that the microscopic parameters and initial states are consistent with the macroscopic ones.

For simplicity, we take the microscopic parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma
constant over populations and equal to their macroscopic values. In the same way, having chosen a connection coefficient
c_{JK}
from a population
K
to a population
J
, we set the weight of the connection from any neuron
k\in K
to any
j\in J
to be
W_{jk}=\nicefrac{{c_{JK}}}{{\lvert K\rvert}}
. However, we do not assume the thresholds to be constant: we rather assume that thresholds in a population
J
follow a logistic distribution with mean
\theta_{J}
and scaling factor
s_{\theta_{J}}
, having cumulative distribution function

F_{\theta_{J}}(x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\Bigl(-\dfrac{x-\theta_{J}}{s_{\theta_{J}}}\Bigr)}.

Finally, we always specify the distribution of the microscopic initial state from two pieces of information for each population
J
:

i
)

values
\mathcal{A}_{J}(0)
and
\mathcal{R}_{J}(0)
for the initial expectations of its active and refractory fractions;

ii
)

a number
n_{J}
of distinct neuronal states assumed to divide its size
\lvert J\rvert
.

To do this, we define a collection
\bigl\{Z_{J}^{\ell}:J\in{P},\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n_{J}\}\bigr\}
of independent random variables taking values
1
,
i
and
0
with probabilities
\mathcal{A}_{J}(0)
,
\mathcal{R}_{J}(0)
and
\mathcal{S}_{J}(0)=1-\mathcal{A}_{J}(0)-\mathcal{R}_{J}(0)
respectively. Then, we split each population
J
in
n_{J}
subgroups of
\nicefrac{{\lvert J\rvert}}{{n_{J}}}
neurons, and set the initial state of all neurons of the
\ell
th subgroup as the random variable
Z_{J}^{\ell}
. For example, if we choose
n_{J}=\lvert J\rvert
for each population, this simply amounts to taking the initial states of all neurons as independent random variables which are identically distributed over populations. If instead
n_{J}=\nicefrac{{\lvert J\rvert}}{{10}}
for each population, then the network is split in subgroups of 10 neurons whose initial states are perfectly correlated, but the states of these subgroups are still independent and identically distributed over populations.

This completely specifies the distribution of the initial state
X_{0}
, thus specifying initial expectations and covariances as well. For example, the expectation of the active fraction of population
J
is

\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\operatorname{Re}X_{0}^{j}\big]=\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{J}}\frac{\lvert J\rvert}{n_{J}}\,\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\operatorname{Re}Z_{J}^{\ell}\big]=\frac{1}{n_{J}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{J}}\mathcal{A}_{J}(0)=\mathcal{A}_{J}(0),

as it should be. The same pattern works for the other two states. Then, similarly,

\mathrm{C}_{AA}^{JJ}(0)=\frac{1}{n_{J}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{J}}\mathrm{Var}\big[\operatorname{Re}Z_{J}^{\ell}\big]=\frac{1}{n_{J}}\mathcal{A}_{J}(0)\bigl(1-\mathcal{A}_{J}(0)\bigr),

and again the same pattern works to compute initial variances for the other two states. Covariances between the fractions of a population in two distinct states can then be computed from initial variances, and since the initial states of neurons from distinct populations are always independent, any initial covariance between fractions of two distinct populations is zero.

This method of choosing the initial state allows us to ensure that the initial state used with the second-order system is consistent with the initial distribution used in simulations of the underlying stochastic process. The distribution is admittedly somewhat artificial, but it allows to introduce between neuronal states nonzero correlations whose magnitude can be modified through the parameter
n_{J}
, while still being convenient for numerical simulations.

Indeed, the rate at which the average of trajectories converges to the average of the oscillations depends on the relative size of the noise with respect to the oscillation amplitude, which depends on the network’s size: the less neurons there are in the network, the faster the oscillations will dampen. However, regardless of the network’s size, the stochastic trajectories should always become distributed all over the cycle after some time, and this is what our second-order model captures, at least qualitatively. It is possible that in order to increase the precision of the prediction, one would have to include higher-order moments in the model.

5  Discussion

We demonstrated the importance of accounting for covariances between states of neural populations when modeling the activity of large stochastic neural networks. Our observations were based on the low-dimensional dynamical system in
eq.

3.11
, which we derived mostly by developing a novel method for estimating the expected value of random variables as in
eqs.

3.9
and
3.10
, and solving the corresponding moment closure problem. We achieved this while explicitly describing the proportion of refractory neurons in each population. Our numerical experiments showed that in some cases, the mean-field solutions greatly differ from both the average of realizations of the stochastic model and the solutions of our second-order model, and that our model succeeds in improving the prediction of the network’s activity as compared to the mean-field model.

There is a first class of examples (see Figures
4
and
5
) in which the inclusion of covariances leads to predictions that match much more closely the behavior of the high-dimensional stochastic system. This can occur even if after a brief transient period, all covariances go to zero. The presence of two attractors in the mean-field system explains well this phenomenon. Indeed, if the initial conditions lie close to the separatrix between the basins of attraction, the qualitative features of the solution can be highly sensitive to small perturbations of the early system behavior. Failing to account for covariances is akin to not using all the available information about the system’s initial state, which may lead to predicting convergence to the wrong attractor. A lot of research has been done recently for systems with only one attracting point, in which case injecting information about covariances increases the order of convergence
(
Gast et al. 2019
)
. In our case, covariances have more dramatic effects as they may make the system jump basin of attraction.

There is yet another situation in which the covariances may dramatically affect the solutions, as some covariances can remain positive throughout the simulations. As shown on Figure
6
, the solution of the mean-field system can display sustained oscillations while the solution of the second-order model converges to a fixed point. This can be explained by the presence of positive covariances, which impacts the activation functions of neuron populations by making them more linear. Since the presence of multiple fixed points or oscillations in the mean-field model depends on the nonlinearity of the activation function, changing its shape is likely to change the topology of the solutions.

Despite the improvement achieved by our second-order model in predicting the macroscopic behavior of the stochastic process as compared to the mean-field model, it appears from the results shown on Figures
5
and
6
that our second-order model can still not predict the macroscopic behavior of the underlying stochastic process with complete accuracy. Given the nonlinearity of our model and the presence of multiple attractors, obtaining rigorous theorems on rates of convergence or on the quality of our dimension reduction would be difficult. Indeed, the accuracy of the reduction of a high-dimensional stochastic system to a low-dimensional dynamical system is not easy to determine analytically. In the case where the system has a single attracting point, this problem has recently been investigated by
Gast & Van 2017
;
Gast et al. 2019
;
Gast 2020
, but we are not aware of more general results. In order to obtain a more accurate model, it is possible that one could build a different second-order solution to the moment closure problem posed by
eq.

2.5
, but it is also possible that one would have to find a higher-order solution involving at least third-order moments.

We argue for the necessity of taking into account the inherently stochastic nature of neuron activity. This stochastic nature does not come from the way that mathematical modeling of neurons is performed, but from omnipresent biological mechanisms such as vesicle release or random channel state transition. Our results show that using a naive approach to handle this stochasticity, such as a mean-field approximation, can lead to false conclusions, and that a second-order solution to the moment closure problem posed by
eq.

2.5
is more appropriate in several situations. Our work thus emphasizes the importance of pursuing the study of higher moments in models of neuronal activity. In particular, the observation that the solutions of our second-order model correspond to the averages of many trajectories of the stochastic model invites further theoretical investigation. We believe that the mathematical tools developed here will help deriving more effective low-dimensional approximations of stochastic processes on networks, contributing to a better understanding of large-scale dynamical phenomena in complex systems.

A.1  The nonautonomous microscopic first-order evolution equation

First, we derive the expressions of the derivatives of the probabilities

\displaystyle p_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}^{j}=1]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}\big],

\displaystyle r_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}^{j}=i]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{j}\big],

\displaystyle q_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}^{j}=0]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\big].

where, as in the main text, the continuous-time Markov chain
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
takes its values in
E\vcentcolon=\{0,1,i\}^{N}
,
N
being the number of neurons in the network. An entry
X_{t}^{j}
is then interpreted as the state of neuron
j
at time
t
.

To obtain the expression of the derivative of
p_{j}(t)
, let
\Delta t>0
. Conditioning on the state of the network at time
t
,

\displaystyle p_{j}(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in E}\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}=x]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in E}\bigl(\operatorname{Re}x_{j}+\operatorname{Im}x_{j}+1-\lvert x_{j}\rvert\bigr)\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}=x].

Because
x_{j}
has a value in
\{0,1,i\}
, exactly one of
\operatorname{Re}x_{j}
,
\operatorname{Im}x_{j}
or
1-\lvert x_{j}\rvert
is 1 while the other two are 0. Hence, we can now split the sum in three parts: one for each possible state of
j
at time
t
. Each conditional probability
\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]
can then be expressed from transition rates according to the relations given in
eq.

2.1
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x,X_{t}^{j}=0]

\displaystyle=a_{j}(\eta,x)\Delta t+o(\Delta t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x,X_{t}^{j}=1]

\displaystyle=1-\beta_{j}(\eta)\Delta t+o(\Delta t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x,X_{t}^{j}=i]

\displaystyle=o(\Delta t).

Using these relations, we obtain that as
\Delta t\downarrow 0
,

\displaystyle p_{j}(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in E}\operatorname{Re}x_{j}\bigl(1-\beta_{j}(\eta)\Delta t\bigr)\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}=x]+\sum_{x\in E}\bigl(1-\lvert x_{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(\eta,x)\Delta t\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}=x]+o(\Delta t).

These sums are now expectations of functions of
X_{t}
, and we find that

\displaystyle p_{j}(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\bigl(1-\beta_{j}(\eta)\Delta t\bigr)p_{j}(t)+\Delta t\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\big]+o(\Delta t).

as
\Delta t\downarrow 0
. Rearranging and dividing through by
\Delta t
yields

\dot{p}_{j}(t)=-\beta_{j}(\eta)p_{j}(t)+\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\big].

The derivatives of
r_{j}
and
q_{j}
are obtained in the same way. In these two cases, the transition probabilities
\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=i\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]
and
\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=0\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]
are expressed from other transition rates, and we obtain

\displaystyle\SwapAboveDisplaySkip\dot{r}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{j}(\eta)r_{j}(t)+\beta_{j}(\eta)p_{j}(t)

and

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\big]+\gamma_{j}(\eta)r_{j}(t).

A.2  The nonautonomous macroscopic first-order evolution equation

Now, we use the expressions of the derivatives of
p_{j}
,
q_{j}
and
r_{j}
obtained above to find the derivatives of the expected fractions of populations

\mathcal{A}_{J}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[A_{t}^{J}],\qquad\mathcal{R}_{J}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[R_{t}^{J}]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{S}_{J}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[S_{t}^{J}],

where

,

and

are respectively the fractions of active, refractory and sensitive neurons in population
J
at time

.

To obtain an expression for the derivative of
\mathcal{A}_{J}
, we use the idea described in the main text: we average the derivatives
\dot{p}_{j}
over

using the linearity of expectations and derivatives. From the expression of

\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}(t)=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\bigg[-\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{j}(\eta)\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}+\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)\bigg].

Introducing for
\xi\in\{0,1,i\}

J_{t}^{\xi}\vcentcolon=\{j\in J:X_{t}^{j}=\xi\}

\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}(t)=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\bigg[-\frac{A_{t}^{J}}{\lvert J_{t}^{1}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{1}}\beta_{j}(\eta)+\frac{S_{t}^{J}}{\lvert J_{t}^{0}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{0}}a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\bigg],

(A.1)

where we used the identities
\lvert J_{t}^{1}\rvert=\lvert J\rvert A_{t}^{J}
and
\lvert J_{t}^{0}\rvert=\lvert J\rvert S_{t}^{J}
. Now, assuming that the number of neurons in
J
is large, we expect the number of neurons in
J
that are active at time

to be large as well. Since the random variables
\beta_{j}
for
j\in J_{t}^{1}
are independent and identically distributed, the law of large numbers motivates the approximation

\frac{1}{\lvert J_{t}^{1}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{1}}\beta_{j}(\eta)\approx\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\beta_{j}]=\vcentcolon\beta_{J},

(A.2)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}
denotes the expectation on
(H,{H},\mu)
. The other term can be handled in a similar way. First, we approximate the input

\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{jk}(\eta)\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{k}+Q_{J}=\sum_{K\in{P}}\sum_{k\in K_{t}^{1}}W_{jk}(\eta)+Q_{J}\approx\sum_{K\in{P}}\lvert K\rvert A_{t}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[W_{jk}]+Q_{J}.

To simplify notation, we define the input in population
J
at time
t

B_{t}^{J}\vcentcolon=\sum_{K\in{P}}c_{JK}A_{t}^{K}+Q_{J}\qquad\text{with}\qquad c_{JK}\vcentcolon=\lvert K\rvert\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[W_{jk}]

for
j\in J
and
k\in K
. This leads to approximate, for
j\in J
,

a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\approx\alpha_{j}(\eta)\mathbf{1}_{\{B_{t}^{J}>\theta_{j}\}}(\eta),

and the law of large numbers now motivates the approximation

\frac{1}{\lvert J_{t}^{0}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{0}}a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\approx\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\alpha_{j}\mathbf{1}_{\{B_{t}^{J}>\theta_{j}\}}]=\alpha_{J}F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J}),

(A.3)

where
\alpha_{J}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\alpha_{j}]
and
F_{\theta_{J}}
denotes the cumulative distribution function of
\theta_{j}
for
j\in J
.

Using the approximations from
eqs.

A.2
and
A.3
in the expression of
\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}
given in
eq.

A.1
yields

\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{J}(t)\approx-\beta_{J}\mathcal{A}_{J}(t)+\alpha_{J}\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[S_{t}^{J}F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})].

The same method can be applied to find approximate expressions for the derivatives of
\mathcal{R}_{J}
and
\mathcal{S}_{J}
. This leads us to model the macroscopic dynamics of the network by the differential equations (
2.5
).

A.3  The nonautonomous microscopic second-order evolution equation

As a first step towards finding the derivatives of covariances given in
eq.

3.1
, we now find expressions for the derivatives of the probabilities

\displaystyle p_{jk}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}^{j}=X_{t}^{k}=1]=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{k}],

\displaystyle r_{jk}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}^{j}=X_{t}^{k}=i]=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{j}\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{k}],

\displaystyle\rho_{jk}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}^{j}=1,X_{t}^{k}=i]=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{k}].

The idea is exactly the same as in the case of the probabilities
p_{j}
,
r_{j}
and
q_{j}
. We will give the details of the calculations only for the case of
\rho_{jk}
, as it illustrates all of the relevant ideas.

Let
\Delta t>0
. Then

\rho_{jk}(t+\Delta t)=\begin{multlined}\sum_{x\in E}\bigl(\operatorname{Re}x_{j}+\operatorname{Im}x_{j}+1-\lvert x_{j}\rvert\bigr)\bigl(\operatorname{Re}x_{k}+\operatorname{Im}x_{k}+1-\lvert x_{k}\rvert\bigr)\\
\times\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1,X_{t+\Delta t}^{k}=i\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}=x].\end{multlined}

The sum now gets split into nine parts, one for each of the possible states for the pair
(j,k)
. Again, the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1,X_{t+\Delta t}^{k}=i\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]
can be expressed in terms of transition rates. These expressions are all derived from the general statement that for any
x,y\in E
, as
\Delta t\downarrow 0
,

\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}=y\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x]=\delta_{xy}+m^{\eta}(x,y)\Delta t+o(\Delta t),

where
m^{\eta}(x,y)
is the
(x,y)
entry of the generator
M^{\eta}
, as in the main text. It follows from this statement that as
\Delta t\downarrow 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1,X_{t+\Delta t}^{k}=i\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x,X_{t}^{j}=1,X_{t}^{k}=i]

\displaystyle=1-\bigl(\beta_{j}(\eta)+\gamma_{k}(\eta)\bigr)\Delta t+o(\Delta t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1,X_{t+\Delta t}^{k}=i\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x,X_{t}^{j}=0,X_{t}^{k}=i]

\displaystyle=a_{j}(\eta,x)\Delta t+o(\Delta t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t+\Delta t}^{j}=1,X_{t+\Delta t}^{k}=i\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}X_{t}=x,X_{t}^{j}=1,X_{t}^{k}=1]

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}(\eta)\Delta t+o(\Delta t),

while the transition probabilities from states
x
where
j
and
k
have other states are all
o(\Delta t)
. Using these relations, we see that as
\Delta t\downarrow 0
,

\displaystyle\rho_{jk}(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in E}\operatorname{Re}x_{j}\operatorname{Im}x_{k}\bigl(1-\bigl(\beta_{j}(\eta)+\gamma_{k}(\eta)\bigr)\Delta t\bigr)\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}=x]

\displaystyle\hskip 62.59605pt+\sum_{x\in E}\bigl(1-\lvert x_{j}\rvert\bigr)\operatorname{Im}x_{k}\,a_{j}(\eta,x)\Delta t\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}=x]

\displaystyle\hskip 62.59605pt+\sum_{x\in E}\operatorname{Re}x_{j}\operatorname{Re}x_{k}\,\beta_{k}(\eta)\Delta t\mathbb{P}^{\eta}[X_{t}=x]+o(\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\bigl(1-\bigl(\beta_{j}(\eta)+\gamma_{k}(\eta)\bigr)\Delta t\bigr)\rho_{jk}(t)

\displaystyle\hskip 62.59605pt+\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{k}a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\big]\Delta t+\beta_{k}(\eta)p_{jk}(t)\Delta t+o(\Delta t).

Rearranging and dividing through by
\Delta t
finally yields

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{jk}(t)

\displaystyle=-\bigl(\beta_{j}(\eta)+\gamma_{k}(\eta)\bigr)\rho_{jk}(t)+\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{k}a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\big]+\beta_{k}(\eta)p_{jk}(t).

(A.4a)

The same method allows to find

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{p}_{jk}(t)&=-\bigl(\beta_{j}(\eta)+\beta_{k}(\eta)\bigr)p_{jk}(t)+\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{k}a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\big]\\
&\hskip 156.49014pt+\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\big[\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{k}\rvert\bigr)a_{k}(\eta,X_{t})\big],\end{split}

(A.4b)

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{jk}(t)

\displaystyle=-\bigl(\gamma_{j}(\eta)+\gamma_{k}(\eta)\bigr)r_{jk}(t)+\beta_{j}(\eta)\rho_{jk}(t)+\beta_{k}(\eta)\rho_{kj}(t).

(A.4c)

We could find similar equations to describe the evolution of similar probabilities with
j
or
k
being sensitive, but these can always be expressed as functions of
p_{jk}
,
r_{jk}
,
\rho_{jk}
and
\rho_{kj}
since each entry
X_{t}^{j}
is always in
\{0,1,i\}
.

A.4  The nonautonomous macroscopic second-order evolution equation

We now find the derivatives of covariances between active and refractory fractions of populations of the network. We first find the derivatives of the expectations

\mathrm{E}_{AA}^{JK}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[A_{t}^{J}A_{t}^{K}],\qquad\mathrm{E}_{RR}^{JK}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[R_{t}^{J}R_{t}^{K}]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{E}_{AR}^{JK}(t)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[A_{t}^{J}R_{t}^{K}]

from the differential equations (
A.4
), using the same strategy as for first moments. As in the microscopic case we only detail the case of
\mathrm{E}_{AR}^{JK}
, as the other two are similar.

By linearity of expectations and derivatives,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{E}}_{AR}^{JK}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\biggl[\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert\lvert K\rvert}\sum_{j\in J,k\in K}\Bigl(-\bigl(\beta_{j}(\eta)+\gamma_{k}(\eta)\bigr)\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{k}

\displaystyle\hskip 110.96574pt+\bigl(1-\lvert X_{t}^{j}\rvert\bigr)\operatorname{Im}X_{t}^{k}a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})+\beta_{k}(\eta)\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{j}\operatorname{Re}X_{t}^{k}\Bigr)\biggr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\eta}\biggl[-\frac{A_{t}^{J}R_{t}^{K}}{\lvert J_{t}^{1}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{1}}\beta_{j}(\eta)-\frac{A_{t}^{J}R_{t}^{K}}{\lvert K_{t}^{i}\rvert}\sum_{k\in K_{t}^{i}}\gamma_{k}(\eta)

\displaystyle\hskip 110.96574pt+\frac{S_{t}^{J}R_{t}^{K}}{\lvert J_{t}^{0}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{0}}a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})+\frac{A_{t}^{J}A_{t}^{K}}{\lvert K_{t}^{1}\rvert}\sum_{k\in K_{t}^{1}}\beta_{k}(\eta)\biggr].

In the same way as in the case of first-order moments, the law of large numbers motivates the approximations

\frac{1}{\lvert J_{t}^{1}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{1}}\beta_{j}(\eta)\approx\beta_{J},\qquad\frac{1}{\lvert K_{t}^{i}\rvert}\sum_{k\in K_{t}^{i}}\gamma_{k}(\eta)\approx\gamma_{K}

and

\frac{1}{\lvert J_{t}^{0}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{0}}a_{j}(\eta,X_{t})\approx\alpha_{J}F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J}),

and we find the approximate equation

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{E}}_{AR}^{JK}(t)

\displaystyle=-(\beta_{J}+\gamma_{K})\mathrm{E}_{AR}^{JK}(t)+\alpha_{J}\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[S_{t}^{J}R_{t}^{K}F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})]+\beta_{K}\mathrm{E}_{AA}^{JK}(t).

(A.5a)

The same method leads to

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{E}}_{AA}^{JK}(t)

\displaystyle=-(\beta_{J}+\beta_{K})\mathrm{E}_{AA}^{JK}(t)+\alpha_{J}\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[S_{t}^{J}A_{t}^{K}F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J})]+\alpha_{K}\mathbb{E}^{\eta}[A_{t}^{J}S_{t}^{K}F_{\theta_{K}}(B_{t}^{K})],

(A.5b)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{E}}_{RR}^{JK}(t)

\displaystyle=-(\gamma_{J}+\gamma_{K})\mathrm{E}_{RR}^{JK}(t)+\beta_{J}\mathrm{E}_{AR}^{JK}(t)+\beta_{K}\mathrm{E}_{AR}^{KJ}(t).

(A.5c)

By definition of the covariance,
\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{JK}=\mathrm{E}_{AR}^{JK}-\mathcal{A}_{J}\mathcal{R}_{K}
, and similar relations hold for other combinations of fractions of populations. Therefore, the expressions of the derivatives of the covariances
\mathrm{C}_{AA}^{JK}
,
\mathrm{C}_{RR}^{JK}
and
\mathrm{C}_{AR}^{JK}
given in
eq.

3.1
follow from
eq.

A.5
along with the derivatives of
\mathcal{A}_{J}
and
\mathcal{R}_{J}
given in
eq.

2.5
.

A.5  Limits of the approximations

We start by remarking that, even though we motivate the approximations of the averages of transition rates (e.g. in
eqs.

A.2
and
A.3
) by the law of large numbers, it would not be obvious to make the argument fully rigorous by taking a limit where the sizes of all populations grow infinitely large. The reason for this is that the sets that are needed to be large, which are the subpopulations of active, sensitive and refractory neurons in each population, are themselves random on
(\Omega,{F},\mathbb{P}^{\eta})
, but the probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\eta}
is a function on
(H,{H},\mu)
. Therefore, the setting cannot be easily translated to a simple sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables, and we settle for understanding
eqs.

A.2
and
A.3
as approximations.

We expect the approximations

\frac{1}{\lvert J_{t}^{1}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{1}}\beta_{j}(\eta)\approx\beta_{J}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{\lvert J_{t}^{i}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{i}}\gamma_{j}(\eta)\approx\gamma_{J}

to be good approximations for large populations regardless of the specific distributions of
\beta_{j}
and
\gamma_{j}
in
J
, as long as these remain independent and identically distributed in
J
. Indeed, we do not expect the specific values of
\beta_{j}
and
\gamma_{j}
to have an impact on the probabilities for
j
to be active and refractory at time
t
, so we expect the empirical distributions of
\beta_{j}
and
\gamma_{j}
over
J_{t}^{1}
and
J_{t}^{i}
to approximate reasonably well the actual distributions when
J
is large.

However, the case of the corresponding approximations for the activation rates,

\frac{1}{\lvert J_{t}^{0}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{0}}\alpha_{j}(\eta)\mathbf{1}_{T_{j}(X_{t})}(\eta)\approx\frac{1}{\lvert J_{t}^{0}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{t}^{0}}\alpha_{j}(\eta)\mathbf{1}_{\{B_{t}^{J}>\theta_{j}\}}(\eta)\approx\alpha_{J}F_{\theta_{J}}(B_{t}^{J}),

(A.6)

requires more care. Assuming that every neuron of
J
is given the same input
B_{t}^{J}
, the last approximation should hold only when the distribution of
\theta_{j}
in
J
is not too spread out. Indeed, only the neurons in
J
whose thresholds are lower than their input have a nonzero probability to activate, so it is possible for the specific value of the threshold of a neuron to have an effect on the probability that it is sensitive at a given time. This could lead the empirical distribution of the thresholds in the subpopulation
J_{t}^{0}
to be biased towards higher thresholds. Nevertheless, since the activation probabilities only depend on the sign of the difference between the input and the threshold, we do not expect this effect to be important when the distribution of the thresholds is peaked enough. Similar arguments also imply that the first approximation in
eq.

A.6
should only hold when the distributions of the weights
W_{jk}
are peaked enough.

B.1  Verification of the assumptions for a normal distribution

Suppose that the thresholds in population
J
follow a normal distribution with mean
\theta_{J}
and variance
\sigma_{\theta_{J}}^{2}
, which has density

F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(b)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\theta_{J}}\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(b-\theta_{J})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\theta_{J}}^{2}}\Bigr).

This distribution is indeed unimodal and symmetric. Moreover, its cumulative distribution function
F_{\theta_{J}}
is smooth, and the density
F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}
is positive, so assumptions
1)
and
2)
are satisfied.

To verify the other two assumptions, we compute
g_{J}
for this distribution.

F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}(b)=\frac{\theta_{J}-b}{\sigma_{\theta_{J}}^{3}\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(b-\theta_{J})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\theta_{J}}^{2}}\Bigr)=\frac{\theta_{J}-b}{\sigma_{\theta_{J}}^{2}}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(b),\qquad\text{so}\qquad g_{J}(b,v)=\frac{v}{2\sigma_{\theta_{J}}^{2}}.

Then

is bounded so assumptions
3)
is satisfied, and it is positive so

is directly satisfied since

does not depend on its first argument.

Therefore, the conclusions of Theorem

hold if the thresholds in population

follow a normal distribution.

B.2  Verification of the assumptions for a logistic distribution

\theta_{J}

, which has cumulative distribution function

F_{\theta_{J}}(b)=\sigma\Bigl(\frac{b-\theta_{J}}{s_{\theta_{J}}}\Bigr)\qquad\text{where}\qquad\sigma(x)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}.

The logistic function

\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma(1-\sigma)
. This property allows to compute

F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{s_{\theta_{J}}}F_{\theta_{J}}(1-F_{\theta_{J}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{s_{\theta_{J}}}F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(1-2F_{\theta_{J}}).

Since the range of
F_{\theta_{J}}
is
(0,1)
, it follows that
F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}>0
and assumption
2)
is satisfied. Then, for
b\neq\theta_{J}
,

g_{J}(b,v)=\frac{v}{2s_{\theta_{J}}}\frac{1-2F_{\theta_{J}}(b)}{\theta_{J}-b},

and
g_{J}
is extended to a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
using l’Hospital’s rule. Since
g_{J}(\cdot,1)
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, in order to prove that it is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
it suffices to prove that it is bounded outside of the compact interval
[\theta_{J}-1,\theta_{J}+1]
, and this is easy to see: if
\lvert b-\theta_{J}\rvert>1
, then
\lvert g_{J}(b,1)\rvert<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2s_{\theta_{J}}}}
since
F_{\theta_{J}}
takes its values in
(0,1)
. This shows that assumption
3)
is satisfied.

To verify the last assumption, notice that for
b\neq\theta_{J}
and any
v\geq 0
,

(\theta_{J}-b)\partial_{1}g_{J}(b,v)=\frac{v}{2s_{\theta_{J}}}\Bigl(\frac{1-2F_{\theta_{J}}(b)}{\theta_{J}-b}-2F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(b)\Bigr)=g_{J}(b,v)\Bigl(1-\frac{2(\theta_{J}-b)F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(b)}{1-2F_{\theta_{J}}(b)}\Bigr).

Then, with
x\vcentcolon=\nicefrac{{(b-\theta_{J})}}{{s_{\theta_{J}}}}
,

1-2F_{\theta_{J}}(b)=-\frac{1-e^{-x}}{1+e^{-x}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad F_{\theta_{J}}^{\prime}(b)=\frac{1}{s_{\theta_{J}}}\frac{e^{-x}}{(1+e^{-x})^{2}},

so

(\theta_{J}-b)\partial_{1}g_{J}(b,v)=g_{J}(b,v)\Bigl(1-\frac{2xe^{-x}}{1-e^{-2x}}\Bigr)=g_{J}(b,v)\Bigl(1-\frac{x}{\sinh x}\Bigr).

Since
\lvert x\rvert\leq\lvert\sinh x\rvert
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, it follows that
(\theta_{J}-b)\partial_{1}g_{J}(b,v)\geq 0
because
g_{J}
is nonnegative. Since this holds whenever
b\neq\theta_{J}
, it holds for
b=\theta_{J}
by continuity, and assumption
4)
holds.

Therefore, the conclusions of Theorem
1
hold if the thresholds in population
J
follow a logistic distribution.
