Calibration Error for Decision Making
Lunjia Hu, Yifan Wu

Abstract
Calibration allows predictions to be reliably interpreted as probabilities by decision makers. We propose a decision-theoretic calibration error, the Calibration Decision Loss ( CDL ), defined as the maximum improvement in decision payoff obtained by calibrating the predictions, where the maximum is over all payoff-bounded decision tasks. Vanishing CDL guarantees the payoff loss from miscalibration vanishes simultaneously for all downstream decision tasks. We show separations between CDL and existing calibration error metrics, including the most well-studied metric Expected Calibration Error ( ECE ). Our main technical contribution is a new efficient algorithm for online calibration that achieves near-optimal O ⁡ ( log ⁡ T T ) O(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}}) expected CDL , bypassing the Ω ⁡ ( T − 0.472 ) \Omega(T^{-0.472}) lower bound for ECE by Qiao and Valiant (2021) .



A previous version of the paper was titled “Predict to Minimize Swap Regret for All Payoff-Bounded Tasks”. We also thank Mingda Qiao for helpful discussions about the online calibration literature.

Introduction

Given a sequence of predictions indicating, say, the chances of rain each day for a period of
T
days, an intuitive way to assess the quality of these predictions is to check for calibration: for example, among the days predicted to have a 60% chance of rain, is the fraction of rainy days indeed 60%?
Formally, suppose a prediction of
q_{i}\in[0,1]
is received on
n_{i}
days, and among the
n_{i}
days,
m_{i}
days are actually rainy.
Calibration, a notion that originated from the forecasting literature
(
Dawid, 1982
)
, requires the relationship

m_{i}=q_{i}n_{i}

to hold for every prediction value
q_{i}
.
The most well-studied calibration error metric is the Expected Calibration Error (
ECE
), defined as
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i}|m_{i}-q_{i}n_{i}|
, or equivalently written as the average prediction bias
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i}n_{i}|q_{i}-\frac{m_{i}}{n_{i}}|
,
where
|q_{i}-\frac{m_{i}}{n_{i}}|
is the absolute bias when
q_{i}
is predicted. This gives a well-defined calibration error metric: a predictor is perfectly calibrated if and only if its
ECE
is zero.

In this work, we focus on the value of calibration for decision making.
In the economics literature, the value of predictions is quantified by the payoff from decision making
(
Blackwell, 1951
)
. Consider a decision maker who needs to choose an action
a\in A

to maximize their decision payoff
U(a,\theta)
, an arbitrary function of the action
a

(e.g. take an umbrella or not) and the true binary state
\theta\in\{0,1\}

(e.g. rainy or not).
When the true state
\theta

is unknown and only a prediction
p\in[0,1]

is given, the decision maker can trust the prediction and take the action
a
that maximizes the expected payoff
\E_{\theta\sim p}[U(a,\theta)]

, where “trust” means the decision maker
assumes
that the distribution of the state
is indeed as predicted (
\Pr[\theta=1]=p
).
Thus, the prediction manifests its value in the payoff received by the decision maker who best responds to the prediction as if the predictions were correct.

The Decision Loss from Miscalibration

The examples above seem to suggest that calibration is a
valuable
property of a predictor for downstream decision makers. Is there a way to precisely
quantify
this value? Does the widely used calibration error
ECE
give the right quantification? Our main result reveals a fundamental gap between
ECE
and the value of calibration for decision making. In fact,
ECE
overestimates the decision loss from miscalibration.

ECE

every
downstream task?

To answer this question, we propose a calibration error for decision making, the Calibration Decision Loss (
CDL
), which quantifies the worst-case payoff loss caused by miscalibration of a predictor. Our main result demonstrates a fundamental separation between
CDL
and existing error metrics including
ECE
. Inspired by this separation, we give a new efficient algorithm for online prediction that minimizes
CDL
at a faster rate than what is possible for
ECE
(see
Section

1.2
for more details).

Our definition of
CDL
can be decomposed into two steps. First, for a fixed decision task, miscalibration incurs a loss to the decision maker, which we call Calibration Fixed Decision Loss (CFDL).
Consider a decision maker who best responds to a miscalibrated predictor as in
Example

1.1
. We define CFDL as the payoff increase (averaged over
T
rounds) that the decision maker could have achieved if we had calibrated the predictions as in
Example

1.2
.
Second, since perfect calibration guarantees trustworthiness for all decision makers, we measure the calibration error by defining CDL as the worst-case (i.e., maximum) CFDL over all decision tasks with payoffs bounded in
[0,1]
.
Here we restrict the payoffs to be bounded solely for the purpose of normalization: the CFDL of a decision task scales proportionally if we multiply the payoffs by any positive constant, and it remains the same if any constant is added to the payoffs. Beyond that, we make no additional restrictions on the decision tasks. In particular, we allow each decision task to have an arbitrarily large action space
A
, and the CDL is the maximum CFDL over all such tasks.
Thus, vanishing CDL implies that the payoff loss from miscalibration vanishes for all decision tasks.

Similar to ECE, our CDL is a well-defined calibration error metric, with the basic property that a sequence of predictions is perfectly calibrated if and only if its CDL is zero, as implied by
Foster and Vohra (1997)
.

1.2
Online Calibration

Let us now consider the algorithmic task of making sequential predictions for
T
rounds. The goal is to achieve asymptotic calibration, meaning that the predictions are arbitrarily “close” to perfect calibration for sufficiently large
T
, with “closeness” measured by some calibration error. Though it may seem impossible,
a classic and remarkable result by
Foster and Vohra (1998)
shows that asymptotic calibration can be achieved without
any
knowledge of what the state will be in each round.

Concretely,
Foster and Vohra (1998)
consider the following
online binary prediction
setting. The algorithm (i.e., predictor) interacts with an adversary for
T
rounds. In each round
t=1,\ldots,T
, the predictor makes a prediction
p_{t}\in[0,1]
and the adversary reveals the true state
\theta_{t}\in\{0,1\}
.
The only knowledge that the predictor can use to produce
p_{t}
is the history
(p_{1},\theta_{1},\ldots,p_{t-1},\theta_{t-1})
, which may contain zero information about the new state
\theta_{t}
. The adversary, on the other hand, can choose
\theta_{t}
arbitrarily, based on the history
and the predictor’s strategy
, so as to maximize the calibration error of the predictions.

Foster and Vohra (1998)
focus on
ECE
as the metric for calibration error. They prove the existence of a randomized algorithm that guarantees
O(T^{-1/3})
expected ECE, where the expectation is over the randomness of the algorithm.
Their use of a randomized algorithm breaks the impossibility of asymptotic calibration if a deterministic prediction strategy were used.
To see this impossibility, if the algorithm predicts deterministically, the adversary can pick
\theta_{t}=0
whenever the prediction
p_{t}
is above
0.5
, and
\theta_{t}=1
otherwise, yielding
\textsc{ECE}\geq 1/2
. In contrast, the predictor in
Foster and Vohra (1998)
draws
p_{t}
randomly from a distribution. The adversary, knowing the predictor’s strategy, can choose
\theta_{t}
based on this distribution, but it can no longer choose
\theta_{t}
based on the realized value of
p_{t}
drawn from the distribution.

This remarkable first result of
Foster and Vohra (1998)
opened up the literature of online asymptotic calibration, with the focus of error metric mainly on
ECE
.
On the upperbound side, subsequent work constructs polynomial-time algorithms for
ECE
minimization
(
Foster, 1999
;
Foster and Hart, 2021
, e.g.)
. On the lowerbound side, a recent breakthrough of
Qiao and Valiant (2021)
proves an
\Omega(T^{-0.472})
lower bound for ECE, improving over the long-known natural
\Omega(T^{-1/2})
bound.
2
2

2

Subsequent to our work,
Dagan et al. (2024)
show there exists a sequential prediction strategy that achieves
O(T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}-\varepsilon})
expected
ECE
for some
\varepsilon>0
. They also give an improved lowerbound for
ECE
over the
\Omega(T^{-0.472})
in
Qiao and Valiant (2021)
.

It is unclear, however, that for decision making, ECE is the calibration error metric we wish to optimize.
The economic value of making good predictions lies in helping downstream decision makers achieve better payoff.
If the goal of calibration is to generate trustworthy predictions that induce no payoff loss to all decision makers, what we should really minimize is
CDL
, rather than ECE.

Prior to our work, nothing was known about
CDL
minimization beyond what is implied by minimizing
ECE
. Specifically, the relationship
\textsc{CDL}\leq 2\,\textsc{ECE}
can be inferred from
Kleinberg et al. (2023)
.
Therefore, any ECE minimization algorithm also guarantees that
CDL
vanishes at the same rate (i.e., up to a factor of
2
).
The interesting question is to go beyond this implication: can we achieve a better error rate for CDL than that is possible for ECE, surpassing the
\Omega(T^{-0.472})
lower bound?

Our work gives a positive answer.
We give an efficient randomized algorithm that guarantees
O(T^{-1/2}\log T)
expected
CDL
(
Theorem

5.1
).
This error rate surpasses the
\Omega(T^{-0.472})
lowerbound for
ECE
and is optimal up to a logarithmic factor: there is a natural
\Omega(T^{-1/2})
lower bound for CDL minimization from drawing each state
\theta_{t}
independently and uniformly from
\{0,1\}
.
3
3

3

Consider the decision task with two actions (

U(a,\theta)=\mathbb{I}\left[a=\theta\right]

By definition, our algorithm guarantees that for every downstream decision task, the payoff loss from miscalibration (
CFDL

Our work establishes a comprehensive understanding of the optimal rate for CDL, compared to the significant gaps in the current best upper and lower bounds for the other two recently popular metrics in online asymptotic calibration (see

Certainly, our result would be impossible without a separation between
CDL
and
ECE
. This separation has been hinted in our examples earlier, and we will give a more in-depth explanation in
Section

1.3.2
with an overview of the techniques in this paper.

Our result implies a substantial strengthening of an independent result by
Roth and Shi (2024)
on
swap regret
minimization. Swap regret minimization has been studied extensively in the literature of online learning
(
Hart and Mas-Colell, 2000
;
Blum and Mansour, 2007
)
. Consider a fixed decision task repeated for
T
rounds. The swap regret is the payoff increase (averaged over
T
rounds) when the decision maker is allowed to apply a mapping
\sigma:A\to A
and swap each action to another action in hindsight. The swap regret is stronger than the more commonly studied external regret, which only allows the decision maker to swap each action to a fixed action in hindsight.
Foster and Vohra (1999)
have observed that a predictor is perfectly calibrated if and only if it guarantees no swap regret for every downstream decision maker who trusts the prediction.
Roth and Shi (2024)
propose an algorithm that achieves
O\Big(|A|\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{T}}\Big)
swap regret for every downstream decision maker, where
|A|
is the number of actions.

In our paper, our algorithm guarantees the same near-optimal
O(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}})
swap regret for every downstream decision maker simultaneously, removing the dependence on the number
|A|
of actions in
Roth and Shi (2024)
.
This directly follows from the fact that swap regret is upperbounded by
CFDL
for a fixed a decision task. To see this, first recall that
CFDL
is the improvement in payoff when predictions are calibrated to the conditional empirical frequencies. While the calibrated predictions may suggest different actions to the decision maker, this improvement can be equivalently written as the regret when, in hindsight, the decision maker is allowed to swap actions whenever they receive a different prediction.
Thus,
CFDL
is stronger since the modification rule is finer-grained than that of the swap regret. If two predictions suggest the same best-response action to a decision maker, the modification rule in
CFDL
allows the decision maker to swap the actions differently, while the swap regret does not.

The
U-calibration
error
UCal
, introduced by
Kleinberg et al. (2023)
, is closely related to our
CDL
, despite being qualitatively weaker (i.e.
UCal
being zero is necessary but insufficient for calibration). Both errors are defined as the maximum payoff increase over decision tasks: CDL is the maximum payoff increase from calibrating the predictions; whereas U-calibration is the maximum payoff increase (a.k.a.
external regret
) from changing every prediction
p_{t}
to the best fixed prediction (i.e., predicting the overall average
(\theta_{1}+\cdots+\theta_{T})/T
every round).
4
4

4

For a fixed decision task, the external regret may be negative, but the U-calibration error is non-negative because it is the maximum over all payoff-bounded decision tasks (including the degenerate task where the payoff is always zero).
Therefore, the U-calibration error lowerbounds CDL.
To see why
UCal
is not a well-defined calibration error, consider the miscalibrated predictor such that
(p_{t},\theta_{t})=(1/4,0)
for
T/2
rounds, and
(p_{t},\theta_{t})=(3/4,1)
for the remaining
T/2
rounds. The miscalibrated predictor always induces better or equal payoff compared to the best fixed prediction
1/2
, giving zero U-calibration error. See a detailed discussion in
Example

4.5
.

While our result appears very similar to the result of
Kleinberg et al. (2023)
, we use substantially different techniques since our
CDL
is qualitatively stronger.

Kleinberg et al. (2023)
give a randomized algorithm that guarantees an optimal
O(T^{-1/2})
expected U-calibration error. Their prediction algorithm ensures that each best-responding decision maker takes actions as if they are locally running the classic Hedge algorithm
(
Arora et al., 2012
, see)
,
which is specific to the external regret. Thus, their guarantee does not directly extend to our CFDL.

1.3.2
Online
CDL
Minimization: Technical Overview

In this section, we give an overview of our techniques for achieving near-optimal
CDL
. The key idea behind our
O(T^{-1/2}\log T)
guarantee for
CDL
comes from the observation that the
CDL
can often be significantly smaller than
ECE
, despite their linear relationship in the worst-case. This allows us to bypass the
\Omega(T^{-0.472})
lower bound for
ECE
. Here we provide a typical example where the
CDL
is much smaller than
ECE
. We will first describe the samples from a miscalibrated predictor, then show
ECE
is higher than
CDL
by calculation. Based on the intuition from this example, we establish a general lemma (
Lemma

1.3
) which plays a crucial role in our analysis.

Miscalibrated Predictor.

We now describe the observed samples from a miscalibrated predictor. The predictions are discretized to a finite set
Q:=\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}\}\subseteq[0,1]
, where
q_{i}=i/m
for
i=1,\ldots,m
.
We view each
q_{i}
as a “bucket”, and thus our predictor makes predictions that fall into these buckets.
We use
\mathbb{I}\left[\cdot\right]
to denote the 0-1 indicator function:
\mathbb{I}\left[\text{statement}\right]=1
if the statement is true, and
\mathbb{I}\left[\text{statement}\right]=0
if the statement is false.

For a sequence of
T
predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{T})\in Q^{T}
made by our predictor and the corresponding true states
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{T})\in\{0,1\}^{T}
, let
n_{i}
denote the number of predictions in bucket
i
:

n_{i}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}\left[p_{t}=q_{i}\right],

(1)

and let
\widehat{q}_{i}
denote the empirical average of the true outcomes corresponding to the
n_{i}
predictions:

\widehat{q}_{i}:=\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\theta_{t}\mathbb{I}\left[p_{t}=q_{i}\right].

(2)

Assuming
\sqrt{T}
is an integer, let us choose
m=\sqrt{T}
. Assume for simplicity that each bucket contains the same number of predictions:
n_{i}=\sqrt{T}
for every
i=1,\ldots,m
.
A typical guarantee one can often obtain using existing online learning techniques is the following bound on the deviation between
\widehat{q}_{i}
and
q_{i}
:

|\widehat{q}_{i}-q_{i}|\lesssim 1/\sqrt{n_{i}}=T^{-1/4}.

Thus, in this example, we construct
|\widehat{q}_{i}-q_{i}|=T^{-1/4}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
for simplicity.

ECE
.

ECE
of the example above can be computed as

\textsc{ECE}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}|q_{i}-\widehat{q}_{i}|=T^{-1/4}.

(3)

This is even worse than the
\textsc{ECE}=O(T^{-1/3})
guarantee by the algorithm of
Foster and Vohra (1998)
, mainly because here we discretize the prediction space into
m=\sqrt{T}
buckets, but the optimal choice would be
m\approx T^{1/3}
.

CFDL
and
CDL
.

However, the
CDL
in this example is much smaller: in fact we have
\textsc{CDL}\leq O(T^{-1/2})
. To prove this fact, let us first consider a specific decision task with action space
A=\{0,1\}
, where the payoff is given by

U(a,\theta)=\mathbb{I}\left[\theta=a\right]\quad\text{for $a,\theta\in\{0,1\}$.}

The best response strategy is to take a threshold at
0.5
:

a^{*}(p)=\mathbb{I}\left[p>0.5\right]\quad\text{for $p\in[0,1]$}.

The calibration fixed decision loss (CFDL) on decision task
U
is defined as the improvement in payoff after the decision maker calibrates the predictor:

\displaystyle\textsc{CFDL}_{U}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigg[U(a^{*}(\sigma(p_{t})),\theta_{t})-U(a^{*}(p_{t}),\theta_{t})\bigg],

(4)

where
\sigma
is the mapping that moves each prediction to the actual empirical frequency.

We can decompose the calibration loss bucket-wise as follows:

\displaystyle\textsc{CFDL}_{U}(\bm{a},\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}\left[p_{t}=q_{i}\right]\bigg[U(a^{*}(\widehat{q}_{i}),\theta_{t})-U(a^{*}(q_{i}),\theta_{t})\bigg]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}\E_{\theta\sim\widehat{q}_{i}}[U(a^{*}(\widehat{q}_{i}),\theta)-U(a^{*}(q_{i}),\theta)].\qquad\text{($\theta\in\{0,1\}$ is drawn such that $\Pr[\theta=1]=\widehat{q}_{i}$)}

(5)

If
q_{i}
and
\widehat{q}_{i}
belong to the same half of the interval
[0,1]
, i.e.,
q_{i},\widehat{q}_{i}\in[0,0.5]
or
q_{i},\widehat{q}_{i}\in(0.5,1]
, we have
a^{*}(\widehat{q}_{i})=a^{*}(q_{i})
and thus

\E_{\theta\sim\widehat{q}_{i}}[U(a^{*}(\widehat{q}_{i}),\theta)-U(a^{*}(q_{i}),\theta)]=0.

(6)

If
q_{i}
and
\widehat{q}_{i}
belong to different halves of the interval, a simple calculation gives

\displaystyle\E_{\theta\sim\widehat{q}_{i}}[U(a^{*}(\widehat{q}_{i}),\theta)-U(a^{*}(q_{i}),\theta)]

\displaystyle=\E_{\theta\sim\widehat{q}_{i}}[U(a^{*}(\widehat{q}_{i}),\theta)]-\E_{\theta\sim\widehat{q}_{i}}[U(a^{*}(q_{i}),\theta)]

\displaystyle=\max(\widehat{q}_{i},1-\widehat{q}_{i})-\min(\widehat{q}_{i},1-\widehat{q}_{i})

\displaystyle=2|\widehat{q}_{i}-0.5|

\displaystyle\leq 2|q_{i}-\widehat{q}_{i}|.

(7)

Moreover, if
q_{i}
and
\widehat{q}_{i}
belong to different halves of the interval, by our assumption of
|\widehat{q}_{i}-q_{i}|=T^{-1/4}
, we have
|q_{i}-0.5|\leq T^{-1/4}
.
Plugging (
6
) and (
7
) into (
5
), we get

\textsc{CFDL}_{U}(\bm{a},\bm{\theta})\leq\frac{2}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}|q_{i}-\widehat{q}_{i}|\mathbb{I}\left[|q_{i}-0.5|\leq T^{-1/4}\right].

(8)

Note that the number of
i
’s satisfying
|q_{i}-0.5|\leq T^{-1/4}
is
O(T^{1/4})
. Thus, by our assumption of
n_{i}=\sqrt{T}
and
|q_{i}-\widehat{q}_{i}|=T^{-1/4}
, we get
\textsc{CFDL}_{U}(\bm{a},\bm{\theta})=O(T^{-1/2})
.

While this bound above on
CFDL
is for a specific decision task, we can extend it to
every
payoff-bounded decision task and get
\textsc{CDL}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})=O(T^{-1/2})
. This follows from a result of
Li ⓡ al. (2022)
showing that any payoff-bounded decision task can be expressed as a convex combination of tasks with
V-shaped
payoffs (see
Section

3.2
for more details). Such decision tasks are all very similar to the one we consider here, and we can similarly obtain an
O(T^{-1/2})
bound for the calibration loss for each of them. This reduction to V-shaped payoffs also played a crucial role in the
O(T^{-1/2})
U-calibration guarantee of
Kleinberg et al. (2023)
.

Intuition Generalized.

In our example above, we make the simplifying assumption that the number of predictions in each bucket is the same. However, the
O(T^{-1/2})
bound on the
CDL
holds without this assumption while only losing a logarithmic factor. That is, we have the following lemma:

Lemma 1.3

(Informal special case of
Lemma

5.2
)
.

Let
T,m
be positive integers satisfying
m=\Theta(\sqrt{T})
. Define
Q=\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}\}\subseteq[0,1]
where
q_{i}=i/m
for every
i=1,\ldots,m
.
Given a sequence of predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{T})\in Q^{T}
and realized states
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{T})\in\{0,1\}^{T}
, define
n_{i}
and
\widehat{q}_{i}
as in (
1
) and (
2
).

Assume
|\widehat{q}_{i}-q_{i}|\leq O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{i}}})
for every
i=1,\ldots,m
. Then

\textsc{CDL}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})\leq O(T^{-1/2}\log T).

The intuition behind the lemma can be understood by analyzing the contribution of each bucket to
CDL
. In
ECE
, as expressed in
Equation

3
, the bias
n_{i}|q_{i}-\widehat{q}_{i}|
in every bucket contributes to the average, but in our example above, only a minority of the buckets make positive contribution to the
CDL
, as shown in (
8
). In general, for any specific decision task with V-shaped payoff, we show that the contribution to
CDL
from many buckets
i
is significantly less than the bias
n_{i}|q_{i}-\widehat{q}_{i}|
. This allows us to prove the upper bound on
CDL
in
Lemma

1.3
, which would not hold if
CDL
were replaced by
ECE
.

Given
Lemma

1.3
, it remains to show that the guarantee
|\widehat{q}_{i}-q_{i}|\leq O(1/\sqrt{n}_{i})
can indeed be (approximately) achieved in the online binary prediction setting.
We use the result from
Noarov et al. (2023)
which, as stated, shows an efficient algorithm that gives us a bound only on the maximum of the expectation
\max_{i}({\mathbf{E}}\left[|\widehat{q}_{i}-q_{i}|\right]-O(1/\sqrt{n}_{i}))
, where the expectation is over the randomness in the algorithm.
We refine their analysis and give a bound on the expectation of the maximum
{\mathbf{E}}\left[\max_{i}(|\widehat{q}_{i}-q_{i}|-O(1/\sqrt{n}_{i}))\right]
(
Lemma

5.5
). As we show in
Lemma

5.2
(a generalized version of
Lemma

1.3
), this bound is sufficient for us to obtain
\E[\textsc{CDL}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})]\leq O(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}})
.
We also note that a simpler minimax proof, similar to the one by
Hart (2022)
, also allows us to show the existence of a randomized algorithm that approximately guarantees
|\widehat{q}_{i}-q_{i}|\leq O(1/\sqrt{n}_{i})
and thus, by our
Lemma

5.2
again, achieves
\E[\textsc{CDL}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})]\leq O(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}})
(see
Appendix

A
). However, this proof does not come with an explicit construction or any computational efficiency guarantee.

1.5
Related Work

1.5.1
Calibration Error Metrics

While perfect calibration has an intuitive and clear definition, it is a non-trivial and subtle question to meaningfully quantify the calibration error of predictions that are not perfectly calibrated.
ECE
is one of the most popular calibration measures, but it lacks continuity: slightly perturbing perfectly calibrated predictions can significantly increase
ECE
.
To address this issue,
recently
Błasiok et al. (2023a)
developed a theory of
consistent
calibration measures by introducing the
distance to calibration
as a central notion. This theory has facilitated rigorous explanations of an interesting empirical phenomenon called “calibration out of the box” in deep learning

(
Błasiok et al., 2023b
;
Błasiok et al., 2024
)
.

As a relaxation of calibration,
Kleinberg et al. (2023)
consider the utility of predictions to downstream decision makers and introduce U-calibration error, the maximum external regret over payoff-bounded decision tasks.
Kleinberg et al. (2023)
design an algorithm that achieves an optimal
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
U-calibration, which is a necessary but insufficient condition for calibration.

1.5.2
Online Calibration Algorithms

Recent research has made significant progress in proving upper and lower bounds on the optimal rate achievable for both
ECE
and the distance to calibration in online binary prediction, though significant gaps remain between the current best upper and lower bounds.
For
ECE
minimization,
Foster and Vohra (1998)
shows there exists a randomized algorithm that achieves
O(T^{-1/3})
expected
ECE
, which remains the best known upper bound. Existence proofs and constructions of such algorithms have been further explored in several subsequent works
(
Hart, 2022
;
Foster and Hart, 2021
)
.
A recent work
(
Qiao and Valiant, 2021
)
show a lower bound of
\Omega(T^{-0.472})
to online
ECE
minimization.
For the distance to calibration,
Qiao and Zheng (2024)
give a non-constructive minimax-based proof for an
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
upper bound and an
\Omega(T^{-1/3})
lower bound for the same problem. Soon afterwards,
Arunachaleswaran et al. (2024)
provide a construction of an efficient algorithm that achieves the
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
upper bound for the distance to calibration.

The literature on online regret minimization for all downstream decision makers is technically closest to our paper.
Kleinberg et al. (2023)
observe that the swap regret of actions for any payoff-bounded decision task is linearly upperbounded by
ECE
. This observation quantitatively justifies the qualitative equivalence between no swap regret and calibration in
Foster and Vohra (1997)
. However, the
\Omega(T^{-0.472})
lowerbound presents a barrier in efficient swap regret minimization via
ECE
minimization.
To overcome this barrier from
ECE
, several relaxations of
CDL
have been considered by recent works to achieve
\tilde{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
regret rates in online binary prediction.

Kleinberg et al. (2023)
achieve the
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})

external
regret maximized over payoff-bounded tasks, a necessary but insufficient condition for asymptotic calibration.
On the other hand,
Roth and Shi (2024)
show
O(|A|\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{T}})
swap regret bounds that depend additionally on the number of actions
|A|
in the downstream decision task, which can be loose when a decision task has many or even infinitely many actions.

1.5.3
Omniprediction

Treating prediction and decision making as separate steps allows us to train a single predictor and use it to solve multiple decision tasks with different utility/loss functions. This separation of training and decision making is the idea behind omniprediction, introduced recently by
Gopalan et al. (2022)
, where the goal is to train a single predictor that allows each downstream decision maker to incur comparable or smaller loss than any alternative decision rule from a benchmark class.
Notions from the algorithmic fairness literature (e.g. multicalibration and multiaccuracy
(
Hebert-Johnson et al., 2018
;
Kim et al., 2019
)
) have been used to obtain omnipredictors in various online and offline (batch) settings
(
Gopalan et al., 2022
;
Gopalan et al., 2023a
;
Hu et al., 2023
;
Gopalan et al., 2023b
;
Gopalan et al., 2024
;
Garg et al., 2024
;
Noarov et al., 2023
;
Kim and Perdomo, 2023
)
.
Omniprediction allows better efficiency than training a different model from scratch for each decision task, and it also allows the predictor to be robust to changes in the loss function.

1.5.4
Swap Regret Minimization

Swap regret minimization algorithms have been studied extensively in the online learning literature
(
Hart and Mas-Colell, 2000
;
Hart and Mas-Colell, 2001
;
Blum and Mansour, 2007
;
Hart and Mas-Colell, 2013
;
Anagnostides et al., 2022
, e.g.)
. In game theory, the swap regret is known for its connection to correlated equilibrium.
Foster and Vohra (1997)
first show vanishing swap regret implies convergence to correlated equilibria. Recently,
Peng and Rubinstein (2023)
;
Dagan et al. (2023)
prove a lowerbound on the swap regret, which is polynomial in the number of actions.
Meanwhile, the calibration literature
(
Kleinberg et al., 2023
;
Noarov et al., 2023
;
Roth and Shi, 2024
)
differs from the swap regret minimization literature in two aspects: 1) it focuses on developing a robust strategy that minimizes swap regret simultaneously for all decision makers, and 2) it focuses on minimizing swap regret for the special payoff structure of decision tasks. As a special payoff structure, a decision task restricts the adversary to only be able to select a state. Equivalently, the adversary can select payoff from a low rank matrix with the same rank of the state space.
Our result also implies the lowerbound on swap regret is strictly weaker when there exists a special low-rank constraint on payoff matrix.
While the lowerbound on general swap regret minimization depends polynomially on the number of actions, swap regret minimization of decision tasks does not have such dependence given restricted state space.

1.5.5
Optimization of Scoring Rules

CDL
is defined as the maximum swap regret over all decision tasks. Since the payoff in a decision task can be equivalently represented by proper scoring rules (see
Section

2.3
), the computation of
CDL
is an optimization problem of scoring rules. A recent literature
(
Li ⓡ al., 2022
;
Neyman et al., 2021
;
Hartline ⓡ al., 2023
)
studies the optimization of scoring rules, where
Li ⓡ al. (2022)
is the most relevant paper.
Li ⓡ al. (2022)
present two results that are helpful to our problem: 1) under their different optimization objective, the optimal scoring rule can be computed via linear programming, and 2) any bounded scoring rule can be decomposed into a linear combination of V-shaped scoring rules. Following their idea, we design a linear program that computes
CDL
in polynomial time. Our
O(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}})

CDL
result also uses this linear decomposition of scoring rules (see
Section

3.2
).

2.1
Measures of Calibration Error

In this section, we define empirical calibration on
T
samples and calibration error metrics. Over
T
samples, the predictions are restricted to fall in a finite space
Q=\{q_{i}\in[0,1]\}_{i}
. Let
n_{i}=\sum_{t}\mathbb{I}\left[p_{t}=q_{i}\right]
be the count of prediction being
q_{i}
in
T
samples, and
\widehat{q}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{t}\mathbb{I}\left[p_{t}=q_{i}\right]\theta_{t}}{n_{i}}
be the empirical distribution of the realized state conditioning on prediction is
q_{i}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Given
T
samples, predictor is
empirically calibrated
if for each prediction
q_{i}\in Q
, the prediction is consistent with its conditional empirical distribution, i.e.
q_{i}=\widehat{q}_{i}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given
T
samples, we can
empirically calibrate
a prediction by swapping predictions to their conditional empirical frequencies, i.e. by applying swap mapping
\sigma^{*}
to predictions with
\sigma^{*}(q_{i})=\widehat{q}_{i}
.

While there is only one natural and clear definition of perfect calibration, there are various metrics for measuring the calibration error.

Foster and Vohra (1998)
defines the expected calibration error (ECE) which is a measure of calibration error popularly used in the literature.
ECE
measures the average absolute distance between the prediction and the empirical distribution.

Definition 2.3

(
ECE
)
.

Given
T
samples of predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{t})_{t\in[T]}
and corresponding realizations
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{t\in[T]}
of states.
ECE
is

\textsc{ECE}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}|p_{t}-\sigma^{*}(p_{t})|.

Equivalently,
\textsc{ECE}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{q_{i}\in Q}n_{i}|q_{i}-\widehat{q}_{i}|
.

An alternative metric is the
K_{2}
calibration error, the average squared distance between a prediction and the empirical distribution.
Theorem

2.23
shows it is possible to achieve
\tilde{O}(T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
worst-case expected
K_{2}
error.

Definition 2.4

(
K_{2}
calibration error)
.

Given
T
samples of predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{t})_{t\in[T]}
and corresponding realizations
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{t\in[T]}
of states, the
K_{2}
calibration error is

K_{2}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{q_{i}\in Q}n_{i}(q_{i}-\widehat{q}_{i})^{2}.

In addition to
ECE
and
K_{2}
calibration error, we also compare to the smooth calibration error introduced by
Kakade and Foster (2008)
. Unlike
ECE
and
K_{2}
, the smooth calibration error is continuous in predictions.

Definition 2.5

(Smooth Calibration Error,
Kakade and Foster, 2008
)
.

Given
T
samples of predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{t})_{t\in[T]}
and corresponding realizations
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{t\in[T]}
of states. The smooth calibration error is a supremum over the set
\Sigma
of
1
-Lipschitz functions
\sigma:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
:

\textsc{smCal}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{T}\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sum_{t}\sigma(p_{t})(p_{t}-\theta_{t}).

The definition above is equivalent to
K_{1}
without the
1
-Lipschitz constraint on
\sigma
. Taking the following non-Lipschitz
\sigma
yields
ECE
.

\sigma(q_{i})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }q_{i}-\widehat{q}_{i}\geq 0;\\
-1,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The smooth calibration error is polynomially related to the distance to calibration, which measures the absolute distance to the closest calibrated prediction.

Definition 2.6

(Distance to Calibration,
Błasiok et al., 2023a
)
.

Given
T
samples of predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{t})_{t\in[T]}
and corresponding realizations
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{t\in[T]}
of states. The distance to calibration is

\textsc{distCal}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{T}\min_{\bm{\widehat{p}}:K_{1}(\bm{\widehat{p}},\bm{\theta})=0}\sum_{t}|p_{t}-\widehat{p}_{t}|.

Lemma 2.7

(
Błasiok et al., 2023a
)
.

Smooth calibration error
smCal
is polynomially related to distance to calibration
distCal
.

\textsc{smCal}\leq\textsc{distCal}\leq\sqrt{32\,\textsc{smCal}}.

2.2
Decision Making and Swap Regret

Before introducing predictions and calibration, we define decision making and swap regret.
A decision task is defined by three components. Throughout the paper, we normalize the payoff in the decision task to
[0,1]
.

•

The agent takes action
a\in A
.

•

A payoff-relevant state
\theta\in\Theta
realizes.

•

The agent obtains payoff
U:A\times\Theta\to[0,1]
as a function of the action and the state.
6
6

6

U
can be an arbitrary function.

The decision is evaluated by the average performance when the decision maker repeatedly faces an identical decision task
(A,\Theta,U)
in
T
rounds. We define the swap regret the same as in
Roth and Shi (2024)
.

Definition 2.8

(Swap Regret)
.

Given a sequence of
T
actions
\bm{a}=(a_{t})_{t\in[T]}
and realization
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{t\in[T]}
of states, and fix a decision task with payoff rule
U
, the swap regret of the decision maker is

\textsc{Swap}_{U}(\bm{a},\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{T}\max_{\sigma:A\to A}\sum_{t\in[T]}\bigg[U(\sigma(a_{t}),\theta_{t})-U(a_{t},\theta_{t})\bigg].

Kleinberg et al. (2023)
aim to minimize the external regret for all decision tasks with bounded payoff. We define the external regret here for comparison.

Definition 2.9

(External Regret)
.

Given a sequence of
T
actions
\bm{a}=(a_{t})_{t\in[T]}
and realization
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{t\in[T]}
of states, and fix a decision task with payoff rule
U
, the external regret is calculated against the best fixed action,

\textsc{Ext}_{U}(\bm{a},\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{T}\max_{a\in A}\bigg[U(a,\theta_{t})-U(a_{t},\theta_{t})\bigg].

(9)

Kleinberg et al. (2023)
define the U-calibration error, the maximum external regret when the decision maker best responds to the predictions. Note that no U-calibration error is necessary but insufficient for calibration.

Definition 2.10

(U-calibration Error)
.

Let
\bm{p}=(p_{t})_{t\in[T]}
be a sequence of
T
predictions and let
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{t\in[T]}
be the realization of states. Consider a decision maker with payoff function
U:A\times\{0,1\}\to[0,1]
who best responds to the predictions by taking
a_{t}=a^{*}(p_{t})=\argmax_{a\in A}{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim p_{t}}\left[U(a,\theta)\right]
. Let
\bm{a}_{U}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{T})
be the vector of best-response actions. The U-calibration error is the maximum external regret over decision tasks with bounded payoff in
[0,1]
:

\textsc{UCal}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})=\sup_{U}\textsc{Ext}_{U}(\bm{a}_{U},\bm{\theta}),

where the supremum is over all payoff functions
U:A\times\{0,1\}\to[0,1]
with arbitrary action spaces
A
.

2.3
Payoff as Proper Scoring Rules

For ease of notation throughout the paper, we will write the payoff as a function of the prediction assuming the agent trusts the prediction and best responds to it. In this section, we introduce this payoff as a function of the prediction and show its equivalence to proper scoring rules.

When the agent is assisted with a sequence of predictions, the agent obtains payoff by acting in response to the prediction. If the prediction is calibrated, the best response
a^{*}
is a function of the prediction:

a^{*}(p)=\argmax_{a\in A}{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim p}\left[U(a,\theta)\right].

(10)

The payoff is equivalently a function of the prediction. We present the payoff in a scoring rule
S:\Delta(\Theta)\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
, representing the utility directly as a function of the prediction.

Definition 2.11

(Scoring rule induced by decision task)
.

Given a decision task
(A,\Theta,U)
and its corresponding best response function
a^{*}
, we define an induced scoring rule
S_{U}:\Delta(\Theta)\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
such that for any prediction
p\in\Delta(\Theta)
and state
\theta\in\Theta
,

S_{U}(p,\theta)=U(a^{*}(p),\theta).

That is,
S_{U}(p,\theta)
is the payoff of a decision maker who chooses the best response
a^{*}(p)
based on the prediction
p
.

Frongillo and Kash (2014)
observe that scoring rules induced by a decision task are equivalent to the class of proper scoring rules
(
McCarthy, 1956
)
. Proper scoring rules are defined such that the expected score is maximized when the prediction is the true distribution. Evaluated with a proper scoring rule, the predictor has an incentive to truthfully report the distribution in order to maximize expected score.

Definition 2.12

(Proper Scoring Rule)
.

A scoring rule
S
is proper if for any
p^{\prime}\in\Delta(\Theta)
that is not the true distribution
\theta
is generated,

{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim p}\left[S(p,\theta)\right]\geq{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim p}\left[S(p^{\prime},\theta)\right].

Claim

2.13
shows that 1) the scoring rule induced by a decision task is a proper scoring rule, and 2) any proper scoring rule measures the best-response payoff in a decision task.

Claim 2.13
.

Proper scoring rules and payoffs in decision task are equivalent:

1.

Any scoring rule
S_{U}
induced by a decision task
(A,\Theta,U)
is proper.

2.

For any proper scoring rule
S
, there exists a decision task with payoff
U
, such that
U(a^{*}(p),\theta)=S(p,\theta)
for every prediction
p
.

Proof.

2 is straight forward by defining
A=\Delta(\Theta)
, the action space as the prediction space.

To see 1, notice that the score is the best-response score with
S_{U}(p,\theta)=U(a^{*}(p),\theta)
. Hence, if the prediction is
p^{\prime}\neq p
not the distribution where
\theta
is drawn, the agent obtains a payoff weakly lower than the payoff by best responding to the true distribution.

{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim p}\left[S(p^{\prime},\theta)\right]={\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim p}\left[U(a^{*}(p^{\prime}),\theta)\right]\leq{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim p}\left[U(a^{*}(p),\theta)\right]={\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim p}\left[S(p,\theta)\right].\qed

Following this equivalence between proper scoring rules and best-respond payoffs, we will introduce our results in a proper scoring rule
S
instead of the decision payoff
U
.

2.4
Calibration Fixed Decision Loss

In the previous section, we assume that the agent trusts the prediction and best responds to it. If, however, the agent best responds to miscalibrated predictions, she suffers the Calibration Fixed Decision Loss (
CFDL
), the improvement in payoff when she empirically calibrates the predictions.

Definition 2.14

(
CFDL
)
.

Given
T
samples of predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{t})_{t\in[T]}
and corresponding realizations
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{t\in[T]}
of states, fixing a proper scoring rule
S
, the Calibration Fixed Decision Loss (
CFDL
) is

\textsc{CFDL}_{S}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\bigg[S(\sigma^{*}(p_{t}),\theta_{t})-S(p_{t},\theta_{t})\bigg],

where
\sigma^{*}
is the swap mapping that empirically calibrates the predictor (see
Definition

2.2
).

The
CFDL
has an equivalent formalization as prediction swap regret, where the agent is allowed to swap predictions in hindsight. Note that the prediction swap regret is weakly stronger than the swap regret (
Definition

2.8
) on the same decision task.

Definition 2.15

(Prediction Swap Regret)
.

Given a sequence of
T
predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{t})_{t\in[T]}
and realization
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{t\in[T]}
of states, fixing a proper scoring rule
S
, the prediction swap regret is

\textsc{PSwap}_{S}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})=\max_{\sigma:\Delta(\Theta)\to\Delta(\Theta)}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\bigg[S(\sigma(p_{t}),\theta_{t})-S(p_{t},\theta_{t})\bigg].

Proposition

2.16
shows
CFDL
is equivalent to prediction swap regret.

Proposition 2.16
.

Given a sequence of
T
predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{t})_{t\in[T]}
and realization
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{t\in[T]}
of states, fixing a proper scoring rule
S
, CFDL equals the prediction swap regret:

\textsc{CFDL}_{S}=\textsc{PSwap}_{S}.

Proposition

2.16
follows directly from the properness of scoring rules. Conditioning on the prediction is
q_{i}
, the state follows the empirical distribution:

\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{t\in[T]}S(p,\theta_{t})\mathbb{I}\left[p_{t}=q_{i}\right]={\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim\widehat{q}_{i}}\left[S(p,\theta)\right].

By properness of the scoring rule, predicting the empirical distribution maximizes the expected score (average score). Thus, the optimal swap function in
Definition

2.15
is
\sigma^{*}
which empirically calibrates the predictor.

The prediction swap regret is stronger than the swap regret in
Definition

2.8
. If an algorithm generates a sequence of predictions with low prediction swap regret, then the agent has low swap regret if they best respond to the predictions. To see this, notice that the modification rule in prediction swap regret has more power. The prediction swap regret allows the agent to modify the action conditioning on each prediction. If two predictions have the same best-response action, the swap regret does not allow the agent to apply different modification to the two predictions.

2.5
Online Binary Calibration

We focus on the classic online prediction problem studied by
Foster and Vohra (1998)
. The goal is to generate calibrated predictions in the long run, even if the states are adversarially selected. Unless otherwise specified, we present results under the binary prediction setting, i.e. the state space is
\Theta=\{0,1\}
.
In this setup, a predictor (algorithm)
F
makes a prediction
p_{t}\in[0,1]
at each round
t=1,2,\ldots
, and an adversary
A
picks a binary state (outcome)
\theta_{t}\in\{0,1\}
.
Both the prediction
p_{t}
and the state
\theta_{t}
can depend on the past history
h_{t-1}=(p_{1},\theta_{1},\dots,p_{t-1},\theta_{t-1})
, but they cannot depend on each other. That is, we can assume without loss of generality that the algorithm chooses
p_{t}
and the adversary reveals
\theta_{t}
simultaneously. The transcript
h_{t}=(p_{1},\theta_{1},\ldots,p_{t},\theta_{t})
is a function of the strategies of the predictor
F
and the adversary
A
. That is,
h_{t}=h_{t}(F,A)
. The predictions in
T
rounds are evaluated by a calibration error metric.

We allow the predictor to be randomized, in which case we can view its strategy as a distribution
{\mathcal{F}}
over deterministic strategies
F
. When we use an error metric, say
ECE
, to evaluate the predictions made by our predictor in
T
rounds, our goal is to minimize the expected value of the error metric w.r.t. the worst-case adversary
A
, i.e., we want
{\mathbf{E}}_{F\sim{\mathcal{F}}}\left[\textsc{CDL}(h_{T}(F,A))\right]
to be small for every adversary
A
.

Previous results for different calibration errors are listed here.

ECE

There exists a gap between the known upperbound and lowerbound for
ECE
.

Upperbound

Theorem 2.21

(
Foster and Vohra, 1998
, see also
Hart, 2022
;
Foster and Hart, 2021
)
.

There exists a randomized online binary prediction algorithm that guarantees
O(T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}})
expected
ECE
.

Lowerbound

Theorem 2.22

(
Qiao and Valiant, 2021
)
.

For any randomized online binary prediction algorithm, the
ECE
w.r.t. the worst-case adversary is
\Omega(T^{-0.472})
.

K_{2}
Calibration Error

As a variant of
ECE
,
K_{2}
calibration error has an upperbound result from the literature.

Upperbound

Theorem 2.23

(
Roth, 2022
)
.

There exists a randomized online binary prediction algorithm that guarantees
O(T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\log T)
expected
K_{2}
calibration error.

Smooth Calibration Error

Błasiok et al. (2023a)
propose the smooth calibration error.

Upperbound

Qiao and Zheng (2024)
prove the existence of an algorithm that achieves
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
distance to calibration, following which
Arunachaleswaran et al. (2024)
give a construction of such an algorithm. The result implies
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
smooth calibration error, since the distance to calibration upperbounds the smooth calibration error.

Theorem 2.24

(
Qiao and Zheng, 2024
;
Arunachaleswaran et al., 2024
)
.

There exists a randomized online binary prediction algorithm that guarantees
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
expected distance to calibration.

Lowerbound

Theorem 2.25

(
Qiao and Zheng, 2024
)
.

For any randomized online binary prediction algorithm, the smooth calibration error w.r.t. the worst-case adversary is
\Omega(T^{-\frac{2}{3}})
.

3
Calibration Decision Loss

In this section, we introduce our calibration error metric, the Calibration Decision Loss (CDL), meaning the decision loss resulted from miscalibration. In
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
, we discuss connections between
CDL
and Bregman divergences that will be useful for obtaining our main result in
Section

5
. We show an efficient algorithm for computing
CDL
in
Section

3.3
.

Definition 3.1

(
CDL
)
.

Given a sequence of
T
predictions
\bm{p}=(p_{t})_{t\in[T]}
and realization
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{t\in[T]}
of states, we define the Calibration Decision Loss (
CDL
) as

\textsc{CDL}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})=\sup_{S\in[0,1]}\textsc{CFDL}_{S}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta}),

(11)

where the supremum is over all proper scoring rules
S
with range bounded in
[0,1]
.

The
CDL
as defined above is equal to the maximum swap regret (
Definition

2.8
) of best responding decision makers with payoffs bounded in
[0,1]
:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\bm{p}=(p_{t})_{t\in[T]}
be a sequence of
T
predictions and let
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{t\in[T]}
be the realization of states. Consider a decision maker with payoff function
U:A\times\{0,1\}\to[0,1]
who best responds to the predictions by taking
a_{t}=a^{*}(p_{t})=\argmax_{a\in A}{\mathbf{E}}_{\theta\sim p_{t}}\left[U(a,\theta)\right]
. Let
\bm{a}_{U}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{T})
be the vector of best-response actions. We have

\textsc{CDL}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})=\sup_{U}\textsc{Swap}_{U}(\bm{a}_{U},\bm{\theta}),

where the supremum is over all payoff functions
U:A\times\{0,1\}\to[0,1]
with arbitrary action spaces
A
.

Proof.

For any decision task with payoff function
U
, the prediction swap regret for the corresponding scoring rule
S
is higher than the swap regret:
\textsc{Swap}_{U}(\bm{a}_{U},\bm{\theta})\leq\textsc{PSwap}_{S_{U}}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})
(
Claim

2.17
). Thus,
\textsc{CDL}\geq\sup_{U}\textsc{Swap}_{U}(\bm{a}_{U},\bm{\theta})
. On the other hand, for any scoring rule
S
, construct a decision task with payoff
U
as in the proof of
Claim

2.13
, by setting
A=[0,1]
. The resulting decision task has
\textsc{PSwap}_{S}=\textsc{Swap}_{U}
.
Thus we have
\textsc{CDL}=\sup_{U}\textsc{Swap}_{U}(\bm{a}_{U},\bm{\theta})
.
∎

By definition, if
CDL
vanishes, then
CFDL
(and thus swap regret) also vanishes for every downstream agent.

Proposition 3.3
.

If
\textsc{CDL}=\tilde{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
, then both
\textsc{CFDL}=\tilde{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
and
\textsc{Swap}=\tilde{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
simultaneously for every downstream decision task with payoff bounded in
[0,1]
.

As an important preparation for our main result in

Each proper scoring rule induces a Bregman divergence, measuring the loss from incorrect prediction. The Bregman divergence is defined with the convex function of a scoring rule. See

Theorem 3.4

(

A scoring rule is proper if and only if there exists a convex function

(12)

{\mathbf{E}}\left[\theta\right]=p

.

Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i\in[m]}n_{i}\textsc{Breg}_{S_{j}}(q_{i},\widehat{q}_{i})

3.3
Computation of
CDL

We allow the state space to be non-binary in this section. By solving a linear program, the
CDL
can be computed in time polynomial in the size of the prediction space
|Q|
and the state space
|\Theta|
.

Theorem 3.11
.

Given a sequence of predictions
\bm{p}
and states
\bm{\theta}
, suppose
Q
is the space of predictions.
CDL
can be computed in time polynomial in
|Q|
and
|\Theta|
.

Proof.

The computation of
CDL
is an optimization problem over the space of proper scoring rules. We follow the idea in
Li ⓡ al. (2022)
;
Kleinberg et al. (2023)
. The optimal scoring rule can be computed by solving a linear program. Let
\widehat{Q}=\{\widehat{q}_{i}\}_{i}
be the set of empirical distributions for each
q_{i}\in Q
. Define
\widehat{\Theta}=\{\tilde{\theta}\mid j\in|\Theta|,\tilde{\theta}_{j}=1,\tilde{\theta}_{j^{\prime}\neq j}=0\}
as the set of indicator predictions of a certain state. Define the space of predictions as
\mathcal{Q}=Q\cup\widehat{Q}\cup\widehat{\Theta}
. We set the scores
s_{q,\theta},\forall q\in\mathcal{Q},\theta\in\Theta
as variables in the linear program.

\displaystyle\max_{S}\qquad

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{q\in Q,\theta\in\Theta}(s_{\widehat{q},\theta}-s_{q,\theta})\widehat{q}(\theta)

s.t.

\displaystyle s_{q,\theta}\in[0,1],\quad\forall q\in\mathcal{Q},\theta\in\Theta

(bounded payoff)

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta}q(\theta)s_{q,\theta}\geq\sum_{\theta}q(\theta)s_{q^{\prime},\theta},\quad\forall q,q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}

(properness)

The following proper scoring rule achieves the worst case

We are now ready to prove that
Algorithm

1
guarantees a small value of
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})
in expectation:

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
C>0
be the absolute constant from
Lemma

5.3
.
Assume
m,T\geq 2
and
\varepsilon=1/T
in
Algorithm

1
.
Let
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{T})
be the predictions made by
Algorithm

1
on the adversarially chosen states
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{T})
.
Define
\mathcal{D}
as in (
19
) for
\alpha=C\sqrt{\log(mT)},\beta=1/m
. Then,

{\mathbf{E}}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})\right]\leq O(\log(mT)),

where the expectation is over the randomness of
Algorithm

1
.

Remark 5.6
.

We define
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})
in (
19
) as a maximum over the buckets
i
. Thus,
Lemma

5.5
shows an upper bound on the “expectation of maximum”, which is stronger than guarantee stated in
Noarov et al. (2023)
on the “maximum of expectation”. In our proof below, we use a slightly more careful analysis than what is used by
Noarov et al. (2023)
to achieve the stronger guarantee.

Proof of
Lemma

5.5
.

For every
i=1,\ldots,m
, by the definition of
G_{i}
in
Lemma

5.2
, we have

\displaystyle G_{i}=n_{i}|q_{i}-\widehat{q}_{i}|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}\left[p_{t}=q_{i}\right](p_{t}-\theta_{t})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}\left[p_{t}=q_{i}\right](\widetilde{p}_{t}-\theta_{t})\right|+n_{i}/m

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma=\pm 1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}l_{i,\sigma}(\widetilde{p}_{t},\theta_{t})+n_{i}/m

(by (
25
))

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma=\pm 1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}l_{t,i,\sigma}+n_{i}/m.

(by (
29
))

Therefore, by (
27
),

\displaystyle G_{i}-n_{i}/m-\alpha\sqrt{n_{i}}\leq\left(\max_{\sigma=\pm 1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}l_{t,i,\sigma}\right)-\alpha\sqrt{n_{i}}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[m],\sigma^{\prime}=\pm 1}{w_{t,i^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}}l_{t,i^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}+C\log(mT).

By (
26
),

0\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i\in[m],\sigma=\pm 1}{w_{t,i,\sigma}}l_{t,i,\sigma}+C\log(mT).

Combining the two inequalities above, we get

\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i\in[m],\sigma=\pm 1}{w_{t,i,\sigma}}l_{t,i,\sigma}+C\log(mT).

(30)

By the definition of
h_{t}
in (
28
), the guarantee of
h_{t}(s_{t})\leq\epsilon
, and the fact that
\widetilde{p}_{t}
is drawn from
s_{t}
, we get

{\mathbf{E}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i\in[m],\sigma=\pm 1}{w_{t,i,\sigma}}l_{t,i,\sigma}\right]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\epsilon\leq 1.

(31)

Combining (
30
) and (
31
), we get

{\mathbf{E}}\left[\mathcal{D}(\tilde{\bm{p}},\bm{\theta})\right]\leq O(\log(mT)).\qed

We now complete the proof of our main theorem.

Proof of
Theorem

.

We choose
\epsilon=1/T
in
Algorithm

and set
m=\Theta(\sqrt{T}/\log T)
.
Following the setting of

We have

Appendix A
Minimax Proof for Minimizing
CDL

We prove the existence of an algorithm that achieves
O(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}})

CDL
via the minimax theorem. The minimax theorem allows us to assume that the adversary’s (randomized) strategy is fixed and known by the predictor. Our proof demonstrates a remarkable difference between
ECE
and
CDL
- simply by truthfully reporting the mean of each state (conditioned on the history and rounded to a suitable finite subset of
[0,1]
), our predictor achieves the
O(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}})
CDL rate that nearly matches the natural
\Omega(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
lower bound.

In this setup, a predictor
F
makes a prediction
p_{t}\in[0,1]
at each time step
t=1,2,\ldots
, and an adversary
A
picks an outcome
\theta_{t}\in\{0,1\}
. Both
p_{t}
and
\theta_{t}
are chosen based on the history
h_{t-1}=(p_{1},\theta_{1},p_{2},\theta_{2},\ldots,p_{t-1},\theta_{t-1})
. The goal of the predictor is to minimize
\textsc{CDL}(h_{T})
.

We can identify a predictor
F
by its strategy at each time step. That is, we can write
F=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{T})
, where
F_{t}
is a function mapping from
h_{t-1}
to
p_{t}
. Similarly, we can identify the adversary
A
by its strategy
A_{t}
at each time step
t
, writing
A
as
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{T})
. Given the strategies
F
and
A
of both players, we use
h_{F,A}
to denote the history
h_{T}=(p_{1},\theta_{1},\ldots,p_{T},\theta_{T})
generated by executing these strategies.

We will allow the predictor to randomize and play a mixed strategy which can be identified as a probability distribution
{\mathcal{F}}
over all predictor strategies
F
.

Theorem A.1

(Existence)
.

There exists a mixed predictor strategy
{\mathcal{F}}
such that

\max_{A}\E_{F\sim{\mathcal{F}}}[\textsc{CDL}(h_{F,A})]=O\Big(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}}\Big),

where the maximum is over all strategies
A
of the adversary.

In fact, we will prove a stronger version of
Theorem

A.1
, where we restrict the predictor to make predictions
p_{t}
in a discretized space
Q=\{q_{i}=\frac{i}{m}\}_{i\in[m]}\subseteq[0,1]
.
We say a predictor strategy
F=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{T})
is
discretized
if each
F_{t}
is a function mapping from history
h_{t-1}
to
p_{t}\in Q
. This discretization makes the (pure) strategy space of the predictor to be finite, allowing us to apply the minimax theorem.

We prove the following statement which implies
Theorem

A.1
immediately: there exists a positive integer
m
such that

\min_{{\mathcal{F}}}\max_{A}\E_{F\sim{\mathcal{F}}}[\textsc{CDL}(h_{F,A})]=O\Big(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}}\Big),

where the minimum is over a distribution
{\mathcal{F}}
over discretized predictor strategies
F
, and the maximum is over all strategies
A
of the adversary.

By the minimax theorem,

\min_{{\mathcal{F}}}\max_{A}\E_{F\sim{\mathcal{F}}}[\textsc{CDL}(h_{F,A})]=\max_{{\mathcal{A}}}\min_{F}\E_{A\sim{\mathcal{A}}}[\textsc{CDL}(h_{F,A})].

Thus, it suffices to reverse the order of play and prove
Lemma

A.2
.

Lemma A.2
.

There exists a positive integer
m
such that

\max_{{\mathcal{A}}}\min_{F}\E_{A\sim{\mathcal{A}}}[\textsc{CDL}(h_{F,A})]\leq O\Big(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}}\Big).

where the minimum is over a deterministic discretized predictor strategy
F
, and the maximum is over a distribution over strategies
A
of the adversary.

To show
Lemma

A.2
, consider the truthful strategy of the predictor. At round
t
, let
\tilde{p}_{t}
denote the conditional expectation of the adversary’s choice
\theta_{t}
given past history. The predictor outputs the closest value
p_{t}
in
Q
to
\tilde{p}_{t}
.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{T})
and
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{T})
be the sequence of predictions and states generated when the predictor uses the truthful strategy above.
For
\alpha\geq 0
and
\beta=\frac{1}{m}
, define
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})
as in
Equation

19
. We have

{\mathbf{E}}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})\right]\leq 2T^{2}m\exp{(-\alpha^{2}/2)}.

Proof of
Lemma

A.3
.

For bucket
i
, construct random variables
X_{1}^{(i)},\dots X_{T}^{(i)}
such that
X_{j}^{(i)}=\theta_{t_{j}}-\widetilde{p}_{t_{j}}
, where
t_{j}
is the index of the
j
-th round in which the predictor predicts
q_{i}
. If the number
n_{i}
of rounds with prediction
q_{i}
is smaller than
j
, we define
X_{j}^{(i)}=0
.
For any
n\in[T]
, by Azuma’s inequality,

\Pr\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}^{(i)}\right|>\alpha\sqrt{n}\right]\leq 2\exp{(\frac{-\alpha^{2}}{2})}.

By the union bound,

\Pr\left[\exists n\in[T],i\in[m],\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}^{(i)}\right|>\alpha\sqrt{n}\right]\leq 2Tm\exp{(\frac{-\alpha^{2}}{2})}.

(32)

Therefore, with probability at least
1-2Tm\exp{(\frac{-\alpha^{2}}{2})}
, for every
i\in[m]
, we have

\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}X_{j}^{(i)}\right|\leq\alpha\sqrt{n_{i}}.

This implies

G_{i}=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}(p_{t_{j}}-\theta_{t_{j}})\right|\leq\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}(\widetilde{p}_{t_{j}}-\theta_{t_{j}})\right|+n_{i}/m\leq\alpha\sqrt{n_{i}}+\beta n_{i}.

Thus,
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})=0
with probability at least
1-2Tm\exp{(\frac{-\alpha^{2}}{2})}
.
Since
\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})\leq T
,

{\mathbf{E}}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})\right]\leq 2T^{2}m\exp{(\frac{-\alpha^{2}}{2})}.

∎

Proof of
Lemma

A.2
.

Taking
\alpha=2\sqrt{\log(Tm)}
in
Lemma

A.3
, we get

{\mathbf{E}}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})\right]\leq 2.

We choose
m=\Theta(\sqrt{T}/\log(T))
buckets and set
\beta=\frac{1}{m}
. By
Lemma

5.2
,

\displaystyle\textsc{CDL}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4m}{T}\mathcal{D}(\bm{p},\bm{\theta})+\frac{4\alpha}{\sqrt{T}}+4\beta+O\Big(\frac{\alpha^{2}m\log m}{T}\Big).

\displaystyle\leq O\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}\log T}\Big)+O\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\log T}}{\sqrt{T}}\Big)+O\Big(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}}\Big)+O\Big(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}}\Big)

\displaystyle=O\Big(\frac{\log T}{\sqrt{T}}\Big).\qed
