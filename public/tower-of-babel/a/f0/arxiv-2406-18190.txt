Explicit Analytic Continuation of Euler Products
Brandon Alberts

Abstract
. The generating series of a number of different objects studied in arithmetic statistics can be built out of Euler products. Euler products often have very nice analytic properties, and by constructing a meromorphic continuation one can use complex analytic techniques, including Tauberian theorems to prove asymptotic counting theorems for these objects. One standard technique for producing a meromorphic continuation is to factor out copies of the Riemann zeta function, for which a meromorphic continuation is already known. This paper is an exposition of the “Factorization Method” for meromorphic continuation. We provide the following three resources with an eye towards research in arithmetic statistics: (1) an introduction to this technique targeted at new researchers, (2) exposition of existing works, with self-contained proofs, that give a continuation of Euler products with constant or Frobenain coefficients to the right halfplane Re ⁡ ( s ) > 0 {\rm Re}(s)>0 (away from an isolated set of singularities), and (3) explicit statements on the locations and orders of all singularities for these Euler products.



1.
Introduction

The generating Dirichlet series of arithmetic objects are very often built from Euler products. An important step in understanding the distribution of such arithmetic objects is constructing meromorphic continuations of the relevant Euler products.

There are two well-worn approaches to this problem:

(1)

Functional Equation Method:
The primary method for constructing meromorphic continuations of a Dirichlet series is by proving the existence of a functional equation. This is done for the Riemann zeta function and many others including Dedekind zeta functions, Dirichlet and Hecke
L
-functions, and the
L
-functions associated to elliptic curves and other abelian varieties.

This type of construction betrays deep and powerful arithmetic structure, sometimes going beyond our current understanding of the subject. For this reason, it is not surprising to find that constructing a functional equation can be very involved and sometimes not possible for certain Dirichlet series.

(2)

Factorization Method:
This is a more accessible approach for constructing meromorphic continuations of a Dirichlet series that builds on our knowledge of already existing examples. The Dirichlet series is decomposed in terms of (usually as a sum or product of) Dirichlet series for which one factor has a meromorphic continuation proven by other means, and the other factor converges absolutely on a larger region. In the case of Euler products, this is usually done by writing the Dirichlet series as a product of
\zeta(s)^{k}
(or similar
L
-functions) with another absolutely convergent Euler product.

This method would never work in a vacuum - it requires us to build off of a collection of Dirichlet series with Euler products whose meromorophic continuations we already know.

Often, the Factorization Method is easier than proving the existence of a functional equation, and tends to work for a larger class of Dirichlet series. Consider the following example:

Example 1.1
.

Consider

\displaystyle\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s})=\zeta(s)^{2}\prod_{p}(1-3p^{-2s}+2p^{-3s}).

This can be checked locally by verifying that

1+2p^{-s}=(1-p^{-s})^{-2}(1-3p^{-2s}+2p^{-3s})

for every prime
p
. This is a standard example of constructing a meromorphic continuation. While the left-hand side is absolutely convergent for
{\rm Re}(s)>1
, the right-hand side is a product of
\zeta(s)^{2}
(which is meromophic on all of
\mathbb{C}
) and an Euler product with converges absolutely on
{\rm Re}(s)>1/2
. Not only does this extend the region of meromorphicity, we can immediately identify the only pole in this region as being
s=1
of order
2
. Moreover, the nonzero limit

\lim_{s\to 1}(s-1)^{2}\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s})

is given explicitly by the convergent Euler product

\prod_{p}(1-3p^{-2}+2p^{-3}).

Example
1.1
showcases a common target for work in arithmetic statistics:
s=1
is the
rightmost singularity
of the Dirichlet series, which means that it is the singularity with the largest real part. In arithmetic statistics, meromorphic continuations of
\sum a_{n}n^{-s}
are primarily constructed on an open neighborhood of the rightmost singularity so that the Selberg–Delange method can be used to convert the rightmost singularity into asymptotic information for the sum of coefficients function
\sum_{n<X}a_{n}
. (The Selberg–Delange method is also colloquially referred to as a “Tauberian theorem”). The location, order, and coefficient of the rightmost singularity together provide the necessary information to compute the main term in the asymptotic growth rate.

This paper has been separated into three parts in order to address three separate goals:

(1)

We present an expository introduction to common techniques and heuristics for using the Factorization Method to compute the rightmost singularity, geared towards research in arithmetic statistics.

This is done in Part
I
. We intend for these sections to serve as an introduction of these methods to new researchers in the area, with a particular focus on computational examples. This section could be included with a first course in analytic number theory: a typical introduction to generating Dirichlet series provides ample background.

(2)

We give an exposition of previous work on continuations for certain families of Euler products via the Factorization Method, together with self-contained proofs, in Part
II
. The results we explore have a particular focus on cases that are known to be common in arithmetic statistics research.

We mostly focus on an older program of study, tracing back to work of Estermann
[
Est28
]
, for determining the largest region on which a continuation exists. This is referred to as finding the “natural boundary” for the function. These works typically do not always provide explicit information about the locations and orders of any singularities, although such information can usually be teased out of their proofs. We will primarily focus on constructing the analytic continuations considered in
[
Est28
]
and in
[
Dah52
,
Kur78
,
Kur86a
,
Kur86b
,
LW20
,
Mor88
]
following Estermann’s work. We will briefly discuss the theory of the natural boundary of Euler products in Section
12
, although our focus will be on the construction of analytic continuations.

The program following Estermann’s work produces the data underlying analytic continuation all at once, packaged as an infinite product of powers of the Riemann zeta function (see Section
8
as well as the sequence
b_{n}(Q)
in Section
9
). This is in contrast to more modern results in arithmetic statistics, which focus on explicit computation of one singularity at a time (from right to left) as is necessary to apply the Selberg–Delange method. Researchers in arithmetic statistics are typically less concerned with the natural boundary. A non-exhaustive list of such papers includes the analytic sections of
[
Alb21
,
FLN19
,
KMTB15
,
Woo09
]
, where the necessary meromorphic continuations are proven as analytic lemmas. Most of these results reprove special cases of previously known meromorphic continuations, only as far to the left as necessary to reveal the rightmost singularity.

(3)

Finally, in Part
III
we give statements for continuations for certain families of Euler products via the Factorization Method which include explicit descriptions of the locations and orders of any singularities. In order to make these results more accessible for computation, we prove new explicit formulas for the coefficients of certain log power series in Theorem
14.1
and Theorem
15.3
.

We tailor these results for use in arithmetic statistics research, specifically providing enough information for researchers to apply the Selberg–Delange method to produce an asymptotic main term, power saving error terms when possible, and secondary asymptotic terms (which correspond to the singularities to the left of the rightmost singularity) when these exist.

Our intention is for this paper to be a good reference for research in arithmetic statistics. The Euler products considered in this paper are not an exhaustive list for which the Factorization Method will work. We restrict ourselves to the most relevant cases to research in arithmetic statistics, but with some modification these techniques will apply to numerous other cases as well. Towards this end, we include some remarks and technical lemmas in greater generality than we will use.

Part I
Calculating the Rightmost Singularity

2.
A Recipe for the Factorization Method

In Example
1.1
, we provided a factorization of the Euler product and verified it directly. However, in practice you will typically need to construct the decomposition from scratch. The Factorization Method for actually computing the rightmost singularity via this type of decomposition will always broadly follow the same steps:

(1)

Identify the
minimum degree term(s)
in each Euler factor (excluding the constant term) as described in Heuristic
3.1
. Use these to identify
a
and
b
for which the heuristic predicts a pole at
s=1/a
of order
b
.

(2)

Multiply the top and bottom by a
strategic Euler factor
corresponding to each minimum degree term. This factor is chosen to make the following two steps work as intended.

(3)

Separate the denominators, and pull them the outside. This product should match the Euler product of a Dirichlet series with a well-known meromorphic continuation, such as the Riemann Zeta function or an
L
-function.

(4)

Multiply out the numerators. The minimum degree terms should cancel out, and the resulting Euler product should converge absolutely on a larger region.

Let’s walk through this process to recreate the factorization in Example
1.1
.

Example 2.1
.

We again consider the Dirichlet series

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s}).

We now apply the four steps to the Factorization method:

(1)

The minimum degree term of each Euler factor is
2p^{-s}
. The degree is
a=1
, and the leading coefficient is a constant value
b=2
.

(2)

We choose the strategic Euler factor
(1-p^{-as})^{b}
based on the
a
and
b
values from step (1). In our case this is
(1-p^{-s})^{2}
, so that multiplying top and bottom gives

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\prod_{p}\frac{(1+2p^{-s})(1-p^{-s})^{2}}{(1-p^{-s})^{2}}.

We will see why this choice is the right one in steps (3) and (4).

(3)

Pulling out the denominators, the product

\prod_{p}\frac{1}{(1-p^{-s})^{2}}

is recognized to be the Euler product for
\zeta(s)^{2}
. This is part of the reason that
(1-p^{-s})^{2}
is a good choice. Thus, we have shown

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\zeta(s)^{2}\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s})(1-p^{-s})^{2}.

(4)

Next, we multiply out the remaining numerator

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(s)

\displaystyle=\zeta(s)^{2}\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s})(1-2p^{-s}+p^{-2s})

\displaystyle=\zeta(s)^{2}\prod_{p}(1-3p^{-2s}+2p^{-3s}).

Notice that the minimum degree terms cancel out, leaving
p^{-2s}
as the smallest remaining degree. This is the other reason that
(1-p^{-s})^{2}
is a good choice. We will see in Example
4.2
how to conclude that the remaining Euler product converges absolutely on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/2
.

We’ve bolded the names “
minimum degree term(s)
” and “
strategic Euler factor
” in the Factorization Method on purpose. This is merely an outline of a general method, not a theorem, where our goal in defining these terms is to make it so that steps (3) and (4) succeed. This can be more of an art than a science, and it depends very strongly on which “Dirichlet series with a well-known meromorphic continuation” you plan or expect to factor out in step (3).

We will explore several families of Dirichlet series where the best choice of minimum degree terms and strategic Euler factors are well understood, and we will explain where the choices come from. The example

bp^{-as}\longmapsto(1-p^{-as})^{b}

is a good one to keep in mind as we explore more involved cases. However, we warn the reader that applying the Factorization Method to other Euler products may require some creativity.

3.
A Heuristic for the Rightmost Singularity

Many researchers are adept at predicting the location of the rightmost pole of an Euler product, often times without needing to perform any computations. This is because they know the Factorization Method suggests that the rightmost singularity comes from the minimum degree terms, which is easily read off the Euler product in nice cases.

This has developed into a heuristic:

Heuristic 3.1
.

Consider the Euler product

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\prod_{p}\left(1+b_{p}p^{-as}+\cdots\right).

If
b_{p}
and the higher degree terms are “reasonable”, then each of the following hold:

(i)

\mathcal{Q}(s)
converges absolutely on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/a
,

(ii)

There exists a meromorphic continuation of
\mathcal{Q}(s)
to (a branch cut of) an open neighborhood of
{\rm Re}(s)\geq 1/a

(iii)

The rightmost singularity is at
s=1/a
with order
b
given by the average value of
b_{p}
, i.e.

b:=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\sum_{p\leq x}b_{p}.

Moreover, a branch cut is required in (ii) if and only if
b\not\in\mathbb{Z}
.

A “singularity of order
b
” at
s=1/a
means that
(s-1/a)^{b}\mathcal{Q}(s)
is holomorphic and nonzero at
s=1/a
. If
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, we interpret this to mean that
\mathcal{Q}(s)
has no singularities in an open neighborhood of
{\rm Re}(s)\geq 1/a
. If
b=0
, we would predict
\mathcal{Q}(1/a)\neq 0
, and if
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{<0}
we would predict
\mathcal{Q}(1/a)=0
is a root of order
|b|
.

Example
1.1
is an excellent example of this. The Euler product

\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s})

has minimum degree term
p^{-s}
(so
a=1
) with leading coefficient a constant
b=2
. This corresponds to the pole at
s=1
of order
2
inherited from the
\zeta(s)^{2}
factor.

We are being purposefully vague with the term “reasonable” in this heuristic. This is basically an educated guess, which can fail in the wrong conditions. Certainly the average value
b
needs to exist, and moreover each Euler factor needs to converge.

The results we summarize and prove in this paper will imply the following useful result:

Corollary 3.2
.

Heuristic
3.1
is true if both of the following hold:

(1)

The Euler factors
1+b_{p}z^{a}+\cdots
converge absolutely for all
|z|<1
and are nonzero at
z=p^{-1/a}
, and

(2)

The function
p\mapsto b_{p}
is Frobenian, that is it factors through the Artin map of a finite extension
K/\mathbb{Q}
for all but finitely many primes.

We will also see several examples beyond this corollary where the Factorization Method works.

Remark 3.3
.

There are cases where the Factorization Method produces the rightmost singularity, but for which Heuristic
3.1
is false. One source of such an issue is suggested by Corollary
3.2
(a) - any Euler factor which vanishes at
s=1/a
will change the order of the singularity.

4.
Absolute Convergence of Euler Products

The Factorization Method shines by expanding the region of absolute convergence after factoring out copies of the Riemann zeta function. In Example
1.1
, the Dirichlet series

\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s})

converges absolutely on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1
, while

\zeta(s)^{-2}\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s})=\prod_{p}(1-3p^{-2s}+2p^{-3s})

converges absolutely on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/2
.

Absolute convergence of an Euler product is readily checked by taking the logarithm, and checking absolute convergence of the resulting sum. However, rather than composing with a log function each time, we can use the following theorem as a shortcut:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{Z}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
be a multiplicative function. Then, for each
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the generating Dirichlet series
\sum f(n)n^{-s}
converges absolutely if and only if the series on prime powers

\sum_{p\text{ prime}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f(p^{k})p^{-ks}

converges absolutely.

This is a special case of classical results for the absolute convergence of infinite products, some examples of which can be found in
[
Kno90
, Chapter VII]
. Using this theorem, together with a convergence test from a typical Calculus course, we can check the absolute convergence claim in Example
1.1
.

Example 4.2
.

We will prove that

\prod_{p}(1-3p^{-2s}+2p^{-3s})

converges absolutely on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/2
, as claimed in Example
1.1
. By Theorem
4.1
, this is equivalent to the convergence of the series

\sum_{p}\left\lvert\frac{-3}{p^{-2s}}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\frac{2}{p^{-3s}}\right\rvert=\sum_{p}\frac{3}{p^{2{\rm Re}(s)}}+\frac{2}{p^{3{\rm Re}(s)}}.

By the
p
-series convergence test, this series converges if and only if both
2{\rm Re}(s)>1
and
3{\rm Re}(s)>1
, which is equivalent to
{\rm Re}(s)>1/2
.

For nearly every Euler product we consider in this paper, the process of checking absolute convergence will be the same: apply Theorem
4.1
, then compare the sum over prime powers with a
p
-series to determine the region of absolute convergence. Sometimes other convergence tests, like the ratio test, will be useful as well.

In practice, Theorem
4.1
and a Calculus convergence test is typically sufficient for the cases of interest in arithmetic statistics. For this reason, we will leave the process of checking absolute convergence in subsequent examples as exercises for the reader.

We include a proof of Theorem
4.1
here for the sake of completeness, based on pre-existing proofs such as those found in
[
Kno90
]
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Given that
f
is multiplicative, the absolute Dirichlet series formally factors as an Euler product

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|f(n)n^{-s}|=\prod_{p}\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|f(p^{k})p^{-s}|\right).

Suppose first that the Dirichlet series
\sum f(n)n^{-s}
converges absolutely. Then certainly the sum

\sum_{p\text{ prime}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|f(p^{k})p^{-ks}|\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|f(n)n^{-s}|

converges absolutely by the Comparison test, as it is a sum over a subset of terms in the original series.

Conversely, suppose the sum over prime powers converges absolutely. The partial sums of
\sum|f(n)n^{-s}|
necessarily form an increasing sequence. It suffices to show that this sequence is also bounded, as any bounded and monotonic sequence is convergent. The partial sums are bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}|f(n)n^{-s}|

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{p\leq N}\left(1+\sum_{k\leq\log_{2}(N)}|f(p^{k})p^{-s}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{p\leq N}\exp\left(\sum_{k\leq\log_{2}(N)}|f(p^{k})p^{-s}|\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{p\leq N}\sum_{k\leq\log_{2}(N)}|f(p^{k})p^{-s}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\sum_{p}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|f(p^{k})p^{-s}|\right).

Thus, absolute convergence of the sum over prime powers shows the partial sums of
\sum|f(n)n^{-s}|
form a bounded sequence. This concludes the proof.
∎

5.
Examples with Constant Coefficients

Following Example
2.1
, whenever the coefficients of the Euler factors do not depend on
p
we can perform the Factorization Method without modifying the argument. Often this will verify Heuristic
3.1
, however zeros and singularities of individual Euler products can cause the heuristic to fail.

Given a power series
Q(z)=1+bz^{a}+\cdots\in 1+z\mathbb{C}[[z]]
with constant coefficients, consider the corresponding Euler product

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\prod_{p}Q(p^{-s}).

We take
bp^{-as}
to be the minimum degree term, matching the usual notions of “degree” and “term” from the language of power series. As described in Example
2.1
, we can make a strategic choice for Euler factor by the dictionary

bp^{-as}\longmapsto(1-p^{-as})^{b}.

Wood proves that the Factorization method with this choice of strategic Euler factor will always work when the coefficients do not depend on
p
in
[
Woo09
, Lemma 2.12]
, although these cases were a standard result at the time. Estermann proved the first result of this kind when
h(z)
is a polynomial
[
Est28
]
, while Dahlquist dealt with the general cases that
h(z)
is holomorphic
[
Dah52
]
. When one takes care to keep track of the orders of the singularities in Estermann and Dahlquist’s proofs, their methods reproduce the rightmost singularity achieved by Wood. We discuss the statements and give self-contained proofs of Estermann and Dahlquist’s results in Part
II
.

Remark 5.1
.

Notice that we specifically claimed that Wood’s, Estermann’s, and Dahlquist’s results imply that the Factorization Method always works on Euler products with constant coefficients. We
did not
claim that Heuristic
3.1
is always true in these cases. Frei–Loughran–Newton remark on the possibility that Heuristic
3.1
can fail in
[
FLN19
, Remark 2.4]
. We will see an example of this heuristic failing in Example
5.5
.

Let’s apply the Factorization Method to a new example, slightly more complicated than Example
2.1
.

Example 5.2
.

Consider the Euler product

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\prod_{p}(1+(1+i)p^{-2s}-p^{-3s}).

We now perform the four steps of the Factorization Method.

(1)

The lowest degree term is given by
(1+i)p^{-2s}
so that
a=2
and
b=1+i
.

Using Heuristic
3.1
, we can now predict that the rightmost singularity is at
s=1/2
of order
1+i
. This singularity would have non-integral order! This means that we predict that
(s-1/2)^{1+i}\mathcal{Q}(s)
is holomorphic on a neighborhood of
{\rm Re}(s)\geq 1/2
. In particular, we expect that we can only achieve meromorphic continuation of
\mathcal{Q}(s)
on a branch cut. However, as we shall see, the rest of the argument is unaffected.

(2)

We multiply the top and bottom by our strategic factor
(1-p^{-as})^{b}=(1-p^{-2s})^{1+i}
.

(3)

Pulling the denominators out front, we decompose the Euler product as

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\zeta(2s)^{1+i}\prod_{p}(1+(1+i)p^{-2s}-p^{-3s})(1-p^{-2s})^{1+i}.

(4)

In order to distribute the Euler factors, we must expand
(1-p^{-2s})^{1+i}
according to the binomial Taylor series. For the purposes of this example, we only need to first few terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(s)

\displaystyle=\zeta(2s)^{1+i}\prod_{p}(1+(1+i)p^{-2s}-p^{-3s})\left(1-(1+i)p^{-2s}+O(p^{-4s})\right)

\displaystyle=\zeta(2s)^{1+i}\prod_{p}\left(1-p^{-3s}+O(p^{-4s})\right).

Among
{\rm Re}(s)>0
, the implied constant in the big-oh can be bounded independent of
p
using the remainder theorem for Taylor series. We leave it as an exercise to the reader to check that this Euler product converges absolutely on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/3
.

Thus,
\mathcal{Q}(s)
inherits the singularity of
\zeta(2s)^{1+i}
at
s=1/2
of order
1+i
, verifying Heuristic
3.1
for this Euler product.

Remark 5.3
.

We need to be careful with functions like
(s-1/2)^{1+i}
or
\zeta(2s)^{1+i}
. Complex exponential functions, like the square root function, are technically multi-valued complex functions. This is why branch cuts are required. Throughout this paper, we take the convention

z^{\alpha}=e^{\alpha\log(z)},

where
\log(z)
is some branch cut of the log function. You may think of this as the principal branch cut if you like, cutting the complex plane along the negative real axis.

In practice, it is convenient in arithmetic statistics to only work with branch cuts that cut a line to the left of a singularity. This is because of how the Selberg–Delange method works by moving contour integrals from right to left. This is always possible, as the only thing a branch cut needs to do is prevent one from drawing a loop around the singularity.

We can widen the families of Euler products we consider by allowing finitely many primes to “break the pattern” established by the other Euler factors. These exceptional factors are merely pushed to the side, to be consider at the end. This happens fairly often in arithmetic statistics, where certain wild ramification behavior can induce different Euler factors.

Example 5.4
.

The generating series of absolute quadratic discriminants is given by

(1+4^{-s}+2\cdot 8^{-s})\prod_{p\neq 2}(1+p^{-s}).

The way that
2
divides a quadratic discriminant is more varied than for odd primes, which comes from the wild ramification type of
2
in quadratic extensions.

We can shove the factor at
2
to the side, and complete to remaining product with a better factor obeying the established pattern:

\frac{1+4^{-s}+2\cdot 8^{-s}}{1+2^{-s}}\prod_{p}(1+p^{-s})=\frac{1+4^{-s}+2\cdot 8^{-s}}{1+2^{-s}}\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}.

Thus, the rightmost pole is at
s=1
of order
1
, agreeing with the average value of

b_{p}=\begin{cases}0&p=2\\
1&p\neq 2.\end{cases}

It is often the case that number theoretic facts hold for “all but finitely many primes”. This example shows that finitely many primes are no obstacle for meromorphic continuation.

We caution the reader that bad primes may not be obvious at first glance, and that they can break Heuristic
3.1
as we see in the next example.

Example 5.5
.

Consider the Euler product

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(s)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\frac{1}{1-2p^{-s}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s}+4p^{-2s}+\cdots).

It does not look like we have any bad primes, as all the Euler factors look the same. Let’s try out the Factorization Method.

(1)

The lowest degree term is
2p^{-s}
of degree
a=1
with constant coefficient
b=2
.

Thus, Heuristic
3.1
predicts the rightmost pole is at
s=1
of order
2
. We proceed with the next steps of the method.

(2)

We multiply the top and bottom by the strategic Euler factor
(1-p^{-s})^{2}
to produce

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(s)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\frac{1}{(1-p^{-s})^{2}}\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s}+4p^{-2s}+\cdots)(1-p^{-s})^{2}.

(3)

We distribute the Euler factors

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(s)

\displaystyle=\zeta(s)^{2}\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s}+4p^{-2s}+\cdots)(1-2p^{-s}+p^{-2s})

\displaystyle=\zeta(s)^{2}\prod_{p}(1+p^{-2s}+2p^{-3s}+4p^{-4s}+\cdots),

It is tempting to skip part of step (4) in the Factorization method and say it is left as an exercise to the reader

Except this is not true! The remaining Euler factors are geometric series

7.
Further Applications of the Factorization Method

In this section we consider three other variations of Euler products that the reader may encounter. The Factorization Method can be applied to all of these types of Euler products, and many more beyond what we consider in this paper.

7.1.
Euler Products over Number Fields

Euler products can also be considered over primes of fields other than
\mathbb{Q}
. In this case, the Riemann zeta function can be replaced with the Dedekind zeta function, Dirichlet
L
-functions with Hecke
L
-functions, and so on.

In the case of Frobenian Euler products, this will always work out because of the following:

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a finite extension. If
\mathfrak{p}\mapsto H_{\mathfrak{p}}(z)
is Frobenian over
K
, then

p\mapsto h_{p}(z):=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p}H_{\mathfrak{p}}(z^{f_{\mathfrak{p}}})

is Frobenian over
\mathbb{Q}
.

By shifting perspective back down to
\mathbb{Q}
, we can re-use meromorphic continuation results, such as Moroz’s results
[
Mor88
]
, for Euler products over rational primes. From a theoretical perspective, there is no distinction.

From a practical perspective, it is often convenient to take the Euler product over the largest field possible. This is because the Dedekind zeta functions group together a lot of disparate
L
-functions. This can be used to state decompositions more concisely, and also as a shortcut for confirming holomorphicity on larger regions without needing to assume the Artin Conjecture.

Example 7.2
.

Consider the Euler product

\prod_{\mathfrak{p}}(1+2|\mathfrak{p}|^{-s})

over the finite places of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})/\mathbb{Q}
. Here,
|\mathfrak{p}|
denotes the norm of
\mathfrak{p}
down to
\mathbb{Q}
.

On the one hand, this decomposes into a Frobenian Euler product over
\mathbb{Q}

\prod_{p\equiv 0,1\mod 3}(1+2p^{-s})^{2}\prod_{p\equiv 2\mod 3}(1+2p^{-2s}).

The reader can confirm that the rightmost pole is at
s=1
of order
2
, verifying Heuristic
3.1
, by using the strategic Euler factors from Dirichlet
L
-functions as in Example
6.2
.

On the other hand, we can also factor the Euler product by treating it as an Euler product over primes of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})
with constant coefficients.

(1)

The minimum degree term is then
2|\mathfrak{p}|^{-s}
.

(2)

We multiply top and bottom by the strategic factor
(1-|\mathfrak{p}|^{-s})^{2}
in analogy with Example
2.1
.

(3)

This produces a decomposition

\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})}(s)^{2}\prod_{\mathfrak{p}}(1-3|\mathfrak{p}|^{-2s}+2|\mathfrak{p}|^{-3s}).

(4)

The remaining Euler product converges absolutely on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/2
by an argument analogous to Example
4.2
.

Thus, we see the pole at
s=1
of order
2
was inherited from the Dedekind zeta function
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})}(s)^{2}
.

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

Let
E/K
be the finite extension for which
\mathfrak{p}\mapsto H_{\mathfrak{p}}(z)
factors through the map
\mathfrak{p}\mapsto\left(\frac{E/K}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)
.

We first check that the Euler products are even equal. Let
|\mathfrak{p}|
denote that norm of
\mathfrak{p}
down to
\mathbb{Q}
, so that

\displaystyle\prod_{p}h_{p}(p^{-s})

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p}H_{\mathfrak{p}}(p^{-f_{\mathfrak{p}}s})

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p}H_{\mathfrak{p}}(|\mathfrak{p}|^{-s})

\displaystyle=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}}H_{\mathfrak{p}}(|\mathfrak{p}|^{-s}).

Next, we confirm that
p\mapsto h_{p}(z)
is Frobenian. Indeed, consider that
\left(\frac{E/\mathbb{Q}}{p}\right)
determines the sequence of pairs
f_{\mathfrak{p}},\left(\frac{E/K}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)
. The inertia degree is given by the order of the image of
\left(\frac{E/\mathbb{Q}}{p}\right)
in
\textnormal{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})
. Meanwhile,
\left(\frac{E/K}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=\left(\frac{E/\mathbb{Q}}{p}\right)^{f_{\mathfrak{p}}}
. As these pairs determine
h_{p}(z)
, we have proven it is Frobenian over
E/\mathbb{Q}
.
∎

7.2.
An Example with Growing Coefficients

While our focus for this paper is on Euler products with constant or Frobenian coefficients, the Factorization Method will work for more general Euler products. These, however, need not be “reasonable” in the context of Heuristic
3.1
, and the heuristic will often fail.

Example 7.3
.

Consider the Dirichlet series

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\prod_{p}(1+p^{-s}+(p+1)p^{-2s}).

The lowest degree term appears to be
p^{-s}
, so we can take
a=1
and
b=1
. Thus, Heuristic
3.1
suggests that its rightmost singularity is a simple pole at
s=1
.

However, we can demonstrate that this is not true. Performing steps (2) and (3) of the Factorization Method by multiplying top and bottom by
1-p^{-s}
, we factor this series as

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\zeta(s)\prod_{p}(1+p^{1-2s}-(p+1)p^{-3s}).

However, the remaining Euler product still diverges at
s=1
. We repeat this process for another strategic Euler factor
1-p^{1-2s}
, which implies

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\zeta(s)\zeta(2s-1)\prod_{p}(1-(p+1)p^{-3s}-p^{2-4s}-(p+1)p^{1-5s}).

The right hand Euler product converges absolutely for
{\rm Re}(s)>3/4
. Meanwhile,
both

\zeta(s)
and
\zeta(2s-1)
have a simple pole at
s=1
, implying that
\mathcal{Q}(s)
has a pole of order
2
at
s=1
.

It is certainly possible to generalize Heuristic
3.1
to include Euler products whose coefficients are polynomial in
p
, like Example
7.3
, where we might require that both
p^{-s}
and
p^{1-2s}
be considered “minimum degree terms”. We would need to give a more general description of what we mean by a “term”, how to define the degree of a term, and how to choose a corresponding strategic Euler factor. However, such Euler products can be much more complicated. See Section
12
for some references and a brief discussion on the natural boundary of such Euler products, which is still not known in every case.

7.3.
Additive Factorizations

While the Factorization Method has primarily been described with multiplicative language, a lot of structure can be seen by first taken the logarithm and then decomposing the resulting infinite sums.

Example 7.4
.

Consider the Euler product

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\prod_{p}\exp(b_{p}p^{-s})=\prod_{p}\left(1+b_{p}p^{-s}+\frac{b_{p}}{2}p^{-2s}+\cdots\right).

We claim that if the average value of
b_{p}
exists
with a power savings
, then Heuristic
3.1
is true for this Euler product. By a power savings, we mean that

\sum_{p\leq x}b_{p}=b\pi(x)+O(x^{1-\delta})

for some
\delta>0
.

Rather than multiplying top and bottom by some strategic factor, we take the log

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{Q}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}b_{p}p^{-s},

then add and subtract a strategic factor:

\displaystyle\sum_{p}bp^{-s}+\sum_{p}(b_{p}-b)p^{-s}

The first piece will contribute the singularity as in step (3). Consider that

\exp\left(\sum_{p}bp^{-s}\right)=\prod_{p}\exp(bp^{-s})=\prod_{p}\left(1+bp^{-s}+\frac{b^{2}}{2}p^{-2s}+\cdots\right).

Applying the Factorization Method by multiplying top and bottom by
(1-p^{-s})^{b}
, we can factor this series as
\zeta(s)^{b}
times an Euler product absolutely convergent on
{\rm Re}(s)>1/2
. We leave the details as an exercise to the reader, as they are similar to the previously considered examples with constant coefficients. Thus, this term contributes a singularity at
s=1
of order
b
.

The second piece can be bounded as in step (4) using the power savings. We rewrite the series as an integral using a trick classically applied to the Riemann zeta function (called a Mellin transform):

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{p}(b_{p}-b)p^{-s}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{p}(b_{p}-b)(-s)\int_{p}^{\infty}x^{-s-1}\ dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert s\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{p\leq x}(b_{p}-b)\right)x^{-s-1}\ dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq|s|\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{1-\delta}x^{-{\rm Re}(s)-1}\ dx.

This integral converges if and only if
1-\delta-{\rm Re}(s)-1<-1
, or equivalently
{\rm Re}(s)>1-\delta
. By the comparison theorem, the second piece converges in this region. Consequently, the series

\exp\left(\sum_{p}(b_{p}-b)p^{-s}\right)=\prod_{p}\exp((b_{p}-b)p^{-s})

is holomorphic
and nonzero
on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1-\delta
, as the exponential function has no zeros.

Thus,
\mathcal{Q}(s)
has an analytic continuation to a branch cut of an open neighborhood of
{\rm Re}(s)\geq 1
, with a singularity at
s=1
of order
b
.

The use of Mellin transforms is an important trick in arithmetic statistics, and plays a major role in the Selberg–Delange method. The reader may find the general formula useful:

n^{-s}=-s\int_{n}^{\infty}x^{-s-1}\ dx,

where the integral converges absolutely on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>0
.

7.4.
Further References

It is not possible to adequately treat every useful case of the factorization method. Generally speaking, anytime you have a meromorphic function with an Euler product, those Euler factors can be used as “strategic factors” in step (2) of the Factorization Method.

We acknowledge a couple more scenarios below where this happens.

•

Any reasonable
L
-function in the sense of
[
IK04
, Section 5.1]
has a meromorphic continuation and Euler product, and so can be used to give strategic Euler factors in step (2). These include many arithmetically
L
-functions discussed above such as Dirichlet
L
-functions, Hecke
L
-functions, and (conjecturally) Artin
L
-functions, but also include automorphic
L
-functions and the
L
-functions attached to varieties (conjecturally automorphic).

•

Any multiple Dirichlet series, that is a Dirichlet series of more than one complex variable, which has an Euler product can also be used to give strategic factors in step (2). Generally we would want to consider different variables as having equal weight in step (1), so an Euler product
\prod_{p}(1+p^{-s}+p^{-w})
has minimum degree terms
p^{-s}
and
p^{-w}
. For some examples, see
[
BEL07
,
Del13
,
Del14
]
.

8.
Iterating the Factorization Method

The rightmost singularity predicted by Heuristic
3.1
is not the end of the Factorization Method’s usefulness. It may have occurred to the reader that the remaining Euler product could be analytically continued
again
by repeating the Factorization Method.

Example 8.1
.

Let’s revist the Euler product

\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s})=\zeta(s)^{2}\prod_{p}(1-3p^{-2s}+2p^{-3s})

from Example
1.1
. We previously showed that this function has a meromorphic continuation to the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/2
with a pole at
s=1
of order
2
.

We can beat that result by applying the Factorization Method to the remaining Euler product. By multiplying top and bottom by
(1-p^{-2s})^{-3}
, we compute

\displaystyle\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s})

\displaystyle=\zeta(s)^{2}\zeta(2s)^{-3}\prod_{p}\left(1+O(p^{-3s})\right).

Theorem
4.1
implies that the remaining Euler product converges absolutely on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/3
.

We have now shown that
\mathcal{Q}(s)
has a meromorphic continuation to the larger region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/3
with a pole of order
2
at
s=1
, and poles at any of the nontrivial zeroes of
\zeta(2s)
that may land in this region. The Riemann Hypothesis predicts that the nontrivial zeroes of
\zeta(2s)
all have real part
1/4<1/3
, but the Riemann Hypothesis is still unproven at this time.

Seeing this example, you might be thinking “What if I did the Factorization Method again? and
again
? and
again
? Can we do better?” Congratulations, you just described the method used by Estermann
[
Est28
]
, Dahlquist
[
Dah52
]
, and others
[
Kur78
,
Kur86a
,
Kur86b
,
Mor88
]
to meromorphically continue Euler products to their natural boundary!

Indeed, it turns out that the Factorization Method can be applied countably many times to produce a
zeta product
. For the Euler product in Example
1.1
, this looks like

\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s})=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\zeta(ns)^{b_{n}}

for some sequence of numbers
b_{n}
. The first example of such a zeta product appears in
[
LW20
]
for the Dirichlet series
\exp(\sum p^{-s})
. What is remarkable is that the tail of the zeta product

\prod_{n\geq N}\zeta(ns)^{b_{n}}

converges absolutely on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/N
. Thus, taking a limit as the tail gets smaller, the zeta product produces a continuation to the region
{\rm Re}(s)>0
away from a set of isolated singularities which occur either at
s=1/n
or
s=
a zero of
\zeta(ns)
. These singularities are poles precisely when the corresponding number
b_{n}
is a integer.

Part
II
of this paper concerns the existence of this type of zeta product, using it to produce a meromorphic continuation. Part
III
of this paper concerns the sequence
b_{n}
of exponents, where we give explicit results so that the orders of any singularities or poles can be easily computed.

Part II
Existence of Analytic Continuations

9.
History of Analytic Continuations using the Factorization Method

9.1.
Euler Products with Constant Coefficients

The first general result applying the Factorization Method is due to Estermann
[
Est28
]
.

Theorem 9.1

(Estemann
[
Est28
]
)
.

Let
h(z)\in 1+z\mathbb{Z}[z]
be a polynomial with integer coefficients. Then the Euler product

\prod_{p}h(p^{-s})^{-1}

has a meromorphic continuation to the right half-plane
{\rm Re}(s)>0
.

If the roots of
h(z)
all have magnitude
1
then this Euler product can be meromorphically continued to
\mathbb{C}
, otherwise
{\rm Re}(s)=0
is the natural boundary of this function.

The
natural boundary
is the boundary of the largest open set on which the function can be meromorphically continued. Estermann’s proof of analytic continuation can be understood as iterating the Factorization Method infinitely many times to produce a zeta product

\prod_{p}h(p^{-s})^{-1}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\zeta(ns)^{b_{n}}

for some sequence
(b_{n})
. All the roots of
h(z)
having magnitude
1
is equivalent to the sequence
(b_{n})
having at most finitely many nonzero terms, making this function a finite product of zeta functions and therefore meromorphic on
\mathbb{C}
. Otherwise, the sequence
(b_{n})
has infinitely many nonzero terms. In this case, Estermann then proves that every point on the
{\rm Re}(s)=0
is the limit point of zeros or poles of
\zeta(ns)^{b_{n}}
as
n
varies from
1
to
\infty
, making every point on this line an essential singularity.

Dahlquist
[
Dah52
]
then expanded on Estermann’s work to include non-polynomial Euler factors. The proof outline is essentially the same, where Dalhquist needs to separate out Euler factors that contribute singularities or zeros.

Theorem 9.2

(Dahlquist
[
Dah52
]
)
.

Let
Q(z)
be holomorphic on (a branch cut of) the complex plane, save for a set of isolated singularities, holomorphic at
z=0
with
Q(0)=1
, and such that the closed unit ball does not contain a limit point of zeros of singularities of
Q(z)
. Then the Euler product

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\prod_{p}Q(p^{-s})

has an analytic continuation to (a branch cut of) the right halfplane
{\rm Re}(s)>0
, save for a set of isolated singularities.

If
Q(z)
is not a polynomial whose roots all have magnitude
1
, then the line
{\rm Re}(s)=0
is the natural boundary for
\mathcal{Q}(s)
(up to branch cuts).

We are careful to avoid the term meromorphic here, as Dahlquist’s work necessarily allows for singularities of non-integral order. Dahlquist does not mention branch cuts in their theorem statements, although we know that any singularity of non-integral order requires a branch cut that prevents one from drawing a loop around the singularity. This is distinct from Estermann’s original work, in which their Euler product is truly meromorphic on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>0
.

We give self-contained proofs of the analytic continuations in Estermann’s Theorem
9.1
and Dahlquist’s Theorem
9.2
. The existence of an analytic continuation to a branch cut of
{\rm Re}(s)>0
is proven in Theorem
10.1
. In the context of Estermann’s theorem, it is a consequence of Theorem
13.1
that all of the singularities are poles so that no branch cut is necessary. We summarize the main idea behind the proof that
{\rm Re}(s)=0
is the natural boundary in Section
12
, though we leave many of the details to the original papers.

9.2.
Frobenian Euler Products

Results of similar strength are true for Frobenian Euler products. Kurokawa
[
Kur78
,
Kur86a
,
Kur86b
]
and Moroz
[
Mor88
]
prove an analogous statement to Estermann’s result, with Moroz’s proof being a streamlined version of Kurokawa’s work in
[
Kur78
]
.

Theorem 9.3

(Kuokawa
[
Kur78
]
, Moroz
[
Mor88
]
)
.

Let
h_{p}(z)\in 1+zR(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))[z]
be a polynomial with coefficients
a_{n}\in R(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))
in the ring of virtual characters, that is the ring of integer linear combinations of irreducible characters of
E/\mathbb{Q}
. Then the Euler product

\prod_{p}h_{p}(p^{-s})^{-1}=\prod_{p}\left(1+\sum a_{n}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}(E/\mathbb{Q}))p^{-ns}\right)^{-1}

has a meromorphic continuation to the right half-plane
{\rm Re}(s)>0
.

If the roots of
h_{p}(z)
is unitary then this Euler product can be meromorphically continued to
\mathbb{C}
, otherwise
{\rm Re}(s)=0
is the natural boundary of this function.

Just like Estermann, Kurokawa and Moroz prove this result by applying the Factorization Method to relate the Euler product to a zeta product

\prod_{n}\prod_{\rho}L(ns,\rho)^{b_{n,\rho}}

for some sequence
(b_{n,\rho})
, up to the ramified places of the Galois representations
\rho
. This product is finite if and only if
h_{p}(z)
is of the form
\det(I-\psi(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z)
for some finite dimensional Galois representation
\psi
and almost all places
p
, which is how Kurakawa and Moroz define
unitary
.

We also give a self-contained proof of the meromorphic continuations in Theorem
9.3
. The existence of an analytic continuation to a branch cut of
{\rm Re}(s)>0
is proven in Theorem
10.2
, and it is a consequence of Theorem
13.4
that all of the singularities are poles so that no branch cut is necessary. We again summarize the main idea behind the proof that
{\rm Re}(s)=0
is the natural boundary in Section
12
, though we leave many of the details to the original papers.

The author was unable to find a result for general Frobenian Euler products analogous to Dahlquist’s Theorem
9.2
in the literature, although a proof is readily constructed by combining key features of Dahlquist’s and Moroz’s work. The self-contained proof of Theorem
10.2
we present readily implies the following result:

Theorem 9.4
.

Let
p\mapsto Q_{p}(z)
be a Frobenian map over a finite extension
E/\mathbb{Q}
, where each function
Q_{p}(z)
is holomorphic on (a branch cut of) the open unit disk, save for a set of isolated singularities, and is holomorphic at
z=0
with
Q(0)=1
. Then the Euler product

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\prod_{p}Q_{p}(p^{-s})

has an analytic continuation to (a branch cut of) the right halfplane
{\rm Re}(s)>0
, save for a set of isolated singularities.

Just like with Dahlquist’s results, we remark that it is necessary to allow for singularities of non-integral order in Theorem
9.4
. In particular, it is important to avoid using the word “meromorphic” for this result.

Remark 9.5
.

It is reasonable to to expect that, unless
Q_{p}(z)
is a unitary polynomial,
{\rm Re}(s)=0
is the natural boundary for
\mathcal{Q}(s)
in Theorem
9.4
(up to branch cuts). The main ideas described in Section
12
apply equally well to this case, but the details of the argument are outside the scope of this paper.

10.
Constructing Analytic Continuations

The analytic continuation to (a branch cut of) the right-half plane
{\rm Re}(s)>0
is done up to
{\rm Re}(s)>\epsilon
for arbitrarily small
\epsilon
. This is so that
\zeta(ns)
is absolutely convergent on this region for all but finitely many values of
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, or in other words only finitely many of these functions can contribute a pole.

Theorem 10.1
.

Let
Q(z)
be holomorphic on (a branch cut of) the open unit disk, save for a set of isolated singularities, which is holomorphic at
z=0
with
Q(0)=1
. Let
r
be the smallest magnitude of a singularity of
Q(z)
and let
\mathcal{Q}(s)=\prod Q(p^{-s})
be the corresponding Euler product.

(1)

There exists a sequence of complex numbers
b_{n}(Q)=b_{n}
such that
\log Q(z)=-\sum b_{n}\log(1-z^{n})
as formal power series centered at
z=0
, and

(2)

For each
\epsilon>0
,

\mathcal{Q}(s)\prod_{p\leq r^{-1/\epsilon}}Q(p^{-s})^{-1}\prod_{n\leq 1/\epsilon}\zeta(ns)^{-b_{n}}

is an absolutely convergent Dirichlet series (and so holomorphic) on the right-half plane
{\rm Re}(s)>\epsilon
.

The existence of an analytic continuation to
{\rm Re}(s)>0
(up to a branch cut) in Estermann’s Theorem
9.1
and Dahlquist’s Theorem
9.2
follows immediately from this result by taking
\epsilon\to 0
. Moreover, we see that a branch cut is needed if and only if one of the
Q(p^{-s})
requires a branch cut or one of the
b_{n}
is not an integer. While Estermann and Dahlquist did describe the sequence
b_{n}
as we do in Theorem
10.1
(i), this sequence is evident from their proofs.

We are now able to see that the Factorization Method is, secretly, linear algebra. The steps of the method are calculating the first nonzero coefficient in the expansion of
\log Q(z)
with respect to the basis
\{-\log(1-z^{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
. Step (1) and (2) are identifying the smallest basis vector that has a nonzero coefficient, step (3) is factoring the Euler product as

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\zeta(as)^{b_{a}}\left(\mathcal{Q}(s)\zeta(as)^{-b_{a}}\right),

and step (4) is proving a special case of Theorem
10.1
, that
\mathcal{Q}(s)\zeta(as)^{-b_{a}}
converges absolutely on a larger region. Iterating the Factorization Method finds each successive coefficient. By doing the basis decomposition all at once at the start, Theorem
10.1
gives the analytic continuation that results from applying the Factorization Method “infinitely many times”.

We will discuss some of the intricacies of the infinite dimensional linear algebra we need in Section
11
, but modulo these details we are ready to prove this result.

Proof.

Q(z)
is equal to a power series in some neighborhood of
z=0
with constant term
1
, so that we can view
\log Q(z)\in z\mathbb{C}[[z]]
as a formal power series. Part (i) follows from Proposition
11.1
, which shows that
\{-\log(1-z^{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
is a Schauder basis of
z\mathbb{C}[[z]]
(i.e. a basis allowing for infinite linear combinations). This shows the existence of the sequence
b_{n}(Q)=b_{n}
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. We then expand

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(s)\prod_{p\leq r^{-1/\epsilon}}Q(p^{-s})^{-1}\prod_{n\leq 1/\epsilon}\zeta(ns)^{-b_{n}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\leq r^{-1/\epsilon}}\left(\prod_{n\leq 1/\epsilon}(1-p^{-ns})^{b_{n}}\right)\prod_{p>r^{-1/\epsilon}}\left(Q(p^{-s})\prod_{n\leq 1/\epsilon}(1-p^{-ns})^{b_{n}}\right).

The product over
p\leq r^{-1/\epsilon}
is finite, and so necessarily absolutely convergent. For
{\rm Re}(s)>\epsilon
and
p>r^{-1/\epsilon}
, we have
|p^{-s}|<r
.
Q(z)
is equal to its Taylor series on the ball
|z|<r
by
Q(z)
not having any singularities in this region, and thus so is
\log Q(z)
. We can then write

\displaystyle Q(p^{-s})\prod_{n\leq 1/\epsilon}(1-p^{-ns})^{b_{n}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\log Q(p^{-s})+\sum_{n\leq 1/\epsilon}b_{n}\log(1-(p^{-s})^{n})\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\sum_{n>1/\epsilon}b_{n}\log(1-(p^{-s})^{n})\right).

The series inside the exponential converges absolutely for
|p^{-s}|<r
because it is a sum of two series that converge absolutely on this region. By the remainder theorem from calculus, it follows that

-\sum_{n>1/\epsilon}b_{n}\log(1-z^{n})=O(z^{1/\epsilon})

on the region
|z|<r
, which implies

\displaystyle Q(p^{-s})\prod_{n\leq 1/\epsilon}(1-p^{-ns})^{b_{n}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(O(p^{-s/\epsilon})\right)=1+O(p^{-s/\epsilon})

on the region
|p^{-s}|<r
. As discussed above, this includes the region
{\rm Re}(s)>\epsilon
for each
p>r^{-1/\epsilon}
. Thus

\displaystyle\prod_{p>r^{-1/\epsilon}}\left(Q(p^{-s})\prod_{n\leq 1/\epsilon}(1-p^{-ns})^{b_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p>r^{-1/\epsilon}}\left(1+O(p^{-s/\epsilon})\right),

which converges absolutely as
{\rm Re}(s)>\epsilon
implies
{\rm Re}(s)/\epsilon>1
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We give an analogous result for Frobenian Euler products.

Theorem 10.2
.

Let
p\mapsto Q_{p}(z)
be Frobeian with respect to the finite extension

(1)

There exists a sequence of complex numbers

and irreducible characters

(2)

For each
\epsilon>0
,

Theorem
10.2
implies the existence of an analytic continuation to (a branch cut of)

in the statements of Kurokawa’s and Moroz’s Theorem
9.3
as well as in the original Theorem

generalizing Dahlquist’s work. Just like in the constant coefficients case, we see that a branch cut is needed if and only if one of the

The proof of Theorem
10.2
is completely analogous to that of Theorem
10.1
. We include it for the sake of completeness.

Proof.

For each prime
Q_{p}(z)
is equal to a power series in some neighborhood of
z=0
with constant term
1
, so that for all but finitely many primes we can view
\log Q_{p}(z)\in zR(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))[[z]]

is the ring of virtual characters
\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})\to\mathbb{C}

\{-\log\det(I-\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z^{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
is a Schauder basis of
zR(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))[[z]]
(i.e. a basis allowing for infinite linear combinations). This shows the existence of the sequence
b_{n,\rho}(Q)=b_{n,\rho}
.

Fix
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(s)\prod_{p\leq r^{-1/\epsilon}}Q_{p}(p^{-s})^{-1}\prod_{n\leq 1/\epsilon}\prod_{\rho}L(ns,\rho)^{-b_{n}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\leq r^{-1/\epsilon}}\left(\prod_{n\leq 1/\epsilon}\prod_{\rho}\det(I-\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})p^{-ns})^{b_{n,\rho}}\right)\prod_{p>r^{-1/\epsilon}}\left(Q(p^{-s})\prod_{n\leq 1/\epsilon}\prod_{\rho}\det(I-\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})p^{-ns})^{b_{n}}\right).

{\rm Re}(s)>\epsilon

\displaystyle Q_{p}(p^{-s})\prod_{n\leq 1/\epsilon}\prod_{\rho}\det(I-\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})p^{-ns})^{b_{n}}

11.
Some Infinite Dimensional Linear Algebra

The Factorization Method is, secretly, a basis decomposition. In the constant coefficient case, the expression
\log Q(z)=-\sum b_{n}\log(1-z^{n})
is an expression of
\log Q(z)
as an element of the span of
\{-\log(1-z^{n})\}
. However, it is important to recognize that this is the span when allowing convergent infinite linear combinations.

Let
V
be a normed vector space. A
Schauder basis
for
V
is a set of vectors
\{v_{n}\}
such that every vector in
V
can be written as a convergent linear combination of the
\{v_{n}\}
, and the only linear combination of
\{v_{n}\}
that converges to
0
is the trivial one.

Given a power series
f(z)
, define
{\rm ord}(f)
to be the smallest degree term with a nonzero coefficient. This makes
z\mathbb{C}[[z]]
into a normed vector space with norm given by

||f(z)||=\frac{1}{{\rm ord}(f)}.

Consider the sequence of monomials
\{z^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
in
z\mathbb{C}[[z]]
. By definition of the ring of power series, these vectors form a Schauder basis for
z\mathbb{C}[[z]]
.

Proposition 11.1
.

The sequence
\{-\log(1-z^{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
is a Schauder basis for
z\mathbb{C}[[z]]
.

Thus, any power series
\log Q(z)\in z\mathbb{C}[[z]]
can be written as a convergent linear combination of
-\log(1-z^{n})
. The coefficients of this linear combination are precisely the sequence
b_{n}
. We can prove something similar for Frobenian power series.

Proposition 11.2
.

Let
E/\mathbb{Q}
be a finite extension and
R(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))
the ring of complex valued virtual characters. The sequence
\{-\log\det(I-\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})z^{n})\}_{n\geq 1,\rho}
is a Schauder basis for
zR(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))[[z]]
, where
\rho:\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})\to{\rm GL}(V)
varies over the irreducible representations of
\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})
.

These propositions are proven by showing that the linear transformation from the standard basis to these vectors is lower triangular in a certain infinite dimensional sense with nonzero diagonal. Just like their finite dimensional counterparts, these infinite dimensional lower triangular matrices are invertible.

Lemma 11.3
.

Let
W
be a vector space with a Schauder basis
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
indexed by a partially ordered set
I
. Suppose further that
I
satisfies a Northcott property, namely that for each
i\in I
the set
\{j\in I:j<i\}
is finite. We call a matrix
T=[t_{i,j}]

lower triangular
if
t_{i,j}\neq 0
implies
i\geq j
.

Every lower triangular matrix whose diagonal entries are nonzero is invertible.

Proof.

Consider the equation
T\vec{v}=\vec{c}
. We prove that this equation has a unique solution by strong induction on the index set
I
. We note that the Northcott property implies a version of being well-ordered: If
S\subset I
is nonempty, then there exists some
i\in S
. The set

S\cap\{j\leq i:j\in I\}

is finite and is closed under
<
as a subset of
S
, and therefore must contain a minimal element of
S
.

We construct the coordinates of the vector
\vec{v}
by induction. For the base case, let
i_{0}\in I
be any minimal element (note that this may not be unique). The
i_{0}
th row of
T
satisfies

t_{i_{0},j}=\begin{cases}\text{nonzero}&j=i_{0}\\
0&j\neq i_{0},\end{cases}

because
i_{0}
is the only index
\leq i_{0}
. Therefore, the product along the
i_{0}
th row is given by

c_{i_{0}}=\sum_{j}t_{i_{0},j}v_{j}=t_{i_{0},i_{0}}v_{i_{0}}.

Thus
v_{i_{0}}=t_{i_{0},i_{0}}^{-1}c_{i_{0}}
exists and is unique. This is true regardless of which minimal element we choose, so the base case may be considered to be “all minimal elements of
I
”.

We proceed via strong induction. Choose some
i\in I
. Suppose that for each
j<i
, there is a unique solution for
v_{j}
. Then multiplying through the
i
th row gives

c_{i}=\sum_{j}t_{i,j}v_{j}=\sum_{j\leq i}t_{i,j}v_{j}

by
T
lower triangular. We note that, by the definition of the ordering on
I
, this sum is finite so we do not need to worry about convergence issues. Solving for
v_{i}
we find that

v_{i}=t_{i,i}^{-1}\left(c_{i}-\sum_{j<i}t_{i,j}v_{j}\right),

which exits and is unique by the inductive hypothesis.

The result then follows from strong induction on a well-ordered set and gives an iterative formula for each
v_{i}
value.
∎

We can now prove the propositions.

Proof of Proposition
11.1
.

Let
T:z\mathbb{C}[[z]]\to z\mathbb{C}[[z]]
be the linear transformation sending
Tz^{n}=-\log(1-z^{n})
. If we write
T=[t_{i,j}]
as a matrix with respect to the standard basis
\{z^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}t_{j,n}z^{n}=Tz^{n}=-\log(1-z^{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}z^{kn}.

Thus,
t_{j,n}=0
if
j\neq kn
for some integer
k\geq 1
, so in particular if
j<n
. This implies
T
is lower triangular. Moreover,
t_{n,n}=1
is nonzero for each
n\geq 1
, which by Lemma
11.3
implies that
T
is invertible. Thus,
\{-\log(1-z^{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
is the image of a basis under an invertible linear transformation, and so too must be a basis.
∎

Proof of Proposition
11.2
.

The vector space
zR(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))[[z]]
has standard basis given by
\{{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})z^{n}\}_{n\geq 1,\rho}
, where
\rho:\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})\to V
varies over the irreducible representations of
\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})
. This is a basis because the irreducible characters form a basis for
R(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))
.

Let
T:zR(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))[[z]]\to zR(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))[[z]]
be the linear transformation sending
T{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})z^{n}=-\log\det(I-\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})z^{n})
. If we write
T=[t_{(i,\gamma),(j,\psi)}]
as a matrix with respect to the standard basis
\{{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})z^{n}\}_{n\geq 1,\rho}
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}t_{(i,\gamma),(n,\rho)}{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})z^{n}=Tz^{n}=-\log\det(I-\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})z^{n})=1+{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})z^{n}+O(z^{2n}).

Put a partial ordering on the set

\{(i,\gamma):i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},\gamma\text{ irreducible representation of }\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})\}

given by
(i,\gamma)<(j,\psi)
if
i<j
. This set is well-ordered by
E/\mathbb{Q}
finite, and so admitting at most finitely many irreducible representations. Thus,
t_{(i,\gamma),(n,\rho)}=0
if
i\neq kn
for some integer
k\geq 1
, so in particular if
i<n
. By definition, this means that
t_{(i,\gamma),(n,\rho)}=0
if
(i,\gamma)<(n,\rho)
which implies
T
is lower triangular. Moreover,
t_{(n,\rho),(n,\rho)}=1
is nonzero for each
n\geq 1
, which by Lemma
11.3
implies that
T
is invertible. Thus,
\{-\log\det(I-\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})z^{n})\}_{n\geq 1,\rho}
is the image of a basis under an invertible linear transformation, and so too must be a basis.
∎

12.
Determining the Natural Boundary

The primary goal of Estermann’s work, and the work that followed, was the determination of the natural boundary. This is done by proving that every point on the line
{\rm Re}(s)=0
is a limit of zeros or singularities, and therefore are essential singularities.

Study of the natural boundary of Dirichlet series has continued far beyond the cases discussed in this paper. One case of particular interest is that of Euler products of the form

\mathcal{W}(s):=\prod_{p}W(p,p^{-s}),

where
W(X,Y)\in\mathbb{C}(X,Y)
is a rational function. Perhaps surprisingly, the natural boundary of
\mathcal{W}(s)
is still not known for every such rational function
W(X,Y)
. Similar to Example
7.3
,
\mathcal{W}(s)
can be factored as an infinite product of functions of the form
\zeta(ns+m)
for integers
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. The horizontal shift makes it more difficult to exclude the possibility that the zeros of each factor are cancelled out by zeros of other factors. The zeta functions of certain nilpotent groups are naturally given by this type of Euler product, and for this reason the analytic theory of the natural boundary for this type of Euler product is summarized in the book “Zeta Functions of Groups and Rings” by du Sautoy and Woodward
[
DSW08
, Section 5]
.

The conjectural location of the natural boundary for
\mathcal{W}(s)
when
W(X,Y)
is a polynomial can be found in
[
Bho10
, Conjecture 1.4]
. Some references concerning the natural boundaries for these and other Dirichlet series include
[
BSP07
,
EVC20
,
HKP21
]
. Of particular interest is
[
BSP16
]
, where it is shown that the natural boundary need not be of the form
{\rm Re}(s)=\beta
for a real number
\beta
, and
[
BSP10
]
, where it is shown that there must exist Euler products
\mathcal{W}(s)
of this form for which Estermann’s argument cannot work to produce the natural boundary.

The analog of the natural boundary for Dirichlet series of multiple variables has also been considered, in particular for those arising as the height zeta functions of varieties. For some examples, see
[
Del13
,
Del14
,
Ess12
,
BEL07
]
.

The full details of arguments proving the existence of natural boundaries are outside the scope of this paper, as we are primarily concerned with the explicit construction of a continuation. However, we can briefly summarize the main idea in the language of Theorem
10.1
and Theorem
10.2
. We refer the reader to the corresponding papers
[
Est28
,
Dah52
,
Kur78
,
Kur86a
,
Kur86b
,
Mor88
]
for the details.

Sketch of the Argument Determining the Natural Boundary.

Let
Q(z)
be as in Theorem
10.1
(respectively Theorem
10.2
), with corresponding sequence
b_{n}
(resp.
b_{n,\rho}
). Assume further that
Q(z)
is holomorphic on a branch cut of the entire complex plane, rather than just the open unit disk.

If the sequence
b_{n}
(resp.
b_{n,\rho}
) has at most finitely many nonzero terms, then it is clear from the proofs of Theorem
10.1
(resp. Theorem
10.2
) that
\mathcal{Q}(s)
factors as a finite product of complex powers of Artin
L
-functions times a function which is holomorphic on a branch cut of
\mathbb{C}
. Thus,
\mathcal{Q}(s)
itself is holomorphic on a branch cut of the the entire complex plane.

Otherwise, for each
n
such that
b_{n}
(resp.
b_{n,\rho}
) is nonzero the factor
\zeta(ns)^{b_{n}}
(resp.
L(ns,\rho)^{b_{n}}
) contributes a zero or an isolated singularity at each root of
\zeta(ns)
(resp.
L(ns,\rho)
). For each
n
, consider the set

D_{n}(\delta,t_{0})=\left\{s=\sigma+it:\frac{1}{2n+1}\leq\sigma\leq\frac{1}{2n-1},\ t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+\delta\right\}

for each
t_{0}
and
\delta
. It suffices to show that for all
\delta,t_{0}
, there exist infinitely many
n
for which
D_{n}(\delta,t_{0})
contains a zero or singularity of
\mathcal{Q}(s)
. This would imply that
s=0+it_{0}
is a limit point of zeros or singularities of
\mathcal{Q}(s)
, and therefore must be an essential singularity of
\mathcal{Q}(s)
. Doing this for each
t_{0}
implies that
{\rm Re}(s)=0
is a wall of essentialy singularities, past which we cannot continue
\mathcal{Q}(s)
.

It is known that 100% of the zeros of
\zeta(s)
(resp.
L(s,\rho)
) in the critical strip have real part in
[1/2-\epsilon,1/2+\epsilon]
. The set
D_{n}(\delta,t_{0})
intersects the critical line of
\zeta(ms)
(resp.
L(ms,\rho)
) for at most one value of
m
, so that
D_{n}(\delta,t_{0})
is expected to contain asymptotically more zeros of
\zeta(ns)
(resp.
L(ns,\rho)
) than for
\zeta(ms)
(resp.
L(ms,\rho)
) with
m\neq n
. Thus, no matter what the sequence
\{b_{n}\}
is, as long as
b_{n}\neq 0
we expect at least one root of
\zeta(ns)
(resp.
L(ns,\rho)
) to not be cancelled out in the product of
L
-functions giving
\mathcal{Q}(s)
. This corresponds to a zero or singularity of
\mathcal{Q}(s)
in
D_{n}(\delta,t_{0})
.

There is more work needed to make this rigorous, in particular checking that the zeros of
L(s,\rho)
and
L(s,\psi)
do not have significant overlap for different representations
\rho
and
\psi
. The bunching up of zeros near the critical strip of
\zeta(ns)
as
n\to\infty
is the main idea behind these proofs, but the actual proofs involve deep results about the locations of zeros of Artin
L
-functions.
∎

Part III
Explicit Descriptions of Singularities

13.
Statement of Explicit Results

The singularities in Theorem
10.1
are determined by the singularities of
Q(p^{-s})
or of
\zeta(ns)^{b_{n}(Q)}
. The singularities of
Q(p^{-s})
(if there are any) are determined by the choice of function
Q(z)
. Meanwhile, the singularities of
\zeta(ns)^{b_{n}(Q)}
are determined by the pole or zeros of
\zeta(s)
together with the value of
b_{n}(Q)
.

Thus, to understand the singularities of
\mathcal{Q}(s)
, it suffices to understand

•

The zeros and singularities of
Q(z)
,

•

The zeros and singularities of
\zeta(s)
, and

•

the values of the sequence
b_{n}(Q)
.

In the cases of interest, the zeros and singularities of
Q(z)
are often evident from the function being considered. While we do not completely understand the zeros of
\zeta(s)
, in many applications it is sufficient to know that the nontrivial zeros all lie in the critical strip. Problems that require more information about the zeros of
\zeta(s)
may use the zero-free region or prove results conditional on the Riemann Hypothesis.

That leaves the sequence
b_{n}(Q)
. Theorem
10.1
(i) gave this sequence as the coefficients of a basis decomposition. By taking more care to be explicit with the linear algebra, we can prove some equivalent descriptions for this sequence.

Theorem 13.1
.

Let
Q(z)\in 1+z\mathbb{C}[[z]]
be a power series. Let
b_{n}=b_{n}(Q)
be the unique sequence of complex numbers such that

\log Q(z)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}\log(1-z^{n}).

Then the following hold:

(1)

Write
\log Q(z)=\sum q_{n}z^{n}
. Then
b_{1}=q_{1}
, and for
n>1
the sequence is given by the recursive formula

b_{n}=q_{n}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d>1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d}b_{n/d}.

(2)

If
Q(z)\in 1+z\mathbb{Z}[[z]]
, then
b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem
13.1
(i) is useful for performing practical calculations. In applications, it is often only necessary to know finitely many terms of the sequence
b_{n}
, which provides enough information to determine the locations and orders of singularities in some right-half plane
{\rm Re}(s)>\delta>0
. Theorem
13.1
(ii) is useful for a more abstract reason - we know by Theorem
10.1
that if
Q(z)
is meromorphic and
b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
for each
n
, then
\mathcal{Q}(s)
is meromorphic on
{\rm Re}(s)>0
. No branch cuts needed. For example, Theorem
13.1
(ii) implies that the continuation in Estermann’s Theorem
9.1
is a meromorphic continuation.

Remark 13.2
.

Theorem
13.1
(ii) comes from an explicit formula giving the values of
b_{n}
in terms of the coefficients of
\log Q(z)
, as opposed to the recursive formula in Theorem
13.1
(i). It is possible to extract a closed form expression for
b_{n}
from the proof of Theorem
13.1
, but we opt not to include this as the expression is more complicated than it is worth.

We now use Theorem
13.1
to quickly give the locations of several singularities in Example
1.1
.

Example 13.3
.

Consider the Euler product in Example
1.1
,

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\prod_{p}(1+2p^{-s}).

This corresponds to the entire function
Q(z)=1+2z
, which is a polynomial with integer coefficients. Thus Theorem
10.1
implies that
\mathcal{Q}(s)
has a meromorphic continuation to the right halfplane
{\rm Re}(s)>0
.

Let’s explicitly give the meromorphic continuation of
\mathcal{Q}(s)
to the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/6
. Noting that the radius of convergence for
\log Q(z)=\log(1+2z)
is
r=1/2
, Theorem
10.1
implies that

G(s)=\mathcal{Q}(s)\prod_{p\leq 2^{6}}(1+2p^{-s})^{-1}\prod_{n=1}^{6}\zeta(ns)^{-b_{n}}

is absolutely convergent on this region. Thus

\mathcal{Q}(s)=G(s)\prod_{p\leq 2^{6}}(1+2p^{-s})\prod_{n=1}^{6}\zeta(ns)^{b_{n}}

in the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/6
. Given that
Q(z)\neq 0
on the positive real line, it follows that
G(s)\neq 0
and
Q(p^{-s})=1+2p^{-s}\neq 0
for real
s\in(1/6,\infty]
. It now suffices to calculate
b_{1},b_{2},...,b_{6}
to compute the poles.

We compute the Taylor series

\log Q(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}2^{n}}{n}z^{n}=2z-2z^{2}+\frac{8}{3}z^{3}-4z^{4}+\frac{32}{5}z^{5}-\frac{32}{3}z^{6}+\cdots,

so that the recursive formula in Theorem
13.1
(i) implies

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle=2

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle=-2-\frac{1}{2}b_{1}=-3

\displaystyle b_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{3}-\frac{1}{3}b_{1}=2

\displaystyle b_{4}

\displaystyle=-4-\frac{1}{4}b_{1}-\frac{1}{2}b_{2}=-3

\displaystyle b_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{32}{5}-\frac{1}{5}b_{1}=6

\displaystyle b_{6}

\displaystyle=\frac{-32}{3}-\frac{1}{6}b_{1}-\frac{1}{3}b_{2}-\frac{1}{2}b_{3}=-11.

Thus, we have proven that

\mathcal{Q}(s)=\zeta(s)^{2}\zeta(2s)^{-3}\zeta(3s)^{2}\zeta(4s)^{-3}\zeta(5s)^{6}\zeta(6s)^{-11}G(s)\prod_{p\leq 2^{6}}(1+2p^{-s}),

where
G(s)
is holomorphic and nonzero on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>1/6
. We now see that all singularities in this region are inherited from the zeta functions, although some may be (partially) cancelled out by the (non-real) zeros of
1+2p^{-s}
for
p\leq 2^{6}
.

A similar result holds for Frobenian Euler products, which implies that the continuation in Kurakawa’s and Moroz’s Theorem
9.3
is a meromorphic continuation.

Theorem 13.4
.

Let
Q_{p}(z)\in 1+zR(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))[[z]]
be a power series, where
E/\mathbb{Q}
is a finite extension and
R(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))
is the ring of virtual characters
\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})\to\mathbb{C}
. Let
b_{n,\rho}=b_{n,\rho}(Q)
be the unique sequence of complex numbers indexed by positive integers
n
and irreducible representations
\rho:\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})\to{\rm GL}(V)
such that

\log Q_{p}(z)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\rho}b_{n,\rho}\log\det(I-\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})z^{n}).

Then the following hold:

(1)

Write
\log Q_{p}(z)=\sum q_{n,\rho}{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}|V^{I_{p}})z^{n}
and define the recursive sequence
r_{n,\rho}
by
r_{1,\rho}=q_{1,\rho}
for each
\rho
and

r_{n,\rho}=q_{n,\rho}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d>1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\gamma\text{ irrep.}}\frac{\langle\rho^{\otimes d},\gamma\rangle}{d}r_{n/d,\gamma}

for each
\rho
. Then the sequence
b_{n,\rho}
is given by

b_{n,\rho}=\sum_{d\mid n}\sum_{\gamma\text{ irrep.}}\langle\gamma^{\otimes_{\rm cyc}d},\rho\rangle r_{n/d,\gamma},

where the cyclic tensor power
\gamma^{\otimes_{\rm cyc}d}
is defined in Definition
15.1
.

(2)

If
Q_{p}(z)\in 1+zR(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}),\mathbb{Z})[[z]]
where
R(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}),\mathbb{Z})\subseteq R(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))
is the set of integer linear combinations of the irreducible characters, then
b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

In order to prove Theorem
13.1
and Theorem
13.4
, we will need new results in linear algebra, combinatorics, and representation theory.

14.
The Log Factorization Theorem

Let
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
be the set of sequence of natural numbers. For any
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
, define

|\mathbf{n}|:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}n_{i}.

We let
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
be the subset of
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
for which
|\mathbf{n}|<\infty
.

The following theorem formally takes place in the power series ring
\mathbb{C}[[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},...]]
in countably many variables. The monomials in this space are parametrized by
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
via the correspondence

\mathbf{n}\mapsto\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}:=\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}x_{i}^{n_{i}}.

The subspace of power series without a constant term is a normed vector space with respect to the norm

||f(\mathbf{x})||=\frac{1}{{\rm ord}(f)},

where
{\rm ord}(f)
is the smallest integer
m
for which there exists an
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
with
|\mathbf{n}|=m
and the coefficient of
\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}
in
f
is nonzero. This is defined so that the monomials
\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}\}
form an Schauder basis for
\mathbb{C}[[\mathbf{x}]]
.

The vector space of power series in countably many variables is an extremely flexible object, and by specializing the variables in particular ways we can push properties of this vector space to any of the power series rings we have considered so far.

The following theorem does all the linear algebra and combinatorics necessary for Theorems
13.1
and
13.4
. It is most naturally presented in terms of multi-variable power series, which can be specialized as needed.

Theorem 14.1
.

For any
N\geq 1
, the identity

-\log\left(1-\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}x_{i}\right)=-\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbb{N})^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\{\mathbf{0}\}}c_{\mathbf{n}}\log(1-\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}})

converges absolutely uniformly in the region cut out by

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|x_{i}|<1-\epsilon

for any choice of
\epsilon>0
, where the coefficients are given by the following equivalent formulas:

(i)

c_{\mathbf{n}}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{n}|}\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mathbf{n})}\mu(d){|\mathbf{n}/d|\choose\mathbf{n}/d},

where
|\mathbf{n}|=n_{0}+n_{1}+\cdots
,
\gcd(\mathbf{n})=\gcd(n_{0},n_{1},\cdots)
, and
\mathbf{n}/d=(n_{0}/d,n_{1}/d,n_{2}/d,...)
; and

(ii)

c_{\mathbf{n}}
equals the number of orbits of length
|\mathbf{n}|
in

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z})=\{(A_{1},A_{2},...):A_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z},\ |A_{i}|=n_{i},\ A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset\text{ when }i\neq j\}

under the action by
\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z}
defined by

1.(A_{1},A_{2},...)\mapsto(\{a+1:a\in A_{1}\},\{a+1:a\in A_{2}\},...).

In particular, (ii) implies
c_{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbb{N}
.

This theorem is proven by essentially decomposing the vector on the left hand side in terms of the basis
\{-\log(1-\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}})\}_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\{\mathbf{0}\}}
. This computation is done explicitly via the proof of Lemma
11.3
in order to produce to description of the coefficients in Theorem
14.1
(i).

However, the combinatorial description in Theorem
14.1
(ii) is where the magic really happens. By proving that the coefficients
c_{\mathbf{n}}
are all natural numbers, we can specialize this theorem to any number of vector spaces of power series (including those in Theorem
13.1
and Theorem
13.4
) in order to describe basis decompositions in such vector spaces explicitly in terms of this sequence. This gives us the necessarily leverage to determine when other basis decompositions of power series with respect to a “log basis” have integer coefficients.

Example 14.2
.

Consider the function
-\log(1-x-y)
. This function has two variables, so it suffices to consider
x_{1}=x
,
x_{2}=y
, and
x_{i}=0
for each
i\geq 3
. In particular,
\log(1-\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}})\neq 0
if and only if
n_{i}=0
for
i\geq 3
.

We can then write

-\log(1-x-y)=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathbb{N}\\
(i,j)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}c_{(i,j)}\log(1-x^{i}y^{j}).

The constants are given explicitly by alternating sums of binomial coefficients

c_{(i,j)}=\frac{1}{i+j}\sum_{d\mid\gcd(i,j)}\mu(d){i/d+j/d\choose i/d}.

Theorem
14.1
implies that
c_{(i,j)}
are all nonnegative integers. A noticeably nice subcase is that whenever
\gcd(i,j)=1
, then

c_{(i,j)}=\frac{1}{i+j}{i+j\choose i}.

Also nice to know is that

c_{(i,0)}=\begin{cases}1&i=1\\
0&i>1.\end{cases}

We can truncate the summation by putting all terms of sufficiently large degree into an error term. For example:

\displaystyle\log(1-x-y)=

\displaystyle c_{(1,0)}\log(1-x)+c_{(0,1)}\log(1-y)+c_{(2,0)}\log(1-x^{2})

\displaystyle+c_{(1,1)}\log(1-xy)+c_{(0,2)}\log(1-y^{2})+O(\text{degree }3)

\displaystyle\log(1-x-y)=

\displaystyle\log(1-x)+\log(1-y)+\log(1-xy)+O(\text{degree }3).

The right-hand side appears like the log of a product of Euler factors of
L
-functions, meanwhile the left-hand appears like the log of a power series Euler factor. This is what allows us to connect Euler products and zeta products together. The uniform convergence is necessary to produce right halfplanes of convergence. The fact that
c_{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbb{N}
is particularly useful, and will allow us to quantify when branch cuts are necessary in the meromorphic continuations.

Proof.

We expand the lefthand side formally via the Taylor series for
-\log(1-T)
in order to express it in terms of the standard basis
\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}\}
of monomials, under which we find that

\displaystyle-\log\left(1-\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}x_{i}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\\
|\mathbf{n}|=k\end{subarray}}{k\choose\mathbf{n}}\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbb{N})^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\{\mathbf{0}\}}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{n}|}{|\mathbf{n}|\choose\mathbf{n}}\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}.

We formally change bases to
\{-\log(1-x^{\mathbf{n}})\}
by following along the proof of Lemma
11.3
. The Taylor series decomposition of the basis vectors is given by

-\log(1-\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}\mathbf{x}^{k\mathbf{n}},

where
k\mathbf{n}=(kn_{0},kn_{1},kn_{2},...)
. Thus, the linear transformation sending
\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}
to
-\log(1-\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}})
has a matrix given by
A=[a_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}]
for

a_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{d}&\mathbf{n}=d\mathbf{m}\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

The matrix
A
being lower triangular in the sense of Lemma
11.3
, but we will need to compute the inverse matrix explicitely. We do so by solving the equation
AB=I
. Write
B=[b_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}]
and
I=[\delta_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}]
the identity matrix. This gives a system of equations

\delta_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}=\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\{\mathbf{0}\}}a_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{j}}b_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{m}}=\sum_{d\mid\mathbf{n}}\frac{1}{d}b_{\frac{\mathbf{n}}{d},\mathbf{m}},

where we write
d\mid\mathbf{n}
to mean
d\mid n_{i}
for each
i
(or in other words, there exists a
\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
with
\mathbf{n}=d\mathbf{j}
). This is a Dirichlet convolution between the function
d\mapsto 1/d
and
d\mapsto b_{\frac{\mathbf{n}}{\gcd(\mathbf{n})}*d,\mathbf{m}}
evaluated at
\gcd(\mathbf{n})
, where we define
\gcd(\mathbf{n})=\gcd(n_{1},n_{2},...)
and note that
d\mid\mathbf{n}
iff
d\mid\gcd(\mathbf{n})
. The Dirichlet inverse of
d\mapsto 1/d
is
d\mapsto\mu(d)/d
because the function is completely multiplicative, so by convoluting on both sides by this function we find that

\displaystyle b_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}=b_{\frac{\mathbf{n}}{\gcd(\mathbf{n})}*\gcd(\mathbf{n}),\mathbf{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mathbf{n})}\frac{\mu(d)}{d}\delta_{\frac{\mathbf{n}}{\gcd(\mathbf{n})}*\frac{\gcd(\mathbf{n})}{d},\mathbf{m}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\mu(d)}{d}&\mathbf{n}=d\mathbf{m}\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

We have successfully inverted the linear transformation, where we remark that the formula is particularly nice falling out of a Möbius inversion.

We now apply the
\{x^{\mathbf{n}}\}\to\{-\log(1-\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}})\}
change of basis matrix to the
\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}\}
-coefficients of
-\log(1-\sum x_{i})
to get

\displaystyle c_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{N}-\{\mathbf{0}\}}b_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{m}|}{|\mathbf{m}|\choose\mathbf{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mathbf{n})}\frac{\mu(d)}{d}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{n}/d|}{|\mathbf{n}/d|\choose\mathbf{n}/d}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{n}|}\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mathbf{n})}\mu(d){|\mathbf{n}/d|\choose\mathbf{n}/d}.

This formally proves (i).

Part (ii) is simple to check given the combinatorial construction. Consider that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z})
has
{|\mathbf{n}|\choose\mathbf{n}}
elements by definition of the multinomial coefficient. Letting
f(d)
denote the number of orbits of length
d
(so necessarily
f(d)\neq 0
implies
d\mid|\mathbf{n}|
), we note that
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z})
has an orbit of length
d
iff
|\textnormal{Stab}_{\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z}}(A)|=|\mathbf{n}|/d
, or equivalently
\textnormal{Stab}_{\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z}}(A)=d\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z}
as the orders of subgroups of
\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z}
are unique. Thus

\displaystyle|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z})|

\displaystyle=|\operatorname{Fix}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z})}(|\mathbf{n}|)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid|\mathbf{n}|}\#\{A\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z}):\textnormal{Stab}_{\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z}}(A)=d\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid|n|}d\cdot f(d)

By Möbius inversion, we find that

|\mathbf{n}|\cdot f(|\mathbf{n}|)=\sum_{d\mid|\mathbf{n}|}\mu(d)|\operatorname{Fix}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z})}(|\mathbf{n}|/d)|.

We now completely describe the sets
\operatorname{Fix}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z})}(|\mathbf{n}|/d)
. An element
A=(A_{1},...,A_{N})\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z})
is fixed by
|\mathbf{n}|/d
if and only if whenever
a\in A_{i}
, then so too is the arithmetic progression
a,a+\frac{|\mathbf{n}|}{d},a+2\frac{|\mathbf{n}|}{d},...
. This implies two things for us:

•

First, it implies that each
A_{i}
is a disjoint union of arithmetic progressions of length
\frac{|\mathbf{n}|}{|\mathbf{n}|/d}=d
, so in particular
d\mid|A_{i}|=n_{i}
. Thus, we have proven that
|\operatorname{Fix}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z})}(|\mathbf{n}|/d)|=0
if
d\nmid\gcd(\mathbf{n})
,

•

Second, it implies that
A
must be in the image of the embedding

\pi^{*}:\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}/d}\left(\mathbb{Z}/\frac{|\mathbf{n}|}{d}\mathbb{Z}\right)\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z})

given by
(B_{1},B_{2}...)\mapsto(\pi^{-1}(B_{1}),\pi^{-1}(B_{2}),...)
for
\pi:\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/\frac{|\mathbf{n}|}{d}\mathbb{Z}
the quotient map.

It is trivial to see that the converse holds as well, proving that if
d\mid\gcd(\mathbf{n})
then

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Fix}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z})}(|\mathbf{n}|/d)|

\displaystyle=|\im(\pi^{*})|

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}/d}\left(\mathbb{Z}/\frac{|\mathbf{n}|}{d}\mathbb{Z}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle={|\mathbf{n}/d|\choose\mathbf{n}/d},

and is
0
otherwise. Thus we have proven that

|\mathbf{n}|\cdot f(|\mathbf{n}|)=\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mathbf{n})}\mu(d){|\mathbf{n}/d|\choose\mathbf{n}/d}=|\mathbf{n}|c_{\mathbf{n}},

concluding the proof of (ii).

Lastly, we must prove uniform convergence. We can do this using some crude upper bounds. We certainly have by (ii)

c_{\mathbf{n}}\leq|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{Z}/|\mathbf{n}|\mathbb{Z})|={|\mathbf{n}|\choose\mathbf{n}}.

So we consider the upper bound

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\{\mathbf{0}\}}{|\mathbf{n}|\choose\mathbf{n}}|-\log(1-\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}))|.

When
|x_{j}|\leq\sum|x_{i}|<1-\epsilon
this implies
|\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}|<(1-\epsilon)^{|\mathbf{n}|}\leq 1-\epsilon<1
, so that the remainder theorem for Taylor series implies that
-\log(1-\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}})=\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}+O(M\mathbf{x}^{2\mathbf{n}})
where

M=\sup_{|t|<1-\epsilon}(1-t)^{-2}\leq\epsilon^{2}.

Thus,
|\log(1-\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}})|=O(|\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}|)
. We use this to produce an upper bound

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\{\mathbf{0}\}}{|\mathbf{n}|\choose\mathbf{n}}|\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{n}}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|x_{i}|\right)^{k}.

This is a geometric series, which converges uniformly on the region
\sum|x_{i}|<1-\epsilon
as required.
∎

15.
Cyclic Tensor Powers

It is necessary for Theorem
13.4
to consider the following construction:

Definition 15.1
.

Let
\rho:G\to GL(V)
be a finite dimensional representation of
G
over the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space
V
. For any positive integer
n
, fix a primitive
n^{\rm th}
root of unity
\zeta
. Define the
cyclic tensor power
of
\rho
by
n
to be
\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}n}:G\to GL(V^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}n})
, where

V^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}n}:=V^{\otimes n}/\langle(\zeta^{m}-m).v\rangle,

where
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
acts on
V^{\otimes n}
by
m.(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n})=a_{1+m}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n+m}
under the identification
a_{i}=a_{j}
whenever
i\equiv j\mod n
.

One readily verifies that
V^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}n}
is a quotient
G
-module of the tensor power
V^{\otimes n}
as the
G
action commutes with scalar multiples and permuting the entries of pure tensors.

While it is unnecessary for our purposes, we remark that the isomorphism class of
V^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}n}
is independent of the choice of primitive root
\zeta
. Indeed, any other choice of primitive root of unity is given by
\zeta^{m}
for some
m
coprime to
n
and the isomorphism
\{m\}:V^{\otimes n}\to V^{\otimes n}
defined by
v_{1}\otimes\cdots v_{n}\mapsto v_{1\cdot m}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{n\cdot m}
induced by the permutation action of
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
sends

\displaystyle(\zeta^{d}-[d]).v

\displaystyle\to(\zeta^{d}\{m\}-[d\cdot m]\{m\}).v

\displaystyle=((\zeta^{m^{-1}})^{dm}-[dm]).\{m\}v.

This implies
\{m\}
sends
V^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}n}
to the corresponding quotient with
\zeta^{m^{-1}}
as the chosen primitive root. By applying the same argument to
\{m^{-1}\}
(which exists modulo
n
), we conclude that this is an isomorphism and that the choice of primitive root does not matter.

Example 15.2
.

Let
\rho:G\to{\rm GL}(V)
be a representation. Let’s examine the cyclic tensor powers by small integers.

(1)

\rho^{\otimes_{\rm cyc}1}=\rho
. Indeed, the only primitive first root of unity is
1
itself, while
\mathbb{Z}/1\mathbb{Z}
acts trivially on anything. Thus

V^{\otimes 1}/\langle(1^{1}-1).v\rangle=V/\langle 0\rangle\cong V.

(2)

\rho^{\otimes_{\rm cyc}2}=\Lambda_{2}\rho
is the representation associated to the exterior algebra
\Lambda_{2}(V)
. This is because

V^{\otimes 2}/\langle((-1)^{1}-1).(v\otimes w)\rangle=V^{\otimes 2}/\langle-v\otimes w-w\otimes v\rangle=\Lambda_{2}V.

(3)

\rho^{\otimes_{\rm cyc}3}
is
not
isomorphic to
\Lambda_{3}\rho
. The construction becomes more subtle from
3^{\rm rd}
powers onwards. Inside of
V^{\otimes_{\rm cyc}3}
, the classes of
u\otimes v\otimes w
and
v\otimes u\otimes w
need not be related, unlike in the exterior algebra. However, there is a cyclic relationship between the following classes:

[u\otimes v\otimes w]=\zeta_{3}[w\otimes u\otimes v].

The appearance of a complex coefficient in this relationship is also distinct from the exterior algebra.

The cyclic tensor power can be used to identify the character
{\rm tr}(\rho(g))
using only characteristic polynomials.

Theorem 15.3
.

Let
\rho:G\to GL(V)
be a finite dimensional representation of
G
over the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space
V
. Then

-\log(1-{\rm tr}(\rho(g))z)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\log(\det(I-\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}n}(g)z^{n})),

where the sum converges absolutely in the region
|z|<1
and uniformly on the region
|z|<1-\epsilon
.

While
1-{\rm tr}(\rho(g))z
is not itself a characteristic polynomial, this result shows that it equals the convergent product of infinitely many characteristic polynomials. This will be immensely useful for getting a handle on the sequence
b_{n,\rho}(Q)
appearing in Theorem
13.4
.

Proof of Theorem
15.3
.

We utilize a standard fact for characteristic polynomials of representations, that

-\log(\det(I-\varphi(g)z))=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm tr}(\varphi(g^{k}))}{k}z^{k}

for
|z|<1
.

Thus, it follows that

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\log(\det(I-\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}n}(g)z^{n}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm tr}(\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}n}(g^{k}))}{k}z^{nk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{d\mid k}\frac{{\rm tr}(\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g^{k/d}))}{k/d}\right)z^{k}.

In order to prove the equality formally, it suffices to prove that the
k^{\rm th}
coefficient is equal to
{\rm tr}(\rho(g))^{k}/k
.

Fix a basis for
V
, and therefore a basis of pure tensors of any tensor power labelled
\mathcal{B}_{\otimes d}
. Choose a representative of each nonzero equivalence class of pure tensor powers to form a basis for
V^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}
which we label
\mathcal{B}_{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}
. To compute the trace of
\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g)
, it suffices to know the coefficient of the basis element
[a]
in the vector
\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g)[a]
, which we denote
\langle\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g)[a],[a]\rangle
, implying

\displaystyle{\rm tr}(\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[a]\in\mathcal{B}_{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}\end{subarray}}\langle\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g)[a],[a]\rangle.

We note that
\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g)[a]=[\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a]
and that
[m.a]=\zeta^{m}[a]
so that the representatives of
[a]
are all of the form
\zeta^{-m}(m.a)
. Thus

\displaystyle\langle\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g)[a],[a]\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle[\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a],[a]\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,\zeta^{-m}(m.a)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\zeta^{-m}\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,m.a\rangle

We seek to expand the summation to a sum over
\mathcal{B}_{\otimes d}
, essentially undoing the quotient defining
V^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}
. A pure tensor
a=a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n}
has
[a]\neq 0
if and only if
\textnormal{Stab}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(a)=1
, which implies the orbit under the
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
action is necessarily of size
d
. We remark on two things:

(1)

If
a
is a representative of the basis element
[a]
of
V^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}
with
\textnormal{Stab}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(a)=1
, then

\frac{\langle\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g)[m.a],[m.a]\rangle}{\langle[m.a],[m.a]\rangle}=\frac{\langle\zeta^{m}\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g)[a],\zeta^{m}[a]\rangle}{\langle\zeta^{m}[a],\zeta^{m}[a]\rangle}=\langle\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g)[a],[a]\rangle

is independent of the choice of representative under the
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
action. (Note that
[m.a]
is not a basis element, so it is important that we include it in the denominator).

(2)

If
a
is a pure tensor with
\textnormal{Stab}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(a)=\ell\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
for
\ell\neq d
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\zeta^{-m}\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,m.a\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,m.a\rangle\left(\sum_{j=0}^{d/\ell-1}\zeta^{-(m+j\ell)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\zeta^{-m}\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,m.a\rangle\left(\sum_{j=0}^{d/\ell-1}(\zeta^{-\ell})^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

as we note that
\zeta^{-\ell}
is a primitive
(d/\ell)^{\rm th}
root of unity and
d/\ell\neq 1
.

Noting that the trace is the same for all bases, we partition
\mathcal{B}_{\otimes d}
into
d
lifts of bases for
V^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}
(which can be gotten from the orbits of a lift of
\mathcal{B}_{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}
) together with the collection of pure tensors
a
with
\textnormal{Stab}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(a)\neq 1
. This produces the sum

\displaystyle{\rm tr}(\rho^{\otimes_{\rm cyc}d}(g))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}_{\otimes d}}\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\zeta^{-m}\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,m.a\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\zeta^{-m}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}_{\otimes d}}\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,m.a\rangle.

We next claim that the inner summation depends only on
\gcd(d,m)
. Indeed, for any
n
coprime to
d
, consider the isomorphism
\{n\}:V^{\otimes d}\to V^{\otimes d}
sending

a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{d}\mapsto a_{n}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{nd}.

This map certainly preserves pure tensors and commutes with
\rho^{\otimes d}(g)
(as
\rho^{\otimes d}(g)
acts coordinate wise). We also note that

\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)\{n^{-1}\}a,m.\{n^{-1}\}a\rangle=\langle\{n^{-1}\}\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,\{n^{-1}\}((nm).a)\rangle=\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,((nm).a)\rangle,

where the last equality follows from
\{n^{-1}\}
being an isomorphism which restricts to a bijection on the basis of pure tensors. Thus, we have proven

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{B}_{\otimes d}\\
\textnormal{Stab}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(a)=1\end{subarray}}\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,m.a\rangle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{B}_{\otimes d}\\
\textnormal{Stab}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(a)=1\end{subarray}}\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,((nm).a)\rangle

whenever
\gcd(n,d)=1
, showing that this sum depends only on
\gcd(d,m)
. Partitioning as such, we find that

\displaystyle{\rm tr}(\rho^{\otimes_{\rm cyc}d}(g))=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{q\mid d}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}_{\otimes d}}\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,q.a\rangle\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq m<d\\
\gcd(m,d)=q\end{subarray}}\zeta^{-m}\right).

The sum of roots of unity can be evaluated using the Möbius function trick

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq m<d\\
\gcd(m,d)=q\end{subarray}}\zeta^{-m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq m<d\\
q\mid m\\
\gcd(m/q,d/q)=1\end{subarray}}\zeta^{-m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq m<d\\
q\mid m\end{subarray}}\zeta^{-m}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\mid d/q\\
\ell\mid m/q\end{subarray}}\mu(\ell)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\mid d/q}\mu(\ell)\sum_{r=0}^{d/q\ell-1}\zeta^{-rq\ell}

\displaystyle=\mu(d/q).

Therefore we conclude that

{\rm tr}(\rho^{\otimes_{\rm cyc}d}(g))=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{q\mid d}\mu(d/q)\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}_{\otimes d}}\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,q.a\rangle.

We choose an identification
(\mathcal{B}_{\otimes q})^{d/q}\leftrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{\otimes d}
via

b_{i}=a_{1+iq}\otimes a_{2+iq}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{q+iq}

for each
i=1,...,d/q
. The action by
q
on
a
sends
b_{i}
to
b_{i+1}
, taking
i
modulo
d/q
, so we can write

\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,q.a\rangle=\prod_{i=1}^{d/q}\langle\rho^{\otimes q}(g)b_{i},b_{i+1}\rangle.

We make use of a general fact about powers of matrices: If
A
is an invertible matrix on a finite dimensional vector space
V
with basis
\mathcal{B}
, then

\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}}\langle A^{n}a,v\rangle=\sum_{a_{1},...,a_{n}\in\mathcal{B}}\langle Aa_{1},a_{2}\rangle\langle Aa_{2},a_{3}\rangle\cdots\langle Aa_{n},v\rangle.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}_{\otimes d}}\langle\rho^{\otimes d}(g)a,q.a\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{b_{1},...,b_{q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\otimes d/q}}\prod_{i=1}^{d/q}\langle\rho^{\otimes q}(g)b_{i},b_{i+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{\otimes q}}\langle\rho^{\otimes q}(g)^{d/q}b,b\rangle

\displaystyle={\rm tr}(\rho(g^{d/q}))^{q}

and so

{\rm tr}(\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g))=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{q\mid d}\mu(d/q){\rm tr}(\rho(g^{d/q}))^{q}.

We conclude the proof by evaluating

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\log(\det(I-\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}n}(g)z^{n}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{d\mid k}\frac{{\rm tr}(\rho^{\otimes_{\text{cyc}}d}(g^{k/d}))}{k/d}\right)z^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k}\left(\sum_{d\mid k}\sum_{q\mid d}\mu(d/q){\rm tr}(\rho((g^{k/d})^{d/q}))^{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k}\left(\sum_{tyz=k}\mu(y){\rm tr}(\rho((g^{k/ty})^{ty/t}))^{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k}\left(\sum_{tyz=k}\mu(y){\rm tr}(\rho((g^{k/t})))^{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k}\sum_{t\mid k}{\rm tr}(\rho((g^{k/t})))^{t}\left(\sum_{y\mid k/t}\mu(y)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k}{\rm tr}(\rho(g^{k/k}))^{k}

\displaystyle=-\log(1-{\rm tr}\rho(g)z).

∎

16.
Proofs of Explicit Results

We are now prepared to proof Theorem
13.1
and Theorem
13.4
.

Proof of Theorem
13.1
.

Part (i) follows from the fact that the linear transformation
z\mathbb{C}[[z]]\to z\mathbb{C}[[z]]
sending
z^{n}\mapsto-\log(1-z^{n})
is an invertible lower triangular matrix, as was used in the proof of Proposition
11.1
. The recursive formula then follows from the inductive construction in the proof of Lemma
11.3
.

Part (ii) will follow from specializing Theorem
14.1
. Firstly, we specialize to
x_{i}=q_{i}z^{i}
so that

\displaystyle\log Q(z)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\{\mathbf{0}\}}c_{\mathbf{n}}\log\left(1-\prod(q_{i}z^{i})^{n_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
\sum in_{i}=k\end{subarray}}c_{\mathbf{n}}\log\left(1-\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}z^{k}\right).

Next, we take advantage of the fact that
\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}
is an integer. If
\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}=0
this term vanishes. If
\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}>0
, then we can specialize Theorem
14.1
to
x_{i}=1
for
i=1,2,...,\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}
and
x_{i}=0
for
i>\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}
. This implies

\displaystyle\log\left(1-\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}z^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
i>\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}\Rightarrow m_{i}=0\end{subarray}}c_{\mathbf{m}}\log(1-z^{k|\mathbf{m}|}).

If
\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}<0
, then we likewise specialize Theorem
14.1
to
x_{i}=-1
for
i=1,2,...,|\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}|
and
x_{i}=0
for
i>\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}
. This implies

\displaystyle\log\left(1-\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}z^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
i>|\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}|\Rightarrow m_{i}=0\end{subarray}}c_{\mathbf{m}}\log(1-(-1)^{|\mathbf{m}|}z^{k|\mathbf{m}|})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
i>|\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}|\Rightarrow m_{i}=0\end{subarray}}c_{\mathbf{m}}\log(1-z^{k|\mathbf{m}|})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
i>|\mathbf{q}^{\mathbf{n}}|\Rightarrow m_{i}=0\\
2\nmid|\mathbf{m}|\end{subarray}}c_{\mathbf{m}}\log(1-z^{2k|\mathbf{m}|}).

By composing these linear combinations and combining like terms, we find that the coefficients
b_{n}
of
-\log(1-z^{n})
in this decomposition are comprised of sums and differences of
c_{\mathbf{n}}c_{\mathbf{m}}
for various
\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Theorem
14.1
(ii) implies that these are integers, so sums and differences of these values are necessarily also integers. Thus
b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
, concluding the proof.
∎

The proof of Theorem
13.4
is similar, although we need to use a slightly different linear transformation.

Proof of Theorem
13.4
.

Unlike in Proposition
11.2
, we do not directly consider the linear transformation sending
{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z^{n}\mapsto-\log\det(I-\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z^{n})
. Instead, we break this transformation into two pieces.

First, consider the linear transformation
zR(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))[[z]]\to zR(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}))[[x]]
sending
{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z^{n}\mapsto-\log(1-{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z^{n})
. Let
T=[t_{(n,\rho),(m,\gamma)}]
be the matrix of this transformation with respect to the standard basis. By expanding

\displaystyle-\log(1-{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z^{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})^{d}z^{dn}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}{\rm tr}\rho^{\otimes d}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z^{dn}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\gamma\text{ irrep.}}\frac{\langle\rho^{\otimes d},\gamma\rangle}{n}{\rm tr}\gamma(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z^{dn},

we can determine that the coefficients are given by

t_{(n,\rho),(m,\gamma)}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\text{ irrep.}}\frac{\langle\rho^{\otimes m/n},\gamma\rangle}{n}&n\mid m\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Thus,
T
is a lower triangular matrix with nonzero diagonal. Following the construction solving a linear system in the proof of Lemma
11.3
, the sequence
r_{n,\rho}
determined by the basis decomposition

\log Q_{p}(z)=-\sum_{n,\rho}r_{n,\rho}\log(1-{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z^{n})

is given precisely by the recurrence relation in part (i).

By Theorem
15.3
, we know that

\displaystyle\log(1-{\rm tr}\rho(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z^{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\log\det(I-\rho^{\otimes_{\rm cyc}d}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z^{dn})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\gamma\text{ irrep.}}\langle\rho^{\otimes_{\rm cyc}d},\gamma\rangle\log\det(I-\gamma(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})z^{dn}).

Plugging this in above and combining like terms concludes the proof of part (i).

Part (ii) is proven very similarly to Theorem
13.1
(ii). When
\log Q_{p}(z)\in zR(\textnormal{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}),\mathbb{Z})[[z]]
, the sequence
r_{n,\rho}
are determined to be integers by exactly the same argument as for Theorem
13.1
(ii), together with the fact that
\langle\rho^{\otimes d},\gamma\rangle
is an integer (given by the number of times
\gamma
appears as an irreducible component of
\rho^{\otimes d}
). Similarly,
\langle\gamma^{\otimes_{\rm cyc}d},\rho\rangle
is always an integer so that part (i) implies
b_{n,\rho}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎
