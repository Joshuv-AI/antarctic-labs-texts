Exciton-assisted electron tunneling in van der Waals heterostructures
Lujun Wang, Sotirios Papadopoulos, Fadil Iyikanat, Jian Zhang, Jing Huang, Kenji Watanabe, Takashi Taniguchi, Michel Calame, Mickael L. Perrin, F. Javier García de Abajo, Lukas Novotny

Abstract
The control of elastic and inelastic electron tunneling relies on materials with well defined interfaces. Van der Waals materials made of two-dimensional constituents form an ideal platform for such studies. Signatures of acoustic phonons and defect states have been observed in current-to-voltage ( I ​ – ​ V I\textendash V ) measurements. These features can be explained by direct electron-phonon or electron-defect interactions. Here, we use a novel tunneling process that involves excitons in transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs). We study tunnel junctions consisting of graphene and gold electrodes separated by hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) with an adjacent TMD monolayer and observe prominent resonant features in I ​ – ​ V I\textendash V measurements. These resonances appear at bias voltages that correspond to TMD exciton energies. By placing the TMD outside of the tunneling pathway, we demonstrate that this phonon-exciton mediated tunneling process does not require any charge injection into the TMD. This work demonstrates the appearance of optical modes in electrical transport measurements and introduces a new functionality for optoelectronic devices based on van der Waals materials.



I
INTRODUCTION

The isolation of two-dimensional (2D) crystals combined with advances in fabrication techniques have enabled the realization of a new type of material, known as van der Waals (vdW) heterostructures, in which different atomic layers are assembled together in a desired sequence
Geim and Grigorieva 2013
. Tailored heterostructures comprising graphene, hexagonal boron nitride (hBN), transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), and other 2D materials are currently designed to display properties that are absent in the individual constituents, thus providing a platform for fundamental studies
Wallbank et al. 2016
;
Ma et al. 2016
;
Yankowitz et al. 2012
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Cao et al. 2018
;
Rivera et al. 2015
and novel device applications
Koppens et al. 2014
;
Massicotte et al. 2016
;
Withers et al. 2015
. In this respect, tunnel junctions with different material combinations form an interesting system for investigating electron tunneling processes. Previous experiments have shown phonon-assisted resonant electron tunneling in metal-insulator junctions
Chynoweth et al. 1962
, in conventional semiconductor heterostructures
Eaves et al. 1985
, and in graphene-based systems
Brar et al. 2007
;
Zhang et al. 2008
;
Wehling et al. 2008
;
Vdovin et al. 2016
. Similarly, exciton-assisted resonant tunneling has been observed in conventional semiconductor quantum wells
Cao et al. 1995
;
Cao and Willander 1997
. Plasmon-assisted resonant tunneling has been investigated in metallic quantum well structures hosting silver nanorods
Qian et al. 2021
and graphene-based structures
Enaldiev et al. 2017
. Furthermore, evidence for defect-assisted resonant tunneling has been observed in hBN-based junctions
Chandni et al. 2015
.

Here, we demonstrate exciton-assisted resonant electron tunneling in vdW tunnel junctions. Our electron transport measurements reveal distinct resonant peaks that coincide in energy with TMD excitons. We investigate the
I\textendash V
characteristics of TMD/graphene/hBN/Au tunnel junctions and compare them with TMD-free reference structures. Resonances observed in differential conductance (
\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}V
) measurements agree with TMD exciton energies despite the fact that the TMD is placed outside the electron tunneling pathway. These resonances can be the explained by a one-step process involving indirect excitons and a two-step process involving both phonons and direct excitons. Both of these processes conserve energy and in-plane momentum. Owing to the large exciton binding energies of TMD monolayers
Wang et al. 2018
;
Goryca et al. 2019
, such as WS
2
, MoS
2
, WSe
2
, and MoSe
2
, the resonant features can be observed at room temperature. While exciton-phonon interactions in TMDs have been investigated by optical methods
Barati et al. 2022
;
Funk et al. 2021
;
Meneghini et al. 2022
;
Du et al. 2019
, in our study we observe this interaction directly in electronic transport measurements, shedding light on the ways excitons are involved in the conservation of momentum during tunneling. Beyond its fundamental interest, our work establishes a new platform for the investigation of the physical processes involved in the electrical generation of excitons in TMDs.

III
CONCLUSION

In conclusion, we have demonstrated exciton-assisted resonant electron tunneling in graphene/hBN/Au tunnel junctions with a TMD monolayer placed in proximity to the graphene layer. This process manifests as an abrupt increase in the tunnel current when the bias electron energy
eV_{\mathrm{b}}
matches an exciton energy, resulting in a resonance peak in the
\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}V
curve. An observed blueshift of the resonant peak with decreasing temperature is consistent with that of the corresponding excitons revealed by optical spectroscopy
Hanbicki et al. 2015
;
Jadczak et al. 2017
. We find that the exciton states giving rise to the main resonance peaks in the
\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}V
curves are different for the four studied TMD devices. We theoretically explain our measurements by electron tunneling mechanisms that involve either indirect or direct excitons. Indirect excitons can supply both the energy and in-plane momentum required to tunnel from graphene to gold, while direct excitons require additional in-plane momentum supplied by phonons. Our findings are further substantiated by optical measurements, which reveal excitonic light emission driven by inelastic electron tunneling. In our devices, the TMD layer is placed outside of the electron tunneling path, which allows us to suppress exciton generation by direct charge injection. This device structure provides a platform not only for studying fundamental aspects of tunneling processes, but also for exploring novel device functionalities for optoelectronics, all-electrical sensing and spectroscopy.

IV
METHODS

Sample fabrication.
All flakes are mechanically exfoliated from bulk crystals in air (hBN) or in an argon-based glovebox (TMD and graphene). The heterostructures are first assembled using a standard pick-up technique with a PDMS/PC stamp
Zomer et al. 2014
, then transferred onto the prefabricated Au electrodes on a glass substrate in the glovebox.

Electrical and optical measurements.
Room temperature electrical measurements are performed using a Keithley 2602B source meter. Low-temperature measurements are performed in a variable temperature probe station. Currents are measured using a Femto DDPCA-300 current amplifier. An ADwin Pro II DAC is used to apply the bias voltage and read the output voltage of the current amplifier. For optical measurements, the samples are mounted on a Nikon TE300 inverted microscope under ambient conditions. The emitted light is collected by a
\times 100
objective (NA=0.9) and analysed using an Andor iXon Ultra camera and a Princeton Instruments Acton SpectraPro 300i spectrometer.

Theoretical calculations.
The screened interaction in Eqs. (
1
) is obtained by combining first-principles calculations of the nonlocal conductivity of TMD monolayers, the random-phase-approximation nonlocal response of graphene, and the specular-reflection model for the nonlocal response of the gold surface based on the Lindhard permittivity for the bulk metal. The anisotropic response of hBN is accounted for through a local permittivity tensor. To deal with indirect TMD excitons, the response of the heterostructure is calculated with inclusion of umklapp processes for the reflection and transmission coefficients of the TMD layer, which permeate the screened interaction, such that it becomes a tensor labeled by TMD reciprocal lattice vectors and wave vectors within the 1BZ. The derivation of Eqs. (
1
) further involves the analysis of the electron potential landscape in the heterostructure, which is incorporated through the corresponding electron Green function. A detailed self-contained analysis of these elements is presented in the Supplementary Sec.
S5.6
–
S5.9
, along with graphical information on the TMD nonlocal conductivities and the screened interaction of our heterostructures.

(S6b)

\displaystyle{\rm i}kA+B\psi^{\prime}_{1}(0)+C\psi^{\prime}_{2}(0)=n_{0}\times 2m_{\rm e}/\hbar^{2},

(S6c)

\displaystyle\kappa D+B\psi^{\prime}_{1}(d)+C\psi^{\prime}_{2}(d)=n_{d}\times 2m_{\rm e}/\hbar^{2}.

(S6d)

) and (

n_{0}=0

Likewise, we evaluate

\displaystyle\partial_{z^{\prime}}G_{0}(z^{\prime},z,\varepsilon)|_{z^{\prime}=0}=\left\{\begin{matrix}[l]A^{\prime}\,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}kz},&&\text{$z<0$},\\
\\

with coefficients determined by the equations

\displaystyle A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}\psi_{1}(0)-C^{\prime}\psi_{2}(0)=2m_{\rm e}/\hbar^{2},

(S7a)

\displaystyle D^{\prime}-B^{\prime}\psi_{1}(d)-C^{\prime}\psi_{2}(d)=0,

(S7b)

\displaystyle{\rm i}kA^{\prime}+B^{\prime}\psi^{\prime}_{1}(0)+C^{\prime}\psi^{\prime}_{2}(0)=0,

(S7c)

\displaystyle\kappa D^{\prime}+B^{\prime}\psi^{\prime}_{1}(d)+C^{\prime}\psi^{\prime}_{2}(d)=0.

(S7d)

This solution if obtained by first writing

(with

S5.6.3
Inelastic electron tunneling assisted by excitations in the heterostructure

The tunneling process can be assisted by the creation of an excitation in the heterostructure, which absorbs the energy and momentum change undergone by the transferred electron. In this section, we derive a general expression for the inelastic tunneling current that encapsulates such excitations
n
of energies
\hbar\omega_{n}
in the dielectric response function. Although we consider electron tunneling from graphene to gold in this work, the formalism presented in this section can be applied to any combination of two conductive materials separated by an insulating layer and subject to a relative bias. Starting from an electron in an initial graphene state
i
of wave function
\varphi_{i}({\bf r})
and energy
\hbar\varepsilon_{i}
, the final electron wave function component of energy
\hbar(\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{n})
associated with an additional excitation
n
is given by

\displaystyle\psi_{n,i}({\bf r})=\int d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\;G_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{n})\bra{n}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}({\bf r}^{\prime})\ket{0}{\varphi}_{i}({\bf r}^{\prime})

(S8)

within first-order perturbation in the interaction Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{1}
of matrix elements

\displaystyle\bra{n}\mathcal{H}_{1}({\bf r}^{\prime})\ket{0}=-e\int d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\,\frac{\rho_{n0}({\bf r}^{\prime})}{|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|},

(S9)

sandwiched by the ground and excited states of the heterostructure,
\ket{0}
and
\ket{n}
, respectively. Here,
\rho_{n0}({\bf r})=\bra{n}\hat{\rho}({\bf r})\ket{0}
is the matrix element of the charge density operator
\hat{\rho}({\bf r})=\sum_{j}q_{j}\,\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j})
, incorporating all electrons and nuclei of charges
q_{j}
at positions
{\bf r}_{j}
in the system. The Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{1}
thus describes the Coulomb interaction between such charges and the tunneling electron in a completely general fashion. The details of the structure are additionally captured by the unperturbed Green function
G_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\varepsilon)
, which remains general in this section, but we then specify it for the heterostructure under consideration by using the methods described in Sec.
S5.6.2
.

From the wave functions given by Eq. (
S8
), we obtain the tunneling current density (charge moving downwards and entering the gold surface per unit time and unit area) as

\displaystyle J=\frac{e\hbar}{m_{\rm e}}\sum_{n,i}f_{T}(\hbar\varepsilon_{i})\,\big\{1-f_{T}[\hbar(\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{n})]\big\}\;{\rm Im}\big\{\psi_{n,i}^{*}({\bf r})\partial_{z}\psi_{n,i}({\bf r})\big\}\big|_{z=0^{-}},

(S10)

where we sum over all initial occupied states
i
and material excitations
n
that lead to final unoccupied gold states of energy
\hbar(\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{n})
, as imposed by using the Fermi–Dirac distribution
f_{T}
at temperature
T
. The gold surface is placed at
z=0
, so we evaluate the current right below that plane at
z=0^{-}
in Eq. (
S10
). From Eqs. (
S8
)–(
S10
), we find

\displaystyle J=\frac{e^{3}\hbar}{m_{\rm e}}\sum_{n,i}f_{T}(\hbar\varepsilon_{i})\,\big\{1-f_{T}[\hbar(\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{n})]\big\}\int d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\int d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime\prime}\int d^{3}{\bf r}_{1}\int d^{3}{\bf r}_{2}

(S11)

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\Big\{G_{0}^{*}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{n})\,\partial_{z}G_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime\prime},\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{n})\big|_{z=0^{-}}\;{\varphi}^{*}_{i}({\bf r}^{\prime}){\varphi}_{i}({\bf r}^{\prime\prime})\,\frac{\rho_{0n}({\bf r}_{1})\rho_{n0}({\bf r}_{2})}{|{\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{1}||{\bf r}^{\prime\prime}-{\bf r}_{2}|}\Big\}.

Following a method similar to the derivation of the electron energy-loss probability in electron microscopy (see Ref.
García de Abajo 2010
), this expression can be written in terms of a response function by exploiting the identities
Pines and Noziéres 1966

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\big\{\chi({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)\big\}=-\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\sum_{n}\rho_{0n}({\bf r})\rho_{n0}({\bf r}^{\prime})\,\delta(\omega_{n}-\omega),

(S12a)

\displaystyle W^{\rm ind}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)=\int d^{3}{\bf r}_{1}\int d^{3}{\bf r}_{2}\,\frac{1}{|{\bf r}-{\bf r}_{1}|}\,\chi({\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2},\omega)\,\frac{1}{|{\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{2}|},

(S12b)

where
\chi({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)
is the nonlocal susceptibility and
W^{\rm ind}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)
is the induced part of the screened interaction. The latter is defined as the potential created at a position
{\bf r}
by a time-dependent unit charge of magnitude
{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega t}
placed at
{\bf r}^{\prime}
(see Secs.
S5.7
and
S5.8
for explicit calculations of this quantity in the planar heterostructures under consideration). Using Eqs. (
S12
) to manipulate Eq. (
S11
), we obtain

\displaystyle J=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{3}\hbar^{2}}{\pi m_{\rm e}}\sum_{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega\;f_{T}(\hbar\varepsilon_{i})\,\big\{1-f_{T}[\hbar(\varepsilon_{i}-\omega)]\big\}\int d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\int d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime\prime}\;{\rm Im}\big\{-W({\bf r}^{\prime},{\bf r}^{\prime\prime},\omega)\big\}

(S13)

\displaystyle\times{\rm Im}\Big\{G_{0}^{*}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\varepsilon_{i}-\omega)\,\partial_{z}G_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime\prime},\varepsilon_{i}-\omega)\big|_{z=0^{-}}\;{\varphi}^{*}_{i}({\bf r}^{\prime}){\varphi}_{i}({\bf r}^{\prime\prime})\Big\},

where we have substituted the induced part of the screened interaction by the full interaction because
W({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)-W^{\rm ind}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)=1/|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|
is a real function.

S5.6.4
Indirect exciton-assisted one-step tunneling

We now apply Eq. (
S13
) to discuss one-step transitions from graphene states
i
assisted by the creation of indirect excitons in the TMD layer. In what follows, the sum over
i
is understood to be restricted to occupied graphene states and we set
T=0
, such that
1-f_{T}[\hbar(\varepsilon_{i}-\omega)]=\Theta[\hbar(\varepsilon_{i}-\omega)-{E_{\rm F}^{\rm Au}}]
in expressed in terms of the step function
\Theta
and the gold Fermi energy
{E_{\rm F}^{\rm Au}}
. Accordingly, we rewrite Eq. (
S13
) as

\displaystyle J^{\rm ind-ex}=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{3}\hbar^{2}}{\pi m_{\rm e}}\sum_{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega\;\Theta\big[\hbar(\varepsilon_{i}-\omega)-{E_{\rm F}^{\rm Au}}\big]\int d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\int d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime\prime}\;{\rm Im}\big\{-W({\bf r}^{\prime},{\bf r}^{\prime\prime},\omega)\big\}

(S14)

\displaystyle\times{\rm Im}\Big\{G_{0}^{*}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\varepsilon_{i}-\omega)\,\partial_{z}G_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime\prime},\varepsilon_{i}-\omega)\big|_{z=0^{-}}\;{\varphi}^{*}_{i}({\bf r}^{\prime}){\varphi}_{i}({\bf r}^{\prime\prime})\Big\}.

Momentum mismatch is then considered to be bridged by large wave vectors
{\bf k}_{\parallel}
in the screened interaction, contributed by indirect excitons in the TMD layer.

Assuming isotropy and translational invariance in the in-plane response, one can write the screened interaction as

\displaystyle W({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}_{\parallel}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\bf k}_{\parallel}\cdot({\bf R}-{\bf R}^{\prime})}\,W(k_{\parallel},z,z^{\prime},\omega)

(S15)

in terms of momentum components
W(k_{\parallel},z,z^{\prime},\omega)
. However, for the large values of
{\bf k}_{\parallel}
under consideration, the in-plane atomic lattice can play a substantial role, imprinting a lattice periodicity on the screened interaction, which can be written as

\displaystyle W({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)=\sum_{{\bf G}{\bf G}^{\prime}}\int_{\rm 1BZ}\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}_{\parallel}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}({\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G})\cdot{\bf R}}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}({\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}^{\prime})\cdot{\bf R}^{\prime}}\,W_{{\bf G}{\bf G}^{\prime}}({\bf k}_{\parallel},z,z^{\prime},\omega).

(S16)

This expression, which generalizes Eq. (
S15
) by considering
W_{{\bf G},{\bf G}^{\prime}}({\bf k}_{\parallel},z,z^{\prime},\omega)
components labeled by TMD reciprocal lattice vectors
{\bf G}
and
{\bf G}^{\prime}
and restricting the
{\bf k}_{\parallel}
integral to the 1BZ, is general to describe the linear optical response of a TMD layer including atomic periodicity. In our analysis, we neglect lattice contributions to the Green function as a less relevant effect than in the excitonic optical response of the TMD material.

Upon insertion of Eqs. (
S4
) and (
S16
) into Eq. (
S14
), noticing the plane-wave dependence of the wave functions on in-plane coordinates, we can readily carry out the integrals over
{\bf R}^{\prime}
and
{\bf R}^{\prime\prime}
. Further averaging the current over in-plane positions
{\bf R}
, we obtain

\displaystyle J^{\rm ind-ex}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A}\,\frac{e^{3}\hbar^{2}}{\pi m_{\rm e}}\sum_{i}\sum_{\bf G}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\omega\;\Theta\big[\hbar(\varepsilon_{i}-\omega)-{E_{\rm F}^{\rm Au}}\big]

(S17)

\displaystyle\times\int_{\rm 1BZ}\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}_{\parallel}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int dz^{\prime}\int dz^{\prime\prime}\;{\rm Im}\big\{-W_{{\bf G}{\bf G}}({\bf k}_{\parallel},z^{\prime},z^{\prime\prime},\omega)\big\}

\displaystyle\times\,{\rm Im}\Big\{G_{0}^{*}(0,z^{\prime},\varepsilon_{i}-\omega-\hbar|{\bf Q}_{i}-{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}-{\bf K}_{g}|^{2}/2m_{\rm e})

\displaystyle\quad\;\;\;\times\partial_{z}G_{0}(z,z^{\prime\prime},\varepsilon_{i}-\omega-\hbar|{\bf Q}_{i}-{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}-{\bf K}_{g}|^{2}/2m_{\rm e})\big|_{z=0^{-}}\;\;{\varphi}^{*}_{i}(z^{\prime}){\varphi}_{i}(z^{\prime\prime})\Big\},

where
{\bf K}_{g}
is the wave vector at the graphene K
g
point and we find contributions from different Brillouin zones labeled by
{\bf G}
. We note that the average over
{\bf R}
has eliminated nondiagonal
W_{{\bf G}{\bf G}^{\prime}}
components (
{\bf G}\neq{\bf G}^{\prime}
) from this expression. Incidentally, the rightmost argument of the function
G_{0}(z,z^{\prime},\varepsilon)
in Eq. (
S17

\hbar\varepsilon
relative to the gold CBB, and consequently, the sums over
i
and
{\bf G}
need to be restricted to only yield positive values of such argument.

We proceed by using the fact that the initial graphene states
i
are spatially localized near the
z=d
plane [i.e., the out-of-plane spatial extension of
\varphi_{i}(z)
(
\sim\pm 1
Å
de Vega and García de

relative to the graphene K

{\bf K}_{g}
, and their energies

close to

is then trivially yielding an overall constant. Putting these elements together, we transform Eq. (

\displaystyle J^{\rm ind-ex}\propto

\displaystyle\sum_{\bf G}\int_{0}^{eV_{\rm b}/\hbar}\!\!\!d\omega\int_{\rm 1BZ}d^{2}{\bf k}_{\parallel}\;{\rm Im}\big\{-W_{{\bf G}{\bf G}}({\bf k}_{\parallel},d,d,\omega)\big\}

(S18)

\displaystyle\times\,{\rm Im}\Big\{G_{0}^{*}(0,d,E_{\rm F}^{\rm g}/\hbar-\omega-\hbar|{\bf G}-{\bf K}_{g}-{\bf k}_{\parallel}|^{2}/2m_{\rm e})

\displaystyle\quad\;\;\;\times\partial_{z}G_{0}(z,d,E_{\rm F}^{\rm g}/\hbar-\omega-\hbar|{\bf G}-{\bf K}_{g}-{\bf k}_{\parallel}|^{2}/2m_{\rm e})\big|_{z=0^{-}}\Big\}.

Finally, we argue that

is a strongly decreasing function of
\varepsilon
, and therefore, the lowest values of the argument

in Eq. (

for each reciprocal lattice vector

{\bf K}_{g}

\displaystyle J^{\rm ind-ex}\propto

\displaystyle\sum_{\bf G}\int_{\rm 1BZ}d^{2}{\bf k}_{\parallel}\;{\rm Im}\big\{-W_{{\bf G}{\bf G}}({\bf k}_{\parallel},d,d,eV_{\rm b}/\hbar)\big\}\;\int d\varphi_{{\bf K}_{g}}\;\delta({\bf G}-{\bf K}_{g}-{\bf k}_{\parallel})

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\sum_{\bf G}\int_{\rm 1BZ}d^{2}{\bf k}_{\parallel}\;{\rm Im}\big\{-W_{{\bf G}{\bf G}}({\bf k}_{\parallel},d,d,eV_{\rm b}/\hbar)\big\}\;\delta\big(K_{g}-|{\bf G}-{\bf k}_{\parallel}|\big).

(S19)

S5.6.5
Two-step tunneling assisted by phonon and exciton creation

We now analyze two-step processes involving phonon excitation followed by the creation of nearly direct excitons as an additional channel contributing to electron tunneling from graphene to gold and also producing an excitonic signature in the
dI/dV
curves.

Quasi-elastic phonon-assisted transitions.
The phonon energies at the K point are large compared with
{k_{\rm B}}T
at room temperature
T
, and therefore, we can safely neglect phonon absorption processes. Starting from a graphene state
i
(see Sec.
S5.6.2
), within first-order perturbation theory, the excitation of a phonon
p
of wave vector
{\bf K}_{g}+{\bf Q}_{p}
(i.e., with
{\bf Q}_{i}
relative to the graphene K
g
point) and frequency
\omega_{p}
contributes with an electron wave function
\tilde{\varphi}_{ip}({\bf r})=\varphi_{i}(z)\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}({\bf Q}_{i}-{\bf Q}_{p})\cdot{\bf R}}/\sqrt{A}
of energy
\hbar(\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{p})
, where

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{ip}({\bf r})=\int d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}G_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{p})\,\mathcal{H}_{p}^{\rm ph}({\bf r}^{\prime})\,\varphi_{i}({\bf r}^{\prime})

(S22)

is expressed in terms of the electron-phonon coupling Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{\rm ph}_{p}({\bf r}^{\prime})
and the 3D electron Green function
G_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\varepsilon)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int d^{2}{\bf Q}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\bf Q}\cdot({\bf R}-{\bf R}^{\prime})}\,G_{0}(z,z^{\prime},\varepsilon-\hbar Q^{2}/2m_{\rm e})
[see Sec.
S5.6.2
for details of this function and the definitions of
\varphi_{i}(z)
,
{\bf Q}_{i}
, and
A
]. Separating the in- and out-of-plane dependence of the phonon Hamiltonian as
\mathcal{H}^{\rm ph}_{p}({\bf r}^{\prime})=\mathcal{H}^{\rm ph}_{p}(z^{\prime}){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}({\bf K}_{g}+{\bf Q}_{p})\cdot{\bf R}}
, we can recast Eq. (
S22
) into

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{ip}(z)=\int dz^{\prime}\;

\displaystyle G_{0}\big(z,z^{\prime},\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{p}-\hbar|{\bf Q}_{i}-{\bf Q}_{p}|^{2}/2m_{\rm e}\big)

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{H}_{p}^{\rm ph}(z^{\prime})\,\varphi_{i}(z^{\prime}),

and noticing again that
\varphi_{i}(z)
is tightly localized around
z=d
, we can approximate this expression as

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{ip}(z)\approx C_{p}\,G_{0}\big(z,d,\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{p}-\hbar|{\bf Q}_{i}-{\bf Q}_{p}|^{2}/2m_{\rm e}\big),

(S23)

where the constant
C_{p}
depends on the specific phonon mode
p
under consideration. In addition, as tunneling is expected to involve large phonon momentum transfers that place the electron near the
{\rm\Gamma}
point, only a reduced region in the phonon bands contributes to the process, and therefore, we assume
C_{1}\equiv|C_{p}|
to be roughly independent of
p
. The tunneling current per unit area traversing the hBN/Au interface is finally obtained as

\displaystyle J^{\rm ph}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e\hbar}{m_{\rm e}}\sum_{ip}\Theta\big[\hbar(\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{p})-{E_{\rm F}^{\rm Au}}\big]\;{\rm Im}\big\{\tilde{\varphi}_{ip}^{*}({\bf r})\partial_{z}\tilde{\varphi}_{ip}({\bf r})\big\}\big|_{z=0^{-}}

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{C_{1}^{2}}{A}\,\frac{e\hbar}{m_{\rm e}}\sum_{ip}\Theta\big[\hbar(\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{p})-{E_{\rm F}^{\rm Au}}\big]\;{\rm Im}\Big\{G_{0}^{*}\big(z,d,\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{p}-\hbar|{\bf Q}_{i}-{\bf Q}_{p}|^{2}/2m_{\rm e}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\times\partial_{z}G_{0}\big(z,d,\varepsilon_{i}-\omega_{p}-\hbar|{\bf Q}_{i}-{\bf Q}_{p}|^{2}/2m_{\rm e}\big)\Big\}\Big|_{z=0^{-}},

which is evaluated at a position
z=0^{-}
right inside the metal (see Fig.
S15
and Sec.
S5.6.3
), with the step function restricting the sum over
i
and
p
such that only final unoccupied electron gold states above the Fermi level
{E_{\rm F}^{\rm Au}}
are included.

Phonon+exciton-assisted tunneling.
In the two-step channel, after a phonon has been created, a direct exciton absorbs most of the energy lost by the tunneling electron. This second step is described by Eq. (
S13
) in Sec.
S5.6.3
, with
\varphi_{i}
substituted by the phonon-scattered wave function
\tilde{\varphi}_{ip}
given in Eq. (
S23

S5.7
Screened interaction under the in-plane isotropic and translational-invariant approximations

Tunneling electrons couple to excitations in the structure through the screened interaction
W({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)
. Because the distances involved are small compared with the optical wavelength at frequency
\omega
, we can safely neglect retardation effects and treat the interaction in the electrostatic limit. The structure under consideration is sketched in Fig.
S19
a and consists of a layer of 2D materials separated by a distance
d
from a gold surface. The region in between the two of them is filled with hBN, and the medium above the 2D materials is also hBN.

The layer of 2D materials here considered consists of a TMD monolayer on top of graphene. We describe it in the zero-thickness approximation through a nonlocal wave-vector- and frequency-dependent surface conductivity
\sigma(k_{\parallel},\omega)
. This approximation simplifies the algebra considerably and yields reasonable results in comparison to finite-thickness layers
Rodríguez Echarri et al. 2019
. We thus set
\sigma(k_{\parallel},\omega)=\sigma_{\rm TMD}(k_{\parallel},\omega)+\sigma_{\rm g}(k_{\parallel},\omega)
as the sum of the surface conductivities of the TMD and graphene monolayers. A nonlocal version of the former is calculated from first principles (see Sec.
S5.9
), whereas the nonlocal conductivity of graphene is modeled in the RPA
Wunsch et al. 2006
.

(

The interfaces in our structure are described by first defining Fresnel’s reflection coefficients, as shown in Fig.
S19
b,c. More precisely, only p-polarization coefficients survive in the electrostatic limit. For convenience, we define in-plane coordinates
{\bf R}=(x,y)
and work in the space of in-plane wave vectors
{\bf k}_{\parallel}=(k_{x},k_{y})
, with electrostatic quantities such as the electric potential written as
\phi({\bf r},\omega)=(2\pi)^{-2}\int d^{2}{\bf k}_{\parallel}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\bf k}_{\parallel}\cdot{\bf R}}\,\phi({\bf k}_{\parallel},z,\omega)
.

We start by considering the electrostatic potential created by a point charge of time-dependent magnitude
{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega t}
placed at the origin in bulk hBN. The momentum components of such potential are given by
[2\pi/k_{\parallel}\bar{\epsilon}(\omega)]\,{\rm e}^{-q|z|}
, where
\bar{\epsilon}(\omega)=\sqrt{\epsilon_{x}(\omega)\epsilon_{z}(\omega)}
and
q=k_{\parallel}\sqrt{\epsilon_{x}(\omega)/\epsilon_{z}(\omega)}
, with the square root sign chosen to yield positive real parts. Now, with the 2D material layer placed at
z=d
, scattering of an
incident
potential component
{\rm e}^{-q(z-d)}
coming from the
z<d
region gives rise to a
transmitted
potential
t_{\rm 2D}{\rm e}^{-q(z-d)}
at
z>d
and an
incident
+
reflected

{\rm e}^{-q(z-d)}-r_{\rm 2D}{\rm e}^{q(z-d)}
at
z<d
. (Incidentally, note that the reflection coefficient is preceded by a
-
sign in this expression for the potential
Rodríguez Echarri et al. 2019
.) The corresponding Fresnel coefficients are determined by imposing the continuity of the potential at
z=d
, as well as the condition that the jump in the normal electric displacement is
4\pi
times the surface charge produced through the conductivity
\sigma(k_{\parallel},\omega)
. Writing such charge in terms of the current through the continuity equation, and this in turn as the conductivity times the in-plane electric field, we are left with a set of two linear equations from which we obtain

\displaystyle r_{\rm 2D}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-{\rm i}\omega\bar{\epsilon}(\omega)/[2\pi k_{\parallel}\sigma(k_{\parallel},\omega)]},

(S29a)

\displaystyle t_{\rm 2D}

\displaystyle=1-r_{\rm 2D}

(S29b)

for the Fresnel coefficients of the 2D layer, which generalize previous results
García de Abajo 2014
to deal with an anisotropic host medium. We note that the
z
dependence of the potential components inside hBN is simply given by
{\rm e}^{\mp q(z-d)}
for upward (
-
sign) and downward (
+
sign) components (see Fig.
S19
b).

Given the small thickness of the hBN tunnel layer, nonlocal effects in the response of gold can play an important role, and therefore, we use the nonlocal bulk permittivity of this material
\epsilon_{\rm Au}(k,\omega)
. As a reasonably accurate prescription, we express
\epsilon_{\rm Au}(k,\omega)
as the sum of a conduction-electron component, treated in the RPA, and a local contribution associated with interband transitions, as described in detail elsewhere
García de Abajo 2008
. In addition, we adopt the specular-reflection model
Ritchie and Marusak 1966
;
García de Abajo 2008
;
García de Abajo 2010
to incorporate surface effects through a surface response function

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rm s}(k_{\parallel},z,\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{\parallel}}{\pi}\int\frac{dk_{z}}{k^{2}}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{z}z}}{\epsilon_{\rm Au}(k,\omega)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2k_{\parallel}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk_{z}}{k^{2}}\frac{\cos(k_{z}z)}{\epsilon_{\rm Au}(k,\omega)},

where

. Incidentally, this quantity becomes

\displaystyle r_{\rm Au,BN}

\displaystyle=-r_{\rm BN,Au}=\frac{\bar{\epsilon}(\omega)\epsilon_{\rm s}(k_{\parallel},\omega)-1}{\bar{\epsilon}(\omega)\epsilon_{\rm s}(k_{\parallel},\omega)+1},

(S30a)

\displaystyle t_{\rm Au,BN}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\bar{\epsilon}(\omega)\epsilon_{\rm s}(k_{\parallel},\omega)+1},

(S30b)

\displaystyle t_{\rm BN,Au}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\,\bar{\epsilon}(\omega)\epsilon_{\rm s}(k_{\parallel},\omega)}{\bar{\epsilon}(\omega)\epsilon_{\rm s}(k_{\parallel},\omega)+1}

(in operator notation), which reduces to
{\rm e}^{-k_{\parallel}|z|}
in
(k_{\parallel},z)

\epsilon_{\rm s}(k_{\parallel},z,\omega)/\epsilon_{\rm s}(k_{\parallel},\omega)

García de Abajo 2010

S5.8
Screened interaction including the atomic lattice periodicity

A more rigorous description of the screened interaction incorporating the in-plane atomic corrugation of the TMD layer may become necessary when large wave vectors are involved, such as in the current experiment (i.e., for momentum transfers
\sim{\rm\Gamma}
K
g

S5.6.4

S18

S16

We obtain

{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega t}
placed at
{\bf r}^{\prime}
with

taken right above the 2D material layer. Using the notation and methods of Sec.
S5.7
, the direct potential in an infinite hBN host medium can be written in a form analogous to Eq. (
S16

\displaystyle\phi^{\rm dir}({\bf r},\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf G}}\int_{\rm 1BZ}\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}_{\parallel}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}({\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G})\cdot{\bf R}}\phi^{\rm dir}_{\bf G}({\bf k}_{\parallel},z,\omega),

where

\displaystyle\phi^{\rm dir}_{\bf G}({\bf k}_{\parallel},z,\omega)=\frac{2\pi}{|{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}|\bar{\epsilon}}\,{\rm e}^{-q_{\bf G}|z-d|}

and we define

q_{\bf G}=|{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}|\sqrt{\epsilon_{x}(\omega)/\epsilon_{z}(\omega)}
similar to

in Sec.
S5.7
, but with
k_{\parallel}
substituted by
|{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}|
. We now substitute the reflection and transmission coefficients

and

of the 2D material layer by reflection and transmission tensors of components
r_{{\rm 2D},{\bf G}{\bf G}^{\prime}}
and
t_{{\rm 2D},{\bf G}{\bf G}^{\prime}}

{\bf G}^{\prime}
) and reflected/transmitted (
{\bf G}
) reciprocal lattice vectors (see below). In addition, the reflection coefficient at the BN–Au interface is still described by Eq. (
S30a

{\bf k}_{\parallel}

{\bf G}

\displaystyle W_{{\bf G}{\bf G}^{\prime}}({\bf k}_{\parallel},d,d,\omega)=\frac{2\pi}{|{\bf k}_{\parallel}+{\bf G}^{\prime}|\bar{\epsilon}}\bigg[\delta_{{\bf G}{\bf G}^{\prime}}-r_{{\rm 2D},{\bf G}{\bf G}^{\prime}}

(S34)

\displaystyle-\!\!\sum_{{\bf G}_{1}{\bf G}_{2}}\!t_{{\rm 2D},{\bf G}{\bf G}_{2}}\,{\rm e}^{-q_{{\bf G}_{2}}d}\,\Delta_{{\bf G}_{2}{\bf G}_{1}}\,r_{{\rm BN,Au},{\bf G}_{1}}\,{\rm e}^{-q_{{\bf G}_{1}}d}\,t_{{\rm 2D},{\bf G}_{1}{\bf G}^{\prime}}\bigg],

S5.9
First-principles nonlocal optical response of TMD monolayers

The exciton-dominated nonlocal optical conductivities of monolayer TMDs constitute a central ingredient in our theory. We perform first-principles calculations in the framework of many-body perturbation theory to appropriately describe the excited-state properties of the considered materials. The ground-state wave functions and one-electron eigenenergies are obtained by performing density-functional-theory (DFT) calculations with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)
Perdew et al. 1996
exchange–correlation functional as implemented in the Quantum Espresso code
Giannozzi et al. 2009
. In addition, the spin-orbit interaction is included using optimized norm-conserving pseudopotentials
Hamann 2013
;
Schlipf and Gygi 2015
with a kinetic energy cutoff of 80 Ry. We employ the Coulomb-cutoff technique
Rozzi et al. 2006
at the edges of the unit cells in the out-of-plane direction with a vacuum layer of 37 atomic units.

Using the DFT output, we perform Bethe–Salpeter-equation (BSE) calculations within the YAMBO code
Marini et al. 2009
;
Sangalli et al. 2019

W
0

Hedin 1965
;
Onida et al. 2002
