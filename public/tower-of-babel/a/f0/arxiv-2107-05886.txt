Abstract
We study the power of the bounded-width consistency algorithm in the context of the fixed-template Promise Constraint Satisfaction Problem (PCSP). Our main technical finding is that the template of every PCSP that is solvable in bounded width satisfies a certain structural condition implying that its algebraic closure-properties include weak near unanimity polymorphisms of all large arities. While this parallels the standard (non-promise) CSP theory, the method of proof is quite different and applies even to the regime of sublinear width. We also show that, in contrast with the CSP world, the presence of weak near unanimity polymorphisms of all large arities does not guarantee solvability in bounded width. The separating example is even solvable in the second level of the Sherali-Adams (SA) hierarchy of linear programming relaxations. This shows that, unlike for CSPs, linear programming can be stronger than bounded width. A direct application of these methods also show that the problem of q q -coloring p p -colorable graphs is not solvable in bounded or even sublinear width, for any two constants p p and q q such that 3 ≤ p ≤ q 3\leq p\leq q . Turning to algorithms, we note that Wigderson’s algorithm for O ⁡ ( n ) O(\sqrt{n}) -coloring 3 3 -colorable graphs with n n vertices is implementable in width 4 4 . Indeed, by generalizing the method we see that, for any ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 smaller than 1 / 2 1/2 , the optimal width for solving the problem of O ⁡ ( n ϵ ) O(n^{\epsilon}) -coloring 3 3 -colorable graphs with n n vertices lies between n 1 − 3 ​ ϵ n^{1-3\epsilon} and n 1 − 2 ​ ϵ n^{1-2\epsilon} . The upper bound gives a simple 2 Θ ⁡ ( n 1 − 2 ​ ϵ ​ log ⁡ ( n ) ) 2^{\Theta(n^{1-2\epsilon}\log(n))} -time algorithm that, asymptotically, beats the straightforward 2 Θ ⁡ ( n 1 − ϵ ) 2^{\Theta(n^{1-\epsilon})} bound that follows from partitioning the graph into O ⁡ ( n ϵ ) O(n^{\epsilon}) many independent parts each of size O ⁡ ( n 1 − ϵ ) O(n^{1-\epsilon}) .

Introduction

The input to the constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) is a set of
variables, each ranging over a specified domain of values, as well as
a set of constraints, each binding a finite set of variables to take
values in a relation from a specified set of relations. The problem
asks to find an assignment of values to the variables in such a way
that all the constraints are satisfied. It was first pointed out by
Feder and Vardi
[
28
]
that the CSP can be modelled as
the homomorphism problem for relational structures. In this view, the
input to the CSP is a pair of relational structures, the

instance

\operatorname{{\bf I}}
and the
constraint language

\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, and we
are asked to find a homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
; i.e., a map from
the domain of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to the domain of
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
in such a way that all the
relations are preserved. In the
fixed-template
variant of the
problem, the constraint language is fixed and part of the definition
of the problem, and the instance is the only input. The fixed-template
CSP with template
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
is denoted by
\operatorname{CSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}})
.

As confirmed in retrospect, one of the most important problems raised
by the seminal work of Feder and Vardi was that of characterizing the
class of fixed-template CSPs of
bounded width
. In short, these
are the fixed-template CSPs whose instances are always correctly
solved by the so-called consistency algorithm with a fixed bound on
its
width
. This class of CSPs was later studied in depth in the
work of Kolaitis and
Vardi
[
37
,
36
]
, where it was shown
to constitute a robust fragment of the class of polynomial-time
solvable CSPs that admits several equivalent reformulations.

After two decades of work in the area, the question of fully
characterizing the CSPs of bounded-width was eventually answered in
the work of Barto and Kozik
[
9
]
. Besides resolving one of
the key problems in
[
28
]
, the Barto-Kozik Theorem
produced many important new insights for the theory of CSPs
itself. This line of work eventually led to the
proof of the celebrated Feder-Vardi Dichotomy Conjecture, due to
Zhuk
[
51
]
and, independently,
Bulatov
[
18
]
. This completed the program started by
Feder and Vardi
[
28
]
, Jeavons, Cohen, and
Gyssens
[
33
]
, and Bulatov, Jeavons and
Krokhin
[
19
]
, that aimed to characterize
the class of all polynomial-time solvable fixed-template CSPs by the
algebraic closure properties of their templates. By today, it is fair
to say that almost all important questions raised by the early work of
Feder and Vardi for the fixed-template CSP seem to have been resolved.

Recently, Brakensiek and Guruswami
[
12
]
have put
forward a generalization of the fixed-template CSP as a natural next
step in the development of the theory. In the
Fixed Template
Promise Constraint Satisfaction Problem
(PCSP) the template is a
fixed pair
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
of relational structures such that there is a
homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
. The problem has two variants:

Search variant of
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
: The instance is a relational
structure
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
with the promise that there is a homomorphism
from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}

and we are asked to find a homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
.

Decision variant of
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
: The instance is a
relational structure
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
and we are asked to distinguish the
case in which there is a homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
from the
case in which there is not even a homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}

to
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
. For this variant the promise is that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
fullfils one
of these two conditions.

To motivate the generalization, it is useful to look at some special
cases. First, it is obvious that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
is just the same
problem as
\operatorname{CSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}})
, hence any fixed-template CSP is a
fixed-template PCSP. Second, consider the following graph-coloring
problem. We are given a graph
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
with the promise that it has an
unknown proper
p
-coloring for a certain number of colors
p
, and we
are asked to find a proper
q
-coloring for a fixed put potentially
larger target number of colors
q
. By viewing the
proper
q
-colorings of
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
as the homomorphisms into the complete
graph
\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q}
with
q
vertices,
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q})
models the
problem of approximating the chromatic number of a graph. We refer to
this as the Approximate Graph Coloring Problem with parameters
p

and
q
. The special case with
p=q
is exactly the same
as
\operatorname{CSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q})
, the standard Graph
q
-Coloring Problem for
undirected graphs, which, for
q\geq 3
, is one of the twenty-one
NP-complete problems of Karp
[
34
]
. The problem
of 4-coloring 3-colorable graphs,
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{3},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{4})
, was shown
NP-hard in the early 1990’s, using the theory of probabilistically
checkable proofs (PCP)
[
35
]
. Not until two decades later
has it been shown that the problem of 5-coloring 3-colorable graphs is
NP-hard, and, more generally, that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{2p-1})
is
NP-hard for any
p\geq 3

[
8
]
. The problem of exactly
identifying the pairs
(p,q)
with
p\leq q
for
which
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q})
is NP-hard is one of the leading open
problems in the area, with deep connections with the theories of PCPs
and hardness of approximation
[
43
]
, and the notorious Unique
Games Conjecture
[
27
]
.

The theory of Promise PCSP has been further developped (see

[
6
,
7
,
8
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
29
,
39
,
48
]

for example) and applied to other problems beyond graph coloring. The
goal of this paper is to initiate a study of the power of the
consistency algorithm for the fixed-template PCSP. Following

[
8
]
, in this new context, the consistency algorithm can be
thought of in the following terms. Fix a PCSP template
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})

and an integer
k\geq 1
. We say that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is

solvable in width
k
if, for every instance
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
, it holds
that if
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
is
k
-consistent with respect to the
left-template
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, then there is a homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to the
right-template
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
. Here, as in the standard consistency algorithm
for the standard CSP, an instance
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
is said to
be
k
-consistent with respect to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
if the algorithm that
checks if the set of all subinstances of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
with at most
k

elements admits a system of compatible homomorphisms into
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
does
not find a blatant contradiction. For each fixed integer
k
, this
algorithm runs in polynomial time. When
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is solvable
in width
k
we also say that the template
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})

has

width
k
.

A key insight from the theory of CSPs is that the computational
complexity of a fixed-template CSP is governed by the algebraic
structure of the polymorphisms of its
template
[
33
]
. This phenomenon led to the so-called

algebraic approach
to CSPs, of which the aforementioned
Barto-Kozik, Zhuk, and Bulatov Theorems are prime examples. Indeed,
the governing power of polymorphisms is so general that the phenomenon
has been observed to hold for other ways of measuring the complexity
of the problem. In particular, it applies to the analysis of bounded
width
[
42
]
, to the more general settings of descriptive
complexity
[
1
]
and propositional proof
complexity
[
3
]
, and even to different variants of
the CSP itself. For PCSPs, a suitable definition of polymorphisms has
been put forward to show that the computational and width complexity
of a PCSP is, again, governed by the polymorphisms of its
template
[
8
]
.

A natural question at this point is whether the analogue of the
Barto-Kozik Theorem holds for PCSPs. In the language of polymorphisms,
the Barto-Kozik Theorem for CSPs can be stated as the equivalence of
the following two statements:

(a)

\operatorname{CSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}})
is solvable in some bounded width,

(b)

\operatorname{CSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}})
admits weak near unanimity (WNU) polymorphisms of all large arities.

In definition, this last condition means that, for any large enough
integer
m
, there exists a homomorphism
f:\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{m}\to\operatorname{{\bf S}}
from
the
m
-power
\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{m}
into
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
that satisfies the
weak near
unanimity
(WNU) identities:

f(y,x,x,\ldots,x)=f(x,y,x,\ldots,x)=\cdots=f(x,\ldots,x,x,y),

(1)

for all choices of
x
and
y
in the domain of
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
.

Turning to PCSP, for a fixed template
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
, consider the
following statements:

(a’)

\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is solvable in some bounded width,

(b’)

\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
admits WNU polymorphisms of all large arities.

Following
[
8
]
, a WNU polymorphism of
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is a
homomorphism
f:\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{m}\to\operatorname{{\bf T}}
from the
m
-power
\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{m}
of the
left-template
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
into the right-template
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
that satisfies
Equation (
1
) for all
x
and
y
in the domain of
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
.

The first technically novel result of this paper is that (b’) is a

necessary
but
not sufficient
condition for (a’):

Main Result
: (a’)
\Longrightarrow
(b’)
\not\Longrightarrow
(a’).

To prove that (a’)
\Longrightarrow
(b’) we first establish a
structural result about the template that may be of independent
interest. We show that any template
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
whose PCSP is solvable
in some bounded width satisfies the following property: either a
proper subset of
S\times S
can be obtained by composing two binary
projections of the relations in
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, where
S
is the domain
of
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, or else the problem is trivial because
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
contains a
reflexive tuple and then
any
instance admits a homomorphism
to
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
. In its proof we need to resort to a probabilistic
construction of a large instance
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
that is
sparse enough
to
be
k
-consistent with respect to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, for any fixed
k
, but

dense enough
to not admit a homomomorphisms into
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
.

The reasoning that goes into the analysis of the probabilistic
construction of this instance is reminiscent of the methods for
proving lower bounds for Resolution in propositional proof complexity,
going back to the influential work of Chvátal and
Szeméredi
[
23
]
and posterior follow-ups
(particularly,
[
10
]

and
[
44
]
). Indeed, as in these related works,
our analysis shows that the random instance
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
is
k
-consistent
with respect to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
for
k
as large as
\epsilon n
, where
n
is
the number of elements in
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
, and
\epsilon
is a fixed positive
constant that depends only on
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
and
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
. This means that the
necessary condition (b’) applies also to all PCSPs that are solvable
in
sublinear width
; i.e., in width
k=k(n)=o(n)
, where
n

is the number of elements in the instance. It should be pointed out
that, for standard CSPs, it was already known that the Barto-Kozik
Theorem can also be strengthened to show that a fixed-template CSP is
solvable in bounded width if and only if it is solvable in sublinear
width (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
).

To prove that (b’)
\not\Longrightarrow
(a’), we analyze the
polymorphisms of a specific
Boolean
PCSP template, i.e., one
with a two-element domain
\{0,1\}
,
namely
(2\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}4\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT},4\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{NAE}\text{-}\mathrm{SAT}})
. We first show that this template
admits WNU polymorphisms of all large arities, and then apply the
structural result of the previous paragraph to conclude that it is not
solvable in bounded width. This analysis also led us to conclude that,
for any two integers
s
and
r
such that
0<s<r
and
r>2
,
the Boolean
\operatorname{PCSP}(s\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}r\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT},r\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{NAE}\text{-}\mathrm{SAT}})
is not solvable in bounded
width, but is solvable in the second level of the Sherali-Adams
hierarchy applied to its basic linear programming relaxation. This is
in sharp constrast with the status for standard CSPs for which, as is
known, the fixed-template CSPs that are solvable in bounded-width and
those that are solvable in some fixed-level of the Sherali-Adams
hierarchy coincide (this follows, e.g., from the Barto-Kozik Theorem
combined with the results in
[
1
]

and
[
2
]
). To show that
\operatorname{PCSP}(s\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}r\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT},r\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{NAE}\text{-}\mathrm{SAT}})

can be solved in the second level of the Sherali-Adams hierarchy we
build on the recent results on Boolean PCSPs from

[
12
]
.

As a corollary to the aforementioned structural result we obtain a
complete classification of the Approximate Graph Coloring Problems
that are solvable in bounded width. We show that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q})

is not solvable in bounded width for
any
two constants
p

and
q
, such that
p\leq q
, unless
p=1
or
p=2
.

Corollary 1
.

For any two integers
p
and
q
such
that
1\leq p\leq q
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q})
is solvable by the consistency algorithm in width
3
,

(b)

\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q})
is solvable by the consistency algorithm in bounded width,

(c)

\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q})
is solvable by the consistency algorithm in sublinear width,

(d)

p=1
or
p=2
.

We note that this classification agrees with the one
predicted by the NP-hardness results that would follow from certain
variants of the Unique Games Conjecture
[
27
]
, but ours is
unconditional.

Turning to upper bounds for the Approximate Graph Coloring Problem, we
observe that the well-known algorithm due to Wigderson

[
47
]
that properly colors any 3-colorable graph
with
n
vertices with

colors is implementable in
width
4
. We generalize Wigderson’s algorithm to show that, for any

, the problem of

-colorable from non-

-colorable graphs

. This leads
to a simple algorithm that properly colors any
3
-colorable graph
with
O(n^{\epsilon})
colors in
time
2^{\Theta(n^{1-2\epsilon}\log(n))}
. Asymptotically, this beats
the straightforward

time-bound that
follows from partitioning the graph into
O(n^{\epsilon})
many
independent parts each of size
O(n^{1-\epsilon})
. As a nearly
matching lower bound, we show that the same problem cannot be solved
in width less than

. The problem of closing the gap
between the
1-3\epsilon
in the lower bound and the

the upper bound is left as an intriguing open problem.

Homomorphisms, CSPs and PCSPs

Let
\sigma
be a signature and let
\operatorname{{\bf A}}
and
\operatorname{{\bf B}}
be
\sigma

with domains

and
B

, where

\operatorname{{\bf a}}\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf A}}}

. We shall
use
\operatorname{{\bf A}}\rightarrow\operatorname{{\bf B}}
to denote the statement that there exists an

\operatorname{{\bf A}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf B}}
.

We write
\operatorname{CSP}(\operatorname{{\bf A}})
to denote the following computational problem:

given an input
\sigma
-structure
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
, decide
whether
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\rightarrow\operatorname{{\bf A}}
or
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\not\rightarrow\operatorname{{\bf A}}
. The

\operatorname{{\bf A}}
is the

of the

\operatorname{CSP}(\operatorname{{\bf A}})
. If

, we

to denote the following computational promise

given an input
\sigma
-structure
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
, distinguish the

,

The pair of structures
(\operatorname{{\bf A}},\operatorname{{\bf B}})
is the

of the

. Since it is assumed

, note that either
\operatorname{{\bf A}}
is the empty
structure, or
\operatorname{{\bf B}}
cannot be trivial in the sense that not all the

\operatorname{{\bf B}}
can be empty. Note also that the definition of the

\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf A}},\operatorname{{\bf B}})
assumes that the input structure
\operatorname{{\bf I}}

to denote

\sigma

Relaxations for (P)CSP

A common heuristic method to solve an instance
\operatorname{{\bf I}}

of
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
consists in solving a polynomial-time solvable
relaxation of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\rightarrow\operatorname{{\bf S}}
. If the relaxation turns out to be
not feasible, then we can infer for sure
that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\not\rightarrow\operatorname{{\bf S}}
. Although the converse is not necessarily
true, for some templates
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
it can be guaranteed that, if the
relaxation is feasible, then
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\rightarrow\operatorname{{\bf T}}
. For such templates,
the heurisitic method is a valid polynomial-time algorithm
for
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
. In the present paper we will focus mostly on
relaxations based on local consistency.

3.1
Local consistency algorithm

Fix a signature
\sigma
. Let
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
be an instance with domain
I
,
let
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
be a constraint language with domain
S
, and let
k
be a
positive integer. A mapping
f:X\rightarrow S
with
X\subseteq I
is
a
partial
homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
if it is an homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}{|_{X}}

to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
. A

k
-strategy on
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
and
\operatorname{{\bf S}}

[
38
]
is any nonempty collection
\mathscr{H}
of partial
homomorphisms from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
such that:

1.

the family
\mathscr{H}
it is closed under restrictions, i.e.,
for every
h
in
\mathscr{H}
and every
X\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(h)
, the
restriction
h|_{X}
of
h
to
X
is in
\mathscr{H}
,

2.

the family
\mathscr{H}
has the extension property up to
k
,
i.e., for every
h
in
\mathscr{H}
with
|\operatorname{dom}(h)|<k
and
every
x\in I\setminus\operatorname{dom}(h)
, there exists
f
in
\mathscr{H}

such that
h\subseteq f
and
\operatorname{dom}(f)=\operatorname{dom}(h)\cup\{x\}
.

We write
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{k}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
to denote the statement that there exists
a
k
-strategy on
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
and
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
. It follows directly from
the definitions that, if
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\to\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, then
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{k}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
. Indeed,
if
h
is a homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, then the
collection
\mathscr{H}=\{h|_{X}\mid X\subseteq I,|X|\leq k\}
is
a
k
-strategy on
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
and
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
.

There is an algorithm that, given
(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}},k)
as input, decides
whether there exists a
k
-strategy and does so in time polynomial
in
(|I|+|S|)^{k}
. This algorithm, usually called
the
(k-1)
-consistency algorithm, starts by placing in
\mathscr{H}

all partial homomorphisms from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
with domain size at
most
k
and repeatedly removes from
\mathscr{H}
those
h
that
falsify any one of the two conditions in the definition
of
k
-strategy. The algorithm stops when it reaches a fixed-point. If
the fixed-point obtained is non-empty, then it necessarily is
a
k
-strategy. Otherwise, it can be safely concluded that
no
k
-strategy exists. Since, as seen above,
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\to\operatorname{{\bf S}}

implies
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{k}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, one could use the consistency algorithm as a
partial-check to decide whether
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
is homomorphic to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
. This
is the basis of the
width heuristic
for CSP which, following

[
8
]
, we now extend to PCSPs.

Let
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
be a PCSP template of signature
\sigma
; i.e.,
\operatorname{{\bf S}}

and
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
are
\sigma
-structures such that
\operatorname{{\bf S}}\to\operatorname{{\bf T}}
.
Let
k=k(n)
be an integer function. We say that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})

is solvable in width
k(n)
, or that the template
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})

has width
k(n)
, if for every
\sigma
-structure instance
\operatorname{{\bf I}}

with
n
elements it holds that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{k(n)}\operatorname{{\bf S}}

implies
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\to\operatorname{{\bf T}}
. Note that this generalizes the definition of
width for CSPs since, whenever
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})=\operatorname{CSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}})
is
solvable in width
k(n)
, the condition
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{k(n)}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
is
necessary
and
sufficient for
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\to\operatorname{{\bf S}}
. We note that
every
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is solvable in width at most
k(n)

for
k(n)=n
. Whenever it is solvable in width
k(n)
for
some
k(n)=O(1)
, we say that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is solvable
in
bounded width
, or that
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})

has
bounded
width. Whenever it is solvable in width
k(n)
for some
k(n)=o(n)
,
we say that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is solvable in
sublinear width
, or
that
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})

has
sublinear width.

In the particular case of CSPs, the strength of bounded width is well
understood. The breakthrough of Barto and Kozik
[
9
]
, in
combination with
[
5
]
, yields the following
characterization of CSP templates of bounded width:

Theorem 1
.

[
9
,
5
]

For every structure
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
the following are equivalent:

1.

\operatorname{{\bf T}}
has bounded width,

2.

\operatorname{{\bf T}}
has width
\max(r,3)
where
r
is the maximum arity in
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
,

3.

\operatorname{Pol}(\operatorname{{\bf T}})
contains an
m
-ary WNU for every integer
m\geq 3
.

We illustrate Theorem
1
with an example that will
become useful in Section
6
.

Example 2
.

Consider the problem of deciding whether a graph
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}

is
2
-colorable, i.e,
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbf{K}_{2})
. If
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
contains a
cycle
a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n}=a_{0}
of odd length
n
,
then there is no
k
-strategy on
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
and
\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{2}
: an easy
inductive argument shows that every such strategy would need to
contain for every
j\geq 1
a partial homomorphism
f

with domain
\{a_{0},a_{j}\}
satisfying
f(a_{0})=f(a_{j})

if
j
is even and
f(a_{0})\neq f(a_{j})
if
j
is odd, which is not
possible because
n
is odd and
a_{n}=a_{0}
.
Consequently,
\mathbf{K}_{2}
has width
3
.

One can alternatively look at this algebraically and note that, for
every integer
m\geq 3
, the set
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathbf{K}_{2})
of polymorphisms
of
\mathbf{K}_{2}
contains the function
\varphi:[2]^{m}\rightarrow[2]

that returns the majority of its arguments. These are WNUs and,
therefore, by virtue of Theorem
1
, it follows also
that
\mathbf{K}_{2}
has width
3
. Indeed, it was already observed
in
[
28
]
that a structure
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
has bounded width
whenever
\operatorname{Pol}(\operatorname{{\bf T}})
contains a majority operation
f
, i.e, an
operation
f:S^{3}\rightarrow S
satisfying

f(y,x,x)\approx f(x,y,x)\approx f(x,x,y)\approx x

(7)

Note that the first two identities in (
7
) alone are the
WNU identities in (
5
) for arity
3
.

Let us now include sublinear width into the picture. It has been known
that Theorem
1
can be strengthened to also include

sublinear width
in the characterization. Concretely,
items
1
–
3
are also equivalent to item
0
below:

0
.

\operatorname{{\bf T}}
has sublinear width.

On one hand, clearly
1
implies
0
. On the other hand, it
is known that, if
3
fails, then
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
is able to

simulate
the constraint language corresponding to systems of
equations over a non-trivial finite Abelian group, for which linear
width lower bounds are known (see,
e.g.,
[
3
]
). Thus,
0
implies

3
. As it turns out, this implication will also follow from our
main result in Section
4
.

As in the CSP world, whether a fixed-template PCSP is solvable in
bounded width is controlled by the polymorphisms of the
template
[
8
]
.

Theorem 2
.

[
8
]

Let
(\operatorname{{\bf S}}_{1},\operatorname{{\bf T}}_{1})

(\operatorname{{\bf S}}_{2},\operatorname{{\bf T}}_{2})

.

2.

If
(\operatorname{{\bf S}}_{1},\operatorname{{\bf T}}_{1})

(\operatorname{{\bf S}}_{2},\operatorname{{\bf T}}_{2})
.

We note here that, although not explicitly addressed in

the proof for the bounded width statement in

works also for sublinear width; i.e., the
proof of Item

in Theorem

.

3.2
Linear programming and Sherali-Adams hierarchy

In this subsection we shall introduce a more general family of
relaxations based on linear programming. Despite the fact that,
in general, these relaxations are seemingly more
powerful, in the realm of fixed-template CSPs they
turn out to be not more powerful than local consistency. However, it
will follow from our results that for fixed-template
PCSPs they are, indeed, strictly more powerful.

Fix a signature
\sigma
. Let
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
be an instance with
domain
I=[n]
, and let
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
be a constraint language with
domain
S=[p]
, where
n
and
p
are positive integers. For
each
R\in\sigma
of arity
r
,
let
Z_{R}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{rp}
denote the set of characteristic
vectors
v_{\operatorname{{\bf t}}}
as
\operatorname{{\bf t}}
ranges over the set of
r
-tuples
in
R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}}
, seen as maps from
[r]
to
[p]
; formally,

v_{\operatorname{{\bf t}}}=(v_{\operatorname{{\bf t}},1,1},\ldots,v_{\operatorname{{\bf t}},1,p},\ldots,v_{\operatorname{{\bf t}},r,1},\ldots,v_{\operatorname{{\bf t}},r,p}),

(8)

where
v_{\operatorname{{\bf t}},i,j}=1
if
\operatorname{{\bf t}}(i)=j
and
v_{\operatorname{{\bf t}},i,j}=0

if
\operatorname{{\bf t}}(i)\not=j
, for all
i\in[r]
and
j\in[p]
.
Let
P_{R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{rp}
denote the convex hull
of
Z_{R}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{rp}
. The polytope
P_{R}
can be described
by a linear program of the form
A_{R}x\leq b_{R}
, where
x
is a
vector of
rp
many variables,
A_{R}
is an integer matrix with at
most
2^{rp}
many rows, and
b_{R}
is an integer vector of dimension
at most
2^{rp}
. Let
\mathrm{IP}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
denote the 0-1 linear
program that has one variable
x_{u\mapsto a}
for each
u\in I

and
a\in S
and the following defining equalities and inequalities:

x_{u\mapsto 1}+\cdots+x_{u\mapsto p}=1

for
u\in I
,

(I1)

A_{R}x_{\operatorname{{\bf u}}}\leq b_{R}

for
R\in\sigma
and
\operatorname{{\bf u}}\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf I}}}
,

(I2)

x_{u\mapsto a}\in\{0,1\}

for
u\in I
and
a\in S
,

(I3)

where, for
\operatorname{{\bf u}}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})
, we define

x_{\operatorname{{\bf u}}}:=(x_{u_{1}\mapsto 1},\ldots,x_{u_{1}\mapsto p},\ldots,x_{u_{r}\mapsto 1},\ldots,x_{u_{r}\mapsto p}).

(9)

The
direct LP relaxation
of
\mathrm{IP}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
, denoted
by
\mathrm{LP}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
, is the linear program that is obtained by
replacing the 0-1 constraints (I3) by their
relaxation
0\leq x_{u\mapsto a}\leq 1
. We note
that
\mathrm{LP}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
is equivalent to the LP obtained by taking
the so-called
basic linear programming
relaxation introduced in
the optimization variants MAX-CSP of CSP (see,
e.g.,
[
15
,
21
,
25
,
26
,
31
,
40
,
41
]
), and turning the
objective function into a family of inequalities. This is a weak
relaxation. In order to obtain stronger LP relaxations we apply the
method of Sherali and Adams
[
45
]

to
\mathrm{IP}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
.

Fix an integer
k\geq 1
. Recall that
M_{k}(I,S)
is used to denote
the set of maps from a subset of
I
of cardinality at most
k

into
S
. Let
\operatorname{SA}^{k}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
denote the linear program that has one
variable
x_{f}
for each
f\in M_{k}(I,S)
and the following defining
equalities and inequalities:

x_{\emptyset}=1

(T1)

x_{f\cup\{u\mapsto 1\}}+\cdots+x_{f\cup\{u\mapsto p\}}=x_{f}

for
f\in M_{k-1}(I,S)
and
u\in I\setminus\operatorname{dom}(f)
,

(T2)

A_{R}x_{f,\operatorname{{\bf u}}}\leq b_{R}x_{f}

for
f\in M_{k-1}(I,S)
,
R\in\sigma
and
\operatorname{{\bf u}}\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf I}}}
,

(T3)

0\leq x_{f}\leq 1

for
f\in M_{k}(I,S)
,

(T4)

where, for
f\in M_{k-1}(I,S)
and
\operatorname{{\bf u}}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})
, we define

\displaystyle x_{f,\operatorname{{\bf u}}}:=(x_{f\cup\{u_{1}\mapsto 1\}},\ldots,x_{f\cup\{u_{1}\mapsto p\}},\ldots,x_{f\cup\{u_{r}\mapsto 1\}},\ldots,x_{f\cup\{u_{r}\mapsto p\}}).

(10)

It is obvious that
\operatorname{SA}^{1}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
is basically the same linear
program as
\mathrm{LP}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
: in case
k=1
, the
set
M_{k-1}(I,S)
contains only the empty map
f=\emptyset
,
and
x_{\emptyset}=1
by (T1) in
\operatorname{SA}^{1}
. Furthermore,
since
M_{k}(I,S)\subseteq M_{k+1}(I,S)
, it is clear that
each
\operatorname{SA}^{k+1}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
is at least as strong
as
\operatorname{SA}^{k}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
. To understand the relaxation, think of each new
inequality of
\operatorname{SA}^{k+1}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
as obtained from multiplying an
inequality in
\operatorname{SA}^{k}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
by a variable
x_{u\mapsto a}
,
simplifying the results by the
rules
x_{u\mapsto a}^{2}=x_{u\mapsto a}

and
x_{u\mapsto a}x_{u\mapsto b}=0
whenever
a\not=b
(these
relations are true in all solutions of
\mathrm{IP}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
), and
finally linearizing all the quadratic terms by introducing new
variables for the products.

It is worth noting that, according to the Sherali-Adams method as
defined in
[
45
]
, it would seem that our definition
of
\operatorname{SA}^{k+1}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
is missing the inequalities that can be
obtained from those in
\operatorname{SA}^{k}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
from multiplication
by
1-x_{u\mapsto a}
. However, we note that these multiplications
are redundant: the equalities of type (T1) and (T2) imply
that
1-x_{u\mapsto a}=\sum_{b\in[p]\setminus\{a\}}x_{u\mapsto b}
, which means that a multiplication
by
1-x_{u\mapsto a}
can be obtained as a positive linear
combination of multiplications by
x_{u\mapsto b}
for
b\not=a
.

We write
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{\operatorname{SA}^{k}}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
if the linear program
\operatorname{SA}^{k}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})

is feasible. It follows directly from the definitions that
if
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\to\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, then
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{\operatorname{SA}^{k}}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
. Indeed, if
h
is a
homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, then the assignment that
sets
x_{f}=1
if
f\subseteq h
, and
x_{f}=0

if
f\not\subseteq h
, is a feasible solution to the linear
program. Furthermore, it also follows directly from the definitions
that if
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{\operatorname{SA}^{k}}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, then
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{k}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
; indeed,
if
(x_{f}\mid f\in M_{k}(I,S))
is a feasible solution
for
\operatorname{SA}^{k}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
, then the
collection
\mathscr{H}=\{f\in M_{k}(I,S)\mid x_{f}\not=0\}
is
a
k
-strategy for
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
and
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
; the non-emptiness condition
follows from (T1), the closure under restrictions and the extension
property up to
k
follow from (T2), and the condition that every map
in
\mathscr{H}
is a partial homomorphism follows from (T3) and the
choice of the polytope
P_{R}
.

Since the feasibility problem for linear programs is solvable in
polynomial time, there is an algorithm that, given
(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}},k)
as
input, decides whether
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{\operatorname{SA}^{k}}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
and does so in time
polynomial in
(|I|+|S|)^{k}
. This algorithm is the
width-
k

Sherali-Adams heuristic
for CSPs which, as was the case for the
width-
k
consistency algorithm, can also be used as a heuristic for
PCSPs. If
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is a PCSP template and
k(n)
is an integer
function, then we say that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})

is solvable in
SA-width
k(n)
, or that the template
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
has

SA-width
k(n)
, if for every
\sigma
-structure instance
\operatorname{{\bf I}}

with
n
elements it holds that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{\operatorname{SA}^{k(n)}}\operatorname{{\bf S}}

implies
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\to\operatorname{{\bf T}}
. It follows from the fact that it is always the
case that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{n}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, where
n
is the number of elements
of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
, that every
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is solvable in SA-width at
most
k(n)
for
k(n)=n
. Whenever it is solvable in
SA-width
k(n)
for some
k(n)=O(1)
, we say that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})

is solvable in
bounded SA-width
, or that
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
has
bounded SA-width.

The SA relaxations have been intensively used in optimization versions
of CSP
[
22
,
30
,
46
,
50
]
. Our setup
is, again, slightly different because the constraints of the instance
must be encoded in the polytope-defining inequalities, rather than in
the objective function, as is done in the
optimization variants. Still, the family of SA relaxations commonly
used for optimization can be readily adapted to our setting by turning
the objective function into a set of inequalities (see

[
20
]
). The LP family obtained in this way, although not
equivalent to ours, has the same power up to constants. Indeed, the
notion of bounded SA-width remains unaltered.

The strength of SA-width
k
for
k=1
is well understood. Indeed, it
is shown in
[
8
]
(Theorem 7.9) that
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
has
SA-width
1
if and only if
\operatorname{Pol}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
has symmetric functions of
all arities, where an operation is symmetric if its output is
independent of the order of its input elements. The picture is also
clear if
\operatorname{{\bf S}}=\operatorname{{\bf T}}
(i.e., in the case of CSPs). In particular, it
follows from combining the results in
[
1
]

and
[
2
]
with those in
[
9
]
that
if
\operatorname{CSP}(\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is solvable in bounded SA-width, then it is solvable in
bounded width as well. Furthermore, combining the results
in
[
9
]
and
[
5
]
it can be concluded
that, additionally,
\operatorname{CSP}(\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is solvable in
relational
width 2
, which, in turn, can be shown to imply that it is solvable
in SA-width
2
.

4
Main result and applications

In this section we state the main structural result about PCSP
templates of bounded width, derive from it the algebraic consequence
about the presence of WNUs of arities three and more, and apply it to
compare the relative power of bounded width and bounded SA-width.

4.1
Structure of PCSP templates of bounded width

If
R\subseteq A^{r}
is a relation of arity
r
over the set
A
, then
we write
\mathscr{G}(R)
to denote the set of all binary projections
of
R
; i.e.,
\mathscr{G}(R)=\{\operatorname{pr}_{i,j}R\mid i,j\in[r],\,i\neq j\}
.
If
\sigma
is a signature and
\operatorname{{\bf A}}
is a
\sigma
-structure, then we
define
\mathscr{G}(\operatorname{{\bf A}})=\mathscr{G}(\prod_{\sigma\in R}R^{\operatorname{{\bf A}}})
. The main
technical result of this paper is the following.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
be a PCSP template that has sublinear width. If for
all
U,V\in\mathscr{G}(\operatorname{{\bf S}})
we
have
U\circ V=S^{2}
, then
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
is reflexive; i.e., there
exists
a\in T
such that each relation in
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
contains the
reflexive tuple
(a,a,\dots,a)
. In particular, this holds
if
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
has bounded width.

An example of a PCSP template that satisfies the condition of
Theorem
3
is
(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{2},\operatorname{{\bf H}})
for any graph
\operatorname{{\bf H}}
having at
least one edge. Indeed, the composition of the edge relation
of
\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{2}
with itself is the equality relation on
[2]
, which is not
the full binary relation
[2]\times[2]
. This is consistent with the
fact that
\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{2}
, and hence
(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{2},\operatorname{{\bf H}})
, has width three (see
Example
2
). An example of a
PCSP template that does
not
satisfy the condition of
Theorem
3
is
(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf H}})
for any integer
p\geq 3
and
any self-loop free graph
\operatorname{{\bf H}}
such that
\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p}\to\operatorname{{\bf H}}
. Indeed, as
it is easy to check, if
p\geq 3
then the composition of the edge
relation of
\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p}
with itself is the full binary
relation
[p]\times[p]
, but
\operatorname{{\bf H}}
is not reflexive (since it is
self-loop free). We revisit these examples in
Section
6
.

The proof of Theorem
3
will be given in
Section
5
. We devote the rest of this section to derive
some applications.

4.2
Algebraic implications

We first derive some algebraic implications. Recall the definition of
WNU polymorphism from Example
1
in
Section
2
. Recall also that, by
Theorem
1
, if
\operatorname{CSP}(\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is solvable in bounded width,
then
\operatorname{Pol}(\operatorname{{\bf T}})
must contain a WNU of every arity
m\geq 3
. It
follows from Theorem
3
that this also holds for Promise
CSPs.

Corollary 2
.

Let
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
be a PCSP template of sublinear or bounded
width. Then,
\operatorname{Pol}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
contains a WNU of arity
m
for
every
m\geq 3
.

Proof.

Let
m\geq 3
, let
\mathscr{M}=\operatorname{Pol}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
, and
let
\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime}=(\{x,y\},R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime}})
be the structure with a
2
-element
domain
\{x,y\}
where
R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime}}
is the
m
-ary relation that
contains precisely all tuples in
\{x,y\}^{m}
in which
y
appears
exactly once (as in Example
1
). As pointed out in
Observation
1
, there is a homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime}

to
\operatorname{{\bf F}}(\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime};\mathscr{M})
, and, hence,
(\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime},\operatorname{{\bf F}}(\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime};\mathscr{M}))

is a legit PCSP template. Also, by
Observation
2
there is a minor homomorphism
from
\mathscr{M}
to
\operatorname{Pol}(\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime},\operatorname{{\bf F}}(\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime};\mathscr{M}))
. Therefore,
by Theorem
2
, the PCSP
template
(\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime},\operatorname{{\bf F}}(\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime};\mathscr{M}))
has sublinear width. Then, it
follows from Theorem
3
that
R^{\operatorname{{\bf F}}(\operatorname{{\bf A}}^{\prime};\mathscr{M})}

contains a reflexive tuple, which implies, by Example
1
,
that
\mathscr{M}
contains an
m
-ary WNU.
∎

Next we argue that the converse to Corollary
2
does not
hold. For positive integers
s
and
r
, let
s\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}r\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT}
be the
structure with domain
\{0,1\}
and a single relation
R^{s\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}r\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT}}
of arity
r

containing all tuples with exactly
s
many
1
’s. Let
r\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{NAE}\text{-}\mathrm{SAT}}
be
the structure with domain
\{0,1\}
and a single relation
R^{r\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{NAE}\text{-}\mathrm{SAT}}}
of arity
r

containing all tuples in
\{0,1\}^{r}
except the two reflexive
tuples
(0,0,\ldots,0)
and
(1,1,\ldots,1)
. Clearly,
if
0<s<r
, then there is a homomorphism from
s\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}r\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT}

to
r\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{NAE}\text{-}\mathrm{SAT}}
, so
(s\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}r\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT},r\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{NAE}\text{-}\mathrm{SAT}})
is a PCSP template.

Lemma 1
.

There is a PCSP template
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
that does not have sublinear or
bounded width such that
\operatorname{Pol}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
contains a WNU of arity
m

for every
m\geq 3
. Concretely, setting
\operatorname{{\bf S}}=2\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}4\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT}

and
\operatorname{{\bf T}}=4\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{NAE}\text{-}\mathrm{SAT}}
gives such an example.

Proof.

Let
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
and
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
be set as in the second part of the lemma. It
follows directly from Theorem
3
that
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
does
not have sublinear width. However,
\operatorname{Pol}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
contains, for
every
m\geq 3
, the WNU
h
or arity
m
, that returns the majority
element in the input if exists and else (that is, in case of ties)
it returns the first element. That is,
h
is the following
function:

h(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a&\text{if }|\{x_{i}\mid x_{i}=a\}|>m/2\\
x_{1}&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

To see that this is a WNU, use the assumption that
m\geq 3
. To see
that this a polymorphism of
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
, we argue by double
counting. Any
m\times m
matrix that has all its rows in
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
has
the same number of
0
’s and
1
’s. Therefore, either all columns also
have the same number of
0
’s and
1
’s, in which case
h
returns the
first row of the matrix, which is not reflexive, or some column has
more
0
’s than
1
’s and some column has more
1
’s than
0
’s, in
which case
h
returns also a non-reflexive tuple.
∎

4.3
Separation of width from SA-width

Another application of Theorem
3
is the separation of
bounded width and bounded SA-width for PCSPs. This separation is
another evidence that the family of PCSPs is a more rich family of
problems than classical CSPs from an algorithmic point of view.
This separation is obtained combining our result with the following
lemma, which in turn, builds upon
[
12
]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\operatorname{{\bf S}}=s\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}r\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT}
and
\operatorname{{\bf T}}=r\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{NAE}\text{-}\mathrm{SAT}}
where
s
and
r
are
integers such that
0<s<r
. Then
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
has SA-width
2
.

Proof.

We start by noticing that a simple double counting argument shows
that
\operatorname{Pol}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
contains, for every odd
m\geq 3
, the
alternating threshold
operation
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
defined to
be
1
if
\sum_{i\in[m]}(-1)^{i-1}x_{i}>0
and
0

otherwise. Indeed, let
\operatorname{{\bf t}}_{1},\dots,\operatorname{{\bf t}}_{m}
be any sequence of tuples
in
R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}}
and let
O
and
E
be, respectively, the odd and even
numbers in
[m]
. It we use
|\operatorname{{\bf t}}|_{1}
to denote the total number
of
1
’s occurring in tuple
\operatorname{{\bf t}}
, we observe that

\sum_{i\in O}|\operatorname{{\bf t}}_{i}|_{1}=s+\sum_{i\in E}|\operatorname{{\bf t}}_{i}|_{1}

(11)

It follows that there exist
j_{0},j_{1}\in[r]

satisfying

\sum_{i\in O}\operatorname{{\bf t}}_{i}(j_{0})\geq\sum_{i\in E}\operatorname{{\bf t}}_{i}(j_{0})\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\sum_{i\in O}\operatorname{{\bf t}}_{i}(j_{1})\leq\sum_{i\in E}\operatorname{{\bf t}}_{i}(j_{1}).

(12)

We note that
for the existence of
j_{1}
we
use
s<r
. Consequently,
f(\operatorname{{\bf t}}_{1},\dots,\operatorname{{\bf t}}_{m})
contains at least
a
0
and a
1
(in coordinates
j_{0}
and
j_{1}

respectively). Then
f(\operatorname{{\bf t}}_{1},\dots,\operatorname{{\bf t}}_{m})\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf T}}}
as desired.

It then follows from (
[
12
]
, Section 3.2) that any
instance
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
of
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
is homomorphic to
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
whenever
the following
augmented
\operatorname{SA}^{1}
feasibility
condition holds:
for each variable
v\in I
there exists a
b_{v}\in\{0,1\}
such
that there is a feasible solution of
\operatorname{SA}^{1}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
that
sets
x_{v\mapsto b_{v}}
to
1
. We are left to show that
if
\operatorname{SA}^{2}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
has a feasible solution, then the
augmented
\operatorname{SA}^{1}
feasibility condition holds.

To avoid confusion we use
x^{\prime}_{f}
to refer to the variables
in
\operatorname{SA}^{2}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
and
x_{f}
to refer to the variables
in
\operatorname{SA}^{1}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
. Fix
v\in I
. It follows from the first three
types of constraint in the definition of
\operatorname{SA}^{2}
that there exists
some
b\in\{0,1\}
that
x^{\prime}_{v\mapsto b}>0
;
let
d:=x^{\prime}_{v\mapsto b}
. Now construct a solution
of
\operatorname{SA}^{1}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
as follows: for every
u\in I
and
a\in B
,
set
x_{u\mapsto a}
to
x^{\prime}_{u\mapsto a,v\mapsto b}/d
. By choice of
d
we have
x_{v\mapsto b}=1
as
desired. It is easy to verify that the assignment thus defined is a
feasible solution of
\operatorname{SA}^{1}(\operatorname{{\bf I}},\operatorname{{\bf S}})
.
∎

Hence, from Theorem
3
and Lemma
2
we have:

Corollary 3
.

There is a PCSP template
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
that has
SA-width
2
, and hence bounded SA-width, but does not have
sublinear or bounded width. Concretely,
setting
\operatorname{{\bf S}}=s\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}r\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT}
and
\operatorname{{\bf T}}=r\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{NAE}\text{-}\mathrm{SAT}}
where
s
and
r

are integers such that
0<s<r
and
r>2
gives such an
example.

It should be pointed out that the condition that
r>2
in
Corollary
3
is necessary. Indeed, the
template
1\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}2\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT}
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{K}_{2}
, and we already
know that
\mathbf{K}_{2}
, and hence
(1\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}2\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT},2\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{NAE}\text{-}\mathrm{SAT}})
, has width
three (see Example
2
). It is also easy to see that,
consistently with this,
(1\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}2\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT},2\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{NAE}\text{-}\mathrm{SAT}})
satisfies the
condition of Theorem
3
: the composition of the binary
relation of
1\mathrm{\text{-}\mathrm{IN}\text{-}}2\mathrm{\text{-}}\mathrm{SAT}
with itself is the equality relation
on
\{0,1\}
, which is not the full binary relation
\{0,1\}^{2}
.

5
Proof of Theorem
3

The proof is structured in five parts. The first part sets the
stage. In the second part we define a probability distribution on
instances and prove that a random instance
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
sampled from this
distribution has, with high probability, two key properties: (a) it is

dense enough
to guarantee that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\not\to\operatorname{{\bf T}}
, and (b) it is
also
sparse enough
, in a sense compatible with (a), to
guarantee that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{k}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
, as will be proved in the next
part. In the third part we show that any instance
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
that satisfies
the sparsity condition indeed satisfies
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{k}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
. In the
fourth part we discuss the setting of parameters that satisfies all
the required conditions to prove the theorem. Finally, the fifth part
of the proof focuses on the special case of the theorem that applies
to digraphs (structures with a single binary relation). For this
special case we are able to slightly improve the parameters; this
special case for (di)graphs will be used in
Section
6
.

5.1
Setting the stage

Let
\sigma
be a fixed signature and let
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
be a PCSP template
of signature
\sigma
that has sublinear width.
Let
\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime}:=(S,\prod_{R\in\sigma}R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}})

and
\operatorname{{\bf T}}^{\prime}:=(T,\prod_{R\in\sigma}R^{\operatorname{{\bf T}}})
and note that
\operatorname{{\bf S}}\to\operatorname{{\bf T}}

implies
\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime}\to\operatorname{{\bf T}}^{\prime}
. Since the signature
\sigma
is finite and
fixed, the template
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
has sublinear width if and only if the
template
(\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime},\operatorname{{\bf T}}^{\prime})
has sublinear width. Also, it holds
that
\mathscr{G}(\operatorname{{\bf S}})=\mathscr{G}(\operatorname{{\bf S}}^{\prime})
, and that
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
is reflexive if and
only if
\operatorname{{\bf T}}^{\prime}
is reflexive. Therefore, it suffices to prove the
theorem when the signature
\sigma
consists of a single relation
symbol
R
. Furthermore, since any unary relation is either reflexive
or empty, we may assume that the arity
r
of
R
is at least
2
;
i.e.,
r\geq 2
.

Let
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
satisfy the following assumptions:

A1:

for all
U,V\in\mathscr{G}(\operatorname{{\bf S}})
we have
U\circ V=S^{2}
,

A2:

for all
a\in T
, the reflexive tuple
(a,a,\ldots,a)
is not in
R^{\operatorname{{\bf T}}}
.

Let
p=|S|
and
q=|T|
be the cardinalities of the domains
of
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
and
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
, respectively, and let
k=k(n)
be an integer
function such that
k(n)=o(n)
. Our goal is to show that there exist
arbitrarily large instances
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
that witness that the PCSP
template
(\operatorname{{\bf S}},\operatorname{{\bf T}})
does not have width
k
; i.e., the instance
\operatorname{{\bf I}}

is such that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{k}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
and
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\not\rightarrow\operatorname{{\bf T}}
. We show
that such an instance
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
exists by the probabilistic method.

In anticipation for the proof, in addition to the data
r,k,p,q
, we
fix some real parameters
\delta,\beta,\alpha,c,d
, as well as an
integer parameter
n
. These parameters are required to satisfy the
following conditions:

C1:

0<\delta\leq 1/((r+1)(3r+1))
,

C2:

0<\beta\leq(1+\delta)/(r-1)
,

C3:

0<\alpha\leq(\beta/d)^{1/(r-1)}(r/e)^{r/(r-1)}
,

C4:

c\geq kp/\delta
,

C5:

n\geq\max\{c/(\alpha\beta),q\}
,

C6:

1\leq d\leq n^{r-1}
,

C7:

p_{1}(r,d,n,q)+p_{2}(r,d,n,\alpha,\beta)<1
,

where

\displaystyle p_{1}(r,d,n,q):=q^{n}\exp{\left({-{dn}/{(r^{r}q^{r-1}}}\right)},

(13)

\displaystyle p_{2}(r,d,n,\alpha,\beta):=\textstyle{\sum_{v=1}^{\lfloor{\alpha n}\rfloor}\left({(n/v)^{1-(r-1)\beta}d^{\beta}e^{1+(r+1)\beta}r^{-r\beta}\beta^{-\beta}}\right)^{v}}.

(14)

We have separated the first six conditions C1–C6 from the last one C7
because the first six are easily feasible by themselves; fulfilling
Condition C7 simultaneously is more delicate. At the end of the proof
we discuss a settings of the parameters
\delta,\beta,\alpha,c,d

and
n
that satisfy Conditions C1–C7. For now, we assume that the
conditions are feasible.

5.2
Probabilistic construction

Let
H
denote a random Erdős-Rényi
r
-uniform hypergraph
with
n
vertices and edge probability
d/n^{r-1}
;
i.e.,
V(H)=[n]
, and each
r
-element subset
C\subseteq[n]
is
or is not an edge in
E(H)
, independently, with
probability
d/n^{r-1}
. Note that
d/n^{r-1}
is a proper probability
by Condition C6. Let
\operatorname{{\bf I}}=\operatorname{{\bf I}}(H)
be the (random) instance with
domain
[n]
that has one
r
-tuple
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C}
in
R^{\operatorname{{\bf I}}}
for
each
C\in E(H)
, where
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C}
is obtained by ordering the elements
in
C
is some arbitrary way.

Lemma 3
.

The probability that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
is homomorphic to
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
is at
most
p_{1}(r,d,n,q)
.

Proof.

Let
h
be any mapping from the domain
I
of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to the
domain
T
of
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
. Let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{q}
be the partition induced
by
h
; i.e.,
S_{i}=h^{-1}(i)
for
i=1,\ldots,q
.
Let
n_{i}:=|S_{i}|
. By Assumption A2, the relation
R^{\operatorname{{\bf T}}}
does
not contain a reflexive tuple. Therefore, for all
i\in[q]

and
C\subseteq S_{i}
we have
h(\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C})\not\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf T}}}
. By
independence, the probability that
h(\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C})
belongs to
R^{\operatorname{{\bf T}}}

for every
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C}\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf I}}}
is at most
(1-d/n^{r-1})^{N}
,
where
N=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\binom{n_{i}}{r}
. Now note
that
\binom{n_{i}}{r}\geq(n_{i}/r)^{r}
, and that, subject to the
constraints
x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{q}x_{i}=n
, the
sum
\sum_{i=1}^{q}(x_{i}/r)^{r}
is minimized
at
x_{1}=\cdots=x_{q}=n/q
. It follows
that
N\geq q(n/(rq))^{r}=n^{r}/(r^{r}q^{r-1})
.
Therefore,
(1-d/n^{r-1})^{N}\leq\exp(-dn^{r}/(n^{r-1}r^{r}q^{r-1}))\leq\exp(-dn/(r^{r}q^{r-1}))
. By the union bound the probability
that there is a homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
is at
most
q^{n}\exp(-dn/(r^{r}q^{r-1})
, which equals
p_{1}(r,d,n,q)
.
∎

We say that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
is
(\alpha,\beta)
-sparse
if every
substructure
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
with
v
elements
satisfying
1\leq v\leq\alpha n
has less than
\beta v
many
tuples. Equivalently,
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
is
(\alpha,\beta)
-sparse if every
substructure
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
with at most
\alpha n
elements and at
least
\beta v
many tuples has more than
v
elements. The argument
for the lemma below is almost the same as the one for Lemma 1
in
[
23
]
except that we need to apply it to our
probability model. Also as in
[
23
]
, we use the
following Chernoff-like bound

\sum_{i=\lceil{tm}\rceil}^{m}{m\choose i}\gamma^{i}(1-\gamma)^{m-i}\leq\left({e\gamma/t}\right)^{tm},

(15)

which holds for every integer
m\geq 1
, every
real
\gamma\in[0,1]
, and every real
t\in(\gamma,1]
.

Lemma 4
.

The probability that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
is not
(\alpha,\beta)
-sparse is at
most
p_{2}(r,d,n,\alpha,\beta)
.

Proof.

For each integer
v
such that
1\leq v\leq\alpha n
,
set
m_{v}={v\choose r}
. Setting
p_{0}=d/n^{r-1}
, the probability
that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
is not
(\alpha,\beta)
-sparse is bounded by

\sum_{v=r}^{\lfloor{\alpha n}\rfloor}{n\choose v}\sum_{i=\lceil{\beta v}\rceil}^{m_{v}}{m_{v}\choose i}p_{0}^{i}(1-p_{0})^{m_{v}-i}.

(16)

Now set
t_{v}=\beta v/m_{v}
. Using
{v\choose r}<(ve/r)^{r}
note
that
t_{v}>\beta r^{r}/(e^{r}v^{r-1})\geq d/n^{r-1}=p_{0}

for
v\leq\alpha n
since
\alpha
satisfies Condition C3.
If
t_{v}>1
, then the inner sum in (
16
) is zero, while
if
t_{v}\leq 1
, then we have
t_{v}\in(p_{0},1]
,
so (
15
) applies to bound (
16
) by

\sum_{v=r}^{\lfloor{\alpha n}\rfloor}{n\choose v}\left({ep_{0}/t_{v}}\right)^{t_{v}m_{v}}.

(17)

Using
{n\choose v}\leq(ne/v)^{v}
and
{v\choose r}\leq(ve/r)^{r}
,
we bound this further by

\sum_{v=r}^{\lfloor{\alpha n}\rfloor}\left({(n/v)^{1-(r-1)\beta}d^{\beta}e^{1+(r+1)\beta}r^{-r\beta}\beta^{-\beta}}\right)^{v},

(18)

which is bounded by
p_{2}(r,d,n,\alpha,\beta)
.
∎

Lemma 5
.

There exists a structure
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
with
n
elements that is not
homomorphic to
\operatorname{{\bf T}}
and is
(\alpha,\beta)
-sparse. In
particular,
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
is such that for every substructure
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}

and every integer
m\geq 0
, if
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
has
m
many tuples
and
m\leq c
, then
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
has more than
(r-1)m/(1+\delta)

elements.

Proof.

The existence of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
follows directly from the assumption that
Condition C7 holds and Lemmas
3

and
4
. For the second part of the statement,
assume that
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
is a substructure of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
that has
v
many
elements and
m
many tuples, with
m\leq c

and
v\leq(r-1)m/(1+\delta)
. In
particular
v\leq m/\beta\leq c/\beta\leq\alpha n
since
\beta

satisfies Condition C2 and
n
satisfies Condition C5. But
\operatorname{{\bf I}}

is
(\alpha,\beta)
-sparse and it follows that
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
has less
than
\beta v\leq m
many tuples; a contradiction.
∎

5.3
Proof of consistency

Still assuming that the setting of parameters satisfies the
conditions C1–C7, let
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
be a structure as in the second part of
Lemma
5
. We shall now prove that
\operatorname{{\bf I}}\leq_{k}\operatorname{{\bf S}}
.

The proof adapts the notion of
boundary
from

[
44
]
; this was used to prove size lower
bounds for the Resolution proof complexity of random CSPs. Let
\operatorname{{\bf J}}

be any substructure of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
. A set
D\subseteq J
is said to be a

boundary set of
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
if it is non-empty and every homomorphism
from
\operatorname{{\bf J}}|_{J\setminus D}
to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
extends to a homomorphism
from
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
. We introduce two types of subsets
D\subseteq J

of
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
and show that any set of any of these types is a boundary set
of
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
.

We define the
degree
of an element in
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
to be the number of
tuples of
R^{\operatorname{{\bf J}}}
in which it appears. We say that
D\subseteq J

is of type (1) if
D=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r-1}\}
, where
x_{1},\dots,x_{r-1}

are all distinct and have degree one in
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
, and there
exists
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C}\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf J}}}
with
D\subseteq C
. In this case we say
that the set
C

witnesses
that
D
is of type (1). We say
that
D\subseteq J
is of type (2)
if
D=\{x_{1},y_{1},\dots,x_{r-2},y_{r-2},z\}
,
where
x_{1},y_{1},\dots,x_{r-2},y_{r-2}
are all distinct and have degree
one in
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
, also
z
is distinct from the rest of elements in
D

and has degree two in
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
, and there exist two different
tuples
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C_{1}},\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C_{2}}\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf J}}}
such
that
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r-2},z\}\subseteq C_{1}

and
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{r-2},z\}\subseteq C_{2}
. In this case we say that
the sets
C_{1},C_{2}

witness
that
D
is of type (2). We check
below that, since
\mathscr{G}(R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}})
satisfies Assumption A1, every set
of these two types is a boundary set.

Lemma 6
.

For every substructure
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
and every
D\subseteq J
,
if
D
is of type (1) or (2) in
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
, then
D
is a boundary set
of
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
.

Proof.

Fix
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
and
D
as in the hypothesis and let
h
be a
homomomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf J}}|_{J\setminus D}
to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
.

Firstly, assume that
D=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r-1}\}
is of type (1), and
let
C
witness so; i.e.,
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C}\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf J}}}
and
D\subseteq C
.
Let
i_{0}\in[r]
be such that
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C}(i_{0})\in J\setminus D
. By
Assumption A1 on
\mathscr{G}(R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}})
we have that

\operatorname{pr}_{i_{0}}R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}}=S.

(19)

Consequently, there is a tuple
\operatorname{{\bf a}}\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}}
such
that
\operatorname{{\bf a}}(i_{0})=h(\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C}(i_{0}))
. We extend
h
to map every element
of
J
by setting
h(\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C}(i))=\operatorname{{\bf a}}(i)
for
every
i\in[r]\setminus\{i_{0}\}
. Since each
x\in D
appears
in
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C}
but in no other tuple of
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
, the result is a
homomorphism from
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
.

Secondly, assume that
D=\{x_{1},y_{1},\dots,x_{r-2},y_{r-2},z\}
is of
type (2), and let
C_{1},C_{2}
witness so;
i.e.,
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C_{1}},\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C_{2}}\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf J}}}
are distinct,
and
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r-2},z\}\subseteq C_{1}

and
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{r-2},z\}\subseteq C_{2}
. For
j=1,2
,
let
i_{j}\in[r]
be such that
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C_{j}}(i_{j})\in J\setminus D
and
let
k_{j}\in[r]\setminus\{i_{j}\}
be such that
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C_{j}}(k_{j})=z
.
Again, by Assumption A1 on
\mathscr{G}(R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}})
, we
have that

\operatorname{pr}_{i_{1},k_{1}}R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}}\circ\operatorname{pr}_{k_{2},i_{2}}R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}}=S^{2}.

(20)

Consequently, there exist
tuples
\operatorname{{\bf a}}_{1},\operatorname{{\bf a}}_{2}\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf S}}}
such
that
\operatorname{{\bf a}}_{j}(i_{j})=h(\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C_{j}}(i_{j}))
for
j=1,2

and
\operatorname{{\bf a}}_{1}(k_{1})=\operatorname{{\bf a}}_{2}(k_{2})
. We extend

to map every element
of
J
by setting
h(\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C_{j}}(i))=\operatorname{{\bf a}}_{j}(i)
for
j=1,2
and
every
i\in[r]\setminus i_{j}
. Since each

appears
in
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C_{1}}
or in
\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C_{2}}
(or in both, in case of

) but in
no other tuple of
\operatorname{{\bf J}}

\operatorname{{\bf J}}

to
\operatorname{{\bf S}}
.
∎

Lemma 7
.

For every substructure
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
of
\operatorname{{\bf I}}
and every integer
m\geq 0
,
if
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
has
m
many tuples and
m\leq c
, then
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
has at
least
\delta m
many pairwise disjoint boundary sets.

Proof.

We can assume that
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
does not contain elements of degree zero
since every boundary set of any substructure obtained by removing
elements of degree
0
from
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
is also a boundary set of
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
.
We shall show that the total number of boundary sets in
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
that
are of type (1) or (2) is at least
\delta m
. Since any two
distinct boundary sets of these types must be disjoint, the claim
will follow.

Let
\mathscr{C}=\{C\mid\operatorname{{\bf v}}_{C}\in R^{\operatorname{{\bf J}}}\}
.
For
C=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}\in\mathscr{C}
, define

\operatorname{sdr}(C):=\textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{r}1/d_{i}}

(21)

where
d_{i}
is the degree of
x_{i}
in
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
(

stands for sum
of degree reciprocals). Since
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
does not have elements of degree
zero, it is easy to see that
\sum_{C\in\mathscr{C}}\operatorname{sdr}(C)
is
equal to the number
v
of elements in
\operatorname{{\bf J}}
. The idea of the proof is
the following. Since, by Lemma
5
we have
that
v\geq(r-1)m/(1+\delta)
, there is a large number of
sets
C\in\mathscr{C}

with
\operatorname{sdr}(C)\geq(r-1)/(1+\delta)
. Since
\delta
is small
enough each one of these sets must have at least
r-2
vertices of
degree one, and one vertex of degree at most two. From this large pool
of sets it is not difficult to find a large number of witnesses for
boundary sets of type (1) or (2). We formalize this below.

Let
\mathscr{D}
be a collection of boundary sets of types (1) and (2)
with the largest possible cardinality and assume towards a
contradiction that
|\mathscr{D}|<\delta m
. We partition
\mathscr{C}

in three sets
\mathscr{C}_{1}
,
\mathscr{C}_{2}
,
\mathscr{C}_{3}
. The
set
\mathscr{C}_{1}
contains the witness
C\in\mathscr{C}
of every
boundary set of type (1) in
\mathscr{D}
, and exactly one among the
two sets
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathscr{C}
that witness some boundary set of
type (2) in
\mathscr{D}
. Note
that
|\mathscr{C}_{1}|=|\mathscr{D}|<\delta m
. The
set
\mathscr{C}_{2}
contains all
C\in\mathscr{C}\setminus\mathscr{C}_{1}

such that
\operatorname{sdr}(C)>r-4/3
. The set
\mathscr{C}_{3}
contains the

all
C\in\mathscr{C}\setminus(\mathscr{C}_{1}\cup\mathscr{C}_{2})
.
Note that every
C\in\mathscr{C}\setminus\mathscr{C}_{1}
must contain at
least two elements of degree larger than one: otherwise it would be
the witness of a boundary set of type (1), against the maximality
of
\mathscr{D}
. In particular, for
any
C\in\mathscr{C}\setminus\mathscr{C}_{1}
, we have
\operatorname{sdr}(C)\leq r-1
.
Note also that
\operatorname{sdr}(C)\leq r
for every
C\in\mathscr{C}
,
and
\operatorname{sdr}(C)\leq r-4/3
for every
C\in\mathscr{C}_{3}
.

Let
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
be reals in
[0,1]
such
that
|\mathscr{C}_{1}|=\alpha_{0}m
,
|\mathscr{C}_{2}|=(1-\alpha_{0}-\beta_{0})m
,
and
|\mathscr{C}_{3}|=\beta_{0}m
. Since each boundary set
in
\mathscr{D}
contributes exactly one set to
\mathscr{C}_{1}

and
|\mathscr{D}|<\delta m
, we have
\alpha_{0}<\delta
. We shall

\beta_{0}\leq 3r\delta
. Assume otherwise;
i.e.,
\beta_{0}>3r\delta
. Recall
that

and, hence,

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\leq|\mathscr{C}_{1}|r+|\mathscr{C}_{2}|(r-1)+|\mathscr{C}_{3}|(r-4/3)

(22)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}mr+(1-\alpha_{0}-\beta_{0})m(r-1)+\beta_{0}m(r-4/3)

(23)

\displaystyle=m(r-1+\alpha_{0}-\beta_{0}/3)

(24)

\displaystyle<m(r-1)(1-\delta),

(25)

where the first inequality follows from the fact
that
\mathscr{C}_{1},\mathscr{C}_{2},\mathscr{C}_{3}
is a partition
of
\mathscr{C}
and the already noted bounds on

for the

in these sets, the first equality follows from the choices
of
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}

and the assumption that
\beta_{0}>3r\delta

. Combined
with (

It follows that
|\mathscr{C}_{2}|\geq(1-(1+3r)\delta)m
.
Let

. Since

, the
set
C
must contain at least

elements of degree one. We also
know that the remaining two elements in

must have degree at least
two since, otherwise,

would witness a boundary set of type (1),
against the maximality of
\mathscr{D}
. Again from

it
follows that at least one of the two remaining elements must have

6
Approximate Chromatic Number

For a graph
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
with
n
vertices, there is always an integer
q

satisfying
1\leq q\leq n
such that
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}\to\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q}
; the smallest
such
q
is the
chromatic number
of
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
. Any homomorphism
from
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
to
\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q}
is called a proper
q
-coloring of
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
. The
problem of properly coloring a graph with as few as possible number
of colors has a long history. Finding the exact chromatic number is of
course one of the classical NP-hard problems, straight from Karp’s 21
list
[
34
]
. The exact computational complexity of the
problem of approximating the chromatic number is much less understood,
despite the important progress on the problem since the discovery of
the PCP Theorem. In this section we study the width complexity
of the problem.

6.1
Constant chromatic numbers

In the regime of constant chromatic numbers, it is conjectured that
the problem of
q
-coloring
p
-colorable graphs is NP-hard for any
two constants
p
and
q
such that
3\leq p\leq q
. The larger the
gap between
q
and
p
, the stronger the NP-hardness result.
For
p=3
, the current best result of this type, due to Barto, Bulín,
Krokhin, and Opršal
[
8
]
, is that it is
NP-hard to
5
-color
3
-colorable graphs. For constant
p\geq 4
,
the current best such result, due to Wrochna and Živný

[
49
]
, is that it is NP-hard
to
q(p)
-color
p
-colorable graphs,
where
q(p):=\binom{p}{\lfloor{p/2}\rfloor}-1
. This improved over
the previously known point of NP-hardness for the
weaker
q(p)=\exp(\Omega(p^{1/3}))
, which holds for sufficiently
large
p

[
32
]
, and for the even weaker
q(p)=2p-1
, which
holds for all
p\geq 3

[
8
]
. The full
conjecture stating that the problem of
q
-coloring
p
-colorable
graphs is NP-hard for any two constants
p
and
q
such
that
3\leq p\leq q
is known to follow from certain variants of the

Unique Games Conjecture
(UGC)
[
27
]
.

These results predict that the promise
problems
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{3},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{5})
and
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q(p)})

for
p\geq 4
are not solvable in bounded width, unless P = NP. The
UGC-based results predict that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q})
is not solvable in
bounded width for any two constants
p
and
q
such
that
3\leq p\leq q
, unless either the suitable variants of the UGC
fail or P = NP. We show that our Main
Theorem
3
confirms all these predictions,
unconditionally, and in the stronger sense of ruling out, not only
solvability in constant width, but solvability in sublinear width:

Theorem 4
.

For any two integers
p
and
q
such
that
1\leq p\leq q
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q})
is solvable by the consistency
algorithm in width
3
,

(b)

\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q})
is solvable by the consistency
algorithm in bounded width,

(c)

\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q})
is solvable by the consistency
algorithm in sublinear width,

(d)

p=1
or
p=2
.

Proof.

If
p=1
, then the problem is trivial and solvable in width 2, and
hence in width 3. If
p=2
, then the problem is solvable in
width
3
since, in this case, the problem amounts to detecting
whether
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
is 2-colorable, which is solvable in width 3 (see
Example
2
). For
p
and
q
such
that
3\leq p\leq q
, we
have
\mathscr{G}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p})=E^{\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p}}=\{(i,j)\in[p]^{2}\mid i\not=j\}
, and
E^{\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p}}\circ E^{\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p}}=[p]^{2}
, while
E^{\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q}}
is
clearly irreflexive. Therefore, Theorem
3
implies
that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{p},\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{q})
is not solvable in sublinear width, which
completes the proof.
∎

6.2
Algorithms with sublinear guarantees

Turning to upper bounds, a well-known algorithm of
Wigderson
[
47
]
shows that the problem
of
O(\sqrt{n})
-coloring 3-colorable graphs is solvable in polynomial
time. We observe that, in its decision variant, Wigderson’s algorithm
can be thought of as a
width four

Theorem 5
.

For every graph
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
, if
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}\leq_{4}\mathbf{K}_{3}
,
then
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}\rightarrow\mathbf{K}_{3\lceil{\sqrt{n}\rceil}}
, where
n

is the number of vertices of
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
.

Proof.

Fix a graph
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
with vertex-set
V=[n]
.
Let
t=\lceil{\sqrt{n}}\rceil
. Assume
that
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}\leq_{4}\mathbf{K}_{3}
. We prove, by induction on
m
, that for
every
Y\subseteq V
with
|Y|\leq mt
, we
have
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{Y}\rightarrow\mathbf{K}_{t+2m}
.
Since
t+2\lceil{n/t}\rceil\leq 3t
, the claim will follow by
setting
m=\lceil{n/t}\rceil
and
Y=V
.

Fix
m
and
Y\subseteq V
with
|Y|\leq mt
. If
m=1

or
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{Y}
has all vertices of degree less than
t
, then
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{Y}

is
t
-colorable,
so
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{Y}\rightarrow\mathbf{K}_{t}\rightarrow\mathbf{K}_{t+2m}
and
the claim is proved. Assume then that
m>1
and
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{Y}
has some
vertex
v\in Y
of degree at least
t
.
Let
N\subseteq Y\setminus\{v\}
be the neighborhood of
v

in
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{Y}
. Since
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}\leq_{4}\mathbf{K}_{3}
,
also
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{N\cup\{v\}}\leq_{4}\mathbf{K}_{3}
,
implying
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{N}\leq_{3}\mathbf{K}_{2}
. But then (see Example
2
)
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{N}

is
2
-colorable and hence
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{N\cup\{v\}}
is
3
-colorable.
Let

be a 3-coloring
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{N\cup\{v\}}
using the
colors
\{a,a+1,a+2\}
, where

and
h_{v}(v)=a
.
Let
X=Y\setminus(N\cup\{v\})
.
Then
|X|<|Y|-t\leq(m-1)t
and hence, by induction

\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{K}_{t+2(m-1)}
. Let
g
be a
homomorphism from
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{X}
to
\mathbf{K}_{t+2m-2}
and note
that

is a homomorphism from
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{Y}

to
\mathbf{K}_{t+2m}
; to see this, observe that the only possible
color in
\operatorname{img}(g)\cap\operatorname{img}(h_{v})
is

, but

is not
adjacent in
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{Y}
to any vertex in
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{X}
,
since

by choice of

. The proof is
complete.
∎

In the rest of section we study the optimal width
k=k(n)
that
guarantees that, for any graph
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
with
n
vertices it holds
that
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}\leq_{k(n)}\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{3}
implies
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}\to\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{O(n^{\epsilon})}
for
arbitrary but fixed
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
.

As a first observation, it is straightforward to show
that
k(n)\leq\lceil{n^{1-\epsilon}}\rceil
suffices; i.e., for
every graph
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
with
n
vertices,
if
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}\leq_{\lceil{n^{1-\epsilon}}\rceil}\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{3}
,
then
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}\to\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{3\lceil{n^{\epsilon}}\rceil}
. Indeed, if
V
denotes
the set of vertices of
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}
, then one just selects a
subset
X\subseteq V
of
\lceil{n^{1-\epsilon}}\rceil
many
vertices, properly colors
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{X}
with three colors using the
assumption that
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}\leq_{\lceil{n^{1-\epsilon}}\rceil}\operatorname{{\bf K}}_{3}
, and
proceeds to the rest of the graph
\operatorname{\mathbf{G}}|_{V\setminus X}
with new
colors. Overall this uses at most
3\lceil{n^{\epsilon}}\rceil
colors.
We show that, by generalizing Wigderson’s method, this width upper
bound of
O(n^{1-\epsilon})
can be improved
to
O(n^{1-2\epsilon})
. This builds on some of the ideas of
Blum
[

, who showed that Wigderson’s algorithm can be
improved to

-color

-colorable graphs in polynomial
time. Note that we do not achieve polynomial time, only sublinear
width, but we also show that this is necessary: for
any
\gamma<1-3\epsilon

does
not

suffice. Note the slight gap between the
1-2\epsilon
in the upper
bound and the
1-3\epsilon
in the lower bound.

Theorem 6
.

Fix a
real

and an integer function

such
that

6.3
Discussion and an open problem

Some discussion on the runtime of the algorithm in
Theorem
7
is in order. On one hand, the simple
observation we made just before the statement of
Theorem
7
that width
\lceil{n^{1-\epsilon}}\rceil

suffices already gives a very simple algorithm that
properly
\Theta(n^{\epsilon})
-colors
3
-colorable graphs with
n

vertices in subexponential
2^{\Theta(n^{1-\epsilon})}
-time. The
algorithm of Theorem
7
is only slightly more
complicated and asymptotically beats this. On the other hand, using
more sophisticated techniques, it was shown in
[
4
]

that, for any desired approximation factor
f
, there is
a
2^{\tilde{\Theta}(n/(f\log(f)+f\log(f)^{2}))}
-time randomized
algorithm that approximates the chromatic number of a graph with
n

vertices within a factor of
f
. For
f=\Theta(n^{\epsilon})
, this
gives a
2^{\Theta(n^{1-\epsilon-o(1)})}
-time randomized algorithm
for the problem of
\Theta(n^{\epsilon})
-coloring
3
-colorable graphs
with
n
vertices. Interestingly, the simple width-based algorithm
from Theorem
7
also beats this, and is deterministic
(but of course it applies only to our problem and not to the more
general problem of approximating the chromatic number).

Whether the runtime
2^{\Theta(n^{1-2\epsilon}\log(n))}
of
Theorem
7
can be beaten is an interesting question
left open by our work. Our width lower bound of
n^{1-3\epsilon}
has
as a consequence that
2^{\Omega(n^{1-3\epsilon})}
appears to be a
lower limit on the runtime of any width-based algorithm. The obstacle
to improving the width lower bound from
n^{1-3\epsilon}

to
n^{1-2\epsilon}
is Condition C1’, which is an improvement for the
special case of graphs over Condition C1 of the general case. Ideally,
Condition C1’ should be improved further to Condition C1” defined
as
0<\delta<1
. We do not know if this is possible; we leave it
as an open problem.
