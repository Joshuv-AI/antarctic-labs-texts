Abstract
In this paper we study the inverse spectral problem for Jacobi-type pencils. By a Jacobi-type pencil we mean the following pencil J 5 − λ ​ J 3 J_{5}-\lambda J_{3} , where J 3 J_{3} is a Jacobi matrix and J 5 J_{5} is a semi-infinite real symmetric five-diagonal matrix with positive numbers on the second subdiagonal. In the case of a special perturbation of orthogonal polynomials on a finite interval the corresponding spectral function takes an explicit form.

Introduction

The theory of operator pencils in Banach spaces has many applications and it is actively developing nowadays, see
[
7
,
8
,
10
]
. By operator pencils or operator polynomials one means polynomials of complex variable
\lambda
whose coefficients are linear bounded operators acting in a Banach space
X

\displaystyle L(\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\lambda^{j}A_{j},

where
A_{j}\colon X\rightarrow X
,
j=0,\dots,m
. As it was noted in Rodman’s book
[
10
]
, there exist three broad topics in this theory which are the main foci of interest:

(a)

linearization, i.e., reduction to a linear polynomial;

(b)

various types of factorizations;

(c)

the problems of multiple completness of eigenvectors and generalized eigenvectors.

If
X
is a Hilbert space and all operators
A_{j}
are self-adjoint, the pencil
L(\lambda)
is said to be self-adjoint. In the case
m=1
(
m=2
) the pencil is called linear (respectively quadratic). For the recent progress on quadratic operator pencils and their applications we refer to the book of Möller and Pivovarchik
[
8
]
.

Linear self-adjoint operator pencils has no spectral theory which could be compatible with the theory of single or commuting self-adjoint operators. The generalized eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle(A_{0}+\lambda A_{1})x=0,\qquad x\in X,

is essentially more generic than the usual eigenvalue problem for a self-adjoint operator. We can explain this difference by the following astonishing result from the theory of linear matrix pencils.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
9
, Theorem 15.2.1, p. 341]
)
.

Any real square matrix
B
can be written as
B=AM^{-1}
or
B=M^{-1}A
where
A
and
M
are suitable symmetric matrices.

Nevertheless, the general spectral theory for linear operator pencils has old and new contributions. In particular, it has applications to quotients of bounded operators and to the study of electron waveguides in graphene, see
[
5
]
and references therein.

Recently, Ben Amara, Vladimirov and Shkalikov investigated the following linear pencil of differential operators
[
2
]

\displaystyle(py^{\prime\prime})^{\prime\prime}-\lambda(-y^{\prime\prime}+cry)=0.

(1.1)

The initial conditions are
y(0)=y^{\prime}(0)=y(1)=y^{\prime}(1)=0
, or
y(0)=y^{\prime}(0)=y^{\prime}(1)=(py^{\prime\prime})^{\prime}(1)+\lambda\alpha y(1)=0
. Here
p,r\in C[0,1]
are uniformly positive, while the parameters
c
and
\alpha
are real. Equation (
1.1
) has several physical applications, including a motion of a partially fixed bar with additional constraints in the elasticity theory
[
2
]
.

It turns out that equation (
1.1
) is related to certain generalized orthogonal polynomials, just as the Sturm–Liouville differential operator is related to the orthogonal polynomials on the real line (OPRL).

The theory of orthogonal polynomials has numerous old and new contributions and applications, see
[
4
,
6
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
This theory is closely related to spectral problems for Jacobi matrices. The direct and inverse spectral problems for Jacobi matrices (with matrix elements) were described, e.g., in
[
1
,
3
]
. For various other types of spectral problems we refer to historical notes in
[
15
]
. Recall the following basic definition from
[
16
]
.

Definition 1.2
.

A set
\Theta=(J_{3},J_{5},\alpha,\beta)
, where
\alpha>0
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
J_{3}
is a Jacobi matrix and
J_{5}
is a semi-infinite real symmetric five-diagonal matrix with positive numbers on the second subdiagonal, is said to be
a Jacobi-type pencil
(
of matrices
)
.

From this definition we see that matrices
J_{3}
and
J_{5}
have the following form

\displaystyle J_{3}=\left(\begin{matrix}b_{0}&a_{0}&0&0&0&\cdots\\
a_{0}&b_{1}&a_{1}&0&0&\cdots\\
0&a_{1}&b_{2}&a_{2}&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{matrix}\right),\qquad a_{k}>0,\quad b_{k}\in\mathbb{R},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(1.2)

\displaystyle J_{5}=\left(\begin{matrix}\alpha_{0}&\beta_{0}&\gamma_{0}&0&0&0&\cdots\\
\beta_{0}&\alpha_{1}&\beta_{1}&\gamma_{1}&0&0&\cdots\\
\gamma_{0}&\beta_{1}&\alpha_{2}&\beta_{2}&\gamma_{2}&0&\cdots\\
0&\gamma_{1}&\beta_{2}&\alpha_{3}&\beta_{3}&\gamma_{3}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{matrix}\right),\qquad\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\gamma_{n}>0,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(1.3)

With a Jacobi-type pencil of matrices
\Theta
one associates a system of polynomials
\{p_{n}(\lambda)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, such that

\displaystyle p_{0}(\lambda)=1,\qquad p_{1}(\lambda)=\alpha\lambda+\beta,

and

\displaystyle(J_{5}-\lambda J_{3})\vec{p}(\lambda)=0,

(1.4)

where
\vec{p}(\lambda)=(p_{0}(\lambda),p_{1}(\lambda),p_{2}(\lambda),\dots)^{\rm T}
. Here the superscript
{\rm T}
means the transposition. Polynomials
\{p_{n}(\lambda)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
are said to be
associated to the Jacobi-type pencil of matrices
\Theta
.

Observe that for each system of orthonormal polynomials on the real line with
p_{0}=1
one may choose
J_{3}
to be the corresponding Jacobi matrix (which elements are the recurrence coefficients),
J_{5}=J_{3}^{2}
, and
\alpha
,
\beta
being the coefficients of
p_{1}
(
p_{1}(\lambda)=\alpha\lambda+\beta
).

One can rewrite relation (
1.4
) in the scalar form

\displaystyle\gamma_{n-2}p_{n-2}(\lambda)+(\beta_{n-1}-\lambda a_{n-1})p_{n-1}(\lambda)+(\alpha_{n}-\lambda b_{n})p_{n}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+(\beta_{n}-\lambda a_{n})p_{n+1}(\lambda)+\gamma_{n}p_{n+2}(\lambda)=0,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(1.5)

where
p_{-2}(\lambda)=p_{-1}(\lambda)=0
,
\gamma_{-2}=\gamma_{-1}=a_{-1}=\beta_{-1}=0
.

Let us return to the differential equation (
1.1
) and explain how it is related to polynomials from (
1.5
). The idea is the same as in the classical case. We consider a partition of the interval
[0,1]

\displaystyle 0=x_{0}<x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{N-1}<x_{N}=1,

with a uniform step
h
. Set
y_{j}:=y(x_{j})
,
p_{j}:=p(x_{j})
,
r_{j}:=r(x_{j})
,
0\leq j\leq N
. Then we replace the derivatives in (
1.1
) by the corresponding discretizations
y^{\prime}(x_{j})\approx\frac{y_{j+1}-y_{j}}{h}
,
y^{\prime\prime}(x_{j})\approx\frac{y_{j+1}-2y_{j}+y_{j-1}}{h^{2}}
. We obtain the following equation

\displaystyle p_{j-1}y_{j-2}-2(p_{j}+p_{j-1})y_{j-1}+(p_{j+1})+4p_{j}+p_{j-1})y_{j}-2(p_{j+1}+p_{j})y_{j+1}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+p_{j+1}y_{j+2}-\lambda h^{2}\big(y_{j-1}-\big(2-h^{2}cr_{j}\big)y_{j}+y_{j+1}\big)=0.

Denote

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}=p_{j+1}+4p_{j}+p_{j-1},\qquad\beta_{j}=-2(p_{j+1}+p_{j}),\qquad\gamma_{j}=p_{j+1},

\displaystyle a_{j}=h^{2},\qquad b_{j}=\big({-}2-h^{2}cr_{j}\big)h^{2}.

Then we get

\displaystyle\gamma_{j-2}y_{j-2}+\beta_{j-1}y_{j-1}+\alpha_{j}y_{j}+\beta_{j}y_{j+1}+\gamma_{j}y_{j+2}+\lambda\big(a_{j-1}y_{j-1}+b_{j}y_{j}+a_{j}y_{j+1}\big)=0.

If we set
\widetilde{\lambda}=-\lambda
, we obtain a relation of the form (
1.5
). Thus, relation (
1.5
) forms a discrete grid model for the equation (
1.1
). The boundary conditions
y(0)=0
,
y^{\prime}(0)=0
correspond to the convention
y_{-2}=y_{-1}=0
. Instead of
[0,1]
we can consider
[0,+\infty)
. The conditions on the right end of the bar we replace by conditions on the left end which are controlled by
\alpha
and
\beta
.

In this paper we shall introduce and study the direct and inverse spectral problems for Jacobi type pencils. We shall also consider a special perturbation of the class of orthonormal polynomials on a finite real interval. In that case it is possible to obtain a more convenient integral representation for the corresponding spectral function.

Preliminaries

In this section, for the convenience of the reader, we recall basic definitions and results from
[
16
]
. Then we state the direct and inverse spectral problems for the Jacobi-type pencils. The direct problem will be solved immediately, while the inverse problem will be considered in the next section. Set

\displaystyle u_{n}:=J_{3}\vec{e}_{n}=a_{n-1}\vec{e}_{n-1}+b_{n}\vec{e}_{n}+a_{n}\vec{e}_{n+1},

\displaystyle w_{n}:=J_{5}\vec{e}_{n}=\gamma_{n-2}\vec{e}_{n-2}+\beta_{n-1}\vec{e}_{n-1}+\alpha_{n}\vec{e}_{n}+\beta_{n}\vec{e}_{n+1}+\gamma_{n}\vec{e}_{n+2},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

Here and in what follows by
\vec{e}_{k}
with negative
k
we mean (vector) zero. The following operator

\displaystyle Af=\frac{\zeta}{\alpha}(\vec{e}_{1}-\beta\vec{e}_{0})+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\xi_{n}w_{n},

\displaystyle f=\zeta\vec{e}_{0}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\xi_{n}u_{n}\in l_{2,{\rm f\/in}},\qquad\zeta,\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{C},

(2.1)

with
D(A)=l_{2,{\rm f\/in}}
is said to be
the associated operator for the Jacobi-type pencil
\Theta
. Notice that in the sums in (
2.1
) only finite number of
\xi_{n}
are nonzero. We shall always assume this in the case of elements from the linear span. In particular, we have

\displaystyle AJ_{3}\vec{e}_{n}=J_{5}\vec{e}_{n},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

and therefore

\displaystyle AJ_{3}=J_{5}.

As usual, the matrices
J_{3}
and
J_{5}
define linear operators with the domain
l_{2,{\rm f\/in}}
which we denote by the same letters.

For an arbitrary non-zero polynomial
f(\lambda)\in\mathbb{P}
of degree
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
f(\lambda)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{d}d_{k}\lambda^{k}
,
d_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
, we set
f(A)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{d}d_{k}A^{k}
. Here
A^{0}:=E|_{l_{2,{\rm f\/in}}}
. For
f(\lambda)\equiv 0
, we set
f(A)=0|_{l_{2,{\rm f\/in}}}
. The following relations hold
[
16
]

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{n}=p_{n}(A)\vec{e}_{0},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\big(p_{n}(A)\vec{e}_{0},p_{m}(A)\vec{e}_{0}\big)_{l_{2}}=\delta_{n,m},\qquad n,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(2.3)

Denote by
\{r_{n}(\lambda)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
,
r_{0}(\lambda)=1
, the system of polynomials satisfying

\displaystyle J_{3}\vec{r}(\lambda)=\lambda\vec{r}(\lambda),\qquad\vec{r}(\lambda)=(r_{0}(\lambda),r_{1}(\lambda),r_{2}(\lambda),\dots)^{\rm T}.

(2.4)

These polynomials are orthonormal on the real line with respect to a (possibly non-unique) non-negative finite measure
\sigma
on
\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R})
(Favard’s theorem). Consider the following operator

\displaystyle U\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\xi_{n}\vec{e}_{n}=\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\xi_{n}r_{n}(x)\right],\qquad\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{C},

(2.5)

which maps
l_{2,{\rm f\/in}}
onto
\mathcal{P}
. Here by
\mathcal{P}
we denote a set of all (classes of equivalence which contain) polynomials in
L^{2}_{\sigma}
. Denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}=UAU^{-1}.

(2.6)

The operator
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}
is said to be
the model representation in
L^{2}_{\sigma}
of the associated operator
A
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
16
]
)
.

Let
\Theta=(J_{3},J_{5},\alpha,\beta)
be a Jacobi-type pencil. Let
\{r_{n}(\lambda)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
,
r_{0}(\lambda)=1
, be a system of polynomials satisfying (
2.4
) and
\sigma
be their
(
arbitrary
)
orthogonality measure on
\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R})
. The associated polynomials
\{p_{n}(\lambda)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
satisfy the following relations

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{n}(\mathcal{A})(1)\overline{p_{m}(\mathcal{A})(1)}{\rm d}\sigma=\delta_{n,m},\qquad n,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(2.7)

where
\mathcal{A}
is the model representation in
L^{2}_{\sigma}
of the associated operator
A
.

We have now recalled all basic notations and results which we shall need in the present paper. We introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\Theta=(J_{3},J_{5},\alpha,\beta)
be a Jacobi-type pencil and
\{p_{n}(\lambda)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be the associated polynomials to
\Theta
. A sesquilinear functional
S(u,v)
,
u,v\in\mathbb{P}
, satisfying the following relation

\displaystyle S(p_{n},p_{m})=\delta_{n,m},\qquad n,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

is said to be
the spectral function of the Jacobi-type pencil
\Theta
.

In a usual manner, we may introduce the corresponding direct and inverse spectral problems.
The direct spectral problem for a Jacobi-type pencil
\Theta
consists in searching for answers on the following questions:

1)

Does the spectral function exist?

2)

If the spectral function exists, is it unique?

3)

If the spectral function exists, how to find it (or them)?

By relation (
2.3
) we see that the spectral function always exists. By the linearity it follows that the spectral function is unique. It has the following representation

\displaystyle S(u,v)=\big(u(A)\vec{e}_{0},v(A)\vec{e}_{0}\big)_{l_{2}},\qquad u,v\in\mathbb{P},

(2.8)

where
A
is the associated operator for
\Theta
. By (
2.8
) we obtain that

\displaystyle\overline{S(u,v)}=S(v,u),\qquad u,v\in\mathbb{P},

(2.9)

and

\displaystyle S(u,u)\geq 0,\qquad u\in\mathbb{P}.

(2.10)

Moreover, the following integral representation holds

\displaystyle S(u,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(\mathcal{A}_{\sigma})(1)\overline{v(\mathcal{A}_{\sigma})(1)}{\rm d}\sigma,\qquad u,v\in\mathbb{P}.

(2.11)

Thus, the direct spectral problem for
\Theta
is solved in full.

The inverse spectral problem for a Jacobi-type pencil
\Theta
consists in searching for answers on the following questions:

(a)

Is it possible to reconstruct the Jacobi-type pencil
\Theta=(J_{3},J_{5},\alpha,\beta)
using its spectral function? If it is possible, what is the procedure of the reconstruction?

(b)

What are necessary and sufficient conditions for a sesquilinear functional
\sigma(u,v)
,
u,v\in\mathbb{P}
, to be the spectral function of a Jacobi-type pencil
\Theta=(J_{3},J_{5},\alpha,\beta)
?

Questions (a) and (b) will be investigated in the next section.

3
The inverse spectral problem for a Jacobi-type pencil

An answer to the question (b) of the inverse spectral problem is given by the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

A sesquilinear functional
S(u,v)
,
u,v\in\mathbb{P}
, satisfying relations (
2.9
), (
2.10
), is the spectral function of a Jacobi-type pencil if and only if it admits the following integral representation

\displaystyle S(u,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(\mathcal{A})(1)\overline{v(\mathcal{A})(1)}{\rm d}\sigma,\qquad u,v\in\mathbb{P},

(3.1)

where
\sigma
is a non-negative measure on
\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R})
with all finite power moments,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\sigma=1,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g(x)|^{2}{\rm d}\sigma>0,

for any non-zero complex polynomial
g
, and
\mathcal{A}
is a linear operator in
L^{2}_{\sigma}
with the following properties:

(i)

D(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{P}
;

(ii)

for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}x^{k}=\xi_{k,k+1}x^{k+1}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\xi_{k,j}x^{j},

(3.2)

where
\xi_{k,k+1}>0
,
\xi_{k,j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
0\leq j\leq k
;

(iii)

the operator
\mathcal{A}\Lambda_{0}
is symmetric, here by
\Lambda_{0}
we denote the operator of the multiplication by an independent variable in
L^{2}_{\sigma}
restricted to
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Necessity.
Let
\Theta=(J_{3},J_{5},\alpha,\beta)
be a Jacobi-type pencil and
A
be the associated operator of
\Theta
. Define polynomials
\{r_{n}(\lambda)\}
, the measure
\sigma
, the operators
U
and
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}
as in the introduction, see (
2.4
), (
2.5
), (
2.6
). Let
S(u,v)
be the spectral function of the pencil
\Theta
. It has an integral representation (
2.11
). It remains to check that
\sigma
and
\mathcal{A}
possess all the required properties in the statement of the theorem.

Since
\sigma
is generated by the Jacobi matrix
J_{3}
, it has all required properties as in the statement of the theorem. The operator
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}
is linear and defined on
\mathcal{P}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The associated operator
A
of a Jacobi-type pencil
\Theta
has the following properties:

1)

for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
it holds

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{n}=d_{n,n}A^{n}\vec{e}_{0}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}d_{n,j}A^{j}\vec{e}_{0},

(3.3)

where
d_{n,n}>0
,
d_{n,j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
0\leq j\leq n-1
;

2)

for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
it holds

\displaystyle A^{n}\vec{e}_{0}=c_{n,n}\vec{e}_{n}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_{n,j}\vec{e}_{j},

(3.4)

where
c_{n,n}>0
,
c_{n,j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
0\leq j\leq n-1
;

3)

for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
it holds

\displaystyle A\vec{e}_{n}=f_{n,n+1}\vec{e}_{n+1}+\sum_{j=0}^{n}f_{n,j}\vec{e}_{j},

(3.5)

where
f_{n,n+1}>0
,
f_{n,j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
0\leq j\leq n
.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

Let
p_{n}(\lambda)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}d_{n,j}\lambda^{j}
,
d_{n,n}>0
,
d_{n,j}\in\mathbb{R}
, be the associated polynomials of the pencil. By (
2.2
) we conclude that relation (
3.3
) holds.

Let us check relation (
3.4
) by the induction argument. For the cases
n=0,1
relation (
3.4
) obviously holds, see the definition of
A
. Assume that (
3.4
) holds for
n=1,2,\dots,r
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
. By (
3.3
