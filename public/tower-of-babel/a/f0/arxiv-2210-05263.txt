Classical Nucleation Theory for Active Fluid Phase Separation
M.E. Cates, C. Nardini

Abstract
Classical nucleation theory (CNT), linking rare nucleation events to the free energy landscape of a growing nucleus, is central to understanding phase-change kinetics in passive fluids. Nucleation in non-equilibrium systems is much harder to describe because there is no free energy, but instead a dynamics-dependent quasi-potential that typically must be found numerically. Here we extend CNT to a class of active phase separating systems governed by a minimal field-theoretic model (Active Model B+). In the small noise and supersaturation limits that CNT assumes, we compute analytically the quasi-potential, and hence nucleation barrier, for liquid-vapor phase separation. Crucially to our results, detailed balance, although broken microscopically by activity, is restored along the instanton trajectory, which in CNT involves the nuclear radius as the sole reaction coordinate.



Active fluids dissipate energy at the microscale: each constituent particle extracts energy from the environment and uses it to overcome frictional or viscous drag and create motion
Ramaswamy 2017
;
Marchetti et al. 2013
. Phase separation is ubiquitous in active systems: as in equilibrium, it can stem from attractive forces

;
Alarcón et al. 2017

Beysens et al. 2000
;
Nagai and Honda 2001
;
Sussman et al. 2018
. Phase separation can also emerge for purely repulsive motile particles

;
Cates and Tailleur 2015
;
Fily and Marchetti 2012
, a situation with no equilibrium counterpart. Recently it was shown that active phase separation can displays non-equilibrium features at the macroscopic scale, such as negative surface tensions
Fausti et al. 2021
;
Tjhung et al. 2018
;
Bialké et al. 2015
, mesoscopic currents in the steady state
Tjhung et al. 2018
;
Stenhammar et al. 2014
;
Caporusso et al. 2020
;
Singh and Cates 2019
, or highly dynamical clustering
Theurkauff et al. 2012
;
Buttinoni et al. 2013
;
Van Der Linden et al. 2019
. Below we address the simplest case where the active system undergoes bulk fluid-fluid phase separation. Although at first sight this resembles closely the equilibrium case
Speck et al. 2014
;
Tailleur and Cates 2008
;
Fodor et al. 2016
;
Farage et al. 2015
;
Maggi et al. 2015
;
Szamel 2016
;
Nardini et al. 2017
, detailed balance remains broken mesoscopically in the presence of density gradients

;
Martin et al. 2021
. In phase-field type models, the resulting interfacial activity alters the binodal densities at coexistence

;
Solon et al. 2018
. It must likewise be accounted for to properly define the pressure in particle-based models
Solon et al. 2015
.

A crucial feature of phase-separating systems is homogeneous nucleation, a rare event causing the formation of a distinct phase by growth of a nucleus within the bulk of a metastable parent phase. This growth is driven by noise until a critical radius is reached whereafter it proceeds spontaneously.
In passive fluids, Classical Nucleation Theory (CNT)

;
Oxtoby 1992
states that the probability of nucleating a liquid droplet in a vapor with supersaturation
\epsilon
is given, within the large deviations limit of low temperature

\mathbb{P}\asymp\exp\left(-U_{eq}(R_{c})/k_{B}T\right)
. Here,
\asymp
stands for logarithmic equivalence
Touchette 2009
and

is the Boltzmann constant. In three spatial dimensions, the free energy barrier is given by

\displaystyle U_{eq}(R_{c})=\frac{4\pi}{3}\sigma_{eq}R_{c,eq}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(R_{c},T)\qquad d=3\,

(1)

R_{c,eq}=2\sigma_{eq}/(f^{\prime}(\phi_{s})\Delta\phi-\Delta f)
and
\sigma_{eq}
is the surface tension of the interface. Here,
\Delta\phi=\phi_{2}-\phi_{1}
and

CNT holds for small supersaturation (
\epsilon\ll|\phi_{1}|

The vast literature on CNT has

aimed at testing it experimentally and numerically

;
Karthika et al. 2016

; at describing systems where multiple pathways to nucleation are present

It has been suggested that CNT might be extended to address nucleation in phase-separating active systems

;
Redner et al. 2016
;
Levis et al. 2017

Redner et al. 2016

Classical nucleation theory is one prominent instance of large deviation theory (LDT)
Touchette 2009
;
Bertini et al. 2015

and physical chemistry

Ravelet et al. 2004

In thermal equilibrium systems, event rates can be found from the free energy barrier,

via (
1

(although dynamical methods can also be used

;

The situation is very different in non-equilibrium systems such as active matter. Within LDT

;

;

Computing the quasi-potential and/or instanton represents an intrinsically dynamical problem which, even in the small noise limit of LDT, is rarely achievable analytically. (Only for a few minimal models was the quasi-potential found either exactly

;

;
Mallick et al. 2022

;

;

;
Ferré and Touchette 2018
;
Yan et al. 2022
;
Zakine and Vanden-Eijnden 2022

;

;

In this Letter we extend CNT to active fluid phase separation, using statistical field theory. We can thereby access analytically nucleation rates and quasi-potentials for a generic class of non-equilibrium, many-body systems. This is possible because, although activity breaks detailed balance, this is restored along the instanton trajectory, which in CNT involves a single reaction coordinate (the droplet radius) with noise that we infer from the infinite-dimensional Langevin equation for the order parameter field. Our results are given for Active Model B+ (AMB+)
Nardini et al. 2017
;
Tjhung et al. 2018
, a canonical field theory for active phase separation. However, the analysis route just outlined should be open whenever CNT’s precept of a single reaction coordinate is applicable.

The theory derived in this Letter describes nucleation in AMB+ so long as the active surface tension
\sigma
and the capillary wave tension
\sigma_{cw}
are positive. Sufficiently high activity can cause capillary waves to become unstable:
\sigma_{cw}<0
is indeed found at
\lambda,\zeta
sufficiently large and positive
Fausti et al. 2021
, not shown in Fig.
2
. Also, sufficiently strong activity can turn the Ostwald process into reverse while
\sigma_{cw}>0
:
\sigma<0
is indeed found at
\zeta,\lambda
sufficiently negative for liquid droplets (or positive for vapor bubbles)
Tjhung et al. 2018
. When this happens,
R_{c}(\epsilon)
is a stable fixed point for the relaxational dynamics: a droplet grows (shrinks) if it is initially smaller (larger) than
R_{c}
. In this case the critical radius for nucleation is not
R_{c}
but expected to be much smaller. Such nucleation might perhaps be captured by including the terms of order
R_{t}^{-3}
in (
11
): we leave this idea to future work.

In conclusion, we have extended classical nucleation theory to address phase separation in active fluids. It is very unusual, if not unique, for the probability of rare fluctuations to be obtained analytically in a strongly nonequilibrium system, with continuously many degrees of freedom, as done here. This was achieved because detailed balance, although violated in the full dynamics, is restored at large deviation level for the reaction coordinate, as shown by explicit construction of the Langevin equation for droplet size (
11
). A key element to this was to compute the noise in this equation, which we found to be unaffected by the active terms although these drastically alter the (quasi-)potential landscape.

Our results were derived within AMB+, a canonical field theory for phase separation in active fluids. More generally, the techniques developed here could help elucidate the nucleation dynamics in more specific active models, by addressing field theories obtained by explicit coarse-graining
Tjhung et al. 2018
;
Solon et al. 2018
;
Bickmann and Wittkowski 2020
. Our techniques might also help in understanding nucleation in the presence of non-equilibrium chemical reactions
Ziethen et al. 2022
;
Cho and Jacobs 2022
, relevant for describing the inner structure of cells
Hyman et al. 2014
;
Li and Cates 2020
or when the density evolution is coupled to other slow fields
Tiribocchi et al. 2015
;
Singh and Cates 2019
;
Tjhung et al. 2012
. More speculatively, our results might be a step towards understanding nucleation, considered a key ingredient of cancer mestastasis
Basan et al. 2009
, in biological tissues.

Confirming numerically our analytical results poses a significant challenge; this is because CNT is valid in the regime where the nucleation barrier is much larger than the noise amplitude (
U(R_{c})\gg D
) and the supersaturation is small, so that the nucleation rate is also very small and the critical radius is large. Even for passive fluids, where CNT has been widely verified both experimentally and in particle models
Oxtoby 1992
;
Karthika et al. 2016
,
we are not aware of any computational work on Model B that addressed nucleation in the CNT regime. Such a challenge can likely be addressed by employing recently developed algorithms dedicated to sample rare events in systems far from equilibrium
Grafke and Vanden-Eijnden 2019
;
Nemoto and Sasa 2014
;
Ferré and Touchette 2018
;
Yan et al. 2022
;
Zakine and Vanden-Eijnden 2022
;
Kikuchi et al. 2020
; in fact, our exact results create a potential benchmark for these codes. A key question is whether algorithms can be created to automatically identify a low dimensional subspace of one or more reaction coordinates, without relying on detailed mechanistic analysis of the type presented above.

As discussed in

\displaystyle\mu_{\zeta}=-\zeta\int d\mathbf{x}_{1}\nabla\cdot(\nabla^{2}\phi\nabla\phi)(\mathbf{x}_{1})G(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{1})

\displaystyle\dot{R}_{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,\psi_{R_{t}}^{\prime}(\nabla^{-2}\varphi_{R_{t}}^{\prime})=

A.3
Noise term

We compute here the statistics of the noise entering Equation (11). To do so we start from defining

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,\psi^{\prime}_{R_{t}}\nabla^{-2}\chi

(23)

where we recall that
\chi
is a Gaussian white noise with zero average and variance

\displaystyle\langle\chi(r_{1},t_{1})\chi(r_{2},t_{2})\rangle=-\frac{2D}{S_{3}}\nabla^{2}\left(\frac{\delta(r_{1}-r_{2})}{r_{1}^{2}}\right)\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\,.

Because

is also Gaussian with zero average. We are thus left with computing its correlations. Integrating by parts, it is easy to show that

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\chi}(t_{1})\bar{\chi}(t_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2D}{S_{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,\psi^{\prime}_{R_{t}}(r-R_{t})

\displaystyle\nabla^{-2}\left(\frac{\psi^{\prime}_{R_{t}}(r-R_{t})}{r^{2}}\right)\,\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\,.

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\chi}(t_{1})\bar{\chi}(t_{2})\rangle=\frac{2D}{S_{3}R_{t}}

\displaystyle\left\{2\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,\psi^{\prime}_{R_{t}}\psi_{R_{t}}+2\psi_{+}\delta\psi\right\}\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\,+\mathcal{O}(R_{t}^{-2})

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\chi}(t_{1})\bar{\chi}(t_{2})\rangle=\frac{2D}{S_{3}R_{t}}(\delta\psi)^{2}\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\,+\mathcal{O}(R_{t}^{-2})\,.

(25)

A.4

We show here that, in the limit of small supersaturation, the critical radius in Equation (9) reduces to

\displaystyle R_{c}=\frac{(d-1)\sigma}{\epsilon\Delta\psi f^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{1})}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{0},1/(\epsilon R_{t}))\,.

Using the definitions of the transformed variables

\displaystyle\psi(\phi_{\pm})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi(\phi_{1,2})+\frac{\alpha_{\pm}}{R_{t}}\left.\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\phi}\right|_{\phi=\phi_{1,2}}+\mathcal{O}(R_{t}^{-2})

\displaystyle g(\phi_{\pm})

\displaystyle g(\phi_{1,2})+\frac{\alpha_{\pm}}{R_{t}}\left.\frac{\partial g}{\partial\phi}\right|_{\phi=\phi_{1,2}}+\mathcal{O}(R_{t}^{-2})

We can now expand

Appendix C
Expression for the surface tension
\sigma

A simple numerical procedure to obtain the binodals
\phi_{1,2}
and the interfacial tension

The binodals

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\phi_{1})=\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=f^{\prime}(\phi_{2})

(32)

\displaystyle(\mu\psi-g)_{1}=(\mu\psi-g)_{2}\,

(33)

where the explicit expression of
\psi
is given in the main text.
The transformed variable

is defined by
\partial g/\partial\psi=\partial f/\partial\phi

when

\displaystyle g(\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{a}{\bar{\zeta}^{2}}+\frac{6b}{\bar{\zeta}^{4}}+a\frac{e^{\bar{\zeta}\phi}}{\bar{\zeta}^{3}}\bar{\zeta}(1-\bar{\zeta}\phi)

\displaystyle b\frac{e^{\bar{\zeta}\phi}}{\bar{\zeta}^{4}}[\bar{\zeta}^{3}\phi^{3}-3\bar{\zeta}^{2}\phi^{2}+6\bar{\zeta}\phi-6]

Although an analytic solution for

We then show that

\displaystyle\sigma=\int_{\phi_{1}}^{\phi_{2}}d\varphi\,\sqrt{w(\varphi)}\left\{K-\zeta\left[\psi(\varphi)-\psi({\varphi_{2}})\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\mu_{1}[\psi-\psi(\phi_{1})]

\displaystyle g-g(\phi_{1})

Using the definition of the transformed variables to evaluate the last integral and solving for
