A randomized small-block Lanczos method for large-scale null space computations
Daniel Kressner, Nian Shao

Abstract
Computing the null space of a large sparse matrix A A is a challenging computational problem, especially if the nullity—the dimension of the null space—is large. When using a block Lanczos method for this purpose, conventional wisdom suggests to use a block size d d that is not smaller than the nullity. In this work, we show how randomness can be utilized to allow for smaller d d without sacrificing convergence or reliability. Even d = 1 d=1 , corresponding to the standard single-vector Lanczos method, becomes a safe choice. This is achieved by using a small random diagonal perturbation, which moves the zero eigenvalues of A 𝖳 ​ A A^{\mathsf{T}}A away from each other, and a random initial guess. We analyze the effect of the perturbation on the attainable quality of the null space and derive convergence results that establish robust convergence for d = 1 d=1 . As demonstrated by our numerical experiments, a smaller block size combined with restarting and partial reorthogonalization results in reduced memory requirements and computational effort. It also allows for the incremental computation of the null space, without requiring a priori knowledge of the nullity.

Introduction

This work is concerned with computing the null space of a large sparse matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, a problem
that finds numerous applications. For example, the connected components of an undirected graph can be identified by the null space of the graph Laplacian
[
18

. In computational topology, the null space of cohomology matrices reflects the generators of the cohomology groups. The nullity helps determine the Betti numbers of a topological space
[
7
]
. Furthermore, null space calculations play a crucial role in various engineering problems, such as system dimensionality reduction
[
34
]
, finite element models
[
29
]
, linear equality constrained state estimation
[
13
]
, grasp control
[
25
]
, computer vision
[
36
]
, and multivariate polynomial root finding
[
2
]
.

In several of these applications, the dimension of the null space is not necessarily small and can easily reach a few hundred or even thousands.
For small- to medium-sized matrices, the singular value decomposition (SVD)
[
10
]
or a suitably modified QR decomposition
[
8
]
are the methods of choice for
computing null spaces. For large-sized matrices, these dense methods become too expensive. There are several structured null space solvers to deal with specific large-scale situations, such as
[
3
]
for banded matrices,
[
11
]
for matrices with small nullity and amenable to sparse LU factorization with partial pivoting,
[
9
]
for sparse matrices amenable to a rank-revealing sparse QR factorization
[
4
]
, and
[
22
]
for a very tall and skinny matrix.

When
A
is large and has no particular structure beyond being sparse, one needs to resort to Krylov subspace methods, which utilize matrix-vector products with
A
and its transpose
A^{\mathsf{T}}
. Let
N
denote the null space dimension of
A
. When
N=1
, standard choices include the Lanczos method for computing the smallest eigenvalue and eigenvector of
A^{\mathsf{T}}A
or the mathematically equivalent, but numerically more reliable

A

[
10
, Chapter 10]
. When
N>1
, the Lanczos method (with a single starting vector) becomes unreliable and prone to miss components of the null space. In fact, in exact arithmetic only a one-dimensional subspace of the null space can be extracted via Lanczos
[
23
, Section 12.2]
. Block Lanczos methods address this issue by utilizing a block of
d>1
starting vectors instead of a single starting vector
[
23
, Section 13.10]
.
Common wisdom (see
[
23
, Section 13.12]
for examples) and existing convergence analyses all suggest to choose

not smaller than the size of the eigenvalue cluster of interest. Applied to null space computation this would require

and in exact arithmetic this condition is indeed necessary. The presence of round-off error blurs the situation.

Experiments with other matrices lead to similar findings, suggesting that round-off error breaks some of the curse incurred by eigenvalue clusters, but it does not fully address the convergence issues of single-vector or small-block Lanczos methods either.

The situation described above is reminiscent of recent work by Meyer, Musco and Musco
[
19
]
on the single-vector Lanczos method for low-rank approximation. Taking the randomness of the starting vector into account they establish a convergence result that still requires the (larger) singular values to be distinct but the dependence of the complexity on the gaps between eigenvalues is very mild, in fact logarithmic. Although single-vector or small-block Krylov subspace methods do not satisfy gap-independent convergence bounds

Inspired by
[
19
]
, we propose a randomized small-block Lanczos method for null space calculation, outlined in

First, in low-rank approximation, the largest few singular values are usually unknown and, in most applications, they do not form tight large clusters. In the context of null spaces, the desired singular values form one large cluster of zeros. Second, while convergence to the relevant invariant subspace is not necessary for low-rank approximation
[
6
]
, such convergence is imperative to obtain a good null space approximation.

Proposition 5

\lVert V_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}B_{0}V_{N}\rVert\leq\lVert V_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}BV_{N}\rVert=\lambda_{N}\leq\lambda_{N}^{(0)}+\lVert B-B_{0}\rVert,

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\min}\big(V_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}B_{0}V_{\perp}\big)-\lambda_{N}^{(0)}\geq\lambda_{\min}\big(V_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}BV_{\perp}\big)-\lambda_{N}^{(0)}-\|B-B_{0}\|

\displaystyle=\lambda_{N+1}-\lambda_{N}^{(0)}-\|B-B_{0}\|\geq\lambda_{N+1}^{(0)}-\lambda_{N}^{(0)}-\|B-B_{0}\|>0.

2.3
Principal angles and null space residual

To study the impact of perturbations on subspaces, we recall the definition and basic properties of principal angles. For later purposes, it will be helpful to admit subspaces of unequal dimension, as in
[
10

Definition 7
.

Let the columns of
V_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k_{0}}
and
V_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k_{1}}
form orthonormal bases of subspaces
\mathcal{V}_{0}
and

\mathcal{V}_{1}
, respectively. Let
k=\min\{k_{0},k_{1}\}
and let
1\geq\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\dotsb\geq\sigma_{k}\geq 0
denote the singular values of
V_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}V_{1}
.
Then the principal angles
\angle_{t}(V_{0},V_{1})\in[0,\pi/2]
,
t=1,\dotsc,k
, between
\mathcal{V}_{0}
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}
are defined by

\cos\angle_{t}(\mathcal{V}_{0},\mathcal{V}_{1})=\sigma_{t}.

For brevity, we will write
\angle_{t}(\Omega_{0},\Omega_{1})
instead of
\angle_{t}(\mathcal{V}_{0},\mathcal{V}_{1})
for arbitrary, not necessarily orthonormal, basis for
\mathcal{V}_{0}
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}
.

Note that the principal angles in
definition

7
are sorted in increasing order, and it is often the largest principal angle
\angle_{k}(V_{0},V_{1})
that is of interest. In particular, it is well known that

\sin\angle_{k}(V_{0},V_{1})=\lVert V_{0}V_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}-V_{1}V_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\rVert

holds when
k_{0}=k_{1}
, implying that the orthogonal projectors are close for small
\angle_{k}(V_{0},V_{1})
.
The other principal angles give partial information; knowing that some of them are small allows us to conclude that
\mathcal{V}_{0}
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}
nearly share a common subspace. In the context of null spaces, the following result establishes a residual bound in terms of principal angles.

Proposition 8
.

Consider the setting of
definition

7
and a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Then for every
t=1,\dotsc,k
there exists a matrix
W_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}\times t}
with orthonormal columns such that

\lVert AV_{1}W_{t}\rVert\leq\lVert AV_{0}\rVert+\lVert A\rVert\sin\angle_{t}(V_{0},V_{1}).

Proof.

Let
W_{t}
be the right singular vectors corresponding to the largest
t
singular values of
V_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}V_{1}
. Then

\displaystyle\|(I-V_{0}V_{0}^{\mathsf{T}})V_{1}W_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\|x\|=1}\|(I-V_{0}V_{0}^{\mathsf{T}})V_{1}W_{t}x\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\|x\|=1}\big(\|V_{1}W_{t}x\|^{2}-\|V_{0}V_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}V_{1}W_{t}x\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\min_{\|x\|=1}\|V_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}V_{1}W_{t}x\|^{2}=1-\sigma_{t}^{2}=\sin^{2}\angle_{t}(V_{0},V_{1}).

The proof is concluded by the triangular inequality:

\displaystyle\lVert AV_{1}W_{t}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert AV_{0}V_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}V_{1}W_{t}\rVert+\lVert A(I-V_{0}V_{0}^{\mathsf{T}})V_{1}W_{t}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert AV_{0}\rVert+\lVert A\rVert\sin\angle_{t}(V_{0},V_{1}).

3.1.1
The case
\ell=t
. Proof of

Following
[

, the ability of a (single-vector) Krylov subspace to capture a subspace is closely related to properties of Vandermonde matrices.

Lemma 12
.

For
\lambda_{1}<\dotsb<\lambda_{N}\leq\lambda_{N+1}\leq\dotsb\leq\lambda_{n}
and
1\leq t\leq N
, consider the
Vandermonde matrices

\mathsf{Van}_{N}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\lambda_{1}&\ldots&\lambda_{1}^{t-1}\\
1&\lambda_{2}&\ldots&\lambda_{2}^{t-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\lambda_{N}&\ldots&\lambda_{N}^{t-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\lambda_{N+1}&\ldots&\lambda_{N+1}^{t-1}\\
1&\lambda_{N+2}&\ldots&\lambda_{N+2}^{t-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\lambda_{n}&\ldots&\lambda_{n}^{t-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

Then

\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\mathsf{Van}_{N}^{\dagger}\rVert\leq\frac{\sqrt{n-N}}{(t-1)!}\Bigl(\frac{2}{\mathsf{relgap}}\Bigr)^{t-1}

with
\mathsf{relgap}
defined as in
eq.

7
.

Proof.

For an arbitrary vector
x=[x_{1},\dotsc,x_{t}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
, we have

\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{N}x\rVert^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p^{2}(\lambda_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}x\rVert^{2}=\sum_{j=N+1}^{n}p^{2}(\lambda_{j}),

with the polynomial
p(\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{t}x_{k}\lambda^{k-1}
. By the assumptions,

\mathsf{Van}_{N}
has full column rank and

\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\mathsf{Van}_{N}^{\dagger}\rVert^{2}=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{t}}\frac{\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}x\rVert^{2}}{\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{N}x\rVert^{2}}=\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{t-1}}\frac{\sum_{j=N+1}^{n}p^{2}(\lambda_{j})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}p^{2}(\lambda_{i})},

(12)

where
\mathcal{P}_{t-1}
denotes the vector space containing all polynomials of degree at most
t-1
. Choosing the Lagrange basis

p_{i}(\lambda)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq t\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}\frac{\lambda-\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k}}\in\mathcal{P}_{t-1},\quad i=1,\dotsc,t,

we can express an arbitrary polynomial
p\in\mathcal{P}_{t-1}
as

p(\lambda)=p(\lambda_{1})p_{1}(\lambda)+\dotsb+p(\lambda_{t})p_{t}(\lambda).

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

p^{2}(\lambda)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}p^{2}(\lambda_{i})\sum_{i=1}^{t}p_{i}^{2}(\lambda)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}p^{2}(\lambda_{i})\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\lvert p_{i}(\lambda)\rvert\Bigr)^{2}.

(13)

To process the last term, we assume that
\lambda_{N+1}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{n}
, which implies

\lvert\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k}\rvert/(\lambda-\lambda_{k})\geq\lvert i-k\rvert\cdot\mathsf{relgap}
for
1\leq i\neq k\leq t
. In turn,

\sum_{i=1}^{t}\lvert p_{i}(\lambda)\rvert=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq t\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}\frac{\lambda-\lambda_{k}}{\lvert\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k}\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{\mathsf{relgap}^{t-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq t\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lvert i-k\rvert}=\Bigl(\frac{2}{\mathsf{relgap}}\Bigr)^{t-1}\frac{1}{(t-1)!},

where the last equality utilizes the combinatorial identity

\sum_{i=1}^{t}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq t\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lvert i-k\rvert}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{1}{(i-1)!(t-i)!}=\frac{1}{(t-1)!}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\binom{t-1}{i-1}=\frac{2^{t-1}}{(t-1)!}.

Plugging this bound into (
13
) for
\lambda=\lambda_{j}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=N+1}^{n}p^{2}(\lambda_{j})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}p^{2}(\lambda_{i})}\leq\frac{n-N}{\bigl((t-1)!\bigr)^{2}}\Bigl(\frac{2}{\mathsf{relgap}}\Bigr)^{2(t-1)}.

Combined with
eq.

12
, this completes the proof.

By leveraging the randomness of
\omega

proposition

allows us to prove

Proof of

.

Because

Letting

3.1.2

Our proof crucially relies on a simple but important observation from

Lemma 13
.

For a symmetric matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\dotsb<\lambda_{N}<\lambda_{N+1}\leq\dotsb\leq\lambda_{n}
, let
V_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}
be an orthonormal basis of the invariant subspace associated with
\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{N}
. Choose integers
t,\ell
such that
1\leq t\leq N
and
t\leq\ell\leq n
.
For an
n\times t
matrix
\Omega
such that
\mathrm{rank}(V_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega)=t
, let

\tan\angle_{t}(V_{N},K)\leq\frac{\tan\angle_{t}(V_{N},\Omega)}{\mathsf{Cheb}_{\ell-t}\Bigl(1+2\frac{\lambda_{N+1}-\lambda_{N}}{\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{N+1}}\Bigr)},

(17)

where
\mathsf{Cheb}_{\ell-t}
is the Chebyshev polynomial
eq.

5
of degree
\ell-t
.

Proof.

Let
p(\lambda)=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\lambda+\dotsb\alpha_{\ell-t}\lambda^{\ell-t}
be such that
p(\lambda)\not=0
for

\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{N}
. Defining the matrix
H_{K}=[\alpha_{0}I_{t},\alpha_{1}I_{t},\dotsc,\alpha_{\ell-t}I_{t}]^{\mathsf{T}}
, we have that

V_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}KH_{K}=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-t}\alpha_{i}V_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}B^{i}\Omega=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-t}\alpha_{i}\Lambda_{N}^{i}V_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega=p(\Lambda_{N})V_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega,

and an analogous formula holds when
V_{N}
is replaced by
V_{\perp}
.
By assumption,
p(\Lambda_{N})
is invertible and thus
V_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}KH_{K}
has rank
t
. Additionally, using that

\mathrm{span}\{KH_{K}\}\subset\mathrm{span}\{K\}

and
the expression for
\tan\angle_{t}
from
proposition

9
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\tan\angle_{t}(V_{N},K)

\displaystyle\leq\tan\angle_{t}(V_{N},KH_{K})=\lVert V_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}KH_{K}(V_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}KH_{K})^{\dagger}\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert p(\Lambda_{\perp})V_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega(V_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega)^{\dagger}p(\Lambda_{N})^{-1}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\max\limits_{N+1\leq j\leq n}\lvert p(\lambda_{j})\rvert}{\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq N}\lvert p(\lambda_{i})\rvert}\lVert V_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega(V_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega)^{\dagger}\rVert=\frac{\max\limits_{N+1\leq j\leq n}\lvert p(\lambda_{j})\rvert}{\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq N}\lvert p(\lambda_{i})\rvert}\tan\angle_{t}(V_{N},\Omega).

The proof is completed by setting

and using that a Chebyshev polynomial is bounded by one in magnitude inside
[-1,1]
and monotone outside that interval.

4
Practical techniques for implementation

In this section, we discuss several improvements and implementation details to make

1

4.1
Restarting

algorithm

1
needs to store the orthonormal basis
Z_{\ell}
of the Krylov subspace to allow for reorthogonalization (see below) and Ritz vector computations. As the number of iterations
\ell
required for convergence increases, storing
Z_{\ell}
becomes challenging due to memory limitations. Additionally, the cost of Rayleigh–Ritz extraction grows significantly with
\ell
. To address these issues, we employ a restart mechanism, akin to the Krylov–Schur method
[
32
,
37
]
.

To conveniently describe our restarting mechanism, we use the superscripts
\cdot^{(\mathrm{old})}
and
\cdot^{(\mathrm{new})}
to denote variables before and after restarting, respectively.
We assume that the dimension of the Krylov subspace before restarting is
\ell d
and that

\ell_{0}d
Ritz pairs are retained after restarting, where
\ell_{0}<\ell
and
d
represents the block size of the initial matrix
\Omega
.

Suppose we have a Lanczos decomposition of the form

BZ_{\ell}^{(\mathrm{old})}=Z_{\ell}^{(\mathrm{old})}T_{\ell}^{(\mathrm{old})}+Q_{\ell+1}^{(\mathrm{old})}E_{\ell+1}^{(\mathrm{old})},

(23)

where
[Z_{\ell}^{(\mathrm{old})},Q_{\ell+1}^{(\mathrm{old})}]
is a matrix with orthonormal columns,
E_{\ell+1}^{(\mathrm{old})}=[0,\Gamma_{\ell}^{(\mathrm{old})}]
, and

T_{\ell}^{(\mathrm{old})}=\begin{bmatrix}\Theta^{(\mathrm{old})}&F^{(\mathrm{old})}&&&&\\
(F^{(\mathrm{old})})^{\mathsf{T}}&\Xi_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{old})}&\Gamma_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{old})}&&&\\
&(\Gamma_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{old})})^{\mathsf{T}}&\Xi_{\ell_{0}+2}^{(\mathrm{old})}&\Gamma_{\ell_{0}+2}^{(\mathrm{old})}&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&(\Gamma_{\ell-2}^{(\mathrm{old})})^{\mathsf{T}}&\Xi_{\ell-1}^{(\mathrm{old})}&\Gamma_{\ell-1}^{(\mathrm{old})}\\
&&&&(\Gamma_{\ell-1}^{(\mathrm{old})})^{\mathsf{T}}&\Xi_{\ell}^{(\mathrm{old})}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(24)

is a block tridiagonal matrix, partitioned such that
\Theta^{(\mathrm{old})}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{0}d\times\ell_{0}d}
contains the information from the previous cycle. To restart and keep
\ell_{0}d
Ritz vectors, we apply the Rayleigh–Ritz procedure:

T_{\ell}^{(\mathrm{old})}V_{Z}^{(\mathrm{new})}=V_{Z}^{(\mathrm{new})}\Theta^{(\mathrm{new})}\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{\ell_{0}}^{(\mathrm{new})}=Z_{\ell}^{(\mathrm{old})}V_{Z}^{(\mathrm{new})}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell_{0}d},

where
V_{Z}^{(\mathrm{new})}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell d\times\ell_{0}d}
is an orthonormal basis of the invariant subspace corresponding to the
\ell_{0}d
smallest eigenvalues of
T_{\ell}^{(\mathrm{old})}
. Multiplying
V_{Z}^{(\mathrm{new})}
from the right to the Lanczos decomposition
eq.

23
leads to

BZ_{\ell_{0}}^{(\mathrm{new})}=Z_{\ell_{0}}^{(\mathrm{new})}\Theta^{(\mathrm{new})}+Q_{\ell+1}^{(\mathrm{old})}E_{\ell+1}^{(\mathrm{old})}V_{Z}^{(\mathrm{new})}.

We restart the block Lanczos method by defining the quantities

\displaystyle Q_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}=Q_{\ell+1}^{(\mathrm{old})}\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}=[Z_{\ell_{0}}^{(\mathrm{new})},Q_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(\ell_{0}+1)d},

\displaystyle F^{(\mathrm{new})}=(E_{\ell+1}^{(\mathrm{old})}V_{Z}^{(\mathrm{new})})^{\mathsf{T}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Xi_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}=(Q_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})})^{\mathsf{T}}BQ_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})},

\displaystyle\bigl(I-Z_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}(Z_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})})^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)BQ_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}=Q_{\ell_{0}+2}^{(\mathrm{new})}\Gamma_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})},

where the last line denotes a QR decomposition. After restarting, the new Lanczos decomposition takes the form

\displaystyle BZ_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}

\displaystyle=[BZ_{\ell_{0}}^{(\mathrm{new})},BQ_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}]

\displaystyle=[Z_{\ell_{0}}^{(\mathrm{new})},Q_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}]\begin{bmatrix}\Theta^{(\mathrm{new})}&(E_{\ell+1}^{(\mathrm{old})}V_{Z}^{(\mathrm{new})})^{\mathsf{T}}\\
E_{\ell+1}^{(\mathrm{old})}V_{Z}^{(\mathrm{new})}&\Xi_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}\end{bmatrix}+Q_{\ell_{0}+2}^{(\mathrm{new})}[O,\Gamma_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}]

\displaystyle=Z_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}\begin{bmatrix}\Theta^{(\mathrm{new})}&F^{(\mathrm{new})}\\
(F^{(\mathrm{new})})^{\mathsf{T}}&\Xi_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}\end{bmatrix}+Q_{\ell_{0}+2}^{(\mathrm{new})}E_{\ell_{0}+2}^{(\mathrm{new})},

where
E_{\ell_{0}+2}^{(\mathrm{new})}=[O,\Gamma_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})}]
, and the relationship

E_{\ell+1}^{(\mathrm{old})}V_{Z}^{(\mathrm{new})}=(Q_{\ell+1}^{(\mathrm{old})})^{\mathsf{T}}(BZ_{\ell_{0}}^{(\mathrm{new})}-Z_{\ell_{0}}^{(\mathrm{new})}\Theta^{(\mathrm{new})})=(Q_{\ell_{0}+1}^{(\mathrm{new})})^{\mathsf{T}}BZ_{\ell_{0}}^{(\mathrm{new})}

is used.

4.2
Partial reorthogonalization

In floating-point arithmetic, round-off error compromises the orthogonality of the basis computed by the block Lanczos method, leading to the emergence of multiple converged Ritz values, so called ghost eigenvalues. To obtain reliable information about the null space and its dimension, it is imperative to maintain the orthogonality of Lanczos vectors with reorthogonalization strategies.

Commonly employed reorthogonalization schemes include selective reorthogonalization
[
24
]
, partial reorthogonalization
[
31
]
, and full reorthogonalization. In our implementation, we use partial reorthogonalization, which is cheaper than full reorthogonalization and more effective than selective reorthogonalization
[
30
]
. Following the strategies described in
[
33
,
12
]
, we define a
W
-recurrence to monitor loss of orthogonality. For brevity, we omit the superscripts
\cdot^{(\mathrm{old})}
and
\cdot^{(\mathrm{new})}
regarding restarting. Additionally, we focus on the reorthogonalization of the new basis vector
Q_{\ell+1}
versus
Z_{\ell}=[Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{\ell}]
.

Supposing that
\ell_{0}d
Ritz vectors were retained in the last restart, and

\Theta=\mathrm{diag}\{\Theta_{1},\dotsc,\Theta_{\ell_{0}}\}\quad\text{and}\quad F=[F_{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\dotsc,F_{\ell_{0}}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}},

the recurrence
eq.

23
in finite precision can be written as

\displaystyle BQ_{i}

\displaystyle=Q_{i}\Theta_{i}+Q_{\ell_{0}}F_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}+\Delta_{i}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq\ell_{0},

(25)

\displaystyle BQ_{\ell_{0}+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{0}}Q_{i}F_{i}+Q_{\ell_{0}+1}\Xi_{\ell_{0}+1}+Q_{\ell_{0}+2}\Gamma_{\ell_{0}+1}^{\mathsf{T}}+\Delta_{\ell_{0}+1},

\displaystyle BQ_{j}

\displaystyle=Q_{j-1}\Gamma_{j-1}+Q_{j}\Xi_{j}+Q_{j+1}\Gamma_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}+\Delta_{j}\quad\text{for}\quad\ell_{0}+2\leq j\leq\ell,

where the matrices denoted with
\Delta
account for round-off errors. Following
[
12
,
23
]
, we may assume that the matrices
Q_{k}
have orthonormal columns and

\lVert\Delta_{k}\rVert\leq d\sqrt{n}\mathbf{u}\lVert B\rVert=:\mathbf{u}_{d}

(26)

for
1\leq k\leq\ell+1
, where
\mathbf{u}
denotes the machine precision.
Different from full orthogonalization, we allow
Q_{i}
and
Q_{j}
to be not (numerically) orthogonal to each other for
i\not=j
.
The basic idea of partial reorthogonalization is to monitor this loss of orthogonality by examining
\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell+1}\rVert
for
1\leq k\leq\ell
. If this value exceeds a certain threshold
\mathbf{u}_{\mathsf{RO}}
, typically chosen as
\mathbf{u}_{\mathsf{RO}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{u}}
, we perform reorthogonalization. Because computing
\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell+1}\rVert
is computationally expensive, we instead aim to calculate and use an upper bound for this value. Reorthogonalization is not performed if the upper bound is below
\mathbf{u}_{\mathsf{RO}}
, potentially cutting down computational costs while maintaining a decent level of orthogonality.

To derive an upper bound for
\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell+1}\rVert
, we take
j=\ell
in the third recurrence in
eq.

25
. Multiplying with
Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}
from the left and rearranging terms yields the inequality

\displaystyle\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell+1}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\Gamma_{\ell}^{-1}\rVert\bigl\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}BQ_{\ell}-Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell-1}\Gamma_{\ell-1}-Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell}\Xi_{\ell}-Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{\ell}\bigr\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\Gamma_{\ell}^{-1}\rVert\bigl(\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}BQ_{\ell}\rVert+\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell-1}\rVert\lVert\Gamma_{\ell-1}\rVert+\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell}\rVert\lVert\Xi_{\ell}\rVert+\lVert Q_{k}\rVert\lVert\Delta_{\ell}\rVert\bigr).

For
1\leq k\leq\ell_{0}
, the term
\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}BQ_{\ell}\rVert
is bounded by using the symmetry of
B
and the first recurrence in
eq.

25
:

\displaystyle\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}BQ_{\ell}\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert Q_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}BQ_{k}\rVert\leq\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell}\rVert\lVert\Theta_{k}\rVert+\lVert Q_{\ell_{0}}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell}\rVert\lVert F_{k}\rVert+\lVert Q_{\ell}\rVert\lVert\Delta_{k}\rVert.

Combining these two inequalities and using
eq.

26
yields

\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell+1}\rVert\leq\lVert\Gamma_{\ell}^{-1}\rVert\bigl(\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell}\rVert(\lVert\Theta_{k}\rVert+\lVert\Xi_{\ell}\rVert)+\lVert Q_{\ell_{0}}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell}\rVert\lVert F_{k}\rVert+\lVert Q_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\ell-1}\rVert\lVert\Gamma_{\ell-1}\rVert+2\mathbf{u}_{d}\bigr).

We keep track of the recursive inequalities above using a strictly upper triangular matrix
W
, such that each entry
w_{i,j}
is an upper bound on
\lVert Q_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{j}\rVert
for
1\leq i<j\leq\ell+1
. Assuming that columns
1,\ldots,\ell
have been determined, the entries
1,\ldots,\ell
of column
\ell+1
are determined by the recurrences above as follows:

w_{k,\ell+1}=\left\{\begin{aligned} \lVert\Gamma_{\ell}^{-1}\rVert\Bigl(&w_{\ell_{0}+1,\ell}(\lVert\Theta_{k}\rVert+\lVert\Xi_{\ell}\rVert)+w_{\ell_{0},\ell}\lVert F_{k}\rVert\\
&+w_{k,\ell-1}\lVert\Gamma_{\ell-1}\rVert+2\mathbf{u}_{d}\Bigr),&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\text{for }1\leq k\leq\ell_{0}.\\
\lVert\Gamma_{\ell}^{-1}\rVert\Bigl(&w_{k,\ell}(\lVert\Xi_{k}\rVert+\lVert\Xi_{\ell}\rVert)+\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell_{0}}w_{i,\ell}\lVert F_{i}\rVert\\
&+w_{k+1,\ell}\lVert\Gamma_{k}\rVert+w_{k,\ell-1}\lVert\Gamma_{\ell-1}\rVert+2\mathbf{u}_{d}\Bigr),&\!\!\!\!\!\!\text{for }k=\ell_{0}+1.\\
\lVert\Gamma_{\ell}^{-1}\rVert\Bigl(&w_{k,\ell}(\lVert\Xi_{k}\rVert+\lVert\Xi_{\ell}\rVert)+w_{k+1,\ell}\lVert\Gamma_{k}\rVert\\
&+w_{k-1,\ell}\lVert\Gamma_{k-1}\rVert+w_{k,\ell-1}\lVert\Gamma_{\ell-1}\rVert+2\mathbf{u}_{d}\Bigr),&\text{for }\ell_{0}+2\leq k\leq\ell.\end{aligned}\right.

Note that the computation of the column
\ell+1
only involves the previous two columns
\ell-1
and
\ell
, consistent with the three-term recurrence of the Lanczos method.
The entries of the initial two columns
\ell_{0}+1
and
\ell_{0}+2
of
W
are set to

\mathbf{u}_{d}
because
Q_{\ell_{0}+1}
and
Q_{\ell_{0}+2}
are reorthogonalized versus
Z_{\ell_{0}}
immediately after restarting.
We monitor the loss of orthogonality using
W
.
If
w_{k,\ell+1}>\mathbf{u}_{\mathsf{RO}}
for some
k
, we reorthogonalize
Q_{\ell}
and
Q_{\ell+1}
versus
Z_{\ell-1}
and set the strictly upper triangular entries of the corresponding columns
\ell
and
\ell+1
of
W
to
\mathbf{u}_{d}
.

4.4
Preconditioning

In
remark

16
, we established that the number of iterations
\ell
needed for convergence depends on the effective condition number
\kappa=\overline{\sigma}/\underline{\sigma}
. Preconditioning can be used to reduce this number.

4.4.1
Inner preconditioning

Given an
m\times m
symmetric positive preconditioner
P_{L}
for
AA^{\mathsf{T}}
, one may replace
B=A^{\mathsf{T}}A+\epsilon D
by

B_{L}=A^{\mathsf{T}}P_{L}^{-1}A+\epsilon D

within the Lanczos method. Besides maintaining the null space (the null spaces of
A^{\mathsf{T}}P_{L}^{-1}A
and
A
are identical), the effective condition number is improved to
\kappa=\lVert(A^{\mathsf{T}}P_{L}^{-1}A)(A^{\mathsf{T}}P_{L}^{-1}A)^{\dagger}\rVert^{1/2}
. For
m\gg n
, using such a preconditioner may be expensive because
P_{L}
is an
m\times m
matrix.

4.4.2
Outer preconditioning

Given an invertible matrix
P_{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
P_{R}P_{R}^{\mathsf{T}}
is a preconditioner for
A^{\mathsf{T}}A
, one may replace
B=A^{\mathsf{T}}A+\epsilon D
by

B_{R}=P_{R}^{-1}A^{\mathsf{T}}AP_{R}^{-\mathsf{T}}+\epsilon D

within the Lanczos method. This transformation improves the effective condition number to
\kappa=\lVert(AP_{R}^{-\mathsf{T}})(AP_{R}^{-\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\rVert
. However, unlike inner preconditioning, this method necessitates the application of both
P_{R}^{-1}
and
P_{R}^{-\mathsf{T}}
in a single Lanczos step. Moreover, an additional step of
P_{R}^{-\mathsf{T}}V
is necessary to post-process the output because
V
approximates the null space of
AP_{R}^{-\mathsf{T}}
instead of
A
.

Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a novel randomized small-block Lanczos method that incorporates modern Krylov subspace techniques such as restarting and partial reorthogonalization to compute null spaces of large sparse matrices. For the examples considered, our implementation outperforms both the default SVD-based null space solver and the traditional block Lanczos method. Additionally, we provide a theoretical analysis of the convergence for the single-vector case, that is,
d=1
. It remains an open problem to derive convergence bounds that reflect the good performance observed for small
d>1
.
