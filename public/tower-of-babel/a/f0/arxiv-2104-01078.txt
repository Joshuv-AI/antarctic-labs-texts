Blind Exploration and Exploitation of Stochastic Experts
Noyan C. Sevüktekin and Andrew C. Singer

Abstract
We present blind exploration and exploitation (BEE) algorithms for identifying the most reliable stochastic expert based on formulations that employ posterior sampling, upper-confidence bounds, empirical Kullback-Leibler divergence, and minmax methods for the stochastic multi-armed bandit problem. Joint sampling and consultation of experts whose opinions depend on the hidden and random state of the world becomes challenging in the unsupervised, or blind, framework as feedback from the true state is not available. We propose an empirically realizable measure of expert competence that can be inferred instantaneously using only the opinions of other experts. This measure preserves the ordering of true competences and thus enables joint sampling and consultation of stochastic experts based on their opinions on dynamically changing tasks. Statistics derived from the proposed measure is instantaneously available allowing both blind exploration-exploitation and unsupervised opinion aggregation. We discuss how the lack of supervision affects the asymptotic regret of BEE architectures that rely on UCB1, KL-UCB, MOSS, IMED, and Thompson sampling. We demonstrate the performance of different BEE algorithms empirically and compare them to their standard, or supervised, counterparts.

II-C

Problem Definition

The first objective of the BEE problem is to identify the best expert in a population while actively consulting members of that group on tasks that change from one consultation to another. The following notion of regret, written here in normalized form, formally captures this phenomenon:

R_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}\left(X^{*}(t)=Y(t)\right)-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}\left(X_{I_{t}}(t)=Y(t)\right).

(6)

Here
X^{*}

X^{*}=X_{i^{*}}

I_{t}\in[M]

\lim\limits_{T\rightarrow\infty}R_{T}=\max_{i\in[M]}p_{i}-\lim\limits_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}\left(X_{I_{t}}(t)=Y(t)\right).

(7)

The first term is a direct consequence of the ergodicity of the processs
\mathds{1}\left(X^{*}(t)=Y(t)\right)

(8)

III
Unsupervised Estimation of Competences with Instantaneous Updates

Two seemingly conflicting statistical phenomena should be reconciled to facilitate sampling and consultation of experts based only on their opinions. On one hand, standard index policies for exploration and exploitation require observations from a joint probability distribution that does not evolve in time. On the other hand, opinions
\left\{X_{1}(t),\cdots,X_{M}(t)\right\}
are based on the hidden state

and thus, their statistics change randomly in time. Furthermore, an operational challenge exists: Multi-armed bandit problems often allow observations to be available instantly so that the player can decide which arm to pull next. This is in sharp contrast to the unsupervised opinion aggregation strategies such as SML, EM, BP from

Fortunately, there exists an empirically realizable measure of competence that is only based on opinions. Conceptually, consider a compact measure, for instance, an ideal ruler for measuring the length of a two-dimensional line. The length of any such line can be measured reliably by this ruler and these measurements can be used to determine which line is the longest. Recall that the true competence of an expert can be measured experimentally via:

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\mathds{1}\left(X_{i}(t)=Y(t)\right)=p_{i}.

(10)

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed techniques that allow exploration-exploitation algorithms to operate in a regime where the rewards are observed indirectly from opinions on dynamically changing tasks. The proposed measure of competence not only allows instantaneous inference, which commonly requires either state feedback or block processing, but also enables the use of a near-optimal opinion aggregation rule in real time. Furthermore, we point out that the formal analysis of the convergence and competence ordering for BEE and SWARM algorithms are exciting open problems with the numerical evidence presented here indicating their strong potential.

Observe that the law of total probability yields the following:

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}(t)=1\right)=\sum_{j\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}(t)=1\;\middle|\;Y(t)=j\right)\mathbb{P}\left(Y(t)=j\right)

for every task, we can further write that:

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}(t)=1\right)=\frac{p_{i}}{2}+\frac{1-p_{i}}{2}=\frac{1}{2},

(31)

which indicates that
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}(t)\right]=0
,
\forall t
. The ergodicity of the opinion process yields eq. (

). Note that this hold for every expert.

Proof of Property
1

We drop the time dependence, such as
\left\{X_{i}(t),Y(t)\right\}
, for notational clarity and instead write
\left\{X_{i},Y\right\}
. The proof is a direct application of the law of total probability:

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}=V\left(\left\{X_{k}:k\in\mathcal{C}\right\}\right)\right)

(32)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}=Y,V\left(\left\{X_{k}:k\in\mathcal{C}\right\}\right)=Y\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}\neq Y,V\left(\left\{X_{k}:k\in\mathcal{C}\right\}\right)\neq Y\right)

(33)

Since
j\notin\mathcal{C}
, the opinion
X_{j}
is conditional independent of
V\left(\left\{X_{k}:k\in\mathcal{C}\right\}\right)
and thus, whether an opinion and votes over another set of opinions is correct or incorrect is independent of one another. Formally:

\mathds{1}\left(X_{i}=Y\right)\perp\mathds{1}\left(V\left(\left\{X_{k}:k\in\mathcal{C}\right\}\right)=Y\right).

(34)

Therefore, one can write:

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}=Y\right)\mathbb{P}\left(V\left(\left\{X_{k}:k\in\mathcal{C}\right\}\right)=Y\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}\neq Y\right)\mathbb{P}\left(V\left(\left\{X_{k}:k\in\mathcal{C}\right\}\right)\neq Y\right),

(35)

\displaystyle=p_{i}\mathbb{P}\left(V\left(\left\{X_{k}:k\in\mathcal{C}\right\}\right)=Y\right)+(1-p_{i})\left(1-\mathbb{P}\left(V\left(\left\{X_{k}:k\in\mathcal{C}\right\}\right)=Y\right)\right),

(36)

\displaystyle=p_{i}p_{\mathcal{C}}+(1-p_{i})\left(1-p_{\mathcal{C}}\right).

(37)

Now consider the difference between pseudo competences:

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{i}-\tilde{p}_{j}

\displaystyle=p_{i}p_{\mathcal{C}}+(1-p_{i})\left(1-p_{\mathcal{C}}\right)-p_{j}p_{\mathcal{C}}+(1-p_{j})\left(1-p_{\mathcal{C}}\right),

(38)

\displaystyle=\left(p_{i}-p_{j}\right)p_{\mathcal{C}}+\left(1-p_{i}-\left(1-p_{j}\right)\right)\left(1-p_{\mathcal{C}}\right),

(39)

\displaystyle=\left(p_{i}-p_{j}\right)p_{\mathcal{C}}-\left(p_{i}-p_{j}\right)\left(1-p_{\mathcal{C}}\right)

(40)

\displaystyle=\left(p_{i}-p_{j}\right)\left(2p_{\mathcal{C}}-1\right),

(41)

which yields that as long as
