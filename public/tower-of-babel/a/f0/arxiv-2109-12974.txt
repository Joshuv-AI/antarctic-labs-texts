Bilateral Trade: A Regret Minimization Perspective A preliminary version of this paper appeared at the 22nd ACM Conference on Economics and Computation ( Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2021 ) .
Nicolò Cesa-Bianchi, Tommaso Cesari, Roberto Colomboni, Federico Fusco, Stefano Leonardi

Abstract
Bilateral trade, a fundamental topic in economics, models the problem of intermediating between two strategic agents, a seller and a buyer, willing to trade a good for which they hold private valuations. In this paper, we cast the bilateral trade problem in a regret minimization framework over T T rounds of seller/buyer interactions, with no prior knowledge on their private valuations. Our main contribution is a complete characterization of the regret regimes for fixed-price mechanisms with different feedback models and private valuations, using as a benchmark the best fixed-price in hindsight. More precisely, we prove the following tight bounds on the regret: • Θ ⁡ ( T ) \Theta\bigl(\sqrt{T}\bigr) for full-feedback (i.e., direct revelation mechanisms). • Θ ⁡ ( T 2 / 3 ) \Theta\bigl(T^{2/3}\bigr) for realistic feedback (i.e., posted-price mechanisms) and independent seller/buyer valuations with bounded densities. • Θ ⁡ ( T ) \Theta(T) for realistic feedback and seller/buyer valuations with bounded densities. • Θ ⁡ ( T ) \Theta(T) for realistic feedback and independent seller/buyer valuations. • Θ ⁡ ( T ) \Theta(T) for the adversarial setting.

Introduction

In the bilateral trade problem, two strategic agents —a seller and a buyer— wish to trade some good. They both privately hold a personal valuation for it and strive to maximize their respective quasi-linear utility.
The burden of designing a mechanism to reach an agreement is usually delegated to a third party. This scenario arises naturally in many internet applications, such as ridesharing systems like Uber or Lyft, where trades between sellers (drivers) and buyers (riders) are managed by a mechanism designed by the platform.

In general, an ideal mechanism for the bilateral trade problem would optimize the efficiency, i.e., the social welfare resulting by trading the item, while enforcing incentive compatibility (IC) and individual rationality (IR).
The assumption that makes a two-sided mechanism design more complex than its one-sided counterpart is budget balance (BB): the mechanism cannot subsidize or make a profit from the market.
Unfortunately, as Vickrey observed in his seminal work
(
Vickrey 1961
)
, the optimal incentive compatible mechanism maximizing social welfare for bilateral trade may not be budget balanced.

A more general result due to Myerson and Satterthwaite
(
Myerson and Satterthwaite 1983
)
shows that there are some problem instances where a fully efficient mechanism for bilateral trade that satisfies IC, IR, and BB does not exist.
This impossibility result holds even if prior information on the buyer and seller’s valuations is available, the truthful notion is relaxed to Bayesian incentive compatibility (BIC), and the budget balance constraint is loosened to weak budget balance (WBB).
To circumvent this obstacle, a long line of research has focused on the design of approximating mechanisms that satisfy the above requirements while being nearly efficient.

These approximation results build on a Bayesian assumption: seller and buyer valuations are drawn from two distributions, which are both known to the mechanism designer.
Although in some sense necessary —without any information on the priors there is no way to extract any meaningful approximation result
(
Dütting et al. 2021
)
— this assumption is unrealistic.
Following a recent line of research
(
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2015
;
Lykouris et al. 2016
;
Daskalakis and Syrgkanis 2016
)
, in this work we study this basic mechanism design problem in a regret minimization setting.
Our goal is bounding the total loss in efficiency experienced by the mechanism in the long period by learning the salient features of the prior distributions.

At each time
t
, a new seller/buyer pair arrives.
The seller has a private valuation
S_{t}\in[0,1]
representing the smallest price she is willing to accept in order to trade.
Similarly, the buyer has a private value
B_{t}\in[0,1]
representing the highest price that she will pay for the item.
The mechanism sets a price
P_{t}\in[0,1]
which results in a trade if and only if
S_{t}\leq P_{t}\leq B_{t}
.

There are two common utility functions that reflect the performance of the mechanism at each time step: the social welfare, which sums the utilities of the two players after the trade (and remains equal to the seller’s valuation if no trade occurs), and the gain from trade, consisting in the net gain in the utilities.
In formulae, for each price
p\in[0,1]
,

•

Social Welfare
:
\mathrm{SW}_{t}(p):=\mathrm{SW}(p,S_{t},B_{t}):=S_{t}+(B_{t}-S_{t})\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\leq p\leq B_{t}\}
.

•

Gain from Trade
:
\gft\nolimits_{t}(p):=\gft(p,S_{t},B_{t}):=(B_{t}-S_{t})\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\leq p\leq B_{t}\}
.

We begin by investigating the standard assumption in which

(S_{1},B_{1}),(S_{2},B_{2}),\ldots
are i.i.d. random vectors, supported in
[0,1]^{2}
, representing the valuations of seller and buyer respectively (stochastic i.i.d. setting).
We also consider the case where
(S_{1},B_{1}),(S_{2},B_{2}),\ldots
is an arbitrary deterministic process
(s_{1},b_{1}),(s_{2},b_{2}),\dots
(adversarial setting).

In our online learning framework, we aim at minimizing the
regret
of the mechanism over a time horizon
T
:

\max_{p\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gft\nolimits_{t}(p)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gft\nolimits_{t}(P_{t})\right]\;.

Note that since
\gft\nolimits_{t}(P_{t})=\mathrm{SW}_{t}(P_{t})-S_{t}
and
S_{t}
does not depend on the choice of
p
, gain from trade and social welfare lead to the same notion of regret.

Hence, the regret is the difference between the expected total performance of our algorithm, which can only
sequentially learn
the distribution,
and our reference benchmark, corresponding to the the best fixed-price strategy assuming
full knowledge
of the distribution.
Our main goal is to design strategies with asymptotically vanishing time-averaged regret with respect to the best fixed-price strategy or, equivalently, regret sublinear in the time horizon
T
.

The class of fixed-price mechanisms is of particular importance in bilateral trade as they are simple to implement, clearly truthful, individually rational, budget balanced, and enjoy the desirable property of asking the agents very little information. Moreover, it can be shown that fixed prices are the
only
direct revelation mechanisms which enjoy budget balance, dominant strategy incentive compatibility, and ex-post individual rationality
(
Colini-Baldeschi et al. 2016
)
.

To complete the description of the problem, we need to specify the feedback obtained by the mechanism after each sequential round. We propose two main feedback models:

•

Full feedback.
In the full-feedback model, the pair
(S_{t},B_{t})
is revealed to the mechanism after the
t
-th trading round. The information collected by this feedback model corresponds to
direct revelation mechanisms
, where the agents publicly declare their valuations in each round, and the price proposed by the mechanism at time
t
only depends on past bids.

•

Realistic feedback.
In the harder realistic feedback model, only the relative order between
S_{t}
and
P_{t}
and between
B_{t}
and
P_{t}
are revealed after the
t
-th round. This model corresponds to
posted price mechanisms
, where seller and buyer separately accept or refuse the posted price. The price computed at time
t
only depends on past bids, and the values
S_{t}
and
B_{t}
are never revealed to the mechanisms.

1.2
Technical Challenges

In this section, we sum up the technical challenges for various instances of our problem.

Full feedback.

The full-feedback model fits nicely in the learning with expert advice framework
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
)
.
Each price
p\in[0,1]
can be viewed as an expert, and the revelation of
S_{t}
and
B_{t}
allows the mechanism to compute
\gft\nolimits_{t}(p)
for all
p
, including the mechanism’s own reward
\gft\nolimits_{t}(P_{t})
.
A common approach to reduce the cardinality of a continuous expert space is to assume some regularity (e.g., Lipschitzness) of the reward function, so that a finite grid of representative prices can be used. This approach yields a
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
bound under density boundedness assumptions on the joint distribution of the seller and the buyer.
By exploiting the structure of the reward function
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\gft\nolimits_{t}(\cdot)\bigr]
, we obtain a better regret bound (by a log factor) without any assumptions on the distribution (other than iid).
In
Eq.

2
, we show how to decompose the expression of the expected gain from trade in pieces that can be quickly learned via sampling. The full feedback received in each new round is used to refine the estimate of the actual gain from trade as a function of the price, while the posted prices are chosen so to maximize it. A
Follow the Leader
strategy is shown to achieve a
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T})
bound in the stochastic (iid) setting (
Theorem

1
).
This holds for arbitrary joint distributions of the seller and the buyer.
In particular, even when the buyer and seller have a correlated behavior.
The main issue for the lower bound is that the (expected) gain from trade cannot be chosen arbitrarily: we can only control its shape indirectly as a function of the distribution of the seller/buyer pair.
By designing a suitable family of such distributions, we build a reduction showing that the full-feedback bilateral trade problem is harder than a corresponding
2
-action partial monitoring game with a known
\Omega\bigl(\sqrt{T}\bigr)
lower bound (
Theorem

2
).

Realistic Feedback.

Here, at each time
t
, only
\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\leq P_{t}\}
and
\mathbb{I}\{P_{t}\leq B_{t}\}
are revealed to the learner.
In contrast to the full-feedback model, this is not enough to reconstruct the gain from trade
\mathrm{GFT}_{t}
at time
t
: if the trade does not occur, it is unclear which prices would have resulted in a trade.
Moreover, in contrast to bandit problems
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
)
, this feedback is not even enough to determine
\mathrm{GFT}_{t}(P_{t})
: if the trade occurs, there is no way to infer the difference
B_{t}-S_{t}
directly.
Thus, we cannot directly rely on known bandits tools to tackle the two competing goals of estimating the underlying distributions (exploration) while optimizing the estimated gain from trade (exploitation).
Instead, we show how to decompose the expected gain from trade at price
p
into a
global
part that can be quickly estimated by uniform sampling on the
[0,1]
interval, and a
local
part that can be learned by posting
p
.

Theorem

3
shows that our
Algorithm

3
(Scouting Bandits) can take advantage of this decomposition by relying on any bandit algorithm to learn the local part of the expected gain from trade.
We derive a sublinear regret of
\mathcal{O}(T^{2/3})
in a stochastic (iid) setting in which the valuations of the seller and the buyer are independent of each other (iv) and have bounded densities (bd).
The lower bound presents challenges similar to those of the full-feedback model, with additional hurdles due to the specific nature of the realistic feedback that lead to a harder
\Omega(T^{2/3})
rate (
Theorem

4
).
Dropping the (iv) assumption leads to a pathological
lack of observability
phenomenon, in which it is impossible to distinguish between two scenarios with significantly different optimal prices (
Theorem

5
).
Dropping the (bd) assumption leads to a
needle in a haystack
, a different pathological phenomenon in which all prices but one suffer a high regret, and it is essentially impossible to find this optimal price among a continuum of suboptimal prices (
Theorem

6
).

Adversarial setting.

Here, the valuations of the buyer and the seller form an arbitrary deterministic process generated by an oblivious adversary.
In this setting, learning is impossible.
Indeed, using a construction inspired by the Cantor ternary set, we show that even under a full-feedback model, no strategy can lead to a sublinear worst-case regret
(
Theorem

7
).

Further Related Work

The study of the bilateral trade problem dates back to the already mentioned seminal works of Vickrey
(
Vickrey 1961
)
and Myerson and Satterthwaite
(
Myerson and Satterthwaite 1983
)
.
A more recent line of research focuses on Bayesian mechanisms that achieve the IC, BB, and IR requirements while approximating the optimal social welfare or the gain form trade. Blumrosen and Dobzinski
(
Blumrosen and Dobzinski 2014
)
proposed the
median mechanism
that sets a posted price equal to the median of the seller distribution and shows that this mechanism obtains an approximation factor of
2
to the optimal social welfare. Subsequent work by the same authors
(
Blumrosen and Dobzinski 2016
)

improved the approximation guarantee to
e/(e-1)
through a randomized mechanism whose prices depend on the seller distribution in a more intricate way.
In
(
Colini-Baldeschi et al. 2016
)
it is demonstrated that all DSIC mechanisms that are BB and IR must post a fixed price to the buyer and to the seller.
The same result has been previously proven under stronger assumptions in
(
Hagerty and Rogerson 1987
)
.

In a different research direction aimed to characterize the information theoretical requirements of two-sided markets mechanisms,
(
Dütting et al. 2021
)
show that setting the price equal to a single sample from the seller distribution gives a
2
-approximation to the optimal social welfare.
In a parallel line of work, the harder objective of approximating the
gain from trade
has been considered.
An asymptotically tight fixed-price
O\big(\log\frac{1}{r}\big)
approximation bound is also achieved in
(
Colini-Baldeschi et al. 2017
)
, with
r
being the probability that a trade happens (i.e., the value of the buyer is higher than the value of the seller). A BIC
2
-approximation of the second best with a simple mechanism is obtained in
(
Brustle et al. 2017
)
.

In the following, we discuss the relationship between the approximation results mentioned above and the regret analysis we develop in this work that compares online learning mechanisms against the best ex-ante fixed-price mechanism. First of all, in the realistic feedback setting, the approximation mechanisms for bilateral trade cannot be easily implemented. For example, the single sample
2
-approximation to the optimal social welfare
(
Dütting et al. 2021
)
requires multiple rounds of interaction in order to obtain, approximately, a random sample from the distribution. The median mechanism of
(
Blumrosen and Dobzinski 2014
)
requires an even larger number of rounds in order to estimate the median of the seller distribution.
Furthermore, here we note that these two more demanding approaches may yield worst performances than the best ex-ante fixed price
1
1

1

Consider a seller with value
\varepsilon>0
or
0
with equal probability and a buyer with value
1
. The best fixed price has welfare of
1
. For small
\varepsilon
, the median and the sample mechanism, respectively, obtains a welfare close to
1/2
and
3/4
.
. This implies that there are instances where our online learning approach converges to a mechanism that is strictly better than the median or sample mechanisms, even assuming they have full knowledge of the underlying distributions.

There is a vast body of literature on regret analysis in (one-sided) dynamic pricing and online posted price auctions —see, e.g., the excellent survey published by
(
den Boer 2015
)
and the tutorial slides by
(
Slivkins and Zeevi 2015
)
. In their seminal paper, Kleinberg and Leighton prove a
O(T^{2/3})
upper bound (ignoring logarithmic factors) on the regret in the adversarial setting
(
Kleinberg and Leighton 2003
)
. Later works show simultaneous multiplicative and additive bounds on the regret when prices have range
[1,h]

(
Blum et al. 2004
;
Blum and Hartline 2005
)
. These bounds have the form
\varepsilon\,G_{T}^{\star}+O\big((h\ln h)/\varepsilon^{2}\big)
ignoring
\ln\ln h
factors, where
G_{T}^{\star}
is the total revenue of the optimal price
p^{\star}
. Recent improvements on these results prove that the additive term can be made
O(p^{\star}\bigl(\ln h)/\varepsilon^{2}\bigr)
, where the linear scaling is now with respect to the optimal price rather than the maximum price
h

(
Bubeck et al. 2017
)
. Other variants consider settings in which the number of copies of the item to sell is limited
(
Agrawal and Devanur 2014
;
Babaioff et al. 2015
;
Badanidiyuru et al. 2018
)
, buyers act strategically in order to maximize their utility in future rounds
(
Amin et al. 2013
;
Devanur et al. 2019
;
Mohri and Medina 2014
;
Drutsa 2018
)
, or there are features associated with the goods on sale
(
Cohen et al. 2020
)
. In the stochastic setting, previous works typically assume parametric
(
Broder and Rusmevichientong 2012
)
, locally smooth
(
Kleinberg and Leighton 2003
)
, or piecewise constant demand curves
(
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2019
;
den Boer and Keskin 2020
)
.

The Decomposition Lemma

In this section, we present a key lemma whose purpose is to decompose the gain from trade into terms that depend only on the outcome of yes/no questions.
This result allows leveraging DKW inequalities in the proofs of our upper bounds.
Moreover, it shows how to use the limited feedback available to reconstruct the expected gain from trade in the realistic feedback settings.
Furthermore, it leads to an easy proof of the existence of the maximum of the expected gain from trade, under no assumptions on the seller and buyer distributions.
We defer the proofs of the results of this section to Appendix
A
.

Lemma 1

(Decomposition lemma)
.

Fix any price
p\in[0,1]
. Then, for any
s,b\in[0,1]
,

\mathrm{GFT}(p,s,b)=\int_{[p,1]}\mathbb{I}[s\leq p\leq\lambda\leq b]\,\mathrm{d}\lambda+\int_{[0,p]}\mathbb{I}[s\leq\lambda\leq p\leq b]\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\;.

(1)

Furthermore, let
S
and
B
be two
[0,1]
-valued random variables:

•

Then

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{GFT}(p,S,B)\bigr]=\int_{[p,1]}\mathbb{P}[S\leq p\leq\lambda\leq B]\,\mathrm{d}\lambda+\int_{[0,p]}\mathbb{P}[S\leq\lambda\leq p\leq B]\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\;.

(2)

•

If
U
is uniform on
[0,1]
and independent of
(S,B)
, then

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{GFT}(p,S,B)\bigr]=\mathbb{P}[S\leq p\leq U\leq B]+\mathbb{P}[S\leq U\leq p\leq B]\;.

(3)

•

If
U
is uniform on
[0,1]
and
S,B,U
are independent, then

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{GFT}(p,S,B)\bigr]=\mathbb{P}[S\leq p]\mathbb{P}[p\leq U\leq B]+\mathbb{P}[p\leq B]\mathbb{P}[S\leq U\leq p]\;.

(4)

•

If
U
is uniform on
[p,1]
,
V
is uniform on
[0,p]
and
(U,V)
is independent of
(S,B)
, then

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{GFT}(p,S,B)\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(1-p)\mathbb{I}\{S\leq p\leq U\leq B\}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[p\mathbb{I}\{S\leq V\leq p\leq B\}\bigr]\;.

(5)

We now present a corollary of our Decomposition lemma relating the regularity of the distributions (specifically, the boundedness of the densities) to the regularity of the expected gain from trade (i.e., its Lipschitzness).

{corollary}

If
S
and

4
Full-Feedback Stochastic (iid) Setting

We begin by considering the full-feedback model (corresponding to direct revelation mechanisms) in a stochastic environment, where the seller/buyer pairs
(S_{1},B_{1}),(S_{2},B_{2}),\ldots
are
[0,1]^{2}
-valued i.i.d. random vectors, without any further assumptions on their common distribution (in particular,
S_{1}
and
B_{1}
could be arbitrarily correlated).
Here, at the end of each round, sellers and buyers declare their actual valuations to the learner.
The incentive-compatibility is guaranteed by the fact that the posted prices do not depend on the declared valuations at each specific round, but only on past ones, so that there is no point in misreporting.

In
Section

4.1
, we show that a
Follow the Leader
approach, which we call Follow the Best Price (FBP,

5.1
Scouting Bandits: from Realistic Feedback to Multi-Armed Bandits

The main challenge in designing low-regret algorithms with realistic feedback lies in the fact that posting a price
does not
reveal the corresponding gain from trade.
We can observe this phenomenon by looking at the Decomposition lemma (
4

While the local terms
\mathbb{P}[S\leq p]

from

This prevents direct applications of well-established algorithms, such as action elimination or UCB

A naive approach to tackle this issue could be estimating the CDFs of

B

6
Adversarial Setting: Linear Lower Bound Under Full Feedback

In this section, we prove that even in the simpler full-feedback case, no strategy can achieve worst-case sublinear regret in an adversarial setting.
Lower bounds for the adversarial setting have a slightly different structure that the stochastic ones. The idea of the proof is to build, for any strategy, a
hard
sequence of sellers and buyers’ valuations
(s_{1},b_{1}),(s_{2},b_{2}),\ldots
which causes the algorithm to suffer linear regret for any horizon
T
.

Theorem 7
.

In the full-feedback adversarial (adv) setting, for all horizons
T
, the minimax regret
R^{\star}_{T}
satisfies

R^{\star}_{T}:=\inf_{\alpha}\sup_{(s_{1},b_{1}),(s_{2},b_{2}),\ldots}R_{T}(\alpha)\geq cT\;,

where

\alpha

Proof.

We begin by fixing any strategy
\alpha
of the learner.
This is a sequence of functions
\alpha_{t}
mapping the past feedback
(s_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(s_{t-1},b_{t-1})
, together with some internal randomization, to the price
P_{t}
to be posted by the learner at time
t
.
In other words, the strategy maintains a distribution
\nu_{t}
over the prices that is updated after observing each new pair

, to determine a seller/buyer pair

7
Breaking Linear Lower Bounds: Weakly Budget Balanced Results

In this section, we show how to break the linear lower bound of
Section

5.3
without requiring the independence of the valuations of the seller and the buyer.
To do so, we move from a budget balance to a
weak
budget balance mechanism.
In this setting, rather than posting a single price, the learner can post two (possibly distinct) prices
0\leq p\leq p^{\prime}\leq 1
,
p
to the seller, and
p^{\prime}
to the buyer.
This condition allows the platform to extract money from the trade but not to subsidize it.
Naturally, this changes the benchmark:
if the learner posts a pair
(p,p^{\prime})\in[0,1]^{2}
and the valuations of the seller and the buyer are
(s,b)\in[0,1]^{2}
, the net gain of the seller is
p-s
while that of the buyer is
b-p^{\prime}
.
Thus, the gain from trade in this setting becomes

\displaystyle\mathrm{GFT}\colon[0,1]^{2}\times[0,1]^{2}

\displaystyle\to[0,1]\;,

\displaystyle(p,p^{\prime},s,b)

\displaystyle\mapsto(b-p^{\prime}+p-s)\mathbb{I}\{s\leq p\leq p^{\prime}\leq b\}\;.

Note that posting the same price
p=p^{\prime}
to both the seller and the buyer leads to the old definition of gain for trade

(b-s)\mathbb{I}\{s\leq p\leq b\}
,
which we denoted by
\mathrm{GFT}(p,s,b)
in previous sections.
For this reason and to keep the notation lighter, we will denote
\mathrm{GFT}(p,p,s,b)
simply by
\mathrm{GFT}(p,s,b)
here.

We design a explore-then-exploit algorithm, that we call Scouting Blindits (
Algorithm

4
).
In the exploration phase (scouting phase), the high-level idea is to leverage the Decomposition lemma (
5
) to build an accurate estimate
\widehat{F}_{k}+\widehat{G}_{k}
of the gain from trade at each point
q_{k}
of a suitably fine grid.
By the bounded density assumption, which implies the Lipschitzness of the expected gain from trade, this is sufficient to approximate
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{GFT}(\cdot,S_{1},B_{1})\bigr]
uniformly, so that the price
\widehat{P}^{\star}
that maximizes the estimates
\widehat{F}_{k}+\widehat{G}_{k}
is an approximate maximizer of gain from trade.
In the exploitation phase (blind phase) the algorithm posts
\widehat{P}^{\star}
blindly to both the seller and the buyer, ignoring all the feedback it receives.
This implies that Scouting Blindits is actually budget balanced during the whole blind phase (which, after tuning, constitutes the majority of time).

T_{0}
, grid size
K
;

\widehat{F}_{i}\leftarrow 0
,

;

for

//

if

t
is odd

draw
U_{t}
from
[q_{k},1]
uniformly at random;

post the prices
(q_{k},U_{t})
and observe feedback
\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\leq q_{k}\leq U_{t}\leq B_{t}\}
;

let
\widehat{F}_{k}\leftarrow\widehat{F}_{k}+\frac{1}{T_{0}}(1-q_{k})\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\leq q_{k}\leq U_{t}\leq B_{t}\}
;

else

draw
V_{t}
from
[0,q_{k}]
uniformly at random;

post the prices
(V_{t},q_{k})
and observe feedback
\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\leq V_{t}\leq q_{k}\leq B_{t}\}

8
Learning with One Bit

In this section, we discuss the (im)possibility of learning with less than a realistic feedback.
We start by noting that Scouting Blindits requires only one bit of feedback
\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\leq P_{t}\leq P^{\prime}_{t}\leq B_{t}\}
, i.e., whether or not the trade occurred at time
t
if
P_{t}
was posted to the seller and
P^{\prime}_{t}
to the buyer.
In this setting, one can therefore achieve sublinear regret without observing the two bits
\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\leq P_{t}\}
and
\mathbb{I}\{P^{\prime}_{t}\leq B_{t}\}
provided by realistic feedback.
Thus, it is natural to wonder whether the single bit
\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\leq P_{t}\leq B_{t}\}
is sufficient for obtaining sublinear regret bounds also in the budget balanced setting.

This is not the case
: even under the further assumptions of bounded densities (bd) and independent valuations (iv), a single bit in the budget balance setting does not provide sufficient observability.
Indeed, consider a first instance in which the seller
S
and buyer
B
have uniform distributions on
[0,1]
, independent of each other.
In this case, the only maximizer of the expected gain from trade is
p^{\star}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.
As a second instance, consider two independent distributions of the seller
S^{\prime}
and buyer
B^{\prime}
with densities (bounded by
2
and even infinite differentiable)
f_{S^{\prime}}(s):=4(4-2s^{3}+s^{2})/(s^{3}-s^{2}+4)^{2}
and
f_{B^{\prime}}(b)=b(b-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})(b-1)+1
respectively.
Then, for all
p\in[0,1]
, we have
\mathbb{P}[S\leq p\leq B]=\mathbb{P}[S^{\prime}\leq p\leq B^{\prime}]
.
Therefore, the two instances are indistinguishable under the single-bit feedback, but a direct verification shows that in the second instance,
p^{\star}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
is
not
a maximizer of the expected gain from trade.
Leveraging these facts and the continuity of the gain from trade in the two instances leads to a linear minimax regret, using the same ideas as in
Theorem

5
.

9
Conclusions

This work initiates the study of the bilateral trade problem in a regret minimization framework.
We designed algorithms and proved tight bounds on the regret rates achieved under various feedback and private valuation models.

Our work opens several possibilities for future investigation.
One first and natural research direction is related to the more general settings of two-sided markets with multiple buyers and sellers, different prior distributions, and complex valuation functions.
A second direction is related to the tight characterization of the regret rates for weak budget balance mechanisms (which we proved are strictly better than the budget balance rates in some cases).
Finally, we believe other classes of markets, which assume prior knowledge of the agent’s preferences, could be fruitfully studied in a regret minimization framework.

\varphi\colon[0,1]\times[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]^{2}
,
\bigl(p,(s,b)\bigr)\mapsto(s,b)
.
This corresponds to the seller and the buyer revealing their valuations at the end of a trade.

Realistic:

the feedback space
\mathcal{Z}
is the boolean square
\{0,1\}^{2}
and the feedback function is
\varphi\colon[0,1]\times[0,1]^{2}\to\{0,1\}^{2}
,
\bigl(p,(s,b)\bigr)\mapsto\bigl(\mathbb{I}\{s\leq p\},\mathbb{I}\{p\leq b\}\bigr)
.
This corresponds to the seller and the buyer accepting or rejecting a trade at a price
p
.

C.2.3
Adversary’s Behavior

Stochastic (iid):

the adversary’s behavior
\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iid}}
consists of products of a single probability on
\mathcal{Y}=[0,1]^{2}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iid}}
if and only if there exists a probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]^{2}
such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\otimes_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mu
.
This corresponds to a stochastic i.i.d. environment, where however the valuations of the seller and the buyer could be correlated.

We will also investigate the following stronger assumptions.

Independent valuations (iv):

the adversary’s behavior
\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iv}}
is the subset of
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iid}}
in which the valuations of the seller and the buyer are independent, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iv}}
if and only if there exist two probability measures
\mu_{S},\mu_{B}
on
[0,1]
such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\otimes_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\,(\mu_{S}\otimes\mu_{B})
.

Bounded density (bd):

for a fixed
M\geq 1
, the adversary’s behavior
\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{bd}}^{M}
is the subset of
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iid}}
in which the joint distribution of the valuations of buyer and seller has a density bounded by
M
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{bd}}^{M}
if and only if there exists a density
f\colon[0,1]^{2}\to[0,M]
such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\otimes_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\,(f\mu)
, where
\mu=\mu_{L}\otimes\mu_{L}
.

Independent valuations with bounded density (iv+bd):

for a fixed
M\geq 1
, the adversary’s behavior
\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iv+bd}}^{M}
is the subset
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iv}}\cap\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{bd}}^{M}
of
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iid}}
.

Adversarial (adv):

the adversary’s behavior
\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{adv}}
consists of products of Dirac measures on
\mathcal{Y}=[0,1]^{2}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{adv}}
if and only if there exists a sequence
(s_{t},b_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\subset[0,1]^{2}
such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\otimes_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\,\delta_{(s_{t},b_{t})}
.
This corresponds to a deterministic, oblivious, and adversarial environment.

Appendix D
Two Key Lemmas on Simplifying Sequential Games

In this section we introduce some useful techniques that could be of independent interest for proving lower bounds in sequential games.
The idea is to give sufficient conditions for a given game to be harder than another, where the second one has a known lower bound on its minimax regret.

At a high level, the first lemma shows that if the adversary’s actions are independent of each other, a game
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}
is easier than game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}
if
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}
can be embedded in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}
in such a way that:

1.

The optimal player’s actions of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}
are no better than the ones in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}
.

2.

The suboptimal player’s actions of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}
no worse than the ones in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}
.

3.

At distributional level, the quality of the feedback in
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}
is no worse than that in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}
.

The proof is deferred to Appendix
D.1
.

Lemma 3

(Embedding)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}:=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z},\rho,\varphi,\mathscr{P})
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}:=(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}},\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}},\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}},\widetilde{\rho},\widetilde{\varphi},\widetilde{\mathscr{P}})
be two games,
\mathscr{S},\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
their respective sets of scenarios,
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}},(\widetilde{Y}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
their adversaries’ actions, and
T\in\mathbb{N}
a horizon.
Assume that

Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{T}
are
\mathbb{P}
-independent for any scenario
\mathbb{P}\in\mathscr{S}
,
\widetilde{Y}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{Y}_{T}
are
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
-independent for any scenario
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
, and
that there exist

\widetilde{\mathscr{f}}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
,

\mathscr{g}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}\to\mathcal{Z}
,
and

\mathscr{h}\colon\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}\to\mathscr{S}

satisfying:

1.

\sup_{\widetilde{x}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}\bigl[\widetilde{\rho}(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{Y}_{t})\bigr]\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{h}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}})}\bigl[\rho(x,Y_{t})\bigr]
for any scenario
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
.

2.

\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}\bigl[\widetilde{\rho}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathscr{f}}(x),\widetilde{Y}_{t}\bigr)\bigr]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{h}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}})}\bigl[\rho(x,Y_{t})\bigr]
for any time
t\in[T]
, scenario
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
, and action
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

3.

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathscr{g}\left(\widetilde{\varphi}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathscr{f}}(x),\widetilde{Y}_{t}\bigr)\right)}=\bigl(\mathscr{h}(\widetilde{\mathbb{P}})\bigr)_{\varphi(x,Y_{t})}
for any time
t\in[T]
, scenario
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
, and action
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Then

R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}})\geq R^{\star}_{T}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}})
.

The second lemma addresses feedback with uninformative (i.e., scenario-independent) components.
At a high level, if the feedback of some of the player’s actions has one or more uninformative components, the game can be simplified by getting rid of them.
The player can achieve this by simulating the uninformative parts of the feedback using her randomization.
The proof is deferred to Appendix
D.1
.

Lemma 4

(Simulation)
.

Let
\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}
be two sets,
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}:=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z},\rho,\varphi,\mathscr{P})
a game with
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{W}
,
\mathscr{S}
its set of scenarios,
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
its adversary’s actions,
\pi\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{V}
the projection on
\mathcal{V}
, and
T\in\mathbb{N}
a horizon.
Assume that

Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{T}
are
\mathbb{P}
-independent for any scenario
\mathbb{P}\in\mathscr{S}
and that there exist disjoint sets
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{X}
such that
\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{X}
and

1.

For any time
t\in[T]
and action
x\in\mathcal{I}
there exists
\psi_{t,x}\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{W}
such that, for all
\mathbb{P}\in\mathscr{S}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\varphi(x,Y_{t})}=\mathbb{P}_{\pi\bigl(\varphi(x,Y_{t})\bigr)}\otimes(\mu_{L})_{\psi_{t,x}}\;.

2.

For any time
t\in[T]
and action
x\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists
\gamma_{t,x}\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{Z}
such that, for all
\mathbb{P}\in\mathscr{S}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\varphi(x,Y_{t})}=(\mu_{L})_{\gamma_{t,x}}\;.

Let
*\in\mathcal{V}
and define

\widetilde{\varphi}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{V},\ (x,y)\mapsto\begin{cases}\pi\bigl(\varphi(x,y)\bigr)\;,&\text{ if }x\in\mathcal{I},\\
*\;,&\text{ if }x\in\mathcal{U}.\end{cases}

Define the game
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}:=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{V},\rho,\widetilde{\varphi},\mathscr{P})
.
Then

R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}})\geq R^{\star}_{T}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}})
.

(\widetilde{V}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be respectively the sequence of player’s actions and the feedback sequence associated with the strategy
\widetilde{\alpha}
. For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
, define also
\widetilde{Z}_{t}:=\beta_{t}\bigl(\widetilde{X}_{t},\widetilde{V}_{t},\zeta(U_{t})\bigr)
. Note that for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that
\widetilde{X}_{t}=\alpha_{t}\bigl(\xi(U_{1}),\dots,\xi(U_{t}),\widetilde{Z}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{Z}_{t-1}\bigr)
.

Fix a scenario
\mathbb{P}\in\mathscr{S}
.
Note first that
\mathbb{P}_{\xi(U_{1})}=\mathbb{P}_{U_{1}}
, and since
X_{1}=\alpha_{1}(U_{1})
and
\widetilde{X}_{1}=\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}(U_{1})=\alpha_{1}\bigl(\xi(U_{1})\bigr)
, we also have that
\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{X}_{1},\xi(U_{1})}=\mathbb{P}_{X_{1},U_{1}}=:\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.
Now, up to a set with
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
-probability zero, if
x_{1}\in\mathcal{X}
and
u_{1}\in[0,1]
, using Items (
1
) and (
2
), we have that

\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{Z}_{1}\mid\widetilde{X}_{1}=x_{1},\xi(U_{1})=u_{1}}=\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{1}\bigl(\widetilde{X}_{1},\widetilde{\varphi}(\widetilde{X}_{1},Y_{1}),\zeta(U_{1})\bigr)\mid\widetilde{X}_{1}=x_{1},\xi(U_{1})=u_{1}}=\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{1}\bigl(x_{1},\widetilde{\varphi}(x_{1},Y_{1}),\zeta(U_{1})\bigr)}\\
\begin{aligned} &=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{1}\Bigl(x_{1},\pi\bigl(\varphi(x_{1},Y_{1})\bigr),\zeta(U_{1})\Bigr)}&\text{if }x_{1}\in\mathcal{I}\\
\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{1}\bigl(x_{1},*,\zeta(U_{1})\bigr)}&\text{if }x_{1}\in\mathcal{U}\\
\end{cases}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}_{\Bigl(\pi\bigl(\varphi(x_{1},Y_{1})\bigr),\psi_{1,x_{1}}\bigl(\zeta(U_{1})\bigr)\Bigr)}&\text{if }x_{1}\in\mathcal{I}\\
\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{1,x_{1}}\bigl(\zeta(U_{1})\bigr)}&\text{if }x_{1}\in\mathcal{U}\\
\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}_{\pi\bigl(\varphi(x_{1},Y_{1})\bigr)}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{\psi_{1,x_{1}}\bigl(\zeta(U_{1})\bigr)}&\text{if }x_{1}\in\mathcal{I}\\
\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{1,x_{1}}\bigl(\zeta(U_{1})\bigr)}&\text{if }x_{1}\in\mathcal{U}\\
\end{cases}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}_{\pi\bigl(\varphi(x_{1},Y_{1})\bigr)}\otimes\bigl(\mathbb{P}_{\zeta(U_{1})}\bigr)_{\psi_{1,x_{1}}}&\text{if }x_{1}\in\mathcal{I}\\
\bigl(\mathbb{P}_{\zeta(U_{1})}\bigr)_{\gamma_{1,x_{1}}}&\text{if }x_{1}\in\mathcal{U}\\
\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}_{\pi\bigl(\varphi(x_{1},Y_{1})\bigr)}\otimes\bigl(\mu_{L}\bigr)_{\psi_{1,x_{1}}}&\text{if }x_{1}\in\mathcal{I}\\
\bigl(\mu_{L}\bigr)_{\gamma_{1,x_{1}}}&\text{if }x_{1}\in\mathcal{U}\\
\end{cases}=\mathbb{P}_{\varphi(x_{1},Y_{1})}=\mathbb{P}_{\varphi(X_{1},Y_{1})\mid X_{1}=x_{1},U_{1}=u_{1}}=\mathbb{P}_{Z_{1}\mid X_{1}=x_{1},U_{1}=u_{1}}\;.\end{aligned}

So, if
A_{1}\subset\mathcal{Z}
and
D\subset\mathcal{X}\times[0,1]
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{Z}_{1},\bigl(\widetilde{X}_{1},\xi(U_{1})\bigr)}(A_{1}\times D)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{Z}_{1}\mid\widetilde{X}_{1}=x_{1},\xi(U_{1})=u_{1}}(A_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{X}_{1},\xi(U_{1})}(x_{1},u_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathbb{P}_{Z_{1}\mid X_{1}=x_{1},U_{1}=u_{1}}(A_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{X_{1},U_{1}}(x_{1},u_{1})=\mathbb{P}_{Z_{1},(X_{1},U_{1})}(A_{1}\times D)\;,

from which it follows that
\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{Z}_{1},\widetilde{X}_{1},\xi(U_{1})}=\mathbb{P}_{Z_{1},X_{1},U_{1}}
.
By induction, suppose that for
t\in[T-1]
we have that

\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{Z}_{t},\dots,\widetilde{Z}_{1},\widetilde{X}_{t},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{1},\xi(U_{t}),\dots,\xi(U_{1})}=\mathbb{P}_{Z_{t},\dots,Z_{1},X_{t},\dots,X_{1},U_{t},\dots,U_{1}}\;.

Then, using independence we have that

\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{Z}_{t},\dots,\widetilde{Z}_{1},\widetilde{X}_{t},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{1},\xi(U_{t+1}),\xi(U_{t}),\dots,\xi(U_{1})}=\mathbb{P}_{Z_{t},\dots,Z_{1},X_{t},\dots,X_{1},U_{t+1},U_{t},\dots,U_{1}}\;.

Furthermore, since
X_{t+1}=\alpha_{t+1}(U_{1},\dots,U_{t+1},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{t})
and

\widetilde{X}_{t+1}=\widetilde{\alpha}_{t+1}(U_{1},\dots,U_{t+1},\widetilde{V}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{V}_{t})=\alpha_{t+1}(\xi(U_{1}),\dots,\xi(U_{t+1}),\widetilde{Z}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{Z}_{t})

we have that

\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{Z}_{t},\dots,\widetilde{Z}_{1},\widetilde{X}_{t+1},\widetilde{X}_{t},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{1},\xi(U_{t+1}),\xi(U_{t}),\dots,\xi(U_{1})}=\mathbb{P}_{Z_{t},\dots,Z_{1},X_{t+1},X_{t},\dots,X_{1},U_{t+1},U_{t},\dots,U_{1}}=:\mathbb{Q}_{t+1}\;.

Now, up to a set with
\mathbb{Q}_{t+1}
-probability zero, if
x_{1},\dots,x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{X}
,
u_{1},\dots,u_{t+1}\in[0,1]
and
z_{1},\dots,z_{t}\in\mathcal{Z}
, using the
\mathbb{P}
-independence of
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{t+1}
and Items (
1
)–(
2
), we have that

\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{Z}_{t+1}\mid\widetilde{Z}_{t}=z_{t},\dots,\widetilde{Z}_{1}=z_{1},\widetilde{X}_{t+1}=x_{t+1},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{1}=x_{1},\xi(U_{t+1})=u_{t+1},\dots,\xi(U_{1})=u_{1}}\\
\begin{aligned} &=\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{t+1}\bigl(\widetilde{X}_{t+1},\widetilde{\varphi}(\widetilde{X}_{t+1},Y_{t+1}),\zeta(U_{t+1})\bigr)\mid\widetilde{Z}_{t}=z_{t},\dots,\widetilde{Z}_{1}=z_{1},\widetilde{X}_{t+1}=x_{t+1},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{1}=x_{1},\xi(U_{t+1})=u_{t+1},\dots,\xi(U_{1})=u_{1}}\\
&=\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{t+1}\bigl(x_{t+1},\widetilde{\varphi}(x_{t+1},Y_{t+1}),\zeta(U_{t+1})\bigr)}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{t+1}\Bigl(x_{t+1},\pi\bigl(\varphi(x_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\bigr),\zeta(U_{t+1})\Bigr)}&\text{if }x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{I}\\
\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{t+1}\bigl(x_{t+1},*,\zeta(U_{t+1})\bigr)}&\text{if }x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{U}\\
\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}_{\Bigl(\pi\bigl(\varphi(x_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\bigr),\psi_{{t+1},x_{t+1}}\bigl(\zeta(U_{t+1})\bigr)\Bigr)}&\text{if }x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{I}\\
\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{{t+1},x_{t+1}}\bigl(\zeta(U_{t+1})\bigr)}&\text{if }x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{U}\\
\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}_{\pi\bigl(\varphi(x_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\bigr)}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{\psi_{{t+1},x_{t+1}}\bigl(\zeta(U_{t+1})\bigr)}&\text{if }x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{I}\\
\mathbb{P}_{\gamma_{{t+1},x_{t+1}}\bigl(\zeta(U_{t+1})\bigr)}&\text{if }x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{U}\\
\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}_{\pi\bigl(\varphi(x_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\bigr)}\otimes\bigl(\mathbb{P}_{\zeta(U_{t+1})}\bigr)_{\psi_{{t+1},x_{t+1}}}&\text{if }x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{I}\\
\bigl(\mathbb{P}_{\zeta(U_{t+1})}\bigr)_{\gamma_{{t+1},x_{t+1}}}&\text{if }x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{U}\\
\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}_{\pi\bigl(\varphi(x_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\bigr)}\otimes\bigl(\mu_{L}\bigr)_{\psi_{{t+1},x_{t+1}}}&\text{if }x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{I}\\
\bigl(\mu_{L}\bigr)_{\gamma_{{t+1},x_{t+1}}}&\text{if }x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{U}\\
\end{cases}\\
&=\mathbb{P}_{\varphi(x_{t+1},Y_{t+1})}=\mathbb{P}_{\varphi(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\mid Z_{t}=z_{t},\dots,Z_{1}=z_{1},X_{t+1}=x_{t+1},\dots,X_{1}=x_{1},U_{t+1}=u_{t+1},\dots,U_{1}=u_{1}}\\
&=\mathbb{P}_{Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{t}=z_{t},\dots,Z_{1}=z_{1},X_{t+1}=x_{t+1},\dots,X_{1}=x_{1},U_{t+1}=u_{t+1},\dots,U_{1}=u_{1}}\;.\end{aligned}

So, if
A_{t+1}\subset\mathcal{Z},D\subset\mathcal{Z}^{t}\times\mathcal{X}^{t+1}\times[0,1]^{t+1}
, we have that

\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{Z}_{t+1},\bigl(\widetilde{Z}_{t}\dots,\widetilde{Z}_{1},\widetilde{X}_{t+1},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{1},\xi(U_{t+1}),\dots,\xi(U_{1})\bigr)}(A_{t+1}\times D)\\
\begin{aligned} &=\int_{D}\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{Z}_{t+1}\mid\widetilde{Z}_{t:1}=z_{t:1},\widetilde{X}_{t+1:1}=x_{t+1:1},\bigl(\xi(U_{t+1}),\dots,\xi(U_{1})\bigr)=u_{t+1:1}}(A_{t+1})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{t+1}(z_{t:1},x_{t+1:1},u_{t+1:1})\\
&=\int_{D}\mathbb{P}_{Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{t:1}=z_{t:1},X_{t+1:1}=x_{t+1:1},U_{t+1:1}=u_{t+1:1}}(A_{t+1})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{t+1}(z_{t:1},x_{t+1:1},u_{t+1:1})\\
&=\mathbb{P}_{Z_{t+1},\bigl(Z_{t},\dots,Z_{1},X_{t+1},\dots,X_{1},U_{t+1},\dots,U_{1}\bigr)}(A_{t+1}\times D)\\
\end{aligned}

from which it follows that
\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{Z}_{t+1:1},\widetilde{X}_{t+1:1},\bigl(\xi(U_{t+1}),\dots,\xi(U_{1})\bigr)}=\mathbb{P}_{Z_{t+1:1},X_{t+1:1},U_{t+1:1}}
.
In particular, for each
t\in[T]
we have that
\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}}=\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{X}_{t}}
. So, for each
t\in[T]
, using the
\mathbb{P}
-independence of
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{t}
, we have that

\mathbb{P}_{X_{t},Y_{t}}=\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y_{t}}=\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{X}_{t}}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y_{t}}=\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{X}_{t},Y_{t}}\;,

and then

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{X_{t},Y_{t}}}\bigl[\rho\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{X}_{t},Y_{t}}}\bigl[\rho\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[\rho(\widetilde{X}_{t},Y_{t})\bigr]\;.

In conclusion

R_{T}^{\mathbb{P}}(\alpha)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho(x,Y_{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})\right]=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\biggl(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\rho(x,Y_{t})\right]-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})\right]\biggr)\\
=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\biggl(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\rho(x,Y_{t})\right]-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\rho(\widetilde{X}_{t},Y_{t})\right]\biggr)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho(x,Y_{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho(\widetilde{X}_{t},Y_{t})\right]=R_{T}^{\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{\alpha})\;.

Since
\mathbb{P}
was arbitrary, it follows that
R_{T}^{\mathscr{S}}(\alpha)=R_{T}^{\mathscr{S}}(\widetilde{\alpha})
. Since
\alpha
was arbitrary, it follows that

R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}})=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}})}R_{T}^{\mathscr{S}}(\alpha)=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}})}R_{T}^{\mathscr{S}}(\widetilde{\alpha})\geq\inf_{\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathscr{A}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}})}R_{T}^{\mathscr{S}}(\alpha^{\prime})=R^{\star}_{T}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}})\;.

∎

Appendix E
\sqrt{T}
Lower Bound Under Full-Feedback (iv+bd)

In this section, we prove that in the full-feedback case, no strategy can beat the
\sqrt{T}
rate that we proved in

Theorem

1
when the seller/buyer pair
(S_{t},B_{t})
is drawn i.i.d. from an unknown fixed distribution, not even under the further assumptions that the valuations of the seller and buyer are independent of each other and have bounded densities.

The idea of the proof is to build a family of scenarios
\mathbb{P}^{\pm\varepsilon}
parameterized by
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
, like in
Fig.

1
.
The only way to avoid suffering linear regret in a scenario
\mathbb{P}^{\pm\varepsilon}
is to identify the sign of
\pm\varepsilon
.
Leveraging the Embedding and Simulation lemmas (
Lemmas

3
and
4
), this construction leads to a reduction to a two-action expert problem, which has a know lower bound on the regret of order
\sqrt{T}
.

Theorem 9

(
Theorem

2
, restated)
.

In the full-feedback stochastic (iid) setting with independent valuations (iv) and densities bounded by a constant
M\geq 4
(bd), for all horizons
T\in\mathbb{N}
, the minimax regret satisfies

R^{\star}_{T}=\Omega\bigl(\sqrt{T}\bigr)\;.

Proof.

Fix any horizon
T\in\mathbb{N}
and any
M\geq 4
.
Recalling Appendix
C.2
, the full-feedback stochastic (iid) setting with independent valuations (iv) and densities bounded (bd) by
M
is a game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}:=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z},\rho,\varphi,\mathscr{P})
, where
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]
,
\mathcal{Y}=[0,1]^{2}
,
\mathcal{Z}=[0,1]^{2}
,
\rho=\gft
,
\varphi\colon\bigl(p,(s,b)\bigr)\mapsto(s,b)
, and
\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iv+bd}}^{M}
.
Define, for each
\varepsilon\in[-1,1]
, the densities
f_{S,\varepsilon}=2(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{I}_{[0,\frac{1}{4}]}+2(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{I}_{[\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{4}]}
and
f_{B}=2\mathbb{I}_{[\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}]\cup[\frac{3}{4},1]}
.
Fix the adversary’s behavior
\mathscr{P}_{1}
as the subset of
\mathscr{P}
whose elements have the form
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varepsilon}:=\otimes_{t\in\mathbb{N}}(f_{S,\varepsilon}\mu_{L}\otimes f_{B}\mu_{L})
, for some
\varepsilon\in[-1,1]
.
Since
\mathscr{P}_{1}\subset\mathscr{P}
, the game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{1}:=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z},\rho,\varphi,\mathscr{P}_{1})
is easier than
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}
(i.e.,
R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}})\geq R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{1})
) by the Embedding lemma (
Lemma

3
) with
\widetilde{\mathscr{f}}
and
\mathscr{g}
as the identities, and
\mathscr{h}
as the inclusion.
Now, define
\rho_{1}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
,
\left(p,(s,b)\right)\mapsto(b-s)\mathbb{I}\left\{s\leq\frac{1}{4}\leq b\right\}\mathbb{I}\left\{p\leq\frac{1}{2}\right\}+(b-s)\mathbb{I}\left\{s\leq\frac{3}{4}\leq b\right\}\mathbb{I}\left\{p>\frac{1}{2}\right\}
and note that, defining
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{2}:=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z},\rho_{1},\varphi,\mathscr{P}_{1})
, by the Embedding lemma with
\widetilde{\mathscr{f}},\mathscr{g},\mathscr{h}
as the identities, we have that the game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{2}
is easier than the game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{1}
(i.e.,
R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{1})\geq R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{2})
).
Then, let
\mathcal{Z}_{3}:=\{0,1\}\times\bigl[0,\frac{1}{4}\bigr]\times[0,1]
and
\varphi_{3}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}_{3}
,
\bigl(p,(s,b)\bigr)\mapsto\bigl(\mathbb{I}\{s\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}\},\,s\mathbb{I}\{s\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}\}+(s-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})\mathbb{I}\{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\leq s\leq\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}\},\,b\bigr)
.
Define the game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{3}:=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z}_{3},\rho_{1},\varphi_{3},\mathscr{P}_{1})
.
By the Embedding lemma with
\widetilde{\mathscr{f}},\mathscr{h}
as the identities and
\mathscr{g}\colon\mathcal{Z}_{3}\to\mathcal{Z}
,
(i,\widetilde{s},b)\mapsto\bigl(\widetilde{s}i+(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\widetilde{s})(1-i),\,b\bigr)
, we have that the game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{3}
is easier than the game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{2}
(i.e.,
R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{2})\geq R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{3})
).
Next, let
\varphi_{4}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}_{3}
,
\bigl(p,(s,b)\bigr)\mapsto\mathbb{I}\{s\leq\frac{1}{4}\}
, and define the game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{4}:=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z}_{3},\rho_{1},\varphi_{4},\mathscr{P}_{1})
.
Let
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be the adversary’s actions in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{4}
.
A tedious computation verifies that for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in\mathcal{X}
, and scenarios
\mathbb{P}
of game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{3}
,
\mathbb{P}_{\varphi_{3}(p,Y_{t})}=\mathbb{P}_{\pi(\varphi_{3}(p,Y_{t}))}\otimes(\nu\otimes f_{B}\mu_{L})
, where
\pi\colon\mathcal{Z}_{3}\to\{0,1\}
is the projection on the first component
\{0,1\}
of
\mathcal{Z}_{3}
and
\nu
is the uniform distribution on
[0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}]
.
By the well-known Skorokhod representation
(
Williams 1991
, Section 17.3)
, there exists
\psi\colon[0,1]\to[0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}]\times[0,1]
such that
\nu\otimes f_{B}\mu_{L}=(\mu_{L})_{\psi}
.
Thus, by the Simulation lemma (
Lemma

4
) with
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{U}=\varnothing
, the game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{4}
is easier than
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{3}
(i.e.,
R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{3})\geq R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{4})
).
Finally, consider the game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{5}:=\bigl(\{1,2\},\{1,2\},\{0,1\},\rho_{5},\varphi_{5},\mathscr{P}_{5}\bigr)
, where in matrix notation,
\rho_{5}=\bigl[\rho_{5}(i,j)\bigr]_{i,j\in\{1,2\}}
and
\varphi_{5}=\bigl[\varphi_{5}(i,j)\bigr]_{i,j\in\{1,2\}}
are given by

\rho_{5}:=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&3/8\\
3/8&1/2\end{bmatrix}\;,\qquad\varphi_{5}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\;,

and
\mathscr{P}_{5}
is the set of all measures
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\varepsilon}
of the form
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\varepsilon}=\otimes_{t=1}^{\infty}\bigl(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\delta_{1}+\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\delta_{2}\bigr)
for some
\varepsilon\in[-1,1]
, where
\delta_{i}
is the Dirac measure at
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Thus, letting
\mathscr{S}_{4}
and
\mathscr{S}_{5}
be the two sets of scenarios in games
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{4}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{5}
respectively (note that
\mathscr{S}_{4}
coincides with the set of scenarios of
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{1}
) and using again the Embedding lemma, this time with
\widetilde{\mathscr{f}}\colon[0,1]\to\{1,2\}
,
p\mapsto\mathbb{I}\{p\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\}+2\mathbb{I}\{p>\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\}
,
\mathscr{g}\colon\{0,1\}\to\{0,1\}
,
i\mapsto i
, and
\mathscr{h}\colon\mathscr{S}_{5}\to\mathscr{S}_{4}
,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mu}_{L}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mu}_{L}
, we obtain that
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{5}
is easier than
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{4}
(i.e.,
R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{4})\geq R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{5})
).
This last game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{5}
is an online learning problem with full information (also known as learning with expert advice), whose minimax regret is known to be lower bounded by
\frac{1}{8\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{T}

(
Cover 1965
)
.
In conclusion, we proved that
R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}})\geq R^{\star}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}_{5})\geq\frac{1}{8\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{T}
.
∎

Appendix F
Proof of
T^{2/3}
Lower Bound Under Realistic Feedback (iv+bd)

In this section we give a detailed proof of our
T^{2/3}
lower bound of
Section

5.2

which hinges in a non-trivial way on our Embedding and Simulation lemmas (
Lemmas

3
and
4
).
We denote Bernoulli distributions with parameter
\lambda
by
\mathrm{Ber}_{\lambda}
.

Theorem 10

(
Theorem

4
, restated)
.

In the realistic-feedback stochastic (iid) setting with independent valuations (iv) and densities bounded by a constant
M\geq 24
(bd), for all horizons
T\in\mathbb{N}
, the minimax regret satisfies

R^{\star}_{T}\geq\frac{11}{672}T^{2/3}\;.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary horizon
T\in\mathbb{N}
and any
M\geq 24
.
Recalling Appendix
C.2
, the realistic-feedback stochastic (iid) setting with independent valuations (iv) and densities bounded (bd) by
M
is a game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}:=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z},\rho,\varphi,\mathscr{P})
, where
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]
,
\mathcal{Y}=[0,1]^{2}
,
\mathcal{Z}=\{0,1\}^{2}
,
\rho=\gft
,
\varphi\colon\bigl(p,(s,b)\bigr)\mapsto\bigl(\mathbb{I}\{s\leq p\},\,\mathbb{I}\{p\leq b\}\bigr)
, and
\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iv+bd}}^{M}
.
The idea of the proof is to build a sequence of games, each one easier than the former, the last of which has a known lower bound on its minimax regret.
In the first step we limit the adversary’s behavior to a parametric family which is easily manageable and well-represents the difficulty of the problem (see
Fig.

2
).
In the second step, we increase the reward of suboptimal actions in order to have only three possible expected-reward values in each scenario.
In the third and fifth steps we increase the feedback, presenting it in a way that highlights that only its first component is informative.
In step four and six, we simulate-away the uninformative parts of the feedback.
Finally, in step 7 we show that the resulting game is harder than a known partial monitoring game with minimax regret of order at least
T^{2/3}
.

Step 1.

Let
\vartheta:=\nicefrac{{1}}{{48}}

Appendix H

In this section, we prove that in the realistic-feedback case, no strategy can achieve sublinear regret without any limitations on how concentrated the distributions of the valuations of the seller and buyer are, not even if they are independent of each other (iv).

The idea of the proof is that if the two distributions are very concentrated in a small region, finding an optimal price is like finding a needle in a haystack.
Each strategy that (at each time step) receives as feedback only a finite number of bits, as in our realistic setting, can assign positive probability to at most a countable set of points.
Thus one could find concentrated distributions of the buyer and seller that have a unique optimal point in which the strategy has zero probability of posting prices at all time steps, and such that
all

z_{1},\dots,z_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{2}
,

\bar{\alpha}_{t+1,z_{1:t}}\colon[0,1]^{t+1}\to[0,1],\quad u_{1:t+1}\mapsto\alpha_{t+1}(u_{1:t+1},z_{1:t})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\nu_{t+1,z_{1:t}}:=(\otimes_{s=1}^{t+1}\mu_{L})_{\bar{\alpha}_{t+1,z_{1:t}}}\;.

Define also the set
A_{1}:=\bigl\{x\in\left[0,1\right]\mid\nu_{1}[\{x\}]>0\bigr\}
and, for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the union

A_{t+1}:=\bigcup_{z_{1:t}\in\{0,1\}^{2}}\bigl\{x\in\left[0,1\right]\mid\nu_{t,z_{1:t}}[\{x\}]>0\bigr\}
.
Note that, for each
t\in\mathbb{N},A_{t}
is countable, being the union of
4^{t-1}
countable sets.
Then
A:=\bigcup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}A_{t}
is countable.
Since
B:=[\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2},\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}]
has the power of continuum, we have that the same holds for
B\setminus A
.
In particular,
B\setminus A
is non-empty.
Pick
x^{\star}\in B\backslash A
and define
\mu_{S}:=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{x^{\star}}
,

\mu_{B}:=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{x^{\star}}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1}
,
and
\mathbb{P}:=\left(\otimes_{t\in\mathbb{N}}(\mu_{S}\otimes\mu_{B})\right)\otimes\boldsymbol{\mu}_{L}\in\mathscr{S}
. Then for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[\rho(x^{\star},Y_{t})\bigr]=\frac{x^{\star}+(1-x^{\star})+1}{4}\;.

On the other hand,
\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=x^{\star}]=\nu_{1}[\{x^{\star}\}]=0
and for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that

\mathbb{P}[X_{t+1}=x^{\star}]=\mathbb{P}\left[\alpha_{t+1}(U_{1},\dots,U_{t+1},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{t})=x^{\star}\right]\\
\begin{aligned} &=\sum_{z_{1},\dots,z_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{2}}\mathbb{P}\left[\alpha_{t+1}(U_{1},\dots,U_{t+1},z_{1},\dots,z_{t})=x^{\star}\cap Z_{1}=z_{1}\cap\dots\cap Z_{t}=z_{t}\right]\\
&\leq\sum_{z_{1},\dots,z_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{2}}\mathbb{P}\left[\alpha_{t+1}(U_{1},\dots,U_{t+1},z_{1},\dots,z_{t})=x^{\star}\right]=\sum_{z_{1},\dots,z_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{2}}\nu_{t+1,z_{1},\dots,z_{t}}\left[\{x^{\star}\}\right]=0\;,\end{aligned}

which in turn gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})\bigr]=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\rho\bigl(X_{t},(0,x^{\star})\bigr)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\rho\bigl(X_{t},(x^{\star},1)\bigr)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\rho\bigl(X_{t},(0,1)\bigr)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\rho\bigl(X_{t},(x^{\star},x^{\star})\bigr)\right]}{4}

\displaystyle\hskip 29.25865pt=\frac{x^{\star}\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}}\bigl[[0,x^{\star}]\bigr]+(1-x^{\star})\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}}\bigl[[x^{\star},1]\bigr]+1}{4}=\frac{x^{\star}\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}}\bigl[[0,x^{\star})\bigr]+(1-x^{\star})\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}}\bigl[(x^{\star},1]\bigr]+1}{4}

\displaystyle\hskip 29.25865pt\leq\frac{\max(x^{\star},1-x^{\star})+1}{4}=\frac{x^{\star}+(1-x^{\star})+1-\min(x^{\star},1-x^{\star})}{4}\;.

So, if
T\in\mathbb{N}
we get

R^{\mathbb{P}}_{T}(\alpha)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho(x^{\star},Y_{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})\right]\geq\frac{\min(x^{\star},1-x^{\star})}{4}T\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{8}T.

Since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary, we get, for all
T\in\mathbb{N}
,

R_{T}^{\mathscr{S}}(\alpha)=\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathscr{S}}R^{\mathbb{P}}_{T}(\alpha)\geq\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\frac{1-\varepsilon}{8}T=\nicefrac{{T}}{{8}}
.
Since
\alpha
was arbitrary we get, for each
T\in\mathbb{N}
,

R^{\star}_{T}=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}}R_{T}^{\mathscr{S}}(\alpha)\geq\nicefrac{{T}}{{8}}
.
∎

Appendix I
Adversarial Setting: Linear Lower Bound Under Full Feedback

In this section, we give a more detailed proof of
Theorem

7
with a notation consistent with our abstract setting of sequential games.

Theorem 13

(
Theorem

7
, restated)
.

In the full-feedback adversarial (adv) setting, for all horizons
T\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

R^{\star}_{T}\geq\frac{1}{4}T\;.

Proof.

Recalling Appendix
C.2
, the full-feedback adversarial (adv) bilateral trade setting is a game
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}:=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z},\rho,\varphi,\mathscr{P})
, where
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]
,
\mathcal{Y}=[0,1]^{2}
,
\mathcal{Z}=[0,1]^{2}
,
\rho=\gft
,
\varphi\colon\bigl(p,(s,b)\bigr)\mapsto(s,b)
, and
\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{adv}}
.
Let
\mathscr{S}
be the set of scenarios of
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}
.
Fix a strategy
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}
and an
\varepsilon\in(0,1/18)
.
Define
\bar{\alpha}_{1}:=\alpha_{1}
,
\nu_{1}:=(\mu_{L})_{\bar{\alpha}_{1}}
, and

\begin{cases}c_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon,\ d_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon,\ s_{1}:=0,\ b_{1}:=d_{1},&\text{ if }\nu_{1}\bigl[\bigl[0,\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\bigr]\bigr]\leq\frac{1}{2}\;,\\
c_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon,\ d_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon,\ s_{1}:=c_{1},\ b_{1}:=1,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

If
t\in\mathbb{N}
, suppose we defined
\bar{\alpha}_{t},\nu_{t},c_{t},d_{t},s_{t},b_{t}
and let

\bar{\alpha}_{t+1}:[0,1]^{t+1}\to[0,1],(u_{1},\dots,u_{t+1})\mapsto\alpha_{t+1}\left(u_{1},\dots,u_{t+1},(s_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(s_{t},b_{t})\right),

\nu_{t+1}:=\bigl(\otimes_{s=1}^{t+1}\mu_{L}\bigr)_{\bar{\alpha}_{t+1}}
, and

\begin{cases}c_{t+1}:=c_{t},\ d_{t+1}:=d_{t}-\frac{2\varepsilon}{3^{t}},\ s_{t+1}:=0,\ b_{t+1}:=d_{t+1},&\text{if }\nu_{t+1}\bigl[\bigl[0,c_{t}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3^{t}}\bigr]\bigr]\leq\frac{1}{2}\;,\\
c_{t+1}:=c_{t}+\frac{2\varepsilon}{3^{t}},\ d_{t+1}:=d_{t},\ s_{t+1}:=c_{t+1},\ b_{t+1}:=1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then
(\bar{\alpha}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}},(\nu_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}},(c_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}},(d_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}},(s_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}},(b_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
are well-defined by induction and satisfy:

•

For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
d_{t}-c_{t}=\frac{\varepsilon}{3^{t-1}}
.

•

For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
c_{1}\leq c_{2}\leq c_{3}\leq\dots\leq c_{t}\leq d_{t}\leq\dots\leq d_{3}\leq d_{2}\leq d_{1}
.

•

\exists!x^{\star}\in\bigcap_{t=1}^{\infty}[c_{t},d_{t}]
.

•

For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
\rho\left(x^{\star},(s_{t},b_{t})\right)=b_{t}-s_{t}\geq\frac{1-3\varepsilon}{2}
.

•

For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{P}\bigl[\alpha_{t}\bigl(U_{1},\dots,U_{t},(s_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(s_{t-1},b_{t-1})\bigr)\in[s_{t},b_{t}]\bigr]\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

Now, define
\mathbb{P}:=\left(\otimes_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\delta_{(s_{t},b_{t})}\right)\otimes\boldsymbol{\mu}_{L}\in\mathscr{S}
. Then, for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\rho\left(X_{t},Y_{t}\right)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[\rho\Bigl(\alpha_{t}\bigl(U_{1},\dots,U_{t},(s_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(s_{t-1},b_{t-1})\bigr),(s_{t},b_{t})\Bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}\right)\mathbb{P}\bigl[\alpha_{t}\bigl(U_{1},\dots,U_{t},(s_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(s_{t-1},b_{t-1})\bigr)\in[s_{t},b_{t}]\bigr]\leq\frac{1}{4}+\frac{3\varepsilon}{4}\;,

and so, for each
T\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle R^{\mathbb{P}}_{T}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho(x^{\star},Y_{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})\right]=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho(x^{\star},(s_{t},b_{t}))-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\rho\left(X_{t},Y_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}(b_{t}-s_{t})\bigl(1-\mathbb{P}\bigl[\alpha_{t}\bigl(U_{1},\dots,U_{t},(s_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(s_{t-1},b_{t-1})\bigr)\in[s_{t},b_{t}]\bigr]\bigr)\geq\frac{1-3\varepsilon}{4}T\;.

Since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary, we get, for all
T\in\mathbb{N}
,

R_{T}^{\mathscr{S}}(\alpha)=\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathscr{S}}R^{\mathbb{P}}_{T}(\alpha)\geq\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1/18)}\frac{1-3\varepsilon}{4}T=\frac{T}{4}
.
Since
\alpha
arbitrarity, we get, for each
T\in\mathbb{N}
,

R^{\star}_{T}=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}}R_{T}^{\mathscr{S}}(\alpha)\geq\frac{T}{4}
.
∎

Appendix J
DKW Inequalities

We begin this section by presenting the univariate DKW inequality as proved in
(
Massart 1990
)
.

Theorem 14
.

If
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space and
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
\mathbb{P}
-i.i.d. sequence of random variables, then, for any
\varepsilon>0
and all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds

\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}\{X_{k}\leq x\}-\mathbb{P}[X_{1}\leq x]\right\rvert>\varepsilon\right]\leq 2\operatorname{exp}\bigl(-2m\varepsilon^{2}\bigr)\;.

We now present a bivariate DKW inequality which can be proved by applying the VC-type bound of
(
Anthony and Bartlett 2009
, Theorem 4.9; see also Lemmas 4.4, 4.5, and 4.11 for the explicit constants)
.

Theorem 15
.

There exist positive constants
m_{0}\leq 1200
,
c_{1}\leq 13448
,
c_{2}\geq 1/576
such that, if
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space,
(X_{n},Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
\mathbb{P}
-i.i.d. sequence of two-dimensional random vectors, then, for any
\varepsilon>0
and all
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m\geq m_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}
, it holds

\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}\{X_{k}\leq x,Y_{k}\leq y\}-\mathbb{P}[X_{1}\leq x,Y_{1}\leq y]\right\rvert>\varepsilon\right]\leq c_{1}\operatorname{exp}\bigl(-c_{2}m\varepsilon^{2}\bigr)\;.
