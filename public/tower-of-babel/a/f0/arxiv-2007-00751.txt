Next-to-leading order Balitsky-Kovchegov equation beyond large N c N_{\mathrm{c}}
T. Lappi, H. Mäntysaari, A. Ramnath

Abstract
We calculate finite- N c N_{\mathrm{c}} corrections to the next-to-leading order (NLO) Balitsky-Kovchegov (BK) equation. We find analytical expressions for the necessary correlators of six Wilson lines in terms of the two-point function using the Gaussian approximation. In a suitable basis, the problem reduces from the diagonalization of a six-by-six matrix to the diagonalization of a three-by-three matrix, which can easily be done analytically. We study numerically the effects of these finite- N c N_{\mathrm{c}} corrections on the NLO BK equation. In general, we find that the finite- N c N_{\mathrm{c}} corrections are smaller than the expected 1 / N c 2 ∼ 10 % 1/N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\sim 10\% . The corrections may be large for individual correlators, but have less of an influence on the shape of the amplitude as a function of the dipole size. They have an even smaller effect on the evolution speed as a function of rapidity.



I
Introduction

In hadronic collisions at high energies, large gluon densities are created by the emission of soft gluons carrying a small fraction of the longitudinal momentum of the parent
Abramowicz:2015mha
.
Nonlinear dynamics of gluons becomes important in such an environment, where parton densities eventually grow to become on the order of the inverse of the QCD coupling
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}
.
To describe QCD in this region, the Color Glass Condensate (CGC) effective field theory
Gelis:2010nm
has been developed.

In the CGC framework, cross sections for various scattering processes can be expressed in terms of correlators of Wilson lines.
A Wilson line describes the eikonal propagation of a parton in the strong color field of the target.
The energy dependence of the target color fields, and thus cross sections, is obtained by solving the so-called Jalilian-Marian–Iancu–McLerran–Weigert–Leonidov–Kovner (JIMWLK) equation
JalilianMarian:1996xn
;
JalilianMarian:1997jx
;
JalilianMarian:1997gr
;
Iancu:2001md
.
This is a perturbative evolution equation that describes the Bjorken-
x
dependence of a Wilson line.
In phenomenological applications, it is usually convenient to work directly in terms of the Wilson line correlators, and to solve instead the Balitsky-Kovchegov (BK) equation
Kovchegov:1999yj
;
Balitsky:1995ub
for the dipole operator (correlator of two Wilson lines), which can be obtained from the JIMWLK equation in the large-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
limit.

The CGC framework has been used extensively in phenomenological applications at leading order (LO) in
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}
, with the evolution equations resumming contributions
\sim\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}\ln 1/x
to all orders.
Running coupling effects derived in Refs.
Kovchegov:2006vj
;
Gardi:2006rp
;
Albacete:2007yr
;
Balitsky:2006wa
(see also
Lappi:2012vw
) can also be taken into account.
The non-perturbative initial condition for the small-
x
evolution is obtained by performing fits to the HERA structure function data
Abramowicz:2015mha
;
H1:2018flt
, for example in Refs.
Albacete:2010sy
;
Albacete:2012rx
;
Lappi:2013zma
;
Mantysaari:2018zdd
(see also
Rezaeian:2012ji
;
Mantysaari:2018nng
).
The obtained initial condition can then be used for various calculations, for example particle production in proton-nucleus collisions
Tribedy:2011aa
;
Goncalves:2012bn
;
Lappi:2013zma
;
Ducloue:2015gfa
;
Ducloue:2016pqr
;
Albacete:2016tjq
;
Ducloue:2017kkq
;
Mantysaari:2019nnt
.
In the future, the nuclear deep inelastic scattering (DIS) experiments at the Electron Ion Collider (EIC)
Aschenauer:2017jsk
;
Accardi:2012qut
in the US, at the LHeC
AbelleiraFernandez:2012cc
at CERN and at the EicC in China
Chen:2018wyz
will provide a vast amount of precise data from clean DIS processes.
These experiments will be able to probe the nuclear structure where nonlinearities are enhanced by roughly
A^{1/3}
higher densities compared to the proton.
Before the EIC, similar studies limited to the photoproduction region can be performed in ultra-peripheral heavy-ion collisions
Bertulani:2005ru
;
Klein:2019qfb
.

In order to quantitatively study nonlinear dynamics in high-energy scattering processes (and especially at the future EIC), it is crucial to move beyond LO accuracy.
The next-to-leading order (NLO) evolution equations are available: the NLO BK equation was derived in Ref.
Balitsky:2008zza
and the NLO JIMWLK equation was derived in Refs.
Balitsky:2013fea
;
Kovner:2013ona
.
Similarly, the impact factors are becoming available at NLO for some processes: inclusive DIS

;
Beuf:2017bpd
;

;

;

(in the case of massless quarks), exclusive vector meson production
Boussarie:2016bkq
;
Escobedo:2019bxn
(see also
Lappi:2020ufv
) and particle production in proton–nucleus collisions
Chirilli:2012jd
.
However, the phenomenological applications of these are still developing

;
Ducloue:2016shw
;
Watanabe:2015tja
;
Altinoluk:2014eka
;
Stasto:2013cha
;
Liu:2019iml

The BK equation is usually solved in the large-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
limit. In the LO case, the large-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
limit makes it possible to express the four-point correlator of fundamental representation Wilson lines in terms of the two-point function.
In detailed numerical studies, it has been shown that the finite-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
corrections are smaller than the naive expectation of
\mathcal{O}(1/N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2})

Rummukainen:2003ns
;
Kovchegov:2008mk
.
At NLO, the equation involves six-point functions of fundamental Wilson lines that must similarly be expressed in terms of the two-point function in order to close the equation.
The purpose of this work is to see if the finite-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
corrections are similarly small in the case of the NLO equation, where all corrections of the order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}
are taken into account.

In order to numerically solve the BK equation at finite
N_{\mathrm{c}}
, we use the Gaussian approximation
Fujii:2006ab
;
Marquet:2010cf
;
Dominguez:2011wm

to derive analytical parametric equations for the six-point correlators in terms of the two-point correlators.
We study numerically the finite-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
corrections to the these correlators, and also their effect on the NLO BK evolution.
In addition to the BK equation, higher-point correlators are needed in the calculations of multi-particle correlations in the CGC framework, see eg. Refs.
Marquet:2007vb
;
Lappi:2012nh
;
Dusling:2017aot
;
Dominguez:2012ad
.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
In Section II, we introduce the NLO BK equation and provide both the large-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
and finite-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
expressions for the correlators that will be studied.
In Section III, we introduce the Gaussian approximation, explain the diagrammatic notation used in the rest of the paper and then explain the analytical calculation done for finding the parametric equations for the six-point correlators.
Section IV contains the numerical results obtained from using the analytical expressions for the six-point correlators to solve the BK equation.
Finally, we end with a few concluding remarks and a summary of our main results.

The interpretation of the NLO BK equation is that one considers all possible ways to emit either one or two gluons, at transverse coordinates
\ib{z}
and
\ib{z}^{\prime}
, from the boosted dipole consisting of quarks at transverse coordinates
\ib{x}
and
\ib{y}
.
The effect of the boost is that instead of the original dipole projectile, the original quarks and the emitted gluons scatter off the target color field.
As such, the evolution can be seen to describe the evolution of the projectile probing the target structure.
On the other hand, the emitted gluons can also be taken to be a part of the target wave function, in which case the boost corresponds to the evolving target color field as probed by the original projectile.
For a more detailed discussion on the NLO evolution in the projectile or target wave function, the reader is referred to Ref.
Ducloue:2019ezk
.

The NLO BK equation is known to be unstable

III
Six-point functions in the Gaussian approximation

III.1
The Gaussian approximation

At large
N_{\mathrm{c}}
, all Wilson line operators present in the NLO BK equation can be expressed solely in terms of dipole correlators, as can be seen from Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
).
At finite
N_{\mathrm{c}}
, on the other hand, the higher-point functions
\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{x,z}}}}S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{z,y}}}}\right\rangle

\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{x,z}}}}S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{z,z^{\prime}}}}}S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{z^{\prime},y}}}}\right\rangle

into a normal exponential. This, in turn, makes it possible to relate higher-point functions to the two-point function without any further assumptions about the
\eta
dependence of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. In the terminology of Ref.
Marquet:2010cf
, we use a “rigid exponentiation” instead of the “Gaussian Truncation”. The “Gaussian Truncation” would imply equating the parametrization rapidity
\eta
with the evolution rapidity
Y
. See the related discussion in Refs.
Iancu:2011ns
;
Lappi:2016gqe
.
, as is usually done when employing the Gaussian approximation (see e.g.
Blaizot:2004wu
;
Blaizot:2004wv
;
Fujii:2006ab
;
Dominguez:2008aa
;
Dominguez:2011wm
;
Dumitru:2011vk
;
Lappi:2012nh
) in phenomenological applications.
We henceforth omit the explicit
\eta
dependence of
\mathcal{G}
for brevity.

As an example of the procedure for finding the parametric equation for a correlator using Eq. (
25
), we illustrate the steps for the dipole operator
\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{x,y}}}}\right\rangle
.
A more practical form of Eq. (
25
) is to write the integral over the parametrization rapidity
\eta
in differential form.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{x,y}}}}\right\rangle=-\mathcal{M}(\eta)\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{x,y}}}}\right\rangle,

(31)

where
\mathcal{M}
is the so-called transition matrix formed by operating with the argument of the exponential in Eq. (
25
) on the operator of interest.
In this case, there are four contributions from acting with the Lie derivatives on a product of two Wilson lines:

\displaystyle L^{{a}}_{\ib{{{u_{\textrm{1}}}}}}L^{{a}}_{\ib{{{u_{\textrm{2}}}}}}\Big(U_{\ib{{x}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{y}}}\Big)=L^{{a}}_{\ib{{{u_{\textrm{1}}}}}}L^{{a}}_{\ib{{{u_{\textrm{2}}}}}}\;\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{emp2}}}\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tar2}}}\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{emp2}}}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\;\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{emp2}}}\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tar2}}}\parbox{30.39113pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{gtuu}}}\;+\;\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{emp2}}}\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tar2}}}\parbox{30.39113pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{gtud}}}

\displaystyle+\;\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{emp2}}}\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tar2}}}\parbox{30.39113pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{gtdu}}}\;+\;\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{emp2}}}\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tar2}}}\parbox{30.39113pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{gtdd}}}\;.

From this sum, we need to factorize out the original operator
U_{\ib{{x}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{y}}}
, so we use the Fierz identity

\displaystyle 2\;\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{fierz_tt}}}\;=\;\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{fierz_2}}}\;-\;\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\;\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{fierz_1}}}\;.

There is only one way to join the endpoints of the Wilson lines into a singlet operator. This is to trace over them, i.e. wedging them between
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}}}\;\parbox{12.26942pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1l2}}}
and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}}}\;\parbox{12.26942pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1r2}}}\;
.
Doing so and performing the remaining operations in the exponent of Eq. (
25
), we get

\partial_{\eta}\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{x,y}}}}\right\rangle=-C_{\mathrm{F}}\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,y}}}^{\prime}\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{x,y}}}}\right\rangle\\
\implies\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\;\parbox{9.65717pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1l2}}}\parbox{20.0525pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tar2}}}\parbox{9.65717pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1r2}}}\;=\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{x,y}}}}\right\rangle=e^{-C_{\mathrm{F}}\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,y}}}},

(50)

where
C_{\mathrm{F}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}-1}{2N_{\mathrm{c}}}
.
Since the operators

and

were normalized, the initial condition at
\eta=0
for the differential equation (
31
) is given by trivial Wilson lines equal to the identity matrix in the absence of a color field.
This is the well-known parametric equation for the dipole correlator
Marquet:2010cf
in the Gaussian approximation.

In the case of
n
-point correlators larger than the dipole, the operator
\left\langle\mathcal{O}[U]\right\rangle
in Eq. (
25
) is actually an
n\times n
matrix of correlators, denoted
\mathcal{A}(\eta)
, and Eq. (
31
) becomes an
n\times n
matrix differential equation

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}(\eta)=-\mathcal{M}(\eta)\mathcal{A}(\eta).

(51)

By construction,
\mathcal{M}
is a symmetric matrix, so there are at most
\sum^{n}_{i=1}i=n(n+1)/2
distinct elements, not
n\times n
.

For example, a product of six Wilson lines is represented in this notation as

\displaystyle U_{\ib{{z}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{z^{\prime}}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{v}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{y}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{x}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{w}}}=\;\parbox{44.0616pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tarlabel}}}\;.

(the haphazard assignment of coordinate labels is convenient for the NLO BK equation and will become clear when constructing the transition matrix).
The notation here means that this product is actually a matrix with six open indices on the left and another six on the right; we denote them as

\displaystyle\parbox{24.0675pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{empljk}}}\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tar}}}\parbox{23.96873pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{empril}}}\;.

Since there are six possible ways to join the endpoints of these Wilson lines to form a correlator, Eq. (
51
) is a six-by-six matrix differential equation, as opposed to the much simpler one-dimensional problem illustrated for the dipole correlator.

In analogy to the procedure for the dipole operator, the procedure to use Eq. (
51
) to find parametric equations for the six-point correlators is as follows:

1.

Choose a multiplet basis, represented as a column vector
\ib{B}
, of
n=6
color structures for the space of all six-point correlators.
Each element will have six open color indices, which can contract with the open indices on the left of
U_{\ib{{z}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{z^{\prime}}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{v}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{y}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{x}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{w}}}
.
For example, one choice for an element of
\ib{B}
could be

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}}\;\parbox{13.54619pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1l}}}\parbox{24.0675pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{emprjk}}}\;=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}}\delta_{j_{1},k_{1}}\delta_{j_{2},k_{2}}\delta_{j_{3},k_{3}}

and the corresponding element for the other end of the Wilson line is then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}}\;\parbox{23.96873pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{emplil}}}\parbox{12.26942pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1r}}}\;=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}}\delta_{i_{1},l_{1}}\delta_{i_{2},l_{2}}\delta_{i_{3},l_{3}}.

The prefactor is a normalization constant found by squaring the basis element.

2.

Construct the correlator matrix
\mathcal{A}
by taking
\ib{B}\left(U_{\ib{{z}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{z^{\prime}}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{v}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{y}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{x}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{w}}}\right)\ib{B}^{\mathrm{T}}
. The elements of
\ib{B}
on the left are contracted with the open color indices on the left of the Wilson lines, and the elements of
\ib{B}^{\mathrm{T}}
with the open indices on the right.
For example, using the basis element shown above, we have for one of the 36 elements in
\mathcal{A}
,

\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}\;\parbox{13.54619pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1l}}}\parbox{24.0675pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{emprjk}}}\parbox{30.09pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tar}}}\parbox{23.96873pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{emplil}}}\parbox{12.26942pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1r}}}\\
\\
=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}\mathrm{tr}\left({{U_{\ib{{z}}}U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{z^{\prime}}}}}}\right)\mathrm{tr}\left({{U_{\ib{{v}}}U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{y}}}}}\right)\mathrm{tr}\left({{U_{\ib{{x}}}U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{w}}}}}\right).

(60)

3.

Construct the transition matrix
\mathcal{M}
by summing (for each element in
\mathcal{A}
) all possible one-gluon diagrams obtained with the double Lie derivative operator and rewriting the result in terms of elements of
\mathcal{A}
.

4.

Solve Eq. (
51
) by exponentiating
\mathcal{M}
to find expressions for each element in
\mathcal{A}
, using as an initial condition the correlator matrix
\mathcal{A}
corresponding to Wilson lines equal to the identity matrix.

III.2
Choosing a basis

Starting from a product of six Wilson lines, there are six ways to form multiplets by joining endpoints in all possible ways:

\displaystyle\parbox{10.40195pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1l}}}\qquad\parbox{18.2327pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m2l}}}\qquad\parbox{25.91942pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m3l}}}\qquad\parbox{18.2327pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m4l}}}\qquad\parbox{18.27837pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m5l}}}\qquad\parbox{18.68239pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m6l}}}\;.

The simplest way to construct an orthonormal basis from these would be to use color algebra to choose

\displaystyle\ib{B}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}}\;\parbox{10.40195pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1l}}}\\
\\
\sqrt{\frac{4}{N_{\mathrm{c}}d_{\mathrm{A}}}}\;\parbox{16.1424pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1la}}}\\
\\
\sqrt{\frac{4}{N_{\mathrm{c}}d_{\mathrm{A}}}}\;\parbox{18.94586pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1lb}}}\\
\\
\sqrt{\frac{4}{N_{\mathrm{c}}d_{\mathrm{A}}}}\;\parbox{16.1424pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1lc}}}\\
\\
\frac{1}{i}\sqrt{\frac{8}{N_{\mathrm{c}}d_{\mathrm{A}}}}\;\parbox{19.47986pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{fl}}}\\
\\
\sqrt{\frac{8}{C_{\mathrm{d}}d_{\mathrm{A}}}}\;\parbox{19.47986pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{dl}}}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(67)

The blue lines denote gluons and the last two elements of
\ib{B}
represent the anti-symmetric and symmetric structure constants, respectively,
f_{abc}=-2i\mathrm{tr}\left({{[t_{a},t_{b}],t_{c}}}\right)
and
d_{abc}=2\mathrm{tr}\left({{\{t_{a},t_{b}\},t_{c}}}\right)
.
The color factors are
d_{\mathrm{A}}=N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}-1
and
C_{\mathrm{d}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}-4}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}
.
The next step would be to use this basis to construct the correlator matrix
\mathcal{A}
and the transition matrix
\mathcal{M}
.
However, doing so results in a matrix differential equation
\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}(\eta)=-\mathcal{M}(\eta)\mathcal{A}(\eta)
, whose complicated solution is the matrix exponential of a six-by-six matrix
\mathcal{M}
.

For our case, a better way to proceed is to exploit the structure of the six-point correlators that are actually needed for the NLO BK equation.
Since there are only four distinct coordinates in these particular correlators, we make the coordinate assignments

\displaystyle\parbox{44.0616pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tarlabel}}}\qquad\xrightarrow[\ib{v}\to\ib{z^{\prime}}]{\ib{w}\to\ib{z}}\qquad\parbox{43.91104pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tarlabel2}}}\;.

It is easy to see from this that there is one way to join the endpoints such that in the limit
\ib{v}\to\ib{z^{\prime}},\ib{w}\to\ib{z}
, four Wilson lines cancel (due to unitarity).
The result simplifies to a single trace:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}}\;\parbox{18.68239pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m6l}}}\quad\parbox{20.0525pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tar}}}\quad\parbox{18.68239pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m6r}}}\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}}\quad\xrightarrow[\ib{v}\to\ib{z^{\prime}}]{\ib{w}\to\ib{z}}\quad\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\mathrm{tr}\left({{U_{\ib{{x}}}U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{y}}}}}\right).

So choosing
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}}\;\parbox{18.68239pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m6l}}}\;
as one of our basis elements allows one dimension of our six-dimensional space of operators to decouple, giving the equation for the dipole correlator.
Similarly, the choice of two more particular basis elements results in two correlators that reduce to four-point functions; one due to the limit
\ib{v}\to\ib{z^{\prime}}
and the other due to the limit
\ib{w}\to\ib{z}
. Thus, we can expect to choose a further two basis elements such that two more dimensions decouple from the remaining five, corresponding to the equation for the four-point operators.
These two basis elements can be chosen as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N_{\mathrm{c}}d_{\mathrm{A}}}}

\displaystyle\left[-\;\parbox{18.2327pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m2l}}}\;+\;\parbox{25.91942pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m3l}}}\;\right]

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N_{\mathrm{c}}d_{\mathrm{A}}}}

\displaystyle\left[-\;\parbox{18.2327pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m2l}}}\;-\;\parbox{25.91942pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m3l}}}\;+\frac{2}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\;\parbox{18.68239pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m6l}}}\;\right].

We will choose the remaining three basis elements such that they are orthonormal to the three already chosen, resulting in the final basis vector

\tilde{\ib{B}}\coloneqq\\
\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{N_{\mathrm{c}}\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{A}}C_{\mathrm{d}}}}\left[\frac{N_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}\parbox{10.40195pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1l}}}-\parbox{18.2327pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m2l}}}-\parbox{25.91942pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m3l}}}-\parbox{18.2327pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m4l}}}+\frac{N_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}\parbox{18.27837pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m5l}}}+\frac{2}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\parbox{18.68239pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m6l}}}\right]\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N_{\mathrm{c}}d_{\mathrm{A}}}}\left[-\;\parbox{10.40195pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m1l}}}\;+\;\parbox{18.27837pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m5l}}}\;\right]\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}d_{\mathrm{A}}}}\left[-\;\parbox{18.2327pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m4l}}}\;+\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\;\parbox{18.68239pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m6l}}}\;\right]\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N_{\mathrm{c}}d_{\mathrm{A}}}}\left[-\;\parbox{18.2327pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m2l}}}\;+\;\parbox{25.91942pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m3l}}}\;\right]\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N_{\mathrm{c}}d_{\mathrm{A}}}}\left[-\;\parbox{18.2327pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m2l}}}\;-\;\parbox{25.91942pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m3l}}}\;+\frac{2}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\;\parbox{18.68239pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m6l}}}\;\right]\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}}\;\parbox{18.68239pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m6l}}}\end{pmatrix}.

(78)

Since this basis is orthonormal, the correlator matrix at the initial condition
\mathcal{A}(\eta=0)
will just be the identity matrix.

III.3
Constructing the correlator matrix and the transition matrix

Due to this choice of basis
\tilde{\ib{B}}
, the full matrix differential equation (
51
) now decouples into three independent equations.
This allows us to forego exponentiating a six-by-six matrix; at most we will need to exponentiate a three-by-three matrix, which can be done analytically.

To form the correlator matrix
\mathcal{A}
, we take the product
\tilde{\ib{B}}\left(U_{\ib{{z}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{z^{\prime}}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{v}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{y}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{x}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{w}}}\right)\tilde{\ib{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}
and set
\ib{w}\to\ib{z}
and
\ib{v}\to\ib{z^{\prime}}
.
To form the transition matrix, we act with the argument of the exponential in Eq. (
25
) on the operator
U_{\ib{{z}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{z^{\prime}}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{v}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{y}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{x}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{w}}}
, then wedge the result between the basis vectors and set
\ib{w}\to\ib{z}
and
\ib{v}\to\ib{z^{\prime}}
:

\tilde{\ib{B}}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\int^{\eta}\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime}\int_{\ib{u}_{1},\ib{u}_{2}}G_{\ib{u}_{1},\ib{u}_{2}}(\eta^{\prime})L^{{a}}_{\ib{{{u_{\textrm{1}}}}}}L^{{a}}_{\ib{{{u_{\textrm{2}}}}}}\right]\\
\times\left(U_{\ib{{z}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{z^{\prime}}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{v}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{y}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{x}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{w}}}\right)\tilde{\ib{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Bigg|_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ib{w}\to\ib{z}\\
\ib{v}\to\ib{z^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}.

(79)

Diagrammatically, this is equivalent to summing all possible ways of attaching one gluon line on the operator

, using the Fierz identity to replace the gluon vertices, and finally closing the Wilson line endpoints on the left and right using the basis vector.
For example, the element
(6,6)
of the correlator matrix
\mathcal{A}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(6,6)}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\tilde{B}_{6}\left(U_{\ib{{z}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{z^{\prime}}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{v}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{y}}}\otimes U_{\ib{{x}}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{\ib{{w}}}\right)\tilde{B}_{6}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}\;\parbox{18.68239pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m6l}}}\parbox{20.0525pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{tar}}}\parbox{18.68239pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{{m6r}}}\;.

We then sum all the diagrams in which a gluon is attached to this diagram so that it joins any two of the six Wilson lines on the right of the target interaction.
Using the Fierz identity in Eq. (
III.1
), we may write the resulting expression in terms of diagrams with no gluons.
After making the substitutions
\ib{w}\to\ib{z}
and
\ib{v}\to\ib{z^{\prime}}
, the result will be a linear combination of elements of the operator matrix
\mathcal{A}(\eta)
. From this linear combination, one can read off the elements of column
6
of the transition matrix
\mathcal{M}
.
The explicit expressions for the elements of
\mathcal{A}(\eta)
in terms of the Wilson line correlators are shown in Appendix
A
.

Performing this procedure for each diagram in
\mathcal{A}(\eta)
, we get the full transition matrix

\mathcal{M}(\eta)\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ib{w}\to\ib{z}\\
\ib{v}\to\ib{z^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{M}_{{{3}}}&0&0\\
0&\mathcal{M}_{{{2}}}&0\\
0&0&\mathcal{M}_{{{1}}}\end{pmatrix}(\eta),

(83)

where the subscripts refer to the dimension of the sub-matrix.

The first (one-dimensional) transition sub-matrix is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{{1}}}(\eta)=C_{\mathrm{F}}\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,y}}}^{\prime}

(84)

and upon exponentiation, gives the parametric equation for the dipole correlator as shown in Eq. (
50
).
When inverted, this equation can be used to express the two-point function
\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,y}}}
in terms of the dipole correlator. This will be needed to evaluate the higher-point functions in terms of the dipole
\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{x,y}}}}\right\rangle
.

The second (two-dimensional) transition sub-matrix is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{{2}}}(\eta)=\frac{N_{\mathrm{c}}}{4}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{M}_{{{2}}}^{(1,1)}&\mathcal{M}_{{{2}}}^{(1,2)}\\
\mathcal{M}_{{{2}}}^{(1,2)}&\mathcal{M}_{{{2}}}^{(1,1)}\end{pmatrix}(\eta),

(85)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{{2}}}^{(1,1)}(\eta)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,z}}}^{\prime}+\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{y,z}}}^{\prime}-\frac{2}{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,y}}}^{\prime}+\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,z^{\prime}}}}^{\prime}+\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{y,z^{\prime}}}}^{\prime},

(86)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{{2}}}^{(1,2)}(\eta)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,z}}}^{\prime}+\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{y,z}}}^{\prime}-\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,z^{\prime}}}}^{\prime}-\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{y,z^{\prime}}}}^{\prime}.

(87)

The matrix differential equation

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{{{2}}}(\eta)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{M}_{{{2}}}(\eta)\mathcal{A}_{{{2}}}(\eta)

(88)

then gives a coupled system of
2\times 2
differential equations, out of which 2 are linearly independent, corresponding to the fact that the same transition matrix operates separately on each of the two columns of
\mathcal{A}_{{{2}}}
.
The exponential solution for this system of equations gives the known parametrization for the four-point correlator with one repeated coordinate
Marquet:2010cf

\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{x,z}}}}S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{z,y}}}}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}e^{-C_{\mathrm{F}}\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,y}}}}\\
+\frac{2C_{\mathrm{F}}}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}e^{-C_{\mathrm{F}}\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,y}}}}e^{-\frac{N_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}(\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,z}}}+\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{y,z}}}-\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,y}}})}.

(89)

The third and final (three-dimensional) transition sub-matrix is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{{3}}}(\eta)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{N_{\mathrm{c}}}{4}\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}&\frac{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}C_{\mathrm{d}}}}{4}\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}&0\\
\frac{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{c}}C_{\mathrm{d}}}}{4}\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}&\frac{N_{\mathrm{c}}}{4}\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}&-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}\\
0&-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}&\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},

(90)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}

\displaystyle:=C_{\mathrm{F}}\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,y}}}+N_{\mathrm{c}}\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{z,z^{\prime}}}},

(91)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,z}}}+\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{y,z}}}-\frac{2}{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,y}}}+\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,z^{\prime}}}}+\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{y,z^{\prime}}}}+2\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{z,z^{\prime}}}},

(92)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,z}}}-\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{y,z}}}-\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,z^{\prime}}}}+\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{y,z^{\prime}}}}

(93)

and the primes on the
\Gamma
’s in Eq. (
90
) denote derivatives in
\eta
.
Exponentiating this matrix is the last step required to get expressions for the remaining six-point correlators in
\mathcal{A}_{{{3}}}
.

III.4
Exponentiating the transition matrix
\mathcal{M}_{{{3}}}

In order to obtain the six-point functions, it is necessary to
solve the differential equation

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}_{{{3}}}(\eta)=-\mathcal{M}_{{{3}}}(\eta)\mathcal{A}_{{{3}}}(\eta).

(94)

Solving this equation is equivalent to exponentiating the matrix
\mathcal{M}_{{{3}}}
, as shown above in the cases of the two- and four-point functions.
To exponentiate
\mathcal{M}_{{{3}}}
, we consider two different cases:
\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}=0
and
\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}\neq 0
.

When
\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}=0
,
\mathcal{M}_{{{3}}}
in Eq. (
90
) becomes diagonal and we directly obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{{{3}}}(\eta)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-\frac{N_{\mathrm{c}}}{4}\Gamma_{1}}&0&0\\
0&e^{-\frac{N_{\mathrm{c}}}{4}\Gamma_{1}}&0\\
0&0&e^{-\Gamma_{0}}\end{pmatrix}.

(95)

When
\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}\neq 0
, the matrix elements of
\mathcal{A}_{{{3}}}
are calculated by matrix-exponentiating the full
\mathcal{M}_{{{3}}}
in Eq. (
90
), giving

It is now possible to express these six-point functions entirely in terms of dipole correlators using Eq. (

This makes it possible to write the NLO BK equation from Eq. (
2
) solely in terms of dipole correlators.
In such a closed form, it can be solved directly, as was done in the large-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
case in Refs.
Lappi:2015fma
;
Lappi:2016fmu

To verify the validity of Eqs. (
120
) and (
121
), we perform two checks.
Firstly, the Gaussian approximation has the built-in property that it should be consistent in color algebra.
This means that taking any coincidence limit in which coordinates are made equal in Eqs. (
120
) and (
121
), should reduce them to the relevant expressions for the lower-point functions Eqs. (
50
) and (
89
).
For example, setting
\ib{z}\to\ib{x}
and
\ib{z^{\prime}}\to\ib{y}
in Eq. (
120
), we reproduce the equation for the dipole (
50
), as expected.

Secondly, when Eq. (
120
) is taken in the dilute limit, where the Wilson lines are expanded as

\displaystyle U_{\ib{{x}}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda_{a}(\ib{x})t^{a}}

(122)

\displaystyle=1-\lambda_{a}(\ib{x})t^{a}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2}),\hskip 22.99988pt\lambda_{a}(\ib{x})\in\mathbb{R},

(123)

Eq. (
120
) should be the same up to order
\lambda^{2}
as the parametric equation for the large-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
counterpart operator.
In the case of correlator
\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{x,z}}}}S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{z,z^{\prime}}}}}S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{z^{\prime},y}}}}\right\rangle
, the large-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
result is just the factorized product of dipole correlators

\displaystyle\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{x,z}}}}\right\rangle\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{z,z^{\prime}}}}}\right\rangle\left\langle S^{({{2}})}_{\ib{{{z^{\prime},y}}}}\right\rangle=e^{-C_{\mathrm{F}}(\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{x,z}}}+\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{z,z^{\prime}}}}+\mathcal{G}_{\ib{{z^{\prime},y}}})}.

(124)

After some algebra, Eq. (
120
) can be shown to give the same result up to order
\mathcal{G}
.

We note that in Ref.
Dusling:2017aot
, correlators of up to eight Wilson lines have been calculated at finite
N_{\mathrm{c}}
. The difference between that work and ours is that the authors there are solving the system for a general configuration of coordinates, where it is difficult to find a basis such that the transition matrix would become block diagonal. Consequently, an analytical approach as presented in this paper is not possible.
Instead, the authors numerically exponentiate the transition matrix, which is a much more expensive computational procedure than what is needed here.

IV
Numerical results

We now study numerically the obtained six-point correlators, Eqs. (

V
Conclusions

In this work, we have studied the six-point correlators in the NLO BK equation using the Gaussian approximation.
This allowed us to express these higher-point correlators in terms of the dipole operator.
In using our analytical results, we have seen numerically that the overall finite-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
corrections to the NLO-like part of the BK equation are somewhat smaller than what is naively expected.
However, one needs to state the actual quantity being compared in order to quantify this correction.

When correlators are considered between Wilson lines separated by large distances relative to
1/Q_{\mathrm{s}}
,
1/N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}
suppressed corrections may be considerable.
Despite these potentially large corrections to individual correlators, these configurations do not contribute much to the right side of the BK equation.
Therefore, we find a somewhat smaller, although still significant, effect on the shape of the dipole amplitude as a function of
r
.
The finite-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
corrections are watered down further when one considers the evolution speed of
Q_{\mathrm{s}}
as a function of rapidity, especially once the evolution settles towards its asymptotic form away from the initial condition.
In general, finite-
N_{\mathrm{c}}
corrections need to be considered carefully when evaluating the NLO BK equation, since they may have a non-negligible effect at the required accuracy.
