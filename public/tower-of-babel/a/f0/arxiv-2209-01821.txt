Positivity and discretization of Fredholm integral operators
Magdalena Nockowska-Rosiak, Christian Pötzsche

Abstract
We provide sufficient conditions for vector-valued Fredholm integral operators and their commonly used spatial discretizations to be positive in terms of an order relation induced by a corresponding order cone. It turns out that reasonable Nyström methods preserve positivity. Among the projection methods, persistence is obtained for the simplest ones based on polynomial, piecewise linear or specific cubic interpolation (collocation), as well as for piecewise constant basis functions in a Bubnov-Galerkin approach. However, for semi-discretizations using quadratic splines or sinc \sinc -collocation we demonstrate that positivity is violated. Our results are illustrated in terms of eigenpairs for Krein-Rutman operators and form the basis of corresponding investigations for nonlinear integral operators.

Introduction

Fredholm integral operators canonically arise in numerous applications ranging from a classical fixed-point formulation of linear elliptic boundary value problems
[
1
]
to Fréchet derivatives of recent models in theoretical ecology
[
15
, pp. 23ff]
. Often their Green’s function resp. kernel is positive. This is understood that such a matrix-valued function preserves an order relation induced by an order cone. For instance, dispersal kernels used in theoretical ecology preserve an order relation in order to capture predator-prey or symbiotic relationships between different species, or differential operators satisfy a maximum principle which transfers to positivity of their inverses via the Green’s function. Moreover, for example
[
7
]
provides sufficient conditions that Hammerstein operators can be transformed into an order-preserving form.

Dealing with positive operators has several merits. First, positivity allows an application of the Krein-Rutman theorem
[
5
, p. 226, Thm. 19.2]
,
[
24
, p. 290, Thm. 7.C]
or
[
14
]
with profound consequences to the bifurcation behavior of related nonlinear problems. Second,
[
2
]
gives results on the distribution of secondary eigenvalues. Finally, criteria for various types of positivity in linear operators are fundamental to obtain corresponding results for nonlinear integral operators of Urysohn or Hammerstein type
[
17
,
18
]
. Beyond providing sufficient conditions for positivity of Fredholm integral operators, in this paper we also discuss the question whether this property is preserved under discretizations. The latter aspect is crucial in numerical simulations and computations in order to preserve or capture properties of the original problem.

More detailled, the texture of this paper is as follows: After introducing our notation, Sect.
2
provides sufficient conditions on kernels such that the associated Fredholm operators on the continuous or integrable functions are positive over domains of finite measure. In essence these properties carry over from the kernels having values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
to the integral operators mapping into a space of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-valued functions. Persistence issues under full spatial discretization of Nyström type are tackled in Sect.
3
. It suffices to assume positive quadrature weights for the sake of positivity. This property is satisfied for a large class of integration methods
[
4
,
11
]
and, besides guaranteeing well-posedness and computational stability
[
9
,
10
]
, provides another reason for the use of positive weights in numerical quadrature. This convenient prospect changes in Sect.
4
when addressing semi-discretizations of projection type. Here the situation is more subtle and the class of positivity-preserving schemes appears to be rather small. Among the collocation methods we indeed observe that polynomial and piecewise linear collocation preserves positivity, while popular other methods (e.g. quadratic splines,
\sinc
methods) do not. Among the Bubnov-Galerkin methods, at least piecewise constant approximation works. Nevertheless, based on positivity-preserving interpolation methods
[
20
,
21
,
23
]
it appears to be possible to construct projection operators, which are positive at least on subsets of the state space. Pointing towards applications, Sect.
5
discusses order-preserving properties of typical dispersal kernels and illustrates our results by means of a failure in the Krein-Rutman theorem when using quadrature schemes having negative weights. Moreover, we compare numerical errors for various projection methods when approximating the dominant eigenvalue of a positive operator. For the reader’s convenience, an appendix collects basic results on cones in Banach spaces and positive operators, an explicit formula for the inverse of tridiagonal matrices and lists the quadrature rules in Nyström methods throughout the text.

3
Nyström methods

In numerics or simulations of Fredholm operators, the involved integrals can be evaluated only approximately. One achieves this by applying discretization methods from the numerical analysis of integral equations. The most natural and popular discretizations of integral operators are based on Nyström methods (see
[
3
, pp. 100ff]
or
[
9
, pp. 128ff]
), where one replaces the integrals by integration (quadrature, cubature) rules.

In this section, we suppose that
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{\kappa}
is compact with Lebesgue measure
\lambda_{\kappa}(\Omega)>0
. For a continuous function

and we refer to App.

for concrete examples.

We say that an integration rule (

n

4
Projection methods

Let
X(\Omega)
denote a normed space of functions
u:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
being for instance continuous
X(\Omega)=C(\Omega)
or square-integrable
X(\Omega)=L^{2}(\Omega)
. Projections methods approximate elements of an infinite-dimensional function space
X(\Omega)
by elements from suitable finite-dimensional subspaces
X_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

For this purpose, we choose linearly independent functions
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d_{n}}\in X(\Omega)
and linearly independent
\psi_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\psi_{d_{n}}^{\prime}\in X(\Omega)^{\prime}
. Then
X_{n}:=\spann\left\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d_{n}}\right\}
is denoted as
ansatz space
and has dimension
d_{n}
. If
\phi_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\phi_{d_{n}}^{\prime}\in X(\Omega)^{\prime}
are functionals satisfying
\phi_{i}^{\prime}(\phi_{j})=\delta_{ij}
for all
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d_{n}
, then

\displaystyle\pi_{n}:X(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to X_{n},

\displaystyle\pi_{n}u

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}\phi_{j}^{\prime}(u)\phi_{j}

(4.1)

defines a bounded projection onto
X_{n}
. The above functionals are related by

P_{n}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}^{\prime}\\
\vdots\\
\phi_{d_{n}}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}^{\prime}\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{d_{n}}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}

(4.2)

with an (invertible) matrix
P_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{n}\times d_{n}}
. This approach extends to vector-valued functions
u:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and operators acting on a Cartesian product
X(\Omega)^{d}
in terms of the projection

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}

\displaystyle\in L(X(\Omega)^{d},X_{n}^{d}),

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}u

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\pi_{n}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\pi_{n}u_{d}\end{pmatrix}=\sum^{d_{n}}_{j=1}\phi_{j}(\cdot)\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{j}^{\prime}(u_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\phi_{j}^{\prime}(u_{d})\end{pmatrix}.

(4.3)

Remark 4.1
(discrete projection methods)

Projection methods applied to integral operators
{\mathscr{K}}
in (

F

) merely yield semi-discretizations, that is, although the operators
\Pi_{n}{\mathscr{K}}
are finite-dimensional, they still contain integrals to be evaluated. Hence, in order to arrive at full discretizations, it remains to apply a Nyström method to
\Pi_{n}{\mathscr{K}}
. In this procedure, positivity properties are preserved, provided the quadrature rules (

Q
n

) satisfy the criteria derived in Sect.
3
.

Remark 4.2
(convergence of projection methods)

In the spirit of the above Rem.
3.6
it is reasonable to restrict to convergent projection methods (
4.3
), that is

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pi_{n}u=u\quad\text{for all }u\in X(\Omega)^{d}.

Thanks to
[
10
, p. 175, Exer. 8.23]
this in turn yields
\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left\|\Pi_{n}\right\|<\infty
.

On the other hand, the applicability of Thm.

Remark 4.5
(basis transformation)

The assumption of Thm.
4.3
is invariant under basis transformations. Indeed, if
T\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{n}\times d_{n}}
is invertible, then also the functions

\bar{\phi}_{i}:=\sum_{l=1}^{d_{n}}T_{il}\phi_{l}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}\quad\text{for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant d_{n}

define a basis
\left\{\bar{\phi}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\phi}_{d_{n}}\right\}
of
X_{n}
. Due to
\bar{\phi}_{i}(x_{j})=\sum_{l=1}^{d_{n}}T_{il}\phi_{l}(x_{j})
it has the collocation matrix
\bar{P}_{n}=TP_{n}
. This implies

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\sigma}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bar{\sigma}_{d_{n}}\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}\begin{pmatrix}(\bar{P}_{n}^{-1})_{1j}\bar{\phi}_{j}\\
\vdots\\
(\bar{P}_{n}^{-1})_{d_{n}j}\bar{\phi}_{j}\end{pmatrix}=\bar{P}_{n}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\phi}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bar{\phi}_{d_{n}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\bar{P}_{n}^{-1}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{n}}\begin{pmatrix}T_{1l}\phi_{l}\\
\vdots\\
T_{d_{n}l}\phi_{l}\end{pmatrix}=\bar{P}_{n}^{-1}T\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\phi_{d_{n}}\end{pmatrix}=P_{n}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\phi_{d_{n}}\end{pmatrix}

and consequently
\bar{\sigma}_{i}=\sigma_{i}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant d_{n}
.

4.1.1
Lagrange bases

Suppose that
\left\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d_{n}}\right\}
is a

of the ansatz space

, i.e. it satisfies the interpolation condition
\phi_{i}(x_{j})=\delta_{ij}
for all
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d_{n}
. Hence, the collocation matrix

is the identity and the projections read as

\Pi_{n}u=\sum_{j=0}^{d_{n}}\phi_{j}(\cdot)u(x_{j})\quad\text{for all }u\in C(\Omega)^{d}.

Concrete examples are as follows, in which

is equipped with a grid

,

.

(piecewise linear collocation)

Example 4.7
(polynomial interpolation)

Cubic splines

In contrast, let us next construct positivity preserving projection methods as follows:

Corollary 4.8
(cubic splines)

The piecewise defined cubic spline

\displaystyle(\Pi_{n}u)(x):=

\displaystyle\left(1-3(\tfrac{x-x_{i}}{h_{i}})^{2}+2(\tfrac{x-x_{i}}{h_{i}})^{3}\right)u(x_{i})

(4.10)

\displaystyle+\left(3(\tfrac{x-x_{i}}{h_{i}})^{2}-2(\tfrac{x-x_{i}}{h_{i}})^{3}\right)u(x_{i+1})\quad\text{for all }x\in[x_{i},x_{i+1}],\,0\leqslant i<n

yields a
C[a,b]_{+}^{d}
-positive projection
\Pi_{n}:C[a,b]^{d}\to C^{1}[a,b]^{d}
satisfying the estimate
\left\|\Pi_{n}\right\|_{L(C[a,b]^{d})}\leqslant 2
.

Although (
4.10
) gives rise to a positivity preserving spline, its derivative vanishes in all the collocation points
x_{j}
and thus
\Pi_{n}u
has rather unpleasant approximation properties. This is also exemplified by a low convergence rate in numerical simulations (see Ex.
5.2
) based on (
4.10
).

{@proof}
[Proof.]
(I) Given nonnegative real numbers
s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}
, the cubic
C^{1}
-spline
s:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
, piecewise defined as

\displaystyle s(x)=s_{i}+(x-x_{i})m_{i}

\displaystyle+(\tfrac{x-x_{i}}{h_{i}})^{2}\left(3(s_{i+1}-s_{i})-h_{i}(2m_{i}+m_{i+1})\right)

\displaystyle+(\tfrac{x-x_{i}}{h_{i}})^{3}\left(h_{i}(m_{i}+m_{i+1})-2(s_{i+1}-s_{i})\right)

for

,
0\leqslant i<n
is investigated in
[

. It satisfies the interpolation conditions

, as well as

for
0\leqslant i\leqslant n
, where

are free parameters. According to
[

, the spline

is nonnegative on the interval

if and only if

\displaystyle W_{i}:=

\displaystyle\left\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\,-3s_{i}\leqslant h_{i+1}x,\,h_{i+1}y\leqslant 3s_{i+1}\right\}

\displaystyle\cup\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\,0\leqslant 36s_{i}s_{i+1}(x^{2}+xy+y^{2}-3\tau_{i+1}(x+y)+3\tau_{i+1}^{2})

\displaystyle+3(s_{i+1}x-s_{i}y)(2h_{i+1}xy-3s_{i+1}x+3s_{i}y)

\displaystyle+4h_{i+1}(s_{i+1}x^{3}-s_{i}y^{3})-h_{i+1}^{2}x^{2}y^{2}\}

(II) Let

4.2

For a second class of projection methods, assume

and that
\left\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d_{n}}\right\}
forms a basis of a subspace

we require orthogonality
(\pi_{n}u-u,\phi_{i})=0

leading to functionals

. This results in the positively definite

Theorem 4.10
(positivity of
\Pi_{n}
on

)

Assume that every basis element
\phi_{i}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant d_{n}
, has nonnegative values. If the Gramian matrix
P_{n}
is monomial, then
\Pi_{n}
is
L^{2}(\Omega)_{+}^{d}
-positive.

{@proof}

[Proof.]
Let
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
,
0\prec u
and
y^{\prime}\in Y_{+}^{\prime}
. Thanks to Lemma
2.9
(a) this implies
0\leqslant\langle\!\!\langle u(y),y^{\prime}\rangle\!\!\rangle
for
\mu
-a.a.

. Since the Gramian matrix
P_{n}
is assumed to be monomial, we obtain from
[
13
, p. 2, Thm. 1.1]
that the inverse is nonnegative. Hence, if we write
\phi_{j}^{\prime}(u)=((u_{k},\phi_{j}))_{k=1}^{d}
, then (

) implies

\displaystyle\langle\!\!\langle(\Pi_{n}u)(x),y^{\prime}\rangle\!\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\!\!\langle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}\phi_{j}^{\prime}(u)\phi_{j}(x),y^{\prime}\rangle\!\!\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}\phi_{j}(x)\langle\!\!\langle\phi_{j}^{\prime}(u),y^{\prime}\rangle\!\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}\phi_{j}(x)\langle\!\!\langle\sum_{i=1}^{d_{n}}(P_{n}^{-1})_{ij}\int_{\Omega}u(y)\phi_{i}(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(y),y^{\prime}\rangle\!\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{n}}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}(y)\underbrace{\langle\!\!\langle u(y),y^{\prime}\rangle\!\!\rangle}_{\geq 0}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(y)\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}\phi_{j}(x)(P_{n}^{-1})_{ij}\geq 0

For orthogonal bases
\left\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d_{n}}\right\}

5.2
Krein-Rutman eigenfunctions

Let
X(\Omega)
be a Banach space of real-valued functions over
\Omega
. Given a compact Fredholm operator
{\mathscr{K}}\in L(X(\Omega)^{d})
, the Krein-Rutman Thm.
A.3
provides conditions guaranteeing that its spectral radius

is the dominant eigenvalue with an associate nonnegative eigenfunction
u^{\ast}\in X(\Omega)^{d}
. In this section, we investigate in which sense this property is preserved under discretization.

For this purpose, consider an abstract eigenvalue problem

{\mathscr{K}}u=\lambda u\quad\text{in }X(\Omega)^{d}

(5.1)

for a Fredholm operator
{\mathscr{K}}
. Numerical approaches to (
5.1
) approximate
(\lambda,u)\in{\mathbb{C}}\times X(\Omega)
by eigenpairs
(\lambda_{n},\hat{v}^{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{dN_{n}}
of related problems

K^{n}\hat{v}^{n}=\lambda_{n}\hat{v}^{n},

(5.2)

where the matrices

depend on the particular discretization method. Nevertheless,

consists of block matrices

\displaystyle K^{n}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}K_{11}&\ldots&K_{1d}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
K_{d1}&\ldots&K_{dd}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\hat{v}^{n}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
\vdots\\
v_{d}\end{pmatrix},

with also
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{n}}
and
K_{ij}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{n}\times N_{n}}
depending on the method.

Nevertheless, in our numerical computations we rely on the
Matlab
functions

to approximate the dominant eigenvalue of

and

when also the corresponding eigenvectors are of interest.

Conclusion

We provided sufficient conditions that positivity properties in a general class of matrix-valued kernels
K
transfer to Fredholm operators (

F

) over compact domains
\Omega
. In addition, the persistence of this property under Nyström and projection methods is studied. As a result, when solving numerical problems involving such operators, we recommend to restrict to Nyström methods with positive weights. Among the projection methods, collocation with piecewise linear basis functions yields positivity-preserving semi-discretizations and a combination with the Midpoint Rule (
C.1
) or the Trapezoidal Rule (
C.2
) leads to a corresponding scheme. Although there are further positivity preserving collocation methods, they have less favorable properties. For Bubnov-Galerkin methods, piecewise constant approximation is suitable and can be combined with e.g. the Midpoint Rule (
C.1
) in order to arrive at feasible schemes preserving positivity.

Appendix A
Cones and positive operators

Let
(X,\left\|\cdot\right\|)
denote a real Banach space with dual space
X^{\prime}
and the duality pairing
\left\langle x,x^{\prime}\right\rangle:=x^{\prime}(x)
. A nonempty closed and convex subset
X_{+}\subseteq X
is called
(order) cone
, if
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}X_{+}\subseteq X_{+}
and
X_{+}\cap(-X_{+})=\left\{0\right\}
hold. Equipped with such a cone one arrives at an
ordered Banach space

X
. Let us assume
X_{+}\neq\left\{0\right\}
throughout and for elements
x,\bar{x}\in X
we write

\displaystyle x\leqslant\bar{x}\quad

\displaystyle:\Leftrightarrow\quad\bar{x}-x\in X_{+},

\displaystyle x<\bar{x}\quad

\displaystyle:\Leftrightarrow\quad\bar{x}-x\in X_{+}\setminus\left\{0\right\},

(A.1)

\displaystyle x\ll\bar{x}\quad

\displaystyle:\Leftrightarrow\quad\bar{x}-x\in X_{+}^{\circ};

the latter relation requires
X_{+}^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
and one speaks of a
solid cone

X_{+}
. A cone is
total
, if
X=\overline{X_{+}-X_{+}}
. Solid cones satisfy
X=X_{+}-X_{+}
and are total.

By means of the
dual cone

X_{+}^{\prime}:=\left\{x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}:\,0\leqslant\left\langle x,x^{\prime}\right\rangle\text{ for all }x\in X_{+}\right\}
we can characterize the elements of
X_{+}
and
X_{+}^{\circ}
as follows:

Lemma A.1

(a)

X_{+}^{\prime}\neq\{0\}
and for every
x\in X
the following holds:

\displaystyle x\in X_{+}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow 0\leqslant\left\langle x,x^{\prime}\right\rangle\quad\text{for all }x^{\prime}\in X_{+}^{\prime},

\displaystyle x\in X_{+}\setminus\left\{0\right\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow 0<\left\langle x,x_{0}^{\prime}\right\rangle\quad\text{for some }x_{0}^{\prime}\in X_{+}^{\prime}\setminus\left\{0\right\}.

(b)

If
X_{+}
is solid, then for every
x\in X
the following holds:

\displaystyle x\in X_{+}^{\circ}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow 0<\left\langle x,x^{\prime}\right\rangle\quad\text{for all }x^{\prime}\in X_{+}^{\prime}\setminus\left\{0\right\},

\displaystyle x\in\partial X_{+}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow 0=\left\langle x,x_{0}^{\prime}\right\rangle\quad\text{for some }x_{0}^{\prime}\in X_{+}^{\prime}\setminus\left\{0\right\}.

(A.2)

{@proof}

[Proof.]
Due to
[
5
, p. 222, Prop. 19.3(a–b)]
it remains to verify the direction “
\Leftarrow
” in the first equivalence of (b). Let
0<\left\langle x,x^{\prime}\right\rangle
for all
x^{\prime}\in X_{+}^{\prime}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
and we deduce
x\in X_{+}
from (a). Using the contraposition of (
A.2
) we derive
x\notin\partial X_{+}
which implies that
x\in X_{+}^{\circ}
.
∎

A bounded linear mapping

is called

•

When working with several cones, we write
