Abstract
. A construction of product measures is given for an arbitrary sequence of measure spaces via outer measure techniques without imposing any condition on the underlying measure spaces. This approach concludes finally the problem of the existence of product measures in an elementary manner. Moreover, the L p L_{p} spaces of this measures are simplified in terms of finite product measures following the approach of [ 21 ] . This decomposition simplifies infinite dimensional integration and gives to this theory a computational framework.

1.
Introduction

In this article, we go back to a central issue in the foundational period of measure theory in the beginning of the 20th century, whose importance has been declining due to the production of certain partial results sufficienty general for its application. We talk about the construction of product measures on infinite product spaces. We start by presenting in a compact way the results obtained up to date.

The classical theory of product measures deals with two measure spaces
(X,\Sigma_{X},\mu_{X})
and
(Y,\Sigma_{Y},\mu_{Y})
in order to construct the product measure space
(X\times Y,\Sigma_{X}\otimes\Sigma_{Y},\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y})
, where
\Sigma_{X}\otimes\Sigma_{Y}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathcal{R}_{X\times Y}:=\left\{A\times B:A\in\Sigma_{X},\ B\in\Sigma_{Y}\right\}
and
\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
is a measure on
\Sigma_{X}\otimes\Sigma_{Y}
satisfying the identity

(1)

(\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y})(A\times B)=\mu_{X}(A)\cdot\mu_{Y}(B)\text{ for every }A\in\Sigma_{X},B\in\Sigma_{Y}.

The most common method to prove the existence of this measure is through the celebrated Caratheodory extension theorem as follows. Denote by
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{R}_{X\times Y})
the family of finite unions of elements of
\mathcal{R}_{X\times Y}
, then it is easy to verify that
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{R}_{X\times Y})
is an algebra of subsets of
X\times Y
and that every element of
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{R}_{X\times Y})
can be written as a finite union of pairwise disjoint members of
\mathcal{R}_{X\times Y}
. Define the set function

\mu_{0}:\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{R}_{X\times Y})\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow[0,+\infty],\quad\biguplus_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\times B_{i}\mapstochar\rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{X}(A_{i})\cdot\mu_{Y}(B_{i}).

It is classical (see e.g.
[
4
,
7
]
), that the function
\mu_{0}
is well defined and
\sigma
-additive on
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{R}_{X\times Y})
. Hence by Caratheodory extension theorem, there exists a measure
\mu
on the
\sigma
-algebra
\Sigma_{X}\otimes\Sigma_{Y}
that extends
\mu_{0}
. Therefore,
\mu
satisfies identity (
1
) for each
A\in\Sigma_{X}
and
B\in\Sigma_{Y}
. Moreover if
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
are
\sigma
-finite,
\mu
is the unique measure on
(X\times Y,\Sigma_{X}\otimes\Sigma_{Y})
satisfying (
1
).

Consider now a sequence
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of measure spaces. The problem is to generalize the classical product measure construction to countable many, i.e., to construct the infinite product measure space

\left(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}\right)
where
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the cylinder sets

\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}:=\left\{\bigtimes_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}\times\bigtimes_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}:C_{i}\in\Sigma_{i},\ \forall i\in\{1,2,...,m\}\text{ and }m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

and
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
is a measure on
\left(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i}\right)
satisfying an analogue of identity (
1
) for this general setting. For instance, if the finiteness condition
\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})\in[0,+\infty)
holds, the measure
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
must satisfy the identity

(2)

\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}\left(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}\times\bigtimes_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}(C_{i})\cdot\prod_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})

for each
\bigtimes_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}\times\bigtimes_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. Along this paper we use the following definition of infinite product for a sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset[0,+\infty)
,

\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}a_{i}:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}.

The first attempt to address this problem was for the particular case of probability spaces. In 1933, A. Kolmogoroff proved in
[
13
]
the existence of a probability measure
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}m_{[0,1]}
on the measurable space
\left([0,1]^{\mathbb{N}},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{B}_{[0,1]}\right)
, where
\mathcal{B}_{[0,1]}
and
m_{[0,1]}
are the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
[0,1]
and the Lebesgue measure of
[0,1]
respectively, such that identity (
2
) holds for every

\bigtimes_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}\times\bigtimes_{i=m+1}^{\infty}[0,1]\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{B}_{[0,1]})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}.

Kolmogoroff’s proof was based on the compactness of the product space
[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
, provided by the Tychonoff result of 1930,
[
23
]
. More general cases were discussed by Z. Lomnicki and S. Ulam in 1934 on the reference
[
15
]
. In 1943, S. Kakutani generalized for general probability spaces the results of Kolmogoroff, Lomnicki and Ulam proving in
[
11
]
the next celebrated result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
a family of probability spaces, there exists a unique probability measure
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
on the measurable space
(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i})
satisfying for every
\mathscr{C}=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}\times\bigtimes_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
the following identity

\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}(C_{i}).

Kakutani’s proof of this result has become standard in Probability and Measure Theory. The key tool of the proof relies on a result of E. Hopf (cf.
[
9
]
,
[
24
, Theorem 3.2]
). In 1996, S. Saeki gives in
[
20
]
a new proof of Theorem
1.1
, proving it in a more natural terms without the use of Hopf’s result. It must be observed that if the measure spaces
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are not of probability but satisfy the finiteness condition
\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{j})\in[0,+\infty)
, then normalizing each measure space, it can be also proven as a rather direct consequence of Theorem
1.1
, the existence of a measure on
(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i})
satisfying identity (
2
).

On other hand, if
\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})=+\infty
, identity (
2
) is no longer useful for the construction of infinite product measures since its value is always infinite. Nevertheless, we can ask the measure to verify the new identity

(3)

\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}\left(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}\right)=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(C_{i})

for each
\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is the set of
finite rectangles
on
\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i}
defined by

\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}:=\left\{\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}:C_{i}\in\Sigma_{i},\ \forall i\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(C_{i})\in[0,+\infty)\right\}.

Therefore, the natural extension of the classical theory for the nonfinite case is the product measure space
\left(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}\right)
where
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
is a measure satisfying (
3
).
The construction of this measures had not been ignored in the last century and several attempts had been made trying to formalize it.

In 1963, E.O. Elliott and A.P. Morse published a paper
[
6
]
constructing this kind of product spaces through a reformulation of the classical infinite product called
plus product
. Let
\mathfrak{a}=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive real numbers and
\mathscr{A}(\mathfrak{a})=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:a_{n}>1\}
, then they defined the
plus product
of the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by

{\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}}^{+}a_{n}:=\prod_{n\in\mathscr{A}(\mathfrak{a})}a_{n}\cdot\prod_{n\notin\mathscr{A}(\mathfrak{a})}a_{n},

with the convention
0\cdot+\infty=+\infty\cdot 0=0
and setting the value of the empty product to 1. The main purpose of defining this concept lies in the fact that the plus product exists for every positive sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of real numbers, which does not happen with the classic product (take for instance the sequence defined by

a_{n}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2&\text{if }n\equiv 0\mod 2\\
\frac{1}{2}&\text{if }n\equiv 1\mod 2.\end{array}\right.

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
). The classical and the plus product do not, in general, coincide, but if the condition
\prod_{n\in\mathscr{A}(\mathfrak{a})}a_{n}<+\infty
is satisfied, they do. We define the set of
finite plus rectangles
by

\mathcal{F}^{+}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}:=\left\{\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}:C_{i}\in\Sigma_{i},\forall i\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }{\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}}^{+}\mu_{i}(C_{i})\in[0,+\infty)\right\}.

Elliot and Morse proved that given a family of measure spaces
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a measure
\lambda_{EM}
on the measurable space
\left(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i}\right)

satisfying for every
\mathscr{C}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}\in\mathcal{F}^{+}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, the following identity

(4)

\lambda_{EM}(\mathscr{C})={\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}}^{+}\mu_{i}(C_{i}).

It must be observed that if a finite plus rectangle
\mathscr{C}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}\in\mathcal{F}^{+}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies
{\prod}^{+}_{i}\mu_{i}(C_{i})\neq 0
, then its plus product must coincide with the classical product
\prod_{i}\mu_{i}(C_{i})
. However, if
{\prod}^{+}_{i}\mu_{i}(C_{i})=0
, the products does not, in general, coincide. In consequence, this result does not stablish the existence of the required product measure since there are substantial sequences satisfying
{\prod}^{+}_{i}\mu_{i}(C_{i})=0
with nonzero classical product (take for instance the sequence
a_{n}=\exp((-1)^{n+1}/n)
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
).

In 2004, R. Baker, proved in
[
3
, Theorem I]
(see also
[
2
]
), the existence of the required product measure for the particular case in which the involved spaces
\{(X_{i},d_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are locally compact metric spaces and satisfy the property
\mathcal{D}
:

(
\mathcal{D}
)

For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta>0
, there exists a sequence
\{A_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{B}_{X_{i}}
such that
d_{i}(A_{j})<\delta
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
and

X_{i}=\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}A_{j}

where
d_{i}(A_{j})
stands for the diameter of
A_{j}
in
X_{i}
and
\mathcal{B}_{X_{i}}
for the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
X_{i}
.

He proved that given a sequence
\{(X_{i},d_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying the previous properties and a family of regular Borel measures
\{\mu_{X_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, each
\mu_{X_{i}}
defined in the measurable space
(X_{i},\mathcal{B}_{X_{i}})
, there exists a measure
\lambda_{B}
on the measurable space
(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}X_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{B}_{X_{i}})
satisfying for every
\mathscr{C}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{B}_{X_{i}},\mu_{X_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, the following identity

\lambda_{B}(\mathscr{C})=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{X_{i}}(C_{i}).

In 2005, P. A. Loeb and D. A. Ross gave in
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
another attempt of formalizing the product measure via Nonstandard Analysis techniques and Loeb Measures
[
1
, §4]
. They established that given a sequence of Hausdorff topological spaces
\{(X_{i},\tau_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and a corresponding sequence of regular Borel measure spaces
\{(X_{i},\mathcal{B}_{X_{i}},\mu_{X_{i}})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a measure
\lambda_{LR}
on the measurable space
(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}X_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{B}_{X_{i}})
such that if
K_{i}\subset X_{i}
is compact for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}K_{i}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{B}_{X_{i}},\mu_{X_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, then the following identity holds

\lambda_{LR}\left(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}K_{i}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu_{X_{i}}(K_{i}).

Finally, in 2011 G.R. Pantsulaia presented in
[
16
, Theorem 3.10]
the best generalization of product measures to countable many up to date. He proved the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of
\sigma
-finite measure spaces satisfying
\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\neq\emptyset
, then there exists a measure
\lambda_{P}
on
\left(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i}\right)
satisfying for each
\mathscr{C}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
the identity

\lambda_{P}(\mathscr{C})=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(C_{i}).

This result provides a standard prove of the existence of the product measure without imposing any topological assumption over the involved spaces, in contrast with the constructions of Baker and Loeb & Ross. However, the construction of Pantsulaia requires the measure spaces
(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})
to be
\sigma
-finite for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
and the constructed measure
\lambda_{P}
is not the restriction of an outer measure, that it is not a necessary condition but it is rather natural and facilitates several computations like Fubini theorem’s proof.

The aim of this article is to finish this story by constructing the product measure
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
for any sequence of measure spaces
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, without imposing any restriction on them. This establishes an analogue Kakutani’s result Theorem
1.1
for general product measure spaces and states the most general existence result, concluding with an affirmative answer the problem of the existence of product measures. The main result of this article is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of measure spaces, then there exists a measure
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
on the measurable space
(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i})
satisfying for each
\mathscr{C}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
the identity

\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(\mathscr{C})=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(C_{i}).

Furthermore, as will see later, this measure is constructed in an elementary way using the Caratheodory extension theorem and greatly simplifies the proofs given by Baker, Loeb & Ross and Pantsulaia. On the other hand, this measure is constructed by means of outer measure techniques, which will allow to use Fubbini’s Theorem to prove an extension of the Jessen’s result
[
10
]
for this measure that establishes a simplification of the spaces
L_{p}(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i})
for
1\leq p<+\infty
. Roughly speaking, we decompose
L_{p}(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i})
in terms of
L_{p}(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i})
, for each
1\leq p<+\infty
. This decomposition establishes a generalization of Theorem 3.4 of a recent publication of the author
[
21
, Theorem 3.4]
for general product measures. Moreover, it allows to consider infinite dimensional functions as a sequence of finite dimensional ones and stabilises a computational method to compute the integral of this kind of maps.

This paper is organized as follows. In section one, we give a result that states that the volume of finite rectangles
\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
behaves like the classical volume of cubes in finite dimensions. This result will be crucial for the construction of the required measure. In section two, we construct the product measure proving the main result of this article, Theorem
1.3
. This theorem concludes the problem of the construction of product measures in infinite products. Finally, in section three and four we simplify the structure of
L_{p}
spaces of this measure in terms of finite product measures.

2.
A Key Result

Given an arbitrary sequence of measure spaces
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, we will prove the existence of a measure
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
on the measurable space
(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i})
satisfying the identity

(5)

\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(\mathscr{C})=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(C_{i})

for each
\mathscr{C}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. We will suppose that
\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\neq\emptyset
, since in the contrary, the identity (
5
) holds for every measure. The existence of such measure in the case in which the finiteness condition
\prod_{i}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})\in[0,+\infty)
holds, follows from Theorem
1.1
.

In this section, we will prove the main tool that will be used to prove the existence of the required measure. This result states that the volume of finite rectangles behaves like the volume of finite dimensional rectangles. Let
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an arbitrary family of measure spaces. We define the
volume
map
\vol:\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
by

\vol\left(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}\right)=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(C_{i}).

It will be proved that the
\vol
map is a good choice for the extension of the classical volume formula. Along this article, the notation
\uplus
stands for the disjoint union of subsets.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of measure spaces,
\mathscr{C}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\mathscr{C}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
pairwise disjoint. Then

(1)

If
\mathscr{C}=\biguplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathscr{C}_{n}
,

(6)

\vol(\mathscr{C})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\vol(\mathscr{C}_{n}).

(2)

If
\biguplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathscr{C}_{n}\subset\mathscr{C}
,

(7)

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\vol(\mathscr{C}_{n})\leq\vol(\mathscr{C}).

(3)

Suppose that
\{\mathscr{C}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are not necessarily pairwise disjoint, then if
\mathscr{C}\subset\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathscr{C}_{n}
,

\vol(\mathscr{C})\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\vol(\mathscr{C}_{n}).

For the proof of Theorem
2.1
, it will be necessary to prove some lemmas.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be two real positive sequences such that

(1)

a_{n}\leq b_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(2)

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}b_{n}\in[0,+\infty)

then

(8)

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}\in[0,+\infty).

Moreover,

(9)

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}\leq\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}b_{n}.

Proof.

If
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}b_{n}=0
, the result is evident. Suppose
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}b_{n}\in(0,+\infty)
. Then, since
(a_{n}/b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset[0,1]
, the partial products are monotone and bounded by
1
. In consequence
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}\in[0,+\infty)
. Using elementary properties of the limit

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\prod_{n=1}^{m}a_{n}=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\prod_{n=1}^{m}b_{n}\prod_{n=1}^{m}\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}=\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}b_{n}\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}\in[0,+\infty).

This concludes the proof of (
8
). For (
9
) just note that the partial products satisfies
\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{n}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}b_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

The following statements hold:

(1)

Let
\mathscr{C}_{1},\mathscr{C}_{2}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, then
\mathscr{C}_{1}\cap\mathscr{C}_{2}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover, for each
i\in\{1,2\}

(10)

\vol(\mathscr{C}_{1}\cap\mathscr{C}_{2})\leq\vol(\mathscr{C}_{i}).

(2)

Let
\mathscr{C}_{1}\in\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathscr{C}_{2}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, then
\mathscr{C}_{1}\cap\mathscr{C}_{2}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

Firstly, given
\mathscr{C}_{1},\mathscr{C}_{2}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with

\mathscr{C}_{1}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{1}^{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathscr{C}_{2}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{2}^{i},

it is apparent that

\mathscr{C}_{1}\cap\mathscr{C}_{2}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{1}^{i}\cap C_{2}^{i}.

Since
\Sigma_{i}
is a
\sigma
-algebra,
C_{1}^{i}\cap C_{2}^{i}\in\Sigma_{i}
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, since
\mu_{i}(C_{1}^{i}\cap C_{2}^{i})\leq\mu_{i}(C_{1}^{i})
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(C_{1}^{i})=\vol(\mathscr{C}_{1})\in[0,+\infty)
, it follows from Lemma
2.2
, that

\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(C_{1}^{i}\cap C_{2}^{i})\in[0,+\infty).

Thus

. Inequality (

For the second, denote

\mathscr{C}_{1}=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{m}C_{1,i}\times\bigtimes_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathscr{C}_{2}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{2,i},

∎

3.
Construction of the Measure

For the prove of the existence of a product measure for an arbitrary family of measure spaces
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, we consider the outer measure
\mu^{\ast}:\mathcal{P}(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i})\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
, defined by

(14)

\mu^{\ast}(A):=\text{inf}\left\{\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\vol(\mathscr{C}_{n}):\{\mathscr{C}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\text{ and }A\subset\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathscr{C}_{n}\right\}

for every
A\in\mathcal{P}(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i})
and we set
\mu^{\ast}(A):=+\infty
if no such cover exists. Along this article, the notation
\mathcal{P}(A)
stands for the power set of the set
A
. It is straightforward to prove that
\mu^{\ast}
defines an outer measure, see e.g.
[
18
, Chapter 1, Theorem 4]
. We will prove that this outer measure is, in fact, a measure on the
\sigma
-algebra
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i}
and that it satisfies identity (
5
) for each
\mathscr{C}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. We will make use of the following set theoretic Lemma whose prove is straightforward.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
C_{i}\subset\Omega_{i}
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
and set
\mathscr{C}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}
, then

(15)

\mathscr{C}^{c}=\biguplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n-1}C_{i}\times C_{n}^{c}\times\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{+\infty}\Omega_{i}\right).

Theorem 3.2

(
Measurability
)
.

Every set in
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\mu^{\ast}
-measurable.

Proof.

Take
\mathscr{C}\in\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
B\in\mathcal{P}(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i})
. We have to prove the inequality

(16)

\mu^{\ast}(B)\geq\mu^{\ast}(B\cap\mathscr{C})+\mu^{\ast}(B\cap\mathscr{C}^{c})

holds. Consider
\varepsilon>0
and a family
\{\mathscr{B}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

B\subset\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathscr{B}_{n}

and

(17)

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\vol(\mathscr{B}_{n})\leq\mu^{\ast}(B)+\varepsilon.

By the definition of
\mu^{\ast}
, if the cover
\{\mathscr{B}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
does not exist, then necessarily
\mu^{\ast}(B)=+\infty
and the inequality (
16
) follows. Suppose such cover exists, then by Lemma
3.1
, there exists
\{\mathscr{C}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
pairwise disjoint of the form

\mathscr{C}_{i}=\bigtimes_{j=1}^{n_{i}-1}C_{i,j}\times C_{i,n_{i}}^{c}\times\bigtimes_{j=n_{i}+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{j}

for some
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and
C_{i,j}\in\Sigma_{i}
for each
j\in\{1,2,...,n_{i}\}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{n}

\displaystyle=(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C})\uplus(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C}^{c})=(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C})\uplus\left(\biguplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C}_{i})\right).

By Lemma
2.3
, it follows that
\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C},\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C}_{i}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and therefore, by Theorem
2.1
item (1), we arrive to the identity

\vol(\mathscr{B}_{n})=\vol(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C})+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\vol(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C}_{i}).

Hence, by equation (
17
) and the definition of the outer measure
\mu^{\ast}
, (
14
), it becomes apparent that

\displaystyle\mu^{\ast}(B)+\varepsilon\geq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\vol(\mathscr{B}_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\vol(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C})+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\vol(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\vol(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C})+\sum_{(n,i)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}\vol(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C}_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\mu^{\ast}(B\cap\mathscr{C})+\mu^{\ast}(B\cap\mathscr{C}^{c}).

The last step follows from the following inclusion and the definition of infumum

\displaystyle B\cap\mathscr{C}^{c}

\displaystyle\subset\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathscr{B}_{n}\right)\cap\mathscr{C}^{c}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C}^{c})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\biguplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C}_{i})=\bigcup_{(n,i)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}(\mathscr{B}_{n}\cap\mathscr{C}_{i}).

Taking
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, inequality (
16
) holds for every
B\in\mathcal{P}(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i})
and therefore
\mathscr{C}
is
\mu^{\ast}
-measurable.
∎

In Theorem
3.2
we have proved that
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a subfamily of the Caratheodory
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{C}
associated to the outer measure
\mu^{\ast}
, and therefore by Caratheodory extension theorem,
\mu^{\ast}
defines a measure on
\sigma(\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}})=\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i}
. We will denote

\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}:=\mu^{\ast}\big|_{\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i}}.

Finally, we will prove that the outer measure
\mu^{\ast}
satisfies identity (
5
) for each
\mathscr{C}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Proposition 3.3

(
Volume
)
.

For each
\mathscr{C}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, the following identity holds

\mu^{\ast}(\mathscr{C})=\vol(\mathscr{C}).

Proof.

Let
\{\mathscr{C}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a cover of
\mathscr{C}
, i.e.,

\mathscr{C}\subset\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathscr{C}_{n}.

By Theorem
2.1
item (3), we deduce the following inequality

\vol(\mathscr{C})\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\vol(\mathscr{C}_{n}).

Then, taking the infimum over all such covers, we stablish that
\vol(\mathscr{C})\leq\mu^{\ast}(\mathscr{C})
. Finally, considering the particular cover
\{\mathscr{C}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by

\mathscr{C}_{n}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathscr{C}&\text{ if }n=1\\
\emptyset&\text{ if }n\neq 1\end{array}\right.

it follows from the definition of
\mu^{\ast}
that

\mu^{\ast}(\mathscr{C})\leq\vol(\mathscr{C})\leq\mu^{\ast}(\mathscr{C})

which implies
\vol(\mathscr{C})=\mu^{\ast}(\mathscr{C})
. This finished the proof.
∎

Therefore,
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
is a measure on
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i}
satisfying identity (
5
). In conclusion, we have proved the main result of this article. The following existence result.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of measure spaces, then there exists a measure
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
on the measurable space
(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i})
satisfying for each
\mathscr{C}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
the identity

\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(\mathscr{C})=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(C_{i}).

4.
Decomposition Theorem for Finite Measures

In the remaining sections, the Lebesgue spaces of infinite product measures are studied and simplified in terms of
L_{p}
of finite product ones, for the particular case in which a finiteness condition is satisfied. Let
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of measure spaces satisfying the finiteness condition

\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})\in(0,+\infty).

Consider the measure space
\left(\mathscr{C},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}\right)
, where
\mathscr{C}=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{i}
and
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
is the measure constructed in the last section in terms of the outer measure (
14
). In this section we will deal with the space

L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}\right)

for
1\leq p<+\infty
. To simplify the notation, throughout this paper we will use indistinctly the notations
L_{p}(\mathscr{C})
and
L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{\infty}\Sigma_{i},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}\right)
; and analogously
L_{p}(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i})
and
L_{p}(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma_{i},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i})
. Moreover, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we will use the notation
\Omega^{n}
to denote
\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i}
. Consider for
1\leq p<+\infty
, the vector space

\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right):=\bigg\{(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\bigtimes_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right):\left.\left\|\frac{f_{n}}{\prod_{i=n}^{m}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}-f_{m}\right\|_{L_{p}\left(\Omega^{m}\right)}\xrightarrow[n,m\rightarrow\infty]{}0\bigg\}\right/\sim,

where given
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\bigtimes_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{p}(\Omega^{n})
, we identify the sequences
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sim(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
if

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|f_{n}-g_{n}\|_{L_{p}(\Omega^{n})}=0.

Moreover, in the definition of
\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}(\Omega^{n})
, we are identifying for every
n<m
,
S^{m}_{n}(f)\equiv f
, where
S_{n}^{m}
is the linear embedding
S_{n}^{m}:L_{p}(\Omega^{n})\hookrightarrow L_{p}(\Omega^{m})
defined by

S_{n}^{m}(f):\Omega^{m}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\cdots,\omega_{m})\mapstochar\rightarrow f(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\cdots,\omega_{n})

for each
f\in L_{p}(\Omega^{n})
. Hereinafter, we will use this identifications. If we consider the functional

\|\cdot\|_{\lim L_{p}}:\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}(\Omega^{n})\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow[0,+\infty),\quad(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mapstochar\rightarrow\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|f_{n}\|_{L_{p}(\Omega^{n})}

then, for each
1\leq p<+\infty
, the pair

(\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right),\|\cdot\|_{\lim L_{p}})
defines a Banach space.
It is easily seen following
[
21
]
that
\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
is a realization of the inductive limit of the sequence
(L_{p}(\Omega^{n}),S_{n}^{n+1})
in the category
\mathfrak{Ban}
whose objects are Banach spaces and whose morphisms are linear isometries. The main result of this section states that the next spaces are isometrically isomorphic for
1\leq p<+\infty

(18)

L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)\simeq\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right),

and consequently we would have the embedding

L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)\hookrightarrow\bigtimes_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right),

that allows us to consider functions defined in spaces of infinite dimensions as a sequence of finite dimensional ones.

4.1.
Preliminary Lemmas

We will give some lemmas concerning some dense subspaces of
L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)
and
\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)

that will be useful for the proof of (
18
). Throughout this section, we will identify for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the spaces

\displaystyle L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)\simeq\left\{f\cdot\chi_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}:f\in L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)\right\}\subset L_{p}(\mathscr{C}),

where
\chi_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}
stands for the characteristic function of the set
\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The subspace
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
is dense in
L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)
for each
1\leq p<+\infty
.

Proof.

By definition, we have

\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\Sigma_{i}=\sigma(\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}).

Consequently since
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an algebra of subsets, by Lemma 3.4.6 of
[
5
]
, the space
\text{Span}\big\{\chi_{R}:R\in\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\big\}
is dense in
L_{p}(\mathscr{C})
. Finally, due to the inclusion

\text{Span}\big\{\chi_{R}:R\in\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\big\}\subset\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right),

the result follows.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{p}^{N}
be the subspace of
\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{p}^{N}:=\bigg\{(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\bigtimes_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right):\exists g\in L_{p}\left(\Omega^{N}\right),\ f_{n}=\frac{g}{\prod_{i=N+1}^{n}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})},\ \forall n\geq N\bigg\},

then for each
1\leq p<+\infty
, the subspace
\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{F}_{p}^{N}
is dense in
\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}(\Omega^{n})
.

Proof.

Let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
and consider the sequence
(\mathbf{F}^{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
defined by

\mathbf{F}^{m}_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if }n<m\\
\frac{f_{m}}{\prod_{i=m+1}^{n}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}&\mbox{if }n\geq m\end{array}\right.

for each
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, by definition

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{F}^{m}-(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|_{\lim L_{p}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|\mathbf{F}^{m}_{n}-f_{n}\|_{L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\|\frac{f_{m}}{\prod_{i=m+1}^{n}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}-f_{n}\right\|_{L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)}

and since
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
, it is apparent that

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\|\mathbf{F}^{m}-(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|_{\lim L_{p}}=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\|\frac{f_{m}}{\prod_{i=m+1}^{n}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}-f_{n}\right\|_{L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)}=0.

Thus we have proved

(\mathbf{F}^{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\xrightarrow[m\rightarrow\infty]{}(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ \text{ in }\ \underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right).

Consequently since
(\mathbf{F}^{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{F}_{p}^{N}
, we conclude that the subspace
\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{F}_{p}^{N}
is dense in
\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}(\Omega^{n})
.
∎

Another key result will be the Jessen’s theorem. This theorem was proved by B. Jessen in 1934 in his PhD Thesis
[
10
]
and nowadays is proved by martingales techniques
[
22
, Theorem 7.16]
. However, Jessen proved the result only for probability spaces. We need a version for the general case in which the involved spaces
(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})
are not necessarily of probability. This version is given in the next result which proof can be adapted
mutatis mutandis
from the proofs of Theorems II, III and IV of Baker’s paper
[
3
]
. It must be observed that the extra conditions imposed by Baker on the measure spaces are not used in the proofs of Theorems II, III and IV.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\{(\Omega_{i},\Sigma_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of measure spaces satisfying the finiteness condition
\prod_{i}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})\in(0,+\infty)
, and consider
f\in L_{p}(\mathscr{C})
with
1\leq p<+\infty
, then if we denote
\omega^{n}=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},...,\omega_{n})
,
\overline{\omega}_{n}=(\omega_{n+1},\omega_{n+2},...)
and respectively for the variable
x
,

(1)

\displaystyle\int_{\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i}}f(\omega^{n},\overline{x}_{n})\ d\omega\in L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n-1},\overline{\omega}_{n-1})\ d\omega\in L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)

for
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
-a.e.
x\in\mathscr{C}
and every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(2)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i}}f(\omega^{n},\overline{x}_{n})\ d\omega=\int_{\mathscr{C}}f

for
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
-a.e.
x\in\mathscr{C}
and also with convergence in
L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)
.

(3)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n-1},\overline{\omega}_{n-1})\ d\omega=f

for
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}
-a.e.
x\in\mathscr{C}
and also with convergence in
L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)
.

It is remarkable that Baker in his article
[
3
]
does not spell correctly the name
Jessen
and instead he use
Jensen
.

4.2.
Decomposition Theorem

Once the necessary machinery has been presented, we proceed to prove the identification (
18
).

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
1\leq p<+\infty
and consider the operators

\mathfrak{T}:\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right),\quad(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mapstochar\rightarrow\lim_{L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)}\frac{f_{n}}{\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}

and

\mathfrak{S}:L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right),\quad f\mapstochar\rightarrow\left(\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.

Then
\mathfrak{T}
is an isometric isomorphism with
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{T}^{-1}
. In particular,
L_{p}(\mathscr{C})
is isometrically isomorphic to
\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}(\Omega^{n})
,

L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)\simeq\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right).

Proof.

Firstly, note that given
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
, the sequence

\left(\frac{f_{n}}{\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)

is Cauchy in
L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)
since by Fubini’s theorem

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{f_{n}}{\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}-\frac{f_{m}}{\prod_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}\right\|_{L_{p}(\mathscr{C})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}\left\|\frac{f_{n}}{\prod_{i=n+1}^{m}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}-f_{m}\right\|_{L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{f_{n}}{\prod_{i=n+1}^{m}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}-f_{m}\right\|_{L_{p}\left(\Omega^{m}\right)}\xrightarrow[n,m\rightarrow\infty]{}0.

Thus
\mathfrak{T}
is well defined and it is linear. On the other hand, by Theorem
4.3
item (1), if
f\in L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)
, necessarily

\left(\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\bigtimes_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right).

By Theorem
4.3
, the next sequence converges

\lim_{L_{p}(\mathscr{C})}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega=\lim_{L_{p}(\mathscr{C})}\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega=f

and consequently for
n<m
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{\prod_{i=n+2}^{m+1}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega-\int_{\bigtimes_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{m},\overline{\omega}_{m})\ d\omega\right\|_{L_{p}(\mathscr{C})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=m+2}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})\cdot\Bigg\|\frac{\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega}{\prod_{i=n+2}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}-\frac{\int_{\bigtimes_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{m},\overline{\omega}_{m})\ d\omega}{\prod_{i=m+2}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}\Bigg\|_{L_{p}(\mathscr{C})}\xrightarrow[n,m\rightarrow\infty]{}0.

We have proved that

\left(\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)

or equivalently
\mathfrak{S}(f)\in\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
. This establishes that
\mathfrak{S}
is well defined and that it is linear. Once we have proved the consistency of the linear operators
\mathfrak{T}
and
\mathfrak{S}
, we will prove that they are isometries. Take
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
, then

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{T}(f_{n})_{n}\|_{L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)}

\displaystyle=\left\|\lim_{L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)}\frac{f_{n}}{\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}\right\|_{L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Bigg\|\frac{f_{n}}{\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}\Bigg\|_{L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})\Bigg\|\frac{f_{n}}{\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}\Bigg\|_{L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|f_{n}\|_{L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)}=\|(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|_{\lim L_{p}}.

Thus,
\mathfrak{T}
is an isometry. On the other hand, if
f\in L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)
, we compute

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{S}(f)\|_{\lim L_{p}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\|_{\lim L_{p}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\|\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega\right\|_{L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\|\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega\right\|_{L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)}\right)\cdot\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\|\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega\right\|_{L_{p}(\mathscr{C})}=\left\|\lim_{L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)}\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega\right\|_{L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)}

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)}

where the last step follows from Theorem
4.3
item (3). Therefore,
\mathfrak{S}
is also an isometry. Finally we will see that
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{T}^{-1}
. Consider
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{F}_{p}^{N}
and let

g\in L_{p}(\Omega^{M})
such that
f_{n}=\frac{g}{\prod_{i=M+1}^{n}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}
for
n\geq M
, then if we denote
g^{n}=g(x^{n-1},\overline{\omega}_{n-1})
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{S}\circ\mathfrak{T})(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=\mathfrak{S}\left(\lim_{L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)}\frac{g}{\prod_{i=M+1}^{n}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})\cdot\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}\right)=\frac{\mathfrak{S}(g)}{\prod_{i=M+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=M+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}\left(\int_{\bigtimes_{i=2}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}g^{2}\ d\omega,...,\int_{\bigtimes_{i=M}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}g^{M}\ d\omega,g\prod_{i=M+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i}),g\prod_{i=M+2}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i}),...\right)

\displaystyle=\left(f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{M-1},\ g,\frac{g}{\mu_{M+1}(\Omega_{M+1})},\ \frac{g}{\prod_{i=M+1}^{M+2}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})},...\right)=(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},

The last steps are justified by the equivalence relation defined on
\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
. Thus, we have proved that if
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{F}_{p}^{N}

(\mathfrak{S}\circ\mathfrak{T})(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.

Since
\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{F}_{p}^{N}
is dense in
\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
and
\mathfrak{S}\circ\mathfrak{T}
is continuous (since it is an isometry), it is apparent that

\mathfrak{S}\circ\mathfrak{T}=I_{\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)}.

On the other hand, consider
f\in\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
. Then
f\in L_{p}(\Omega^{M})
for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
and if we denote
f^{n}=f(x^{n-1},\overline{\omega}_{n-1})
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{T}\circ\mathfrak{S})(f)=\mathfrak{T}\left(\int_{\bigtimes_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f(x^{n},\overline{\omega}_{n})\ d\omega\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{T}\Bigg(\int_{\bigtimes_{i=2}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f^{2}\ d\omega,...,\int_{\bigtimes_{i=M}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}}f^{M}\ d\omega,\ f\prod_{i=M+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i}),\ f\prod_{i=M+2}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})...\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\lim_{L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)}\frac{f\cdot\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}{\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\Omega_{i})}=f.

Consequently, if
f\in\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
, it is apparent that

(\mathfrak{T}\circ\mathfrak{S})(f)=f.

Since
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{p}\left(\Omega^{n}\right)
is dense in
L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)
and
\mathfrak{T}\circ\mathfrak{S}
is continuous, we conclude that

\mathfrak{T}\circ\mathfrak{S}=I_{L_{p}\left(\mathscr{C}\right)}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

4.3.
Example

It is convenient to give an example to clarify the theory. For this reason we will state briefly Example 3.8 of
[
21
]
. Let us consider the sequence of measure spaces
\{([0,1],\mathcal{B}_{[0,1]}.m_{[0,1]})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
where
\mathcal{B}_{[0,1]}
is the
Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
[0,1]
and
m_{[0,1]}
the Lebesgue measure. Consider the infinite product measure space

\left([0,1]^{\mathbb{N}},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{B}_{[0,1]},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}m_{[0,1]}\right).

The space
[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
is called
Hilbert cube
. We will use Theorem
4.4
to compute the integral of the function

f:[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\ f(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\log(x_{n})|}{(a+n-1)^{2}}}}.

By Theorem 3.7 of
[
21
]
, for
a>0
, we have

(19)

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{[0,1]^{k}}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{k}\frac{|\log(x_{n})|}{(a+n-1)^{2}}}}=\frac{\Gamma\left(a+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma(a)}.

Define the functions
f_{k}:[0,1]^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

f_{k}(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{k})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{k}\frac{|\log(x_{n})|}{(a+n-1)^{2}}}},

then
f_{k}\geq 0
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and we have

(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\bigtimes_{k\in\mathbb{N}}L_{1}[0,1]^{k}.

Besides, it is evident that
f_{k}\geq f_{k+1}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and hence if
k>l

\displaystyle\|f_{l}-f_{k}\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{k}}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{k}}|f_{l}-f_{k}|\ dx=\int_{[0,1]^{l}}f_{l}\ dx-\int_{[0,1]^{k}}f_{k}\ dx

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{l}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{l}\frac{|\log(x_{n})|}{(a+n-1)^{2}}}}\ dx-\int_{[0,1]^{k}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{k}\frac{|\log(x_{n})|}{(a+n-1)^{2}}}}\ dx,

and this implies by (
19
) that
\|f_{l}-f_{k}\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{k}}\xrightarrow[k,l\rightarrow\infty]{}0
. Therefore, we have

(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\underrightarrow{\lim}\ L_{1}[0,1]^{k}

with norm

\|(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|_{\lim L_{1}}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|f_{k}\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{k}}=\frac{\Gamma\left(a+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma(a)}.

Finally, by Theorem
4.4
we get

\mathfrak{T}(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\log(x_{n})|}{(a+n-1)^{2}}}}\in L_{1}\left([0,1]^{\mathbb{N}},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{B}_{[0,1]},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}m_{[0,1]}\right).

Therefore, we have that
\mathfrak{T}(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=f
and

f=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\log(x_{n})|}{(a+n-1)^{2}}}}\in L_{1}\left([0,1]^{\mathbb{N}},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{B}_{[0,1]},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}m_{[0,1]}\right).

Moreover, since
\mathfrak{T}
is an isometric isomorphism, we get

\int_{[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}}f\ d\mu=\|f\|_{L_{1}\left([0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}\right)}=\|(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|_{\lim L_{1}}=\frac{\Gamma\left(a+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma(a)},

where
\mu=\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}m_{[0,1]}
.

5.
Decomposition Theorem for Infinite Measures

In this section, we will give the analogue of Theorem
4.4
for the case of the sequence of measure spaces
\{(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}},m_{\mathbb{R}})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
where
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}}
is the borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathbb{R}
and
m_{\mathbb{R}}
is the Lebesgue measure of
\mathbb{R}
. Observe that this sequence does not satisfy the finiteness condition since
\prod_{i}m_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R})=+\infty
and therefore Theorem
4.4
cannot be applied directly. Consider the corresponding product measure space

\left(\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{R},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}},\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}m_{\mathbb{R}}\right).

Let us denote
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:=\bigtimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{R}
. It should be observed that
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}}
where we are considering on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
the product topology. Moreover, it becomes apparent that the measure
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}m_{\mathbb{R}}
is, in fact, the
Baker measure

\lambda_{B}
, constructed in
[
3
, Theorem I]
. Thus, we will denote the measure
\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{N}}m_{\mathbb{R}}
by
\lambda_{B}
. For the sake of notation, hereinafter, we will also denote for each
\mathfrak{a}=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{N}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}

[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}:=\bigtimes_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[a_{n},a_{n}+1)\quad\text{and}\quad[0,1)^{m}_{\mathfrak{a}}:=\bigtimes_{n=1}^{m}[a_{n},a_{n}+1),

where
\mathcal{N}:=\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
. The next result simplifies considerably the integration issues on the measure space
(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}},\lambda_{B})
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}},\lambda_{B})
such that
f\geq 0
, then the set

\mathcal{O}_{f}=\left\{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{N}:\int_{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}}f\ d\lambda_{B}\neq 0\right\}

is countable and

(20)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}}f\ d\lambda_{B}=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{O}_{f}}\int_{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}}f\ d\lambda_{B}.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{O}_{f}
is uncountable and let
\mathfrak{F}
be a finite subset of
\mathcal{O}_{f}
. Therefore, since

[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}\cap[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{b}}=\varnothing\quad\text{ for each }\quad\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in\mathcal{N},\quad\mathfrak{a}\neq\mathfrak{b},

it is apparent that

\int_{\biguplus\left\{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}:\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{F}\right\}}f\ d\lambda_{B}=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{F}}\int_{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}}f\ d\lambda_{B}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}}f\ d\lambda_{B}.

Thus, recalling the definition of uncountable sum, we arrive at

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{O}_{f}}\int_{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}}f\ d\lambda_{B}:=\sup\left\{\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{F}}\int_{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}}f\ d\lambda_{B}:\mathfrak{F}\subset\mathcal{O}_{f}\text{ finite}\right\}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}}f\ d\lambda_{B}.

Since
\mathcal{O}_{f}
is uncountable and

\int_{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}}f\ d\lambda_{B}>0,

for each
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{O}_{f}
, by Proposition 0.20 of
[
7
]
,

\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{O}_{f}}\int_{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}}f\ d\lambda_{B}=+\infty

and this gets a contradiction since
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}},\lambda_{B})
. Therefore
\mathcal{O}_{f}
is countable. Now, we prove identity (
20
). Let
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}},\lambda_{B})
with
f\geq 0
, then since
\mathcal{O}_{f}
is countable,
f|_{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}}=0

\lambda_{B}
-a.e. for each
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{N}\backslash\mathcal{O}_{f}
. Hence, since

\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}=\biguplus\left\{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}:\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{N}\right\}

it follows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}}f\ d\lambda_{B}=\int_{\biguplus\{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}:\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{O}_{f}\}}f\ d\lambda_{B}=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{O}_{f}}\int_{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}}f\ d\lambda_{B}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Define for each
\mathfrak{a}=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{N}
, the translation

T_{\mathfrak{a}}:[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}},\quad(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mapstochar\rightarrow(x_{n}-a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.

Then, it is apparent that
T_{\mathfrak{a}}([0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}})=[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover, since
\lambda_{B}
is translation invariant, we have that

(21)

\lambda_{B}(A)=\lambda_{B}(T_{\mathfrak{a}}(A))=\lambda_{B}(T_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}(A))\text{ for each }A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}}.

Define for each
1\leq p<+\infty
, the vector space

\ell_{p}(L_{p}([0,1)^{\mathbb{N}},\lambda_{B}),\mathcal{N})\subset\bigoplus_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{N}}L_{p}([0,1)^{\mathbb{N}},\lambda_{B})

consisting in all sequence
\mathfrak{f}=(f_{\mathfrak{a}})_{\mathfrak{a}}
satisfying

(1)

f_{\mathfrak{a}}\in L_{p}([0,1)^{\mathbb{N}},\lambda_{B})
for each
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{N}
.

(2)

f_{\mathfrak{a}}=0

\lambda_{B}
-a.e. for each
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{N}\backslash\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{f}}
for some countable subset
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{f}}\subset\mathcal{N}
.

(3)

\|\mathfrak{f}\|_{p,\oplus}<+\infty
, where

(22)

\|\mathfrak{f}\|_{p,\oplus}:=\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{f}}}\int_{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}}|f_{\mathfrak{a}}|^{p}\ d\lambda_{B}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Under this definitions, it is clear that the pair

\left(\ell_{p}(L_{p}([0,1)^{\mathbb{N}},\lambda_{B}),\mathcal{N}),\|\cdot\|_{p,\oplus}\right)

defines a normed space. It must be observed that the restriction of
\lambda_{B}
to

is the product measure

constructed in section II. This measure satisfies the finiteness condition

and therefore Theorem
4.4
can be applied to simplify the structure of
L_{p}([0,1)^{\mathbb{N}},\lambda_{B})
for each
1\leq p<+\infty
and to compute in a simple manner each term of (
22
). Hence, the structure of the space
\ell_{p}(L_{p}([0,1)^{\mathbb{N}},\lambda_{B}),\mathcal{N})
is simply determined. The next result establishes that the spaces

and

establishing an analogue of the Theorem
4.4
for this case. As a direct consequence we stablish the embedding

Theorem 5.2
.

The following spaces are isometrically isomorphic for

Fix

Given

5.1

f|_{[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}_{\mathfrak{a}}}=0

\lambda_{B}

\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{N}\backslash\mathcal{O}_{f}
. Consequently, by identity (
20
), it follows that

\displaystyle\|f\|^{p}_{L_{p}(\lambda_{B})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}}|f|^{p}\ d\lambda_{B}=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{O}_{f}}\int_{[0,1)_{\mathfrak{a}}^{\mathbb{N}}}|f|^{p}\ d\lambda_{B}.

On the other hand, for each
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{N}

)

for each

. Moreover

(24)
