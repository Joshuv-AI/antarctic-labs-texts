Higher-Order GFDM for Linear Elliptic Operators Thanks: Preprint
Heinrich Kraus, Jörg Kuhnert, Pratik Suchde

Abstract
We present a novel approach of discretizing diffusion operators of the form ∇ ⋅ ( λ ∇ u ) \nabla\cdot(\lambda\nabla u) in the context of meshfree generalized finite difference methods. Our ansatz uses properties of derived operators and combines the discrete Laplace operator with reconstruction functions approximating the diffusion coefficient λ \lambda . Provided that the reconstructions are of a sufficiently high order, we prove that the order of accuracy of the discrete Laplace operator transfers to the derived diffusion operator. We show that the new discrete diffusion operator inherits the diagonal dominance property of the discrete Laplace operator and fulfills enrichment properties. Our numerical results for elliptic and parabolic partial differential equations show that even low-order reconstructions preserve the order of the underlying discrete Laplace operator for sufficiently smooth diffusion coefficients. In experiments, we demonstrate the applicability of the new discrete diffusion operator to interface problems with point clouds not aligning to the interface and numerically prove first-order convergence.



1
Introduction and governing equations

Meshfree methods grew in popularity due to their applicability to problems where classical mesh-based methods struggle due to the high computational cost of meshing and re-meshing algorithms. We present a generalized finite difference method (GFDM) that stems from a generalization of the SPH method. It expands the capabilities of SPH for free surface problems by consistent discrete differential operators from a moving least squares (MLS) finite difference ansatz
[
1
]
. This method proved itself in practice in the simulation of free surface flows with complex geometries in industrial applications
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. Our eventual goal is to extend this method to allow phase change simulations in a one-fluid model where the phase change occurs across a diffuse phase change region and not at a sharp interface dividing the domain into phases
[
5
,
6
]
. In such models, the diffusion operator, abbreviated by

\Delta_{\lambda}u:=\nabla\cdot(\lambda\nabla u),

(1)

appears in the momentum equation where
\lambda
represents the viscosity and
u
is the velocity, and in the energy equation where
u
is the temperature and
\lambda
is the heat conductivity. The heat conductivity can exhibit jumps in phase change or compound material simulations. Additionally, the viscosity can jump by several orders of magnitude when modeling solid materials as extremely viscous fluids. With this in view, it is necessary to study the case of strong discontinuities in the diffusion coefficient
\lambda
, leading to an interface problem. We only consider the case of a linear diffusion operator in this paper.

Classical mesh-based methods, such as the finite element method and the finite volume method, facilitate the discretization of diffusion operators as in
equation

1
because they require the weak or integral form of partial differential equations
[
7
,
8
]
. In contrast, the GFDM that we present in
section

2
uses differential operators in their strong form with an MLS-ansatz to enforce consistency conditions for the discrete operators
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. The MLS-ansatz lacks stability features that are intrinsic to mesh-based methods such as diagonal dominance which is essential for fulfilling the discrete maximum principle for elliptic problems
[
12
,
13
]
. We present a one-dimensional correction technique to enforce diagonal dominance upon the discrete operators. Furthermore, we investigate derived operators with an emphasis on derived operators from the discrete Laplace operator. This will be the foundation for the new discrete diffusion operator in
section

3
.

Applying the MLS-based GFDM to the diffusion operator (
1
) in its strong form requires the gradient of the diffusion coefficient
\nabla\lambda
to enforce consistency conditions. The gradient needs to be approximated for problems where the gradient cannot be computed manually. For sufficiently smooth
\lambda
, it is reasonable to approximate the gradient, however, for discontinuous
\lambda
this approach leads to instabilities for elliptic interface problems
[
14
]
.
A common approach to tackle discontinuities in the diffusion coefficient is to divide the domain into subdomains on which the diffusivity is smooth. Such domain decompositions require explicit interface conditions between the subdomains and points on the interface
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. In the present work, we assume an unknown interface location rendering it impossible to perform a domain decomposition without prior interface identification. Moreover, placing points on the interface is impossible in a diffuse interface scenario.
Yoon and Song
[
19
]
, Kim et al.
[
20
]
, and Suchde and Kuhnert
[
21
]
incorporate enrichment to improve the discrete diffusion operator by adding non-differentiable functions to the consistency conditions in assumed interface regions.

The new discrete diffusion operator presented in
section

3
does not require the gradient
\nabla\lambda
. Instead, we derive a discrete diffusion operator from the discrete Laplace operator by weighing its coefficients with reconstructions of
\lambda
. Thus, neither do we treat interface points specially nor do we require knowledge of the interface location. Moreover, the new operator inherits diagonal dominance from the discrete Laplace operator and satisfies enrichment properties similar to those enforced by Suchde and Kuhnert
[
21
]
.

We test the new discrete diffusion operator in
section

4
using the parabolic heat equation

{\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}}=\Delta_{\lambda}u+q

(2)

and the elliptic Poisson’s equation

-\Delta_{\lambda}u=q.

(3)

It is straightforward to incorporate non-homogeneous Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions in GFDM, but in this paper, we only impose homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions
u|_{\partial\Omega}=0
on the boundary of a domain
\Omega
.

2
Generalized finite difference method

Our formulation of GFDM is based on the discretization of a closed domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
by a point cloud
\Omega_{h}=\set{\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{N}}.
The indexed function
h\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is called interaction radius or smoothing length and establishes a concept of connectivity through the discrete balls given by
B_{i}=\set{\bm{x}\in\Omega_{h}\mid\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i}\rVert\leq h(\bm{x}_{i})}
defining the stencils
\mathcal{S}_{i}=\set{j\mid\bm{x}_{j}\in B_{i}}.
Since a point with index
i
is always a part of its stencil,
i\in\mathcal{S}_{i}
, it is convenient to define the neighbors
\mathcal{N}_{i}=\mathcal{S}_{i}\setminus\set{i}
.

Throughout the document, we use the Landau notation
O(h^{p})
without specifying the limit
h\to 0
. This limit means that the interaction radius
h
converges uniformly to the zero function.

2.1
Differential operator discretization

Our formulation of GFDM discretizes linear differential operators
D
in their strong form at each point
\bm{x}_{i}\in\Omega_{h}
. We compute coefficients
c_{ij}^{D}
to represent the discrete differential operator
D_{i}
at
\bm{x}_{i}
by

Du(\bm{x}_{i})\approx D_{i}u=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{D}u_{j},

(4)

where
u_{j}=u(\bm{x}_{j})
. The numerous ways to calculate the coefficients
c_{ij}^{D}
divide GFDM into distinct formulations, for example, RBF methods
[
22
]
, RBF-FD methods
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
, or Voronoi-based finite volume methods (FVM)
[
26
]
. Milewski
[
27
]
applies a random walk technique to obtain the coefficients and stencils, while Davydov and Safarpoor
[
17
]
select the stencils based on quality measures. Both approaches generally lead to non-radial neighborhoods
B_{i}
.

For our MLS formulation, we define a test function set
\Phi_{i}
and enforce exact reproducibility

D\phi(\bm{x}_{i})=D_{i}\phi,\quad\forall\phi\in\Phi_{i}.

(5)

If the number of neighbors coincides with the number of test functions,
\lvert B_{i}\rvert=\lvert\Phi_{i}\rvert
,
equation

5
is solvable, provided that the resulting matrix is invertible. But the MLS method originates from neighborhoods that have more points than there are test functions,
\lvert B_{i}\rvert>\lvert\Phi_{i}\rvert
. To solve
equation

5
in this case, we impose a minimization

\min\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}\left(\frac{c_{ij}^{D}}{w_{ij}}\right)^{2}

(6)

with weights
w_{ij}=w(\lVert\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i}\rVert/h_{i})
given by a decreasing weight function
w\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
that we set as
w(r)=\exp(-r)
. Generally, the choice of the weight function influences the discretization error but is not a subject of this paper
[
28
]
.

Together with
equation

6
, we rewrite
equation

5
in matrix form

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\frac{\lVert\bm{W}_{i}^{-1}\bm{c}_{i}^{D}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2}

(7a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\bm{K}_{i}\bm{c}_{i}^{D}=\bm{b}_{i}^{D}

(7b)

where
\bm{W}_{i}
is a diagonal matrix with the weights
w_{ij}
,
\bm{c}_{i}^{D}
is the vector containing the coefficients
c_{ij}^{D}
and
\bm{K}_{i}
and
\bm{b}_{i}
are the matrix and right-hand side resulting from the reproducibility conditions in
equation

5
. The optimization problem has the unique solution

\bm{c}_{i}^{D}=\bm{W}_{i}^{2}\bm{K}_{i}^{T}\bm{A}_{i}^{-1}\bm{b}_{i}^{D}

(7c)

with

\bm{A}_{i}=\bm{K}_{i}\bm{W}_{i}^{2}\bm{K}_{i}^{T}.

(7d)

We use monomials up to degree
r

\Phi_{i}=\set{(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}}{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq r}

(8)

to enforce the consistency of the discrete differential operators. In the above, the multi-index notation with the conventions
\bm{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
and
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}
for
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
and
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is used. We will further use the notation
\bm{\alpha}!=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}!
and
\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
.

The condition number of the matrix
\bm{A}_{i}
in
equation

7d
depends on the smoothing length
h
and tends to
\infty
when
h\to 0
. It becomes independent of the smoothing length for scaled monomials
(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}/h_{i}^{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert}
but for clarity, we use the unscaled monomials in the present work
[
29
]
.

Monomial test functions enable us to discretize basic differential operators, such as the Laplace operator
\Delta
or directional derivatives
{\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{v}}}
. To illustrate our formulation of the GFDM, let us consider the Laplace operator and monomial test functions. The reproducibility conditions from
equation

5
read

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}=\begin{cases}2\delta_{kl},&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{l},\\
0,&\text{else},\end{cases}

(9)

where
\bm{e}_{k}
and
\bm{e}_{l}
are the
k
-th and
l
-th standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, and
\delta_{kl}
is the Kronecker delta symbol. According to these consistency conditions, the right-hand side
\bm{b}_{i}^{\Delta}
in
equation

7b
is independent of
\bm{x}_{i}
. On the other hand, by expanding the diffusion operator (
1
) for a sufficiently smooth diffusivity
\lambda

\Delta_{\lambda}u=\langle\nabla\lambda,\nabla u\rangle+\lambda\Delta u,

(10)

it is straightforward to derive the consistency conditions

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}=\begin{cases}\partial_{k}\lambda(\bm{x}_{i}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k},\\
2\delta_{kl}\lambda(\bm{x}_{i}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{l},\\
0,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(11)

In contrast to
\bm{b}_{i}^{\Delta}
, the right-hand side
\bm{b}_{i}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}
depends on
\bm{x}_{i}
and needs the gradient
\nabla\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})
or an approximation thereof. The new discrete diffusion operator circumvents the necessity of explicitly calculating the diffusivity gradient and the right-hand side for the optimization problem (
7
).

2.2
Diagonal dominance

As we pointed out in our previous work
[
14
]
, diagonal dominance is an essential feature of discrete diffusion operators.

Definition 2.1
.

A discrete differential operator
D_{i}
is called diagonally dominant if it is consistent for constant functions,
c_{ii}^{D}\neq 0
, and its coefficients
c_{ij}^{D}
satisfy the sign condition

c_{ij}^{D}c_{ii}^{D}\leq 0

(12)

for all
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
.

Diagonally dominant discrete Laplace operators with
c_{ij}^{\Delta}\geq 0
for
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
lead to an M-matrix and the solution fulfilling the discrete maximum principle for Poisson’s equation
[
12
,
30
]
. Diagonal dominance is also crucial for solving the parabolic heat equation, where the lack of diagonal dominance leads to severe instabilities
[
11
]
.

Because the optimization problem (
7
) does not enforce diagonal dominance, a correction technique was proposed by Suchde
[
11
]
. For the correction, we use a second discrete operator
\bm{c}_{i}^{0}\in\ker{\bm{K}_{i}}
approximating the zero functional
f\mapsto 0
non-trivially by prescribing
c_{ii}^{0}=1
in the optimization. The corrected operator

\widehat{\bm{c}}_{i}^{D}=\bm{c}_{i}^{D}+\widehat{\alpha}\bm{c}_{i}^{0}

(13)

satisfies the same consistency conditions as
\bm{c}_{i}^{D}
. One way to obtain the coefficient
\widehat{\alpha}
is to formulate a minimization problem that penalizes large off-diagonal entries relative to the diagonal entry

\widehat{\alpha}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}\frac{(c_{ij}^{D}+\alpha c_{ij}^{0})^{2}}{(c_{ii}^{D}+\alpha c_{ii}^{0})^{2}}.

(14)

The advantage of this minimization approach is its unique solvability and the low computational cost. But a disadvantage is that the sign condition (
12
) is not necessarily fulfilled. The correction technique in
equation

13
with the minimization (
14
) performs well on high-quality point clouds but fails to provide diagonally dominant operators on lower-quality stencils
[
11
,
14
]
. The measurement and enhancement of point cloud quality are subject to current research.

2.3
Derived operators

In classical finite difference methods, operators can be derived from existing operators. Suppose a uniform grid with uniform spacing
h>0
in 1D, the second derivative is approximated by
u^{\prime\prime}(x_{i})\approx(u_{i-1}-2u_{i}+u_{i+1})/h^{2}
. Multiplying the coefficients by
(x_{j}-x_{i})/2
, where
j\in\set{i-1,i,i+1}
, we derive a new operator that approximates the first derivative
u^{\prime}(x_{i})\approx(u_{i+1}-u_{i-1})/2h.
Repeating this step with
(x_{j}-x_{i})^{2}/2
yields an approximation operator that is the arithmetic average
u(x_{i})\approx(u_{i+1}+u_{i-1})/2.

The same holds for GFDM, where multiplying the coefficients of a discrete differential operator with a monomial
d_{ij}^{\widetilde{D}}=c_{ij}^{D}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}
may lead to an approximation of a different linear operator
\widetilde{D}
. We will use this property in combination with the discrete Laplace operator in
section

3
to derive a discrete diffusion operator.

For the remainder of the document, we assume that the discrete Laplace operator is accurate of order
p\geq 2
, fulfilling the consistency conditions from
equation

9
for all monomials up to degree
p
and

\Delta u(\bm{x}_{i})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}u_{j}+O(h^{p}).

The consistency conditions (
9
) for the discrete Laplace operator lead to
c_{ij}^{\Delta}=O(h^{-2})
for
h\to 0
, similar to the classical finite difference method. The following theorem describes which operators can be derived from the discrete Laplace operator.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\xi\in C^{q}(\Omega)
then the coefficients

c_{ij}^{\xi}=\xi_{j}c_{ij}^{\Delta},\quad c_{ii}^{\xi}=-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\xi_{j}c_{ij}^{\Delta},

where
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
fulfill

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\xi}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{0},\\
2\partial_{k}\xi(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k},\\
2\delta_{kl}\xi(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+1}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{l},\\
O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert-1}),&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert=0
, then we obtain a constant monomial which, by definition of the coefficients
c_{ij}^{\xi}
, leads to

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\xi}=0.

Thus, we only consider the case
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert>0
. Using the definition of the coefficients
c_{ij}^{\xi}
and a Taylor expansion of
\xi
, we write

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\xi}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\xi_{j}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\Big[\sum_{\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert\leq q}\frac{\partial^{\bm{\beta}}\xi(\bm{x}_{i})}{\bm{\beta}!}\left(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i}\right)^{\bm{\beta}}+O(h^{q+1})\Big]c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert\leq q}\Big[\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}}\Big]\frac{\partial^{\bm{\beta}}\xi(\bm{x}_{i})}{\bm{\beta}!}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}O(h^{q+1}).

We split the outer summation over the multi-indices in the first term, such that in the interior sum only monomials of degree
\leq p
appear and write

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\xi}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}=\sum_{\lvert\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}\rvert\leq p}\Big[\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}}\Big]\frac{\partial^{\bm{\beta}}\xi(\bm{x}_{i})}{\bm{\beta}!}+r_{i,\bm{\alpha}}

(15)

with the remainder

\displaystyle r_{i,\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p+1\leq\lvert\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}\rvert\leq q+\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert}\Big[\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}}\Big]\frac{\partial^{\bm{\beta}}\xi(\bm{x}_{i})}{\bm{\beta}!}

(16a)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}O(h^{q+1}).

(16b)

Let us first take a closer look at the first term in the remainder
r_{i,\bm{\alpha}}
from
equation

16
. Since we assume that
p\geq 2
and thus
p+1\geq 3
, we obtain

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}}=O(h^{p})

for each
\bm{\beta}
such that
p+1\leq\lvert\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}\rvert\leq q+\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert
. Additionally, from
c_{ij}^{\Delta}=O(h^{-2})
and
(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}=O(h^{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert})
we conclude for the second term in
equation

16

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}O(h^{q+1})=O(h^{q+\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert-1})

which leads to the final representation of the remainder

r_{i,\bm{\alpha}}=O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert-1}).

Now, we examine the first term on the right-hand side of
equation

15
. Since the sum is dependent on
\bm{\alpha}
, we perform a case distinction.

i)

For
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert=1
, we represent the multi-index with
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k}
for some
k\in\set{1,\dots,d}
. By the consistency conditions of
c_{ij}^{\Delta}
, the interior sum in
equation

15
is non-zero if and only if
\bm{\beta}=\bm{e}_{k}
and it becomes

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\xi}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}=\partial_{k}\xi(\bm{x}_{i})\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{2\bm{e}_{k}}+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q}).

Together with
equation

9
, we obtain

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\xi}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}=2\partial_{k}\xi(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q}).

ii)

Similarly, for
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert=2
, we express the multi-index by

\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{l}

for
k
,
l\in\set{1,\dots,d}
and the sum is non-zero only for
\bm{\beta}=0
and
k=l
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\xi}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\xi(\bm{x}_{i})\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{l}}+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+1})

\displaystyle=2\delta_{kl}\xi(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+1}).

iii)

For
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\geq 3
, the consistency conditions
\Delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}+\bm{{\beta}}}|_{i}=0
for
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert+\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert\leq p
directly lead to

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\xi}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}+\bm{{\beta}}}=O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert-1}).

∎

Theorem

2.2
states that the derived gradient operator, given by the coefficients
\bm{d}_{ij}^{\nabla}=\frac{c_{ij}^{\Delta}}{2}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, fulfills

\nabla u(\bm{x}_{i})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}\bm{d}_{ij}^{\nabla}u_{j}+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q})

(17)

for functions
u\in C^{q}(\Omega)
. Moreover, we obtain an interpolation operator with
d_{ij}^{1}=\frac{c_{ij}^{\Delta}}{2}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{2\bm{e}_{k}}
for
k\in\set{1,\dots,d}
. Note that all derived operators with
\bm{\alpha}\neq\bm{0}
have vanishing diagonal entries
d_{ii}=0
.

2.4
Voronoi-based finite volume method

We demonstrated in our previous work that the Voronoi-based finite volume method is a GFDM. This formulation automatically satisfies properties such as the discrete Gauss theorem and diagonal dominance
[
14
]
. To obtain the Voronoi-based finite volume method, we define the Voronoi cell surrounding a point
\bm{x}_{i}

\Omega_{i}=\set{\bm{x}\in\Omega\mid\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i}\rVert<\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{j}\rVert\text{ for all }j\neq i}.

(18)

In the finite volume method, a function
u
is approximated by cell averages

u(\bm{x}_{i})\approx\frac{1}{\lvert\Omega_{i}\rvert}\int_{\Omega_{i}}u(\bm{x}){\,\mskip 0.0mu{}{\mathrm{d}V}\mskip 0.0mu}.

(19)

This is a second-order approximation only at the centroid of the Voronoi cell
\Omega_{i}
and not at
\bm{x}_{i}
. To discretize the Laplace operator, we split the boundary of
\Omega_{i}
into line segments (in 2D) or surfaces (in 3D)
\Gamma_{ij}=\overline{\Omega}_{i}\cap\overline{\Omega}_{j}
that lie between the cells corresponding to points
\bm{x}_{i}
and
\bm{x}_{j}
. Applying the cell average ansatz (
19
) on
\Delta u(\bm{x}_{i})
, Gauss’s theorem and quadrature rules yield the discrete Laplace operator

\Delta u(\bm{x}_{i})\approx\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{1}{\lvert\Omega_{i}\rvert}\int_{\Gamma_{ij}}{\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bm{n}}}{\,\mskip 0.0mu{}{\mathrm{d}S}\mskip 0.0mu}\approx\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{\lvert\Gamma_{ij}\rvert}{\lvert\Omega_{i}\rvert}\frac{u_{j}-u_{i}}{\lVert\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i}\rVert}.

In the above equation, we used a central finite difference discretization to approximate the directional derivative at the mid-point
\bm{x}_{ij}=\frac{\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{x}_{j}}{2}
between
\bm{x}_{i}
and
\bm{x}_{j}
. With the coefficients
f_{ij}^{\Delta}=\frac{\lvert\Gamma_{ij}\rvert}{\lvert\Omega_{i}\rvert\lVert\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i}\rVert}
for
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
, we obtain the GFDM formulation as in
equation

4

\Delta u(\bm{x}_{i})\approx\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}f_{ij}^{\Delta}(u_{j}-u_{i}).

(20)

Following similar steps, we establish a discrete diffusion operator

\Delta_{\lambda}u(\bm{x}_{i})\approx\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\lambda_{ij}f_{ij}^{\Delta}(u_{j}-u_{i})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}f_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}(u_{j}-u_{i})

(21)

where approximations
\lambda_{ij}\approx\lambda(\bm{x}_{ij})
are added in front of the coefficients
f_{ij}^{\Delta}
.

3
Derived discrete diffusion operator

The new discrete diffusion operator is inspired by the Voronoi-based finite volume method, generalizing it to arbitrary neighborhoods that are not based on a mesh. Trask et al.
[
31
]
presented a similar idea that requires a globally computed graph connecting the point cloud. Seifarth
[
32
]
also successfully applied ideas from classical finite volume methods to generalized finite difference methods to solve transport equations on static point clouds. Kwan-Yu et al.
[
33
]
used ideas from mesh-based methods to construct conservative differential operators. Their discrete differential operators, however, are computed globally, while we compute our differential operators locally.

Recall that the discrete diffusion operator obtained from the finite volume method, given by the coefficients
f_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}=\lambda_{ij}f_{ij}^{\Delta}
, is basically a scaled Laplace operator where the coefficients
f_{ij}^{\Delta}
stem from geometric properties of the Voronoi cell. But instead of
f_{ij}^{\Delta}
, we use the coefficients
c_{ij}^{\Delta}
computed by the MLS approach in
equation

7
to define the new coefficients

\displaystyle c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{ij}c_{ij}^{\Delta},\;j\in\mathcal{N}_{i},

(22a)

\displaystyle c_{ii}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\lambda_{ij}c_{ij}^{\Delta},

(22b)

leading to the derived diffusion operator (DDO)

\Delta_{\lambda}u(\bm{x}_{i})\approx\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}(u_{j}-u_{i})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}u_{j}.

(23)

In the above
\lambda_{ij}
are, similar to the finite volume approach, reconstructions of the diffusivity
\lambda
at the mid-point
\bm{x}_{ij}=\frac{\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{x}_{j}}{2}
. The derived diffusion operator also generalizes the finite volume method, reinterpreting the coefficients
c_{ij}^{\Delta}
with an underlying, hidden notion of meshless volumes and surfaces. Note that the discrete diffusion operator in
equation

22
does not need an explicit calculation of
\nabla\lambda
as opposed to the MLS-based approach.

3.1
Reconstruction functions

First, let us investigate the reconstructions
\lambda_{ij}
. For each point
\bm{x}_{i}\in\Omega_{h}
, we use a reconstruction function
\lambda_{i\to}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
to define the reconstructions with
\lambda_{ij}=\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{j})
.
Lemma

3.1
presents a constraint on the reconstruction function to reconstruct values at the mid-point
\bm{x}_{ij}
, assuming sufficient regularity of the diffusivity
\lambda
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\lambda\in C^{q}(\Omega)
and the reconstruction function
\lambda_{i\to}
fulfill

\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})=\frac{\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})}{2^{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert}}

for each
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq q,
then

\lambda_{ij}=\lambda\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{x}_{j}}{2}\right)+O(h^{q+1}).

Proof.

We use Taylor’s formula to obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{ij}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{j})=\sum_{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq q}\frac{\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})}{\bm{\alpha}!}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}+O(h^{q+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq q}\frac{\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})}{\bm{\alpha}!}\frac{(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}}{2^{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert}}+O(h^{q+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq q}\frac{\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})}{\bm{\alpha}!}\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i}}{2}\right)^{\bm{\alpha}}+O(h^{q+1})

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{x}_{j}}{2}\right)+O(h^{q+1}).

∎

Example 3.2
.

Some examples of first-order reconstruction functions that fulfill the properties from
lemma

3.1
are the three Pythagorean means

\displaystyle\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda(\bm{x})}{2},

(24a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=2\frac{\lambda_{i}\lambda(\bm{x})}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda(\bm{x})},

(24b)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}\lambda(\bm{x})},

(24c)

which are the arithmetic, harmonic, and geometric mean, respectively. Reconstruction functions based on Pythagorean means provide symmetric reconstructions
\lambda_{ij}=\lambda_{ji}
and are first-order accurate.

Numerically computing the gradient
\nabla_{i}\lambda
and using a Taylor expansion

\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x})=\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla_{i}\lambda,\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i}\rangle,

(25a)

or a skew Taylor expansion

\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x})=\lambda(\bm{x})-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla_{i}\lambda,\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i}\rangle

(25b)

yields first-order accurate reconstruction functions. Empirically, these lead to worse results than the Pythagorean means, possibly due to the asymmetry of the reconstructions
\lambda_{ij}\neq\lambda_{ji}
, while requiring additional computational steps.

A higher-order scheme follows from a one-dimensional Hermite interpolation

\lambda_{ij}=\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}{2}+\frac{1}{8}\langle\nabla_{i}\lambda-\nabla_{j}\lambda,\bm{d}_{ij}\rangle

(26)

with the directional vector
\bm{d}_{ij}=\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i}
. In this case, the reconstructions are symmetric and do not require more computation time than the Taylor series-based first-order reconstructions in
equation

25
.

3.2
Consistency conditions

Restricting ourselves to reconstruction functions that fulfill the property from
lemma

3.1
, we derive the consistency conditions for the derived discrete diffusion operator.

Theorem

3.3
shows that the discrete diffusion operator meets the consistency conditions from
equation

11
approximately, as opposed to the exact reproducibility property of the discrete Laplacian.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\lambda\in C^{q}(\Omega)
and the reconstruction functions
\lambda_{i\to}
fulfill the reconstruction property in
lemma

3.1
, then

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{0},\\
\partial_{k}\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k},\\
2\delta_{kl}\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+1}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{l},\\
O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert-1}),&\text{else},\end{cases}

holds for all
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
.

Proof.

We apply
theorem

2.2
with the reconstruction function
\lambda_{i\to}
instead of
\xi
and the cases of
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert=0
and
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\geq 3
follow immediately. For
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\in\set{1,2}
we obtain the consistency conditions

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}=\begin{cases}2\partial_{k}\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k},\\
2\delta_{kl}\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+1}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{l},\\
\end{cases}

and applying
lemma

3.1
yields

\displaystyle 2\partial_{k}\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})

\displaystyle=\partial_{k}\lambda(\bm{x}_{i}),

\displaystyle 2\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})

\displaystyle=2\lambda(\bm{x}_{i}),

concluding the proof.
∎

Theorem

3.3
allows us to derive the accuracy of the discrete diffusion operator. As stated in
theorem

3.4
, the new discrete diffusion operator inherits the accuracy of the underlying discrete Laplace operator with an additional error term that depends on the accuracy of the reconstruction function.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
u\in C^{p+2}(\Omega)
,
\lambda\in C^{q}(\Omega)
, and the reconstruction function
\lambda_{i\to}
fulfill the reconstruction property in
lemma

3.1
, then

\Delta_{\lambda}u(\bm{x}_{i})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}u_{j}+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q}).

Proof.

To prove
theorem

3.4
, we use a Taylor expansion of
u
and the results from
theorem

3.3
. We write

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}u_{j}=\sum_{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq{p+1}}\frac{\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}u(\bm{x}_{i})}{\bm{\alpha}!}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}O(h^{p+2}).

With
c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}=O(h^{-2})
and separating the first sum into derivatives of order zero, one, two, and higher order, this is rewritten as

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}u_{j}

\displaystyle=u(\bm{x}_{i})\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\partial_{k}u(\bm{x}_{i})\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{e}_{k}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum_{k,l=1}^{d}\frac{\partial_{kl}^{2}u(\bm{x}_{i})}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{l}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum_{3\leq\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq p+1}\frac{\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}u(\bm{x}_{i})}{\bm{\alpha}!}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}+O(h^{p}).

With
theorem

3.3
and
equation

10
, this sum simplifies to

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}u_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\partial_{k}u(\bm{x}_{i})\partial_{k}\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})+\sum_{k,l=1}^{d}\delta_{kl}\partial_{kl}^{2}u(\bm{x}_{i})\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q})

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla u(\bm{x}_{i}),\nabla\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})\rangle+\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})\Delta u(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q})

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\lambda}u(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q}).

∎

Theorems

3.3
and
3.4
show that it is sufficient to satisfy monomial consistency conditions approximately to obtain an accurate discrete diffusion operator. When applied to monomials of degree 1, the diffusion operator naturally leads to a gradient approximation of
\lambda
. Especially for problems with discontinuous diffusivities, this leads to a
“natural”
notion of a gradient.

3.3
Diagonal dominance

As we pointed out before, diagonal dominance is especially important for elliptic problems to satisfy the maximum principle discretely. The following corollary formulates sufficient conditions for diagonally dominant derived diffusion operators.

Corollary 3.5
.

The derived diffusion operator in
equation

22
for point
\bm{x}_{i}
is diagonally dominant if
c_{ij}^{\Delta}\geq 0
and
\lambda_{ij}>0
for each neighbor
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
.

The Pythagorean means (
24
) fulfill the positivity condition
\lambda_{ij}>0
if
\lambda>0
. In contrast, the Taylor-based reconstructions in
equation

25
and gradient reconstruction in
equation

26
may violate positivity and require a correction of the discrete gradients
\nabla_{i}\lambda
or the usage of limiters.

3.4
Enrichment

A popular ansatz for discretizing diffusion operators is an enrichment where the idea is to solve a system similar to
equation

7
with more test functions to obtain a more stable operator. Yoon and Song
[
19
]
and Kim et al.
[
20
]
include a weakly discontinuous wedge function in the differential operator calculation, and Suchde and Kuhnert
[
21
]
use scaled monomials of the form
u(\bm{x})=(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}/\lambda(\bm{x})
. Applying the diffusion operator to
u=f/\lambda
yields
\Delta_{\lambda}u=\Delta f-\langle\nabla f,\nabla\log\lambda\rangle-f\Delta\log\lambda,

from which it is straightforward to derive new enriched consistency conditions.
Theorem

3.6
states that these consistency conditions hold numerically.

Theorem 3.6
.

If
\lambda\in C^{q}(\Omega)
and the reconstruction functions
\lambda_{i\to}
fulfill the reconstruction property in
lemma

3.1
, then for each
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}\frac{(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j})^{\bm{\alpha}}}{\lambda(\bm{x}_{j})}=\begin{cases}-\Delta\log\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{0},\\
-\partial_{k}\log\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k},\\
2\delta_{kl}+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+1}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{l},\\
O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert-1}),&\text{else},\end{cases}

holds.

Proof.

First, let us consider the case of
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\geq 1
. In this case, we can rewrite the operator as

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}\frac{(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}}{\lambda_{j}}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\lambda_{j}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}.

Reinterpreting the prefactor
\lambda_{ij}/\lambda_{j}
as evaluations of a new
“reconstruction function”

\kappa_{i\to}(\bm{x})=\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x})/\lambda(\bm{x})
and applying
theorem

2.2
directly yields

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}\frac{(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}}{\lambda_{j}}=\begin{cases}2\partial_{k}\kappa_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k},\\
2\delta_{kl}\kappa_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+1}),&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{l},\\
O(h^{p})+O(h^{q+\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert-1}),&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Obviously
\kappa_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})=1
and the partial derivative with respect to the
k
-th coordinate reads

\partial_{k}\kappa_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})=\frac{\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})\partial_{k}\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})-\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})\partial_{k}\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})}{\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})^{2}}\\
=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}\log\lambda(\bm{x}_{i}).

Now we consider
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{0}
. To start with, we reformulate the first consistency condition of the discrete Laplace operator (
9
)

0=\frac{\lambda_{ii}}{\lambda_{i}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\frac{\lambda_{ii}}{\lambda_{i}}+c_{ii}^{\Delta}\frac{\lambda_{ii}}{\lambda_{i}}.

(27)

We rewrite the derived diffusion operator as above and subtract
equation

27
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\left(\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\lambda_{j}}-\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\lambda_{i}}\right)-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\frac{\lambda_{ii}}{\lambda_{i}}-c_{ii}^{\Delta}\frac{\lambda_{ii}}{\lambda_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\left(\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\lambda_{j}}-\frac{\lambda_{ii}}{\lambda_{i}}\right)-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\lambda_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\lambda_{j}}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\lambda_{i}}.

Because
c_{ij}^{\Delta}
depict a discrete Laplace operator of order
p
, this reduces to

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}=\Delta\frac{\lambda_{i\to}}{\lambda}\biggr\rvert_{\bm{x}=\bm{x}_{i}}-\frac{\Delta\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})}{\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})}+O(h^{p}).

We use the identity

\Delta\frac{\lambda_{i\to}}{\lambda}=\frac{\Delta\lambda_{i\to}-2\langle\nabla\lambda_{i\to},\nabla\log\lambda\rangle-\lambda_{i\to}\Delta\log\lambda+\lambda_{i\to}\lVert\nabla\log\lambda\rVert^{2}}{\lambda}

and apply
lemma

3.1
to
\lambda_{i\to}
and its derivatives to obtain the consistency condition

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}=-\Delta\log\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})+O(h^{p}).

∎

Following a similar idea and applying our new operator to scaled monomials of the form

q_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{x})=\frac{(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}}{\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x})},

(28)

we derive further consistency conditions.

Theorem 3.7
.

For each
1\leq\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq p

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}\frac{(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}}{\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{j})}=\begin{cases}2\delta_{kl},&\text{if }\bm{\alpha}=\bm{e}_{k}+\bm{e}_{l},\\
0,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Furthermore, if
\lambda\in C^{2}(\Omega)
and the reconstruction functions
\lambda_{i\to}
fulfill the reconstruction property in
lemma

3.1
then

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{j})}=-\frac{\Delta\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})}{4\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})}+O(h^{p}).

Proof.

Multi-indices with
1\leq\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq p
lead to the same consistency conditions that are used for the discrete Laplace operator in
equation

9

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}q_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_{j})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\lambda_{ij}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\frac{(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}}{\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{j})}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}.

For
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert=0
and thus
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{0}
we obtain

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{j})}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\lambda_{i}}\right)=-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\lambda_{i}}=-\frac{\Delta\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})}{\lambda(\bm{x}_{i})}+O(h^{p}).

Applying
lemma

3.1
to
\Delta\lambda_{i\to}(\bm{x}_{i})
concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem

3.7
illustrates that the discrete diffusion operator, applied to test functions as in
equation

28
, behaves like the discrete Laplace operator. The only exception is the case of
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{0}
which is similar to the respective case in
theorem

3.6
.

Remark 3.8
.

We observed that enforcing all of these enriched consistency conditions, along with the consistency conditions from
theorem

3.3
, in an MLS optimization as in
equation

7
leads to a discrete diffusion operator that does not perform as well as the derived diffusion operator.

3.5
An alternative view

We can also obtain the new discrete diffusion operator by representing the diffusion operator in terms of other, well-known, operators and discretizing them instead. For example, the representation

\Delta_{\lambda}u=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta(\lambda u)+\lambda\Delta u-u\Delta\lambda\right)

leads to

\displaystyle\Delta_{\lambda}u(\bm{x}_{i})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\lambda_{j}u_{j}+\lambda_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}u_{j}-u_{i}\Delta_{i}\lambda\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}{2}c_{ij}^{\Delta}u_{j}-\frac{u_{i}\Delta_{i}\lambda}{2}.

By reordering the sums and using the fact that
c_{ii}^{\Delta}=-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}
, we rewrite the diagonal entry

\displaystyle c_{ii}^{\Delta_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}c_{ii}^{\Delta}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda_{i}c_{ii}^{\Delta}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c_{ij}^{\Delta}\lambda_{j}\right)=-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}{2}c_{ij}^{\Delta},

leading to the derived diffusion operator with arithmetic averaging. Similarly, reformulating the diffusion operator as in
equation

10
or as
\Delta_{\lambda}u=\Delta(\lambda u)-\langle\nabla\lambda,\nabla u\rangle-u\Delta\lambda
and approximating the gradient
\nabla u
with the derived gradient operator in
equation

17
leads to the Taylor reconstructions in
equation

25
.

5
Final remarks

In this paper, we derived a new discretization method of the diffusion operator based on weighting the discrete Laplace operator by using reconstruction functions. We proved that the discrete diffusion operator preserves the consistency order from the discrete Laplace operator with additional error terms that arise from the reconstruction, and investigated diagonal dominance and enrichment properties. Other features such as conservation or the extension to anisotropic diffusion coefficients are relevant topics that might be featured in future research.

We tested the derived diffusion operator and showed that even first-order reconstructions conserve the fourth-order accuracy of the discrete Laplace operator for problems with smooth diffusivity. For interface problems, we demonstrated the applicability of the new discrete diffusion operator, and numerically showed first-order accuracy for such problems. The results were on par with the Voronoi-based finite volume method.
