Learning Latent Part-Whole Hierarchies for Point Clouds
Xiang Gao, Wei Hu, and Renjie Liao

Abstract
Strong evidence suggests that humans perceive the 3D world by parsing visual scenes and objects into part-whole hierarchies. Although deep neural networks have the capability of learning powerful multi-level representations, they can not explicitly model part-whole hierarchies, which limits their expressiveness and interpretability in processing 3D vision data such as point clouds. To this end, we propose an encoder-decoder style latent variable model that explicitly learns the part-whole hierarchies for the multi-level point cloud segmentation. Specifically, the encoder takes a point cloud as input and predicts the per-point latent subpart distribution at the middle level. The decoder takes the latent variable and the feature from the encoder as an input and predicts the per-point part distribution at the top level. During training, only annotated part labels at the top level are provided, thus making the whole framework weakly supervised. We explore two kinds of approximated inference algorithms, i.e . , most-probable-latent and Monte Carlo methods, and three stochastic gradient estimations for learning discrete latent variables, i.e . , straight-through, REINFORCE, and pathwise estimators. Experimental results on the PartNet dataset show that the proposed method achieves state-of-the-art performance in not only top-level part segmentation but also middle-level latent subpart segmentation.

However, few attempts have been made for 3D point clouds—a set of points irregularly sampled from continuous surfaces that represent 3D objects or scenes.
With the maturity of techniques like the depth sensing, the laser scanning and the image processing, point clouds become widely available in applications including autonomous driving, robotics, and immersive telepresence.

Explicitly modeling of such part-whole hierarchies for point clouds is challenging for the following reasons.
First, it is extremely expensive to annotate semantic labels of individual points within part-whole hierarchies, thus making the powerful supervised learning paradigm impractical.
The more parts a 3D object has or the deeper a part-whole hierarchy is, the more annotation efforts are required.
Second, due to the fact that point clouds are a set of irregularly sampled points, one needs to build perception models that are permutation invariant and 3D-geometry aware.
This introduces more constraints in modeling compared to 2D images.
Third, the more parts at different levels, the more expressive a part-whole hierarchy is and the more complicated the inference and learning algorithms become.
It is thus challenging to design efficient inference and learning that can achieve the best trade-off between expressiveness and tractability.

In this paper, we propose a discrete latent variable model that learns part-whole hierarchies for point clouds in a weakly-supervised manner.
Given a set of point clouds, we assume some annotated labels at some higher levels of the part-whole hierarchy are given,
e.g
.
, the class label of the whole set of point clouds or the part labels at the highest possible level.
We treat all part or sub-part labels of individual points at remaining lower levels of the part-whole hierarchy as categorical latent variables.
For example, for point cloud segmentation problems in our experiments, we consider two levels,
i.e
.
, annotated part labels are given in the top level whereas sub-part labels in the bottom level are latent.
We consider these two levels since current public datasets only provide annotations for these two levels so that we can quantitatively evaluate our latent variable models.
Nevertheless, as discussed in Section

We design an encoder that takes raw point clouds as input and predicts the conditional probability distribution of these latent variables.
We then draw samples (
i.e

, latent sub-part labels in the bottom level) from the conditional distribution and feed them to a decoder that predicts the top-level part labels of the input point cloud.
As shown later, our modeling technique is general in that the latent variable module can be easily plugged into popular deep learning models to learn meaningful latent random variables in other domains/tasks.
Since the state space of all latent variables is huge, approximated Bayesian inference methods are needed.
Even worse, our latent variables are discrete, thus disabling the popular differentiable reparameterization tricks (

The whole framework falls under the weakly-supervised learning paradigm as we have supervised annotations only at higher levels but not lower levels.
To summarize, our main contributions are as follows.

•

•

Experiments on weakly-supervised multi-level point cloud segmentation datasets demonstrate that the proposed method not only achieves better top-level performances but also outperforms existing methods in learning part-whole hierarchies.

III-A

Probabilistic Model for Classification

We first introduce the common probabilistic model (without latent variables) for classification and then move on to the latent variable model.
In classical supervised learning of probabilistic classifiers, the typical learning objective is to minimize the following cross-entropy,

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\quad\mathbb{E}_{q_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})}\left[H(q_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}),p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}))\right]

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\quad\mathbb{E}_{q_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})}\left[-\mathbb{E}_{q_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\right],

(1)

where
H(q,p)
is the cross-entropy between two distributions
q
and
p
.

\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
denote the input data and its label.

p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
is the classifier that needs to be learned and
\theta
is the set of model parameters.

q_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})
is the unknown data distribution.

q_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
is the ground truth label distribution conditioned on data.
The joint distribution
q_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})q_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})
specifies the generation process of observed data and labels.
Since we can only access data distribution via drawing i.i.d. samples and
q_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
is a Dirac delta measure, the objective in Eq. (
III-A
) is further approximated by the Monte Carlo (MC) estimation in practice,

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\quad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}_{i}|\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{i}),

(2)

where
\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim q_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})
is the
i
-th sampled training data and
\mathbf{y}_{i}
is the corresponding ground truth class label.
For better readability, we will drop the sample index from now on.
When
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
is parameterized by a deep neural network, the above probabilistic classifier achieves impressive results in many domains.
However, it fails to capture complex or conceptual properties of the underlying data.
For example, in our context, a probabilistic classifier for segmenting 3D objects can not identify parts that are shared among them.

III-B

Latent Variable Model for Part-Whole Hierarchies

To quantitatively model those unobservable factors that are important for both solving the downstream tasks (
e.g
.
, segmenting the whole 3D object) and describing the underlying data (
e.g
.
, segmenting the 3D object parts), we build a latent variable model.
In particular, we are given an input point cloud of a 3D object as
\mathbf{x}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{m}\}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a 3-dimension vector that specifies the 3D coordinates of the
i
-th point.

m
is the total number of points sampled from the underlying 3D object.
For 3D point cloud segmentation, given any point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, we denote its semantic class label as a categorical random variable
\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\{1,2,\cdots,K\}
.
To model part-whole hierarchies, we introduce latent variables
\mathbf{z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{z}_{m}\}
to represent the object part labels of individual points.
Here
\mathbf{z}_{i}\in\{1,2,\cdots,C\}
is again a categorical random variable, indicating the part label of the
i
-th point.
Since the part label is latent, we do not know its semantic meaning during training.
But after training, we can evaluate the accuracy of the learned part labels by grounding the latent variables to annotated part labels via bipartite matching.
More details about this matching step is explained in Section
VI-A3
.
The total number of parts
C
is a hyperparameter.
Note that we only consider a two-level part-whole hierarchy due to the simplicity and the availability of annotated datasets.
We discuss how to generalize our method to more levels in Section
VII-A
.

Since we introduce the latent variable, the conditional distribution of the classifier is decomposed as,

p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{z}}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x}),

(3)

where the set of model parameters includes both
\theta
and
\phi
.
We use these two separate notations for better explaining the learning algorithm in Section
V
.
To ease the discussion, we call
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
as the
encoder
which encodes the input point cloud
\mathbf{x}
as latent codes (
i.e
.
, part labels)
\mathbf{z}
in a probabilistic way.
For the tractability, we assume the conditional independence for part labels of individual points given the input,

p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}_{i}|\mathbf{x}).

(4)

Similarly, we call
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})
as the
decoder
which decodes the semantic class label of individual points from the latent code
\mathbf{z}
and the input
\mathbf{x}
.
For instance, if the sample
\mathbf{x}
is a point cloud of a chair, we wish to encode the coordinates of individual points of
\mathbf{x}
as the probabilities of being latent parts like “surface” or “frame”.
Then from the latent codes and the original coordinates, we hope to decode the semantic label
\mathbf{y}
as “seat”, “back”, or “leg”.
The overall model is illustrated in Fig.
2
.
This problem is an instance of the weakly supervised learning since we only have annotated segmentation labels
\mathbf{y}
at the top level during training.
We do not have any supervision on part label
\mathbf{z}
in the bottom level.
The ground-truth annotations of part labels are only used for the final evaluation.

III-C

Encoder

We now introduce the specific design of our encoder which constructs the probability of the latent variable
\mathbf{z}_{i}
given the input point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
.
As demonstrated in Fig.
2
, we first learn the feature representations
\mathbf{h}_{e,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{F_{e}}
with
F_{e}
channels of the input point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
using a stack of linear layers, each followed by a batch normalization (BN)
[
63
]
and a ReLU activation
[
64
]
.
The subscript
e
emphasizes that these notations correspond to the encoder.
Based on these representations, we propose a message passing layer to propagate information among points so that the predicted latent part label of the current point depends on its local neighboring points, as illustrated in Fig.
3
.
In particular, we treat each point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
in the point cloud as a node and build a k-NN graph.
We denote
\mathcal{N}(i)
as the set of
K
nearest neighbors of node
i
.
Each point is also associated with a normal vector
\mathbf{n}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which is estimated via plan fitting as discussed in detail in Section
VI-A1
.
We then perform the smoothing operation as

\mathbf{h}_{e,i}^{{}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{W}\left(\tilde{a}_{i,i}\mathbf{h}_{e,i}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)\setminus i}\tilde{a}_{i,j}\mathbf{h}_{e,j}\right),

(5)

where
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{F_{e}\times F_{e}}
is the learnable parameters, the normalized edge weight between node
i
and node
j
is denoted as
\tilde{a}_{i,j}=a_{i,j}/\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}(i)}a_{i,k}
where we use the absolute cosine similarity between normal vectors to compute the unnormalized edge weight,
i.e
.
,
a_{i,j}=|\cos(\mathbf{n}_{i},\mathbf{n}_{j})|
.
Specifically, we first collect the feature vectors
\mathbf{h}_{j}
from the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(i)
and then perform a weighted sum based on their normal-vector-based similarities to the center node
i
.
Having obtained the smoothed feature representation
\mathbf{h}_{e,i}^{{}^{\prime}}
, we employ one linear layer followed by a softmax to output the probability distribution
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
After estimating the probability distribution
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i})
, we perform sampling to acquire
L
categorical (one-hot encoded) samples
\{\mathbf{z}_{i}^{(l)}|\mathbf{z}_{i}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{C},l=1,\cdots,L\}
.
We use the superscript
(l)
to denote the sample index.
As shown in Fig.
2
, we treat the
L
samples and the feature representations
\mathbf{h}_{e,i}
as the input of the decoder to further estimate the label
\mathbf{y}_{i}
for any point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
.

An illustration of the smoothing module in the encoder.
We first construct the
K
-nearest-neighbor graph based on the input features, then use majority voting to obtain the voted neighborhood set, and finally employ a message passing layer to smooth the features.

III-D

Decoder

At the decoder
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})
, we first employ
C
linear layers to map the representation
\mathbf{h}_{e,i}
into
C
separate representations in parallel which correspond to individual latent part classes.
We then concatenate these
C
representations to obtain the feature representation matrix
\mathbf{H}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{F_{d}\times C}
and compute the point representation
\mathbf{h}_{d,i}=\mathbf{H}_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}
that is conditioned on the latent variable
\mathbf{z}_{i}
.
Since
\mathbf{z}_{i}
is a one-hot encoded categorical random variable, the conditioning is equivalent to selecting the corresponding column of
\mathbf{H}_{i}
based on the latent part label.
It is clear that the representation
\mathbf{h}_{d,i}
depends on the input point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and latent part label
\mathbf{z}_{i}
.

We then adopt several linear layers followed by a BN and a ReLU activation except that the last linear layer is followed by the softmax to estimate the probability distribution of label
\mathbf{y}_{i}
according to Eq. (
3
).

\begin{split}p(\mathbf{y}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i})&\approx\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(l)}|\mathbf{z}_{i}^{(l)},\mathbf{x}_{i})=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(l)}|\mathbf{h}_{d,i}^{(l)}).\end{split}

(6)

We use
L
samples to approximate the true classifier.

IV
Inference Algorithm

In this section, we introduce the explored inference algorithms.
In the inference, given the input 3D point cloud
\mathbf{x}
, we would like to infer its latent lower-level part labels,
i.e
.
, computing
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
, and infer its higher-level class label,
i.e
.
, computing
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
.
Note that inferring the latent part labels is straightforward since we only need to run the forward pass of the encoder to obtain the probability
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
and then take the
argmax
of the probability as the most probable latent part label.
However, the inference for the class label is tricky since it requires the marginalization over latent variable
\mathbf{z}
.
The marginalization is only tractable in low-dimension space.
In our case,
\mathbf{z}
lies in the product space
\{1,2,\cdots,C\}^{m}
where
m
is the number of points in the point cloud, thus making the inference intractable.
There exists a vast literature on approximated inference for probabilistic graphical models
[
65
]
.
For example, one can introduce a variational approximated distribution and perform variational inference by maximizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO).
However, this requires introducing either an iterative optimization procedure (standard variational inference
[
66
,
65
]
) or a learned neural network based approximated variational distribution (the so-called amortized inference
[
14
,
67
]
).
Instead of the variational inference, we resort to the following two simple yet effective approximated inference algorithms.

IV-A

Most-Probable-Latent Inference

Given model parameters
\theta
and
\phi
, we could compute the conditional distribution of class labels via the following approximated inference.
We first perform the
\argmax
operation on the encoder distribution
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
to acquire the latent variable
\mathbf{z}^{\ast}
that indicates the most probable part label in the lower level.
This is why we name it as
most-probable-latent inference
(MPL).
Then, we compute the probability as,

\begin{split}p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})&\approx p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{\ast},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}^{\ast}|\mathbf{x})\\
\quad\mathbf{z}^{\ast}&=\argmax_{\mathbf{z}}p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x}).\end{split}

(7)

We can predict the class label by taking the
\argmax
of the
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
at the higher level.
It is easy to see that this algorithm provides a lower bound of the true conditional probability,

\begin{split}p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})&=\sum_{\mathbf{z}}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})\\
&\geq p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{\ast},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}^{\ast}|\mathbf{x}).\end{split}

(8)

This approximated inference algorithm is deterministic and only requires one forward pass for the encoder and the decoder respectively, thus being very efficient.

IV-B

Monte Carlo Inference

We can also approximately infer the label
\mathbf{y}
based on the Monte Carlo estimation.
In particular, we have

\begin{split}p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})&=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}\left[p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})\right]\approx\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{(l)},\mathbf{x}),\\
\mathbf{z}^{(l)}&\sim p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x}).\end{split}

(9)

In practice, we first compute the distribution
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
via one forward pass of the encoder.
Then, we sample
L
part labels from it, compute the approximated
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
by running
L
forward passes of the decoder, and finally take the
\argmax
.
The Monte Carlo estimator in Eq. (
9
) is unbiased.
Also, the law of large numbers guarantees that it is consistent.

V
Learning Algorithm

In this section, we delve into the details of learning algorithms.
To learn a probabilistic classifier, one typically maximizes the log likelihood of the target label
\mathbf{y}
conditioned on the observed data
\mathbf{x}
as shown in Eq. (
2
).
In our case, based on Eq. (
2
) and Eq. (
3
), the resultant training objective is

\displaystyle\min_{\theta,\phi}\quad\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)\triangleq-\log\left(\sum_{\mathbf{z}}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})\right).

(10)

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is often employed to learn the entire model end-to-end, which requires the gradient of the objective function in Eq. (
10
) w.r.t.
parameters
\theta
and
\phi
.
In the following, we introduce the gradient estimation methods under the previously proposed approximated inference methods,
i.e
.
, Eq. (
7
) or Eq. (
9
), respectively.

V-A

Gradients for Most-Probable-Latent Inference

In particular, the objective function in this case is,

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)&\approx-\log\left(p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{\ast},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}^{\ast}|\mathbf{x})\right)\\
\mathbf{z}^{\ast}&=\argmax_{\mathbf{z}}p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x}).\end{split}

(11)

Let us first assume that
\mathbf{z}^{\ast}
is differentiable w.r.t.

\phi
.
Then we have the following gradients,

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\theta}&=-\frac{\partial\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{\ast},\mathbf{x})}{\partial\theta}\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}&=-\frac{\partial\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{\ast},\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{z}^{\ast}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}^{\ast}}{\partial\phi}-\frac{\partial\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}^{\ast}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\phi}.\end{split}

(12)

If the previous assumption holds, then we can use Eq. (
12
) to compute the SGD update.
However,
\mathbf{z}
in our case is a discrete (or categorical) random variable, thus breaking the assumption that
\mathbf{z}^{\ast}
is differentiable w.r.t.

\phi
.

V-A
1
Straight-Through Estimator

To overcome the above challenge, we resort to the straight-through estimator (STE)
[
15
]
.
STE is a simple and widely used technique for estimating gradients of discrete random variables.
The core idea is to relax the discrete random variable to the continuous domain and ignore the non-differentiable transformation function when computing the gradient.
In the most-probable-latent inference, given the output probability distribution of the encoder network
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})=\bm{\pi}=[\bm{\pi}_{1},...,\bm{\pi}_{C}]
(typically obtained by applying the softmax), the latent part label
\mathbf{z}^{\ast}
is computed as
\mathbf{z}^{\ast}=\argmax_{i}\bm{\pi}_{i}
.
STE just approximates the gradient by ignoring the
\argmax
in the backward pass,

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}&=-\frac{\partial\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{\ast},\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{z}^{\ast}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}^{\ast}}{\partial\phi}-\frac{\partial\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}^{\ast}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\phi}\\
&\approx-\frac{\partial\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{\ast},\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{z}^{\ast}}\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}_{\mathbf{z}^{\ast}}}{\partial\phi}-\frac{\partial\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}^{\ast}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\phi},\end{split}

(13)

where
\bm{\pi}_{\mathbf{z}^{\ast}}=\max_{i}\bm{\pi}_{i}
.
Albeit being simple, there are no theoretical guarantees for STE on,
e.g
.
, consistency and unbiasedness.
But it still serves as a popular baseline method due to its good empirical performances on some tasks.

V-B

Gradients for Monte Carlo Inference

For Monte Carlo inference in Eq. (
9
), we can derive the gradient w.r.t.
decoder parameters
\theta
as follows,

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\theta}&=-\frac{\partial\log\sum_{\mathbf{z}}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\theta}\\
&=-\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\mathbf{z}}\frac{\partial p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})}{\partial\theta}p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})\\
&=-\frac{1}{Z}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}\left[\frac{\partial p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})}{\partial\theta}\right],\end{split}

(14)

where
Z=\sum_{\mathbf{z}}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
.
Since the decoder
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})
will be constructed using a differentiable neural network, the gradient term within the expectation is available.
Therefore, one can estimate the gradient w.r.t.
decoder parameters
\theta
as,

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\theta}\approx-\frac{1}{\hat{Z}L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{\partial p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{(l)},\mathbf{x})}{\partial\theta},

(15)

where
\hat{Z}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{(l)},\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\sim p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
.
Now let us turn to estimating the gradients w.r.t. the encoder parameters
\phi
,

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}&=-\frac{\partial\log\sum_{\mathbf{z}}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\phi}\\
&=-\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\mathbf{z}}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\phi}.\end{split}

(16)

Since we use a differentiable neural network to construct the encoder, the gradient
{\partial p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}/{\partial\phi}
is available.
However, comparing Eq. (
14
) to Eq. (
16
), we can find that there does not exist an expectation term for the encoder, which prevents us from using Monte Carlo estimation as in Eq. (
15
).
To address this issue, we explore the REINFORCE and pathwise gradient estimators.

V-B
1
REINFORCE Gradient Estimator

REINFORCE estimator
[
16
]
,
a.k.a
.
the score function estimator
[
68
]
or the likelihood ratio method
[
69
]
, is a widespread and general-purpose method to deal with the aforementioned estimation problem.
It leverages a simple log-derivative trick and applies to both discrete and continuous random variables.

In our context, when the probability mass function
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
is differentiable w.r.t. encoder parameters
\phi
, the log-derivative trick is,

\frac{\partial p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\phi}=p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\phi}.

(17)

The gradients of the encoder in Eq. (
16
) thus become,

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}&=-\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\mathbf{z}}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\phi}\\
&=-\frac{1}{Z}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\phi(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}}\left[\frac{\partial\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\phi}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})\right].\end{split}

(18)

Now we have the gradient rewritten in the form of an expectation, which enables the usage of Monte Carlo estimation.
Note that REINFORCE estimator in our context has a close relationship with reinforcement learning (RL) as below,

-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\phi(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}}\left[\underbrace{\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})}{Z}}_{\text{Reward}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\log\underbrace{p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}_{\text{Policy}}\right].

(19)

The discrete part label
\mathbf{z}
can be treated as an action, the encoder
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
thus corresponds to the policy network, and the term involving decoder
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})/Z
corresponds to the reward function.
Our goal is to learn the policy parameters
\phi
to maximize our expected reward.

We can now construct a Monte Carlo estimator as below,

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}\approx-\frac{1}{\hat{Z}L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{\partial\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\phi}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{(l)},\mathbf{x}),

(20)

where
\hat{Z}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{(l)},\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\sim p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
.

Variance Reduction
The REINFORCE estimator is unbiased but may come with high variances.
One can further reduce the variance via the control variate method
[
70
]
,
a.k.a
.
baseline in RL.
In particular, if we introduce another random variable
B
(
i.e
.
, the so called
control variate
) which does not depend on
\mathbf{z}
, we have the following identity,

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\phi(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}}\left[\frac{\partial\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\phi}B\right]=0.

(21)

Relying on the above observation, we can derive an alternative REINFORCE estimator:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}\approx\frac{1}{\hat{Z}L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left[\frac{\partial\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}|\mathbf{x})}{\partial\phi}\left(p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{(l)},\mathbf{x})-B\right)\right].

(22)

In practice, we found setting
B=1
works well.

V-B
2
Pathwise Gradient Estimator

The pathwise gradient estimator
[
71
]
,
a.k.a
.
reparameterization trick
[
14
]
, leverages the change-of-varaible technique to rewrite the target random variable as a function of a base random variable which is typically drawn from a simple distribution.
In our context, since
\mathbf{z}
is a discrete (or categorical) random variable indicating the part label, one can reparameterize the sample path using the following Gumbel-max trick
[
72
]
.

\begin{split}\mathbf{z}&=g_{\phi}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\epsilon})=\argmax\left(\log(\bm{\pi})+\bm{\epsilon})\right)\\
\bm{\epsilon}[i]&\sim\mathrm{Gumbel}(0,1)\qquad\forall i=1,\cdots,C,\end{split}

(23)

where
\bm{\pi}=p_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})=[\bm{\pi}_{1},...,\bm{\pi}_{C}]
is again the probability output by the encoder via the softmax, thus depending on
\mathbf{x}
and
\phi
in a differentiable way.
The base distribution of
\bm{\epsilon}=[\bm{\epsilon}_{1},...,\bm{\epsilon}_{C}]
is the standard Gumbel distribution.

\bm{\epsilon}[i]
denotes the
i
-th element of the vector
\bm{\epsilon}
.
However, the corresponding function
g_{\phi}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\epsilon})
is not differentiable w.r.t.

\phi
due to the argmax operator.
To facilitate gradient-based learning, the Gumbel-softmax trick
[
17
,
18
]
instead approximates the argmax operator using a softmax operator which preserves the differentiability.
Specifically, with the one-hot encoding of
\mathbf{z}
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbf{z}[i]&\approx g_{\phi}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\epsilon})[i]=\frac{\exp\left\{(\log(\bm{\pi}[i])+\bm{\epsilon}[i])/\tau\right\}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C}\exp\left\{(\log(\bm{\pi}[j])+\bm{\epsilon}[j])/\tau\right\}},\end{split}

(24)

where the base distribution is again the standard Gumbel distribution,
i.e
.
,
\forall i
,
\bm{\epsilon}[i]\sim\mathrm{Gumbel}(0,1)
, and
\tau
is the temperature parameter.
We perform the ablation study on the temperature in Section
VI-B2
.

\mathbf{z}[i]=1
indicates that the part label is
i
.
In Gumbel-max trick,
\mathbf{z}
corresponds to the vertices of the probability simplex, thus taking discrete values from a finite set.
In Gumbel-softmax trick,
\mathbf{z}
is relaxed to a continuous domain,
i.e
.
, the whole probability simplex.

Based on the Gumbel-softmax reparameterization in Eq. (
24
), we can estimate the gradient under Monte Carlo inference.
Specifically, we have

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}&\approx-\frac{\partial\log\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{(l)},\mathbf{x})\right)}{\partial\phi}\\
&=-\frac{1}{L\hat{Z}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{\partial p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{(l)},\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{z}^{(l)}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}^{(l)}}{\partial\phi},\end{split}

(25)

where
\hat{Z}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}^{(l)},\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{z}^{(l)}
is drawn via the Gumbel-softmax distribution.
Note that the pathwise gradient estimator is biased due to the fact the Gumbel-softmax trick uses softmax to approximate the argmax operator.
But the variance of the estimator is often found to be low.

Failure Case Analysis

We show several failure cases of our models in Fig.
5
.

1.

Over-smoothed bottom-up representations.
We notice that in Fig.
5(a)
, the hands and dial of the clock at the middle level are not properly segmented. This is partly due to the fact that normal vectors of all points are very similar. Our models may learn over-smoothed representations at the middle level even though the different parts are distinguishable.

2.

Lack of top-down guidance.
As illustrated in Fig.
5(b)
, we see that at the middle level, our model sometimes misclassifies the boards at the top and bottom of the shelf, though they exhibit similar structures while belonging to different categories. Similarly, in Fig.
5(c)
, we see that our models only segment the table into two or three sub-parts despite that there are are way more sub-parts annotated in the ground truth. We argue that such cases are too difficult to distinguish solely based on bottom-up information as these sub-parts are structurally very similar. More top-down guidance or prior information is required to inform the model during learning.

VII
Discussion and Conclusion

VII-A

Discussion

Generalization to more levels.

Our proposed latent variable model can be generalized to multiple levels in a straightforward manner.
From Eq. (
3
), we can further decompose the conditional distribution of the latent variable as,

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}_{T}}p_{\theta_{T}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{T},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi_{T}}(\mathbf{z}_{T}|\mathbf{x}),

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad p_{\phi_{T}}(\mathbf{z}_{T}|\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}_{T-1}}p_{\theta_{T-1}}(\mathbf{z}_{T}|\mathbf{z}_{T-1},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi_{T-1}}(\mathbf{z}_{T-1}|\mathbf{x}),

\displaystyle p_{\phi_{2}}(\mathbf{z}_{2}|\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}_{1}}p_{\theta_{1}}(\mathbf{z}_{2}|\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{x})p_{\phi_{1}}(\mathbf{z}_{1}|\mathbf{x}),

(26)

where the conditional distribution of the classifier
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
is decomposed into
T+1
levels: the latent class label at each level is encoded by the latent variable
\mathbf{z}_{1},...,\mathbf{z}_{T}
.
Eq. (
VII-A
) assumes the interaction between adjacent two levels.
More complicated interaction among different levels could be explored in the future.

Limitations.

Since the proposed model is weakly supervised, the representation learning of the unsupervised middle level may be strongly influenced by the supervised top level in the absence of prior information.
Hence, the segmentation results of the middle level may tend to be similar to ones at the top level.
Also, our proposed model requires Monte Carlo sampling during training, which can be inefficient when the number of levels is large.

VII-B

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a novel latent variable model for learning part-whole hierarchies on 3D point clouds in a weakly-supervised way.
We design an encoder-decoder framework that explicitly captures part-whole hierarchies of point clouds.
We explore two simple yet effective approximated inference algorithms and three gradient estimation methods for learning discrete latent variables.
Experimental results show that our models achieve the state-of-the-art performance on weakly-supervised multi-level point cloud segmentation.
In the future, we plan to generalize our model to more levels, and apply to more applications such as weakly supervised point cloud classification.
