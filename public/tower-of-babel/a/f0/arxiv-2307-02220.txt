Spherical Basis Functions in Hardy Spaces with Localization Constraints
C. Gerhards, X. Huang

Abstract




Abstract.

Subspaces obtained by the orthogonal projection of locally supported square-integrable vector fields onto the Hardy spaces
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
, respectively, play a role in various inverse potential field problems since they characterize the uniquely recoverable components of the underlying sources. Here, we consider approximation in these subspaces by a particular set of spherical basis functions. Error bounds are provided along with further considerations on norm-minimizing vector fields that satisfy the underlying localization constraint. The new aspect here is that the used spherical basis functions are themselves members of the subspaces under consideration.

1
Introduction

It is well-known that the space of square-integrable vector fields on the sphere can be decomposed as follows:

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})=H_{+}(\mathbb{S})\oplus H_{-}(\mathbb{S})\oplus D(\mathbb{S}),

(1.1)

where
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
denote the Hardy spaces of inner and outer harmonic gradients, respectively, and
D(\mathbb{S})
the space of tangential divergence-free vector fields (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
8
]
, with roots in terms of vector spherical harmonic representations going back to
[
5
]
). Applications to inverse magnetization problems and the separation of magnetic fields into contributions of internal and external origin are various and can be found, e.g., in
[
2
,
14
,
15
,
23
,
25
,
27
,
30
]
.
Concerning the inverse magnetization problem, knowing the magnetic field in the exterior of a magnetized sphere, only the
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
-component of the underlying magnetization can be reconstructed uniquely. However, if it is known a priori that the magnetization is locally supported in some proper subdomain of the sphere, also the
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
-component is determined uniquely (which may help for the reconstruction of certain properties of the magnetization, e.g., the direction of an underlying inducing field or the susceptibility in the case of a known direction of the inducing field, e.g.,
[
11
,
12
,
21
,
22
,
31
]
). Therefore, it is of interest to have a suitable setup that allows the computation of the
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
-component based on knowledge of the
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
-component under the assumption that the underlying vector field is locally supported. A first step is to investigate appropriate approximation methods in
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
, respectively, under such localization constraints.

The latter is the main motivation and content of the paper at hand. More precisely, let
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
be the space of square-integrable vector fields that are locally supported in a subdomain
\Sigma^{c}\subset\mathbb{S}
, and let
P_{+}(L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3}))
and
P_{-}(L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3}))
be the subspaces obtained by orthogonal projection onto the corresponding Hardy space
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
, respectively. Here, we will investigate approximation in these projected subspaces. The specific vectorial Slepian functions from, e.g.,
[
26
,
27
]
can address this to a certain extent. However, their construction requires joint optimization of the
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
-,
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
-, and
D(\mathbb{S})
-contributions and often focuses on a spectral representation. Furthermore, the computations have to be redone for every new subdomain of the sphere. Spherical basis functions, on the other hand, have the advantage that only their centers need to be adapted if the subdomain under consideration changes. Additionally, the construction as presented here allows for a separate treatment of the
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
- and
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
-contributions, respectively.

The course of this paper will be as follows: It is known from
[
13
]
that the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
onto the Hardy space
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
produces the subspace
P_{+}(L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3}))=B_{+}(D_{+,\Sigma})
, where
B_{+}
denotes a particular vectorial linear operator that is expressible via layer potentials (cf. Section
3.1
for details and notations) and
D_{+,\Sigma}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
denotes the scalar function space

\displaystyle D_{\Sigma,+}=L^{2}(\Sigma^{c})+(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)V_{\Sigma}

(1.2)

(cf. Section
3.2
for details and notations; for now, we let
K
denote the double layer potential and
V_{\Sigma}
the space of functions whose single layer potential is harmonic in
\Sigma
). Thus, since the operator
B_{+}
is bounded and invertible, the problem of finding adequate vectorial spherical basis functions for the approximation of the
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
-component of locally supported vector fields reduces to finding adequate (scalar) spherical basis functions for approximation in the subspace
D_{+,\Sigma}
. The latter is, in fact, what we focus on in this paper (a similar statement holds true for the
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
-component and a related subspace
D_{-,\Sigma}
). The structure of
D_{+,\Sigma}
according to (
1.2
) indicates that it suffices to investigate approximation in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c})
and approximation in
V_{\Sigma}
. The former is discussed in Section
4.1
, the latter in Section
4.2
. An approximation result for locally supported spherical basis functions similar to the one in Section
4.1
has already been obtained, e.g., in
[
19
]
. However, in their case, the support of the used spherical basis functions does not necessarily lie within
\Sigma^{c}
, while we want to enforce this support condition in order to guarantee that the functions used for approximation are members of
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c})
themselves. In a Euclidean setup, the latter has been achieved in
[
28
]
. We simply transfer their result to the sphere by applying the spherical techniques used, e.g., in
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
and the stereographic projection. For the study of approximation in
V_{\Sigma}
we rely on regularized fundamental solutions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator, as described, e.g., in
[
9
,
7
,
10
]
. These spherical basis functions have the advantage that they are locally harmonic and naturally lead to a function system that is contained in
V_{\Sigma}
itself. Both results are then joined in Section
4.3
to obtain the desired approximation property in
D_{+,\Sigma}
, which is stated in Theorem
4.7
. As indicated before, the new aspect here is not the approximation property in general but that the investigated spherical basis functions lead to vectorial functions that are themselves members of the projected subspace
P_{+}(L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3}))
of
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
. Sections
3.3
and
4.4
additionally provide some considerations on locally supported vector fields of minimum norm and their representation in the given context. Sections
2
and
3
gather the required notations and background material, some numerical illustration is provided in Section
5
.

2
General preliminaries

We define some necessary notations required throughout the course of this paper and gather some known results on spherical basis function interpolation, cubature on the sphere, and the fundamental solution for the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the sphere.

2.1
Sobolev spaces on the sphere

Let
\mathbb{S}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x|=1\}
denote the unit sphere. We restrict ourselves to the three-dimensional case to simplify some considerations, especially in Sections
2.4
and
4.2
, although they should also hold in higher dimensional setups. Let
\{Y_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N},k=-n,\ldots,n}
denote an orthonormal set of real spherical harmonics of order
n
and degree
k
(each spherical harmonic of degree
n
and order
k
is the restriction of a homogeneous, harmonic polynomial of degree
n
to the sphere; details can be found, e.g., in
[
10
]
). For an infinitely often differentiable function
f:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
of class
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S})
, one can write
f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\hat{f}_{n,k}Y_{n,k}
with Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}_{n,k}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}f(y)Y_{n,k}(y)d\omega(y)
, where
\omega
denotes the surface measure on the sphere. When dealing with such Fourier coefficients, we often say that we are arguing in spectral domain. The Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
of smoothness
s\in\mathbb{R}
is defined as the closure of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S})
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}
induced by the inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2s}\hat{f}_{n,k}\,\hat{g}_{n,k},

(2.1)

i.e.,
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})=\overline{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S})}^{\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}}
. In particular, it holds that
H^{0}(\mathbb{S})=L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
, the space of square-integrable functions, and that
C^{0}(\mathbb{S})\subset H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
for
s>1
, where
C^{0}(\mathbb{S})
denotes the set of continuous functions on the sphere. By
\|\cdot\|_{C^{0}(\mathbb{S})}
we denote the supremum norm. At some occasions, we use the notation
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle
to indicate the space of functions
f
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
with vanishing mean
\langle f,1\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}=0
.

We denote by
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}
the (surface) gradient on the sphere. For
f
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{S})
, its gradient
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}f
is well-defined and lies in the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})
of square-integrable vector fields, which is equipped with the inner product

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\mathbf{f}(y)\cdot\mathbf{g}(y)\,d\omega(y)

(2.2)

and the corresponding norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}
. Typically, we denote vector fields
\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
by bold-face letters. The (surface) divergence operator
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\cdot
is defined as the negative adjoint
-\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}^{*}
(with respect to the
L^{2}
-inner product) and we define the Laplace-Beltrami operator via
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}=\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}=-\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}^{*}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}
.

If
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}
is a Lipschitz domain with connected boundary, then the space of square-integrable locally supported vector fields on
\Sigma
is denoted by
L^{2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R}^{3})
, naturally embedded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})
via extension by zero. Furthermore, we define
H^{s}(\Sigma)
as the closure of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Sigma)=\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}):\textnormal{supp}(f)\subset\Sigma\}
with respect to the
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}
-norm. Thus,
H^{s}(\Sigma)
is naturally embedded in
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
.

Remark 2.1
.

For
f
in
H^{2}(\mathbb{S})
it holds that
(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}f)^{\wedge}_{n,k}=-n(n+1)\hat{f}_{n,k}
and, consequently, that
((-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}+\frac{1}{4})f)^{\wedge}_{n,k}=\big(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}\hat{f}_{n,k}
. Thus, one can (formally) see from (
2.1
) that a function
f
is in
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
if and only if
(-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}+\frac{1}{4})^{\frac{s}{2}}f
is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
.

A function
f:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
\mathbf{p}
-zonal (for some fixed
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{S}
) if there exists a function
F:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(x)=F(x\cdot\mathbf{p})
for all
x\in\mathbb{S}
. In that case, we define
\mathcal{R}_{x}f(y)=f(z)
, for
z\in\mathbb{S}
chosen such that
x\cdot y=z\cdot\mathbf{p}
. This allows the definition of the convolution
f*g
of two functions
f
and
g
via

f\ast g(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\mathcal{R}_{x}f(y)\,g(y)d\omega(y),\quad x\in\mathbb{S}.

(2.3)

For
F:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, the notation
F*g
is meant in the sense
F\ast g(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}F(x\cdot y)\,g(y)d\omega(y)
, naturally relating to the definition (
2.3
).

Proposition 2.2
.

If
f
is a
\mathbf{p}
-zonal function in
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
and
g
is in
H^{k}(\mathbb{S})
, for some
s,k\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\|f\ast g\|_{H^{s+k+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\mathbb{S})}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\|g\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{S})},

(2.4)

where
C>0
is a constant depending on
s
and
k
.

A proof of the proposition above is provided in Appendix
A.1
. Furthermore, the following estimate will be helpful.

Proposition 2.3
.

[
4
, Theorem 24]

Let
s>1
. Then
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
is a Banach algebra, i.e., there exists a constant
C>0
, depending on
s
, such that for any
f,g
in
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
, the following inequality holds

\|fg\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\|g\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}.

(2.5)

To conclude this section, we want to mention that occasionally it is convenient to rely on known results in the Euclidean setup by using the stereographic projection. Given a fixed pole
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{S}
, let
P_{stereo}:\mathbb{S}\setminus\{\mathbf{p}\}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the stereographic projection as defined, e.g., in
[
8
, Def. 2.34]
.

Typically we do not indicate the pole explicitly in the notation of the stereographic projection. If it should be required for clarity, we write
P_{stereo,\mathbf{p}}
instead of
P_{stereo}
. The stereographic projection of a set
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}\setminus\{\mathbf{p}\}
is given by
P_{stereo}(\Sigma)=\{P_{stereo}(x):x\in\Sigma\}
. Furthermore, the stereographic projection of a function
f:\mathbb{S}\setminus\{\mathbf{p}\}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined via

\displaystyle P_{stereo}f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad y\mapsto f(P_{stereo}^{-1}(y)).

(2.6)

The following are some useful estimates involving the stereographic projection.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{S}
be fixed and
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}
be a Lipschitz domain with connected boundary whose closure satisfies
\overline{\Sigma}\subset\mathbb{S}\setminus\{\mathbf{p}\}
. Then there exist constants
C>c>0
such that, for any
x,y\in\Sigma
,

\displaystyle c|x-y|

\displaystyle\leq|P_{stereo}(x)-P_{stereo}(y)|\leq C|x-y|,

(2.7)

\displaystyle c\,\omega(\Sigma)

\displaystyle\leq\mu(P_{stereo}(\Sigma))\leq C\,\omega(\Sigma),

(2.8)

where
\mu
denotes the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\omega
the surface measure on the sphere
\mathbb{S}
.

Let
s\geq 0
, then there exist constants
C\geq c>0
(possibly different from above), depending on
s
, such that for any
f\in H^{s}(\Sigma)
the following inequalities hold true:

\displaystyle c\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\leq\|P_{stereo}f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}.

(2.9)

Throughout the course of this article, we allow all appearing constants to depend on the subdomain
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}
without explicitly mentioning this. In cases where the constant does not depend on the subdomain, this should become clear from the context.

2.2
Cubature rules on the sphere

Given a set of points
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{S}
and weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
, then the corresponding cubature rule
Q_{X}
takes the form

Q_{X}f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}f(x_{i}).

(2.10)

We say that
Q_{X}
has polynomial precision of degree
L
if
Q_{X}f=\int_{\mathbb{S}}f(y)d\omega(y)
for all spherical polynomials of degree at most
L
, i.e., for all functions of the form
f=\sum_{n=0}^{L}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\hat{f}_{n,k}Y_{n,k}
. If all weights are positive, i.e., if
w_{i}>0
for all
i=1,,\ldots,N
, then we call
Q_{X}
a cubature rule with positive weights. The spatial distribution of the points in
X
is paramount for the existence of cubature rules with a prescribed polynomial precision and positive weights. An important parameter that provides information on the spatial distribution is the mesh width of
X
relative to some subset
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}
, which is defined by

\displaystyle h_{X,\Sigma}=\sup_{y\in\Sigma}\min_{x_{i}\in X\cap\Sigma}\|y-x_{i}\|.

(2.11)

If
\Sigma=\mathbb{S}
, we typically abbreviate
h_{X,\Sigma}
simply by
h_{X}
.
The separation distance is defined by

\displaystyle q_{X,\Sigma}=\frac{1}{2}\min_{x_{i},x_{j}\in X\cap\Sigma\atop x_{i}\not=x_{j}}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|,

(2.12)

again, we drop the index
\Sigma
if
\Sigma=\mathbb{S}
.

Theorem 2.5
.

(Existence of cubature rules
[
16
, Theorem 6]
)
There exists a constant
C>0
such that following holds true: For every pointset
X\subset\mathbb{S}
and for every
L\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying

h_{X}\leq\frac{C}{L},

(2.13)

there exist positive weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}>0
such that the corresponding cubature rule
Q_{X}
has polynomial precision of degree
L
.

The existence of cubature rules with polynomial precision of degree
L
and positive weights then guarantees the following error estimate for our later considerations in Section
4.2
.

Theorem 2.6
.

(Error estimate for cubature rules
[
16
, Theorem 11]
)
Let
s>1
and
X\subset\mathbb{S}
. Then there exist a constant
C>0
that depends on
s
such that, for every
L\in\mathbb{N}
and for every cubature rule
Q_{X}
with polynomial precision of degree
L
and positive weights, the worst-case error in
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
can be estimated by

\sup_{f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{S}),\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\leq 1}\left|Q_{X}f-\int_{\mathbb{S}}f(y)d\omega(y)\right|\leq\frac{C}{L^{s}}.

(2.14)

2.3
Spherical basis functions and interpolation

In this section, we briefly recapitulate some definitions and results for spherical basis functions derived from classical radial basis functions, as indicated, e.g., in
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\psi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be compactly supported with
\textnormal{supp}(\psi)\subset[0,1]
such that
\psi(|\cdot|):\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
is a positive definite radial basis function. Then we define
\Psi:\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
via
\Psi(x,y)=\psi(|x-y|)
and introduce scaled versions, for
\delta>0
, via

\displaystyle\Psi_{\delta}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{\delta},\frac{y}{\delta}\right),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{S}.

(2.15)

We call these restrictions to the sphere
spherical basis functions
. Often, we use the notation
\Psi_{x}
and
\Psi_{\delta,x}
to more concisely express the functions
\Psi(x,\cdot)
and
\Psi_{\delta}(x,\cdot)
, respectively.

Further, observing that
\Psi(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\hat{\Psi}_{n}\,Y_{n,k}(x)Y_{n,k}(y)
for adequate coefficients
\hat{\Psi}_{n}\in[0,\infty)
, we define a norm
\|\cdot\|_{\Psi}
via

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\Psi}^{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\frac{\hat{f}_{n,k}^{2}}{\hat{\Psi}_{n}},\qquad f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}).

(2.16)

The native space corresponding to
\Psi
is then given as the closure of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S})
with respect to this norm
\|\cdot\|_{\Psi}
. Analogous definitions hold for
\Psi_{\delta}
, where the involved coefficients are denoted by
\left(\Psi_{\delta}\right)^{\wedge}_{n}
.

A popular choice for the underlying
\psi
in Definition
2.7
that we will use in our numerical examples is, e.g., the Wendland function (cf.
[
32
,
33
]
)

\displaystyle\psi(r)

\displaystyle=(1-r)_{+}^{4}(4r+1),

(2.17)

which corresponds to the native space
H^{\frac{5}{2}}(\mathbb{S})
, although various other choices are equally valid. A closed-form representation of its Legendre coefficients is provided in
[
17
]
.

Proposition 2.8
.

[
17
, Thm. 4.7]
Let
\Psi_{\delta}
be given as in Definition
2.7
, and the underlying
\psi
be given as in (
2.17
). Then,
\Psi_{\delta}
has the following spherical harmonic expansion

\displaystyle\Psi_{\delta}(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\hat{\Psi}^{\delta}_{n}\,Y_{n,k}(x)Y_{n,k}(y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2n+1}{4\pi}\hat{\Psi}^{\delta}_{n}\,P_{n}(x\cdot y),

(2.18)

with coefficients

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}^{\delta}_{n}=\frac{\pi}{7}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\left(-n,n+1,\frac{5}{2};4,\frac{9}{2};\frac{\delta^{2}}{4}\right),

(2.19)

where
{}_{3}F_{2}
denotes the general hypergeometric function.

Next, we state some known properties for interpolation via spherical basis functions.

Proposition 2.9
.

[
18
, Theorem 3.3]
Let
X\subset\mathbb{S}
have meshwidth
h_{X}
, and let
s>1
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
, depending on
s
, such that, for all
f
in
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
with
f|_{X}=0
, it holds

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\leq Ch_{X}^{s}\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}.

(2.20)

We do not actually need the spherical estimate from Proposition
2.9
in the later course of this paper, it is stated mainly for illustration purposes. For the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we will infact use the stereogrpahic projection in order to retreat to the Euclidean setup and rely on related results from
[
24
,
28
]
.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
\Psi
be as in Definition
2.7
with native space
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
, for some
s>1
. Moreover, let
X\subset\mathbb{S}
and denote by
I_{X,\Psi}:H^{s}(\mathbb{S})\to\textnormal{span}\{\Psi_{x}:x\in X\}
the interpolation operator that is characterized by
I_{X,\Psi}(f)|_{X}=f|_{X}
. Then, it holds

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\Psi}^{2}=\|f-I_{X,\Psi}(f)\|_{\Psi}^{2}+\|I_{X,\Psi}(f)\|_{\Psi}^{2}.

Some useful results on the spectral behaviour of the scaled spherical basis functions and on the boundedness of the corresponding interpolation operator are the following.

Proposition 2.11
.

[
20
, Theorem 6.2]

Let
\Psi
be as in Definition
2.7
where
\psi(|\cdot|)
is a radial basis funtion in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with native space
H^{s+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for some
s>1
, then
\Psi
is a spherical basis function with native space
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
. Moreover, there exist constants
C\geq c>0
, depending on
s
, such that

c(1+\delta n)^{-2s}\leq\left({\Psi}_{\delta}\right)^{\wedge}_{n}\leq C(1+\delta n)^{-2s}.

(2.21)

As a result, there exists constants
\bar{C}\geq\bar{c}>0
, depending on
s
, such that, for any
f
in
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
,

\bar{c}\|f\|_{\Psi_{\delta}}\leq\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\leq\bar{C}\delta^{-s}\|f\|_{\Psi_{\delta}}.

(2.22)

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
\Psi
satisfies the condition in Proposition
2.11
. Given
X\subset\mathbb{S}
, let
c>1
be such that
h_{X}<cq_{X}
, and let the scaling factor
\delta
satisfy
\nu q_{X}<\delta<c\nu q_{X}
for some
\nu\geq 1
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
, depending on
c
,
\nu
, and
s
, such that it holds for any
f
in
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
that

\|I_{X,\Psi_{\delta}}(f)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S})}.

(2.23)

Proposition
2.12
is a simplified spherical version of
[
28
, Theorem 3.5]
. A proof can be found the Appendix
A.2
.

2.4
Regularized fundamental solution

The function
G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};\cdot,\cdot):\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}:x\not=y\}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};x,y)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln(1-x\cdot y)+\frac{1}{4\pi}(1-\ln 2).

(2.24)

is called fundamental solution with respect to
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}
and is twice continuously differentiable on its domain. It satisfies

\Delta_{\mathbb{S},y}G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};x,y)=-\frac{1}{4\pi},\quad x\not=y,

(2.25)

and it has the Fourier expansion, for
x\not=y
,

\displaystyle G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};x,y)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\frac{1}{-n(n+1)}Y_{n,k}(x)Y_{n,k}(y)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2n+1}{-n(n+1)}P_{n}(x\cdot y),

(2.26)

where
P_{n}
denotes the Legendre polynomial of degree
n
(see, e.g.,
[
10
]
). Furthermore, Green’s third formula yields for
f
in
H^{3+\kappa}(\mathbb{S})
, with
\kappa>0
, that

\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}}f(y)d\omega(y)+\int_{\mathbb{S}}G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};x,y)\Delta_{\mathbb{S},y}f(y)d\omega(y),\quad x\in\mathbb{S}.

(2.27)

From (
2.26
), we can easily check, for fixed
x\in\mathbb{S}
, that
G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};x,\cdot)
lies in the Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
for any
s<1
but not for
s\geq 1
. Regularization of the fundamental solution around its singularity leads to higher smoothness and is considered here for later use. A once continuously differentiable regularization will suffice for our purposes and a particular choice is presented in Definition
2.13
, although various other choices are possible as well. The concept is described and applied in more detail, e.g., in
[
9
,
7
,
8
,
10
]
.

Definition 2.13
.

For fixed
\rho\in(0,2)
, we define the once continuously differentiable regularized fundamental solution
G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
via

\displaystyle G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln(1-x\cdot y)+\frac{1}{4\pi}(1-\ln 2),&x\cdot y\leq 1-\rho,\\
\frac{1-x\cdot y}{4\pi\rho}+\frac{1}{4\pi}(\ln\rho-\ln 2),&x\cdot y>1-\rho.\end{cases}

(2.28)

A later on useful notation will be the following, with
x,\bar{x}\in\mathbb{S}
fixed,

\displaystyle G^{\rho}_{x,\bar{x}}=G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};x,\cdot)-G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};\bar{x},\cdot).

(2.29)

Remark 2.14
.

It is directly obvious that
G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}:x,\cdot)
and
G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}:x,\cdot)
only differ on the spherical cap

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rho}(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{S}:x\cdot y>1-\rho\}

(2.30)

with center
x
and polar radius
\rho\in(0,2)
. Thus,
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho}_{x,\bar{x}}
is locally supported in
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}(x)\cup\mathcal{C}_{\rho}(\bar{x})
. One should note that the spherical basis functions in Section
2.3
as well the mesh width
h_{X}
in Section
2.2
are defined with respect to the Euclidean distance, i.e., they relate to the spherical ball

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{r}(x)\cap\mathbb{S}=\{y\in\mathbb{S}:|x-y|<r\}

(2.31)

with center
x
and (Euclidean) radius
r>0
. The distinction between these two notions of spherical caps and spherical balls is solely made to pay tribute to the origins of the two different setups, not because they are conceptually different. In order for
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}(x)
and
\mathcal{B}_{r}(x)\cap\mathbb{S}
to cover the same area, one needs to choose the radii to be related via
r=(2\rho)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

As already mentioned, the regularization (
2.28
) is only one of many possible choices. It has been chosen solely so that we can rely on some already known explict computations that lead to the following properties.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let the regularized fundamental solution
G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};\cdot,\cdot)
be given as in Definition
2.13
.

1.

It holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\|G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}:x,\cdot)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\sim\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\|\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}:x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\sim\rho^{-\tfrac{1}{2}},

(2.32)

where the notation
f_{\rho}\sim g_{\rho}
is meant in the sense that there exist constants
C\geq c>0
such that
c\,g_{\rho}\leq f_{\rho}\leq C\,g_{\rho}
for all sufficiently small
\rho>0
.

2.

For
f
of class
C^{0}(\mathbb{S})
, it holds that (cf.
[
8
, Remark 8.19]
)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{S}}\left(G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}:x,y)-G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};x,y)\right)f(y)d\omega(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\rho\ln(\rho)\|f\|_{C^{0}(\mathbb{S})},

(2.33)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{S}}\left(\nabla_{\mathbb{S},x}G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}:x,y)-\nabla_{\mathbb{S},x}G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};x,y)\right)f(y)d\omega(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{C^{0}(\mathbb{S})},

(2.34)

with some constant
C>0
.

3.

G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};\cdot,\cdot)
has the following spherical harmonic expansion (cf.
[
10
, Lemma 4.7]
)

\displaystyle G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\hat{G}^{\rho}_{n}\,Y_{n,k}(x)Y_{n,k}(y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2n+1}{4\pi}\hat{G}^{\rho}_{n}\,P_{n}(x\cdot y),

(2.35)

with coefficients

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{\rho}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\rho,

(2.36)

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{\rho}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\rho-\frac{1}{24}\rho^{2},

(2.37)

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{\rho}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{n+1}(1-\rho)-P_{n-1}(1-\rho)}{2n(n+1)(2n+1)}-\frac{2-\rho}{2n(n+1)}P_{n}(1-\rho)

(2.38)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2\rho}\frac{P_{n}(1-\rho)-P_{n+2}(1-\rho)}{(2n+1)(2n+3)}-\frac{1}{2\rho}\frac{P_{n-2}(1-\rho)-P_{n}(1-\rho)}{(2n+1)(2n-1)},\quad n\geq 2.

3
Preliminaries on Hardy spaces and localization constraints

In order to state the desired relations between
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
under localization constraints, we very briefly recapitulate classical layer potentials and the definition of Hardy spaces. Subsequently, we state the subspaces of
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
in which we want to perform approximation. This forms the foundation for most considerations in the later course of this paper.

3.1
Layer potentials and Hardy spaces

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
we define the (boundary) single layer potential
S\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{S})
via

Sf(x)=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|}\ d\omega(y),\quad x\in\mathbb{S}.

S
is bounded and invertible. Furthermore, we define the operators
K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle
and
K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
, where
I
is the identity operator and
K
is the (boundary) double layer potential

Kf(x)=-\textnormal{p.v.}\,\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}(y)\cdot(x-y)}{|x-y|^{3}}f(y)\ d\omega(y),\quad x\in\mathbb{S},

with
\boldsymbol{\eta}(y)=y
simply denoting the unit normal vector to the sphere at the location
y\in\mathbb{S}
. Both operators
K+\tfrac{1}{2}I
and
K-\tfrac{1}{2}I
are invertible and self-adjoint. The operator
K+\tfrac{1}{2}I
preserves constant functions while the operator
K-\tfrac{1}{2}I
annihilates them. Latter is the reason why we formally define it only on the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle
, although we will not require this particular distinction throughout most parts of the paper. For more information on (boundary) layer potentials we refer the reader, e.g., to
[
6
,
29
]
and references therein. Here, we merely use them to define the operators
B_{+}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
B_{-}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})
given by

\displaystyle B_{+}=\boldsymbol{\eta}\ \big(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)+\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S,

\displaystyle B_{-}=\boldsymbol{\eta}\ \big(K+\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)+\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S.

(3.1)

The Hardy spaces are then defined as the ranges of the corresponding operators
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle H_{+}(\mathbb{S})=B_{+}(L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle),

\displaystyle H_{-}(\mathbb{S})=B_{-}(L^{2}(\mathbb{S})).

(3.2)

This notation will be useful for the purposes of the paper at hand; in particular, it allows to reduce considerations in the vectorial Hardy spaces to considerations in the scalar
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
space. For the statement that these definitions are equivalent to the more classical definition of Hardy spaces via non-tangential limits of harmonic gradients, we refer the reader to
[
3
]
. There, one can also find the statement of the Hardy-Hodge decomposition

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})=H_{+}(\mathbb{S})\oplus H_{-}(\mathbb{S})\oplus D(\mathbb{S}),

(3.3)

where
D(\mathbb{S})=\{\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3}):\mathbf{f}\textnormal{ is tangential and }(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S)^{*}\mathbf{f}=0\}
denotes the space of tangential, divergence-free vector fields on the sphere. Note again that
B_{+}
can also be considered as acting on the full
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
where it annihilates constant functions. Furthermore, it should be mentioned that the operators
B_{+}
,
B_{-}
closely resemble the operators
\tilde{o}^{(1)}
,
\tilde{o}^{(2)}
in
[
8
,
10
,
11
]
(however, the notation of
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
more naturally allows considerations on Lipschitz surfaces, e.g., in
[
3
]
, while the notation of
\tilde{o}^{(1)}
and
\tilde{o}^{(2)}
is rather specific to the sphere).

Remark 3.1
.

Some basic calculations show that, for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
, it holds
(Sf)^{\wedge}_{n,k}=-\frac{1}{2n+1}\hat{f}_{n,k}
(see, e.g.,
[
8
, Sect. 2.5.2]
). Analogously, it holds
(Kf)^{\wedge}_{n,k}=\frac{1}{4n+2}\hat{f}_{n,k}
. Thus, the application of the single and double layer potential can be easily computed in spectral domain.

3.2
Localization constraints

Definition 3.2
.

For the remainder of this paper, we assume that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}
, with
\overline{\Sigma}\not=\mathbb{S}
, is a Lipschitz domain with connected boundary. Let
\Sigma^{c}=\mathbb{S}\setminus\overline{\Sigma}
be the open complement of
\Sigma
, which again is a Lipschitz domain with connected boundary. By
P_{+}:L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})\to H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
P_{-}:L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})\to H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
we denote the orthogonal projections onto
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
, respectively. For any vector field
\mathbf{f}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we abbreviate its Hardy components by
\mathbf{f}_{+}=P_{+}(\mathbf{f})
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}=P_{-}(\mathbf{f})
. Additionally,
P_{\Sigma}:L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\to L^{2}(\Sigma)
denotes the projection onto
L^{2}(\Sigma)
, and we define the auxiliary space

\displaystyle V_{\Sigma}=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}):\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\cdot(\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf)|_{\Sigma}=0\textnormal{ as distribution on }\Sigma\}

(3.4)

of functions whose single layer potential is harmonic in
\Sigma
.

In order to comply with the notation in
[
13
]
, we will implicitly assume that
\textnormal{supp}(\mathbf{f})\subset\Sigma^{c}
if we say that a vector field is locally supported, i.e., we assume that
\mathbf{f}
is in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
. The upcoming theorem (which is a collection of results from
[
13
]
) states the desired unique relation between the
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
-contribution
\mathbf{f}_{+}
and the
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
-contribution
\mathbf{f}_{-}
of such a locally supported vector field
\mathbf{f}
. In particular, it characterizes the relevant subspaces
P_{+}(L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3}))\subset H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
P_{-}(L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3}))\subset H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
.

Theorem 3.3
.

It holds that
P_{+}(L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3}))=B_{+}(D_{+,\Sigma})
and
P_{-}(L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3}))=B_{-}(D_{-,\Sigma})
, with

\displaystyle D_{+,\Sigma}

\displaystyle=L^{2}(\Sigma^{c})+\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right)V_{\Sigma},

(3.5)

\displaystyle D_{-,\Sigma}

\displaystyle=L^{2}(\Sigma^{c})+\left(K-\frac{1}{2}I\right)V_{\Sigma}.

(3.6)

Both
D_{+,\Sigma}
and
D_{-,\Sigma}
are dense but not closed in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
and, consequently,
B_{+}(D_{+,\Sigma})
and
B_{-}(D_{-,\Sigma})
are dense but not closed in
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
, respectively. Furthermore, we define the linear mappings
\tau_{ptm}:D_{+,\Sigma}\to D_{-,\Sigma}
and
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}:D_{-,\Sigma}\to D_{+,\Sigma}/\langle 1\rangle
by

\displaystyle\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}

\displaystyle=\left[P_{\Sigma}\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right)\right]^{-1}P_{\Sigma}-I

(3.7)

\displaystyle\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}

\displaystyle=-\left[P_{\Sigma}\left(K-\frac{1}{2}I\right)\right]^{-1}P_{\Sigma}-I+\langle\cdot,1\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}.

(3.8)

Both operators are surjective and unbounded; they are inverse to each other in the sense
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}\circ\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}=I|_{D_{-,\Sigma}}
and
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}\circ\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}=I|_{D_{+,\Sigma}/\langle 1\rangle}
. It holds that for
f\in D_{+,\Sigma}
, the function
g=\tau_{ptm}(f)
is the unique function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
such that
B_{+}f+B_{-}g
differs from a locally supported vector field in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
only by a tangential divergence-free vector field from
D(\mathbb{S})
. Vice versa, if
\mathbf{f}
is in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\mathbf{f}_{+}=B_{+}f
with
f\in D_{+,\Sigma}
, then
\mathbf{f}_{-}=B_{-}\tau_{ptm}(f)
.

Remark 3.4
.

Observing that the operators
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
are bounded and invertible, Theorem
3.3
implies that, for the construction of vectorial spherical basis functions in the Hardy subspaces of interest, it suffices to find adequate spherical basis functions in the scalar-valued spaces
D_{+,\Sigma}
and
D_{-,\Sigma}
. Therefore, for the remainder of the paper, in order to simplify notations, we focus on the scalar setup of the spaces
D_{+,\Sigma}
,
D_{-,\Sigma}
, and the operators
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
,
\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}
. The corresponding results for the vectorial setup are obtained immediately by application of the operators
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
.

3.3
Norm minimizing localized vector fields

Remembering that the original motivation to study localization constraints came from the study of non-uniqueness properties for inverse magnetization problems, we may ask the following question: given a magnetic field in the exterior of the sphere
\mathbb{S}
, what is the magnetization of minimal norm that is localized within a given source region and that produces the given magnetic field? In terms of the paper at hand, the corresponding question would be: given the
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
-contribution of some vector field
\mathbf{f}
in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
, what is the vector field
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}
in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
of minimal norm such that
f_{+}=\tilde{f}_{+}
? This question is answered in the next proposition.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
f
be in
D_{+,\Sigma}
and set
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}=B_{+}f+B_{-}\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)+\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{df}
with

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{df}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-B_{+}f-B_{-}\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f),&\textnormal{ on }\Sigma,\\
-\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}h,&\textnormal{ on }\Sigma^{c},\end{array}\right.

and
h\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{c})
such that
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}h=0
on
\Sigma^{c}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}h=\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot(B_{+}f+B_{-}\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f))
on
\partial\Sigma
(the latter two properties are meant in the distributional sense; and
\boldsymbol{\nu}
denotes the unit vector that is tangential to
\mathbb{S}
, normal to the boundary
\partial\Sigma
, and points into the exterior of
\Sigma
, i.e., into
\Sigma^{c}
). Then it holds that
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}
is in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
,
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{df}
is in
H_{df}(\mathbb{S})
, and

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{f}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\min\left\{\|\mathbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}:\mathbf{f}=B_{+}f+\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{d}\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3}),\,\mathbf{g}\in H_{-}(\mathbb{S}),\,\mathbf{d}\in H_{df}(\mathbb{S})\right\}.

(3.11)

Proof.

The statement that
\mathbf{f}_{df}
of the form (
3.5
) exists and is in
H_{df}(\mathbb{S})
has been shown in
[
13
, Lemma A.1]
. By construction it is then clear that
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}
vanishes on
\Sigma
, i.e.,
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}
is in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
. It remains to show (
3.11
). From the previous section it is known that
\mathbf{g}=B_{-}\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)
is necessary in order to enable
B_{+}f+\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{d}
to be in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
, so that only the choice of
\mathbf{d}
needs to be investigated. Due to the orthogonality of
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
,
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
, and
H_{df}(\mathbb{S})
, we know

\displaystyle\|B_{+}f+B_{-}\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)+\mathbf{d}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}=\|B_{+}f+B_{-}\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}+\|\mathbf{d}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}.

(3.12)

Furthermore, any
\mathbf{d}
that guarantees
B_{+}f+B_{-}\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)+\mathbf{d}
to vanish on
\Sigma
must coincide with
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{df}
on
\Sigma
. Thus, we obtain
\mathbf{d}=\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{df}+\tilde{\mathbf{d}}
, for some
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}\in H_{df}(\mathbb{S})
with
\textnormal{supp}(\tilde{\mathbf{d}})\subset\Sigma^{c}
. We can compute further,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{d}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{df}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}+\|\tilde{\mathbf{d}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}+2\langle\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{df},\tilde{\mathbf{d}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\|\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{df}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}+\|\tilde{\mathbf{d}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}.

The last summand in the first row vanishes due to the local support condition on
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}
and the fact that
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{df}
is expressible as a gradient on
\Sigma^{c}
. The minimization of the norm of
\mathbf{d}
therefore leads to
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}=0
, which concludes the proof.
∎

4
Spherical basis functions related to Hardy spaces

We now consider spherical basis functions contained in and suited for approximation in
D_{+,\Sigma}
and
D_{-,\Sigma}
, respectively. Remembering the characterizations (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), we first deal with approximation in the space
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c})
, then with approximation in
V_{\Sigma}
, and, subsequently, in Section
4.3
we join both considerations to deal with approximation in the actual spaces
D_{+,\Sigma}
and
D_{-,\Sigma}
. From Remark
3.4
we know that this suffices for approximation in the corresponding subspaces of
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
. Again, throughout, we assume that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}
is a Lipschitz domain with connected boundary.

4.1
Approximation in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c})

Simply for notational convenience, we drop the index ”
c
” in this subsection and consider approximation in
L^{2}(\Sigma)
instead of
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c})
. Obviously, all results equally hold true on
\Sigma^{c}
just by taking the complement. We prove the upcoming theorem following step by step the argumentation in the proof of the main result in
[
28
]
for the Euclidean setup, solely adapting it to the spherical setup where necessary. The main framework for the spherical setup has already been described in Section
2.3
, based on, e.g.,
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
and the stereographic projection.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X_{n}\subset\mathbb{S}
be pointsets with associated mesh widths
h_{n}=h_{X_{n},\Sigma}
and separation widths
q_{n}=q_{X_{n},\Sigma}
that satisfy
c_{1}\gamma h_{n}\leq h_{n+1}\leq\gamma h_{n}
and
q_{n}\leq h_{n}\leq c_{2}q_{n}
, for some fixed constants
c_{1},\gamma\in(0,1)
and
c_{2}\geq 1
. Furthermore, let
\Psi
be as in Proposition
2.11
with native space
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
, for some
s>1
, and choose scaling factors
\delta_{n}=\nu h_{n}
, for some fixed
\nu>1
. Eventually, set
\tilde{X}_{n}=\{x\in X_{n}:\mathcal{B}_{\delta_{n}}(x)\cap\mathbb{S}\subset\Sigma\}
. Then, for sufficiently large
\nu
and sufficiently small
\gamma,h_{1}
, the following holds true: For any
f
in
H^{s}(\Sigma)
, there exists a
f_{n}
in
\textnormal{span}\{\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i}}:x_{i}\in\tilde{X}_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,n\}\subset H^{s}(\Sigma)
with

\displaystyle\|f-f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\leq C\eta^{n}\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})},

(4.1)

where the constants
C>0
and
\eta\in(0,1)
depend on
\nu
,
\gamma
,
c_{2}
,
h_{1}
, and
s
.

Before we proceed to the proof, we mention the following proposition that states some technical properties of Lipschitz domains with a tube around the boundary removed.

Proposition 4.2
.

[
28
, Lemma 4.2, Lemma 4.3]

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain that satisfies the interior cone condition with parameters
r
and
\theta
(reflecting the radius and the opening angle of the cone). Let
c>\max\{2,\frac{\pi}{\theta\sin(\pi/8)}\}
be fixed. Then there exist constants
C,\delta_{0}>0
so that, for any
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
, there exists a Lipschitz domain
K_{\delta}\subset\Omega
satisfying

1.

\Omega_{c\delta}\subset K_{\delta}\subset\Omega_{\delta}
, where
\Omega_{\delta}=\{x\in\Omega:\inf_{y\in\partial\Omega}\|x-y\|>\delta\}
,

2.

\mu(\Omega\setminus K_{\delta})\leq C\,c\delta
, where
\mu
denotes the Lebesgue measure,

3.

K_{\delta}
satisfies the interior cone condition with parameters
\tilde{\theta}=\min\{\frac{\pi}{5},\frac{\theta}{2}\}
and
\tilde{r}=\frac{3r\sin\tilde{\theta}}{8(1+1\sin\tilde{\theta})}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.
We choose
f_{n}
to be the function obtained by a multiscale interpolation procedure as indicated, e.g., in
[
20
]
. Namely, set
e_{0}=f
, iterate by setting, for
i=1,\ldots,n
,

\displaystyle s_{i}=I_{\tilde{X}_{i},\Psi_{\delta_{i}}}(e_{i-1}),\qquad e_{i}=e_{i-1}-s_{i},

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
and

be such that condition (

\kappa\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]

be fixed. Then, for

\displaystyle\|f-f_{\rho,L}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S})}\leq C\left(\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}+\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}L^{-(1+\kappa)}\right)\|\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}f\|_{H^{1+\kappa}(\mathbb{S})},

(4.14)

with a constant
C>0
depending on
\kappa

Proof.

We can assume
\langle f,1\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}=0

\textnormal{span}\{1_{\mathbb{S}},G^{\rho}_{x,\bar{x}}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};\cdot):x\in X\}

, and

be a cubature rule with corresponding (not yet specified) weights

. Setting

, we get

and by Green’s third theorem, we have
f=\int_{\mathbb{S}}G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};y,\cdot)F(y)d\omega(y)
. In partiuclar,

is continuous and allows pointwise evaluations. Finally, observing that
\int_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};\bar{x},\cdot)F(y)d\omega(y)=0

\displaystyle\left\|f-\sum_{x_{i}\in X}F(x_{i})w_{i}\,G^{\rho}_{x_{i},\bar{x}}\right\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S})}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{\mathbb{S}}G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};y,\cdot)F(y)d\omega(y)-\sum_{x_{i}\in X}F(x_{i})w_{i}\,G^{\rho}_{x_{i},\bar{x}}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};\cdot)\right\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S})}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left\|\int_{\mathbb{S}}G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};y,\cdot)F(y)d\omega(y)-\int_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}:y,\cdot)F(y)d\omega(y)\right\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S})}}_{=I_{1}}

(4.15)

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\left\|\int_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho}_{y,\bar{x}}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}:\cdot)F(y)d\omega(y)-\sum_{x_{i}\in X}F(x_{i})w_{i}\,G^{\rho}_{x_{i},\bar{x}}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}:\cdot)\right\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S})}}_{=I_{2}}.

2.15

\displaystyle I_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{\mathbb{S}}G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};y,\cdot)F(y)d\omega(y)-\int_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}:y,\cdot)F(y)d\omega(y)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}G(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};y,\cdot)F(y)d\omega(y)-\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}:y,\cdot)F(y)d\omega(y)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}

in

for

is a generic constant that may change. In order to estimate

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho}_{y,\bar{x}}F(y)d\omega(y)-\sum_{x_{i}\in X}F(x_{i})w_{i}\,G^{\rho}_{x_{i},\bar{x}}(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}};\cdot)\right\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S})}

Proposition 4.5

Let

. Then, for any

, there exists

with
\mathcal{B}_{h\sin\theta}(y)\subset P_{stereo}(\Sigma)
and
\|y-P_{stereo}(x)\|=h
(see, e.g.,
[

). Now, let

(where

) of Proposition

(where

instead of
\Sigma^{c}
, we obtain for the overall set
X_{n}^{\prime}=\{x\in X_{n}:x\in\Sigma\textnormal{ or }x\in\tilde{X}_{n}\}
that
h_{X^{\prime}_{n}}\leq\tilde{C}h_{X_{n}}
.

From Corollary
4.4
and the bounded invertibility of
S:L^{2}(\mathbb{S})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{S})
, we now get the existence of a
f_{n}
in
\textnormal{span}\{1_{\mathbb{S}},S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x,\bar{x}}:x\in\tilde{X}_{n}\}\subset V_{\Sigma}
such that

\displaystyle\|f-f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|Sf-Sf_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\rho_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\rho_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}L_{n}^{-(1+\kappa)}\right)\|\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}Sf\|_{H^{1+\kappa}(\mathbb{S})}

\displaystyle=C\left(\rho_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\rho_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{n}^{\frac{1+\kappa}{2}}\right)\|\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}Sf\|_{H^{1+\kappa}(\mathbb{S})}

\displaystyle\leq C\rho_{n}^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\|f\|_{H^{2+\kappa}(\mathbb{S})},

(4.23)

where
C>0
is a generic constant, which may change in every step of estimation but which eventually depends on
\kappa
and
\bar{c}
. The desired assertion (
4.21
) follows from the observation that
\rho_{n}=L_{n}^{-2}\leq\bar{c}^{-2}h_{X_{n}}^{2}
.
∎

4.3
Approximation in the subspaces
D_{\pm,\Sigma}

Due to the structure of
D_{+,\Sigma}
and
D_{-,\Sigma}
, the considerations in the two previous Sections
4.1
and
4.2
directly provide the following theorem.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
X^{\prime}_{n}\subset\mathbb{S}
and
\delta_{n}>0
satisfy the conditions of Theorem
4.1
(with
\Sigma
substituted by
\Sigma^{c}
). Furthermore, let
X_{n}\subset\mathbb{S}
and
\rho_{n}>0
satisfy the conditions of Theorem
4.6
, and
\bar{x}\in\Sigma^{c}
with
\mathcal{C}_{\rho_{1}}(\bar{x})\subset\Sigma^{c}
be fixed. We define the sets
\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{n}=\{x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}_{n}:\mathcal{B}_{\delta_{n}}(x^{\prime})\cap\mathbb{S}\subset\Sigma^{c}\}
and
\tilde{X}_{n}=\{x\in X_{n}:\mathcal{C}_{\rho_{n}}(x)\subset\Sigma^{c}\}
. Additionally, let
\Psi
be as in Definition
2.7
with native space
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
, for
s=2+\kappa
with a fixed
\kappa\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Then the following holds true: For every
f
in
D_{+,\Sigma}/\langle 1\rangle
, there exists a
f_{n}
in
\textnormal{span}\big\{(K+\frac{1}{2}I)S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x,\bar{x}},\,\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i}^{\prime}}:\,x\in\tilde{X}_{n},\,x_{i}^{\prime}\in\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,n\big\}\subset D_{+,\Sigma}
with

\displaystyle\|f-f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\leq C\left(\eta^{n}+h_{X_{n}}^{\kappa}\right)\left(\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}+\|\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\right).

(4.24)

Analogously, for every
g
in
D_{-,\Sigma}
, there exists a
g_{n}
in
\textnormal{span}\big\{(K-\frac{1}{2}I)S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x,\bar{x}},\,\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i}^{\prime}}:\,x\in\tilde{X}_{n},\,x_{i}^{\prime}\in\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,n\big\}\subset D_{-,\Sigma}
with

\displaystyle\|g-g_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\leq C\left(\eta^{n}+h_{X_{n}}^{\kappa}\right)\left(\|\tau_{\textnormal{mtp}}(g)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}+\|g\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\right).

(4.25)

The constants
C>0
and
\eta\in(0,1)
depend on the parameters listed in Theorems
4.1
and
4.6
.

Proof.

According to Theorem
3.3
we can write
f=h+\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right)k
with
h\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{c})
and
k\in V_{\Sigma}
. Applying
\tau_{ptm}
yields
\tau_{ptm}(f)=-h+k-(K+\frac{1}{2}I)k=k-f
. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle k=f+\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)

\displaystyle h=f-\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right)k=-\left(K-\frac{1}{2}I\right)f-\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right)\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)

(4.26)

Since
K\pm\frac{1}{2}I:H^{s}(\mathbb{S})\to H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
is bounded, we have
\|k\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}+\|\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\right)
and
\|h\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}+\|\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\right)
, for some constant
C>0
. Now, let
h_{n}\in\textnormal{span}\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{\Psi_{\delta_{i},x^{\prime}}:x^{\prime}\in\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{i}\}
be an approximation of
h
according to Theorem
4.1
and
k_{n}\in\textnormal{span}\{S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x,\bar{x}}:x\in\tilde{X}_{n}\}
an approximation to
k
according to Theorem
4.6
, then
f_{n}=h_{n}+(K+\frac{1}{2}I)k_{n}
is in
D_{+,\Sigma}
and satisfies the estimate (
4.24
). The estimate (
4.25
) follows along the same lines.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

It can easily be seen from (
3.7
) that

\displaystyle\tau_{ptm}\left((K+\tfrac{1}{2}I)S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x,\bar{x}}\right)=-(K-\tfrac{1}{2}I)S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x,\bar{x}},\quad\tau_{ptm}\left(\Psi_{\delta_{n},x^{\prime}}\right)=-\Psi_{\delta_{n},x^{\prime}},

under the assumptions of Theorem
4.7
. Therefore, for any
f
in the finite-dimensional subspace
\textnormal{span}\{(K+\frac{1}{2}I)S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x,\bar{x}},\,\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i}^{\prime}}:\,x\in\tilde{X}_{n},\,x_{i}^{\prime}\in\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,n\}\subset D_{+,\Sigma}
, one directly obtains the corresponding
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)
in
D_{-,\Sigma}
without much additional computational effort. Yet, one should keep in mind the unboundedness of the operator
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}
when mapping between the infinite-dimensional spaces
D_{+,\Sigma}
and
D_{-,\Sigma}
.

4.4
Norm minimization and vectorial approximation

In Section
3.3
we have briefly discussed norm minimizing vector fields with localization constraints. A corresponding statement with respect to the spherical basis functions considered above is presented next. For a given function
f
in
D_{+,\Sigma}
that is expanded in terms of those spherical basis functions, the norm minimizing vector field
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}
that is supported in
\Sigma^{c}
and that satisfies
\tilde{f}_{+}=f
can be expressed fairly explicitly.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
\rho\in(0,2)
and
x,\bar{x}\in\mathbb{S}
be fixed. Then we denote by
N^{\rho}_{x,\bar{x}}:\Sigma^{c}\to\mathbb{R}
the solution in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c})/\langle 1\rangle
to the following Neumann boundary value problem:
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}N^{\rho}_{x,\bar{x}}=0
in
\Sigma^{c}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}N^{\rho}_{x,\bar{x}}=\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho}_{x,\bar{x}}
on
\partial\Sigma
.

Remark 4.10
.

If
x,\bar{x}\in\Sigma
, then clearly
N^{\rho}_{x,\bar{x}}=G^{\rho}_{x,\bar{x}}
. Otherwise, if
x,\bar{x}\in\Sigma^{c}
, which is the relevant case for our setup, an explicit expression for
N^{\rho}_{x,\bar{x}}
is more tedious to obtain. However, for the special case of
\Sigma^{c}
being a spherical cap, which is sufficient for many considerations, an explicit expression of
N^{\rho}_{x,\bar{x}}
is known: namely,
N^{\rho}_{x,\bar{x}}(y)=\Phi^{(N)}(x,y)-\Phi^{(N)}(\bar{x},y)
, with
\Phi^{(N)}
as derived in
[
8
, Sec. 6.4.5]
for the construction of a Neumann Green function on spherical caps.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let the general setup be as in Theorem
4.7
. Furthermore, let
f
be in
\textnormal{span}\big\{(K+\frac{1}{2}I)S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x,\bar{x}},\,\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i}^{\prime}}:\,x\in\tilde{X}_{n},\,x_{i}^{\prime}\in\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,n\big\}\subset D_{+,\Sigma}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle f=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right)S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}c_{i,k}\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i,k}^{\prime}},

(4.27)

with
c_{\ell},c_{i,k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
M,K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
. For
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}=B_{+}f+B_{-}\tilde{f}_{-}+\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{df}
with

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{-}=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\left(K-\frac{1}{2}I\right)S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}c_{i,k}\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{j,k}}

(4.28)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{df}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}},&\textnormal{ on }\Sigma,\\
-\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}N^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}},&\textnormal{ on }\Sigma^{c},\end{array}\right.

the following holds true:
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}
is in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
,
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{df}
is in
H_{df}(\mathbb{S})
, and

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{f}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\min\left\{\|\mathbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}:\mathbf{f}=B_{+}f+\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{d}\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3}),\,\mathbf{g}\in H_{-}(\mathbb{S}),\,\mathbf{d}\in H_{df}(\mathbb{S})\right\}.

(4.31)

Proof.

We already know that
\tilde{f}_{-}=\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)
is necessary in order to enable
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}
to be in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
. The representation (
4.28
) then directly follows from Remark
4.8
. Furthermore, we can compute

\displaystyle B_{+}f+B_{-}\tilde{f}_{-}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\eta}\left(K-\frac{1}{2}I\right)\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right)S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}c_{i,k}\boldsymbol{\eta}\left(K-\frac{1}{2}I\right)\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i,k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle-\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\eta}\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right)\left(K-\frac{1}{2}I\right)S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}c_{i,k}\boldsymbol{\eta}\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right)\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i,k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\scalebox{0.95}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right)G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}c_{i,k}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i,k}^{\prime}}$}}

(4.32)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}\left(K-\frac{1}{2}I\right)G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}c_{i,k}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i,k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}c_{i,k}\boldsymbol{\eta}\,\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i,k}^{\prime}}.

Due to the properties of the point sets
\tilde{X}_{i}^{\prime}
in Theorem
4.7
and the assumption
x_{i,k}^{\prime}\in\tilde{X}_{i}^{\prime}
, the functions
\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i,k}^{\prime}}
vanish on
\Sigma
. Therefore, the expression above implies
B_{+}f+B_{-}\tilde{f}_{-}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}}
on
\Sigma
. Furthermore, on the boundary
\partial\Sigma
, it holds that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\left(B_{+}f+B_{-}\tilde{f}_{-}\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}},

(4.33)

since
\boldsymbol{\nu}
is orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{\eta}
by definition. The choice
h=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}N^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}}
then satisfies
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}}h=0
on
\Sigma^{c}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}h=\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\left(B_{+}f+B_{-}\tilde{f}_{-}\right)
on
\partial\Sigma
. The assertion of Proposition
4.11
is now a direct consequence of Proposition
3.5
.
∎

Furthermore, the above computations also guide us to an error estimate for the joint approximation in
D_{+,\Sigma}
and
D_{-,\Sigma}
. In fact, this yields a slightly different proof of Theorem
4.7
and it provides a slightly more general statement, since it guarantees the existence of a sequence
f_{n}
such that both
f_{n}
and
\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{n})
satisfy the desired error estimate.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let the general setup be as in Theorem
4.7
and let
\mathbf{f}
be in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Then there exists a
\mathbf{f}_{n}
in
\textnormal{span}\big\{\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x,\bar{x}},\,\boldsymbol{\eta}\,\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i}^{\prime}}:\,x\in\tilde{X}_{n},\,x_{i}^{\prime}\in\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,n\big\}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{f}_{n}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}c_{i,k}\boldsymbol{\eta}\,\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i,k}^{\prime}}
for some
c_{\ell},c_{i,k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
M,K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

\displaystyle\|(\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-})-\mathbf{f}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq C\left(\eta^{n}+h_{X_{n}}^{\kappa}\right)\left(\|f_{+}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}+\|f_{-}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\right),

(4.34)

where
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}+\mathbf{f}_{df}=B_{+}f_{+}+B_{-}f_{-}+\mathbf{f}_{df}
reflects the Hardy-Hodge decomposition of
\mathbf{f}
. The constants
C>0
and
\eta\in(0,1)
depend on the parameters listed in Theorems
4.1
and
4.6
.
Furthermore, the functions

\displaystyle f_{+}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\left(K+\frac{1}{2}I\right)S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}c_{i,k}\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i,k}^{\prime}},

(4.35)

\displaystyle f_{-}^{n}

\displaystyle=\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{+}^{n})=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}c_{\ell}\left(K-\frac{1}{2}I\right)S^{-1}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x_{\ell},\bar{x}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{i}}c_{i,k}\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i,k}^{\prime}},

(4.36)

with
c_{\ell},c_{i,k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
M,K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
as above, lie in
D_{+,\Sigma}
and
D_{-,\Sigma}
, respectively, and satisfy

\displaystyle\|f_{+}-f^{n}_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}

\displaystyle\leq{C}\left(\eta^{n}+h_{X_{n}}^{\kappa}\right)\left(\|f_{+}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}+\|f_{-}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\right),

(4.37)

\displaystyle\|f_{-}-f^{n}_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}

\displaystyle\leq{C}\left(\eta^{n}+h_{X_{n}}^{\kappa}\right)\left(\|f_{+}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}+\|f_{-}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\right).

(4.38)

Proof.

It is well-known known that any
\mathbf{f}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})
can be decomposed into the Helmholtz decomposition
\mathbf{f}=\boldsymbol{\eta}f_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}+\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf_{cf}+\mathbf{f}_{df}
, with
f_{\boldsymbol{\eta}},f_{cf}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
and
\mathbf{f}_{df}
in
H_{df}(\mathbb{S})
(see, e.g.,
[
8
,
10
]
). Since the statement of the theorem assumes that
\mathbf{f}
is in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c},\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we get that
f_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
must be in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{c})
and
f_{cf}
must be in
V_{\Sigma}
. Theorems
4.1
and
4.6
then guarantee the existence of an
\mathbf{f}_{n}^{1}
in
\textnormal{span}\left\{\boldsymbol{\eta}\,\Psi_{\delta_{i},x_{i}^{\prime}}:\,x_{i}^{\prime}\in\tilde{X}^{\prime}_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,n\right\}
and an
\mathbf{f}_{n}^{2}
in
\textnormal{span}\left\{\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}G^{\rho_{n}}_{x,\bar{x}}:\,x\in\tilde{X}_{n}\right\}
such that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\eta}f_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}-\mathbf{f}_{n}^{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle\leq C\eta^{n}\|f_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})},

(4.39)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf_{cf}-\mathbf{f}_{n}^{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle\leq Ch_{X_{n}}^{\kappa}\|f_{cf}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}.

(4.40)

Combining the two estimates above and observing that
\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}=\boldsymbol{\eta}f_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}+\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf_{cf}
as well as that
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
are bounded and invertible, we directly obtain (
4.34
), with a possibly modified constant
C>0
. The desired estimates (
4.35
) and (
4.36
) follow from (
4.34
) when reading the equations in (
4.32
) backwards and when observing the mutual orthogonality of the Hardy spaces
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
.
∎

6
Conclusion

We have investigated a set of spherical basis functions suitable for the approximation in subspaces of the Hardy space
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
that are obtained by orthogonal projection of locally supported vector fields. The new aspect has been that the considered spherical basis functions lead to vectorial functions that are themselves members of these subspaces. In this sense they are related to certain vector spherical Slepian functions but may have some advantages since they do not require simultaneous computations in
H_{+}(\mathbb{S})
,
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
,
H_{df}(\mathbb{S})
and only their centers need to be adapted for new domains of support. However, they do not feature an additional optimization in spectral domain that Slepian functions typically have. The obtained theoretical approximation results have been illustrated by a numerical example in Section
5
. Remark
4.8
shows that the derived spherical basis functions further allow a simple mapping into
H_{-}(\mathbb{S})
, given the underlying localization constraints, which can be of interest for various inverse magnetization problems. The latter, however, requires further studies on possible regularization strategies that go beyond the scope of the paper at hand (a first naïve estimate is provided in the Appendix
A.3
).

A.1
Proof of Proposition
2.2

Proof.

If
f
is a
\mathbf{p}-
zonal function, then

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{f}_{n}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}Y_{n,k}(p)Y_{n,k}(x),

(A.1)

for coefficients
\hat{f}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. From the addition theorem for spherical harmonics, we get

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2s}\hat{f}_{n}^{2}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}Y_{n,k}(p)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2s}\hat{f}_{n}^{2}\frac{2n+1}{4\pi}.

(A.2)

Thus, it must hold

\displaystyle|\hat{f}_{n}|\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{-s-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

(A.3)

Let
g\in H^{k}(\mathbb{S})
with spherical harmonic coefficient
\hat{g}_{n,k}
. The convolution defined in (
2.3
) then has the spectral expression

\displaystyle(f\ast g)^{\wedge}_{n,k}=\hat{f}_{n}\hat{g}_{n,k}

(A.4)

Hence we get by use of (
A.3
) that

\displaystyle\|f\ast g\|_{H^{s+k+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\hat{f}_{n}^{2}\hat{g}_{n,k}^{2}(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2s+2k+1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\frac{2\pi\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}}{(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2s+1}}\hat{g}_{n,k}^{2}(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2s+2k+1}

\displaystyle=2\pi\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\hat{g}_{n,k}^{2}(n+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2k}

(A.5)

\displaystyle=2\pi\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}^{2}\|g\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{S})}^{2},

which concludes the proof.
∎

A.3
A Bounded Extremal Problem for Approximation in
D_{\pm,\Sigma}

We are interested in the following bounded extremal problem: given
f_{e}=f_{+}+e
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
and
c>0
, find
f_{+}^{n,e}
in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
such that

\displaystyle f_{+}^{n,e}=\textnormal{argmin}\{\|f_{e}-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}:g\in\mathcal{D}_{n},\,\|\tau_{ptm}(g)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\leq c\}.

(BEP)

The finite-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{D}_{n}\subset D_{+,\Sigma}
be defined as in (
5.4
). In order to quantify the weak convergence of
\tau_{ptm}(f_{+}^{n,e})
to
\tau_{ptm}(f_{+})
, we further consider the following adjoint bounded extremal problem: For some
y\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
and some bound
t>0
, find
h_{t}\in\textnormal{dom}(\tau_{ptm}^{*})
such that
h_{t}=\textnormal{argmin}\{\|y-h\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}:h\in\textnormal{dom}(\tau_{ptm}^{*}),\,\|\tau_{ptm}^{*}(h)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\leq t\}
. For convenience, we denote

\displaystyle J_{y}(t)=\|y-h_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}.

(A.8)

The existence of a solution
h_{t}
and the guarantee that
J_{y}(t)
tends to zero as
t\to\infty
follows along the same lines as for the original problem (e.g.,
[
13
, App. A.4]
). With this notation, we can now deduce the following estimate.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
f_{e}=f_{+}+e
be in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
, with
f_{+}
in
D_{+,\Sigma}
and
\|e\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}<\varepsilon
. Let all further setup be as in Theorem
4.7
and set
\delta_{n}=C\left(\eta^{n}+h_{X_{n}}^{\kappa}\right)\left(\|f_{+}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}+\|\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{+})\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S})}\right)
, with constants
C>0
and
\eta\in(0,1)
as indicated in Theorem
4.7
. If
f_{+}^{n,e}
is the solution to (
BEP
), then, for any
y\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
and
c>\|\tau_{ptm}(f_{+})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}
, we have that

\displaystyle\|f_{+}^{n,e}-f_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\leq 2\varepsilon+\delta_{n}

(A.9)

and

\displaystyle\left|\langle\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{+}^{n,e}),y\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}-\langle\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{+}),y\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\right|\leq\inf_{t>0}\left\{2cJ_{y}(t)+t(2\varepsilon+\delta_{n})\right\}.

(A.10)

Proof.

Without loss of generality we assume
c\geq\|\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f)\|+\delta_{n}
. Theorem
4.7
then guarantee the existence of an
\mathfrak{f}_{n}
in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
such that
\|f_{+}-\mathfrak{f}_{n}\|\leq\delta_{n}
and
\|\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(\mathfrak{f}_{n})\|\leq c
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{f}_{n}
lies in the feasible set of (
BEP
). Thus,

\displaystyle\|f_{+}^{n,e}-f_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{+}^{n,e}-f_{e}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}+\varepsilon\leq\|\mathfrak{f}_{n}-f_{e}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathfrak{f}_{n}-f_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}+\|f_{+}-f_{e}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}+\varepsilon\leq\delta_{n}+2\varepsilon.

We then get that, for any
t>0
and any
y
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
,

\displaystyle\left|\langle\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{+}^{n,e}),y\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}-\langle\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{+}),y\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\langle\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{+}^{n,e})-\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{+}),y-h_{t}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\right|+\left|\langle\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{+}^{n,e}-f_{+}),h_{t}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\|\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{+}^{n,e})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}+\|\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}(f_{+})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\right)J_{y}(t)+\left|\langle f_{+}^{n,e}-f_{+},\tau_{\textnormal{ptm}}^{*}(h_{t})\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2cJ_{y}(t)+(2\varepsilon+\delta_{n})t.

Taking the infimum over
t
leads to the desired assertion.
∎

The proposition above guarantees that
f_{+}^{n,e}
converges to
f_{+}
and that
\tau_{ptm}(f_{+}^{n,e})
weakly converges to
\tau_{ptm}(f_{+})
as
n\to\infty
and
\varepsilon\to 0
(assuming that both
f_{+}
and
\tau_{ptm}(f_{+})
are in
H^{s}(\mathbb{S})
). The rate of the weak convergence clearly depends on the behaviour of
J_{y}(t)
with respect to
t
. A more precise study of this, however, is beyond the scope of the paper at hand.
