Shape Optimization of hemolysis for shear thinning flows in moving domains
Valentin Calisti 1 , Šárka Nečasová 2

Abstract
. We consider the 3 3 D problem of shape optimization of blood flows in moving domains. Such a geometry is adopted to take into account the modeling of rotating systems and blood pumps for instance. The blood flow is described by generalized Navier-Stokes equations, in the particular case of shear-thinning flows. For a sequence of converging moving domains, we show that a sequence of associated solutions to blood equations converges to a solution of the problem written on the limit moving domain. Thus, we extended the result given in (Sokołowski, Stebel, 2014, in Evol. Eq. Control Theory ) for q ≥ 11 / 5 q\geq 11/5 , to the range 6 / 5 < q < 11 / 5 6/5<q<11/5 , where q q is the exponent of the rheological law. This shape continuity property allows us to show the existence of minimal shapes for a class of functionals depending on the blood velocity field and its gradient. This allows to consider in particular the problem of hemolysis minimization in blood flows, namely the minimization of red blood cells damage.



Keywords:

Shape optimization, generalized Navier-Stokes, moving domains, shear thinning blood flows

1.
Introduction

For patients suffering from heart failure, blood pumps may be essential,
but there are risks of complications associated with this type of device,
such as bleeding, thrombosis and infections
[
30
]
.
The other detrimental consequence we are concerned with in this article is
hemolysis
,
which refers to the destruction of red blood cells (RBCs), leading to a release of free hemoglobin in the blood plasma.
For instance, this can be toxic and provoke renal failure.

The natural question that arises is how to minimize hemolysis.
Numerous
studies have focused on the numerical optimization of design parameters of blood flows or pumps.
We refer among others
to
[
1
]

where a Carreau-Yasuda model is considered,
and to
[
2
]
concerning a Tesla-type pump and the minimization of hemolysis.
To generalize this approach, we address the theoretical shape optimization of these pumps, enabling us to broaden the set of admissible solutions.
Since the state of hemolysis in the blood depends directly on the gradient of the blood velocity field (see Example
1.2
),
the present work therefore focuses on the shape optimization of blood flows in moving domains.

The main obstacle to be overcome is the following:
the non-Newtonian behavior of blood has important effects on blood flows and should be taken into account (see, e.g.
[
18
]
).
Indeed, human blood is mainly composed of plasma
and RBCs,
and their interaction yields a
shear-thinning
behavior,
that is the
viscosity

\nu
decreases when the shear rate increases.
To take this effect into account, we assume that the blood viscosity depends non-linearly
on the symmetric gradient of the velocity field
\mathrm{D}\bm{u}
.
Namely, we assume that the
viscous stress tensor

\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{D}\bm{u}):=\nu(\mathrm{D}\bm{u})\mathrm{D}\bm{u}
is given by:

(1.1)

\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{D}\bm{u}):=\left(1+\lvert\mathrm{D}\bm{u}\rvert\right)^{q-2}\mathrm{D}\bm{u},\quad\text{for }6/5<q<2,\quad\text{and }\quad\mathrm{D}\bm{u}:=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\bm{u}+\nabla\bm{u}^{\top}).

We first note that for
q=2
, the classical Newtonian viscous stress tensor is retrieved, for a constant
viscosity
\nu=1
.
Fluids satisfying (
1.1
)
with
q>2
are called
shear thickening fluids

(e.g. cornflour batter).
Blood, on the contrary, is a
shear thinning fluid
with
q<2

(see, e.g.
[
9
]
, where
q\simeq 1.22
).
For this type of rheological law, we have no uniqueness results for weak solutions, which complicates the study of the shape sensitivity of the problem.

To the best of our knowledge, the
shape optimization analysis of shear-thinning fluids has never been addressed.
In
[
28
]
, the existence of shapes minimizing generic functionals is given for non-Newtonian fluids with

q\geq 11/5
in moving domain.
Here, we extend this result to the range
6/5<q<11/5
(Section
3
), relying on the existence result provided in
[
25
]
.
This leads to the proof (Section
4
) of the main result written in Theorem
1.1
.
After a presentation of the geometry and the blood model in Subsection
1.1
,
we introduce the shape optimization problem in Subsection
1.2
, together with the main result.
In particular, this allows us to show that there exists optimal shapes for the hemolysis minimization problem in moving domains (see Example
1.2
).

1.2.
Shape optimization problem and main result

The shape optimization of Navier-Stokes
equations
has been widely addressed since the late 90s.
For references on
shape sensitivity
of unsteady incompressible case, see among others
[
11
]
,
tackling domain variations of low regularity.
For incompressible Navier-Stokes
in moving domains, the shape differentiability is studied in

[
20
]
,
and in
[
29
]

for shear-thickening fluids in 2D.

We investigate the following family of shape optimization problems:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\text{Find }(\Omega^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*})\in\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}\text{ such that: }

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\Omega^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*})\leq\mathcal{J}(\Omega,\mathbf{V}),\quad\forall(\Omega,\mathbf{V})\in\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V},

where
\mathcal{J}(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
is a shape functional which depends on the solution
\bm{u}
of blood problem (
1.4
)-(
1.1
) defined on the domain
Q
associated to
(\Omega,\mathbf{V})\in\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
.
Here
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{V}
are respectively the classes of admissible initial domains
\Omega
and velocity fields
\mathbf{V}
, which will be specified later in Section
2.1
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathbf{V})\in\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
, we define
\mathcal{P}(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
as the set of solutions to the blood problem (
1.4
)-(
1.1
).
We will see that in the framework of non-Newtonian fluids where the stress tensor is expressed by (
1.1
) for some
q>6/5
, we don’t have the uniqueness of the solutions.
Thus, we define the shape functional as follows:

(1.6)

\mathcal{J}(\Omega,\mathbf{V}):=\inf_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega,\mathbf{V})}\{j(\Omega,\mathbf{V};\bm{u})\},

where the following assumptions are required for the functional
j(\Omega,\mathbf{V};\bm{u})
.

(J.1)

For any
(\Omega,\mathbf{V})\in\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
, and any
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
,
it holds

0\leq j(\Omega,\mathbf{V};\bm{u})
.

(J.2)

The functional
j
is lower semicontinuous.
Namely, let
(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})\longrightarrow(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
be a converging sequence in
\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
, and let
\bm{u}^{k}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})
be any associated sequence of solution converging to some
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
(the definitions of the respective convergences are given in Definition
2.1
and Theorem
3.1
). Then:

j(\Omega,\mathbf{V};\bm{u})\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}j(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k};\bm{u}^{k}).

First, we can notice that from assumption
(J.1)
,
\mathcal{J}(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
is well-defined.
Then we will see in Section
4
that assumptions
(J.1)
and
(J.2)
are used to prove existence of a minimum for Problem (
1.5
), using the
Direct Method of Calculus of Variations
.
This main result of the present article is given below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{J}(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
be the shape functional defined in (
1.6
), where
j
satisfies assumptions
(J.1)
-
(J.2)
.
Then, the shape optimization problem (
1.5
) admits at least one solution
(\Omega^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*})
in the class of admissible domains and velocities
\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
given by (
2.1
)-(
2.2
).

Example 1.2

(Hemolysis minimization)
.

The primary motivation for this work is to find shapes that minimize RBCs damage in blood flows.
The
hemolysis index
, denoted by
h(\bm{u})
, is a scalar field introduced to measure RBCs damage, and can be defined in several ways.
Different models stand, depending on whether the
viscous stress
or the
strain
is considered as a source of hemolysis production (stress/strain-based models), and depending on the chosen scalar measure of these tensors.
The original formulation of the hemolysis index is the following (see
[
33
]
for a review):

h(\bm{u})=C\left\lvert\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{D}\bm{u})\right\rvert^{\alpha}t^{\beta},

where
t
is the time exposure,

\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{D}\bm{u})
is the viscous stress tensor defined in (
1.1
),
and
C
,
\alpha
and
\beta
are positive constants given by the experimental literature.
Let us define for any
1\leq r<\infty
:

\displaystyle j_{r}(\Omega,\mathbf{V};\bm{u})

\displaystyle:=\lVert h(\bm{u})\rVert_{L^{r}L^{r}(t)}^{r}:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega_{t}}\lvert h(\bm{u})\rvert^{r}\,dx\,dt,

and the related shape functionals:

\mathcal{J}_{r}(\Omega,\mathbf{V}):=\inf_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega,\mathbf{V})}\{j_{r}(\Omega,\mathbf{V};\bm{u})\}
.
We straightforwardly notice that
h(\bm{u})
is at least as regular as
\left\lvert\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{D}\bm{u})\right\rvert^{\alpha}
because
\beta>0
so that
t^{\beta}\in L^{\infty}(\mathfrak{Q})
.
Let
(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})\longrightarrow(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
,
and let
(\bm{u}^{k})_{k\geq 0}
(resp.
\bm{u}
) be the associated solutions to Problem (
1.4
)-(
1.1
) written on
(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})
(resp.
(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
).
It will be shown
– see (
3.12
) and
Section
3.2.2
–
that there exists a subsequence s.t.

\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{D}\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{k})\rightharpoonup\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{D}\widetilde{\bm{u}})
weakly in
L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{Q})
and a.e. in
\mathfrak{Q}
,
where
\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{k}
(resp.
\widetilde{\bm{u}}
) denotes the extension of
\bm{u}^{k}
(resp.
\bm{u}
) to
\mathfrak{Q}
by
\mathbf{V}^{k}
(resp.
\mathbf{V}
),
and
q^{\prime}\in(2,6)
is the conjugate of the rheological exponent
q
in (
1.1
).
Thus for any
\alpha\leq q^{\prime}
,
and any
1\leq r\leq q^{\prime}/\alpha
,
we have by definition that
j_{r}(\Omega,\mathbf{V};\bm{u})
is well defined and satisfies Assumption
(J.1)
.
From the weak and a.e. convergences written above, it also holds
h(\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{k})\rightharpoonup h(\widetilde{\bm{u}})
weakly in
L^{r}(\mathfrak{Q})
.
By definition of the extensions, we have
j_{r}(\Omega,\mathbf{V};\bm{u}^{k})=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\lvert h(\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{k})\rvert^{r}\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D\setminus\Omega_{t}}\lvert h(\mathbf{V}^{k})\rvert^{r}\,dx\,dt
.
From the strong convergence
\mathbf{V}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{V}
(see Definition
2.1
),
and by lower semicontinuity of the norm for the weak convergence, Assumption
(J.2)
is satisfied as well.
Then, from Theorem
1.1
, there exists
(\Omega^{*,r},\mathbf{V}^{*,r})\in\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
which minimizes
\mathcal{J}_{r}
, that is to say which minimizes the
L^{r}
-norm of the hemolysis index.
It has to be noticed that this example fits to empirical values, with for instance
q\simeq 1.22
(see
[
9
]
) and
\alpha\simeq 2.42
(or
\alpha\simeq 1.99
, see
[
33
]
for other values and references).

2.
Shape optimization settings

First, we define the classes of admissible initial domains and velocity fields
and the associated notion of convergence.
As is standard practice in shape optimization, these classes are chosen to
enjoy compactness properties, and to be
regular enough for the resolution of the boundary value problems.
Then, we introduce the functional framework for moving domains: the
generalized Bochner spaces
.
From this, we define what a weak solution is for the blood problem.

2.1.
Optimization settings: moving domains

Let
D
be a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and
c_{\mathcal{O}},c_{\mathcal{V}}>0
be two
constants.
Denoting by
\mathcal{C}(c_{\mathcal{O}})
the class of open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
having the
c_{\mathcal{O}}
-cone property
(see
[
13
, Def. 2.4.1]
),
we define the class of admissible initial domains:

(2.1)

\mathcal{O}=\{\Omega\in\mathcal{C}(c_{\mathcal{O}})\mid\overline{\Omega}\subset D\},

and the class of admissible velocity fields:

(2.2)

\mathcal{V}=\{\mathbf{V}\in C^{1,1}([0,T],C^{1,1}_{0,\mathrm{div}}(D)^{3})\mid\lVert\mathbf{V}\rVert_{C^{1,1}}\leq c_{\mathcal{V}}\},

where

C^{1,1}([0,T],C^{1,1}_{0,\mathrm{div}}(D)^{3}):=\{\mathbf{V}\in C^{1,1}([0,T]\times\overline{D})^{3}\mid\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits\mathbf{V}\subset[0,T]\times D,\ \mathop{\rm div}\nolimits_{\bm{x}}\mathbf{V}=0\}
.

Let us motivate these definitions.
First, it is equivalent for
\Omega
to have the cone property and to have a Lipschitz boundary
[
13
, Thm. 2.4.7]
,
which is enough to solve the PDEs in this article.
Secondly, for
\mathbf{V}\in\mathcal{V}
, the associated flows
\bm{\varphi}
given by (
1.2
) are
C^{1}
-diffeomorphisms
[
7
, Chap. 4, Thm. 4.4]
,
so that each layer
\Omega_{t}
has also a Lipschitz boundary
[
14
, Thm. 4.1]
.
For
\mathbf{V}
, we need uniform
C^{1,1}
regularity
for compactness reason, see below.

Now let
((\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k}))_{{k}\geq 0}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
.
For any
k\geq 0
, we define the transformation
\bm{\varphi}^{k}
as the unique solution to problem (
1.2
) associated to
\mathbf{V}^{k}
.
Thus we define as in (
1.3
):

(2.3)

\Omega_{t}^{k}:=\bm{\varphi}^{k}(t,\Omega^{k}),\quad Q^{k}:=\bigcup_{t\in(0,T)}\{t\}\times\Omega_{t}^{k},\quad\Sigma^{k}:=\bigcup_{t\in(0,T)}\{t\}\times\partial\Omega_{t}^{k}.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that
((\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k}))_{{k}\geq 0}
converges to
(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
in
\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
, and we will simply write
(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})\longrightarrow(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
, if the two following convergences hold:

(1)

\Omega^{k}\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\longrightarrow}\Omega
in Hausdorff topology (see
[
13
, Sec. 2.2.3]
for a definition),

(2)

\mathbf{V}^{k}\longrightarrow\mathbf{V}
in
C^{1}([0,T]\times\overline{D})
.

From this definition we can state the following classical compactness result.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
((\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k}))_{k\geq 0}\subset\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
.
Then there exists
(\Omega^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*})\in\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
, and a subsequence
((\Omega^{k^{\prime}},\mathbf{V}^{k^{\prime}}))_{k^{\prime}\geq 0}
such that
(\Omega^{k^{\prime}},\mathbf{V}^{k^{\prime}})\longrightarrow(\Omega^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*})
as
k^{\prime}\rightarrow+\infty
.

The compactness of
\mathcal{O}
for the Hausdorff topology is well-known
[
13
, Thm. 2.4.10]
,
and for
(\mathbf{V}^{k})_{k\geq 0}\subset\mathcal{V}
, the uniform bound
\lVert\mathbf{V}^{k}\rVert_{C^{1,1}}\leq c_{\mathcal{V}}
and
Arzelà-Ascoli Theorem provide a subsequence

\mathbf{V}^{k^{\prime}}\longrightarrow\mathbf{V}
in
C^{1}
-norm, and it can be additionally show that actually
\mathbf{V}\in\mathcal{V}
.

As a consequence, we have the following important properties (see
[
28
]
).
Let
(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})\longrightarrow(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
in
\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
.
Then for every cylinder
[T_{1},T_{2}]\times\overline{K}\subset Q
, where
K
is an open subset
K\subset\subset D
and
0<T_{1}<T_{2}<T
, there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
k\geq k_{0}
:

[T_{1},T_{2}]\times\overline{K}\subset Q^{k}
.
We give a slightly more general result, that we will need later on to consider compactly supported test functions for writing the weak formulation of blood flows for a sequence of converging moving domain
((\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k}))_{k\geq 0}
. Let
\epsilon>0
, we define the two following open sets:

(2.4)

\Omega_{(\epsilon)}:=\left\{x\in\Omega\;\big|\;\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(x,\partial\Omega)>\epsilon\right\},\quad\text{and}\quad\Omega^{(\epsilon)}:=\left\{x\in\Omega\;\big|\;\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(x,\partial\Omega)<\epsilon\right\}.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
((\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k}))_{k\geq 0}
be a sequence converging to
(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
in
\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
.
Let
Q_{K}\subset\underline{Q}
be a compact subset, where
\underline{Q}
is defined by:

\underline{Q}:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\{t\}\times\Omega_{t}
.
Then, there exists an integer
k_{0}
, such that for any
k\geq k_{0}
, we have

Q_{K}\subset\underline{Q}^{k}:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\{t\}\times\Omega^{k}_{t}
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, we start defining the sets
K_{\delta}:=\Omega_{(\delta)}
,

\underline{Q}_{K_{\delta}}:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\{t\}\times\bm{\varphi}(t,K_{\delta})
,
and

\underline{Q}_{\varepsilon}:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\{t\}\times\Omega_{t(\varepsilon)}
,
recalling that
\Omega_{t(\varepsilon)}
is defined by (
1.3
) and (
2.4
).
Because
Q_{K}
is compact and
Q_{K}\subset\underline{Q}
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
Q_{K}\subset\underline{Q}_{\varepsilon}
.
Let
\delta<\varepsilon/c_{\mathcal{V}}
.
In view of
\lvert\bm{\varphi}(t,X)-\bm{\varphi}(t,Y)\rvert\leq c_{\mathcal{V}}\lvert X-Y\rvert
holding
\forall t\in[0,T]
,
\forall X,Y\in\Omega
,
one can check that
\bm{\varphi}^{-1}(t,\Omega_{t(\varepsilon)})\subset K_{\delta}
, and then

\bm{\varphi}(t,\bm{\varphi}^{-1}(t,\Omega_{t(\varepsilon)}))\subset\bm{\varphi}(t,K_{\delta})
,
so that finally
\underline{Q}_{\varepsilon}\subset\underline{Q}_{K_{\delta}}
.
Then we can show that there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\geq N_{0}
,
it holds
\underline{Q}_{K_{\delta}}\subset\underline{Q}^{k}_{K_{\delta/2}}:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\{t\}\times\bm{\varphi}^{k}(t,K_{\delta/2})

(from the convergence

\mathbf{V}^{k}\longrightarrow\mathbf{V}

in
C^{1}([0,T]\times\overline{D})
).
Finally, from the Hausdorff convergence

\Omega^{k}\longrightarrow\Omega
,
there exists a
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\geq N_{1}
, the inclusion
\overline{K_{\delta/2}}\subset\subset\Omega^{k}
holds, which implies
\underline{Q}^{k}_{K_{\delta/2}}\subset\underline{Q}^{k}
, and finishes the proof by setting
k_{0}:=\max\{N_{0},N_{1}\}
.
∎

2.2.
Functional settings

We recall the definition of
generalized Bochner spaces
(see
[
25
]
), for a time-dependent family of separable Banach spaces
(B(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
such that
B(t)\hookrightarrow L^{1}(\Omega_{t})
, and
1\leq r\leq\infty
.
We define:

L^{r}((0,T),B(t)):=\Big\{u\in L^{1}(Q)\;\Big|\;u(t)\in B(t)\text{ for a.e. }t\in(0,T),\ t\mapsto\lVert u(t)\rVert_{B(t)}\in L^{r}((0,T))\Big\}

and we define its norm as follows:

\lVert u\rVert_{L^{r}_{0,T}B(t)}:=\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert u\rVert_{B(t)}^{r}\,dt\bigg)^{\frac{1}{r}}.

Let
u\in B(t)
, and
v
belong to the dual space
B(t)^{\prime}
.
In the following, we will write the duality product between
u
and
v
as:

\langle v,u\rangle_{B(t)}
.
In the particular case where
u,v\in L^{2}(\Omega_{t})
, we will write:

(v,u)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t})}:=\int_{\Omega_{t}}v(x)u(x)\,dx
.
For
d
-dimensional vector functions
\bm{u}\in L^{r}((0,T),B(t)^{d})
and
\bm{v}(t)\in(B(t)^{d})^{\prime}
, the norm and duality product will simply be written
\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{L^{r}_{0,T}B(t)}
and
\langle\bm{v}(t),\bm{u}(t)\rangle_{B(t)}
.
For the sake of readability,
when no ambiguity, these spaces will simply be denoted as

L^{r}B(t)^{d}:=L^{r}((0,T),B(t)^{d})
.

Now we define the
generalized time derivative
of
u\in L^{r}B(t)
as follows:
\partial_{t}u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Q)
such that

\langle\partial_{t}u,\eta\rangle:=-\langle u,\partial_{t}\eta\rangle

for all
\eta\in\mathcal{D}(Q)
,
recalling that
L^{r}B(t)\hookrightarrow L^{1}(Q)
.
From there, we define:

H^{1}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega_{t})):=\left\{u\in L^{2}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega_{t}))\mid\partial_{t}u\in L^{2}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega_{t}))\right\}
.

We finally introduce the Banach spaces
B(t)
involved in the present study.
We recall that

W^{1,q}_{\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega_{t})^{3}
and
L^{q}_{\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega_{t})^{3}
denote the closure of

\{\bm{u}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{t})^{3}\mid\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\bm{u}=0\}
in
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{W^{1,p}}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{p}}
norms respectively,
and

W^{1,q}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega_{t})^{3}
and
L^{q}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega_{t})^{3}
are the closure of

\{\bm{u}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega_{t})^{3}\mid\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\bm{u}=0\}
in
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{W^{1,p}}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{p}}
norms respectively.
Then we define:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\underline{\mathrm{X}}{\hskip 1.07639pt}_{q}(D)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\bm{u}\in H^{1}((0,T),L^{2}(D)^{3})\cap L^{q}((0,T),W^{1,q}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(D)^{3})\ \big|\ \bm{u}(T,\cdot)=0\right\},

\displaystyle\underline{\mathrm{X}}{\hskip 1.07639pt}_{q}^{k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\bm{u}\in H^{1}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega^{k}_{t})^{3})\cap L^{q}((0,T),W^{1,q}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k}_{t})^{3})\ \big|\ \bm{u}(T,\cdot)=0\right\},

and we shall simply write
\underline{\mathrm{X}}{\hskip 1.07639pt}_{q}
when
\Omega^{k}_{t}
is replaced by
\Omega_{t}
.
The condition
\bm{u}(T,\cdot)=0
makes sense because it can be shown that the time evaluation is well-defined for any
\bm{u}\in H^{1}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega_{t})^{3})
, and for any
t\in[0,T]
holds
\bm{u}(t)\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega_{t})^{3}
.

2.3.
Blood flows equations in moving domains

Let
((\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k}))_{k\geq 0}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
.
We consider the following equations for blood flows posed on the resulting sequence of non cylindrical domains defined in (
2.3
):

(2.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\bm{u}^{k}}{\partial t}+\bm{u}^{k}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}^{k}-\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{D}\bm{u}^{k}))+\nabla\pi^{k}&=\bm{f}&\text{ in }Q^{k},\\
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\bm{u}^{k}&=0&\text{ in }Q^{k},\\
\bm{u}^{k}(0)&=\bm{u}_{0}^{k}&\text{ in }\Omega^{k},\\
\bm{u}^{k}&=\mathbf{V}^{k}&\text{ on }\Sigma^{k},\end{aligned}\right.

where for any
\bm{v}
,
\mathrm{D}\bm{v}
is defined in (
1.1
) and:

(2.7)

\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{D}\bm{v}):=\left(1+\lvert\mathrm{D}\bm{v}\rvert\right)^{q-2}\mathrm{D}\bm{v},\quad\text{for }6/5<q<2.

We assume that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
:

(U.1)

\bm{u}^{k}_{0}\in L^{2}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(D)^{3}
,

(U.2)

\bm{u}^{k}_{0}=\mathbf{V}^{k}(0)
in
D\setminus\Omega^{k}
,

(U.3)

and
\bm{u}^{k}_{0}\rightharpoonup\bm{u}_{0}
weakly in
L^{2}(D)
.

With these assumptions, if we consider that
(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})\longrightarrow(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
, then we have
\bm{u}_{0}=\mathbf{V}(0)
in
D\setminus\Omega
.
From
[
25
, Thm. 49]
, there exists a
weak solution

\bm{u}^{k}
to Problem (
2.6
)-(
2.7
), the definition of which is given below.

Definition 2.4
.

Let the rheology exponent be
6/5<q<11/5
, the body force

\bm{f}\in L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{Q})^{3}\hookrightarrow L^{q^{\prime}}(Q^{k})^{3}
, and the initial data

\bm{u}_{0}^{k}\in L^{2}_{\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k})^{3}
such that
\bm{u}_{0}^{k}-\mathbf{V}^{k}(0)\in L^{2}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k})^{3}
.
We say that
\bm{u}^{k}\in L^{\infty}L^{2}_{\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k}_{t})^{3}\cap L^{q}W^{1,q}_{\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k}_{t})^{3}
is a weak solution of (
2.6
)-(
2.7
), if the following hods:

(2.8)

-\int_{0}^{T}(\bm{u}^{k},\partial_{t}\bm{\eta})_{L^{2}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\mathbf{g}(\bm{u}^{k}),\bm{\eta}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\mathbf{T}(\bm{u}^{k}),\bm{\eta}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}\\
=\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{f},\bm{\eta}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}+(\bm{u}_{0}^{k},\bm{\eta}(0))_{L^{2}(\Omega^{k})},\quad\forall\bm{\eta}\in\underline{\mathrm{X}}{\hskip 1.07639pt}_{p}^{k},

for
p\geq(5q/6)^{\prime}
,
where the function spaces are given in Section
2.2
, and where we define:

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{g}(\bm{u}),\bm{\eta}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}

\displaystyle:=-\int_{\Omega^{k}_{t}}(\bm{u}(t)\otimes\bm{u}(t)):\nabla\bm{\eta}(t),

(2.9)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{T}(\bm{u}),\bm{\eta}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega^{k}_{t}}\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{D}\bm{u}(t)):\mathrm{D}\bm{\eta}(t).

Let us comment on this definition.

On the one hand, from the assumption on the regularity of
\bm{u}^{k}
, Sobolev injection theorem, and interpolation, we have
\bm{u}^{k}\otimes\bm{u}^{k}\in L^{5q/6}L^{5q/6}(t)^{3\times 3}
, which guarantees the definition of
\mathbf{g}(\bm{u})
.
Given the range of
q
, we have
p\geq 2
allowing the
L^{2}
products, and we also have
q^{\prime}\geq p^{\prime}
, so that the term depending on the body force
\bm{f}
is well-defined.
On the other hand,
it can be shown that for any
1<q<\infty
, the stress tensor defined in (
2.7
) satisfies the following properties (see e.g.,
[
12
, Chap.III, Sec. 2.2.1]
),
for all
\bm{A},\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}}
:

(i)

Coercivity:

\mathbf{S}(\bm{A}):\bm{A}\geq c_{1}\lvert\bm{A}\rvert^{q}-c_{2},

(ii)

Growth:

\lvert\mathbf{S}(\bm{A})\rvert\leq c_{3}(1+\lvert\bm{A}\rvert^{q-1})
,

(iii)

Monotonicity:

(\mathbf{S}(\bm{A})-\mathbf{S}(\bm{B})):(\bm{A}-\bm{B})>0,

for some constants
c_{1},c_{3}>0
and
c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
depending on
q
.
The regularity assumed for the weak solution and the growth condition
(ii)
yield

\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{D}\bm{u})\in L^{q^{\prime}}L^{q^{\prime}}(t)^{3\times 3}
, so that the term
\mathbf{T}(\bm{u})
is well-defined.
Classically, property
(i)
is used to obtain the energy inequality in Proposition
3.4
, and
(iii)
allows for identifying the stress limit in Section
3.2.2
.
Thus, more general rheological laws satisfying
(i)
-
(iii)
can suit the present study (see
[
25
]
).

3.
Shape continuity of blood flows

In this section, we show that
for a converging sequence of moving domains
((\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k}))_{k\geq 0}
, and for an associated sequence of solutions
(\bm{u}^{k})_{k\geq 0}
, one can extract a subsequence converging to the blood solution associated to the limit moving domain.
This composes the following main result of this section.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})\rightarrow(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
in
\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
,

\bm{u}^{k}_{0}

satisfying
(U.1)
-
(U.3)
,
and let
\bm{u}^{k}
be a weak solution of (
2.6
)-(
2.7
).
Let
\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{k}
be
the extension of
\bm{u}^{k}
to
\mathfrak{Q}
by
\mathbf{V}^{k}
.
Then, there exists
\widetilde{\bm{u}}\in L^{\infty}L^{2}_{\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(D)^{3}\cap L^{q}W^{1,q}_{\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(D)^{3}
and a subsequence
such that
\bm{u}^{k^{\prime}}\rightharpoonup\widetilde{\bm{u}}
weakly in
L^{q}W^{1,q}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(D)^{3}

In Section
3.1
, we first recall the existence result proved in
[
25
]
, which gives solutions

for each moving domain

For doing this, we need to consider compactly supported test functions, so that we finally conclude with a density argument.

3.1.
Existence of solutions and uniform energy estimate

Proposition 3.2

(1)

)-(

(2)

3.1.1.
Equivalent homogeneous Dirichlet problem

To solve Problem (
2.6
)-(
2.7
),
the first step is to homogenize the boundary condition, that is to find:

\bm{w}^{k}=\bm{u}^{k}-\mathbf{V}^{k}

solution in

of problem (
2.8
) for which
\mathbf{g}(\bm{u})
,
\mathbf{T}(\bm{u})
, and
\bm{f}
are respectively replaced by:

(3.1)

\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{V}^{k}}(\bm{u}):=\mathbf{g}(\bm{u}+\mathbf{V}^{k}),\quad\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{V}^{k}}(\bm{u}):=\mathbf{T}(\bm{u}+\mathbf{V}^{k}),\quad\text{ and }\quad\bm{f}_{\mathbf{V}^{k}}:=\bm{f}-\partial_{t}\mathbf{V}^{k},

Namely,

\bm{w}^{k}

(3.2)

3.1.2.
Approximation

In order to solve (

\mathbf{S}^{M}(\mathrm{D}\bm{u}):=\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{D}\bm{u})+\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{A}(\mathrm{D}\bm{u})

,
for

2.4
.
We denote by
\mathbf{T}^{M}
and
\mathbf{T}^{M}_{\mathbf{V}^{k}}

by
\mathbf{S}^{M}
,

Finally the initial data is regularized: we consider

(\bm{w}_{0,M}^{k})_{M\geq 0}\subset W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k})^{3}

\bm{w}_{0,M}^{k}\longrightarrow\bm{w}_{0}^{k}
strongly in

We notice that

Thus we can apply the following result (see
[

Proposition 3.3
.

Let the body force

\bm{f}\in L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{Q})^{3}
,
and let
(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})\in\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
.

(1)

Let the initial data be

\bm{w}_{0,M}^{k}\in W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k})^{3}

There exists a

\bm{w}_{M}^{k}\in L^{\infty}L^{2}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k}_{t})^{3}\cap L^{p}W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k}_{t})^{3}

(3.3)

\int_{0}^{T}\Big[-(\bm{w}^{k}_{M},\partial_{t}\bm{\eta})_{L^{2}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}+\langle\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{V}^{k}}(\bm{w}^{k}_{M}),\bm{\eta}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}+\langle\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{V}^{k}}^{M}(\bm{w}^{k}_{M}),\bm{\eta}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}\Big]\\

(2)

We have that for every
M\in\mathbb{N}
, every weak solution
\bm{w}^{k}_{M}
, every

, and every
\bm{\eta}\in H^{1}L^{2}(\Omega^{k}_{t})^{3}\cap L^{p}W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k}_{t})^{3}

(3.4)

(3)

There exists a not relabeled subsequence
(\bm{w}_{M}^{k})_{M\geq 0}

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{k}_{M}

\displaystyle\xrightharpoonup[M\rightarrow\infty]{}\bm{w}^{k}\qquad\text{ weakly in }L^{q}W^{1,q}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k}_{t})^{3},

In order to pass to the limit as

3.1.3.
Uniform energy estimate

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\bm{w}_{M}^{k}
be a
solution of Problem (
3.3
).
Then, for some constant
C>0
which does not depend on
k
, the following estimate holds:

(3.5)

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{w}_{M}^{k}\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{2}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}^{2}+\lVert\bm{w}_{M}^{k}\rVert_{L^{q}W^{1,q}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}^{q}+\frac{1}{M}\lVert\mathrm{D}\bm{w}_{M}^{k}\rVert_{L^{p}(Q^{k})}^{p}\leq C.

Proof.

We recall that
(\bm{w}^{k}_{0,M})_{M\geq 0}
is chosen in
W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k})^{3}
such that
\bm{w}^{k}_{0,M}
converges strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega^{k})^{3}
to
\bm{w}^{k}_{0}\in L^{2}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega^{k})^{3}
,
and the solution
\bm{w}^{k}_{M}
is such that
\bm{w}^{k}_{M}(0)=\bm{w}^{k}_{0,M}
.
Thus for
M
large enough,

(\bm{w}^{k}_{0,M},\bm{w}^{k}_{M}(0))_{L^{2}(\Omega^{k})}=\lVert\bm{w}^{k}_{0,M}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{k})}\leq 2\lVert\bm{w}^{k}_{0}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{k})}
.
From the condition
(U.2)
on
\bm{u}^{k}_{0}
and the definition of
\bm{w}_{0}^{k}
, we have that

\lVert\bm{w}_{0}^{k}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega^{k})}\leq\lVert\bm{u}^{k}_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(D)}+\lVert\mathbf{V}^{k}(0)\rVert_{L^{2}(D)}
.
Furthermore, from the weak convergence of
(\bm{u}^{k}_{0})_{k\geq 0}
in
L^{2}(D)^{3}
, we have a positive constant
C_{0}
such that
\lVert\bm{u}^{k}_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(D)}\leq C_{0}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
and by definition of
(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})\longrightarrow(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
, we have
\lVert\mathbf{V}^{k}\rVert_{L^{2}(D)}\leq\lvert D\rvert^{1/2}c_{\mathcal{V}}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Due to these considerations, by setting
\bm{\eta}=\bm{w}^{k}_{M}
in (
3.4
), and rewriting the last term of the right hand side, we get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\bm{w}^{k}_{M}(s),\bm{w}^{k}_{M}(s))_{L^{2}(\Omega^{k}_{s})}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{s}(\mathbf{T}^{M}_{\mathbf{V}^{k}}\langle\bm{w}^{k}_{M}),\bm{w}^{k}_{M}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{s}\langle\bm{f}_{\mathbf{V}^{k}},\bm{w}^{k}_{M}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}-\int_{0}^{s}\langle\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{V}^{k}}(\bm{w}^{k}_{M}),\bm{w}^{k}_{M}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}+K_{0},

where
K_{0}=2(C_{0}^{2}+\lvert D\rvert c_{\mathcal{V}}^{2})
. We can directly compute that:

(3.7)

-\int_{0}^{s}\langle\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{V}^{k}}(\bm{w}^{k}_{M}),\bm{w}^{k}_{M}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}=\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\Omega^{k}_{t}}(\mathbf{V}^{k}\otimes\mathbf{V}^{k}):\nabla\bm{w}^{k}_{M}-\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\Omega^{k}_{t}}(\bm{w}^{k}_{M}\otimes\bm{w}^{k}_{M}):\nabla\mathbf{V}^{k}.

The second term of the right hand side of (
3.7
) is bounded by
c_{\mathcal{V}}\int_{0}^{s}\lVert\bm{w}^{k}_{M}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega^{k}_{s})}
.
Regarding the first term, we would like to have the
L^{q}
-norm of
\nabla\bm{w}^{k}_{M}
.
Recalling that
(\mathbf{V}^{k}\otimes\mathbf{V}^{k})\in L^{\infty}(\mathfrak{Q})^{3\times 3}
, we can write:

\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\Omega_{t}}(\mathbf{V}^{k}\otimes\mathbf{V}^{k}):\nabla\bm{w}^{k}_{M}\leq\int_{0}^{s}\lVert\mathbf{V}^{k}\otimes\mathbf{V}^{k}\rVert_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}\lVert\nabla\bm{w}^{k}_{M}\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}.

Then, the first term of the right hand side of (
3.7
) can be bounded by:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}\lVert\mathbf{V}^{k}\otimes\mathbf{V}^{k}\rVert_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}\lVert\nabla\bm{w}^{k}_{M}\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{s}\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{q}\lVert\mathrm{D}\bm{w}^{k}_{M}\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}^{q}+\frac{c_{\mathcal{K}}^{q^{\prime}}}{q^{\prime}\varepsilon^{1/(q-1)}}\lVert\mathbf{V}^{k}\otimes\mathbf{V}^{k}\rVert_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega^{k}_{t})}^{q^{\prime}}\Big)

In order to identify nonlinear terms, classical compactness lemmas cannot be directly applied in the context of moving domains.
Among other methods, the Landes–Mustonen compactness principle is used in

and

Finally, for

We have now all the ingredients to identify the convective limit.
Let

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{V}},\bm{\eta}\rangle_{W^{1,p}(\Omega_{t})}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega_{t}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{V}}:\nabla\bm{\eta}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega_{t}}\Big(\sum_{i=-1}^{2N-2}\chi_{i}\Big)\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{V}}:\nabla\bm{\eta}\,dx\,dt

3.2.2.
Identification of the stress

We can apply the exact same procedure as in
[
25
, Sec. 5.2]
.
Let us give some details.
We start rewriting the equation in term of
\widetilde{\bm{u}}=\widetilde{\bm{w}}+\mathbf{V}

From (

\bm{H}^{2}_{k}:=\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u}-\bm{u}^{k}\otimes\bm{u}^{k}

In

\displaystyle\bm{h}_{k}

\displaystyle\xrightharpoonup[\hskip 8.19447pt]{}\bm{0}

\displaystyle\xrightharpoonup[\hskip 8.19447pt]{}\bm{0}

Density of compactly supported test functions

Let us define the space

4.
Existence of a minimal shape

We apply the direct method of calculus of variations.

Proof.

In view of assumption
(J.1)
, we have that
\mathcal{J}(\Omega,\mathbf{V})
is well-defined and non-negative for all
(\Omega,\mathbf{V})\in\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
.
Because
\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
is not empty, we thus have that

\mathrm{I}_{\mathcal{J}}:=\inf_{(\Omega,\mathbf{V})\in\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}}\{\mathcal{J}(\Omega,\mathbf{V})\}

exists.
Let
((\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k}))_{k\geq 0}\subset\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
be a minimizing sequence for
\mathcal{J}
.
From Proposition
2.2
, we can consider a not relabeled subsequence
((\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k}))_{k\geq 0}
converging to some
(\Omega^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*})\in\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{V}
in the sense of Definition
2.1
.
Let
m\geq 1
.
From Theorem
3.1
, and from the definition (
1.6
) of
\mathcal{J}
, we have that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\bm{u}^{k}_{m}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})
, such that:

(4.1)

\mathcal{J}(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})\leq j(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k};\bm{u}^{k}_{m})<\mathcal{J}(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})+\frac{1}{m}.

Still from Theorem
3.1
, let
(\bm{u}^{k}_{m})_{k\geq 0}
be a not relabeled subsequence converging to some
\bm{u}_{m}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*})
.
Letting
k
go to
\infty
in (
4.1
), recalling that
(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k})
is a minimizing sequence, and taking into account the lower semicontinuity property
(J.2)
of
j
, we can write:

\mathrm{I}_{\mathcal{J}}\leq\mathcal{J}(\Omega^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*})\leq j(\Omega^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*};\bm{u}_{m})\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}j(\Omega^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k};\bm{u}^{k}_{m})\leq\mathrm{I}_{\mathcal{J}}+\frac{1}{m}.

Letting

, we obtain

\mathcal{J}(\Omega^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*})=\mathrm{I}_{\mathcal{J}}
,
so that
(\Omega^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*})
is a solution to Problem (
1.5
).
∎

5.
Conclusion

We have shown the existence of minimal shapes for blood flows in
moving domains,
by proving the shape continuity of the velocity solutions.
Blood is described by generalized Navier-Stokes equations (power law
q>6/5
modeling shear-thinning fluids).
This model allows for considering prescribed movements of the solid domain surrounding and inside the blood (ex: blood pump modeling),
and to tackle the hemolysis index minimization problem (see Example
1.2
).

The blood velocity is here assumed to fit the one of the domain on its boundary.
To go further, one could consider more general BCs
(ex: prescribed velocity on the inlet; Navier type BCs,
see
[
4
,
3
]
);
artificial BCs
on the outlet).
It could also be interesting to take into account the elasticity of blood vessels, carrying out the same kind of analysis based on the work of
[
16
]
.

Although our analysis has led to an existence result, the non-uniqueness of the solutions prevents from studying the shape differentiability of the problem.
This is an impediment to any numerical study based on shape gradient method.
In order to circumvent this lack of uniqueness, to compute shape derivatives, and to tackle numerically the blood damage minimization, one could work with strong solutions (unique locally in time), or with 2D Newtonian fluids.
Otherwise, regardless of the model chosen, the analysis of the convergence of a numerical scheme would then be a fundamental issue.

Finally, concerning the hemolysis index in the framework of numerical computation, a more practical way of modeling would be to consider transport equation (see, e.g.
[
26
,
33
]
).
Thus, as a first step, it would be interesting to consider the same shape continuity result for transport equations,
and then to study the shape sensitivity of transport equations.

\bm{\eta}_{n}:=\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi^{-1}}}\overline{\bm{\eta}}_{n}\in C^{\infty}_{0}\Big(\bigcup_{t\in[0,T-1/n)}\{t\}\times\Omega_{t,(c_{\mathcal{V}}/n)}\Big),

so that

\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\bm{\eta}_{n}=0
.

\bullet

Convergence of smooth approximations.

We now show that:

(A.8)

\lVert\bm{\eta}_{n}-\bm{\eta}\rVert_{H^{1}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega_{t})}\xrightharpoonup[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0\quad\text{ and }\quad\lVert\bm{\eta}_{n}-\bm{\eta}\rVert_{L^{p}_{0,T}W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega_{t})}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0.

First, we notice that from Lemma
A.2
(applied for
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi^{-1}}}
) and in view of the identity
\bm{\eta}=\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi^{-1}}}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}
,
there exists a constant
C>0
such that:

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\eta}_{n}-\bm{\eta}\rVert_{H^{1}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega_{t})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\lVert\overline{\bm{\eta}}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}\rVert_{H^{1}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\nabla_{x}(\overline{\bm{\eta}}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta})\rVert_{L^{2}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}\right),

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\eta}_{n}-\bm{\eta}\rVert_{L^{p}_{0,T}W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega_{t})}

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert\overline{\bm{\eta}}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}\rVert_{L^{p}_{0,T}W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega)}.

Thus,
proving (
A.8
)
amounts to showing that:

\lVert\partial_{t}\overline{\bm{\eta}}_{n}-\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}\rVert_{L^{2}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}\xrightharpoonup[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0\quad\text{ and }\quad\lVert\overline{\bm{\eta}}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}\rVert_{L^{p}_{0,T}W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega)}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0.

We proceed in two steps. We show
(1)
first that
\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}
, and
(2)
second that
\mathcal{B}[\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}]\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, in both cases for the required norms

\lVert\partial_{t}(\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}
weak and

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{p}_{0,T}W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega)}
strong.

(1)

Convergence of the main term
\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}
.

From (
A.7
), we
have
\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}\rVert_{L^{p}L^{p}(t)}\longrightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
From (
A.6
) and because the supports of the involved functions are compactly supported in
[-T,T]\times\Omega
, we can interchange the time derivative and the convolution.
Thus, we estimate the norm of the weak time derivative as follows:

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{t}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}-\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}\rVert_{L^{2}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\big\lVert\omega_{n}*\partial_{t}(\xi_{n}\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}-\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}})\big\rVert_{L^{2}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big\lVert\omega_{n}*\partial_{t}\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}-\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}\big\rVert_{L^{2}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}

(A.9)

\displaystyle\leq\big\lVert\partial_{t}(\xi_{n}\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}-\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}})\big\rVert_{L^{2}_{-T,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big\lVert\omega_{n}*\partial_{t}\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}-\partial_{t}\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}\big\rVert_{L^{2}_{-T,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}.

We directly notice that the second term in (
A.9
) goes to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Regarding the first term, we have:

(A.10)

\hskip 28.45274pt\big\lVert\partial_{t}((\xi_{n}-1)\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}})\big\rVert_{L^{2}_{-T,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
\leq\big\lVert(\xi_{n}-1)\partial_{t}\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}\big\rVert_{L^{2}_{-T,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big\lVert\partial_{t}(\xi_{n})\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}\big\rVert_{L^{2}_{-T,T}L^{2}(\Omega)},

where the first term of the right hand side goes to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
By definition,
\partial_{t}\xi_{n}=0
outside
I_{b}\times\Omega_{ab}\cup I_{ab}\times\Omega_{a}
, where, using definition (
2.4
), we set:

\displaystyle I_{b}\times\Omega_{ab}

\displaystyle:=\Big(-T+\frac{b}{n},T-\frac{b}{n}\Big)\times\big(\Omega_{(b/n)}\big)^{(a/n)},

\displaystyle I_{ab}\times\Omega_{a}

\displaystyle:=\Big(-T+\frac{b}{n},T-\frac{b}{n}\Big)\setminus\Big[-T+\frac{a}{n},T-\frac{a}{n}\Big]\times\Omega_{(a/n)}.

By definition, for any
x\in\Omega_{ab}
and for any
t\in I_{ab}
, we have that
n<a\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(x,\partial\Omega)^{-1}
and
n<a\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(t,\{-T,T\})^{-1}
.
Then we compute the norm of the second term of the right hand side of (
A.10
) on these two subsets respectively.
We also note that for a.e.
t\in(-T,T)
,

\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}(t,\cdot)\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
,
and for a.e.
x\in\Omega
,

\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}(\cdot,x)\in W^{1,2}_{0}((-T,T))
,
so that, taking also into account
(iii)
and Hardy’s inequality for
\Omega
(whose constant is denoted by
c_{\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}}
), we have:

\displaystyle\int_{I_{b}}\int_{\Omega_{ab}}\left\lvert\partial_{t}(\xi_{n})\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq(Ca)^{2}\int_{I_{b}}\left\lVert\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\cdot,\partial\Omega)^{-1}\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega_{ab})}^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq(Cac_{\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}})^{2}\int_{I_{b}}\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,dt.

Thus,
\partial_{t}(\xi_{n})\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}\longrightarrow 0
in
L^{2}((-T,T),L^{2}(\Omega)^{3})
by absolute continuity of Lebesgue integral,
because
\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\cdot,\partial\Omega)^{-1}\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}\in L^{2}(\Omega)

and
\lvert\Omega_{ab}\rvert\rightarrow 0
. The same arguments holds for the integration over
I_{ab}\times\Omega_{a}
, rewriting:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{a}}\int_{I_{ab}}\left\lvert\partial_{t}(\xi_{n})\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq(Ca)^{2}\int_{\Omega_{a}}\left\lVert\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(t,\{-T,T\})^{-1}\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(I_{ab})}^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq(Cac_{\mathcal{H}_{T}})^{2}\int_{\Omega_{a}}\left\lVert\partial_{t}\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}((-T,T))}^{2}\,dx,

where
c_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}
is the Hardy’s inequality constant associated to the interval
(-T,T)
.
All terms going to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
in (
A.10
), we finally have that

\lVert\partial_{t}\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}-\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}\rVert_{L^{2}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0
.
Following exactly the same explanations given in the present Item
(1)
, we show as well that
\lVert\nabla_{x}\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}-\nabla_{x}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}\rVert_{L^{p}_{0,T}L^{p}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0
.
So far, we have shown that:

(A.11)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}\quad\text{ strongly in }L^{p}((0,T),W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega)),

(A.12)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}\quad\text{ strongly in }H^{1}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega)).

(2)

Convergence of the correction term
\mathcal{B}[\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}]
.

From Proposition
A.1
, we can write:

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{B}[\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}]\rVert_{L^{p}_{0,T}W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq c_{\mathcal{B}}(\Omega)\lVert\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}\rVert_{L^{p}_{0,T}L^{p}(\Omega)},

and in view of (
A.11
),
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}\rightarrow\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\overline{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\varphi}}\bm{\eta}}=0
in
L^{p}((0,T),L^{p}(\Omega))
, so that
\mathcal{B}[\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}]\rightarrow 0
in
L^{p}((0,T),W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega)^{3})
. Then from (
A.1
) and (
A.2
):

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}[\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}]\rVert_{L^{2}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathcal{B}[\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\partial_{t}\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}]\rVert_{L^{2}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq c_{\mathcal{B}}(\Omega)\lVert\partial_{t}\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}\rVert_{L^{2}_{0,T}L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Thus, in view of (
A.12
),
\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}[\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}]
is bounded in
L^{2}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega)^{3})
.
Let
\bm{\varphi}\in(C^{\infty}_{0}((0,T))\times\Omega)^{3}
, we have:

\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega}\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}[\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}]\cdot\bm{\varphi}\,dt\,dx=-\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega}\mathcal{B}[\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}]\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\varphi}\,dt\,dx,

and the right hand side goes to
0
in view of (
A.11
).
Thus we have a subsequence such that
\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}[\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}]\rightharpoonup 0
weakly in
L^{2}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega)^{3})
.
Finally, we have shown that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}]

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0\quad\text{ strongly in }L^{p}((0,T),W^{1,p}_{0,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits}(\Omega)^{3}),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}[\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\widetilde{\bm{\eta}}_{n}]

\displaystyle\xrightharpoonup[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0\quad\text{ weakly in }L^{2}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}).

This concludes the proof.
