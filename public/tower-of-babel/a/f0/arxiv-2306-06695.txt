The arc complexes of partially decorated hyperbolic polygons
Pallavi Panda

Abstract




Abstract.

We consider two families of hyperbolic polygons: ideal and ideal once-punctured, some of whose spikes are decorated with horoballs. We show that the arc complexes of these two families of surfaces, generated by edge-to-edge arcs and edge-to-decorated-spike arcs, are closed piecewise linear balls. This is proved in a completely combinatorial setting: compact polygons whose vertices are assigned red or blue colouring. In order to prove the ballness, we show that these simplicial complexes are pseudo-manifolds and use shellability to conclude. As a consequence, we parametrise weakly-lengthening deformations of the partially decorated hyperbolic polygons.

1
Introduction

In this paper, we study the topology of a finite combinatorial object associated to two types of hyperbolic surfaces: decorated ideal polygons and decorated once-punctured polygons.

An ideal
n
-gon, denoted by
\Pi_{n}^{\Diamond}
is the convex hull in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
of
n\geq 3
points in the boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. This surface is homeomorphic to a disk with
n
points removed from its boundary. The missing points are called
spikes
and the infinite hyperbolic geodesic joining two consecutive spikes is called an
edge
. A once-punctured
n
-gon, denoted by
\Pi^{\times}_{n}
, with (
n\geq 1
), is obtained from an ideal (
n+2
)-gon by identifying two consecutive edges. The resulting surface is homeomorphic to a punctured disk with
n
points removed from its boundary.

We will refer to both these families as hyperbolic polygons. A hyperbolic polygon with
n
spikes is said to be
partially decorated
if
0\leq r\leq n
of its spikes are equipped with horoballs. When
r=0
, the polygon is said to be
undecorated
. When
r=n
, the polygon is said to be
fully decorated
. We denote by
\Pi_{n,r}
(resp.
\Pi^{\times}_{n,r}
) the family of ideal
n
-gons (resp. once-punctured
n
-gons) with
r
decorated spikes. This construction is motivated by Penner’s decorated Teichmuller theory, where he studied general fully decorated hyperbolic surfaces.

The
arc complex

\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
of such a partially decorated surface
\Pi
is a pure clique (simplicial) complex using certain embedded arcs and their intersectionality. This was first introduced by Harvey
[
6
]
for general hyperbolic surfaces with boundary. In our case, the 0-simplices are given by the isotopy classes of two types of arcs: edge-to-edge arcs, and decorated-spike-to-edge arcs. Two such 0-simplices are joined by a 1-simplex if there exists representatives in their isotopy classes that are disjoint. The arc family corresponding to a maximal simplex is called a
triangulation
of the polygon. One can construct a flip graph whose vertices are given by triangulations; two vertices are joined by an edge if it is possible to obtain one triangulation from the other by exchanging only one arc.

Penner
[
9
]
proved that the arc complexes of undecorated ideal polygons as well as undecorated once-punctured polygons are piecewise linear spheres. In general, arc complexes are locally non-compact with infinite diameter. The two families of hyperbolic polygons are amongst a short list of exceptions. The flip graph of undecorated
\Pi_{n}^{\Diamond}
is the 1-skeleton of the famous polytope called
associahedron
, discovered by Tamari
[
14
]
, and then rediscovered by Stasheff
[
13
]
. Sleator–Tarjan–Thurston
[
12
]
and Pournin
[
10
]
have determined the diameter of this polytope using hyperbolic and combinatorial methods. The flip graph of the undecorated
\Pi^{\times}_{n}
is the 1-skeleton of the polytope named
cyclohedron
or type-B associahedron, introduced by Bott-Taubes. It is also the flip graph of centrally symmetric triangulations of an ideal polygon. See
[
4
]
for the work of Panina–Gordon on the triangulations of this surface.

The main result of this paper is to give the topology of the arc complexes of partially decorated polygons. We prove that

Theorem A
.

For
n\geq 3
and
2\leq r\leq n
, the arc complex
\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
of a partially decorated ideal polygon
\Pi\in\widehat{\Pi}_{n,r}
is
PL
-homeomorphic to a closed ball of dimension
n+r-4
.

Theorem B
.

For
n\geq 1
and
1\leq r\leq n
, the arc complex
\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
of a partially decorated once-punctured polygon
\Pi\in\widehat{\Pi}^{\times}_{n,r}
is
PL
-homeomorphic to a closed ball of dimension
n+r-2
.

The motivation behind this topological result comes from hyperbolic geometry. A
horoball connection
is the geodesic segment subtended by the horoball decorations of two spikes. The length of this segment measures the distance between the two decorated spikes. The (lambda) lengths of the horoball connections were used by Penner to give a cellular decomposition of the decorated Teichmuller space of a surface. An
admissible
deformation of a partially decorated polygon with at least one horoball connection is the set of all infinitesimal deformations of the hyperbolic metric that uniformly lengthens all horoball connections. This set forms an convex polyhedral cone, denoted by
\Lambda(m)
. In
[
8
]
, we prove that the cone over the arc complex of decorated ideal polygons as well as decorated once-punctured polygons, parametrises this admissible cone.

This work was motivated by the work of Danciger–Guéritaud–Kassel
[
2
]
, who proved a similar result in the case of convex cocompact hyperbolic surfaces. The admissible cone is formed by the set of all deformations that uniformly lengthen every closed geodesic. To a weighted sum of pairwise disjoint arcs, they assigned an infinitesimal deformation obtained by gluing strips along these arcs. This map is called the
strip map
. Such a deformation is admissible. Strip deformations were first defined by Thurston
[
15
]
. Goldman–Labourie–Margulis, in
[
5
]
, proved that the admissible cone forms an open convex cone. Moreover, a hyperbolic metric along with an admissible deformation corresponds to a flat Lorentzian non-compact 3-manifold called a
Margulis spacetime
, whose fundamental group is given by a finitely generated free group.
We generalised these results to general decorated hyperbolic surfaces
[
7
]
. An admissible deformation in this case is an infinitesimal deformations that uniformly lengthens every horoball connection. In
[
7
]
, we showed that the second definition implied the first one. Finally, we also gave a parametrisation of Margulis spacetimes, decorated by affine lightlike lines.

The boundary of the admissible cone consists of weakly-lengthening deformations. It is very difficult to study these deformations using strip deformation because the polytope has infinitely many facets and the full arc complex is not a PL-manifold in general. Nevertheless, we take advantage of the small surfaces like decorated polygons that have finite ball-like arc complexes in order to get a better insight of the weakly lengthening deformations of a general surface. In particular, we get a parametrisation of the boundary using strip deformations:

Theorem C
.

Let
\Pi
be a partially decorated (possibly once-punctured) polygon with a metric
m\in\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
. Then the strip map
f:C\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)\longrightarrow T_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
is a homeomorphism onto
\overline{\Lambda(m)}
.

The proofs of our main theorems
A
,
B
, are done in a completely combinatorial and topological setting. To a partially decorated (resp. once-punctured) hyperbolic polygon
\Pi\in\widehat{\Pi}_{n,t}
one can associate a compact (resp. once-punctured) polygon
\mathcal{P}
with
n+r
vertices,
r
of which are coloured red and the rest are colored blue. The red vertices correspond to the decorated spikes and the blue vertices correspond to the boundary edges of the hyperbolic polygon. Then the subcomplex
\mathcal{Y}(\mathcal{P}_{m})
of the arc complex
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{P})
, generated by
R-B
arcs and
B-B
arcs is isomorphic to the corresponding hyperbolic polygon. In fact, we prove something stronger:

Theorem D
.

The subcomplex
\mathcal{Y}(\mathcal{P}_{m})
of a polygon
\mathcal{P}_{m}
with any
R-B
bicolouring is a closed ball of dimension
m-4
. Similarly, the subcomplex
\mathcal{Y}(\mathcal{P}_{m}^{\times})
of a once-punctured polygon
\mathcal{P}_{m}^{\times}
with any bicolouring is a closed ball of dimension
m-2
.

The proof of our main theorems are motivated by the work of Wilson
[
16
]
. He proved that the arc and curve complex of a Möbius strip with marked points on the boundary is spherical. He also reproved the sphericity of the arc complexes of a convex polygon and a once-punctured polygon. The main ingredient he used was the shellability of pure simplicial complexes. This is an ordering of all the maximal simplices of the complex so that the intersection of the
k
-th simplex with the union of the first
(k-1)
simplices is always a pure complex of codimension one. He gave shelling orders for the arc complexes of all three surfaces and then concluded using a result by Danaraj and Klee
[
1
]
which states that a shellable
d
-pseudo-manifold without boundary is a combinatorial sphere.
We follow a similar approach.

As a final corollary, we have that,

Theorem E
.

Let
\Pi
be a partially decorated
n
-gon with
r
decorated spikes and
h\leq r
marked boundary horoball connections. Then, the arc complex
\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
is PL-homeomorphic to a closed ball of dimension
n+r-h-4
(resp.
n+r-h-2
) , for
\Pi\in\widehat{\Pi}_{n,r}
(resp.
\widehat{\Pi}^{\times}_{n,r}
), except when
h=r=n
, in which case there are no permitted arcs.

This helps us parametrise the maximal dimensional facets of the admissible cone using strip deformations. A facet on the boundary of the admissible cone corresponds to all those infinitesimal deformations that lengthen every horoball connection except one.

Organisation of the paper.

The paper is structured into two parts in the following way:
The first consists of combinatorial and topological proofs. The second part discusses the application in hyperbolic geometry. Section

recapitulates the necessary vocabulary and results from simplicial complexes, arc complexes and hyperbolic geometry. Section
3
contains the proofs of our main theorems. Section

describes the link between the arc complexes of the Euclidean polygons and the decorated hyperbolic polygons.

Setup

2.1
Simplicial complex

In this section we recall relevant definitions and results on finite simplicial complexes.

A simplicial complex is called
pure
if all of its maximal simplices have the same dimension.
The
dual graph
of a simplicial complex is the graph whose vertices are the maximal simplices and two vertices are joined by an edge if the corresponding maximal simplices share a codimension 1 face. A pure simplicial complex is said to be
strongly connected
if its dual graph is connected.

A
d
-pseudo-manifold with boundary
is a pure strongly connected
d
-simplicial complex in which every
(d-1)
-simplex is contained in atmost two
d
-simplices. Note that the boundary of such a simplicial complex is formed by all
(d-1)
-simplices that are contained in exactly one
d
-simplex.

Next we recall the definition of a shelling of pure simplicial complexes.

Let
X
be a pure finite simplicial complex of dimension
d
. A
shelling
of
X
is an enumeration of its maximal simplices
\mathcal{T}:(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})
such that for every
1\leq k\leq n
, the intersection
\left(\bigcup\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}C_{j}\right)\bigcap C_{k}
is a pure simplicial complex of dimension
d-1
.

The following is a lemma linking shellability and join of two simplicial complexes that we shall use in the proof of our main theorems. See
[
11
]
for a proof.

Lemma 2.1
.

Two complexes
X,Y
are shellable if and only if
X\Join Y
is shellable.

We will use the following result by Danaraj and Klee
[
1
]
to prove our main theorems.

Theorem 2.2
.

A shellable
d
-pseudo-manifold with boundary is
PL
-homeomorphic to a closed ball of dimension
d
.

2.3
Shelling of finite arc complexes

Wilson gave an equivalent definition for shelling in
[
16
]
, in the context of arc complexes.

Property 1

(Wilson)
.

Let
X
be a pure simplicial complex. There exists an enumeration of all the maximal faces of
X
, denoted by
\mathcal{T}:(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})
, such that for every two positive integers
j
and
k
satisfying
j<{k}\leq n
, there exists a positive integer
i<k
such that

(i)

C_{i}\cap C_{k}
is a codimension one simplex,

(ii)

C_{j}\cap C_{k}\subset C_{i}\cap C_{k}
.

In
[
16
]
(Proposition 3.5), Wilson proved that in the context of finite arc complexes, there is an equivalence between shellability and Property (
1
).

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose that
S_{g,n}
is a surface with finite arc complex. Then the complex
\mathcal{A}(S_{g,n})
is shellable if and only if it satisfies Property (
1
).

In the following Lemma we show that the same holds for any pure subcomplex of the arc complex.

Lemma 2.7
.

A pure codimension zero subcomplex
X
of
\mathcal{A}(S_{g,n})
is shellable if and only if it satisfies Property (
1
).

Proof.

We show that the enumeration
\mathcal{T}=C_{1}\ldots,C_{n}
given by the property works as a shelling order. So we need to show that for every
1\leq k\leq n
, the simplicial complex
B_{k}:=\left(\bigcup\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}C_{j}\right)\bigcap C_{k}
is a pure simplicial complex of dimension
d-1
, where
d
is the dimension of the subcomplex
X
as well as
\mathcal{A}(S_{g,n})
. Let
\sigma\subset B_{k}
be any simplex of codimension more than one. Then it is contained in
C_{j}\cap C_{k}
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
. Since
X
is a subcomplex of the arc complex, the complex
C_{j}\cap C_{k}
is in fact a simplex. From Property (
1
) we get that there is an
i<k
such that
C_{i}\cap C_{k}
is of dimension
d-1
and
C_{j}\cap C_{k}\subset C_{i}\cap C_{k}\subset B_{k}
. Hence we get that the simplex
\sigma
is not maximal in
B_{k}
and the dimension of any maximal simplex containing
\sigma
is
d-1
.

Conversely, let us suppose that
X
is shellable with a shelling order
\mathcal{T}_{n}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
. Consider two maximal simplices
T_{j},T_{k}
with
j<k\leq n
. If
T_{j}\cap T_{k}=\varnothing
, there is nothing to show. So we assume that
T_{j}\cap T_{k}\neq\varnothing
. Once again, the intersection
T_{j}\cap T_{k}
is a simplex because
X
is a subcomplex of the arc complex. Let
B_{k}:=\left(\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{k-1}T_{i}\right)\cap T_{k}
as before. From the hypothesis,
B_{k}
is a pure simplicial complex of dimension
d-1
. So we get that
T_{j}\cap T_{k}
is contained in a
(d-1)
-simplex
T_{i}\cap T_{k}
, where
T_{i}
is a maximal simplex with
i<k
. This

satisfies the two conditions of Property (
1
).
∎

Main theorems

3.1
Bicoloured convex polygon

In this section we prove that the subcomplex
\mathcal{Y}(\mathcal{P}_{m})\subset\mathcal{A}\left(\mathcal{P}_{m}\right)
is PL-homeomorphic to a closed ball. Firstly we show that this subcomplex is a pseudo-manifold with boundary when the bicolouring is non-trivial.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
m\geq 4
and any non-trivial bicolouring of
\mathcal{P}_{m}
, the subcomplex
\mathcal{Y}(\mathcal{P}_{m})
of
\mathcal{A}\left(\mathcal{P}_{m}\right)
is pure of dimension
m-4
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be a simplex of
\mathcal{Y}(\mathcal{P}_{m})
of dimension
k\geq 1
. The diagonals corresponding to the 0-simplices of
\sigma
decompose the polygon
\mathcal{P}_{m}
into

We triangulate each one of them with a fan triangulation based at one such blue vertex. Since the dimension of the maximal simplex in the uncoloured polygon is
m-4
, we get that any maximal simplex containing
\sigma
is of dimension
m-4
.
∎

4
Applications: decorated hyperbolic polygons

4.1
Hyperbolic geometry

In this section we recall a few definitions from hyperbolic geometry which will be used in Section
4
to give the application of our main theorems in the context of hyperbolic polygons, as previewed in the introduction.

The hyperbolic plane, denoted by
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, is the unique (up to isometry) complete simply-connected Riemannian 2-manifold of constant curvature equal to -1. Its boundary, denoted by
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
, is homeomorphic to a circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
The subset
\{z=x+iy\in\C\,|\,y>0\}
of the complex plane is the upper half-space model of the hyperbolic space of dimension 2.
The geodesics are given by semi-circles whose centres lie on
\R
or straight lines that are perpendicular to
\R
. We shall call the former as
horizontal
and the latter as
vertical
geodesics. The boundary at infinity
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
is given by
\R\cup\{\infty\}
. The orientation-preserving isometry group is given by
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
that acts by Möbius transformations on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. This group has three types of elements: elliptic (one fixed point in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
), parabolic (one fixed point in
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
) and hyperbolic (two fixed points in
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
).

Horoball connection.

A horocycle based at a point in
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
is the orbit of a parabolic element fixing that point. A horoball is the convex hull of a horocycle. A geodesic, whose endpoints are decorated with pairwise disjoint horoballs, is called a
horoball connection
. The length of a horoball connection is the hyperbolic length of the geodesic segment subtended by the two horoballs.

4.2
The polygons

Ideal polygons.

An
ideal
n
-gon
, denoted by
\Pi_{n}^{\Diamond}
, is defined as the convex hull in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
of
n\,(\geq 3)
distinct points (called
vertices
) on
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
. The
edges
are infinite geodesics of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
joining two consecutive vertices. Two consecutive edges meet at a point on
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
, forming a
spike
. The restriction of the hyperbolic metric to an ideal polygon gives it a metrically complete finite-area hyperbolic metric with geodesic boundary.

Punctured polygons.

For
n\geq 2
, an
ideal once-punctured
n
-gon
, denoted by
\Pi^{\times}_{n}
, is another non-compact hyperbolic surface with geodesic boundary, obtained from an ideal
(n+2)
-gon, by identifying two consecutive edges using a parabolic element of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
that fixes the common vertex. The edges of the polygon are the connected components of the boundary. The vertices are the quotients of the vertices of
\Pi_{n+2}^{\Diamond}
.

A spike at a vertex
v
of an ideal (possibly punctured) polygon is said to be
decorated
if a horoball, based at
v
is added.

Decorated Polygons.

A

is a hyperbolic polygon with some of its spikes decorated with horoballs. For
n\geq 3
and
2\leq r\leq n
, we denote by
\widehat{\Pi}_{n,r}
, the set of all ideal
n
-gons with
r

\widehat{\Pi}^{\times}_{n,r}
for
n\geq 1
and
1\leq r\leq n
. See Fig.
11

The deformation space of a partially decorated ideal polygon
\Pi\in\widehat{\Pi}_{n,r}

n\geq 3
) (resp. once-punctured polygon
\Pi\in\widehat{\Pi}^{\times}_{n,r}

n+r-3
(resp.
n+r-1
). See
[
8
]
for a proof.

Admissible deformations.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the set of all horoball connections of the (possibly once-punctured) polygon
\Pi
. Then we can define the following smooth positive function for every
\beta\in\mathcal{H}
:

\begin{array}[]{ccl}l_{\beta}:&\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)\longrightarrow&\R_{>0}\\
&m\mapsto&\text{length of $l_{\beta}$ w.r.t $m$.}\end{array}

Given a hyperbolic polygon
\Pi
with metric
m
, a vector in the tangent space

is called an

of
\Pi

When
\mathcal{H}\neq\varphi

such that for all
\beta\in\mathcal{H}

\mathrm{d}l_{\beta}(v)\geq Kl_{\beta}.

The
admissible cone

\Lambda(m)
of a metric

on a decorated polygonal surface

is defined to be the set of all admissible deformations of the given metric. The admissible cone is open and convex in

In the case of a decorated polygon
\Pi\in\widehat{\Pi}_{n,r}

4.4

A strip deformation of a polygon is done by cutting the surface along a geodesic arc and gluing a strip of the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, without any shearing. In particular, along a finite arc we glue a hyperbolic strip and along a ray-type arc we glue a parabolic strip.

Given a polygon
\Pi
endowed with a metric
m\in\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
, a strip template is the following data:

•

an
m
-geodesic representative
\alpha_{g}
from every isotopy class
\alpha
of arcs in
\mathcal{K}
, along which the strip deformation is performed,

•

a point
p_{\alpha}\in\alpha_{g}
where the waist of the strip being glued must lie.

For more detailed discussion, see
[
8
]
.

Given an isotopy class of arcs
\alpha
of a polygon
\Pi
and a strip template
\{(\alpha_{g},p_{\alpha},w_{\alpha})\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}}
adapted to the nature of
\alpha
for every

,
define the
infinitesimal strip deformation

\begin{array}[]{cc}f_{\alpha}:&\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)\longrightarrow T\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)\\
&m\mapsto\frac{dF_{t\alpha}(m)}{dt}|_{t=0}\end{array}

where
F_{t\alpha}(m)
is obtained from
m
by strip deforming along
\alpha
with a fixed waist

and the width as

.

The
infinitesimal strip map
is defined as:

\begin{array}[]{ccrcl}\mathbb{P}f&:&\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{P}^{+}(T_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi))\\
&&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\dim\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)}c_{i}\alpha_{i}&\mapsto&\left[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\dim\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)}c_{i}f_{\alpha_{i}}(m)\right]\end{array}

\mathbb{P}^{+}(\Lambda(m))

\simeq\R^{2n-4}\,(\text{resp. }\R^{2n-2}
), where
\Lambda(m)
is the admissible cone.

We also proved a version of the above theorem for the undecorated ideal polygons and once-punctured ideal polygons. In these cases the arcs are finite with endpoints on non-consecutive edges of the polygon. We show that the arc complex parametrises the entire positively projectivised deformation space in these cases.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\Pi
be an ideal polygon
\Pi_{n}^{\Diamond}

) (resp. a once punctured polygon

\simeq\mathbb{S}^{n-4}\,(\text{resp. }\mathbb{S}^{n-2})
is a homeomorphism.

Combining these two results we get that

Theorem 4.6
.

\Pi

n
-gon
\widehat{\Pi}_{n,r}
(

) (resp. a decorated once-punctured

-gon
\widehat{\Pi}^{\times}_{n,r}
(

) with a decorated metric

. Then the projectivised infinitesimal strip map

, when restricted to the pruned arc complex

4.5
Extension of the strip map

In this section, we extend the strip map to the full arc complex and prove that it is a homeomorphism onto the closure of the (projectivised) admissible cone.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
\Pi
be a partially decorated (possibly once-punctured) polygon with a metric
m\in\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
. Then the projectivised strip map
\mathbb{P}f:\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{+}(T_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi))
is a homeomorphism onto
\mathbb{P}^{+}(\overline{\Lambda(m)})
.

Before giving the proof, we illustrate the theorem with three examples.

:

These are shown in Figures

,

.

Firstly we consider an ideal quadrilateral with two decorated spikes. There are two possible configurations, which are given by the two rows in Figure

. In both the cases there is only one horoball connection, named
\beta
, coloured in red. From Theorem
3.6
, we get that the arc complex of this polygon is PL-homeomorphic to a closed ball of dimension 2. The arc complexes for both the configurations are shown in the middle panel of Fig.
14
.

The images of the projectivised strip map are shown in the right panel. In this affine chart, the admissible cone covers the entire plane. Its boundary is the circle at infinity given by the positive projectivisation of the hyperplane
\{\mathrm{d}l_{\beta}=0\}\subset T_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
, containing all the infinitesimal deformations that do not lengthen the horoball connecction
\beta
but gives motion to the undecorated spikes.

In the second example we have a once-punctured bigon with both its spikes decorated. There are four horoball connections denoted by
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{4}
. The arc complex is again a two dimensional closed ball. The boundary of the admissible cone is formed by the four hyperplanes, given by
\{\mathrm{d}l_{\gamma_{i}}=0\}\subset T_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)\}
, for
i=1,2,3,4
.

Proof.

Firstly, we show that the image of
\mathbb{P}f
is contained in
\mathbb{P}^{+}(\overline{\Lambda(m))}
.
Recall that the set
\overline{\Lambda(m)}
is the intersection of finitely many hyperspaces of the form

\{u\in T_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)\mid\mathrm{d}l_{\beta}(m)(u)\geq 0\},

where
\beta
is a horoball connection of
\Pi
.
From Theorem
4.6
, we know that
\mathbb{P}f(\mathcal{PA}(\Pi))=\mathbb{P}^{+}(\Lambda(m))
.
Now let
x\in\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)\setminus\mathcal{PA}(\Pi)
. Then
x
is contained in the interior of a unique boundary simplex
\sigma_{x}
. Then the arc family corresponding to
\sigma_{x}
is disjoint from some horoball connection
\beta_{0}
.
We know from
[
8
]
that

\mathrm{d}l_{\beta}(m)(f(x))\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}>0,&\text{when $x$ intersects $\beta$},\\
=0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

So
\mathbb{P}f(x)\in\mathbb{P}^{+}(\{\mathrm{d}l_{\beta_{0}}=0\}\cap\partial\overline{\Lambda(m)})
.

It suffices to prove the homeomorphism in the deprojectivised case:

f:C(\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))\longrightarrow\overline{\Lambda(m)},

where
C(\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))
is homeomorphic to
\R_{>0}\times{\mathbb{B}}^{N-1}
, using Theorems
3.6
,
3.11
. Here
N
is the dimension of
T_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
. The admissible cone
\overline{\Lambda(m)}
is a also closed convex cone of
T_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)\setminus\{0\}.
In the following we prove that
f
is a local homeomorphism and a proper map. This implies that
f
is a covering map. Since both these manifolds
C(\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))
and
\overline{\Lambda(m)}
are simply connected and have the same dimension, we can then conclude that
f
is a homeomorphism.

Firstly, we show that the deprojectivised strip map is proper. Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n}\in C(\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))
be a sequence such that
x_{n}\to\infty
. Since the arc complex
\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
is a finite simplicial complex, we can assume (up to extracting a subsequence) that
x_{n}\in C(\sigma)
, where
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
is a simplex. Then we can write
x_{n}=\sum\limits_{i}c_{i}^{n}\alpha_{i}
where
\alpha_{i}\in\sigma^{(0)}
,
c_{i}^{n}\geq 0
and
w(x_{n}):=\sum\limits_{i}c_{i}^{n}>0
. Then, the hypothesis
x_{n}\to\infty
implies that, up to a subsequence, either
w(x_{n})\to 0
or
w(x_{n})\to\infty
. We need to prove that
f(x_{n})\to\infty
in
\overline{\Lambda(m)}
. In other words, we need to show that the norm of
f(x_{n})
tends to either 0 or to
\infty
. From Theorem
4.7
, we know that the set of infinitesimal strip deformations
\{f_{\alpha_{i}}(m)\mid\alpha_{i}\in\sigma^{(0)}\}
is linearly independent. So there exists
k,K>0
such that
kw(x_{n})\leq f(x_{n})\leq Kw(x_{n})
. This proves that
f(x_{n})\to\infty
.

Now we prove that the deprojectivised strip map
f
is a local homeomorphism. Given a point
x\in C(\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))
, denote by
[x]
its image in
\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
. Suppose that
x
is contained in the interior of
C(\sigma_{[x]})
, for a simplex
\sigma_{[x]}
of
\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
containing
[x]
. It suffices to show that this map
f
is a homeomorphism for all
d:=\operatorname{codim}\left(\sigma_{[x]}\right)\geq 0
.
Suppose that
d=0
. Then from Theorem
4.7
, we have that
\{f_{\alpha}(m)\mid\alpha\in\sigma_{[x]}^{(0)}\}
is a basis of
T_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)
. So
f
is a linear homeomorphism in the interior of
C(\sigma_{[x]})
.
Next suppose that
d=1
. The case when
\sigma_{[x]}
is a internal simplex of
\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
follows from Theorem
4.4
. Suppose that
\sigma_{[x]}
is a boundary simplex. Then it is contained in exactly one face of codimension 0, say
\Delta
, and
f(\sigma_{[x]})\in\partial\Lambda(m)
. Furthermore, from the codimension 0 case we know that
f(C(\Delta))
is non-degenerate. Hence,
f(C(\sigma_{[x]}))
is a codimension one boundary facet of
\overline{\Lambda(m)}
.

Now suppose that the deprojectivised strip map is a local homeomorphism around all points
x\in C(\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))
such that
\operatorname{codim}\left(\sigma_{[x]}\right)=0,1,\ldots,d-1
. We already know that
f
is a local homeomorphism when
x
is in the interior of
C(\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))
. So we need to treat only the case when
x\in\partial\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
such that
\operatorname{codim}\left(\sigma_{[x]}\right)=d
. We can write
x=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\sigma_{[x]}^{(0)}}c_{\alpha}\alpha
, where
c_{\alpha}>0
. Let
y
be a point close to
x
in
C(\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))
. Let
y^{\prime}:=y-x=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\sigma_{[x]}^{(0)}}c^{\prime}_{\alpha}\alpha+\sum\limits_{\beta\in\mathrm{Lk}^{(0)}}c^{\prime}_{\beta}\beta
, where
c^{\prime}_{\alpha}\geq 0
. Since
y=x+y^{\prime}\in C(\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))
, we have that
c^{\prime}_{\beta}\in\R
. Define
g(y^{\prime}):=f(x+y^{\prime})-f(x)
and extend it to the full tangent cone of
C\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
at
x
to get

g:C\operatorname{Link}(\sigma_{[x]},\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))\times\R^{N-d}\longrightarrow\mathrm{T}_{f(x)}T_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi).

Now for
y^{\prime}:=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\sigma_{[x]}^{(0)}}c^{\prime}_{\alpha}\alpha\in C(\sigma_{[x]})
,
y^{\prime\prime}:=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\sigma_{[x]}^{(0)}}c^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha}\alpha+\sum\limits_{\beta\in\mathrm{Lk}^{(0)}}c^{\prime\prime}_{\beta}\beta\in C\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)
, we have that

g(y^{\prime})+g(y^{\prime\prime})=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\sigma_{[x]}^{(0)}}(c^{\prime}_{\alpha}+c^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha})\alpha+\sum\limits_{\beta\in\mathrm{Lk}^{(0)}}c^{\prime}_{\beta}\beta=g(y^{\prime}+y^{\prime\prime}).

Furthermore, we have that for
t>0
,
g(ty^{\prime})=tg(y)
. So the local homeomorphism of
f
at
x
is equivalent to homeomorphism of the map given by

\overline{g}:\operatorname{Link}(\sigma_{[x]},\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{+}(T_{m}\mathfrak{D}(\Pi)/\operatorname{span}\left\{f(\sigma_{[x]})\right\}),

From Theorems
3.6
and
3.11
, we know that
\operatorname{Link}(\sigma_{[x]},\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)))\simeq{\mathbb{B}}^{d-1}
. So
\overline{g}
becomes a map defined between two spaces both homeomorphic to
{\mathbb{B}}^{d-1}
.
Let
y\in\operatorname{Link}(\sigma_{[x]},\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))
, contained in the interior of the simplex
\sigma_{y}
of the link. Then we have

\operatorname{Link}(\sigma_{y},\operatorname{Link}(\sigma_{[x]},\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right)))=\operatorname{Link}(\sigma_{[x]}\cup\sigma_{y},\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}{\mathbb{B}}^{d^{\prime}-1},&\text{ if }\sigma_{[x]}\cup\sigma_{y}\in\partial\mathcal{A}\left(\Pi\right),\\
\mathbb{S}^{d^{\prime}-1},&\text{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

where
d^{\prime}:=\operatorname{codim}\left(\sigma_{[x]}\cup\sigma_{y}\right)<d=\operatorname{codim}\left(\sigma_{[x]}\right)
. By induction hypothesis,
\overline{g}
is a local homeomorphism around
y
. So
\overline{g}
is a local homeomorphism between two simply connected spaces, hence a global homeomorphism. This concludes the proof of local homeomorphism of
f
.
