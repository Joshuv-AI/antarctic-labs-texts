Minimax Optimal Deep Neural Network Classifiers Under Smooth Decision Boundary
Tianyang Hu Ruiqi Liu Zuofeng Shang Guang Cheng, [1em] Department of Statistics, Purdue University

Abstract
Deep learning has gained huge empirical successes in large-scale classification problems. In contrast, there is a lack of statistical understanding about deep learning methods, particularly in the minimax optimality perspective. For instance, in the classical smooth decision boundary setting, existing deep neural network (DNN) approaches are rate-suboptimal, and it remains elusive how to construct minimax optimal DNN classifiers. Moreover, it is interesting to explore whether DNN classifiers can circumvent the “curse of dimensionality” in handling high-dimensional data. The contributions of this paper are two-fold. First, based on a localized margin framework, we discover the source of suboptimality of existing DNN approaches. Motivated by this, we propose a new deep learning classifier using a divide-and-conquer technique: DNN classifiers are constructed on each local region and then aggregated to a global one. We further propose a localized version of the classical Tsybakov’s noise condition, under which statistical optimality of our new classifier is established. Second, we show that DNN classifiers can adapt to low-dimensional data structures and circumvent the “curse of dimensionality” in the sense that the minimax rate only depends on the effective dimension, potentially much smaller than the actual data dimension. Numerical experiments are conducted on simulated data to corroborate our theoretical results.



1
Introduction

Deep learning has achieved many breakthroughs in modern classification tasks, especially for natural images
(
Deng et al. 2009
;
Krizhevsky et al. 2012
;
He et al. 2016
;
Nguyen et al. 2017
)
.
In contrast to the huge empirical success, statistical understanding of DNN classifiers is still lacking as to why neural networks perform better than traditional classification methods, particularly for high-dimensional structured data.
Most theoretical works revolving around neural network classifiers focus on the generalization perspective, developing error bounds for trained classifiers on unseen data
(
Vapnik 1999
;
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
;
Zhang et al. 2016
;
Cao and Gu 2019
)
.
However, derivation of generalization bounds mostly relies on the complexity of the DNN class, which is often independent of the data distribution.
Deep learning is not better than traditional methods for every data set and the success of DNN classifiers should not only be contributed to the effectiveness of neural networks, but also task-specific properties such as data structures, noise distributions, etc.
As a result, almost all generalization error bounds are vacuous and often don’t reflect the actual generalization performance
(
Dziugaite and Roy 2017
;
Jiang et al. 2019
)
.
Without involving the concept of minimax optimality, such bounds may provide little guidance for practical applications.

There is a rich literature on minimax optimal estimation using DNNs
(
Schmidt-Hieber 2020
;
Schmidt-Hieber 2019
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Kohler et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2020
)
, under which
task-specific
and
statistical optimal
results can be derived.
By making specific assumptions on the data, the performance of an estimation method can be sharply characterized by the convergence rate of the estimation error (upper bound).
In the mean time, a task-specific lower bound on the convergence rate can also be derived, independent of the estimation method.
When the convergence rate of a certain method matches the lower bound, statistical optimality is achieved.
Furthermore, for high-dimensional structured data, if the convergence rate is “dimension-free” in the sense that it doesn’t depend on the original data dimension
d
, but on an effective dimension
d^{*}\ll d
, the “curse of dimensionality” can be alleviated.
It is interesting to explore whether minimax optimal DNN approaches with dimension-free properties can be established in the classification setting.
Existing literature on this front is limited, and most works either treat classification as regression by estimating the conditional class probability instead of the decision boundary
(
Kohler et al. 2020
;
Kohler and Langer 2020
;
Bos and Schmidt-Hieber 2021
;
Hu et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022b
;
Wang et al. 2022a
;
Wang and Shang 2022
)
or settle for an upper bound on the misclassification risk
(
Kim et al. 2021
;
Steinwart et al. 2007
;
Hamm and Steinwart 2020
)
.
Unlike regression problems where one intends to estimate the unknown regression functions, the goal of classification is to recover the unknown
decision boundary
separating different classes. Properties of the decision boundary are critical and the analysis is more demanding.
In literature, the smooth decision boundary assumption and Tsybakov’s low noise condition
(
Mammen and Tsybakov 1999
)
are commonly adopted, whereas minimax optimal DNN classifiers under these assumptions are nonexistent, not even for simpler classification methods such as support vector machine (SVM)
(
Hamm and Steinwart 2020
)
.
There is a clear gap between DNN classifier’s empirical success and its suboptimality.
In this work, we aim to address the issue of rate suboptimality in the smooth boundary setting and also provide new insights to how DNNs avoid the “curse of dimensionality".
The main contributions of this paper are:

•

By dissecting the 0-1 loss excess risk of DNN classifiers, we identify the potential origin of the suboptimality.
Motivated by this, we propose a new deep learning classifier using a divide-and-conquer technique: DNN classifiers are constructed on each local region and then aggregated to a global one.
We further propose a novel
localized
version of the classical Tsybakov’s noise condition, which enables us to develop both lower and upper bounds on the convergence rate and establish statistical optimality for DNN classifiers with proper architectures, i.e., depth, width, sparsity, etc.

•

In the proposed localized smooth decision boundary setting, we show that DNN classifiers can adapt to low-dimensional structures underlying the high-dimensional data and circumvent the “curse of dimensionality" in the sense that the optimal rate of convergence only depends on some effective dimension
d^{*}
, potentially much smaller than the data dimension.

This work is
the first
to establish the
minimax optimal
convergence rate for DNN classifiers under the classical smooth decision boundary setting, with a novel localized Tsybakov’s noise condition.
The proposed separation condition facilitates a finer-grained understanding of classification, which, DNN classifiers can take full advantage of by utilizing the divide-and-conquer strategy.
The optimality proof relies on delicate constructions of DNNs where the representation power, structural flexibility, composition nature all play critical roles.
The ability to adapt to low-dimensional data structures while achieving the optimal convergence rate showcases the power of deep learning and provides insights for understanding DNN’s empirical success in classifying high-dimensional data.

2
Preliminary

Let
\mathbb{Q}
be the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For any function
f(\bm{x}):\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, denote

\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}|f(\bm{x})|
and
\|f\|_{p}=(\int_{\mathcal{X}}|f(\bm{x})|^{p}d\bm{x})^{1/p}
for
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
For a vector
\bm{x}
,
\|\bm{x}\|_{p}
denotes its
p
-norm, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
.

L_{p}
and
l_{p}
are used to distinguish function norms and vector norms.
For two given sequences of real numbers
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we write
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}
for all sufficiently large
n
. Let
\Omega(\cdot)
be the counterpart of
O(\cdot)
that
a_{n}=\Omega(b_{n})
means
a_{n}\gtrsim b_{n}
.
In addition, we write
a_{b}\asymp b_{n}
if
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
and
a_{n}\gtrsim b_{n}
.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, denote
a\vee b=\max\{a,b\}
and
a\wedge b=\min\{a,b\}
.
We use
\mathbb{I}
to denote the indicator function.
For a set
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denote
\partial G
as its boundary and
G^{\circ}
as its interior.
For two sets
G_{1},G_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let their symmetric difference be
d_{\triangle}(G_{1},G_{2})=\mathbb{Q}(G_{1}\triangle G_{2})=\mathbb{Q}\left((G_{1}\backslash G_{2})\cup(G_{2}\backslash G_{1})\right).

2.1
Binary classification

Denote
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the feature vector and
y\in\{-1,1\}
as the label.
Assume the classes are balanced, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(y=1)=1/2
, and

\bm{x}\ |\ y=1\sim p(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\ |\ y=-1\sim q(\bm{x}),

where
p,q
are two bounded densities on
\mathcal{X}
.
The goal of classification is to find the optimal classifier
C^{*}
with the lowest expected
0-1 loss

R(C)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}[\mathbb{I}\{C(\bm{x})\neq y\}].

C^{*}
corresponds to the optimal decision region
G^{*}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X},p(\bm{x})-q(\bm{x})\geq 0\}
by assigning label 1 if
\bm{x}\in G^{*}
and label
-1
otherwise.
Equivalently, we can consider real-valued functions
f
and classification can be done by
C(\bm{x})=\mbox{sign}(f(\bm{x}))
.
With a slight abuse of notation, we use
R(C),R(G)
and
R(f)
interchangeably.

Without knowing the data distribution,
C^{*}
is not directly calculable.
Instead, we observe data set
\mathcal{D}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and estimate
C^{*}
by the empirical risk minimizer, i.e.,

\widehat{C}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}R_{n}(C),\mbox{ where }R_{n}(C)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{C(\bm{x}_{i})\neq y_{i}\}.

In practice, the empirical 0-1 loss minimizer is not computationally feasible because minimizing such loss over
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is NP hard
(
Bartlett et al. 2006
)
.
An alternative approach is to replace the 0-1 loss with other computationally friendly surrogate losses
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, e.g. hinge loss
\phi(z)=(1-z)_{+}=\max\{1-z,0\}
, logistic loss
\phi(z)=\log(1+\exp(-z))
, etc.
Even though surrogate losses are used in practice, 0-1 loss gives the most fundamental characterization of the classification problem and is of great theoretical interest.
This work focuses on 0-1 loss and surrogate losses such as hinge loss, cross-entropy are reserved for future work.

The performance of an estimated classifier
\widehat{C}
can be evaluated by its
excess risk
defined as

\mathcal{E}(\widehat{C},C^{*})=R(\widehat{C})-R(C^{*}),

which measures the closeness between
\widehat{C}
and
C^{*}
in terms of the expected 0-1 loss.
Convergence rate refers to how fast
\mathcal{E}(\widehat{C},C^{*})
converges to zero with respect to
n
and it is the focus on this paper.
Knowing the relationship between
C^{*}
and
G^{*}
, classification can also be seen as nonparametric estimation of sets, and the 0-1 excess risk of an estimator
\widehat{G}
can be written as

\mathcal{E}(\widehat{G},G^{*})=d_{p,q}(\widehat{G},G^{*})=\int_{\widehat{G}\triangle G^{*}}|p(\bm{x})-q(\bm{x})|\mathbb{Q}(d\bm{x}).

There are two key factors governing the rate of convergence in classification: the complexity of the decision boundary and how concentrated the data are near the decision boundary, which we will introduce in Section
2.2
and
2.3
respectively.

2.2
Smooth boundary assumption

In statistics literature, one of the most common boundary assumptions is called the smooth boundary fragments
(
Mammen and Tsybakov 1999
;
Tsybakov 2004
;
Korostelev and Tsybakov 2012
)
.
Recall that a function has Hölder smoothness index
\beta
if all partial derivatives up to order
\lfloor\beta\rfloor
exist and are bounded and the partial derivatives of order
\lfloor\beta\rfloor
are
\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor
Hölder continuous, where
\lfloor\beta\rfloor
denotes the largest integer strictly smaller than
\beta.
The ball of
\beta
-Hölder functions with radius
R
is then defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(d,\beta,R)=\Big\{

\displaystyle f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}:

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{\alpha}:|\bm{\alpha}|<\beta}\|\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}f\|_{\infty}+\sum_{\bm{\alpha}:|\bm{\alpha}|=\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\,\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bm{x},\bm{y}\in D}}{{\bm{x}\neq\bm{y}}}}\frac{|\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}f(\bm{x})-\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}f(\bm{y})|}{|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|_{\infty}^{\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}\leq R\Big\},

(2.1)

where we use multi-index notation, i.e.,
\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}=\partial^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots\partial^{\alpha_{r}}
with
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
and
|\bm{\alpha}|:=|\bm{\alpha}|_{1}.

R
controls the radius of the function space and as long as
R<\infty
, the function space doesn’t fundamentally change. For simplicity, we omit the radius and write
\mathcal{H}(d,\beta,R)
as
\mathcal{H}(d,\beta)
.

For
d\geq 2
, let
\bm{x}_{-d}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d-1})
.
The smooth boundary fragment setting assumes the optimal set
G^{*}
to have the form

\displaystyle G_{f^{*}}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:f^{*}(\bm{x}_{-d})-x_{d}\geq 0,f^{*}\in\mathcal{H}(d,\beta)\}.

(2.2)

Denote
\mathcal{G}^{*}_{\beta}=\{G_{f^{*}}:f^{*}\in\mathcal{H}(d,\beta)\}
to be all such sets (hypothesis class).
Due to the smoothness of
f^{*}
, the complexity of the hypothesis class
\mathcal{G}^{*}_{\beta}
, which is usually measured by
bracketing entropy
, can be controlled. To be more specific, for any
\delta>0
, the bracketing number
\mathcal{N}_{B}(\delta,\mathcal{G},d_{\triangle})
is the minimal number of set pairs
(U_{j},V_{j})
such that

(a)

For each
j
,
U_{j}\subset V_{j}
and
d_{\triangle}(U_{j},V_{j})\leq\delta
;

(b)

For any
G\in\mathcal{G}
, there exists a pair
(U_{j},V_{j})
such that
U_{j}\subset G\subset V_{j}
.

Simply denote
\mathcal{N}_{B}(\delta)=\mathcal{N}_{B}(\delta,\mathcal{G},d_{\triangle})
if no confusion arises.
The bracketing entropy is defined as
H_{B}(\delta)=\log{\mathcal{N}_{B}(\delta,\mathcal{G},d_{\triangle})}
. In the smooth boundary fragment case,
Mammen and Tsybakov 1999
showed that

H_{B}(\delta,\mathcal{G}_{\beta}^{*},d_{\triangle})\lesssim\delta^{-\frac{d-1}{\beta}}.

For more flexibility, the boundary fragments assumption can be extended to be more general that
G^{*}
consists of unions and intersections of smooth hyper-surfaces defined as in (
2.2
)
(
Kim et al. 2021
;
Tsybakov 2004
)
.
In this work, we focus on the basic case (
2.2
) for simplicity.

2.3
Separation assumption

The following Tsybakov’s noise condition
(
Mammen and Tsybakov 1999
)
quantifies how separated
p
and
q
are near the decision boundary:

(N)

There exists constants
c,T>0
and
\kappa\in[0,\infty]
such that for any
0\leq t\leq T
,

\mathbb{Q}\left(\{\bm{x}:|p(\bm{x})-q(\bm{x})|\leq t\}\right)\leq ct^{\kappa}.

(N) is a population-level assumption and
\kappa
is referred to as the noise exponent. (N) trivially holds for
\kappa=0
. The bigger the
\kappa
, the more separated the data around the decision boundary, and hence, the easier the classification.
In the extreme case where
p,q
have different supports,
\kappa
can be arbitrarily large (
\infty
) and the boundary recovery is the easiest. To another extreme where
\mathbb{Q}\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}:p(\bm{x})=q(\bm{x})\}>0
, there exists a region where different classes are indistinguishable. In this case,
\kappa=0
and the optimal decision boundary is hard to estimate in that region.

Under the smooth boundary fragment assumption (
2.2
) with smoothness
\beta
and the Tsybakov’s noise condition (N) with noise exponent
\kappa
,
Mammen and Tsybakov 1999
showed that the optimal rate of convergence for the 0-1 loss excess risk is

\inf_{C_{n}\in\mathcal{C}}\sup_{G^{*}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}_{\beta}}\mathcal{E}(C_{n},G^{*})=\Omega\left(n^{-\frac{\beta(\kappa+1)}{\beta(\kappa+2)+(d-1)\kappa}}\right),

(2.3)

where
\mathcal{C}
can be any classifier family.
Note that the “curse of dimensionality" does occur in this bound. As
d
gets larger, the rate becomes extremely slow.

2.4
DNN in classification

We consider DNN with rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation that
\sigma(z)=\max\{z,0\}
. For a ReLU neural network
f_{n}
indexed by the sample size, let
L_{n}
be the number of layers,
N_{n}
be the maximum width of all the layers,
S_{n}
be the total number of non-zero weights,
B_{n}
be maximum absolute values of all the weights.
Denote
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\mathcal{F}(L_{n},N_{n},S_{n},B_{n})
as a DNN family with structural constraints specified by
L_{n},N_{n},S_{n},B_{n}
. In this work, depth
L_{n}
and width
N_{n}
are the primary focuses.

Convergence rate of DNN classifiers has been investigated in literature.

Kim et al. 2021
derived fast convergence rates of ReLU DNN classifiers learned using the hinge loss (
\phi(z)=\max(0,1-z)
).
Under the smooth boundary fragment assumption (
2.2
) and Tsybakov’s noise condition (N), the empirical hinge loss minimizer

\widehat{f}_{\phi,n}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(y_{i}f(\bm{x}_{i})),

within some DNN family with carefully selected
L_{n},N_{n},S_{n},B_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{G^{*}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}(\widehat{f}_{\phi,n},G^{*})\right]\lesssim\left(\frac{\log^{3}n}{n}\right)^{\frac{\beta(\kappa+1)}{\beta(\kappa+2)+(d-1)(\kappa+1)}}.

(2.4)

Their result is rate
suboptimal
comparing to the minimax lower bound (
2.3
).
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the only existing convergence rate result for DNN classifiers under the smooth boundary setting.
The same suboptimal rate is referred to as “best known rates" for SVM
(
Hamm and Steinwart 2020
)
.
Other theoretical works on convergence rate of DNN classifiers are carried out under other assumptions, e.g., separable
(
Zhang 2000
;
Hu et al. 2021
)
, teacher student setting
(
Hu et al. 2020
)
, smooth conditional probability
(
Audibert et al. 2007
;
Steinwart et al. 2007
;
Kohler et al. 2020
)
, etc.
Classification by estimating the conditional probability is usually referred to as "plug-in" classifiers and it’s worth noting that it essentially reduces classification to regression. In comparison, estimating the decision boundary is a more fundamental setting for classification
(
Hastie et al. 2009
)
and it is the focus of this work.

3
Main results – optimal rate of convergence

Under the classical smooth boundary fragment setting, DNN classifiers have not been proved optimal in recovering the decision boundary, which casts doubt on the empirical success of DNNs in classification.
In this section, we first investigate the potential reason behind the suboptimality.

The noise exponent
\kappa
in the Tsybakov’s noise condition (N) plays a critical role in determining the convergence rate. In general, bigger
\kappa
implies better separation, and hence easier boundary recovery. However, if we take a closer look at the excess risk by decomposing it into the stochastic (empirical) error and the approximation error (using DNN to approximate smooth functions), bigger
\kappa
benefits the former but is harmful for the later (the approximation error is amplified by a power of
1+1/\kappa
as in Lemma
7.1
). In other words, the bottleneck for the stochastic error is the region with the smallest separation while that for the approximation error is the region with the largest separation.
If the separation along the decision boundary is inconsistent with sharp changes, the overall estimation performance of DNN classifiers could be harmed. The optimal rate of convergence can be achieved under extra separation consistency condition.
Complementary to the classical Tsybakov’s noise condition (N), which indicates that the separation is not too small, the following condition ensures that the separation cannot be too large as well.

(N
+
)

There exist constants
c_{1},T>0
and
\kappa\in[0,\infty]
such that for any
0\leq t\leq T
,

\mathbb{Q}\left(\{\bm{x}\in G:|p(\bm{x})-q(\bm{x})|\leq t\}\right)\geq c_{1}t^{\kappa}

holds for any positive-measure set
G\subset\mathcal{X}
containing the decision boundary, i.e.,
\partial G^{*}\cap G^{\circ}
is not empty.

If (N) and (N
+
) both hold with the same exponent
\kappa
, the separation is guaranteed to be consistent along the decision boundary.
The following theorem utilizes this extra condition and shows that optimal rate of convergence can be achieved for DNN classifiers.

Lemma 3.1
.

(Informal)

Under assumptions (N) and the smooth boundary fragment assumption (
2.2
), if we further assume (N
+
), then the empirical 0-1 loss minimizer within a ReLU DNN family with proper size achieves the optimal 0-1 loss excess risk convergence rate of
{n^{-\frac{\beta(\kappa+1)}{\beta(\kappa+2)+(d-1)\kappa}}}
.

Lemma
3.1
is a special case of Theorem
3.5
and the proof can be found in the Section
7.3
. The improvement to optimality stems from a better approximation error bound, which follows from the separation consistency guaranteed by condition (N
+
).
However, assuming identical separation over the whole support, i.e., the same
\kappa
for both (N) and (N
+
), is too strong.
It becomes much more realistic if we put this assumption on a small neighborhood, or a small segment of the decision boundary. If the densities
p,q
are not too irregular, the separation is expected to be locally consistent.

To this end, we conduct convergence analysis in a local region of the decision boundary and consider a localized version of the classical Tsybakov’s noise condition. For simplicity,
\mathcal{X}
is assumed to be
[0,1]^{d}
in the remaining part of the paper. Extension to compact
\mathcal{X}
is straightforward.

3.1
Localized separation condition

Recall the boundary assumption that
G^{*}=\{\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d}:f^{*}(\bm{x}_{-d})-x_{d}\geq 0\}
where
f^{*}
is some smooth function from
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
to
\mathbb{R}
. Denote the decision boundary to be
\partial G^{*}:=\{\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d}:f^{*}(\bm{x}_{-d})=x_{d}\}
.
Every point
\bm{x}\in\partial G^{*}
can be written as
\bm{x}=(\bm{x}_{-d},f^{*}(\bm{x}_{-d}))
.
Without loss of generality, assume
\mathbb{Q}(\partial G^{*})=0
.
Notice that even though
f^{*}
defines the decision boundary, it has nothing to do with the separation. The separation condition, e.g., (N), depends on properties of the data densities
p,q
around the decision boundary.
To quantify the separation, define for each
\bm{x}_{-d}\in[0,1]^{d-1}
,

m_{\bm{x}_{-d}}(t):=|p((\bm{x}_{-d},f^{*}(\bm{x}_{-d})+t))-q((\bm{x}_{-d},f^{*}(\bm{x}_{-d})+t))|,

which captures the how
|p(\bm{x})-q(\bm{x})|
changes along the direction of
x_{d}
on each point of the decision boundary.
For ease of notation, we write
m_{\bm{x}_{-d}}(t)
and
m_{\bm{x}}(t)
when no confusion arises.
Notice that
m_{\bm{x}}(0)=0
by definition and we want to characterize how
m_{\bm{x}}(t)
behaves when
t
is close to 0, i.e., close to the decision boundary.
Moreover, for every
\bm{x}\in\partial G^{*}
, define the localized separation exponent

K(\bm{x})=\sup\{k\geq 0:\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{m_{\bm{x}}(t)}{|t|^{1/k}}>0\}.

K(\bm{x})
characterizes the separation condition locally at
\bm{x}_{-d}
, i.e. how separated are
p
and
q
on each point of the decision boundary, along the direction of
x_{d}
.
For some
\bm{x}\in\partial G^{*}
, if the directional derivative of
p-q
tangent to the decision boundary is finite and non-zero, then
K(\bm{x})=1
.
Note that if
K(\bm{x})
is regular enough, e.g., continuous, Tsybakov’s noise condition (N) with exponent
\kappa
implies that
\kappa\leq\inf_{\bm{x}\in\partial G^{*}}K(\bm{x})
and (N
+
) implies
\kappa\geq\sup_{\bm{x}\in\partial G^{*}}K(\bm{x})
.
Consider the following separation conditions which form a localized version of the Tsybakov’s noise condition.

(M1)

There exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
small enough and a constant
0<C_{\epsilon_{0}}<\infty
such that for all
\bm{x}\in\partial G^{*}
and any
0<t<\epsilon_{0}
,

\frac{1}{C_{\epsilon_{0}}}\leq\frac{m_{\bm{x}}(t)}{|t|^{1/K(\bm{x})}}\leq C_{\epsilon_{0}}.

(M2)

K(\bm{x})
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for some
0<\alpha\leq 1
, i.e.
there exists constant
C_{K}
such that for any
\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}\in\partial G^{*}
,

|K(\bm{x}_{1})-K(\bm{x}_{2})|\leq C_{K}\|\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}\|_{2}^{\alpha}.

Illustration of the localized separation condition (M1) when
d=2
. The blue plane represents
\mathcal{X}
and the curved blue line is the optimal decision boundary. Fix some
(x_{1},x_{2})
in the decision boundary. Along the
x_{1}
direction (black line), the density difference
p-q
is plotted in the green plane as the green line. (M1) defines the noise exponent
K(x_{1})
locally at
x_{1}
. If
p-q
is linear as shown,
K(x_{1})=1
.

Remark 3.2

(Justification of (M1,M2))
.

The proposed separation condition is a localized version of the classical Tsybakov’s noise condition (N) where (M1) corresponds to (N) in a local region and (M2) ensures consistency among nearby regions.
(M1,M2) is stronger than (N), only because it’s a finer characterization of (N) and we expect it to hold if the data distribution is not too irregular.

Many existing settings are special examples of (M1,M2)
, e.g., in the separable case,
K(\bm{x})\equiv\infty
;
if
p,q
are piecewise linear, then (M) holds with
K(\bm{x})\equiv 1
;
if
p,q
are Hölder smooth with smoothness more than 2, e.g., Gaussian mixtures, then (M1,M2) holds and
K(\bm{x})\leq 1
.
Like (N), our condition is more of theoretical interest and hard to verify for real data. Still, the developed results can contribute to the understanding of DNN’s empirical success in classification, e.g., how they can take advantage of better class separation and low-dimensional data structures.

(M1,M2) is a slightly stronger characterization of the boundary separation.
The following lemma shows that (M1) implies (N) and (N
+
). The proof can be found in Section
7
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Denote
\kappa^{-}=\inf_{\bm{x}\in\partial G^{*}}K(\bm{x})
and
\kappa^{+}=\sup_{\bm{x}\in\partial G^{*}}K(\bm{x})
. Then condition (M1) implies that (N) holds with
\kappa=\kappa^{-}
and (N
+
) holds with
\kappa=\kappa^{+}
.

Although (M1,M2) and (N) are closely related, the fundamentals of the classification problem, measured by the convergence lower bound, might be different.
Below we investigate the 0-1 loss excess risk lower bound under our proposed separation condition.

Theorem 3.4
.

Under the smooth boundary fragments setting (
2.2
) with smoothness
\beta
. Assume condition (M1) and let
\kappa^{-}=\inf_{\bm{x}\in\partial G^{*}}K(\bm{x})
. For any function space
\mathcal{F}
, the 0-1 loss excess risk has the following lower bound,

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}\in\mathcal{F}}\sup_{G^{*}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\widehat{f},G^{*})]\gtrsim\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\frac{\beta(\kappa^{-}+1)}{\beta(\kappa^{-}+2)+(d-1)\kappa^{-}}}.

The convergence lower bound under (M1) only depends on
\kappa^{-}
and has the same form compared to (
2.3
) under (N). This is expected since (M1,M2) is a localized version of (N).

3.2
Localized convergence analysis

Defining function
K(\bm{x})
enables us to consider local convergence behaviours. As can be seen from Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.3
, if the separation described by
K(\bm{x})
is consistent, i.e.,
\kappa^{+}\approx\kappa^{-}
, the convergence rate can be improved to be optimal. Such consistency is expected within any small-enough local region due to condition (M2).
To further investigate the interplay between
\kappa^{+}
and
\kappa^{-}
, we conduct localized convergence analysis in this section.

There are many ways to construct local regions. Since we are considering the smooth boundary fragment setting (
2.2
), where
x_{d}
is a special dimension, a natural choice for the local regions is equal-sized grids in the
\bm{x}_{-d}
space.
Choose integer
M>0
and divide
[0,1]^{d}
into
M^{d-1}
regions

[0,1]^{d}=\bigcup_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d-1}=1}^{M}D_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{d-1})},

where
D_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{d-1})}:=\{\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d}:x_{1}\in[\frac{j_{1}-1}{M},\frac{j_{1}}{M}),\cdots,x_{d-1}\in[\frac{j_{d-1}-1}{M},\frac{j_{d-1}}{M}]\}
. Denote the grid points to be
\bar{\bm{x}}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d-1}}
.
For ease of notation, let
\bm{j}_{-d}=(j_{1},\cdots,j_{d-1})
and denote
J_{M}
as all
M^{d-1}
combinations of
{\bm{j}_{-d}}
’s described above.
Correspondingly, divide the data set as
\mathcal{D}=\cup_{\bm{j}_{-d}\in J_{M}}\mathcal{D}_{\bm{j}_{-d}}
where
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{j}_{-d}}=\{(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}:\bm{x}\in D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}\}
.
Similarly, the 0-1 loss can be decomposed into

\displaystyle d_{p,q}(\widehat{G}_{n},G^{*})=

\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{G}_{n}\triangle G^{*}}|p(\bm{x})-q(\bm{x})|d\bm{x}=\sum_{\bm{j}_{-d}\in J_{M}}\int_{(\widehat{G}_{n}\triangle G^{*})\cap D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}}|p(\bm{x})-q(\bm{x})|d\bm{x}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{j}_{-d}\in J_{M}}d_{\bm{j}_{-d}}(\widehat{G}_{n},G^{*}).

The empirical 0-1 loss,
R_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}{\{f(\bm{x}_{i})y_{i}<0\}}
, can also be decomposed into
M^{d-1}
parts, i.e.,
R_{n}(G)=\sum_{\bm{j}_{-d}\in J_{M}}R_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
where

R_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\bm{j}_{-d}}|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}{\{\bm{x}_{i}\in D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}:f(\bm{x}_{i})y_{i}<0\}}.

Now we focus on each local region
D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}
.
Similar to that in the whole region
[0,1]^{d}
, we have the following theorem on the local 0-1 loss excess risk convergence rate.

Theorem 3.5
.

Under assumption (M1), further assume that for some
\bm{j}_{-d}\in J_{M}
,
\kappa^{-}\leq K(\bm{x})\leq\kappa^{+}
for all
\bm{x}\in D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}
.
Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
be a ReLU DNN family
1
1

1

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
is used to denote DNN family for local estimation.

is reserved for the global estimator DNN family.
with size in the order of

\widetilde{N}_{n}\widetilde{L}_{n}\asymp{n^{\ \frac{\kappa^{+}(\kappa^{-}+1)(d-1)/2}{(\kappa^{-}+2)(\kappa^{+}+1)\beta+(d-1)\kappa^{+}(\kappa^{-}+1)}}}\cdot\log^{2}(n).

Let the empirical 0-1 loss minimizer be

\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}}:=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}R_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(f).

(3.1)

Then the 0-1 loss excess risk satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{G^{*}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}(R_{{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}})-R_{{\bm{j}_{-d}}}({G^{*}}))=\widetilde{O}\left(n^{-\frac{(\kappa^{-}+1)\beta}{(\kappa^{-}+2)\beta+\mathbf{\left(\frac{\kappa^{-}+1}{\kappa^{+}+1}\right)}(d-1)\kappa^{+}}}\right),

Note that (

Remark 3.6

(Origin of sub-optimality)
.

Our local convergence rate in Theorem
3.5
closely resembles those established under the original Tsybakov’s noise condition (N).
On one hand, the convergence rate bottleneck is indeed the minimum value of
K(\bm{x})
in that region and
\kappa^{-}
plays the same role as
\kappa
in (N).
On the other hand, the extra term in the denominator
(\kappa^{-}+1)/(\kappa^{+}+1)
reveals the potential
source of the suboptimality
of existing results. If no assumption is made on
\kappa^{+}
, one allows
\kappa^{+}\to\infty
so that
\kappa^{+}/(\kappa^{+}+1)\to 1
, the convergence rate reduces to the suboptimal one (
2.4
) obtained in
Kim et al. 2021
and
Hamm and Steinwart 2020
. However, if
\kappa^{+}=\kappa^{-}
, i.e.,
K(\bm{x})
is constant, optimal rate is attainable.
This is consistent with our numerical findings in which the regular DNN classifier performs well when
K(\bm{x})
is constant; see Figure
5
.
Condition (M2) says that
K(\bm{x})
is locally smooth, i.e.,
\kappa^{+}\approx\kappa^{-}
as long as the local region is small.

In order to achieve the fastest convergence rate, Theorem
3.5
requires
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
to have proper size. If
\kappa^{-}=\kappa^{+}=\kappa
, the size constraint is

\widetilde{N}_{n}\widetilde{L}_{n}=\widetilde{O}\left(n^{\ \frac{\kappa(d-1)/2}{(\kappa+2)\beta+(d-1)\kappa}}\right).

The bigger the
\kappa
, the larger the required size, and the faster the convergence rate.
In case the size constraint is not met, the the following corollary gives the corresponding convergence results.

Corollary 3.7
.

Under the same setting as Theorem
3.5
, denote
r_{0}=\frac{\kappa^{+}(\kappa^{-}+1)(d-1)}{(\kappa^{-}+2)(\kappa^{+}+1)\beta+(d-1)\kappa^{+}(\kappa^{-}+1)}
and let
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
’s size satisfy

\widetilde{N}_{n}\widetilde{L}_{n}=\Omega(n^{r/2})
for some
0<r<1
.
If
r<r_{0}
, the approximation error dominates and the 0-1 loss excess risk convergence rate becomes

\widetilde{O}\left(n^{-\frac{r\beta(\kappa^{+}+1)}{(d-1)\kappa^{+}}}\right)
. If
r>r_{0}
, the stochastic error dominates and the convergence rate is

\widetilde{O}\left(n^{-\frac{(1-r)(\kappa^{-}+1)}{\kappa^{-}+2}}\right)
.

3.3
Construction of the global estimator

In this section, we proceed from a localized analysis to the global one and evaluate the overall excess risk convergence rate.
The goal is to construct a global classifier that takes advantage of the developed results for each region
D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}
, such that Theorem
3.5
can be applied locally.
To be more specific, denote the properly sized network family (according to Theorem
3.5
) for each region as
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
, with width
\widetilde{N}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
and depth
\widetilde{L}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
be the DNN family for global estimation, with depth
L_{n}
and width
N_{n}
. Denote the global empirical minimizer within
\mathcal{F}_{n}
to be

\widehat{f}_{n}:=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}R_{n}(f).

(3.2)

Then, it’s ideal if for any
\bm{j}_{-d}\in J_{M}
,
\widehat{f}_{n}
satisfies

R_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}({\widehat{f}_{n}})=\min_{f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}}R_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}({f}).

(3.3)

Note that
\widehat{f}_{n}
degenerates to the regular DNN classifier when
M=1
.
For general
M>1
, we aim to construct
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
in a divide-and-conquer fashion such that (
3.3
) is satisfied with high probability.
Below we present the construction of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and the probabilistic argument.

Recall the definition of
D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}
, where we divide
[0,1]^{d}
along
\bm{x}_{-d}
into
M^{d-1}
equally sized regions.
For some
0<\xi\ll 1/M
, define a small region around all grid points
\bar{\bm{x}}_{\bm{j}_{-d}}
’s as

\displaystyle D_{\xi}=\{\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d}:\|\bm{x}_{-d}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{\bm{j}_{-d}}\|_{\infty}\leq\xi,\ \bm{j}_{-d}\in J_{M}\}.

D_{\xi}
with
d=2,M=2
is illustrated in Figure
2
. We aim to show that (
3.3
) holds outside
D_{\xi}
for some carefully chosen
\xi
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}
.
To this end, define event

E_{\xi}:=\{\bm{x}_{i}\notin D_{\xi}:\forall i=1,2,\ldots,n\}.

Since
p(\bm{x})
and
q(\bm{x})
are both bounded densities (by
c_{0}
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x\in D_{\xi})

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\mathbb{Q}(D_{\xi})\leq 2c_{0}M\xi(d-1).

Therefore, if we choose
M
such that
nM\xi(d-1)\to 0
as

, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{\xi})

\displaystyle\geq(1-2c_{0}M\xi(d-1))^{n}\to 1.

In the remaining of the analysis, we assume that
E_{\xi}
happens.
For any
f_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
, we make modifications and further construct
{f}^{+}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
that satisfies the following properties:

(P1)

On
D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}\backslash D_{\xi}
,
{f}^{+}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}={f}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
;

(P2)

Outside
D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}
,
{f}^{+}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}=0
;

(P3)

{f}^{+}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{+}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
where
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{+}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
is slightly larger than
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
with depth
\widetilde{L}^{+}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}=\widetilde{L}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}+O(1)
and width
\widetilde{N}^{+}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}=2\widetilde{N}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
.

The construction details and verification of (P1) to (P3) are deferred to Section
7.4
.
Let’s proceed with the properties of
{f}^{+}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{+}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}
.
By (P2),
{f}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}^{+}
is zero outside
D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}
and we can combine them together to define

\displaystyle{f}_{n,\Sigma}(\bm{x}_{-d})=\sum_{\bm{j}_{-d}\in J_{M}}{f}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}^{+}(\bm{x}_{-d}).

(3.4)

Easy to see that
{f}_{n,\Sigma}(\bm{x})
is still a ReLU network.
Correspondingly, define such structured DNN family to be

stacked in parallel for

Overall, the size of
\mathcal{F}_{n}

L_{n}\lesssim\max_{\bm{j}_{-d}}\{\widetilde{L}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}\}

Recall that a larger

Thus the requirement on

is mainly determined by the region with the biggest

Due to the formulation of

,

can be written in the form of (

\widehat{f}_{n}=\sum_{\bm{j}_{-d}\in J_{M}}\widehat{f}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}

Denote

to

\widetilde{f}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}}\|f-f^{*}\|_{\infty}

Under event

\displaystyle R_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}({\widehat{f}_{n}})=R_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}(\widehat{f}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}})=\min_{f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}}R_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}({f})\leq R_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}(\widetilde{f}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}).

and property (P1).

3.4
Global convergence analysis

In this section, we evaluate the convergence rate of the global classifier
\widehat{f}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
.
Through our construction process, we have shown that for any
\bm{j}_{-d}\in J_{M}
, (
3.3
) satisfies under
E_{\xi}
.
Intuitively, even though
K(\bm{x})
can vary along the decision boundary, the convergence rate will be dominated by the region around
\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{x}}K(\bm{x})
. We have the following theorem on the overall convergence rate.

Theorem 3.9
.

Under the smooth boundary fragments setting (
2.2
),
assume conditions (M1,M2). Denote
\kappa^{-}=\inf_{\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d}}K(\bm{x}),\kappa^{+}=\sup_{\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d}}K(\bm{x})
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
be a ReLU DNN family with proper architectures specified in Section
3.3
and size constraint

{N}_{n}{L}_{n}=O\left(n^{\ \frac{\kappa^{+}(d-1)/2}{(\kappa^{+}+2)\beta+(d-1)\kappa^{+}}}\cdot\log^{d+1}(n)\right).

Then, with probability tending to one, the empirical 0-1 loss minimizer within
\mathcal{F}_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\sup_{G^{*}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}(R(\widehat{f}_{n})-R({G^{*}}))=\widetilde{O}\left(n^{-\frac{(\kappa^{-}+1)\beta}{(\kappa^{-}+2)\beta+(d-1)\kappa^{-}}}\right).

Notice that the size constraint on
N_{n}L_{n}
only concerns
\kappa^{+}
while the final convergence rate only depends on
\kappa^{-}
. This is consistent with Remark
3.6
where we state that the bottleneck for convergence is
\kappa^{-}
while that for approximation is
\kappa^{+}
. The “with high probability" argument comes from the
E_{\xi}
, which is an artifact of the proof and may be relaxed.

The convergence rate in Theorem
3.9
matches the lower bound in Theorem
3.4
and is statistically optimal up to a logarithmic term.
Combining Theorems
3.5
and
3.9
,
we conclude that, when
K(\bm{x})
is highly non-constant in the sense that
\kappa^{+}\to\infty
, the proposed localized classifier
\widehat{f}_{n}
has faster rate of convergence than the regular DNN classifier.

4
Main results – breaking the “curse of dimensionality"

High-dimensional data are often structured. Taking images as an example, nearby pixels are often highly correlated while distant ones are more independent. The support of
d
-dimensional real images is speculated to be degenerate since not every combination of pixel values is relatable to real-life objects. To understand why deep learning models are not struggling in ultra-high dimensions, it is of great importance to investigate how well they adapt to various structures underlying the data.
To this end, many low-dimensional structures have been investigated, e.g., low-dimensional manifold
(
Schmidt-Hieber 2019
;
Hamm and Steinwart 2020
)
, hierarchical interaction max-pooling model
(
Kohler et al. 2020
)
, teacher student setting
(
Hu et al. 2020
)
, etc.

In this section, as a proof of concept, we focus on a particular structural assumption – compositional smoothness structure
(
Schmidt-Hieber 2020
)
– and show that DNN classifiers can adapt to this low-dimensional structure and optimal convergence rate that only depends on the effective dimension is achievable.

4.1
Smooth boundary with compositional structure

Recall the smooth boundary fragment setting (
2.2
). Instead of
\beta
-smooth, we assume
f^{*}
to be compositions of smooth functions.
This assumption is first considered in
Schmidt-Hieber 2020
for the regression function and here we are borrowing it for classification.
Assume
f^{*}
is of the form

f^{*}=h_{l}\circ h_{q-1}\circ\ldots\circ h_{1}\circ h_{0}

(4.1)

where each
h_{i}:[a_{i},b_{i}]^{d_{i}}\rightarrow[a_{i+1},b_{i+1}]^{d_{i+1}}
and denote the components of

h_{i}
by
\{h_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{d_{i+1}}
. Let
t_{i}
be the maximal number of variables
h_{ij}
’s depend on. Thus, each
h_{ij}
is a
t_{i}
-variate function.
We further assume that each function
h_{ij}
shares the same Hölder smoothness
\beta_{i}.

Define

\bar{\beta}_{i}:=\beta_{i}\prod_{j=i+1}^{l}(\beta_{j}\wedge 1),\quad\quad i^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{i=0,1,\cdots,l}\ n^{-\frac{2\bar{\beta}_{i}}{2\bar{\beta}_{i}+t_{i}}}.

The overall effective smoothness and effective dimension of
f^{*}
in (
4.1
) can be described by
\beta^{*}=\bar{\beta}_{i^{*}}
and
d^{*}=t_{i^{*}}
, respectively.
In this setting,
n^{-{2\beta^{*}}/({2\beta^{*}+d^{*}})}
is proven to be the best possible square loss estimation rate from regression
(
Schmidt-Hieber 2020
)
.
Let
\mathcal{C}(d^{*},\beta^{*})
be the corresponding classifier class.
The compositional smoothness is a generalization of the regular smoothness. If
f^{*}(\bm{x})
is a
(d-1)
-dimensional
\beta
-smooth function as in (
2.2
), then
l=0,\beta^{*}=\beta,d^{*}=d-1
.

4.2
Optimal rate of convergence

We first establish the following convergence rate lower bound under the proposed setting.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume condition (M1) with noise exponent
\kappa=\inf_{\bm{x}\in\partial G^{*}}K(\bm{x})
and the compositional smoothness structure (
4.1
) on the boundary fragment assumption (
2.2
). The 0-1 loss excess risk has the following lower bound

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}_{n}}\sup_{C^{*}\in\mathcal{C}(d^{*},\beta^{*})}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\widehat{f}_{n},C^{*})]\gtrsim\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\frac{\beta^{*}(\kappa+1)}{\beta^{*}(\kappa+2)+d^{*}\kappa}}.

The convergence rate lower bound adapts to the compositional smoothness structure and only depends on
\beta^{*}
and
d^{*}
.
Next, we evaluate how well DNNs can recover the decision boundary.

To handle the compositional smoothness, we utilize the corresponding ReLU DNN family in
Schmidt-Hieber 2020
as our building block and go through the same construction process as in Section
3.3
.
To distinguish from the regular case in Section
3

∗

Locally, similar to the requirement in Theorem
3.5

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}^{*}

\widetilde{N}_{n}^{*}\asymp n^{\ \frac{\kappa^{+}(\kappa^{-}+1)d^{*}}{(\kappa^{-}+2)(\kappa^{+}+1)\beta^{*}+d^{*}\kappa^{+}(\kappa^{-}+1)}},\quad\widetilde{S}_{n}^{*}\asymp n^{\ \frac{\kappa^{+}(\kappa^{-}+1)d^{*}}{(\kappa^{-}+2)(\kappa^{+}+1)\beta^{*}+d^{*}\kappa^{+}(\kappa^{-}+1)}}\cdot\log(n),

which is sparsely connected.
Similarly, we extend
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}^{*}
to
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n,\bm{j}_{-d}}^{+*}
and stack them together to get
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{*}
for global estimation as in Section
3.3
.
Similar to Theorem
3.9
, the DNN family
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{*}
in Theorem

is also constructed with structures and sparsely-connected. The size requirement is slightly different comparing to that in Theorem

Theorem 4.2
.

Under the compositional smoothness setting (

Let

\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}1

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{*}}\sup_{C^{*}\in\mathcal{C}(d^{*},\beta^{*})}\mathbb{E}(R(\widehat{f}_{n})-R({C^{*}}))=\widetilde{O}\left(n^{-\frac{(\kappa^{-}+1)\beta^{*}}{(\kappa^{-}+2)\beta^{*}+\kappa^{-}d^{*}}}\right).

To further illustrate the power of our Theorem
4.2

\displaystyle f^{*}(\bm{x}_{-d})=\sum_{i<d}f^{*}_{i}(x_{i})=h_{1}\circ h_{0},

(4.2)

where
h_{0}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d-1})=(g_{1}(x_{1}),\cdots,g_{d-1}(x_{d-1}))

In this case,
l=1,\bm{d}=(d-1,d-1),\bm{t}=(1,d-1)
. Assume each

has the same smoothness

Corollary 4.3
.

Under the same setting as in Theorem

) on the boundary function. Then the excess risk convergence rate becomes

The compositional smoothness assumption (

In our localized analysis, various other low-dimensional structural assumptions on the decision boundary can be utilized to break the “curse of dimensionality", e.g., low-dimensional manifold support
(

, low intrinsic dimension
(

, etc.
We conjecture that as long as DNNs can adapt to these assumptions in the regression setting and achieve optimal convergence rates, such adaptions can be potentially extended to the smooth boundary fragment classification setting.

6
Discussion

This work aims to close the gap between DNN’s empirical success and its rate suboptimality in recovering smooth decision boundaries with Tsybakov’s noise condition (N).
Through a finer-grained analysis of the convergence rate, we uncover a potential source of the suboptimality, i.e., inconsistency of the boundary separation.
Correspondingly, we propose a new DNN classifier using a divide-and-conquer technique and a novel separation condition that is a localized version of the classical (N), under which optimal convergence rates are established for DNN classifiers with proper architectures. The established statistical optimality can adapt to low-dimensional structures underlying the data and in turn, may explain the absence of the “curse of dimensionality" in practice.

However, there are many limitations of this work.
First, the nonparametric framework analyzes properties of the empirical risk minimizer, e.g., convergence rate, sample complexity, etc., but doesn’t provide guidance to practical optimization and how to find them.
Second, the proposed localized separation condition, like Tsybakov’s noise condition, is of theoretical interest and cannot be verified for real data such as natural images. Similarly, this work only considers the 0-1 loss, which is also out of theoretical interest rather than practical relevance since it’s the most natural and fundamental classification loss.
For future work, it is intriguing to study how DNN classifiers perform under popular surrogate losses such as hinge loss or cross-entropy.
Our numerical experiments conducted using cross-entropy indicate similar results may also hold for empirical surrogate loss minimizers.
Our theory can be further strengthened if we could relax the structural requirement for the DNN family to be more general, e.g, fully-connected, or extend the analysis to popular networks such as convolutional neural networks
(
Krizhevsky et al. 2012
)
, residual neural networks
(
He et al. 2016
)
, and Transformers
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
.

We believe that the proposed localized separation condition and the corresponding localized analysis are general and potentially transferable to other classification methods, as long as they can take advantage of the divide-and-conquer strategy.
We hope more investigations can be inspired along this line.

Technical details

This paper studies classification under the smooth boundary fragment setting (
2.2
) with Tsybakov’s noise condition
(

)
.
First, we provide proof for Lemma
3.3
, which characterizes the relationship between the classical (N) and the proposed intermediate (N
+
).

Proof of Lemma
3.3

Proof.

Recall the definition of
K(\bm{x})
and condition (M1) that there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
and
0<C_{\epsilon_{0}}<\infty
such that for all
\bm{x}\in\partial G^{*}
and any
0<t<\epsilon_{0}
,

\frac{1}{C_{\epsilon_{0}}}\leq\frac{m_{\bm{x}}(t)}{|t|^{1/K(\bm{x})}}\leq C_{\epsilon_{0}}.

The key idea is to treat
\bm{x}_{-d}
and
x_{d}
separately. For any
0<t<{\epsilon_{0}^{1/\kappa^{+}}}/{C_{\epsilon_{0}}}
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d}:|p(\bm{x})-q(\bm{x})|\leq t)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{I}\{|p(\bm{x})-q(\bm{x})|\leq t\}dx_{d}d\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathbb{I}\{m_{\bm{x}_{-d}}(u)\leq t\}dud\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{I}\{\frac{u^{1/K(\bm{x})}}{C_{\epsilon_{0}}}\leq t\}dud\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{I}\{{u^{1/\kappa^{-}}}\leq tC_{\epsilon_{0}}\}dud\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle=2\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}{(C_{\epsilon_{0}}t)^{\kappa^{-}}}d\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle=2{C_{\epsilon_{0}}t}^{\kappa^{-}}.

The first equality is by definition. The second inequality is due to condition (M1), which indicates that locally,
m_{\bm{x}}\geq{\epsilon_{0}^{1/K(x)}}/{C_{\epsilon_{0}}}\geq{\epsilon_{0}^{1/\kappa^{+}}}/{C_{\epsilon_{0}}}.

Now consider (N
+
) and a set
G\subset[0,1]^{d}
containing part of the decision boundary.
Notice that only lower bound is concerned so if suffices to prove the inequality for a subset
\subset G
. Without loss of generality, let the subset be
[0,\epsilon_{G}]^{d}
.
The analysis is similar to that in
[0,1]
and
for
t
small enough we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\bm{x}\in G:|p(\bm{x})-q(\bm{x})|\leq t)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,\epsilon_{G}]^{d-1}}\int_{-\epsilon_{G}}^{\epsilon_{G}}\mathbb{I}\{m_{\bm{x}_{-d}}(u)\leq t\}dud\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{[0,\epsilon_{G}]^{d-1}}\int_{-{\epsilon_{G}}}^{\epsilon_{G}}\mathbb{I}\{C_{\epsilon_{0}}{u^{1/K(\bm{x})}}\leq t\}dud\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{[0,\epsilon_{G}]^{d-1}}\int_{-{\epsilon_{G}}}^{\epsilon_{G}}\mathbb{I}\{{u^{1/\kappa^{+}}}\leq t/C_{\epsilon_{0}}\}dud\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle\geq C_{G}t^{\kappa^{+}},

where
C_{G}
is some constant depending on the set
G
and
C_{\epsilon}
.
∎

Next, we prove some useful lemmas utilizing the proposed localized separation condition (M1,M2).
Let
G_{f}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}:f(\bm{x}_{d})-x_{d}\geq 0\}
. Then
we have the following lemma characterizing the relationship between
d_{\triangle}
and
d_{p,q}
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Under assumption (M1), further assume on some
D\subset\mathcal{X}
,
0<\kappa^{-}\leq K(\bm{x})
for all
\bm{x}\in D
. For any set
G=G_{f}\subset D

satisfying
\|f-f^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{0}
, the following inequality holds

d_{\triangle}(G,G^{*})^{\frac{\kappa^{-}+1}{\kappa^{-}}}\lesssim d_{p,q}(G,G^{*}).

Proof.

Let
\delta(\bm{x}_{-d}):=|f(\bm{x}_{-d})-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{-d})|\leq\epsilon_{0}
.
Consider
G\triangle G^{*}
in dimension
x_{d}
and
\bm{x}_{-d}
separately and write
G\triangle G^{*}=\left((G\triangle G^{*})_{-d},(G\triangle G^{*})_{d}\right)
. Then

\displaystyle d_{\triangle}(G,G^{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{(G\triangle G^{*})_{-d}}\int_{(G\triangle G^{*})_{d}}dx_{d}d\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle=\int_{(G\triangle G^{*})_{-d}}\delta(\bm{x}_{-d})d\bm{x}_{-d}

Applying assumption (M1) and Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle d_{p,q}(G,G^{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{G\triangle G^{*}}|p(\bm{x})-q(\bm{x})|d\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{(G\triangle G^{*})_{-d}}\int_{0}^{\delta({\bm{x}_{-d}})}m_{\bm{x}}(t)dtd\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{(G\triangle G^{*})_{-d}}\int_{0}^{\delta({\bm{x}_{-d}})}\frac{1}{C_{\epsilon_{0}}}t^{1/\kappa^{-}}dtd\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{C_{\epsilon_{0}}(1+1/\kappa^{-})}\int_{(G\triangle G^{*})_{-d}}{\delta({\bm{x}_{-d}})^{\frac{\kappa^{-}+1}{\kappa^{-}}}}d\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{C_{\epsilon_{0}}(1+1/\kappa^{-})}d_{\triangle}(G,G^{*})^{\frac{\kappa^{-}+1}{\kappa^{-}}}

∎

Convergence rate proof road map:

As stated before, the excess risk can be decomposed into the stochastic (empirical) error and the approximation error.
As DNN classifiers get larger, the variance becomes larger while the bias gets smaller.
An optimal trade-off can be achieved by carefully choosing the size of the DNN function space, or equally, the approximation error.
The better the characterization of the two types of error, the tighter the convergence upper bound.
In the following, we investigate these two aspects separately.

7.1
Approximation error

DNNs are universal approximators
(
Cybenko 1989
)
.
Approximating the smooth function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{H}(d,\beta)
in (
2.2
) using a DNN family
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
, has been well-established in literature. Note that here we use
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
to distinguish from the overall DNN classifier family
\mathcal{F}
.
Among others
(
Yarotsky 2017
;
Kohler and Langer 2021
)
,

Lu et al. 2020
show that if
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
is large enough with depth
\widetilde{L}=O(L\log(L))
and width
\widetilde{N}=O(N\log(N))
, the
L_{\infty}
-approximation error is in the order of
O(N^{-2\beta/d}L^{-2\beta/d})
;

Schmidt-Hieber 2020
proves that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a neural network
\widetilde{f}_{n}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
with
\widetilde{L}_{n}=O(\log n)
layers and

\widetilde{S}_{n}=O(\epsilon^{-d/\beta}\log n)
non-zero weights such that

\|\widetilde{f}_{n}-f^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
. The detailed theorems are listed below and they cover all the cases considered in this paper.
It’s worth emphasizing that approximation of smooth functions is not the focus of this work. We aim to better utilize existing approximation results to sharpen the bias bound of the excess risk.

Lemma 7.2

(Theorem 1.1 in
(
Lu et al. 2020
)
)
.

Given a smooth function
f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}(d,\beta)
with
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, for any
N,L\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, there exists a function
f
implemented by a ReLU DNN with width
C_{1}(N+2)\log_{2}(8N)
and depth
C_{2}(L+2)\log_{2}(4L)+2d
such that

\|f-f_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leq C_{3}\|f\|_{C^{s}([0,1]^{d})}N^{-2s/d}L^{-2s/d},

where
C_{1}=17s^{d+1}3^{d}d
,
C_{2}=18s^{2}
, and
C_{3}=85(s+1)^{d}8^{s}
.

Lemma 7.3

(Approximation Part of Theorem 1 in
Schmidt-Hieber 2020
)
.

Consider the
d
-variate nonparametric regression model for composite regression function
f_{0}
in the class
\mathcal{H}(l,\bm{d},\bm{t},\bm{\beta},R).
There exists
\widetilde{f}_{n}
in the network class
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{\textup{DNN}}(L_{n},N_{n},S_{n},B_{n},F_{n})
with
L_{n}\lesssim\log_{2}n
,

B_{n}=1
,

F_{n}\geq\max(R,1)
,

N_{n}\lesssim\max_{i=0,\cdots,q}n^{\frac{t_{i}}{2\bar{\beta}_{i}+t_{i}}},\quad S_{n}\lesssim\max_{i=0,\cdots,q}n^{\frac{t_{i}}{2\bar{\beta}_{i}+t_{i}}}\log n.

such that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{f}_{n}-f_{0}\|_{\infty}\lesssim n^{-{\beta^{*}}/({2\beta^{*}+d^{*}})}.

Notice that the approximation error bounds in the above lemmas are almost rate-optimal. Detailed descriptions and proofs can be found in the referenced papers.
Other neural network approximation results can also be potentially used in our localized analysis as long as the approximation rate is optimal up to a logarithmic term.

Two types of smooth functions are considered, one is general Hölder smooth function and the other is compositional smooth function.
Since the former is a special case of the latter, most of the proofs are done on the more general compositional smooth setting (
4.1
).

7.2
Stochastic error

The techniques for controlling the stochastic error are from literature on
empirical process
where covering/bracketing number/entropy are widely used to upper bound the empirical error. More details can be found in
van de Geer 2000
;
Kosorok 2007
.

However, there are difficulties when it comes to DNNs. A subtle difference in analyzing neural networks lies in their complexity measurement. To be more specific, for some real-value function space
\mathcal{F}
, let the covering entropy be
H(\delta,\mathcal{F},L_{\infty})
. The typical entropy bound is of the form
A\delta^{-\rho}
where
A,\rho
are some constants, e.g., for
d
-variant
\beta
-smooth functions,
\rho=d/\beta
.
In comparison, that for DNNs is of the form
A\log(1/\delta)
where
A=O(N^{2}L^{2})
, which diverges with network size.

Lemma 7.4

(Lemma 5 of
Schmidt-Hieber 2020
; Lemma 3 of
Suzuki 2018
)
.

For any
\delta>0
, a ReLU network family
\mathcal{F}
satisfies

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F}(L,N,S,B),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\leq 2L(S+1)\log(\delta^{-1}(L+1)(N+1)(B\vee 1)).

The difference makes existing results not directly applicable and non-trivial modifications have to be made. Below, we prove some empirical error bounds specifically for DNNs.
Following the notations from
van de Geer 2000
and
Tsybakov 2004
,
let

v_{n}(h)=\sqrt{n}\int h(\bm{x})d(P_{n}-P),

where
P
denotes the data distribution, i.e.
\bm{x}\sim P
and
P_{n}
denotes the empirical distribution of
\bm{x}_{1},\cdots,\bm{x}_{n}
.

Lemma 7.5

(Theorem 5.11 in
van de Geer 2000
)
.

For some function space
\mathcal{H}
with
\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\|h(\bm{x})\|_{\infty}\leq K
and
\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\|h(\bm{x})\|_{L_{2}(P)}\leq R
.
If
a>0
satisfies:
(1)
a\leq C_{1}\sqrt{n}R^{2}/K
;
(2)
a\leq 8\sqrt{n}R
;

(3)\quad a\geq C_{0}\left(\int_{a/64\sqrt{n}}^{R}H_{B}^{1/2}(u,\mathcal{F},L_{2}(P))du\vee R\right);

and (4)
C_{0}^{2}\geq C^{2}(C_{1}+1)
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\sqrt{n}\int hd(P_{n}-P)\right|\geq a\right)\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}}{C^{2}(C_{1}+1)R^{2}}\right),

where
P_{n}
is the empirical counterpart of
P
.

The next lemma investigates the modulus of continuity of the empirical process. It’s similar to Lemma 5.13 in
van de Geer 2000
but with a key difference in the entropy assumption (
7.1
), where the entropy bound contains
n
.

Lemma 7.6
.

For a probability measure
P
, let
\mathcal{H}_{n}
be a class of uniformly bounded (by 1) functions

in
L_{2}(P)
depending on
n
. Suppose that the
\delta
-entropy with bracketing satisfies for all

small enough,
the inequality

\displaystyle H_{B}(\delta,\mathcal{H}_{n},L_{2}(P))\leq A_{n}\log(1/\delta),

(7.1)

where
0<A_{n}=o(n)
.
Let
h_{0n}
be a fixed element in
\mathcal{H}_{n}
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{n}(\delta)=\{h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}:\|h_{n}-h_{0n}\|_{L_{2}(P)}\leq\delta\}
.
Then there exist constants
D_{1}>0,D_{2}>0
such that for a sequence of i.i.d. random variables
\bm{x}_{1},\cdots,\bm{x}_{n}
with probability distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}(\sqrt{{A_{n}}/{n}})}\left|\int(h_{n}-h_{0n})d(P_{n}-P)\right|\geq T{\frac{A_{n}}{{n}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{TA_{n}}{8C^{2}}\right)

and for
n

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{n};\\

\displaystyle\leq D_{2}e^{-A_{n}x}

for all

.

Proof.

The main tool for the proof is Lemma

. Replace
\mathcal{H}
with
\mathcal{H}_{n}(\delta)
in Lemma

and take

,

and

a=\frac{1}{2}C_{1}A_{n}^{1/2}\delta

Then (1) is satisfied if

\delta\geq\sqrt{\frac{A_{n}}{n}},

(7.2)

under which, (2) and (3) is trivially satisfied when

Choosing

sufficiently large will ensure (4). Thus, for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}(\delta)}\left|\sqrt{n}\int(h_{n}-h_{0n})d(P_{n}-P)\right|\geq\frac{C_{1}}{2}{A_{n}}^{1/2}\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{C_{1}A_{n}}{16C^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{n};\\

if

∎

7.3
Localized convergence analysis

Let
\rho=\frac{d-1}{\beta}

Proof of Theorem
3.5

Proof.

For ease of notation, we will write

For any

\displaystyle d_{\bm{j}_{-d}}(\widetilde{f}_{n},f^{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}:G_{\widetilde{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}}}\triangle G_{f^{*}}}|p(\bm{x})-q(\bm{x})|d\bm{x}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}}\int_{0}^{\epsilon}m_{\bm{x}}(t)dtd\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\epsilon_{0}}\int_{D_{\bm{j}_{-d}}}\int_{0}^{\epsilon}t^{1/K(\bm{x})}dtd\bm{x}_{-d}

\displaystyle\leq{\frac{C_{\epsilon_{0}}}{M^{d-1}(1+1/\kappa^{+})}\epsilon^{\frac{\kappa^{+}+1}{\kappa^{+}}}}

Since
\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}}
is the empirical risk minimizer within

, we have
R_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}})\leq R_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}}({\widetilde{f}_{n}})
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle d_{{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}},f^{*})\leq

\displaystyle d_{{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(\widetilde{f}_{n},f^{*})+[R_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(\widetilde{f}_{n})-R_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(f^{*})-d_{\bm{j}_{-d}}(\widetilde{f}_{n},f^{*})]

\displaystyle+[R_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(f^{*})-R_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}})+d_{\bm{j}_{-d}}(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}},f^{*})]

\displaystyle:\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\epsilon_{0}}}{M^{d-1}(1+1/\kappa^{+})}\epsilon^{\frac{\kappa^{+}+1}{\kappa^{+}}}+I(\widetilde{f}_{n},f^{*})+I(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}},f^{*}).

For

, we have

\displaystyle I(\widetilde{f}_{n},f^{*})\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}:\|f-f^{*}\|_{1}\\

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{A_{n}d_{\triangle}(\widetilde{f}_{n},f^{*})}{{n}}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}:\|f-f^{*}\|_{1}\\

is from the assumption (

).

\displaystyle=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{A_{n}}{{n}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{A_{n}d_{\triangle}(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}},f^{*})}{{n}}}\ O_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

By construction,

. Hence

\displaystyle d_{{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}},f^{*})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\epsilon_{0}}}{M^{d-1}(1+1/\kappa^{+})}\epsilon^{\frac{\kappa^{+}+1}{\kappa^{+}}}+O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{A_{n}}{{n}}\right)+

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{A_{n}\left(d_{\triangle}(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}},f^{*})+\epsilon\right)}{{n}}}\ O_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

The last term dominates the second term if

.

. Then the first term

Omitting the approximation error, i.e.

by Lemma

\displaystyle d_{{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}},f^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{A_{n}}{{n}}}d^{1/2}_{\triangle}(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}},f^{*})\ O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{A_{n}}{{n}}}d_{{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}},f^{*})^{\frac{\kappa^{-}}{2(\kappa^{-}+1)}}\ O_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

which simplifies to

\displaystyle d_{{\bm{j}_{-d}}}(\widehat{f}_{n,{\bm{j}_{-d}}},f^{*})=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left({\frac{{A_{n}}}{n}}\right)^{\frac{\kappa^{-}+1}{\kappa^{-}+2}}.

From Lemma

\displaystyle c_{2}\eta^{\kappa}

After verifying
(f_{\omega},g_{0})\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{G}_{h}}
for all
\omega\in\Omega
, we now establish how fast can

S:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}_{1}|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{f}[d_{\triangle}(\widetilde{G}_{m,n},G^{*})|y_{1},\cdots,y_{m}]

go to zero. To this end, we use the Assouad’s lemma stated in
(
Korostelev and Tsybakov 2012
)

which is adapted to the estimation of sets.

For
j=1,\cdots,M
and for a vector
\omega=(\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{M})
, we write

\displaystyle\omega_{j0}=(\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{j-1},0,\omega_{j+1},\cdots,\omega_{M})

\displaystyle\omega_{j1}=(\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{j-1},1,\omega_{j+1},\cdots,\omega_{M})

For
i=0
and
i=1
, let
P_{ji}
be the probability measure corresponding to the distribution of
x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}
when the underlying density is
f_{\omega_{ji}}
. Denote the expectation w.r.t.
P_{ji}
as
\mathbb{E}_{ji}
. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{j}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}:\frac{1}{2}+\widetilde{b}_{\omega_{j0}}(\bm{x})<x_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\widetilde{b}_{\omega_{j1}}(\bm{x})\}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}:b_{\omega_{j0}}(x_{1})<\left(x_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1/B}\leq b_{\omega_{j1}}(x_{1})\}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}:b_{\omega_{j0}}(x_{1})<\left(x_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1/B}\leq b_{\omega_{j0}}(x_{1})+\psi_{j}(x_{1})\}

Then

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{M}Q\{\mathcal{D}_{j}\}\int\min\{dP_{j1},dP_{j0}\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{j}(x_{1})^{B}dx_{1}\int\min\{dP_{j1},dP_{j0}\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}\int\phi(Mt)^{B}dt\int\min\{dP_{j1},dP_{j0}\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}\int\phi(Mt)^{B}dt\left[1-H^{2}(P_{10},P_{11})/2\right]^{n}

where
H(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Hellinger distance.

\displaystyle H^{2}(P_{10},P_{11})=

\displaystyle\int\left[\sqrt{f_{\omega_{10}}(\bm{x})}-\sqrt{f_{\omega_{11}}(\bm{x})}\right]^{2}d\bm{x}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\Bigg\{\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}+\psi_{1}(x_{1})^{B}}\left[1-\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\frac{1}{2}+\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}-x_{2}}{c_{2}}\right)^{1/\kappa}}\right]^{2}dx_{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\left[\sqrt{1-b_{3}(\omega_{10})}-\sqrt{1-b_{3}(\omega_{11})}\right]^{2}dx_{2}\Bigg\}dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{(\tau M^{-\beta^{*}})^{B}-\psi_{1}(x_{1})^{B}}^{(\tau M^{-\beta^{*}})^{B}}\left[1-\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{v}{c_{2}}\right)^{1/\kappa}}\right]^{2}dvdx_{1}

\displaystyle+|b_{3}(\omega_{10})-b_{3}(\omega_{11})|^{2}

For the first term,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}-\psi_{1}(x_{1})^{B}}^{\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}}\left[1-\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{v}{c_{2}}\right)^{1/\kappa}}\right]^{2}dvdx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}-\psi_{1}(x_{1})^{B}}^{\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}}\left(\frac{v}{c_{2}}\right)^{2/\kappa}dvdx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa c_{2}^{-2/\kappa}}{\kappa+2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}\right)^{1+2/\kappa}-\left(\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}-\psi_{1}(x_{1})^{B}\right)^{1+2/\kappa}dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa c_{2}^{-2/\kappa}}{\kappa+2}\left(\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}\right)^{1+2/\kappa}\int\left(1-(1-\phi(Mt)^{B})^{1+2/\kappa}\right)dt

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(M^{-\beta^{*}(1+2/\kappa)-1}\right)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{1/2}^{1/2+b_{\omega}(x_{1})^{B}}\left[\frac{\frac{1}{2}+\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}-x_{2}}{c_{2}}\right]^{1/\kappa}dx_{2}dx_{1}=b_{3}(\omega)\left[\frac{1}{2}-b_{\omega}(x_{1})^{B}\right]

yields

\displaystyle b_{3}(\omega_{11})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}-b_{\omega_{11}}(x_{1})^{B}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{1/2}^{1/2+b_{\omega_{11}}(x_{1})^{B}}\left[\frac{\frac{1}{2}+\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}-x_{2}}{c_{2}}\right]^{1/\kappa}dx_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Mc_{2}^{-1/\kappa}}{\frac{1}{2}-\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}(1-\phi(Mx_{1}))}^{\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}}u^{1/\kappa}dudx_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{Mc_{2}^{-1/\kappa}\tau^{B}}{(\frac{1}{2}-\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}})(1+1/\kappa)}M^{-\beta^{*}(1+1/\kappa)}\int(1-(1-\phi(Mt)^{B})^{1+1/\kappa})dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{2}^{-1/\kappa}\tau^{B}}{(\frac{1}{2}-\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}})(1+1/\kappa)}M^{-\beta^{*}(1+1/\kappa)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(M^{-\beta^{*}(1+1/\kappa)})

Hence
|b_{3}(\omega_{11})-b_{3}(\omega_{10})|=\mathcal{O}(M^{-\beta^{*}(1+1/\kappa)-1})
and we have

\displaystyle H^{2}(P_{10},P_{11})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(M^{-\beta^{*}(1+2/\kappa)-1}\vee M^{-\beta^{*}(2+2/\kappa)-2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(M^{-\beta^{*}(1+2/\kappa)-1}\right)

Now choose
M
as the smallest integer that is larger or equal to

n^{\frac{\kappa}{(2+\kappa)\beta^{*}+\kappa}}

Then we have
H^{2}(P_{10},P_{11})\leq C^{*}n^{-1}\left(1+o(1)\right)
for some constant
C^{*}
depending only on
\kappa,c_{2},\tau,\phi
and

\int\min\{dP_{j1},dP_{j0}\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\frac{C^{*}}{2}n^{-1}(1+o(1))\right]^{n}\geq C_{1}^{*}

for
n
large enough and
C_{1}^{*}
is another constant. Thus for
n
large enough,

\displaystyle S\geq\frac{1}{2}C_{1}^{*}\tau^{B}M^{-\beta^{*}}\int\phi(t)dt\geq C_{2}^{*}n^{-\frac{\kappa\beta^{*}}{(2+\kappa)\beta^{*}+\kappa}}

The constant
C_{2}^{*}
only depends on
\kappa,c_{2},\tau
and
\phi
.

Combining all the results so far yields that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\widetilde{G}_{m,n}}\sup_{(f,g)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{G}_{h}}}(n\wedge m)^{\frac{\beta^{*}\kappa}{\beta^{*}(\kappa+2)+d^{*}\kappa}}\mathbb{E}_{f,g}[d_{\triangle}(\widetilde{G}_{m,n},G^{*})]>0

holds when
d^{*}=1
. Using Lemma
7.9
, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\widetilde{G}_{m,n}}\sup_{(f,g)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{G}_{h}}}(n\wedge m)^{\frac{\beta^{*}(\kappa+1)}{\beta^{*}(\kappa+2)+d^{*}\kappa}}\mathbb{E}_{f,g}[d_{f,g}(\widetilde{G}_{m,n},G^{*})]>0

∎

Note that under the composition smoothness assumption on the decision boundaries, existing upper bounds are suboptimal. The sub optimality comes from the
\kappa
term in the denominator of the rates and the
\log n
term. Instead of
\kappa
as in the lower bound, we have
\kappa+1
in the upper bound.

7.7
Numerical experiment details

All experiments are conducted using PyTorch. The ReLU neural networks are coded with the
torch.nn
package, with the default initialization. Sample size is chosen to be 1000 for the experiments in Figure
5
.
The neural network training is done by stochastic gradient descent (initial learning rate=0.1, momentum=0.9, weight decay=0.001). The batch size is chosen to be 100. The total iteration number is 10000, with learning rate decayed by 1/10 every 2000 steps.
To make a fair comparison, we fix the random seed for the data generating process. The randomness comes from network initialization and batch selection. Test accuracy is evaluated by sampling one million test data points.
When producing Figure
5
, we choose
k=10
and
n=200,800,3200,12800
. Each point in the figure is an average from 10 replications.
