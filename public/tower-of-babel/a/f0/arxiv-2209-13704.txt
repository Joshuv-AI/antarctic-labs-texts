Satisfiability degrees for BCK-algebras
C. Matthew Evans

Abstract
. We investigate the satisfiability degree of some equations in finite BCK-algebras; that is, given a finite BCK-algebra and an equation in the language of BCK-algebras, what is the probability that elements chosen uniformly randomly with replacement satisfy that equation? Specifically we consider the equations for the excluded middle, double negation, commutativity, positive implicativity, and implicativity. We give a sufficient condition for an equation to have a finite satisfiability gap among commutative BCK-algebras, and prove that the law of the excluded middle has a gap of 1 3 \frac{1}{3} , while the positive implicative and implicative equations have gap 1 9 \frac{1}{9} . More generally, though, in the language of BCK-algebras, we show that double negation, commutativity, positive implicativity, and implicativity all fail to have finite satisfiability gap. We provide bounds for the probabilities in these cases.



1.
Introduction

A fascinating result of Gustafson
[
9
]
states that in a finite non-Abelian group
G
, the probability that two elements commute is at most
\frac{5}{8}
. There is a substantial literature about commuting probabilities in finite groups; we refer the reader to the survey
[
6
]
by Das, Nath, and Pournaki, and the bibliography contained therein. One can generalize the commuting probability for groups in two natural directions:

(1)

What can we say about commuting probabilities in other algebraic systems?

(2)

What is the probability that an algebraic system satisfies some specified first-order formula?

For an example of (1), MacHale
[
16
]
investigated commuting probability for finite rings. While less has been written about commuting probabilities in the case of rings, there is some recent work in
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
, and
[
1
]
.

There are several examples of (2) in the case of groups. Probabilities for some commutator-like equations were considered in
[
7
]
and
[
15
]
, while
[
14
]
studies probabilities for some equations generalizing the commutativity equation.

A recent paper of Bumpus and Kocsis
[
4
]
generalizes in both directions by considering probability questions for the class of Heyting algebras, which are the algebraic semantics for intuitionistic logic. Inspired by their work, the present paper considers probabilities for some equations in the language of BCK-algebras, a class of algebraic structures introduced by Imai and Iséki
[
10
]
which are the algebraic semantics for a non-classical logic having only implication. These probabilities are formalized as follows:

Definition 1.1
.

Given a first-order language
\mathcal{L}
, a finite
\mathcal{L}
-structure
M
, and an
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots x_{n})
in
n
free variables, the quantity

\ds(\varphi,M)=\frac{|\,\{\,(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in M^{n}\mid\varphi(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots a_{n})\,\}|}{|M|^{n}}

is the
degree of satisfiability
of the formula
\varphi
in the structure
M
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
T
be a theory over a first-order language
\mathcal{L}
and
\varphi
an
\mathcal{L}
-formula in
n
free variables. We say that
\varphi
has
finite satisfiability gap

\varepsilon
in
T
if there is a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that, for every finite model
M
of
T
, either
\ds(\varphi,M)=1
or
\ds(\varphi,M)\leq 1-\varepsilon
.

Taking Gustasfon’s result as an example in the language of groups, the equation
xy=yx
has finite satisfiability gap
\frac{3}{8}
: every finite group either has degree of satisfiability 1 (if it is Abelian) or no larger than
\frac{5}{8}
.

The paper is structured as follows: in the next section we define the class of BCK-algebras and introduce the equations we will consider throughout the paper.

In section 3, we give sufficient conditions for an equation in the language of commutative BCK-algebras to have a finite satisfiability gap. In section 4 we consider two different equations in one variable: the law of the excluded middle and double negation. We show the law of the excluded middle has finite satisfiability gap using the result of section 3, but also show that double negation does not have finite satisfiability gap among bounded BCK-algebras. We find algebras realizing bounds on the probability for double negation. In section 5 we consider three different equations in two variables: commutativity, positive implicative, and implicative. None of these have a finite satisfiability gap in the language of BCK-algebras, and we gives algebras realizing bounds for these probabilities. However, the latter two equations do have finite satisfiability gap among commutative BCK-algebras.

Provided here is a summary of the various bounds discussed in the paper. Let
\mathbf{A}
be a BCK-algebra of order
n
. Let
\cd(\mathbf{A})
denote the commuting degree,
\dnd(\mathbf{A})
the double negation degree,
\pid(\mathbf{A})
the positive implicative degree, and
\id(\mathbf{A})
the implicative degree.

•

If
\mathbf{A}
is non-commutative, then

\frac{3n-2}{n^{2}}\leq\cd(\mathbf{A})\leq\frac{n^{2}-2}{n^{2}}\,.

•

If
\mathbf{A}
is non-commutative and bounded, then

\frac{2}{n}\leq\dnd(\mathbf{A})\leq\frac{n-1}{n}\,.

•

If
\mathbf{A}
is not positive implicative, then

\frac{4n-4}{n^{2}}\leq\pid(\mathbf{A})\leq\frac{n^{2}-1}{n^{2}}\,.

•

If
\mathbf{A}
is not implicative, then

\frac{4n-4}{n^{2}}\leq\id(\mathbf{A})\leq\frac{n^{2}-1}{n^{2}}\,.

•

If
\mathbf{A}
is linear but not positive implicative, then

\frac{n^{2}+3n-2}{2n^{2}}\leq\pid(\mathbf{A})\leq\frac{n^{2}-1}{n^{2}}\,.

•

If
\mathbf{A}
is linear but not implicative, then

\frac{n^{2}+3n-2}{2n^{2}}\leq\id(\mathbf{A})\leq\frac{n^{2}-1}{n^{2}}\,.

2.
Preliminaries

Definition 2.1
.

A
BCK-algebra
is an algebra
\mathbf{A}=\langle A;\boldsymbol{\cdot},0\rangle
of type
(2,0)
such that

(BCK1)

\bigl[(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}z)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(z\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=0

(BCK2)

\bigl[x\boldsymbol{\cdot}(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=0

(BCK3)

x\boldsymbol{\cdot}x=0

(BCK4)

0\boldsymbol{\cdot}x=0

(BCK5)

x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=0
and
y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x=0
imply
x=y
.

for all
x,y,z\in A
.

These algebras are partially ordered by:
x\leq y
if and only if
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=0
. Note that 0 is the least element by (BCK4). One can also show that
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}0=x
for all
x\in\mathbf{A}
. If the Hasse diagram for
\mathbf{A}
is a chain, we will say
\mathbf{A}
is
linear
. A
bounded BCK-algebra
is an algebra
\mathbf{A}=\langle A;\boldsymbol{\cdot},0,1\rangle
of type
(2,0,0)
such that
\langle A;\boldsymbol{\cdot},0\rangle
is a BCK-algebra and
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}1=0
for all
x\in A
.

Define a term operation
\wedge
by
x\wedge y:=y\boldsymbol{\cdot}(y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x)
. The element
x\wedge y
is a lower bound for
x
and
y
, but in general is not the greatest lower bound of
x
and
y
. As it turns out,
x\wedge y=\glb\{x,y\}
if and only if
x\wedge y=y\wedge x
. If this equation holds for all
x,y\in\mathbf{A}
, we say
\mathbf{A}
is
commutative
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is bounded, define the term operation
\neg x:=1\boldsymbol{\cdot}x
. While we always have
\neg\neg\neg x=\neg x
, in general
\neg\neg x\leq x
. That is, we cannot always eliminate a double negative in a bounded BCK-algebra.

Finally, if
\mathbf{A}
is bounded and commutative, define the term operation

x\vee y:=\neg(\neg x\wedge\neg y)\,.

We note that the term-reduct
\langle A;\wedge,\vee\rangle
is a distributive lattice
[
20
]
.

In this paper we consider the following equations:

(DN)

\neg\neg x=x

(EM)

x\vee\neg x=1

(T)

x\wedge y=y\wedge x

(E
1
)

x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}y

(I)

x\boldsymbol{\cdot}(y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x)=x

If
\mathbf{A}
satisfies (E
1
) we say it is
positive implicative
, and if
\mathbf{A}
satisfies (I) we say it is
implicative
. The classes of commutative, positive implicative, and implicative BCK-algebras are important subclasses of BCK-algebras; in fact, each of these subclasses is a variety. Further, a BCK-algebra is implicative if and only if it is both commutative and positive implicative. For more detail on the elementary properties of BCK-algebras – particularly the term operations
\neg
,
\wedge
, and
\vee
– we refer the reader to
[
12
,
13
,
17
]
.

We now give methods for constructing new BCK-algebras which will be used throughout the paper. Let
\{\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be a family of BCK-algebras such that
A_{\lambda}\cap A_{\mu}=\{0\}
for
\lambda\neq\mu
, and let
U
denote the union of the
A_{\lambda}
’s. Equip
U
with the operation

x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=\begin{cases}x\boldsymbol{\cdot}_{\lambda}y&\text{if $x,y\in A_{\lambda}$}\\
x&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

Then
U
is a BCK-algebra we will call the
BCK-union
of the
\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}
’s and denote it by
\mathbf{U}=\bigsqcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}
. This construction first appears in
[
12
]
, though only in the case
|\Lambda|=2
. For a full proof that this is a BCK-algebra, see
[
8
]
.

Next, given any BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
of order
n-1
, we construct a new BCK-algebra of order
n
by appending a new top element, call it
\top
, and extending the BCK-operation as follows:

\displaystyle x\boldsymbol{\cdot}\top

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\top\boldsymbol{\cdot}\top

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\top\boldsymbol{\cdot}x

\displaystyle=\top

for all
x\in\mathbf{A}
. This is known as

\mathbf{A}
, which we will denote
\mathbf{A}\oplus\top
. Iséki’s extension always yields a bounded non-commutative BCK-algebra having
\mathbf{A}
as a maximal ideal

]
.

3.
Sufficient conditions for finite satisfiability gap

In this section we give sufficient conditions for an equation to have a finite satisfiability gap in the language of commutative BCK-algebras. Suppose
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
is an equation in
k
free variables in the language of commutative BCK-algebras. For a finite BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, let

S(\mathbf{A})=\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathbf{A}^{k}\,\mid\,\varphi^{\mathbf{A}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\}

so that

\ds(\varphi,\mathbf{A})=\frac{|S(\mathbf{A})|}{|\mathbf{A}|^{k}}\,.

Lemma 3.1
.

Degree of satisfiability is multiplicative; that is,
\ds(\varphi,\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})=\ds(\varphi,\mathbf{A})\cdot\ds(\varphi,\mathbf{B})
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\bigl((a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(a_{k},b_{k})\bigr)\in S(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\varphi^{\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}}\bigl((a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(a_{k},b_{k})\bigr)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\bigl(\varphi^{\mathbf{A}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\,,\,\varphi^{\mathbf{B}}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k})\bigr)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in S(\mathbf{A})\text{ and }(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k})\in S(\mathbf{B})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\bigl((a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\,,\,(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k})\bigr)\in S(\mathbf{A})\times S(\mathbf{B})\,

which tells us
|S(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})|=|S(\mathbf{A})\times S(\mathbf{B})|=|S(\mathbf{A})||S(\mathbf{B})|
. Therefore,

\ds(\varphi,\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})=\frac{|S(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})|}{|\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}|^{k}}=\frac{|S(\mathbf{A})||S(\mathbf{B})|}{|\mathbf{A}|^{k}|\mathbf{B}|^{k}}=\ds(\varphi,\mathbf{A})\cdot\ds(\varphi,\mathbf{B})\,.

∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
\varphi
is an equation in the language of commutative BCK-algebras, and consider the sequence
d_{n}:=\ds(\varphi,\mathbf{C}_{n})
. Let

\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}=\{d_{m}\mid d_{m}<1\}\,.

If
\mathcal{D}\varphi
has a maximum, say
d_{m_{0}}
, then
\varphi
has finite satisfiability gap
\varepsilon=1-d_{m_{0}}
among commutative BCK-algebras.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite commutative BCK-algebra. By Theorem
2.2
we have

\mathbf{A}\cong\mathbf{C}_{j_{1}}\times\mathbf{C}_{j_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathbf{C}_{j_{k}}\,,

where each
j_{i}\geq 2
. Applying Lemma
3.1
, we have

\ds(\varphi,\mathbf{A})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}d_{j_{i}}\,.

If
\{d_{j_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{k}\cap\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}=\varnothing
, then
d_{j_{i}}=1
for
1\leq i\leq k
, and we have
\ds(\varphi,\mathbf{A})=1
.

On the other hand, if
\{d_{j_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{k}\cap\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}\neq\varnothing
, then for each
1\leq i\leq k
we have either
d_{j_{i}}=1
or
d_{j_{i}}\leq d_{m_{0}}
. So
\ds(\varphi,\mathbf{A})\leq d_{m_{0}}=1-\varepsilon
, and
\varphi
has finite satisfiability gap
\varepsilon
.
∎

4.
Equations in one variable

4.1.
Excluded middle degree

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}
is a bounded commutative BCK-algebra of order
n
and put

E(\mathbf{A})=\{\,x\in\mathbf{A}\,\mid\,x\vee\neg x=1\,\}\,.

Define the
excluded middle degree of
\mathbf{A}
, denoted
\emd(\mathbf{A})
, to be the degree of satisfiability of the equation
x\vee\neg x=1
; that is,

\emd(\mathbf{A})=\frac{|E(\mathbf{A})|}{n}\,.

This is just the probability that a randomly chosen element satisfies the law of the excluded middle.

It is easy to check that
0\vee\neg 0=1=1\vee\neg 1
, so
|E(\mathbf{A})|\geq 2
. It is known that
E(\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{A}
if and only if
\mathbf{A}
is positive implicative
[
13
]
, meaning
\emd(\mathbf{A})=1
if and only if
\mathbf{A}
is positive implicative. We show that if
\mathbf{A}
is not positive implicative, then
\emd(\mathbf{A})\leq\frac{2}{3}
. That is, the law of the excluded middle has finite satisfiability gap
\frac{1}{3}
in the language of bounded commutatutive BCK-algebras.

Lemma 4.1
.

\emd(\mathbf{C}_{n})=\frac{2}{n}

Proof.

As observed above, we have
\{0,n-1\}\in E(\mathbf{C}_{n})
. Take
k\in C_{n}\setminus\{0,n-1\}
. Then

k\vee\neg k=k\vee(n-1-k)=\max\{k,n-k-1\}\neq n-1

so
k\notin E(\mathbf{C}_{n})
. Thus,
|E(\mathbf{C}_{n})|=2
and
\emd(\mathbf{C}_{n})=\frac{2}{n}
.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

The equation
x\vee\neg x=1
has finite satisfiability gap
\frac{1}{3}
in the language of bounded commutative BCK-algebras.

Proof.

Recall the set
\mathcal{D}
defined in Theorem
3.2
. From Lemma
4.1
we have
d_{n}=\frac{2}{n}
. So
d_{2}=1
and
\mathcal{D}
has maximum
d_{3}=\frac{2}{3}
. Apply Theorem
3.2
.

The gap is realized by the algebra
\mathbf{C}_{3}
.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

In the language of bounded commutative BCK-algebras, the equations
x=1
and
\neg x=1
both have finite satisfiability gap
\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

We could prove this with an application of Theorem
3.2
, but we give here a simple argument. Every bounded commutative BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
has at least two elements, so

\ds(x=1,\mathbf{A})=\frac{|\{y\in\mathbf{A}\,\mid\,y=1\}|}{|\mathbf{A}|}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{A}|}\leq\frac{1}{2}\,.

Similarly, since double negation holds in bounded commutative BCK-algebras
[
13
]
, the equation
\neg x=1
holds if and only if
x=0
, and we have
\ds(\neg x=1,\mathbf{A})\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

Both gaps are realized by the algebra
\mathbf{2}
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

We include Proposition
4.3
in order to compare our situation with that of Heyting algebras discussed in
[
4
]
. In that paper, the authors prove that, up to logical equivalence, the only formulas in one variable that have finite satisfiability gap in the language of Heyting algebras are
x=1
,
\neg x=1
, and
x\vee\neg x=1
. To do this they use the characterization of the free Heyting algebra on one generator as the Rieger-Nishimura lattice.

It is known that bounded commutative BCK-algebras are term-equivalent to MV-algebras
[
18
]
, and the free
n
-generated MV-algebra
\mathcal{MV}_{n}
is (isomorphic to) the MV-algebra of McNaughton functions
[0,1]^{n}\to[0,1]

[
5
]
. However, even with this knowledge, it is not clear to the author how to characterize the formulas in one variable with finite satisfiability gap in the language of bounded commutative BCK-algebras.

The situation is more dire in the language of BCK-algebras – or even commutative BCK-algebras – as the author is unaware of any characterization of the free algebras in these cases.

4.2.
Double negation degree

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a bounded BCK-algebra of order
n
and put

D(\mathbf{A})=\{\,x\in\mathbf{A}\,\mid\,\neg\neg x=x\,\}\,.

Define the
double negation degree of
\mathbf{A}
, denoted
\dnd(\mathbf{A})
, to be the degree of satisfiability of the equation
\neg\neg x=x
; that is,

\dnd(\mathbf{A})=\ds(\text{DN},\mathbf{A})=\frac{|D(\mathbf{A})|}{n}\,.

This is just the probability that a randomly chosen element is fixed under double negation.

It is easy to check that
\neg\neg 0=0
and
\neg\neg 1=1
, so
|D(\mathbf{A})|\geq 2
. If
\mathbf{A}
is commutative then
D(\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{A}

[
13
]
, and so
\dnd(\mathbf{A})=1
. But if
\mathbf{A}
is non-commutative then we have

\frac{2}{n}\leq\dnd(\mathbf{A})\leq\frac{n-1}{n}\,.

We will show these bounds are sharp for
n\geq 3
. For example, one computes
\dnd(\mathbf{3}^{\text{p}})=\frac{2}{3}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

For each
n\geq 3
, there is a bounded non-commutative BCK-algebra
\mathbf{B}
of order
n
realizing the lower bound
\dnd(\mathbf{B})=\frac{2}{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be any BCK-algebra of order
n-1
and consider Iséki’s extension
\mathbf{A}\oplus\top
. Notice that

\neg\neg x=\top\boldsymbol{\cdot}(\top\boldsymbol{\cdot}x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if $x\in\mathbf{A}$}\\
\top&\text{ if $x=\top$}\end{cases}\,,

so only 0 and
\top
are fixed by double negation. Hence,
\dnd(\mathbf{A}\oplus\top)=\frac{2}{n}
.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

For each

\mathbf{A}

\dnd(\mathbf{A})=\frac{n-1}{n}

Corollary 4.7
.

The equation
\neg\neg x=x
has no finite satisfiability gap in the language of bounded BCK-algebras.

By Theorems
4.5
and
4.6
, we can make the double negation degree as close to 0 as we like or as close to 1 as we like. For an algebra of order
n
, the possible double negation degrees are

\Bigl\{\,\frac{2}{n}\,,\,\frac{3}{n}\,,\,\ldots\,,\,\frac{n-1}{n}\,,\,1\,\Bigr\}\,.

Empirically, the author has observed that for orders 3, 4, and 5, every possible double negation degree is obtained by some algebra. We conjecture this is true in general but do not yet have a proof.

Conjecture 4.8
.

For each
n\geq 3
, every possible double negation degree is obtained by some bounded BCK-algebra. Consequently, every rational in
\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1]
is the double negation degree of some BCK-algebra.

5.
Equations in two variables

5.1.
Commuting degree

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a BCK-algebra of order
n
. Let

C(\mathbf{A})=\{\,(x,y)\in A^{2}\,\mid\,x\wedge y=y\wedge x\,\}\,.

Define the
commuting degree
of
\mathbf{A}
, denoted
\cd(\mathbf{A})
, to be the degree of satisfiability of the commutativity equation
x\wedge y=y\wedge x
. That is,

\cd(\mathbf{A})=\frac{|C(\mathbf{A})|}{n^{2}}\,.

This is the probability that two elements chosen uniformly randomly (with replacement) commute with one another.

In any BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, we have
x\wedge 0=0\wedge x
for all
x\in\mathbf{A}
, and every element commutes with itself by (BCK3). So every pair of the form
(x,0)
,
(0,x)
, and
(x,x)
is in
C(\mathbf{A})
. Thus,
|C(\mathbf{A})|\geq 3n-2
and

\cd(\mathbf{A})\geq\frac{3n-2}{n^{2}}\,.

Of course, if
\mathbf{A}
is commutative then
\cd(\mathbf{A})=1
, but if
\mathbf{A}
is non-commutative we have

\frac{3n-2}{n^{2}}\leq\cd(\mathbf{A})\leq\frac{n^{2}-2}{n^{2}}\,.

We will show these bounds are sharp.

Suppose
\mathbf{U}=\bigsqcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}
is a BCK-union of a family of BCK-algebras. If
a,b\in\mathbf{U}
where
a\in\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}
and
b\in\mathbf{A}_{\mu}
with
\lambda\neq\mu
, then
a
and
b
necessarily commute:

\displaystyle a\wedge b

\displaystyle=b\boldsymbol{\cdot}(b\boldsymbol{\cdot}a)=b\boldsymbol{\cdot}b=0

\displaystyle b\wedge a

\displaystyle=a\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\boldsymbol{\cdot}b)=a\boldsymbol{\cdot}a=0\,.

Thus, heuristically, we expect that the commuting degree is larger when an algebra has many incomparable elements, and smaller when an algebra has many comparable elements. Indeed, we will see this is the case, but we note that if all non-zero elements are incomparable, then the underlying poset admits a unique BCK-product which is necessarily commutative
[
12
]
, so the commuting degree is 1 for such an algebra.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are BCK-algebras with
|\mathbf{A}|=n
,
|\mathbf{B}|=m
,
\cd(\mathbf{A})=\frac{k}{n^{2}}
, and
\cd(\mathbf{B})=\frac{\ell}{m^{2}}
. Then

\cd(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B})=\frac{k+\ell+2(n-1)(m-1)-1}{(n+m-1)^{2}}\,.

Proof.

First note that
|\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B}|=n+m-1
since the BCK-union shares a 0 element, but the algebras are otherwise disjoint.

Next, among the
k
commuting pairs in
\mathbf{A}
and the
\ell
commuting pairs in
\mathbf{B}
, the pair
(0,0)
is double counted, so
C(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B})\geq k+\ell-1
. But by our observations above, all elements of
\mathbf{A}
commute with all elements of
\mathbf{B}
. Thus, for all
x\in\mathbf{A}\setminus\{0\}
and all
y\in\mathbf{B}\setminus\{0\}
, the pairs
(x,y)
and
(y,x)
are in
C(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B})
. There are
2(n-1)(m-1)
such pairs.

Hence,

\cd(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B})=\frac{k+\ell+2(n-1)(m-1)-1}{(n+m-1)^{2}}\,.

∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a BCK-algebra with
|\mathbf{A}|=n
and
\cd(\mathbf{A})=\frac{k}{n^{2}}
. Then

\cd(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{2})=\frac{k+2n+1}{(n+1)^{2}}\,.

Proof.

The algebra
\mathbf{2}
is commutative, so apply Proposition
5.1
with
\ell=4
and
m=2
.
∎

For each
n\geq 3
, there is a non-commutative BCK-algebra of order
n
realizing the maximum commuting degree
\frac{n^{2}-2}{n^{2}}

Proof.

For

, recall the algebra

. The only non-commuting pairs are

and

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{n-1}\sqcup\mathbf{2}\text{ for $n>3$\,.}

For

Corollary 5.4

In the language of BCK-algebras, the equation

Next we consider the lower bound for commuting degree.

Proposition 5.5
.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
is a BCK-algebra with
|\mathbf{A}|=n
and
\cd(\mathbf{A})=\frac{k}{n^{2}}
. Then

\cd(\mathbf{A}\oplus\top)=\frac{k+3}{(n+1)^{2}}\,.

Proof.

We must have
(0,\top)
,
(\top,0)
, and
(\top,\top)
in
C(\mathbf{A}\oplus\mathbf{1})
, but note that
\top
does not commute with any non-zero element of
\mathbf{A}
:

\displaystyle x\wedge\top

\displaystyle=\top\boldsymbol{\cdot}(\top\boldsymbol{\cdot}x)=\top\boldsymbol{\cdot}\top=0\,,

\displaystyle\top\wedge x

\displaystyle=x\boldsymbol{\cdot}(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}\top)=x\boldsymbol{\cdot}0=x

for any non-zero
x\in\mathbf{A}
. Thus,
|C(\mathbf{A}\oplus\top)|=k+3
and
\cd(\mathbf{A}\oplus\top)=\frac{k+3}{(n+1)^{2}}
.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

For each
n\geq 3
, there is a BCK-algebra of order
n
realizing the minimum commuting degree
\frac{3n-2}{n^{2}}
.

Proof.

For
n=3
, the algebra
\mathbf{3}^{\text{p}}
has commuting degree
\frac{7}{9}
, as mentioned in the proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Define a new family of BCK-algebras as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{3}^{\text{p}}\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}_{n-1}\oplus\top\text{ for $n>3$\,.}

Assume
\cd(\mathbf{M}_{n-1})=\frac{3(n-1)-2}{(n-1)^{2}}
for some
n>3
. By Proposition
5.5
, we have

\cd(\mathbf{M}_{n})=\cd(\mathbf{M}_{n-1}\oplus\top)=\frac{\bigl(3(n-1)-2\bigr)+3}{n^{2}}=\frac{3n-2}{n^{2}}\,,

and the result follows by induction.
∎

Since the
\mathbf{M}_{n}
’s are linear, the above result verifies our intuition that commuting degree is small when there are many comparable elements.

To borrow terminology from
[
4
]
, one might say that the
\mathbf{B}_{n}
’s are “deceptively” non-commutative: they are as close as possible to being commutative without actually being commutative. On the other hand, the
\mathbf{M}_{n}
’s are transparently non-commutative, being as far away as possible from commutativity.

For a BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
of order
n
, the possible commuting degrees are

\Bigl\{\,\frac{3n-2}{n^{2}}\,,\,\frac{3n}{n^{2}}\,,\,\frac{3n+2}{n^{2}}\,,\,\ldots\,,\,\frac{n^{2}-2}{n^{2}}\,,\,1\,\Bigr\}\,.

Empirically, the author has observed that for orders 3, 4, and 5, every possible commuting degree is obtained by some algebra. We conjecture this is true in general but do not yet have a proof.

Conjecture 5.7
.

For each
n\geq 3
, every possible commuting degree is obtained by some BCK-algebra.

5.2.
Positive implicative degree

Consider a BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
of order
n
and recall the equation (E
1
). Let

P(\mathbf{A})=\{\,(x,y)\in\mathbf{A}^{2}\,\mid\,(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y\,\}\,,

and define the
positive implicative degree of
\mathbf{A}
, denoted
\pid(\mathbf{A})
, to be the degree of satisfiability of the equation
(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y
; that is,

\pid(\mathbf{A})=\frac{|P(\mathbf{A})|}{n^{2}}\,.

One checks that all pairs of the form
(0,x)
,
(x,0)
, and
(x,x)
are in
P(\mathbf{A})
for all
x\in\mathbf{A}
, so
|P(\mathbf{A})|\geq 3n-2
. But also, any pair
(x,y)
such that
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=0
is in
P(\mathbf{A})
. That is, if
x\leq y
, then
(x,y)\in P(\mathbf{A})
. This makes
P(\mathbf{A})
generally harder to count than its counterpart
C(\mathbf{A})
.

The simplest possible case is that all non-zero elements of
\mathbf{A}
are incomparable. However, such a poset admits a unique BCK-product and the corresponding algebra will always be positive implicative (in fact, implicative)
[
12
]
, so
\pid(\mathbf{A})=1
for such an algebra.

Thus, in order for
\mathbf{A}
to fail (E
1
), there must be at least one pair
(x,y)
of non-zero elements that are comparable. We will show that, among algebras that fail (E
1
), the maximum positive implicative degree is achieved by an algebra having exactly one pair of non-zero elements
(x,y)
that are comparable, though we note this is not the only method to achieve the maximum.

Proposition 5.8
.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are BCK-algebras with
|\mathbf{A}|=n
,
|\mathbf{B}|=m
,
\pid(\mathbf{A})=\frac{k}{n^{2}}
, and
\pid(\mathbf{B})=\frac{\ell}{m^{2}}
. Then

\pid(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B})=\frac{k+\ell+2(n-1)(m-1)-1}{(n+m-1)^{2}}\,.

Consequently,

\pid(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{2})=\frac{k+2n+1}{(n+1)^{2}}\,.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Proposition
5.1
. By the definition of
\boldsymbol{\cdot}
on
\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B}
, if
x\in\mathbf{A}\setminus\{0\}
and
y\in\mathbf{B}\setminus\{0\}
, we have

\displaystyle(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}y

\displaystyle=x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y

\displaystyle(y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x)\boldsymbol{\cdot}x

\displaystyle=y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x

and so
(x,y)
and
(y,x)
are in
P(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B})
for all such pairs.

Thus,

\pid(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B})=\frac{k+\ell+2(n-1)(m-1)-1}{(n+m-1)^{2}}\,.

Finally, we note
\mathbf{2}
is positive implicative, so
\pid(\mathbf{2})=1
.
∎

Proposition 5.10

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a BCK-algebra with
|\mathbf{A}|=n

\pid(\mathbf{A}\oplus\top)=\frac{k+2n+1}{(n+1)^{2}}\,.

\boldsymbol{\cdot}
on
\mathbf{A}\oplus\top

\displaystyle(\top\boldsymbol{\cdot}x)\boldsymbol{\cdot}x

\pid(\mathbf{A}\oplus\top)=\frac{k+2n+1}{(n+1)^{2}}

\pid(\mathbf{3}^{\text{c}})=\frac{8}{9}=\frac{3^{2}+3(3)-2}{2(3)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+1)^{2}+3(n+1)-2}{2}\,,

and therefore we have
\pid(\mathbf{C}_{n+1})=\frac{(n+1)^{2}+3(n+1)-2}{2(n+1)^{2}}
. The result holds by induction.
∎

Corollary 5.13
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is a finite linear BCK-algebra, then
\frac{1}{2}<\pid(\mathbf{A})\leq 1
.

We close this section by observing that while the positive implicative equation has no finite satisfiability gap in the language of BCK-algebras, it does have a gap in the language of commutative BCK-algebras.

Corollary 5.14
.

In the language of commutative BCK-algebras, the equation (E
1
) has finite satisfiability gap
\frac{1}{9}
.

Proof.

We saw in Theorem
5.12
that
\pid(\mathbf{C}_{n})
is a decreasing sequence, so
\mathcal{D}
has maximum
\frac{8}{9}
. Applying Theorem
3.2
gives the result.
∎

5.3.
Implicative degree

Consider a BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
of order
n
and recall the equation (I). Let

I(\mathbf{A})=\{\,(x,y)\in\mathbf{A}^{2}\,\mid\,x\boldsymbol{\cdot}(y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x)=x\,\}\,,

and define the
implicative degree of
\mathbf{A}
, denoted
\id(\mathbf{A})
, to be the degree of satisfiability of the equation
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}(y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x)=x
; that is,

\id(\mathbf{A})=\frac{|I(\mathbf{A})|}{n^{2}}\,.

One checks that all pairs of the form
(0,x)
,
(x,0)
, and
(x,x)
are in
I(\mathbf{A})
for all
x\in\mathbf{A}
, so
|I(\mathbf{A})|\geq 3n-2
. But also, any pair
(x,y)
such that
y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x=0
is in
I(\mathbf{A})
. That is, if
x\geq y
, then
(x,y)\in I(\mathbf{A})
. Counting the elements of
I(\mathbf{A})
is therefore similar to counting the elements of
P(\mathbf{A})
.

As we noted in Section
5.2
, if all non-zero elements of
\mathbf{A}
are incomparable,
\mathbf{A}
admits a unique BCK-product which is implicative, so
\id(\mathbf{A})=1
for such an algebra.

Thus, in order for
\mathbf{A}
to fail (I), there must be at least one pair
(x,y)
of non-zero elements that are comparable. We will see that, among algebras that fail (I), the bounds on implicative degree are actually the same as those for positive implicative degree, and the same algebras witness those bounds.

Proposition 5.15
.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are BCK-algebras with
|\mathbf{A}|=n
,
|\mathbf{B}|=m
,
\id(\mathbf{A})=\frac{k}{n^{2}}
, and
\id(\mathbf{B})=\frac{\ell}{m^{2}}
. Then

\id(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B})=\frac{k+\ell+2(n-1)(m-1)-1}{(n+m-1)^{2}}\,.

Consequently,

\id(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{2})=\frac{k+2n+1}{(n+1)^{2}}\,.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Propositions
5.1
and
5.8
. By the definition of
\boldsymbol{\cdot}
on
\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B}
, if
x\in\mathbf{A}\setminus\{0\}
and
y\in\mathbf{B}\setminus\{0\}
, we have

\displaystyle x\boldsymbol{\cdot}(y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x)

\displaystyle=x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=x

\displaystyle y\boldsymbol{\cdot}(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)

\displaystyle=y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x=y

and so
(x,y)
and
(y,x)
are in
I(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B})
for all such pairs.

Thus,

\id(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{B})=\frac{k+\ell+2(n-1)(m-1)-1}{(n+m-1)^{2}}\,.

∎

Theorem 5.16
.

For each
n\geq 3
, there is a BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
of order
n
realizing the upper bound
\id(\mathbf{A})=\frac{n^{2}-1}{n^{2}}

Proof.

Recall the family of algebras
\mathbf{P}_{n}
used in Theorem
5.9

\id(\mathbf{3}^{\text{c}})=\frac{8}{9}
, and by an induction we have
\id(\mathbf{P}_{n})=\frac{n^{2}-1}{n^{2}}
using Proposition
5.15
.
∎

We consider now the minimum implicative degree. Suppose
\mathbf{A}
is a BCK-algebra of order
n
with a unique atom
a
. The transpose of Table

indicates the pairs in
\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}
that must be in
I(\mathbf{A})

Theorem 5.17

For

Lemma 6.1
.

For each
n\geq 3
, the algebra
\mathbf{D}_{n}
is a non-commutative BCK-algebra.

Proof.

It is easy to check that
\mathbf{D}_{n}
satisfies (BCK3), (BCK4), and (BCK5). Since
\mathbf{D}_{n}
is a one-element extension of
\mathbf{C}_{n}
, it only remains to check (BCK1) and (BCK2) with the top element
n
in place of some (or all) of the variables
x
,
y
and
z
.

Consider (BCK2). If
x=y=n
, or if
y=n
, it is easy to see (BCK2) is satisfied. So suppose
x=n
. If
y=0
it is again an easy check, so assume
0<y<n
. We have

n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=\begin{cases}n-y-1&\text{ if $y\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-2\}$}\\
1&\text{ if $y=n-1$}\end{cases}\,,

and so

n\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=\begin{cases}y&\text{ if $y\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-2\}$}\\
n-2&\text{ if $y=n-1$}\end{cases}\,.

Thus, for
y\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-2\}
, we have
\bigl[n\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=y\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=0
, and for
y=n-1
we have
\bigl[n\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=(n-2)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n-1)=0
. So
\mathbf{D}_{n}
satisfies (BCK2).

Showing (BCK1) is more involved. Based on how we defined
\boldsymbol{\cdot}
on
\mathbf{D}_{n}
, we make the following observations: for all
a,b,c\in\mathbf{D}_{n}
,

(1)

a\boldsymbol{\cdot}n=0

(2)

n\boldsymbol{\cdot}a\leq n

(3)

If
a\leq b
, then
a\boldsymbol{\cdot}b=0

(4)

If
a\leq b
, then
a\boldsymbol{\cdot}c\leq b\boldsymbol{\cdot}c

(5)

If
a\leq b
, then
n\boldsymbol{\cdot}b\leq n\boldsymbol{\cdot}a
.

(We note that the order
\leq
being used is the natural order. We cannot use the BCK-order on
\mathbf{D}_{n}
yet!)

Notice also that if none of
x
,
y
, or
z
are equal to
n
, then (BCK1) is satisfied since we are in the subalgebra
\mathbf{C}_{n}
which is known to be a BCK-algebra. This leaves seven cases to consider. If two or more of
x
,
y
, and
z
are equal to
n
, it is a straightforward computation to show that (BCK1) is satisfied using the observations above.

If
y=n
, then

\bigl[(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}z)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(z\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=\bigl[(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}n)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}z)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(z\boldsymbol{\cdot}n)=\bigl[0\boldsymbol{\cdot}(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}z)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(z\boldsymbol{\cdot}n)=0\boldsymbol{\cdot}(z\boldsymbol{\cdot}n)=0\,.

If
z=n
, then

\bigl[(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}n)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=\bigl[(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}0\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=0\,.

The last equality follows by observations (3) and (4): since
x<n
, we have
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y<n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y
, and thus
(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=0
.

The last case to consider is the case where
x=n
. If
y=0
, then (BCK1) reduces to (BCK2) which we proved above. If
z=0
, then

\bigl[(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}0)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(0\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=\bigl[(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}n\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}0=(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}n=0

since
n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y\leq n
. We may assume then that
0<y,z<n
. If
z\leq y
, then
n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y\leq n\boldsymbol{\cdot}z
by observation (5), and so

\bigl[(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}z)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(z\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=0\boldsymbol{\cdot}(z\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=0\,.

Finally, suppose
0<y<z<n
. Note that
y\in\{1,2\ldots,n-2\}
, so
n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=n-y-1
and
z\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=z-y
. If
z=n-1
, then

\bigl[(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}z)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(z\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=\bigl[(n-y-1)\boldsymbol{\cdot}1\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n-1-y)=(n-y-2)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n-y-1)=0

since
n-y-2<n-y-1
. If
z\neq n-1
, then
n\boldsymbol{\cdot}z=n-z-1
, in which case
(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}z)=(n-y-1)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n-z-1)=(n-y-1)-(n-z-1)=z-y
, and thus we have

\bigl[(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}z)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(z\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=(z-y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(z-y)=0\,.

Hence,
\mathbf{D}_{n}
satisfies (BCK1) and
\mathbf{D}_{n}
is a BCK-algebra.

Lastly, we check that
(n,n-1)
is a non-commuting pair:

\displaystyle(n-1)\wedge n

\displaystyle=n\boldsymbol{\cdot}\bigl(n\boldsymbol{\cdot}(n-1)\bigr)=n\boldsymbol{\cdot}1=n-2

\displaystyle n\wedge(n-1)

\displaystyle=(n-1)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\bigl((n-1)\boldsymbol{\cdot}n\bigr)=(n-1)\boldsymbol{\cdot}0=n-1

and so
\mathbf{D}_{n}
is non-commutative.
∎
