Rank-two reflexive sheaves on the projective space with second Chern class equal to four
Marcos Jardim, Alan Muniz

Abstract
. We study rank-two reflexive sheaves on ℙ 3 {\mathbb{P}^{3}} with c 2 = 4 c_{2}=4 , expanding on previous results for c 2 ≤ 3 c_{2}\leq 3 . We show that every spectrum not previously ruled out is realized. Moreover, moduli spaces are studied and described in detail for c 1 = − 1 c_{1}=-1 or 0 0 and c 3 ≥ 8 c_{3}\geq 8 .

14F06, 14D20

1.
Introduction

Stable rank-
2
reflexive sheaves have been the subject of an influential article by Hartshorne
[
16
]
more than 40 years ago, and numerous authors have studied them since then. Most relevant for the present paper, Chang provided in
[
7
]
a complete description of the moduli spaces when the second Chern class
c_{2}\leq 3
. While Miró-Roig
[
27
]
and Chang
[
6
]
established existence results for sheaves with
c_{2}\geq 4
and a given third Chern class. This work aims to study moduli spaces of stable rank-
2
reflexive sheaves with
c_{2}=4
.

Beyond Chern classes, rank-
2
reflexive sheaves admit finer numerical invariants, also introduced by Hartshorne in
[
16
]
, called the
spectrum
. This is a multi-set of integers satisfying some strict conditions, cf. Theorem
4
below.
Our first step is to list all possible spectra for
c_{2}=4
, cf. Tables
1
and
2
below. Two of these spectra (marked with asterisks) were known to be unrealizable. In particular, there are no rank-
2
reflexive sheaves
F
with
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})=(-1,4,14)
, cf.
[
27
, Theorem A]
or
[
6
, Proposition 2]
. As part of our work, we exhibit sheaves evincing each other spectra.

Let
{\mathcal{R}}(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
denote the moduli space of stable rank-
2
reflexive sheaves
F
with Chern classes
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
. For
c_{2}=4
, essentially two situations have been studied in the literature: the case of locally free sheaves (i.e.,
c_{3}=0
) and the case of maximal
c_{3}
(that is,
c_{3}=14
when
c_{1}=0
and
c_{3}=16
when
c_{1}=-1
). Let us revise the available results. The case of vector bundles was described by Barth
[
1
]
and Chang
[
5
]
(for
c_{1}=0
) and by Bănică and Manolache
[
3
]
(for
c_{1}=-1
). These authors have shown that each of the moduli spaces
{\mathcal{R}}(c_{1},4,0)
consists of exactly two irreducible components; both are of expected dimension 29 in the case
c_{1}=0
, while one of the components is oversized in the case
c_{1}=-1
. For maximal
c_{3}
, the moduli spaces were described by Hartshorne in
[
16
]
and Chang
[
6
]
. More precisely, both
{\mathcal{R}}(0,4,14)
and
{\mathcal{R}}(-1,4,16)
are irreducible, nonsingular, and rational of dimension 29, cf.
[
6
, Theorem 5]
and
[
16
, Theorem 9.2]
, respectively. Note that the former has the expected dimension, while the latter is oversized. This paper examines the remaining cases for
{\mathcal{R}}(c_{1},4,c_{3})
. To be precise, we prove the following results.

Theorem 1
.

Set
c_{1}=0
.

(i)

For
1\leq c_{3}\leq 6
, the moduli scheme
{\mathcal{R}}(0,4,c_{3})
has a generically reduced and irreducible component of expected dimension
29
.

(ii)

The moduli scheme
{\mathcal{R}}(0,4,8)
is generically smooth, unirational of dimension
29
. Moreover, the reduction
{\mathcal{R}}(0,4,8)_{\rm red}
is irreducible.

(iii)

The moduli scheme
{\mathcal{R}}(0,4,10)
is smooth, irreducible, unirational of dimension
29
.

(iv)

The moduli scheme
{\mathcal{R}}(0,4,12)
possesses two irreducible components

•

{\mathcal{R}}(0,4,12)_{0}
is smooth, unirational of dimension
29
;

•

{\mathcal{R}}(0,4,12)_{1}
is non-reduced of dimension 29, and
\big({\mathcal{R}}(0,4,12)_{1}\big)_{\rm red}
is smooth and rational.

Further details on the structure of
{\mathcal{R}}(0,4,8)
,
{\mathcal{R}}(0,4,10)

2.
Spectrum and cohomology of rank-
2
reflexive sheaves

We start by collecting basic facts about the rank-
2
reflexive sheaves on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
, with Chern classes denoted by
c_{1},c_{2}
and
c_{3}
. We will assume that the sheaves are normalized, i.e.,
c_{1}\in\{-1,0\}
, and that
c_{2}=4
. In this case, the Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch Theorem reads as follows.

Lemma 3

(HRR)
.

Let
F
be a normalized rank-
2
reflexive sheaf with second Chern class
c_{2}=4
. Then,

\chi(F(l))=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}l^{3}+2l^{2}-\frac{1}{3}l+\frac{c_{3}}{2}-6,&\text{if }c_{1}=0;\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}l^{3}+\frac{3}{2}l^{2}-\frac{11}{6}l+\frac{c_{3}}{2}-5,&\text{if }c_{1}=-1.\end{cases}

In particular,
c_{3}
is even.

Given a normalized rank-
2
reflexive sheaf
F
on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
, such that
h^{0}(F(-1))=0
, there exists a unique list of integers
\{k_{1},\dots,k_{c_{2}(F)}\}
, encoding partial information on its cohomology, called the
spectrum
of
F
. It was first defined by Barth and Elencwajg
[
2
]
for locally free sheaves and later extended to reflexive sheaves by Hartshorne
[
16
, Section 7]
. The following properties characterize the spectrum of
F
:

S1)

h^{1}(F(p))=\sum_{i}h^{0}({\mathbb{P}^{1}},{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(k_{i}+p+1))
, for each
p\leq-1
;

S2)

h^{2}(F(p))=\sum_{i}h^{1}({\mathbb{P}^{1}},{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(k_{i}+p+1))
, for each
p\geq-3
if
c_{1}=0
and
p\geq-2
if
c_{1}=-1
.

Moreover,
c_{3}=-2\sum k_{i}+c_{1}c_{2}
.

In the other direction, for given
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, and
c_{3}
, one may determine all the possible spectra using the following criteria, cf.
[
16
, Theorem 7.5]
.

Theorem 4

(Hartshorne)
.

Let
F
be a normalized rank-
2
reflexive sheaf such that
h^{0}(F(-1))=0
and let
\{k_{i}\}
be its spectrum, then:

(i)

If there exists
k>0
in the spectrum, then
1,2,\dots,k
also occur in the spectrum;

(ii)

If there exists
k<-1
in the spectrum, then
-1,-2,\dots,k
also occur if
c_{1}=0
, and
-2,\dots,k
also occur if
c_{1}=-1
.

In addition, if
F
is stable, then:

(i)

If there exists
k>0
in the spectrum, then
0
also occurs;

(ii)

If there exists
k<-1
in the spectrum, then
-1
also occurs and, if
c_{1}=0
then, either
0
also occurs or
-1
occurs at least twice.

Remark 5
.

There is no stable rank-two reflexive sheaf whose spectrum is either

If

Lemma 7
.

If
F
is a stable rank-
2
reflexive sheaf with
c_{2}=4
, then
h^{0}(F(2))>0
. Moreover, if either when
c_{1}=0
and
c_{3}\geq 10
or
c_{1}=-1
and
c_{3}\geq 12
, then
h^{0}(F(1))>0
.

Proof.

Stability implies that
h^{3}(F(k))=0
for
k\geq-4
; then, from the formulas in Lemma
3
and Lemma

, we get

(1)

\chi(F(2))=h^{0}(F(2))-h^{1}(F(2))=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{c_{3}}{2}+4,&\text{if }c_{1}=0;\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{c_{3}}{2},&\text{if }c_{1}=-1.\end{cases}

If either
c_{1}=0
, or
c_{1}=-1
and
c_{3}>0
, it immediately follows that
h^{0}(F(2))>0
. If
c_{1}=-1
and
c_{3}=0
, this was proved in
[

. Their argument is as follows. Assuming by contradiction that
h^{0}(F(2))=0
, one would get
F
is
3

F(3)
vanishes along a smooth curve of degree
10
and genus
6
. Moreover,
C
is canonical, i.e.,
\omega_{C}=\mathcal{O}_{C}(1)
. However, every such curve is contained in a quartic surface (cf.
[
12
, p.58]
), which is absurd since
h^{0}(F(2))=h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(4))
.

The formulas in Lemma
3
also yield

(2)

\chi(F(1))=h^{0}(F(1))-h^{1}(F(1))+h^{2}(F(1))=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{c_{3}}{2}-4,&\text{if }c_{1}=0;\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{c_{3}}{2}-5,&\text{if }c_{1}=-1.\end{cases}

When either
c_{1}=0
and
c_{3}\geq 10
, or
c_{1}=-1
and
c_{3}\geq 12
, we get
h^{0}(F(1))>0
as desired.
∎

3.
Serre’s construction

Let
F
be a rank-
2
reflexive sheaf and let
\sigma\in H^{0}(F(k))
, for some
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, without zeros in codimension one. Then
\sigma
induces an exact sequence

(3)

0\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\longrightarrow F(k)\overset{\sigma^{\vee}}{\longrightarrow}\mathscr{I}_{C}(2k+c_{1})\longrightarrow 0

where
C
is the vanishing locus of
\sigma
; this is a curve, i.e., a locally Cohen–Macaulay scheme of pure dimension one. Note that the sequence above corresponds to a nontrivial element of
\xi\in\Ext^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(2k+c_{1}),{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}})\simeq H^{0}(\omega_{C}(4-2k-c_{1}))
and that the sheaf
F
is reflexive if and only if
\xi
, as a section of
\omega_{C}(4-2k-c_{1})
, vanishes in dimension 0 at most.

On the other hand, given a pair
(C,\xi)
consisting of a curve and a section in
H^{0}(\omega_{C}(4-2k-c_{1}))
vanishing only in dimension
0
, one produces a pair
(F,\sigma)
consisting of a rank-
2
reflexive sheaf and a global section
\sigma\in H^{0}(F(k))
. This is the content of the Serre correspondence; for details, we refer to
[
16
, §4]
. The sheaf
F
is stable if and only if
H^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(k+c_{1}))=0
.

When
c_{2}(F)=4
, Lemma
7
implies that it is enough to consider
k=1,2
. It may be the case that
h^{0}(F(1))>0
, and
F(1)
corresponds to a curve
C
satisfying:

(4)

\deg(C)=c_{1}+5\quad{\rm and}\quad p_{a}(C)=\dfrac{c_{3}}{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}(c_{1}+5)(c_{1}-2)+1.

Due to stability,
h^{0}(F)=h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(2+c_{1}))=0
, i.e.,
C
is not contained in a surface of degree
c_{1}+1
or less.

When
h^{0}(F(1))=0
, the sheaf
F(2)
corresponds to a curve
C
satisfying:

(5)

\deg(C)=2c_{1}+8\quad{\rm and}\quad p_{a}(C)=\dfrac{c_{3}}{2}+(c_{1}+4)c_{1}+1.

Moreover,
C
is not contained in a surface of degree
c_{1}+2
or less.

Also important is to consider a relative version of the Serre correspondence, i.e., the correspondence between families of pairs. This is well-known to specialists, though rarely spelled out in articles. Let us explain this in some detail.

Given a flat family of curves
{\mathcal{C}}
regarded as a subvariety of the appropriate Hilbert Scheme
\rm Hilb^{d,g}({\mathbb{P}^{3}})
of curves of degree
d
and arithmetic genus
g
, let
\mathbf{C}\subset{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
be the universal curve and
\mathscr{I}_{\mathbf{C}}
be the universal ideal sheaf on
{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
. Let
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
be the projections of
{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
onto its first and second factors, respectively. If we assume that
{\mathcal{C}}
is integral and that the function
C\mapsto h^{0}(\omega_{C}(4-2k-c_{1}))
is constant on
{\mathcal{C}}
, then the sheaf of relative
\Ext
groups

\mathcal{E}\coloneqq{\mathcal{E}}{\it xt}^{1}_{\pi_{1}}(\mathscr{I}_{\mathbf{C}}(k+c_{1}),\pi_{2}^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-k))

is a vector bundle over
{\mathcal{C}}
parameterizing pairs
(C,\xi)
. Indeed, the complex
\mathcal{L}^{\bullet}
in
[
23
, Corollary 1.2]
has all the properties needed to prove a version of Grauert’s Theorem
[
15
, III Corollary 12.9]
for the variation of
\Ext
. In the analytic category, this was done by Bănică, Putinar, and Schumacher in
[
4
, Satz 3, (ii)]
.

Moreover, for
k>0
and
c_{1}\in\{0,1\}
, we have
\Hom(\mathscr{I}_{C}(k+c_{1}),{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-k))=0
for every
C
. By
[
23
, Corollary 4.5]
, there exists a universal extension on
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\times{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
:

0\longrightarrow p_{2}^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-k)\otimes p_{1}^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}(1)\longrightarrow\mathbf{F}\longrightarrow(\rho\times 1)^{*}\mathscr{I}_{\mathbf{C}}\otimes p_{2}^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(k+c_{1})\longrightarrow 0

where
p_{i}
are the canonical projections of
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\times{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
and
\rho\colon\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\to{\mathcal{C}}
is the structural morphism. Then
\mathbf{F}
is a flat family of torsion-free sheaves. We are interested in the following open subset of
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})
:

\mathcal{U}\coloneqq\big\{(C,\xi)\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\mid{\rm either}~\dim(\xi)_{0}=0~{\rm or}~(\xi)_{0}=\emptyset\big\},

where
(\xi)_{0}
denotes the zero locus of
\xi
as a section of
\omega_{C}(4-2k-c_{1})
. Hence,
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{U}}
is a flat family of reflexive sheaves. If, moreover,
h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(k+c_{1}))=0
for every
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
, then
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{U}}
is a family of stable reflexive sheaves. We obtain a morphism

\Psi\colon\mathcal{U}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}),

where
c_{2}
and
c_{3}
depend on
d
,
g
and
k
, as in
[
16
, Theorem 4.1]
. The fiber over
F
can be identified with an open subset of
\mathbb{P}\big(H^{0}(F(k))\big)
. The closure of the image
{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq\overline{\Psi(\mathcal{U})}
is an integral scheme parameterizing sheaves that correspond to curves in
{\mathcal{C}}
. To summarize this discussion, we state the following proposition.

Proposition 8
.

Fix
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and
c_{1}\in\{-1,0\}
. Let
{\mathcal{C}}\in\rm Hilb^{d,g}({\mathbb{P}^{3}})
be an integral family of curves of degree
d
and arithmetic genus
g
such that
h^{0}(\omega_{C}(4-2k-c_{1}))
and
h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(2k+c_{1}))
are constant on
{\mathcal{C}}
, and
h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(k+c_{1}))=0
for every
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Then Serre correspondence gives an irreducible family of sheaves
\mathcal{F}\subset{\mathcal{R}}(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
. Moreover, the dimensions are related by the following formula:

(6)

\dim{\mathcal{F}}+h^{0}(F(k))=\dim{\mathcal{C}}+h^{0}(\omega_{C}(4-2k-c_{1})).

Remark 9
.

The formula in display (
6
) relates the dimensions of a family of curves to the corresponding family of sheaves. We refer to
[
22
]
for a deformation theoretic approach to Serre correspondence. In particular, for a formula similar to (
6
) regarding Zariski tangent spaces to the respective moduli spaces, cf.
[
22
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Finally, we also implemented the Serre correspondence in the computer algebra system Macaulay2
[
11
]
. In the ancillary file
refshsetup.m2
, we have the function
Serre(C, k)
, which computes a random extension of
\mathscr{I}_{C}
by
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-k)
, if it exists. This computational approach will be used to provide explicit examples throughout the text.

3.1.
Lemmas about curves

We will narrow down the types of curves relevant to our work. As usual, by a curve, we mean a Cohen-Macaulay subscheme of
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
of pure dimension one. One tool we will need to describe these curves is Liaison (or Linkage) Theory; our primary reference is
[
25
, III]
.

Let
C\subset{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
be a curve and
X
be the complete intersection of two surfaces of degrees
s
and
t
containing
C
. Applying
{\mathcal{H}}{\it om}_{{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}}(-,\mathcal{O}_{X})
to the canonical map
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{C}
, we get that

{\mathcal{H}}{\it om}_{{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}}(\mathcal{O}_{C},\mathcal{O}_{X})\subset\mathcal{O}_{X}
is the ideal sheaf
\mathscr{I}_{\Gamma/X}
of a curve
\Gamma\subset X

\mathscr{I}_{C/X}={\mathcal{H}}{\it om}_{{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}}(\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma},\mathcal{O}_{X})
. We say that

and

P.1

\deg(C)+\deg(\Gamma)=st

p_{a}(C)-p_{a}(\Gamma)=(\deg(C)-\deg(\Gamma))\frac{1}{2}(s+t-4)
;

P.2

For any

h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(n))=h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{\Gamma}(s+t-n-4)
;

P.3

For any
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have
h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(n))=h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{X}(n))+h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma}(s+t-n-4))

M_{C}\simeq M_{\Gamma}^{*}(4-s-t)\coloneqq\Ext^{4}_{R}(M_{\Gamma},R(-s-t))

For proof, cf.
[

.

From (

) and (

), we only need to deal with curves of degrees
4,5,6
, and

. Curves of degree

were described by Nollet and Schlesinger in
[

. However, not much is known in the literature about curves of higher degrees. We start by classifying some quintic curves appearing in our setting.

Recall that a curve
C
is called extremal if it is not planar and
h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(2))\geq 2
. In this case,
C
is either a twisted cubic, an elliptic quartic, or it contains a planar subcurve of degree one less than its degree. The terminology comes from the Rao function: extremal curves attain the maximum possible values for the Rao function. Analogously, subextremal curves have the maximal Rao functions among non-extremal curves. We refer to
[
19
, §9]
for properties of extremal and subextremal curves.

Lemma 10
.

Let
C
be a non-planar curve of degree
5
on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
. If
h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(2))>0
, then one of the following holds:

(i)

h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(2))=2
and
C
is extremal, there exists
C^{\prime}\subset C
a plane quartic;

(ii)

h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(2))=1
and
C
is either

(a)

a divisor of type
(0,5)
on a smooth quadric. In particular,

h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(t))=\begin{cases}4,&t\in\{0,3\},\\
6,&t\in\{1,2\},\\
0,&{\text{o}therwise.}\end{cases}

(b)

a divisor of type
(1,4)
on a smooth quadric. In particular,

h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(t))=\begin{cases}2,&t\in\{1,2\},\\
0,&{\text{o}therwise.}\end{cases}

(c)

a subextremal curve. In particular,

h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(t))=\begin{cases}2-p_{a}(C),&t\in\{1,2\},\\
\max\{1-p_{a}(C)+t,0\},&t\leq 0,\\
\max\{4-p_{a}(C)-t,0\},&t\geq 3.\end{cases}

(d)

a negative genus curve on a double plane that is not subextremal.

Proof.

Assume that
r=h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(2))\geq 2
, i.e.,
C
is extremal. By the previous paragraph,
C
must contain a planar quartic subcurve since
\deg(C)=5
. Now assume that
h^{0}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(2))=1
and let
Q
be the quadric containing
C
.

If
Q
is smooth, then
C
is a divisor of bidegree
(a,5-a)
on
Q\simeq{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\times{\mathbb{P}^{1}}
, and
0\leq a\leq 2
. It follows that either:

•

a=0
and
C
is linearly equivalent on
Q
to a disjoint union of
5
lines. In particular, one may compute:
h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C})=4
,
h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(1))=6
,
h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(2))=6
,
h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(3))=4
, and
h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(l))=0
for
l<0
or
l>3
.

•

a=1
and
C
can be linked to a disjoint union of
3
lines by the complete intersection of
Q
and a quartic surface containing
C
. Hence, one may compute
h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(1))=h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(2))=2
and
h^{1}(\mathscr{I}_{C}(l))=0
for
l\not\in\{1,2\}
.

•

a=2
and
C
is linked to a line by the intersection of
Q
with a cubic surface. Hence,
C
is ACM of genus
2
, which is subextremal.

If
Q
is a quadratic cone, then
Q\simeq\mathbb{P}_{(1,1,2)}
embedded by
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}_{(1,1,2)}}(2)
, see
[
14
, p.128]
. The weighted degree map yields
\Pic(Q)\simeq 2{\mathbb{Z}}\subset Cl(Q)\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}
, cf.
[
9
, §4]
. Then, any Weil divisor on
Q
of even degree is Cartier. Moreover, any Cartier divisor of
Q
comes from the embedding into
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
. This is to say that any curve in
Q
of even degree is a complete intersection. Furthermore, any odd-degree curve in
Q
is linked to a general line through the cone’s vertex, so it is ACM. In our case,
C
has degree
5
, so it is an ACM curve of genus
2
.

If
Q=H_{1}\cup H_{2}
is the union of two distinct planes, then taking
C\cap H_{1}
yields

0\longrightarrow\mathscr{I}_{Y}(-1)\overset{h_{1}}{\longrightarrow}\mathscr{I}_{C}\longrightarrow\mathscr{I}_{Z/H_{1}}(-P)\longrightarrow 0

where
P
is the one-dimensional component of
C\cap H_{1}
, and
Z
is a zero-dimensional scheme whose support is contained in
L\coloneqq H_{1}\cap H_{2}
. Also, the residual curve
Y
is contained in
H_{2}
. Note that
\deg(C)=\deg(Y)+\deg(P)
, and, up to exchanging
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, we may assume that
\deg(P)>\deg(Y)
. Since
C
is neither planar nor extremal,
\deg(P)=3
and
C
is a subextremal curve, cf.
[
19
, Proposition 9.9]
. Moreover,
p_{a}(C)=2-h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{Z})
.

If
Q=V(h^{2})
is a double plane, then let
H=V(h)
, let
P
be the maximal one-dimensional subscheme of
C\cap H
, and let
Y
be the residual scheme to
C\cap H
in
C
. Thus,

0\longrightarrow\mathscr{I}_{Y}(-1)\overset{h}{\longrightarrow}\mathscr{I}_{C}\longrightarrow\mathscr{I}_{Z/H}(-p)\longrightarrow 0

where
Z
is a
0
-dimensional subscheme of
H
and
p=\deg P
. Due to
[
19
, Proposition 2.1]
,
Z\subset Y\subset P\subset H
and
\deg Y=5-p
and
p_{a}(C)=2-h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{Z})
. Since
C
is not extremal,
p\leq 3
. On the other hand,

because

, hence
p=3
. If

is contained in a line, then

is subextremal; this is always the case when
p_{a}(C)\geq 0
.

∎

For curves of degree

Lemma 11
.

Let

be a degree-

Let

to a curve

of degree

and genus

.

Now assume

divides every element of

. Taking the intersection

, we get a curve of degree

and a zero-dimensional scheme

, satisfying

where

is the residual curve. Note that

and

. On the other hand,

.
∎

Next, we use our knowledge of curves to provide upper bounds for the global sections of the corresponding sheaves.

Let

(ii)

If

5.
A class of unobstructed reflexive sheaves

Given a reflexive sheaf
F
on
{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
(of arbitrary rank), one can find a locally free sheaf
L=\bigoplus_{i}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(a_{i})
and an epimorphism
L\twoheadrightarrow F
; the kernel of this epimorphism, call it
E
, is necessarily locally free by the Auslander–Buchsbaum formula, or one can use the exact sequence

(18)

0\longrightarrow E\overset{\varphi}{\longrightarrow}L\longrightarrow F\longrightarrow 0

to show that
{\mathcal{E}}{\it xt}^{p}(E,{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}})=0
for
p>0
.

In this section, we will present some sufficient conditions that allow us to compute
\extd^{1}(F,F)
and guarantee that
F
is unobstructed as a point in the corresponding moduli space of reflexive sheaves (that is,
\extd^{2}(F,F)=0
).

Given two locally free sheaves
E
and
L
such that
\rk(E)<\rk(L)
, set

(19)

\mathbb{M}_{0}\coloneqq\big\{\,\varphi\in\Hom(E,L)\mid{\rm either}~D(\varphi)=\emptyset~{\rm or}~\dim D(\varphi)=0\,\}

where
D(\varphi)\coloneqq\{\,x\in{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\mid\varphi(x)\textrm{ not injective}\,\}
is the degeneration locus of the morphism
\varphi
. It follows that
F_{\varphi}\coloneqq\coker(\varphi)
is reflexive for every
\varphi\in\mathbb{M}_{0}
.

Consider also the group

(20)

\mathbb{G}\coloneqq\left(\Aut(E)\times\Aut(L)\right)\Big/\kappa^{*}\cdot{\rm id}.

Note that
\mathbb{G}
acts on
\mathbb{M}_{0}
as follows:

(g,h)\cdot\varphi\coloneqq h\circ\varphi\circ g^{-1}.

It is not difficult to see that if
\varphi^{\prime}=(g,h)\cdot\varphi
, then
F_{\varphi^{\prime}}\simeq F_{\varphi}
. If
F_{\varphi}
is
\mu
-stable for every
\varphi\in\mathbb{M}_{0}
, we obtain a natural modular morphism

\Psi\colon\mathbb{M}_{0}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{P})\quad\textrm{given by}\quad\Psi(\varphi)=\big[F_{\varphi}\big]~,

where
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{P})
denotes the moduli space of
\mu
-stable reflexive sheaves with Hilbert polynomial
\mathbf{P}(t)\coloneqq P_{L}(t)-P_{E}(t)
, and
[F]
denotes the isomorphism class of a sheaf
F
as a point in
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{P})
.

Lemma 17
.

If
\Hom(L,E)=\Ext^{1}(E,L)=0
, then an isomorphism
f\colon F_{\varphi}\overset{\sim}{\to}F_{\varphi^{\prime}}
lifts to unique isomorphisms
g\in\Aut(E)
and
h\in\Aut(L)
such that
\varphi^{\prime}=h\circ\varphi\circ g^{-1}
. If, in addition,

\extd^{1}(F_{\varphi},F_{\varphi})=\homd(E,L)-\homd(E,E)-\homd(L,L)+1

for every
\varphi\in\mathbb{M}_{0}
, then
\mathbb{M}_{0}
dominates an irreducible component of
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{P})
.

Proof.

Consider the following commutative diagram with exact rows

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{E\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\varphi}
\scriptstyle{g}
\textstyle{L\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\tilde{f}}
\scriptstyle{h}
\textstyle{F_{\varphi}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{0}
\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{E\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\varphi^{\prime}}
\textstyle{L\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{F_{\varphi^{\prime}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0}

where
\tilde{f}
is the obvious composition; the hypothesis implies that
\Hom(L,F_{\varphi^{\prime}})\simeq\Hom(L,L)
, thus
\tilde{f}
lift to a unique
h\in\Hom(L,L)
, and then one can find a unique
g\in\Hom(E,E)

to find their inverses. Therefore, the fibers of
\Psi
are
\mathbb{G}

\Psi^{-1}([F_{\varphi}])=\mathbb{G}\cdot\varphi
. A similar computation shows that the stabilizer of any
\varphi\in\mathbb{M}_{0}
is trivial.

Regarding the second claim, note that

\dim\mathbb{M}_{0}-\dim\mathbb{G}=\homd(E,L)-\homd(E,E)-\homd(L,L)+1.

If this quantity, which is equal to the dimension of the image of
\Psi
,
is equal to
\extd^{1}(F,F)
, then the conclusion follows.
∎

Next, we give a vanishing criterion for the obstruction space
\Ext^{2}(F,F)
that will be useful later.

Lemma 18
.

If
F
is a
k
-regular reflexive sheaf satisfying
h^{0}(F(k-3))=h^{1}(F(k-4))=0
, then
\Ext^{2}(F,F)=0
.

Proof.

Since
F
is
k
-regular, there is an epimorphism
\varepsilon\colon H^{0}(F(k))\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-k)\twoheadrightarrow F
; setting
E\coloneqq\ker(\varepsilon)
, one can check that
E
is
(k+1)
-regular. Applying the functor
\Hom(F,-)
to the exact sequence

0\longrightarrow E\longrightarrow H^{0}(F(k))\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-k)\longrightarrow F\longrightarrow 0

we obtain, setting
r\coloneqq h^{0}(F(k))
, the exact sequence

\Ext^{2}(F,{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-k))^{\oplus r}\longrightarrow\Ext^{2}(F,F)\longrightarrow\Ext^{3}(F,E)\longrightarrow\Ext^{3}(F,{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-k))^{\oplus r}.

Serre duality yields
\Ext^{p}(F,{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-k))\simeq H^{3-p}(F(k-4))^{*}
, and our hypothesis says that these vanish for
p=2,3
. It thus follows that
\Ext^{2}(F,F)\simeq\Ext^{3}(F,E)
.

To conclude the proof, we apply the functor
\Hom(F,-)
to the evaluation epimorphism

H^{0}(E(k+1))\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-k-1)\twoheadrightarrow E

to see that

\left(H^{0}(F(k-3))^{\oplus s}\right)^{*}\simeq\Ext^{3}(F,{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-k-1))^{\oplus s}\twoheadrightarrow\Ext^{3}(F,E)

where
s\coloneqq h^{0}(E(k+1))
; since
h^{0}(F(-k-3))=0
by hypothesis, we conclude that
\Ext^{2}(F,F)=0
, as desired.
∎
