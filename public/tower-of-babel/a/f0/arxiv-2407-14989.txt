Nonparametric Estimation of Ordinary Differential Equations: Snake and Stubble
Christof Schötz

Abstract
We study nonparametric estimation in dynamical systems described by ordinary differential equations (ODEs). Specifically, we focus on estimating the unknown function f : ℝ d → ℝ d f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d} that governs the system dynamics through the ODE u ˙ ​ ( t ) = f ​ ( u ​ ( t ) ) \dot{u}(t)=f(u(t)) , where observations Y j , i = u j ​ ( t j , i ) + ε j , i Y_{j,i}=u_{j}(t_{j,i})+\varepsilon_{j,i} of solutions u j u_{j} of the ODE are made at times t j , i t_{j,i} with independent noise ε j , i \varepsilon_{j,i} . We introduce two novel models – the Stubble model and the Snake model – to mitigate the issue of observation location dependence on f f , an inherent difficulty in nonparametric estimation of ODE systems. In the Stubble model, we observe many short solutions with initial conditions that adequately cover the domain of interest. Here, we study an estimator based on multivariate local polynomial regression and univariate polynomial interpolation. In the Snake model we observe few long trajectories that traverse the domain on interest. Here, we study an estimator that combines univariate local polynomial estimation with multivariate polynomial interpolation. For both models, we establish error bounds of order n − β 2 ​ ( β + 1 ) + d n^{-\frac{\beta}{2(\beta+1)+d}} for β \beta -smooth functions f f in an infinite dimensional function class of Hölder-type and establish minimax optimality for the Stubble model in general and for the Snake model under some conditions via comparison to lower bounds from parallel work.



Keywords:
Ordinary Differential Equations, Nonparametric Regression, Minimax Optimal, Rate of Convergence

1
Introduction

In statistics, we want to extract information from observations of the world. The workings of the world are described in physics (and many other sciences) with differential equations
[
Str24
]
. Hence, statistical models of dynamical systems described by differential equations seem to be not only compelling but almost inevitable for study.

One of the simplest non-trivial instance of such a model can be described in the equations

Y_{i}=u(t_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}\,,i=1,\dots,n\qquad\dot{u}(t)=f(u(t))\,,

where
\dot{u}
denotes the time-derivative, i.e.,
(\mathrm{d}u)/(\mathrm{d}t)
.
Here,
u\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the solution to an ordinary differential equation (ODE) described by an unknown function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It is measured at times
t_{1}\leq\dots\leq t_{n}
, which creates our observations
Y_{i}
by adding measurement noise
\varepsilon_{i}
to the true state of the system
u(t_{i})
. There are different objectives one could have for such a model: reconstructing the solution
u
in the observed time interval
[t_{1},t_{n}]
(
reanalysis
, borrowing a term from climate modeling), predicting a future state
u(t)
,
t>t_{n}
(
forecasting
, as in weather forecast), or estimation of
f
(
learning the dynamics
of the dynamical system). Here, we are interested in the estimation of
f
, which is fundamental also for the other tasks as an estimate
\hat{f}
allows us to create an estimate
\hat{u}
by solving
\dot{\hat{u}}(t)=\hat{f}(\hat{u}(t))
.

We want to estimate
f
in a nonparametric model, i.e., we assume that
f
belongs to an infinite dimensional class of smooth functions, but we do not make any assumptions on its precise functional form. The parametric version of this model, in which
f
belongs to a finite dimensional class of functions (e.g., polynomials of fixed degree) has been studied in
[
Bru08
,
QZ10
,
GK12
,
DK15
]
. There, it is shown that the typical parametric
\sqrt{n}
-rate of convergence can be achieved. An overview of the state of the art for statistics of dynamical systems (mostly concerned with parametric settings) can be found in
[
MMP15
,
RH17
,
Dat21
]
.

In the nonparametric setting, different algorithms for estimation have been proposed, e.g.,
[
Hei+18
,
Che+18
,
GR21
]
.
In
[
Mar+23
]
, theoretical results for learning dynamics nonparametrically are shown, but in a density estimation context that is rather different from the regression-type model studied here. To the best of the author’s knowledge, only
[
Lah+24
]
takes a theoretical view on the ODE regression problem (additionally to proposing a new algorithm based on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces). They show an error bound of order
n^{-1/4}
(root mean squared error) for the reanalysis problem
[
Lah+24
, Theorem 1]
. This result cannot be optimal as standard nonparametric regression for
u
(ignoring the ODE constraints) yields an error of smaller order, namely
n^{-\beta/(2\beta+1)}
, where
\beta
describes the smoothness of
u
(in the case of
[
Lah+24
, Theorem 1]
, we have
\beta=3
).

Estimation in the nonparametric setting seem much harder than in the parametric one: If
f
is a smooth function with bounds on its derivative but otherwise unrestricted, an observation
Y_{i}
only yields information on
f(x)
for
x
close to
u(t_{i})
, i.e., the information is local. In contrast, each observation from an ODE where
f
is known to be a polynomial of degree at most
N
carries information for all coefficients of said polynomial, i.e., the information is global. What makes estimation in a nonparametric ODE model more difficult than in a nonparametric regression model (which also suffers from locality of information) is the fact that the location of the observation
u(t_{i})
is itself depends on the unknown
f
.

We introduce two models, called
Snake model
and
Stubble model
, which provide a remedy of this problem. For each of the two models, we propose estimators and study their rate of convergence. We find that in both instances, one obtains an error bound (root mean squared error) of order

n^{-\frac{\beta}{2(\beta+1)+d}}

(1)

for
\beta
-smooth functions
f
under optimal circumstances. This error bound is minimax optimal as we have corresponding lower bounds in the parallel work
[
SS24
]
.

1.1
Contributions

We now give more details on the contributions of this work.

A general model.

In general, we assume the true model function
f^{\star}
to be an element of Hölder-type smoothness class denoted as
\bar{\Sigma}^{d\to d}(\beta,L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\beta\right\rrbracket}})
, which means that the
k
-th derivative of
f
for
k\in{\left\llbracket 0,\beta\right\rrbracket}:=\{0,\dots,\beta\}
is bounded by
L_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, i.e.,
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|D^{k}f(x)\|_{\mathsf{op}}\leq L_{k}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathsf{op}}
is the operator norm. Then, for given initial conditions
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
, we observe the solution
t\mapsto U(f^{\star},x_{j},t)
of the ODE
\dot{u}(t)=f^{\star}(u(t))
with
U(f^{\star},x_{j},0)=x_{j}
at time points
i\Delta\!t
with time step
\Delta\!t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, i.e.,

Y_{j,i}=U(f^{\star},x_{j},i\Delta\!t)+\varepsilon_{j,i}\,,\ j\in{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket},i\in{\left\llbracket 1,n_{j}\right\rrbracket}\,.

In total, we have
n=\sum_{j=1}^{m}n_{j}
observations.
The noise variables
\varepsilon_{j,i}
are assumed to be independent, centered, and to have a finite second moment. Because of the dependency of the location of the observation
u(t_{j,i})
on
f^{\star}
, in general, consistent estimation in all of
[0,1]^{d}
is impossible. Thus, we introduce the Stubble model and the Snake model, which restrict the general model.

The Stubble model.

We use the notation
\asymp
and
\preccurlyeq
to mean
asymptotically equal
and
asymptotically lower than
(up to a positive constant), respectively (see
Notation

2.3
).
In the Stubble model, we observe many (
m\asymp n
) short (
n_{j}\preccurlyeq 1
) solutions. We assume that their initial conditions
x_{j}
cover the domain of interest
[0,1]^{d}
suitably. For this model, we construct an estimator
\hat{f}
based on a multivariate local polynomial estimator and a univariate polynomial interpolation, which is similar to the Adams–Bashforth method for numerical solutions of ODEs
[
But16
, Chapter 24]
. We obtain

\mathbf{E}\!\left[\left\|\hat{f}(x)-f^{\star}(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\preccurlyeq\left(\Delta\!t^{2}n\right)^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+d}}+\Delta\!t^{2\beta}

(2)

for all
x\in[0,1]^{d}
, see
Corollary

3.13
. The rate is shown to be minimax optimal by comparing it to lower bounds in
[
SS24
]
.

The Snake model.

In the Snake model, we observe few (
m\preccurlyeq 1
) long (
n_{j}\asymp n
) solutions. We require the trajectories
U(f^{\star},x_{j},[0,n_{j}\Delta\!t])
to cover the domain of interest
[0,1]^{d}
suitably.
For this model, we construct an estimator
\hat{f}
based on a univariate local polynomial estimator and a multivariate polynomial interpolation.
Then

\sup_{x\in[0,1]^{d}}\left\|\hat{f}(x)-f^{\star}(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}\in\mathbf{O}_{\mathbf{P}}\!\left(\delta^{2\beta}+\left(\Delta\!t\log n\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2(\beta+1)+1}}\right)\,,

(3)

see
Corollary

4.18
, where we want to view
\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
for now as the largest distance between a point
x\in[0,1]^{d}
and its closest state
U(f^{\star},x_{j},t)
,
t\in[0,n_{j}\Delta\!t]
. This interpretation is true for the case
\beta=1
, but if
\beta>1
, the definition is more complex and the result more restrictive. By comparing it to lower bounds in
[
SS24
]
, the rate is shown to be minimax optimal if
\delta
and
\Delta\!t
are in a certain relation. For the rate to be optimal, we essentially require observations to be rather dense in time, and temporally distant parts of the trajectories
U(f^{\star},x_{j},[0,n_{j}\Delta\!t])
to be distant enough in state space.

Connection and further results.

Note the complementary nature of the two models and their estimators. In spite of this, in the optimal setting regarding
\Delta\!t
and
\delta
, we obtain an error bound of the order given in (
1
) in both models (up to a
\log
-factor when considering the sup-norm), see
Corollary

3.15
and
Corollary

4.20
.
For both models, we begin our discussion with the case
\beta=1
, which allows for simple estimators and a gentle introduction to the main ideas as well as slightly stronger and more specific results.
See
Theorem

3.2
and
Corollary

3.4
for the Stubble model, and
Theorem

4.4
and
Corollary

4.6
for the Snake model. For the case of general smoothness
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
, we first show
black box
results for estimation strategies that can be used with an arbitrary regression estimator,
Theorem

3.11
and
Theorem

4.17
, respectively. If the chosen regression estimator achieves the minimax rate for a standard nonparametric regression problem, the estimation strategies achieve the rates (
2
) and (
3
), respectively. In
Corollary

3.13
and
Corollary

4.18
, we apply the general results with the (minimax optimal) local polynomial estimator as regression estimator.
Furthermore, we study how the smoothness of
f
influences the smoothness of
t\mapsto U(f,x,t)
and the smoothness of
x\mapsto U(f,x,t)
in
Corollary

E.7
and
Corollary

E.12
, respectively, which may be of independent interest.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some basic concepts related to ODEs, we introduce a general statistical model for observing solutions of ODEs, we explain why this model requires further restriction in order to be a useful model, and we introduce some terminology for a standard regression problem that will later allow us to construct ODE estimators in a generic (black box) fashion.

2.1
Ordinary Differential Equations

We introduce some basic terminology and fundamental properties concerning ODEs. See also
[
Har02
,
Arn06
]
.

Notation 2.1
.

(i)

Let
\mathbb{Z}
be the set of integers and
\mathbb{R}
the set of reals. For
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R}\}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
, denote
\mathbb{K}_{>a}=\{x\in\mathbb{K}\mid x>a\}
.
Define
\mathbb{K}_{\geq a}
,
\mathbb{K}_{<a}
,
\mathbb{K}_{\leq a}
accordingly.

(ii)

Set
\mathbb{N}:=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1},\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
a\leq b
. Set
{\left\llbracket a,b\right\rrbracket}:=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq a}\cap\mathbb{Z}_{\leq b}
. Set
{\left\llbracket a\right\rrbracket}:={\left\llbracket 1,a\right\rrbracket}
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For a differentiable function
u\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denote its derivative as
\dot{u}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then

\dot{u}(t)=f(u(t))\qquad\text{for }t\in\mathbb{R}

(4)

is an
autonomous
,
first-order
,
ordinary differential equation
.
It is of first-order, as (
4
) only depends on the first derivative of
u
. It is autonomous, as the right-hand side term
f(u(t))
only depends on
t
via
u
. In contrast, a non-autonomous, first-order ODE has the form
\dot{u}(t)=g(t,u(t))
for a function
g\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Any differentiable
u\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that fulfills (
4
) is a
solution
to the ODE. The domain of
u
is called
time
. The codomain of
u
is called
state space
. A single element of the state space is a
state
. The image of
u
is called
trajectory
.
We call
f
the
model function
of the ODE. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consider the requirement

u(0)=x\,.

(5)

We call
x
the
initial conditions
. We call (
4
) together with (
5
)
initial value problem
(IVP). If
u
fulfills (
4
) and (
5
), it is a solution to the IVP.

Assume that
f
is (globally) Lipschitz continuous. Then the IVP (
4
), (
5
) has a unique solution (Picard–Lindelöf theorem, also known as the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem). Denote this solution as
U(f,x,\cdot)\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},t\mapsto U(f,x,t)
.
The function
U(f,\cdot,\cdot)\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called
flow
of the ODE (
4
).
Note the
semigroup property
of the flow,

\displaystyle U(f,x,0)

\displaystyle=x\,,

\displaystyle U(f,x,s+t)

\displaystyle=U(f,U(f,x,s),t)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
.
For a given time step
\Delta\!t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, the mapping
x\mapsto U(f,x,\Delta\!t)
is called
propagator
. If
u
is a solution to the ODE (
4
), then
u(t+\Delta\!t)=U(f,u(t),\Delta\!t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, we call

\Upsilon(f,\Delta\!t,x):=U(f,x,\Delta\!t)-x=U(f,x,\Delta\!t)-U(f,x,0)

the
increment
.
If
u
is a solution to the ODE (
4
), then
u(t+\Delta\!t)=u(t)+\Upsilon(f,\Delta\!t,u(t))
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

The setting of autonomous, first-order ODEs is not a strong restriction: Consider the
d
-dimensional, non-autonomous ODE of order
\ell
,

v^{(\ell)}(t)=g(t,v(t),v^{(1)}(t),\dots,v^{(\ell-1)}(t))\,,

(6)

where
v^{(k)}
for
k\in{\left\llbracket 0,\ell\right\rrbracket}
denotes the
k
-th derivative of the
\ell
-times differentiable function
v\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
g\colon\mathbb{R}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a function. We represent the derivatives and the time variable with new variables:
u_{k}=v^{(k-1)}
for
k\in{\left\llbracket\ell\right\rrbracket}
and
u_{\ell+1}(t)=t
. For
u(t)=(u_{1}(t),\dots,u_{\ell+1}(t))^{\!\top}\!
, we obtain the ODE

\begin{pmatrix}\dot{u}_{1}(t)\\
\vdots\\
\dot{u}_{\ell-1}(t)\\
\dot{u}_{\ell}(t)\\
\dot{u}_{\ell+1}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{2}(t)\\
\vdots\\
u_{\ell}(t)\\
g(u_{\ell+1}(t),u_{1}(t),\dots,u_{\ell}(t))\\
1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(7)

It is of the form
\dot{u}(t)=f(u(t))
for a suitably chosen
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}}
, where
\tilde{d}:=d\ell+1
. Hence, (
7
) is a
\tilde{d}
-dimensional, autonomous, first-order ODE. If
u
is a solution to the ODE (
7
), then
u_{1}
is a solution of the ODE (
6
).

2.2
Formal Description of the General Model

We introduce a general model for observations from solutions of an ODE. It is similar to the one in
[
Lah+24
]
.

Notation 2.2
.

(i)

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
a\leq b
. For
i\in{\left\llbracket a,b\right\rrbracket}
, let
x_{i}
be some object. Set
x_{{\left\llbracket a,b\right\rrbracket}}:=(x_{i})_{i\in{\left\llbracket a,b\right\rrbracket}}
.

(ii)

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\left\|x\right\|_{2}
denote the Euclidean norm. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
, denote the operator norm as
\left\|A\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}:=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|v\|_{2}=1}\|Av\|_{2}
.
Let
d_{\mathsf{x}},d_{\mathsf{y}}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{y}}}
.
Denote the sup norm of
f
as
|f|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}}}\left\|f(x)\right\|_{2}
. For linear operators, like the derivative
Df(x)
, we abuse the notation slightly and set
|Df|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}}}\left\|Df(x)\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}
. For more details on derivatives and their norms, see appendix
B
.

(iii)

Let
d_{\mathsf{x}},d_{\mathsf{y}},\beta\in\mathbb{N}
. Denote the set of
\beta
-times continuously differentiable functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{y}}}
as
\mathcal{D}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}},\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{y}}})
. Let
L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\beta\right\rrbracket}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}\cup\{\infty\}
. Denote by
\bar{\Sigma}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}\to d_{\mathsf{y}}}(\beta,L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\beta\right\rrbracket}})\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}},\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{y}}})
the set of
\beta
-times continuously differentiable functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{y}}}
with
\left|D^{k}f\right|_{\infty}\leq L_{k}
for
k\in{\left\llbracket 0,\beta\right\rrbracket}
.
More details on derivatives and the smoothness class
\bar{\Sigma}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}\to d_{\mathsf{y}}}(\beta,L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\beta\right\rrbracket}})
are described in appendix
B
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be the dimension of the state space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
be the smoothness parameter. Let
L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\beta\right\rrbracket}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be the Lipschitz parameters. Let
\mathcal{F}_{d,\beta}:=\bar{\Sigma}^{d\to d}(\beta,L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\beta\right\rrbracket}})
be the smoothness class. Let the true model function be
f^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,\beta}
. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be the number of observed solutions and let
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be their initial conditions. For
j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket}
, let
n_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
be the number of observations for the
j
-th solution. Denote the total number of observations as
n:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}n_{j}
. For
j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket}
, let
T_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be the observation duration of the
j
-th solution. For
j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket},i\in{\left\llbracket 0,n_{j}\right\rrbracket}
, let
t_{j,i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be the observation times such that
0=t_{j,0}\leq t_{j,1}\leq\dots\leq t_{j,n_{j}}=T_{j}
.
Let
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be the variance bound of the noise. Let the noise variables
(\epsilon_{j,i})_{j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket},i\in{\left\llbracket n_{j}\right\rrbracket}}
be independent
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random variables such that
\mathbf{E}[\epsilon_{j,i}]=0
and
\mathbf{E}[\|\epsilon_{j,i}\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
. For
j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket}
,
i\in{\left\llbracket n_{j}\right\rrbracket}
, let the observations be

Y_{j,i}:=U(f^{\star},x_{j},t_{j,i})+\epsilon_{j,i}\,.

We observe
Y_{j,i}
and know
x_{j}
and
t_{j,i}
, but
f^{\star}
is unknown and to be estimated.
We assume
d
,
\beta
,
L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\beta\right\rrbracket}}
, and
\sigma
to be fixed and we are interested in upper bounds when
n\to\infty
for the mean squared error for estimators
\hat{f}
of
f^{\star}
on the domain of interest
[0,1]^{d}
.

2.3
Need for Restriction of the General Model

We argue that the model introduced in section
2.2
is not suitable for estimation of the model function in a fixed domain of interest and give first informal descriptions of two possible remedies of the problem: the Snake model and the Stubble model.

Notation 2.3
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Write

\displaystyle a_{n}\preccurlyeq b_{n}

\displaystyle\ \text{ for }\limsup_{n\to\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}<\infty\,,

\displaystyle a_{n}\prec b_{n}

\displaystyle\ \text{ for }\limsup_{n\to\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}=0\,,

\displaystyle a_{n}\asymp b_{n}

\displaystyle\ \text{ for }a_{n}\preccurlyeq b_{n}\text{ and }b_{n}\preccurlyeq a_{n}\,.

Define
\succ
and
\succcurlyeq
accordingly.

For the general model, consistent estimation is not possible when considering the domain of interest
[0,1]^{d}
. We can easily construct settings, where we will never make any observation in say in the ball
\mathrm{B}^{d}(0,1/3)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\|x\|_{2}\leq 1/3\}
, i.e.,
u(t_{j,i})\not\in\mathrm{B}^{d}(0,1/3)
for all
j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket},i\in{\left\llbracket 0,n_{j}\right\rrbracket}
, no matter how large
n
is (e.g., a model function that results in periodic circular trajectories centered at the origin with radius
2/3
when started from initial conditions
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{0\}^{d-2}
with
\|x_{j}\|_{2}=2/3
). As we are considering a nonparametric class of model functions
f^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,\beta}
, we have, for the maximal risk of any estimator
\hat{f}
,

\sup_{f^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,\beta}}\mathbf{E}_{f^{\star}}\!\left[\left\|f^{\star}(0)-\hat{f}(0)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\succcurlyeq 1\,.

This behavior is different from parametric ODE models (finite dimensional class
\mathcal{F}_{d,\beta}
): In the nonparametric setting, observations contain only local information (information about
f^{\star}(x)
for
x
close to
u(t_{j,i})
), whereas in a parametric setting, observations typically contain global information so that consistent estimation is possible under mild restrictions
[
Bru08
,
QZ10
,
GK12
,
DK15
]
.

In this work, we introduce two restrictions to the general nonparametric model that make consistent estimation possible:

In the
Stubble model
(section
3
), we require the initial conditions
(x_{j})_{j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket}}
to suitably cover the domain of interest
[0,1]^{d}
. By doing so, we ensure that we obtain information from every part of the domain of interest. In this model, we assume to observe many short trajectories, i.e.,
m\succ 1
and
\max_{j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket}}n_{j}\preccurlyeq 1
, which looks like
stubble
.

In the
Snake model
(section
4
), we directly require the trajectories
U(f^{\star},x_{j},[0,T_{j}])
to cover the domain of interest
[0,1]^{d}
. In this model, we assume to observe few long trajectories, i.e.,
\min_{j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket}}n_{j}\succ 1
. In the extreme case, we have
m=1
. Consistent estimation on all of
[0,1]^{d}
with one observed solution is only possible, if its trajectory covers
[0,1]^{d}
suitably. Thus, this trajectory must not intersect itself, as otherwise it would mean that the solution is periodic. This behavior of self-avoidance in a bounded domain resembles the video game
Snake

[
Don10
, chapter 22]
.

3
The Stubble Model and Estimation of the Increment Map

In the Stubble model, we observe many solutions to an ODE with different initial conditions. For each solution, we have only a few observations. The initial conditions are known and located so that they cover the domain of interest.

In this section, we first present an explicit estimation procedure for a Liptschitz-continuous class of model functions with optimal rate of convergence of the mean squared error at a point. The estimation procedure is based on a local constant estimator for the increment map and a linear interpolation for the model function. In the second part of this section, we generalize these results: For a general Hölder-smoothness class, we present a black box estimation strategy based on an arbitrary multivariate regression estimator for different increment maps and a univariate polynomial interpolation for the model function. If the nonparametric estimator enjoys certain optimality criteria with respect to the standard nonparametric regression problem, it induces an optimal procedure for nonparametric ODE estimation.

Proof

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume

.
Let

Then

Proof.

By the definition of

Plugging this into the definition of

This is the value of

Lemma

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\Delta\!t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Assume

. Let
\iota:=\Upsilon(f,\Delta\!t,\cdot)
.
Then

\sup_{x\in[0,1]^{d}}\left\|\frac{\iota(x)}{\Delta\!t}-f(x)\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}L_{0}L_{1}\Delta\!t\,.

Proof.

Let
x\in[0,1]^{d}
. With (
12
), as
f
is

-Lipschitz,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\iota(x)}{\Delta\!t}-f(x)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{\Delta\!t}\int_{0}^{\Delta\!t}f(U(f,x,s))-f(x)\mathrm{d}s\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{1}}{\Delta\!t}\int_{0}^{\Delta\!t}\left\|U(f,x,s)-x\right\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s\,.

Using
U(f,x,s)=x+\int_{0}^{s}f(U(f,x,r))\mathrm{d}r
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Delta\!t}\left\|U(f,x,s)-x\right\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\Delta\!t}\int_{0}^{s}\left\|f(U(f,x,r))\right\|_{2}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\Delta\!t}\int_{0}^{s}L_{0}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}L_{0}\Delta\!t^{2}\,.

Combining the above inequalities yields

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\iota(x)}{\Delta\!t}-f(x)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}L_{0}L_{1}\Delta\!t\,.

As this inequality holds for all

∎

3.2
General

We generalize the Lipschitz setting of section

If that estimator achieves the optimal rate of convergence for a standard nonparametric regression problem, the resulting ODE estimator is also optimal in a minimax sense.

4
The Snake Model and Estimation of the Observed Solutions

In the Snake model, we observe one (or a few) solution(s) to an ODE. For each solution, we have many observations. The associated estimation problem depends strongly on how the trajectories of the solutions are located in the state space.

In this section, we first present an explicit estimation procedure for a Lipschitz-continuous class of model functions and an upper bound on the maximal risk in sup norm. The estimation procedure is based on a local linear estimator for the solutions and a nearest neighbor interpolation for the model function. It is rate-optimal in some settings. In the second part of this section, we generalize these results: For a general Hölder-smoothness class, we present a black box estimation strategy based on a generic nonparametric estimator for the solutions and a multivariate polynomial interpolation for the model function. If the nonparametric estimator enjoys certain optimality criteria with respect to the standard nonparametric regression problem, it induces a procedure for nonparametric ODE estimation that is optimal in some settings.

4.1

In this specific instance of the general ODE estimation model, we consider Lipschitz-continuous model functions

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|\hat{u}(t)-u^{\star}(t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\left(\frac{T\log(n)}{n}\right)^{\frac{4}{5}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|\hat{\dot{u}}(t)-\dot{u}^{\star}(t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\left(\frac{T\log(n)}{n}\right)^{\frac{2}{5}}\,.

Together with

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\delta^{2}+\mathbf{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|\hat{u}(t)-u^{\star}(t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\mathbf{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|\hat{\dot{u}}(t)-\dot{u}^{\star}(t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\delta^{2}+\left(\frac{T\log(n)}{n}\right)^{\frac{4}{5}}+\left(\frac{T\log(n)}{n}\right)^{\frac{2}{5}}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\delta^{2}+\left(\frac{T\log(n)}{n}\right)^{\frac{2}{5}}\,.

4.2
General

We now want to consider estimation under higher order smoothness. We generalize the estimation procedure of section
4.1.2
as follows: We replace the local linear estimation of the solution
u^{\star}
and its derivative
\dot{u}^{\star}
by arbitrary regression estimators
\mathcal{E}^{1\to d}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{1\to d}
, respectively. E.g., this could be local polynomial estimators of the appropriate degree. Furthermore, the nearest neighbor interpolation is replaced by multivariate polynomial interpolation of the appropriate degree. The convergence rate results are given in a black box fashion, i.e., they depend on the convergence rates of the chosen regression estimators
\mathcal{E}^{1\to d}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{1\to d}
.

.

Multivariate Polynomial Interpolation

The estimation strategy for the model described in section

Definition 4.11

(Polynomial interpolation)
.

Let
\ell,d_{\mathsf{x}},d_{\mathsf{y}}\in\mathbb{N}
.

(i)

Set

N:=N_{{d_{\mathsf{x}}},\ell}:=\dim(\mathcal{P}_{d_{\mathsf{x}},\ell})=\begin{pmatrix}\ell+{d_{\mathsf{x}}}\\
{d_{\mathsf{x}}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(ii)

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}}
, denote the vector of monomials of degree at most
\ell
in
d_{\mathsf{x}}
dimensions as

\psi(x):=\psi_{\ell}(x):=(x^{\alpha})_{|\alpha|\leq\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,.

For
M\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}})^{M}
, denote
\Psi(\mathbf{x}):=\Psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}):=\left(\psi(x_{1}),\dots,\psi(x_{M})\right)^{\!\top}\!\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
.

(iii)

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}}
,
\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}})^{N}
with
\Psi(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,
\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{y}}})^{N}=\mathbb{R}^{N\times{d_{\mathsf{y}}}}
, denote the
componentwise polynomial interpolation
as

I(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\cdot):=I_{\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\cdot)\colon\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{y}}},\,x\mapsto\begin{cases}\psi(x)^{\!\top}\!\Psi(\mathbf{x})^{-1}\mathbf{y}&\text{ if }\Psi(\mathbf{x})\text{ is invertible,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Remark 4.12
.

(i)

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{x}}})^{N}
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{y}}})^{N}
.
The function
I(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\cdot)
is an
interpolation
as
I(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},x_{k})=y_{k}
. It is
polynomial
as
x\mapsto\Pi_{k}I(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},x)\in\mathcal{P}_{d_{\mathsf{x}},\ell}
for each
k\in{\left\llbracket d_{\mathsf{y}}\right\rrbracket}
. It is
componentwise
as

I(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},x)=\left(I\!\left(\mathbf{x},\left(\Pi_{k}y_{1},\dots\Pi_{k}y_{N}\right),x\right)\right)_{k\in{\left\llbracket d_{\mathsf{y}}\right\rrbracket}}\,.

(ii)

The definition of
I(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\cdot)=0
if
\Psi(\mathbf{x})
is not invertible is for convenience only. The value 0 is not of importance.

(iii)

If
d_{\mathsf{x}}=1
, the matrix
\Psi(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is also called
Vandermonde matrix
. It is invertible if and only if all
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
are distinct. In the multivariate case, conditions for invertibility of
\Psi(\mathbf{x})
(and hence, unique existence of the polynomial interpolation) are more complex, see
[
Olv06
, Theorem 4.1]
.

The geometric configuration of the base points
\mathbf{x}
for the multivariate polynomial interpolation influences the approximation quality. We next define a normalization that removes location and scale from
\mathbf{x}
to isolate the geometric configuration.

Definition 4.13

(Normalization)
.

Let
d,N\in\mathbb{N}
.
For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
with
\diam(\mathbf{x})>0
, let

\eta_{\mathbf{x}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,x\mapsto\frac{x-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}x_{k}}{\diam(\mathbf{x})}\,.

Furthermore, denote
\eta_{\mathbf{x}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=(\eta_{\mathbf{x}}(\tilde{x}_{1}),\dots,\eta_{\mathbf{x}}(\tilde{x}_{M}))
for
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=(\tilde{x}_{1},\dots,\tilde{x}_{M})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{M}
,
M\in\mathbb{N}
.

Next, we define the set
\mathcal{H}
of sets of base points
\mathbf{x}
that can be used in a polynomial interpolation to suitably approximate the function value at
x
, the set
\mathcal{G}
of points

with

, and an (almost) optimal choice

of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 4.14
.

Let
d,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
N=\dim(\mathcal{P}_{d,\ell})
.
Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

.

(i)

Let
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Define

as the set of all sets
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{N}
such that

and
\left\|\Psi(\eta_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}))^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\leq s
.

(ii)

Fix a constant
D\in(1,\infty)
.
Fix a constant
\mu\in(0,1/N)
.
Define
\mathcal{G}_{\ell,\delta,s}(\mathcal{X})
as the set of all points

with following property: There is a set of points
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell,s,\mu}(\mathcal{X},x)

.

(iii)

Let

be an element

such that

\left\|\Psi(\eta_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\diam(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{\ell+1}\leq 2\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell,2s,0}(\mathcal{X},x)}\left\|\Psi(\eta_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}))^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\diam(\mathbf{x})^{\ell+1}\,.

Remark 4.15
.

The parameters
\mu,s,D
are fixed and do not change with
n
. They ensure the stability of the multivariate polynomial interpolation and are used in some technical arguments of the main proof.

(i)

For the polynomial interpolation, we have to solve a system of linear equations given by the matrix
\Psi(\mathbf{x})
. The requirements
\left\|\Psi(\eta_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}))^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\leq s
and

ensure stability and precision of the obtained approximation.

(ii)

The lower bound on the diameter of the interpolation base points,

, ensures the stability of the normalization
\eta_{\mathbf{x}}
, where we divide by
\diam(\mathbf{x})
.

(iii)

Multivariate polynomial approximation results are known for points

in the convex hull of the base points
\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, see
Lemma

D.6
. To ensure that

belongs also to the convex hull of sufficiently accurate estimates of the base points

, we employ the concept of the

-interior of the convex hull: Specifically,

with

implies

if the estimation error is small enough relative

.

5
Extensions

This section explores simple extensions of error bounds proven in this article and highlights open questions in settings where further research is needed to achieve comprehensive results.

So far, we have shown upper bounds on the error for estimating the model function of an autonomous, first order ODE in two different settings:
In the Stubble model of
m\asymp n
short (
n_{j}\asymp 1
) trajectories with equidistant measurement times, we obtained a bound on the point-wise mean squared error, see
Corollary

3.13
.
In the Snake model of a single trajectory (
m=1
) with
n_{1}\asymp n
measurements with a flexible time step design and sub-Gaussian noise, we obtained a bound on the sup-norm error in probability, see
Corollary

4.18
.

Both results are available in a black box form (
Theorem

3.11
and
Theorem

4.17
, respectively), where arbitrary nonparametric regression estimators can be plugged in. This makes these results quite powerful. E.g., we can apply the black box results with adaptive nonparametric regression estimators to obtain adaptive ODE estimators. In this manner, questions regarding hyperparameter optimization can be answered in the same way as for the regression problem. Only the multivariate polynomial interpolation in the general case of the Snake model with
\beta\geq 2
requires additional hyperparameters (
s
,
D
) regarding the stability of the interpolation. But note that the major purpose of the proposed estimator in this setting is the mathematical derivation of upper bounds on the estimation error (which are minimax optimal in some settings) rather than demonstrating a practical algorithm. In particular, computational requirements may be unfeasible. In contrast, in the Snake model with
\beta=1
and in the Stubble model, the proposed estimators do not suffer from such obstacles for application.

It is straight forward to extend the error bounds in the Stubble model to integrated and sup-norm loss (the latter typically requires sub-Gaussian noise and adds a factor that is polynomial in
\log(n)
to the variance part of the error). Furthermore, the bound in expectation implies the bound in probability, so that we can obtain results that are weaker but more directly comparable to those of the Snake model.
An extension of the Snake model results to point-wise or integrated errors (without the log-factor) seems more difficult as our proof already requires a bound on the maximal error of the initial regressions, independent of the target error measure.

Further trivial extensions include: going from
m=1
to
m\asymp 1
in the Snake model (even some slowly growing
m
seems possible without much difficulty); non-equidistant timesteps in the Stubble model given that they are equal between different trajectories and asymptotically equal; treating non-autonomous and higher order ODEs as autonomous first order ODEs, see section
2.1
.

Finally, an open question that is more difficult to answer is how we construct a suitable estimator with minimax optimal rates of convergence in the cases with few or an intermediate amount of trajectories (
m\succcurlyeq 1
,
n_{j}\succ 1
) that is not (optimally) covered by the estimator of the Snake model, i.e., in the case

\delta\prec\left(\frac{T}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(\beta+1)+1}}\,.

Note that this setting is particularly interesting for the study of chaotic systems
[
Str24
]
, where one expects to revisit points arbitrarily close to past points in the future of the same long trajectory, i.e.,
\delta
is small.
Some practical algorithms for such a setting are compared in
[
Sch+24
]
in a simulation study.

Appendix A
Relation to Other Models

The ODE models introduced in this article are novel, and the problem of nonparametric ODE estimation has received almost no attention in the mathematical statistics literature. Thus, it is prudent to compare these models with more established ones.

Regression on the solutions.
The ODE model can be viewed as a regression model, with the ODE as a constraint on the regression function
u^{\star}=U(f^{\star},x,\cdot)
in
[0,T]
. Solutions have a smoothness parameter
\tilde{\beta}=\beta+1
if the model function is
\beta
-smooth. Thus, we can solve the reanalysis problem, i.e., estimation of
u^{\star}
in
[0,T]
, with a the standard nonparametric rate of convergence for the squared error

\mathbf{E}\!\left[\left\|\hat{u}(t)-u^{\star}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\preccurlyeq\left(\frac{T}{n}\right)^{\frac{2(\beta+1)}{2(\beta+1)+1}}

by ignoring the ODE-constraint and using a suitable estimator
\hat{u}
, see, e.g.,
[
Tsy09
]
. Furthermore, the derivative of the solution can similarly be estimated with

\mathbf{E}\!\left[\left\|\hat{\dot{u}}(t)-\dot{u}^{\star}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\preccurlyeq\left(\frac{T}{n}\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2(\beta+1)+1}}\,.

As
\dot{u}^{\star}(t)=f^{\star}(u^{\star}(t))
, this essentially yields an estimate of
f^{\star}
on the set
u^{\star}([0,T])
. This view is taken for our estimator in the Snake model of section
4
.

Minimax rate of nonparametric regression.

For standard nonparametric estimation of the
s
-th derivative of a
\tilde{\beta}
-smooth regression function
g^{\star}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
(or
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
), we have the minimax rate

\mathbf{E}\!\left[\left\|\widehat{D^{s}g}(x)-D^{s}g^{\star}(x)\right\|^{2}\right]\preccurlyeq n^{-\frac{2(\tilde{\beta}-s)}{2\tilde{\beta}+d}}\,,

(33)

see, e.g., appendix
C
.
In the ODE model, we estimate a first derivative (
s=1
) in
d
-dimensions of the solutions that have smoothness
\tilde{\beta}=\beta+1
. So, the rate of convergence that we obtain in this work (
1
) fits the formula of the minimax regression rate (
33
), even though the models are quite different.

Errors in variables.

Assume that time steps are constant, i.e.,
t_{i+1}-t_{i}=\Delta\!t
. Consider the so-called propagator
x\mapsto U(f^{\star},x,\Delta\!t)
. If
\Delta\!t\to 0
, we can infer
f
from it, as

\frac{U(f^{\star},x,\Delta\!t)-x}{\Delta\!t}=\frac{U(f^{\star},x,\Delta\!t)-U(f^{\star},x,0)}{\Delta\!t}\xrightarrow{\Delta\!t\to 0}f^{\star}(x)\,.

Estimating the propagator from the data
(Y_{i-1},Y_{i})
can be viewed as an errors-in-variables regression problem, e.g.,
[
MRS12
]
. In such models, the response
g(x_{i})
as well as the predictors
x_{i}
are unknown and observed with noise. Specifically, the data are given as
Z_{i}=g(x_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
and
X_{i}=x_{i}+\xi_{i}
, where
\varepsilon_{i}
and
\xi_{i}
are noise terms. Setting
(X_{i},Z_{i})=(Y_{i-1},Y_{i})
aligns the problem of propagator estimation in the ODE model with errors-in-variables regression. The Stubble model of section
3
avoids the difficulties attached to errors-in-variables by assuming known initial conditions and observing many solutions for only a short time.

Nonparametric time series analysis.

The ODE model with the sequence
(Y_{i})
can be viewed from the perspective of nonparametric time series analysis, where discrete-time stochastic processes in one variable (time) are studied. See, e.g.,
[
FY03
, Chapter 6]
. On one hand, the focus on discrete time and often only one state dimension, allows for a deep understanding of more complex time evolutions than in an one-dimensional ODE model. On the other hand, it seems that the structure of higher dimensional, time continuous ODEs with measurement noise is typically not captured in such models.

Stochastic differential equations.

The noise in stochastic differential equation (SDE) models, e.g.,
[
Str16
,
CG20
]
can be described as
system noise
in contrast to the
measurement noise
of the ODE model: It changes the state of the system and solutions are stochastic processes. In the ODE model, the state of the system is not influenced by the noise and solutions are deterministic. In comparison to a model with observed solutions of a SDE in one variable (time), estimation in the ODE model seems more difficult: The observation
Z_{t}
at time
t
of the solution
(Z_{t})
in the SDE model is the true state of the system at that time. If the SDE is Markovian, then the distribution of the future states
(Z_{t^{\prime}})_{t^{\prime}>t}
only depends on the now known state
Z_{t}
. In the ODE model, we do not know the true state
u^{\star}(t)
, but only a noisy observation of it. Even though the future evolution is deterministic, it can be hard to predict if the initial state is not known perfectly (see the topic of
chaotic systems
, e.g., in
[
Str24
]
). Despite these differences, there are similarities between the literature on SDE drift estimation and the two models discussed in this article:
[
Str16
]
considers a single, increasingly long trajectory of an SDE solution, analogous to the Snake model, while
[
CG20
]
considers an increasing number of solutions over a fixed time interval, akin to the Stubble model.

Hidden markov models.

As the true state of the system
u^{\star}(t)
is hidden from us and only noisily observed, the ODE model is connected to hidden Markov models (HMMs), e.g.,
[
BRR98
]
. In HMMs, one observes
Z_{i}
which depends on the unobserved random variable
X_{i}
, which in turn depends on
X_{i-1}
. But
Z_{i}
is independent of
X_{i-1}
and
Z_{i-1}
given
X_{i}
. If we set
X_{i}=u^{\star}(t_{i})
,
Z_{i}=Y_{i}
. We can view the ODE model as a HMM. In contrast to HMMs, the transition from
u^{\star}(t_{i})
to
u^{\star}(t_{i+1})
is deterministic, and the time and state domains are continuous.

A bound on the derivative yields the Lipschitz constant, as the next lemma shows.

Lemma B.3
.

Let

.
Let

Proof of
Lemma

Let

\left|D^{k-1}_{\mathbf{v}}f(x)-D^{k-1}_{\mathbf{v}}f(\tilde{x})\right|\leq\sup_{\tilde{v}\in\mathbb{D}_{d}}\left|D^{k}_{(\mathbf{v},\tilde{v})}f\right|_{\infty}\left\|x-\tilde{x}\right\|_{2}

\left\|D^{k-1}f(x)-D^{k-1}f(\tilde{x})\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\leq\left|D^{k}f\right|_{\infty}\left\|x-\tilde{x}\right\|_{2}\,.

\displaystyle=D_{\mathbf{v}}q_{x}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(x)R_{i}(x)\,.

Definition C.12

(Sub-Gaussian process)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
be a metric space.
A real-valued stochastic process
(Z_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
is
sub-Gaussian
if

\mathbf{P}\!\left(\left|Z_{x}-Z_{x^{\prime}}\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\rho(x,x^{\prime})^{2}}\right)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Lemma C.13
.

Let
\epsilon_{i}
be independent, centered, sub-Gaussian random variables with variance parameters
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
Let
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
be a metric space.
Let
b_{i}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Define
Z_{x}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(x)\epsilon_{i}
.
Then
Z_{x}
is sub-Gaussian with variance parameter
v_{x}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(x)^{2}v_{i}
.
Furthermore,
(Z_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
is sub-Gaussian with
\rho(x,x^{\prime})^{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(b_{i}(x)-b_{i}(x^{\prime})\right)^{2}v_{i}
.

Proof.

The results stem from the well-known fact that a linear combination of independent sub-Gaussian variables is again sub-Gaussian. It can be checked using the following equivalent condition for sub-Gaussian:
A centered random variable
X
is sub-Gaussian with variance parameter
v
if and only if
\mathbf{E}[\exp(\lambda X)]\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}v}{2}\right)
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
∎

Definition C.14

(Covering Number)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
be a metric space,
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}

Lemma C.15
.

Let

x^{\otimes k}=x^{\otimes(k-1)}\otimes x
.
Denote

M:=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}d^{k}=\frac{d^{\ell+1}-1}{d-1}\,.

Define the monomial transform without commutativity
\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
as

\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}(x):=\begin{pmatrix}x^{\otimes 0}\\
x^{\otimes 1}\\
\vdots\\
x^{\otimes\ell}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Our intermediate goal is to find a matrix
\tilde{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
with the property

\tilde{\psi}(Bx+b)=\tilde{A}\tilde{\psi}(x)\,.

For this, we first recall some properties of the Kronecker product:
The Kronecker product is bilinear. In particular,

M_{1}\otimes(M_{2}+M_{3})=M_{1}\otimes M_{2}+M_{1}\otimes M_{3}\ \text{and}\ (M_{1}+M_{2})\otimes M_{3}=M_{1}\otimes M_{3}+M_{2}\otimes M_{3}

for compatible matrices
M_{1},\dots,M_{3}
.
Moreover, The mixed-product property of the Kronecker product states

\left(M_{1}\otimes M_{2}\right)\left(M_{3}\otimes M_{4}\right)=\left(M_{1}M_{3}\right)\otimes\left(M_{2}M_{4}\right)

for compatible matrices
M_{1},\dots,M_{4}
.
The Kronecker product is not commutative, but
M_{1}\otimes M_{2}
and
M_{2}\otimes M_{1}
are
permutation equivalent
: There are permutation matrices
P,Q
such that

M_{1}\otimes M_{2}=P(M_{2}\otimes M_{1})Q

for compatible matrices
M_{1},M_{2}
.

Now, using the mixed-product property, we obtain by induction

\left(Bx\right)^{\otimes k}=B^{\otimes k}x^{\otimes k}\,.

(45)

Next, the bilinearity and the permutation equivalence imply, also using induction, there are matrices
\bar{A}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{k}\times d^{j}}

\left(b+x\right)^{\otimes k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\bar{A}_{j}x^{\otimes j}\,.

(46)

Thus, there are matrices
\tilde{A}_{0},\tilde{A}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
such that

\tilde{\psi}(x+b)=\tilde{A}_{0}\tilde{\psi}(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{\psi}(Bx)=\tilde{A}_{1}\tilde{\psi}(x)

and, hence,

\tilde{\psi}(Bx+b)=\tilde{A}\tilde{\psi}(x)

with
\tilde{A}=\tilde{A}_{0}\tilde{A}_{1}
.
From (
45
), we can see that
\tilde{A}_{1}
is block diagonal with blocks
B^{\otimes k}
.
From (
46
), we can see that
\tilde{A}_{0}
is block triangular with identity blocks
I_{d^{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{k}\times d^{k}}
on the diagonal.
Hence,
\tilde{A}
is block triangular with blocks
B^{\otimes k}
on the diagonal.
For the determinant of the Kronecker product, we have

\det(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})=\det(M_{1})^{d_{2}}\det(M_{2})^{d_{1}}

(47)

for
M_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}}
and
M_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times d_{2}}
.
Thus,

\det\!\left(\tilde{A}\right)=\prod_{k=0}^{\ell}\det\!\left(B^{\otimes k}\right)=\det(B)^{\kappa}\,,

where

\kappa:=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}kd^{k-1}=\begin{cases}\frac{\ell(1+\ell)}{2}&\text{ if }d=1\\
\frac{1+\ell d^{\ell+1}-(\ell+1)d^{\ell}}{(d-1)^{2}}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

To see this, show inductively using (
47
) that

\det(B^{\otimes k}):=\det(B)^{kd^{k-1}}\,.

Now, we have the existence of
\tilde{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
with

\tilde{\psi}(Bx+b)=\tilde{A}\tilde{\psi}(x)

and the property that if
B
is invertible so is
\tilde{A}
. In the final step, we covert from
\tilde{\psi}
to
\psi
via a linear transformation of full rank:
Let
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M},\tilde{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
be linear mappings that fulfill

\psi_{\ell}(x)=Z\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}(x)=\tilde{Z}\psi_{\ell}(x)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Note that the rank of
Z
and of
\tilde{Z}
is
N
, i.e., both matrices have full rank.
Set

A:=Z\tilde{A}\tilde{Z}\,.

Then

\psi(Bx+b)=Z\tilde{\psi}(Bx+b)=Z\tilde{A}\tilde{\psi}(x)=Z\tilde{A}\tilde{Z}\psi(x)=A\psi(x)\,.

Moreover, if
B
is invertible, so is
\tilde{A}
. Then, as
\tilde{A}
,
\tilde{Z}
, and
Z
have full rank,
A
also has full rank and is invertible.
∎

Lemma D.8

(Perturbed normalization)
.

Let
N,d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}),\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{N}
.
Assume

\max_{k\in{\left\llbracket N\right\rrbracket}}\left\|\eta_{\mathbf{x}}(x_{k})-\eta_{\mathbf{z}}(z_{k})\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{4\gamma}{\diam(\mathbf{x})-2\gamma}\,.

Proof.

By

4.13
,

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathbf{x}}(x_{k})-\eta_{\mathbf{z}}(z_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}}{\diam(\mathbf{x})}-\frac{z_{k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}}{\diam(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}}{\diam(\mathbf{x})}-\frac{x_{k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}}{\diam(\mathbf{z})}+\frac{x_{k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}}{\diam(\mathbf{z})}-\frac{z_{k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}}{\diam(\mathbf{z})}\,.

Using the definition of
\gamma
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\left(x_{k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\right)-\left(z_{k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma\,,

\displaystyle\left|\diam(\mathbf{x})-\diam(\mathbf{z})\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma\,.

As we assume
2\gamma<\diam(\mathbf{x})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\eta_{\mathbf{x}}(x_{k})-\eta_{\mathbf{z}}(z_{k})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x_{k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\right\|_{2}\left|\frac{1}{\diam(\mathbf{z})}-\frac{1}{\diam(\mathbf{x})}\right|+\frac{2\gamma}{\diam(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle\leq\diam(\mathbf{x})\left|\frac{1}{\diam(\mathbf{x})-2\gamma}-\frac{1}{\diam(\mathbf{x})}\right|+\frac{2\gamma}{\diam(\mathbf{x})-2\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\gamma}{\diam(\mathbf{x})-2\gamma}\,.

Thus,

\max_{k\in{\left\llbracket N\right\rrbracket}}\left\|\eta_{\mathbf{x}}(x_{k})-\eta_{\mathbf{z}}(z_{k})\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{4\gamma}{\diam(\mathbf{x})-2\gamma}\,.

Lemma D.9

(Perturbed monomials)
.

Let
d,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and

.
Then

\left\|\psi_{\ell}(x)-\psi_{\ell}(z)\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{N}\ell\left\|x-z\right\|_{2}\,,

where

is a constant depending only on
d,\ell
.

Proof.

We first need following simple result:
For

, we have

\left|a_{1}a_{2}-b_{1}b_{2}\right|\leq\left|a_{1}a_{2}-a_{1}b_{2}\right|+\left|a_{1}b_{2}-b_{1}b_{2}\right|\leq\left|a_{2}-b_{2}\right|+\left|a_{1}-b_{1}\right|\,.

(49)

By induction, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and

, we obtain

\left|\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}-\prod_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left|a_{i}-b_{i}\right|\,.

(50)

Now, denote the vector components
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=x
and
(z_{1},\dots,z_{d})=z
.
Recall
Notation

3.8
for monomials.
Let
i\in{\left\llbracket d\right\rrbracket}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with
\left|\alpha\right|\leq\ell
. Then, as

, we obtain from (

),

\left|x^{\alpha}-z^{\alpha}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\left|x_{i}-z_{i}\right|\,.

Thus, using Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle\left\|\psi_{\ell}(x)-\psi_{\ell}(z)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\left|\alpha\right|\leq\ell}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\left|x_{i}-z_{i}\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\left|\alpha\right|\leq\ell}\left|\alpha\right|\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\left|x_{i}-z_{i}\right|^{2}

∎

Lemma D.10

(Perturbed inverse matrix)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Assume that
A
is invertible and

\omega:=\left\|A-B\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\left\|A^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}<1\,.

Then
B
is invertible and

\left\|A^{-1}-B^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\leq\frac{\omega}{1-\omega}\left\|A^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\,.

Proof.

For
k\in{\left\llbracket d\right\rrbracket}
, let
\sigma_{k}(A)
be the
k
-th singular value of
A
in increasing order, i.e.,

\sigma_{1}(A)\leq\dots\leq\sigma_{d}(A)\,.

Then
\sigma_{1}(A)>0
as
A
is invertible. Furthermore,
\|A^{-1}\|_{\mathsf{op}}=\sigma_{1}(A)^{-1}
.
By Weyl’s Theorem, we have

\left|\sigma_{k}(A)-\sigma_{k}(B)\right|\leq\left\|A-B\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}

for all

.
In particular,

\sigma_{1}(B)\geq\sigma_{1}(A)-\left\|A-B\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}=\sigma_{1}(A)(1-\omega)>0\,.

Thus,
B
is invertible.
Next, we have

\displaystyle A^{-1}-B^{-1}=A^{-1}(B-A)B^{-1}\,.

Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|A^{-1}-B^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|A-B\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\left\|A^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\left\|B^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|A-B\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\left\|A^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\left(\sigma_{1}(A)(1-\omega)\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{1-\omega}\left\|A^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\,.

∎

E.1
Difference Bound

Lemma E.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be Lipschitz continuous. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Set
u:=U(f,x,\cdot)
. Let
v\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable with
v(0)\leq x
and
\dot{v}(t)\leq f(v(t))
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Then

v(t)\leq u(t)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Proof.

Assume there is
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with
v(t_{1})>u(t_{1})
. Then the first intersection time
t_{0}:=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mid v(t)>u(t)\}
exists. As
v
and
u
are continuous, we have
v(t_{0})=u(t_{0})
.
Let
t_{2}=\sup\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq t_{0}}\mid\forall s\in[t_{0},t]\colon v(s)\geq u(s)\}
. Because of the existence of
t_{1}
and continuity of
u
and
v
, we have
t_{2}>t_{0}
.
Let
L\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be the Lipschitz constant of
f
.
Set
\delta(t):=v(t)-u(t)
. Let
t\in[t_{0},t_{2}]
. Then

\displaystyle\delta(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\dot{\delta}(s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t}f(v(s))-f(u(s))\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq L\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\delta(s)\mathrm{d}s\,.

As
\delta
is continuous, there is
t_{\mathsf{max}}\in\argmax_{t\in[t_{0},\min(t_{0}+1/L,t_{2})]}\delta(t)
. By definition of
t_{0}
and
t_{\mathsf{max}}
, we have
\delta(t_{\mathsf{max}})>0
. Thus, using
\delta(t_{0})=0
, the continuity of
\delta
, and
t_{\mathsf{max}}-t_{0}\leq 1/L
, we obtain

\delta(t_{\mathsf{max}})\leq L\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{\mathsf{max}}}\delta(s)\mathrm{d}s<L\left(t_{\mathsf{max}}-t_{0}\right)\delta(t_{\mathsf{max}})\leq\delta(t_{\mathsf{max}})\,,

a contradiction.
∎

Lemma E.2
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
L\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Let

. Let

. Denote

,

. Then, for

\left\|u(t)-v(t)\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\exp(Lt)-1}{L}\left|f-g\right|_{\infty}\,.

(52)

In particular, if

, then

\left\|u(t)-v(t)\right\|_{2}\leq 2t\left|f-g\right|_{\infty}\,.

Proof.

Set
C:=\left|f-g\right|_{\infty}
.
We represent the right-hand side of (

) with the solution

Next consider the left-hand side of (

Define

\displaystyle w(t)

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{t}f(u(s))-g(v(s))\mathrm{d}s\right\|_{2}

Thus,

E.1
yields,

Lemma E.4
.

Let

Then

Proof.

For

is true.
Now assume

E.3
Smoothness of Solutions in the Initial Conditions

We state a simple consequence of the basic form of Grönwall’s inequality, which we will make use of below.

Lemma E.8

(Grönwall’s inequality)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
u\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be differentiable. Let
L\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Assume

\left\|\dot{u}(t)\right\|_{2}\leq L\left\|u(t)\right\|_{2}\qquad\text{for }t\in\mathbb{R}\,.

Then, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|u(t)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|u(0)\right\|_{2}\exp(L\left|t\right|)\,,

\displaystyle\left\|u(t)-u(0)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|u(0)\right\|_{2}\left(\exp(L\left|t\right|)-1\right)\,.

Proof.

We assume below that
t>0
and
u(t)\neq 0
for all
s\in[0,t]
.
If
t<0
, consider
\tilde{u}(t)=u(-t)
instead. If there are
s\in[0,t]
with
u(s)=0
, the proof applies to all intervals
[a,b]
with
u(\tilde{s})\neq 0
for
\tilde{s}\in[a,b]
, which yields an upper bound that is at least as strong as claimed.

Assume
t>0
and
u(t)\neq 0
. Set
v(t):=\left\|u(t)\right\|_{2}
. Then

\dot{v}(t)=\frac{u(t)^{\!\top}\!\dot{u}(t)}{\left\|u(t)\right\|_{2}}\leq\left\|\dot{u}(t)\right\|_{2}\leq L\left\|u(t)\right\|_{2}=Lv(t)\,.

Thus, by Grönwall’s inequality in its standard form, we have

v(t)\leq v(0)\exp(Lt)\,,

which is the first inequality that we want to prove.
Now, we can calculate

\displaystyle\left\|u(t)-u(0)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\dot{u}(s)\right\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq L\int_{0}^{t}v(s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq L\left\|u(0)\right\|_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\exp(Ls)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\left\|u(0)\right\|_{2}\left(\exp(Lt)-1\right)\,.

∎

Lemma E.9

(Derivative with respect to initial conditions)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
L_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Let
f\in\bar{\Sigma}^{d\to d}(1,\infty,L_{1})
.
Then, for all
t\geq 0
,

\left|DU(f,\cdot,t)\right|_{\infty}\leq\exp(L_{1}t)\quad\text{and}\quad\left|D\Upsilon(f,t,\cdot)\right|_{\infty}\leq\exp(L_{1}t)-1\,.

Proof.

Let
v\in\mathbb{D}_{d}
be a direction.
Set
u(t):=u_{x}(t):=D_{x,v}U(f,x,t)
denote the derivative of the solution
U(f,x,t)
with respect to the initial conditions
x
.
Then, using the chain rule
Lemma

B.5

(ii)
,

\displaystyle\dot{u}(t)

\displaystyle=D_{x,v}\dot{U}(f,x,t)

\displaystyle=D_{x,v}\left(f(U(f,x,t))\right)

\displaystyle=Df(U(f,x,t))[D_{x,v}U(f,x,t)]

\displaystyle=Df(U(f,x,t))[u(t)]\,.

Furthermore,

u(0)=D_{x,v}U(f,x,0)=v\,.

We have established the differential inequality

\left\|\dot{u}(t)\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|(Df)(U(f,x,t))[u(t)]\right\|_{2}\leq L_{1}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{2}

with
\|u(0)\|_{2}=1
. Thus, by
Lemma

E.8
,

\left\|u(t)\right\|_{2}\leq\exp(L_{1}\left|t\right|)\,.

Hence,

\displaystyle\left|DU(f,\cdot,t)\right|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{D}_{d}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|D_{x,v}U(f,x,t)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(L_{1}t)\,.

Set
u^{\circ}(t):=u(t)-u(0)
. Then,
\dot{u}^{\circ}=\dot{u}
and
u^{\circ}(0)=0
. Thus, using
Lemma

E.8
again,

\left\|u^{\circ}(t)\right\|_{2}\leq\exp(L_{1}t)-1\,.

We obtain,

\left|D\Upsilon(f,t,\cdot)\right|_{\infty}=\left|DU(f,\cdot,t)-DU(f,\cdot,0)\right|_{\infty}\leq\exp(L_{1}t)-1\,.

∎

For higher order derivatives of
U(f,x,t)
with respect to

, we used the tree derivatives in

E.6
. For higher order derivatives of

Definition E.10
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

(i)

Let
\mathbb{A}_{k}
be the set of partitions of
{\left\llbracket k\right\rrbracket}
.

(ii)

Let

\mathbf{v}

\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{v}):=\left\{(v_{A_{1}},\dots,v_{A_{\ell}})\,\kern-1.2pt\mathopen{}\left|\vphantom{(v_{A_{1}},\dots,v_{A_{\ell}})}\,(A_{1},\dots,A_{\ell})\in\mathbb{A}_{k}\right.\mathclose{}\kern-1.2pt\right\}\,.

(iii)

Let
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{D}_{d}^{k}
. Let

.
Let
\pi=(\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{v})
.
Denote the
partition derivative
of

as

D^{\pi}(f\circ g)(x):=(D^{\ell}f)(g(x))[D_{\mathbf{v}_{1}}g(x),\dots,D_{\mathbf{v}_{\ell}}g(x)]\,.

The following theorem is a version of Faà di Bruno’s formula
[
Cra05
]
.

Theorem E.11
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{D}_{d}^{k}
. Let
f\in\mathcal{D}^{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Assume that
f
is Lipschitz continuous.
Then

D_{x,\mathbf{v}}\dot{U}(f,x,t)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{v})}D^{\pi}\left(f\circ U(f,\cdot,t)\right)(x)\,.

Proof.

For
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{D}_{d}^{k}
, denote
u_{\mathbf{v}}(x,t):=D_{x,\mathbf{v}}U(f,x,t)
.
We prove the statement by induction over
k
. See the proof of
Lemma

E.9
for
k=1
.
Assume the statement is true for all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{D}_{d}^{k}
.
Let
v\in\mathbb{D}_{d}
and
\mathbf{v^{\prime}}:=(\mathbf{v},v)\in\mathbb{D}_{d}^{k+1}
. Then

\displaystyle\dot{u}_{\mathbf{v^{\prime}}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=D_{x,v}\dot{u}_{\mathbf{v}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\mathbf{v_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{v_{\ell}})\in\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{v})}D_{x,v}\left(D^{\ell}f(U(f,x,t))[u_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}(x,t),\dots,u_{\mathbf{v_{\ell}}}(x,t)]\right)

By the product rule,
Lemma

B.5

(i)
,

\displaystyle D_{x,v}\left((D^{\ell}f)(U(f,x,t))[u_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}(x,t),\dots,u_{\mathbf{v_{\ell}}}(x,t)]\right)

\displaystyle=D_{x,v}\left(D^{\ell}f(U(f,x,t))\right)[u_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}(x,t),\dots,u_{\mathbf{v_{\ell}}}(x,t)]

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\ +\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left((D^{\ell}f)(U(f,x,t))[u_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}(x,t),\dots,D_{x,v}u_{\mathbf{v_{j}}}(x,t),\dots,u_{\mathbf{v_{\ell}}}(x,t)]\right)\,.

By the chain rule,
Lemma

B.5

(ii)
,

\displaystyle D_{x,v}\left(D^{\ell}f(U(f,x,t))\right)[u_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}(x,t),\dots,u_{\mathbf{v_{\ell}}}(x,t)]=D^{\ell+1}f(U(f,x,t))[u_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}(x,t),\dots,u_{\mathbf{v_{\ell}}}(x,t),u_{v}(x,t)]\,.

The set of all partitions of
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
can be created from the set of all partitions of
\mathbf{v}
as follows: Let

\mathbf{v_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{v_{\ell}}
be a partition of
\mathbf{v}
. Then

and

(the order of the partition components does not matter). Iterating this construction over all partitions of
\mathbf{v}
creates all partitions of
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
.

Now putting all previous equations in this proof together, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{x,\mathbf{v}^{\prime}}\dot{U}(f,x,t)

\displaystyle=\dot{u}_{\mathbf{v^{\prime}}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\mathbf{v_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{v_{\ell}})\in\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{v}^{\prime})}D^{\ell}f(U(f,x,t))[u_{\mathbf{v_{1}}}(x,t),\dots,u_{\mathbf{v_{\ell}}}(x,t)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{v}^{\prime})}D^{\pi}\left(f\circ U(f,\cdot,t)\right)(x)\,.

.

(55)
