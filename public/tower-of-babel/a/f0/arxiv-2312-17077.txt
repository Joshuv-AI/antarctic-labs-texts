Projected Langevin Monte Carlo algorithms in non-convex and super-linear setting 1 1 footnotemark: 1
Chenxu Pang, Xiaojie Wang, Yue Wu

Abstract
It is of significant interest in many applications to sample from a high-dimensional target distribution π \pi with the density π ⁡ ( d ​ x ) ∝ e − U ⁡ ( x ) ​ ( d ​ x ) \pi(\text{d}x)\propto e^{-U(x)}(\text{d}x) , based on the temporal discretization of the Langevin stochastic differential equations (SDEs). In this paper, we propose an explicit projected Langevin Monte Carlo (PLMC) algorithm with non-convex potential U U and super-linear gradient of U U and investigate the non-asymptotic analysis of its sampling error in total variation distance. Equipped with time-independent regularity estimates for the associated Kolmogorov equation, we derive the non-asymptotic bounds on the total variation distance between the target distribution of the Langevin SDEs and the law induced by the PLMC scheme with order 𝒪 ⁡ ( d max ⁡ { 3 ​ γ / 2 , 2 ​ γ − 1 } ​ h ​ | ln ⁡ h | ) \mathcal{O}(d^{\max\{3\gamma/2,2\gamma-1\}}h|\ln h|) , where d d is the dimension of the target distribution and γ ≥ 1 \gamma\geq 1 characterizes the growth of the gradient of U U . In addition, if the gradient of U U is globally Lipschitz continuous, an improved convergence order of 𝒪 ⁡ ( d 3 / 2 ​ h ) \mathcal{O}(d^{3/2}h) for the classical Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) scheme is derived with a refinement of the proof based on Malliavin calculus techniques. To achieve a given precision ϵ \epsilon , the smallest number of iterations of the PLMC algorithm is proved to be of order 𝒪 ⁡ ( d max ⁡ { 3 ​ γ / 2 , 2 ​ γ − 1 } ϵ ⋅ ln ⁡ ( d ϵ ) ⋅ ln ⁡ ( 1 ϵ ) ) {\mathcal{O}}\big(\tfrac{d^{\max\{3\gamma/2,2\gamma-1\}}}{\epsilon}\cdot\ln(\tfrac{d}{\epsilon})\cdot\ln(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon})\big) . In particular, the classical Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) scheme with the non-convex potential U U and the globally Lipschitz gradient of U U can be guaranteed by order 𝒪 ⁡ ( d 3 / 2 ϵ ⋅ ln ⁡ ( 1 ϵ ) ) {\mathcal{O}}\big(\tfrac{d^{3/2}}{\epsilon}\cdot\ln(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon})\big) . Numerical experiments are provided to confirm the theoretical findings. AMS subject classification: 60H35, 65C05, 65C30. Key Words: Langevin Monte Carlo sampling, total variation distance, non-convex potential, super-linear setting, projected scheme, Kolmogorov equations.

Introduction

Sampling from a high-dimensional (
d\gg 1
) target distribution
\pi
plays a crucial role in various fields such as Bayesian inference, statistical physics, machine learning and computational biology and has been a subject of recent intensive research efforts.
For example, evaluating the expectation of some functional
\phi
with respect to
\pi
:

\pi(\phi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\pi(\text{d}x),

(1.1)

is of great interests in applications in the area of
Bayesian statistics.
A typical approach of sampling is to set the target measure
\pi
as the invariant measure of the stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and undertake an appropriate numerical scheme that discretizes such SDEs in time.
More precisely,
we consider a class of sampling methods based on the following overdamped Langevin stochastic differential equations (SDEs) of Itô type:

\text{d}X_{t}=-\nabla U(X_{t})\,\text{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\text{d}W_{t},\quad X_{0}=x_{0},\quad t>0,

(1.2)

where

W_{\cdot}=\left(W_{1,\cdot},\dots,W_{d,\cdot}\right)^{T}:[0,\infty)\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued standard Brownian motion with respect to
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\in[0,T]}

and

the initial datum

x_{0}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is assumed to be
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable.
Under certain conditions,
the dynamics of (
1.2
)

admits a unique invariant distribution

\pi

with the density proportional to
x\mapsto e^{-U(x)}
.

To asymptotically sample from
\pi
,

which is the the invariant distribution of (
1.2
),

one notable example is the unadjusted Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC for short) algorithm, which corresponds to the well-known Euler-Maruyama scheme of the Langevin SDE (
1.2
),
given by

\widetilde{Y}_{n+1}=\widetilde{Y}_{n}-\nabla U\big(\widetilde{Y}_{n}\big)h+\sqrt{2h}\xi_{n+1},\quad\widetilde{Y}_{0}=x_{0},

(1.3)

where
h\in(0,1)
represents the uniform timestep and
\xi_{k}=(\xi_{1,k},\dots,\xi_{d,k})^{T}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
are i.i.d standard
d
-dimensional Gaussian vectors.

Non-asymptotic analysis focuses on the explicit dependency of the error with respect to the algorithm parameters, e.g., step size, rather than explaining the asymptotic behavior as the algorithm iterates to infinity or the step size tends to zero.
Non-asymptotic convergence analysis for the LMC algorithm (
1.3
)
is typically investigated with

(i)
global Lipschitz continuity condition
:

there exists a positive constant
\bar{L}>0
such that

\|\nabla U(x)-\nabla U(y)\|\leq\bar{L}\|x-y\|,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\text{ and }

(1.4)

(ii)
strong convexity condition
:

there exists a positive constant
\widetilde{L}>0
such that

\left\langle x-y,\nabla U(x)-\nabla U(y)\right\rangle\geq\widetilde{L}\|x-y\|^{2},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1.5)

In recent years, working with condition (i)
and
(ii), non-asymptotic error analysis between the target distribution and the law of the LMC algorithm under various metrics, such as the Wasserstein distance and the total variation distance has been well established (see
[
11
,
10
,
12
]
).

Except for a few rare cases, it is extremely hard for the

Langevin

SDE (
1.2
) to satisfy either condition (i) or (ii). A commonly used counterexample in quantum mechanics is the double-well potential presented in Example
2.5
.
What if the drift
\nabla U
grows superlinearly?
Conventional LMC algorithm loses its powers when attempting to sample from the target distribution inherited by (
1.2
).
For example, as claimed by
[
16
,
23
]
, for a large class of SDEs with super-linear growth coefficients, the Euler-Maruyama scheme (
1.3
) leads to divergent numerical approximations in both finite and infinite time intervals.
Therefore,
a convergent numerical algorithm for the Langevin SDEs (
1.2
) with non-globally Lipschitz drift is necessary.
Recent years have witnessed a considerable growth in construction and analysis of convergent schemes for SDEs in the non-globally Lipschitz setting (see
[
31
,
17
,
19
,
29
,
32
,
2
,
3
,
30
]
).
To deal with the super-linear drift of the Langevin SDEs (
1.2
), authors in
[
6
]
used a tamed Langevin algorithm to obtain the non-asymptotic error bounds on 2-Wasserstein distance and total variation distance.

Beside the challenge of the super-linear drift, relaxing the strongly-convex potential, i.e. condition (ii), may lead to possible collapse of the classical method in the non-asymptotic error analysis
[
22
]
,
where
the contractivity condition can be replaced by a
contractivity at infinity
condition (see Assumption
2.2
).
Recently,
working on such a relaxed condition and demonstrating the non-asymptotic error analysis of the corresponding Langevin SDEs (
1.2
) have indeed gained appreciable attention (see
[
27
,
22
,
25
]
for example).
Nevertheless,
it is worth mentioning that a majority of existing works investigate this topic via the Wasserstein distance error bounds.
In
[
22
]
, authors obtained the non-asymptotic upper bound on 1-Wasserstein distance and 2-Wasserstein distance of order
1/2
and
1/4
, respectively, under globally Lipschitz condition.
If the Lipschitz condition is weakened by a polynomial growth condition, authors in
[
25
]
improved the 1-Wasserstein error bound and the 2-Wasserstein error bound with respective convergence order of
1
and
1/2
.

In this paper,
we investigate the non-asymptotic convergence of the target distribution admitted by (
1.2
)
in total variation distance (
2.7
)-(
2.10
)
in non-convex and non-globally Lipschitz setting (see Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.4
below).
The setting of total variation distance
allows us to consider bounded and measurable function
\phi\in B_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in
(
1.1
).

Classical examples include
indicator and step functions
(see (
7.2
)).
As far as we know, the investigation of approximation errors in weak approximations of (
1.1
) without fulfilling conditions (i) and (ii) is still in its initial phases.

The present article turns to handle the Langevin SDEs (
1.2
) with super-linear growing nonlinearities. To this end, we propose a projected Langevin Monte Carlo (PLMC) algorithm
with a uniform timestep
h\in(0,1)
as follows:

Y_{n+1}=\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})-\nabla U\left(\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\right)h+\sqrt{2h}\xi_{n+1},\quad Y_{0}=x_{0},

(1.6)

where

\xi_{k}=(\xi_{1,k},\dots,\xi_{d,k})^{T}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
are i.i.d standard
d
-dimensional Gaussian vectors and

\mathscr{P}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the projection operator given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}(x):=\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min\big\{1,\vartheta(\tfrac{d}{h})^{1/2\gamma}\|x\|^{-1}\big\}x,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\ \gamma>1,\\
x,\ \hskip 107.00012pt\text{for}\ \gamma=1,\end{array}\right.\end{split}\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1.7)

Here
\gamma
determined in Assumption
2.1
below characterizes the growth of the gradient of
U
and
\vartheta\geq 1
, independent of the stepsize
h
and the dimension
d
,
can be customized to prevent
the numerical approximations (
1.6
) from being projected too often in the iteration for the case
\gamma>1
.
In particular,
our PLMC algorithm (
1.6
) reduces to the classical LMC algorithm (
1.3
) for the Langevin SDEs (
1.2
)
with a Lipschitz continuous drift, i.e.
\gamma=1
.

The key idea of this paper is to turn non-asymptotic bounds on the total variation distance between the law of the numerical approximation and the target distribution to the weak error analysis in the setting of non-smooth test functions
\phi\in B_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
We mention that, the seminal work
[
1
]
obtained order one weak convergence in finite time of the Euler scheme for SDEs with globally Lipschitz and smooth coefficients, where the test functions are only assumed to be measurable and bounded.
Different from
[
1
]
, the present weak error analysis is carried out in the infinite time horizon and the drift coefficient
f
of SDEs possibly grows superlinearly.
These two aspects force us to encounter two major obstacles.

The first one is to get a couple of a priori estimates that are independent of time and stepsize, including the uniform moment bounds of the PLMC scheme (
1.6
) and the time-independent regularity estimates of the Kolmogorov equation,
which is challenging due to the loss of condition (ii)

and to the test functions not being smooth.
Another one is the discontinuity of the proposed PLMC algorithm (
1.6
), which results in further difficulties in handling the weak error via the Kolmogorov equation.
Novel techniques are used to circumvent these two difficulties.
Discrete arguments are adopted to obtain the uniform moment bounds of the PLMC algorithm (
1.6
) (see the proof of Lemma
3.4
).

Moreover,
the Bismut-Elworthy-Li formula (Lemma
4.4
), combined with the Markov property of the transition semigroup
u(\cdot,\cdot)
as shown in (
4.1
), is used to derive the time-independent regularity estimates of the Kolmogorov equation (see Section
4
).
To handle the discontinuity of the PLMC algorithm (
3.11
),
we introduce the continuous-time version (
5.2
) at each step to fully exploit the Kolmogorov equation (see (
4.2
)).

For the weak error analysis,
given any terminal time
T\in(0,\infty)
such that
Nh=T
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
we separate the error

\big|\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(Y^{x_{0}}_{N})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(X^{x_{0}}_{T})\right]\big|
, i.e.,
\big|\mathbb{E}\left[u(T,x_{0})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[u(0,Y^{x_{0}}_{N})\right]\big|
based on the associated Kolmogorov equation (see (
4.2
)) into two parts as
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
in (
5.8
).
The first part
J_{1}

is of
order

\mathcal{O}(d^{2\gamma-1}h)
,

which can be considered as a direct consequence of the time-independent regularity of
u(t,\cdot)
(see Theorem
4.5
) and the convergence of the projected operator
\mathscr{P}(\cdot)
(see Lemma
5.2
).
By virtue of the continuous version of the PLMC algorithm (
5.2
), the Kolmogorov equation and the Itô formula, the second error term
J_{2}

can be proved to be

\mathcal{O}(d^{\max\{3\gamma/2,2\gamma-1\}}h|\ln h|)

(see Theorem
5.3
and its proof for more details).

For the the Lipschitz case, i.e.,
\gamma=1
,
we can improve the estimate of
J_{2}
with the help of a refinement of the proof based on Malliavin calculus techniques and thus obtain the improved convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(d^{3/2}h)
(see Theorem
6.5
).
Theorem
5.3
and
Theorem
6.5

pave the way to analyzing the smallest number of iterations of the PLMC scheme (
1.6
) required to approach the target distribution inherited by (
1.2
) on the total variation distance with given precision, also known as the mixing time,
which has been remaining an active field
[
18
,
8
]
in recent years.
More precisely,
it is shown that, to achieve a given precision
\epsilon
,
the smallest number of iterations of the
PLMC scheme (
1.6
)
is of order
{\mathcal{O}}\big(\tfrac{d^{\max\{3\gamma/2,2\gamma-1\}}}{\epsilon}\cdot\ln(\tfrac{d}{\epsilon})\cdot\ln(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon})\big)
for
\gamma>1
,
and of order
{\mathcal{O}}\big(\tfrac{d^{3/2}}{\epsilon}\cdot\ln(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon})\big)
for
\gamma=1
.
(see Proposition
2.9
).

We summarize our main contributions as follows:

•

A projected Langevin Monte Carlo algorithm, capable of dealing with super-linear systems and covering the classical Langevin Monte Carlo algorithm, is presented.

•

Non-asymptotic bounds on the total variation distance between the law of the PLMC algorithm and the target distribution, inherited by (
1.2
), are established for non-convex potential.

•

The smallest number of iterations of the projected Langevin Monte Carlo scheme required to approach the target distribution, admitted by (
1.2
), in the total variation distance with given precision is shown.

The rest of this article is organized as follows.
The next section formulates the primary setting and shows the main result of this paper.
In Section
3
,
we present some a priori estimates of both SDE and the PLMC algorithm.
Section
4
reveals the Kolmogorov equation and its regularity estimates.
In Section
5
, the time-independent weak error analysis between SDE and the PLMC scheme is given. Section
6
is devoted to the optimal weak convergence rate for the Lipschitz case
\gamma=1
.
Some numerical tests are shown to illustrate our theoretical findings in Section
7
. Finally, the Appendix contains the detailed proof of several auxiliary lemmas.

For any

Consequently, if

\mathbb{R}^{d}

(2.8)

Recalling that for any
\varphi\in B_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\varphi

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi_{k}(x)|<\infty

, for all

(see
[
5
]

\mu_{1}
and

\displaystyle\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|_{\text{TV}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \varphi\neq 0}\dfrac{\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\mu_{1}(\text{d}x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\mu_{2}(\text{d}x)\right|}{\|\varphi\|_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|\varphi\|_{0}\leq 1}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\mu_{1}(\text{d}x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\mu_{2}(\text{d}x)\right|.

X
and any Borel probability distribution

(2.10)

we set up a non-convex framework.

Assumption 2.1
.

(Globally polynomial growth condition.)
Assume the drift coefficient
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
of SDE (
1.2
) is twice continuously differentiable in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and there exists some constant
\gamma\in[1,\infty)
such that,

\displaystyle\left\|D^{2}f(x)(v_{1},v_{2})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1+\|x\|)^{\max\{0,\gamma-2\}}}\|v_{1}\|\cdot\|v_{2}\|,\quad\forall x,v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.11)

Note that Assumption
2.1
immediately implies

\displaystyle\left\|Df(x)v_{1}-Df(\tilde{x})v_{1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1+\|x\|+\|\widetilde{x}\|)^{\max\{0,\gamma-2\}}}\|x-\tilde{x}\|\cdot\|v_{1}\|,\quad\forall x,\tilde{x},v_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(2.12)

\displaystyle\left\|Df(x)v_{1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|x\|)^{\gamma-1}\|v_{1}\|,\quad\forall x,v_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

which in turn yields

\displaystyle\left\|f(x)-f(\tilde{x})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|x\|+\|\tilde{x}\|)^{\gamma-1}\|x-\tilde{x}\|,\quad\forall x,\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(2.13)

\displaystyle\left\|f(x)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|x\|)^{\gamma},\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

In what follows, we formulate the
contractivity at infinity
condition as shown in
[
22
]
.

Assumption 2.2
.

(Contractivity at infinity condition.)
For the drift coefficient
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
of SDE (
1.2
),
there exist some positive constants
\widetilde{a}_{1}>\widetilde{a}_{2}>0
,
\mathcal{R}>0

Remark 2.3

It is noteworthy that Assumption

Compared with the usual but strict strong convexity condition on

Proof of Lemma

.

Equipped with Lemma

To achieve this, the strong-Feller property and the irreducibility property need to be validated.

\big|P_{t}\phi(x_{1})-P_{t}\phi(x_{2})\big|\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}C(t)\|\phi\|_{0}}\cdot\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|,\quad\forall t>0,\quad x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

&\leq\underbrace{(1-a_{1}h)^{p}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\Xi_{n+1})^{p}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]}_{=:\mathbb{I}_{1}}\\
&\quad+\underbrace{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}\mathcal{C}_{p}^{\ell}(Cdh)^{\ell}(1-a_{1}h)^{p-\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\Xi_{n+1})^{p-\ell}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]}_{=:\mathbb{I}_{2}},\end{split}

(3.19)

where
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}_{p}:=p!/(\ell!(p-\ell)!)
.

Further analysis is based on estimates of
\mathbb{I}_{1}
and
\mathbb{I}_{2}
.

For the estimate of
\mathbb{I}_{1}
:

The key component
of the estimate
\mathbb{I}_{1}

is

(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\Xi_{n+1})^{p}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right].

(3.20)

We use the binomial expansion theorem again to deduce

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\Xi_{n+1})^{p}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]=\sum_{i=0}^{p}\mathcal{C}_{p}^{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\Xi_{n+1}^{i}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right].\\
\end{split}

(3.21)

Then, the estimate for
\mathbb{I}_{1}
is decomposed
further into the following three steps.

Step I: estimate of

\mathbb{E}\left[\Xi_{n+1}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]

Based on the property of the Gaussian random variable and the fact that
\xi_{n+1}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
, we deduce

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{j,n+1}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{j,n+1}|^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]=1,\quad j\in\{1,\ldots,d\},

(3.22)

resulting in

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Xi_{n+1}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[I_{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]=\dfrac{C_{\gamma}dh}{(1-a_{1}h)\left(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2}\right)}.

(3.23)

Step II: estimate of

\mathbb{E}\left[\Xi^{2}_{n+1}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]

Recalling some power properties of the Gaussian random variable, we derive that,
\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\xi_{j,n+1}\right)^{2\ell-1}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\xi_{j,n+1}\right)^{2\ell}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]=(2\ell-1)!!,\quad\forall\ n\in\mathbb{N},\ j\in\{1,\ldots,d\},

(3.24)

where

(2\ell-1)!!:=\Pi_{i=1}^{\ell}(2i-1)
.

Since
\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})
is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
-measurable and

\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{j,n+1}\|\xi_{n+1}\|^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]=0,\quad\forall\ n\in\mathbb{N},\ j\in\{1,\ldots,d\},

(3.25)

one can obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[I_{1}I_{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]=0.

(3.26)

By (
3.22
), we immediately deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(I_{1})^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{8h\mathbb{E}\left[\big|\sum^{d}_{j=1}[\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})]_{j}\ \xi_{j,n+1}\big|^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]}{(1-a_{1}h)^{2}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{2}}

(3.27)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{8h\sum^{d}_{j=1}\big|[\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})]_{j}\big|^{2}}{(1-a_{1}h)^{2}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{8h}{(1-a_{1}h)^{2}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})},

where
\mathscr{P}(x)=([\mathscr{P}(x)]_{1},\cdots,[\mathscr{P}(x)]_{d})^{T}
.

In addition, for any
\ell\in[2,\infty)\cap\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|I_{2}|^{\ell}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\displaystyle\leq}\dfrac{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}C_{\gamma}^{\ell}\times(2\ell-1)!!\times d^{\ell}h^{\ell}}{(1-a_{1}h)^{\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{\ell}}.

(3.28)

In summary, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Xi_{n+1}^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[I_{1}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[I_{2}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]

(3.29)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{8h}{(1-a_{1}h)^{2}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})}+\dfrac{3C^{2}_{\gamma}d^{2}h^{2}}{(1-a_{1}h)^{2}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{2}}.

Step III: estimate of

\mathbb{E}\left[\Xi^{\ell}_{n+1}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]
,
\ell\in[3,p]\cap\mathbb{N}

It follows from (
3.24
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that, for
\ell\in[3,p)\cap\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|I_{1}|^{\ell}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Ch^{\ell/2}\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\xi_{n+1}\|^{\ell}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]}}{(1-a_{1}h)^{\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{\ell}}

(3.30)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Cd^{\ell/2}h^{\ell/2}}}{(1-a_{1}h)^{\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{\ell/2}}.

Bearing (
3.28
) and the fundamental inequality
(a+b)^{\ell}\leq 2^{\ell-1}(a^{\ell}+b^{\ell})
, for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
\ell\in[1,p]\cap\mathbb{N}
, in mind,

we deduce

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Xi_{n+1}^{\ell}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(I_{1}+I_{2})^{\ell}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]

(3.31)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\ell-1}\Big(\mathbb{E}\left[|I_{1}|^{\ell}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[|I_{2}|^{\ell}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{Cd^{\ell/2}h^{\ell/2}}{(1-a_{1}h)^{\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{\ell/2}}+\dfrac{Cd^{\ell}h^{\ell}}{(1-a_{1}h)^{\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{\ell}}.

Combining
Step I
\sim
Step III
and the fact
(1-a_{1}h)^{\ell}\geq(1-a_{1}h)^{p}
,
\ell\in[1,p]\cap\mathbb{N}
, yields,

for some constants
C=C(p)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{1}

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\displaystyle\leq(1-a_{1}h)^{p}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p}\Bigg(1+\dfrac{pC_{\gamma}dh}{(1-a_{1}h)\left(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2}\right)}+\dfrac{4p(p-1)h}{(1-a_{1}h)^{2}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})}}

(3.32)

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\displaystyle+\dfrac{C^{2}_{\gamma}p(p-1)d^{2}h^{2}}{2(1-a_{1}h)^{2}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{2}}+\sum_{\ell=3}^{p}\dfrac{Cd^{\ell/2}h^{\ell/2}}{(1-a_{1}h)^{\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{\ell/2}}+\dfrac{Cd^{\ell}h^{\ell}}{(1-a_{1}h)^{\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{\ell}}\Bigg)}

\displaystyle\leq(1-a_{1}h)^{p}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p}+Cdh(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-1}+Cd^{2}h^{2}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell=3}^{p}Cd^{\ell/2}h^{\ell/2}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-\ell/2}+Cd^{\ell}h^{\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-\ell}.

For any
k\in[1,p]
and
\epsilon_{1}\in(0,a_{1}/4p)
,
employing the Young inequality yields

\displaystyle Cd^{k}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-k}\leq\epsilon_{1}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p}+C_{\epsilon_{1}}d^{p},

(3.33)

where
C_{\epsilon_{1}}=C(a_{1},C_{f},p,\gamma)>0
.

Therefore, the estimate (
3.32
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(1-a_{1}h)^{p}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p}+p\epsilon_{1}h(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p}+C_{\epsilon_{1}}d^{p}h.

(3.34)

For the estimate of
\mathbb{I}_{2}
:

To handle
\mathbb{I}_{2}
, one follows similar arguments of treating
\mathbb{I}_{1}
,

but does not require as fine an estimate as
\mathbb{I}_{1}
.
For

the step size
h\in(0,1)
and

\ell\in[1,p]\in\mathbb{N}
,
there exist some constants
C=C(p)>0
such that,

\displaystyle(1-a_{1}h)^{p-\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\Xi_{n+1})^{p-\ell}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]

(3.35)

\displaystyle\leq(1-a_{1}h)^{p-\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-\ell}+Cdh(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-\ell-1}+Cd^{2}h^{2}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-\ell-2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=3}^{p-\ell}Cd^{i/2}h^{i}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-\ell-i/2}+Cd^{i}h^{i}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-\ell-i}

\displaystyle\leq C\left[(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-\ell}+d^{p-\ell}\right],

where we have used the Young inequalities as

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a^{i/2}b^{p-\ell-i/2}\leq\tfrac{ia^{p-\ell}}{2(p-\ell)}+\tfrac{(p-\ell-i/2)b^{p-\ell}}{p-\ell},\quad a^{i}b^{p-\ell-i}\leq\tfrac{ia^{p-\ell}}{p-\ell}+\tfrac{(p-\ell-i)b^{p-\ell}}{p-\ell},\quad a,b\geq 1.

(3.36)

Putting the estimate (
3.35
) into
\mathbb{I}_{2}
leads to,
for some constant
C=C(a_{1},a_{2},C_{f},\vartheta,\gamma,p)>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{2}\leq Ch\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}d^{\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-\ell}+Chd^{p}.

(3.37)

In addition,
using the Young inequality yields,

for
\ell\in[1,p]\cap\mathbb{N}
and a sufficiently small constant

\epsilon_{2}\in(0,a_{1}/4p)
,

Chd^{\ell}(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p-\ell}\leq\epsilon_{2}h(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p}+C_{\epsilon_{2}}d^{p}h.

(3.38)

Consequently, we derive the final estimate of
\mathbb{I}_{2}
as below,

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq p\epsilon_{2}h(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p}+C_{\epsilon_{2}}d^{p}h,

(3.39)

where
C_{\epsilon_{2}}=C(a_{1},a_{2},C_{f},\vartheta,\gamma,p)>0
.

Combining the estimates of
\mathbb{I}_{1}
and
\mathbb{I}_{2}
:

Plugging the estimates of
\mathbb{I}_{1}
and
\mathbb{I}_{2}
and taking expectations on the both sides yield

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\|Y_{n+1}\|^{2})^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\big[1-a_{1}h+p(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})h\big]\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p}\right]+C_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}}d^{p}h

(3.40)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\tfrac{a_{1}}{2}h\right)\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\|\mathscr{P}(Y_{n})\|^{2})^{p}\right]+C_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}}d^{p}h,

where
\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}\in(0,a_{1}/2p)
.
Recalling

h\in(0,\min\{1/2a_{1},2a_{1}/(a_{1}+2C^{2}_{f}),1\})
and using
Lemma
3.3
we show,
for
C_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}}=C(a_{1},a_{2},C_{f},\vartheta,\gamma,p)>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\|Y_{n+1}\|^{2})^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\tfrac{a_{1}}{2}h\right)\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\|{Y}_{n}\|^{2})^{p}\right]+C_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}}d^{p}h

(3.41)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\tfrac{a_{1}}{2}h\right)^{n+1}\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\|{x}_{0}\|^{2})^{p}\right]+\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(1-\dfrac{a_{1}}{2}h\right)^{i}C_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}}d^{p}h

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\tfrac{a_{1}}{2}t_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\|{x}_{0}\|^{2})^{p}\right]+\tfrac{2C_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}}}{a_{1}}d^{p},

where we have used the fact that for any
x>0
,
1-x\leq e^{-x}
.
The proof is completed.
∎

4
Kolmogorov equation and regularization estimates

To carry out the weak error analysis,
for
\phi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we introduce the function
u(\cdot,\cdot):[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

u(t,x):=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi\left(X^{x}_{t}\right)\right],

(4.1)

and

consider the Kolmogorov equation associated with SDE (
1.2
):

\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u(t,x)=Du(t,x)f(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}D^{2}u(t,x)\big(e_{j},e_{j}\big),\quad t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
u(0,x)=\phi(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(4.2)

where
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\{1,\cdots,d\}}
is denoted as the orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Moreover, we recall the definition of the classical solution to (
4.2
) (see
[
7
, Definition 1.6.1]
for details) as follows.

Definition 4.1
.

(Classical solutions)
A function
u:[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called to be a classical solution of the Kolmogorov equation (
4.2
) if

(i)

u(\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous on
[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(ii)

for any
t>0,u(t,\cdot)\in C_{b}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
,

(iii)

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
u(\cdot,x):(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable with continuous derivative
\partial_{t}u(t,x)
,

(iv)

\partial_{t}u(t,x)
,
Du(t,x)
and
D^{2}u(t,x)
are continuous from
(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, respectively.

(v)

u(\cdot,\cdot)
verifies the problem (
4.2
).

Owing to
[
7
, Theorem 1.6.2]
,

u(t,x)
is the unique
classical
solution of the Kolmogorov equation (
4.2
) under previous assumptions.
In the following, we attempt to derive
more delicate estimates of
u
and its derivatives,
which are essential for the subsequent error analysis,
are absent in
[
7
]
.
Before proceeding further,
let us
revisit the mean-square differentiability of random functions,
quoted from
[
33
,
28
]
.

Definition 4.2
.

(Mean-square differentiable)
Let
\Psi:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\psi_{i}:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be random functions satisfying

\lim_{\tau_{1}\rightarrow 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}\left[\Psi\left(x+\tau_{1}e_{i}\right)-\Psi(x)\right]-\psi_{i}(x)\right|^{2}\right]=0,\quad\forall i\in\{1,2,\cdots,d\},

(4.3)

where
e_{i}
is the unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the
i
-th element being
1
. Then
\Psi
is called to be mean-square differentiable, with
\psi=(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{d})
being the derivative (in the mean-square differentiable sense) of
\Psi
at
x
.
Also, we denote
\mathcal{D}_{(i)}\Psi=\psi_{i}
and
\mathcal{D}\Psi(x)=\psi
.

The above definition can be generalized to vector-valued functions in a component-wise manner.
As a result,
for every
t\in[0,\infty)
, we take the function
X^{(\cdot)}_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and write its derivative as
\mathcal{D}X^{x}_{t}\in L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\mathcal{D}^{2}X^{x}_{t}\in L(\mathbb{R}^{d},L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
The following lemma states some a priori estimates of the mean-square derivative of solutions
\{X^{x}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.4
hold. Then, for all
t\in[0,\infty)
and
x,v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{D}X^{x}_{t}v_{1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq e^{Lt}\left\|v_{1}\right\|,

(4.4)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|\mathcal{D}^{2}X^{x}_{t}(v_{1},v_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq Ce^{(3L+1/2)t}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left[\textbf{1}_{\gamma\in[1,2]}+\textbf{1}_{\gamma\in(2,\infty)}(d^{\gamma/2-1}+\|x\|^{\gamma-2})\right]\left\|v_{1}\right\|\cdot\left\|v_{2}\right\|},

(4.5)

where

L>0
comes from (
2.15
).

The proof of Lemma
4.3
is

postponed

to Appendix
B.1
.
We remark that

in existing results

with the strongly-convex condition (
1.5
), one can prove the mean-square derivatives of solutions
\{X^{x}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

decrease

exponentially in time
t
and use the chain rule to obtain

the regularity properties of
u(t,\cdot)
and its derivatives for some smooth enough test functions (see
[
28
]
).
However, such arguments do not work in our setting. On the one hand, as shown by Lemma
4.3
, the derivatives of
\{X^{x}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
increase exponentially in time.

On the other hand,

the chain rule breaks down for non-smooth test functions
\phi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Here we resort to
the Bismut-Elworthy-Li formula (see
[
7
]
).

Lemma 4.4
.

(Bismut-Elworthy-Li formula
[
7
,
13
]
)
Let
\{X^{x}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the solutions of the Langevin SDE (
1.2
) with the initial state
X^{x}_{0}=x
. If
\{X^{x}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is mean-square differentiable,
then for any
\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
t>0
and
x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\left\langle D\big(P_{t}\varphi(x)\big),v\right\rangle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}t}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle\mathcal{D}X^{x}_{s}v,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\big\rangle\varphi\left(X^{x}_{t}\right)\right],

(4.6)

where
P_{t}(\cdot)
defines a transition semigroup of the corresponding SDEs (
1.2
) as
P_{t}\varphi(x)=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X^{x}_{t})]
.

Recalling
P_{t}\phi(x)=u(t,x)
by (
4.1
),
the Bismut-Elworthy-Li formula paves the way to presenting an expression for the derivatives of

where it only requires
\phi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Combining Lemma
4.3
with the Bismut-Elworthy-Li formula,
we are now in the position to obtain some regularization estimates with regard to the derivatives of
u(t,\cdot)
,
t>0
.

Theorem 4.5
.

If Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.4
hold, then the function
u(t,\cdot)\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Moreover,

for
t\in(0,1]
,

\displaystyle\left|Du(t,x)v_{1}\right|\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}C\dfrac{\|\phi\|_{0}}{\sqrt{t}}}\|v_{1}\|,\quad\forall x,v_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(4.7)

and

\displaystyle\left|D^{2}u(t,x)(v_{1},v_{2})\right|\leq C\dfrac{\|\phi\|_{0}}{t}\left[\textbf{1}_{\gamma\in[1,2]}+\textbf{1}_{\gamma\in(2,\infty)}(d^{\gamma/2-1}+\|x\|^{\gamma-2})\right]\|v_{1}\|\cdot\|v_{2}\|,\quad\forall x,v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(4.8)

For
t\in(1,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\big|Du(t,x)v_{1}\big|\leq C_{\star}\|\phi\|_{0}e^{-c_{\star}(t-1)}\left(d^{1/2}+\|x\|\right)\|v_{1}\|,\quad\forall x,v_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(4.9)

and

\displaystyle\left|D^{2}u(t,x)(v_{1},v_{2})\right|\leq C_{\star}\|\phi\|_{0}e^{-c_{\star}(t-1)}(d^{1/2}+\|x\|)\left[\textbf{1}_{\gamma\in[1,2]}+\textbf{1}_{\gamma\in(2,\infty)}(d^{\gamma/2-1}+\|x\|^{\gamma-2})\right]\|v_{1}\|\cdot\|v_{2}\|,

(4.10)

\displaystyle\forall x,v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Proof of Theorem
4.5
.

The Bismut-Elworthy-Li formula (see Lemma
4.4
or
[
7
,
13
]
) states that for some function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
belonging to
C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists
\mathcal{K}(\Phi,x)>0
, which depends on
\Phi
and
x
, such that,

|\Phi(x)|\leq\mathcal{K}(\Phi,x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(4.11)

then we can calculate the first and the second derivative of

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V(t,x)}:=\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X^{x}_{t})]

(4.12)

with respect to
x
.

Recalling (
B.1
),
we use the same notations
\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x)
and
\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(t,x)
to denote the first and the second derivatives of
X^{x}_{t}
with respect to
x
for

, respectively.

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle D{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V(t,x)}v_{1}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}t}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\eta^{v_{1}}(s,x),\text{d}W_{s}\right\rangle\Phi\left(X^{x}_{t}\right)\right].

(4.13)

By (
4.11
), Lemma
4.3
, the Hölder inequality and the Itô isometry, we obtain that, for any
v_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\big|DV(t,x)v_{1}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\|\Phi\left(X^{x}_{t}\right)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})}}{\sqrt{2}t}\Big\|\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\eta^{v_{1}}(s,x),\text{d}W_{s}\right\rangle\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\|\mathcal{K}(\Phi,X^{x}_{t})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})}}{\sqrt{2}t}\left(\int_{0}^{t}e^{2Ls}\text{d}s\right)^{1/2}\|v_{1}\|

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(e^{2Lt}-1)^{1/2}}{2L^{1/2}t}\|\mathcal{K}(\Phi,X^{x}_{t})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})}\|v_{1}\|.

As a consequence of (
4.14
),
one can derive the strong Feller property of

corresponding to the Langevin SDEs (

Indeed, one can choose

\left|u(t,x_{1})-u(t,x_{2})\right|\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}C(t)\|\phi\|_{0}}\cdot\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|,\quad\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad t>0,

Going back to the proof,

)

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V(t,x)}=\mathbb{E}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}\left(t/2,X^{x}_{t/2}\right)\right]{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=P_{t/2}\big(V\left(t/2,\cdot\right)\big)\left(X^{x}_{t/2}\right)},

6.1
Introduction to Malliavin calculus

In this part, we give a brief introduction to Malliavin calculus, which is a key tool for us to obtain the optimal convergence rate.
For details, one can refer to the classical monograph
[
26
]
.
Denote
H=L^{2}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})

as the space of all measurable functions

\varphi:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\int_{0}^{T}\|\varphi(t)\|^{2}\text{d}t<\infty
.
In the following, we use

W(v)
,

v=([v(\cdot)]_{1},\cdots,[v(\cdot)]_{d})^{T}\in H
,
to denote the isonormal Gaussian process
as

W(v):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{T}[v(t)]_{i}\mathrm{d}W_{i,t}.

(6.1)

Moreover, denote
C^{\infty}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
as
the set of all infinitely continuously differentiable
functions

g:\mathbb{R}^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

such that
g
and all of its partial derivatives have
polynomial growth.
Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the class of smooth random variables such that a random
variable
F\in\mathcal{S}
has the form

F=g\left(W\left(v_{1}\right),\ldots,W\left(v_{M}\right)\right),

(6.2)

where

g\in C^{\infty}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
and

v_{1},\ldots,v_{M}\in H
,
M\geq 1
.
We are in a position to give the definition of the derivative operator
\mathscr{D}
.

Definition 6.1
.

(Derivative operator
\mathscr{D}
)
The derivative of a smooth random variable
F
of the form (
6.2
) is the
H-
valued random variable given by

\mathscr{D}_{t}F=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\partial_{i}g\left(W\left(v_{1}\right),\ldots,W\left(v_{M}\right)\right)v_{i}(t),\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

(6.3)

Further, given
v\in H
,
denote
\mathscr{D}^{v}F
by the Malliavin derivative with respect to
F
along the direction
v
as follows:

\mathscr{D}^{v}F:=\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle v(s),\mathscr{D}_{s}F\right\rangle\text{d}s.

(6.4)

For any
p\geq 1
,
denote
by
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
as
the closure of the class of smooth random variables in
\mathcal{S}
with
respect to the norm

\|F\|_{\mathbb{D}^{1,2}}:=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|F|^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathscr{D}_{s}F\|^{2}\text{d}s\right]\right)^{1/2}.

(6.5)

We then present the definition of the divergence operator
\delta

as well as
the useful integration by parts formula.

Definition 6.2
.

(Divergence operator
\delta
)
Denote by
\delta
the adjoint of the operator
\mathscr{D}
.
That is,

\delta

is an unbounded operator on
L^{2}(\Omega,H)
with values in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
such that:

(i)

The domain of
\delta
, denoted by
\operatorname{Dom}\delta
, is the set of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued square integrable random variables
u\in L^{2}(\Omega,H)
such that

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{D}^{u}F\right]\right|\leq C\|F\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})},

(6.6)

for all
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
, where
C
is some constant depending on
u
.

(ii)

If
u
belongs to
\operatorname{Dom}\delta
, then
\delta(u)
is the element of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
characterized by

\mathbb{E}\left[F\delta(u)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{D}^{u}F\right],

(6.7)

for any
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
.

The next lemma
gives the important calculus rule (see
[
26
, Proposition 1.3.3]
), which allows us to factor out a scalar random variable in a divergence.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and
u\in\operatorname{Dom}\delta
such that
Fu\in L^{2}(\Omega,H)
. Then
Fu\in\operatorname{Dom}\delta
and the following equality is true

\delta(Fu)=F\delta(u)-\mathscr{D}^{u}F,

(6.8)

provided the right-hand side of (
6.8
) is square integrable.

[T-N_{1}h,T-h)
,
is required in

remove the logarithmic term

and
achieve the optimal convergence rate.

In the following,
we set

without any loss of generality.
Then,
for the grid points
t_{n}
,
t_{n+1}

satisfying

,
where

,
one is able to find a grid point

such that,

(6.9)

Since the
LMC algorithm (
1.3
), denoted by

, is equivalent to the PLMC algorithm (

) with
\gamma=1
,
we still use (

) as the continuous extension of the LMC algorithm in which

.
Also, we introduce the following process:

where

\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We directly observe that the process (

) is adapted with respect to

,

, and

and Lemma

.
Thanks to

,
we deduce that

and

\delta(b_{\ell})=\int_{t_{m}}^{t_{n+1}}\left\langle b_{\ell},\text{d}W_{s}\right\rangle.

(6.11)

Now we are in the position to show the Malliavin regularity estimates of the process (

) with respect to the direction

.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let Assumptions

2.1
,
2.2
and
2.4

hold with

.
Given the direction

as defined by (

,

depending on the drift

and

and, for

,

\mathscr{D}^{b_{\ell}}\mathbb{Y}^{i}(s)=\tfrac{s-t_{m}}{t_{n+1}-t_{m}}e_{\ell}.

(6.13)

Moreover,
there exists some positive constant
C
depending on
f
and
p
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathscr{D}^{b_{\ell}}\mathbb{Y}^{n}(s)-\mathscr{D}^{b_{\ell}}\mathbb{Y}^{n}(t_{n})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 2h,

(6.14)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathscr{D}^{b_{\ell}}f\big(\mathbb{Y}^{n}(s)\big)-\mathscr{D}^{b_{\ell}}f\big(\mathbb{Y}^{n}(t_{n})\big)\right\|_{L^{2p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})}

Thus the proof of estimate (
6.12
) is finished.
Before proceeding further,

) holds true
at any grid point in time interval

as

A.1
Proof of Lemma
3.1

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

First we define a stopping time as

\tau_{n}=\inf\{s\geq 0:\|X_{s}\|>n\}.

(A.1)

Applying the Itô formula to

e^{cpt\land\tau_{n}}(\epsilon_{3}+\|X_{t\land\tau_{n}}\|^{2})^{p}

for some constants

c\in(0,2a_{1})
,

\epsilon_{3}>0
and using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality show

\displaystyle e^{cpt\land\tau_{n}}\left(\epsilon_{3}+\|X_{t\land\tau_{n}}\|^{2}\right)^{p}

(A.2)

\displaystyle\leq(\epsilon_{3}+\|x_{0}\|^{2})^{p}+cp\int_{0}^{t\land\tau_{n}}e^{cps}\left(\epsilon_{3}+\|X_{s}\|^{2}\right)^{p}\text{d}s+2p\int_{0}^{t\land\tau_{n}}e^{cps}\left(\epsilon_{3}+\|X_{s}\|^{2}\right)^{p-1}\left\langle X_{s},f(X_{s})\right\rangle\text{d}s

\displaystyle+2\sqrt{2}p\int_{0}^{t\land\tau_{n}}e^{cps}\left(\epsilon_{3}+\|X_{s}\|^{2}\right)^{p-1}\left\langle X_{s},\text{d}W_{s}\right\rangle+2p(2p-1)d\int_{0}^{t\land\tau_{n}}e^{cps}\left(\epsilon_{3}+\|X_{s}\|^{2}\right)^{p-1}\text{d}s.

Taking expectations on the both sides of (
A.2
), using Assumption
2.4
and letting
\epsilon_{3}\rightarrow 0^{+}
yield

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{cp{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(t\land\tau_{n})}}\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X_{t\land\tau_{n}}}\|^{2p}\right]

(A.3)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}\|^{2p}\right]+p\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t\land\tau_{n}}e^{cps}\Big[(-2a_{1}+c)\|X_{s}\|^{2p}+(4pd-2d+2a_{2})\|X_{s}\|^{2p-2}\Big]\text{d}s\right],

where the property of the Itô integral was used that

2\sqrt{2}p\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t\land\tau_{n}}e^{cps}\|X_{s}\|^{2(p-1)}\left\langle X_{s},\text{d}W_{s}\right\rangle\right]=0.

(A.4)

Using the Young inequality

ab^{2p-2}\leq\hat{\epsilon}b^{2p}+\tfrac{1}{p}\left(\tfrac{\hat{\epsilon}p}{p-1}\right)^{1-p}a^{p},

(A.5)

for any
a,b>0
,

\hat{\epsilon}>0
,

and
p\geq 1

yields

(4pd-2d+2a_{2})\|X_{s}\|^{2p-2}\leq\hat{\epsilon}\|X_{s}\|^{2p}+\tfrac{1}{p}\left(\tfrac{\hat{\epsilon}p}{p-1}\right)^{1-p}(4pd-2d+2a_{2})^{p}.

(A.6)

Choosing
c\in(0,2a_{1})
and

\hat{\epsilon}=2a_{1}-c
,
by (
A.6
)
we obtain that the following estimate holds true

\left(-2a_{1}+c\right)\|X_{s}\|^{2p}+\big(4pd-2d+2a_{2}\big)\|X_{s}\|^{2p-2}\leq\tfrac{(4p-2+2a_{2})^{p}}{p}\left(\tfrac{p-1}{(2a_{1}-c)p}\right)^{p-1}d^{p}.

(A.7)

As a result, we deduce that

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{cp\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(t\land\tau_{n})}\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X_{t\land\tau_{n}}}\|^{2p}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}\|^{2p}\right]+\tfrac{(4p-2+2a_{2})^{p}}{p}\left(\tfrac{p-1}{(2a_{1}-c)p}\right)^{p-1}d^{p}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\int_{0}^{t\land\tau_{n}}e^{cps}\text{d}s.}

(A.8)

Due to the Fatou Lemma, letting
n\rightarrow\infty
gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\|X_{t}\|^{2p}\right]\leq e^{-cpt}\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}\|^{2p}\right]+\tfrac{(4p-2+2a_{2})^{p}}{p}\left(\tfrac{p-1}{(2a_{1}-c)p}\right)^{p-1}d^{p}.

(A.9)

The proof is completed. ∎

B.1
Proof of Lemma
4.3

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

The existence and the uniqueness of the mean-square derivatives up to the second order can be derived owing to Remark
2.3

(see
[
7
, Proposition 1.3.5]
or
[
34
, Appendix C]
).

For simplicity, we denote

\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x):=\mathcal{D}X^{x}_{t}v_{1},\quad\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(t,x):=\mathcal{D}^{2}X^{x}_{t}(v_{1},v_{2}),\quad\forall x,v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(B.1)

Part I: estimate of the first variation process

It follows from Remark
2.3
that,

\langle Df(x)y,y\rangle\leq L\|y\|^{2},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(B.2)

Moreover, for the first variation process of SDE (
1.2
), one gets

\text{d}\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x)=Df(X^{x}_{t})\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x)\,\text{d}t,\quad\eta^{v_{1}}(0)=v_{1}.

(B.3)

Taking the temporal derivative of
\|\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x)\|^{2}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\text{d}\|\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\left\langle\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x),Df(X^{x}_{t})\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x)\right\rangle\leq 2L\|\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x)\|^{2},

(B.4)

which leads to

\displaystyle\|\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x)\|^{2}\leq e^{2Lt}\|v_{1}\|^{2}.

(B.5)

Part II: estimate of the second variation process

Similarly, due to the variation approach, we have the second variation process with respect to SDEs (
1.2
) as follows,

\displaystyle\text{d}\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(t,x)=\Big(Df(X^{x}_{t})\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(t,x)+D^{2}f(X^{x}_{t})\big(\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x),\eta^{v_{2}}(t,x)\big)\Big)\text{d}t,\quad\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(0)=0.

(B.6)

Following the same argument as before and the Young inequality, we deduce that

\displaystyle\text{d}\|\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(t,x)\|^{2}

(B.7)

\displaystyle=\Big(2\left\langle\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(t,x),Df(X^{x}_{t})\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(t,x)\right\rangle+2\left\langle\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(t,x),D^{2}f(X^{x}_{t})\big(\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x),\eta^{v_{2}}(t,x)\big)\right\rangle\Big)\text{d}t

\displaystyle\leq(2L+1)\|\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(t,x)\|^{2}\text{d}t+\left\|D^{2}f(X^{x}_{t})\big(\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x),\eta^{v_{2}}(t,x)\big)\right\|^{2}\text{d}t.

The key issue is to estimate
\left\|D^{2}f(X^{x}_{t})(\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x),\eta^{v_{2}}(t,x))\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})}
, where, by Assumption
2.1
, the range of
\gamma
deserves to be discussed carefully.

In the case
1\leq\gamma\leq 2
, we employ Assumption
2.1
and
(
B.5
) to arrive at

\displaystyle\left\|D^{2}f(X^{x}_{t})\big(\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x),\eta^{v_{2}}(t,x)\big)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x)\right\|\cdot\left\|\eta^{v_{2}}(t,x)\right\|

(B.8)

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{2Lt}\left\|v_{1}\right\|\cdot\left\|v_{2}\right\|,\quad\forall x,v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

In the case
\gamma>2
, using Assumption
2.1
, Lemma
3.1

and (
B.5
) yields,

\displaystyle\left\|D^{2}f(X^{x}_{t})\big(\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x),\eta^{v_{2}}(t,x)\big)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(B.9)

\displaystyle\leq C\big\|\left(1+\|X^{x}_{t}\|\right)^{\gamma-2}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})}\left\|\eta^{v_{1}}(t,x)\right\|\cdot\left\|\eta^{v_{2}}(t,x)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{2Lt}\left(d^{\gamma/2-1}+\|x\|^{\gamma-2}\right)\left\|v_{1}\right\|\cdot\left\|v_{2}\right\|,\quad\forall x,v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Combining (
B.8
) with (
B.9
) above, we obtain,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(t,x)\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(2L+1)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(s,x)\|^{2}\right]\text{d}s

(B.10)

\displaystyle+Ce^{4Lt}\left[\textbf{1}_{\gamma\in[1,2]}+\textbf{1}_{\gamma\in(2,\infty)}(d^{\gamma-2}+\|x\|^{2\gamma-4})\right]\left\|v_{1}\right\|^{2}\cdot\left\|v_{2}\right\|^{2},\quad\forall x,v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

leading to

\displaystyle\|\xi^{v_{1},v_{2}}(t,x)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq Ce^{(3L+1/2)t}\left[\textbf{1}_{\gamma\in[1,2]}+\textbf{1}_{\gamma\in(2,\infty)}(d^{\gamma/2-1}+\|x\|^{\gamma-2})\right]\left\|v_{1}\right\|\cdot\left\|v_{2}\right\|,\ \forall x,v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(B.11)

with the help of the Gronwall inequality.
The proof is thus completed.

\left\langle x-y,f(x)-f(y)\right\rangle\leq-\|x-y\|^{2},\quad\forall x,y\notin B(0,\sqrt{2}).

(E.3)

One may assume
x\in B(0,\sqrt{2})
and
y\notin B(0,\sqrt{2})
such that
\|x-y\|\geq\mathcal{R}

without loss of generality. Setting
z=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y

\lambda\in(0,1)
such that
\|z\|\ =\sqrt{2}
.
It is also straightforward to derive that

z-y=\lambda(x-y),\quad\text{and}\quad x-z=(1-\lambda)(x-y).

(E.4)

Moreover, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, (
E.1
),
(
E.3
) and (
E.4

\displaystyle\left\langle x-y,f(x)-f(y)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle x-y,f(x)-f(z)\right\rangle+\left\langle x-y,f(z)-f(y)\right\rangle

(E.5)

\displaystyle=\left\langle x-y,f(x)-f(z)\right\rangle+\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\left\langle z-y,f(z)-f(y)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle x-y,f(x)-f(z)\right\rangle-\lambda\|x-y\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\|x\|+\|z\|)^{2}\|x-y\|\cdot\|x-z\|-\lambda\|x-y\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\big[\lambda-(2\sqrt{2}+1)^{2}(1-\lambda)\big]\|x-y\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\big[1-(4\sqrt{2}+10)(1-\lambda)\big]\|x-y\|^{2},

\|x\|,\|z\|\leq\sqrt{2}

\|x-y\|\geq\mathcal{R}

(E.6)

Choosing
