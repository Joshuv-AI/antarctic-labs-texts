Character polynomials for two rows and hook partitions University of Western Ontario
Ahmed Umer Ashraf

Abstract
Representation theory of the symmetric group 𝔖 n \mathfrak{S}_{n} has a very distinctive combinatorial flavor. The conjugacy classes as well as the irreducible characters are indexed by integer partitions λ ⊢ n \lambda\vdash n . We introduce class functions on 𝔖 n \mathfrak{S}_{n} that count the number of certain tilings of Young diagrams. The counting interpretation gives a uniform expression of these class functions in the ring of character polynomials, as defined by [ Mur37 ] . A modern treatment of character polynomials is given in [ OZ15 ] . We prove a relation between these combinatorial class functions in the (virtual) character ring. From this relation, we were able to prove Goupil’s generating function identity [ Gou99 ] , which can then be used to derive Rosas’ formula [ Ros00 ] for Kronecker coefficients of hook shape partitions and two row partitions.



1
Introduction

Representation theory of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
employs a good amount of combinatorics of (integer) partitions of
n
. The irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are indexed by partitions. One way to generate these irreducible representations is through constructing a vector space
M^{\lambda}
generated by equivalence classes of tableaux, called
tabloids
of shape
\lambda
. And then show that each
M^{\lambda}
contains an irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
as a subspace. The number of tabloids of shape
\lambda
can also be viewed as certain tilings of Young diagram of shape
\lambda
. This motivates us to define class functions over
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
that count certain tilings we call
brick tilings
. In this section, we review representations theory of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and define these class functions. We also recall Doubilet’s inversion formula, and face numbers of permutohedron which will be of use in the later sections.

1.1
Partitions and compositions

A
partition

\lambda
of a positive integer
n
, denoted as
\lambda\vdash n
, is a weakly decreasing sequence
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{r})
of positive integers adding up to
n
. The positive integers
\lambda_{i}
are called
parts
of
\lambda
, and the number of parts is called the
length
of
\lambda
, denoted as
\ell(\lambda)
. If we want to emphasize that
\ell(\lambda)=r
, we write
\lambda\vdash_{r}n
. In relation to
\lambda
, the integer
n
is called the
weight
of
\lambda
, and it is denoted by
|\lambda|
. It is also useful to write
\lambda=(1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\dots,n^{m_{n}})
, where
m_{i}
denote the multiplicity of
i
in the partition
\lambda
. Given a partition
\lambda\vdash n
, the
reduced partition

\left<\lambda\right>
is a partition of
n-\lambda_{1}
defined as
\left<\lambda\right>=(\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{r})\vdash|\lambda|-\lambda_{1}
. Similarly, for a partition
\lambda\vdash k
, and a positive integer
n\geq k+\lambda_{1}
, the
augmented partition

\lambda[n]
is a partition of
n
defined as
\lambda[n]=(n-k,\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r})
. In other words, the
reduced partition

\left<\lambda\right>
is a partition we get by removing the first part of
\lambda
, while the augmented partition is a partition we get by augmenting a suitable first part to
\lambda
. We identify partition
\lambda
with its Young diagram, which is a finite collection of unit cells arranged in left justified rows with
\lambda_{i}
cells in the
i
th row. A Young tableau of shape
\lambda
, is a labeling of the cells of the Young diagram of
\lambda
with integers
1,2,\dots,n
, with each number occurring exactly once.

A composition
\mu
of a positive integer
n
, denoted as
\mu\vDash n
, is a sequence
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{r})
of positive integers adding to
n
. We extend the definitions and notation introduced above for partitions to compositions. Given a composition
\mu
, we denote by
\tilde{\mu}
the partition obtained by rearranging the parts of
\mu
in weakly decreasing order.

For any positive integer
n
, let
\mathsf{Comp}(n)
denote the set of all compositions of
n
. We define a partial order on
\mathsf{Comp}(n)
in the following manner: Given two compositions
\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{s})
and
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{t})
in
\mathsf{Comp}(n)
, we say that
\mu
covers
\nu
, and write
\nu\lessdot\mu
if
\ell(\mu)=\ell(\nu)+1
and there exists a unique
j
such that

\displaystyle\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\nu_{i}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ &\text{for}\penalty\ i<j\\
\nu_{i}+\nu_{i+1}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ &\text{for}\penalty\ i=j\\
\nu_{i+1}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ &\text{for}\penalty\ i>j\end{cases}

i.e. the covering relations are given by adding adjacent entries. The partial order on
\mathsf{Comp}(n)
induced by this relation is denoted by
\leq
. For instance, in figure
1
we see the Hasse diagram of this partial order on
\mathsf{Comp}(5)
.

We identify the abstract group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with the group of permutations on the set
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Let
w
be an element of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, then
w
can be written as a product of pairwise disjoint cycles, called
cyclic factors
of
w
. Let
r
denote the number of these cyclic factors including the fixed points (1-cycles). Let
l_{i}
be their lengths for
i=1,\dots,r
. By choosing an element
j_{i}
for the
i
-th cyclic factor, we can write

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(j_{i},wj_{i},\cdots,w^{l_{i}-1}j_{i})

We can make this notation unique by choosing
j_{i}
such that for all positive integer
m
,

\displaystyle j_{i}\geq w^{m}j_{i}

and for all
i=1,2,\dots,r-1
, take

\displaystyle j_{i}<j_{i+1}

Such a unique decomposition is called
canonical cycle decomposition
of
w
. This plays an important role in Foata’s first fundamental bijection
[
FS78
]
. Note that
l=(l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{r})
is a composition of
n
. The underlying partition
\tilde{l}
is called the
cycle type
of
w
and is denoted as
\mathrm{cyc}(w)
. We know that two permutations
u,w
belong to the same conjugacy class in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
if and only if
\mathrm{cyc}(u)=\mathrm{cyc}(w)
.

Example 1.1
.

If
w=947213865\in\mathfrak{S}_{9}
, then we have the canonical cycle decomposition

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=(4,2)(8,6,3,7)(9,5,1)

with
\mathrm{cyc}(w)=(4,3,2)\vdash 9
.

1.2
Representation theory of symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}

A tabloid
[t]
of shape
\lambda
is an equivalence class of Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
, where we consider two tableaux
t
and
t^{\prime}
equivalent if the entries in each row of
t
agrees with the corresponding entries in row of
t^{\prime}
. Given the set
T(\lambda)
of all Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
, there is a natural action of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
on
T(\lambda)
by just permuting the labels of tableaux. This induces an action on tabloids. Given a Young tableau
t
, the polytabloid
e_{t}
associated to
t
is defined as the linear combination

\displaystyle e_{t}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\pi\in C_{t}}\mathrm{sign}(\pi)\pi[t]

where
C_{t}
is the column group associated to
t
, i.e. the subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
consisting of permutations that only permute elements within each column of
t
. For each partition
\lambda\vdash n
,
\mathbb{C}
-linear combination of polytabloids of shape
\lambda
gives an irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
over
\mathbb{C}
. This is referred as Specht module
S^{\lambda}
corresponding to
\lambda\vdash n
in the literature
[
Ste12
]
. Let
\mathrm{Cl}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
denote the vector space of class functions on the group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
over
\mathbb{C}
. The characters of Specht modules,
(\chi^{\lambda})_{\lambda\vdash n}
, gives a basis for
\mathrm{Cl}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
. There is a scalar product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
on
\mathrm{Cl}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
defined as

\displaystyle\left<\chi^{\lambda},\chi^{\mu}\right>

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\chi^{\mu}(\sigma)

and extended linearly. The decomposition of the permutation character in terms of the irreducible character basis
\{\chi^{\mu}\}
is given by Young’s rule, which gives:

\zeta^{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu\unlhd\lambda}K_{\mu\lambda}\chi^{\mu}

(1)

where
K_{\mu\lambda}
, are the Kostka numbers, and the sum is over all partitions
\mu
which are less than or equal to
\lambda
in the
dominance order
.

1.3
Brick tilings

A
brick
of length
j
is a labelled horizontal array of
j
unit cells. We will view it as a
1\times j
rectangle. To each
j
-cycle
a=(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{j})
in the canonical cycle decomposition of
w
, we can associate a brick of length
j
with
i
th square labelled
a_{i}
. Given
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
where
w
has cycle type
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r})\vdash n
, we denote by
B_{w}
the set of associated bricks of length
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r}
corresponding to each cyclic factor in the canonical cycle decomposition of
w
. Note that the
1
-cycles correspond to
1\times 1
square bricks. A
tiling
of a diagram
\lambda\vdash n
by a set of bricks
B
is a covering of the diagram
\lambda
with bricks from
B
such that no brick is used twice and each cell of
\lambda
is covered by some brick from
B
.
An
ordered brick tiling
of
\lambda\vdash k
(or
\lambda\vDash k
) by
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is a tiling of Young diagram of
\lambda
by bricks from
B_{w}
, where no brick is in more than one row and the order of the bricks in a row is irrelevant. To be more precise, the ordered brick tiling of shape
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{k})
with
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is an ordered tuple
(S_{1},S_{2},\cdots,S_{r})
of disjoint subsets of
B_{w}
such that

\displaystyle\bigg|\bigcup_{U\in S_{j}}U\bigg|=\lambda_{j}

for all
j=1,\cdots,r
. The set of brick tilings of
\lambda\vdash n
by
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is denoted by
B_{w}(\lambda)
. So in each element
(S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{r})
of
B_{w}(\lambda)
with
\ell(\lambda)=r
, the set
S_{i}
represent the set of bricks used to tile the
i
-th row of Young diagram of
\lambda
. Notice since the order of tiles in a row does not matter, therefore there is no ambiguity in this notation. If need be, we write
T_{i}
explicitly using the canonical cycle decomposition.

We can define an equivalence relation among brick tilings of shape
\lambda
as follows: Two brick tilings
S=(S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{r})
and
T=(T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{r})
of partition
\lambda\vdash_{r}n
, are equivalent if one is a permutation of other i.e.

\displaystyle(S_{1},\cdots,S_{r})

\displaystyle=(T_{\pi(1)},\cdots,T_{\pi(r)})\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for some}\penalty\ \pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}

We refer to these equivalence classes of tilings as
unordered brick tilings
of
\lambda
by
w
, and we denote the set of these equivalence classes by
\widetilde{B}_{w}(\lambda)
. We say a brick tiling is
crackless
whenever we have exactly one tile in each row. Otherwise, we say it is
cracked
. A
crack
in a brick tiling is the occurrence of two tiles in one row of a Young diagram. If a row contains
c
many tiles, we say it has
c-1
cracks, and the number of cracks in a brick tiling is sum of number of cracks in its rows. For a
T
in
\widetilde{B}_{w}(\lambda)
(resp.
B_{w}(\lambda)
), we call
\lambda
the
shape of
T
and denote it by
\mathrm{sh}(T)
. Furthermore, for any subset
A=\{b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{r}\}
of
B_{w}
, the
shape of
A
, denoted as
\mathrm{sh}(A)
is the sequence of lengths of bricks
b_{i}
in decreasing order.

Tiling class functions

In this section, we define class functions on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
that count the number of different types of brick tilings. Let

\displaystyle:=\penalty\ \text{number of ordered brick tilings of $\lambda$ by $w$ i.e. \penalty\ }|\widetilde{B}_{w}(\lambda)|

\displaystyle\xi^{\lambda}(w)

\displaystyle:=\penalty\ \text{number of unordered brick tilings of $\lambda$ by $w$ i.e.\penalty\ }|{B}_{w}(\lambda)|

\displaystyle\eta^{\lambda}(w)

\displaystyle:=\penalty\ \text{number of unordered crackless brick tilings of $\lambda$ by $w$}

\mathfrak{S}_{n}

1.5

Doubilet’s inversion formula

Recall the Young’s rule which states that

\displaystyle M^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{\mu\unlhd\lambda}K_{\mu\lambda}S^{\lambda}

where
K_{\mu\lambda}
are the Kostka numbers. If we denote by
\chi^{\lambda}
the irreducible character of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
corresponding to the Specht module
S^{\lambda}
, then this implies

\displaystyle\zeta^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\unlhd\lambda}K_{\mu\lambda}\chi^{\mu}

We also know that for partitions
\lambda,\mu\vdash n
, the coefficients
K_{\mu\lambda}\neq 0
if and only if
\mu\unlhd\lambda
. Furthermore,
K_{\lambda\lambda}=1
. This implies that the Kostka matrix
K=(K_{\mu\lambda})
is invertible. Therefore, we can write the irreducible character
\chi^{\lambda}
as a linear combination of
\zeta^{\lambda}
’s. Such an inversion formula was given by Doubilet in
[
Dou73
]
, which states

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\\
\widetilde{\sigma\lambda}\vdash n\end{subarray}}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\zeta^{\widetilde{\sigma\lambda}}

where
\sigma\lambda
is the sequence defined as

\displaystyle\sigma\lambda=(\lambda_{1}+\sigma(1)-1,\lambda_{2}+\sigma(2)-2,\cdots,\lambda_{n}+\sigma(n)-n)

(\sigma\lambda)_{i}=\lambda_{i}+\sigma(i)-i
for all
i=1,2,\dots,n
and
\widetilde{\sigma\lambda}
is the rearrangement of this sequence in a weakly decreasing order. Since
\lambda_{i}=0
for
\ell(\lambda)<i\leq n
, this implies that for
\widetilde{\sigma\lambda}
to be a partition, we should have

\displaystyle\sigma(i)-i

\displaystyle\geq 0

equivalently
\sigma(i)\geq i
for all
\ell(\lambda)<i\leq n
. This implies
\sigma(n)=n
and since
\sigma
is a bijection and hence injective
\sigma(n-1)=n-1
. Inductively, we have
\sigma(i)=i
for
\ell(\mu)<i<n
. Let
\overline{\mathfrak{S}}_{\ell}(\lambda)
be the subgroup of permutations that pointwise fix all the elements from
\ell(\lambda)+1
to
n
. Then, the above observation implies that the sum on the right of the inversion formula can be taken over
\sigma\in\overline{\mathfrak{S}}_{\ell(\lambda)}
such that
\widetilde{\sigma\lambda}\vdash n
. Hence we can restate the inversion formula as:

Lemma 1.7
.

(Doubilet’s inversion formula) Let
\lambda\vdash n
, then keeping the notation of this section, we have

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\overline{\mathfrak{S}}_{\ell(\lambda)}\\
\widetilde{\sigma\lambda}\vdash n\end{subarray}}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\zeta^{\widetilde{\sigma\lambda}}

For our purposes later, we will be able to reduce this sum to a smaller indexing set using the condition that
\sigma\lambda
needs to be a composition of
n
. This enforces that none of the parts of
\sigma\lambda
should be less than
1
. For the time being, we show a simple example.

Example 1.8
.

For a positive integer
n\geq 1
, take the partition
\lambda=(n-1,1)\vdash n
, then

\displaystyle\chi^{(n-1,n)}

\displaystyle=\zeta^{(n-1,1)}-\zeta^{(n)}

which reminiscent of the fact that the
M^{(n-1,1)}=S^{(n-1,1)}\oplus M^{(n)}
, where we know that
S^{n-1,n}
is the regular representation and
M^{(n)}
is the trivial representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

1.6

Face numbers of permutohedron

The standard permutohedron is an example of a convex polytope associated to permutations. To each permutation
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we associate a point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle p_{w}

\displaystyle=(w(1),w(2),\cdots,w(n))\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

The
standard permutohedron

\Pi_{n}
is defined to be the convex hull of these points, i.e.

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{conv}\{p_{w}:w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\}

Note that for each
p_{w}
, the sum of all the coordinates equals
1+2+\cdots+n
, i.e.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{w,i}=1+2+\cdots+n=\binom{n+1}{2}

This means that
\Pi_{n}
lies in a hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and consequently
\dim(\Pi_{n})\leq n-1
. Furthermore, for each
p_{w}
, sum of any
k
coordinates is atleast
1+2+\dots+k
, i.e. for any
I\subset[n]

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}p_{w,i}\geq 1+2+\cdots+|I|=\binom{|I|+1}{2}

It is known classically that these inequalities are enough to describe
\Pi_{n}
. We need the following known result (see for example
[
Zie95
]
) from polytope theory.

Theorem 1.9
.

The standard permutohedron
\Pi_{n}
is a simple
(n-1)
-dimensional convex polytope given by

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=n\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\geq\binom{|I|+1}{2},\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ I\subset[n]\right\}

with face numbers given by

\displaystyle f_{k}(\Pi_{n})

\displaystyle=(n-k)!\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
n-k\end{Bmatrix}

for
k=0,\cdots,n-1
, where
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}
is the Stirling number of the second kind (that counts the partitions of
[n]
into
k
blocks).

Since
\Pi_{n}
is simple convex polytope, its dual
\Pi_{n}^{*}
is a simplicial convex polytope. We can consider the simplicial complex
\Delta_{n-1}:=\partial\Pi_{n}^{*}
(the boundary complex of
\Pi_{n}^{*}
). Its face numbers are given by

\displaystyle f_{k-1}(\Delta_{n-1})

\displaystyle=(k+1)!\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k+1\end{Bmatrix}

for
k=0,1,\cdots,n-1
. This implies that the reduced Euler characteristic of
\Delta_{n-1}
is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k-1}f_{k-1}(\Delta_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k-1}(k+1)!\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k+1\end{Bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k-1}k!\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}

Since
\Delta_{n-1}
is homeomorphic to the
(n-2)
-dimensional sphere
S^{n-2}
, its reduced Euler characteristic equals to
(-1)^{n-2}
. This can be rewritten as

\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k}k!\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}=(-1)^{n}

(5)

We will use this identity in later section.

2

Combinatorics of tiling class functions

Characters of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are examples of class functions. What that means is that they are constant over conjugacy classes in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. This implies that they only depend on cycle structure of evaluating permutation. Simple examples of class functions are
c_{i}
’s which count the number of cycles of length
i
in a permutation. It is a result of Frobenius
[
Fro04
]
that the every irreducible character of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is a polynomial function of
c_{i}
’s, called
character polynomials
. They were also studied later by Murnaghan
[
Mur51
]
and Specht
[
Spe60
]
. Macdonald mentions them in
[
Mac79
]
and attribute them to Frobenius. Garsia and Goupil
[
GG09
]
gave an umbral construction of these polynomials. Kerber also studied them in his book
[
Ker99
]
on group actions. Recently, they have reoccurred in the context of representation stability
[
CEF15
]
. We study our tiling class functions as polynomials in
c_{i}
’s. The main result in this section is identity
2.5
, which equates two alternating sums of class functions. We provide two proofs of it: one using homology on poset of brick tilings, and the other using reduced Euler characteristic of boundary complex of dual polytope to permutohedron.

2.1

Character polynomials

The class functions
\{c_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are defined as

\displaystyle c_{i}:\mathfrak{S}_{n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle\longmapsto\penalty\ \text{number of cycles of $w$ of length $i$}

Let
\mathbb{Q}[c_{1},c_{2},\cdots]
be the ring of polynomials in
c_{i}
’s with rational coefficient. We call a polynomial
q(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots)\in\mathbb{Q}[c_{1},c_{2},\cdots]
a
class polynomial
. Frobenius
[
Fro04
]
showed the following

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{\ell})\vdash n
and let
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be a permutation of cycle type
\mathrm{cyc}(w)=\mu=(\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{r})\vdash n
, then
\chi^{\lambda}(w)
equals the coefficient of
x_{1}^{\lambda_{1}+\ell-1}x_{2}^{\lambda+\ell-2}\cdots x_{\ell}^{\lambda_{\ell}}
in the expansion of

\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq\ell}(x_{i}-x_{j})\prod_{i=1}^{r}(x_{1}^{\mu_{i}}+x_{2}^{\mu_{i}}+\cdots+x_{\ell}^{\mu_{i}})

The character polynomial
q_{\lambda}(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots)
is defined as the
unique
polynomial in
\mathbb{Q}[c_{1},c_{2},\cdots]
such that for all partitions
\lambda\vdash k
and
n\geq\lambda_{1}+k
, we have

\chi^{\lambda[n]}(w)=q_{\lambda}(c_{1}(w),c_{2}(w),\cdots)

(6)

for all
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Note that the character polynomial
q_{\lambda}
as a polynomial does not depend on
n
, and hence gives a uniform description of irreducible characters corresponding to augmented partition
\lambda[n]
for all symmetric groups at the same time (with
n\geq\lambda_{1}+k
). This is quite remarkable.

Example 2.2
.

The character polynomial for empty partition is the constant function
1
, as it corresponds to the trivial representation. The character polynomial for
\lambda=1
is given by

\displaystyle q_{(1)}

\displaystyle=c_{1}-1

as
\lambda[n]=(n-1,1)
corresponds to the regular representation.

For
\lambda\vdash k
, there is a special family of class polynomials called
binomial class polynomials

\binom{c}{\lambda}
defined as

\displaystyle\binom{c}{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{c_{i}}{m_{i}(\lambda)}

where
m_{i}(\lambda)
is the multiplicity of
i
in
\lambda
. As an example, for
\lambda=(4,2,2,1,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\binom{c}{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\binom{c_{4}}{1}\binom{c_{2}}{2}\binom{c_{1}}{2}

Notice that for
\lambda\vdash k
and
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with
n\geq k
, having a unordered crackless tiling of
\lambda
by
\pi
is equivalent to choosing
m_{i}(\lambda)
bricks from all
c_{i}(w)
bricks of
w
of length
i
, for each
i=1,\cdots,n
. This implies

\eta^{\lambda}=\binom{c}{\lambda}

(7)

as class functions on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. This implies the generating function equality

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{\mu\vdash k}\eta^{\mu}\right)t^{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1+t^{i})^{c_{i}}

(8)

in the ring of formal power series
\mathbb{Q}[c_{1},c_{2}\cdots][[t]]
.

\sigma\in\overline{\mathfrak{S}}_{k+1}
such that
\sigma(i)\geq i-1
. Note that there is no condition on
\sigma(1)
as
n\geq 2k
. So we can say that

\displaystyle\sigma(i)\geq i-1

for all
i=1,\dots,k+1
.
This implies such a permutation is uniquely determined by the set

\displaystyle S(\sigma)=\{(i,\sigma(i)):\sigma(i)\geq i\}

Fixing

, we see that there are
2^{j-1}
such permutations and each such permutation gives a composition
\mu\vDash j
. On the other hand, given a composition
\mu\vDash j
, we can construct
\sigma
by taking

S(\sigma)=\{(1,k+1-j),(k+1-j+1,k+1-j+\mu_{1}),\\
(k+1-j+\mu_{1}+1,k+1-j+\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}),\cdots\}

(11)

And for such a
\sigma
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma\lambda[n]

\displaystyle=(n-\sigma(1),\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{\ell})

\displaystyle\left<\sigma\lambda[n]\right>

\displaystyle=(\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{\ell})\vDash j

Combining these we can prove the following theorem:

Theorem 2.4
.

For
\lambda=(1^{k})\vdash k
and
n\geq 2k
, we have the following equality of class functions

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda[n]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j+1}\left(\sum_{\mu\vDash j}(-1)^{\ell(\mu)}\zeta^{\mu}\right)

Proof.

Due to the observations above, we can simplify equation
10
as follows: Fix
\sigma(1)=k+1-j
, now each permutation gives a unique composition
\mu
of
j
, so we sum over all compositions of
j
. And we do this over all possible images
\sigma(1)
, which can be any integer from 1 to
k+1
. This translates to
j
varying from
0
to
k
.

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda[n]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{k+1}}}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\zeta^{\widetilde{\sigma\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\overline{\mathfrak{S}_{k+1}}}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\zeta^{\left<\widetilde{\sigma\lambda}\right>}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k+1-j}\left(\sum_{\mu\vDash j}(-1)^{\sum\mu+1}\zeta^{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k+1-j}\left(\sum_{\mu\vDash j}(-1)^{\ell(\mu)+j}\zeta^{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k+1}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(\sum_{\mu\vDash j}(-1)^{\ell(\mu)}\zeta^{\mu}\right)

In above, we appropriately kept track of the sign as the cycle type of
\sigma
with
\sigma(1)=k+1-j
that give rise to composition
\mu
is given by the weakly decreasing rearrangement of

. This gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell(\mu)}(\mu_{i}+1)\bigg)+k+1-j

\displaystyle=\ell(\mu)+k+1

This explains the simplification in the sum above.
∎

Now we move to an interesting observation that simplifies further the calculation of
\chi^{\lambda[n]}
for
\lambda=(1^{k})\vdash k
where

. This result should be thought of as the main result of this paper.

Theorem 2.5
.

For a positive integer

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\vDash j}(-1)^{\ell(\mu)}\zeta^{\mu}

Stability for sequence of polynomials and power series

We say a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomials

,

to

\displaystyle[x^{k}]f_{m}

\displaystyle=[x^{k}]f_{n}=[x^{k}]f

denote the coefficient of
x^{k}
in
f
. If
f
exists, then it is seen to be unique. This definition can also be generalized to multivariable case. We say a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of multivariable polynomials

to

\displaystyle[x_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots x_{r}^{k_{r}}]f_{m}

\displaystyle=[x_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots x_{r}^{k_{r}}]f_{n}=[x^{k}]f

.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-x}\frac{1}{1-y}

\displaystyle=[c_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots c_{r}^{k_{r}}]\frac{g}{1-t}

for
k_{i}>m_{i}
for all
i
.

Example 3.3
.

Consider the element
f\in\mathbb{Q}[x][[t]]
given by

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=1+(1+x)t+(1+x+x^{2})t+\cdots

then
g=\frac{1}{1-x}
, and therefore
f
stabilizes to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-x}\frac{1}{1-t}

3.2

The cycle-index generating function

Recall the
cycle index
of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
which is defined as

\displaystyle Z(\mathfrak{S}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{c_{i}(w)}

It is well known, for example from
[
Cam99
]
, that the generating function
\Gamma
of cycle indices of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}Z(\mathfrak{S}_{n})t^{n}

can also be written as

\Gamma=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{i}t^{i}}{i}\right)

(14)

This is a very useful result as indicated by the following example:

Example 3.4
.

Consider the formal equality

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}Z(\mathfrak{S}_{n})t^{n}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{i}t^{i}}{i}\right)

Evaluating the formal partial derivative
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}
of both sides at
x_{i}=1
for all
i=1,2,\cdots
, gives the following

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}c_{k}(w)\bigg)t^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\frac{t^{k}}{1-t}

which is saying that the expected number of
k
cycles in a permutation of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
for
n\geq k
equals
\frac{1}{k}
. This is a classical result in combinatorial probability theory
[
B1́2
]
.

Some other identities we would like to highlight here are

\Gamma[x_{i}\longrightarrow x^{i}]=\frac{1}{1-xt}

(15)

and

\Gamma[x_{i}\longrightarrow 1-x^{i}]=\frac{1-xt}{1-t}

(16)

where the notation
\Gamma[x_{i}\longrightarrow f(x_{i})]
means substituting
f(x_{i})
for
x_{i}
in the expression for
\Gamma
.

3.3

Goupil’s generating function identity

Going back to our identity
2.5
, we consider the class function

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\vdash j}(-1)^{\ell(\mu)+1}\eta^{\mu}

from the identity
8
, we have the following generating function

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{\mu\vdash j}(-1)^{\ell(\mu)+1}\eta^{\mu}\right)t^{j}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\bigg(1-(-t)^{i}\bigg)^{c_{i}}

Using this and theorem
2.4
we have another proof of the following identity of Goupil
[
Gou99
]
for generating function for hook characters

Theorem 3.5
.

For
\lambda=(1^{k})\vdash k
, we have the following generating function

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q_{(1^{k})}t^{k}\doteq\frac{1}{1+t}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\bigg(1-(-t)^{i}\bigg)^{c_{i}}

(17)

Proof.

Since from theorem
2.4
, we have

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda[n]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j+1}\left(\sum_{\mu\vDash j}(-1)^{\ell(\mu)}\zeta^{\mu}\right)

which corresponds to alternating sum of class function whose generating function is given by equation
17
. Now recall that the generating function for alternating sum of a sequence can be constructed by multiplying the generating function by
\frac{1}{1+t}
. This gives the required result.
∎

3.4

Rosas’ formula for certain Kronecker coefficients

Let
\lambda,\mu
and
\nu
be partitions of
n
. The Kronecker coefficients
g^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
are defined as the coefficient of
\chi^{\lambda}
in the expansion of
\chi^{\mu}\chi^{\nu}
into irreducible characters:

\displaystyle g^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}({\mathfrak{S}_{n}})=\left<\chi^{\lambda},\chi^{\mu}\chi^{\nu}\right>_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\chi^{\mu}(\sigma)\chi^{\nu}(\sigma)

Using the equation
17
for generating function of irreducible hook character, here we will derive the formula for Kronecker coefficients indexed by hooks given by Rosas
[
Ros00
]
, which says:

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\overline{g}_{k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}}
be the reduced Kronecker coefficients corresponding to the triple
((n-k_{1},1^{k_{1}}),(n-k_{2},1^{k_{2}}),(n-k_{3},1^{k_{3}}))
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\overline{g}_{k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}}x^{k_{1}}y^{k_{2}}z^{k_{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+xyz}{(1-xy)(1-yz)(1-xz)}

Proof.

We start with the substituion
x_{i}\leftarrow(1-(-x)^{i})
in the cycle index function

\displaystyle Z(\mathfrak{S}_{n})[x_{i}\leftarrow(1-(-x)^{i})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-(-x)^{i})^{a_{i}(w)}

\displaystyle[x^{k}]\frac{1}{1+x}Z(\mathfrak{S}_{n})[x_{i}\leftarrow(1-(-x)^{i})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\chi^{(1^{k})[n]}(w)

This suggests that if we let
Y_{n}=Z(\mathfrak{S}_{n})[x_{i}\leftarrow(1-(-x)^{i})(1-(-y)^{i})(1-(-z)^{i})]
, we have

\displaystyle[x^{k_{1}}y^{k_{2}}z^{k_{3}}]\frac{1}{(1+x)(1+y)(1+z)}Y_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\chi^{(1^{k_{1}})[n]}(w)\chi^{(1^{k_{2}})[n]}(w)\chi^{(1^{k_{3}})[n]}(w)

\displaystyle=g_{k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}}

Now notice that
Y:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Y_{n}t^{n}
is given by substituting
[x_{i}\leftarrow(1-(-x)^{i})(1-(-y)^{i})(1-(-z)^{i})]
in
\Gamma
which using equation
14
becomes

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{i}}{i}t^{i}\right)[x_{i}\leftarrow(1-(-x)^{i})(1-(-y)^{i})(1-(-z)^{i})]

Now using equation
16
, we have

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-t}\frac{(1+xyzt)(1+xt)(1+yt)(1+zt)}{(1-xyt)(1-yzt)(1-xzt)}

Note that the identity

\displaystyle W:=\frac{1}{1-t}\frac{1}{(1-xyt)(1-yzt)(1-xzt)}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{a+b+c=j}(xy)^{a}(yz)^{b}(xz)^{c}\right)t^{n}\right)

implies that
W
as member of
\mathbb{Q}[x,y,z][[t]]
stabilize to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-t}\frac{1}{(1-xy)(1-yz)(1-xz)}

which in turn implies that
\frac{1}{(1+x)(1+y)(1+z)}Y
stabilizes to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-t}\frac{(1+xyz)}{(1-xy)(1-yz)(1-xz)}

but
\frac{1}{(1+x)(1+y)(1+z)}Y
is the generating function of
g_{k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}}
. This implies that the generating function for the reduced Kronecker coefficients
\overline{g}_{k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{(1+xyz)}{(1-xy)(1-yz)(1-xz)}

∎
