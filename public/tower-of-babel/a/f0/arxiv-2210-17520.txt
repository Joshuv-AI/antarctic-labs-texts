Fully Adaptive Composition for Gaussian Differential Privacy
Adam Smith, Abhradeep Thakurta

Abstract
We show that Gaussian Differential Privacy, a variant of differential privacy tailored to the analysis of Gaussian noise addition, composes gracefully even in the presence of a fully adaptive analyst. Such an analyst selects mechanisms (to be run on a sensitive data set) and their privacy budgets adaptively, that is, based on the answers from other mechanisms run previously on the same data set. In the language of Rogers, Roth, Ullman and Vadhan, this gives a filter for GDP with the same parameters as for nonadaptive composition.



Let
\mathcal{D}
be a domain of data records, and
\mathcal{R}
be a set of potential outputs for a mechanism (without loss of generality,
\{0,1\}^{*}
). We say a randomized function (henceforth, a
mechanism
)
\mathcal{M}:D^{*}\to\mathcal{R}
satisfies
\mu
-
Gaussian differential privacy
(or
\mu
-GDP)
[
1
]
if for every pair of neighboring data sets
D
and
D^{\prime}
, the distributions
\mathcal{M}(D)
and
\mathcal{M}(D^{\prime})
are “at least as hard to tell apart” as two Gaussian random variables with standard deviation 1 and means 0 and
\mu
. Specifically, if
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, then we ask that that exists a randomized map
F
(depending on
D
and
D^{\prime}
) such that
\mathcal{M}(D)\sim F(Z)
and
\mathcal{M}(D^{\prime})\sim F(\mu+Z)
. One can also characterize GDP, via Blackwell’s theorem, in terms of bounds on the ROC curve for all tests that aim to distinguish
\mathcal{M}(D)
from
\mathcal{M}(D^{\prime})

[
1
]
; we will not need that characterization here.

We prove that Gaussian Differential Privacy (GDP)
[
1
]
composes even when the analyst is fully adaptive; the privacy parameters adds up in exactly the same way as in nonadaptive composition. In the language of
[
3

Consider an interaction between a data curator
\mathcal{M}_{0}

D

{\sf Adv}
. In round

What restriction can the curator impose to ensure that the overall interaction is
\mu_{0}

\mu_{0}

\mu_{i}

\sum_{i}\mu_{i}^{2}\leq\mu)^{2}

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{0}
be an interactive mechanism (as above) which ensures that for every analyst, every sequence of queries
(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mu_{1}),...,(\mathcal{M}_{T},\mu_{T})
satisfies

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}^{2}\leq\mu_{0}^{2}\ .

Then
\mathcal{M}_{0}
satisfies
\mu_{0}
-GDP.

A different proof of this statement was given independently and concurrently by Koskela et al.
[
2

.

Proof.

Fix two neighboring data sets
D
and
D^{\prime}
. For the purposes of analyzing privacy, we can assume w.l.o.g. that the mechanism receives as input just a bit
b\in\{0,1\}
specifying which of
D
and
D^{\prime}
to use. A mechanism
\cal A
is then
\mu
-GDP if there is a randomized function
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{R}
such that
\mathcal{A}(b)=F(b\cdot\mu+Z)
where
Z\sim N(0,1)
.

Now consider the following interaction procedure between a mechanism
\mathcal{A}
and an adversary
{\sf Adv}
: in round
i
,
{\sf Adv}
sends
\mu_{i}
to
\mathcal{A}
and receives
H_{i}=b\cdot\mu_{i}+Z_{i}
, where
Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a fresh draw from the standard Gaussian distribution. The mechanism continues to answer queries as long as
\sum_{i}\mu_{i}^{2}\leq\mu_{0}^{2}
.
The adversary may select
\mu_{i}
based on
H_{1},..,H_{i-1}
, but the mechanism applied at each round always consists of additive Gaussian noise. We first show that to prove Theorem
1
, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{A}
is
\mu_{0}
-GDP.

Claim 2
.

If, for all
{\sf Adv}
, the mechanism
\mathcal{A}
defined above satisfies
\mu_{0}
-GDP, then so is
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.

Proof.

The proof follows from the simulation-based definition of GDP. Given an adversary
{\sf Adv}^{\prime}
that interacts with
\mu_{0}
and a pair of data sets
D
and
D^{\prime}
, we can construct an adversary
{\sf Adv}
that interacts with
\mathcal{A}
and simulates the transcript of
\mathcal{M}_{0}
’s interaction with
{\sf Adv}^{\prime}
: for each query
(\mu_{i},\mathcal{M}_{i})
issued by
{\sf Adv}^{\prime}
, the adversary
{\sf Adv}
constructs a (possibly randomized) function
F_{i}
such that
\mathcal{M}_{i}(D)\sim F_{i}(Z_{i})
and
\mathcal{M}_{i}(D^{\prime})\sim F_{i}(\mu_{i}+Z_{i})
. It then issues the query
\mu_{i}
to
\mathcal{A}
, receives
H_{i}
, and feeds
F_{i}(H_{i})
as a response to
{\sf Adv}^{\prime}
. If
\mathcal{A}
satisfies
\mu_{0}
-GDP, then there is a randomized function
F^{*}
such that
F^{*}(b\cdot\mu_{0}+Z_{0})
faithfully simulates the transcript of the interaction of
\mathcal{A}(b)
with
{\sf Adv}
when
Z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The internal messages that
{\sf Adv}
feeds to
{\sf Adv}^{\prime}
then correctly simulate the interaction of
{\sf Adv}^{\prime}
with
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.
∎

To show that mechanism
\mathcal{A}
indeed satisfies
\mu_{0}
-GDP, we explicitly specify the postprocessing function
F^{*}
(depending on
\mu_{0}
) that takes as input
W_{0}\leftarrow b\mu_{0}+Z_{0}
, where
Z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and simulates the responses
\mathcal{A}(b)
would give to a sequence of (adaptively chosen) messages
\mu_{i}
.

Without loss of generality, we make two simplifying assumptions: First, we assume the sequence of queries satisfies
\sum_{i=1}^{T}\mu_{i}^{2}\leq\mu_{0}^{2}
. This can be enforced by having
\mathcal{A}
(and
F^{*}
) refuse to answer queries that would push the sum over the threshold. The analyst/adversary can predict this decision on its own, so it conveys no additional information. Second, we assume that
\mu_{0}=1
, since we can replace the actual queries
\mu_{i}
with
m_{i}=\frac{\mu_{i}}{\mu_{0}}
and rescale answers appropriately.

Warm-up: An Online Simulation for Nonadaptive Adversaries.

Suppose (with significant loss of generality!) that the analyst is not adaptive. That is, it decides ahead of time on the sequence
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},..,m_{T})
, with
\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}\leq 1
, that it will ask as queries.

On input
W_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(b,1)
and this sequence,
F^{*}
must compute a sequence of variables
W_{i}
such that
W_{1},...,W_{T}
are jointly distributed as
\sim\mathcal{N}(b\mathbf{m},\mathbb{I})
. However, we will get an appropriate simulator by imposing an additional requirement: it must do this computation
online
. That is,
W_{i}
can depend on
(m_{1},...,m_{i})
and any internal state, but not on future queries
m_{i+1},...,m_{T}
.

A natural approach is to respond with
m_{i}W_{0}
at each round
i
. This sequence has the correct mean
b\mathbf{m}
; unfortunately, its covariance matrix is
\mathbf{m}^{\top}\mathbf{m}
, which is incorrect. One can fix this by adding a component of
independent
random noise
(U_{1},...,U_{T})
, distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{T})
where
\Sigma_{T}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{m}^{\top}\mathbf{m}
. Since
\|\mathbf{m}\|\leq 1
, the matrix
\Sigma_{T}
is PSD and thus a valid covariance matrix for a multivariate Gaussian distribution. The sequence
W_{i}=m_{i}W_{0}+U_{i}
still has the right mean, and its covariance matrix is now
\mathbf{m}^{\top}\mathbf{m}+\Sigma_{T}=\mathbb{I}
, as desired. If the
m_{i}
were known ahead of time, we would be done, since the simulator could generate
(U_{1},...,U_{T})
before the start of the interaction, and add the
U_{i}
’s as the interaction proceeds.
The issue is that we have set our selves the more stringent goal of executing the simulation without knowing the
m_{i}
’s ahead of time. The challenge, therefore, is to sample the
U_{i}
’s online, as the values
m_{i}
are revealed.

To see why this is possible, recall that the Cholesky decomposition of
\Sigma_{T}
gives a lower-triangular matrix
L
such that
\Sigma_{T}=LL^{\top}
. The matrix
L
is uniquely determined if we require that
L
have exactly
rank(\Sigma_{T})
positive diagonal elements and
T-rank(\Sigma_{T})
columns that are all zero. We refer to this
L
as the
canonical Cholesky factor
of
\Sigma_{T}
.

For any
i
between 1 and
T
, let
L_{i}
denote the
i\times i
submatrix of
L
restricted to the first
i
rows and columns. We can write
L
as four blocks, with
L_{i}
on the top left and zeros on the top right:

L=\begin{bmatrix}L_{i}&\mathbf{0}_{i\times(T-i)}\\
\vdots&\ddots\\
\end{bmatrix}

The product
LL^{\top}
can also be written in block form, where the top left
block is
L_{i}L_{i}^{\top}
. This must equal the corresponding submatrix of
\Sigma_{T}
, which we know is
\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{m}_{i}\mathbf{m}_{i}^{\top}
where
\mathbf{m}_{i}=(m_{1},...,m_{i})
. That is,

LL^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}L_{i}L_{i}^{\top}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\ddots\\
\end{bmatrix}=\Sigma_{T}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{m}_{i}\mathbf{m}_{i}^{\top}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\ddots\\
\end{bmatrix}\,.

Thus,
L_{i}
is the canonical Cholesky factor of
\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{m}_{i}\mathbf{m}_{i}^{\top}
, which is the covariance matrix we need for
U_{1},...,U_{i}
. We can compute
L_{i}
given only the queries
(m_{1},...m_{i})
from the first
i
rounds.

We obtain the following simple algorithm for
F^{*}
, on input
W_{0}
:

1.

Generate
V_{1},...,V_{T}
i.i.d. from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

2.

For
i=1
to
T
:

(a)

Receive
m_{i}
from
{\sf Adv}
.

(b)

Let
\mathbf{m}_{i}=(m_{1},...,m_{i})
and let
L_{i}
be the canonical Cholesky factor
1
1

1

Specifically, we can write
L_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}L_{i-1}&\mathbf{0}\\
-m_{i}\left(L_{i-1}^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{i-1}\right)^{\top}&\frac{1-\left\|\mathbf{m}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{1-\left\|\mathbf{m}_{i-1}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\end{bmatrix}
.

of
\Sigma_{i}=\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{m}_{i}\mathbf{m}_{i}^{\top}
.

(c)

Let
U_{i}
be the last entry of
L_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}
where
\mathbf{v}_{i}=(V_{1},...,V_{i})
.

(d)

Output
W_{i}=m_{i}W_{0}+U_{i}
.

For every fixed
\mathbf{m}
and
b
, if
W_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(b,1)
, then the joint distribution of the responses
W_{1},...,W_{T}
is
\mathcal{N}(b\mathbf{m},\mathbb{I})
. In particular, for every setting of
W_{1},..,W_{i-1}
, the random variable
W_{i}
is distributed as
\mathcal{N}(bm_{i},1)
(which is the distribution of the response sent by
\mathcal{A}(b)
). This proves the result for the limited case when
\mathbf{m}
is fixed ahead of time by
{\sf Adv}
.

Fully Adaptive Composition.

We now argue that the same simulation works for fully adaptive queries, where
m_{i}
may depend on the interaction so far. Our analysis of the noninteractive setting showed the following:
for every setting of
b
and
W_{1},..,W_{i-1}
and the first
i
queries
m_{1},...,m_{i}
, the random variable
W_{i}
is distributed as
\mathcal{N}(bm_{i},1)
. Furthermore, it is computed using only the values
W_{0}
,
m_{1},...,m_{i}
and
w_{1},...,w_{i-1}
.

Because of the statement holds for all
m_{i}
(and not, say, just with high probability), the statement continues to hold when
m_{i}
is selected by
{\sf Adv}
. By induction over the rounds
i
, the transcript of an interaction with
F^{*}(W_{0})
(where
W_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(b,1)
) will be identically distributed to that of an interaction with
\mathcal{A}(b)
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎
