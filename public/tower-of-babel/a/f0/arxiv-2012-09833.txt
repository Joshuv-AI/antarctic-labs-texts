Bayesian semiparametric modelling of covariance matrices for multivariate longitudinal data
Georgios Papageorgiou

Abstract




Abstract

The article develops marginal models for multivariate longitudinal responses. Overall, the model
consists of five regression submodels, one for the mean and four for the covariance matrix, with the latter
resulting by considering various matrix decompositions.
The decompositions that we employ are intuitive, easy to understand, and they do not rely on any assumptions
such as the presence of an ordering among the multivariate responses. The regression submodels
are semiparametric, with unknown functions represented by basis function expansions. We use spike-slap
priors for the regression coefficients to achieve variable selection and function regularization,
and to obtain parameter estimates that account for model uncertainty.
An efficient Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm for posterior sampling is developed.
The simulation studies presented investigate the effects of priors on posteriors, the gains that one may have when
considering multivariate longitudinal analyses instead of univariate ones, and whether these gains
can counteract the negative effects of missing data.
We apply the methods on a highly unbalanced longitudinal dataset with four responses observed over of period of
20
years.

Keywords
: Cholesky decomposition; Clustering; Model averaging; Semiparametric regression; Variable selection; Variance-correlation separation

1
Introduction

There is a variety of systems that evolve over time and are too intricate to be appropriately characterized
by a single outcome variable. Consider for example ‘cognition’, the topic of the data example presented later in this article.
According to the American Psychological Association’s Dictionary of Psychology, cognition is a term that refers
to ‘all forms of knowing and awareness, such as perceiving, conceiving, remembering, reasoning, judging,
imagining, and problem solving’. Cognition is a complex, not directly observable, system that evolves over time.
Measuring cognitive function relies on administering multiple psychometric tests, and investigating how it evolves
and declines in the elderly requires observations over multiple years. Analysing datasets generated by such investigations
requires flexible regression models for multivariate longitudinal responses. The main goal of this article is to
develop, within a Bayesian framework, marginal models for multivariate longitudinal continuous responses,
non-parametrically linking the mean vectors and covariance matrices to the covariates. The current literature
on modelling covariance matrices for multivariate longitudinal data relies on decompositions of the covariance matrices that are either
difficult to interpret or that make assumptions that are not tenable. Here, we avoid all such decompositions and
we only utilize those that are easy to understand and justify.

In order to set the notation, let
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{j}
denote a vector of
p
responses observed at time point
t_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,n
,

\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}
the
np
-dimensional vector of all responses, and

\Sigma
the covariance matrix of
Y
.
Matrix
\Sigma
must satisfy the positive definiteness (pd) condition, and that creates a
major challenge when modelling its elements as functions of covariates.
To ensure that this condition is satisfied, it is necessary to factorized
\Sigma
into a product of matrices.
The decomposition
(
Hamilton, 1994
)

\mbox{\boldmath$L$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}^{\top}=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}

(1)

has been utilized for modelling multivariate longitudinal data by
Xu & Mackenzie, 2012
,
Kim & Zimmerman, 2012
,
Kohli et al., 2016
and
Lee et al., 2020
.
Matrices
L
and
D
have the following structures

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cc}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mbox{\boldmath$I$}&\mbox{\boldmath$0$}&\dots&\mbox{\boldmath$0$}\\
-\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{21}&\mbox{\boldmath$I$}&\dots&\mbox{\boldmath$0$}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{n1}&-\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{n_{2}}&\dots&\mbox{\boldmath$I$}\\
\end{array}\right],&\mbox{\boldmath$D$}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{1}&\mbox{\boldmath$0$}&\dots&\mbox{\boldmath$0$}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$0$}&\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{2}&\dots&\mbox{\boldmath$0$}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mbox{\boldmath$0$}&\mbox{\boldmath$0$}&\dots&\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{n}\\
\end{array}\right],\end{array}

where submatrices
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{jk}
are unconstrained,
j=2,\dots,n,k<j,
while submatrices
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{j}
are pd,
j=1,\dots,n
,
and all submatrices in
L
and
D
are of dimension
p\times p
.
The following interpretation that has appeared in, among others,

Pourahmadi, 1999
and
Daniels & Pourahmadi, 2002
for the univariate case, and
Xu & Mackenzie, 2012
and
Kim & Zimmerman, 2012
for the multivariate
case, provides justification for the lack of constraints on the elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{jk}
and the pd constraint on the elements of

\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{j}
. For the sake of simplicity we assume that vectors
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{j}
have zero mean.
Let
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}_{j}
denote the linear least squares predictor of
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{j}

based on its predecessors
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{j-1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{1},
and let
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{j}=\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{j}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}_{j}

denote the prediction error. It can be shown that the predictor
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}_{j}
is expressed in terms of the negatives of
the submatrices of
L
as

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{jk}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{k}.

(12)

Further, the prediction error variance is given by the corresponding block of
D
i.e.
\text{var}(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{j})=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{j}
.
To see how factorization (
1
) is satisfied,
let
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}
.
We have that
\text{var}(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$})=\text{var}(\mbox{\boldmath$L$}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$})=\mbox{\boldmath$L$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}^{\top}
,
and since consecutive prediction errors are uncorrelated, it follows that

\mbox{\boldmath$L$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}^{\top}=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}
.
The sets of matrices
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{jk}:j=2,\dots,n,k<j\}
and

\{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{j}:j=1,\dots,n\}
have been termed the generalized autoregressive matrices and
innovation covariance matrices of
\Sigma

(
Xu & Mackenzie, 2012
)
.

A simple modification of (
1
) can provide an alternative interpretation.
Since
\mbox{\boldmath$L$}^{-1}
has the same block triangular structure as
L
, the decomposition

\mbox{\boldmath$L$}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(\mbox{\boldmath$L$}^{-1})^{\top}=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{\ast},

(13)

also holds, where
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{\ast}
has the same structure as
D
.
This decomposition, for univariate responses, has been studied by
Zhang & Leng, 2012
,
and for multivariate ones by
Feng et al., 2016
. With this decomposition, the submatrices of
L
that are below the main diagonal can
be interpreted as moving average coefficient matrices in a time series context, hence, again, they are unconstrained.

In this article, we adopt the decomposition in (
1
) that leads to the interpretation in (
12
).
Since matrices
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{jk}
are unconstrained, their elements can be modelled utilizing semiparametric regression models.
These are described in Section
2
.

The decompositions in (
1
) and (
13
) have been unanimous among researchers modelling multivariate longitudinal data.
By contrast, there have been several proposals for modelling the innovation covariance matrices
\{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{j}:j=1,\dots,n\}
.
These are reviewed in the next paragraph.
The difficulty in modelling these matrices arises from having to satisfy the pd condition and from the absence of
any natural ordering in the elements of the errors
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{j}
.

Based on the spectral decomposition and the matrix logarithmic transformation
(
Chiu et al., 1996
)
,
Xu & Mackenzie, 2012
model matrices
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{j},j=1,\dots,n,

in terms of the explanatory variable ‘time’,
t
. Further, based on the modified Cholesky decomposition
(
Pourahmadi, 1999
;
Pourahmadi, 2000
)
, i.e.
the decomposition in (
1
) applied for
p=1
,
Kim & Zimmerman, 2012
model the innovation covariance matrices as functions of
t
.
Lastly,
Kohli et al., 2016
avoid decompositions and instead model each
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{j}^{-1}
using linear covariance models
(
Anderson, 1973
)
, that is,

\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{j}^{-1}=\alpha_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$M$}_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{j}\mbox{\boldmath$M$}_{j}
, where

\mbox{\boldmath$M$}_{k}
are known pd matrices and
\alpha_{k}
are unknown weights,
k=1,\dots,j
.
The spectral decomposition and the modified Cholesky decomposition outside the context of longitudinal studies, lack simple statistical
interpretation. Further, the linear covariance model does not express the matrices in terms of covariates and its implementation in practice
can be difficult. For instance, the best performing model in the study of
Kohli et al., 2016
does not guarantee that the estimated covariance matrix is pd.

A decomposition, however, that is statistically simple and intuitive, was proposed by
Barnard et al., 2000
.
It separates the matrices
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{j}
into diagonal matrices of innovation variances
\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{j}

and innovation correlation matrices
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{j}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{j}=\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{j}^{1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{j}\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{j}^{1/2}
.
With this decomposition it is easy to model the innovation variances in terms of covariates
as the only constrained they must satisfy is the positiveness. This was the approach taken by
Lee et al., 2020

and the approach that we take in this article too.

As was remarked by
Pourahmadi, 2007
, of the available decompositions, the separation of variances and correlations is the least effective in
satisfying the pd condition. Clearly, this condition must now be satisfied by the innovation correlation matrices

\{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{j}:j=1,\dots,n\}
. To overcome this condition, and model the elements of a single correlation matrix
R
in terms of covariates,

Zhang et al., 2015
utilize the Cholesky decomposition
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}=\mbox{\boldmath$T$}\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{\top}
, parametrizing the non-zero elements of
T

using hyperspherical coordinates. In the applications presented by
Zhang et al., 2015
, the elements of
R
are modelled in terms
of covariate ‘lag’.
Lee et al., 2020
follow the same approach as
Zhang et al., 2015
, but merely reparametrise the innovation correlation matrices
without modelling their elements in terms of covariates. In the data analysis they presented,
this resulted in a common, time invariant, innovation correlation matrix, even though they stressed the importance
of correctly specifying the correlation structure.

Here, we take a different, more intuitive approach, by specifying a normal prior on the Fisher’s
z
transformation of the
nonredundant elements of the matrices
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{j}=\{r_{jkl}\}
,

\log\{(1+r_{jkl})/(1-r_{jkl})\}/2\sim N(\mu_{cj},\sigma^{2}_{cj})I[\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{j}\in\mathcal{C}],

(14)

where
\mathcal{C}
denotes the space of correlation matrices and
I[.]
the indicator function that maintains positive definiteness.
In addition, the indicator function truncates the range of the correlations and induces dependence among them
(
Daniels & Kass, 1999
)
.
Due to this truncation, parameters
\mu_{cj}
and
\sigma^{2}_{cj}
are no longer interpreted as the mean and variance of the distribution.
Here, we model both parameters as unknown functions of time
t
,

\mu_{cj}=\eta_{0}+f_{\mu}(t_{j})
and
\log\sigma^{2}_{cj}=\omega_{0}+f_{\sigma}(t_{j})
.

The model in (
14
) can be restrictive because it only allows for a single function
\mu_{cj}
that is common to all correlations.
However, it is conceivable that correlations evolve differently over time. Failing to specify a prior
that allows for the needed flexibility can have a negative impact on the estimated correlations, especially in small samples
(
Daniels & Kass, 1999
)
.
Here, this flexibility is reached by considering mixtures of normal distributions

\log\{(1+r_{jkl})/(1-r_{jkl})\}/2\sim\sum_{h}\pi_{h}N(\mu_{cjh},\sigma^{2}_{cj})I[\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{j}\in\mathcal{C}]
.
We refer to this model as the ‘grouped correlations model’
(
Liechty et al., 2004
)
. We also consider a
‘grouped variables model’ that clusters the variables instead of the correlations and it is more structured
than the grouped correlations model.

The overall model consists of
5
regression submodels. These are the models for the mean of the response vector, the
elements of the generalized autoregressive matrices, the innovation variances, and the
2
parameters
of distribution of the innovation correlation matrices. In the approach presented here, each regression
model involves nonparametrically modelled effects, represented utilizing basis function expansions.
We enable flexible estimation by utilizing several basis functions
and we implement variable selection and function regularization by utilizing spike-slab priors (see e.g.
George & McCulloch, 1997
).
The work presented in this article builds upon the work of
Chan et al., 2006
and
Papageorgiou, 2018

who describe methods for univariate response regression with nonparametric models for the mean and variance,
and the work of
Papageorgiou & Marshall, 2020
who presented methods for multivariate response regression
with nonparametric models for the means, the variances and the correlation matrix.

We develop an efficient stochastic search variable selection algorithm by using Zellner’s g-prior
(
Zellner, 1986
)
that allows integrating out the regression coefficients in
both the mean function of the responses
E(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$})
and the parameter
\mu_{cj}
(or
\mu_{cjh}
) of the innovation correlation matrices.
In addition, the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm generates the variable selection indicators in blocks
(
Chan et al., 2006
;
Papageorgiou, 2018
)
and selects
the MCMC tuning parameters adaptively
(
Roberts & Rosenthal, 2001
)
.

The remainder of this article is arranged as follows. Section
2
describes the model in detail and Section
3

describes the main elements of the MCMC algorithm. All the details of the MCMC algorithm are available in the supplementary material.
In Section
4
we present results from
2
simulation studies. The first one investigates how posteriors,
based on different priors, concentrate around the true covariance and correlation matrices, while the
second one investigates the gains that one may have, in terms of reduced posterior mean squared error (MSE),
when fitting multivariate longitudinal models instead of univariate ones. Additionally, in the second study we examine
the effects that missing data have on the posterior MSE and whether adding more than one response to the model can
counteract these effects. Section
5
applies the methods on a highly unbalanced dataset on cognitive function
and depressive symptomatology, with
4
responses observed over of period of
20
years.
Section
6
concludes the paper with a brief discussion.
All the methods described in this article are freely available in the R package BNSP
(
Papageorgiou, 2020
)
.

2
Multivariate longitudinal response model

Let
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ij}=(Y_{ij1},\dots,Y_{ijp})^{\top}
denote the vector of
p
responses
observed on individual
i,i=1,\dots,n,
at time point
t_{ij}
,
j=1,\dots,n_{i}
. Here, we allow the observational time points
t_{ij}
to be unequally spaced.
We let
T
denote the ordered set of all unique observational times,
T=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{M}\}
, and we denote its cardinality by
M
.
Further, let
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}_{ij}
denote the covariate vector that is observed along with
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ij}
and that may include time,
other time-dependent covariates and time-independent ones.
In addition, let
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}=(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{in_{i}}^{\top})^{\top}
denote the
i
th response vector.
With
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{i}=E(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{i}=\text{cov}(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})
, the overall model takes the form

\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{i},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{i}),i=1,2,\dots,n.

In the following subsections we detail how the means
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{i}
and covariance matrices
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{i}
are modelled semiparametrically in terms of covariates.

2.1
Mean model

The means
\mu_{ijk}=E(Y_{ijk}),k=1,\dots,p,
are modelled utilizing semiparametric regression methods

\mu_{ijk}=\beta_{k0}+\sum_{l=1}^{K_{1}}u_{ijl}\beta_{kl}+\sum_{l=K_{1}+1}^{K}f_{\mu,k,l}(u_{ijl}),

(15)

where
u_{ijl},l=1,\dots,K_{1},
are regressors with parametrically modelled effects,

u_{ijl},l=K_{1}+1,\dots,K,
are regressors with effects that are modelled as unknown functions,
and
K
denotes the total number of effects that enter the
p
mean models.

Unknown functions are represented utilizing basis function expansions

f_{\mu,k,l}(u_{ijl})=\sum_{s=1}^{q_{\mu l}}\beta_{kls}\kappa_{\mu ls}(u_{ijl})=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ijl}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{kl},

(16)

where
q_{\mu l}
is fixed and it represents the maximum number of basis functions that can be used in modelling
f_{\mu,k,l}(.)
. Further,

\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ijl}=(\kappa_{\mu l1}(u_{ijl}),\dots,\kappa_{\mu lq_{\mu l}}(u_{ijl}))^{\top}

is the vector of basis functions and

\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{kl}=(\beta_{kl1},\dots,\beta_{klq_{\mu l}})^{\top}

is the vector of the corresponding coefficients.
Here, the basis functions of choice are the radial basis functions, given by

\kappa_{1}(u)=u,\kappa_{2}(u)=|u-\xi_{1}|^{2}\log\left(|u-\xi_{1}|^{2}\right),\dots,\kappa_{q-1}(u)=|u-\xi_{q-1}|^{2}\log\left(|u-\xi_{q-1}|^{2}\right)
, where

\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{q-1}
are fixed knots.

Now, model (
15
) can be linearised as

\mu_{ijk}=\beta_{k0}+\sum_{l=1}^{K_{1}}u_{ijl}\beta_{kl}+\sum_{l=K_{1}+1}^{K}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ijl}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{kl}=\beta_{k0}+\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ij}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k},=(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ij}^{\ast})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}^{\ast},

(17)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ij}=(u_{ij1},\dots,u_{ijK_{1}},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}_{ij,K_{1}+1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}_{ijK})^{\top}

and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}=(\beta_{k1},\dots,\beta_{kK_{1}},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k,K_{1}+1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{kK}^{\top})^{\top}
.
Further,
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ij}^{\ast}=(1,\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ij}^{\top})^{\top}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}^{\ast}=(\beta_{k0},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}^{\top})^{\top}
.

The implied model for the mean of vector
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ij}
is

\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{ij}=E(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ij})=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}+\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{ij}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{ij}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast},

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}=(\beta_{10},\dots,\beta_{p0})^{\top}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{ij}=\mbox{\boldmath$I$}_{p}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ij}^{\top}
,
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{p}^{\top})^{\top}
.
In the final expression,
\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{ij}^{\ast}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast}
are as
\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{ij}
and
\beta
but with

\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ij}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}
replaced by
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ij}^{\ast}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}^{\ast},k=1,\dots,p
.

Recalling that
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
denotes the
i
th response vector, we may write the mean model as

E(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})=\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{i}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast}
, where

\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{i}^{\ast}=[(\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{i1}^{\ast})^{\top},(\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{i2}^{\ast})^{\top},\ldots,(\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{in_{i}}^{\ast})^{\top}]^{\top}
.
Alternatively, the model can be written in the usual form
E(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$})=\mbox{\boldmath$X$}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast},

where
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$X$}^{\ast}=[(\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{1}^{\ast})^{\top},\dots,(\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{n}^{\ast})^{\top}]^{\top}
.

We note that
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ij}
is a maximal vector covariates and it is common to the
k
responses. By
introducing binary indicators for variable selection, we allow each response to have its own set of covariates.
Vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{k}=(\gamma_{k1},\dots,\gamma_{kK_{1}},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{k,K_{1}+1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{kK}^{\top})^{\top}

has the same length and structure are vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}
, introduced just below (
17
), and its elements are binary indicators
that select which regressors enter the mean model of the
k
th response.
Given
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{k}
, model (
17
) is written as

\displaystyle\mu_{ijk}=\beta_{k0}+\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ijk}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{\gamma_{k}}=(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ijk}^{\ast})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{\gamma_{k}}^{\ast},

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{\gamma_{k}}
consists of all non-zero elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{k}

and
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ijk}
of the corresponding elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ij}
.

Letting
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{p}^{\top})^{\top}
denote the vector of binary indicators and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{\gamma}^{\ast}=((\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{\gamma_{1}}^{\ast})^{\top},\dots,(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{\gamma_{k}}^{\ast})^{\top})^{\top}
the vector of non-zero regression coefficients for the
p
responses,
we can write the regression model for the mean of
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
as

E(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})=\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{\gamma i}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{\gamma}^{\ast}
. Similarly, we can write

E(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$})=\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{\gamma}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{\gamma}^{\ast}
.

2.2
Covariance model

Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{i}
denote the covariance matrix of
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
.
As was discussed in the introduction, to model the elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{i}
in terms of covariates, we beging by considering the
factorization
\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}^{\top}=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}
, where
matrices
\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}
have the form shown in (
1
).
The next two subsections describe semiparametric models for
the generalized autoregressive matrices
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{ijk}:j=2,\dots,n_{i},k<j\}
and the innovation covariance matrices

\{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{ij}:j=1,\dots,n_{i}\}
.

2.3
Generalized autoregressive matrices

For
\phi_{ijklm}
, the
(l,m)
element of
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{ijk}
,
l,m=1,\dots,p
, we consider the following semiparametric model

\phi_{ijklm}=\psi_{lm0}+\sum_{b=1}^{B_{1}}v_{ijkb}\psi_{lmb}+\sum_{b=B_{1}+1}^{B}f_{\phi,l,m,b}(v_{ijkb})=\psi_{lm0}+\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{ijk}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{lm}=(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{\ast}_{ijk})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{lm}^{\ast},

(18)

where the developments in (
18
) follow along the same lines as those for the mean model, detailed in (
15
) - (
17
).
Functions
f_{\phi,l,m,b}(v_{ijkb})=\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{ijkb}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{lmb}
are linearised utilizing a fixed number
q_{\phi b}
of basis functions,
b=B_{1}+1,\dots,B
.
Vector
\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{\ast}_{ijk}
denotes the maximal set covariates that is common to the
p^{2}
autoregressive coefficients.
Further, vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{lm}^{\ast}=(\psi_{lm0},\psi_{lm1},\dots,\psi_{lmB_{1}},\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{lm,B_{1}+1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{lmB}^{\top})^{\top}
consists of the regression coefficients, grouped by the effect they model.
Vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{lm}
is the corresponding vector of binary indicators that selects which coefficients enter the model of the
(l,m)
autoregressive coefficient.
We note that here the intercepts are subject to selection.
Given
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{lk}
, model (
18
) is expressed as

\displaystyle\phi_{ijklm}=(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{\ast}_{ijklm})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{\xi_{lm}}^{\ast},

(19)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{\xi_{lm}}^{\ast}
consists of all non-zero elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{lm}^{\ast}

and
\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{ijklm}^{\ast}
of the corresponding elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{ijk}^{\ast}
.

Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}^{\ast}=((\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{11}^{\ast})^{\top},\dots,(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{1p}^{\ast})^{\top},(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{21}^{\ast})^{\top},\dots,(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{pp}^{\ast})^{\top})^{\top}
be the vector of all regression coefficients, and

\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{11}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{1p}^{\top},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{21}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{pp}^{\top})^{\top}

be the vector of all binary indicators. Further, let
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{\xi}^{\ast}
be the vector of all non-zero coefficients.
Now, from (
12
) and the definition of the prediction error, we have

\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{ijk}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ik}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}[\mbox{\boldmath$I$}_{p}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ik}^{\top}\otimes(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{\ast}_{ijk})^{\top}]_{\xi}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{\xi}^{\ast}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{ij}=\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{ij\xi}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{\xi}^{\ast}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{ij},

(20)

that represents a dynamic linear model, since the design matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{ij\xi}=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}[\mbox{\boldmath$I$}_{p}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ik}^{\top}\otimes(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{\ast}_{ijk})^{\top}]_{\xi}
involves the predecessors of
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ij}
.
Note that, by using
\xi
as subscript in a matrix
[.]_{\xi}
, we mean the matrix with the columns that correspond to the zero elements of
\xi
removed.
When the mean of
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ij}
is not zero, we
replace
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ij}
in (
20
) by its centred version
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ij}-\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{ij}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast}
. This leads to

\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ij}-\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{ij}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast}=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{ijk}(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ik}-\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{ik}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast})+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{ij},

(21)

which can be written in the more familiar form of a multivariate mixed model

\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{ij}=\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{ij}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast}+\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{ij\xi\beta}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{\xi}^{\ast}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{ij},

(22)

where the design matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{ij\xi\beta}
depends on
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast}
.

2.4
Innovation covariance matrices

For modelling the innovation covariance matrices
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{ij},i=1,\dots,n,j=1,\dots,n_{i}
,
we begin by employing the separation strategy of
Barnard et al., 2000
, by which
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{ij}
is decomposed into a diagonal matrix of variances
\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{ij}=\text{diag}(\sigma^{2}_{ij1},\dots,\sigma^{2}_{ijp})
and a correlation matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{ij}
,

\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{ij}=\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{ij}^{1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{ij}\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{ij}^{1/2}.

(23)

The next subsections consider models for the diagonal elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{ij}
and the correlation matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{ij}
.

2.4.1
Diagonal innovation variance matrices

It is easy to model matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{ij}
in terms of covariates as the only requirement on its diagonal elements is that they are nonnegative. It is clear that the following semiparametric model satisfies this requirement

\log\sigma^{2}_{ijk}=\alpha_{k0}+\sum_{l=1}^{L_{1}}w_{ijl}\alpha_{kl}+\sum_{l=L_{1}+1}^{L}f_{\sigma,k,l}(w_{ijl})=\alpha_{k0}+\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{ij}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}.

(24)

Similar models for innovation variances have appeared in
Leng et al., 2010
and
Lin & Pan, 2013
.

Consider now vectors of indicator variables for selecting the elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{ij}
that enter the
k
th variance regression model.
In line with the indicator variables for the mean and autoregressive models, these are denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{k}=(\delta_{k1},\dots,\delta_{kL_{1}},\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{k,L_{1}+1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{kL}^{\top})^{\top}
.
Given
\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{k}
, model (
24
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\log\sigma^{2}_{ijk}=\alpha_{k0}+\mbox{\boldmath$w$}^{\top}_{ijk}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{\delta_{k}},

or equivalently

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{ijk}=\exp(\alpha_{k0})\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$w$}^{\top}_{ijk}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{\delta_{k}})=\sigma^{2}_{k}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$w$}^{\top}_{ijk}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{\delta_{k}}).

(25)

2.4.2
Time-varying innovation correlation matrices

The approach we take for modelling the correlation matrices
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{ij},i=1,\dots,n,j=1,\dots,n_{i}
, extends the work of
Liechty et al., 2004
and
Papageorgiou & Marshall, 2020
who proposed models for correlation matrices in the context of cross-sectional studies. In the context of longitudinal studies, we propose to model these matrices utilizing time as the only covariate. Hence, we use symbols
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}=\{r_{tkl}\},t\in T,
to denote these matrices. Below we describe three priors, termed ‘common correlations’, ‘grouped correlations’ and ‘grouped variables’ priors.

2.4.3
Common correlations

The common correlations model is defined as follows

f(\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}|\mu_{ct},\sigma^{2}_{ct})=\pi(\mu_{ct},\sigma^{2}_{ct})\prod_{k<l}\left\{\exp\{-(g(r_{tkl})-\mu_{ct})^{2}/2\sigma^{2}_{ct}\}J[g(r_{tkl})\rightarrow r_{tkl}]\right\}I[\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}\in\mathcal{C}],

(26)

where
\mathcal{C}
denotes the space of correlation matrices,
I[.]
is the indicator function that ensures that the correlation matrix is pd
and
\pi(.,.)
is the normalizing constant

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(\mu_{ct},\sigma_{ct}^{2})=\int_{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}\in\mathcal{C}}\prod_{k<l}\left\{\exp\{-(g(r_{tkl})-\mu_{ct})^{2}/2\sigma^{2}_{ct}\}J[g(r_{tkl})\rightarrow r_{tkl}]\right\}dr_{tkl}.

A typical choice for
g(r)
is the Fisher’s
z
transformation
g(r)=\log([1+r]/[1-r])/2
that leads to
J[g(r)\rightarrow r]=(1-r)^{-1}(1+r)^{-1}
.

We model the parameters of this distribution using nonparametric regression, with time
t
as the only independent variable

\displaystyle\mu_{ct}=\eta_{0}+f_{\mu}(t)=\eta_{0}+\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\mu t}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$},

(27)

\displaystyle\log\sigma^{2}_{ct}=\omega_{0}+f_{\sigma}(t)=\omega_{0}+\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\sigma t}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}.

(28)

Equivalently, we can write (
28
) as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{ct}=\exp(\omega_{0})\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\sigma t}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})=\sigma^{2}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\sigma t}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}).

If the indicator function were not present in (
26
), then the model described in
(
26
) - (
28
) could be thought of as a semi-parametric regression
model for the mean and the variance of normally distributed responses (see e.g.
Chan et al., 2006
and
Papageorgiou, 2018
).
Let vector
\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{t}
consist of the non-redundant elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t},t\in T
,
and let
\mbox{\boldmath$r$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{t_{1}}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{t_{M}}^{\top})^{\top}
. Subject to the pd constraint, we can express the common correlations model as

\displaystyle g(\mbox{\boldmath$r$})=\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}^{\ast}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$},\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\sigma^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})),

(29)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}^{\ast}=(\eta_{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}^{\top})^{\top}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{\ast}
is a design matrix with rows equal to
\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\mu t}^{\ast}=(1,\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\mu t}^{\top})^{\top}

and
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})
is a diagonal matrix with elements equal to

\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\sigma t}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}/2),t\in T
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\mu t}^{\ast}
and
\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\sigma t}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}/2)

appear in the corresponding matrices
d=p(p-1)/2
times, the number of unique elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}
.

Introducing now vectors of indicators
\nu
and
\varphi
for selecting the elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\mu t}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\sigma t}
that enter the models for
\mu_{ct}
and
\sigma^{2}_{ct}
, respectively, model (
29
) can be written as

\displaystyle g(\mbox{\boldmath$r$})=\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{\ast}_{\nu}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu}^{\ast}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$},\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\sigma^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi})),

(30)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu}^{\ast}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi}
consist of the non-zero elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}^{\ast}
and
\omega
, and

\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{\ast}_{\nu}
consists of the columns of
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{\ast}
that correspond to the non-zero elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}^{\ast}
.

2.4.4
Grouped correlations

The common correlations model is restrictive as it implies that all correlations evolve in the same way over time.
It is important, however, from a theoretical standpoint to estimate the correlation structure correctly.
Hence, here we generalize the model to include multiple surfaces by
considering the following prior

\displaystyle f(\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}|\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{ct},\sigma^{2}_{ct},\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})=\pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{ct},\sigma^{2}_{ct},\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle\times\prod_{k<l}\left\{\sum_{h=1}^{H}I[\lambda_{kl}=h]\exp[-(g(r_{tkl})-\mu_{cth})^{2}/2\sigma^{2}_{ct}]\right\}J[g(r_{tkl})\rightarrow r_{tkl}]I[\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}\in\mathcal{C}],

(31)

where
H
denotes the number of surfaces in the model,
\lambda_{kl}
is the surface assignment indicator and

\displaystyle\mu_{cth}=\eta_{h0}+f_{\mu h}(t)=\eta_{h0}+\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\mu th}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu_{h}}=(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\mu th}^{\ast})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu_{h}}^{\ast}.

In the above,
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{h}
are surface specific regression coefficients and
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{h}
are surface specific variable selection indicators,
h=1,\dots,H
.

Let vector
\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{th}
consist of the non-redundant elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t},t\in T
, assigned to surface
h
.
Further, let
\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{h}=(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{t_{1}h}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{t_{M}h}^{\top})^{\top}
. Subject to the pd constraint, we can express model (
2.4.4
) as

\displaystyle g(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{h})=\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{\ast}_{\nu_{h}}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu_{h}}^{\ast}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{h},h=1,\dots,H,

(32)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{h}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\sigma^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{2}_{h}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi}))
.

2.4.5
Grouped variables

Here we describe another clustering model that, instead of correlations, clusters the variables.
We have the following prior

\displaystyle f(\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}|\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{ct},\sigma^{2}_{ct},\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})=\pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{ct},\sigma^{2}_{ct},\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle\times\prod_{k<l}\left\{\sum_{h_{1},h_{2}=1}^{G}I[\lambda_{k}=h_{1}]I[\lambda_{l}=h_{2}]\exp[-(g(r_{tkl})-\mu_{cth_{1}h_{2}})^{2}/2\sigma^{2}_{ct}]\right\}J[g(r_{tkl})\rightarrow r_{tkl}]I[\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}\in\mathcal{C}],

where

\displaystyle\mu_{cth_{1}h_{2}}=\eta_{h_{1}h_{2}0}+f_{\mu h_{1}h_{2}}(t)=\eta_{h_{1}h_{2}0}+\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\mu th_{1}h_{2}}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu_{h_{1}h_{2}}}=(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\mu th_{1}h_{2}}^{\ast})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu_{h_{1}h_{2}}}^{\ast},

and
G
is the number of groups in which the variables are distributed, creating
H=G(G+1)/2
clusters
for the correlations.

2.5
Prior specification

In this section we specify the priors for the model parameters.
We begin by describing the priors of the parameters of the mean model.
For
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{\gamma}^{\ast}
we specify a g-prior
(
Zellner, 1986
)
,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{\gamma}^{\ast}|c_{\beta},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},c_{\beta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma})^{-1}),

(33)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}^{\ast}_{\gamma}
, and
\Sigma
is the covariance matrix of the
Y
, that is
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=\text{diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{i},i=1,\dots,n)
.
Further, the prior for
c_{\beta}
is taken to be an inverse Gamma,
c_{\beta}\sim\text{IG}(a_{\beta},b_{\beta})
.

Consider now the vectors
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{k}=(\gamma_{k1},\dots,\gamma_{kK_{1}},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{k,K_{1}+1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{kK}^{\top})^{\top},k=1,\dots,p,
of
variable selection indicators for the mean functions. We specify independent binomial priors for each of their
K
subvectors,

\displaystyle P(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{kl}|\pi_{\mu kl})=\pi_{\mu kl}^{N(\gamma_{kl})}(1-\pi_{\mu kl})^{q_{\mu l}-N(\gamma_{kl})},l=1,\dots,K,

(34)

where, for parametric effects,
N(\gamma_{kl})=\gamma_{kl}
and
q_{\mu l}=1
,
l=1,\dots,K_{1}
,
while for nonparametric effects,
N(\gamma_{kl})=\sum_{s=1}^{q_{\mu l}}\gamma_{kls}

and
q_{\mu l}
was defined in (
16
),
l=K_{1}+1,\dots,K.
Independent Beta priors are specified for

\pi_{\mu kl}\sim\text{Beta}(c_{\mu kl},d_{\mu kl})
,
k=1,\dots,p,l=1,\dots,K
.

Moving on to the priors of the parameter of the autoregressive coefficients, a normal prior is specified for the non-zero coefficients
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{\xi lm}^{\ast},l,m=1,\dots,p,
of the model in (
19
)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{\xi lm}^{\ast}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},c_{\psi lm}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$I$}).

For the scale parameter
c_{\psi lm}^{2}
, we consider inverse Gamma and half-normal priors,
c_{\psi lm}^{2}\sim\text{IG}(a_{\psi lm},b_{\psi lm})
and

c_{\psi lm}\sim N(0,\phi^{2}_{\psi lm})I[c_{\psi lm}>0]
.
Further, the specification of the priors for the vectors of variable selection indicators
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{lm}=(\xi_{lm0},\xi_{lm1},\dots,\xi_{lmB_{1}},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{lm,B_{1}+1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{lmB}^{\top})^{\top},l,m=1,\dots,p,

follows the same pattern as for
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{k}
indicators. That is, independent binomial priors are specified for each of the
1+B
subvectors
of
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{lm}
,

\displaystyle P(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{lmb}|\pi_{\phi lmb})=\pi_{\phi lmb}^{N(\xi_{lmb})}(1-\pi_{\phi lmb})^{q_{\phi b}-N(\xi_{lmb})},b=0,\dots,B,

where
N(\xi_{lmb})=\xi_{lmb}
and
q_{\phi b}=1
for
b=0,\dots,B_{1},

and
N(\xi_{lmb})=\sum_{s=1}^{q_{\phi b}}\xi_{lmbs}

and
q_{\phi b}
was defined after (
18
) for
b=B_{1}+1,\dots,B.

Independent Beta priors are specified for

\pi_{\phi lmb}\sim\text{Beta}(c_{\phi lmb},d_{\phi lmb}),l,m=1,\dots,p.

Next, we describe the priors for the innovation variance parameters. First, for
\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{\delta_{k}}
we specify independent normal priors

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{\delta_{k}}|c_{\alpha k},\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{k}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},c_{\alpha k}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$I$}),k=1,\dots,p.

Further, we consider inverse Gamma and half-normal priors for
c_{\alpha k}
,

c_{\alpha k}^{2}\sim\text{IG}(a_{\alpha k},b_{\alpha k})
and

c_{\alpha k}\sim N(0,\phi^{2}_{\alpha k})I[c_{\alpha k}>0],k=1,\dots,p.

For the scale parameter
\sigma^{2}_{k}
in (
25
),
k=1,\dots,p,
we also consider inverse Gamma and half-normal priors,

\sigma^{2}_{k}\sim\text{IG}(a_{\sigma k},b_{\sigma k})
and

\sigma_{k}\sim N(0,\phi^{2}_{\sigma k})I[\sigma_{k}>0]
.
Continuing with the priors for the vectors of indicators

\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{k}=(\delta_{k1},\dots,\delta_{kL_{1}},\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{k,L_{1}+1}^{\top},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{kL}^{\top})^{\top}
,
k=1,\dots,p,
we specify
independent binomial priors for each of their
L
subvectors,

\displaystyle P(\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{kl}|\pi_{\sigma kl})=\pi_{\sigma kl}^{N(\delta_{kl})}(1-\pi_{\sigma kl})^{q_{\sigma l}-N(\delta_{kl})},l=1,\dots,L,

where
N(\delta_{kl})
and
q_{\sigma l}
are analogous to
N(\gamma_{kl})
and
q_{\mu l}
(and
N(\xi_{lmb})
and
q_{\phi b}
).
We specify independent Beta priors for

\pi_{\sigma kl}\sim\text{Beta}(c_{\sigma kl},d_{\sigma kl}),k=1,\dots,p,l=1,\dots,L.

Lastly, we describe the priors placed on the parameters of the models of the innovation correlation matrices. Starting with the ‘common correlations model’ in (
30
), let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{\ast}
.
The prior for the non-zero part of
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}^{\ast}
is the following g-prior

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu}^{\ast}|c_{\eta},\sigma^{2},\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$},\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},c_{\eta}\sigma^{2}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu})^{-1}).

(35)

Further, the prior for
c_{\eta}
is specified as
c_{\eta}\sim\text{IG}(a_{\eta},b_{\eta}).

Furthermore, we specify a binomial prior for vector
\nu

\displaystyle P(\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}|\pi_{\nu})=\pi_{\nu}^{N(\nu)}(1-\pi_{\nu})^{q_{R\mu}-N(\nu)},

where
q_{R\mu}
denotes the number of basis functions used in linearising
f_{\mu}(t)
in (
27
), and
N(\nu)=\sum_{j=1}^{q_{R\mu}}\nu_{j}
.
A Beta prior is specified for
\pi_{\nu}\sim\text{Beta}(c_{\nu},d_{\nu})
.

Further, the prior for
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi}
is specified as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi}|c_{\omega},\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},c_{\omega}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$I$}).

For
c_{\omega}
we consider inverse Gamma and half-normal priors,

c_{\omega}^{2}\sim\text{IG}(a_{\omega},b_{\omega})
and
c_{\omega}\sim N(0,\phi^{2}_{c_{\omega}})I[c_{\omega}>0].

Continuing with the prior for vector
\varphi
, this is taken to be

\displaystyle P(\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}|\pi_{\varphi})=\pi_{\varphi}^{N(\varphi)}(1-\pi_{\varphi})^{q_{R\sigma}-N(\varphi)},

where
q_{R\sigma}
denotes the number of basis functions used in linearising
f_{\sigma}(t)
in (
28
) and
N(\varphi)=\sum_{j=1}^{q_{R\sigma}}\varphi_{j}
.
We specify a Beta prior for

\pi_{\varphi}\sim\text{Beta}(c_{\varphi},d_{\varphi})
.
Further, we specify inverse Gamma and half-normal priors for
\sigma^{2}
,

\sigma^{2}\sim\text{IG}(a_{\sigma},b_{\sigma})
and
\sigma\sim N(\sigma;0,\phi^{2}_{\sigma})I[\sigma>0].

Next we describe the priors for the cluster specific parameters
(\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{h},\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{h})

and prior weights
w_{h},h=1,\dots,H,
of the grouped correlations model.
Firstly, conditionally on
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{h}
, the non-zero elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{h}

are specified to have the following prior

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu_{h}}|c_{\eta},\sigma^{2},\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$},\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},c_{\eta}\sigma^{2}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu_{h}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu_{h}})^{-1}),

if the cluster is non-empty,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu_{h}}|c_{\eta},\sigma^{2},\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$},\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},c_{\eta}\sigma^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$I$}),

\displaystyle\text{if the cluster is empty}.

(36)

Secondly, for vectors
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{h}
we specify binomial priors

\displaystyle P(\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{h}|\pi_{\nu_{h}})=\pi_{\nu_{h}}^{N(\nu_{h})}(1-\pi_{\nu_{h}})^{q_{R\mu}-N(\nu_{h})},

with the prior on
\pi_{\nu_{h}}
being
\pi_{\nu_{h}}\sim\text{Beta}(c_{\nu},d_{\nu}),h=1,\dots,H.

Lastly, the prior weights
w_{h}
are constructed utilizing the so called stick-breaking process
(
Ferguson, 1973
;
Sethuraman, 1994
)
.
Let
v_{h},h=1,\dots,H-1,
be independent draws from a
\text{Beta}(1,\alpha^{*})
distribution. We have:

w_{1}=v_{1}
, for
2\leq l<H
,
w_{l}=v_{l}\prod_{h=1}^{l-1}(1-v_{h})
, and

w_{H}=\prod_{h=1}^{H-1}(1-v_{h})
. We take the concentration parameter
\alpha^{*}
to be unknown
and we assign to it a gamma prior
\alpha^{*}\sim\text{Gamma}(a_{\alpha*},b_{\alpha*})
with mean
a_{\alpha*}/b_{\alpha*}
.

In the numerical illustrations that we present in this article, we use the following priors. For
c_{\beta}
we specify

\text{IG}(1/2,np/2),
as a
p
-variate analogue to the prior of
Liang et al., 2008
. For
\pi_{\mu kl},\pi_{\phi lmb},\pi_{\sigma kl},\pi_{\nu},
and
\pi_{\varphi}
we specify Beta
(1,1)
priors.
The priors on
c_{\alpha k},k=1,\dots,p,
and
c_{\omega}
are specified to be IG
(1.1,1.1)
. Further, for
c_{\psi lm},l,m=1,\dots,p
,
\sigma_{k},k=1,\dots,p
, and
\sigma
we define HN
(2)
priors.
Furthermore, for
c_{\eta},
we specify
\text{IG}(1/2,Md/2)
, again, as an analogue to the prior of
Liang et al., 2008
.
Lastly, the prior on the DP concentration parameter is specified as
\alpha^{\ast}\sim\text{Gamma}(5,2)
.

3
Posterior Sampling

Posterior sampling is carried out by expressing the likelihood function in different ways and using the expression that is more computationally convenient for each step of the MCMC algorithm. Further, where possible, we integrate out parameters to improve mixing, and where beneficial, we augment the model with additional parameters to make sampling easier. Here we only present the main concepts and a detailed description of all MCMC steps is available in the supplementary material.

We first consider the likelihood that is obtained from the normality assumption,
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{\gamma i}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast}_{\gamma},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{i}),i=1,\dots,n
.
Due to the factorization in (
1
), the quadratic form
Q=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{\gamma i}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast}_{\gamma})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{i}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{\gamma i}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast}_{\gamma})
of the likelihood may be written as

\displaystyle Q=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{i})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{ij}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{ij})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{ij}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{ij}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{ij}),

(37)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{i}^{\ast}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast}
.

Further, recalling (
21
) and the definition of prediction error, and the separation of variances and correlations in (
23
),
Q
may be written as

\displaystyle Q=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{ij}^{\top}[\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{ij}^{1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t_{j}}\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{ij}^{1/2}]^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{ij}=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{i\in O_{t}}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0pt\epsilon}}_{it}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}^{-1}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0pt\epsilon}}_{it},

(38)

where
\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0pt\epsilon}}_{ij}=\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{ij}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{ij}
. In the first equality, matrix
R
is subscripted by
t_{j}
because here we model its elements in terms of covariate ‘time’

only. In the second equality,
T
denotes the set of unique observational time points and
O_{t}
the set of sampling units observed at time point
t
. We denote the cardinality of
O_{t}
by
n_{t}
.

In addition, recalling (
20
) and its centred versions in (
21
) and (

), we can write (

) as

\displaystyle Q=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{ij}-\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{ij\xi\beta}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{\xi}^{\ast})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{ij}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{ij}-\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{ij\xi\beta}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{\xi}^{\ast}),

(39)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{ij\xi\beta}=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}[\mbox{\boldmath$I$}_{p}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{ik}^{\top}\otimes(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{\ast}_{ijk})^{\top}]_{\xi}
.
We take
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{ij\xi\beta}
to be a matrix of zeros when
j=1
.

To improve mixing of the MCMC algorithm, we can integrate out vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\ast}_{\gamma}
from the likelihood of
Y

\displaystyle f(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$})=(2\pi)^{-\frac{Np}{2}}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{n}|\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{i}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big)(c_{\beta}+1)^{-\frac{N(\gamma)+p}{2}}\exp(-S/2),

(40)

where

S=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptY}}^{\top}\big\{I-\frac{c_{\beta}}{1+c_{\beta}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma})^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma}^{\top}\big\}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptY}},

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptY}}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}
,

N=\sum_{i=1}^{n}n_{i}
,

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}^{\ast}_{\gamma}
and

N(\gamma)+p
is the total number of columns in
\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{\gamma}^{\ast}
.

To write function
S
in a computationally convenient way, first note that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptY}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptY}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{i}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
,
which, due to (
1
), may be written as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
.
Further, due to the separation of variances and correlations in (
23
), we may further express the latter as

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{i}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{i}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{i}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
=

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0ptY}}_{i}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{i}^{-1}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0ptY}}_{i}
, where
\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0ptY}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{i}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}
.
Furthermore, we write the last expression as

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0pty}}_{ij}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t_{j}}^{-1}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0pty}}_{ij}
.
Likewise, we may write that

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{\gamma i}^{\ast})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{\gamma i}^{\ast}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{i}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{\gamma i}^{\ast})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{i}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{i}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{\gamma i}^{\ast}=

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}(\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0ptX}}_{\gamma ij}^{\ast})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t_{j}}^{-1}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0ptX}}_{\gamma ij}^{\ast}
.
In addition,

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptY}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{\gamma i}^{\ast})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$L$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}(\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0ptX}}_{\gamma ij}^{\ast})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t_{j}}^{-1}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0pty}}_{ij}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0pty}}_{ij}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t_{j}}^{-1}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0pty}}_{ij}-

\displaystyle c_{\beta}(1+c_{\beta})^{-1}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}(\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0pty}}_{ij})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t_{j}}^{-1}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0ptX}}_{\gamma ij}^{\ast}][\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}(\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0ptX}}_{\gamma ij}^{\ast})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t_{j}}^{-1}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0ptX}}_{\gamma ij}^{\ast}]^{-1}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}(\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0ptX}}_{\gamma ij}^{\ast})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t_{j}}^{-1}\check{\boldsymbol{\hskip 0.0pty}}_{ij}].

The most computationally expensive step of the MCMC algorithm is the one that samples from the posterior of the parameters of the correlation matrices. This is because acceptance probabilities of the Metropolis-Hastings step involve the ratio of the normalizing constants of the density in
(
26
) (or the ones in (
2.4.4
) and (
2.4.5
)). Calculating this ratio is very computationally demanding, but it
can be avoided by introducing the ‘shadow prior’
(
Liechty et al., 2004
)
.
The basic idea is to introduce latent variables
\theta_{tkl}
between the correlations
r_{tkl}
and the means
\mu_{ct}
. This modifies prior (
26
) as follows

f(\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{t},\tau^{2})=\pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{t},\tau^{2})\prod_{k<l}\exp[-(g(r_{tkl})-\theta_{tkl})^{2}/2\tau^{2}]J[g(r_{tkl})\rightarrow r_{tkl}]I[\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}\in\mathcal{C}],

(41)

where

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{t},\tau^{2})=\int_{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{t}\in\mathcal{C}}\prod_{k<l}\exp[-(g(r_{tkl})-\theta_{tkl})^{2}/2\tau^{2}]J[g(r_{tkl})\rightarrow r_{tkl}]dr_{tkl},

and
\tau
is a fixed, small constant. Further, variables
\theta_{tkl}
are independently distributed with time specific means and variances,

\theta_{tkl}\sim N(\mu_{ct},\sigma^{2}_{ct})
. Hence, the semiparametric model (
30
) now applies on the unconstrained
\theta
,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{\ast}_{\nu}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu}^{\ast}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$},\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\sim N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\sigma^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi})).

(42)

Sampling from the posterior of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{t}=\{\theta_{tkl}\}
still involves the ratio of the normalising constants of the density in (
41
), but that, as was argued by
Liechty et al., 2004
, for small
\tau
, can reasonably be approximated by one.

Considering now the ‘grouped correlations’ model, with the introduction of the shadow prior, model (
2.4.4
) becomes the same as the one in (
41
). The difference is that here variables
\theta_{tkl}
are independent with conditional distribution

\theta_{tkl}|\lambda_{kl}=h\sim N(\mu_{cth},\sigma^{2}_{ct})
. Further, letting
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{h}
be analogous to
\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{h}
, defined above (
32
),
it is easy to see that semiparametric model (
32
) now applies on the unconstrained
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{h}

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{h}=\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{\ast}_{\nu_{h}}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu_{h}}^{*}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{h},h=1,\dots,H.

(43)

Additionally, letting
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{H})^{\top}
, model (
43
) may be written in vectorized form in precisely the same way as (
42
).

To improve mixing of the MCMC algorithm over the parameters of the models of the correlation matrices, we can integrate out vector
\eta
from the likelihood of
\theta
. The marginal of
\theta
, computed from (
42
) and (
35
), is

\displaystyle f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$},c_{\eta},\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$},\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$},\sigma^{2})\propto|\sigma^{2}D^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi})|^{-\frac{1}{2}}(c_{\eta}+1)^{-\frac{N(\nu)+1}{2}}\exp\{-S^{\ast}/2\sigma^{2}\},

(44)

where
S^{\ast}=S^{\ast}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$},c_{\eta},\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$},\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$})=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\theta}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\theta}}-\frac{c_{\eta}}{1+c_{\eta}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\theta}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu})^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\theta}}
,
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\theta}}=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi})\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}
.

Lastly, under the grouped correlations model, the marginal likelihood of
\theta
is given by

\displaystyle f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$},c_{\eta},\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$},\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$},\sigma^{2})\propto|\sigma^{2}D^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi})|^{-\frac{1}{2}}(c_{\eta}+1)^{-\frac{N(\nu)+H}{2}}\exp\{-S^{\ast}/2\sigma^{2}\},

where
N(\nu)=\sum_{h}N(\nu_{h})
, and

S^{\ast}=S^{\ast}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$},\mbox{\boldmath$c$}_{\eta},\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$},\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$})=\sum_{h}S^{\ast}_{h}=\sum_{h}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\theta}}^{\top}_{h}\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}-\frac{c_{\eta}}{1+c_{\eta}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu_{h}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu_{h}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu_{h}})^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu_{h}}^{\top}\right\}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\theta}}_{h}
.

4
Simulation Studies

We present results from two simulation studies. The first one examines how posteriors, based on different priors, concentrate around the true covariance and
correlation matrices, while the second one investigates the gains that one may have, in terms of reduced posterior mean squared error (MSE), when fitting multivariate longitudinal models instead of univariate ones. Additionally, in the second study we examine the effects that missing data have on the posterior MSE and whether adding more than one response to the model can counteract these effects.

4.1
Effects of priors on the posteriors of matrices

We consider a trivariate system of responses, observed over

4.2
Gains

To investigate the potential gains associated with fitting multivariate longitudinal models and the effects of missing data on posterior distributions, we consider a data-generating mechanism that consists of
3
responses,
Y_{1t},Y_{2t},Y_{3t},
observed over
M=6
time points,
t=-0.5,-0.3,\dots,0.5
. The
3
responses are generated from a multivariate normal distribution with
mean
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{t}=(\beta_{10}+\beta_{11}t,0,0)^{\top}
, that is, the mean of the first response is a linear function of
t
,
while the other
2
responses have constant zero mean. Further, the
18\times 18
covariance matrix is taken to be

\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{1}(\rho_{1})\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{23}(\rho_{2})
,
where
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{1}(\rho_{1})=\{\rho_{1}^{|k-l|}\}
is the marginal covariance matrix of
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{1}
and

\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{23}(\rho_{2})
has diagonal elements equal to
1
and all off diagonal elements equal to
\rho_{2}
.
The choice of autoregressive covariance structure
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{1}
is a standard one. It implies that correlations between observations on
Y_{1}
at different time points decrease exponentially fast as the lag increases. The value of
\rho_{1}
is not of particular interest here, and hence it is fixed to
\rho_{1}=0.5
, implying that correlations get halved every time the lag increases by
0.2
. The structure of
\Sigma
indicates that
Y_{1}
is equally correlated with
Y_{2}
and
Y_{3}
at all time points. This correlation is equal to
\rho_{2}
at lag
0
and it gets halved when the lag increases by
0.2
.

The chosen mean and covariance structures are fairly simple, and this suffices for the purposes of the current study.
The main interest here is on the quality of the estimate of the mean function of the first response.
We examine how this quality, as measured by the posterior MSE, or it’s components bias and variance, depends on the dimension of the response in the fitted model, the value of the correlation coefficient
\rho_{2}
, the sample size
n
, and the percentage of missing data. The effect of the dimension of the response is evaluated by fitting
1
-,
2
-, and
3
-dimensional response models. The effect of the correlation coefficient is examined by letting
\rho_{2}
take values in the set
\{0.2,0.4,0.6,0.8\}
.
The effect of the sample size is assessed by letting
n
take values in the set
\{100,300\}
. Lastly, the effect of missing data is investigated by allowing
0\%
,
25\%
and
50\%
chance of missing observations, utilizing a missing completely at random mechanism.

Considering a
3
-dimensional response model as an example, the mean functions are modelled using
\mu_{tk}=\beta_{k0}+\beta_{k1}t
,

k=1,2,3
. This specification is correct for the first response and wrong for the other two responses.
Further, the autoregressive coefficients and innovation variances are modelled using

\phi_{jklm}=\phi_{lm0}+f_{\phi,l,m}(t_{j}-t_{k})
and
\log\sigma^{2}_{tk}=\alpha_{k0}+f_{\sigma,k}(t)
,
where
f_{\phi,l,m}(t_{j}-t_{k})
and
f_{\sigma,k}(t)
include
5
and
6
knots, respectively, allowing for the possibility of non-linear effects.
Lastly, we fit the common correlations model (
26
) with constant mean,
\mu_{ct}=\eta_{0},
and constant variance,
\log\sigma^{2}_{ct}=\omega_{0}
.
These choices for
\mu_{ct}
and
\log\sigma^{2}_{ct}
were arrived at after experimentation with more complex models that showed
the more complex models to not be necessary.

The regression coefficients are taken to be
\beta_{10}=0
and
\beta_{11}=2.95
, where the value of
\beta_{11}
was so chosen to achieve a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) equal to
1
. The SNR is defined as
\text{SNR}=(\text{SST}-\text{SSE})/\text{SSE}
, where SST the total sum of squares SST=
\sum_{i,j}(y_{ij1}-\bar{y}_{1})^{2}
and SSE the error sum of squares SSE=
\sum_{i,j}(y_{ij1}-\hat{y}_{ij1})^{2}
. Predictions
\hat{y}_{ij1}
were obtained by fitting the true data-generating model.

For all models we obtain
3\times 10^{4}
posterior samples of which we discard the first
10^{4}
as burn in, and of the remaining
2\times 10^{4}
we keep one in two. Based on the
10^{4}
retained samples, we obtain samples for the parameter of main interest,

\mu_{t1}=E(Y_{t1}|t)=\beta_{10}+\beta_{11}t
, by replacing the regression coefficients
\beta_{10}
and
\beta_{11}
by the corresponding sampled values,
\mu_{t1}^{(s)}=\beta_{10}^{(s)}+\beta_{11}^{(s)}t,s=1,\dots,10^{4}
.
We compare fitted models in terms of their posterior bias and variance in estimating
\mu_{t1}
.
Using subscript
p
to denote quantities obtained from models with fitted response dimension
p=1,2,3
,
the posterior bias and variance are computed as

B_{p}(t)=\sum_{s}(\mu_{t1,p}^{(s)}-\bar{\mu}_{t1,p})^{2}
and
V_{p}(t)=(\bar{\mu}_{t1,p}-\mu_{t1})^{2}
,
where
\bar{\mu}_{t1,p}
is the mean of
\mu_{t1,p}^{(s)},s=1,\dots,10^{4}
.
Further, because
B_{p}(t)
and
V_{p}(t)
are computed for
t=-0.5,-0.3,\dots,0.5
, results are summarised by computing sums:

B_{p}=\sum_{t}B_{p}(t)
and
V_{p}=\sum_{t}V_{p}(t)
. Results presented below are based on
30
replicate datasets.

Table
2
compares model performance by reporting the ratio
100B_{p}/B_{1}
,
that we refer to as relative bias, while Table
3
compares model performance
by reporting the ratio
100V_{p}/V_{1}

p=2,3
.
We can see from Table

that relative bias decreases as the correlation
\rho_{2}
between
the responses increases, for all
n
,
p
, and missingness probabilities.
Comparing the left half of the Table, that refers to
2
-dimensional response models, to the right half, that refers to

3

When it is
0\%
,
2
- and
3
-dimensional models fair almost equally, but as the missingness probability
increases, the bias of the
3

2

Table

displays the results on relative variances. We can observe that
as correlations between the responses increase, the relative variance decreases, for all
n

p
, and missingness probabilities.
The relative variance for
3

2
-dimensional models, for
all missingness probabilities. These results are very much in line with what one would expect given the results of
Zellner, 1962
.

Application

The dataset that we analyse here is a random subsample of
n=500
subjects from the Paquid prospective cohort study
(
Letenneur et al., 1994
)

and it is available in the R package
lcmm

(
Proust-Lima et al., 2020
)
. The Paquid study aimed at investigating cerebral and functional aging in individuals aged 65 years and older, and it involved individuals living in south-western France. Longitudinal observations over a maximum period of
20
years were collected on
3
cognitive tests and depressive symptomatology. The cognitive tests evaluated the global mental status using the Mini Mental State Examination (
y_{1}
), verbal fluency using the Isaacs Set Test (
y_{2}
), and visual memory using the Benton’s Visual Retention Test (
y_{3}
). Depressive symptomatology was measured by the Center for Epidemiological Study Depression scale (
y_{4}
).
We note that in the first
3
responses higher scores indicate better cognitive function performance, while in the fourth response, higher scores indicate more symptoms.
The available covariates are a binary gender indicator,
x_{1}=1
for males and
x_{1}=0
for females, a binary indicator of educational level,
x_{2}=1
for individuals who graduated from primary school and
x_{2}=0
otherwise, age at entry in the cohort
x_{3}
, and time of observation
t
.

Because the distribution of
y_{1}
is very asymmetric, we analyse a normalized version of it
(
Philipps et al., 2014
)
.
Furthermore, to avoid computational problems, we scale the time of observation,
t=0,1,2,\dots,20
, by dividing by
20
,
hence,
t=0,0.05,0.1,\dots,1
.
Further, we centre and scale the age at entry
x_{3}
by subtracting the minimum age and dividing by
15
.
Lastly, we note that the dataset is highly unbalanced,
with individuals observed at no more than
9
time points of the possible
21
.
The number of individuals observed at each time point decreases quickly:
there are about
300
observations at
t=0.1
, about
200
at
t=0.2
,
about
150
between
t=0.3
and
t=0.4
, and thereafter the number of observations
stays well below
100
, with only
18
individuals observed at the last time point.

We model the mean using

\mu_{ijk}=\beta_{k0}+\beta_{k1}x_{i1}+\beta_{k2}x_{i2}+f_{\mu,k,3}(x_{3i})+f_{\mu,k,4}(t_{ij}),k=1,\dots,4,

(48)

where
f_{\mu,k,3}(x_{3i})
and
f_{\mu,k,4}(t_{ij})
are modelled using up to
10
and
8
knots,
equivalently
11
and
9
basis functions, respectively.
These knots are chosen as the unique quantiles of
x_{3}

and
t
that correspond to the
10
equally spaced probabilities between
0
and
1
. Although for
x_{3}

there are
10
unique quantiles, for
t
there are only
8
, hence the difference in the number of knots for modelling these 2 functions.
Thus, for modelling the means there are in total
92
parameters, of which
88
are subject to selection.

Further, the
84\times 84
covariance matrix includes
3570
unique elements.
These are modelled using
4
regression models that we describe next. First,
the autoregressive coefficients are modelled using

\phi_{ijklm}=\psi_{lm0}+f_{\phi,l,m}(t_{ij}-t_{ik}),l,m=1,\dots,4,

(49)

where
f_{\phi,l,m}(t_{ij}-t_{ik})
is modelled using up to
10
knots. These are selected as the unique quantiles of
lag,
t_{ij}-t_{ik}
, that correspond to the
10
equally spaced probabilities between
0
and
1
.
Thus, there are up to
192
parameters for modelling the autoregressive coefficients and all of these are subject to selection.

The innovation variances are modelled as

\log\sigma^{2}_{ijk}=\alpha_{k0}+f_{\sigma,k,1}(x_{3i})+f_{\sigma,k,2}(t_{ij}),k=1,\dots,4,

(50)

where
f_{\sigma,k,1}(x_{3i})
and
f_{\sigma,k,2}(t_{ij})
are modelled using up to
10
and
8
knots, just as was done for the mean functions.
Hence, there are up to
84
parameters for modelling the innovation variances, of which
4
are always in the model, while
the other
80
are subject to selection.

Lastly, we describe the model for the correlation matrices. Since the
4
responses can be naturally clustered into the group of
3
variables
that measure cognitive function and the group of
1
variable that measures depressive symptomatology, a grouped variables model is a priori justifiable. Despite that, we prefer the less structured grouped correlations model, and let the data decide if a grouped variables model is preferable.
The parameters of the correlation matrices are modelled using

\mu_{cth}=\eta_{h0}+f_{\mu h}(t_{ij})
and

\log\sigma^{2}_{ct}=\omega_{0}+f_{\sigma}(t_{ij})

As the
4\times 4
correlation matrices have
6
unique elements, we allow up to
H=6
different trajectories for the means
\mu_{cth},h=1,\dots,6
.
Functions
f_{\mu h}(t_{ij})
and
f_{\sigma}(t_{ij})
are modelled using up to
10
knots.
These are selected as the unique quantiles of
t
that correspond to the
10
equally spaced probabilities between
0
and
1
. Although for modelling innovation variances there are
8
unique quantiles, here there are
10
, as there is a single correlation matrix observed at each time point.
Hence, there are up to
72
parameters for modelling
\mu_{cth}
, of which
66
are subject to selection.
For modelling
\log\sigma^{2}_{ct}
there are
12
parameters, of which
11
are subject to selection.
This brings the number of parameters for modelling the covariance matrix to
360
,
10
times smaller
than the number of unique elements in the covariance matrix.

Results presented below are based on
25\times 10^{3}
posterior samples that are obtained from
10^{5}
iterations of the MCMC sampler, with the first
5\times 10^{4}

discarded as burn-in, and of the remaining keeping every second sample.

Concerning the mean regression models, results in the form of posterior means and
80\%
credible intervals are presented in the Figure of the supplementary material.
Covariate
x_{1}
(gender) has little to no effect on the means of three cognitive function responses but it has an important effect
on the mean of depressive symptomatology, indicating that males have, on average, less symptoms than females.
Further, covariate
x_{2}
(education) has important effects on the means of the responses relating to cognitive function,
with those who graduated from primary school having higher mean cognitive function scores.
In addition,
x_{2}
appears to have no effect on mean depressive symptomatology.
Covariates
x_{3}
(age) and
t
(time) have negative linear (or almost linear) associations with the means of the
3
responses that relate to cognitive function.
Furthermore,
x_{3}
has a more complex than linear relationship with mean depressive symptomatology, but, due to the high
uncertainty, this relationship could also be seen as flat.
Lastly,
t
has positive linear association with the mean of fourth response, indicating that the average depressive
symptomatology increases with time.
Visually, the covariates have simple relationships with the mean responses. This can also be confirmed by the small number
of regression coefficients that are selected to fit the mean functions: of the
88
, on average,
16.1
or
18.3\%
were selected during MCMC sampling.

Results on the autoregressive coefficient regression models are shown in Figure
4
.
Recalling the discussion around (
12
), the fitted models presented in the diagonal of Figure
4
correspond to
the coefficients for predicting responses based on passed observations on the same response. These 4 curves have similar shapes: they start from around
0.4
to
0.5

and they decrease towards
0
. Based on univariate data analyses,
Pan & MacKenzie, 2006
and
Papageorgiou, 2012

reported similar curves.
The remaining plots include a variety of fitted curves: complex and clearly important (such as
\phi_{jk12}
and
\phi_{jk13}
),
complex but not very important (such as
\phi_{jk14}
and
\phi_{jk23}
)
and flat at 0 (such as
\phi_{jk34}
and
\phi_{jk41}
).
For fitting these curves, of the
192
regression coefficients, on average,
62.6
or
32.6\%
were selected during MCMC sampling.

Results relating to innovation standard deviations are displayed in Figure
5
. The first row shows almost flat fitted curves

\exp(f_{\sigma,1,1}(x_{3})/2)
and
\exp(f_{\sigma,2,1}(x_{3})/2)
, and more complex fitted curves

\exp(f_{\sigma,3,1}(x_{3})/2)
and
\exp(f_{\sigma,4,1}(x_{3})/2)
. The four fitted curves

\exp(f_{\sigma,k,2}(t)/2)

increases and they

increases.
For fitting these curves, of the
80
regression coefficients, on average,
29.1
or
36.4\%
were selected during MCMC sampling.

Figure
6
shows the fitted models of the parameters of the innovation correlation matrices.
The first plot shows the clustering structure that the MCMC sampler visited most often:
for
94.5\%
of the MCMC samples, two clusters were formed, the first being
\{(1,2),(1,3),(2,3)\}
(denoted by the darker colour on the plot)
while the second being

While this clustering was obtained by a ‘grouped correlations’ model, it is also compatible with the
‘grouped variables’ model, the first cluster consisting of variables

(the results on the cognitive tests) and the second consisting of
variable
4
(the test result on depressive symptomatology). The other 2 plots in the Figure show the fitted curves for
\mu_{cth},h=1,2
,
and
\sigma_{ct}
. The fitted curve
\mu_{t1}
starts at around
0.3
, decreases below
0.2
and then increases to just below
0.4
.
The fitted curve
\mu_{t2}
is much simpler: it is constant just below
0
for all time points.
For fitting these curves, of the
66
regression coefficients, on average,
23.7
or
35.9\%
were selected during MCMC sampling.
The fitted smooth curve for
\sigma_{ct}
is shown in the third plot of Figure
6
.
For fitting this curve, of the
11
regression coefficients, on average,
5.7
or
51.4\%
were selected during MCMC sampling.

Hence, overall, for fitting the
3570
unique elements of the
84\times 84
covariance matrix, the model selects, on average,
132.1

of the available
360
parameters, per MCMC iteration.

We conclude this section by providing an interpretation of the results in terms of the elements of the covariance matrix
\Sigma
of the 4
responses. To do so, we construct two covariance matrices based on the sampled parameter values,
both for time points
t=0,0.15,0.30,0.45,0.60,0.75,0.90
and for
x_{3}
equal to the lower
(x_{3}=0.18)
and upper
(x_{3}=0.73)

quartiles of age. With these choices we can examine the effects of time
t
and age
x_{3}
on the elements of
\Sigma
.
For every iteration of the MCMC sampler (that is not discarded), we first obtain the innovation variances from (
50
).
Based on these and on the sampled correlation matrices
R
,
using (
23
), we reconstruct the innovation covariance matrices
D
. Further, we obtain the autoregressive coefficients from (

6
Discussion

The article describes a Bayesian framework for the analysis of multivariate longitudinal Gaussian responses,
with nonparametric models for the means, the elements of the generalized autoregressive matrices, the elements of
the innovation variance matrices, and the
2
parameters of the innovation correlation matrices. The use of spike-slab priors
is important here as it allows for automatic variable selection and function regularization, simultaneously in the
5
submodels. Further, it obviates the need for model selection based on information criteria, see e.g.
Pan & Mackenzie, 2003
,
and it allows for the uncertainty in the model choice to propagate in the model parameter estimates. In addition, the
automatic variable selection allows each regression equation to have its own set of covariates. For example, looking back at
(
48
), it may appear that the responses have the same mean model.
However, the binary indicators for variable selection allow each response to have its own mean model. The same is true for the autoregressive coefficients and innovation variances.

The major assumption of the framework is that the errors have a multivariate Gaussian distribution.
It is worthwhile to relax this assumption, and to that end, in future work, we will be exploring the use of the multivariate skew-normal distribution
(
Azzalini & Valle, 1996
)
.

Here we provide all details of the MCMC sampler of the three correlation models. We note that for all steps that involve tuning parameters, the values of these are chosen adaptively
(
Roberts & Rosenthal, 2009
)

to achieve an acceptance probability of
20\%-25\%

(
Roberts & Rosenthal, 2001
)
.

7.1
MCMC algorithm for the common correlations model

Starting from the common correlations model, the algorithm proceeds as follows:

1.

The elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{kl},k=1,\dots,p,l=1,\dots,K,

are updated in random order and in blocks of random size
(
Chan et al., 2006
)
. Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Bkl}
be a block
of elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{kl}
.
The proposed value for
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Bkl}
is obtained from its prior with the remaining elements
of
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{kl}
, denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Ckl}
, kept at their current value.
The proposal pmf is obtained from the binomial prior in (
34
) with
\pi_{\mu kl}
integrated out

\displaystyle p(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Bkl}|\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Ckl})=\frac{p(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{kl})}{p(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Ckl})}=\frac{\text{Beta}(c_{\mu kl}+N(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{kl}),d_{\mu kl}+q_{\mu l}-N(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{kl}))}{\text{Beta}(c_{\mu kl}+N(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Ckl}),d_{\mu kl}+q_{\mu l}-L(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Bkl})-N(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Ckl}))},

where
L(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Bkl})
denotes the length of
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Bkl}
i.e. the size of the block.
For this proposal pmf, the acceptance probability of the Metropolis-Hastings move reduces
to the ratio of the likelihoods in (
40
)

\displaystyle\min\left\{1,(c_{\beta}+1)^{\{N(\gamma^{C})-N(\gamma^{P})\}/2}\exp\{(S^{C}-S^{P})/2\}\right\},

where superscripts
P
and
C
denote proposed and currents values respectively.

2.

Parameter
c_{\beta}
is updated from the marginal (
40
) and the IG
(a_{\beta},b_{\beta})
prior

\displaystyle f(c_{\beta}|\dots)\propto(c_{\beta}+1)^{-\frac{N(\gamma)+p}{2}}\exp(-S/2)c_{\beta}^{-a_{\beta}-1}\exp(-b_{\beta}/c_{\beta}).

To sample from the above, we utilize a normal approximation. Let

\ell(c_{\beta})=\log\{f(c_{\beta}|\dots)\}
. We utilize a normal proposal density

N(\hat{c}_{\beta},-g^{2}/\ell^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{c}_{\beta}))
, where
\hat{c}_{\beta}
is the mode
of
\ell(c_{\beta})
found using a Newton-Raphson algorithm,

\ell^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{c}_{\beta})
is the second derivative of
\ell(c_{\beta})
evaluated at the mode, and
g^{2}
is a tuning parameter.
With superscripts
P
and
C
denoting proposed and currents values, the acceptance probability
is the minimum between one and

\frac{f(c_{\beta}^{P}|\dots)}{f(c_{\beta}^{C}|\dots)}\frac{N(c_{\beta}^{C};\hat{c}_{\beta},-g^{2}/\ell^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{c}_{\beta}))}{N(c_{\beta}^{P};\hat{c}_{\beta},-g^{2}/\ell^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{c}_{\beta}))}.

3.

Pairs
(\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{kl},\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{kl}),k=1,\dots,p,l=1,\dots,L,
are updated simultaneously.
Similarly to the updating of
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{kl}
, the elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{kl}
are updated in random
order and in blocks of random size. Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{Bkl}
denote a block. Blocks
\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{Bkl}
and the whole vector

\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{kl}
are generated simultaneously. As was mentioned by
Chan et al., 2006

vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{kl}
, instead of subvector
\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{Bkl}
, is necessary
in order to make
\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{kl}
consistent with the proposed value of
\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{kl}
.

Generating the proposed value for
\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{Bkl}
is done in a similar way as was done for
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Bkl}
.
Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}^{P}_{kl}
denote the proposed value of
\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{kl}
. Next, we describe how
the proposed vale for
\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{\delta^{P}_{kl}}
is obtained.
To avoid clutter, proposed values
\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}^{P}_{\delta^{P}_{kl}}
will be denoted by the simpler
\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{kl}^{P}

The development that follows is in the spirit of
Chan et al., 2006
who built on the work of
Gamerman, 1997

Let
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{\gamma}^{C}=\{c_{\beta}/(1+c_{\beta})\}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma})^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptX}}_{\gamma}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pty}}

denote the current value of the posterior mean of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{\gamma}^{\ast}

Define the current squared residuals

\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}^{P}_{kl}

, for a small value of
\tau^{2}
, can reasonably be assumed to be unity.

11.

The elements of
\nu
are updated in random order and in blocks of random size. Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{B}
be such a block. The proposed value for
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{B}
is obtained from its prior with the remaining elements of
\nu
, denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{B^{C}}
, kept at their current value, in a similar way as was done for
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Bkl}
.
The acceptance probability reduces to the ratio of the likelihoods in (
44
)

\displaystyle\min\left\{1,\frac{(c_{\eta}+1)^{-\frac{N(\nu^{P})+1}{2}}\exp\{-(S^{\ast})^{P}/2\sigma^{2}\}}{(c_{\eta}+1)^{-\frac{N(\nu^{C})+1}{2}}\exp\{-(S^{\ast})^{C}/2\sigma^{2}\}}\right\},

where superscripts
P
and
C
denote proposed and currents values respectively.

12.

Vectors
\omega
and
\varphi
are updated following a similar approach as that for step 3. The elements of
\varphi
are updated in random order and in blocks of random size. Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{B}
denote a block. Blocks
\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{B}
and the whole vector
\omega
are generated simultaneously.
Generating the proposed value for
\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{B}
is done in a similar way as was done for
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{Bkl}
. Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}^{P}
denote the proposed value of
\varphi
. Next, we describe how the proposed vale for
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi^{P}}
is obtained.
Let
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{\nu}^{C}=\{c_{\eta}/(1+c_{\eta})\}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu})^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\theta}}

denote the current value of the posterior mean of
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu}
. Define the current squared residuals

e_{tkl}^{C}=(\theta_{tkl}-(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}^{\ast}_{t\nu})^{\top}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{\nu}^{C})^{2},

t\in T,k=1,\dots,p-1,l>k
.
These will have an approximate
\sigma^{2}_{t}\chi^{2}_{1}
distribution, where
\sigma^{2}_{t}=\sigma^{2}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{t}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})
. The latter defines a Gamma generalized linear model (GLM) for the squared
residuals with mean
E(\sigma^{2}_{t}\chi^{2}_{1})=\sigma^{2}_{t}=\sigma^{2}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{t}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})
, which, utilizing a
\log
-link, can be thought of as Gamma GLM with an offset term:
\log(\sigma^{2}_{t})=\log(\sigma^{2})+\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{t}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}
. Given the proposed value of
\varphi
, denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}^{P}
, the proposal density for
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi^{P}}
is derived utilizing the one step iteratively reweighted least squares algorithm. This proceeds as follows. First define the transformed observations

\displaystyle d_{tkl}^{C}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{C})=\log(\sigma^{2})+\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{t}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{C}+\frac{e_{tkl}^{C}-(\sigma^{2}_{t})^{C}}{(\sigma^{2}_{t})^{C}},

where superscript
C
denotes current values. Further, let
\mbox{\boldmath$d$}^{C}
denote the vector of
d_{tkl}^{C}
.

Next we define

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}(\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}^{P})=(c_{\omega}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$I$}+\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{\varphi^{P}}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{\varphi^{P}})^{-1}\text{\;and\;}\hat{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\omega}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}^{P},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{C})=\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}_{\varphi^{P}}\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{\varphi^{P}}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$d$}^{C},

where
Z
is the design matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{\ast}
without the intercept column. The proposed value
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi^{P}}^{P}
is obtained from a multivariate normal distribution with mean
\hat{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\omega}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}^{P},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{C})
and covariance
h\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{P})
, denoted as

N(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi^{P}}^{P};\hat{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\omega}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}^{P},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{C}),h\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}(\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}^{P}))
, where
h
is a tuning parameter.

Let
N(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi^{C}}^{C};\hat{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\omega}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}^{C},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{P}),h\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}(\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}^{C}))
denote the proposal density
for taking a step in the reverse direction, from model
\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}^{P}
to
\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}^{C}
. Then the acceptance probability of the pair
(\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}^{P},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{P}_{\varphi^{P}})
is

\displaystyle\min\left\{1,\frac{|D^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{P}_{\varphi^{P}})|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\{-S^{P}/2\sigma^{2}\}}{|D^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{C}_{\varphi^{C}})|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\{-S^{C}/2\sigma^{2}\}}\frac{(2\pi c_{\omega})^{-\frac{N(\varphi^{P})}{2}}\exp\{-\frac{1}{2c_{\omega}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{P}_{\varphi^{P}})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{P}_{\varphi^{P}}\}}{(2\pi c_{\omega})^{-\frac{N(\varphi^{C})}{2}}\exp\{-\frac{1}{2c_{\omega}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{C}_{\varphi^{C}})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{C}_{\varphi^{C}}\}}\frac{N(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi^{C}}^{C};\hat{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\alpha}}_{\varphi^{C}},h\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}_{\varphi^{C}})}{N(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi^{P}}^{P};\hat{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\alpha}}_{\varphi^{P}},h\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}_{\varphi^{P}})}\right\}.

13.

We update
\sigma^{2}
utilizing the marginal (
44
) and the either of the two prior specifications.
The full conditional that corresponds to the IG
(a_{\sigma},b_{\sigma})
prior is

\displaystyle f(\sigma^{2}|\dots)\propto(\sigma^{2})^{-\frac{Md}{2}-a_{\sigma}-1}\exp\{-(S^{\ast}/2+b_{\sigma})/\sigma^{2}\},

where
M
denotes the number of unique observational times and
d=p(p-1)/2
. The above is recognized as IG
(Md/2+a_{\sigma},S^{\ast}/2+b_{\sigma})
.

The full conditional that corresponds to the half-normal prior
\sigma\sim N(\sigma;0,\phi^{2}_{\sigma})I[\sigma>0]
is

\displaystyle f(\sigma^{2}|\dots)\propto(\sigma^{2})^{-\frac{Md}{2}}\exp\{-S^{\ast}/(2\sigma^{2})\}\exp\{-\sigma^{2}/(2\phi^{2}_{\sigma})\}I[\sigma>0].

Proposed values are obtained from
\sigma^{2}_{p}\sim N(\sigma^{2}_{c},f_{1}^{2})
, where
\sigma^{2}_{c}
denotes the current value and
f_{1}^{2}
is a tuning parameter. Proposed values are accepted with probability
f(\sigma^{2}_{p}|\dots)/f(\sigma^{2}_{c}|\dots)
.

14.

Parameter
c_{\eta}
is updated from the marginal (
44
) and the IG
(a_{\eta},b_{\eta})
prior

\displaystyle f(c_{\eta}|\dots)\propto(c_{\eta}+1)^{-\frac{N(\nu)+1}{2}}\exp\{-S^{\ast}/2\sigma^{2}\}(c_{\eta})^{-a_{\eta}-1}\exp\{-b_{\eta}/c_{\eta}\}.

To sample from the above, we utilize a normal approximation to it. Let

\ell(c_{\eta})=\log\{f(c_{\eta}|\dots)\}
. We utilize a normal proposal density

N(\hat{c}_{\eta},-g^{2}/\ell^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{c}_{\eta}))
where
\hat{c}_{\eta}
is the mode
of
\ell(c_{\eta})
, found using a Newton-Raphson algorithm,

\ell^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{c}_{\eta})
is the second derivative of
\ell(c_{\eta})
evaluated at the mode,
and
g^{2}
is a tuning parameter.
At iteration
u+1
the acceptance probability is the minimum between one and

\frac{f(c_{\eta}^{(u+1)}|\dots)}{f(c_{\eta}^{(u)}|\dots)}\frac{N(c_{\eta}^{(u)};\hat{c}_{\eta},-g^{2}/\ell^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{c}_{\eta}))}{N(c_{\eta}^{(u+1)};\hat{c}_{\eta},-g^{2}/\ell^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{c}_{\eta}))}.

15.

Concerning parameter
c_{\omega}
, the full conditional that corresponds to the IG
(a_{\omega},b_{\omega})
prior is
another inverse Gamma density IG
(a_{\omega}+N(\varphi)/2,b_{\omega}+\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi}/2)
.

The full conditional that corresponds to the half-normal prior
\sqrt{c_{\omega}}\sim N(\sqrt{c_{\omega}};0,\phi^{2}_{c_{\omega}})I[\sqrt{c_{\omega}}>0]
is

\displaystyle f(c_{\omega}|\dots)\propto c_{\omega}^{-N(\varphi)/2}\exp\{-\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi}/(2c_{\omega})\}\exp\{-c_{\omega}/(2\phi^{2}_{c_{\omega}})\}I[\sqrt{c_{\omega}}>0].

We obtain proposed values
c_{\omega}^{P}\sim N(c_{\omega}^{C},f_{2}^{2})
, where
c_{\omega}^{C}
denotes the current value and
f_{2}^{2}
is a tuning parameter. Proposed values are accepted with probability
f(c_{\omega}^{P}|\dots)/f(c_{\omega}^{C}|\dots)
.

16.

The sampler utilizes the marginal in (
44

However, if samples are required from the posterior of
\eta

from

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu}|\dots\sim N(\frac{c_{\eta}}{1+c_{\eta}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu})^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\theta}},\frac{\sigma^{2}c_{\eta}}{1+c_{\eta}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu})^{-1}),

7.2
MCMC algorithm for the grouped correlations model

The algorithm for the ‘grouped correlations’ model requires
3
additional steps (the first
3
below) and a modification to a step from the algorithm of the ‘common correlations’ model (the last one below). We describe these next.

1.

Update
v_{h}\sim\text{Beta}(d_{h}+1,d-\sum_{l=1}^{h}d_{h}+\alpha^{\ast}),h=1,\dots,H-1,

where
d=p(p-1)/2
is the number of correlations, and
d_{h}
is the number of correlations allocated in the
h
th cluster.
Given
v_{h}
, update the weights
w_{h},h=1,\dots,H
.

2.

To calculate the cluster assignment probabilities first
define
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{kl}=(\theta_{1kl},\dots,\theta_{Mkl})^{\top}
to be the profile of the
(k,l)
correlation. Based on (
43
), we have the following posterior probability

\displaystyle P(\lambda_{kl}=h|\dots)\propto w_{h}N(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{kl};\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{*}_{kl\nu_{h}}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}^{*}_{\nu_{h}},\sigma^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{kl}^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi})).

Here matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{kl}^{2}
is diagonal of dimension
M
with elements
given by
\exp\{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{\varphi t}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi}\},t\in T
, and matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{kl\nu_{h}}^{\ast}
has
M
rows
given by
(\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{th}^{\ast})^{\top},t\in T,
i.e. it is the subset of
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{\ast}_{\nu_{h}}
that corresponds to

\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{kl}
. Vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{\nu_{h}}^{*}
is imputed from its posterior, or if the cluster is empty
from prior (
36
).

3.

We update concentration parameter
\alpha^{\ast}
using the method described by
Escobar & West, 1995
.
With the
\alpha^{*}\sim\text{Gamma}(a_{\alpha*},b_{\alpha*})
prior,
the posterior can be expressed as a mixture of two Gamma distributions

\alpha^{\ast}|\eta,k\sim\pi_{\eta}\text{Gamma}(a_{\alpha*}+k,b_{\alpha*}-\log(\eta))+(1-\pi_{\eta})\text{Gamma}(a_{\alpha*}+k-1,b_{\alpha*}-\log(\eta)),

(55)

where
k
is the number of non-empty clusters,

\pi_{\eta}=(a_{\alpha*}+k-1)/\{a_{\alpha*}+k-1+n(b_{\alpha*}-\log(\eta))\}
and

\eta|\alpha^{\ast},k\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha^{\ast}+1,d).

(56)

Hence, the algorithm proceeds as follows: with
\alpha^{\ast}
and
k
fixed at their current values, we sample
\eta
from
(
56
). Then, based on the same
k
and the newly sampled value of
\eta
, we sample a new
\alpha^{*}
from (
55
).

4.

A modification to step 12. of the previous algorithm is needed. Here, we calculate the squared residuals based on

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{\nu_{h}}^{C}=\{c_{\eta}/(1+c_{\eta})\}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu_{h}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu_{h}})^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0ptZ}}_{\nu_{h}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\hskip-2.0pt\theta}}_{h}
.
Furthermore, function
S^{*}
is needed for this step.

7.3
MCMC algorithm for the grouped variables model

The algorithm for the ‘grouped variables’ model differs from that of the ‘grouped correlations’ model in the calculation of the cluster assignment probabilities.
Here, they are computed as follows.
Recall that
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{kl}=(\theta_{1kl},\dots,\theta_{Mkl})^{\top}
and let

w_{h}
be the prior probability that a variable is assigned to cluster

We have the following posterior probability

\displaystyle P(\lambda_{k}=h|\dots)\propto w_{h}\prod_{l\neq k}N(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{kl};\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}^{*}_{kl\nu_{h\lambda_{l}}}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}^{\ast}_{\nu_{h\lambda_{l}}},\sigma^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{kl}^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{\varphi})).
