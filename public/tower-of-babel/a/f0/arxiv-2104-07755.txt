Central limit theorems for the ( 2 + 1 2+1 )-dimensional directed polymer in the weak disorder limit
Simon Gabriel

Abstract
. In this article, we present an invariance principle for the paths of the directed random polymer in space dimension two in the subcritical intermediate disorder regime. More precisely, the distribution of diffusively rescaled polymer paths converges in probability to the law of Brownian motion when taking the weak disorder limit. So far analogous results have only been established for d ≠ 2 d\neq 2 . Along the way, we prove a local limit theorem which allows us to factorise the point-to-point partition function of the directed polymer into a product of two point-to-plane partition functions.

Primary: 82D60; Secondary: 60F17, 60K37, 82B44

1.
Introduction and main results

The directed polymer model describes a random walk
whose law is exponentially tilted by a random environment. The strength of the environment is described by a non-negative parameter
\beta\;\geqslant\;0
, corresponding to the inverse temperature, which we will refer to as disorder strength.
Individual models may vary but the most common definition is as follows:
consider the law
{\bf P}_{N,x}
of a nearest neighbour random walk of length
N
starting at
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Furthermore, let
\omega=(\omega_{n,z})_{(n,z)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
be a family of random variables with law
\mathbb{P}
(independent of
{\bf P}_{N,x}
).
For a fixed realisation of
\omega
, the directed polymer measure of length
N
and disorder strength
\beta\;\geqslant\;0
is then defined using the following change of measure

\displaystyle{\bf P}_{\beta,N,x}^{\omega}(dS):=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}(0,x,N,\star)}\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\beta\,\omega_{n,S_{n}}-\lambda(\beta))\right){\bf P}_{N,x}(dS),

where
\lambda(\beta)
is a positive constant, which we will fix in the subsequent section. The denominator

\displaystyle Z_{\beta,N}(0,x,N,\star):={\bf E}_{N,x}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\beta\,\omega_{n,S_{n}}-\lambda(\beta))\right)\right]

is a (random) normalising constant, called the (point-to-plane) partition function, making
{\bf P}_{\beta,N,x}^{\omega}
a probability measure. Here,
\star
denotes the free boundary condition of the endpoint
S_{N}
to take arbitrary values in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. The notation for the partition function might seem overloaded at this point but will become clear below.

The original model goes back to the physics literature
[
23
]
where directed random polymers were introduced to study the interface in two-dimensional Ising models with random interactions.
Subsequently, the model was studied by the mathematical community
[
24
,
10
]

and attracted attention because of its application to stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs), see for example
[
4
,
7
]
.
But even on its own, the directed random polymer remains an interesting mathematical model.
We refer to
[
14
,
13
]
and references therein for an overview of the polymer literature.

Henceforth we will choose
{\bf P}_{N,x}
to be the path measure of the symmetric simple random walk of length
N
starting at
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Without loss of generality we will assume
x=0
and omit the space index, thus, simply writing
{\bf P}_{N}={\bf P}_{N,0}
and
{\bf E}_{N}
for the corresponding expectation.
Moreover, let
\omega=(\omega_{n,z})_{(n,z)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
be a collection of i.i.d. real random variables satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\omega_{n,z}]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\omega_{n,z}^{2}]=1,\qquad\lambda(\beta):=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\beta\omega_{n,z}}]<\infty\;\;\forall\beta>0\text{ small enough},

which we will refer to as disorder.
For technical reasons, we also require a concentration inequality for the law
\mathbb{P}
. More precisely, we assume the existence of
\gamma>1
and
C_{1},C_{2}\in(0,\infty)
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and convex,
1
-Lipschitz
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|f(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n})-M_{f}|\;\geqslant\;t)\;\leqslant\;C_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{t^{\gamma}}{C_{2}}\right),

(1.1)

where
(\omega_{i})_{1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;n}
is a subset of the family of random variables introduced above and
M_{f}
is a median of
f(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n})
. Condition (
1.1
) guarantees control on the negative tail of the environment and is for example satisfied whenever
\omega
is bounded or Gaussian; see
[
30
]
for an even wider class of potential laws and more details. See also Remark
2.5
for a discussion and possible approach to weaken this assumption.

It was shown in
[
10
]
, that the partition function’s limit, as
N
diverges, is either positive or equal zero
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely.
The monotonicity in
\beta
of this behaviour was proven in
[
19
]
: for arbitrary dimension
d
there exists a
\beta_{c}=\beta_{c}(d)
such that
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{\beta,N}(0,0,N,\star)\begin{cases}>0&\text{ if }\beta\in\{0\}\cup(0,\beta_{c}),\\
=0&\text{ if }\beta>\beta_{c}.\end{cases}

The subcritical phase is referred to as
weak disorder regime
, whereas the supercritical phase is known as the
strong disorder regime
.
In particular, they established that
\beta_{c}=0
whenever
d\;\leqslant\;2
.
In the interest of seeing a limit of
Z_{\beta,N}(0,0,N,\star)
with non-trivial fluctuations in
d\;\leqslant\;2
,
\beta
needs to be rescaled as a function of
N
appropriately; see
[
2
,
17
]
and the account we give in Section
1.3
.
Note that the concept of considering polymers when the disorder strength is scaled as a function of
N
appeared already earlier in the physics literature
[
6
,
11
]
.

The case of dimension two is special: after rescaling
\beta=\beta_{N}\sim\widehat{\beta}\sqrt{\pi(\log N)^{-1}}
, for some
\widehat{\beta}\;\geqslant\;0
, we see a phase transition in
\widehat{\beta}

[
17
]
. More precisely, the limit
\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)
is strictly positive if
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)
and vanishes if
\widehat{\beta}\;\geqslant\;1
.
Note that the
\log
-scaling of
\beta_{N}
, but not the corresponding phase transition in
\widehat{\beta}
, was already observed in
[
5
]
.
In the one-dimensional case no such phase transition exists and the limiting partition function is strictly positive for
\beta=\beta_{N}\sim\widehat{\beta}N^{-1/4}
and arbitrary
\widehat{\beta}>0
.

1.1.
Main result

In this paper we consider directed random polymers in two space-dimensions.
We are particularly interested in the asymptotic behaviour of paths under the measure
{\bf P}^{\omega}_{\beta,N}
in the large
N
limit. The first step is to determine the scaling exponent
\xi>0
such that
N^{-\xi}S_{N}
under
{\bf P}^{\omega}_{\beta,N}
has a non-trivial (random) limit, before determining the exact limiting distribution of the rescaled endpoint. Here, we say the polymer is diffusive if
\xi=\tfrac{1}{2}
, subdiffusive if
\xi<\tfrac{1}{2}
and superdiffusive otherwise.
After establishing the endpoint distribution, the next natural step is to determine the limit law of the paths
N^{-\xi}(S_{n})_{0\;\leqslant\;n\;\leqslant\;N}
. As we will see, this is not straightforward due to the random environment, cf. Remark
1.9
.

Consider the directed polymer measure
{\bf P}_{\beta,N}^{\omega}
introduced above. The random measure is supported on the space
\{(S_{n})_{n}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{2})^{N+1}\}
, more precisely its support is given by the subset
\Omega_{0,N}
of nearest neighbour paths starting at the origin.
Because we use the space
C[0,1]:=C([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{2})
equipped with the supremum-norm as reference space for the paths, we introduce the mapping
\pi_{N}:\Omega_{0,N}\mapsto C[0,1]
given by

\displaystyle X^{(N)}_{t}:=\left(\pi_{N}(S)\right)_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(S_{\lfloor tN\rfloor}+(tN-\lfloor tN\rfloor)(S_{\lfloor tN\rfloor+1}-S_{\lfloor tN\rfloor})\right),

(1.2)

which embeds discrete nearest-neighbour paths in the space of continuous functions by linearly interpolating between integer points and rescaling space-time diffusively. Furthermore, we equip
C[0,1]
with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}
which implies measurability of the projection maps
\pi_{N}
.

The paper’s main contribution is an invariance principle for rescaled polymer paths. Along the way we determine the limiting finite-dimensional distributions of the rescaled polymers.
We take the weak disorder limit, which is the large
N
limit when scaling
\beta=\beta_{N}\sim\widehat{\beta}\sqrt{\pi(\log N)^{-1}}
where
0<\widehat{\beta}<1
. More precisely, we consider

\displaystyle\beta_{N}:=\frac{\widehat{\beta}}{\sqrt{R_{N}}},\quad\text{ where }\quad R_{N}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\bf P}_{N}(S_{n}=z)^{2}=\tfrac{\log N}{\pi}+O(1)

(1.3)

denotes the
replica overlap
of the simple random walk.

Our first result states that the finite-dimensional distributions of the quenched polymer measure
\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}
converge to the ones of Brownian motion with diffusion matrix
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}I_{2}
in
\mathbb{P}
-probability.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)
and
\beta_{N}
be as in (
1.3
), then for any
0\;\leqslant\;t_{1}<\ldots<t_{k}\;\leqslant\;1
we have

\displaystyle\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},{N}}^{\omega}\big((X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{k}})\in\cdot\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}{\bf P}\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(W_{t_{1}},\ldots,W_{t_{k}}\big)\in\cdot),\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{P}\text{-probability},

where
{\bf P}
denotes the Wiener measure on
C[0,1]
.
We wrote
\pi_{N}^{\ast}
for the push-forward operation under
\pi_{N}
.

Convergence of finite-dimensional distributions and tightness of the disorder-averaged polymer measure (cf. Lemma
3.3
) suffice to show that the limiting rescaled polymer paths have the same law as Brownian motion.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)
and
\beta_{N}
be as in (
1.3
).
Then

\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}{\bf P}\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W\in\cdot\,\big),\qquad\text{as }N\to\infty,\quad\text{in }\mathbb{P}\text{-probability},

where
{\bf P}
denotes the Wiener measure on
C[0,1]
.

Despite the random polymer converging to a stochastic process (which is independent of the disorder) on a macroscopic scale, the disorder influences the behaviour of the polymer on small scales.
On the microscopic level, the disorder prevails and we can deduce a local limit theorem,
which allows to compare the microscopic polymer transition probabilities to the ones of Brownian motion, weighted by random multiplicative factors which depend on the rescaled transition space-time points.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)
,
\beta_{N}
as in (
1.3
) and

\displaystyle(z_{j})_{j=1}^{k}=(z_{j}(N))_{j=1}^{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\text{ such that }\lim_{N\to\infty}\tfrac{z_{j}}{\sqrt{N}}=:x_{j}\text{ exists},

\displaystyle(m_{j})_{j=1}^{k}=(m_{j}(N))_{j=1}^{k}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }\lim_{N\to\infty}\tfrac{m_{j}}{N}=:t_{j}\in(0,1)\text{ exists with }0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}<1.

Then,

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{N}{2}\Big)^{k}\,{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}(S_{m_{1}}=z_{1},\ldots,S_{m_{k}}=z_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}:e^{Y^{-}(t_{j},x_{j})}:\,:e^{Y^{+}(t_{j},x_{j})}:\prod_{j=1}^{k}p_{\frac{1}{2}(t_{j}-t_{j-1})}(x_{j}-x_{j-1}),

(1.4)

where
Y_{j}^{\pm}
’s are i.i.d. centred Gaussians with variance
\log(1-\widehat{\beta}^{2})^{-1}
. We used the shorthand notation
:e^{Y}:=e^{Y-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]}
for the Wick exponential.

The local limit theorem above reinforces the picture that the considered subcritical intermediate disorder regime is indeed the region where effects of disorder start to emerge in a non-trivial way.

Remark 1.4
.

We excluded the case
t_{k}=1
from Proposition
1.3
, since it would only give rise to a single factor
:e^{Y^{-}(1,x)}:
. However, the proof can be repeated almost verbatim to include this case.

1.2.
Comparison to the literature

We give a short overview on the literature of diffusivity of directed random polymers.
Consider the two dimensional case and
\beta_{N}
scaled as in (
1.3
), then it was proven in
[
17
]
that the diffusively rescaled field

\{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,\lfloor\sqrt{N}x\rfloor,\lfloor tN\rfloor,\star)\,:\,t>0,\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\}

converges to the solution of the stochastic heat equation with additive
space-time white noise, but to the author’s best knowledge there are no results on diffusivity of the polymer paths in this case.
Subcritical scalings
\beta_{N}^{2}\ll R_{N}^{-1}
in
d=2
were considered by Feng
[
22
]
, who proved diffusivity of the polymer endpoint.
However, under such subcritical scalings the partition function’s variance vanishes in the large
N
limit, which essentially brings us to the situation of setting
\widehat{\beta}=0
.
Theorem
1.1
and
1.2
, on the other hand, consider a critical scaling under which the partition function converges to a non-trivial random variable and a transition (in
\beta
) of the polymer path behaviour is expected.
Our result not only covers the diffusivity of the polymer endpoint but fully determines the behaviour of the limiting polymer paths in the corresponding subcritical regime under diffusive scaling.

In dimension
d\;\geqslant\;3
, diffusivity of the directed random polymer in the weak disorder regime was first proven to hold with probability one in
[
24
]
for sufficiently small disorder strength. The works by Bolthausen
[
10
]
and Kifer
[
28
]
simplified and extended the original result further. The first invariance principles were deduced in
[
3
,
37
]
where, for
\beta>0
in the
L^{2}
-phase, almost sure convergence to the law of Brownian motion with dimension-dependent diffusion matrix was achieved.
Later, it was extended to the full weak disorder regime by Comets and Yoshida
[
19
]
in the sense of a functional central limit theorem which holds in probability.
Theorem
1.2
can be viewed as the analogous result in
d=2
.

Recently, Junk
[
25
,
26
]
gave an alternative proof of determining the limit of the polymer endpoint distribution (for bounded bond disorder) in
d\;\geqslant\;3
by introducing a comparison principle for partition functions of distinct parameters
\beta
. This allows them to transform the polymer endpoint distribution into the one of the simple random walk with a multiplicative error, that converges to one.

A result for the case of
d=1
was presented in papers by Alberts, Khanin and Quastel. In
[
2
]
they showed that transition probabilities of the discrete polymer measure admit a random limit when space-time is scaled diffusively. Because every
\beta>0
lies in the strong disorder regime, they also relied on an intermediate disorder scaling:
\beta_{N}\sim\widehat{\beta}N^{-1/4}
.
In
[
1
]
they constructed the corresponding continuum polymer measure using the random field of transition probabilities that arose in
[
2
]
.
As opposed to
d\;\geqslant\;3
, the limiting distribution of polymer paths turned out to be singular w.r.t. the Wiener measure when scaled diffusively, while maintaining the same basic properties as Brownian motion.

An analogous result was also shown for the continuum disordered pinning model in
[
15
]
. The advances, both for the pinning model and the
(1+1)
-dimensional directed polymer, then motivated to provide a general skeleton for the study of weak disorder scaling limits of discrete systems, see
[
16
]
.

A natural extension of the invariance principle in Theorem
1.2
is to strengthen the result to
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. convergence, similar to the results for the
L^{2}
-phase in
d\;\geqslant\;3

[
3
,
37
]
.
We want to point out that such results, holding with probability one, usually exploit the fact that the sequence of partition functions
(Z_{\beta,N})_{N}
forms a martingale.
In the two-dimensional case, this property is lost due to the dependency
\beta=\beta_{N}
, which is why we do not expect our methods to yield an almost sure invariance principle.

The statement of the local limit theorem, Proposition
1.3
, is reminiscent of the construction in
[
1
]
, where the limiting field of partition functions was used to construct the continuum directed polymer in
(1+1)
-dimension.
Because in
d=1
the random (macroscopic) field induced by the limiting partition functions is continuous in its time and space points, the constructed polymer measure is the correct limiting object.
In
d=2
this is however not the case anymore, which leads to substantial different behaviour of the polymer paths on a microscopic and macroscopic level. This (rough) structure of the partition function requires substantial work in order to establish the limiting polymer’s behaviour.

Remark 1.5
.

Theorem
1.1
and
1.2
should hold for a larger class of symmetric random walks which satisfy a local limit theorem in the sense of
[
17
, Hypothesis 2.4]
and their replica overlap fulfils
R_{N}\to\infty
as a slowly varying function.

Remark 1.6
.

Instead of studying discrete polymers, we could have similarly worked with polymers in the continuous space-time domain
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
where the simple random walk is replaced by a Brownian motion and the disorder is given by a space-time white noise
\xi
. The corresponding polymer measure is then defined by the following Gibbsian tilt

{\bf P}^{\xi}_{\beta_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon}(dX)\propto\exp\left(\beta_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{1}\xi^{\varepsilon}(s,X_{s})ds-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\|j\|_{2}^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}\right){\bf P}(dX),

where
\xi^{\varepsilon}=\xi(t,\cdot)\ast j_{\varepsilon}

with
j_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-2}j(\cdot/\varepsilon)
for some
j\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
As we turn off the mollification
\varepsilon\to 0
, we will need to tune the disorder strength like
\beta_{\varepsilon}=\widehat{\beta}\sqrt{2\pi/\log\varepsilon^{-1}}
, similar to the discrete polymer. The corresponding version of Theorem
1.2
in the continuum then reads as follows: for every
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)

\displaystyle{\bf P}^{\xi}_{\beta_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}{\bf P},\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{P}\text{-probability}.

(1.5)

Since all properties of the partition function of the discrete polymer also hold in the continuum, see
[
17
,
18
]
,
the proof of the above fact should follow along the same lines.
However, we refrain from giving a full proof to keep the paper at a reasonable length.

1.3.
Background and outline of the proof

Before presenting the outline of the proofs, we want to motivate the choice of the disorder scaling
\beta_{N}
.
A second moment calculation of the partition function, which was already performed in
[
24
]
, however for a polymer model of slightly different form, provides the following heuristic:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta,N}(0,0,N,\star)^{2}]

\displaystyle={\bf E}_{N}^{\otimes 2}\Big[\prod_{n=1}^{N}e^{(\lambda(2\beta)-2\lambda(\beta))\mathds{1}_{S_{n}=S_{n}^{\prime}}}\Big]={\bf E}_{N}^{\otimes 2}\Big[\prod_{n=1}^{N}(1+\sigma^{2}\mathds{1}_{S_{n}=S_{n}^{\prime}})\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sigma^{2k}\sum_{1\;\leqslant\;n_{1}<\cdots<n_{k}\;\leqslant\;N}{\bf E}_{N}^{\otimes 2}\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{S_{n_{i}}=S_{n_{i}}^{\prime}}\Big],

where
S
and
S^{\prime}
are two independent random walks of length
N
and
\sigma
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma=\sigma(\beta):=\sqrt{e^{\lambda(2\beta)-2\lambda(\beta)}-1}.

(1.6)

Note that
\lambda(2\beta)-2\lambda(\beta)\sim~\beta^{2}
for small
\beta>0
and therefore
{\lim_{\beta\to 0}\beta/\sigma(\beta)=1}

[
18
, Equation (2.15)]
.
We upper bound the sum by ignoring the ordering of
n_{i}
’s which yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta,N}(0,0,N,\star)^{2}]\;\leqslant\;\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sigma^{2k}\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\bf P}_{N}(S_{n}=z)^{2}\Big)^{k}.

Recalling the replica overlap from (
1.3
) and considering the fact that
\sigma(\beta)\sim\beta
for small
\beta>0
, this suggests that the correct rescaling is given by
\beta=\beta_{N}:=\widehat{\beta}/\sqrt{R_{N}}
, whenever
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)
. Throughout the paper we will write
\sigma_{N}:=\sigma(\beta_{N})
.

Indeed, it was proven in
[
17
]
that under
\beta_{N}
the partition function
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)
converges to a non-trivial (random) limit whenever
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)
, see also (
1.12
) below.
Moreover, they noticed the existence of a transition on the finer scale with
\widehat{\beta}_{c}=1
denoting the critical point where the
L^{2}
-norm of the partition function blows up in the limit.
Whenever
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)
and
\beta_{N}
is scaled as above, we speak of the
intermediate weak disorder regime
.

Theorem
1.2
, in the present paper, states that rescaled polymer paths in the intermediate weak disorder limit behave like the ones of Brownian motion.
However, this is not a straightforward consequence of the positivity of the limiting partition function,
but requires precise estimates quantifying the correlation structure of the limiting field.
The first step towards the main result is to show convergence of the finite-dimensional distributions of the (rescaled) directed polymer measure to the ones of Brownian motion, cf. Theorem
1.1
.
We begin by observing that for
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}

\displaystyle{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}(S_{m_{1}}=z_{1},\ldots,S_{m_{k}}=z_{k})

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}\prod_{j=1}^{k+1}{\bf E}_{N}\left[e^{\sum_{n=m_{j-1}+1}^{m_{j}}\left(\beta_{N}w_{n,S_{n}}-\lambda(\beta_{N})\right)}\mathds{1}_{S_{m_{j}}=z_{j}}\Big|S_{m_{j-1}}=z_{j-1}\right],

where
m_{0}=z_{0}=0
,
m_{k+1}=N
and
z_{k+1}=\star
. For our purposes it will be more convenient to rewrite the above expression in terms of the expectations conditioned on both the start and end point, i.e.

\displaystyle{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}(S_{m_{1}}=z_{1},\ldots,S_{m_{k}}=z_{k})

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}\prod_{j=1}^{k+1}{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j-1},z_{j-1}\mid m_{j},z_{j})\,q_{m_{j}-m_{j-1}}(z_{j}-z_{j-1}),

(1.7)

where we introduced the point-to-point partition functions

\displaystyle{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j-1},z_{j-1}\mid m_{j},z_{j}):={\bf E}_{N}\left[e^{\sum_{n=m_{j-1}+1}^{m_{j}}\left(\beta_{N}\omega_{n,S_{n}}-\lambda(\beta_{N})\right)}\Big|S_{m_{j-1}}=z_{j-1},\,S_{m_{j}}=z_{j}\right]

and the shorthand
q_{n}(z)
denoting the transition probability
{\bf P}_{N}(S_{n}=z)
of the simple-random walk.

Remark 1.7
.

Note that the point-to-point partition function
{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)
also takes the disorder at the endpoint into consideration. However, as it will turn out, it is more natural to compare the product of point-to-plane partition functions to the point-to-point partition function

\displaystyle Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z):={\bf E}_{N}\left[e^{\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\beta_{N}\omega_{n,S_{n}}-\lambda(\beta_{N})\right)}\Big|S_{0}=0,\,S_{N}=z\right],

(1.8)

not taking the endpoint-disorder into account.
The distinction of the point-to-point partition functions’ notation may be very subtle, but so is the difference between them.
In fact, the difference between (
1.8
) and
{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)
vanishes in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
:

\displaystyle\|{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)-Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{E}[(e^{\beta_{N}\omega_{N,z}-\lambda(\beta_{N})}-1)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\|Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)\|_{2}^{2}(e^{\lambda(2\beta_{N})-2\lambda(\beta_{N})}-1),

where we used the independence property of the disorder in the first equality. Because the first term on the r.h.s. is uniformly bounded in
N
and
\lambda(2\beta_{N})-2\lambda(\beta_{N})\sim\beta_{N}^{2}
, the
L^{2}
-difference vanishes.
We refer to both
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)
and
{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)
as point-to-point partition function since the meaning will be clear from the context.

With the slight abuse of notation, we will write for
0\;\leqslant\;s<t\;\leqslant\;1

{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(sN,y\mid tN,z)

instead of
{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(\lfloor sN\rfloor,y\mid\lfloor tN\rfloor,z)
. Similarly, for the point-to-plane partition function. Furthermore, for future reference, we introduce the plane-to-point partition function, which is defined as

\displaystyle Z_{\beta_{N},N}(\widetilde{m},\star,m,z):={\bf E}_{N}\left[e^{\sum_{n=\widetilde{m}}^{m-1}\left(\beta w_{n,S_{n}}-\lambda(\beta)\right)}\Big|S_{m}=z\right].

(1.9)

One can think of it as the partition function of a polymer starting in
(m,z)
and evolving backwards in time.
For convenience, we will refer to both point-to-line and line-to-point as point-to-plane whenever the context is clear.

Having representation (
1.3
) at hand, we see the necessity to understand the limiting behaviour of point-to-point partition functions, before analysing the finite-dimensional distributions of the polymer measure.
In order to manage such point-to-point partition functions, we prove a local limit theorem (Proposition
2.1
) which states that they can be approximated by the product of two point-to-plane partition functions, i.e.

\displaystyle{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\,|\,N,z)=Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,\tfrac{N}{2},\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}(\tfrac{N}{2},\star,N,z)+\varepsilon_{N}\,,

(1.10)

with
\varepsilon_{N}
vanishing in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
.
Factorisations of this nature were proven in
d\;\geqslant\;3

[
36
,
38
,
12
,
33
]
.
During completion of this paper, Nakajima and Nakashima
[
34
]
proved independently a result similar to Proposition
2.1
in the continuous space-time setting, see also Remark
2.3
. They use the local limit theorem to extend the class of SPDEs and initial conditions that admit Edwards-Wilkinson fluctuations.

We want to put particular emphasis on
[
36
]
because of the similarities in their work and our proof of Proposition
2.1
.
Their proof of the local limit theorem of the form (
1.10
) uses the fact that polynomial chaos components, (
2.5
), can be factorised using a single random walk transition probability which is of order
q_{N}(z)

[
36
, Theorem 2]
. Our proof of Lemma
2.6
resembles this approach.
Additionally, they explain how a central limit theorem for the end-point distribution can be obtained from the above factorisation
[
36
, Theorem 4]
.
It is interesting to note that Sinai proved the local limit theorem under the condition

\displaystyle\sigma(\beta)^{2}R_{\infty}<1\,,

(1.11)

where
R_{\infty}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}R_{N}
, which is only finite in dimension
d\;\geqslant\;3
.
Formally generalising condition (
1.11
) w.r.t. the weak disorder limit in
d=2
, it reads
\sigma(\beta_{N})^{2}R_{N}<1
which is equivalent to our assumption
\widehat{\beta}<1
due to
\sigma(\beta_{N})\sim\beta_{N}
.

As a consequence of (
1.10
), the limiting distribution of point-to-point partition functions can be deduced from the corresponding point-to-plane partition functions approximating it.
In
[
17
, Theorem 2.12]
, Caravenna, Sun and Zygouras proved that finite families of partition functions of directed polymers converge jointly to a multivariate log-normal distribution. More precisely, consider the collection of space-time points
((n_{i},z_{i}))_{1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;k}=((n_{i}(N),z_{i}(N)))_{1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;k}
such that for every
1\;\leqslant\;~i,j~\;\leqslant\;~k

\displaystyle R_{N-n_{i}}/R_{N}\to 1,\quad\text{ as }N\to\infty,

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}

\displaystyle R_{|n_{i}-n_{j}|\vee|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{2}}/R_{N}=\zeta_{i,j}\in[0,1]\quad\text{ exists},

then

\displaystyle\left(Z_{\beta_{N},N}(n_{i},z_{i},N,\star)\right)_{1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;k}\to(:e^{Y_{i}}:)_{1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;k},

(1.12)

where
(Y_{i})_{1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;k}
is a multivariate Gaussian with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}Y_{j}]=\log\frac{1-\widehat{\beta}^{2}\zeta_{i,j}}{1-\widehat{\beta}^{2}}\qquad\forall 1\;\leqslant\;i,j\;\leqslant\;k.

(1.13)

Particularly, the result holds for space-time points
((n_{i},z_{i}))_{1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;k}
having positive macroscopic distance, in which case the corresponding tuple
(Y_{i})_{1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;k}
consists of independent Gaussians because
\zeta_{i,j}=\mathds{1}_{i\neq j}
.

Remark 1.8
.

This fact is precisely the reason for the different behaviour of the limiting polymer law in
d=2
compared to
d=1
.
In the one-dimensional case the partition functions have non-trivial dependency in the large
N
limit for macroscopically separated space-time points, see
[
2
]
, leading to a path measure singular w.r.t. the Wiener measure.
In the two-dimensional setting (
1.13
) implies that partition functions started from macroscopically separated points will have independent limits, leading to an self-averaging effect for the polymer measure.
However, for points having vanishing macroscopic distance, the limiting field will have non-trivial dependency.

After having dealt with the approximation of point-to-point partition functions, we can move on to the convergence of quenched polymer marginals.
The greatest difficulty when dealing with the finite-dimensional marginals of the form (
1.3
) is the fact that none of the point-to-point partition functions is independent of the denominator
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)
.
We outline the approach taken in this paper; for the sake of simplicity, we only explain the following for the end-point distribution.

First, we prove that the limiting annealed polymer marginal, i.e.
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[{\bf P}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}S_{N}\in\cdot)]
, agrees with the ones of Brownian motion, cf. Lemma
3.2
.
In fact, we show the much stronger result that the quenched marginal can be approximated in
L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
by a simplified representation, without the partition function
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)
in the denominator:

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\Big\|{\bf P}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}S_{N}\in B)-\sum_{z\in\sqrt{N}B}Z_{\beta_{N},N}(\tfrac{N}{2},\star,N,z)q_{N}(z)\Big\|_{1}=0\,,

(1.14)

using the factorisation in (
1.10
).
Here and throughout the paper
\sqrt{N}B
denotes the set
\{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,:\,\tfrac{z}{\sqrt{N}}\in B\}
.
The expectation of the latter representation is immediate, which yields the annealed limit
{\bf P}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{1}\in B)
.

In order to conclude convergence of the quenched marginal, the natural next step would be to prove that
\sum_{z\in\sqrt{N}B}Z_{\beta_{N},N}(\tfrac{N}{2},\star,N,z)q_{N}(z)
converges to its mean in
L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
. Instead, we show the stronger convergence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
, i.e.

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\Big\|\sum_{z\in\sqrt{N}B}Z_{\beta_{N},N}(\tfrac{N}{2},\star,N,z)q_{N}(z)-{\bf P}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{1}\in B)\Big\|_{2}=0\,,

since this reduces to a second moment calculation.
Estimating the second moment of
\sum_{z\in\sqrt{N}B}Z_{\beta_{N},N}(\tfrac{N}{2},\star,N,z)q_{N}(z)
, requires careful evaluation of the partition functions’ covariance structure, cf. Lemma
4.3
, leading to a law-of-large number like behaviour.
Together with (
1.14
) this yields
L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
-convergence of the quenched end-point distribution.

When calculating second moments, we essentially introduce a second independent copy of the polymer before averaging over the environment, see also
[
19
]
.
A difference in our approach is that we exploit the fact that in
d=2
the subcritical regime coincides with the
L^{2}
-regime.
In other words, the positivity of the limiting partition function happens exactly in the regime where the
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
-norm remains uniformly bounded.
On the other hand, in
d\;\geqslant\;3
this is not the case as the
L^{2}
-regime is a strict subset of the weak disorder regime. Thus, Comets-Yoshida constructed, taking only advantage of the positivity of the limiting partition function, a (random) inhomogeneous Markov chain characterising the limiting discrete polymer measure with infinite time horizon.

After proving convergence of finite-dimensional distributions, cf. Theorem
1.1
, we show that for any fixed function
F\in C_{b}(C[0,1])

\displaystyle\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[F(X)]\to{\bf E}[F(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W)],\qquad\text{as }N\to\infty,\quad\text{in }\mathbb{P}\text{-probability},

(1.15)

by blending in ideas from the classical Donsker’s invariance principle:
using tightness of the annealed polymer measure, it suffices to restrict the polymer paths to a compact set
K\subset C[0,1]
when testing against a function
F\in C_{b}(C[0,1])
.
The Stone-Weierstrass theorem then states that
F
can be approximated uniformly by cylinder functions on
K
, i.e. functions that only depend on finitely many marginals of the polymer path.
Together with Theorem
1.1
this yields the functional central limit theorem.

Lastly, we prove equivalence of functional central limit theorem and invariance principle by using a countable weak convergence determining family of functions, cf. Proposition
4.9
.
This yields weak convergence of the polymer measures as stated in Theorem
1.2
.
Note that the same argument allows rewriting the functional central limit theorem for
d\;\geqslant\;3
in
[
19
]
in terms of an invariance principle, cf. Corollary
4.11
.

Remark 1.9
.

We want to stress that (
1.15
) is not yet a classical invariance principle stating convergence of the polymer measure, but only a central limit theorem stating convergence when paths are tested against individual test functions. To emphasise this point, we note that a ‘true’ invariance principle (in
\mathbb{P}
-probability) reads as follows: for every sequence
(N_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{N}
there exists a subsequence
(N_{j_{m}})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
and a set
\overline{\Omega}\subset\Omega
of full measure such that

\pi_{N_{j_{m}}}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N_{j_{m}}},{N_{j_{m}}}}[F(X)]\to{\bf E}[F(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W)]\quad\forall F\in C_{b}(C[0,1]),

for every
\omega\in\overline{\Omega}
. In (
1.15
) on the other hand, we fix a function
F\in C_{b}(C[0,1])
for which there exists a subsequence and a set
\overline{\Omega}
of full mass, on which the convergence holds.
The dependency of
(N_{j_{m}})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\overline{\Omega}
on
F
does not allow us to exchange the order of quantifiers without further reasoning.

1.4.
Structure of the article

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we prove that point-to-point partition functions can be approximated by the point-to-plane partition functions which we introduced above.
In Section
3
we use this fact to prove convergence of the annealed finite-dimensional distributions of the polymer measure to the ones of a Brownian motion with diffusion matrix
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}I_{2}
, where
I_{2}
denotes the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
.
Together with a tightness argument this yields an annealed invariance principle, cf. Proposition
3.1
.
Section
4
is divided into three parts. First, we prove Theorem
1.1
, where we use the self-averaging behaviour described above. Next, we present the proof of the invariance principle, cf. Theorem
1.2
, where we exploit the tightness of the annealed polymer measure.
Lastly, we show the local limit theorem for the polymer marginals on microscopic scales, Proposition
1.3
.

1.5.
Notation

Throughout the paper,
q_{n}(z)
denotes the shorthand for the transition probabilities
{{\bf P}_{N}(S_{n}=z)}
of the simple random walk. Its continuous counterpart, the density of a centred Gaussian variable on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with variance
t
is written as
p_{t}(x)
; we also write
\lambda(\cdot)=\lambda^{(d)}(\cdot)
for the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Moreover,
\|\cdot\|_{p}
denotes the
L^{p}(\mathbb{P})
-norm, i.e.

\|\cdot\|_{p}^{p}:=\mathbb{E}[|\cdot|^{p}],

for every
p>0
.
Lastly, we will write
a_{N}\sim b_{N}
for sequences if
\lim_{N\to\infty}\,a_{N}/b_{N}=1
.

2.
Approximation of the point-to-point partition function

This section’s main result is given by the following Proposition which states that point-to-point partition functions can be locally uniformly approximated by the product of a point-to-plane and a plane-to-point partition function.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)
,

x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
r>0
be arbitrary,
then
for
0<s^{+}<t^{-}<1
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\sqrt{N}B(x,r)\\
\text{s.t. }q_{N}(z)>0\end{subarray}}\left\|{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)-Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,s^{+}N,\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t^{-}N,\star,N,z)\right\|_{2}\to 0.

The statement remains true when replacing
{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)
with
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)
, which we introduced in (
1.8
).

Remark 2.2
.

Proposition
2.1
also holds for
s^{+}=1
and
t^{-}=0
w.r.t.
L^{1+\delta}(\mathbb{P})
-convergence for some
\delta>0
small enough. This can be shown following the same steps in the proof of Proposition
2.1
.
After completion of this paper, it was proved that
\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}[{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{p}]<\infty
for arbitrary
p>0

[
20
,
32
]
. The stronger moment estimates, allow to lift the mode of convergence from
L^{1+\delta}(\mathbb{P})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
.

Throughout this section, the point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
plays the role of the macroscopic endpoint of the polymer path. In particular,
\sqrt{N}B(x,r)
includes all microscopic points which are close to
x
on a macroscopic scale in the large
N
limit which is important in Sections
3
and
4
.
Note that Proposition
2.1
remains true when replacing the initial time
s=0
and final time
t=1
with arbitrary values
0\;\leqslant\;s<t\;\leqslant\;1
, i.e. when considering partition functions
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(sN,0\mid tN,z)
or
{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(sN,0\mid tN,z)
.

Remark 2.3
.

A similar result was obtained recently and independently by Nakajima and Nakashima
[
34
, Theorem 2.8]
. They proved that the point-to-point partition function of a directed random polymer in the continuum can be approximated in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
by the product of point-to-plane partition functions with mesoscopic time-horizon if the distance between start and terminal space-point is not too large.
Similar to the present paper, they show that contributions to the point-to-point partition function only come from the environment close to start and endpoint, before they replace the Brownian Bridge measure by two Brownian motions running independently forward and backward in time.
For the partition function of a polymer of length
N
, an equivalent result to
[
34
, Theorem 2.8]
for the discrete case would read as follows

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:\,|z|\;\leqslant\;\sqrt{N\log N}\\
\text{s.t. }q_{N}(z)>0\end{subarray}}\left\|{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)-Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,l_{N},\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}(N-l_{N},\star,N,z)\right\|_{2}^{2}\to 0\,,

(2.1)

for

l_{N}=N^{1-(\log N)^{\gamma-1}}
with
\gamma\in(0,1)
.
In contrast to Proposition
2.1
,
here the radius of uniformity is
\sqrt{N\log N}
. This is due to keeping track of vanishing rates in their proof, which allows to strengthen the result.
Likewise, exact evaluation of the quantities in Lemma
A.1
using the local limit theorem should allow to increase the radius of uniformity in Proposition
2.1
to the same order. However, in regards of our main result this is not necessary.

Before continuing, we remind the reader that the partition function
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)
can be written in terms of a discrete chaos expansion
[
17
,
18
]
:

\displaystyle Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)

\displaystyle={\bf E}_{N}\Big[e^{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}(\beta_{N}\omega_{n,z}-\lambda(\beta_{N}))\mathds{1}_{S_{n}=z}}\Big]

(2.2)

\displaystyle={\bf E}_{N}\Big[\prod_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}(1+\sigma_{N}\eta_{n,z}\mathds{1}_{S_{n}=z})\Big]=1+\sum_{k=1}^{N}Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0,N,\star),

where
Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0,N,\star)
is defined below and

\eta_{n,z}=\eta_{n,z}^{(N)}:=\frac{1}{\sigma_{N}}(e^{\beta_{N}\omega_{n,z}-\lambda(\beta_{N})}-1)

being centred i.i.d. random variables with unit variance.
In the last equality of (
2.2
) we expanded the products which gives rise to the
k
-th homogeneous chaos denoted by

\displaystyle Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0,N,\star):=\sigma_{N}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\;\leqslant\;n_{1}<\cdots<n_{k}\;\leqslant\;N\\
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{n_{i}-n_{i-1}}(z_{i}-z_{i-1})\eta_{n_{i},z_{i}}\right),

(2.3)

with
(n_{0},z_{0})
denoting the origin
(0,0)\in\mathbb{N}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.
Note that the terms in the series expansion above are orthogonal in the sense that
\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0,N,\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(j)}(0,0,N,\star)]=0
, whenever
k\neq j
, due to the different number of disorder-terms considered. Throughout the paper, we will use this fact in second moment computations without further explanation.

An analogous expansion holds for the plane-to-point partition function
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,\star,N,z)
with

\displaystyle Z^{(k)}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,\star,N,z):=\sigma_{N}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\;\leqslant\;n_{1}<\cdots<n_{k}\;\leqslant\;N-1\\
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{n_{i+1}-n_{i}}(z_{i+1}-z_{i})\eta_{n_{i},z_{i}}\right),

(2.4)

where we assumed
(n_{k+1},z_{k+1})=(N,z)
and used the symmetry of the transition probabilities of the simple random walk.
Similarly, for the point-to-point partition function we write

\displaystyle Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{N}Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0\mid N,z)

and

\displaystyle Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0\mid N,z):=\sigma_{N}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\;\leqslant\;n_{1}<\cdots<n_{k}\;\leqslant\;N-1\\
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{n_{i}-n_{i-1}}(z_{i}-z_{i-1})\eta_{n_{i},z_{i}}\right)\frac{q_{N-n_{k}}(z-z_{k})}{q_{N}(z)}.

(2.5)

We point out that the point-to-plane partition function can be recovered by taking the average over all possible endpoints and include the endpoint-disorder:

\displaystyle Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)=\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)q_{N}(z)=\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)e^{\beta_{N}\omega_{N,z}-\lambda(\beta_{N})}q_{N}(z).

The proof of Proposition
2.1
relies on the fact that the main contribution of the point-to-point partition function comes from two mesoscopic sized subsets of the space-time domain around the start and terminal point.
These space-time areas are the same as the ones giving main contribution to the partition functions
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)
, see
[
18
]
.
For a microscopic reference point
(n,z)\in\mathbb{N}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, we define such sets both forward and backward in time:

A^{\pm}_{N}(n,z):=\{(m,y)\,:\,|y-z|\;\leqslant\;{N}^{1/2-a_{N}/4}\;\text{and}\;0\;\leqslant\;\pm(m-n)\;\leqslant\;N^{1-a_{N}}\},

where
a_{N}:=(\log N)^{\gamma-1}
for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

As already mentioned above, only samples inside of
A^{+}_{N}(0,0)\cup A^{-}_{N}(N,z)
will contribute to the
L^{2}
-limit of
Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0\mid N,z)
. On this account, we introduce the following decomposition

Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0\mid N,z)=Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k),A}(0,0\mid N,z)+\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0\mid N,z),

where
Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k),A}(0,0\mid N,z)
denotes the sum

\displaystyle\sigma_{N}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\;\leqslant\;n_{1}<\cdots<n_{k}\;\leqslant\;N-1\\
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\\
(n_{i},z_{i})\in A^{+}_{N}(0,0)\cup A^{-}_{N}(N,z)\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{n_{i},z_{i}}\,q_{n_{i}-n_{i-1}}(z_{i}-z_{i-1})\right)\frac{q_{N-n_{k}}(z-z_{k})}{q_{N}(z)}

(2.6)

and
\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0\mid N,z)
the corresponding remainder.
This restriction can be thought of as ‘turning off’ the disorder outside the two boxes; for visualisation the reader may refer to Figure
1
below.
Similarly, we separate

\displaystyle Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)=Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(0,0\mid N,z)+\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)

(2.7)

with
Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(0,0\mid N,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k),A}(0,0\mid N,z)
.
Sometimes we will also use this notation for point-to-plane partition functions. Then,
Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(j),A}(0,0,N,\star)
and
Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(j),A}(0,\star,N,z)
will stand for the multi-linear polynomials in (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) with disorder restricted to
A^{+}_{N}(0,0)
or
A^{-}_{N}(N,z)
, respectively.
Moreover, we want to mention that
Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(j),A}(0,0,N,\star)=0
for all
j>N^{1-a_{N}}
, hence,

\displaystyle Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(0,0,N,\star)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor N^{1-a_{N}}\rfloor}Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(j),A}(0,0,N,\star)\,,

(2.8)

similarly for the line-to-point partition function.
Lastly, note that the size of
A^{\pm}_{N}
only depends on the level of approximation
N
and not the point-to-point partition function’s final time.

We will frequently make use of higher-order and negative moment estimates of the partition function. We summarise equations (3.12), (3.14) and (3.15) from
[
18
]
in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4

(Caravenna-Sun-Zygouras)
.

Let
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)
, then

(i)

there exists a
\delta=\delta(\widehat{\beta})>0
and a constant
C^{\prime}_{\widehat{\beta}}<\infty
such that for every
p\in[2,2+\delta]

\displaystyle\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{p}\right]\;\leqslant\;C^{\prime}_{\widehat{\beta}}\,,\quad\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(0,0,N,\star)^{p}\right]\;\leqslant\;C^{\prime}_{\widehat{\beta}}

\displaystyle\quad\text{ and }\quad\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{p}\right]\;\leqslant\;C^{\prime}_{\widehat{\beta}}(a_{N})^{p/2}.

(ii)

for every
p>0
there exists a constant
\widehat{C}_{\widehat{\beta},p}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{-p}\right]\;\leqslant\;\widehat{C}_{\widehat{\beta},p}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(0,0,N,\star)^{-p}\right]\;\leqslant\;\widehat{C}_{\widehat{\beta},p}.

Because
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)
and
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,\star,N,z)
have the same distribution, all statements in Lemma
2.4
hold for the plane-to-point partition function, too.

Remark 2.5
.

That Lemma
2.4
(ii) holds for arbitrary negative moments, is a consequence of
the concentration inequality (
1.1
) and a left-tail estimate, see
[
18

2.4
, whereas the 2-norms of
{Z}^{A}_{\beta_{N},N}
and
{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}
are uniformly bounded in
N
.
Note that all statements in the proof hold uniformly on balls
\sqrt{N}B(x,r)
, therefore the local uniformity
in the statement of the Proposition follows. This completes the proof.
∎

Before we move on to the next section, we state and prove the analogous result of Lemma
2.4
(i) for the point-to-point partition function:

Corollary 2.8

(Hypercontractivity)
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
r>0
be arbitrary.
For every
\widehat{\beta}<1
there exists a
\delta=\delta(\widehat{\beta})>0
and
C>0
such that

(i)

\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{z\in\sqrt{N}B(x,r)}\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)^{2+\delta}]\;\leqslant\;C,

(ii)

\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{z\in\sqrt{N}B(x,r)}\mathbb{E}[{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)^{2+\delta}]\;\leqslant\;C.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
r>0
.
First, we prove statement (i) for the point-to-point partition function
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)
, ignoring the endpoint-disorder.
From the proof of Proposition
2.1
and Lemma
2.4
, we know that
\sup_{z\in\sqrt{N}B(x,r)}\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)^{2}]
is uniformly bounded in
N
.
Moreover, the point-to-point partition function still has the form of a multi-linear polynomial.
In order to lift the boundedness to the
(2+\delta)
-moment for
\delta>0
sufficiently small, we apply the hypercontractivity property, see for example Appendix B in
[
18
]
. It is easy to check that the necessary conditions are still satisfied. Thus, we may estimate the
p
-th moment,
p\;\geqslant\;2
, by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)^{p}]\;\leqslant\;\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{p}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0\mid N,z)^{2}]\Big)^{\frac{p}{2}}.

(2.21)

Here,
c_{p}
is a constant which only depends on
p
and the law of the noise
\eta^{(N)}
.
In
[
18
, Theorem B.1]
it was additionally shown that
\lim_{p\downarrow 2}c_{p}=1
.
In order to show that the sum is uniformly bounded in
N
, we split it into the two (by now well known) parts:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{p}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0\mid N,z)^{2}]=\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{p}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A,(k)}(0,0\mid N,z)^{2}]+\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{p}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0\mid N,z)^{2}],

(2.22)

due to orthogonality.
The second term on the r.h.s. can be upper bounded using (
2.2
) and Lemma
B.1

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{p}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}^{(k)}(0,0\mid N,z)^{2}]

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;C\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{p}^{2k}k^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(\sigma_{N}^{2}R_{N})^{k-j}\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}^{(j)}(0,0,N,\star))^{2}]

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;a_{N}\,C\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{p}^{2k}k^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(\sigma_{N}^{2}R_{N})^{k-j}(\sigma_{N}^{2}R_{N})^{j/2}j^{2}\;\leqslant\;a_{N}\,C\sum_{k=0}^{N}k^{5}(c_{p}^{4}\sigma_{N}^{2}R_{N})^{k/2}\,,

and vanishes because
c_{p}^{2}\widehat{\beta}<1
for
p\;\geqslant\;2
small enough, where we used
the fact that
c_{p}^{2}\sigma_{N}\sqrt{R_{N}}\sim c_{p}^{2}\widehat{\beta}
. The first term on the r.h.s. of (
2.22
), on the other hand, can be estimated by

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{p}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A,(k)}(0,0\mid N,z)^{2}]

\displaystyle\qquad\;\leqslant\;\bigg(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|N-n|<2N^{1-a_{N}}\\
|z-y|<2N^{1/2-a_{N}/4}\end{subarray}}\frac{q_{n}(y)}{q_{N}(z)}\bigg)\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{p}^{2k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A,(j)}(0,0,N,\star)^{2}]\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A,(k-j)}(0,\star,N,z)^{2}]

\displaystyle\qquad\;\leqslant\;\bigg(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|N-n|<2N^{1-a_{N}}\\
|z-y|<2N^{1/2-a_{N}/4}\end{subarray}}\frac{q_{n}(y)}{q_{N}(z)}\bigg)\sum_{k=0}^{N}(k+1)c_{p}^{2k}(\sigma_{N}^{2}R_{N})^{k},

where we performed a similar estimate as in the proof of Lemma
2.6
in the first inequality, recall display (
2.9
). In the second step we simply used the fact that
\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A,(j)}(0,0,N,\star)^{2}]\;\leqslant\;(\sigma_{N}^{2}R_{N})^{j}
(and similarly for the line-to-point partition function).
Again for
p\;\geqslant\;2
small enough,
c_{p}
can be absorbed into
\widehat{\beta}
, whereas Lemma
A.1
(i) gives that the supremum converges to one. Overall, this yields that (
2.21
) is uniformly bounded in
N
.

The estimate for
{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)
follows now from (i). Recall that

\displaystyle{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)=e^{\beta_{N}\omega_{N,z}-\lambda(\beta_{N})}Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z),

where we included again the disorder at the endpoint separately. We then have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)^{p}]=\mathbb{E}[(e^{\beta_{N}\omega_{N,z}-\lambda(\beta_{N})})^{p}]\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid N,z)^{p}],

using the independence property of the disorder
\omega
. Choosing
p<2+\delta
, where
\delta=\delta(\widehat{\beta})
from part (i), we know the second term is uniformly bounded. It remains to estimate

\mathbb{E}[(e^{\beta_{N}\omega_{N,z}-\lambda(\beta_{N})})^{p}]=e^{\lambda(p\beta_{N})-p\lambda(\beta_{N})}.

By Taylor expansion we can write

\lambda(p\beta_{N})-p\lambda(\beta_{N})\sim\frac{1}{2}p(p-1)\beta_{N}^{2},

which yields boundedness of the exponential.
This concludes the proof of (ii).
∎

3.
The annealed polymer measure

After proving the factorisation of point-to-point partition functions, we can finally start analysing the limiting polymer measure.
As dealing with the annealed polymer measures first will substantially simplify the required steps in the quenched case,
we define the disorder-averaged measure
\mu_{\beta_{N},N}
on
(C[0,1],\mathcal{F})
, by

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta_{N},N}(B):=\mathbb{E}[\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}(B)]\qquad\forall B\in\mathcal{F}.

(3.1)

This section’s main result is an invariance principle for the paths of the annealed polymer measure.

Proposition 3.1

(Annealed invariance principle)
.

For
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta_{N},N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}{\bf P}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W\in\cdot\,),\quad\text{as }N\to\infty,

where we recall that
{\bf P}
denotes the Wiener measure on
C[0,1]
.

We begin by showing that the limiting finite-dimensional distributions of
\mu_{\beta_{N},N}
agree with the ones of a Brownian motion with diffusion matrix
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}I_{2}
.
In the section’s second part we prove the required tightness in
C[0,1]
. Together with the identification of finite-dimensional distributions, this yields Proposition
3.1
.

Instead of determining the finite-dimensional distributions of the interpolated paths, it suffices to work with the corresponding starting point of the interpolation. To see this, recall that under
\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}
we have

\displaystyle X_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}S_{\lfloor tN\rfloor}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(tN-\lfloor tN\rfloor)(S_{\lfloor tN\rfloor+1}-S_{\lfloor tN\rfloor}).

(3.2)

Because the simple random walk has a finite range transtition kernel, the second term vanishes for
N
large and we are left with
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}S_{\lfloor tN\rfloor}
.
Thus, the weak limits of
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}S_{\lfloor tN\rfloor}
and
X_{t}
(under
\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}
) must coincide if they exist. We will assume
tN\in\mathbb{N}
for the sake of notation.

Let
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{k}\;\leqslant\;1
. Throughout the remaining sections it will be convenient to fix a partitioning of the time intervals
(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
and
(t_{k},1]
using
t_{i}^{\pm}
’s such that

\displaystyle t_{i-1}^{+}<t_{i}^{-}<t_{i}<t_{i}^{+}.

(3.3)

Now, recalling the representation in (
1.3
), the idea when proving convergence of the marginal distributions is to replace the first point-to-point partition function using Proposition
2.1
:

\displaystyle\frac{{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid t_{1}N,z_{1})}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}\simeq\frac{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,t_{1}N,\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,\star,t_{1}N,z_{1})}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)},

(3.4)

where ‘
\simeq
’ should be understood as approximation in
L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
for large
N
. Because both the denominator and the first term in the numerator only depend on the disorder in a small neighbourhood around the starting point, they converge to the same limit and should cancel as
N
diverges.
We are then left with the remaining point-to-point partition functions which can be analysed separately due to independence of the disorder on disjoint time intervals.

Having the above approach in mind, we introduce a shorthand notation for the following constellation of terms.
Consider
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{k}<1
, we define

\displaystyle Q_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}\big((t_{i},B_{i})_{i=1}^{k}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{i}\in\sqrt{N}B_{i}\\
1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;k\end{subarray}}Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}^{-}N,\star,t_{1}N,z_{1})\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{j}N,z_{j},t_{j}^{+}N,\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{j+1}^{-}N,\star,t_{j+1}N,z_{j+1})\Big)

(3.5)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{k}N,z_{k},t_{k}^{+}N,\star)\prod_{j=1}^{k}q_{(t_{j}-t_{j-1})N}(z_{j}-z_{j-1}).

Whenever
t_{k}=1
, the partition function
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{k}N,z_{k},t_{k}^{+}N,\star)
is dropped, since it depends on the disorder outside of
\{0,\ldots,N\}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)
and
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{k}\;\leqslant\;1
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\Big\|{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}S_{t_{1}N}\in B_{1},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}S_{t_{k}N}\in B_{k}\big)-Q_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}\big((t_{i},B_{i})_{i=1}^{k}\big)\Big\|_{1}=0,

(3.6)

for every choice of bounded measurable sets
(B_{i})_{i=1}^{k}\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k}
satisfying

\lambda(\partial B_{i})=0
.

In particular, this implies

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}S_{t_{1}N}\in B_{1},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}S_{t_{k}N}\in B_{k}\big)\big]={\bf P}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{t_{1}}\in B_{1},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{t_{k}}\in B_{k}).

(3.7)

Proof.

We only elaborate the steps for the case
k=2
and
t_{2}=1
; the general statement follows along the same lines modulo more involved notation. Let
(B_{i})_{1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;2}
be bounded sets with boundary of Lebesgue-measure zero.
Throughout the proof, we assume
B(x_{i},r_{i})
’s to be balls large enough such that they cover the bounded sets
B_{i}
.
We write
t_{0}=0
and
z_{0}=0
for the starting point of the random polymer.

First, we will replace the point-to-point partition function
{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid t_{1}N,z_{1})
in (
1.3
) by its point-to-plane counterparts, using Proposition
2.1
. Afterwards, we exchange the arising term
{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,t_{0}^{+}N,\star)}
with
{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}
to cancel the point-to-plane partition function in the denominator.
In other words, we want to show that

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{i}\in\sqrt{N}B(x_{i},r_{i})\\
\text{s.t. }q_{t_{1}N}(z_{1})>0\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}N,z_{1}\mid N,z_{2})}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}\left({\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid t_{1}N,z_{1})-{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}^{-}N,\star,t_{1}N,z_{1})}\right)\bigg\|_{1}

(3.8)

vanishes in the large
N
limits.
Afterwards, the remaining point-to-point partition function can be replaced using again Proposition
2.1
, which yields (
3.6
).

Instead of showing the above convergence in (
3.8
) directly, we divide the statement into two, more manageable, terms by introducing the intermediate term
{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,t_{0}^{+}N,\star)}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}^{-}N,\star,t_{1}N,z_{1})}
.

•

We start with the replacement of the point-to-point partition function following Proposition
2.1
. First, we apply the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality which yields

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}N,z_{1}\mid N,z_{2})}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}\left({\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid t_{1}N,z_{1})-{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,t_{0}^{+}N,\star)}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}^{-}N,\star,t_{1}N,z_{1})}\right)\bigg\|_{1}

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\|Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{-1}\|_{2}\|{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}N,z_{1}\mid N,z_{2})\|_{2}

(3.9)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\|{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0\mid t_{1}N,z_{1})-{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,t_{0}^{+}N,\star)}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}^{-}N,\star,t_{1}N,z_{1})}\|_{2}.

Here we used independence of the disorder on the disjoint time intervals
(0,t_{1}N]
and
(t_{1}N,N]
in addition to the fact
that partition functions
{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(sN,0\mid tN,z)
only depend on the disorder
\omega
in
(sN,tN]\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.
The last term in (
•
‣
3
) vanishes uniformly in
z\in\sqrt{N}B(x_{1},r_{1})
due to Proposition
2.1
, whereas the first and second term are uniformly bounded, cf. Lemma
2.4
and Corollary
2.8
, respectively.

•

Lastly, we can estimate the remaining norm using again the fact that we have arbitrary good control of negative moments of
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)
.
Using

\displaystyle{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,t_{0}^{+}N,\star)}-{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}

\displaystyle={\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,t_{0}^{+}N,\star)}-{\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)},

the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,t_{0}^{+}N,\star)}-{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}^{-}N,\star,t_{1}N,z_{1})}{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}N,z_{1}\mid N,z_{2})\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\left\|\frac{{\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,t_{0}^{+}N,\star)}-{\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}\right\|_{2}\left\|{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}^{-}N,\star,t_{1}N,z_{1})}{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}N,z_{1}\mid N,z_{2})\right\|_{2}.

The second term on the r.h.s. is uniformly bounded by Lemma
2.4
(ii). The first term, on the other hand, can be estimated using Hölder’s inequality and the trivial estimate
\|a-b\|\;\leqslant\;\|a\|+\|b\|
:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,t_{0}^{+}N,\star)-{\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}\right\|_{2}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\|Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{-1}\|_{2+\delta^{-1}}(\|\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,t_{0}^{+}N,\star)\|_{2+\delta}\,+\,\|\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)\|_{2+\delta}),

where
\delta>0
is choosen sufficently small. Because
\|\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)\|_{2+\delta}
and
\|\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,t_{0}^{+}N,\star)\|_{2+\delta}
converge to zero using Lemma
2.4
(i), while the first term is uniformly bounded, this proves convergence to zero of the expression in (
3.8
).

Having proven that (
3.8
) vanishes, it is only left to replace
{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}N,z_{1}\mid N,z_{2})
by
Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}N,z_{1},t_{1}^{+}N,\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{2}^{-}N,\star,N,z_{2})

which holds again by means of Proposition
2.1
. This concludes the proof of (
3.6
).

Overall, (
3.6
) implies

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}(S_{t_{1}N}\in\sqrt{N}B_{1},S_{N}\in\sqrt{N}B_{2})]

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Q_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}((t_{i},B_{i})_{i=1}^{2})]

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{i}\in\sqrt{N}B_{i}\\
1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;2\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{2}q_{(t_{j}-t_{j-1})N}(z_{j}-z_{j-1}),

where we used the fact that
\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}^{-}N,\star,t_{1}N,z_{1})Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{1}N,z_{1},t_{1}^{+}N,\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}(t_{2}^{-}N,\star,N,z_{2})]=1
.
It is well known that the simple random walk in
d=2
converges in law to Brownian motion with diffusion matrix
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}I_{2}
, see for example
[
31
, Theorem 7.6.1]
.
∎

Note that Lemma
3.2
is enough to deduce weak convergence of finite-dimensional distributions. In order to lift the convergence result to the annealed polymer measures, we require tightness.

Lemma 3.3
.

The family of annealed polymer measures
(\mu_{\beta_{N},N})_{N}
is tight in
\mathcal{M}_{1}(C[0,1])
.

Proof.

We prove the tightness of
(\mu_{\beta_{N},N})_{N}
by following
[
27
, Theorem 16.5]
which states that it suffices to show that for every
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mu_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{\delta}(X)\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon)=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},{N}}^{\omega}(m_{\delta}(X)\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon)]=0,

(3.11)

where
m_{\delta}(\varphi)
denotes the modulus of continuity on the Wiener space, i.e.

\displaystyle m_{\delta}(\varphi):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\;\leqslant\;s,t\;\leqslant\;1\\
|t-s|<\delta\end{subarray}}\left|\varphi_{t}-\varphi_{s}\right|\qquad\forall\;\varphi\in C[0,1].

Next, we use the same trick as stated in

[
19
, Lemma 4.2]
for random polymers in
d\;\geqslant\;3
. See also

[
1
, Lemma 4.2]
for a similar application in the case of the continuum random polymer in
d=1
. For every
{\bf P}_{N}
-integrable
Y
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star){\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[Y]]={\bf E}_{N}[Y].

Thus, for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)\,\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}(m_{\delta}(X)\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon)]=\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{N}(m_{\delta}(X)\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon).

(3.12)

However, the sequence of rescaled, interpolated simple random walks is known to be tight in the Wiener space. Hence, the right hand side of equation (
3.12
) vanishes when taking first the limit superior
N\to\infty
and then the limit
\delta\to 0
.
In particular, this implies for any
\lambda>0
that

\displaystyle\lambda\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}\{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)\;\geqslant\;\lambda\}\,\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}(m_{\delta}(X)\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon)]\rightarrow 0,\qquad\text{as }N\to\infty,\;\delta\to 0.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}\{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)<\lambda\}\,\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}(m_{\delta}(X)\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon)]

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\lambda\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{-1}],

where we first dropped the inner probability before applying Markov’s inequality. Once more we make use of the fact that
\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{-1}]
is uniformly bounded in
N
which overall yields

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{\delta}(X)\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}(m_{\delta}(X)\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon)]

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}\{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)\;\geqslant\;\lambda\}\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}(m_{\delta}(X)\;\geqslant\;\varepsilon)]+\lambda\widehat{C},

for some
\widehat{C}=\widehat{C}_{\widehat{\beta}}>0
.
Taking first the limits
N\to\infty
,
\delta\to 0
and lastly the limit
\lambda\to 0
finally yields (
3.11
).
Thus, tightness of
(\mu_{\beta_{N},N})_{N}
follows.
∎

Combining now both Lemma
3.2
and Lemma
3.3
, we can show that annealed polymer paths converge in distribution to the ones of Brownian motion.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

We first note that Lemma
3.2
implies convergence of the finite-dimensional marginals of the annealed polymer measure.
Let
0<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{k}\;\leqslant\;1
be arbitrary.
Tightness of the marginals follows from the fact that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, we can choose bounded continuity sets
B_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

{\bf P}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{t_{1}}\in B_{1},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{t_{k}}\in B_{k})>1-\varepsilon\,.

Thus, using Lemma
3.2
, the corresponding polymer marginals satisfy

\displaystyle\inf_{N>\overline{N}}\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}S_{\lfloor t_{1}N\rfloor}\in B_{1},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}S_{\lfloor t_{k}N\rfloor}\in B_{k}\big)\big]>1-\varepsilon\,,

for some
\overline{N}=\overline{N}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
. This can be lifted to all
N\in\mathbb{N}
by extending the sets
B_{i}
.
Therefore, the laws of

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(S_{\lfloor t_{1}N\rfloor},\ldots,S_{\lfloor t_{k}N\rfloor})
are tight and converge weakly to the law of

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(W_{t_{1}},\ldots,W_{t_{k}})
, since bounded continuity sets suffice to identify the limiting measure uniquely.
The same holds for
(X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{k}})
, see (
3.2
) and the discussion below, i.e.

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta_{N},N}(X_{t_{1}}\in\cdot,\ldots,X_{t_{k}}\in\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}{\bf P}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{t_{1}}\in\cdot,\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{t_{k}}\in\cdot).

Recall now from Lemma
3.3
that the annealed polymer measures
(\mu_{\beta_{N},N})_{N}
are tight. Hence, convergence of the annealed polymer measures is immediate, because weak accumulation points of
(\mu_{\beta_{N},N})_{N}
are determined by their finite-dimensional distributions, see for example
[
8
, Theorem 7.1]
.
∎

4.
The quenched polymer measure

After proving the invariance principle for the annealed polymer measures in the previous section, we can now proceed to prove our main results for the quenched polymer measures.
In the first part of the section, we show convergence of finite-dimensional distributions of the rescaled, interpolated polymer paths, cf. Theorem
1.1
.
Afterwards, we prove Proposition
4.7
which is a functional central limit theorem for the polymer paths when tested against individual test functions in
C_{b}(C[0,1])
.
We then explain how the functional central limit theorem can be lifted to an invariance principle, cf. Proposition
4.9
, using a countable convergence determining class of functions.
Lastly, we prove the local limit theorem, Proposition
1.3
, for the polymer marginals on microscopic scales.

4.1.
Finite-dimensional distributions

4.1.1.
Self-averaging of the random polymer

We either have

Before stating the full proof of Lemma

Lemma 4.3
.

Let

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,z\in\sqrt{N}B(x,r)\\

Proof.

Let
y,z\in\sqrt{N}B(x,r)
such that
|y-z|>2N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a_{N}}{4}}
. First, we expand the expectation in the statement

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}(sN,y,tN,\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}(sN,z,tN,\star)]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(sN,y,tN,\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(sN,z,tN,\star)]+\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(sN,y,tN,\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(sN,z,tN,\star)]

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(sN,y,tN,\star)\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(sN,z,tN,\star)]+\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(sN,y,tN,\star)\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(sN,z,tN,\star)].

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma

in the large

For the remaining term we use the fact that

which implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(sN,y,tN,\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(sN,z,tN,\star)]=\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(sN,y,tN,\star)]\mathbb{E}[Z_{\beta_{N},N}^{A}(sN,z,tN,\star)]=1.

4.1.2.
Tightness and uniqueness of the limit

In Corollary
4.5
we showed convergence of polymer marginals evaluated on bounded, factorised, continuity sets in
L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
. This can be lifted to unbounded measurable sets
U\subset(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k}
satisfying the same properties.
However, we want to stress that this does not imply weak convergence of the polymer marginals yet, since exceptional points of the disorder can depend on the choice of sets
U
.
Nevertheless, we are able to show weak convergence of quenched finite-dimensional distributions in probability, since probability measures on
(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k}
are uniquely identified by evaluation on a countable family of sets.

We begin by recalling a standard result: a sequence of random variables on a metric space converges in probability if and only if every subsequence has a further subsequence which converges almost surely, see for example
[
27
, Lemma 4.2]
.
Thus, in order to show the convergence in probability of the marginal distributions, it suffices to prove tightness along sufficiently many subsequences
and identify the limit points using a
\pi
-system. This step is motivated by the recent article
[
25
]
, where Junk showed convergence of the polymer-endpoint distribution in bond disorder for
d\;\geqslant\;3
using a very similar approach.

For the
\pi
-system, on which we will identify the limiting finite-dimensional distributions, we choose half-open cylinders on
(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{k}:=\{[a_{1},b_{1})\times\cdots\times[a_{2k},b_{2k})\subset(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k}\,:\,a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}\text{ and }a_{i}<b_{i}\text{ for }1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;2k\},

which generates the Borel-sigma-algebra on
(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k}
. Note that
\mathcal{E}^{k}
has countably many elements and let
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be an arbitrary enumeration of them. We omitted the dependency of
E_{i}
’s on
k
for the sake of a lighter notation.

We start by showing that there exist sufficiently many subsequences along which finite-dimensional distributions evaluated on the
\mathcal{E}^{k}
converge almost surely.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
0\;\leqslant\;t_{1}<\ldots<t_{k}\;\leqslant\;1
, then for every sequence
(N_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{N}
there exists a subsequence
(N_{j_{m}})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\Omega_{1}=\Omega_{1}((N_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}},(t_{i})_{1\;\leqslant\;i\;\leqslant\;k})\subset\Omega
with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{1})=1
such that for every

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\pi_{N_{j_{m}}}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N_{j_{m}}},{N_{j_{m}}}}^{\omega}((X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{k}})\in E)={\bf P}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(W_{t_{1}},\ldots,W_{t_{k}})\in E)\quad\forall E\in\mathcal{E}^{k}.

Proof.

Let
(N_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be an arbitrary sequence in
\mathbb{N}
.
We prove the lemma only for a single marginal

, the multi-marginal case follows along the same lines.
Corollary
4.5
implies that for every

and

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(|\pi_{N_{j}}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N_{j}},{N_{j}}}^{\omega}(X_{t}\in E_{i})-{\bf P}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{t}\in E_{i})|>\varepsilon)=0.

In particular, for every

there exists a

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\pi_{N_{j}}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N_{j}},{N_{j}}}^{\omega}(X_{t}\in E_{i})-{\bf P}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{t}\in E_{i})|>\tfrac{1}{m})\;\leqslant\;m^{-1}2^{-m}\quad\forall j\;\geqslant\;M_{i,m}.

We define a subsequence of

4.2.
An invariance principle for polymer paths

We are finally ready to prove Theorem
1.2
.
Using tightness of the annealed polymer paths, cf. Lemma
3.3
, and convergence of the finite-dimensional distributions, cf. Theorem
1.1
, we can prove the desired result.
The steps resemble very much the ones when proving the invariance principle for the simple random walk. However, due the double randomness of paths and the environment, cf. Remark
1.9
, we require an additional argument to conclude the full invariance principle.

We begin by proving a functional central limit theorem:

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\widehat{\beta}\in(0,1)
and
\beta_{N}
as in (
1.3
).
Then for every
F\in C_{b}(C[0,1])

\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[F(X)]\to{\bf E}[F(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W)],\qquad\text{as }N\to\infty,\quad\text{in }\mathbb{P}\text{-probability},

where
{\bf E}
is the expectation w.r.t. the Wiener measure on
C[0,1]
.

Remark 4.8
.

Note that the convergence in the functional central limit theorem above also holds in
L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
, since the random variables
(\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[F(X)])_{N}
are uniformly bounded by
\|F\|_{\infty}
. In particular, this implies convergence of expectations which is equivalent to the annealed invariance principle, cf. Proposition
3.1
.

Proof.

Let
F\in C_{b}(C[0,1])
. The statement of the theorem is equivalent to

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(|\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[F(X)]-{\bf E}[F(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W)]|>\varepsilon)=0\quad\forall\varepsilon>0.

(4.5)

In the following, we fix
\varepsilon>0
and choose
\delta>0
arbitrary. Let then
K=K(\delta,\varepsilon,F)\subset C[0,1]
be a compact set such that

\displaystyle{\bf P}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W\in K^{c})\;\leqslant\;\frac{\varepsilon}{8\|F\|_{\infty}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{\beta_{N},N}(K^{c})=\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}[\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}(K^{c})]\;\leqslant\;\frac{\delta\,\varepsilon}{8\|F\|_{\infty}}.

(4.6)

A set
K
with these properties exists due to tightness of the Wiener measure and tightness of the annealed polymer measure, see Lemma
3.3
.

Throughout the remainder of the proof, we will denote by
\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}:C[0,1]\mapsto(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k}
the projection of a path onto previously chosen coordinates
0\;\leqslant\;t_{1}<\ldots<t_{k}\;\leqslant\;1
, i.e.

\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}(X)=(X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{k}})\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k}.

Restricting the domain of
F
to the compact set
K
, we can approximate
F
uniformly by cylinder functions on
C(K,\mathbb{R})
, i.e. functions that only depend on finitely many coordinates of the path, using the Stone-Weierstrass theorem
[
29
, Theorem 13.4]
. More precisely, there exist
0\;\leqslant\;t_{1}<\ldots<t_{k}\;\leqslant\;1
and a continuous
f:\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}(K)\mapsto\mathbb{R}
such that
\|f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}\|_{K,\infty}\;\leqslant\;\|F\|_{\infty}
and

\displaystyle\|F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}\|_{K,\infty}:=\sup_{\varphi\in K}|F(\varphi)-f(\varphi_{t_{1}},\ldots,\varphi_{t_{k}})|<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

(4.7)

Using the Tietze extension theorem
[
35
, Theorem 20.4]
, we can extend
f
continuously from
\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}(K)
to
(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k}
. At the same time, this yields a continuous extension of
f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}
on
C[0,1]
. Without loss of generality, the extension can be chosen in such a way that
\|f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}\|_{\infty}\;\leqslant\;\|f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}\|_{K,\infty}\;\leqslant\;\|F\|_{\infty}
.
Estimating now
(F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}})
on
K
and
K^{c}
respectively, after applying the triangle inequality, yields

\displaystyle|\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[(\mathds{1}_{K}+\mathds{1}_{K^{c}})(F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}})(X)]-{\bf E}[(\mathds{1}_{K}+\mathds{1}_{K^{c}})(F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}})(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W)]|

\displaystyle\qquad\;\leqslant\;2\|F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}\|_{K,\infty}+2\|F\|_{\infty}|\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf P}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}(K^{c})+{\bf P}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W\in K^{c})|.

Thus, due to (
4.7
) and the choice of
K
in (
4.6
), we have

\displaystyle|\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[(F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}})(X)]-{\bf E}[(F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}})(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W)]|\;\leqslant\;\frac{3\varepsilon}{4}+2\|F\|_{\infty}\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf P}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}(K^{c}).

(4.8)

All together, we can upper bound the term of interest

\displaystyle|\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[F(X)]-{\bf E}[F(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W)]|

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;|\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[f(X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{k}})]-{\bf E}[f(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{t_{1}},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{t_{k}})]|

\displaystyle\qquad+|\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[(F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}})(X)]-{\bf E}[(F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}})(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W)]|,

which implies in particular that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[F(X)]-{\bf E}[F(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W)]|>2\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\qquad\;\leqslant\;\mathbb{P}(|\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[f(X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{k}})]-{\bf E}[f(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{t_{1}},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{t_{k}})]|>\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{P}(|\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[(F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}})(X)]-{\bf E}[(F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}})(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W)]|>\varepsilon).

The first term on the right vanishes as
N\to\infty
due to Theorem
1.1
. The second term, on the other hand, can be further upper bounded using (
4.8
), such that

\displaystyle\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}(|\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf E}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}[(F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}})(X)]-{\bf E}[(F-f\circ\Pi_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}})(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W)]|>\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\qquad\;\leqslant\;\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\big(\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf P}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}(K^{c})>\tfrac{\varepsilon}{8\|F\|_{\infty}}\big)\;\leqslant\;\frac{8\|F\|_{\infty}}{\varepsilon}\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}[\pi_{N}^{\ast}{\bf P}^{\omega}_{\beta_{N},N}(K^{c})]\;\leqslant\;\delta,

where we applied Markov’s inequality before using (
4.6
) again.
Because
\delta>0
can be chosen arbitrarily small after taking the large
N
limit, (
4.5
) follows. This concludes the proof.
∎

It is only left to lift the functional central limit theorem (in
\mathbb{P}
-probability) to an invariance principle as stated in Theorem
1.2
.
In fact, we can show more generally the equivalence of functional central limit theorem and invariance principle for random probability measures:

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
(S,d)
be a separable, complete metric space and
(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})
a probability space. Moreover, let
(\mathrm{P}_{N}^{\omega})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be random probability measures
and
\mathrm{P}
a deterministic probability measure on
(S,\mathcal{F})
, here
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Borel-
\sigma
-algebra.
Then the following two statements are equivalent

(i)

for every
F\in C_{b}(S)
,
\mathrm{E}_{N}^{\omega}[F]\to\mathrm{E}[F]
in
\mathbb{P}
-probability,

(ii)

\mathrm{P}_{N}^{\omega}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathrm{P}
in
\mathbb{P}
-probability,

where
\mathrm{E}_{N}^{\omega}
and
\mathrm{E}
denote the expectations w.r.t.
\mathrm{P}_{N}^{\omega}
and
\mathrm{P}
, respectively.
The statement remains true when replacing convergence in probability with almost sure convergence.

First, we remind the reader that a set of functions
\mathcal{A}\subset C_{b}(S)
, where
S
Polish, is called
weak convergence determining
, if for
\nu_{n},\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(S)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int F\,d\nu_{n}=\int F\,d\nu\,\quad\forall F\in\mathcal{A}\,,

implies
\nu_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\nu
.

The following lemma, which is a Corollary of
[
9
, Lemma 2]
, states that we can always find such a family of functions which is countable, provided the probability measures are defined on a Polish space.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
(S,d)
be a separable and complete metric space, then there exists a countable algebra
\mathcal{A}=\{F_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{b}(S)
that is weak convergence determining.

Proof.

First, note that the family
\overline{\mathcal{A}}\subset C_{b}(S)
of uniformly continuous functions with bounded support strongly separates points.
Then
[
9
, Lemma 2]
yields existence of a countable subfamily
\mathcal{A}:=\{F_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\overline{\mathcal{A}}
that strongly separates points and remains closed under multiplication. Lastly applying
[
21
, Theorem 3.4.5]
, which states that an algebra in
C_{b}(S)
that strongly separates points is convergence determining, finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.9
.

We begin by proving the direction (i) to (ii).
Let
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{b}(S)
be a countable family of functions that is weak convergence determining, which existence is guaranteed by Lemma
4.10
.
For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{E}^{\omega}_{N}[F_{i}]=\mathrm{E}[F_{i}],\quad\text{in }\mathbb{P}\text{-probability}.

(4.9)

Now a diagonal argument, as we performed it in the proof of Lemma
4.6
, yields that for every sequence
(N_{j})_{j}\subset\mathbb{N}
there exists a further subsequence
(N_{j_{m}})_{m}
and a set
\overline{\Omega}\subset\Omega
, with
\mathbb{P}(\overline{\Omega})=1
, such that for every
\omega\in\overline{\Omega}

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\pi_{N_{j_{m}}}^{\ast}\mathrm{E}^{\omega}_{{N_{j_{m}}}}[F_{i}]=\mathrm{E}[F_{i}]\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Because
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is weak convergence determining, this implies
\mathrm{P}^{\omega}_{{N_{j_{m}}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathrm{P}
, as
m\to\infty
, for every
\omega\in\overline{\Omega}
. This is equivalent to weak convergence in
\mathbb{P}
-probability as stated in (ii).

The reverse direction, from (ii) to (i), is immediate.
Moreover, replacing convergence in
\mathbb{P}
-probability with
\mathbb{P}
-almost-sure convergence, we can simply use that
for every
F_{i}
there exists a set
\Omega_{i}\subset\Omega
with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{i})=1
such that (
4.9
) holds pointwise for every
\omega\in\Omega_{i}
. Taking now the countable intersection over all such
\Omega_{i}
’s, we conclude
\mathrm{P}_{N}^{\omega}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathrm{P}

\mathbb{P}
-almost surely.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The invariance principle for the polymer measures follows now directly from the functional CLT in Proposition
4.7
and Proposition
4.9
.
∎

Instead of taking the detour via the functional central limit theorem first, we could have also argued that

also concludes the invariance principle from the functional CLT in higher dimensions

, which was – to the authors’ best knowledge – not yet mentioned in the literature.

Corollary 4.11

(Invariance principle for

, weak disorder)
.

Let
\widehat{\beta}\;\geqslant\;0
such that weak disorder holds, i.e.
\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{\widehat{\beta},N}(0,0,N,\star)>0

\displaystyle\pi_{N}^{*}{\bf P}_{\widehat{\beta},N}^{\omega}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}{\bf P}\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}W\in\cdot\,\big),\qquad\text{as }N\to\infty,\quad\text{in }\mathbb{P}\text{-probability}\,,

with

4.3.
Local limit theorem for the polymer marginals

We want to close this section by proving the local limit theorem for the marginals of the polymer measure, Proposition
1.3
.
Recall from (
1.3
) that the finite-dimensional distributions of the discrete polymer measure can be written as

\displaystyle{\bf P}_{\beta_{N},N}^{\omega}(S_{m_{1}}=z_{1},\ldots,S_{m_{k}}=z_{k})=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}\prod_{j=1}^{k+1}{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j-1},z_{j-1}\mid m_{j},z_{j})\,q_{m_{j}-m_{j-1}}(z_{j}-z_{j-1}),

where
m_{0}=z_{0}=0
,
m_{k+1}=N
and
z_{k+1}=\star
.
Together with Proposition
2.1
and
[
17
, Theorem 2.12]
, this suffices to deduce Proposition
1.3
.

Proof of Proposition
1.3
.

By the local limit theorem, we know that
\tfrac{N}{2}\,q_{n_{i}-n_{i-1}}(z_{i}-z_{i-1})
converges to
p_{(t_{i}-t_{i-1})/2}(x_{i}-x_{i-1})
. It is only left to show convergence in distribution of the partition functions on the l.h.s. of (
1.4
).
We want to show that, as
N
diverges,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{1}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{k+1}{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j-1},z_{j-1}\mid m_{j},z_{j})-\prod_{j=1}^{k}Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j}^{-},\star,m_{j},z_{j})Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j},z_{j},m_{j}^{+},\star)\bigg\|_{1}\to 0,

(4.10)

where
(m_{j}^{\pm})_{j=1}^{k}
are non-negative integers such that

\displaystyle 0\;\leqslant\;m_{j}^{-}<m_{j}<m_{j}^{+}<m_{j+1}^{-}<N\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|m_{j}^{\pm}-m_{j}|}{N}>0.

For example, we can choose
m_{1}^{-}=0
and
m^{\pm}_{j}=\lfloor m_{j}\pm\tfrac{1}{3}|m_{j\pm 1}-m_{j}|\rfloor
for the remaining variables.

Using a chain of triangle inequalities, we will justify the convergence in (
4.10
).
First, we note that

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{1}{Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)}\prod_{j=1}^{k+1}{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j-1},z_{j-1}\mid m_{j},z_{j})-Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,\star,m_{1},z_{1})\prod_{j=2}^{k+1}{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j-1},z_{j-1}\mid m_{j},z_{j})\bigg\|_{1}

vanishes, as
N
tends to infinity.
The proof follows the same lines as the one of (
3.8
) in Lemma
3.2
.
Next, we replace the remaining point-to-point partition functions
({\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j-1},z_{j-1}\mid m_{j},z_{j}))_{j=2}^{k+1}
with their point-to-plane counterparts from Proposition
2.1
. For the sake of brevity, we restrict ourselves to
k=2
for the remainder of this proof, the general case follows using a telescopic sum argument in the subsequent step. We have

\displaystyle\bigg\|Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,\star,m_{1},z_{1})\prod_{j=2}^{3}{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j-1},z_{j-1}\mid m_{j},z_{j})

\displaystyle\qquad-Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,\star,m_{1},z_{1})\Big(Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{1},z_{1},m_{2}^{-},\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{1}^{+},\star,m_{2},z_{2})\Big)Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{2},z_{2},N,\star)\bigg\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|Z_{\beta_{N},N}(0,\star,m_{1},z_{1})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\|{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{1},z_{1}\mid m_{2},z_{2})-Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{1},z_{1},m_{2}^{-},\star)Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{1}^{+},\star,m_{2},z_{2})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\|Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{2},z_{2},N,\star)\|_{1}.

Here, we made use of the disorder’s independence on the disjoint time intervals
(0,m_{1}]
,
(m_{1},m_{2}]
and
(m_{2},N]
, and applied the fact that
{\gothfamily Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{k},z_{k}\mid N,\star)=Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{k},z_{k},N,\star)
. The middle term on the r.h.s. vanishes due to Proposition
2.1
, whereas the additional terms are all equal to one. This finally yields (
4.10
) by adding and subtracting the above introduced intermediate terms and applying the triangle inequality.

The last step consists of determining the limiting distribution of the partition functions in

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{k}Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j}^{-},\star,m_{j},z_{j})Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j},z_{j},m_{j}^{+},\star).

Using
[
17
, Theorem 2.12]
, see (
1.12
) and the discussion thereafter, we know the limit of such point-to-plane partition functions is given by independent log-normal random variables:

\displaystyle(Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j}^{-},\star,m_{j},z_{j}),Z_{\beta_{N},N}(m_{j},z_{j},m_{j}^{+},\star))_{j=1}^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}(:e^{Y^{-}(t_{j},x_{j})}:,\,:e^{Y^{+}(t_{j},x_{j})}:)_{j=1}^{k},

where
Y^{\pm}(t_{j},x_{j})
are independent centred Gaussian random variables with variance
\log(1-\widehat{\beta}^{2})^{-1}
.
∎

Appendix A
Transition kernel asymptotics

In the proof of Proposition
2.1
we need to handle the ratio of random walk transition probabilities. The following lemma allows us to either ignore such ratios or at least bound them uniformly:

Lemma A.1
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
r>0
,

(i)

let
a_{N}:=(\log N)^{\gamma-1}
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
, then we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\sqrt{N}B(x,r)\\
\text{s.t. }q_{N}(z)>0\end{subarray}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|N-n|<2N^{1-a_{N}}\\
|z-y|<2{N}^{1/2-a_{N}/4}\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{q_{n}(y)}{q_{N}(z)}-1\right|\to 0\quad\text{as }N\to\infty.

(A.1)

(ii)

there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\sqrt{N}B(x,r)\\
\text{s.t. }q_{N}(z)>0\end{subarray}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\;\geqslant\;N/k\\
y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{q_{n}(y)}{q_{N}(z)}\;\leqslant\;Ck

(A.2)

for all
N
large enough.

Proof.

(i)
We want to apply the local limit theorem for simple random walks
[
31
]
, which is why we write

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|N-n|<2N^{1-a_{N}}\\
|z-y|<2{N}^{1/2-a_{N}/4}\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{q_{n}(y)}{q_{N}(z)}-1\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N\,q_{N}(z)}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|N-n|<2N^{1-a_{N}}\\
|z-y|<2{N}^{1/2-a_{N}/4}\end{subarray}}\left|N\,q_{n}(y)-N\,q_{N}(z)\right|.

Because
N\,q_{N}(z)
converges uniformly in
z
and its limit is lower bounded by
2\,\inf_{\widetilde{x}\in B(x,r)}p_{1/2}(\widetilde{x})
whenever
q_{N}(z)>0
, we can ignore the factor in front of the supremum. Adding and subtracting
2\,p_{1/2}(z/\sqrt{N})
yields

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|N-n|<2N^{1-a_{N}}\\
|z-y|<2{N}^{1/2-a_{N}/4}\end{subarray}}\left|N\,q_{n}(y)-N\,q_{N}(z)\right|

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|N-n|<2N^{1-a_{N}}\\
|z-y|<2{N}^{1/2-a_{N}/4}\end{subarray}}\Big(\left|N\,q_{n}(y)-2\,p_{\frac{1}{2}}(z/\sqrt{N})\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left|2\,p_{\frac{1}{2}}(z/\sqrt{N})-N\,q_{N}(z)\right|\Big).

The second term on the r.h.s. vanishes uniformly in
z
by the local limit theorem. Because
y
and
z
are arbitrary close on the macroscopic scale, the first term vanishes for the same reason.

(ii) We begin by noting that for
z\in\sqrt{N}B(x,r)
with
q_{N}(z)>0
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\;\geqslant\;N/k\\
y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{q_{n}(y)}{q_{N}(z)}\;\leqslant\;k\,\frac{1}{N\,q_{N}(z)}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\;\geqslant\;N/k\\
y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}{n\,q_{n}(y)},

where we may again ignore the factor
N\,q_{N}(z)
for the same reason as in (i). Hence, it suffices to prove the existence of a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\;\geqslant\;N/k\\
y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}{n\,q_{n}(y)}\;\leqslant\;C\qquad\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}\,,

for
N
large enough.
We make the following choice for
C
:

\displaystyle\sup_{{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}}n\,q_{n}(y)

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\sup_{{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}}\left(|{n\,q_{n}(y)}-2p_{\frac{1}{2}}(y/\sqrt{n})|+|2p_{\frac{1}{2}}(y/\sqrt{n})|\right)

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\sup_{{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}}|{n\,q_{n}(y)}-2p_{\frac{1}{2}}(y/\sqrt{n})|+|2p_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)|=:C_{n}.

Since the first term on the r.h.s. converges in
n
by the local limit theorem,
C_{n}
is uniformly bounded in
n
.
Thus, we have

\sup_{n\;\geqslant\;N/k}\sup_{{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}}n\,q_{n}(y)\;\leqslant\;\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}C_{n}=:C<\infty.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Decay of remainders in the polynomial chaos expansion

We still owe the reader a rigorous justification for the exponential decay of second moments of
\widehat{Z}^{(k)}_{\beta_{N},N}
, which we use in the proofs of Lemma
2.7
and Corollary
2.8
.

Lemma B.1
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{Z}^{(k)}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{2}\right]\;\leqslant\;C\,k^{2}\left(\sigma_{N}^{2}R_{N}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}\,a_{N}\,,

where
C
is independent of
k
and
N
.

The proof of this statement can be found in
[
18
, Section 3.4]
.
The original proof makes use of more precise estimates to show that
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Z}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{2}]
decays like
a_{N}
. One can follow the same steps using less sophisticated estimates to get an uniform estimate on
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Z}^{(k)}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{2}]
in terms of
a_{N}
instead, which yields the same qualitative bound.
We sketch the argument for the sake of completeness:

Considering
\widehat{Z}^{(k)}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)
for some
k\;\leqslant\;N
,
there is at least one sample
(n_{j},z_{j})
outside the box
A_{N}^{+}(0,0)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{Z}^{(k)}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{2}\right]\;\leqslant\;\sigma_{N}^{2k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\;\leqslant\;l_{1},\ldots,l_{k}\;\leqslant\;N\\
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\mathds{1}_{l_{j}>\frac{1}{k}N^{1-a_{N}}}+\mathds{1}_{l_{j}\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{k}N^{1-a_{N}},\,|z_{j}|\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{k}N^{1/2-a_{N}/4}}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{l_{i}}^{2}(z_{i})\,,

where we extended the range of time-differences
l_{i}=n_{i}-n_{i-1}
to all of
\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Using once more the identity
\sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}q_{l}^{2}(z)=R_{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{Z}^{(k)}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{2}\right]\;\leqslant\;k\,\sigma_{N}^{2k}R_{N}^{k-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\;\leqslant\;l\;\leqslant\;N\\
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}\left(\mathds{1}_{l>\frac{1}{k}N^{1-a_{N}}}+\mathds{1}_{l\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{k}N^{1-a_{N}}\,,|z|\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{k}N^{1/2-a_{N}/4}}\right)q_{l}^{2}(z)\,.

Now, the contribution of the two indicator functions can be considered separately. We follow the exact same steps as in Section 3.4 of
[
18
]
:

•

For the contribution of large time-jumps, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R_{N}}\sum_{\frac{1}{k}N^{1-a_{N}}\;\leqslant\;l\;\leqslant\;N}q_{2l}(0)\;\leqslant\;C\frac{1}{R_{N}}\sum_{\frac{1}{k}N^{1-a_{N}}\;\leqslant\;l\;\leqslant\;N}\frac{1}{l}\;\leqslant\;C^{\prime}\,\frac{a_{N}\log N+\log k}{\log N}\;\leqslant\;2C^{\prime}\,k\,a_{N}\,,

where we used additionally the crude estimates
\tfrac{\log k}{\log N}\;\leqslant\;k\,a_{N}
in the last inequality.

•

On the other hand, the contribution of the second term is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R_{N}}\sum_{1\;\leqslant\;l\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{k}N^{1-a_{N}}}\sum_{|z|>\frac{1}{k}N^{1/2-a_{N}/4}}q_{l}^{2}(z)\;\leqslant\;Ce^{-\eta\frac{N^{\frac{a_{N}}{2}}}{k}}\,,

for some uniform
\eta>0
,
using Gaussian estimates for the simple random walk. Then, for
N
large enough

\displaystyle C\,k\,(\sigma_{N}^{2}R_{N})^{k}e^{-\eta\frac{N^{\frac{a_{N}}{2}}}{k}}\;\leqslant\;\begin{cases}C\,k(\sigma_{N}^{2}R_{N})^{k}e^{-\eta N^{\frac{a_{N}}{4}}}\,,&\quad\text{if }k\;\leqslant\;(N^{a_{N}/2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,\\
C\,k(\sigma_{N}^{2}R_{N})^{\frac{k}{2}}(\widehat{\beta}+\delta)^{N^{\frac{a_{N}}{4}}}\,,&\quad\text{if }k>(N^{a_{N}/2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,\end{cases}

(B.1)

where
\delta>0
small enough such that
\widehat{\beta}+\delta<1
.
Note that,
c^{N^{\frac{a_{N}}{4}}}=o(a_{N})
for any
c\in(0,1)
, because

\displaystyle(\log N)^{\gamma}{c^{N^{\frac{a_{N}}{4}}}}=(\log N)^{\gamma}c^{\exp(\frac{1}{4}(\log N)^{\gamma})}\;\leqslant\;(\log N)^{\gamma}c^{\frac{1}{4}(\log N)^{\gamma}}\to 0\,,\quad\text{ as }N\to\infty\,,

which includes in particular the case
c=\max\{\widehat{\beta}+\delta,e^{-\eta}\}
.

Adding up all above estimates yields the desired upper bound of
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Z}^{(k)}_{\beta_{N},N}(0,0,N,\star)^{2}]
from Lemma
B.1
.
