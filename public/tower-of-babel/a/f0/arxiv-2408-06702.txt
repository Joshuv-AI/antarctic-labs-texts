Optimizing RPL Routing Using Tabu Search to Improve Link Stability and Energy Consumption in IoT Networks
Mehran Tarif, Abbas Mirzaei, Babak Nouri-Moghaddam

Abstract
In the Internet of Things (IoT) networks, the Routing Protocol for Low-power and Lossy Networks (RPL) is a widely adopted standard due to its efficiency in managing resource-constrained and energy-limited nodes. However, persistent challenges such as high energy consumption, unstable links, and suboptimal routing continue to hinder network performance, affecting both the longevity of the network and the reliability of data transmission. This paper proposes an enhanced RPL routing mechanism by integrating the Tabu Search optimization algorithm to address these issues. The proposed approach focuses on optimizing the parent and child selection process in the RPL protocol, leveraging a composite cost function that incorporates critical parameters, including Residual Energy, Transmission Energy, Distance to Sink, Hop Count, Expected Transmission Count (ETX), and Link Stability Rate. Through extensive simulations, we demonstrate that our method significantly improves link stability, reduces energy consumption, and enhances the packet delivery ratio, leading to a more efficient and longer-lasting IoT network. The findings suggest that Tabu Search can effectively balance the trade-offs inherent in IoT routing, providing a practical solution for improving the overall performance of RPL-based networks.



Keywords: Internet of Things, RPL, Tabu Search Algorithm, Link Stability

Proposed Idea

In this paper, we propose a method based on the Tabu Search algorithm to optimize the parent and child selection process in the RPL protocol. This optimization aims to reduce energy consumption, improve link stability, and increase network lifetime. This method uses a composite cost function that includes parameters such as Residual Energy, Transmission Energy, Distance to Sink, Hop Count, ETX, and Link Stability Rate to search for the best possible routes in the network.

5
Formulation of the Optimality Cost Functional

Let us consider a network topology abstracted as a directed graph
G=(V,E)
, where
V
represents the set of vertices corresponding to the nodes in the network, and
E
denotes the set of directed edges corresponding to the communication links between these nodes. For a particular routing path
P\subset G
, we define the optimality cost functional
\mathcal{C}(P)
as a comprehensive weighted sum of several key metrics, each of which encapsulates a fundamental aspect of the network’s performance and operational efficiency.

\mathcal{C}(P)=\sum_{e\in P}\left(\lambda_{1}\cdot\frac{1}{E_{r}(e)}+\lambda_{2}\cdot E_{t}(e)+\lambda_{3}\cdot\frac{1}{d(e)}+\lambda_{4}\cdot h(e)+\lambda_{5}\cdot\text{ETX}(e)+\lambda_{6}\cdot\frac{1}{L_{s}(e)}\right)

(1)

In this expression,
e\in P
refers to an individual edge along the path
P
, and the terms within the summation are defined as follows:

•

E_{r}(e)
: The residual energy available at the node corresponding to edge
e
. This metric is inversely proportional to the cost, reflecting the desirability of routing through nodes with higher remaining energy to prolong network longevity.

•

E_{t}(e)
: The energy expenditure required for data transmission across edge
e
. This term directly influences the cost, as minimizing energy consumption is paramount for maintaining network sustainability in energy-constrained environments.

•

d(e)
: The Euclidean distance between the nodes connected by edge
e
. The inverse of this distance is included in the cost function to favor shorter transmission distances, which typically correlate with reduced energy consumption and higher transmission reliability.

•

h(e)
: The hop count increment associated with traversing edge
e
. Hop count serves as a proxy for the number of intermediary nodes that data must pass through before reaching its destination, where a lower hop count is generally preferred to minimize latency and potential points of failure.

•

\text{ETX}(e)
: The Expected Transmission Count, representing the average number of transmissions needed to achieve successful data delivery over edge
e
. A lower ETX value indicates higher link quality and reliability, thus reducing the overall cost.

•

L_{s}(e)
: The Link Stability Rate, which quantifies the historical reliability and stability of the communication link represented by edge
e
. Higher stability rates are inversely proportional to the cost, encouraging the selection of more reliable links to enhance network robustness.

The coefficients
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{6}
are positive real numbers, i.e.,
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
for
i=1,2,\dots,6
. These coefficients are critical as they determine the relative importance of each parameter within the cost function. The vector of these coefficients, denoted by
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{6})
, must satisfy the following normalization constraint:

\sum_{i=1}^{6}\lambda_{i}=1

(2)

This constraint ensures that the cost function
\mathcal{C}(P)
remains a balanced representation of all involved factors, preventing any single parameter from disproportionately influencing the routing decision unless specifically intended by the network’s operational priorities.

The optimal path selection problem can then be formulated as an optimization problem, where the goal is to find the path
P^{*}
that minimizes the cost functional
\mathcal{C}(P)
across all feasible paths in the network graph
G
:

P^{*}=\arg\min_{P\subset G}\mathcal{C}(P)

(3)

Here,
P^{*}
denotes the path that yields the lowest possible value of the cost functional, thereby representing the most efficient and reliable route according to the defined metrics.

This optimization framework is designed to capture the intricate trade-offs between various performance criteria, such as energy efficiency, link reliability, and hop count, which are inherently intertwined in the operation of IoT networks. The multi-faceted nature of the cost function allows it to adapt dynamically to different network conditions and requirements. For instance, by adjusting the weighting coefficients
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, the network can prioritize energy conservation in scenarios with limited power resources, or it can emphasize link stability and reliability in mission-critical applications where data integrity and transmission success are paramount.

The theoretical underpinning of this approach is grounded in the principles of multi-objective optimization, where the cost functional
\mathcal{C}(P)
represents a scalarization of the vector of objectives. The chosen path
P^{*}
is thus a Pareto-optimal solution because no other path exists that can simultaneously improve one objective without worsening another. This property ensures that the selected route is globally optimal concerning the weighted sum of the involved metrics rather than locally optimal for any single metric.

In practical implementation, the optimization problem may be solved using heuristic or metaheuristic algorithms, such as Tabu Search or Genetic Algorithms, which are well-suited to navigating the complex and potentially non-convex search space characteristic of large-scale IoT networks. These algorithms iteratively explore the solution space, guided by the cost functional
\mathcal{C}(P)
, and employ mechanisms such as memory structures or evolutionary operators to escape local minima and converge towards a global optimum.

In summary, the proposed optimality cost functional provides a mathematically rigorous and flexible framework for routing in IoT networks. It enables the dynamic selection of routes that balance multiple performance criteria in response to the network’s evolving conditions and objectives. The mathematical formulation encapsulates the essence of the trade-offs inherent in IoT routing, ensuring that the chosen path is efficient regarding energy and resources and reliable and resilient in the face of network uncertainties.

5.1
Lemma

Lemma:
The cost functional
\mathcal{C}(P)
defined above is a convex function over the set of all feasible paths
P\subset G
, given that each component function
\frac{1}{E_{r}(e)},E_{t}(e),\\
\frac{1}{d(e)},h(e),\text{ETX}(e),
and
\frac{1}{L_{s}(e)}
is convex over their respective domains.

5.2
Proof

Proof:
To prove the convexity of
\mathcal{C}(P)
, we must demonstrate that for any two feasible paths
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
in the graph
G
and any
\theta\in[0,1]
, the following inequality holds:

\mathcal{C}(\theta P_{1}+(1-\theta)P_{2})\leq\theta\mathcal{C}(P_{1})+(1-\theta)\mathcal{C}(P_{2})

(4)

Since
\mathcal{C}(P)
is a weighted sum of the individual functions
f_{i}(e)
with positive coefficients
\lambda_{i}
, and each
f_{i}(e)
is convex, the functional
\mathcal{C}(P)
is convex by the definition of convexity and the preservation of convexity under positive weighted sums.

Step 1:
Consider the convexity of each component function
f_{i}(e)
:

•

\frac{1}{E_{r}(e)}
is convex because the reciprocal of a linear function is convex over a positive domain.

•

E_{t}(e)
is convex if energy consumption is a linear or convex function of distance and power.

•

\frac{1}{d(e)}
is convex because the reciprocal of a linear function is convex.

•

h(e)
is linear and therefore convex.

•

\text{ETX}(e)
, being a mean transmission count, is convex as it represents a weighted sum of success probabilities.

•

\frac{1}{L_{s}(e)}
is convex as it is the reciprocal of a stability function.

Step 2:
Given that
\mathcal{C}(P)
is a sum of these convex functions, the convexity of
\mathcal{C}(P)
follows directly from the convexity of each
f_{i}(e)
and the non-negative weights
\lambda_{i}
.

\mathcal{C}(\theta P_{1}+(1-\theta)P_{2})=\sum_{e\in\theta P_{1}+(1-\theta)P_{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{6}\lambda_{i}\cdot f_{i}(e)\right)\leq\theta\sum_{e\in P_{1}}\mathcal{C}(e)+(1-\theta)\sum_{e\in P_{2}}\mathcal{C}(e)

(5)

Therefore,
\mathcal{C}(P)
is convex, completing the proof.

5.3
Induction Proof for Path Optimization

Induction Hypothesis:
Assume that for a path
P_{n}
of length
n
, the path cost functional
\mathcal{C}(P_{n})
has been minimized according to the given criteria.

Base Case:
For
n=1
, the path consists of a single edge, and the depreciation of
\mathcal{C}(P_{1})
is trivial, as it corresponds to evaluating
\mathcal{C}(e_{1})
for the single edge
e_{1}
.

Inductive Step:
Assume the hypothesis holds for a path
P_{n}
of length
n
. Consider a path
P_{n+1}
formed by adding an edge
e_{n+1}
to
P_{n}
. The cost of the extended path
P_{n+1}
is given by:

\mathcal{C}(P_{n+1})=\mathcal{C}(P_{n})+\mathcal{C}(e_{n+1})

(6)

By the inductive hypothesis,
\mathcal{C}(P_{n})
is minimized. To minimize
\mathcal{C}(P_{n+1})
, we choose
e_{n+1}
such that
\mathcal{C}(e_{n+1})
is minimal while preserving the convexity conditions discussed earlier.

Thus, by the principle of mathematical induction, the path cost functional
\mathcal{C}(P)
is minimized for all
n
, proving the optimality of the selected path.

: The RPL protocol served as the baseline for routing, with and without the Tabu Search optimization. The Tabu Search optimization aimed to enhance the RPL’s performance by selecting more energy-efficient and stable routes.

Energy Consumption
: Nodes were initialized with 1000 Joules of energy, with energy consumption modeled based on data transmission and reception activities. The energy model assumed battery-powered nodes typical of IoT deployments.

: The protocol’s performance was evaluated using key metrics, including PDR, energy consumption, link stability, and throughput. These metrics comprehensively assessed the protocol’s efficiency, reliability, and sustainability.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed an enhanced RPL routing protocol by integrating the Tabu Search optimization algorithm to address the challenges of high energy consumption, unstable links, and suboptimal routing in IoT networks. Through extensive simulations conducted using NS-2.34, the results demonstrated that the Tabu Search-optimized RPL (TABURPL) outperforms other RPL variants in several vital aspects, including significant improvements in energy efficiency and reduced routing control overhead, both of which are critical for prolonging network lifetime and minimizing congestion. Although TABURPL tends to select longer paths regarding hop count, this trade-off is justified by the protocol’s ability to prioritize link stability and energy efficiency, ensuring more reliable data transmission even under challenging network conditions. Future research could explore hybrid optimization techniques, dynamic weighting strategies for cost function parameters, and the integration of machine learning models to further enhance TABURPL’s adaptability and performance. Additionally, extending the evaluation to more extensive and dynamic network topologies and real-world IoT deployments will be essential in validating the scalability and practical applicability of the proposed approach in diverse IoT environments.
