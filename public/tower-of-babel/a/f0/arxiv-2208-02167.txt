Abstract
. We study the ℓ 1 \ell^{1} -summability of functions in the d d -dimensional torus 𝕋 d {\mathbb{T}}^{d} and so-called ℓ 1 \ell^{1} -invariant functions. Those are functions on the torus whose Fourier coefficients depend only on the ℓ 1 \ell^{1} -norm of their indices. Such functions are characterized as divided differences that have cos ⁡ θ 1 , … , cos ⁡ θ d \cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d} as knots for ( θ 1 ​ … , θ d ) ∈ 𝕋 d ({\theta}_{1}\,\ldots,{\theta}_{d})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d} . It leads us to consider the d d -dimensional Fourier series of univariate B-splines with respect to its knots, which turns out to enjoy a simple bi-orthogonality that can be used to obtain an orthogonal series of the B-spline function.

1.
Introduction

We consider a problem originating from the
\ell^{1}
-summability of multivariate Fourier series. Let
f
be a

2\pi
-periodic function in
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
and let
\hat{f}_{\alpha}
be the Fourier coefficient of
f
with the multi-index

{\alpha}=({\alpha}_{1},\ldots,{\alpha}_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Let

S_{n}^{(1)}(f;{\theta})=\sum_{|{\alpha}|\leq n}\hat{f}_{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\alpha}\cdot{\theta}},\quad{\theta}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},

be the
n
-th
\ell^{1}
-partial sum of its Fourier series, where
|{\alpha}|=|{\alpha}|_{1}=|{\alpha}_{1}|+\cdots+|{\alpha}_{d}|
, so that the
summation is over indices in the
\ell^{1}
-ball of radius
n
. The
\ell^{1}
-summability has been studied in

[
2
,
5
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
and it is closely related to the summability of Fourier series in orthogonal
polynomials on the cube
[
12
]
. The Dirichlet kernel of
S_{n}^{(1)}(f)
turns out to be a divided difference to
be defined below in the form

D_{n}^{(1)}({\theta})=[\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d}]G_{n,d},\qquad{\theta}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},

where
G_{n,d}
is a function of one variable as shown in
[
2
,
12
]
(see (
2.2
) in the next section).
The divided difference can be written as an integral with a Peano kernel; in particular, for a
(d-1)
-times differentiable function
F:[-1,1]\to{\mathbb{C}}
,

[\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d}]F=\int_{-1}^{1}F^{(d-1)}(u)M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})\mathrm{d}u,

where
u\mapsto M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})
is the B-spline function, which is a piecewise polynomial
function in
C^{d-2}([-1,1])
with
\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d}
as its knots (see the next section for its definition).
Motivated by the
\ell^{1}
-summability and functions defined by the above divided difference, we call a function

\ell^{1}
-invariant if
\hat{f}_{\alpha}=\hat{f}_{\beta}
whenever
|{\alpha}|=|{\beta}|
and study properties of such functions.

Our analysis is partially motivated by the study in
[
2
]
, where the
\ell^{1}
-summability of the Fourier
transform in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, defined by

R_{\rho,\mathrm{d}}^{(1)}(f;x)=\int_{|v|_{1}\leq\rho}\hat{f}(v)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}v\cdot x}\mathrm{d}v,\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\rho\geq 0,

is treated and its associated Dirichlet kernel is shown to be given as a divided difference,

{\mathfrak{D}}_{\rho,\mathrm{d}}(x)=\left[x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{d}^{2}\right]{\mathfrak{G}}_{\rho,d},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where
{\mathfrak{G}}_{\rho,d}
is a function of one variable. The Fourier transform of the B-spline function

x\mapsto M_{d-1}(u|x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{d}^{2})
, considered as a function of its knots, is analyzed in
[
2
]
,
which turns out to enjoy a rich structure and provides necessary tools for studying the class of
\ell^{1}
-invariant
functions
f(\|\cdot\|_{1})
defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In particular, it leads to a characterization of
f:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}
so that
x\mapsto f(\|\cdot\|_{1})
is a positive definite function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

We will show that the
\ell^{1}
-invariant functions on the torus are all given by divided differences with
knots
\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d}
and we will study the Fourier series of the
B
-spline
{\theta}\mapsto M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})
, which is
\ell^{1}
-invariant. While the Fourier transform of the B-spline

x\mapsto M_{d-1}(u|x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{d}^{2})
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfies an integral recursive relation in dimension
d
, the
Fourier coefficients of the
B
-spline
x\mapsto M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})
on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
satisfy
a somewhat surprising biorthogonal relation with a family of polynomials. Let
m_{n,d}
denote the Fourier
coefficients of the
B
-spline function with the index
|{\alpha}|=n
. Then there is a sequence of polynomials

h_{n,d}
, given in terms of the Gegenbauer polynomials, such that
\{m_{n,d}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
and

\{h_{n,d}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
are biorthogonal in the sense that

\int_{-1}^{1}m_{n,d}(u)h_{\ell,d}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u=\delta_{n,\ell},\qquad n,\ell=0,1,2,\ldots.

This orthogonal relation can be used to derive the Fourier orthogonal series of the B-spline function in the
Gegenbauer polynomials explicitly; the first term of the series gives, in particular, that

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})(d-1)!}(1-u^{2})_{+}^{\frac{d-2}{2}},

an identity that appears to be new. We will also give a characterization of
\ell^{1}
-invariant functions that are
either positive definite or strictly positive definite on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.

The paper is organized as follows. We recall the definition and basic properties of
\ell^{1}
-summability in the
next section and establish several necessary identities. The Fourier orthogonal series of the B-spline with
respect to its knot is given in the third section. The positive definite functions of
\ell^{1}
-invariant functions are
discussed in the fourth section.

2.
\ell^{1}
-summability on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}

Let
f
be a
2\pi
-periodic function defined on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. If
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
, then the Fourier series of
f
is
defined by

f(x)=\sum_{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\hat{f}_{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\alpha}\cdot x},\;x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\quad\hbox{with}\quad\hat{f}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}f(y)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\alpha}\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}y,\;\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

We study the class of periodic functions that we call
\ell^{1}
-invariant.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
f:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called
\ell^{1}
-invariant if

\hat{f}_{\alpha}=\hat{f}_{{\beta}}\quad\hbox{whevever $|{\alpha}|=|{\beta}|$ for ${\alpha},{\beta}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}$}.

We denote the Fourier coefficient
\hat{f}_{\alpha}
of such a function by
\hat{f}_{|{\alpha}|}
.

If
f
is
\ell^{1}
-invariant, then its Fourier series is of the form

(2.1)

f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{f}_{n}E_{n}(x),\qquad E_{n}(x)=\sum_{|{\alpha}|=n}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\alpha}\cdot x},\quad x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},

where
|{\alpha}|
is the
\ell^{1}
-norm, that is
|{\alpha}|:=|{\alpha}_{1}|+\cdots+|{\alpha}_{d}|
, of
{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

A function
f
on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
is called
\ell^{1}
-summable if its partial sum
S_{n}^{(1)}f
over the expanding

\ell^{1}
-ball, defined by

S_{n}^{(1)}f(x)=\sum_{|{\alpha}|\leq n}\hat{f}_{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\alpha}\cdot x},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},

converges to
f
. The partial sum can be written as an integral operator

S_{n}^{(1)}f(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}f(y)D_{n,d}(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y=f*D_{n,d}(x),

where the kernel
D_{n,d}
is the analog of the Dirichlet kernel defined by

D_{n,d}(y):=\sum_{|{\alpha}|\leq n}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\alpha}\cdot y},\qquad y\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}.

It is shown in
[
2
,
12
]
that the kernel
D_{n,d}

can be written as a divided difference

D_{n,d}(x)=[\cos x_{1},\ldots,\cos x_{d}]G_{n,d},

where
G_{n,d}
is a univariant function defined by

(2.2)

G_{n,d}(\cos{\theta})=(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor}2\cos\tfrac{{\theta}}{2}(\sin{\theta})^{d-2}\times\begin{cases}\cos(n+\tfrac{1}{2}){\theta}&\hbox{for $d$ even},\\
\sin(n+\tfrac{1}{2}){\theta}&\hbox{for $d$ odd}.\end{cases}

We briefly recall the notion of a divided difference of a function that is at least continuous. Let
f
be a real or
complex function on
{\mathbb{R}}
, and let
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. The
m
-th divided difference of
f
at the (pairwise distinct)
knots,
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
in
{\mathbb{R}}
is defined inductively as

[x_{0}]\,f=f(x_{0})\quad\hbox{and}\quad[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]\,f=\frac{[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m-1}]\,f-[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]\,f}{x_{0}-x_{m}}.

The divided difference is a symmetric function of the knots. The knots of the divided difference may coalesce.
In particular, if all knots coalesce and if the function is sufficiently differentiable, then the divided difference collapses to

(2.3)

[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]f=\frac{f^{(m)}(x_{0})}{m!}\quad\hbox{if $x_{0}=x_{1}=\cdots=x_{m}$}.

Our analysis depends heavily on an
integral representation of the divided difference, for which we need the definition of B-spline.
For
x_{0}<\cdots<x_{m}
, the B-spline of order
m
with knots
x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}
is defined by

{\mathbb{R}}\ni u\quad\to\quad M_{m}(u|x_{0},\ldots,x_{m})=[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]\left\{\frac{(\,\cdot\,-u)_{+}^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\right\}.

The B-spline vanishes outside the interval
(x_{0},x_{n})
and it is strictly positive on the interval itself, and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{m}(u|x_{0},\ldots,x_{m})\;\,\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{m!}.

For better reference, we state the integral representation of the divided difference as a lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be
m
-times continuously differentiable. Then

[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]\,f=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{(m)}(u)M_{m}(u|x_{0},\ldots,x_{m})\,\mathrm{d}u.

We shall also need the B-splines’ recurrence relation (Powell, 1982)

\displaystyle M_{m+1}(u|x_{0},\ldots,x_{m},x_{m+1})\,

\displaystyle=\frac{(u-x_{0})M_{m}(u|x_{0},\ldots,x_{m})}{x_{m+1}-x_{0}}

\displaystyle+\frac{(x_{m+1}-u)M_{m}(u|x_{1},\ldots,x_{m+1})}{x_{m+1}-x_{0}}.

We first write the function
E_{n}
as an integral against the B-spline. We will need the Gegenbauer polynomials,
which are orthogonal polynomials with respect to the weight function

w_{\lambda}(t)=(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}},\quad{\lambda}>-\tfrac{1}{2},

on the interval
[-1,1]
. The Gegenbauer polynomial of degree
n
is denoted by
C_{n}^{\lambda}
and normalized by

C_{n}^{\lambda}(1)=\frac{(2{\lambda})_{n}}{n!}
. The Gegenbauer polynomials satisfy the orthogonality

c_{\lambda}\int_{-1}^{1}C_{n}^{\lambda}(t)C_{m}^{\lambda}(t)w_{\lambda}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{{\lambda}}{n+{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(1)\delta_{n,m},\qquad n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},

where
c_{\lambda}
is a constant so that
c_{\lambda}\int_{-1}^{1}w_{\lambda}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=1
and
(a)_{n}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)

denotes the Pochhammer symbol. For convenience, we also define

Z_{n}^{\lambda}(t):=\frac{n+{\lambda}}{{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(t).

The generating function of the Gegenbauer polynomials is given by

\frac{1}{(1-2rt+r^{2})^{{\lambda}}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}^{\lambda}(t)r^{n},\qquad 0\leq r<1.

Throughout this paper we define
C_{n}^{\lambda}(t)=0
whenever
n<0
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
\theta=({\theta}_{1},\ldots,{\theta}_{d})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, the function
E_{n}
satisfies

(2.4)

\displaystyle E_{n}(\theta)\,

\displaystyle=[\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d}]H_{n,d}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}h_{n,d}(u)M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})\,\mathrm{d}u,

where
H_{n,d}
and
h_{n,d}
are defined by

(2.5)

\displaystyle H_{n,d}(\cos{\theta})=2(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor}(\sin{\theta})^{d-1}\times\begin{cases}-\sin(n{\theta})&\hbox{for $d$ even},\\
\cos(n{\theta})&\hbox{for $d$ odd},\end{cases}

and
h_{n,d}
is a polynomial of degree
n
given by

(2.6)

\displaystyle h_{n,d}(u)\,

\displaystyle=(d-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{d}(-1)^{j}\binom{d}{j}C_{n-2j}^{d}(u)

\displaystyle=(d-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{d-1}{j}Z_{n-2j}^{d-1}(u).

Proof.

By its definition,
E_{n}({\theta})=D_{n}({\theta})-D_{n-1}({\theta})
, so that
E_{n}({\theta})
is a divided difference of

H_{n,d}=G_{n,d}-G_{n-1,d}
, from which (
2.4
) follows readily with
h_{n,d}=H_{n,d}^{(d-1)}
.
Moreover, the identity (
2.5
) is an immediate consequence of (
2.2
) and elementary
trigonometric identities. Now, it is shown in
[
2
]
that

g_{n,d}(t)=G_{n,d}^{(d-1)}(t)=f_{n,d}(t)+f_{n-1,d}(t),

where
f_{n,d}
is given in terms of the Gegenbauer polynomials by

f_{n,d}(t)=(d-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{d-1}{j}C_{n-2j}^{d}(t).

Using the relation
[
7
, (4.7.29)]

(n+{\lambda})C_{n}^{{\lambda}}(t)={\lambda}\left[C_{n}^{{\lambda}+1}(t)-C_{n-2}^{{\lambda}+1}(t)\right]

with
{\lambda}=d-1
, we then obtain

h_{n,d}=f_{n,d}-f_{n-2,d}=(d-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{d}(-1)^{j}\binom{d-1}{j}\left[C_{n-2j}^{d}(t)-C_{n-2j-2}^{d}(t)\right]

which is the second expression of
h_{n,d}
in (
2.6
) by recursion with
Z_{n}^{d}
. Furthermore, the first identity in (
2.6
)
follows from
\binom{d-1}{j}+\binom{d-1}{j-1}=\binom{d}{j}
and

h_{n,d}=f_{n,d}-f_{n-2,d}=(d-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{d}(-1)^{j}\left(\binom{d-1}{j}+\binom{d-1}{j-1}\right)C_{n-2j}^{d},

where we define for convenience
\binom{d-1}{m}=0
if
m=-1
or
m=d
.
∎

Let
N_{d}(n)=\#\{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}:|{\alpha}|=n\}
be the cardinality of the set
\{{\alpha}:|{\alpha}|=n\}
. Then,

N_{d}(n)=E_{n}(0)
. As a consequence of the identities (
2.4
) and (
2.6
), we obtain

N_{d}(n)=E_{n}(0)=\frac{h_{n,d}(1)}{(d-1)!}=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\frac{(-d)_{j}(2d)_{n-2j}}{j!(n-2j)!},

where
(a)_{n}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)
is the Pochhammer symbol.
The last sum can be written as a hypergeometric
{}_{3}F_{2}
function evaluated at
1
,
but the series is
not balanced so it does not have a closed-form formula. The first values of
N(n,d)
are given below

N_{2}(n)=4n,\quad N_{3}(n)=2n^{2}+1,\quad N_{4}(n)=\frac{4}{9}n(n^{2}+2).

The function
h_{n,d}
satisfies a generating function identity which we state as the following

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
0\leq r<1
. Then

(2.7)

(d-1)!\frac{(1-r^{2})^{d}}{(1-2ru+r^{2})^{d}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h_{n,d}(u)r^{n}.

Proof.

By the explicit formula of
h_{n,d}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(d-1)!}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h_{n,d}(u)r^{n}\,

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{d}(-1)^{j}\binom{d}{j}C_{n-2j}^{d}(u)r^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d}(-1)^{j}\binom{d}{j}\sum_{n=2j}^{\infty}C_{n-2j}^{d}(u)r^{n-2j}r^{2j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d}(-1)^{j}\binom{d}{j}r^{2j}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}^{d}(u)r^{n}

\displaystyle=(1-r^{2})^{d}\frac{1}{(1-2ru+r^{2})^{d}},

where we have used the generating function of the Gegenbauer polynomials.
∎

Our next result is of interest in itself, which gives an explicit formula for the divided difference of the function

P_{r}(t):=\frac{1}{1-2rt+r^{2}},\quad 0\leq r<1,\quad t\in[-1,1].

Proposition 2.5
.

For
0\leq r<1
,

(2.8)

\displaystyle(d-1)!\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1}{(1-2ru+r^{2})^{d}}

\displaystyle M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-2r\cos{\theta}_{i}+r^{2})}.

In particular,

(2.9)

[\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d}]P_{r}=\frac{(2r)^{d-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-2r\cos{\theta}_{i}+r^{2})}.

Proof.

We start with the elementary identity

\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-2r\cos\phi+r^{2}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r^{n}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\phi}.

Reorganizing the
d
-fold product of this identity and setting
{\theta}=({\theta}_{1},\ldots,{\theta}_{d})
as above, we obtain the equalities

(2.10)

\displaystyle\frac{(1-r^{2})^{d}}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-2r\cos{\theta}_{i}+r^{2})}\,

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r^{n}\sum_{|{\alpha}|=n}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\alpha}\cdot{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r^{n}E_{n}({\theta})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r^{n}[\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d}]H_{n,d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r^{n}\int_{-1}^{1}h_{n,d}(u)M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})\,\mathrm{d}u,

from which the identity (
2.8
) follows from the generating function of
h_{n,d}
. Now, it is easy to verify that

P_{r}^{(d-1)}(u)=\frac{(d-1)!(2r)^{d-1}}{(1-2ru+r^{2})^{d}},

so that the left-hand side of (
2.8
) can be identified with the divided difference of
P_{r}
,
which gives (
2.9
).
∎

3.
Fourier series of B-spline with respect to its knots

As a function of its knots, the B-spline function is a periodic function on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,

{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\ni{\theta}\mapsto M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}},

for each
u
, and we also define for convenience

{\mathcal{M}}_{d}({\alpha};{\theta}):=M_{d-1}(\cos\alpha|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d}).

Studying this case is sufficient since for
|u|\geq 1
,
M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})=0
by definition, for all
{\theta}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. We first show that it is an
integrable function on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
u\in(-1,1)
, the function
{\theta}\mapsto M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})
is in
L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
.

Proof.

Let
u=\cos{\alpha}
for
0<{\alpha}<\pi
be fixed. Since the function
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}({\alpha};\cdot)
is obvious even in each of
its variables, we only need to consider
{\theta}\in[0,\pi]^{d}
. Furthermore, the divided difference is a symmetric
function of its knots, the function
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}({\alpha};\cdot)
is a symmetric function and it is nonnegative, so
we only need to show that it is an
L^{1}
function on the domain

\triangle_{d}=\{{\theta}=({\theta}_{1},\ldots,{\theta}_{d})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}:0\leq{\theta}_{d}\leq{\theta}_{d-1}\leq\cdots\leq{\theta}_{1}\leq\pi\}.

Indeed, the above consideration leads readily to

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}=2^{d}d!\int_{\triangle_{d}}M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}.

We start with the case
d=2
; the univariate case is trivial by continuity and
compact support. On the domain
\triangle_{2}
, the function is given by

{\mathcal{M}}_{2}({\alpha};{\theta})=\frac{\chi_{[{\theta}_{1},{\theta}_{2}]}({\alpha})}{\cos{\theta}_{2}-\cos{\theta}_{1}}=\begin{cases}0&{\alpha}\leq{\theta}_{2}\\
\frac{1}{\cos{\theta}_{2}-\cos{\theta}_{1}}&{\theta}_{2}<{\alpha}<{\theta}_{1}\\
0&{\alpha}\geq{\theta}_{1}\end{cases}.

Hence, it follows readily that

\displaystyle\int_{\triangle_{2}}{\mathcal{M}}_{2}({\alpha};{\theta})\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}\,

\displaystyle=\int_{\alpha}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{\cos{\theta}_{2}-\cos{\theta}_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\alpha}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{2\sin\frac{{\theta}_{1}-{\theta}_{2}}{2}\sin\frac{{\theta}_{1}+{\theta}_{2}}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi}{\min\{\sin\frac{{\alpha}}{2},\cos\frac{{\alpha}}{2}\}}\int_{\alpha}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{{\theta}_{1}-{\theta}_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}_{1}

by elementary trigonometric inequalities. The last integral is evidently bounded for
0<{\alpha}<\pi
, so that

{\mathcal{M}}_{1}({\alpha};{\theta})\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
for
0<{\alpha}<\pi
.

For
d>2
, we use induction on
d
and the already stated recurrence relation from above for B-splines. Since

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{d+1}(\alpha|{\theta})=\,

\displaystyle\frac{\cos\alpha-\cos{\theta}_{1}}{\cos{\theta}_{d+1}-\cos{\theta}_{1}}{\mathcal{M}}_{d}(\alpha|{\theta}_{1},\ldots,{\theta}_{d})

\displaystyle+\frac{\cos{\theta}_{d+1}-\cos\alpha}{\cos{\theta}_{d+1}-\cos{\theta}_{1}}{\mathcal{M}}_{d}(\alpha|{\theta}_{2},\ldots,{\theta}_{d+1})

and
{\mathcal{M}}_{d+1}(\alpha|{\theta})=0
if
\alpha\not\in({\theta}_{1},{\theta}_{d+1})
, it follows that for
\alpha\in[0,\pi]
and

\theta\in\triangle_{d}
,

{\mathcal{M}}_{d+1}(\alpha|{\theta})\leq{\mathcal{M}}_{d}(\alpha|{\theta}_{1},\ldots,{\theta}_{d})+{\mathcal{M}}_{d}(\alpha|{\theta}_{2},\ldots,{\theta}_{d+1}).

Consequently, the integrability of
{\mathcal{M}}_{d+1}({\alpha}|{\theta})
follows eventually from the case
d=1
by induction.
∎

Since
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}({\alpha};\cdot)
is a nonnegative integrable function, we can expand it into multiple Fourier series,
which leads us to consider the Fourier coefficients of the B-spline function as a function of its knots. More
interestingly, we consider the
\ell^{1}
-sum of its Fourier coefficients.

Definition 3.2
.

For
d\geq 2
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we define

m_{n,d}(u):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})\frac{E_{n}({\theta})}{N_{d}(n)}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}.

By the definition of
E_{n}({\theta})
,
m_{n,d}
is the
\ell^{1}
mean of the Fourier transform of the B-spline function

{\theta}\mapsto M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})
with respect to its knots.

Theorem 3.3
.

The family of functions
\{m_{n,d}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
and the family
\{h_{n,d}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
are biorthogonal;
more precisely,

(3.1)

\int_{-1}^{1}m_{n,d}(u)h_{n^{\prime},d}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u=\delta_{n,n^{\prime}},\qquad n,n^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

Proof.

Multiplying the first identity of (
2.10
) by
E_{n}({\theta})
and integrating over
{\theta}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, we obtain

r^{n}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\frac{(1-r^{2})^{d}}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-2r\cos{\theta}_{i}+r^{2})}\frac{E_{n}({\theta})}{N_{d}(n)}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}.

Using (
2.8
) and exchanging the order of integrals on the right-hand side, we obtain

\displaystyle r^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-1)!}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{(1-r^{2})^{d}}{(1-2ru+r^{2})^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})\frac{E_{n}({\theta})}{N_{d}(n)}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=(d-1)!\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{(1-r^{2})^{d}}{(1-2ru+r^{2})^{d}}m_{n,d}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}h_{k,d}(u)m_{n,d}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\,r^{k},

where the last step follows from (
2.7
). Since the above identity holds for
|r|<1
, comparing
the coefficients of
r^{n}
proves (
3.1
) by linear independence.
∎

Using the orthogonality, we can now derive a series expansion of
m_{n,d}
. Let us start with
d=2
.

Proposition 3.4
.

For
n=0,1,2,\ldots
,
m_{n,2}(u)=0
if
|u|\geq 1
, and furthermore

(3.2)

m_{n,2}(\cos{\alpha})=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((n+2k+1){\alpha})}{n+2k+1},\qquad 0<{\alpha}<\pi.

Proof.

Let
n\geq 0
be fixed. Since
m_{n,2}(\pm 1)=0
, we assume that
m_{n,2}(u)
contains a factor
\sqrt{1-u^{2}}

and takes the form

m_{n,2}(u)=\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\frac{U_{n+2k}(u)}{n+2k+1},

where the coefficients
a_{k}
are real numbers that will be determined by the biorthogonality of (
3.1
) and
U_{n}
are as usual the Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind, satisfying
U_{n}=C^{1}_{n}
.
Using the orthogonality of
U_{n}
,

\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}U_{n}(t)U_{m}(t)\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t=\delta_{n,m},\qquad n,m\geq 0,

and the second explicit formula, in (
2.6
),

h_{\ell,2}(u)=(\ell+1)U_{\ell}(u)-(\ell-1)U_{\ell-2}(u),\qquad\ell\geq 0,

we see that (
3.1
) becomes, for
\ell=0,1,\ldots
,

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell,n}=\int_{-1}^{1}m_{n,2}(u)h_{\ell,2}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{U_{n+2k}(u)}{n+2k+1}h_{\ell,2}(u)\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u.

If
\ell
and
n
have different parity, then both the right-hand and the left-hand side are zero. Assume now
that
\ell
and
n
have the same parity. If
\ell<n
, then the right-hand side is trivially zero by the orthogonality
of
U_{n}
. Thus, we only need to consider
\ell=n+2j
for
j=0,1,\ldots
, for which the identity becomes

\delta_{j,0}=a_{j}-a_{j-1},\qquad j=0,1,2,\ldots,

where
a_{-1}=0
, so that
a_{0}=1
and
a_{j}=a_{j-1}
for
j\geq 0
. Hence,
a_{j}=1
for
j=0,1,\ldots
.
Now, setting
u=\cos{\alpha}
, then
\sqrt{1-u^{2}}U_{n+2k}(u)=\sin((n+2k+1){\alpha})
, which gives the expression

m_{n,d}
in (
3.2
).
∎

It turns out, surprisingly, that the expression (
3.2
) can be written, for each
n
, as a final sum.

Theorem 3.5
.

For
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, and
0<{\alpha}<\pi
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle m_{2n,2}(\cos{\alpha})\,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\sin((2k+1){\alpha})}{2k+1},

(3.4)

\displaystyle m_{2n+1,2}(\cos{\alpha})\,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{{\alpha}}{\pi}-\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\sin(2k{\alpha})}{2k}.

In particular, we obtain

(3.5)

m_{0,2}(u)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}M_{1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\cos{\theta}_{2})\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}_{2}=\begin{cases}0&\,u\leq-1,\\
\frac{1}{2}&\,-1<u<1,\\
0&\,u\geq 1.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
be odd
2\pi
periodic functions so that their restriction on
[0,\pi]
are defined by

f_{0}({\theta})=\begin{cases}0&{\theta}=0,\\
\frac{\pi}{4}&0<{\theta}<\pi,\\
0,&{\theta}=\pi,\end{cases}\qquad\hbox{and}\quad f_{1}({\theta})=\begin{cases}\frac{{\theta}}{2}&0\leq{\theta}<\pi,\\
0,&{\theta}=\pi.\end{cases}

A quick computation shows that the Fourier series of
f_{0}
is given by

f_{0}({\theta})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(2k+1){\theta}}{2k+1},\qquad-\pi\leq{\theta}\leq\pi,

where the convergence is pointwise. This gives immediately (
3.5
). Furthermore,
for
m_{2n,2}
, we obtain from (
3.2
)

m_{2n,2}(\cos{\alpha})=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2k+1){\alpha})}{(2k+1)}=\frac{2}{\pi}\left[\frac{\pi}{4}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\sin((2k+1){\alpha})}{(2k+1)}\right],

which is (
3.3
). Another quick computation shows that the Fourier series of
f_{1}(\theta)
is

f_{1}({\theta})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}\sin(n{\theta})}{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2n+1){\theta})}{2n+1}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(2n{\theta})}{2n},

which shows in particular, together with the Fourier series of
f_{0}
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(2n{\theta})}{2n}=\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{{\theta}}{2},\qquad 0<{\theta}<\pi.

Thus, for
m_{2n+1,2}
, we obtain from (
3.2
) that

m_{2n+1,2}(\cos{\alpha})=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(2k{\alpha})}{2k}=\frac{2}{\pi}\left[\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{{\alpha}}{2}-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\sin(2k{\alpha})}{2k}\right],

which is (
3.4
).
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

For
d>2
, the function
m_{n,d}
is of the form

(3.6)

m_{n,d}(u)=(1-u^{2})^{d-\frac{3}{2}}\frac{c_{d-1}}{(d-1)!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(d-1)_{k}}{k!}\frac{C_{n+2k}^{d-1}(u)}{C_{n+2k}^{d-1}(1)},\qquad-1\leq u\leq 1,

where
c_{d-1}
is the normalization constant defined by its reciprocal

c_{d-1}^{-1}=\int_{-1}^{1}(1-u^{2})^{d-\frac{3}{2}}\mathrm{d}u=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(d-\frac{1}{2}\right)}{(d-1)!}.

Proof.

For
d>2
, the function
u\mapsto M_{d-1}(u|\cos\{\cdot\}_{1},\ldots,\cos\{\cdot\}_{d})
has support set in
(-1,1)

and has
d-2
continuous derivatives, it follows that
m_{n,d}
is a continuous and
m_{n,d}^{(j)}(\pm 1)=0
for
j=0,1,\ldots,d-2
. We assume that
m_{n,d}
has the series expansion

m_{n,d}(u)=\frac{c_{d-1}}{(d-1)!}(1-u^{2})^{d-\frac{3}{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}^{n}\frac{C_{n+2k}^{d-1}(u)}{C_{n+2k}^{d-1}(1)},

where the coefficients
a_{k}^{n}
are to be determined by the biorthogonality, and the choice of index
n+2k

comes from (
3.1
) and the parity of
h_{n,d}
. Now, the orthogonality of the Gegenbauer polynomials is
equivalent to

(3.7)

c_{d-1}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{C_{n}^{d-1}(u)}{C_{n}^{d-1}(1)}Z_{m}^{d-1}(u)(1-u^{2})^{d-\frac{3}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u=\delta_{n,m}.

Using the second explicit formula of
h_{n,d}
in (
2.6
), which shows that the term of the highest
degree in
h_{n,d}
is
(d-1)!Z_{n}^{d-1}
, whereas the term of the lowest degree in
m_{n,d}
contains
C_{n}^{d-1}
,
or the term
k=0
in the sum, the orthogonality (
3.7
) implies that the identity (
3.1
)
for
n^{\prime}=n
becomes

\displaystyle a_{0}^{n}=\delta_{n,n}=1,\quad\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0};

moreover, for
n^{\prime}=n+2\ell
and
\ell=1,2,3,\ldots
, (
3.1
) becomes

\displaystyle 0=\int_{-1}^{1}m_{n,d}(u)h_{n+2\ell,d}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u=(d-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{d-1}{j}a_{\ell-j}^{n}.

Thus, using
a_{j}^{n}=0
for
j<0
and recalling that
a_{0}^{n}=1
, we see that
a_{j}^{n}
satisfy

(3.8)

\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}(-1)^{j}\binom{d-1}{j}a_{\ell-j}^{n}=\delta_{0,\ell},\qquad\ell=0,1,\ldots,d-1.

It shows, in particular, that
a_{j}^{n}
are independent of
n
and they can be determined recursively so that
the solution is unique. It turns out that the solution (
3.8
) is given
explicitly by
a_{j}=(d-1)_{j}/j!
. To verify that this is indeed the case, we use the identity

a_{\ell-j}=\frac{(d-1)_{\ell-j}}{(\ell-j)!}=\frac{(d-1)_{\ell}(-\ell)_{j}}{\ell!(2-\ell-d)_{j}},

which follows from
(-x)_{\ell-j}=(-x)_{\ell}(-1)^{j}(1-\ell-x)_{j}
, and
\binom{d-1}{j}=(-1)^{j}(-d+1)_{j}/j!
to write
the right-hand side of (
3.8
) as a hypergeometric function,

\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{d-1}{j}a_{\ell-j}^{n}=\frac{(d-1)_{\ell}}{\ell!}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}-\ell,\,1-d\\
2-d-\ell\end{matrix};1\right)=\frac{(d-1)_{\ell}(1-\ell)_{\ell}}{\ell!(2-d-\ell)_{\ell}},

where the last step follows from the Chu-Vandermonde identity. Since
(-\ell+1)_{\ell}=0
for
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, this
verifies that
a_{j}=(d-1)_{j}/j!
is the solution of (
3.8
).
∎

Theorem 3.7
.

For
d>2
and
-1\leq u\leq 1
,

\displaystyle m_{0,d}(u)\,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}M_{d-1}(u|\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d})\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})(d-1)!}(1-u^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}.

Proof.

Let
g(u)=(1-u^{2})^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}
. We compute the Fourier-Gegenbauer coefficients
\hat{g}^{d-1}_{n}

defined by

\displaystyle\hat{g}^{d-1}_{n}\,

\displaystyle=c_{d-1}\int_{-1}^{1}g(t)C_{n}^{{d-1}}(t)(1-t^{2})^{d-\frac{3}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=c_{d-1}\int_{-1}^{1}C_{n}^{{d-1}}(t)(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

By the parity of
C_{n}^{d-1}
,
\hat{g}^{d-1}_{n}=0
if
n
is odd. To compute
\hat{g}_{2n}^{d-1}
, we
use the connection coefficients of Gegenbauer polynomials
[
1
, Theorem 7.1.4’, p. 360]
, which gives

C_{2n}^{d-1}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(d-1)_{2n-k}(\frac{d-1}{2})_{k}}{(\frac{d-1}{2}+1)_{2n-k}k!}Z_{2n-2k}^{\frac{d-1}{2}}(u).

Using the orthogonality of
Z_{m}^{\frac{d-1}{2}}
, we then conclude that

\hat{g}^{d-1}_{n}=\frac{c_{d-1}}{c_{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\frac{(d-1)_{n}(\frac{d-1}{2})_{n}}{(\frac{d-1}{2}+1)_{n}n!}=\frac{c_{d-1}}{c_{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\frac{(d-1)_{n}}{n!}\frac{d-1}{2n+d-1}.

(Note that
c_{\cdot}
is also defined for a non-integral index.)
Consequently, the Fourier-Gegenbauer expansion of
g
is given by

g(u)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{g}_{n}^{d-1}\frac{C_{n}^{d-1}(u)}{h_{n}^{d-1}}=\frac{c_{d-1}}{c_{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(d-1)_{n}}{n!}\frac{C_{2n}^{d-1}(u)}{C_{2n}^{d-1}(1)},

where
h_{n}^{\lambda}
denotes the
L^{2}([-1,1],(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}})
norm of
C_{n}^{\lambda}(t)
and it is equal to

h_{n}^{\lambda}=\frac{{\lambda}}{n+{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(1),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.

Comparing with (
3.6
) with
n=0
, we see that

(3.9)

m_{0,d}=(1-u^{2})^{d-\frac{3}{2}}\frac{c_{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{(d-1)!}g(u)=\frac{c_{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{(d-1)!}(1-u^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}},

which is the stated result.
∎

In the case of
d=2
, it is easy to see that the explicit formula (
3.2
) implies the relation

m_{n,2}(u)-m_{n+2,2}(u)=\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\sin(n+1){\alpha}}{n+1}=\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\frac{U_{n}(u)}{U_{n}(1)},\quad u=\cos{\alpha}.

The following corollary gives a
d
-dimensional version of this recursive relation for
m_{n,d}
.

Corollary 3.8
.

For
d\geq 2
,
n=0,1,2,\ldots
,

(3.10)

(d-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{d-1}{j}m_{n+2j,d}(u)=c_{d-1}(1-u^{2})^{d-\frac{3}{2}}\frac{C_{n}^{d-1}(u)}{C_{n}^{d-1}(1)}.

Proof.

Using the explicit formula of
m_{n,d}
in (
3.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle(d-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{d-1}{j}m_{n+2j,d}(u)

\displaystyle\qquad=(1-u^{2})^{d-\frac{3}{2}}c_{d-1}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{d-1}{j}\sum_{k=j}^{\infty}\frac{(d-1)_{k-j}}{(k-j)!}\frac{C_{n+2k}^{d-1}(u)}{C_{n+2k}^{d-1}(1)}

\displaystyle\qquad=(1-u^{2})^{d-\frac{3}{2}}c_{d-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{d-1}{j}\frac{(d-1)_{k-j}}{(k-j)!}\frac{C_{n+2k}^{d-1}(u)}{C_{n+2k}^{d-1}(1)},

where we have used the convention that
(d-1)_{k-j}=0
if
j>k
and
\binom{d-1}{j}=0
if
j>d-1
.
The stated result then follows from (
3.8
).
∎

In particular, the identity (
3.10
) shows that the finite combination of
m_{n,d}
in the
left-hand is a polynomial of degree
n
multiplied by
(1-t^{2})^{d-\frac{3}{2}}
. The recursive formula can be
used to determine
m_{n,d}
if we know the first

elements

.
However, notice that the explicit expression of

in (

) contains the factor

, which has a power different from the power

in the right-hand side of

) is unlikely to hold for

;
in particular,

will not be a polynomial when

is even for

.

4.
Positive definite
\ell^{1}
-invariant functions

A function
f\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
is a positive definite function (PDF) if for every
\Xi_{N}=\{\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{N}\}
of
pairwise distinct points in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, the matrix

f[\Xi_{N}]=\left[f(\Theta_{i}-\Theta_{j})\right]_{i,j=1}^{N}

is nonnegative definite; it is called a strictly positive definite function (SPDF) if the matrix is positive definite.
Let
\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
denote the set of PDFs on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. By the definition of PDF, the
space
\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
is closed under linear combination with nonnegative coefficients; that is, if
f,g\in\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
and
c_{i}
and nonnegative constants, then
c_{1}f+c_{2}g\in\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
. The PDFs on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}

are characterized by the following theorem

Theorem 4.1
.

A function
f\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
is a PDF on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
if and only if the Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}_{\alpha}
are
nonnegative for all
{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
.

One direction of the theorem follows readily from the closeness of
\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
and that the exponential
functions
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\alpha}\cdot x}\in\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
for all
{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
, since

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{i}c_{j}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\alpha}\cdot(\Theta_{i}-\Theta_{j})}=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\alpha}\cdot\Theta_{i}}\right|^{2}\geq 0.

In the other direction, if
f
is PDF on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
then by the periodicity of
f
,

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}f(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}f(\theta-\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi.

The right-hand side is nonnegative if
f
is a trigonometric polynomial, as can be seen by applying a positive
cubature rule for the integral over
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and using the positive definiteness of
f
. In particular, this shows
that the left-hand side integral is nonnegative. Since
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\alpha}\cdot{\theta}}
is PDF, it follows from Schur’s theorem that

\hat{f}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}f({\theta})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\alpha}\cdot{\theta}}\,\mathrm{d}{\theta}\geq 0,\qquad\forall{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}.

Recall that a function
f:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
\ell^{1}
-invariant if
\hat{f}_{\alpha}=\hat{f}_{\beta}
for all
|{\alpha}|=|{\beta}|
,

{\alpha},{\beta}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
. These functions are given as follows.

Theorem 4.2
.

A function
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
is
\ell^{1}
-invariant if and only if

(4.1)

f({\theta}_{1},\ldots,{\theta}_{d})=[\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d}]F_{d},

where
F_{d}:[-1,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a
(d-1)
-times differentiable function and satisfies

(4.2)

F_{d}(\cos{\theta})=2(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{d+1}{2}\rfloor}(\sin{\theta})^{d-1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\begin{cases}-\sin(n{\theta})&\hbox{for $d$ even},\\
\cos(n{\theta})&\hbox{for $d$ odd},\end{cases}

with a sequence of real numbers
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
.

Proof.

If
f
is the given divided difference of
F_{d}
given in (
4.2
), then

f({\theta})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}[\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d}]H_{n,d}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}E_{n}({\theta}).

By the definition of
E_{n}({\theta})
, it follows readily that
\hat{f}_{\alpha}=\hat{f}_{\beta}
if
|{\alpha}|=|{\beta}|
. Moreover,
when its knots coalesce, the divided difference becomes a derivative as we have mentioned before.
By (
2.3
) and the
(d-1)
-th differentiability of
F
,
f
is continuous.

In the other direction, if
f
is
\ell^{1}
-invariant, then its Fourier series is given by (
2.1
).
By (
2.4
), we obtain that
f({\theta}_{1},\ldots,{\theta}_{d})=[\cos{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\cos{\theta}_{d}]F
with
F
given by

F(u)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{f}_{n}H_{n,d}(u),

so that
F
is of the form (
4.2
) with
a_{n}=\widehat{f}_{n}
by (
2.5
). The continuity of

f
requires that
F
has continuous derivatives of
(d-1)
order by (
2.3
).
∎

If
f
is
\ell^{1}
-invariant, then
\hat{f}_{\alpha}=\hat{f}_{|{\alpha}|}
, so that
\hat{f}_{\alpha}\geq 0
if and only if

\hat{f}_{n}\geq 0
in (
2.1
). Consequently, the following characterization of PDFs
holds.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
f\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
be
\ell^{1}
-invariant. Then
f
is a PDF on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
if and only if
f
is given by
(
4.1
) and (
4.2
) with
\hat{f}_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.

The SPDFs have been characterized in
[
4
, Theorem 1]
, where it is proved that a PDF function is also
SPDF if and only if the set of indices
\mathcal{G}=\{\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\mid\hat{f}_{\alpha}>0\}

intersects all the translations of each subgroup of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
that has the form

(a_{1}{\mathbb{Z}},a_{2}{\mathbb{Z}},\ldots,a_{d}{\mathbb{Z}}),\quad a_{1},{\alpha}_{2},\ldots,a_{d}\in{\mathbb{N}}.

More precisely, for every pair of vectors
\gamma\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
there exists a
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with

\hat{f}_{\alpha}>0
and
\alpha_{j}=\gamma_{j}+z_{j}\beta_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
. For
\ell^{1}
-invariant functions, the
SPDFs are characterized below.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
f\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
be
\ell^{1}
-invariant. Then
f
is a SPDF on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
if and only if
f
is given by
(
4.1
) and (
4.2
),
f
is PDF and for any pair
n<\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
there is an

m\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
\hat{f}_{n+m\ell}>0
or
\hat{f}_{(\ell-n)+m\ell}>0
.

Proof.

We prove that the given condition is equivalent to the condition given in
[
4
]
, if we assume the kernel
to be
\ell^{1}
-summable.

We start with sufficiency. Assume
f
is PDF and satisfies the condition of the theorem. For any two vectors
\gamma\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
we can assume without loss of generality that
0\leq\gamma_{j}\leq\beta_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots d
.
We define
n=|\gamma|
and
\ell=|\beta|
. Then there exists an
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
with

\hat{f}_{n+\ell m}>0
or
\hat{f}_{(\ell-n)+\ell m}>0
. If
\hat{f}_{n+\ell m}>0
, then

\hat{f}_{\gamma+m\beta}=\hat{f}_{n+\ell m}>0

since
|\gamma+m\beta|=n+m\ell
. Whereas if
\hat{f}_{(n-\ell)+\ell m}>0
, we can choose
m^{\prime}=m+1
.
Now
|\gamma-m^{\prime}\beta|=|\gamma-\beta-m\beta|=(\ell-n)+m\ell
implies

\hat{f}_{\gamma-m^{\prime}\beta}=\hat{f}_{(n-\ell)+\ell m}>0.

For the necessity, assume the assumption of the theorem does not hold, then there exists
\ell>n\in{\mathbb{N}}

such that
\hat{f}_{n+m\ell}=0
and
\hat{f}_{(\ell-n)+\ell m}=0
for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Set

\gamma=(n,0,\ldots,0)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},\quad\beta=(\ell,\ldots,\ell)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

Then for any
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
define
\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
,
\alpha_{j}=\gamma_{j}+z_{j}\beta_{j}
, so that with
m=|z|
,

|\alpha|=|n+z_{1}\ell|+\ell\sum_{j=2}^{d-1}|z_{j}|=\begin{cases}n+m\ell,&z_{1}\geq 0,\\
(\ell-n)+(m-1)\ell,&z_{1}<0.\end{cases}

Thereby, there would be no coefficients
\hat{f}_{\alpha}>0
,
\alpha_{j}=\gamma_{j}+z_{j}\beta_{j}
for this choice
of
\gamma
and
\beta
, contradicting strict positive definiteness.
∎
