Affine Connection Approach to the Realization of Nonholonomic Constraints by Strong Friction Forces
Vaughn Gzenda, Robin Chhabra*

Abstract
In this paper, we study an affine connection approach to realizing nonholonomic mechanical systems mediated by viscous friction forces with large coefficients, viewed as a singular perturbation of the nonholonomic system. We show that the associated slow manifold is represented coordinate-free as the image of a section over the nonholonomic distribution. We propose a novel invariance condition based on covariant derivatives and prove that this condition is equivalent to the classical invariance condition based on time derivatives. Accordingly, we propose a novel recursive procedure to approximate the slow manifold based on the covariant derivatives of a formal power series expansion of the section. Using this recurrence relation, we derive, up to second order, approximations of the slip velocities residing in the slow manifold, as well as the associated approximated dynamics up to first order. Lastly, we illustrate our approach with a case study of a vertical rolling disk.

Nonholonomic mechanics, Perturbation methods, Reduced-order modelling, Riemannian Geometry

Introduction

Rolling without slipping is a constraint that appears in many mechanical systems, such as vehicle systems, and is commonly viewed as an example of an ideal nonholonomic constraint
Bloch and Brogliato (2004)
. Nonholonomic constraints normally refer to point-wise linear constraints on the velocity space at each configuration, which cannot be described by a derivative of a set of constraints on the configuration space
Bloch and Brogliato (2004)
. Hence, such constraints are then viewed as a nonintegrable linear distribution of the tangent bundle to the configuration manifold
Bloch and Brogliato (2004)
. However, the nonholonomic constraint forces are only valid for velocities in the distribution. In practice, nonholonomic constraints in vehicle systems are often violated, resulting in an accumulation of errors for standard nonholonomic model-based control laws. To model the velocities violating the nonholonomic constraints, the ideal nonholonomic constraint forces may be modelled as dissipative friction, whose limit at large coefficients approximates the ideal nonholonomic system
Eldering (2016)
. The large friction coefficients introduce a steep linear relationship between the friction force and the motion of the contact point that is analogous to the linear regime of Bakker-Pacejka magic formula
Pacejka and Bakker (1992)
. Mathematically, the system with large coefficients may be treated as a slow/fast singular perturbation where the dissipative friction forces act on a much faster time scale than the nominal variables resulting in an exponentially attractive invariant manifold. This manifold – often called the slow manifold – physically represents small violations of the nonholonomic constraints.

Nonholonomic mechanical systems form a class of constrained systems whose velocity constraints have a natural differential geometric structure
Bloch and Brogliato (2004)
;
Bates and Śniatycki (1993)
;
Bloch et al. (2009)
;
Bloch (2010)
. Classically, the equations of motion are derived using Lagrange-d’Alembert’s (L-d’A) variational principle – which is an adaptation of Hamilton’s variational principle – whereby the variations are constrained to the nonholonomic distribution and enforced with Lagrange multipliers that do no work on the distribution
Bloch and Brogliato (2004)
. Some examples of nonholonomic constraints include rolling without slipping, no sliding, and conserved angular momenta, among
others
Bloch and Brogliato (2004)
;
Bullo and Lewis (2019)
. The kinetic energy of the mechanical system may be interpreted geometrically as a Riemannian metric tensor
Petersen (2006)
. Equivalent to the Lagrange-d’Alembert principle, Lewis and Bullo
Lewis (1998)
;
Lewis (2000)
use Riemannian geometry to model the nonholonomic dynamics with affine connections. An affine connection is a geometric object that enables a notion of differentiation of vector fields on a smooth manifold. A unique affine connection is derived from the metric tensor called the Levi-Civita affine connection that encodes Coriolis, centrifugal, and centripetal accelerations. In
Bullo and Lewis (2019)
, the nonholonomic constraints may be viewed as the kernel of a projection map onto the complement of the nonholonomic distribution. In the affine connection framework,
Bullo and Lewis (2019)
, the nonholonomic constraints are then enforced using Lagrange multipliers that are solved for using a covariant derivative of the complementary projection map. As such, Lewis introduces an affine connection called the nonholonomic connection, based on the Levi-Civita connection and the covariant of the orthogonal projection map, modelling the necessary accelerations required to maintain the nonholonomic constraints. The geometric interplay of affine connections and nonintegrable distributions is studied in detail in
Lewis (1998)
. Other equivalent approaches to nonholonomic mechanics include symplectic geometry
Bates and Śniatycki (1993)
;
Chhabra and Emami (2014)
, Poisson geometry
Van der Schaft and Maschke (1994)
, Gauss’s Principle of Least Constraint
Bloch and Brogliato (2004)
;
Bullo and Lewis (2019)
and Kane’s equations
Kane and Levinson (1985)
.

Singularly perturbed systems (SPS) manifest in various domains of science and engineering, emerging when a dynamical system relies on scalable parasitic parameters like small time constants, masses, friction,
etc
. Accordingly, SPS have natural applications in electric circuits
Sannuti (1981)
, chemistry
Zagaris et al. (2004)
, neuroscience
Rubin and Terman (2002)
, among others. Slow/fast systems are a particular case of a SPS that involve two distinct time scales, delineated by a singular parameter
\epsilon
. Tikhonov established that when the spectra of the linearized fast dynamics lies in the left half-plane, the flow converges to an invariant (slow) manifold as
\epsilon
tends to 0
Tikhonov (1952)
. The functional relationship between the slow and fast variables is dictated by an
invariance condition
and a formal power series expansion is a common approach to approximate the slow manifold
Verhulst (2005)
. Fenichel examined the persistence of the invariant manifold under small perturbations of
\varepsilon
, utilizing the concept of Normally Hyperbolic Invariant Manifold (NHIM) and under the assumption of compactness in the state space
Fenichel and Moser (1971)
. In
Fenichel (1979)
, Fenichel utilizes a geometric approach to investigate asymptotic expansions of the graph representation of the invariant manifold. In the Riemannian geometric context, Eldering extends Fenichel’s findings to non-compact state spaces, assuming regularity under bounded geometry conditions
Eldering (2013)
. Eldering’s work opens up a pathway for the coordinate-independent examination of such systems. If a mechanical system possesses a noncompact configuration space
Q
and is endowed with a symmetry group
G
, where the group action leads to a compact
shape space

Q/G

Marsden and Ratiu (1998)
, then it follows that
Q
has bounded geometry
Eldering (2013)
;
Eldering (2016)
. In practical applications, numerous mechanical systems exhibit symmetry groups, as seen in mobile vehicles that yield compact shape spaces
Chhabra and Emami (2014)
. Hence, Eldering’s geometric singular perturbation techniques
Eldering (2013)
can be employed for the analysis of such systems.

The physical realization of nonholonomic mechanical systems by strong viscous friction forces was treated classically by Brendelev and Karapetian who mainly focused on convergence to the ideal nonholonomic dynamics
Brendelev (1981)
;
Karapetian (1981)
. More recently, Eldering used the geometric singular perturbation theory of NHIM’s
Eldering (2013)
to generalize these convergence results for systems with bounded geometry. Common to all these models, the contact forces are modelled as a Rayleigh dissipation function (RDF) defined by a symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor. Rayleigh dissipation functions
have been used to model surface contact forces following first physical principles
Minguzzi (2015)
. To realize the nonholonomic constraints, the RDF is constructed such that its kernel is identified with the nonholonomic distribution ensuring that the contact forces do no work on the distribution, mimicking the L-d’A principle, yet the dynamics are exponentially attractive to the distribution. At the limit of large friction coefficients, Eldering proves the existence of a persistent exponentially attractive NHIM and shows that in a nonstandard frame spanning the distribution and its complement, the slow manifold may be represented as the graph over the nonholonomic variables
Eldering (2016)
. It is shown that this graph may be represented as a formal power series, which is used to approximate the slow manifold up to first order
Eldering (2016)
based on the classical invariance condition.

Antali and Stepan considered the physical realization of the rolling phenomenon of wheels by dry friction forces
Antali and Stepan (2019)
. Their analysis is based on the crossing dynamics of extended Filippov systems
Antali and Stepan (2018)
. A Filippov system is described by a piece-wise smooth vector field with discontinuities on a
discontinuity manifold

Filippov (2013)
. Extended Filippov (EF) systems are Filippov systems with codimension-2 discontinuity manifolds and describe Coulomb rigid body dynamics
Antali and Stepan (2018)
. The EF approach has been used to analyze the spinning and sliding behavior of rigid bodies under contact
Antali and Varkonyi (2022)
. Recently, bifurcation analysis of EF systems has also been developed to analyze slip and sliding dynamics of a wheel motion
Antali (2023)
. In contrast to the slow-fast realization by viscous friction forces
Eldering (2016)
, the Coulomb friction in
Antali and Stepan (2019)
is both nonsmooth and nonlinear in velocities. The wheel rolling phenomenon has also been captured as a limit of piece-wise continuous holonomic constraints
Ruina (1998)
. Other historical approaches to the realization of nonholonomic constraints include coupling of an external force to generate nonholonomic dynamics
Bloch and Rojo (2008)
.

In this paper, we study realizations of nonholonomic systems as slow/fast systems using an affine connection approach inspired by the work of Bullo and Lewis
Bullo and Lewis (2019)
;
Lewis (1998)
;
Lewis (2000)
. In our geometric approach, we represent the slow manifold coordinate-independent as the image of a section over the nonholonomic distribution. We introduce a novel invariance based on a covariant derivative along the integral curves on the slow manifold. We prove that this novel invariance condition is equivalent to the invariance condition based on time derivatives
Eldering (2016)
. We propose a recursion to approximate the velocities in the slow manifold based on covariant derivatives of geometric quantities. Using the recursion, we derive approximations of equations of motion. Lastly, we study an example system of a vertical rolling disk. The major contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

1.

formulating a coordinate-free representation of the slow manifold as the image of a nonlinear section over the nonholonomic distribution,

2.

formulating a novel invariance condition based on covariant derivatives, and proving its equivalence to the classical invariance condition,

3.

proposing a novel recurrence relation to approximate the slip velocities described by the slow manifold,

4.

using the recurrence relation to derive higher-order perturbations of the equations of motion.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
provides a self-contained summary of the differential geometric structures used in the remainder of the article. This includes a review of Riemannian geometry, affine connection systems, nonholonomic affine connection systems, and the geometry of the second tangent bundle including the procedure to take covariant derivatives of general (nonlinear) tangent bundle maps. In Section
3
, we derive the equations of motion for a slow fast realization of nonholonomic systems and represent the slow manifold as the image of a section. Using this model, in Section
4
, we derive the relationship between the reaction forces and the slow manifold and calculate zeroth, first and second order approximations of the slip velocities, and zeroth and first-order dynamical equations of motion. Lastly, in Section
5
we consider our approach with a case study of a vertical rolling disk.

2.2.2
Covariant Derivative of a Tangent Bundle Map

In this subsection, we find the covariant derivative of a general (nonlinear) tangent bundle map. This covariant derivative is essential for the results in subsequent sections. The authors have not found this result explicitly stated in the literature. Lastly, we provide a matrix formulation of the equations for practical computations.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{h}:TQ\rightarrow TQ
be a general (not necessarily linear) vector bundle map over the tangent bundle
\pi_{TQ}:TQ\rightarrow Q
. Let
\boldsymbol{v},\,\boldsymbol{w}:Q\rightarrow TQ
be a sections. Then, the covariant derivative of
\boldsymbol{h}\circ\boldsymbol{w}
along
\boldsymbol{v}
is

\displaystyle\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{(\boldsymbol{h}\circ\boldsymbol{w})}=\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{w})+D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}\left(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{w}}\right),

(28)

where the vertical Jacobian
D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{V}}D\boldsymbol{h}\big|_{\mathcal{V}TQ}
is given locally by

\displaystyle D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{w}})=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{h}^{m}}{\partial w^{l}}\left(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{w}}\right)^{l}\frac{\boldsymbol{\partial}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}q^{m}},

(29)

and the horizontal connection
\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{V}}D\boldsymbol{h}\big|_{\mathcal{H}TQ}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{w})}=\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{h}^{m}}{\partial q^{k}}v^{k}+\Gamma^{m}_{ki}v^{k}\boldsymbol{h}^{i}-\Gamma^{l}_{ki}w^{k}v^{i}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{h}^{m}}{\partial w^{l}}\right)\frac{\boldsymbol{\partial}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}q^{m}}.

(30)

Proof.

See appendix
7.2

Remark 8
.

The map
\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
may be constructed as follows. Let
\Phi(\boldsymbol{q})
be a frame spanning
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\oplus\mathcal{D}^{\bot}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
. Define linear maps in this frame with the subscript
\Phi
, so,
[\mathbb{Q}_{\Phi}]=\Phi^{-1}[\mathbb{Q}]\Phi
,
[\mathcal{P}^{\bot}_{\Phi}]=\Phi^{-1}[\mathcal{P}^{\bot}]\Phi
and
[\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}_{\Phi}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}]=\Phi^{-1}[\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}]\Phi
and the equation
[\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}^{\bot}]=[\mathbb{Q}_{\Phi}][\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}_{\Phi}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}]
is given by the block matrix equation of the form

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{O}\\
\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{I}\end{bmatrix}}_{[\mathcal{P}^{\bot}_{\boldsymbol{q}}]}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{O}\\
\mathbb{O}&\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}\end{bmatrix}}_{[\mathbb{Q}_{\Phi}]}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{O}\\
\mathbb{O}&\widetilde{\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}}\end{bmatrix}}_{[\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}_{\Phi}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}]}.

(55)

Therefore, we set
\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}=\widetilde{\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}}^{-1}
and perform the inverse transformation
[\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{q}}]=\Phi[\mathbb{Q}_{\Phi}]\Phi^{-1}
to obtain the result.

Theorem 1

(Generating Equation for the Slow Manifold)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow TQ
be the section defining the invariant manifold
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
with respect to the dynamics (
50
)-(
52
). Let
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\varepsilon^{k}\boldsymbol{h}^{(k)}
be an expansion of the section by a formal power series. Then, the slow manifold may be recursively approximated from the following generating equation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})=\varepsilon\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\circ\left[(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathcal{P}^{\bot}})(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})-\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\boldsymbol{d}V^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}})\right]

\displaystyle\small+\varepsilon\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\circ\left[(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathcal{P}^{\bot}})(\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon})+(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{P}^{\bot}})(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})+(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{P}^{\bot}})(\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon})\right]

\displaystyle\small-\varepsilon\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\circ D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\left[(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathcal{P}})(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{d}V^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}})\right]

\displaystyle\small-\varepsilon\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\circ D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\left[(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{P}})(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})+(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathcal{P}})(\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon})+(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{P}})(\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon})\right]

(56)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-\varepsilon\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\circ\left[\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{D}}}{\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})+\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}{\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})\right].

Proof.

By analogy with the ideal nonholonomic case
Lewis (2000)
, take the covariant derivative of the latter invariant manifold equation (
52
)

\displaystyle\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\left(\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)}=\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\left(\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\circ\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)}

(57)

and substitute the affine connection system (
50
)-(
51
). The left-hand side is given by

\displaystyle\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\left(\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathcal{P}^{\bot}\right)(\boldsymbol{v})+\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\left(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathcal{P}^{\bot}\right)(\boldsymbol{v})-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{v})^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}-\mathcal{P}^{\bot}\left(\boldsymbol{d}V^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}\right).

On the other hand, according to Proposition
2
and the chain rule, we find the right-hand side

\displaystyle\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\left(\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\circ\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)}=D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\left(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\left(\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)}\right)+\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)}

\displaystyle\small=D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\left(\left(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\mathcal{P}}\right)(\boldsymbol{v})+\mathcal{P}\left(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)\right)+\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)}

\displaystyle\small=D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\left((\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\mathcal{P}})(\boldsymbol{v})\right)-D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{P}\left(\boldsymbol{d}V^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}\right)\right)+\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v}))}.

Putting both sides of this calculation together, we find the following identity for the dissipative forces

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}(\boldsymbol{v})=-(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\mathcal{P}^{\bot}})(\boldsymbol{v})+\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\boldsymbol{d}V^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}})

(58)

\displaystyle+D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\left((\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\mathcal{P}})(\boldsymbol{v})\right)+\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v}))}-D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{d}V^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}})\right)

Lastly, we restrict (
58
) to the velocities
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
to obtain the generating equation for
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}+\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{P}^{\bot}\right)(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}+\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\boldsymbol{d}V^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}})

\displaystyle-\varepsilon D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}(-\boldsymbol{d}V^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}})-\varepsilon\left(\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}+\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\right)(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})

\displaystyle+\varepsilon D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\left((\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}+\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{P}})(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}+\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon})\right).

(59)

By Lemma
4
and linearity we obtain the generating equation (
56
).
∎

Remark 9
.

Note that there is an
\varepsilon
in all terms on the right-hand side of (
56
). Thus, any power series expansion of
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}
results in an order higher on the right-hand side than the left-hand side. Therefore, the generating equation (
56
) provides a recurrence relation for the approximation of
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}
.

Theorem 2

(Equivalence of Reaction Forces)
.

The generating equation for
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})
in (
56
) results in the same reaction forces
-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})
as those reaction forces resulting from the classical invariance condition

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\boldsymbol{v})\big|_{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\circ\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v})\big|_{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}.

(60)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that the potential
V=0
. The equations of motion (
50
)-(
52
) can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}=-\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}(\boldsymbol{v})

(61)

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}=\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}\iff\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\circ\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v}).

(62)

Taking a Lie derivative of (
52
) along the along these dynamics we find

\displaystyle\left[\sum^{n}_{k=1}\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}^{\bot}}{\partial q^{k}}v^{k}\right]\boldsymbol{v}-[\mathcal{P}^{\bot}][\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v})]\boldsymbol{v}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[\mathcal{P}^{\bot}][\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}]\boldsymbol{v}=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{q},\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v}))\right]\boldsymbol{v}+[D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}]\left[\sum^{n}_{k=1}\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial q^{k}}v^{k}\right]\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\quad-[D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}][\mathcal{P}][\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v})]\boldsymbol{v}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}][\mathcal{P}][\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}]\boldsymbol{v}.

where the quantities in the square brackets are the compact matrix notation introduced in Remark
3
. As
\mathcal{D}=\ker\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}
, the last term vanishes. Let
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
be a velocity in the slow manifold, then the reaction forces are restricted to
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
are given by

\displaystyle\small-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}})=-\left[\sum^{n}_{k=1}\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}^{\bot}}{\partial q^{k}}v^{k}_{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}\right]\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}+[\mathcal{P}^{\bot}][\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}})]\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}

(63)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\small+\left[\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})\right]\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}+[D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}]\left[\sum^{n}_{k=1}\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial q^{k}}v^{k}_{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}\right]\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}-[D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}][\mathcal{P}][\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}})]\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}.

Contrastingly, the reaction forces
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}
resulting from the generating equation (
56
) are calculated from the equation

\displaystyle\small-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}(\boldsymbol{v})=-(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}}{\mathcal{P}^{\bot}})(\boldsymbol{v})+D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}}{\mathcal{P}})(\boldsymbol{v})+\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\nabla}_{\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}}{\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v}))}.

as was observed in equation (
58
). By Remark
3
the covariant derivatives in this equation may be expressed in matrix form by

\displaystyle\small-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}(\boldsymbol{v})=-\left[\sum^{n}_{k=1}\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}^{\bot}}{\partial q^{k}}v^{k}\right]\boldsymbol{v}-[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v})][\mathcal{P}^{\bot}]\boldsymbol{v}+[\mathcal{P}^{\bot}][\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v})]\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\small+[D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}]\left[\sum^{n}_{k=1}\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial q^{k}}v^{k}\right]\boldsymbol{v}+D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v})][\mathcal{P}]\boldsymbol{v}-[D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}][\mathcal{P}][\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v})]\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\small+\left[\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}}{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})\right]\boldsymbol{v}+[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v})]\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})-[D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}][\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v})][\mathcal{P}]\boldsymbol{v}.

(64)

Then, restricting to
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}
, and using
\mathcal{P}^{\bot}\circ\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}
terms cancel and therefore, these friction forces (
64
) restricted to
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
are identical to (
63
).
∎

Corollary 2.1

(Equivalence of Tangent Conditions)
.

The dynamics defined by (
50
)-(
52
) restricted to
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
are tangent to the manifold
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

In local coordinates (
50
)-(
52
) are equivalent to the equations of motion on
TTQ
given by (
41
). Following singular perturbation theory, the classical invariance equation (
60
) ensures that the reaction forces result in dynamics tangent to
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
.
By Theorem
2
, the reaction friction forces described by (
56
) are equivalent to the reaction forces resulting from the classical invariance equation. Therefore, the dynamics (
50
)-(
52
) along with the generating equation (
56
) result in dynamics tangent to
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
.
∎

Corollary 2.2

(Equivalence of Invariance Conditions)
.

The classical invariance condition (
60
) for the dynamics (
50
)-(
52
)
is equivalent to the condition

\displaystyle\overset{}{\nabla}_{\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}}{(\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\boldsymbol{v}))}\big|_{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}=\overset{}{\nabla}_{\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}}{(\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}\circ\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{v}))}\big|_{\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}.

Proof.

By Theorem
2
and Corollary
2.1

.
∎

.

The later covariant derivative invariance condition (

4.2
Approximation of Slip Velocities

In this section, we employ the generating equation (
56
) from Theorem
1
to compute the zeroth, first and second order approximations of the slow manifold
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}
. Expand
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}
as a formal power series

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{h}^{(0)}+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\boldsymbol{h}^{(2)}+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

(66)

Further expanding the power series allows for higher order approximations of
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
.

4.2.1
Zeroth Order Approximation of Slip Velocities

Lets consider the zeroth order term of the slow manifold
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{h}^{(0)}+O(\varepsilon)
. Observe that when
\varepsilon=0
we have

\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=\mathcal{D}_{0}=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in TQ\,|\,\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{h}^{(0)}\}.

Since
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}
is a perturbation of the nonholonomic distribution, we find that
\boldsymbol{h}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Then, to find the zeroth order velocity, solve the identity
\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{0}
for
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathcal{D}
.

Remark 11
.

The zeroth order approximation of the slow fast velocities are equivalent to the nonholonomic velocities.

4.2.2
First Order Approximation of Slip Velocities

Let us assume that section
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}
is taken to first order, so we expand
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}+O(\varepsilon^{2})
. Then according to the generating equation (
56
) to this first order slip velocity, we find

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}=\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\circ\left[(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathcal{P}^{\bot}})(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})-\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\boldsymbol{d}V^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}})\right].

(67)

Remark 12
.

The first order approximation of the slow fast velocities are proportional to the nonholonomic Lagrange multipliers (
15
). This is consistent with previous results
Eldering (2013)
.

4.2.3
Second Order Approximation of Slip Velocities

Lastly, assume that section
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}
is taken to second order, so we expand the formal power series as
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\boldsymbol{h}^{(2)}+O(\varepsilon^{3})
. Then according to the generating equation (
56
) to this first order slip velocity, we find

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}^{(2)}=\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\circ\left[(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}}{\mathcal{P}^{\bot}})(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})+(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathcal{P}^{\bot}})(\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)})\right]

(68)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\circ\left[D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}\left((\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathcal{P}})(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})\right)-D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}\,\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{d}V^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}})\right]-\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\circ\left[\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}}{\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})\right].

Remark 13
.

Higher order approximations of
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}
may be calculated recursively in terms of higher order covariant derivatives
\overset{}{\nabla}{}^{n}
. However, in practice, first and second order approximations suffice. This is due to the fact that the friction forces
-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}(\boldsymbol{v})
in (
50
) restricted to the second order approximation results in a first order force
-\varepsilon\mathbb{F}\mathcal{R}^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(2)})
that is a function of zeroth and first-order velocities.

4.3
Approximation of Slip Dynamics

In this section, we use the approximations of the slip velocities
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{h}^{(2)}+O(\varepsilon)
to approximate the zeroth and first order dynamics. Due to the recurrence relation defined by the generating equation (
56
) on
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}
we observe that the zeroth order approximation of the dynamics (
50
)-(
52
) depends on the first order approximation
\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}
. Furthermore, the first order approximation of the dynamics (
50
)-(
52
) depends on the second order approximation
\boldsymbol{h}^{(2)}
, and so on. Therefore, the order of the slip velocities
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}
is always one order above the order of the dynamics.

4.3.1
Zeroth Order Dynamics

By the equations of motion (
50
) and the first order approximation of the slow manifold
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}+O(\varepsilon^{2})
, we find the zeroth order equations of motion are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}-(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathcal{P}^{\bot}})(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{d}V^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}})

(69)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}\cong\mathcal{D}.

(70)

Remark 14
.

The zeroth order equations of motion (
69
) are exactly the nonholonomic equations of motion (
12
)-(
14
). This result is consistent with previous realizations of slow/fast nonholonomic systems
Eldering (2016)
.

4.3.2
First Order Dynamics

Using the generating function (

) we obtain the first and second order approximations
\boldsymbol{h}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\boldsymbol{h}^{(2)}+O(\varepsilon^{3})
to obtain (
67
) and (
68
), the former gives the slip velocity and the later gives the reaction force. Restricting the dynamics to the first order approximation of slip, we find the dynamics:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}-(\overset{}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathcal{P}^{\bot}})(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})-\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{d}V^{\sharp_{\mathbb{G}}})\\

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}})

(71)

where the first order approximation of the slip
\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}
is given by (
67
), and vertical derivative
D^{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}
and horizontal
\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}}{\boldsymbol{h}^{(1)}}
is calculated according to equation (

) and (

).

5
Case Study: Vertical Rolling Disk

To illustrate the theory, we consider a vertical rolling disk for simplicity. However, our approach may be applied to other systems with (linear) nonholonomic rolling constraints such as wheeled mobile robots
Samson et al. (2016)
and vehicle systems
Chhabra (2016)
. For the traditional derivation of the nonholonomic equations for the vertical rolling disk using the Lagrange-d’Alembert variational principle see
(
Bloch and Brogliato, 2004
, Sec. 1.4)
and for the affine-connection approach see
(
Bullo and Lewis, 2019
, Sec. 4.5)
, and
Lewis (2000)
.

The configuration space for a planar vertical rolling disk is
Q=SE(2)\times SO(2)
with local coordinates
\boldsymbol{q}=(\theta,x,y,\varphi)
and local coordinates for velocities
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{\theta},v_{x},v_{y},v_{\varphi})
. The Riemannian metric defining the kinetic energy is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\text{diag}\left(I,m,m,J\right)

(72)

where
m
is the mass of the disk,
I
is the moment of inertia normal to the plane and
J
is the moment of inertia about the rolling axis. Note that as
\mathbb{G}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
is constant, the corresponding Christoffel symbols
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{0}
for all
(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{v})\in TQ

i.e.
, the metric
\mathbb{G}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
is flat.

The nonholonomic constraint one-forms

\displaystyle a_{q}^{1}=\boldsymbol{d}x-R\cos\theta\boldsymbol{d}\varphi,\quad a_{q}^{2}=\boldsymbol{d}y-R\sin\theta\boldsymbol{d}\varphi,

(73)

enforcing rolling without slipping are given by

\displaystyle a_{q}^{1}(v_{\theta},v_{x},v_{y},v_{\varphi})=v_{x}-R\cos\theta v_{\varphi}=0,

(74)

\displaystyle a_{q}^{2}(v_{\theta},v_{x},v_{y},v_{\varphi})=v_{y}-R\sin\theta v_{\varphi}=0,

(75)

where
R
is the radius of the disk. This constraint defines a nonholonomic distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\ker(a^{1}_{q})\cap\ker(a^{2}_{q})
which are spanned by the vector fields

\displaystyle\frac{\boldsymbol{\partial}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}\theta},\quad R\cos\theta\frac{\boldsymbol{\partial}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}x}+R\sin\theta\frac{\boldsymbol{\partial}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}y}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\partial}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}\varphi}.

(76)

Using the musical isomorphisms, we find that the
\mathbb{G}
-orthogonal complement spanned by the orthogonal complement
\mathcal{D}^{\bot}=\left(\mathcal{D}^{\circ}\right)^{\flat_{\mathbb{G}}}
is spanned by the vector fields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\frac{\boldsymbol{\partial}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}x}-\frac{R}{J}\cos\theta\frac{\boldsymbol{\partial}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}\varphi},\quad\frac{1}{m}\frac{\boldsymbol{\partial}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}y}-\frac{R}{J}\sin\theta\frac{\boldsymbol{\partial}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}\varphi}.

(77)

The projection map
\mathcal{P}:TQ\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
onto the distribution
\mathcal{D}
is constructed by the vector fields spanned by (
76
) and the metric (
72
) and is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{I}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}}&0&0&0\\
0&\cos^{2}\theta&\frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta&\frac{J}{mR}\cos\theta\\
0&\frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta&\sin^{2}\theta&\frac{J}{mR}\sin\theta\\
0&\frac{\cos\theta}{R}&\frac{\sin\theta}{R}&\frac{J}{mR^{2}}\end{bmatrix}

(78)

where
\mathcal{I}:=\frac{mR^{2}}{J+mR^{2}}
is a ratio of inertia.

Similarly, the projection map
\mathcal{P}^{\bot}:TQ\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\bot}
onto the distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\bot}
is constructed by the vector fields spanned by (
76
) and the metric (
72
) and is given by

\displaystyle\small\mathcal{P}^{\bot}=\mathcal{I}\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
0&\frac{J+mR^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{mR^{2}}&-\frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta&-\frac{J}{mR}\cos\theta\\
0&-\frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta&\frac{J+mR^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}{mR^{2}}&-\frac{J}{mR}\sin\theta\\
0&-\frac{\cos\theta}{R}&-\frac{\sin\theta}{R}&1\end{bmatrix}.

(79)

The nonholonomic constraints may then be expressed by the equation
\mathcal{P}^{\bot}(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{0}
, from which we find the constrained velocities
\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathcal{D}
is given by

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we developed an affine connection approach for realizing nonholonomic mechanical systems. We formulated the slow/fast dynamics in a coordinate-independent manner in terms of sections, projection maps and covariant derivatives. We proposed a novel invariance condition for slow/fast realizations of nonholonomic constraints and proved that this novel condition is equivalent to the classical approach. We proposed a recursive procedure to approximate higher order slip velocities and their associated dynamics. We conclude the paper with some remarks regarding the advantages of the proposed formalism.

1.

The geometric formalism is coordinate-independent and suitable for symbolic computation.

2.

The proposed slow-fast decomposition permits a recursive procedure to approximate the invariant manifold representing slip velocities. In the context of vehicle systems, the slow manifold may be physically interpreted as a deviation from the ideal no-slip constraints. Approximations of the slow manifold provide estimates of these deviations and have applications in model-based slip estimation and localization in vehicle systems.

3.

Higher order approximations of slip velocities are based on objects that can be interpreted geometrically and may give insight into the geometric structure of other equivalent approaches.

The future directions of this research include the following: (i) Designing a recursive controller robust to the uncertainties in the estimation of the friction coefficients and external forces; (ii) Use of higher-order approximations of the slow-manifold for in-the-loop motion estimation of vehicle systems; and (iii) symmetry reduction for slow-fast realizations of nonholonomic systems.
