A new asymptotic representation and inversion method for the Student’s t t distribution
A. Gil, J. Segura and N. M. Temme

Abstract
Some special functions are particularly relevant in applied probability and statistics. For example, the incomplete beta function is the cumulative central beta distribution. In this paper, we consider the inversion of the central Student’s- t t distribution which is a particular case of the central beta distribution. The inversion of this distribution functions is useful in hypothesis testing as well as for generating random samples distributed according to the corresponding probability density function. A new asymptotic representation in terms of the complementary error function, will be one of the important ingredients in our analysis. As we will show, this asymptotic representation is also useful in the computation of the distribution function. We illustrate the performance of all the obtained approximations with numerical examples.

Introduction

There is a very close relationship between some special functions and some of the most popular distribution functions in statistics.
For example, the incomplete beta function
(
Paris:2010:INC
, §8.17)
is the central beta distribution. Particular cases include other well-known distributions such as the geometric, binomial, negative binomial or the central Student’s-
t
distribution. Therefore, standard methods for the computation and inversion
of special functions
Gil:2007:NSF
can also be applied to evaluate and invert distribution functions. The problem of inversion appears, for example,
when computing percentage points of the distribution functions; also, it is closely related to the generation of random variates from a continuous probability density function needed, for example, in Monte Carlo or quasi-Monte Carlo methods.

We have considered the central beta distribution in a previous publication
Gil:2017:IBE
.
In this paper, we focus on the computation and inversion of the central Student’s-
t
distribution, which has multiple applications in science and engineering (for an application in physics, see for example
Rover:2011:RSD
). As we mentioned before, this distribution function is a particular case of the central beta
distribution but which requires particular analysis.
An asymptotic representation in terms of
the complementary error function, will play a key role in our analysis.
The performance of the approximations obtained will be illustrated with numerical examples.

There is a vast literature on Student’s
t
distribution. An extensive overview can be found at
Johnson:1995:CUD
; see also the references
contained therein.
For an historical account and the origin of this distribution, we refer to
Zabell:2008:OAA
. For a generalization from the viewpoint of special functions, see
Koepf:2006:GSD
.

Some useful expressions for the analysis of the central Student’s-t distribution are:

•

a) Probability density function:

f_{n}(t)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}n+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{n\pi}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}n\right)}\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}n-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\,B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}n\right)}\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}n-\frac{1}{2}},

(1.1)

where
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
B(p,q)
is the Beta integral;

, not necessarily an integer.

•

b) Cumulative distribution function:

F_{n}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_{n}(t)\,dt,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.2)

•

c) Incomplete beta function:

I_{x}(p,q)=\frac{1}{B(p,q)}\int_{0}^{x}t^{p-1}(1-t)^{q-1}\,dt,\quad B(p,q)=\frac{\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)}{\Gamma(p+q)}.

(1.3)

From the integral representation, we have:

I_{x}(p,q)=1-I_{1-x}(q,p).

(1.4)

•

d) Cumulative distribution function in terms of the incomplete beta function:

F_{n}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}I_{\frac{x^{2}}{n+x^{2}}}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}n\right)=1-\frac{1}{2}I_{\frac{n}{n+x^{2}}}\left(\frac{1}{2}n,\frac{1}{2}\right),&{\rm if\ }x\geq 0,\\
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}I_{\frac{x^{2}}{n+x^{2}}}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}n\right)=\frac{1}{2}I_{\frac{n}{n+x^{2}}}\left(\frac{1}{2}n,\frac{1}{2}\right),&{\rm if\ }x\leq 0.\\
\end{array}\right.

(1.5)

•

e) Cumulative distribution function in terms of the Gauss hypergeometric functions:

F_{n}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{{{\lower 2.58334pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}\over{\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 2$}}}+\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}\,B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}n\right)}}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{c}\hskip-10.0pt\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}n+\frac{1}{2}\end{array}\\
\begin{array}[]{c}\hskip-10.0pt\frac{3}{2}\end{array}\end{array}\hskip-8.0pt;\,-\frac{x^{2}}{n}\right),&{\rm if\ }x\in\mathbb{R},\\

\begin{array}[]{c}\hskip-10.0pt\frac{1}{2}n+1\end{array}\end{array}\hskip-8.0pt;\,-\frac{n}{x^{2}}\right),}&{\rm if\ }x\geq 0,\\
\displaystyle{\frac{(1-y)^{\frac{1}{2}n}y^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{nB\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}n\right)}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{c}\hskip-10.0pt1,\frac{1}{2}\end{array}\\
\begin{array}[]{c}\hskip-10.0pt\frac{1}{2}n+1\end{array}\end{array}\hskip-8.0pt;\,-\frac{n}{x^{2}}\right),}&{\rm if\ }x\leq 0,\\
\end{array}\right.

(1.6)

where

2
Asymptotic expansion of the Student’s
t
cumulative distribution function

The second and third representation in (
1.6
) can be used for large values of
n
by using the standard power series of the hypergeometric functions. It is not necessarily that
x^{2}>n
, but a condition
x^{2}/n={\cal O}(1)
is needed. To obtain a large-
n
asymptotic representation, whether or not
x
is large, we use a method that we have used for other cumulative distribution functions; see
(
Temme:2015:AMI
, Chapter 36)
.

We use in (
1.1
) the substitution
t=s\sqrt{n}
. This gives

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{\;}c@{\;}l@{}}F_{n}(x)&=&\displaystyle{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\,B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}n\right)}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}n-\frac{1}{2}}\,dt}\\[8.0pt]
&=&\displaystyle{\frac{1}{B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}n\right)}\int_{-\infty}^{x/\sqrt{n}}\left(1+s^{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}n-\frac{1}{2}}\,ds.}\end{array}

(2.1)

We write
u^{2}=\ln(1+s^{2})
, with the condition
{\rm sign}(u)={\rm sign}(s)
, and obtain

F_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}n\right)}\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}e^{-\frac{1}{2}nu^{2}}g(u)\,du,

(2.2)

where

g(u)=\sqrt{\frac{u^{2}}{1-e^{-u^{2}}}},\quad\xi^{2}=\ln\left(1+\frac{x^{2}}{n}\right),\quad{\rm sign}(\xi)={\rm sign}(x).

(2.3)

Using the method of
(
Temme:2015:AMI
, §36.1)
we find that
F_{n}(x)
can be written in the form

F_{n}(x)={{\lower 2.58334pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}\over{\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 2$}}}{\rm erfc}\left(-\xi\sqrt{n/2}\right)-\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}n\xi^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\,\beta(n)}B_{n}(\xi),\quad\beta(n)=\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi}}\,B\left({{\lower 2.58334pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}\over{\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 2$}}},{{\lower 2.58334pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}\over{\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 2$}}}n\right),

(2.4)

where we have introduced the complementary error function

{\rm erfc}\,z=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{z}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt.

(2.5)

The function
B_{n}(\xi)
has the asymptotic expansion

B_{n}(\xi)\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{C_{k}(\xi)}{n^{k}},\quad n\to\infty,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R},

(2.6)

where the coefficients follow from the recursive scheme

C_{k}(\xi)=\frac{g_{k}(\xi)-g_{k}(0)}{\xi},\quad g_{k+1}(u)=\frac{d}{du}\frac{g_{k}(u)-g_{k}(0)}{u},

(2.7)

with
g_{0}(u)=g(u)
defined in (
2.3
). The first coefficients are

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{\;}c@{\;}l@{}}C_{0}(\xi)&=&\displaystyle{\frac{g(\xi)-1}{\xi}},\\[8.0pt]
C_{1}(\xi)&=&\displaystyle{-\frac{4g(\xi)^{3}-4g(\xi)\xi^{2}+\xi^{2}-4}{4\xi^{3}}},\\[8.0pt]
C_{2}(\xi)&=&\displaystyle{\frac{96g(\xi)^{5}-128g(\xi)^{3}\xi^{2}+32g(\xi)\xi^{4}-\xi^{4}+8\xi^{2}-96}{32\xi^{5}}}.\end{array}

(2.8)

The function
\beta(n)
has the expansion

\beta(n)\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{D_{k}}{n^{k}},\quad n\to\infty,\quad D_{k}=g_{k}(0).

(2.9)

The first coefficients are

D_{0}=1,\quad D_{1}={{\lower 2.58334pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}\over{\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 4$}}},\quad D_{2}={{\lower 2.58334pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}\over{\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 32$}}},\quad D_{3}=-{{\lower 2.58334pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 5$}}\over{\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 128$}}}.

(2.10)

These coefficients can be expressed in terms of the coefficients
a_{k}
of
\displaystyle{g(u)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}u^{k}}
. We have
(see
(
Temme:2015:AMI
, Remark 36.2)
)

D_{k}=\left({{\lower 2.58334pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}\over{\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 2$}}}\right)^{k}2^{k}a_{2k},\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots\,.

(2.11)

(

3
Inversion of the Student’s
t
cumulative distribution function

The inversion problem is: find
x
that satisfies the equation

F_{n}(x)=p,\quad 0<p<1.

(3.1)

We consider three different approaches, the first one is for small values of
p-\frac{1}{2}
, which will give small values of
x
. The second method is for small values of
p
, which gives large negative values
x
. Thirdly we use the uniform approximation of §
2
, which will be valid for a large range of
x
, including the values near
x=0
.

3.1
Inversion for small values of
p-\frac{1}{2}

We use the first representation in (
1.6
), and write the inversion problem in the form

x\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}x^{2k}=q,\quad q=\left(p-{{\lower 2.58334pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}\over{\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 2$}}}\right)\sqrt{n}\,B\left({{\lower 2.58334pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}\over{\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 2$}}},{{\lower 2.58334pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}\over{\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 2$}}}n\right),

(3.2)

and the
c_{k}
follow from the coefficients of the hypergeometric function:

c_{k}=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)_{k}\left(\frac{1}{2}n+\frac{1}{2}\right)_{k}}{k!\,\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)_{k}}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{n^{k}}.

(3.3)

The solution of the equation in (
3.1
) has the expansion
\displaystyle{x=q\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x_{k}q^{2k}}
, and the first coefficients are

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{\;}c@{\;}l@{}}x_{0}&=&1,\quad\displaystyle{x_{1}=\frac{n+1}{6n},\quad x_{2}=\frac{(n+1)(7n+1)}{120n^{2}},}\\[8.0pt]

(3.4)

We see that the shown coefficients are bounded for large values of

3.4

We use the representation given in (

4
Concluding remarks

We have presented approximations for the inversion problem (
3.1
) of the central Student-
t
distributions. To obtain the approximations, different methods have been considered depending on the values of
p
. In particular, one of the key elements in our analysis was
the use of an asymptotic representation of the distribution function in terms of the complementary error function. Numerical tests have shown that the approximations obtained are, in all cases, accurate. Also, they are easy to compute, which is
an important advantage when using the inverse to generate random variates distributed according to central Student-
t
probability density function.
