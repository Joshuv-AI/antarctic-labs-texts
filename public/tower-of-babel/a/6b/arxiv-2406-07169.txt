RDM: Recurrent Diffusion Model for Human Motion Generation


Abstract
Human motion generation is a challenging task due to its high dimensionality and the difficulty of generating fine-grained motions. Diffusion methods have been proposed due to their high sample quality and expressiveness. Early approaches treat the entire sequence as a whole, which is computationally expensive and restricts sequence length. In contrast, autoregressive diffusion models generate longer sequences. However, their reliance on fully denoising previous frames complicates training and inference. Consequently, we propose RDM, a new recurrent diffusion formulation inspired by Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs). RDM explicitly conditions the diffusion process on preceding noisy frames, avoiding the cost of full denoising. To overcome the challenge of maintaining its probabilistic nature, we employ Normalizing Flows to model recurrent connections. Our evaluations demonstrate RDM’s effectiveness: it achieves comparable performance to autoregressive baselines and generates long sequences that remain aligned with the text. RDM also skips diffusion steps during inference, reducing computational cost by up to 18 × 18\times over autoregressive baselines. Project page: https://www.mirgahney.com/rfdm.io/ .

Introduction

Human motion generation is crucial in computer animation, with applications spanning gaming to robotics.
Despite notable progress, it remains challenging due to the vast space of possible motions and the sophisticated equipment required to acquire the data.
Therefore, automating motion generation from natural signals, such as text, offers an intuitive interface for users and significantly reduces the effort involved.

Variational Auto-Encoders (VAEs)
[
Kingma and Welling(2013)
]
demonstrated success in mapping various modalities to motion
[
Li et al.(2021)Li, Yang, Ross, and Kanazawa
,
Tseng et al.(2022)Tseng, Castellon, and Liu
,
Petrovich et al.(2022)Petrovich, Black, and Varol
,
Tevet et al.(2022)Tevet, Gordon, Hertz, Bermano, and Cohen-Or
]
.
Nevertheless, VAEs inherently impose restrictions on the target distribution, limiting their expressiveness.

In contrast, diffusion models
[
Sohl-Dickstein et al.(2015)Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan, and Ganguli
]
do not impose restrictions on target distributions
[
Tevet et al.(2023)Tevet, Raab, Gordon, Shafir, Cohen-or, and Bermano
]
and exhibit excellent capacity for many-to-many distribution matching
[
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
]
.
MotionDiffuse
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Cai, Pan, Hong, Guo, Yang, and Liu
]
and MDM
[
Tevet et al.(2023)Tevet, Raab, Gordon, Shafir, Cohen-or, and Bermano
]
are pioneering works that employ diffusion methods
[
Sohl-Dickstein et al.(2015)Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan, and Ganguli
]
for human motion generation.
MLD
[
Chen et al.(2023b)Chen, Jiang, Liu, Huang, Fu, Chen, and Yu
]
further advanced this by applying diffusion in latent space, demonstrating efficient training and inference.

However, these approaches
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Cai, Pan, Hong, Guo, Yang, and Liu
,
Chen et al.(2023b)Chen, Jiang, Liu, Huang, Fu, Chen, and Yu
,
Tevet et al.(2023)Tevet, Raab, Gordon, Shafir, Cohen-or, and Bermano
]
, which we term
volume-diffusion
, treat the entire sequence as a monolithic input (
Fig.

2
(a)).
Consequently, this monolithic treatment contributes to motion incoherence and makes handling variable or extended sequence lengths computationally prohibitive, owing to the high cost of diffusing an entire sequence at once.

Figure 1:

On the other hand, autoregressive (b) and recurrent diffusion (c) extend sequences beyond the horizon (

).

Two primary approaches emerged to address the challenge of generating motion via sequential segments.
TEDi
[
Zhang et al.(2024)Zhang, Liu, Aberman, and Hanocka
]
extends diffusion by incorporating a temporally varying noise level within the forward process while ignoring the reverse.
Other methods
[
Zhao et al.(2025)Zhao, Li, and Tang
,
Shi et al.(2024)Shi, Wang, Jiang, Lin, Dai, and Peng
,
Meng et al.(2024)Meng, Xie, Peng, Han, and Jiang
,
Tevet et al.(2025)Tevet, Raab, Cohan, Reda, Luo, Peng, Bermano, and van de Panne
,
Han et al.(2023)Han, Peng, Dong, Xu, Ren, Shen, and Li
]
condition only the reverse process on previously estimated clean frames, ignoring the forward, which we term
autoregressive-diffusion
(
Fig.

2
(b)).
Although these approaches allow segmented generation, their autoregressive nature complicates training and inference, as it necessitates fully denoising preceding frames to generate future frames.

To overcome these limitations, we propose
Recurrent Diffusion Model (RDM)
, a novel diffusion framework for generative temporal modelling.
RDM leverages a recurrent formulation, analogous to Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)
[
Rumelhart et al.(1986)Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams
,
Jordan(1986)
]
, extending sequences beyond the training horizon with better-aligned motion (
Figs.

2
and
1
(c)).
Within this recurrent formulation, both the forward (noise addition) and reverse (denoising) diffusion processes are explicitly conditioned on previous noisy frames.
This design entangles denoising and future frame prediction tasks, providing flexibility over segment lengths and counts.
Note that removing recurrence turns RDM into a volume diffusion model.
We will demonstrate that this “recurrence” improves results (
Tab.

1
) and significantly reduces computational complexity compared to baselines (
Tab.

3
).

Although this recurrent formulation offers compelling properties, a key challenge is preserving its probabilistic nature.
This is because the recurrent transformation does not inherently guarantee valid probability distributions.
Such deviations theoretically invalidate the diffusion model’s loss, thereby compromising training and leading to incorrect samples.
To address this, we utilize normalizing flows
[
Rezende and Mohamed(2015)
,
Dinh et al.(2017)Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein, and Bengio
]
to model temporal dependencies, thereby preserving the probability under recurrent transformations.
Our contributions are:

•

A novel recurrent diffusion formulation that leverages Normalizing Flows to model spatiotemporal dependencies via noisy hidden states, establishing a non-Markovian framework for motion synthesis.

•

A flexible generation mechanism that models sequential segments interactively, enabling adaptable temporal lengths without relying on monolithic volume diffusion.

•

An efficient staircase strategy that reduces inference latency by skipping redundant diffusion steps, achieving up to
18\times
speedup over autoregressive baselines while maintaining motion fidelity.

These merits are demonstrated in text-to-motion generation tasks on the KIT-ML
[
Plappert et al.(2016)Plappert, Mandery, and Asfour
]
and HumanML3D
[
Guo et al.(2022a)Guo, Zou, Zuo, Wang, Ji, Li, and Cheng
]
datasets.

Related Work

2.1
Conditioned Motion Generation

Variational Auto-Encoders (VAEs)
[
Kingma and Welling(2013)
]
have been widely used in human motion modelling.
Notable works include Motion Transformation VAE (MT-VAE)
[
Yan et al.(2018)Yan, Rastogi, Villegas, Sunkavalli, Shechtman, Hadap, Yumer, and Lee
]
, which decomposes motion into atomic units with an LSTM encoder-decoder, and Aliakbarian
et al
\rdmaOneDot

[
Aliakbarian et al.(2020)Aliakbarian, Saleh, Salzmann, Petersson, and Gould
]
, which integrates root variations with previous poses to reduce noise.
Recently, MotionCLIP
[
Tevet et al.(2022)Tevet, Gordon, Hertz, Bermano, and Cohen-Or
]
introduces a transformer-based motion autoencoder, whereas AvatarCLIP
[
Hong et al.(2022)Hong, Zhang, Pan, Cai, Yang, and Liu
]
employs a volume rendering model for geometry and texture generation.
TEMOS
[
Petrovich et al.(2022)Petrovich, Black, and Varol
]
, similar to ACTOR
[
Petrovich et al.(2021)Petrovich, Black, and Varol
]
, learns joint motion and text spaces with transformers.
Lastly, MoMask
[
Guo et al.(2024)Guo, Mu, Javed, Wang, and Cheng
]
models human motion as hierarchical tokens, with a Masked Transformer predicting base tokens from text and filling sequences during generation.

Despite their plausible results, VAE-based methods are often limited in their expressiveness due to inherent constraints imposed on the learned distributions.

In contrast to VAEs, Normalizing Flows (NFs)
[
Rezende and Mohamed(2015)
]
train efficiently with exact maximum likelihood.
Inspired by GLOW
[
Kingma and Dhariwal(2018)
]
, MoGlow
[
Henter et al.(2020)Henter, Alexanderson, and Beskow
]
proposes an autoregressive normalization network for motion sequences.
Normalizing flows, however, are limited in their capacity to model complex distributions
[
Kirichenko et al.(2020)Kirichenko, Izmailov, and Wilson
,
Wu et al.(2020)Wu, Köhler, and Noe
]
.

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
[
Goodfellow et al.(2014)Goodfellow, Pouget-Abadie, Mirza, Xu, Warde-Farley, Ozair, Courville, and Bengio
]
have been explored for motion generation.
HP-GAN
[
Barsoum et al.(2017)Barsoum, Kender, and Liu
]
uses a sequence-to-sequence model for probabilistic human motion prediction with a custom loss.
Harvey
et al
\rdmaOneDot

[
Harvey et al.(2020)Harvey, Yurick, Nowrouzezahrai, and Pal
]
address motion blurriness based on adversarial recurrent networks.
Additionally, Wang
et al
\rdmaOneDot

[
Wang et al.(2020)Wang, Yu, Zhao, Zhang, Zhou, Yuan, and Chen
]
decouples the generator into a smooth latent transition and a global skeleton decoder for individual latent frames.
Although GANs do not impose explicit constraints on the target distribution, they are hard to train and often suffer from mode collapse
[
Durall et al.(2020)Durall, Chatzimichailidis, Labus, and Keuper
,
Kossale et al.(2022)Kossale, Airaj, and Darouichi
]
.

2.2
Diffusion Methods for Motion Generation

Inspired by non-equilibrium thermodynamics, Sohl-Dickstein
et al
\rdmaOneDot

[
Sohl-Dickstein et al.(2015)Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan, and Ganguli
]
propose a Markov chain that gradually adds noise to data (
forward
) and learns to recover it with a neural network (
reverse
).
With the success of diffusion models
[
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
,
Rombach et al.(2022)Rombach, Blattmann, Lorenz, Esser, and Ommer
]
, research shifted to temporal data such as videos
[
Ho et al.(2022)Ho, Salimans, Gritsenko, Chan, Norouzi, and Fleet
,
Chen et al.(2023a)Chen, Wu, Xie, Wu, Li, Xia, Xiao, and Lin
]
, trajectory planning
[
Chen et al.(2024)Chen, Martí Monsó, Du, Simchowitz, Tedrake, and Sitzmann
]
, and human motion
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Cai, Pan, Hong, Guo, Yang, and Liu
,
Chen et al.(2023b)Chen, Jiang, Liu, Huang, Fu, Chen, and Yu
]
.

Volume diffusion.

MotionDiffuse
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Cai, Pan, Hong, Guo, Yang, and Liu
]
is among the first to apply diffusion to text-to-motion generation using transformer generator networks on the SMPL pose space
[
Loper et al.(2015)Loper, Mahmood, Romero, Pons-Moll, and Black
]
.
MLD
[
Chen et al.(2023b)Chen, Jiang, Liu, Huang, Fu, Chen, and Yu
]
and MotionMamba
[
Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Liu, Reid, Hartley, Zhuang, and Tang
]
learn a latent space via a transformer VAE and apply diffusion within it, using a transformer denoiser and a Mamba-based
[
Dao and Gu(2024)
]
SSM denoiser, respectively.
Using diffusion in the latent space enables them to train and sample orders of magnitude faster.
However, this heavily depends on the quality of the latent space learned by the VAE, which requires careful tuning and regularization and can produce artefacts
[
Hoogeboom et al.(2024)Hoogeboom, Mensink, Heek, Lamerigts, Gao, and Salimans
]
.
Recently, Light-T2M
[
Zeng et al.(2025)Zeng, Huang, Wu, and Zheng
]
exploits local and global sequence information via an efficient Mamba denoiser, achieving SOTA performance at only
10\%
of MoMask’s
[
Guo et al.(2024)Guo, Mu, Javed, Wang, and Cheng
]
size.

Despite their success, these approaches generate temporal predictions limited to a short horizon
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Cai, Pan, Hong, Guo, Yang, and Liu
,
Chen et al.(2023b)Chen, Jiang, Liu, Huang, Fu, Chen, and Yu
]
.

Autoregressive diffusion.

TEDi
[
Zhang et al.(2024)Zhang, Liu, Aberman, and Hanocka
]
extends DDPM to the temporal dimension through temporally varying denoising, generating motion frames during inference and feeding in newly noised frames.
AMD
[
Han et al.(2023)Han, Peng, Dong, Xu, Ren, Shen, and Li
]
employs an autoregressive inverse process, an early effort to integrate temporal constraints into diffusion.
DART
[
Zhao et al.(2025)Zhao, Li, and Tang
]
learns motion primitives in the latent space while UniPhys
[
Wu et al.(2025)Wu, Karunratanakul, Luo, and Tang
]
encodes variable motion frames into the latent space.
Meng
et al
\rdmaOneDot

[
Meng et al.(2024)Meng, Xie, Peng, Han, and Jiang
]
retain 3D features to ensure a uniform distribution before autoregressively denoising with a masked transformer.
Meanwhile, CloSD
[
Tevet et al.(2025)Tevet, Raab, Cohan, Reda, Luo, Peng, Bermano, and van de Panne
]
and A-MDM
[
Shi et al.(2024)Shi, Wang, Jiang, Lin, Dai, and Peng
]
apply the same autoregressive conditioning in input pose space rather than latent space.

As discussed in
Sec.

1
, these autoregressive diffusion methods
[
Zhao et al.(2025)Zhao, Li, and Tang
,
Shi et al.(2024)Shi, Wang, Jiang, Lin, Dai, and Peng
,
Meng et al.(2024)Meng, Xie, Peng, Han, and Jiang
,
Tevet et al.(2025)Tevet, Raab, Cohan, Reda, Luo, Peng, Bermano, and van de Panne
,
Han et al.(2023)Han, Peng, Dong, Xu, Ren, Shen, and Li
]
condition only the reverse process on previously estimated frames, inheriting the same training and inference complications.

[
Rumelhart et al.(1986)Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams
,
Jordan(1986)
]
, we developed our
Recurrent Diffusion Model (RDM)
for temporal modelling, ensuring both forward and reverse processes follow temporal constraints by autoregressively adding and removing noise, departing from previous diffusion models
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Cai, Pan, Hong, Guo, Yang, and Liu
,
Chen et al.(2023b)Chen, Jiang, Liu, Huang, Fu, Chen, and Yu
,
Zhao et al.(2025)Zhao, Li, and Tang
,
Shi et al.(2024)Shi, Wang, Jiang, Lin, Dai, and Peng
,
Meng et al.(2024)Meng, Xie, Peng, Han, and Jiang
,
Tevet et al.(2025)Tevet, Raab, Cohan, Reda, Luo, Peng, Bermano, and van de Panne

Unlike Diffusion-Forcing
[
Chen et al.(2024)Chen, Martí Monsó, Du, Simchowitz, Tedrake, and Sitzmann
]

By leveraging normalizing flows

,
Dinh et al.(2017)Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein, and Bengio
,
Kingma and Dhariwal(2018)
]
to model temporal transitions, our method captures more complex distributions than simple Gaussians, boosting capacity.
RDM also skips diffusion steps during inference, reducing computational costs.

3
Background

3.1
Diffusion Models

Diffusion models
[
Sohl-Dickstein et al.(2015)Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan, and Ganguli
,
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
]
add noise to data via a
forward process
and recover it through a
reverse process
, both following a Markov chain.
Given a pair
(x^{(j)},u^{(j)})
, where
x^{(j)}
is a sequence of poses and
u^{(j)}
the description, each
x^{(j)}
has
F
frames
(H^{i\;(j)})\;i\in\{1,2,...,F\}
, with
H^{i\;(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
as the pose of the
i
-th frame.
The reverse process
p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t})
is modelled as a Gaussian transition learned with a neural network;

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x_{0:T})

\displaystyle:=p(x_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t})

(1)

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{N}(x_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(x_{t},t,c),\Sigma)

Here,
\mu_{\theta}
is parameterized by neural networks, while
\Sigma
can be either learned jointly or held fixed.
Here,
c
denotes textual description embeddings extracted with a pretrained neural network.
The approximate posterior distribution
q(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1})
of the diffusion model is called the
forward process
or
diffusion process
.
This process gradually adds Gaussian noise to the data according to a variance scheduler
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},...,\beta_{T}
, which can be learned
[
Sohl-Dickstein et al.(2015)Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan, and Ganguli
]
or fixed:

\displaystyle q(x_{1:T}|x_{0})

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(x_{t}|x_{t-1})

(2)

\displaystyle q(x_{t}|x_{t-1})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{N}(x_{t};\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}x_{t-1},\beta_{t}\mathbf{I})

Training is performed by optimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the forward and reverse processes.
As suggested by Ho
et al
\rdmaOneDot

[
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
]
, choosing a specific parametrization of
\mu_{\theta}(x_{t},t,c)
, yields the loss:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[w(t)\|x-f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,c)\|^{2}\right]

(3)

Here,
f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,c)
is a learnable neural network, and the weight
w(t)
can be freely specified.
Efficient training can be achieved using Monte Carlo integration to approximate the expectation.
We uniformly sample the diffusion step
t
and minimize the loss using stochastic gradient descent.

3.2
Normalizing Flows

A normalizing flow
[
Rezende and Mohamed(2015)
]
is a transformation
Y=f(X)
that transforms one random variable
X
into another
Y
via successive volume-preserving, invertible and bijective
K
transformations
f_{K}\circ f_{K-1}\circ...\circ f_{1}
(
Eq.

4
).

We use normalizing flows for modelling temporal human motion.
Given a data point
x^{(j)}
with
F
frames from a distribution
q(\mathcal{X})
, we split it into
L
segments
\{x^{i}\}_{i=0}^{L}
, each with equal length.
Normalizing flow predicts the next segment distribution
x^{i+1}\sim P_{X^{i+1}}
from the previous
x^{i}\sim P_{X^{i}}
.
Applying the change of variable theorem, we obtain:

\displaystyle x^{i+1}

\displaystyle=x_{K}=f_{K}\circ f_{K-1}\circ...\circ f_{1}(x^{i})

(4)

\displaystyle P_{X^{i+1}}(x^{i+1})

\displaystyle=P_{X^{i}}(x^{i})\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left|\det\left(\frac{\partial f_{k}(x_{k-1})}{\partial x_{k-1}}\right)\right|^{-1}

(5)

Samples from the transformed distribution
P_{X^{i+1}}
are generated by sampling from the base distribution
x^{i}\sim P_{X^{i}}
, then applying the transformations in
Eq.

4
to get
x^{i+1}
.

]
.

frames (right of the dashed line); green/red circles mark correct/broken dribbling frames.

4
Recurrent Diffusion Model

We extend diffusion models
[
Sohl-Dickstein et al.(2015)Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan, and Ganguli
,
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
]
into the temporal dimension by establishing a recurrent diffusion framework.
Specifically, we structure the generative process over a two-dimensional dependency grid across diffusion and temporal steps (
Fig.

3
).

Formally, let
i
denote the temporal step and
t
the diffusion step.
As shown in
Fig.

3
, the diffusion state
x^{i}_{t}
is jointly conditioned on the preceding diffusion state within the same temporal segment,
x^{i}_{t-1}
(intra-segment denoising), and the corresponding diffusion state from the previous temporal segment,
x^{i-1}_{t}
(inter-segment recurrence).
This formulation enforces global temporal continuity across sequential segments while preserving fine-grained motion within each segment.

However, a critical challenge arises: recurrent conditioning on intermediate noisy frames does not inherently guarantee valid probability distributions.
This mathematically invalidates the standard diffusion training objective (
Eq.

3
), as the KL divergence between the forward and reverse processes becomes intractable or undefined, leading to degraded sample quality during inference.
To resolve this, we model the recurrent transformations using invertible Normalizing Flows
[
Rezende and Mohamed(2015)
,
Dinh et al.(2017)Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein, and Bengio
]
.
Leveraging their invertibility and tractable Jacobians, we ensure the underlying distributions remain valid; we next detail the forward and reverse processes and the resulting training loss.

4.1
Forward Process

Given a sequence and text data sample pair
(x^{(j)},u^{(j)})
, we begin by splitting each sequence into
L
segments, denoted as
\{x^{i}_{0}\}_{i=0}^{L}
, each of equal length.

For the initial segment,
x^{0}_{0}
, RDM applies Gaussian noise in a manner consistent with prior diffusion models; this process is termed
diffusion-only
.
In contrast, for all subsequent segments
\{x^{i}_{0},\;i>0\}
, our approach diverges from previous works
[
Sohl-Dickstein et al.(2015)Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan, and Ganguli
,
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
]
.
Here, RDM adds noise following the
diffusion-flow
as illustrated in
Fig.

3
.
Within the
diffusion-flow
, noise addition is conditioned on the previously diffused segment within the same temporal step (
x^{i}_{t-1}
) and the current diffusion step of the previous temporal segment (
x^{i-1}_{t}
).

To obtain the noisy grid samples
\{x_{t}^{i}\}_{i=0}^{L}
for
t=1,...,T
, RDM first computes
x^{0}_{t}
within the
diffusion-only
, then applies normalizing flow to the corresponding temporal step.
Consistent with Ho et al.
[
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
]
, the step size is controlled by the variance scheduler
{\beta_{t}\in(0,1)}_{t=0}^{T}
for
t=0,...,T
.
The approximate posterior is defined as:

\displaystyle q(x^{1:L}_{1:T}|x^{0}_{0})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{L}\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(x^{i}_{t}|x^{i}_{t-1},x^{i-1}_{t})

(6)

4.2
Reverse Process

The reverse process follows the 2D grid in reverse.
While the
diffusion-only
reverse process uses the formulation from
[
Sohl-Dickstein et al.(2015)Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan, and Ganguli
,
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
]
, for
diffusion-flow
segments
\{x^{i}_{t-1},i>0\}
, the reverse process leverages temporal relations.
The generative distribution depends on the previous diffusion step (
x^{i}_{t}
) and the previous segment (
x^{i-1}_{t-1}
), as follows:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{1:T}|x^{i}_{0},x^{i-1}_{T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1})

(7)

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0:T})=p(x^{0}_{T})\prod_{i=1}^{L}\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1})

(8)

In the
diffusion-only
segments, with a small
\beta_{t}
, the reverse distribution is Gaussian
[
Feller(1949)
,
Sohl-Dickstein et al.(2015)Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan, and Ganguli
]
, requiring only mean and variance estimation.
Conversely, for
diffusion-flow
, the distribution
q(x^{i}_{t}|x^{i}_{t-1},x^{i-1}_{t})
is unknown because of the nonlinear flow transformations.
Consequently, the reverse distribution
p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1})
is also unknown, which presents a challenge for sampling.

To sample from this intractable distribution, RDM exploits the invertibility of normalizing flows
[
Rezende and Mohamed(2015)
]
.
It inverts the step to
diffusion-only
where distributions are Gaussian, then samples and applies the forward flow to reach the target temporal step, as described next:

\displaystyle x^{0}_{t-1}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{N}(x^{0}_{t-1},f_{\phi}^{(-i)}(\mu_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},t,i,c)),(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\mathbf{I})

(9)

\displaystyle x^{i}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=f_{\phi}^{(i)}(x^{0}_{t-1})

(10)

where
\mu_{\theta}
is a learnable neural network (denoiser); both
(-i)
and
(i)
indicate traversing the inverse and forward flow
i
times, respectively;
f_{\phi}
represents the flow model parameterized by
\phi
.

c:=\varphi(u^{(j)})
, where
\varphi
is a pretrained CLIP model
[
Radford et al.(2021)Radford, Kim, Hallacy, Ramesh, Goh, Agarwal, Sastry, Askell, Mishkin, Clark, Krueger, and Sutskever
]
that extracts embeddings from the textual description.

4.3
Normalizing Flow

We use a real-valued non-volume preserving (Real-NVP)
[
Dinh et al.(2017)Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein, and Bengio
]
as a probabilistic transformation, as described in the following equations:

\displaystyle[\sigma_{\phi},\mu_{\phi}]=f_{\phi}(x^{i-1}_{t},c))

(11)

\displaystyle x^{i}_{t}=x^{i-1}_{t}\odot\text{exp}(\sigma_{\phi}(x^{i-1}_{t},c))+\mu_{\phi}(x^{i-1}_{t},c)

where
\odot
is element-wise multiplication, and both
\sigma_{\phi}
and
\mu_{\phi}
are deep feed-forward networks.
When the flow
f_{\phi}
is restricted to a linear transformation, the reverse-process distributions remain Gaussian in closed form, with the mean shifted and the variance scaled by the flow (
Sec.

I.1.1
), reducing to a loss of the same form as Ho et al.
[
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
]
with additional weighting terms. We use the more expressive non-linear coupling by default; the linear case above shows the flow need not add substantial complexity.

4.4
Training

During training, RDM maximizes the variational lower bound (VLB) of the log-likelihood of the 2D grid, which is simplified to (see
appendix

I
for full derivation):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{VLB}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{D_{kl}(q(x^{0:L}_{T}|x^{0:L}_{0})\|p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{T}))}_{\mathcal{L}^{0:1}_{T}}+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{D_{kl}(q(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t},x^{0:L}_{0})\|p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t}))}_{\mathcal{L}^{0:L}_{t-1}}

(12)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{q}[\underbrace{-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0}|x^{0:L}_{1}))}_{\mathcal{L}^{0:L}_{0}}]

4.4.1
KL computation

The KL terms
\mathcal{L}^{0}_{t-1}
within the
diffusion-only
segments are straightforward, following Gaussian distributions; RDM computes them in closed form, similar to Ho
et al
\rdmaOneDot

[
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
]
.
In contrast, the intermediate KL terms
\mathcal{L}^{1:L}_{t-1}
pose a significant challenge because they follow unknown distributions, which is a consequence of the nonlinear flow transformations.

Learning the posterior of unknown distributions often requires methods like Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
[
Metropolis et al.(1953)Metropolis, Rosenbluth, Rosenbluth, Teller, and Teller
,
Vono et al.(2022)Vono, Paulin, and Doucet
]
, but with
1000
diffusion steps, these methods are infeasible due to their slow convergence.
This raises a crucial question: how can we learn the reverse process efficiently?

To train efficiently, RDM uses the invertibility of normalizing flows (
Sec.

3.2
) to derive a closed-form loss.
It employs an inverse flow to map predicted samples from temporal step
i
back to temporal step
0
(
diffusion-only
), where the distributions are Gaussian, and then computes the KL in closed form.
The result is then transformed back to temporal step
i
via the forward flow.

Consistent with Ho
et al
\rdmaOneDot

[
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
]
, we learn only the mean of the distribution and fix the variance to
\sigma_{t}=\beta_{t}
.
This approach yields a formula similar to that of
[
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t-1}^{i}=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[w(t)\|x^{i}_{0}-f_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},t,i)\|^{2}\right]

(13)

Here,
x^{i}_{0}
is the clean segment at temporal step
i
and
f_{\theta}
is a learnable neural network that predicts a clean segment; refer to
appendix

I
for a full derivation.

4.4.2
Training protocol

We optimize the parameters of both the flow (
\phi
) and the denoiser (
\theta
) jointly by minimizing the expectation in
Eq.

13
.
This expectation is approximated via Monte Carlo integration, where we uniformly sample a diffusion step
t
and a temporal step
i
at each iteration.
The training sequence is then processed by traversing the normalizing flow to reach the sampled temporal position within the dependency grid (see
Fig.

3
).
The complete training sequence and the explicit diffusion update mechanics are detailed in Algorithm
1
.

4.4.3

Unlike existing autoregressive frameworks
[
Zhao et al.(2025)Zhao, Li, and Tang

Shi et al.(2024)Shi, Wang, Jiang, Lin, Dai, and Peng

Meng et al.(2024)Meng, Xie, Peng, Han, and Jiang

Tevet et al.(2025)Tevet, Raab, Cohan, Reda, Luo, Peng, Bermano, and van de Panne

Han et al.(2023)Han, Peng, Dong, Xu, Ren, Shen, and Li
]

Fig.

3
).
Empirically, we find that setting the initial step of the staircase equal to the total number of segments yields an optimal balance between computational complexity and synthesis quality (see
appendices

E
and
F

5
Experiments

The primary objective of our evaluation is to quantify the performance and stability of the recurrent diffusion framework across distinct generative tasks.
Unlike volume-diffusion methods where the computational cost scales prohibitively with sequence length, segment-based generation must instead manage the potential accumulation of temporal errors across sequential segments.
For a controlled comparison across segment configurations, we denoise in pose space rather than latent space, following MotionDiffuse
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Cai, Pan, Hong, Guo, Yang, and Liu
]
and Light-T2M
[
Zeng et al.(2025)Zeng, Huang, Wu, and Zheng
]
: pose-space frames skip the pretrained VAE, so segment length and count are simply chunking parameters we can vary freely to ablate their impact on generative stability, without retraining a latent encoder per configuration.

and
2
.

]
, MLD
[

]
, MotionMamba
[
Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Liu, Reid, Hartley, Zhuang, and Tang
]
, Light-T2M
[
Zeng et al.(2025)Zeng, Huang, Wu, and Zheng
]
— non-diffusion approaches — TEMOS

6
Conclusion

We introduce Recurrent Diffusion Model (RDM), a temporal diffusion framework that enforces temporal constraints by recurrently adding and removing noise.
RDM’s volume method matches SOTA volume results. While the recurrent version is on par with autoregressive diffusion, it generates sequences beyond the training horizon with significantly lower inference complexity. We believe RDM advances temporal diffusion models and naturally extends into the latent space, though these avenues remain for future research.

Limitations.

Although RDM can generate sequences beyond the training horizon, its numerical stability depends on the flow, which is challenging as the number of segments increases.
Additionally, generated frames become noisier over time, especially with a few recurrence steps. (See
Sec.

D.1
)

D.1
Numerical instability

While we attempted to adapt normalizing flows for temporal modelling by augmenting them with an LSTM, they still suffer from numerical instability, particularly during multi-step inverse flow computations.
The primary source of this instability is the
\sigma_{\phi}
function within the mapping, where large values can cause an overflow of the inverse mapping

We mitigated this by clamping the values to an acceptable range using a HardTanh function with limits of

While this successfully resolved the instability in the loss, it did not address the issue of very long recurrence.
We identify this as a limitation of our current method, and a more comprehensive analysis of this long-recurrence behaviour will be addressed in future work.

Appendix E
Notes on recurrent formulation properties

The design of our 2D grid exhibits inherent symmetries, which can be exploited to skip diffusion sampling steps, leading to more efficient inference.
While this primarily benefits inference, a different configuration allows for efficient training, albeit with a trade-off in flexibility during the inference phase.
This section will detail these properties and their implications for computational efficiency.

Diffusion-flow sample symmetries.

By design, we can arrive at
diffusion-flow
samples through multiple mathematically equivalent sampling paths (see
Fig.

9
).
Empirically, however, these paths yield varying sample quality and computational efficiency.
Specifically, paths that rely on the normalizing flow for temporal sampling produce lower quality, as the flow has less capacity than the denoiser and is not as well-suited for temporal modelling.
In terms of efficiency, paths that require fewer diffusion steps are more computationally efficient, particularly with larger sequence lengths, as the denoising process is the most expensive operation.
This design, therefore, allows for an explicit trade-off between computational efficiency and sample quality.

2D grid symmetries.

Since normalizing flows are invertible transformations, they allow for two distinct grid formulations for conditioning the reverse process: one with a forward flow and one with an inverse flow. We can thus condition the reverse process on either
x^{i+1}_{t-1}
or
x^{i-1}_{t-1}
.
While both options are mathematically equivalent, conditioning on
x^{i-1}_{t-1}
is more numerically stable as it avoids computing an additional flow inverse transformation.
We adopted this latter approach to parameterize our reverse flow and the associated loss function, as will be described in the next section.

Appendix F
RDM sampling

Unlike previous methods
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Cai, Pan, Hong, Guo, Yang, and Liu
,
Chen et al.(2023b)Chen, Jiang, Liu, Huang, Fu, Chen, and Yu
,
Han et al.(2023)Han, Peng, Dong, Xu, Ren, Shen, and Li
,
Zeng et al.(2025)Zeng, Huang, Wu, and Zheng
]
, our approach does not require sampling Gaussian noise for the full sequence.
Instead, we leverage the flow model to skip diffusion steps by sampling in a “staircase" through our 2D grid.
This significantly enhances the computational efficiency and throughput when dealing with temporal data (see
algorithm

2
).

The point at which we initiate this staircase sampling presents a unique trade-off between computational complexity and sampling quality.
One extreme, which we term “disentangled sampling", involves generating the first segment with
diffusion-only
and then rolling out the rest using only the normalizing flow.
This offers the lowest computational complexity but results in the lowest sample quality, as it effectively disentangles the diffusion and temporal axis.
The other extreme is to initiate the staircase from the beginning with our
diffusion-flow
approach.
This path yields the highest computational complexity but also produces better sample quality.
A comparison of these strategies is detailed in

Appendix G
Segment-based diffusion baselines

Relation to latent-space diffusion.
RDM and latent-space diffusion models improve efficiency along complementary axes: latent models reduce spatial dimensionality by compressing each frame, or the whole sequence, into a compact code, whereas RDM restructures long-range
temporal
dependencies via its 2D recurrent grid, independent of the representation space used for individual frames. Because the two mechanisms are decoupled, RDM could in principle be combined with a latent-space denoiser for compounding efficiency gains; we leave this combination for future work.

To maintain flexibility and facilitate fair comparisons, we chose to denoise in pose space, similar to MotionDiffuse
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Cai, Pan, Hong, Guo, Yang, and Liu
]
.
This decision was driven by the inherent limitations of latent diffusion methods, which require training a different VAE for each configuration of the number of segments, thereby complicating our ability to experiment with different recurrence lengths.

While our recurrent diffusion framework is agnostic to the choice of denoiser, we adopted the denoiser backbone from MotionDiffuse
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Cai, Pan, Hong, Guo, Yang, and Liu
]
.
We chose this for its excellent performance and its widespread use in other baselines
[
Han et al.(2023)Han, Peng, Dong, Xu, Ren, Shen, and Li
,
Shi et al.(2024)Shi, Wang, Jiang, Lin, Dai, and Peng
,
Zhang et al.(2023)Zhang, Guo, Pan, Cai, Hong, Li, Yang, and Liu
]
, which establishes MotionDiffuse as our closest comparison point.

For a rigorous evaluation, we trained custom MotionDiffuse
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Cai, Pan, Hong, Guo, Yang, and Liu
]
baselines to handle variable sequence horizons, mirroring our training setup.
This baseline, which takes corrupted motion segments and predicts the next clean segments, was trained in two settings: one with
4
segments (MD-49) and another with
7
segments (MD-28).
These MD-x baselines effectively represent RDM’s architecture without recurrent connections, with diffusion applied to each segment independently. This effectively makes MD-x serve as an ablation of RDM’s recurrent connection.

]

2e^{-4}

.
Our full training takes

We train the full model for

We train the flow and denoiser together to minimize the reconstruction loss (see

).

For the FLOPs, we use the

We ran both experiments on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU.

Architectures.

We use
4/7
segments for a maximum sequence length of
196
, yielding a segment length of
49/28
frames.
For normalizing flow, we used a
1

256

256

6

For the diffusion denoiser, we utilise a similar temporal transformer-based architecture to that of MotionDiffuse
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Cai, Pan, Hong, Guo, Yang, and Liu

We used

[

, respectively.
As for the diffusion model, the number of diffusion steps is

To incorporate temporal conditioning, we use positional encoding with different frequencies for the diffusion step number and the frame number, each mapped into latent space with a separate shallow MLP.

[

We use the forward normalizing flow according to the 2D grid defined in the previous section

As discussed in the main text, our forward diffusion process across the grid is defined by the following equations:

\displaystyle q(x^{i}_{1:T}|x^{i}_{0},x^{i-1}_{T})

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(x^{i}_{t}|x^{i}_{t-1},x^{i-1}_{t})

(16)

\displaystyle q(x^{1:L}_{1:T}|x^{0}_{0},x^{0-1}_{T})

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(x^{1:L}_{t}|x^{1:L}_{t-1})

(17)

\displaystyle q(x^{1:L}_{1:T}|x^{0}_{0},x^{0-1}_{T})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{L}\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(x^{i}_{t}|x^{i}_{t-1},x^{i-1}_{t})

(18)

As we are using a 2D grid with forward normalizing flow, our reverse diffusion process is defined by the next equations:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{1:T}|x^{i}_{0},x^{i-1}_{T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1})

(19)

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0:T})=p(x^{0:L}_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t})

(20)

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0:T})=p(x^{0}_{T})\prod_{i=1}^{L}\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1})

(21)

Let us begin by equating our data log probability
\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0}))
as follows:

\displaystyle-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0}))

\displaystyle\leq-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0}))+D_{kl}(q(x^{0:L}_{1:T}|x^{0:L}_{0})\|p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{1:T}|x^{0:L}_{0}))

\displaystyle=-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0}))+\mathbb{E}_{x^{0:L}_{1:T}\sim q(x^{0:L}_{1:T}|x^{0:L}_{0})}\big[\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{1:T}|x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0:T})/p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0})}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{1:T}|x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0:T})}\big]

Then let
\mathcal{L}_{VLB}=\mathbb{E}_{q(x^{0:L}_{0:T})}\big[\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{1:T}|x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0:T})}\big]\geq-\mathbb{E}_{q(x^{0:L}_{0:T})}p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0})

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{VLB}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{1:T}|x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0:T})}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log\frac{\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(x^{0:L}_{t}|x^{0:L}_{t-1})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t})}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{T}))+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{t}|x^{0:L}_{t-1})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t})}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{T}))+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{t}|x^{0:L}_{t-1})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t})}+\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{1}|x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0}|x^{0:L}_{1})}\big]

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{VLB}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{T}))+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\big(\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t},x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t})}.\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{t}|x^{0:L}_{0})}{q(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{0})}\big)

\displaystyle+\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{1}|x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0}|x^{0:L}_{1})}\big]\;\;\text{(Bayes's rule)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{T}))+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t},x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t})}+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{t}|x^{0:L}_{0})}{q(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{0})}+\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{1}|x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0}|x^{0:L}_{1})}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{T}))+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t},x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t})}+\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{T}|x^{0:L}_{0})}{q(x^{0:L}_{1}|x^{0:L}_{0})}+\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{1}|x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0}|x^{0:L}_{1})}\big]

(Intermediate terms cancels)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{T}|x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{T})}+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t},x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t})}-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0}|x^{0:L}_{1}))\big]\;\;\text{(Rearranging logs)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\underbrace{D_{kl}(q(x^{0:L}_{T}|x^{0:L}_{0})\|p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{T}))}_{\mathcal{L}^{0:1}_{T}}+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{D_{kl}(q(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t},x^{0:L}_{0})\|p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t}))}_{\mathcal{L}^{0:L}_{t-1}}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{q}[\underbrace{-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0}|x^{0:L}_{1}))}_{\mathcal{L}^{0:L}_{0}}]\big]

Let us label each term in the lower bound separately and further expand them:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{0:1}_{T}

\displaystyle=D_{kl}(q(x^{0:L}_{T}|x^{0:L}_{0})\|p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{T}))=\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log\big(\frac{q(x^{0}_{T}|x^{0}_{0})\prod_{i=1}^{L}q(x^{i}_{T}|x^{i}_{0},x^{i-1}_{T})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0}_{T})\prod_{i=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{T}|x^{i-1}_{T})}\big)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log\frac{q(x^{0}_{T}|x^{0}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0}_{T})}+\sum_{i=1}^{L}\log\frac{q(x^{i}_{T}|x^{i}_{0},x^{i-1}_{T})}{p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{T}|x^{i-1}_{T})}\big]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{D_{kl}(q(x^{0}_{T}|x^{0}_{0})\|p_{\theta}(x^{0}_{T}))}_{L^{0}_{T}}+\sum_{i=1}^{L}\underbrace{D_{kl}(q(x^{i}_{T}|x^{i}_{0},x^{i-1}_{T})\|p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{T}|x^{i-1}_{T}))}_{L^{i}_{T}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{0:L}_{0}

\displaystyle=-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{0}|x^{0:L}_{1}))=-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0}_{0}|x^{0}_{1}))-\log(\prod_{i=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{0}|x^{i-1}_{0},x^{i}_{1}))

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{0}_{0}|x^{0}_{1}))}_{\mathcal{L}^{0}_{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{L}\underbrace{-\log(p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{0}|x^{i-1}_{0},x^{i}_{1}))}_{\mathcal{L}^{i}_{0}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{0:L}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=D_{kl}(q(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t},x^{0:L}_{0})\|p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t}))=\log\frac{q(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t},x^{0:L}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0:L}_{t-1}|x^{0:L}_{t})}

\displaystyle=\log\big(\frac{q(x^{0}_{t-1}|x^{0}_{t},x^{0}_{0})\prod_{i=1}^{L}q(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},x^{i}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0}_{t-1}|x^{0}_{t})\prod_{i=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1})}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log\frac{q(x^{0}_{t-1}|x^{0}_{t},x^{0}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{0}_{t-1}|x^{0}_{t})}+\sum_{i=1}^{L}\log\frac{q(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},x^{i}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1})}]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{D_{kl}(q(x^{0}_{t-1}|x^{0}_{t},x^{0}_{0})\|p_{\theta}(x^{0}_{t-1}|x^{0}_{t}))}_{\mathcal{L}^{0}_{t-1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{L}\underbrace{D_{kl}(q(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},x^{i}_{0})\|p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1}))}_{\mathcal{L}^{i}_{t-1}}

Every KL term in

The remaining are the KL terms in

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{i}_{t-1}

I.1.1
The case of linear flow

As we restrict our flow to a linear transformation, the resulting distributions remain Gaussian.
All KL terms in
\mathcal{L}^{1:}_{.}
are computed in closed form, with mean shifted and standard deviation scaled according to the normalizing flow.
Depending on the parametrization, we arrive at the same loss as Ho
et al
\rdmaOneDot

[

with different weight terms.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{i}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=D_{KL}(\mathcal{N}(x^{i}_{t-1};\mu(x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},x^{i}_{0}),\Sigma_{t})\|\mathcal{N}(x^{i}_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},t,i),\Sigma_{\theta}))

(23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t-1}^{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\tilde{w}(t)\|x^{i}_{0}-f_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},t,i)\|^{2}\right]\;\;\text{(Same as diffusion loss)}

(24)

Where
\tilde{w}(t)
represents the signal-to-noise ratio, accounting for the flow transformation as well, and can take different forms depending on the chosen parameterization.
Here we show the case of the parametrization in Ho

\rdmaOneDot

\displaystyle\tilde{w}(t)=\frac{\beta^{2}_{t}}{2\sigma^{2}_{t}\alpha_{t}(1-\bar{\alpha})}|det\frac{\partial f_{\phi}}{\partial x^{i}_{0}}|^{-1}

I.1.2
The generic case of non-linear flow

Since we employ a generic nonlinear flow, each KL term in
\mathcal{L}^{1:}_{.}
(except
\mathcal{L}^{1:}_{0}
) compares two unknown distributions defined via a normalizing flow.

Learning the posterior of these distributions typically requires techniques like Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
[
Metropolis et al.(1953)Metropolis, Rosenbluth, Rosenbluth, Teller, and Teller
,
Vono et al.(2022)Vono, Paulin, and Doucet
]
. However, MCMC methods suffer from slow convergence, making learning computationally infeasible across
1000
diffusion steps.
This raises a crucial question: how can we efficiently learn the reverse process?

To bypass MCMC bottlenecks, we leverage the geometric properties of normalizing flows to derive an exact, closed-form solution for these KL terms through a three-step mapping procedure:

1.

Inverse Flow Mapping:
We apply the inverse flow to map the complex samples
x^{i}_{t}
back to the baseline,
diffusion-only
state
x^{0}_{t}
.

2.

Gaussian Evaluation:
In this
diffusion-only
latent space, the distributions collapse to standard Gaussians, allowing us to compute the KL divergence analytically.

3.

Temporal Forward Push:
We push the calculated distribution back to temporal step
i
via the forward flow.

Ultimately, this mapping transforms the complex objective into a simple KL divergence between two normal distributions, scaled by additional weighting terms derived from the inverse Jacobian determinant of the flow transformation:

\displaystyle\log\big(q_{\phi}(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},x^{i}_{0})\big)

\displaystyle=\log\mathcal{N}(x^{0}_{t-1})-\sum_{j=1}^{i}\log|det\frac{\partial f_{\phi}}{\partial x^{0}_{0}}|\;\;\text{(Flow properties)}

\displaystyle\log\big(p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t-1}|x^{i}_{t},x^{j-1}_{t-1})\big)

\displaystyle=\log\mathcal{N}(f^{-i}(x^{i}_{t-1}))-\sum_{j=1}^{i}\log|det\frac{\partial f_{\phi}}{\partial x^{i}_{0}}|\;\;\text{(Flow properties)}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{i}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}q_{\phi}(x^{0}_{t-1}|x^{0}_{t},x^{0}_{0})\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(x^{0}_{t-1};\mu(x^{0}_{t-2},x^{0}_{0}),\beta_{t})}{\mathcal{N}(f^{-i}(x^{i}_{t-1});\mu_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},t,i),\Sigma_{\theta})}\big|det\frac{\partial f_{\phi}}{\partial x^{i}_{0}}\big|^{-1}

(Flow properties)

The KL between two Gaussians is computed in closed form, and following a similar parametrization to Ho
et al
\rdmaOneDot

[
Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel
]
, the loss is further reduced to the difference between means, as depicted in the following equation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q^{0}}[w(t)|det\frac{\partial f_{\phi}}{\partial x^{i}_{0}}|^{-1}\|\mu(x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},x^{i}_{0})-\mu_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},t,i)\|^{2}]\;\;\text{(Same as diffusion loss)}

(25)

The question remains: how to get the means of the distribution at the
diffusion-only
.
As the forward process follows a Markovian chain, the mean is simply the previously sampled noisy segment
x^{0}_{t-1}
.
We choose to predict the clean segment, and we get the predicted mean following the grid (using the inverse flow and the forward diffusion) as follows:

\displaystyle\mu(x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},x^{i}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mu}_{t}\left(f^{(-i)}_{\phi}(x^{i}_{t}),x^{0}_{0}\right)

(26)

\displaystyle\mu_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t},x^{i-1}_{t-1},t,i)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mu}_{t}\left(f^{(-i)}_{\phi}(x^{i}_{t}),\hat{x}^{0}_{0}\right)

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t-1}^{i}

\displaystyle=w_{t}\|x^{0}_{0}-\hat{x}^{0}_{0}\|^{2}_{2}

(28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t-1}^{i}

\displaystyle=w_{t}\|f^{(-i)}_{\phi}(x^{i}_{0})-f^{(-i)}_{\phi}(\hat{x}^{i}_{0})\|^{2}_{2}

(29)

where
\tilde{\mu}_{t}(\cdot,\cdot)
represents the analytical posterior mean of the diffusion process in the Gaussian baseline space, defined as:

\tilde{\mu}_{t}(z_{t},z_{0})=\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}z_{t}+\frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\beta_{t}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}z_{0}

(30)

By defining the inputs in terms of our inverted flow mappings—where
z_{t}=f^{(-i)}_{\phi}(x^{i}_{t})
and
z_{0}=x^{0}_{0}
—we smoothly isolate the flow transformations. Cancelling out identical terms during the subtraction of
\mu
and
\mu_{\theta}
isolates the data reconstruction error, yielding the simplified loss formulations shown above.

We found that the previous loss
Eq.

29
is unstable and difficult to optimize because it relies on the numerical stability of the inverse flow and assumes a perfect flow model.
This makes the diffusion model’s performance depend on the flow’s effectiveness.
Therefore, we decided to optimize the subsequent loss instead.
As the flow is a smooth mapping, we can employ the Taylor expansion series around
0
, which leads us to the following equation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t-1}^{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q^{0}}[w(t)|det\frac{\partial f_{\phi}}{\partial x^{i}_{0}}|^{-1}\|x^{i}_{0}-f_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t},f^{i-1}_{t-1},t,i)\|^{2}]\;\;\text{(Same as diffusion loss)}

(31)
