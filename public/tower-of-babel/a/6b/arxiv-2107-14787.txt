On hamiltonian cycles in Cayley graphs of order 𝒑 ​ 𝒒 ​ 𝒓 ​ 𝒔 pqr\mkern-1.8mus
Dave Witte Morris

Abstract
. Let G G be a finite group. We show that if | G | = p ​ q ​ r ​ s |G|=pqrs , where p p , q q , r r , and s s are distinct odd primes, then every connected Cayley graph on G G has a hamiltonian cycle.



1.
Introduction

Definition 1.1

(cf.
[
5
, p. 34]
)
.

If
A
is a subset of a finite group
G
, then the corresponding
Cayley graph

\Cay(G;A)
is the undirected graph whose vertices are the elements of
G
, and such that vertices
g
and
h
are adjacent if and only if
g^{-1}h\in A\cup A^{-1}
, where
A^{-1}=\{\,a^{-1}\mid a\in A\,\}
.

It is easy to see (and well known) that
\Cay(G;A)
is connected if and only if
A
is a generating set of
G
. Several papers show that all connected Cayley graphs of certain orders are hamiltonian:

Theorem 1.2

(see
[
7
,
8
,
11
]
and references therein)
.

Let
A
be a generating set of a finite group
G
. If
|G|
has any of the following forms
(
where
p
,
q
, and
r
are distinct primes, and
k
is a positive integer
)
, then
\Cay(G;A)
has a hamiltonian cycle:

(1)

kp
, where
k\leq 47
,

(2)

kpq
, where
k\leq 7
,

(3)

pqr
,

(4)

kp^{2}
, where
\leq 4
,

(5)

kp^{3}
, where
k\leq 2
.

(6)

p^{k}
.

The purpose of this note is to add
pqrs
to the list when it is odd:

Theorem 1.3
.

If
p
,
q
,
r
, and
s
are distinct odd primes, then every connected Cayley graph of order
pqrs
has a hamiltonian cycle.

Remark 1.4
.

To remove
1.3
’s assumption that the primes are odd, it would suffice to show that every connected Cayley graph of order
2pqr
is hamiltonian (where
p
,
q
, and
r
are distinct odd primes). An important first step in this direction was taken by F. Maghsoudi
[
8
]
, who handled the case where one of the primes is
3
.

Unfortunately, current methods do not seem to be sufficient to remove the assumption that
p
,
q
,
r
, and
s
are distinct. For example, it is not known that all Cayley graphs of order
9p^{2}
or
3p^{3}
are hamiltonian (cf.
[
7
]
).

2.
Preliminaries

We use the following fairly standard notation.

Notation 2.1
.

Let
A
be a subset of a finite group
G
.

(1)

e
is the identity element of
G
,

(2)

|g|
is the order of an element
g
of
G
.

(3)

G^{\prime}=[G,G]
is the
commutator subgroup
of
G
.

(4)

Z(G)=\{\,z\in G\mid\text{$zg=gz$ for all $g\in G$}\,\}
is the
centre
of
G
.

(5)

A sequence
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{m}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m})
of elements of
A\cup A^{-1}
represents the walk in
\Cay(G;A)
that visits the vertices

e,a_{1},a_{1}a_{2},\ldots,a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{m}.

Also, we use
a^{k}
and
a^{-k}
to represent the sequences
(a,a,\ldots,a)
and
(a^{-1},a^{-1},\ldots,a^{-1})
of length
k
.

Most cases of
1.3
are easy consequences of three known results that are collected in the following
Section
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
2
, p. 257]
, Durnberger
[
3
]
, Morris
[
9
]
)
.

Let
A
be a generating set of a nontrivial finite group
G
, and assume that
|G|
is odd. If either

(0)

|G^{\prime}|=1
,
or

(1)

|G^{\prime}|
is prime,
or

(2)

|G^{\prime}|
is the product of
2
distinct primes,

then
\Cay(G;A)
has a hamiltonian cycle.

Remark 2.3
.

The assumption that
G
has odd order is not necessary in parts (
0
) and (

) of

.
Steps toward removing this assumption from part (
2
) were taken in
[

]
and
[

]
.

For ease of reference, we record a few other known facts:

Lemma 2.4

(“Factor Group Lemma”
[
12
, §2.2]
)
.

Suppose

•

A
is a subset of the group
G
,

•

N
is a cyclic, normal subgroup of
G
,

•

C=(a_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
is a hamiltonian cycle in
\Cay(G/N;A)
,
and

•

\mathbb{V}(C)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}
generates

.

Then
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m})^{|N|}
is a hamiltonian cycle in
\Cay(G;A)
.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
7
, Lem. 2.27]
)
.

Let
A
generate a finite group
G
and let

, such that the subgroup generated by

is normal in

. If
\Cay\bigl(G/\langle b\rangle;A\bigr)
has a
hamiltonian cycle,
and
\langle b\rangle\cap Z(G)=\{e\}
, then
\Cay(G;A)
has a hamiltonian cycle.

Lemma 2.6

(cf.
[
6
, Thm. 9.4.3, p. 146]
and
[
8
, Lem. 2.16]
)
.

Assume
G
is a finite group of square-free order. Then

(1)

G^{\prime}
and
G/G^{\prime}
are cyclic,

(2)

G^{\prime}\cap Z(G)=\{e\}
,
and

(3)

if

, such that
\langle a,G^{\prime}\rangle=G
, then

(a)

does not commute with any nontrivial element of
G^{\prime}
,
and

(b)

|a|=|G/G^{\prime}|
.

Proposition 2.7

(Maghsoudi
[
8
, Prop. 3.1]
)
.

Assume
G
is a finite group, such that
|G|
is a product of four distinct primes. If

is an irredundant generating set of

, such that

, then
\Cay(G;A)
has a hamiltonian cycle.

3.
Proof of
1.3

Let
A
be an irredundant generating set of a finite group
G
, such that
|G|=pqrs
. We wish to show that
\Cay(G;A)
has a hamiltonian cycle.
Although some of the details are new, the line of argument here is quite standard. (See the proofs in
[
8
]
, for example.)

Let

\overline{G}=G/G^{\prime}.

Note that
G
is solvable by
2.6
(
1
), so
G^{\prime}\neq G
. Therefore
|G^{\prime}|
has at most
3
prime factors. We may assume it has exactly
3
, for otherwise
2.2
applies. Hence, we may assume
|G^{\prime}|=qrs
(so
|\overline{G}|=p
). Since
G^{\prime}
is cyclic (see
2.6
(
1
)), we have

G^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}_{q}\times\mathcal{C}_{r}\times\mathcal{C}_{s},

where
\mathcal{C}_{n}
denotes a (multiplicative) cyclic group of order
n
.
Then each element
\gamma
of
G^{\prime}
can be written uniquely in the form

\text{$\gamma=\gamma_{q}\,\gamma_{r}\,\gamma_{s}$,
\ where $\gamma_{q}\in\mathcal{C}_{q}$, $\gamma_{r}\in\mathcal{C}_{r}$, and $\gamma_{s}\in\mathcal{C}_{s}$}.

Let
a\in A
, such that
\langle\overline{a}\rangle=\overline{G}
. Then
|a|=p
by
2.6
(
3b
). Let
b
be another element of
A
, so we may write
b=a^{k}\gamma
, where
\gamma\in G^{\prime}
(and
0\leq k<p
).

Case 1 .
Assume
A\cap G^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.

Then we may assume
b\in G^{\prime}
. Since
G^{\prime}
is cyclic (see
2.6
(
1
)), and it is a standard exercise in undergraduate abstract algebra to show that every subgroup of a cyclic normal subgroup is normal, we know that
\langle b\rangle\triangleleft G
. Let
\widehat{G}=G/\langle b\rangle
. Since
|G|=pqrs
has only 4 prime factors, we know that
|\widehat{G}|
has at most 3 prime factors, so we see from part (
1
), (
2
), or (
3
) of
1.2
that
\Cay(\widehat{G};A)
has a hamiltonian cycle. Since

\langle b\rangle\cap Z(G)\subseteq G^{\prime}\cap Z(G)=\{e\}

(see
2.6
(
2
)),
we can now conclude from
2.5
that
\Cay(G;S)
has a hamiltonian cycle, as desired.

Case 2 .
Assume
|A|=2

(
and
A\cap G^{\prime}=\emptyset
)
.

This means that
A=\{a,b\}
, so
\langle a,b\rangle=G
. This implies
\langle\gamma\rangle=G^{\prime}
, so
\gamma_{q}
,
\gamma_{r}
, and
\gamma_{s}
are nontrivial. Also, we have
k\geq 1
, since
A\cap G^{\prime}=\emptyset
. Let

C=(\overline{b},\overline{a}^{-(k-1)},\overline{b},\overline{a}^{p-k-1}),

so
C
is a (well known) hamiltonian cycle in
\Cay(\overline{G};\overline{A})
. The voltage of
C
is

\mathbb{V}(C)=ba^{-(k-1)}ba^{p-k-1}=a^{k}\gamma\cdot a^{-(k-1)}\cdot a^{k}\gamma\cdot a^{p-k-1}=a^{k}\cdot\gamma_{q}\gamma_{r}\gamma_{s}\cdot a\cdot\gamma_{q}\gamma_{r}\gamma_{s}\cdot a^{-k-1}.

Since
|G|
is odd, we know that
a
does not invert any nontrivial element of
G^{\prime}
, so the two occurrences of
\gamma_{q}
in this product do not cancel each other, and similarly for the occurrences of
\gamma_{r}
and
\gamma_{s}
. Therefore, this voltage projects nontrivially to
\mathcal{C}_{q}
,
\mathcal{C}_{r}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{s}
, so it generates
G^{\prime}
. Hence,
2.4
provides a hamiltonian cycle in
\Cay(G;A)
.

Case 3 .
Assume
|A|\geq 3

(
and
A\cap G^{\prime}=\emptyset
)
.

Let
c
be a third element of
A
. We may assume
|A|<4
(for otherwise
2.7
applies), so
A=\{a,b,c\}
. Write
c=a^{\ell}\gamma^{\prime}
(with
\gamma^{\prime}\in G^{\prime}
).
Since
\langle a,b,c\rangle=G
, we must have
\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=G^{\prime}
, but the fact that
A
is irredundant implies
\langle\gamma\rangle\neq G^{\prime}
and
\langle\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\neq G^{\prime}
.
If
|\gamma|=q
, then we must have
|\gamma^{\prime}|=rs
. However, this implies
\langle b,c\rangle=G
, which contradicts the fact that the generating set
A
is irredundant.
So
|\gamma|
and
|\gamma^{\prime}|
must each have precisely two prime factors. Therefore, we may assume without loss of generality that
|\gamma|=qr
and
|\gamma^{\prime}|=rs
. Then

(A)

\displaystyle\text{$\gamma=\gamma_{q}\,\gamma_{r}$ and $\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma^{\prime}_{r}\,\gamma^{\prime}_{s}$},

and each of
\gamma_{q}
,
\gamma_{r}
,
\gamma^{\prime}_{r}
, and
\gamma^{\prime}_{s}
is nontrivial.

We may assume
k,\ell\leq(p-1)/2
(by replacing some generators with their inverses, if necessary). We may also assume, without loss of generality, that
\ell\leq k
.
(If
k\neq\ell
, this implies
\ell\leq(p-3)/2
.)
Also note that
k,\ell\geq 1
, since
A\cap G^{\prime}=\emptyset
.

Subcase 3.1 .

Assume
p=3
.

This implies
\overline{a}=\overline{b}=\overline{c}
, so we have the following two hamiltonian cycles in
\Cay(\overline{G};A)

\text{$C_{1}=(\overline{a},\overline{b},\overline{c})$ \ and \ $C_{2}=(\overline{a},\overline{c},\overline{b})$}.

Their voltages are

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(C_{1})

\displaystyle=abc=a\cdot a\gamma\cdot a\gamma^{\prime}=a^{2}\,\gamma_{q}\,\gamma_{r}\,a\,\gamma^{\prime}_{r}\,\gamma^{\prime}_{s}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(C_{2})

\displaystyle=acb=a\cdot a\gamma^{\prime}\cdot a\gamma=a^{2}\,\gamma^{\prime}_{r}\,\gamma^{\prime}_{s}\,a\,\gamma_{q}\,\gamma_{r}.

In each of these voltages, there is nothing that could cancel the factor
\gamma_{q}
or the factor
\gamma^{\prime}_{s}
. So both voltages project nontrivially to
\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\mathcal{C}_{s}
.

Therefore, we may assume that both voltages project trivially to
\mathcal{C}_{r}
, for otherwise we have a voltage that projects nontrivially to all three factors, and therefore generates
G^{\prime}
, so
2.4
applies. Hence

a^{2}\gamma_{r}a\gamma^{\prime}_{r}=\mathbb{V}(C_{1})_{r}=e=\mathbb{V}(C_{2})_{r}=a^{2}\gamma^{\prime}_{r}a\gamma_{r},

so, letting
x=\gamma^{\prime}_{r}\,\gamma_{r}^{-1}
, we have

, which means that
x
commutes with
a
. However, we also know from
2.6
(
3a
) that
a
does not commute with any nontrivial element of
G^{\prime}
. Therefore, we must have
x=e
, which means
\gamma^{\prime}_{r}=\gamma_{r}
. Also, since
a
does not invert any nontrivial element of
G^{\prime}
(since
a
has odd order), we know that
\gamma_{r}a\gamma_{r}\neq a
. Therefore

\mathbb{V}(C_{1})_{r}=a^{2}\cdot\gamma_{r}a\gamma^{\prime}_{r}=a^{2}\cdot\gamma_{r}a\gamma_{r}\neq a^{2}\cdot a=e.

This voltage therefore projects nontrivially to all three factors of
G^{\prime}
, so it generates
G^{\prime}
. Hence,
2.4
applies.

Subcase 3.2 .

Assume
\overline{a}
,
\overline{b}
, and
\overline{c}
are not all distinct.

We may assume
\overline{a}=\overline{c}
(which means

Therefore

\text{$C_{1}=(\overline{b},\overline{a}^{-(k-1)},\overline{b},\overline{c},\overline{a}^{p-k-2})$
\ and \ $C_{2}=(\overline{b},\overline{a}^{-(k-1)},\overline{b},\overline{a},\overline{c},\overline{a}^{p-k-3})$}.

Their voltages are

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(C_{1})

\displaystyle=ba^{-(k-1)}bca^{p-k-2}=a^{k}\gamma\cdot a^{-(k-1)}\cdot a^{k}\gamma\cdot a\gamma^{\prime}\cdot a^{p-k-2}=a^{k}\,\gamma_{q}\gamma_{r}\,a\,\gamma_{q}\gamma_{r}\,a\,\gamma^{\prime}_{r}\gamma^{\prime}_{s}\,a^{-k-2}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(C_{2})

\displaystyle=ba^{-(k-1)}baca^{p-k-3}=a^{k}\gamma\cdot a^{-(k-1)}\cdot a^{k}\gamma\cdot a\cdot a\gamma^{\prime}\cdot a^{p-k-3}=a^{k}\,\gamma_{q}\gamma_{r}\,a\,\gamma_{q}\gamma_{r}\,a^{2}\,\gamma^{\prime}_{r}\gamma^{\prime}_{s}\,a^{-k-3}.

Since

does not invert
\gamma_{q}
(because

\gamma_{q}
in these voltages cannot cancel each other. Hence, both voltages project nontrivially to
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. Also, there is nothing in either voltage that could cancel the single occurrence of
\gamma^{\prime}_{s}

\mathcal{C}_{s}
.

Therefore, in order to apply
2.4
, it suffices to show that at least one of these voltages projects nontrivially to
\mathcal{C}_{r}
. If not, then both projections are trivial, so

\displaystyle a^{k}\gamma_{r}a\gamma_{r}a\cdot\gamma^{\prime}_{r}a\cdot a^{-k-3}

\displaystyle=a^{k}\gamma_{r}a\gamma_{r}a\gamma^{\prime}_{r}a^{-k-2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}(C_{1})_{r}

\displaystyle=e

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}(C_{2})_{r}

\displaystyle=a^{k}\gamma_{r}a\gamma_{r}a^{2}\gamma^{\prime}_{r}a^{-k-3}

\displaystyle=a^{k}\gamma_{r}a\gamma_{r}a\cdot a\gamma^{\prime}_{r}\cdot a^{-k-3},

which implies

. This contradicts the fact that

does not centralize any nontrivial element of

(see
2.6
(
3a
)).

Assume
