Finding best approximation pairs for two intersections of closed convex sets
Heinz H. Bauschke, Shambhavi Singh, Xianfu Wang

Abstract
The problem of finding a best approximation pair of two sets, which in turn generalizes the well known convex feasibility problem, has a long history that dates back to work by Cheney and Goldstein in 1959. In 2018, Aharoni, Censor, and Jiang revisited this problem and proposed an algorithm that can be used when the two sets are finite intersections of halfspaces. Motivated by their work, we present alternative algorithms that utilize projection and proximity operators. Our modeling framework is able to accommodate even convex sets. Numerical experiments indicate that these methods are competitive and sometimes superior to the one proposed by Aharoni et al.

Introduction

Throughout this paper, we assume that

Y
is
a finite-dimensional real Hilbert space with inner product

\left\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\right\rangle\colon Y\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
,

and induced norm
\|\cdot\|
.
Let
m\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
, set

I:=\{1,\ldots,m\}
and suppose that

(\forall i\in I)\;\;\text{$A_{i}$ and $B_{i}$ are nonempty closed convex subsets of $Y$}

such that

A:=\bigcap_{i\in I}A_{i}\neq\varnothing\;\;\text{and}\;\;B:=\bigcap_{i\in I}B_{i}\neq\varnothing.

(We assume here without loss of generality that there are as many
sets
A_{i}
as
B_{j}
; otherwise, we can either “copy” sets or use
the full space
Y
itself.)
It will occasionally be convenient to work with the convention

A_{m+1}=A_{1}
,
A_{m+2}=A_{2}
, etc.; or, more formally,

A_{n}=A_{1+\mathrm{rem}(n-1,m)}
and

B_{n}=B_{1+\mathrm{rem}(n-1,m)}
.
We also assume that the projection operators
P_{A_{i}}

and
P_{B_{i}}
are “easy” to compute while the
projections
P_{A}
and
P_{B}
are “hard” and not readily available
(unless
m=1
).
The problem we are interested in is to
find a
best approximation pair
, i.e., to

\text{Find $(\bar{a},\bar{b})\in A\times B$
such that~}\|\bar{a}-\bar{b}\|=\inf_{(a,b)\in A\times B}\|a-b\|.

(1)

(Note that this problem is actually a generalization of the famous

convex feasibility problem

which asks to find a point in
A\cap B

provided that this intersection is nonempty which we do not assume here!)
This problem has a long history, and the first systematic study was
given by Cheney and Goldstein in 1959
[
12
]
; see also, e.g.,
[
3
]
,

[
4
]
, and
[
6
]
.
These works, however, assume that
the projection operators
P_{A}
and
P_{B}
are explicitly
available, which essentially means that
m=1
.
Recently, Aharoni, Censor, and Jiang (see
[
1
]
) tackled the general case.
Indeed, assuming that the sets
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
are
halfspaces
,
they presented a new algorithm
— which we call
ACJ
for simplicity —
for solving
Eq.

1
where they do not require knowledge of the projectors
P_{A}

and
P_{B}
onto the corresponding polyhedra
A
and
B
.

The purpose of this paper is to provide other approaches to solving

Eq.

1
. We also provide the required proximity operators
as well as numerical comparisons.
The algorithms considered will rely only on the operators
P_{A_{i}}
and
P_{B_{i}}

and some other operators that are available in closed form.

The algorithms presented will work for general closed convex sets, not just polyhedra as long as the projection operators onto the individual
sets making up the intersections are available.
Implementable formulae for the underlying algorithmic operators are provided.
Numerical experiments, similar to one in Aharoni et al.’s paper, are also performed.
Our results show that other algorithms should be seriously
considered for solving
Eq.

1
, especially if
m
is small.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
In
Section

2
, we consider reformulations of
Eq.

1

that are more amenable to the algorithms discussed in
Section

3
.
These algorithms rely on computable formulae which we
present in
Section

4
.
We present a small example on which these algorithms
are run and convergence is observed using different metrics in
Section

5
.
In
Section

5.1
and
Section

5.2
, we consider
examples where the solutions are known or unknown,
respectively.
We also discuss the (positive) effect of pairing up constraints
(see
Section

5.3
).
We conclude the paper in
Section

6
with a brief
summary of our findings.

The notation employed in this paper is fairly standard and follows largely
[
5
]
to
which we also refer the reader for general background material.
For the reader’s convenience, let us review some notions from convex analysis
that are of fundamental importance to this paper.
The
indicator function
of a set
C
is written as

\iota_{C}
; we have
\iota_{C}(x)=0
if
x\in C
and
\iota_{C}(x)=+\infty
otherwise. The corresponding
distance function

is
d_{C}(x)=\inf_{c\in C}\|x-c\|
.
If
C
is convex, closed, and nonempty, then for every
x
,
there exists a unique point
P_{C}(x)\in C
such that

d_{C}(x)=\|x-P_{C}(x)\|
. The corresponding operator
P_{C}
is called
the
projection operator
or simply
projector
of
C
.
For instance,
if
A
is the
halfspace

A=\big\{{x\in Y}~\big|~{\left\langle{a},{x}\right\rangle\leq\alpha}\big\}
,
where
a\neq 0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, then

P_{A}(x)=x-\frac{\max\{0,\left\langle{a},{x}\right\rangle-\alpha\}}{\|a\|^{2}}a.

(2)

More generally, if
f
is a function that is convex, lower semicontinuous,
and proper, then for every
x
, the function

y\mapsto f(y)+\tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
has a unique minimizer which is the
celebrated
proximal mapping
or
prox operator
of
f
,
written
\operatorname{Prox}_{f}
.
Note that if
f=\iota_{C}
, then we recover the projection operator
P_{C}
:

\operatorname{Prox}_{\iota_{C}}=P_{C}
.
A vector
y
is a
subgradient
of
f
at
x
if
for every
h
, we have
f(x)+\left\langle{y},{h}\right\rangle\leq f(x+h)
;
the set of all subgradients at
x
is the
subdifferential

of
f
at
x
, written
\partial f(x)
.

2
Modeling
Eq.

1

In the product Hilbert space

X:=Y\times Y,

we define
nonempty closed convex sets by

(\forall i\in I)\;\;C_{i}:=A_{i}\times B_{i},

along with their intersection

(\forall i\in I)\;\;C:=\bigcap_{i\in I}C_{i}=A\times B.

Note that the projector onto
C_{i}
is still easy to compute; indeed,

P_{C_{i}}\colon(x,y)\mapsto(P_{A_{i}}x,P_{B_{i}}y)
.
The problem
Eq.

1
is thus equivalent to

minimize
h(x,y)
subject to
(x,y)\in C
,

(3)

where

h(x,y)=\alpha\|x-y\|^{p},

(4)

\alpha>0
, and
p\geq 1
.
Note that
h
has full domain and it is convex —
but
h
is
not
strictly convex because it has
many minimizers:
\big\{{(x,x)}~\big|~{x\in Y}\big\}
.
Also note that if
p>1
, then
h
is differentiable.

The problem
Eq.

1
can thus also be alternatively thought of as

minimize
h(x,y)+\sum_{i\in I}\iota_{C_{i}}(x,y)
,

(5)

which features a nonsmooth objective function.
In the next section, we survey various algorithms that could be used to solve
the problem
Eq.

1
or its reformulations.
We also consider the case when
Eq.

5
is approximated by

minimize
h(x,y)+\sum_{i\in I}Ld_{C_{i}}(x,y)
,

(6)

for some “large” constant
L
.

3
Algorithms for solving
Eq.

1

In this section, we discuss various algorithms.
The algorithms in
Section

3.1
and
Section

3.2
are able to solve the
original problem exactly while those in the remaining subsections can
be viewed as attempting to solve a perturbed problem.

3.1
The Aharoni–Censor–Jiang Algorithm

This algorithm was recently proposed by
Aharoni, Censor, and Jiang in
[
1
]
.
We denote their algorithm as
ACJ
.

ACJ

builds on the earlier
HLWB
algorithm.
(The letters in
HLWB
signify relevant
works
by Halpern
[
15
]
,
by Lions
[
20
]
,
by Wittmann
[
23
]
,
and by Bauschke
[
2
]
;
the name
HLWB
was coined by
Censor in
[
11
]
.)

ACJ
can be viewed
as

an alternating version of
HLWB
to find a solution of
Eq.

1
.
Here is the description of
ACJ
.
First, we fix a sequence
(\lambda_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of positive real numbers such that

\lambda_{k}\to 0,\quad\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\lambda_{k}=\infty,\quad\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{k+m}|<\infty

(7)

and also an increasing (not necessarily strictly though)
sequence of natural numbers
(n_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}

such that

n_{k}\to\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{k>k_{0}}\prod_{n>n_{k_{0}}}^{n_{k}}(1-\lambda_{n})<\infty.

(8)

For instance,
Eq.

7
–
Eq.

8
hold
when
(\forall{k\in{\mathbb{N}}})

\lambda_{k}=\frac{1}{k+1}

and
n_{k}=\lfloor 1.1^{k}\rfloor
(see
[
1
, page 512]
).
Next, given our sequence of sets
(A_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

and
{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,
we define the operator

Q_{A,n}\colon Y\times Y\to Y\colon(w,w^{\prime})\mapsto w_{n},

(9)

where
w_{0}=w^{\prime}
and
w_{n}

is computed iteratively via

(\forall i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\})\quad w_{i+1}=\lambda_{i+1}w+(1-\lambda_{i+1})P_{A_{i+1}}(w_{i}).

(10)

The operator
Q_{B,n}
, for
(B_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,
is defined analogously.
Finally, we initialize
(x_{0},y_{0})\in X\times X
,
and iteratively update via

\displaystyle(\forall{k\in{\mathbb{N}}})\quad(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\big(Q_{B,n_{k}}(y_{k},y_{k}^{\prime}),y_{k}\big),&\text{if $k$ is odd;}\\
\big(x_{k},Q_{A,n_{k}}(x_{k},x_{k}^{\prime})\big),&\text{if $k$ is even,}\end{cases}

(11)

and where

(x^{\prime}_{k},y^{\prime}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
X\times X
is a bounded sequence that
can either be fixed beforehand, e.g.,
(x^{\prime}_{k},y^{\prime}_{k})=(y_{0},x_{0})
, or dynamically updated using, e.g.,

(x^{\prime}_{0},y^{\prime}_{0})=(y_{0},x_{0})
and
(x^{\prime}_{k},y^{\prime}_{k})=(y_{k-1},x_{k-1})
for
k\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
.

The main result of
[
1
]
yields the convergence of

(x_{k},y_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
to a solution of
Eq.

1
provided that

C\neq\emptyset
and each
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
is
a
halfspace
.
We refer the reader to
Eq.

2
for the formula
for the projection onto a halfspace which is required by
ACJ
.

Remark 3.1
.

Note that
ACJ

takes into account the order of the sets while
the problem
Eq.

1
does not.
It is a nice feature of
ACJ
that it works throughout in the
“small” space
X\times X
.
On the other hand, we are not aware of any extension of

ACJ
to the case when the sets underlying the intersections
are not halfspaces. This is an interesting topic for further research.

3.2
Douglas–Rachford Algorithm

This algorithm, abbreviated as
DR
,
can be traced back to the paper by Douglas and Rachford

[
14
]
although its relevance to optimization was brought to light
later in the seminal paper by Lions and Mercier
[
21
]
.

DR
can deal with problems of the form
Eq.

5
, and
it implicitly operates in the space
X^{m+1}
.
First, set

f_{0}(x,y):=\alpha\|x-y\|^{p}
with
\alpha>0
and
p\geq 1
,
as well as

f_{1}:=\iota_{C_{1}},\ldots,f_{m}:=\iota_{C_{m}}
and
I_{0}:=\{0\}\cup I
.
Second, fix a parameter
0<\lambda<2
(the default being
\lambda=1
).

Now initialize
\mathbf{z}_{0}:=(z_{0,0},z_{0,1},\ldots,z_{0,m})=\big((x_{0,0},y_{0,0}),(x_{0,1},y_{0,1}),\ldots,(x_{0,m},y_{0,m})\big)\in X^{m+1}
.
Given
\mathbf{z}_{k}=(z_{k,0},z_{k,1},\ldots,z_{k,m})\in X^{m+1}
,
set

\displaystyle\bar{z}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{i\in I_{0}}z_{k,i}

(12a)

\displaystyle(\forall i\in I_{0})\;\;x_{k,i}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{Prox}_{f_{i}}(2\bar{z}_{k}-z_{k,i})

(12b)

\displaystyle(\forall i\in I_{0})\;\;z_{k+1,i}

\displaystyle:=z_{k,i}+\lambda(x_{k,i}-\bar{z}_{k})

(12c)

to obtain the update
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}:=(z_{k+1,0},z_{k+1,1},\ldots,z_{k+1,m})
.

If
i\in I
, then the prox operators corresponding to
f_{i}
is simply
the projector
P_{C_{i}}
.

In particular, if each
C_{i}=A_{i}\times B_{i}
is the Cartesian product
of two halfspaces, then we may utilize
Eq.

2
twice to compute

P_{C_{i}}=\operatorname{Prox}_{f_{i}}
.

The prox operator
\operatorname{Prox}_{f_{0}}
will be computed in closed form for
p\in\{1,2\}

in
Section

4.1
below.

When
p=1
, which produced better numerical results, then

f_{0}(x,y)=\alpha\|x-y\|
and

\displaystyle\operatorname{Prox}_{f_{0}}(x,y)=(x,y)-\frac{1}{\max\big\{2,\|x-y\|/\alpha\big\}}\big(x-y,y-x\big).

(13)

It is well known
(see, e.g.,
[
5
, Proposition 28.7]
) that the sequence
(\bar{z}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
will converge to a solution of
Eq.

5
, i.e., of
Eq.

1
.

Remark 3.2
.

The
DR
approach does not care about the order of the sets presented —
unlike,
ACJ
!
A downside is that it operates in the larger space
X^{m+1}

which can become an issue if
m
is large.
On the positive side, if
C_{1}\cap\cdots\cap C_{m}=\varnothing
,
then
(\bar{z}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
will converge to a minimizer of
f_{0}
over the set of least-squares
solutions (see
[
7
, Corollary 6.8]
for further information).
Finally, it does not require the constraint sets
C_{i}
to be
Cartesian product of halfspaces.

3.3
Dual-Based Proximal Method

We largely follow Beck’s
[
8
, Section 12.4.2]

(see also
[
9
]
and
[
13
]
for further background
material)
but slightly modify the algorithms
presented there to give two additional methods for solving
Eq.

1
.
We will work with the form given in
Eq.

5

where
h(x,y)
needs to be
\varepsilon
-strongly convex, for some
\varepsilon>0
,
which precludes using
\alpha\|x-y\|^{p}
directly.
However, below we will add

\varepsilon\frac{1}{2}(\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})
to
this last function to obtain the required
\varepsilon
-strong convexity.

We point out that by adding this energy term and solving the
corresponding new perturbed optimization problem, the solution obtained
does not solve the original problem exactly.

The first method considered is the Dual Proximal Gradient method,
which — following
[
8
]
— we abbreviated as
DPG
.
Because the algorithm requires strong convexity of the objective
function, we consider

f_{0}(x,y):=\alpha\tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}+\varepsilon\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}\big)\;\;\text{with $\alpha>0$
and $\varepsilon>0$}.

(14)

We also set
f_{1}:=\iota_{C_{1}},\ldots,f_{m}:=\iota_{C_{m}}
.
Second, fix a parameter
L\geq m/\varepsilon
.

Now initialize
\mathbf{z}_{0}:=(z_{0,1},\dots,z_{0,m})=\big((x_{0,1},y_{0,1}),\ldots,(x_{0,m},y_{0,m})\big)\in X^{m}
, and update it using

\displaystyle s_{k}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i\in I}z_{k,i}

(15a)

\displaystyle x_{k}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname*{argmax}_{w\in X}\big[\left\langle{w},{s_{k}}\right\rangle-f_{0}(w)\,\big]

(15b)

\displaystyle(\forall i\in I)\;\;z_{k+1,i}

\displaystyle:=z_{k,i}-\frac{1}{L}x_{k}+\frac{1}{L}P_{f_{i}}(x_{k}-Lz_{k,i})

(15c)

to obtain
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}:=(z_{k+1,1},\dots,z_{k+1,m})
.
This is the primal representation of DPG,
see
[
8
, page 356]
, which is most convenient in our setting.
Once again, the prox operators corresponding to
f_{i}

for
i\in I
are just the projectors
P_{C_{i}}
.

If the sets
C_{i}
are Cartesian products of halfspaces, we may use

Eq.

2
to compute
P_{C_{i}}
.
The closed form for the argmax operator in
Eq.

15b

is given by

\displaystyle x_{k}=\frac{1}{(2\alpha+\varepsilon)\varepsilon}\big((\alpha+\varepsilon)u_{k}+\alpha v_{k},(\alpha+\varepsilon)v_{k}+\alpha u_{k}\big),\quad\text{where $s_{k}=(u_{k},v_{k})$.}

(16)

This formula will be proved in
Section

4.2
below.
For sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
,
the primal sequence
(x_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
approximates a solution of

Eq.

5
and hence of
Eq.

1

provided that the relative interiors of the sets
C_{i}
form
a nonempty intersection (see
[
8
, page 362]
).
Note that we do not expect that the primal sequence converges to an
exact solution of
Eq.

1
because the objective function

f_{0}
in
Eq.

14
is not identical to the one required to tackle

Eq.

1
.

An accelerated version of
DPG
,
known as Fast Dual Proximal Gradient or simply
FDPG
,
applies a FISTA-type acceleration
(see
[
8
, Section 12.3]
and
[
9
]
.)
Here is how
FDPG
proceeds:
Starting with
\mathbf{z}_{0}
as before,
initialize
\textbf{w}_{0}:=\mathbf{z}_{0}
,
t_{0}:=1
,
and update via

\displaystyle s_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i\in I}w_{k,i}

(17a)

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname*{argmax}_{v\in X}\big[\left\langle{v},{s_{k}^{\prime}}\right\rangle-f_{0}(v)\,\big]

(17b)

\displaystyle(\forall i\in I)\;\;z_{k+1,i}

\displaystyle:=w_{k,i}-\frac{1}{L}u_{k}+\frac{1}{L}P_{f_{i}}(u_{k}-Lw_{k,i})

(17c)

\displaystyle t_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4t_{k}^{2}}}{2}

(17d)

\displaystyle(\forall i\in I)\;\;w_{k+1,i}

\displaystyle:=z_{k,i+1}+\left(\frac{t_{k}-1}{t_{k+1}}\right)(z_{k,i+1}-z_{k,i})

(17e)

to get the primal sequence of interest

x_{k+1}:=\operatorname*{argmax}_{v\in X}\big[\left\langle{v},{s_{k+1}}\right\rangle-f_{0}(v)\,\big],\quad\text{where}\quad s_{k+1}:=\sum_{i\in I}z_{k+1,i}.

(18)

Again, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
,
the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
approximates a solution “close”
to that of
Eq.

5

— but not exactly —
and (as a consequence of
Eq.

1
)
provided that the relative interior of
C
is not empty.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that although a smaller
\varepsilon
ensures a solution
that is closer to that of the original problem
Eq.

1
,
it also increases the lower bound for
L
,
which in turn reduces the step size for each iteration
as seen in
Eq.

15c
and
Eq.

17c
,
and so the speed of convergence reduces as well.
These algorithms are not affected by the order of the sets presented,
but like
DR
, they operate in a “large”
space (here
X^{m}
). This may become a problem when
m
is large.

3.4
Proximal Distance Algorithm

The Proximal Distance Algorithm, or
PDA
for short,
was first introduced by Lange and Keys
[
19
]
.

It is motivated by the framework of MM algorithms,
where MM stands for majorize/minimize or for minorize/maximize
depending on the underlying problem.
This framework was pioneered by Lange; see, e.g.,
his book
[
18
]
on this topic.
It can be interpreted as a prox-gradient method
applied to the function
\tfrac{1}{\rho}h+\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in I}\tfrac{1}{2}d^{2}_{C_{i}}
, where the penalty parameter is in theory driven to
+\infty

(see
[
18
, Section 5.5]
for a gentle introduction).
The parameter
\rho
has to be carefully driven to infinity.
We will apply
PDA
to the problem formulation given by
Eq.

5
.

Set
h(x,y):=\alpha\|x-y\|
,
which is
\sqrt{2}\alpha
-Lipschitz
by
Proposition

4.2
, for
\alpha>0
.
Also, write
z=(x,y)
.
The
PDA
with starting point
z_{0}\in X

generates a sequence
(z_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
via

z_{k+1}:=\operatorname{Prox}_{\rho_{k}^{-1}h}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{m}P_{C_{i}}z_{k}\Big),

(19)

where

\displaystyle\operatorname{Prox}_{\rho_{k}^{-1}h}(x,y)=(x,y)-\frac{1}{\max\big\{2,\rho_{k}\|x-y\|/\alpha\big\}}\big(x-y,y-x\big)

(20)

by
Eq.

36
and where

(\rho_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of positive (and “sufficiently large”) parameters.

If the sets
C_{i}
are Cartesian products of halfspaces, we may use

Eq.

2
to compute
P_{C_{i}}
.
Under suitable choices of the parameter sequences, the

sequence

(z_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
approximates
a solution of
Eq.

5
.
Lange and Keys recommend
\rho_{k}=\min\{(1.2)^{k}\rho_{0},\rho_{\text{max}}\}

but other choices may yield better performance (see
[
19
, Sections 4 and 5]
and

[
16
]
for details).
Keys, Zhou, and Lange also point out
a Nesterov-style accelerated version of PDA (
accPDA
for short),
which proceeds as follows:

\displaystyle w_{k}

\displaystyle:=z_{k}+\frac{k-1}{k+2}(z_{k}-z_{k-1}),

(21a)

\displaystyle z_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{Prox}_{\rho_{k}^{-1}h}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{m}P_{C_{i}}w_{k}\Big).

(21b)

See
[
16
, Algorithm 1 and Section 3]
for further information.
Note that because
\rho_{k}\leq\rho_{\text{max}}<+\infty
,
both
PDA
and
accPDA
find a solution of the penalized but not
of the original problem.

3.5
Stochastic Subgradient Descent

The roots of stochastic gradient descent can be traced back
to two key papers from the early 1950s co-authored by
Robbins and Monro
[
22
]
and
by Kiefer and Wolfowitz
[
17
]
;
see also
[
10
]
for a recent survey.
The method has since been generalized to many different settings.
We follow largely the presentation in
[
8
]
.

Set
f_{0}(x,y):=\alpha\|x-y\|
and

(\forall i\in I)

f_{i}:=Ld_{C_{i}}
, where
L>0
.
Then
f_{0}
is
\sqrt{2}\alpha
-Lipschitz

and (by
Eq.

35
below)

\displaystyle f_{0}^{\prime}(z)=f_{0}^{\prime}(x,y)=\alpha\big(\operatorname{sign}(x-y),-\operatorname{sign}(x-y)\big),

(22)

where “
\operatorname{sign}
” is defined in
Eq.

25
.

The other functions
f_{i}
are
L
-Lipschitz.
Moreover, for
i\in I
, we have

f_{i}^{\prime}(z)=L\operatorname{sign}(z-P_{C_{i}}z)\in\partial f_{i}(z)

(23)

by, e.g.,
[
5
, Example 16.62]
.

If the sets
C_{i}
are Cartesian products of halfspaces, we may use

Eq.

2
to compute
P_{C_{i}}
.
Now Stochastic Subgradient Descent, which we abbreviate as

SSD
(see
[
8
, Section 8.3]
for further
information), applied to
Eq.

6
generates a sequence via

z_{k+1}:=z_{k}-\eta_{k}f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(z_{k}),

(24)

where
(\eta_{k})_{{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}}
is a sequence of positive parameters
(typically constant or a constant divided by
\sqrt{k+1}
)
and where
f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(z_{k})\in\partial f_{i_{k}}(z_{k})

where
i_{k}
is uniformly sampled from
I_{0}:=\{0\}\cup I
.

Under appropriate conditions, the sequence generated by
SSD
approximates
a minimizer of the function
\alpha\|x-y\|+L\sum_{i\in I}d_{C_{i}}
.
Note that for large
L
, the distance functions converge pointwise
to the corresponding indicator functions, but they are different for fixed
L
.
In this sense,
SSD
finds a perturbed but not exact solution of the original problem.

4
Useful operators

In this section, we collect formulae for operators
that are used later in our numerical experiments.

4.1
Prox and (sub)differential operators

Denote the standard unit ball by
\mathbb{B}
:

\mathbb{B}:=\big\{{y\in Y}~\big|~{\|y\|\leq 1}\big\}
.
It will be convenient to define the generalized signum functions on
Y
via

\operatorname{sign}(x):=\begin{cases}x/\|x\|,&\text{if $x\neq 0$;}\\
0,&\text{if $x=0$}\end{cases}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\operatorname{Sign}(x):=\begin{cases}\{x/\|x\|\},&\text{if $x\neq 0$;}\\
\mathbb{B},&\text{if $x=0$.}\end{cases}

(25)

By
[
5
, Example 16.32]
, we have

(\forall x\in Y)\;\;\operatorname{sign}(x)\in\operatorname{Sign}(x)=\partial\|\cdot\|(x).

(26)

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
.
Then the prox operator of the function

h\colon Y\times Y\to\mathbb{R}\colon(x,y)\mapsto\alpha\tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}.

(27)

is given by

\operatorname{Prox}_{h}\colon(x,y)\mapsto\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}\big((1+\alpha)x+\alpha y,\alpha x+(1+\alpha)y\big).

(28)

Moreover,

\nabla h\colon(x,y)\mapsto\big(\alpha(x-y),\alpha(y-x)\big)

is
(1+2\alpha)
-Lipschitz continuous.

Proof
. Set
z=(x,y)\in Y\times Y
and

B\colon Y\times Y\to Y\colon(x,y)\mapsto\sqrt{\alpha}(x-y)
.
Then

h(z)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|Bz\|^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle{Bz},{Bz}\right\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle{B^{*}Bz},{z}\right\rangle

(29)

and thus

\nabla h=B^{*}B
.
It follows that

\operatorname{Prox}_{h}=(\operatorname{Id}+B^{*}B)^{-1}.

(30)

Write
B
in block matrix form,

B=\sqrt{\alpha}\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Id},-\operatorname{Id}\end{bmatrix}
.
Then
B^{*}=\sqrt{\alpha}\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Id},-\operatorname{Id}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}
,

\nabla h=B^{*}B=\alpha\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Id}&-\operatorname{Id}\\
-\operatorname{Id}&\operatorname{Id}\end{bmatrix},

(31)

and

\operatorname{Id}+\nabla h=\operatorname{Id}+B^{*}B=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\alpha)\operatorname{Id}&-\alpha\operatorname{Id}\\
-\alpha\operatorname{Id}&(1+\alpha)\operatorname{Id}\end{bmatrix}.

(32)

The largest eigenvalue of the very last matrix is
1+2\alpha

which implies that
1+2\alpha
is the sharp Lipschitz constant of
\nabla h
.
Finally,

\operatorname{Prox}_{h}=(\operatorname{Id}+\nabla h)^{-1}=\big(\operatorname{Id}+B^{*}B\big)^{-1}=\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}\begin{bmatrix}(1+\alpha)\operatorname{Id}&\alpha\operatorname{Id}\\
\alpha\operatorname{Id}&(1+\alpha)\operatorname{Id}\end{bmatrix}

(33)

and the result follows.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\alpha>0
.
The function

h\colon Y\times Y\to\mathbb{R}\colon(x,y)\mapsto\alpha\|x-y\|,

(34)

is
\alpha\sqrt{2}
-Lipschitz
and a convenient selection of
\partial h
is given by

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto\alpha\big(\operatorname{sign}(x-y),-\operatorname{sign}(x-y)\big)

(35a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\big(\alpha(x-y)/\|x-y\|,\alpha(y-x)/\|y-x\|\big),&\text{if $x\neq y$};\\
\big(0,0\big),&\text{if $x=y$}\end{cases}

(35b)

\displaystyle\in\big\{{\alpha(s,-s)}~\big|~{s\in\operatorname{Sign}(x-y)}\big\}

(35c)

\displaystyle=\partial h(x,y).

(35d)

The prox operator of
h
is given by

\operatorname{Prox}_{h}\colon(x,y)\mapsto(x,y)-\frac{1}{\max\big\{2,\|x-y\|/\alpha\big\}}\big(x-y,y-x\big).

(36)

Proof
. Set

A=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Id},-\operatorname{Id}\end{bmatrix}
,

z=(x,y)
,
and
f(z)=\|Az\|
.
Then
Az=x-y
and
h=\alpha f
.
Furthermore,

\|Az\|^{2}=\|x-y\|^{2}=\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}-2\left\langle{x},{y}\right\rangle\leq 2\|x\|^{2}+2\|y\|^{2}=2(\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})=2\|z\|^{2}

which shows that
f
is
\sqrt{2}
-Lipschitz and therefore

h
is
\alpha\sqrt{2}
-Lipschitz.
The subdifferential formula follows using

[
5
, Proposition 16.6(i) and Example 16.32]
.
Note that, for
\beta\geq 0
,

A^{\intercal}=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Id}\\
-\operatorname{Id}\end{bmatrix},\;\;AA^{\intercal}=2\operatorname{Id},\;\;(AA^{\intercal}+\beta\operatorname{Id})^{-1}=\frac{1}{2+\beta}\operatorname{Id},

(37)

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{Id}-A^{\intercal}(AA^{\intercal}+\beta\operatorname{Id})^{-1}A

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Id}&0\\
0&\operatorname{Id}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Id}\\
-\operatorname{Id}\end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2+\beta}\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Id},-\operatorname{Id}\end{bmatrix}

(38a)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Id}&0\\
0&\operatorname{Id}\end{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{2+\beta}\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Id}&-\operatorname{Id}\\
-\operatorname{Id}&\operatorname{Id}\end{bmatrix}

(38b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2+\beta}\begin{bmatrix}1+\beta&1\\
1&1+\beta\end{bmatrix}.

(38c)

We now discuss cases.

Case 1:

\|x-y\|\leq 2\alpha
.

In view of
Eq.

37
(with
\beta=0
),
this is equivalent to

\|(AA^{\intercal})^{-1}Az\|\leq\alpha
.
Using
[
8
, Lemma 6.68]
and
Eq.

38
(with
\beta=0
), we obtain
(switching back to row vector notation for convenience)

\operatorname{Prox}_{h}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(x+y,x+y)=(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}(x-y,y-x).

(39)

Case 2:

\|x-y\|>2\alpha
.

In view of
Eq.

37
(with
\beta=0
),
this is equivalent to

\|(AA^{\intercal})^{-1}Az\|>\alpha
.
Using again
Eq.

37
(with general
\beta\geq 0
),
we set and obtain

\displaystyle g(\beta)

\displaystyle=\|(AA^{\intercal}+\beta\operatorname{Id})^{-1}Az\|^{2}-\alpha^{2}

(40a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2+\beta)^{2}}\|x-y\|^{2}-\alpha^{2}.

(40b)

Because
\alpha>0
and
\beta\geq 0
,
we obtain the equivalences:

g(\beta)=0

\Leftrightarrow

\|x-y\|=\alpha(2+\beta)

\Leftrightarrow

\beta=\|x-y\|/\alpha-2
.
Set

\beta^{*}:=\frac{\|x-y\|}{\alpha}-2>0

(41)

so that
g(\beta^{*})=0
.
Moreover,

2+\beta^{*}=\|x-y\|/{\alpha}
and

1+\beta^{*}=(\|x-y\|-\alpha)/\alpha
.
Hence
Eq.

38
(with
\beta=\beta^{*}
) turns into

\displaystyle\operatorname{Id}-A^{\intercal}(AA^{\intercal}+\beta^{*}\operatorname{Id})^{-1}A

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2+\beta^{*}}\begin{bmatrix}1+\beta^{*}&1\\
1&1+\beta^{*}\end{bmatrix}

(42a)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\|x-y\|}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\|x-y\|-\alpha}{\alpha}&1\\
1&\frac{\|x-y\|-\alpha}{\alpha}\end{bmatrix}

(42b)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}-\frac{\alpha}{\|x-y\|}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{bmatrix}.

(42c)

Using
[
8
, Lemma 6.68]
and
Eq.

42
, we obtain
(switching back to row vector notation for convenience)

\operatorname{Prox}_{h}(x,y)=(x,y)-\frac{\alpha}{\|x-y\|}(x-y,y-x).

(43)

Finally, the formula given in
Eq.

36

follows by combining
Eq.

39
and
Eq.

43
.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

4.2
Argmax operator

Consider, for
\alpha>0
and
\varepsilon>0
,

f_{0}(x,y):=\alpha\tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}+\varepsilon\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}),

(44)

which is a perturbation of
\alpha\tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}

that is
\varepsilon
-strongly convex.

Given
(u,v)\in X
,
the dual-based proximal methods of

Section

3.3
require from us to
find the unique maximizer of

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left\langle{(u,v)},{(x,y)}\right\rangle-f_{0}(x,y)

(45a)

\displaystyle=\left\langle{u},{x}\right\rangle+\left\langle{v},{y}\right\rangle-\alpha\tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}-\varepsilon\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}).

(45b)

We now derive an explicit formula for this maximizer:

Proposition 4.3
.

Given
\alpha>0
,
\varepsilon>0
,
and
(u,v)\in X
,
the unique maximizer of
Eq.

45
is

(x,y)=\frac{1}{(2\alpha+\varepsilon)\varepsilon}\big((\alpha+\varepsilon)u+\alpha v,(\alpha+\varepsilon)v+\alpha u\big).

(46)

Proof
. Because
f_{0}
is strongly convex,
we employ standard convex calculus
to find the maximizer by
finding the zero of the gradient of the function in

Eq.

45
. That is, we need to solve

\displaystyle(0,0)

\displaystyle=\big(u-\alpha x+\alpha y-\varepsilon x,v-\alpha y+\alpha x-\varepsilon y\big);

(47)

or equivalently (switching to more formal column vector notation),

\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+\varepsilon&-\alpha\\
-\alpha&\alpha+\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix}.

(48)

Because

\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+\varepsilon&-\alpha\\
-\alpha&\alpha+\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\frac{1}{(2\alpha+\varepsilon)\varepsilon}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+\varepsilon&\alpha\\
\alpha&\alpha+\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}

(49)

we obtain the announced formula.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

5
Numerical experiments

We start by discussing metrics — in the sense of “standard of measurement”
not in the sense of topology —
to evaluate the quality of the the iterates
for the different algorithms proposed in
Section

3
.
To make the measurement uniform over the different algorithms,
we use this metric once for each prox or proj evaluation.
For the same purpose,
we consider
A_{i}
or
B_{i}
as unit inputs in these operators.
So, for example, as
DR
projects to all
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
along with a prox evaluation, we get a total of
2m+1
operations.
Since it is not possible to obtain the final output of a given iteration before computing all the proxes,
we repeat the final output given by the iteration
2m+1
times.
Again for the sake of uniformity, we only repeat the final output of each iteration,
regardless of whether intermediate updates can be calculated.
We shall consider two cases: in the first, true solutions are known
(and assumed to be unique)
while in the second, they aren’t.
The former case allows us to inspect the progress of the iterates towards the solution,
while in the latter case a metric is needed to gauge the performance of the algorithms.
The latter scenario is the one most realistic for applications.

5.2
What to do in the absence of known solutions

Figure 4:
Using

In general, one has no access to true solutions,
so it becomes necessary to measure performance by a metric
different from
Eq.

50
.
We propose the measure

D_{\delta}(x,y):=\|x-y\|+\frac{1}{\delta}\sum_{i\in I}d_{C_{i}}(x,y),

(54)

where
\delta>0
.
Because the problem asks to find a point in
A\times B
,
feasibility is of greater importance than
minimizing
\|x-y\|
;
thus, a smaller value of
\delta
is
desirable to stress feasibility.
(One could also envision a “dynamic” metric,
where
\delta\to 0
as the number of iterations increases,
but we have not tested this.)
Revisiting the problem considered in
Example

5.1
,
we show in
Fig.

4
the convergence plot
using the parameter
\delta=1/10
.
This time, the horizontal axis was taken with a log scaling to
increase readability of the resulting graph.
The behaviour of the algorithms in the plot on the right,
which includes the feasibility conditions along with the distance between
x
and
y
, resembles the one seen in
Fig.

2
.

6
Conclusion

We revisited the recent study by Aharoni, Censor, and Jiang
on finding best approximation pairs of two polyhedra.
The framework we proposed works for two sets that are themselves
finite intersections of closed convex sets with “simple” projections.
Several algorithms were proposed and the required
prox operators were computed. Our numerical experiments suggested
that other algorithms deserve serious consideration.
