Strong negation in the Theory of Computable Functionals TCF
Nils Köpp ORCID:0000-0002-8280-4744, Iosif Petrakis ORCID:0000-0002-4121-7455

Abstract
. We incorporate strong negation in the theory of computable functionals TCF \mathrm{TCF} , a common extension of Plotkin’s PCF and Gödel’s system T , by defining simultaneously strong negation A N A^{\textnormal{{N}}} of a formula A A and strong negation P N P^{\textnormal{{N}}} of a predicate P P in TCF \mathrm{TCF} . As a special case of the latter, we get strong negation of an inductive and a coinductive predicate of TCF \mathrm{TCF} . We prove appropriate versions of the Ex falso quodlibet and of double negation elimination for strong negation in TCF \mathrm{TCF} . We introduce the so-called tight formulas of TCF i.e., formulas implied by the weak negation of their strong negation, and the relative tight formulas. We present various case-studies and examples, which reveal the naturality of our definition of strong negation in TCF \mathrm{TCF} and justify the use of TCF as a formal system for a large part of Bishop-style constructive mathematics.

2.
The Theory of Computable Functionals TCF

In this section we describe the theory of computable functionals
\mathrm{TCF}
.
For all notions and results on
\mathrm{TCF}
that are used here without further explanation or proof, we refer
to
[
SW12
]
.

The term-system of TCF,
\mathbf{T}^{+}
, is an extension of Gödels
T
. Namely, the simple types are enriched with free algebras
\iota
, generated by a list of constructors, as extra base types. Particularly, we have the usual strictly positive data-types, for example

\mathbb{N}\coloneqq\langle\mathbf{0}:\mathbb{N},\mathbf{S}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrowtriangle\mathbb{N}\rangle,\qquad\alpha\times\beta\coloneqq\langle[\_\,,\,\_]:\alpha\rightarrowtriangle\beta\rightarrowtriangle\alpha\times\beta\ \rangle.

Moreover,
nesting into already defined algebras with parameters is allowed e.g., given lists
\mathbb{L}(\alpha)
and boole
\mathbb{B}
, the finitely branching boolean-labelled and number-leafed trees is defined via the algebra
\mathbb{T}
with constructors

\mathbf{Leaf}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrowtriangle\mathbb{T},\quad\mathbf{Branch}:\mathbb{L}[\alpha/\mathbb{T}]\times\mathbb{B}\rightarrowtriangle\mathbb{T}.

The term-system
\mathbf{T}^{+}
is
simple-typed lambda-calculus
. The
constants
are
constructors
or
program constants

\mathtt{D}:\vec{\rho}\rightarrowtriangle\tau
, defined by a system of consistent left-to-right computation rules. Particularly, we have
recursion
and
corecursion operators

\mathcal{R}_{\iota}
respectively,
{{}^{co}{\mathcal{R}}}_{\iota}
, and the destructor
\mathcal{D}_{\iota}
for any free algebra
\iota
. In that sense, it is regarded as an extension of
Gödels

T
. In general terms are partial and if needed, computational properties e.g., totality have to be asserted by proof.

\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\xi

\displaystyle t,s\in\mathbf{T}^{+}

\displaystyle\Coloneqq\ x^{\rho},y^{\rho}\ \big|\ \left(\lambda_{x^{\rho}}t^{\sigma}\right)^{\rho\rightarrowtriangle\sigma}\ \big|\ \left(t^{\rho\rightarrowtriangle\sigma}s^{\rho}\right)^{\sigma}\ \big|\ \mathtt{C}^{\vec{\rho_{i}}\to\iota}_{i}\ \big|\ \mathtt{D}^{\vec{\rho}\rightarrowtriangle\tau}.

The logic of TCF is minimal first-order predicate-logic extended with axioms for predicates.

{defi}
[Derivations and axioms]
Derivations are defined according to the rules of natural deduction for minimal logic. For
\wedge
,
\vee
and
\exists
we use the following axioms:

\displaystyle(\wedge)^{+}:A\to B\to A\wedge B,

\displaystyle(\vee)^{+}_{0}:A\to A\vee B,

\displaystyle(\exists)^{+}:\forall_{x}(A\to\exists_{x}A),

\displaystyle(\vee)^{+}_{1}:B\to A\vee B,

\displaystyle(\wedge)^{-}:(A\to B\to C)\to A\wedge B\to C,

\displaystyle(\vee)^{-}:(A\to C)\to(B\to C)\to A\vee B\to C,

\displaystyle(\exists)^{-}:\forall_{x}(A\to B)\to\exists_{x}A\to B

\displaystyle(x\textnormal{\ not\ free\ in\ }B).

Predicates
\mathbf{I}\coloneqq\mu_{X}(\Phi(X)\subseteq X)
and
\mathbf{J}\coloneqq\nu_{Y}(Y\subseteq\Psi(Y)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I})^{+}:\Phi[X/\mathbf{I}]\subseteq\mathbf{I},\quad

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I})^{\mu}[P]:(\Phi[\mathbf{I}\cap P]\subseteq P)\to(\mathbf{I}\subseteq P),

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J})^{-}:\mathbf{J}\subseteq\Psi[X/\mathbf{J}],

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J})^{\nu}[Q]:(Q\subseteq(\Psi[\mathbf{I}\cup Q]))\to(Q\subseteq\mathbf{J}).

Assuming concrete representations

\mathbf{I}\coloneqq\mu_{X}\big(\forall_{\vec{x}_{i}}(\vec{A}_{i}(X)\to X\vec{t}_{i})\big)_{i<k},\quad\mathbf{J}\coloneqq\nu_{Y}\big(\forall_{\vec{y}_{j}}(\vec{B}_{j}(Y)\to Y\vec{s}_{j})\big)_{j<m},

the axioms are of the following form:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I})^{+}_{i}:\forall_{\vec{x}_{i}}(\vec{A}_{i}(\mathbf{I})\to\mathbf{I}\vec{t}_{i}),\qquad(\mathbf{I})^{\mu}[P]:\big(\forall_{\vec{x}_{i}}(\vec{A}_{i}(\mathbf{I}\cap P))\to P\vec{t}\;\big)_{i<k}\to\mathbf{I}\subseteq P,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J})^{-}:\mathbf{J}\vec{y}\to\bigvee_{j<m}\exists_{\vec{y}_{j}}\big(\vec{y}\equiv\vec{s}_{j}\wedge\bigwedge\vec{B_{j}}(\mathbf{J})\big),

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J})^{\nu}[Q]:\forall_{\vec{y}}\Big(Q\vec{y}\to\bigvee_{j<m}\exists_{\vec{y}_{j}}\big(\vec{y}\equiv\vec{s}_{j}\wedge\bigwedge\vec{B_{j}}(\mathbf{J}\cup Q)\big)\Big)\to P\subseteq\mathbf{J}.

Here
\equiv
is the Leibniz-equality (defined below) and
\vec{x}\equiv\vec{y}:=\bigwedge_{i}(x_{i}\equiv y_{i})
.

Remark 2
.

If
\mathbf{I}\coloneqq\mu_{X}(\Phi(X)\subseteq X)
is a non-recursive inductive predicate i.e.,
X
does not occur freely in
\Phi
, the greatest- and least-fixed-point coincide. Namely, let
\mathbf{J}\coloneqq\nu_{X}(\Phi(X)\subseteq X)
, then the axioms for
\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{J}
are as follows.

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I})^{+}:\Phi\subseteq\mathbf{I},\quad

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J})^{-}:\mathbf{J}\subseteq\Phi,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I})^{\mu}[P]:(\Phi\subseteq P)\to(I\subseteq P),\quad

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J})^{\nu}[Q]:(Q\subseteq\Phi)\to(Q\subseteq\mathbf{J}).

{defi}

[Leibniz equality]

Leibniz equality
\equiv
is the inductive predicate

\equiv\;\coloneqq\mu_{X}\forall_{x}(X\,xx)

and codifies the least reflexive relation. Arithmetical falsity and weak negation are directly defined as the following formulae:

\bot\coloneqq\mathtt{f}\equiv\mathtt{t},\qquad\neg A\coloneqq A\to\bot.

Remark 3
.

The two axioms associated to Leibniz equality
\equiv
are

(\equiv)^{+}:\forall_{x}x\equiv x,\qquad(\equiv)^{\mu}[P]:\forall_{x}Pxx\to\forall_{x,y}(x\equiv y\to Pxy).

With these axioms it is immediate to show that
\equiv
is an equivalence relation. Moreover, we can prove the indiscernibility of identicals

\vdash x\equiv y\to A(x)\to A(y).

Although we work in minimal logic, we can prove the intuitionistic Ex-falso principle from arithmetical falsity
\bot
. Notice that for any two terms
t,s
of the same type
\tau
we can define a program constant
\mathtt{or}_{ts}\;\mathtt{f}=t
,
\mathtt{or}_{ts}\;\mathtt{t}=s
. Assuming
\bot
then gives
\mathtt{or}_{ts}\;\mathtt{f}\equiv\mathtt{or}_{ts}\;\mathtt{t}
, so

\bot\vdash t\equiv s.

Then by induction on formula, we can extend
EfQ
to (almost) all formulas. Namely, we need to exclude some non-terminating inductive predicates e.g.,
\mu_{X}(X\to X)
. For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to inductive predicates
\mathbf{I}

that are inhabited i.e., there exists a term
t
with
t\in\mathbf{I}
. In particular, this is the case if
\mathbf{I}
has a clause
(i<k)
and
X
does not occur freely in any of the premises
A_{i\nu}
.

In the following Proposition we demand all inductive predicates occurring in a formula to be inhabited. As we are using Minimal Logic, Ex-falso with
\bot\coloneqq 0=1
is a Theorem and it fails for non-inhabited inductive predicates
\mathbf{I}
, since these are literally empty i.e., even assuming
0=1
, it is impossible to prove
t\in\mathbf{I}
, for any term
t
. In practice, non-inhabited inductive predicates are not useful.

Proposition 4

(
EfQ
)
.

For any formula
A
, such that all inductive predicates occurring in
A
are inhabited, we have that

\big\{\bot\to X\,|\,X\in\mathbf{PP}(A)\big\}\vdash\bot\to A.

In the following sections, we will often prove statements by
simultaneous induction on formulas and predicates
. In such a proof, in the case of a predicate
\mathbf{I}\coloneqq\pi_{X}\big(\forall_{\vec{x}_{i}}(\vec{A}_{i}(X)\to X\vec{t}_{i})\big)_{i<k}
(
\pi=\mu,\nu)
, we will have to show that
\mathbf{I}
has the desired property, assuming that all premises in all clauses i.e.,
A_{im}(X)
for
i<k,m<|\vec{A}_{i}|
, already have this property. The only distinguishing feature of the formulas
A_{im}
, at this point, is
X\in\mathbf{SP}(A_{im})
, by which either
X\not\in

\mathbf{FP}(A_{im})
i.e.,
A_{im}
is a parameter-premise, or
X\in\mathbf{PP}(A_{im})
and all strictly-positive predicate-formulas
P\vec{t}
, where
X
occurs freely, are of the form:

\displaystyle P\vec{t}=X\vec{t}

\displaystyle\mathrm{i.e.,\ }A_{im}\mathrm{\ is\ a\ recursive\ premise},

\displaystyle P\vec{t}=\vec{t}\in\mathbf{J}(X)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i.e.,\ }A_{im}\mathrm{is\ a\ nested\ premise\ with\ }\mathbf{I}\neq\mathbf{J}\in\mathcal{P}.

This could be an unpleasant case-distinction, but we will avoid it in the next section with the
monotonicity
-property, that we prove below. Its proof is straightforward, using induction on the definition of
\mathbf{SP}(\cdot)
, and it is omitted.

Proposition 5

(Monotonicity)
.

Let
X\in\mathbf{SP}(A),\mathbf{SP(R})
be strictly positive in a formula
A(X)
, respectively a predicate
R(X)
. If
P,Q\neq R
are predicates, then

\vdash(P\subseteq Q)\to(A[X/P]\subseteq A[X/Q]),\qquad\quad\vdash(P\subseteq Q)\to(R[X/P]\subseteq R[X/Q].

3.
Strong Negation in TCF

We first motivate the definition of strong negation by giving a BHK-like explanation, that explains at the same time, how
proofs
, respectively
refutations
, can be combined to
witness
the assertion or
refutation
of compound statements.

Conjunction:

A
proof
of
A\wedge B
is a
proof
of
A
together
proof
of
B
.

Any
refutation
of either
A
or
B

refutes

A\wedge B
.

Disjunction:

A
proof
of
A\vee B
is either a
proof
of
A
or
B
.

A
refutation
of
A
together with a
refutation
of
B
refutes
A\vee B
.

Universal:

A
proof
of
\forall_{x}A
is a routine assigning to any term
t
a
proof
of
A(t)
.
A
refutation
of
\forall_{x}A
is a term
t
together with a
refutation
of
A(t)
.

Existence:

A term
t
with a
proof
of
A(t)
is a
proof
of
\exists_{x}A
.

A
refutation
of
\exists_{x}A
consists of a routine, which assigns a
refutation
of
A(t)
to any term
t
.

Implication:

A
proof
of
A\to B
is a routine, which assigns an proof of
A
a proof of
B
.

A
proof
of
A
together with a
refutation
of
B

refutes

A\to B
.

A logical system for constructive mathematics, which treats proof and refutations both as primitives, which are in some sense symmetric or dual, certainly sounds interresting. Here though, we prefer to stay inside the theory TCF i.e., a refutation is given by a proof of the strong negation. To clarify, we add one more rule namely,

Strong Negation:

A
proof
of the strong negation of
A
is a
refutation
of
A
.

Any
refutation
of
A
is
refutes
a
proof
of the strong negation of
A

To begin with, consider the following instructive example.

{exa}
[Accessible Part of a Relation]

If
\prec
is a binary relation, then the accessible (from below) part of
\prec
is inductively defined with the introduction rule

\forall_{x}\big(\forall_{y}(y\prec x\to y\in\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec})\to x\in\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}\big)

i.e.,
x\in\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}
if any chain
x_{0}\prec\dots\prec x
below
x
is finite. Hence,
x\not\in\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}
means that not all chains below
x
are finite. We can assert the failure of
x\in\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}
by exhibiting an infinite chain below
x
. Formally,
x\in\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}^{\mathbf{N}}
exactly if there is
y\prec x
with the same property. We introduce
\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}^{\mathbf{N}}
as a coinductive predicate with closure rule

x\in\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}^{\mathbf{N}}\to\exists_{y}\big(y\prec x\wedge y\in\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}^{\mathbf{N}}\big).

The corresponding greatest fixed-point axiom is

(\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}^{\mathbf{N}})^{\nu}[Q]:\forall_{x}\big(x\in Q\to\exists_{y}(y\prec x\wedge y\in\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}^{\mathbf{N}}\cup Q)\big)\to Q\subseteq\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}^{\mathbf{N}}.

It corresponds exactly to the inaccessible part of
\prec
, namely,
x\in\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}^{\mathbf{N}}
if there is an infinite chain
\dots\prec y_{1}\prec y_{0}\prec x
. If for example, we consider the direct-predecessor order
\prec\coloneqq\mu_{X}(\forall_{n}n\prec\mathtt{S}n)
on natural numbers, then
n\in\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}\leftrightarrow n\in\mathbf{T}_{\mathbb{N}}
. Now we define the infinite natural number as the program constant

\infty:\mathbb{N},\qquad\infty=\mathtt{S}\infty.

Particularly,
\infty\prec\mathtt{S}\infty=\infty
and the premise of
(\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}^{\mathbf{N}})^{\nu}[\{n\,|\,n\equiv\infty\}]
is derivable i.e.,

\vdash\exists_{y}(y\prec\infty\wedge y\in(\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}^{\mathbf{N}}\vee y\equiv\infty)).

Hence,
\infty\in\mathbf{Acc}_{\prec}^{\mathbf{N}}
.

Next, we define strong negation
A^{\textnormal{{N}}}
of a formula
A
of
\mathrm{TCF}
by simultaneous recursion on
the definition of
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathrm{\mathcal{P}}
.

{defi}

To every formula
A(\vec{x})
with free variables ranging over
\vec{x}
we assign a formula
A^{\mathbf{N}}(\vec{x})
by the following rules:

\displaystyle(P\vec{t})^{\mathbf{N}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\ P^{\mathbf{N}}\vec{t},

\displaystyle(A\vee B)^{\mathbf{N}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\ A^{\mathbf{N}}\wedge B^{\mathbf{N}},

\displaystyle(A\to B)^{\mathbf{N}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\ A\wedge B^{\mathbf{N}},

\displaystyle(\forall_{x}A)^{\mathbf{N}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\ \exists_{x}A^{\mathbf{N}},

\displaystyle(A\wedge B)^{\mathbf{N}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\ A^{\mathbf{N}}\vee B^{\mathbf{N}},

\displaystyle(\exists_{x}A)^{\mathbf{N}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\ \forall_{x}A^{\mathbf{N}}.

Assuming a fixed assignment
X\mapsto X^{\prime},X^{\prime\prime},X^{\prime\prime\prime},\dots
of predicate variables to new distinct predicate variables of the same arity, we assign to each predicate
P
of arity
\vec{\tau}
a predicate
P^{\mathbf{N}}
of the same arity by

X^{\mathbf{N}}\coloneqq(X)^{\prime},\qquad\{\vec{x}\,|\,A\}^{\mathbf{N}}\coloneqq\{\vec{x}\,|\,A^{\mathbf{N}}\}

and
(X^{(n)})^{\prime}\coloneqq X^{(n+1)}
. Furthermore, for two distinct predicate variables
X\neq Y
we require
X^{(n)}\neq Y^{(m)}

For predicates
\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{J}
of the form

\mathbf{I}\coloneqq\mu_{X}\vec{K},\qquad\mathbf{J}\coloneqq\nu_{X}\vec{K},\qquad\vec{K}(X)=\big(\forall_{\vec{x}_{i}}(\vec{A}_{i}(X)\to X\vec{t}_{i})\big)_{i<k},

i.e., they are the least, respectively greatest, fixed-point of the same list of clauses,
the definition of
\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}
and
\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}\coloneqq\nu_{X^{\prime}}\Big(\forall_{\vec{y}}\big(X^{\prime}\vec{y}\to\bigwedge_{i<k}(\forall_{\vec{x}_{i}}(\vec{y}\equiv\vec{t}_{i}\to B_{i}))\big)\Big),

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}\coloneqq\mu_{X^{\prime}}\Big(\forall_{\vec{y}}\big(\bigwedge_{i<k}(\forall_{\vec{x}_{i}}(\vec{y}\equiv\vec{t}_{i}\to B_{i}))\to X^{\prime}\vec{y}\big)\Big).

Here, for
n_{i}:=\mathtt{lth}(\vec{A}_{i})>0
we have

B_{i}:=\bigvee_{\nu<n_{i}}A_{i\nu}^{\mathbf{N}}.

If
n_{i}=0
then
B_{i}\coloneqq\mathtt{f}\equiv\mathtt{t}
i.e., in this case we get the weak negation
\neg(\vec{y}\equiv\vec{t}_{i})
.

Remark 6
.

Recall that the
predicate
\mathbf{I}
, as in the definition above, is equivalent to its operator-form

\vdash\mathbf{I}\vec{y}\leftrightarrow\bigvee_{i<k}\exists_{\vec{x}_{i}}\big(\vec{y}\equiv\vec{t}_{i}\wedge\bigwedge\vec{A}_{i}(\mathbf{I})\big).

Similarly, for
\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}
, which has only one clause, we get

\vdash\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}\vec{y}\leftrightarrow\bigwedge_{i<k}\big(\forall_{\vec{x}_{i}}(\vec{y}\equiv\vec{t}_{i}\to\bigvee_{\nu<n_{i}}A_{i\nu}^{\mathbf{N}}(\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}))\big),

and the right formula is almost strong negation of the previous one on the right. In fact, they differ only by a classical implication in place of a material implication.

Remark 7
.

Note that the logical connectives
\wedge,\vee,\exists
do not need to be introduced as primitives and can be seen as special cases of inductive predicates. In the latter case, their strong negations according to Definition
3
is consistent e.g.,
disjunction and conjunction may be defined via

A\vee^{\prime}B\coloneqq\mu_{X}(A\to X,B\to X),\qquad A\wedge^{\prime}B\coloneqq\mu_{Y}(A\to B\to Y).

strong negations of these predicates are

(A\vee^{\prime}B)^{\mathbf{N}}\coloneqq\nu_{X^{\prime}}(A^{\mathbf{N}}\wedge^{\prime}B^{\mathbf{N}}\to X^{\prime}),\qquad(A\wedge^{\prime}B)^{\mathbf{N}}\coloneqq\nu_{Y^{\prime}}(A^{\mathbf{N}}\vee^{\prime}B^{\mathbf{N}}\to Y^{\prime}),

and since these are non-recursive we obtain

(A\vee^{\prime}B)^{\mathbf{N}}\leftrightarrow A^{\mathbf{N}}\wedge^{\prime}B^{\mathbf{N}},\qquad(A\wedge^{\prime}B)^{\mathbf{N}}\leftrightarrow A^{\mathbf{N}}\vee^{\prime}B^{\mathbf{N}}.

{exa}

[Even and odd]

We define even and odd numbers as the inductive predicates
\mathbf{Ev},\mathbf{Od}
, respectively, with the following introduction rules:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &0\in\mathbf{Ev},\\
&1\in\mathbf{Od},\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} &\forall_{n}(n\in\mathbf{Ev}\to(n+2)\in\mathbf{Ev}),\\
&\forall_{n}(n\in\mathbf{Od}\to(n+2)\in\mathbf{Od}).\end{aligned}

Their strong negations are then coinductive predicates with the closure rules:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &n\in\mathbf{Ev}^{\mathbf{N}}\to n\not\equiv 0\wedge\forall_{m}\big(n\equiv(m+2)\to m\in\mathbf{Ev}^{\mathbf{N}}\big),\\
&n\in\mathbf{Od}^{\mathbf{N}}\to n\not\equiv 1\wedge\forall_{m}\big(n\equiv(m+2)\to m\in\mathbf{Od}^{\mathbf{N}}\big).\end{aligned}

With the least-fixed-point axioms of
\mathbf{Ev},\mathbf{Od}
we easily verify that

\forall_{n}\big(n\in\mathbf{Ev}\to n\in\mathbf{Od}^{\mathbf{N}}\big),\qquad\forall_{n}\big(n\in\mathbf{Od}\to n\in\mathbf{Ev}^{\mathbf{N}}\big).

Furthermore, we prove
\infty\in\mathbf{Ev}^{\mathbf{N}},\mathbf{Od}^{\mathbf{N}}
, since
\infty\neq\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}
and
\infty\equiv(\mathtt{SS}\infty)
.
Hence, to prove the other direction we have to restrict to total natural numbers, and we get

\forall_{n\in\mathbf{T}_{\mathbb{N}}}\big(n\in\mathbf{Ev}\leftrightarrow n\in\mathbf{Od}^{\mathbf{N}}\big),\qquad\forall_{n\in\mathbf{T}_{\mathbb{N}}}\big(n\in\mathbf{Od}\leftrightarrow n\in\mathbf{Ev}^{\mathbf{N}}\big).

Remark 8
.

Although we can represent simultaneous least- and greatest fixed-points via nesting, the theory
\mathrm{TCF}
may also be
directly extended with simultaneous definitions i.e.,

(\mathbf{I},\mathbf{J})\coloneqq\mu_{X,Y}\big[\Phi(X,Y)\subseteq X,\Psi(X,Y)\subseteq Y\big],

where
X,Y
must be strictly positive in both
\Phi,\Psi
and with simultaneous least fixed-point axiom then is

\Phi[\mathbf{I}\cap P,\mathbf{J}\cap Q]\subseteq P\to\Psi[\mathbf{I}\cap P,\mathbf{J}\cap Q]\subseteq P\to(\mathbf{I},\mathbf{J})\subseteq(P,Q).

Definition
3
can, more or less, be directly extended to cover simultaneous definitions. E.g., consider the simultaneous definition of Even and Odd

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Ev}_{\mathrm{sim}},\mathbf{Od}_{\mathrm{sim}})\overset{\mu}{\coloneqq}\Big[

\displaystyle\big[0\in\mathbf{Ev}_{\mathrm{sim}},\forall_{n}(n\in\mathbf{Od}_{\mathrm{sim}}\to(n+1)\in\mathbf{Ev}_{\mathrm{sim}})\big],

\displaystyle\big[\forall_{n}(n\in\mathbf{Ev}_{\mathrm{sim}}\to(n+1)\in\mathbf{Od}_{\mathrm{sim}})\big]\Big].

Then strong negation will be

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Ev}^{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathrm{sim}},\mathbf{Od}^{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathrm{sim}})\overset{\nu}{\coloneqq}\Big[

\displaystyle\big[\forall_{n}\big(n\not\equiv 0\wedge\forall_{m}(n\equiv(m+1)\to m\in\mathbf{Od}^{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathrm{sim}})\to n\in\mathbf{Ev}^{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathrm{sim}}\big)\big],

\displaystyle\big[\forall_{n}\big(\forall_{m}(n\equiv(m+1)\to m\in\mathbf{Ev}^{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathrm{sim}})\to n\in\mathbf{Od}^{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathrm{sim}}\big)\big]\Big].

Particularly, we derive

\displaystyle\vdash\forall_{k}(k\equiv 1\to k\not\equiv 0\wedge\forall_{m}(k\equiv(m+1)\to m\in\mathbf{Od}^{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathrm{sim}}\vee m\equiv 0)),

\displaystyle\vdash\forall_{l}(l\equiv 0\to\forall_{m}(l\equiv(m+1)\to m\in\mathbf{Ev}^{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathrm{sim}}\vee m\equiv 0)),

since
k\equiv 1,k\equiv(m+1)\vdash m\equiv 0
and
l\equiv 0,l\equiv(m+1)\vdash m\equiv 0
. For the latter we use transitivity, as
m\equiv\big[\mathrm{if}\ ((m+1)\neq 0)\ m\ 0\big]\equiv\big[\mathrm{if}\ (0\neq 0)\ m\ 0\big]\equiv 0
. Then we have derived the premise of the simultaneous greatest fixed-point axiom
(\mathbf{Ev}^{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathrm{sim}},\mathbf{Od}^{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathrm{sim}})^{\nu}\big[\{(k,l)\,|\,k\equiv 1\wedge l\equiv 0\}\big]
, so that

\vdash\big(1\in\mathbf{Ev}^{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathrm{sim}}\big)\wedge 0\in\big(\mathbf{Od}^{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathrm{sim}}\big).

We prove, that strong negation of arithmetical falsity is derivable i.e.,
\vdash\bot^{\mathbf{N}}
. Furthermore, strong negation cancels weak negation.

Lemma 9
.

We have that
\vdash\mathtt{f}\equiv^{\mathbf{N}}\mathtt{t}
and
\vdash(\neg A)^{\mathbf{N}}\leftrightarrow A
.

Proof 3.1
.

Strong negation of Leibniz-equality is

\equiv^{\mathbf{N}}\coloneqq\mu_{X^{\prime}}\big(\forall_{\vec{y}}(\forall_{x}(y_{0}\equiv x\wedge y_{1}\equiv x\to\bot)\to\vec{y}\in X^{\prime})\big).

As we can rename the bound predicate variables
X
and
\equiv
is non-recursive, we get

\vdash(x\equiv^{\mathbf{N}}y)\leftrightarrow\big(\mu_{X}\big(\forall_{\vec{y}}\forall_{x}(\vec{y}\not\equiv(x,x))\to\vec{y}\in X\big)\big)xy.

By the transitivity of
\equiv
we have
\mathtt{f}\equiv x\wedge\mathtt{t}\equiv x\to\mathtt{f}\equiv\mathtt{t}
, so
\forall_{x}\neg((\mathtt{f},\mathtt{t})\equiv(x,x))
and hence
\mathtt{f}\equiv^{\mathbf{N}}\mathtt{t}
.
Furthermore,
\vdash(\neg A)^{\mathbf{N}}\leftrightarrow A
since by definition
(\neg A)^{\mathbf{N}}\coloneqq A\wedge(\mathtt{f}\equiv^{\mathbf{N}}\mathtt{t})
.

Remark 10
.

If we consider the equality of booleans
=_{\mathbb{B}}
as the inductive predicate with introduction rules
\mathtt{f}=_{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathbf{N}}\mathtt{f}
and
\mathtt{t}=_{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathbf{N}}\mathtt{t}\overset{\mu}{\coloneqq}(\mathtt{f}=_{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathbf{N}}\mathtt{f},\mathtt{f}=_{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathbf{N}}\mathtt{f})
, then its strong negation is

=_{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathbf{N}}\overset{\nu}{\coloneqq}\forall_{\vec{b}}\big((b_{0}\not\equiv\mathtt{f}\vee b_{1}\not\equiv\mathtt{f})\wedge(b_{0}\not\equiv\mathtt{t}\vee b_{1}\not\equiv\mathtt{t})\to b_{0}=_{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathbf{N}}b_{1}\big).

Thus we immediately get
(\mathtt{f},\mathtt{t}),(\mathtt{t},\mathtt{f})\in=_{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathbf{N}}
. But this is not all, namely, if
b_{0}
is provably a partial term i.e.,

\vdash b_{0}\not\equiv\mathtt{f}\wedge b_{0}\not\equiv\mathtt{t}
, then
(b,b_{0}),(b_{0},b)\in=_{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathbf{N}}
. Hence,
=_{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathbf{N}}
also contains all pairs of booleans where at least one of the components is partial. In the canonical model of
\mathrm{TCF}
(see
[
SW12
]
, Chapter 6) strong negation
\mathbf{K}^{\mathbf{N}}
of some inductive or coinductive predicate
\mathbf{K}
can be seen as the weak complement of
\mathbf{K}\subseteq\{\vec{t}:\vec{\tau}\}
.

{exa}

[Closure of a relation]

Let
\simeq
be a binary relation. We define its reflexive, symmetric and transitive closure as the inductive predicate
\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq)
with the introduction rules

\displaystyle x\simeq y\to(x,y)\in\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq),

\displaystyle(x,x)\in\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq),

\displaystyle(x,y)\in\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq)\to(y,x)\in\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq),

\displaystyle(x,y)\in\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq)\to(y,z)\in\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq)\to(x,z)\in\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq).

Its strong negation is then the greatest fixed-point with closure rule

\displaystyle(x,z)\in(\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq))^{\mathbf{N}}\to\big(

\displaystyle(x\simeq^{\mathbf{N}}z)\ \wedge

\displaystyle(x\not\equiv z)\ \wedge

\displaystyle((z,x)\in(\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq))^{\mathbf{N}})\ \wedge

\displaystyle(\forall_{y}((x,y)\in(\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq))^{\mathbf{N}}\vee(y,z)\in(\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq))^{\mathbf{N}}))\big).

From this closure rule we get that
(\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq))^{\mathbf{N}}
is an apartness relation. Moreover, we have that
\simeq\,\subseteq\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq)
and
(\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq))^{\mathbf{N}}\subseteq\,\simeq^{\mathbf{N}}
i.e.,

\vdash x\simeq y\to(x,y)\in\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq),\qquad\vdash(x,y)\in\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq)^{\mathbf{N}}\to x\simeq^{\mathbf{N}}y.

Hence the embedding
\simeq\subseteq\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq)
is (provably) a strong implication in the sense of Rasiowa
[
Ras74
]
.
Even if
\simeq
is already an equivalence relation particularly,
\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq)\subseteq\simeq
, then in general we cannot prove that
\simeq^{\mathbf{N}}\subseteq(\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq))^{\mathbf{N}}
. It remains to prove symmetry and co-transitivity of
\simeq^{\mathbf{N}}
. However, this does provide a method to define an apartness-relation
\#
for any equivalence relation
\simeq\subseteq S\times S
, namely

\#:=(\mathbf{Cl}(\simeq))^{\mathbf{N}}_{S\times S}.

Note that, by definition,
\#\subseteq\simeq^{\mathbf{N}}
however, in general we do not get the other direction. Particularly,
\#
might be smaller than strong negation of
\simeq
.

4.
Properties of Strong Negation in TCF

Next we prove some fundamental properties of
A^{\textnormal{{N}}}
in
\mathrm{TCF}
. In particular, we establish that
A^{\mathbf{N}}
behaves like a negation particularly, that asserting
A^{\mathbf{N}}
is inconsistent with asserting
A
. Moreover, we prove that
\mathbf{NN}
-elimination holds.

The proofs will be given by induction on formulas, so we will also have to deal with the case of predicate variables. For the proofs to work, we will need to assume that the
predicate variable
X^{\prime}
associated to
X
behaves like a strong (or weak) negation. We introduce the following notation:

{defi}

For
X
of arity
\vec{\tau}
and corresponding variables
\vec{x}
we define the
Ex-falso-
(
\mathbf{EF}
) and
double-negation-elimination
(

Remark 17
.

We have established that the operation
(\cdot)^{\mathbf{N}}
shares properties with weak and classical negation and is strong i.e., (disregarding predicate variables)

•

can express inconsistency
A,A^{\mathbf{N}}\vdash\bot
and hence is stronger than weak negation
\vdash A^{\mathbf{N}}\to\neg A
,

•

admits double-negation-elimination
\vdash A^{\mathbf{NN}}\to A
,

•

definitionally,
(A\wedge B)^{\mathbf{N}}=A^{\mathbf{N}}\vee B^{\mathbf{N}}
and
\forall_{x}A^{\mathbf{N}}=\exists_{x}A^{\mathbf{N}}
.

Next we will develop further properties of
A^{\mathbf{N}}
. Particularly, we can easily exhibit counterexamples to the following schemas, usually associated with negations, either intuitionistic or classical

\displaystyle\nvdash(B^{\mathbf{N}}\to A^{\mathbf{N}})\to A\to B,\quad

\displaystyle\nvdash(A\to B)\to B^{\mathbf{N}}\to A^{\mathbf{N}},

\displaystyle\nvdash A\to A^{\mathbf{NN}},

\displaystyle\nvdash A^{\mathbf{N}}\to A^{\mathbf{NNN}},

\displaystyle\nvdash(A^{\mathbf{N}}\to A)\to A,

\displaystyle\nvdash((A\to B)^{\mathbf{N}}\to C)\to A\to B\vee C.

These stark differences to other negations are firstly due to the weakness of the implication-connective and the resulting, rather strong,
(A\to B)^{\mathbf{N}}=A\wedge B^{\mathbf{N}}
. Secondly, the involutive part of the definition of strong negation, i.e.,
\forall/\penalty\exists
and
\wedge/\penalty\vee
, is also asymmetric, since
\exists/\penalty\vee
-statements tend to carry more information than
\forall/\penalty\wedge
-statements. Particularly, this explains, why proof by strong contraposition is not admissible. Namely,

\nvdash(B^{\mathbf{N}}\to A^{\mathbf{N}})\to A\to B,

is usually the case, if
B
is a (non-trivial)
\exists
- or
\vee
-formula. Moreover, the other directions failure

\nvdash(A\to B)\to B^{\mathbf{N}}\to A^{\mathbf{N}},

is most often due to
A
being an
\to
-,
\forall
-, or
\wedge
-formula.
As an example take the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
with equality
=_{\mathbb{R}}
and apartness-relation
\#_{\mathbb{R}}=(=_{\mathbb{R}})^{\mathbf{N}}
and consider the following pair of strong contra-positives

\displaystyle\vdash\forall_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}}(x\#_{\mathbb{R}}0\wedge y\#_{\mathbb{R}}0\to(x\cdot y)\#_{\mathbb{R}}0),

\displaystyle\nvdash\forall_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}}((x\cdot y)=_{\mathbb{R}}0\to x=_{\mathbb{R}}0\vee y=_{\mathbb{R}}0).

For a proof of the latter we need at least the Lesser Limited Principle of Omniscience (
LLPO
) or, equivalently, the dichotomy of reals

\forall_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big(0\leq_{\mathbb{R}}x\vee x\leq_{\mathbb{R}}0\big),

which is not derivable constructively.

In contrast to weak negation, which is nesting implications on the left, strong negation
A^{\mathbf{N}}
consumes implications. This is the reason for the failure
A\to A^{\mathbf{NN}}
, which actually holds for implication-free formulas. Most of the negative statements above can be explained by the fact, that proofs of
A^{\mathbf{N}}
underlie the same restriction as proofs of any other positive formula.

As remarked above, if there are no implications, then
A^{\mathbf{N}}
simply flips
\wedge/\vee
and
\forall/\exists
. Particularly
A^{\mathbf{NN}}
is an involution.

For all implication-free formulas

By straightforward induction on formulas.

Remark 21
.

As there are undecidable formulas, in general we have neither

\vdash(A\to B)\to B^{\mathbf{N}}\to A^{\mathbf{N}}\qquad\text{nor}\qquad\vdash(A\leftrightarrow B)\to(A^{\mathbf{N}}\leftrightarrow B^{\mathbf{N}}).

Constructively, the strong contrapositive
B^{\mathbf{N}}\to A^{\mathbf{N}}
can be used to obtain more informative definitions. As an example consider the classical least-upper-bound
lub(x,S)
of a non-empty and bounded set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}

lub(x,S)\coloneqq\forall_{y}(\forall_{z}(z\in S\to z\leq y)\to x\leq y),

provided
x
is known to be an upper bound already.
Namely, if

then any other upper bound of
S
is above
x

sup(x,S)\coloneqq(\forall_{y}(y<x\to\exists_{z}(z\in S\wedge y<z)),

the constructive definition of the least upper bound and computationally much more useful.

Hence, it is meaningful to investigate formulas

Note, that in the case of an implication the induction hypotheses is only used for the conclusion and
\mathbf{PP}(A\to B)=\mathbf{PP}(B)

Case
\mathbf{I}\coloneqq\mu_{X}(\Phi(X)\subseteq X)
:

\mathbf{I}
is inductive, so the induction hypothesis is

\mathbf{Ih}_{\mathbf{I}}(X,X^{\prime}):\neg X\to X^{\prime}\vdash_{\mathrm{c}}\neg\Phi(X)\to\Phi^{\mathbf{N}}(X^{\prime}).

From this, we have to provide a derivation of
\neg\mathbf{I}\subseteq\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}
,
and since
\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}\coloneqq\nu_{X^{\prime}}(X^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi^{\mathbf{N}}(X^{\prime}))
, we may use the greatest fixed-point axiom
(\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}})^{\nu}[\neg\mathbf{I}]
for that purpose. Then it suffices to show

\neg\mathbf{I}\to\Phi^{\mathbf{N}}[\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}\cup\neg\mathbf{I}].

We instantiate the induction hypothesis to

\mathbf{Ih}_{\mathbf{I}}[\mathbf{I},\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}\cup\neg\mathbf{I}]\neg\mathbf{I}\to\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}\cup\neg\mathbf{I}\vdash_{\mathrm{c}}\neg\Phi[\mathbf{I}]\to\Phi^{\mathbf{N}}[\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}\cup\neg\mathbf{I}].

The open assumptions holds trivially and since
\mathbf{I}\leftrightarrow\Phi[\mathbf{I}]
, we also have
\neg\mathbf{I}\leftrightarrow\neg\Phi[\mathbf{I}]
and hence

\vdash_{\mathrm{c}}\neg\mathbf{I}\to\Phi^{\mathbf{N}}[\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}\cup(\neg\mathbf{I})].

Case
\mathbf{J}\coloneqq\nu_{Z}(Z\subseteq\Psi(Z))
:

\mathbf{J}
is a coinductive predicate and we have the induction hypothesis

\mathbf{Ih}_{\mathbf{J}}(Z,Z^{\prime}):\neg Z\to Z^{\prime}\vdash_{\mathrm{c}}\neg\Psi(Z)\to\Psi^{\mathbf{N}}(Z^{\prime}).

The goal is to find a derivation of
\neg\mathbf{J}\subseteq\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}
. Since we presently find ourselves in classical logic, we may replace implications with their contrapositive and delete double weak negations on the way. Particularly, from the fixed-point axiom
(\mathbf{J})^{\nu}[\neg\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}]
we get

\vdash_{\mathrm{c}}\big(\neg\Psi[\mathbf{J}\cup\neg\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}]\subseteq\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}\big)\to\neg\mathbf{J}\subseteq\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}.

Moreover, by substitution in the induction hypothesis

\mathbf{Ih}_{\mathbf{J}}[\mathbf{J}\cup\neg\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}},\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}]:\neg\big(\mathbf{J}\cup\neg\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}\big)\subseteq\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}\vdash_{\mathrm{c}}\neg\Psi\big[\mathbf{J}\cup\neg\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}\big]\to\Psi^{\mathbf{N}}[\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}].

The assumption on the left is classically derivable via
\vdash\neg(\mathbf{J}\cup\neg\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}})\leftrightarrow\neg\mathbf{J}\cap\neg\neg\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}
and the stability
\mathbf{St}_{\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}}
. Finally,
\Psi^{\mathbf{N}}[\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}]\subseteq\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{N}}
, and the proof is completed.

5.
Tight Formulas

In
\mathrm{CM}
an inequality
\neq_{X}
associated to a set
(X,=_{X})
is a binary relation, which is irreflexive and symmetric. It is called tight, if for every
x,x{{}^{\prime}}\in X
we have that

\neg(x\neq_{X}x{{}^{\prime}})\to x=_{X}x{{}^{\prime}}
. If the inequality
\neq_{X}
is equivalent to the strong inequality on
X

induced by
=_{X}
i.e.,

x\neq_{X}x{{}^{\prime}}\Leftrightarrow(x=_{X}x{{}^{\prime}})^{\textnormal{{N}}}
, then
(X,=_{X},\neq_{X})
is called a

strong
set, and then
=_{X}
is called tight, if
\neq_{X}
is tight. Similarly, a subset
A
of a strong
set
X
is called a
tight subset
, if
\big(A^{\neq}\big)^{c}\subseteq A
. Within
\mathrm{TCF}
we may now express the
tightness
of a formula
A
as

\vdash(A^{\mathbf{N}}\to\bot)\to A.

{defi}

We call a formula
A
tight, if
\vdash(A^{\mathbf{N}}\to\bot)\to A
.

In relation to strong implication, tight formulas are of interest, since they allow proof by strong contrapositive.

Lemma 24
.

Assume
A,B
are formulae, then

\mathbf{EF}_{\mathbf{PP}(A)}\cup\{\neg(B^{\mathbf{N}})\to B\}\vdash(B^{\mathbf{N}}\to A^{\mathbf{N}})\to A\to B.

Proof 5.1
.

Assume
B
is tight,
B^{\mathbf{N}}\to A^{\mathbf{N}}
and
A
. By tightness of
B
, we further assume
B^{\mathbf{N}}
and prove
\bot
. The latter follows with
\mathbf{EfQ}
and Lemma
12
for the formula
A
.

Remark 25
.

As we are working in minimal logic, we are not especially attached to the arithmetical expression
\bot
. Due to the negative occurrence of
\bot
in the definition of tightness above, we can replace it with something stronger and in fact, with any formula, inconsistent with
A^{\mathbf{N}}
, particularly we have
\bot\vdash A
and
A^{\mathbf{N}},A\vdash\bot
, so

\vdash(A^{\mathbf{N}}\to\bot)\leftrightarrow(A^{\mathbf{N}}\to A).

Then
A
is tight if and only if

\vdash(A^{\mathbf{N}}\to A)\to A.

In the partial setting of
TCF
very few (non-trivial) formulas will be tight. The term
tightness
is commonly used as an attribute of a relation and relations usually come with an intended domain. E.g., the pointwise equality on unary natural numbers is

\approx_{\mathbb{N}}\;\overset{\mu}{\coloneqq}\big[0\approx_{\mathbb{N}}0,\forall_{n,m}(n\approx_{\mathbb{N}}m\to\mathtt{S}n\approx_{\mathbb{N}}\mathtt{S}m)\big],

and the intended domain are the
total
natural numbers

\mathbf{T}_{\mathbb{N}}\overset{\mu}{\coloneqq}[0\in\mathbf{T}_{\mathbb{N}},\forall_{n}(n\in\mathbf{T}_{\mathbb{N}}\to(\mathtt{S}n)\in\mathbf{T}_{\mathbb{N}})].

The relation
\approx_{\mathbb{N}}
is only going to be tight if we restrict appropriately namely,

\vdash\forall_{n,m\in\mathbf{T}_{\mathbb{N}}}\big(\neg(n\approx_{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbf{N}}m)\to n\approx_{\mathbb{N}}m\big).

In light of the discussion above, we introduce the notion of
relative tightness
. Particularly, our notion will capture relations on cartesian products of finite, positive length i.e., we consider relativisations of the form

\forall_{x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}}\big(C_{0}(x_{0})\to\dots\to C_{n-1}(x_{n-1})\to A\big),

where
\mathbf{fv}(A)=\vec{x}
are all the free variables of the formula
A
and
\vec{C}
are independent in the sense
\mathbf{fv}(C_{i})=\{x_{i}\}
.

{defi}

Let
A
be a formula with free variables
\mathbf{fv}(A)=\vec{x}
(
|\vec{x}|=n>0
) and
P
a predicate of arity
\vec{\tau}
(
|\vec{\tau}|=m>0)
. For
i<n
let
C_{i}
be a formula with
\mathbf{fv}(C_{i})=\{x_{i}\}
. We call
A

tight relative to

\vec{C}
,
A\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{C}}
, if

\vdash\forall_{\vec{x}}\big(\vec{C}\to\neg A^{\mathbf{N}}\to A\big).

Furthermore, let
S_{l}
be a predicate of arity
\tau_{l}
, for each
l<m
. We say that
P
is
tight relative
to
\vec{S}
, if

\vdash\forall_{\vec{y}^{\vec{\tau}}}\big((y_{l}\in S_{l})_{l<m}\to\neg(\vec{y}\in P^{\mathbf{N}})\to\vec{y}\in P\big).

Some basic properties are collected in the Proposition below.

Proposition 26
.

Let
A,\vec{C}
be formulae according to the requirements of Definition
5
and, additionally assume
\mathbf{PP}(A)=\{\,\}
. Then the tightness of
A
relative to
\vec{C}
is located in between (strong) decidability and stability on
\vec{C}
, respectively i.e.,

\displaystyle\vdash\forall_{\vec{x}}\big(\vec{C}\to A\vee A^{\mathbf{N}}\big)\;\Rightarrow

\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{C}},

\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{C}}\;\Rightarrow\;\vdash\forall_{\vec{x}}\big(\vec{C}\to\neg\neg A\to A\big).

Moreover, if
A\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{C}}
, then the formula
\forall_{\vec{x}}(\vec{C}\to A)
is tight.

Proof 5.2
.

The first two are easy consequences of Proposition
4
Lemma
12
. For the third we need to prove

\vdash\neg\big(\forall_{\vec{x}}(\vec{C}\to A)\big)^{\mathbf{N}}\to\forall_{\vec{x}}(\vec{C}\to A).

From the assumption

\neg\big[\forall_{\vec{x}}(\vec{C}\to A)\big]^{\mathbf{N}}\leftrightarrow\forall_{\vec{x}}\,\neg\Big[\Big(\!\bigwedge_{i<n}C_{i}\Big)\wedge A^{\mathbf{N}}\Big],

and
\vec{x}
with
\vec{C}
, we obtain
\neg A^{\mathbf{N}}
and finally
A
from
A\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{C}}
.

Next we characterize the interaction of the logical connectives with the notion of relative tightness. As anticipated, it behaves well in combination with
\to,\wedge,\forall
.

Lemma 27
.

Let
A,B
be formulas then

\begin{gathered}A\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{C}(\vec{x}),D(y)}\;\Rightarrow\;\big(\forall_{y}(D(y)\to A)\big)\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{C}},\qquad\quad B\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{D}(\vec{y})}\;\Rightarrow\;(A\to B)\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{D}(\vec{y}),\vec{R}(\vec{x})},\\[5.0pt]
\left.\begin{aligned} &A\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{C}_{A}(\vec{x}),\vec{D}_{A}(\vec{y})},\\
&B\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{C}_{B}(\vec{x}),\vec{D}_{B}(\vec{z})}\end{aligned}\right\}\;\Rightarrow\;\big(A\wedge B\big)\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{C}_{A}\wedge\vec{C}_{B},\vec{D}_{A},\vec{D}_{B}}\end{gathered}

where in the second case,
\vec{x}\coloneqq\mathbf{fv}(A)\setminus\mathbf{fv(B)}
and for all
i<|\vec{x}|
set
R_{i}\coloneqq x_{i}\equiv x_{i}
and, in the last case
\vec{C}_{A}\wedge\vec{C}_{B}=\big(C_{Ai}(x_{i})\wedge C_{Bi}(x_{i})\big)
.

Proof 5.3
.

Case 1:

We need to prove

\vdash\forall_{\vec{x}}\big(\vec{C}(\vec{x})\to\neg\big[\exists_{y}\big(D(y)\wedge A^{\mathbf{N}}\big)\big]\to\forall_{y}(D(y)\to A)\big).

We use the equivalence
\vdash\neg[\exists_{y}(D\wedge A^{\mathbf{N}})]\leftrightarrow\forall_{y}\neg[D\wedge A^{\mathbf{N}}],

which we can combine with the fact
\vdash\neg[D\wedge A^{\mathbf{N}}]\to D\to\neg A^{\mathbf{N}},

and the assumption.

Case 2:

The goal is to find a derivation supporting

\vdash\forall_{\vec{x},\vec{y}}\Big(\big[\vec{D}(\vec{y}),(x_{i}\equiv x_{i})_{i<|\vec{x}|}\big]\to\neg\Big[A\wedge B^{\mathbf{N}}\big]\to A\to B\big),

from the assumption
B\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{D}(\vec{y})}
i.e.,

\vdash\forall_{\vec{y}}\big(\vec{D}(\vec{y})\to\neg B^{\mathbf{N}}\to B\big).

and follows in a similar way, using
\vdash\neg[A\wedge B^{\mathbf{N}}]\to A\to\neg B^{\mathbf{N}}
and
(\equiv)^{+}
.

Case 3:

The goal is unfolded to

\forall_{\vec{x}}\big(\vec{E}\to\neg\big[A^{\mathbf{N}}\vee B^{\mathbf{N}}\big]\to A\wedge B\big]\big).

Here we employ the fact
\vdash\neg[A^{\mathbf{N}}\vee B^{\mathbf{N}}]\leftrightarrow[(\neg A^{\mathbf{N}})\wedge(\neg B^{\mathbf{N}})]
.

Hence, we now consider predicates
\mathbf{I}

\vec{\tau}

\mathbf{I}\coloneqq\omega_{X}\big[\forall_{\vec{x_{i}}}\big(\vec{A}_{i}(X)\to\vec{B}_{i}\to X\vec{t}_{i}\big)\big]_{i<k}\quad(\omega=\mu,\nu).

In order to make an assumption
\neg[\vec{x}\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}]
useful, we introduce a reasonable domain-restriction for predicates.

{defi}

For
\mathbf{I}
as above with

\mathbf{I}
is

\vec{t}_{i}
do not overlap i.e., for any non-constant map

\vdash\forall_{\vec{x_{i}},\vec{z}}\,\neg(\vec{t}_{i}\equiv(t_{(f\,m)m})_{m<k}),

where
\vec{z}

Remark 28
.

Recall, that for
\mathbf{I}

\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}
, we always have the property

\vdash\vec{s}\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}\leftrightarrow\bigwedge_{i<k}\forall_{{x}_{i}}\big[\vec{s}\equiv\vec{t}_{i}\to\bigvee\vec{A}_{i}\,\!\!^{\mathbf{N}}[X^{\prime}/\mathbf{I}^{\mathbf{N}}\big].

Assume, that we have proven, that the terms
\vec{s}
are not of the form of
\vec{t}_{i}
(
i<k

\mathbf{I}
i.e.,

\vdash\bigwedge_{i<k}\forall_{\vec{x}_{i}}\vec{s}\not\equiv\vec{t}_{i}.

Then we can prove

Usually it is not hard to prove relative tightness at all, but really, the goal is to find simple and reasonable

[Similarity]

\displaystyle t(\approx_{\iota})^{\mathbf{N}}s\to\bigwedge_{i<k}\bigg(\forall_{\vec{x}_{i},\vec{y}_{i}}\Big((t,s)\equiv(\mathtt{C}_{i}\vec{x}_{i},\mathtt{C}_{i}\vec{y}_{i})\to\bigvee_{\kappa}(x_{i\kappa}\approx y_{i\kappa})^{\mathbf{N}}\Big)\bigg).

\displaystyle 0\approx_{\mathbb{N}}0,\qquad\forall_{n,m}(n\approx_{\mathbb{N}}m\to\mathtt{S}n\approx_{\mathbb{N}}\mathtt{S}m),

[Cauchy reals, equality and apartness]

We consider the Cauchy-reals of type
\mathbb{R}:=(\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Q})\times(\mathbb{\mathbb{P}\to\mathbb{N}})
i.e., rational sequences with an explicit modulus of Cauchyness, where
\mathbb{P}
is the type of positive integers. Furthermore, we restrict to
x,y
of type
\mathbb{R}
that are total, namely,
x\in\mathbf{T}_{\mathbb{R}}\Leftrightarrow x=(x_{s},x_{m})
, where
x_{s},x_{m}
are total functions, and consider pairs
(x_{s},x_{m})
that are Cauchy-sequences i.e.,

\forall_{p}\forall_{n,k\geq(x_{m}p)}\big(|(x_{s}\,n)-(x_{s}\,k)|\leq_{\mathbb{Q}}2^{-p}\big).

Nonnegativity and positivity are defined by

\displaystyle 0\leq_{\mathbb{R}}x\coloneqq\forall_{p}\exists_{n\geq(x_{m}p)}\big(-2^{-p}<_{\mathbb{Q}}(x_{s}\,n)\big),

\displaystyle 0<_{\mathbb{R}}x\coloneqq\exists_{p}\forall_{n\geq(x_{m}\,p)}\big(2^{-p}\leq_{\mathbb{Q}}(x_{s}\,n)\big),

respectively. The less (
<_{\mathbb{Q}}
) and less-than-or-equal (
\leq_{\mathbb{Q}}
) relation for rational numbers are terms of type
\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{B}
, which are decidable on total inputs. In particular,
q_{0}\leq_{\mathbb{Q}}q_{1}=\mathtt{b}\leftrightarrow q_{1}<_{\mathbb{Q}}q_{0}=\mathtt{\overline{b}}
. Hence strong negation of nonnegativity is

(0\leq_{\mathbb{R}}x)^{\mathbf{N}}\coloneqq\exists_{p}\forall_{n\geq(x_{m}p)}\big(-2^{-p}>_{\mathbb{Q}}(x_{s}\,n)\big).

Then we immediately get
(0\leq_{\mathbb{R}}x)^{\mathbf{N}}\leftrightarrow(0<_{\mathbb{R}}-x)
. Notice that due to the Cauchyness of

we get

\displaystyle\vdash(0\leq_{\mathbb{R}}x)\leftrightarrow\forall_{p}\big(-2^{-p}\leq(x_{s}(x_{m}\,p))\big),

\displaystyle\vdash(0<_{\mathbb{R}}x)\leftrightarrow\exists_{p}\big(2^{-p}\leq(x_{s}(x_{m}(p+1)))\big).

If we define

\displaystyle x\leq_{\mathbb{R}}y\coloneqq 0\leq_{\mathbb{R}}y-x,\qquad x<_{\mathbb{R}}y\coloneqq 0<_{\mathbb{R}}y-x,

\displaystyle x=_{\mathbb{R}}y\coloneqq x\leq_{\mathbb{R}}y\wedge y\leq_{\mathbb{R}}x,

then strong negation of equality on reals is

(x=_{\mathbb{R}}y)^{\mathbf{N}}\coloneqq y<_{\mathbb{R}}x\vee x<_{\mathbb{R}}y,

\mathbb{R}
. We also write
