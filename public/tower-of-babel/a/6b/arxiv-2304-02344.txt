From ABC to KPZ
G. Cannizzaro, P. Gonçalves, R. Misturini, A. Occelli

Abstract
We study the equilibrium fluctuations of an interacting particle system evolving on the discrete ring with N ∈ N N\in\mdmathbb N points, denoted by T N \mdmathbb T_{N} , and with three species of particles that we name A , B A,B and C C , but such that at each site there is only one particle. We prove that proper choices of density fluctuation fields (that match those from nonlinear fluctuating hydrodynamics theory) associated to the (two) conserved quantities converge, in the limit N → ∞ N\to\infty , to a system of stochastic partial differential equations, that can either be the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation or the Stochastic Burgers equation. To understand the cross interaction between the two conserved quantities, we derive a general version of the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma which is of independent interest.



1
Introduction

One of the major open problems in statistical mechanics is the characterisation of the macroscopic evolution equations from the large-scale description of the conserved quantities in Newtonian particle systems. By replacing a deterministic dynamics with a stochastic one, many mathematical techniques have been developed in the last forty years and very interesting results have been obtained. The first class of results is related to the well-known hydrodynamic limit, which consists of deriving the space-time evolution equations for the conserved quantities of a system from the underlying random evolution of its microscopic counterpart. The second is related to the description of the fluctuations of the random microscopic system around its typical profile. In the former case, the limit is deterministic and given in terms of a solution to a PDE, the hydrodynamic equation; while in the latter, the limit is random and given in terms of a solution to a stochastic PDE.
The focus of this article is on the second problem and more specifically our goal is to derive the fluctuations
for a model that has
more than one
conservation law. To illustrate our results we first give a brief overview on
what happens for a simplified version of our model which has only one conservation law.

1.1
The exclusion process

One of the most studied interacting particle systems (and perhaps the most classical one) is the exclusion process. Let us consider its evolution on the discrete one dimensional torus with
N
sites, that we denote by
\mdmathbb T_{N}
. At each site of
\mdmathbb T_{N}
there can be at most one particle and, after an exponential clock of rate
1
, particles at the bond
\{x,y\}
exchange their positions independently at a rate given by a transition probability
p(\cdot)
. We restrict ourselves to the case in which particles only move to nearest neighbour sites so that if
z
is the size of the jump, then
p(z)=0
if
|z|>1.

When
p(\cdot)
is symmetric i.e.
p(1)=p(-1)=1/2
, the system is the well known symmetric simple exclusion process (SSEP); when
p(1)=1-p(-1)=p\neq 1/2
the system is the so-called asymmetric simple exclusion process (ASEP) and when
p(1)-p(-1)=E/N
,
\gamma\in[0,\infty]
, the system is the weakly asymmetric simple exclusion process (WASEP), which interpolates between the SSEP (for
\gamma=\infty
) and the ASEP (for
\gamma=0
and
E=2p-1
). Since particles only swap positions on
\mdmathbb{T}_{N}
, the conserved quantity is the number of particles, i.e.
\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb T_{N}}\eta_{t}(x)
where the variable
\eta_{t}(x)
denotes the number of particles at site
x
and time
t
. From the exclusion rule
\eta_{t}(x)\in\{0,1\}
and if
\eta_{t}(x)=1
, we say that the site
x
is occupied, and empty otherwise.
The invariant measures are the Bernoulli product measures of parameter
\varrho\in(0,1)
, that indicates the density of particles.

We consider the system speeded up in the time scale
tN^{a}
, where
a>0
and we define the empirical measure associated to the unique conserved quantity, i.e. the number of particles, as

\pi_{t}^{N}(du):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb T_{N}}\eta_{tN^{a}}(x)\,\delta_{\frac{x}{N}}\left(du\right),

where for
\tilde{u}\in\mdmathbb T
the notation
\delta_{\tilde{u}}(du)
is for the Dirac measure at
\tilde{u}
.
The hydrodynamic equation for
\pi^{N}
depends on the asymmetry, which in turn determines the relevant time-scale:
for SSEP the time scale is diffusive,
tN^{2}
, and the hydrodynamic equation is the heat equation given by
\partial_{t}\varrho(t,u)=\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\varrho(t,u)
; for ASEP the time scale is hyperbolic,
tN
, and the equation is the inviscid Burgers equation
\partial_{t}\varrho(t,u)=E\nabla F(\varrho(t,u))
, for
F(\varrho)=\varrho(1-\varrho)
; for WASEP with
\gamma=1
,
the time scale is again diffusive,
tN^{2}
, and the equation is the viscous Burgers’ equation

\partial_{t}\varrho(t,u)=1/2\Delta\varrho(t,u)+E\nabla F(\varrho(t,u))\,,

where
F
is the same as before, see
[
14
]
for a proof on the weakly asymmetric case and references therein for the other cases.

Now we turn to the description of the fluctuations around the hydrodynamic limit. The study of non-equilibrium fluctuations is usually very intricate as it requires a good knowledge of the decay of correlations of the system, which is generally difficult to have. From now on, we assume that the system starts from the Bernoulli product measure of parameter
\varrho
.
We define the fluctuation field associated to the density
as the linear functional defined on a smooth test function
f
as

{\mathcal{Y}^{N}_{t}({f})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}f(\tfrac{x}{N})(\eta_{tN^{a}}(x)-\varrho)}.

Once again, the strength of the asymmetry plays a crucial role in the type of limit
\mathcal{Y}

we can get for
\mathcal{Y}^{N}
: for SSEP in the diffusive time scaling
tN^{2}
,
it is given by the solution to a Ornstein–Uhlenbeck equation;
for WASEP with weak asymmetry, i.e.
\gamma>1/2
, and under diffusive scaling,
by the solution to a Ornstein–Uhlenbeck equation,
while for
\gamma=1/2
by the energy solution to the stochastic Burgers (SB) equation
(see Definition
2.5
); and finally for the ASEP in hyperbolic scaling
tN
,
the fluctuations are linearly transported in time with a velocity given by
(1-2\varrho)(1-2p)
.
Note that if
\varrho=1/2
, then the evolution for the limit field in ASEP in the hyperbolic time scale is trivial
and the same holds even for
\varrho\neq 1/2
, as can be seen by redefining the field in a time moving frame with the velocity
(1-2\varrho)(p-q)N^{a-1}t
.
To get non-trivial fluctuations we have to speed up time and choose
a=3/2
. Upon doing so
the limiting field is the so-called KPZ fixed point (KPZ-fp)
[
35
]
, which has been constructed in
[
34
]
. In
[
17
]
it was proved that up to the time scale
tN^{\delta}
with
\delta<1/3
there is no evolution of the field. In fact, the results of
[
17
]
applied to WASEP show that below the line
a=\tfrac{4}{3}(\gamma+1)
the evolution is trivial, but in fact, this behaviour goes up to the line
a=\gamma+3/2
and in all this line the limit is the KPZ-fp, see
[
35
]
.

Summarising, for simple exclusion processes by changing the strength of the asymmetry, the system has either diffusive behaviour (Gaussian fluctuations, for
\gamma>1/2
), or KPZ behaviour (when
\gamma\leq 1/2
) and in between, for
\gamma=1/2
, there is the SB equation.
In fact, these large-scale statistics can be obtained from a variety of different microscopic models and, therefore, they are universal, in the sense that the limit does not depend on the details of the underlying microscopic model.

1.2
Universality for one component systems

To characterise the universality classes for one-component systems,
let
h=h(t,u)
be the stochastic process encoding the quantity of interest for our model (usually called height function),
and
r,z
be two constants. We consider the space-time renormalization group operator with exponents
1:1/r:z/r
given by

\mathfrak{R}_{\lambda}h(t,u)=\lambda^{-1}h(t\lambda^{z/r},u\lambda^{-1/r}).

A universality class consists of the basin of attraction of the limit
H:=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\mathfrak{R}_{\lambda}h
under the operation of rescaling
\mathfrak{R}_{\lambda}
defined above.
For one component systems, as the exclusion processes described above, several universality classes arise: the Edwards-Wilkinson (EW) class whose exponents are 1:2:4 and the super-diffusive KPZ class, whose scaling exponents are 1:2:3. The connection between these two universality classes, namely the EW and the KPZ, is the KPZ equation or the SBE.
In the case of the simple exclusion process for an asymmetry of order
O(N^{-\gamma})
, the crossover goes from diffusive behaviour, i.e. the EW class (corresponding to the phase where symmetry dominates,
\gamma>1/2
), to KPZ for

\gamma<1/2
and time scale
a=\frac{3}{2}+\gamma
, and the transition goes through the SB (or the KPZ) equation, corresponding to the phase where both symmetry and asymmetry have the same impact
\gamma=1/2
, see
[
35
,
43
]
.

We highlight that the list of universality classes is not exhausted by those described above. In
[
11
]
, it was introduced
a temperature-dependent model which reduces to the classical ballistic deposition model at zero temperature (and thus conjectured to display KPZ-type fluctuations),
but whose infinite-temperature version is a random interface whose large-scale statistics are neither EW nor KPZ.
Its scaling limit is the Brownian Castle (BC), a renormalization fixed point, whose scaling exponents are 1:1:2.
The BC is itself conjectured to be universal, in that any interface model which displays
both horizontal and vertical fluctuations but no smoothing should belong to its basin of attraction.
Moreover, while for SEP it is the asymmetry whose tuning determines the crossover from EW to KPZ
and under a suitable scaling leads to the SB equation, for the connection between BC and EW a similar role is played by the smoothing.
As shown in
[
12
]
, there is an uncountable family of (different) processes, the so-called
\nu
-Brownian Castles,
for
\nu
a probability measure on
[0,1]
, which interpolate BC and EW and therefore represent the analogue
of the SB equation in this context.

1.3
Nonlinear Fluctuation Hydrodynamic Theory

Universality classes are identified by exponents and scaling functions that characterise the macroscopic behaviour of the fluctuations of the thermodynamical quantities of interest in a microscopic system. To see what universality classes might pop up and how the aforementioned exponents and scaling functions arise, we outline the approach of Nonlinear Fluctuation Hydrodynamics Theory (NLFH), which precisely describes the fluctuations of the conserved quantities of multi-component systems in terms of stochastic PDEs. The starting point of NLFH is the hydrodynamic scenario in a strong asymmetric regime (to make an analogy to WASEP, this corresponds to
\gamma=0
or simply bear in mind ASEP).

Suppose that our microscopic system has a family of invariant measures parametrised by a quantity
\varrho
and
let us denote by
\mu_{\varrho}
the corresponding measure,
with
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mu_{\varrho}}
the average with respect to it.
Assume that the hydrodynamic equation for the thermodynamical quantity of interest is (in the hyperbolic scaling
tN
) given by

\partial_{t}\varrho(t,u)+\partial_{x}j(t,u)=0,

where

j(t,u)=\langle j\rangle_{\mu_{\varrho(t,u)}}
and
j
is the instantaneous current of the system, and from now on we will always denote the derivative with respect to the first variable (i.e. time) by
\partial_{t}
and
\partial_{x}
that with respect to
the second (i.e. space). Writing the current in terms of the density
\varrho
, then we have
\partial_{x}j(\varrho(t,u))=j^{\prime}(\varrho(t,u))\partial_{x}\varrho(t,u)
so that the previous display reads

\partial_{t}\varrho(t,u)+j^{\prime}(\varrho(t,u))\partial_{x}\varrho(t,u)=0.

Now we add a diffusion term
D\partial_{x}^{2}\varrho(t,u)
and a conservative noise
B\partial_{x}\xi
, where
\xi
is a space-time white-noise. The idea now consists of expanding
\varrho(t,u)
around its stationary value
\varrho
as
\varrho(t,u)=\varrho+Y(t,u)
. Before proceeding, let us see what happens in ASEP. In this case the averaged current is given by
j(\varrho)=(p-q)\varrho(1-\varrho)
. For simplicity let us take
p=1
, but the same argument works for any
p\in(1/2,1]
. Then we obtain

\partial_{t}Y(t,u)=D\partial_{x}^{2}Y(t,u)-\partial_{x}(1-2\varrho)Y(t,u)+\partial_{x}(Y(t,u))^{2}-B\partial_{x}\xi_{u}(t),

which is nothing but a stochastic Burgers equation.
Note that in the equation above, the first term on the right-hand side is the diffusive term, the second is a drift that can be removed via a Galileian transformation, i.e. by looking at the system in a time dependent moving frame,
and the third, the quadratic term, comes from the strong asymmetric regime.
Such a term cannot be removed and is the reason why different limit behaviours are observed
in the symmetric, in which this term is absent, and asymmetric cases.

For a multi-component system, we follow the same procedure and,
since by the NLFH theory the universal behaviour is dictated by
the quadratic term, we neglect the higher order contributions in the expansion of
\varrho(t,u)
.
In other words, we expand the current-density relation up to second order and we get

\partial_{t}Y(t,u)=D\Delta Y(t,u)-j^{\prime}{(\varrho)}\nabla Y(t,u)+\frac{j^{\prime\prime}(\varrho)}{2}\nabla(Y(t,u))^{2}-B\nabla\xi_{u}(t).

As above, by changing variables
u\to u-j^{\prime}(\varrho)
(Galileian transformation) the drift term disappears
and therefore we derive once more a stochastic Burgers equation.

In order to obtain a much richer diagram (than the one for the simple exclusion explained above)
in which different universality classes arise and interplay non-trivially with each other,
we now turn our attention to multi-component systems, which are the main focus of the present paper.
More precisely, consider a system with
M
conserved quantities and let
\vec{\varrho}
be the vector whose
\alpha
-th entry denotes the
\alpha
-th quantity,
\alpha\in\{1,2,\cdots,M\}
.
The hydrodynamic equation is then given by a system of conservation laws:
\partial_{t}\varrho_{\alpha}(t,u)+\nabla j_{\alpha}(t,u)=0
, where
\alpha\in\{1,2,\cdots,M\}
.
Once again, we expand the density as
\varrho_{\alpha}(t,u)=\varrho_{\alpha}+Y_{\alpha}(t,u)

and express the associated current
j_{\alpha}(\vec{\varrho})
as a function of
\vec{\varrho}
.
Adding a diffusion and a noise term, in the form of an
M
-dimensional space-time white noise
\vec{\xi}
, and neglecting higher order contributions, we reach

\partial_{t}\vec{Y}=-\nabla\Big\{J\vec{Y}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{M}\vec{Y}^{T}H^{\alpha}\vec{Y}+D\nabla\vec{Y}+B\vec{\xi}\Big\},

where
J
is the jacobian of the current matrix whose entries are given by
J^{\alpha}_{\beta}=\frac{\partial j_{\alpha}}{\partial\varrho_{\beta}}
and
H^{\alpha}_{\beta,\delta}
are the Hessians of the current matrix whose entries are given by
H^{\alpha}_{\beta,\delta}=\frac{\partial^{2}j_{\alpha}}{\partial\varrho_{\beta}\partial\varrho_{\delta}}
.
At this point we transform the fluctuation fields
\vec{Y}
into normal fields
\vec{\phi}
via
\vec{\phi}=R\vec{Y}
.
The matrix
R
is chosen to diagonalise the Jacobian
J
, i.e.
RJR^{-1}={\rm diag}(v_{\alpha})
,
where
\{v_{\alpha},{\alpha\in\{1,2,\cdots,M\}}\}

are the eigenvalues of
J
. The evolution of
\vec{\phi}
is then given by

\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}(t,u)=-\nabla\Big\{v_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}+\vec{\phi}^{T}G^{\alpha}\vec{\phi}+(\tilde{D}\nabla\vec{\phi})_{\alpha}+(\tilde{B}\vec{\xi})_{\alpha}\Big\}

(1.1)

where
\tilde{D}=RDR^{-1}
,
\tilde{B}=RB
and
G^{\alpha}
are the coupling matrices given by

G^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\beta=1}^{M}R_{\alpha,\beta}(R^{-1})^{T}H^{\beta}R^{-1}.

Note that the SPDE in (
1.1
) has a drift term given by
v_{\alpha}
, which suggests that the normal field
\phi_{\alpha}
should be taken in a moving frame with velocity
v_{\alpha}
.

In order to derive the universal large-scale behaviour, the NLFH looks at the structure function of
\vec{\phi}

which is defined as

{S_{\alpha,\beta}(t,u)=\langle\phi_{\alpha}(t,u)\phi_{\beta}(0,0)\rangle_{\mu_{\varrho}}\,.}

(1.2)

Now, under the strict hyperbolicity condition which requires all velocities
v_{\alpha}
to be different,
the off-diagonal components (
\alpha\neq\beta
) of the structure function are expected to decay very fast
and thus should not contribute to the asymptotic limit.
The behaviour of the diagonal elements, i.e.
S_{\alpha}(t,u)=S_{\alpha,\alpha}(t,u)
, should instead be given
by

S_{\alpha}(t,u)\sim(C_{\alpha}t)^{-1/{z_{\alpha}}}f_{\alpha}\Big((C_{\alpha}t)^{-1/{z_{\alpha}}}(u-v_{\alpha}t)\Big)\,,

where
z_{\alpha}
is a dynamical exponent and
f_{\alpha}
is a scaling function.
To deduce the value of
z_{\alpha}
and
the form of
f_{\alpha}
, the effect of the nonlinearity and the noise must be suitably balanced and this can be done
via a memory kernel, that we now discuss. Since the normal
fields solve (
1.1
), we see that
S_{\alpha}:=S_{\alpha,\alpha}
in (
1.2
) satisfies

\partial_{t}S_{\alpha}(t,u)=-v_{\alpha}\partial_{x}S_{\alpha}(t,u)+\tilde{D}_{\alpha,\alpha}\partial_{x}^{2}S_{\alpha}(t,u)+\int_{0}^{t}\int RdvS_{\alpha}(t-s,u-v)\partial_{x}^{2}M_{\alpha,\alpha}(s,v)

where
M_{\alpha,\alpha}
is the memory kernel and is given by

M_{\alpha,\alpha}(s,v)=2\sum_{\beta,\delta}(G_{\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha})^{2}S_{\beta}(s,v)S_{\delta}(s,v)\,.

(1.3)

Once again, under the strict hyperbolicity condition, the off-diagonal terms should not contribute
so that the dynamical exponent
z_{\alpha}
and the scaling function
f_{\alpha}
, and consequently the
corresponding universality class,
should be determined by the diagonal ones. Neglecting the off-diagonals, the
memory kernel reduces to

M_{\alpha,\alpha}(s,v)=2\sum_{\beta}(G_{\beta,\gamma}^{\alpha})^{2}(S_{\beta}({s,v}))^{2}
.

Let us now describe the possible limits. To this end, we introduce the set
\mdmathbb I_{\alpha}
of those indices such that

G^{\alpha}_{\beta,\beta}
in (
1.3
) is not zero, i.e.

\mdmathbb I_{\alpha}:=\{\beta:G^{\alpha}_{\beta,\beta}\neq 0\}
.
For two component systems (which is the case for the model studied in this article) one can get

•

diffusive behaviour if
\mdmathbb I_{\alpha}=\emptyset
, i.e. the EW universality class for the field
\alpha
corresponding to
z_{\alpha}=2
and
f_{\alpha}
the usual heat kernel,

•

KPZ behaviour if
\alpha\in\mdmathbb I_{\alpha}
, corresponding to
z_{\alpha}=3/2
,

which are the same obtained for systems with only one conservation law (see the discussion above for SEP).
But now if for the normal field
\alpha
the self-coupling term vanishes i.e.
G_{\alpha,\alpha}^{\alpha}=0
, but for
\beta
we have
\beta\in I_{\alpha}
, i.e.
G^{\alpha}_{\beta,\beta}\neq 0
, then the dynamical exponent is equal to
z_{\alpha}=\min_{\beta\in I_{\alpha}}\{1+\frac{1}{z_{\beta}}\}
and the scaling function is a
z_{\alpha}
-stable distribution given in Fourier space by

\hat{S}(t,k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big\{-iv_{\alpha}tk-C_{\alpha}t|k|^{z_{\alpha}}\times(1-iA_{\alpha}\sgn(k)\tan(\tfrac{\pi z_{\alpha}}{2}))\Big\},

where
\hat{S}
denotes the Fourier transform given by
\hat{S}(t,k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int RS(t,u)e^{-iku}du
,
C_{\alpha}
is a constant and
A_{\alpha}\in[-1,1]
an asymmetry.
Now, since
z_{\alpha}
satisfies
z_{\alpha}=\min_{\beta\in I_{\alpha}}\{1+\frac{1}{z_{\beta}}\}
,
we necessarily have that for all
n
,
z_{\alpha}=F_{n+3}/F_{n+2}

where
F_{n}
is the Fibonacci number defined by the recursion
F_{n+2}=F_{n+1}+F_{n}
and
F_{1}=F_{2}=1
.
As a consequence, if in the system there is not a normal field with dynamical exponent
z_{\alpha}=2
or
3/2
,
then the only possibility is to have
z_{\alpha}=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
, the Golden number, for all the fields. The reason why only this type of
z_{\alpha}
-Lévy stable distributions appear with
z_{\alpha}
given as above, is still mysterious.
The predictions for systems with two conservation laws was first derived in
[
42
]
,
while for
n
conservation laws in
[
32
]
.

Let us stress that the assumption that the current-density relation is strictly-hyperbolic (i.e. the velocities of the normal fields are all different) is crucial because it
formally
allows to neglect the crossed terms

S_{\alpha}(s,v)S_{\beta}(s,v)
,
\beta\neq\alpha
,
and focus only on the diagonals.
One of the goals of this article is to give for the first time a rigorous mathematical proof of
the fact that non-diagonal terms of coupling matrices are indeed negligible.
To do so, we consider a multi-component model with two conservation laws that we now describe.

1.4
The particle exchange model

We study a generalization of the simple exclusion process by allowing three types of particles, that we name
A,B
and
C
. In the
ABC
model each site of the one-dimensional torus
\mdmathbb{T}_{N}=\mdmathbb{Z}/N\mdmathbb{Z}
is occupied by one and only one particle, which can be of type
A
,
B
or
C
. In the
classical

ABC
model, introduced by Evans et. al in
[
15
,
16
]
, particles exchange their positions with nearest neighbours particles with the asymmetric rates:
AB\to BA
,
BC\to CB
,
CA\to AC
with rate
q=q(N)<1
and
BA\to AB
,
CB\to BC
,
AC\to CA
with rate
1
. For this dynamics on the torus the invariant measure is explicitly known only in the case that the number of particles of each species are equal, in which case it is given by a Gibbs measure of a certain Hamiltonian having long range pair interactions. In this context, in the weakly asymmetric regime
q=1-O\left(\frac{\beta}{N}\right)
introduced by Clincy et al. in
[
13
]
, we recently obtained in
[
21
]
the system of hydrodynamic equations (with boundary conditions) that describes the evolution of the density field of each species for the dynamics in a open interval connected with reservoirs (in which case the invariant measure is not explicitly know).

In the present work we consider an
ABC
model with different rates. The
ABC
model is a particular case of the general
n
-component particle exchange model that is presented in
[
38
]
and, as argued therein, can also be seen as a fluctuating directed polymer in
d\geq 2
.
We now assume that the interaction rates depend on three constants
E_{A},E_{B}
and
E_{C}
: for
(\alpha,\beta)\in\{A,B,C\}
the transposition
(\alpha,\beta)\to(\beta,\alpha)
occurs at rate
1+\frac{E_{\alpha}-E_{\beta}}{2N}
, as it is summarised in Figure
1
below.

1+\tfrac{E_{B}-E_{A}}{2N}
1+\tfrac{E_{C}-E_{A}}{2N}

It turns out, as discussed in
[
37
]
, that for this model, in each irreducible class, the invariant measure is uniform over all possible configurations (see Lemma
2.1
). As discussed in
[
9
]
the hydrodynamic limit for the density of particles
A
and
B
, in the diffusive time scaling
a=2
and for
\gamma=1
, is given by the system of equations (
2.6
). In the present work we are interested in the fluctuations around the hydrodynamic limit.

There are three important cases to distinguish depending on the choice of rates,
apart from the case
E_{A}=E_{B}=E_{C}
, which is trivial as the dynamics does not distinguish particles of type
A
,
B
or
C
for which the limit of the fluctuations is Ornstein–Uhlenbeck.
The case
(I)
is when
E_{A}=E_{B}
so that
A
and
B
are exchanged at rate
1
.
The case
(II)
is when
E_{B}=E_{C}

and particles of type
B
and
C
are exchanged at rate
1
.
Let us already mention here that the fact that two types of particles exchange at rate
1
suggests that one normal field
should have diffusive behaviour. And, in case
(II)
for example, if we look at the particles of type
A
, since the dynamics does not distinguish
between
B
and
C
, we can conclude that the field of particles of type
A
behaves as in WASEP. The case
(III)
is the most general and all exchange rates are weakly asymmetric,
with an intensity regulated by the parameter
\gamma
.

We note that the dynamics above conserves two quantities: the total number of particles of type
A
and the total number of particles of type
B
. Nevertheless any linear combination of these two quantities is again conserved. The invariant measures of this model are explicit: for any constant densities
\rho_{A}
,
\rho_{B}
and
\rho_{C}=1-\rho_{A}-\rho_{B}
, the product measure
\nu_{\rho}
given on
x\in\mdmathbb T_{N}
and
\alpha\in\{A,B,C\}
by

\nu_{\rho}(\eta:\eta(x)=\alpha)=\rho_{\alpha}
,
is an invariant measure. Since the dynamics of each type of particle depends on the other,
the evolution equations are not closed and it is a priori unclear how to derive the limiting equations.
Nevertheless, according to NLFH, the normal fields can be identified and predictions can be made
(see Appendix
A
, in which this is done in detail).

Alternatively, to identify the normal fields we can (and will) proceed as follows.
First, we analyse the action of the infinitesimal generator on the occupation variables for particles of types
A
and
B
,
derive their instantaneous current and centre all variables.
This then will allow us to evaluate the generator on a generic field given by a linear combination of
the centred occupation variables for
A
and
B
.
The expression we obtain will display drift terms that blow up in the limit and we force these
to be zero by passing the generic field to a time moving frame.
At this point, the derivation of the correct normal fields boils downs to solving a system of two equations
with two unknowns: the velocity of the moving frame and the constant defining the linear combination of fields (in principle
the constants are two, but, by linearity, one can always be fixed to be

This procedure is carried out in Section

normal fields as those predicted by the NLFH theory.

Once blowing up terms are removed from the evolution equations, we are left with higher order terms which,
in this specific model, are quadratic and can be written as products of the occupation variables for
particles of type
A
or
B

The coefficients in front of these quadratic terms are nothing but the entries the coupling matrices.

For the multi-species WASEP we consider, the special structure of these coupling matrices implies that

1.5
Our contribution

The main contribution of this article is twofold. First, we rigorously determine the large-scale behaviour
of the non-diagonal terms of the coupling matrices.
In our context, this means that, the quadratic terms due to crossed products of normal modes associated to
particles of type
A
and
B
,
only interact via their initial total mass
and
do not produce any additional non-linear
term in the limit
. The result is shown in wide generality as its assumptions only
impose mild conditions on the moments of the fields, so that
in particular it holds irrespectively of the specific dynamics one considers
and even allows for products of more than two fields,
but we are restricted to the diffusive time scale.
Let us stress that, while on the one hand this puts on firm ground the assumption in NLFH
that off-diagonal terms are negligible (at least in diffusive time scale), on the other we
do observe
a contribution
which is though trivial as it only involves their initial total masses (which are conserved quantities of the system).
This is something which was not predicted by mode coupling because it only looks at the fluctuations of the normal modes
at a fixed time.

Second, we show that, in the two component model we consider, if the asymmetry is weak,
\gamma>1/2
,
then the limit of both fields is again diffusive, so the system falls in the EW universality class.
More interestingly, we carry out a full analysis of the case
\gamma=1/2
, in which both the EW and the KPZ
universality classes arise.
To be precise, we prove that any normal field whose coupling matrices has only non-zero non-diagonal entries,
has again diffusive behaviour. From this result we then show that in cases
(I)
and
(II)

one mode is diffusive while the other is KPZ.
For the case
(III)
, we show instead that both fields have indeed KPZ behaviour
(since we are restricted to the choice
\gamma=1/2
, when we say KPZ behaviour,
we mean that the fluctuations are given in terms of the SB equation).
In other words, we rigorously prove that, for
\gamma=1/2
,
all the predicted results summarised in Figure
2
hold true
.

Let us stress that since the development of NLFH theory very little advances have been made for multi-component systems (for results on the Lévy limits with parameters
3/2
or
5/3
see
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
10
,
18
,
26
,
36
]
).
For some models (as, for example, chain of oscillators with the Fermi–Pasta–Ulam potential),
even the understanding of the correct linear combinations of fields has not been successfully addressed
(apart from numerical simulations).
Up to our knowledge, prior to this work, only the case of diffusive behaviour, Lévy-
3/2
and Lévy-
5/3

have been derived rigorously. This is the first time in which diffusion/KPZ, as well as KPZ for both modes has
been obtained.
In
[
2
]
systems of coupled KPZ equations appear but the strict hyperbolicity condition was lacking,
so that, contrary to our case, the resulting equations are coupled.

Let us now comment on the technical aspects of our work.
To obtain the KPZ behaviour we extend the second order Boltzmann–Gibbs principle derived in
[
19
,
20
]

to the setting of multi-component systems. The Boltzmann–Gibbs principle allows
to replace products of occupation variables with uniform averages in big microscopic boxes,
which then enables us to close the equations for each of the normal fields and
ultimately identify the limit as energy solutions of the SB equation.
In order to prove that the contribution from crossed terms is negligible,
we need a further refinement of the aforementioned principle, according to which,
instead of taking uniform averages,
we suitably distribute the mass in such a way that, in the macro-limit, this can be well approximated by a smooth function.
Once this is in place, the control over the crossed term boils down to rewrite it in Fourier and
apply a version of Riemann–Lebesgue lemma for stochastic processes which satisfy suitable moment bounds.
Here, the strict hyperbolicity condition is essential, as if it fails the crossed terms might survive in the limit
(see e.g. the zero-range model studied in
[
2
]
where this indeed happens).

To conclude this introduction, we mention future work and possible extensions of our result.
The first important open problem is to go beyond the diffusive time scale and to show that in fact the results presented in the table above can be obtained in the whole range of the parameter
\gamma>0
. Moreover, it would also be interesting to push forward our results when the system is evolving in the infinite lattice or in the open interval but in the presence of a Glauber dynamics at the boundary.

Another possible direction is to consider particle exchange models in which more than one particle per site is allowed.
We believe that in this case the scenario could be richer and possibly other universal behaviours might appear.
At last, it would be very interesting to apply our results to Hamiltonian models
as the chain of oscillators and that in
[
8
]
.
Our contributions might provide useful insights which could help to derive the normal fields
and determine their large-scale behaviour.

Remark 2.10

does not see the diverging velocity. To witness, let

standard periodic SSEP in diffusive time scale and evolving on

be its density fluctuation field.
Let

Notice that for any

we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\mathcalboondox Y}^{N}_{s}(T_{N^{\alpha}s}f)\mathrm{d}s

In particular, for every

and the latter converges in law to a

In other words, even though we look at the process in the wrong time-frame the integral

With this definition at hand, we have

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,\alpha}_{s},\overleftarrow{\rho_{\ell}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle=\frac{1}{{N}\ell}\sum_{y}\overleftarrow{\rho}\Big(\frac{y-x}{N}\Big)\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{y}(s),\quad\textrm{and}\quad\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,\alpha}_{s},\overrightarrow{\rho_{\ell}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle=\frac{1}{{N}\ell}\sum_{y}\overrightarrow{\rho}\Big(\frac{y-x}{N}\Big)\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{y}(s)

Therefore, modulo negligible terms,

\displaystyle{\mathcalboondox B}^{N,+}_{t}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{E}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}T_{\frac{E}{3}N^{3/2}s}f\left(\tfrac{x}{N}\right)\times

\displaystyle\times\Big\{\Big(\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,A}_{s},\overleftarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle+\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,B}_{s},\overleftarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle\Big)\Big(\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,A}_{s},\overrightarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle-\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,B}_{s},\overrightarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle+\Big(\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,A}_{s},\overleftarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle-\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,B}_{s},\overleftarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle\Big)\Big(\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,A}_{s},\overrightarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle+\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,B}_{s},\overrightarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle\Big)\Big\}\,.

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}T_{\frac{E}{3}N^{3/2}s}f\left(\tfrac{x}{N}\right)\Big(\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,A}_{s},\overleftarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle+\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,B}_{s},\overleftarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle\Big)\Big(\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,A}_{s},\overrightarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle-\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,B}_{s},\overrightarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle\times\bigg(\left\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,A}_{s},\overrightarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\left(\cdot-\tfrac{x+\frac{E}{3}N^{3/2}s}{N}\right)\right\rangle-\left\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,B}_{s},\overrightarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\left(\cdot-\tfrac{x+\frac{E}{3}N^{3/2}s}{N}\right)\right\rangle\bigg)

where the last passage follows arguing as in (

defined with opposite velocities.
Applying the same strategy for the second summand, we get

\displaystyle{\mathcalboondox B}^{N,+}_{t}(f)=\frac{E}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}f\left(\tfrac{x}{N}\right)\Big\{

\displaystyle\mathcalboondox{{\displaystyle Z}}^{N,-}_{s}\left(\overleftarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\left(\tfrac{x+\frac{2}{3}EN^{3/2}s}{N}\right)\right){{\mathcalboondox Z}}^{N,+}_{s}(\overrightarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\tfrac{x}{N}))

\Big(D_{1}\bar{\xi}^{A}_{x+1}(s)+D_{2}\bar{\xi}^{B}_{x+1}(s)\Big)-\Big(D_{1}\bar{\xi}^{A}_{x+1}(s^{-})+D_{2}\bar{\xi}^{B}_{x+1}(s^{-})\Big)=D_{1}-D_{2}\,.

From this we get

\Big|{\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{N,\pm}(f)-{\mathcalboondox Z}_{s^{-}}^{N,\pm}(f)\Big|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big(f(\tfrac{x}{N})-f(\tfrac{x+1}{N})\Big)(D_{2}-D_{1})\leq\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}

and, since the right-hand side does not depend on
s
, point 2 trivially follows.
For point 3, we first state a crucial lemma whose proof is provided at the end of the section.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
f\in{\mathcalboondox D}(\mdmathbb{T})
, it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mdmathbb E_{\nu_{\rho}}\left[\left(\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{N}(f)\rangle_{t}-\mdmathbb E_{\nu_{\rho}}[\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{N}(f)\rangle_{t}]\right)^{2}\right]=0.

The previous statement, together with Lemma
3.1
completes the verification of the
assumptions of Theorem
4.2
. Hence, the sequence
\{{{\mathcalboondox M}}^{N}(f)\}_{N}
is tight and converges in
law to the Gaussian process whose quadratic variation is given by the right hand side of (
3.4
).

It remains to prove the validity of (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) for
\{{{\mathcalboondox M}}^{N}(f)\}_{N}
.
For the first, by Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality, (
3.4
) and (
4.16
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}_{\nu_{\rho}}\left[\sup_{t\leq T}\Big({{\mathcalboondox M}}^{N}_{t}(f)\Big)^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}_{\nu_{\rho}}\left[\langle{{\mathcalboondox M}}^{N}(f)\rangle_{T}\right]+\mathbbm{E}_{\nu_{\rho}}\left[\sup_{t\leq T}\Big|{{\mathcalboondox M}}^{N}_{t}(f)-{{\mathcalboondox M}}^{N}_{t^{-}}(f)\Big|^{2}\right]

(4.17)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle T\|\nabla f\|^{2}_{2}+N^{-3}\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}^{2}\,.

For the latter instead, recalling the expression of the quadratic variation of the martingale given in (
3.3
),
we see that for any
0\leq s<t\leq T
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}_{\nu_{\rho}}\left[({{\mathcalboondox M}}^{N}_{t}(f)-{{\mathcalboondox M}}^{N}_{s}(f))^{2}\right]

(4.18)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}_{\nu_{\rho}}\Bigg[\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{d}r\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x}c_{x}(\eta)(\nabla_{N}T_{vN^{2}r}f(\tfrac{x}{N}))^{2}\big[(D_{1}\xi^{A}_{x+1}(r)+D_{2}\xi^{B}_{x+1}(r))-(D_{1}\xi^{A}_{x}(r)+D_{2}\xi^{B}_{x}(r))\big]^{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\lesssim(t-s)\|\nabla f\|^{2}_{2}\,.

To summarise, each of the terms
{\mathcalboondox I}^{N}(f)
,
{\mathcalboondox B}^{N}(f)
,
{\mathcalboondox R}^{N}(f)
and
{\mathcalboondox M}^{N}(f)
is tight,
which implies tightness for
{\mathcalboondox Z}^{N}(f)
. Moreover, (
4.7
), (
4.18
), and (
4.14
)
give (
4.2
), while (
4.1
) follows by (
4.17
) and the uniform (in
N
) continuity
of
{\mathcalboondox I}^{N}(f)
,
{\mathcalboondox B}^{N}(f)
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

Note that
the expectation in the statement of the lemma is equal to

\displaystyle\mdmathbb E_{\nu_{\rho}}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x}(\nabla_{N}T_{vN^{2}s}f(\tfrac{x}{N}))^{2}\Big[c_{x}(\eta)\Big\{\Big(D_{1}\xi^{A}_{x+1}+D_{2}\xi^{B}_{x+1})

\displaystyle-(D_{1}\xi^{A}_{x}+D_{2}\xi^{B}_{x})\Big\}^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{9}(D_{1}^{2}+D_{2}^{2}-D_{1}D_{2})\Big]\bigg)^{2}\bigg].

From the computations in the proof of Lemma
3.1
we know that

\displaystyle c_{x}(\eta)\Big\{\Big(D_{1}\xi^{A}_{x+1}+D_{2}\xi^{B}_{x+1})-(D_{1}\xi^{A}_{x}+

\displaystyle D_{2}\xi^{B}_{x})\Big\}^{2}-\frac{4}{9}(D_{1}^{2}+D_{2}^{2}-D_{1}D_{2})

(4.19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{1}^{2}\Big\{\sum_{\alpha}c^{\alpha,A}\xi^{\alpha}_{x}\xi^{A}_{x+1}+\sum_{\beta}c^{A,\beta}\xi^{A}_{x}\xi^{\beta}_{x+1}-\frac{4}{9}\Big\}

\displaystyle+D_{2}^{2}\Big\{\sum_{\alpha}c^{\alpha,B}\xi^{\alpha}_{x}\xi^{B}_{x+1}+\sum_{\beta}c^{B,\beta}\xi^{B}_{x}\xi^{\beta}_{x+1}-\frac{4}{9}\Big\}

\displaystyle-2D_{1}D_{2}\Big\{c^{B,A}\xi^{B}_{x}\xi^{A}_{x+1}+c^{A,B}\xi_{x}^{A}\xi_{x+1}^{B}-\frac{2}{9}\Big\}.

The proof of the lemma is complete once we show that the second moment of the time integral of each
of the previous summands goes to
0
as
N\to\infty
. As they can all be analysed similarly, we
limit ourselves to treat the first.
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mdmathbb\displaystyle E_{\nu_{\rho}}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x}(\nabla_{N}T_{vN^{2}s}f(\tfrac{x}{N}))^{2}\Big\{\sum_{\alpha}c^{\alpha,A}\xi^{\alpha}_{x}\xi^{A}_{x+1}+\sum_{\beta}c^{A,\beta}\xi^{A}_{x}\xi^{\beta}_{x+1}-\frac{4}{9}\Big\}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

(4.20)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t^{2}}{N^{2}}\sum_{x,y}\nabla_{N}f(\tfrac{x}{N})^{2}\nabla_{N}f(\tfrac{y}{N})^{2}\times

\displaystyle\times\mdmathbb E_{\nu_{\rho}}\bigg[\Big\{\sum_{\alpha}c^{\alpha,A}\xi^{\alpha}_{x}\xi^{A}_{x+1}+\sum_{\beta}c^{A,\beta}\xi^{A}_{x}\xi^{\beta}_{x+1}-\frac{4}{9}\Big\}\Big\{\sum_{\alpha}c^{\alpha,A}\xi^{\alpha}_{y}\xi^{A}_{y+1}+\sum_{\beta}c^{A,\beta}\xi^{A}_{y}\xi^{\beta}_{y+1}-\frac{4}{9}\Big\}\bigg]\,.

Note now that for
|x-y|>1
the variables
\xi^{\alpha}_{x}\xi^{\beta}_{x+1}
and

\xi^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{y}\xi^{\beta^{\prime}}_{y+1}
are independent for any value
of
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\{A,B\}
. Since they are also centred, the expectation
at the right hand side is
0
.
When instead
x\in\{y,y+1\}
the expectation can be easily seen to be bounded
so that overall (
4.20
) is of order
N^{-1}
and therefore vanishes in the limit.
∎

4.2
Characterisation of the limit points

From the results in the previous section,
we know that the sequences
\{{\mathcalboondox Z}^{N,\pm}\}_{N\in\mdmathbb{N}}
,
\{{\mathcalboondox I}^{N,\pm}\}_{N\in\mdmathbb{N}}
,

\{{\mathcalboondox B}^{N,\pm}\}_{N\in\mdmathbb{N}}
,
\{{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,\pm}\}_{N\in\mdmathbb{N}}
and

\{{\mathcalboondox R}^{N,\pm}\}_{N\in\mdmathbb{N}}
, the latter vanishing,
are tight with respect to the uniform topology on
D([0,T];{\mathcalboondox D}^{\prime}(\mdmathbb{T}))
.
Prohorov’s theorem ensures the existence of a converging subsequence that, abusing notation, we will still denote by
N
.
Let
{\mathcalboondox Z}^{\pm}
,
{\mathcalboondox I}^{\pm}
,
{\mathcalboondox B}^{\pm}
and
{\mathcalboondox M}^{\pm}
be
their almost surely continuous limits.

We are left to show that
{\mathcalboondox Z}^{\pm}
are either solutions to the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck equation or
stationary energy solutions of the stochastic Burgers equation in the sense of Definitions
2.6
and
2.5
. Once this is proven, since any of these solutions is unique by Theorem
2.7
,
the convergence of the whole sequence follows.

We begin with item (i) in Definition
2.5
.
Note that, by computing the characteristic function at any time
t
of the field
{\mathcalboondox Z}_{t}^{N,\pm}

we obtain that
{\mathcalboondox Z}_{t}^{N,\pm}
converges in distribution to a spatial white noise of variance

\textrm{Var}(D_{1}\xi_{x}^{A}+D_{2}\xi_{x}^{B})=\frac{2}{9}(D_{1}^{2}+D_{2}^{2}-D_{1}D_{2})\,,

(4.21)

where the previous equality can be verified by arguing as in the proof of Lemma
3.1
.
Above for a random variable
X
, we denoted by
\textrm{Var}(X)
its variance with respect to
\nu_{\rho}
.

Now, we turn to items (ii)-(iii).
The initial field
\{{\mathcalboondox Z}^{N,\pm}_{0}\}_{N\in\mdmathbb N}
can be treated as in the previous point.
The martingale terms
\{{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,\pm}\}_{N\in\mdmathbb{N}}
were thoroughly analysed in the
previous section, where it was shown that
{\mathcalboondox M}^{\pm}
is a mean-zero Gaussian process
with quadratic variation given by the right-hand side of (
3.4
).
For the linear term
{\mathcalboondox I}^{\pm}
, recall that

{\mathcalboondox I}_{t}^{N,\pm}(f)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s{\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{N,\pm}(\Delta_{N}f)\,.

Since
\{{\mathcalboondox Z}^{N,\pm}\}_{N}
converges in the space
C([0,T];{\mathcalboondox D}^{\prime}(\mdmathbb{T}))
, the limit
must be such that for any
f\in{\mathcalboondox D}(\mdmathbb T)

{\mathcalboondox I}_{t}^{\pm}(f)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s{\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{\pm}(\Delta f)\,.

At this point, it remains to study the quadratic term
{\mathcalboondox B}^{\pm}
, which is the most delicate.
Recall (
3.17
).

In all the cases that we distinguished in Section
3
,
from the second-order Boltzmann-Gibbs principles, Theorems
6.2
and
6.4
,
(
3.17
) vanishes in
L^{2}(\mdmathbb P_{\nu_{\rho}})
if
\gamma>1/2
,
while for
\gamma=1/2
it can be written as the sum of terms that can be either of the form

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}f\left(\tfrac{x}{N}\right){\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{N,\pm}({\iota^{1}_{\varepsilon}}(\tfrac{x}{N})){\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{N,\pm}({\iota^{2}_{\varepsilon}}(\tfrac{x}{N}))

(4.22)

where
\lambda_{\pm}
is a constant and
\iota^{i}_{\varepsilon}\in\{\overleftarrow{\iota_{\varepsilon}},\overrightarrow{\iota_{\varepsilon}}\}
,
i=1,2
,
or

\mathfrak{A}^{\pm}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}f\left(\tfrac{x}{N}\right){\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{N,\pm}({\rho^{1}_{\varepsilon}}(\tfrac{x}{N})){\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{N,\mp}\Big({\rho^{2}_{\varepsilon}}\left(\tfrac{x+2v_{\pm}N^{2}s}{N}\right)\Big)

(4.23)

for some constants
\mathfrak{A}^{\pm}
, where

\rho^{i}_{\varepsilon}\in\{\overleftarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}},\overrightarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\}
,
i=1,2
.
To control this latter integral we would like to apply Theorem
5.1
but,
while condition
2
clearly holds with
\alpha=1/2
as can be seen
by a simple application of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality together with (
4.1
) and (
4.2
),
condition
1
. is violated for the points
k_{1}=k\in\mdmathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
k_{2}=0
.
Indeed, for these values of
k_{1},k_{2}
we have
k_{1}+k_{2}=k\neq 0
,

\widehat{\rho^{1}_{\varepsilon}}(k_{1})\widehat{\rho^{2}_{\varepsilon}}(0)\neq 0
but

v_{1}^{N}k_{1}+v_{2}^{N}k_{2}=0\times k+2v_{\pm}N^{2}\times 0=0
. This is the place at which
the linear transport term in (
2.20
) comes about.
To see this, note first that, by definition,
\widehat{\rho^{2}_{\varepsilon}}(0)=\int\rho^{2}_{\varepsilon}(u)\mathrm{d}u=1
and

{\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{N,\mp}(1)={\mathcalboondox Z}_{0}^{N,\mp}(1)=\overline{{\mathcalboondox Z}}^{N,\mp}

(4.24)

where we used that the total mass is conserved and denoted the time-independent quantity
\overline{{\mathcalboondox Z}}^{N,\mp}
.
Hence, we rewrite (
4.23
) as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}^{\pm}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}

\displaystyle f\left(\tfrac{x}{N}\right){\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{N,\pm}({\rho^{1}_{\varepsilon}}(\tfrac{x}{N})){\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{N,\mp}\Big({\rho^{2}_{\varepsilon}}\left(\tfrac{x+2v_{\pm}N^{2}s}{N}\right)-1\Big)

(4.25)

\displaystyle+\mathfrak{A}^{\pm}\overline{{\mathcalboondox Z}}^{N,\mp}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}f\left(\tfrac{x}{N}\right){\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{N,\pm}({\rho^{1}_{\varepsilon}}(\tfrac{x}{N}))\,.

We can now apply Theorem
5.1
which ensures that,
as
\varepsilon>0
is fixed, the first summand vanishes in the limit
N\to\infty
.
The second instead is the product of two terms which are tight (it is
not
the product of fields anymore!)
and therefore it converges, in the double limit
N\to\infty
and
\varepsilon\to 0
, to

\mathfrak{A}^{\pm}\overline{{\mathcalboondox Z}}^{\mp}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s{\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{\pm}(\nabla f)\,.

(4.26)

We turn to (
4.22
), for which, by tightness of
\{{\mathcalboondox B}^{N,\pm}\}_{N\in\mdmathbb N}
,
we get

\displaystyle{\mathcalboondox B}^{\varepsilon,\pm}_{t}(f)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}f\left(\tfrac{x}{N}\right){\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{N,\pm}({\iota^{1}_{\varepsilon}}(\tfrac{x}{N})){\mathcalboondox Z}_{s}^{N,\pm}({\iota^{2}_{\varepsilon}}(\tfrac{x}{N}))\,.

(4.27)

The process at the left hand side coincides with that in (
2.16
).
Indeed, even if neither
\overleftarrow{\iota_{\varepsilon}}
nor
\overrightarrow{\iota_{\varepsilon}}

belong to
{\mathcalboondox D}(\mdmathbb T)
, they can be approximated by elements in the latter space
(see also the footnote before (
2.16
)).
For further details, we refer the reader to
[
19
, Section 5.3]
.
Now, by computations similar to those performed in the proof of (
4.14
), it is not hard to see that
for any
0<\delta\leq\varepsilon<1
, we have

\mdmathbb{E}_{n}\bigg[\bigg|({\mathcalboondox B}^{N,\pm}_{t}(f)-{\mathcalboondox B}^{N,\pm}_{s}(f))-({\mathcalboondox B}^{\delta,\pm}_{t}(f)-{\mathcalboondox B}^{\delta,\pm}_{s}(f))\bigg|^{2}\bigg]\lesssim\bigg((t-s)\varepsilon+\frac{(t-s)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}N}\bigg)\|\nabla f\|^{2}_{2}

so that, by (
4.27
) and taking the limit in
N
, we obtain

\mdmathbb{E}\big[\big|({\mathcalboondox B}^{\varepsilon,\pm}_{t}(f)-{\mathcalboondox B}^{\varepsilon,\pm}_{s}(f))-({\mathcalboondox B}^{\delta,\pm}_{t}(f)-{\mathcalboondox B}^{\delta,\pm}_{s}(f))\big|^{2}\big]\lesssim\varepsilon(t-s)\|\nabla f\|^{2}_{2}

(4.28)

which, on the one hand guarantees that
{\mathcalboondox B}^{\varepsilon,\pm}
has unique limit in
\varepsilon\to 0
, and on the other
provides the energy estimate (
2.17
).
Hence, both (ii) and (iii) of Definition
2.5
hold.

At last, we note that all the arguments above hold
mutatis mutandis
for the reversed process

\{{\mathcalboondox Z}^{N,\pm}_{T-t}:t\in[0,T]\}
whose dynamics is generated by the adjoint operator
L_{N}^{*}
,
so that also item (iv) is satisfied.

At last, we prove that
{\mathcalboondox M}^{+}
and
{\mathcalboondox M}^{-}
are independent, which amounts to verify that
for all
f,g\in{\mathcalboondox D}(\mdmathbb T)
,
({\mathcalboondox M}^{+}_{t}(f))_{t}
and
({\mathcalboondox M}^{-}_{t}(g))_{t}
are independent.
This in turn follows once we show that the cross-variation
\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{+}(f),{\mathcalboondox M}^{-}(g)\rangle_{t}=0.

By polarization, for any
N\geq 1
, we have

2\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,+}(f),{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,-}(g)\rangle_{t}=\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,+}(f)+{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,-}(g)\rangle_{t}-\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,+}(f)\rangle_{t}-\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,-}(g)\rangle_{t}\,,

and we will prove

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\mdmathbb E_{\nu_{\rho}}\Big[\Big(\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,+}(f)+{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,-}(g)\rangle_{t}-\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,+}(f)\rangle_{t}-\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,-}(g)\rangle_{t}\Big)^{2}\Big]=0,

which completes the proof.

A simple computation similar to that in (
3.3
) shows that

\displaystyle\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,+}(f)+{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,-}(g)\rangle_{t}=

\displaystyle N^{a-3}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x}c_{x}(\eta)(\nabla_{N}T_{v_{+}N^{2}s}f(\tfrac{x}{N}))^{2}\big[\xi^{A}_{x+1}-\xi^{A}_{x}+D_{2}^{+}(\xi^{B}_{x+1}-\xi^{B}_{x})\big]^{2}\mathrm{d}s

(4.29)

\displaystyle+N^{a-3}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x}c_{x}(\eta)(\nabla_{N}T_{v_{-}N^{2}s}g(\tfrac{x}{N}))^{2}\big[\xi^{A}_{x+1}-\xi^{A}_{x}+D_{2}^{-}(\xi^{B}_{x+1}-\xi^{B}_{x})\big]^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+N^{a-3}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x}c_{x}(\eta)\nabla_{N}T_{v_{+}N^{2}s}f(\tfrac{x}{N})\nabla_{N}T_{v_{-}N^{2}s}g(\tfrac{x}{N})\times

\displaystyle\times 2\big[\xi^{A}_{x+1}-\xi^{A}_{x}+D_{2}^{+}(\xi^{B}_{x+1}-\xi^{B}_{x})\big]\big[\xi^{A}_{x+1}-\xi^{A}_{x}+D_{2}^{-}(\xi^{B}_{x+1}-\xi^{B}_{x})\big]\mathrm{d}s\,.

Now, the first two terms on the right-hand side equal
\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,+}(f)\rangle_{t}
and
\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{N,-}(g)\rangle_{t}
, respectively, so that we only need to show that the variance of the last vanishes as
N\to+\infty
.
To this end, we first note that its mean is zero since

\displaystyle\mdmathbb E_{\nu_{\rho}}\Big[\big(\xi^{A}_{x+1}-\xi^{A}_{x}+D_{2}^{+}(\xi^{B}_{x+1}-\xi^{B}_{x})\big)\big(\xi^{A}_{x+1}-\xi^{A}_{x}+D_{2}^{-}(\xi^{B}_{x+1}-\xi^{B}_{x})\big)\Big]=\frac{2}{9}(2-(D_{2}^{+}+D_{2}^{-})+2D_{2}^{+}D_{2}^{-})=0

(4.30)

where the last equality holds provided
D_{2}^{+},D_{2}^{-}
are chosen as in cases
(I-III)
.
Here, we are crucially relying on the choice of constants dictated by mode coupling theory.
Turning to the second moment, we perform computations similar to those
in the proof of Lemma
4.3
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for
a=2
, we have

\displaystyle\mdmathbb\displaystyle E_{\nu_{\rho}}\Big[\Big(N^{a-3}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x}c_{x}(\eta)\nabla_{N}T_{v_{+}N^{2}s}f(\tfrac{x}{N})\nabla_{N}T_{v_{-}N^{2}s}g(\tfrac{x}{N})\times

\displaystyle\times\big[\xi^{A}_{x+1}-\xi^{A}_{x}+D_{2}^{+}(\xi^{B}_{x+1}-\xi^{B}_{x})\big]\big[\xi^{A}_{x+1}-\xi^{A}_{x}+D_{2}^{-}(\xi^{B}_{x+1}-\xi^{B}_{x})\big]\mathrm{d}s\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t}{N^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\sum_{x,y}\nabla_{N}T_{v_{+}N^{2}s}f(\tfrac{x}{N})\nabla_{N}T_{v_{-}N^{2}s}g(\tfrac{x}{N})\nabla_{N}T_{v_{+}N^{2}s}f(\tfrac{y}{N})\nabla_{N}T_{v_{-}N^{2}s}g(\tfrac{y}{N})\times

\displaystyle\times\mdmathbb E_{\nu_{\rho}}\Big[c_{x}(\eta)c_{y}(\eta)\big(\xi^{A}_{x+1}-\xi^{A}_{x}+D_{2}^{+}(\xi^{B}_{x+1}-\xi^{B}_{x})\big)\big(\xi^{A}_{x+1}-\xi^{A}_{x}+D_{2}^{-}(\xi^{B}_{x+1}-\xi^{B}_{x})\big)\times

\displaystyle\times\big(\xi^{A}_{y+1}-\xi^{A}_{y}+D_{2}^{+}(\xi^{B}_{y+1}-\xi^{B}_{y})\big)\big(\xi^{A}_{y+1}-\xi^{A}_{y}+D_{2}^{-}(\xi^{B}_{y+1}-\xi^{B}_{y})\big)\Big]

and, arguing as in (
4.19
)-(
4.20
), since the variables are independent and centred,
the terms in the last sum that survive are only those for which
|x-y|\leq 1
. Therefore,
the last term vanishes as
N\to+\infty
.

Finally we prove that for every
t\in[0,T]
the processes
{\mathcalboondox Z}^{+}_{t}
and
{\mathcalboondox Z}^{-}_{t}
are uncorrelated. To this end we note that for any
N
and for any
f,g\in{\mathcalboondox D}(\mdmathbb T)
, we have that
{\mathcalboondox Z}^{N,+}_{t}(f)
and
{\mathcalboondox Z}^{N,-}_{t}(g)
are uncorrelated i.e.

\mdmathbb E_{\nu_{\rho}}[{\mathcalboondox Z}^{N,+}_{t}(f){\mathcalboondox Z}^{N,-}_{t}(g)]=0,

(4.31)

and this is a consequence of the fact that for any
x,y\in\mdmathbb T_{N}

\mdmathbb E_{\nu_{\rho}}[(\bar{\xi}^{A}_{x}+D_{2}^{+}\bar{\xi}^{B}_{x})(\bar{\xi}^{A}_{y}+D_{2}^{-}\bar{\xi}^{B}_{y})]=0

(4.32)

for any choice of
D_{2}^{\pm}
in all the cases
(I-III)
. This is an easy computation that is similar to the one in (
4.30
). Again we note that in the last result it is crucial to use the choice of the constants fixing the fields that we obtained from mode coupling theory.
With this the proof of Theorem
2.9
is completed.

5
Crossed fields

The goal of this section is to show that those integrals displaying the product of fluctuation fields evolving on
different time frames, vanish in the large
N
limit. We state the main result in wider generality as we believe
its scope goes beyond the model treated in the present work, so that the theorem below might be of independent interest.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
T>0
,
n\in\mdmathbb{N}
and, for every
N\in\mdmathbb{N}
, let
\{{\mathcalboondox X}^{i,N}\colon i=1,\dots,n\}

be
n
time-dependent random fields taking values in
D([0,T];{\mathcalboondox D}^{\prime}(\mdmathbb{T}))
and defined on the same probability space.
Let
\{v_{i}^{N}\}_{N}
,
i=1,\dots,n
be
n
sequences of constants
and
\varphi_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, be smooth functions on
\mdmathbb{T}
.

We make the following assumptions

1.

the constants
\{v_{i}^{N}\}_{N}
and the functions
\varphi_{i}
are such that if for
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\in\mdmathbb{Z}
with

\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\neq 0
,
\Pi_{i}\hat{\varphi}_{i}(k_{i})\neq 0
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}v_{i}^{N}\right|=\infty\,,

(5.1)

2.

there exist
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and a constant
C>0
such that
for any
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in{\mathcalboondox D}(\mdmathbb{T})
, the following bounds hold uniformly in
N

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\bigg[\sup_{s\leq T}|{\mathcalboondox P}^{N}_{s}|\bigg]\leq C\prod_{i=1}^{n}\|\Delta f_{i}\|_{2}\vee\|f_{i}\|_{\infty}\,,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\Big[|{\mathcalboondox P}^{N}_{t}-{\mathcalboondox P}^{N}_{s}|\Big]\leq C(t-s)^{\alpha}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\|\Delta f_{i}\|_{2}\,,\qquad\text{for all $0\leq s\leq t\leq T$,}

(5.3)

where, for
s\in[0,T]
, we denoted by
{\mathcalboondox P}^{N}_{s}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcalboondox X}^{i,N}_{s}(f_{i})
.

Then, for any
t\in[0,T]
and
f\in{\mathcalboondox D}(\mdmathbb{T})
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\frac{1}{N}\sum_{z\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}f\left(\tfrac{z}{N}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcalboondox X}^{i,N}_{s}\left(\varphi_{i}\left(\tfrac{z+v_{i}^{N}s}{N}\right)\right)\right|\right]=0\,.

(5.4)

The proof of the previous result relies on a version of the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma which is stated in Appendix
B
.
To be able to use it, we need to introduce some notation.
Let
\phi
be a real-valued function on
\mdmathbb{T}
. We define the
discrete Fourier transform
of
\phi
as

{\mathcalboondox F}^{N}\phi(k):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}\phi(\tfrac{x}{N})e_{-k}(\tfrac{x}{N})\,,\qquad\text{for $k\in\mdmathbb{N}$, }

(5.5)

where
e_{k}(x):=e^{2\pi\iota kx}
, for
x\in\mdmathbb{T}
, and
\iota:=\sqrt{-1}
, the complex unit.
The usual properties of the Fourier transform hold in the discrete context as well. For the reader’s convenience,
we recall them here without proof. The following inversion holds

\phi(\tfrac{x}{N}):=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\mathcalboondox F}^{N}\phi(k)e_{k}(\tfrac{x}{N})\,,\qquad\text{for $x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}$, }

(5.6)

For any
a\in\mdmathbb{R}
the discrete Fourier transform of
\phi(a)(\cdot):=\phi(\cdot-a)
is given by

{\mathcalboondox F}^{N}[\phi(a)](k)={\mathcalboondox F}^{N}\phi(k)e_{-k}\left(\frac{\lfloor aN\rfloor}{N}\right)\,,\qquad\text{for all $k\in\mdmathbb{N}$,}

(5.7)

where
\lfloor a\rfloor
denotes the integer part of
a
.
Plancherel’s identity holds, i.e.

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}\phi(\tfrac{x}{N})\psi(\tfrac{x}{N})=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\mathcalboondox F}^{N}\phi(k){\mathcalboondox F}^{N}\psi(-k)\,.

(5.8)

If
\phi\in L^{2}(\mdmathbb{T})
, then for every
k\in\mdmathbb{N}

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathcalboondox F}^{N}\phi(k)=\hat{\phi}(k):=\int{T}\phi(x)e_{-k}(x)\mathrm{d}x\,.

(5.9)

We are now ready to prove the result.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

At first, we want to write the integrand in (
5.4
), in terms of its discrete Fourier transform.
To do so, notice that, for
i=1,\dots,n
and any
w\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}
, applying first (
5.6
)
and then (
5.7
)
to
\varphi_{i}(\tfrac{w}{N})(\cdot)
, we get

{{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}_{s}\Big(\varphi_{i}(\tfrac{w}{N})\Big)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}_{s}(e_{-k}){\mathcalboondox F}^{N}[\varphi_{i}(\tfrac{w}{N})](-k)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}_{s}(e_{-k}){\mathcalboondox F}^{N}\varphi_{i}^{\varepsilon}(-k)e_{k}(\tfrac{w}{N})\,.

Hence, using the above and (
5.8
),
we see that the integrand in (
5.4
) satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{z\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}

\displaystyle\nabla_{N}f\left(\tfrac{z}{N}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcalboondox X}^{i,N}_{s}\left(\varphi_{i}\left(\tfrac{z+v_{i}^{N}s}{N}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{n}=0}^{N-1}{\mathcalboondox F}^{N}(\nabla_{N}f)(-k_{[1:n]})\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcalboondox F}^{N}\varphi_{i}(-k_{i}){{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}_{s}(e_{-k_{i}})\bigg)e^{\iota 2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\frac{\lfloor v_{i}^{N}s\rfloor}{N}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{n}=0}^{N-1}F^{N}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},k_{[1:n]})\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n}{{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}_{s}(e_{-k_{i}})\bigg)e^{\iota 2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\frac{\lfloor v_{i}^{N}s\rfloor}{N}}\,,

where we introduced the shorthand notation
k_{[1:n]}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}
and defined
F^{N}
according to

F^{N}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},k_{[1:n]}):={\mathcalboondox F}^{N}(\nabla_{N}f)(-k_{[1:n]})\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcalboondox F}^{N}\varphi_{i}(-k_{i})\bigg).

(5.10)

Now, the term we need to control is given by

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}

\displaystyle\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\frac{1}{N}\sum_{z\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}f\left(\tfrac{z}{N}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcalboondox X}^{i,N}_{s}\left(\varphi_{i}\left(\tfrac{z+v_{i}^{N}s}{N}\right)\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}\bigg[\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{n}=0}^{N-1}F^{N}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},k_{[1:n]})\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n}{{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}_{s}(e_{-k_{i}})\bigg)e^{\iota 2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\frac{\lfloor v_{i}^{N}s\rfloor}{N}}\bigg|\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}\bigg[\bigg|\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{n}=0}^{N-1}F^{N}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},k_{[1:n]})\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n}{{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}_{s}(e_{-k_{i}})\bigg)e^{\iota 2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\frac{\lfloor v_{i}^{N}s\rfloor}{N}}\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{n}=0}^{\infty}|F^{N}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},k_{[1:n]})|\mathbbm{E}\bigg[\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n}{{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}_{s}(e_{-k_{i}})\bigg)e^{\iota 2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\frac{\lfloor v_{i}^{N}s\rfloor}{N}}\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\bigg]\,.

(5.11)

At this point, we want to take the limit in
N
at both sides of the previous expression.
Note first that by (
5.2
) and in view of (
5.9
), we have the bound

\displaystyle|F^{N}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},k_{[1:n]})

\displaystyle|\mathbbm{E}\bigg[\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n}{{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}_{s}(e_{-k_{i}})\bigg)e^{\iota 2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\frac{\lfloor v_{i}^{N}s\rfloor}{N}}\mathrm{d}s\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim t|k_{[1:n]}||\hat{f}(k_{[1:n]})\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n}|\hat{\varphi}_{i}(k_{i})|\bigg)\mathbbm{E}\bigg[\sup_{s\leq T}\bigg|\prod_{i=1}^{n}{{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}_{s}(e_{-k_{i}})\bigg|\bigg]

\displaystyle\lesssim t|k_{[1:n]}||\hat{f}(k_{[1:n]})|\prod_{i=1}^{n}|\hat{\varphi}_{i}(k_{i})||k_{i}|^{4}\,.

(5.12)

Now, the functions
\varphi_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
are infinitely differentiable (and compactly supported, as they live on
\mdmathbb{T}
),
which implies that their Fourier transform decays faster than any polynomial.
Hence, (
5.12
) is summable in
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}
.
Furthermore, invoking (
5.9
) once more, it is immediate to see that
the limit in
N
of
F^{N}
is given by

F(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},k_{[1:n]}):=-2\pi\iota k_{[1:n]}\hat{f}(-k_{[1:n]})\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\varphi}_{i}(-k_{i})\bigg)

(5.13)

which is nothing but the continuum counterpart of (
5.10
).
By the dominated convergence theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{n}=0}^{\infty}|F^{N}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},k_{[1:n]})|\mathbbm{E}\bigg[\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n}{{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}_{s}(e_{-k_{i}})\bigg)e^{\iota 2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\frac{\lfloor v_{i}^{N}s\rfloor}{N}}\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{n}=0}^{\infty}|F(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},k_{[1:n]})|\lim_{N}\mathbbm{E}\bigg[\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n}{{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}_{s}(e_{-k_{i}})\bigg)e^{\iota 2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\frac{\lfloor v_{i}^{N}s\rfloor}{N}}\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\bigg]\,.

Now, if
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}
are such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\neq 0
or

\Pi_{i}\hat{\varphi}_{i}(k_{i})\neq 0
, then the corresponding summand is
0
by (
5.13
).
In all other cases, (
5.1
) holds and this together with the fact that
the processes
\{{{\mathcalboondox X}}^{i,N}(e_{-k_{i}})\colon i=1,\dots,n\}
satisfy condition
2
,
imply that Proposition
B.1
is applicable and therefore the inner limit is
0
, thus the proof is concluded.
∎

6
Second order Boltzmann–Gibbs principle

In this section we present two formulations of the second order order Boltzmann–Gibbs principle for degree-two terms of type
\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}
, involving one or two species, i.e. for
\alpha,\beta\in\{A,B,C\}
.

6.1
A second order Boltzmann–Gibbs principle I

The version of the Boltzmann–Gibbs principle here proposed is a multi-species generalization of Theorem 1 in
[
20
]
. The goal is to replace terms of the form
\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}
by their respective centred averages over boxes of microscopically big (and macroscopically small) size. For this reason we introduce the left and right averages over boxes of size
\ell
to be chosen appropriately. For
\ell\in\mdmathbb{N}
and
x\in\mdmathbb{Z}
we introduce the averages on boxes of size
\ell
, one to the right of
x
, one to the left:

\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell}_{x}=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{y=x+1}^{x+\ell}\xi^{\alpha}_{y},\qquad\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell}_{x}=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{y=x-\ell}^{x-1}\xi^{\alpha}_{y}.

(6.1)

Remark 6.1
.

This version of the principle is used in the analysis of the quadratic terms for the KPZ fields in cases
(I)
and
(II)
.

We first observe that our dynamics satisfies Assumptions 2.1, 2.2 and 2.3 of
[
20
]
. In fact, the invariant measure
\nu_{\rho}
is of product form and it is such that
\int_{{}_{N}}\xi^{\alpha}_{x}(\eta)\nu_{\rho}(\mathrm{d}\eta)=\rho_{\alpha}
for each
x
and each
\alpha
.

The associated Dirichlet form, defined on local functions
f\in L^{2}(\nu_{\rho})
, is given by

D_{N}(f)=-\int_{{}_{N}}f(\eta)L_{N}f(\eta)\nu_{\rho}(\mathrm{d}\eta),

(6.2)

and, using the fact that
c^{\alpha,\beta}_{x}(\eta^{x,x+1})=c^{\alpha,\beta}_{x}(\eta)\frac{\nu_{\rho}(\eta^{x,x+1})}{\nu_{\rho}(\eta)}
, it can be decomposed as

D_{N}(f)=\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{T}_{N}}I_{x,x+1}^{N}(f),

(6.3)

where

I^{N}_{x,x+1}(f)=\frac{N^{2}}{2}\int_{{}_{N}}c_{x}^{\alpha,\beta}(\eta)(f(\eta^{x,x+1})-f(\eta))^{2}\nu_{\rho}(\mathrm{d}\eta).

(6.4)

And finally we recall that the instantaneous current is given by (
3.8
).

Now we can state the theorem. For a function
v\in\ell^{2}(\mdmathbb{Z})
denote

\|v\|^{2}_{2,N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{Z}}v(x)^{2}<\infty.

For a function
\phi:{}_{N}\to\mdmathbb{R}
, let
\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\nu_{\rho}}}
be the
L^{2}(\nu_{\rho})
-norm

\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\nu_{\rho}}}=\int_{{}_{N}}\phi(\eta)^{2}\nu_{\rho}(\mathrm{d}\eta).

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\{A,B\}
. Uniformly over
L\in\mdmathbb{N}
,
t>0
, and function
v\in\ell^{2}(\mdmathbb{Z})
, we have

\mathbbm{E}_{\nu_{\rho}}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{Z}}v(x)\Big\{\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}(s)\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}(s)-\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,L}_{x}(s)\big[\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,L}_{x}(s)(1-\delta_{\alpha\beta})+\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,L}_{x}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\big]+\frac{\chi_{\alpha}}{L}\Big\}\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\\
\lesssim t\left[\frac{L}{N^{a-1}}+\frac{tN}{L^{2}}\right]\|v\|^{2}_{2,N},

(6.5)

where
\chi_{\alpha}=\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\mathbbm{E}_{\nu_{\rho}}[(\xi^{\alpha}_{x}-\xi^{\alpha}_{x+1})^{2}]
.

Proof.

Let’s start with the case
\alpha\neq\beta
. Let
\ell_{0}\in\mdmathbb N
. We can split the integrand in (
6.5
) in the following terms.

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}-\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,L}_{x}\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,L}_{x}=

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}(\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}-\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,\ell_{0}}_{x})

(6.6)

\displaystyle+\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,\ell_{0}}_{x}(\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}-\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell_{0}}_{x})

(6.7)

\displaystyle+\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell_{0}}_{x}(\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,\ell_{0}}_{x}-\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,L}_{x})

(6.8)

\displaystyle+\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,L}_{x}(\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell_{0}}_{x}-\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,L}_{x})

(6.9)

To obtain a bound on (
6.6
) and (
6.7
) we apply the “One-block estimate”, Proposition 5 of
[
20
]
. For (
6.8
) and (
6.9
) we need a “multi-scale argument”, shown in Proposition 8 of
[
20
]
.

Applying Proposition 5 of
[
20
]
to (
6.6
) with
\phi(\tau_{x}\xi^{\alpha}_{0})=\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}
, we obtain

\mathbbm{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{Z}}v(x)\Big\{\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}(\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}-\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,\ell_{0}}_{x})\Big\}\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\leq Ct\frac{\ell_{0}^{2}}{N^{a-1}}\|v\|^{2}_{2,N},

(6.10)

and we do the same for (
6.7
) taking
\psi(\tau_{x}\xi^{\beta}_{0})=\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,\ell_{0}}_{x}
:

\mathbbm{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{Z}}v(x)\Big\{\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,\ell_{0}}_{x}(\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}-\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell_{0}}_{x})\Big\}\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\leq Ct\frac{\ell_{0}^{2}}{N^{a-1}}\|v\|^{2}_{2,N}.

(6.11)

For (
6.8
) and (
6.9
) we apply Proposition 8 of
[
20
]
respectively:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{Z}}v(x)\Big\{\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell_{0}}_{x}(\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,\ell_{0}}_{x}-\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,L}_{x})\Big\}\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\leq C{t}\frac{1}{N^{a-1}}\left(L+\frac{\ell^{2}_{0}}{L}\right)\|v\|^{2}_{2,N}

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{Z}}v(x)\Big\{\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,L}_{x}(\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}-\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell_{0}}_{x})\Big\}\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\leq C{t}\frac{1}{N^{a-1}}\left(L+\frac{\ell^{2}_{0}}{L}\right)\|v\|^{2}_{2,N}

We conclude the proof for
\alpha\neq\beta
putting together all the aforementioned estimates.

When
\alpha=\beta
, we have more terms in the initial decomposition. In fact,

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x+1}-(\overrightarrow{\xi}_{x}^{\alpha,L})^{2}+\frac{\chi_{\alpha}}{L}=

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}(\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x+1}-\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell_{0}}_{x})

(6.12)

\displaystyle+\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell_{0}}_{x}(\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}-\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell_{0}}_{x})

(6.13)

\displaystyle+\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell_{0}}_{x}(\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell_{0}}_{x}-\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,L}_{x})

(6.14)

\displaystyle+\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,L}_{x}(\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell_{0}}_{x}-\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x})

(6.15)

\displaystyle+\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,L}_{x}-(\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,L}_{x})^{2}+\frac{(\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}-\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x+1})^{2}}{2L}

(6.16)

\displaystyle-\frac{(\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}-\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x+1})^{2}}{2L}+\frac{\chi_{\alpha}}{L}.

(6.17)

The first four terms are analogous to the terms we saw above: to estimate (
6.12
), (
6.13
) and (
6.15
) we use Proposition 5 of
[
20
]
; for (
6.14
) we apply the multiscale argument, Proposition 8 of
[
20
]
. For the last two terms, (
6.16
) and (
6.17
) we need different arguments. The estimate for (
6.16
) is a consequence of Proposition 7 of
[
20
]
.
For the last term (
6.17
) we apply Proposition 9 of
[
20
]
.
∎

6.2
A second-order Boltzmann–Gibbs principle II

Here we propose an alternative version of the second order Boltzmann–Gibbs principle, where instead of replacing the occupation variable
\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}
with left/right averages over a box around
x
of size
\ell=\ell(N)
, we use smooth functions supported over
[x,x+\ell]
or
[x-\ell,x]
.

Remark 6.3
.

This version of the principle is used in the analysis of the quadratic terms of the diffusive fields: here we need that the fields are evaluated on smooth functions in order to apply Theorem
5.1
. We also observe that, since in this case the fields have different velocities, they never live on the same frame and consequently they are never correlated, so we do not need to study the variance terms even for the same species.

For
\ell>0
, we defined the functions

\overleftarrow{\rho_{\ell}}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{\ell}\overleftarrow{\rho}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\overrightarrow{\rho_{\ell}}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{\ell}\overrightarrow{\rho}\,,

(6.18)

where
\overleftarrow{\rho}
and
\overrightarrow{\rho}
are smooth functions supported on
[-\ell,0]
and
[0,\ell]
, respectively,
such that

\smallint_{-\ell}^{0}\overleftarrow{\rho_{\ell}}(u)du=1\quad\textrm{and}\quad\smallint_{0}^{\ell}\overrightarrow{\rho_{\ell}}(u)du=1.

(6.19)

Since the final goal is to write Dynkin’s formula in terms of the fluctuation density fields
{\mathcalboondox Y}^{N,A}_{s}
and
{\mathcalboondox Y}^{N,B}_{s}
, the first step is to replace the occupation variable
\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}
and
\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}
, for
\alpha,\beta\in\{A,B\}
, with the integral of
\overleftarrow{\rho_{\ell}}
and
\overrightarrow{\rho_{\ell}}
with respect to centred empirical measure
\bar{\pi}^{N,\alpha}_{s}
and
\bar{\pi}^{N,\beta}_{s}
respectively, where
\bar{\pi}^{N,\alpha}_{s}
is given in (
3.26
). Recall (
3.27
).
We can rephrase the Boltzmann-Gibbs principle as follows.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\{A,B\}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, any
t>0
, and for any function
v\in\ell^{2}(\mdmathbb{Z})

\mathbbm{E}_{\nu_{\rho}}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}ds\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{Z}}v(x)\Big\{\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}(s)\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}(s)-\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,\alpha}_{s},\overleftarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,\beta}_{s},\overrightarrow{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle\Big\}\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\lesssim t\left[{\varepsilon}+\frac{t}{N\varepsilon^{2}}\right]\|v\|^{2}_{2,N}.

(6.20)

The proof follows the same steps as in the original version, i.e. we need to find estimates for each term in (
6.6
),(
6.7
),(
6.8
),(
6.9
), where now in place of
\overleftarrow{\xi}^{\alpha,\ell}_{x}
and
\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\beta,\ell}_{x}
we have

\bar{\pi}^{N,\alpha}_{x}(\overleftarrow{\rho_{\ell}}):=\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,\alpha}_{s},\overleftarrow{\rho_{\ell}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle\quad\text{ and }\quad\bar{\pi}^{N,\beta}_{x}(\overrightarrow{\rho_{\ell}}):=\langle\bar{\pi}^{N,\beta}_{s},\overrightarrow{\rho_{\ell}}(\cdot-\tfrac{x}{N})\rangle.

This implies that we have to reformulate Proposition 5 and 6 of
[
20
]
in terms of the functions
\overleftarrow{\rho_{\ell}}
and
\overrightarrow{\rho_{\ell}}
.

Proposition 6.5

(One-block estimate)
.

Fix
N
and
\ell_{0}\in\mdmathbb{N}
. Let
\phi,\psi:{}_{N}\to\mdmathbb{R}
be local functions with zero mean with respect to the invariant measure
\nu_{\rho}
and such that the support of
\phi
does not intersect the set of points
\{0,\dots,\ell_{0}\}
, and the support of
\psi
does not intersect the set of points
\{-\ell_{0},\dots,-1\}
. Then, for any
t>0
,
v\in\ell^{2}(\mdmathbb{Z})
and
\alpha,\beta\in\{A,B\}

\mathbbm{E}_{\nu_{\rho}}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{Z}}v(x)\phi(\tau_{x}\xi^{\alpha}_{0}(s))\Big[\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}(s)-\bar{\pi}^{N,\beta}_{x}(\overrightarrow{\rho}_{\ell_{0}/N})\Big]\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\lesssim\frac{t\ell_{0}^{2}}{N}\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\nu_{\rho}}}\|v\|^{2}_{2,N},

(6.21)

\mathbbm{E}_{\nu_{\rho}}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{Z}}v(x){\psi}(\tau_{x}\xi^{\beta}_{1}(s))\Big[\bar{\xi}^{\alpha}_{x}(s)-\bar{\pi}^{N,\alpha}_{x}(\overleftarrow{\rho}_{\ell_{0}/N})\Big]\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\lesssim\frac{t\ell_{0}^{2}}{N}\|\psi\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\nu_{\rho}}}\|v\|^{2}_{2,N}.

(6.22)

Proof.

We show the proof only of (
6.21
), since the other is completely analogous. By
[
29
, Lemma 2.4]
we bound the expectation in (
6.21
) from above by

Ct\Big\|\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{Z}}v(x)\phi(\tau_{x}\xi^{\alpha}_{0})\Big[\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}-\bar{\pi}^{N,\beta}_{x}(\overrightarrow{\rho}_{\ell_{0}/N})\Big]\Big\|_{-1}^{2}

where the
H_{-1}
-norm is defined in
[
29
, Equation (1.23)]
thorough a variational formula, and in particular the previous expression is equal to

C_{1}t\sup_{f\in L^{2}({\nu_{\rho}})}\left\{2\int_{{}_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{Z}}v(x)\phi(\tau_{x}\xi^{\alpha}_{0})\Big[\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}-\bar{\pi}^{N,\beta}_{x}(\overrightarrow{\rho}_{\ell_{0}/N})\Big]f(\eta)\nu_{\rho}(\mathrm{d}\eta)-D_{N}(f)\right\},

(6.23)

where
D_{N}(f)
is the Dirichlet form (
6.2
).
First we observe that

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}-\bar{\pi}^{N,\beta}_{x}(\overrightarrow{\rho}_{\ell_{0}/N})=\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}-\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+1}^{x+\ell_{0}}\overrightarrow{\rho}(\tfrac{y-x}{N})\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{y}

Now the key point is to use the fact that
\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+1}^{x+\ell_{0}}\overrightarrow{\rho}(\tfrac{y-x}{N})\sim 1
to write the previous display as

\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+1}^{x+\ell_{0}}\overrightarrow{\rho}(\tfrac{y-x}{N})\Big)+\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+1}^{x+\ell_{0}}\overrightarrow{\rho}(\tfrac{y-x}{N})(\xi^{\beta}_{x+1}-\xi^{\beta}_{y}).

Since
\bar{\rho}(\cdot)
is smooth (in fact,
C^{1}
is enough), an elementary computation shows that

\begin{split}\Big|1-\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+1}^{x+\ell_{0}}\overrightarrow{\rho}(\tfrac{y-x}{N})\Big|&=\Big|\int_{0}^{\ell_{0}/N}\frac{N}{\ell_{0}}\overrightarrow{\rho}(u)du-\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=1}^{\ell_{0}}\overrightarrow{\rho}(\tfrac{y}{N})\Big|\\
&=\Big|\int_{0}^{\ell_{0}}\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\overrightarrow{\rho}(\tfrac{v}{N})dv-\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=1}^{\ell_{0}}\overrightarrow{\rho}(\tfrac{y}{N})\Big|\\
\leq&\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=1}^{\ell_{0}}\int_{y-1}^{y}\Big|\overrightarrow{\rho}(\tfrac{v}{N})-\overrightarrow{\rho}(\tfrac{y}{N})\Big|dv\\
\leq&\frac{1}{\ell_{0}N}\sum_{y=1}^{\ell_{0}}\|\overrightarrow{\rho}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}=O(N^{-1}).\end{split}

This means that we can sum and subtract the leftmost term of (
6.24
) to (
6.21
) and we note that, by stationary, it holds

\mathbbm{E}_{\nu_{\rho}}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mdmathbb{Z}}v(x)\phi(\tau_{x}\xi^{\alpha}_{0}(s))\bar{\xi}^{\beta}_{x+1}\Big[1-\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+1}^{x+\ell_{0}}\overrightarrow{\rho}(\tfrac{y-x}{N})\Big]\mathrm{d}s\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\lesssim\frac{t}{N}\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\nu_{\rho}}}\|v\|^{2}_{2,N},

Now since the rightmost term of (
6.24
)
can be written as

\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\overrightarrow{\rho}(\tfrac{y-x}{N})(\xi^{\beta}_{z}-\xi^{\beta}_{z+1}).

we can proceed exactly as in the proof of Proposition 5 of
[
20
]
to obtain the desired estimate.
∎

Proposition 6.6

(Two-blocks estimate)
.

Fix
\ell_{k}\in\mdmathbb{N}

\ell_{k+1}=2\ell_{k}

,

and

(6.25)

Appendix A
Mode coupling theory

In this section we give a sketch of the application of the mode coupling theory developed in
[
38
]
to our specific model. Note that
since the system has two conserved quantities,
\rho^{A}
and
\rho^{B}
(because as we mentioned above
\rho^{C}=1-\rho^{A}-\rho^{B}
), it is possible to have an estimate on the scaling exponent and form of the expected fluctuation limits for quantities
A
and
B
. We refer the interested reader to
[
31
,
38
]
.

Recall (
3.8
). Then

\displaystyle\mdmathbb E[j^{A}_{x,x+1}]

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{A}-E_{B}}{N}\rho^{A}\rho^{B}+\frac{E_{A}-E_{C}}{2N}\Big(2\rho^{A}(1-\rho^{A})-2\rho^{A}\rho^{B}\Big)\,,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\mdmathbb E[j^{B}_{x,x+1}]

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{B}-E_{A}}{N}\rho^{A}\rho^{B}+\frac{E_{B}-E_{C}}{2N}\Big(2\rho^{B}(1-\rho^{B})-2\rho^{A}\rho^{B}\Big)\,.

From this we get that the current of the system is equal to

\displaystyle j(\rho)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\nabla\rho^{A}+\frac{1}{N}\Big(\rho^{A}(1-\rho^{A})(E_{A}-E_{C})-\rho^{A}\rho^{B}(E_{B}-E_{C})\Big)\\
-\nabla\rho^{B}+\frac{1}{N}\Big(\rho^{B}(1-\rho^{B})(E_{B}-E_{C})-\rho^{A}\rho^{B}(E_{A}-E_{C})\Big)\end{pmatrix}.

(A.2)

From last identity, the Jacobian matrix is thus given by

J=\frac{1}{N}\begin{pmatrix}(1-2\rho^{A})(E_{A}-E_{C})-\rho^{B}(E_{B}-E_{C})&-\rho^{A}(E_{B}-E_{C})\\
-\rho^{B}(E_{A}-E_{C})&-\rho^{A}(E_{A}-E_{C})+(1-2\rho^{B})(E_{B}-E_{C})\end{pmatrix}\,.

(A.3)

To obtain the normal modes we need to find the matrix
R
that diagonalizes
J
,
RJR^{-1}={\rm diag}(v_{\pm})
, where

v_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2N}[(E_{A}-E_{C})(1-3\rho^{A})+(E_{B}-E_{C})(1-3\rho^{B})\pm\delta],\\

(A.4)

are the eigenvalues of the matrix
J
. Above

\delta=\sqrt{[(E_{A}-E_{C})(1-\rho^{A})-(E_{B}-E_{C})(1-\rho^{B})]^{2}+4(E_{A}-E_{C})(E_{B}-E_{C})\rho^{A}\rho^{B}}.

(A.5)

The corresponding eigenvectors are given by

\tau_{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2(E_{A}-E_{C})\rho^{B}}[(E_{A}-E_{C})(1-\rho^{A})-(E_{B}-E_{C})(1-\rho^{B})\pm\delta]\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(A.6)

Let us now rewrite the previous expressions adapted to the case (I).
As observed in Remark
2.4
, case (II),
E_{B}-E_{A}=E_{C}-E_{A}=E\neq 0
, can be deduced by the previous one.

A.1
Case (I):
E_{A}-E_{C}=E_{B}-E_{C}=E

In this case the expressions above simplify. We get the jacobian matrix

J=\frac{1}{N}\begin{pmatrix}E(1-2\rho_{A})-E\rho_{B}&-E\rho_{A}\\
-E\rho_{B}&E(1-2\rho_{B})-E\rho_{A}\end{pmatrix}

and the eigenvalues are

v_{+}:=-\frac{1}{N}E(\rho_{A}+\rho_{B}-1)\quad\textrm{and}\quad v_{-}:=-\frac{1}{N}E(2\rho_{A}+2\rho_{B}-1)

and the corresponding eigenvectors are
\tau_{+}=(-1,1)
and
\tau_{-}=(\frac{\rho_{A}}{\rho_{B}},1)
.
The matrix
R^{-1}
and
R
are given by

R^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&\frac{\rho_{A}}{\rho_{B}}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\textrm{and}\quad R=\frac{1}{\rho_{A}+\rho_{B}}\begin{pmatrix}-\rho_{B}&\rho_{A}\\
{\rho_{B}}&\rho_{B}\end{pmatrix}

The hessians are

{\mathcalboondox H}^{1}=\frac{1}{N}\begin{pmatrix}-2E&-E\\
-E&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\textrm{and}\quad{\mathcalboondox H}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\begin{pmatrix}0&-E\\
-E&-2E\end{pmatrix}\,.

is equal to

(R^{-1})^{T}{\mathcalboondox H}^{1}R=\frac{1}{\rho_{A}+\rho_{B}}\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{E(\rho_{A}+\rho_{B})}{2N}\\
\frac{-E\rho_{A}}{2N}-\rho_{B}(E(\frac{\rho_{A}}{2N\rho_{B}})-\frac{E}{2N})&\rho_{A}(-E(\frac{\rho_{A}}{2N\rho_{B}})-\frac{E}{2N})-\frac{E\rho_{A}}{2N}\end{pmatrix}

(R^{-1})^{T}{\mathcalboondox H}^{2}R=\frac{1}{\rho_{A}+\rho_{B}}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{E(\rho_{A}+\rho_{B})}{2N}\\
\frac{E\rho_{B}}{2N}+\rho_{B}(-E(\frac{\rho_{A}}{2N\rho_{B}})-\frac{E}{N})&\rho_{B}(-E(\frac{\rho_{A}}{2N\rho_{B}})-\frac{E}{N})-\frac{E\rho_{A}}{2N}\end{pmatrix}\,.

The coupling matrices
G^{1}
and
G^{2}
are such that
G^{1}_{1,1}=0=G^{2}_{1,1}
, but also
G^{2}_{2,2}\neq 0
. This means that the first mode is OU and the second is KPZ and this is independent from the choice of the densities
\rho^{A},\rho^{B}
and

.

We note that the predictions from
[
38
]
are done for the strong asymmetric regime, corresponding to the choice

The equal density case

Appendix B
A version of Riemann-Lebesgue lemma

In this appendix, we provide a version of the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma for products of stochastic processes
for which a bound on the supremum and increments is available.
In the context of the present paper, the following proposition is used in
Section
5
to determine the behaviour of the time-integral of the product of processes living in different
time-frames.

Proposition B.1
.

Let
\{v_{i}^{N}\}_{N}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, be
n
diverging sequence of constants.
Let
T>0
and, for every
N\in\mdmathbb{N}
, let
\{{\mathcalboondox A}^{i,N}\colon i=1,\dots,n\}_{N}
be
n

real–valued stochastic processes on
[0,T]

defined on the same probability space.
Assume that there exists
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
C>0
, such that uniformly in
N
,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\bigg[\sup_{s\leq T}|{\mathcalboondox P}_{s}^{N}|\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C\,,

(B.1)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[|{\mathcalboondox P}_{t}^{N}-{\mathcalboondox P}_{s}^{N}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(t-s)^{\alpha}\,,\qquad\text{for all $s,t\in[0,T]$,}

(B.2)

where, for
s\in[0,T]
, we denoted by
{\mathcalboondox P}^{N}_{s}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcalboondox A}^{i,N}_{s}
.

Then, for any
t\in[0,T]
and any integers
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\in\mdmathbb{Z}
such that the limit in (
5.1
) holds,
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcalboondox A}_{s}^{i,N}e^{-2\pi\iota k_{i}\frac{\lfloor v_{i}^{N}s\rfloor}{N}}\mathrm{d}s\right|\right]=0\,,

(B.3)

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
denotes the integer part of
\cdot
.

Proof.

Let us introduce the following notation,

\widetilde{{\mathcalboondox A}}_{s}^{i,N}:={\mathcalboondox A}_{s}^{i,N}e^{-2\pi\iota k_{i}\frac{\lfloor v_{i}^{N}s\rfloor-v_{i}^{N}s}{N}}\,,\qquad\widetilde{{\mathcalboondox P}}^{N}_{s}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{{\mathcalboondox A}}_{s}^{i,N}\,,\qquad C_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}v_{i}^{N}

(B.4)

and let
{\mathcalboondox I}^{N}
be the integral inside the expectation of (
B.3
).
Let
N
be fixed and, without loss of generality, assume
C_{N}>0
. Note that

\displaystyle{\mathcalboondox I}^{N}=\int_{0}^{t}\widetilde{{\mathcalboondox P}}_{s}^{N}e^{-2\pi\iota C_{N}s}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{C_{N}}}\widetilde{{\mathcalboondox P}}_{s}^{N}e^{-2\pi\iota C_{N}s}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\frac{1}{C_{N}}}^{t}\widetilde{{\mathcalboondox P}}_{s}^{N}e^{-2\pi\iota C_{N}s}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{C_{N}}}\widetilde{{\mathcalboondox P}}_{s}^{N}e^{-2\pi\iota C_{N}s}\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t-\frac{1}{C_{N}}}\widetilde{{\mathcalboondox P}}^{N}_{s+\frac{1}{C_{N}}}e^{-2\pi\iota C_{N}s}\mathrm{d}s

where we used that
e^{-2\pi\iota}=1
, and similarly

{\mathcalboondox I}^{N}=\int_{0}^{t-\frac{1}{C_{N}}}\widetilde{{\mathcalboondox P}}_{s}^{N}e^{-2\pi\iota C_{N}s}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t-\frac{1}{C_{N}}}^{t}\widetilde{{\mathcalboondox P}}_{s}^{N}e^{-2\pi\iota C_{N}s}\mathrm{d}s.
