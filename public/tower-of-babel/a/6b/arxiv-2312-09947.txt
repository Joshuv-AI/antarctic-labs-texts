Abstract
. Can large language models assist in data discovery? Data discovery predominantly happens via search on a data portal or the web, followed by assessment of the dataset to ensure it is fit for the intended purpose. The ability of conversational generative AI (CGAI) to support recommendations with reasoning implies it can suggest datasets to users, explain why it has done so, and provide information akin to documentation regarding the dataset in order to support a use decision. We hold 3 workshops with data users and find that, despite limitations around web capabilities, CGAIs are able to suggest relevant datasets and provide many of the required sensemaking activities, as well as support dataset analysis and manipulation. However, CGAIs may also suggest fictional datasets, and perform inaccurate analysis. We identify emerging practices in data discovery and present a model of these to inform future research directions and data prompt design.



1.
Introduction

Dataset discovery, or identifying secondary data useful for research, business, or individual purposes, is a vital activity in contemporary life
(
Börner et al. 2018
;
Jia et al. 2022
)
. This is increasingly true for not only data experts, but also for more casual users, who typically do not require large scale, well promoted datasets
(
Börner et al. 2018
)
. Dataset discovery is composed of two parts: searching for data, and assessing the data to ensure it is fit for the intended purpose. This second part is often characterized as sense-making
(
Chapman et al. 2020
)
. The predominant methods of discovery currently are searching on a data portal (e.g. data.europa.eu) or via a search engine, including specialist search such as Google Dataset Search
(
Ibáñez and Simperl 2022
)
.

Making data useable requires tools that support individuals’ “data journey”
(
Koesten and Simperl 2021
;
Kacprzak et al. 2019
)
. This includes specifying a goal and finding the data they need; exploring new sources of data; assessing the data’s relevance and credibility, and deriving insights. Additionally, to support non-expert users, such tools need to function effectively in low- to no-code interactions that are suitable to populations with diverse economic, cultural and educational backgrounds
(
Hayes et al. 2023
;
Schneider et al. 2023
)
.

Current data journeys involve creating ad hoc workflow processes on the fly over a number of tools, many of which are not easy for non-experts to use
(
Börner et al. 2018
)
. A typical set of search and sense-making activities might look as shown in Figure
1
:

Figure 1.
Screenshots of a data discovery journey showing (a) dataset search on Google Data Search; (b) previewing the data on data.gov.uk website; (c) opening the data in excel; (d) data visualisation in excel and (e) evaluation information search on Google.

While the user is able to find, explore and use the data, many issues remain. The user is not able to be explicit in their goal in undertaking the search, but only to best estimate the keywords that might locate data that aligns somewhat with the needs. Although the search tool offers summaries and previews, the user must engage with further tools to evaluate and explore the data, and then still more tools to actually employ it for the purpose for which it was sought.

Further, data needs are complex, and can typically stretch over multiple search sessions, resulting in complex queries. These are poorly supported in web-based keyword search and can be better addressed with conversational search
(
Radlinski and Craswell 2017
)
.

Conversational generative artificial intelligence (CGAI) tools such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT and Google’s Bard have changed how we access information online
(
Hayes et al. 2023
)
. We are therefore interested in exploring how they could change people’s interactions with data on the web. Given the ability of CGAI to summarise large, complex sources, explain results, and write code, we hypothesise that chatbots may also be able to find data matching a user goal and help the user understand its scope, quality, and provenance, and to use the data productively. This may also be supported further by the increased interactivity offered by conversational search, which has been established as empowering non-expert users
(
McMillan and Hwang 2002
)
.

In this paper we explore whether, given a specific domain, are useful datasets suggested? Do the rationales make sense? Does CGAI help answer critical questions on data provenance, licensing, format and more, and how do users assess this? We begin to consider what a new generation of data tools, enabled by conversational generative AI, might offer to users, and how people might engage with them. Our research questions, therefore, are:

•

RQ1: How does CGAI support state of the art dataset discovery technologies?

•

RQ2: How do people express their information needs when using CGAI for data discovery?

•

RQ3. Which of the main sense-making data discovery activities previously identified are supplied by CGAI?

To address these questions we held workshops with users to explore how they would interact with CGAI when engaging in data discovery. The key contributions of this paper are a set of guidelines for data prompting with CGAI and a new prompting-centric human-data interaction framework.

2.
Background Literature

In this section we contextualise our research in (1) the state of the art of dataset discovery and (2) CGAI for information retrieval.

2.1.
Dataset discovery

The manipulation, analysis, and sensemaking of data has been described as ‘data discovery’
(
Chapman et al. 2020
)
. The data we refer to here is structured (organised in tables, spreadsheets, databases and so on) and its potential scale is vast: as of 2023, Google Dataset Search indexed more than 45 million datasets from more than 13,000 websites
(
Noy and Benjelloun 2023
)
. However, the data sought may not reside in a single dataset but across multiple sources, in a variety of formats, with no common ID, making sensemaking more complex
(
Elmqvist 2011
)
.

Figure 2.
Koesten et al’s (2017) framework for interaction with structured data, showing the 5 steps of the data journey for users of secondary data.
(
Koesten et al. 2017
)
.

There are five key steps in the journey of interacting with structured data, which can be seen in Figure
2
, above
(
Koesten et al. 2017
)
. The first step of the data journey for the user is to define their motivation - why they want to use data. This task purpose is crucial to the process of selecting the appropriate dataset. Currently, there is no useful explicit way for users to define their task motivation in search, although search engines bring a wealth of implicit knowledge about previous user behaviour to the results they present. However, this excludes wider context about the task at hand
(
Shah and Bender 2022
)
.

The second step is that of search itself. Data search is a relatively recent field, with the first widely available dedicated dataset search on the web receiving a full launch as recently as January 2020
(
Noy 2020
)
. The primary forms in which data is made available to people is via data repositories and portals (usually dedicated to a specific institution or domain); via the web, accessed by search engines; and through legal mechanisms such as Freedom of Information requests. When people need to look for data, they employ keyword or faceted search on portals or search engines. Additionally, people find data through personal recommendation
(
Ibáñez and Simperl 2022
)
. However, access to data does not equate enabling citizens to make use of the data
(
Vakulenko et al. 2020
)
. “There is a disconnect between what datasets are available, what dataset a user needs, and what datasets a user can actually find, trust and is able to use”
(
Chapman et al. 2020
)
. A solution to this challenge has not yet been satisfactorily implemented, although there have been improvements in supporting understanding of the data before downloading, such as providing visualisations and summarisation
(
Koesten et al. 2020
)
.

Studies of online search behaviour pre-date the world wide web
(
Marchionini 2006
)
and find that searches range from analytical, precise and carefully planned, to “on the fly”
(
Marchionini and Shneiderman 1988
)
. Users often find it challenging to describe their information needs because of a lack of background knowledge, and their understanding of an information need often evolves during the search process
(
Vakulenko et al. 2020
)

(
Radlinski and Craswell 2017
)
. It has been suggested that when in need of information users actually want an domain expert, but turn to search engines in the absence of this
(
Metzler et al. 2021
)
. Unlike web searches for information, users searching for data are unlikely to expect an exact dataset match to their needs, but rather to locate a relevant subset of topical data
(
Kacprzak et al. 2017
)
. Geospatial and temporal descriptions, constraints (eg open licence) and granularity are the most used parameters to specify data parameters in search
(
Kacprzak et al. 2019
)
.

Data repositories aim to make data search easier, but come with their own complications: they tend to use metadata rather than keyword search
(
Koesten et al. 2017
)
and data users and publishers may use different terms for datasets. One study found that a search for “housing stock” on the UK government data website found almost twice as many datasets as a search for “housing supply”
(
Frank and Walker 2016
)
. Much of the time in learning search tasks is devoted to examining and comparing results and reformulating queries to discover the boundaries of meaning for key concepts
(
Marchionini 2006
)
. Users also keep search terms short: the majority of searches on portals, both on the portal and via the web, are composed on average of less than 5 words
(
Kacprzak et al. 2019
)
.

Further, the information retrieval task at hand may require an entire set of results from multiple different sources with interdependencies. A familiar example of this is booking a holiday, which requires a variety of interdependent travel and accommodation requirements to be met
(
Radlinski and Craswell 2017
)
. This kind of complex query requires “memory” of search interactions that is not yet met by the facilities of the web, and currently users searching for different elements of a holiday need to manually manage a range of concurrent searches which are not automatically informed by the parameters of each separate element.

Sensemaking is a key condition for the secondary reuse of data and comprises the third and fourth steps of the data journey
(
Koesten et al. 2017
;
Koesten et al. 2021
)
. Summary statistics or visualisations are often suggested as a way of exploring quantitative data, but this already implies a decision by the user that the data is worth engaging with, and that it has been assessed as fit for use
(
Brolcháin et al. 2017
;
Segel and Heer 2010
;
Wang 1998
)
. To facilitate this, ‘immediate intelligibility’ is a key requirement for users when discovering data. However, without detail on how and why data was initially collected, this may present a challenge for even the most competent domain expert
(
Frank and Walker 2016
)
. These and other assessments of whether a dataset is fit for purpose, such as its granularity, provenance or format, have been described as ‘data evaluation’
(
Koesten et al. 2017
)
. Such evaluations tend to be dataset and task specific rather than applicable to all datasets
(
Koesten et al. 2020
;
Frank and Walker 2016
)
. Users may need to make as many as 20 types of evaluations, related to the relevance, useability and quality of data
(
Koesten et al. 2017
)
.

A second set of activities take place once the user begins to explore the data prior to use
(
Koesten et al. 2017
)
. These may include visualisations (provided or created by the user themselves) and a review of documentation, if available. Text summaries are often used to reduce cognitive and time cost during information seeking activities online
(
Gambhir and Gupta 2017
)
. Research with dataset text summaries suggests that they do not necessarily contain all the required information (with methodology and provenance most frequently being ignored); that abstractive summaries require extensive natural language generation (NLG); and that they are subject to the idiosyncrasies of the people creating them, rather than being standardised
(
Koesten et al. 2020
;
Gambhir and Gupta 2017
)
.Further, while it may be possible to generate a convincing summary of data itself, it may be the metadata or context that is more important for sensemaking
(
Wiseman et al. 2017
)
.

The multi-step complexity outlined above suggests that powerful NLG tools such as conversational search could be of great value to users requiring data. Conversational search can build understanding and semantic coherence over multiple rounds of interaction, while also supporting user needs through clarification and suggestion,
(
Radlinski and Craswell 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2018
)
. Conversational search is particularly useful for exploratory information-seeking contexts where users have insufficient background knowledge
(
Schneider et al. 2023
)
. While there is a substantial corpus of research on this, pre-dating the existence of large language models, there is little to no research on conversational dataset search
(
Schneider et al. 2023
;
Vakulenko et al. 2020
)
.

2.2.
CGAI for information retrieval

Generative AI refers to a class of artificial intelligence models that can create new data based on patterns and structures learned from existing data. Recently there has been explosive growth in conversational agents based on Large Language Models (LLMs). Such conversational generative artificial intelligence (CGAI) can be used to address natural language tasks, commonly include writing essays or literature reviews of scientific papers, and reading and summarising reports
(
Salvagno et al. 2023
;
Alkaissi and McFarlane 2023
;
Sallam 2023
)
. They can also be used to answer complex natural language questions and support the answers with justifications. Further, many LLMs operate in multiple spoken languages and can therefore both translate text and justify the constructions used in translation, as well as providing text in many coding languages
(
Mialon et al. 2023
)
. CGAI has been used in a number of text manipulation and generation tasks such as creating financial research , structuring unstructured information and detecting stance in social media posts
(
Dowling and Lucey 2023
;
Wei et al. 2023
;
Aiyappa et al. 2023
)
. It has been found to outperform crowd workers on text annotation tasks such as identifying types of social media posts
(
Gilardi et al. 2023
)
.

In conversational search powered by LLMs, users are not restricted to keywords, and are able to state requests in a number of different ways. This suggests they can include more description about their information need in their questions. However, while this may increase the information available to the model to generate an appropriate answer, misleading or unnecessary information may adversely affect results
(
Zuccon and Koopman 2023
)
. The solution to this is generally held to lie in prompt engineering; designing the requests in such a way that they minimise error and maximise potential for appropriate results
(
Bowman 2023
)
. This requires a certain level of awareness of potential errors, skill in devising prompts to reduce such errors and assessing the effectiveness of prompts, which is challenging to non-experts
(
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
. Prompts can also be used to drive the LLM to initiate question asking, by presenting a task that requires the agent to discover more information from the user to complete it
(
White et al. 2023
)
. Research into using GPT to extract unstructured to structured information has shown that greater success is achieved when the information extraction task is broken down into easier sub-tasks, which again requires specific skills
(
Wei et al. 2023
)
. A further strategy for improving search is to specify a domain-expert persona for the LLM to adopt. However, this has been found to also increase bias in language tasks
(
Salewski et al. 2023
)
.

LLMs do not have access to contemporaneous, proprietary or personal data. Consequently, enabling access to external data sources, and augmenting CGAI for retrieval of information, in particular through detecting and calling APIs, is a major area of research
(
Halevy and Dwivedi-Yu 2023
;
Jin et al. 2023
)
. Another is addressing the issue of hallucinations. A hallucination is where a plausible answer returned is not based on existing fact, and is ‘made up’
(
Aiyappa et al. 2023
)
. Hallucinations are of particular interest in our research as they often occur in the form of references to sources that do not actually exist
(
Alkaissi and McFarlane 2023
)
. The ability to identify hallucinations presupposes a certain amount of pre-existing knowledge on the part of the user, as LLMs are unable to directly refer to supporting documents in their training data
(
Metzler et al. 2021
)
There is currently no reliable way for developers to steer the behaviour of LLMs, and even less so by inexperienced users
(
Bowman 2023
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
. The ability of LLMs to fluently produce apparently coherent text may mask the fact that this text may be of much lesser quality than it first appears (containing very little truth or fact), or that it may accidentally be damaging
(
Bender et al. 2021
)
.

Explaination is desirable (and in some cases, legally required) in intelligent systems. It makes it possible for users to act appropriately, is important for discovering and understanding novel concepts, and supports users’ trust in an application
(
Hoffman et al. 2023
;
Confalonieri et al. 2021
)
. A working definition of explainable AI (XAI) is ”Given an audience, an explainable Artificial Intelligence is one that produces details or reasons to make its functioning clear or easy to understand”
(
Arrieta et al. 2020
)
. Explanations have some basic requirements, but they are largely varied based on the requirements, background knowledge, and so forth of the audience. Explanations are used to justify decisions, control function, improve accuracy and discover new knowledge
(
Vilone and Longo 2020
)
. There are as many as
36
various notions of explanations, including efficiency (whether the explanation supports faster decision making), faithfulness (whether definitions truly reflect the datapoints) and robustness (the consistency of the results of the method
(
Vilone and Longo 2020
)
.

Explanations have a social dimension - they constitute an element of a dialogue that aims to transfer knowledge, therefore engaging the beliefs of the giver and recipient of the explanation
(
Vilone and Longo 2020
)
. There is concern that explainability is dealt with primarily technically and less attention is spent on whether the explainability system is effective and efficiently human-centred
(
Mueller et al. 2021
)
. While much attention is currently being paid to understanding how models such as ChatGPT work, much of this insight is currently speculative
(
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
. Consequently, most explanations for LLMs are post-hoc (the dataset, training methods and algorithms are opaque) and most focus on single data points, rather than the whole model. It is, of course, theoretically possible for a user to request that ChatGPT or other model ‘reflects’ on its answer and explains how it arrived at it
(
White et al. 2023
)
.

2.3.
Summary

People both need to locate and understand datasets in order to reuse them. This is often done as part of work, both paid and volunteer. This requires skills around crafting appropriate search, looking in appropriate locations and then assessing the fitness for use of the data. Despite improvement on the supply side, there is still a huge time and cognitive cost on the demand side. Conversational generative artificial intelligence (CGAI) tools such as ChatGPT and Bard have changed how we access information online
(
Sallam 2023
)
. We are therefore interested in exploring how they could change people’s interactions with data on the web. Given the ability of CGAI to summarise large, complex sources, explain results, and write code, we hypothesise that chatbots may also be able to find data matching a user goal and help the user understand its scope, quality, and provenance, and to use the data productively. In the next section we describe our user studies with conversational dataset search.
.

3.
Methodology

To explore how users might engage in conversational dataset search, we invited a gender-balanced group of users to three workshops conducted in two languages, in which we asked them, “to think of one dataset (or group of datasets) that you would like to discover, using [CGAI agent]. (This can be a dataset you know exists, or a completely new-to-you dataset.) Once [agent] has found something (this may require more than one prompt), explore what it has returned to see if it is usable”. For further details see Appendix
A
.

The pilot workshop was conducted in London, UK with users with PhD level understanding of AI and large language models. This was to confirm the format of the workshop and protocol. Eight participants took part in the workshop in person, and 2 trialled a self-guided, asynchronous process following the protocol closely. After this workshop we made two adjustments. Firstly we decided to audio record the “in the room” discussion as this provided rich data and background that supported the data in the questionnaires. Secondly, we found the asynchronous process yielded substantially less research data so abandoned this mode. We used the ChatGPT web interface at chat.openai.com, which does not allow any special features or the uploading of large amounts of text or changes of hyperparameters such as temperature settings
(
Zuccon and Koopman 2023
)
.

For our following workshops, we aimed to ask non-specialist users in a real world context to define a problem they have, and then think about what data they could use to address it. As it was important to our research to reflect a real world scenario, we aimed to work with people who were staff or activists in resource-constrained organisations, who would strongly benefit from using data and understanding its accuracy and provenance. We accessed these groups via personal networks.

Our second workshop was held in Berlin, Germany with professionals who were engaged in political activism. This used the ChatGPT4 web interface, in order to benefit from any relevant improved performance, for instance the ability to input longer texts. All participants were present in person. The third workshop, in London, comprised data professionals with familiarity with the data economy and how data is used, but whose roles did not involve active coding. The audio recordings were uploaded onto our institutional OneDrive, transcribed and (where necessary) translated using the OneDrive translate facility. All translations were checked by a German speaker for accuracy.

The audio transcriptions, questionnaires, and chat session files were then uploaded into NVIVO for thematic coding by two of the researchers. Two of the authors individually coded a sample of the chat transcripts and long form questionnaire answers inductively for RQ1 and RQ2, and coded a sample of the chat transcripts deductively for RQ3. The resulting code lists were compared and conflicting results were discussed. This was repeated twice for both the deductive codes and inductive codes. The resulting code books are attached as Appendix

We calculated the length of the chat sessions by counting the number of queries asked, the responses when interacting with the conversational agent, and their number of words.

3.2.1.
Ethics

The interview study was approved by Author 1’s institution’s Ethical Advisory Committee via the Minimal Risk Procedure. Informed verbal (audio recordings and chat transcripts) and written (questionnaire) consent was given by the participants. No personal information was used in the analysis.

4.1.1.
Success rates of conversational dataset search

In order to assess our participants’ previous experience with datset search, we asked them what methods they have previously used to find datasets. While one participant had not previously searched for datasets at all, most of the participants had used Google search and specific data portals (Figure

). In addition, we asked participants how familiar they were with the conversational agents (Figure

4.1.4.
Self-assessment of efficacy

We asked the users to evaluate how effective conversational search was. Many agreed that “as pure search, it is inferior [to googling]”, largely due to the failure to offer links and the lack of trust in the sources. However, they highlighted that looking for and exploring data inside CGAI meant that ‘next steps’ could be integrated into the process. One noted that using an agent “would probably give me a stronger idea of the nuances, internal politics, and where to look next based on what those organisations are connected to. But it would make sense to do that as a next step following this kind of query”. Another noted “if you have [to] deal with further steps separately, namely the application of BGH case law to concrete contractual clauses and the construction of arguments - I am faster than if I do this “manually”, i.e. using Google/specialist databases.”

4.1.5.
Explanations

We also identified activity in the conversation transcripts where users asked the tool to explain why it has suggested the data it has (n=7). In existing keyword search, users cannot ask why information has been returned to them.

The majority of the interactions that requested explanations regarded links and sources, with participants asking, “so you can’t give me the link to it? I don’t understand why not” and “But this data should be available from your sources”. The responses mostly focused on the explanation that the agent did not have access to the web or that it was under development, or sometimes that it was “only a language model and don’t have the capacity to understand and respond.” One participant asked, “I hear that you’ve given URLs and links to other people who are using your services. Why can’t you link to the papers you’ve pulled from to answer my questions?” In answer to this, Bard returned the common “under development” reply but also noted that it was unable to link to copyright material or websites that “might contain adult content”. While these explanations are not informative about the specific case, they are generally informative about the rules imposed on the agent.

We also found participants asking questions about the sources of the CGAI’s knowledge, for instance, “Where does your knowledge of the global distribution of birds come from?” The answer to this revealed that the agent did “not have direct access to my training data” and that “my knowledge is based on patterns in the data I saw during my training.”

Participants confronted the agents about veracity of responses. One participant asked ChatGPT “are you lying?” In response, Chat noted that it did not operate within the context of ‘truth’ or “lies’ but noted that the study it had referred to in the previous response was ‘misleading” as it was a “fictitious example”. Another queried the language used by challenging, “So by ‘estimating’ do you mean you guessed?” GPT4 responded that “Estimating” in this context does not mean “guessing” and stated that it makes, “an informed statement or prediction based on the available information”. 4.1.6.
Summary

Conversational search was largely characterised by users as a “useful” experience even if few (n=
3
) people felt it was wholly successful in terms of discovering a dataset. The number of words in each query were considerably longer on average than current search, and there was a very long tail. Explanations were largely limited to reasons that the agent could not perform a given task, and not always accurate. However, the ability to integrate further usage tasks into the conversation, such as returning code to access an API, was seen as a positive aspect.

4.2.
RQ2: How do people express their information needs when using CGAI for data discovery?

In this section we explore the prompts users employ when attempting to discover datasets.

4.2.1.
Key terms in conversational dataset search

We performed a visual representation of the most frequently occuring terms used by users in interacting with the agents as shown in Figure
8
.

Figure 8.
Terms frequently used in interaction with CGAI

This suggests that, as with keyword search, users were specifying their data needs geospatially (“london, göttingen, cities”) and temporally (“years, 2022, 2021”). We can see evidence of requests for related evidence (research, study, survey) as well as format based requests (lists, graphs, databases). As noted in Table 6, the average number of words in a question was just under 20. However, amongst the outliers was a 71 word request that specified two kinds of content (street names and postcodes) of a location (EU country) as well as stating conditions around licence, timeliness and format (“no PDFs”).

4.2.2.
Expression of information needs as data prompts

In our thematic analysis we identified three main ways in which people requested datasets. One was requesting the dataset directly, eg, “can you find me the German credit fairness dataset” [datasetrequest].

Another was where the dataset was described, as in “can you provide me with a list of available datasets about Islamophobia in Italian containing Tweets labelled as hate speech” [describeddatasets]. One approach that yielded results for the user was to explicitly suggest a document that might contain the data they sought, “for example a survey which asks people about their reasons for opting for junk foods”.

A last format was implied datasets [implieddatasets], where the request might ask, “What has been the average property price in London, from 2000 to 2010?” Some users varied between these modes of asking, with one user describing a dataset in
4
different ways and then asking
3
further times for an implied version. We noted evidence of requests being made in a way that implied memory of the previous request, for instance “what about ’Global South’ cities”. One such implied dataset request was for a list of judgements on cosmetic repair in German case law. The CGAI recognised the implied request, and replied, “I do not have direct access to a complete database of case law or the exact wording of specific clauses in BGH rulings. However, I can mention a few general cases in which the Federal Court of Justice (BGH) has issued rulings on cosmetic repair clauses until 2021.” Consequently, it curated a small, if necessarily incomplete, dataset for the user. Although no users made requests based on personas, one did ask for an “ELI5” response - “explain like I’m 5 (years old)”, which ChatGPT4 happily complied with, giving examples using fantasy friends and apples and bananas.

The most common question after the initial engagement with the agent was for links [linkrequest]. Separating out the various parts of a request was more common than creating a compound request.Another approach taken by participants was to request the source of the dataset [sourcerequest],rather than simply ask for a link. A user who self-defined as being more experienced with CGAI attempted to use slightly different language to access the datasets. Rather than simply asking for links, which they were aware would result in a “I can’t access the web” reply, they asked “Can you provide directions on how to access the datasets?” [accessdatasets]. In fact, this resulted in the return of links that were wholly inaccurate, which motivated the user to request working links [linkrequest], thus bringing them back to the request. Overall, the majority (n=20) of users requested a link or source as a follow up question.

Alongside link requests, users asked for more information about the dataset itself [additionaldatasetinformation]. Such questions might be general, eg, the licences of datasets or more technical, such as “Which of these datasets provide emotion annotations in the Valence-Arousal space?” However, a major issue with such questions was that the answers often were attended with caveats such as, “Please note that these are fictional examples, ” Users sometimes requested further information about the context of the dataset [furthercontextinformation], rather than the dataset itself. This included requests such as, “What can you tell me on the NeurIPS conference, then?”, “were the annotators of the dataset experts in their field?”

We also found instances of a question being repeated after a particpant had interacted with the agent and supplied it with information. Interestingly, this did not necessarily improve results. In multiple cases, a repeated question that the agent had initially attempted to answer met with responses on a second asking such as, “I’m a text-based AI and can’t assist with that.”

Users requested information on how to use or analyse the dataset provided [advicerequest]. These included the technical and specific: “Explain how would you go about performing an analysis of the social network of the people contained in the Offshore Leaks Database:” and “what are the limitations of tree canopy cover analysis?” as well as the more general, such as, “Can I compare this dataset to other cities”.

Generative AI is not restricted to narrative texts and we saw some examples of people requesting other forms of information to be returned [multimodaloutput], for example, “Write down some code in Python I can use to analyse the Offshore Leaks Database;”, “Can you give me the first 10 triples of each dataset”. Users asked for data visualisations, for instance, “can you make a graph to include the comparative tree canopy cover for all of the cities you have mentioned?”. A particularly positive experience was had by a user who asked if GPT4 could query a REST API, “And then he actually built me corresponding requests, which I can then use”. This integration of further activities [furtheractivity] appeared on a number of occasions, with users requesting drafts of letters, opinions on texts and drafts of legal justification (and a draft of an invalid legal clause). One user asked “can you send a Freedom of Information request too?” which Bard agreed to do, and offered, “to keep you updated on my progress.”

Participants also countered some of the information provided by various agents [disagree], such as, “That plot doesn’t show what I wanted. Can you try again?” This occurred over a range of answer types including lists and data visualisations. We also saw instances of participants providing information to the agents [provideinformation], for instance, “The MusicEmo dataset does not exist, that is why you could not find any link for it. Can you remove it from the list please?” In these examples, participants are contending both with the agent failing to provide existing datasets and hallucinating non-existent datasets. In this instance, the participant engaged repeatedly with the agent to achieve an accurate list. The third “input” interaction we identified was when users referred back to earlier conversation [Conversationreminder], for instance, ’what is the role the 110,000 asylum seekers in France are playing in the overall homelessness you gave me at the beginning of the conversation’.”.

4.2.3.
Summary

This research question has attempted to investigate how participants express themselves when attempting to discover data. We find that, offered more natural ways of defining their information needs, users describe datasets in a variety of direct and indirect ways. Similarly, we see variety in the requests (links, access, sources) that follow the suggestion of a dataset(s). While most participants engage with these two steps, what follows after is less defined. Participants may, dependent on their needs and what is offered, explore the data further, seek advice on using the data or even enter into argument with the agent.

4.3.
RQ3: Which of the main sense-making data discovery activities previously identified are supplied by CGAI?

We base our analysis here on the framework of interaction with structured data discovery
(
Koesten et al. 2017
)
. They present the sense-making aspect of data discovery as consisting of “evaluation” and “exploration”. Here we explore which aspects of evaluation and exploration were prompted by users, and to what extent the CGAI met them.

4.3.1.
Evaluation

Evaluation is broken down into three aspects; relevance, useability and quality, with multiple sub categories. The number of users engaging with each of these sub-categories is provided in Appendix
C
.

Relevance: Relevance is the extent to which the suggested dataset meets the task needs of the user. Relevance assessments include looking at the data context, coverage, summarisation, original purpose and granularity.
As noted above, users often ask for context in the form of either reports or papers data first appeared in, or in which it is subsequently used. Conversational agents are well placed to provide this. However, although sometimes they did suggest texts, they also sometimes claimed they could not do it because of the limitations of being an LLM. Coverage was also requested, with users asking, “Which countries do you have data for?” and “Do these datasets provide full tracks or audio clips?”. Again, success was mixed, but the agents were certainly able to understand and address the requests. At one point, ChatGPT3.5, in the absence of being able to suggest a dataset with appropriate coverage, recommended, “you can consider creating your own dataset by using various methods such as data scraping or using APIs to collect text data from various sources such as social media platforms”.
Although people often use generative AI to write documents it is often more powerful in reading them, so summarisation is intuitively a task where they could perform well. Users asked for, a “general description” of a datasets contents, as well as “a few example records contained in the dataset” to better understand what the data looks like. Users also asked for specific parts of the text to test that the agent could regurgitate them - “What was the clause in the 1st judgment?” Unsurprisingly, once an agent was working with a confirmed text it performed well.
“Granularity” (the measure of the level of detail in a data structure) is one of the
4
key types of dataset descriptors used in search and there were a number of requests of this type
(
Kacprzak et al. 2019
)
. There were also requests further into the conversation. However, these were rarely successful as the agent did not have access to the actual dataset, for instance requests to break down student data by subject. Regardless, the agent was still able to provide information on granularity, for instance, when asked for data “By street and by postcode” they provided a suggestion of the dataset but noted, “This data may still require further processing to extract all street names and postcodes”.

Useability: In this section the framework covers aspects of documentation, licensing, access, machine readability, language, labelling, ability to share and format. Users requested information that might be found in documentation, particularly definitions, eg “what is classified as tree canopy cover”, “how does the Food Foundation define ’poorest households’?” and “can you give me the definition of homelessness”. In these cases, the agent provided a number of definitions and sources, but these were not necessarily those used in the dataset. The agents also provided guidance on whether documentation was available, or should be consulted.

The agents sometimes supplied unprompted licence information or advice with the dataset suggestions. In one case, having suggested a dataset, “the agent told me that there might be problems after all, that the license and the data are not so available after all. That was a dead end.” Whether the data was licenced or not - the veracity of this interaction - is unverified. This is positive as few participants actually requested licence information, but it is a necessity for use.

Regarding access, the agents often suggested contacting the data publishers directly for access information. More detail is sometimes provided: in one interaction, the chat agent suggests a number of datasets for the user to investigate. The user then asks whether one of the datasets, Earth Engine, costs to use the data, to which the chat agent gives a full overview of the costs. Again, the accuracy of this is unverified. Another form of unprompted advice seen was around potential considerations for use of the data. ChatGPT4 discussed cost, access, privacy, and licences, and recommended contacting the data publisher directly for more information on this.

In terms of language, the models we used worked in around 40 major languages. As well as searching in both English and German, users requested datasets in other languages, for instance, “Can you provide me with a list of available datasets about Islamophobia in Italian containing Tweets labelled as hate speech or not?”. The agents were able to move easily between languages.

There were also occasions on which the agent requested the user to provide information, such as when Bard asked one user to share the code they had used to create a graph, as, “Your answers to these questions will help me to understand what went wrong and how I can fix it.” and asked another user to provide detailed information so it could draft a letter. These also demonstrate the facility to build on complex queries, retaining context from previous rounds of requests.

Quality: Under the heading of quality the framework examines issues of consistency, completeness, collection methods, provenance, and what has not been included in the data set. Users asked about and received answers on collection methods such as, “Can you explain what machine learning techniques were used to form the dataset?”, and “What studies, books, internet articles, etc. did you use?”. One way that users sought to understand the quality of the data was by asking whether the data was used by other organisations. For example, one user asked whether “any of these [have] been used in research publications?”, while another asked if a “study [has] nevertheless been cited in scientific papers?”
In terms of provenance, as we saw in Section 4.2.2, there are a great many questions from users about sources of data and figures. However, equally, this is one of the more unreliable aspects of the agents. Firstly, the number of questions about sources is provoked by their opacity regarding the sources of their information, and secondly, there is frequent admission of unreliability, including fictitious examples.

4.3.2.
Exploration

In “exploration” the major issue is that the datasets are not returned within the agent, so basic visual scans and an assessment of basic errors are challenging. Visualisations and data samples were returned in response to requests, although in at least one case, the visualisation was inaccurate. We found examples of metadata and documentation being addressed in the chat sessions, (although again, we are not necessarily testing the veracity of the claims). Interestingly, the references to metadata in the chats were suggestions by the agents, rather than requests by the users. Along with the finding above that explicit reference to documentation was only made by the agents, these results imply that the agents are able to supply information to support data exploration even when they are unprompted or they cannot return the datasets themselves. However, while this is often thorough, it is necessarily generic and not specific to the dataset in question.
We found that on occasion the LLMs would describe the data in sufficient detail as to include at least some descriptions of the content of the columns, as it did in the tree dataset. We also explored the data for related aspects, such as “columns”. We found two references. One was an instance of ChatGPT 3.5 describing how to clean data; the other was a comment in the questionnaire from a user who was concerned that subheaders would make it difficult for ChatGPT to identify appropriate data:“a matter of fact, these kinds of in depth information are contained as sub-columns in datasets and not as primary class labels.”

4.3.3.
Summary

The evaluation and exploration tasks outlined above are crucial as it is based on these that people make the decision to use data (or not). We find that the majority of these elements are identifiable in the chats in some way. Of particular interest is the fact that the agents do, on occasion, suggest generic exploration and evaluation tasks without prompting, and also request input from the users on data evaluation tasks.

5.
Discussion

What, then, are the emerging user practices around dataset search with conversational tools?

Conversational dataset discovery enables the explicit enactment of the entire process of dataset discovery. The task can be specified in text by the user, rather than existing as an implicit motivator. Similarly, the data use can be integrated into the process. Overall, therefore, dataset discovery can be greatly enhanced by CGAI. We first discuss
6
areas of promise, then review
4
areas of challenge, before presenting
5
guidelines for prompting data. We then present our model for user-dataset discovery interaction using conversational agents.

5.1.
Curation - promise

A key aspect of the agents is the curation of data. Unlike keyword search where (often) thousands of potential dataset links are returned, far fewer datasets were suggested by by the CGAI in response to each query. Given appropriate web connectivity, it can be extrapolated that CGAIs should be able to select only the most relevant datasets from the web and provide them to users enhanced with supporting information. Another form of curation was the returning of a particular number of datasets in response to user specification. Finally, a third type of curation in evidence was that seen in response to implied dataset requests, such as the suggestion of the German case law examples, which again has great potential for future use.

5.2.
Complex queries - promise

Complex queries have long been an idea in research papers, but CGAI allows this to be realised at scale with non-expert users. While we saw longer, more detailed queries these did not necessarily correlate with identifying datasets more accurately. However, the ability to “remember” the conversation, to return and reexplore earlier interactions, and to build on sensemaking with usage activities in the same space is beneficial.

5.3.
Feedback - promise

A key strength of CGAI is the ability to incorporate information fed by users during interactions. However, while users incorporated correction and redirection strategies, this met with varying levels of success. Unsurprisingly the users with the greatest success in this area were those in the more expert group. This suggests that users can control managing this issue with increased familiarity.

5.4.
Supplemental information -promise

Unprompted information supplied by the agents often supported key areas of concern with data such as access, licence, cost, and so on. In some cases it made users aware of issues, but did not resolve those issues, for instance, in such responses as “be aware of licenses”, while not actually identifying the license in question. This suggests that while CGAI may not be reliable for data discovery, it may well have potential as a tool of data literacy, as users are not required to come to search with existing knowledge about what aspects of evaluation and exploration are important.

5.5.
Advice - promise

Similarly, CGAI has potential as supporting tools for data skills, via the provision of advice. Such advice includes, "it gave me guidance on finding [datasets] in the future” or advice on usage of the data.

5.6.
Multimodality - promise

However, the aspect of CGAI that offers perhaps the most potential as a tool for data is their ability to provide responses in a number of modalities. The ad hoc creation of visualisations and summaries enhances exploration and reduces the skillset a user requires; similarly the ability to provide code supports usage.

5.7.
Hallucinations - challenge

Where the fiction created by CGAI is very closely correlated with the truth this does not present a problem; nor where the user is very expert in the domain. A third area in which this is not problematic is where the agent provides general rather than specific information, such as “look in these kinds of reports” or “check the metadata”.
A somewhat positive response to the fictitious nature of the responses is that users worked hard to counterbalance this, asking for sources, evidence and provenance, in an attempt to navigate around admissions of unreliability. However, there are aspects of hallucinations that work against commonly agreed positive strategies for data search. An example of this is text summarisation. Text summarisation is seen as an accessible and technically achieveable exploration technique. The UK’s Office of National Statistics, for example, a major producer of public data, has experimented with integrating LLM technology into its website search, so that users can extract data from its text bulletins
(
Banks et al. 2023
)
. However, while text summarisation is a strength of CGAI it does not help if the report or text summary is entirely fictitious, and the user is not aware of this fact.
Domain expertise remains of importance to counteract hallucination, but a key strategy is verification, and this depends on moving outside of the conversation to check other sources, thus affecting the ability to conduct the dataset discovery process entirely within the conversation.

5.8.
Explanations - challenge

There is no ground truth for explanations or correct reasons the agents could offer. In terms of a global explanation, this most frequently took the shape of statements about limitations including “As an AI language model, I don’t have real-time data access”; “I have a cut off date for training data of September 2021”; As noted, the bland “I’m not able to help with that, as I’m only a language model” was the most frequently used statement about agents’ capabilities. Local explanations about particular responses are generally generic. However, the most problematic aspect of agents’ own capabilities is the unreliability and opacity of its own post-hoc explanations. This opacity includes information supplied after the event regarding its ability to operate within the boundaries of “truth” and admitting it may therefore be “misleading”. The existence of multiple drafts, each providing potentially differing explanations, further undermines any reliability in this area.

5.9.
Inconsistency - challenge

We saw great inconsistency between results, even where the same prompt was used. A step by step approach has been found to be successful to counteract this in some maths and reasoning tasks. Recursive prompting might address this, however, that is beyond the scope of this study. Instead, ways need to be found to work around, or indeed, with inconsistency. Such approaches may lie in triangulation of information, either within answers (or drafts) or with external sources.

5.10.
Capability expectation - challenge

Conversational agents are not search engines with super powers. Users may expect them to able to return a link or csv file, but this is not currently what happens. In other words, users need to approach conversational dataset search less in the anticipation that they will receive a magic bullet answer, and more in the expectation of receiving support to find and use an appropriate dataset, which may happen using a different tool on the web. Further, it may be that for “information veracity, information literacy and serendipity” reducing human interactions with web search is not desirable

1.

SELECT THE CORRECT PROMPT FORMAT FOR THE DATA

Naming a dataset explicitly is most likely to return the dataset without much supporting information (unless this is specified in the prompt); describing a dataset is likely to return suggestions of datasets with some supporting information and implying a dataset is likely to return a narrative description of some key data points.

2.

MIX REQUEST PROMPTS FOR MORE EFFECTIVE DISCOVERY

The different types of requests can be employed iteratively, in order to build a better picture of the data. For example, a user might begin with an ‘implied’ prompt in order to understand the bigger narrative of a dataset, and then move to a ‘described’ prompt in order to explore more about the data itself.

3.

USE RESULTS AS A BASE FOR IMPROVEMENT

It is common for users to request CGAI to supply a draft of a text, such as a letter or report, which they then edit and improve. Similarly, the results of data discovery can be used as a basis for guided information retrieval.

4.

USE REMINDERS

The CGAI responds to the prompts of the users but is fundamentally passive. Building towards the goal should include explicitly reminding the CGAI of the task and previous interactions in the conversation.

5.

EXPLOIT THE CODING ABILITIES OF CGAI

For instance, instead of asking for a visualisation, ask for code to create a visualisation. An incorrect visualisation with no explanation of how it was created is harder to debug than incorrect code.

6.
Conclusion

Making data useable requires tools that support individuals’ data journeys. This includes specifying a goal and finding the data they need; exploring new sources of data; assessing the relevance and credibility, and deriving insights. In this study we explored whether, given a specific domain, can conversational agents based on large language models suggest useful datasets? Do the rationales make sense? Does CGAI help answer critical questions on data provenance, licensing, format and more, and how do users assess this?

We find that CGAI can suggest relevant dataset areas, and allows more engagement with the data and contextual information. However, we also found that the suggestion of datasets and provision of information, especially more obscure data, are unreliable; that suggestions are prone to confusing levels of inconsistency; and domain expertise and web search are often required to establish veracity. Its real strength currently is incorporating advice on evaluation and usage, and supplying code in various forms to support this, reducing the need for advanced data skills to work with data effectively. Of particular note is the fact that users are able to supply explicit feedback, corrections and information to the CGAI to improve outcomes.

Thus, until these issues are addressed, CGAI will not fulfil its very real promise of being an effective end to end data discover tool. Our contributions towards this are i. a model that describes user interaction with CGAI when engaging in dataset discovery and ii. a set of guidelines for data prompting.

6.1.
Limitations

In terms of the users we worked with this is very specifically about data literate people operating in highly served languages. We cannot extrapolate to less data literate people in under served languages. In terms of the agents, given the unstable and changing nature of the answers even within workshops, it may be that entirely different responses would be elicited from the agents if any number of circumstances changed, for instance, increased access to the web, more use of temperature settings, and so on.

6.2.
Future Work

More work needs to be done on prompting agents in a way that reduces the domain knowledge required to elicit datasets. Our findings further suggest that a valuable follow up study would be to pre-locate datasets and ask the agent to explore it, to understand what further activities and services of data use agents could consistently provide. It is key to investigate the use of conversational agents in under-served languages. This will not only let us further explore the integration of AI into learning and society, but also more broadly understand the support that this technology offers to these communities. Given the datasets on which large language models are trained we hypothesize there may be substantial differences in the ability to discover and make sense of relevant datasets.
