Strong divisibility sequences and sieve methods
Tim Browning, Matteo Verzobio

Abstract
. We investigate strong divisibility sequences and produce lower and upper bounds for the density of integers in the sequence which only have (somewhat) large prime factors. We focus on the special cases of Fibonacci numbers and elliptic divisibility sequences, discussing the limitations of our methods. At the end of the paper there is an appendix by Sandro Bettin on divisor closed sets, that we use to study the density of prime terms that appear in strong divisibility sequences.

11N36 (11A41, 11A51, 11B39, 11B83, 11G05)

1.
Introduction

The arithmetic of sparse integer sequences continues to present a considerable challenge in analytic number theory. In this paper we shall use elementary sieve theory to prove some crude
arithmetical properties of sparse sequences that admit a suitable divisibility structure. We begin by discussing some well-known sparse sequences and related literature.

Shifted exponentials

Given
a>1
and non-zero
b\in\mathbb{Z}
,
a notorious open problem is to determine whether the sequence
a^{n}-b
takes infinitely many prime values as
n
runs over
\mathbb{N}
. These sequences are sparse, having only
O(\log N)
elements in the interval
[1,N]
. Recent work of Grantham and Granville
[
7
]
speculates on this for the sequence
a\cdot 2^{n}-b
when
\gcd(a,b)=1
and
a>0
; they conjecture that either there are finitely many primes in the sequence, or else the number of primes in the interval
[1,N]
grows like
c_{a,b}\log N
for a suitable constant
c_{a,b}>0
.
(When
a=b=1
, which is the case of Mersenne primes, their heuristic
suggests the asymptotic formula should hold with
c_{1,1}=e^{\gamma}/\log 2
.)
In the opposite direction,
in his book on sieve methods
[
12
]
, Hooley conjectured that almost all numbers in the sequence
2^{n}+5
are composite.
Hooley’s conjecture has now been resolved by
Järviniemi and Teräväinen
[
13
]
, subject to GRH and a form of the pair correlation conjecture.

Markoff numbers

The sequence of Markoff numbers is obtained by listing
all coordinates that arise as positive integer solutions to the Diophantine equation

x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}=3x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}
. Thanks to work of
Bourgain, Gamburd and Sarnak
[
2
]
, we know that almost all Markoff numbers are composite.
This is a sparse sequence, since the interval
[1,N]
contains
O((\log N)^{2})

elements of the sequence, by work of Zagier
[
25
]
.

Elliptic curves

Let
E
be an elliptic curve defined by a Weierstrass equation with integer coefficients
and let

P\in E(\mathbb{Q})
be a non-torsion point.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we may define

nP=(x_{n}:y_{n}:z_{n})
, for relatively coprime
x_{n},y_{n},z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
As we shall see in Section
4
, we must have
x_{n}=a_{n}b_{n}
and
z_{n}=b_{n}^{3}
, for
a_{n},b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. The sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is even sparser than the previous examples, containing only
O(\sqrt{\log N})
integers from the interval
[1,N]
.
Nonetheless, it is still possible to prove non-trivial results about their arithmetic.
In
[
15
, Section 4.4]
, Kowalski uses the large sieve to
show that elements of the sequence with few prime divisors have density
0
. More recently, similar sequences have been studied by
Bhakta, Loughran, Rydin Myerson and Nakahara

[
1
]
in the context of local solubility for families of conics parametrised
by elliptic curves. In

[
1
, Thm. 1.1]
, for example, they show that the set of
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which
y_{n}
is a sum of two squares has density
0
, if
P
belongs to the connected component of the identity of
E(\mathbb{R})
. Due to the fact that the sequence is so sparse, it
has been conjectured by Einsiedler, Everest and Ward
[
5
]
that
there are only finitely many prime numbers appearing in the sequence
b_{n}
.

Our results in this paper pertain to any
strong divisibility sequence
(SDS), which is a sequence of positive integers
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\gcd(x_{n},x_{m})=x_{\gcd(n,m)}.

for all
m,n\geqslant 1
. We may henceforth assume without loss of generality that
x_{1}=1
, on noting that the general case can be deduced from this case by applying the results to the sequence
x_{n}/x_{1}
.
The most basic example of a SDS is the
sequence
x_{n}=n
, for which our results are all well-known or trivial. Further examples are provided by
elliptic divisibility sequences
,
corresponding to the sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

discussed above, and

Lucas sequences of the first kind
. The latter include
sequences of

Fibonacci numbers

F_{n}=\frac{\phi^{n}-(-\phi)^{-n}}{\sqrt{5}},

where
\phi
is the golden ratio,
as well as
Mersenne numbers

2^{n}-1
.

There is a rich literature around the arithmetic of
such numbers; for example,
in 1965 Erdős
[
6
]
conjectured that
\frac{1}{n}P(2^{n}-1)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, where

P(2^{n}-1)

is the size of the largest prime factor of
2^{n}-1
.
Stewart
[
24
]
has verified this conjecture in the wider context of Lucas sequences, showing in particular that
P(2^{n}-1)>n\exp(c\log n/\log\log n)
, for a suitable constant
c>0
.
In a different direction,
Luca and Stănică
[
19
]
provide heuristics about the size of

\omega(x_{n})
for the Lucas sequences
x_{n}=(a^{n}-1)/(a-1)
, for integer
a>1
, conjecturing that

\omega(x_{n})\geqslant(1+o(1))\log n\log\log n
for almost all
n
.
Recent work of Kontorovich and Lagarias
[
14
]
posits a new
toral affine sieve conjecture
,
under which it is possible to study the total number of prime divisors of products like
F_{n}F_{n+1}
. Thus it follows from
[
14
, Theorem 1.5]
that

\Omega(F_{n})+\Omega(F_{n+1})\gg\log n

for all sufficiently large
n
, conditionally on their toral affine sieve conjecture.
Finally, a classification of those SDS which are simultaneously a
linear recurrence sequence
has been completed recently by Granville
[
8
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a SDS and
let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Define
m_{d}
to be the smallest positive integer such that
d\mid x_{m_{d}}
. If no such integer exists, we put
m_{d}=\infty
.

There are two major challenges inherent in trying to apply sieve theory to a SDS.
We shall see in Remark
2.2
that

(1.1)

\#\{n\leqslant N:d\mid x_{n}\}=\frac{N}{m_{d}}+O(1),

so that we have an associated
density function

g(d)=1/m_{d}
.
Unfortunately, in most cases of interest the function
g(d)
is not multiplicative, whereas
one of the basic sieve axioms is that
g(d)
should be a non-negative multiplicative function, with

0<g(p)\leqslant 1
for any prime
p
.
The second issue to contend with is the fact that a SDS can grow exponentially, as in the case of Lucas sequences, or even
quadratic exponentially, as in the case of elliptic divisibility sequences. Most successful applications of sieve theory involve sequences with at most polynomial growth.

The following is one of our main results and is based on the most basic sieve of
Eratosthenes–Legendre.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a SDS with the property that there
exists

\alpha>0
such that, for all but finitely many primes
p
, we have

m_{p}<p^{\alpha}.

Then

\#\left\{n\leqslant N:p\mid x_{n}\implies p>\tfrac{1}{2}(\log N)(\log\log N)\right\}\gg\frac{N}{\log\log N}.

This result provides a lower bound for the number of
z
-rough numbers in a SDS

\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
n\leqslant N
and
z=\tfrac{1}{2}(\log N)(\log\log N)
.
We will see that the hypothesis of the theorem is satisfied by Lucas sequences of the first kind, and by elliptic divisibility sequences.
Moreover, note that if
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are SDS that satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem
1.2
, then also the sequence
\{\gcd(x_{n},y_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the hypothesis.

We shall illustrate our most general results with the sequence
\{F_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of Fibonacci numbers. Note that
\Omega(F_{n})=O(n)
, since

\Omega(m)=O(\log m)
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. (In fact, the latter bound is optimal, as one sees by considering the case in which
m
is a prime power.)
The following shows that we can often improve on this trivial bound for
\Omega(F_{n})
.

Corollary 1.3
.

We have

\#\{n\leqslant N:\Omega(F_{n})\leqslant\frac{n}{\log\log n}\}\gg\frac{N}{\log\log N}.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
1.2
, on noting that

\Omega(F_{n})\leqslant\frac{n}{\log\log n}
if
p>C\log N
for all
p\mid F_{n}
, for a suitable constant
C>0
.
∎

Under additional hypotheses, we can also prove an upper bound for the kind of set considered in
Theorem
1.2
. We say that the term
x_{n}
has a
primitive prime divisor
if there exists a prime
q
such that
q\mid x_{n}
, but
q\nmid x_{k}
for
k<n
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a SDS with the property that

x_{n}
has a primitive prime divisor
for all but finitely many terms.
Then, there exists a strictly increasing continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 1}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
n\leqslant f(z)
implies
x_{n}\leqslant z
, for all
z\geqslant 1
. Moreover, for any such function
f
, we have

\#\left\{n\leqslant N:p\mid x_{n}\implies p>z\right\}\ll\frac{N}{\log f(z)}+f(z)^{2},

for any
z
such that
f(z)>1
.

According to
[
20
, Theorem 1]
and

[
23
, Proposition 10]
, respectively,
both Lucas sequences of the first kind (except for some trivial examples) and elliptic divisibility sequences satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem
1.4
.

Our next result is concerned with the density of prime terms that appear in a SDS
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
It is well-known that a Mersenne number
2^{n}-1
is composite if
n
is composite,
so that the set of Mersenne primes among the sequence
\{2^{n}-1\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has
density
0
.
We shall generalise this result to a more general SDS, as follows.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a SDS and let

\mathscr{A}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:x_{n}=1\}.
Assume that
\mathscr{A}
has density
0
. Then the set

\{n\in\mathbb{N}:x_{n}\text{ is prime}\}

has density
0
.

Apart from
some trivial examples, Lucas sequences of the first kind satisfy the hypothesis of the theorem,
as do elliptic divisibility sequences.
Moreover, the assumption on the density of
\mathscr{A}
cannot be removed, as we will show in Remark
3.6
.
The proof of Theorem
1.5
relies
on an auxiliary fact that is provided for us by Sandro Bettin in Appendix
A
. This states that,
given any divisor closed set of density
0
, the product set formed with the set of primes continues to have density
0
.

As an application of our main results, let us record upper and lower bounds for the cardinality of

z
-rough numbers in the Fibonacci sequence
\{F_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
n\leqslant N
and
z=\frac{1}{2}(\log N)(\log\log N)
. (In fact, the following result shows that the set of
n\leqslant N
for which
F_{n}
is
z
-rough has density
0
.)

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
\{F_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence of Fibonacci numbers. Then

\frac{N}{\log\log N}\ll\#\left\{n\leqslant N:p\mid F_{n}\implies p>\tfrac{1}{2}(\log N)(\log\log N)\right\}\ll\frac{N}{\log\log\log N}.

Proof.

Recall that the assumptions of Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
hold for the Fibonacci sequence.
The lower bound therefore follows from Theorem
1.2
. For the upper bound, we put
z=\tfrac{1}{2}(\log N)(\log\log N)
and deduce from Theorem
1.4
that

\#\left\{n\leqslant N:p\mid F_{n}\implies p>\tfrac{1}{2}(\log N)(\log\log N)\right\}\ll\frac{N}{\log f(z)}+f(z)^{2}.

If
f(z)=\log_{\phi}(\sqrt{5}z-1)
, then it is clear that
F_{n}\leqslant z
if
n\leqslant f(z)
. The desired upper bound is now obvious.
∎

Much of this paper is devoted to understanding the size of the expected main term in the application of sieve methods to a SDS, with the associated quantities

(1.2)

(If
m_{d}=\infty
, then we count
\mu(d)/m_{d}
as
0
in this sum.)

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a SDS with the property that there
exists

\alpha>0
such that
m_{p}<p^{\alpha}

for all but finitely many primes.
Then

M(\Pi_{z})\gg\frac{1}{\log z}

where
\Pi_{z}=\prod_{p<z}p
.

Consider the SDS given by Mersenne numbers
x_{n}=2^{n}-1
. In this case
computational evidence seems to suggest that

M(\Pi_{z})\sim\frac{1}{\log z},

as

.

2.
Properties of
m_{d}

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a strong divisibility sequence (SDS), so that

\gcd(x_{n},x_{m})=x_{\gcd(n,m)}

for all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Clearly this implies that
x_{m}\mid x_{n}
if
m\mid n
, so that
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
divisibility sequence
. As we pointed out in the introduction, we will work under the assumption
x_{1}=1
; the general case can be deduced from this case by applying the results to the sequence
x_{n}/x_{1}
. We begin by collecting together some basic properties of the function
m_{d}
from Definition
1.1
.

Lemma 2.1
.

We have
d\mid x_{n}
if and only if
m_{d}\mid n
.

Proof.

If
m_{d}\mid n
, then
d\mid x_{m_{d}}\mid x_{n}
since we are working with divisibility sequences. If
d\mid x_{n}
, then
d\mid\gcd(x_{m_{d}},x_{n})=x_{\gcd(n,m_{d})}
. By definition,
m_{d}
is the smallest positive integer such that
x_{m_{d}}
is divisible by
d
, whence
\gcd(n,m_{d})\geqslant m_{d}
. This happens only if
m_{d}\mid n
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Note that (
1.1
) is an immediate consequence of Lemma

2.1
.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
k\mid j
then
m_{k}\mid m_{j}
.

Proof.

Let
k\mid j
. Then
k\mid j\mid x_{m_{j}}
, whence
Lemma
2.1
implies that

m_{k}\mid m_{j}
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

m_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}=[m_{d_{1}},m_{d_{2}}],

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the least common multiple.

Proof.

Since
d_{1}\mid[d_{1},d_{2}]
, we have
m_{d_{1}}\mid m_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}
by Lemma
2.3
. In the same way,
m_{d_{2}}\mid m_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}
and so
[m_{d_{1}},m_{d_{2}}]\mid m_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}
.

The sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a divisibility sequence and so
x_{m_{d_{1}}}\mid x_{[m_{d_{1}},m_{d_{2}}]}
. It follows that
d_{1}\mid x_{m_{d_{1}}}\mid x_{[m_{d_{1}},m_{d_{2}}]}
and, similarly,
d_{2}\mid x_{[m_{d_{1}},m_{d_{2}}]}
. Thus,
[d_{1},d_{2}]\mid x_{[m_{d_{1}},m_{d_{2}}]}
and
Lemma
2.1
yields
m_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}\mid[m_{d_{1}},m_{d_{2}}]
.
∎

We now turn to the estimation of the sum
M(\Pi)
in
(
1.2
), for suitable
\Pi\in\mathbb{N}
. Our first goal is to prove the lower bound in Theorem
1.7
. For this we shall apply a suitable result based on inclusion–exclusion.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}
be divisors of
n
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d\nmid n_{j},\forall j\leqslant k\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\sum_{I\subseteq\{1,\dots k\}\cup\emptyset}(-1)^{\#I}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid\gcd(n_{i})_{i\in I}\end{subarray}}\mu(d),

where we follow the convention that
\gcd(n_{i})_{i\in I}=n
when
I=\emptyset
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
k
. Assume
k=1
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d\nmid n_{1}\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\end{subarray}}\mu(d)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n_{1}\end{subarray}}\mu(d),

as required. We now prove the lemma for
k>1
. Let
D=\{d\mid n:d\nmid n_{j},\forall j\leqslant k-1\}
. Then we may write

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d\nmid n_{j},\forall j\leqslant k\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in D\\
d\nmid n_{k}\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in D\end{subarray}}\mu(d)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in D\\
d\mid n_{k}\end{subarray}}\mu(d).

By induction,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in D\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\sum_{I\subseteq\{1,\dots k-1\}\cup\emptyset}(-1)^{\#I}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid\gcd(n_{i})_{i\in I}\end{subarray}}\mu(d).

Note that if
d\in D
is such that
d\mid n_{k}
, then
d\mid n_{k}
and
d\nmid\gcd(n_{k},n_{i})
for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1
. Hence, by induction, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in D\\
d\mid n_{k}\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n_{k}\\
d\nmid\gcd(n_{j},n_{k}),\forall j\leqslant k-1\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\sum_{I\subseteq\{1,\dots k-1\}\cup\emptyset}(-1)^{\#I}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid\gcd(n_{i},n_{k})_{i\in I}\end{subarray}}\mu(d).

The statement of the lemma follows.
∎

Next, the following key result allows us to express
M(\Pi)
as a sum of non-negative terms.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\Pi\in\mathbb{N}
be square-free and such that
m_{\Pi}<\infty
. Then

M(\Pi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\mid m_{\Pi}\\
\gcd(x_{j},\Pi)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\varphi(m_{\Pi}/j)}{m_{\Pi}},

where
\varphi(\cdot)
is the Euler
\varphi
-function.

Proof.

Recall from Lemma
2.3
that
m_{d}\mid m_{\Pi}
if
d\mid\Pi
. Thus

M(\Pi)=\sum_{d\mid\Pi}\frac{\mu(d)}{m_{d}}=\sum_{l\mid m_{\Pi}}\frac{1}{l}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{d}=l\\
d\mid\Pi\end{subarray}}\mu(d).

Now
m_{d}=l
if and only if we have
d\mid x_{l}
, with
d\nmid x_{l^{\prime}}
for each proper divisor
l^{\prime}\mid l
.
Let
p_{1},\dots p_{k}
be the distinct prime divisors of
l
. It follows that
m_{d}=l
if and only if
d\mid x_{l}
and
d\nmid x_{l/p_{i}}
, for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
.
Hence

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{d}=l\\
d\mid\Pi\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid\gcd(x_{l},\Pi)\\
d\nmid\gcd(x_{l/p},\Pi),\forall p\mid l\end{subarray}}\mu(d).

We now seek to
Lemma
2.5
, for which we
note that

\gcd(x_{l/p_{i}})_{i\in I}=x_{\gcd(l/p_{i})_{i\in I}}=x_{l/\prod_{i\in I}p_{i}},

since
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a SDS.
Hence

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{d}=l\\
d\mid\Pi\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\sum_{I\subseteq\{1,\dots k\}\cup\emptyset}(-1)^{\#I}\sum_{d\mid\gcd(x_{l/p_{I}},\Pi)}\mu(d),

where
p_{1},\dots p_{k}
are the distinct prime divisors of
l
and
p_{I}=\prod_{i\in I}p_{i}
.
The inner sum is
1
if
\gcd(x_{l/p_{I}},\Pi)=1
, and
0
otherwise.
It therefore follows that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{d}=l\\
d\mid\Pi\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\mid l\\
\gcd(x_{l/k},\Pi)=1\end{subarray}}\mu(k).

Putting
l=jk
, we see that

M(\Pi)=\sum_{l\mid m_{\Pi}}\frac{1}{l}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=jk\\
\gcd(x_{j},\Pi)=1\end{subarray}}\mu(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\mid m_{\Pi}\\
\gcd(x_{j},\Pi)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{j}\sum_{k\mid m_{\Pi}/j}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}.

By the properties of the Euler-
\varphi
function, we have

M(\Pi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\mid m_{\Pi}\\
\gcd(x_{j},\Pi)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{j}\frac{\varphi(m_{\Pi}/j)}{m_{\Pi}/j}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\mid m_{\Pi}\\
\gcd(x_{j},\Pi)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\varphi(m_{\Pi}/j)}{m_{\Pi}},

as desired.
∎

When
x_{1}=1
the first term in this sum is
\varphi(m_{\Pi})/m_{\Pi}
and we get a lower bound for
M(\Pi)
by focusing on this term and ignoring the terms associated to
j>1
.
(Note that for the trivial sequence
x_{n}=n
this is actually sharp, since then

M(\Pi)=\sum_{d\mid\Pi}\frac{\mu(d)}{d}=\frac{\varphi(\Pi)}{\Pi},

for any
\Pi\in\mathbb{N}
.)
The following result is concerned with lower bounding

\varphi(m_{\Pi})/m_{\Pi}
, under an additional assumption, for suitable
\Pi\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a SDS with the property that there
exists
\alpha>0

such that
m_{p}<p^{\alpha}

for all but finitely many primes. Let
S
be the finite set of primes
p
such that
m_{p}=\infty
. Let
\Pi_{z,S}=\prod_{p<z,p\notin S}p
. Then

\frac{\varphi(m_{\Pi_{z,S}})}{m_{\Pi_{z,S}}}\gg\frac{1}{\log z}.

Proof.

On enlarging
\alpha
, we can assume that
m_{p}<p^{\alpha}
for all
p\notin S
.
Let us write
\Pi=\Pi_{z,S}
to ease notation, noticing that

\log(\Pi)=\sum_{p\leqslant z}\log p+O(1)=\theta(z)+O(1),

where
\theta(z)
is the Chebyshev function. Thus
\log(\Pi)\sim z
as
z\to\infty
, by the prime number theorem.
We have the familiar lower bound

\varphi(d)\gg\frac{d}{\log\log d}.

By Lemma
2.4
and the hypotheses of the lemma, it follows that

m_{\Pi}\leqslant\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant z\\
p\notin S\end{subarray}}m_{p}<\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant z\\
p\notin S\end{subarray}}p^{\alpha}=\Pi^{\alpha}.

But then

\log\log m_{\Pi}\leqslant\log\alpha+\log\log\Pi\leqslant\log z+O(1).

We have therefore proved that

\frac{\varphi(m_{\Pi})}{m_{\Pi}}\gg\frac{1}{\log\log m_{\Pi}}\gg\frac{1}{\log z},

as required.
∎

It turns out that the
lower bound in
Lemma
2.7

is actually sharp under a further assumption on the SDS. This is the object of the following result.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a SDS.
Let
S
be the finite set of primes
p
such that
m_{p}=\infty
.
Assume that there exists
\delta>0
such that, for all but finitely many primes
q
, there exists a prime
p<q^{\delta}
such that
q\mid m_{p}
. Then

\frac{\varphi(m_{\Pi_{z,S}})}{m_{\Pi_{z,S}}}\ll\frac{1}{\log z}.

Proof.

Write
\Pi=\Pi_{z,S}
, as previously and note that

\frac{\varphi(m_{\Pi})}{m_{\Pi}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\mid m_{\Pi}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right).

The product will only get larger if we reduce the number of factors in the product. Now

q\mid m_{\Pi}
if and only if there exists
p\mid\Pi
such that
q\mid m_{p}
.
By hypothesis, we have
q\mid m_{p}
for at least one
p\mid\Pi
for
q\ll z^{1/\delta}
, up to finitely many exceptions. Hence

\frac{\varphi(m_{\Pi})}{m_{\Pi}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\mid m_{\Pi}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\ll\prod_{q\ll z^{1/\delta}}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\ll\frac{1}{\log z},

by Mertens theorem.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

Both Lucas sequences of the first kind and CM elliptic divisibility sequences satisfy the assumption of Lemma
2.8
, assuming GRH. For elliptic divisibility sequences with CM, this will be proved in Proposition
5.4
. For Lucas sequences,
as explained by Lenstra
[
17
, Lemma 2.5]
,
we have
q\mid m_{p}
if and only if the Frobenius of
p
belongs to certain conjugacy classes in a field depending only on
q
. But then one concludes using an effective form of the Chebotarev density theorem
[
16
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Let
S
be the finite set of primes as in Lemma
2.7
. If
d\mid\Pi_{z}
and
d\nmid\Pi_{z,S}
, then there exists
p\in S
such that
p\mid d
. So,
m_{d}=\infty
. Hence, on recalling the definition (
1.2
), we have

M(\Pi_{z})=M(\Pi_{z,S})
.
Lemma
2.6
implies that

M(\Pi_{z,S})\geqslant\frac{\varphi(m_{\Pi_{z,S}})}{m_{\Pi_{z,S}}},

since
x_{1}=1
.
But then
Lemma
2.7
yields the desired lower bound.
∎

3.
Eratosthenes–Legendre sieve and primes in a SDS

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a SDS satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
.
Let
\mathscr{P}
be a set of rational primes, and let
z
and
N
be two positive parameters.
We define

A(N,z)=\left\{n\leqslant N:\gcd(x_{n},\Pi)=1\right\},

where

(3.1)

\Pi=\Pi(z,\mathscr{P})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathscr{P}\\
p\leqslant z\end{subarray}}p.

We can now prove the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
M(\Pi)
be given by (
1.2
). Then

\#A(N,z)=NM(\Pi)+O(2^{\omega(\Pi)}).

Proof.

It follows from inclusion–exclusion that

\#A(N,z)=\sum_{d\mid\Pi}\mu(d)\#A_{d},

where
A_{d}=\{n\leqslant N:d\mid x_{n}\}
.
On appealing to (
1.1
), we deduce that

\#A(N,z)=N\sum_{d\mid\Pi}\frac{\mu(d)}{m_{d}}+O(2^{\omega(\Pi)}),

as required.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

In this result we take
\mathscr{P}
to be the set of all rational primes.
We have
\omega(\Pi)\leqslant z/\log z
in Lemma
3.1
, by the prime number theorem.
Taking
z=\frac{1}{2}(\log N)(\log\log N)
, we note that
2^{z/\log z}\leqslant\sqrt{N}
for sufficiently large values of
N
. Hence
it follows from Lemma
3.1
that

\#A(N,z)-NM(\Pi)\ll\sqrt{N}.

Theorem
1.7
now yields the statement of the theorem.
∎

We next proceed by searching for an upper bound for
\#A(N,z)
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathscr{F}
be the family of strictly increasing continuous functions

f:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 1}\to\mathbb{R}

such that
\lim_{z\to\infty}f(z)=\infty
.

Bearing this definition in mind, we may establish the following result.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a SDS such that, for all but finitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{n}
has a primitive prime divisor. Then there exists
f\in\mathscr{F}
such that

n\leqslant f(z)\implies x_{n}\leqslant z,

for all
z\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

Firstly, we notice that

{x_{n}}
cannot admit a constant subsequence, since
there is a primitive prime divisor for all but finitely many terms.
Hence
\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=\infty
and we may form an
unbounded increasing sequence
y_{n}=\max_{i\leqslant n}\{x_{i}\}
. Let
f_{1}:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 1}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 1}
be such that
f_{1}(z)=\max\{n\geqslant 1:z\geqslant y_{n}\}
. Hence,
f_{1}
is an increasing step-function that goes to infinity. Fix
z\geqslant 1
and let
n\leqslant f_{1}(z)=m
. But then
y_{m}\leqslant z
and
x_{n}\leqslant y_{m}\leqslant z
. Let
f
be a strictly increasing continuous function that goes to infinity such that
f(z)\leqslant f_{1}(z)
for all
z\geqslant 1
. Then, if
n\leqslant f(z)
, we will have
n\leqslant f_{1}(z)
and so
x_{n}\leqslant z
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
C
be such that, for all
n>C
,
x_{n}
has a primitive prime divisor.
Let
n\geqslant 1
be divisible by a prime
p
with
C<p<f(z)
. Let
q
be a primitive prime divisor of
x_{p}
. Thus

q\mid x_{p}\leqslant z

since
p\leqslant f(z)
. Hence,
q\mid x_{p}\mid x_{n}
and then
n\notin A(N,z)
.
It therefore follows that

A(N,z)\subseteq\{n\leqslant N:p\mid n\implies p\leqslant C\text{ or }p\geqslant f(z)\}.

But then an application of the Selberg sieve
[
4
, Theorem 7.1.1 and Corollary 7.1.2]
yields

\#\{n\leqslant N:p\mid n\implies p\leqslant C\text{ or }p\geqslant f(z)\}\ll\frac{N}{\log f(z)}+f(z)^{2},

which gives our desired upper bound for
\#A(N,z)
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
be a SDS such that, for all but finitely many terms,
x_{n}
has a primitive prime divisor and let
f
be as in Lemma
3.3
. Let
g:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant f(1)}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
f\circ g=\operatorname{Id}
(which exists since
f
is continuous and strictly increasing).
Then

\#\left\{n\leqslant N:p\mid x_{n}\implies p>g\left(N^{1/3}\right)\right\}\ll\frac{N}{\log N}.

Proof.

Fix
N
and let
z=g(N^{1/3})
. Then,

\log(f(z))f(z)^{2}\leqslant f(z)^{3}=f(g(N^{1/3}))^{3}=(N^{1/3})^{3}=N.

It follows that

f(z)^{2}\leqslant\frac{N}{\log f(z)},

whence

\#\left\{n\leqslant N:p\mid x_{n}\implies p>z\right\}\ll\frac{N}{\log f(z)}+f(z)^{2}\ll\frac{N}{\log f(z)}\ll\frac{N}{\log N},

by Theorem
1.4
.
∎

In Corollary
1.6
we applied
Theorem
1.4
to get an upper bound for
\#A(N,z)
for the sequence
\{F_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of Fibonacci numbers, with

z=\tfrac{1}{2}(\log N)(\log\log N)
.
In doing so we used the observation that

F_{n}\leqslant z
if
n\leqslant f(z)
,
with the choice
f(z)=\log_{\phi}(\sqrt{5}z-1)
.
Note that
f\circ g=\operatorname{Id}
,
with
g(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}(\phi^{z}+1)
. But then Corollary
3.4
implies that

\#\left\{n\leqslant N:p\mid F_{n}\implies p>\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\phi^{N^{1/3}}\right\}\ll\frac{N}{\log N}.

It is interesting to compare this result with Lemma
3.1
.
For
z
of order
\phi^{N^{1/3}}
, the error term

O(2^{\omega(\Pi_{z})})
is
much larger than
N
, rendering the estimate meaningless.

Remark 3.5
.

We can clearly prove an analogue of Corollary
1.6
for the sequence

x_{n}=2^{n}-1
of Mersenne numbers, using instead the function
f(z)=\log_{2}(z+1)
.
In fact, we claim that

M(\Pi_{z})\ll\frac{1}{\log\log z},

for this sequence, where
\Pi_{z}=\prod_{p\leqslant z}p
.
To see this we combine
Lemma
3.1

with Theorem
1.4
, in order to deduce that

\displaystyle NM(\Pi_{z})

\displaystyle\ll\#A(N,z)+2^{z/\log z}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{N}{\log f(z)}+f(z)^{2}+2^{z/\log z}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{N}{\log\log z}+\log^{2}z+2^{z/\log z}.

The claim follows on dividing through by
N
and taking the limit as
N\to\infty
.

We conclude this section by proving Theorem
1.5
,
which gives a mild condition under which the set of primes in a SDS has density
0
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a SDS.
Let
\mathscr{A}
be the set of indexes
n
such that
x_{n}=1
and assume that
\mathscr{A}
has density
0
.
Assume that
x_{n}=q
is a prime and write
n=\prod_{i\leqslant u}p_{i}^{a_{i}}
. For all
i\leqslant u
, let
n_{i}=n/p_{i}^{a_{i}}
and notice that
x_{n_{i}}
is equal to
1
or
q
(since it must be a divisor of
q
). Moreover,
\gcd_{i\leqslant u}(n_{i})=1
. If, for all
i\leqslant u
we have
x_{n_{i}}=q
, then

x_{1}=x_{\gcd_{i\leqslant u}(n_{i})}=\gcd_{i\leqslant u}(x_{{n_{i}}})=q,

which is not true. Thus, there exists
i\leqslant u
such that
x_{n_{i}}=1
and then
n_{i}\in\mathscr{A}
. Therefore,
n
can be written as the product of a prime power and an element in
\mathscr{A}
. Let
\mathscr{P}^{\infty}
be the set of prime powers and
\mathscr{P}^{\infty}\cdot\mathscr{A}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:n=rx,~r\in\mathscr{P}^{\infty},~x\in\mathscr{A}\}
. In conclusion, we have shown that

\{n\in\mathbb{N}:x_{n}\text{ is prime }\}\subseteq\mathscr{P}^{\infty}\cdot\mathscr{A}.

Note that
\mathscr{A}
is divisor closed, meaning that

d\in\mathscr{A}
whenever

n\in\mathscr{A}
and
d\mid n
. This follows, since
x_{d}\mid x_{n}=1
.
We conclude by
applying Theorem
A.1
, which shows that if
\mathscr{A}
has density
0
and is divisor closed, then
\mathscr{P}^{\infty}\cdot\mathscr{A}
has density
0
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Notice that the assumption that
\mathscr{A}
has density
0
cannot be removed. Indeed, let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence defined by

x_{n}=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if $2\mid n$,}\\
1&\text{ if $2\nmid n$.}\end{cases}

This is a SDS and we see that
\mathscr{A}
has density
1/2
. Moreover,
x_{n}
is prime for a positive density set of indices.

4.
Elliptic divisibility sequences

The goal of this section is to prove some properties of elliptic divisibility sequences, and in particular that they satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
1.2
.
Let
E
be a rational elliptic curve defined by a Weierstrass equation with integer coefficients. Let
P\in E(\mathbb{Q})
be a non-torsion point. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

nP=(x_{n}:y_{n}:z_{n})\in\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{Q})

with
x_{n},y_{n},z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\gcd(x_{n},y_{n},z_{n})=1
. If we add the hypothesis
z_{n}>0
, then the choice of
(x_{n}:y_{n}:z_{n})
is unique. Since the
Weierstrass equation defining the curve has integer coefficients, so it follows that

x_{n}=a_{n}b_{n}
and
z_{n}=b_{n}^{3}
, for
a_{n},b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Indeed, if
p^{k}\|z_{n}
then
p^{k}\mid x_{n}^{3}
, by the equation defining the curve. But then
\gcd(p,y_{n})=1
and
p^{k}\|x_{n}^{3}
. Hence,
z_{n}
is a cube and
p^{j}\|x_{n}
if
p^{3j}\|z_{n}

The sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an
elliptic divisibility sequence
(EDS). Moreover,
possibly after a change of variables, we can henceforth assume that
b_{1}=1
.
As explained by Silverman
[
23
, Section 2]
, it follows from
properties of the formal group of an elliptic curve that the
sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a SDS.

Let
p
be a prime. Note that
m_{p}
, as given by Definition
1.1
, is the smallest positive integer such that
m_{p}P
is the identity
element
(0:1:0)\in E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
on reducing modulo
p
. Furthermore, we note that
p\mid b_{n}
if and only if
nP
reduces to the identity modulo
p
.
The next result shows that an EDS satisfies the hypothesis in Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
p
is a prime of good reduction for
E
, then
m_{p}\mid\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. In particular,
m_{p}\leqslant p+1+2\sqrt{p}
.
If
p
is not a prime of good reduction for
E
, then there exists a constant
C_{E}
depending only on
E
such that
m_{p}\leqslant C_{E}p
.

Proof.

Assume that
p
is a prime of good reduction. Then the order of
P
in
E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
divides the cardinality of the group
E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. But, by definition,
m_{p}
is the order of
P
in
E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. Thus

m_{p}\mid\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})\leqslant p+2\sqrt{p}+1,

by the Hasse bound.

Assume that
p
is not a prime of good reduction.
Then it follows from
[
22
, Corollary C.15.2.1]
that there exists a constant
C^{\prime}
, depending only on
E
, such that
mP
is non-singular in
E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
for at least one
m<C^{\prime}
. The group of non-singular points in
E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
has order at most
2p+1
. We conclude as before.
∎

Building on this,
we can prove general upper and lower bounds for
m_{d}
, for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
While not directly used in our work,
the following result shows that
m_{d}
can vary quite widely in size.

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, depending only on
E
and
P
, such that

C_{1}\sqrt{\log d}\leqslant m_{d}\leqslant C_{2}^{\omega(d)}d,

for any square-free
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

We begin by proving the lower bound.
Let
\varepsilon>0
.
As explained by Silverman
[
23
, Lemma 8]
, Siegel’s theorem implies that

(4.1)

(1-\varepsilon)n^{2}\hat{h}(P)+O_{\varepsilon,E}(1)\leqslant\log b_{n}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)n^{2}\hat{h}(P)+O_{\varepsilon,E}(1),

where
\hat{h}(P)>0
is the
canonical height of the point
P
.
We know
d\mid b_{m_{d}}
and so it follows that
\log d\leqslant\log b_{m_{d}}
, whence

\log d\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)m_{d}^{2}\hat{h}(P)+O_{\varepsilon,E}(1)
.
The lower bound easily follows.

Turning to the upper bound, let
d=p_{1}\dots p_{r}
and let

\Delta_{E}
denote the discriminant of
E
.
It follows from Lemma
2.4
that
m_{d}\leqslant m_{p_{1}}\dots m_{p_{r}}
.
Applying Lemma
4.1
, we deduce that

m_{p}\leqslant\begin{cases}p(1+2p^{-1/2}+p^{-1})&\text{ if $p\nmid\Delta_{E}$},\\
C_{E}p&\text{ if $p\mid\Delta_{E}$},\end{cases}

for any prime
p
.
But then it follows that

m_{d}\leqslant d\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid d\\
p\nmid\Delta_{E}\end{subarray}}(1+2p^{-1/2}+p^{-1})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid d\\
p\mid\Delta_{E}\end{subarray}}C_{E}\leqslant C_{2}^{\omega(d)}d,

for a suitable constant
C_{2}>0
.
∎

Note that it follows from (
4.1
) that the EDS
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
contains only
O(\sqrt{\log N})
elements in the interval
[1,N]
.

5.
A Chebotarev argument for EDS

In Lemma
2.7
we proved a lower bound for
\varphi(m_{\Pi})/m_{\Pi}

and in
Lemma
2.8
we proved a matching upper bound, subject to some additional hypotheses on the SDS. The goal of this section is to check that the hypotheses hold for EDS,
associated to a
rational elliptic curve
E
and a non-torsion point
P\in E(\mathbb{Q})
.
Throughout this section we shall need to work under the assumption that GRH holds, and we shall assume that
E
has CM, with
\operatorname{End}(E)=\kappa
.

Given an ideal
I
in
\kappa
, let
\kappa_{I}=\kappa(E[I])
and
L_{I}=\kappa_{I}(P/I)
. Since
E
is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, it follows that
\kappa
has class number one (as proved in
[
21
, Theorem II.4.3]
). By
P/I
we mean a point
Q
in
E(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
such that
\alpha Q=P
if
I=(\alpha)
. Notice that the choice of
Q
is not unique but the field
L_{I}
does not depend on this choice.
Building on work of Gupta and Murty
[
9
,
10
]
, we can establish the following result.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
p
a prime that splits in
\kappa
and let
q\nmid 6p
be a prime. Let
\pi_{p}
be the Frobenius of
p
in
\kappa
. Then
q\mid m_{p}
if and only if there is a prime
\mathfrak{q}_{1}
over
q
and
k_{1}\geqslant 1
such that
\pi_{p}
splits completely in
\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}
and does not split completely in
\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}}
and
L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{\pi_{p}}=\mathscr{O}_{\kappa}/\pi_{p}\mathscr{O}_{\kappa}
where
\mathscr{O}_{\kappa}
is the ring of integers of
\kappa
and notice that
\mathbb{F}_{\pi_{p}}=\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
Recall that the ring of integers of
\kappa
is a principal ideal domain, since
E
is a rational elliptic curve.
We start by doing the case when
q
splits in
\kappa
, so that

q=\mathfrak{q}_{1}\mathfrak{q}_{2}
in
\kappa
. Recall
from
[
10
, Lemma 3]

that
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})=N(\pi_{p}-1)
.
Given
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we claim that
\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{j}
divides
(\pi_{p}-1)
if and only if
\pi_{p}
splits completely in
\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{j}}
. If
\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{j}
divides
(\pi_{p}-1)
then

\pi_{p}\equiv 1\bmod\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{j},

and it follows that
\pi_{p}
acts trivially on
E[\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{j}]
. Therefore,
\pi_{p}
splits completely in
\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{j}}
. Conversely, if
\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{j}
does not divide
(\pi_{p}-1)
, then
\pi_{p}
does not act trivially on
E[\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{j}]
and
\pi_{p}
does not split completely in
\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{j}}
.

Let
k_{1}
(respectively,
k_{2}
) be the largest integer such that
\pi_{p}\equiv 1\bmod{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}
(respectively, modulo
{\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}}}
). Thus
\pi_{p}
splits completely in
\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}
(respectively,
\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}}}
) and does not split completely in
\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}}
(respectively,
\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}+1}}
). We have
\pi_{p}-1=\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}}I
for
I
an ideal coprime with
\mathfrak{q}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{q}_{2}
.
Hence
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})=q^{k_{1}+k_{2}}N(I)
and so the
q
-primary part of
E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
has order
q^{k_{1}+k_{2}}
.
Moreover, the
q
-primary part of
E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is
E[\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}]\times E[\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}}]
, since
\pi_{p}
acts trivially on
E[\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}]
(respectively,
E[\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}}]
) and does not act trivially on
E[\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}]
(respectively,
E[\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}+1}]
). Here, we are using that
E[\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}]
and
E[\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}}]
have trivial intersection, since the two ideals are coprime.

Let
E(\mathbb{F}_{\pi_{p}})=E[\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}]\times E[\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}}]\times G
with
G
a group of order coprime with
q
.
Let
\overline{P}
be the reduction modulo
\pi_{p}
of
P
and note that
q\mid m_{p}
if and only if
q
divides the order of
\overline{P}
. Write
\overline{P}=P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{3}
with
P_{1}\in E[\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}]
,
P_{2}\in E[\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}}]
, and
P_{3}\in G
. Note that multiplication by
\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}
is an isomorphism on
E[\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}}]
and
G
. Hence
\overline{P}
is divisible by
\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}
if and only if
P_{1}
is divisible by
\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}
.
This happens if and only if
P_{1}=0
. In the same way,
\overline{P}
is divisible by
\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}}
if and only if
P_{2}=0
. Hence,
q
does not divide the order of
\overline{P}
if and only if
P_{1}=0
and
P_{2}=0
. It follows that
\overline{P}
has order divisible by
q
if and only if
\overline{P}/\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}
does not have a solution in
E(\mathbb{F}_{\pi_{p}})
or
\overline{P}/\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}}
does not have a solution in
E(\mathbb{F}_{\pi_{p}})
. Hence
\pi_{p}
does not have a first-degree prime factor in
L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}
or in
L_{\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k_{2}}}
. For more details, see the proof of
[
9
, Lemma 2]
. Since
L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}/\kappa
is Galois, this happens if and only if
\pi_{p}
does not split completely. In summary,
q
divides
m_{p}
if and only if there exists a prime
\mathfrak{q}_{1}
over
q
and a strictly positive integer
k_{1}
such that
\pi_{p}
splits completely in
\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}
, does not split completely in
\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}}
, and does not split completely in
L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}
.

Assume now that
q
does not split.
In this case there is only one prime
\mathfrak{q}_{1}
in
\kappa
over
q
. Let
k_{1}
be such that
(\pi_{p}-1)=\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}I
, with
I
and
\mathfrak{q}_{1}
coprime. Proceeding as above,
E(\mathbb{F}_{\pi_{p}})=E[\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}]\times G
with
G
of order coprime with
q
.
We conclude as before.
∎

Armed with this result, we can now use the Chebotarev density theorem to assess the density of rational primes that split in the
endomorphism ring of
E
and satisfy the property
q\mid m_{p}
, for a fixed prime
q
. The analogous result for the property
q\mid\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
has been proved by Cojocaru
[
3
, Section 2.2]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume that
E
has CM with
\operatorname{End}(E)=\kappa
, and assume GRH. Let
q>3
be a prime of good reduction. Then there exists
\delta_{q}\geqslant 0
such that

\#\{p\leqslant x:p\text{ splits in }\kappa,q\mid m_{p}\}=\delta_{q}\operatorname{li}(x)+O(x^{1/2}\log^{3}x),

where the implied constant depends on
E
and
P
.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{q}(x)=\#\{p\leqslant x:p\text{ splits in }\kappa,q\mid m_{p}\}.

Given a prime
\mathfrak{q}_{1}
in
\kappa
, define

A_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}=\left\{p\leqslant x:\begin{array}[]{l}\pi_{p}\text{ splits completely in }\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}\\
\pi_{p}\text{ does not split completely in }\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}}\text{ or }L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}\end{array}\right\}.

It follows from
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
(see also
[
10
, Lemma 7]
) and an inclusion-exclusion argument, that

(5.1)

\begin{split}\#A_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}=~&\frac{\operatorname{li}(x)}{[\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}:\kappa]}-\frac{\operatorname{li}(x)}{[\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}}:\kappa]}-\frac{\operatorname{li}(x)}{[L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}:\kappa]}+\frac{\operatorname{li}(x)}{[L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}+1}:\kappa]}\\
&+O(x^{1/2}(\log x+k_{1}\log q)).\end{split}

Thus

\#A_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}=\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}\operatorname{li}(x)+O(x^{1/2}(\log x+k_{1}\log q)),

with

(5.2)

\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}=\frac{1}{[\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}:\kappa]}-\frac{1}{[\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}}:\kappa]}-\frac{1}{[L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}:\kappa]}+\frac{1}{[L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}}:\kappa]}.

In a similar way, if
q=\mathfrak{q}_{1}\mathfrak{q}_{2}
, then

\#(A_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}\cap A_{\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}})=\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1},\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}\operatorname{li}(x)+O(x^{1/2}(\log x+\max\{k_{1},k_{2}\}\log q)),

for a suitable constant
\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1},\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}
.

Assume that
(q)=\mathfrak{q}_{1}\mathfrak{q}_{2}
splits in
\kappa
.
By Lemma
5.1
, we have

\{p\leqslant x:p\text{ splits in }\kappa,q\mid m_{p}\}=\left(\cup_{k_{1}\geqslant 1}A_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}\right)\bigcup\left(\cup_{k_{2}\geqslant 1}A_{\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}\right).

Note that, by definition,
A_{k_{1},\mathfrak{q}_{1}}\cap A_{k_{1}^{\prime},\mathfrak{q}_{1}}=\emptyset
for
k_{1}\neq k_{1}^{\prime}
. Hence it follows that

\displaystyle\pi_{q}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1}\geqslant 1}\#A_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}+\sum_{k_{2}\geqslant 1}\#A_{\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}-\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\geqslant 1}\#(A_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}\cap A_{\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}).

If
k_{1}>\frac{\log 2x}{\log q}
, then
q^{k_{1}}>2x>\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. As we noted during the proof of Lemma
5.1
, we have

q^{k_{1}}\mid\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
if
p\in A_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}
. Thus,

A_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}
is empty if
k_{1}>\frac{\log 2x}{\log q}
.
Putting
K=\frac{\log 2x}{\log q}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\pi_{q}(x)=~

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}\leqslant K}\operatorname{li}(x)\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}+\sum_{k_{2}\leqslant K}\operatorname{li}(x)\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}\leqslant K\\
k_{2}\leqslant K\end{subarray}}\operatorname{li}(x)\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1},\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}

\displaystyle+O(K^{2}x^{1/2}(\log x+K\log q)).

Putting

\delta_{q}=\sum_{1\leqslant k_{1}}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}+\sum_{1\leqslant k_{2}}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}-\sum_{1\leqslant k_{1},k_{2}}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1},\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}},

we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\pi_{q}(x)-\delta_{q}\operatorname{li}(x)\right|\ll~

\displaystyle x^{1/2}\log^{3}x

\displaystyle+\operatorname{li}(x)\left(\left|\sum_{k_{1}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}+\sum_{k_{2}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}-\sum_{\max\{k_{1},k_{2}\}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1},\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}\right|\right).

Notice that

\sum_{k_{1}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}+\sum_{k_{2}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}-\sum_{\max\{k_{1},k_{2}\}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1},\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}\geqslant 0

and so

\left(\left|\sum_{k_{1}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}+\sum_{k_{2}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}-\sum_{\max\{k_{1},k_{2}\}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1},\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}\right|\right)\leqslant\sum_{k_{1}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}+\sum_{k_{2}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}.

We have

[\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{k_{i}}}:\kappa]=q^{k_{i}-1}(q-1)
, for
i=1,2
, since
q
is a prime of good reduction and coprime to
6
.
By (
5.2
),

\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{i},k_{i}}\leqslant\frac{4}{[\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{k_{i}}}:\kappa]}=\frac{4}{q^{k_{i}-1}(q-1)}.

By this inequality, one can easily show that
\delta_{q}
is well-defined.
Moreover,

\operatorname{li}(x)\sum_{k_{1}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}+\operatorname{li}(x)\sum_{k_{2}>K}\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{2},k_{2}}\leqslant\frac{8\operatorname{li}(x)}{q-1}\sum_{k>\log 2x/\log q}\frac{1}{q^{k-1}}\leqslant 1

and then

\left|\pi_{q}(x)-\delta_{q}\operatorname{li}(x)\right|\ll x^{1/2}\log^{3}x.

Finally,
\delta_{q}\geqslant 0
since

\delta_{q}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\pi_{q}(x)}{\operatorname{li}(x)}\geqslant 0.

This completes the proof of the lemma when
q
splits in
\kappa
; the case in which
q
does not split is similar.
∎

We will need some control over the dependence of the leading constant
\delta_{q}
on
q
, which we achieve in the following result.

Lemma 5.3
.

There exists
\lambda>0
, depending only on
E
and
P
, such that

\delta_{q}>q^{-\lambda}

for all primes
q>3

of good reduction.

Proof.

We do the case
q
split, the other case being similar. Notice that

A_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}\subseteq\{p\leqslant x:p\text{ splits in }\kappa,q\mid m_{p}\},

and so
\delta_{q}\geqslant\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}
. Thus we just need to prove that
\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}\geqslant q^{-\lambda}
for some
\mathfrak{q}_{1}\mid q
and
k_{1}\geqslant 1
. By (
5.2
), we have

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{[\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}:\kappa]}-\frac{1}{[L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}:\kappa]}-\frac{1}{[\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}+1}}:\kappa]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q-1)q^{k_{1}-1}}-\frac{1}{[L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}:\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}][\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}:\kappa]}-\frac{1}{(q-1)q^{k_{1}}}.

If
L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}\neq\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}
, then
[L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}:\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k_{1}}}]\geqslant 2
and so

\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{1},k_{1}}\geqslant\frac{1}{(q-1)q^{k_{1}-1}}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{q^{k_{1}+1}}.

It is known that there exists a constant
M
(that depends only on
E
and
P
) such that for all prime
q
and
k\geqslant M
, we have
L_{q^{k}}\neq\kappa_{q^{k}}
(see
[
11
, Lemma 14]
. For a reference on this problem, see
[
18
, Section 6]
). Let
k\geqslant M
and assume that
L_{\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{k}}=\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{k}}
for
i=1,2
and
\mathfrak{q}_{1}\mathfrak{q}_{2}=q
. Then,

\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}^{k}}=\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k}}\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k}}=L_{\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{k}}L_{\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{k}}=L_{q^{k}},

contradiction. Hence,
L_{\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{k}}\neq\kappa_{\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{k}}
for
i
equal
1
or
2
. Therefore, for all
k\geqslant M
,

\delta_{\mathfrak{q}_{i},k}\geqslant\frac{1}{q^{k+1}}

and the lemma easily follows.
∎

We are finally ready to prove that the hypotheses
in Lemma
2.8
are valid for an EDS, under suitable assumptions.
Let
\lambda>0
be the constant appearing in the previous result.

Proposition 5.4
.

Assume that
E
has CM and assume GRH.
Let
q>3
be a
prime of good reduction. If
q
is large enough, then there exists
p\leqslant q^{3\lambda}
such that
q\mid m_{p}
.

Proof.

Let
x=q^{3\lambda}
. Lemma

5.3
implies that

\delta_{q}\operatorname{li}(x)>\frac{\operatorname{li}(x)}{q^{\lambda}}=\operatorname{li}(x)x^{-\frac{1}{3}}\gg\frac{x^{\frac{2}{3}}}{\log x}.

Hence Lemma
5.2
yields

\#\{p\leqslant x:p\text{ splits in }\kappa,q\mid m_{p}\}\gg\frac{x^{\frac{2}{3}}}{\log x}.

Therefore, if
q
is large enough, then

\#\{p\leqslant x:p\text{ splits in }\kappa,q\mid m_{p}\}>0.

∎

We finally achieved the goal of this section, that was to show, under suitable assumptions, that elliptic divisibility sequences satisfy the hypothesis of Lemma
2.8
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
E
be a rational elliptic curve defined by a Weierstrass equation with integer coefficients. Let
P\in E(\mathbb{Q})
be a non-torsion point
and let
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the associated elliptic divisibility sequence. Assume that
E
has CM and that GRH holds.
Let
\Pi_{z}=\prod_{p<z}p
. Then,

\frac{1}{\log z}\ll\frac{\varphi(m_{\Pi_{z}})}{m_{\Pi_{z}}}\ll\frac{1}{\log z}.

Proof.

Follows from Lemma
2.7
, Lemma
2.8
, and Proposition
5.4
.
∎

6.
Sieving on Koblitz primes

In this section we keep the focus on EDS and return to our argument in Section
3
, involving the Eratosthenes–Legendre sieve. However, rather than working with the set of all primes, we consider the effect of taking
\mathscr{P}
to be the set of primes
p
for which

E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
has prime order. The infinitude of this set is an open question.

Conjecture 6.1

(Koblitz conjecture)
.

Let
E
be a non-CM elliptic curve defined
over
\mathbb{Q}
, with conductor
N_{E}
, which is not
\mathbb{Q}
-isogenous to a curve with non-trivial
\mathbb{Q}
-torsion. Then there exists a
positive constant
C_{E}
, depending on
E
, such that

\#\left\{p\leqslant x:\text{$p\nmid N_{E}$ and $\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})$ is prime}\right\}\sim C_{E}\frac{x}{\log^{2}x},

as
x\to\infty

It follows from this conjecture that

(6.1)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\nmid N_{E}\\
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})\text{ is prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}<\infty.

The convergence of this sum has been established by Cojocaru
[
3
, Cor. 8]

for non-CM elliptic curves
E
over
\mathbb{Q}
,
under the assumption that GRH holds.

Let
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an EDS
associated to a non-CM elliptic curve
E
and a point
P\in E(\mathbb{Q})
of infinite order.
Let

A(N,z)=\left\{n\leqslant N:\gcd(b_{n},\Pi)=1\right\},

where
\Pi=\Pi(z,\mathscr{P})
is given by (
3.1
)
and

\mathscr{P}=\{p:\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})\text{ is prime}\}.

If
\mathscr{P}
is empty, then trivially
\#A(N,z)=N
. Since the goal of this section is to estimate
\#A(N,z)
, we shall assume that
\mathscr{P}
is not empty (which follows if we assume the Koblitz conjecture).
We begin by establishing the following result.

Lemma 6.2
.

Assume that Conjecture
6.1
or GRH holds.
Then there exists a decreasing function

h(z):\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 2}\to(0,1]
with
\lim_{z\to\infty}h(z)=\delta\in(0,1)
, such that

\#A(N,z)=Nh(z)+O(2^{\omega(\Pi)}).

Proof.

In view of Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to study
M(\Pi)
.
Lemma
2.6
yields

M(\Pi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\mid m_{\Pi}\\
\gcd(b_{j},\Pi)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\varphi(m_{\Pi}/j)}{m_{\Pi}}.

Let
j\mid m_{\Pi}
. If
j\neq 1
, then
m_{p}\mid j
for at least one
p\mid\Pi
, since
m_{p}
is prime for each
p\in\mathscr{P}
. Thus
p\mid b_{j}
and then
\gcd(b_{j},\Pi)\neq 1
. Let
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
be a subset of primes such that
for each
m\in\{m_{p}:p\in\mathscr{P}\}
there exists a unique
p^{\prime}\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
for which

m_{p^{\prime}}=m
. Then

M(\Pi)=\frac{\varphi(m_{\Pi})}{m_{\Pi}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant z\\
p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{m_{p}}\right).

Let

h(z)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant z\\
p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{m_{p}}\right).

If the set
\{p\leqslant z:p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\}
is empty, we put
h(z)=1
. Notice that, for
z
large enough, the set is not empty and then
h(z)<1
.
The function
h
is clearly decreasing, positive, and satisfies
h(z)\leqslant 1
for all
z
.
We may write

h(z)=\exp\left(\log\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant z\\
p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{m_{p}}\right)\right)\right)=\exp\left(-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant z\\
p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{nm_{p}^{n}}\right).

It follows from the Hasse bound that
m_{p}\geqslant p/2
, whence

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant z\\
p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\geqslant 2}\frac{1}{nm_{p}^{n}}=O\left(1\right).

Furthermore, it follows from (
6.1
) that the sum

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant z\\
p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{p}}

has a limit as
z\to\infty
. So,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant z\\
p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{nm_{p}^{n}}=O(1).

The statement of the lemma follows.
∎

Conditionally under
Conjecture
6.1
or GRH, we are now ready to prove that a positive proportion of elements of the sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are devoid of primes small prime factors
p
for which
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is prime.

Theorem 6.3
.

Assume that
E
is a non-CM elliptic curve.
Assume that Conjecture
6.1
or GRH holds, and let

\mathscr{P}=\{p:\#E(\mathbb{F}_{p})\text{ is prime}\}
. Then
there exists
\delta\in(0,1)
such that

\#\{n\leqslant N:(\text{$p\mid b_{n}$ and $p\in\mathscr{P}$})\implies p>\log N\}\sim\delta N,

as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

We take
z=\log N
, noting that

\#\{p\in\mathscr{P}:p\leqslant z\}\ll\frac{\log N}{\log\log N},

whence
2^{\omega(\Pi_{z})}\ll N^{1/\log\log N}
.
Given
\varepsilon>0
, it follows from Lemma
6.2
that

N(\delta-\varepsilon)-2^{\omega(\Pi_{z})}<\#A(N,z)<N(\delta+\varepsilon)+2^{\omega(\Pi_{z})},

if
z
is sufficiently large in terms of
\varepsilon
.
The statement of the theorem follows.
∎

Appendix A
By Sandro Bettin

On the natural density of the product of a divisor closed set

and the set of primes

Given
\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathscr{A}\cdot\mathscr{B}:=\{ab\mid a\in\mathscr{A},b\in\mathscr{B}\}
. Also, for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, let
\mathbb{P}_{m}:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid\omega(n)\leqslant m\}
, where
\omega(n)=\sum_{p|n}1
. Finally, we say that
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is divisor closed if whenever
n\in\mathscr{A}
and
d|n
one has
d\in\mathscr{A}
. Notice that if
\mathscr{A}
is divisor closed then so is
\mathscr{A}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{m}
for all
m
.

The goal of this appendix is to prove the following result.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
m\geqslant 0
. If
\mathscr{B}
is a divisor closed set of density
0
, then also
\mathscr{B}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{m}
has density
0
.

Remark A.2
.

The theorem does not hold without the assumption that
\mathscr{B}
is divisor closed. Indeed, if
q_{N}
is a sequence of primes going sufficiently slowly to infinity with
N
, then
\mathscr{B}=\cup_{N}\{n\in(e^{(N-1)^{2}},e^{N^{2}}]\mid n\equiv 1\ ({\rm mod\ }q_{N})\}
has density
0
, but
\mathscr{B}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{1}
has density
1
. This can be proved easily using the fundamental lemma of sieve theory. We leave the details to the interested reader.

A set
\mathscr{B}
is divisor closed if and only if its complement
\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\mathscr{B}
is a set of multiples, where we remind that, given a (finite or infinite) sequence
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, the set of multiples of
\mathscr{A}
is
\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}):=\{da\mid d\in\mathbb{N},a\in\mathscr{A}\}
.
We say that a sequence
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is a Behrend sequence if
\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A})
has density
1
.
We also let
{\bf t}({\mathscr{A}})=1-\delta(\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}))
, with
\delta
denoting the logarithmic density. We state two well-known results on Behrend sequences (see
[
2
, (0.68) and Corollary 0.14]
).

Lemma A.3
.

We have that
\mathscr{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is a Behrend sequence if and only if
{\bf t}(\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. If
\mathscr{A}\cup\mathscr{B}
is a Behrend sequence, then so is at least one of
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
.

Ruzsa and Tenenbaum
[
1
, Theorem 2]
showed that any Behrend sequence can be split into an infinite disjoint union of Behrend sequences. A modification of their proof gives the following result (see also
[
2
, Corollary 0.15]
).

Proposition A.5
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a Behrend sequence. Then
\mathscr{A}
contains infinitely many disjoint Behrend sequences
\mathscr{A}_{1},\mathscr{A}_{2},\dots
such that
(a,b)=1
for all
a\in\mathscr{A}_{i}
,
b\in\mathscr{A}_{j}
with
i\neq j
.

Proof.

We start with the observation that if
\mathscr{B}
is a Behrend sequence and
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is a finite set then
\mathscr{B}[S]:=\{b\in\mathscr{B}\mid(b,s)=1\ \forall s\in S\}
is a Behrend sequence. This follows immediately from Lemma
A.4
since
\mathscr{M}(S)
has density smaller than
1
.

Let
\mathscr{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots\}
and let
\mathscr{B}_{1}:=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n_{1}}\}
with
n_{1}
such that
{\bf t}({\mathscr{B}_{1}})<1/2
, as possible by Lemma
A.3
. Also, let
\mathscr{A}^{(1)}:=\mathscr{A}[\mathscr{B}_{1}]=\{a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},\dots\}
, which is a Behrend set by the above observation. Next, we let
\mathscr{B}_{2}:=\{a^{\prime}_{1},\dots,a^{\prime}_{n_{2}}\}
, with
n_{2}
such that
{\bf t}({\mathscr{B}_{2}})<1/3
, and
\mathscr{A}^{(2)}:=\mathscr{A}^{(1)}[\mathscr{B}_{2}]
. Repeating this process we define sequences
\mathscr{B}_{1},\mathscr{B}_{2},\dots
such that
{\bf t}({\mathscr{B}_{j}})<1/(j+1)
for all
j\geqslant 1
. By construction
(b_{i},b_{j})=1
if
b_{i}\in\mathscr{B}_{i},b_{j}\in\mathscr{B}_{j}
with
i\neq j
. In order to conclude it suffices to let
\mathscr{A}_{i}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathscr{N}_{i}}\mathscr{B}_{n}
, where
\mathscr{N}_{1},\mathscr{N}_{2},\dots
is any sequence of disjoint infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
. Indeed,
{\bf t}({\mathscr{A}_{i}})\leqslant\inf_{n\in\mathscr{N}_{i}}(1/(1+n))=0
and thus
\mathscr{A}_{i}
is a Behrend sequence by Lemma
A.3
.
∎

Remark A.6
.

In general it is not possible to choose the sequences
\mathscr{A}_{i}
so that
\bigcup_{i}\mathscr{A}_{i}=\mathscr{A}
. For example, this is clearly not possible in the case

\mathscr{A}=\{p_{1}p_{2}\mid p_{1},p_{2}\text{ distinct primes}\}.

Given a sequence
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, we let

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{*}:=\{[a_{1},\dots,a_{m}]\,\mid m\geqslant 2,\ a_{i}\in\mathscr{A},\ (a_{1},\dots,a_{m})=1\}.

From Proposition
A.5
one immediately obtains the following corollary, which would hold also if we fix
m
to be any integer greater than or equal to
2
.

Corollary A.7
.

If
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is a Behrend sequence, then so is
\mathscr{A}^{*}
.

Lemma A.8
.

We have that
\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}^{*})=(\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}))^{*}
. In particular, if
\mathscr{A}
is the set of multiples of some set, then so is
\mathscr{A}^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}^{*})
. Thus
n=\ell\cdot[a_{1},\dots,a_{m}]
with
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geqslant 2
,
a_{i}\in{\mathscr{A}}
and
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})=1
. For any prime
p
, let
j_{p}\in\{1,\dots,m\}
be such that
v_{p}(a_{j_{p}})=v_{p}([a_{1},\dots,a_{m}])
, with
v_{p}
denoting the
p
-adic valuation, and let

a_{j}^{\prime}:=a_{j}\prod_{p|\ell,j_{p}=j}p^{v_{p}(\ell)}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A})
for
j=1,\dots,m
. Then,
n=[a^{\prime}_{1},\dots,a^{\prime}_{m}]
and
(a^{\prime}_{1},\dots,a^{\prime}_{m})=1
and so
n\in(\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}))^{*}
.

Vice versa, let
n\in(\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}))^{*}
. Then
n=[\ell_{1}a_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}a_{m}]
with
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geqslant 2
,
a_{i}\in{\mathscr{A}}
and
(\ell_{1}a_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}a_{m})=1
. Thus
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})=1
and
[a_{1},\dots,a_{m}]
divides
n
and so
n\in\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}^{*})
.
∎

Proposition A.9
.

Let
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}^{*})=(\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}))\cdot\mathbb{P}_{1}.

Proof.

We can assume
1\notin\mathscr{A}
since otherwise
\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A})=\mathbb{N}
and the claim is trivial.

Let
n\in(\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}))\cdot\mathbb{P}_{1}
so that there exist a prime
p
and
r\geqslant 0
such that
p^{r}|n
and
n/p^{r}\notin\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A})
. If we had
n\in\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}^{*})
, then by Lemma
A.8
we would have
n=[a_{1},\dots,a_{m}]
for some
m\geqslant 2
and
a_{i}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A})
with
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})=1
.
Since
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})=1
, then there exists
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
such that
p\nmid a_{j}
. In particular,
a_{j}
divides
n/p^{r}
and so
n/p^{r}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A})
, which is a contradiction. Thus,
n\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}^{*})
.

Now, let
n\notin(\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}))\cdot\mathbb{P}_{1}
. Since
1\notin\mathscr{A}
then
(\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}))\cdot\mathbb{P}_{1}
contains the prime powers. In particular, the prime factor decomposition of
n
,
n=p_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots p_{m}^{r_{m}}
, has

m\geqslant 2
.
By hypothesis for all
j=1,\dots,m
we have
n/p_{j}^{r_{j}}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A})
. Also, clearly
n=[n/p_{1}^{r_{1}},\dots,n/p_{m}^{r_{m}}]
with
(n/p_{1}^{r_{1}},\dots,n/p_{m}^{r_{m}})=1
and thus
n\in(\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}))^{*}=\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A}^{*})
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
A.1
.

We have
\mathbb{P}_{m}=\mathbb{P}_{1}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{m-1}
for
m\geqslant 1
and thus, since
\mathscr{B}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{1}
is also divisor closed if
\mathscr{B}
is, then by induction we can assume
m=1
.

Let
\mathscr{A}:=\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\mathscr{B}
. In particular,
\mathscr{A}
is a set of multiples of density
1
and in fact
\mathscr{M}(\mathscr{A})=\mathscr{A}
, so that
\mathscr{A}
is a Behrend sequence. The theorem then follows by Corollary
A.7
and Proposition
A.9
.
∎
