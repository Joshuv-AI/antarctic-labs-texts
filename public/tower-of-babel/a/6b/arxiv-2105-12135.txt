Gravitational wave echo of relaxion trapping
Abhishek Banerjee, Eric Madge, Gilad Perez, Wolfram Ratzinger, Pedro Schwaller

Abstract
To solve the hierarchy problem, the relaxion must remain trapped in the correct minimum, even if the electroweak symmetry is restored after reheating. In this scenario, the relaxion starts rolling again until the backreaction potential, with its set of local minima, reappears. Depending on the time of barrier reappearance, Hubble friction alone may be insufficient to retrap the relaxion in a large portion of the parameter space. Thus, an additional source of friction is required, which might be provided by coupling to a dark photon. The dark photon experiences a tachyonic instability as the relaxion rolls, which slows down the relaxion by backreacting to its motion, and efficiently creates anisotropies in the dark photon energy-momentum tensor, sourcing gravitational waves. We calculate the spectrum of the resulting gravitational wave background from this new mechanism, and evaluate its observability by current and future experiments. We further investigate the possibility that the coherently oscillating relaxion constitutes dark matter and present the corresponding constraints from gravitational waves.

The weakness of the gravitational force ensures that gravitational waves (GWs) can store information about the history of our Universe.
On the other hand, though, it also implies that only dramatic and cosmological events may leave imprints that can be observed at present or in the near future.
GW detectors are, for instance, sensitive to primordial phase transitions, inflation, and oscillatory motion of ultralight fields (see, e.g., Refs.

In this work, we introduce a different mechanism that leads to the production of GWs in the early Universe.
In the following, we demonstrate the idea using the relaxion framework
Graham et al. 2015

At early times, the relaxion is trapped in a local minimum.
However, assuming that the reheating temperature is above the electroweak (EW) scale,

II
Setup

In this work, we consider the relaxion
\phi
coupled to a dark photon field
X_{\mu}
,

-\mathcal{L}\supset V(H,\phi)+\frac{r_{X}}{4}\,\frac{\phi}{f_{\phi}}\,X_{\mu\nu}\widetilde{X}^{\mu\nu}\,,

(3)

with the potential of the relaxion field
\phi
and Higgs doublet
H
given by

V(H,\phi)=V_{\text{roll}}(\phi)+\mu_{H}^{2}(\phi)|H|^{2}+\lambda|H|^{4}+V_{\text{br}}(H,\phi)\,,

(4)

where
\lambda
is the Higgs quartic coupling and

\displaystyle V_{\text{roll}}(\phi)

\displaystyle=-cg\Lambda^{3}\phi\,,

(5a)

\displaystyle\mu_{H}^{2}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{2}-g\Lambda\phi\,,

(5b)

\displaystyle V_{\text{br}}(H,\phi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Lambda_{\text{br}}^{4}}{v_{H}^{2}}|H|^{2}\cos\frac{\phi}{f_{\phi}}\,.

(5c)

Here,
c
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
number,
g
is a dimensionless parameter,
\Lambda
is the Higgs mass cutoff scale,
\Lambda_{\text{br}}
is the backreaction scale,
v_{H}=\langle|H|\rangle=$174\text{\,}\mathrm{GeV}$
is the Higgs vacuum expectation value, and
f_{\phi}
is the decay constant of the relaxion.

During inflation, the relaxion rolls down the linear slope of its potential
V_{\text{roll}}
.
It thereby scans the Higgs mass parameter
\mu^{2}(\phi)
.
Once
\mu^{2}
crosses zero, the Higgs acquires a nonvanishing vacuum expectation value, triggering the breaking of the EW gauge symmetry.
The Higgs then backreacts creating wiggles in the relaxion potential via
V_{\text{br}}
.
Once the Higgs backreaction balances the rolling potential, the relaxion is trapped in the first minimum it encounters.
Choosing
cg\Lambda^{3}f_{\phi}\sim\Lambda_{\text{br}}^{4}
, we end up with a weak-scale expectation value for the Higgs boson, solving the hierarchy problem.
The relaxion mass and the relaxion-Higgs mixing angle can then be written as
Banerjee et al. 2020
;
Flacke et al. 2017

\displaystyle m_{\phi}^{2}\simeq\frac{\Lambda_{\rm br}^{6}}{f_{\phi}^{2}\Lambda v_{H}}\,,\quad\sin\theta_{h\phi}\simeq\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{m_{\phi}^{4}f_{\phi}\Lambda^{2}}{v_{H}\,m_{h}^{6}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\!,

(6)

in terms of the theory parameters.
Here,
m_{h}=$125\text{\,}\mathrm{GeV}$
is the Higgs mass.

II.1
Relaxion and dark photon evolution

After reheating, the EW symmetry will be restored due to thermal corrections to the potential, provided that the reheating temperature is above the EW phase transition temperature.
As a consequence, the relaxion will start rolling again, leading to exponential production of dark photon modes.
To see the interplay, the coupled differential equations describing the evolution of the spatially homogeneous relaxion and the dark photon modes are given by

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime\prime}+2aH\theta^{\prime}+\frac{a^{2}}{f_{\phi}^{2}}\frac{\partial V_{\rm roll}}{\partial\theta}=-\frac{a^{2}}{f_{\phi}^{2}}\,\frac{r_{X}}{4\,a^{4}}\left<X_{\mu\nu}\tilde{X}^{\mu\nu}\right>,

(7)

\displaystyle X^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(k,\tau)+(k^{2}-\lambda\,k\,r_{X}\theta^{\prime})X_{\lambda}(k,\tau)=0\,,

(8)

where
\theta=\phi/f_{\phi}
and primes denote derivatives with respect to conformal time
\tau
with
a\,d\tau=dt
.
We have written the dark photon in terms of its Fourier modes
X_{\lambda}(\mathbf{k},\tau)
in Coulomb gauge,
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{X}=0
, as

\hat{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x},\tau)=\int\!\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum\limits_{\lambda=\pm}X_{\lambda}(k,\tau)\mathbf{\varepsilon}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{k})\hat{a}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{k})+\text{h.c.}

(9)

Here,
\lambda
denotes the dark photon helicity, and
\mathbf{\varepsilon}_{\lambda}
are the corresponding circular polarization vectors.

As evident from
Eq.

8
, dark photon modes with
0<k<\lambda r_{X}\theta^{\prime}
are tachyonic and will experience exponential growth compared to the vacuum fluctuations.
The resulting dark photon spectrum then features anisotropies in its energy-momentum tensor which will act as a source for GW production, leading to a stochastic GW background
Easther and Lim 2006
;
Dufaux et al. 2009
;
Adshead et al. 2018
;
Machado et al. 2019
;
Machado et al. 2020
;
Ratzinger et al. 2021
.
Furthermore, since only modes of the helicity with the same sign as the relaxion velocity can become tachyonic, the rolling relaxion will produce a circular polarized dark photon background.
In our case, as we choose
\theta^{\prime}>0
, only the positive-helicity modes are exponentially produced.
For these modes, the solution to the equations of motion is given in the Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin (WKB) approximation by

X_{+}(k,\tau)=\frac{e^{g(k,\tau)}}{\sqrt{2\,\Omega(k,\tau)}}\,,

(10)

where

\Omega^{2}(k,\tau)=k\,r_{X}|\theta^{\prime}(\tau)|-k^{2}>0
is the corresponding tachyonic frequency and
g(k,\tau)=\int^{\tau}\!\!d\tau^{\prime}\,\Omega(k,\tau^{\prime})
.
The approximation holds for
|\Omega^{\prime}/\Omega^{2}|\ll 1
.

At early times, just after reheating, the friction from dark photons can be neglected.
Assuming radiation domination, the relaxion velocity can then be written as

\theta^{\prime}(\tau)=\frac{\Lambda_{\rm br}^{4}}{5f_{\phi}^{2}}\left(\frac{a_{\text{rh}}}{\tau_{\text{rh}}}\right)^{2}\!\tau^{3}\left[1-\left(\frac{\tau_{\text{rh}}}{\tau}\right)^{5}\right]\,,

(11)

where we imposed
\theta^{\prime}(\tau_{\text{rh}})=0
.
Subsequently, due to the exponential production, the dark photon friction becomes comparable to the other terms in the equation of motion,
Eq.

7
, and asymptotes to the slope of the rolling potential.
The timescale
\tau_{\text{pp}}
at which particle production kicks in can be determined by

\frac{\langle X_{\mu\nu}\tilde{X}^{\mu\nu}\rangle(\tau_{\rm pp})}{4a^{4}(\tau_{\rm pp})}\approx\frac{\tilde{k}^{4}e^{2g(\tilde{k},\tilde{\tau})}}{4\pi^{2}a^{4}}\sim\frac{\Lambda_{\text{br}}^{4}}{r_{X}}\,,

(12)

where
\tilde{k}
is the mode that dominates the
\langle X\tilde{X}\rangle
term, given by the saddle point

\left.\partial g(k,\tau)/\partial k\right|_{\tilde{k}}=0
. After
\tau_{\text{pp}}
, due to the balance between the potential slope and the backreaction from the dark photon, the relaxion field velocity becomes proportional to the Hubble rate and evolves as
Choi et al. 2017
;
Banerjee et al. 2019

\theta^{\prime}(\tau)\approx\frac{\xi}{r_{X}}\,a(\tau)H(\tau)\left(1+\epsilon\log\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\text{pp}}}\right)\approx\frac{\xi}{r_{X}\tau}\,,

(13)

with a small logarithmic correction (
\epsilon\!\ll\!1
).
We defined here the parameter
\xi=\frac{r_{X}|\theta^{\prime}|}{aH}
at
\tau_{\text{pp}}
.
From
Eq.

12
we then obtain
\xi\sim\mathcal{O}(10-100)
with a mere logarithmic dependence on the relaxion parameters
Choi et al. 2017
.
The dominating
k
-mode at each epoch then becomes
\tilde{k}/a\sim r_{X}\dot{\theta}(t)\sim\xi H
.

Once the Universe has cooled sufficiently, the EW phase transition occurs, and the wiggles of the backreaction potential reappear.
The rolling of the relaxion between reheating and
T_{\text{ra}}
leads to a displacement from the minimum in which it originally settled during inflation by

\Delta\theta=\int\limits_{\tau_{\text{rh}}}^{\tau_{\text{ra}}}\!d\tau\,\theta^{\prime}\approx\frac{\xi}{4\,r_{X}}\left[1+\log\frac{H_{\text{pp}}^{2}}{H_{\text{ra}}^{2}}\right].

(14)

For the relaxion to remain trapped in this minimum, we need to require that the displacement is less than the distance between the minimum and the next maximum,
\Delta\theta\lesssim 2\delta
, where
\delta=\Lambda_{\text{br}}^{2}/(\Lambda v_{H})

Banerjee et al. 2019
;
Banerjee et al. 2020
.
This sets a lower bound on the coupling to the dark photons.
For smaller couplings, the dark photon friction is insufficient to prevent the relaxion from rolling into one of the neighbouring minima.
The relaxion then needs to traverse
\Delta\theta\sim\mathcal{O}(n)
to end up in the
n
-th minimum, where
n=1
denotes the minimum in which it stopped during inflation, extending the parameter space of the theory.
However, going beyond the first minimum requires a careful adjustment of the initial conditions
to let the relaxion stop exactly at the bottom of the
n
-th minimum at reappearance.
Otherwise, the time required for the relaxion to reach the bottom would exceed the age of the Universe.
We thus simply assume
r_{X}=\xi/(2\delta)
in the following.

3

\delta=\frac{\Lambda_{\text{br}}^{2}}{\Lambda v_{H}}<\frac{v_{H}}{\Lambda}

r_{X}\gtrsim 10^{2}\left(\frac{\Lambda}{$1\text{\,}\mathrm{TeV}$}\right)\left(\frac{\xi}{$10$}\right)

II.2
Relaxion dark matter

After the reappearance of the Higgs backreaction potential, the displaced relaxion begins to oscillate around the minimum of its potential, providing a candidate for ultralight DM as discussed in Ref.
Banerjee et al. 2019
.
Assuming the maximal displacement of
\Delta\theta=2\delta
, the relaxion relic abundance is given by

\Omega_{\phi}=\frac{2\,m_{\phi}^{2}f_{\phi}^{2}\delta^{2}}{3M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}H_{0}^{2}}\frac{g_{*s}(T_{0})T_{0}^{3}}{g_{*s}(T_{\text{osc}})T_{\text{osc}}^{3}}\,,

(15)

where
T_{\text{osc}}\sim\min[T_{\text{ra}},\sqrt{m_{\phi}M_{\text{Pl}}}]
is the temperature when the relaxion starts to oscillate.
Requiring that the relaxion provides all of DM, i.e.,
\Omega_{\phi}h^{2}=0.12

Aghanim et al. 2020

\displaystyle\!\!\!f_{\phi}\sim$5\text{\times}{10}^{9}\text{\,}\mathrm{GeV}$\left(\frac{\Lambda}{$1\text{\,}\mathrm{TeV}$}\right)^{\!\frac{2}{5}}\!\left(\frac{T_{\text{osc}}}{$1\text{\,}\mathrm{GeV}$}\right)^{\!\frac{9}{10}}\!\left(\frac{$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$}{m_{\phi}}\right)\!.

(16)

As we are considering coherently oscillating relaxion DM here, its mass needs to be less than approximately
10\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}
in order to be described by a classical field
Kolb and Turner 1990
.
For this range of relaxion masses, the possible decay channels are into two photons and two dark photons,
\Gamma_{\phi}=\Gamma_{\gamma\gamma}+\Gamma_{XX}
.
The decay rate into a dark photon pair is given by

\Gamma_{XX}=\frac{r_{X}^{2}}{64\pi}\frac{m_{\phi}^{3}}{f_{\phi}^{2}}\,,

(17)

while the decay width of
\phi\to\gamma\gamma
is subdominant compared to that of
\phi\to XX
as it is suppressed by the square of the relaxion-Higgs mixing angle
Banerjee et al. 2019
, which in turn is bounded from above by
\sin\theta_{h\phi}\lesssim v_{H}/f_{\phi}

Flacke et al. 2017
.
The relaxion lifetime hence becomes

\tau_{\phi}\sim$20\text{\,}\mathrm{Gyr}$\left(\frac{25}{\xi}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{T_{\text{osc}}}{$1\text{\,}\mathrm{GeV}$}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$}{m_{\phi}}\right)^{5},

(18)

where we have chosen

r_{X}=\xi/(2\delta)
.
Since the decay of DM into relativistic particles
affects the spectrum of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) at
low-
\ell
multipoles, the lifetime is constrained as
\tau_{\phi}>$160\text{\,}\mathrm{Gyr}$

Audren et al. 2014
.

As shown in Refs.
Agrawal et al. 2018
;
Kitajima et al. 2018a
;
Kitajima et al. 2021
;
Ratzinger et al. 2021
,
for

, an oscillating ALP may introduce a second phase of tachyonic dark photon production, which could suppress the DM abundance by up to two orders of magnitude. This condition is satisfied in parts of the parameter space where the field displacement is maximal, since there

II.3
Constraints

A successful cosmological relaxation of the Higgs mass requires
the inflation sector to dominate the total energy density,
3H_{\text{I}}^{2}M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}\gtrsim\Lambda^{4}
, as well as that the classical motion of the relaxion dominates over quantum fluctuation during inflation,
(\Delta\phi)_{\rm cl}\gtrsim H_{\rm I}/2\pi

Here,
H_{\text{I}}
is the Hubble scale during inflation.
Combining these two constraints, we get an upper bound on the cutoff scale
\Lambda

\displaystyle\Lambda\lesssim\left(\frac{2\pi\sqrt{3}\,M_{\text{Pl}}^{3}\Lambda_{\text{br}}^{4}}{f}\right)^{1/6}\!\!.

(19)

As we are considering a Higgs-dependent backreaction potential, we also require
\Lambda_{\text{br}}\lesssim v_{H}

Graham et al. 2015
;
Flacke et al. 2017

\displaystyle M_{\text{Pl}}\gtrsim f_{\phi}\gtrsim\Lambda\gtrsim 4\pi v_{H}\sim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{TeV}$\,.

\displaystyle v_{H}\gtrsim T_{\text{ra}}\gtrsim T_{\rm BBN}\sim$10\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}$\,.

For masses below the
\mathrm{eV}
scale, the relaxion can further mediate long-range forces.

Additional constraints arise when coupling the relaxion to a dark photon field (see

For the dark photon induced friction to trap the relaxion, reappearance has to occur sufficiently late for the dark photon to be produced, i.e.,

This sets a lower bound on the relaxion mass,
m_{\phi}\gtrsim\sqrt{10}\,\delta H_{\text{ra}}

If this condition is satisfied, throughout its evolution from reheating to reappearance, the relaxion continuously produces dark photons, depositing energy density into the latter.

\rho_{X}(t_{\rm ra})=\frac{1}{a^{4}(t_{\rm ra})}\int^{t_{\rm ra}}\!\!dt^{\prime}a^{4}(t^{\prime})\dot{V}=\frac{1}{2}\,m_{\phi}^{2}f_{\phi}^{2}\,.

For the Universe to remain radiation dominated, we require
\rho_{X}(T_{\text{ra}})
to be smaller than the radiation energy density at
T_{\text{ra}}
, i.e.
\rho_{X}(T_{\text{ra}})\lesssim 3M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}H_{\text{ra}}^{2}\sim T_{\text{ra}}^{4}
; otherwise, the relaxion dominates the total energy density of the Universe.
After
T_{\text{ra}}
, dark photon production stops, and the corresponding energy density redshifts as that of radiation.

The dark photon is further constrained by its contribution to the energy density of the Universe at low temperatures, typically parametrized in terms of the effective number of neutrino species
N_{\text{eff}}
.
For temperatures
T\lesssim m_{e}
, after electrons and positrons have annihilated, the dark photon energy density (or of any additional relativistic species for that matter) can be written in terms of its contribution
\Delta N_{\text{eff}}
to
N_{\text{eff}}
as

\rho_{X}(T)=\frac{7\,\pi^{2}}{120}\Delta N_{\text{eff}}\left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{\frac{4}{3}}T^{4},

(23)

where the
4/11
factor accounts for the difference between the neutrino and photon temperature.
Using
Eq.

22
and the current
95\text{\,}\%
C.L. limit
N_{\text{eff}}=3.27\pm 0.29
from CMB data
Aghanim et al. 2020
combined with local measurements of the Hubble rate
Riess et al. 2018
, as well as
N_{\text{eff}}^{\text{SM}}=3.046
in the Standard Model, we obtain a lower limit on the reappearance temperature as a function of the relaxion mass and decay constant

\displaystyle T_{\text{ra}}\gtrsim 2.5\,g_{*s,\text{ra}}^{-1/3}\,\sqrt{m_{\phi}f_{\phi}}\,,

(24)

where
g_{*s,\text{ra}}
is the number of entropic degrees of freedom at reappearance.

If we assume that
the relaxion accounts for the full DM abundance, then plugging
Eq.

16
to
Eq.

24
, we get,

T_{\text{ra}}\gtrsim$450\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}$\left(\frac{67}{g_{*s,\text{ra}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(\frac{\Lambda}{$10\text{\,}\mathrm{TeV}$}\right)^{\!\frac{4}{11}}.

(25)

Here, we also assume that the relaxion starts to oscillate at
T_{\text{ra}}
, which is only true for a sufficiently heavy relaxion.
As we require
\Lambda\gtrsim 4\pi v_{H}\sim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{TeV}$
, this sets a lower bound of
T_{\text{ra}}\gtrsim$240\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}$
on the reappearance temperature for the relaxion DM scenario to be realized.

III
Gravitational waves

Let us now consider the stochastic GW background generated from anisotropies in the energy-momentum tensor of the dark photon produced during the postinflationary evolution of the relaxion.
In particular, we focus here on the GWs sourced during the rolling of the relaxion between reheating and the EW phase transition.
Further gravitational radiation can be generated when the relaxion oscillates around the minimum of its potential, as explored in Refs.
Machado et al. 2019
;
Machado et al. 2020
;
Ratzinger et al. 2021
;
Salehian et al. 2021
;
Soda and Urakawa 2018
;
Kitajima et al. 2018b
;
Chatrchyan and Jaeckel 2021
in the context of general ALP models, or in a confining phase transition that generates the backreaction potential
Barducci et al. 2021
.
In combination, the various sources may lead to an interesting and rich GW spectrum with multiple peaks.

The spectrum of a stochastic background of GWs is characterized by its fractional energy density, i.e. the energy density normalized to the critical value,
\rho_{c}^{0}=3M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}H_{0}^{2}
, per logarithmic frequency interval,

\Omega_{\text{GW}}(f)=\frac{1}{\rho_{c}^{0}}\frac{d\rho_{\text{GW}}}{d\log f}\,,

(27)

where the total GW energy density is given by

\rho_{\text{GW}}=\frac{M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}}{4}\left<\dot{h}_{ij}\dot{h}^{ij}\right>=\int\!\frac{df}{f}\frac{d\rho_{\text{GW}}}{d\log f}\,.

(28)

Here,
h_{ij}
denotes the GW metric perturbations, and the dot indicates the derivative with respect to cosmic time
t
.
Switching to conformal time
\tau
,
dt=a\,d\tau
, where
a
is the scale factor of the Universe, the metric reads

ds^{2}=a(\tau)\left\{d\tau^{2}-\right[\delta_{ij}+h_{ij}(\mathbf{x},\tau)\,\left]dx^{i}dx^{j}\right\}\,.

(29)

During radiation domination, the Einstein equations in the linear regime for the metric perturbations in momentum space using transverse-traceless gauge become

(\partial_{\tau}^{2}+k^{2})\,a(\tau)\,h_{ij}(\mathbf{k},\tau)=\frac{2\,a(\tau)}{M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}}\Pi_{ij}(\mathbf{k},\tau)\,,

(30)

where
k=|\mathbf{k}|
is the comoving wave number.
The anisotropic stress tensor
\Pi_{ij}
relates to the energy-momentum tensor
T_{ij}
, via

\Pi_{ij}(\mathbf{k},\tau)=\Lambda_{ij}^{ab}(\mathbf{k})T_{ab}(\mathbf{k},\tau)
,
where
\Lambda^{ab}_{ij}=P_{i}^{\,a}P_{j}^{\,b}-\frac{1}{2}P_{ij}P^{ab}
with
P_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-k_{i}k_{j}/k^{2}
being the projector that extracts the transverse and traceless part
Caprini and Figueroa 2018
.
The equations of motion are then solved by (neglecting the
a^{\prime\prime}
term which vanishes in a radiation-dominated universe i.e. for
a\propto\tau
)

\hat{h}_{ij}(\mathbf{k},\tau)=\frac{2}{M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}}\,\int^{\tau}\!\!\!d\tau^{\prime}\,\frac{a(\tau^{\prime})}{a(\tau)}\,\hat{\Pi}_{ij}(\mathbf{k},\tau)\,\mathcal{G}(k,\tau,\tau^{\prime})\,,

(31)

where
\mathcal{G}(k,\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\sin[k(\tau-\tau^{\prime})]/k
is the causal Green’s function.
We denote here the operator form of any quantity
Q
by
\hat{Q}
.

III.1
Gravitational wave production

The GW energy density per logarithmic interval in the comoving momentum
k
of a generic stochastic source at conformal time
\tau
is given by
Caprini and Figueroa 2018

\frac{d\rho_{\text{GW}}}{d\log k}(k,\tau)=\frac{k^{3}}{4\pi^{2}M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}a^{4}(\tau)}\int\limits_{\tau_{i}}^{\tau}\!\!d\tau^{\prime}\!\!\int\limits_{\tau_{i}}^{\tau}\!\!d\tau^{\prime}a(\tau^{\prime})\,a(\tau^{\prime\prime})\,\times\\
\cos[k(\tau^{\prime}-\tau^{\prime\prime})]\,\Pi^{2}(k,\tau^{\prime},\tau^{\prime\prime})\,,

(32)

where
\tau_{i}
is the time at which the GW source starts operating and
\Pi^{2}(k,\tau^{\prime},\tau^{\prime\prime})
is the unequal time correlator of the anisotropic stress, defined as

\langle 0|\hat{\Pi}^{ab}(\mathbf{k},\tau)\hat{\Pi}^{\ast}_{ab}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})|0\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\Pi^{2}(k,\tau,\tau^{\prime})
.
In our case, the GWs are generated between reheating and reappearance; hence
\tau_{i}=\tau_{\text{rh}}
and
\tau\leq\tau_{\text{ra}}
.
As the GWs produced before the relaxion reaches its terminal velocity will, however, be subdominant, we can take
\tau_{i}=\tau_{\text{pp}}
, so to first approximation, the GW signature becomes independent of the temperature to which the Universe was reheated.

The dark photon anisotropic stress sourcing the GWs can be written in terms of the dark electric and magnetic fields as

\hat{\Pi}_{ab}(\mathbf{k},\tau)=-\frac{\Lambda_{ab}^{ij}(\mathbf{k})}{a^{2}(\tau)}\int\!\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\big[\hat{E}_{i}(\mathbf{q},\tau)\hat{E}_{j}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q},\tau)\,+\\
\hat{B}_{i}(\mathbf{q},\tau)\hat{B}_{j}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q},\tau)\big]\,.

(33)

Focusing on the dominant modes which have completed their phase of maximal tachyonic growth,
q\gtrsim r_{X}|\theta^{\prime}|/2
,
we find that GWs with momentum
k
are dominantly produced at the time of reappearance.
For frequencies below the peak, both dark photon modes in
Eq.

33
have momenta close to the one that experiences maximal growth at reappearance,
|\mathbf{q}|\sim|\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}|\sim r_{X}|\theta^{\prime}_{\text{ra}}|/2
, whereas above the peak, one of the contributing modes must have a larger momentum and therefore have exited the tachyonic band before reappearance.
Details of the calculation are deferred to
Appendix

C
.
The present-day GW spectrum can then be written as

\Omega_{\text{GW}}(f)=\Omega_{\text{GW}}^{\text{peak}}\,\mathcal{S}_{\xi}\left({f}/{f_{\text{peak}}}\right)

(34)

with the peak frequency

f_{\text{peak}}=\frac{k_{\text{ra}}}{2\pi\,a_{0}}=\frac{r_{X}|\theta^{\prime}_{\text{ra}}|}{2\pi\,a_{0}}=\frac{a_{\text{ra}}}{a_{0}}\frac{\xi\,H_{\text{ra}}}{2\pi}\,,

(35)

where
k_{\text{ra}}
is the mode that exits the tachyonic band at reappearance, and the peak amplitude

\!\Omega_{\text{GW}}^{\text{peak}}=\frac{1}{\rho_{c}^{0}}\,\frac{a_{\text{ra}}^{4}}{a_{0}^{4}}\,\frac{1}{M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}H_{\text{ra}}^{2}}\,\frac{\Lambda_{\text{br}}^{8}}{4\,r_{X}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\rho_{c}^{0}}\,\frac{a_{\text{ra}}^{4}}{a_{0}^{4}}\,\frac{m_{\phi}^{4}f_{\phi}^{4}}{M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}\xi^{2}H_{\text{ra}}^{2}}\,.\!

(36)

We used
Eq.

13
here as well as
\xi\simeq 2\delta r_{X}
and
\Lambda_{\text{br}}^{4}=m_{\phi}^{2}f_{\phi}^{2}/\delta
to recast
Eqs.

35
and
36
into the estimations in
Eqs.

1
and
2
.
The spectral shape
\mathcal{S}_{\xi}
is given by

\mathcal{S}_{\xi}(x)=\frac{1}{1+\frac{48}{5}(x-1)^{4}+\frac{$19\,965$}{256\,\xi^{2}}\left(x^{-3}+3\,x-4\right)}\,.

(37)

Note that, similar to the oscillating axion case
Machado et al. 2019
;
Machado et al. 2020
;
Salehian et al. 2021
;
Ratzinger et al. 2021
, we obtain a GW spectrum with an unpolarized low-frequency tail and a chiral spectrum above the peak.

III.2
GW detection

Having predicted the GW spectrum generated by the relaxion dynamics, we can now evaluate its detectability in current and future experiments.
A stochastic GW background can be detected in a given experiment if its signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
\varrho
exceeds a threshold value
\varrho_{\text{thr}}
.
The SNR is given by
Thrane and Romano 2013

\varrho^{2}=T_{\text{obs}}\!\!\int\limits_{f_{\text{min}}}^{f_{\text{max}}}\!\!\!df\left[\frac{\Omega_{\text{GW}}(f)}{\Omega_{n}(f)}\right]^{2}\,,

(38)

where
T_{\text{obs}}
is the period of observation,
(f_{\text{min}},f_{\text{max}})
is the frequency range of the detector, and
\Omega_{n}(f)
is the detector’s noise spectrum converted to fractional energy density.
For a cross-correlated measurement in a network of detectors, as, for instance, a pulsar timing array (PTA), the noise spectrum has to be replaced by the effective noise
\Omega_{\text{eff}}
of the network (see Ref.
Thrane and Romano 2013
for further details), and the SNR is given by
Eq.

38
with an additional factor of 2.

We present here the projected sensitivities of the planned space-based Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA)
Amaro-Seoane et al. 2017
;
Robson et al. 2019
,
the planned Square-Kilometre Array (SKA)
Janssen et al. 2015
PTA observatory,
6
6

6

We assume here that the prospective foreground from supermassive black hole binaries can be subtracted.
Further details on the sensitivities can be found in Ref.
Breitbach et al. 2019
.

and the proposed microhertz experiment
\mu
Ares
Sesana et al. 2019
.
In addition, we evaluate current limits from the NANOGrav 11 year dataset
Arzoumanian et al. 2018
.
Other GW observatories such as ground-based interferometers or potential LISA-successor experiments in the decihertz regime do not cover the frequency range in which a signal is expected in our scenario.

Recently, NANOGrav has further reported strong evidence for a common-spectrum stochastic process across the pulsars in their 12.5 year dataset
Arzoumanian et al. 2020
, which might be due to a GW background.
However, a GW detection could not be established yet, due to the lack of conclusive evidence regarding the interpulsar correlations of this process.
Nonetheless, we also present here an interpretation of this signal as the GW background generated in our model, fitting our signal to the NANOGrav data based on the procedure outlined in Ref.
Ratzinger and Schwaller 2021
.

Furthermore, GWs contribute to the total amount of radiation in the Universe and are therefore subject to constraints on
N_{\text{eff}}
.
This sets an upper bound on the peak amplitude of the spectrum.
Using
Eq.

23
, we obtain

h^{2}\Omega_{\text{GW}}^{\text{peak}}\leq\frac{a_{\text{rec}}^{4}}{a_{0}^{4}}\frac{7\pi^{2}}{120}\left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{\frac{4}{3}}\frac{T_{\text{rec}}^{4}}{\rho_{c}^{0}/h^{2}}\frac{\Delta N_{\text{eff}}}{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}(\xi)}\approx${10}^{-6}$,\!

(39)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}(\xi)=\int\!d\log x\,\mathcal{S}_{\xi}(x)\simeq$0.81.5$
is the integral over the spectral shape and
T_{\text{rec}}
is the photon temperature at recombination.

An even stronger bound is obtained from the contribution of the dark photons to
\Delta N_{\text{eff}}
. Since the GWs are sourced by dark photon anisotropies, this directly leads to an upper bound on the GW amplitude which is stronger than the one from the direct contribution of the GWs to
\Delta N_{\text{eff}}
.
Plugging
Eq.

24
into
Eq.

36
, we obtain

h^{2}\Omega_{\text{GW}}^{\text{peak}}\lesssim${10}^{-8}$\left(\frac{g_{\ast,\text{ra}}}{106.75}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{3}}\left(\frac{25}{\xi}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta N_{\text{eff}}^{X}}{0.52}\right)^{2},

(40)

where the superscript
X
indicates that this is the dark photon contribution to
\Delta N_{\text{eff}}
. A similar constraint on the GW amplitude was already observed in Ref.
Ratzinger and Schwaller 2021
in the audible axion scenario. There, it was noted that the parameter region that provides the best fit to the stochastic GW background that was potentially observed by the NANOGrav collaboration
Arzoumanian et al. 2020
is in tension with constraints on
\Delta N_{\text{eff}}
.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we have considered the possibility of probing the relaxion, which was proposed to ameliorate the Higgs hierarchy problem, via gravitational waves.
A coupling to dark photons tames the relaxion excursion after reheating and thus in turn opens up a large fraction of the parameter space which was excluded in the minimal scenario without dark photons.
Furthermore, dark photon production after reheating can act as a source for the generation of a stochastic gravitational wave background.
The gravitational waves are sourced by the anisotropies that are induced by the tachyonic production of dark photons
between the electroweak phase transition and big bang nucleosynthesis.
Hence, instead of the inflationary dynamics responsible for solving the hierarchy problem, we are probing here the late-time dynamics of the relaxion.

We have shown that this stochastic gravitational wave background can be probed by various current (NANOGrav) and future (SKA and
\mu
Ares) gravitational wave detectors.
In addition, we also highlight the parameter range in which our gravitational wave signal can account for the common-spectrum process observed in the most recent NANOGrav data
Arzoumanian et al. 2020
.
Alongside the existing theoretical constraints, we have presented the relaxion parameter space which can be detected or excluded by the gravitational wave observatories in
Fig.

1
.

We find that the spectral shape of the gravitational wave signal in our model falls as the fourth power of the frequency above the peak, unlike a steeper falling gravitational wave signals generated by other axionlike field dynamics
Machado et al. 2019
;
Machado et al. 2020
;
Ratzinger et al. 2021
;
Co et al. 2021
, whereas it behaves like
f^{3}
in the infrared, as expected based on causality arguments.
An observation of the spectrum in the range around the peak should allow for a determination of the reappearance temperature, while the amplitude can be used to determine the product of the relaxion mass and decay constant.

Furthermore, we have shown that the relaxion can constitute dark matter in the present Universe in the mass range of
${10}^{-11}\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$\lesssim m_{\phi}\lesssim${10}^{-6}\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$
and
${10}^{-2}\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$\lesssim m_{\phi}\lesssim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$
.
While this scenario cannot be constrained with current NANOGrav data, most of the dark matter parameter space will be accessible by SKA and/or
\mu
Ares in the future.
Hence, with the advent of gravitational wave astronomy, we are now facing promising prospects for probing the relaxation of the electroweak scale via the stochastic gravitation wave background generated when stopping the relaxion, independently of whether the relaxion constitutes dark matter or not.

Appendix B
Dark photon spectrum

As discussed in
Section

II.1
, since
\theta^{\prime}>0
in our case, positive-helicity dark photon modes with
k<k_{\text{ex}}=r_{X}\theta^{\prime}
are tachyonic and experience exponential growth, whereas modes outside the tachyonic band oscillate.
In particular, the amplitude of the oscillating modes does not change in time.
At any time, the peak of the dark photon spectrum is given by the mode that experiences maximal growth with
k=k_{\text{m}}(\tau)=|r_{X}\theta^{\prime}|/2
.
We therefore take the ansatz

X_{+}(k,\tau)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}_{k}\cos\left(k\tau-\xi\right)&\text{for}\ k_{\text{m}}<k<k_{\text{pp}},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(45)

where we neglect the negative-helicity modes as well as all modes that did not experience maximal growth yet, since these are exponentially suppressed.
We treat modes with
k_{\text{ex}}<k<k_{\text{m}}
like the oscillating modes in order to include the peak of the spectrum in our estimate.

Neglecting the energy produced before particle production, the dark photon energy density is given by

\rho_{X}=\int\limits_{k_{\text{m}}}^{k_{\text{pp}}}\!\frac{dk\,k^{2}}{4\pi^{2}a^{4}}\left(|X^{\prime}|^{2}+k^{2}|X|^{2}\right)=\!\int\limits_{k_{\text{m}}}^{k_{\text{pp}}}\!\!\frac{dk}{k}\,\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{k}|^{2}k^{5}}{4\pi^{2}a^{4}},

(46)

where
k_{\text{pp}}=k_{\text{ex}}(\tau_{\text{pp}})
is the mode that exits the tachyonic band at particle production.
On the other hand, we can determine the energy density spectrum of the dark photons from the relaxion energy density as

\displaystyle\rho_{X}

\displaystyle=-\!\int\limits_{\tau_{\text{pp}}}^{\tau}\!d\eta\,\frac{a^{4}(\eta)}{a^{4}(\tau)}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}\,\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\eta}\approx\int\limits_{k_{\text{m}}}^{k_{\text{pp}}}\!\frac{dk}{k}\,\frac{k_{\text{m}}^{4}}{k^{4}}\frac{\xi\Lambda_{\text{br}}^{4}}{r_{X}}\,,

(47)

where we assumed that at each time energy is dominantly transferred into the maximally growing mode, which goes like
k_{\text{m}}\propto 1/\tau
.
Hence, we can rewrite
\mathcal{A}_{k}=\mathcal{A}_{X}k^{-9/2}
with

\mathcal{A}_{X}=\frac{\pi}{2}\,\Lambda_{\text{br}}^{2}\,\sqrt{\frac{\xi}{r_{X}}}\,a_{\text{ra}}^{2}k_{\text{ra}}^{2}\,,

(48)

where
k_{\text{ra}}
is the mode that exits the tachyonic band at reappearance,
k_{\text{ra}}=\xi/\tau_{\text{ra}}
.
The dark photon spectrum therefore becomes

X_{+}(k,\tau)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}_{X}k^{-\frac{9}{2}}\cos\left(k\tau-\xi\right)&\text{for}\ k_{\text{m}}<k<k_{\text{pp}},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(49)

Appendix C
Calculation of the gravitational wave spectrum

The energy density of a stochastic GW background is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{GW}}(\mathbf{x},\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}}{4\,a^{2}(\tau)}\left<\hat{h}^{\prime}_{ab}(\mathbf{x},\tau)\hat{h}^{\prime ab}(\mathbf{x},t)\right>

(50)

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}}{8\pi^{2}a^{2}(\tau)}\int\!dk\,k^{2}\mathcal{P}_{h^{\prime}}(k,\tau)\,,

where
\mathcal{P}_{h^{\prime}}
is the power spectral density of
h^{\prime}_{ab}
defined by
\langle\hat{h}^{\prime ab}(\mathbf{k},\tau)\hat{h}^{\prime\ast}_{ab}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\tau)\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\mathcal{P}_{h^{\prime}}(k,\tau)
.
We hence obtain the GW spectrum

\frac{d\rho_{\text{GW}}}{d\log k}(k,\tau)=\frac{M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}k^{3}}{8\pi^{2}a^{2}(\tau)}\mathcal{P}_{h^{\prime}}(k,\tau)\,.

(51)

Using the solution
Eq.

31
, we obtain

\mathcal{P}_{h^{\prime}}(k,\tau)=\frac{2}{M_{\text{Pl}}^{4}a^{2}(\tau)}\int\limits_{\tau_{\text{pp}}}^{\tau}\!\!d\tau^{\prime}\!\!\int\limits_{\tau_{\text{pp}}}^{\tau}\!\!d\tau^{\prime\prime}\,\cos[k(\tau^{\prime}-\tau^{\prime\prime})]\,\times\\
a(\tau^{\prime})\,a(\tau^{\prime\prime})\,\Pi^{2}(k,\tau^{\prime},\tau^{\prime\prime})

(52)

with the unequal time correlator again defined as

\langle 0|\hat{\Pi}^{ab}(\mathbf{k},\tau)\hat{\Pi}^{\ast}_{ab}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})|0\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\Pi^{2}(k,\tau,\tau^{\prime})
,
where we averaged over one period in
\tau
to get rid of the
\cos[k(2\tau-\tau^{\prime}-\tau^{\prime\prime})]
part.

Inserting
Eqs.

33
and
10
into
Eqs.

52
and
51
and rewriting the dark electric and magnetic fields in terms of the dark photon modes,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{k},\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\lambda}X^{\prime}_{\lambda}(k,\tau)\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{k})\,\hat{a}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{k})+\text{h.c.},

(53a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{k},\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\lambda}\lambda\,k\,X_{\lambda}(k,\tau)\,\mathbf{\varepsilon}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{k})\,\hat{a}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{k})+\text{h.c.},

(53b)

we obtain the spectrum at the time of reappearance as

\frac{d\rho_{\text{GW}}}{d\log k}=\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}a_{\text{ra}}^{4}}\int\!\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,|\Sigma_{ab}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})|^{2}\times\\
\left(|I_{c}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})|^{2}+|I_{s}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})|^{2}\right)\,,

(54)

where we used

and the invariance of the integrand upon interchanging
\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}
.
We only considered here the positive helicity modes and defined

\Sigma_{ab}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})=\Lambda_{ab}^{ij}(\mathbf{k})\varepsilon_{i}^{+}(\mathbf{q})\varepsilon_{j}^{+}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})

as well as

I_{c/s}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})=-\int\limits_{\tau_{\text{pp}}}^{\tau_{\text{ra}}}\!\frac{d\tau}{a(\tau)}\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\cos(k\tau)\\
\sin(k\tau)\rule{0.0pt}{12.0pt}\end{array}\right\}\chi(q,l,\tau)

(55)

with
\chi(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q},\tau)=X_{+}^{\prime}(q,\tau)X_{+}^{\prime}(l,\tau)+qX_{+}(q,\tau)\,lX_{+}(l,\tau)

and

(56)

for

Expressing the momenta in terms of
k_{\text{ra}}

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho_{\text{GW}}}{d\log k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Lambda_{\text{br}}^{8}\xi^{2}}{r_{X}^{2}H_{\text{ra}}^{2}M_{\text{Pl}}^{2}}\ x^{3}\int\limits_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\infty}\!dr\int\limits_{-1}^{1}\!d\cos\theta\,\frac{|\Sigma_{ab}(x,r,\cos\theta)|^{2}}{512\,r^{5}s^{7}}\left(|\tilde{I}_{c}(x,r,s,\xi)|^{2}+|\tilde{I}_{s}(x,r,s,\xi)|^{2}\right)\ \Theta\left(s-1/2\right)\,,

where the remaining integrals only depend on

and
\xi

and
