Uniform twisted homological stability
Jeremy Miller, Peter Patzt, Dan Petersen, Oscar Randal-Williams

Abstract
. We prove a homological stability theorem for families of discrete groups (e.g. mapping class groups, automorphism groups of free groups, braid groups) with coefficients in a sequence of irreducible algebraic representations of arithmetic groups. The novelty is that the stable range does not depend on the degree of polynomiality of the coefficient system. Combined with earlier work of Bergström–Diaconu–Petersen–Westerland this proves the Conrey–Farmer–Keating–Rubinstein–Snaith predictions for all moments of the family of quadratic L L -functions over function fields, for sufficiently large odd prime powers.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Four instances of the main theorem

Recent work of
[
BDPW23
]
investigated connections between moments of families of quadratic
L
-functions over rational function fields, and stable homology of braid groups with twisted coefficients, proving in particular that an improved range of homological stability for the braid groups with these coefficients would imply a certain asymptotic formula for all moments. The goal of this paper is to prove such an improved stable range. In fact, the main result of the paper will be an abstract stability theorem applicable to multiple families of groups and coefficients. But rather than try to state the abstract form of the theorem in the introduction, we will give four important special cases. The hypotheses of the theorem are such that for all families of groups and coefficients to which the theorem applies, existing theorems in the literature already imply homological stability. What is new is the
shape
of the stable range.

It will be convenient to formulate our homological stability theorems in terms of vanishing of relative homology. Recall that if
H\to G
is a homomorphism, then vanishing of the relative homology group
H_{d}(G,H;\mathbb{Z})
for all
d\leq n
means exactly that
H_{n}(H;\mathbb{Z})\to H_{n}(G;\mathbb{Z})
is surjective, and
H_{d}(H;\mathbb{Z})\to H_{d}(G;\mathbb{Z})
is an isomorphism for
d<n
. The analogous statement is also true for homology with other coefficients.

1.1.1.
Mapping class groups

Let
\mathrm{Mod}_{g}^{1}
be the mapping class group of an oriented genus
g
surface with a boundary component, and consider the natural map
\smash{\mathrm{Mod}_{g}^{1}\to\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})}
. A partition
\lambda
is a descending integer sequence
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots
which eventually is constantly zero; we write
l(\lambda):=\max\{i:\lambda_{i}\neq 0\}
and
|\lambda|:=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
for the
length
and
size
of a partition. Irreducible algebraic representations of the symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}
are indexed by their highest weight, which is a partition
\lambda
of length
l(\lambda)\leq g
. For a partition
\lambda
, let
V_{\lambda}(2g)
be the irreducible rational representation of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}
of highest weight
\lambda
, if
l(\lambda)\leq g
, and set
V_{\lambda}(2g)=0
if
l(\lambda)>g
. We have a stabilisation map
\smash{\mathrm{Mod}_{g-1}^{1}\to\mathrm{Mod}_{g}^{1}}
given by gluing on a torus with two boundary components.

Harer
[
Har85
]
proved homological stability for the mapping class groups. Subsequent improvements were given in
[
Iva93
,
Bol12
]
, where stability was also proven for
polynomial coefficient systems
, such as
V_{\lambda}
(which is polynomial of degree
|\lambda|
). We prove the following stability theorem in this paper:

Theorem 1.1
.

The group
H_{d}(\mathrm{Mod}_{g}^{1},\mathrm{Mod}_{g-1}^{1};V_{\lambda}(2g),V_{\lambda}(2g-2))
vanishes for
d<\tfrac{1}{2}(g-\tfrac{1}{2}l(\lambda))
.

The best stable range known previously for these coefficients
[
GKRW19
, Example 5.20]
is that these groups vanish for
d<\tfrac{2}{3}(g-|\lambda|)
. Hence we prove a stable range with a worse slope, but with a much better dependence on
\lambda
. In particular, in the regime
|\lambda|\gg g
all previously known bounds gave no stable range at all.

Note in particular that if
l(\lambda)>g
, then
V_{\lambda}(2g)=V_{\lambda}(2g-2)=0
, so the vanishing in
1.1
holds for entirely trivial reasons. We therefore deduce that
H_{d}(\mathrm{Mod}_{g}^{1},\mathrm{Mod}_{g-1}^{1};V_{\lambda}(2g),V_{\lambda}(2g-2))=0
for
d<\tfrac{1}{4}g
, for
any
coefficient system

, which is now a
uniform
stable range.

1.1.2.
Automorphism groups of free groups

Let
\mathrm{Aut}(F_{n})

1.3.
Polynomiality

The standard approach to proving homological stability with twisted coefficients is to prove stability for
polynomial coefficient systems
, a notion going back to work of Dwyer
[
Dwy80
]
. The coefficient systems occurring in Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
,
1.3
, and
1.4
are all polynomial (of degree
|\lambda|
or
|\lambda|+|\mu|
, respectively), and the best previously known stable ranges quoted from the literature in connection with Theorems
1.1
–
1.4
are all valid more generally for any polynomial coefficient system. Now for a
general
polynomial coefficient system, easy examples show that the stable range must be allowed to depend on the degree of polynomiality; in the generality of arbitrary polynomial coefficients the traditional bounds are going to be sharp. The stable ranges obtained in Theorems
1.1
–
1.4
, with no dependence on the degree of polynomiality, shows that these specific coefficient systems defined by systems of irreducible representations are somehow rather special in the class of all polynomial coefficient systems.

Let us make the discussion in the previous paragraph more explicit.
We remind the reader that given a sequence of groups

\Gamma_{0}\to\Gamma_{1}\to\Gamma_{2}\to\Gamma_{3}\to\ldots

for which we want homological stability, a
coefficient system
consists of a sequence

V(0)\to V(1)\to V(2)\to V(3)\to\cdots

with each
V(n)
a
\Gamma_{n}
-module, with suitably equivariant stabilisation maps. The
shift
of
V
is the coefficient system defined by
(\Sigma V)(n)=V(n+1)
, and
V
is inductively defined to be
polynomial of degree

\leq r
if
V\to\Sigma V
is injective with cokernel polynomial of degree
\leq(r-1)
. The inductive definition of polynomiality makes it well suited for inductive arguments, and the standard proof of homological stability with polynomial coefficients goes by induction over the degree of polynomiality. The proof is a version of the
Quillen argument
, which is by far the most common technique for proving homological stability results, and in fact the definition of polynomiality almost appears tailor-made to slot neatly into the Quillen argument.

The stable ranges obtained from the standard Quillen argument with polynomial coefficients are of the form
4
4

4

If the coefficient system is
split
then one can improve this to
A\cdot(n-r)-B
. This is better since, without additional bells and whistles, one always has
A\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
.

H_{d}(\Gamma_{n},\Gamma_{n-1};V(n),V(n-1))=0\qquad\text{for }d<A\cdot n-B-r,

where
A
and
B
are constants, and
r
is the degree of polynomiality. In particular, the stable range depends on
r
, and in a situation where
n
and
r
go to infinity at the same rate the stable range may effectively become zero. This is not a bug, or an artifact of a poorly constructed argument — in general, it is simply not possible to do better:

Example 1.7
.

Let
\Gamma_{n}=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the symmetric group on
n
letters, and let
V(n)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n-r}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\mathbb{Z}
. The family
\{V(n)\}
is a polynomial coefficient system of degree
r
, and Shapiro’s lemma shows that
H_{d}(\Gamma_{n};V(n))=H_{d}(\Gamma_{n-r};\mathbb{Z})
, so that the stable range for the symmetric groups with these coefficients is precisely the stable range for constant coefficients, offset by
r
.
\blacksquare

Example 1.8
.

Consider
\Gamma_{n}=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(\mathbb{Z})
, and let
V(n)=\mathbbm{k}^{2n}
be the defining representation of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
, for
\mathbbm{k}
a field of characteristic zero. The family
\{V(n)^{\otimes r}\}
is a polynomial coefficient system of degree
r
. The stabilisation maps

H_{0}(\Gamma_{n-1};V(n-1)^{\otimes 2s})\to H_{0}(\Gamma_{n};V(n)^{\otimes 2s})

are isomorphisms for
n>s
, and this is
sharp
; stabilisation is not surjective for
n=s
. This follows by explicit computation. Working instead with cohomology, the first fundamental theorem of invariant theory for the symplectic group says that
H^{0}(\Gamma_{n};V(n)^{\otimes 2s})
is spanned by all ways of partitioning the set
\{1,\ldots,2s\}
into
2

n>s
, there are none, but for
n\leq s
there are always nontrivial relations. For a review of the first and second fundamental theorems of invariant theory in this context, see e.g.
[
LZ15
, Section 4]
.
\blacksquare

Thus even in the most simple and natural examples, the stable range must be allowed to depend on the degree of polynomiality.

1.4.
Work of Borel

One starting point of the present paper is that there are important examples of twisted homological stability theorems in which one has a uniform stable range for certain polynomial coefficient systems of arbitrarily high degree. More specifically, one such uniform homological stability theorem comes out of the results of Borel on the stable cohomology of arithmetic groups
[
Bor74
,
Bor81
]
.

Consider an arithmetic group
\Gamma
inside a semisimple algebraic group
G
over
\mathbb{Q}
. Borel shows firstly that in a stable range, the cohomology
H^{*}(\Gamma;\mathbb{R})
can be computed as the
L^{2}
-cohomology of the associated locally symmetric space for
\Gamma
. Moreover, the
L^{2}
-cohomology can in a range be computed in terms of
G
-invariant differential forms on the symmetric space of
G
; in particular, the latter does not depend on the particular choice of
\Gamma
. If
\{G_{n}\}
is a family of classical groups and
\Gamma_{n}
is an arbitrary family of arithmetic subgroups, then for
n\to\infty
the consequence is that
H^{*}(\Gamma_{n};\mathbb{R})
stabilises to an answer that can be computed purely representation-theoretically.

Moreover, Borel also proved twisted homological stability. If in the above setting
V
is a real irreducible algebraic representation of
G
, then one can similarly calculate
H^{*}(\Gamma;V)
in terms of differential forms. When
V
is nontrivial, the outcome of Borel’s argument is simply that
H^{*}(\Gamma;V)
vanishes in a stable range. What is important for us is that although Borel in his original paper gave a stable range depending on
V
, it is in fact possible to give a uniform stable range depending only on the group
G
, under mild hypotheses.

Borel’s work on stable cohomology is of a fundamentally different nature than any of the standard machinery for homological stability — at its core, it is a theorem about automorphic forms, proven by transcendental techniques. Borel’s methods only prove homological stability rationally, and analogous integral assertions are completely false.

Remark 1.9
.

Example

1.8
does not contradict the uniformity in Borel’s results: it is only for systems of
irreducible
representations that we see the uniform homological stability, and the decomposition of
V(n)^{\otimes s}
into irreducibles depends nontrivially upon
n
, although it stabilises for
n
sufficiently large with respect to
s
, according to Littlewood’s stable branching rule.

The examples of

In each of these examples, homological stability of the family
\{\Gamma_{n}\}
is known via the Quillen argument. Thus, they satisfy homological stability
integrally
, with
arbitrary
polynomial coefficient systems, but with a stable range depending on the degree of polynomiality. But one may instead take real (or rational) coefficients, and consider the
very specific
systems of polynomial coefficients obtained by pulling back a sequence of irreducible algebraic representations from the family of groups
\{Q_{n}\}
. Borel tells us that for this particular type of coefficients, the homology of
Q_{n}
stabilises with a uniform stable range, independent of the polynomial degree. It is natural to ask whether the same is true for the homology of
\Gamma_{n}
with the same coefficients.

Now, there is in general no reason why a stable range for
H_{\ast}(Q_{n};V(n))
should propagate to a stable range for
H_{\ast}(\Gamma_{n};V(n))
, but our main theorem gives conditions under which it is possible. If
V
is a coefficient system for the family
\{Q_{n}\}
, then we prove a stable range for
H_{\ast}(\Gamma_{n};V(n))
, which is bounded in terms of three quantities:

(1)

The connectivity of the
destabilisation
or
splitting complexes
of the family of groups
\{\Gamma_{n}\}
.

(2)

The connectivity of the
destabilisation
or
splitting complexes
of the family of groups
\{Q_{n}\}
.

(3)

The stable range of all
shifts

H_{\ast}(Q_{n},(\Sigma^{a}V)(n))=H_{\ast}(Q_{n},V(n+a))
, for all
a\geq 0
.

Estimating the connectivities in
1
and
2
is what one would usually do to prove homological stability for the respective families of groups
\{\Gamma_{n}\}
and
\{Q_{n}\}
. Condition
3
seems awkward to verify in general. What makes it possible in our situation is that we are working with families of classical groups, and the embeddings
Q_{n}\to Q_{n+a}
have well-understood

, which allow us to explicitly decompose
\operatorname{Res}^{Q_{n+a}}_{Q_{n}}V(n+a)
into irreducibles; even better, the branching rule is very nearly independent of
n
, which allows us to express each shift
\Sigma^{a}V
in terms of (truncated versions of) other coefficient systems of the same form as
V
.

The truncation mentioned in the preceding sentence is what gives rise to the dependence on the lengths of the partitions in
1.1
,
1.2
,
1.3
, and
1.4

is a nontrivial even tensor power of the determinant. This does not contradict Borel’s work, since

is not semisimple, and Borel’s theorem only applies to

1.6.
Sharpness

We do not expect any of

,
1.2
,
1.3
, or

An easy remark is that if

, and

for

H_{d}(\Gamma_{\infty};V)=0
for
d<f(N)
.
This gives an upper bound for what stable range could hold for a coefficient system

or
W_{\lambda\mu}
, given the knowledge of the stable homology with these twisted coefficients.

In

,
1.2
, and
1.4

are known by

[

[
Lin22

[

. The groups

These considerations show that the best possible slope of stability in

that could hold uniformly for all partitions is at most

1.7.
Relation to Torelli groups

Another motivation for proving uniform twisted homological stability theorems is the work of Hain
[
Hai97
]
giving quadratic presentations of the Malcev completion of the Torelli subgroup of the mapping class group for

(later improved to
g\geq 4

[
Hai15
]

for
d=1,2
and
all
partitions

and
g\geq 3

[
Joh80
]
, and when
d=2

[
Kab98
]
has proven purity of their mixed Hodge structure when

, using a delicate algebro-geometric argument; this purity suffices to carry out the argument. Now

implies that we can completely calculate

in any degree

g
sufficiently large, which in particular would allow a simplified version of Hain’s proof to carry through (but only for

It is a well-known open problem to prove an analogue of Hain’s results for the Torelli subgroups of
\mathrm{Aut}(F_{n})
, and one may hope that
1.2
will be useful in obtaining such an analogue.
1.2
by itself cannot be immediately applied to obtain a quadratic presentation of the Torelli Lie algebra of

\mathrm{Aut}(F_{n})

Hain’s work may be seen as an early precursor to the general theory of
representation stability

[
CF13
]
and our

[

) of the homology of the Torelli groups.
Uniform representation stability for the homology of the Torelli groups is known in degree
1
since the work of Johnson
[
Joh85
]
and was recently proven in degree

by Minahan and Putman
[
MP25
]
. If uniform representation stability of the homology of the Torelli groups were known in general, then a uniform stable range for the homology

would be an immediate consequence. Similarly, Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
, and
1.4
may be considered as weak evidence for uniform representation stability of the homology of the respective Torelli subgroups of
\mathrm{Aut}(F_{n})
,
\mathrm{HMod}_{g}^{1}
, and
\beta_{n}
.

2.
A general theorem for propagating homological stability

Our goal in this section is to formulate and prove a general theorem for propagating homological stability properties for the family of groups
\{Q_{n}\}
to the family of groups
\{\Gamma_{n}\}
, taking as axioms certain desirable features of both families of groups and of a coefficient system
V
for the family
\{Q_{n}\}
.
In Section
4
we will then verify these axioms in the cases described in Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
,
1.3
, and
1.4
. In those examples the family
\{Q_{n}\}
consists of arithmetic groups, and satisfies uniform homological stability by (analogues of) the work of Borel: the general result will imply that the family
\{\Gamma_{n}\}
does too. But the theorem can in principle be used in other ways as well (cf. Subsection
1.8
). We will express this result in the language developed in
[
GKRW18
]
, and rely on that work as well as
[
RW24
]
for some technical support.

2.1.
Formulation

Our basic datum will be a morphism

p:(\mathsf{G},\oplus,0)\longrightarrow(\mathsf{Q},\oplus,0)

of braided strict monoidal groupoids, both of which have monoid of objects
\mathbb{N}
, with
p
the identity map on objects. We abbreviate

\Gamma_{n}:=\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathsf{G}}(n)\quad\quad\quad Q_{n}:=\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathsf{Q}}(n).

We assume that:

(1)

The induced maps
p_{n}:\Gamma_{n}\to Q_{n}
are surjective, with kernels denoted
K_{n}
.

(2)

\Gamma_{0}
is trivial, and hence
Q_{0}
and
K_{0}
are trivial too.

(3)

The maps
\Gamma_{a}\times\Gamma_{b}\to\Gamma_{a+b}
and
Q_{a}\times Q_{b}\to Q_{a+b}
are injective.

Remark 2.1
.

Let us comment on how essential these assumptions are, making use of the notation we will introduce over the next few pages. Assumption
1
ensures that the canonical map
\overline{\mathbf{R}}\to\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
is 1-connected: this is a crucial step of the argument. Assumption
2
ensures that
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
and
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}}
are augmented
E_{2}
-algebras, and hence that
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
is too: this is necessary to define the derived
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
-module indecomposables and hence to even formulate assumption
1
of Theorem
2.2
.

Assumption
3
is not necessary for Theorem
2.2
, but is used in formulating Propositions
2.9
and
2.11
, which allow us to verify assumption
1
of Theorem
2.2
in terms of simplicial complexes.

Finally, following
[
Kra19
,
RW24
]
and looking forward to Proposition
2.9
, it seems likely that a variant of our results can be obtained for
p:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{Q}
a morphism of groupoids which are modules over the free braided monoidal groupoid on one generator. We leave this to the interested reader.

2.1.1.
\mathsf{G}
- and
\mathsf{Q}
-graded simplicial modules

Fix a field
\mathbbm{k}
of characteristic zero, and let
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}
denote the category of simplicial
\mathbbm{k}
-modules. We encourage the reader to mentally replace this by the category
\mathsf{Ch}_{\mathbbm{k}}
of non-negatively graded chain complexes, if they are more comfortable in that context: the difference is purely technical, and allows us to directly quote results from
[

]
, but at a conceptual level there is no difference. We will work in the categories
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
and
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{Q}}
of functors from
\mathsf{G}
or
\mathsf{Q}
to
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}
. These are endowed with the projective model structure
[
GKRW18
, Section 7.3.1]
, induced by the standard model structure on simplicial modules
[
GKRW18
, Section 7.2.3]
. For an object
X
of one of these categories, we define bigraded homology groups as the simplicial homotopy groups:

H_{n,d}(X):=\pi_{d}(X(n)).

This is the same as the homology of the associated chain complex of normalised chains. Equivalently, first let
S^{d}:=\Delta^{d}_{\bullet}/\partial\Delta^{d}_{\bullet}\in\mathsf{sSet}_{*}
denote the pointed simplicial
d
-sphere, and
S^{d}_{\mathbbm{k}}:=\mathbbm{k}[S^{d}]/\mathbbm{k}[\mathrm{pt}]\in\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}
denote its reduced
\mathbbm{k}
-linearisation: the homotopy groups of
S^{d}_{\mathbbm{k}}
are the reduced
\mathbbm{k}
-homology of
S^{d}
. Then let
S^{n,d}_{\mathbbm{k}}:=n_{*}S^{d}_{\mathbbm{k}}\in\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
denote the left Kan extension of
S^{d}_{\mathbbm{k}}
along the inclusion
\{n\}\to\mathsf{G}
. (Explicitly,
S^{n,d}_{\mathbbm{k}}
is supported on the object
n
, and evaluates here to
\mathbbm{k}[\Gamma_{n}]\otimes_{\mathbbm{k}}S^{d}_{\mathbbm{k}}
with its evident
\Gamma_{n}
-action.) This is cofibrant in
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
, as
S^{d}_{\mathbbm{k}}
is cofibrant in
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}
by definition of its model structure
[
GKRW18
, Section 7.2.3]
and the left Kan extension
n_{*}(-)
is a left Quillen functor
[
GKRW18
, Example 7.11]
. All
X\in\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
are fibrant, by definition of the projective model structure and the fact that all objects of
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}
are fibrant. We therefore have
H_{n,d}(X)\cong[S^{n,d}_{\mathbbm{k}},X]_{\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}}
, the set of morphisms from
S^{n,d}_{\mathbbm{k}}
to
X
in the homotopy category of
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
. Similarly for
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{Q}}
.

The symmetric monoidal structure
\otimes_{\mathbbm{k}}
on
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}
and the braided monoidal structures on
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{Q}
endow
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
and
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{Q}}
with braided monoidal structures by Day convolution, which we denote by
\otimes
.
The unit for this monoidal structure is the functor which is
\mathbbm{k}
at
0\in\mathbb{N}
and 0 otherwise: in other words it is

. Explicitly, in
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
for example, it is given by the formula

(X\otimes Y)(n):=\operatorname*{colim}_{\oplus/n}X(-)\otimes_{\mathbbm{k}}Y(-)\cong\bigoplus_{a+b=n}\mathrm{Ind}_{\Gamma_{a}\times\Gamma_{b}}^{\Gamma_{n}}X(a)\otimes_{\mathbbm{k}}Y(b),

where

denotes the overcategory of the functor

at the object

.

A fundamental construction will be the left Kan extension

, given by

It is strong monoidal

with respect to the Day convolution monoidal structures described above. It is left adjoint to restriction

, and admits a left derived functor

-orbits, giving a spectral sequence

Identifying

with a chain complex by the Dold–Kan correspondence, this is known as the hyperhomology spectral sequence (denoted

2.1.2.
Coefficient systems

In each case, the constant functor with value
\mathbbm{k}
defines a commutative monoid object, denoted
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}}
and
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
respectively. Following
[
GKRW18
, Section 19.1]
, a

for the family of groups

is simply a left
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
-module. Spelled out, this consists of a functor
V:\mathsf{Q}\to\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}
, i.e.
\mathbbm{k}[Q_{n}]
-modules

, equipped with
Q_{a}\times Q_{b}
-equivariant maps

\phi_{a,b}:\mathbbm{k}\otimes V(b)\longrightarrow V(a+b)

which are suitably associative. Restricted to functors taking values in the subcategory
\mathsf{Mod}_{\mathbbm{k}}\subset\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}
of

-modules this recovers several well-known notions: if
\mathsf{Q}
is the groupoid of finite sets and bijections then a coefficient system is equivalent to an
\mathit{FI}
-module of Church–Ellenberg–Farb
[
CEF15
, Definition 2.1.1]
; if
\mathsf{Q}
is the groupoid of finite-rank free modules over a ring and isomorphisms between them then a coefficient system is equivalent to a
\mathit{VIC}
-module of Putman–Sam
[
PS17
, Section 1.3]
; more generally, a coefficient system in this sense is equivalent to one in the sense of Randal-Williams–Wahl
[
RWW17
, Section 4.1]
, namely a functor from the enveloping homogeneous category

to

-modules.

This structure in particular provides a map
\phi_{1,n-1}:V(n-1)\to V(n)
equivariant with respect to

, using which we can form the map

H_{*}(Q_{n-1};V(n-1))\longrightarrow H_{*}(Q_{n};V(n)).

In fact this map already exists at the level of chain complexes
C_{*}(Q_{n-1};V(n-1))\to C_{*}(Q_{n};V(n))
, and we write
H_{*}(Q_{n},Q_{n-1};V(n),V(n-1))
for the homology of the mapping cone of this chain map.
Similarly, a coefficient system for the groups
\{\Gamma_{n}\}
is a left
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}}
-module. As

is lax monoidal and

, any
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
-module

gives a
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}}
-module

. We often abuse notation by writing

for the latter too.

Given a coefficient system

, its

is the functor
\Sigma V
given by
V(-\oplus 1)
. This again has the structure of a coefficient system, with the structure maps
(\Sigma\phi)_{a,b}=\phi_{a,b+1}
.

2.1.3.
Derived indecomposables

The final ingredient for formulating our theorem is more technical. The commutative algebra object
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}}
in the braided monoidal category
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
is not usually cofibrant (because the
\mathbbm{k}[\Gamma_{n}]
-module
\mathbbm{k}
is not usually free). It cannot usually be replaced by a cofibrant object still having the structure of a
commutative
algebra, but it can be replaced by a cofibrant object having the weaker structure of a
unital
E_{2}
-algebra
(cf.
[
GKRW18
, Section 12]
). We will use this concept in our proofs, but for stating the theorem we can work with a more elementary and explicit notion. (The notation is chosen to fit with
[
GKRW18
]
.)

Define an object
\overline{{T}}\in\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
by

\overline{{T}}(n):=\mathbbm{k}[E_{\bullet}\Gamma_{n}],

where
E_{\bullet}\Gamma_{n}
is the simplicial set given by the two-sided bar construction
B_{\bullet}(\Gamma_{n},\Gamma_{n},*)
of
\Gamma_{n}
acting on itself and on a point: this is contractible, and has a free left
\Gamma_{n}
-action so
\overline{{T}}
is cofibrant. The monoidal structure on
\mathsf{G}
gives homomorphisms
\Gamma_{n}\times\Gamma_{m}\to\Gamma_{n+m}
. The functors
\mathsf{Groups}\overset{E_{\bullet}(-)}{\to}\mathsf{sSet}\overset{\mathbbm{k}[-]}{\to}\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}
are both strong symmetric monoidal (when
\mathsf{Groups}
and
\mathsf{sSet}
are both given the cartesian monoidal structure), giving maps

\overline{{T}}(n)\otimes_{\mathbbm{k}}\overline{{T}}(m)\longrightarrow\overline{{T}}(n+m)

which are equivariant for
\Gamma_{n}\times\Gamma_{m}\to\Gamma_{n+m}
: these assemble to a morphism
\mu:\overline{{T}}\otimes\overline{{T}}\to\overline{{T}}
in
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
. The identity element
e\in\Gamma_{0}
gives a morphism
\iota:S_{\mathbbm{k}}^{0,0}\to\overline{{T}}
, and the strictness of the monoidal structure makes
\overline{\mathbf{T}}:=(\overline{{T}},\mu,\iota)
into a unital and associative algebra object in
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
. As each
E_{\bullet}\Gamma_{n}
is contractible, there is an equivalence
\overline{\mathbf{T}}\overset{\sim}{\to}\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}}
of associative algebras, exhibiting
\overline{{T}}
as a cofibrant replacement of
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}}
in
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
.

As the left Kan extension
p_{*}
is lax (in fact even strong) monoidal, the object

\overline{\mathbf{R}}:=p_{*}(\overline{\mathbf{T}})

is then an associative algebra in
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{Q}}
, and as
\overline{\mathbf{T}}
is cofibrant in
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
it is a model for the derived Kan extension
\mathbb{L}p_{*}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}})
. In particular, using assumption
1
it has

H_{n,d}(\overline{\mathbf{R}})=\pi_{d}\left(\operatorname*{colim}_{p/n}\mathbbm{k}[E_{\bullet}\Gamma_{n}]\right)\cong\pi_{d}\left(\operatorname*{hocolim}_{p/n}\mathbbm{k}\right)\cong H_{d}(K_{n};\mathbbm{k}),

given by the homology of the kernels
K_{n}
of the surjective homomorphisms
p_{n}:\Gamma_{n}\to Q_{n}
. As these homology groups in degree 0 are all
\mathbbm{k}
, Postnikov truncation provides a map of associative algebras
\overline{\mathbf{R}}\to\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
, making
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
into a left module over
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
. There is also an augmentation
\epsilon:\overline{\mathbf{R}}\to S^{0,0}_{\mathbbm{k}}
, so we may form the relative tensor product

S^{0,0}_{\mathbbm{k}}\otimes_{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(-):\overline{\mathbf{R}}\text{-}\mathsf{mod}\longrightarrow\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{Q}}.

In
[
GKRW18
, Section 9.4.2]
this functor is denoted
Q^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(-)
, and it is explained there that it is a left Quillen functor and so has a left derived functor
Q_{\mathbb{L}}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(-)
. Following that reference, we will write

H_{n,d}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(-):=H_{n,d}(Q_{\mathbb{L}}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(-)).

We will need to understand something about
H_{n,d}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})
to apply our methods.

2.1.4.
Statement of the theorem

Our generic theorem for propagating twisted homological stability then takes the following form.

Theorem 2.2
.

In the setting just described, suppose that
\nu\in(0,\infty)
is such that

(1)

H_{m,k}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})=0

for
k<\nu\cdot m+1
and
m\geq 1
.

Now fix
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
and a coefficient system
V
, and suppose that

(2)

H_{d-b}(Q_{n-a},Q_{n-1-a};\Sigma^{a}V(n-a),\Sigma^{a}V(n-1-a))=0
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
with
b\geq\nu\cdot a
.

Then
H_{d}(\Gamma_{n},\Gamma_{n-1};V(n),V(n-1))=0
.

In our applications it will be convenient to check the following (less general) variants of Axiom
2
.

Corollary 2.3
.

In the setting just described, suppose Axiom
1
holds with slope
\nu
, that
V
is a coefficient system, and that
\tau\geq 0
is such that

(2)

H_{d}(Q_{n},Q_{n};\Sigma^{a}V(n),\Sigma^{a}V(n))=0
whenever
d<\nu\cdot(n-\tau)
and
a\geq 0
.

Then
H_{d}(\Gamma_{n},\Gamma_{n-1};V(n),V(n-1))=0
whenever
d<\nu\cdot(n-\tau)
.

Proof.

We apply
2.2
. Suppose
d
and
n
satisfy
d<\nu\cdot(n-\tau)
, and
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
b\geq\nu\cdot a
.
Then
d-b<\nu\cdot(n-a-\tau)
. Hence if
Axiom
2
holds, then

H_{d-b}(Q_{n-a},Q_{n-1-a};\Sigma^{a}V(n-a),\Sigma^{a}V(n-1-a))=0,

which verifies Axiom
2
.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

In the setting just described, suppose Axiom
1
holds with slope
\nu
, that
V
is a coefficient system, and that
\theta>0
,
\beta\geq 0
and
\tau\geq 0
are such that

(2)

H_{d}(Q_{n},Q_{n};\Sigma^{a}V(n),\Sigma^{a}V(n))=0
whenever
d<\theta\cdot(n-\tau)
, and
a\geq 0
, and
n>\beta
.

Then
H_{d}(\Gamma_{n},\Gamma_{n-1};V(n),V(n-1))=0
for
d<\min(\nu,\theta)\cdot(n-\max(\tau,\beta))
.

Proof.

We apply
2.3
. Since Axiom
1
is satisfied with slope
\nu
, it is certainly satisfied with slope
\min(\nu,\theta)
. We need to check that Axiom
2
implies that

H_{d}(Q_{n},Q_{n};\Sigma^{a}V(n),\Sigma^{a}V(n))=0\text{ whenever }d<\min(\nu,\theta)\cdot(n-\max(\tau,\beta)).

If
n-\max(\tau,\beta)\leq 0
then the conclusion is vacuously true. If
n-\max(\tau,\beta)>0
then we certainly have
n>\beta
, and also
\min(\nu,\theta)\cdot(n-\max(\tau,\beta))<\theta\cdot(n-\tau)
.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

In interpreting Axioms
2
,
2
,
2
, and their conclusions, we consider
Q_{i}
and
\Gamma_{i}
for
i<0
to be the “empty group”: the classifying space of the empty group is the empty space, all of whose homology groups are zero. This explains in particular why
\tau
must be nonnegative in Axioms
2
and
2
.

Remark 2.6
.

A coefficient system consists of a large amount of data: an infinite family of modules
V(m)
, and an infinite family of maps between them, satisfying an infinite list of equations. In the situation of
2.2
, careful inspection of Axiom
2
shows that very little of this data can play any role in the proof of the theorem. Indeed, the module
V(m)
only appears in the hypotheses of the theorem for
m\in\{n,n-1\}
, since
\Sigma^{a}V(n-a)=\operatorname{Res}^{Q_{n}}_{Q_{n-a}}V(n)
, and
\Sigma^{a}V(n-1-a)=\operatorname{Res}^{Q_{n-1}}_{Q_{n-1-a}}V(n-1)
. In fact, there is no loss of generality in assuming that
V(m)=0
for
m\notin\{n,n-1\}
, and hence that the coefficient system
V
is of the form described in the following
2.7
, whose proof we omit.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
n
be fixed,
A
be a
\mathbbm{k}[Q_{n}]
-module,
B
be a
\mathbbm{k}[Q_{n-1}]
-module, and
\rho:\mathbbm{k}\otimes B\to\mathrm{Res}^{Q_{n}}_{Q_{1}\times Q_{n-1}}A
be a
Q_{1}\times Q_{n-1}
-equivariant map. Then there is a unique coefficient system
V
for the family
\{Q_{m}\}
having

V(m)=\begin{cases}A&m=n\\
B&m=n-1\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and structure map
\phi_{1,n-1}:\mathbbm{k}\otimes V(n-1)\to V(n)
given by
\rho
.∎

2.2.
Methods for verifying Axiom
1

In practice, it can be difficult to directly verify Axiom
1
(unless
\mathsf{G}
happens to be the free braided monoidal groupoid on one generator — see
4.5
). We therefore provide two companion results,
2.9
and
2.11
, which say that it suffices to estimate the connectivities of certain absolute invariants of the braided monoidal groupoids
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{Q}
. Before giving them, we first give a simple lemma which describes
H_{n,d}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})
in low degrees. This will be useful in verifying Axiom
1
, and often allow for improvements to the slope
\nu
.

Lemma 2.8
.

(1)

H_{n,0}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})
is just
\mathbbm{k}
supported at
n=0
. Similarly
H_{0,d}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})
is just
\mathbbm{k}
supported at
d=0
.

(2)

H_{n,1}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})=0
for all
n
.

(3)

If
\mu\in(0,\infty)
and
\xi\in(-\infty,1]
are such that
H_{n,d}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})=0
for
d<\mu\cdot n+\xi
and
n\geq 1
, then Axiom
1
is satisfied with
\nu=\tfrac{\mu}{2-\xi}
.

(4)

Suppose that
N\geq 0
is such that for each
n>N
the image of the stabilisation map
H_{1}(K_{N};\mathbbm{k})\to H_{1}(K_{n};\mathbbm{k})
generates the target as a
\mathbbm{k}[Q_{n}]
-module. Then
H_{n,2}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})=0
for all
n>N
.

(5)

If both
3
and
4
are satisfied, then Axiom
1
is satisfied with
\nu=\min(\tfrac{1}{N},\tfrac{2\mu}{3-\xi})
.

Proof.

The map
H_{n,d}(\overline{\mathbf{R}})=H_{d}(K_{n};\mathbbm{k})\to H_{n,d}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})
is an isomorphism when
d=0
and an epimorphism when
d=1
(as
H_{*,1}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})=0
), so that the relative homology satisfies
H_{n,d}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}},\overline{\mathbf{R}})=0
for
d\leq 1
. As
H_{*,0}(\overline{\mathbf{R}})=\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
, it follows from
[
GKRW18
, Corollary 11.14 (ii)]
that the Hurewicz map
\mathbbm{k}\otimes_{\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}}H_{*,*}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}},\overline{\mathbf{R}})\to H_{*,*}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}},\overline{\mathbf{R}})
is an isomorphism in bidegrees
(n,d)
with
d\leq 2
, so that
H_{n,d}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}},\overline{\mathbf{R}})=0
for
d\leq 1
. Using
H_{0,*}(\overline{\mathbf{R}})\cong H_{*}(K_{0};\mathbbm{k})
, and assumption
2
that
K_{0}
is trivial, one sees that
H_{n,d}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}},\overline{\mathbf{R}})
vanishes for
n=0
too. The long exact sequence for a pair then shows that

\displaystyle H_{*,0}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})

\displaystyle=H_{*,0}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\overline{\mathbf{R}})=\mathbbm{k}\text{ supported in grading 0},

\displaystyle H_{*,1}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})

\displaystyle=H_{*,1}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\overline{\mathbf{R}})=0,

\displaystyle H_{0,*}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})

\displaystyle=H_{0,*}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\overline{\mathbf{R}})=\mathbbm{k}\text{ supported in degree 0}.

This proves
1
and
2
.

For
3
, observe first that
H_{n,d}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})=0
for
d<\tfrac{\mu}{2-\xi}\cdot n+1
and
n\geq 1
whenever
d\leq 1
, by
1
and
2
. But
\tfrac{\mu}{2-\xi}\cdot n+1\leq\mu\cdot n+\xi
whenever the left-hand side is
\geq 2
(so that
n\geq\tfrac{2-\xi}{\mu}
).

For
4
, choose a map
\bigoplus_{i\leq N}n_{*}(\Sigma H_{1}(K_{n};\mathbbm{k}))\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}
that is an isomorphism on
H_{n,1}(-)
for
n\leq N
, and extend it to a
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
-module map
\overline{\mathbf{R}}\otimes\left(\bigoplus_{i\leq N}n_{*}(\Sigma H_{1}(K_{n};\mathbbm{k}))\right)\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}

with cofibre an
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
-module
\mathbf{C}
. The long exact sequence on homology groups shows that
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}=H_{*,0}(\overline{\mathbf{R}})=H_{*,0}(\mathbf{C})
, and has a portion

\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}a+b=n\\
b\leq N\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Ind}_{Q_{a}\times Q_{b}}^{Q_{n}}(\mathbbm{k}\boxtimes H_{1}(K_{b};\mathbbm{k}))\longrightarrow H_{1}(K_{n};\mathbbm{k})\longrightarrow H_{n,1}(\mathbf{C})\longrightarrow 0.

By the assumption the left-hand map is surjective, so
H_{*,1}(\mathbf{C})=0
. It follows that
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
can be constructed as an
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
-module from
\mathbf{C}
by attaching cells of homological degree
\geq 3
, and therefore

H_{*,2}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\mathbf{C})\longrightarrow H_{*,2}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})

is surjective. On the other hand, the long exact sequence on
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
-module homology for the cofibre sequence defining
\mathbf{C}
shows that
H_{n,2}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\mathbf{C})=0
for
n>N
.

For
5
, observe first that
H_{n,d}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})=0
for
d<\min(\tfrac{1}{N},\tfrac{2\mu}{3-\xi})\cdot n+1
and
n\geq 1
whenever
d\leq 2
, by
1
,
2
and
4
. But
\min(\tfrac{1}{N},\tfrac{2\mu}{3-\xi})\cdot n+1\leq\tfrac{2\mu}{3-\xi}\cdot n+1\leq\mu\cdot n+\xi
whenever the left-hand side is
\geq 3
(so that in particular
n\geq\tfrac{3-\xi}{\mu}
.)
∎

The following
2.9
and
2.11
should be thought of as being philosophically very similar. The former takes as input an estimate of the connectivities of the
destabilisation complexes
of
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{Q}
, in the sense of
[
RWW17
, Definition 2.1]
, and the latter works instead with their
E_{1}
-splitting complexes
in the sense of
[
GKRW18
, Definition 17.9]
. An estimate of the connectivity of either of these families of complexes will imply an estimate of the connectivity of the other, under mild hypotheses
[
RW24
, Proposition 7.1]
, but having both

2.9
and
2.11
will allow for better stable ranges in practice.
2.9
allows for a connectivity estimate of any slope, and a small offset, whereas
2.11
only treats the case that the
E_{1}
-splitting complexes satisfy what
[
GKRW18
]
calls the
standard connectivity estimate
; it seems that the splitting complexes are in any case most useful when the standard connectivity estimate holds.

In order to define the space of destabilisations, we use assumption
3
which implies that the stabilisation maps
\Gamma_{n-p-1}\to\Gamma_{n}
are injective. Then the semisimplicial set
W_{\bullet}(\mathsf{G};n)
has
p
-simplices

W_{p}(\mathsf{G};n):=\operatorname*{colim}_{(-\oplus(p+1))/n}\mathsf{G}(-,n)\cong\frac{\Gamma_{n}}{\Gamma_{n-p-1}}

and face maps induced by certain maps
-\oplus(p+1)\overset{\sim}{\to}-\oplus 1\oplus p
formed using the braided monoidal structure of
\mathsf{G}
, see
[
RWW17
, Definition 2.1]
for details.

Proposition 2.9
.

Suppose that
\nu\in(0,\infty)
,
\xi^{\prime}\in[-1,\infty)
, and
\xi^{\prime\prime}\in(-\infty,\infty)
satisfy

(1)

\widetilde{H}_{d}(W_{\bullet}(\mathsf{G};n);\mathbbm{k})=0
for
d<\nu\cdot n+\xi^{\prime}-1
and
n\geq 1
,

(2)

\widetilde{H}_{d}(W_{\bullet}(\mathsf{Q};n);\mathbbm{k})=0
for
d<\nu\cdot n+\xi^{\prime\prime}-1
and
n\geq 1
.

Then
H_{n,d}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})=0

for
d<\nu\cdot n+\xi
and
n\geq 1
, where
\xi:=\min(\xi^{\prime\prime},1+\xi^{\prime})
.

Remark 2.10
.

The strategy of proof of Proposition
2.9
will be identical to that of
[
RW24
, Theorem 6.2 (ii)]
, which concerns a similar result in the setting
\mathsf{G}=\mathsf{Q}
but with
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
replaced by a general
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
-module (such a result was originally proved as
[
Pat20
, Theorem 5.7]
). Technicalities arising from working in
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{Q}}
and with
E_{2}
-algebras obfuscate the argument a little, so let us first describe the essential idea in a simplified situation.

Let
S\to R
be a map of differential graded
\mathbbm{k}
-algebras equipped with an additional
\mathbb{N}
-grading (i.e. of associative algebra objects in
\mathsf{Ch}(\mathbbm{k})^{\mathbb{N}}
),
M
be a left
R
-module, and
\epsilon:R\to\mathbbm{k}
be an augmentation such that both
S\to R\to\mathbbm{k}
are isomorphisms in grading 0. We assume that

(1)

H_{n,d}(\mathbbm{k}\otimes_{S}^{\mathbb{L}}R)=0
for
d<\nu\cdot n+\xi^{\prime}
and
n\geq 1
,

(2)

H_{n,d}(\mathbbm{k}\otimes_{S}^{\mathbb{L}}M)=0
for
d<\nu\cdot n+\xi^{\prime\prime}
and
n\geq 1
.

We wish to deduce that
H_{n,d}(\mathbbm{k}\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{R}M)=0
for
d<\nu\cdot n+\xi
and
n\geq 1
. That is, we wish to estimate the
R
-module cells needed to construct the
R
-module
M
by knowing the
S
-module cells needed to construct the
S
-module
M
and the
S
-module cells needed to construct
R
.

To do so, we consider
\mathbbm{k}\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{S}M\simeq(\mathbbm{k}\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{S}R)\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{R}M
and filter the right
R
-module
\mathbbm{k}\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{S}R
by minus its grading. The associated graded of this filtration may be identified with
\mathbbm{k}\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{S}R
, but the right
R
-module structure has been trivialised: it is now induced by
\epsilon:R\to\mathbbm{k}
. This filtered object yields a spectral sequence of signature

E^{1}_{n,p,q}=\bigoplus_{a+b=p+q}H_{-p,a}(\mathbbm{k}\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{S}R)\otimes H_{n+p,b}(\mathbbm{k}\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{R}M)\Longrightarrow H_{n,p+q}(\mathbbm{k}\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{S}M).

The assumption that
S\to R\to\mathbbm{k}
are both isomorphisms in grading 0 means that
H_{0,*}(\mathbbm{k}\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{S}R)=\mathbbm{k}
and so
H_{n,d}(\mathbbm{k}\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{R}M)\cong E^{1}_{n,0,d}
. Assumption (b) shows that
E^{\infty}_{n,0,d}=0
for
d<\nu\cdot n+\xi^{\prime\prime}
and
n\geq 1
, and an induction and assumption (a) shows that the targets of the differentials out of
E^{r}_{n,0,d}
are trivial for
d<\min(1+\nu\cdot n+\xi^{\prime}+\xi,1+\nu\cdot n+\xi^{\prime})
under the same conditions: thus
E^{1}_{n,0,d}=0
for
d<\nu\cdot n+\xi
, as long as
\xi^{\prime}\geq-1
and
\xi\leq\min(\xi^{\prime\prime},1+\xi^{\prime})
.

The argument we will give below is essentially this one, though in the slightly more complicated category
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{Q}}
and with
R
corresponding to
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
,
S
corresponding to the free
E_{2}
-algebra on
S^{1,0}_{\mathbbm{k}}
, and
M
corresponding to
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
. Then
H_{n,d}(\mathbbm{k}\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{R}M)
corresponds to
H_{n,d}^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})
. It is preceded by some translation between the assumption
1
and
2
in the statement of Proposition
2.9
to the analogue of assumptions (a) and (b) in this sketch. The results of
[
RW24
]
allow us to relate destabilisation complexes to derived indecomposables (derived tensor products in this remark).

Proof of Proposition
2.9
.

We change our perspective on
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
slightly, by giving a more conceptual construction of it. The commutative algebra
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}}
in the braided monoidal category
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
is in particular a unital
E_{2}
-algebra object in this category. Using assumption
2
again it has a unique augmentation
\epsilon:\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}}\to S_{\mathbbm{k}}^{0,0}
, and we write
(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}})_{>0}
for its kernel, which is a nonunital
E_{2}
-algebra. The category
\mathsf{Alg}_{E_{2}}(\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}})
admits a projective model structure transferred from the standard model structure on
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
, as discussed in
[
GKRW18
, Section 9.2.1]
, using that all objects of
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}
are fibrant
[
GKRW18
, Section 7.2.3]

\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}

[

\mathsf{Alg}_{E_{2}}(\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}})
are also cofibrant in
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}})
. The non-unital
E_{2}
-algebra
(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}})_{>0}
admits a cofibrant replacement
\mathbf{T}\overset{\sim}{\to}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}})_{>0}\in\mathsf{Alg}_{E_{2}}(\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}})
, which is also cofibrant in
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
. This in turn can be unitalised-and-strictified to an associative monoid
\overline{\mathbf{T}}
(see
[
GKRW18
, Section 12.2.1]
), which is cofibrant in
\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{G}}
by
[
GKRW18
, Lemma 12.7 (i)]
and is equivalent to
\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{G}}
as an associative algebra. Using
[
GKRW18
, Lemma 12.8]
, this
\overline{\mathbf{T}}
is equivalent as an associative algebra to the explicit associative algebra of the same name we defined earlier. In particular, the associative algebra
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
we defined earlier is equivalent as an associative algebra (or

-algebra) to the unitalisation-and-strictification of the non-unital

-algebra

\mathbf{R}:=p_{*}(\mathbf{T})\in\mathsf{Alg}_{E_{2}}(\mathsf{sMod}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{\mathsf{Q}}).

Following
[
RW24

, let us write
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}:=\overline{\mathbf{E}_{2}(S^{1,0}_{\mathbbm{k}})}

. A choice of map

.

We now translate the assumptions in the proposition into statements about derived indecomposables. Working momentarily in the category

3.1.
Even and odd symplectic groups

The images of the integral Burau representation form a family of congruence subgroups of the
even and odd
symplectic groups, as introduced by Gelfand–Zelevinsky
[
GZ85
]
. They were introduced to “interpolate” between the usual symplectic groups — in many situations, it may seem that there ought to be some such family of “missing” groups.

Definition 3.1
.

The
odd symplectic group

\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-1}
is the subgroup of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
stabilising a unimodular vector.

Remark 3.2
.

The even and odd symplectic groups may be considered as algebraic groups over
\mathbb{Z}
, but we will really only be interested in their integer points.

The first goal of this section is to make the system of all even and odd symplectic groups into a braided monoidal groupoid. The monoidal structure is somewhat subtle. In particular, it does
not
restrict to the usual block-sum of matrices when restricted to the classical even symplectic groups. In fact, the even symplectic groups do not even form a subgroupoid, as the indexing in the groupoid is “off by one” compared to the classical one — the groupoid is of the form
\coprod_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{Sp}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z})
, so to speak. Moreover, unlike the block-sum, the braiding is
not
a symmetry; it is genuinely braided monoidal.

3.1.1.
A monoidal groupoid of even and odd symplectic groups

Let
\mathsf{M}
be the following groupoid. Objects are triples
(M,\langle-,-\rangle,\phi)
where
M
is an abelian group,
\langle-,-\rangle
is a skew-symmetric bilinear form on
M
, and
\phi:M\to\mathbb{Z}
is linear. Morphisms are isomorphisms of this data. We generally denote an object of
\mathsf{M}
by
M
, leaving the remaining data implicit in the notation.

The category
\mathsf{M}
admits a monoidal structure given by

(M,\langle-,-\rangle_{M},\phi_{M})\oplus(M^{\prime},\langle-,-\rangle_{M^{\prime}},\phi_{M^{\prime}})=(M\oplus{M^{\prime}},\langle-,-\rangle_{M\oplus{M^{\prime}}},\phi_{M\oplus{M^{\prime}}})

where for
x,y\in M
and
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in M^{\prime}
we have

\langle x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime}\rangle_{M\oplus M^{\prime}}=\langle x,y\rangle_{M}+\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle_{M^{\prime}}+\phi_{M}(x)\phi_{M^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})-\phi_{M}(y)\phi_{M^{\prime}}(x^{\prime}),

and
\phi_{M\oplus{M^{\prime}}}(x+x^{\prime})=\phi_{M}(x)+\phi_{M^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})
. The sum of two morphisms is the block-sum.

Let
\mathbb{Z}
denote the object of
\mathsf{M}
given by the triple
(\mathbb{Z},0,\mathrm{id})
.
Let
\mathsf{T}
denote the full subgroupoid spanned by the objects
\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n}
for
n\geq 0
. Write
T_{n}=\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n})
. We denote the standard basis for
\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n}
by
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
. Explicitly,
\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n}
has the bilinear form

\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle=\begin{cases}1&i<j\\
0&i=j\\
-1&i>j\end{cases},

and
\phi(e_{i})=1
for all
i
. The monoidal structure furnishes maps
T_{n}\times T_{m}\to T_{n+m}
, given by block-sum of matrices. The following is also proven in
[
BPS24
, Lemma 2.1]
and
[
SW24
, Proposition 2.17]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

There are isomorphisms
T_{n}\cong\mathrm{Sp}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

Let
M=\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n}
, and define a new basis for
M
by

\displaystyle e_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=e_{1}

\displaystyle e_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=e_{2}

\displaystyle e_{3}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=e_{1}-e_{2}+e_{3}

\displaystyle e_{4}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=e_{1}-e_{2}+e_{4}

\displaystyle e_{5}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=e_{1}-e_{2}+e_{3}-e_{4}+e_{5}

\displaystyle e_{6}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=e_{1}-e_{2}+e_{3}-e_{4}+e_{6},

and so on.

If
n
is even, then one checks that
e_{1}^{\prime},\dots,e_{n}^{\prime}
is a standard symplectic basis for
M
with respect to
\langle-,-\rangle
. Via the symplectic form, we can identify
\phi\in M^{\vee}
with a unimodular vector in
M
, and so
T_{n}
is identified with the stabiliser of this unimodular vector inside
\mathrm{Sp}(M)
.

If
n
is odd, this basis shows instead that
\langle-,-\rangle
has a rank one kernel
K
, spanned by the unimodular vector
e_{n}^{\prime}
, and that the form descends to a symplectic form on
M/K
. But the map
\phi
splits the short exact sequence
0\to K\to M\to M/K\to 0
, since
\phi(e_{n}^{\prime})=1
. This gives a canonical identification
M/K\cong\ker(\phi)
and picks out a distinguished generator of
K
. Thus
T_{n}\cong\mathrm{Sp}(M/K)
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

The element
v_{n}:=e_{1}-e_{2}+\ldots+(-1)^{n-1}e_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n}
is fixed by every element of
T_{n}
.

Proof.

If
n
is even, then in the preceding proof we identified
\phi
with a unimodular vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n}
using the symplectic form, and one checks that this vector is precisely
v_{n}
. Explicitly, this means that
v_{n}
is the unique vector such that
\langle v_{n},x\rangle=\phi(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n}
.

If
n
is odd, then in the preceding proof we argued that
K
has a distinguished generator
e_{n}^{\prime}=v_{n}
. Explicitly,
v_{n}
is the unique vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n}
satisfying
\phi(v_{n})=1
and
\langle v_{n},-\rangle
is identically zero.
∎

Definition 3.5
.

By the

of

or

we mean the representation

.

Remark 3.6
.

The defining representation may be considered as a homomorphism

Remark 3.7
.

Another definition of odd symplectic groups, different from Gelfand–Zelevinsky’s, was independently proposed by Proctor
[

]
. We denote his groups by

, which he defines as the subgroup of

stabilising a skew-symmetric form of maximal rank. Equivalently,

3.1.2.
The Burau representation

The Burau representation is a well-studied sequence of representations of the braid groups, whose definition we now recall. In general the representation has coefficients in the Laurent polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[t,t^{-1}]
. We will only be interested in its specialization to
t=-1
, which is called the
integral
Burau representation.

Definition 3.8
.

The (unreduced)
integral Burau representation
of the braid group
\beta_{n}
is the representation which maps the standard generator
\sigma_{i}
of
\beta_{n}
(where
i=1,\dots,n-1
) to the block-matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{1}_{i-1}&0&0\\
0&B&0\\
0&0&\mathbf{1}_{n-i-1}\end{pmatrix}

where
B=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

We could replace
B
by its transpose in this definition, and we would get an isomorphic representation of the braid group. Indeed, transposing
B
amounts to conjugating by the matrix
\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,1,-1,1,\dots)
. For this reason there are differing conventions in the literature for exactly how the Burau matrices are defined.

Definition 3.9
.

The
reduced integral Burau representation
is the rank
n-1
subrepresentation of the integral Burau representation, given by
\beta_{n}
acting on
\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}=0\}
.

There is a “reflection” automorphism of the braid groups
\rho:\beta_{n}\to\beta_{n}
given geometrically by interchanging under- and over-crossings, or algebraically by sending the standard generators
\sigma_{i}
to their inverses. These automorphisms are natural with respect to stabilisation of braids. Pulling back a representation
W
of
\beta_{n}
along
\rho
gives a new representation
\rho^{*}W
, the
reflection
of
W
. This will not usually be isomorphic to
W
. However, there is an isomorphism

(3.1)

(\rho,\mathrm{Id})_{*}:H_{*}(\beta_{n};\rho^{*}W)\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}H_{*}(\beta_{n};W),

so the homologies of
\beta_{n}
with coefficients in these two representations are isomorphic. It will be convenient for us to work with the reflection of the Burau representation rather than the Burau representation itself, as this will give more conveniently described destabilisation complexes (
3.14
), but by (
3.1
) it makes no difference at the level of homology or of homological stability.

Remark 3.15

, top of page 558]

Remark 3.16
.

The complexes

were proven to be slope

. Their argument is rather different from the one we use for

In our proof of high connectivity, we will quote results from Charney and Mirzaii–van der Kallen. Their papers are neither written in the setting of simplicial complexes nor semisimplicial sets, but in terms of “posets of sequences”, following Maazen

Definition 3.17
.

A

is a semisimplicial set

such that

3.2.1.
Transitivity arguments

Let
R
be a ring,
J
an ideal,
G
an algebraic group over
R
. In many situations one wants to know surjectivity of the reduction mod
J
homomorphism
G(R)\to G(R/J)
. One standard method for proving such results is to show that
G(R/J)
is generated by elementary matrices, and lifting elementary matrices. Alternatively, if
R
is e.g. the ring of integers in a global field, a powerful sledgehammer for proving surjectivity is the strong approximation theorem (see Platonov–Rapinchuk
[
PR94
, Chapter 7]
). We will need only the case that
R=\mathbb{Z}
,
J=2\mathbb{Z}
, and
G
is (a parabolic subgroup of)
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
or
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}
. Thus the following simple argument will suffice for our purposes.

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
R
be ring, and
\mathfrak{m}
a maximal ideal, such that the induced map
R^{\times}\to(R/\mathfrak{m})^{\times}
is surjective.
Let
G
be an
R
-split reductive group scheme, or a parabolic subgroup thereof. Then
G(R)\to G(R/\mathfrak{m})
is surjective.

Proof.

Let
B\subset G
be a split Borel. Since
R/\mathfrak{m}
is a field, we see from the Bruhat decomposition that
G(R/\mathfrak{m})
is generated by
B(R/\mathfrak{m})
and
W
, where
W
is the Weyl group. (If
G
is a parabolic subgroup of an ambient reductive group
G^{\prime}
, then we take
W
to be the corresponding parabolic subgroup of the Weyl group of
G^{\prime}
.) So it suffices to show that
B(R)\to B(R/\mathfrak{m})
is surjective. But
B
has a composition series whose factors are isomorphic to
\mathbb{G}_{a}
or
\mathbb{G}_{m}
, and both
\mathbb{G}_{a}(R)\to\mathbb{G}_{a}(R/\mathfrak{m})
and
\mathbb{G}_{m}(R)\to\mathbb{G}_{m}(R/\mathfrak{m})
are surjective.∎

Remark 3.19
.

The reduction-mod-

is surjective precisely for

Denote by

Definition 3.23
.

Let
M
be a symplectic module. A

for

Lemma 3.24
.

The group
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})[2]
acts transitively on the set of partial isotropic bases (or partial symplectic bases) in
\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
whose reduction mod
2
is a fixed partial isotropic basis (or partial symplectic basis) in

.

Proof.

This proof is very similar to
[
MvdK01
, Lemma 5.3]
.

It is enough to assume that the fixed partial isotropic basis (or partial symplectic basis) in
(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{2g}
is given by the standard vectors
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
(or
e_{1},f_{1},\dots,e_{k},f_{k}
), because by
[
MvdK01
, Lemma 5.3]
there is a symplectic matrix

that sends the fixed partial basis to the standard one, and we can lift this symplectic matrix to
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
.

We will prove the statement by induction on
k
, the length of the partial bases. If
k=1
, the partial isotropic case is given by
3.22
. For the partial symplectic case, let
(a_{1},b_{1})
be a symplectic pair in
\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
that reduces mod
2
to
(e_{1},f_{1})
in
(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{2g}
. We already know that we can send
a_{1}
to
e_{1}
by a matrix

. It follows that

Ab_{1}=2\alpha_{1}e_{1}+f_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{g}(2\alpha_{i}e_{i}+2\beta_{i}f_{i})

for some

.
The map

\displaystyle e_{1}

\displaystyle\longmapsto e_{1}

\displaystyle e_{i}

\displaystyle\longmapsto e_{i}+2\beta_{i}e_{1}\qquad i>1

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle\longmapsto f_{1}-2\alpha_{1}e_{1}-\sum_{i=2}^{g}(2\alpha_{i}e_{i}+2\beta_{i}f_{i})

\displaystyle f_{i}

\displaystyle\longmapsto f_{i}-2\alpha_{i}e_{1}\qquad i>1

is an element in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})[2]
that sends
Ab_{1}
to
f_{1}
.

For

is part of a partial symplectic basis

is a symplectic pair in the complement of

(a_{k},b_{k})
to
(e_{k},f_{k})
by a symplectic matrix of the complement that reduces to the identity mod

. And so we are done.

Lemma 3.41
.

Let
\sigma
be a

\mathit{IX}_{n}
. Then

To ease notation in the following proof, we define

for

. By
3.32

on

(which preserves the subcomplexes

and

Consider the map

to the basis vector

. Then

if and only if:

(4)

\langle u_{i},h_{j}\rangle=0

3.3.
The connectivity argument

We now proceed to the argument for high connectivity of
X_{n}
. The road map is as follows:

•

Deduce from Charney’s work and a bad simplex argument that
Y_{n}
is highly connected.

•

Use a nerve lemma of Mirzaii–van der Kallen to deduce high connectivity of
\mathit{IX}_{n}
from high connectivity of
Y_{n}
.

•

Use the nerve lemma again to deduce high connectivity of
X_{n}
from high connectivity of
\mathit{IX}_{n}
.

Definition 3.42
.

Let
W
be a simplicial complex. We define
{W}^{\mathrm{dir}}
to be the directed simplicial complex whose
p
-simplices are the
p
-simplices of
W
with an order of their vertices. (Thus there are
(p+1)!
simplices of
{W}^{\mathrm{dir}}
for each
p
-simplex of
W
.)

Remark 3.43
.

The geometric realization of
{W}^{\mathrm{dir}}
contains the geometric realization of
W
as a retract, by arbitrarily choosing an ordering of the vertices of
W
. Hence if
{W}^{\mathrm{dir}}
is
d
-connected, then so is
W
.

We will need the notion of a complex being
weakly Cohen–Macaulay
, or wCM for short.

Definition 3.44
.

A simplicial complex
W
is
wCM of dimension
d
if for all simplices
\sigma\subset W
,
\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(\sigma)
is
(d-\dim(\sigma)-2)
-connected. In particular, taking
\sigma=\varnothing
,
W
itself must be
(d-1)
-connected.

Definition 3.45
.

Let
R
be a ring,
J
an ideal. Let
V
be a finitely generated free
R
-module, and
\mathcal{B}
a partial basis of
V/J=V\otimes_{R}R/J
. Let
\mathit{PB}(V;\mathcal{B})
be the simplicial complex with simplices
\{x_{0},\dots,x_{p}\}
where

(1)

x_{0},\dots,x_{p}
is a partial basis of
V
.

(2)

\{x_{0},\dots,x_{p}\}
modulo
J
is a subset of
\mathcal{B}
.

The complex
\mathit{PB}(V;\mathcal{B})
is a subcomplex of the well-known complex of partial bases
\mathit{PB}(V)
, which drops condition (2).

The following theorem follows directly from Charney
[
Cha84
, Theorem on p. 2094]
.

Theorem 3.46
.

Let
m
be the Bass stable rank of
(R,J)
. Let
\mathcal{B}
be the first
t
basis vectors
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{t}\}
in
(R/J)^{n}
, with

. Then
\mathit{PB}(R^{n};\mathcal{B})
is wCM of dimension

.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=\{v_{0},\dots,v_{p}\}
be a
p
-simplex of
\mathit{PB}(R^{n};\mathcal{B})
. We need to see that
\mathrm{Lk}_{PB(R^{n};\mathcal{B})}(\sigma)
is
(t-m-p-2)
-connected.

Let
v
denote the ordered tuple
(v_{0},\dots,v_{p})
of the vertices in
\sigma
and
I=\ker(R^{n}\to(R/J)^{n})
. Then the barycentric subdivision of
{\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(R^{n};\mathcal{B})}(\sigma)}^{\mathrm{dir}}
is isomorphic to the geometric realization of the poset which Charney denotes
\mathcal{O}_{J}(R^{t}+I)\cap U_{v}
, and which she proves to be

-connected. (Note that the integer that Charney denotes

equals

.
∎

Since

Lemma 3.48
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated free abelian group, and
U,V
be direct summands of
M
. Let
\mathcal{B}
be a partial basis for
V/2
. There exists a complement
W
of

in

such that
\mathcal{B}\cap((V\cap W)/2)
has at least
|\mathcal{B}|-\operatorname{rank}U
many elements.

Proof.

After possibly shrinking
V
, we may assume that
\mathcal{B}
is a basis for
V/2
. Choose a map
p:M\to V
splitting the inclusion. Let
T
be the saturation of
p(U)
in
V
; that is,
T
consists of those elements of
V
for which a nonzero multiple lies in
p(U)
. By construction,
V/T
is torsion-free, so that
T
is a direct summand of
V
. Also,
\operatorname{rank}T=\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}T\otimes\mathbb{Q}=\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}p(U)\otimes\mathbb{Q}\leq\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}U\otimes\mathbb{Q}=\operatorname{rank}U
.

Consider the surjection
V/2\to(V/T)/2
. Since
\mathcal{B}
is a basis for
V/2
, we can choose a subset
\mathcal{L}
of
\mathcal{B}
which maps to a basis of
(V/T)/2
. We have

|\mathcal{L}|=\dim_{\mathbb{Z}/2}(V/T)/2=\dim_{\mathbb{Z}/2}V/2-\dim_{\mathbb{Z}/2}T/2=|\mathcal{B}|-\operatorname{rank}T\geq|\mathcal{B}|-\operatorname{rank}U.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a basis for
T/2
, so
\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{L}
is a basis for
V/2
.

Denote by
\mathrm{GL}(V,T)
the parabolic subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}(V)
fixing
T
setwise. The mod 2 reduction map
\mathrm{GL}(V,T)\to\mathrm{GL}(V/2,T/2)
is surjective by
3.18
. It follows from surjectivity that we can lift the basis
\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{L}
for
V/2
to a basis
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
for
V
, such that
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
is a basis of
T
. In more detail, first choose a basis
\mathcal{C}
of
V
for which the first
\mathrm{rank}(T)
vectors form a basis of the summand
T
. Let
A
be the element in
\mathrm{GL}(V/2)
that sends
\mathcal{C}/2
to
\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{L}
. Then
A
is in
\mathrm{GL}(V/2,T/2)
, since it maps

to itself. Choose a preimage

, using

. Then

applied to

gives a basis
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
of

such that
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
is a basis of

and reduces to
\mathcal{K}
mod
2
, and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
reduces to
\mathcal{L}
mod
2
.

Let
T^{\prime}=\operatorname{Span}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. Since

, and

is surjective, we have
M=p^{-1}(T)\oplus p^{-1}(T^{\prime})
. Now
U\subseteq p^{-1}(T)
is a direct summand: indeed, this is equivalent to
U
being equal to its saturation in
p^{-1}(T)
, and

is by assumption equal to its saturation in the larger module

. Let
U^{\prime}
be a complement of
U
in
p^{-1}(T)
, and let

. Then

is a complement of

, and

, so
\mathcal{B}\cap((V\cap W)/2)\supseteq\mathcal{L}
, which we have seen has at least

many elements.
∎

We will apply a version of the “bad simplex argument”. There are many variants of the bad simplex argument in the literature; the following statement is exactly
[
GRW18
, Proposition 2.5]
.

Proposition 3.49
.

Let
X
be a simplicial complex and
Y\subset X
a full subcomplex. Suppose that there exists an integer
n
such that whenever a nonempty simplex
\tau\subset X
has no vertex in
Y
, then
\mathrm{Lk}_{X}(\tau)\cap Y
is
(n-\dim(\tau)-1)
-connected. Then the pair
(X,Y)
is
n
-connected.

We now show the following generalization of
3.47
. The case where
\sigma
is in
\mathit{PB}(M;\mathcal{B})
already appears in Charney’s paper.

Lemma 3.50
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated free abelian group,
V
a summand of
M
, and
\mathcal{B}
a partial basis of
V/2
. Let
\sigma
be a
q
-dimensional simplex of
\mathit{PB}(M)
, possibly empty. Then
\mathit{PB}(V;\mathcal{B})\cap\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(M)}(\sigma)
is wCM of dimension
(|\mathcal{B}|-3-q)
.

Proof.

The case that
\sigma
is empty is precisely
3.47
, and we can therefore assume that
\sigma
is nonempty. We first show that
\mathit{PB}(V;\mathcal{B})\cap\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(M)}(\sigma)
is
(|\mathcal{B}|-4-q)
-connected. We prove this by induction on the quantity
(|\mathcal{B}|-4-q)
; that is, we assume the claimed statement for all data
V
,
\mathcal{B}
, and
\sigma
with strictly smaller value of
(|\mathcal{B}|-4-q)
. The base case is that all spaces are
(-2)
-connected.

Let
U
be the summand of
M
spanned by
\sigma
. Using
3.48
, choose a complement
W
of
U
such that
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\mathcal{B}\cap((V\cap W)/2)
has at least
|\mathcal{B}|-q-1
elements.
There is an inclusion

(3.4)

\mathit{PB}(V\cap W;\mathcal{B}^{\prime})\hookrightarrow\mathit{PB}(V;\mathcal{B})\cap\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(M)}(\sigma)

because every partial basis of
W
forms a partial basis together with the elements of
\sigma
. It suffices to show that this inclusion
is
(|\mathcal{B}|-4-q)
-connected, since
\mathit{PB}(V\cap W;\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
is
(|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|-3)
-connected by
3.47
, and
|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|\geq|\mathcal{B}|-q-1
. To do so we check the conditions of
3.49
.

First, the inclusion is a full subcomplex because every partial basis of
V
consisting of vectors in
V\cap W
is a partial basis of
V\cap W
. Next, assume
\tau=\{t_{0},\dots,t_{p}\}
is a

p
-simplex of
\mathit{PB}(V;\mathcal{B})\cap\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(M)}(\sigma)
with no vertices in
\mathit{PB}(V\cap W;\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
.
Using the decomposition
M=U\oplus W
, let
t_{i}=t^{U}_{i}+t^{W}_{i}
with
t^{U}_{i}\in U
and
t^{W}_{i}\in W
. Since the vectors of
\sigma
give a basis of
U
, and
\sigma\cup\tau
is a partial basis of
M
,
\tau^{W}=\{t^{W}_{0},\dots,t^{W}_{p}\}
is a partial basis of
W
. We claim that

\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(V;\mathcal{B})\cap\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(M)}(\sigma)}(\tau)\cap\mathit{PB}(V\cap W;\mathcal{B}^{\prime})=\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(W)}(\tau^{W})\cap\mathit{PB}(V\cap W;\mathcal{B}^{\prime}).

Indeed, take a simplex
\rho\subset\mathit{PB}(V\cap W;\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
. Then
\rho
lies in the left-hand side if and only if
\sigma\cup\tau\cup\rho
is a partial basis of
M
, and on the right-hand side if and only if
\tau^{W}\cup\rho
is a partial basis of
W
.
But it is easy to check that
\sigma\cup\tau\cup\rho
is a partial basis in
M
if and only if

\sigma\cup\tau^{W}\cup\rho
is a partial basis in
M
, if and only if
\tau^{W}\cup\rho
is a partial basis of

.

Now
\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(W)}(\tau_{W})\cap\mathit{PB}(V\cap W;\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
is
(|\mathcal{B}|-p-q-5)
-connected by inductive hypothesis. By
3.49
the inclusion (

) is
(|\mathcal{B}|-4-q)
-connected, as required.

To show that
\mathit{PB}(V;\mathcal{B})\cap\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(M)}(\sigma)
is in fact wCM of dimension
(|\mathcal{B}|-3-q)
, let

be a simplex of it and observe that
\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(V;\mathcal{B})\cap\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(M)}(\sigma)}(\tau)=\mathit{PB}(V;\mathcal{B})\cap\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(M)}(\sigma\cup\tau)
, which by the above is

.
∎

Lemma 3.51
.

Let

. If a topological space is
\frac{k}{2}
-connected, then it is
\frac{k-2}{4}
-connected.

Proof.

We have
\lfloor\frac{k-2}{4}\rfloor\leq\max(\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor,-2)
.

Lemma 3.52
.

Let
\sigma
be a

-dimensional simplex of

, possibly empty. Then

is
\frac{n-2q-11}{4}
-connected.

Proof.

Let
M=\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n}
.

Consider a set of vectors
\mathcal{B}\subset M/2
such that
\{\rho(x)\}_{x\in\mathcal{B}}
are pairwise disjoint
2
-element sets, each of which is also disjoint from
\rho(v)
for each vertex
v
of
\sigma
. Notice that
\mathcal{B}
is necessarily a partial basis mod
2
. Since
\sigma
lies in

, the sets
\{\rho(v)\}_{v\in\sigma}
are also pairwise disjoint
2
-element sets. Hence
|\mathcal{B}|
can take any value up to
\lfloor\frac{n-2q-2}{2}\rfloor

However, we choose such a family with
|\mathcal{B}|=\lfloor\frac{n-2q-3}{2}\rfloor
, i.e. with one element fewer than what is maximal if

is even. The reason is that it will be important for us that the vectors
\mathcal{B}\cup\{v_{n}\}
are a partial basis mod

, and if

then this fails in the exceptional case that

and
n
is even, where instead

mod
2
would be the sum of all the elements of
\mathcal{B}
.

Let
V=\ker\phi\cap\sigma^{\perp}
, where
\sigma^{\perp}
denotes the subspace of vectors

\langle x,v\rangle=0
for each vertex

of
\sigma
. There is then a short exact sequence

(3.5)

where the components of the right-hand arrow are given by

and
\langle-,v\rangle=0
for

in
\sigma
. The only thing which is not clear is surjectivity. Consider first the case that

is even. Then
\langle-,-\rangle
is a symplectic form on

(
3.3
). Note that
\ker\phi\cap\sigma^{\perp}=(\sigma\cup\{v_{n}\})^{\perp}
, since

for

even (

). Since
\sigma\cup\{v_{n}\}
is a partial basis for

, the functionals
\phi
and

for

are a partial basis for

. The case that

is odd is similar. In this case the pairing
\langle-,-\rangle
is a symplectic form on

(

), and

spans a summand of

, since

for all

. Then the functionals

for

are a partial basis for

, which is a summand of

.

Reducing (

) modulo 2 shows that

consists of those vectors in

, in the kernel of

, which are perpendicular to

for all

.

We notice that a simplex

in this case.

Define a subcomplex

\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(V;\mathcal{B}\cup\{v_{n}\})\cap\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(M)}(\sigma)}(v_{n})&n\text{ even.}\end{cases}

.

-connected using

is
(\frac{n-2q-11}{4}-p-1)

Then

\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(V;\mathcal{C}(\tau)\cup\{v_{n}\})\cap\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathit{PB}(M)}(\tau\cup\sigma)}(v_{n})&n\text{ even.}\end{cases}

Since

4.
Verifying the axioms

The goal in this section is to deduce Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
,
1.3
, and
1.4
from the generic
2.2
, i.e. verifying that Axioms
1
and
2
of
2.2
are satisfied in the respective situations.

We begin with Axiom
1
, which requires putting together various proofs of high connectivity of simplicial complexes from the literature (or from
Section

3
, in the case of

). We then turn to Axiom
2
, which requires some amount of representation theory, and in particular a detailed study of the behaviour of the various coefficient systems under shifting.

4.1.
The groupoids and their connectivities

We return to the situation of Sections
1
and
2
: we have
\mathsf{G}=\coprod_{n}\Gamma_{n}
a family of groups arising as symmetries of a sequence of topological objects, and
\mathsf{Q}=\coprod_{n}Q_{n}
a family of arithmetic groups arising by taking the induced symmetries on homology.

4.1.1.
Mapping class groups

We take
\Gamma_{n}
to be the mapping class group of a genus
n
surface with a boundary component, and
Q_{n}=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(\mathbb{Z})
. The groupoid
\mathsf{G}
has a monoidal structure given by “pair of pants”-product. In fact the pair of pants-product makes the classifying space
\coprod_{n}B\Gamma_{n}
into an
E_{2}
-algebra, as first observed by Miller
[
Mil86
]
, which in particular endows
\mathsf{G}
with a braiding. See
[
RWW17
, Section 5.6]
for a careful construction. The monoidal structure on
\mathsf{Q}
is simply given by block-sum of matrices, which is in an evident way symmetric monoidal.

Surjectivity of
\Gamma_{n}\to\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(\mathbb{Z})
is a classical fact in surface topology — the symplectic group is generated by transvections, and each transvection can be lifted to a Dehn twist in the mapping class group.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
\mathsf{G}
is the groupoid of mapping class groups, and
\mathsf{Q}
the groupoid of integral symplectic groups, then Axiom
1
of
2.2
is satisfied with
\nu=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

The splitting complexes

satisfy the standard connectivity estimate by
[
GKRW19
, Theorem 3.4]
; the splitting complexes
S_{\bullet}^{E_{1}}(\mathsf{Q};n)
satisfy the standard connectivity estimate by
[
vdKL11
]
.
2.11
therefore implies that
H^{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}_{n,d}(\underline{\mathbbm{k}}_{\mathsf{Q}})=0
for
d<\tfrac{2}{3}\cdot n
. The kernels
K_{n}
are the Torelli groups
\mathcal{I}_{n,1}
, and Johnson
[
Joh85
]
has proved that the Johnson homomorphism

is an isomorphism for
n\geq 3
. He has also proved
[
Joh80
, Theorem 1]
that it is an epimorphism for
n\geq 2
. As the image of
\Lambda^{3}_{\mathbbm{k}}H_{1}(\Sigma_{2,1};\mathbbm{k})\to\Lambda^{3}_{\mathbbm{k}}H_{1}(\Sigma_{n,1};\mathbbm{k})
generates the target as an

-representation,

implies that

for

4.1.2.
Automorphisms of free groups

We take
\Gamma_{n}=\mathrm{Aut}(F_{n})
the automorphism group of a free group on
n

. The groupoid
\mathsf{G}
is symmetric monoidal in an evident way, being equivalent to the maximal subgroupoid of the category of finitely generated free groups, with monoidal structure given by the free product. The monoidal structure on

Surjectivity of

is an equally classical fact as in the mapping class group case, proven by lifting elementary matrices to the analogues of Dehn twists inside
\mathrm{Aut}(F_{n})

4.2.
Stable real cohomology of arithmetic groups

Let
G
be a semisimple algebraic group over
\mathbb{Q}
,
\Gamma\subset G
an arithmetic subgroup, and
V
an irreducible real representation of
G
. Borel
[
Bor74
,
Bor81
]
has famously calculated the cohomology

in a stable range. For the proof, Borel studies the homomorphism

(4.1)

(\mathcal{H}^{k}\otimes V)^{G}\to H^{k}(\Gamma;V),

where the left-hand side of (
4.1
) denotes the space of
G
-equivariant
V
-valued harmonic
k
-forms on the symmetric space of
G
. The left-hand side of (
4.1
) is very computable: for nontrivial
V
, it vanishes, and for constant coefficients it coincides with the cohomology of the compact dual of

. The goal, then, is to show that (
4.1
) is an isomorphism in a range of degrees.

Many authors have subsequently revisited and/or improved on Borel’s work. We will make no attempt at a complete historical survey, but mention in particular
[
VZ84
,
Fra98
,
Gro13
]
. The recent papers
[
Tsh19
,
LS19
,
BS23
]
all show, under different hypotheses, that (

) is an isomorphism for all

. This bound is in particular independent of

, unlike the one originally obtained by Borel. Intriguingly,
[
BS23
]
establish such a result by means of geometric group theory, rather than by automorphic methods, and

is allowed to be any unitary representation.

We will be interested in the groups
G=\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}
and
G=\mathrm{SL}_{n}
, whose real ranks are

and

, respectively. In these cases Borel’s results (with subsequent improvements) read as follows:

Theorem 4.6
.

Let

,
\Gamma\subset G
be an arithmetic subgroup, and

be an irreducible real representation of

. Then for

one has

, if

is nontrivial; the cohomology with trivial coefficients agrees below degree

with a polynomial algebra with generators in degrees
2,6,10,\ldots

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
G=\mathrm{SL}_{n}
,
\Gamma\subset G
be an arithmetic subgroup, and
V
be an irreducible real representation of

. Then for
k<n-1
one has

H^{k}(\Gamma;V)=0
, if
V
is nontrivial; the cohomology with trivial coefficients agrees below degree

5,9,13,\ldots

Both

and

are special cases of
[
BS23
, Theorem C]
.

Remark 4.8
.

The homomorphism (
4.1
) is known to be

in a significantly larger range of degrees. In particular, for

and

In our applications to automorphisms of free groups and mapping class groups of handlebodies we will be more concerned with the groups

is not semisimple. We have instead the following.

.

Let

be an irreducible real representation of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
. Let

be an arithmetic subgroup containing the diagonal matrices with entry

for

is an even tensor power of the determinant representation. If

Proof.

Let

By Shapiro’s lemma, one has

If

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{k}(\Gamma\cap\mathrm{SL}_{n-1};V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{k}(\Gamma\cap\mathrm{GL}_{n-1};V)=0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{k}(\overline{\Gamma};V)}

4.3.
The groups and the coefficients

We will be interested in sequences of representations of three families of algebraic groups:

\displaystyle\ldots\subset\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}\subset\mathrm{Sp}_{2n+2}\subset\mathrm{Sp}_{2n+4}\subset\ldots

\displaystyle\ldots\subset\mathrm{GL}_{n}\subset\mathrm{GL}_{n+1}\subset\mathrm{GL}_{n+2}\subset\ldots

\displaystyle\ldots\subset\mathrm{Sp}_{n-1}\subset\mathrm{Sp}_{n}\subset\mathrm{Sp}_{n+1}\subset\ldots

In all three cases, there is a monoidal structure present: for any ring
R
, the families

\coprod_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(R),\qquad\coprod_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{GL}_{n}(R),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\coprod_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{Sp}_{n-1}(R)

form braided monoidal groupoids satisfying the conditions
2
and
3
of §
2.1
: their monoids of objects are the natural numbers, and writing
Q_{n}
for
\mathrm{Aut}(n)
one has that
Q_{0}
is trivial, and
Q_{a}\times Q_{b}\to Q_{a+b}
is injective. In the first two cases, the monoidal structure is the obvious one, given by block-sum of matrices, and the braiding is given by the evident symmetry. The monoidal structure in the third case was described in detail in
Section

3
.

4.3.1.
The coefficient systems

Let us now define the coefficient systems of interest to us for the above three families of groups. All of our examples will come from “highest weight theory” — algebraic representations of the groups in question can be naturally indexed by partitions; fixing a partition defines a system of representations, and the family of coefficient systems thus defined will satisfy homological stability with a uniform stable range, say when
R=\mathbb{Z}
.

That said, we will not actually appeal to highest weight theory in defining the relevant coefficient systems — indeed, the irreducible representation of a classical group associated with a dominant weight is unique only up to noncanonical isomorphism, which is hardly enough data to define a meaningful coefficient system. We will therefore instead follow Weyl’s approach
[
Wey39
]
to constructing the irreducible representations of the classical groups, by realizing them inside tensor powers of the defining representation via the invariant theory of the symmetric groups. To avoid repetition, representations are always considered over a characteristic zero base field
\mathbbm{k}
.

By a
partition
we mean a descending sequence of nonnegative integers
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots)
which eventually reaches zero. We set
|\lambda|=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
, and
l(\lambda)=\max\{i:\lambda_{i}\neq 0\}
. Partitions with
|\lambda|=r
are in bijection with irreducible representations of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{r}
, and we denote by
\sigma_{\lambda}
the representation associated with
\lambda
, a Specht module. We define the
Schur functor

S^{\lambda}
associated to
\lambda
by

S^{\lambda}(V):=V^{\otimes r}\otimes_{\mathfrak{S}_{r}}\sigma_{\lambda},

where
r=|\lambda|
. It is classical that
S^{\lambda}(V)
is nonzero if and only if
\dim(V)\geq l(\lambda)
, and that
S^{\lambda}(V)
is always an irreducible representation of
\mathrm{GL}(V)
. The representations of the form
S^{\lambda}(V)
are precisely the
polynomial
representations of
\mathrm{GL}(V)
.

4.3.2.
Symplectic groups.

Let
V(n)=\mathbbm{k}^{n}
be the defining representation of
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}
, where
n
can be even or odd (the defining representations of odd symplectic groups were defined in
3.5
). Then
S^{\lambda}(V(n))
is generally not irreducible as a representation of
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}
. Let
V(n)^{\langle r\rangle}\subset V(n)^{\otimes r}
be the subspace of
traceless tensors
, i.e. the joint kernel of the
\binom{r}{2}
evident contraction maps
V(n)^{\otimes r}\to V(n)^{\otimes(r-2)}
. Define, for
r=|\lambda|
,

(4.2)

V_{\lambda}(n):=S^{\lambda}(V(n))\cap V(n)^{\langle r\rangle},

the intersection taking place inside
V(n)^{\otimes r}
.

If
n=2g
is even, then this definition is due to Weyl. He showed that
V_{\lambda}(n)
is nonzero if and only if
g\geq l(\lambda)
, that
V_{\lambda}
is always an irreducible representation of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}
, and that all irreducible representations of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}
arise in this way.

If
n
is odd, then this definition of
V_{\lambda}(n)
was proposed by Proctor
[
Pro88
]
. He showed that the groups
V_{\lambda}(n)
“naturally” interpolate between the irreducible representations of the usual symplectic groups; one precise statement is in particular that there exist character formulas for the representations
V_{\lambda}(n)
nearly identical to the classical Weyl character formula. Another natural such family of representations interpolating between the irreducible representations of the usual symplectic groups was independently proposed by Shtepin
[
Sht93
, Definition 4.2]
. It is not immediately clear that the two proposed definitions agree — and we will not need to know this — although the representations have the same character. The odd symplectic groups are not reductive, and the representations
V_{\lambda}(n)
are not irreducible (but they are indecomposable).

Let
M_{\lambda}(n):=V_{\lambda}(2n)
and
E_{\lambda}(n):=V_{\lambda}(n-1)
. Then
M_{\lambda}
is a coefficient system for the groupoid
\coprod_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(\mathbb{Z})
, and
E_{\lambda}
is a coefficient system for the groupoid
\mathsf{T}=\coprod_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{Sp}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z})
. The construction is the same in both cases, so we will be content to explain it for
M_{\lambda}
. The definition (
4.2
) makes sense more generally if
V(n)
is any vector space with a bilinear form, and even more generally if
V(n)
is replaced with a
coefficient system

M
equipped with a map
M\boxtimes M\to\underline{\mathbbm{k}}
. We apply this to
M=\{V(2n)\}
. It forms a coefficient system with structure maps
\phi_{a,b}:\mathbbm{k}\otimes M(b)\to M(a+b)
given by the inclusion of the last
2b
basis vectors. The map
M\boxtimes M\to\underline{\mathbbm{k}}
is given by the symplectic form; it is a map of coefficient systems since the
\phi_{a,b}
are isometric embeddings. Hence we also get a coefficient system
M_{\lambda}
with
M_{\lambda}(n)=S^{\lambda}(M(n))\cap M(n)^{\langle r\rangle}
. In fact it is a polynomial coefficient system for the symplectic groups, of degree
|\lambda|
, consisting by construction of a sequence of irreducible representations of the symplectic groups.

4.3.3.
General linear groups.

The coefficient systems we will consider for the general linear groups, in connection with automorphisms of free groups and handlebody groups, are of a very similar nature.
Let
W(n)=\mathbbm{k}^{n}
be the defining representation of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
. Define the subspace of
traceless tensors

W(n)^{\langle r,s\rangle}\subset W(n)^{\otimes r}\otimes(W(n)^{\ast})^{\otimes s}

to be the joint kernel of the
rs
evident contraction maps

W(n)^{\otimes r}\otimes(W(n)^{\ast})^{\otimes s}\to W(n)^{\otimes r-1}\otimes(W(n)^{\ast})^{\otimes s-1}.

For a pair of partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
, with
|\lambda|=r
and
|\mu|=s
, define

W_{\lambda,\mu}(n)=\big(S^{\lambda}(W(n))\otimes S^{\mu}(W(n)^{\ast})\big)\cap\big(W(n)^{\langle r,s\rangle}\big),

the intersection taking place inside
W(n)^{\otimes r}\otimes(V(n)^{\ast})^{\otimes s}
. Then

is nonzero if and only if

is an irreducible representation of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
. All irreducible representations of the general linear groups arise in this way.

The

form a coefficient system, with structure maps
\phi_{a,b}:\mathbbm{k}\otimes W(b)\to W(a+b)
given by the inclusion of the last
b
basis vectors; similarly, the
\{W(n)^{*}\}
form a coefficient system, with structure maps

basis vectors. These endow the

with the structure of a coefficient system, and just as in the symplectic groups case the

form a sub-coefficient system.

4.4.
Littlewood complexes

It will be important for us to understand how the coefficient systems
M_{\lambda}
,
E_{\lambda}
, and
W_{\lambda\mu}
described in the previous subsection behave with respect to the shift operator
\Sigma
on coefficient systems. Since

\Sigma M_{\lambda}(n)\cong\operatorname{Res}^{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n+2}}_{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}}M_{\lambda}(n+1),

determining how
M_{\lambda}
behaves under shifts entails in particular understanding a
branching rule
for how irreducible representations decompose under restriction along
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Sp}_{2n+2}
; similarly for
E_{\lambda}
and
W_{\lambda\mu}
. Such branching rules are already known (
4.22
). However, it will be useful for us to understand each
\Sigma M_{\lambda}
as a coefficient system (and similarly for
E_{\lambda}
and
W_{\lambda\mu}
), which requires somewhat more refined information than just understanding each representation
\Sigma M_{\lambda}(n)
separately. To carry this out we will use the
Littlewood complexes
introduced by Sam–Snowden–Weyman
[
SSW13
]
. Let us first recall their construction. We will freely use the language of
analytic functors
, as described carefully in
[
BDPW23
, Section 2]
. There are two parallell constructions, one for symplectic groups and one for general linear groups.

4.4.1.
Littlewood complexes for symplectic groups

Let
V
be a vector space with a skew-symmetric bilinear form
\omega:\wedge^{2}V\to\mathbbm{k}
. Consider the analytic functor

A\mapsto\Phi_{V}(A):=\mathrm{Sym}(A\otimes V\oplus(\wedge^{2}A)[-1])

from graded vector spaces to graded vector spaces.
We may in fact think of it as a functor from graded vector spaces to graded coalgebras, thinking of
\mathrm{Sym}(-)
as the cofree conilpotent cocommutative coalgebra functor. To define a coderivation on
\Phi_{V}(A)
, it suffices to describe its projection onto the cogenerators. We define a map

\partial:\mathrm{Sym}(A\otimes V\oplus\wedge^{2}A[-1])\to(A\otimes V)[1]\oplus\wedge^{2}A

as the composite

\mathrm{Sym}(A\otimes V\oplus\wedge^{2}A[-1])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{proj.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathrm{Sym}^{2}(A\otimes V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{proj.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\wedge^{2}A\otimes\wedge^{2}V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{id}\otimes\omega}}{{\longrightarrow}}\wedge^{2}A\hookrightarrow(A\otimes V)[1]\oplus\wedge^{2}A.

Since
\partial
has degree
1
, it extends to a degree

\Phi_{V}(A)
. We may think of
\Phi_{V}
as an analytic functor from graded vector spaces to cocommutative dg coalgebras, with the differential on
\Phi_{V}(A)
given by
\partial
.

An equivalent description is that

A\mapsto(A\otimes V)[-1]\oplus(\wedge^{2}A)[-2]

may be seen as a graded Lie algebra in the category of analytic functors, with two-step nilpotent bracket given by the map

\wedge^{2}((A\otimes V)[-1])=(\mathrm{Sym}^{2}(A\otimes V))[-2]\to(\wedge^{2}A\otimes\wedge^{2}V)[-2]\to(\wedge^{2}A)[-2].

Then
\Phi_{V}(A)
is the Chevalley–Eilenberg complex of this Lie algebra. This perspective is taken in
[
Sam15
]
.

Either way, the Taylor coefficients of
\Phi_{V}
form a symmetric sequence in cochain complexes. We denote by
L^{\ast}_{(r)}(V)
the

\mathfrak{S}_{r}
-modules. If
|\lambda|=r

\lambda

by
L_{\lambda}^{\ast}(V)

dual Littlewood complex
associated with
\lambda
and
V
. We call it the dual Littlewood complex, since it is the linear dual of the complex defined by Sam–Snowden–Weyman
[
SSW13
]

\mathrm{Sp}(V)
acts naturally on
\Phi_{V}

L^{\ast}_{\lambda}(V)
is a complex of
\mathrm{Sp}(V)
-modules.

It is easy to describe the zeroth cohomology of
L_{\lambda}^{\ast}(V)
. We have
L^{0}_{(r)}(V)=V^{\otimes r}
, and
L^{1}_{(r)}(V)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{S}_{2}\times\mathfrak{S}_{r-2}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{r}}\mathrm{sgn}_{2}\boxtimes V^{\otimes(r-2)}
. The differential
\partial:L^{0}_{(r)}(V)\to L^{1}_{(r)}(V)
is the sum of the

is the defining representation of
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}

\mathrm{Sp}_{n}

If

is the defining representation of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}

has at most one nonzero cohomology group, that this cohomology group is always an irreducible representation of

\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}

\Sigma E_{\lambda}
carries an increasing filtration

such that

.

Proof.

We take the filtration

on

to be the one induced from the filtration

If

for

by

The same argument as in

Theorem 4.17
.

The coefficient system
\Sigma L^{\ast}_{\lambda}(M)

4.4.3.
Littlewood complexes for general linear groups

We will now define a version of the Littlewood complexes for the general linear groups. We permit ourselves to be brief, as the results are completely parallell to those for symplectic groups.

Let
W
be a vector space. Consider the analytic functor of two variables

(A,A^{\prime})\mapsto\Psi_{W}(A,A^{\prime}):=\mathrm{Sym}(A\otimes W\oplus A^{\prime}\otimes W^{\ast}\oplus A\otimes A^{\prime}[-1])

from pairs of graded vector spaces to graded vector spaces. As in the symplectic case,
\Psi_{W}(A,A^{\prime})
is a cocommutative coalgebra. We define a coderivation on
\Psi_{W}(A,A^{\prime})
of degree 1, extending the map

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sym}(A\otimes W\oplus A^{\prime}\otimes W^{\ast}\oplus A\otimes A^{\prime}[-1])\to A\otimes W[1]\oplus A^{\prime}\otimes W^{\ast}[1]\oplus A\otimes A^{\prime}

given by the composite

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sym}(A\otimes W\oplus A^{\prime}\otimes W^{\ast}\oplus A\otimes A^{\prime}[-1])\to\mathrm{Sym}^{2}(A\otimes W\oplus A^{\prime}\otimes W^{\ast}\oplus A\otimes A^{\prime}[-1])\to

\displaystyle\to A\otimes W\otimes A^{\prime}\otimes W^{\ast}\to A\otimes A^{\prime}\hookrightarrow A\otimes W[1]\oplus A^{\prime}\otimes W^{\ast}[1]\oplus A\otimes A^{\prime}.

The Taylor coefficients of
\Psi_{W}
form a bisymmetric sequence. We let
K_{\lambda\mu}^{\ast}(W)
be the
(\lambda,\mu)
-isotypical components of these Taylor coefficients. This is the
dual Littlewood complex
for the general linear groups. Instead of taking
W
to be a vector space, it can itself be a coefficient system. If
W
is the coefficient system given by the defining representations of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
, then
H^{0}(K_{\lambda\mu}^{\ast}(W))=W_{\lambda,\mu}
, by an argument much like the one in the symplectic case.

Theorem 4.19

(Sam–Snowden–Weyman)
.

Let
W
be the defining representation of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
, and suppose
n\geq l(\lambda)+l(\mu)-1
. Then
H^{i}(K_{\lambda\mu}(W))=0
for

.

We let
W
be the coefficient system given by family of defining representations of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. The short exact sequence of coefficient systems

0\to W\to\Sigma W\to\underline{\mathbbm{k}}\to 0

is split. Using this splitting, we obtain an short exact sequence

0\to W^{\ast}\to\Sigma W^{\ast}\to\underline{\mathbbm{k}}\to 0.

From this we get two 2-step filtrations on
\Psi_{\Sigma W}(A,A^{\prime})
: one (denoted
F
) induced from the 2-step filtration of
\Sigma W
, and one (denoted
G
) from the 2-step filtration of
\Sigma W^{\ast}
. We have

\operatorname{Gr}^{F}_{i}\operatorname{Gr}^{G}_{j}\Psi_{\Sigma W}(A,A^{\prime})\cong\Psi_{W}(A,A^{\prime})\otimes\mathrm{Sym}^{i}(A)\otimes\mathrm{Sym}^{j}(A^{\prime}),

by arguments like those for (
4.4
). From this, arguments like those for
4.12
and
4.17
prove the following result.

Theorem 4.20
.

The coefficient system
\Sigma K^{\ast}_{\lambda\mu}(W)
carries two filtrations
F
and
G
such that

\operatorname{Gr}^{G}_{j}\operatorname{Gr}^{F}_{i}\Sigma K^{\ast}_{\lambda\mu}(W)\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda\searrow_{i}\nu}\bigoplus_{\mu\searrow_{j}\xi}K^{\ast}_{\nu\xi}(W).

Now arguments exactly like those for
4.16
and
4.18
prove the following.

Corollary 4.21
.

Let
\lambda
and
\mu
be partitions, and let
m=l(\lambda)+l(\mu)
. The coefficient system
\Sigma W_{\lambda,\mu}
carries two filtrations

and
G
such that

\operatorname{Gr}^{G}_{j}\operatorname{Gr}^{F}_{i}\Sigma W_{\lambda,\mu}\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda\searrow_{i}\nu}\bigoplus_{\mu\searrow_{j}\xi}\tau_{\geq(m-1)}W_{\nu,\xi}.

Remark 4.22
.

4.21
implies a branching rule for the inclusion
\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{GL}_{n}
: if
\lambda,\mu
are partitions with
l(\lambda)+l(\mu)\leq n
, then

\operatorname{Res}^{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}}W_{\lambda,\mu}(n)\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda\searrow\nu}\bigoplus_{\mu\searrow\xi}W_{\nu,\xi}(n-1).

Indeed,

expresses

as an iterated extension of representations

, and the extension is necessarily a direct sum, since
\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}
is reductive. This formula is classical, going back at least to Weyl. By the same argument,

implies a branching rule for the inclusion
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
: if

is any partition with

, then

\operatorname{Res}^{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}}_{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}}V_{\lambda}(2n)\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda\searrow\mu}\bigoplus_{\mu\searrow\nu}V_{\nu}(2n-2).

This formula was likely first obtained by Zhelobenko.
Finally,

implies a branching rule for
\mathrm{Sp}_{n-1}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Sp}_{n}
. One finds that

\operatorname{Res}^{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}}_{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-1}}V_{\lambda}(2n)
carries a filtration

such that

\operatorname{Gr}^{F}\operatorname{Res}^{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}}_{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-1}}V_{\lambda}(2n)\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda\searrow\mu}V_{\mu}(2n-1),

a result due to Shtepin
[

. The filtration does not in general split; the extensions are nontrivial. Similarly,

\operatorname{Res}^{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-1}}_{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}}V_{\lambda}(2n-1)\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda\searrow\mu}V_{\mu}(2n-2).

4.5.
Twisted stability for even and odd symplectic groups

For the groupoids
\coprod_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\coprod_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, twisted homological stability follows from Borel’s work. For the system of even and odd symplectic groups, twisted homological stability is less immediate. Our goal in this section is to prove homological stability of the even and odd symplectic groups with
E_{\lambda}
coefficients. Our strategy will be to use a theorem of Kostant to express the homology of the odd symplectic groups in terms of that of the even symplectic groups in a stable range.

We first make the observation that the inclusion
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Sp}_{2n+1}
always splits. Intrinsically (but not canonically), the splitting is given by projection to the Levi factor; in coordinates,
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n+1}
is the subgroup of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n+2}
of block-matrices of the form

\begin{bmatrix}A&*&0\\
0&1&0\\
*&*&1\end{bmatrix}

where
A\in\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
, and the projection
f:\mathrm{Sp}_{2n+1}\to\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
takes such a matrix to
A
. Let
U_{2n-1}=\ker(f)
be the unipotent radical of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-1}
, the
(2n-1)
-dimensional Heisenberg group.

Proposition 4.23
.

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be an arithmetic subgroup of
U_{2n-1}
, and
\lambda
a partition. Consider
V_{\lambda}(2n)
as a representation of
U_{2n-1}
via the inclusions
U_{2n-1}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-1}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
. The homology groups
H_{\ast}(\Gamma^{\prime};V_{\lambda}(2n))
are representations of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}
, and the multiplicities of the trivial representation are given by

\dim H_{d}(\Gamma^{\prime};V_{\lambda}(2n))^{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}}=\begin{cases}1&l(\lambda)\leq 1\text{ and }d\in\{0,2n-1\}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The proof of
4.23
is a computation using Kostant’s theorem in Lie algebra cohomology. It suffices to prove
4.23
in the case that the ground field
\mathbbm{k}
from which we take our coefficients is the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
. We assume this for the rest of the proof of
4.23
, to be consistent with the literature on Lie algebra cohomology.

4.5.1.
Kostant’s theorem

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite dimensional complex reductive Lie algebra. Let
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathfrak{g}
be a parabolic subalgebra, with nilpotent radical
\mathfrak{u}
and Levi subalgebra
\mathfrak{l}
. Let
V(\lambda)
be the finite-dimensional irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{g}
of highest weight
\lambda
. Kostant’s theorem
[
Kos61
, Theorem 5.14]
computes the Lie algebra cohomology

H^{q}(\mathfrak{u};V(\lambda))

as a representation of
\mathfrak{l}
. See also e.g. Vogan
[
Vog81
, Chapter 3, §2]
for a textbook treatment.

We let
W
be the Weyl group of
\mathfrak{g}
,
W_{P}
the Weyl group of
\mathfrak{l}
, and
W^{P}
the set of distinguished coset representatives of
W/W_{P}
. We denote by
\rho
the half-sum of all positive roots of
\mathfrak{g}
. For
w\in W
and
\lambda
a weight of
\mathfrak{g}
, we define

w\bullet\lambda=w(\lambda+\rho)-\rho.

If
w\in W^{P}
, then
w^{-1}\bullet(-)
takes dominant weights of
\mathfrak{g}
to dominant weights of
\mathfrak{l}
. We denote by
\ell:W\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
the length function of
W
.

Theorem 4.24

(Kostant)
.

With notation as above we have

H^{q}(\mathfrak{u};V(\lambda))=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in W^{P}\\
\ell(w)=q\end{subarray}}V_{\mathfrak{l}}(w^{-1}\bullet\lambda),

where
V_{\mathfrak{l}}(\mu)
denotes the irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{l}
of highest weight
\mu
.

Proof of
4.23
.

Let
G=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
,
P\subset G
the parabolic subgroup stabilizing a line, and
L
its Levi factor
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}\times\mathbb{G}_{m}
. Note that we have

1\to\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-1}\to P\to\mathbb{G}_{m}\to 1,

and that the unipotent radical of
P
is
U_{2n-1}
. We apply Kostant’s theorem, with
\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{l}
,
\mathfrak{u}
the Lie algebras of
G
,
P
,
L
, and
U_{2n-1}
.

First of all, by Nomizu’s theorem
[
Nom54
]
, we have
H_{*}(\Gamma^{\prime};V_{\lambda}(2n))=H_{*}(\mathfrak{u};V_{\lambda}(2n))
.

All algebraic representations of the usual symplectic group are self-dual (this fails for the odd symplectic groups). Applied to
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{l}
this means in particular that Kostant’s theorem in this case dualizes to a homology isomorphism

H_{q}(\mathfrak{u};V(\lambda))=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in W^{P}\\
\ell(w)=q\end{subarray}}V_{\mathfrak{l}}(w^{-1}\bullet\lambda).

We have that
W
is the hyperoctahedral group, i.e. the group of signed permutations, on
n
letters. The vector
\rho
is
(n,n-1,\ldots,1)
, the smallest regular weight of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
. The subgroup
W_{P}
is the group of signed permutations on
n-1
letters, which we think of as the subgroup stabilising the first entry. We have
w\in W^{P}
precisely when

w^{-1}(\rho)_{2}>w^{-1}(\rho)_{3}>\dots>w^{-1}(\rho)_{n}>0.

Thus for
w\in W^{P}
,
w^{-1}(\rho)_{1}
can be any element of
\{\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots,\pm n\}
and the remaining entries are uniquely determined by the condition of being positive and in descending order. For
\lambda
a dominant weight of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
and
w\in W^{P}
, the dominant weight of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}
given by
w^{-1}\bullet\lambda
is simply the vector obtained by deleting the first entry of the vector
w^{-1}\bullet\lambda
.

Example 4.25
.

Take
n=5
. Let
w
be the unique element of
W^{P}
with
w^{-1}(\rho)_{1}=-3
. Then we will have

w^{-1}(\rho)=(-3,5,4,2,1)

and for
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4},\lambda_{5})
we have

w^{-1}\bullet\lambda=(-8-\lambda_{3},1+\lambda_{1},1+\lambda_{2},\lambda_{4},\lambda_{5}).

The corresponding dominant weight of
\mathrm{Sp}_{8}
is obtained by deleting the first entry in the vector, giving
(1+\lambda_{1},1+\lambda_{2},\lambda_{4},\lambda_{5})
.
\blacksquare

To finish the proof of
4.23
, we need to determine for which values of
\lambda
and
w
the weight
w^{-1}\bullet\lambda
gives the trivial representation of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}
, i.e the all zero vector of length
(n-1)
. By pondering
Example

4.25
, it is clear that this happens precisely when
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},0,\ldots,0)
and moreover

w^{-1}(\rho)=(n,n-1,n-2,\ldots,1)\qquad\text{or}\qquad w^{-1}(\rho)=(-n,n-1,n-2,\ldots,1).

That is, we must have
l(\lambda)\leq 1
and
w\in\{\mathrm{id},\theta\}
, where
\theta
denotes the element that swaps the sign of the first entry. We have
\ell(\mathrm{id})=0
and
\ell(\theta)=2n-1
. The result follows.∎

Proposition 4.26
.

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be an arithmetic subgroup of
U_{2n-1}
, and
\lambda
a partition. Consider
V_{\lambda}(2n-1)
as a representation of
U_{2n-1}
. The homology groups
H_{\ast}(\Gamma^{\prime};V_{\lambda}(2n-1))
are representations of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}
, and the multiplicities of the trivial representation are given by

\dim H_{d}(\Gamma^{\prime};V_{\lambda}(2n-1))^{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}}=\begin{cases}1&\lambda=(0)\text{ and }d\in\{0,2n-1\}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

The restriction of
V_{\lambda}(2n)
to
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-1}
comes with a filtration

obtained from
4.16
. We thus obtain a spectral sequence

E^{1}_{pq}=H_{p+q}(\Gamma^{\prime};\operatorname{Gr}^{F}_{p}V_{\lambda}(2n))\implies H_{p+q}(\Gamma^{\prime};V_{\lambda}(2n)).

By semisimplicity, we also get a spectral sequence of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}
-invariants:

E^{1}_{pq}=H_{p+q}(\Gamma^{\prime};\operatorname{Gr}^{F}_{p}V_{\lambda}(2n))^{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}}\implies H_{p+q}(\Gamma^{\prime};V_{\lambda}(2n))^{\mathrm{Sp}_{2n-2}}.

The graded pieces
\operatorname{Gr}^{F}_{p}V_{\lambda}(2n)
are determined by
4.16
. We have
\operatorname{Gr}^{F}_{0}V_{\lambda}(2n)\cong V_{\lambda}(2n-1)
. If

with

occurs as a summand of some

, in which case

From this, the proof is by induction on

. The base case

. When

4.6.1.
The symplectic case

Lemma 4.30
.

∎

\mathsf{Q}

4.6.2.
The general linear group case

We now want to prove an analogue of
4.31
and
4.32
for the general linear groups and the coefficient systems
W_{\lambda\mu}
.

Definition 4.33
.

If
\lambda
and
\mu
are partitions, then we define the function

N_{\lambda\mu}(n):=\min\left\{a\;\middle|\;\begin{aligned} &\smash{\operatorname{Res}^{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n-a}}W_{\lambda\mu}(n)}\text{ contains a summand}\\
&\text{ isomorphic to \smash{$\mathrm{det}^{\otimes 2k}$} for $k\in 2\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$}\end{aligned}\right\}.

If the above set is empty — this happens if and only if
W_{\lambda\mu}(n)=0
, or
W_{\lambda\mu}(n)
is an odd tensor power of the determinant representation — then
N_{\lambda\mu}(n)=\infty
.

Lemma 4.34
.

Let
0\leq a<n
, and let
k>0
. Let
\lambda
and
\mu
be partitions with
l(\lambda)+l(\mu)\leq n
. Then the representation
\operatorname{Res}^{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n-a}}W_{\lambda\mu}(n)
contains
\det^{\otimes k}
as a summand if and only if
\lambda_{1+a}\leq k
and
\lambda_{n-a}\geq k
, and
\operatorname{Res}^{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n-a}}W_{\lambda\mu}(n)
contains
\det^{\otimes-k}
as a summand if and only if
\mu_{1+a}\leq k
and
\mu_{n-a}\geq k
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the first assertion, as the second follows by taking duals. We do induction on
a
, the base case
a=0
being that
W_{\lambda\mu}(n)\cong\det^{\otimes k}
if and only if
\lambda=(k^{n})
and
\mu=(0)
.

In general,
\operatorname{Res}^{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n-a}}W_{\lambda\mu}(n)
contains
\det^{\otimes k}
as a summand, if and only if there exist
(\nu,\xi)
with
\lambda\searrow\nu
and
\mu\searrow\xi
and
\operatorname{Res}^{\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n-a}}W_{\nu\xi}(n-1)
contains
\det^{\otimes k}
as a summand (using the branching rule of
4.22
), if and only there exists
\nu
with
\lambda\searrow\nu
and
\nu_{a}\leq k
and
\nu_{n-a}\geq k
(by the induction hypothesis), if and only if
\lambda_{1+a}\leq k
and
\lambda_{n-a}\geq k
(since
\nu_{i}
will take on all values between
\lambda_{i+1}
and
\lambda_{i}
). ∎

Corollary 4.35
.

N_{\lambda\mu}(n)\geq\min(n-\lambda_{2}^{\prime},n-\mu_{2}^{\prime})
, where
\lambda^{\prime}
and
\mu^{\prime}
denote the conjugate partitions of
\mu
and
\lambda
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\operatorname{Res}^{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n-a}}W_{\lambda\mu}(n)
contains
\det^{\otimes 2k}
as a summand. Suppose first that
k>0
. By
4.34
we must have
\lambda_{n-a}\geq 2k\geq 2
, so
n-a\leq\lambda_{2}^{\prime}
. If
k<0
then we have instead
\mu_{n-a}\geq 2k\geq 2
, so
\mu_{n-a}\geq 2
, so
n-a\leq\mu_{2}^{\prime}
.
∎

4.6.3.
Uniform stability in the general linear group cases

Let us finally explain in what sense
1.2
and
1.3
are theorems of “uniform homological stability”. At this point, it will be convenient to use a different convention for how to parametrise representations of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
, in terms of tuples
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that
w_{1}\geq w_{2}\geq\dots\geq w_{n}
. To such a tuple
w
we associate a pair of partitions
(\lambda,\mu)
, where
\lambda
consists of the positive entries of the tuple
w
, and
\mu
consists of minus the negative entries of
w
, written in reverse order. This assignment sets up a bijection between
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that
w_{1}\geq w_{2}\geq\dots\geq w_{n}
, and pairs of partitions
(\lambda,\mu)
with
l(\lambda)+l(\mu)\leq n
. We call
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
the
highest weight
of a representation. If the irreducible representation
V
has highest weight
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
, then the determinant twist
V\otimes\mathrm{det}^{\otimes k}
has highest weight
(w_{1}+k,\dots,w_{n}+k)
, for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
V
be an irreducible representation of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
of highest weight
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
. Assume first that
n
is odd. Choose
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
w_{(n+1)/2}+2k\in\{0,1\}
. Let
\lambda
and
\mu
be the partitions such that
W_{\lambda\mu}(n)\cong V\otimes\det^{\otimes 2k}
as representations of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
. Then necessarily
\max(\lambda_{2}^{\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime})\leq(n-1)/2
. An analogous argument when
n
is even gives a determinant twist for which
\max(\lambda_{2}^{\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime})\leq n/2
. Applying
1.2
iteratively, we find that the stabilisation homomorphism

H_{d}(\mathrm{Aut}(F_{n});V)\cong H_{d}(\mathrm{Aut}(F_{n});W_{\lambda\mu}(n))\longrightarrow H_{d}(\mathrm{Aut}(F_{\infty});W_{\lambda\mu})

is surjective for
d=\lfloor\tfrac{n-1}{4}\rfloor
and an isomorphism below this degree. Hence the homology
H_{\ast}(\mathrm{Aut}(F_{n});V)
is completely determined, as
V
ranges over all algebraic representation of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
, in terms of the stable homology (as computed by Lindell
[
Lin22
]
), in a range growing linearly with
n
. The same argument produces the same sort of uniformity for the twisted homology of the handlebody mapping class groups, using
1.3
.
