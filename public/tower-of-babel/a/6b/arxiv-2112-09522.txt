A Hopf lemma for the regional fractional Laplacian
Nicola Abatangelo 1 , Mouhamed Moustapha Fall 2 , and Remi Yvant Temgoua 2,3

Abstract
. We provide a Hopf boundary lemma for the regional fractional Laplacian ( − Δ ) Ω s (-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega} , with Ω ⊂ ℝ N \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N} a bounded open set. More precisely, given u u a pointwise or weak super-solution of the equation ( − Δ ) Ω s ​ u = c ⁡ ( x ) ​ u (-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}u=c(x)u in Ω \Omega , we show that the ratio u ⁡ ( x ) / ( dist ​ ( x , ∂ Ω ) ) 2 ​ s − 1 u(x)/(\textnormal{dist}(x,\partial\Omega))^{2s-1} is strictly positive as x x approaches the boundary ∂ Ω \partial\Omega of Ω \Omega . 1.
Introduction and main results

Let
s\in(1/2,1)
and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}\penalty\ (N\geq 2)
be a bounded domain with
C^{1,1}
boundary. The regional fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}
of a function
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

(1.1)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}u(x)=c_{N,s}P.V.\int_{\Omega}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{{|x-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy=c_{N,s}\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{{|x-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy,

provided that the limit exists. We recall that “
P.V.
” stands for the Cauchy principal value and that the normalization constant
c_{N,s}
is explicitly given by

c_{N,s}:=\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{1-\cos(\zeta_{1})}{{|\zeta|}^{N+2s}}\;d\zeta\bigg)^{-1}=s(1-s)\frac{2^{2s}\,\Gamma(\frac{N+2s}{2})}{\pi^{N/2}\,\Gamma(2-s)}.

For functions

C^{2s+\varepsilon}_{loc}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
for some
\varepsilon>0
, the integral in (
1.1
) is finite.
In this way then, we say that (
1.1
) is defined
pointwisely
in
\Omega
.

Let us mention here that while the Hopf lemma is usually used to run a moving plane method in the case of the fractional Laplacian, as recalled above, this does not seem to be the case for the regional fractional Laplacian. The moving plane method for

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a function

Definition 1.2

We say that a function

is a weak super-solution of (

u\in H^{s}(\Omega)

{\mathcal{E}}(u,\varphi)\geq\int_{\Omega}cu\varphi\qquad\text{for any }\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega),\ \varphi\geq 0\text{ in }\Omega.

Definition 1.3

We say that a function

is a distributional super-solution of (

and

\int_{\Omega}u\,(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}\varphi\geq\int_{\Omega}cu\varphi\qquad\text{for any }\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega),\ \varphi\geq 0\text{ in }\Omega.

In this case, we briefly write

(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}u\geq c(x)u\qquad\text{in }{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\Omega).

Remark 1.4

2.
Preliminaries

We collect in this section some notations and useful tools.
For
s\in(0,1)
,
H^{s}(\Omega)
denotes the space of functions
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

[u]^{2}_{H^{s}(\Omega)}:=\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)^{2}}{{|x-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dx\;dy<\infty.

It is a Hilbert space endowed with the norm

\|u\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}:=\big(\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+[u]^{2}_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\big)^{1/2}.

We denote by
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
the completion of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}
. It is known that for
s\in(1/2,1)
,
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)}=[\cdot]_{H^{s}(\Omega)}
(which is equivalent to the usual one in
H^{s}(\Omega)
thanks to a Poincaré-type inequality) and it can be characterized as follows

H^{s}_{0}(\Omega):=\big\{u\in H^{s}(\Omega):u=0\ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega\big\}.

Next, we define
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)_{+}
by

H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)_{+}:=\big\{u\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega):u\geq 0\ \text{in}\ \Omega\big\}.

For
u,v\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
, we consider the symmetric, continuous, and coercive bilinear form

{\mathcal{E}}(u,v):=\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(v(x)-v(y)\big)}{{|x-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dx\;dy.

The first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}
in
\Omega
can be defined by

(2.1)

\lambda_{1}(\Omega)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{{\mathcal{E}}(u,u)}{\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}.

It holds
\lambda_{1}(\Omega)>0
, with the corresponding eigenfunction unique and strictly positive in
\Omega
.

Given
x\in\Omega
and
r>0
, we denote by
B_{r}(x)
the open ball centred at
x
with radius
r
.
We denote by
u^{+}:=\max\{u,0\}
and
u^{-}:=\max\{-u,0\}
the positive and negative part of
u
respectively. We also recall that, if
u\in H^{s}(\Omega)
, then
u^{+},u^{-}\in H^{s}(\Omega)
as well: this follows from a simple calculation, indeed
u=u^{+}-u^{-}
and

[u]_{H^{s}(\Omega)}^{2}={\mathcal{E}}(u,u)={\mathcal{E}}(u^{+},u^{+})-2{\mathcal{E}}(u^{+},u^{-})+{\mathcal{E}}(u^{-},u^{-})

where

{\mathcal{E}}(u^{+},u^{-})=\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\big(u^{+}(x)-u^{+}(y)\big)\big(u^{-}(x)-u^{-}(y)\big)}{{|x-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dx\;dy=\\
=-c_{N,s}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{u^{+}(x)\,u^{-}(y)}{{|x-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dx\;dy\leq 0.

3.
Proof of the Hopf lemma: the case of pointwise super-solutions

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1.5
. Before doing this, we need one key result: we state and prove a strong maximum principle for pointwise super-solutions of (
1.3
).

Proposition 3.1

(Strong maximum principle for pointwise super-solutions)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded open set.
Let
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
u:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a lower semicontinuous function super-solution (in the sense of Definition
1.1
) of (
1.3
).

(i)

If
c\leq 0
in
\Omega
and
u\geq 0
on
\partial\Omega
, then either
u
vanishes identically in
\Omega
, or
u>0
in
\Omega
.

(ii)

If
u\geq 0
in
\overline{\Omega}
, then either
u
vanishes identically in
\Omega
, or
u>0
in
\Omega
.

Proof.

Before going into the proof, we start by proving that the function
u
is nonnegative in
\overline{\Omega}
as long as the hypotheses of assertion
(i)
are satisfied.

Let us assume that
c\leq 0
in
\Omega
,
u\geq 0
on
\partial\Omega
, and that
u
does not vanish identically on
\Omega
. Then we claim that

(3.1)

u\geq 0\quad\text{in}\quad\overline{\Omega}.

Assume to the contrary that (
3.1
) does not hold, that is,
u
is negative somewhere in
\Omega
. Then, using that
\overline{\Omega}
is compact together with the hypotheses of lower semicontinuity of
u
, a negative minimum of the function
u
must be achieved in
\Omega
. In other words, there exists
x_{0}\in\Omega
such that

(3.2)

u(x_{0})=\min_{x\in\Omega}u(x)<0.

Combining (
3.2
) with
u\geq 0
on
\partial\Omega
, it follows that

(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}u(x_{0})=c_{N,s}P.V.\int_{\Omega}\frac{u(x_{0})-u(y)}{{|x_{0}-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy<0.

But, since by assumption
c(x_{0})\leq 0
, we have that
c(x_{0})u(x_{0})\geq 0
.
Therefore

\displaystyle 0>(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}u(x_{0})\geq c(x_{0})u(x_{0})\geq 0.

which is a contradiction. Consequently, claim (
3.1
) follows.

So we can now suppose
u\geq 0
in
\Omega
. Suppose that
u\not\equiv 0
in
\Omega
and let us prove that

(3.3)

u>0\qquad\text{in }\Omega.

First of all, we recall that by the lower semicontinuity of
u
, there exist
x_{1}\in\Omega
and
\varepsilon_{1},r>0
such that

u(y)\geq\varepsilon_{1}\qquad\text{for all}\quad y\in B_{r}(x_{1})\subset\Omega.

If the inequality (
3.3
) were not true, that is, if
u(\tilde{x})=0
at some
\tilde{x}\in\Omega
, then it would hold

(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}u(\tilde{x})=c_{N,s}P.V.\int_{\Omega}\frac{-u(y)}{{|\tilde{x}-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy\leq c_{N,s}P.V.\int_{B_{r}(x_{1})}\frac{-u(y)}{{|\tilde{x}-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy<0

Therefore

\displaystyle 0>(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}u(\tilde{x})\geq c(\tilde{x})u(\tilde{x})=0,

a contradiction. Thus, the strict inequality
u>0
in
\Omega
must hold true.
∎

Having the above strong maximum principle, we can now give the proof of Theorem
1.5
by following some ideas in
[
13
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

From Proposition
3.1
it follows that

(3.4)

u(x)>0\qquad\text{for all }x\in\Omega

provided that
u
does not vanish identically in
\Omega
. In other words, if
u
does not vanish identically in
\Omega
, then for every compact subset
K\subset\Omega
we have

(3.5)

\inf_{y\in K}u(y)>0.

Now suppose that
u
does not vanish identically in
\Omega
and let us prove (
1.4
). To this end, it suffices to construct a barrier for
u
in terms of the solution problem (
1.8
). Let
u_{tor}
denote the pointwise solution of (
1.8
).

Next, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

(3.6)

v_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}u_{tor}(x)\qquad\text{for }x\in\overline{\Omega}.

Then, by definition and (
1.9
), by the boundedness of
\Omega
it follows that

(3.7)

v_{n}\rightarrow 0\qquad\text{uniformly in }\overline{\Omega}\text{ as }n\to\infty.

We wish now to show that there exists some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(3.8)

u\geq v_{n}\qquad\text{in }\overline{\Omega},\text{ for any }n\geq n_{0}.

In order to prove (
3.8
), we argue by contradiction: suppose that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the function
w_{n}
defined by

w_{n}:=v_{n}-u\qquad\text{in }\overline{\Omega}

is positive somewhere in
\overline{\Omega}
. Then, using that
w_{n}=v_{n}-u=-u\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega
and the compactness of
\overline{\Omega}
, a positive maximum of the upper semicontinuous function
w_{n}
(since
u
is lower semicontinuous by assumption) must be achieved at some
x_{n}\in\Omega
, that is, there exists
x_{n}\in\Omega
such that

(3.9)

w_{n}(x_{n})=\max_{x\in\Omega}w_{n}(x)>0.

This implies together with (
3.4
) that
0<u(x_{n})<v_{n}(x_{n})
. From this and thanks to (
3.7
), we find that

(3.10)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}u(x_{n})=0.

Recalling (
3.5
), we deduce from (
3.10
) that
x_{n}\rightarrow\partial\Omega
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Taking this into account, one deduces that for any compact set
K\subset\Omega
there exists
h>0
such that
|x_{n}-y|\geq h>0
for any
y\in K
and
n
sufficiently large. As a direct consequence, there exist two positive constants
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}>0
, independent of
n
such that

(3.11)

\gamma_{1}<\int_{K}\frac{dy}{{|x_{n}-y|}^{N+2s}}<\gamma_{2}\qquad\text{for $n$ sufficiently large (depending on $K$)}.

Thus we have

(3.12)

c(x_{n})u(x_{n})\leq(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}u(x_{n})=c_{N,s}\int_{K}\frac{u(x_{n})-u(y)}{{|x_{n}-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy+c_{N,s}P.V.\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\frac{u(x_{n})-u(y)}{{|x_{n}-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy.

We now aim at estimating the integrals on the right-hand side of the above inequality. Concerning the first integral, we notice that by (
3.5
), there exists a positive constant
\gamma_{3}>0
such that
u(y)\geq\gamma_{3}
for
y\in K
. As a consequence of this and by using (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), it follows that

(3.13)

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{K}\frac{u(x_{n})-u(y)}{{|x_{n}-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy\leq-\gamma_{1}\gamma_{3}<0.

Regarding the second integral in (
3.12
), we first recall that since
x_{n}
is the maximum of
w_{n}
in
\overline{\Omega}
, then by (
3.9
)

u(x_{n})-u(y)\leq v_{n}(x_{n})-v_{n}(y).

Using this, the second integral in (
3.12
) can be estimated as follows:

(3.14)

P.V.\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\frac{u(x_{n})-u(y)}{{|x_{n}-y|}^{N+2s}}\ dy\leq P.V.\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\frac{v_{n}(x_{n})-v_{n}(y)}{{|x_{n}-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy.

Moreover, a simple calculation yields

(3.15)

c_{N,s}P.V.\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\frac{v_{n}(x_{n})-v_{n}(y)}{{|x_{n}-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy=(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}v_{n}(x_{n})-c_{N,s}\int_{K}\frac{v_{n}(x_{n})-v_{n}(y)}{{|x_{n}-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy.

Now, from (
3.6
) and (
1.8
), it follows that

(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}v_{n}(x_{n})=\frac{1}{n}(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}u_{tor}(x_{n})=\frac{1}{n}.

This yields

(3.16)

(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}v_{n}(x_{n})\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty.

Combining (
3.16
), (
3.7
) and (
3.11
), we observe that the right-hand side in the equality (
3.15
) goes to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
and therefore

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P.V.\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\frac{v_{n}(x_{n})-v_{n}(y)}{{|x_{n}-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy=0.

Consequently, from (
3.14
), we get

(3.17)

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}P.V.\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\frac{u(x_{n})-u(y)}{{|x_{n}-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy\leq 0.

However, using that
c
is bounded, it follows from (
3.10
) that

(3.18)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}c(x_{n})u(x_{n})=0.

Finally, (
3.17
) and (
3.13
) into (
3.12
), lead to a contradiction with (
3.18
). Therefore, the inequality (
3.8
) follows for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
large enough.
∎

Lemma 4.1

Let

. Then
u
is a weak super-solution (in the sense of Definition
1.2
) of (
1.3
) if and only if

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(u,v)\geq\int_{\Omega}cuv\qquad\text{for any }v\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)_{+}.

Proof.

Fix
v\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)_{+}
and let
{(\psi_{n})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
a sequence of nonnegative functions converging to
v
in the
H^{s}(\Omega)
-norm. By Definition
1.2
we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(u,\psi_{n})\geq\int_{\Omega}cu\psi_{n}\qquad\text{for any }n\in\mathbb{N}.

On the left-hand side we have the convergence
{\mathcal{E}}(u,\psi_{n})\to{\mathcal{E}}(u,v)
as
n\to\infty
by construction; so, let us deal with right-hand side. By the Sobolev embedding we have
\psi_{n}\to v
as
n\to\infty
in
L^{2^{*}_{s}}(\Omega)
, with
2^{*}_{s}=\frac{2N}{N-2s}
. So, we have the convergence

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}cu\psi_{n}\longrightarrow\int_{\Omega}cuv\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

if
cu\in L^{\frac{2N}{N+2s}}(\Omega)
where
\frac{2N}{N+2s}=(2^{*}_{s})^{\prime}
is the conjugate exponent of
2^{*}_{s}
, which is what we show next. This indeed follows from the Hölder inequality:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\big|cu\big|^{\frac{2N}{N+2s}}=\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\big|c\big|^{\frac{N}{2s}}\bigg)^{\frac{4s}{N+2s}}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\big|u\big|^{\frac{2N}{N-2s}}\bigg)^{\frac{N-2s}{N+2s}}<\infty.

Then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(u,v)=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{E}}(u,\psi_{n})\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}cu\psi_{n}=\int_{\Omega}cuv.

∎

Proposition 4.2

(Strong maximum principle for weak super-solutions)
.

Let
c\in L^{q}(\Omega)
, with
q>\frac{N}{2s}
, and
u\in H^{s}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}_{loc}(\Omega)
be a weak super-solution of

(4.1)

(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}u=c(x)u\qquad\text{in }\Omega.

(i)

If
c\leq 0
in
\Omega
and
u\geq 0
on
\partial\Omega
, then either
u
vanishes identically in
\Omega
or
u>0
in
\Omega
.

(ii)

If
u\geq 0
in
\Omega
, then either
u
vanishes identically in
\Omega
or
u>0
in
\Omega
.

Proof.

We first recall the following elementary inequality:

(4.2)

\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(u^{-}(x)-u^{-}(y)\big)\leq-\big(u^{-}(x)-u^{-}(y)\big)^{2},\qquad\text{for any }x,y\in\Omega.

Assume then
c\leq 0
in
\Omega
and
u\geq 0
on
\partial\Omega
. Then
u^{-}=0
on
\partial\Omega
. Moreover, by standard arguments, we also know
u^{-}\in H^{s}(\Omega)
. Therefore
u^{-}\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)_{+}
. Hence, by testing (
4.1
) on
u^{-}
(which is allowed by Lemma
4.1
), we have from inequality (
4.2
) that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}c(x)u(x)u^{-}(x)\;dx\leq{\mathcal{E}}(u,u^{-})\leq-{\mathcal{E}}(u^{-},u^{-}).

Moreover,
u=u^{+}-u^{-}
with
u^{+}u^{-}\equiv 0
in
\Omega
, which yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}c(x)u^{-}(x)^{2}\;dx\geq{\mathcal{E}}(u^{-},u^{-})\geq\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\|u^{-}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where
\lambda_{1}(\Omega)
has been defined in (
2.1
). Since
\lambda_{1}(\Omega)>0
, then from the nonpositivity of
c
it follows

\|u^{-}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0

implying that
u^{-}=0
a.e. in
\Omega
, that is,
u\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
.

So we can at this point assume that
u\geq 0
in
\Omega
. Note that the fact that
u
is a weak super-solution implies in particular that
u
is also a distributional super-solution. Indeed, for any
\psi\in\ C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
,
\psi\geq 0
in
\Omega
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}cu\psi\leq{\mathcal{E}}(u,\psi)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(\psi(x)-\psi(y)\big)}{{|x-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dx;dy

\displaystyle=c_{N,s}\int_{\Omega}u(x)\,P.V.\int_{\Omega}\frac{\psi(x)-\psi(y)}{{|x-y|}^{N+2s}}\;dy\;dx=\int_{\Omega}u(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}\psi.

Using this remark, we can use Proposition
1.7
.
∎

5.
Proof of the strong maximum principle for distributional super-solutions

This last section is devoted to the proof of Proposition
1.7
. In the following, we assume that
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a distributional super-solution (in the sense of Definition
1.3
) of (
1.3
) and that
c
satisfies the assumptions in (
1.10
).

5.1.
Regional v. restricted fractional Laplacian

Note that

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}_{\Omega}\psi=(-\Delta)^{s}\psi-\kappa_{\Omega}\psi\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad\kappa_{\Omega}(x)=c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}\frac{dy}{{|x-y|}^{N+2s}}\quad\text{for }x\in\Omega,

where we recall (
1.2
),
so that Definition
1.3
is equivalent to (if we extend
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
)

(5.1)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u\geq\big(c+\kappa_{\Omega}\big)u\quad\text{in }{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\Omega),\qquad c\in L^{q}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega),\ q>\frac{N}{2s}.

5.2.
Approximation and representation of distributional solutions

Consider a solution
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to[0,+\infty)
to (
5.1
) with

(5.2)

\displaystyle u\in L^{\alpha}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega),\qquad\alpha>\frac{Nq}{2sq-N},\penalty\ \text{and}\quad u=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.

Take
\eta_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{\varepsilon})
a mollifier. If we take an open
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
then for any
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega^{\prime}),\ \psi\geq 0,
it holds
\psi*\eta_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
for
\varepsilon
small independently of
\psi
and we can say

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(u*\eta_{\varepsilon}\big)(-\Delta)^{s}\psi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\big(\eta_{\varepsilon}*(-\Delta)^{s}\psi\big)=\int_{\Omega}u(-\Delta)^{s}\big(\psi*\eta_{\varepsilon})\geq\int_{\Omega}\big(c+\kappa_{\Omega}\big)u\big(\psi*\eta_{\varepsilon}\big)=\\
=\int_{\Omega}\Big[\Big(\big(c+\kappa_{\Omega}\big)u\Big)*\eta_{\varepsilon}\Big]\psi

which implies that

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}\big(u*\eta_{\varepsilon}\big)\geq\Big(\big(c+\kappa_{\Omega}\big)u\Big)*\eta_{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{in }\Omega^{\prime}.

As
u*\eta_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega^{\prime}})
, the above inequality also holds in a pointwise sense.
We can then exploit a Green representation on
u*\eta_{\varepsilon}
(see
[
4
]
) to deduce that for any
x\in\Omega^{\prime\prime}\subset\subset\Omega^{\prime}
and
0<r<\textnormal{dist}(\Omega^{\prime\prime},\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega^{\prime})

(5.3)

\big(u*\eta_{\varepsilon}\big)(x)\geq r^{2s}\int_{B_{1}}G(0,y)\Big[\Big(\big(c+\kappa_{\Omega}\big)u\Big)*\eta_{\varepsilon}\Big](x+ry)\;dy\;+\\
+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}}P(0,y)\big(u*\eta_{\varepsilon}\big)(x+ry)\;dy.

Here we have used the kernels
G
and
P
which are respectively the Green function and the Poisson kernel of the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
on the unitary ball
B_{1}
, which are explicitly known, see
[
4
]
:

\displaystyle G(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{N,s}}{{|x-y|}^{N-2s}}\int_{0}^{\frac{(1-|x|^{2})(1-|y|^{2})}{|x-y|^{2}}}\frac{t^{s-1}}{(t+1)^{N/2}}\;dt

\displaystyle x,y\in B_{1},

\displaystyle P(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{N,s}}{|x-y|^{N}}\bigg(\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{|y|^{2}-1}\bigg)^{s}

\displaystyle x\in B_{1},\ y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}.

From now on, we assume that
u\geq 0
in
\Omega
. We want to send
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
5.3
) and deduce a representation for
u
.
For the Poisson integral we use the nonnegativity of
u
and the Fatou’s Lemma to say

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}}P(0,y)\big(u*\eta_{\varepsilon}\big)(x+ry)\;dy\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}}P(0,y)\,u(x+ry)\;dy.

For the Green integral we use that

\displaystyle G(0,\cdot)\in L^{p}(B_{1})\qquad\text{for any }p\in\Big[1,\frac{N}{N-2s}\Big)

and

\Big\|\Big(\big(c+\kappa_{\Omega}\big)u\Big)*\eta_{\varepsilon}\Big\|_{L^{\beta}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leq C\Big\|\big(c+\kappa_{\Omega}\big)u\Big\|_{L^{\beta}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leq C\|cu\|_{L^{\beta}(\Omega^{\prime})}+C\big\|\kappa_{\Omega}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})}\|u\|_{L^{\alpha}(\Omega^{\prime})}\\
\text{for any }\beta\in\Big(\frac{N}{2s},\alpha\Big)

where, moreover, by the Hölder inequality

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}{|cu|}^{\beta}\leq\|c\|^{\frac{1}{\beta}}_{L^{q}(\Omega^{\prime})}\big\|u^{\beta}\big\|_{L^{q/(q-\beta)}(\Omega^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\qquad\text{for }\ \frac{N}{2s}<\beta<q,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}u^{\beta q/(q-\beta)}<\infty

\displaystyle\qquad\text{for }\ \frac{\beta q}{q-\beta}<\alpha,

where the second inequality holds for
\beta
close to
\frac{N}{2s}
in view of (
5.2
).
Therefore, using the weak topology in Lebesgue spaces,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{B_{1}}G(0,y)\Big[\Big(\big(c+\kappa_{\Omega}\big)u\Big)*\eta_{\varepsilon}\Big](x+ry)\;dy=\\
=\int_{B_{1}}G(0,y)\,\big(c+\kappa_{\Omega}\big)(x+ry)\,u(x+ry)\;dy.

Thus

(5.4)

\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega^{\prime\prime}.

5.3.
The Hardy-Littlewood maximal function

Recall that, given

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{M}[f]\big\|_{L^{p}(K)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}(K)}\qquad\text{for }p>1\text{ and }K\subset\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}\text{ measurable}.

\displaystyle=C\big\|u\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})}^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\int_{B_{1}}\bigg(\frac{1}{\rho_{j}^{N}}\int_{B_{\rho_{j}}(x_{j})}u(x+r_{j}y)\;dx\bigg)\;dy.
