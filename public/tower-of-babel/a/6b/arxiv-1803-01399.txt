Ancient Solutions to Curve Shortening with Finite Total Curvature
Sigurd Angenent, Qian You

Abstract
. We construct ancient solutions to Curve Shortening in the plane whose total curvature is uniformly bounded by gluing together an arbitrary chain of given Grim Reapers along their common asymptotes.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Ancient solutions

A family of plane curves
\{\mathcal{C}_{t}:t_{0}<t<t_{1}\}
moves by Curve Shortening if for some parametrization
\mathcal{C}:{\mathbb{R}}\times(t_{0},t_{1})\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
one has

(1)

\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{C}}{\partial t}\right)^{\perp}=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{C}}{\partial s^{2}}.

Here
(\partial_{t}\mathcal{C})^{\perp}
is the component of the velocity vector
\mathcal{C}_{t}
which is perpendicular to the curve, and
\partial/\partial s
stands for the arc length derivative along the curve.

An ancient solution of Curve Shortening is a solution
\mathcal{C}_{t}
that is defined for all
t\in(-\infty,t_{1})
, for some
t_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. While it can be shown that for any reasonably smooth initial plane curve
\mathcal{C}_{0}
a unique solution
\{\mathcal{C}_{t}:0<t<t_{1}\}
to Curve Shortening exists with
\mathcal{C}_{0}
as initial curve, the requirement that a solution be defined for all times
t\in(-\infty,t_{1})
for some given
t_{1}
is much more restrictive. Two ancient solutions that have been known for a long time are the shrinking circle (with radius
r(t)=\sqrt{2(T-t)}
, and some arbitrary but fixed center), and the translating soliton known as the
Grim Reaper
. This last curve is up to rotation and translation defined by the equation

(2)

x-C=\frac{1}{v}G\bigl(v(y-a)\bigr)+vt,\text{ for }0<y<\frac{\pi}{v},t\in{\mathbb{R}},

where
v>0
is the velocity of the soliton, and where, by definition,

G(y)=-\ln\sin y.

There is a short list of other known examples of ancient solutions to Curve Shortening. The
Abresch-Langer curves
are compact, immersed curves that shrink self similarly to their center of mass. Except for the circle, none of these curves are embedded. The
paper-clip solution,
found by Angenent
[
4
]
and also by Nakayama, Iizuka, and Wadati
[
11
]
(Figure
1
, top) is a convex embedded curve which for
t\to-\infty
is asymptotically described by two Grim Reaper solutions with the same asymptotes, moving toward each other. Another ancient solution is the
Yin-Yang curve,
which is a spiral shaped so that it evolves simply by rotating at a steady rate. (The curve was introduced by Altschuler in
[
2
]
. See also
[
9
,
1
]
.) There is also the
Ancient Sine Wave
discovered by Nakayama, Iizuka, and Wadati (see
[
11
]
and also Figure
1
.) Both the paper-clip and the ancient sine wave have explicit parametrizations.

Figure 1.
Ancient convex solutions to Curve Shortening: the paperclip solution (top), the Yin-Yang rotating soliton
[
2
]
(bottom left), and the Ancient Sine Curve
[
11
]
(bottom right).

One can try to order ancient solutions by their total curvature. The total curvature of a plane curve
\mathcal{C}
(compact or not) is defined to be

K(\mathcal{C})=\int_{\mathcal{C}}|\kappa|\,\mathrm{d}s

It is well known that the total curvature is a monotone quantity along a family of curves
\mathcal{C}_{t}
that evolve by Curve Shortening
[
8
,
3
]
. One has

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}K(\mathcal{C}_{t})=-2\sum_{P_{i}(t)}\left|\frac{\partial\kappa}{\partial s}\Bigr|_{P_{i}}\right|

where the sum is taken over all inflection points
P_{i}(t)
of the curve
\mathcal{C}_{t}
.

The total curvature of a closed curve is always at least
2\pi
, and a closed curve
\mathcal{C}
is convex if and only if its total curvature is exactly
2\pi
. The Daskalopoulos-Hamilton-Sesum theorem
[
6
]
is a classification of all ancient solutions of this type. They showed that any ancient embedded, convex, and compact solution must be either a shrinking circle, or else the ancient paper-clip solution.

At the other extreme there are ancient solutions of infinite total curvature, e.g. the Yin-Yang curve, and the Ancient Sine Wave.

1.2.
Main result

We construct a large number of ancient solutions with uniformly bounded total curvature. To be specific, let two sets of numbers
\{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}\}
and
\{C_{1},\dots,C_{n}\}
be given. Then we will construct an ancient solution
\{\mathcal{C}_{t}\mid t<0\}
of Curve Shortening that crosses the
y
-axis at
n+1
different points
\{P_{0}(t),\dots,P_{n}(t)\}
and for which for each
k=1,2,\dots,n
, the arc
P_{k-1}(t)P_{k}(t)
converges to the translating soliton
\mathcal{G}_{k}(t)
defined by

(3)

\mathcal{G}_{k}(t):\qquad x-C_{k}=G_{k}(y,t)=(-1)^{k}\left\{\frac{1}{v_{k}}G\bigl(v_{k}|y-a_{k-1}|\bigr)+v_{k}t\right\}

as
t\to-\infty
. Here

v_{k}=\frac{\pi}{|a_{k}-a_{k-1}|}

is the asymptotic velocity of the
k^{\rm th}
arc in the solution
\mathcal{C}_{t}
that we construct.

Figure 2.
Two ancient solutions constructed in this paper from the same data
\{a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}
and
\{C_{0},C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}\}
. The solution on top is compact, the bottom solution is asymptotic to the two horizontal lines
y=a_{0}
,
y=a_{n}
.

The solutions we construct come in two varieties: compact, and non compact. To obtain compact solutions we assume that there is a final arc
P_{n}(t)P_{0}(t)
connecting the last and first intersection points. As
t\to-\infty
this arc is asymptotic to the translating soliton
x=G_{n+1}(y,t)
, given by (
3
), with velocity
v_{0}=v_{n+1}=\pi/|a_{0}-a_{n}|
.

To obtain non-compact solutions we let the initial arc ending at
P_{0}(t)
be asymptotic to the straight line
y=a_{0}
, and similarly, we let the final arc be asymptotic to the line
y=a_{n}
. See Figure
2
.

For any compact immersed curve the number of intersections with a straight line, such as the
y
-axis, must be even. Therefore one can only construct non-compact solutions when
n
is odd.

We will construct these solutions in three stages. It turns out that convex ancient solutions are easier to construct. An immersed curve in the plane is convex if it has no inflection points, i.e. if the curvature never vanishes on the curve. Such curves need not be embedded (e.g. consider a cardioid). Thus we first construct ancient convex immersed solutions in section
2
. Then, in section
3
, we construct embedded ancient solutions. Finally, in section
4
we combine the results and arguments from sections
2
and
3
to deal with the most general case of ancient solutions that are neither embedded nor convex.

2.
Construction of ancient convex solutions

2.1.
The convexity assumption

In this section we assume that the asymptotes appear in alternating order, i.e.

(4)

\textit{$\forall k\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ either }a_{k}<\min\left\{a_{k-1},a_{k+1}\right\}\textit{ or }a_{k}>\max\left\{a_{k-1},a_{k+1}\right\}

in which we agree to define
a_{n+1}=a_{0}

2.4.
The broken solution

Assuming the convexity hypothesis (
4
) we introduce a family of piecewise smooth curves
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{t}
such that
\{\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{t}\}_{t\leq t_{0}}
is a solution to Curve Shortening, except at those points where it is not smooth. For simplicity we will describe the construction of the noncompact ancient solution with data
\{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}\}
and
\{C_{1},\dots,C_{n}\}
. The construction of the compact curve with the same data is very similar.

We have a collection of translating solitons
\mathcal{G}_{k}(t):\;x=G_{k}(y,t)
, for
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\mathcal{G}_{k}(t)
and
\mathcal{G}_{k+1}(t)
share a common asymptote
y=a_{k}
. There is a

such that for all

\mathcal{G}_{k}(t)

\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{t}
to be the concatenation of the following arcs (see also Figure

•

\mathcal{G}_{1}(t)

•

\mathcal{G}_{i}(t)

•

\mathcal{G}_{n}(t)
starting at

.

2.5.
The really-old-but-not-ancient solutions

The curves
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
are not smooth so they are not a solution of Curve Shortening. To construct an actual solution near
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
, we introduce a sequence
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
of solutions to Curve Shortening, each defined on some time interval starting at
t=-j
, and with initial value

\mathcal{C}_{j}(-j)=\bar{\mathcal{C}}(-j).

Since
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(-j)
is a piecewise smooth curve the solution
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
exists for
t\in[-j,T_{j})
for some
T_{j}>-j
. We will show that

(7)

T_{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\inf_{j\geq 1}T_{j}>-\infty.

In addition, we will show that one can extract a subsequence of the solutions
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
that converges on any time interval
[T_{-},T_{*}]
with
T_{-}<T_{*}
. The limit of such a convergent subsequence is then our desired ancient solution.

Our proof of (
7
) and the convergence of some subsequence requires two ingredients: the fact that the smooth curves
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
“lie on one side” of
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
, and an estimate for the area between the two curves. To explain this in more detail let us first compare the solution
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
and the broken solution
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
for
t>-j
.

2.6.
Orientation of
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)

We can orient each smooth arc of the broken solution
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
by traversing it in such a way that the curve always “bends to the left.” More precisely, we choose the unit tangent and normal
\{{\boldsymbol{T}},{\boldsymbol{N}}\}
at any smooth location to be a right handed basis for which
{\boldsymbol{N}}
points in the direction of curvature (
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{ss}=k{\boldsymbol{N}}
with
k>0
). It is clear from the construction in §
2.4
of the broken curve that all corner points are “convex” in the sense that these local orientations match at the corner points (see also figures
3
,
4
, and
5
). In any small neighborhood of a point on the curve
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
we can therefore distinguish unambiguously between points that lie to the left of the curve (i.e. in the direction of
{\boldsymbol{N}}
) and points to the right of the curve.

2.7.
Separation between
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
and
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)

The broken solution
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
consists of smooth arcs
A_{i-1}(t)A_{i}(t)
(
1\leq i\leq n
) which evolve by Curve Shortening. Since
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
is built out of these arcs at time
t=-j
, it will be very close to
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
for
t>-j
close to
-j
. Initially it is only at the corners
A_{i}(t)
where the deviation of
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
from
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
is considerable. A local analysis of the solutions near any corner point shows that, since the corners are convex, the solution
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
will lie to the left of the broken solution
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
. In fact, the asymptotic shape of the smooth solution
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
near any corner
A_{i}(t)
is given by a “Brakke wedge” (
[
5
,
1
]
) See Figure
6
.

\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
and
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)

In general, the fact that the corners of
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
are convex implies that if
\mathcal{C}^{*}(t)
is a solution of Curve Shortening that starts at time

in a narrow strip to the left of
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(-j)
, then
\mathcal{C}^{*}(t)
will remain on the left of
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
because the maximum principle keeps it from crossing the smooth parts of
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
, and a smooth curve on the left of
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
cannot touch any of the corner points. By approximating the initial curve
\mathcal{C}_{j}(-j)=\bar{\mathcal{C}}(-j)
with smooth curves
\mathcal{C}_{j,\eta}
that run parallel to
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(-j)
at a distance no more than

on the left, and then letting

, we find that the same holds for
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
. Thus it follows that initially, for
t
shortly after

, the whole solution
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
will lie in a narrow strip on the left of
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
(i.e. in the direction of its normal). See Figure

2.8.
The area between
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
and
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)

For as long as
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
is close to
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
, and lies on the left of
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
we consider the area
A_{j}(t)
of the strip enclosed by the two curves. This area is to be counted with multiplicity as some points may be enclosed more than once. We define this area to be

(8)

A_{j}(t)=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)}xdy-\int_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)}xdy,

where both curves are given the orientation described in §
2.6
.

2.9.
Area growth lemma

The area
A_{j}(t)
increases according to

(9)

A_{j}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Theta_{k}(t).

There exist
T_{0}<0
and there exist constants
C,\delta>0
such that
A_{j}(t)
is bounded by

(10)

A_{j}(t)\leq Ce^{\delta t}

for all
t\leq T_{0}
and for all
j\geq T_{0}
.

Proof.
A family of arcs
\mathcal{C}:[a,b]\times(t_{0},t_{1})\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
that evolve by Curve Shortening sweeps out area according to

\frac{dA}{dt}=\int_{\mathcal{C}}Vds=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\kappa ds=\bigl[\Delta\theta\bigr]_{\partial\mathcal{C}}.

In other words, the rate at which the arc sweeps out area is exactly the change in the tangent angle
\theta
as one goes along the curve. The rate at which a piecewise smooth solution to Curve Shortening sweeps out area is obtained by summing
[\Delta\theta]_{\mathcal{C}}
over each smooth arc.

For the smooth curves
\mathcal{C}_{j}
the change in
\theta
is
n\pi
:

\bigl[\Delta\theta\bigr]_{\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)}=n\pi.

Namely, the curve starts at the first asymptote
y=a_{0}
and then makes
n
turns before ending at the last asymptote
y=a_{n}
. For the broken curves
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
the tangent angle also starts at
\theta=0
and ends at
n\pi
, but along the way
\theta
makes small jumps of size
\Theta_{i}(t)
at each corner point
A_{i}(t)
. Thus for the broken solution we get

\bigl[\Delta\theta\bigr]_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)}=n\pi-\sum_{k}\Theta_{k}(t).

Subtracting the two we find that the rate at which the area between
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
and
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
grows is as given by (
9
).

Once we have proved (
9
), it follows immediately from (
6
) that
A_{j}^{\prime}(t)\leq Ce^{\delta t}
for some
C>0
, and for any

\delta<\min_{1\leq k\leq n}v_{k-1}v_{k}.

Since
A_{j}(-j)=0
, we have

A_{j}(t)=\int_{-j}^{t}A_{j}^{\prime}(\tau)\,d\tau\leq C\int_{-j}^{t}e^{\delta\tau}\,d\tau\leq\frac{C}{\delta}e^{\delta t},

which then leads to the estimate (
10
).

Lemma

For any point
P
on
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
there is a point
Q
on
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
such that the distance between
P
and

is at most
Ce^{\delta t/2}
.

To prove the Lemma we let

and let

is disjoint from

, if

. Moreover, the curve

.

2.11.
Conclusion of the construction in the convex case

It is now possible to complete the argument that was started in §
2.5
. Our estimates show that the solution
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
lies in a strip of width at most
Ce^{\delta t}
to the left of the broken solution
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
, at least for as long as
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
exists. The constants
C
and
\delta
do not depend on
j
.

We claim that the time
T_{j}
at which
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
becomes singular is bounded from below by some
T_{*}
that does not depend on
j
(cf. (
7
)). If this were not the case, then according to Grayson’s description
[
8
]
of nearly singular curves, there would be a sequence of times
t_{j}\to-\infty
at which the curve
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t_{j})
would have an arc of arbitrarily short length (say, no more than
2^{-j}
) with total curvature arbitrarily close to
\pi
(say, at least
\pi-2^{-j}
). This is impossible because
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t_{j})
is a smooth convex curve without inflection points which stays in a strip of width
Ce^{\delta t_{j}}
to the left of
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t_{j})
.

3.
Construction of embedded ancient non convex solutions

3.1.
Embedded eternal solutions

We again assume that a finite sequence of heights
a_{0},\dots,a_{n}
and a corresponding sequence of horizontal shifts
C_{1},\dots,C_{n}
are given. Instead of the convexity hypothesis (
4
) we now assume that the
a_{i}
are monotone

(11)

a_{0}<a_{1}<\dots<a_{n-1}<a_{n}\,.

These data determine a sequence of Grim Reapers
\mathcal{G}_{k}(t)
as in (
3
). The monotonicity (
11
) of the heights
a_{i}
implies that the
\mathcal{G}_{k}(t)
are disjoint, and that two consecutive Grim Reapers
\mathcal{G}_{k}(t)
and
\mathcal{G}_{k+1}(t)
have the line
y=a_{k}
as common asymptote.

We will now construct an ancient solution
\mathcal{C}(t)
which lies within the strip
a_{0}<y<a_{n}
, and which for
t\to-\infty
is asymptotic to the Grim Reapers
\mathcal{G}_{k}(t)
. By construction our solution will be a graph
x=u(y,t)
over the interval
a_{0}<y<a_{n}
. Because of this it will never become singular and is therefore in fact an “eternal solution.”

The outline of our construction is the same as for the convex ancient solutions from §
2
: we begin by defining an approximate solution
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
of Curve Shortening for
t<-T_{*}
obtained by gluing together the given family of Grim Reapers. Then we consider the old-but-not-ancient solutions
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
to Curve Shortening that at
t=-j
start with
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)=\bar{\mathcal{C}}(-j)
. We then show that the
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
are so close to the approximate solutions
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
that one can extract a convergent subsequence whose limit is an ancient solution.

The approximate solution

\eta\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
with
\eta^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
,
\eta(x)=0

, and
\eta(x)=1

(when

We can then replace the two Grim Reaper branches

\bar{g}(x,t)=\eta(x)g_{k+1}(x,t)+\bigl(1-\eta(x)\bigr)g_{k}(x,t).

It is easy to verify that

g_{k}(x,t)\leq\bar{g}(x,t)\leq g_{k+1}(x,t),\text{ and }\bar{g}_{x}(x,t)>0.

We repeat this procedure for each
k=1,\dots,n-1
which, in the end, results in a curve
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
with the following properties for all

4.
Construction of general ancient non convex solutions

So far we have shown how one can glue together Grim Reapers so as to construct ancient solutions that are either convex or that are embedded. In this sections we show how one can combine the two constructions to produce ancient solutions by gluing any finite chain of Grim Reapers with matching asymptotes.

4.1.

Let

5.1.
Proof of Lemma
2.2

Without loss of generality we may assume that
a_{k\pm 1}>a_{k}
, and that

\displaystyle G_{k}(y,t)=v_{k}t+C_{k}+\frac{1}{v_{k}}G\bigl(v_{k}(y-a_{k})\bigr),

\displaystyle G_{k+1}(y,t)=-v_{k+1}t-C_{k+1}-\frac{1}{v_{k+1}}G\bigl(v_{k+1}(y-a_{k})\bigr).

Here we have used
G(s)=G(\pi-s)
to arrive at the expression for
G_{k}
.

Let
b_{k}=a_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\min\{|a_{k-1}-a_{k}|,|a_{k+1}-a_{k}|\}
.

Existence of the intersection between the two Grim Reapers

and
x=G_{k+1}(y,t)
in the interval

follows from the fact that as

and

\displaystyle G_{k}(b_{k},t)=C_{k}+\frac{1}{v_{k}}G(v_{k}(b_{k}-a_{k}))+v_{k}t\to-\infty,

\displaystyle G_{k+1}(b_{k},t)=-C_{k+1}-\frac{1}{v_{k+1}}G(v_{k+1}(b_{k}-a_{k}))-v_{k+1}t\to+\infty.

Thus
G_{k+1}(y,t)-G_{k}(y,t)
changes sign on the interval
(a_{k},b_{k})
.

Uniqueness of the intersection follows from the fact that
G_{k}
is decreasing while
G_{k+1}
is increasing on the interval
(a_{k},b_{k})
.

To find the asymptotic location of the intersection we solve the two equations

, resulting in

(18a)

\displaystyle y-a_{k}=\frac{1}{v_{k}}\arcsin e^{v_{k}(x-v_{k}t-C_{k})}

(18b)

\displaystyle y-a_{k}=\frac{1}{v_{k+1}}\arcsin e^{-v_{k+1}(x+v_{k+1}t+C_{k+1})}.

As
t\to-\infty
, the two Grim Reapers separate and their intersection approaches their common asymptote
y=a_{k}
, so we may assume that
\eta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}y-a_{k}\to 0
. We then get

\displaystyle\eta+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3})=\frac{1}{v_{k}}e^{v_{k}(x-v_{k}t-C_{k})},

\displaystyle\eta+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3})=\frac{1}{v_{k+1}}e^{-v_{k+1}(x+v_{k+1}t+C_{k+1})}.

Use
\eta+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3})=\eta(1+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}))=\eta e^{\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})}
,

v_{k}(x-v_{k}t-C_{k})-\ln v_{k}=-v_{k+1}(x+v_{k+1}t+C_{k+1})-\ln v_{k+1}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}).

and rearrange terms

(v_{k}+v_{k+1})x=\bigl(v_{k}^{2}-v_{k+1}^{2}\bigr)t+v_{k}C_{k}-v_{k+1}C_{k+1}+\ln\frac{v_{k}}{v_{k+1}}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}).

Finally we find

(19)

x=\bigl(v_{k}-v_{k+1}\bigr)t+c_{k}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})

with

c_{k}=\frac{v_{k}C_{k}-v_{k+1}C_{k+1}+\ln v_{k}/v_{k+1}}{v_{k}+v_{k+1}}.

Substitute
x-v_{k}t=-v_{k+1}t+c_{k}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})
in (

) to get

\eta+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3})=\frac{1}{v_{k}}e^{v_{k}v_{k+1}t+c_{k}^{\prime}}\bigl(1+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})\bigr),\text{ i.e. }\eta=\mathcal{O}\bigl(e^{v_{k}v_{k+1}t}\bigr),

for some constant
c_{k}^{\prime}
. Together with (
19

5
).

Since the arcs
\mathcal{A}_{i}
evolve by Curve Shortening, we have

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle=x_{ss}+\lambda(u,t)x_{s}

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=y_{ss}+\lambda(u,t)y_{s}

where
\lambda
is the tangential velocity of the parametrization. Thus we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}ydx

\displaystyle=\bigl[yx_{t}\bigr]_{\partial\mathcal{A}_{i}}+\int_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}\bigl(y_{ss}x_{s}-x_{ss}y_{s}\bigr)ds.

Finally, if
\theta
is the tangent angle to the arc, then

x_{s}=\cos\theta,\quad y_{s}=\sin\theta,\quad x_{ss}=-(\sin\theta)\,\theta_{s},\quad y_{ss}=(\cos\theta)\,\theta_{s}.

Hence

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}ydx

\displaystyle=\bigl[yx_{t}\bigr]_{\partial\mathcal{A}_{i}}+\int_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}\bigl(y_{ss}x_{s}-x_{ss}y_{s}\bigr)ds

\displaystyle=\bigl[yx_{t}\bigr]_{\partial\mathcal{A}_{i}}+\int_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}\theta_{s}\,ds

\displaystyle=\bigl[yx_{t}+\theta\bigr]_{\partial\mathcal{A}_{i}}.

If we now add this over all smooth arcs
\mathcal{A}_{i}(t)
, then the
yx_{t}
terms cancel because each corner point
A_{k}(t)
contributes the same term twice, once from each arc ending at the corner point. At the end of the asymptotes
y
is bounded (converges to either
a_{0}
or
a_{n}
), while
x_{t}
vanishes.

The
[\theta]_{\partial\mathcal{A}_{i}(t)}
terms together add up to the difference between the changes of
\theta
along
\mathcal{C}_{j}(t)
and
\bar{\mathcal{C}}(t)
, so that we recover the result in (
9
).
