The rank of random matrices over finite fields Thanks: Gao’s research is supported by ARC DE170100716 and ARC DP160100835.
Amin Coja-Oghlan, Pu Gao

Abstract
. We determine the rank of a random matrix 𝑨 \boldsymbol{A} over a finite field with prescribed numbers of non-zero entries in each row and column. As an application we obtain a formula for the rate of low-density parity check codes. This formula verifies a conjecture of Lelarge [Proc. IEEE Information Theory Workshop 2013]. The proofs are based on coupling arguments and the interpolation method from mathematical physics. MSC: 05C80, 60B20, 94B05



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and motivation

Random matrices over finite fields count among the most basic objects of probabilistic combinatorics.
Their study goes back to the early days of the discipline
[
27
]
.
More recently they have been at the centre of an exciting development in coding theory.
The subject of a tremendous amount of research over the past 20 years,
low density parity check
(‘ldpc’) codes have become a mainstay of modern communications standards; you probably carry some around in your pocket
[
61
]
.
The codebook of an ldpc code comprises the kernel of a sparse random matrix over a finite field.
Celebrated recent results establish that ldpc codes meet the Shannon bound, i.e., that they are information-theoretically optimal
[
33
]
.
The practical relevance of these results derives from the fact that ldpc codes based on matrices drawn from carefully tailored distributions even admit efficient decoding algorithms
[
47
,
61
]
.
In addition, sparse random matrices over finite fields have been studied extensively in the theory of random constraint satisfaction problems, e.g.,
[
1
,
24
,
25
,
36
,
60
]
.

Despite the great interest in the subject certain fundamental questions remained open.
The most obvious one concerns the rank.
Although this parameter was already studied in early contributions
[
5
,
6
,
45
]
, there has been no general formula for the rank of sparse random matrices, where the number of non-zero entries grows linearly with the number of rows.
The present paper delivers such a formula.
To be precise, we will determine the rank of a sparse random matrix with prescribed row and column degrees (viz. number of non-zero entries).
Ldpc codes are based on precisely such random matrices as a diligent choice of the degrees greatly boosts the code’s performance
[
61
]
.
The rank of the random matrix is directly related to the
rate
of the ldpc code, defined as the nullity of the matrix divided by the number of columns, arguably the most basic parameter of any linear code.
Lelarge
[
48
]
noticed that an upper bound on the rank of the random matrix, and thus a lower bound on the rate of ldpc codes,
follows from the result on the matching number of random bipartite graphs from
[
13
]
.
He conjectured the bound to be tight.
We prove this conjecture.

In fact, there is an interesting twist.
Lelarge observed that a sophisticated but mathematically non-rigorous approach from statistical physics called the cavity method renders a wrong ‘prediction’ as to the rank for certain degree distributions.
1
1

1

The derivation of this erroneous prediction was posed as an exercise in
[
53
, Chapter 19]
.

This discrepancy merits attention because the cavity method has by now been brought to bear on a very wide range of practically relevant problems, ranging from signal processing to machine learning
[
64
]
.
The proof of the rank formula that we develop sheds light on the issue.
Specifically, the ‘replica symmetric’ version of the cavity method predicts that the rank can be expressed analytically as the optimal solution to a variational problem.
A priori, this variational problem asks to optimise a functional called the Bethe free entropy over an infinite-dimensional space of probability measures.
Such problems have been tackled in the physics literature numerically by means of a heuristic called population dynamics.
For the rank problem this was carried out by Alamino and Saad
[
3
]
.
But thanks to the algebraic nature of the problem we will be able to dramatically simplify the variational problem, arriving at a humble one-dimensional optimisation task.
The main result of the paper shows that the optimal solution to this one-dimensional problem does indeed yield the rank.
The formula matches Lelarge’s conjecture.
Furthermore, the solution can be lifted to a solution to the original infinite-dimensional problem.
For certain degree distributions the solution thus obtained is of a new type, different from the solutions that surfaced in the experiments from
[
3
]
or the heuristic derivations from
[
53
]
.
The fact that the heuristic approaches missed the actual optimiser explains the discrepancy between the physics predictions and mathematical reality.

In the following paragraphs we will introduce the model and state the main results.
A discussion of related work and a detailed comparison with the physics predictions follow in Section
1.5
.

1.2.
The rank formula

Let
q\geq 2
be a prime power and let
{\boldsymbol{\chi}}
be an
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}=\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\left\{{0}\right\}
-valued random variable.
Moreover, let
\boldsymbol{d}\geq 1,\boldsymbol{k}\geq 3
be integer-valued random variables such that
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{d}^{r}]+\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{k}^{r}]<\infty
for a real
r>2
and set
d=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{d}]
,
k=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{k}]
.
Let
n>0
be an integer divisible by the greatest common divisor of the support of
\boldsymbol{k}
and let
{\boldsymbol{m}}\,\sim\,{\rm Po}(dn/k)
.
Further, let
(\boldsymbol{d}_{i},\boldsymbol{k}_{i},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{i,j})_{i,j\geq 1}
be copies of
\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{k}
,
{\boldsymbol{\chi}}
, respectively, mutually independent and independent of
{\boldsymbol{m}}
.
Given

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\boldsymbol{k}_{i},

(1.1)

draw a simple bipartite graph
\boldsymbol{G}
comprising a set
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\}
of
check nodes
and a set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
of

variable nodes
such that the degree of
a_{i}
equals
\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
and the degree of
x_{j}
equals
\boldsymbol{d}_{j}
for all
i,j
uniformly at random.
Then let
\boldsymbol{A}
be the
{\boldsymbol{m}}\times n
-matrix with entries

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{1}\{a_{i}x_{j}\in E(\boldsymbol{G})\}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{i,j}.

Thus, the
i
’th row of
\boldsymbol{A}
contains precisely
\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
non-zero entries and the
j
’th column contains precisely
\boldsymbol{d}_{j}
non-zero entries.
Standard arguments show that
\boldsymbol{A}
is well-defined for large enough
n
, i.e., (
1.1
) is satisfied and there exists a simple
\boldsymbol{G}
with the desired degrees with positive probability; see Proposition
1.9
.
We call
\boldsymbol{G}
the
Tanner graph
of
\boldsymbol{A}
.

Since
\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{k}
have finite means the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
is sparse, i.e., the expected number of non-zero entries is
O(n)
.
Yet because the degree distributions are subject only to the modest condition
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{d}^{r}]+\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{k}^{r}]<\infty
, the typical maximum number of non-zero entries per row or column may be as large as
n^{1/2-\Omega(1)}
.
Natural choices of
\boldsymbol{d}
and
\boldsymbol{k}
include one-point distributions, truncated Poisson distributions as well as power laws.
Additionally, clever choices of
\boldsymbol{d}
and
\boldsymbol{k}
that facilitate the construction of error-correcting codes have been proposed
[
61
]
.

The following theorem, the main result of the paper, provides a formula for the asymptotic rank of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
Let
D(x)
and
K(x)
denote the probability generating functions of
\boldsymbol{d}
and
\boldsymbol{k}
, respectively.
Since
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{d}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{k}^{2}]<\infty
, the functions
D(x),K(x)
are continuously differentiable on
[0,1]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\Phi(\alpha)

\displaystyle=D\left(1-K^{\prime}(\alpha)/k\right)+\frac{d}{k}\left({K(\alpha)+(1-\alpha)K^{\prime}(\alpha)-1}\right).

Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\rank(\boldsymbol{A})}{n}=1-\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Phi(\alpha)\qquad\mbox{in probability.}

The upper bound
\rank(\boldsymbol{A})/n\leq 1-\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Phi(\alpha)+o(1)
w.h.p. was previously derived by Lelarge
[
48
]
from the Leibniz determinant formula and the formula for the matching number of a random bipartite graphs from
[
13
]
.
Thus, the lower bound on the rank constitutes the main contribution of this paper.
Nonetheless, we will also give an independent proof of the upper bound, which is more direct and
signifincantly shorter than
[
13
,
48
]
.
2
2

2

Lelarge deals with degrees s.t.
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{d}^{2}],\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{k}^{2}]<\infty
, whereas
we assume
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{d}^{r}],\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{k}^{r}]<\infty
for an
r
arbitrarily close to but greater than 2.

1.3.
The rate of ldpc codes

Theorem
1.1
implies a universal formula for the rate of ldpc codes.
To be precise, the standard construction of ldpc codes assumes that
\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{k}
are bounded random variables
[
61
]
.
Then it makes sense to prescribe the variable and check degrees exactly rather than just in distribution.
Indeed, assuming that

n
such that
n{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{d}=j}\right]
is an integer for every
j\in{\text{supp}\left(\boldsymbol{d}\right)}
and that

m=dn/k
is an integer such that
m{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{k}=j}\right]
is integral for each
j\in{\text{supp}\left(\boldsymbol{k}\right)}
,
let
\mathfrak{D}
be the event that
{\boldsymbol{m}}=m
and that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{d}_{i}=\ell\}

\displaystyle=n{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{d}=\ell}\right]\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{k}_{i}=\ell\}=m{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{k}=\ell}\right]\qquad\mbox{for all integers $\ell$}.

The
rate of the
(\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{k})
-ldpc code of block length
n
is defined as

\rate_{n}(\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{k})=n^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[{\nul(\boldsymbol{A})\mid\mathfrak{D}}\right].

Theorem 1.2
.

We have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\rate_{n}(\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{k})=\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Phi(\alpha).

1.4.
The 2-core

In several examples the solution to
\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Phi(\alpha)
has a natural combinatorial interpretation
in terms of the Tanner graph
\boldsymbol{G}
.
Indeed, define the
2-core
of
\boldsymbol{G}
as the subgraph
\boldsymbol{G}_{*}
obtained by repeating the following operation.

While there is a variable node
x_{i}
of degree one or less, remove that variable node along with the adjacent check node (if any).

Of course, the 2-core might be empty.
We will see momentarily how the 2-core is related to the rank.

Extending prior results that dealt with the case that the degrees of all check nodes coincide
[
20
]
,
we compute the likely number of variable and check nodes in the 2-core.
Since
\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{k}
have finite second moments,

\rho=\max\{x\in[0,1]:\Phi^{\prime}(x)=0\}

(1.2)

is well-defined.
So are
D^{\prime\prime}(x),K^{\prime\prime}(x)
for
x\in[0,1]
.
Let

\phi(\alpha)=1-\alpha-\frac{1}{d}D^{\prime}\left(1-K^{\prime}(\alpha)/{k}\right)

(1.3)

so that
\Phi^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{d}{k}K^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)\phi(\alpha)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{k}
are such that
\phi^{\prime}(\rho)<0
.
Let

{\boldsymbol{n}}^{*}
and
{\boldsymbol{m}}^{*}
be the number of variable and constraint nodes in the 2-core, respectively.
Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\boldsymbol{n}}^{*}}{n}

\displaystyle=1-D\left(1-\frac{K^{\prime}(\rho)}{k}\right)-\frac{K^{\prime}(\rho)}{k}D^{\prime}\left(1-\frac{K^{\prime}(\rho)}{k}\right),

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\boldsymbol{m}}^{*}}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{k}K(\rho)\quad\mbox{in probability.}

(1.4)

We observe that the expressions on the r.h.s. of (
1.4
) evaluate to zero if
\rho=0
.

Theorem
1.3
yields an upper bound on the rank of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
Indeed, upper bounding the rank is equivalent to lower bounding the nullity.
To this end, we count solutions to
\boldsymbol{A}x=0
where
x_{i}=0
for all variables in the 2-core.
Since the number of check nodes that have a neighbour outside the 2-core is
m-{\boldsymbol{m}}^{*}
and the number of variable nodes outside is
n-{\boldsymbol{n}}^{*}
, we obtain
\nul(\boldsymbol{A})\geq n-{\boldsymbol{n}}^{*}-({\boldsymbol{m}}-{\boldsymbol{m}}^{*})
.
Invoking Theorem
1.3
and using
\Phi^{\prime}(\rho)=0
, we find that

\rank(\boldsymbol{A})/n\leq 1-\Phi(\rho)+o(1)
w.h.p. Since the rank is also trivially upper-bounded by
{\boldsymbol{m}}
and
1-\Phi(0)=d/k\sim{\boldsymbol{m}}/n
w.h.p., these purely combinatorial deliberations show that w.h.p.

\displaystyle\rank(\boldsymbol{A})/n\leq 1-\max\{\Phi(0),\Phi(\rho)\}+o(1).

(1.5)

This graph-theoretic upper bound is tight if all vectors in
\ker(\boldsymbol{A})
are constant zero on the 2-core.
The following theorem shows that (
1.5
) is indeed tight in several interesting cases.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that

(i)

either
\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{d})=0
or
\boldsymbol{d}\,\sim\,{\rm Po}_{\geq\ell}(\lambda)
for an integer
\ell\geq 1
and
\lambda>0
, and

(ii)

either
\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{k})=0
or
\boldsymbol{k}\,\sim\,{\rm Po}_{\geq\ell^{\prime}}(\lambda^{\prime})
for an integer
\ell^{\prime}\geq 3
and
\lambda^{\prime}>0
.

Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\rank(\boldsymbol{A})/n

\displaystyle=1-\max\{\Phi(0),\Phi(\rho)\}

\displaystyle\mbox{in probability}.

Moreover, assuming (i) and (ii) we have
\phi^{\prime}(\rho)<0
unless
{\mathbb{P}}({\boldsymbol{d}}=1)=0
and
2(k-1){\mathbb{P}}({\boldsymbol{d}}=2)>d
.

Remark 1.5
.

If
{\mathbb{P}}({\boldsymbol{d}}=1)=0
and
2(k-1){\mathbb{P}}({\boldsymbol{d}}=2)>d
, then the 2-core comprises the entire graph
{\bf G}
.
But the 2-core may be instable, i.e., a subgraph obtained by deleting just a few nodes may have a much smaller or empty 2-core.

1.5.
Related work

The rank problem has been studied both via rigorous methods in the combinatorics and coding theory communities and via physics-inspired non-rigorous approaches.

1.5.1.
Rigorous work

Kovalenko
[
45
]
studied the rank of dense random matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, where each entry takes the value
0
or
1
with equal probability.
The result was subsequently extended to dense matrices over arbitrary finite fields
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with entries drawn from non-uniform distributions
[
46
]
.
The rank problem for sparser random matrices with an average number of
\Omega(\log n)
non-zero entries per row
was tackled by Balakin
[
5
,
6
]
, Blömer, Karp and Welzl
[
9
]
and Cooper
[
21
]
.
Random matrices with precisely two non-zero entries per row have also been studied extensively
[
42
,
43
]
.
In this very particular situation the value of one variable implies that of the other,
and thus the rank problem is intimately related to the component and cycle structure of the Tanner graph.
Kolchin’s monograph
[
41
]
presents much of this classical work.

More recently sparse matrices where the number of non-zero entries scales linearly with the number of columns have received a great deal of attention.
Miller and Cohen
[
55
]
investigated the biregular case, i.e.,
\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{d})=\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{k})=0
and
q=2
.
Furthermore, Méasson, Montanari and Urbanke derived a sufficient condition for
\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{k}
to yield a random matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
of full rank w.h.p.
[
52
]
.
In the case
q=2
and

this problem is equivalent to the
k
-XORSAT problem, a well-studied constraint satisfaction problem.
The most prominent case is that
\boldsymbol{d}\,\sim\,{\rm Po}(d)
.
In this case the precise threshold
d_{k}
for the mean
d
up to which the random matrix typically has full rank was determined by
Dubois and Mandler
[

]
for
q=2

For

The real rank of discrete random matrices has, of course, been studied as well.
For instance, Komlós
[
44
]
showed that a random

Moreover, Bordenave, Lelarge and Salez

A wealth of references on low-density parity check codes can be found in Richardson and Urbanke’s monograph
[

Chapter 3 contains a detailed discussion of the impact of the degree sequence.
The objective in coding theory is to recover the original codeword sent from the information received through a noisy channel.
This problem can be studied from both an algorithmic and an information-theoretic viewpoint.
Of course, the results depend on the channel model.
Ldpc codes are optimal in either respect on the binary erasure channel, where bits may be deleted but not altered

1.5.2.
The cavity method (and its caveats)

The cavity method, an analytic but non-rigorous technique inspired by the statistical mechanics of disordered systems, comes in two instalments, the simpler
replica symmetric ansatz
and the more elaborate
one-step replica symmetry breaking ansatz
(‘1RSB’).
The replica symmetric ansatz predicts that the rank of
\boldsymbol{A}
converges in probability to the solution of an optimisation problem on an infinite-dimensional space of probability measures.
To be precise, let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Identify this space with the standard simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
.
Further, let
\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be the (infinite-dimensional) space of probability measures on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
Given
\pi\in\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
let
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i,j})_{i,j\geq 1}
be a sequence of samples from
\pi
.
In addition, define
\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}
by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}=\ell}\right]

\displaystyle=\ell\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{k}=\ell}\right]/k

\displaystyle(\ell\geq 0),

(1.6)

and let
(\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be copies of
\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}
.
The random variables
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h,i})_{h,i\geq 1}
,
(\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are mutually independent
and independent of
\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{k}
and the
({\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{h,i})_{h,i\geq 1}
.
The
Bethe free entropy
is the functional
\mathcal{B}:\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[{\log_{q}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\prod_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}}\sum_{\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{i}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sum_{j=1}^{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{i}}\sigma_{j}{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{i,j}=0}\right\}\prod_{j=2}^{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i,j}(\sigma_{j})}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{k}\mathbb{E}\left[{(\boldsymbol{k}-1)\log_{q}\sum_{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{k}}\sigma_{i}{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{1,i}=0}\right\}\prod_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{k}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}(\sigma_{i})}\right].

The replica symmetric ansatz predicts that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\nul\boldsymbol{A}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})}\mathcal{B}(\pi)

in probability.

(1.7)

For a detailed (heuristic) derivation of the Bethe free entropy and the prediction (
1.7
) we refer to
[
53
, Chapter 14]
and
[
3
]
.
But let us briefly comment on the intended semantics of
\pi
.
Consider the Tanner graph
\boldsymbol{G}
representing the random matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
.
Suppose that variable node
x_{i}
and check node
a_{j}
are adjacent.
Then for
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
we define the
message

\mu_{\boldsymbol{A},x_{j}\to a_{i}}(\sigma)
from
x_{j}
to
a_{i}
as follows.
Obtain
\boldsymbol{A}_{x_{j}\to a_{i}}
from
\boldsymbol{A}
by changing the
ij
’th matrix entry to zero; this corresponds to deleting the
x_{j}
-
a_{i}
-edge from the Tanner graph.
Then
\mu_{\boldsymbol{A},x_{j}\to a_{i}}(\sigma)
is the probability that in a random vector
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\ker\boldsymbol{A}_{x_{j}\to a_{i}}
we have
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}=\sigma
.
In other words,
\mu_{\boldsymbol{A},x_{j}\to a_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is the marginal distribution of the value assigned to
x_{j}
in a random vector from the kernel of
\boldsymbol{A}_{x_{j}\to a_{i}}
.
Further, define
\pi_{\boldsymbol{A}}
as the empirical distribution of the messages
\mu_{\boldsymbol{A},x_{j}\to a_{i}}
over the edges of the Tanner graph;
in symbols,

\displaystyle\pi_{\boldsymbol{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}\neq 0\}\delta_{\mu_{\boldsymbol{A},x_{j}\to a_{i}}}\in\mathcal{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

A bit of linear algebra (that seems to have gone unnoticed in the physics literature) reveals that the messages actually have a very special form.
Namely, for any adjacent

on

In effect, the rank should come out as

\displaystyle=\alpha\delta_{\delta_{0}}+(1-\alpha)\delta_{q^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}}

In fact, a simple calculation yields

Theorem

But a question that remains open is whether the Bethe free entropy admits other ‘spurious’ maximisers

3

Proof strategy

In this section we survey the techniques upon which the proofs of the theorems stated in Section

We will also compare these techniques with those employed in prior work.
Indeed, in contrast to much of the previous work on the rank of random matrices, based on combinatorial and graph-theoretic considerations

The protagonist of this approach is the Boltzmann distribution.

2.1.
The Boltzmann distribution

Suppose that
A
is an
m\times n
-matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Borrowing a term from statistical physics, we refer to the probability distribution
\mu_{A}
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

\displaystyle\mu_{A}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{1}\{\sigma\in\ker(A)\}q^{-\nul(A)}

Thus,

induces the uniform distribution on the kernel of

.
We denote a random vector drawn from
\mu_{A}
by
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{A}=(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{A,1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{A,n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n})
where

is apparent.

The Boltzmann distribution is important to us because the proofs of the main results hinge on coupling arguments.
Roughly speaking, we will be dealing with matrices obtained from
\boldsymbol{A}
by adding a few rows and columns.
We will need to study the ensuing change in nullity.
Crucially, the total number of new rows and columns will typically be bounded (i.e., independent of
n
), and each will merely contain a bounded number of non-zero entries.
Let us investigate how the Boltzmann distribution can then be harnessed to trace the nullity.

Suppose that the
m^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}
-matrix

m\times n
-matrix

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\

Fact 2.1

Let

\displaystyle\nul(A^{\prime})-\nul(A)

\displaystyle=\log_{q}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{J},\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{I}}\mu_{A,I}\left({\tau}\right)\prod_{h=1}^{m^{\prime}-m}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sum_{i\in I}A^{\prime\prime}_{hi}\tau_{j}+\sum_{j\in J}A^{\prime\prime\prime}_{h,j-n}\sigma_{j}=0}\right\}.

Proof.

Inside the logarithm we sum for every

divided by

.
∎

To calculate the r.h.s. of (

The following lemma, whose proof consists of a few lines of linear algebra, marks a first step.

Lemma 2.2

For any matrix

2.2.
The Aizenman–Sims–Starr scheme

We are going to tackle the rank problem by way of calculating the nullity of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
We will prove matching upper and lower bounds on the nullity via two separate arguments.
The proof of the upper bound on the nullity (which yields the lower bound on the rank) is based on a type of coupling argument that is colloquially referred to as the ‘Aizenman–Sims–Starr scheme’.
Originally developed to cope with models of a rather different look in mathematical physics, applied to the nullity the argument rests upon the observation that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A})]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1})]-\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{n})].

(The inequality is easily verified by writing a telescoping sum.)
In order to estimate the right hand side, it seems natural to couple
\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{n}
so as to write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1})]-\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{n})]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1})-\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{n})}\right].

(2.3)

Indeed, if we were to find a coupling under which
\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1}
results from
\boldsymbol{A}_{n}
by (randomly) adding, say, one column along with a few rows, then we could bring the machinery from Section
2.1
to bear.

The immediate issue is that the random matrices
\boldsymbol{A}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1}
do not lend themselves to an easy coupling.
In fact,
\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1}
may not even be defined (due to divisibility issues).
But even if it is, the structure of
\boldsymbol{A}_{n}
appears too rigid to allow for
\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1}
to be easily described as ‘
\boldsymbol{A}_{n}
plus one column and a few rows’.
Hence, to facilitate couplings we introduce an auxiliary model that resembles the configuration model from the theory of random graphs and that allows for a bit of wiggling room.

Specifically, we fix a parameter
\varepsilon>0
along with a large enough
\Theta=\Theta(\varepsilon)>0
dependent on
\varepsilon

\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n}
constructed as follows.

{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}\,\sim\,{\rm Po}((1-\varepsilon)dn/k)

\boldsymbol{\theta}\in[\Theta]
uniformly at random.
Moreover, let

be copies of
\boldsymbol{d}

{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Further, let

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}}\left\{{a_{i}}\right\}\times[\boldsymbol{k}_{i}],\quad\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\left\{{x_{j}}\right\}\times[\boldsymbol{d}_{j}].

Proposition 2.4

For any function
\Theta=\Theta(\varepsilon)\geq 0

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}n^{-1}\left|{\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon})-\nul(\boldsymbol{A})}\right|=0

likely presence of a linear number of cavities.
Roughly speaking, under the coupling
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n+1}
is obtained from
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n}
by adding one column and a (typically bounded) random number of rows.
The check nodes corresponding to the new rows will have non-zero entries at random cavities of
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n}
.
We will estimate the difference of the nullities via Fact
2.1
.
Indeed, the purpose of the check nodes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is to ensure that the Boltzmann distribution

\mu_{\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n}}
is sufficiently symmetric w.h.p. More specifically, while Lemma
2.3
requires that a
random
set of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
variables be frozen, the checks
p_{1},\ldots,p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
just freeze the first
\boldsymbol{\theta}
variables.
But since the distribution of
\boldsymbol{G}_{\varepsilon}-\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}
is invariant under permutations of the variable nodes,
both constructions are equivalent.
Therefore, we will be able to combine Fact
2.1
, Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
to prove
Proposition
2.5
.

As an immediate consequence of Proposition
2.5
we obtain the desired upper bound on the nullity.

Corollary 2.6
.

We have

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon})]\leq\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Phi(\alpha).

Proof.

Proposition
2.5
yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left[{\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,1})]+\sum_{N=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,N+1})]-\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,N})]}\right]\leq\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Phi(\alpha)+o_{\varepsilon}(1).

Taking the double limit
n\to\infty
followed by
\varepsilon\to 0
yields the assertion.
∎

2.3.
The interpolation method

A lower bound on the nullity of
\boldsymbol{A}
that matches the upper bound from Proposition
2.4
and Corollary
2.6
was deduced in
[
48
]
from the formula for the matching number of random bipartite graphs from
[
13
]
.
But the proof of that formula, reliant on the contraction method in combination with local weak convergence, is far from elementary.
Here we present a new direct proof of the lower bound.
We adapt another technique from mathematical physics, the interpolation method, to the rank problem.
The basic idea is to construct a family of random matrices
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}(t)
parametrised by
t\in[0,1]
.
At
t=1
we obtain precisely the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}
.
At the other extreme,
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}(0)
is a block diagonal matrix whose nullity can be read off easily.
To establish the lower bound we will control the derivative of the nullity with respect to
t
.
By comparison to applications of the interpolation method to other problems, the construction here is relatively elegant.
In particular, throughout the interpolation we will be dealing with an actual random matrix, rather than some other, more contrived object.

Figure 2.
Left: sketch of the component of
x_{i}
at
t=0
; the check
p_{i}
is present iff
i\leq\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Right: sketch of the factor graph
\boldsymbol{G}_{\varepsilon}(t)
for
0<t<1
, with the
a_{i,j}
coloured black and the other colours as in the left figure.

Let us inspect the construction in detail.
Apart from
t
and
\varepsilon
we need two further parameters: an integer
\Theta=\Theta(\varepsilon)\geq 0
and a real
\beta\in[0,1]
, chosen such that

\Phi(\beta)=\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Phi(\alpha).

(2.4)

Further, let

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle\,\sim\,{\rm Po}((1-\varepsilon)tdn/k),

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\,\sim\,{\rm Po}((1-\varepsilon)(1-t)dn/k)

(2.5)

be independent Poisson variables.
Also let
(\boldsymbol{k}_{i},\boldsymbol{k}_{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}_{i}^{\prime\prime})_{i\geq 1}
and
(\boldsymbol{d}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be copies of
\boldsymbol{k}
and
\boldsymbol{d}
, respectively, mutually independent and independent of
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}(t)
,
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)
.
Additionally, choose
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in[\Theta]
uniformly and independently of everything else.

The Tanner graph
\boldsymbol{G}_{\varepsilon}(t)
has variable nodes

\displaystyle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}

and

\displaystyle(x_{i,j,h})_{i\in[{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)],\,j\in[\boldsymbol{k}_{i}],\,h\in[\boldsymbol{k}_{i}^{\prime}-1]}.

Moreover, let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be a random set that contains each of the variable nodes
x_{i,j,h}
with probability
\beta
independently.
Then the check nodes are

\displaystyle a_{1},\ldots,a_{{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}(t)},

\displaystyle(b_{i,j})_{i\in[{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}],\,j\in[\boldsymbol{k}_{i}^{\prime}]},

\displaystyle p_{1},\ldots,p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

\displaystyle f_{i,j,h}\quad\mbox{ for each }x_{i,j,h}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}.

To define the edges of the Tanner graph let
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t)
be a random maximal matching of the complete bipartite graph with vertex sets

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\left\{{x_{i}}\right\}\times[\boldsymbol{d}_{i}],\qquad\left({\bigcup_{i=1}^{{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}(t)}\left\{{a_{i}}\right\}\times[\boldsymbol{k}_{i}]}\right)\cup\left\{{b_{i,j}^{\prime}:i\in[{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)],\ j\in[\boldsymbol{k}_{i}^{\prime}]}\right\}.

For each matching edge
(x_{i},s,a_{j},t)\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t)
insert an edge between
x_{i}
and
a_{j}
into the Tanner graph and
for each
(x_{i},s,b_{j,h})\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t)
insert an edge between
x_{i}
and
b_{j,h}
.
Thus,
\boldsymbol{G}_{\varepsilon}(t)
may contain multi-edges.
Further, add an edge between
x_{i}
and
p_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
add an edge between
x_{i,j,h}
and
a_{i,j}
as well as an edge between
x_{i,j,h}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
and
f_{i,j,h}
.
Finally, let
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}(t)
be the random matrix induced by
\boldsymbol{G}_{\varepsilon}(t)
.

The semantics is as follows.
The checks
a_{i}
will play exactly the same role as before, i.e., each is adjacent to
\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
of the variable nodes
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
w.h.p. By contrast, each
b_{i,j}
is adjacent to precisely one of the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
.
In addition,
b_{i,j}
is adjacent to the
\boldsymbol{k}_{i}^{\prime}-1
variable nodes
x_{i,j,h}
,
h\in[\boldsymbol{k}_{i}^{\prime}-1]
.
These variable nodes, in turn, are adjacent only to
b_{i,j}
and to
f_{i,j,h}
if
x_{i,j,h}\in\mathcal{F}
.
The checks
f_{i,j,h}
are unary, i.e.,
f_{i,j,h}
simply forces
x_{i,j,h}
to take the value zero.
Finally, each of the checks
p_{i}
is adjacent to
x_{i}
only, i.e.,
p_{1},\ldots,p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
just freeze
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

For
t=1
the Tanner graph contains
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}(1)\,\sim\,{\rm Po}((1-\varepsilon)dn/k)
‘real’ checks
a_{i}
and none of the checks
b_{i,j}
or
f_{i,j,h}
.
In effect,
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}(1)
is distributed precisely as
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}
from Section
2.2
.
By contrast, at
t=0
we have
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}(0)=0
, i.e., there are no checks
a_{i}
involving several of the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
.
As a consequence, the Tanner graph decomposes into
n
connected components, one for each of the
x_{i}
.
In fact, each component is a tree comprising
x_{i}
, some of the checks
b_{j,h}
and their proprietary variables
x_{j,h,s}
along with possibly a check
f_{j,h,s}
that freezes
x_{j,h,s}
to zero.
For
i\in[\boldsymbol{\theta}]
there is a check
p_{i}
freezing
x_{i}
to zero as well.
Thus,
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}(0)
is a block diagonal matrix consisting of
n
blocks, one for each component.
In effect, the rank of
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}(0)
will be easy to compute.
Finally, for
0<t<1
we have a blend of the two extremal cases.
There will be some checks
a_{i}
and some
b_{i,j}
with their retainer variables and checks; see Figure
2
.

We are going to study the nullity of
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}(t)
for
t\in[0,1]
.
But since the newly introduced variables
x_{i,j,h}
inflate the nullity, we subtract a correction term to retain the same scale throughout the process.
In addition, we need a correction term to make up for the greater total number of check nodes in
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}(0)
by comparison to
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}(1)
.
Thus, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{t}

\displaystyle=\nul\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}(t)+|\mathcal{F}_{t}|-\sum_{i=1}^{{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k}_{i}^{\prime}-1),

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}}(\boldsymbol{k}_{i}-1)(\beta^{\boldsymbol{k}_{i}}-1).

The following two statements summarise the interpolation argument.
First, we compute
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{0}]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

For any fixed
\theta\geq 0
we have

n^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{0}]=D(1-K^{\prime}(\beta)/k)+dK^{\prime}(\beta)/k-d+o_{\varepsilon}(1).

The next proposition provides monotonicity.

Proposition 2.8
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\Theta=\Theta(\varepsilon)>0
such that

\frac{1}{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{t}+\mathcal{Y}_{t}]\geq o_{\varepsilon}(1)

uniformly
t\in(0,1)
.

As an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.7
and Proposition
2.8
we obtain a lower bound on the nullity that matches the upper bound from Proposition
2.6
.

Corollary 2.9
.

We have

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon})]\geq\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Phi(\alpha).

Proof.

Lemma
2.8
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nul\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\nul\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}(1)]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{1}+\mathcal{Y}_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Y}_{1}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{0}+\mathcal{Y}_{0}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Y}_{1}]-o_{\varepsilon}(n)=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{0}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Y}_{1}]-o_{\varepsilon}(n).

(2.6)

Further,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Y}_{1}]

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{k}\left({\beta K^{\prime}(\beta)-k+1-K(\beta)}\right)+o_{\varepsilon}(1),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{0}]

\displaystyle=-d+dK^{\prime}(\beta)/k+D(1-K^{\prime}(\beta)/k)+o_{\varepsilon}(1)

\displaystyle\mbox{[by Lemma~\ref{Lemma_interpol2}]}.

Hence,

n^{-1}(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{0}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Y}_{1}])=\Phi(\beta)+o_{\varepsilon}(1)=\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Phi(\alpha)+o_{\varepsilon}(1)

by (
2.4
).
Thus, the assertion follows from (
2.6
).
∎

Combining Proposition
2.4
, Proposition
2.6
and Corollary
2.9
, and a standard concentration argument for
\nul{\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}}
(see Lemma
5.7
), we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2.4.
Discussion

Much of the prior work on the rank of random matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
relies on relatively elementary techniques such as the second moment method
[
24
,
25
,
34
]
, or the idea of bounding the number of linearly dependent row sets via the first moment method
[
41
,
60
]
.
Other proof strategies depend on graph-theoretic arguments such as close control of the 2-core and the ‘mantle’
[
22
]
.
By contrast, the present paper harnesses two ideas from mathematical physics, the Aizenman-Sims-Starr scheme and the interpolation method.
Both were originally invented to investigate the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick spin glass model
[
2
,
35
]
.
Yet over the recent years these techniques have found several uses in ‘diluted’ models defined on sparse random structures, e.g.,
[
8
,
30
,
49
,
57
]
.
In some of these papers the idea of carving out ‘cavities’ to facilitate coupling arguments is used as well.
Also the combintion of the Aizenman-Sims-Starr scheme and the interpolation method to prove matching upper and lower bounds has been applied successfully to other problems such as the Viana–Bray spin glass model or the stochastic block model (e.g.,
[
18
,
19
,
58
]
).

Yet the only prior application of the Aizenman-Sims-Starr scheme to the rank problem that we are aware of
is our previous paper on random matrices with
\boldsymbol{k}=k
constant and
\boldsymbol{d}\,\sim\,{\rm Po}(d)

[
4
]
.
The Aizenman-Sims-Starr scheme was used there to derive an upper bound on the nullity, like in the present paper.
But in the present setting matters are complicated very significantly by the fact that we work with general degree sequences.
Indeed, while the Poisson distribution lends itself easily to coupling arguments due to its memorylessness, in the present paper the couplings require delicate manoeuvres, as we will see in Section
3
.
The possible presence of nodes of very high degrees adds to the intricacy.
While coupling arguments have previously been developed for graphs with given degrees (e.g.,
[
23
,
49
,
57
]
),
a new subtle construction is needed to carry out the very accurate calculations required for the Aizenman-Sims-Starr scheme.

The use of the interpolation method to lower bound the nullity is a further technical novelty
as, to our knowledge, this technique has not been applied to the rank problem previously.
Indeed, in most prior work there was no need for a sophisticated lower bound argument
because the graph-theoretic bound (
1.5
) was tight
[
4
,
22
]
.
The interpolation scheme for the rank problem is conceptually more elegant than prior applications of the method to other problems.
The reason is that normally the interpolation is set up in terms of the Bethe free energy functional that ‘lives’ on an infinite dimensional space of probability measures; cf. Section
1.5.2
.
Then the construction of the interpolation scheme has to incorporate a distributional parameter
\pi
.
In effect, the combinatorial interpretation of the structures at ‘times’
t\in(0,1)
is not exactly straightforward.
By contrast, because in the rank problem the infinite dimensional variational problem collapses to a one-dimensional optimisation, the interpolation argument merely requires a real parameter
\beta\in[0,1]
and the intermediate structures
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon}(t)
are actual matrices.

Finally, it would be interesting to see if the present methods can be extended to other problems of an algebraic flavour.
Natural candidates would be systems of linear equations over rings rather than fields or systems of equations over non-Abelian groups.

The Aizenman-Sims-Starr scheme

In this section we prove Proposition

.
As set out in Section

, we are going to bound the difference of the nullities of

and
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n}

.
We begin by coupling the random variables
\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n+1})
and
\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n})

3.1.
The coupling

Let
\boldsymbol{M}=(\boldsymbol{M}_{j})_{j\geq 3}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=({\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{j})_{j\geq 3}
be sequences of Poisson variables with means

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{M}_{j}]

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon){\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{k}=j}\right]dn/k,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{j}]

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon){\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{k}=j}\right]d/k.

(3.1)

\boldsymbol{\theta}
and the
(\boldsymbol{d}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
.
Further, let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{j}^{+}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{M}_{j}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{j},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 3}\boldsymbol{M}_{j},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}^{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 3}\boldsymbol{M}_{j}^{+}.

(3.2)

Since
\sum_{j\geq 3}\boldsymbol{M}_{j}\,\sim\,{\rm Po}((1-\varepsilon)dn/k)

) is consistent with the earlier convention that
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}\,\sim\,{\rm Po}((1-\varepsilon)dn/k)
.

The random vectors
\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{M}
naturally define a random Tanner (multi-)graph
\boldsymbol{G}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}}
with variable nodes
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}

and check nodes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
a_{i,j}
,
i\geq 3
,
j\in[\boldsymbol{M}_{i}]
.
Its edges are induced by a random maximal matching
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}}
of the complete bipartite graph with vertex classes

\displaystyle\bigcup_{h=1}^{n}\{x_{h}\}\times[\boldsymbol{d}_{h}]\quad\mbox{and}\quad\bigcup_{i\geq 3}\bigcup_{j=1}^{\boldsymbol{M}_{i}}\{a_{i,j}\}\times[i].

(x_{h},s,a_{i,j},t)\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}}

x_{h}
and
a_{i,j}
in the Tanner graph.
In addition, there is an edge between
p_{i}
and
x_{i}
for every

.
Let
\boldsymbol{A}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}}=\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{G}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}})
be the corresponding random matrix.
The random matrix
\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1,\boldsymbol{M}^{+}}
and its associated Tanner graph
\boldsymbol{G}_{n+1,\boldsymbol{M}^{+}}
are defined analogously.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
\theta>0
we have

\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n})]=\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}})]
,

\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n+1})]=\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1,\boldsymbol{M}^{+}})]
.

Proof.

We defined
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n}
as the
n\times{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}
-matrix with target column and row degrees drawn from
\boldsymbol{d}
and
\boldsymbol{k}
independently with a
\boldsymbol{\theta}\times\boldsymbol{\theta}
identity matrix attached at the bottom.
In effect, because
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\varepsilon}
is a Poisson variable, the number of rows of with target degree
j
is distributed as
\boldsymbol{M}_{j}
, and these numbers are mutually independent.
Hence,
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}}
are identically distributed, and so are their nullities.
The same argument applies to
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n+1}
.
∎

Up to this point we merely introduced a new description of
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{\varepsilon,n+1}
.
To actually construct a coupling we introduce a third random matrix whose nullity we can easily compare to

\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}})
and
\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1,\boldsymbol{M}^{+}})
.
Specifically, let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i}\geq 0
be the number of checks
a_{i,j}
,
j\in[\boldsymbol{M}_{i}^{+}]
, adjacent to the last variable node
x_{n+1}
in
\boldsymbol{G}_{n+1,\boldsymbol{M}^{+}}
.
Also let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i})_{i\geq 3}
and set

\boldsymbol{M}_{i}^{-}=(\boldsymbol{M}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i})\vee 0.

(3.3)

Consider the random Tanner graph
\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{G}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}^{-}}
and the corresponding random matrix
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{A}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}^{-}}
induced by a random matching
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}^{-}}
as above.
For each variable
x_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, let
{\mathcal{C}}
be the set of clones from
\bigcup_{i\in[n]}\{x_{i}\}\times[\boldsymbol{d}_{i}]
that
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}^{-}}
leaves unmatched.
We call the elements of
{\mathcal{C}}

cavities
.

Now, obtain the Tanner graph
\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}
from
\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}
by adding
new check nodes
a^{\prime\prime}_{i,j}
with target degree
i
for each
i\geq 3
,
j\in[\boldsymbol{M}_{i}-\boldsymbol{M}_{i}^{-}]
.
The new checks are joined by a random maximal matching of the complete bipartite graph with vertex classes
{\mathcal{C}}
and

\bigcup_{i\geq 3}\bigcup_{j\in[\boldsymbol{M}_{i}-\boldsymbol{M}_{i}^{-}]}\{a_{i,j}^{\prime\prime}\}\times[i],

i.e., for each matching edge we insert a corresponding variable-check edge.
Then
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime\prime}
is obtained from
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
by adding rows corresponding to the new checks and representing each new edge of
\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}
by a matrix entry chosen independently according to
{\boldsymbol{\chi}}
.
Thus, the matrix entries of
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
,
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime\prime}
corresponding to the edges of
\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}
coincide.

Analogously, obtain
\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime\prime}
by adding one variable node
x_{n+1}
as well as check nodes
a_{i,j}^{\prime\prime\prime}
,
i\geq 3
,
j\in[{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i}]

and
b_{i,j}^{\prime\prime\prime}
,
i\geq 3
,
j\in[\boldsymbol{M}_{i}^{+}-\boldsymbol{M}_{i}^{-}-{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i}]
to
\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}
.
The new checks are connected to
\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}
via a random maximal matching of the complete bipartite graph with vertex classes
{\mathcal{C}}
and

\bigcup_{i\geq 3}\left({\bigcup_{j\in[\boldsymbol{M}_{i}-\boldsymbol{M}_{i}^{-}]}\{a_{i,j}^{\prime\prime\prime}\}\times[i-1]\cup\bigcup_{j\in[\boldsymbol{M}_{i}^{+}-\boldsymbol{M}_{i}^{-}-{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i}]}\{b_{i,j}^{\prime\prime\prime}\}\times[i]}\right).

For each matching edge we insert the corresponding variable-check edge and
in addition each of the check nodes
a_{i,j}^{\prime\prime\prime}
gets connected to
x_{n+1}
by exactly one edge.
Finally,
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime\prime\prime}
is obtained by adding one row for each of the new checks as well as one column representing
x_{n+1}
.
As always, the entries representing the new edges of the Tanner graph are drawn independently according to
{\boldsymbol{\chi}}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have
\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime\prime})]=\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{n,\boldsymbol{M}})]+o(1)
and
\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime\prime\prime})]=\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1,\boldsymbol{M}^{+}})]+o(1).

The proof of Lemma
3.2
is tedious but straightforward.
We defer it to Section
3.5
.

As a next step we are going to calculate the differences
\nul(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime\prime\prime})-\nul(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime})
and
\nul(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime\prime})-\nul(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime})
.
We obtain expressions of one parameter of
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
, namely the fraction of cavities ‘frozen’ to zero.
Naturally, each vector
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}}
lifts to
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\mathcal{C}}}
via
(x_{i},h)\in{\mathcal{C}}\mapsto\sigma(x_{i})
; in words, the value of the clone
(x_{i},h)
is nothing but the value of the underlying variable
x_{i}
.
Accordingly, the Boltzmann distribution
\mu_{\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}}
induces a probability distribution
\mu_{\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime},{\mathcal{C}}}
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\mathcal{C}}}
:

\displaystyle\mu_{\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime},{\mathcal{C}}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=q^{-\nul(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime})}\left|{\left\{{\sigma\in\ker(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}):\forall(x_{i},h)\in{\mathcal{C}}:\sigma_{i}=\tau_{x_{i},h}}\right\}}\right|

\displaystyle(\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\mathcal{C}}}).

Further, Lemma
2.2
shows that for each cavity
(x_{i},h)
there are two possibilities: either the Boltzmann marginal
\mu_{\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime},x_{i}}
is the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, or

frozen

In Sections

Lemma 3.3
.

We have

Lemma 3.4
.

We have
\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime\prime})-\nul(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime})]=d\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\alpha}K^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]/k-d+o_{\varepsilon}(1).

Proposition

is an immediate consequence of Lemmas

–

While proving Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
in full detail requires a fair bit of work because we are dealing with very general degree distributions

, it is not at all difficult to fathom where the right hand side expressions in Lemmas

–
3.4
come from.
Regarding Lemma

, we notice that with probability

the degree of

in

is
\boldsymbol{d}_{n+1}
.
Moreover, the degrees of the neighbours of

are distributed approximately as the size-biased version
\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}
of
\boldsymbol{k}
because the probability that a given clone of

is matched to a clone of a specific check node is proportional to the degree of that check node.
Hence, the degrees of the new checks added to

should approximately be distributed as
\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\boldsymbol{d}}
.
These new checks are attached to random cavities of
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.
Hence, for each

the probability of picking

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{i}}

\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
.
By contrast, if at least one adjacent cavity is unfrozen, and if we assume as per Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
that the values that a random

assigns to the cavities are uniform and independent, then the probability that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
satisfies the new check equals

.
Thus, by Fact
2.1
the nullity drops by one for each such check.
In summary, this heuristic calculation leads us to expect that
\mathbb{E}[\nul(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime\prime})-\nul(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime})]=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{i}}-1)]+o_{\varepsilon}(1)

Rewriting this expression in terms of the generating function

Similar reasoning explains the expression in Lemma

Indeed, recalling (

will change the nullity by

\displaystyle d\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\boldsymbol{k}}-1]/k+o_{\varepsilon}(1).

(3.4)

Moreover, concerning the addition of

and its adjacent checks

First, that all the cavities adjacent to some

\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
.

In effect,

Then any other check

Otherwise, if

has at least one unfrozen neighbour, and if we assume as per Lemmas

and
2.3

\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\ker(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime})
satisfies

equals

.
Thus, Fact
2.1
shows that the nullity drops by one for each such check.
Hence, because as in the previous paragraph the degrees of the checks adjacent to

\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\boldsymbol{d}}

frozen’ scenario comes to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{d}\prod_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}}(1-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{i}-1})+\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{i}-1}-1)}\right]+o_{\varepsilon}(1).

(3.5)

Second, there is the scenario that

Then each of the adjacent checks contains at least one unfrozen variable among

Assuming that the values that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\ker(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime})
assigns to these variables are uniform and independent

2.3

we deduce from Fact

Hence, the unfrozen case contributes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{(1-\boldsymbol{d})\prod_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}}(1-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{i}-1})}\right]+o_{\varepsilon}(1).

Summing (

5.1.
The local limit theorem

We recall the local limit theorem for sums of independent random variables.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
26
, p. 130]
)
.

Suppose that
(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is a sequence of i.i.d. variables that take values in
\mathbb{Z}
such that the greatest common divisor of the support of
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
is one.
Also assume that
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{2}]=\sigma^{2}\in(0,\infty)
.
Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|{\sqrt{n}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}=z}\right]-\frac{\exp(-(z-n\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{X}_{1}])^{2}/(2n\sigma^{2}))}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}}\right|

\displaystyle=0.

As an application of Theorem
5.1
we obtain the following estimate.
Because
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{d}^{r}]+\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{k}^{r}]<\infty
for an
r>2
, the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\left\{{\max_{i\in[n]}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}+\max_{i\in[{\boldsymbol{m}}]}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}\leq\sqrt{n}/\log^{9}n}\right\}\qquad\mbox{satisfies}\qquad{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{M}}\right]=1-o(1).

(5.1)

Corollary 5.2
.

If
\gcd(\boldsymbol{k})
divides
n
, then
{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{D}}\right]=\Theta(n^{-1/2})
and
{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{M}}\right]=\Theta(n^{-1/2})
.

Proof.

For
{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{M}}\right]

there are several cases to consider.
First, that
\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{d})=\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{k})=0
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{k}
are both atoms.
Since
{\boldsymbol{m}}
is a Poisson variable with mean
dn/k
we find
{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{M}}\right]={\mathbb{P}}\left[{{\boldsymbol{m}}=dn/k}\right]=\Theta(n^{-1/2})
.

Second, suppose that
\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{d})>0
but
\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{k})=0
.
Then Theorem
5.1

and (
5.1
) show that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\left|{dn-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}}\right|\leq\sqrt{n}\wedge k\mbox{ divides }\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}\mid\mathcal{M}}\right]

\displaystyle=\Omega(1).

(5.2)

Further, given
\left|{dn-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}}\right|\leq\sqrt{n}
and
k|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}
, the event
k{\boldsymbol{m}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}

has probability
\Theta(n^{-1/2})
by the local limit theorem for the Poisson distribution.

The case that
\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{d})=0
but
\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{k})>0
can be dealt with similarly.
Indeed, pick a large enough number
L>0
and let
I=\left\{{i\in[{\boldsymbol{m}}]:\boldsymbol{k}_{i}>L}\right\}
,
{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\prime}=|I|
,
{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\prime\prime}={\boldsymbol{m}}-|I|
,
S^{\prime}=\sum_{i\in I}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}

and
S^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{i\in[{\boldsymbol{m}}]\setminus I}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
.
Then
{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\prime\prime}
are stochastically independent, and so are
S^{\prime},S^{\prime\prime}
.

\displaystyle=\Omega(1).

to

Lemma 5.5

Let

be the number of multi-edges of the Tanner graph and let

Proof.

To estimate the
\ell
th factorial moments of

for
\ell\geq 1
, we split the random variable into a sum of indicator variables.
Specifically, let

be the set of all families

with

edges between check
a_{i}
and variable
x_{i}
for each
i

Then

\displaystyle\prod_{h=1}^{\ell}Y-h+1

\displaystyle=\sum_{(a_{i},y_{i},w_{i})_{i\in[\ell]}\in U_{\ell}}Y((a_{i},y_{i},w_{i})_{i\in[\ell]}).

Moreover, letting

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y((a_{i},y_{i},w_{i})_{i\in[h]})\mid(\boldsymbol{d}_{i})_{i\in[n]},(\boldsymbol{k}_{i})_{i\in{\boldsymbol{m}}}]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{(dn)^{w}}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}{\binom{\boldsymbol{d}_{y_{i}}}{w_{i}}}{\binom{\boldsymbol{k}_{a_{i}}}{w_{i}}}w_{i}!\kern 5.0pt.

(5.9)

Now, for a sequence

\displaystyle=o(1).

(5.10)

Finally, invoking (

\displaystyle\sim\lambda^{\ell},\qquad\mbox{where}

(5.11)

Combining (

), (

) completes the proof.

Proof of Lemma
2.3

For

[n]

2.2
