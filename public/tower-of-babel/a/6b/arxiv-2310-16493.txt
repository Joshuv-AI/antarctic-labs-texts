Apartness and the elimination of strong forms of extensionality
Benno van den Berg 1

Abstract
. We introduce a new version of arithmetic in all finite types which extends the usual versions with primitive notions of extensionality and extensional equality. This new hybrid version allows us to formulate a strong form of extensionality, which we call converse extensionality. Inspired by Brouwer’s notion of apartness, we show that converse extensionality can be eliminated in a way which improves on results from our previous work. We also explain how standard proof-theoretic interpretations, like realizability and functional interpretations, can be extended to such hybrid systems, and how that might be relevant to proof-mining.



1.
Introduction

The standard approach towards the semantics of constructive logic is to explain the meaning of a mathematical statement in terms of what counts as evidence in its favour. This idea is the starting point for the Brouwer-Heyting-Kolmogorov interpretation, various realizability interpretations and type theory.

However, occasionally one also finds the idea of explaining the meaning of a proposition in terms of “counter-evidence”: a piece of data that serves as evidence
against
a proposition. Examples of this are Brouwer’s notion of apartness and Gödel’s Dialectica interpretation.

Brouwer came up with his notion of apartness in order to explain what it means that two real numbers
r
and
s
are equal. This is not so easy; however, saying what is needed to show that they are distinct (apart, in Brouwer’s terminology) is much easier. Indeed, this one does by providing two non-overlapping intervals with rational endpoints
q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}
such that
r\in[q_{0},q_{1}]
and
s\in[q_{2},q_{3}]
. Brouwer then defines equality of two reals as the impossibility of obtaining evidence for their apartness.

With respect to (extensional) equality of functions, we are in a similar situation. It is easy to see what should be a counterargument against their equality, and much less clear how one provides an argument in favour of their equality. Indeed, to give a counterargument against the equality of functions
f
and
g
means to point to an input on which they yield distinct outputs. Given this, equality of the functions
f
and
g
can then be defined as the impossibility of any counterargument against their equality.

If one takes the idea of counter-evidence seriously, it is natural to say that for an implication
\varphi\to\psi
to be valid, not only should evidence for
\varphi
give rise to evidence for
\psi
(as in the traditional account), but counter-evidence for
\psi
should also give rise to counter-evidence for
\varphi
. And this is what happens in Gödel’s Dialectica interpretation. One can see this clearly when one looks at the Dialectica interpretation of the following extensionality principle:

(1)

\displaystyle\forall f:(\sigma\to 0)\to(\tau\to 0).\forall x,y:\sigma\to 0.

\displaystyle\big(\,(\forall u:\sigma.xu=_{0}yu)\to(\forall v:\tau.fxv=_{0}fyv)\,\big).

(Here 0 stands for the type of natural numbers.) This says that the functional
f
preserves extensional equality: if you give it functions
x
and
y
as input and these are extensionally equal, then the outputs should also be extensionally equal. Note that counter-evidence against the equality of
x
and
y
is a
u:\sigma
such that
xu\not=_{0}yu
and counter-evidence against the equality of
fx
and
fy
is a
v:\tau
such that
fxv\not=_{0}fyv
. So if counter-evidence flows backwards along an implication, it should be possible to find such a
u
from such a
v
. That is, there should be a functional
Z
such that

(2)

\displaystyle\forall f:(\sigma\to 0)\to(\tau\to 0).\forall x,y:\sigma\to 0.\forall v:\tau.

\displaystyle fxv\not=_{0}fyv\to x(Zfxyv)\not=_{0}y(Zfxyv);

this is essentially the Dialectica interpretation of the original statement. (Indeed, it is equivalent to it, since
=_{0}
is decidable.) In earlier work we referred to (
2
) as a
converse extensionality principle
. It is a strong form of extensionality, which implies, but is not implied by, the extensionality principle (
1
). This means that if we wish to Dialectica interpret extensionality, we are asked to witness converse extensionality, which is not always possible. Indeed, one can find some striking limitative results in the appendix of
[
5
]
, written by W.A. Howard.

What is not excluded by these results, however, is that converse extensionality can be eliminated in a manner similar to the well-known elimination of extensionality due to Gandy and Luckhardt (see
[
4
, Section 10.4]
); by this we mean that we can interpret systems with such extensonality principles in systems which do not possess them. Indeed, the main contribution of this paper is to show that that is indeed possible for converse extensionality using precisely Brouwer’s notion of apartness: this elimination procedure will be called the
\alpha
-translation
(
\alpha
for apartness).

This is not the first time that the we have looked at apartness and converse extensionality. Indeed, the contents of this paper are similar to that of
[
2
]
, written together with Robert Passmann. In the conclusion to that paper we suggested that it might be possible to obtain stronger results if one could have non-extensional witnesses for principles like converse extensionality. In this paper we show that that is indeed the case. As a result, we manage to eliminate converse extensionality for all finite types, instead of for very low types only; in addition, we will do this without using continuity principles.

The main obstacle that we needed to overcome in order to have non-extensional witnesses for converse extensionality is that we had to find a good setting where extensional and non-extensional objects can peacefully co-exist. To do this, we will introduce a hybrid system for arithmetic. By this we mean an extension of a neutral system like
{\textsf{HA}}^{\omega}
with primitive notions of extensionality and extensional equality for which we postulate some axioms. Since the principle of converse extensionality is most naturally understood as talking about the interaction between extensional and non-extensional objects, this will be the right setting to formulate it.

The contents of this paper are therefore as follows. We will introduce our hybrid system in Section 2. In Section 3 we will present a useful technical result concerning the extensionality axiom. In Section 4 we will show that Gandy and Luckhardt’s elimination of extensionality can be seen as a two-step process with our hybrid system as an intermediate destination. In Section 5 we will present our
\alpha
-translation and show how it eliminates converse extensionality. In Section 6 we will briefly discuss how proof-theoretic interpretations like modified realizability and functional interpretations can be extended to hybrid systems. Finally, we conclude the paper with some discussion and directions for future research in Section 7.

Remark 2.4
.

Despite being very natural, we have decided not to include the axiom
x=_{\sigma}y\to{\textsf{ext}}_{\sigma}(x)\to{\textsf{ext}}_{\sigma}(y)
. While almost all results in this paper would still have gone through if we had included this axiom, there is one crucial exception, which we will discuss in Subsection 6.2 below.

Remark 2.5
.

If we assume surjectivity of pairing (that is,
\mathsf{pair}(\mathsf{fst}\,x)(\mathsf{snd}\,x)\equiv x
), then the axiom
{\textsf{ext}}_{\sigma\times\tau}(x)\leftrightarrow{\textsf{ext}}_{\sigma}(\mathsf{fst}\,x)\land{\textsf{ext}}_{\tau}(\mathsf{snd}\,x)
is redundant. Indeed, the left-to-right direction follows from the fact that

and
\mathsf{snd}
are extensional in combination with the axiom saying that extensional functions send extensional input to extensional output. The right-to-left direction follows from the same axiom in combination with extensionality of
\mathsf{pair}
and surjectivity of pairing.

3.
A closer look at extensionality

In this section we will take a closer look at the extensionality axiom
\mathsf{EXT}
in
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}
, which says that for all finite types
\sigma
and
\tau
we have:

\mathsf{EXT}_{\sigma,\tau}:\quad{\textsf{ext}}_{\sigma\to\tau}(f)\to{\textsf{ext}}_{\sigma}(x)\to{\textsf{ext}}_{\sigma}(y)\to x=y\to fx=fy.

Our goal in this section is to show that if this axiom holds in
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}
for types
\sigma
and
\tau
of the form
\sigma^{\prime}\to 0
and
\tau^{\prime}\to 0
, respectively, then it holds for all types
\sigma
and
\tau
.

The reason this is useful is that it allows us to reformulate the axiom
\mathsf{EXT}
in a way which no longer uses the notion of extensional equality. Of course, we have seen in Remark
2.6
a general method for eliminating extensional equality, but if the type
\sigma
is very complicated (for instance, if it contains many nested function types), the formula which this produces for
x=_{\sigma}y
may also be very complicated. However, the formula
f=_{\tau\to 0}g
is equivalent to
\forall^{{\textsf{ext}}{}}\!x:\tau.fx\equiv_{0}gx
, which is rather simple. So it follows from the results in this section that in
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}
the axiom
\mathsf{EXT}
is equivalent to the following principle:

\displaystyle\mathsf{EXT}^{\prime}:\quad{\textsf{ext}}_{(\sigma\to 0)\to(\tau\to 0)}(f)\to{\textsf{ext}}_{\sigma\to 0}(x)\to{\textsf{ext}}_{\sigma\to 0}(y)\to

\displaystyle\forall^{{\textsf{ext}}{}}\!u:\sigma.xu\equiv_{0}yu\to\forall^{{\textsf{ext}}{}}\!v:\tau.fxv\equiv_{0}fyv.

The point is that
\mathsf{EXT}^{\prime}
is a simple reformulation of
\mathsf{EXT}
which no longer uses the notion of extensional equality.

For the transparent presentation of our result, we will work in
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}
and use a tiny bit of category theory. We will write
\mathcal{C}
for the following category:

Objects:

The objects of
\mathcal{C}
are the finite types.

Morphisms:

The morphisms
\alpha\to\beta
in
\mathcal{C}
are equivalence classes of closed terms
t
of type
\alpha\to\beta
; here we regard
t,t^{\prime}:\alpha\to\beta
as equivalent if
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}\vdash t=t^{\prime}
(which is equivalent to
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}\vdash\forall^{{\textsf{ext}}{}}\!x:\alpha.tx=_{\beta}tx^{\prime}
).

Note that we always have
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}\vdash{\textsf{ext}}(t)
for any morphism
t
in this category, by Proposition
2.9
. The identities
\alpha\to\alpha
are given by
\lambda x:\alpha.x
, while the composition of
s:\alpha\to\beta
and
t:\beta\to\gamma
is
\lambda x:\alpha.t(sx)
. The axioms of a category are easily verified.

A morphism
t:\alpha\to\beta
in this category will be called
strong
if

{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}\vdash{\textsf{ext}}_{\alpha}(x)\to{\textsf{ext}}_{\alpha}(y)\to x=_{\alpha}y\to tx=_{\beta}ty

(note that this independent of the choice of representative, and
\mathsf{EXT}
implies that every morphism is strong). Clearly, identities are strong and the strong maps are closed under composition, so we obtain a subcategory
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathcal{C}
having the same objects as
\mathcal{C}
and whose morphisms are the strong morphisms of
\mathcal{C}
.

Let us first investigate to which extent these categories are cartesian closed.

Lemma 3.1
.

The type
\alpha\times\beta
is the categorical product of the types
\alpha
and
\beta
in both
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

The projection maps are
\mathsf{fst}:\alpha\times\beta\to\alpha
and
\mathsf{snd}:\alpha\times\beta\to\beta
, respectively. The axiom
x=_{\sigma\times\tau}y\leftrightarrow\mathsf{fst}\,x=_{\sigma}\mathsf{fst}\,y\land\mathsf{snd}\,x=_{\tau}\mathsf{snd}\,y
implies that both maps are strong.

If
s:\gamma\to\alpha
and
t:\gamma\to\beta
are morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
, then so is

(s,t):\equiv\lambda x:\sigma.\mathsf{pair}(sx)(tx):\gamma\to\alpha\times\beta.

We have
\mathsf{fst}\circ(s,t)=s
and
\mathsf{snd}\circ(s,t)=t
as morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
.

It remains to show that
(s,t):\gamma\to\alpha\times\beta
is the unique morphism with these properties and that
(s,t)
will be strong as soon as
s
and
t
are. However, all of this follows from the axiom
x=_{\sigma\times\tau}y\leftrightarrow\mathsf{fst}\,x=_{\sigma}\mathsf{fst}\,y\land\mathsf{snd}\,x=_{\tau}\mathsf{snd}\,y
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

The type
\alpha\to\beta
is the categorical exponential of the types
\alpha
and
\beta
in
\mathcal{C}
if we assume surjectivity of pairing; if we assume
\mathsf{EXT}_{\alpha,\beta}
, then it is the exponential in
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

We have a morphism
{\rm ev}:(\alpha\to\beta)\times\alpha\to\beta
in
\mathcal{C}

\lambda x:(\alpha\to\beta)\times\alpha.(\mathsf{fst}\,x)(\mathsf{snd}\,x).

If

is a morphism in
\mathcal{C}
, then we obtain a morphism

\lambda x:\gamma.\lambda y:\alpha.h(\mathsf{pair}\,x\,y).

If we assume surjectivity of pairing or if

{\rm ev}\circ(H\times 1_{\alpha})=h
. If

has the same property, then

Hxy\equiv{\rm ev}\circ(H\times 1)(\mathsf{pair}\,x\,y)=h(\mathsf{pair}\,x\,y)={\rm ev}\circ(H^{\prime}\times 1)(\mathsf{pair}\,x\,y)=H^{\prime}xy

It remains to show that
{\rm ev}

Finally, suppose that

x=x^{\prime}

. Because

is strong, this implies that

Hxy=h(\mathsf{pair}\,x\,y)=h(\mathsf{pair}\,x^{\prime}\,y)=Hx^{\prime}y;

we conclude that

is strong as well.
∎

The following technical definition is our most important tool in this section.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be finite types. We will say that
\alpha
is a
strong retract
of
\beta
if there are strong maps
i:\alpha\to\beta
and
r:\beta\to\alpha
such that
r\circ i=1_{\alpha}
.

Our next goal is to show that any type
\sigma
is a strong retract of one of the form
\tau\to 0
. The proof relies on the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

(i)

If
\alpha
is a strong retract of
\beta
and
\beta
is a strong retract of
\gamma
, then
\alpha
is a strong retract of
\gamma
.

(ii)

If
\alpha_{0}
is a strong retract of
\beta_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}
of
\beta_{1}
, then
\alpha_{0}\times\alpha_{1}
is a strong retract of
\beta_{0}\times\beta_{1}
.

(iii)

If
\alpha
is a strong retract of
\beta
, then
\gamma\to\alpha
is a strong retract of
\gamma\to\beta
.

(iv)

If
\alpha
is strongly isomorphic to
\beta
, then
\gamma\to\alpha
is strongly isomorphic to
\gamma\to\beta
.

(v)

(\alpha\to\beta)\times(\gamma\to\delta)
is a strong retract of
\alpha\times\beta\to\gamma\times\delta
.

(vi)

\alpha\to\beta\to\gamma
is a strong retract of
\alpha\times\beta\to\gamma
.

Proof.

Points (i) and (ii) are simple exercises in category theory, with (ii) using Lemma
3.1
.

(iii): If
i:\alpha\to\beta
and
r:\beta\to\alpha
are strong maps such that
r\circ i=1
, then

i^{\prime}:\equiv\lambda x:\gamma\to\alpha.\lambda y:\gamma.i(xy)

and

r^{\prime}:\equiv\lambda x:\gamma\to\beta.\lambda y:\gamma.r(xy)

define strong maps
i^{\prime}:(\gamma\to\alpha)\to(\gamma\to\beta)
and
r^{\prime}:(\gamma\to\beta)\to(\gamma\to\alpha)
such that
r^{\prime}\circ i^{\prime}=1
. Indeed, if we have extensional

such that
x=x^{\prime}
, then
i^{\prime}x=i^{\prime}x^{\prime}
will follow as soon as we have
i^{\prime}xy=i^{\prime}x^{\prime}y
for any extensional

. We have

because

and

is extensional, and

i^{\prime}xy\equiv i(xy)=i(x^{\prime}y)\equiv i^{\prime}x^{\prime}y,

because
i
is strong. So
i^{\prime}
is strong and a similar argument shows that
r^{\prime}
is strong as well.

To show
r^{\prime}\circ i^{\prime}=1
, we pick extensional
x:\gamma\to\alpha
and
y:\gamma
and show that
(r^{\prime}\circ i^{\prime})xy=xy
. But we have

(r^{\prime}\circ i^{\prime})xy\equiv r((i^{\prime}x)y)\equiv r(i(xy))=xy,

using that

is extensional and

.

(iv): By
\alpha
and
\beta
being strongly isomorphic, we mean that they are isomorphic in the category

. If in the situation as in (iii) above, we also have

, then we must have

as well, by symmetry.

(v): In the forward directon there is:

i:\equiv\lambda h:(\alpha\to\beta)\times(\gamma\to\delta).\lambda y:\alpha\times\beta.\mathsf{pair}((\mathsf{fst}\,h)(\mathsf{fst}\,y))((\mathsf{snd}\,h)(\mathsf{snd}\,y)),

while in the backward direction there is:

r:\equiv\lambda k:\alpha\times\beta\to\gamma\times\delta.\mathsf{pair}(\lambda x:\alpha.\mathsf{fst}(k(\mathsf{pair}\,x\,0)))(\lambda z:\beta.\mathsf{snd}(k(\mathsf{pair}\,0\,z)),

where

is a closed term as in Lemma
2.1
. By arguments similar to those that we have seen before, both operations can be shown to be strong, while

.

(vi): In the forward directon there is:

i:\equiv\lambda f:\alpha\to\beta\to\gamma.\lambda x:\alpha\times\beta.f(\mathsf{fst}\,x)(\mathsf{snd}\,x),

while in the backward direction there is:

r:\equiv\lambda g:\alpha\times\beta\to\gamma.\lambda x:\alpha.\lambda y:\beta.g(\mathsf{pair}\,x\,y).

Both operations can be shown to be strong, while
r\circ i=1
. (Note that if we had surjectivity of pairing, we could show

as well.)
∎

Proposition 3.5

Every type
\sigma

\tau\to 0
.

Proof.

We prove this statement by induction on the structure of

For type 0, we have maps

∎

In this section we will indicate how interpretations like modified realizability and the Diller-Nahm functional interpretation can be extended to
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}

6.1.
Modified realizability

Kreisel’s modified realizability interpretation can be seen as an interpretation of
{\textsf{HA}}^{\omega}
into itself. This can be extended to an interpretation of
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}
into itself by adding the following clauses:

\displaystyle x\,{\textsf{mr}}\,{\textsf{ext}}(y)

\displaystyle:\equiv

\displaystyle{\textsf{ext}}(y),

\displaystyle x\,{\textsf{mr}}\,y=z

\displaystyle:\equiv

\displaystyle y=z.

This interpret also validates principles like
\mathsf{EXT}
or
\mathsf{CEXT}
, assuming they hold in the interpreting system: in particular,
mr
can also be extended to an interpretation of
{\textsf{HE-HA}}^{\omega}
into itself.

6.2.
A functional interpretation

It is also possible to set up a functional interpretation of
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}
into itself. For this we choose the version of
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}
without a primitive notion of extensional equality, using Remark
2.6
. In that case we only have
ext
as a new symbol, which we will interpret as itself. For our functional interpretation, we choose the Diller-Nahm variant of Gödel’s original Dialectica interpretation
[
3
]
: indeed, we have to, because
ext
is an atomic formula which is not decidable.
2
2

2

Not to mention that we have written this paper with
{\textsf{HA}}^{\omega}
as our base system, for which Gödel’s original interpretation does not work, because for higher types
\sigma
the equality predicate
\equiv_{\sigma}
cannot be shown to be decidable in
{\textsf{HA}}^{\omega}
. Indeed, for Gödel’s Dialectica interpretation to work we need all atomic formulas to be decidable; the benefit of the Diller-Nahm variant is that it also works when this is not the case.

It is not hard to see that in this way the Diller-Nahm variant also works as an interpretation of
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}
into itself. Note, however, that this interpretation need not interpret the formula

x=y\to{\textsf{ext}}(x)\to{\textsf{ext}}(y).

This has been our main reason for excluding this axiom from
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}
.

Let us what happens with extensionality, for which we take the formulation

\displaystyle\mathsf{EXT}^{\prime}:\quad{\textsf{ext}}_{(\sigma\to 0)\to(\tau\to 0)}(f)\to{\textsf{ext}}_{\sigma\to 0}(x)\to{\textsf{ext}}_{\sigma\to 0}(y)\to

\displaystyle\forall^{{\textsf{ext}}{}}\!u:\sigma.xu\equiv_{0}yu\to\forall^{{\textsf{ext}}{}}\!v:\tau.fxv\equiv_{0}fyv,

as in Corollary
3.7
. The Diller-Nahm functional interpretation asks us in this case to find a functional

\displaystyle\mathsf{CEXT}:\quad\exists Z.\,\forall^{\textsf{ext}}f:(\sigma\to 0)\to(\tau\to 0).\,\forall^{\textsf{ext}}x,y:\sigma\to 0.\,\forall^{\textsf{ext}}v:\tau.

7.
Conclusion

We have introduced hybrid systems for arithmetic,
{\textsf{H-HA}}^{\omega}
and
{\textsf{HE-HA}}^{\omega}
, and shown how it can be used to formulate a strong extensionality principle, which we have dubbed converse extensionality. Inspired by Brouwer’s notion of apartness, we have defined a proof-theoretic interpretation, which we have called the
\alpha
-translation, which can be used to eliminate this converse extensionality principle.

One potential application of this work is in proof-mining
[
4
]
, in particular, in proof-mining arguments using extensionality principles. One standard way to treat these is by first eliminating them using the elimination of extensionality and then applying functional interpretation. This would correspond to going from
{\textsf{E-HA}}^{\omega}
to
{\textsf{HA}}^{\omega}
along the top of the following (non-commuting) diagram, combining elimination of extensionality (EE) and the (Diller-Nahm) functional interpretation (DN).

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\textsf{HE-HA}}^{\omega}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\textsf{HA}}^{\omega}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\textsf{HE-HA}}^{\omega}+\mathsf{CEXT}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rm DN}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rm Th.4.1}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rm EE}

The
\alpha
-translation provides an alternative to this, by allowing us to go along the bottom of the diagram above. Since the
\alpha
-translation of equality statements looks easier than their functional interpretation, this deserves further exploration.

In addition, the idea of a hybrid system to formulate and study hybrid principles should be more widely applicable. For instance, there are discontinuous functionals realizing continuity principles (
[
5
, Theorem 2.6.7]
or
[
6
]
), and one could also imagine non-majorizable functionals realizing majorizability statements. We believe this is another promising direction for future research.
