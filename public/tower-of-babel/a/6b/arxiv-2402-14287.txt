Tropical Fermat-Weber Polytropes
John Sabol, David Barnhill, Ruriko Yoshida, Keiji Miura

Abstract
We study the geometry of tropical Fermat-Weber points in terms of the symmetric tropical metric over the tropical projective torus. It is well-known that a tropical Fermat-Weber point of a given sample is not unique and we show that the set of all possible Fermat-Weber points forms a polytrope. To prove this, we show that the tropical Fermat-Weber is the dual of a minimum-cost flow problem, and that its polytrope is a bounded cell of a tropical hyperplane arrangement given by both max- and min-tropical hyperplanes with apices given by the sample. We also define tropical Fermat-Weber gradients and provide a gradient descent algorithm that converges to the Fermat-Weber polytrope.



1
Introduction

Consider a sample
\mathcal{S}:=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
, and suppose we wish to find a point
x
that minimizes the sum of distances from
x
to all points in
\mathcal{S}
with respect to the distance metric
{\bf d}
. This problem is known as the
Fermat-Weber location problem
. Since
Weiszfeld
, there has been much work done with an Euclidean metric (for example, see
Brimberg
and references within). This optimal point
x^{*}
is called a
Fermat-Weber point
of the sample
\mathcal{S}
, and it is defined as

x^{*}=\argmin_{x}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf d}(x,p_{i}).

Fermat-Weber points are a form of geometric median which describe central tendency in data and which, like their one-dimensional counterparts, are robust against outliers. They have many applications in statistical analysis and optimization, particularly in the area of phylogenetics. For example,
bayesEstimator
used Fermat-Weber points to define a Bayes estimator for a phylogenetic tree reconstruction given an alignment as the minimizer of the expected loss, while
Com_neci_2023
showed a correspondence between Fermat-Weber points and optimal solutions of a transportation problem to compute consensus trees.

In this paper we consider Fermat-Weber points over the tropical projective torus,
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
, using the
tropical metric

d_{\mathrm{tr}}
and call these points
tropical Fermat-Weber points
. For our paper
d_{\mathrm{tr}}
refers to the
symmetric
metric, which distinguishes it from the
asymmetric
metric considered in
Com_neci_2023
and
Comneci2024
. Details on the equivalence relation for the quotient defining
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
and the definition of
d_{\mathrm{tr}}
are provided in
Section

2
. It is well known that a tropical Fermat-Weber point of a given sample
\mathcal{S}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
is not generally unique, and in 2017 Lin et al. proposed a method for finding tropical Fermat-Weber points as optimal solutions to a linear program
(
LSTY
)
. Later, Lin and Yoshida studied the geometry of the set of tropical Fermat-Weber points, which we denote
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
, proving that
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
defines a classically convex polytope in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
(which is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
) and providing conditions for which
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
is a singleton
(
Lin2016TropicalFP
)
.

Thus, our object of interest is the polytope defined by the set of tropical Fermat-Weber points of a given sample. More precisely, we consider the set

\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S}):=\left\{x^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}\middle|\ x^{*}=\argmin_{x}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\mathrm{tr}}(x,p_{i})\right\}

(1)

for
\mathcal{S}:=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
.
Our main contribution is that
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
is
tropically
convex over
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
. This means that the polytope defined by the set of tropical Fermat-Weber points of a sample
\mathcal{S}
is a
polytrope
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
since
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
is known to be classically convex
(
Lin2016TropicalFP
)
.
Additional contributions of this research are as follows:

•

We introduce an alternative formulation to the one proposed in
LSTY
, proving that it is dual to a minimum-cost flow problem. This provides an intuitive geometric interpretation and connection to several concepts in the literature.

•

We prove that
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
is a bounded cell in the common refinement given by the max-plus and min-plus
covector decompositions
, which are generated by max- and min-plus
tropical hyperplane arrangements
with apices
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
. It then follows that
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
is a polytrope.

•

We provide a precise definition and formula for
tropical Fermat-Weber gradients
and
subdifferentials
, proving that
x^{*}
is a Fermat-Weber point if and only if zero is an element of its tropical Fermat-Weber subdifferential.

•

We introduce a
tropical Fermat-Weber gradient descent
algorithm that converges to
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
, enabling application to data at a much larger scale.

Tropical Basics

Here we establish notations, definitions, and some required facts to support our methods while briefly reminding readers of some basics from tropical geometry. For details, we refer to
joswigBook
. Through this paper we denote
[d]:=\{1,\makebox[10.00002pt][c]{.\hfil.\hfil.},d\}
and
[n]:=\{1,\makebox[10.00002pt][c]{.\hfil.\hfil.},n\}
for natural numbers
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
. We use
i,j,k
as indices.

2.1
Tropical Building Blocks

Definition 1

(Tropical Semiring)
.

Over the tropical semiring
\mathbb{R}^{d}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}_{\max}:=(\,{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty\},\oplus,\odot)
, tropical addition
(\oplus)
and multiplication
(\odot)
are defined as
a\oplus b:=\max\{a,b\}
and
a\odot b:=a+b
where
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty\}
.

This semiring has
-\infty
as the identity element under addition, i.e.,
a\oplus-\infty=a
for any
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and
0
as the identity element under multiplication. Extension to scalar multiplication and vector addition through componentwise application yields the
tropical semimodule

(\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}_{\max}^{d},\oplus,\odot)
.

Throughout this paper we will use
\mathbb{R}^{d}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}_{\max}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}_{\max}^{d}
to denote elements in
{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty\}
and
(\,{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty\})^{d}
, sometimes dropping the
max
subscript when the meaning is clear from context. For
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}^{d}
, the
support
of
u

\mathrm{supp}(u)
is the set of indices
j\in[d]
for which
u_{j}
is finite. While we define our semiring here using the
max-plus
algebra, there is a corresponding
min-plus
version denoted by
\mathbb{R}^{d}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}_{\min}
with elements in
{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
, where
a\oplus b:=\min\{a,b\}
, and with
\infty
as the additive identity. The isomorphism between
max-plus
and
min-plus
semirings is reflective around the origin, demonstrated by the equality
\max\{x,y\}=-\min\{-x,-y\}
. Since our paper frequently leverages both
max
and
min
concepts simultaneously, we will specify which version is intended where such distinctions are necessary.

Definition 2

(Tropical Projective Space and Tropical Projective Torus)
.

The tropical projective space
\mathbb{TP}^{d-1}=(\mathbb{R}^{d}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}^{d}\setminus\{-\infty\mathbf{1}\})\!/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
is a quotient space modulo the subspace generated by the span of the all-ones vector
\mathbf{1}:=(1,\makebox[10.22217pt][c]{.\hfil.\hfil.},1)
. The tropical projective torus
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
is the subset of points in
\mathbb{TP}^{d-1}
with finite coordinates.

The tropical projective torus is homeomorphic to
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
via the bijective map

(x_{1},\makebox[10.00002pt][c]{.\hfil.\hfil.},x_{d})+{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbf{1}}=(0,x_{2}-x_{1},\makebox[10.00002pt][c]{.\hfil.\hfil.},x_{d}-x_{1})+{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbf{1}}\mapsto(0,x_{2}-x_{1},\makebox[10.00002pt][c]{.\hfil.\hfil.},x_{d}-x_{1}).

(2)

In general this mapping is not unique. We will apply
x_{1}=0
(as above) as our primary convention noting that this differs from the
canonical projection
, which projects uniquely into the non-negative orthant so that at least one coordinate of
x
is zero.

Definition 3

(Tropical Cones)
.

A tropical cone in the tropical semimodule
\mathbb{R}^{d}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}^{d}
is a subsemimodule given by

\mathrm{tpos}(M)=\left\{\lambda_{1}\odot p_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\lambda_{n}\odot p_{n}\mid\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1},p_{i}\in M\right\},

(3)

where
M
defines a set of generators and
n\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of generators. Such a cone is called polyhedral if it is finitely generated.

Since every tropical cone contains the point
-\infty\mathbf{1}
, the set
\mathrm{tpos}(M)
is always non-empty. A subset of
\mathbb{TP}^{d-1}
is
tropically convex
if it arises as the image of a tropical cone in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}^{d}
under canonical projection.

Definition 4

(Tropical Polytopes)
.

A tropical polytope
\mathcal{P}
is the tropical convex hull of finitely many points
\mathcal{S}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
. It is the smallest tropically convex subset containing
\mathcal{S}
, and it coincides with the set of all tropical linear combinations

\mathrm{tconv}(\mathcal{S})=\{a_{1}\odot p_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus a_{n}\odot p_{n}\mid a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}\}.

(4)

Tropical polytopes are tropically convex sets, which means that for any
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
and for any
u,v\in\mathcal{P}
we have
a\odot u\oplus b\odot v\in\mathcal{P}
. A set which is tropically convex in terms of
max-plus
is not (in general) tropically convex in terms of
min-plus
. When a set is tropically convex in both conventions we call it
bi-tropically convex
. It is easy to show that classical convexity is a natural byproduct of bi-tropical convexity
(
joswigBook
)
but that the converse does not hold. Since we earlier defined a
polytrope
to be a set that is both classically and tropically convex, it follows that bi-tropical convexity is a sufficient condition for a polytrope.

Definition 5

(Generalized Hilbert Projective Metric)
.

For any points
u\,,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
, the tropical distance, or tropical metric,
d_{\mathrm{tr}}
between
u
and
v
is defined as:

d_{\mathrm{tr}}(u,v):=\max_{j\in[d]}\bigl\{u_{j}-v_{j}\bigr\}-\min_{k\in[d]}\bigl\{u_{k}-v_{k}\bigr\}.

(5)

Note that
d_{\mathrm{tr}}
defined in
Eq.

5
is equivalently defined regardless of whether one is operating in the
max-plus
or
min-plus
semiring, hence its characterization as a
symmetric
metric.

With our tropical metric, it is natural to consider a
tropical ball
, which is analogous to its Euclidean counterpart but with its radius measured using the tropical metric.

Definition 6

(Tropical Ball
(

For a given point

\mathcal{B}_{r}(u)=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}\mid d_{\mathrm{tr}}(x,u)\leq r\}.

Definition 7

(Tropical Hyperplanes
(
joswigBook
)
)
.

Given a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}_{max}^{d}
with a support of at least two elements, the max-tropical hyperplane
\mathcal{H}^{\max}_{v}
is the set of points
x\in\mathbb{TP}^{d-1}
for which the following maximum is attained at least twice:

\max_{j\in[d]}\{x_{j}+v_{j}\}.

(7)

A min-tropical hyperplane
\mathcal{H}^{\min}_{v}
can be equivalently defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}_{min}^{d}
with
max
replacing
min
in
Eq.

7
. With
v
being the normal vector of
\mathcal{H}^{\max}_{v}
, we have
-v
as the
apex
of
H^{\max}_{v}
. The requirement on the support of
v
ensures that the hyperplane is non-empty. As with tropical balls, any
\mathcal{H}^{\max}_{v}
can be viewed as a translation of
\mathcal{H}^{\max}_{\mathbf{0}}
, the tropical hyperplane with apex at the origin
(
Joswig
, Proposition 2.6)
. In
Fig.

1
(right), we see
\mathcal{H}^{\max}_{-u}
(solid lines) superimposed with
\mathcal{H}^{\min}_{-u}
(dashed lines), where
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
. In this case, each hyperplane individually partitions
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
into exactly
d

sectors
.

Definition 8

(Tropical Sectors from Section 5.5 in
(
joswigBook
)
)
.

For

, the
{j}^{\mathrm{th}}
closed sector of
\mathcal{H}^{\max}_{v}
is defined as

{S}^{\max,j}_{v}:=\bigcap_{k\in\mathrm{supp}(v)\setminus\{j\}}\left\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mid x_{j}+v_{j}\geq x_{k}+v_{k}\right\}.

(8)

When the inequality in

is strict, we call it the
{j}^{\mathrm{th}}

open sector
of
\mathcal{H}^{\max}_{v}
and write

. Closed sectors are the topological closure of open sectors. Every closed sector of a tropical hyperplane is a tropical cone with respect to both max and min, implying that their projections to the tropical projective torus are bi-tropically convex
(
joswigBook
)
, and their unions form
tropical halfspaces

(
Joswig
)
. When

has full support,

Sectors correspond to indices of maximal (resp. minimal) elements of their normal vector. That is, for

, we can conveniently rephrase

as

and equivalently,

{S}^{\max,j}_{-v}=v+{S}_{\mathbf{0}}^{\max,j}\quad\text{and}\quad{S}^{\min,k}_{-v}=v+{S}_{\mathbf{0}}^{\min,k},

(9)

where we sometimes omit the

2.2
Tropical Configurations

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{TP}^{d-1}
be a sample of points, which we associate to a
{n}{\mkern-1.0mu\times\mkern-1.0mu}{d}
-matrix
V
with coefficients
v_{ij}
in
\mathbb{T}

{i}^{\mathrm{th}}
row of

corresponds to

, and we use both notations interchangeably. When we write

row vector of
V

Definition 9

(Tropical Hyperplane Arrangement)
.

For
\mathcal{S}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{TP}^{d-1}
, the tropical hyperplane arrangement, denoted
\mathcal{T}^{max}(\mathcal{S})
, is the union of
n
tropical hyperplanes with apices given by the points in
\mathcal{S}
. That is,

\mathcal{T}^{\max}(\mathcal{S}):=\mathcal{H}^{\max}_{-p_{1}}\cup\ldots\cup\mathcal{H}^{\max}_{-p_{n}}.

(11)

We say that the arrangement has full support if
V
has only finite coefficients. Recall how each hyperplane generates a normal complex that decomposes
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
into non-empty closed sectors. Then for a union of several hyperplanes (i.e. a hyperplane arrangement) we get a common refinement of the normal complexes called the
covector decomposition
of
V
, or
\mathrm{CovDec}(V)

Definition 10

(Covector Decomposition)
.

The covector decomposition induced by
V
is a polyhedral subdivision of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
generated by the tropical hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{T}(V)

Each cell
C

(
joswigBook

.
Ardila

x\in\mathbb{TP}^{d-1}
given such an arrangement. These
types
encode the combinatorial structure of a tropical hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{T}(V)
as an
n
-tuple where the
{i}^{\mathrm{th}}
element corresponds to the set of closed sectors in
\mathcal{H}_{-v_{i}}
that contain
x
. Since sector inclusion changes only when
x
crosses a face of a hyperplane in
\mathcal{T}
, all points within a common face are of the same type.

One can similarly encode the same combinatorial structure via a

-tuple whose
{j}^{\mathrm{th}}
element records the indices for the set of points
v_{i}\in V
whose
{j}^{\mathrm{th}}
closed sector contains
x
. We follow

and call this a

. Covectors, which are tuples (not vectors), correspond to cells in
\mathrm{CovDec}(V)
in the same way that types correspond to faces in
\mathcal{T}(V)

2.3
Max and Min Together

Thus far we have primarily operated in the max-plus tropical semiring. Since we now wish to employ max-plus and min-plus concepts concurrently, we will work in the tropical projective torus
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
, which works for both max and min simultaneously. Hence, in what follows we assume that all points in a sample
\mathcal{S}
have full support, or equivalently, all entries in
V
are finite.

To distinguish between max-plus and min-plus objects we update our notation as follows. Max-plus objects will retain our original notation:
t,\,\tau,\,T,\,G,\,\#t,\,\#\tau
. For objects derived using min-plus algebra we add an overhead bar:
\overline{t},\,\overline{\tau},\,\overline{T},\,\overline{G},\,\#\overline{t},\,\#\overline{\tau}
. Similar to our definition of
y_{i}
, let us use
z_{i}
to denote the minimum achieved by the equality subgraph of
\overline{G}_{V}(x)\subseteq B(V)=K_{n\times d}
. Since we assume
V
has full support, we have

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle=\min_{k\in[d]}(x_{k}-v_{ik})=x_{j}-v_{ij}\quad\forall j\in\overline{t}_{V}(x),

(19)

\displaystyle\leq x_{j}-v_{ij}\quad j\in[d],

so that
\overline{\mathcal{E}}(V):=\{z_{i}-x_{j}\leq-v_{ij}\mid j\in[d]\,\}
defines the
min-plus
envelope of
V
. It is important to note our decision to be consistent in the direction of the inequality descriptions for both max and min envelopes. Our convention on
G_{V}(x)
(where arcs extended from
N
to
D
) encodes the face of
\mathcal{E}(V)
given by
x_{j}-y_{i}=v_{ij}
for
(i,j)\in G_{V}(x)
. Then by the same token, the face of
\overline{\mathcal{E}}(V)
given by
z_{i}-x_{j}=-v_{ij}
should encode the arcs in
\overline{G}_{V}(x)
. The natural interpretation then is that
\overline{G}_{V}(x)
has arcs
from

D

to

N
. Since we prefer the interpretation of fine-type matrices as encoding the adjacency of their corresponding covector graph, we specify
\overline{T}_{V}(x)
to be
{d}{\mkern-1.0mu\times\mkern-1.0mu}{n}
rather than
{n}{\mkern-1.0mu\times\mkern-1.0mu}{d}
, and have
\overline{\tau}
record the row sums vice column sums.

With our min-plus objects properly distinguished, we are ready to introduce the following new
bi-tropical
objects.

Definition 14

(Tropical Half-Sectors)
.

An open tropical half-sector is the nonempty intersection of an open max-plus sector with that of a open min-plus sector, both with apex
u
, i.e.,

S^{\circ j,k}_{-u}:=S^{\circ\max,j}_{-u}\cap S^{\circ\min,k}_{-u},

(20)

where
j,k\in[d]
index the max/min-plus hyperplane sectors respectively.

From the definition, it is straightforward to see that if
j=k
then no such intersection is possible. Thus, our definition of tropical half-sectors
implies

j\neq k
even when considering their topological closure, which form
closed
tropical half-sectors in the same sense as
Definition

8
. When we use the term “half-sectors” it is assumed we mean
closed
half-sectors unless otherwise noted.

Fig.

1
(right) depicts the half-sector generated by the second sector of a max-plus hyperplane intersected and the third sector of a min-plus hyperplane. As intersections of tropical sectors, tropical half-sectors remain bi-tropically convex. Viewed as translates from the origin, tropical half-sectors are also polyhedral cones, which leads to the following definition.

Definition 15

(Face Fan of
\mathcal{B}(\bf 0)
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the polyhedral fan generated by the set of all closed half-sectors, i.e.,

\mathcal{F}:=\{\,{S}_{\bf 0}^{j,k}\mid j,k\in[d]\,\},

(21)

which we call the face fan of the tropical ball
\mathcal{B}(\bf 0)
.

In general, the face fan of a polytope
\mathcal{P}
is the set of all cones spanned by proper faces of
\mathcal{P}

(
Ziegler1995
)
. That is to say, each
{S}^{j,k}
defines a cone that is facet-defining for
\mathcal{B}(\bf 0)
. Thus,
\mathcal{F}
has cardinality
|\mathcal{F}|=d(d-1)
and each cone in
\mathcal{F}
corresponds to the indices of the maximal and minimal elements of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
. Since
\mathcal{F}
encodes only the maximal and minimal indices, rather than a complete ordering on
[d]
, we can view
\mathcal{F}
as a coarsening of the
braid arrangement

(
CJS
)
.

We again consider a translation of the tropical ball from its center at the origin, to some arbitrary point
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
, and admit a slight abuse of terminology by calling
\mathcal{F}(u):=\mathcal{F}+u
the
face fan
of
\mathcal{B}(u)
. Viewed as a hyperplane arrangement,
\mathcal{F}(u)
provides a common refinement of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
in the manner one would expect, i.e., as the union of normal complexes corresponding to both max- and min-plus hyperplanes. More precisely,

\mathcal{F}(u):=\mathcal{H}^{\max}_{-u}\cup\mathcal{H}^{\min}_{-u}=\mathcal{H}^{\max}_{-u}\cup-\mathcal{H}^{\max}_{-u}=\{\,{S}_{-u}^{j,k}\mid j,k\in[d]\,\},

(22)

which decomposes
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
into
d(d-1)
cells of maximum dimension
(
joswigBook
)
. If we consider a collection of points each representing an apex for
\mathcal{F}
we arrive at a bi-tropical version of the covector decomposition.

Definition 16

(Bi-Tropical Covector Decomposition)
.

For an
{n}{\mkern-1.0mu\times\mkern-1.0mu}{d}
-matrix

generated by the union of max-plus and min-plus hyperplanes each with apices at

for all

. It is precisely the union of covector decompositions generated by each version of the tropical semiring.

Each cell

in

corresponds to a unique
pair
of max-plus and min-plus covectors, along with their associated covector graphs and fine-type matrices. As intersections of tropical cones, these cells remain bi-tropically convex, and each cell can be written as

C\in\mathrm{BCD}(V)=\left\{\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}~\Bigg|\begin{aligned} \,x_{j}-x_{k}&\geq v_{ij}-v_{ik}\quad\forall(i,j)\in G_{V}(C),k\in[d],\\

(23)

This shows that each bounded cell
C
of
\mathrm{BCD}(V)
is a polytrope. Additionally, as they are the intersection of cells from max-plus and min-plus covector decompositions, the inclusion-reversing relationships between cells from
Eq.

23
extend to the bi-tropical case. This provides the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
and for each point

select a half-sector. Then the intersection of these half-sectors is either empty, or it defines (not necessarily uniquely) a unique maximal cell in
\mathrm{BCD}(\mathcal{S})
.

Proof.

Proof of
Lemma

1
.
To each half-sector we can associate max and min covectors using
Eq.

20
and
Definition

11
. The associated covector graphs
G,\,\overline{G}
contain a minimal number of arcs by construction. By inequalities in
Eq.

23
, these translate to a minimal system of linear inequalities describing the intersection of the half-sectors. If this intersection is empty, then there is nothing to prove. If it is non-empty, then the intersection must define a cell in
\mathrm{BCD}(\mathcal{S})
as half-sectors provide the finest possible refinement. Since the inequality description given by the half-sectors is minimal, the cell itself must be the maximal one described by the intersection. Uniqueness follows from convexity of the covector cells.
∎

3
Computing Tropical Fermat-Weber Points

Lin2016TropicalFP
showed that
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
is a classical polytope in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
and offered a linear program (LP) with
x^{*}\in\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
as its optimal solution. In this section, we propose an alternative formulation, one that is more efficient to formulate, and whose dual program corresponds precisely with a
minimum-cost flow
problem. This alternative formulation makes explicit the fact that
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
is fully characterized as a cell of the sample’s bi-tropical covector decomposition
\mathrm{BCD}(\mathcal{S})
.

3.1
The Tropical Fermat-Weber Objective Function

We begin by examining the tropical Fermat-Weber function,
f_{V}(x)
, introduced in
Eq.

1
, which we will sometimes shorten to
f(x)
when
V
is arbitrary or clear from context. Given a
{n}{\mkern-1.0mu\times\mkern-1.0mu}{d}
matrix
V
with all finite entries, we have

f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\mathrm{tr}}(x,v_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x),

(24)

where
v_{i}
is a row of
V
, and
f_{i}
computes the tropical distance between
x
and
v_{i}
. Recall that a function,
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is convex (in the classic sense) if
f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)v)\leq\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(v)
for any
x,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
and
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
.

Lemma 2

(Proposition 6 in
Lin2016TropicalFP
)
.

f_{V}(x)
is a convex function.

Proof.

Proof of
Lemma

2
.
Consider a single
f_{i}(x)
from (
24
). We show that
f_{i}(x)=d_{\mathrm{tr}}(x,p_{i})=d_{\mathrm{tr}}(x-p_{i},\mathbf{0})
is a convex function. For
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
and
x,\,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
,

\displaystyle f_{i}(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{j\in[d]}(\lambda x_{j}+(1-\lambda)v_{j})-\min_{j\in[d]}(\lambda x_{j}+(1-\lambda)v_{j})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{j\in[d]}(\lambda x_{j})+\max_{j\in[d]}((1-\lambda)v_{j})-\min_{j\in[d]}(\lambda x_{j})-\min_{j\in[d]}((1-\lambda)v_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda f_{i}(x)+(1-\lambda)f_{i}(v).

Thus,
f_{i}(x)
is convex. Sums of convex functions are convex, so we are done.
∎

Establishing the convexity of
f_{V}(x)
automatically establishes the convexity of
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
, and ensures that any
local minimizer
of
f_{V}(x)
is also a
global minimizer
.

3.2
A Tropical Fermat-Weber Linear Program

Consider a particular
i\in[n]
and define
w_{i}
as

\displaystyle w_{i}:=d_{\mathrm{tr}}(x,v_{i})=d_{\mathrm{tr}}(v_{i},x)

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in[d]}\{v_{ij}-x_{j}\}-\min_{k\in[d]}\{v_{ik}-x_{k}\},

(25)

\displaystyle\geq(v_{ij}-x_{j})-(v_{ik}-x_{k})\quad\forall j,k\in[d].

By definition,
w_{i}
is the tropical distance between
v_{i}
and
x
. Any case where
j=k
clearly achieves
Eq.

25
across all indices, so we only need to consider constraints of the form in
Eq.

25
for cases
1\leq j\neq k\leq d
since the case for
j=k
is redundant. Adding two constraints from
Eq.

25
indexed by
(j,k)
and
(k,j)
yields the implicit constraint
2w_{i}\geq 0
, a result that reinforces the fact that
d_{\mathrm{tr}}
is a metric in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
(Proposition 5.19 in
(
joswigBook
)
). This shows the boundedness of our objective function.

Let
V
be an
{n}{\mkern-1.0mu\times\mkern-1.0mu}{d}
matrix with entries in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
. Then applying all sets of inequalities from
Eq.

25
produces the linear program primal formulation originally provided in
Lin2016TropicalFP
:

\displaystyle(\mathrm{P0})

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{subject~to}

\displaystyle w_{i}+x_{j}-x_{k}

\displaystyle\geq v_{ij}-v_{ik}

\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n,

\displaystyle 1\leq j\neq k\leq d,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product. Since any choice of
x
in this primal problem can be made feasible by large enough
w
, the primal feasible region is always non-empty. The problem is of size
{nd(d-1)}{\mkern-1.0mu\times\mkern-1.0mu}{(n+d)}
and each constraint contains exactly three non-zero entries. We note that the quadratic growth in constraints can become prohibitive as
d
becomes large. Let us then propose the following minor modifications to
(\mathrm{P0})
. First, we split
w_{i}
into two separate variables (
y_{i},\,z_{i}
) which we recall from
Eq.

17
and
Eq.

19
as,

w_{i}=\max_{j\in[d]}\{x_{j}-v_{ij}\}-\min_{k\in[d]}\{x_{k}-v_{ik}\}=y_{i}-z_{i}.

Additionally, we switch to maximization by negating the coefficients in our objective function, and swap the direction of the constraint inequalities. The result is the following primal formulation, along with its dual program:

\displaystyle(\mathrm{P1})

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle+\langle-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{subject~to}

\displaystyle x_{j}-y_{i}

\displaystyle\leq v_{ij}

\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n,

\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq d,

\displaystyle\quad[\boldsymbol{\pi}_{ij}]

\displaystyle~~z_{i}-x_{j}

\displaystyle\leq-v_{ij}

\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n,

\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq d,

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{\phi}_{ji}]

\displaystyle(\mathrm{D1})

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\pi}\geq 0,\boldsymbol{\phi}\geq 0}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\pi}\rangle+\langle-\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{subject~to}

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{ij}-\phi_{ij}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad 1\leq j\leq d,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{x}_{j}]

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}-\pi_{ij}

\displaystyle=-1

\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{y}_{i}]

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_{ji}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n.

\displaystyle[\mathbf{z}_{i}]

We use
[\boldsymbol{\pi}]
,
[\boldsymbol{\phi}]
,
[\mathbf{x}]
, etc., to denote blocks of constraints in the primal/dual formulations. Our new primal problem is of size
{(2dn)}{\mkern-1.0mu\times\mkern-1.0mu}{(d+2n)}
and requires only two non-zero elements per constraint, a substantial improvement over
(\mathrm{P0})
. The true value of this reformulation, however, comes by realizing that
(\mathrm{D1})
is a transhipment problem, and therefore also a min-cost flow (
\mathrm{mcf}
) problem on
|N|=d+2n
nodes and
|\mathcal{A}|=2dn
arcs. Figure
5

Lemma 3

The tropical Fermat-Weber linear program has a binary optimal dual solution.

Proof.

Proof of

.

Binary optimal dual solutions are convenient in that they support our earlier intuition regarding the correspondence of dual variables to half-sectors of
\mathrm{BCD}(V)

Equivalently, optimal

\pi^{*}_{ij}=1\Rightarrow(i,j)\in{G}(x^{*}),\qquad\phi^{*}_{ki}=1\Rightarrow(k,i)\in\overline{G}(x^{*}).

Proof of

4
Tropical Fermat-Weber Gradients

Despite the improvements provided by formulations like
(\mathrm{D2})
, larger data sets may still prove prohibitive in certain applications. Indeed, the complexity results from
Section

3
need to be stated in terms of
n
and
d
, i.e., the size of
V
. We defer these results at the end of the section, where we compare them against the iterative method we now propose. Here we define the tropical Fermat-Weber gradient and show that it is well-defined and constant over any full-dimensional cell of
\mathrm{BCD}(\mathcal{S})
. For any point in a cell of
\mathrm{BCD}(\mathcal{S})
that is not full-dimensional, we show that the subdifferential is defined by the cone generated by the set of gradients that come from the interior of all full-dimensional adjacent cells. To avoid the need to enumerate all full-dimensional adjacent cells, we propose a
Fermat-Weber oracle
via a max-flow problem that determines optimality of a proposed point when it is non-differentiable with respect to the tropical Fermat-Weber gradient. A tropical perspective on gradient descent was also explored in
talbut2024tropicalgradientdescent
, where they focused on problems exhibiting tropical (but not necessarily classical) convexity using quasiconvex functions defined in
Comneci2024
, who applied them in computing asymmetric tropical Fermat-Weber points. Here we focus exclusively on the tropical (symmetric) Fermat-Weber problem.

Let
V\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
and
C
be any full-dimensional cell of
\mathrm{BCD}(V)
with
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
distinct, arbitrary points in its relative interior, i.e.,
x,\,y\in\mathrm{relint}(C)
. Define
\delta:=y-x
as the component-wise difference and let
f_{V}(x)=\sum_{i}d_{\mathrm{tr}}(x,v_{i})=\sum_{i}w_{i}
be the sum of tropical distances from
x
to all points in
V
as before. Similarly, let
\gamma_{i}
be the corresponding tropical distances from
y
to
v_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. Then from
Definition

5
we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in[d]}\{y_{j}-v_{ij}\}-\min_{j\in[d]}\{y_{j}-v_{ij}\}

(26)

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in[d]}\{x_{j}+\delta_{j}-v_{ij}\}-\min_{j\in[d]}\{x_{j}+\delta_{j}-v_{ij}\}

\displaystyle=(x_{k}+\delta_{k}-v_{ik})-(x_{\ell}+\delta_{\ell}-v_{i\ell})

\displaystyle=w_{i}+\delta_{k}-\delta_{\ell},

where the last two equalities hold since by definition of covectors, the maximal and minimal indices of
(x-v_{i})
do not vary for any
x\in\mathrm{relint}(C)
. That is, if
k
denotes the index that achieves the maximum in
x-v_{i}
, then
k
also achieves the maximum in
y-v_{i}
. The same is true for
\ell
regarding the minimum. Since
x,\,y\in\mathrm{relint}(C)
,
k,\,\ell
are the unique elements of the
{i}^{\mathrm{th}}
max/min tropical types of
C
, i.e.
t_{V}(C)_{i}=k
and
\overline{t}_{V}(C)_{i}=\ell
. Since this is true for every
i\in[n]
, we have

\displaystyle f(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(w_{i}+\delta_{t(x)_{i}}-\delta_{\overline{t}(x)_{i}}\right)

(27)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T(x)_{ij}\delta_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{T}(x)_{ji}\delta_{j}

\displaystyle=f(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\#\tau(x)_{j})\delta_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\#\overline{\tau}(x)_{j})\delta_{j}

\displaystyle=f(x)+\langle\#\tau(x)-\#\overline{\tau}(x),\delta\rangle.

The summation over
i
of
T(y)_{ij}
counts the number of times the index
j
appears in the
{i}^{\mathrm{th}}
element of the covector. The equality in the second line is justified by the fact that each row of the fine-type matrices have only a single non-zero element, meaning that no element of
\delta
is double-counted over the summation. Letting
\delta\rightarrow\bf{0}
, we have

\nabla f(x)=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow\bf{0}}\frac{f(x+\delta)-f(x)}{\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert}=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow\bf{0}}\frac{\langle\#\tau(x)-\#\overline{\tau}(x),\delta\rangle}{\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert}=\#\tau(x)-\#\overline{\tau}(x).

(28)

This derivation shows explicitly that the gradient of
f_{V}(x)
is defined by the difference in max-plus and min-plus coarse-types of the full-dimensional cell
C
. It then follows that the gradient will only change upon crossing over a hyperplane boundary from one hyperplane sector into another.

Definition 18

(Tropical Fermat-Weber Gradient)
.

For
V\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
and
C
, a full-dimensional cell of
\mathrm{BCD}(V)
, the tropical Fermat-Weber gradient at any point
x\in\mathrm{relint}(C)
is the difference between the max- and min-plus coarse-types of
C
. That is,

\nabla f_{V}(x):=\#\tau_{V}(x)-\#\overline{\tau}_{V}(x)\quad\forall x\in\mathrm{relint}(C).

(29)

From the definition we see that
\nabla f_{V}(x)=0\iff\#\tau_{V}(x)=\#\overline{\tau}_{V}(x)
, that is, the maximal and minimal coarse-types must
balance
. Since coarse-types record the degree of nodes in
D
for the their respective covector graphs, we see that this requirement is equivalent to the flow-balance constraints given by
[\mathbf{x}]
in
(\mathrm{D1})
. For cells of maximal dimension, a tropical Fermat-Weber gradient is well-defined, as both covectors
\tau
and
\overline{\tau}
are uniquely determined. The next lemma follows immediately.

Lemma 4
.

For
\mathcal{S}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
and
x\in C
for some full-dimensional cell of
\mathrm{BCD}(\mathcal{S})
,

\nabla f_{\mathcal{S}}(x)=\boldsymbol{0}\iff\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})=C.

Proof.

Proof of
Lemma

4
.
For sufficiency, suppose
\nabla f_{\mathcal{S}}(x)=\boldsymbol{0}
. By
Lemma

2
,
f_{\mathcal{S}}(x)
is convex, and so any local minima is a global optimum. Thus,
x\in\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
. Then by the result of
Theorem

2
,
C\subseteq\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
. For necessity, let
x\in\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
. Then
x
is optimal in
(\mathrm{P1})
. Strong duality then requires balance of flow be achieved across each node
D
in
\mathcal{G}
. This is equivalent to
\nabla f_{\mathcal{S}}(x)=\mathbf{0}
.
∎

Lemma

4
does not apply to points on the boundary of
\mathrm{BCD}(\mathcal{S})
since the gradient is not defined there. Indeed, trying to apply
Eq.

26
to lower-dimensional cells would result in double-counting elements of
\delta
in
Eq.

27
. There is, however, an interesting exception when considering samples for which all points are in
general position
, that is, the matrix
V
has no
{k}{\mkern-1.0mu\times\mkern-1.0mu}{k}
-submatrix which is tropically singular for
2\leq k\leq\min(n,d)
. In these instances, fine-types at a point
v_{i}\in V
will have a sum of one in every row (column) of
T_{V}(p_{i})
(
\overline{T}_{V}(p_{i})
) except for the
{i}^{\mathrm{th}}
one, which will have ones in every entry. We see then that
\nabla f_{V\setminus v_{i}}(v_{i})
is well-defined and that if the gradient of this subset equals zero, then zero must exist in the subdifferential when considering the entire set. That is,
\nabla f_{V\setminus v_{i}}(v_{i})=\boldsymbol{0}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{0}\in\partial f_{V}(p_{i})
. This observation allows us to leverage a slightly modified version of
Eq.

27
when computing gradients at points within the sample itself. It is worth noting that if a point
v_{i}\in V
is a tropical Fermat-Weber point, then by Lemma 8 in
Lin2016TropicalFP

\mathrm{FW}(V)=v_{i}
, i.e., the point
v_{i}
is the unique point in the set. In any event, we still require a method to determine Fermat-Weber membership at points along hyperplane boundaries. Therefore, in the next section we develop the notion of subgradients for points on the boundary of
\mathrm{BCD}(\mathcal{S})
.

4.1
Tropical Fermat-Weber Subgradients

Recall that a subgradient of
f
at a point
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a vector
g\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

f(x)\geq f(x_{0})+\langle g,x-x_{0}\rangle\quad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(30)

The set of all such vectors at a point is called the
subdifferential
of
f
at
x_{0}
. We first define the tropical Fermat-Weber subdifferential as a set and then show how any vector satisfying the subgradient condition
Eq.

30
must be contained in the cone generated by this set.

Definition 19

(Tropical Fermat-Weber Subdifferential)
.

Given a set of points
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
for
i\in[n]
, the tropical Fermat-Weber subdifferential of a point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
is the cone generated by the tropical Fermat-Weber gradients of all maximal covector cells in an open neighborhood of
x
.

Lemma 5
.

Any tropical Fermat-Weber subgradient is an element of the subdifferential of
f_{V}(x)
.

Proof.

Proof of
Lemma

5
.
Let
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
and
v_{i}\in V
such that the maximum of
x-v_{i}
achieved at least twice and/or the minimum of
x-v_{i}
achieved at least twice. Let
J^{*}
denote the set of indices that achieve the maximum, and
K^{*}
the set that achieves the minimum. Note that it is possible that
J^{*}=K^{*}=[d]
if
x=v_{i}
. For any selection
(j,k\mid j\in J,k\in K)
with
j\neq k
(recall this is not a restriction), take
e_{j}-e_{k}
as the corresponding unit-vector direction. With this selection of
j
and
k
the gradient in
Eq.

26
becomes well-defined, and we denote it as
g_{jk}
. Now, consider some small
\epsilon>0
. We perturb the point
x
according to
\tilde{x}=x+(e_{j}-e_{k})\epsilon
and note that

f_{V}(\tilde{x})\geq f_{V}(x)+\langle g_{jk},\tilde{x}-x\rangle,

(31)

which satisfies the supporting hyperplane condition. Note that if
\tilde{x}
is in the interior of a cell of maximal dimension, then by construction
\nabla f_{V}(\tilde{x})=g_{jk}
. Since
j,k
were arbitrary, this condition holds over all
j\neq k
, meaning that any gradient of a maximal cell in a neighborhood of
x
will also satisfy the supporting hyperplane condition. That is to say, any subgradient of
Definition

19
satisfies this condition.
∎

Lemma 6
.

x\in\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})\iff\mathbf{0}\in\partial f_{\mathcal{S}}(x)
.

Proof.

Proof of
Lemma

6
.
Necessity is a result of
Lemma

5
and
Lemma

4
. Sufficiency is an immediate consequence of
Lemma

2
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

For

,

Proof.

Proof of

.
For any lower-dimensional cell

is the maximal cell with this property, and uniqueness is provided by

.
∎

4.2
A Tropical Fermat-Weber Gradient Descent Algorithm

Let

,

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathrm{mf}
denote the max-flow problem over

,
\rho^{*}
be its optimal solution, and
\mathcal{M}^{*}
be its optimal value. Then
x\in\mathrm{FW}(V)\iff\mathcal{M}^{*}=n

Proof.

Proof of
Proposition

3
.
By construction of
\Gamma(x)
, the only way in which
\mathcal{M}^{*}=n
is if
\rho^{*}
is a feasible flow in
(\mathrm{D2})
. Let
y_{i}=x_{j}-v_{ij}
such that
\rho^{*}_{ij}=1
, and similarly let
z_{i}=x_{j}-v_{ij}
such that
\rho^{*}_{ji}=1
. Then

\displaystyle\langle-\mathbf{\mathbf{1}},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle+\langle\mathbf{\mathbf{1}},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j:\rho^{*}_{ij}=1}v_{ij}-x_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j:\rho^{*}_{ji}=1}x_{j}-v_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i,j)\in\Gamma(x)}v_{ij}\rho^{*}_{ij}-\sum_{(j,i)\in\Gamma(x)}v_{ij}\rho^{*}_{ji}

\displaystyle=w(\rho^{*}).

Equality in the second line follows because of balance of flow across

y_{i},\,\ z_{i}

(x,y,z)

are optimal. Alternatively, see Theorem 9.7 in

∎

Proof of

5
Discussion

In this paper we show that the set of all tropical Fermat-Weber points with respect to
d_{\mathrm{tr}}
forms a polytrope, a bounded cell of the union of max-plus and min-plus covector decompositions generated by max- and min-plus tropical hyperplane arrangements defined by the set of tropical hyperplanes with apices
\mathcal{S}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
. This cell
C
, which may not be full-dimensional, has as its external description a set of
n
half-sectors (one for each point in
\mathcal{S}
) which are solutions to a
\mathrm{mcf}
problem. Consequently, by running a shortest path algorithm over the residual graph, we obtain the tropical vertices of
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})
. Additionally, we explicitly defined the tropical Fermat-Weber gradient and subdifferential, leveraging the close connection of the
\mathrm{mf}
and
\mathrm{mcf}
problems to developed a gradient descent algorithm that requires significantly less overhead memory requirements than the optimization formulation originally proposed in
Lin2016TropicalFP
.

Throughout we only considered samples with uniform weighting on the points, which is to say we considered an
unweighted
Fermat-Weber calculation. Recently
cox2023
studied positively-weighted tropical Fermat-Weber points using the
asymmetric tropical metric
, showing that the set of all weighted tropical Fermat-Weber points with respect to the asymmetric tropical metric forms a tropical polytope. Future research should investigate the geometry of the set of all positively-weighted tropical Fermat-Weber points with respect to the symmetric tropical metric
d_{\rm tr}
, namely:
a weighted tropical Fermat-Weber point of a sample
\mathcal{S}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}
defined as

x^{*}=\argmin_{x}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}{d}_{\rm tr}(x,p_{i}),

where
0\leq\lambda_{i}\leq 1
for
i=1,\ldots n
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1
. One can easily show that the set of all weighted tropical Fermat-Weber points with respect to the symmetric tropical metric
d_{\rm tr}
of a given sample
\mathcal{S}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
forms a classical convex polytope in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
by
Lemma

2
.
Here we have the following conjecture:

Conjecture 1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
. Then the set

\mathrm{FW}_{W}(\mathcal{S}):=\left\{x^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}\middle|\ x^{*}=\argmin_{x}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}{d}_{\rm tr}(x,p_{i}),0\leq\lambda_{i},\mbox{ for }i\in[n],\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1\right\}

forms a polytrope in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
.

Given the interest in applying symmetric Fermat-Weber points to various areas in phylogenetics, it is natural to consider how methods proposed here might apply to datasets possessing a particular structure, e.g., that of coalescent trees. Such trees can be defined on a finite metric space with
[m]:=\{1,\ldots,m\}
and represented by a
nonnegative symmetric
{m}{\mkern-1.0mu\times\mkern-1.0mu}{m}
-matrix
D=(d_{ij})
with zero entries on the diagonal such that all triangle inequalities are satisfied:

d_{ik}\leq d_{ij}+d_{jk}\quad\hbox{for all}\,\,\,i,j,k\,\,\,\hbox{in}\,\,\,[m]:=\{1,2,\ldots,m\}.

If we consider strengthening these inequalities to:

d_{ik}\leq{\rm max}(d_{ij},d_{jk})\quad\hbox{for all}\,\,\,i,j,k\in[m],

then the metric
D
is called an
ultrametric
. The set of all ultrametrics contains the
ray
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{1}
spanned by the all-one metric
\mathbf{1}
, which is defined by
d_{ij}=1
for
1\leq i<j\leq m
. The image of the set of ultrametrics, denoted as
\mathcal{U}_{m}
, in the quotient space
{\mathbb{R}}^{\binom{m}{2}}/{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}
is called the
space of ultrametrics
. It is well known that
\mathcal{U}_{m}

is the support of a pointed simplicial fan of dimension
m-2

(
AK
;
MS
;
GD
;
SS
)
.

LSTY
showed that even if
\mathcal{S}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{U}_{m}
,
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})\not\subset\mathcal{U}_{m}
.
Aliatimis2024
showed that a Fermat-Weber point in
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{U}_{m}
is an estimated species tree from the set of gene trees
\mathcal{S}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{U}_{m}
under their proposed model with the tropical metric. Therefore it is of interest to study the geometry and combinatorics of
\mathrm{FW}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{U}_{m}
.
