On the sum of character codegrees of finite groups
Mark L. Lewis, Quanfu Yan

Abstract
. Let χ \chi be an irreducible character of a group G . G. We denote the sum of the codegrees of the irreducible characters of G G by S c ​ ( G ) = ∑ χ ∈ Irr ⁡ ( G ) cod ⁡ ( χ ) . 1
Introduction

All groups considered in this paper are finite. Let
G
be a finite group and
C_{n}
be a cyclic group of order
n
. We write
{\rm Irr}(G)
to denote the set of complex
irreducible characters of
G
. For

\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G),
define the number

{\rm cod}(\chi)=\frac{|G:\ker\chi|}{\chi(1)}

to be the
codegree
of
\chi.
This definition of codegree was first introduced by Qian, Wang and Wei in
[
11
]
. To see a few of the papers where codegree has been studied, consult
[
9
,
12
,
13
,
2
]
.

Recently, a number of papers have considered the relationship between the sum of degrees (denoted by
T(G)
) of the irreducible characters of a group
G
and the structure of that group. For example, it was proved in
[
10
]
by Isaacs, Loukaki and Moreto that if
T(G)\leqslant 3k(G)
,
T(G)<\frac{3}{2}k(G)
or
T(G)<\frac{4}{3}k(G),
then
G
is respectively solvable, supersolvable or nilpotent, where
k(G)
denotes the number of conjugacy classes of
G.
Motivated by these papers, we consider the sum of the codegrees and want to see what can be sold regarding the sum.
In this paper, we denote the sum of codegrees of irreducible characters of
G
by

S_{c}(G)=\sum_{\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)}{\rm cod}(\chi).

For an abelian group
G
of order
n
, it is well known that the set
{\rm Irr}(G)
forms an abelian group and
G\cong{\rm Irr}(G)
and the codegree of
\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)
is the order of
\chi
in the group
{\rm Irr}(G).
It follows that
S_{c}(G)
is equal to the sum of element orders of
G.
In 2009, Amiri, Jafarian Amiri and Isaacs proved in
[
1
]
that for any noncyclic group
H
, the sum of element orders of
H
is strictly less than the sum of element orders of the cyclic group of order
|H|
. Hence, it follows that when
G
is an abelian group of order
n
, then
S_{c}(G)\leqslant S_{c}(C_{n})
with equality if and only if
G
is cyclic.

Inspired by the idea of the result in
[
1
]
and the above observation, we consider the following question:

Question A
.

Let
G
be a finite group of order
n
. Is it true that
S_{c}(G)\leqslant S_{c}(C_{n}),
where
C_{n}
is a cyclic group of order
n?

We know the question is true for abelian groups based on the result of Amiri, Jafarian Amiri and Isaacs in
[
1
]
. Therefore, the next natural class of groups to look at is nilpotent groups. Perhaps not surprisingly, we can answer the questions for nilpotent groups.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
G
is a nilpotent group of order
n
, then
S_{c}(G)\leqslant S_{c}(C_{n})
with equality if and only if
G
is cyclic.

For metacyclic groups that are semidirect products of coprime orders and nonabelian, we get a stronger result as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G=C_{n}\rtimes C_{m}
be a semidirect product of two cyclic groups
C_{n}
and
C_{m},
where
(n,m)=1
and
C_{m}
is not normal in
G.
Then
S_{c}(G)<\frac{8}{21}S_{c}(C_{nm}).

In fact, if for the largest prime, say
p
, dividing the order of
G,
a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
is either not normal or nonabelian, we can get the result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
G
be a group of order
n
and
P
a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
, where
p
is the largest prime divisor of the order of
G.
If either
P
is not normal in
G
or
P
is nonabelian, then
S_{c}(G)\leqslant S_{c}(C_{n}).

Next we discuss how the number of prime divisor of the order of
G
affects the sum of codegrees. Amazingly, we can show that for groups whose order is divisible by up to
t=99
distinct primes, the question has a positive answer.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G
be a group of order
n
with
|\pi(G)|=t\leqslant 99,
where
\pi(G)
denotes the set of all primes dividing the order of
G.
Then
S_{c}(G)\leqslant S_{c}(C_{n}).

With the above results, one is led to believe that the question will have a positive answer for all finite groups. However, by utilizing the properties of the Euler product and the Riemann zeta function, we construct a family of examples indicating the question will not always have a positive answer. Thus, we have the following.

Theorem 1.5
.

There is an integer
n
and a group
G
of order
n
so that
S_{c}(G)>S_{c}(C_{n}).

In Section 2, we present some basic properties of
S_{c}(G)
. In section 3, we discuss about several classes of groups where the question can be answered positively. We will present the counterexamples in Section 4.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we present some results from the literature and prove some preliminary lemmas. When working with irreducible characters, one needs to understand the conjugacy classes of the group. In particular, we here focus on the number of conjugacy classes and give the following lemmas.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a non-abelian
p
-group and
k(G)
be the number of conjugacy classes of
G
.
Then
k(G)<\frac{p+1}{p^{2}}|G|
.

[
Proof]Let

be the maximal order of
{\rm C}_{G}(x)
for

.
By the class equation for

, we have that

|G|\geqslant|Z(G)|+(k(G)-|Z(G)|)\frac{|G|}{m}

and then

k(G)\leqslant m+|Z(G)|-\frac{m|Z(G)|}{|G|}<m+|Z(G)|=|G|(\frac{m}{|G|}+\frac{|Z(G)|}{|G|}).

Observe that
|G/Z(G)|\geqslant p^{2}
and
\frac{m}{|G|}\leqslant\frac{1}{p}
as
G
is nonabelian.
It follows that
k(G)<\frac{p+1}{p^{2}}|G|
.

We also need the following observations of Guralnick and Robinson from
[
4
]

Lemma 2.2

(See
[

Let

(2)

where

If
P
is not normal in
G,
then
\frac{k(G)}{|G|}\leqslant\frac{1}{p}.

Now we prove the following lemma, which plays an important role while considering nilpotent groups.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let

be a direct product of

S_{c}(G)\leqslant S_{c}(H)\cdot S_{c}(K)
. Furthermore, the equality holds if
(|H|,|K|)=1

[
Proof]

Let
\chi\in{\rm Irr}(H)
and
\psi\in{\rm Irr}(K)

\ker\chi\times\ker\psi\leqslant\ker(\chi\times\psi)

{\rm cod}(\chi\times\psi)\leqslant{\rm cod}(\chi)\cdot{\rm cod}(\psi).
By
[
7
, Theorem 4.21]
, the characters
\chi\times\psi

\chi\in{\rm Irr}(H)
and
\psi\in{\rm Irr}(K)
are exactly the irreducible characters of
G
. Hence
S_{c}(G)\leqslant S_{c}(H)\cdot S_{c}(K).

Now assume
(|H|,|K|)=1
. Let

for some

and
k\in K.
Then

\chi\times\psi(hk)=\chi(h)\psi(k)=\chi(1)\psi(1).

It follows that
h\in{Z}(\chi)
and
k\in{Z}(\psi)
.
Let
\chi_{{Z}(\chi)}=\chi(1)\lambda
and
\psi_{Z(\psi)}=\psi(1)\mu
,
where
\lambda
and
\mu
are linear characters of
Z(\chi)
and
Z(\psi)
, respectively.
So
\lambda(h)=\mu(k)^{-1}
. Let
o(g)
denote its order for an element
g\in G.
If either
\lambda(h)\neq 1
or
\mu(k)\neq 1
, then
o(h)=o(k)
, a contradiction with
(|H|,|K|)=1
.
It follows that
\lambda(h)=\mu(k)=1
and therefore
h\in\ker{\chi}
and
k\in\ker\psi
. Hence,
\ker\chi\times\ker\psi=\ker(\chi\times\psi)
, which gives that

{\rm cod}(\chi\times\psi)=\frac{|H\times K:\ker(\chi\times\psi)|}{\chi\times\psi(1)}={\rm cod}(\chi)\cdot{\rm cod}(\psi).

Thus
S_{c}(G)=S_{c}(H)\cdot S_{c}(K)
.

The hypothesis
(|H|,|K|)=1
cannot be dropped from the second statement of Lemma

. For example,
S_{c}(C_{2}\times C_{2})=7
and
S_{c}(C_{2})\cdot S_{c}(C_{2})=9>7
. We next compute the sum of codegrees of cyclic groups.

.

(1)

(4)

[

.

\displaystyle S_{c}(C_{p^{a}})+(k(G)-|G:G^{\prime}|)p^{n-1}

4
A family of examples to Question A.

In this section, we begin with some well-known concepts and results. Let
\mathbb{P}
be the set of all primes. We write
\zeta(s)
to denote the Riemann zeta function, which is defined as
\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}
for Re
(s)>1.
The following are some basic properties of several Euler products attached to

Theorem 280 in
[
5
]
shows that if

then

is convergent and

\zeta(s)=\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}}\frac{1}{1-p^{-s}}.

It follows immediately from
\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}}(1+\frac{1}{p^{s}})\cdot\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}}(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}})=\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}}(1-p^{-2s})
that

\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}}(1+\frac{1}{p^{s}})=\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}.

The construction of examples also relies on the Dirichlet’s theorem on arithmetic progressions (see
[
5
, Theorem 15]
), which states that: If

and

are positive integers and
(a,b)=1
, then there are infinitely many primes in the arithmetic progression
\{ak+b|k\ {\rm is\ an\ integer}\}
and the sum of the reciprocals of the prime numbers in the progression diverges. In other words,

\sum_{p\equiv b({\rm mod}\ a)}\frac{1}{p}=\infty.

In addition, to demonstrate the existence of the group

we construct, we need a short fact: A semidirect product
C_{p}^{n}\rtimes C_{q}
exists for distinct primes

and

if and only if

for some

Now we have all components to construct examples. To enhance readability, we present the family of examples as follows.
