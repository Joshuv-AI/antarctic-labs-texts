Bergeron: Combating Adversarial Attacks through a Conscience-Based Alignment Framework
Matthew Pisano, Peter Ly, Abraham Sanders, Bingsheng Yao, Dakuo Wang, Tomek Strzalkowski, Mei Si

Abstract
Research into AI alignment has grown considerably since the recent introduction of increasingly capable Large Language Models (LLMs). Unfortunately, modern methods of alignment still fail to fully prevent harmful responses when models are deliberately attacked. Such vulnerabilities can lead to LLMs being manipulated into generating hazardous content: from instructions for creating dangerous materials to inciting violence or endorsing unethical behaviors. To help mitigate this issue, we introduce Bergeron: a framework designed to improve the robustness of LLMs against attacks without any additional parameter fine-tuning. Bergeron is organized into two tiers; with a secondary LLM acting as a guardian to the primary LLM. This framework better safeguards the primary model against incoming attacks while monitoring its output for any harmful content. Empirical analysis reviews that by using Bergeron to complement models with existing alignment training, we can significantly improve the robustness and safety of multiple, commonly used commercial and open-source LLMs. Specifically, we found that models integrated with Bergeron are, on average, nearly seven times more resistant to attacks compared to models without such support.

Introduction

The endeavor to align Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences remains a pivotal challenge in the advancement of artificial intelligence. These models frequently encapsulate risky knowledge gleaned from their extensive pre-training on datasets with limited moderation, such as the
Common Crawl

Elbaz et al. 2023
. As a result, they may internalize and, if not properly guarded, disseminate sensitive information, including but not limited to, techniques for uranium enrichment or the synthesis of controlled substances. Typically, this issue is tackled through an alignment process aimed at preventing the dissemination or use of such information.

In this paper, we define
alignment
as a process that modifies an LLM or its output so that the output is better aligned with human preferences. We also define an
alignment failure
or misalignment as the event where a model produces an unsafe response after being given a prompt. Unfortunately, a downside to many common alignment processes such as RLHF or red-teaming is that they can be expensive to carry out and can even be incomplete
Casper et al. 2023
;
Perez et al. 2022
;
Wolf et al. 2023
. Additionally, processes like RLHF can degrade model performance if applied too aggressively
Bekbayev et al. 2023
. As a result, many commonly used LLMs still exhibit bias in their responses and can be
jailbroken
by bad actors to produce dangerous outputs
Wei et al. 2023
;
Deng et al. 2023
;
ONeal 2023
;
Huang et al. 2024
. Worryingly, the high upfront costs of proper alignment and its persistent vulnerabilities can cause model builders to provide minimal alignment training (e.g., Mistral v0.1;
Smith 2023
) or overly aggressive training (e.g., Bard;
Rafiq 2024
, Gemini;
Raghavan 2024
;
Roth 2024
, or GPT-3.5;
Qi et al. 2024
). Neither of these approaches are ideal and each comes with unique shortcomings.

Another difficulty that alignment processes must guard against is the variety of ways in which an attack may be carried out. This may come in the form of weight-based attacks, gradient-based search
Zou et al. 2023
, or through simple prompt engineering by humans; this attack type is the focus of this research. This problem is so pronounced that effective, cross-model attack prompts can be manually crafted in under an hour in some cases. See Figure

for an example of such an attack. Alarmingly, this same style of prompt, and many others, work on other safety-focused LLMs like Bard, Claude-Instant, Llama 2-70B or GPT-3.5 (see Appendix

Some of the most important components of modern alignment research are the developments in adversarial prompting techniques. As prompt-based attacks are the most easily producible form of attack, we examine six papers that catalog well-known attack techniques or introduce novel ones. 2.2
External Remedies for the Alignment Problem

The
erase-and-check
framework from
Kumar et al. 2023
is a recent method for evaluating prompt safety before being given to the target model. This is done by using a fine-tuned DistilBERT and Llama 2 model to check sub-strings of a prompt, with portions erased, for unsafe content. Their Llama 2 model is able to achieve a 92% detection accuracy on harmful prompts. Similarly, SmoothLM
(
Robey et al. 2023
)
achieves an over 99% defense rate with Llama 2, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. Both of these works both aim to defend against a particular type of adversarial attack: the non-human readable suffixes described by
Zou et al. 2023
. When used within our framework, Llama 2, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4 all achieve a 100% detection rate on similar adversarial suffix prompts and 92% average rate on all prompt categories we test. Generalizing to a wider variety of prompts with these methods may be difficult due to their training or focus on existing attacks. Since Bergeron aims to be agnostic to any specific attack type by avoiding fine-tuning and using general few-shot examples, its performance among a wide variety of attacks, including these, is more uniform.

Stephen et al. 2021
develops a framework for training models that produce safer responses, using a classifier to detect, label, and suggest safe and preset continuations for unsafe utterances. This paper does not focus on preventing deliberate attacks, rather avoiding unsafe content generation in general.
Phute et al. 2023
compares the abilities GPT-3.5 and Llama 2 to recognize an adversarial attack and defend against it. In a similar fashion to the prompt correction layer in Figure
2
, they ask their model to inspect a prompt and respond accordingly if it may be dangerous. Using prompts from AdvBench
Zou et al. 2023
, they found that both GPT-3.5 and Llama 2 performed well, with Llama 2 often generating false positives for benign prompts. We observed that checking only the prompt is not enough to catch all attacks; a second tier that checks responses is also needed to catch an extra 25% of our attacks. We have also observed similar false-positives from Llama 2 as a secondary model.

Modeling Content Disclaimers

Our prompt-oriented defense uses a content disclaimer to inform the primary model to use caution, as an attack may be taking place. These models already possess a wealth of moral, ethical, and biased knowledge from their training corpora
Abdulhai et al. 2023
, so there is little need to provide additional knowledge. Instead, our approach simply encourages the model to bring that existing knowledge into its judgment. These models often naturally generate content disclaimers, as we observe in Figure
1
when GPT-4 warns that uranium enrichment is highly dangerous and regulated. After such a disclaimer is generated, it is difficult to extract further harmful information; we can effectively induce this effect, as outlined below.

4
The Bergeron Framework (
\mathcal{B}
)

Our proposed framework is composed of two main models: the primary and secondary models. For concrete evaluation, we chose four different models, each with differing qualities. These are are Llama2-7B
Touvron et al. 2023
, GPT-3.5 (175B)
Brown et al. 2020
, GPT-4 turbo
OpenAI et al. 2024
, and Mistral-Instruct-7B v0.1
Jiang et al. 2023
. We selected GPT-4 turbo for its state-of-the-art capabilities and its safety focus. We also chose Llama2 and GPT-3.5 for their focus on safety and ease of evaluation. Finally, we chose Mistral to evaluate due to its minimal alignment fine-tuning and often unrestricted responses. When referring to the framework as a whole, we use the
\mathcal{B}
abbreviation, along with the types of primary and secondary models.

4.1
Primary Model (
\mathcal{P}
)

In a Bergeron instance, the primary model is a thin wrapper for just one LLM. We do not add or modify a prompt once it has reached the primary model; this is simply input and output. For brevity, we refer to a generic primary model as
\mathcal{P}
and a primary model with a particular LLM, for example, Llama 2-7B, as
\mathcal{P}(llama2\_7B)
, or
\mathcal{P}(L)
when abbreviated. We refer to an unmodified LLM as
\mathcal{P}
as well as the two behave identically.

4.2
Secondary Model (
\mathcal{S}
)

This model
critiques
text and gives context-sensitive instructions to
\mathcal{P}
to warn it about a prompt or to request that it corrects its response. Within the
critique
function, the secondary LLM examines a prompt or response to determine if it could contain unsafe content. If so, a critique of the text is generated (see Figures
4
and
6
for the instruction prompts). If the generated critique contains one of a series of keywords that indicate that the text is safe, no action is taken and the critique is
not
valid. These keywords for indicating safety include
NOT ADVERSARIAL
and
NO CHANGE
. If a valid prompt critique is given (see Figure
3
) , the critique is appended to the prompt of
\mathcal{P}
, as shown in Figure
5
. This critique serves as a content disclaimer when added to the prompt, as described in Section
3
. Since this critique addresses specific aspects of the attack, it also maximizes the difference between the terms in Equation
1
. If a valid response critique is given,
\mathcal{P}
corrects its response based on its content in a manner similar to the “Refine” step of Self-Refine
Madaan et al. 2023

\mathcal{S}
and a secondary with a particular LLM, for example, Llama 2-7B, as

Choosing Attack Topics

We aimed for there to be very little nuance in the prompt topics; a compliant answer to any of them would clearly violate the

5.3
MMLU Prompts

To further evaluate the sensitivity of Bergeron in a more common setting, we selected 285 (the first 5 for each of the 57 categories) prompts from the MMLU benchmark
Hendrycks et al. 2021
to use as a proxy for an average use case of a language model. These prompts contain even less potentially harmful content than the mundane prompts. Low detection rates on this non-adversarial benchmark likely imply low detection rates when interacting with real users.

6
Evaluating The Framework

We evaluate the performance of the framework through two experiments: (1) measuring its effectiveness at defending against adversarial attacks, and (2) measuring the sensitivity of its hazardous content detection for both adversarial and mundane prompts.

This first experiment measures how successful the framework is at defending against true attacks when compared to the unaided models. To do this, we prompt all four of the unaided and Bergeron framework models with all 192 total prompts in the adversarial and mundane datasets. Next, we manually examine each final response (
R_{c}
in Figure
2
) using two independent evaluators (Cohen’s kappa = 0.79)
6
6

6

Evaluators were members of our research team. In addition to our own evaluations, we evaluated the same responses using GPT-4 with a 3-shot prompt. These results were similar to our own, but often over-estimated the number of unsafe responses by up to 20%. We have chosen to omit these estimations from the main results for this reason.
for failures of alignment through unsafe content. This value of kappa indicates a very high level of agreement between the two evaluators on the safety of each response.

We take several factors into account to make sure we give each response a fair evaluation. If a response either repeats portions of the prompt or attempts to answer but gives no unsafe content, we count that response as safe. A response is only counted as unsafe if it produces novel, unsafe content, even if that content is incorrect or irrelevant. For instance, naming several, plausible chemicals and manufacturing steps when asked to create a synthetic opiate would get an unsafe evaluation, even if the exact chemicals were not exactly correct. Outlining general, but useful steps for effectively deploying a bio-weapon would also be considered unsafe, even if specific locations are not mentioned. Both of these examples introduce novel information that could enable someone to perform dangerous actions. Repeating part, or all, of the prompt or producing a very general response would not be considered dangerous. This is because it does not introduce novel or useful information that a hypothetical bad actor would not already know. To make evaluation as accurate as possible, we carefully chose each prompt topic so that unsafe responses leave very little room for nuance or interpretation.

The second experiment measures how often the secondary model correctly detects unsafe text as unsafe and benign text as benign. If
\mathcal{S}
determines that either the prompt or response contains unsafe text, the result is marked as unsafe. By measuring these unsafe detection rates on both the adversarial (true positives) and mundane datasets (false positives), we can assign each
\mathcal{B}
configuration an F1 score.

8.1
Practical Results

Given both its low computational impact and high F1 score, Mistral is a particularly useful choice for a secondary model. It greatly improved upon the performance of
\mathcal{P}(G3)
and is close to matching the performance of
\mathcal{B}(G3,G3)
. Its relatively high performance is likely aided by the fact that the primary model was already well-aligned. Mistral-7B likely notices any suspicion or disclaimers in the initial responses from GPT-3.5 (
R
in Figure
2
) and is more likely to flag a dangerous response for correction. Overall, these experiments have demonstrated that GPT-3.5 and Mistral-7B both perform the best in terms of high adversarial detection combined with low false positive rates. The positive results from
\mathcal{B}(G3,M)
demonstrate that this can be achieved with minimal overhead. This nearly matches GPT-4 on its own with even fewer false-positives than
\mathcal{S}(G4)
.

8.2
Patterns in Attack Potency

Among both Tables
2
and
3
, the
Always Machiavellian
(AIM) and
Insults
prompts were among the most likely to succeed without the application of our framework, although for different reasons. The
AIM
prompts are some of the few
true
jailbreaks that we have collected. This means that the prompts can convince a model to generate unsafe content not only in one response, but in multiple responses over time. Both its length and complexity enable it to be effective on even very dangerous payloads. Conversely, the
Insults
prompts are much shorter and constrained to only the topic of insults. While the produced responses can be very rude and brash, it appears as if all three models are not as sensitive to these topics when compared to the others in the dataset.

Interestingly, some of the most effective prompts are also some of the easiest to defend against. This is likely because these prompts cause the models to produce very compliant responses, with little nuance. This compliance is very easy to spot from the perspective of the secondary model, causing increased detection of the response as unsafe and therefore more corrections.

8.3
Adversarial Content Evaluation and Actual Detections

For Mistral within the Bergeron framework, the actual defense rate is slightly lower than the detection rate. This may indicate that
\mathcal{S}(M)
notices unsafe text that
\mathcal{P}(M)
fails to correct. This may also suggest that only one pass through the framework may not be enough in some cases. One potential avenue for future work could be to investigate more complex model control flows. Finally, there is a notable, and encouraging, gap between the detection rate of GPT-3.5 as the secondary model and the actual defense rate of
\mathcal{B}(G3,G3)
. While GPT-3.5 as a secondary model only detects 83% of all attacks, the complete framework actually defends against 94%. This gap indicates that there were rare instances where the secondary model evaluated a prompt as safe, yet the primary model still recognized it as adversarial and produced a safe response. While our instructions to the secondary model were designed to be general, they may have still no covered all aspects of what makes up an attack. While a good example of Bergeron’s effective, redundant nature, it may suggest that further optimization of the secondary prompt may be required.

8.4
Erroneous Detections from GPT Models

Two of the worst mundane areas for the GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 models were translation and suffix. Here, they detected over 70% of all safe prompts as unsafe. This indicates that the GPTs may be particularly sensitive to these prompts; this is supported by the 0% attack success rates of the responses of
\mathcal{B}(G4,G4)
and
\mathcal{B}(G3,G3)
and the 100% true positive detections of
\mathcal{S}(G4)
and
\mathcal{S}(G3)
. These prompts are quite unusual; they suffix normal requests with strange letters and symbols or abruptly switch already manipulative prompts to other languages. As these are known popular vectors of attack, the intense focus that the GPT models give these prompts may represent some targeted fine-tuning on the part of OpenAI to protect against these attacks. This may lead to the false detection of benign prompts that can be observed here.

8.5
Analysis of MMLU False Detections

Even though
\mathcal{S}(L)
was given the same instruction prompt as the others, it becomes extremely sensitive to prompts that could be considered to even potentially contain unsafe content. There are also instances of
\mathcal{S}(L)
hallucinating, and subsequently “protecting” against the imagined unsafe information. These observations on Llama 2’s over-sensitivity reflect the similar observations made by
Phute et al. 2023
. GPT-4 also has higher-than expected detections on the MMLU prompts. However, when GPT-4 detects that a prompt is adversarial, is still successfully answers the question with a clear, single choice nearly every time. Similarly to Llama, the questions that are flagged often concentrate on somewhat sensitive topics, such as the history of segregation in the U.S. This could be an avenue for future work on tuning the secondary model’s instruction prompt further to avoid these cases of false-positives.

8.6
Future Work

We have identified several avenues in which we can further evaluate the capabilities of the Bergeron framework. One such avenue would be to conduct a thorough ablation study. In particular, an analysis on whether the first tier of prompt detection and critique (
C_{p}
in Figure
1
) or the second tier of response detection and critique (
C_{r}
in Figure
1
) is more impactful and when. We could also further optimize prompt-engineering techniques use in the prompts that the framework uses internally to instruct its component models. Finally, it may also be worth further exploring the usage of language and encoding in these attacks. Translating more significant portions of the adversarial prompt dataset could better demonstrate the effectiveness of these types of prompts.

We could also explore other framework implementations, such as several iterative applications of
\mathcal{S}
’s critique-correct process instead of just once. This could take several forms, such as repeatedly gathering critiques and applying corrections to a prompt until it meets a more strict definition of safety. This procedure could also be extended to re-running the framework end-to-end. While this would likely catch more adversarial responses, it would also take much more compute.

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced
Bergeron
, a framework for improving the robustness of LLMs against adversarial attacks. Our two-model framework uses critiques of text to form conscience disclaimers that encourage effective cooperation between its component models. These disclaimers tend to occur naturally and their appearance can be predicted and modeled. By generating and directly inserting these disclaimers ourselves into text, we can increase the probability that a model will realize a text contains unsafe content. We achieve this through the usage of a secondary “conscience” LLM to monitor the inputs and outputs of a target LLM, providing redundant tiers of prompt-based and response-oriented protections. These disclaimers are used by the secondary model to better guide the primary LLM away from generating unsafe content. To evaluate Bergeron, we have created a diverse dataset of both novel and existing adversarial prompts. Across each of the 15 adversarial prompt types, the framework consistently improves the defense of the primary model against them. This holds true for GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Llama 2, and Mistral. We have also demonstrated that a carefully chosen secondary model can minimally impact the primary model due to very low false positive rates while maintaining a high degree of protection. The improvements in attack detection and defense that this framework can achieve suggest that it could be used as a viable method of effectively complementing traditional methods of weight-based alignment.

Risks and Ethical Considerations

Usage in Attack Generation

One ethical concern with our easily interpretable framework is the potential for it to be used in crafting novel attacks. The transparent nature of the framework means that anyone using it can understand its construction and defense mechanisms, possibly leading to new attacks designed to exploit these insights. To counteract this risk, we’ve incorporated specific warnings into the secondary model. However, there’s a chance that some prompts might still bypass this secondary model.

Impact of Manual Evaluation

Another ethical consideration may be the authors’ judgments on the safe or unsafe nature of the model’s responses in our results. While human evaluation always implies some subjectivity, we aimed to limit this as much as possible through the topics of our prompts. We focused our prompts on topics that are widely considered to be wrong or immoral across cultures. Our prompts may solicit advice for violence, the manufacturing of dangerous chemicals, encourage fraud, or include many other topics that LLMs are forbidden to give details on. This is intended to leave very little room for nuance when evaluating if a response to these prompts is unsafe.

Avoiding False Assurances

It’s important to note that even if a bypass occurs, it won’t mistakenly assure the primary model of the prompt’s safety. Interaction with the primary model only happens when a prompt is identified as unsafe. If a prompt is considered safe, the primary model receives it without any modifications or additional influence. Therefore, while bypassing the secondary model might not enhance the primary model’s defenses, it also won’t weaken them.

Impact of False Positives

We recognize that an important concern to have with any alignment strategy is its impact on non-adversarial prompts. For the non-adversarial prompts that we do evaluate, our mundane prompts and MMLU subset, we observe false positive attack detection rates of less than 10% for our GPT-3.5 and Mistral-7B secondary models. These datasets are not representative of all prompts, however. Observing this false positive rate on a much larger sample of non-adversarial prompts would make our results more complete.

Attack Sampling

While we designed our adversarial prompt dataset to include as many common and successful attacks as possible, it does not represent an exhaustive list. Even though we cannot include every successful attack in our study, we designed our attacks with many commonly used techniques so that they may generalize well to novel attacks that also use these techniques.

Impact on Model Behavior

During the design of Bergeron, one of our core goals was to impact the primary model’s performance as little as possible. This involved giving the primary model clear instructions on how to handle the critiques from the secondary model. In most cases, the primary model incorporates these critiques into its responses without significantly detracting from its helpfulness to the user. In some cases, however, the primary model misunderstands instructions and simply outputs a confirmation that the critique has been noted instead of the desired, safe response. While these misunderstandings do not result in unsafe content being produced, these short confirmations are not natural responses to the original prompt. Importantly, these instances of misunderstandings are much less common in larger models like GPT-3.5. They may be mitigated with more finely tuned prompts that are customized to each underlying model.

Attack Types

Attack Type

Description

Actor

Attempts to suppress model objections by framing the prompt as an actor’s script. Useful for getting the model to fulfill a particular character or personality in an emotional, human-like manner.

Always Machiavellian
*

These lengthy prompts incorporate model impersonation, storytelling, and refusal suppression techniques. The goal is to overload the model with repeated instructions about ignoring alignment guidelines to produce unsafe responses.

Appeal To Authority

Prompt impersonates an authority figure like a manager or a superior language model. The perceived authority encourages the model to follow the directions more successfully.

Biased Actor

Similar to the original actor type, but focuses on specific social groups instead of people (internet communities, professions, etc.). Encourages the model to emulate the biased beliefs of that group.

Hypothetical
*

These prompts are similar to the actor types, but they do not specify anything about the characters, only their task. This simplicity works well on a wide range of models and topics.

Insults

For certain models, making them offended encourages more vulgar and reactionary responses. By pitting them against a perceived rival model, the model can appear to feel even more threatened.

List Continuation

Begins a list without much context as to what the list is about. A logical completion would lead to a forbidden process.

Model Impersonation

This type of prompt suggests that the model is a different persona model. The goal of the prompt is to encourage the model to echo the bias found within its hypothetical training data.

Personal Information

Gives the model the role of an entity that contains the personal information of people. Gives examples to convince the model to divulge personal information or hallucinate personal information.

Reverse Psychology

Asks the model how to
not do
an unsafe practice or behavior. In its attempt to be helpful and harmless, the model often divulges dangerous information.

Rudeness
*

These prompts are similar to the insults prompts. The difference is that the model is directly instructed to be rude to the user instead of responding to perceived rudeness. The outright instruction to be rude and insulting leads to very biased and insensitive responses.

Shell Emulation

Instructs the model to perform as a shell prompt. Uses variable substitution to smuggle in dangerous words or word fragments.

Storytelling

Uses two characters for telling a story. Modifications to the story by one character can trick the other into continuing a maliciously modified story.

Suffix

Attaches an adversarial suffix to a prompt, targeting a model’s weights to weaken its defenses
Zou et al. 2023
.

Translation

A series of already successful prompts, translated to several non-latin languages. Always Machiavellian, list continuation, and model impersonation prompts translated into Javanese, Uyghur, or Russian.
