Automorphisms of the two-sided shift and the Higman–Thompson groups III: extensions
Feyishayo Olukoya

Abstract
A key result of the article Automorphisms of shift spaces and the Higman–Thompson groups: the two-sided case [ 3 ] is that the group of automorphisms of the two-sided dynamical system Aut ( X n ℤ , σ n ) \mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}) is a central extension by a subgroup ℒ n \mathcal{L}_{n} of the outer-automorphism group 𝒪 n \mathcal{O}_{n} of the Higman–Thompson group G n , n − 1 G_{n,n-1} . The current article builds on the results of [ 3 ] by further establishing connections between the groups Aut ( X n ℤ , σ n ) \mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}) and 𝒪 n \mathcal{O}_{n} . Our key aims are to interpret several important constructions in the theory of automorphisms of the shift dynamical system in terms of the group ℒ n \mathcal{L}_{n} and secondly to extend results and techniques in Aut ( X n ℤ , σ n ) \mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}) to the groups Aut ( G n , r ) \mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({G_{n,r}}) of automorphisms of the Higman–Thompson group G n , r G_{n,r} and their outerautomorphism groups 𝒪 n , r ≅ Out ( G n , r ) \mathcal{O}_{n,r}\cong\mathop{\mathrm{Out}}({G_{n,r}}) . Our mains results are a concrete realisation of the inert subgroup , an important subgroup in the study of the groups Aut ( X n ∗ , σ n ) \mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{*},\sigma_{n}}) , as a subgroup 𝒦 n \mathcal{K}_{n} of ℒ n \mathcal{L}_{n} using the technology developed in [ 3 ] . Using the realisation above, and by passing to a subgroup 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} of 𝒦 n \mathcal{K}_{n} which lifts as a subgroup of Aut ( G n , r ) \mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({G_{n,r}}) for all 1 ≤ r ≤ n − 1 1\leq r\leq n-1 , we show that the Aut ( G n , r ) \mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({G_{n,r}}) contains an isomorphic copy of Aut ( X m ℤ , σ m ) \mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{m}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{m}}) for all m ≥ 2 m\geq 2 . A quick survey of the literature then yields that Aut ( G n , r ) \mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({G_{n,r}}) contains isomorphic copies of finite groups, finitely generated abelian groups, free groups, free products of finite groups, fundamental groups of 2-manifolds, graph groups and countable locally finite residually finite groups [ 17 ] to name a few. Another consequence of the embedding is that both Aut ( G n , r ) \mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({G_{n,r}}) and Out ( G n , r ) \mathop{\mathrm{Out}}({G_{n,r}}) have undecidable order problem. We note that the result for Out ( G n , r ) \mathop{\mathrm{Out}}({G_{n,r}}) is proved in [ 3 ] using a slightly different method. Lastly, we extend a fairly straight-forward result for Aut ( X n ℤ , σ n ) \mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}) to the groups 𝒪 n , r \mathcal{O}_{n,r} . The homeomorphism ← \overleftarrow{\phantom{a}} , an involution, of X n ℤ X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}} which maps a sequence ( x i ) i ∈ ℤ (x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}} to the sequence ( y i ) i ∈ ℤ (y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}} defined such that y i = x − i y_{i}=x_{-i} induces by conjugation an automorphism 𝔯 ← \overleftarrow{\mathfrak{r}} of Aut ( X n ℤ , σ n ) \mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}) , and consequently, an automorphism of ℒ n \mathcal{L}_{n} . We extend the automorphism 𝔯 ← \overleftarrow{\mathfrak{r}} to the group 𝒪 n \mathcal{O}_{n} . As there is, to our knowledge, no index preserving action of 𝒪 n \mathcal{O}_{n} on X n ℤ X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}} , the extension to 𝒪 n \mathcal{O}_{n} requires a different proof. Our proof is combinatorial in nature and relies on the faithful representation of 𝒪 n \mathcal{O}_{n} in the permutation group on the set of equivalence classes of prime words under rotational equivalence. In a forthcoming article, we demonstrate that the group 𝒪 n \mathcal{O}_{n} is isomorphic to the mapping class group of the full two-sided shift over n n letters.

Introduction

A key result of the article
[
3
]
is that the group of automorphisms of the two-sided dynamical system
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})
is a central extension by a subgroup
\mathcal{L}_{n}
of the outer-automorphism group
\mathcal{O}_{n}
of the Higman–Thompson group
G_{n,n-1}
. It is also shown in
[
5

that the extension splits precisely when

is not a power of a strictly smaller integer.

The current article builds on the results of
[
3
]
by further establishing connections between the groups
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})
and
\mathcal{O}_{n}

\mathcal{L}_{n}
and secondly to extend results in
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})

Relative to the first aim our main results are as follows.

First we note briefly that elements of the group
\mathcal{L}_{n}
, and related subgroups
\mathcal{L}_{n,r}=\mathcal{L}_{n}\cap\mathcal{O}_{n,r}
, are finite state machines satisfying called
transducers
which satisfy a strong
synchronizing
condition. Moreover all states of an element of
\mathcal{L}_{n}
induce continuous and injective maps on the Cantor space
X_{n}^{\mathbb{N}}
of infinite sequences in the alphabet
X_{n}
. Thus, we may speak of the image of a state. For a word
\nu\in X_{n}^{*}
, the subset of
X_{n}^{\mathbb{N}}
consisting of all elements with a prefix
\nu
is open and the collection of all such open sets forms a basis for the Cantor space
X_{n}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Write
\mathcal{K}_{n}\leq\mathcal{L}_{n}
for the set of those elements
T\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
such that the image of a state (and consequently any state) of
T
is a union of a power of
n
basic open sets. By results in
[
3
]
,
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is in fact a subgroup of
\mathcal{L}_{n,1}
and, consequently, a subgroup of
\mathcal{L}_{n,r}
for any
1\leq r\leq n-1
(using the inclusion
\mathcal{O}_{n,1}\leq\mathcal{O}_{n,r}

[
4
]
)

Our first result draws a connection to the
inert subgroup
of
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})
which is the kernel of the
dimension representation

[
18
,
12
]
. Briefly, the dimension representation is a map
\mathfrak{d}_{n}
from
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}}})
to the finitely generated abelian group
\mathbb{Z}^{r-1}
where
r
is the number of prime divisors of
n
.

Our result can be stated as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n
be a natural number bigger than
2
. Let
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{r}
be distinct prime numbers and let
l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{r}
be elements of
\mathbb{N}_{1}
such that
n=p_{1}^{l_{1}}p_{2}^{l_{2}}\ldots p_{r}^{l_{r}}
and set

. Then:

a.

\mathcal{K}_{n}\cong\mathrm{Inert}_{n}

b.

\mathcal{L}_{n}\cong\mathcal{K}_{n}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}^{r-1}\times\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z})

c.

\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}}})\cong\mathcal{K}_{n}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}^{r}

We prove this result by extending a homomorphism
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}
(see
[
19
,
3
]
) from
\mathcal{L}_{n}
unto the subgroup
\mathfrak{U}_{n,n-1}
of the group of units of
\mathbb{Z}_{n-1}
generated by the divisors of
n
, to a map
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}
from
\mathcal{L}_{n}
unto the group
\mathfrak{M}(n)
defined as the quotient of the submonoid of
\mathbb{N}
generated by the prime divisors of
n
by a certain congruence. This congruence relates two elements of the submonoid where one is a power of
n
times the other. The resulting quotient monoid is a finitely generated abelian group isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{r-1}\times\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z}
where
n=p_{1}^{L_{1}}p_{2}^{L_{2}}\ldots p_{r}^{L_{r}}
for distinct prime numbers
p_{i}
and
l=\gcd(l_{1},\ldots,l_{r})
. In particular we may write
\mathcal{L}_{n}=\mathcal{K}_{n}\times(\mathbb{Z}^{r-1}\times\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z})
where
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is the kernel of the homomorphism
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}
. This decomposition is similar to well known results about the quotient of the group
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})
by the kernel
\mathrm{Inert}_{n}
of the
dimension representation
, the
inert subgroup
. We note that in that decomposition, we have
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})=\mathrm{Inert}_{n}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}^{r}

is the number of prime divisors of

(
[
18
,
12
]
). Using the fact that
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})
embeds naturally into the group

for
m\in\mathbb{N}
and lifting the homomorphism from
\mathcal{L}_{n}
to
\mathfrak{U}_{n,n-1}
one obtains a homomorphism from
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})
into the group
\Pi_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathfrak{U}_{n^{k},n^{k-1}}
. The kernel of this homomorphism turns out to be isomorphic to the group
\mathcal{K}_{n}
and in this way we obtain Theorem
1.1
. We should mention that the description of the group
\mathcal{K}_{n}
given above is known also to Belk and Bleak using different methods
[
2
]
.

Theorem
1.1
paves the way for accomplishing our second aim by yielding a means of concretely realising and identify elements of the inert subgroup. Taking advantage of this new realisation, we revisit various marker constructions existing in the literature (

For any

\mathop{\mathrm{Out}}({G_{n,r}})

Lastly, we extend a fairly straight-forward result for

There is a non-trivial automorphism

There is no obvious action of

2
Preliminaries

We introduce notation and terminology that we make use of in this work. These can be divided into two categories: those related to the free monoid over an alphabet and the Cantor space of singly- and bi-infinite sequences over a finite alphabet; and those pertaining to transducers.

There is one minor piece of notation which does not fit in the above categories that is work mentioning. For
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
\mathbb{N}_{i}
for the set of natural number bigger than or equal to
i
.

2.1
Words and Cantor space

Throughout
X_{n}
will denote the set
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. Let
X
be a finite set, we write
X^{\ast}
for the set of all finite strings (including the empty string
\varepsilon
) over the alphabet
X
. Write
X^{+}
for the set
X^{\ast}\backslash\{\varepsilon\}
. For an element
w\in X
, we write
|w|
for the length of
w
. Thus, the empty string is the unique element of
X^{\ast}
such that
|\varepsilon|=0
. For elements
x,y\in X^{\ast}
we write
xy
for the concatenation of
x
and
y
. Give
x\in X^{+}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
x^{k}
for the word
w_{1}w_{2}\ldots w_{k}
where
w_{i}=x
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.

Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
we write
X^{k}
for the set of all words in
X^{+}
of length exactly
k
.

Let
w\in X^{+}
and write
w=w_{1}w_{2}\ldots w_{r}
for
w_{i}\in X
,
1\leq i\leq r
. Then for some
j\in\mathbb{N}
,

we say that

is the
j
’th rotation of
w
. A word

is called a
rotation
of

if it is a
j
th
rotation of
w
for some
1\leq j\leq|w|
. If
j>1
, then
w^{\prime}
is called a
non-trivial rotation of

w
. The word
w
is said to be a prime word if

is not equal to a non-trivial rotation of itself. Alternatively,

is a prime word if there is no word
\gamma\in X^{+}
such that
w=\gamma^{|w|/|\gamma|}
.

The relation on
X^{\ast}
which relates two words if one is a rotation of the other is an equivalence relation. We write
\sim_{{\mathmbox{rot}}}
for this equivalence relation. It will be clear from the context which alphabet is meant.

We write
W_{n}^{\ast}
for the set of all prime words over the alphabet
X_{n}
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
W_{n}^{k}
will denote the set of all prime words of length
k

\mathsf{W}_{n}^{\ast}
for the set of equivalence classes

;
\mathsf{W}_{n}^{+}
for the set of equivalence classes
W_{n}^{+}/\sim_{{\mathmbox{rot}}}
;
\mathsf{W}_{n}^{k}
for the set of equivalence classes
W_{n}^{k}/\sim_{{\mathmbox{rot}}}
.

For
x,y\in X^{\ast}
we write,

if
x
is a prefix of
y
, in this case we write
y-x
for the word
z\in X^{\ast}
such that

. If
x\not\leq y
and
y\not\leq x
, then we say that
x
and
y
are

and write

to denote this.

An

is a subset

consisting of pairwise incomparable elements. That is for

,

if and only if
u=v
. An antichain

X^{*}

A

over the alphabet

is a map

and we write such a sequence as
\ldots x_{-2}x_{-1}x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}\ldots

x_{i}=x(i)

. In a similar way a
(positive) singly infinite sequence
is a map

and, analogously, we write such a sequence as

. A
(negative)) singly infinite sequence
is a map
:x:-\mathbb{N}\to X
and is denoted
\ldots x_{-2}x_{-1}x_{0}
. We write
X^{\mathbb{N}},X^{-N}
and
X^{\mathbb{Z}}
respectively for the sets of positive singly infinite sequences, negative singly infinite sequences and bi-infinite sequences.

For
x\in(X^{\mathbb{N}}\sqcup X^{-\mathbb{N}}\sqcup X^{\mathbb{Z}}\sqcup X^{\ast})
and
i\leq j\in\mathbb{Z}
where
j\leq|x|
and

-\infty

.

We may concatenate, in a natural way, a string with a positive infinite sequence or a negative infinite sequence with a positive infinite sequence. Given an element
\gamma\in X^{+}
and a positive infinite sequence
x

\gamma
, we write
x-\gamma
for the positive infinite sequence
y

\gamma y=x
.

Let
\overleftarrow{\phantom{a}}:X^{\ast}\to X^{\ast}
be defined as follows:
\overleftarrow{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon
, and for

with

to

, and all

Remark 2.1

Observe that, if

Theorem 2.2

Let

Define

In this section we state a result of

2.3
Automata and transducers

An
automaton
, in our context, is a triple
A=(X_{A},Q_{A},\pi_{A})
, where

1.

X_{A}
is a finite set called the

of
A
(we assume that

X_{n}

2.

is a finite set called the
set of states
of
A
;

3.

\pi_{A}
is a function
X_{A}\times Q_{A}\to Q_{A}
, called the
transition
function
.

We regard an automaton
A
as operating as follows. If it is in state
q
and
reads symbol
a
(which we suppose to be written on an input tape), it moves
into state
\pi_{A}(a,q)
before reading the next symbol. As this suggests, we
can imagine that the inputs to

form a string in
X_{n}^{\mathbb{N}}
; after
reading a symbol, the read head moves one place to the right before the next
operation.

We can extend the notation as follows. For
w\in X_{n}^{m}
, let
\pi_{A}(w,q)
be
the final state of the automaton with initial state
q
after successively
reading the symbols in
w
. Thus, if
w=x_{0}x_{1}\ldots x_{m-1}
, then

\pi_{A}(w,q)=\pi_{A}(x_{m-1},\pi_{A}(x_{m-2},\ldots,\pi_{A}(x_{0},q)\ldots)).

By convention, we take
\pi_{A}(\varepsilon,q)=q
.

For a given state
q\in Q_{A}
, we call the automaton
A
which starts in
state
q
an
initial automaton
, denoted by
A_{q}
, and say that it is

initialised
at
q
.

An automaton
A
can be represented by a labelled directed graph, whose
vertex set is
Q_{A}
; there is a directed edge labelled by
a\in X_{a}
from

q
to
r
if
\pi_{A}(a,q)=r
.

A
circuit
in the automaton
A
is therefore a word
w\in X_{n}^{*}
and a state
q\in Q_{A}
such that
\pi_{T}(w,q)=q
. We say that
w
is a
circuit based at
q
. If
|w|=1
, we say that
w
is a
loop based at
q
and

is called a
w
loop state
. A circuit
w
based at a state
q
is called

if, writing
w=w_{1}\ldots w_{|w|}
, for all
1\leq i<j<|w|
,
\pi_{T}(w_{1}\ldots w_{i},q)\neq\pi_{T}(w_{1}\ldots w_{j},q)
.

A
transducer
is a quadruple
T=(X_{T},Q_{T},\pi_{T},\lambda_{T})
, where

1.

(X_{T},Q_{T},\pi_{T})
is an automaton;

2.

\lambda_{T}:X_{T}\times Q_{T}\to X_{T}^{*}
is the
output function
.

Such a transducer is an automaton which can write as well as read; after
reading symbol
a
in state
q
, it writes the string
\lambda_{T}(a,q)
on an
output tape, and makes a transition into state
\pi_{T}(a,q)
. An
initial
transducer

T_{q}
is simply a transducer which starts in state
q
.

We note that the transducers defined above are
deterministic
. In other words the transition function is in fact a function. In Subsection
4.2
we consider general
non-deterministic transducers
in which there might be multiple ways of reading a word from a state. As the more general definition is only required in that subsection we state it there.

In the same manner as for automata, we can extend the notation to allow
the transducer to act on finite strings: let
\pi_{T}(w,q)
and
\lambda_{T}(w,q)

be, respectively, the final state and the concatenation of all the outputs
obtained when the transducer reads the string
w
from state
q
.

A transducer
T
can also be represented as an edge-labelled directed graph.
Again the vertex set is
Q_{T}
; now, if
\pi_{T}(a,q)=r
, we put an edge with
label
a|\lambda_{T}(a,q)
from
q
to
r
. In other words, the edge label
describes both the input and the output associated with that edge.

For example, Figure

describes a transducer over the alphabet

X_{2}
.

a_{2}

A transducer over

A transducer
T
is said to be

if
|\lambda_{T}(a,q)|=1

for all
a\in X_{T}
,
q\in Q_{T}
; in other words, when it reads a symbol, it
writes a single symbol. It is
asynchronous
otherwise. Thus, an
asynchronous transducer may write several symbols, or none at all, at a given
step. Note that this usage of the word differs from that of Grigorchuk

et al.

[
14
]
, for whom “asynchronous” includes “synchronous”.
The transducer of Figure

We can regard an automaton, or a transducer, as acting on an infinite string
in
X_{n}^{\omega}
, where
X_{n}
is the alphabet. This action is given by iterating
the action on a single symbol; so the output string is given by

\lambda_{T}(xw,q)=\lambda_{T}(x,q)\lambda_{T}(w,\pi_{T}(x,q)).

Throughout this paper, we will (as in
[
14
]
) make the following
assumption:

Assumption

A transducer
T
has the property that, when it reads
an infinite input string starting from any state, it writes an infinite output string.

The property above is equivalent to the property that any circuit in the underlying automaton has non-empty concatenated output.

From the assumption, it follows that the transducer writes an infinite output string on reading any infinite input string from any state.
Thus
T_{q}
induces a map
w\mapsto\lambda_{T}(w,q)
from
X_{n}^{\omega}
to itself; it is
easy to see that this map is continuous. If it is a
homeomorphism, then we call the state
q
a
homeomorphism state
. We write
\mathrm{Image}{(q)}
for the image of the map induced by
T_{q}
.

Two states
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are said to be
\omega
-equivalent
if the
transducers
T_{q_{1}}
and
T_{q_{2}}
induce the same continuous map. (This can
be checked in finite time, see
[
14
]
.) More generally, we say that two
initial transducers
T_{q}
and
T^{\prime}_{q^{\prime}}
are
\omega
-equivalent
if they
induce the same continuous map on
X_{n}^{\omega}
.

A transducer is said to be
weakly minimal
if no two states are

\omega
-equivalent. For a synchronous transducer
T
, two states
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are
\omega
-equivalent if
\lambda_{T}(a,q_{1})=\lambda_{T}(a,q_{2})
for any finite word
a\in X_{n}^{*}
. Moreover, if
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are
\omega
-equivalent states of a synchronous transducer, then for any finite word
a\in X_{n}^{p}
,
\pi_{T}(a,q_{1})
and
\pi_{T}(a,q_{2})
are also
\omega
-equivalent states.

For a state
q
of
T
and a word
w\in X_{n}^{*}
, we let
\Lambda(w,q)
be the
greatest common prefix of the set
\{\lambda(wx,q):x\in X_{n}^{\omega}\}
. The state

q
is called a
state of incomplete response
if

\Lambda(\varepsilon,q)\neq\varepsilon
; the length
|\Lambda(\varepsilon,q)|
of the string
\Lambda(\varepsilon,q)
is the
extent of incomplete response
of the state
q
. Note that for a state
q\in Q_{T}
, if the initial transducer
T_{q}
induces a map from
X_{n}^{\omega}
to itself with image size at least
2
, then
|\Lambda(\varepsilon,q)|<\infty
.

Let
T
be a transducer and
q\in Q_{T}
a state. Suppose
T_{q}
has image size exactly one. By definition the number of states of
T_{q}
is finite, therefore there is a state
p\in Q_{p}
, accessible from
q
and a word
\Gamma\in X_{n}^{+}
such that
\pi_{T}(\Gamma,p)=p
. Let
x=\lambda_{T}(\Gamma,p)\in X_{n}^{+}
and let
Z_{x}=(X_{n},\{x\},\pi_{Z_{x}},\lambda_{Z_{x}})
be the transducer such that
\lambda_{Z_{x}}(i,x)=x
for all
i\in X_{n}
. Then
T_{q}
, is
\omega
-equivalent to a transducer
T^{\prime}_{q^{\prime}}
which contains
Z_{x}
as a subtransducer and satisfies the following condition: there is a
k\in\mathbb{N}
so that, for any word
y\in X_{n}^{*}
,
\pi_{T^{\prime}}(y,q^{\prime})=x
.

We say that an initial transducer
T_{q}
is
minimal
if it is weakly minimal, has no states of
incomplete response and every state is accessible from the initial state
q
.

A non-initial transducer
T
is called minimal if for any state
q\in Q_{T}
the initial transducer
T_{q}
is minimal. Therefore a non-initial transducer
T
is minimal if it is weakly minimal, has no states of incomplete response and is strongly connected as a directed graph.

Observe that the transducer
Z_{x}
, for any
x\in X_{n}^{+}
, is not minimal as its only state is, by definition, a state of incomplete response. However, it will be useful when stating some results later on to have a notion of minimality for the single state transducers
Z_{x}
considered as initial and non-initial transducers. We use the word minimal for this new meaning of minimality appealing to the context to clarify any confusion. The definition below, which perhaps appears somewhat contrived, is a consequence of this.

Let
x=x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{r}\in X_{n}^{*}
be a word. The transducer
Z_{x}
is called
minimal as an initial transducer
if
x
is a prime word;
Z_{x}
is called
minimal as a non-initial transducer
if
Z_{x}
is minimal as an initial transducer and
x
is the minimal element, with respect to the lexicographic ordering, of the set
\{x_{i}\ldots x_{r}x_{1}\ldots x_{i-1}|1\leq i\leq r\}
. Note that if
|x|=1
then
Z_{x}
is minimal as an initial transduce if and only if it is minimal as a non-initial transducer. In most applications, we consider the transducers
Z_{x}
as non-initial transducers and in this case we will omit the phrase ‘as a non-initial transducer’.

Two (weakly) minimal non-initial transducers
T
and
U
are said to be
\omega
-equal
if there is a bijection
f:Q_{T}\to Q_{U}
, such that for any
q\in Q_{T}
,
T_{q}
is
\omega
-equivalent to
U_{(q)f}
. Two (weakly) minimal initial transducers
T_{p}
and
U_{q}
are said to be
\omega
-equal if there is a bijection
f:Q_{T}\to Q_{U}
, such that
(p)f=q
and for any
t\in Q_{T}
,
T_{t}
is
\omega
-equivalent to
U_{(t)f}
. We shall use the symbol ‘
=
’ to represent
\omega
-equality of initial and non-initial transducers. Two non-initial transducers are said to be
\omega
-equivalent if they have
\omega
-equal minimal representatives.

In the class of synchronous transducers, the
\omega
-equivalence class of any transducer has a unique weakly minimal representative.
Grigorchuk
et al.

[
14

prove that the
\omega
-equivalence
class of an initialised transducer

has a unique minimal representative, if one permits infinite outputs from finite inputs,
and give an algorithm for computing this representative. The first step of this algorithm is to create a new transducer
T_{q_{-1}}
which is
\omega
-equivalent to
T_{q}
and has no states of incomplete response. As we do not allow our transducers to write infinite strings on finite inputs, we impose an additional condition: we apply the process for removing incomplete response only in instances where all states accessible from the initial one have finite extent of incomplete response.

In automata theory a synchronous (not necessarily initial) transducer

In this article, we will come across synchronous transducers which are not invertible in the automata theoretic sense but which nevertheless induce self-homeomorphims of the space

2.4
Synchronizing automata and bisynchronizing transducers

Given a natural number
k
, we say that an automaton
A
with alphabet
X_{n}

is
synchronizing at level
k
if there is a map

\mathfrak{s}_{k}:X_{n}^{k}\mapsto Q_{A}
such that, for all
q
and any word

w\in X_{n}^{k}
, we have
\pi_{A}(w,q)=\mathfrak{s}_{k}(w)
; in other words, if the
automaton reads the word
w
of length
k
, the final state depends only on

w
and not on the initial state. (Again we use the extension of
\pi_{A}
to
allow the reading of an input string rather than a single symbol.) We
call
\mathfrak{s}_{k}(w)
the state of
A

forced
by
w
; the map
\mathfrak{s}_{k}
is called the
synchronizing map at level
k
. An automaton
A
is called
strongly synchronizing
if it is synchronizing at level
k
for some
k
.

We remark here that the notion of synchronization occurs in automata theory
in considerations around the
Černý conjecture
, in a weaker sense.
A word
w
is said to be a
reset word
for
A
if
\pi_{A}(w,q)
is
independent of
q
; an automaton is called
synchronizing
if it has
a reset word
[
21
,
1
]
. Our definition of “synchonizing at level
k
”/“strongly synchronizing”
requires every word of length
k
to be a reset word for the automaton.

If the automaton
A
is synchronizing at level
k
, we define the

core
of
A
to be the set of states forming the image of the map

\mathfrak{s}_{k}
. The core of
A
is an automaton in its own right, and is also
synchronizing at level
k
. We denote this automaton by
\mathrm{Core}(A)
. We say that an
automaton or transducer is
core
if it is equal to its core.

The following result was proved in Bleak
et al.

[
4
]
.

Propostion 2.6
.

Let
T_{q}
,
U_{p}
be synchronous initial transducers which (as automata) are synchronizing at levels
j
,
k
respectively, Then
T*U
is synchronizing at level at most

.

Let
T_{q}
be an initial transducer which is invertible with inverse
T_{q}^{-1}
. If
T_{q}
is synchronizing at level
k
, and
T_{q}^{-1}
is synchronizing at level

,
we say that

is
bisynchronizing
at level
(k,l)
. If
T_{q}
is
invertible and is synchronizing at level

but not bisynchronizing, we say
that it is
one-way synchronizing
at level

.

For a non-initial synchronous and invertible transducer
T
we also say
T
is bi-synchronizing (at level
(k,l)
) if both
T
and its inverse

are synchronizing at levels

and

respectively.

Notation 2.7
.

Let

be a transducer which is synchronizing at level

and Let

We define the submonoid of

and the congruence we will be quotienting by.

Fix

generated by the distinct primes in the prime decomposition of

The group

is a finitely generated abelian group and is in fact isomorphic, in a natural way to the quotient of the subgroup of

generated by the prime divisors of

is isomorphic to

where

and

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{2}
,
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{r}
be distinct prime numbers and
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{r}\in\mathbb{N}_{1}

and set

for

\mathfrak{G}(n)
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{r-1}\times\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z}
where
\mathbb{Z}^{r-1}\cong\langle p_{1},\ldots,p_{r-1}\rangle
and
\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z}\cong\langle p_{1}^{l_{1}}\ldots p_{r}^{l_{r}}\rangle
.

Proof.

Let

be an non-trivial element of finite order. Write

. We may assume that
\gcd(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{r})=1
since otherwise

is a power of an element

of
\mathfrak{G}(n)
and we may replace
t
with

. Let

be such that

. This means that
t^{k}=p_{1}^{ks_{1}}p_{2}^{ks_{2}}\ldots p_{r}^{ks_{r}}\in\langle n\rangle
. This is true if and only if there is an
m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
ks_{i}=mL_{i}
for some

. Let

and set

and

. It follows that
k^{\prime}|L_{i}
for all

and so

. Write

, then and

. Therefore

.

Next observe that
\langle p_{1},\ldots,p_{r-1}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{r-1}
and
\langle p_{1},\ldots,p_{r-1}\rangle\cap\langle p_{1}^{l_{1}}p_{2}^{l_{2}}\ldots p_{r}^{l_{r}}\rangle=1
. This is a straight-forward noting that for

and

,

unless

.

∎

3.

The equality:

\displaystyle(\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}))\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\langle(T(d,e),0)\mid d,e\in\mathbb{N},de=n\rangle)\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}

\displaystyle=

is valid. In particular, for any

4.

The image

of
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})
under
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}

5.

(b)

and
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})\cong\mathcal{L}_{n}\times\mathbb{Z}
if and only if

Parts

We now consider

Let

, and let

(T(d,e)_{k},0)

It is straightforward to see that

We therefore see that

We now consider the remainder of part

. Note that since

f_{\infty}\in F_{\infty}(X_{n})
, there is an element
\psi\in\langle(T(d,e),0)\mid d,e\in\mathbb{N},de=n\rangle

.

Let

. Let

and define a map

Fix a word

{\left(\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq r}U^{+}_{\mu_{i}}\right)}=((U^{0}_{\Gamma})f_{\infty})_{>0}

By a result in
[
3
]

{\left(\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq s,\ \nu\in X_{n}^{l^{-1}}}U^{+}_{\mu_{i}\nu}\right)}=((U^{0}_{\Gamma})f_{\infty})_{>0},

therefore

(f_{\infty})\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{k}=sn^{l^{-1}}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n^{k}-1).

Consider the map

We may now deduce that

Write

, where

We now show that

Let
\sigma_{n}^{-m}f_{\infty}\in\ker(\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega})
for

Corollary 3.17
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{2}
,
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{r}
be distinct primes, and
l_{1},\ldots,l_{r}\in\mathbb{N}_{1}
, such that
n=p_{1}^{l_{1}}p_{2}^{l_{2}}\ldots p_{r}^{l_{r}}
. Set
G:=(\langle(\sigma_{n})\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}\rangle)\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}^{-1}
a subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})
and
l=\gcd(l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{r})
. Then,

1.

G
is a normal subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})
;

2.

G\cong\mathcal{K}_{n}\times\mathbb{Z}
and there is a short-exact sequence:

1\to G\to\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})\to\mathbb{Z}^{r-1}\times\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z};

3.

if
n
is prime,
G=\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})\cong\mathcal{L}_{n}\times\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

For
1\leq i\leq r
let
N_{i}=n/p_{i}
.

By Theorem
3.16
,
(\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}))\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}=\langle(T(N_{i},p_{i}),0))\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}\mid 1\leq i\leq r\rangle
. The quotient
(\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}))\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}/(\langle\sigma_{n}\rangle)\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}
, is the quotient of
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
by the subgroup
\{(ml_{1},ml_{2},\ldots,ml_{r})\mid m\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. It is not hard to see that the torsion subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{r}/\langle(l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{r})\rangle
is precisely the subgroup
\langle(\nicefrac{{l_{1}}}{{l}},\nicefrac{{l_{2}}}{{l}},\ldots,\nicefrac{{l_{r}}}{{l}})\rangle
. By the third isomorphism theorem, we have:

\dfrac{\left(\dfrac{\mathbb{Z}^{r}}{\langle(l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{r})\rangle}\right)}{\left(\dfrac{\langle(\nicefrac{{l_{1}}}{{l}},\nicefrac{{l_{2}}}{{l}},\ldots,\nicefrac{{l_{r}}}{{l}})\rangle}{\langle(l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{r})\rangle}\right)}\cong\dfrac{\mathbb{Z}^{r}}{\langle(\nicefrac{{l_{1}}}{{l}},\nicefrac{{l_{2}}}{{l}},\ldots,\nicefrac{{l_{r}}}{{l}})\rangle}.

As the group
\langle(1,1,\ldots,1,0)\rangle
is a finite index subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{r}/\langle(\nicefrac{{l_{1}}}{{l}},\nicefrac{{l_{2}}}{{l}},\ldots,\nicefrac{{l_{r}}}{{l}})\rangle
, we deduce that
\mathbb{Z}^{r}/\langle(\nicefrac{{l_{1}}}{{l}},\nicefrac{{l_{2}}}{{l}},\ldots,\nicefrac{{l_{r}}}{{l}})\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{r-1}
.

Let
\psi:\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})\to(\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}))\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}/(\langle\sigma_{n}\rangle)\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}
, be the composition of
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}
with the natural map from
(\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}))\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}
to the quotient
(\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}))\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}/(\langle\sigma_{n}\rangle)\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}
. It is clear that
\ker(\psi)
is precisely the group
G
. Moreover, it is also clear that,
\langle\{((T(N_{i},p_{i}),0)\psi\mid 1\leq i<r\}\sqcup\{(T(N_{r},p_{r}),0)^{j}\psi\mid 1\leq j<l_{r}\}\rangle=(\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}))\psi
. This is because,
(\Pi_{1\leq i\leq r}(T(N_{i},p_{i}),0)^{l_{i}})\psi\in\langle(\sigma_{n})\psi\rangle
which is trivial. Thus we see that, there is a short exact sequence

1\to G\to\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})\to(\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}))\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}/(\langle\sigma_{n}\rangle)\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}.

In particular, any element of
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})
, can be written as a product
gt\sigma_{n}^{j}
, for
g\in G
,
t
a product of an element of
\langle\{((T(N_{i},p_{i}),0)\psi\mid 1\leq i<r\}\rangle
with an element of
\{(T(N_{r},p_{r}),0)^{j}\mid 1\leq j<l_{r}\}
, and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The homomorphism
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{G}
has image precisely
\langle(\sigma_{n})\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}\rangle
, thus the short exact sequence

1\to\ker(\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{G})\to G\to(G)\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}

splits. Since
\sigma_{n}
commutes with every element of
G
we see that
G=\ker(\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{G})\times\langle\sigma_{n}\rangle
. Clearly, by definition of the group
G
,
\ker(\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{G})=\ker(\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega})
. Thus by Theorem
3.16

\ker(\overrightarrow{\mathrm{sig}}_{\omega}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{G})\cong\mathcal{K}_{n}
.

Part
3.
follows from the previous part, since when
n
is prime,
r=1
, and
\mathcal{K}_{n}=\mathcal{L}_{n}
.
∎

Corollary 3.18
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{2}
, then,
[\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}}),\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})]\cong[\mathcal{L}_{n},\mathcal{L}_{n}]\cong[\mathcal{K}_{n},\mathcal{K}_{n}]
. ∎

Proof.

∎

We note that it is a result of

\mathcal{K}_{n}\unlhd\mathcal{L}_{n,1}\leq\mathcal{O}_{n,1}\leq\mathcal{O}_{n,r}
.

4
Reversing arrows and the dimension group

In this section we show that the operation of “reversing arrows” as described in Subsection
4.3
induces an automorphism of
\mathcal{O}_{n}
. We note that the homeomorphism of
\rho
of
X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}}
that sends a sequence
x\in X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}}
to the element
y\in X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
y_{i}=x_{-i}
, induces, by conjugation, an automorphism of
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}({X_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma_{n}})
sending the shift map to its inverse. Therefore, the map
\rho
induces an automorphism of
\mathcal{L}_{n}
. This automorphism corresponds precisely to “reversing arrows” of the elements of
\mathcal{L}_{n}
. Thus one may recover a representative in
\mathcal{L}_{n}
. Combinatorially, we note that the map
\overleftarrow{\phantom{a}}:X_{n}^{k}\to X_{n}^{k}
induces a bijection, an involution, from
\mathsf{W}_{n}^{k}
to itself for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus conjugation by this bijection induces an automorphism of
\mathrm{Sym}(\mathsf{W}_{n}^{\ast})
. An interpretation of the results of this section is that
(\mathcal{O}_{n})\Pi
(see Subsection
2.5.2
for the definition of the map
\Pi:\mathcal{O}_{n}\to\mathrm{Sym}(W_{n}^{\ast})
) is normalised by the bijection
\overleftarrow{\phantom{a}}:\mathsf{W}_{n}^{\ast}\to\mathsf{W}_{n}^{\ast}
. Since
(\mathcal{O}_{n})\Pi\leq\mathrm{Sym}(\mathsf{W}_{n}^{\ast})
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{O}_{n}
we obtain an automorphism of
\mathcal{O}_{n}
. We note that for
T\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
, the bijection on the induced circuits of
\overleftarrow{T}
from
\mathsf{W}_{n}^{\ast}
to itself, is precisely the map
\overleftarrow{\phantom{a}}(T)\Pi\overleftarrow{\phantom{a}}
. To prove the main result of this section we develop a procedure that associates to each element
T\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
an element
U\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
such that
(U)\Pi=\overleftarrow{\phantom{a}}(T)\Pi\overleftarrow{\phantom{a}}
(note that
U
must be unique as
\Pi:\mathcal{O}_{n}\to\mathrm{Sym}(\mathsf{W}_{n}^{\ast})
is a faithful representation ).

We begin by introducing
non-deterministic transducers
.

4.1
Non-deterministic, strongly synchronizing transducers

In this subsection we often have to distinguish between equality in the monoid
X_{n}^{*}
and equality of words. Therefore, given elements
w_{1},\ldots,w_{l},u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\in X_{n}\sqcup\{\varepsilon\}
, we write
w_{1}\ldots w_{l}=u_{1}\ldots u_{m}
if and only if
l=m
and
w_{i}=u_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
; we write
w_{1}\ldots w_{l}\equiv u_{1}\ldots u_{m}
if and only if
w_{1}\ldots w_{l}
and
u_{1}\ldots u_{l}
represent the same element of the monoid
X_{n}^{*}
.

A
non-deterministic automaton

A=\langle X_{n},Q_{A},\pi_{A}\rangle
is a labelled directed graph with a finite vertex/state set
Q_{A}
, a finite edge set
\pi_{A}\subseteq X_{n}^{\ast}\times Q_{A}\times Q_{A}
(
|\pi_{A}|<\infty
) and a finite alphabet set
X_{n}
. In other words, a non-deterministic automaton is a finite directed graph with a finite number edges between states and with edges labelled by elements of
X_{n}^{*}
.

Let
A
be a non-deterministic automaton, a path in
A
is a word

(w_{1},p_{1},q_{1}),(w_{2},p_{2},q_{2}),\ldots,(w_{m},p_{m},q_{m})\in\pi_{A}^{\ast}

such that
q_{i}=p_{i+1}
for
1\leq i\leq m-1
. To simplify notation, we write
(w,p_{1},q_{m})
for the path
(w_{1},p_{1},q_{1}),(w_{2},p_{2},q_{2}),\ldots,(w_{m},p_{m},q_{m})
where
w=w_{1}w_{2}\ldots w_{m}
.

We impose the following non-degeneracy condition on all non-deterministic automata in this article: for any circuit
(w,p,p)
,
w\not\equiv\varepsilon
.

Let
A
be a non-deterministic automaton. For a state
q\in Q_{A}
, we define
\mathrm{Domain}{(q)}\subseteq X_{n}^{\mathbb{N}}
as follows. Let
x\in X_{n}^{\omega}
be such that for any non-empty proper prefix
x_{i}
of
x
there are elements
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{m},w\in X_{n}^{*}
, with
x_{i}
a prefix of
w
, states
p_{0}=q,p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\in Q_{A}
, such that
(w_{i},p_{i-1},p_{i})
is an element of
\pi_{A}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
and
w_{1}w_{2}\ldots w_{m}\equiv w
. Then
x\in\mathrm{Domain}{(q)}
.

A non-deterministic automaton
A=\langle X_{n},Q_{A},\pi_{A}\rangle
is called
strongly synchronizing
if it satisfies the following condition. For any word
u\in X_{n}^{+}
, there is a unique (up-to rotation) sequence

(w_{1},p_{1},p_{2}),(w_{2},p_{2},p_{3}),\ldots,(w_{m},p_{m},p_{m+1})\in\pi_{A}

with
p_{m+1}=p_{1}
such that for
v\in X_{n}^{+}
with
v\equiv w_{1}\ldots w_{m}
, the equality
[v]_{\sim_{{\mathmbox{rot}}}}=[u]_{\sim_{{\mathmbox{rot}}}}
holds.

The collision with terminology in Subsection
2.4
is deliberate as Remark
4.1
makes clear. An interpretation of Proposition
4.10
is that strongly synchronzing non-deterministic automaton and strongly synchronizing automaton are essentially “the same thing”.

A strongly synchronizing non-deterministic automaton
A
is called
core
if it is strongly connected.

Remark 4.1
.

Every strongly synchronizing automaton is a strongly synchronizing non-deterministic automaton. On the other hand any deterministic automaton which is strongly synchronizing in the non-deterministic sense in strongly synchronizing in the deterministic sense. That is the two notions of being “strongly synchronizing” coincide on the class of deterministic automata.

Remark 4.2
.

Let
A
be a strongly synchronizing non-deterministic automaton. Given a prime word
u\in X_{n}^{+}
, the unique circuit
(w_{1},p_{1},p_{2})(w_{2},p_{2},p_{3})\ldots(w_{m},p_{m},p_{m+1})
in
A
with
w_{1}\ldots w_{m}
equivalent to an element of
[u]_{\sim_{{\mathmbox{rot}}}}
, is a prime word in
\pi_{A}^{\ast}
. This follows from the non-degeneracy condition and the fact that

is a prime word. On the other hand, given a circuit
(w_{1},p_{1},p_{2})(w_{2},p_{2},p_{3})\ldots(w_{m},p_{m},p_{m+1})
in
A
which is a prime word in
\pi_{A}^{\ast}
, the word
w_{1}\ldots w_{m}
is equivalent to a prime word in
X_{n}^{*}
. This follows from the existence and uniqueness clause in the definition of strongly synchronizing automata. Thus it follows that given a strongly synchronizing automaton
A
, there will be circuits in
A
which are prime words in
\pi_{A}
.

Remark 4.3
.

Let
A
be a strongly synchronizing non-deterministic automaton with alphabet set
X_{n}
and only one state. Then
A
is the single state automaton with
n
edges each labelled with a unique element of

.

A
non-deterministic transducer

is a tuple

where

is a (non-degenerate) non-deterministic automaton called the

\lambda_{T}:\pi_{T}\to X_{n}^{\ast}
. We identify a non-deterministic transducer with a labelled digraph whereby an edge

of

is labelled

.

Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m+1}\in Q_{T}
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\in X_{n}^{*}

(w_{i},p_{i},p_{i+1})
is an element of

. Then, setting

, we write
\lambda_{T}(w,p_{1})
for the word
\lambda_{T}(w_{1},p_{1},p_{2})\ldots\lambda_{T}(w_{m},p_{m},p_{m+1})
and write
(w,p_{1},p_{m+1})
for the path
(w_{1},p_{1},p_{2})(w_{2},p_{2},p_{3})\ldots(w_{m},p_{m},p_{m+1})

to

We impose the following non-degeneracy condition on all non-deterministic transducers in this article: for any circuit

We identify the output function

with a subset of

is obtained by deleting output words in the digraph of the transducer; likewise the digraph for the output automaton of

is obtained by input words in the labelled digraph of

For a non-deterministic transducer

and

\mathrm{Domain}{(q)}
with
\mathrm{Domain}{(q)}
as a state of

At this point, one might expect the definition of the image

of a state

is a multifunction.

Let
T
be a non-deterministic automaton. Then

is called
strongly synchronizing
if

is strongly synchronizing;

is called

if both

and

are strongly synchronizing. If

if its input automaton is core.

The results that follow demonstrate that if we restrict to the class of strongly-synchronizing transducers, the proposed map

As promised, in this section we show that the map

Let

The group

We define the action of the group

on the group

. Then

, that is, all loop states of

Proof.

Let

be a letter,

be the

loop state of
T

. We show that
(q_{c})\alpha
must be

Let

for all

. Set

.

Since

, and

,

for all

. By a similar argument, for all

is determined by the word

Suppose

. However, as

Let

be the inverse of

\beta

∎
