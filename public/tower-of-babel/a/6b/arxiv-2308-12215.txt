[SoK] The Challenges of Machine Learning for Trust and Safety: A Case Study on Misinformation Detection
Madelyne Xiao, Jonathan Mayer

Abstract
We examine the disconnect between scholarship and practice in applying machine learning to trust and safety problems, using misinformation detection as a case study. We systematize literature on automated detection of misinformation across a corpus of 270 well-cited papers in the field. We then examine subsets of papers for data and code availability, design missteps, reproducibility, and generalizability. We find significant shortcomings in the literature that call into question claimed performance and practicality. Detection tasks are often meaningfully distinct from the challenges that online services actually face. Datasets and model evaluation are often non-representative of real-world contexts, and evaluation frequently is not independent of model training. Data and code availability is poor. Models do not generalize well to out-of-domain data. Based on these results, we offer recommendations for evaluating machine learning applications to trust and safety problems. Our aim is for future work to avoid the pitfalls that we identify.



I
INTRODUCTION

Online services face a daunting task: There is an unceasing deluge of user-generated content, on the order of hundreds of thousands of posts per minute on certain well-known social media platforms
[
259
]
. Some of that content is false, hateful, harassing, extremist, or otherwise problematic. How can platforms reliably and proactively identify these “trust and safety” issues?

Machine learning has proven an attractive approach in the academic literature, leading to large bodies of scholarship on misinformation detection, toxic speech classification, and other core trust and safety challenges
[
146
,
341
,
342
]
. The conceptual appeal of machine learning is that it could address the massive scale of user-generated content on large platforms and the capacity constraints of small platforms. Recent work claims impressive performance statistics, and that near-term deployment is practical. As a case in point, OpenAI recently announced that it was deploying a content moderation system based on GPT-4 to detect problematic content online; according to the company, performance on detection tasks exceeds that of human moderators with “modest training.” Even so, OpenAI’s head of safety systems admitted that human advisors would still be necessary to “adjudicate borderline cases”
[
339
]
. This accords with our finding that, in practice, trust and safety functions at online services remain largely manual—driven by user reports and carried out by human moderators. Additionally, thresholds (e.g., detection precision or recall) for determining where and when automated processes require human intervention are underspecified
[
336
,
337
]
.

In this work, we investigate the significant disconnect between scholarship and practice in applications of machine learning to trust and safety problems. Our project is inspired by recent research that has identified shortcomings in machine learning applications for many problem domains, including information security
[
315
,
320
]
. We use misinformation detection as a case study for trust and safety problems, because the topic has recently generated a rich literature with diverse methods and claimed successes. Misinformation detection also has substantive complexities that are common for trust and safety problems: linguistic and cultural nuance, sensitivity to context, and rapidly evolving circumstances.

Evaluating responses to misinformation is societally urgent: The veracity of published news is vitally important to democratic processes, public health, and press integrity. This was particularly evident in 2016, during the U.S. presidential election, and again in 2020, in the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic. During those years, false and misleading information was rampant on well-known social media platforms
[
287
,
316
,
317
,
319
]
. In response to public outcry over coordinated misinformation campaigns during the 2016 presidential election, Twitter and Facebook announced new measures in 2017 to counteract the spread of misinformation, including automated misinformation detection methods that would surface potentially false rumors for further vetting by third-party fact-checking organizations
[
324
,
323
,
325
]
.

These machine learning approaches do not appear to have been successful. Leaked Facebook documents from the time of the COVID-19 pandemic revealed that the platform’s “ability to detect VH [vaccine hesitancy] comments is bad in English—and basically non-existent elsewhere”
[
336
]
. Employees acknowledged that automated interventions—including machine learning classifiers—were grossly underequipped to counter vaccine-related misinformation.

Meanwhile, in the academic literature, publications routinely report optimistic performance figures for machine learning models to detect misinformation. In the literature review that we conduct for this work, among publications that report performance metrics, about 70% of papers report over 80% accuracy on at least one detection task (see SI); some of these works report near-perfect performance. What explains this discrepancy between scholarship and practice?

We seek to answer four discrete research questions, which collectively shed light on the shortcomings of automated misinformation detection.

RQ1.

How
well-suited
are existing misinformation detection methods to the needs of online services, specifically social media platforms that host user-generated content?

RQ2.

Are there identifiable
errors
in dataset curation and method development in ML-driven misinformation detection studies?

RQ3.

How
reproducible
are published ML-driven misinformation detection methods?

RQ4.

How
generalizable
are published ML-driven misinformation detection methods to out-of-domain data (i.e., text corpora that discuss topics that don’t appear in the training set)?

We address these research questions in three ways. First, we conduct a broad literature review and synthesis. We taxonomize existing ML-driven misinformation detection systems, with a focus on feature types, detection targets, and errors in method development and dataset design. Second, we attempt to obtain code and data for a subset of prior work. Third, we test several widely-cited approaches for replication and generalizability. We arrive at the following results by applying these methods.

•

Detection tasks in scholarship are often steps removed from the misinformation content moderation challenges that platforms face. Detection targets may be of limited consequence or may be readily identifiable through manual means.

•

Datasets used in publications are often unrealistic. Model evaluation often lacks independence from training and rarely involves close emulation of a real-world deployment.

•

Data and code availability problems pervade the literature, inhibiting replication. Where these are available, we are generally able to replicate prior work.

•

Prior work has poor generalizability when classifying content beyond what was included in the training dataset.

Based on these observations, we recommend a rubric for future work that proposes ML models to identify trust and safety concerns.

METHODS

In this section, we describe the following: 1) terminology and taxonomies, 2) our methodology for selecting papers for our literature review, and 3) our approach to replication analysis and author outreach.

Taxonomies.
Through an iterative process of reading and inductive coding of our papers, we develop a taxonomy of five “information scopes.” As we read each paper in our corpus, we noted the
operative unit
of misinformation detection for the classifier or method described—the smallest semantic or organizational unit of information that the method attempted to classify as true or false—and sorted each paper into emergent categories. One reader made three separate coding passes over the corpus, varying the objectives of her annotation on each pass: In the first pass, she specifically sought to identify taxonomies for classification and detection; on the second, she noted method design errors and performance scores; on the final pass, she noted actual versus stated detection targets. Our final taxonomy comprises five information scopes:
claims, news articles, social media posts, users/authors, and websites.
With the exception of
claims
, we consider these scopes
in the context of
online services—e.g., evaluation of news articles and websites whose links might be posted to a social media platform. We note three principle approaches to misinformation detection:
direct verification of claims, detection of deceptive language,
and
analysis of propagation patterns or context signals
. We also note the types of features included in each model’s feature set, and sort observed feature sets into five categories:
textual, network-based, infrastructural, user- or author credential-based,
and
multimodal features
. We also note performance and accuracy scores of published methods, if the authors report this information. Additional details about our corpus construction and paper coding process can be found in
Section

II
; our annotation guide can be found in our SI.

We adopt the terminology and taxonomies of previous work in our examination of ML errors
[
304
,
305
,
315
]
. In the context of data mining, Kaufman et al. define

as “the introduction of information about the target of a data mining problem that should not be legitimately available to mine from”
[

. (An example of this, encountered in both machine learning and data science contexts, is training of a model on inputs that also belong to a test dataset.) Here, we cast a broader net and examine other, related design errors in the context of ML-based methods, including those described by Kapoor et al. (their work also includes dependencies and duplicates in datasets, temporal leakage, and poor feature selection within their taxonomy of ML design errors)
[

. In general, these errors lead to inflated model performance in an experimental setting and underperformance in real-world deployments. Though the precise nature of these errors varies by dataset, information scope, and choice of model, we broadly categorize them as errors related to

and
model training

Corpus curation.
As a first-pass relevancy check, we used automated keyword matching to confirm that all collected papers did, in fact, address misinformation and automated fact-checking methods; in the case of any ambiguity, we skimmed the abstract of the paper in question. We then annotated research papers that passed this check for scope of detection method (e.g., article-level, claim-level) and feature set under analysis (e.g., stylometric features, source credibility); our annotation guide appears in our online SI.

II-B

Reproducibility and Replicability

Toward understanding the reproducibility of existing detection methods (
RQ3
), we conducted replication analyses of a selection of published methods in our corpus; where necessary, we contacted paper authors to request access to data and code. We describe our methodology for both of these processes in this section.

Paper selection and author outreach.
We sorted our full text corpus by information scope. Within each scope, we sorted papers in order of decreasing citation count. We then proceeded as follows:

1.

We attempted to source the full methods and datasets for the most cited paper within each information scope.

2.

If we were unable to find this information during an independent web search, we reached out to the paper’s lead author(s) to request access.

3.

If this request was unsuccessful—if the author did not respond, or confirmed that the dataset or code was no longer available—we returned to step 1 for the next most highly-cited paper in our corpus within that scope.

Replication analyses.
In order to reproduce results published in a selection of papers and perform cross-cutting analyses on out-of-domain and out-of-sample datasets, we conduct a series of replication analyses on a subset of papers. We chose methods from disparate information scopes, and which consider a variety of different feature types. We attempted to reproduce results reported in each paper on the datasets mentioned therein (we contacted authors when necessary to obtain datasets and code). We developed the following framework for evaluating the reproducibility and generalizability of existing methods:

•

Reproducibility.
We reproduce published results with code and data reported in the original publication. We evaluate availability of code and data and, where possible, compare our analysis outcomes with those reported in the original paper (
RQ3
).

•

Explainability.
Toward understanding the contributions of specific feature types to overall classifier performance, and why certain approaches work (or don’t work), we perform feature ablation studies when appropriate.

•

Replicability and generalizability.
Model perfor-mance on novel datasets will be useful for determining the generalizability of existing detection methods to different contexts (
RQ4
). For models that were explicitly tested on specific misinformation narratives (e.g., 2020 stolen election narratives), on specific timeframes, or on specific types of misinformation (parody, satire, etc), we develop current datasets to test method performance on diverse information domains. As much as possible, we attempt to imitate real world contexts.

Ethical Considerations

Datasets used for this study were already publicly available or were obtained with permission from study authors. Datasets were anonymized by their original authors. We submitted our outreach study as a protocol and received approval from our institutional review board.

III
RELATED WORK

Relevance to existing trust and safety operations.
We situate our work within existing literature—published in both academic and non-academic venues—that describes trust and safety operations in commercial settings (i.e., social media platforms). Though Twitter and Facebook have previously advertised human-in-the-loop processes for automatically flagging potentially misinformative posts for manual vetting by third-party fact-checking organizations, criteria for deploying tiered responses and seeking human intervention are murky
[
323
]
. (A
larger
volume of user reports might be necessary for takedowns versus warnings, and classifier recall thresholds for requiring human intervention might be
higher
for COVID-19 narratives versus celebrity gossip, for instance.) Furthermore, evidence from the leaked Facebook Files suggests that automated content moderation methods rely heavily on proxy signals for identifying hate speech and misinformation on the platform; as a result, automated approaches were generally employed for first-line detection of putative rumoring narratives, with the burden of actual fact verification still falling to human content moderators
[
336
]
.

Critical examinations of ML-driven methods.
This work is the latest entry in a body of literature that critically examines ML-driven solutions to subfield-specific problems, including security and privacy issues

[
315
,
320
,
304
,
342
]
. Prior studies in the same vein have evaluated ML approaches to network security and established frameworks for explaining the mechanics of black box ML- and AI-driven approaches
[
320
]
. In particular, Jacobs et al. describe three instances of model underspecification in published ML solutions for detecting patterns in network traffic: evidence of shortcut learning, presence of spurious correlations, and vulnerability to out-of-distribution samples
[
320
]
. These three issues approximately map on to methods design issues we discuss in this work; namely, overfitting on training data, data dependencies, and generalizability to out-of-domain data. This study also considers recent work that examines the risks of automated content moderation powered by large language models
[
340
]
.

Feasibility of detection.
Most of the work we review presupposes that automated detection of misinformation is possible at all. We note that this is, in itself, a strong assumption to make: Published work in psychology, linguistics, and philosophy of language has previously called into question the feasibility of using semantic and syntactic features to determine the veracity of text statements and the usefulness of binary true/false classifications of information, particularly in the absence of unified definitions of misinformation, disinformation, and information
[
338
,
190
]
. On the other hand, some researchers maintain that misinformative texts are characterized by indelible “fingerprints” that distinguish them from non-misinformative texts: for instance, emotional language and reduced lexical diversity
[
168
,
311
]
. We use this debate to inform our discussion of extra-textual feature sets for misinformation detection, and to call into question established NLP approaches to misinformation detection.

ANALYSIS

IV-A

Overview

As described in
Section

I
, we organize our paper analysis by research question. Within our discussion of each research question, we discuss information scope-specific issues pertaining to method and dataset design. Our taxonomy of information scopes is as follows:
claims, news articles, social media posts, users/authors,
and
whole websites
. For each paper under analysis in each information scope, we additionally note the type of
feature
set(s) that the detection method examines. We describe our taxonomy of features in Methods (
Section

II
).

Across all five information scopes, we identify potential areas of need for automated interventions; evaluate the goodness of fit of existing methods to those needs; and interrogate the model design and data curation approaches of those methods. In our analysis of method-target fit, we find that 1) the actual utility of automated interventions for context-rich information scopes (news articles, whole websites) is unclear; 2) the vast majority of methods skirt the difficulties of performing actual fact verification by detecting proxy signals for false rumors, including evidence of virality, anomalous patterns of user engagement, and emotional language; and 3) authors often train text-based methods on narrowly-defined information domains, and fail to control for data dependencies that might inflate model performance. In our analysis of data curation and method design, we find that 1) testing on current or real-time data is rare; 2) a priori assumptions about user behaviors are rampant; and 3) methods that purport to employ content-agnostic multimodal features lack explainability and adequate feature normalization.

In

1
, we present a summary of our study corpus composition, organized by year and information scope. From 2016 to 2017, we note an appreciable increase—both as a proportion of our corpus, and as a percentage of overall search results—in the number of published papers that examine misinformation at the level of whole news articles and social media posts. Though we have not conducted an in-depth investigation of publication trends, it seems likely that this spike was in response to the proliferation of misinformation on social media during the 2016 presidential elections (note, in particular, the dramatic uptick in raw numbers of news article- and social media post-scoped papers). Interest in misinformation detection remains high, despite decreased representation of papers post-2019 in our corpus (more than 2,000 misinformation detection preprints and papers have been published since the start of 2023, according to Google Scholar).

IV-B

Systematization of literature

We present takeaways from our reading and coding of misinformation detection literature. In this systematization section, we pay particular attention to issues pertaining to
RQ1
and
RQ2
: method-target fit and data curation.

IV-B
1
Method-target fit

We discuss the usefulness of available and proposed detection methods to individual
information scopes
: claims, news articles, social media users/posts, and whole websites. We find that detection tasks at the claim and post scopes stand to benefit the most from automated interventions, absent the context that news readers might use to evaluate the credibility of whole news articles or websites. Unfortunately, we also find that claim- and post-scoped methods in existing detection literature have limited generalizability and efficacy in early detection.

Claims.
We define a single “claim” as the smallest semantic unit of fact or misinformation. There is a real need for fast, reliable misinformation detection at this scope, particularly in real time settings: Research deployments of automated methods have been applied in the context of checking statements made by political candidates during debates in real-time, and for flagging possibly spurious statements on social media (e.g., tweets)
[
60
,
27
,
278
,
5
]
. At this scope, automated misinformation detection methods proceed along one of the following three lines: 1) stylometric, semantic, and syntactic analysis of text contents, 2) knowledge-based comparison of statements to ground-truth claims (fact-checking), or 3) crowd- or context-informed inference of text credibility. Of all information scopes, we find that claim-scoped detection methods are most consistent in their attempts to perform actual fact-checking of text statements. Features are mostly textual. Claim-scoped papers constitute about 15% of our corpus.

Off-the-shelf NLP methods, including part-of-speech (POS) tagging and sentiment analysis, have limited use at this scope, for the simple reason that single claims are often insufficient inputs (we provide a brief summary of claim-level NLP methods in this section and elaborate further on whole-text applications in our discussion of article-scoped approaches).

At the level of single claims, semantic feature analysis is limited to the identification of structured statements, usually as a precursor to knowledge graph (KG) construction. At the most basic level, these claims might take the form of subject-predicate-object (SPO) statements (e.g., "I like pie")
[
17
,
15
]
. In general, nouns (subjects and objects) become nodes on the KG, and predicates become edges. Some KG-based methods estimate the probability of the existence of an edge between two distinct nodes and identify shortest paths between edges in order to “fact-check” SPO statements
[
17
,
155
]
. Other claim-scoped approaches use word embedding methods to quantify the semantic “distance” between ground-truth claims and claims to be checked
[
261
,
17
,
277
,
101
]
. While these distance metrics are, at best, a kind of proxy for the veracity of text statements—semantic proximity as an indicator for the likelihood of a relationship between two entities—the methods that employ them still bear the greatest resemblance to manual fact-verification, compared to methods that appear in other information scopes. The efficacy of these approaches is highly dependent upon 1) choice of ground-truth reference and 2) the coverage and granularity of data included in KGs. We discuss article-scoped approaches to detection next.

News articles.
We consider all news-oriented writing of length 100 words or greater to fall within this scope. Full-length news articles have sufficient text contents to make certain off-the-shelf NLP approaches (e.g., stance detection, authorship detection) feasible, absent any external ground-truth reference for fact verification. This is the second-largest scope we consider, and comprises about 24% of the full paper corpus. Most methods at this scope rely on purely textual features to perform classification.

We observe that methods at the article scope generally adopt one of three approaches: 1) pure NLP analysis of article contents to identify language features particular to misinformative or deceptive language; 2) analysis of article contents and headlines to identify potentially clickbait-y titles or discussion related to suspected misinformation narratives; and 3) analysis of user responses in conjunction with analysis of text contents. (There is a rough correspondence with 1) and 3) for claim-scoped approaches, described in the previous section.) Features are mostly
textual
or
user/author-based
. We notice that, of these three approaches, authors whose methods align with 1) and 2) claim that article- or content-only approaches are better-suited for real-time or early detection of misinformation (detection of propagation patterns generally requires a degree of virality)
[
35
,
33
,
83
,
62
,
47
]
. Meanwhile, authors whose methods consider multimodal feature sets in addition to pure text contents argue that classifiers trained only on text contents risk overfitting to their training data, and do not generalize well to out-of-domain datasets
[
104
,
220
,
61
]
.

The actual utility of automated methods for misinformation detection for news articles is unclear. On moderated online fora, for instance, manual review of new content is frequently very effective at early detection and removal of misinformative articles: A 2016 study of Wikipedia hoaxes found that 90% of hoax articles were flagged at the source within one hour of “patrolling” by trusted editors.
Though time-to-detection did increase drastically thereafter, this was applicable to only a small percentage of hoax articles—the authors claim that one in a hundred hoax articles survives past one year
[
7
]
. This finding mirrors our own observation, from our review of the Facebook and Twitter Files, that a good deal of misinformation detection is still performed manually by human content moderators
[

,
336

Social media posts and users.
We discuss social media post- and user-scoped methods together because we encountered similar data curation and methods design issues within both scopes. Social media post-scoped papers formed approximately 24% of our corpus (66 papers). User-scoped papers formed approximately 15% of our corpus (40 papers). Feature sets for methods at both scopes are generally multimodal, and include
textual
,
user/author-based
, and
network-based
features. As mentioned previously, there is a real need for fast detection of misinformation and false rumors at this scope, given the volume of content posted to popular social media platforms. Even so, we observe that a majority of methods at this scope rely on patterns of sharing and propagation that implicitly associate virality with the existence of rumoring narratives
[
3
,
214
,
35
]
. We note that these assumptions disallow

For the detection of both Sybils and accounts that spread misinformation, anomalous patterns of account registration and user interaction serve as proxies for the presence of bad actors
[

,

]

Websites.
As with article-scoped methods, we noted intervention fit issues at the whole website scope (comprising only 2% of the full corpus, or 6 papers; feature sets are

,

, and

In oversampling from fringe and novel news sites, many article- and website-scoped misinformation detection methods likely overestimate the extent to which such content is represented in the typical reader’s news diet, even though many paper authors motivate their methods by citing increasing reader distrust of mainstream media outlets. In fact, these fringe sites are likely in the long tail of the distribution of sites most commonly viewed by news readers.

Still, methods for detecting suspicious website infrastructure are useful, particularly on social media platforms, where links to news articles are often shared without additional context. All authors within the whole website scope state that a primary motivation for their work is

Tellingly, about 82% of authors cited the 2016 presidential election as motivation for development of their automated detection method, e.g.

2
Proxy targets for detection

We discuss targets for detection across all five information scopes. In particular, we note that the vast majority of methods do not perform actual fact verification, and instead detect proxy signals for false rumors (e.g., evidence of virality, anomalous patterns of user engagement), and that authors often train and test text-based methods on single rumoring narratives or information domains.

Within the
claim
scope, certain context-aware methods consider extra-textual features to inform their classification. A number of methods rely on the “wisdom of crowds” to crowd-source credibility scores or (counter-)evidence for claims
[
114
,
121
]
. We note a number of papers that essentially automate the manual fact-checking procedure that a human web user might follow. These methods proceed by 1) querying a database or search engine for a certain claim, 2) seeking the sourcing for this claim among search results, and 3) verifying the credibility of these sources, or counting the raw number of relevant hits
[
202
,
15
,
48
]
. Equating hits or mentions with positive proof of veracity is a dangerous (and possibly false) equivalency, particularly when existing misinformation literature also assumes that misinformative claims tend to be outstandingly viral
[
3
]
.

At the
article
scope, we note that 44% of papers claiming to detect misinformation actually detect second- or
n
th-order effects of the existence of misinformation. That is, in lieu of actual fact verification, many methods detect
proxy
signals that might be indicative of the existence of misinformation, then rely on a priori assumptions about these signals—e.g., user behavior, stylometric properties—to associate them with misinformation. For instance, a number of methods detect
genre signals
(satire, parody, hyperpartisanship), and assume that genre, writing style, or political inclination are synonymous with (mis)informative reportage
[
226
,
11
,
14
,
19
]
. A significant number of methods include text sentiment in their feature sets, or explicitly report on the advantages of including sentiment analysis in misinformation detection methods—in general, these authors associate evidence of strong emotion (and strongly negative emotion, in particular) with misinformation
[
38
,
19
,
14
,
67
,
261
,
220
,
222
,
62
,
82
]
.

We argue that overreliance on text sentiment—and, more generally, unimodal feature sets and linguistic properties of text—as a proxy indicator for misinformation makes detection methods more vulnerable to adversarial attacks. Only one paper within the article scope tested in an adversarial setting: Its authors found that, while stylometry-based misinformation detection had an accuracy rate greater than 80% on routine tasks, this score dropped to about 50% in adversarial cases
[
200
]
. Malicious actors might edit a factually correct news story to be misinformative, or include subtly misinformative statements in the body of an otherwise true news article, without substantively changing authorial style. We investigate the efficacy of this circumvention method in our replication analysis, in Section
IV-C2
.

At the
social media post
and
user
scopes, many methods that examine user behavior assume that certain propagation patterns are unique to engagement with misinformative texts
[
299
,
104
]
. Multiple methods select tweet topics and hashtags that were retweeted a certain number of times by users, or that achieved “virality” according to some threshold of popularity predetermined by the paper authors. It is perhaps unsurprising, then, when the same papers conclude that high levels of user buy-in or peaks in user interactions with these tweet topics are signals for misinformation
[
239
,
230
,
23
]
. As we discuss in greater detail in
Section

IV-B3
, a priori assumptions about user engagement with misinformation or the popularity of misinformative narratives might 1) preferentially surface already-viral misinformation, making the resulting detection methods that are trained on these biased datasets unusable for
early detection
, or 2) yield overoptimistic predictions of detection efficacy in an experimental setting. It is unclear if these assumptions are necessary and useful constraints for delineating detection targets, or if they detrimentally limit detection capacity and increase susceptibility to circumvention. Techniques for detecting spam and Sybil accounts in security literature seem to impose similar constraints on the assumed activity of dishonest nodes: They assume, for instance, that Sybils will form well-connected neighborhoods
[
183
]
, or (seemingly contradictorily) that compromised Sybils will refrain from connecting with additional Sybils, to avoid detection
[
179
]
.

In general, these methods—at the user and post scopes
and

whole website
scopes—employ multimodal feature sets for detection—for the former two, these feature sets include user credentials, user graphs, and sharing/post propagation statistics; for the latter, these feature sets include infrastructure features such as site age and URL structure. While multimodality might go some way toward preventing potentially malicious actors from taking advantage of automated detection (as we describe in preceding paragraphs, in regards to unimodal feature sets), well-considered
feature normalization
is crucial for ensuring the explainability and efficacy of models employing these multimodal features, but is often absent or undisclosed
[
37
,
300
]
.

IV-B
3
Data curation and method design

We discuss issues with dataset curation and classifier design. In particular, we note that most authors do not test on current or real-time data; methods that detect propagation patterns also tend to make a priori assumptions about user behaviors; and that methods employing multimodal features often lack model explainability and adequate feature normalization.

Lack of uniform standards for describing misinformation.
The few papers in our corpus that propose to perform actual fact verification generally rely on existing compendia of real and fake news sites in order to establish ground truth. Unfortunately, choice of ground truth site or labeled dataset can significantly influence the outcome of analysis (Bozarth et al. found that perceived prevalence of misinformation in a corpus of 2016 election news varied from 2% to 40%, depending on choice of ground-truth reference website
[
16
]
). This is due in large part to lack of uniform standards across these sites: These external references do not follow any standard taxonomy for describing misinformation—some sites’ classifications are far more granular than others—or only label news articles from specific information domains (e.g. politics)
[
282
,
283
]
.

KG-based methods generally construct their graphs from static snapshots of existing online knowledge databases. Though it is easier to quality-control these references—they are generally single-source, or, in the case of DBpedia, are fairly reputable—there is empirical evidence that hard-coding predicates or subjects into structured representations of knowledge databases yields classifiers with limited versatility. Additionally, it is unclear how scalable and deployable a knowledge graph-based method might be in a real-time setting: It would be impractical to continually update such a graph in a rapidly evolving news landscape, where communication is often semantically inconsistent and non-textual media are common (images and videos are common elements of social media posts, for instance). Furthermore, there might not be a ground-truth reference available online to serve as a point of comparison for breaking news or primary-source statements. In fact, of the 40 papers we analyzed that discussed methods with network-based components, very few (about 7%) tested on real-time data
[
45
,
61
,
154
]
. We discuss the limitations of KG-based approaches in our replication analysis (see
Section

IV-C1
).

Lack of current datasets.
While well-annotated, current datasets are in short supply across all information scopes, this deficit is particularly glaring at the article scope: Most news outlets that consistently produce factually correct news articles do not produce comparably large corpora of verifiably misinformative news articles, and vice versa. In the absence of well-labeled corpora of truthful and misinformative articles, many authors use existing data. A few datasets appear to be go-tos for methods at this scope, despite the time-specificity of news and rumors; the LIAR dataset (first presented in
[
30
]
) is among these
[
167
,
222
,
62
]
. The dataset was released online in 2017, and includes manually labeled statements from the PolitiFact fact-checking website published over the course of a decade (from 2007 to 2017). The many papers that reference the LIAR dataset were published between 2017 and 2023, and rarely (in fact, only two papers within this scope) perform additional testing on current or real-time data. Other datasets, including those published by BuzzFeed News, Gossip Cop, Media Bias/Fact Check, and the Fake News Challenge, are also temporally distant from the papers that cite them
[
261
,
104
,
167
,
62
]
. As a result, reported performance on these datasets is not indicative of method performance in a current or real-world context.

Overfitting to information domains.
Much as claim-scoped methods risk overfitting to particular syntactic structures, misinformation detection methods scoped to full articles are often overfitted to specific information domains or rumoring narratives. We observe a number of methods that select one or more topics of interest (e.g., the 2016 presidential election, the Boston Marathon bombing), train a classifier on these topics, then test this classifier on a different set of texts that discuss the
same
topic
[
260
,
203
,
134
]
. These methods do well when tested on in-domain texts; many report accuracy well above 80% (see SI). As we show in our replication analysis (
Section

IV-C2
), these methods perform significantly worse on out-of-domain texts.

Limited social media data accessibility.
We note that a majority of published papers did not include links to their methods or datasets (and, in many cases, these methods and data were not made available to us, even after we reached out to the lead author of the paper to request access). About 60% of user- and post-scoped methods (40 papers) relied on data obtained through the Twitter API, and classify individual tweets or user accounts as misinformative or factually correct (e.g.,
[
278
,
246
,
96
,
169
,
51
]
). Unfortunately, in April 2023, Twitter shut down its free academic API—the platform now only permits access to a limited number of tweets each month with a paid subscription plan. We identified multiple Twitter datasets posted pre-API shutdown that were missing full tweet contents or user data. These omissions are due to the Twitter API’s Terms of Service, which only permit limited disclosure of “…Tweet IDs, Direct Message IDs, and/or User IDs…”
[
292
]
.

In total, we attempted to locate datasets and code for 50 different papers. Thirty-six (72%) of these analyzed tweet corpora, and 42 (84%) of these were user- or post-scoped. We were able to independently source complete methods or data for fourteen (28%) of these. Of the 27 authors we eventually contacted about data availability, nine responded; six of those authors were able to provide method code or partial datasets.

Strong assumptions about user behavior.
Misinformation researchers commonly reference the SIR (susceptible-infected-recovered) epidemiological model and its variations to model information spread—analogously, newsreaders who have not been exposed to a misinformation narrative are “susceptible”; “infected” newsreaders are those who buy into a misinformation narrative; and “recovered” news readers were once (but are no longer) credulous of a misinformation narrative
[
125
,
312
]
. Many papers within this scope make normative theoretical assumptions about user behaviors: some works assume, for instance, a homogeneous population of newsreaders, with identical probabilities of “infection” and reinfection; others assume that, once infected, a user cannot be re-infected
[
193
]
. One method assumes that, in the event that a true and a false story reach a user simultaneously, the user will believe the true story first
[
251
]
. We noted several methods that did not distinguish between social media posts that actively promoted false rumors and those that merely discussed or even debunked them
[
125
,
55
]
; other methods calculated composite statistics with questionable feature normalization; e.g., one method calculated a credibility score for a whole news event as an average of the credibility scores for all posts that discussed this event (notably, the dataset was designed to have a balance, 50/50, of true and false rumor tweets)
[
247
]
. Ideally, such a score might be weighted by post visibility or originating account followership.

We note that most of these assumptions yield
conservative
estimates of misinformation spread. In general, when making theoretical estimates of real-world behaviors, we would recommend the opposite approach (e.g., assumption of heightened susceptibility to infection and possibility of reinfection). While we can point out errors in theoretical assumptions about user behaviors, it is difficult to evaluate possible real-world impacts of these assumptions on detection efficacy, particularly in the absence of accessible current data. We previously discussed the extent of accessibility and availability issues within our corpus (see
Limited social media data accessibility
).

Dataset bias and inconsistency.
For both training and testing, methods scoped to whole website detection generally relied on published lists of websites with accompanying credibility scores
[
37
,
300
,
1
,
286
]
. As we discussed previously, however, these references did not have uniform taxonomies for classifying site credibility, and it was often the case that these pre-labeled lists were biased towards older, more visible real news and fake news outlets, or were restricted to specific information domains
[
37
]
. We note papers that perform website infrastructure analysis on contemporaneous snapshots (circa 2019) of websites in their corpora, even though the text-based features for the same analysis were drawn from datasets published in 2016 and 2017
[
286
]
. This constitutes a serious source of temporal leakage. Hounsel et al. perform testing in real-time—one of the few studies we reviewed, and the only study at this scope, that managed to do so
[
1
]
. We note, however, that there is an imbalance in the proportion of inactive misinformation and real news sites in the training set for this study—34% of the sites in the misinformation training set were active, while
all
of the websites in the real news training set were active. As result, the classifier might have been overfit to features specific to those inactive websites.

IV-C

Replication Studies

We conduct replicability and reproducibility studies on three different papers within our corpus (our selection criteria are described in
Section

II-B
), at the claim, article, and whole website scopes. We conducted an author outreach survey in lieu of a replication study for user- and post-scoped methods, in light of data accessibility issues (see
Section

IV-B3
). For each work, we evaluate replicability and generalizability (
RQ3
,
RQ4
). Where possible, we 1) replicate reported results; 2) inspect datasets for potential dependencies; 3) perform ablation analyses to understand individual feature performance; and 4) test on current data.

IV-C
1
Ciampaglia et al. (2015)

We replicate the published paper from 2015 by Ciampaglia et al., in which the authors design a knowledge graph-based method called
knowledge linker

[
17
]
for evaluating the veracity of
claims
. In particular, the authors construct confusion matrix and Wikipedia Knowledge Graph (WKG) representations of databases of subject-predicate-object (SPO) statements extracted from Wikipedia information boxes (the authors claim that the “least controversial” information can be found there), then used semantic proximity (derived from path length between subject and object nodes) to assign truth values to individual claims. The authors reported > 0.90 ROC for classifier performance on test sets of statements about country capitals, film directors, and presidential spouses (predicates are
is-capital-of, is-married-to, is-directed-by
); notably, this type of information is likely to be found in a Wikipedia information box, or in the body text of a Wikipedia article about a prominent person or place. While we were able to reproduce a result from the original paper, we were unable to generate an updated confusion matrix on current data, as data pre-processing code was not made available to us.

Reproducibility of published results.
Original data were obtained from the paper authors; we were able to independently locate their method code on
Github
. We replicated the movies and directors confusion matrix presented in the original paper (the link prediction method was broken). We present a heat map representation of this result in
Figure

2
(a larger version of this graphic is in our SI). We note that knowledge linker’s confusion matrix method is predicate-agnostic—high scores only denote high probability of a
connection
, in general, between a subject and an object. The nature of this connection is unspecified. As such, this approach to fact checking surfaces many more
false positives
than
false negatives
. Within the movies and directors test data represented in
Figure

2
, we note four prominent false positive results (annotated in the figure): Francis Ford Coppola
wrote the screenplay for
“Patton” (A); George Cukor
was replaced as a director of
“Gone with the Wind” (B); and Robert Redford
starred in
“The Sting” and “Out of Africa” (C, D). While each person is strongly connected to his respective film, he did not
direct
this film. This approach might be useful for surfacing checkable statements or putative relationships; more granular automated or manual checking of these statements is needed to determine actual factuality, however.

Fig. 2:
Replication analysis of Ciampaglia et al.: Heatmap of movie-director relationships, from a DBpedia dataset. Data is not binarized. Labeled points A, B, C, and D are false positive relationships.

Generalizability of published results.

A KG-based approach has limited generalizability, by design—the breadth of checkable statements is determined by what information is contained within the KG. We can only query relationships between films and their directors within the confusion matrix presented in
Figure

2
; in order to query COVID-19 misinformation, for instance, we would have to construct a new KG from an entirely new set of SPO statements. Knowledge linker’s KG approach has additional shortcomings: The link prediction task is currently agnostic to
negation
, so that
is-A
and
is-not-A
relationships might register as equally likely; and the method is agnostic to non-binary predicates (e.g.,
is-mostly-A
,
is-A-20%-of-the-time
). While Ciampaglia et al. explicitly state that knowledge linker is a proof-of-concept for more sophisticated KG methods, we argue that solely KG-based approaches to misinformation detection are difficult to scale and make generalizable. Designers of KG-based methods necessarily make tradeoffs between the granularity of the relationships hard-coded into KG edges and the speed and generalizability of their approach. Methods incorporating additional textual or contextual features might be faster and more accurate in a rapidly shifting news ecosystem. We discuss these feature types and methods in the sections that follow.

2
Nasir et al. (2021)

We reproduced results from a study published in 2021 by Nasir et al
[
134
]
. The authors propose a neural net-based approach to the classification of
news articles
. The method employs a hybrid deep learning model that combines convolutional and recurrent neural networks for the classification of real and fake news. The authors report results for tests on two datasets: the ISOT dataset (45,000 news stories, equally distributed across true and false categories, as labeled by PolitiFact) and the FA-KES dataset (804 news stories about the Syrian war, 426 true and 376 false)
[
296
,
297

. We were able to replicate original paper results and run the method on updated datasets of news articles.

Reproducibility of published results.

Both ISOT and FA-KES are readily available online, and we were able to run the original method of Nasir et al. without issue
[
296
,
297
]
. The method code was not publicly available online (the authors sent us their code base after we emailed them a request for access). Weights and parameters for their trained model are published in the original paper
[

. We were able to reproduce results originally reported in their published paper (see ISOT-

and FA-KES-

Data dependencies and overfitting on training data.
Nasir et al. report exceptionally high classification precision on the ISOT dataset—approximately 1.00, or nearly absolute accuracy. (By comparison, the classifier has about 0.521 accuracy on the FA-KES news data after training on ISOT.) While the original paper by Nasir et al. explicitly describes the provenance of ISOT data—all

articles were sourced from Reuters—the authors do not mention that the body text of all
true

true
articles. This text was absent from all
false
-labeled articles. While it seems likely that the CNN simply learned a shortcut—the presence of a source name in all factual articles, and the absence of this source name in all misinformative articles—a scrubbed version of the dataset without geotag-Reuters name pairs posted comparable accuracy scores (see ISOT-

Baly et al. (2018)

We reproduce results from a study published in 2018 by Baly et al
[

. In summary: the authors propose a multimodal approach to the classification of

[

Reproducibility of published results.

Classifier code is available on
Github
. All features extracted for the original analysis were captured in a series of json files. On the one hand, this meant that we were able to readily reproduce results reported in the paper; on the other, this meant that certain elements of the dataset (follower counts on social media, Wikipedia page contents) were out of date. As we did not find documentation in the method repository for re-extraction of these features, we were restricted to conducting our tests on data that were already available. We decided to bin bias labels into
left
,
center
, and
right

We labeled all “extreme-left,” “left,” and “left-center” sites as “left”; retained the labels of all “center”-labeled sites; and labeled all “extreme-right,” “right,” and “right-center” sites as “right.” Classifier performance on the resulting three-way bias classification task accords with the results reported by Baly et al. on the same task.

Limited model explainability.

From our reading of the paper by Baly et al. and our analysis of their code base, we find only limited discussion of feature normalization and the contributions of individual feature sets to overall model performance. The authors trained their model on a set of news articles from an undisclosed source; in order to extract feature vectors for the
article
feature set for each site in the EMNLP18 dataset, the authors took the unweighted average of 141 GloVe features over the set of retrieved articles (article selection process is not disclosed). There is no discussion of the semantic features included within this set of 141, or of how these features were chosen.

Target-problem mismatch.
Upon manual review of the original Media Bias/Fact Check dataset, we found that about 13% (137/1066) of websites in the EMNLP18 dataset did not qualify as news websites—and, in fact, MBFC labeled about 80% (109/137) of these as ‘Organizations or Foundations’
[
295

. Many of these websites are operated by think tanks, non-governmental organizations, political action committees, or special interest groups; they include the World Health Organization, Citizens United, and the National Rifle Association. We removed these non-news sites from the EMNLP18 dataset and re-ran the classifier on the sanitized dataset, using all features and five-fold cross-validation. The classifier achieved an accuracy of 0.602 on its fact classification task, about 5% worse than its performance on the full dataset (0.654). It achieved only 0.320 accuracy on its bias classification task—significantly worse than its performance on the full dataset (0.569).

Other characteristics of the websites included in the EMNLP18 dataset, including country of origin and information domain, called into question the appropriateness of the classification task proposed by Baly et al. to the data on hand: About 16% of websites (170/1066) are non-U.S. in origin, and about 3% of websites (27/1066) report on science news. Bias classification would ideally be sensitive to the unique political climates of different nation-states (a “left” label in Egypt is arguably different from a “left” label in Norway, for instance) and the basic relevance of
political
bias, in particular, to the information published on the website (in lieu of a political leaning label, MBFC labels some science news sites as “Pro-science” or “Pseudo-science”). Fact classification would ideally be sensitive to the objectives—stated or not—of published material on these websites, many of which are opinion-editorial sites.

In lieu of pure fact- or bias-classification, it seems likely that the method of Baly et al. learned at least a few shortcuts in the EMNLP18 dataset that serve as confounders to actual fact or bias detection: Absent these signals, the classifier performed appreciably worse on certain tasks. The composition of EMNLP18 suggests that, while the authors intended to build a multimodal classifier, they might have instead constructed a text classifier that learned textual dependencies pertaining to house style, tone, and topic, such as those discussed in our analysis of Nasir et al.

V
DISCUSSION

We focus our discussion of results on those issues identified by our literature review and investigated in greater depth in our replication analyses: fit, data curation, explainability, reproducibility, and generalizability. Additionally, we provide recommendations for evaluating ML-driven trust and safety interventions and link these recommendations to major takeaways of the present study.

V-A

Results

As we discussed in the Introduction to this work, the sheer volume of content posted to social media platforms and on news sites suggests that there might be a real need for fast, automated misinformation detection methods. As we found in the course of our literature review and replication analyses, however, 1) the actual benefits of automated interventions are limited, in certain cases, when weighed against the potential costs of implementation and method error, and 2) there were serious discrepancies between existing detection methods’ proposed and actual performance. The nature of these differences varied by detection target and information scope. We summarize our findings below.

RQ1
:
Fit.
Very few methods that claimed to detect misinformation performed actual fact verification: Instead, they targeted
n
th order proxy signals that were frequently steps away from promised detection targets. These differences were particularly noticeable in methods that relied heavily on text- and network-based features to perform classification. In those cases, semantic/syntactic signatures and propagation patterns served as proxies for the existence of misinformation. We demonstrated, through our own replication studies, that it is easy to circumvent approaches that rely on style-based cues to perform proxy detection. Additionally, in the absence of current or complete social media post data, we argued that most network-based methods are poorly-equipped to perform early detection of false rumors—this is by design, as the propagation patterns these methods purport to detect are frequently only noticeable after a significant degree of rumor spread or virality.

RQ2
:
Data curation and model explainability.
Lack of access to current, well-annotated datasets remains a serious problem for current and future misinformation research. Across existing datasets, taxonomies for classifying misinformation were inconsistent. Testing on contemporaneous data was uncommon among those papers we annotated, and testing in real-time settings was even rarer. Proof-of-concept experiments oftentimes did not control for data dependencies. Authors describing black-box methods—particularly those employing neural nets or other forms of unsupervised learning—did not disclose feature sets retrieved by their methods. Model explainability for these approaches—if available at all—was very limited. For methods employing multimodal feature sets, temporal consistency across disparate feature sets was poorly controlled, and feature normalization was often questionable.

RQ3
:
Reproducibility.
We noted widespread code and data availability issues: Fewer than 30% of our attempts to locate code
and
data, or obtain this information from authors, were successful. The code and datasets we
were
able to retrieve were frequently unusable or out of date. In fact, we were able to reproduce and replicate results on published
and
current data for only one of our replication studies. In that single case, we found that the article-scoped method performed no better than random (0.50 accuracy) on a current, mixed-domain dataset (
Section

IV-C2
). Additionally, for the significant number of methods that purported to detect false rumors on Twitter, the shutdown of the Twitter Academic API poses an existential risk to reproducibility of results and usefulness of dehydrated datasets.

RQ4
:
Generalizability.
Methods that were trained on single-source or single-domain datasets appeared to perform well on data from the same source, or within the same domain; these methods, unsurprisingly, performed poorly on multi-source or out-of-domain topics. These discrepancies are closely tied to undisclosed or uncontrolled-for data dependencies, which we discuss in RQ2. Though we did not attempt to test detection methods on out-of-scope data—e.g., we did not test claim-scoped methods on datasets of whole news articles—it seems unlikely that these methods would fare well in out-of-scope contexts, particularly in light of their already-poor performance on in-scope datasets.

V-B

Recommendations for practitioners

We offer the following recommendations for approaching and implementing potential ML-driven interventions in trust and safety operations. We relate each recommendation to a finding from our literature review or replication studies.

Evaluate intervention fit and detection targets at the point of problem formulation.

More than 40% of article-scoped methods detect nth order targets for misinformation. Some of these proxy targets, including house style and text sentiment, are easily circumvented (
Section

IV-C2
).
What signals—or proxy signals—will the intervention detect? What are the potential risks of a false negative or false positive result (and are they worth the potential benefits of e.g., greater speed)? What is the actual detection target, and how closely does it approximate the classification task to be performed? In an adversarial setting, would a malicious actor be able to circumvent the automation?

Test on current data, and in real time.

Fewer than 10% of methods within our whole corpus tested on contemporaneous data, and fewer than 5% tested in a real-time setting.
To understand how an ML intervention might work
in practice
, we recommend testing in a real-time context—or, at the very least, on a current dataset. As much as possible, the testing environment should emulate the deployment setting.

Consider possible dependencies and confounders in training and test data.

In our replication analyses of Baly et al. and Nasir et al., we observed instances of possible shortcut learning that inflated model performance on experimental datasets.
Carefully evaluate training and test datasets for dependencies that might become false signals or sources of noise, that might limit the generalizability of the classifier, or that might induce unexpected shortcuts during the model training process. Remove (or otherwise account for) these dependencies as possible.

Make model explainability a priority.

In our replication analysis of Baly et al., we noted serious issues with model explainability and feature normalization: Contributions of individual feature types to overall model performance were unclear, and the addition of non-textual feature sets did not appear to improve classification performance by a significant margin.
We recommend that authors prioritize model explainability at the point of design and construction. In the case of methods employing multimodal feature sets, we recommend that authors 1) make explicit the normalization process employed by their method to preprocess data and produce feature vectors, 2) clearly describe the contribution of individual feature sets to overall model performance.

Make code and data freely available.

We noted widespread issues with data availability and code functionality in our author outreach and replication studies (
Section

IV-C
). For methods that claim to conduct actual fact-verification, third-party reference databases should be current and comprehensive.
Maintain and update code and data described in published work. Clearly describe data provenance and code functionality in code releases, and include instructions for use.
