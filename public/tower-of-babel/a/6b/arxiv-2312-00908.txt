Non-standard Stochastic Control with Nonlinear Feynman-Kac Costs
René Carmona & Mathieu Laurière & Pierre-Louis Lions

Abstract
. We consider the conditional control problem introduced by P.L. Lions in his lectures at the Collège de France in November 2016. In his lectures, Lions emphasized some of the major differences with the analysis of classical stochastic optimal control problems, and in so doing, raised the question of the possible differences between the value functions resulting from optimization over the class of Markovian controls as opposed to the general family of open loop controls. The goal of the paper is to elucidate this quandary and provide elements of response to Lions’ original conjecture. First, we justify the mathematical formulation of the conditional control problem by the description of practical model from evolutionary biology. Next, we relax the original formulation by the introduction of soft as opposed to hard killing, and using a mimicking argument, we reduce the open loop optimization problem to an optimization over a specific class of feedback controls. After proving existence of optimal feedback control functions, we prove a superposition principle allowing us to recast the original stochastic control problems as deterministic control problems for dynamical systems of probability Gibbs measures. Next, we characterize the solutions by forward-backward systems of coupled non-linear Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) very much in the spirit of the Mean Field Game (MFG) systems. From there, we identify a common optimizer, proving the conjecture of equality of the value functions. Finally we illustrate the results by convincing numerical experiments.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the conditional control problem introduced by P.L. Lions in his lectures at the Collège de France in November 2016. See
[
15
]
. As originally stated, the problem does not fit in the usual categories of stochastic control problems considered in the literature, so its solution requires new ideas, if not new technology. In his lectures, Lions emphasized some of the major differences with the analysis of classical stochastic optimal control problems, and in so doing, raised the question of the possible differences between the value functions resulting from optimization over the class of Markovian controls as opposed to the general family of open loop controls merely assumed to be adapted. The equality of the values of these optimization problems is accepted as a
folk theorem
in the classical theory of stochastic control. However, optimizing an objective function whose values strongly depend upon the past history of the controlled trajectories of the system is a strong argument in favor of differences between the optimization results over these two classes of control processes. The goal of this paper is to elucidate this quandary and provide elements of response to Lions’ original conjecture.

A standard stochastic control problem is concerned with the minimization of an expected cost when the latter is incurred by a controller, and is aggregated over a specific time horizon.
In the problem considered in this paper, the aggregation over time is done through the integral over the time horizon of
conditional
expectations of instantaneous and terminal costs. Instead of accumulating expected costs incurred by controlling a single stochastic process over time, the distribution of the process over which the expectation is computed changes at each time because of the conditional nature of the expectations. Intuitively, at each time
t
, the expected running cost at that time could be interpreted as the limit of an expectation over a large particle system of the Fleming-Viot type. See for example
[
9
]
. Still, there does not seem to be a single particle system which could be used for different times.

This formulation of the optimization is highly unusual. For this reason, we provide a simple model of evolutionary biology to motivate and justify the mathematical formulation of the optimization problem. We argue that egalitarian resource sharing leads naturally to the formulation of a fitness criterion in terms of time aggregation of conditional expectations of instantaneous rewards. Such a special form of egalitarian cooperation has been observed in many species (see for example the study of social spiders in
[
11
]
).

The purpose of the present paper is to provide a thorough analysis of an instance of this new form of conditional control with complete proofs. Our approach is to work with a relaxed version of the problem in which we replace the hard conditioning of Lions original proposal, by a soft conditioning. Still, the main thrust of the paper is to highlight and take advantage of the role played by the distribution of the state in the evaluation of the costs. In both cases (feedback Markovian and open loop controls), we reformulate the problem as a standard control problem in infinite dimensions, the controlled dynamics being given by the time evolution of a flow of probability measures obtained by distorting and renormalizing the original distributions of the state, pretty much in the same way Gibbs measures are introduced in statistical physics. See for example
[
17
]
.

Given the introduction of the problem in
[
15
]
and the work
[
1
]
on the large time asymptotics of the Markovian case, the contributions of the present paper are summarized in the following list:
1) justification of the mathematical formulation of the conditional control problem by the description of practical models from evolutionary biology (see Section
2.2
);
2) relaxation of the original exit problem formulation into the analysis of smoother (distorted) Feynman-Kac semigroups (see Section
2.3
);
3) reformulation in the case of Markovian feedback controls, of the original stochastic control problems as a deterministic control problems over a space of probability measures, the time evolution of the states being given by controlled dynamical systems of Gibbs measures;
4) proof of a non-local superposition principle in Subsection
3.3
, guaranteeing that the deterministic formulation is equivalent to the original optimization problem;
5) existence of optimal feedback control functions, derivation of a form of maximum principle; 6) characterization of optimality by a forward-backward system of non-linear, non-local partial differential equations (PDEs) very much in the spirit of the Mean Field Game (MFG) systems
[
14
]
, and analysis of the system (see Sections
3.1
and
4.4
);
7) introduction of mimicking arguments to reduce the open loop optimization problem to an optimization over a class of feedback controls (see Theorem
3
);
8) characterization of the solutions by similar forward-backward systems of coupled non-linear Partial Differential Equations (see Sections
3.6.6
and
4.4.3
);
9) identification of the PDE systems and proof of the value equality conjecture (see Theorem
4
); and finally,
9) convincing numerical experiments illustrating the validity of our result (see Section
5
).
We managed to prove the results we were after without proving existence and uniqueness of solutions of the forward-backward system of non-linear, non-local PDEs characterizing optimality. So for the sake of completeness, we provide a complete proof of existence and uniqueness of classical solutions for small data, namely short time horizon and small terminal condition.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the problem of optimal control with conditional exit, we provide a motivation, and we propose an approximate problem with nonlinear Feynman-Kac semigroups, on which we focus in the sequel. In Section
3
, we present a detailed analysis of the problem with feedback Markovian controls. Among other things, we prove existence of optimal controls and a non-local superposition principle of independent interest, see Theorem
1
in Subsection
3.3
, and a form of maximum principle tailored to the present non-local dynamical equations. There, we also provide a viscosity analysis of the forward-backward PDE system characterizing the optimum. In Section
4
, we analyze the problem with open-loop controls: we show the equivalence with feedback Markovian controls for an extended state leading to our proof of the existence of a common optimal control, and consequently of the equality of the value functions. We conclude with Section
5
reporting on numerical experiments corroborating our theoretical result. As mentioned above the appendix contains a detailed proof of the well posedness (i.e. existence and uniqueness of a classical solution) for the fundamental forward-backward PDE system in the case of small data (i.e. time horizon and terminal condition).

2.
The Conditional Exit Control Problem

For the sake of definiteness, we review the conditional control problem originally introduced by P.L. Lions.
Let
D
be a bounded open domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a smooth boundary
\partial D
. Let us
denote by
C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of continuous functions of time
t\in[0,\infty)
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and by
C_{0}([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
the subspace of those functions
x\in C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
x(0)=0
. If
x\in C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we denote by
\tau^{x}_{D}
the first exit time of the path
x
from
D
, namely the quantity:

(1)

\tau^{x}_{D}=\inf\{t\geq 0;\,x(t)\notin D\}

with the convention that
\inf\emptyset=\infty
. We shall skip the superscript
x
and/or the subscript
D
when their values are clear from the context.

2.1.
The Optimization Problem

We consider an optimization problem which is underpinned by a controlled state process
\mathbf{X}=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
whose dynamics are given by:

(2)

dX_{t}=\alpha_{t}dt+\sigma dW_{t}

(3)

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|\alpha_{t}|^{p}dt<\infty,

for some
p\geq 1
, and we shall use
p=2
most often.

The goal of the optimization problem is to minimize a cost
J^{\tau}(\bm{\alpha})
associated to the control process
\bm{\alpha}
. This cost is derived from a running cost function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and a terminal cost function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
(whose regularity properties will be specified later on) in the form:

(4)

\begin{split}J^{\tau}(\bm{\alpha})&=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t},\alpha_{t})|\tau^{X}_{D}\geq t]\;dt+\mathbb{E}[g(X_{T})|\tau^{X}_{D}\geq T]\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[f(X_{t},\alpha_{t})\textbf{1}_{\tau^{X}_{D}\geq t}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{P}[\tau^{X}_{D}\geq t]}dt+\frac{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[g(X_{T})\textbf{1}_{\tau^{X}_{D}\geq T}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{P}[\tau^{X}_{D}\geq T]}.\end{split}

We shall assume that for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the function
A\ni\alpha\mapsto f(x,\alpha)
is convex.
Moreover, for illustration purposes, we shall often restrict ourselves to the case of separable running cost functions
f
of the form:

(5)

f(x,\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}+\tilde{f}(x)

for some bounded measurable function
\tilde{f}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.2.
Evolutionary Biology Motivation

The mathematical formulation of the above optimization problem is non-standard, and except for the original lectures of P.L. Lions
[
15
]
and the numerical experiments presented in
[
1
]
, we do not know of any reported mathematical analysis of such a model. However, we claim that it is very natural from the perspective of the study of populations of altruistic individuals in high resource environments practicing egalitarian resource sharing. The analysis of the evolution of these populations could be based on dynamical models of the following type. We consider the evolution of identical individuals foraging for food independently of each other, in a safe territory. We assume that the outcome of foraging is random, and that at the end of each time period (one can think of a period as the weaning period for an offspring generation) the food is shared among the surviving individuals in an egalitarian manner which allots the same amount of food to each member still alive.

Let us denote by
D
the territory, and let us assume that the individuals disappear or die when they leave the territory.
If for
i=1,\cdots,N_{t}
we denote by
X^{i}_{t}
the positions at time
t
of the
N_{t}
individuals still alive at the beginning of period
t+1
, we assume that foraging will take those who survive (i.e. do not exit the territory) to positions
X^{i}_{t+1}
at the end of the period, and that they will have accumulated the amount
f(X_{t}^{i})
of resources (say food for example). Resource sharing takes place in the following form: all the resources are first aggregated, and then redistributed in equal amounts to the surviving members of the population. In other words, the resource allocated to each individual still alive is:

\frac{1}{N_{t+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t+1}}f(X^{i}_{t+1}).

So an individual still alive at the end of the
T
-th period will have benefitted from the resources:

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{1}{N_{t+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t+1}}f(X^{i}_{t+1}).

Not surprisingly, in the limit of small foraging periods, the summation over time will converge toward the integral between
0
and
T
of the resource enjoyed at time
t
. What is more interesting is the form of the integrand when the size, say
N
, of the population increases. Indeed, notice first that the ratio
N_{t}/N
converges toward the probability that a typical individual is still alive at time
t
, in other words
\mathbb{P}[\tau>t]
if we use the notation
\tau
for the time of death of the individual. Recall that the latter is the first exit time of the domain
D
. Next, the quantity

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}f(X^{i}_{t})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(X^{i}_{t})\textbf{1}_{\tau(X^{i})>t}

converges toward
\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t})\textbf{1}_{\tau>t}]
and the integrand at time
t
is indeed given by the conditional expectation of the resource at time
t
given that the individual is still alive at that time. Optimization of the fitness of the individuals still alive naturally leads to the conditional control problem which we propose to study in this paper.

2.3.
Control of Nonlinear Feynman-Kac Semigroups

We shall not solve the model with hard killing through the exit time introduced above. Instead, we shall provide a complete analysis of a relaxed version of the model based on
soft killing
.
Indeed, it is natural to consider the following generalization of the original model proposed by P.L. Lions.
Working with the same basic controlled state equation (
2
), we can generalize the conditioning by considering a measurable function
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto[0,\infty]
which we assume to be non-negative for the sake of simplicity.

V
could as well be bounded below, or even have some negative singularities of a specific type, but we shall not worry about this type of generality in this paper.
The goal of the new formulation of the control problem is still to minimize a cost
J^{V}(\bm{\alpha})
associated to a control process
\bm{\alpha}
, and this cost is still derived from a running cost function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and a terminal cost function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
in the form:

(6)

J^{V}(\bm{\alpha})=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[f(X_{t},\alpha_{t})e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\Bigr]}dt+\frac{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[g(X_{T})e^{-\int_{0}^{T}V(X_{s})ds}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{T}V(X_{s})ds}\Bigr]}.

Lions’ model based on conditioning the state to remain in a given domain
D
is recovered by considering the function
V=V^{\infty}
given by:

(7)

V^{\infty}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x\in D\\
\infty&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

in which case:

(8)

\int_{0}^{t}V^{\infty}(X_{s})ds=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }X_{s}\in\overline{D},\;0\leq s\leq t\\
\infty&\text{if }X_{s}\notin\overline{D}\text{ for some }0\leq s\leq t,\end{cases}

so that:

e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V^{\infty}(X_{s})ds}=\textbf{1}_{[X_{s}\in\overline{D},\;0\leq s\leq t]}=\textbf{1}_{[\tau_{D}\geq t]},

where
\tau_{D}=\tau^{X}_{D}
is the first exit time of the domain
D
defined in (
1
).
Accordingly:

(9)

J^{V^{\infty}}(\bm{\alpha})=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[f(X_{t},\alpha_{t})\big|\tau_{D}\geq t\Bigr]dt+\mathbb{E}\Bigl[g(X_{T})\big|\tau_{D}\geq T\Bigr],

which is indeed the case considered earlier in (
4
). In what follows, we approximate
V^{\infty}
by potential functions
V^{n}=nV^{1}
where
V^{1}
is a continuous approximation of the indicator function of the domain
D
. To be specific, we choose
V^{1}(x)=\chi^{\epsilon}(d(x,D))
where
d(x,D)
denotes the distance from
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to the domain
D
,
\epsilon>0
is an arbitrary fixed number whose specific value will not matter, and
\chi^{\epsilon}
is the continuous function:

(10)

\chi^{\epsilon}(d)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }d\leq 0\\
\text{linear }&\text{if }0\leq d\leq\epsilon\\
1&\text{if }d\geq\epsilon.\end{cases}

Notice that since we assume that the boundary
\partial D
is smooth, we can modify
\chi^{\epsilon}(d)
when
0\leq d\leq\epsilon
in such a way that
\chi^{\epsilon}
can also be assumed to be smooth.
The choice of this family of potential functions is justified by the following simple result.

Lemma 1
.

If
\mathbf{X}=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies
X_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\alpha_{s}ds+W_{t}
for some
x_{0}\in D
and
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is admissible, then for any bounded function
g

(11)

\mathbb{E}[g(X_{T})\;|\;\tau_{D}>T]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[g(X_{T})e^{-n\int_{0}^{T}V^{1}(X_{s})ds}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-n\int_{0}^{T}V^{1}(X_{s})ds}]}.

Similarly, if
\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[|f(X_{t},\alpha_{t})|]dt<\infty
, we also have:

(12)

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t},\alpha_{t})\;|\;\tau_{D}>t]dt=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t},\alpha_{t})e^{-n\int_{0}^{t}V^{1}(X_{s})ds}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-n\int_{0}^{t}V^{1}(X_{s})ds}]}.

Proof.

Notice that if
\tau_{D}\geq T
,
X_{t}\in D
for
0\leq t\leq T
and
\int_{0}^{T}V^{1}(X_{s})ds=0
. On the other hand, if
\tau_{D}<T
, the set of times
t\leq T
for which
X_{t}\notin D
is of positive Lebesgue’s measure which implies that
\int_{0}^{T}V^{1}(X_{s})ds>0
since
V^{1}(x)>0
if
x\notin D
. Consequently,

\lim_{n\to\infty}e^{-n\int_{0}^{T}V^{1}(X_{s})ds}=\textbf{1}_{\tau_{D}\geq T}

and since
g
is bounded, Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem gives:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[g(X_{T})e^{-n\int_{0}^{T}V^{1}(X_{s})ds}]=\mathbb{E}[g(X_{T})\textbf{1}_{\tau_{D}\geq T}]

and using the same result with
g\equiv 1
for the denominator, we get the desired limit (
11
). The argument needed for the proof of (
12
) involving the running cost is exactly the same.
∎

Remark 1
.

Notice that the above lemma does not say anything when
x_{0}\notin D
. Indeed in this case, since the boundary is smooth, we have
\tau_{D}=0
almost surely, and the left hand sides of (
11
) and (
12
) are referring to conditional expectations with respect to an event of probability zero.

The advantages of a soft killing given by a bounded continuous potential function
V
are twofold: 1) it sets all the equations in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and avoids having to deal with boundary conditions on
\partial D
; 2) it makes technical proofs easier as it provides continuity with respect to the time variable
t
which may not be available otherwise. To be specific, we shall make the following assumptions.

2.4.
Assumptions

Our analysis is predicated on the following assumptions which will be in force throughout the remainder of the paper, even if many of the individual results still hold under weaker conditions.

Assumption 1

The running cost and the terminal cost functions satisfy (recall that the action space

The function

2.5.
Approximation by Bounded Controls

\begin{split}|(i)|&\leq\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl|\tilde{f}(X_{t})-\tilde{f}(X^{K}_{t})\bigr|e^{-A_{t}}\bigr]dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl(|\alpha_{t}|^{2}-|\alpha^{K}_{t}|^{2}\bigr)e^{-A_{t}}\bigr]dt\\
&\leq e^{T}\|\tilde{f}\|_{Lip1}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|X_{t}-X^{K}_{t}|dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl||\alpha_{t}|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{|\alpha_{t}|>K}e^{-A_{t}}\bigr]dt\\
&\leq Te^{T}\|\tilde{f}\|_{Lip1}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|\alpha_{t}|\mathbf{1}_{|\alpha_{t}|>K}dt+\frac{e^{T}}{2}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|\alpha_{t}|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{|\alpha_{t}|>K}dt\\
&\leq\frac{e^{T}}{2}\varepsilon(K)+T^{3/2}e^{T}\|\tilde{f}\|_{Lip1}\varepsilon(K)^{1/2}\end{split}

if we use the notation introduced in (
16
) for
\varepsilon(K)
,
and use Hölder’s inequality. Similarly

(22)

\begin{split}|(ii)|&\leq e^{T}\|g\|_{Lip1}\mathbb{E}[|X_{T}-X^{K}_{T}|]\\
&\leq e^{T}\|g\|_{Lip1}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|\alpha_{t}|\mathbf{1}_{|\alpha_{t}|>K}dt\\
&\leq e^{T}\|g\|_{Lip1}\varepsilon(K)^{1/2}.\end{split}

Next

(23)

\begin{split}|(iii)|&\leq\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[|\tilde{f}(X^{K}_{t})|\Bigl|\frac{e^{-A_{t}}}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}-\frac{e^{-A^{K}_{t}}}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{K}_{t}}]}\Bigr|\Bigr]dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[|\alpha^{K}_{t}|^{2}\Bigl|\frac{e^{-A_{t}}}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}-\frac{e^{-A^{K}_{t}}}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{K}_{t}}]}\Bigr|\Bigr]dt\\
&\leq\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}\mathbb{E}[|e^{-A_{t}}-e^{-A^{K}_{t}}|]+\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{K}_{t}}]\Bigl(\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{K}_{t}}]}\Bigr)\Bigr)dt\\
&\hskip 45.0pt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{K}_{t}}]}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[|\alpha^{K}_{t}|^{2}\Bigl|e^{-A_{t}}-e^{-A^{K}_{t}}\Bigr|\Bigr]dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[|\alpha^{K}_{t}|^{2}e^{-A_{t}}\Bigl|\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{K}_{t}}]}\Bigr|\Bigr]dt\\
&\leq 2e^{T}\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[|e^{-A_{t}}-e^{-A^{K}_{t}}|]dt\\
&\hskip 45.0pt+\frac{e^{T}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[|\alpha^{K}_{t}|^{2}|e^{-A_{t}}-e^{-A^{K}_{t}}|\Bigr]dt+\frac{e^{T}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[|\alpha^{K}_{t}|^{2}\frac{e^{-A_{t}}}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}\Bigr]\mathbb{E}[|e^{-A_{t}}-e^{-A^{K}_{t}}|]dt\\
&\leq\bigl(2e^{T}\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}+\frac{T^{3/2}}{2}e^{2T}\|V\|_{Lip1}\varepsilon(K)^{1/2}+\frac{e^{T}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[|\alpha^{K}_{t}|^{2}|e^{-A_{t}}-e^{-A^{K}_{t}}|\Bigr]dt.\end{split}

Finally

(24)

\begin{split}|(iv)|&\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[|g(X^{K}_{t})|\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{T}}]}|e^{-A_{T}}-e^{-A^{K}_{T}}|\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[|g(X^{K}_{t})|\;e^{-A^{K}_{T}}\bigl|\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{T}}]}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{K}_{T}}]}\bigr|\bigr]\\
&\leq\|g\|_{\infty}e^{T}\mathbb{E}[|e^{-A_{T}}-e^{-A^{K}_{T}}|]+\|g\|_{\infty}e^{2T}\mathbb{E}[|e^{-A_{T}}-e^{-A^{K}_{T}}|]\\
&\leq 2\|g\|_{\infty}e^{2T}T\|V\|_{Lip1}\varepsilon(K)^{1/2}.\end{split}

Putting together (
21
), (
22
), (
23
), and (
24
) gives the desired estimate (
15
). Clearly,

\lim_{K\nearrow\infty}\varepsilon(K)=0,

and we already argued that
\varpi(K)
also converges to
0
.
∎

The first obvious consequence of the above result is that the search for the infimum

(25)

J^{V*}=\inf_{\bm{\alpha}}J^{V}(\bm{\alpha})

over all the adapted processes satisfying the integrability condition (
3
) with
p=2
can be restricted to bounded control processes
\bm{\alpha}
. However, if and when we are able to prove that this infimum is attained, it will require extra work to prove that the minimizer is in fact a bounded control.

3.
The Case of Markovian Feedback Controls

3.1.
Formulation of the problem

In this section, we restrict the optimization problem to controls processes
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
of the form:

(26)

\alpha_{t}=\phi_{t}(X_{t})

where
\phi:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto A
is a (deterministic) measurable function, and
\mathbf{X}=(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
(which we shall sometimes denote
\mathbf{X}^{\phi}
to emphasize the dependence upon the feedback function
\phi
) satisfies:

(27)

dX_{t}=\phi_{t}(X_{t})dt+dW_{t}.

We will refer to such controls as
Markovian
controls or
feedback
controls.
For a given feedback control function
\phi
, the existence of the controlled state process requires the solution of a stochastic differential equation. So in order to be admissible, a feedback function should be at a minimum, a measurable functions
\phi
for which such a solution exists and satisfies

(28)

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\int_{0}^{T}|\phi_{t}(X_{t})|^{p}\Bigr]<\infty,

with
p=2
in order for the objective function we plan to minimize to make sense.
We shall denote by
\Phi^{(p)}
the set of admissible feedback control functions, namely the set of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued measurable functions
\phi

on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which the stochastic differential equation (
27
) has a weak solution satisfying (
28
).
In some cases, we shall make a stronger assumption on the feedback controls. For this reason, we introduce the space
\Phi^{(\infty)}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued bounded measurable functions on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In dimension
d=1
, all the measurable bounded functions
\phi
are in all the
\Phi^{(p)}
because of the classical result of Zvonkin
[
22
]
which guarantees existence of strong solutions for (
27
). In dimension
d>1
existence and uniqueness of a strong solutions still hold for bounded drifts because the volatility is the identity matrix. More general volatility terms could be accommodated by Veretennikov’s extension
[
21
]
which provides a large class of bounded measurable functions
\phi
for which the result still holds in more general non-degenerate cases. However, as we shall point out in several instances, our main requirement is the existence of weak solutions, and this is guaranteed under much weaker conditions on
\phi
as long as the volatility is the identity matrix.

Throughout the section, we alternatively use the notation
\bm{\phi}
or
\bm{\alpha}
for the control, even if
\phi
is only the feedback function determining the actual control process
\bm{\alpha}
through the solution of the equation (
27
).

3.2.
A Non-local Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov (FPK) Equation

Given the form of the running and terminal costs, the objective function (
6
) can be rewritten in the form:

(29)

J(\phi)=\int_{0}^{T}\int f(x,\phi_{t}(x))\mu_{t}(dx)\;dt+\int g(x)\mu_{T}(dx),

where we use the notation
\mu_{t}
for the probability measure:

(30)

\mu_{t}(dx)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\delta_{X_{t}}(dx)e^{-A_{t}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]},\hskip 20.00003pt0\leq t\leq T,

where from now on, we use the notation
A_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds
. Notice that
t\mapsto\mu_{t}
is continuous for the topology of weak convergence of probability measures, so in the sequel, we shall restrict ourselves to continuous flows of probability measures.

Lemma 3
.

The measure valued function
t\mapsto\mu_{t}
defined in (
30
) satisfies the (non-local) forward Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov (FPK) equation:

(31)

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\mu_{t}-\div(\phi_{t}\mu_{t})-(V-<\mu_{t},V>)\mu_{t},

in the sense of Schwartz distributions.

The notation
<\mu,V>
used in formula (
31
) stands for
<\mu,V>=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\mu(dx)
.

Proof.

If
\varphi
is a smooth function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with compact support, Itô’s formula gives:

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}<\mu_{t},\varphi>&=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{t})e^{-A_{t}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}\\
&=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\varphi(X_{t})+\phi_{t}(X_{t})\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi(X_{t})\Bigr)e^{-A_{t}}\\
&\hskip 75.0pt-\varphi(X_{t})V(X_{t})e^{-A_{t}}\Bigr]+\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{t})e^{-A_{t}}]\frac{\mathbb{E}[V(X_{t})e^{-A_{t}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]^{2}}\\
&=<\mu_{t},\;\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\varphi+\phi_{t}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi-(V-<\mu_{t},V>)\varphi>\\
&=<\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\mu_{t}-\div_{x}(\phi_{t}\mu_{t})-(V-<\mu_{t},V>)\mu_{t},\;\varphi>\end{split}

where we used stochastic integration by parts and the fact that
\varphi
has compact support.
∎

For general existence and uniqueness results for classical, i.e. without the third term in the right hand side of (
31
), FPK equations, together with existence and regularity results for possible density for the solutions, we refer the interested reader to
[
5
, Chapter 6]
and references therein. However, these results are not general enough to cover the case of equation (
31
) of interest to us. Indeed, as far as we can tell, the mean field nature of equation (
31
) makes it escape the realm of those results. For the purpose of our analysis, we proceed in the following way. Given a measurable feedback function
\phi
for which there exists a weak solution
X_{t}
of the stochastic differential equation

(32)

dX_{t}=\phi_{t}(X_{t})dt+dW_{t},

Lemma
3
guarantees that the flow of probability measures
\mu_{t}
defined by (
30
) with
A_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds

is continuous and is a solution of (
31
). This is mostly what we shall need for the existence of solutions of (
31
). Still the following properties of such solutions will come handy in the sequel.

Proposition 1
.

Let us assume that
\bm{\phi}=\bigl(\phi_{t}(x)\bigr)_{0\leq t\leq T,\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued measurable function on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is a measurable flow of probability measures satisfying

(33)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi_{t}(x)|^{p}\mu_{t}(dx)dt<\infty,

for some
p\geq 1
. If
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
solves the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation (
31
) in the sense of distributions, then

(i)

\bm{\mu}(dx,dt)=\mu_{t}(dx)dt=\rho_{t}(x)dxdt
for some non-negative measurable function
\rho\in L^{r}_{loc}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every
r\in[1,(d+2)^{\prime})
.

(ii)

If
p>d+2
, the density
\rho
can be chosen to belong to the Sobolev spaces
\mathcal{H}^{1,p}(I\times K)
for every closed interval
I\subset(0,T)
and compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(iii)

If
\phi
is bounded, the continuous version of the density is strictly positive, hence bounded below away from
0
on every closed interval
I\subset(0,T)
and compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

We use a prime
p^{\prime}
to denote the conjugate exponent of
p\in[0,\infty)
. (i) is a direct consequence of
[
5
, Corollary 6.3.2]
with
c=V-<\mu_{t},V>
. (ii) is a direct consequence of
[
5
, Corollary 6.4.3]
, given the fact that we use the definition of the space
\mathcal{H}^{1,p}
which can be found on page 245 of this book. As for (iii), it follows directly from the properties of
\nu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t})
.
∎

3.3.
A Form of Superposition Principle

In this subsection, we start with a couple
(\bm{\phi},\bm{\mu})
where
\bm{\phi}=\bigl(\phi_{t}(x)\bigr)_{0\leq t\leq T,\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued measurable function on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is a measurable flow of probability measures satisfying

(34)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\;|\phi_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu_{t}(dx)dt<\infty

and the non-local FPK equation (
31
), and we construct a stochastic process
\mathbf{X}=(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
solution of the state stochastic differential equation (
27
) which is related to
\mu_{t}
through formula (
30
). This result should be viewed as a non-linear or non-local superposition principle in the spirit of the superposition principle proved by Trevisan in
[
20
]
in the classical case.

Notice that since the potential function
V
is bounded, assumption (
34
) is equivalent to our prior assumption (
28
).

Lemma 4
.

If
(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
solves the FPK equation (
31
), then the flow
(\gamma_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
of non-negative measures defined by

(35)

\gamma_{t}=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}<\mu_{s},V>ds}\mu_{t}

is the unique solution of the linear PDE

(36)

\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\gamma_{t}-\div(\phi_{t}\gamma_{t})-V\gamma_{t}

with initial condition
\gamma_{0}=\mu_{0}
.

Proof.

If
\gamma_{t}
is defined by (
35
) in Lemma
4
, and
\varphi
is a test function, we have

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}<\gamma_{t},\varphi>&=<\mu_{t},V>e^{\int_{0}^{t}<\mu_{s},V>ds}<\mu_{t},\varphi>+e^{\int_{0}^{t}<\mu_{s},V>ds}\frac{d}{dt}<\mu_{t},\varphi>\\
&=-<\mu_{t},V><\gamma_{t},\varphi>+e^{-\int_{0}^{t}<\mu_{s},V>ds}<\mu_{t},\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi+\phi_{t}\nabla\varphi-(V-<\mu_{t},V>)\varphi>\\
&=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}<\mu_{s},V>ds}<\mu_{t},\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi+\phi_{t}\nabla\varphi-V\varphi>\\
&=<\gamma_{t},\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi+\phi_{t}\nabla\varphi-V\varphi>.\end{split}

Uniqueness is a consequence of
[
5
, Theorem 9.3.6]
and the fact that
\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi_{t}(x)|\gamma_{t}(dx)dt<\infty
which is implied by our assumption (
34
) and the definition (
35
).
∎

Lemma 5
.

There exists a weak solution
(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
of the stochastic differential equation (
27
) for which

(37)

\gamma_{t}(dx)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\delta_{X_{t}}(dx)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr].

Proof.

For each
\epsilon>0
, let
\rho_{\epsilon}
be the
d
-dimensional Gaussian density with mean
0
and variance
\epsilon
times the identity matrix in dimension
d
, and for each
t\in[0,T]
, let

(38)

\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon}=\gamma_{t}*\rho_{\epsilon},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\phi_{t}^{\epsilon}=\frac{d\bigl((\phi_{t}\gamma_{t})*\rho_{\epsilon}\bigr)}{d(\gamma_{t}*\rho_{\epsilon})},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptV^{\epsilon}_{t}=\frac{d\bigl((V\gamma_{t})*\rho_{\epsilon}\bigr)}{d(\gamma_{t}*\rho_{\epsilon})}.

\gamma^{\epsilon}_{t}
is the unique solution of the linear FPK equation

(39)

\partial\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon}-\div(\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon})-V^{\epsilon}_{t}\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon}.

Indeed, for each test function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}
we have:

\begin{split}\partial_{t}<\varphi,\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon}>&=\partial_{t}<\varphi*\rho_{\epsilon},\gamma_{t}>\\
&=<\frac{1}{2}\Delta(\varphi*\rho_{\epsilon})+\phi_{t}\nabla(\varphi*\rho_{\epsilon})-V(\varphi*\rho_{\epsilon}),\gamma_{t}>\\
&=<\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi,\gamma_{t}*\rho_{\epsilon}>+<\nabla\varphi,(\phi_{t}\gamma_{t})*\rho_{\epsilon}>-<\varphi,(V\gamma_{t})*\rho_{\epsilon}>\\
&=<\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi+\frac{d\bigl((\phi_{t}\gamma_{t})*\rho_{\epsilon}\bigr)}{d\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon}}\nabla\varphi-\frac{d\bigl((V\gamma_{t})*\rho_{\epsilon}\bigr)}{d\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon}}\varphi,\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon}>\\
&=<\varphi,\frac{1}{2}\Delta\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon}-\div\bigl(\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon}\bigr)-V_{t}^{\epsilon}\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon}>.\end{split}

Using
[
3
, Lemma 8.1.10]
we get

(40)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\bm{\phi}_{t}^{\epsilon}|^{2}d\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon}dt\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\bm{\phi}_{t}|^{2}d\gamma_{t}dt\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\bm{\phi}_{t}|^{2}d\mu_{t}dt<\infty

because of (
34
). Using once more
[
5
, Theorem 9.3.6]
, we conclude that for each
\epsilon>0
,
(\gamma_{t}^{\epsilon})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is the unique solution of (
39
).

For each
\epsilon>0
,
[
5
, Theorem 6.6.2]
gives existence of a narrowly continuous flow
(\nu^{\epsilon}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
of probability measures satisfying

\partial_{t}\nu^{\epsilon}_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\nu^{\epsilon}_{t}-\div(\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}\nu^{\epsilon}_{t})

and using the superposition theorem (see for example
[
20
, Theorem 2.5]
) we deduce the existence of a probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}
solving the martingale problem in the sense that for every test function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(41)

\varphi(X_{t})-\varphi(X_{0})-\int_{0}^{t}[\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi(X_{s})+\phi^{\epsilon}_{s}(X_{s})\nabla\varphi(X_{s})]ds

where
(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is the coordinate process on
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is a
\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}
-martingale for the canonical filtration of
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and for each
t\in[0,T]
,
\nu^{\epsilon}_{t}
is the law of
X_{t}
under
\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}
.

We conclude the proof assuming momentarily the result of Lemma
6
below.

\mathbb{P}
being a solution of the martingale problem for the drift
\phi_{t}
, for each test function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, integration by parts implies that

(42)

\varphi(X_{t})e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}-\varphi(X_{0})-\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{0}^{s}V(X_{r})dr}\bigl[\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi(X_{s})+\phi_{s}(X_{s})\nabla\varphi(X_{s})-V(X_{s})\varphi(X_{s})\bigr]ds

is a zero-expectation
\mathbb{P}
-martingale. So if we define the flow of measures
(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
by

\tilde{\gamma}_{t}(dx)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[\delta_{X_{t}}(dx)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]

then, taking
\mathbb{P}
-expectation of (
42
) we get

<\varphi,\tilde{\gamma}_{t}>=<\varphi,\tilde{\gamma}_{0}>+\int_{0}^{t}<\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi+\phi_{t}\nabla\varphi-V\varphi,\tilde{\gamma}_{s}>ds

which shows that
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}
satisfies the PDE (
36
), and by uniqueness, that
\tilde{\gamma}_{t}=\gamma_{t}
and consequently, that formula (
37
) holds.
∎

Lemma 6
.

The family
(\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
is tight and any limit point
\mathbb{P}
solves the martingale problem (
41
) with
(\phi_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
instead of
(\phi^{\epsilon}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}

Proof.

Step 1. Tightness.
For
\delta>0
, we denote by
\omega_{x}(\delta)=\sup_{0\leq s,t\leq T,\;|t-s|\leq\delta}|x(t)-x(s)|
the
\delta
-modulus of continuity of any function
x\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For
\delta^{\prime}>0
we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}[\omega_{\cdot}(\delta)>\delta^{\prime}]&\leq\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}\Bigl[\sup_{|t-s|\leq\delta}|\int_{s}^{t}\phi^{\epsilon}_{r}(X_{r})dr|>\delta^{\prime}/2]+\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}[\sup_{|t-s|\leq\delta}|W_{t}-W_{s}|>\delta^{\prime}/2\Bigr]\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}\Bigl[\sup_{|t-s|\leq\delta}\delta^{1/2}\bigl(\int_{0}^{T}|\phi^{\epsilon}_{r}(X_{r})|^{2}dr\bigr)^{1/2}>\delta^{\prime}/2\Bigr]+\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}\bigl[\sup_{0\leq r\leq\delta}|W_{r}|>\delta^{\prime}/2\bigr]\\
&\leq\frac{4\delta}{\delta^{\prime 2}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}}\Bigl[\int_{0}^{T}|\phi^{\epsilon}_{r}(X_{r})|^{2}dr\Bigr]+o(\delta)\\
&\leq\frac{4\delta}{\delta^{\prime 2}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2}\nu^{\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt+o(\delta)\end{split}

and we conclude that for each
\delta^{\prime}>0
, we have

\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}\sup_{\epsilon>0}\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}[\omega_{\cdot}(\delta)>\delta^{\prime}]=0

because

\sup_{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2}\nu^{\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt\leq\sup_{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2}\gamma^{\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt<\infty

is implied by (
40
). Given the fact that the probability measures
(\nu^{\epsilon}_{0})_{\epsilon}
are tight because the
(\gamma^{\epsilon}_{0})_{\epsilon}
are, we conclude that the
(\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon}
are themselves tight on
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Step 2. Any limit point
\mathbb{P}
solves the martingale problem for
(\phi_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
.
Let us fix
0\leq s<t\leq T
and let
Y
be a bounded continuous function on
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
measurable with respect to the past up to time
s
. For any test function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and for aany
\epsilon>0
we have:

0=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}}\Bigl[Y\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})-\varphi(X_{s})-\int_{s}^{t}[\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi(X_{r})+\phi^{\epsilon}_{r}(X_{r})\nabla\varphi(X_{r})]dr\bigr]\Bigr].

In order to prove the same equality for
\mathbb{P}
and
\phi
instead of
\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}
and
\phi^{\epsilon}
, it is enough to prove:

(43)

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}}\Bigl[Y\int_{s}^{t}\phi^{\epsilon}_{r}(X_{r})\nabla\varphi(X_{r})]dr\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[Y\int_{s}^{t}\phi_{r}(X_{r})\nabla\varphi(X_{r})]dr\Bigr].

Given a bounded Lipschitz continuous function
\tilde{\phi}\in C([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
with compact support, we have:

(44)

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}}\Bigl[Y\int_{s}^{t}\tilde{\phi}_{r}(X_{r})\nabla\varphi(X_{r})]dr\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[Y\int_{s}^{t}\tilde{\phi}_{r}(X_{r})\nabla\varphi(X_{r})]dr\Bigr]

since
\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}
converges weakly toward
\mathbb{P}
.
So in order to prove (
43
) it is be enough to prove

(45)

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}}\Bigl[Y\int_{s}^{t}[\phi^{\epsilon}_{r}(X_{r})-\tilde{\phi}_{r}(X_{r})]\nabla\varphi(X_{r})]dr\Bigr]=0

if one can choose
\tilde{\phi}
so that the expectation

(46)

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[Y\int_{s}^{t}[\phi_{r}(X_{r})-\tilde{\phi}_{r}(X_{r})]\nabla\varphi(X_{r})]dr\Bigr]

can be made as small as desired. Prompted by the definition of
\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}
given in (
38
), we define
\tilde{\phi}^{\epsilon}_{r}(x)
by

\tilde{\phi}^{\epsilon}_{r}(x)=\frac{d\bigl((\tilde{\phi}_{r}\gamma_{r})*\rho_{\epsilon}\bigr)}{d(\gamma_{r}*\rho_{\epsilon})}.

We first prove (
45
). Since
Y
and
\nabla\varphi
are bounded, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}}\Bigl[Y\int_{s}^{t}[\phi^{\epsilon}_{r}(X_{r})-\tilde{\phi}_{r}(X_{r})]\nabla\varphi(X_{r})]dr\Bigr]&\leq\|Y\|_{\infty}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}}\Bigl[\int_{s}^{t}|\phi^{\epsilon}_{r}(X_{r})-\tilde{\phi}_{r}(X_{r})|dr\Bigr]\\
&\leq\|Y\|_{\infty}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{\epsilon}_{r}(x)-\tilde{\phi}_{r}(x)|\nu^{\epsilon}_{r}(dx)dr\\
&\leq\|Y\|_{\infty}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}e^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{\epsilon}_{r}(x)-\tilde{\phi}_{r}(x)|\gamma^{\epsilon}_{r}(dx)dr.\end{split}

We use the fact that

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{\epsilon}_{r}(x)-\tilde{\phi}_{r}(x)|\gamma^{\epsilon}_{r}(dx)dr&\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{\epsilon}_{r}(x)-\tilde{\phi}^{\epsilon}_{r}(x)|\gamma^{\epsilon}_{r}(dx)dr+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\tilde{\phi}^{\epsilon}_{r}(x)-\tilde{\phi}_{r}(x)|\gamma^{\epsilon}_{r}(dx)dr\\
&=(i)+(ii)\end{split}

and we estimate separately
(i)
and
(ii)
.

\begin{split}(i)&=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl|\frac{d\bigl((\phi_{t}\gamma_{t})*\rho_{\epsilon}\bigr)}{d(\gamma_{t}*\rho_{\epsilon})}-\frac{d\bigl((\tilde{\phi}_{t}\gamma_{t})*\rho_{\epsilon}\bigr)}{d(\gamma_{t}*\rho_{\epsilon})}\Bigr|\;d(\gamma_{t}*\rho_{\epsilon})dt\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi_{t}(x)-\tilde{\phi}_{t}(x)|\;\gamma_{t}(dx)dt\end{split}

where we used
[
3
, Lemma 8.1.10]
, and this quantity can be made as small as desired by density of the Lipschitz continuous functions with compact support in
L^{1}\bigl([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\gamma_{t}(dx)dt\bigr)
. Next,

\begin{split}(ii)&=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl|[(\tilde{\phi}_{t}(x)\gamma_{t})*\rho_{\epsilon}](x)-\tilde{\phi}_{t}(x)(\gamma_{t}*\rho_{\epsilon})(x)\Bigr|dxdt\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\epsilon}(x-y)\tilde{\phi}_{t}(y)\gamma_{t}(dy)-\tilde{\phi}_{t}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\epsilon}(x-y)\gamma_{t}(dy)\Bigr|dxdt\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\epsilon}(x-y)|\tilde{\phi}_{t}(y)-\tilde{\phi}_{t}(x)|\gamma_{t}(dy)dxdt\\
&\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\epsilon}(x-y)(|x-y|\wedge 1)\;\gamma_{t}(dy)dxdt\\
&=CT\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\epsilon}(z)\,(|z|\wedge 1)\;dz\end{split}

where we used Fubini’s theorem, the Lipschitz property of
\tilde{\phi}
, and the fact that
\gamma_{t}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\leq 1
, and this quantity converges to
0
as
\epsilon\searrow 0
. This completes the proof since the above argument also proves (
46
).
∎

We now state as a theorem the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 1

(Non-local Superposition Principle)
.

Let us assume that the couple
(\bm{\phi},\bm{\mu})
is such that
\bm{\phi}=\bigl(\phi_{t}(x)\bigr)_{0\leq t\leq T,\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued measurable function on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is a measurable flow of probability measures satisfying (
34
) and the non-local FPK equation (
31
) in the sense of distributions.

Then there exists a weak solution
\mathbf{X}=(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
of the stochastic differential equation (
32
) satisfying (
34
), and such that for each
t\in[0,T]
, the probability measure
\mu_{t}
is given by (
30
). Moreover,

(47)

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|^{2}]<\infty.

Proof.

The existence of
\mathbf{X}
follows the set of lemmas proven above. We only need to prove the bound (
47
). Itô’s formula gives:

|X_{t}|^{2}=|X_{0}|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}[2\phi_{s}(X_{s})\cdot X_{s}+d]ds+2\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}\cdot dW_{s}

and taking expectations we get

\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|^{2}]\leq\Bigl(\mathbb{E}[|X_{0}|^{2}]+d\,T+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi_{t}(x)|^{2}d\nu_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[|X_{s}|^{2}]ds

and we conclude using Gronwall’s inequality.
∎

3.4.
Reformulation of the Optimization Problem

In this subsection we reformulate the relaxed optimization problem over stochastic state processes as a deterministic control problem on a space of probability measures.

For
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we now denote by
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}(\mu_{0})
the set of couples
(\theta,\mu)
where
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a non-negative measure on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the form
\mu(dt,dx)=\mu_{t}(dx)dt
for a measurable flow
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\theta\in\mathcal{M}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued measure on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the form
\theta(dt,dx)=\theta_{t}(dx)dt
for a measurable flow
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, satisfying

(48)

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\mu_{t}+\div(\theta_{t})+(V-<\mu_{t},V>)\mu_{t}=0

in the sense of distributions, and with initial condition
\mu_{|t=0}=\mu_{0}
.

We denote by
\mathbb{A}^{(2)}=\mathbb{A}^{(2)}(\mu_{0})
the subset of
\mathbb{A}(\mu_{0})
of couples
(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})
for which
\theta_{t}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu_{t}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and for which there exists a measurable function
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni(t,x)\mapsto\phi_{t}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\frac{d\theta_{t}}{d\mu_{t}}(x)=\phi_{t}(x)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{t}(x)^{2}\mu_{t}(dx)dt<\infty.

If
(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{A}^{(2)}(\mu_{0})
, Theorem
1
implies that there exists a process
\mathbf{X}=(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
satisfying
dX_{t}=\phi_{t}(X_{t})dt+dW_{t}
, (
34
) and (
47
), and such that the probability measures
\mu_{t}
are given by

\mu_{t}(dx)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\delta_{X_{t}}(dx)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}.

Later on, we shall use the analog
\mathbb{A}^{(2)}_{[s,T]}
of
\mathbb{A}^{(2)}
defined over the interval
[s,T]
instead of
[0,T]
.
We now introduce the functional
J
defined on
\mathbb{A}
by

(49)

J(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int f(x,\phi_{t}(x))\mu_{t}(dx)\;dt+\int g(x)\mu_{T}(dx),\quad&\text{if }(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{A}^{(2)}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

When
(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{A}^{(2)}
we use the notations
J(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})
and
J(\bm{\phi},\bm{\mu})
interchangeably.

3.5.
Existence of an Optimal Control

Next, we state and prove the existence of an optimal Markovian control.

Proposition 2
.

There exists a couple
(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})=(\phi_{t},\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}\in\mathbb{A}^{(2)}
minimizing
J(\bm{\phi},\bm{\mu})
over
\mathbb{A}
.

Proof.

The idea is to consider a minimizing sequence, and to show that it converges in a suitable sense to a minimizer of
J(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})
.
\mathbb{A}^{(2)}
being non-empty,

J^{*}:=\inf_{(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{A}}J(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})<\infty

and because of the definition (
49
), we can limit the search for a minimizer to
\mathbb{A}^{(2)}
.

Step 1
. Let
(\bm{\phi}^{n},\bm{\mu}^{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a
minimizing sequence in
\mathbb{A}^{(2)}
. Since
\sup_{n}J(\bm{\phi}^{n},\bm{\mu}^{n})<\infty
, we have

(50)

C_{\phi}:=\sup_{n}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{n}_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt<\infty,

from which we argue that, extracting a sub-sequence if necessary, the sequence
(\theta^{n})_{n\geq 0}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued measures defined by

\theta^{n}(dt,dx)=\phi^{n}_{t}(x)\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt

converges weakly toward a measure
\theta(dt,dx)\in\mathcal{M}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Moreover, and for similar reasons, we may assume without any loss of generality, that
\bigl(\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt\bigr)_{n\geq 0}
converges weakly toward a non-negative measure which is necessarily of the form
\mu_{t}(dx)dt
. The fact that the sequences
(\theta^{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(\mu^{n})_{n\geq 0}
are tight despite the fact that the state space is not compact is a simple consequence of

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|\;|\phi^{n}_{t}(x)|\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt\leq\Bigl[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr]^{1/2}\Bigl[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{n}_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr]^{1/2}<c

for a finite constant
c
independent of
n
. Indeed, using the test function
\varphi(x)=|x|^{2}
in (
48
) we get:

(51)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}<|x|^{2},\mu^{n}_{t}>&=<\frac{1}{2}\Delta|x|^{2}+\phi^{n}_{t}(x)\nabla|x|^{2}-(V(x)-<V,\mu_{t}>)|x|^{2},\mu^{n}_{t}>\\
&\leq d+2<x\cdot\phi^{n}_{t}(x),\mu^{n}_{t}>+<|x|^{2},\mu^{n}_{t}>\\
&\leq d+4<|\phi^{n}_{t}(x)|^{2},\mu^{n}_{t}>+5<|x|^{2},\mu^{n}_{t}>\\
&\leq d+4C_{\phi}+5<|x|^{2},\mu^{n}_{t}>\end{split}

and Gronwall’s inequality gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)\leq e^{5T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}\mu_{0}(dx)+C^{\prime},

for a positive constant
C^{\prime}
independent of
n
.

Step 2
. We now show that
\theta(dt,dx)
is of the form
\theta(dt,dx)=\phi_{t}(x)\mu_{t}(dx)dt
for some
\bm{\phi}=(\phi_{t}(x))_{t,x}\in L^{2}(\mu_{t}(dx)dt)
. If
h\in C_{b}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a bounded continuous function on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have:

(52)

\begin{split}|<\theta,h>|&=\lim_{n\to\infty}|<\theta^{n},h>|\\
&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Bigl|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(t,x)\cdot\phi^{n}_{t}(x)\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr|\\
&\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|h(t,x)|^{2}\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{n}_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr)^{1/2}\;\\
&\leq C_{\phi}^{1/2}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|h(t,x)|^{2}\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr)^{1/2}\\
&=C_{\phi}^{1/2}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|h(t,x)|^{2}\mu_{t}(dx)\;dt\Bigr)^{1/2}\end{split}

which shows that
\theta
is a bounded linear form on the Hilbert space
L^{2}\bigl(\mu_{t}(dx)dt\bigr)
, proving the existence of

\bm{\phi}\in L^{2}(\mu_{t}(dx)dt)
such that
\theta(dt,dx)=\phi_{t}(x)\mu_{t}(dx)dt
.

Step 3
. For each integer
n\geq 0
, since
(\phi^{n}_{t},\mu^{n}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}\in\mathbb{A}^{(2)}
, for each test function
(t,x)\mapsto\varphi(t,x)
in
C^{1,2}_{b}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, namely a smooth function with enough bounded derivatives so we can use integration by parts and push the derivatives from
\mu_{t}
to
\varphi
, and hopefully not have boundary terms to deal with, we have:

\int_{0}^{T}\hskip-4.0pt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl[\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\varphi(t,x)-(V(x)-<V,\mu^{n}_{t}>)\varphi(t,x)\Bigr]\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt=-\int_{0}^{T}\hskip-4.0pt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{x}\varphi(t,x)\cdot\phi^{n}_{t}(x)\,\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt.

We can pass to the limit
n\to\infty
using the convergence of
\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt
in the left hand side and the convergence of
\phi^{n}_{t}(x)\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt
in the right hand side to conclude that
(\phi_{t},\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}\in\mathbb{A}^{(2)}
.

Step 4
. For the sake of convenience, we shall use the notation

\tilde{J}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\nu})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\;|\psi_{t}(x)|^{2}\nu_{t}(dx)dt.

For each
\epsilon>0
and for each
t\in[0,T]
, we define
\theta^{\epsilon}_{t}=\theta_{t}*\rho_{\epsilon}
, and
\mu_{t}^{\epsilon}=\mu_{t}*\rho_{\epsilon}
where
(\rho_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}

is an approximate identity (say a Gaussian density in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with variance
\epsilon
,
\rho_{\epsilon}(x)=(2\pi x)^{-d/2}\exp(-|x|^{2}/2\epsilon)
), and where the operation of convolution is done component by component when appropriate. We then define
\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)
as the density of
\theta^{\epsilon}_{t}
with respect to
\mu_{t}^{\epsilon}
.
Using
[
3
, Lemma 8.1.10]
we get

(53)

\tilde{J}(\bm{\phi}^{\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon})\leq\tilde{J}(\bm{\phi},\bm{\mu})

and since for each
t\in[0,T]
,
\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}
and
\theta^{\epsilon}_{t}
converge weakly toward
\mu_{t}
and
\theta_{t}
respectively, using the fact that the functional

(\theta,\mu)\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl|\frac{d\theta(x)}{d\mu(x)}\Bigr|^{2}\mu(dx)

is lower semi continuous (see for instance Theorem 2.34 and Example 2.36 in
[
2
]
), we conclude that:

(54)

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\tilde{J}(\bm{\phi}^{\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon})=\tilde{J}(\bm{\phi},\bm{\mu}).

Similarly, for each integer
n\geq 1
we define
\theta^{n,\epsilon}_{t}=\theta^{n}_{t}*\rho_{\epsilon}
, and
\mu_{t}^{n,\epsilon}=\mu^{n}_{t}*\rho_{\epsilon}
, and
\phi^{n,\epsilon}_{t}
as the density of
\theta^{n,\epsilon}_{t}
with respect to
\mu_{t}^{n,\epsilon}
. Notice that for each
\epsilon>0
,
\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon}
and
\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon}
converge weakly toward
\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon}
and
\bm{\theta}^{\epsilon}
respectively.
For the sake of notation we define
\phi^{(\epsilon,K)}_{t}(x)=(-K)\vee\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\wedge K
where the operations of minimum and maximum are interpreted component by component. We have:

(55)

\begin{split}\tilde{J}(\bm{\phi}^{\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon})&=\lim_{K\nearrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi^{(\epsilon,K)}_{t}(x)\cdot\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt\\
&=\lim_{K\nearrow\infty}\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi^{(\epsilon,K)}_{t}(x)\cdot\phi^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(x)\mu^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\leq\lim_{K\nearrow\infty}\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}\Bigl[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{(\epsilon,K)}_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr]^{1/2}\Bigl[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr]^{1/2}\\
&=\lim_{K\nearrow\infty}\Bigl[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{(\epsilon,K)}_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr]^{1/2}\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}\Bigl[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr]^{1/2}\\
&=\Bigl[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr]^{1/2}\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}\Bigl[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr]^{1/2}.\end{split}

Notice that

(56)

\begin{split}\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt&=\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}J(\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon})-\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{f}(x)\mu^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt-\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mu^{n,\epsilon}_{T}(dx)\\
&=\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}J(\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon})-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{f}(x)\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mu^{\epsilon}_{T}(dx)\\
\end{split}

because we assume that
\tilde{f}
and
g
are bounded and continuous.
Notice that (
56
) implies that

\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}J(\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon})\geq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{f}(x)\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mu^{\epsilon}_{T}(dx)

which in turn implies that, if
\tilde{J}(\bm{\theta}^{\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon})=0
,

\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}J(\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon})\geq J(\bm{\phi}^{\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon}).

On the other hand, if
\tilde{J}(\bm{\theta}^{\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon})>0
,
(
55
) implies

(57)

\begin{split}\tilde{J}(\bm{\theta}^{\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon})&\leq\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}J(\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon})-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{f}(x)\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mu^{\epsilon}_{T}(dx)\\
&=\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}J(\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon})+\tilde{J}(\bm{\theta}^{\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon})-J(\bm{\theta}^{\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon}),\end{split}

implying that

J(\bm{\theta}^{\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon})\leq\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}J(\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon})

holds in all cases. Now

(58)

\begin{split}J(\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon})&=\tilde{J}(\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon})+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{f}(x)\mu^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mu^{n,\epsilon}_{T}(dx)\\
&\leq\tilde{J}(\bm{\theta}^{n},\bm{\mu}^{n})+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{f}(x)\mu^{n,\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mu^{n,\epsilon}_{T}(dx)\\
\end{split}

if we use once more (
53
) from
[
3
, Lemma 8.1.10]
. Consequently:

(59)

\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}J(\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon})\leq\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}\tilde{J}(\bm{\theta}^{n},\bm{\mu}^{n})+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{f}(x)\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}(dx)dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mu^{\epsilon}_{T}(dx)\\

and

(60)

\begin{split}\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}J(\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon})&\leq\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}\tilde{J}(\bm{\theta}^{n},\bm{\mu}^{n})+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{f}(x)\mu_{t}(dx)dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mu_{T}(dx)\\
&\leq\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}J(\bm{\theta}^{n},\bm{\mu}^{n})-\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{f}(x)\mu^{n}_{t}(dx)dt-\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mu^{n}_{T}(dx)\\
&\hskip 95.0pt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{f}(x)\mu_{t}(dx)dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mu_{T}(dx)\\
&=J^{*},\end{split}

from which we deduce, using (
54
):

J(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})=\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}J(\bm{\theta}^{\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon})\leq\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}J(\bm{\theta}^{n,\epsilon},\bm{\mu}^{n,\epsilon})\leq J^{*}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2
.

The above argument shows that there is no loss of generality in limiting the search for optima to the subset of admissible feedback control functions satisfying

(61)

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|\phi_{t}(X_{t})|^{2}\;dt\leq K

for a large enough constant
K>0
. Recall (
50
) and the fact that the expectation is over a process
(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
satisfying the state dynamics (
27
) driven by the control
\bm{\phi}
and that

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|\phi_{t}(X_{t})|^{2}\;dt\leq e^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\phi_{t}(X_{t})|^{2}e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}=e^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi_{t}(x)|^{2}\;\mu_{t}(dx)dt.

3.6.
Solution of the Deterministic Control Problem

In line with the computations of, and the notations used in the previous subsections, the running and terminal cost functions of the deterministic infinite dimensional control problem are defined as:

(62)

F^{(1)}(\mu,\phi)=\int f(x,\phi(x))\;\mu(dx),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptG^{(1)}(\mu)=\int g(x)\;\mu(dx),

for
\mu
and
\phi
as above. So at east formally, the definition of the corresponding Hamiltonian
\mathbb{H}^{(1)}
should be:

(63)

\mathbb{H}^{(1)}(\mu,\varphi,\phi)=<\frac{1}{2}\Delta\mu-\div(\phi\mu)-(V-<\mu,V>)\mu,\;\varphi>+F^{(1)}(\mu,\phi)\\

where the bracket
<\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,>
stands for the duality between measures and functions, and coincides with the inner product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},dx)
. After integration by parts of the first term, the definition of this Hamiltonian reads:

(64)

\mathbb{H}^{(1)}(\mu,\varphi,\phi)=-\frac{1}{2}<\nabla\mu,\nabla\varphi>+<\mu,\phi\nabla\varphi>-<\mu,V\varphi>+<\mu,V><\mu,\varphi>+F^{(1)}(\mu,\phi)

which is well defined for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as long as
\nabla\mu
in the sense of distributions belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},dx;\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},dx)
with a gradient (in the sense of distributions) belonging to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},dx;\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mu;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
\phi
a
A
-valued measurable function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
\int|\phi(x)|^{2}\mu(dx)<\infty
.

3.6.1.
The Adjoint PDE

Let us assume that
(\bm{\phi},\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{A}^{(2)}
. We say that the function
u
is an adjoint variable (or a co-state) if it satisfies the PDE
\partial_{t}u=-(\delta\mathbb{H}^{(1)}/\delta\mu)(\mu,u,\phi)
with terminal condition
u_{T}(x)=(\delta G^{(1)}/\delta\mu)(x)=g(x)
in the sense of distributions. Here the notation
\delta/\delta\mu
stands for the
flat derivative
which we now define.
Referring to
[
8
, Definition 5.43]
the flat derivative (also called the linear functional derivative) of a function
F:\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{k})\to\mathbb{R}
of measures on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
for some integer
k
, is defined to satisfy:

(65)

F(\mu^{\prime})-F(\mu)=\int_{0}^{1}\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu}(\theta\mu^{\prime}+(1-\theta)\mu)(x)\bigl[\mu^{\prime}-\mu\bigr](dx)d\theta.

Note that this notion of flat derivative is only defined up to a constant, but this will not matter in the present analysis.
Accordingly, the adjoint equation reads:

(66)

\partial_{t}u=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}u-\phi_{t}\cdot\nabla_{x}u+(V-<\mu,V>)u-V<\mu,u>-f\bigl(\cdot,\phi_{t}(\cdot)\bigr),

which can be rewritten as

(67)

0=\partial_{t}u+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}u+\phi_{t}\cdot\nabla_{x}u-(V-<\mu,V>)u+V<\mu,u>+\frac{1}{2}|\phi_{t}|^{2}+\tilde{f}

in the case of separable running cost functions of the form
(
5
).

The fact that from now on, we deal with Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) requires a strengthening of the assumptions made so far. In particular, assumption (
28
) will be replaced by

Assumption 3
.

(68)

K_{\phi}:=\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}|\phi_{s}(X^{t,x}_{s})|^{2}ds<\infty

where
\mathbf{X}^{t,x}=(X^{t,x}_{s})_{t\leq s\leq T}
satisfies the state equation
dX_{s}=\phi_{s}(X_{s})ds+dW_{s}
over the interval
[t,T]
with initial condition
X_{t}=x
.

This assumption is obviously satisfied when
\phi
is bounded. More generally
it is satisfied for larger classes of functions
\phi
. For example,
[
10
]
proves that Assumption
3
is satisfied if
\phi\in L^{q}\bigl([0,T];L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\bigr)
for some

(69)

p\geq 2,\hskip 10.00002ptq>2,\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{d}{p}+\frac{2}{q}<1.

Note also that earlier works like
[
13
]
used a local version of assumption
3
in the sense that it is only required to be satisfied for
\phi\mathbf{1}_{|x|\leq n}\in L^{q}\bigl([0,T];L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\bigr)
for every integer
n\geq 1
.

Proposition 3
.

In the case of separable cost functions, for each continuous flow
(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and each feedback function
\phi
satisfying the strong assumption
3
, the adjoint PDE (
67
) admits a solution in the sense of viscosity.

Proof.

First notice that if
u
is a classical solution of (
66
), the
variation of the constant formula
and Itô’s formula give a form of Feynman-Kac implicit representation

(70)

\begin{split}&u_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\int_{t}^{r}\bigl(V(X_{\tau})-<\mu_{\tau},V>\bigr)d\tau}\bigl[<\mu_{r},u_{r}>V(X_{r})+f\bigl(X_{r},\phi_{r}(X_{r})\bigr)\bigr]dr\\
&\hskip 155.0pt+g(X_{T})e^{-\int_{t}^{T}\bigl(V(X_{\tau})-<\mu_{\tau},V>\bigr)d\tau}\;\Bigl|\;X_{t}=x\Bigr]\end{split}

where the expectation
\mathbb{E}
is over the process
(X_{s})_{t\leq s\leq T}
satisfying (
27
) starting from
X_{t}=x
at time
t
. The first step of the proof is to treat (
70
) as a fixed point equation for the function
u
, and show that such an equation has a unique solution. This is identifying
u
as a
mild solution
of equation (
66
). Next, we argue that the latter is in fact the unique solution of an affine BSDE, and we conclude that it is a viscosity solution of (
66
) using a classical result of the theory of BSDEs.

Step 1
. For each
u\in C_{b}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
where
C_{b}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the Banach space of bounded continuous functions on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
\Phi(u)
the right hand side of (
70
) seen as a function of
(t,x)
. Using the fact that
f(x,\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}+\tilde{f}(x)
with
\tilde{f}
bounded, assumption
3
implies that
\mathbb{E}[\int_{t}^{T}|\phi_{r}(X_{r})|^{2}]dr|X_{t}=x]
is uniformly bounded in
t
and
x
, and it is straightforward to check that
\Phi(u)\in C_{b}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which we equip with the norm

\|u\|_{\alpha}=\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\alpha t}|u_{t}(x)|

for a fixed number
\alpha>1
. If
u^{1}
and
u^{2}
are two functions in this Banach space,

(71)

\begin{split}\|\Phi(u^{1})-\Phi(u^{2})\|_{\alpha}&=\sup_{t,x}e^{\alpha t}\Bigl|\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\int_{t}^{r}\bigl(V(X_{\tau})-<\mu_{\tau},V>\bigr)d\tau}|<\mu_{r},u^{1}_{r}-u^{2}_{r}>|V(X_{r})dr\;\Bigl|\;X_{t}=x\Bigr]\Bigr|\\
&\leq\sup_{t,x}e^{\alpha t}\int_{t}^{T}e^{r-t}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u^{1}_{r}(y)-u^{2}_{r}(y)|dr\\
&\leq\sup_{t}e^{\alpha t}\int_{t}^{T}e^{(1-\alpha)r-t}e^{\alpha r}\sup_{x}|u^{1}_{r}(x)-u^{2}_{r}(x)|dr\\
&\leq\|u^{1}-u^{2}\|_{\alpha}\sup_{t}e^{(\alpha-1)t}\int_{t}^{T}e^{(1-\alpha)r}dr\\
&=\frac{1-e^{(1-\alpha)T}}{\alpha-1}\|u^{1}-u^{2}\|_{\alpha},\end{split}

and the fraction is smaller than
1
when
\alpha
is large enough, because
e^{(1-\alpha)T}\to 0
and
1/(\alpha-1)\to 0
as
\alpha\to+\infty
which proves that
\Phi
is a strict contraction since
\alpha>e^{T}
.
Its unique fixed point is what we shall use as solution of the adjoint equation (
66
).

Step 2
. In order to match the notation of
[
18
, Remark 3.3]
we set
c(t,x)=-[V(x)-<\mu_{t},V>]
and

h(t,x)=<\mu_{t},u_{t}>V(x)+f\bigl(x,\phi_{t}(x)\bigr)
. Notice that the function
c
is bounded and since the function
u
constructed above as a fixed point is bounded, for each
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have:

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\int_{t}^{T}|h(s,X^{t,x}_{s})|ds\Bigr]\leq C+\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\int_{t}^{T}|\phi_{s}(X^{t,x}_{s})|^{2}dt\Bigr]\leq C+K_{\phi}<\infty

where
(X^{t,x}_{s})_{t\leq s\leq T}
is the solution of
dX_{s}=\phi_{s}(X_{s})ds+dW_{s}
over the interval
[t,T]
and initial condition
X_{t}=x
. For each
(t,x)
we denote by
(Y^{t,x}_{s},Z^{t,x}_{s})_{t\leq s\leq T}
the unique solution of the affine BSDE:

dY^{t,x}_{s}=-[c(s,X^{t,x}_{s})Y^{t,x}_{s}+h(s,X^{t,x}_{s})]ds+Z^{t,x}_{s}dW_{s}

with terminal condition
Y^{t,x}_{T}=g(X^{t,x}_{T})
. Being
linear
, this BSDE has an explicit solution

(72)

Y^{t,x}_{s}=g(X^{t,x}_{T})e^{\int_{s}^{T}c(\tau,X^{t,x}_{\tau})d\tau}+\int_{s}^{T}h(r,X^{t,x}_{r})e^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\tau,X^{t,x}_{\tau})d\tau}dr-\int_{s}^{T}e^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\tau,X^{t,x}_{\tau})d\tau}Z^{t,x}_{r}dW_{r}

and taking
s=t
, one recovers the Feynman-Kac formula

Y^{t,x}_{t}=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[g(X^{t,x}_{T})e^{\int_{t}^{T}c(\tau,X^{t,x}_{\tau})d\tau}+\int_{t}^{T}h(r,X^{t,x}_{r})e^{\int_{t}^{r}c(\tau,X^{t,x}_{\tau})d\tau}dr\\
\Bigr]

which is exactly our formula (
70
) if we set
u(t,x)=Y^{t,x}_{t}
. Strictly speaking, since
f(x,\phi_{t}(x))=\tilde{f}(x)+\frac{1}{2}|\phi_{t}(x)|^{2}
, the assumptions of
[
18
]
would require that
|\phi_{t}(x)|
be of polynomial growth. However, given the special (linear) nature of the BSDE involved, the fact that
\int_{t}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{t}(X^{t,x}_{s})|^{2}ds<\infty
for every
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is enough for the argument of
[
18
]
to go through. We conclude using

[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
.
∎

Remark 3

(
A first a-priori bound.
)
.

We claim that under the weaker assumption
61
, if
u
admits the Feynman-Kac representation (
70
), then

(73)

|<\mu_{t},u_{t}>|\leq e^{2(T-t)}\bigl(\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}+\|g\|_{\infty}\bigr)+\frac{e^{T}}{4}(e^{2(T-t)}+1).

Recall that we denote by
\nu_{t}
the marginal distribution of the state
X_{t}
controlled by the drift
\phi_{t}
. So by definition of the probability measure
\mu_{t}
, for any non-negative random variable
Y
which depend only upon the future after time
t
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{t}=x]\mu_{t}(dx)\leq e^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{t}=x]\nu_{t}(dx)=e^{T}\mathbb{E}[Y]

where the last expectation is with respect to the state process with initial distribution
\mu_{0}
.
So integrating both sides of the Feynman-Kac representation (
70
) with respect to
\mu_{t}
we get:

(74)

\begin{split}&<\mu_{t},u_{t}>=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\int_{t}^{r}\bigl(V(X_{\tau})-<\mu_{\tau},V>\bigr)d\tau}\bigl[<\mu_{r},u_{r}>V(X_{r})+f\bigl(X_{r},\phi_{r}(X_{r})\bigr)\bigr]dr\\
&\hskip 155.0pt+g(X_{T})e^{-\int_{t}^{T}\bigl(V(X_{\tau})-<\mu_{\tau},V>\bigr)d\tau}\;\Bigl|\;X_{t}=x\Bigr]\mu_{t}(dx)\end{split}

and if we limit ourselves to feedback functions satisfying (
61
), we get:

\begin{split}|<\mu_{t},&u_{t}>|\\
&\leq\int_{t}^{T}e^{r-t}|<\mu_{r},u_{r}>|\,dr+e^{T-t}\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}+\frac{e^{T}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[\int_{t}^{T}|\phi_{s}(X_{s})|^{2}]ds\,|X_{t}=x]\nu_{t}(dx)+e^{T-t}\|g\|_{\infty}\\
&\leq e^{T-t}\bigl(\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}+\|g\|_{\infty}\bigr)+\frac{e^{T}}{2}K_{\phi}+\int_{t}^{T}e^{r-t}|<\mu_{r},u_{r}>|dr\end{split}

and using the form of Gronwall inequality in Lemma
7
we get:

e^{t}|<\mu_{t},u_{t}>|\leq e^{2T-t}\bigl(\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}+\|g\|_{\infty}\bigr)+\frac{e^{T}}{4}(e^{2T-t}+1).

Remark 4

(
A stronger a-priori bound.
)
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
3
, we have:

(75)

\|u_{t}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{e^{t}}{2}\bigl(e^{2(T-t)}-1\bigr)\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}+\frac{e^{2T}}{2}K_{\phi}+e^{T}\|g\|_{\infty}.

Indeed, computing the supremum of the left hand side of the Feynman-Kac representation (
70
) instead of integrating with respect to
\mu_{t}
, we get:

\begin{split}|u_{t}(x)|&\leq\int_{t}^{T}e^{r-t}\bigl(\|u_{r}\|_{\infty}+\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}\bigr)\;dr+\int_{t}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\phi_{r}(X_{r})|^{2}\,|\,X_{t}=x\bigr]+e^{T-t}\|g\|_{\infty}\end{split}

and

e^{t}\|u_{t}\|_{\infty}\leq\int_{t}^{T}e^{r}\|u_{r}\|_{\infty}dr+(e^{T}-e^{t})\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}+e^{t}K_{\phi}+e^{T}\|g\|_{\infty}.

Finally, we get (
78
) using Lemma
7
with
\zeta(t)=e^{T}\|g\|_{\infty}+e^{t}K_{\phi}+(e^{T}-e^{t})\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}
,
c=1
and
\xi(t)=e^{t}\|u_{t}\|_{\infty}
.

3.6.2.
Analysis of the Adjoint PDE

In this subsection, we assume that
(\bm{\phi},\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{A}^{(2)}(\mu_{0})
. In particular,
\bm{\mu}
solves the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation driven by
\bm{\phi}
, and we denote by
\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
the marginal distributions of the state process
\mathbf{X}=(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
whose existence is guarateed by our form of the superposition principle proven in Theorem
1
.

For the sake of later reference, we provide without proof, a simple version of Gronwall’s lemma which we shall use repeatedly in the sequel.

Lemma 7
.

Let us assume that
\xi(t)\leq\zeta(t)+c\int_{t}^{T}\xi(r)dr
, then

(76)

\xi(t)\leq\zeta(t)+ce^{c(T-t)}\int_{t}^{T}\zeta(r)e^{-c(T-r)}dr.

Lemma 8
.

If
\tilde{\bm{\phi}}=(\tilde{\phi}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}\in C_{b}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\tilde{F}=(\tilde{F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}\in C_{b}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
are smooth enough, then the equation

(77)

0=\partial_{t}v_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta v_{t}+\tilde{\phi}_{t}\cdot\nabla v_{t}-(V-<\mu_{t},V>)v_{t}+V<\mu_{t},v_{t}>+\tilde{F}

with bounded terminal condition
v_{T}=g
, has a unique classical solution satisfying the upper bound

(78)

\|v_{t}\|_{\infty}\leq e^{2T-t}\Bigl(\|g\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{F}\|_{\infty}\Bigr).

Moreover, when
g=0
, this solution satisfies:

(79)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla v_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu_{t}(dx)dt\leq 4e^{4T}\|\tilde{F}\|_{\infty}^{2}\bigl(1+\frac{3}{2}T+\|\tilde{\bm{\phi}}-\bm{\phi}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\bm{\mu})}\bigr).

Proof.

Equation (
77
) is not a standard PDE because of the presence of the non-local term
V<\mu_{t},v_{t}>
in the right hand side. The solution is obtained by constructing a fixed point to the mapping
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\mapsto\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{v}})=\mathbf{v}
where
\mathbf{v}
is the unique classical solution of the regular linear PDE

0=\partial_{t}v_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta v_{t}+\tilde{\phi}_{t}\cdot\nabla v_{t}-(V-<\mu_{t},V>)v_{t}+V<\mu_{t},\tilde{v}_{t}>+\tilde{F},

with terminal condition
v_{T}=g
. From now on, we let
\mathbf{v}
be a solution to (
77
).
The first a-priori bound (
78
) follows easily from the Feynman-Kac representation in terms of the solution
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=(\tilde{X}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
of the state stochastic differential equation
d\tilde{X}_{t}=\tilde{\phi}_{t}(\tilde{X}_{t})dt+dW_{t}
. Indeed,
\tilde{\phi}
being bounded, it satisfies Assumption
3
and we can repeat the argument of Remark
4
: from (
77
) we get:

\begin{split}v_{t}(x)&=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\int_{t}^{r}\bigl(V(\tilde{X}_{\tau})-<\mu_{\tau},V>\bigr)d\tau}\bigl[<\mu_{r},v_{r}>V(\tilde{X}_{r})+\tilde{F}(r,\tilde{X}_{r})\bigr]dr\\
&\hskip 155.0pt+g(\tilde{X}_{T})e^{-\int_{t}^{T}\bigl(V(\tilde{X}_{\tau})-<\mu_{\tau},V>\bigr)d\tau}\;\Bigl|\;\tilde{X}_{t}=x\Bigr]\end{split}

from which we get

\begin{split}|v_{t}(x)|&\leq\int_{t}^{T}e^{r-t}\bigl(\|v_{r}\|_{\infty}+\|\tilde{F}\|_{\infty}\bigr)\;dr+e^{T-t}\|g\|_{\infty}\end{split}

and

e^{t}\|v_{t}\|_{\infty}\leq\int_{t}^{T}e^{r}\|v_{r}\|_{\infty}dr+(e^{T}-e^{t})\|\tilde{F}\|_{\infty}+e^{T-t}\|g\|_{\infty}.

Finally, we get (
78
) using Lemma
7
with
\zeta(t)=e^{T-t}\|g\|_{\infty}+(e^{T}-e^{t})\|\tilde{F}\|_{\infty}
,
c=1
and
\xi(t)=e^{t}\|v_{t}\|_{\infty}
.

The derivation of the a-priori bound
(
79
) is more involved.
We notice that since
\bm{\mu}
solves the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation driven by
\bm{\phi}
, a simple integration by parts implies that if
\varphi
is a smooth bounded function on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
we must have:

(80)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl[\partial_{t}\varphi^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\varphi^{2}+\phi_{t}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi^{2}-(V-<\mu_{t},V>)\varphi^{2}\Bigr]\mu_{t}(dx)dt=<\mu_{T},\varphi^{2}_{T}>-<\mu_{0},\varphi^{2}_{0}>.

as long as the above integrals exist. Using (
77
) we get:

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t}(x)\bigl(\tilde{F}_{t}(x)+\tilde{\phi}_{t}\cdot\nabla_{x}v_{t}(x)\bigr)\mu_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\hskip 15.0pt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t}(x)\Bigl(-\partial_{t}v_{t}(x)-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}v_{t}(x)+(V(x)-<\mu_{t},V>)v_{t}(x)-V(x)<\mu_{t},v_{t}>\Bigr)\mu_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\hskip 15.0pt=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl(\partial_{t}v^{2}_{t}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}v^{2}_{t}(x)+\phi_{t}(x)\cdot\nabla_{x}v^{2}_{t}(x)-(V(x)-<\mu_{t},V>)v^{2}_{t}(x)\Bigr)\mu_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\hskip 55.0pt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}(V(x)-<\mu_{t},V>)v^{2}_{t}(x)-V(x)<\mu_{t},v_{t}>v_{t}(x)\\
&\hskip 105.0pt+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla_{x}v_{t}(x)|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\phi_{t}(x)\cdot\nabla_{x}v^{2}_{t}(x)\Bigr)\mu_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\hskip 15.0pt=\frac{1}{2}<v^{2}_{0},\mu_{0}>+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}(V(x)-<\mu_{t},V>)v^{2}_{t}(x)-V(x)<\mu_{t},v_{t}>v_{t}(x)\\
&\hskip 105.0pt+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla_{x}v_{t}(x)|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\phi_{t}(x)\cdot\nabla_{x}v^{2}_{t}(x)\Bigr)\mu_{t}(dx)dt\end{split}

where we use (
89
) with
\varphi=v
and the fact that
v_{T}=0
. Consequently:

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla_{x}v_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\leq 2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t}(x)\bigl(\tilde{F}_{t}(x)+\tilde{\phi}_{t}\cdot\nabla_{x}v_{t}(x)\bigr)\mu_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\hskip 15.0pt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl((V(x)-<\mu_{t},V>)v^{2}_{t}(x)+2V(x)<\mu_{t},v_{t}>v_{t}(x)-\phi_{t}(x)\cdot\nabla_{x}v^{2}_{t}(x)\Bigr)\mu_{t}(dx)dt\\
&=2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t}(x)\tilde{F}_{t}(x)\mu_{t}(dx)dt+2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t}(x)\bigl(\tilde{\phi}_{t}(x)-\phi_{t}(x)\bigr)\cdot\nabla_{x}v_{t}(x)\mu_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\hskip 15.0pt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl((V(x)-<\mu_{t},V>)v^{2}_{t}(x)+2V(x)<\mu_{t},v_{t}>v_{t}(x)\Bigr)\mu_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\leq 2\|v\|_{\infty}\|\tilde{F}\|_{L^{1}(\bm{\mu})}+2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v^{2}_{t}(x)\bigl|\tilde{\phi}_{t}(x)-\phi_{t}(x)\bigr|^{2}\mu_{t}(dx)dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla_{x}v_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu_{t}(dx)dt+3T\|v\|_{\infty}^{2},\end{split}

where we used the inequality
ab\leq a^{2}+b^{2}/4
. Consequently

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla_{x}v_{t}(x)|^{2}\mu_{t}(dx)dt\leq 4\|v\|_{\infty}\|\tilde{F}\|_{L^{1}(\bm{\mu})}+6T\|v\|^{2}_{\infty}+4\|v\|^{2}_{\infty}\|\tilde{\bm{\phi}}-\bm{\phi}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\bm{\mu})}

and we conclude using the a-priori bound (
78
) and the fact that integrals with respect to
\bm{\nu}
are controlled by integrals with respect to
\bm{\mu}
.
∎

Remark 5
.

Remark
4
implies that the proofs of the above a-priori bounds do not really need that
\tilde{\phi}
is bounded, but merely that it satisfies assumption
3
. We stated with the boundedness assumption for the sake of simplicity.

We now tackle the issue of existence, uniqueness and regularity of solutions of the adjoint equation.

Theorem 2
.

If
(\bm{\phi},\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{A}^{(2)}(\mu_{0})
is such that
\bm{\phi}
is bounded, the viscosity solution of the adjoint equation is a bounded continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose first order derivatives in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the sense of distributions are functions in
L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{\mu})
and
L_{loc}^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},dt\,dx)
.

Proof.

Let us introduce the family
(\bm{\phi}^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
of bounded smooth Markovian feedback functions defined by
\phi_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)=[\phi_{t}*\rho^{\epsilon}](x)
where the convolution is performed component by component, and where
(\rho^{\epsilon})_{0<\epsilon\leq 1}
is an approximate identity, the support of
\rho^{1}
being included in the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For every
p\in[1,\infty)
, the
C^{\infty}
bounded functions
\bm{\phi}^{\epsilon}
converges to
\bm{\phi}
in
L^{p}_{loc}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},dx\,dt)
when
\epsilon\searrow 0
, and for each
\epsilon>0
,
\|\bm{\phi}^{\epsilon}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\bm{\phi}\|_{\infty}
. For each
\epsilon>0
we set
F^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{2}|\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)|^{2}+\tilde{f}(x)
, we denote by
w^{\epsilon}
the classical solution of the PDE (
77
) for
\tilde{\phi}_{t}(x)=\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)
and
\tilde{F}_{t}(x)=F^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)
, and we set
v^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)=w^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\chi^{\epsilon}(x)
where
\chi^{\epsilon}
is
C^{\infty}
,
\chi^{\epsilon}(x)=1
if
|x|\leq 1/\epsilon
and
\chi^{\epsilon}(x)=0
if
|x|\geq 1+1/\epsilon
. We have:

0=\partial_{t}w^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta w^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)+\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\cdot\nabla w^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)-(V(x)-<\mu_{t},V>)w^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)+V(x)<\mu_{t},w^{\epsilon}_{t}>+F^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)

and we can replace
w^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)
by
v^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)
whenever
|x|\leq 1/\epsilon
.

Applying the a-priori estimates (
78
) to
w^{\epsilon}

we get that for each compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

\limsup_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\|v^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times K,dt\,dx)}\leq\limsup_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\|w^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times K,dt\,dx)}<\infty

giving the existence of
v\in L^{2}_{loc}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},dt\,dx)
for which, after extracting a sub-sequence if needed,

v=\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}v^{\epsilon}

locally (i.e. for each compact subset
K
) for the weak topology. Also, the a-priori estimates (
78
) implies that the
v^{\epsilon}
’s form a bounded set in
L^{2}(\bm{\mu})
, so extracting a further sub-sequence if necessary, one can assume that
v^{\epsilon}
converges weakly toward
v
in
L^{2}(\bm{\mu})
implying that, extracting a further subsequence if needed, for almost every
t\in[0,T]
:

(81)

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}<\mu_{t},w^{\epsilon}_{t}>=\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}<\mu_{t},v^{\epsilon}_{t}>=\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}<1,v^{\epsilon}_{t}>_{L^{2}(\mu_{t})}=<1,v_{t}>_{L^{2}(\mu_{t})}=<\mu_{t},v_{t}>.

Furthermore, the a-priori estimate (
79
) implies that one can extract a further sub-sequence for which
\nabla_{x}v^{\epsilon}
converges weakly in
L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{\mu};\mathbb{R}^{d})
toward a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued function
\tilde{\varphi}
in
L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{\mu};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
This measurable vector field
\tilde{\varphi}
can be identified with the gradient (in the sense of distributions) of
v
. Indeed, if
\varphi
is a smooth test function with compact support in
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{x}v^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\varphi_{t}(x)dxdt=-\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{x}\varphi_{t}(x)v^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)dxdt=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{x}\varphi_{t}(x)v_{t}(x)\;dxdt

and since
\bm{\phi}
is assumed to be bounded

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{x}v^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\varphi_{t}(x)dx=\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{x}v^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\frac{\varphi_{t}(x)}{\rho_{t}(x)}\mu_{t}(dx)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{\varphi}_{t}(x)\varphi_{t}(x)\;dx

where we used part (iii) of Proposition
1
to benefit from the fact that the continuous density
\rho
is locally strictly positive and bounded below away from
0
. This shows that the gradient in the sense of distributions of
v
is a function (namely the function
\tilde{\varphi}
), and that
\nabla_{x}v^{\epsilon}
converges weakly in
L_{loc}^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},dx\,dt;\mathbb{R}^{d})
toward
\tilde{\varphi}=\nabla_{x}v
. This implies that

(82)

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{t}(x)\;\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\cdot\nabla w^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\;dxdt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{t}(x)\;\phi_{t}(x)\cdot\nabla v_{t}(x)\;dxdt

where
\nabla v_{t}(x)
is the function
\tilde{\varphi}_{t}(x)
identified as the gradient in the sense of distributions of the function
v
constructed above. Indeed, if
\epsilon>0
is small enough,

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{t}(x)\;\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\cdot\nabla w^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\;dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{t}(x)\;\phi_{t}(x)\cdot\nabla v_{t}(x)\;dxdt\\
&\hskip 45.0pt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{t}(x)\;\bigl(\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)-\phi_{t}(x)\bigr)\cdot\nabla v^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\;dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{t}(x)\;\phi_{t}(x)\cdot\bigl(\nabla v^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)-\nabla v_{t}(x)\bigr)\;dxdt.\end{split}

The first term goes to
0
because

\limsup_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi_{t}(x)|\;|\nabla v^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)|^{2}\;dxdt<\infty

since the
L^{2}(\bm{\mu})
-norm of
\nabla_{x}v^{\epsilon}
is uniformly bounded because of the a-priori estimate (
79
) and the fact that the density of
\mu_{t}
is bounded from below away from
0
on the support of
\varphi
, and the fact that
\phi^{\epsilon}
converges toward
\phi
in
L^{2}_{loc}(dxdt)
. As for the second term, it also converges toward
0
because
\nabla_{x}v^{\epsilon}
converges toward
\tilde{\varphi}=\nabla_{x}v

weakly in
L^{2}_{loc}(dxdt)
.
Again, if
\varphi
is a smooth test function with compact support in
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

(83)

\begin{split}&\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl[-\partial_{t}\varphi_{t}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi_{t}(x)-(V(x)-<\mu_{t},V>)\varphi_{t}(x)\Bigr]v^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)dxdt\\
&\hskip 35.0pt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl[-\partial_{t}\varphi_{t}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi_{t}(x)-(V(x)-<\mu_{t},V>)\varphi_{t}(x)\Bigr]v_{t}(x)dxdt.\end{split}

Moreover

(84)

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{t}(x)|\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)|^{2}dxdt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{t}(x)|\phi_{t}(x)|^{2}dxdt.

Putting together the limits (
81
), (
3.6.2
), (
83
) and (
84
) we find that

(85)

\begin{split}&0=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl(\bigl[-\partial_{t}\varphi_{t}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi_{t}(x)-(V(x)-<\mu_{t},V>)\varphi_{t}(x)\bigr]v_{t}(x)+\varphi_{t}(x)\phi_{t}(x)\cdot\nabla_{x}v_{t}(x)\\
&\hskip 95.0pt+\bigl[<\mu_{t},v_{t}>V(x)+\frac{1}{2}|\phi_{t}(x)|^{2}+\tilde{f}(x)\bigr]\varphi_{t}(x)\Bigr)dxdt.\end{split}

once more, given the test function
\varphi
, we use the fact that
w^{\epsilon}
and
v^{\epsilon}
coincide on the support of
\varphi
for
\epsilon>0
small enough.
This proves that
v
is a weak solution (in the sense of distributions) of the adjoint equation.
∎

3.6.3.
A Maximum Principle

This subsection is devoted to the statement and the proof of a version of the Pontryagin maximum principle tailored to our needs. Throughout the subsection we assume that
\bm{\phi}=(\phi_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is a bounded measurable feedback control function, that
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is the solution of the corresponding FPK equation (
31
), and that
u
is a solution of the associated adjoint equation (
66
).
If
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is another bounded measurable feedback control function, for each
\epsilon>0
, we define
\bm{\phi}^{\epsilon}=(\phi^{\epsilon}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
as
\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}=\phi_{t}+\epsilon\beta_{t}
, and we denote by
\bm{\mu}^{\epsilon}=(\mu^{\epsilon}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
the solution of the FPK equation driven by the control
\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}
.

Lemma 9
.

For each
t\in[0,T]
and each bounded measurable function
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the limit

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}<\varphi,\frac{\mu_{t}^{\epsilon}-\mu_{t}}{\epsilon}>

exists and is given by the integral of
\varphi
with respect to a finite signed measure
\lambda_{t}
such that
\lambda_{t}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=0
and satisfying the PDE

(86)

\partial_{t}\lambda_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\lambda_{t}-\div(\phi_{t}\lambda_{t})-\div(\beta_{t}\mu_{t})+<\lambda_{t},V>\mu_{t}-(V-<\mu_{t},V>)\lambda_{t}

in the sense of distributions with initial condition
\lambda_{0}=0
.

Proof.

If we denote by
X_{t}
(resp.
X^{\epsilon}_{t}
) the state controlled by
\phi_{t}
(resp.
\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}
), i.e. the solution of the stochastic differential equation
dX_{t}=\Phi_{t}(X_{t})dt+dW_{t}
(resp.
dX^{\epsilon}_{t}=\Phi^{\epsilon}_{t}(X^{\epsilon}_{t})dt+dW_{t}
), Girsanov’s theorem gives:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\epsilon}(<\varphi,\mu_{t}^{\epsilon}>-<\varphi,\mu_{t}>)&=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Bigl(\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi(X^{\epsilon}_{t})e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X^{\epsilon}_{s})ds}\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X^{\epsilon}_{s})ds}\bigr]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}\Bigr)\\
&=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Bigl(\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi(X^{\epsilon}_{t})e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X^{\epsilon}_{s})ds}\bigr]-\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X^{\epsilon}_{s})ds}\bigr]}\\
&\hskip 45.0pt-\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]\Bigl(\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X^{\epsilon}_{s})ds}\bigr]}\Bigr)\Bigr)\\
&=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigl(e^{\epsilon\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{s}(X_{s})dW_{s}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|\beta_{s}(X_{s})|^{2}ds}-1\bigr)\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X^{\epsilon}_{s})ds}\bigr]}\\
&\hskip 45.0pt-\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X^{\epsilon}_{s})ds}\bigr]-\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X^{\epsilon}_{s})ds}\bigr]}\end{split}

from which we conclude

(87)

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}<\varphi,\frac{\mu_{t}^{\epsilon}-\mu_{t}}{\epsilon}>=\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})\bigl(\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{s}(X_{s})dW_{s}\bigr)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}-<\varphi,\mu_{t}>\frac{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl(\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{s}(X_{s})dW_{s}\bigr)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]}

because all the moments of the random variable
e^{-\epsilon\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{s}(X_{s})dW_{s}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|\beta_{s}(X_{s})|^{2}ds}
are finite, because

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X^{\epsilon}_{s})ds}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr],

and because

\begin{split}\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X^{\epsilon}_{s})ds}\bigr]-\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigr]\Bigr)&=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\bigl[e^{\epsilon\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{s}(X_{s})dW_{s}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|\beta_{s}(X_{s})|^{2}ds}-1\bigr]\Bigr)\\
&=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl(\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{s}(X_{s})dW_{s}\bigr)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\Bigr].\end{split}

Notice that the right hand side of (
87
) is a linear form in
\varphi
which defines a signed measure
\lambda_{t}
with total mass
0
.
In order to identify the PDE satisfied by
\lambda_{t}
one can use the rules of Itô calculus to compute the time derivative of
<\varphi,\lambda_{t}>
by computing the derivative of the right hand side of (
87
). Since these computations are long and tedious, we choose a more direct approach.
Subtracting one FPK equation from another we find that

\partial_{t}\frac{\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}-\mu_{t}}{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\frac{\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}-\mu_{t}}{\epsilon}-\div\bigl(\phi_{t}\frac{\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}-\mu_{t}}{\epsilon}\bigr)-\div\bigl(\frac{\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}-\phi_{t}}{\epsilon}\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}\bigr)+<\frac{\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}-\mu_{t}}{\epsilon},V>\mu_{t}-(V-<\mu_{t},V>)\frac{\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}-\mu_{t}}{\epsilon}

so taking the limit
\epsilon\searrow 0
we derive the PDE (
86
).
∎

Lemma 10
.

\nabla u
is square integrable with respect to the measure
|\lambda|
in the sense that

(88)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla u_{t}(x)|^{2}|\lambda_{t}|(dx)dt<\infty.

The strategy of the proof is similar to the proof of estimate (
79
) in Lemma
8
.

Proof.

The PDE (
86
) implies that for any test function
\varphi
we have

(89)

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int\Bigl[\partial_{t}\varphi^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi^{2}+\phi_{t}\nabla\varphi^{2}-(V-<\mu_{t},V>)\varphi^{2}\Bigr]\lambda_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\hskip 45.0pt+\int_{0}^{T}\int\Bigl[\beta_{t}\nabla\varphi^{2}+<V,\lambda_{t}>\varphi^{2}\Bigr]\mu_{t}(dx)dt=<\varphi^{2},\lambda_{T}>.\end{split}

Using the fact that
u
solves the adjoint equation (
66
) we can write

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int u_{t}\bigl[f\bigl(.,\phi_{t}(.)\bigr)+\phi_{t}\nabla u_{t}\bigr]\lambda_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\hskip 35.0pt=\int_{0}^{T}\int u_{t}\bigl[-\partial_{t}u_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta u_{t}+(V-<\mu_{t},V>)u_{t}-V<\mu_{t},u_{t}>\bigr]\lambda_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\hskip 35.0pt=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int\bigl[\partial_{t}u_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta u_{t}^{2}+\phi_{t}\nabla u_{t}^{2}-(V-<\mu_{t},V>)u_{t}^{2}\bigr]\lambda_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\hskip 75.0pt+\int_{0}^{T}\int\bigl[\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u_{t}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\phi_{t}\nabla u_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(V-<\mu_{t},V>)u_{t}^{2}-V<\mu_{t},u_{t}>u_{t}\bigr]\lambda_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\hskip 35.0pt=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int\bigl[\beta_{t}\nabla u_{t}^{2}+<V,\lambda_{t}>u_{t}^{2}\bigr]\mu_{t}(dx)dt+\frac{1}{2}<u_{T}^{2},\lambda_{T}>\\
&\hskip 75.0pt+\int_{0}^{T}\int\bigl[\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u_{t}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\phi_{t}\nabla u_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(V-<\mu_{t},V>)u_{t}^{2}-V<\mu_{t},u_{t}>u_{t}\bigr]\lambda_{t}(dx)dt\end{split}

from which we get

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla u_{t}|^{2}\lambda_{t}(dx)dt&=\int_{0}^{T}\int u_{t}\bigl[f\bigl(.,\phi_{t}(.)\bigr)+\phi_{t}\nabla u_{t}\bigr]\lambda_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\hskip 35.0pt-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int\bigl[\beta_{t}\nabla u_{t}^{2}+<V,\lambda_{t}>u_{t}^{2}\bigr]\mu_{t}(dx)dt-\frac{1}{2}<u_{T}^{2},\lambda_{T}>\\
&\hskip 35.0pt-\int_{0}^{T}\int\bigl[\frac{1}{2}\phi_{t}\nabla u_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(V-<\mu_{t},V>)u_{t}^{2}-V<\mu_{t},u_{t}>u_{t}\bigr]\lambda_{t}(dx)dt\\
&\leq\|u\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{\infty}|\lambda|([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})+2\|u\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\phi\|_{\infty}^{2}|\lambda|([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
&\hskip 35.0pt+\frac{1}{8}\int_{0}^{T}|\int\nabla u_{t}|^{2}\lambda_{t}(dx)dt+\frac{1}{2}\|\beta\|_{\infty}\Bigl(T\|u\|_{\infty}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\int|\nabla u_{t}|^{2}\mu_{t}(dx)dt\Bigr)\\
&\hskip 35.0pt+2(\|u\|_{\infty}^{2}+\|u\|_{\infty})|\lambda|([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
&\hskip 35.0pt+2T\|\phi\|_{\infty}^{2}\|u\|_{\infty}^{2}+\frac{1}{8}\int_{0}^{T}\int|\nabla u_{t}|^{2}\lambda_{t}(dx)dt\end{split}

from which we conclude.
∎

Proposition 4
.

Let
\bm{\phi}=(\phi_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
be a bounded measurable feedback control function, let
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
be the corresponding solution of the FPK equation (
31
), and let
u
the solution of the corresponding adjoint equation (
66
).
If
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is another bounded measurable feedback control function, we have:

(90)

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}J(\bm{\phi}+\epsilon\bm{\beta})\Bigr|_{\epsilon=0}=\int_{0}^{T}<\beta_{t}(\nabla u_{t}+\phi_{t}),\mu_{t}>\,dt.

As a result, if
\bm{\phi}
is a critical point, then

(91)

\phi_{t}(x)=-\nabla u_{t}(x),\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\mu_{t}-a.s.\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\hskip 10.00002pta.e.\;t\in[0,T].

Proof.

We use freely the notations introduced at the beginning of the subsection and in the statement of the previous lemma.

Step 1.
We derive a first expression for the derivative (
90
).

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigl[J(\bm{\phi}+\epsilon\bm{\beta})-J(\bm{\phi})\bigr]\\
&\hskip 25.0pt=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(<\tilde{f},\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}>+\frac{1}{2}<|\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}|^{2},\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}>-<\tilde{f},\mu_{t}>-\frac{1}{2}<|\phi_{t}|^{2},\mu_{t}>\Bigr)\;dt+\frac{1}{\epsilon}<g,\mu^{\epsilon}_{T}-\mu_{T}>\\
&\hskip 25.0pt=\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(<\tilde{f},\frac{\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}-\mu_{t}}{\epsilon}>+\frac{1}{2}<|\phi_{t}|^{2},\frac{\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}-\mu_{t}}{\epsilon}>+\frac{1}{2}<\frac{|\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}|^{2}-|\phi_{t}|^{2}}{\epsilon},\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}>\Bigr)dt+<g,\frac{\mu^{\epsilon}_{T}-\mu_{T}}{\epsilon}>\end{split}

so that

(92)

\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigl[J(\bm{\phi}+\epsilon\bm{\beta})-J(\bm{\phi})\bigr]=\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(<\tilde{f},\lambda_{t}>+\frac{1}{2}<|\phi_{t}|^{2},\lambda_{t}>+<\beta_{t}\phi_{t},\mu_{t}>\Bigr)dt+<g,\lambda_{T}>.

where we used the stronger form of convergence toward
\lambda_{t}
proven in Lemma
9
because
|\phi_{t}|^{2}
is merely bounded measurable and not necessarily continuous.

Step 2.
We now prove the formula

(93)

<u_{T},\lambda_{T}>=\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(<\beta_{t}\nabla u_{t},\mu_{t}>-\bigl(<\tilde{f},\lambda_{t}>+\frac{1}{2}<|\phi_{t}|^{2},\lambda_{t}>\bigr)\Bigr)\,dt

where the function
u
is the solution of the adjoint equation driven by the control
\bm{\phi}
. Recall that Theorem
2
says that
u
is bounded and continuous and that its derivatives in the sense of distributions are square integrable functions with respect to
\bm{\mu}
.

Let us define the functions
u_{t}^{n}(x)=\chi^{n}(x)u_{t}(x)
and
\lambda_{t}^{n}(dx)=\chi^{n}(x)\lambda_{t}(dx)
where the sequence
(\chi^{n})_{n\geq 1}
of
C^{\infty}
functions is such that
0\leq\chi^{n}(x)\leq 1
,
\chi^{n}(x)=1
if
|x|\leq n
,
\chi^{n}(x)=0
if
|x|>n+1
and its derivatives of all orders are uniformly bounded. We first notice that

(94)

<u_{T},\lambda_{T}>=\lim_{n\nearrow\infty}<u^{n}_{T},\lambda^{n}_{T}>

because
u_{T}
is bounded. Next,
using the facts that
\partial_{t}u^{n}_{t}=\chi^{n}\partial_{t}u_{t}
,
\partial_{t}\lambda^{n}_{t}=\chi^{n}\partial_{t}\lambda_{t}
and
\lambda_{0}=0
, we get:

\begin{split}<u^{n}_{T},\lambda^{n}_{T}>&=<u^{n}_{0},\lambda^{n}_{0}>+\int_{0}^{T}<u^{n}_{t},\partial_{t}\lambda^{n}_{t}>+\int_{0}^{T}<\partial_{t}u^{n}_{t},\lambda^{n}_{t}>\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(<\chi^{n}u_{t},\frac{1}{2}\chi^{n}\Delta\lambda_{t}>-<\chi^{n}u_{t},\chi^{n}\div(\phi_{t}\lambda_{t})>-<\chi^{n}u_{t},\chi^{n}\div(\beta_{t}\mu_{t})>\\
&\hskip 95.0pt+<\lambda_{t},V><\chi^{n}u_{t},\chi^{n}\mu_{t}>-<\chi^{n}u_{t},\chi^{n}(V-<\mu_{t},V>)\lambda_{t}>\\
&\hskip 25.0pt+<-\frac{1}{2}\chi^{n}\Delta u_{t},\chi^{n}\lambda_{t}>-<\chi^{n}\phi_{t}\nabla u_{t},\chi^{n}\lambda_{t}>+<\chi^{n}(V-<\mu_{t},V>)u_{t},\chi^{n}\lambda_{t}>\\
&\hskip 95.0pt-<\mu_{t},u_{t}><\chi^{n}V,\chi^{n}\lambda_{t}>-\frac{1}{2}<\chi^{n}|\phi_{t}|^{2},\chi^{n}\lambda_{t}>-<\chi^{n}\tilde{f},\chi^{n}\lambda_{t}>\Bigr)\;dt.\end{split}

Using integration by parts we get:

\begin{split}<\chi^{n}u_{t},\chi^{n}\Delta\lambda_{t}>&=<(\chi^{n})^{2}u_{t},\Delta\lambda_{t}>\\
&=-<(\chi^{n})^{2}\nabla u_{t},\nabla\lambda_{t}>-<\nabla(\chi^{n})^{2}\;u_{t},\nabla\lambda_{t}>\\
&=-<(\chi^{n})^{2}\nabla u_{t},\nabla\lambda_{t}>+<\nabla\bigl(\nabla(\chi^{n})^{2}u_{t}\bigr),\lambda_{t}>,\end{split}

and similarly

\begin{split}<\chi^{n}\Delta u_{t},\chi^{n}\lambda_{t}>&=<\Delta u_{t},(\chi^{n})^{2}\lambda_{t}>\\
&=-<\nabla u_{t},(\chi^{n})^{2}\nabla\lambda_{t}>-<\nabla(\chi^{n})^{2}\nabla u_{t},\lambda_{t}>,\end{split}

so that

\lim_{n\to\infty}<\chi^{n}u_{t},\chi^{n}\Delta\lambda_{t}>-<\chi^{n}\Delta u_{t},\chi^{n}\lambda_{t}>=\lim_{n\to\infty}<\nabla\bigl(\nabla(\chi^{n})^{2}u_{t}\bigr),\lambda_{t}>-\lim_{n\to\infty}<\nabla(\chi^{n})^{2}\nabla u_{t},\lambda_{t}>=0

because
u_{t}
is bounded, and
\nabla u_{t}
is square integrable for
\lambda_{t}

Remark 6
.

Both equations in the above system are strongly coupled, and the difficulty in the second equation is twofold. The combination of the nonlinear term
|\nabla_{x}u|^{2}
and the non-local term
<\mu,u>
prevents us from appealing directly to standard results from PDEs or BSDEs to study the well-posedness of the system.

The sense in which equation (
103a

\nabla_{x}u
is well defined as a function: it should be understood in the sense of distributions. However, the situation with the second equation is not as clear, in part because of the presence of non-local and non-linear terms. While the notion of viscosity solution could be viewed as a natural option, we shall refrain from using this interpretation for two independent reasons. Firstly, the comparison constraints involved in the definition of a viscosity solution would need to be modified to account for the non-local terms. Secondly, viscosity solutions are not always differentiable, and our derivation of equation (
103b
) was based on the substitution in the adjoint equation, of the identification of the optimal control as the gradient of its own adjoint function (i.e. co-state). In order to avoid this oxymoron created by a seemingly circular argument, we choose to search for solutions by first freezing the non-local terms, and then, work with
mild solutions
in order to avoid the possible lack of differentiability of the solution. The following lemma highlights the fact that, absent the non-local nature of the equations, viscosity and mild solutions are the same in our setting. We believe that such a result is part of the folklore on the subject. See for example
[
4
]
. We state it in the form we need for further references, and we give a proof for the sake of completeness.

(118)

U^{(2)}(t,\mu)=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta_{t},\;\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\theta},\;X_{t}\sim\mu}\Bigl(\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[f(X_{s},\alpha_{s})e^{-\int_{t}^{s}V(X_{u})du}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[e^{-\int_{t}^{s}V(X_{u})du}\Bigr]}ds+\frac{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[g(X_{T})e^{-\int_{t}^{T}V(X_{s})ds}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[e^{-\int_{t}^{T}V(X_{s})ds}\Bigr]}\Bigr).

The initial condition
X_{t}\sim\mu
stipulating that the distribution of
X_{t}
is
\mu
, reduces to the classical case of a deterministic initial condition when
\mu
is a pointwise measure, say
\mu=\delta_{x}
for some
x\in D
.

4.2.
Technical Preliminary: the Mimicking Theorem

The proof of the main result of this section is based on an application of the mimicking theorem, sometimes
referred to as the Markovian projection theorem. It is originally
due to Gyongy
[
12
]
. We shall make use of the extension proven by Brunick and Shreve in
[
6
]
.

We introduce the following notation to check easily the conditions required for the result of
[
6
, Theorem 3.6]
which we want to use. Recall that we use the standard notation
C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
for the space of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued continuous function on
[0,\infty)
, and we use the notation
C_{0}([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
for the set of elements
x\in C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
x(0)=0
. We define the set
\mathcal{E}
to be the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
. Next we define the function
\Phi:\mathcal{E}\times C_{0}([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})\to C([0,\infty);\mathcal{E})
by:

\Phi\bigl((e_{1},e_{2}),x\bigr)=\Bigl(e_{1}+x,e_{2}+\int_{0}^{\cdot}V(x_{s})ds\Bigr).

It should be clear that
e_{1}+x
is the notation for the function
[0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto e_{1}+x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and that
e_{2}+\int_{0}^{\cdot}V(x_{s})ds
is the notation for the function
[0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto e_{2}+\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds\in\mathbb{R}
. Also, for the sake of convenience, we shall often write
(e_{1},e_{2},x)

instead of
((e_{1},e_{2}),x)
. It is clear that the range of
\Phi
is contained in the space
C([0,\infty);\mathcal{E})

and that
\Phi
is continuous because the potential function
V
is continuous and bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Next, we check that
\Phi
is an
updating function
in the sense of
[
6
, Definition 3.1]
. Obviously:

[\Phi(e_{1},e_{2},x)](0)=\Bigl(e_{1}+x(0),e_{2}+\int_{0}^{0}V(e_{1}+x(u))du\Bigr)=(e_{1},e_{2}).

If
x
is any function on
[0,\infty)
, for any
t\geq 0
, we denote by
x^{t}
the function stopped at time
t
, namely the function:

[0,\infty)\ni s\mapsto x^{t}(s)=x(s\wedge t).

In particular:

[\Phi(e_{1},e_{2},x)]^{t}(s)=\Bigl(e_{1}+x(s\wedge t),e_{2}+\int_{0}^{s\wedge t}V(e_{1}+x(u))du\Bigr),

which is equal to

[\Phi(e_{1},e_{2},x^{t})]^{t}(s)=\Bigl(e_{1}+x^{t}(s),e_{2}+\int_{0}^{s\wedge t}V(e_{1}+x^{t}(u))du).

Finally, we check the third property of
[
6
, Definition 3.1]
. Indeed:

[\Phi(e_{1},e_{2},x)](t+s)=\Bigl(e_{1}+x(t+s),e_{2}+\int_{0}^{t+s}V(e_{1}+x(u))du\Bigr)

while by definition, the right hand side of the desired equality is equal to:

\begin{split}&\Bigl[\Phi\Bigl([\Phi(e_{1},e_{2},x)](t),x(t+\cdot)-x(t)\Bigr)\Bigr](s)\\
&\enskip=\Bigl([\Phi(e_{1},e_{2},x)](t)_{1}+x(t+s)-x(t)),[\Phi(e_{1},e_{2},x)](t)_{2}+\int_{0}^{s}V([\Phi(e_{1},e_{2},x)](t)_{1}+x(t+u)-x(t))du\Bigr)\\
&\enskip=(e_{1}+x(t+s),e_{2}+\int_{0}^{t}V(e_{1}+x(u))du+\int_{0}^{s}V(e_{1}+x(t)+x(t+u)-x(t))du\Bigr)\end{split}

which is equal to
[\Phi(e_{1},e_{2},x)](t+s)
as desired.

4.3.
Reformulation Based on a Special Class of Feedback Controls

Let us consider
\theta\in\Theta
, let us fix momentarily an admissible control process
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{A}^{\theta}
, let us denote by
\mathbf{X}=(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
the associated controlled process
\mathbf{X}^{\alpha}
over the interval
[0,T]
, and let us set
A_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}V(X^{\alpha}_{s})ds
.
We now use
[
6
, Theorem 3.6]
with the updating function
\Phi
introduced above in Subsection
4.2
.
We define the measurable function
\psi
from
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
with values in
A
by:

(119)

\psi(t,x,a)=\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{t}\;|\;X_{t}=x,\,A_{t}=a]

as the expectation of
\alpha_{t}
with respect to a regular version of the conditional probability given
(X_{t},A_{t})
. It is possible to choose a measurable version such that for
t\notin N
where
N\subset[0,\infty)
is of zero Lebesgue’s measure we have
\psi(t,X_{t},A_{t})=\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{t}\;|\;X_{t},\,A_{t}]

\mathbb{P}
-almost surely. Moreover, there exists a stochastic basis
\hat{\theta}=(\hat{\Omega},\hat{\mathcal{F}},\{\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{t},\hat{\mathbb{P}},\hat{\mathbf{W}})
supporting processes
\hat{\mathbf{X}}=(\hat{X}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
and
\hat{\mathbf{A}}=(\hat{A}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
satisfying:

(120)

\begin{cases}\hat{X}_{t}&=\hat{X}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\psi(s,\hat{X}_{s},\hat{A}_{s})ds+\hat{W}_{t}\\
\hat{A}_{t}&=\int_{0}^{t}V(\hat{X}_{s})ds\end{cases}

and such that for each
t\geq 0
, the joint law of the pair
(X_{t},A_{t})
under
\mathbb{P}
in the original stochastic basis
\theta
coincides with the joint law of
(\hat{X}_{t},\hat{A}_{t})
under
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
in the new stochastic basis
\hat{\theta}
.
As a result:

(121)

\begin{split}J^{V}(\bm{\alpha})&=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[f(X_{t},\alpha_{t})e^{-A_{t}}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[e^{-A_{t}}\Bigr]}dt+\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[g(X_{T})e^{-A_{T}}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[e^{-A_{T}}\Bigr]}\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[f(X_{t},\alpha_{t})|X_{t},A_{t}]e^{-A_{t}}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[e^{-A_{t}}\Bigr]}dt+\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[g(X_{T})e^{-A_{T}}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[e^{-A_{T}}\Bigr]}\\
&\geq\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[f(X_{t},\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{t}|X_{t},A_{t}])e^{-A_{t}}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[e^{-A_{t}}\Bigr]}dt+\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[g(X_{T})e^{-A_{T}}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[e^{-A_{T}}\Bigr]}\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\Bigl[f(\hat{X}_{t},\psi_{t}(\hat{X}_{t},\hat{A}_{t}))e^{-\hat{A}_{t}}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\Bigl[e^{-\hat{A}_{t}}\Bigr]}dt+\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\Bigl[g(\hat{X}_{T})e^{-\hat{A}_{T}}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\Bigl[e^{-\hat{A}_{T}}\Bigr]}\\
&=J^{V}(\hat{\bm{\alpha}}),\end{split}

where the admissible control process
\hat{\bm{\alpha}}=(\hat{\alpha}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is defined as
\hat{\alpha}_{t}=\psi(t,\hat{X}_{t},\hat{A}_{t})
, and where we used the convexity in
\alpha
of
f(x,\alpha)
for
x
fixed, and the fact that the joint law of the pair
(X_{t},A_{t})
under
\mathbb{P}
is the same as the joint law of
(\hat{X}_{t},\hat{A}_{t})
under
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
. This implies that:

J^{V}(\bm{\alpha})\geq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta,\;\bm{\alpha}^{s}\in\mathbb{A}^{\theta,(S)}}J^{V}(\bm{\alpha}^{s})

where, for a given stochastic basis
\theta\in\Theta
, we denote by
\mathbb{A}^{\theta,(S)}
the set of special admissible control processes defined as the subset of
\mathbb{A}^{\theta}
formed by the
\bm{\alpha}
of the form
\alpha_{t}=\psi(t,X_{t},A_{t})
given by a feedback (measurable) function
\psi
of the state process
\mathbf{X}
controlled by
\bm{\alpha}
and the process
\mathbf{A}
given by
A_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds
. Taking the infimum on the left hand side we get:

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta,\;\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{A}^{\theta}}J^{V}(\bm{\alpha})\geq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta,\;\bm{\alpha}^{s}\in\mathbb{A}^{\theta,(S)}}J^{V}(\bm{\alpha}^{s})

and since the left hand side is obviously not greater than the right hand side because
\mathbb{A}^{\theta,(S)}\subset\mathbb{A}^{\theta}
, we conclude that these two infima are identical. Since the same argument applies for all
t\in[0,T]
with initial condition
X_{t}=x
, we conclude that the value function
U^{(2)}
introduced earlier in (
118
) can be computed by minimizing over control processes in feedback form given by deterministic functions of time and the couple
(X_{t},A_{t})
.

To streamline the notations, in analogy with the analysis of the Markovian feedback functions, we shall denote by
\Psi
the set of admissible extended feedback control functions, namely the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued measurable functions
\psi
on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
for which the stochastic differential system (
120
) has a weak solution satisfying

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|\psi_{t}(X_{t},A_{t})|^{2}dt<\infty,

and we shall use the notation
J^{V}(\psi)
for
J^{V}(\bm{\alpha})
when the control process
\bm{\alpha}
is given by
\alpha_{t}=\psi_{t}(X_{t},A_{t})
.
We have thus proved the following result, which is a cornerstone of our analysis from now on.

Theorem 3
.

It holds:

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta,\;\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{A}^{\theta}}J^{V}(\bm{\alpha})=\inf_{\psi\in\Psi}J^{V}(\psi).

4.4.
Reformulation as a Deterministic Control Problem

From now on, we limit the open loop optimization problem to open loop control processes
\bm{\alpha}
of the form

\alpha_{t}=\psi_{t}(X_{t},A_{t})
for measurable feedback functions
\psi\in\Psi
of time and the couple
(X_{t},A_{t})
.
Given such a feedback function
\psi
the definition we chose of the set
\Psi
of admissible extended feedback functions includes the existence of a corresponding controlled process, in other words, a solution of the stochastic differential equation

(122)

\begin{cases}dX_{t}&=\psi_{t}(X_{t},A_{t})dt+dW_{t}\\
dA_{t}&=V(X_{t})dt,\end{cases}

with the same initial condition for
X_{0}
and
A_{0}=0
.
At first glance, this system of stochastic differential equations appears to be degenerated and existence of strong solutions is not guaranteed. However, one can look at the first of the above equations as an equation of the form
dX_{t}=b(t,X_{\cdot})dt+dW_{t}
with a progressively measurable drift
b
which depends upon the past of the trajectory, and existence and uniqueness of weak solutions for those equations is guaranteed for example when
\psi
is bounded. In fact, given the analysis of the Markov feedback case performed in the previous section, we shall limit ourselves in this section to bounded open loop control processes
\bm{\alpha}
, and hence to bounded measurable extended feedback control functions
\psi
. This is all we need since we already appealed to the theory of weak solutions to reduce the open loop optimization problem to what we are considering now, and for which
the cost function
\bm{\alpha}\mapsto J^{V}(\bm{\alpha})
which depends upon the values of the couple
(X_{t},A_{t})
and its distribution can be rewritten in the form:

(123)

\begin{split}J^{V}(\psi)&=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[f(X_{t},\psi_{t}(X_{t},A_{t}))e^{-A_{t}}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[e^{-A_{t}}\Bigr]}dt+\frac{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[g(X_{T})e^{-A_{T}}\Bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[e^{-A_{T}}\Bigr]}\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(\int\mu^{(2)}_{t}(dx,da)f(x,\psi(x,a))\Bigr)dt+\int\mu^{(2)}_{T}(dx,da)g(x)\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}<\mu^{(2)}_{t},f(\cdot,\psi(\cdot,\cdot))>dt+<\mu^{(2)}_{T},g>,\end{split}

where we use the notation
\mu^{(2)}_{t}
for the Gibbs probability measure:

(124)

\mu^{(2)}_{t}(dx,da)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\delta_{(X_{t},A_{t})}(dx,da)e^{-A_{t}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]},\hskip 20.00003pt0\leq t\leq T.

Lemma 12
.

The measure valued function
t\mapsto\mu^{(2)}_{t}
satisfies the forward FPK equation:

(125)

\partial_{t}\mu=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\mu-\div_{x}(\psi_{t}\mu)-V\partial_{a}\mu-(V-<\mu,V>)\mu,

with initial condition
\mu_{|t=0}(dx,da)=\mu_{0}(dx)\delta_{0}(da)
in the sense of Schwartz distributions.

Now, the notation
<\mu,V>
used in formula (
125
) should be understood as
<\mu,V>=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)\mu(dx,da)
or
<\mu,V>=<\tilde{\mu},V>=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\tilde{\mu}(dx)
if we use the notation
\tilde{\mu}
for the first marginal of
\mu
.

Proof.

If
\varphi
is a smooth function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
with compact support, Itô’s formula gives:

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}<\mu^{(2)}_{t},\varphi>&=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{t},A_{t})e^{-A_{t}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}\\
&=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\varphi(X_{t},A_{t})+\psi_{t}(X_{t},A_{t})\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi(X_{t},A_{t})+\partial_{a}\varphi(X_{t},A_{t})V(X_{t})\Bigr)e^{-A_{t}}\\
&\hskip 75.0pt-\varphi(X_{t},A_{t})V(X_{t})e^{-A_{t}}\Bigr]+\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{t},A_{t})e^{-A_{t}}]\frac{\mathbb{E}[V(X_{t})e^{-A_{t}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]^{2}}\\
&=<\mu^{(2)}_{t},\;\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\varphi+\psi_{t}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi+V\partial_{a}\varphi-(V-<\mu^{(2)}_{t},V>)\varphi>\\
&=<\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\mu^{(2)}_{t}-\div_{x}(\psi_{t}\mu^{(2)}_{t})-V\partial_{a}\mu^{(2)}_{t}-(V-<\mu^{(2)}_{t},V>)\mu^{(2)}_{t},\;\varphi>\end{split}

where we used stochastic integration by parts and the fact that
\varphi
has compact support.
∎

For the purpose of our analysis, we resolve the existence problem for the non-local FPK equation (
125
) as before. Given
\psi\in\Psi
, Lemma
12
guarantees that the flow of probability measures
\mu_{t}=\mu^{(2)}_{t}
defined by (
124
) with
A_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds
and
X_{t}
being a weak solution of the stochastic differential equation

dX_{t}=\psi_{t}\Bigl(X_{t},\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds\Bigr)dt+dW_{t}

is a solution of (
125
). This is all we shall need for the existence of solutions of (
125
).

The proof of Theorem
1
did not depend upon the fact that the state stochastic differential equation was not degenerate, so a similar superposition principle holds in the present situation, and our original optimization problem reduces to the deterministic control problem of the minimization of the functional:

(126)

J(\psi)=\int_{0}^{T}F^{(2)}(\mu^{(2)}_{t},\psi_{t})dt+G^{(2)}(\mu^{(2)}_{T})

under the dynamical constraint (
31
), where the running and terminal cost functions
F^{(2)}
and
G^{(2)}
are given by:

(127)

F^{(2)}(\mu,\psi)=\int\int\mu(dx,da)f(x,\psi(x,a)),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptG^{(2)}(\mu)=\int\int\mu(dx,da)g(x),

for
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})
the space of finite measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Note that as per our discussion of the integral of the potential
V
, the terminal cost
G^{(2)}(\mu)
only depends upon the first marginal of the measure
\mu
.

The following lemma guarantees the existence of an optimal control for the problem at hand. It is proven exactly in the same way as Proposition
2
, so we do not repeat the proof.

Lemma 13
.

There exists an optimal admissible feedback function
\hat{\psi}
.

This type of problem is usually approached by computing the value function of the problem as a solution of an HJB equation written in terms of the Hamiltonian of the problem once minimized over the admissible controls.
For later purposes we note that:

(128)

\frac{\delta F^{(2)}}{\delta\mu}(\mu,\psi)(x,a)=f(x,\psi(x,a))\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\frac{\delta G^{(2)}}{\delta\mu}(\mu)(x,a)=g(x).

For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})
,
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})
the space of infinitely differentiable functions with compact support in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
, and
\psi\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+};A)
the space of bounded measurable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we define the Hamiltonian
\mathbb{H}^{(2)}
by:

(129)

\begin{split}\mathbb{H}^{(2)}(\mu,\varphi,\psi)&=<\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\mu-\div_{x}(\psi\mu)-V\partial_{a}\mu-(V-<\mu,V>)\mu,\;\varphi>+F^{(2)}(\mu,\psi)\\
&=<\mu,\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\varphi+\psi\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi+V\partial_{a}\varphi-(V-<\mu,V>)\varphi+f(\cdot,\psi(\cdot,\cdot))>.\end{split}

4.4.1.
The Adjoint PDE

Let us assume that
\psi
is an admissible feedback control function and let
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
satisfies the associated FPK equation (
125
). We define the corresponding adjoint variable as the function
u

solving the PDE
\partial_{t}u=-\partial_{\mu}\mathbb{H}^{(2)}(\mu,u,\psi)
with terminal condition
u_{T}(x,a)=\partial_{\mu}G(x)=g(x)
. In the present situation, this adjoint equation reads:

(130)

\partial_{t}u=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}u-\psi_{t}\cdot\nabla_{x}u-V\partial_{a}u+(V-<\mu,V>)u-V<\mu,u>-f\bigl(\cdot,\psi_{t}(\cdot,\cdot)\bigr),

which can be rewritten as

(131)

0=\partial_{t}u+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}u+\psi_{t}\cdot\nabla_{x}u+V\partial_{a}u-(V-<\mu,V>)u+V<\mu,u>+\frac{1}{2}|\psi_{t}|^{2}+\tilde{f}

in the case of separable running cost function as defined in (
5
).

Lemma 14
.

For each bounded admissible feedback function
\psi\in\Psi
, if
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is the associated flow of probability measures given as a solution of the FPK equation (
125
), the adjoint PDE (
130
) admits a solution in the sense of viscosity.

Proof.

The proof is exactly the same as the proof of Lemma
3
, so we refrain from giving it.
∎

4.4.2.
Minimization of the Hamiltonian.

For the sake of convenience, the following assumption will make it easier to refer to the minimized Hamiltonian.

Assumption 5
.

We assume the existence of a function
\hat{\psi}:\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R})\ni(\mu,\varphi)\mapsto\hat{\psi}[\mu,\varphi]\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R};A)

such that:

\forall(\mu,\varphi)\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}),\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{\psi}[\mu,\varphi]\in\arginf_{\psi\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R};A)}\mathbb{H}^{(2)}(\mu,\varphi,\psi).

In the important case of interest given by separable running cost functions of the form (
5
), we can compute explicitly such a function
\hat{\psi}
.
Indeed, using (
129
) we find:

(132)

\begin{split}\mathbb{H}^{(2)}(\mu,\varphi,\psi)&=<\mu,\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\varphi+\psi\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi+V\partial_{a}\varphi-(V-<\mu,V>)\varphi+f(\cdot,\psi(\cdot,\cdot))>\\
&=\int\hskip-6.0pt\int\mu(dx,da)\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\varphi(x,a)+V(x)\partial_{a}\varphi(x,a)-(V(x)-<\mu,V>)\varphi(x,a)+\tilde{f}(x)\\
&\hskip 35.0pt+\nabla_{x}\varphi(x,a)\cdot\psi(x,a)+\frac{1}{2}|S\psi(x,a)|^{2}\Bigr]\end{split}

and we can minimize under the integral signs (i.e. for
x
and
a
fixed) leading to the minimizer (assuming for simplicity that
A=\mathbb{R}^{d}
):

(133)

\psi[\mu,\varphi](x,a)=-\nabla_{x}\varphi(x,a)

\mu
almost everywhere, and also on the support of the measure
\mu
because of the smoothness of the test function
\varphi
. As in the case of Markovian feedback functions, we notice that the above argument only requires the differentiability of the test function in the
x
variable.

4.4.3.
The Forward-Backward PDE System

As in the case of Markov feedback control functions considered in Subsection
3.6.3
, we can derive an infinite dimensional version of the necessary condition of the classical Pontryagin maximum principle similar to Proposition
4
, and conclude that if
\hat{\psi}
is an optimal control which happens to be bounded, if
\hat{\mu}
is the corresponding controlled state and if
\hat{u}
is a solution of the corresponding adjoint equation, then we can check as before that the partial derivatives of
\hat{u}
with respect to
x
in the sense of distributions are actually bounded measurable functions and that:

\mathbb{H}^{(2)}(\hat{\mu}_{t},\hat{u}_{t},\hat{\psi}_{t})=\inf_{\psi}\mathbb{H}^{(2)}(\hat{\mu}_{t},\hat{u}_{t},\psi),\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt0\leq t\leq T.

So in the case of a separable running cost function, formula (
133
) says that the optimal control
\hat{\psi}
(recall that its existence is guaranteed by Lemma
13
) must satisfy
\hat{\psi}_{t}(x,a)=-\nabla_{x}\hat{u}_{t}(x,a)
, and injecting this formula in the adjoint equation and the FPK equation we see that the couple
(\hat{\mu},\hat{u})
is a solution of the forward backward PDE system on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
:

(134)

\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}\mu=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\mu+\div_{x}(\nabla_{x}u\;\mu)-V\partial_{a}\mu-(V-<\mu,V>)\mu\\
&0=\partial_{t}u+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}u-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla_{x}u|^{2}+V\partial_{a}u-(V-<\mu,V>)u+V<\mu,u>+\tilde{f}.\end{cases}

As in the previous section, we assume that one of the optimal feedback functions whose existence we know, is bounded, and we work with such an optimal feedback control function.
Following the same steps, we argue below that the corresponding solution of the adjoint equation has a gradient which can be identified with the negative of the optimal control thanks to the appropriate version of the Pontryagin maximum principle. Notice that while we do not claim uniqueness for the solutions of the PDE system (
134
), we know that if we have a bounded optimal feedback control, then existence of a solution is guaranteed.

Proposition 6
.

Let
\hat{\psi}
be an optimal feedback control function which is bounded, let
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
be the corresponding solution of the FPK equation (
125
), and let
\hat{u}
be the solution of the corresponding adjoint equation. Then,

(i)

the unique viscosity solution of the second equation of (
134
) depends only upon the space variable
x
and is independent of
a
;

(ii)

furthermore, if
\tilde{f}\in C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the second equation in (
134
) has a unique classical solution and the negative of its gradient is an optimal feedback control.

Proof.

Unsurprisingly, the proof follows the same steps as before.

Step 1.
Let

Part (ii).

Let

\mu

\nabla u
, see the forward equation in (

\mathcal{K}

We provide an upper bound on the second moment of
\mu_{t}
.
We have, with
\phi=-\nabla u
,

\begin{split}\partial_{t}<|x|^{2},\mu_{t}>&=<\frac{1}{2}\Delta|x|^{2}+\phi_{t}(x)\nabla|x|^{2}-(V(x)-<V,\mu_{t}>)|x|^{2},\mu_{t}>\\

&\leq d+4<|\phi_{t}(x)|^{2},\mu_{t}>+5<|x|^{2},\mu_{t}>\\
&\leq d+4C_{2}+5<|x|^{2},\mu_{t}>\end{split}

so:

<|x|^{2},\mu_{t}>\,\leq\,<|x|^{2},\mu_{0}>+tC_{d,C_{2}}+C\int_{0}^{t}<|x|^{2},\mu_{s}>ds.

Since
<|x|^{2},\mu_{0}>+tC_{d,C_{2}}
is non-decreasing in
t
, Gronwall’s inequality gives

(160)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}\mu_{t}(dx)\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}\mu_{0}(dx)+TC_{d,C_{2}}\right)e^{CT}.

As for the first bound in the definition of
\mathcal{K}
, we use the definition of
\mu
. Contrary to the usual MFG PDE system, here we cannot interpret
\mu
as the law of an Itô process. However, we can argue as follows using the characterization of the Wasserstein-1 distance using Lipschitz functions. Let us recall that, thanks to the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality theorem, we have:

W_{1}(\mu_{s},\mu_{t})=\inf_{\varphi:1-Lip}\int\varphi(x)d(\mu_{s}(x)-\mu_{t}(x))=\inf_{\varphi:1-Lip,\varphi(0)=0}\int\varphi(x)d(\mu_{s}(x)-\mu_{t}(x))

where the second equality holds because
\mu_{s}
and
\mu_{t}
both have total mass equal to
1
so we can replace
\varphi(\cdot)
by
\varphi(\cdot)-\varphi(0)
without changing the integral. Let
\varphi
be a
1
-Lipschitz function such that
\varphi(0)=0
. Since we assume that
m_{0}
has compact support,
\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{0})|^{2}]^{1/2}
is bounded by a constant
C_{m_{0}}
depending only on the support of
m_{0}
. For any
s\neq t
in
[0,T]
, we have:

\displaystyle<\mu_{s}-\mu_{t},\varphi>

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{s})e^{-A_{s}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{s}}]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{t})e^{-A_{t}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\varphi(X_{s})-\varphi(X_{t}))e^{-A_{s}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{s}}]}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{t})(e^{-A_{s}}-e^{-A_{t}})]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{s}}]}+\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{t})e^{-A_{t}}]\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{s}}]}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}\right).

We bound each of the three terms. Below, it is important that
C
is a constant whose value is independent of
\varphi
.

•

For the first term:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\varphi(X_{s})-\varphi(X_{t}))e^{-A_{s}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{s}}]}\right|

\displaystyle\leq e^{T}\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{s})-\varphi(X_{t})|]

\displaystyle\leq e^{T}\mathbb{E}[|X_{s}-X_{t}|]

\displaystyle\leq e^{T}\mathbb{E}[|\int_{t}^{s}-\nabla u(r,X_{r})dr|]

\displaystyle\leq e^{T}\|\nabla u\|_{\infty}|s-t|

\displaystyle\leq C_{T,C_{2}}|s-t|.

•

For the second term:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{t})(e^{-A_{s}}-e^{-A_{t}})]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{s}}]}\right|

\displaystyle\leq e^{T}\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{t})||e^{-A_{s}}-e^{-A_{t}}|]

\displaystyle\leq e^{T}\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{t})|^{2}]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[|e^{-A_{s}}-e^{-A_{t}}|^{2}]^{1/2}.

We first note that
A_{s},A_{t}\in[0,T]
and the exponential function is locally Lipschitz so it is Lipschitz on
[0,T]
. So
|e^{-A_{s}}-e^{-A_{t}}|\leq C|A_{s}-A_{t}|\leq C|s-t|
. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{t})|^{2}]^{1/2}\leq\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{0})|^{2}]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{t})-\varphi(X_{0})|^{2}]^{1/2}
, and
\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{0})|^{2}]^{1/2}<C_{m_{0}}
, while

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{t})-\varphi(X_{0})|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}-X_{0}|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[|\int_{0}^{t}-\nabla u(r,X_{r})dr|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq(T\|\nabla u\|_{\infty})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq T^{2}C_{2}^{2}.

•

To bound
\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{t})e^{-A_{t}}]\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{s}}]}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}\right)
, we first note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{t})|e^{-A_{t}}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{t})-\varphi(X_{0})|]+\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{0})|]

\displaystyle\leq TC_{2}+C_{m_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{T,C_{2},m_{0}}

with similar arguments as above, and moreover
\left|\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{s}}]}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}\right|=\left|\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{s}}]\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}]}(\mathbb{E}[e^{-A_{t}}-e^{-A_{s}}])\right|\leq C_{T}|t-s|
with similar arguments as above.

So we have shown that: there exists a constant
C_{T,C_{2},m_{0}}
such that for every 1-Lipschitz function
\varphi
and every

,
<\mu_{s}-\mu_{t},\varphi>\leq C_{T,C_{2},m_{0}}|t-s|
. Taking the supremum over 1-Lipschitz functions
\varphi
yields:

\sup_{s\neq t}\frac{W_{1}(\mu_{s},\mu_{t})}{|s-t|^{1/2}}\leq C_{1}=\sqrt{T}C_{T,C_{2},m_{0}}
, where
C_{T,C_{2},m_{0}}
is bounded for bounded
T
.

Combining this result with (

Hence, for this

Remember that we assumed

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\int_{0}^{r}F^{1}(s,W_{s},\mu^{1}(s))ds}\|\check{F}\|_{\infty}dr\right]

\displaystyle\leq TC(T+1)(\|\delta\tilde{u}\|_{\infty}+\|\delta\nabla\tilde{u}\|_{\infty}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{1}(\mu^{1}_{t},\mu^{2}_{t})),

where the constant
C
depends on
C_{2}
,
V
and
\|V\|_{\infty}
.

Using again the local Lipschitz property of the exponential function, we deduce that a similar bound holds for
\|\nabla\delta u\|_{\infty}
except that the constant
C
depends on
C_{2}
,
\|V\|_{\infty}
and
\|\nabla V\|_{\infty}
.

Part (v). Bound on
\|D^{2}\delta w\|_{\infty}
.

We can take one more derivative in (
164
) and proceed as we did for
\nabla w
by using Feynman-Kac formula. Using the fact that
u^{i}\in\mathcal{U}
, we deduce that

(169)

\|D^{2}\delta w\|_{\infty}\leq TC(T+1)(\|\delta\tilde{u}\|_{\infty}+\|\delta\nabla\tilde{u}\|_{\infty}+\|\delta D^{2}\tilde{u}\|_{\infty}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{1}(\mu^{1}_{t},\mu^{2}_{t}))

where the constant
C
depends on
C_{2}
,
V
,
\|\nabla V\|_{\infty}
and
\|D^{2}V\|_{\infty}
. Using again the local Lipschitz property of the exponential function, we deduce that a similar bound holds for
\|D^{2}\delta u\|_{\infty}
except that the constant
C
depends on
C_{2}
,
\|V\|_{\infty}
,
\|\nabla V\|_{\infty}
and
\|D^{2}V\|_{\infty}
.

Part (vi). Bound on
\|\partial_{t}\delta w\|_{\infty}
.

Note that
\delta w
solves:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta w(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta\delta w(t,x)+\delta w(t,x)F^{1}(t,x,\mu^{1}(t))+w^{1}(t,x)\delta F(t,x),

where we recall that:

F^{1}(t,x,\mu)=-(V(x)-<\mu,V>)\tilde{u}^{1}(t,x)+V(x)<\mu,\tilde{u}^{1}(t)>+\tilde{f}(x)

and

\displaystyle\delta F(t,x)

\displaystyle=-(V(x)-<\mu^{1}(t),V>)\delta\tilde{u}(t,x)+<\delta\mu(t),V>)u^{2}(t,x)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt+V(x)<\mu^{1}(t),\delta\tilde{u}(t)>+V(x)<\delta\mu(t),\tilde{u}^{2}(t)>.

To bound
\|\partial_{t}\delta w\|_{\infty}
, we will combine the following inequalities:

•

for
D^{2}\delta w
: (
169
)

•

for
\delta w
: (
168
)

•

for
F^{1}
: it is bounded (by a constant depending only on the model’s parameters and
C_{2},C_{3}
) since we assumed
\tilde{f}
is bounded by
C_{\tilde{f}}
,
g
is bounded by
C_{g}
,
(t,x)\mapsto\tilde{u}^{1}(t,x)
is bounded by
C_{\tilde{u}^{1}}\leq C_{2}
, and
2C_{\tilde{u}^{1}}+C_{\tilde{f}}\leq C_{3}

•

for

•

for
\delta F
:

\displaystyle\|\delta F(t,x)\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|V\|_{\infty}\|\delta\tilde{u}\|_{\infty}+C(\|u^{2}\|_{\infty}+\|\tilde{u}^{2}\|_{\infty})\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{1}(\mu^{1}_{t},\mu^{2}_{t}),

.

Combining the above bounds, we obtain:

,
C_{3}
,

,

and

. Using again the local Lipschitz property of the exponential function, we deduce that a similar bound holds for

.

Part (vii). Conclusion.

Combining the above results, we have:

(170)

\|\delta u\|_{\mathcal{U}}\leq\max\{T,\|V\|_{\infty},K(T,C_{g})\}(T+1)C(\|\delta\tilde{u}\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{1}(\mu^{1}_{t},\mu^{2}_{t})).

, on

A.3.
Auxiliary lemma to bound
W_{1}

Lemma 19
.

Let
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
. Let
\mu_{i}=\Psi_{2}(u_{i}),i=1,2
. Then

W_{1}(\mu^{1}_{t},\mu^{2}_{t})\leq TC_{T,L_{\nabla u^{2}}}\|\delta\nabla u\|_{\infty},

where
C
is bounded when
T,L_{\nabla u^{2}}
are bounded.

Proof of Lemma
19
.

First step: Bounding
W_{1}(\mu^{1}_{t},\mu^{2}_{t})
by
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}|^{2}\right]
.

Let us recall that, thanks to the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality theorem, we have:

W_{1}(\mu_{s},\mu_{t})=\inf_{\varphi:1-Lip}\int\varphi(x)d(\mu_{s}(x)-\mu_{t}(x))=\inf_{\varphi:1-Lip,\varphi(0)=0}\int\varphi(x)d(\mu_{s}(x)-\mu_{t}(x))

where the second equality holds because
\mu_{s}
and
\mu_{t}
both have total mass equal to
1
so we can shift the function by a constant without changing the integral. Let
\varphi
be a
1
-Lipschitz function such that
\varphi(0)=0
. Since we assume that
m_{0}
has compact support,
\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{0})|^{2}]^{1/2}
is bounded by a constant
C_{m_{0}}
depending only on the support of
m_{0}
.

Let
\varphi
be a 1-Lipschitz function satisfying
\varphi(0)=0
. We have:

\displaystyle<\mu^{1}_{t}-\mu^{2}_{t},\varphi>

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X^{1}_{t})e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X^{2}_{t})e^{-A^{2}_{t}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{2}_{t}}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\varphi(A^{1}_{t})-\varphi(X^{2}_{t}))e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X^{2}_{t})(e^{-A^{1}_{t}}-e^{-A^{2}_{t}})]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}+\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X^{2}_{t})e^{-A^{2}_{t}}]\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{2}_{t}}]}\right).

We bound each of the three terms. Below, it is important that
C
is a constant whose value is independent of
\varphi
(except for the fact that
\varphi
has Lipschitz constant
1
). Its value may depend on the Lipschitz constant of
V
.

•

\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\varphi(X^{1}_{t})-\varphi(X^{2}_{t}))e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}\right|\leq\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X^{1}_{t})-\varphi(X^{2}_{t})|^{2}]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[e^{-2A^{1}_{t}}]^{1/2}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}\leq C\mathbb{E}[|X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}|^{2}]^{1/2}
using Hölder inequality and the fact that
A^{1}_{t}\in[0,T]
.

•

\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X^{2}_{t})(e^{-A^{1}_{t}}-e^{-A^{2}_{t}})]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}\right|\leq C\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X^{2}_{t})||e^{-A^{1}_{t}}-e^{-A^{2}_{t}}|]\leq C\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X^{2}_{t})|^{2}]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[|e^{-A^{1}_{t}}-e^{-A^{2}_{t}}|^{2}]^{1/2}
. We first note that
A^{1}_{t},A^{2}_{t}\in[0,T]
and the exponential function is locally Lipschitz so it is Lipschitz on
[0,T]
. So
|e^{-A^{1}_{t}}-e^{-A^{2}_{t}}|\leq C|A^{1}_{t}-A^{2}_{t}|\leq C\int_{0}^{t}|V(X^{1}_{s})-V(X^{2}_{s})|ds\leq C\int_{0}^{t}|X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s}|ds
. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X^{2}_{t})|^{2}]^{1/2}\leq\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{0})|^{2}]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X^{2}_{t})-\varphi(X_{0})|^{2}]^{1/2}
, and
\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X_{0})|^{2}]^{1/2}<+\infty
since we assume
m_{0}
has compact support, while
\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X^{2}_{t})-\varphi(X_{0})|^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[|X^{2}_{t}-X_{0}|^{2}]\leq C
where the last constant
C
depends on
\|u\|_{\mathcal{U}}
. As a consequence:

\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X^{2}_{t})(e^{-A^{1}_{t}}-e^{-A^{2}_{t}})]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}\right|\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[|X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s}|^{2}]^{1/2}ds

•

To bound
\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X^{2}_{t})e^{-A^{2}_{t}}]\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{2}_{t}}]}\right)
, we first note that
\mathbb{E}[|\varphi(X^{2}_{t})|e^{-A^{2}_{t}}]\leq C
(roughly same argument as above), and moreover
\left|\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{2}_{t}}]}\right|=\left|\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{2}_{t}}]}(\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{2}_{t}}-e^{-A^{1}_{t}}])\right|\leq C|\mathbb{E}[e^{-A^{2}_{t}}-e^{-A^{1}_{t}}]|\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[|X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s}|^{2}]^{1/2}ds
(same argument as above).

So overall, collecting terms:

\displaystyle<\mu^{1}_{t}-\mu^{2}_{t},\varphi>

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}[|X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}|^{2}]^{1/2},

where the constant
C
is independent of
\varphi
but may depend on
T
(while remaining bounded for bounded
T
) and on the Lipschitz constant of
V
. As consequence,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{1}(\mu^{1}_{t},\mu^{2}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}[|X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}|^{2}]^{1/2}.

Second step: Bounding
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}|^{2}\right]
by
\|\nabla\delta u\|_{\infty}
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[|\nabla u^{1}_{s}(X^{1}_{s})-\nabla u^{2}_{s}(X^{2}_{s})|^{2}\right]ds

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[|\nabla u^{1}_{s}(X^{1}_{s})-\nabla u^{2}_{s}(X^{1}_{s})|^{2}\right]ds+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[|\nabla u^{2}_{s}(X^{1}_{s})-\nabla u^{2}_{s}(X^{2}_{s})|^{2}\right]ds\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(t\|\nabla\delta u\|_{\infty}+L_{\nabla u^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s}|^{2}ds\right]\right)

where
L_{\nabla u^{2}}
is the supremum over
t\in[0,T]
of the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla u^{2}_{t}(\cdot)
.

By Grönwall’s lemma,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}|^{2}\right]\leq TCe^{TL_{\nabla u^{2}}}\|\nabla\delta u\|_{\infty},

where
C
is independent of
u^{i}
.

Combining the the results of the two steps above, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{1}(\mu^{1}_{t},\mu^{2}_{t})\leq TCe^{TL_{\nabla u^{2}}}\|\nabla\delta u\|_{\infty},

where
C
is independent of
u^{i},i=1,2
and is bounded for bounded
T
.
∎
