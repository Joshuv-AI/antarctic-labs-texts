Droplets in Acoustic Fields: A Unified Theory from Migration to Splitting
Jeyapradhap Thirisangu, 1† Varun Kumar Rajendran, 1† Snekan Selvakannan, 1 Sujith Jayakumar, 1 E Hemachandran, 2 and Karthick Subramani 1

Abstract
We present a comprehensive theoretical framework governing the dynamics of droplets in acoustic fields, applicable to all droplet sizes, from the Rayleigh limit ( D << λ D<<\lambda ) and beyond ( D ≳ λ D\gtrsim\lambda ). Our theory elucidates the nature of acoustic forces and incorporates the effects of interfacial tension, enabling predictions of droplet migration, deformation, and splitting under suitable conditions. Importantly, we demonstrate droplet deformation and splitting through bulk acoustic wave (BAW) silicon-glass microscale experiments, which validate the proposed theory.

The applied high-frequency acoustic fields, particularly in the MHz range and above, vary on fast time scales (
t_{f}\sim 1/\omega\sim

t_{s}

{g}_{f}e^{-i\omega t_{f}}

g=g_{s}(\textbf{\emph{r}},t_{s})+g_{f}(\textbf{\emph{r}},t_{s})e^{-i\omega t_{f}}.

(2)

In a homogeneous fluid, the amplitude of the fast time fields (
g_{f}
) remains constant and does not change with the slow time scale (
t_{s}

g_{f}
becomes dependent on the slow time scale. This dependence arises because

is affected by spatial variations in density (
\rho_{s}

{c}_{s}

\frac{\partial g_{s}}{\partial t}\ll\frac{\partial g_{f}}{\partial t}
). To derive the fast and slow time governing equations, the following assumptions are employed:
1) The magnitude of the fast time scale velocity field is much smaller than the speed of the sound of the medium (
\left|\textbf{\emph{v}}_{f}\right|\ll\ {c}
) or acoustic Mach number,
ma_{f}=\left|\textbf{\emph{v}}_{f}\right|/{c}\ll\ 1
.
2) The slow time scale velocity magnitude is much smaller than the fast time scale velocity,
\left|\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}\right|\ll\ \left|\textbf{\emph{v}}_{f}\right|
or the hydrodynamic Mach number,
ma_{s}=\left|\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}\right|/{c}\ll\ ma_{f}
.
3) The gravity and interfacial forces can only contribute to the slow time scale fields and create negligible effects on fast time scale fields.
In this work, the assumption
\bm{\nabla}p_{s}\ll\bm{\nabla}p_{f}
in fast time scale equations cannot be employed, as in previous studies
[

Before delving into a thorough analysis of the interplay between acoustic (
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac}
) and interfacial forces (
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{\sigma}
), these findings can be roughly explained as follows: Acoustic forces try to move high-impedance droplet/fluid to the nearest node. If
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac}<<\textbf{\emph{f}}_{\sigma}
, the droplet migrates to the node and behaves like a rigid particle without noticeable deformation (Fig.
1
c). In contrast, significant droplet deformation occurs if
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac}\gtrsim\textbf{\emph{f}}_{\sigma}
. For droplets smaller than
\lambda/2
, high-impedance droplets are squeezed towards the node (Fig.
1
a). If the size of a droplet exceeds
\lambda/2
, multiple nodes can exist within the droplet, then
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac}
act to squeeze the droplet toward each node. If the
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac}
is sufficiently strong, this deformation can potentially lead to the droplet splitting (Fig.
1
b).

A thorough analysis follows to elucidate the results comprehensively. The time-averaged terms in momentum Eq.
4a
(Eulerian form) acts as an acoustic body force (
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac}
), can also be expressed as the divergence of the Reynolds stress tensor,

\begin{split}\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac}&=-\langle\rho_{f}\partial_{t}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{f}\rangle-\langle\rho_{s}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{f}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{f}\rangle=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\langle\rho_{s}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{f}\otimes\textbf{\emph{v}}_{f}\rangle.\end{split}

(5)

The above equation can be simplified further by neglecting the acoustic streaming effects as follows
[
33
]
,

\begin{split}\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac}&=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\nabla}(\kappa_{s}\langle|p_{f}|^{2}\rangle-\rho_{s}\langle|\textbf{\emph{v}}_{f}|^{2}\rangle)\\
&-\frac{1}{2}(\langle|p_{f}|^{2}\rangle\bm{\nabla}\kappa_{s}+\langle|\textbf{\emph{v}}_{f}|^{2}\rangle\bm{\nabla}\rho_{s})=\bm{\nabla}\phi_{{ac}}+\textbf{\emph{f}}_{{ac1}}.\end{split}

(6)

For the standing acoustic wave applied along the x-direction, (pressure
p_{f}=p_{a}\sin(kx)
and velocity
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{f}=\frac{p_{a}}{i\rho_{s}c_{s}}\cos(kx)
) the non-gradient body force term
({f}_{{ac1}})
becomes
[
33
]
,

\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac1}=-E_{ac}\cos({2kx})\bm{\nabla}\hat{Z},

(7)

where
E_{ac}=p_{a}^{2}/(4\rho_{avg}c_{avg}^{2})
is the acoustic energy density,
p_{a}
is the pressure amplitude,
Z=\rho_{s}c_{s}
denotes impedance,
\hat{Z}=Z/Z_{avg}
. By substituting Eq.
6
and Eq.
7
into the Eq.
4a
and rewriting surface Dirac delta function as Heaviside step function (
H
) results in,

\begin{split}\rho_{s}\partial_{t}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}+\rho_{s}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}=\bm{\nabla}p_{s}^{1}+\eta\bm{\nabla}^{2}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}\\
-E_{ac}\cos({2kx})\bm{\nabla}\hat{Z}+\sigma k\bm{\nabla}H,\end{split}

(8)

where
p_{s1}=p_{s}+{\phi}_{ac}
. It is important to note that assuming the amplitude of acoustic fields or
E_{ac}
remains steady (i.e., unaffected by time or motion of the droplet) in Eq.
8
provides a significantly simpler theoretical approach for studying the droplets compared to standard method of solving two-way coupled fast and slow time equations. This constant
E_{ac}
assumption allows us to directly solve the slow time scale equations (Eq.
8
, in conjunction with the continuity equation (Eq.
4a
) and transport equation (Eq.
4c
) without needing to solve the fast time scale equations. This approach significantly reduces computational time while still capturing all essential qualitative results, as shown in Fig.
2
(a and b). Although the governing equation encompasses all aspects of droplet phenomena, the non-dimensional acoustic Bond number obtained from the momentum equation
\left({\emph{f}}_{ac1}/{\emph{f}}_{\sigma}=E_{ac}\cos(2kx)(Z_{2}-Z_{1})/(\sigma k\right))
completely fails to characterize droplet behaviours (including migration, deformation, and splitting). For example, the above predicts maximum acoustic force at node (
x=0
) and antinode (
x=\pi
), which is clearly not the case. The fundamental problem lies in the fact that
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac1}
and
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{\sigma}
do not entirely compete with each other. Instead, a considerable portion of both
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac1}
and
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{\sigma}
contributes solely to pressure. To address this, one can take the curl of the entire equation, which effectively eliminates the pressure contributions, resulting in

\begin{split}\bm{\nabla}\times(\rho_{s}\partial_{t}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s})+\bm{\nabla}\times(\rho_{s}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s})=\bm{\nabla}\times\left(\eta\bm{\nabla}^{2}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}\right)\\
-\bm{\nabla}\times(E_{ac}\cos({2kx})\bm{\nabla}\hat{Z}\ )+\bm{\nabla}\times(\sigma\kappa\bm{\nabla}H\ ).\end{split}

(9)

Eventually, the acoustic and interfacial forces balance each other, bringing the system to equilibrium or rest (
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}=0
) as shown in Fig. (
1
and
2
), and the above equation simplifies to,

-\bm{\nabla}\times(E_{ac}\cos({2kx})\bm{\nabla}\hat{Z}\ )+\bm{\nabla}\times(\sigma\kappa\nabla H\ )=0.

(10)

The dimensionless form of the above equation results in (
x^{\ast}={x}/{L_{c}}
,
y^{\ast}={y}/{L_{c}}
, and
\kappa^{\ast}={\kappa}{L_{c}}
),

Bo\frac{\partial H}{\partial y^{\ast}}=\left(\frac{\partial\kappa^{\ast}}{\partial x^{\ast}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial y^{\ast}}-\frac{\partial\kappa^{\ast}}{\partial y^{\ast}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^{\ast}}\right),

(11a)

Bo=\frac{2kE_{ac}\sin(2kx)\Delta\hat{Z}}{\sigma/L_{c}^{2}},

(11b)

where
\Delta\hat{Z}=(Z_{2}-Z_{1})/Z_{avg}
. The acoustic Bond number (
Bo
) obtained from the vorticity equation completely captures the interplay between the acoustic and interfacial tension forces. Remarkably, it agrees with the acoustic Bond number governing the stability of immiscible fluids under acoustic fields
[
34
]
. Before demonstrating its accuracy in characterizing droplet deformation and splitting, it is crucial to recognize that acoustics body force
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac1}
(Eq.
7
) can be alternatively expressed similar to the acoustic force component present in the above
Bo
in
11b

(
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac1}=-E_{ac}\cos({2kx})\bm{\nabla}\hat{Z}=-\bm{\nabla}\left(\cos({2kx})\right)-2kE_{ac}\sin({2kx})\hat{Z}\bm{e}_{x}

\bm{e}_{x}
is the unit normal vector along the
x

\left(\textbf{\emph{f}}_{\sigma}=\sigma\kappa\bm{\nabla}H=\bm{\nabla}\left(\sigma\kappa H\right)-\sigma H\bm{\nabla}\kappa\right)

8

\begin{split}\bm{\nabla}\rho_{s}\partial_{t}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}+\rho_{s}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}=\bm{\nabla}p_{s2}+\eta\bm{\nabla}^{2}\langle\textbf{\emph{v}}_{s}\rangle\\
-2kE_{ac}\sin({2kx})\hat{Z}\bm{e}_{x}-\sigma H\bm{\nabla}\kappa,\end{split}

(12)

where
p_{s2}=p_{s1}+\cos({2kx})\hat{Z}+\sigma\kappa H
. Solving Eq.

, we can use the following equation as an acoustic body force,

\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac2}=-2kE_{ac}\sin({2kx})\hat{Z}\bm{e}_{x}.

(13)

The advantage of
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac2}
(Eq.
13

\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac1}
(Eq.
7
) is that the structure of
\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac2}

Eq.

\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac2}
is particularly noteworthy as it enables an intuitive and straightforward prediction/explanation of droplet behavior as follows. Initially, with the acoustic field turned off and the droplet being spherical or circular (

\sigma H\bm{\nabla}\kappa

\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac2}

\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac2}
is weak for the given
\sigma

R

\textbf{\emph{f}}_{ac2}
is strong for the given

Here, we proceed to show how the
Bo
(Eq.
11b
) characterizes droplet deformation and splitting by using the appropriate
L_{c}
. For droplet deformation when high impedance droplet is accommodated between two antinodes (
D<\lambda/2
), the relevant characteristic length becomes droplet radius
L_{c}=R
. Since as
R
increases, interfacial forces becomes weaker. The force acting on the portion of the droplet present at node and antinode is zero, thus appropriate to take
\sin(2kx)\approx\sin(2k\bar{R})
, where
\bar{R}
is the centroid of the droplet. The acoustic bond number (
Bo_{d}
) governing the droplet deformation becomes (Fig.
3
a),

Bo_{d}=\frac{2kE_{ac}\sin(2k\bar{R})\Delta\hat{Z}}{\sigma/{R}^{2}}.

(14)

For splitting, the relevant characteristic length
L_{c}
is
\lambda/2
as acoustic force tries to break/deform the bigger droplet into multiple smaller droplets of size
\lambda/2
as shown in Fig.
2
. Since the bigger droplet covers multiple nodes and antinodes, the droplet’s position and initial radius will not play any major role in splitting,
\sin(2kx)\approx 1
, the bond number governing the splitting becomes (as shown in Fig.
3
b),

Bo_{s}=\frac{2kE_{ac}\Delta\hat{Z}}{\sigma/(\frac{\lambda}{2})^{2}}.

(15)

After splitting,
Bo_{d}
further characterizes the deformation of the daughter droplets.

Discussion: This work can serve as a fundamental tool for studying droplets in acoustic fields. The presented theoretical framework can be employed to study a range of acoustic phenomena, including the acoustic levitation of droplets (such as migration, deformation, and breakup), acoustic fountains, acoustic streaming within droplets, and droplet coalescence, among other phenomena.
