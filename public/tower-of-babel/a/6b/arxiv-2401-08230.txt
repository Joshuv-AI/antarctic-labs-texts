L-series of Eisenstein series vanishing at critical values
Johann Franke

Abstract
. Using the relations between rational functions and Eisenstein series, as well as the inferences for cotangent sums and period polynomials, we work out a precise description for Eisenstein series whose L L -series vanish at certain critical values. This is possible for small weights compared to the level of the Eisenstein series. For large weights we give a partial result and determine subspaces with simultaneous vanishing properties.

1.
Introduction

Cotangent sums can be expressed in the form:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}\cot\left(\frac{\pi hn}{N}\right).

Here,
a_{n}
are arbitrary complex numbers, and
\gcd(h,N)=1
. They reoccur in number-theoretical contexts. For instance, by choosing
a_{n}:=\frac{n}{N}
, the so-called
Vasyunin sum
(see
[
4
]
)

\displaystyle V(h,N):=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{n}{N}\cot\left(\frac{\pi hn}{N}\right)

satisfies the reciprocity law:

\displaystyle V\left(h,k\right)+V\left(k,h\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(2\pi)-\gamma}{\pi}(k+h)+\frac{k-h}{\pi}\log\left(\frac{h}{k}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{hk}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}\left(\frac{h}{k}\right)^{it}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\frac{1}{4}+t^{2}}.

Here
\zeta(s):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-s}
denotes the
Riemann zeta function
. They have been proven to be of importance in the Nyman–Beurling criterion for the Riemann Hypothesis, see
[
4
]
and for more information on Nyman–Beurling,
[
1
]
. For some applications, it is beneficial to consider generalizations

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}\cot^{\nu}\left(\frac{\pi hn}{N}\right)

with integers
\nu\geq 0
. A classical example is

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\cot^{2}\left(\frac{\pi n}{N}\right)=\frac{(N-1)(N-2)}{3},

valid for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Berndt and Yeap
[
3
]
were able to generalize (
1
) to

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\cot^{2n}\left(\frac{\pi j}{N}\right)=(-1)^{n}N-(-1)^{n}2^{2n}\sum_{j_{0}=0}^{n}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},...,j_{2n}\geq 0\\
j_{0}+j_{1}+\cdots+j_{2n}=n\end{subarray}}\prod_{r=0}^{2n}\frac{B_{2j_{r}}}{(2j_{r})!}\right)N^{2j_{0}}

valid for
n,N\in\mathbb{N}
. Here,
B_{n}
denotes the
n
-th Bernoulli number. The proofs behind these identities are of complex analytic nature. In
[
10
]
, (
2
) was extended to the case of Dirichlet characters, establishing a connection to values of Dirichlet
L
-functions
L(\chi;s):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi(n)n^{-s}
. In this paper, we take a step further and provide a structural description of the mentioned connections. These structures are “modular” in nature. The background for this is the interplay between rational functions and modular forms, which was first elaborated in
[
9
]
. Note that the relationship between so called hyperbolic cotangent sums and Eisenstein series has also already been considered by Berndt and Straub
[
2
]
in the context of Ramanujan identities for odd zeta values. A crucial ingredient for our point of view is the concept of weak functions, which are meromorphic and 1-periodic functions defined throughout
\mathbb{C}
, having simple poles only at rational places and vanishing at
\pm i\infty
. The location of poles at
\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
determines the level
N
of a weak function
\omega\in W_{N}
. Pairs of weak functions of possibly different levels can generate modular forms, thus giving rise to a linear map from
W_{M}\otimes W_{N}
to a space of modular forms. These show up to be Eisenstein series with specific properties. In this paper, we exploit the Eichler duality developed in
[
10
]
to provide precise statements about values of
L
-series for certain Eisenstein series. At this point, we recall that to any modular form
f(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}e^{\frac{2\pi in}{\lambda}}
of weight
k
for a congruence subgroup
\Gamma
(where
\lambda>0
is some contant determined by
\Gamma
) we define the corresponding
L
-series by

(3)

\displaystyle L(f;s):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{s}}\qquad(\mathrm{Re}(s)>k).

Let
k\geq 3
be an integer. The spaces
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}
prove advantageous, spanning exactly from

\displaystyle E_{k}\left(\chi,\psi;p_{2}\tau\right):=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus{(0,0)}}\chi(m)\psi(n)(mp_{2}\tau+n)^{-k}

with odd prime numbers
p_{1},p_{2}
and nontrivial Dirichlet characters
\chi
and
\psi
modulo
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
such that
\chi(-1)\psi(-1)=(-1)^{k}
.

Theorem 1.1

(see also Theorem
4.3
)
.

Let
p_{1},p_{2}
be odd primes,
3\leq k\leq\min\{p_{1}-2,p_{2}-2\}
an integer, and
0\leq\ell_{1}\leq p_{1}-2
,
0\leq\ell_{2}\leq p_{2}-2
, with
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\leq k-1
. Let
\mathcal{S}\subset\{0,\ldots,k-2\}
be an arbitrary subset. Then there exists a computable subspace
U\subset W_{p_{1}}^{0}\otimes W_{p_{2}}^{0}
(depending on
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
and
\mathcal{S}
) and an equally computable linear map
\xi\colon U\to\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}
, such that the following sequence is exact:

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow U\overset{\xi}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}\overset{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},k}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\longrightarrow 0,

where the linear map
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},k}\colon\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}\to\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},k}(f):=\frac{(-2\pi i)^{k}}{(k-2)!}\frac{p_{2}^{k-1}}{4\pi^{2}}\left(\binom{k-2}{\ell}i^{1-\ell}(2\pi)^{-\ell-1}\Gamma(\ell+1)L\left(f;\ell+1\right)p_{1}^{\ell}\right)_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}.

We would like to emphasize at this point that the shape of the spaces
U
results precisely from the formalism of the weak functions. Moreover, how to precisely compute the space
U
and the map
\xi
is described further below. It should be emphasized that this is also achievable algorithmically by determining the kernel of a Vandermonde matrix with cotangent bases, see Proposition
3.2
. Background of this is the interplay between cotangent sums and Taylor coefficients of weak functions, as obtained in
[
10
]
. This process gives rise to the selection of an appropriate basis for
W_{p_{1}}^{0}\otimes W_{p_{2}}^{0}
to describe
U
. With Theorem
1.1
, vector spaces of modular forms, whose
L
-values vanish at specific critical points, can be precisely described, provided that the weight
k
is not too large compared to the primes
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. We can also derive dimension formulas in these cases, see Corollary
4.5
.

The case of a large weight
k
is more difficult, and precise statements are probably no longer possible here with our method. However, one can give estimates in the form that modular forms can be calculated whose
L
-values vanish in certain ranges. Of particular interest here are “boundary regions” of the form
\{1,\ldots,\ell_{1}\}\cup\{k-\ell_{2},\ldots,k-1\}
, because the simultaneous vanishing of
L
-values can be directly related here to the complex analytic zero orders of weak functions. The definition of these orders are provided in Definition
3.12
.

Theorem 1.2

(see also Theorem
4.6
)
.

Let
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
be two odd prime numbers and
k\geq 3
be an integer. Let
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
be integers such that
\max\{0,p_{2}-k-1\}\leq\ell_{1}\leq p_{1}-2
,
\max\{0,p_{1}-k-1\}\leq\ell_{2}\leq p_{2}-2
, and
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\leq k-1
. We assign
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
a space

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}:=\left<f\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}\Big|\ \mathrm{ord}\left(\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}^{-1}(f)\right)\geq(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\right>,

where
\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}
is the isomorphism defined in (
22
). Then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}\subset\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\{0,\ldots,\ell_{1}-1\}\cup\{k-1-\ell_{2},\ldots,k-2\}}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})},

where
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\{0,\ldots,\ell_{1}-1\}\cup\{k-1-\ell_{2},\ldots,k-2\}}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}
is the subspace of all elements

f\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}
such that
L(f;j)=0
for all
1\leq j\leq\ell_{1}
and
k-\ell_{2}\leq j\leq k-1
.

Note that Theorem
1.2
, quite unlike Theorem
1.1
, is particularly applicable to large weights. As far as the author knows, cusp forms do not come from rational functions, so the methods presented do not seem to be applicable to these types of modular forms. Nevertheless, critical
L
-values in the context of cusp forms are of utmost importance for mathematics, for example in the context of the conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (see for example
[
12
]
,
[
13
]
). Note that recently Males, Mono, Rolen and Wagner
[
15
]
characterized the vanishing of twisted central
L
-values attached to newforms of square-free level in terms of so-called local polynomials and the action of finitely many Hecke operators thereon.

Example 1.3
.

We demonstrate the numerical aspect of Theorem
1.2
by providing an explicit example. Let
\chi_{5}
be the Dirichlet character modulo
5
with
\chi_{5}(2)=i
. Note that the interested reader can find more details in Example
4.7
. For every even weight
k\geq 4
, the
\Gamma_{1}(25)
modular form

\displaystyle f(\tau)=CE_{k}(\overline{\chi_{5}},\overline{\chi_{5}};5\tau)+5\left(E_{k}(\overline{\chi_{5}},\chi_{5};5\tau)+E_{k}(\chi_{5},\overline{\chi_{5}};5\tau)\right)+\overline{C}E_{k}(\chi_{5},\chi_{5};5\tau),

where

\displaystyle C:=-i(-3-4i)^{\frac{3}{4}}(-3+4i)^{\frac{1}{4}},

has the

-function

\displaystyle L(f;s)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(-2\pi i)^{k}}{5^{k}(k-1)!}\left(C\mathcal{G}(\overline{\chi_{5}})L(\overline{\chi_{5}};s)L(\chi_{5};s-k+1)+5\mathcal{G}(\chi_{5})L(\overline{\chi_{5}};s)L(\overline{\chi_{5}};s-k+1)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+5\mathcal{G}(\overline{\chi_{5}})L(\chi_{5};s)L(\chi_{5};s-k+1)+\overline{C}\mathcal{G}(\chi_{5})L(\chi_{5};s)L(\overline{\chi_{5}};s-k+1)\right).

It satisfies

\displaystyle L(f;1)=L(f;2)=L(f;k-2)=L(f;k-1)=0.

Note that, as
\chi_{5}
is an odd character, only the zeros at
s=1
and

are non-trivial.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we recall some preliminaries in the theory of Eisenstein series and weak functions. In Section 3 we develop a theory of orders of weak functions, and establish connections to the ordering of holomorphic functions of several variables. Finally, in Section 4 we apply this to certain Eisenstein series and their
L
-series.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we collect some basic facts and known results needed for our later investigations.

2.1.
Discrete Fourier transforms

For any Dirichlet character
\psi
modulo
N
we define the Gauss sum
\mathcal{G}(\psi):=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\psi(n)e^{2\pi in/N}
. For the generalized Gauss sum it will be more convenient to use the more general notion of the
discrete Fourier transform
, which is defined for
N
-periodic functions
f
by

(\mathcal{F}_{N}f)(j):=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}f(n)e^{-\frac{2\pi ijn}{N}}.

Note that we have an inverse transformation

(\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{N}g)(j):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}g(n)e^{\frac{2\pi ijn}{N}}.

2.2.
Eisenstein series

In this section, we recall some basic facts about Eisenstein series.

Definition 2.1
.

For two Dirichlet characters
\chi
and
\psi
modulo
N_{\chi}
and
N_{\psi}
and any integer
k\geq 3
we define the corresponding Eisenstein series via

(4)

\displaystyle E_{k}\left(\chi,\psi;\tau\right):=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}}\chi(m)\psi(n)(m\tau+n)^{-k}.

As usual, we denote

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(N):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\Big|\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&*\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\ \pmod{N}\right\}.

The following is well-known.

Theorem 2.2

(see Chapter 7 of
[
17
]
)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\chi
and
\psi
be Dirichlet characters modulo
N_{\chi}
and
N_{\psi}
satisfying
\chi(-1)\psi(-1)=(-1)^{k}
. Then we have the following.

(i)

Every Eisenstein series admits a Fourier series that converges on the entire upper half plane. It is given by

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76228ptE_{k}\left(\chi,\psi;\tau\right)=2L(\psi;k)\chi(0)+\frac{2(-2\pi i)^{k}}{N_{\psi}^{k}(k-1)!}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{d|m}d^{k-1}(\mathcal{F}_{N_{\psi}}\psi)(-d)\chi\left(\frac{m}{d}\right)\right)q^{\frac{m}{N_{\psi}}},

where as usual
q:=e^{2\pi i\tau}
. In particular, if
\psi
is a primitive character, this simplifies to

\displaystyle E_{k}\left(\chi,\psi;\tau\right)=2L(\psi;k)\chi(0)+\frac{2(-2\pi i)^{k}}{N_{\psi}^{k}(k-1)!}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{d|m}d^{k-1}\overline{\psi}(d)\chi\left(\frac{m}{d}\right)\right)q^{\frac{m}{N_{\psi}}}.

(ii)

All the
E_{k}(\chi,\psi;\tau)
are holomorphic modular forms of weight
k
for the group

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(N_{\chi},N_{\psi}):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\Big|c\equiv 0\ \pmod{N_{\chi}},b\equiv 0\ \pmod{N_{\psi}}\right\}.

More precisely, one has the transformation law

\displaystyle E_{k}(\chi,\psi;\tau)|_{k}\gamma=\nu(\gamma)E_{k}(\chi,\psi;\tau),

where the multiplier system
\nu:\Gamma_{0}(N_{\chi},N_{\psi})\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
is defined by
\nu\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right):=\chi(d)\overline{\psi}(d)
. In particular, for integers
N_{\chi}\mid M
and
N_{\psi}\mid N
all
E_{k}(\chi,\psi;N\tau)
are modular forms of weight
k
for the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{1}(MN)
with trivial multiplier system.

In general, Eisenstein series can be seen as the counter part of cusp forms in the theory of modular forms. Let
\mathcal{M}_{k}(\Gamma)
be the space of weight
k
modular forms for the congruence subgroup
\Gamma
. It is easy to see that the subset of cusp forms, i.e., modular forms that vanish in all cusps
\mathbb{Q}\cup\{i\infty\}
, forms a subspace
\mathcal{S}_{k}(\Gamma)
of
\mathcal{M}_{k}(\Gamma)
. The so called Eisenstein space can be defined as the quotient

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma):=\mathcal{M}_{k}(\Gamma)/\mathcal{S}_{k}(\Gamma).

In the following, we mainly focus on the congruence subgroups
\Gamma_{1}(N)
since it appears that this choice is the most natural for our purposes. The useful proposition below presents a basis for the space
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))
.

Theorem 2.3

(see Theorem 4.5.2 of
[
7
]
)
.

Let
k\geq 3
and
N>2
be integers. Let the set
A_{N,k}
consist of all triples
(\chi,\psi,t)
, where
\chi
and
\psi
are characters modulo
N_{\chi}
and
N_{\psi}
, respectively, and
t
is a positive integer, such that the following is satisfied:

(i)

The characters
\psi
and
\chi
are primitive and satisfy
\chi(-1)\psi(-1)=(-1)^{k}
.

(ii)

For the moduli
N_{\chi}
and
N_{\psi}
, the divisor relation
N_{\chi}N_{\psi}t\mid N
holds.

Then, the system
\left\{E_{k}\left(\chi,\psi;tN_{\psi}\tau\right)\right\}_{(\chi,\psi,t)\in A_{N,k}}
defines a basis of
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))
.

Let
f(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}q^{\frac{n}{\lambda}}
be a modular form of weight
k
for a congruence subgroup
\Gamma
with
L
-series
L(f;s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}n^{-s}
. Note that the parameter
\lambda>0
is chosen to be the width of the cusp
i\infty
with respect to
\Gamma
. It makes sense considering the completed
L
-series, usually denoted by
\Lambda
:

\displaystyle\Lambda(f;s):=\left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\right)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(f;s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(ix)x^{s-1}\mathrm{d}x.

As we have
a_{n}=O(n^{k-1})
for modular forms of weight
k\geq 3
the series for
L(f;s)
converges absolutely for values
s
with
\mathrm{Re}(s)>k
. It can be continued to a holomorphic function on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{k\}
with a possible pole at
s=k
and satisfies a functional equation. One can describe the
L
-series corresponding to Eisenstein series in terms of Dirichlet
L
-series as follows.

Theorem 2.4

(see Theorem 4.7.1. and p. 271 of
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\chi
and
\psi
be primitive Dirichlet characters modulo
N_{\chi}
and
N_{\psi}
, respectively. Then we obtain for
f(\tau):=E_{k}(\chi,\psi;N_{\psi}\tau)
the
L
-series

\displaystyle L(f;s)=\frac{2(-2\pi i)^{k}\mathcal{G}(\psi)}{N_{\psi}^{k}(k-1)!}L(\chi;s)L\left(\overline{\psi};s-k+1\right).

As in the case of cusp forms, one can also define the concept of newforms for Eisenstein series.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
N
be a positive integer and
\chi
,
\psi
be primitive characters modulo
N_{\chi}
and
N_{\psi}
, respectively, such that
N=N_{\chi}N_{\psi}
and
\chi(-1)\psi(-1)=(-1)^{k}
. Then we call the Eisenstein series
E_{k}(\chi,\psi;N_{\psi}\tau)
a newform of level
N
. We denote the space generated by newforms of level
N
by
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))^{\mathrm{new}}
. In the case of fixed conductors
u
and
v
with
uv=N
, we write

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))_{0}^{\mathrm{new},(u,v)}:=\left<E_{k}(\chi,\psi;v\tau)\Big|\chi\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{prim}}_{0}(u),\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{prim}}_{0}(v)\right>,

which is clearly a subspace of
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))^{\mathrm{new}}
. Here,
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{prim}}_{0}(N)
is the set of all non-principal, primitive characters modulo
N
.

In our following work, it makes sense that we specialize in modular forms that vanish at the cusps
0
and
i\infty
. In the case of Eisenstein series, this means that we have to restrict ourselves to non-principal characters. This is an easy consequence of Theorem
2.2
(i). This motivates our definition of the spaces
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))_{0}^{\mathrm{new},(u,v)}
above. The reader is reminded that we will assign a
0
to vector spaces that are constrained with respect to this principle. For example, on the weak functions side, this means that we have a removable singularity in
z=0
. In this work, we focus on spaces
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(MN))
, where
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
. It is natural for us to only consider the newforms with characters modulo
M
and
N
, respectively, and we denote the corresponding subspace by

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(MN))_{0}^{(M,N)}

(5)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt:=\left<E_{k}(\chi,\psi;N\tau)\Big|\chi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\mathrm{prim}}(N_{\chi}),\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\mathrm{prim}}(N_{\psi}),N_{\chi}\mid M,N_{\psi}\mid N,(\chi\psi)(-1)=(-1)^{k}\right>.

It is easy to verify, that this is indeed a subspace of
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(MN))
and that the generating elements are linearly independent.

Proposition 2.6
.

We have
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(MN))_{0}^{(M,N)}\subset\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(MN))
, and the generating elements in (
5
) are linearly independent.

Proof.

By assumption, the characters
\chi
modulo
N_{\chi}
and
\psi
modulo
N_{\psi}
are primitive. Since we have

\displaystyle E_{k}\left(\chi,\psi;N\tau\right)=E_{k}\left(\chi,\psi;\frac{N}{N_{\psi}}N_{\psi}\tau\right)

with
t=\frac{N}{N_{\psi}}
and
N_{\chi}N_{\psi}t=N_{\chi}N\mid MN
, the claim follows immediately with Theorem
2.3
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

Note that, by Theorem
2.2
, all modular forms in space
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(MN))_{0}^{(M,N)}
have the property of vanishing in the cusps
0
and
i\infty
.

We set
B_{d}
to be the linear operator

\displaystyle(f|B_{d})(z):=f|_{k}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}d&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)(z)=f(dz),

for arbitrary values of

. Shifting the argument of a modular form in the above way does essentially not effect its completed

-series, since we have the formula

(6)

\displaystyle\Lambda(f|B_{d};s)=d^{-s}\Lambda(f;s).

2.3.
Weak functions and modular forms

In this section we recall the theory around weak functions, that were introduced as rational functions in
[
9
]
in the context of modular forms.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
N
be a positive integer. We call a meromorphic function on the entire plane
\omega
a weak function of level
N
, if it is 1-periodic, holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}
with possible poles of order at most 1 in
z=\tfrac{j}{N}\in\mathbb{Q}
and satisfies the grwoth condition

\displaystyle\omega(x+iy)=O\left(|y|^{-A}\right),\qquad y\rightarrow\infty,

for all values
A>0
. We collect all weak functions with level
N
in the vector space
W_{N}
.

Remark 2.9
.

As in the theory of modular forms, the notion of level is not uniquely determined at first, since this can be raised by lifts to smaller congruence subsets. This is differentiated with the concept of new and old forms. Something similar is possible on the weak functions side. So we can speak of a new function with level
N
if there is a real pole at a point
\frac{j}{N}
with
\gcd(j,N)=1
.

By Liouville’s theorem it is immediate that

\displaystyle\omega(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\beta^{\omega}(j)\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{j}{N})-e(z)},\qquad e(z):=e^{2\pi iz},

for some coefficients
\beta^{\omega}(j)
satsifying

(7)

\displaystyle\beta^{\omega}(1)+\beta^{\omega}(2)+\cdots+\beta^{\omega}(N)=0.

In particular, the spaces
W_{N}
are finite dimensional. In addition, weak functions are very closely linked to Eisenstein series. To see this, for an integer
k
and a pair
\omega\otimes\eta
define the following holomorphic function on the upper half plane:

(8)

\displaystyle\vartheta_{k}(\omega\otimes\eta;\tau):=-2\pi i\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}}\mathrm{res}_{z=x}\left(z^{k-1}\eta(z)\omega(\tau z)\right).

Due to symmetry arguments, the map
\vartheta_{k}
on
W_{M}\otimes W_{N}
is highly non-injective. Depending on
k
, it makes sense to restrict to suitable subspaces. Let
W_{N}=W_{N}^{+}\oplus W_{N}^{-}
be the decomposition into even and odd functions, respectively. Then we put

\displaystyle(W_{M}\otimes W_{N})_{k}:=\begin{cases}W_{M}^{+}\otimes W_{N}^{+}\oplus W_{M}^{-}\otimes W_{N}^{-},&\qquad\text{if }k\text{ is even},\\
W_{M}^{+}\otimes W_{N}^{-}\oplus W_{M}^{-}\otimes W_{N}^{+},&\qquad\text{if }k\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

We also say that even and odd functions
\omega
have positive or negative sign
\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)
, respectively, and put (if possible)
\mathrm{sgn}(\omega\otimes\eta):=\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)\mathrm{sgn}(\eta)
. As
\vartheta_{k}(\omega\otimes\eta;\tau)
induces a periodic function, so we can find a Fourier series.

Proposition 2.10
.

For
\omega\otimes\eta\in(W_{M}\otimes W_{N})_{k}
and
k\geq 3
, we have the Fourier expansion

\displaystyle\vartheta_{k}(\omega\otimes\eta;\tau)=2N^{1-k}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d|m}\left(d^{k-1}\beta^{\eta}(d)\left(\mathcal{F}_{M}\beta^{\omega}\right)\left(\frac{m}{d}\right)\right)q^{\frac{m}{N}}.

This function satisfies an important transformation law.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
\omega
and
\eta
be weak functions of level
N
. We then have
\vartheta_{k}(\omega\otimes\eta;\tau+N)=\vartheta_{k}(\omega\otimes\eta;\tau)
and

(9)

\displaystyle\vartheta_{k}\left(\omega\otimes\eta;-\frac{1}{\tau}\right)=-\tau^{k}\vartheta_{k}(\eta\otimes\widehat{\omega};\tau)+2\pi i\,\mathrm{res}_{z=0}\left(z^{k-1}\eta(z)\widehat{\omega}\left(\frac{z}{\tau}\right)\right),

where
\widehat{\omega}(z):=\omega(-z)
is again weak of level
N
.

The concepts of Fourier transforms are useful when expressing terms of the form
\vartheta_{k}(\omega\otimes\eta;\tau)
as modular forms, as the followig proposition shows.

Proposition 2.12

(see
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\chi
and
\psi
be non-principal Dirichlet characters modulo
M
and
N
, respectively. Then the following identity holds:

(10)

\displaystyle E_{k}(\chi,\psi;\tau)=\frac{\psi(-1)(-2\pi i)^{k}}{N(k-1)!}\vartheta_{k}\left(\omega_{\mathcal{F}_{M}^{-1}(\chi)}\otimes\omega_{\mathcal{F}_{N}(\psi)};\tau\right).

In particular, if
\chi
and
\psi
are primitive and hence conjugate up to a constant under the Forurier transform, formula (
10
) simplifies to the important identity

(11)

\displaystyle E_{k}(\chi,\psi;\tau)=\frac{\chi(-1)(-2\pi i)^{k}\mathcal{G}(\psi)}{N(k-1)!\mathcal{G}(\overline{\chi})}\vartheta_{k}(\omega_{\overline{\chi}}\otimes\omega_{\overline{\psi}};\tau).

In summary, the following statements can be made about purely complex analytic means:

Theorem 2.13
.

Let
k\geq 3
and
M,N>1
be integers and define the congruence subgroup

\Gamma_{1}(M,N):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(M,N)\Big|a\equiv d\equiv 1\pmod{MN}\right\}.

Let
M_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(M,N))
the space of weight
k
holomorphic modular forms for
\Gamma_{1}(M,N)
. There is a homomorphism

W_{M}\otimes W_{N}\longrightarrow M_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(M,N))

\omega\otimes\eta\longmapsto\vartheta_{k}(\omega\otimes\eta;\tau):=-2\pi i\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}}\mathrm{res}_{z=x}\left(z^{k-1}\eta(z)\omega(z\tau)\right).

In the case that
k=1
and
k=2
the map stays well-defined under the restriction that the function
z\mapsto z^{k-1}\eta(z)\omega(z\tau)
has a removable singularity in
z=0
.

In some situations, the occurrence of singularities in
z=0
is pathological. For instance, Theorem
2.13
shows that in these cases modular forms are no longer generated for small weights. In this paper we also want to avoid such singularities. Therefore, it is convenient that we restrict ourselves to the subspaces
W_{N}^{0}\subset W_{N}
consisting of all weak functions which have removable singularities in
z=0
. Of course we can (and need) to play the same game with
W_{N}^{0}
regarding even and odd parts, and put

\displaystyle(W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0})_{k}:=(W_{M}\otimes W_{N})_{k}\cap(W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0}).

In addition, it makes sense to include Dirichlet character theory when choosing a basis of
W_{N}^{0}
. This has the great advantage that we get correspondences to Eisenstein series on characters on the other side. The situation of a prime level is particularly easy.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
p
be an odd prime. For any Dirichlet character
\chi
modulo
p
, put

\displaystyle\omega_{\chi}(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\chi(j)\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{j}{p})-e(z)}.

Then,
(\omega_{\chi})_{\chi\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{prim}}_{0}(p)}
is a basis of
W_{p}^{0}
. Furthermore, the sets
(\omega_{\chi\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{prim}}_{0}(p),\chi(-1)=\mp 1})
define a basis for
W_{p}^{0,\pm}
, respectively.

Proof.

Every weak function
\omega\in W_{p}^{0}
has a removable singularity in
z=0
, so it is of the form

\displaystyle\omega(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\beta^{\omega}(j)\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{j}{p})-e(z)}.

The assertion now follows with the facts that every nonprincipal Dirichlet character modulo the prime
p
is already primitive, the orthogonality relations and (
7
), and that these characters are all linearly independent. For the second claim note that
\omega_{\chi}
is an even or odd function, if and only if the generating coefficients
\beta^{\omega}
define odd or even functions, respectively.
∎

Of course, similar statements apply to non-prime levels as well, but in this paper we want to focus on prime levels, so we will not go into this further.

As in the case of primitive characters
\chi
and
\psi
, the functions
\vartheta_{k}(\omega_{\chi}\otimes\omega_{\psi};\tau)
are essentially the corresponding Eisenstein series, we can easily conclude the following proposition.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
be prime numbers and
k\geq 3
an integer. Then the linear map

\displaystyle\vartheta_{k}\colon\left(W_{p_{1}}^{0}\otimes W_{p_{2}}^{0}\right)_{k}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}

\displaystyle\omega\otimes\eta

\displaystyle\longmapsto\vartheta_{k}(\omega\otimes\eta;p_{2}\tau)

is an isomorphism.

Proof.

The elements
(\omega_{\chi_{1}}\otimes\omega_{\chi_{2}})_{\chi_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\mathrm{prim}(p_{1})},\chi_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\mathrm{prrim}}(p_{2}),\chi_{1}(-1)\chi_{2}(-1)=(-1)^{k}}
define a basis of
(W^{0}_{p_{1}}\otimes W^{0}_{p_{2}})_{k}
by Proposition
2.14
. Recall that with (
11
)

\displaystyle E_{k}(\chi,\psi;p_{2}\tau)=\frac{\chi(-1)(-2\pi i)^{k}\mathcal{G}(\psi)}{N(k-1)!\mathcal{G}(\overline{\chi})}\vartheta_{k}(\omega_{\overline{\chi}}\otimes\omega_{\overline{\psi}};p_{2}\tau),

so there is a 1-1-correspondence between basis vectors of
\left(W_{p_{1}}^{0}\otimes W_{p_{2}}^{0}\right)_{k}
and
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}
by Proposition
2.6
(note that the only divisors of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are
\{1,p_{1}\}
and
\{1,p_{2}\}
, but there are no non-principal characters modulo
1
). The claim now follows.
∎

Another tool which we require is the
Fourier transform of a weak function
. We transform the coefficient function
\beta^{\omega}(j)
of some weak function
\omega
and use the result to construct a new weak funtion. If
\beta(N)=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{F}_{N}(\beta)(n)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\beta(j)e^{-\frac{2\pi inj}{N}}=N\beta(N)=0.

On the other hand, we clearly have
\mathcal{F}_{N}(\beta)(0)=0
and hence the function
\mathcal{F}_{N}(\beta)
defines again a weak function

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}\omega(z):=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\mathcal{F}_{N}(\beta)(n)\frac{e(z)}{e\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)-e(z)}.

This gives rise to the statement that
\mathcal{F}_{N}
defines an automorphism on the space
W_{N}^{\mathrm{ord}\geq 0}
, when considering the inverse transform

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}^{-1}(\beta)(j):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta(n)e^{\frac{2\pi ijn}{N}}.

2.4.
Eichler integrals and period polynomials

To any modular form
f(\tau)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}q^{\frac{n}{\lambda}}
of weight
k\geq 2
for some congruence subgroup that vanishes in the cusps in
\tau=0
and
\tau=i\infty
, we can associate an Eichler integral. It has the form

\mathcal{I}(f;\tau):=\frac{(-2\pi i)^{k-1}}{(k-2)!}\int\limits_{\tau}^{i\infty}f(z)(z-\tau)^{k-2}\mathrm{d}z.

This integral represents a holomorphic and periodic function on the upper half plane and is tied to the so-called
period polynomial

P(f;\tau)
of
f
via the functional equation

\mathcal{I}\left(f;\tau\right)-(-1)^{k}\tau^{k-2}\mathcal{I}\left(f^{*};-\frac{1}{\tau}\right)=:P(f;\tau),

where
f^{*}=f|_{k}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Explicitely, we have a correspondence to the critical values of the
L
-series associated to
f
via

P(f;\tau)=(-1)^{k}\sum_{n=0}^{k-2}{k-2\choose n}i^{1-n}\Lambda(f;n+1)\tau^{k-2-n}.

We mention that period polynomials have several applications. They arise natuarally in the context of the Eichler-Shimura isomorphism (see
[
5
]
, Chapter 11), derivatives of
L
-functions
[
6
]
, Manin’s Periods Theorem
[
16
]
and the theory of transcendental numbers
[
11
]
.

Since Eisenstein series come from rational functions, we can express their Eichler integrals in terms of residues of weak functions. The easiest case of primitive characters is presented in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.16

(see Theorem 4.15 of
[
10
]
)
.

Let
k\geq 3
be an integer,
\chi
and
\psi
be two primitive Dirichlet characters with
\chi(-1)\psi(-1)=(-1)^{k}
and
f(\tau)=E_{k}(\chi,\psi;\tau)
. We then have the following identity between rational functions:

\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-2}{k-2\choose\ell}i^{1-\ell}\Lambda(f;\ell+1)\tau^{\ell}=\frac{4\pi^{2}\chi(-1)}{N_{\psi}^{k-1}N_{\chi}(k-1)}\mathrm{res}_{z=0}\left(z^{1-k}\omega_{\psi}(z)\omega_{\chi}\left(\frac{N_{\psi}z\tau}{N_{\chi}}\right)\right).

This is a special consequence of a duality principle called
Eichler duality
, which was formulated using rational functions in
[
10
]
. It takes on a particularly simple form here, as the characters involved are primitive and therefore (except for complex conjugation) eigenfunctions under the discrete Fourier transformation. Note that we can reformulate this theorem regarding the choice of the modular form
f
. When setting
g(\tau):=(f|B_{N_{\psi}})(\tau)=E_{k}(\chi,\psi;N_{\psi}\tau)
in
\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N_{\psi}N_{\chi}))
, one finds with (
6
) that

(12)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-2}{k-2\choose\ell}i^{1-\ell}\Lambda(g;\ell+1)N_{\chi}^{\ell+1}\tau^{\ell}=\frac{4\pi^{2}\chi(-1)}{N_{\psi}^{k}(k-1)}\mathrm{res}_{z=0}\left(z^{1-k}\omega_{\psi}(z)\omega_{\chi}\left(z\tau\right)\right).

3.
The order of weak functions

There is a close relationship between the zero order of a weak function at the origin and the zero behavior of the
L
-series corresponding to its Eisenstein series. Therefore, the aim of this section is to study the spaces

\displaystyle W_{N}^{0,\mathrm{ord}\geq\ell}:=\{\omega\in W^{0}_{N}\mid\mathrm{ord}_{z=0}(\omega)\geq\ell\}

in detail. Note that
W_{N}^{0,\mathrm{ord}\geq 0}=W_{N}^{0}
. The behavior of order encodes important information about
L
-series at critical values. Since we are mainly interested in the order of a weak function in
z=0
, we will
\mathrm{ord}_{z=0}(\omega)
simply call order of
\omega
and write
\mathrm{ord}(\omega)
. Although we plan to focus entirely on prime levels later, we will show some results in this section for more general levels if the general case is not harder than the prime case.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
N\geq 3
,
m\geq 0
be integers. Define the following
(m+1)\times(N-1)
-Vandermonde matrix:

\displaystyle\mathrm{CotM}(N,m):=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&\cdots&1\\
\cot\left(\tfrac{\pi}{N}\right)&\cot\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{N}\right)&\cot\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{N}\right)&\cdots&\cot\left(\frac{(N-1)\pi}{N}\right)\\
\cot^{2}\left(\tfrac{\pi}{N}\right)&\cot^{2}\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{N}\right)&\cot^{2}\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{N}\right)&\cdots&\cot^{2}\left(\frac{(N-1)\pi}{N}\right)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\cot^{m}\left(\tfrac{\pi}{N}\right)&\cot^{m}\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{N}\right)&\cot^{m}\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{N}\right)&\cdots&\cot^{m}\left(\frac{(N-1)\pi}{N}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

In the next proposition we give a formula for the order of a weak function.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\omega\not=0
be a weak function of level
N
that has a removable singulariy in
z=0
, this means
\beta(0)=0
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\omega(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\beta^{\omega}(j)\frac{e(z)}{e\left(\frac{j}{N}\right)-e(z)}.

Then we have the formula

\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}(\omega)=\sup\left\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\begin{pmatrix}\beta^{\omega}(1)\\
\beta^{\omega}(2)\\
\vdots\\
\beta^{\omega}(N-1)\end{pmatrix}\in\ker(\mathrm{CotM}(N,m))\right\}.

In the case that
\omega=0
, this formula formally gives
\mathrm{ord}(\omega)=\infty
.

Proof.

In
[
10
]
the local Taylor expansion of
\omega
around
z=0
was calculated in terms of cotangent sums:

\displaystyle\omega(z)

\displaystyle=\omega(i\infty)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\beta^{\omega}(j)-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{u=0}^{\nu+1}\delta_{\nu+1}(u)\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\beta^{\omega}(r)\cot^{u}\left(\frac{\pi r}{N}\right)\right)(z\pi)^{\nu}

(13)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{u=0}^{\nu+1}\delta_{\nu+1}(u)\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\beta^{\omega}(r)\cot^{u}\left(\frac{\pi r}{N}\right)\right)(z\pi)^{\nu}.

The
\delta_{\nu}(u)
are rational numbers that can be calculated explicitely by

\displaystyle\delta_{\nu}(u):=\frac{i^{\nu+u}}{(\nu-1)!}\sum_{\ell=u-1}^{\nu-1}(-1)^{\nu+\ell-u}2^{\nu-1-\ell}S^{*}(\nu-1,\ell)\left({\ell\choose u}-{\ell\choose u-1}\right).

Here, the numbers
S^{*}(n,m)
are related to the Stirling numbers of the second kind
\left\{{n\atop m}\right\}
by the equation

\displaystyle S^{*}(n,m):=m!\left\{{n\atop m}\right\}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{j}{m\choose j}(m-j)^{n},\qquad m\leq n.

We have
\delta_{\nu}(\nu)\not=0
for all
\nu\geq 1
. Note that
\omega(i\infty)=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\beta(j)=0
in the case
\omega
is weak and continuous in
z=0
. Let

\displaystyle\mu:=\sup\left\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\begin{pmatrix}\beta^{\omega}(1)\\
\beta^{\omega}(2)\\
\vdots\\
\beta^{\omega}(N-1)\end{pmatrix}\in\ker(\mathrm{CotM}(N,m))\right\}.

This is equivalent to

\displaystyle\forall 0\leq r\leq\mu:\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\beta^{\omega}(j)\cot^{r}\left(\frac{\pi j}{N}\right)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\beta^{\omega}(j)\cot^{\mu+1}\left(\frac{\pi j}{N}\right)\not=0.

By (
13
) one easily sees that
\mathrm{ord}_{z=0}(\omega(z))\geq\mu
. On the other hand, when looking at the
\mu
-th coefficient in the Taylor expansion, we obtain

\displaystyle\omega^{(\mu)}(0)=\frac{\mu!i\pi^{\mu}}{2}\delta_{\mu+1}(\mu+1)\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\beta^{\omega}(j)\cot^{\mu+1}\left(\frac{\pi j}{N}\right)\not=0.

This shows
\mathrm{ord}_{z=0}(\omega(z))\leq\mu
and we coclude the proposition.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

We can use Proposition
3.2
to extract an algorithm to find weak functions of higher order. For fixed level, one only has to find elements in the kernel of a matrix which of course can be achieved after a finite number of steps. One may use the following explicit inverse, provided in
[
14
]
:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1&1\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&\cdots&x_{n-1}&x_{n}\\
x_{1}^{2}&x_{2}^{2}&\cdots&x_{n-1}^{2}&x_{n}^{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{1}^{n-1}&x_{2}^{n-1}&\cdots&x_{n-1}^{n-1}&x_{n}^{n-1}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=(b_{k,\ell})_{1\leq k,\ell\leq n}

where

\displaystyle b_{k,\ell}=\frac{(-1)^{n-\ell}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\
m\not=k\end{subarray}}^{n}(x_{k}-x_{m})}S_{n-\ell}(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{k-1},x_{k+1},...,x_{n})

with

\displaystyle S_{n}(y_{1},...,y_{m}):=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{n}\leq m}y_{j_{1}}y_{j_{2}}\cdots y_{j_{n}}\qquad\qquad(0\leq n\leq m).

Note that Proposition
3.2
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 3.4
.

We have
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(W^{0,\mathrm{ord}\geq\ell}_{N})=\max\{0,N-\ell-2\}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2
we conclude
W^{0,\mathrm{ord}\geq\ell}_{N}\cong\mathrm{ker}(\mathrm{CotM}(N,\ell))
as complex vector spaces. So it suffices to compute the dimension of the latter. This is simple linear algebra. The matrix
\mathrm{CotM}(N,\ell)
has full rank
r=\min\{N-1,\ell+1\}
, hence we obtain with the rank formula:

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathrm{ker}(\mathrm{CotM}(N,\ell)))=(N-1)-r=\begin{cases}0,&\qquad\text{if}\,N-1\leq\ell+1\\
N-\ell-2,&\qquad\text{if}\,N-1>\ell+1.\end{cases}

This proves the corollary.
∎

In the following, it is convenient to choose a basis
\alpha^{(N)}_{0},\alpha^{(N)}_{1},...,\alpha^{(N)}_{N-3}
of
W_{N}^{0}
with increasing order, i.e.,
\mathrm{ord}(\alpha^{(N)}_{j})=j
. We have the following.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
N
be a positive integer. Then, there exists a basis
\{\alpha_{j}^{(N)}\}_{j=0,...,N-3}
of the space
W_{N}^{0}
, such that the following is satisfied for all
0\leq j\leq N-3
:

(i)

We have
\alpha_{j}^{(N)}(z)=z^{j}+O(z^{N-2})
as
z\to 0
, and in particular
\mathrm{ord}(\alpha^{(N)}_{j})=j
.

(ii)

We have
\alpha^{(N)}_{j}(-z)=(-1)^{j}\alpha^{(N)}_{j}(z)
.

Proof.

Obviously,

\displaystyle W_{N}^{0}=W_{N}^{0,\mathrm{ord}\geq 0}\subset W_{N}^{0,\mathrm{ord}\geq 1}\subset W_{N}^{0,\mathrm{ord}\geq 2}\subset\cdots\subset W_{N}^{0,\mathrm{ord}\geq N-3},

and these spaces have dimensions
N-2,N-1,...,1
by Corollary
3.4
. So we can successively add basis vectors
\alpha_{j}^{(N)}(z)
that have descending order, i.e., we have a power series expansion

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}^{(N)}(z)=a_{j}z^{j}+a_{j+1}z^{j+1}+\cdots,\qquad a_{j}\not=0,

Note that we can assume that
\alpha_{j}^{(N)}(-z)=(-1)^{j}\alpha_{j}^{(N)}(z)
, as otherwise we can consider

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}_{j}^{(N)}(z):=\alpha_{j}^{(N)}(z)+(-1)^{j}\alpha_{j}^{(N)}(-z),

which has again order
j
but satisfies the desired relation (ii). Of course we can normalize each term to achieve first coefficient 1. We are only left to show that we can achieve
\alpha_{j}^{(N)}(z)=z^{j}+O(z^{N-2})
. There is nothing to show in the cases
j\in\{N-3,N-4\}
. For
j=N-5
, we have
\alpha_{N-5}^{(N)}(z)=z^{N-5}+b_{1}z^{N-3}+O(z^{N-2})
for some
b_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
, so consider
\alpha_{N-5}^{(N)}(z)-b_{1}\alpha_{N-3}^{(N)}(z)
instead. By inductively continuing this procedure, we obtain the assertion.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Note that the algorithm proposed in Remark
3.3
can be used to calculate the basis vectors
\alpha_{j}^{(N)}
.

We give an example.

Example 3.7
.

Let
N=5
. Put
\mu_{1}:=\cot(\frac{\pi}{5})=\sqrt{1+\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}}
and
\mu_{2}:=\cot(\frac{2\pi}{5})=\sqrt{1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}}
. We find, using

\displaystyle\ker\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&1\\
\mu_{1}&\mu_{2}&-\mu_{2}&-\mu_{1}\\
\mu_{1}^{2}&\mu_{2}^{2}&\mu_{2}^{2}&\mu_{1}^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\left<\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}}\\
\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}}\\
-1\end{pmatrix}\right>

that

\displaystyle\alpha^{(5)}_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{5i}{4\pi^{2}\sqrt{5+2\sqrt{5}}}\left(\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{1}{5})-e(z)}-\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}}\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{2}{5})-e(z)}+\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}}\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{3}{5})-e(z)}-\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{4}{5})-e(z)}\right)

\displaystyle=z^{2}+O\left(z^{4}\right).

Similarly, we find

\displaystyle\alpha^{(5)}_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{5}i}{4\pi}\left(\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{1}{5})-e(z)}-\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{2}{5})-e(z)}-\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{3}{5})-e(z)}+\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{4}{5})-e(z)}\right)

\displaystyle=z+O\left(z^{3}\right),

\displaystyle\alpha^{(5)}_{0}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2-\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}}}\left(\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{1}{5})-e(z)}-\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{2}{5})-e(z)}+\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{3}{5})-e(z)}-\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{4}{5})-e(z)}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{i\left(15+\sqrt{5}\right)}{4\sqrt{5+2\sqrt{5}}}\left(\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{1}{5})-e(z)}-\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}}\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{2}{5})-e(z)}+\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}}\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{3}{5})-e(z)}-\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{4}{5})-e(z)}\right)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{1}{5})-e(z)}+c_{2}\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{2}{5})-e(z)}-c_{2}\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{3}{5})-e(z)}-c_{1}\frac{e(z)}{e(\frac{4}{5})-e(z)}

\displaystyle=1+O\left(z^{4}\right),

with

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle:=-i\,\sqrt{\frac{25}{2}-\frac{11\sqrt{5}}{2}},\qquad c_{2}:=i\,\sqrt{\frac{25}{2}+\frac{11\sqrt{5}}{2}}.

The property of ascending order is very useful. With its help we can immediately give bases for the subspaces
W_{N}^{0,\mathrm{ord}\geq\ell}
just introduced:

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
0\leq\ell\leq N-3
. The set
\{\alpha_{j}^{(N)}\}_{j=\ell,...,N-3}
is a basis of
W_{N}^{0,\mathrm{ord}\geq\ell}
.

Proof.

Immediate with Corollary
3.4
and Proposition
3.5
.
∎

Now, we take a closer look at the spaces

(14)

\displaystyle V_{M,N}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}:=W_{M}^{0,\mathrm{ord}\geq\ell_{1}}\otimes W_{N}^{0,\mathrm{ord}\geq\ell_{2}}.

The following is an immediate consequence of Corollary
3.4
.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
0\leq\ell_{1}\leq M-2
and
0\leq\ell_{2}\leq N-2
. We then have the formula

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\left(V_{M,N}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}\right)=(M-\ell_{1}-2)(N-\ell_{2}-2).

We can use the elements
\alpha_{j}^{(M)}
and
\alpha_{j}^{(N)}
, to give a basis for
V_{M,N}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}
. Ideed, for
0\leq\ell_{1}\leq M-3
and
0\leq\ell_{2}\leq N-3
, note that

(15)

\displaystyle V_{M,N}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\geq\ell_{1}\\
d\geq\ell_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}\left(\alpha_{c}^{(M)}\otimes\alpha_{d}^{(N)}\right)

with Proposition
3.8
.

While we could easily assign orders to elements from
W_{M}
and
W_{N}
, it is not obvious how we should interpret elements in
V_{M,N}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}
in this respect. One starting point is to assign elementary tensors pairs
(\mathrm{ord}\geq\ell_{1},\mathrm{ord}\geq\ell_{2})
and this notion of order in the case of space
V_{M,N}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}
is so far to be understood rather abstractly. The next step is to define orders for arbitrary elements in
W^{0}_{M}\otimes W^{0}_{N}
. In the following, we reinterpret it into a more tangible term using complex analysis. Before we can do this, we need the following.

Definition 3.10
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be nonngative integers. Define
\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\nu,\mu)}
to be the vector space of meromorphic functions
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C})
, such that locally

\displaystyle f(z,\tau)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{j}(\tau)z^{j}

with polynomials
P_{j}(\tau)
(of degree at most
j
), such that
j-\mathrm{deg}(P_{j}(\tau))\geq\mu
and
j-\mathrm{deg}(\tau^{j}P_{j}(\frac{1}{\tau}))\geq\nu
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

For the previous definition one should note that we use the convention
\deg(0):=-\infty
. We also note the following.

Proposition 3.11
.

The space

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}:=\bigoplus_{(\nu,\mu)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}}\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\nu,\mu)}

is a graded algebra via the component multiplication induced by

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\nu_{1},\mu_{1})}\times\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\nu_{2},\mu_{2})}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2},\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})},

\displaystyle(f,g)

\displaystyle\mapsto fg.

For
f_{1}\in\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\nu_{1},\mu_{1})}
and
f_{2}\in\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\nu_{2},\mu_{2})}
we have

\displaystyle f_{1}+f_{2}\in\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\},\min\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\})}.

Proof.

We only have to show that the multiplication is well defined. For
f(z,\tau):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{j}(\tau)z^{j}
and
g(z,\tau):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}Q_{j}(\tau)z^{j}
we find with standard Cauchy convolution

\displaystyle(fg)(z,\tau)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{j}P_{n}(\tau)Q_{j-n}(\tau)\right)z^{j}.

Obviously, the product series converges again locally around
0
for all values of
\tau
. Now we have

\displaystyle j-\deg(P_{n}(\tau)Q_{j-n}(\tau))=n+(j-n)-\deg(P_{n}(\tau))-\deg(Q_{j-n}(\tau))\geq\mu_{1}+\mu_{2},

and the proof of
j-\deg(\tau^{j}P_{n}(\frac{1}{\tau})Q_{j-n}(\frac{1}{\tau}))\geq\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}
works the same. Since

\deg\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}P_{j}(\tau)Q_{n-j}(\tau)\right)\leq\max_{0\leq j\leq n}\{\deg(P_{j}(\tau)Q_{n-j}(\tau))\},

the proof of the first assertion is complete. For the second assertion, consider

\displaystyle f_{1}(z,\tau)+f_{2}(z,\tau)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(P_{1,j}(\tau)+P_{2,j}(\tau))z^{j}.

Note that
j-\deg(P_{1,j}(\tau)+P_{2,j}(\tau))\geq j-\max\{\deg(P_{1,j}(\tau)),\deg(P_{2,j}(\tau))\}=\min\{j-\deg(P_{1,j}(\tau)),j-\deg(P_{2,j}(\tau))\}=\min\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\}
. Similarly, we find
j-\deg(\tau^{j}(P_{1,j}(\frac{1}{\tau})+P_{2,j}(\frac{1}{\tau})))\geq j-\max\{\deg(\tau^{j}P_{1,j}(\frac{1}{\tau})),\deg(\tau^{j}P_{2,j}(\frac{1}{\tau}))\}=\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}
.
∎

We note that there are embeddings
\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\nu_{1},\mu_{1})}\to\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\nu,\mu)}
if
\mu\leq\mu_{1}
and
\nu_{1}\leq\nu
.

Although the definition of the vector space
\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)
and its subspaces
\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\mu,\nu)}
is useful, it is not yet entirely sufficient to introduce precisely the notion of order in two variables. However, the foundation for the motivation has already been laid:

Definition 3.12
.

We say that a, for all
\tau
locally convergent, power series

\displaystyle f(z,\tau):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{j}(\tau)z^{j},\qquad(P_{j}\in\mathbb{C}[X],\deg(P_{j})\leq j)

has order

\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}(f):=\left(\min_{j\geq 0}j-\mathrm{deg}\left(\tau^{j}P_{j}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\right)\right),\min_{j\geq 0}j-\mathrm{deg}(P_{j}(\tau))\right).

We also put
\mathrm{ord}(0):=(\infty,\infty)
.

It is easy to prove the following important observation.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
f
and
g
be globally meromorphic functions analytic in a neighborhood of 0 with
\mathrm{ord}_{z=0}(f(z))=\mu
and
\mathrm{ord}_{z=0}(g(z))=\nu
. Put
h(z,\tau):=f(z)g(z\tau)
. Then we have
\mathrm{ord}(h)=(\nu,\mu)
. In particular,
h\in\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\nu,\mu)}
.

Proof.

Using the Taylor expansions
f(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
and
g(w):=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}b_{m}w^{m}
we find

\displaystyle f(z)g(z\tau)=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}\right)\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}b_{m}\tau^{m}z^{m}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{j}a_{j-n}b_{n}\tau^{n}\right)z^{j}.

Since by assumption
a_{0}=a_{1}=\cdots=a_{\mu-1}=0
and
b_{0}=b_{1}=\cdots=b_{\nu-1}=0
, but
a_{\mu}b_{\nu}\not=0
we find for all
j\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\deg\left(\sum_{n=0}^{j}a_{j-n}b_{n}\tau^{n}\right)=\deg\left(\sum_{n=0}^{j-\mu}a_{j-n}b_{n}\tau^{n}\right)\leq j-\mu

and

\displaystyle\deg\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j-n}b_{n}\tau^{j-n}\right)=\deg\left(\sum_{j=0}^{j-\nu}a_{j-n}b_{n}\tau^{j-n}\right)\leq j-\nu.

Now choose
j:=\nu+\mu\geq 0
. In this particular case, we find

\displaystyle\deg\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\nu+\mu}a_{\nu+\mu-n}b_{n}\tau^{n}\right)=\deg\left(a_{\mu}b_{\nu}\tau^{\nu}+\sum_{n=0}^{\nu-1}a_{\nu+\mu-n}b_{n}\tau^{n}\right)=\nu=\nu+\mu-\mu.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\deg\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\nu+\mu}a_{\nu+\mu-n}b_{n}\tau^{\nu+\mu-n}\right)=\deg\left(a_{\mu}b_{\nu}\tau^{\mu}+\sum_{n=0}^{\mu-1}a_{\nu+\mu-n}b_{n}\tau^{\nu+\mu-n}\right)=\mu=\mu+\nu-\nu.

This proves
\mathrm{ord}(h)=(\nu,\mu)
, and clearly
h\in\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\nu,\mu)}
.
∎

The following approach is motivated by Proposition
3.13
. We are able to construct meromorphic functions of two variables
z
and
\tau
from the data
\omega\otimes\eta
as follows: On elementary tensors define a linear map
\Xi_{M,N}
by

(16)

\displaystyle\omega\otimes\eta\longmapsto\eta(z)\omega(z\tau),

and of course this extends to a linear map
W^{0}_{M}\otimes W_{N}^{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)
by Proposition
3.13
. Our next goal is to prove that this map is injective, and hence, that we do not lose information when going from the spaces
W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0}
to meromorphic functions. To do so, it is useful to introduce a notion for the subspace of
\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)
genrated by weak pairs
\omega\otimes\eta
. We put

\displaystyle J^{0}_{M,N}:=\left<\eta(z)\omega(z\tau)\ \Big|\ \omega\otimes\eta\in W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0}\right>.

Also put

\displaystyle J_{M,N,k}^{0}:=\left<\eta(z)\omega(z\tau)\ \Big|\ \omega\otimes\eta\in\left(W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0}\right)_{k}\right>

and denote the corresponding restricted map
(W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0})_{k}\to J_{M,N,k}^{0}
by
\Xi_{M,N,k}
.
We can identify both spaces.

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
(a_{c,d})_{0\leq c\leq M-3,0\leq d\leq N-3}
be arbitrary complex numbers and

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}:=\{(c,d)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}\colon 0\leq c\leq M-3,c\leq d\leq c+N-3\}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq c\leq M-3\\
0\leq d\leq N-3\end{subarray}}a_{c,d}\alpha^{(N)}_{d}(z)\alpha_{c}^{(M)}(z\tau)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq c\leq M-3\\
c\leq d\leq c+N-3\end{subarray}}a_{c,d-c}\tau^{c}z^{d}+\sum_{(n,m)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{M}}c_{m,n}\tau^{m}z^{n}

for some complex
c_{m,n}
. In particular, the linear maps
\Xi_{M,N}\colon W^{0}_{M}\otimes W_{N}^{0}\rightarrow J^{0}_{M,N}
and
\Xi_{M,N,k}\colon(W^{0}_{M}\otimes W_{N}^{0})_{k}\rightarrow J^{0}_{M,N,k}
described by (
16
) are both isomorphisms.

Proof.

The first part is a simple application of the identity theorem for power series. By construction we have for
0\leq c\leq M-3
and
0\leq n\leq N-3

\displaystyle\alpha^{(N)}_{d}(z)\alpha_{c}^{(M)}(z\tau)

\displaystyle=\left(\tau^{c}z^{c}+\sum_{j\geq M-2}a_{j}\tau^{j}z^{j}\right)\left(z^{d}+\sum_{j\geq N-2}b_{j}z^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\tau^{c}z^{c+d}+\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{M}}r_{m,n}\tau^{m}z^{n}

for some complex numbers
r_{m,n}
. Since we have the bijection
\{0,\ldots,M-3\}\times\{0,\ldots,N-3\}\to\mathcal{M}
with
(c,d)\mapsto(c,c+d)
the claim now follows with the Identity theorem for power series. Again by the identity theorem the claimed isomorphisms hold, as the monomials
\tau^{c}z^{d}
are linearly independent.
∎

Remark 3.15
.

This argument, built on the elementary theory of rational functions, can be truncated by means of Eisenstein series. This concerns in particular the case of prime
M
and
N
, since the isomorphism from Proposition
2.15
factorizes over
J_{M,N}^{0}
.

Corollary 3.16
.

Let
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
be odd primes and
k\geq 3
. Then we have an isomorphism
J_{p_{1},p_{2},k}^{0}\to\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}
given by
\eta(z)\omega(z\tau)\mapsto\vartheta_{k}(\omega\otimes\eta;p_{2}\tau)
.

Proof.

Immediate with Propositions
2.15
and
3.14
.
∎

The following is a simple, but useful observation.

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subset\{0,\ldots,M-3\}\times\{0,\ldots,N-3\}
be non-empty and
(a_{c,d})_{(c,d)\in\mathcal{S}}
be a family of non-zero complex numbers. Then we have the formula

\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}\left(\sum_{(c,d)\in\mathcal{S}}a_{c,d}\alpha_{d}^{(N)}(z)\alpha_{c}^{(M)}(z\tau)\right)=\left(\min_{(c,d)\in\mathcal{S}}c,\min_{(c,d)\in\mathcal{S}}d\right).

Proof.

Note that
\mathrm{ord}(\alpha_{n}^{(N)}(z)\alpha_{m}^{(M)}(z\tau))=(m,n)
by Proposition
3.13
. Applying Proposition
3.11
multiple times, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}\left(\sum_{(c,d)\in\mathcal{S}}a_{c,d}\alpha_{d}^{(N)}(z)\alpha_{c}^{(M)}(z\tau)\right)\geq\left(\min_{(c,d)\in\mathcal{S}}c,\min_{(c,d)\in\mathcal{S}}d\right)

componentwise. On the other hand, we find with Proposition
3.14

\displaystyle\sum_{(c,d)\in\mathcal{S}}a_{c,d}\alpha_{d}^{(N)}(z)\alpha_{c}^{(M)}(z\tau)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq c\leq M-3\\
c\leq d\leq c+N-3\\
(c,d-c)\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}a_{c,d-c}\tau^{c}z^{d}+\sum_{(n,m)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{M}}c_{m,n}\tau^{m}z^{n}.

In particular, there are non-trivial monomials of the form
a_{\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c,d_{1}-\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c}\tau^{\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c}z^{d_{1}}
and
a_{c,\min_{(\ell,d)\in\mathcal{S}}d}\tau^{c_{1}}z^{(\min_{(\ell,d)\in\mathcal{S}}d)+c_{1}}
, where
\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c\leq d_{1}\leq\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c+N-3
and
0\leq c_{1}\leq M-3
are fixed integers. Writing
\sum_{(c,d)\in\mathcal{S}}a_{c,d}\alpha_{d}^{(N)}(z)\alpha_{c}^{(M)}(z\tau)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{j}(\tau)z^{j}
, we note that

\displaystyle P_{d_{1}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=a_{\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c,d_{1}-\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c}\tau^{\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c}+\sum_{j=\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c+1}^{d_{1}}a_{j}\tau^{j},

\displaystyle P_{(\min_{(\ell,d)\in\mathcal{S}}d)+c_{1}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=a_{c,\min_{(\ell,d)\in\mathcal{S}}d}\tau^{c_{1}}z^{(\min_{(\ell,d)\in\mathcal{S}}d)+c_{1}}+\sum_{j=0}^{c_{1}-1}b_{j}\tau^{j}

for some complex
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
, as otherwise the minimality is violated. We conclude, again componentwise,

\displaystyle\left(\min_{j\geq 0}j-\mathrm{deg}\left(\tau^{j}P_{j}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\right)\right),\min_{j\geq 0}j-\mathrm{deg}(P_{j}(\tau))\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(d_{1}-\deg\left(\tau^{d_{1}}\left(a_{\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c,d_{1}-\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c}\tau^{-\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c}+\sum_{j=\min_{(c,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}}c+1}^{d_{1}}a_{j}\tau^{-j}\right)\right),\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left(\min_{(\ell,d)\in\mathcal{S}}d\right)+c_{1}-\deg\left(a_{c,\min_{(\ell,d)\in\mathcal{S}}d}\tau^{c_{1}}z^{(\min_{(\ell,d)\in\mathcal{S}}d)+c_{1}}+\sum_{j=0}^{c_{1}-1}b_{j}\tau^{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\min_{(c,d)\in\mathcal{S}}c,\min_{(c,d)\in\mathcal{S}}d\right).

This proves the claim.
∎

We can now define an order on the space
W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0}
by analogy with the previous results.

Definition 3.18
.

Let

\displaystyle 0\not=f:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq m\leq M-3\\
0\leq n\leq N-3\end{subarray}}a_{m,n}\left(\alpha_{m}^{(M)}\otimes\alpha_{n}^{(N)}\right).

Then we define

\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}(f):=\left(\min\{0\leq m\leq M-3\ \colon\ \exists\ 0\leq n\leq N-3\text{ with }a_{m,n}\not=0\},\right.

\displaystyle\left.\min\{0\leq n\leq N-3\ \colon\ \exists\ 0\leq m\leq M-3\text{ with }a_{m,n}\not=0\}\right).

We also put
\mathrm{ord}(0):=(\infty,\infty)
.

Note that if the order is finite in one component, it is also finite in the other one.

Proposition 3.19
.

For all
F\in W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0}
, we have
\mathrm{ord}(F)=\mathrm{ord}(\Xi_{M,N}(F))
, where
\Xi_{M,N}
is the isomorphism in Proposition
3.14
.

Proof.

This is immediate with Proposition
3.17
and Definition
3.18
.
∎

The following theorem summarizes the above discussion in a useful fact that establishes a simple connection between the abstract order on
W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0}
and the zero order known from complex analysis.

Theorem 3.20
.

Let
0\leq\ell_{1}\leq M-3
and
0\leq\ell_{2}\leq N-3
. There is an order and sign preserving isomorphism given by

(17)

\displaystyle\Xi_{M,N}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}\colon V_{M,N}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}

\displaystyle\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}J_{M,N}^{0}\cap\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}

\displaystyle\omega\otimes\eta

\displaystyle\longmapsto((z,\tau)\mapsto\eta(z)\omega(z\tau))

on elementary tensors between vector spaces induced by (
16
), where
V_{M,N}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}
is defined in (
14
).

Proof.

It is clear that the map is sign preserving. By construction and Definition
3.10
the space
J_{M,N}^{0}\cap\mathbb{C}[[z]](\tau)^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}
contains all functions
(z,\tau)\mapsto f(z,\tau)\in J_{M,N}^{0}
staisfying
\mathrm{ord}(f)\geq(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
. Now the claim is immediate with Propositions and
3.17
and
3.19
.
∎

For our investigations, we still lack a means to infer vanishing coefficients of polynomials in the power series expansion in
\alpha_{n}^{(N)}(z)\alpha_{m}^{(M)}(z\tau)
from properties of the basis vectors
\alpha_{m}^{(M)}\otimes\alpha_{n}^{(N)}
for
W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0}
. Let
T\geq 0
be an integer. For the polynomial spaces

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\tau]^{T\geq\deg}:=\{P\in\mathbb{C}[\tau]\colon\deg(P)\leq T\}

and subsets
\mathcal{S}\subset\{0,...,T\}
we consider linear maps

\displaystyle\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},T}\colon\mathbb{C}[z]^{T\geq\deg}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{S}|}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{T}a_{j}\tau^{j}

\displaystyle\longmapsto(a_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{S}}.

To extract the polynomials
P_{j}(\tau)
in the power series expansions of
\eta(z)\omega(z\tau)
, we can use the residue map

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}_{T}\colon J^{0}_{M,N}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}[\tau]^{T\geq\deg},

\displaystyle\eta(z)\omega(z\tau)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathrm{res}_{z=0}\left(z^{-(T+1)}\eta(z)\omega(z\tau)\right).

We can prove the following.

Theorem 3.21
.

Let
M
,
N
and
T
be integers. Let
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
be integers such that
0\leq\ell_{1}\leq M-2
,
0\leq\ell_{2}\leq N-2
, and
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\leq T+1
. Choose

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\subset\{0,1,...,\ell_{1}-1\}\cup\{T+1-\ell_{2},T+2-\ell_{2},\ldots,T\}\subset\{0,\ldots T\}.

Then we have

(18)

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(m\notin\mathcal{S}\wedge T-n\notin\mathcal{S}\right)\\
\vee\left((m,T-n)\in\mathcal{S}^{2}\wedge m\not=T-n\right)\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}\left(\alpha_{m}^{(M)}\otimes\alpha_{n}^{(N)}\right)\subset\ker\left(\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},T}\circ\mathrm{Res}_{T}\circ\Xi_{M,N}\right).

If additionally
T\leq\min\{M-3,N-3\}
, then, for any
\mathcal{S}\subset\{0,1,\ldots,T\}
, we have the exact sequence

(19)

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{m\notin\mathcal{S}\vee m\not=T-n}\mathbb{C}\left(\alpha_{m}^{(M)}\otimes\alpha_{n}^{(N)}\right)\overset{\Xi_{M,N}}{\longrightarrow}J_{M,N}^{0}\overset{\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},T}\circ\mathrm{Res}_{T}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\longrightarrow 0.

Proof.

During the proof we write

\displaystyle\alpha_{m}^{(M)}(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a_{m,\ell}^{(M)}z^{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{M-3}\delta_{m,\ell}z^{\ell}+O\left(z^{M-2}\right)

where the last equality follows from Proposition
3.5
. We first show (
18
). Recall that

\displaystyle\Xi_{M,N}\left(\alpha_{m}^{(M)}\otimes\alpha_{n}^{(N)}\right)=\alpha_{n}^{(N)}(z)\alpha_{m}^{(M)}(z\tau)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{T}a_{m,\ell}^{(M)}a_{n,T-\ell}^{(N)}\tau^{\ell}.

Applying
\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},T}\circ\mathrm{Res}_{T}
this equals

\displaystyle\left(a_{m,\ell}^{(M)}a_{n,T-\ell}^{(N)}\right)_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}.

If we assume
m\notin\mathcal{S}
, we find for all
0\leq\ell\leq\ell_{1}-1
and
\ell\in\mathcal{S}
that
a_{m,\ell}^{(M)}a_{n,T-\ell}^{(N)}=\delta_{m,\ell}a_{n,T-\ell}^{(N)}=0
(note that
\ell_{1}-1\leq M-3
by assumption). Likewise, assuming
T-n\notin\mathcal{S}
, we obtain for all
\ell\in\mathcal{S}
with
T+1-\ell_{2}\leq\ell\leq T
the equality
a_{m,\ell}^{(M)}a_{n,T-\ell}^{(N)}=a_{m,\ell}^{(M)}\delta_{n,T-\ell}=0
, since
n=T-\ell
implies
\ell=T-n\in\mathcal{S}
, which contradicts our assumption.
Now assume
(m,T-n)\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
and
m\not=T-n
. In this case we find
a_{m,\ell}^{(M)}a_{n,T-\ell}^{(N)}=\delta_{m,\ell}\delta_{n,T-\ell}=0
. This proves (
18
).

Next we show that for all
\mathcal{S}\subset\{0,1,\ldots,T\}
the sequence (
19
) is exact if we additionally assume
T\leq\min\{M-3,N-3\}
. Note that the resctriction of
\Xi_{M,N}
is injective by Proposition
3.14
. Under the restriction
T\leq\min\{M-3,N-3\}
we also obtain

\displaystyle\left(a_{m,\ell}^{(M)}a_{n,T-\ell}^{(N)}\right)_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}=\left(\delta_{m,\ell}\delta_{n,T-\ell}\right)_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}.

For arbitrary
\ell\in\mathcal{S}
, note that this is not the zero vector if and only if there is some
\ell\in\mathcal{S}
such that
\ell=m
and
T-\ell=n
, i.e.,
m=T-n
and
m\in\mathcal{S}
. Consequently, it is zero if and only if
m\notin\mathcal{S}
or
T-m\not=n
. This proves by linear independency of the non-zero components

\displaystyle\Xi_{M,N}\left(\bigoplus_{m\notin\mathcal{S}\vee m\not=T-n}\mathbb{C}\left(\alpha_{m}^{(M)}\otimes\alpha_{n}^{(N)}\right)\right)=\ker\left(\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},T}\circ\mathrm{Res}_{T}\right).

Finally, for any
(\lambda_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
we can find the pre-image
\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{\ell}\alpha_{T-\ell}^{(N)}(z)\alpha^{(M)}_{\ell}(z\tau)\in J_{M,N}^{0}
which proves that
\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},T}\circ\mathrm{Res}_{T}\colon J_{M,N}^{0}\to\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
is onto. The theorem is proved.
∎

In the context of modular forms we need the above theorem with respect to a fixed weight
k
. As we have introduced the spaces
(W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0})_{k}
in order to eleminate trivial canceling when going from rational functions to modular forms, we should enure that Theorem
3.21
still works “when restricting to
(W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0})_{k}
”.

Corollary 3.22
.

Let
M
,
N
and
T
be integers. Let
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
be integers such that
0\leq\ell_{1}\leq M-2
,
0\leq\ell_{2}\leq N-2
, and
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\leq T+1
. Choose

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\subset\{0,1,...,\ell_{1}-1\}\cup\{T+1-\ell_{2},T+2-\ell_{2},\ldots,T\}\subset\{0,\ldots T\}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(m\notin\mathcal{S}\wedge T-n\notin\mathcal{S}\right)\\
\vee\left((m,T-n)\in\mathcal{S}^{2}\wedge m\not=T-n\right)\\
m+n\equiv T\pmod{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}\left(\alpha_{m}^{(M)}\otimes\alpha_{n}^{(N)}\right)\subset\ker\left(\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},T}\circ\mathrm{Res}_{T}\circ\Xi_{M,N,T}\right).

If additionally
T\leq\min\{M-3,N-3\}
, then, for any
\mathcal{S}\subset\{0,1,\ldots,T\}
, we have the exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\notin\mathcal{S}\vee m\not=T-n\\
m+n\equiv T\pmod{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}\left(\alpha_{m}^{(M)}\otimes\alpha_{n}^{(N)}\right)\overset{\Xi_{M,N,T}}{\longrightarrow}J_{M,N,T}^{0}\overset{\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},T}\circ\mathrm{Res}_{T}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\longrightarrow 0.

Proof.

As we have
\alpha_{m}^{(M)}\otimes\alpha_{n}^{(N)}\in(W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0})_{T}
if and only if
m+n\equiv T\pmod{2}
, the first claim follows directly with Theorem
3.21
. A similar observation holds for the exact sequence, as the pre-images of
\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},T}\circ\mathrm{Res}_{T}
are part of the subspace
J_{M,N,T}^{0}
.
∎

4.
Applications to
L
-series of Eisenstein series

Fix a weight
k\geq 3
. Depending on that
k
is odd or even, we obtain different kernels of the map

\displaystyle\omega\otimes\eta\longmapsto\vartheta_{k}(\omega\otimes\eta;\tau):=-2\pi i\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}}\mathrm{res}_{z=x}\left(z^{k-1}\eta(z)\omega(z\tau)\right).

So we only focus on elements with the right sign. A critical tool for the main proofs is Eichler duality, so Fourier transforms will play a significant role. For the sake of clarity and the convenience of the reader, we work these out very explicitly. First, we put
M=p_{1}
and
N=p_{2}
with odd prime numbers
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, and write the elements
\alpha_{c}^{(p_{1})}
and
\alpha_{d}^{(p_{2})}
(with
0\leq c\leq p_{1}-2
,
0\leq d\leq p_{2}-2
) as linear combinations in the
\omega_{\chi}
:

(20)

\displaystyle\alpha_{c}^{(p_{1})}=\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\mathrm{prim}}(p_{1})}a_{\chi}(c)\omega_{\chi},\qquad\alpha_{d}^{(p_{2})}=\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\mathrm{prim}}(p_{2})}a_{\psi}(d)\omega_{\psi}.

Note that this is possible as for primes all non-principal characters are primitive. Corresponding to this, we now consider the change of basis
\alpha_{c}^{(p_{1})}\otimes\alpha_{d}^{(p_{2})}\mapsto\widetilde{\alpha}_{c}^{(p_{1})}\otimes\widehat{\alpha}_{d}^{(p_{2})}
defined by

(21)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\alpha}_{c}^{(p_{1})}:=\sum_{\chi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\mathrm{prim}}(p_{1})}\chi(-1)\mathcal{G}(\chi)a_{\chi}(c)\omega_{\overline{\chi}},\qquad\widehat{\alpha}_{d}^{(p_{2})}:=\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\mathrm{prim}}(p_{2})}\mathcal{G}(\psi)a_{\psi}(d)\omega_{\overline{\psi}},

where
\mathcal{G}(\chi)
,
\mathcal{G}(\psi)
are the Gauss sums of
\chi
,
\psi
and the numbers
a_{\chi}(c)
and
a_{\psi}(d)
are defined in (
20
). We can show that these elements again give a basis.

Lemma 4.1
.

The elements in (
21
) give a basis of
W_{p_{1}}^{0}
and
W_{p_{2}}^{0}
, respectively.

Proof.

We only give a proof for
p_{1}
as the case
p_{2}
is similar. Write
\{\chi_{1},...,\chi_{p_{1}-2}\}
for the non-principal characters modulo
p_{1}
. Put

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}a_{\chi_{1}}(0)&a_{\chi_{2}}(0)&\cdots&a_{\chi_{p_{1}-2}}(0)\\
a_{\chi_{1}}(1)&a_{\chi_{2}}(1)&\cdots&a_{\chi_{p_{1}-2}}(1)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{\chi_{1}}(p_{1}-3)&a_{\chi_{2}}(p_{1}-3)&\cdots&a_{\chi_{p_{1}-2}}(p_{1}-3)\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{1}(-1)\mathcal{G}(\chi_{1})&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\chi_{2}(-1)\mathcal{G}(\chi_{2})&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\chi_{p_{1}-2}(-1)\mathcal{G}(\chi_{p_{1}-2}).\end{pmatrix}.

Note that (
20
) implies

\displaystyle A\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{\chi_{1}}\\
\omega_{\chi_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{\chi_{p_{1}-2}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{0}^{(p_{1})}\\
\alpha_{1}^{(p_{1})}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha_{p_{1}-3}^{(p_{1})}\end{pmatrix}.

Since this is a change of basis,
A
is regular. The same holds for
B
, as all involved characters are primitive. Let
S
be the swapping matrix sending
(\omega_{\chi})
to
(\omega_{\overline{\chi}})
. With (
21
) we conclude that

\displaystyle ABS\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{\chi_{1}}\\
\omega_{\chi_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{\chi_{p_{1}-2}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}^{(p_{1})}\\
\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}^{(p_{1})}\\
\vdots\\
\widetilde{\alpha}_{p_{1}-3}^{(p_{1})}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
ABS
is regular, this is again a change of basis, and the claim follows.
∎

Example 4.2
.

Let
\chi_{5}

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}^{(5)}=-\frac{\sqrt[4]{3-4i}5^{\frac{3}{4}}}{4\sqrt{2}\pi^{2}}\omega_{\chi_{5}}+\frac{\sqrt[4]{3+4i}5^{\frac{3}{4}}}{4\sqrt{2}\pi^{2}}\omega_{\overline{\chi_{5}}},

where all roots are taken in the principal branch. We can use this to compute

and

explicitely. We have
\mathcal{G}(\chi_{5})=i\sqrt[4]{-15+20i}
and
\mathcal{G}(\overline{\chi_{5}})=i\sqrt[4]{-15-20i}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\alpha}_{2}^{(5)}=\frac{5i\sqrt[4]{7+24i}}{4\sqrt{2}\pi^{2}}\omega_{\overline{\chi_{5}}}-\frac{5i\sqrt[4]{7-24i}}{4\sqrt{2}\pi^{2}}\omega_{\chi_{5}},\quad\widehat{\alpha}_{2}^{(5)}=-\frac{5i\sqrt[4]{7+24i}}{4\sqrt{2}\pi^{2}}\omega_{\overline{\chi_{5}}}+\frac{5i\sqrt[4]{7-24i}}{4\sqrt{2}\pi^{2}}\omega_{\chi_{5}}.

In the natural mapping from rational functions to modular forms we can interpose just this change of basis. We define

(22)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}\colon J_{p_{1},p_{2},k}^{0}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}

\displaystyle\alpha_{d}^{(p_{2})}(z)\alpha_{c}^{(p_{1})}(z\tau)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\vartheta_{k}\left(\widetilde{\alpha}_{c}^{(p_{1})}\otimes\widehat{\alpha}_{d}^{(p_{2})};p_{2}\tau\right).

Note that this map is an isomorphism by Corollary
3.16
and Lemma
4.1
.

We have the following key theorem, that describes spaces of Eisenstein series with
L
-series vanishing at specific critical values precisely for small weights.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
p_{1},p_{2}
be odd primes,
3\leq k\leq\min\{p_{1}-2,p_{2}-2\}
an integer, and
0\leq\ell_{1}\leq p_{1}-2
,
0\leq\ell_{2}\leq p_{2}-2
, with
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\leq k-1
. Let
\mathcal{S}\subset\{0,\ldots,k-2\}
be an arbitrary subset. Consider the homomorphism
\xi_{p_{1},p_{2},k}:(W_{p_{1}}^{0}\otimes W_{p_{2}}^{0})_{k}\to\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}
defined by

\displaystyle\alpha_{c}^{(p_{1})}\otimes\alpha_{d}^{(p_{2})}\longmapsto\vartheta_{k}\left(\widetilde{\alpha}_{c}^{(p_{1})}\otimes\widehat{\alpha}_{d}^{(p_{2})};p_{2}\tau\right).

Then we have an exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\bigoplus\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\notin\mathcal{S}\vee m\not=k-2-n\\
m+n\equiv k\pmod{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}\left(\alpha_{m}^{(p_{1})}\otimes\alpha_{n}^{(p_{2})}\right)\overset{\xi_{p_{1},p_{2},k}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}\overset{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},k}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\longrightarrow 0,

where the linear map
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},k}\colon\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}\to\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},k}(f):=\frac{(-2\pi i)^{k}}{(k-2)!}\frac{p_{2}^{k-1}}{4\pi^{2}}\left(\binom{k-2}{\ell}i^{1-\ell}(2\pi)^{-\ell-1}\Gamma(\ell+1)L\left(f;\ell+1\right)p_{1}^{\ell}\right)_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.22
, putting
M:=p_{1}
,
N:=p_{2}
and
T:=k-2
, it suffices to show that the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bigoplus\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\notin\mathcal{S}\vee m\not=k-2-n\\
m+n\equiv k\pmod{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}\left(\alpha_{m}^{(p_{1})}\otimes\alpha_{n}^{(N)}\right)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J_{p_{1},p_{2},k}^{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{S}|}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Xi_{p_{1},p_{2},k-2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\xi_{p_{1},p_{2},k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},k-2}\circ\mathrm{Res}_{k-2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},k}}

commutates, as the above sequence is exact and
\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}
is an isomorphism. By definition it is clear that
\xi_{p_{1},p_{2},k}=\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}\circ\Xi_{p_{1},p_{2},k}
. So we are left to show that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},k}=\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},k-2}\circ\mathrm{Res}_{k-2}\circ\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}^{-1}
. To show this w use Eichler duality, and it suffices to do it for basis vectors. We find with (
21
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},k}\left(\vartheta_{k}\left(\widetilde{\alpha}_{c}^{(p_{1})}\otimes\widehat{\alpha}_{d}^{(p_{2})};p_{2}\tau\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{(-2\pi i)^{k}}{(k-2)!}\frac{p_{2}^{k-1}}{4\pi^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},k-2}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-2}{\ell}i^{1-\ell}(2\pi)^{-\ell-1}\Gamma(\ell+1)L\left(\vartheta_{k}\left(\widetilde{\alpha}_{c}^{(p_{1})}\otimes\widehat{\alpha}_{d}^{(p_{2})};p_{2}\tau\right);\ell+1\right)(p_{1}\tau)^{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{(-2\pi i)^{k}}{(k-2)!}\frac{p_{2}^{k-1}}{4\pi^{2}}\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},k-2}\left(\sum_{\chi,\psi}\chi(-1)a_{\chi}(c)a_{\psi}(d)\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-2}{\ell}i^{1-\ell}(2\pi)^{-\ell-1}\Gamma(\ell+1)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\left.\times\mathcal{G}(\chi)\mathcal{G}(\psi)L\left(\vartheta_{k}\left(\omega_{\overline{\chi}}\otimes\omega_{\overline{\psi}};p_{2}\tau\right);\ell+1\right)(p_{1}\tau)^{\ell}\right)

and with
M=p_{1}
and
N=p_{2}
in (
11
)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{(-2\pi i)^{k}}{(k-2)!}\frac{p_{2}^{k-1}}{4\pi^{2}}\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},k-2}\left(\sum_{\chi,\psi}\chi(-1)a_{\chi}(c)a_{\psi}(d)\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-2}{\ell}i^{1-\ell}(2\pi)^{-\ell-1}\Gamma(\ell+1)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\times\left.\mathcal{G}(\chi)\mathcal{G}(\psi)\frac{p_{2}(k-1)!\mathcal{G}(\overline{\chi})}{\chi(-1)(-2\pi i)^{k}\mathcal{G}(\psi)}L\left(E_{k}(\chi,\psi;p_{2}\tau);\ell+1\right)(p_{1}\tau)^{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{(-2\pi i)^{k}}{(k-2)!}\frac{p_{2}^{k-1}}{4\pi^{2}}\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},k-2}\left(\sum_{\chi,\psi}\chi(-1)a_{\chi}(c)a_{\psi}(d)\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-2}{\ell}i^{1-\ell}(2\pi)^{-\ell-1}\Gamma(\ell+1)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\left.\times\frac{p_{1}p_{2}(k-1)!}{(-2\pi i)^{k}}L\left(E_{k}(\chi,\psi;p_{2}\tau);\ell+1\right)(p_{1}\tau)^{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{(-2\pi i)^{k}}{(k-2)!}\frac{p_{2}^{k-1}}{4\pi^{2}}\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},k-2}\left(\sum_{\chi,\psi}\chi(-1)a_{\chi}(c)a_{\psi}(d)\frac{p_{2}(k-1)!}{(-2\pi i)^{k}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-2}{\ell}i^{1-\ell}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\left.\times\Lambda(E_{k}(\chi,\psi;p_{2}\tau);\ell+1)p_{1}^{\ell+1}\tau^{\ell}\right).

With
N_{\chi}=p_{1}
and
N_{\psi}=p_{2}
in (

) this equals to

\displaystyle\frac{(-2\pi i)^{k}}{(k-2)!}\frac{p_{2}^{k-1}}{4\pi^{2}}\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},k-2}\left(\sum_{\chi,\psi}\chi(-1)a_{\chi}(c)a_{\psi}(d)\frac{p_{2}(k-1)!}{(-2\pi i)^{k}}\frac{4\pi^{2}\chi(-1)}{p_{2}^{k}(k-1)}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\left.\times\mathrm{res}_{z=0}\left(z^{1-k}\omega_{\psi}(z)\omega_{\chi}\left(z\tau\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{(-2\pi i)^{k}}{(k-2)!}\frac{p_{2}^{k-1}}{4\pi^{2}}\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},k-2}\left(\frac{(k-2)!}{(-2\pi i)^{k}}\frac{4\pi^{2}}{p_{2}^{k-1}}\sum_{\chi,\psi}a_{\chi}(c)a_{\psi}(d)\mathrm{res}_{z=0}\left(z^{1-k}\omega_{\psi}(z)\omega_{\chi}\left(z\tau\right)\right)\right)

and with (

)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},k-2}\left(\mathrm{res}_{z=0}\left(z^{1-k}\alpha^{(p_{2})}_{d}(z)\alpha_{c}^{(p_{1})}\left(z\tau\right)\right)\right)=\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},k-2}\left(\mathrm{Res}_{k-2}\left(\alpha_{d}^{(p_{2})}(z)\alpha_{c}^{(p_{1})}(z\tau)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\mathrm{Coeff}_{\mathcal{S},k-2}\left(\mathrm{Res}_{k-2}\left(\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}^{-1}\left(\alpha_{c}^{(p_{1})}\otimes\alpha_{d}^{(p_{2})}\right)\right)\right).

This proves the theorem.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Note that the diagram in the beginning of the proof of Theorem
4.3
stays commutative, as long as we define
\xi_{p_{1},p_{2},k}
to be the map
\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}\circ\Xi_{p_{1},p_{2},k-2}\colon V\to\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}
for any subspace
V\subset W_{p_{1}}^{0}\otimes W_{p_{2}}^{0}
.

For weights
k\geq 3
, subsets
\mathcal{S}\subset\{0,\ldots,k-2\}
and congruence subgroups
\Gamma
, we consider the spaces
M^{\mathcal{S}}_{k}(\Gamma)
defined by

\displaystyle M^{\mathcal{S}}_{k}(\Gamma):=\left\{f\in M_{k}(\Gamma)\colon L(f;\ell+1)=0\text{ for all }\ell\in\mathcal{S}\right\}.

A realistic looking dimension formula is a direct consequence of the upper theorem.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let the same conditions hold as in Theorem
4.3
. Then we have

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\mathcal{S}}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}=\dim\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}-|\mathcal{S}|.

For large weights (compared to
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
), the situation is more subtle, as we have less control over the weak functions involved. However, Corollary
3.22
at least gives estimates. In this spirit we finally mention a relationship between the order of functions in
J_{p_{1},p_{2}}^{0}
already introduced and the vanishing of critical
L
-values. We introduce the space

\displaystyle V_{M,N,k}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}:=V_{M,N}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}\cap(W_{M}^{0}\otimes W_{N}^{0})_{k}.

While Theorem
4.3
worked well for small weights, Theorem
4.6
can be applied particularly well for large weights.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
be two odd prime numbers and
k\geq 3
be an integer. Let
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
be integers such that
\max\{0,p_{2}-k-1\}\leq\ell_{1}\leq p_{1}-2
,
\max\{0,p_{1}-k-1\}\leq\ell_{2}\leq p_{2}-2
, and
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\leq k-1
. We assign
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
a space

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}:=\left<f\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}\Big|\ \mathrm{ord}\left(\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}^{-1}(f)\right)\geq(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\right>,

where
\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}
is the isomorphism defined in (
22
). Then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}\subset\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\{0,\ldots,\ell_{1}-1\}\cup\{k-1-\ell_{2},\ldots,k-2\}}(\Gamma_{1}(p_{1}p_{2}))_{0}^{(p_{1},p_{2})}.

Proof.

The map
\Xi_{p_{1},p_{2},k-2}
is order preserving by Theorem
3.20
. So
\mathrm{ord}(\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}^{-1}(f))\geq(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
is equivalent to
\mathrm{ord}(\Xi_{p_{1},p_{2},k}^{-1}(\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}^{-1}(f)))\geq(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
. Hence by Theorem
3.20
we find

\displaystyle\Xi_{p_{1},p_{2},k}^{-1}(\widetilde{\vartheta}_{k}^{-1}(f))

\displaystyle\in V_{p_{1},p_{2},k}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1}\leq c\\
\ell_{2}\leq d\\
c+d\equiv k-2\pmod{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}\left(\alpha_{c}^{(M)}\otimes\alpha_{d}^{(N)}\right)

\displaystyle\subset\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(m\notin\mathcal{S}\wedge k-2-n\notin\mathcal{S}\right)\\
\vee\left((m,k-2-n)\in\mathcal{S}^{2}\wedge m\not=k-2-n\right)\\
m+n\equiv k-2\pmod{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}\left(\alpha_{m}^{(M)}\otimes\alpha_{n}^{(N)}\right),

where
\mathcal{S}:=\{0,\ldots,\ell_{1}-1\}\cup\{k-1-\ell_{2},\ldots,k-2\}
, as
c\geq\ell_{1}
and
c\in\mathcal{S}
implies
p_{1}-2\leq k-1+\ell_{2}\leq c\leq p_{1}-3
, which is absurd, and similarly
d\geq\ell_{2}
and
k-2-d\in\mathcal{S}
implies
p_{2}-2\leq k-1-\ell_{1}\leq d\leq p_{2}-3
, a contradiction. The claim now follows with Corollary
3.22
and Remark
4.4
.
∎
