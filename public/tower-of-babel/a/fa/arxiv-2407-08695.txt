Lower T-count with faster algorithms
Vivien Vandaele

Abstract
Among the cost metrics characterizing a quantum circuit, the T T -count stands out as one of the most crucial as its minimization is particularly important in various areas of quantum computation such as fault-tolerant quantum computing and quantum circuit simulation. In this work, we contribute to the T T -count reduction problem by proposing efficient T T -count optimizers with low execution times. In particular, we greatly improve the complexity of TODD , an algorithm currently providing the best T T -count reduction on various quantum circuits. We also propose some modifications to the algorithm which are leading to a significantly lower number of T T gates. In addition, we propose another algorithm which has an even lower complexity and that achieves a better or equal T T -count than the state of the art on most quantum circuits evaluated. We also prove that the number of T T gates in the circuit obtained after executing our algorithms on a Hadamard-free circuit composed of n n qubits is upper bounded by n ⁡ ( n + 1 ) / 2 + 1 n(n+1)/2+1 , which improves on the worst-case T T -count of existing optimization algorithms. From this we derive an upper bound of ( n + 1 ) ​ ( n + 2 ​ h ) / 2 + 1 (n+1)(n+2h)/2+1 for the number of T T gates in a Clifford + T +T circuit where h h is the number of internal Hadamard gates in the circuit, i.e. the number of Hadamard gates lying between the first and the last T T gate of the circuit.



1
Introduction

One of the main tasks of a quantum compiler is to minimize the resources needed to execute a given quantum algorithm.
This step is of considerable importance in the compilation stack as it makes quantum computation more practical and efficient.
To complete this task effectively and decide which optimization to perform, we must first identify the most expensive operations hindering our way towards fast and functional quantum computation.
In this regard the
T
gate is often targeted as it cannot be trivially implemented, for instance via transversal operations, in a fault-tolerant way in most quantum error correcting codes as opposed to Clifford gates.
It implies that implementing a
T
gate is generally much more costly than performing a Clifford operation
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
Also, numerous quantum error correcting codes can be used to perform universal fault-tolerant quantum computation with the Clifford
+T
gate set.
In such a setup, the depth of a quantum circuit, and so the time required to execute it, is generally directly linked to the
T
-depth of the circuit
[
4
]
.
For this reason, much work has been put into the minimization of the
T
-depth in Clifford
+T
circuits
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
But if the number of qubits at disposal is limited, then the depth of the circuit also depends on the number of
T
gates within it.
In addition, the depth of a circuit dictates the minimum number of physical qubits needed to encode the logical qubits utilized to perform the fault-tolerant computation.
The coherence time of the logical qubits must be greater than the time required to execute the whole circuit, and so the required amount of physical qubits per logical qubits increases as the depth of the circuit increases.
We can discern a feedback loop here: if the depth of the circuit is diminished then less physical qubits are required to encode the logical qubits, which frees up qubits that can be used to further lower the depth of the circuit.
The optimization of the
T
-count can intervene at multiple stages of this process.
Firstly, a lower
T
-count can induce a lower
T
-depth and so a lower circuit depth
[
8
]
.
Also, the number of qubits required to implement the
T
gates can depend on the
T
-count.
This is for example the case when the
T
gates are implemented via magic state distillation
[
11
]
.
A lower number of
T
gates can thus lower the number of physical qubits required to implement the circuit.

The optimization of the
T
-count also have important applications outside of fault-tolerant quantum computation.
Numerous quantum compilers designed for NISQ devices are incorporating a step consisting in reducing the number of
T
gates
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
It has been demonstrated by these compilers that reducing the
T
-count can lead to shorter circuits and can help with the minimization of other gates such as the
\mathrm{CNOT}
gate.

Besides compilation,
T
-count minimization also plays an important role in quantum circuits simulation.
As stated by the Gottesman-Knill theorem
[
15
]
, circuits composed of Clifford gates and Pauli measurements can be efficiently simulated by a classical computer.
Extending the gate set by adding the
T
gate allows the simulation of universal quantum circuits at the cost of a significant increase in computational time as no algorithm is currently known to efficiently simulate these circuits.
That is why many simulation techniques have a runtime that scales exponentially with respect to the number of
T
gates
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
.
Minimizing the number of
T
gates is then essential to exploit the full potential of these simulators and to push back the frontier of non-simulable quantum circuits.

State of the art and contributions.

Any unitary gate can be implemented by the Clifford
+T
gate set up to an arbitrary precision.
Therefore, a compilation problem of primordial importance is to find an approximation, over the Clifford
+T
gate set and that uses a little amount of
T
gates, of a given unitary operator to an accuracy within
\epsilon>0
.
A fundamental solution to this problem, and which can be applied to any finite universal gate set, was given by the Solovay-Kitaev theorem
[
21
,
22
]
.
Other approaches designed for the Clifford
+T
gate set were then developed to obtain better approximations
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
.
Further improvements have then been made by introducing measurements
[
26
]
and by using a probabilistic mixture of unitaries
[
27
,
28
]
.
Recently, it has been shown that these two methods can be combined with a novel approach to achieve better results
[
29
]
.
Another important synthesis problem concerns the set of unitary gates which can be exactly implemented over the Clifford+
T
gate set.
Given one of these unitary gates, the problem then consists in finding an exact Clifford
+T
implementation of it using a minimal number of
T
gates.
For this problem, an optimal and efficient algorithm is known for the case of single-qubit unitaries
[
30
]
.

Once a Clifford
+T
implementation of a unitary gate has been found, whether through approximate or exact synthesis, some quantum circuit optimization methods can then be applied to reduce the number of
T
gates in the circuit.
The algorithms developed for this purpose and achieving the best
T
-count reductions are foremostly designed for the restricted class of
\{\mathrm{CNOT},S,T\}
quantum circuits.
The problem of
T
-count optimization for this class of circuits has been well defined by showing its equivalence with the problem of decoding Reed-Muller codes
[
31
]
.
In particular, it was demonstrated that the codewords of the punctured Reed-Muller code of length
2^{n}-1
and order
n-4
are generating the complete set of identities that can be used to optimize the number of
T
gates in
\{\mathrm{CNOT},S,T\}
circuits.
Reducing the number of
T
gates can then be done by finding relevant identities in this large set.
For example it has been shown that a particular subset of identities, called spider nest identities, can be efficiently exploited to reduce the number of
T
gates
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
.
An effective way to find relevant identities that can be applied to reduce the number of
T
gates was given by the
TODD
algorithm
[
36
]
.
However, an important drawback of the
TODD
algorithm is its complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}m^{5})
where
n
is the number of qubits and
m
is the number of
T
gates in the initial circuit, which makes it impractical for circuits of large size.
In Section
3
, we show how the complexity of the
TODD
algorithm can be reduced to
\mathcal{O}(n^{4}m^{3})
.
In addition, we propose some modifications to the
TODD
algorithm which are resulting in a significantly improved reduction in the number of
T
gates.
In the same section we propose another algorithm which has an even lower complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}m^{3})
and that achieves better results than the original
TODD
algorithm on most quantum circuits evaluated.
We also prove that the algorithms presented in Section
3
are producing Hadamard-free circuits in which the
T
-count is upper bounded by
(n^{2}+n)/2+1
where
n
is the number of qubits.
Benchmarks are provided in Section
4
to evaluate the performances, in terms of
T
-count and execution time, of our algorithms on a library of reversible logic circuits and on large-scale quantum circuits.
In Section
5
, we extend our results for minimizing the number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates, where
d
is a non-negative integer.
Finally, in Section
6
, we demonstrate an upper bound for the number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates in a Clifford
+\{R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d}),R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
circuit.
For Clifford
+T
circuits we obtain an upper bound of
(n+1)(n+2h)/2+1
for the number of
T
gates, which can be satisfied in polynomial time and without any ancillary qubit, and where
h
is the number of internal Hadamard gates in the circuit.

Preliminaries

2.1
T
-count optimization in Hadamard-free circuits

We define the set of Pauli operators
\mathcal{P}_{n}
as the set composed of all the tensor products of
n
Pauli matrices, which are defined as follows:

I=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

with a multiplicative factor of
\pm 1
.
A Pauli rotation
R_{P}(\theta)
is defined as follows:

R_{P}(\theta)=\exp(-i\theta P/2)=\cos(\theta/2)I-i\sin(\theta/2)P

for a Pauli operator
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and an angle
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
For instance, the
T
gate is defined as a
\pi/4
Pauli
Z
rotation:

T=R_{Z}(\pi/4).

The Clifford group, denoted
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, is generated by the set of
\pi/2
Pauli rotations acting on
n
qubits

\{R_{P}(\pi/2)\mid P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\}.

A Pauli operator
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
conjugated by a Clifford gate
U\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
is always equal to another Pauli operator
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, i.e.
U^{\dagger}PU=P^{\prime}
.
This fact also holds when a Pauli rotation is conjugated by
U\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
:

U^{\dagger}R_{P}(\theta)U=R_{U^{\dagger}PU}(\theta)=R_{P^{\prime}}(\theta).

(1)

That is why the operation performed by a Clifford
+T
circuit acting on
n
qubits, represented by a unitary gate
U
, can always be described by a sequence of
\pi/4
Pauli rotations and a final Clifford operator
C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}

[
37
]
:

U=e^{i\phi}C\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}R_{P_{i}}(\pi/4)\right)

(2)

where
m
is the number of
T
gates in the circuit and
P_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\setminus\{\pm I^{\otimes n}\}
.

Multiple algorithms to reduce the number of
T
gates are foremostly designed for circuits composed of
\{\text{CNOT},S,T\}
gates.
In order to make use of these algorithms for Clifford
+T
circuits, it is necessary to circumvent the Hadamard gates in the input circuit since these algorithms cannot be directly executed on them.
This can be done using one the two following methods.
The first method consists in dividing the circuit into Hadamard-free subcircuits and Clifford subcircuits containing Hadamard gates.
The number of
T
gates in the Hadamard-free subcircuits can then be optimized using these algorithms.
There exists multiple strategies that can be employed to create such a partition of the circuit.
It is generally preferred to regroup the
T
gates in the least number of Hadamard-free subcircuits as possible to take advantage of the fact that the number of
T
gates in an Hadamard-free circuit can be upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
, where
n
is the number of qubits.
One approach to partition the circuit is to describe the operation performed by the circuit by a sequence of Pauli rotations and a final Clifford operator, as in Equation
2
, and to then reorder the Pauli rotations in the sequence by forming groups of mutually commuting Pauli rotations.
This can for example be done by using the procedure whose pseudo-code is given in Algorithm
1
.
This algorithm has a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(nm^{2})
since checking whether or not two Pauli rotations commute takes
\mathcal{O}(n)
operations and such commutativity checks are done at most
m
times at each iteration of the loop.
This way of grouping the Pauli rotations is not new, an equivalent algorithm (but which has a worst-case complexity of
\mathcal{O}(nm^{3})
) was given in Reference
[
38
]
.
By using the layers of Pauli rotations produced by Algorithm
1

2
can then be rewritten as follows:

U=e^{i\phi}C\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\lvert L\rvert}\prod_{R_{P}\in L_{i}}R_{P}(\pi/4)\right)

(3)

where
\lvert L\rvert
denotes the number of layers in
L

The second method to circumvent the Hadamard gates in the circuit relies on a measurement-based gadget which can substitute a Hadamard gate
[
40
]
.
This gadget, presented in Figure

If all the Hadamard gates in the circuits are gadgetized, then the circuit is Hadamard-free and the number of
T
gates can be optimized using algorithms specifically designed for Hadamard-free circuits.
Only internal Hadamard gates, which are the Hadamard gates comprised between the first and the last
T
gates of the circuit, are required to be gadgetized.
Indeed, if only the internal Hadamard gates are gadgetized then the circuit can be partitioned into an initial and final Clifford circuit and a Hadamard-free circuit in between containing all the
T
gates.
The main drawback of this method is that one additional qubit must be used for each internal Hadamard gate that is gadgetized.
This motivates the minimization of internal Hadamard gates.
To do so, we will use the algorithm presented in Reference
[
41
]
which performs the synthesis of the sequence of Pauli rotations of Equation
2
with a minimal number of internal Hadamard gates.

The classically controlled
X
gate of Figure
1
, can be commuted through the subsequent
R_{Z}
gates of the circuit by relying on the following equality:

R_{Z}(\theta)X=XR_{Z}(-\theta)=XR_{Z}(-2\theta)R_{Z}(\theta)

(5)

In a Clifford
+T
circuit, the angle
\theta
is a multiple of
\pi/4
, and so
-2\theta
is a multiple of
\pi/2
, which results in a rotation implementable using only Clifford gates.
The classically controlled
X
gate is only commuted through the Pauli rotations succeeding the associated Hadamard gate, and not the Pauli rotations preceding it.
Therefore, the optimized circuit in which all Hadamard gates have been gadgetized can be implemented with a measurement depth equal to
\lvert L\rvert-1
, where
\lvert L\rvert
corresponds to the number of layers as in Equation
4
.

2.2
Weighted polynomial and signature tensor

We now describe the established formalism for the optimization of the number of
T
gates in
\{\text{CNOT},S,T\}
circuits.
Let
C
be a
\{\text{CNOT},S,T\}
circuit operating over
n
qubits.
It has been demonstrated
[
5
]
that the action of
C
on a basis state has the form

\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\mapsto U_{f}\lvert g(\bf\boldsymbol{x})\rangle

(6)

where
g:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a linear reversible Boolean function which can be implemented using only CNOT gates
[
42
]
, and

U_{f}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}\omega^{f(\boldsymbol{x})}\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rvert

(7)

where
\omega=e^{i\pi/4}
and
f:\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{8}
is a multilinear polynomial of degree 3 such that

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\alpha}^{n}l_{\alpha}x_{\alpha}-2\sum_{\alpha<\beta}^{n}q_{\alpha,\beta}x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}+4\sum_{\alpha<\beta<\gamma}^{n}c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}\pmod{8}

(8)

where
l_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{8}
,
q_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}
and
c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
In the same way as in Reference
[
43
]
, we will refer to the function
f
as a weighted polynomial due to the fact that each monomial of order
m
has a coefficient weighted by
2^{m-1}
.
It has been shown in Reference
[
43
]
that
U_{f}
belongs to the diagonal subgroup of the third level of the Clifford hierarchy
[
44
]
.
We will use
\mathcal{D}_{3}
to denote this group and
\mathcal{D}^{C}_{3}
to refer to the unitaries of
\mathcal{D}_{3}
which are implementable using only CCZ gates.

The parities of the coefficients for a weighted polynomial
f
can be described by the signature tensor
\mathcal{A}^{(U_{f})}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{(n,n,n)}

[
36
]
, such that
\mathcal{A}^{(U_{f})}
is a symmetric tensor of order 3 satisfying

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\alpha,\alpha,\alpha}

\displaystyle\equiv l_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\pmod{2}

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\sigma(\alpha,\beta,\beta)}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{A}_{\sigma(\alpha,\alpha,\beta)}=q_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle\pmod{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\sigma(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}

\displaystyle\equiv c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}

\displaystyle\pmod{2}

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are satisfying
0\leq\alpha<\beta<\gamma<n
and
\sigma
denotes all permutations of the indices.
For convenience, we will drop the superscript
(U_{f})
from
\mathcal{A}
when it is clear from the context that
\mathcal{A}
is associated with
U_{f}
.
It has been proven in Reference
[
43
]
that
U_{2f}
can be implemented using only Clifford gates for any weighted polynomial
f
.
It implies that two unitaries
U_{f}
and
U_{f^{\prime}}
, where
f
and
f^{\prime}
are weighted polynomials, are Clifford equivalent if the coefficients of
f
and
f^{\prime}
all have the same parity, i.e. if
\mathcal{A}^{(U_{f})}
and
\mathcal{A}^{(U_{f^{\prime}})}
are equal.

2.3
Phase polynomial and parity table

An implementation of the
U_{f}
gate for a weighted polynomial
f
can be described by a phase polynomial via the circuit-polynomial correspondence
[
45
,
46
]
.
A phase polynomial
p
is a linear combination of linear Boolean functions:

p(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}(y^{(i)}_{1}x_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus y^{(i)}_{n}x_{n})\pmod{8}

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
,
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{8}^{n}
an
m\geq 0
.
We will refer to the Boolean vectors
\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}
as the parities of the phase polynomial
p
and to
\boldsymbol{a}
as the weights of
p
.
The parities of a phase polynomial can be described by a Boolean matrix, called parity table and denoted
P
, of size
n\times m
where
n
is the number of qubits and
m
is the number of parities weighted by a non-zero
a_{i}
.
For every weighted polynomial
f
we can find a phase polynomial
p
with weights
\boldsymbol{a}
such that
p(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{x})
for all
\boldsymbol{x}
.
Such phase polynomial can then be used to implement
U_{f}
via a phase polynomial synthesis algorithm which will result in a circuit containing
|\boldsymbol{a}\pmod{2}|

As we are focusing on minimizing the number of

\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}
in the parity table

a_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{2}
.
The number of columns of

For example, the weighted polynomial associated with the CCZ gate is
f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=4x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\alpha\neq\beta\neq\gamma$},\\

(11)

a\in\mathbb{Z}_{8}

a\left(x_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus x_{n}\right)=a\left(\sum_{\alpha}^{n}x_{\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha<\beta}^{n}2x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}+\sum_{\alpha<\beta<\gamma}^{n}4x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}\right)\pmod{8}.

(12)

We provide a proof of Equation
12
, and its more general form, in Section
5.1
.
The coefficients of the weighted polynomial represented in the right-hand side of this equation are
l_{\alpha}=a\pmod{8}
,
q_{\alpha,\beta}=a\pmod{4}
and
c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=a\pmod{2}
.
Equation
12
can then be used for each parity of a given phase polynomial to compute its associated weighted polynomial:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}(y^{(i)}_{1}x_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus y^{(i)}_{n}x_{n})\pmod{8}

(13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\left(\sum_{\alpha}^{n}y_{\alpha}^{(i)}x_{\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha<\beta}^{n}2y_{\alpha}^{(i)}y_{\beta}^{(i)}x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}+\sum_{\alpha<\beta<\gamma}^{n}4y_{\alpha}^{(i)}y_{\beta}^{(i)}y_{\gamma}^{(i)}x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}\right)\pmod{8}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}^{n}l_{\alpha}x_{\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha<\beta}^{n}2q_{\alpha,\beta}x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}+\sum_{\alpha<\beta<\gamma}^{n}4c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}\pmod{8}

where
l_{\alpha},q_{\alpha,\beta}
and
c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
are satisfying

\displaystyle l_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}y_{\alpha}^{(i)}\pmod{8},

(14)

\displaystyle q_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}y_{\alpha}^{(i)}y_{\beta}^{(i)}\pmod{4},

\displaystyle c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}y_{\alpha}^{(i)}y_{\beta}^{(i)}y_{\gamma}^{(i)}\pmod{2}.

Two weighted polynomials
f
and
f^{\prime}
, with associated coefficients
l_{\alpha},q_{\alpha,\beta},c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
and
l^{\prime}_{\alpha},q^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta},c^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
respectively, are equal if and only if
l_{\alpha}=l^{\prime}_{\alpha},q_{\alpha,\beta}=q^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}
and
c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=c^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
.
Indeed, if these equalities are not satisfied, then we can find some vector
\boldsymbol{x}
such that
f(\boldsymbol{x})\neq f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
Therefore, two phase polynomials
p
and
p^{\prime}
are implementing the same operator if and only if their associated weighted polynomials
f
and
f^{\prime}
are equal.
The problem of
T
-count optimization then consists in finding a phase polynomial
p
which implements a given weighted polynomial
f
with a minimal number of
T
gates.
Notice that the coefficients of the weighted polynomial represented in Equation
12
all have the same parity:
l_{\alpha}\equiv q_{\alpha,\beta}\equiv c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\equiv a\pmod{2}
.
And if
a
is even, then the associated rotation of angle
a\pi/4
is a multiple of
\pi/2
and can be implemented using only
S
gates.
That is why a weighted polynomial
f
can be implemented using only
\{\mathrm{CNOT},S\}
gates if and only if all its coefficients have an even parity, in such case we say that
f
is a Clifford weighted polynomial.
Then, a weighted polynomial
p
with an associated parity table
P
is an implentation of a weighted polynomial
f
with an associated signature tensor
\mathcal{A}
up to an operator implementable over the
\{\mathrm{CNOT},S\}
gate set if and only if the equality

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}\wedge P_{\gamma}\rvert\pmod{2}

(15)

is satisfied for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
satisfying
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq\gamma<n
, where
n
is the number of qubits.
Throughout the paper, the notation
\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\rvert
will be used to refer to the Hamming weight of the vector
\boldsymbol{v}
, and the symbol
\wedge
will refer to the logical AND operation.
Let
f^{\prime}
be the weighted polynomial implemented by
p
, then
f
and
f^{\prime}
have the same signature tensor, and
f
can be implemented by performing the synthesis of
p
and the synthesis of the Clifford operator associated with the Clifford weighted polynomial
f-f^{\prime}
.
The problem of finding a phase polynomial implementing a given weighted polynomial with a minimal number of
T
gates and up to an operator implementable over the
\{\mathrm{CNOT},S\}
gate set can then be described by the following third order symmetric tensor rank decomposition (3-STR) problem
[
36
]
.

Problem 1

(3-STR)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{(n,n,n)}
be a symmetric tensor such that

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=\mathcal{A}_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}}

(16)

for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
and
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}
satisfying the set equality
\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}=\{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}\}
.
Find a Boolean matrix
P
of size
n\times m
such that

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}\wedge P_{\gamma}\rvert\pmod{2}

(17)

for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
satisfying
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq\gamma<n
, with minimal
m
.

The
T
-count minimization problem in
\{\mathrm{CNOT},T,S\}
circuits have first been shown to be equivalent to finding a minimum distance decoding in the order
n-4
punctured Reed-Muller code of length
2^{n}-1

[
31
]
, noted
\mathcal{RM}(n-4,n)^{*}
, which is equivalent to the 3-STR problem
[
47
]
.
The complexity class of the 3-STR problem is unknown, however, the related problem of finding the tensor rank of a tensor of order
3
is NP-complete
[
48
]
.
And the more general problem of optimizing the number of
T
gates in a Clifford
+T
circuit is NP-hard
[
49
]
.

An illustrative overview of the process we described for the optimization of the number of
T
gates in Hadamard-free circuits is provided in Figure
4
.
In Section
3
, we present two algorithms attempting to solve the 3-STR problem and we prove that the number of
T
gates in the circuit produced by these algorithms is upper bounded by
(n^{2}+n)/2+1
.
We evaluate the perfomances of our algorithms in the benchmarks of Section
4.1
.
And we generalize our results for the optimization of the number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates, where
d
is a non-negative integer, in Section
5
.

3
T
-count reduction algorithms

In this section we tackle the 3-STR problem as defined in Section
2
.
First, we propose an algorithm for this problem in Subsection
3.1
and we prove that the number of
T
gates in the solution produced by this algorithm is upper bounded by
n(n+1)/2+1
.
Then, in Subsection
3.2
, we show how the complexity of the
TODD
algorithm of Reference
[
36
]
can be reduced and we propose some modifications to this algorithm to improve its performances.

3.1
Third order homogeneous polynomials elimination algorithm

The key mechanism used by our algorithm for reducing the
T
-count is based on the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
P
be a parity table of size
n\times m
and
P^{\prime}=P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{y}^{T}
where
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
are vectors of size
n
and
m
respectively such that

\displaystyle\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{2}

(18)

\displaystyle\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{2}

(19)

\displaystyle\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{2}

(20)

for all
0\leq\alpha<\beta<n
.
Then we have

\lvert P^{\prime}_{\alpha}\wedge P^{\prime}_{\beta}\wedge P^{\prime}_{\gamma}\rvert\equiv\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}\wedge P_{\gamma}\rvert\pmod{2}

(21)

for all
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq\gamma<n
.

Proof.

For all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
satisfying
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq\gamma<n
, we have:

\displaystyle\lvert P^{\prime}_{\alpha}\wedge P^{\prime}_{\beta}\wedge P^{\prime}_{\gamma}\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert(P_{\alpha}\oplus z_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{y})\wedge(P_{\beta}\oplus z_{\beta}\boldsymbol{y})\wedge(P_{\gamma}\oplus z_{\gamma}\boldsymbol{y})\rvert

(22)

\displaystyle=\lvert\left[(P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta})\oplus z_{\beta}(P_{\alpha}\wedge\boldsymbol{y})\oplus z_{\alpha}(P_{\beta}\wedge\boldsymbol{y})\oplus z_{\alpha}z_{\beta}\boldsymbol{y}\right]\wedge(P_{\gamma}\oplus z_{\gamma}\boldsymbol{y})\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}\wedge P_{\gamma}\rvert+z_{\gamma}\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\rvert+z_{\beta}\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\gamma}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\rvert+z_{\alpha}\lvert P_{\beta}\wedge P_{\gamma}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\rvert

\displaystyle+z_{\beta}z_{\gamma}\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\rvert+z_{\alpha}z_{\gamma}\lvert P_{\beta}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\rvert+z_{\alpha}z_{\beta}\lvert P_{\gamma}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\rvert+z_{\alpha}z_{\beta}z_{\gamma}\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\pmod{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}\wedge P_{\gamma}\rvert\pmod{2}

∎

If the conditions of Theorem
1
are satisfied, then the set of columns selected by
\boldsymbol{y}
are forming a weighted polynomial that can be divided into two weighted polynomials
f
and
f^{\prime}
, where
f
is a third order homogeneous weighted polynomial:

f(\boldsymbol{x})=4\sum_{\alpha<\beta<\gamma}^{n}c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}\pmod{8}

(23)

where
c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}\wedge P_{\gamma}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\pmod{2}
, and where
f^{\prime}
is a Clifford weighted polynomial:

f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\alpha}^{n}l_{\alpha}x_{\alpha}+2\sum_{\alpha<\beta}^{n}q_{\alpha,\beta}x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}\pmod{8}

(24)

where
l_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{8}
,
q_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathbb{Z}_{4}
and
l_{\alpha}\equiv q_{\alpha,\beta}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
.
The unitary gate associated with the weighted polynomial
f
belongs to the
\mathcal{D}^{C}_{3}
group: it is implementable using only CCZ gates
[
43
]
.
It has already been shown that such weighted polynomials can be exploited to reduce the
T
-count, notably via the subadditivity theorem of Reference
[
43
]
.
Let
U\in\mathcal{D}_{3}
act on
n
qubits, in the following we define
\tau[U]
as the optimal
T
-count to implement
U
without ancillary qubits:

\tau[U]=\min\{t\mid U=C_{0}T_{1}C_{1}\ldots T_{t}C_{t},\{C_{0},\ldots,C_{t}\}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}^{*}\}

(25)

where
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{*}
is the subgroup of Clifford operators which can be implemented with CNOT and
S
gates.
The subadditivity theorem of Reference
[
43
]
states that if
\tau[U_{1}]\equiv 1\pmod{2}
and
\tau[U_{2}]>0
, then
\tau[U_{1}\otimes U_{2}]<\tau[U_{1}]+\tau[U_{2}]
where
U_{1}\in\mathcal{D}^{C}_{3}
and
U_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{3}
.
Based on Theorem
1
, we can actually remove the condition
\tau[U_{1}]\equiv 1\pmod{2}
, which gives the following theorem.

Theorem 2

(Subadditivity theorem)
.

Let
U_{1}\in\mathcal{D}^{C}_{3}
, and
U_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{3}
.
If
\tau[U_{1}],\tau[U_{2}]>0
, then
\tau[U_{1}\otimes U_{2}]<\tau[U_{1}]+\tau[U_{2}]
.

Proof.

Let
W=\begin{bmatrix}P&Q\end{bmatrix}
be a parity table such that
P
and
Q
are the parity tables associated with the implementation of
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
and which have
\tau[U_{1}]
and
\tau[U_{2}]
columns respectively.
Then, because
U_{1}\in\mathcal{D}^{C}_{3}
,
P
satisfies the following equations:

\displaystyle\lvert P_{\alpha}\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{2}

(26)

\displaystyle\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{2}

(27)

for all
\alpha,\beta
.
Let
\boldsymbol{z}=P_{:,i}\oplus Q_{:,j}
for any
i
and
j
satisfying
0\leq i<\tau[U_{1}]
,
0\leq j<\tau[U_{2}]
, where
P_{:,i}
denotes the
i
th column of the parity table
P
.
And let
P^{\prime}
be a parity table such that

P^{\prime}=\begin{cases}P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}&\text{if $\tau[U_{1}]\equiv 0\pmod{2}$},\\
\begin{bmatrix}P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}&\boldsymbol{z}\end{bmatrix}&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Then, as stated by Theorem
1
, the parity table
P^{\prime}
satisfies

\lvert P^{\prime}_{\alpha}\wedge P^{\prime}_{\beta}\wedge P^{\prime}_{\gamma}\rvert\equiv\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}\wedge P_{\gamma}\rvert\pmod{2}

(28)

for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
.
And so the parity table
W^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}P^{\prime}&Q\end{bmatrix}
, which has at most one more column than
W
, also satisfies

\lvert W^{\prime}_{\alpha}\wedge W^{\prime}_{\beta}\wedge W^{\prime}_{\gamma}\rvert\equiv\lvert W_{\alpha}\wedge W_{\beta}\wedge W_{\gamma}\rvert\pmod{2}

(29)

for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
.
However, we can notice that
P^{\prime}_{:,i}=Q_{:,j}
.
Therefore, by removing these two columns from
W^{\prime}
Equation
29
still holds and
W^{\prime}
has at least one less column than
W
.
The parity table
W^{\prime}
implements the unitary
U_{1}\otimes U_{2}
up to a Clifford operator and has at most
\tau[U_{1}]+\tau[U_{2}]-1
columns, thus we have
\tau[U_{1}\otimes U_{2}]\leq\tau[U_{1}]+\tau[U_{2}]-1<\tau[U_{1}]+\tau[U_{2}]
.
∎

Based on this subadditivity theorem and on Theorem
1
, we can derive the following upper bound on the number of
T
gates in a
\{\mathrm{CNOT},S,T\}
circuit.

Theorem 3
.

The number of
T
gates in an
n
-qubits
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
T
,
S\}
circuit can be upper bounded by

2\lfloor(n^{2}+n)/4\rfloor+1\leq(n^{2}+n)/2+1

(30)

in polynomial time.

Proof.

Let
U
be a unitary gate implementable by a
\{\mathrm{CNOT},S,T\}
gate set, let
P
be a parity table of size
n\times m
which implements
U
up to an operator implementable over the
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
S\}
gate set, and let
L
be a matrix with rows labelled by
(\alpha\beta)
such that

\displaystyle L_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}

(31)

for all
\alpha,\beta
satisfying
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta<n
.
If
P
has strictly more than
(n^{2}+n)/2+1
columns then we can necessarily find a non-zero vector
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfying
L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\neq\boldsymbol{1}
because
L
has
(n^{2}+n)/2
rows.
Note that such vector
\boldsymbol{y}
necessarily satisfies Equations
19
and
20
of Theorem
1
.
We can then divide
P
into two non-empty parity tables
P^{(1)}
and
P^{(2)}
where the column
P_{:,i}
belongs to
P^{(1)}
if and only if
y_{i}=1
and to
P^{(2)}
otherwise.
The parity tables
P^{(1)}
and
P^{(2)}
are implementations of some unitary gates
U_{1}\in\mathcal{D}^{C}_{3}
and
U_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{3}
respectively.
The subadditivity theorem (Theorem
2
) can then be exploited to reduce the number of columns of
P
.
Let
\boldsymbol{z}=P_{:,i}\oplus P_{:,j}
where
i
and
j
are satisfying
y_{i}=1
and
y_{j}=0
, and let

P^{\prime}=\begin{cases}P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{y}^{T}&\text{if $\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\equiv 0\pmod{2}$},\\
\begin{bmatrix}P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{y}^{T}&\boldsymbol{z}\end{bmatrix}&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

By Theorem
1
,
P^{\prime}
implements the same unitary gate as
P
up to an operator implementable over the
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
S\}
gate set.
The parity table
P^{\prime}
has at most one more column than
P
and we have
P^{\prime}_{:,i}=P^{\prime}_{:,j}
.
Therefore, the columns
i
and
j
can be removed from
P^{\prime}
, which entails that
P^{\prime}
has at least one less column than
P
.
We showed that if the number of columns of
P
is strictly greater than
(n^{2}+n)/2+1
, then the number of columns of
P
can be reduced by at least one in polynomial time.
Moreover, if the number of columns of
P
is equal to
(n^{2}+n)/2+1
and is even, then we can necessarily find a non-zero vector
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfying
L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
because
L
has
(n^{2}+n)/2
rows.
If there exist
i
such that
y_{i}=0
then we can reduce the number of columns of
P
as described above.
Otherwise we must have
\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, and so
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfies the Equations of Theorem
1
.
Therefore, if
P^{\prime}=P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{y}^{T}
where
\boldsymbol{z}
is equal to the
i
th column of
P
for any
i
, then
P^{\prime}
implements the same unitary gate as
P
up to an operator implementable over the
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
S\}
gate set.
The parity table
P^{\prime}
has the same number of columns as
P
but its
i
th column is equal to the null vector and can therefore be removed, which leads to a parity table containing
(n^{2}+n)/2
columns.
The polynomial-time procedure described above to reduce the number of columns of
P
can be repeated until
P
has a number of columns lower or equal to

2\lfloor(n^{2}+n)/4\rfloor+1\leq(n^{2}+n)/2+1

(32)

∎

Note that, for
n>5
, this upper bound is better than the previously best known upper bound of
(n^{2}+3n-14)/2

[
43
]
.
The proof of Theorem
3
provides a straightforward algorithm for achieving this upper bound in polynomial time.
We propose some improvements regarding this approach by providing an algorithm whose pseudo-code is given in Algorithm
2
.

The algorithm starts by computing the set
Z
which contains all the vectors
\boldsymbol{z}
which can potentially be used to transform
P
via Theorem
1
and reduce its number of columns:

Z=\{P_{:,i}\oplus P_{:,j}\mid 0\leq i<j<m\}\cup\{P_{:,i}\mid 0\leq i<m\}.

(33)

For each vector
\boldsymbol{z}\in Z
, the algorithm also computes the set
S^{(\boldsymbol{z})}
of pairs of indices
\{i,j\}
satisfying
P_{:,i}\oplus P_{:,j}=\boldsymbol{z}
in the case where
i\neq j
or satisfying
P_{:,i}=\boldsymbol{z}
in the case where
i=j
.
Let
L
be a matrix with rows labelled by
(\alpha\beta)
such that

\displaystyle L_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}

(34)

for all
\alpha<\beta
.
In order to exploit the subadditivity theorem, the vector
\boldsymbol{y}
must satisfy
L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
,
\boldsymbol{y}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\neq\boldsymbol{1}
.
However, in the case where
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{1}
and
\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\equiv 0\pmod{2}
the number of columns of
P
can still be reduced.
Indeed, in such case the vector
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfies all the condition of Theorem
1
, therefore the parity table
P^{\prime}
defined as
P^{\prime}=P\oplus P_{:,i}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}
for any
i
is equivalent to
P
and its
i
th column is equal to the null vector and can therefore be removed, which reduces the number of columns by one.
To summarize, the number of columns of
P
can be reduced if
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfies
L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\neq\boldsymbol{1}
or
\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\equiv 0\pmod{2}
.
If no such
\boldsymbol{y}
is found, Algorithm
2
will stop and return
P
.
Otherwise, the algorithm will select a vector
\boldsymbol{z}
such that the number of duplicated columns plus the number of all-zero columns in the parity table
P^{\prime}=P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{y}^{T}
is maximized.
This number corresponds to the number of columns that can be removed from
P
if
\boldsymbol{z}
is chosen (minus one in the case where
\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\equiv 1\pmod{2}
), and is given by the following objective function:

-\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\pmod{2}+\sum_{\{i,j\}\in S^{(\boldsymbol{z})}}\begin{cases}2(y_{i}\oplus y_{j})&\text{if }i\neq j,\\
y_{i}+2(y_{i}\oplus 1)(\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\pmod{2})&\text{if }i=j.\end{cases}

(35)

The case where

is straightforward, as the
i
th column will be equal to the

th column of
P^{\prime}
if and only if

, which results in the removal of 2 columns.
In the case where
i=j
, the equation

y_{i}+2(y_{i}\oplus 1)(\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\pmod{2})

(36)

is equal to
1
if

because the
i
th column of
P^{\prime}
is the null vector, leading to the removal of 1 column.
Otherwise, if
y_{i}=0
and
\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert
is odd, then Equation
36
is equal to
2
because the
i
th column of
P^{\prime}
will be equal the
\boldsymbol{z}
column vector that is added to
P^{\prime}
in the case where
\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert
is odd, resulting in the removal of 2 columns.
Once the vector
\boldsymbol{z}
maximizing this objective function has been found, Algorithm
2
then computes the new parity table
P^{\prime}
which contains fewer columns, and performs a recursive call.

A variant of this algorithm could consist in finding both vectors
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
that are maximizing the objective function.
However, our experiments on this approach showed that it significantly increases the complexity of the algorithm for a rather marginal gain in the number of
T
gates.
The performances of Algorithm

are evaluated in Section

.

Complexity analysis.

The set

3.2
Improving the
TODD
algorithm

In this section we show how the
TODD
algorithm proposed in Reference
[
36
]
can be improved.
We first describe the algorithm and demonstrate how its complexity can be reduced.
We then propose a modified version of this algorithm to improve its performances.

The key mechanism of the
TODD
algorithm rests on the following theorem, which was first proven in Reference
[
36
]
.
We provide its proof for completeness in Appendix

.

Theorem 4
.

Let
P
be a parity table of size
n\times m
and
P^{\prime}=P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{y}^{T}
where
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
are vectors of size
n
and
m
respectively such that

\displaystyle\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{2}

(37)

\displaystyle\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{2}

(38)

\displaystyle\lvert\left[z_{\alpha}(P_{\beta}\wedge P_{\gamma})\oplus z_{\beta}(P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\gamma})\oplus z_{\gamma}(P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta})\right]\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{2}

(39)

for all
0\leq\alpha<\beta<\gamma<n
.
Then we have

\lvert P^{\prime}_{\alpha}\wedge P^{\prime}_{\beta}\wedge P^{\prime}_{\gamma}\rvert\equiv\lvert P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta}\wedge P_{\gamma}\rvert\pmod{2}

(40)

for all
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq\gamma<n
.

The
TODD
algorithm exploits this theorem for optimizing the number of
T
gates as follows.
Let
P
be a parity table of size
n\times m
, let
\boldsymbol{z}=P_{:,i}\oplus P_{:,j}
where
i\neq j
and let
\chi
be a matrix that contains a row equal to
P_{\alpha}
for every
\alpha
and a row equal to

z_{\alpha}(P_{\beta}\wedge P_{\gamma})\oplus z_{\beta}(P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\gamma})\oplus z_{\gamma}(P_{\alpha}\wedge P_{\beta})

for every triple
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
satisfying
0\leq\alpha<\beta<\gamma<n
.
A vector
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfying the conditions
38
and
39
of Theorem
4
can be found by computed the nullspace of
\chi
, i.e. if
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfies
\chi\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
then
\boldsymbol{y}
also satisfies the conditions
38
and
39
of Theorem
4
.
If
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfies
\chi\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
y_{i}\oplus y_{j}=1
, then the number of columns of

can be reduced.
Let

P^{\prime}=\begin{cases}P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{y}^{T}&\text{if $\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\equiv 0\pmod{2}$},\\
\begin{bmatrix}P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{y}^{T}&\boldsymbol{z}\end{bmatrix}&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Then, as stated by Theorem
4

P
and

are equivalent.
Moreover, we have

because
\boldsymbol{z}=P_{:,i}\oplus P_{:,j}
, therefore these two columns can be removed from

, which results in
P^{\prime}
having at least one less column than

.

If there doesn’t exist any
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfying
\chi\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
y_{i}\oplus y_{j}=1
, then a different
\boldsymbol{z}
vector can be considered.
In the worst case the
TODD
algorithm performs this search for all the vectors
\boldsymbol{z}
in the set
\{P_{:,i}\oplus P_{:j}\mid 0\leq i<j<n\}
, which contains
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
elements.
The matrix
\chi
is composed of
m
columns and
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
rows.
Performing a Gaussian elimination in order to find some
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfying
\chi\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
induces a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}m^{2})
.
This procedure must be performed for the
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})

\boldsymbol{z}
vectors evaluated.
And everytime a simplification is found the algorithm restart the same procedure on the new parity table

, this happens at most
m
times.
Thus, the overall complexity of the
TODD
algorithm is
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}m^{5})
.
The important worst-case complexity of the
TODD
algorithm makes it rapidly impractical for instances of increasing size.
We will demonstrate how this complexity can be reduced to
\mathcal{O}(n^{4}m^{3})
by providing an alternative and more efficient way of checking whether or not there exists a vector
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfying the conditions of Theorem
4
, and therefore avoiding the expensive Gaussian elimination required to solve the system
\chi\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

First, we can notice that Equation
39
of Theorem
4
can be greatly simplified in some cases, depending on the value of
\boldsymbol{z}
.
For instance, if
z_{\alpha}=1
for some
\alpha
and

for all

39
is equal to

\lvert z_{\alpha}(P_{\beta}\wedge P_{\gamma})\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\equiv 0\pmod{2}.

(41)

In this specific case, a vector
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfies Equations
38
and
39
of Theorem
4
if it satisfies
\chi\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
where
\chi
contains a row equal to Equation
38
for all
\alpha
and a row equal to Equation

\alpha\neq\beta\neq\gamma
,
\beta<\gamma

In total,

In the more general case where

and

5
Extension to higher levels of the Clifford hierarchy

In this section, we extend the results of Section
3
for the optimization of the number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates in
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
,
R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
circuits, for any non-negative integer
d
.
These gates appear in various quantum algorithms, such as Shor’s algorithm
[
62
]
, and there exists distillation protocols for implementing them fault tolerantly
[
63
,
64
]
.
Optimizing the number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates may increase the number of
R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})
gates.
However, this is motivated by the fact that the
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gate is typically more costly to implement than the
R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})
gate.

We formalize the problem of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates optimization in
\{\mathrm{CNOT},R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d}),R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
circuits in Subsection
5.1
.
Then, in Subsection
5.2
, we extend Theorem
3
to give an upper bound on the number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates in
\{\mathrm{CNOT},R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d}),R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
circuits, which can be satisfied in polynomial time by generalizing Algorithm
2
.
Finally, in Subsection
5.3
, we show how we can construct algorithms that are similar to Algorithm
3
for optimizing the number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates by providing a generalization of Theorem
6
.

5.1
Symmetric tensor rank decomposition problem

In this subsection we formalize the problem of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates optimization in
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
,
R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
circuits for any non-negative integer
d
, by showing its equivalence to the following symmetric tensor rank decomposition problem.

Problem 2

(
d
-STR)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{(n,\ldots,n)}
be a symmetric tensor of order
d
such that

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}}=\mathcal{A}_{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d}}

(93)

for all
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d}
such that the set equality
\{\alpha_{i},\ldots,\alpha_{d}\}=\{\beta_{i},\ldots,\beta_{d}\}
is satisfied.
Find a Boolean matrix
P
of size
n\times m
such that

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}}=\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d}P_{\alpha_{i}}\big\rvert\pmod{2}

(94)

for all
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
satisfying
0\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\alpha_{d}<n
, with minimal
m
.

We now prove the equivalence between the problem of minimizing the number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates in a
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
,
R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
circuit for any non-negative integer
d
and the
(d+1)
-STR problem.
The following theorem was first proven in Reference
[
31
]
by showing the equivalence between the problem of optimizing
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates and the minimum distance decoding problem for the punctured Reed-Muller code of order
n-d-2
and length
2^{n}-1
, which is equivalent to the
d
-STR problem
[
47
]
.
We provide a more straightforward proof of this theorem in Appendix
[
61
]
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
d
be a non-negative integer.
The
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
-count optimization problem over the
\{\mathrm{CNOT},R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d}),R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
gate set and the
(d+1)
-STR problem are equivalent.

5.2
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
-count upper bound in
\{\mathrm{CNOT},R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d}),R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
circuits

In this section we present an upper bound for the number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates in a
\{\mathrm{CNOT},

R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d}),

R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
circuit, and we present a method for satisfying this upper bound in polynomial time.
We first prove the following theorem, which is a generalization of Theorem
1
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
d
be a non-negative integer, let
P
be a parity table of size
n\times m
and let
P^{\prime}=P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{y}^{T}
where
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
are vectors of size
n
and
m
respectively such that

\displaystyle\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{2}

(95)

\displaystyle\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d}P_{\alpha_{i}}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\big\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{2}

(96)

for all
0\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\alpha_{d}<n
.
Then we have

\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}P^{\prime}_{\alpha_{i}}\big\rvert\equiv\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}P_{\alpha_{i}}\big\rvert\pmod{2}

(97)

for all
0\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\alpha_{d+1}<n
.

Proof.

For all
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d+1}
satisfying
0\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\alpha_{d+1}<n
, we have:

\displaystyle\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}P^{\prime}_{\alpha_{i}}\big\rvert

\displaystyle=\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}(P_{\alpha_{i}}\oplus z_{\alpha_{i}}\boldsymbol{y})\big\rvert

(98)

\displaystyle=\big\lvert\bigoplus_{k_{d+1}=0}^{1}\ldots\bigoplus_{k_{1}=0}^{1}\left[\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}(k_{i}P_{\alpha_{i}}\vee(1-k_{i})z_{\alpha_{i}}\boldsymbol{y})\right]\big\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k_{d+1}=0}^{1}\ldots\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{1}\big\lvert\left[\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}(k_{i}P_{\alpha_{i}}\vee(1-k_{i})z_{\alpha_{i}}\boldsymbol{y})\right]\big\rvert\pmod{2}.

The expression

\displaystyle\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}(k_{i}P_{\alpha_{i}}\vee(1-k_{i})z_{\alpha_{i}}\boldsymbol{y})\big\rvert\pmod{2}

(99)

is equal to

\displaystyle\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}z_{\alpha_{i}}\boldsymbol{y}\big\rvert\pmod{2}

(100)

in the case where
k_{d+1}=\ldots=k_{1}=0
, which is equal to
0
because Equation
95
is satisfied.
Moreover, in the case where
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d+1}
and the equations
k_{d+1}=\ldots=k_{1}=1
and
k_{d+1}=\ldots=k_{1}=0
are not satisfied, Expression

is equal to 0 if there exists

z_{\alpha_{i}}=0
.
Otherwise it is equal to

\displaystyle\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d}P_{\beta_{i}}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}\big\rvert\pmod{2}

(101)

for some
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d}
satisfying
0\leq\beta_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\beta_{d}<n
.
Expression
101
is equal to Equation
96
and is therefore equal to
0
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}(k_{i}P_{\alpha_{i}}\vee(1-k_{i})z_{\alpha_{i}}\boldsymbol{y})\big\rvert\equiv 0\pmod{2}

(102)

for all
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d+1}
such that
k_{i}=0
for some
i
.
Finally, in the case where
k_{d+1}=\ldots=k_{1}=1
, Expression
99
is equal to

\displaystyle\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}P_{\alpha_{i}}\big\rvert\pmod{2}

(103)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}P^{\prime}_{\alpha_{i}}\big\rvert

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k_{d+1}=0}^{1}\ldots\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{1}\big\lvert\left[\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}(k_{i}P_{\alpha_{i}}\vee(1-k_{i})z_{\alpha_{i}}\boldsymbol{y})\right]\big\rvert\pmod{2}

(104)

\displaystyle\equiv\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}P_{\alpha_{i}}\big\rvert\pmod{2}.

∎

Based on Theorem
8
, we can prove the following subadditivity theorem which is a generalization of Theorem
2
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
d
be a non-negative integer, let
U_{1}\in\mathcal{D}^{C}_{d+1}
, and let
U_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{d+1}
where
\mathcal{D}_{d+1}
is the diagonal subgroup of the
(d+1)
th level of the Clifford hierarchy, and
\mathcal{D}_{d+1}^{C}
is the subgroup of
\mathcal{D}_{d+1}
which can be implemented using only
C^{\otimes d}Z
gates.
If
\tau[U_{1}],\tau[U_{2}]>0
, then
\tau[U_{1}\otimes U_{2}]<\tau[U_{1}]+\tau[U_{2}]
where
\tau[U]
is the optimal number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates required to implement
U
without ancillary qubits over the
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
,
R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
gate set.

Proof.

Let
W=\begin{bmatrix}P&Q\end{bmatrix}
be a parity table such that
P
and
Q
are the parity tables associated with the implementation of
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
and which have
\tau[U_{1}]
and
\tau[U_{2}]
columns respectively.
Then, because
U_{1}\in\mathcal{D}^{C}_{d+1}
,
P
satisfies

\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d}P_{\alpha_{i}}\big\rvert\equiv 0\pmod{2}

(105)

for all
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
satisfying
0\leq\alpha_{1}<\ldots\leq\alpha_{d}<n
where
n
is the number of qubits on which
U_{1}
is acting.
Let
\boldsymbol{z}=P_{:,i}\oplus Q_{:,j}
for any
i
and
j
satisfying
0\leq i<\tau[U_{1}]
,
0\leq j<\tau[U_{2}]
, and let
P^{\prime}
be a parity table such that

P^{\prime}=\begin{cases}P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}&\text{if $\tau[U_{1}]\equiv 0\pmod{2}$},\\
\begin{bmatrix}P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}&\boldsymbol{z}\end{bmatrix}&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Then, as stated by Theorem
8
, the parity table
P^{\prime}
satisfies

\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}P^{\prime}_{\alpha_{i}}\big\rvert\equiv\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}P_{\alpha_{i}}\big\rvert\pmod{2}

(106)

for all
0\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\alpha_{d+1}<n
.
And so the parity table
W^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}P^{\prime}&Q\end{bmatrix}
, which has at most one more column than
W
, also satisfies

\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}W^{\prime}_{\alpha_{i}}\big\rvert\equiv\big\lvert\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d+1}W_{\alpha_{i}}\big\rvert\pmod{2}

(107)

for all
0\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\alpha_{d+1}<n
.
However, we can notice that
P^{\prime}_{:,i}=Q_{:,j}
.
Therefore, by removing these two columns from
W^{\prime}
Equation
107
still holds and
W^{\prime}
has at least one less column than
W
.
The parity table
W^{\prime}
implements the unitary
U_{1}\otimes U_{2}
up to an operator implementable over the
\{\mathrm{CNOT},

R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
gate set and has at most
\tau[U_{1}]+\tau[U_{2}]-1
columns, thus we have
\tau[U_{1}\otimes U_{2}]\leq\tau[U_{1}]+\tau[U_{2}]-1<\tau[U_{1}]+\tau[U_{2}]
.
∎

Based on this subadditivity theorem and on Theorem
8
, we can derive the following upper bound on the number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates in a
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
,
R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
circuit.

Theorem 10
.

Let
d
be a non-negative integer.
The number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates in an
n
-qubit
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
,
R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
circuit can be upper bounded by

2\left\lfloor\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{{n\choose i}}{2}\right\rfloor+1\leq\sum_{i=0}^{d}{n\choose i}

(108)

in polynomial time.

Proof.

Let
U
be a unitary gate implementable over the
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
,
R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
gate set, let
P
be a parity table of size
n\times m
which implements
U
up to an operator implementable over the
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
gate set, and let
L
be a matrix with rows labelled by
(\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{k})
such that

\displaystyle L_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}P_{\alpha_{i}}

(109)

for all
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
satisfying
0\leq\alpha_{i}<\ldots<\alpha_{k}<n
and
k
satisfying
1\leq k\leq d
.
If
P
has strictly more than
\sum_{i=0}^{d}{n\choose i}
columns then we can necessarily find a non-zero vector
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfying
L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\neq\boldsymbol{1}
because
L
has
\sum_{i=1}^{d}{n\choose i}
rows.
Note that such vector
\boldsymbol{y}
necessarily satisfies Equations
96
of Theorem
8
.
We can then divide
P
into two non-empty parity tables
P^{(1)}
and
P^{(2)}
where the column
P_{:,i}
belongs to
P^{(1)}
if and only if
y_{i}=1
and to
P^{(2)}
otherwise.
The parity tables
P^{(1)}
and
P^{(2)}
are implementations of some unitary gates
U_{1}\in\mathcal{D}^{C}_{d+1}
and
U_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{d+1}
respectively.
The subadditivity theorem (Theorem
9
) can then be exploited to reduce the number of columns of
P
.
Let
\boldsymbol{z}=P_{:,i}\oplus P_{:,j}
where
i
and
j
are satisfying
y_{i}=1
and
y_{j}=0
, and let

P^{\prime}=\begin{cases}P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{y}^{T}&\text{if $\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\equiv 0\pmod{2}$},\\
\begin{bmatrix}P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{y}^{T}&\boldsymbol{z}\end{bmatrix}&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

By Theorem
8
,
P^{\prime}
implements the same unitary gate as
P
up to an operator implementable over the
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
gate set.
The parity table
P^{\prime}
has at most one more column than
P
and we have
P^{\prime}_{:,i}=P^{\prime}_{:,j}
.
Therefore, the columns
i
and
j
can be removed from
P^{\prime}
, which entails that
P^{\prime}
has at least one less column than
P
.
We showed that if the number of columns of
P
is strictly greater than
\sum_{i=0}^{d}{n\choose i}
, then the number of columns of
P
can be reduced by at least one in polynomial time.
Moreover, if the number of columns of
P
is equal to
\sum_{i=0}^{d}{n\choose i}
and is even, then we can necessarily find a non-zero vector
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfying
L\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
because
L
has
\sum_{i=1}^{d}{n\choose i}
rows.
If there exist
i
such that
y_{i}=0
then we can reduced the number of columns of
P
as described above.
Otherwise we must have
\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, and so
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfies the Equations of Theorem
8
.
Therefore, if
P^{\prime}=P\oplus\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{y}^{T}
where
\boldsymbol{z}
is equal to the
i
th column of
P
for any
i
, then
P^{\prime}
implements the same unitary gate as
P
up to an operator implementable over the
\{\mathrm{CNOT}
,
R_{Z}(2\pi/2^{d})\}
gate set.
The parity table
P^{\prime}
has the same number of columns as
P
but its
i
th column is equal to the null vector and can therefore be removed, which leads to a parity table containing
\sum_{i=1}^{d}{n\choose i}
columns.

The polynomial-time procedure described above to reduce the number of columns of
P
can be repeated until
P
has a number of columns lower or equal to

2\left\lfloor\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{{n\choose i}}{2}\right\rfloor+1\leq\sum_{i=0}^{d}{n\choose i}.

(110)

∎

The overall complexity of the algorithm described in the proof of Theorem
10
is
\mathcal{O}(n^{2d+1}m)
where
m
is the number of
R_{Z}(\pi/2^{d})
gates in the initial circuit.
For

, the algorithm yields a parity table which contains at most one column, which is optimal.
For
d=1
, the algorithm corresponds to Lempel’s matrix factorization algorithm
[

]
, which is optimal.
For
d=2
, the algorithm is similar to the
TOHPE
algorithm (Algorithm

).
Note that the
TOHPE
algorithm has a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}m^{3})
instead of
\mathcal{O}(n^{5}m)
because of the heuristic used by the algorithm to better optimize the number of
T
gates.
The same heuristic can be used in the case where
d>2
, which would lead to an algorithm having a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{d}m^{3})
.

7
Conclusion

We presented several polynomial-time algorithms for reducing the number of
T
gates in a Clifford
+T
circuit.
Benchmarks show that our algorithms consistently achieve the lowest execution times and provide the best
T
-count reduction on almost all the quantum circuits evaluated when compared to state-of-the-art
T
-count optimizers.
As such, our algorithms not only achieve state-of-the-art
T
-count reduction but also offer much greater scalability, thereby allowing efficient
T
-count optimization on larger quantum circuits.

We proved that our algorithms are producing a circuit in which the number of
T
gates is upper bounded by
(n^{2}+n)/2+1
when they are executed on a Hadamard-free circuit, where
n
is the number of qubits.
It has been shown that there exists an asymptotic upper bound of
n^{2}/2-1

[
66
]
.
The question of whether or not there exists a polynomial-time algorithm satisfying this asymptotic upper bound remains open.

We also demonstrated how the number of
T
gates in a Clifford
+T
circuit can be optimized such that it is lower or equal to
(n+1)(n+2h)/2+1
where
n
is the number of qubits and
h
is the number of internal Hadamard gates in the circuit, without using any ancillary qubits and in polynomial time.
This reinforces the interdependence between the problems of optimizing the number of internal Hadamard gates and optimizing the number of
T
gates.
As part of future work, it would be beneficial to more clearly determine the roles that internal Hadamard gates and ancillary qubits have in the
T
-count optimization problem.
