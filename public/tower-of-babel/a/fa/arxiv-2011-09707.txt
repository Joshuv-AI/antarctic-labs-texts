Application of Deep Learning-based Interpolation Methods to Nearshore Bathymetry
Yizhou Qian, Mojtaba Forghani, Jonghyun Lee, Matthew Farthing, Tyler Hesser, Peter K. Kitanidis, Eric F. Darve

Abstract
Nearshore bathymetry, the topography of the ocean floor in coastal zones, is vital for predicting the surf zone hydrodynamics and for route planning to avoid subsurface features. Hence, it is increasingly important for a wide variety of applications, including shipping operations, coastal management, and risk assessment. However, direct high resolution surveys of nearshore bathymetry are rarely performed due to budget constraints and logistical restrictions. Another option when only sparse observations are available is to use Gaussian Process regression (GPR), also called Kriging. But GPR has difficulties recognizing patterns with sharp gradients, like those found around sand bars and submerged objects, especially when observations are sparse. In this work, we present several deep learning-based techniques to estimate nearshore bathymetry with sparse, multi-scale measurements. We propose a Deep Neural Network (DNN) to compute posterior estimates of the nearshore bathymetry, as well as a conditional Generative Adversarial Network (cGAN) that samples from the posterior distribution. We train our neural networks based on synthetic data generated from nearshore surveys provided by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineer Field Research Facility (FRF) in Duck, North Carolina. We compare our methods with Kriging on real surveys as well as surveys with artificially added sharp gradients. Results show that direct estimation by DNN gives better predictions than Kriging in this application. We use bootstrapping with DNN for uncertainty quantification. We also propose a method, named DNN-Kriging, that combines deep learning with Kriging and shows further improvement of the posterior estimates.

2
Contribution

In this work, we propose the use of deep learning techniques within a Bayesian framework that provides uncertainty quantification. We apply it to the interpolation problem of predicting nearshore bathymetry, given sparse point-wise measurements and grid cell average measurements. Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We propose two deep learning-based approaches that can learn highly non-linear and complex distributions automatically through its training data.

–

We trained a conditional Generative Adversarial Network (cGAN) to learn the posterior distribution of nearshore bathymetry. This allows us to sample directly from the posterior distribution and compute different posterior estimates including mean and standard deviation.

–

We trained a fully connected DNN to estimate the posterior mean. Uncertainty quantification is provided by combining our DNN model with bootstrapping in Kriging. This approach is more computationally feasible than cGAN in terms of both training time and optimization of hyper-parameters.

•

We propose a method (DNN-Kriging) that uses Kriging to reduce the error in the DNN’s prediction of the posterior mean. The motivation for this approach is that the DNN is capable of learning fine-scale features, while Kriging can accurately capture smooth components in the error. Here by “fine-scale” features we mean features that usually involve rapid oscillations or large values in the derivatives.

We compare our methods with Kriging and an interpolation that uses a total variation prior (TV). The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
3.1
briefly introduces the Bayesian framework,
3.2
discusses how conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (cGAN) samples from the posterior distribution, and
3.3
describes how Deep Neural Networks (DNN) computes the posterior mean and generates posterior samples implicitly. Section
3.4
proposes DNN-Kriging. Section
5
shows numerical benchmarks for Kriging, DNN, cGAN, TV and DNN-Kriging, on smooth solutions as well as solutions with sharp gradients. Section
4
discusses related work on deep learning-based methods using a Bayesian framework as well as methods that combine DNN and Kriging.

3
Methodology

3.1
Bayesian Inference

In this paper, we consider the problem of finding the unknown data
{\bf x}
of the following inverse problem:

{\bf y}=H{\bf x}+{\bf v}

(1)

where
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is our data,
{\bf y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is our measurement,
{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the noise in our measurements and
H
is a linear forward map. In the framework of Bayesian inference,
{\bf x}
,
{\bf y}
, and
{\bf v}
are considered as realizations from random variables
{\bf X}
,
{\bf Y}
, and
{\bf V}
. The unknown data
{\bf X}
is considered to have certain prior distribution
p_{{\bf X}}^{\text{prior}}({\bf x})
, representing what we know about the unknown data before the measurements. The noise
{\bf v}
follows the distribution
p_{{\bf V}}({\bf v})
. Given the measurement
{\bf y}
, we can then “update” the probabilities of the unknown
{\bf X}
from the “prior” distribution to a “posterior” distribution using the Bayes formula
[
Bolstad and
Curran
]
:

p^{\text{post}}_{{\bf X}}({\bf x})\propto p_{{\bf V}}({\bf y}-H{\bf x})\;p^{\text{prior}}_{{\bf X}}({\bf x})

(2)

The posterior distribution
p^{\text{post}}_{{\bf X}}
not only allows us to find the “most likely” estimate
\hat{x}
in the sense that it maximizes the posterior likelihood, but also provides uncertainty quantification that characterizes how confident we are about our prediction. Leveraging deep learning-based methods’ ability to learn highly complex and non-linear mappings, we propose using conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (cGAN) to sample from
p^{\text{post}}_{{\bf X}}
. We also propose using DNN to estimate the posterior mean
\mathbb{E}({\bf X}|{\bf Y}={\bf y})
, which can be used to generate posterior samples as well through bootstrapping.

3.2
Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)

Generative Adversarial Networks are a class of deep generative models that consists of two types of neural networks
[
Goodfellow et al.
]
: a generator
G
and a discriminator
D
. The generator
G
takes some noise vector
{\bf z}
, which is usually from some normal distribution
p_{{\bf Z}}({\bf z})
, as input to generate fake samples, and the discriminator takes samples as input and tries to classify them as “real” or “fake”. “Real” means that the sample is indeed from the target distribution, and vice versa. The networks are trained in an adversarial manner: the generator
G
tries to generate as realistic samples as possible to fool the discriminator
D
, while
D
tries to accurately distinguish between “real” samples and “fake” samples generated by
G
. Formally, let
{\bf x}
represent the bathymetric samples with some prior distribution
p_{{\bf X}}({\bf x})
, then the objective function of GAN will be:

\begin{split}\min_{G}\max_{D}\ &\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\thicksim p_{{\bf X}}({\bf x})}(\log D({\bf x}))\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{{\bf z}\thicksim p_{{\bf Z}}({\bf z})}(1-\log D(G({\bf z})))\end{split}

(3)

Conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (cGANs) are an extension of GANs
[
Mirza and
Osindero
]
, in which an extra label
{\bf y}
, which represents indirect observations, is passed as input to both the generator
G
and the discriminator
D
. The two networks are trained alternatively using the output of each other, and upon convergence the generator will generate samples consistent with observations. In this work, two different data types are used for labels
{\bf y}
in our cGAN: 1) point-wise sparse measurements and 2) averages over each grid cell. The objective function of cGAN then becomes:

\begin{split}\min_{G}\max_{D}\ &\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\thicksim p_{{\bf X}}({\bf x})}(\log D({\bf x}|{\bf y}))\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{{\bf z}\thicksim p_{{\bf Z}}({\bf z})}(1-\log D(G({\bf z}|{\bf y})))\end{split}

(4)

Despite GAN’s power to directly learn the posterior distribution, it is known that the training process of GAN is, in general, very challenging and requires a significant amount of efforts to tune hyper-parameters.

3.3
Deep Neural Networks (DNN)

Instead of using cGAN, we also consider combining DNN with bootstrapping to sample from the posterior distribution. Our DNN approach relies on the following key mathematical fact. Let
\tau^{*}({\bf y})=\mathbb{E}({\bf X}|{\bf Y}={\bf y})
, then
\tau^{*}
is the solution to the following minimization problem:

\tau^{*}\in\text{argmin}_{\tau:\,\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}(\|{\bf X}-\tau({\bf Y})\|^{2})}

(5)

where the minimization is over all measurable functions from
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Empirically, with training data
({\bf y}_{i},{\bf x}_{i})
that are pairs of realizations of random variable
({\bf Y},{\bf X})
, we may replace the expectation with the average over training data and solve the following minimization problem:

\min_{\tau:\,\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\|{\bf x}_{i}-\tau({\bf y}_{i})\|^{2}}

(6)

Given DNNs’ ability to approximate highly complex functions, it is then natural to optimize over a family of neural networks with trainable parameter
\theta
:

\min_{\theta}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\|{\bf x}_{i}-\tau_{\theta}({\bf y}_{i})\|^{2}}

(7)

Consequently, assuming a high capacity for our DNN and sufficient training data, the optimal neural network
\tau_{\theta^{*}}
is an estimator for the posterior mean:

\tau_{\theta^{*}}({\bf y})\approx\mathbb{E}({\bf X}|{\bf Y}={\bf y})

(8)

We also combine DNN’s posterior mean estimator
\tau_{\theta^{*}}
with bootstrapping from Kriging to generate conditional realizations. To provide motivation, we first briefly review the use of Kriging (or linear Gaussian regression in our case), which assumes a Gaussian prior with mean
{\bf\mu}
and covariance
Q
for
{\bf X}
, as well as a Gaussian noise with zero mean and covariance
R
for
{\bf V}
:

{\bf X}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf\mu},Q),\;{\bf V}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)

(9)

This implies that our posterior is also a Gaussian distribution for a linear map
H
:

\begin{split}p^{\text{post}}_{{\bf X}}(x)\propto\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}({\bf y}-H{\bf x})^{T}R^{-1}({\bf y}-H{\bf x})\\
-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x}-{\bf\mu})^{T}Q^{-1}({\bf x}-{\bf\mu})\Big)\end{split}

(10)

Since the forward map
H
is linear, we can derive the posterior mean in closed form:

{\bf\mu}_{{\bf x}|{\bf y}}=\mathbb{E}({\bf X}|{\bf Y}={\bf y})={\bf\mu}+\Lambda({\bf y}-H{\bf\mu})

(11)

where

\Lambda=(Q^{-1}+H^{T}R^{-1}H)^{-1}H^{T}R^{-1}

(12)

Uncertainty quantification is represented by the posterior covariance matrix:

Q_{{\bf x}|{\bf y}}=(Q^{-1}+H^{T}R^{-1}H)^{-1}

(13)

The quantity
{\bf\mu}_{{\bf x}|{\bf y}}
and
Q_{{\bf x}|{\bf y}}
completely characterize the Gaussian distribution
p^{\text{post}}_{{\bf X}}({\bf x})
. To obtain uncertainty quantification, we can perform a Cholesky factorization
\Lambda=LL^{T}
, sample
u_{i}
from standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and compute conditional realizations
{\bf x}_{ci}=Lu_{i}
. However, sometimes it may be computationally expensive to perform Cholesky decomposition when
n
is large. Instead, it is easier to generate conditional realizations
{\bf x}_{ci}
implicitly by bootstrapping
[
Kitanidis
]
:

{\bf x}_{ci}={\bf x}_{ui}+\Lambda({\bf y}+{\bf v}_{i}-H{\bf x}_{ui})

(14)

where
{\bf x}_{ui}
is an unconditional realization of
{\bf X}
, and
{\bf v}_{i}
is a realization of
{\bf v}
. Eq. (
14
) looks very similar to Eq. (
11
), in the sense that
{\bf x}_{ui}
can be viewed as a perturbed prior mean, and
{\bf y}+{\bf v}_{i}
can be viewed as a perturbed measurement.

We note here that Eq. (
14
) can also be written as follows using Eq. (
11
):

\begin{split}{\bf x}_{ci}&={\bf x}_{ui}-{\bf\mu}+{\bf\mu}+\Lambda({\bf y}+{\bf v}_{i}-H({\bf x}_{ui}-{\bf\mu})-H{\bf\mu})\\
&={\bf x}_{ui}-{\bf\mu}+\mathbb{E}({\bf X}|{\bf Y}={\bf y}+{\bf v}_{i}-H({\bf x}_{ui}-{\bf\mu}))\end{split}

(15)

With our optimal DNN
\tau_{\theta^{*}}({\bf y})
as an estimator for the posterior mean
\mathbb{E}({\bf X}|{\bf Y}={\bf y})
, we can then generate conditional realizations as follows:

{\bf x}_{ci}={\bf x}_{ui}-{\bf\mu}+\tau_{\theta^{*}}({\bf y}+{\bf v}_{i}-H({\bf x}_{ui}-{\bf\mu}))

(16)

The main advantage of this approach is its computational feasibility. A simple fully connected neural network with two hidden layers can be trained reasonably well to directly estimate the posterior mean. Hence, the total time required for training will be much less than that of GAN. It provides an effective confidence interval with minimal effort of tuning the hyper-parameters and network structures, which is also a significant cost for training GAN.

3.4
DNN-Kriging

We also propose a method called DNN-Kriging, which is shown in Fig.
1
. The algorithm is a two-step process. In the first step, we use the training data to train our DNN model and make a prediction
\tau_{\theta^{*}}({\bf y})
that preserves sharp gradients. Next we replace
\mu
with
\tau_{\theta^{*}}({\bf y})
in Eq. (
11
) to make our final prediction:

{\bf x}_{\text{DNN-Kriging}}=\tau_{\theta^{*}}({\bf y})+\Lambda({\bf y}-H\tau_{\theta^{*}}({\bf y}))

(17)

where
\Lambda
is defined in Eq. (
12
). We can generate conditional realizations through the posterior covariance matrix
Q_{{\bf x}|{\bf y}}
. The motivation for this is that DNN is capable of preserving fine-scale features, while Kriging excels at capturing smooth variations of data. Hence, assuming that the error between the reference profile
{\bf x}
and
\tau_{\theta^{*}}({\bf y})
can be characterized by Gaussian priors, we use Kriging to improve DNN’s estimates. Our results show DNN-Kriging can provide better estimates than DNN or Kriging used separately, as it combines the advantage of both methods.

4.2
Combining DNN and Kriging

Most previous works on combining methods of Kriging and DNNs were established based on the idea of Neural Network Residual Kriging (NNRK), which is a hybrid model that combines DNN with Kriging-based methods. In NNRK, DNN is used to estimate the general spatial trend of data, whereas Kriging is used to estimate the residuals of the DNN’s estimate. It has been applied to study a wide range of environmental data and shows improvement over DNN and Kriging

,

]

]
.

. Our method DNN-Kriging is an extension of NNRK to a general Bayesian formulation.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have explored the use of deep learning techniques from a Bayesian perspective to estimate nearshore bathymetry with sparse point-wise measurements and grid cell average measurements. We proposed using conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (cGAN) as a purely data-driven approach to directly sample the posterior distribution. This usually has a challenging training process and requires extra effort for tuning of hyper-parameters. We also proposed using a fully connected DNN to directly estimate the posterior mean of the bathymetry, which can be combined with bootstrapping from Kriging to provide uncertainty quantification. Both approaches provide more accurate predictions than Kriging when sharp changes are present in nearshore surveys. Finally, we proposed a method named DNN-Kriging that combines Kriging’s ability to model smooth variations in the residuals with DNN’s ability to capture fine-scale features. Results show that DNN-Kriging provides the best estimate among all the methods.
