Scaling laws for velocity profile of granular flow in rotating drums
Hiroki Oba, Michio Otsuki

Abstract
We numerically analyze the steady flow of two-dimensional granular materials in a rotating drum using the discrete element method and a continuum model. The velocity fields obtained from both methods are quantitatively consistent. Two distinct regions exist in the granular flow: the surface flow layer and the static flow regime associated with the rigid rotation near the bottom. The thickness of the surface flow layer increases with the drum diameter and shows a slight dependence on the angular velocity of the rotating drum. We derived scaling laws for the velocity profile and surface layer thickness using dimensional analysis applied to the continuum equations. The validity of the scaling laws is confirmed numerically.



I
Introduction

Flows in rotating drums are used in various industrial processes of granular materials, including mixing, breaking, granulating, drying, and chemical reactions.
The form of the flow inside the drum depends on the Froude number
Fr=\Omega^{2}D/2g

(
Mellmann 2001
)
, where
\Omega
is the angular velocity of the rotation,
D
is the diameter of the drum, and
g
is the gravitational acceleration.
For
10^{-4}<Fr<10^{-2}
, a steady flow driven by the rotating drum occurs, as shown in Fig.
1
.
In this steady state, the flow field comprises two regions: the “surface flow layer” and the “static flow regime.”
In the surface flow layer, the granular material flows down along the slope of the free surface.
In the static flow regime, the granular material exhibits a solid-like rigid rotation following the drum wall.

Numerous experimental studies have investigated the dependence of the surface flow layer thickness
h
on
D
and
\Omega
in drums half-filled with granular materials
(
Parker et al. 1997
;
Khakhar et al. 1997
;
Jain et al. 2004
;
Félix et al. 2007
;
Orpe and Khakhar 2007
)
.
In. Ref.
Parker et al. 1997
, it is reported that
h
is proportional to
D
and almost independent of
\Omega
in a drum long enough in the axial direction.
According to Ref.
Félix et al. 2007
,
h
in axially thin rotating drums exhibits a power-law dependence on
\Omega
for sufficiently large
D/d
with the particle diameter
d
.
The experiments in Ref.
Pignatel et al. 2012
suggested that
h
in thin drums obeys
h/d\propto\tilde{Q}^{0.44}
with the dimensionless flow rate
\tilde{Q}=\frac{1}{2}Fr^{1/2}\left(D/d\right)^{3/2}
.
However, the dependence of
h
has not been explained theoretically.

\end{overpic}

Numerical simulations have been conducted to analyze the granular flow in rotating drums.
Several researchers have used the discrete element method (DEM)
(
Yamane et al. 1998
;
Dury et al. 1998
;
Renouf et al. 2005
;
Yang et al. 2003
;
Yang et al. 2008
)
, where the equation of motion for each particle is solved numerically.
However, the numerical cost for the DEM simulation increases with the number of particles in the system with
D\gg d
.
Therefore, direct simulations of the systems used in industrial applications with a large number of particles are challenging.
In recent years, simulation methods based on continuum equations have been proposed, which are free from the restriction on the number of particles.
In Refs.
Zheng and Yu 2015
;
Zheng et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2018
, granular materials are treated as elastoplastic bodies to investigate their mixing driven by rotating drums.
In Refs.
Arseni et al. 2020
;
Santos et al. 2013
, the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) method was used to study the flows in rotating drums, where granular materials are treated as non-Newtonian viscous fluids.
It has been reported that the velocity field in the rotating drums is reproduced by these continuum approaches; however, its dependence on
D
and
\Omega
has not been studied in detail.

Various constitutive relations have been utilized in these continuum approaches.
Among the constitutive relations, the
\mu(I)
-rheology has been applied to various granular flows because of its simplicity
(
Da Cruz et al. 2005
;
Jop et al. 2006
)
.
In the
\mu(I)
-rheology, the shear stress
\tau
of granular materials under uniform shear with the shear rate
\dot{\gamma}
and pressure
p
is given by

\tau=\mu(I)p.

(1)

Here,
\mu(I)
is the bulk friction depending on the inertial number
I=\dot{\gamma}/\sqrt{p/\rho_{s}d^{2}}
, where
\rho_{s}
is the density of the particles comprising the granular materials.
In Ref.
Jop et al. 2006
, a functional form of
\mu(I)
is proposed as follows:

\mu\left(I\right)=\mu_{s}+\frac{\mu_{2}-\mu_{s}}{I_{0}/I+1},

(2)

where

\mu_{s}
,
\mu_{2}
, and
I_{0}
are constants depending on the elasticity, dissipation, and friction of the particles
(
Da Cruz et al. 2005
)
.
The continuum model using the
\mu(I)
-rheology treats granular materials as non-Newtonian fluids with the viscosity
\eta=\mu(I)p/\dot{\gamma}
.
Recently, the constitutive relations incorporating non-local effects in the
\mu(I)
-rheology have been proposed
(
Kamrin and Koval 2012
;
Heyman et al. 2017
;
Kim and Kamrin 2020
)
.
The continuum models using the
\mu(I)
-rheology reproduce various granular flows, including those in rotating drums

(
MiDi 2004
;
Zheng and Yu 2015
;
Zheng et al. 2019
;
Arseni et al. 2020
)
.
Therefore, this constitutive law is expected to explain the dependence of the velocity profile on
D
and
\Omega
.

This study aims to understand the steady flow of granular materials in rotating drums.
We focus on 2D rotating drums half-filled with granular materials for
D/d\gg 1
and obtain the velocity field numerically by DEM and CFD simulations.
The details of the numerical simulations are described in Sec.
II
.
The dependence of the velocity field on
D
and
\Omega
is shown in Sec.
III
, where we demonstrate that the continuum model quantitatively reproduces the flow obtained in the DEM simulation over a broad parameter space.
In Sec.
IV
, scaling laws characterizing the velocity field are derived analytically from the continuum model using the
\mu(I)
-rheology and verified numerically.
The method for calculating the velocity field in the DEM simulation is presented in Appendix
A
.
The values of the parameters
\mu_{s}
,
\mu_{2}
, and
I_{0}
for the
\mu(I)
-rheology are estimated in Appendix
B
.
The packing fraction of granular materials in rotating drums is demonstrated in Appendix
C
.
The position of the free surface is discussed in Appendix
D
.
The details of the derivation for the analytical expression of
h
are shown in Appendix
E
.

II
Numerical Method

II.1
Setup

In this paper, we consider a two-dimensional granular flow in a rotating drum, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.
The origin of the Cartesian coordinate system
(X,Z)
is placed at the center of the drum with diameter
D
.
The polydisperse particles with the minimum diameter
d
are half-filled in the drum.
The drum is rotated counterclockwise with the angular velocity
\Omega
.
The drum diameter
D
and angular velocity
\Omega
are set to
D=150d
,
300d
, and
450d
and
1.26\times 10^{-3}\leq\Omega/\sqrt{g/d}\leq 1.26\times 10^{-2}
to satisfy
10^{-4}<Fr<10^{-2}
, respectively.

II.2
DEM simulation

II.2.1
Time evolution equations

In the DEM simulation
(
Cundall and Strack 1979
;
Shäfer et al. 1996
;
Campbell 2002
)
, we consider
N
particles in a drum.
The dynamics of particle
i
is given by the time evolution equations for the position
{\bf\it r}_{i}=(X_{i},Z_{i})
and angular velocity
\omega_{i}
of particle
i
as follows:

\displaystyle m_{i}\frac{d^{2}{\bf\it r}_{i}}{dt^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}{\bf\it F}_{ij}+m_{i}{\bf\it g},

(3)

\displaystyle I^{\rm p}_{i}\frac{d\omega_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}T_{ij}

(4)

with
{\bf\it g}=(0,-g)
.
Here,
m_{i}
and
I^{\rm p}_{i}
are the mass and moment of inertia of particle
i
, respectively.
The contact force and torque between particle
i
and particle
j
are denoted by
{\bf\it F}_{ij}
and
T_{ij}
, respectively.

The contact force
{\bf\it F}_{ij}
consists of the normal component
{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}
and the tangential component
{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}
as follows:

{\bf\it F}_{ij}={\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}+{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}.

(5)

The normal contact force
{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}
is given by

{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}=\left(-k^{(\mathrm{n})}{\bf\it\delta}^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}-\eta^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}{\bf\it v}^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}\right)\Theta\left(\delta_{ij}\right),

(6)

where
k^{(\mathrm{n})}
and
\eta^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}
are the normal elastic and the normal viscous constants, respectively;
here,
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function satisfying by
\Theta(x)=1
for
x\geq 0
, and
\Theta(x)=0
for otherwise.
The normal displacement
{\bf\it\delta}^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}
, the normal relative velocity
{\bf\it v}^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}
, and the normal unit vector
{\bf\it n}_{ij}
in Eq. (
6
) are respectively given by

\displaystyle{\bf\it\delta}^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}=\delta_{ij}{\bf\it n}_{ij},

(7)

\displaystyle{\bf\it v}^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}=\left\{({\bf\it v}_{i}-{\bf\it v}_{j})\cdot{\bf\it n}_{ij}\right\}{\bf\it n}_{ij},

(8)

\displaystyle{\bf\it n}_{ij}=\frac{{\bf\it r}_{j}-{\bf\it r}_{i}}{\left|{\bf\it r}_{j}-{\bf\it r}_{i}\right|},

(9)

where
{\bf\it v}_{i}
is the velocity of particle
i
, and
\delta_{ij}
is the overlap between particles
i
and
j
given by

\delta_{ij}=\frac{d_{i}+d_{j}}{2}-\left|{\bf\it r}_{j}-{\bf\it r}_{i}\right|

(10)

with the diameter
d_{i}
of particle
i
.

The tangential contact force
{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}
obeys the Coulomb friction law as follows:

{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}=\mathrm{min}\left(\left|{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{t,st})}_{ij}\right|,\;\mu_{\mathrm{p}}\left|{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}\right|\right)\frac{{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{t,st})}_{ij}}{\left|{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{t,st})}_{ij}\right|}\Theta\left(\delta_{ij}\right)

(11)

with the particle friction coefficient
\mu_{\mathrm{p}}
.
Here,
\mathrm{min}(a,b)
in Eq. (
11
) selects the smaller of
a
and
b
, and

{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{t,st})}_{ij}
is given by

{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{t,st})}_{ij}=-k^{(\mathrm{t})}\delta^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}{\bf\it t}_{ij}-\eta^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}{\bf\it v}^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}

(12)

with the tangential spring constant
k^{(\mathrm{t})}
and the tangential viscous constant
\eta^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}
.
The tangential displacement
{\delta}^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}
, the tangential relative velocity
{\bf\it v}^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}
, and the tangential unit vector
{\bf\it t}_{ij}
are given by

\displaystyle\delta^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}=\int_{\mathrm{stick}}dt\ {\bf\it v}^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}\cdot{\bf\it t}_{ij},

(13)

\displaystyle{\bf\it v}^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}=\left\{\left({\bf\it v}_{i}-{\bf\it v}_{j}\right)\cdot{\bf\it t}_{ij}+\frac{1}{2}\left(d_{i}\omega_{i}+d_{j}\omega_{j}\right)\right\}{\bf\it t}_{ij},

(14)

\displaystyle{\bf\it t}_{ij}=\left(-n_{ij,Z},\;n_{ij,X}\right).

(15)

The subscript “
\mathrm{stick}
” in Eq. (
13
) indicates that the integral is performed only when
\left|{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{t,st})}_{ij}\right|<\mu_{\mathrm{p}}\left|{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}\right|
.
The torque
T_{ij}
is given by

T_{ij}=\frac{d_{i}}{2}{\bf\it F}^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}\cdot{\bf\it t}_{ij}.

(16)

II.2.2
Numerical conditions

The diameters of the particles range uniformly from
d
to
1.2d

(
Renouf et al. 2005
)
.
The mass and moment of inertia of particle
i
are given by
m_{i}=\frac{1}{4}\pi\rho_{s}d_{i}^{2}
and
I^{p}_{i}=\frac{1}{8}m_{i}{d_{i}}^{2}
, respectively.
We set
k^{(\mathrm{n})}=2.0\times 10^{5}mg/d
,
k^{(\mathrm{t})}/k^{(\mathrm{n})}=2/7
, and
\mu_{p}=0.50
, where
m
is the mass of the particle with diameter
d

(
Silbert et al. 2001
)
.
The normal viscous constant between particles
i
and
j
is given by

\displaystyle\eta^{(\mathrm{n})}_{ij}=\eta^{(\mathrm{t})}_{ij}=-2\ln{e}\sqrt{\frac{m^{*}_{ij}k^{(\mathrm{n})}}{\pi^{2}+(\ln{e})^{2}}}

(17)

with the coefficient of restitution
e=0.92
and

\displaystyle m^{*}_{ij}=\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{m_{i}+m_{j}}.

(18)

The time evolution equations are numerically solved using the leapfrog algorithm with the time step
\Delta t=1.0\times 10^{-4}\sqrt{d/g}
.

The rough wall surface of the drum is modeled by placing
N_{\rm w}
particles of diameter
d
equally spaced on the circle of diameter
D
to avoid slip at the surface.
The particles constituting the rough wall are rotated around the circle with the angular velocity
\Omega
.
We set
N_{\rm w}=475
,
632
, and
946
for the drums with
D=150d,300d
, and
D=450d
, respectively.
The drum is half-filled with
N
particles, where
N
are set to

7700
,
30600
, and
69200
for
D=150d,300d
, and
450d
, respectively.
Through this numerical simulation, we calculate the velocity distribution in the steady state using the procedure shown in Appendix
A
.

II.3
CFD simulation

In the CFD simulation, we treat the granular bed in the rotating drum as an incompressible non-Newtonian fluid.
The continuum equations for the granular material are numerically solved using the finite difference method with the time step
\Delta t
and grid width
\Delta x
.
The free space in the drum is considered the gas phase to treat the free surface of the granular bed.
The time evolution of the two-phase flow with the granular and gas phases is numerically solved by the volume of fluid (VoF) method
(
Hirt and Nichols 1981
;
Gueyffier et al. 1999
;
Li et al. 2000
)
.
This CFD simulation is an extension of the method implemented in Ref.
Watanabe and Goto 2022
for an incompressible Newtonian fluid in a rotating container.

II.3.1
Governing equations

The entire domain
\mathrm{V}
of the system is expressed as
\mathrm{V}=\mathrm{V_{f}}\cup\mathrm{V_{s}}
with the fluid domain
\mathrm{V_{f}}
including the granular and gas phases and the solid domain
\mathrm{V_{s}}
representing the drum wall.
The mass conservation equation is given by

\nabla\cdot{\bf\it v}=0\;\;\;\;\;\mathrm{in}\;\;\;{\bf\it r}\in\mathrm{V},

(19)

while the momentum conservation equation in the fluid domain is given by

\frac{\partial{\bf\it v}}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot\left({\bf\it v}{\bf\it v}\right)-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p+\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla\cdot{\bf\it\tau}+{\bf\it g}\;\;\;\;\;\mathrm{in}\;\;\;{\bf\it r}\in\mathrm{V_{f}},

(20)

where
\rho
,
{\bf\it v}
, and
p
are the density, velocity, and pressure at
{\bf\it r}=(X,Z)
and time
t
, respectively.
Here,
{\bf\it\tau}
is given by

{\bf\it\tau}=\eta{\bf\it\dot{\gamma}}

(21)

with the viscosity
\eta
and shear rate tensor
{\bf\it\dot{\gamma}}=\nabla{\bf\it v}+(\nabla{\bf\it v})^{T}
.
Notably,
\eta
depends on the position in the fluid domain.
The velocity vector in the solid domain is given by

{\bf\it v}={\bf\it v}_{\mathrm{wall}}\;\;\;\;\;\mathrm{in}\;\;\;{\bf\it r}\in\mathrm{V_{s}},

(22)

with

{\bf\it v}_{\mathrm{wall}}=-\Omega Z{\bf\it e}_{X}+\Omega X{\bf\it e}_{Z}.

(23)

II.3.2
Volume of fluid method

In the VoF method, we introduce the phase volume fraction
\alpha({\bf\it r},t)
of the fluid phase.
Here,
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=0
correspond to the areas occupied only by the granular and gas phases, respectively.
In the interface between the granular and gas phases,
\alpha
satisfies
0<\alpha<1
.
We consider the position with
\alpha=0.5
as the free surface of the granular bed.
The phase volume fraction
\alpha({\bf\it r},t)
obeys the advection equation

\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\alpha{\bf\it v}\right)-\alpha\nabla\cdot{\bf\it v}=0.

(24)

This equation is solved using the WLIC/THINC (weighed line interface calculation/tangent of hyperbola for interface capturing) method
(
Yokoi 2007
)
.
The density
\rho
and viscosity
\eta
are calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\rho_{\mathrm{g}}\alpha+\rho_{\mathrm{air}}\left(1-\alpha\right),

(25)

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\eta_{\mathrm{g}}\left(\dot{\gamma},p\right)\alpha+\eta_{\mathrm{air}}\left(1-\alpha\right).

(26)

Here,
\rho_{\mathrm{air}}
and
\eta_{\mathrm{air}}
are constants representing the density and viscosity in the gas phase, respectively.
The density
\rho_{\mathrm{g}}
in the granular phase is treated as a constant.
The viscosity
\eta_{\mathrm{g}}
in the granular phase is given by

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathrm{g}}\left(\dot{\gamma},p\right)=\frac{\mu(I)p}{\dot{\gamma}}

(27)

in the
\mu(I)
-rheology
(
Jop et al. 2006
)
.
We adopt the functional form of
\mu(I)
as Eq. (
2
).
The inertial number
I
is given by
I=\dot{\gamma}/\sqrt{p/\rho_{s}d^{2}}
with

\dot{\gamma}=\left(\frac{1}{2}{\bf\it\dot{\gamma}}:{\bf\it\dot{\gamma}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(28)

which corresponds to the second invariant of
{\bf\it\dot{\gamma}}
.
At constant pressure
p
, the
\mu(I)
-rheology corresponds to the Bingham model.

The viscosity in Eq. (
27
) diverges in the limit of
\dot{\gamma}\to 0
.
To prevent the divergence in the CFD simulation, we regularize Eq. (
27
) by approximating it with a cutoff
\eta_{\mathrm{g,max}}

(
Arseni et al. 2020
)
as follows:

\eta_{\mathrm{g}}=\mathrm{min}\left[\frac{\mu(I)p}{\dot{\gamma}},\>\eta_{\mathrm{g,max}}\right].

(29)

The rigid rotation in the static flow regime is effectively realized using a sufficiently large value for
\eta_{\mathrm{g,max}}
.

II.3.3
Simplified marker and cell method

In this study, we use the simplified marker and cell (SMAC) method
(
Amsden and Harlow 1970
)
to solve the time evolution equations in the fluid domain.
We denote an arbitrary physical quantity
\mathbf{a}
at the time
t=n\Delta t
by
\mathbf{a}^{n}
with the time step
\Delta t
and an integer
n
.
In the SMAC method,
\mathbf{a}^{(n+1)}
at the time
t=(n+1)\Delta t
is numerically obtained from
\mathbf{a}^{(n)}
in two steps.

First, for the prediction step, the predicted velocity
\mathbf{v}^{*}
in
{\bf\it r}\in\mathrm{V_{f}}
is determined by

\frac{{\bf\it v}^{*}-{\bf\it v}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\frac{3{\bf\it A}^{n}-{\bf\it A}^{n-1}}{2}-\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\nabla S^{n}+\frac{{\bf\it B}^{*}+{\bf\it B}^{n}}{2}+{\bf\it g},

(30)

Here, we have used the second-order Adams–Bashforth method for the advection term

\displaystyle{\bf\it A}=-\nabla\cdot\left({\bf\it vv}\right),

(31)

while the second-order Crank–Nicolson method is used for the viscous term

\displaystyle{\bf\it B}=\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla\cdot\left[\eta\left(\nabla{\bf\it v}+(\nabla{\bf\it v})^{T}\right)\right].

(32)

In the SMAC method, a scholar potential
S
is introduced in Eq. (
30
) to satisfy the mass conservation.
In Eq. (
30
),
{\bf\it B}^{*}
is given by

{\bf\it B}^{*}=\nu_{0}\nabla^{2}{\bf\it v}^{*}+\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\nabla\cdot\left[\eta^{n}\left(\nabla{\bf\it v}^{n}+(\nabla{{\bf\it v}^{n}})^{T}\right)\right]-\nu_{0}\nabla^{2}{\bf\it v}^{n},

(33)

where the first term is treated implicitly, and the other terms are treated explicitly
(
Dodd and Ferrante 2014
)
.
Here,
\nu_{0}
is the constant component of kinematic viscosity given by

\nu_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{air}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{air}}}+\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{g,max}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{g}}}\right).

(34)

For
{\bf\it r}\in\mathrm{V_{s}}
, the predicted velocity
\mathbf{v}^{*}
is given by

{\bf\it v}^{*}={\bf\it v}_{\mathrm{wall}}.

(35)

Next, for the projection step,
{\bf\it v}^{\mathrm{n+1}}
in
{\bf\it r}\in\mathrm{V_{f}}
is obtained as follows:

{\bf\it v}^{\mathrm{n+1}}={\bf\it v}^{*}-\frac{\Delta t}{\rho^{n}}\nabla\sigma.

(36)

Here,
\sigma
is given by the solution of the Poisson equation

\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\nabla\sigma\right)=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\nabla\cdot{\bf\it v}^{*}

(37)

with the boundary condition
\nabla\sigma={\bf\it 0}
at the wall surface, which is derived by taking the divergence of Eq. (
36
) with the mass conservation
\nabla\cdot{\bf\it v}^{n+1}=0
.
The scholar potential
S^{n+1}
is updated as follows:

S^{n+1}=S^{n}+\sigma-\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\nu_{0}\nabla^{2}\sigma.

(38)

In
{\bf\it r}\in\mathrm{V_{s}}
,
{\bf\it v}^{\mathrm{n+1}}
is given by

{\bf\it v}^{\mathrm{n+1}}={\bf\it v}^{*}.

(39)

According to Ref.
(
Amsden and Harlow 1970
)
,
we calculate the pressure
p^{n}
used in Eq. (
29
) as the solution of

\nabla\cdot\left\{\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\nabla p^{n}\right\}=\nabla\cdot\left(-\nabla\cdot\left({\bf\it v}^{n}{\bf\it v}^{n}\right)+\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\nabla\cdot{\bf\it\tau}^{n}+{\bf\it g}\right)

(40)

with the boundary conditions
\nabla p=\alpha\rho{\bf\it g}
at the wall surface, which is derived from the divergence of the momentum conservation Eq. (
20
).

II.3.4
Boundary data immersion method

We treat the flow driven by the rotating drum using the boundary data immersion (BDI) method
(
Weymouth and Yue 2011
)
. The solid domain
\mathrm{V_{s}}
is defined as the circular shell with the inner and outer diameters
D
and
D+16\Delta x
, respectively.
In this method, we introduce a single meta-equation to solve the time evolution equations in the fluid and solid domains simultaneously.
Here, we define the kernel function

\chi(d_{k})=\begin{cases}1\;\;&\mathrm{for}\;\;d_{k}\leq-\epsilon\\
\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\sin\left(\frac{\pi d_{k}}{2\epsilon}\right)\right]\;\;&\mathrm{for}\;\;\left|d_{k}\right|<\epsilon\\
0\;\;&d_{k}\geq\epsilon\end{cases}

(41)

to continuously connect the fluid and solid domains.
Here, the shortest distance from the wall surface at the position
{\bf\it r}
is denoted by
d_{k}({\bf\it r})
, and
\epsilon
is a parameter characterizing the thickness of the interface between the fluid and solid domains.
From Eq. (
41
), the kernel function
\chi
is
1
in the fluid domain and
0
in the solid domain.

Using the BDI method with Eqs. (
30
) and (
35
), the meta-equation for the predicted velocity
{\bf\it v}^{*}
is given by

{\bf\it v}^{*}=\chi\left[\left(1-\frac{\nu_{0}\Delta t}{2}\nabla^{2}\right)^{-1}{\bf\it\mathrm{RHS}}^{n}\right]+\left(1-\chi\right){\bf\it v}_{\mathrm{wall}},

(42)

where

{\bf\it\mathrm{RHS}}^{n}={\bf\it v}^{n}+\Delta t\left[\frac{3{\bf\it A}^{n}-{\bf\it A}^{n-1}}{2}-\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\nabla S^{n}+{\bf\it B}^{n}-\frac{\nu_{0}}{2}\nabla^{2}{\bf\it v}^{n}+{\bf\it g}\right].

(43)

For the projection step, the meta-equation for
{\bf\it v}^{\mathrm{n+1}}
is derived as follows:

{\bf\it v}^{\mathrm{n+1}}={\bf\it v}^{*}-\frac{\Delta t}{\rho^{n}}\chi\nabla\sigma

(44)

from Eqs. (
36
) and (
39
).
The Poisson equation for
\sigma
is obtained as follows:

\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\chi}{\rho^{n}}\nabla\sigma\right)=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\nabla\cdot{\bf\it v}^{*},

(45)

from the divergence of Eq. (
44
).
The Poisson equation for
p^{n}
is given by

\nabla\cdot\left\{\frac{\chi}{\rho^{n}}\nabla p^{n}\right\}=\nabla\cdot\left\{\chi\left(-\nabla\cdot\left({\bf\it v}^{n}{\bf\it v}^{n}\right)+\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\nabla\cdot{\bf\it\tau}^{n}+{\bf\it g}\right)+\left(1-\chi\right)\frac{{\bf\it v}_{\mathrm{wall}}}{\Delta t}\right\},

(46)

which is obtained from the divergence of

{\bf\it v}^{n+1}=\chi\left[{\bf\it v}^{n}+\Delta t\left\{-\nabla\left({\bf\it v}^{n}{\bf\it v}^{n}\right)-\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\nabla p^{n}+\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla\cdot{\bf\it\tau}^{n}+{\bf\it g}\right\}\right]+\left(1-\chi\right){\bf\it v}_{\mathrm{wall}}

(47)

with Eqs. (
20
) and (
22
).
We solve Eqs. (
45
) and (
46
) using the Bi-CGSTAB method
(
van der Vorst 1992
)
.

Note that near the interface between the granular and gas phases, the solution of Eq. (
46
) may result in pressure below
0
, which leads to unphysical behavior when calculating the viscosity of the granular phase
\eta_{\mathrm{g}}
in Eq. (
29
)
(
Chauchat and Médale 2014
;
Lin and Yang 2020
)
.
To prevent this, a sufficiently small positive constant
p_{\mathrm{min}}
is set as the minimum value of the pressure.

IV
Scaling laws

In this section, we theoretically derive scaling laws for the velocity profile of granular flow in a rotating drum using the continuum model.
We consider the mass conservation equation (
19
) and the momentum conservation equation (
20
).
The viscosity
\eta
in Eq. (
20
) is given by Eq. (
27
) with Eq. (
2
).
We neglect the first term on the left-hand side of Eq. (
20
) by considering the steady flow.
Using the drum diameter
D
, angular velocity
\Omega
, and density
\rho_{\mathrm{g}}
, we non-dimensionalize
{\bf\it r}
,
\nabla
,
{\bf\it v}
,
\dot{\gamma}
,
p
, and
\eta_{\mathrm{g}}
as follows:

\tilde{{\bf\it r}}=\frac{{\bf\it r}}{D},\;\;\tilde{\nabla}=\frac{\nabla}{D},\;\;\tilde{{\bf\it v}}=\frac{{\bf\it v}}{\Omega D},\;\;\tilde{\dot{\gamma}}=\frac{\dot{\gamma}}{\Omega},\;\;\tilde{p}=\frac{p}{\rho_{\mathrm{g}}\Omega^{2}D^{2}},\;\;\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{g}}=\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{g}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{g}}\Omega D^{2}}

(50)

with the dimensionless variables
\tilde{{\bf\it r}},\tilde{\nabla},\tilde{{\bf\it v}},\tilde{\dot{\gamma}},\tilde{p}
, and
\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{g}}
.
Here, we have used
D
as a unit of length because we focus on the overall velocity profile in the system with
D\gg d
.
Substituting these equations into Eqs. (
19
), (
20
), and (
27
) with Eq. (
2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{{\bf\it v}}=0,

(51)

\displaystyle-\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\left(\tilde{{\bf\it v}}\tilde{{\bf\it v}}\right)-\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{p}+\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\left[\tilde{\eta_{\mathrm{g}}}\left(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{{\bf\it v}}+\left(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{{\bf\it v}}\right)^{T}\right)\right]-\frac{2}{Fr}{\bf\it e}_{z}=0,

(52)

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{g}}=\mu_{s}\frac{\tilde{p}}{\tilde{\dot{\gamma}}}+\left(\mu_{2}-\mu_{s}\right)\frac{\tilde{p}}{I_{0}\sqrt{\phi\tilde{p}}/(d/D)+\tilde{\dot{\gamma}}},

(53)

where we have introduced the unit vector
{\bf\it e}_{z}
in the
z
direction and the Froude number

Fr=\frac{\Omega^{2}D}{2g}.

(54)

Here, we consider the dependence of the velocity profile on
D
and
\Omega
, and
d/D
and
Fr
are the dimensionless parameters related to them in Eqs. (
51
)-(
53
).
Thus, the dimensionless velocity field
\tilde{{\bf\it v}}({\bf\it r})
is characterized by the Froude number
Fr
and the diameter ratio
d/D
, except for
\mu_{s}
,
\mu_{2}
, and
I_{0}
determined by the properties of particles; this is consistent with the experiments in Ref.
Orpe and Khakhar 2001
.

Since we have studied the system for
d/D\leq 1/150\ll 1
in the previous section, we consider the limit
d/D\to 0
in Eqs. (
51
)-(
53
).
In this limit, the only dimensionless parameter in Eqs. (
51
)-(
53
) is
Fr
.
Hence, the solution
\tilde{{\bf\it v}}\left(\tilde{{\bf\it r}}\right)
of these equation depends only on
Fr
and is written as

\tilde{{\bf\it v}}\left(\tilde{{\bf\it r}}\right)={\bf\it V}\left(\tilde{{\bf\it r}};Fr\right),

(55)

where
{\bf\it V}\left(\tilde{{\bf\it r}};Fr\right)
is a function depending on
Fr
.
Taking the limit
d/D\to 0
in Eq. (
53
) is equivalent to treating the granular material as a simple plastic body with the yield stress proportional to the pressure.
A similar approximation has been introduced in Ref.
Agarwal et al. 2021
.
From Eq. (
55
), the scaling law for the velocity profile
u(z)
at
x=0
is obtained as follows:

\frac{u(z)}{\Omega D}=U\left(\frac{z}{D};Fr\right),

(56)

where
U\left(z/D;Fr\right)
is a scaling function.

Substituting Eq. (
56
) into Eq. (
49
), we obtain

U\left(\frac{h}{D};Fr\right)=0.

(57)

From this equation, we derive the scaling law for
h
as follows:

\frac{h}{D}=H\left(Fr\right),

(58)

where
H(Fr)
is a scaling function.

We verify these scaling laws from the numerical simulations.

illustrates the normalized velocity profile

Comparing the results for different

shows a slight dependence on

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we numerically studied the steady flow of a two-dimensional granular material in a rotating drum using the DEM and CFD simulations with the
\mu(I)
-rheology.
The velocity fields obtained from both methods are quantitatively consistent for various values of the diameter
D
and the angular velocity
\Omega
of the drum.
The granular material exhibits the rigid rotation in the static flow regime near the drum wall while it flows parallel to the free surface in the surface flow layer.
The surface flow layer is characterized by the layer thickness
h
at the center of the drum.
We have analytically obtained scaling laws (Eqs. (
56
) and (
58
)) for the velocity profile and surface layer thickness from dimensional considerations of the continuum equations.
Numerical results verified the validity of these scaling laws.

The scaling law for the surface flow layer thickness based on the
\mu(I)
-rheology predicts
h\sim\mbox{const.}
in the limit of
\Omega\to 0
, which is consistent with the experimental results in Ref.
Parker et al. 1997
. In contrast, other experimental studies
Khakhar et al. 1997
;
Félix et al. 2007
;
Orpe and Khakhar 2007
;
Pignatel et al. 2012
have reported a power-law dependence,
h\sim\Omega^{\alpha}
with a constant
\alpha
, which was obtained from a phenomenological assumption of a constant shear rate.
The cylinder used in Ref.
Parker et al. 1997
is sufficiently long in the axial direction, and the flow can be considered two-dimensional, which may explain the agreement with our theoretical prediction.
Our preliminary three-dimensional simulations suggest that the scaling law (Eqs. (
56
) and (
58
)) holds for granular flow in a sufficiently long rotating cylinder, whereas significant deviations are observed for shorter cylinders. These deviations may be attributed to the influence of sidewall friction, potentially explaining why the experimental results in Refs.
Khakhar et al. 1997
;
Félix et al. 2007
;
Orpe and Khakhar 2007
;
Pignatel et al. 2012
deviate from our scaling laws.
However, the effect of sidewall friction in shorter cylinders and a theoretical explanation for the experimental results in Refs.
Khakhar et al. 1997
;
Félix et al. 2007
;
Orpe and Khakhar 2007
;
Pignatel et al. 2012
warrant further careful investigation.

We studied the rotating drum half-filled with granular materials in this study.
However, a flow similar to Fig.
1
is observed for various values of the fill fraction.
We have not investigated the dependence of the velocity field on the fill fraction; however, we expect the scaling laws derived in this study to apply to systems with different fill fractions because we have not used the condition for the fill fractions in the derivation of the scaling law for the velocity field
{\bf\it v}({\bf\it r})
.

Recently, the
\mu(I)
-rheology has been improved to include the non-local effect
(
Kamrin and Koval 2012
;
Kim and Kamrin 2020
)
.
We ignored its effect and adopted the local
\mu(I)
-rheology in our analysis since we deemed it irrelevant for sufficiently large systems with
D/d\geq 150
.
In fact, the
\mu(I)
-rheology has qualitatively reproduced the velocity fields in our simulation.
However, more detailed work is necessary on the non-local effect on the velocity field in the rotating drum.

Although we obtained the scaling laws by the non-dimensionalization, the forms of the scaling functions are not theoretically derived.
In Ref.
Haji-Sheikh et al. 1984
, the velocity field of an incompressible Newtonian fluid in a rotating drum is analytically calculated.
In. Ref.
Gray 2001
, the streamlines for the granular flow in a rotating drum are derived based on a simple constitutive law.
The development of these analytical methods may lead to a better understanding of the granular flow, including the derivation of the scaling functions.

Appendix E
Boundary between surface flow layer and static flow regime

In this study, the boundary between the surface flow layer and the static flow regime is defined as the position for
u(z)=0
. However, in Ref.
Ding et al. 2002
, the boundary is determined from the difference between
u(z)
and the rigid rotation velocity
-\Omega z
.
For example, we may regard the surface flow layer as the region where the velocity in the granular bed is sufficiently larger than that of the rigid rotation as

\frac{u(z)-\left(-\Omega z\right)}{\Omega D}\geq\alpha_{\mathrm{th}}

(60)

with a threshold
\alpha_{\mathrm{th}}
.
The surface flow layer thickness
h^{\prime}
for this definition satisfies

\frac{u(z=h^{\prime})-\left(-\Omega h^{\prime}\right)}{\Omega D}=\alpha_{\mathrm{th}}.

(61)

If the velocity
u(z)
obeys the scaling law, Eq. (
56
), this equation gives

U\left(\frac{h^{\prime}}{D};Fr\right)-\frac{h^{\prime}}{D}=\alpha_{\mathrm{th}}.

(62)

Solving this equation for
h^{\prime}/D
yields the scaling law for
h^{\prime}
as

\frac{h^{\prime}}{D}=H^{\prime}\left(Fr\right)

(63)

for a given
\alpha_{\mathrm{th}}
,
where
H^{\prime}(Fr)
is a scaling function.
This equation is qualitatively consistent with the scaling law (
58
) for
h
.
This indicates that the surface flow layer thickness shows the same scaling regardless of the definition of
h
.

Appendix F
Surface flow layer thickness
h
for
Fr\ll 1

In this section, we show
H^{(0)}>0
in Eq. (
59
).
The velocity
u(z)
is a decreasing function of
z
and
u(z)=0
at
z=h
.
The volumetric flow rates in the surface flow layer and the static flow regime are given by

Q_{+}=\int_{0}^{h}dz\ u(z),

(64)

and

Q_{-}=-\int_{h}^{D/2}dz\ u(z),

(65)

respectively.
Since the system is in a steady state, these volumetric flow rates satisfy

Q_{+}=Q_{-}.

(66)

The velocity
u(z)
satisfies Eq. (
