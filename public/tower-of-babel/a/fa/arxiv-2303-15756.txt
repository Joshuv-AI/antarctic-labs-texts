Complete non-ambiguous trees and associated permutations: new enumerative results
Thomas Selig and Haoyue Zhu

Abstract
. We study a link between complete non-ambiguous trees (CNATs) and permutations exhibited by Daniel Chen and Sebastian Ohlig in recent work. In this, they associate a certain permutation to the leaves of a CNAT, and show that the number of n n -permutations that are associated with exactly one CNAT is 2 n − 2 2^{n-2} . We connect this to work by the first author and co-authors linking complete non-ambiguous trees and the Tutte polynomial of the associated permutation graph. This allows us to prove a number of conjectures by Chen and Ohlig on the number of n n -permutations that are associated with exactly k k CNATs for various k > 1 k>1 , via various bijective correspondences between such permutations. We also exhibit a new bijection between ( n − 1 ) (n-1) -permutations and CNATs whose permutation is the decreasing permutation n ( n − 1 ) ⋯ 1 n(n-1)\cdots 1 . This bijection maps the left-to-right minima of the permutation to dots on the top row of the corresponding CNAT, and descents of the permutation to empty rows of the CNAT. Keywords: complete non-ambiguous trees, permutations, permutation graphs, Tutte polynomial, acyclic orientations. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 05A19 (Primary); 05A05, 05A15, 05C30 (Secondary).



1.
Introduction

Non-ambiguous trees (NATs) were originally introduced by Aval
et al.
in
[
3
]
as a special case of the tree-like tableaux from
[
5
]
. The combinatorial study of these objects was further developed in
[
4
]
, which included a generalisation of NATs to higher dimensions. In
[
13
]
, the authors described a multi-rooted generalisation of complete NATs (CNATs), and linked this to the so-called Abelian sandpile model.

We can associate a permutation to a CNAT by keeping only its leaf dots (see Section
2.4
). This link between CNATs and permutations was first noted, to the best of our knowledge, in
[
13
]
(see Section 4 in that paper for more details). Chen and Ohlig
[
11
]
initiated the first in-depth combinatorial study of this relationship. In particular, they characterised the set of permutations
\pi
which are associated to a unique CNAT. More generally, they looked into the number of
n
-permutations with
k
associated CNATs, for
k\geq 1
, and provided a number of conjectures on the enumeration of such permutations. They also studied in detail so-called
upper-diagonal
CNATs, which are CNATs where the associated permutation is the decreasing permutation
n(n-1)\cdots 1
, using these to prove a conjecture of Laborde-Zubieta on occupied corners of tree-like tableaux
[
20
]
. Finally, they introduced a natural statistic,
determinant
, on CNATs, and conjectured that CNATs with determinant
+1
and those with determinant
-1
are equinumerous. This conjecture was solved very recently by Aval
[
2
]
through a bijective approach.

In this paper, we will further develop the study initiated in
[
11
]
, using results from
[
13
]
. In that work it was shown that the number of CNATs associated with a given permutation
\pi
is equal to the number of
minimal recurrent configurations
for the Abelian sandpile model on the
permutation graph

G_{\pi}
of
\pi
. Minimal recurrent configurations are in natural bijection with certain acyclic orientations of the graph, and are counted by a specification of the ubiquitous
Tutte polynomial
(see Proposition
2.2
and Theorem
2.12
in this paper). This enumerative result allows us to solve a number of conjectures in
[
11
]
, via various bijective correspondences between permutations with a given number of CNATs.

We also add to the study of upper-diagonal CNATs by providing a new bijection between upper-diagonal CNATs of size
n
and permutations of length
n-1
(see Theorem
3.5
). This new bijection has the following added benefits. Firstly, it is direct, whereas the bijection in
[
11
]
used intermediate objects called tiered trees (see
[
12
]
). Secondly, it maps certain statistics of the upper-diagonal CNATs such as the number of top-row dots, or the number of empty rows, to well-known statistics of the corresponding permutation.

Our paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we recall some necessary definitions of, and notation on, the combinatorial objects that will be considered in this paper. These include graphs, Tutte polynomials, permutations, and CNATs with associated permutations. We also show a number of useful preliminary results that will be useful in the remainder of the paper. At the end of the section we provide the key connection between CNATs and the Tutte polynomial (Theorem
2.12
). In Section
3
, we focus on one specific family of CNATs, called
upper-diagonal CNATs
. We introduce a concept of
labelled CNAT
, and describe a bijection between labelled CNATs and permutations of the label set (Theorem
3.5
), which preserves certain statistics of both objects. This bijection then specialises to a bijection between upper-diagonal CNATs of size
n
and permutations of length
n-1
. The bijection relies on two operations on labelled CNATs, called
top-row decomposition
and
top-row deletion
.

In Section
4
, we focus on counting permutations according to their number of associated CNATs. Specifically, for
k,n\geq 1
we are interested in the set
B(n,k)
of permutations of length
n
which are associated with exactly
k
CNATs. The study of these sets was initiated in
[
11
]
, and we continue that work here by proving a number of conjectures left by the authors. We begin in Section
4.1
by giving characterisations of the sets
B(n,1)
and
B(n,2)
in terms of the so-called
quadrant condition
(Definition
4.1
, Propositions
4.2
and
4.10
). This allows us to describe a simple bijection between the product set
\{2,\cdots,n\}\times B(n,1)
and
B(n+1,2)
(Theorem
4.3
), and deduce the enumerative formula for the latter (Corollary
4.5
). In Section
4.2
we use permutation
patterns
to establish a bijection between the sets
B(n,2)
and
B(n+1,3)
(Theorem
4.16
). We then show in Section
4.3
that the set
B(n,5)
is always empty (Theorem
4.18
). Finally, in Section
4.4
we consider the maximal value of
k
such that
B(n,k)
is non-empty. We show (Theorem
4.24
) that this is achieved for
k=(n-1)!
, and that the unique permutation in
B(n,(n-1)!)
is precisely the decreasing permutation
n(n-1)\cdots 1
whose CNATs were studied in detail in Section
3
. We conclude our paper in Section
5
with a brief summary of our results as well as some open problems and directions for future research.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we introduce the various objects that will be studied and used throughout the paper, alongside the necessary notation. We also state and prove a number of useful preliminary results.

2.1.
Graphs and orientations

Throughout this paper all graphs considered are finite, undirected, and connected. For a graph
G
, we denote
V(G)
and
E(G)
its set of vertices and edges respectively, and write
G=(V(G),E(G))
(here
E(G)
is a multiset since
G
may have multiple edges). An edge
e\in E(G)
is a
bridge
if removing
e
disconnects the graph
G
. A
loop
is simply an edge whose two end-points are the same. A graph
G
is called
simple
if it contains no loops or multiple edges. Two graphs
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
are said to be
isomorphic
if there exists a bijection
\phi:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
such that for any vertices
v,w\in V
, we have
(v,w)\in E
if, and only if,
(\phi(v),\phi(w))\in E^{\prime}
. Given a subset
V^{\prime}\subseteq V(G)
, the
induced
subgraph on
V^{\prime}
is the graph with vertex set
V^{\prime}
and edge set consisting of all edges in
E
whose end-points are both in
V^{\prime}
. We denote it
G\left[V^{\prime}\right]
. More generally, a
subgraph
of
G
is a graph
G^{\prime}
such that
V(G^{\prime})\subseteq V(G)
and
E(G^{\prime})\subseteq E(G)
. It is a
spanning
subgraph if
V(G^{\prime})=V(G)
.

A
cycle
in the graph
G
is a sequence of vertices
v_{0},v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}=v_{0}
(for some
n\geq 3
) such that for every
i>0
,
(v_{i-1},v_{i})
is an edge of
G
, and the vertices
v_{0},v_{1},\cdots,v_{n-1}
are all distinct. The integer
n
is the
length
of the cycle, and we will say that
G

contains
a cycle of length
n
(or
n
-cycle for short) if there exists such a cycle in
G
. The
n
-cycle (graph)
C_{n}
is the graph consisting of a single cycle of length
n
, with no other vertices or edges. If
G
contains a cycle
v_{0},v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}=v_{0}
such that
G\left[\{v_{0},\cdots,v_{n-1}\}\right]
is the
n
-cycle
C_{n}
, we say that
G

induces
an
n
-cycle. A
tree
is a (connected) graph containing no cycle of any length. A
spanning tree
of a graph
G
is a spanning subgraph which is a tree. The
n
-path
(graph) is the graph with vertex set
V(G)=\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}\}
and edge set
E(G)=\{(v_{i},v_{i+1})\}_{1\leq i\leq n-1}
(equivalently the
n
-cycle with any single edge removed).

An
orientation

\mathcal{O}
of a graph
G
is the assignment of a direction to each edge of
G
. Given an orientation
\mathcal{O}
of
G
, and an edge
(v,w)
, we write
v\xrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}w
to indicate that the edge is directed from
v
to
w
in the orientation
\mathcal{O}
, and
v\xleftarrow{\mathcal{O}}w
when it is directed from
w
to
v
. We also write
\mathrm{in}^{\mathcal{O}}_{[}v]
, resp.
\mathrm{out}^{\mathcal{O}}_{[}v]
for the number of incoming edges (edges
v\xleftarrow{\mathcal{O}}w
), resp. outgoing edges (edges
v\xrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}w
), at
v
in the orientation
\mathcal{O}
. A vertex
v
is a
sink
, resp.
source
, of an orientation
\mathcal{O}
if
\mathrm{in}^{\mathcal{O}}_{[}v]=\mathrm{deg}_{[}v]
(all edges are incoming), resp.
\mathrm{out}^{\mathcal{O}}_{[}v]=\mathrm{deg}_{[}v]
(all edges are outgoing).

An orientation is
acyclic
if it contains no directed cycle, i.e. there is no sequence
v_{0},\cdots,v_{n-1}
of vertices such that
v_{0}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}v_{1}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}v_{n-1}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}v_{0}
. It is straightforward to check that an acyclic orientation must have at least one source and at least one sink. For
s\in V(G)
, we see that an acyclic orientation
\mathcal{O}
is
s
-rooted
if
s
is the unique sink of
\mathcal{O}
.

2.2.
Tutte polynomials

A key result that we will use in this paper links CNATs (see Section
2.4
) to the
Tutte polynomial
of a graph. The Tutte polynomial was formally introduced in
[
24
]
in the author’s study graph colouring problems, although it is also implicit in earlier related work by Whitney
[
26
]
. It is a universal (in some sense, see
[
15
, Section 2.4]
) graph invariant, which appears in a number of contexts. For a detailed introduction to the Tutte polynomial and its properties, we refer readers to the book
[
14
]
, in particular Chapters 2 and 3. There are three standard definitions of the Tutte polynomial. In this work, we use the definition in terms of deletion-contraction operations.

Given a graph
G
and an edge
e=(v,w)\in E(G)
, we denote by
G\setminus e
the graph
G
with edge
e

deleted
. That is,
G\setminus e:=\left(V(G),E(G)\setminus\{e\}\right)
. Similarly,
G\cdot e
denotes the graph
G
with edge
e

contracted
. This is the graph
G
in which the two end-points
v
and
w
are replaced by a single vertex
vw
, and edges incident to either
v
or
w
are made incident to
vw
instead (with multiplicity preserved).

Definition 2.1
.

Given a graph
G
and an edge
e\in E(G)
, the
Tutte polynomial
of
G
, denoted
T_{G}
, is a bivariate polynomial defined recursively as follows.

(1)

T_{G}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}T_{G\setminus e}(x,y)+T_{G\cdot e}(x,y)&\text{if }e\text{ is neither a bridge nor a loop},\\
x\cdot T_{G\cdot e}(x,y)&\text{if }e\text{ is a bridge},\\
y\cdot T_{G\setminus e}(x,y)&\text{if }e\text{ is a loop},\\
1&\text{if }G\text{ consists of a single vertex (and no edges)}.\\
\end{array}\right.

In particular, the definition of
T_{G}
does not depend on the choice of edge
e
.

We will in fact only be interested in a specific evaluation of the Tutte polynomial at
x=1
and
y=0
. To simplify notation, we define
t_{G}:=T_{G}(1,0)
for a graph
G
. The parameter
t_{G}
enumerates a number of structures or quantities on the graph
G
, such as spanning trees with no external activity, minimal recurrent configurations of the Abelian sandpile model, or sink-rooted orientations. We use the latter here (see e.g.
[
6
]
for a bijective proof), and refer to
t_{G}
as the
acyclic orientation number
of the graph
G
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
G
be a graph, and
s\in V(G)
a fixed vertex of
G
. Then
t_{G}
is equal to the number of
s
-rooted acyclic orientations of
G
. In particular, this number does not depend on the choice of sink vertex
s
.

We now state a number of useful structural results on how the Tutte polynomial behaves with respect to certain graph constructions. We begin by characterising graphs whose acyclic orientation number is
0
or
1
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
be a graph. Then we have
t_{G}=0
if, and only if,
G
contains a loop. In particular, if
G
is simple, we have
t_{G}\geq 1
.

Proof.

If
G
contains a loop, then applying the loop case of Equation (
1
) and evaluating at
y=0
immediately implies that
t_{G}=0
. Conversely, if
G
is loop-free, fix some arbitrary total order
v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}
of the vertex set
V(G)
. Define an orientation
\mathcal{O}
of
G
by
v_{i}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}v_{j}
if, and only if,
i<j
. Since
G
is loop-free, this does indeed assign a direction to each edge of
G
, and by construction
\mathcal{O}
is a
v_{n}
-rooted acyclic orientation. Proposition
2.2
then implies that
t_{G}\geq 1
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a graph. Then we have
t_{G}=1
if, and only if, all cycles in
G
have length
2
. In particular, if
G
is simple, then
t_{G}=1
if, and only if,
G
is a tree.

Proof.

Suppose that
G
is such that all its cycles have length
2
, and let
e=(v,w)
be a multi-edge corresponding to such a cycle (i.e.
e
has multiplicity at least
2
in
G
). By definition, we have
t_{G}=t_{G\setminus e}+t_{G\cdot e}
. But contracting
e
in
G
yields a loop at the new vertex
vw
, since
e
was a multi-edge in
G
. Therefore
t_{G\cdot e}=0
by Lemma
2.3
, which implies that
t_{G}=t_{G\setminus e}
. Repeating this process until all mutli-edges have been removed, we are left with a tree
G^{\prime}
and
t_{G}=t_{G^{\prime}}
. But now all edges in
G^{\prime}
are bridges, and we may contract them one-by-one until we reach a graph consisting of a single vertex. Applying Equation (
1
) throughout this process (and evaluating at
x=1
immediately yields
t_{G^{\prime}}=1
, as desired.

Conversely, suppose that
G
contains a cycle of length other than
2
. As above, we may remove all multi-edges of
G
without changing the acyclic orientation number. Moreover, if
G
contains a loop, we have
t_{G}=0
by Lemma
2.3
. We therefore assume, without loss of generality, that
G
is simple and contains a cycle of length
k\geq 3
. Let
e
be an edge in such a cycle. Note that
e
is not a bridge. Moreover, since
G
is simple, contracting
e
yields a graph which is loop-free, while deleting
e
yields a simple (connected) graph. Therefore, applying the Deletion-contraction Equation (
1
) at edge
e
, combined with Lemma
2.3
, we get
t_{G}=t_{G\setminus e}+t_{G\cdot e}\geq 1+1=2
, as desired.
∎

Our next result can be thought of as a generalisation of Lemma
2.4
in the simple case. It shows that “pruning” a graph
G
of its tree branches does not affect the acyclic orientation number. Given a graph
G
, we define the
2-core
of
G
, denoted
\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G]
, through the following algorithmic procedure.

(1)

Initialise
H=G
.

(2)

If
H
contains no vertex of degree
1
, move to Step 3. Otherwise, choose a vertex
v
with degree
1
, set
H=H\setminus\{v\}
, and repeat Step 2.

(3)

Output
H:=\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G]
.

Another way of viewing this procedure is that it removes “tree branches” that were attached at some vertices in the graph
G
. An example is shown on Figure
1
below. Note that the output
\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G]
only depends on the choice of vertex

in Step (
2
) if at some stage of the process we reach a graph

which is reduced to a single edge, which occurs in fact exactly when the original graph
G
is a tree. In that case, removing either of the two vertices will yield a graph consisting of a single vertex, and we simply set
\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G]
to be any arbitrary vertex of the original graph.

Lemma 2.5
.

For any graph
G
, we have
t_{G}=t_{\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G]}
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a graph with a vertex
v
of degree
1
, and consider
e
the unique edge incident to
v
. By definition, we have
G\setminus\{v\}=G\cdot e
, and
e
is a bridge in

(deleting it disconnects
v
from the rest of the graph). Therefore we have
t_{G\setminus\{v\}}=t_{G\cdot e}=t_{G}
by Equation (
1
) (bridge case). The result follows by iterating the above until no vertices of degree
1
remain.
∎

Our next lemma concerns graphs whose pruned graph is a cycle. For
k\geq 1
, we say that a graph
G
is a
decorated
k
-cycle
if its pruned graph
\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G]
is the
k
-cycle
C_{k}
. Equivalently,
G
contains a
k
-cycle, and deleting any edge in that
k
-cycle makes
G
a tree.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
G
is a decorated
k
-cycle for some
k\geq 1
, then we have
t_{G}=k-1
.

Proof.

If
G
is a decorated
k
-cycle, then
\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G]
is the
k
-cycle
C_{k}
by definition. Lemma
2.5
then yields that
t_{G}=t_{C_{k}}
. Applying the Deletion-contraction Equation (
1
) gives
t_{C_{k}}=t_{P_{k}}+t_{C_{k-1}}
(where
P_{k}
is the path graph on
k
vertices). Since path graphs are trees, Lemma
2.4
implies that
t_{P_{k}}=1
for any
k\geq 1
, so that
t_{C_{k}}=k-1+t_{C_{1}}
. But
C_{1}
is the graph consisting of a single loop, so Lemma
2.3
implies that
t_{C_{1}}=0
, and the result follows immediately.
∎

Our next lemma shows that adding an edge to a graph
G
strictly increases its acyclic orientation number.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
G
be a
simple
graph, with a pair of
distinct
vertices
v,w
such that
(v,w)
is
not
an edge of
G
. Let
G^{\prime}:=G\cup\{(v,w)\}
be the graph
G
to which we add the edge
(v,w)
. Then we have
t_{G}<t_{G^{\prime}}
.

If
G^{\prime}
is obtained from
G
as above, we say that
G^{\prime}
is an
edge addition
of
G
.

Proof.

Let
G
,
e:=(v,w)
, and
G^{\prime}
be as in the statement of the lemma, i.e.
G=G^{\prime}\setminus e
. By the Deletion-contraction Equation (
1
), we have
t_{G^{\prime}}=t_{G}+t_{G^{\prime}\cdot e}
. Moreover, by construction, since
G
is simple, the graph
G^{\prime}\cdot e
is loop-free. The result then follows from Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Finally, the following result will be widely used throughout Section
4
to obtain bounds on the acyclic orientation numbers of various graphs.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
G
be a simple graph, and
G^{\prime}
a subgraph of
G
. Then we have
t_{G^{\prime}}\leq t_{G}
. Moreover, if the
2
-cores
\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G]
and
\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G^{\prime}]
are
not
isomorphic, then we have
t_{G^{\prime}}<t_{G}
.

Proof.

Fix some vertex
s\in V(G^{\prime})
. Consider the following algorithmic procedure.

(1)

Initialise
H=G^{\prime}
.

(2)

While
V(H)\subsetneq V(G)
, do:

(a)

Choose a vertex
v\in V(G)\setminus V(H)
.

(b)

Since
G
is connected, there exists a path from
v
to
s
in
G
. Consider such a path, and let
w
be the first vertex in
V(H)
encountered on this path.

(c)

Set
H
to be the union of
H
and the above path from
v
to
w
.

(3)

Output
H:=G^{0}
.

By construction, the above process outputs a spanning subgraph
G^{0}
of
G
such that
\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G^{0}]
and
\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G^{\prime}]
are isomorphic (constructing
G^{0}
from
G^{\prime}
only adds “tree branches”). In particular by Lemma
2.5
, we have
t_{G^{0}}=t_{G^{\prime}}
. Then the graph
G
can be reached from
G^{0}
through a (possibly non-empty) sequence of edge additions. Moreover, if
\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G]
and
\mathrm{Core}_{2}\left([\right)G^{\prime}]
are not isomorphic, there must be at least one edge in
E(G)
which is
not
in
E(G^{0})
, in which case this sequence must be non-empty. The results then follow from Lemma
2.7
.
∎

2.3.
Permutations, permutation patterns, and permutation graphs

For

, we let

be the set of permutations of size
n
(called

-permutations for short). We will usually use standard one-line notation for permutations. That is, a permutation

such that every letter in
[n]
appears exactly once in
\pi
. By convention, the unique

\pi=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{n}
, we construct its plot by putting dots in column
i
and row
\pi_{i}
for

. When connecting permutations to CNATs, it will be convenient to label columns from left-to-right and rows from top-to-bottom in this representation, as in Figure

2.4.
Complete non-ambiguous trees and associated permutations

In this section we define (complete) non-ambiguous trees and introduce their associated permutations. Non-ambiguous trees were originally introduced by Aval

Definition 2.11
.

A
non-ambiguous tree
(NAT) is a filling of an
m\times n
rectangular grid, where each cell is either dotted or not, satisfying the following conditions.

(1)

Every row and every column contains at least one dotted cell.

(2)

Aside from the top-left cell, every dotted cell has either a dotted cell above it in the same column, or a dotted cell to its left in the same row, but not both.

Note that the two conditions imply that the top-left cell must always be dotted. The use of the word
tree
to describe these objects comes from the following observation. Given a NAT
T
, we connect every dot
d
not in the top-left cell to its
parent
dot
p(d)
, which is the dot immediately above it in its column or to its left in its row (by Condition (
2
) of the above definition, exactly one of these must exist). This yields a binary tree, rooted at the top-left dot (see Figure
4
).

Following tree terminology, for a NAT
T
, we call the dot lying in the top-left cell the
root dot
, or simply the
root
, of
T
. Similarly, a
leaf dot
is a dot with no dots below it in the same column or to its right in the same row. An
internal dot
is a dot which is not a leaf dot (this includes the root, unless the NAT is a single dotted cell). Given a NAT, it will be convenient to label the columns
1,2,\cdots n
from left to right, and the rows
1,2,\cdots,m
from top to bottom.

A NAT is said to be
complete
if the underlying tree is complete, i.e. every internal dot has exactly two children. More formally, a complete non-ambiguous tree (CNAT) is a NAT in which every dot either has both a dot below it in the same column and a dot to its right in the same row, or neither of these. The
size
of a CNAT is its number of leaf dots, or equivalently one more than its number of internal dots.

Upper-diagonal CNATs and permutations

In

Theorem 3.5

For any label set
\lambda

\Psi:\mathcal{T}\mapsto\Psi(T)
is a bijection from the set of
\lambda

\lambda

\lambda

\Psi(\mathcal{T})
. Finally, the number of empty rows in the underlying CNAT

In particular, if the label set is
\lambda=[n-1]

Remark 3.6
.

Recall that upper-diagonal CNATs are those whose associated permutation is
\mathrm{dec}_{[}n]=n(n-1)\cdots 1
. The corresponding permutation graph
G_{\mathrm{dec}_{[}n]}
is simply the complete graph on
n

. It is straightforward to see that acyclic orientations of

are simply linear orders of its vertex set, i.e.
n

-permutations, so that

by Proposition
2.2
.

On the other hand, the proof of Theorem
2.12
in
[
13
]
goes through an alternate interpretation of the acyclic orientation number
T_{G_{\pi}}
in terms of spanning trees of
G_{\pi}
with no external activity (these are shown to correspond bijectively to CNATs with associated permutation
\pi
). In this context, Theorem
3.5
can be viewed as a bijection between spanning trees with no external activity and sink-rooted acyclic orientations in the special case of complete graphs. Such bijections do in fact exist in the general case (see e.g.
[
6
]
or
[
16
]
). The novelty of our bijection is placing this work directly in the CNAT setting, as well as the preservation of relevant statistics.

Proof.

Since top-row decomposition partitions the label set
\lambda
into two (smaller) label sets, and top-row deletion removes the minimum label
\lambda_{1}
, we have that
\Psi(\mathcal{T})
is a
\lambda
-permutation of
\lambda
by a straightforward induction on the size of
\lambda
using the recursive definition.

Let us now show that if there is a dot in the top row in the column labelled
\lambda_{i}
of
\mathcal{T}
, then
\lambda_{i}
is a left-to-right minimum of the permutation
\Psi(\mathcal{T})
. Again, we proceed by induction on
n
, the size of the label set
\lambda
. For

the result is trivial. Now fix some

, and suppose we have shown this for all label sets
\lambda^{\prime}
of size at most

. Fix a label set
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
of size
n
, and a
\lambda
-labelled CNAT
\mathcal{T}
.

Firstly, consider the case where
\mathcal{T}
has a unique internal dot in the top row, which is necessarily the root, in column labelled
\lambda_{1}=\min\lambda
. By construction
\lambda_{1}
is the first element in the
\lambda
-permutation
\Psi(\mathcal{T})
, and is therefore its unique left-to-right minimum, as desired.

It thus remains to consider the case where
\mathcal{T}
has at least two internal dots in the top row. In this case write the top-row decomposition
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}^{\ell}\oplus\mathcal{T}^{r}
of
\mathcal{T}
. By construction the labels of top-row internal dots of
\mathcal{T}
are exactly those of top-row dots of
\mathcal{T}^{\ell}
, plus the label
\lambda^{r}_{1}
of the right-most dot in that row (the root of
T^{r}
). By the induction hypothesis,
\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\ell})
is a permutation of
\lambda^{\ell}
whose left-to-right minima are the column labels of the top-row dots of
\mathcal{T}^{\ell}
. In particular, this implies that the first letter in
\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\ell})
is strictly less than
\lambda^{r}_{1}
. Moreover, by the above argument
\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{r})
is a permutation of
\lambda^{r}
starting with

(the minimal element of
\lambda^{r}
). This implies that the left-to-right minima in the permutation
\Psi(\mathcal{T})=\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{r})\!\cdot\!\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\ell})
are exactly
\lambda^{r}_{1}
and the left-to-right minima of
\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\ell})
, and the result follows by applying the induction hypothesis to
\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\ell})
.

To show that
\Psi
is a bijection, we construct its inverse recursively, and show it maps left-to-right minima to top-row internal dots in the labelled CNAT. Let
\pi=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{n}
be a permutation of a label set
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
. We define a
\lambda
-labelled CNAT
\mathcal{T}:=\Phi(\pi)
as follows.

•

If
\lambda=\emptyset
(
\pi
is the empty word in this case), we set
T
to be the CNAT consisting of a unique root dot, and
\mathcal{T}=(T,\emptyset)
to be the labelled CNAT (base case).

•

If
\pi_{1}=\lambda_{1}
(the first element of
\pi
is the minimum label in
\lambda
), then we first calculate recursively
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\Phi(\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n})
, and then let
\mathcal{T}
be the unique
\lambda
-labelled CNAT whose top-row deletion is
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
(see Remark
3.3
). By construction the CNAT has a unique dot in the top-row, which is in column labelled
\lambda_{1}
, giving the desired result.

•

Otherwise, define
k=\min\{i\geq 2;\pi_{i}<\pi_{1}\}
, and decompose
\pi=(\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{k-1})(\pi_{k}\cdots\pi_{n}):=\pi^{r}\pi^{\ell}
. In other words, the first element of
\pi^{\ell}
is the second left-to-right minimum in
\pi
. Then calculate
\mathcal{T}^{\ell}=\Phi(\pi^{\ell})
and
\mathcal{T}^{r}=\Phi(\pi^{r})
recursively. By construction, the permutation
\pi^{r}
begins with its minimum label, so the top-row of
\mathcal{T}^{r}
is empty except for the leaf and root dots from the above case. Moreover, the minimum label of
\mathcal{T}^{r}
is
\pi_{1}
, which by definition is greater than all left-to-right minima in
\pi^{\ell}
. By the induction hypothesis, this means exactly that the minimum label of
\mathcal{T}^{r}
is greater than the labels of all the top-row internal dots in
\mathcal{T}^{\ell}
. Remark
3.2
then states that there exists a unique labelled CNAT
\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}^{\ell}\oplus\mathcal{T}^{r}
, and we set
\Phi(\pi)=\mathcal{T}
. By construction, the column labels of the top-row dots of
\mathcal{T}
are those of
\mathcal{T}^{\ell}
plus that of the root of
\mathcal{T}^{r}
, which by the induction hypothesis are exactly the left-to-right minima of
\pi
, as desired.

That
\Psi
and
\Phi
are inverse of each other is straightforward from the construction, which proves the first part of the theorem.

It remains to show that the number of empty rows in
\mathcal{T}
is one plus the number of descents of
\Psi(\mathcal{T})
. For a labelled CNAT
\mathcal{T}
, resp. a permutation
\pi
, let
\mathrm{EmptyRow}\left([\right)\mathcal{T}]
, resp.
\mathrm{Desc}\left([\right)\pi]
, be its number of empty rows, resp. descents. Again, we proceed by induction on the size
n
of the label set
\lambda
. The base case
n=0
is trivial. For
n\geq 1
, suppose we have shown that
\mathrm{EmptyRow}\left([\right)\mathcal{T}^{\prime}]=1+\mathrm{Desc}\left([\right)\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})]
for all
\lambda^{\prime}
of size at most
n-1
and
\lambda^{\prime}
-labelled CNATs
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.

Fix some label set
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
and
\lambda
-labelled CNAT
\mathcal{T}
.

•

If
\mathcal{T}
has a unique internal dot in its top-row, let
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be the top-row deletion of
\mathcal{T}
. Then
\mathrm{EmptyRow}\left([\right)\mathcal{T}]=\mathrm{EmptyRow}\left([\right)\mathcal{T}^{\prime}]=1+\mathrm{Desc}\left([\right)\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})]
by induction. But by construction
\Psi(\mathcal{T})=\lambda_{1}\!\cdot\!\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})
with
\lambda_{1}=\min\lambda
, which immediately implies
\mathrm{Desc}\left([\right)\Psi(\mathcal{T})]=\mathrm{Desc}\left([\right)\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})]
, and the desired result follows.

•

Otherwise, write
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}^{\ell}\oplus\mathcal{T}^{r}
for the top-row decomposition of
\mathcal{T}
. By construction we have
\Psi(\mathcal{T})=\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{r})\!\cdot\!\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\ell})
. Moreover, we know from the previous part of the proof that the first element of
\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{r})
, which is also its smallest element, is strictly greater than all the left-to-right minima of
\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\ell})
, and thus in particular than its first element. This implies that there is a descent between the last element of
\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{r})
and the first element of
\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\ell})
, so that
\mathrm{Desc}\left([\right)\Psi(\mathcal{T})]=\mathrm{Desc}\left([\right)\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{r})]+1+\mathrm{Desc}\left([\right)\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\ell})]
. Applying the induction hypothesis to
\mathcal{T}^{r}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\ell}
, this yields:

\displaystyle\mathrm{EmptyRow}\left([\right)\mathcal{T}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{EmptyRow}\left([\right)\mathcal{T}^{r}]+\mathrm{EmptyRow}\left([\right)\mathcal{T}^{\ell}]

\displaystyle=1+\mathrm{Desc}\left([\right)\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{r})]+1+\mathrm{Desc}\left([\right)\Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\ell})]

\displaystyle=1+\mathrm{Desc}\left([\right)\Psi(\mathcal{T})],

as desired. This completes the proof of the theorem.

∎

4.
Counting CNATs according to their associated permutations

In
[
11
]
the authors were interested in the numbers
\mathrm{cnat}\left([\right)\pi]
enumerated in Theorem
2.12
. More specifically, they were interested in how many permutations
\pi
had the same fixed number of associated CNATs. For
n,k\geq 1
, we define

B(n,k):=\{\pi\in S_{n};\,\mathrm{cnat}\left([\right)\pi]=k\}

to be the set of
n
-permutations associated with exactly
k
CNATs, and
b(n,k):=|B(n,k)|
to be the number of such permutations. For brevity we will simply say that a permutation
\pi\in B(n,k)

has

k
CNATs, and refer to elements of
B(n,k)
as permutations
with
(exactly)
k
CNATs. The main goal of this section is to prove a number of conjectures on the enumeration sequence
\big(b(n,k)\big)_{n,k\geq 1}
from
[
11
]
.

4.1.
Linking permutations with one and two CNATs

In this part, we establish a bijection between
marked
permutations with a single CNAT, and permutations with
2
CNATs (Theorem
4.3
). We begin by describing the set
B(n,1)
of permutations with a single CNAT. In
[
11
]
, the authors gave a characterisation of this set in terms of so-called
L-subsets
. We first reformulate their characterisation in terms of
quadrants
. For an (irreducible)
n
-permutation
\pi
, we let
\Pi=\{(i,\pi_{i});i\in[n]\}
be the points in the plot of
\pi
. Given an index
k\in\{2,\cdots,n\}
, we partition
\Pi
into four quadrants, called
k
-quadrants when the index
k
needs to be explicit, as follows.

•

The upper-left quadrant is
\Pi_{<k,<k}:=\Pi\cap\Big([1,k-1]\times[1,k-1]\Big)
.

•

The lower-left quadrant is
\Pi_{<k,\geq k}:=\Pi\cap\Big([1,k-1]\times[k,n]\Big)
.

•

The upper-right quadrant is
\Pi_{\geq k,<k}:=\Pi\cap\Big([k,n]\times[1,k-1]\Big)
.

•

The lower-right quadrant is
\Pi_{\geq k,\geq k}:=\Pi\cap\Big([k,n]\times[k,n]\Big)
.

below, with
\pi=561243
and

.

Note that since

k-1

(2)

\left|\Pi_{<k,<k}\right|+\left|\Pi_{<k,\geq k}\right|=\left|\Pi_{<k,<k}\right|+\left|\Pi_{\geq k,<k}\right|=k-1,

so that
\left|\Pi_{<k,\geq k}\right|=\left|\Pi_{\geq k,<k}\right|
. Moreover, this number must be strictly positive, since otherwise
\pi
would induce a permutation on
[k-1]
, and therefore be reducible. In words, given an (irreducible)
n
-permutation
\pi
, and an index
k\in\{2,\cdots,n\}
, the lower-left and upper-right quadrants are non-empty, and have the same number of dots.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
. We say that an
n
-permutation
\pi
satisfies the
quadrant condition
if

(3)

\forall k\in\{2,\cdots,n\},\ \left|\Pi_{<k,\geq k}\right|=\left|\Pi_{\geq k,<k}\right|=1.

That is, a permutation satisfies the quadrant condition when every lower-left quadrant (equivalently upper-right quadrant) has exactly one dot.

We now state our characterisation of the set
B(n,1)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\pi
be a permutation. Then
\pi
has a unique CNAT if, and only if,
\pi
satisfies the quadrant condition and has no fixed point.

Proof.

Let
\pi
be an
n
-permutation. For
j\in[n]
, we define the
j
-th
L-subset

L_{j}
of a permutation
\pi
by
L_{j}:=\Pi_{<j+1,<j+1}\setminus\Pi_{<j,<j}
. Therorem 3.11 in
[
11
]
states that a permutation has a unique CNAT if, and only if,
\pi
has no fixed point,
|L_{1}|=0
,
|L_{n}|=2
, and
|L_{j}|=1
for all
j\in\{2,\cdots,n-1\}
. We show that this is equivalent to
\pi
having no fixed point and satisfying the quadrant condition.

Suppose first that
\pi
satisfies the above conditions on its L-subsets, and fix some
k\in\{2,\cdots,n\}
. Then we have
\left|\Pi_{<k,<k}\right|=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}|L_{j}|=k-2
. By Equation (
2
) this implies that
\left|\Pi_{<k,\geq k}\right|=\left|\Pi_{\geq k,<k}\right|=k-1-\left|\Pi_{<k,<k}\right|=1
. Thus
\pi
satisfies the quadrant condition as desired.

Conversely, suppose that
\pi
satisfies the quadrant condition, and has no fixed point. Owing to the fact that
\pi
is irreducible, we have
|L_{1}|=0
(we cannot have
\pi_{1}=1
). Moreover there is always exactly one dot in the rightmost column
n
and exactly one dot in the bottom-most row
n
, which both belong to the L-subset
L_{n}
. Again,
\pi
is irreducible, so these dots must be distinct (we cannot have
\pi_{n}=n
, leading to the equality
|L_{n}|=2
. It therefore remains to show that

(4)

\forall j\in\{2,\cdots,n-1\},\,|L_{j}|=1.

Seeking contradiction, suppose that this is not the case. Because
\pi
is a permutation, there are
n
dots in total in
\Pi
, and therefore
(n-2)
dots in
\bigcup\limits_{j=2}^{n-1}L_{j}
. Thus if the L-condition (
4
) is not satisfied, there must exist
j\in\{2,\cdots,n-1\}
such that the
j
-th L-subset
L_{j}
is empty. In that case, the dot in the column
j
will be placed below row
j
, i.e. in column
j
of the lower-left quadrant
\Pi_{<j+1,\geq j+1}
. Now since
L_{j}
is empty, each dot located in the first
(j-1)
columns should be placed either in the upper-left quadrant
\Pi_{<j,<j}
or the lower-left quadrant
\Pi_{<j+1,\geq j+1}
but before the column
j
. Because
\pi
is irreducible, there must be at most
(j-2)
dots in the upper-left quadrant
\Pi_{<j,<j}
, which means that there must be at least one dot placed in the first
(j-1)
columns of the lower-left quadrant
\Pi_{<j+1,\geq j+1}
, say in some column
j^{\prime}<j
. Hence, in the lower-left quadrant
\Pi_{<j+1,\geq j+1}
there are at least two dots: one in column
j^{\prime}
and one in column
j
. This contradicts the fact that
\pi
satisfies the quadrant condition. Therefore, the L-condition (
4
) is satisfied, and
\pi
has a unique CNAT by
[
11
, Theorem 3.11]
.
∎

For
n\geq 1
, and an index
j\in\{2,\cdots,n\}
, we now define the
insertion operation

\mathrm{Insert}_{[}j]:S_{n}\rightarrow S_{n+1}
as follows. Given a
n
-permutation
\pi
, we construct
\pi^{\prime}:=\mathrm{Insert}_{[}j](\pi)
by inserting a fixed point
j
into the
n
-permutation
\pi
, and increasing the labels of all letters

in the original permutation
\pi
by

. For example, if
\pi=521634
and

, then
\pi^{\prime}=62\textbf{3}1745
(fixed point in bold). It is straightforward to check that
\mathrm{Insert}_{[}j](\pi)
is irreducible if, and only if,
\pi
is irreducible.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Define a map
\Phi:\{2,\cdots,n\}\times S_{n}\rightarrow S_{n+1}
by
\Phi(j,\pi):=\mathrm{Insert}_{[}j](\pi)
for all
j\in\{2,\cdots,n\}
and
\pi\in S_{n}
. Then
\Phi
is a bijection from
\{2,\cdots,n\}\times B(n,1)
to
B(n+1,2)
.

Remark 4.4
.

The insertion index
j\in\{2,\cdots,n\}
can be thought of as
marking
the “space” between
\pi_{j-1}
and
\pi_{j}
in
\pi
, with the insertion operation corresponding to inserting a fixed point in the marked space. The product set
\{2,\cdots,n\}\times B(n,1)
can then be thought of as permutations with a single CNAT, marked in such a space. Hence the bijection of Theorem
4.3
can be interpreted as a bijection between marked
n
-permutations with a single CNAT, and
(n+1)
-permutations with two CNATs.

From
[
11

, we have

for any

. This formula is also implicit from the combination of Theorem

and
[

. Indeed, in that work the authors show that the number of

, and trees are the only simple graphs satisfying

(Lemma

). Together with Theorem

, this enumeration formula immediately implies the following, which answers in the affirmative the conjecture from
[

]

is given by Sequence A001787 in

.

Corollary 4.5
.

For any
n\geq 2
, we have
b(n,2)=(n-2)\cdot 2^{(n-3)}
.

Lemma 4.19

Let

-core

Proof.

Let

. Suppose that

has a cut-vertex

(i.e. removing

disconnects the graph), and let

(for some

Lemma 4.20

If

Proof.

A two-connected graph with at least

4.4.

Our final result in this section looks at the maximum value of

Theorem 4.24
.

For any

, we have

, and this maximum is achieved only for the decreasing permutation
\mathrm{dec}_{[}n]=n(n-1)\cdots 1
. In other words, we have
B(n,(n-1)!)=\{\mathrm{dec}_{[}n]\}
and

Proof.

That

is a consequence of Theorem
3.5
. We therefore need to show that if
\pi\neq\mathrm{dec}_{[}n]
, we have
\mathrm{cnat}\left([\right)\pi]<\mathrm{cnat}\left([\right){\mathrm{dec}_{[}n]}]
. For this, note that the permutation graph
G_{\mathrm{dec}_{[}n]}
associated with the decreasing permutation is the complete graph
K_{n}
on
n
vertices, and that
\mathrm{dec}_{[}n]
is the only permutation whose graph is the complete graph.
Now for
\pi\neq\mathrm{dec}_{[}n]
, the permutation graph
G_{\pi}
is a graph on
n

K_{n}
can therefore be obtained from

through a non-empty sequence of edge additions. By Lemma
2.7
, this implies that
t_{G_{\pi}}<t_{K_{n}}=t_{G_{\mathrm{dec}_{[}n]}}
. The result then follows from Theorem
2.12
.
∎

Conclusion and future perspectives

In this paper, we deepened the combinatorial studies of CNATs initiated in the literature (see e.g.
[
3
,
11
,
2
]
) by exploiting a connection between CNATs with a given permutation and the Tutte polynomial of the corresponding permutation graph that appeared (in equivalent form) in
[
13
]
. In Theorem
3.5
, we provided a new bijection between permutations and so-called upper-diagonal CNATs (CNATs whose associated permutation is the decreasing permutation
n(n-1)\cdots 1
), which has the added benefit of preserving certain statistics of these objects. This bijection is defined recursively, based on two operations on labelled CNATs called top-row decomposition and top-row deletion.

We then investigated the enumeration of permutations with a given number of CNATs, answering a number of conjectures from
[
11
]
. We gave separate characterisations of permutations with exactly
1
,
2
or
3
CNATs in terms of so-called
quadrants
of the permutation’s plot, and in terms of permutation patterns. This allowed us to establish bijections between marked permutations with a single CNAT and permutations with
2
CNATs (Theorem
4.3
), and between permutations with
2
CNATs and permutations with
3
CNATs (Theorem
4.16
). From this we obtained enumerative formulas for permutations with
2
or
3
CNATs (Corollaries
4.5
and
4.17
). Finally, we showed that there are no permutations of any length with exactly
5
CNATs (Theorem
4.18
), and that the maximal number of CNATs associated with a given permutation of length
n
is
(n-1)!
, achieved uniquely for the decreasing permutation
n(n-1)\cdots 1
.

We end this paper with some possible directions for future research.

•

Find a more “direct” combinatorial proof of the fact that there are no permutations with exactly
5
CNATs, as explained in Problem
4.23
.

•

Investigate the enumerative sequences
\big(b(n,k)\big)_{n\geq 1}
for more values of
k
. In this paper, we have looked at the cases
k=1,2,3,5,(n-1)!
. The paper
[
11
]
suggests that the entries for
k=4,6,7
do indeed appear in the OEIS
[
17
]
. As explained in Remark
4.25
, this is not the case for general factorial values of
k
, so it may be that these specific values essentially rely on there being relatively few permutation graphs with the specified acyclic orientation number. For instance, it seems plausible that the only graphs with acyclic orientation number equal to
4
are the two graphs made up of two triangles in Figures
13
and
18(a)
with some trees attached. It also would be particularly interesting to know if there are values of
k
other than
5
for which
b(n,k)
is always
0
.

•

One observation from
[
11
, Table 1]
which lists the first few values of
\big(b(n,k)\big)
is that there are very few values for which
b(n,k)
is odd, other than the
1
’s at the right-hand end of each row implied by Theorem
4.24
. This is also true in our Table
1
above:
b(6,24)=31
is the only other odd value here. Can this observation be quantified in some way?

•

A final direction of future research could be to restrict ourselves to certain subsets of permutations, for example
derangements
(permutations with no fixed point). Fixed points seem to play an important role in the enumeration of permutations according to their number of associated CNATs, since a fixed point implies the existence of a
321
pattern, which in turn means a
3
