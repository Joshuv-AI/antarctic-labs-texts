Abstract
. We first show that in the function realizability topos 𝖱𝖳 ⁡ ( 𝒦 2 ) \mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2}) every metric space is separable, and every object with decidable equality is countable. More generally, working with synthetic topology, every T 0 T_{0} -space is separable and every discrete space is countable. It follows that intuitionistic logic does not show the existence of a non-separable metric space, or an uncountable set with decidable equality, even if we assume principles that are validated by function realizability, such as Dependent and Function choice, Markov’s principle, and Brouwer’s continuity and fan principles.

Are there any uncountable sets with decidable equality in constructive mathematics, or at
least non-separable metric spaces? We put these questions to rest by showing that in the
function realizability topos all metric spaces are separable, and consequently all sets
with decidable equality countable. Therefore, intuitionistic logic does not show existence
of non-separable metric spaces, even if we assume principles that are validated by
function realizability, among which are the Dependent and Function choice, Markov’s
principle, and Brouwer’s continuity and fan principles.

1.
Function realizability

We shall work with the realizability topos
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
, see
[

,
which is based on Kleene’s function realizability
[

]
. We carry out the
bulk of the argument in the internal language of the topos, which is intuitionistic logic

We write
\mathsf{N}
and
\mathsf{R}
for the objects of the natural numbers and the real numbers,
respectively. The Baire space is the object
\mathsf{B}=\mathsf{N}^{\mathsf{N}}
of infinite number
sequences. It is metrized by the metric
u:\mathsf{B}\times\mathsf{B}\to\mathsf{R}
defined by

u(\alpha,\beta)=\lim_{n\to\infty}2^{-\min\{k\leq n\mid k=n\lor\alpha_{k}\neq\beta_{k}\}}.

If the first index at which
\alpha
and
\beta
differ is
k

u(\alpha,\beta)=2^{-k}
.

Proposition 1
.

The realizability topos
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
validates the following principles:

(1)

Countable choice:
a total relation on
\mathsf{N}
has a choice map.

(2)

Extended function choice:
if
S\subseteq\mathsf{B}
is
\lnot\lnot

(3)

Extended continuity principle:
if
S\subseteq\mathsf{B}

S\to\mathsf{B}
is continuous.

(4)

Excluded middle for predicates on
\mathsf{N}
: if
\phi(n)

n\in\mathsf{N}
, then
\forall n\in\mathsf{N}\,.\,\phi(n)\lor\lnot\phi(n)

Proof 1.1
.

(1)

Every realizability topos validates Countable choice, and

[
8

\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
.

(2)

Recall that a subobject
S\subseteq\mathsf{B}
is
\lnot\lnot
-stable when

\lnot\lnot(\alpha\in S)
implies
\alpha\in S
for all
\alpha\in\mathsf{B}
. The
realizability relation on such an
S
is inherited by that of
\mathsf{B}
, i.e., the
elements of
S
are realized by Kleene’s associates. The argument proceeds the same
way as
[
8
, Prop. 4.3.2]
, which shows that choice holds in the case

S=\mathsf{B}
.

(3)

Once again, if
S\subseteq\mathsf{B}
is
\lnot\lnot
-stable, then maps
S\to\mathsf{B}

are realized by Kleene’s associates, just like maps from
\mathsf{B}\to\mathsf{B}
. The argument
proceeds the same way as continuity of maps
\mathsf{B}\to\mathsf{B}

in
[
8
, Prop. 4.3.4]
.

(4)

Let us first show that, for a formula
\psi(n)
whose only parameter is

n\in\mathsf{N}

\forall n\in\mathsf{N}\,.\,\lnot\lnot\psi(n)\Rightarrow\psi(n)

(1)

is realized. The formula
\psi(n)
is interpreted as a subobject of
\mathsf{N}
, which is
represented by a map

. Using a bit of (external) classical
logic and Countable choice we obtain a map
c:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\,.\,f(n)\neq\emptyset\Rightarrow c(n)\in f(n),

which says that
c
can be used to build a realizer for (
1
).

Now, given a formula
\phi(n)
whose only parameter is
n\in\mathsf{N}
, take
\psi(n)
to
be
\phi(n)\lor\lnot\phi(n)
. Because
\lnot\lnot(\phi(n)\lor\lnot\phi(n))

holds, (
1

\forall n\in\mathsf{N}\,.\,\phi(n)\lor\lnot\phi(n)
.

Note that the last part of the previous proposition does
not
state the
validity of the internal statement

\forall\phi\in\Omega^{\mathsf{N}}\,.\,\forall n\in\mathsf{N}\,.\,\phi(n)\lor\lnot\phi(n).

Indeed, such a statement cannot be valid in any realizability topos because it implies
excluded middle (given
p\in\Omega
, consider
\phi(n)\equiv p
). Rather, we have a

schema
which holds for each formula
\phi(n)
.

2.
Metric spaces in function realizability

Henceforth we argue in the internal language of
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
. A minor but common
complication arises because the internal language does not allow quantification
over all objects of the topos. Thus, when we make a statement
\phi_{(X,d)}

about all metric spaces
(X,d)
in the topos, this is to be understood
schematically: given any object
X
and morphism
d:X\times X\to\mathsf{R}
, if
the topos validates “
d
is a metric” then it also validates
\phi_{(X,d)}
.

A consequence of Countable choice is that the object of reals
\mathsf{R}

is a continuous image of
\mathsf{B}
, for instance by composing any bijection

\mathsf{B}\cong\mathsf{Z}^{\mathsf{N}}
with the surjection

\mathsf{Z}^{\mathsf{N}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathsf{R}
defined by taking

\alpha\in\mathsf{Z}^{\mathsf{N}}
to

\alpha_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k}\cdot\max(-1,\min(1,\alpha_{k+1}))
.

We follow the convention that a set
X
is
countable
if there
exists a surjection
\mathsf{N}\twoheadrightarrow X+1
, and we refer to such a
surjection as an
enumeration
of
X
. Note in particular that
the empty set is countable, and that if
X
is inhabited, then it is
countable if and only if there is a surjection
\mathsf{N}\twoheadrightarrow X
.

Recall that a metric space
(X,d)
is
separable
if there exists a countable subset

D\subseteq X
such that, for all
x\in X
and
k\in\mathsf{N}
, there exists
y\in D
such
that
d(x,y)\leq 2^{-k}
.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose
(X,d_{X})
is a separable metric space and
q:X\twoheadrightarrow Y
is a surjection onto
a metric space
(Y,d_{Y})
such that
d_{Y}\circ(q\times q):X\times X\to\mathsf{R}
is
continuous with respect to the metric
d_{X}
. Then
Y
is separable.

Proof 2.1
.

Let
D\subseteq X
be a countable dense subset of
X
. We claim that its image
q(D)

is dense in
Y
. Consider any
y\in Y
and
k\in\mathsf{N}
. There is
x\in X
such that

q(x)=y
. Because
d_{Y}\circ(q\times q)
is continuous at
(x,x)
there exists

m\in\mathsf{N}
such that, for all
z\in X
, if
d_{X}(x,z)\leq 2^{-m}
then

d_{Y}(q(x),q(z))=|d_{Y}(q(x),q(z))-d_{Y}(q(x),q(x))|\leq 2^{-k}.

Since
D
is dense there exists
z\in D
such that

d_{X}(x,z)\leq 2^{-m}
, hence
d_{Y}(y,q(z))=d_{Y}(q(x),q(z))\leq 2^{-k}
.

Proposition 3
.

For any subobject
S\rightarrowtail\mathsf{B}
, the topos
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
validates the statement that
(S,u)

is a separable metric space.

Proof 2.2
.

Let
\mathsf{N}^{*}
be the object of finite sequences of numbers. We write
|a|
for the
length
n
of a sequence
a=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})
. For
\alpha\in\mathsf{B}
and

k\in\mathsf{N}
, let
\alpha{k}=(\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{k-1})
be the prefix
of
\alpha
of length
k
. Given a finite sequence
a=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})
, let

a0^{\omega}
be its padding by zeroes,

a0^{\omega}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1},0,0,0,\ldots).

Notice that
u(\alpha{k}0^{\omega},\alpha)\leq 2^{-k}
for all

\alpha\in\mathsf{B}
and
k\in\mathsf{N}
.

Because
\mathsf{N}^{*}
is isomorphic to
\mathsf{N}
, we may apply Excluded middle for predicates
on
\mathsf{N}
to establish

\forall a\in\mathsf{N}^{*}\,.\,(\exists\alpha\in S\,.\,u(a0^{\omega},\alpha)\leq 2^{-|a|})\lor\lnot(\exists\alpha\in S\,.\,u(a0^{\omega},\alpha)\leq 2^{-|a|}).

By Countable choice there is a map
c:\mathsf{N}^{*}\to S+1
such that, for all

a\in\mathsf{N}^{*}
, if there exists
\alpha\in S
with

u(a0^{\omega},\alpha)\leq 2^{-|a|}
then
c(a)\in S
and

u(a0^{\omega},c(a))\leq 2^{-|a|}
. We claim that
c
enumerates a dense sequence
in
S
. To see this, consider any
\alpha\in S
and
k\in\mathsf{N}
. Because

u(\alpha{k+1}0^{\omega},\alpha)\leq 2^{-k-1}
, we have

c(\alpha{k+1})\in S
and therefore

u(\alpha,c(a{k+1}))\leq\\
u(\alpha,a{k+1}0^{\omega})+u(a{k+1}0^{\omega},c(a{k+1}))\leq 2^{-k-1}+2^{-k-1}=2^{-k}.

Proposition 4

A metric space is separable if its carrier is the quotient of a
\lnot\lnot
-stable

\mathsf{B}

Proof 2.3

Suppose

By Proposition

Theorem 5
.

Every metric space in
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
is separable.

Proof 2.4
.

Consider a metric space
(X,d)
in
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
. The object of reals
\mathsf{R}
is modest because
it has
\lnot\lnot
-stable equality and is a quotient of
\mathsf{B}
. Its power
\mathsf{R}^{X}
is
modest, and because the transpose of the metric
\widetilde{d}:X\rightarrowtail\mathsf{R}^{X}

Theorem 6
.

In
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
every object with decidable equality is countable.

Proof 2.5
.

The precise statement is: for any object
X
in
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
,
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
validates the statement
“if
X
has decidable equality then
X
is countable”.

We argue internally. If equality on
X
is decidable then we may define the discrete
metric
d:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
by

d(x,y)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $x=y$,}\\
1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Because
(X,d)
is separable it contains a countable dense subset
D\subseteq X
. But
then
D=X
, because for any
x\in X
, there is
y\in D
such that
d(x,y)<1/2
,
which implies
x=y
.

The upshot of Theorems
5

and
6
is that in constructive mathematics a
non-separable metric space cannot be constructed, and neither can an uncountable
set with decidable equality, for such constructions could be interpreted in

\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
to give counterexamples to the theorems.

The theorems fail for
families
of objects. For instance, while for any specific
subobject
S\rightarrowtail\mathsf{N}
the statement “
S
is countable” is valid, the internal
statement “every subobject of
\mathsf{N}
is countable” is invalid. It is a variation of

Kripke’s schema

[
7
]
which together with Markov’s principle
implies Excluded middle, as follows.

Proposition 7
.

If every subobject of
\mathsf{N}
is countable and Markov’s principle holds, then Excluded
middle holds as well.

Proof 2.6
.

Let us show that the stated assumptions imply that every truth value
p
is

\lnot\lnot
-stable. From an enumeration of the subobject
\{n\in\mathsf{N}\mid p\}
,
which exists by assumption, we may construct
f:\mathsf{N}\to\{0,1\}
such that
p
is
equivalent to
\exists n\in\mathsf{N}\,.\,f(n)=1
. Markov’s principle says that such a
statement is
\lnot\lnot
-stable.

Corollary 8
.

The statement “not every subobject of
\mathsf{N}
is countable” is
valid in
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
.

Proof 2.7
.

A realizability topos validates Markov’s principle and the negation
of the law of excluded middle.

3.
Intrinsic
T_{0}
-spaces are separable in function realizability

We now generalize our result to
synthetic topology
. We refer the reader
to
[
1
,
4
,
2
]

for comprehensive accounts of synthetic topology, and recall only those concepts
that are needed for our results.
The
Rosolini dominance

[
6
]
is the
subobject of
\Omega
, defined by

\mathsf{\Sigma}=\{p:\Omega\mid\exists\alpha:\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{N}}\,.\,p\Leftrightarrow(\exists n\in\mathsf{N}\,.\,\alpha_{n}=1)\}.

The object
\mathsf{\Sigma}
is modest and can be thought of as an analogue of the

Sierpiński space
, which classifies open subspaces in the category of
topological spaces and continuous maps. This observation is the starting point
of synthetic topology, where the exponential
\mathsf{\Sigma}^{X}
is taken to be the

intrinsic topology
of an object
X
. Thus a subobject of
X
is

intrinsically open
when its characteristic map
X\to\Omega
factors
through the inclusion
\mathsf{\Sigma}\rightarrowtail\Omega
. We call the elements of
\mathsf{\Sigma}

the
open
truth values.

With regards to metric spaces, a fundamental question is how the metric and
intrinsic topologies relate. Because the relation
<
on
\mathsf{R}
is intrinsically
open
[
1
, Prop. 2.1]
, every metric open ball is intrinsically
open. We note that the converse holds for
\lnot\lnot
-stable subobjects
of
\mathsf{B}
.

Proposition 9
.

Every intrinsically open subspace of a
\lnot\lnot
-stable subobject

S\rightarrowtail\mathsf{B}
is a union of open balls.

Proof 3.1
.

The object
\mathsf{\Sigma}
is a quotient of
\mathsf{B}
by the map

\beta\mapsto(\exists n\,.\,{\beta(n)=1})
.
Consider an intrinsically open subset
U\subseteq S
, with characteristic map

u:S\to\mathsf{\Sigma}
. By Extended function choice
u
factors through the
quotient
\mathsf{B}\to\mathsf{\Sigma}
as a map
\overline{u}:S\to\mathsf{B}
. Moreover, by
the Extended continuity principle the map
\overline{u}
is continuous.
Suppose
\alpha\in S
and
u(\alpha)=\top
. There is
n\in\mathsf{N}
such that

\overline{u}(\alpha)(n)=1
. By continuity of
\overline{u}
there is an
open ball
B(\alpha,r)
centered at
\alpha
such that, for all

\beta\in B(\alpha,r)
, we have
\overline{u}(\beta)(n)=1
, therefore

\alpha\in B(\alpha,r)\subseteq U
, as required.

Many standard topological notions may be formulated in synthetic topology. For
instance, an object
X
is an
(intrinsic)
T_{0}
-space
when the transpose

X\to\mathsf{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{\Sigma}^{X}}
of the evaluation map
\mathsf{\Sigma}^{X}\times X\to\mathsf{\Sigma}

is a monomorphism, i.e., when two points in
X
are equal if they have the same
open neighborhoods. Metric spaces are
T_{0}
-spaces because metric open balls are
intrinsically open.

In classical topology arbitrary unions of opens are open, but this is not so in
synthetic topology. We say that
I
is
overt
if
\mathsf{\Sigma}
is closed under

I
-indexed unions. In logical form overtness says that for every

u:I\to\mathsf{\Sigma}
the truth value
\exists i\in I\,.\,u(i)
is open. Because

\mathsf{\Sigma}
is a lattice, Kuratowski-finite objects are overt. The natural numbers

\mathsf{N}
are overt as well, by an application of Countable choice.

In general overtness transfers along
(intrinsically) dense
maps, which
are maps
f:X\to Y
such that, for all
u:Y\to\mathsf{\Sigma}
,

(\exists y\in Y\,.\,u(y))\iff(\exists x\in X\,.\,u(f(x))).

Clearly, if
X
is overt and
f:X\to Y
is dense then
Y
is overt.

Say that
X
is
(intrinsically) separable
when there exists a dense map

\mathsf{N}\to X
. Because
\mathsf{N}
is overt, all intrinsically separable objects are
overt.

Lemma 10
.

Every
\lnot\lnot
-stable subobject of
\mathsf{B}
is intrinsically separable.

Proof 3.2
.

By Proposition
9
, the intrinsic and
metric topologies of a
\lnot\lnot
-stable subobject
S\rightarrowtail\mathsf{B}
agree,
hence so do both kinds of separability. In
Proposition
3
we showed that
S
is metrically
separable.

As noted earlier, every metric space is a
T_{0}
-space, and intrinsic
separability implies metric separability. Thus, the following is a
generalization of Theorem
5
.

Theorem 11
.

In
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
, every intrinsically
T_{0}
-space is intrinsically separable.

Proof 3.3
.

Suppose
X
is a
T_{0}
-space. Then it is a subobject of the modest object

\mathsf{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{\Sigma}^{X}}
, and so it is also modest. Hence there exists a surjection

q:S\twoheadrightarrow X
from a
\lnot\lnot
-stable subobject
S\rightarrowtail\mathsf{B}
. By
Lemma
10
there is a dense map

f:\mathsf{N}\to S
. But a dense map followed by a surjection is dense, and so

q\circ f
witnesses intrinsic separability of
X
.

Finally, a generalization of Theorem
6
is readily
available. An object
X
is
(intrinsically) discrete
when the equality
relation on
X
is open, or equivalently, when every singleton in
X
is open.

Theorem 12
.

In
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
, every intrinsically discrete space is countable.

Proof 3.4
.

An intrinsically discrete space
X
is a
T_{0}
-space because in
X
singletons
are open. By Theorem
11
there is a dense map

f:\mathsf{N}\to X
, which must be a surjection because, again, singletons are
open.

4.
Conclusion

In view of our results, it is natural to wonder what goes wrong
intuitionistically with the classical non-separable spaces, such as

\ell^{\infty}
and
L^{\infty}[0,1]
. Do they somehow become separable? One of
several things can happen. In
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
every map
[0,1]\to\mathsf{R}
is uniformly
continuous so that
L^{\infty}[0,1]
is just the space of uniformly continuous
maps with the supremum norm, which of course is separable. On the other hand,

\ell^{\infty}
cannot even be constructed, at least not using the classical
definition of the norm

\|a\|_{\infty}=\sup_{n\in\mathsf{N}}|a_{n}|.

For a bounded
a:\mathsf{N}\to\mathsf{R}
the supremum need not exist, so that further
restrictions on
a
are required. The most generous attempt would collect into

\ell^{\infty}
all the sequences for which
\|a\|_{\infty}
exists — but doing
so would break the vector space structure, and consequently the definition of
the metric. We could also observe that
\|a\|_{\infty}
is a well-defined

lower
real number, i.e., we may give it as a lower Dedekind cut. How much
of the mathematics of
\ell^{\infty}
, and similar spaces, one can recover this
way was studied by Fred Richman
[
5
]
,

Lastly, we remark that the results are quite closely tied to
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
because of
the non-computable nature of the Excluded middle for predicates on
\mathsf{N}
from
Proposition
1
(apply the principle to the statement
“the
n
-th Turing machine halts” to obtain the halting oracle).
A close cousin of
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
is the Kleene-Vesley topos, which is defined
as the relative realizability topos on the partial combinatory subalgebra
of
\mathcal{K}_{2}
consisting of the computable functions
[
8
, §4.5]
.
Because in this topos all statements must be realized by computable maps, the
last part of the proof of Proposition
1
fails. Indeed, an
uncountable object with decidable equality is readily available, just take a
subset of
\mathbb{N}
which is not computably enumerable, and therefore not countable
internally to the topos.
