Limit Profile for Projections of Random Walks on Groups
Evita Nestoridi, Sam Olesker-Taylor

Abstract
Establishing cutoff—an abrupt transition from ‘not mixed’ to ‘well mixed’—is a classical topic in the theory of mixing times for Markov chains. Interest has grown recently in determining not only the existence of cutoff and the order of its mixing time and window, but the exact shape , or profile , of the convergence inside the window. Classical techniques, such as coupling or ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -bounds, are typically too crude to establish this and there has been a push to develop general techniques [ Tey20 , NO22 , Nes24 ] . We build upon this work, extending from conjugacy-invariant random walks on groups to certain projections. We exemplify our method by analysing the k k -particle interchange process on the complete n n -graph with k ≍ n k\asymp n . This is a projection of the random-transposition card shuffle, which corresponds to k = n k=n , analysed in [ Tey20 ] .

1 Introduction

Developing a theory for determining the limit profiles of Markov chains is a very recent topic in studying Markov chains.
The limit profiles of certain popular Markov chains have been determined a while ago: eg,
random walk on hypercube
[
DGM90
,
NO22
]
,
riffle shuffles
[
BD92
]
,
exclusion process on the circle
[
Lac16
]

and
Ramanujan graphs
[
LP16
]
.
Some more recent examples include
random transpositions and
k
-cycles
[
Tey20
,
NO22
]
,
star transpositions
[
Nes24
]
,
biased card shuffles
[
Zha24
]
,
quantum random transpositions
[
FTW22
]
,
some random walks on random Cayley graphs
[
HO21
]
,
the asymmetric exclusion process
[
BN22
]
,
urn models
[
NO22
]
,
repeated averages
[
Cha+22
]

and
a Moran model
[
Cor23
]
.
All these references are somewhat isolated, case-by-case proofs, without an underlying technique or theory.
In many cases, cutoff was known well before the profile.
This is partially an artefact of the diversity of profile functions:
some are
Poissonian
[
Tey20
]
or Gaussian
[
HO21
]
;
others involve more complicated distributions, such as
Tracy–Widom
[
Zha24
]
or free Meixner
[
FTW22
]
.

Rectifying this lack of general methodology, spectral techniques, which can be applied to a variety of chains, have been introduced recently
[
Tey20
,
NO22
,
Nes24
]
.

[
Tey20
]
kicked things off, introducing an approximation technique, applying it to prove that the total-variation distance to uniformity of the random-transposition shuffle is

d_{\mathsf{TV}}\bigl(\Pois(1+\mathrm{e}^{-c}),\>\Pois(1)\bigr)\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{after}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ after }}{\text{ after }}{\text{ after }}\tfrac{1}{2}n(\log n+c)\hskip 11.49994pt\text{steps asymptotically},

by extending Fourier-analytic techniques of
[
DS81
]
for conjugacy-invariant random walks (
RW

s).
We extended this from transpositions (
2
-cycles) to
k
-cycles
[
NO22
]
.
We extended the approximation technique beyond conjugacy-invariant
RW
s to so-called
Gelfand pairs
and applied it to the multi-urn Ehrenfest diffusion model
[
NO22
]
.
We also extended to reversible Markov chains and applied it to a statistical-physics Gibbs sampler.
A comparison method was introduced in

[
Nes24
]
,
allowing the limit profile to be deduced from a sufficiently similar chain,
and applied it to star transpositions.

The purpose of this paper is to set up a technique for finding the limit profile of a Markov chain which can be viewed as a projection of another.
Gelfand pairs mentioned above are a special case of this.
We apply the technique to the
k
-particle interchange process on the complete
n
-graph, which is a projection of the random-transposition shuffle.
Roughly, it is

d_{\mathsf{TV}}\bigl(\Pois\bigl(\tfrac{k}{n}+\mathrm{e}^{-c}\bigr),\>\Pois\bigl(\tfrac{k}{n}\bigr)\bigr)\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{after}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ after }}{\text{ after }}{\text{ after }}\tfrac{1}{2}n(\log k+c)\hskip 11.49994pt\text{steps asymptotically if}\hskip 11.49994pt\tfrac{k}{n}\asymp 1.

1.1
k
-Particle Interchange Process and Mixing Times

We study the limit profile of the
k
-particle interchange process
(
k
-
IP

).

Definition A
(
k
-IP )
.

Place
k\leq n
distinct particles on the vertices an
n
-graph
G
.
Each vertex gets at most one particle and therefore there are always
n-k
empty sites.
A single step of the
k
-particle interchange process

\mathsf{IP}(k,G)
dynamics chooses an edge uniformly at random:

\bm{\cdot}

if both endpoints have a particle,
then swap the positions of the two particles;

\bm{\cdot}

if only one endpoint does,
then move that particle to the other endpoint;

\bm{\cdot}

if neither endpoint has a particle,
then do nothing.

When
G=K_{n}
is the complete
n
-graph,
we abbreviate
\mathsf{IP}(k,K_{n})
as
k
-
IP
.
Our complete graphs always have self-loops.
Nothing happens upon selection of a self-loop:
it is a lazy step.

We write
\alpha\coloneqq k/n
for the proportion of sites which are occupied.
Our main result evaluates the limit profile for the
k
-
IP
on the complete
n
-graph

when
\alpha\asymp 1
:
it is

d_{\mathsf{TV}}\bigl(\Pois(\alpha+\mathrm{e}^{-c}),\>\Pois(\alpha)\bigr)\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{after}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ after }}{\text{ after }}{\text{ after }}\tfrac{1}{2}n(\log k+c)\hskip 11.49994pt\text{steps}.

The
k
-
IP
is a natural projection of the random-transposition shuffle:
the latter equivalent to
n
-
IP
and

k
-
IP
is obtained from the
n
-
IP
by following only
k
cards.

The objective of this paper is to introduce a method for analysing a
RW
on a space
X
which can be viewed as a projection of some simpler
RW
on a group
G
.
In particular, we study
RW
s on
homogeneous spaces

X=G/K
, where
K\leq G
is a subgroup of
G
.
We derive theory for general
(G,K)
.
For
k
-
IP
,

G=\mathfrak{S}_{n}

and

K=\mathfrak{S}_{n-k}\times\mathfrak{S}_{1}\times...\times\mathfrak{S}_{1}
.

We carefully define
mixing
,
cutoff
and
limit profiles
now. Let
\Omega
be a finite set and
P
a transition matrix on
\Omega
.
Then,
P^{t}(x,y)
is the probability of moving from
x
to
y
in
t
steps,
for all
x,y\in\Omega
and all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
P
is irreducible and aperiodic, then the basic limit theorem of Markov chains
(eg,
[
Nor97
, Theorem 1.8.3]
)
tells us that
P^{t}(x,\cdot)
converges to the (unique) invariant distribution
\pi
as
t\to\infty
with respect to the
total variation
(
TV

) distance
d_{\mathsf{TV}}
:

d_{\mathsf{TV}}\bigl(P^{t}(x,\cdot),\>\pi\bigr)\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{y\in\Omega}\displaystyle}{\sum_{y\in\Omega}}{\sum_{y\in\Omega}}{\sum_{y\in\Omega}}|P^{t}(x,y)-\pi(y)|\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{for}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ for }}{\text{ for }}{\text{ for }}x\in\Omega\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{and}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}t\in\mathbb{N};

ie,
TV
is half the
\ell_{1}
distance.
The (
worst-case
)
\varepsilon
-
mixing time
is then defined,

for
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
, by

t_{\textnormal{mix}}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\inf\bigl\{t\geq 0\bigm|d_{\mathsf{TV}}(t)\leq\varepsilon\bigr\}\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{where}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ where }}{\text{ where }}{\text{ where }}d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\cdot)\coloneqq\mathchoice{\textstyle\max_{x\in\Omega}\displaystyle}{\max_{x\in\Omega}}{\max_{x\in\Omega}}{\max_{x\in\Omega}}d_{\mathsf{TV}}(P^{t}(x,\cdot),\>\pi).

Take a sequence of Markov chains indexed by
N
,
and denote the
TV
distance for the
N
-th chain by
d_{\mathsf{TV}}^{(N)}(\cdot)
.
If there exists
(t_{\star}^{(N)},w_{\star}^{(N)})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}w_{\star}^{(N)}/t_{\star}^{(N)}=0
,

\mathop{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to-\infty}$}}\mathop{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}$}}\,d_{\mathsf{TV}}^{(N)}\bigl(t_{\star}^{(N)}+\alpha w_{\star}^{(N)}\bigr)=1\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{and}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}\mathop{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}$}}\mathop{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}$}}\,d_{\mathsf{TV}}^{(N)}\bigl(t_{\star}^{(N)}+\alpha w_{\star}^{(N)}\bigr)=0,

then the sequence exhibits

at

(t_{\star}^{(N)})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

with
window
order at most

(w_{*}^{(N)})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}.

The existence of cutoff is a fascinating question.
The
product condition
, requiring the product of the
spectral gap
and
mixing time
to diverge, is a necessary condition.
It was proved to be sufficient in
\ell_{p}
-distance mixing with
p>1
by
[
CS08
]
.
It is not sufficient in
TV
, ie
\ell_{1}
, in complete generality—see, eg,
[
LPW17
, Example 18.7]
—but is conjectured so be sufficient in many natural scenarios.
This leaves open the question of characterising the chains for which the product condition implies cutoff in
TV
.
The implication has been established for birth-and-death chains
[
DLP10
]
and, more generally, random walks on trees
[
BHP17
]
, as well as exclusion processes with reservoirs
[
Sal23b
]
.

Significant progress for general chains has been made by
[
Sal23
]
recently.
They gave a sufficient condition for cutoff, based on the concept of
varentropy
, and a condition for
non-negatively curved
Markov chains to satisfy this condition, in
[
Sal23
]
.
Very recently, they showed that this varentropy condition is in fact sharp on sparse expanders in
[
Sal23a
]
.

One can look beyond the cutoff time and window to determine the
limit profile
:

\varphi(\alpha)\coloneqq\mathop{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}$}}\,d_{\mathsf{TV}}^{(N)}\bigl(t_{\star}^{(N)}+\alpha w_{\star}^{(N)}\bigr)\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{for}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ for }}{\text{ for }}{\text{ for }}\alpha\in\mathbb{R},

if the limit exists.
There has been much less investigation into the limit profile than cutoff.

1.2 Limit Profile for
k
-IP on the Complete
n
-Graph

Cutoff for
k
-
IP
on
K_{n}
was established by Lacoin and Leblond
[
LL11
, Theorem 1.2]
whenever
1\ll k\ll\sqrt{n}
, with the correct window.
Their lower bound is valid for all
k
which,
combined with the well-known upper bound for
k=n
, establishes cutoff with the correct window whenever
k\asymp n
.
However, this approach is not refined enough to obtain the limit profile.

Theorem A
(Poisson Profile)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
Suppose that
(n_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(k_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
are diverging sequences of integers with
1\leq k_{N}\leq n_{N}
for all
N
and
k_{N}/n_{N}\to\alpha
as
N\to\infty
. Set

t_{c}(n,k)\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}n(\log k+c)=\tfrac{1}{2}n(\log n+c+\log(k/n))\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{for all}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ for all }}{\text{ for all }}{\text{ for all }}c\in\mathbb{R}.

Then, the
TV
distance
d_{\mathsf{TV}}^{(N)}(\cdot)
to equilibrium for
k_{N}
-
IP
on
K_{n_{N}}
at time
t_{c}(n_{N},k_{N})
satisfies

d_{\mathsf{TV}}^{(N)}\bigl(t_{c}(n_{N},k_{N})\bigr)\to d_{\mathsf{TV}}\bigl(\Pois(\alpha+\mathrm{e}^{-c}),\>\Pois(\alpha)\bigr)\hskip 11.49994pt\text{as $N\to\infty$}.

Remark A.

As with many of these processes based on random transpositions, the limit profile is the same as that observed by looking only at the number of fixed points—here, amongst the
k=\alpha n
particles.
Specifically, the probability that a particle is in its original place is

\bigl(1-(n-1)/\mathchoice{\textstyle\binom{n}{2}\displaystyle}{\binom{n}{2}}{\binom{n}{2}}{\binom{n}{2}}\bigr)^{t_{c}}+\tfrac{1}{n}+o(\tfrac{1}{n})=\tfrac{1}{n}\bigl(1+\mathrm{e}^{-c}/\alpha\bigr)+o(\tfrac{1}{n}).

The total number amongst the
k=\alpha n
labelled particles in the original place is then

\Pois\bigl(\alpha n\bigl(\tfrac{1}{n}(1+\mathrm{e}^{-c}/\alpha)+o(\tfrac{1}{n})\bigr)\bigr)\to\Pois(\alpha+\mathrm{e}^{-c}).

On the other hand, in a uniform permutation, it is
\Pois(\alpha)
.
This all assumes that
\alpha\asymp 1
.
∎

We believe that the profile is Gumbel when
1\ll k\ll n
.
This is a natural extension since

d_{\mathsf{TV}}\bigl(\Pois(e^{-c}),\>\Pois(0)\bigr)=\exp(-e^{c}),

which is the Gumbel tail, under the convention
\Pois(0)=0
.

Conjecture B
(Gumbel Profile)
.

Suppose that
(k_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(n_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
are diverging sequences of integers with
1\leq k_{N}\leq n_{N}
for all
N
and
k_{N}\to\infty
and
k_{N}/n_{N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Set

t_{c}(n,k)\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}n(\log k+c)\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{for}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ for }}{\text{ for }}{\text{ for }}c\in\mathbb{R}.

Then, the
TV
distance
d_{\mathsf{TV}}^{(N)}(\cdot)
to equilibrium for
k_{N}
-
IP
on
K_{n_{N}}
at time
t_{c}(n_{N},k_{N})
satisfies

d_{\mathsf{TV}}^{(N)}\bigl(t_{c}(n_{N},k_{N})\bigr)\to\exp(-e^{-c})\hskip 11.49994pt\text{as $N\to\infty$}.

Remark B.

Our argument should extend to
some

\alpha\ll 1
.
A number of the approximations become more technically challenging in this set-up and it is not clear how far the computations can be pushed.
More details are given in
4.23
in
Section

4.3.4
after the calculations.

If
k
is sufficiently small—specifically, if
k^{2}\log k\ll n
—then all
k
particles will jump to an (almost) uniform space independently before interacting with each other, with high probability.
The mixing time is thus the time to touch all the particles.

\bm{\cdot}

If
k\gg 1
, then this coupon-collector time concentrates, and has Gumbel fluctuations.

\bm{\cdot}

If
k\asymp 1
, then there are finitely many particles, and there is no concentration or cutoff.

In summary, the fluctuations should be Gumbel when
1\ll k\ll\sqrt{n/\log n}
or when
k
is sufficiently close to
n
.
We conjecture that they are Gumbel whenever
1\ll k\ll n
.
∎

The key trick in finding this limit profile is to couple
k
-
IP
with
n
-
IP
with random initial state.
From a representation-theoretic point of view, this allows us to delay evaluating certain complicated Fourier transforms for
k
-
IP
, rather working with the simpler ones for
n
-
IP
.

Obtaining only cutoff is possible working with
k
-
IP
directly; see
[
Sca97
]
.
Indeed, the traces are calculable as the Fourier transforms are still diagonal, but no longer multiples of the identity.
The profile requires more refined information on the diagonals than just the sum.
This extra information seems unmanageable.
Hence the need for the
k
-
IP
-to-
n
-
IP
simplification.

Remark 0.

The case
\alpha=1
, ie
n
-
IP
, is the random-transposition shuffle, analysed recently by
[
Tey20
]
.
The case
\alpha\to 1
is an easy deduction from the fixed-
\alpha
case.
In this case,

t_{c}(n,n)-t_{c}(n,k)=\tfrac{1}{2}n\log\alpha\ll n

and
n
is the order of the cutoff window.

TV
is monotone under projection and the
k^{\prime}
-
IP
can be obtained from the
k
-
IP
, with
k^{\prime}\leq k
, by forgetting about
k-k^{\prime}\geq 0
of the particles.
The claim follows by sandwiching the
k
-
IP
between
k^{\prime}
-
IP
and
n
-
IP
where
k^{\prime}\coloneqq\alpha^{\prime}n
for some fixed
\alpha^{\prime}<1
, arbitrarily close to
1
.
Herein, we assume that
\alpha
is a fixed element of
(0,1)
.
∎

1.3 Approximation Lemma for RWs on Homogeneous Spaces

Cutoff and the
\ell_{2}
limit-profile for the
k
-
IP
on
K_{n}
was established by
[
FJ17
]
by computing the entire spectrum.
The
\ell_{2}
distance requires only the eigenvalues, not the eigenfunctions.
Establishing the
TV
limit-profile requires the eigenfunctions.

[
Tey20
, Lemma 2.1]
introduced an approximation lemma for doing precisely this in the set-up on conjugacy-invariant
RW
s.
One of the main contributions of the current article is an extension of
[
Tey20
]
’s approximation lemma; see
B
.
We need to introduce some standard representation-theoretic terminology in order to set-up this lemma.

First and foremost, we introduce the most fundamental part of representation theory.

Definition B.1
(Representations)
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
V
a finite-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{C}
.
A

\rho

G

V
is an action

(g,v)\mapsto\rho(g)\cdot v:G\times V\to V

such that
\rho(g):V\to V
is an invertible, linear map for all
g\in G
.
Let

d_{\rho}\coloneqq\dim V
denote the
dimension

and

\chi_{\rho}(\cdot)\coloneqq\Tr(\rho(\cdot))
the
character

of
\rho
.
A representation
(\rho,V)
is

if its only
G
-invariant vector subspaces are trivial:

W\leq V
​ and
\rho(G)W\leq W
​ implies
W\in\{\{0\},V\}
.
Let

\widehat{G}
be the set of irreducible representations (

)
and
\widehat{G}^{\star}

A

of a subgroup
K

G
is of the form
gK=\{gk\mid k\in K\}
, for
g\in G
.
Left-cosets

g^{-1}h\in K
.
A set of

\{g_{1}K,...,g_{N}K\}

.

We are going to project
RW
s from a group

to a
homogeneous space
of left costs.

Definition B.2
(Homogeneous Space)
.

Let
G
be a group and
K\leq G

The

.
We associate

.
For
\widetilde{\nu},\widetilde{\nu_{0}}\in\mathcal{M}(X)
,
let
\mathsf{RW}(\widetilde{\nu},\widetilde{\nu_{0}})

X

\widetilde{\nu}
and started from
\widetilde{\nu_{0}}

\widetilde{\nu_{0}}*\widetilde{\nu}^{*t}

.

We require a way of lifting measures from the homogeneous space to the group.

Definition B.3
(Lifting and Conjugacy Invariance)
.

Let
\widetilde{\nu}\in\mathcal{M}(X)
.
Define
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(G)
by lifting

to

:

\nu(g)\coloneqq\widetilde{\nu}(x)/|K|

.
In particular,
\delta_{\mathsf{id}}\in\mathcal{M}(X)

.
Say that
\widetilde{\nu}
is

\nu

G
.
In this case, for a representation
\rho
of
G
, define the
character ratio

q_{\rho}\coloneqq d_{\rho}^{-1}\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{g\in G}\displaystyle}{\sum_{g\in G}}{\sum_{g\in G}}{\sum_{g\in G}}\nu(g)\chi_{\rho}(g).

We now state the main approximation lemma, which is used for finding the limit profile.

(Approximation Lemma)
.

S\subseteq\widehat{G}^{\star}
.
Let
\widetilde{\nu}\in\mathcal{M}(X)

K
-conjugacy invariant.
Let
U\sim\operatorname{Unif}(X)
and

.

3.2 Representation Theory for the Symmetric Group

The
k
-
IP
is fundamentally a projection of the random-transposition shuffle, which is a
RW
on the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
Representation theory for
G=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is well understood.

The set
\widehat{G}=\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
of irreps is indexed by partitions of
n
.
We abuse notation slightly by writing
\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
to denote the irrep canonically associated to the partition
\lambda\vdash n
of
n
.

The set
\mathfrak{T}_{n}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{n}
of transpositions is a conjugacy class, so the Fourier transform
\widehat{\mu_{\mathsf{RT}}}(\lambda)
is a multiple of the identity, by Schur’s lemma.
Taking traces, this multiple is
r_{\lambda}\coloneqq\chi_{\lambda}(\tau)/d_{\lambda}
, where
\tau\in\mathfrak{T}_{n}
is any transposition.
A simple-to-evaluate expression for

is known.

Lemma 3.4
(
[
CST08
, Theorem 10.6.2]
)
.

Let
\lambda\vdash n
.
Write
\lambda^{\prime}\vdash n
for the
transpose
of
\lambda
.
Then,

\textstyle r_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\in[n]}\lambda_{i}\bigl(\lambda_{i}-(2i-1)\bigr)=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i\in[n]}\bigl(\binom{\lambda_{i}}{2}-\binom{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}}{2}\bigr).

This is the fundamental building block for calculating the Fourier transform of our driving measures.
We also need some bounds on the dimensions of the irreducible representations.

Lemma 3.5.

Let
\lambda\vdash n
.
For a box
(i,j)
in the Young diagram of
\lambda
, let

h_{(i,j)}\coloneqq(\lambda_{i}-j)+(\lambda^{\prime}_{j}-i)+1

denote the
hook length
of the box
(i,j)
.
Then, the
hook-length formula
says that

d_{\lambda}=n!\big/\mathchoice{\textstyle\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}\displaystyle}{\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}}{\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}}{\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}}h_{(i,j)}.

In particular, writing
r\coloneqq n-\lambda_{1}
, we have

\mathchoice{\textstyle\binom{n}{r}\displaystyle}{\binom{n}{r}}{\binom{n}{r}}{\binom{n}{r}}d_{\lambda^{\star}}(1-2r/n)\cdot\bm{1}\{r\leq\tfrac{1}{2}n\}\leq d_{\lambda}\leq\mathchoice{\textstyle\binom{n}{r}\displaystyle}{\binom{n}{r}}{\binom{n}{r}}{\binom{n}{r}}d_{\lambda^{\star}}.

Proof.

The hook-length formula is a standard result; see, eg,
[
CST08
, Theorem 4.2.14]
.

The bounds follow from writing the quotient
d_{\lambda}/d_{\lambda^{\star}}
using the hook-length formula:

\frac{d_{\lambda}}{d_{\lambda^{\star}}}=\frac{(n)_{\lambda_{1}}}{\mathchoice{\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda_{1}}\displaystyle}{\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda_{1}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda_{1}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda_{1}}}(\lambda_{1}-i+\lambda^{\prime}_{i})}=\binom{n}{\lambda_{1}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda_{1}}\frac{\lambda_{1}-i+\lambda^{\prime}_{i}}{\lambda_{1}-i+1}.

Clearly,
\lambda^{\prime}_{i}\geq 1
for all

.
Thus,
d_{\lambda}\leq\binom{n}{r}d_{\lambda^{\star}}
.
On the other hand, to minimise the product, each term should be as close to equal as possible.
Hence, given
r=n-\lambda_{1}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}n
, it is minimised by
\lambda=(n-r,r)
:
then,
\lambda^{\star}=(r)
and
d_{\lambda^{\star}}=1
;
so,

d_{\lambda}\geq d_{(n-r,r)}=\binom{n}{r}\frac{n-2r+1}{n-r+1}=\binom{n}{r}\biggl(1-\frac{r}{n-r+1}\biggr)\geq\binom{n}{r}\biggl(1-\frac{2r}{n}\biggr).

The upper bound can also be proved combinatorially.
First, choose which
r=|\lambda^{\star}|
numbers are placed in
\lambda^{\star}
; then, place the remainder in the first row, in increasing order.
This gives an upper bound since not all enumerations will give rise to increasing columns.
∎

Calculating, or estimating, the multiplicities

is fundamentally important for bounding the error term. We recall that

is the multiplicity of the irrep
\lambda

-

.
In particular, the zero-multiplicity irreps can be ignored.

Definition 3.6
(Young Tableaux,

Let

Lemma 3.8
(Multiplicity for
k
-IP )
.

Let

and

.
Let

.

We must fill the Young diagram associated to

through

The

Using the Poisson approximations and known concentration results for hypergeometrics—they concentrate more strongly than the corresponding “with replacement” Binomial distribution
\Bin(A,B/n)
—we are able to well-approximate the relative
\ell_{1}
distance

.
We do this first, then determine precisely the laws of

|\{i\leq L_{\sigma}\mid\Pi(i)=i\}|\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{and}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}|\{i>m\mid\sigma(i)=i\}|,

showing that they are approximately Poisson.
Finally, we briefly justify the replacement of these by their approximations in the
\ell_{1}
-distance formula.

We first estimate the
\ell_{1}
distance from

and

Proposition 4.15.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and

.
Let

and

Let

and

Then,

\mathop{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}$}}\,\bigl|\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{X_{0}+Y_{0}}\mid L\bigr)-1\bigr|\bigr)-d_{1}\bigl(\Pois(\alpha(1+\beta)),\>\Pois(\alpha)\bigr)\bigr|=0.

The first corresponds primarily to the inner expectation. It is straightforward since there are no absolute value signs to cause difficulties.
We omit its simple proof: it goes via the usual moment-generating function (

Lemma 4.16.

Let

and

.
Let

Then,

\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\gamma)^{Z_{0}}\bigr)=\exp(\gamma z).

The next lemma is far more challenging technically.
We do not evaluate it exactly, but rather only up to a term which will be small when we average over

.
This is due to the fact that the averaging is being done
outside

Lemma 4.17.

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
with
0\leq m-L\leq n-m
.
Let
\lambda_{0}=1-\tfrac{m-L}{n-m}
and
Y_{0}\sim\Pois(\lambda_{0})

\lambda_{\star}\coloneqq 1-\tfrac{m}{n}=\tfrac{k}{n}
, ie the value of
\lambda_{0}
when

.
Let
\beta\in(0,\infty)
.
Then,

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta+\beta L/m}(1+\beta)^{Y_{0}}-1\bigr|\bigr)-d_{1}\bigl(\Pois(\lambda_{\star}(1+\beta)),\>\Pois(\lambda_{\star})\bigr)\bigr|

\displaystyle\hskip 22.99988pt\leq\mathrm{e}^{\beta^{2}}\bigl(\exp\bigl(\beta\tfrac{n}{n-m}\bigl|\tfrac{L}{m}-\tfrac{m}{n}\bigr|\bigr)-1\bigr)^{2}+\mathrm{e}^{\beta^{2}}\exp\bigl(\beta\tfrac{n}{n-m}\bigl|\tfrac{L}{m}-\tfrac{m}{n}\bigr|\bigr)\bigl(\exp\bigl(\beta^{2}|\tfrac{L}{m}-\tfrac{m}{n}|\bigr)-1\bigr).

Proof.

Let
Y_{\star}\sim\Pois(\lambda_{\star})
.
We ‘pivot’
L
around
m^{2}/n
to convert
Y_{0}
into
Y_{\star}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta+\beta L/m}(1+\beta)^{Y}-1\bigr|\bigr)-\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{Y_{\star}}-1\bigr|\bigr)

\displaystyle\hskip 22.99988pt=\mathchoice{\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{Y_{*}}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{\beta(L/m-m/n)}(1+\beta)^{Y_{0}-Y_{\star}}-1\bigr|-\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{Y_{\star}}-1\bigr|\bigr)}{\mathbb{E}(\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{Y_{*}}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{\beta(L/m-m/n)}(1+\beta)^{Y_{0}-Y_{\star}}-1\bigr|-\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{Y_{\star}}-1\bigr|)}{\mathbb{E}(\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{Y_{*}}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{\beta(L/m-m/n)}(1+\beta)^{Y_{0}-Y_{\star}}-1\bigr|-\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{Y_{\star}}-1\bigr|)}{\mathbb{E}(\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{Y_{*}}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{\beta(L/m-m/n)}(1+\beta)^{Y_{0}-Y_{\star}}-1\bigr|-\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{Y_{\star}}-1\bigr|)}

The expectation about which we are pivoting is easy to calculate explicitly:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{Y_{\star}}-1\bigr|\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{y\geq 0}\displaystyle}{\sum_{y\geq 0}}{\sum_{y\geq 0}}{\sum_{y\geq 0}}\tfrac{1}{y!}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{\star}}\lambda_{\star}^{y}\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{y}-1\bigr|

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{y\geq 0}\displaystyle}{\sum_{y\geq 0}}{\sum_{y\geq 0}}{\sum_{y\geq 0}}\bigl|\tfrac{1}{y!}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{\star}(1+\beta)}\bigl(\lambda_{\star}(1+\beta)\bigr)^{y}-\tfrac{1}{y!}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{\star}}\lambda_{\star}^{y}\bigr|

\displaystyle=d_{1}\bigl(\Pois(\lambda_{\star}(1+\beta)),\>\Pois(\lambda_{\star})\bigr).

It remains to bound the remaining ‘error’.
Let
L^{\prime}\coloneqq L-m^{2}/n

First, note that

\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{\star}=\frac{m}{n}-\frac{m-L}{n-m}=\frac{m(n-m)-n(m-L)}{n(n-m)}=\frac{L-m^{2}/n}{n-m}=\frac{L^{\prime}}{n-m}.

We now use a simple coupling of two Poisson
RV

if
0<a<b
, then
\Pois(a)+\Pois(b-a)\sim\Pois(b)
,
if
\Pois(a)
and
\Pois(b-a)
are independent.
Thus,

|Y_{0}-Y_{\star}|\sim\Pois(|\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{\star}|).

This is not independent of
Y_{\star}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{Y_{\star}}\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{\beta L^{\prime}}(1+\beta)^{Y_{0}-Y_{\star}}-1\bigr|\bigr){}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 22.99988pt\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-2\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{2Y_{\star}}\bigr)\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\beta|L^{\prime}|/m}(1+\beta)^{Z}-1\bigr){}^{2}\bigr)

The first term is straightforward to calculate and bound using
4.16

Y_{\star}\sim\Pois(\lambda_{\star})
and
\lambda_{\star}\leq 1

\mathbb{E}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-2\beta\lambda_{\star}}(1+\beta)^{2Y_{\star}}\bigr)=\mathrm{e}^{\beta^{2}\lambda_{\star}}\mathbb{E}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-(2\beta+\beta^{2})\lambda_{\star}}(1+2\beta+\beta^{2})^{Y_{\star}}\bigr)=\mathrm{e}^{\beta^{2}\lambda_{\star}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{\beta^{2}}.

Next, we expand the square in the second term and use
4.16
again:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\beta|L^{\prime}|/m}(1+\beta)^{Z}-1\bigr)^{2}\bigr)=\mathrm{e}^{2\beta|L^{\prime}|/m}\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{2Z}\bigr)-2\mathrm{e}^{\beta|L^{\prime}|/m}\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{Z}\bigr)+1

\displaystyle\hskip 22.99988pt=\mathrm{e}^{2\beta|L^{\prime}|/m}\mathrm{e}^{(2\beta+\beta^{2})|\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{\star}|}-2\mathrm{e}^{\beta|L^{\prime}|/m}\mathrm{e}^{\beta|\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{\star}|}+1.

Plugging in the expressions for
\lambda_{0}
and
\lambda_{\star}
, we find that this is equal to

\displaystyle\hskip 22.99988pt\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\mathrm{e}^{2\beta|L^{\prime}|(1/m+1/(n-m))}\mathrm{e}^{\beta^{2}|L^{\prime}|/(n-m)}-2\mathrm{e}^{\beta|L^{\prime}|(1/m+1/(n-m))}+1

\displaystyle\hskip 22.99988pt=\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\beta|L^{\prime}|(1/m+1/(n-m))}-1\bigr)^{2}+\mathrm{e}^{2\beta|L^{\prime}|(1/m+1/(n-m))}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\beta^{2}|L^{\prime}|/m}-1\bigr).

The claim now follows immediately from all these estimates and calculations.
∎

The ‘error bound’ in the previous lemma is, admittedly, pretty confusing.
In particular, it is not ‘small’ unless
L
is ‘close to’
m^{2}/n
.
We do not try to explicitly using the distribution
L\sim\HG(m,m,n)
.
Rather, we use the fact that
L
concentrates very well around its mean
m^{2}/n
; further,
L/m\in[0,1]
, so is uniformly bounded. This will allow us to replace
L/m
by
m/n+o(m/n)
at only an
o(1)
error
even in the
mgf
s
.

Hypergeometric distributions,
which correspond to sampling without replacement,
concentrate more strongly than their Binomial counterparts,
which correspond to sampling with replacement.
This can be made formal by a simple coupling.
The well-known Binomial concentration bound is sufficient for our application.

Lemma 4.18.

Let
L\sim\HG(m,m,n)
.
Let
\gamma,\omega\in(0,\infty)
with
\gamma\omega\leq m
.
Then,

\bigl|\mathbb{E}\bigl(\exp\bigl(\gamma|L/m-m/n|\bigr)\bigr)-1\bigr|\leq 2\gamma\omega/m+2\exp(-2\omega^{2}/m).

Proof.

The claimed bound follows easily from the standard hypergeometric tail bound

\mathchoice{\mathbb{P}\bigl(|L/m-m/n|\geq\omega/m\bigr)}{\mathbb{P}(|L/m-m/n|\geq\omega/m)}{\mathbb{P}(|L/m-m/n|\geq\omega/m)}{\mathbb{P}(|L/m-m/n|\geq\omega/m)}\leq 2\exp(-2\omega^{2}m),

the uniformity
L/m\in[0,1]
and the inequality
\mathrm{e}^{x}-1\leq 2x
for
x\in[0,1]
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathbb{E}\bigl(\exp\bigl(\gamma|L/m-m/n|\bigr)\bigr)-1\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(\exp(\gamma\omega/m)-1\bigr)+\mathchoice{\mathbb{P}\bigl(|L/m-m/n|\geq\omega/m\bigr)}{\mathbb{P}(|L/m-m/n|\geq\omega/m)}{\mathbb{P}(|L/m-m/n|\geq\omega/m)}{\mathbb{P}(|L/m-m/n|\geq\omega/m)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma\omega/m+2\exp(-2\omega^{2}/m).\qed

These results combine easily to estimate the distance under the Poisson approximation.

Proof of
4.15
.

The proposition follows from applying
4.18
to each of the terms in
4.17
,
noting that

|\tfrac{L}{m}-\tfrac{m}{n}|\leq 1\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{and}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}\lambda_{\star}=1-\tfrac{m}{n}=\tfrac{k}{n}=\alpha.

∎

4.3.3 Exact Calculation of Fix-Point Laws

We need to determine the law of
L_{\Sigma}
and understand the number of fixed points of a permutation amongst a certain collection of indices.
We analyse the number of fixed point first.

Lemma 4.19.

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
with
L\leq m
and let
\Pi\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathfrak{S}_{m})
.
Then,

\mathchoice{\mathbb{P}\bigl(|\{i\leq L\mid\Pi(i)=i\}|=r\bigr)}{\mathbb{P}(|\{i\leq L\mid\Pi(i)=i\}|=r)}{\mathbb{P}(|\{i\leq L\mid\Pi(i)=i\}|=r)}{\mathbb{P}(|\{i\leq L\mid\Pi(i)=i\}|=r)}=\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L-r}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{(L)_{r+\ell}}{(m)_{r+\ell}}.

In particular, if
L\gg 1
, then

|\{i\leq L\mid\Pi(i)=i\}|\approx\Pois(\frac{L}{m}).

Proof.

This result is well known when
L=m
.
General
L
requires some adjustment.
Write

\Fix_{L}\pi\coloneqq|\{i\leq L\mid\pi(i)=i\}|\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{and}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}F_{i}\coloneqq\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}\mid\pi(i)=i\}

for
the number of fixed points of
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
amongst the first
L
indices
and
the set of
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
with
i\in[m]
as a fixed point,
respectively.
Then,

\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}\mid\Fix_{L}\pi=0\}=\cap_{i\leq L}F_{i}^{c}=(\cup_{i\leq L}F_{i})^{c}.

By the inclusion–exclusion principle,

|\cup_{i\leq L}F_{i}|=\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{\emptyset\neq J\subseteq[L]}\displaystyle}{\sum_{\emptyset\neq J\subseteq[L]}}{\sum_{\emptyset\neq J\subseteq[L]}}{\sum_{\emptyset\neq J\subseteq[L]}}(-1)^{|J|+1}|\cap_{j\in J}F_{j}|.

By symmetry,

|\cap_{j\in J}F_{j}|=(m-|J|)!.

Hence,

\displaystyle|\cup_{i\leq L}F_{i}|

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\displaystyle}{\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq L}}{\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq L}}{\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq L}}\mathchoice{\textstyle\binom{L}{\ell}\displaystyle}{\binom{L}{\ell}}{\binom{L}{\ell}}{\binom{L}{\ell}}(-1)^{\ell+1}(m-\ell)!=m!\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\displaystyle}{\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq L}}{\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq L}}{\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq L}}\tfrac{1}{\ell!}(-1)^{\ell+1}(L)_{\ell}/(m)_{\ell}.

In particular, combining these gives

|\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}\mid\Fix_{L}\pi=0\}|=m!\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{(L)_{\ell}}{(m)_{\ell}}.

This recovers the well-known approximately-
\Pois(1)
formula when
L=m
.

We now generalise this to

\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}\mid\Fix_{L}\pi=r\}

for
r\geq 1
.
We have

\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}\mid\Fix_{L}\pi=r\}=\cup_{I\subseteq[L]:|I|=r}\bigl((\cap_{i\in I}F_{i})\cap(\cap_{i\notin I}F_{i}^{c})\bigr)

The event
\cap_{i\in I}F_{i}
forces the indices in
I\subseteq[L]
to be fixed points.
The remaining indices of
[L]
, ie
[L]\setminus I
, must be non-fixed points, whilst those in
[m]\setminus[L]
are unrestricted.
There are
\binom{m}{r}
choices for
I
; all are symmetric, so we may assume that
I=\{L-r+1,...,L\}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle|\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}\mid\Fix_{L}\pi=r\}|=\binom{L}{r}(m-r)!\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L-r}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{(L-r)_{\ell}}{(m-r)_{\ell}}.

\displaystyle\hskip 22.99988pt=\frac{(L)_{r}}{r!}\frac{m!}{(m)_{r}}\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L-r}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{(L-r)_{\ell}}{(m-r)_{\ell}}=\frac{m!}{r!}\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L-r}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{(L)_{r+\ell}}{(m)_{r+\ell}}.

The first part lemma now follows since
\Pi\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathfrak{S}_{m})
, so

\mathchoice{\mathbb{P}\bigl(\Fix_{L}\pi=r\bigr)}{\mathbb{P}(\Fix_{L}\pi=r)}{\mathbb{P}(\Fix_{L}\pi=r)}{\mathbb{P}(\Fix_{L}\pi=r)}=|\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\mid\Fix_{L}\pi=r\}|/m!.

This shows that the distribution of
\Fix_{L}\Pi
is approximately
\Pois(L/m)
when
1\ll L\leq m
.
This is well known when
L=m
and follows more generally because
(L-r)_{\ell}/(m-r)_{\ell}\approx(L/m)^{\ell}
when
r,\ell\asymp 1
.
Asymptotically all the mass of the sum comes from
\ell\asymp 1
and
\Fix_{L}\Pi\leq\Fix(\Pi)\approx\Pois(1)
, so the important case is
r,\ell\asymp 1
.
∎

Exactly the same argument can be translated into the event for
\Sigma
.
However, there is a slight subtlety:
the
RV

L_{\Sigma}
is correlated with
|\{i>m\mid\Sigma(i)=i\}|
.
Knowing the value of
L_{\Sigma}
determines the number of indices
i>m
which are mapped to
\Sigma(i)\leq m
, which cannot be fixed points.
Given this, it follows an analogous distribution to that of the previous lemma.

Lemma 4.20.

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
with
0\leq m-L\leq n-m
and
\Sigma\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathbb{P}\bigl(|\{i>m\mid\Sigma(i)=i\}|=r\mid L_{\Sigma}=L\bigr)}{\mathbb{P}(|\{i>m\mid\Sigma(i)=i\}|=r\mid L_{\Sigma}=L)}{\mathbb{P}(|\{i>m\mid\Sigma(i)=i\}|=r\mid L_{\Sigma}=L)}{\mathbb{P}(|\{i>m\mid\Sigma(i)=i\}|=r\mid L_{\Sigma}=L)}=\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq n-2m+L-r}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{(n-2m+L)_{r+\ell}}{(n-m)_{r+\ell}}.

In particular, if
n-2m+L\gg 1
, then

|\{i>m\mid\Sigma(i)=i\}|\approx\Pois(1-\frac{m-L}{n-m})

given
L_{\Sigma}=L
.

Proof.

This follows analogously to the above after taking care of the conditioning.
We have

\displaystyle|\{i\leq m\mid\Sigma^{-1}(i)\leq m\}|=L

\displaystyle\iff|\{i>m\mid\Sigma(i)\leq m\}|=m-L

\displaystyle\iff|\{i>m\mid\Sigma(i)>m\}|=(n-m)-(m-L).

We are choosing uniformly without replacement
n-m
indices
i>m
and conditioning that
(n-m)-(m-L)=n-2m+L
are mapped to indices
j>m
—which is a necessary condition for
i
to be a fixed point, ie
i=j
.
The number of fixed points amongst indices
i>m
is then approximately
\Pois(\frac{n-2m+L}{n-m})=\Pois(1-\frac{m-L}{n-m})
in the precise sense of
4.19
.
∎

The above proof also shows that the two parts are independent given
L_{\Sigma}
.
Recall that

X\coloneqq|\{i\leq L_{\Sigma}\mid\Pi(i)=i\}|\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{and}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}Y\coloneqq|\{i>m\mid\Sigma(i)=i\}|.

Corollary 4.21.

The
RV
s
X

and

Y

are conditionally independent given
L_{\Sigma}
.
Moreover,

X\approx\Pois\bigl(\tfrac{L}{m}\bigr)\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{and}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}Y\approx\Pois\bigl(1-\tfrac{m-L}{n-m}\bigr)\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{given}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ given }}{\text{ given }}{\text{ given }}L_{\Sigma}=L.

4.3.4 Justification of Poisson Approximation

It remains to justify why we can replace

X
and
Y

by their Poisson approximations

X_{0}\sim\Pois(\tfrac{L}{m})\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{and}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}Y_{0}\sim\Pois(1-\tfrac{m-L}{n-m})\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{given}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ given }}{\text{ given }}{\text{ given }}L_{\Sigma}=L.

Proposition 4.22.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
k=\alpha n
.
Let
L\sim\HG(m,m,n)
. Let
X_{0}\sim\Pois(\tfrac{L}{m})
and
Y_{0}\sim\Pois(1-\tfrac{m-L}{n-m})
independently given
L
.
Let
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\coloneqq t_{c}
. Write
\beta\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-c}/\alpha
.
Then,

d_{1}(t)=\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{X_{0}+Y_{0}}\mid L_{\Sigma}\bigr)-1\bigr|\bigr)+o(1).

Proof.

4.13
says that

d_{1}(t)=\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|e^{-\beta}\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{X+Y}\mid L_{\Sigma}\bigr)-1\bigr|\bigr).

We look at the difference between this and the replacement of
(X,Y)
by
(X_{0},Y_{0})
:

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigl|\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|e^{-\beta}\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{X+Y}\mid L\bigr)-1\bigr|\bigr)-\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|e^{-\beta}\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{X_{0}+Y_{0}}\mid L\bigr)-1\bigr|\bigr)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{X+Y}-(1+\beta)^{X_{0}+Y_{0}}\mid L\bigr)\bigr|\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|(1+\beta)^{X+Y}-(1+\beta)^{X_{0}+Y_{0}}\bigr|\bigr).

Given
L
, both
X\to^{d}X_{0}
and
Y\to^{d}Y_{0}
.
This is not sufficient a priori since we are looking at the
mgf
s—the map
z\mapsto(1+\beta)^{z}
is unbounded, so we cannot uniformly bound
(1+\beta)^{X+Y}
.
However, the tails of all of
\{X,Y,X_{0},Y_{0}\}
decay super-exponentially—as factorials, in fact—whilst
z\mapsto(1+\beta)^{z}
is only exponential.
Thus, the convergence is sufficiently strong to deduce that
\Delta\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.
We give further details, but omit some technical ones at the end.

We use the explicit descriptions for the laws of
X
and
Y
given
L
(
4.19
and
4.20
) as well as their conditional independence given
L
(
4.21
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{X+Y}\mid L\bigr)=\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{x,y=0}^{\infty}\displaystyle}{\sum_{x,y=0}^{\infty}}{\sum_{x,y=0}^{\infty}}{\sum_{x,y=0}^{\infty}}(1+\beta)^{x+y}\mathchoice{\mathbb{P}\bigl(X=x\mid L\bigr)}{\mathbb{P}(X=x\mid L)}{\mathbb{P}(X=x\mid L)}{\mathbb{P}(X=x\mid L)}\mathchoice{\mathbb{P}\bigl(Y=y\mid L\bigr)}{\mathbb{P}(Y=y\mid L)}{\mathbb{P}(Y=y\mid L)}{\mathbb{P}(Y=y\mid L)}

\displaystyle\hskip 22.99988pt=\sum_{x,y=0}^{\infty}(1+\beta)^{x+y}\cdot\frac{1}{x!}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i!}\frac{(L)_{x+i}}{(m)_{x+i}}\cdot\frac{1}{y!}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\frac{(n-2m+L)_{y+j}}{(n-m)_{y+j}}

\displaystyle\hskip 22.99988pt=\sum_{x,y}\sum_{i,j}\frac{(-1)^{i+j}}{x!y!i!j!}\biggl(\frac{L}{m}(1+\beta)\biggr)^{x}\biggl(\biggl(1-\frac{m-L}{n-m}\biggr)(1+\beta)\biggr)^{y}\biggl(\frac{L}{m}\biggr)^{i}\biggl(1-\frac{m-L}{n-m}\biggr)^{j}

\displaystyle\hskip 22.99988pt\hskip 45.99976pt\cdot\biggl(\frac{(L)_{x+i}}{(m)_{i+x}}\bigg/\biggl(\frac{L}{m}\biggr)^{x+i}\biggr)\cdot\biggl(\frac{(n-2m+L)_{y+j}}{(n-m)_{y+j}}\bigg/\biggl(1-\frac{m-L}{n-m}\biggr)^{y+j}\biggr),

where the second equality merely rearranges the terms in order to force it to look like

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{X_{0}+Y_{0}}\mid L\bigr)=\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{x}\displaystyle}{\sum_{x}}{\sum_{x}}{\sum_{x}}\tfrac{1}{x}!\mathrm{e}^{-x}\bigl(\tfrac{L}{m}(1+\beta)\bigr)^{x}\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{y}\displaystyle}{\sum_{y}}{\sum_{y}}{\sum_{y}}\tfrac{1}{y}!\mathrm{e}^{-x}\bigl((1-\tfrac{m-L}{n-m})(1+\beta)\bigr)^{y}

It remains to control the ‘error factor’ in our expression for

\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{X+Y}\mid L\bigr).

To this end, let

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{X+Y}\mid L\bigr)-\mathbb{E}\bigl((1+\beta)^{X_{0}+Y_{0}}\mid L\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\sum_{i,j}\frac{(-1)^{i+j}}{x!y!i!j!}\biggl(\frac{L}{m}(1+\beta)\biggr)^{x}\biggl(\biggl(1-\frac{m-L}{n-m}\biggr)(1+\beta)\biggr)^{y}\biggl(\frac{L}{m}\biggr)^{i}\biggl(1-\frac{m-L}{n-m}\biggr)^{j}

\mathchoice{\mathbb{E}\bigl(|\delta|\bigr)}{\mathbb{E}(|\delta|)}{\mathbb{E}(|\delta|)}{\mathbb{E}(|\delta|)}\to 0

as

to complete the proof.

The ratios of falling factorials are always smaller than their corresponding powers, because
L\leq M
.
Also,
m-L\leq n-m
, ie
n-2m+L\geq 0
.
Thus,

\displaystyle|\delta|\leq\sum_{x,y}\sum_{i,j}\frac{(1+\beta)^{x+y}}{x!y!i!j!}\bigl(1-(1-a_{x,i})(1-b_{y,j})\bigr)\hskip 11.49994pt\text{where}

\displaystyle a_{x,i}\coloneqq 1-\frac{(L)_{x+i}}{(m)_{x+i}}\bigg/\biggl(\frac{L}{m}\biggr)^{x+i}\mathchoice{\hskip 11.49994pt\text{and}\hskip 11.49994pt}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}b_{y,j}\coloneqq\frac{(n-2m+L)_{y+j}}{(n-m)_{y+j}}\bigg/\biggl(1-\frac{m-L}{n-m}\biggr)^{y+j}.

\displaystyle|\delta|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,i}\dfrac{(1+\beta)^{x}}{x!i!}a_{x,i}\sum_{y,j}\dfrac{(1+\beta)^{y}}{y!j!}+\sum_{y,j}\dfrac{(1+\beta)^{y}}{y!j!}b_{y,j}\sum_{x,i}\dfrac{(1+\beta)^{x}}{x!i!}

|\delta|\to 0

as

when
\min\{L,n-2m+L\}\geq n^{1/4}
for fixed

, completing the proof.
There are four key facts required for establishing this.

(i)

a_{z,k},b_{z,k}\in[0,1]
for all
(z,k)
.

(ii)

The factorial

grows faster than the exponential

, meaning that asymptotically all the mass of
\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{z}\displaystyle}{\sum_{z}}{\sum_{z}}{\sum_{z}}

.

(iii)

Similarly, asymptotically all the mass of
\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{k}\displaystyle}{\sum_{k}}{\sum_{k}}{\sum_{k}}

k

.

(iv)

The approximations

b_{y,j}\approx 0

On the other hand, a
\Pois(1)

Thus,
\mathchoice{\mathbb{E}\bigl(|\delta|\bigr)}{\mathbb{E}(|\delta|)}{\mathbb{E}(|\delta|)}{\mathbb{E}(|\delta|)}\to 0

.
We omit the technical manipulations required to rigorously establish these claims in this last paragraph.

Remark 4.23.

We recall that
\beta=\mathrm{e}^{-c}/\alpha
and
\alpha=k/n
.
The above proof assumed that
\beta\asymp 1
.
However, all it really needs is for the factorial
1/r!
to beat the exponential
(1+\beta)^{r}
in an appropriate range.
If
\beta\asymp 1
, then asymptotically all the mass of the sums is in the first order-
1

However, if

We have not pushed the technical manipulations to see how large

can be taken.
We
should
be able to handle
some

.
In this case, the limit is

\mathchoice{\mathbb{P}\bigl(\Pois(\mathrm{e}^{-c})=0\bigr)}{\mathbb{P}(\Pois(\mathrm{e}^{-c})=0)}{\mathbb{P}(\Pois(\mathrm{e}^{-c})=0)}{\mathbb{P}(\Pois(\mathrm{e}^{-c})=0)}=\exp(-\mathrm{e}^{-c}),

which is the

of the Gumbel distribution.
