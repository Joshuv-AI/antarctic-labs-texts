Central Limit Theorem for the number of real roots of random orthogonal polynomials
Yen Do, Hoi H. Nguyen, Oanh Nguyen, and Igor E. Pritsker

Abstract
. In this note we study the number of real roots of a wide class of random orthogonal polynomials with gaussian coefficients. Using the method of Wiener Chaos we show that the fluctuation in the bulk is asymptotically gaussian, even when the local correlations are different.



1.
Introduction

Let
\mu
be a compactly supported Borel measure on the real line, and assume that the support of
\mu
contains infinitely many points. Consider the polynomials

p_{n}(x)=\gamma_{n}x^{n}+\dots

with
\gamma_{n}>0
for all
n\geq 0
, such that

\int p_{i}(x)p_{j}(x)d\mu(x)=\delta_{ij},\quad\text{for all }i,j\geq 0.

The study of the roots of these orthogonal polynomials has a long and rich history, and we invite the reader to classics such as
[
19
,
33
]
for more details. In this note, we are interested in several probabilistic aspects of the number of real roots for the following random polynomial, chosen from the span of
p_{1}(x),\dots,p_{n}(x)
:

H_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\xi_{j}p_{j}(x).

We shall assume that
\xi_{j}
are independent standard Gaussian random variables throughout the note.

Let
N_{n}([a,b])
denote the number of real roots of
H_{n}
in a given interval
[a,b]
. Understanding the leading asymptotics for the expected value of
N_{n}
was the subject of many prior investigations in this direction, and let us mention several results of this nature.

Around 1971, Das
[
9
]
considered random Legendre polynomials (corresponding to the measure
d\mu(x)=dx
on
[-1,1]
) and found that the leading asymptotics of
{\mathbb{E}}N_{n}([-1,1])
is
n/\sqrt{3}
. Wilkins
[
32
,
36
]
later estimated the error term in this asymptotic relation. Farahmand
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
also considered the expected number of level crossings for Legendre polynomials where the coefficients
\xi_{j}
’s may have dependent Gaussian distributions.

For random Jacobi polynomials (where
\mu(x)=(1-x)^{a}(1+x)^{b}\textbf{1}_{(-1,1)}
), Das and Bhatt
[
10
]
established that
{\mathbb{E}}N_{n}([-1,1])
has the same leading asymptotics
n/\sqrt{3}
.

These results were generalized by Lubinsky, Pritsker and Xie
[
24
,
25
]
to much more general classes of random orthogonal polynomials. In particular, they showed that the first term in the asymptotics for
{\mathbb{E}}N_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
for many random polynomials remains the same.

All the results mentioned above were for Gaussian polynomials and were proved via the celebrated Kac-Rice formula. We note in passing that the first moment statistics (and higher order moments) of the number of real roots of these polynomials are also universal in terms of randomness (when
\xi_{j}
are not necessarily Gaussian), see a recent joint work with V. Vu of the first author and the third author
[
12
]
; however we will not focus on this aspect in this note.

It is well known that for
\mu(x)=(1-x)^{-1/2}(1+x)^{-1/2}1_{(-1,1)}
we obtain the Chebyshev orthogonal polynomials (of the first type), a special case of Jacobi random orthogonal polynomials. The Chebyshev polynomials satisfy
p_{n}(\cos(x))=\cos(nx)
and thus one may equivalently consider the random trigonometric polynomials

H_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\xi_{j}\cos(jx),\quad x\in[0,2\pi].

This class of random functions was considered by Dunnage
[
14
]
who showed that
{\mathbb{E}}N_{n}([0,2\pi])
is asymptotically equal to
2n/\sqrt{3}
. In
[
31
]
, Qualls considered a slightly different class of trigonometric polynomials (now known as the stationary trigonometric polynomials)

H_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\xi_{j1}\cos(jx)+\xi_{j2}\sin(jx),\quad x\in[0,2\pi]

(1)

and showed that
{\mathbb{E}}N_{n}([0,2\pi])
is also asymptotically equal to
2n/\sqrt{3}
.

It is well-known in the subject that, for the number of real roots, asympotics for the variance are much harder to handle than the expected value: for the variance, one typically has to establish some further cancellation in the applications of the Kac-Rice formula. For the stationary random trigonometric model (
1
), Bogomolny, Bohigas and Leboeuf
[
7
]
argued that
{\operatorname{Var}}(N_{n}([0,2\pi]))
is asymptotically
cn
, and this was verified by Granville and Wigman
[
20
]
, and subsequently by Azaïs and León
[
5
]
via a different method, with an explicit formula for
c
. The variance for the classical trigonometric models considered by Dunnage was computed in
[
34
]
by Su and Shao, and also by Azaïs, Dalmao and León in
[
4
]
. More recently, Lubinsky and Pritsker
[
23
]
were able to provide a general method to compute the variances for many important cases of random orthogonal polynomials. Their results are summarized below.

Theorem 1.1

(Mean and variance for roots of random orthogonal polynomials in the bulk)
.

[
24
,
25
,
23
]
Let
\mu
be a measure with compact support on the real line, that is regular in the sense of Stahl, Totik, and Ullmann (that is
\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}^{1/n}=\frac{1}{cap(\mathbf{supp}(\mu))}
, where
cap
denotes the logarithmic capacity of
\mathbf{supp}(\mu)
). Let
\omega
denote the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the equilibrium measure for the support of
\mu
. Let
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
be a subinterval in the support of
\mu
, such that
\mu
is absolutely continuous there, and its Radon-Nikodym derivative
\mu^{\prime}
is positive and continuous there. Assume moreover, that

\sup_{n\geq 1}\|p_{n}\|_{L_{\infty}[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}<\infty.

Then if
[a,b]\subset(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}N_{n}([a,b])=\left(\frac{\nu_{\mathcal{K}}([a,b])}{\sqrt{3}}+o(1)\right)n,

where
\nu_{\mathcal{K}}
is the equilibrium measure of the support
{\mathcal{K}}
of
\mu
in the sense of logarithmic potential theory.

Furthermore,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}{\operatorname{Var}}(N_{n}([a,b]))=c\int_{a}^{b}\omega(y)dy

where
c
is an explicit absolute positive constant (independent of
{\mathcal{K}},\mu
).

In what follows, for convenience we write

c_{a,b}:=c\int_{a}^{b}\omega(y)dy.

We note that the constant
c
is defined via the normalized sinc function sinc
(x)=\sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)
in a rather complicated way, see
[
23
]
for details. We also recall that
\nu_{\mathcal{K}}
minimizes the energy
I[\nu]=-\int\int\log|z-t|d\nu(t)d\nu(z)
among all probability measures
\nu
with support on
{\mathcal{K}}
, see
[
30
]
and
[
33
]
for more details on properties of equilibrium measures and other facts from potential theory. It remains an interesting problem to extend the above result to the edges covering
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
.

After establishing the leading asymptotics for the variance, one is naturally interested in another interesting and important direction, namely the limiting distribution of the fluctuation of
N_{n}[(a,b)]
around its mean (also known as the standardization of
N_{n}([a,b])
). It was shown in
[
20
]
by Granville and Wigman that for the random trigonometric polynomials (
1
), the limiting distribution for (the fluctuation of)
N_{n}([0,2\pi])
is Gaussian, and this phenomenon is often refered to by various authors as asymptotic normality of
N_{n}([0,2\pi])
. Granville–Wigman’s result was later re-established in
[
5
]
by Azaïs and León using a totally different and very powerful method. Using this method, Azaïs, Dalmao and León
[
4
]
also prove that the limiting distribution for
N_{n}([0,2\pi])
for the random cosine polynomials is Gaussian, thus showed that after standardization the limiting distribution of the number of real roots of the random Chebyshev polynomials also obey the Gaussian law.

In this note, we show that the limiting distribution for the number of real roots for the random polynomials considered in Theorem
1.1
also obeys the Gaussian law, hence extending the phenomenon established in
[
4
]
(for Chebyshev polynomials) to far more general random polynomial ensembles.

Theorem 1.2

(Central Limit Theorem in the bulk, our main result)
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Theorem
1.1
, we have

\frac{N_{n}([a,b])-{\mathbb{E}}N_{n}([a,b])}{\sqrt{c_{a,b}n}}\xrightarrow{d}{\mathbf{N}}(0,1).

This result basically resolves Problem 1.5 from
[
1
]
. There have been exciting developments regarding asymptotic normality for the number of real roots. These include, beside the results of
[
4
,
5
,
20
]
mentioned above, the results
[
13
,
27
]
for random Weyl polynomials and random Weyl series,
[
26
,
28
]
for random Kac polynomials and generalization, and
[
2
,
8
]
for random elliptic polynomials, which were proved using very different methods. For the stationary trigonometric polynomials,
[
20
]
was able to reduce to a model where the correlations over far-apart points vanish, and then used a result of Berk for sum of long-range independent terms, while for the crossings of these polynomials
[
5
]
computed all the central moments rather precisely, For the Kac polynomials and generalizations,
[
28
,
26
]
used comparison methods, while for the Weyl polynomials and series
[
13
]
used the method of cumulants based on a very fine understanding of the correlation functions (see also
[
27
]
). Finally, to handle the classical random trigonometric polynomials
[
8
,
4
]
used the Wiener chaos decomposition, and this is the method we will be using in this note. Among other things, one highlight of our work is that, because of the nature of the random orthogonal polynomials we are working with (see Section
2
), our rescaled process
\overline{T}_{n}
(to be defined below) does not have global limit, but over microscopic intervals it does converge to gaussian stationary processes. Interestingly, these processes are not necessarily the same unless
\omega(.)
is a constant, see Section
6
for further details.

Before proving our result, we deduce a few examples below.

Corollary 1.3

(Szegő condition)
.

Let
\mu
be a measure supported on
[-1,1]
satisfying the Szegő condition

\int_{-1}^{1}\log\mu^{\prime}(x)\frac{dx}{\pi\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}>-\infty.

Let
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
be a subinterval of
(-1,1)
, in which
\mu
is absolutely continuous while
\mu^{\prime}
is positive and continuous in
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
. Assume moreover that its local modulus of continuity

\Omega(t)=\sup\{|\mu^{\prime}(x)-\mu^{\prime}(y)|:x,y\in[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]\text{ and }|x-y|\leq t\},\quad t>0,

satisfies the Dini-Lipschitz condition

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Omega(t)}{t}dt<\infty.

Then for all
[a,b]\subset(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
,
N_{n}(a,b)
satisfies the CLT.

In particular, when
\mu
is the Legendre weight
\mu^{\prime}=1
,
N_{n}(a,b)
satisfies the CLT for any
[a,b]\subset(-1,1)
.

Following
[
23
]
, beyond Szegő condition, we have

.

Theorem
1.2
also applies to the following classes of measures that do not necessarily satisfy Szegő condition:

•

Measures associated with weights supported on several disjoint interval that satisfy the smoothness conditions in the classic paper of Widom
[
35
]
.

•

Measures associated with exponential weights satisfying conditions in
[
22
]
. For example,

\mu^{\prime}(x)=\exp(-\exp_{k}(1-x^{2})^{-\alpha}),x\in(-1,1),

and
\exp_{k}(\cdot)=\exp(\exp(\dots\exp(\cdot)))
denotes the
k
-th iterated exponential.

For future directions, we hope to study the variance (as mentioned above) and the asymptotic normality of the number of roots for the
entire
interval
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
. There are technical problems here, especially in the analysis side as we will not have Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
near the edges
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
. In another direction, it seems interesting to extend the variance estimate and CLT fluctuation to different types of randomness, and this is left for further studies.

1.5.
Some preparations

Here we fix some notations that will be used throughout the proof. Define

T_{n}(t)=H_{n}(t/n)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\xi_{j}p_{j}(t/n),\quad t\in[na,nb]

and

\widetilde{T}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\xi_{j}p_{j}(t/n).

Hence, instead of finding the roots of
H_{n}
in
[a,b]
, we will be working with the number of roots
N([na,nb])
of
T_{n}
(equivalently,
\widetilde{T}_{n}
). We will also use the following notations from
[
23
]
for the reproducing kernel associated with orthogonal polynomials:

K_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}p_{j}(x)p_{j}(y),

(2)

and for nonnegative integers
l,m
, its derivatives are denoted by

K_{n}^{(l,m)}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}p_{j}^{(l)}(x)p_{j}^{(m)}(y).

(3)

The correlation between
\widetilde{T}_{n}(t),\widetilde{T}_{n}(s)
for any
t,s
is

r_{n}(s,t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}p_{j}(s/n)p_{j}(t/n)=\frac{1}{n}K_{n}\left(s/n,t/n\right).

thus in particular the variance of
T_{n}(t)
is

\displaystyle V_{n}(t)^{2}=r_{n}(t,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}(p_{j}(t/n))^{2}=\frac{1}{n}K_{n}\left(t/n,t/n\right).

(4)

Observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{n}(t,t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{j}p_{j}(t/n)p_{j}^{\prime}(t/n)=\frac{2}{n^{2}}K_{n}^{(0,1)}(t/n,t/n),

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}r_{n}(s,t)}{\partial s\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{3}}\sum_{j}p_{j}^{\prime}(s/n)p_{j}^{\prime}(t/n)=\frac{1}{n^{3}}K_{n}^{(1,1)}(s/n,t/n).

(6)

We denote the standardization of
\widetilde{T}_{n}
by
\overline{T}_{n}
:

\overline{T}_{n}(t)=\frac{\widetilde{T}_{n}(t)}{V_{n}(t)}.

Let
\overline{r}_{n}(s,t)
be the correlation of
\overline{T}_{n}(s)
and
\overline{T}_{n}(t)
, then

\displaystyle\overline{r}_{n}(s,t)={\mathbb{E}}(\overline{T}_{n}(s)\overline{T}_{n}(t))=\frac{r_{n}(s,t)}{\sqrt{r_{n}(s,s)r_{n}(t,t)}}=\frac{K_{n}(s/n,t/n)}{\sqrt{K_{n}(s/n,s/n)K_{n}(t/n,t/n)}}.

(7)

For brevity, we let

S_{1}(t):=\overline{T}_{n}(t)=\sum_{j}\xi_{j}q_{j}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}\frac{p_{j}(t/n)}{\sqrt{r_{n}(t,t)}}.

Then
S_{1}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{j}\xi_{j}q_{j}^{\prime}(t)
,

{\mathbb{E}}S_{1}(s)S_{1}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{j}q_{j}(s)q_{j}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\partial\bar{r}_{n}(s,t)}{\partial t}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad{\mathbb{E}}S_{1}^{\prime}(s)S_{1}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{j}q_{j}^{\prime}(s)q_{j}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{r}_{n}(s,t)}{\partial t\partial s}.

In particular,

{\mathbb{E}}((\overline{T}_{n}^{\prime}(t))^{2})={\mathbb{E}}((S_{1}^{\prime}(t))^{2})=\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{r}_{n}(s,t)}{\partial t\partial s}|_{s=t}.

For brevity, we let
v_{n}(t)
be the standard deviation of
\overline{T}^{\prime}_{n}(t)
, namely

v_{n}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{r}_{n}(s,t)}{\partial t\partial s}|_{s=t}}=\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}((\overline{T}_{n}^{\prime}(t))^{2})}.

(8)

Let
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(t)
denote the standardization of
\overline{T}^{\prime}_{n}(t)
:

{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(t):=\frac{\overline{T}^{\prime}_{n}(t)}{v_{n}(t)}=\sum_{j}\xi_{j}\frac{q_{j}^{\prime}(t)}{v_{n}(t)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}\frac{1}{v_{n}(t)}\left[\frac{p_{j}(t/n)}{\sqrt{r_{n}(t,t)}}\right]^{\prime}.

We stress that
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(t)
is not the derivative of
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(t)
, which is not even defined here.

Note that for each
t
by definition
\overline{T}_{n}(t)
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(t)
are independent because they are jointly Gaussian and their correlation is 0. In general, we will define the correlation between
\overline{T}_{n}(s)
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(t)
by

\widetilde{r}^{\prime}_{n}(s,t):={\operatorname{Cov}}(\overline{T}_{n}(s),{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(t))=\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{v_{n}(t)}\sum_{j}\frac{p_{j}(s/n)}{\sqrt{r_{n}(s,s)}}\left[\frac{p_{j}(t/n)}{\sqrt{r_{n}(t,t)}}\right]^{\prime}

(9)

(here we abused notations a bit, since
\tilde{r}^{\prime}_{n}(s,t)
is not a derivative of any function) and the correlation between
{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{n}(s)
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(t)
by

\widetilde{r}^{\prime\prime}_{n}(s,t):={\operatorname{Cov}}({\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(s),{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(t))=\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{v_{n}(s)v_{n}(t)}\sum_{j}\left[\frac{p_{j}(s/n)}{\sqrt{r_{n}(s,s)}}\right]^{\prime}\left[\frac{p_{j}(t/n)}{\sqrt{r_{n}(t,t)}}\right]^{\prime}.

(10)

1.5.1.
Reformulation using the reproducing kernel and its derivatives.

Our goal in this section is to reformulate

\bar{r}_{n}
and its derivatives in terms of

and its derivatives.

We start with

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{r}_{n}(s,t)}{\partial t\partial s}

3.
Wiener Chaos decomposition

In this section, we outline the method to prove Theorem
1.2
. These ideas seem to be standard, but we include them here for completeness. Our approach follows
[
4
]
, see also
[
29
]
for a survey of related background.

Let
B=(B_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in[0,1]}
be a standard Brownian motion defined on some probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbf{P}})
where
{\mathcal{F}}
is generated by
B
.

The process
(\widetilde{T}_{n}),n=1,2,\dots
, described in Section
1.5
, can be defined on the same probability space by

\widetilde{T}_{n}(t):=\int_{0}^{1}\gamma_{n}(t,\lambda)dB_{\lambda}:=\int_{0}^{1}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}(t/n)1_{((j-1)/n,j/n)}(\lambda)\right]dB_{\lambda},

where
1_{((j-1)/n,j/n)}(.)
is the indicator function of the interval
((j-1)/n,j/n)
.

Let
{\mathbf{H}}
be the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{L}}^{2}([0,1],\mathcal{B},\lambda)
with the standard Borel
\sigma
-algebra, Lebesgue measure, and inner product. The map

h\mapsto{\mathbf{B}}(h):=\int_{0}^{1}h(\lambda)dB_{\lambda}

is an isometry between
{\mathbf{H}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{2}(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbf{P}})
, which is also called an isonormal process associated to
{\mathbf{H}}
(see
[
29
, Chapter 8]
). Using this terminology we can write

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{B}}(\gamma_{n}(t,.)),

\displaystyle\bar{T}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{B}}(h_{n}(t,.)),\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{B}}(h^{\prime}_{n}(t,.)),

where

h_{n}(t,\lambda):=\frac{\gamma_{n}(t,\lambda)}{V_{n}(t)}\mbox{ and }h_{n}^{\prime}(t,\lambda):=\frac{\partial_{t}h_{n}(t,\lambda)}{\|\partial_{t}h_{n}(t,.)\|_{2}}.

(18)

Multiple Wiener-Ito Integrals.
Now let
H_{q}(x)
be the Hermite polynomial of degree
q
, defined by

H_{q}(x)=(-1)^{q}e^{x^{2}/2}\frac{\partial^{q}}{\partial t^{q}}e^{-x^{2}/2}.

It is well-known that these polynomials form a complete orthogonal system in
{\mathcal{L}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\phi(dx))
, where
\phi
is the Gaussian density.

For each
q\geq 1
, let
I_{q}^{B}
denote the Multiple Wiener-Ito integral with respect to the Brownian process
B
, obtained by extending linearly from the identity

I_{q}^{B}(h^{\otimes q})=H_{q}({\mathbf{B}}(h))

(19)

where

h^{\otimes q}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{q}):=\prod h(\lambda_{k}).

I_{q}^{B}
can be viewed as a linear isometry between the symmetric tensor product over
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}^{2}([0,1]^{q})
(equiped with the norm
\sqrt{q!}\|.\|_{L^{2}[0,1]^{q}}
) and
{\mathcal{L}}^{2}(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbf{P}})
(for details see for instance
[
29
, Proposition 8.1.2]
).

Wiener Chaos.
Next, one has the following decomposition

L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbf{P}})=\oplus_{q=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{H}}_{q},

where
{\mathcal{H}}_{q}
, the
q
-th Wiener Chaos, is defined as the image of the linear functional
I_{q}^{B}
over
{\mathbf{H}}=L^{2}([0,1],{\mathbf{B}},d\lambda)
, and
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
is the set of constants. More precisely, for any
F\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbf{P}})
there exists a unique sequence
\{f_{q},q\geq 1\}
with
f_{q}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{s}^{2}([0,1]^{q})
such that

F-{\mathbb{E}}F=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}I_{q}^{B}(f_{q})

(see for instance
[
29
, Theorem 8.2.1]
). We note that the constant component has been removed.

Chaos decomposition for the number of zeros, via the Kac-Rice formula.

To prove Theorem
1.2
, we will use the following decomposition.

Theorem 3.1
.

The following holds in
L^{2}
-sense

\frac{N([na,nb])-{\mathbb{E}}N}{\sqrt{c_{a,b}n}}=\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}I_{q}^{\overline{T}_{n}}([na,nb]),

where

I_{q}^{\overline{T}_{n}}([na,nb])=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{a,b}n}}\int_{na}^{nb}f_{q}(\overline{T}_{n}(s),{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(s))v_{n}(s)ds

and

f_{q}(x,y)=\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}b_{q-2l}a_{2l}H_{q-2l}(x)H_{2l}(y),a_{2l}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{(-1)^{l+1}}{2^{l}l!(2l-1)};b_{0}=1,b_{2k}=\frac{H_{2k}(0)}{\sqrt{2\pi}(2k)!},\mbox{ and }b_{2k+1}=0,\forall k.

A similar decomposition was obtained in
[
4
,
5
]
. For completeness, in Section
8
we will present a proof of Theorem
3.1
using a similar argument. We remark that the same proof also shows that the following

-decomposition still holds for any subinterval

of
[na,nb]

N(I)-{\mathbb{E}}N(I)=\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}\int_{I}f_{q}(\overline{T}_{n}(s),{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(s))v_{n}(s)ds.

(20)

Chaining.

In what follows, we will rewrite
I_{q}^{\overline{T}_{n}}([na,nb])
in terms of the Brownian motion
B
and show that it belongs to the
q
th chaos.

Recall that
\overline{T}_{n}(t)={\mathbf{B}}(h_{n}(t,.))
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(t)={\mathbf{B}}(h^{\prime}_{n}(t,.))
, and hence

H_{q-2l}(\overline{T}_{n}(t))H_{2l}({\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(t))=H_{q-2l}({\mathbf{B}}(h_{n}(t,.)))H_{2l}({\mathbf{B}}(h^{\prime}_{n}(t,.))).

As remarked previously,
\overline{T}_{n}(t)
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{\prime}(t)
are orthogonal (i.e. independent), and so
h_{n}(t,.)
and
h^{\prime}_{n}(t,.)
are also orthogonal by isometry. It then follows from
[
29
, Equation (6.4.17)]
and (

) that

H_{q-2l}({\mathbf{B}}(h_{n}(t,.)))H_{2l}({\mathbf{B}}(h^{\prime}_{n}(t,.)))=I_{q}^{B}(h_{n}(t,.)^{\otimes(q-2l)}\otimes h_{n}^{\prime}(t,.)^{\otimes 2l}).

By the stochastic Fubini’s theorem, we can express
I_{q}^{\overline{T}_{n}}([na,nb])
as

\displaystyle I_{q}^{\overline{T}_{n}}([na,nb])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{a,b}n}}\int_{na}^{nb}\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}b_{q-2l}a_{2l}H_{q-2l}({\mathbf{B}}(h_{n}(s,.)))H_{2l}({\mathbf{B}}(h^{\prime}_{n}(s,.)))v_{n}(s)ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{a,b}n}}\int_{na}^{nb}\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}b_{q-2l}a_{2l}I_{q}^{B}(h_{n}(s,.)^{\otimes(q-2l)}\otimes h_{n}^{\prime}(s,.)^{\otimes 2l})v_{n}(s)ds

\displaystyle=I_{q}^{B}(g_{q}^{(n)}),

where

g_{q}^{(n)}(\mathbf{\lambda})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{a,b}n}}\int_{na}^{nb}\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}b_{q-2l}a_{2l}[h_{n}(s,.)^{\otimes(q-2l)}\otimes h_{n}^{\prime}(s,.)^{\otimes 2l}](\lambda)v_{n}(s)ds;\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{q}).

(21)

Multidimensional CLT.
Last but not least, to prove Theorem
1.2
we will need the following CLT criterion extracted from
[
4
, Theorem 4]
and
[

, Theorem 11.8.3]

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume that

F_{n}=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}I_{q}^{B}(f_{q}^{(n)})

such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}(I_{q}^{B}(f_{q}^{(n)})^{2})=\sigma_{q}^{2}<\infty;

(22)

\sigma^{2}=\sum_{q}\sigma_{q}^{2}<\infty;

(23)

and for each
q\geq 1
and
p=0,\dots,q-1
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_{q}^{(n)}\otimes_{p}f_{q}^{(n)}\|_{L^{2}([0,1]^{2q-2p})}=0,

(24)

\lim_{Q\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{q\geq Q}{\mathbb{E}}(I_{q}^{B}(f_{q}^{(n)})^{2})=0.

(25)

Then
F_{n}
converges to
{\mathbf{N}}(0,\sigma^{2})
in distribution as

We recall (see e.g.
[

]
) that for
0\leq p\leq q
the

of functions in the

\displaystyle(f_{q}^{(n)}\otimes_{p}f_{q}^{(n)})(x_{1},\dots,x_{q-p},y_{1},\dots,y_{q-p})

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{p}}f_{q}^{(n)}(z_{1},\dots,z_{p},x_{1},\dots,x_{q-p})f_{q}^{(n)}(z_{1},\dots,z_{p},y_{1},\dots,y_{q-p})dz_{1}\dots dz_{p}.

(26)

In particular, in the special case

this is simply the tensor product

f_{q}^{(n)}\otimes_{0}f_{q}^{(n)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{q},y_{1},\dots,y_{q})=f_{q}^{(n)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{q})f_{q}^{(n)}(y_{1},\dots,y_{q}).

\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{n}(n\theta+\tau/2)v_{n}(n\theta-\tau/2)S_{n,q,{\mathbf{d}}}(n\theta-\tau/2,n\theta+\tau/2)=:f_{q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\theta,\tau).

Thus, the integrand in (
32

for fixed
\tau

\theta

, this integrand is uniformly bounded by
B^{q}
which depends on

but not on

. Hence there exists a function

such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}g_{n,q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\tau)=h_{q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\tau).

Since
|g_{n,q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\tau)|\leq\frac{B^{q}}{(|\tau|+1)^{q}}
by Claim
5.2
, we also have
|h_{q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\tau)|\leq\frac{B^{q}}{(|\tau|+1)^{q}}
. In particular, it is integrable on
{\mathbb{R}}
. Therefore, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
T>0
such that

\int_{|\tau|>T}|h_{q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\tau)|d\tau<\varepsilon.

To complete the proof of Lemma
5.1
, we write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c_{a,b}n}\int_{[na,nb]}\int_{[na,nb]}v_{n}(s)v_{n}(t)S_{n,q,{\mathbf{d}}}(s,t)dsdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{-n(b-a)}^{n(b-a)}g_{n,q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\tau)d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{|\tau|\leq T}g_{n,q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\tau)d\tau+\frac{1}{2}\int_{T<|\tau|\leq n(b-a)}g_{n,q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\tau)d\tau.

By the Dominated Convergence Theorem,

\left|\int_{|\tau|\leq T}g_{n,q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\tau)d\tau-\int_{|\tau|\leq T}h_{q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\tau)d\tau\right|\leq\varepsilon\text{ for sufficiently large }n.

For the remaining term, we have

\left|\int_{T<|\tau|\leq n(b-a)}g_{n,q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\tau)d\tau\right|\leq\int_{T}^{\infty}\frac{2B^{q}\,d\tau}{(\tau+1)^{q}}\leq\frac{2B^{q}}{T^{q-1}}\leq\varepsilon

by choosing
T
sufficiently large compared to
q
and
\varepsilon
. Combining these bounds, we conclude that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{c_{a,b}n}\int_{[na,nb]}\int_{[na,nb]}v_{n}(s)v_{n}(t)S_{q,{\mathbf{d}}}(s,t)dsdt=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}h_{q,{\mathbf{d}}}(\tau)d\tau

proving the desired limit in Lemma
5.1
.
∎

Next we verify Condition (
23
) that
\sigma^{2}=\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}\sigma_{q}^{2}<\infty
. With

because the variance of the LHS term in the decomposition of Theorem

The proof is now complete because the LHS above is exactly
\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}\sigma_{q}^{2}
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Uniformly in
\theta\in(a,b)

\overline{T}_{\infty,\theta}

For convenience let

be the process
\overline{T}_{\infty,\theta}(t)

as in (

{\mathbb{E}}|X^{\prime}(t)|^{2}=\lim_{s\to t}{\mathbb{E}}(\frac{X_{s}-X_{t}}{s-t})^{2}=\lim_{s\to t}\frac{1}{(s-t)^{2}}(2-2r(s,t))=\omega^{2}(\theta)\pi^{2}/3=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}|\overline{T}^{\prime}_{n}(t)|^{2},

where in the last equality we used (

Furthermore

{\mathbb{E}}X(s)X^{\prime}(t)={\mathbb{E}}\lim_{t^{\prime}\to t}X(s)\frac{X(t^{\prime})-X(t)}{t^{\prime}-t}=\lim_{t^{\prime}\to t}\frac{1}{t^{\prime}-t}(r(s,t^{\prime})-r(s,t))=\frac{\partial r(s,t)}{\partial t}=-\omega(\theta)S^{\prime}((s-t)\omega(\theta)).

And so,

\frac{{\mathbb{E}}X(s)X^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}|X^{\prime}(t)|^{2}}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{r}_{n}^{\prime}(s,t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\overline{T}_{n}(s)\overline{T}^{\prime}_{n}(t)}{\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}|\overline{T}^{\prime}_{n}(t)|^{2}}}

where we used (

Lastly,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}X^{\prime}(s)X^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\lim_{s^{\prime}\to s,t^{\prime}\to t}\frac{X(s^{\prime})-X(s)}{s^{\prime}-s}\frac{X(t^{\prime})-X(t)}{t^{\prime}-t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{s^{\prime}\to s,t^{\prime}\to t}\frac{1}{s^{\prime}-s}\frac{1}{t^{\prime}-t}(r(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})-r(s^{\prime},t)-r(s,t^{\prime})+r(s,t))

\displaystyle=-(\omega(\theta))^{2}S^{\prime\prime}((s-t)\omega(\theta)).

After normalizing by
\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}|X^{\prime}(s)|^{2}}
and
\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}|X^{\prime}(t)|^{2}}
, we again obtain the same as
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{r}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(s,t)
in (
5
) which is the corresponding identity for
\overline{T}_{n}
by (

Proof of Theorem
3.1

We will need the following analog of
[
21

on Hermite expansion of Kac-Rice formula.

Lemma 8.1
.

The following holds

(i)

Define

N^{\eta}([na,nb]):=\int_{na}^{nb}\phi_{\eta}(\overline{T}_{n}(s))|{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{n}(s)|v_{n}(s)ds

where
\phi_{\eta}
is the density of the
N(0,\eta)

N^{\eta}([na,nb])
converges almost surely and in

to
N([na,nb])
, and
\mathbb{E}|N^{\eta}([na,nb])|^{2}\to\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])|^{2}
, as
\eta\to 0.

(ii)

The random variable
N^{\eta}([na,nb])
has the chaotic expansion (in
L^{2}
)

N^{\eta}([na,nb])=\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}b^{\eta}_{q-2\ell}a_{2\ell}\int_{na}^{nb}H_{q-2\ell}(\overline{T}_{n}(s))H_{2\ell}({\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{n}(s))v_{n}(s)ds,

where

are the Hermite coefficients of

b^{\eta}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}k!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi_{\eta}(t)H_{k}(t)e^{-t^{2}/2}dt

Assuming this lemma for the moment, we will conclude our main result.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Since
H_{k}
is odd if
k
is odd, it follows immediately that
b^{\eta}_{k}=0=b_{k}
for
k
odd. For
k
even, we have

\lim_{\eta\to 0}b_{k}^{\eta}=\frac{H_{k}(0)}{\sqrt{2\pi}k!}=b_{k}.

Given any
Q
, using Fatou’s lemma and Lemma
8.1
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\mathbb{E}\Big(\Big[\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}b_{q-2\ell}a_{2\ell}\int_{na}^{nb}H_{q-2\ell}(\overline{T}_{n}(s))H_{2\ell}({\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{n}(s))v_{n}(s)ds\Big]^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{\eta\to 0}\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\mathbb{E}\Big(\Big[\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}b^{\eta}_{q-2\ell}a_{2\ell}\int_{na}^{nb}H_{q-2\ell}(\overline{T}_{n}(s))H_{2\ell}({\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{n}(s))v_{n}(s)ds\Big]^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{\eta\to 0}\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big(\Big[\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}b^{\eta}_{q-2\ell}a_{2\ell}\int_{na}^{nb}H_{q-2\ell}(\overline{T}_{n}(s))H_{2\ell}({\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{n}(s))v_{n}(s)ds\Big]^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\liminf_{\eta\to 0}\mathbb{E}|N^{\eta}([na,nb])|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])|^{2}<\infty,

where we used the orthogonality of the chaos (see the discussion in the Chaining section after the statement of Theorem
3.1
), and in the last inequality we used
N([na,nb])\leq n
.

It follows that the following expression converges to a limit
L
in

, as

\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}b_{q-2\ell}a_{2\ell}\int_{na}^{nb}H_{q-2\ell}(\overline{T}_{n}(s))H_{2\ell}({\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{n}(s))v_{n}(s)ds.

Our first goal is to show that, almost surely, this limit

N([na,nb])
. By the triangle inequality,

\|N([na,nb])-L\|_{2}\leq\|N(na,nb)-N^{\eta}([na,nb])\|_{2}+\|N^{\eta}([na,nb])-L\|_{2},

and by Lemma
8.1
part (i) the first term on the right hand side converges to

as
\eta\to 0
. On the other hand, letting

J_{q}:=\int_{na}^{nb}H_{q-2\ell}(\overline{T}_{n}(s))H_{2\ell}({\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{n}(s))v_{n}(s)ds,

using the facts that
\overline{T}_{n}(s)
and
{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{n}(s)
are standard Gaussian, and that
\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)ds=N([na,nb])<\infty
, it can be seen that
J_{q}\in L^{2}
. We then use part (ii) of Lemma
8.1
to write

\|N^{\eta}([na,nb])-L\|_{2}=\left\|\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}(b^{\eta}_{q-2\ell}-b_{q-2\ell})a_{2\ell}J_{q}\right\|_{2}

\leq\left\|\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}(b^{\eta}_{q-2\ell}-b_{q-2\ell})a_{2\ell}J_{q}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\sum_{q=Q+1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}b^{\eta}_{q-2\ell}a_{2\ell}J_{q}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\sum_{q=Q+1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}b_{q-2\ell}a_{2\ell}J_{q}\right\|_{2}

for any fixed
Q
. Thus if
Q
is fixed then for
\eta\to 0
the first term converges to
0
(as
b^{\eta}_{k}\to b_{k}
for fixed
k
). If
\mathcal{P}_{>Q}
denotes the projection onto the (random) subspace
\oplus_{j>Q}{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
, then the second term is exactly
\|\mathcal{P}_{>Q}(N^{\eta}([na,nb]))\|
, and thanks to boundedness of
\mathcal{P}_{>Q}
and thanks to Lemma
8.1
this term converges to
\|\mathcal{P}_{>Q}(N([na,nb]))\|_{2}
. The third term is exactly
\|\mathcal{P}_{>Q}(L)\|_{2}
. Consequently

\limsup_{\eta\to 0}\|N^{\eta}([na,nb])-L\|_{2}\leq\|\mathcal{P}_{>Q}(N([na,nb]))\|_{2}+\|\mathcal{P}_{>Q}L\|_{2}.

Since both
N([na,nb])
and
L
belong to
L^{2}
, by taking
Q
large enough, we obtain that

0\leq\limsup_{\eta\to 0}\|N^{\eta}([na,nb])-L\|_{2}\leq\epsilon

for any
\epsilon>0
, thus the left hand side limit must be zero. Collecting estimates, we arrive at the conclusion that
\|N([na,nb])-L\|_{2}=0
, and hence
L=N([na,nb])
.

Thus, we just showed that the following chaotic expansion holds (in
L^{2}
)

\displaystyle N([na,nb])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}b_{q-2\ell}a_{2\ell}\int_{na}^{nb}H_{q-2\ell}(\overline{T}_{n}(s))H_{2\ell}({\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{n}(s))v_{n}(s)ds

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{c_{a,b}n}\ I_{q}^{\overline{T}_{n}}([na,nb]).

Now, observe that
I_{1}^{\overline{T}_{n}}=0
(as
b_{1}=0
). On the other hand, as
b_{0}=c_{0}=H_{0}=1
, we have

I_{0}^{\overline{T}_{n}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{a,b}n}}\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)ds=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{a,b}n}}\mathbb{E}N([na,nb]),

where in the last equality we used the Kac-Rice formula (in Edelman-Kostlan’s formulation).

Consequently the following expansion holds in
L^{2}

\frac{N([na,nb])-\mathbb{E}N([na,nb])}{\sqrt{c_{a,b}n}}=\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}I_{q}^{\overline{T}_{n}}([na,nb]),

as desired.
∎

Proof of Lemma
8.1
.

(i) We start with almost surely convergence. Let
N([na,nb])(u)
denote the number of crossings of
\overline{T}_{n}
of level
u
.

We first use the area formula
[
18
]
to write

N^{\eta}([na,nb])=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}N([na,nb])(u)\phi_{\eta}(u)du.

We now show that
N([na,nb])(u)
is locally continuous at
u=0
. Since
N([na,nb])(0)
is bounded above by
n
, it suffices to show that

Lemma 8.2
.

Almost surely
\overline{T}_{n}
has no double root in
[na,nb]
.

We will defer the proof of this result to the end of the section. It follows that given any
\epsilon>0
there is some
\delta>0
such that

|N([na,nb])(u)-N([na,nb])(0)|<\epsilon

for
|u|<\delta
. We choose
\eta\ll\delta
and write

\displaystyle N^{\eta}([na,nb])-N([na,nb])=\int_{|u|>\delta}^{\infty}N([na,nb])(u)\phi_{\eta}(u)du

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{|u|>\delta}^{\infty}N([na,nb])\phi_{\eta}(u)du+\int_{|u|\leq\delta}^{\infty}\Big(N([na,nb])(u)-N([na,nb])(0)\Big)\phi_{\eta}(u)du.

Note that
N([na,nb])(0)\leq n
and
1_{|u|>\delta}\phi_{\eta}(u)
decreases to
0
as
\eta\to 0
, thus by dominated convergence the second term converges to
0
as
\eta\to 0
.

Similarly
N([na,nb])(u)\phi_{\eta}(u)1_{|u|>\delta}
decreases to
0
as
\eta\to 0
, and by dominated convergence the first term also converges to
0
as
\eta\to 0
.

The last term is bounded above by

\int_{|u|\leq\delta}\epsilon\phi_{\eta}(u)du<\epsilon\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi_{\eta}(u)du=\epsilon.

Consequently,

\limsup_{\eta\to 0}|N^{\eta}([na,nb])-N([na,nb])|\leq\epsilon

for any
\epsilon>0
. This proves the almost sure convergence of
N^{\eta}([na,nb])
to
N([na,nb])
when
\eta\to 0
.

We now prove that
\lim_{\eta\to 0}\mathbb{E}|N^{\eta}([na,nb])|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])|^{2}
.

By Fatou’s lemma, we first have

\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])|^{2}\leq\liminf_{\eta\to 0}\mathbb{E}|N^{\eta}([na,nb])|^{2}.

Now, let
N^{\prime}([na,nb])
denote the number of zeros of
{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{n}
(equivalently of
\overline{T}^{\prime}_{n}
) in the same interval. We first note the following.

Lemma 8.3
.

We have
N^{\prime}([na,nb])
is square integrable.

We will prove this result later. To proceed, it is clear that
\sup_{u}N([na,nb])(u)\leq 1+N^{\prime}([na,nb])
. As
N([na,nb])(u)\to N([na,nb])
when
u\to 0
, it follows from an application of the Dominated Convergence Theorem that

\lim_{u\to 0}\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])(u)|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])|^{2}.

The above argument also reveals that
\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])(u)|^{2}
is uniformly bounded by
\mathbb{E}|1+N^{\prime}([na,nb])|^{2}<\infty
.

Now, using the area formula we have

\mathbb{E}|N^{\eta}([na,nb])|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}N([na,nb])(u)\phi_{\eta}(u)du\right|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|N([na,nb])(u)|^{2}\phi_{\eta}(u)du

=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])(u)|^{2}\phi_{\eta}(u)du<\infty.

Since
\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])(u)|^{2}
is continuous at
u=0
and uniformly bounded, and since
\phi_{\eta}\to\delta_{0}
in distribution,

\lim_{\eta\to 0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])(u)|^{2}\phi_{\eta}(u)du=\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])(0)|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])|^{2}.

We obtain

\limsup_{\eta\to 0}\mathbb{E}|N^{\eta}([na,nb])|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])|^{2}.

Collecting estimates, we obtain the desired
L^{2}
equality

\lim_{\eta\to 0}\mathbb{E}|N^{\eta}([na,nb])|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])|^{2}.

From this and the almost sure convergence, it follows via an application of Fatou’s lemma that

2\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])|^{2}\leq\liminf_{\eta\to 0}\left(\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])|^{2}+|N^{\eta}([na,nb])|^{2}-|N([na,nb])-N^{\eta}([na,nb])|^{2}\right),

which equals

2\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])|^{2}-\limsup_{\eta\to 0}\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])-N^{\eta}([na,nb])|^{2}.

It follows that

\lim_{\eta\to 0}\mathbb{E}|N([na,nb])-N^{\eta}([na,nb])|^{2}=0,

i.e.,
N^{\eta}([na,nb])\to N([na,nb])
in
L^{2}
.

(ii) By direct computation (and definition of
b^{\eta}_{k}
) we have the Hermite expansions (see also
[
4
]
)

|\xi|=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}H_{k}(\xi),

\phi_{\eta}(\xi)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}^{\eta}H_{k}(\xi).

Denote
X_{s}=\overline{T}_{n}(s)
and
Y_{s}={\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{n}(s)
, both are mean zero Gaussian processes with variance one. Consider

\xi^{L}(s):=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}a_{2\ell}H_{2\ell}(Y_{s}),

\gamma^{Q}(s):=\sum_{k=0}^{Q}b_{k}^{\eta}H_{k}(X_{s}),

N^{Q,L}:=\int_{na}^{nb}\gamma^{Q}(s)\xi^{L}(s)v_{n}(s)ds.

Note that
\xi^{L}(s)
converges in
L^{2}
to
|Y_{s}|
and
\gamma^{Q}
converges in
L^{2}
to
\phi_{\eta}(X_{s})
. By convexity and the triangle inequality, for any
Q,L,Q^{\prime},L^{\prime}

|N^{Q^{\prime},L^{\prime}}-N^{Q,L}|\leq\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)|(\gamma^{Q^{\prime}}(s)-\gamma^{Q}(s))\xi^{L^{\prime}}(s)|ds+\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)|\gamma^{Q}(s)(\xi^{L^{\prime}}(s)-\xi^{L}(s))|ds.

So,

(\mathbb{E}|N^{Q^{\prime},L^{\prime}}-N^{Q,L}|^{2})^{1/2}\leq\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)\big(\mathbb{E}|\gamma^{Q^{\prime}}(s)-\gamma^{Q}(s)|^{2}|\xi^{L^{\prime}}(s)|^{2}\big)^{1/2}ds

+\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)\big(\mathbb{E}|\gamma^{Q}(s)|^{2}(\xi^{L^{\prime}}(s)-\xi^{L}(s))^{2}\big)^{1/2}ds.

Using independence of
X_{s}
and
Y_{s}
, the last right hand side of the above equation is the same as

\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)\big(\mathbb{E}|\gamma^{Q^{\prime}}(s)-\gamma^{Q}(s)|^{2}\big)^{1/2}\big(\mathbb{E}|\xi^{L^{\prime}}(s)|^{2}\big)^{1/2}ds+\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)\big(\mathbb{E}|\gamma^{Q}(s)|^{2}\big)^{1/2}\big(\mathbb{E}(\xi^{L^{\prime}}(s)-\xi^{L}(s))^{2}\big)^{1/2}ds

\leq\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)\big(\mathbb{E}|\gamma^{Q^{\prime}}(s)-\gamma^{Q}(s)|^{2}\big)^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}|X_{s}|^{2})^{1/2}ds+\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)\big(\mathbb{E}|Y_{s}|^{2}\big)^{1/2}\big(\mathbb{E}(\xi^{L^{\prime}}(s)-\xi^{L}(s))^{2}\big)^{1/2}ds.

Note that
\mathbb{E}|X_{s}|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|Y_{s}|^{2}=1
. Using orthogonality,

\mathbb{E}|\gamma^{Q^{\prime}}-\gamma^{Q}|^{2}=\sum_{k\text{ between Q,Q'}}|b_{k}^{\eta}|^{2}k!\to 0\mbox{ as }\min(Q,Q^{\prime})\to\infty

because
\sum_{k\geq 0}|b_{k}^{\eta}|^{2}k!=\mathbb{E}|X_{s}|^{2}=1
.

Similarly,
\mathbb{E}|\xi^{L^{\prime}}-\xi^{L}|^{2}\to 0
as
\min(L,L^{\prime})\to\infty
.

Since
\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)ds=\mathbb{E}N([na,nb])<\infty
, by Dominated Convergence Theorem we obtain

\lim_{\min(Q,L,Q^{\prime},L^{\prime})\to\infty}(\mathbb{E}|N^{Q^{\prime},L^{\prime}}-N^{Q,L}|^{2})^{1/2}=0.

(42)

Note that the above argument works even if
Q^{\prime}=L^{\prime}=\infty
, thus we actually proved that

\lim_{\min(Q,L)\to\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}|N^{Q,L}-\int_{na}^{nb}\phi_{\eta}(X_{s})|Y_{s}|v_{n}(s)ds|^{2}\right)^{1/2}=0.

(43)

To proceed, we observe that for any
(k,\ell)\neq(k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})
and any
s
the following holds:

\mathbb{E}\Big(H_{k}(X_{s})H_{\ell}(Y_{s})H_{k^{\prime}}(X_{s})H_{\ell^{\prime}}(Y_{s})\Big)=0.

(44)

(Indeed, without loss of generality assume
k\neq k^{\prime}
. In this case, by the independence of
X_{s}
and
Y_{s}
, we notice that
\mathbb{E}(H_{k}(X_{s})H_{k^{\prime}}(X_{s}))=0
as
X_{s}
is standard Gaussian.)

Thus by (
44
)

\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{\ell+k\leq Q}b_{k}^{\eta}a_{\ell}\int_{na}^{nb}H_{k}(X_{s})H_{\ell}(Y_{s})v_{n}(s)ds-N^{Q,Q}\Big|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

=\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{q=Q+1}^{2Q}\sum_{\ell=q-Q}^{Q}b_{q-\ell}^{\eta}a_{\ell}\int_{na}^{nb}H_{k}(X_{s})H_{\ell}(Y_{s})v_{n}(s)ds\Big|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\leq\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{q=Q+1}^{2Q}\sum_{\ell=q-Q}^{Q}b_{q-\ell}^{\eta}a_{\ell}H_{k}(X_{s})H_{\ell}(Y_{s})\Big|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}ds

=\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)\Big(\sum_{q=Q+1}^{2Q}\sum_{\ell=q-Q}^{Q}\mathbb{E}\Big|b_{q-\ell}^{\eta}a_{\ell}H_{k}(X_{s})H_{\ell}(Y_{s})\Big|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}ds

\leq\int_{na}^{nb}v_{n}(s)\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big|\xi^{Q}(s)\gamma^{Q}(s)-\xi^{[Q/3]}(s)\gamma^{[Q/3]}(s)\Big|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}ds.

Arguing as in (
42
), the last display converges to
0
as
Q\to\infty
. Henceforth, by (
43
) and by the triangle inequality,
\sum_{\ell+k\leq Q}b_{k}^{\eta}a_{\ell}\int_{na}^{nb}H_{k}(X_{s})H_{\ell}(Y_{s})v_{n}(s)ds
converges to
N^{\eta}([na,nb])
in
L^{2}
as
Q\to\infty
. Note that
a_{k}=0
if
k
is odd, thus this completes the proof of (ii).
∎

It remains to justify the supporting lemmas.

Proof of Lemma
8.2
.

We follow the argument in
[
11
]
. Thanks to the lower bound for
V_{n}
that follows from Lemma
2.2
, it suffices to show that the polynomial (of bounded degree)
\widetilde{T}_{n}
has no double root in any given compact interval. By conditioning on
\xi_{j},j\geq 1
, it is not hard to see that for any
t
there is a constant
C
that may depend on
t,n
such that
\sup_{\gamma}P(|\widetilde{T}_{n}(t)-\gamma|<\epsilon)\leq C\epsilon
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Now assume towards a contradiction that there is some
t\in I:=[na,nb]
such that
\widetilde{T}_{n}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{T}_{n}(t)=0
. We may divide
I
into
O(1/\epsilon)
subintervals of length at most
\epsilon
, and one such interval will contain
t
, and if
c
is the center of this subinterval then using the mean value theorem we can easily show that
|\widetilde{T}_{n}(c)|\leq C\epsilon^{2}
(for some constant
C
that may depend on
n
but independent of
\epsilon
). However, using the union bound and the above small ball inequality, it follows that the probability that there is a subinterval (among the
O(1/\epsilon)
intervals) whose center satisfy such estimate is
O(\epsilon^{2})O(1/\epsilon)=O(\epsilon)
. Consequently the probability that there is a double root of
\widetilde{T}_{n}
in
I
is
O(\epsilon)
for any
\epsilon>0
, which implies the desired claim.
∎

Proof of Lemma
8.3
.

The proof is similar to that of Lemma 4 in
[
4
]
. For brevity, we write
X(t):=\overline{T}^{\prime}_{n}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\xi_{j}q_{j}^{\prime}(t)
. Our goal is to show that, for
N_{X}
being the number of zeros of the process
X
over
[na,nb]
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}N_{X}^{2}<\infty
. For this, we need to consider the behavior of high derivatives of
X
. More specifically we use

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(X^{(6)}(s)-X^{(6)}(t))^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}(\sum_{j=0}^{n}\xi_{j}(q_{j}^{(7)}(s)-q_{j}^{(7)}(t)))^{2}=\sum_{j}(q_{j}^{(7)}(s)-q_{j}^{(7)}(t))^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(\int_{s}^{t}q_{j}^{(8)}(\eta)d\eta)^{2}\leq(\int_{s}^{t}(\sum_{j=0}^{n}q_{j}^{(8)}(\eta)^{2})^{1/2}d\eta)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(s-t)^{2}\max_{\eta\in(s,t)}\sum_{j=0}^{n}(q_{j}^{(8)}(\eta))^{2}\leq C(s-t)^{2},

where we used (v) of Lemma
1.6
in the last estimate and Minkowski’s inequality in the second to last estimate.
As a consequence, Dudley’s theorem (see for instance
[
6
, Theorem 2.10]
) applied to the Gaussian process
(X^{(6)}(t))_{t\in[na,nb]}
(noting that as the metric is bounded by
C|s-t|
by above, the covering number
N_{\varepsilon}
satisfies that
N_{\varepsilon}\leq\min\{C^{\prime}n/\varepsilon,1\}
, where
C^{\prime}
depends on
C,a,b
) implies that

{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{t\in[na,nb]}X_{t}^{(6)}=O\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\log(N_{\varepsilon})^{1/2}d\varepsilon\right)=O(n).

We also note that the density of
X(t)
is bounded since the variance
\sum_{j=0}^{n}(q_{j}^{\prime}(t))^{2}
is bounded away from zero (by (i) of Lemma
1.6
). We can then apply
[
6
, Theorem 3.6]
to the process
X(t)
with
m=2,p=5
, and deduce that
{\mathbb{E}}N_{X}^{2}<\infty
.
∎
