Flow of the zeros of polynomials under iterated differentiation
Andrei Martínez-Finkelshtein, Evguenii A. Rakhmanov

Abstract
. For a monic polynomial Q n Q_{n} of degree n n , let Q n , k Q_{n,k} be its k k -th derivative normalized to be monic. Under the only assumption that the sequence { Q n } \{Q_{n}\} has a weak* limiting zero distribution (an empirical distribution of zeros) represented by a probability measure μ 0 \mu_{0} with compact support in the complex plane, we show that as n , k → ∞ n,k\rightarrow\infty such that k / n → t ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) k/n\rightarrow t\in(0,1) , the Cauchy transform of the normalized zero-counting measure of the polynomials Q n , k Q_{n,k} converges in a neighborhood of infinity to an analytic function, uniquely determined by μ 0 \mu_{0} and t t , that can be written as the Cauchy transform of a measure μ t \mu_{t} , not necessarily uniquely determined unless μ 0 \mu_{0} is supported on the real line. The family of these Cauchy transforms and, when well defined, the corresponding measures μ t \mu_{t} , t ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) t\in(0,1) , whose dependence on the parameter t t can be interpreted as a flow of the zeros under iterated differentiation, has several interesting connections with the inviscid Burgers equation, the fractional free convolution of μ 0 \mu_{0} , or a nonlocal diffusion equation governing the density of μ t \mu_{t} on ℝ \mathbb{R} . We provide an elementary and unified approach that not only recovers, but also explains various phenomena observed in prior works – from Burgers-type PDEs to free probability limits.

1.
Introduction

In recent years, the problem of repeated differentiation of polynomials has been intensively studied from different points of view and in different connections. In this Introduction, we start by formulating our main result. After that, we make a few remarks about the classical roots of the problem and briefly comment on some recent results.

Hereafter, the asymptotic zero distribution of a sequence of polynomials (also known as its empirical distribution of zeros) is understood in terms of the weak-* limits of their zero-counting measures. We define the zero-counting measure of a polynomial
P
as

\chi(P)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\sum_{P(x)=0}\delta_{x},

(1.1)

where the sum is taken over all zeros of
P
with account of multiplicities, and
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac measure (unit mass) supported at
x
. This way,
\chi(P)
is independent of normalization of
P
, and

\chi(P)(\mathbb{C})=\deg(P).

We are interested in the following problem: given a sequence of (monic) polynomials
Q_{n}
,
\deg Q_{n}=n
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and the triangular table of their iterated derivatives

Q_{n,k}(x)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\,\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}Q_{n}(x)=x^{n-k}+\text{lower degree terms},\quad k=0,1,\dots,n-1,

(1.2)

what are the possible limit distributions of the normalized zero-counting measures

\sigma_{n,k}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\frac{1}{n}\chi\left(Q_{n,k}\right)

(1.3)

as
n\to\infty
in such a way that
k/n\to t\in[0,1)
, if we only know that
\sigma_{n,0}
have a weak-* limit, compactly supported on
\mathbb{C}
?

A weaker form of this question deals with the convergence of the logarithmic derivatives

v_{n,k}(z)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\frac{Q^{\prime}_{n,k}(z)}{n\,Q_{n,k}(z)}.

(1.4)

The main result of this paper is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
Q_{n}
be a sequence of polynomials whose zeros belong to a compact convex set
S
. Assume also that the limit

\lim_{n}\frac{Q^{\prime}_{n}(z)}{n\,Q_{n}(z)}=u(z)

exists uniformly on compact subsets of
\Omega=\mathbb{C}\setminus S
.

Then for any
t\in[0,1)
and any sequence
k_{n}
of natural numbers with
k_{n}/n\rightarrow t
we have that

\lim_{n}v_{n,k}(z)=\lim_{n}\frac{Q^{\prime}_{n,k}(z)}{n\,Q_{n,k}(z)}=u(z,t)

(1.5)

uniformly on compact subsets of
\Omega
, where
u(z,t)
is uniquely determined by the equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(z,t)=\frac{1}{u(z,t)}\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}(z,t)

(1.6)

with the initial condition
u(z,0)=u(z)
.

Equation (
1.6
) is a special case of a nonlinear wave equation, known as the inviscid Burgers or Hopf equation.

The Hopf equation is known for a remarkable phenomenon: its solutions can develop singularities (or shocks), even when posed on the real line. However, this behavior does not occur when the initial condition

The fact that in the situation of complex zeros, not much more than (

Let us comment on the classical roots of the problem of zeros of derivatives of polynomials.

In this paper, we address the problem of the macroscopic description of the zeros of polynomials under repeated differentiation, with the most general assumptions stated in Theorem
1.1
. However, our goal is not only to prove (and partially reprove) the results but also to show why they are true. The main feature of our approach is that it is totally elementary and uses only basic facts from complex analysis.

Our main findings can be summarized as follows. The complex Hopf equation (
1.6
) plays the main role. The connection of the problem on the flow of derivatives under differentiation with this equation lies in the elementary fact that the Cauchy transform of the zero-counting measure of a polynomial can be expressed in terms of the logarithmic derivative of this polynomial. As a first step, we show that if its initial value is given by the Cauchy transform of a compactly supported probability measure
\mu_{0}
, then there is a universal domain, depending only on the support of
\mu_{0}
, where not only do the solutions of the equation remain analytic, but they can be written as the Cauchy transform of a measure for all values of the parameter
t\in(0,1)

We show that the solution of the Hopf equation by the method of characteristics is precisely the expression of the fact that
\mu_{t}
can be written as the fractional free convolution of
\mu_{0}

Finally, we show that the nonlocal diffusion from
[

is merely a projection of the Hopf equation onto the real line via the Sohotski-Plemelj identities (or the Stieltjes–Perron inversion formula).

The initial value problem for the Hopf equation, whose existence and analyticity have been established in Theorem

\Omega_{R}
, for any

, and for
any
initial condition given by the Cauchy transform of
any
probability measure supported in

. In particular, if for each

, we denote by

.

As in (

), define for

,

v_{n,k}(z)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\mathcal{C}^{\sigma_{n,k}}(z)=\frac{Q^{\prime}_{n,k}(z)}{n\,Q_{n,k}(z)}=\frac{n-k}{n}\,\frac{Q_{n,k+1}(z)}{Q_{n,k}(z)}.

For all such

is holomorphic in
\overline{\Omega_{r}}

v_{n,k}(z)=\frac{n-k}{n}\,\frac{1}{z}+\mathcal{O}(z^{-2}),\quad z\to\infty.

Taking the logarithmic derivative in both sides of (

v_{n,k+1}(z)-v_{n,k}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{\partial_{z}\,v_{n,k}(z)}{v_{n,k}(z)}.

This identity can be regarded as a discrete version of Hopf’s equation (

), and thus, it is natural to expect that under our assumptions, functions

and

are close for sufficiently large

If additionally

\sigma_{n,0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu_{0},\quad n\to\infty,

(2.20)

These heuristic arguments can be made rigorous, although the trivially looking assertion about the proximity of the solutions to the “discrete” and “continuous” Hopf equations requires some careful analysis due to the nonlinear character of these equations. For convenience of the reader, we formulate Theorem

here, in a more precise form:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let

be a sequence of polynomials with uniformly bounded zeros such that (

) holds.
Then for any

, there exists a positive Borel measure

with
\supp\mu_{t}\subset\conv(\supp\mu_{0})
and
\mu_{t}(\mathbb{C})=1-t

\mathcal{C}^{\mu_{t}}(z)=u(z,t),

(2.21)

where

is the solution of equation (
2.1
) with the initial condition (
2.5
).

Furthermore, for any sequence
k_{n}
of natural numbers with
k_{n}/n\rightarrow t
we have that

\lim_{n}\mathcal{C}^{\sigma_{n,k_{n}}}(z)=\mathcal{C}^{\mu_{t}}(z)

locally uniformly in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\left(\conv(\supp\mu_{0})\right)

Finally, if all zeros of

are real, then
\mu_{t}
is uniquely determined from

, and

\sigma_{n,k_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu_{t},\quad n\to\infty.

Remark 2.5
.

In the statement above,
\conv(K)
denotes the convex hull of the set
K
.

With the assumptions of the theorem, let
T=(1+t)/2
, and let
R=R(T)
be as in Theorem

\lim_{n}\mathcal{C}^{\sigma_{n,k_{n}}}(z)=u(z,t)

locally uniformly in

\sigma_{n,k_{n}}
, there exists a measure

along a subsequence of

As a consequence, the Cauchy transform of the measure

uniquely, which makes the statement of Theorem

Finally, we recast Theorem

in the terminology of free probability; see e.g.

is an analytic function in a neighborhood of the origin. For

fractional free additive convolution

Let

\mu

Recall that
\mathcal{C}^{\mu}

, and that the series in (

, disjoint from
\supp(\mu)
. We assume additionally that

(3.1)

and denote by

the total variation of
\mu
; since
\mu
is signed, in general,
|\mu|(\mathbb{C})\geq m_{0}
.

As a first step, we need some uniform estimates on

:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mu
be a finite Borel and compactly-supported signed measure on
\mathbb{C}
, such that (
3.1
) holds, and let
0<r<+\infty
be such that
\supp(\mu)\subset\mathbb{D}_{r}
. Then for any
R>(1+|\mu|(\mathbb{C}))\,r
,
\mathcal{C}^{\mu}
is analytic in
\overline{\Omega_{R}}
and does not vanish for
z\in\Omega_{R}
.

If in addition
\mu
is a positive (probability) measure, then for
|z|\geq R>2r
,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{9}<\frac{1-r/R}{(1+r/R)^{2}}\leq\left|z\mathcal{C}^{\mu}(z)\right|\leq\frac{R}{R-r},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1-2r/R}{(1+r/R)^{4}}\leq\left|z^{2}\,\frac{d}{dz}\mathcal{C}^{\mu}(z)\right|\leq\frac{1}{(1-r/R)^{2}}<4.

(3.3)

Moreover, if
R>(\sqrt{2}+1)r
, then
C^{\mu}
is univalent (injective) in
\Omega_{R}
, and

\overline{\mathbb{D}_{1/(4R)}}\subset C^{\mu}\left(\overline{\Omega_{R}}\right)\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}_{1/(R-r)}}.

(3.4)

Remark 3.2
.

As formula (
2.13
) in Remark
2.2
(b) shows, for a signed measure
\mu
,
R
in the first assertion of Proposition
3.1
, in general, cannot be made independent of
|\mu|
.

Proof.

For an arbitrary
R>r
, function
z\,\mathcal{C}^{\mu}(z)
is analytic in
\overline{\Omega_{R}}
and can be written as

z\mathcal{C}^{\mu}(z)=1+\int\frac{y}{z-y}\,d\mu(t).

Since for
|z|=R
,

\left|\int\frac{y}{z-y}\,d\mu(t)\right|\leq\frac{r}{R-r}|\mu(\mathbb{C})|,

if
R>r
is such that

\frac{r}{R-r}|\mu|(\mathbb{C})<1\quad\text{or, equivalently,}\quad R>r(1+|\mu|(\mathbb{C})),

then by Rouché’s theorem,
\mathcal{C}^{\mu}(z)\neq 0
in
\overline{\Omega_{R}}
.
In particular, if
\mu
is a probability measure, this is true for any
R>2r
.

Assuming that
\mu
is a probability measure with
\supp(\mu)\subset\mathbb{D}_{r}
, we have
|\mu|(\mathbb{C})=1
, and the bound obtained above gives us that for
|z|=R>2r
,

\left|z\mathcal{C}^{\mu}(z)\right|=\left|1+\int\frac{y}{z-y}\,d\mu(t)\right|\leq 1+\frac{r}{R-r}=\frac{R}{R-r}.

On the other hand,

\left|z\,\mathcal{C}^{\mu}(z)\right|\geq\mathop{\rm Re}\left(z\,\mathcal{C}^{\mu}(z)\right)=\int\mathop{\rm Re}\left(\frac{1}{1-y/z}\right)\,d\mu(y),

which gives us that for
|z|=R>2r
and
|y|\leq r<R
,

\mathop{\rm Re}\left(\frac{1}{1-y/z}\right)=\frac{1-\mathop{\rm Re}(y/z)}{|1-y/z|^{2}}\geq\frac{1-r/R}{(1+r/R)^{2}}>\frac{2}{9},

and the bounds in (
3.2
) follow.

In a similar fashion,

\left|z^{2}\,\frac{d}{dz}\mathcal{C}^{\mu}(z)\right|=\left|\int\frac{1}{(1-y/z)^{2}}\,d\mu(t)\right|\leq\frac{1}{(1-r/R)^{2}}<4,

and

\left|z^{2}\,\frac{d}{dz}\mathcal{C}^{\mu}(z)\right|\geq\mathop{\rm Re}\left(-z^{2}\,\frac{d}{dz}\mathcal{C}^{\mu}(z)\right)=\int\mathop{\rm Re}\frac{1}{\left(1-y/z\right)^{2}}\,d\mu(y).

For
|z|\geq R>2r
and
y\in\mathbb{D}_{r}
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Re}\left(1-y/z\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=1-2\mathop{\rm Re}\left(\frac{y}{z}\right)+\mathop{\rm Re}\left(\frac{y}{z}\right)^{2}=1-2\mathop{\rm Re}\left(\frac{y}{z}\right)+\left(\mathop{\rm Re}\frac{y}{z}\right)^{2}-\left(\mathop{\rm Im}\frac{y}{z}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\mathop{\rm Re}\frac{y}{z}\right)^{2}-\left(\mathop{\rm Im}\frac{y}{z}\right)^{2}\geq\left(1-\mathop{\rm Re}\frac{y}{z}\right)^{2}-\left|\frac{y}{z}\right|^{2}\geq\left(1-\frac{r}{R}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2r}{R}>0,

so that

\mathop{\rm Re}\frac{1}{\left(1-y/z\right)^{2}}=\frac{\mathop{\rm Re}\left(1-y/z\right)^{2}}{\left|1-y/z\right|^{4}}\geq\frac{1-2r/R}{(1+r/R)^{4}},

and hence,

\left|z^{2}\,\frac{d}{dz}\mathcal{C}^{\mu}(z)\right|\geq\frac{1-2r/R}{(1+r/R)^{4}},\qquad|z|\geq R>2r.

This concludes the proof of (
3.3
).

Furthermore, for
R>r
, consider the function

f_{R}(z)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}R\,\mathcal{C}^{\mu}\left(\frac{R}{z}\right)=z\int\frac{1}{1-zy/R}\,d\mu(y)=z+\mathcal{O}\left(z^{2}\right),\quad z\to 0,

(3.5)

defined in the unit disk
D_{1}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z|<1\}
.
We have that

\mathop{\rm Re}\left(\frac{f_{R}(z)}{z}\right)=\int\mathop{\rm Re}\left(\frac{1}{1-zy/R}\right)\,d\mu(y)=\int\frac{1-\mathop{\rm Re}\left(zy\right)/R}{|1-zy/R|^{2}}\,d\mu(y).

Since

1-\frac{\mathop{\rm Re}\left(zy\right)}{R}\geq 1-\frac{\left|zy\right|}{R}\geq 1-\frac{r}{R}>0,

we conclude that
\mathop{\rm Re}(f_{R}(z)/z)>0
in
|z|<1
, and by
[
23
, Theorem 3]
,
f_{R}
is univalent in
|z|<\sqrt{2}-1
(or, equivalently,
\mathcal{C}^{\mu}
is univalent in
\Omega_{(\sqrt{2}+1)R}
).

Moreover, with
R>(\sqrt{2}+1)r
, by the Koebe
1/4
theorem (see, e.g.
[
10
, §2.2]
), the image
f_{R}(\mathbb{D}_{1})
of the unit disk by the function

contains the disk
\overline{\mathbb{D}_{1/4}}
, or in other words, the image of
\overline{\Omega_{R}}
by
\mathcal{C}^{\mu}
contains the disk
\overline{\mathbb{D}_{1/(4R)}}
.
∎

It is well known that the Hopf equation (
2.1
) can be solved by the method of characteristics. In preparation to the analysis of this solution, we prove the following auxiliary result:

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\mu
be a finite Borel signed measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{C}
, such that (
3.1
) holds.
Assume
0<r<+\infty
is such that
\supp\mu\subset\mathbb{D}_{r}
, and let
0<T<1
be fixed. Then there exists an
R>2r
, depending only on the values of
T
,
|m_{1}|
, and
|\mu|(\mathbb{C})

f_{1}(s)=\mathcal{C}^{\mu}\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)=\int\frac{s}{1-sy}\,d\mu(y)

is analytic in
\overline{\mathbb{D}_{1/R}}
, does not vanish for
0<|s|\leq 1/R
, and for each
z\in\mathbb{D}_{1/(2R)}
and each

z-s-\frac{zst}{f_{1}(s)}=0

has a unique solution

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
, if
R>r(1+|\mu|(\mathbb{C}))
then
f_{1}(z)
is analytic and non-vanishing for
0<|z|<1/R
. The assertion about the unisolvence of the equation (

Write

f_{1}(s)=s+s^{2}g(s),

(3.7)

with

g(s)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\int\frac{y}{1-sy}\,d\mu(y),

(3.8)

so that

g(0)=\int y\,d\mu(y)=m_{1},

the first moment of
\mu
, see (

Notice that

|g(s)-g(0)|=\left|\int\frac{y}{1-sy}\,d\mu(y)-\int y\,d\mu(y)\right|=|s|\left|\int\frac{y^{2}}{1-sy}\,d\mu(y)\right|,

and for
|s|=1/R

|g(s)-g(0)|=\frac{1}{R}\,\left|\int\frac{y^{2}}{1-sy}\,d\mu(y)\right|\leq\frac{r^{2}}{R-r}|\mu|(\mathbb{C}).

If we take
R>r(1+|\mu|(\mathbb{C}))
such that

\frac{r^{2}}{R-r}|\mu|(\mathbb{C})<1\quad\text{or, equivalently,}\quad R>r(1+r|\mu|(\mathbb{C})),

then

|g(s)|\leq 1+|m_{1}|,\quad s\in\overline{\mathbb{D}_{1/R}}.

(3.9)

Now, given
T\in(0,1)
, let
R>\max\{r(1+|\mu|(\mathbb{C})),r(1+r|\mu|(\mathbb{C}))\}
be such that

R>\left(1+|m_{1}|\right)\frac{1+T}{1-T}\quad\text{or, equivalently,}\quad 0<\frac{T}{1-(|m_{1}|+1)/R}<\frac{1+T}{2}<1.

Then for

0\leq\frac{|g(s)|}{R}\leq\frac{1+|m_{1}|}{R}<\frac{1-T}{1+T}<1,

so that

\left|\frac{t}{1+sg(s)}\right|\leq\frac{T}{|1-|g(s)|/R|}\leq\frac{T}{1-|g(s)|/R}<\frac{1+T}{2}<1,

and in consequence, for

\left|\frac{zst}{f_{1}(s)}\right|=\left|\frac{zt}{1+sg(s)}\right|<|z|.

(3.11)

Thus, since for

and

we have

we can apply Rouché’s theorem to claim that for every

, the equation (

All assertions have been proved for any

m_{k}(t)=(1-t)^{k}m_{k}(0)-(1-t)^{k}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{f_{k}^{\prime}(u)}{(1-u)^{k+1}}\,du.

(3.21)

Notice that

Integrating in (
3.21
) by parts

, we get that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{f_{k}^{\prime}(u)}{(1-u)^{k+1}}\,du

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{j-1}(k-j)!}{k!}\frac{f_{k}^{(j)}(u)}{(1-u)^{k+1-j}}\right)\bigg|_{0}^{t},

so that

\displaystyle-(1-t)^{k}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\frac{f_{k}^{\prime}(u)}{(1-u)^{k+1}}\,du\right]

\displaystyle=(1-t)^{k}d+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{j}(k-j)!}{k!}\,f_{k}^{(j)}(t)\,(1-t)^{j-1},

where

Combining all in (
3.21

\displaystyle m_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=m_{k}(0)(1-t)^{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}\frac{(k-j)!}{k!}\,\left[f_{k}^{(j)}(t)-f_{k}^{(j)}(0)\,(1-t)^{k-j+1}\right]\,(1-t)^{j-1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{k}\,f^{\prime}_{k}(t)+\frac{1}{k(k-1)}\,f^{\prime\prime}_{k}(t)\,(1-t)+\mathcal{O}((1-t)^{2}),\quad t\to 1.

By (
3.22
),
f^{\prime}_{k}(1)=0
, so that
m_{k}(1)=0
, and

\displaystyle-m_{k}^{\prime}(1)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 1}\frac{m_{k}(t)}{1-t}=\lim_{t\to 1}\left(-\frac{1}{k}\,\frac{f^{\prime}_{k}(t)}{1-t}+\frac{1}{k(k-1)}\,f^{\prime\prime}_{k}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{k}^{\prime\prime}(1)}{k-1}=m_{1}(0)^{k},

which proves the assertion.
∎

5.
Some examples

We finish our exposition by presenting a few simple examples, starting with some paradigmatic zero distributions on the real line.

It is well known that under very mild assumptions on the orthogonality measure on the interval
[-1,1]
(such as having a strictly positive Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to the Lebesgue measure almost everywhere on
[-1,1]
, see, e.g.
[
36
]
), the limit zero distribution of the corresponding orthogonal polynomials is the the equilibrium measure (or arc-sine distribution) of the interval
[-1,1]
, that is,

d\mu_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx,\quad x\in(-1,1),

for which

u(z,0)=\mathcal{C}^{\mu_{0}}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{z^{2}-1}}=\frac{1}{z}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1],

and consequently,

u^{-1}(w,0)=\frac{\sqrt{w^{2}+1}}{w}.

By (
2.11
), solving the equation

z=\frac{\sqrt{w^{2}+1}-t}{w}

we get that

w=u(z,t)=\frac{-tz+\sqrt{z^{2}-(1-t^{2})}}{z^{2}-1}.

Since the residues of the right hand side at
\pm 1
are
0
, we conclude that
\mu_{t}
is absolutely continuous and

d\mu_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\sqrt{1-t^{2}-x^{2}}}{1-x^{2}}\,dx,\qquad x\in[-\sqrt{1-t^{2}},\sqrt{1-t^{2}}],

(5.1)

where we have used the Stieltjes–Perron inversion formula.

Another notable case is the semicircle distribution on the interval
[-1,1]
,

d\mu_{0}(x)=\frac{2}{\pi}\,\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,dx,\quad x\in(-1,1),

corresponding, for instance, to the zero limit distribution of the appropriately rescaled Hermite polynomials. Since

u(z,0)=2\left(z-\sqrt{z^{2}-1}\right)=\frac{1}{z}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1],

and

solving the equation

z=\frac{w^{2}+4-4t}{4w},

we get that

w=u(z,t)=2\left(z-\sqrt{z^{2}-(1-t)}\right).

Again,
\mu_{t}
is absolutely continuous probability measure given by

d\mu_{t}(x)=\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-t-x^{2}}\,dx,\quad x\in[-\sqrt{1-t},\sqrt{1-t}],

(5.2)

the rescaled semicircle distribution for all values of
t\in[0,1)
.

The case of the Marchenko-Pastur distribution on
[0,4]
, given by

d\mu_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{\sqrt{x(4-x)}}{x}\,dx,

that describes the asymptotic behavior of singular values of large random matrices (or equivalently, the asymptotic zero distribution of rescaled Laguerre polynomials) is analyzed in a similar way.

On the other hand, a natural case to consider is of the normalized uniform distribution on
[-1,1]
. It turns out it is remarkably challenging; indeed, in this case

u(z,0)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{z+1}{z-1}\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1],

and

u^{-1}(w,0)=\frac{v+1}{v-1},\quad v=e^{2w}.

However, the equation

z=\frac{v+1}{v-1}-\frac{t}{\log v},\quad v=e^{2w}

(5.3)

is highly transcendental and does not have an explicit solution. We can use this representation to find the support of the measure
\mu_{t}
, which again appears to be a formidable task.

\mu_{0}

]

and equation (

\mu_{0}
.

mP(z+tv)-vP^{\prime}(z+tv)=0,\quad v=\frac{1}{w}.

Using that

P(z+s)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{P^{(j)}(z)}{j!}s^{j}

this yields an algebraic equation for
w=u(z,t)
:

\sum_{j=0}^{m}\left(m-\frac{j}{t}\right)\frac{P^{(j)}(z)}{j!}\,t^{j}w^{m-j}=0

(5.4)

also derived in
[
6
]
.

For instance, in our first example (
1.7
), when
P(z)=z^{2}-1
and
m=2
,

u(z,0)=\frac{z}{z^{2}-1},

and (
5.4
) yields the quadratic equation for
w=u(z,t)
:

(z^{2}-1)w^{2}-(1-2t)zw-(1-t)t=0.

Thus,

u(z,t)=\frac{(1-2t)z-\sqrt{4(t-1)t+z^{2}}}{2\left(z^{2}-1\right)},

and we get the expressions (
1.8
)–(
1.9
) for measure
\mu_{t}
.

Remark 5.1
.

Ravichandran
[
30
]
(see also
[
39
]
) proved that for
t\geq 1/2
, the ratio of the diameters of the sets of zeros of polynomials
Q_{n,k}(x)
, defined in (
1.2
), and
Q_{n}
, for
k=[nt]
is bounded above by
2\sqrt{t(1-t)}
. Obviously, both (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) are in agreement with this bound, and formulas (
1.8
)–(
1.9
) show that the assumption that
t\geq 1/2
cannot be omitted.

On the other hand, in Kalyagin’s example (
1.11
), when
P(z)=z(z^{2}-1)
and
m=3
,
(
5.4
) yields the following cubic equation for
w=u(z,t)
:

3z\left(z^{2}-1\right)w^{3}+(3t-1)\left(3z^{2}-1\right)w^{2}+3t(3t-2)zw-3t^{2}(1-t)=0.

For
t\in(0,1)
, the end-points of the support of
\mu_{t}
can be found from the branch points of
w
, which are solutions of the bi-quadratic equation

9z^{4}+3\left(9t^{2}-6t-2\right)z^{2}-(1-t)(3t-1)^{3}=0,

see
[
20
]
.

Finally, if
P
has complex roots, such as
P(z)=z^{3}-1
, (
5.4
) yields again a cubic equation for
w=u(z,t)
:

(z^{3}-1)w^{3}-(1-3t)z^{2}w^{2}-(2-3t)tzw-(1-t)t^{2}=0,

whose branch points are solutions of the equation

27t^{3}-54t^{2}+27t-4z^{3}=0,

that is, the three cube roots

z=3\sqrt[3]{\frac{t(1-t)^{2}}{4}}.

Appendix A
Auxiliary results

Denote by
\mathcal{H}(R,M,c)
the class of functions
f
satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

f
is analytic in the closed disc
\overline{\mathbb{D}_{R}}
;

(ii)

|f(z)|\leq M
for
z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}_{R}}
;

(iii)

f^{\prime}(0)=c>0
.

Proposition A.1
.

For
R,M,c>0
, let

r_{1}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\min\left\{\frac{R}{2},\,\frac{cR^{2}}{4M}\right\}.

(A.1)

Then for
r\leq r_{1}
, any function
f\in\mathcal{H}(R,M,c)
is univalent in
\overline{\mathbb{D}_{r}}
,

f(\overline{\mathbb{D}_{r}})
covers the disc
\overline{\mathbb{D}_{\rho}}
with
\rho=cr/2
.

Consequently,
f^{-1}
is univalent in
\overline{\mathbb{D}_{\rho}}
and
f^{-1}(\overline{D_{\rho}})\subset\mathbb{D}_{r}
.

Proof.

Let
f(z)=cz+h(z)\in\mathcal{H}(R,M,c)
,
h(z)=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}
. Since
h
is holomorphic in the closed disc
\overline{\mathbb{D}_{R}}
, the Cauchy unequalities imply that
|c_{n}|\leq M/R^{n}
,
n=2,3,\dots
, so that for any
r<R
,

|h(z)|\leq M\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}(r/R)^{n}=M\frac{(r/R)^{2}}{1-(r/R)},\quad z\in\mathbb{D}_{r}.

If
r\leq R/2
then
1-r/R\geq 1/2
, and

|h(z)|\leq 2M(r/R)^{2},\quad z\in\mathbb{D}_{r}.

Also,

r\leq\frac{cR^{2}}{4M}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad cr\geq 4M\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{2}.

This means that if we assume
r\leq r_{1}
, it implies that for
z\in C_{r}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\partial\mathbb{D}_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z|=r\}
,
|cz|\geq 2|h(z)|>0
. This means that the image of the circle
C_{r}
by
f
lies in the annulus
cr/2=\rho\leq|z|\leq 3\rho
and, by Rouché’s theorem, its index with respect to the origin is one. The index of
f(C_{r})
with respect to any point
f(a)\notin f(C_{r})
is constant in any connected component of the complement to
f(C_{r})
, and is equal to the multiplicity of
f(a)
in
D_{r}
. We conclude that this index is
1
for any
a\in\mathbb{D}_{r}
, concluding that
f
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}_{r}
.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}(R,M,c)
and

r_{0}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\min\left\{\frac{R}{2},\,\frac{cR^{2}}{16M}\right\}\leq r_{1}

(A.2)

where
r_{1}
was defined in (
A.1
).

Then for any
r\leq r_{0}
we have
|f^{\prime}(z)|\geq c/2
for
z\in\mathbb{D}_{r}
.

Proof.

Since
f^{\prime}(z)=c+h^{\prime}(z)
,
h(z)=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}
, the Cauchy inequalities imply again that for
z\in\mathbb{D}_{r}
,

\displaystyle|h^{\prime}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}nc_{n}z^{n-1}\right|\leq M\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}n\frac{r^{n-1}}{R^{n}}=\frac{Mr}{R^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n+1)\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{n-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2Mr}{R^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{n-1}=\frac{2Mr}{R^{2}}\frac{1}{(1-r/R)^{2}}=\frac{2Mr}{(R-r)^{2}}.

If
r\leq R/2
, then
|h^{\prime}(z)|\leq 2Mr/r^{2}=2M/r
for
z\in\mathbb{D}_{r}
. On the other hand, if additionally
r\leq cR^{2}/(16M)
then
c\geq 16Mr/R^{2}\geq 4M/r
, which implies that for
z\in\mathbb{D}_{r}
,

|f^{\prime}(z)|\geq|c+h^{\prime}(z)|\geq c-|h^{\prime}(z)|\geq c-2M/r\geq c-8Mr/R^{2}\geq c/2.

∎

Proposition A.3
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{H}(R,M,c)
and for some
\epsilon>0
,
|f(z)-g(z)|<\epsilon
for
z\in\mathbb{D}_{R}
. Then there exist positive
\rho
and
C
, depending only on
R
,
M
, and
c
, such that the inverse functions
f^{-1}
and
g^{-1}
exist in
D_{\rho}
, and

m(\rho)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\max_{\zeta\in\overline{\mathbb{D}_{\rho}}}\left|f^{-1}(\zeta)-g^{-1}(\zeta)\right|\leq C\epsilon.

(A.3)

Proof.

Fix
r_{0}
defined in (
A.2
) and consider
r\in(0,r_{0})
, as long as the corresponding
\rho=cr/2
. By Proposition
A.1
, for any such
r
and
\rho
, functions
f^{-1}(\zeta)
,
g^{-1}(\zeta)
exist, are univalent in
\overline{\mathbb{D}_{\rho}}
and map
\overline{D_{\rho}}
into
\mathbb{D}_{r}
. Thus, for any
\rho\leq c/2
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}
,

\begin{split}|f^{-1}-g^{-1}|(\zeta)&=\left|\int_{0}^{\zeta}\left[(f^{-1})^{\prime}(w)-(g^{-1})^{\prime}(w)\right]dw\right|=\int_{0}^{\zeta}\left|\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(f^{-1}(w))}-\frac{1}{g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))}\right||dw|\\
&\leq\rho\max_{w\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}}\left|\frac{g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))-f^{\prime}(f^{-1}(w))}{g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))f^{\prime}(f^{-1}(w))}\right|\leq\frac{4\rho}{c^{2}}\max_{w\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}}\left|g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))-f^{\prime}(f^{-1}(w))\right|,\end{split}

(A.4)

where we have used Proposition
A.2
. For
w\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}
,

g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))-f^{\prime}(f^{-1}(w))=\left(g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))-f^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))\right)+\left(f^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))-f^{\prime}(f^{-1}(w))\right)

and thus,

\left|g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))-f^{\prime}(f^{-1}(w))\right|\leq\left|(g^{\prime}-f^{\prime})(g^{-1}(w))\right|+\left|f^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))-f^{\prime}(f^{-1}(w))\right|.

(A.5)

Observe that by the Cauchy integral formula, for
f\in\mathcal{H}(R,M,c)
and
z\in\mathbb{D}_{r}
, with any
r<R
, we have
|f^{\prime}(z)|\leq M/(R-r)
and
|2f^{\prime\prime}(z)|\leq M/(R-r)^{2}
.
Thus, since for
w\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}
,
z=g^{-1}(w)\in\mathbb{D}_{r}
, we have

\left|g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))-f^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))\right|=\left|(g^{\prime}-f^{\prime})(z)\right|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{R-r}\leq 2\epsilon

and

\left|f^{\prime}(g^{-1}(w))-f^{\prime}(f^{-1}(w))\right|\leq\max_{z\in\mathbb{D}_{r}}|f^{\prime\prime}(z)|\max_{w\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}}\left|g^{-1}(w)-f^{-1}(w)\right|\leq\frac{M}{2(R-r)^{2}}\,m(\rho)\leq\frac{2M}{R^{2}}\,m(\rho).

Combining these bounds in (
A.5
) we obtain from (
A.4
)

m(\rho)\leq\frac{4\rho}{c^{2}}\left(2\epsilon+\frac{2M}{R^{2}}\,m(\rho)\right).

Thus, selecting for instance
\rho=c^{2}R^{2}/(16M)
we obtain (
A.3
) with
C=M/R^{2}
.
∎
