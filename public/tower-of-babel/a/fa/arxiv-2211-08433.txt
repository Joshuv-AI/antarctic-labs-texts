i-SPin: An integrator for multicomponent Schrödinger-Poisson systems with self-interactions
Mustafa A. Amin

Abstract
We provide an algorithm and a publicly available code to numerically evolve multicomponent Schrödinger-Poisson (SP) systems with a SO( n n ) symmetry, including attractive or repulsive self-interactions in addition to gravity. Focusing on the case where the SP system represents the non-relativistic limit of a massive vector field, non-gravitational self-interactions (in particular spin-spin interactions) introduce complexities related to mass and spin conservation which are not present in purely gravitational systems. We address them with an analytical solution for the ‘kick’ step in the algorithm, where we are able to decouple the multicomponent system completely. Equipped with this analytical solution, the full field evolution is second order accurate, preserves spin and mass to machine precision, and is reversible. Our algorithm allows for an expanding universe relevant for cosmology, and the inclusion of external potentials relevant for laboratory settings.

Spin-1 Schrödinger-Poisson system

We begin with a
3
-component Schrödinger-Poisson system with SO(
3
) symmetry with non-relativistic massive vector fields in mind. That is, the transformation
\psi_{i}\rightarrow R_{ij}\psi_{j}
(with

{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\psi_{3})

3
3

3

We use the Einstein summation convention throughout the paper.
On account of this, we have the following general action that includes both Newtonian gravity and point self-interactions

\displaystyle\!\mathcal{S}_{\rm nr}

\displaystyle\!=\!\!\int\!\!\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}^{3}x\Biggl[\frac{i\hbar}{2}{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\cdot\dot{{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}}+\mathrm{c.c.}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\cdot\nabla{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}+\frac{1}{8\pi G}\Phi\nabla^{2}\Phi-m\Phi{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}-V_{\rm nrel}({\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}},{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger})\Biggr].

(2.1)

Here, the first two terms dictate the usual free field evolution (of each of the field component
\psi_{i}
), while the third and fourth terms account for the Gauss’ law for Newtonian gravity where only the mass density
m{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}=m\psi^{\ast}_{i}\psi_{i}
contributes to the Newtonian potential
\Phi

{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}
, and takes the following form for quartic self-interaction

\displaystyle V_{\rm nrel}({\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger},{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda(\hbar c)^{3}}{8(mc^{2})^{2}}\Big[({\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}})\,({\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger})+2\,({\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}})^{2}\,\Bigr].

(2.2)

In terms of the number density
\rho={\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}
and spin density
\bm{\mathcal{S}}=i\hbar{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}\times{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle V_{\rm nrel}(\rho,\bm{\mathcal{S}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda(\hbar c)^{3}}{8(mc^{2})^{2}}\left[3\rho^{2}-\frac{(\bm{\mathcal{S}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{S}})}{\hbar^{2}}\right]\,.

(2.3)

This admits the following Schrödinger-Poisson system of equations

\displaystyle i\hbar\,\partial_{t}{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}+m\,\Phi\,{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}-\frac{\lambda(\hbar c)^{3}}{4(mc^{2})^{2}}\Big[({\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}})\,{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}+2\,({\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}})\,{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}\,\Bigr]\,,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\Phi

\displaystyle=4\pi Gm\,{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}\,.

(2.4)

2.1
Conserved quantities

The various conserved quantities associated with our non-relativistic system (
2.1
) are

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}},\quad\textrm{and}\quad M=mN,\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\,\,\,\textrm{(particle number and rest mass)}

(2.6)

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{2m}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\Re\left(i\,{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}\cdot\nabla{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\right)\,,\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\,\textrm{(linear momentum)}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\Biggl[\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\,\nabla{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\cdot\nabla{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}-\frac{4\pi Gm^{2}}{2}{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}y}{4\pi|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|}{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}({\bm{y}})\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}(\bm{y})+V_{\rm nrel}\Biggr]\,,\quad\textrm{(energy)}

\displaystyle=\hbar\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,i{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}\times{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\,,\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\,\textrm{(spin angular momentum)}

(2.9)

\displaystyle\bm{L}

\displaystyle=\hbar\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\Re\left(i\,{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}\cdot\nabla{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}\times\bm{x}\right).\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\textrm{(orbital angular momentum)}

(2.10)

2.2
Re-scaled system

In what follows, we shall work with dimensionless quantities. For this purpose, we rescale the fields, space and time in the following fashion

\displaystyle t=\frac{\hbar}{\mathcal{E}}\tilde{t}\qquad{\bm{x}}=\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{m\,\mathcal{E}}}\tilde{\bm{x}}\qquad\Phi=\frac{\mathcal{E}}{m}\tilde{\Phi}\qquad\psi_{i}=\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}^{2}\hbar c}{8\pi Gm}\right)^{1/2}\tilde{\psi}_{i}\,.

(2.11)

Here,
\hbar/\mathcal{E}
serves as a measure of the characteristic time-scales present in the system. In terms of these quantities, the Schrödinger-Poisson system becomes

\displaystyle i\partial_{\tilde{t}}\tilde{\psi}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\nabla}^{2}\tilde{\psi}_{i}+\tilde{\Phi}\,\tilde{\psi}_{i}-\tilde{\lambda}\left[\tilde{\psi}_{j}\tilde{\psi}_{j}\tilde{\psi}^{\ast}_{i}+2\,\tilde{\rho}\,\tilde{\psi}_{i}\right]\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{\nabla}^{2}\tilde{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\rho}\,,

(2.12)

where
\tilde{\rho}\equiv\tilde{\psi}^{\ast}_{i}\tilde{\psi}_{i}
is the re-scaled number density, and

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}=\frac{\lambda\hbar^{4}\mathcal{E}}{4m^{3}\,8\pi G}\approx 0.014\left(\frac{\lambda}{10^{-84}}\right)\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}/mc^{2}}{10^{-12}}\right)\left(\frac{10^{-20}\,{\rm eV}}{m}\right)^{2}\,.

(2.13)

In the description of the algorithm, as well as our numerical code, we use this scaled, dimensionless system of equations (
2.2
). The only choices to be made are the value of
\tilde{\lambda}
and the initial conditions for the field. From now on we shall remove all the tildes to de-clutter our presentation with the understanding that all quantities are dimensionless.

3.1
A review of the scalar system

For the scalar case, the self-interaction only depends on the number density (
V_{\rm nrel}=-\lambda|\psi^{\ast}\psi|^{2}
), leading to the following evolution of the Schrödinger field

\displaystyle i\,\partial_{t}\psi=\mathcal{H}\psi\,\qquad{\rm with}\qquad\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\nabla^{2}/2}+\underbrace{\Phi-\lambda\,\rho}\,,

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\;\;\mathcal{H}_{\rm drift}\;+\;\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}

where we have defined
\mathcal{H}_{\rm drift}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}
as ‘drift’ and ‘kick’ Hamiltonians respectively. Explicitly, the Hamiltonian density has a position basis representation
[\mathcal{H}]_{\bm{x}\,\bm{y}}=[\mathcal{H}_{\rm drift}]_{\bm{x}\,\bm{y}}+[\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}]_{\bm{x}\,\bm{y}}\,,

where the non-local drift and local (diagonal) kick Hamiltonian densities are

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}_{\rm drift}]_{\bm{x}\,\bm{y}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{x}}}\,{\bm{k}}^{2}\,\mathcal{F}_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{y}}}\,,\qquad{\rm and}\qquad[\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}]_{\bm{x}\,\bm{y}}\equiv\left(\Phi(\bm{x})-\lambda\,\rho(\bm{x})\right)\delta^{3}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}).

(3.2)

Here,
\mathcal{F}_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{y}}}\equiv e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{y}}}
is the Fourier transform matrix element and
\int_{\bm{k}}=\int d^{3}k/(2\pi)^{3}
.
5
5

5

In practice, we work with a finite volume
V=L^{3}
, for which
\int_{\bm{k}}\rightarrow V^{-1}\sum_{\bm{k}}
with
\bm{k}=2\pi{\bm{n}}/L
.
With this representation, the formal solution of (
3.1
) is

\displaystyle\psi(\bm{x},t+\epsilon)=\int_{\bm{y}}U({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\epsilon)\,\psi({\bm{y}},t)\,,\qquad{\rm where}\qquad U({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\epsilon)

\displaystyle={\rm T}\,\Bigl[e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int^{t+\epsilon}_{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\mathcal{H}}\Bigr]_{{\bm{x}}\,{\bm{y}}}\,.

(3.3)

Here, “T” stands for time-ordering and
\int_{\bm{y}}=\int d^{3}y
.
6
6

6

Note that in our convention there is no
(2\pi)^{3}
in the spatial integrals. If space is also discretized then this integral becomes a Reimann sum as usual and
\delta^{(3)}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})=V\delta_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
.
The evolution operator
U
satisfies the unitarity relationship

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{z}}U^{\dagger}({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}},\epsilon)U({\bm{z}},{\bm{y}},\epsilon)=\delta^{3}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}})\,.

(3.4)

The unitary evolution operators related to the ‘drift’ and ‘kick’ parts of the Hamiltonian are

\displaystyle U_{\rm drift}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\epsilon)\equiv[e^{-i\epsilon\,\mathcal{H}_{\rm drift}}]_{\bm{x}\,\bm{y}}=\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\bm{k},\bm{x}}\;e^{-i\epsilon\,\bm{k}^{2}/2}\;\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k},\bm{y}}\,,

(3.5)

\displaystyle U_{\rm kick}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\epsilon)\equiv[e^{-i\epsilon\,\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}}]_{\bm{x}\,\bm{y}}=e^{-i\epsilon(\Phi(\bm{x})-\lambda\rho(\bm{x}))}\,\delta^{3}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}).

(3.6)

In terms of
U_{\rm drift}
and
U_{\rm kick}
, the formal solution in (
3.3
) becomes
7
7

7

Equivalently, kick-drift-kick set of operations work equally well in so far as
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
accuracy, mass conservation, and time reversibility is concerned. Since we’ll be using drift-kick-drift set of operations for the vector case ahead, we present the same for the scalar case in order to be consistent in our presentation.

\displaystyle\psi({\bm{x}},t+\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{u}}\;\;U_{\rm drift}({\bm{x}},{\bm{u}},\epsilon/2)\,\int_{\bm{v}}U_{\rm kick}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},\epsilon)\,\int_{\bm{w}}U_{\rm drift}({\bm{v}},{\bm{w}},\epsilon/2)\,\psi({\bm{w}},t)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{p}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\bm{p},\bm{x}}e^{-i\frac{\epsilon}{2}\bm{p}^{2}/2}\int_{\bm{u}}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{p},\bm{u}}e^{-i\epsilon(\Phi({\bm{u}})-\lambda\rho({\bm{u}}))}\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\bm{k},\bm{u}}e^{-i\frac{\epsilon}{2}\bm{k}^{2}/2}\int_{\bm{w}}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k},\bm{w}}\psi({\bm{w}},t)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}).

(3.7)

Note that
\Phi
and
\rho
in
\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}
are evaluated after the first half-drift is performed. This is crucial for accuracy of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
and to maintain time-reversal symmetry.

To see this reversibility in effect, let us first evolve the system forward for half a time step of
\epsilon/2
by using
U_{\rm drift}
and
U_{\rm kick}
:

\displaystyle\psi({\bm{z}},t+\epsilon/2)

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{v}}\;\;U_{\rm kick}({\bm{z}},{\bm{v}},\epsilon/2)\,\int_{\bm{y}}U_{\rm drift}({\bm{v}},{\bm{y}},\epsilon/2)\,\psi({\bm{y}},t)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=e^{-i\frac{\epsilon}{2}(\Phi(\bm{z})-\lambda\rho(\bm{z}))}\,\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\bm{k},\bm{z}}\;e^{-i\frac{\epsilon}{2}\,\bm{k}^{2}/2}\;\int_{\bm{y}}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k},\bm{y}}\,\psi({\bm{y}},t)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})\,,

(3.8)

where
U_{\rm kick}
is evaluated using the field obtained after the half-drift has been performed. In conjugation to this, let us now evolve the system backwards from
t+\epsilon
to
t+\epsilon/2
using time reversed operations:

\displaystyle\psi({\bm{z}},t+\epsilon/2)

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{v}}\,U^{\prime}_{\rm kick}({\bm{z}},{\bm{v}},-\epsilon/2)\,\int_{\bm{x}}U_{\rm drift}({\bm{v}},{\bm{x}},-\epsilon/2)\,\psi({\bm{x}},t+\epsilon)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=e^{i\frac{\epsilon}{2}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bm{z})-\lambda\rho^{\prime}(\bm{z}))}\,\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\bm{k},\bm{z}}\;e^{i\frac{\epsilon}{2}\,\bm{k}^{2}/2}\;\int_{\bm{x}}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k},\bm{x}}\,\psi({\bm{x}},t+\epsilon)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})\,.

(3.9)

Here too,
U^{\prime}_{\rm kick}
is evaluated using the field after the half-drift has been performed. We can invert the above eq. (
3.1
) to get

\displaystyle\psi({\bm{x}},t+\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{v}}U_{\rm drift}^{-1}({\bm{v}},{\bm{x}},-\epsilon/2)\,\int_{\bm{z}}U^{{}^{\prime}\,-1}_{\rm kick}({\bm{z}},{\bm{v}},-\epsilon/2)\,\psi({\bm{z}},t+\epsilon/2)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\bm{k},\bm{x}}\;e^{-i\frac{\epsilon}{2}\,\bm{k}^{2}/2}\;\int_{\bm{v}}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k},\bm{v}}\,e^{-i\frac{\epsilon}{2}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bm{v})-\lambda\rho^{\prime}(\bm{v}))}\,\psi({\bm{v}},t+\epsilon/2)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})\,,

(3.10)

and use it in (

) to get the full unitary evolution from

\displaystyle\psi({\bm{x}},t+\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{u}}\!U_{\rm drift}^{-1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{u}},-\epsilon/2)\int_{\bm{v}}\!U^{{}^{\prime}\,-1}_{\rm kick}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},-\epsilon/2)\int_{\bm{w}}\!U_{\rm kick}({\bm{v}},{\bm{w}},\epsilon/2)\int_{\bm{y}}\!U_{\rm drift}({\bm{w}},{\bm{y}},\epsilon/2)\psi({\bm{y}},t)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})\,.

(3.11)

Now note that since each of the exponential operators is time reversible, i.e.

), proving that the algorithm is indeed time reversible. Furthermore, the evolution accuracy is now
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
and the error is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})
. This can be seen by noting that the re-evaluation of the kick Hamiltonian after the drift step takes into account the leading order correction due to the time integral
\int^{t+\epsilon}_{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\mathcal{H}(t^{\prime})
in (

). See
[
39
]
for some details.

It is worth noting that for the case under consideration, the kick operator only rotates the phase of the field at every point in space, while itself only depending upon the norm
\rho

U_{\rm kick}^{{}^{\prime}\,-1}(\bm{u},\bm{v},-\epsilon/2)
evaluated using the backward half-drifted field from

, is equal to
U_{\rm kick}(\bm{u},\bm{v},\epsilon/2)
evaluated using the forward half drifted field from
t
.

3.1.1
Algorithm summary

Starting with the field
\psi({\bm{x}},t)
at time
t
, it is evolved through a time step
\epsilon/2
according to the differential equation

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\psi\qquad{\rm giving}\qquad\psi^{(1)}({\bm{x}})=\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\bm{k},\bm{x}}\;e^{-i\,\epsilon\,\bm{k}^{2}/4}\int_{\bm{w}}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k},\bm{w}}\,\psi({\bm{w}},t)\,.

(3.12)

Then, it is evolved through a time step
\epsilon
according to the differential equation

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi=(\Phi-\lambda\rho)\psi\qquad{\rm giving}\qquad\psi^{(2)}({\bm{x}})=e^{-i\,\epsilon(\Phi^{(1)}(\bm{x})-\lambda\rho^{(1)}(\bm{x}))}\psi^{(1)}({\bm{x}})\,,

(3.13)

where
\Phi^{(1)}(\bm{x})=(1/2)\nabla^{-2}_{\bm{x}}(\rho^{(1)}(\bm{x})-\bar{\rho}^{(1)})
and overbar represents spatial average.
8
8

8

In practice, the Newtonian potential is computed using Fourier transformation:
\Phi(\bm{x})=-\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\bm{k},\bm{x}}\;\frac{\rho(\bm{k})}{2\bm{k}^{2}}
(without the
\bm{k}=0
mode).
Finally, it is again evolved using the following differential equation through a time step
\epsilon/2

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\psi\qquad{\rm giving}\qquad\psi({\bm{x}},t+\epsilon)=\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\bm{k},\bm{x}}\;e^{-i\,\epsilon\,\bm{k}^{2}/4}\int_{\bm{w}}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k},\bm{w}}\,\psi^{(2)}({\bm{w}})\,.

(3.14)

Note that for every differential evolution above, the corresponding integral evolution is exact. While the half drift steps result in
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
accuracy in the set of drift-kick-drift operations, the successive computation of the kick (after the performance of drift) ensures reversibility. Every operation being unitary ensures unitary evolution throughout.

3.2
Vector system

For the vector case, the differential evolution takes a matrix form

\displaystyle i\,\partial_{t}\psi_{i}=[\mathcal{H}]_{ij}\psi_{j}\qquad{\rm with}\qquad[\mathcal{H}]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\delta_{ij}\,\nabla^{2}/2}+\underbrace{\delta_{ij}(\Phi-2\lambda\,\rho)-\lambda\,\psi^{\ast}_{i}\psi_{j}}\,.

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\quad[\mathcal{H}_{\rm drift}]_{ij}+\qquad\quad[\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}]_{ij}

In particular, note the non-diagonal piece,
\propto\lambda\psi^{\ast}_{i}\psi_{j}
, in
[\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}]_{ij}
. The Hamiltonian density, written explicitly in the position basis, is

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}]_{\bm{x}\,\bm{y}\,;\,ij}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{x}}}\,{\bm{k}}^{2}\,\mathcal{F}_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{y}}}+\left(\Phi(\bm{x})-2\lambda\,\rho(\bm{x})\right)\delta^{3}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}})\right]\delta_{ij}-\lambda\,\psi^{\ast}_{i}(\bm{x})\psi_{j}(\bm{x})\,\delta^{3}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}})\,,

and as always, we have suppressed the time dependence of the fields. Here the latin indices run over the field components, with the (unitary) integral evolution for this system being

\displaystyle\psi_{i}({\bm{x}},t+\epsilon)=\int_{\bm{y}}\,U_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\epsilon)\,\psi_{j}({\bm{y}},t)\quad{\rm with}\quad U_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\epsilon)

\displaystyle={\rm T}\,\Bigl[e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int^{t+\epsilon}_{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\mathcal{H}(t^{\prime})}\Bigr]_{\bm{x}\,\bm{y}\,;\,ij}\,.

(3.16)

The unitary condition is the following

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{z}}\;U_{ik}^{\dagger}({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}},\epsilon)\,U_{kj}({\bm{z}},{\bm{y}},\epsilon)=\delta_{ij}\,\delta^{3}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}})\,.

(3.17)

With these definitions at hand, we shall employ the previously discussed algorithm for scalars, with appropriate modifications appearing due to the non-diagonal kick in the vector SP system.

3.2.1
Algorithm summary

The algorithm proceeds as follows. Starting with the field components
\psi_{i}({\bm{x}},t)
at time
t
, they are ‘drifted’ through a time step
\epsilon/2
according to

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi_{i}=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\psi_{i}\qquad{\rm giving}\qquad\psi^{(1)}_{i}({\bm{x}})=\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\bm{k},\bm{x}}\;e^{-i\epsilon\,\bm{k}^{2}/4}\;\int_{\bm{w}}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k},\bm{w}}\psi_{i}({\bm{w}},t)\,.

(3.18)

Then, every component is ‘kicked’ through a time step
\epsilon
according to

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi_{i}=[\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}]_{ij}\psi_{j}\qquad{\rm giving}\qquad\psi^{(2)}_{i}({\bm{x}})=[U_{\rm kick}(\epsilon)]_{ij}\,\psi^{(1)}_{j}({\bm{x}})\,,

(3.19)

where recall that
[\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}]_{ij}=\delta_{ij}(\Phi-2\lambda\,\rho)-\lambda\,\psi^{\ast}_{i}\psi_{j}
, and
[U_{\rm kick}(\epsilon/2)]_{ij}={\rm T}\exp[-i\int_{t}^{t+\epsilon}\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}(t^{\prime})]_{ij}
. The operator
U_{\rm kick}
is evaluated using the field
\psi^{(1)}_{i}
; its evaluation based on an analytic solution of the kick equation is the key step, which we discuss in detail in the next subsection. The explicit expression for
U_{\rm kick}
is provided in (
3.30
). Once this operation is performed, the fields are again drifted through a time step
\epsilon/2

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi_{i}=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\psi_{i}\qquad{\rm giving}\qquad\psi_{i}({\bm{x}},t+\epsilon)=\int_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\bm{k},\bm{x}}\;e^{-i\epsilon\,\bm{k}^{2}/4}\int_{\bm{w}}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k},\bm{w}}\,\psi^{(2)}_{j}({\bm{w}})\,.

(3.20)

The half drift steps in the set of operations ensure
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
accuracy, while successive computation of the kick ensures reversibility. Since every operation is unitary, the algorithm conserves total mass. It also conserves total spin.

3.2.2
Kick step

We want an exact solution for the kick operation
[U_{\rm kick}]_{ij}\psi_{j}
, in order to ensure reversibility along with mass and spin conservation. This was trivial for the scalar and vector case with no spin-spin interactions since
[\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}]_{ij}
was a constant of motion throughout the kick step. In that case the solution for the kick step was the exponential of the kick Hamiltonian density. With self-interactions, due to the non-diagonal piece in
\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}
, the evolution of each of the field component
\psi_{i}
becomes convoluted via the mixing of the field components and non-linearity of the kick differential equation. In this section we work with the kick step governed by the following differential equation

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi_{i}=[\delta_{ij}(\Phi-2\lambda\rho)-\lambda\psi_{i}^{*}\psi_{j}]\psi_{j}\,.

(3.21)

Working with the vector system, we first quickly outline an approach where the solution to the above 3-level system is obtained by exponentiating a time-independent
3\times 3
matrix. Upon adding and subtracting
\lambda\rho\psi_{i}=(\lambda\psi_{i}\psi^{\ast}_{j})\psi_{j}
in the right hand side, we note that the off-diagonal term
\propto\psi_{i}\psi^{\ast}_{j}-\psi_{i}^{*}\psi_{j}
is the skew-symmetric Hermitian matrix
M_{ij}=-i\varepsilon_{ijk}\mathcal{S}_{k}
, where
\mathcal{S}_{j}=i\,\varepsilon_{jkl}\psi_{k}\psi^{\ast}_{l}
is the spin density.
9
9

9

The relevant equation of motion can also be obtained by varying the potential written in the form (
2.3
) directly.
It can be easily seen that both the spin density and the number density (hence also the gravitational potential
\Phi
) are conserved throughout the kick step.
10
10

10

This is also manifest from the continuity equations in appendix
A.2
, since in this step there are no flow of currents.
Hence, the matrix
M
is constant, rendering the following simple solution

\displaystyle\psi_{i}(t)=[U_{\rm kick}(t-t_{0})]_{ij}\psi_{j}(t_{0})\quad{\rm with}\quad[U_{\rm kick}(\epsilon)]_{ij}=e^{-i(\Phi-3\lambda\rho)\,\epsilon}\,[e^{-i\lambda M\,\epsilon}]_{ij}\,,

(3.22)

where matrix exponentiation is understood, and has the following specific entries

\displaystyle[e^{-i\lambda M\epsilon}]_{ij}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{S}^{2}}\begin{cases}\mathcal{S}_{i}^{2}+(\mathcal{S}^{2}-\mathcal{S}_{i}^{2})\cos\lambda\mathcal{S}\epsilon&i=j\\
\mathcal{S}_{i}\mathcal{S}_{j}(1-\cos\lambda\mathcal{S}\epsilon)-\epsilon_{ijk}\mathcal{S}_{k}\mathcal{S}\sin\lambda\mathcal{S}\epsilon&i,j,k\quad\textrm{distinct}\end{cases}

(3.23)

where no summation is assumed, and
\mathcal{S}
is the magnitude of the spin density. Another complimentary way of evolving the system is to work in the spin basis where the field is decomposed into the three different spin multiplicities
[
11
]
(as opposed to working with Cartesian components as we did here). For example with
{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}=\sum_{\lambda=-1,0,1}\,\psi_{\lambda}\,{\bm{\mathsf{\epsilon}}}^{(\lambda)}_{\hat{z}}
where
{\bm{\mathsf{\epsilon}}}^{(0)}_{\hat{z}}=(0,0,1)^{T}
and
{\bm{\mathsf{\epsilon}}}^{(\pm 1)}_{\hat{z}}=(1,\pm i,0)^{T}/\sqrt{2}
, the evolution of
{\bm{\chi}}^{\prime}=(\psi_{1},\psi_{0},\psi_{-1})
due to the off-diagonal kick step becomes
i\partial_{t}{\bm{\chi}}^{\prime}=\lambda P\,{\bm{\chi}}^{\prime}
, where
P
is also a
3\times 3
constant skew-symmetric Hermitian matrix. This gives the desired solution
{\bm{\chi}}^{\prime}(t)=\exp[-i\lambda P(t-t_{0})]{\bm{\chi}}^{\prime}(t_{0})
. See
[
57
]
for this approach.

In the scheme above, the different field components are mixed due to (iso-)spin-spin coupling. As a result, the expression for
M_{ij}
for an
n
-component system can become convoluted as
n
increases (see seection
5
). Below, we continue with the vector system and present a new solution for the above kick differential equation, in which all the Cartesian field components are decoupled. Only the real and imaginary parts of each Cartesian component remain coupled. This decoupling yields a much simpler evolution for each component, and allows for a straightforward generalization to
n
-component Schrödinger systems (given in section
5
).

We begin with the ansatz
\psi_{j}(t)=e^{-i(\Phi-2\lambda\rho)(t-t_{0})}\chi_{j}(t)
in (
3.21
) to get non-linear differential equations for
\chi_{j}
with only the non-diagonal piece in
\mathcal{H}_{\rm kick}
contributing to its evolution:

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\chi_{i}=-\lambda\,\chi^{\ast}_{i}\,\chi_{j}\chi_{j}\,.

(3.24)

Then, upon multiplying the above with
\chi_{i}
and vector summing, we get
i\partial_{t}(\chi_{j}\chi_{j})=-2\lambda\rho\chi_{j}\chi_{j}
. This equation has a simple solution

\displaystyle\chi_{j}\chi_{j}(t)=re^{i\theta+2i\lambda\rho(t-t_{0})}

(3.25)

where
r
is the magnitude of
\chi_{j}\chi_{j}
(a constant of motion)
11
11

11

This can also be seen by noting that
r^{2}=\rho^{2}-\mathcal{S}_{i}\mathcal{S}_{i}
, and since both
\rho
and
\mathcal{S}_{i}
are constants of motion throughout the kick evolution, so is
r
.
, and
\theta
is its initial phase (at
t=t_{0}
). Owing to this analytical (and simple) evolution of the squared sum of the field components, we can now decouple the different field components by using it in (
3.24
). This renders the following linear differential equation for each of the field components

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\chi_{i}=-\lambda\,r\,e^{i\theta+2i\lambda\rho(t-t_{0})}\,\chi^{\ast}_{i}.

(3.26)

In terms of the real and imaginary parts of
\chi_{i}
:
u^{i}_{1}=\Re[\chi_{i}]
and
u^{i}_{2}=\Im[\chi_{i}]
, this differential equation becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}u=(A\,{\bm{b}}\cdot{\vec{\sigma}})\,u\quad{\rm with}\quad A=\frac{r}{2\rho}\,,\quad{\bm{b}}=(\cos\eta,\,0,\,-\sin\eta)\,,\quad u=(u_{1},u_{2})\,.

(3.27)

We have dropped the vector component index on
u
(related to
``i"
in
\chi_{i}
), since each
\chi_{i}
now evolves the same way, differing only in their initial condition.
We have also re-scaled the time as
\eta\equiv\theta+2\lambda\rho(t-t_{0})
for convenience (with
\eta_{0}=\theta
), and
\sigma_{i}
are the (real) Pauli matrices.
12
12

12

Our convention is
\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&&1\\
1&&0\,\end{pmatrix}\;;\;\sigma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&&1\\
-1&&0\,\end{pmatrix}\;;\;\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&&0\\
0&&-1\,\end{pmatrix}
.

This is the equation for which we seek an analytical solution.
13
13

13

Interestingly, this equation can be mapped to the problem of an electrically charged spin-
1/2
fermion (at rest) in a rotating magnetic field. This can be achieved by making
r/2\rho
imaginary, with its magnitude representing the magnetic moment of the fermion, and identifying
u
as the two component Dirac spinor.

We note that the time dependence of the matrix
{\bm{b}}\cdot\vec{\sigma}
can be stripped off by going to a rotating frame. The corresponding transformation is achieved via the matrix
P=\exp[-\eta\,\sigma_{2}/2]
. Then, with
v=P^{-1}u
and
P^{-1}({\bm{b}}\cdot\vec{\sigma})P=\sigma_{1}
, we get the following transformed equation with the corresponding trivial solution

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}v

\displaystyle=(A\,\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}/2)v\,\implies v(\eta)=\exp\Bigl[(\eta-\eta_{0})\,(A\,\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}/2)\Bigr]v(\eta_{0})\,.

(3.28)

Using
u=Pv
and re-instating quantities in terms of the ‘actual’ time
t
, we therefore have the following solution for
u

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigl[-\lambda\rho(t-t_{0})\,\sigma_{2}\Bigr]\,\exp\Bigl[-\theta\sigma_{2}/2\Bigr]\,\exp\Bigl[2\lambda\rho(t-t_{0})\,(A\,\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}/2)\Bigr]\exp\Bigl[\theta\sigma_{2}/2\Bigr]u(t_{0})\,,

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{U}(t-t_{0})u(t_{0})\,.

(3.29)

With this exact non-diagonal kick evolution, the real and imaginary parts of each of the field component
\chi_{i}
are evolved using
\mathcal{U}(t-t_{0})
, accompanied by a phase translation
e^{-i(\Phi-2\lambda\rho)(t-t_{0})}
due to the diagonal Hamiltonian density. In terms of the field
\psi_{j}
itself, the evolution is

\displaystyle[U_{\rm kick}(\epsilon)]_{ij}\,\psi_{j}=\delta_{ij}e^{-i(\Phi-2\lambda\rho)\epsilon}\Biggl[\Bigl[\mathcal{U}(\epsilon)_{11}+i\,\mathcal{U}(\epsilon)_{21}\Bigr]\Re[\psi_{j}]+\Bigl[\mathcal{U}(\epsilon)_{12}+i\,\mathcal{U}(\epsilon)_{22}\Bigr]\Im[\psi_{j}]\Biggr]\,,

(3.30)

where we used
t-t_{0}=\epsilon
.
Including the drift evolution, the full vector field is therefore evolved according to steps (
3.18
), (
3.19
), and (
3.20
), with the above solution for
U_{\rm kick}
. In Appendix
A.4
, we provide the explicit expression for
\mathcal{U}
. This fetches our desired mass and spin conserving, time-reversible drift + kick + drift algorithm, to evolve the multicomponent/vector SP system. Since both the drift and kick evolutions are now exact, the overall accuracy of the system as compared to the true evolution in (
3.16
), like the scalar case, is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
.

Let us briefly comment on the operator
\mathcal{U}
in regards to the conservation of mass and spin density

\displaystyle\rho=u^{i}_{c}\,\mathcal{U}^{T}_{ca}\,\,\mathcal{U}_{ab}\,u^{i}_{b}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\mathcal{S}_{i}=\varepsilon_{ijk}(u^{j}_{1}\,u^{k}_{2}-u^{k}_{1}\,u^{j}_{2})\det\,\mathcal{U}\,.

(3.31)

While
\det\,\mathcal{U}=1
indicates that the spin density remains unchanged throughout the kick evolution,
\mathcal{U}^{T}\neq\mathcal{U}^{-1}
indicates that the mass density within
each
component is not conserved. This is reflective of the fact that there is no separate U(
1
) symmetry within each component of the vector field, and hence no mass conservation within each component. On the other hand, the total mass density is conserved. This may seem peculiar since
\mathcal{U}^{T}\neq\mathcal{U}^{-1}
. We note that since
r
,
\rho
and
\theta
appearing in the operator are consistent with the initial
\psi_{i}
(meaning
\psi_{j}\psi_{j}(t_{0})=r\,e^{i\theta}
and
\psi^{\ast}_{j}\psi_{j}(t_{0})=\rho
), the total mass density
\rho
is indeed conserved. In general, when this consistency doesn’t hold,
\mathcal{U}
is not
\rho
conserving.

3.2.3
Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition

The Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition ensures that the time step
\epsilon
is sufficiently small in order to resolve the fastest process happening in the simulation. For the drift-kick-drift evolution of the multi-component Schrödinger system, this means resolving the fastest kick and drift processes every time step. The drift evolution is governed by the operator
U_{\rm drift}\sim e^{i(\epsilon/2)\nabla^{2}/2}
dictating that
\epsilon
be at-least as small as
2\pi(\Delta x)^{2}/3
in order to resolve one full rotation of the drift phase.
14
14

14

Here,
\nabla^{2}
gets replaced by
\sum^{3}_{i=1}(\Delta x)^{-2}\times 4\sin^{2}(n_{i}\pi/N)
on a discrete lattice, and we set
n=N/2
along with
\sum\rightarrow 3
in order to maximize the sum over sine functions.
For the diagonal kick step, the operation is governed by
\sim e^{-i\epsilon(\Phi-2\lambda\,\rho)}\sim e^{-i\epsilon\Phi}\,e^{2i\epsilon\lambda\,\rho}
which requires that
\epsilon
be at least as small as the smallest of the two quantities
2\pi|\Phi|^{-1}
and
2\pi|2\lambda\,\rho|^{-1}
. Finally, the non-diagonal kick dictates that epsilon be at least as small as
2\pi|\lambda|^{-1}\times\min[\rho^{-1},r^{-1}]
in order to resolve both the
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
containing parts of the exponentials appearing in
\mathcal{U}
in the previous section. Since
r<\rho
by definition, this condition is less constraining than the one appearing due to diagonal kick. With this, all of the above requirements together result in the following CFL condition:
15
15

15

This is the same as that for a single component (scalar) case, with appropriate rescaling of
\lambda
.

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=2\pi\,\delta\,\min\Biggl[\frac{1}{3}(\Delta x)^{2}\,,\,|\Phi|^{-1}\,,\,|2\lambda\,\rho|^{-1}]\Biggr]\quad{\rm with}\quad\delta\ll 1\,.

(3.32)

In our simulations we take
\delta\sim 1/15
, i.e. we resolve the fastest oscillation in the system by
\sim 15
points within its full
2\pi
cycle.

With the above CFL condition, the time step
\epsilon
can be driven to small values when sufficiently dense regions (with
|\lambda|\rho\gtrsim\{|\Phi|,(\Delta x)^{-2}\}
) start to appear in the course of evolution of the Schrödinger field. Care should be taken in interpreting results in this regime since, in general, large variations in
\rho
over sufficiently small spatial regions can take us out of the domain of our non-relativistic theory. Typically in the repulsive case,
\rho
does not develop large enough variations to endanger our nonrelativistic approximations. However, the situation in more precarious in the attractive self-interaction case.

Consider a spatial region of size
r
and total enclosed mass
M
, with a roughly isotropic mass density around it. The total energy in the region
E_{\rm tot}
is due to contributions from gradient pressure
E_{\nabla}\sim\hbar^{2}M/(mr)^{2}
, gravity
E_{\rm grav}\sim-GM^{2}/r
, and self-interaction
E_{\rm self}\sim-\lambda\,\hbar^{3}M^{2}/(m^{4}c\,r^{3})
. It can be seen that as
|E_{\rm self}+E_{\rm grav}|>E_{\nabla}
(meaning
E_{\rm tot}<0
), there is a runaway possibility (when the attractive self-interaction is relevant) where the system can keep on lowering its energy by either focusing all this mass to ever-smaller regions, and/or by accumulating more mass from its surroundings in a given region.
This process can lead to very large densities in small regions of space.
16
16

16

In practice, density fluctuations can only increase to a certain extent due to the absence of wavenumbers larger than
\sim\Delta x
on a discrete lattice. Nevertheless, formation of such ‘crunched’ regions is indicative of this runaway scenario, and hence a breakdown of the nonrelativistic EFT.

At this point, relativistic corrections (including higher-order terms in the self-interaction which are not present in the system being simulated) cannot be justifiably ignored. Note that this discussion equally applies to a single component SP system (scalar) with point self-interactions, and is not particular to multicomponent systems only. For the demonstration of fidelity of our algorithm/code, we pick
\Delta x
small enough so that
{\rm min}[\ldots]=(\Delta x)^{2}/3
in the CFL condition throughout the duration of the simulation.

In order to test reversibility of our algorithm, we define an asymmetry parameter
\gamma

\displaystyle\gamma(t)\equiv\Biggl(\frac{1}{N}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\Bigl|{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}_{+}(\bm{x},t)-{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}_{-}(\bm{x},t)\Bigr|^{2}\Biggr)^{1/2}\,,

4.3
Convergence and accuracy

Starting from a given initial condition
{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}({\bm{x}},0)
and a time discretization step
\epsilon
, we assume that the simulated field upto time
t
, has the following series representation

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}_{\epsilon}({\bm{x}},t)={\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}_{\rm true}({\bm{x}},t)+\sum^{\infty}_{n=k}{\bm{c}}_{n}({\bm{x}},t)\epsilon^{n}\,.

(4.3)

Here
{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}_{\rm true}
is the true solution in the continuum limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, and
{\bm{c}}_{n}({\bf x},t)
(independent of
\epsilon
) are the error coefficients at different order in
\epsilon
, with
k
giving the leading order accumulated error. We then calculate the separation between three trajectories obtained for three different values of
\epsilon
, and construct the following ratio

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(t)=\frac{\Bigl[\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x|{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}_{\epsilon}(t)-{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}_{\epsilon/2}(t)|^{2}\Bigr]^{1/2}}{\Bigl[\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x|{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}_{\epsilon/2}(t)-{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}_{\epsilon/3}(t)|^{2}\Bigr]^{1/2}}=3^{k}\left(\frac{2^{k}-1}{3^{k}-2^{k}}\right)\,+...

(4.4)

Here ‘…’ represents
\epsilon
dependent terms that we neglect. We find
\mathcal{C}(t)\rightarrow 5.4
, implying
k=2
. Hence the simulated field solution at any given time
t
is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
away from the true solution. Note that this is one order lower than the truncation error of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})
at any time step.

We have also checked that the total energy of the system has the same accuracy as that for the scalar case.

5.1
Expanding Universe

With the inclusion of a scale factor
a(t)
relevant for standard cosmology, the equation of motion reads

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\nabla^{2}}{a^{2}}{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\prime}+\Phi\,{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\prime}-\frac{\lambda}{a^{3}}\Big[({\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\prime}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\prime})\,{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{{}^{\prime}\dagger}+2\,({\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{{}^{\prime}\dagger}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\prime})\,{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\prime}\,\Bigr]\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla^{2}}{a^{2}}\Phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2a^{3}}{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{{}^{\prime}\dagger}\cdot{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\prime}\,.

(5.1)

Here we have rescaled the field as
{\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}^{\prime}={\bm{\mathsf{\Psi}}}\,a^{3/2}
. We only outline the modifications needed in the kick step. With

6
Summary

We have devised an algorithm for evolving multicomponent Schrödinger systems obeying SO(
n
) symmetry and containing both gravitational and non-gravitational self-interactions, in particular (iso-)spin-spin interactions that mix the different field components. The crucial aspect of our algorithm is the kick step, arising due to all the interactions. Approximate extensions of existing scalar algorithms with self-interactions or multicomponent algorithms without (iso-)spin-spin interactions to our system can lead to a loss of spin, mass conservation and also reversibility. An analytic solution for the kick step containing (iso-)spin-spin interactions mitigates these issues.

We provided such an analytic solution for the kick step. We first showed that different field components can be decoupled in their evolution. This was made possible by noticing that the magnitude of
\psi_{i}\psi_{i}
is a constant of motion throughout the kick step, since both the number density and (iso-)spin density remain constant. Owing to this decoupling, we get a reduced
2
-level system in which only the real and imaginary parts of each of the field components remain coupled, with the evolution of each component being the exactly the same. We are then able to find an exact analytical solution for this reduced coupled system. Equipped with this analytical solution, we developed a split-step Fourier algorithm that involves drift-kick-drift set of operations, in analogy to the scalar case. Our algorithm is time reversible, unitary and spin/iso-spin conserving (up to machine precision).

For concreteness, we introduced the above algorithm first for a
3
-component system (vector/ spin-
1
field).
22
22

22

Compared to the earlier version of this paper, in this version we also provide a separate algorithm for the
3
-component case where the different field components are coupled in their evolution. This is similar to the algorithm used in the BEC literature
[
57
]
, but in the spin basis (as opposed to our Cartesian basis). While this algorithm is straightforward for small
n
, the extension to the
n
-component case which we formally provide in section
5
, can become cumbersome when
n
is large.
To demonstrate the fidelity of our algorithm, we showed that for sample simulations (with gravity + attractive or repulsive self-interactions) that total mass and total spin are conserved up to machine precision, and the algorithm maintains time reversibility. The overall accuracy of the algorithm, as compared to the true (continuous) evolution, is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
and is therefore on par with the usually employed split-step Fourier algorithm for a single scalar field, or multiple scalar fields without the aforementioned spin-spin self-interaction.

Our algorithm is general and flexible enough for a wide range of applications in astrophysics, cosmology and condensed-matter physics. We have provided generalizations of the algorithm to include an expanding cosmological background, and external potentials in laboratory systems, and an arbitrary number of components (respecting SO(
n
) symmetry). The computational cost of including self-interactions is only order unity larger than the case without them.

With this paper, we make the code (written in Python) for a multicomponent Schrödinger system with/without gravity + with/without self-interactions, publicly available at
GitHub
.
