Introduction

Let
K=\{K(s,t),s,t\in T\}
be a kernel with the property that for all
{\mathbf{t}_{n}}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
in
T^{n}
, the matrix
\mathbf{{\cal K}(t_{n})}=\{K(t_{i},t_{j}),i,j\in[1,n]\}
determines an
n-
dimensional random variable random variable
(X_{\alpha}(t_{1}),\ldots,X_{\alpha}(t_{n}))
with Laplace transform,

E\left(e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}X_{\alpha}(t_{i})}\right)=\frac{1}{|I+\mathbf{{\cal K}(t_{n})}S(\mathbf{s_{n}})|^{\alpha}},

(1.1)

where
S(\mathbf{s_{n}})
is a diagonal matrix with positive entries
\mathbf{s_{n}}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
, and
\alpha>0
. We refer to
\mathbf{{\cal K}(t_{n})}
as the kernel of
(X_{\alpha}(t_{1}),\ldots,X_{\alpha}(t_{n}))
.
It follows from the Kolmogorov Extension Theorem that
\{K(s,t),s,t\in T\}
determines a stochastic process which we denote by
X_{\alpha}=\{X_{\alpha}(t),t\in T\}
and refer to as an
\alpha-
permanental process with kernel
K
.

There are well known examples of
\alpha-
permanental processes when
\alpha=k/2
.
Let
\eta=\{\eta(t);t\in T\}
be a mean zero Gaussian process with covariance
U=\{U(s,t),s,t\in T\}.
Let
\{\eta_{i};i=1,\ldots,k\}
be independent copies of
\eta
. Define,

Y_{k/2}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\eta^{2}_{i}(t)}{2},\qquad t\in T.

(1.2)

The positive stochastic process
\{Y_{k/2}(t),t\in T\}
is referred to as a chi–square process of order
k
with covariance
U
.
It is well known that for
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}\subset T

E\left(e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}Y_{k/2}(t_{j})}\right)=\frac{1}{|I+\overline{U}(\mathbf{t_{n}})S(\mathbf{s_{n}})|^{k/2}},

(1.3)

where
\overline{U}(\mathbf{t_{n}})
is the
n\times n
matrix,
\{U(t_{j},t_{k});1\leq j,k\leq n\}
.
Therefore
Y_{k/2}=\{Y_{k/2}(t);t\in T\}
is a
k/2
–permanental process with kernel
U
.

It is important to note that whereas (
1.3
) holds for all integers
k
whenever
U
is the covariance of a Gaussian process, it may or may not hold when
k/2

is replaced by some
\alpha>0
that is not a multiple of 1/2. (The question of when
(
1.3
) holds for all
\alpha>0
was raised by Paul Lévy when he asked when does a Gaussian process have infinitely divisible squares. This was answered by
Griffiths and Bapat. An extensive treatment of these results is given in
[
3
, Chapter 13]
.) Nevertheless,
it follows from Eisenbaum and Kaspi,
[
1
, Theorem 3.1]
that if
U
is
the potential density of a transient Markov process on
T
which is finite for all
s,t\in T
, then there exist
\alpha-
permanental processes
\{Y_{\alpha}(t),t\in T\}
with kernel
U
for all
\alpha>0
. I.e., (
1.3
) holds with
k/2
replaced by
\alpha
for all
\alpha>0.

It is at this point that this becomes interesting because in the Eisenbaum–Kaspi theorem the kernel

is not necessarily symmetric, or equivalent to a symmetric matrix; (see
[
5
]
). When it is not, the corresponding permanental processes are not squares of a Gaussian process. They are a new kind of positive stochastic process defined by kernels that need not be symmetric. It is intriguing to study sample path properties of these processes.

In
[
6
]
we consider symmetric Lévy processes and diffusions
Y

Let

Under general smoothness conditions on

s,t\in[0,\delta]
. Using the fact that
u(s,t)
is symmetric we have,

\displaystyle\left(v_{2}^{1/2}(s,t)-v_{2}^{1/2}(t,s)\right)^{2}\leq\big|v_{2}(s,t)-v_{2}(t,s)\big|

(7.16)

\displaystyle\qquad=\big|f(s)g(t)-f(t)g(s)\big|=\big|f(s)(g(t)-g(s))-(f(t)-f(s))g(s)\big|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq f(s)\big|g(s)-g(t)\big|+g(s)\big|f(s)-f(t)\big|.

Consequently,

\sigma^{2}(s,t)\leq C^{\prime}u(|t-s|,|t-s|)+2\left(f(s)\big|g(s)-g(t)\big|+g(t)\big|f(s)-f(t)\big|\right),

(7.17)

and by (
1.16
),

\displaystyle f(s)\big|g(s)-g(t)\big|+g(t)\big|f(s)-f(t)\big|

(7.18)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq|\mu|u(s,s)\big|g(s)-g(t)\big|+|\nu|u(t,t)\big|f(s)-f(t)\big|.

It now follows by a minor generalization of Theorem
2.2
that,

\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{X_{\alpha,2}(t)}{(u(t,t)+C^{\prime}u^{2}(t,t))\log\log 1/t}\leq 1,\qquad\text{a.s.},

(7.19)

for some constant
C^{\prime}
. This implies that

\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{X_{\alpha,2}(t)}{u(t,t)\log\log 1/t}\leq 1,\qquad\text{a.s.}

(7.20)

The lower bound for
X_{\alpha,2}
follows from a minor modification of the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
Let

U_{f,g}
be a non-singular
n\times n
matrix,

\displaystyle\hskip-21.68121pt\left(\begin{array}[]{ cccc }1&f(1)&\ldots&f(n)\\
g(1)&U_{1,1}+g(1)f(1)&\ldots&U_{1,n}+g(1)f(n)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
g(n)&U_{n,1}+g(n)f(1)&\ldots&U_{n,n}+g(n)f(n)\end{array}\right).

where

Let
U_{f,g}^{-1}

U_{f,g}
and

U_{f,g}^{-1}
.
One can check that,

U_{f,g}^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ cccc }1+\rho^{\prime}&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(i)U^{i,1}&\dots&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(i)U^{i,n}\\

\rho^{\prime}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(j)f(i)U^{i,j}.

As in (

U_{f,g}^{i,i}=U^{i-1,i-1},\qquad i=2,\ldots,n+1.

(7.28)

The proof now proceeds like the proof of Theorem
