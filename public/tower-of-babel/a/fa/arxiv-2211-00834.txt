Singularity degree of non-facially exposed faces
Fei Wang, Henry Wolkowicz

Abstract
In this paper, we study the facial structure of the linear image of a convex cone. We define the singularity degree of a face of a convex cone to be the minimum number of steps it takes to expose this face using exposing vectors from the dual cone. We show that the singularity degree of the linear image of a convex cone is exactly the number of facial reduction steps it takes to obtain the minimal face containing the feasible set in the corresponding linear conic optimization problem. Our result generalizes the relationship between the complexity of general facial reduction algorithms and facial exposedness of conic images under a linear transform by Drusvyatskiy, Pataki and Wolkowicz to arbitrary singularity degree. We present our results in the original form and also in its nullspace form. As a major application, we derive an upper bound for the singularity degree of generic frameworks or tensegrities in 2 or 3 dimensional space underlying certain graphical structure.



Keywords:

Singularity degree, exposed face, convex cones, semidefinite programming

AMS subject classifications: 90C22, 90C46, 52C25

Introduction

We consider the following linear
conic optimization problem

in its primal form,

(P)\quad\begin{array}[]{cc}\max&\langle C,X\rangle\\
\mbox{s.t. }&\mathcal{M}(X)=b\in{\mathbb{F}}\\
&X\in\mathcal{K}\subseteq{\mathbb{E}},\end{array}

(1.1)

where

\mathcal{M}:{\mathbb{E}}\to{\mathbb{F}}
is a surjective linear transformation between
finite dimensional Euclidean spaces,
\langle\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}
denotes the
respective inner product, and
\mathcal{K}\subset{\mathbb{E}}
is a convex cone.

The
feasible region,
{\mathcal{F}}
, of this conic
linear optimization problem is denoted

{\mathcal{F}}=\{X\in\mathcal{K}:{\mathcal{M}}(X)=b\}.

(1.2)

Understanding the structure of the image of the convex cone
\mathcal{K}
under a
linear transformation is an important problem in optimization.
Since
{\mathcal{M}}
is surjective and hence an
open mapping
, strict
feasibility (also called Slater’s constraint qualification,

\exists\hat{X}\in{\rm int\,}K,{\mathcal{M}}(\hat{X})=b
) requires that
b\in{\rm int\,}{\mathcal{M}}(K)
.
In addition, the linear image of a convex cone can have very different
structure from the convex cone itself. The linear image of a closed
convex cone is not necessarily closed, see
[
17
]
for
characterizations. The convex cone
\mathcal{K}
can be

facially exposed
, i.e., any
face
f
of
\mathcal{K}
can be written as
f=\mathcal{K}\cap v^{\perp}\unlhd\mathcal{K}
with
v
in the

dual cone,
\mathcal{K}^{*}
, of
\mathcal{K}
, while
simultaneously the linear image of
\mathcal{K}
is
not
facially exposed.

The smallest number of steps of facial reduction required to obtain the
minimum face containing the feasible region is called the
singularity degree
of the system
(
1.2
) over
\mathcal{K}
.
The singularity degree is an intriguing measurement of complexity for

{\mathcal{F}}
, see
e.g., the case when
\mathcal{K}
is the cone of positive semidefinite matrices
[
23
,
22
]
.
Motivated by these results, in this paper we aim to generalize the
notion of singularity degree to the
case of the linear image of
\mathcal{K}
and in particular, for the
case of not facially-exposed cones. We include results on extending
the coordinate shadow theorem
[
9
]
of singularity degree one
to higher singularity degree. Therefore
we obtain an alternative characterization of the singularity degree of a
linear optimization problem. We are also motivated by
the work in
[
7
]
on
the universal rigidity of frameworks where the connections between
facial reduction and stress matrices are discussed.

For a set
S
, we let
\face(S,\mathcal{K})
denote the smallest face of
\mathcal{K}

containing the set
S
.
We note the following related result, reworded in our notation.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
9
, Theorem 4.1]
Let
\mathcal{K}
be closed. Then
for any vector
v
exposing
\face(b,{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{K}))
, the vector
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}v

exposes
\face({\mathcal{F}},\mathcal{K})
.

As mentioned in
[

]
, “there is difficulty when working with

This is the motivation for our study in this paper.

Background: facial structure of a convex cone

We begin with notation and then present some preliminary results.

2.1
Notation

Consider two Euclidean spaces
{\mathbb{E}}
and
{\mathbb{F}}
with inner products

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathbb{E}}}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathbb{F}}}
, respectively. We use
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
when
the meaning is clear.
Define
{\mathcal{M}}:{\mathbb{E}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}
to be a linear surjective mapping
between the Euclidean spaces
{\mathbb{E}}
and
{\mathbb{F}}
. The
adjoint of

{\mathcal{M}}
,
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
, is defines as the unique linear mapping from
{\mathbb{F}}
to
{\mathbb{E}}

such that
\langle{\mathcal{M}}(x),y\rangle_{{\mathbb{F}}}=\langle x,{\mathcal{M}}^{*}(y)\rangle_{{\mathbb{E}}},\forall x\in{\mathbb{E}},\forall y\in{\mathbb{F}}
. Consider a convex
cone
\mathcal{K}\subset{\mathbb{E}}
. We denote
{\rm int\,}(\mathcal{K}),\text{ri\,}(\mathcal{K})
to be the
interior and relative interior
of
\mathcal{K}
, respectively; and we denote the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{K}
as

\mathcal{K}^{\perp}
. For a vector
v
, we let
v^{\perp}:=\{v\}^{\perp}
.
We associate to
\mathcal{K}
its
dual cone

\mathcal{K}^{*}=\{y\in{\mathbb{E}}:\langle y,x\rangle\geq 0,\,\forall x\in\mathcal{K}\}.

A convex subset
f\subseteq\mathcal{K}
is called a

of
\mathcal{K}
,
denoted
f\unlhd\mathcal{K}
, if

x,y\in\mathcal{K},\,\frac{1}{2}(x+y)\in f\implies x\in f,y\in f.

In other words, a face
f
contains all line segments in
\mathcal{K}
whose relative
interior intersects
f
. The
minimal face
containing a set

S\in\mathcal{K}
, denoted
\face(S,C)
, is the intersection of all
faces of
\mathcal{K}
containing
S
. A face
f
of
\mathcal{K}
is an

exposed face
when there exists a vector
v\in\mathcal{K}^{*}
such
that
{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{K}\cap v^{\perp}
. In this case, we say
v
exposes
{\mathcal{F}}
.
The cone
\mathcal{K}
is called
facially exposed
if all faces of

\mathcal{K}
are exposed. Examples of facial exposed cones includes: the
nonnegative orthant
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
;
the cone of positive semidefinite matrices
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
; and the
cone of Euclidean distance matrices
{\mathcal{E}}^{n}
. The linear image of the
(non-polyhedral) cones
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n},{\mathcal{E}}^{n}
are not necessarily facially exposed,
see
[
9
]
.

2.1.1
SDP
,
EDM
cones

We denote
\mathcal{S}^{n}
as the vector space of
n\times n
real symmetric
matrices. The inner product of two symmetric matrices is defined to be
the trace
\langle A,B\rangle=\trace(AB)
. The cones of positive
semidefinite and positive definite matrices in
\mathcal{S}^{n}

are denoted as
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
and
\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}
, respectively. They give rise to the
Löwner partial order

X\succeq Y\iff Y-X\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n},\quad X\succ Y\iff Y-X\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}.

For a general cone, we use
X\succeq_{K}Y,X\succ_{K}Y
.
The cone of positive semidefinite matrices is facially exposed. Let

X\in\relint f,\,f\unlhd\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
,
and
X=UDU^{T}
be its compact (orthogonal) spectral decomposition. Then,
see e.g.,
[
10
]
,

f=\left\{U\begin{bmatrix}R&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}U^{T}:R\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{r}\right\}=\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\cap\left(VV^{T}\right)^{\perp},

where
U
is an orthogonal matrix and
r=\rank(X)
. Here
VV^{T}
is the
exposing vector for the face, i.e., the columns of
V
form a basis for
the orthogonal complement of the range of
U
.

A matrix
D\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
is called an
Euclidean distance matrix

(EDM for short) if there exists
n
points
p_{i},i=1,...,n
in

\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
D_{ij}=||p_{i}-p_{j}||^{2}
for all indices
i,j
. We denote
{\mathcal{E}}^{n}
as the cone of all
n\times n
Euclidean distance matrices. The EDM cone
\mathcal{E}^{n}
is linearly isomorphic to
\mathcal{S}^{n-1}_{+}
.
See e.g., the book
[
2
]
.
The EDM matrices and PSD matrices
(Gram matrices) are connected by the
Lindenstrauss mapping,
K
. There is an one-one correspondence between the centered PSD matrices and EDM matrices by Lindenstrauss mapping.
We state the result as follows,

K:=\mathcal{S}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{n},K(X)_{ij}:=X_{ii}+X_{jj}-2X_{ij}.

with adjoint and
Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse
,

K^{*}(D)=2(\Diag(De)-D),\quad K^{\dagger}(D)=-\frac{1}{2}J_{n}\offDiag(D)J_{n},

respectively. Here
\Diag
is the adjoint of
\diag
, the matrix
J_{n}:=I-\frac{1}{n}ee^{T}
is the orthogonal
projection onto SC, and
\offDiag(D)
refers to zeroing out the diagonal of
D
.

2.2
Facial reduction

In
[
9
]
, the facial exposedness of the linear image of

\mathcal{K}
is shown to be equivalent to the singularity
degree,
[
23
,
22
]
, of (
1.1
) being one.
Note that the singularity degree is the minimum number of steps needed
when applying a general facial reduction
algorithm,
[
5
]
, on a conic optimization problem.

A facial reduction algorithm aims to resolve lack of
strict
feasibility

in the system (
1.1
). The lack of strict feasiblity can
cause many problems in an optimization problem. For example, one can
have a positve duality gap, strong
duality can fail to hold, and stability and numerical issues can arise.
The facial reduction algorithm is motivated by the
following
theorem of alternative
in conic optimization,
see e.g.,
[
5
]
:

Lemma 2.1
.

Exactly one of the following two alternatives hold:

1.

Strict feasibility holds in
equation

1.2

(\exists\hat{X}\in\text{ri\,}(\mathcal{K})\mbox{ such that }{\mathcal{M}}(\hat{X})=b)
.

2.

There exists
v\in{\mathbb{F}}
such that:

\mathcal{K}^{\perp}\not\ni Z={\mathcal{M}}^{*}v\in\mathcal{K}^{*},\mbox{ and }\langle v,b\rangle=0.

(2.1)

We call
Z\in\mathcal{K}^{*}
an
exposing vector
for the face
f\unlhd K
if

X\in f\implies\langle Z,X\rangle=0
.
We see that the so-called
auxiliary system

equation

2.1

is equivalent to the existence of the special exposing vector
Z
.
One can then replace
\mathcal{K}
by the
exposed face

\mathcal{K}\cap Z^{\perp}\unlhd\mathcal{K}
and obtain a smaller problem with a smaller cone.
This also results in a reduction of the number of constraints
in
equation

1.2
to maintain surjectivity,
see
[
5
]
.
This procedure can be repeated until strict feasibility is attained.
We summarize the facial reduction algorithm over a convex cone in

Algorithm

1
, see
[
5
,
10
]
:

Algorithm 1

facial reduction

Input:

(P)

Output:
A sequence of reducing certificates
Z_{i},\,i=0,1,\ldots,k

Set
i\leftarrow 0
, let
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\leftarrow\mathcal{K},\,i=0,1,\ldots,k
,

Step 1:
Find an exposing vector
Z_{i}={\mathcal{M}}^{*}v_{i}\in({\mathcal{F}}_{i})^{*}
,
Z_{i}\notin{\mathcal{F}}_{i}^{\perp},

\langle v_{i},b\rangle=0
.

If no such
Z_{i}
exists, then exit.

Set
{\mathcal{F}}_{i+1}\leftarrow{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\cap Z_{i}^{\perp}

i=i+1

Return to Step 1.

Output: the minimal face
{\mathcal{F}}_{k}
of
\mathcal{K}
containing the feasible region of
(P)
.

Recall that the smallest number of steps of
facial reduction required to obtain the
mimimum face is called the
singularity degree
of the system
(
1.2
) over
\mathcal{K}
.

The SDP cones and EDM cones are “nice” cones
[
17
]
. A closed convex cone
\mathcal{K}
is nice if
\mathcal{K}^{*}+E^{\perp}
is closed for any face
E
of
\mathcal{K}
. Due to this ”niceness”, one can construct a family of extended duals such that strong duality holds by the facial reduction procedure
[
18
,
21
,
20
]
. The length of the sequence in the extended duals is exactly the singularity degree.

Two closely related problems in the literature are the EDM completion problem and PSD completion problem. In those problems, we are given a partial EDM or PSD matrix whose entries are specified only on a subset of positions and the goal is to fill in the missing entries so that the resulting matrix is EDM or PSD. If we consider the position of a specified entry as an edge of a graph, then we can associate a graph
G
to the projection. It appears that the singularity degree of EDM and PSD problems are closely related to the graph
G
.

The relation between the singularity degree of the completion problem and the underlying graph
G
has been an active research area. In
[
9
]
, it is shown that the singularity degree of EDM and PSD completion problem is at most 1 when the underlying graph
G
is chordal. The EDM completion problem is equivalent to finding frameworks with the specified entries (distances) in Euclidean spaces while the PSD completion is equivalent to finding spherical frameworks on a unit sphere. In
[
12
]
, it is shown that a generic universal rigid framework in an Euclidean space has singularity degree at most 1. In
[
24
]
, the worse case singularity degree of a spherical framework underlying a graph
G
is studied, and it is shown to be at most 1 if the
G
is chordal. Also in
[
24
]
, spherical frameworks whose singularity degree grows linearly in the number of vertices are constructed.

To formally define singularity degree of a face, we first start with an example.

The following lemma plays a key role in defining the singularity degree of a face.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
5
, Proposition 3.6]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a convex cone and
{\mathcal{F}}\unlhd\mathcal{K}
, be a proper face.
Then
{\mathcal{F}}\unlhd{\mathcal{L}}\unlhd\mathcal{K}
for some
exposed
face

{\mathcal{L}}\unlhd\mathcal{K},{\mathcal{L}}\neq\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

We include a proof for completeness.
Assume
{\mathcal{F}}
is proper. Let
x\in\relint{\mathcal{F}}
. Then, by definition of
a face, we get
x\in\partial\mathcal{K}
, the boundary of
\mathcal{K}
.

By the hyperplane support version of the
Hahn-Banach separation theorem there is a hyperplane
supporting
\mathcal{K}
at
x
,
[
13
]
. Therefore there exists
s\in\mathcal{K}^{*}
such that

\langle s,x\rangle=t\text{ and }\langle s,y\rangle\geq t,\,\forall y\in\mathcal{K}.

We claim that
t=0
. If
t>0
, then
\lim_{n\infty}\langle s,\frac{x}{n}\rangle=0<t
, a contradiction. Also if
t<0
, then for

n
large enough, we have
\langle s,nx\rangle<t
, again a contradiction.

We now claim that
\langle s,\bar{x}\rangle=0
for any
\bar{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}
. Let
\bar{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}
. Since
x\in\relint{\mathcal{F}}
, we have
x=\lambda_{1}\bar{x}+(1-\lambda_{1})x_{1}
for some
x_{1}\in{\mathcal{F}}
and

\lambda_{1}\in(0,1)
. Therefore
\langle s,x\rangle=0
implies

\langle s,\bar{x}\rangle=\langle s,x_{1}\rangle=0
.

From this it follows that
\emptyset\neq{\mathcal{F}}_{1}=s^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{K}\subset\partial\mathcal{K}
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
is an exposed face of
\mathcal{K}
containing
{\mathcal{F}}
.

∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Algorithm
2
terminates in a finite number of
steps with
{\mathcal{F}}_{d}={\mathcal{F}}_{t}

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{K}
is a cone in a finite dimensional Euclidean space,
\mathcal{K}
is finite dimensional. Since
d_{i}\notin{\mathcal{F}}_{i}^{\perp}
at each iteration of Algorithm
2
, we have
{\mathcal{F}}_{i+1}
is a proper face of
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
, therefore
{\mathcal{F}}_{i+1}
is contained in a proper exposed face of
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
by Lemma
3.1
, hence the dimension is dropped at least by 1. It is easy to see that
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
at each step. If
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
is a proper face of
{\mathcal{F}}_{d}
, then there always exists an exposing vector
d_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{d}^{*}
,
d_{i}\notin{\mathcal{F}}_{d}^{\perp}
and
d_{i}\perp{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
, a contradiction. Therefore
{\mathcal{F}}_{d}={\mathcal{F}}_{t}
.
∎

Definition 3.3
.

The minimal number
d
of steps taken in Algorithm
2
to obtain
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
is defined as the singularity degree of
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
over
\mathcal{K}
.

The minimum number of steps taken in Algorithm
2
can be achieved by a ”greedy” strategy, i.e., we seek
d_{i}\in\text{ri\,}({\mathcal{F}}_{i}^{*}\cap\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\})^{\perp}
. The following lemma shows the correctness of this strategy. We note a similar result about the facial reduction result is in
[
15
, Proposition 13]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume
\bar{{\mathcal{F}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, and let
\bar{d}\in\text{ri\,}(\bar{F}^{*}\cap\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}^{\perp})
and
d\in F^{*}\cap\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}^{\perp}
. Then
\bar{{\mathcal{F}}}\cap\bar{d}^{\perp}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}\cap d^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Since
\bar{{\mathcal{F}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, we have
{\mathcal{F}}^{*}\subseteq\bar{{\mathcal{F}}}^{*}
. Hence
d\in\bar{{\mathcal{F}}}^{*}
and
d\in\bar{F}^{*}\cap\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}^{\perp}
. Since
\bar{d}\in\text{ri\,}(\bar{F}^{*}\cap\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}^{\perp})
, there exists some
\alpha>1
such that

\alpha\bar{d}+(1-\alpha)d\in\bar{F}^{*}\cap\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}^{\perp}

Let
x\in\bar{{\mathcal{F}}}\cap\bar{d}^{\perp}
, then
\langle x,\alpha\bar{d}+(1-\alpha)d\rangle\geq 0
. Since
\langle x,\bar{d}\rangle=0
, we have
\langle x,(1-\alpha)d\rangle\geq 0
, so
\langle x,d\rangle\leq 0
. Since
d\in\bar{{\mathcal{F}}}^{*}
and
x\in\bar{{\mathcal{F}}}
, we have
\langle x,d\rangle\geq 0
. Therefore
\langle x,d\rangle=0
. So
x\in d^{\perp}
and we have
\bar{{\mathcal{F}}}\cap\bar{d}^{\perp}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}\cap d^{\perp}
.
∎

We now have the following proposition following Lemma
3.4
.

Proposition 3.5
.

The minimum number of steps taken in Algorithm
2
is obtained by computing
d_{i}\in\text{ri\,}({\mathcal{F}}_{i}^{*}\cap\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}^{\perp})
in each iteration.

Proof.

Direct consequence from Lemma

.

3.2
The nullspace form

We consider

The first part of this theorem is just a reformulation of Theorem

For the second part, we just need to show there is an isomorphism between the two convex cones

4
Bounding singularity degree for generic frameworks

We begin with the definition of frameworks.

Definition 4.1
.

A configuration
p:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
of a graph
G=(V,E)
assigns positions in
d
-dimensional Euclidean space to the vertices of
G
. A framework is an ordered pair
(G,p)
consisting of a graph
G
and a configuration
p
.

Given a framework, one can define its stress and stress matrices.

Definition 4.2
.

A stress is an assignment
\omega:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of weights to the edges of
(G,p)
. An equilibrium stress is a stress such that

\sum_{j:v_{i}v_{j}\in E}\omega_{ij}(p(v_{i})-p(v_{j}))=0.

for all
v_{i}\in V
; i.e., the net stress at each vertex is 0.

Definition 4.3
.

Given a stress
\omega
for
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define
\Omega=\Omega(\omega)
to be the
|V|\times|V|
symmetric matrix with
off-diagonal entries
-\omega_{ij}
and diagonal entries
\sum_{j}\omega_{ij}
.

In
[
7
]
, the connection between facial reduction and level of PSD stress matrices is established. The sequence of stress matrices computed at each step of facial reduction algorithm provide a certificate of dimensional rigidity and universal rigidity. The minimum number of the level of stress matrices is indeed the singularity degree of the corresponding SDP optimization problem.
See also the book
[
2
]
. We call the
singularity degree of a framework
to be the minimal number of level stress matrices it has

So far, little has been known about the singularity degree of frameworks underlying a particular graph
G
. In
[
7
]
the stress matrices and its connection to facial reduction is studied in the nullspace form. In
[
9
]
, it is shown that the singularity degree of the matrix completion problem for chordal graphs is at most 1. We show that stress matrices are exactly the solutions to the auxiliary problem of the matrix completion problem in its nullspace form. Therefore, a framework underlying a chordal graph can have at most 1 level of stress matrices.

Given a framework
(G,p)
where
G=(V,E)
,
|V|=n
,
let
d_{ij}=\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2},p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r},\,\forall i,j\in E
. We let
d_{E}=\Pi_{E}(d)\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
.
Let
\hat{X}
be a (centered) feasible solution
for the following EDM completion problem:

\mbox{find\; }\hat{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}\mbox{\;such that }\Pi_{E}(K(\hat{X}))=d_{E},\,\hat{X}e=0.

(4.1)

Here
K(\hat{X})
is an EDM, a solution of the EDM completion problem, where
K(\hat{X})_{ij}=X_{ii}+X_{jj}-2X_{ij}
.
If in addition
\hat{X}
is a
unique
feasible solution,
then the framework
(G,p)
is called
universally rigid
.
Necessarily,
\rank(\hat{X})\leq r
.
We note that
\hat{X}e=0
and
\hat{X}=PP^{T}=PQQ^{T}P^{T}
,
for any
Q
orthogonal. Thus
the points are centered,
P^{T}e=0
, but we can allow rotations using
Q
.

The auxiliary problem from theorem of the alternative,
that identifies positive singularity degree for
equation

4.1
,
is the following:

0\neq Z=K^{*}(\Pi_{E}^{*}(v))\succeq 0,\;\langle v,d\rangle=0.

The singularity degree of problem
equation

4.1
is called the
singularity degree
of EDM completion.

In fact we can reformulate the
EDM completion problem
equation

4.1

in the nullspace formulation.
We use the feasible
\hat{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}
given above.
Now the problem is equivalent to finding
y
such that

\hat{X}+{\mathcal{N}}(y)=J\hat{X}J+\sum_{ij\in E^{c}}y_{ij}JE_{ij}J\succeq 0,

(4.2)

where we note that
\range({\mathcal{N}})=\Null(\Pi_{E}\circ K)\cap\mathcal{S}_{c}^{n}
.

The auxiliary problem (theorem of the alternative) for

equation

4.2
is

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\langle\bar{\Omega},J\hat{X}J\rangle&=&0\\
\langle JE_{ij}J,\bar{\Omega}\rangle&=&0,\forall ij\in E^{c}\\
\bar{\Omega}&\in&\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}&\end{array}

The centered solution
\Omega=\bar{J\Omega J}
can be shown to be a PSD
equilibrium stress matrix. In the following, we will just refer PSD
equilibrium stress matrix as
PSD stress matrix
. Since both
\hat{X}
and
\Omega
is centered, we have

\rank(\hat{X})+\rank(\Omega)\leq n-1.

(4.3)

By doing facial reductions on problem
4.2
, we can obtain a sequence of PSD stress matrices
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},...,\Omega_{k}
by using the previous PSD stress matrix to reduce the problem to an equivalent problem over a smaller SDP cone, and the following relation holds.

\rank(\hat{X})+\rank(\Omega_{1})+...,+\rank(\Omega_{k})\leq n-1.

(4.4)

Lemma 4.4
.

Given a framework
(G,p)
, The minimal number of (nonzero) PSD stress matrices of
(G,p)
is equal to the singularity degree of the corresponding EDM completion problem.

Proof.

The singularity degree of framework
(G,p)
is the singularity degree of problem
4.2
, which is just the nullspace form of the EDM completion problem
4.1
. Therefore the two singularity degrees are equal.
∎

We can also characterize universal rigidity using sequence of PSD stress matrices. Given a framework
(G,p)
whose affine span has dimension
d
. Then
(G,p)
is called
dimensionally rigid
if there exists a sequence of PSD stress matrices
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},...,\Omega_{k}
such that

d+\rank(\Omega_{1})+...,+\rank(\Omega_{k})=n-1.

(4.5)

If in addition the member directions
\{p_{i}-p_{j}\}_{\{i,j\}\in E_{G}}
do not lie in a conic (there exists no non-trivial affine flex for
(G,p)
), then it is universally rigid
[
7
]
.

The singularity degree of the matrix completion problem is closely related to the stability of frameworks. A well-known bound for the forward error (distance to the orgininal solution set) in terms of the backward error (amount of constraints violation) of LMI is given in
[
23
]
in the form

\mbox{forward error}=O((\mbox{backward error})^{2^{-s}})

(4.6)

where
s
is the singularity degree of the LMI.

Given a framework
(G,p)
whose affine span has dimension
d
, if there exists at most one level of stress matrix
\Omega
such that
\rank(\Omega)+d=n-1
, and the member directions
\{p_{i}-p_{j}\}_{\{i,j\}\in E_{G}}
do not lie in a conic (there exists no non-trivial affine flex for
(G,p)
), the framework is called
super stable
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
Hence, super stable framework is just universally rigid frameworks with at most one level of PSD stress matrix. It is conjectured in
[
1
]
and proved in
[
12
]
that generic universal rigid frameworks has singularity at most 1. Hence, for generic frameworks, universally rigidity is equivalent to super stability.

4.1
Frameworks underlying chordal graphs

Chordal graphs appear very often in the study of EDM completion problems. A graph is called chordal if any cycle of four or more nodes (vertices) has a chord,
i.e., an edge exists joining any two nodes that are not adjacent in the cycle.

The following fundamental theorem was proved by Bakonyi and Johnson

Theorem 4.5

Let

The following theorem about the singularity of EDM completions of chordal graphs was proved in
[

]
.

Theorem 4.6

For chordal graphs, the singularity degree of the EDM completion problem is at most 1.

We now have the following conclusion about the level of stress matrices of a framework underlying chordal graphs.

Corollary 4.7
.

Given a framework
(G,p)
. If

is chordal, then the level of PSD stress matrices is at most 1.

Proof.

Consequence of Lemma
4.4
and Theorem

.
∎

4.2
Frameworks where the underlying graph has a Laman subgraph

Laman graphs have played in the study of rigidity theory. In particular, by restricting ourselves within generic configurations, a framework is minimally rigid if and only if the underlying graph is Laman

We start with the formal definition of Laman graphs.

Definition 4.8

(Laman graphs)
.

A graph

is Laman if
|E|=2|V|-3
and every subgraph of
G
with

2k-3
edges.

Definition 4.9
.

Let

be a configuration of
n

\mathbb{R}^{d}

is generic if the

do not satisfy any polynomial with rational coefficients.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a Laman-sparse graph with
n
vertices, let
p=\{p_{1},...,p_{n}\}
be a 2d configuration of
G
, let
d_{ij}=\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2},\,\forall ij\in E
be the squared distance between
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
and

be a point in
\mathbb{R}^{E}
whose coordinates are squared distances in the edge set

. If

is generic, then

is also generic.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that
d_{E}
is not generic, then there exists a nonzero polynomial
f
such that
f(d_{1},...,d_{|E|})=0
. Hence
f(...,||p_{i}-p_{j}||^{2},...)=0
, which we can rewrite it as
g(p_{1},...,p_{n})=0=f(...,||p_{i}-p_{j}||^{2},...)
. Since
p
is generic, we know
g\equiv 0
. (
g
is a zero polynomial)

Since
g\equiv 0
, we know
f(d_{1},...,d_{|E|})=0
for any point
(d_{1},...,d_{|E|})
where
(d_{1},...,d_{|E|})
are squared distances of some 2D framework
(G,p)
. Let
D_{E}
be the set of all those points
(d_{1},...,d_{|E|})
. Thus

in the Zariski closure of

(over the complex). Since

is Laman-sparse,

is independent in the algebraic matroid of the 2D Cayley-Menger ideal

has dimension

. Since

has
|E|

on a complex variety of dimension

is a zero polynomial, a contradiction. Hence

has to be generic.
∎

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
(G,p)
be a generic framework of
n
vertices in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

is Laman-sparse, then the level of stress matrices for

Since

is a generic framework in

be the corresponding gram matrix of

We now study the singularity degree of generic framework

where

is a Laman graph plus one edge. To begin with, we introduce the following lemma.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let

be a polynomial in

. If there exist

with

such that

f(x)=f(y)=0\implies f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)=0,\quad\forall\lambda\in[0,1]

Then

f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)=0,\quad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

In other words, if the zero set of a polynomial contains a face, then it must contain the whole affine span of the face.

Proof.

Without loss, let
z=tx+(1-t)y
where
t>1
. Let
d=x-y
, and
z_{0}=\frac{1}{2}x+\frac{1}{2}y
. So
f(z_{0})=0
Then we can compute

by the Taylor expansion at

such that
f(z)=f(z_{0})+\nabla_{d}f(z_{0})(z-z_{0})+\nabla^{2}_{d}f(z_{0})(z-z_{0})^{2}/2+...+\nabla^{(m)}_{d}f(\bar{z})(z-z_{0})^{m}/m!
. It is easy to see

for any

is a polynomial, we have
\nabla^{(m)}_{d}f(\bar{z})=0
for

, the lemma is proved.

We now introduce some basic notions from rigidity theory, which will be used later to derive an upper bound for the singularity degree of generic frameworks in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
where the underlying graph has a Laman subgraph.

Definition 4.13
.

The rigidity matrix of a framework
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted by
R(G,p)
, is an
|E|\times d|V|
natrux where each row corresponds to an edge
(v_{i},v_{j})\in E
and has the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\vdots\\
0\cdots 0\quad p(u)-p(v)\quad 0\cdots 0\quad p(v)-p(u)\quad 0\cdots 0\\
\vdots\end{array}\right]

where the given row corresponding to the edge
uv
, the d-tuple
p(u)-p(v)
occurs in the columns for
u
and the d-tuple
p(v)-p(u)
occurs in the columns for
v
.

Definition 4.14
.

The rigidity map of a framework
(G,p)
is the map
f_{G}:\mathbb{R}^{d|V|}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{|E|}
defined by

f_{G}(p)=(\cdots,||p(v_{i})-p(v_{j})||^{2},\cdots)_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in E},

i.e., the map that returns the squared edge-lengths of a framework.

Note that the rigidity matrix of a framework
(G,p)
is the Jacobian matrix of the rigidity map divided by 2.

Definition 4.15
.

A framework in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is: infinitesimally rigid if
\rank(R(G,p))=dn-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d+1\\
2\end{array}\right)
, infinitesimally flexible if
\rank R(G,p)<dn-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d+1\\
2\end{array}\right)
, independent if the rows of the rigidity matrix are linearly independent, and minimally infinitesimally rigid if it is independent and infinitesimally rigid.

Recall the following well-known theorem characterizing generically minimally rigid frameworks in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Theorem 4.16

(Pollaczek-Geiringer
[
19
]
)
.

A graph
G
is generically minimally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
if and only if

is a Laman graph.

Proposition 4.17
.

A generic 2D framework
(G,p)
where the underlying graph

is a Laman graph plus one edge have singularity degree exactly equal to 1.

Proof.

Let

where the edge set of

is

is a Laman plus one graph and

is the set of the Laman graph while

is the extra one edge.
We consider the rigidity map
f_{G}(\cdot)
and Let
\bar{d}_{E_{1}}=f_{G}(\bar{p})

It is easy to see that the Jacobian matrix of the map

is just the rigidity matrix in Definition

Since
(G,\bar{p})
is a generic framework, the Jacobian matrix of the map

has maximum rank at

which is

by Theorem
4.16

(5.1)

6
Conclusion

We define the singularity degree of any face of a convex cone as the minimum number of steps to expose it by using exposing vectors in a sequence of growing dual cones. Given a convex cone, the linear image of it is also a convex cone. However, the linear image of a facially exposed convex cone is not necessarily facially exposed. We show that the singularity degree of the minimum face containing vector
b
is exactly the singularity degree of the linear conic optimization problem
\{\min c^{T}x\;|\;{\mathcal{M}}(x)=b,x\in\mathcal{K}\}
. We then utilize tools from algebraic geometry and rigidity theory to give an upper bound of the singularity degree of the EDM completion problem related to genericframeworks or tensigrities in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
where
d=2
or
3
. The most interesting case seems to be the case where
G
is a Laman graph plus 2 edge in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, as this is the first case where strict complementarity could possibly fail for a generic framework in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. So far, all the frameworks where strict complementarity fails in the literature are constructed in a way that the coordinates of some points are in some special positions, i.e., the framework is non-generic. It is an interesting open problem to construct generic frameworks with singularity degree more than 1. Although our method is focused on EDM completion problem, it can be adjusted to be analyze other similar SDP problems.
