Extremes of Lévy-driven spatial random fields with regularly varying Lévy measure Journal: Stochastic Processes and Their Applications
Anders Rønn-Nielsen, Mads Stehr

Abstract
We consider an infinitely divisible random field indexed by ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , d ∈ ℕ d\in\mathbb{N} , given as an integral of a kernel function with respect to a Lévy basis with a Lévy measure having a regularly varying right tail. First we show that the tail of its supremum over any bounded set is asymptotically equivalent to the right tail of the Lévy measure times the integral of the kernel. Secondly, when observing the field over an appropriately increasing sequence of continuous index sets, we obtain an extreme value theorem stating that the running supremum converges in distribution to the Fréchet distribution.

Introduction

In this paper we consider the extremal behavior of a spatial Lévy-driven moving average given by

X_{v}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(v-u)\Lambda(\mathrm{d}u),\qquad v\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}},

(1)

where
\Lambda
is an infinitely divisible, independently scattered random measure on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
(i.e. a Lévy basis) and
f
is an appropriate kernel function; see
[
17
, Theorem 2.7]
for necessary and sufficient conditions guaranteeing its existence. Lévy-driven models as in (
1
) form a very general modeling framework used for a wide range of purposes, including modeling of financial assets (
[
4
]
), turbulent flows (
[
3
]
), brain imaging data (
[
13
]
) and wind power prices (
[
5
]
). Estimators for its mean and variogram are suggested in
[
20
]
, and central limit theorems for these estimators are also presented.

In this paper we assume that the Lévy measure
\rho
of the basis
\Lambda

has a regularly varying right tail;
see for instance
[
10
, Appendix A.3]
. The regularly varying distributions are in particular subexponential, and the class covers many interesting heavy-tailed distributions such as the Pareto, Cauchy, Loggamma, stable (of index
<2
) and, in particular, Fréchet distribution. Moreover (and essential to this paper), the set of regularly varying distributions coincide with the maximum domain of attraction of the Fréchet distribution; see
[
10
, Chapter 3]
.

In addition to assuming integrability of
f
and regular variation of the right tail of
\rho
, we consider two different assumptions on the pair
(\Lambda,f)
.
In Assumption
1
we have minimal requirements on
\rho
but we consider a Hölder continuous kernel
f
. In Assumption
2
we have minimal requirements on
f
, whereas we restrict consideration to Lévy bases of finite variation, meaning in particular that the Lévy measure
\rho
has finite first moment in a neighborhood of
0
.

The first part of the paper concerns the asymptotic representation of the tail of
\sup_{v\in B}X_{v}
for some fixed and bounded index set
B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. With
\alpha>0
denoting the regularly varying index of
\rho
we show that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{v\in B}X_{v}>x\bigr)\sim\rho((x,\infty))\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{v\in B}f^{\alpha}(v-u)\mathrm{d}u

(2)

as
x\to\infty
, where
\sim
denotes asymptotic equivalence. In fact we show the more general result that the equivalence holds true even if we replace
X
with
X+Y^{1}
, where
Y^{1}
is an independent field with right tail lighter than
X
. To obtain the representation (
2
) we use a result from
[
22
]
, where we explicitly use that the right tail of
\rho
is subexponential.

Results similar to (
2
) are found in the literature for one-dimensional regularly varying processes, and for spatial fields with lighter tails: In
[
11
]
a field

X_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}f(r,t-s)\Lambda(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}s)

(3)

is studied under the assumption of an underlying regularly varying Lévy measure. Replacing
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
with
\mathbb{R}
and
B
with the fixed interval
[0,h]
, the claim (
2
) is shown for the one-dimensional moving average. If, instead, the spatial Lévy-driven field
X_{v}
has a convolution equivalent Lévy measure
\rho
of strictly positive index (
[
7
,
8
,
15
]
), it is shown in
[
19
]
that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{v\in B}X_{v}>x\bigr)\sim\rho((x,\infty))K\lvert B\rvert,

where
\lvert B\rvert
is Lebesgue measure of
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
K
is a computable constant. A slightly more general result is given in
[
28
]
, in which a space-time Lévy model
X_{v,t}
with a convolution equivalent Lévy measure is considered. For a large class of functionals
\Psi
acting on the field, it is shown that there are constants
c,C
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\Psi(X_{v,t})>x\bigr)\sim C\rho((x/c,\infty))

as
x\to\infty
. For Gaussian random fields the distribution of the supremum can be approximated by the expected Euler characteristic of an excursion set (see
[
1
]
and the references therein).

The second part of the paper concerns the asymptotic distribution of
\sup_{v\in C_{n}}X_{v}
as
n\to\infty
, where
(C_{n})
is a sequence of index sets in
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
increasing appropriately. From extreme value theory for dependent stationary fields we know, assuming some mixing and anti-clustering conditions, that the distribution of the running maximum of a stationary field is determined by its marginal tail; see
[
10
,
14
,
18
]
for detailed treatments of classical extreme value theory, and see
[
12
,
26
,
27
]
for generalizations to stationary, discretely indexed
d
-dimensional spatial fields. In particular, if the marginal tail is in the maximum domain of attraction of the Fréchet distribution (or equivalently it is regularly varying), then the running maximum of the field converges to the Fréchet distribution.
Thus, with (
2
) in mind, we expect (and show) that the distribution of
\sup_{v\in C_{n}}X_{v}
converges to the Fréchet distribution
if the index sets
C_{n}
behave nicely:
We require that
C_{n}
is a union of a fixed number of connected convex bodies (convex, compact sets with non-empty interior) with intrinsic volumes sufficiently bounded relative to the volume of
C_{n}
; see
[
25
, Chapter 4]
for an exposition of convex bodies and their intrinsic volumes. This includes the useful situation where a union of fixed convex bodies is scaled by an increasing real sequence.
With
\alpha>0
denoting the regularly varying index of
\rho
we then show that there are norming constants
(a_{n})
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(a_{n}^{-1}\sup_{v\in C_{n}}X_{v}>x\bigr)\to\exp\bigl(-x^{-\alpha}\rho((1,\infty))\bigr)

(4)

as
n\to\infty
. The result does not follow by the aforementioned papers on discretely indexed fields, as the appropriate mixing and anti-clustering conditions do not easily show for our continuously indexed field. Instead, the proof is based on a conditioning argument, where we apply an independent decomposition
X=Z+Y
and condition on
Y=y
. Here
Z
is a compound Poisson sum, which determines the supremum of
X
, and
Y
represents the light-tailed and high-activity part of
X
.
The limit (
4
) is then obtained by first establishing the conditional result for
Z+y
and then applying ergodic properties of the light-tailed field
Y
. This proof technique almost immediately implies the extended result that (
4
) is also satisfied with
X
replaced by
X+Y^{1}+Y^{2}
, where the stationary and independent fields
Y^{1}
and
Y^{2}
satisfy that
Y^{1}
is ergodic and that
X
has heavier right tails than
Y^{1}
and
\lvert Y^{2}\rvert
.

Extremal results related to the one in the present paper are found in the literature in various forms. In
[
11
]
the running supremum
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}X_{t}
of the one-dimensional moving average (
3
) is shown to converge to the Fréchet distribution as
T\to\infty
. A slightly less general result is given in
[
21
]
, in which the underlying Lévy measure is assumed to be stable with index
<2
.

In
[
27
]
a
d
-dimensional Lévy-driven field with a convolution equivalent Lévy measure is considered under an asymptotic regime that, regarding the sequence of index sets, is identical to the present. Convolution equivalent distributions are in particular in the maximum domain of attraction of the Gumbel distribution, and thus, in this case, the running supremum
\sup_{v\in C_{n}}X_{v}
converges to a power of the Gumbel distribution function
x\mapsto\exp(\mathrm{e}^{-x})
. The proof structure in
[
27
]
has some similarities with the proofs in the present paper, as both rely on conditioning on the light-tailed and heavy-activity part of the field. However, the obvious differences in both tail behavior and limits make the proofs substantially different.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we formally define our Lévy-driven field and provide assumptions on the basis, integration kernel and increasing index sets, before presenting the main results of the paper.
In Section
3
we give some useful results for regularly varying distributions, which are then used to show the tail representation of the supremum of the field in Section
4
. Section
5
is devoted to geometric proofs related to the expansion of the index sets
(C_{n})
, and proofs for the result on the running supremum
\sup_{v\in C_{n}}X_{v}
are found in Section
6
.

2
Definitions and main results

In this section we formally define our random field and state sufficient assumptions after which we can present the main results.
Before doing so, we briefly clarify some notation used throughout the paper. We let
\lvert{}\cdot{}\rvert
denote size in the following sense:
\lvert v\rvert
is the Euclidean norm for a single (one- or multi-dimensional) point
v
,
\lvert A\rvert
is Lebesgue measure of a full-dimensional set
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, and
\lvert A\rvert
is the number of points in a discrete set
A\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
. However, we will at times also use the notation
m
for Lebesgue measure.

We consider a stationary Lévy-driven random field
(X_{v})_{v\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
given as a integral of a kernel function with respect to a Lévy basis. A Lévy basis is an infinitely divisible and independently scattered random measure. The random measure
\Lambda
on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is independently scattered if for all disjoint Borel sets
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
the random variables
(\Lambda(A_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are independent and furthermore satisfy
\Lambda(\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Lambda(A_{n})
. The random measure
\Lambda
is infinitely divisible if
\Lambda(A)
is infinitely divisible for all Borel sets
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
.

In this paper we assume that the Lévy basis
\Lambda
is stationary and isotropic. With
C(\lambda\dagger Y)=\log\mathbb{E}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda Y}
denoting the cumulant function for a random variable
Y
, this means that the random variable
\Lambda(A)
has Lévy-Khintchine representation

C(\lambda\dagger\Lambda(A))=i\lambda a\lvert A\rvert-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\theta\lvert A\rvert+\int_{A\times\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda x}-1-i\lambda x\boldsymbol{1}_{[-1,1]}(x)\bigr)F(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}x)

for all Borel sets
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. Here
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
\theta\geq 0
and
F
is the product measure
m\otimes\rho
of Lebesgue measure
m
and a Lévy measure
\rho
. We furthermore assume that the Lévy measure has a regularly varying right tail: Let
\mathcal{R}_{-\alpha}
denote the set of regularly varying functions (at infinity) of index
-\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, that is,
h\in\mathcal{R}_{-\alpha}
if

\frac{h(tx)}{h(x)}\to t^{-\alpha}\qquad\text{as }x\to\infty

for all
t>0
. Note that this class of functions is closed under asymptotic equivalence (at infinity). We say that a distribution
G
is regularly varying with index
\alpha>0
, writing
G\in\mathcal{RV}_{\alpha}
, if its tail
{\overline{G}}=1-G\in\mathcal{R}_{-\alpha}
is a regularly varying function of index
-\alpha
. We use similar notation for regularly varying random variables, i.e. random variables with a regularly varying distribution. Concerning the Lévy measure
\rho
, we thus assume that the right tail is a regularly varying distribution of index
\alpha>0
, which formally reads
\rho({}\cdot\cap(1,\infty))/\rho((1,\infty))\in\mathcal{RV}_{\alpha}
. Note that regularly varying distributions are in particular subexponential, hence
\mathcal{RV}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
; see for instance
[
10
, Appendix A.3]
.

For the Lévy basis
\Lambda
above, we consider the stationary Lévy-driven field
(X_{v})_{v\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
defined by

X_{v}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(v-u)\Lambda(\mathrm{d}u),

(5)

where
f
is a positive kernel function.
It can be seen from
[
17
, Theorem 2.7]
that the field is well-defined if only there is a
\gamma\in(0,1]
such that
\int_{\lvert y\rvert>1}\lvert y\rvert^{\gamma}\rho(\mathrm{d}y)
is finite and such that the bounded integration kernel
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{\gamma}(u)\mathrm{d}u<\infty
. Throughout the paper we normalize
f
and furthermore require that it is bounded by a decreasing function only depending on
u
through
\lvert u\rvert
.

According to
[
16
, Theorem 2.6]
we can (and will) choose a separable version, also in the literature refereed to as a modification, of the random field
(X_{v})_{v\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
. Similarly we choose a separable version of
(X_{v})_{v\in B}
when we are dealing with a specific index set
B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. Likewise, all other random fields that will be defined throughout the paper will be chosen to be separable versions.

As mentioned in the introduction, we will work under two different assumptions on the Lévy basis and the kernel function. The assumptions differ in that one is less restrictive with respect to the Lévy measure, and the other is less restrictive with respect to the integration kernel. We first give the minimal requirements on the pair
(\Lambda,f)
before presenting the two sets of assumptions. This minimal Assumption
M
, in particular, implies that the field (
5
) is well-defined.

Assumption M
.

The Lévy basis
\Lambda
on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is stationary and isotropic with a Lévy measure
\rho
having a regularly varying right tail of index
\alpha>0
. Moreover, there is
\gamma\in(0,\alpha)
such that

\int_{\lvert y\rvert>1}\lvert y\rvert^{\gamma}\rho(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty.

(6)

The integration kernel
f:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to[0,\infty)
satisfying
f\leq 1
with
f(0)=1
is lower semi-continuous and bounded from above by a decreasing càdlàg function
g
, in the sense that
f(u)\leq g(\lvert u\rvert)
for all
u\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. The function
g
additionally satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{\gamma}(\lvert u\rvert)\mathrm{d}u<\infty,

(7)

where
\gamma\in(0,\alpha)\cap(0,1]
satisfies (
6
).

Note that the integrability in (
6
) along the right tail of
\rho
is already given from the fact that
\rho
is regularly varying. In fact,
\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{\gamma}\rho(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
for all
\gamma<\alpha

(
[
10
, Proposition A3.8]
).
Hence, the integrability (
6
) is simply a requirement on the existence of moments of the finite measure
\rho({}\cdot\cap(-\infty,-1))
. Furthermore, the integrability of
g^{\gamma}
and the fact that
g
is decreasing implies that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{v\in B}g^{\gamma}(\lvert v-u\rvert)\mathrm{d}u<\infty

for all fixed sets
B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. Since
f(\cdot)\leq g(\lvert\cdot\rvert)
and
\alpha>\gamma
, the claim also holds when substituting
g^{\gamma}(\lvert v-u\rvert)
with
g^{\alpha}(\lvert v-u\rvert)
,
f^{\gamma}(v-u)
or
f^{\alpha}(v-u)
. Note that the lower semi-continuity of
f
ensures that the supremum
\sup_{v\in B}
can be replaced by a suitable countable supremum, making the integrand measurable.

Example 1
.

For any infinitely divisible distribution with a regularly varying right tail there is asymptotic equivalence between the tail of the distribution and the tail of the Lévy measure; see
[
9
, Theorem 1]
. In particular, the tail of
\Lambda(A)
will be regularly varying with index
\alpha
if and only if the Lévy measure
\rho
is regularly varying with index
\alpha
.

Example 2
.

Let
0<\alpha<2
. An
\alpha
-stable random field is obtained by letting
\theta=0
and defining

\rho(\mathrm{d}x)=\big(p_{-}\lvert x\rvert^{-(1+\alpha)}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{x<0\}}+p_{+}x^{-(1+\alpha)}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{x>0\}}\big)\mathrm{d}x,\qquad p_{+},p_{-}\geq 0\ ,\ p_{+}+p_{-}>0,

in the Lévy-Khintchine representation of
\Lambda
. Here
\rho
has a regularly varying right (and left) tail with index
\alpha
.

Example 3
.

If the decreasing upper bound
g
is a regularly varying function of index
-(d+\epsilon)/\gamma
for some
\gamma,\epsilon>0
, then (
7
) is satisfied. This includes the simple case where
g(x)=c(1+x)^{-(d+\epsilon)/\gamma}
is a power function of order
-(d+\epsilon)/\gamma
.

Example 4
.

If
h:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a lower semi-continuous function, which satisfies
h\leq 1
,
h(0)=1
and is bounded from above by a decreasing càdlàg function
g:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
satisfying (
7
), then
f:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to[0,\infty)
defined by
f(u)=h(\lvert u\rvert)
will satisfy the requirement from Assumption
M
. With a kernel function of this type, the random field defined by (
5
) will be isotropic. In particular,
f
can be defined by
f(u)=g(\lvert u\rvert)
. Note that
g
is indeed lower semi-continuous, since it is decreasing and càdlàg.

For the first assumption we also require that
f
is Hölder continuous.

Assumption 1
.

The Lévy basis
\Lambda
and integration kernel
f
satisfy Assumption
M
. Moreover,
f
is Hölder continuous with some index
\zeta>0
. That is, there is a constant
C
such that

\lvert f(u_{1})-f(u_{2})\rvert\leq C\lvert u_{1}-u_{2}\rvert^{\zeta}

for all
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
.

Example 5
.

Let
A
be a symmetric, positive definite matrix and define
f
by

f(u)=\exp(-u^{T}A^{-1}u),

where
u^{T}
is a row vector, and
u
is a column vector. Then
f(0)=1
,
f\leq 1
and
f
is Hölder continuous with index 1. Furthermore,
f(\cdot)\leq g(\lvert\cdot\rvert)
with
g(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/\lambda}
, where
\lambda
is the largest eigenvalue of
A
. In particular, defining
f(u)=\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma\lvert u\rvert^{2}}
yields an isotropic random field, cf. Example
4
.

The second assumption corresponds to the case where the Lévy basis is of finite variation, by which we mean that the triple
(a,\theta,\rho)
of its Lévy-Khintchine representation satisfies that of a Lévy process of finite variation.

Assumption 2
.

The Lévy basis
\Lambda
and integration kernel
f
satisfy Assumption
M
. Moreover,
\Lambda
has Lévy-Khintchine representation with
\theta=0
and a Lévy measure
\rho
satisfying

\int_{\lvert y\rvert\leq 1}\lvert y\rvert\rho(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty.

(8)

Example 6
.

An
\alpha
-stable random field with
0<\alpha<1
clearly satisfies the condition in (
8
).

We are now ready to state the first main result of the paper concerning the tail of
\sup X_{v}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(X_{v})_{v\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
be a Lévy-driven field given by (
5
) where the
Lévy basis
\Lambda
and the kernel function
f
satisfy either Assumption
1
or Assumption
2
.
Let
B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a fixed bounded set. Then

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{v\in B}X_{v}>x\bigr)\sim\rho((x,\infty))\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\sup_{v\in B}f^{\alpha}(v-u)\mathrm{d}u

(9)

as
x\to\infty
.

In fact, we show the more general tail result below.
In this theorem, and in the remainder of the paper, we use the notation
y_{+}=y\boldsymbol{1}_{\{y\geq 0\}}
and
y_{+}^{\gamma}=(y_{+})^{\gamma}
for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
(X_{v})_{v\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
be a Lévy-driven field given by (
5
) where the
Lévy basis
\Lambda
and the kernel function
f
satisfy either Assumption
1
or Assumption
2
.
Let
B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a fixed bounded set,
and let
(Y_{v}^{1})_{v}
be a field independent of
(X_{v})_{v}
satisfying

\mathbb{E}\bigl(\sup_{v\in B}Y_{v}^{1}\bigr)_{+}^{\beta}<\infty

(10)

for some
\beta>\alpha
. Then

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{v\in B}(X_{v}+Y_{v}^{1})>x\bigr)\sim\rho((x,\infty))\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\sup_{v\in B}f^{\alpha}(v-u)\mathrm{d}u

as
x\to\infty
.

Next we turn to the assumption on the expansion of the index sets
(C_{n})
in
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
relating to the extremal result. For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we require that
C_{n}
is a
p
-convex set as defined below. In the following, a compact convex set with non-empty interior is called a
convex body
; see e.g.
[
25
, Chapter 4]
.

Definition 1
.

A set
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to be
p
-convex, if it is connected and has the form

C=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}\overline{C}_{i}\,,

where
\overline{C}_{1},\ldots,\overline{C}_{p}
are convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We give the sufficient assumption on the index sets
(C_{n})
used in Theorems
3
and
4
below. Due to stationarity of all fields involved,
we can without loss of generality in later results assume that
0\in C_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Although not formulated in the assumption, this will be assumed in the remainder of the paper. In the assumption, conditions on the so-called
intrinsic volumes
are stated. For
j=0,\dots,d
, the intrinsic volumes
V_{j}(C)
describe the geometry of the convex body
C
. For instance,
V_{0}(C)=1
,
V_{1}(C)
is proportional to the mean width,
V_{d-1}(C)
is half the surface area, and
V_{d}(C)=\lvert C\rvert
equals the volume of
C
. Furthermore, the functionals
V_{j}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathcal{K}
is the set of all convex bodies, satisfy some important properties, among which we mention

(i)

They are non-negative, i.e.
V_{j}(C)\geq 0
for all
C\in\mathcal{K}
.

(ii)

They are homogeneous, i.e.
V_{j}(\gamma C)=\gamma^{j}V_{j}(C)
for all
\gamma>0
.

(iii)

They are monotone, i.e.
C\subseteq D
implies
V_{j}(C)\leq V_{j}(D)
.

For a greater exposition of convex bodies and their intrinsic volumes we refer to
[
25
, Chapter 4]
.

Assumption 3
.

The sequence
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
consists of
p
-convex bodies, where

C_{n}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}C_{n,i}

and
\lvert C_{n}\rvert\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Furthermore,

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{p}V_{j}(C_{n,i})}{\lvert C_{n}\rvert^{j/d}}\quad\text{is bounded in $n$ for each }j=1,\dots,d-1.

(11)

Example 7
.

Let
C=\cup_{i=1}^{p}\overline{C}_{i}
be a
p
-convex set and define the sequence
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by

C_{n}=r_{n}C=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}r_{n}\overline{C}_{i},

where
r_{n}\uparrow\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Then

\lvert C_{n}\rvert=\lvert r_{n}C\rvert=r_{n}^{d}\lvert C\rvert

and
V_{j}(r_{n}\overline{C}_{i})=r_{n}^{j}V_{j}(\overline{C}_{i})
for
j=0,\dots,d
. In particular,

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{p}V_{j}(r_{n}\overline{C}_{i})}{\lvert C_{n}\rvert^{j/d}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{p}V_{j}(\overline{C}_{i})}{\lvert C\rvert^{j/d}}

is constant in
n
for all
j=1,\dots,d-1
. Thus the sequence
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies Assumption
3
.

Before presenting the last main results, we recall that the distributions in
\mathcal{RV}_{\alpha}
are exactly the distributions in the maximum domain of attraction
\mathrm{MDA}(\Phi_{\alpha})
of the Fréchet distribution
\Phi_{\alpha}
, where

\Phi_{\alpha}(x)=\exp(-x^{-\alpha})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{x\geq 0\}};

see
[
10
, Chapter 3]
.
In other words, we know that there are norming constants
(\tilde{a}_{n})_{n}

3
Results for regularly varying distributions

In this section we list some useful results concerning regularly varying distributions.
By assumption,

\rho
has a regularly varying right tail, and we thus obtain by Karamata’s representation theorem
[
10
, Theorem A3.3]
that

\rho((x,\infty))=c(x)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\alpha(y)}{y}\mathrm{d}y\Bigr)\qquad\text{for all }x\geq 1,

where
c(x)\to c>0
and
\alpha(x)\to\alpha
as
x\to\infty
. Thus, for all positive
\gamma<\alpha
there exist constants
C>0
and
x_{0}\geq 1
such that

\frac{\rho((tx,\infty))}{\rho((x,\infty))}\leq Ct^{-\gamma}\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 1,\ x\geq x_{0}.

(15)

We need bounds similar to (
15
) for sums instead of products. The following lemma describes this for arbitrary regularly varying distributions.

Lemma 1
.

Let
G\in\mathcal{RV}_{\alpha}
be a regularly varying distribution of index
\alpha>0
, and let

{\overline{G}}
be its tail. For all
\beta>\alpha
there are constants
K,C\geq 0
and
x_{0}\geq 1
such that

{\overline{G}}(x-y)\leq{\overline{G}}(x)C\bigl(K+y_{+}^{\beta}\bigr)

(16)

for all
x\geq x_{0}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof
.

We show that there are constants
C^{\prime}
and
K^{\prime}
such that

{\overline{G}}(x-y)\leq{\overline{G}}(x)C^{\prime}\bigl(K^{\prime}+y_{+}\bigr)^{\beta}

(17)

is satisfied for all
x\geq x_{0}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
. From this, (
16
) easily follows.

Since
G\in\mathcal{RV}_{\alpha}
and hence
{\overline{G}}\in\mathcal{R}_{-\alpha}
it follows by Karamata’s representation theorem for regularly varying functions that

{\overline{G}}(x)=a(x)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\alpha(z)}{z}\mathrm{d}z\Bigr)\qquad\text{for all }x\geq 1,

for some functions
a(x)\to a>0
and
\alpha(x)\to\alpha
as
x\to\infty
.
Fix
\beta>\alpha
and let
x_{0}\geq 1
be given such that

\alpha(x)<\beta,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lvert a(x)-a\rvert\leq a/3

(18)

for all
x\geq x_{0}
.

Now consider only
x\geq x_{0}
and
y\geq 0
. If
y\leq x-x_{0}
and hence
x-y\geq x_{0}
, we find from
(
18
) above that

\displaystyle\frac{{\overline{G}}(x-y)}{{\overline{G}}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{a(x-y)}{a(x)}\exp\Bigl(\int_{x-y}^{x}\frac{\alpha(z)}{z}\mathrm{d}z\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq 2\Bigl(\frac{x}{x-y}\Bigr)^{\beta}

\displaystyle\leq 2\Bigl(1+\frac{y}{x_{0}}\Bigr)^{\beta}.

If on the other hand
y>x-x_{0}
, and thus
x-y<x_{0}
, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{{\overline{G}}(x-y)}{{\overline{G}}(x)}\leq\frac{1}{{\overline{G}}(x)}

\displaystyle=\Bigl[a(x)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{1}^{x_{0}}\frac{\alpha(z)}{z}\mathrm{d}z\Bigr)\Bigr]^{-1}\exp\Bigl(\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{\alpha(z)}{z}\mathrm{d}z\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\Bigl(\frac{x}{x_{0}}\Bigr)^{\beta}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\Bigl(1+\frac{y}{x_{0}}\Bigr)^{\beta}

where the constant
C_{0}
can be chosen as

C_{0}=\Bigl[\frac{2a}{3}\exp\Bigl(-\int_{1}^{x_{0}}\frac{\alpha(z)}{z}\mathrm{d}z\Bigr)\Bigr]^{-1}.

Choosing
K^{\prime}=x_{0}
and
C^{\prime}=(K^{\prime})^{-\beta}\cdot\max\{2,C_{0}\}
shows (
17
) for
y\geq 0
.

For
y<0
the claim (
17
) reads
{\overline{G}}(x-y)\leq{\overline{G}}(x)C^{\prime}(K^{\prime})^{\beta}
, which is clearly true since
x\mapsto{\overline{G}}(x)
is decreasing and
C^{\prime}(K^{\prime})^{\beta}\geq 2
.
∎

In the proof of Theorem
4
we need to find a sequence
d_{n}
, which tends to infinity at a slower rate than the norming constants
a_{n}
of
\rho
, i.e. the sequence satisfying (
12
).

Lemma 2
.

Let
\beta>\alpha
where
\alpha
is the regularly varying index of
\rho
. There is a sequence
d_{n}\to\infty
of order
d_{n}=o(a_{n})
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\overline{H}(d_{n})}{\rho((a_{n},\infty))}=0

for all distributions
H
on
[0,\infty)
satisfying
\int x^{\beta}H(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty
.

Proof
.

Since
\rho
is regularly varying of index
\alpha>0
, there is a slowly varying function
L
such that
\rho((x,\infty))=x^{-\alpha}L(x)
. Let
\epsilon=\beta-\alpha>0
and define
d_{n}=a_{n}^{1-\epsilon/(2\beta)}
. Then
d_{n}\to\infty
and
d_{n}=o(a_{n})
as
n\to\infty
. Noticing that
a_{n}^{\epsilon/2}L(a_{n})\to\infty
and
d_{n}^{\beta}\overline{H}(d_{n})\to 0
concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Under Assumption

\mathbb{P}(\sup_{v\in B}\lvert Y_{v}^{C}\rvert<\infty)=1

Proof

Let

Theorem 5

for all

Proof
.

The proof follows the lines of the proof of

The field

The purpose of this section is to introduce the geometry necessary to the proof of the extremal results Theorems

A\oplus B=\{a+b\mid a\in A,b\in B\}
. When referring to (discrete or continuous) cubes with side-length equal to
s>0
, we mean a box where all side-lengths equal
s
. Moreover, we say that
C_{r}(u)
is the closed
r
-cube with corner
u\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
if

C_{r}(u)=u+[0,r]^{d}

for
r>0
. Lastly, we let
\mathrm{dist}(u,A)
be the distance from
u\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
to the set
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
.

The following corollary, which is shown in
[
27
]
, is a consequence of the famous Steiner Theorem from convex geometry stating that the volume of the Minkowski sum of a convex body in
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and a ball
B(r)
of radius
r
is a polynomial of degree
d
in
r
.

Corollary 2

(
[
27
, Corollary 1]
)
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex body with boundary
\partial C
and let
r>0
. Then

\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega_{d-j}V_{j}(C)r^{d-j}\leq\lvert\partial C\oplus B(r)\rvert\leq 2\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega_{d-j}V_{j}(C)r^{d-j},

where
V_{j}(C)
is the
j
th intrinsic volume of
C
and
\omega_{j}
is the volume of the
j
-dimensional unit ball.

For the sequence of index sets
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying Assumption
3
we will for each
k,L\in\mathbb{N}
define
t_{n,k,L}\in\mathbb{N}
by

t_{n,k,L}=L\cdot\left\lfloor\sqrt[d]{\lvert C_{n}\rvert/k}\right\rfloor.

The integer
t_{n,k,L}
will serve as the side-length of a sequence of cubes approximating the index sets
C_{n}
. Formally, for each
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
n
large enough relative to
k
, we define the cube
I_{z}^{n,k,L}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
as

\displaystyle I_{z}^{n,k,L}

\displaystyle=z\,t_{n,k,L}+[0,t_{n,k,L})^{d}

\displaystyle=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{d}[z_{i}\,t_{n,k,L},(z_{i}+1)t_{n,k,L}).

Furthermore, we let
P_{n,k,L}
be the set of indices
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for which
I_{z}^{n,k,L}
is contained in
C_{n}
, and we let
Q_{n,k,L}
be the set of indices
z
for which
I_{z}^{n,k,L}
intersects
C_{n}
:

P_{n,k,L}=\{z\in{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\>:\>I_{z}^{n,k,L}\subseteq C_{n}\}\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{n,k,L}=\{z\in{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\>:\>I_{z}^{n,k,L}\cap C_{n}\neq\emptyset\}.

We let the number of such inner and outer approximating boxes be denoted by
p_{n,k,L}=\lvert P_{n,k,L}\rvert
and
q_{n,k,L}=\lvert Q_{n,k,L}\rvert
, respectively.
Note that, by construction,
p_{n,k,L}\leq k/L^{d}
and
q_{n,k,L}\geq k/L^{d}
for values of
n
large enough relative to
k
.
When proving our results we essentially divide
I_{z}^{n,k,L}
into cubes of side-length
L
,
C_{L}(v)
for
v\in(L\mathbb{Z})^{d}
. To this end, define the grid points of
I_{z}^{n,k,L}
by
J_{z}^{n,k,L}=I_{z}^{n,k,L}\cap(L\mathbb{Z})^{d}
, and note that
\lvert J_{z}^{n,k,L}\rvert=(t_{n,k,L})^{d}/L^{d}\sim\lvert C_{n}\rvert/k
as
n\to\infty
.
Lastly, we define a set of grid points which, when continuously filled with cubes
C_{L}(v)
, approximate
C_{n}
from the inside and outside, respectively:

D_{n,k,L}^{-}=\bigcup_{z\in P_{n,k,L}}J_{z}^{n,k,L}\quad\text{and}\quad D_{n,k,L}^{+}=\bigcup_{z\in Q_{n,k,L}}J_{z}^{n,k,L},

which then satisfy

\bigcup_{z\in D_{n,k,L}^{-}}C_{L}(z)\subseteq C_{n}\subseteq\bigcup_{z\in D_{n,k,L}^{+}}C_{L}(z).

(24)

Theorem 6
.

Let
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy Assumption
3
. Then,

(i)

for all
L\in\mathbb{N}
the sequences
p_{n,k,L}
and
q_{n,k,L}
, defined above, satisfy that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}p_{n,k,L}\sim\frac{k}{L^{d}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\limsup_{n\to\infty}q_{n,k,L}\sim\frac{k}{L^{d}}

as
k\to\infty
,

(ii)

for each
k
,
L
and
n
with
n
large enough relative to
k
, it holds that
D_{n,k,L}^{+}\subseteq K_{n,k,L}
, where

is the cube

K_{n,k,L}=\bigcup_{z\in N_{k,L}}J_{z}^{n,k,L},

and
N_{k,L}
is on the form
N_{k,L}=[-c_{k,L}\>,\>c_{k,L}]^{d}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for some

,

(iii)

for all
L\in\mathbb{N}
there exists

such that for all

We note that the discrete index set

in

above does not depend on

. In particular, for any fixed

and

, all

are contained in the same finite collection of (increasing) sets of grid points

.

Proof
.

For each
n,k,L\in\mathbb{N}
define
\tilde{t}_{n,k,L}=L\,\bigl(\lvert C_{n}\rvert/k\bigr)^{1/d}
. Turning to Corollary

, we obtain

\lvert\partial C_{n}\oplus B(\tilde{t}_{n,k,L})\rvert\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lvert\partial C_{n,i}\oplus B(\tilde{t}_{n,k,L})\rvert\leq 2\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega_{d-j}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{p}V_{j}(C_{n,i})\Bigr)\tilde{t}_{n,k,L}^{d-j},

which clearly implies that

\frac{L^{d}}{k}\frac{1}{\tilde{t}_{n,k,L}^{d}}\lvert\partial C_{n}\oplus B(\tilde{t}_{n,k,L})\rvert\leq 2\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega_{d-j}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{p}V_{j}(C_{n,i})}{\lvert C_{n}\rvert^{j/d}}\Bigl(\frac{L}{\sqrt[d]{k}}\Bigr)^{d-j}.

By (
11
) of Assumption
3
we conclude the convergence

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{L^{d}}{k}\frac{1}{\tilde{t}_{n,k,L}^{d}}\lvert\partial C_{n}\oplus B(\tilde{t}_{n,k,L})\rvert\to 0

as
k\to\infty
.
In fact, replacing
L
by

r\cdot L
for any

and realizing that

show that also

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{L^{d}}{k}\frac{1}{\tilde{t}_{n,k,L}^{d}}\lvert\partial C_{n}\oplus B(r\,\tilde{t}_{n,k,L})\rvert\to 0

as
k\to\infty
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
t_{n,k,L}\leq\tilde{t}_{n,k,L}
, and
t_{n,k,L}\sim\tilde{t}_{n,k,L}
as

, we also have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{L^{d}}{k}\frac{1}{t_{n,k,L}^{d}}\lvert\partial C_{n}\oplus B(r\,t_{n,k,L})\rvert\to 0

(25)

as

for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Since

denotes the side-length of the cubes

, we can find an integer
r\in\mathbb{N}
independent of

such that

I_{z}^{n,k,L}\subseteq\partial C_{n}\oplus B(r\,t_{n,k,L})

whenever
I_{z}^{n,k,L}\cap\partial C_{n}\neq\emptyset
. Using that
\lvert I_{z}^{n,k,L}\rvert=t_{n,k,L}^{d}
, we then find

\displaystyle\frac{L^{d}}{k}(q_{n,k,L}-p_{n,k,L})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L^{d}}{k}\frac{1}{t_{n,k,L}^{d}}\Big\lvert\bigcup_{I_{z}^{n,k,L}\cap\partial C_{n}\neq\emptyset}I_{z}^{n,k,L}\Big\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L^{d}}{k}\frac{1}{t_{n,k,L}^{d}}\lvert\partial C_{n}\oplus B(r\,t_{n,k,L})\rvert.

Together with (

) and the fact that

for

, this proves statement
(i)
.

We now show statement
(ii)
. For each fixed

and

, the sequence
(q_{n,k,L})_{n}
is bounded by a constant

for

large enough relative to

. Furthermore, as the origin

, it must also satisfy

. By assumption,

is connected, and thus

consists of at most

points that are pairwise neighbors. In particular,

which implies the desired result. Finally,
(iii)

.

Lemma 7
.

For all

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{v\in C_{L}(0)}\bigl(f^{(t)}(v-u)\bigr)^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}u\sim L^{d}

Proof
.

Since

with

and

, it is seen that the claim follows once we show that

\int_{(C_{L}(0))^{c}}\sup_{v\in C_{L}(0)}\bigl(f^{(t)}(v-u)\bigr)^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}u=o(L^{d})

as

. Since

is increasing in

and bounded by

\int_{(C_{L}(0))^{c}}\sup_{v\in C_{L}(0)}g^{\alpha}(\lvert v-u\rvert)\mathrm{d}u=o(L^{d})

\displaystyle\int_{(C_{L}(0))^{c}}\sup_{v\in C_{L}(0)}g^{\alpha}(\lvert v-u\rvert)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\mu_{j}V_{j}(C_{1}(0))L^{j}\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\alpha}(x)x^{d-j-1}\mathrm{d}x.

\mathrm{dist}(A,B)=\inf\{\lvert a-b\rvert\>:\>a\in A,b\in B\}\geq r
and there are two disjoint sets

, both of which are connected, such that

and

. In particular in the coming lemmas, we consider subsets of
(L\mathbb{Z})^{d}
and require that these are
\gamma_{n}
-separated, where, even though we do not explicitly state it, we assume that
\gamma_{n}\geq L
.

Before proceeding, we define the last piece of convenient notation. For a set
A\subseteq(L\mathbb{Z})^{d}
and a deterministic field
(y_{v})_{v}
we let

M_{L,y}^{(t)}(A)=\max_{z\in A}\sup_{u\in C_{L}(z)}(Z^{(t)}_{u}+y_{u}),

where we simply write
M_{L,y}
for
M_{L,y}^{(\infty)}
, that is, when considering the field
(Z_{v})
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
(Z_{v})_{v}
and
(Y_{v})_{v}
be given by (
19
) and (
20
), respectively. There is a sequence
\gamma_{n}=o(\sqrt[d]{\lvert C_{n}\rvert})
such that for all
\gamma_{n}
-separated
sets
A,B\subseteq K_{n,L}\subseteq(L\mathbb{Z})^{d}
, where at least one is a box, it holds that

\big\lvert\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}(A\cup B)\leq a_{n}x)-\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}(A)\leq a_{n}x)\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}(B)\leq a_{n}x)\big\rvert\leq\alpha_{y,n,L},

where
\alpha_{y,n,L}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for all
L\in\mathbb{N}
and almost all realizations
(y_{v})_{v}
of
(Y_{v})_{v}
.

Proof
.

We choose
\gamma_{n}/2=\lvert C_{n}\rvert^{1/d-\delta}
for some
0<\delta<\min\{\frac{1}{d\alpha},\frac{1}{2d}\}
. In particular,
\gamma_{n}=o(\sqrt[d]{\lvert C_{n}\rvert})
as
n\to\infty
.
Let the sets
A
and
B
be given as in the lemma and define

\mathcal{A}=\bigcup_{v\in A}C_{L}(v)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{v\in B}C_{L}(v)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{K}_{n,L}=\bigcup_{v\in K_{n,L}}C_{L}(v).

Throughout the proof, we assume that
A
is a box, and thus
\mathcal{A}
is a continuous box and in particular a convex body.
Recall that
B(r)
denotes the closed ball in
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
of radius
r\geq 0
with center
0\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. Define
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\mathcal{A}\oplus B(\gamma_{n}/2)
and note that
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{n}=\bigl(\mathcal{A}\oplus B(\gamma_{n})\bigr)^{c}=\bigl(\mathcal{A}_{n}\oplus B(\gamma_{n}/2)\bigr)^{c}
, since
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are
\gamma_{n}
-separated (strictly speaking they are only
(\gamma_{n}-L)
-separated, however, this is equivalent as
n\to\infty
).

For all
v\in\mathcal{A}
let

Z_{v}^{A}=\int_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}f(v-u)\Lambda_{1}(\mathrm{d}u),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{Z}_{v}^{A}=\int_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{c}}f(v-u)\Lambda_{1}(\mathrm{d}u).

Similarly, for all
v\in\mathcal{B}
,

Z_{v}^{B}=\int_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{c}}f(v-u)\Lambda_{1}(\mathrm{d}u),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{Z}_{v}^{B}=\int_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}f(v-u)\Lambda_{1}(\mathrm{d}u).

Since
\Lambda_{1}
is a positive measure all
Z_{v}^{A}
,
\overline{Z}_{v}^{A}
,
Z_{v}^{B}
and
\overline{Z}_{v}^{B}
are non-negative, and we have

\sup_{v\in\mathcal{A}}\overline{Z}^{A}_{v}\leq\int_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{c}}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{A}}f(v-u)\Lambda_{1}(\mathrm{d}u)

and

\sup_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\overline{Z}^{B}_{v}\leq\int_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{B}}f(v-u)\Lambda_{1}(\mathrm{d}u)\,.

If
\alpha<1
, we choose
\gamma<\alpha<\beta
such that
\frac{\gamma}{\beta}>\frac{d-1}{d}
and such that the upper bound
g
has
\gamma
-moment (in accordance with Assumption
M
). If
\alpha\geq 1
, we let
\gamma=1
and choose
\beta>\alpha
such that
\frac{1}{\beta}>d\delta
(recall that
\frac{1}{\alpha}>d\delta
). Let
\epsilon_{n}=\lvert C_{n}\rvert^{1/\beta}
, which clearly satisfies
\epsilon_{n}\to\infty
, and define the events
S_{n}^{A}
and
S_{n}^{B}
by

S^{A}_{n}=\bigl(\sup_{v\in\mathcal{A}}\overline{Z}^{A}_{v}\leq\epsilon_{n}\bigr)\qquad\text{and}\qquad S^{B}_{n}=\bigl(\sup_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\overline{Z}^{B}_{v}\leq\epsilon_{n}\bigr).

First, we show that the probability
\mathbb{P}((S^{A}_{n})^{c})
has an
A
-independent upper bound tending to
0
as
n\to\infty
. For a fixed
n
, we define the sequence
(r_{m})_{m\geq 1}
by
r_{1}=\gamma_{n}/2
and recursively for
m>1
by

\big\lvert(\mathcal{A}\oplus B(r_{m+1}))\setminus(\mathcal{A}\oplus B(r_{m}))\big\rvert=1.

For each
m\geq 1
define the sets

E_{m}=(\mathcal{A}\oplus B(r_{m+1}))\setminus(\mathcal{A}\oplus B(r_{m}))

of unit volume,
and note that their union equals
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{c}
by construction. Utilizing that
\Lambda_{1}
is a positive measure, that
g
is decreasing and that
g(\lvert\cdot\rvert)
constitutes an upper bound to
f(\cdot)
, we find

\int_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{c}}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{A}}f(v-u)\Lambda_{1}(\mathrm{d}u)\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}g(r_{m})\Lambda_{1}(E_{m}).

Let
\Lambda_{1}^{\prime}
be the spot variable of the Lévy basis
\Lambda_{1}
, that is, a random variable equivalent in distribution to
\Lambda_{1}(S)
with
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
satisfying
\lvert S\rvert=1
. Using Markov’s inequality we now find that

\mathbb{P}((S^{A}_{n})^{c})\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}g(r_{m})^{\gamma}\Lambda_{1}(E_{m})^{\gamma}\Bigr)=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\mathbb{E}((\Lambda_{1}^{\prime})^{\gamma})\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}g(r_{m})^{\gamma}.

(37)

Note that the underlying Lévy measure
\rho_{1}
has finite
\gamma
-moment (see e.g.
[
10
, Proposition A3.8]
), and thus also
\Lambda_{1}^{\prime}
has finite
\gamma
-moment (cf.
[
24
, Theorem 25.3]
). Showing that
\mathbb{P}((S^{A}_{n})^{c})\to 0
uniformly in
A
therefore amounts to showing that the sum above is of order
o(\epsilon_{n}^{\gamma})
uniformly in
A
. Clearly, it is enough to consider the sum starting at index
m=2
, which is more convenient. Using the construction of the sets
E_{m}
and the fact that
g
is decreasing, Lemma
6
and the construction of
\mathcal{K}_{n,L}
implies the existence of
n
- and
A
-independent constants
\mu_{j}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{j}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}g(r_{m})^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\int_{E_{m-1}}g(r_{m})^{\gamma}\mathrm{d}u

(38)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\int_{E_{m}}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{A}}g(\lvert v-u\rvert)^{\gamma}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{c}}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{A}}g(\lvert v-u\rvert)^{\gamma}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\mu_{j}V_{j}(\mathcal{A})\int_{\gamma_{n}/2}^{\infty}g(x)^{\gamma}x^{d-j-1}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\mu_{j}V_{j}(\mathcal{K}_{n,L})\int_{\gamma_{n}/2}^{\infty}g(x)^{\gamma}x^{d-j-1}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\tilde{\mu}_{j}\lvert C_{n}\rvert^{j/d}\int_{\gamma_{n}/2}^{\infty}g(x)^{\gamma}x^{d-j-1}\mathrm{d}x.

For each
j=0,\dots,d-1
we have

\displaystyle\lvert C_{n}\rvert^{j/d}\int_{\gamma_{n}/2}^{\infty}g(x)^{\gamma}x^{d-j-1}\,\mathrm{d}x

(39)

\displaystyle\leq\lvert C_{n}\rvert^{(d-1)/d}\int_{\gamma_{n}/2}^{\infty}g(x)^{\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\gamma_{n}/2}^{\infty}g(x)^{\gamma}x^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}x.

The second integral on the right hand side clearly tends to
0
, and in particular it is of order
o(\epsilon_{n}^{\gamma})
as
n\to\infty
. In the case
\alpha<1
, the integrability of
g^{\gamma}
and the construction of
\epsilon_{n}
, gives that the first term on the right hand side is of order
o(\epsilon_{n}^{\gamma})
as
n\to\infty
. In the case
\alpha\geq 1
, where
\gamma=1
, we have for the first term that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon_{n}}\lvert C_{n}\rvert^{(d-1)/d}\int_{\gamma_{n}/2}^{\infty}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\lvert C_{n}\rvert^{d\delta-1/\beta}\Bigl(\frac{\gamma_{n}}{2}\Bigr)^{d-1}\int_{\gamma_{n}/2}^{\infty}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\lvert C_{n}\rvert^{d\delta-1/\beta}\int_{\gamma_{n}/2}^{\infty}g(x)x^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}x

which tends to 0, since we have chosen
\beta,\delta
with
1/\beta>d\delta
.
Thus, combining (
37
)–(
39
), we have shown for all
\alpha>0
that
\mathbb{P}((S_{n}^{A})^{c})
has an upper bound independent of
A
that tends to 0 as
n\to\infty
.

The procedure for evaluating
\mathbb{P}((S_{n}^{B})^{c})
is similar: Let
n
be fixed and define for each
r>0
the set

\mathcal{A}_{n}^{-r}=\mathcal{A}_{n}\setminus(\partial\mathcal{A}_{n}\oplus B(r)),

recalling that
\partial\mathcal{A}_{n}
denotes the boundary of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. Hence,
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{-r}
is the set
\mathcal{A}_{n}
without the strip of thickness
r
closest to its boundary.
Note that
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{-r}
is decreasing in
r
and that it will be empty for
r
large enough. Define for all
m\geq 0
the sequence
s_{m}=r_{m}-\gamma_{n}/2
with
(r_{m})
as above, and define for
m\geq 1
the sets

F_{m}=\mathcal{A}_{n}^{-s_{m}}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{n}^{-s_{m+1}}.

As a consequence of the proof of Lemma
6
, equation (
27
) (see
[
27
]
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\lvert F_{m}\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\mathcal{A}_{n}^{-s_{m}}\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{A}_{n}^{-s_{m+1}}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\mathcal{A}_{n}\oplus B(s_{m+1})\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{A}_{n}\oplus B(s_{m})\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\mathcal{A}\oplus B(r_{m+1})\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{A}\oplus B(r_{m})\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert E_{m}\rvert,

with
E_{m}
defined as above.
Since
\Lambda_{1}
is non-negative, we e.g. have that
\mathbb{E}(\Lambda_{1}(F_{m})^{\gamma})\leq\mathbb{E}(\Lambda_{1}(E_{m})^{\gamma})
, and then, similarly to (
37
), we find

\mathbb{P}((S^{B}_{n})^{c})\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{n}^{\gamma}}\mathbb{E}((\Lambda_{1}^{\prime})^{\gamma})\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}g(r_{m})^{\gamma}.

We can proceed as above to find an upper bound that tends to 0 and is independent of
B
.

Identically to arguments applied in the proof of
[
27
, Lemma 6]
we can derive that

\displaystyle\big\lvert\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}(A\cup B)\leq a_{n}x)-\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}(A)\leq a_{n}x)\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}(B)\leq a_{n}x)\big\rvert

(40)

\displaystyle\leq 2\Bigl(\sum_{v\in K_{n,L}}\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}(\{v\})>a_{n}x-\epsilon_{n})-\sum_{v\in K_{n,L}}\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}(\{v\})>a_{n}x+\epsilon_{n})\Bigr)

\displaystyle+2\Bigl(\mathbb{P}\bigl((S^{A}_{n})^{c}\bigr)+\mathbb{P}\bigl((S^{B}_{n})^{c}\bigr)\Bigr).

We have already seen that the second term above tends to 0 as
n\to\infty
.
Since
a_{n}=\lvert C_{n}\rvert^{1/\alpha}S(\lvert C_{n}\rvert)
for a slowly varying function
S
(cf.
[
10
, p. 131]
), we have
\epsilon_{n}=o(a_{n})
.
From this it is not difficult to see that
\rho((a_{n}x,\infty))\sim\rho((a_{n}x\pm\epsilon_{n},\infty))
as

.
Therefore, realizing that
\lvert C_{n}\rvert\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}(\{v\})>a_{n}x+\epsilon_{n})
and
\lvert C_{n}\rvert\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}(\{v\})>a_{n}x-\epsilon_{n})
have the same limit for fixed

.
This completes the proof.

.

Fix

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v<v^{\prime}\in J_{z}\\

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v<v^{\prime}\in J_{z}}\overline{F}_{L,t}(a_{n}x-y^{*}(v))\overline{F}_{L,t}(a_{n}x-y^{*}(v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(\overline{F}_{L,t}(a_{n}x)\bigr)^{2}\Bigl(\sum_{v\in J_{z}}C\bigl(K+(y^{*}(v))_{+}^{\beta}\bigr)\Bigr)^{2}

for sufficiently large

. Since
\lvert C_{n}\rvert/k\sim\lvert J_{z}\rvert

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,\bigl(\overline{F}_{L,t}(a_{n}x)\bigr)^{2}\Bigl(\sum_{v\in J_{z}}C\bigl(K+(y^{*}(v))_{+}^{\beta}\bigr)\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\Bigl(\tau_{L}^{(t)}C\bigl(K+\mathbb{E}\bigl(\sup_{v\in C_{L}(0)}Y_{v}\bigr)_{+}^{\beta}\bigr)\Bigr)^{2}

almost surely. This is independent of

and of order

as

for all

. This shows (
44
) and (
45
) for the terms in the sum with indices more than

apart.

For notational convenience, define

Now consider

we have for all fixed

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\overline{F}_{L,t}(a_{n}x)}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{u\in C_{L}(v)}Z_{u}^{(t)}>a_{n}x-y,\sup_{u\in C_{L}(v^{\prime})}Z_{u}^{(t)}>a_{n}x-y\bigr)

\displaystyle\to 2-R_{L}^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\sup_{s\in C_{L}(v)\cup C_{L}(v^{\prime})}\bigl(f^{(t)}(s-u)\bigr)^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}u

uniformly for
\lvert y\rvert\leq N
, for all

. Define
y^{*}(v,v^{\prime})=\max\{y^{*}(v),y^{*}(v^{\prime})\}
and similarly for

Combining the uniform convergence above with Theorem

, Lemma

\lvert C_{n}\rvert/k\sim\lvert J_{z}\rvert

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v<v^{\prime}\in J_{z}\\
\lvert v-v^{\prime}\rvert\leq L\sqrt{d}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{u\in C_{L}(v)}(Z_{u}^{(t)}+y_{u})>a_{n}x,\sup_{u\in C_{L}(v^{\prime})}(Z_{u}^{(t)}+y_{u})>a_{n}x\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v<v^{\prime}\in J_{z}\\

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v<v^{\prime}\in J_{z}\\

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau_{L}^{(t)}}{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in(L\mathbb{Z})^{d}\\

Proof of Theorem
3
.

We continue with the notation used throughout this section.
In particular,

\tau_{L}^{(t)}=x^{-\alpha}\rho((1,\infty))\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\sup_{v\in C_{L}(0)}\bigl(f^{(t)}(v-u)\bigr)^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}u

for all fixed
x>0
.
Now consider
J_{z}
for
z\in N_{k,L}
. Recalling the definition of
M_{L,y}
and
M_{L,y}^{(t)}
and the fact that
M_{L,y}^{(t)}\leq M_{L,y}
, it can easily be seen that

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in J_{z}}\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}^{(t)}(\{v\})>a_{n}x)-\sum_{v<v^{\prime}\in J_{z}}\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}^{(t)}(\{v\})>a_{n}x,M_{L,y}^{(t)}(\{v^{\prime}\})>a_{n}x)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}(J_{z})>a_{n}x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in J_{z}}\mathbb{P}(M_{L,y}(\{v\})>a_{n}x).

We now obtain from Lemma
14
that

\displaystyle\Bigl(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\min_{z\in N_{k,L}}\Bigl(1-\sum_{v\in J_{z}}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{u\in C_{L}(v)}(Z_{u}+y_{u})>a_{n}x\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr)^{\tilde{q}_{k,L}}

(48)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{v\in C_{n}}(Z_{v}+y_{v})\leq a_{n}x\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{v\in C_{n}}(Z_{v}+y_{v})\leq a_{n}x\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\max_{z\in N_{k,L}}\Bigl(1-\sum_{v\in J_{z}}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{u\in C_{L}(v)}(Z_{u}^{(t)}+y_{u})>a_{n}x\bigr)+S_{n,k,L}(z)\Bigr)\Bigr)^{\tilde{p}_{k,L}},

where

and
\tilde{q}_{k,L}=\limsup_{n}q_{n,k,L}
and

\tilde{p}_{k,L}=\liminf_{n}p_{n,k,L}
. By Lemma

there is a sequence of functions

satisfying

as

, such that

uniformly in

. Since
\lvert C_{n}\rvert/k\sim\lvert J_{z}\rvert
, we find by Lemma

and
(

\displaystyle\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau_{L}}{k}\Bigr)^{\tilde{q}_{k,L}}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{v\in C_{n}}(Z_{v}+y_{v})\leq a_{n}x\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{v\in C_{n}}(Z_{v}+y_{v})\leq a_{n}x\bigr)

as

, we use the equivalence

(

) to obtain, as

\displaystyle\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\tau_{L}}{L^{d}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{v\in C_{n}}(Z_{v}+y_{v})\leq a_{n}x\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sup_{v\in C_{n}}(Z_{v}+y_{v})\leq a_{n}x\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\tau_{L}^{(t)}}{L^{d}}+o(1)\Bigr).

Secondly, taking the limit

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Following the proofs in this section, it can easily be seen that the claim in Theorem

can be extended as follows:

Consider the stationary field

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl(\sup_{v\in C_{1}(0)}Y_{v}^{2}\bigr)_{+}^{\beta_{2}}<\infty,\qquad\text{and}

Now fix
