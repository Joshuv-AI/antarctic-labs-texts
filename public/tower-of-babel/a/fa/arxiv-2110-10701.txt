Optimizing Strongly Interacting Fermionic Hamiltonians
Matthew B. Hastings, Ryan O’Donnell

Abstract
The fundamental problem in much of physics and quantum chemistry is to optimize a low-degree polynomial in certain anticommuting variables. Being a quantum mechanical problem, in many cases we do not know an efficient classical witness to the optimum, or even to an approximation of the optimum. One prominent exception is when the optimum is described by a so-called “Gaussian state”, also called a free fermion state. In this work we are interested in the complexity of this optimization problem when no good Gaussian state exists. Our primary testbed is the Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev (SYK) model of random degree- q q polynomials, a model of great current interest in condensed matter physics and string theory, and one which has remarkable properties from a computational complexity standpoint. Among other results, we give an efficient classical certification algorithm for upper-bounding the largest eigenvalue in the q = 4 q=4 SYK model, and an efficient quantum certification algorithm for lower-bounding this largest eigenvalue; both algorithms achieve constant-factor approximations with high probability.

Introduction

1.1
Classical optimization

Classical constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs) are very well studied in theoretical computer science, from the perspectives of both worst-case complexity and average-case complexity.
We know many ingenious approximation algorithms — often based on semidefinite programming (SDP)
[
RS09
]
— for efficiently finding solutions within a constant factor of optimal.
At the same time, we also have very precise predictions for the computational intractability of achieving certain approximation factors, subject to conjectures like
\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}

[
Hås01
]
, the Unique Games Conjecture
[
Rag09
]
, and the optimality of Sum-of-Squares (SOS) hierarchies
[
KMOW17
]
.
CSPs involve finding an assignment (typically a Boolean one) to a large number
n
of variables so as to satisfy many given constraints, each a predicate involving at most some
q
variables.
As such, they can often be expressed as finding the maximum value of an
n
-variate degree-
q
polynomial over assignments from
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
; in other words (and in the homogeneous case),

\max\Bigl\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[n]\\
|S|=q\end{subarray}}a_{S}\prod_{j\in S}\chi_{j}\quad:\quad\chi_{j}^{2}=1\ \forall j\Bigr\}.

(1)

For example, the
q=2
case is essentially the “Max-Cut” problem (with edge-weights), and the famous Goemans–Williamson SDP-rounding algorithm and its successors
[
GW95
,
CW04
]
can efficiently find an
O(\log(1/\epsilon))
-factor approximation to the optimum in any worst-case instance when the optimum is at least
\epsilon|a|_{1}
.
Regarding
q=2
in the average case, if the
a_{S}
’s are chosen to be independent standard Gaussian random variables, then we have the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model, and Montanari
[
Mon21
]
has given an efficient algorithm for finding a
(1-o(1))
-factor approximation to the optimum, with high probability.

On the other hand (and oversimplifying the story), CSP optimization tends to be far more intractable once
q\geq 3
.
For example, work of Håstad and successors
[
Hås01
,
MR10
]
, shows that there are worst-case
q=3
instances of (
1
), with
m=O(n)
nonzero coefficients
a_{S}\in\{\pm 1\}
, where the optimum is
(1-\epsilon)|a|_{1}=(1-\epsilon)m
, but it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to find a solution of value at least
m/f(n)
for some slowly growing

(and likely this cannot even be done in time
2^{n^{.99}}
).
Furthermore, for
random
instances of the above form, even though the optimum is typically

we have significant evidence (in the form of SOS hierarchy lower bounds) that any algorithm for certifying the optimum is at most
.99m
requires
2^{\Omega(n)}
time
[
Gri01
]
.

1.2
Fermionic optimization

There exist analogous optimization problems in the quantum setting.
Here we must distinguish two cases.
In one case, called a “spin system”, the Hilbert space of the quantum system has a tensor product structure, and the analogue of (
1
) is called the
q
-Local Hamiltonian problem
.
In the other case, one considers fermionic degrees of freedom, which obey the so-called canonical anticommutation relations.
1
1

1

One can of course consider systems with both fermionic and spin degrees of freedom.

This second case is the focus of our paper.
It is well studied, both from a mathematical physics point of view and for applications in quantum chemistry, condensed matter physics, and materials science.

In addition to being describable in physics terms, a fermionic optimization can be posed rather similarly to (
1
), but with the following differences:
First, the indeterminates
\chi_{j}
are to be assigned
self-adjoint matrices
, rather than numbers, and it is required that they pairwise
anticommute
.
Such matrices are known as Majorana operators.
Second, the quantity to be maximized is the largest
eigenvalue
of the resulting matrix.
Thus a typical fermionic optimization problem may be stated as follows:

\max\Bigl\{\lambda_{\max}(h),\ h=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{q}\}\subseteq[n]\\
j_{1}<\cdots<j_{q}\end{subarray}}a_{S}\chi_{j_{1}}\cdots\chi_{j_{q}}\quad:\quad\chi_{j}^{*}=\chi_{j}\ \forall j,\ \chi_{j}^{2}=\mathbb{1}\ \forall j,\ \chi_{j}\chi_{k}=-\chi_{k}\chi_{j}\ \forall j,k\Bigr\},

(2)

where
\mathbb{1}
denotes the identity operator.
2
2

2

We remark that this problem is only physically natural if
q
is even.
One should also arrange for
h
to be self-adjoint and hence have real eigenvalues; this occurs, e.g., if
q
is a multiple of
4
and each
a_{S}
is real.

Although (
2
) as phrased involves searching over all valid matrix-assignments for the
\chi_{j}
’s, this is not the standard viewpoint in physics.
Rather (as is reviewed in
Section

2.4
), it is without loss of generality to fix a particular canonical assignment of
D
-dimensional “gamma matrices”
\gamma_{j}
to the
\chi_{j}
’s, where
D=2^{n/2}
.
Thus
h
, termed the “Hamiltonian” in physics, may be thought of as an implicitly-represented matrix of exponentially large dimension, and the only task is to determine its largest eigenvalue.
To recall some further physics terminology, the expectation value of the matrix
h
in some pure quantum state
\ket{\psi}
— i.e., the value of vector
\ket{\psi}
under
h
’s quadratic form — is called its “energy”.
Usually in physics one is concerned with the smallest possible energy, termed the ground state energy, but we will follow the computer science convention of seeking the maximum eigenvalue.
(Of course, these are equivalent upon replacing
h
by
-h
.)

The particular case of
q=4
corresponds to finding the binding energy of a molecule with
2
-body interactions, and is therefore one of the most basic computational tasks in quantum chemistry. Here the binding energy is the difference between the ground state energy for some given molecule and the sum of ground state energies of its constituent atoms.

3
3

3

A remark regarding applications in quantum chemistry: in many cases one can define a “particle number operator”
\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n/2}\chi_{2j-1}\chi_{2j}
which commutes with the Hamiltonian
h
. In this case, one may replace the Majorana operators with certain linear combinations called creation and annihilation operators so that every term in the Hamiltonian has an equal number of creation and annihilation operators. For practical purposes, this representation with creation and annihilation operators is convenient, as for such Hamiltonians one immediately knows that the expectation value of an operator that does not commute with the particle number vanishes. However, in this paper we will not focus on this case. Interestingly, though, several of the constraints considered in quantum chemistry can be more readily expressed in terms of the Majorana operators. For example, the so-called “
T_{1}
constraint” is a constraint on expectation values of degree-
4
polynomials that follows from positivity of the “degree-
6
pseudoexpectation” value, and (in the Majorana language) from the fact that a degree-
6
polynomial in Majorana operators with real coefficients is skew-adjoint.

1.3
Computational complexity

The
q=2
case of fermionic optimization (
2
) can be solved by an efficient classical algorithm, involving diagonalizing the Hamiltonian.
Moreover, the algorithm can find an implicit representation of the optimal state, which will be a so-called
(fermionic) Gaussian state

[
TD02
,
Bra05
]
.
However, for
q>2
the problem becomes computationally difficult.
To see this, one may observe that the collection of operators
\mathrm{i}\chi_{2j-1}\chi_{2j}
(for
j=1\dots n/2
) square to the identity and also
commute
.
It follows from this that one can (efficiently) encode any
n
-variable, degree-
q
instance of a Boolean CSP (as in (
1
)) by a
2n
-variable, degree-
2q
instance of the fermionic problem (
2
).
(Furthermore, in this encoding, commuting Boolean assignments correspond to fermionic Gaussian states.)
This reduction shows that the degree-
4
fermionic problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.
In fact, one may also encode the
q
-Local Hamiltonian problem by a degree-
2q
fermionic problem
[
LCV07
]
, and it therefore follows from
[
KKR06
]
that the degree-
4
fermionic optimization problem is even
\mathsf{QMA}
-hard.

That said, there are quite good approximation algorithms for degree-
2
Boolean constraint satisfaction (via Goemans–Williamson
[
GW95
]
and its extensions), whereas for degree-
3
and above there are strong inapproximability results.
The latter observation shows that degree-
6
(and above) fermionic optimization will be hard to approximate well, but leaves open the possible that the
q=4
case (which is most relevant for quantum chemistry) may be a “sweet spot” in terms of complexity.

In this work we focus on such approximation problems.
In the case of quantum optimization problems, though, a fundamentally new difficulty arises.
In the classical case of (
1
), one can naturally certify that the optimum is at least some
\alpha
by providing a witnessing assignment
\chi\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
By contrast, in the quantum case, there may well be no efficient way to classically represent a witness (such as a state
\ket{\psi}
) to the ground state energy, or even to an energy reasonably close to the optimum.
Indeed, assuming
\mathsf{QMA}\neq\mathsf{NP}
, this would be implied by the
quantum PCP conjecture

[
AAV13
]
(at least for local tensor-product Hamiltonians), which posits that determining the ground state energy up to a constant factor
is
\mathsf{QMA}
-hard. A noncomputational implication of the quantum PCP conjecture is the
NLTS (no low-energy trivial states) conjecture

[
FH14
]
, which
posits the nonexistence of a certain class of easily described states with energy close to optimum.
It is natural to define analogous conjectures, fermionic quantum PCP (fqPCP) and fermionic NLTS (fNLTS), for sparse instances of fermionic optimization problems, but this seems not to have been studied.

Besides trying to certify that the optimum of (
2
) is at least some
\alpha
, one may also try to certify that it is at
most
some
\beta
.
For this task, it is natural to consider SDP relaxations (as was done in
[
BH13
]
for the Local Hamiltonian problem), and in particular SOS hierarchies.
Most generally one may use the non-commutative SOS hierarchy, developed in, e.g.,
[
HM04
,
NPA08
,
DLTW08
,
PNA10
]
; it provides increasingly tight upper bounds
\beta_{q}\geq\beta_{q+2}\geq\beta_{q+4}\geq\cdots
on (
2
), with
\beta_{k}
being certifiable in deterministic classical
n^{O(k)}
time.
In the specific context of fermionic optimization, a closely related SDP hierarchy has been studied as a way of characterizing which two-particle reduced density matrices can arise from a many-body system (i.e., which collections of degree-
4
expectation values
\operatorname{tr}(\rho\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l})
can arise from a genuine state
\rho
).
See, e.g.,
[
Col63
,
Erd78
,
Per78
,
ME01
,
Maz12
,
Kly06
]
.
This so-called “
N
-representability problem” is
\mathsf{QMA}
-hard, and is perhaps the fundamental problem of quantum chemistry
[
LCV07
]
.

1.4
Strongly interacting Hamiltonians, and SYK model

As mentioned, in the case
q=2
, the fermionic optimization problem (
2
) is solvable efficiently, with the optimizer being an efficiently-representable “Gaussian state”.
Given this fact, previous research (e.g.
[
BGKT19
]
) has studied how close Gaussian states can come to achieving optimality in the
q=4
case of (
2
), and how efficiently one can optimize over Gaussian states in this case.
In the present work, our main interest is the complexity of optimizing fermionic Hamiltonians when
no
good Gaussian state exists.
A prominent example of this, and our main testbed, is the “SYK model” of random fermionic Hamiltonians.

The
Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev (SYK) model
refers to the case of (
2
) where the coefficients
a_{S}
are chosen to be independent random Gaussians (and where
q
and
n
are even).
4
4

4

In all cases, a prefactor of
\mathrm{i}^{\binom{q}{2}}
should be included to ensure self-adjointness.
We remark that although the case of odd
q
or
n
is not natural physically, these cases are still mathematically interesting.

Contrary to the traditional physics normalization, we will scale by
1/\sqrt{\binom{n}{q}}
so that
|a|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{S}a_{S}^{2}=1
in expectation.
The SYK model has attracted considerable interest in physics for being “maximally chaotic”, and for having a ground state that is, in a sense, as far from being a Gaussian state as is possible for a low-degree Hamiltonian.
With study dating back to
[
FW70
,
BF71
]
, the canonical references for this model are
[
SY93
,
Kit15
]
.
A review from a high energy physics perspective is
[
Ros19
]
.
The physics papers
[
GV16
,
GJV18
]
are rather accessible for those with a mathematics background, and the works
[
FTW19
,
FTW18
,
FTW20
]
give some entirely mathematical results.

Considerations from mathematical physics suggest that for
h
drawn from the degree-
4
SYK model, the maximum value in (
2
) — which we will denote by
\mathrm{Opt}(h)
— is
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
5
5

5

We remark that under the conventional normalization for SYK used in physics, this corresponds to ground state energy
-\Theta(n)
.

with high probability.
Indeed, as we discuss in
Section

3.2
, even finer-grained details are predicted, including that the leading constant hidden in the

should be
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}
.
On the other hand, the only mathematical proofs we are aware of come from
[

]

\omega(1)<\mathrm{Opt}(h)\leq\sqrt{\ln 2}\sqrt{n}

1.5
Summary of our results

We provide a variety of new results concerning efficient optimization of fermionic Hamiltonians.
Primary among these are a rigorous proof that
\mathrm{Opt}(h)=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
(with high probability) for
h
drawn from the degree-
4
SYK model.
Moreover, we prove this via efficient
lower and upper bound certification algorithms
.
For the upper bound, we show that degree-
6
SOS works, and we prove a tighter bound on the numerical constant for degree-
8
SOS:

Theorem 1.1
.

The degree-

\textnormal{poly}(n)
time,
certifies that
\mathrm{Opt}(h)\leq\sqrt{1+\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{n}
with high probability over
h
drawn from the degree-
4
SYK model.
(Moreover, even the degree-
6
SOS relaxation certifies that
\mathrm{Opt}(h)\leq O(\sqrt{n})
with high probability.)

Remark 1.2
.

We also show (
Theorem

6.6
) that degree-
4
SOS
fails
to certify anything better than
\mathrm{Opt}(\boldsymbol{h})\lesssim n
.
On the other hand, our certification procedure can be carried out in a natural “fragment” of degree-

O(n^{4})

It would be interesting to see if the “fragment” introduced here can have practical applications in, e.g., the simulation of quantum chemistry as in

]

Our lower bound, perhaps surprisingly, is via an efficient

It is an outstanding open problem as to whether there is an efficient classical certification algorithm (recall that
Gaussian states are known to only achieve expectation value

Theorem 1.3
.

There is a

With high probability over

As we will discuss, this guarantee

Together,

The NLTS conjecture posits that there exists a family of quantum Hamiltonians which are “spin systems”, in that the Hilbert space has a local tensor product structure, where all terms in the Hamiltonian

First, we give the below nontrivial upper bound for

This theorem is used in establishing

.

Theorem 1.5

Let

2.2
Quasi-Clifford algebras

We work in the algebra
\mathbb{C}^{*}\langle\chi\rangle
of polynomials with complex coefficients over a finite sequence
\chi=(\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{n})
of noncommuting indeterminates.
The empty word is denoted by
1
.
There is a formal adjoint operator
*
on polynomials, and we will assume that all indeterminates
\chi_{j}
are self-adjoint,
\chi_{j}^{*}=\chi_{j}
.
This
*
maps coefficients to their complex conjugates, and reverses words.

We furthermore introduce some relations.
First, we assume each
\chi_{j}
is an
involution
; i.e.,
\chi_{j}^{2}=1
for all
j
.
Next, given an undirected graph
\Gamma=([n],E)
on vertex set
[n]
, we introduce the relation
\chi_{j}\chi_{k}=-\chi_{k}\chi_{j}
for all edges
\{j,k\}\in E
, and the relation
\chi_{j}\chi_{k}=\chi_{k}\chi_{j}
for all nonedges
\{j,k\}\not\in E
.
We denote the resulting algebra by
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
; it is (a special case of) a
quasi-Clifford algebra
, as introduced by Gastineau-Hills
[
GH80
,
GH82
]
.
We will be particularly interested in the complete graph
\Gamma=K_{n}
with
n
even; then
\mathfrak{C}(K_{n})
is the “usual” Clifford algebra in which every pair of distinct indeterminates anticommutes.

Given our relations, it is easy to see that every monomial (product of indeterminates) is equivalent, up to sign change, to a multilinear monomial in which the indeterminates are ordered lexicographically.
Thus we can write a general polynomial
h\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
as

h=h(\chi)=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}a_{S}\chi^{S},\quad\text{where }\chi^{S}\coloneqq\chi_{k_{1}}\chi_{k_{2}}\cdots\chi_{k_{q}}\text{ if }S=\{k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{q}\}\text{ with }k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{q},

(3)

with coefficients
a_{S}\in\mathbb{C}
.
7
7

7

J_{S}
instead of
a_{S}
.
Also,
\chi^{\emptyset}
stands for the empty monomial
1
, and for

we write just

Hence
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
is always finite-dimensional; i.e., it is a “
\dagger
-algebra”, in physics terminology.
We will frequently be interested in the case where
h
is self-adjoint, meaning
h^{*}=h
.
In this case, note that each
a_{S}
will either be real (if
\chi^{S}
is equal to its reverse) or purely imaginary (if
\chi^{S}
equals the negation of its reverse).
Finally, we introduce the following notation (see
Remark

2.16
for justification):

Notation 2.11
.

For
h\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
as in
Equation

3
we write
\operatorname{tr}(h)\coloneqq a_{\emptyset}
.

2.3
The completely anticommuting case

Let us collect here some simple facts concerning the case
\Gamma=K_{n}
, where the indeterminates pairwise anticommute.

Fact 2.12
.

In
\mathfrak{C}(K_{n})
, monomial
(\chi^{S})^{*}=\sigma\chi^{S}
, where

\sigma=(-1)^{\binom{|S|}{2}}=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if $|S|=0,1$ mod $4$,}\\
-1&\text{if $|S|=2,3$ mod $4$.}\end{cases}

Consequently, we can write a generic self-adjoint homogeneous degree-
q
polynomial as

h=\mathrm{i}^{\binom{q}{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[n]\\
|S|=q\end{subarray}}a_{S}\chi^{S},

(4)

where each
a_{S}
is real.

Fact 2.13
.

Let
\chi^{S}
and
\chi^{T}
be monomials in
\mathfrak{C}(K_{n})
.
Then

\chi^{S}\chi^{T}=(-1)^{|S|\cdot|T|-|S\cap T|}\chi^{T}\chi^{S}.

In particular, if
|S|=|T|=q
, then
\chi^{S}
and
\chi^{T}
anticommute iff
q-|S\cap T|
is odd.

Fact 2.14
.

Let
\ell_{a}=a_{1}\chi_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}\chi_{n},\ \ell_{b}=b_{1}\chi_{1}+\cdots+b_{n}\chi_{n}\in\mathfrak{C}(K_{n})
for
\vec{a},\vec{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then
\ell_{a}\ell_{a}=|\vec{a}|^{2}
, and
\ell_{a}\ell_{b}=-\ell_{b}\ell_{a}
.
In particular, if
O\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is an orthogonal matrix and
\ell=O\chi
, then
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n}
are self-adjoint, square to
1
, and pairwise anticommute.
Indeed, they generate an isomorphic copy of
\mathfrak{C}(K_{n})
.

2.4
Representations

By a
representation
of
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
, we will mean a
*
-homomorphism
\pi:\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}
(for some finite
D
) satisfying
\pi(1)=\mathbb{1}
.
Informally,
\pi
is an assignment of matrices (of some fixed dimension
D
) to each
\chi_{j}
such that the assigned matrices satisfy the algebra relations.
The representation theory of quasi-Clifford algebras is completely understood; Gastineau-Hills
[
GH82
]
showed that
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
is always a semisimple algebra and determined its irreducible representations (see also the concrete analysis in
[
Kho08
]
).
We recap it here.

First suppose
n=2r
is even and
\Gamma=([n],\{\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\dots,\{n-1,n\}\})
, the matching graph on
n
vertices.
Then we have an (irreducible)
2^{r}
-dimensional representation
\pi
that assigns

\displaystyle\chi_{1}

\displaystyle=X\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\cdots\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}

\displaystyle\chi_{2}

\displaystyle=\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$Y$}\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\cdots\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}

\displaystyle\chi_{3}

\displaystyle=\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes X\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\cdots\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}

\displaystyle\chi_{4}

\displaystyle=\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$Y$}\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\cdots\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}

\displaystyle\,\cdots

\displaystyle\chi_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\cdots\otimes X

\displaystyle\chi_{n}

\displaystyle=\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\mathbb{1}$}\otimes\cdots\otimes\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$Y$},

where we use the notation
X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
,
Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&\phantom{-}0\end{pmatrix}
,
Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&\phantom{-}0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
for the Pauli matrices.
In fact this
\pi
yields an isomorphism between
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
and
\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
.

As a next example, consider
\mathfrak{C}(\overline{K}_{n})
, the algebra in which all indeterminates
\chi_{j}
pairwise commute.
Then for each
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
we have a
1
-dimensional representation
\pi_{x}
in which
\chi_{j}
is assigned
x_{j}
.
The direct sum
\pi=\bigoplus_{x}\pi_{x}
is a (reducible)
2^{n}
-dimensional representation, which can alternately be expressed as the assignment

\chi_{1}=Z\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{1},\quad\chi_{2}=\mathbb{1}\otimes Z\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{1},\quad\dots,\quad\chi_{n}=\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes Z,

(5)

and this
\pi
yields a
*
-homomorphism

\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}
.

We can also combine the previous two constructions.
If
\Gamma
consists of
r
disjoint edges and
s
isolated vertices (so
n=2r+s
), then there is a
*
-homomorphism to
\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}
for
D=2^{r+s}=2^{n-r}
, via the representation
\pi
which maps the indeterminates to matrices of the form

\displaystyle(\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\mathbb{1}\otimes X\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{1})\otimes(\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{1})\text{ or }(\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\mathbb{1}\otimes Y\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{1})\otimes(\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{1})

\displaystyle\text{or }(\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{1})\otimes(\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\mathbb{1}\otimes Z\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{1}).

As a next observation, suppose
\Gamma
has adjacency matrix
A
, regarded as an element of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times n}
.
Then if we replace the
j
th row with the
j
th plus the
k
th, and do the same columnwise, the resulting
A^{\prime}
is the adjacency matrix of a graph
\Gamma^{\prime}
with
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma^{\prime})\cong\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
, via the isomorphism that has
\chi^{\prime}_{j}=\mathrm{i}^{A_{jk}}\chi_{j}\chi_{k}
and
\chi^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}=\chi_{j^{\prime}}
for
j^{\prime}\neq j
.
By repeating such operations, any
\Gamma
can be transformed to another
\Gamma^{\prime}
consisting of
r
disjoint edges and
s
isolated vertices, where
2r
is the
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-rank of the original
A
.
In this way we can obtain an explicit
*
-homomorphism to a matrix algebra for any particular
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
.

Let us turn to the case of most interest to us, the usual Clifford algebra
\mathfrak{C}(K_{n}
), where we assume for convenience that
n=2r
is even.
In this case, we obtain the well known
2^{r}
-dimensional representation
\pi
of
\mathfrak{C}(K_{n})
given by the Weyl–Brauer
\gamma
matrices:

\gamma_{2k-1}=\underbrace{Z\otimes\cdots\otimes Z}_{k-1\text{ times}}\otimes X\otimes\underbrace{\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{1}}_{r-k\text{ times}},\qquad\gamma_{2k}=\underbrace{Z\otimes\cdots\otimes Z}_{k-1\text{ times}}\otimes Y\otimes\underbrace{\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{1}}_{r-k\text{ times}},\qquad k=1\dots r=n/2.

(6)

Summarizing these results (and applying standard theory, e.g.
[
Dav96
, Cor. III.1.2]
):

Proposition 2.15
.

(
[
GH82
]
.)
Given graph
\Gamma
on
n
vertices, let
2r
be the rank of its adjacency matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, let
s=n-2r
, and let
D=2^{n-r}
.
Then we may define a canonical
D
-dimensional representation

\pi:\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}
.
This
\pi_{\Gamma}
may be expressed as
\bigoplus_{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{s}}\pi_{\Gamma,x}
, where each
\pi_{\Gamma,x}
is irreducible and isomorphic to the Weyl–Brauer representation for
\mathfrak{C}(K_{2r})
from
Equation

6
(and hence is of dimension
2^{r}
).
Finally,
any
representation of
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
is unitarily equivalent to a direct sum
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}
, where each
\pi_{j}
is a copy of one of the
\pi_{\Gamma,x}
’s.

Remark 2.16
.

It follows from
Proposition

2.15
that for any
h\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
and any representation
\pi
we have
\operatorname{tr}(\pi(h))=\operatorname{tr}(h)
(as in
2.11
).
In particular,
\operatorname{tr}(gh)=\operatorname{tr}(hg)
always.

2.5
States, pseudostates, and pseudoexpectations

We recall some standard terminology:

Definition 2.17
.

We say that
h\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
is
positive
if and only if it is a (Hermitian-)square,
h=g^{*}g
for some
g\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
.

Definition 2.18
.

Let
\varrho:\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{C}
be a linear functional (which we take to include the condition
\varrho(h^{*})=\varrho(h)^{*}
, so that
\varrho(h)\in\mathbb{R}
when
h
is self-adjoint).
We say that
\varrho
is
positive
if
\varrho(h)\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
for all positive
h
.
We furthermore say that
\varrho
is a
state
if
\varrho(1)=1
.

Under the canonical
\pi_{\Gamma}
from
Proposition

2.15
, we see that any linear functional
\varrho
is of the form
h\mapsto\operatorname{tr}(\rho\pi_{\Gamma}(h))
for some self-adjoint
\rho\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}
.
From this we infer that
\varrho
is positive if and only if
\rho
is positive semidefinite, and furthermore
\varrho
is a state if and only if
\operatorname{tr}(\rho)=1
— i.e., if and only if
\rho
is a quantum state.
8
8

8

In this work we use the nonstandard convention in which quantum states
\rho
have
\operatorname{tr}(\rho)=1
; this is as opposed to the conventional normalization requiring
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=1
.

Using
\pi_{\Gamma}
to pull back to
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
, we are led to an equivalent definition:

Definition 2.19
.

Abusing terminology, we also say that
\rho\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
is a
state
if it is positive and has
\operatorname{tr}(\rho)=1
.
In this case we use the notation
\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\rho}[h]=\operatorname{tr}(\rho h)
, calling this the
expectation of
h
under
\rho
.

Example 2.20
.

In the case of
\Gamma=\mathfrak{C}(\overline{K}_{n})
, where all indeterminates commute,
for each
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
the following is a state:

\rho_{x}=(1+x_{1}\chi_{1})(1+x_{2}\chi_{2})\cdots(1+x_{n}\chi_{N}).

It satisfies
\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\rho_{x}}[h]=h(x)
.
Under the canonical representation we have that
\pi_{\Gamma}(\rho_{x})\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
has a single nonzero entry in the
(x,x)
position, equal to
2^{n}
.

For the sake of optimization, we will be interested in relaxing the notion of a linear functional
\varrho
being positive.
We begin with the following definition:

Definition 2.21
.

We say that
h\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
is a
degree-
2k
sum of (Hermitian-)squares
(abbreviated
SOS
) if it is expressible as
g_{1}^{*}g_{1}+\cdots+g_{m}^{*}g_{m}
for some polynomials
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
of degree at most
k
.

Remark 2.22
.

Any positive
h\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
is evidently degree-
2k
SOS for some
k\leq n
(see
Equation

3
).
Conversely, any SOS
h
is positive.
This can be shown using the representation
\pi_{\Gamma}
from
Proposition

2.15
: if
h
is SOS then
\pi_{\Gamma}(h)
is a sum of (Hermitian)-squares of matrices, hence
\pi_{\Gamma}(h)
is PSD and thus of the form
G^{*}G
, hence
g\coloneqq\pi_{\Gamma}^{-1}(G)
has
g^{*}g=h
.
Note, however, that
h
being low-degree SOS does not necessarily imply that
h
is the square of a low-degree polynomial.

The following simple fact shows that being degree-
2k
SOS is characterized by a semidefinite program of size
n^{O(k)}
(or
2^{O(n)}
for
k=\Theta(n)
):

Fact 2.23
.

Let
\vec{X}
denote the vector whose entries are the monomials
\chi^{S}
for all
|S|\leq k
.
Then
h\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
is degree-
2k
SOS if and only if there is a complex PSD matrix
H
such that
\braket{X|H|X}=h
within
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
.

Definition 2.24
.

We define a
degree-
2k
pseudoexpectation
to be a linear functional
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\cdot]:\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{C}
with
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[1]=1
and
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[h]\geq 0
for all degree-
2k
SOS
h
(or equivalently,
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[g^{*}g]\geq 0
for all
g
of degree at most
k
).
As before, we may always write
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[h]=\operatorname{tr}(\rho h)
for some self-adjoint
\rho\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
.
We therefore call a self-adjoint
\rho\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
a
degree-
2k
pseudostate
if has
\operatorname{tr}(\rho)=1
and
\operatorname{tr}(\rho h)\geq 0
for all degree-
2k
SOS
h
(equivalently,
\operatorname{tr}(g\rho g^{*})\geq 0
for all
g
of degree at most
k
).
In this case we use the notation
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}_{\rho}[h]=\operatorname{tr}(\rho h)
.

Remark 2.25
.

Whether or not a
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\cdot]
is a degree-
2k
pseudoexpectation is completely determined by the values
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\chi^{S}]
for
|S|\leq 2k
.

In light of the above, we have the following observation which shows that degree-
2k
pseudoexpectations are characterized by a semidefinite program of size
n^{O(k)}
(or
2^{O(n)}
for
k=\Theta(n)
):

Fact 2.26
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\cdot]
be a linear functional on
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
with
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[1]=1
, and let
M
be the matrix having rows and columns indexed by the sets with
|S|\leq k
, and having
(S,T)
-entry equal to
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[(\chi^{S})^{*}\chi^{T}]
.
Then
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\cdot]
is a degree-
2k
pseudoexpectation if and only if
M
is PSD.

2.6
Optimization

Given a self-adjoint polynomial
h\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
, we consider the following optimization problem:

\mathrm{Opt}(h)\coloneqq\max_{\text{states $\rho\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)$}}\{{\mathop{\bf E\/}}_{\rho}[h]\}.

Recalling
\pi_{\Gamma}
from
Proposition

2.15
and the discussion in the previous section, we equivalently have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Opt}(h)

\displaystyle=\max\{\operatorname{tr}(\rho\pi_{\Gamma}(h)):\rho\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times D},\ \rho\text{ PSD},\ \operatorname{tr}(\rho)=1\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\braket{\phi|\pi_{\Gamma}(h)|\phi}:\ket{\phi}\in\mathbb{C}^{D},\ \braket{\phi|\phi}=1\}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}(\pi_{\Gamma}(h)).

In physics terminology, if
h
is a Hamiltonian, then
\mathrm{Opt}(h)
is the ground state energy of
-h
(under
\pi_{\Gamma}
).
As a further equivalence, from
Proposition

2.15
it follows that

\mathrm{Opt}(h)=\max\{\lambda_{\max}(\pi(h)):\text{representations }\pi\text{ of }\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)\};

in other words, the optimization problem seeks the assignment to the indeterminates
\chi_{j}
(satisfying the
(anti-)
commutation relations
\Gamma
) that produces a matrix with largest possible eigenvalue.
We will also sometimes consider the closely related problem of maximizing the operator norm; i.e., finding
\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(h)=\max\{\mathrm{Opt}(h),-\mathrm{Opt}(-h)\}
.
In fact, this makes sense even for non-self-adjoint
h
, if we define

\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(h)\coloneqq\sqrt{\mathrm{Opt}(h^{*}h)}.

Example 2.27
.

In the case of most interest to us,
\mathfrak{C}(K_{n})
with
n
even, the representation
\pi_{\Gamma}
is the one using
\gamma
-matrices from
Equation

6
.
This means that given the (coefficients of the) polynomial
h(\chi)
,
the task is to find the largest eigenvalue of the implicitly determined
2^{n/2}\times 2^{n/2}
matrix
h(\gamma)
.

Example 2.28
.

In the case of
\mathfrak{C}(\overline{K}_{n})
, where all indeterminates commute, the task reduces to optimizing over all the
1
-dimensional representations
\pi_{x}
(described prior to
Equation

5
).
That is, we are given a commutative polynomial
h(\chi)
with real coefficients, and we are optimizing it over the Boolean hypercube,

\mathrm{Opt}(h)=\max_{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\{h(x)\}.

Remark 2.29
.

Supposing
h=\sum_{|S|=q}a_{S}\chi^{S}\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
with
\vec{a}
real, we may ask for a “naive” upper bound on
\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(h)
.
In the fully commuting case,
\Gamma=\overline{K}_{n}
, a reasonable answer is
|\vec{a}|_{1}
; this quantity is sometimes achievable (e.g., if each
a_{S}
is nonnegative, or if
q=1
), and in the computer science literature
|\vec{a}|_{1}=1
is a typical normalization when
q=2
(“Max-Cut”).

On the other hand, in the fully anticommuting case
\Gamma=K_{n}
, it seems that normalizing by
|\vec{a}|\equiv|\vec{a}|_{2}=1
is most natural.
For example, when
q=1
we have
h^{*}h=|\vec{a}|^{2}
(see
2.14
) and hence
\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(h)=|\vec{a}|
.
In addition, normalization by
|\vec{a}|
arises naturally in our generic upper bound using the Lovász Theta Function, presented in
Section

4
(see
Proposition

4.8
).

As we see from
2.27
and
2.28
, given a degree-
q
polynomial
h
by its
n^{O(q)}
coefficients, the naive algorithms for determining
\mathrm{Opt}(h)
have exponential complexity.
This motivates understanding variants of
\mathrm{Opt}(h)
that are computable in polynomial time, foremost of which is the following “SOS relaxation”, which
2.26
shows is expressible as an SDP:

Definition 2.30
.

Given a self-adjoint
h\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
of degree
q
, as well as
k\in\mathbbm{N}^{+}
with
2k\geq q
, we define

\mathrm{SOS}_{2k}(h)=\max\{{\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}}_{\rho}[h]:\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\cdot]\textnormal{ is a degree-$2k$ pseudoexpectation}\}.

(7)

This is a nonincreasing function of
k
, with
\mathrm{SOS}_{2n}(h)=\mathrm{Opt}(h)
.

Alternatively, we may seek to certify
\mathrm{Opt}(h)\leq\beta
by expressing
\beta-h
as a degree-
2k
sum of (Hermitian-)squares.
Finding the smallest
\beta
for which this is possible is the dual SDP for
\mathrm{SOS}_{2k}
, as is well known.
Furthermore, using the fact that we have
\chi_{j}^{2}=1
for all
j
, it is not hard to show that there is no duality gap (see, e.g.,
[
JH16
]
, with the noncommutativity of our setting not affecting the proof).
Thus:

Theorem 2.31
.

Given a self-adjoint
h\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
of degree
q
, as well as
k\in\mathbbm{N}^{+}
with
2k\geq q
, we have

\mathrm{SOS}_{2k}(h)=\min\{\beta:\beta-h\textnormal{ is degree-$2k$ SOS}\}.

(8)

2.23
explains why this characterization is also an SDP.

2.7
Complexity theory

Regarding the optimization problem
\mathrm{Opt}(h)
, there are several computational tasks we will want to consider.
But first we must cover some minor technical details.

Complexity-theoretic niceties.

The input is always considered to be the coefficients of
h
, explicitly given as rational (complex) numbers, as well as the graph
\Gamma
if necessary (i.e., if it isn’t considered fixed to be
K_{n}
or
\overline{K}_{n}
).
Most often we have a fixed constant degree
q
in mind for
h
, in which case the number of coefficients of
h
is bounded by
m=O(n^{q})
.
For the sake of scaling, we typically prefer to assume the normalization
\sum_{S}|a_{S}|^{2}=1
(where
h
is written as in
Equation

3
).
With rational coefficients perhaps only
\sum_{S}|a_{S}|^{2}\approx 1
will be possible, but anyway we will never be interested in cases other than
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)\leq\sum_{S}|a_{S}|^{2}\leq\textnormal{poly}(n)
.
In light of this (and the fact that
\|\pi(X^{S})\|\leq 1
for all
S
), perturbing any coefficient
a_{S}
by a sufficiently small
\pm 1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
will have only negligible effect on
\mathrm{Opt}(h)
, and we may therefore assume that all coefficients
a_{S}
are expressed using
O(\log n)
bits.
(In particular, when we consider Gaussian coefficients
J_{S}
, we may assume they are rounded to
O(\log n)
bits of precision.)
Thus the total input size
L
will generally be
O(n^{q}\log n)
, plus
O(n^{2})
if
\Gamma
needs to be specified.
Finally, when we speak of “computing
\mathrm{Opt}(h)
”, or “solving a semidefinite program”, we mean don’t mean literally exactly, but rather up to an additive precision of any arbitrarily small
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
.
Indeed, the SDP for
\mathrm{SOS}_{2k}(h)
can be computed to additive precision
\exp(-n^{k})
in
n^{O(k)}
time.

Given an instance
h
(and graph
\Gamma
), there are several possible computational tasks concerning
\mathrm{Opt}(h)
(or
\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(h)
):

Exact computation.

Determine
\mathrm{Opt}(h)
. (But this is
\mathsf{QMA}
-hard.)

Upper-bound certification.

Output
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{Opt}(h)\leq\beta
.

Lower-bound certification.

Output
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{Opt}(h)\geq\alpha
.

Naturally, one wants
\beta
as small as possible and
\alpha
as large as possible.
One might also want to upgrade a lower-bound certification algorithm so that it also provides a
witness
to
\mathrm{Opt}(h)\geq\alpha
.
We consider two possibilities:

Implicit lower-bound witnessing.

Output a classical description
9
9

Here it is only fair to allow for a fixed “encoding scheme”

2.7.1
On randomized algorithms and randomized inputs

In this work we will often be interested in

complexity, where the input for the task is
\boldsymbol{h}
drawn from some distribution
\mathcal{H}
on instances (typically
\mathcal{H}=\mathrm{SYK}_{q}
for some
q
).
In this case we should be careful to define the correctness of algorithms.

Except for one case (to be discussed shortly), each certification/witnessing algorithm presented in this paper is deterministic.
Such a deterministic algorithm ALG will have the following properties:
On all inputs
h
, algorithm ALG outputs either a
claim
(such as “
\mathrm{Opt}(h)\leq\sqrt{n}
”) or else outputs “FAIL”.
And furthermore:

(i)

For
all
inputs
h
, if ALG
(h)
is a claim, then that claim is correct.

(ii)

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\boldsymbol{h}\sim\mathcal{H}}[\text{ALG}(\boldsymbol{h})=\text{``FAIL''}]<\epsilon
(for some small
\epsilon
).

We emphasize two things:
On one hand, the user of the ALG gets
100\%
confidence in its claims.
On the other hand, for a given outcome
\boldsymbol{h}=h
, a user
cannot
“boost” the probability of a non-FAIL outcome by repeatedly running the algorithm.
(We also remark that for the task of implicit lower-bound witnessing, there is never a concern about confidence; we assume that from the output description of
\rho
one can efficiently and deterministically compute
\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\rho}[h]
.)

The one exception to this deterministic style of algorithm will be our quantum lower-bound witnessing for
\boldsymbol{h}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{4}(n)
.
In
Section

8
we first show that

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\boldsymbol{h}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{4}(n)}[\mathrm{Opt}(\boldsymbol{h})\geq c_{0}\sqrt{n}]\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(n))

(for some universal constant
c_{0}>0
).
Following that we give an efficient quantum “witnessing” algorithm

that, given any input
h
, applies a sequence of polynomially-many gates to produce a quantum state
\rho
.
One may think of
W

hypothesizing
that
\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\rho}[h]\geq c_{0}\sqrt{n}
.

Now on one hand, there is no obvious
deterministic
way to test this hypothesis, even with a quantum computer.
On the other hand, there
is
an obvious
randomized
certification algorithm
\boldsymbol{V}
that runs efficiently on a quantum computer.
Given
h
, algorithm
\boldsymbol{V}
simply repeatedly prepares

and empirically estimates
\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\rho}[h]
.
By running
\boldsymbol{V}
for
\textnormal{poly}(n)\log(1/\delta)
time, a user can obtain confidence

that the hypothesis
\mathrm{Opt}(h)\geq c_{0}\sqrt{n}
is true (up to any additive
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
, which we can neglect since we aren’t specifying
c_{0}
precisely anyway).
That is, for any user-selected
\delta
, we can obtain a
\textnormal{poly}(n)\log(1/\delta)
-time composite quantum algorithm

that, on input
h
, either outputs “FAIL”, or else outputs a claim of
\mathrm{Opt}(h)\geq c_{0}\sqrt{n}
together with a witnessing quantum state
\rho

It has the following properties:

(i

)

For
all
inputs
h
, if

outputs a claim, then that claim is correct except with probability at most
\delta
(over the internal randomness of

).

(ii
′
)

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\boldsymbol{h}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{4}(n)}[\textbf{ALG}(\boldsymbol{h})=\text{``FAIL''}]<\exp(-\Omega(n))
.

In summary, with very high probability over the random
input

\boldsymbol{h}
, the algorithm produces a

.
Furthermore, for
any
input

We end this section by remarking that for

Thus we arguably have a distributional task, with classical input and output, that is efficiently solvable by a quantum computer, but for which we currently do not know an efficient classical algorithm.

3
Upper bounds and heuristics for random polynomials

In this section we recall (and generalize) the known upper bound on

for
\boldsymbol{h}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{q}(n)

Before this, we recall that analyzing the expected optimum is sufficient for understanding the high-probability optimum.
To be precise, note for
\boldsymbol{p}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{q}(n)
that
\mathrm{Opt}(\boldsymbol{p})
and
\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{p})
are
1/\sqrt{\binom{n}{q}}
-Lipschitz functions of the random coefficients.
Thus standard concentration results for Gaussian measure (see, e.g.,
[
Led01
, Ch. 2]
) imply the following:

Proposition 3.1
.

For
\boldsymbol{p}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{q}(n)
it holds that

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[|\mathrm{Opt}(\boldsymbol{p})-\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathrm{Opt}(\boldsymbol{p})]|\geq\epsilon]\leq 2\exp(-\tbinom{n}{q}\epsilon^{2}/2)

(and the same holds for
\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{p})
).
E.g., for any constant
q\geq 2
there is a constant
C
such that

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[|\mathrm{Opt}(\boldsymbol{p})-\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathrm{Opt}(\boldsymbol{p})]|\geq C/\sqrt{n}]\leq\exp(-n).

3.1
Upper bounds for random polynomials

On the subject of upper bounds for
\mathrm{Opt}(\boldsymbol{p})
when
\boldsymbol{p}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{q}(n)
, the best provable upper bound that is known is
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{p})]\leq\sqrt{\ln 2}\sqrt{n}

One way to prove this
10
10

10

As was pointed out to us by several attendees, including Jerry Li and Cristopher Moore, at the Simons Institute workshop

Since the

In the following

Theorem 3.2
.

Given graph
\Gamma=([n],E)
, suppose that

\boldsymbol{h}(\chi)=\sum_{S}\boldsymbol{J}_{S}\chi^{S}\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)

is self-adjoint,
where the coefficients
(\boldsymbol{J}_{S})_{S\subseteq[n]}

Then provided conditions (i),(ii) below hold, we have

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[|\boldsymbol{h}(1)|\geq\beta]\leq 1/D\quad\implies\quad\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{h})]\leq\beta,

where

from

2.15

The conditions are:

(i)

\displaystyle~\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[|\boldsymbol{h}(1)|\geq\beta]\leq 1/D\text{ holds due to the moment bound }\mathop{\bf E\/}[\boldsymbol{h}(1)^{k}]/\beta^{k}\leq 1/D\text{ for some even $k$;}

(ii)

\displaystyle~\mathop{\bf E\/}[\boldsymbol{J}_{S_{1}}\boldsymbol{J}_{S_{2}}\cdots\boldsymbol{J}_{S_{k}}]\geq 0\ \forall S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\subseteq[n].

Proof.

This follows from

\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{h})]^{k}\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{h})^{k}]=\mathop{\bf E\/}[\|\pi_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{h})\|^{k}]\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}[\operatorname{Tr}(\pi_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{h})^{k})]=D\mathop{\bf E\/}[\operatorname{tr}(\pi_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{h}^{k}))]\\
=D\sum_{S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\subseteq[n]}\mathop{\bf E\/}[\boldsymbol{J}_{S_{1}}\cdots\boldsymbol{J}_{S_{k}}]\operatorname{tr}(\pi_{\Gamma}(\chi^{S_{1}}\cdots\chi^{S_{k}}))\overset{\text{(ii)}}{\leq}D\sum_{S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\subseteq[n]}\mathop{\bf E\/}[\boldsymbol{J}_{S_{1}}\cdots\boldsymbol{J}_{S_{k}}]=D\mathop{\bf E\/}[\boldsymbol{h}(1)^{k}]\overset{\text{(i)}}{\leq}\beta^{k},

∎

3.2
Heuristics for the SYK optimum

Here we describe the heuristic developed in the physics community for predicting
\mathrm{Opt}(\boldsymbol{h})
when
\boldsymbol{h}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{q}(n)
.
We follow the discussion in
[
GJV18
]
; see also
[
FTW19
,
FTW18
,
FTW20
]
for partial formalizations of this heuristic.

Naturally, one can attempt to analyze
\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{h})
, and indeed the entire typical spectrum of
\boldsymbol{h}
, via the (expected) Trace Method.
However, the combinatorics arising when computing
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{h}^{k})]
are rather intricate.
A heuristic one can use is the following.
From
2.13
we see that if
\boldsymbol{S}
and
\boldsymbol{T}
are chosen randomly and independently from the
N\coloneqq\binom{n}{q}
possible monomials, they anticommute with probability

p=p(q,n)=\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[q-|\boldsymbol{S}\cap\boldsymbol{T}|\text{ is odd}]\approx\begin{cases}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[|\boldsymbol{S}\cap\boldsymbol{T}|=1]\approx q^{2}/n&\text{for $q=o(\sqrt{n})$ even,}\\
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[|\boldsymbol{S}\cap\boldsymbol{T}|=0]\approx 1-q^{2}/n&\text{for $q=o(\sqrt{n})$ odd,}\\
\end{cases}.

(Of course, one can easily make this asymptotic approximation more precise if desired.)
Given this, the key ansatz is to let
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\sim\mathfrak{G}(N,p)
denote an Erdős–Rényi random graph, and to model the
N
monomials in
\mathfrak{C}(K_{n})
by the
N
indeterminates of
\mathfrak{C}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
.
That is, for

\boldsymbol{\ell}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{a}_{j}\chi_{j}\in\mathfrak{C}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}),\qquad\boldsymbol{a}_{j}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,1),

the heuristic is

\mathop{\bf E\/}[\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{h}^{k})]\approx\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\boldsymbol{\Gamma},\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\text{'s}}[\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\ell}^{k})].

(10)

Estimating the right side of
Equation

10
is more computationally tractable for constant
k
, and the resulting moment estimates match those of the so-called
\mu
-Gaussian
(or
\mu
-semicircular
) distribution, with orthogonal polynomials the
\mu
-Hermite polynomials
(see, e.g.,
[
ISV87
]
).
11
11

11

These are usually called
q
-Hermite polynomials
in the combinatorics literature, according to the usual notion of
q
-deformity.
Unfortunately we have a notational clash with the SYK degree “
q
”.
In the physics literature this is resolved by writing “
Q
-Hermite”, but we follow the “
\mu
-Hermite” terminology of Speicher
[
Spe92
]
instead.

Here

\mu=\mu(q,n)\coloneqq 1-2p=\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\boldsymbol{S},\boldsymbol{T}\sim\binom{[n]}{q}}[(-1)^{q-|\boldsymbol{S}\cap\boldsymbol{T}|}]\in[-1,+1],

and one easily check that for
q=O(\sqrt{n})
, up to lower-order terms we have

\mu\approx(-1)^{q}\exp(-2r),\qquad r\coloneqq q^{2}/n.

Thus the heuristic prediction is that the spectral density of
\boldsymbol{h}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{q}(n)
will resemble that of the
\mu
-Gaussian density, call it
\nu_{\mu}
.
This density
\nu_{\mu}
has an explicit analytic formula (see, e.g.,
[
BKS97
]
), but rather than reproducing it, we will merely note the following facts:

•

For
\mu\to 1
(corresponding to
q=o(\sqrt{n})

\nu_{\mu}
tends to the standard Gaussian.

•

For
\mu\in(-1,1)

\nu_{\mu}
is the interval
[-\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-\mu}},+\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-\mu}}]
.

•

For
\mu=0
(equivalently,
q=\omega(\sqrt{n})
),
\nu_{\mu}
is the semicircle density.

•

For
\mu\to-1
(corresponding to
q=o(\sqrt{n})
odd), the density
\nu_{\mu}
tends to Rademacher.

Summarizing these heuristics, the belief is that with high probability,

\text{for $\boldsymbol{h}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{q}(n)$,}\quad\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{h})\sim\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-(-1)^{q}\exp(-2q^{2}/n)}}\sim\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{q}\sqrt{n}&\text{for $q=o(\sqrt{n})$ even,}\\
\sqrt{2}&\text{for $q=o(\sqrt{n})$ odd.}\end{cases}

(11)

4
Upper bound certification with the Lovász Theta Function

We first recall the independence number of a graph:

Notation 4.1
.

If
\Gamma=([n],E)
is a graph,
\alpha(\Gamma)
denotes the cardinality of its largest independent set; i.e., the largest
S\subseteq[n]
such that no
\{j,k\}\in E
has both
j,k\in S
.

In the context of
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
, an independent set
S
is one for which the indeterminates
(\chi_{j}:j\in S)
pairwise commute.
Lovász
[
Lov79
]
introduced the following upper bound on
\alpha(\Gamma)
, which is efficiently computable using semidefinite programming:

Notation 4.2
.

The
Lovász Theta Function
assigns to each graph
\Gamma=([n],E)
the number

\vartheta(\Gamma)=\max_{\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}\{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho{\mathbbm{J}}):\rho\geq 0,\ \operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=1,\ \rho_{jk}=0\ \forall\{j,k\}\in E\},

(12)

where
{\mathbbm{J}}
denotes the all-
1
’s matrix.

Fact 4.3
.

There is also an equivalent dual SDP definition,

\vartheta(G)=\min\{\kappa:\exists\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\ \Sigma_{jj}=1\ \forall j\in[n],\ \Sigma_{jk}=0\ \forall\{j,k\}\not\in E,\ \kappa\Sigma\geq{\mathbbm{J}}\}.

(13)

Remark 4.4
.

The “weak duality” bound
\eqref{eqn:primal}\leq\eqref{eqn:dual}
, which is all we will require, is evident from

\operatorname{Tr}(\rho{\mathbbm{J}})\leq\kappa\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Sigma)=\kappa\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=\kappa.

Fact 4.5
.

\alpha(\Gamma)\leq\vartheta(\Gamma)
always holds: if
S
is an independent set, and
\ket{S}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
denotes its indicator, then
\rho=\frac{1}{|S|}\ket{S}\!\bra{S}
achieves
|S|
in
Equation

12
.

Given a graph
\Gamma
, we will be interested in the maximum that
\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(\ell)
can possibly be among self-adjoint linear polynomials
\ell\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
(subject to a scaling constraint on their coefficients).
We therefore introduce the following:

Definition 4.6
.

For a graph
\Gamma=([n],E)
, we define

\Psi(\Gamma)=\max\left\{\mathrm{Opt}_{\pm}(\ell)^{2}=\mathrm{Opt}(\ell^{*}\ell)=\mathrm{Opt}(\ell^{2}):\ell=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\chi_{j}\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma),\ \vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ |\vec{a}|^{2}=1\right\}.

The below two propositions now establish the following sandwiching bound:

\alpha(\Gamma)\leq\Psi(\Gamma)\leq\vartheta(\Gamma).

Proposition 4.7
.

For any graph
\Gamma=([n],E)
we have
\Psi(\Gamma)\geq\alpha(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

If
S
is a maximum independent set in
\Gamma
, then the matrices
(\pi_{\Gamma}(\chi_{j}):j\in S)
pairwise commute and hence can be simultaneously diagonalized.
Take any common eigenvector; it will have associated eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
for
j\in S
.
Now
\ell=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|S|}}\sum_{j\in S}\lambda_{j}\chi_{j}
has an eigenvalue of
\sqrt{|S|}=\sqrt{\alpha(\Gamma)}
under
\pi_{\Gamma}
.
∎

Proposition 4.8
.

For any graph
\Gamma=([n],E)
we have
\Psi(\Gamma)\leq\vartheta(\Gamma)
, and in fact this has a degree-
2
SOS proof.
That is, if
\ell=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\chi_{j}\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
with
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
|\vec{a}|=1
, then
\mathrm{SOS}_{2}(\ell^{*}\ell)\leq\vartheta(\Gamma)
.
Moreoever, this holds even without assuming the commutation relations
\chi_{j}\chi_{k}=\chi_{k}\chi_{j}
for
\{j,k\}\not\in E
; i.e., it only uses the anticommutation relations
\chi_{j}\chi_{k}=-\chi_{k}\chi_{j}
for
\{j,k\}\in E
.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}
be any degree-
2
pseudoexpectation for
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
, which we may also think of as a PSD matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
Now given
\ell=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\chi_{j}
, with
|\vec{a}|=1
, define the PSD matrix
\rho=\ket{a}\!\bra{a}\circ\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}
, where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard (entry-wise) product; i.e.,

\rho_{jk}=a_{j}a_{k}\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\chi_{j}\chi_{k}].

Then
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=|\vec{a}|^{2}=1
and
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho{\mathbbm{J}})=\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\ell^{*}\ell]
.
Furthermore, observe that for
\{j,k\}\in E
we have
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\chi_{j}\chi_{k}]^{*}=\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\chi_{k}^{*}\chi_{j}^{*}]=-\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\chi_{j}\chi_{k}]
; thus
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\chi_{j}\chi_{k}]
and
\rho_{jk}
are purely imaginary.
If we now let
\hat{\rho}
be the real part of
\rho
, we still have all of the following properties —

\hat{\rho}\geq 0,\quad\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho})=1,\quad\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}J)=\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\ell^{*}\ell]

— and now
\hat{\rho}
also has
\hat{\rho}_{jk}=0
for
\{j,k\}\in E
.
This establishes
\widetilde{\mathop{\bf E\/}}[\ell^{*}\ell]\leq\vartheta(\Gamma)
, as required.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Regarding tightness of the bounds
\alpha(\Gamma)\leq\Psi(\Gamma)\leq\vartheta(\Gamma)
, the

4.1

The Lovász Theta upper bound from

Suppose

For

As far as we are aware, this problem has not been previously studied.
The related quantity

4.2
On bounding
\vartheta(K\!G_{\mathrm{even}}^{(n,q)})

Notation 4.19
.

Recall the
Johnson association scheme

J(n,q)
has
\binom{n}{q}
as its points, with
S,T
belonging to the
d
th relation,
d=0\dots q
, iff
\textnormal{dist}(S,T)\coloneqq q-|S\cap T|=d
.
We write

for the adjacency matrix of the
d
th relation, with the superscript dropped when it is understood from context.
Note that
A_{0}=\mathbb{1}
and
\sum_{d=0}^{q}A_{d}={\mathbbm{J}}
.
For simplicity we assume
n>2q
, so that
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
represent connected graphs.

Definition 4.20
.

Given
n
,
q
, and a set of
nonedge distances

\mathcal{D}\subseteq\{1,\dots,q\}
, we write
K\!G_{\mathcal{D}}=K\!G^{(n,q)}_{\mathcal{D}}
for the graph with vertex set
\binom{n}{q}
and adjacency matrix
\sum_{d\not\in\mathcal{D}}A_{d}
.

Example 4.21
.

The Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem
[
EKR61
]
concerns the case
\mathcal{D}=\{1,2,\dots,q-k\}
, for
k\in\mathbbm{N}^{+}
; we write the associated graph as
K\!G_{\mathrm{EKR}_{k}}
.
The theorem says that for sufficiently large

it holds that
\alpha(K\!G_{\mathrm{EKR}_{k}})=\binom{n-k}{q-k}
, with the maximum independent sets being of the form
\{S\subseteq[n]:S\supseteq T\}
for some
|T|=k
.

Example 4.22
.

We are particularly interested in the case when
\mathcal{D}=\{2,4,6,\dots\}
is the set of even integers; this yields the case of
K\!G_{\mathrm{even}}=K\!G_{\mathrm{even}}^{(n,q)}
discussed in
Theorem

4.12
.

Deza, Erdős, and Frankl
[
DEF78
]
more generally showed the following:

Theorem 4.23
.

(
[
DEF78
]
.)
If the elements of
\mathcal{D}
are
d_{1}>d_{2}>\cdots>d_{r}
, then
\alpha(K\!G_{\mathcal{D}})\leq\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{n-q+d_{j}}{d_{j}}
for sufficiently large

.

Indeed, Deza–Erdős–Frankl furthermore showed that unless the divisibility criterion
(d_{1}-d_{2})\mid(d_{2}-d_{3})\mid\cdots\mid(d_{r-1}-d_{r})\mid d_{r}
is satisfied, one has the asymptotically stronger bound
\alpha(K\!G_{\mathcal{D}})\leq O_{q}(n^{r-1})
.

Recall that for any graph at all we have
\alpha(G)\leq\vartheta(G)
.
In the case of graphs coming from association schemes, the Lovász Theta Function reduces to the “linear programming bound” of Delsarte
[
Del73
]
.
The next three facts review this bound in the context of the Johnson scheme.

Proposition 4.24
.

(
[
Del73
, p. 48]
.)
The matrices
A_{0},\dots,A_{q}
are simultaneously diagonalizable, with eigenvalues given by the dual Hahn polynomials:

\mathrm{spec}(A_{d})=\{\tilde{H}_{d}(z):z=0\dots q\},\quad\tilde{H}_{d}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}(-1)^{d-j}\binom{q-j}{d-j}\binom{q-z}{j}\binom{n-q+j-z}{j}.

In particular, the all-
1
’s vector is an eigenvector of
A_{d}
with eigenvalue
\tilde{H}_{d}(0)=\binom{n-q}{d}\binom{q}{d}
.
This is an eigenvalue of multiplicity
1
for all
d>0
.

Lemma 4.25
.

Fix any
K\!G=K\!G^{(n,q)}_{\mathcal{D}}
.
Then in the characterization of
\vartheta(G)
given in
Equation

13
, it is equivalent to minimize only over
\Sigma
such that
\Sigma_{ST}
depends only on
\textnormal{dist}(S,T)
.
That is,

\vartheta(K\!G)=\min\{\kappa:\exists(c_{e})_{e\not\in\mathcal{D}},\ \kappa(\mathbb{1}+\mathop{{\textstyle\sum}}_{e\not\in\mathcal{D}}c_{e}A_{e})\geq{\mathbbm{J}}\}.

Proof.

Given any feasible
\Sigma
for
Equation

13
achieving
\kappa
, we may replace it by
\hat{\rho}=\mathop{\textnormal{avg}}_{\pi\in S_{n}}\{P(\pi)^{\textsf{T}}\Sigma P(\pi)\}
, where
P(\pi)
is the permutation matrix on
\binom{n}{q}
induced by
\pi
.
It is easy to see that
\hat{\rho}
continues to be feasible and achieve
\kappa
, while also having
\hat{\rho}_{ST}
depend only on
\textnormal{dist}(S,T)
.
∎

Corollary 4.26
.

Fix any
K\!G=K\!G^{(n,q)}_{\mathcal{D}}
.
Then we have the following LP characterization:

\vartheta(K\!G)=\min_{(c_{e})_{e\not\in\mathcal{D}}}\left\{\binom{n}{q}\Bigm/p(0):p(1),\dots,p(q)\geq 0\text{ where }p(z)=\sum_{e\not\in\mathcal{D}}c_{e}\tilde{H}_{e}(z)\right\}.

This follows directly from

4.24
.
∎

Proof of

\displaystyle p(0)

Lower degree Sum-of-Squares: certification at degree
6
and fooling at degree

We have shown that, with high probability, degree-
8
sum-of-squares certifies an upper bound to the largest eigenvalue of

for
\mathrm{SYK}_{4}
which is within a constant factor of
\sqrt{n}
.
In this section we consider lower degree sum-of-squares.

We show that also a similar statement holds for degree-

sum-of-squares: with high probability, it also certifies an upper bound to the largest eigenvalue of

for
\mathrm{SYK}_{4}
which is within a constant factor of

Let us explain the reason for the interest in this “fragment”. The interest is

6.1

We call this fragment

Let

To verify that it is a degree-

.
Hence, it suffices to verify that the inequality holds when

In case that

Define a matrix

7
Gaussian states

This section will focus exclusively on
\mathfrak{C}(K_{n})
, where all indeterminates pairwise anticommute.
We will discuss
Gaussian states

[
TD02
,
Bra05
]
, a reasonably broad class of states
\rho
(including all Slater determinant states) for which: (i) there is an efficient representation of
\rho
; (ii) given this representation, and some polynomial

\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\rho}[h]

In particular, these states may be used for classical (implicit) lower-bound witnessing algorithms, as described in

A succinct characterization of Gaussian states is that they are precisely those states that are optimizers for homogeneous degree-
2
Hamiltonians
[

]

To define them more explicitly, first suppose

are self-adjoint, commuting involutions (

Then for any
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in\{\pm 1\}
, the elements

are positive and commuting, and hence

\rho=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1+\lambda_{j}\psi_{j})

is a state (note that the order of the factors is irrelevant).
Indeed, this was already pointed out in
2.20
, just with different letters.
Furthermore, the same is true if we generalize by allowing
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in[-1,1]
.
Additionally, if
\Gamma=K_{n}
with

7.2
Rounding
\mathrm{SDP}_{4,\mathrm{Gauss}}(h)

Let us now describe the SDP relaxation for degree-
4
Hamiltonians introduced in
[
BGKT19
]
.

Notation 7.16
.

Given
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
, let
\mathrm{Vec}(A)\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}
denote the vector formed by stacking the columns of
A
, in order.
Conversely, let
\mathrm{Mat}:\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
denote the inverse operation.
We remark that

\operatorname{Tr}_{1}(\mathrm{Vec}(A)\mathrm{Vec}(B)^{\textsf{T}})=A^{\textsf{T}}B,\qquad\operatorname{Tr}_{2}(\mathrm{Vec}(A)\mathrm{Vec}(B)^{\textsf{T}})=AB^{\textsf{T}},

where
\operatorname{Tr}_{j}
denotes tracing out the
j
th tensor component of
\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}
.

Definition 7.17
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be antisymmetric with
\|\Sigma\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
.
Define
R=\mathrm{Vec}(\Sigma)\mathrm{Vec}(\Sigma)^{\textsf{T}}
.
Note that we have the following properties:

1.

R\geq 0
;

2.

\operatorname{Tr}_{1}(R)\leq\mathbb{1}
(since
\operatorname{Tr}_{1}(R)=\Sigma^{\textsf{T}}\Sigma
and
\|\Sigma\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
);

3.

\operatorname{Tr}_{2}(R)\leq\mathbb{1}
(similarly, since
\operatorname{Tr}_{2}(R)=\Sigma\Sigma^{\textsf{T}}
);

4.

\braket{j_{1}k_{1}|R|j_{2}k_{2}}
changes sign if
j_{1},k_{1}
are interchanged, or if
j_{2},k_{2}
are interchanged (since
\Sigma
is antisymmetric).

Furthermore, if
h=\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{4}}a_{S}\chi^{S}\in\mathfrak{C}(K_{n})
(with
n
even), and we introduce the matrix

H=-\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{4}}a_{S}(\ket{S_{1}S_{2}}\!\bra{S_{3}S_{4}}-\ket{S_{1}S_{3}}\!\bra{S_{2}S_{4}}+\ket{S_{1}S_{4}}\!\bra{S_{2}S_{3}}),

then
\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\rho_{\Sigma}}[h]=\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{\Sigma}h)=\operatorname{Tr}(RH)
.
We thereby define

\mathrm{SDP}_{4,\mathrm{Gauss}}(h)=\max\{\operatorname{Tr}(RH):R\text{ satisfying properties 1--4 above}\},

which is a semidefinite programming relaxation of
\mathrm{Opt}_{\mathrm{Gauss}}(p)
.

Given this SDP,
[
BGKT19
]
observe that the efficient randomized rounding algorithm of So
[
So09
]
establishes
\mathrm{Opt}_{\mathrm{Gauss}}(p)\geq\frac{1}{O(\log n)}\mathrm{SDP}_{4,\mathrm{Gauss}}(p)
.
So’s work is a kind of “noncommutative analogue” of the
\frac{1}{O(\log n)}
-factor approximation algorithm for quadratic programming due to Nestrov
[
Nes98
]
, Nemirosvsky–Roos–Terlaky
[
NRT99
]
, and Megretski
[
Meg01
]
.
In the “commutative case”, said algorithm was generalized by Charikar and Wirth
[

]
to show that there is an
\frac{1}{O(\log(1/\epsilon))}
-factor approximation for Max-Cut whenever the optimum is within a factor of
\epsilon
of the “naive upper bound” (see

).
Here we are able to generalize the Charikar–Wirth result to our noncommutative setting:

Theorem 7.18
.

There is an efficient randomized algorithm that, given

with real
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{[n]}{4}}
satisfying

and

\mathrm{SDP}_{4,\mathrm{Gauss}}(p)=\epsilon n,

with high probability outputs the covariance matrix
\Sigma
of a Gaussian state satisfying

{\mathop{\bf E\/}}_{\rho_{\Sigma}}[h]=\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{\Sigma}h)\geq\frac{\epsilon}{O(\log(1/\epsilon))}n.

Remark 7.19
.

From

\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}
always.
It’s also trivial to show

factor is never worse than
O(\log n)
, recovering

.
In fact, taking care in our proof shows that this approximation factor need not be worse than
(1+o(1))\sqrt{2\ln n}
.

Remark 7.20
.

It is straightforward to observe that our algorithm can be derandomized in
n^{O(\log(1/\epsilon))}
time, which is polynomial in the case

of most interest.
The algorithm employs
n^{2}
Rademacher random variables, and the analysis only needs
4
-wise uniformity for
Equation

25
(see e.g.
[
AH11
, Thm. 4.3]
),
2
-wise uniformity for
Equation

28
, and
(p+2)
-wise uniformity for

, with

.
Thus the derandomization follows from
[
ABI86
]
.

Proof.

By standard semidefinite programming methods
[
GLS81
]
, one can efficiently find a feasible solution
R
achieving
\mathrm{SDP}_{4,\mathrm{Gauss}}(p)=\epsilon n
.
16
16

16

See the technicalities discussed in
Section

2.7
.

We now describe a randomized rounding algorithm that finds the required
\Sigma
with constant probability.
We may boost the success probability arbitrarily close to
1
by repeating the algorithm constantly many times and choosing the best result achieved.

Since
R\geq 0
we may write a Cholesky factorization
R=LL^{\textsf{T}}
which, by property 4 in
Definition

7.17
will satisfy

\bra{k,j}L=-\bra{j,k}L\quad\forall j,k\in[n].

(24)

Now define

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sigma\mathrm{Mat}(L\vec{\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{jk}\sigma A^{(jk)},\quad A^{(jk)}\coloneqq\mathrm{Mat}(L\ket{jk}),

where
\sigma
is a positive constant to be chosen later, and
\vec{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a vector of
n^{2}
independent Rademacher random variables.
Note that each
A^{(jk)}
is antisymmetric, by
Equation

24
; hence
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is always antisymmetric and thus defines a Gaussian pseudostate.
Further,

\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathrm{Vec}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\mathrm{Vec}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{\textsf{T}}]=\sigma^{2}L\mathop{\bf E\/}[\ket{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\!\bra{{\boldsymbol{x}}}]L^{\textsf{T}}=\sigma^{2}R,

from which we see that

\mathop{\bf E\/}[\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}h)]=\sigma^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(RH)=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{SDP}_{4,\mathrm{Gauss}}(h)=\sigma^{2}\cdot\epsilon n.

Since
\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}h)
is a quadratic polynomial in Rademachers, it follows (e.g.
[
O’D14
, Thm. 9.24]
) that

\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}h)\geq\sigma^{2}\cdot\epsilon n\text{ with probability at least }e^{-4}/4>.004.

(25)

(Remark: So
[
So09
]
uses a more complicated construction for
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
that ensures
\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}h)=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{SDP}_{4,\mathrm{Gauss}}(h)
holds with certainty.
He then shows
\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
with high probability with the choice
c\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2e\ln n}}
.
Our methods below would also show this even for
c\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\ln n}}
.)

We now need to study the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
.
It will be mildly more convenient to study the eigenvalues of

\boldsymbol{G}\coloneqq\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{jk}B^{(jk)},\quad\text{where }B^{(jk)}\coloneqq\sigma\mathrm{i}A^{(jk)},

since the matrices
B^{(jk)}
are self-adjoint.
Note that

\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}{A^{(jk)}}^{\textsf{T}}A^{(jk)}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\operatorname{Tr}_{1}(L\ket{jk}\!\bra{jk}L^{\textsf{T}})=\operatorname{Tr}_{1}(R)\leq\mathbb{1}

(and similarly
\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}A^{(jk)}{A^{(jk)}}^{\textsf{T}}\leq\mathbb{1}
), and this is equivalent to

\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}(B^{(jk)})^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\mathbb{1}.

(26)

At this point one can begin to analyze
\boldsymbol{G}
using standard theory for sums of random self-adjoint matrices; e.g.,
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{G})\geq t]\leq ne^{-t^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}
holds
[
Tro12
]
.

Say
\boldsymbol{G}
has (real) eigenvalues
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}
and associated orthonormal eigenvectors
\vec{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{1},\dots,\vec{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{n}
.
Our algorithm may form

\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}=\sum\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}\ket{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}}\!\bra{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}}:|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}|\leq 1\},

which is also self-adjoint, and then
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\mathrm{i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}
.
This
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
is the covariance matrix of a Gaussian state
\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
, and by
7.6
we have

\textbf{err}\coloneqq\left\lvert\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}h)-\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}h)\right\rvert=\left\lvert\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{4}}a_{S}(e_{1}(S)-e_{2}(S)+e_{3}(S))\right\rvert,

(27)

where

\boldsymbol{e}_{1}(S)=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{S_{1}S_{2}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{S_{3}S_{4}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{S_{1}S_{2}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{S_{3}S_{4}}

and
\boldsymbol{e}_{2}(S),\boldsymbol{e}_{3}(S)
are defined similarly (with subscripts
S_{1}S_{3},S_{2}S_{4}
and
S_{1}S_{4},S_{2}S_{3}
, respectively).
By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\textbf{err}^{2}\leq\left\lparen\sum_{S}a_{S}^{2}\right\rparen\left\lparen\sum_{S}(\boldsymbol{e}_{1}(S)-\boldsymbol{e}_{2}(S)+\boldsymbol{e}_{3}(S))^{2}\right\rparen\leq 3\sum_{S}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}(S)^{2}+3\sum_{S}\boldsymbol{e}_{2}(S)^{2}+3\sum_{S}\boldsymbol{e}_{3}(S)^{2}.

We will bound the first sum on the right, with the other two being bounded similarly.
Since

(rs-\widehat{r}\widehat{s})^{2}=(r(s-\widehat{s})+\widehat{s}(r-\widehat{r}))^{2}\leq 2r^{2}(s-\widehat{s})^{2}+2\widehat{s}^{2}(r-\widehat{r})^{2},

we get

\sum_{S}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}(S)^{2}\leq 2\sum_{S}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{S_{1}S_{2}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{S_{3}S_{4}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{S_{3}S_{4}})^{2}+2\sum_{S}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{S_{3}S_{4}}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{S_{1}S_{2}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{S_{1}S_{2}})^{2}.

Here, the first expression on the right is at most

2\left\lparen\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}_{jk}\right\rparen\left\lparen\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{jk}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{jk})^{2}\right\rparen=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{2}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\|_{2}^{2},

where we used the antisymmetry of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
.
We can use similar considerations to bound the second expression, and will also use the evident fact that
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{2}
.
Putting everything together, we conclude

\textbf{err}^{2}\leq\tfrac{9}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|^{2}_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\|^{2}_{2}=\tfrac{9}{2}\|\boldsymbol{G}\|^{2}_{2}\|\boldsymbol{G}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}\|^{2}_{2}.

One easily calculates that
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\|\boldsymbol{G}\|^{2}_{2}]=\operatorname{Tr}(\sum_{jk}(B^{(jk)})^{2})\leq\sigma^{2}n
(by
Equation

26
), and therefore by Markov we have

\textbf{err}^{2}\leq C\sigma^{2}n\cdot\|\boldsymbol{G}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}\|^{2}_{2}\text{ except with probability at most }.001,

(28)

where
C
is a universal constant.

To analyze this final expression we appeal to Khintchine’s inequality with operator coefficients (originally due to Lust-Piquard
[
LP86
]
, improved in
[
LPP91
]
), which tells us that for all
p\geq 1
,

\mathop{\bf E\/}[\|\boldsymbol{G}\|^{p}_{p}]\leq(C^{\prime}\sqrt{p})^{p}\left\lVert\sqrt{\sum_{jk}(B^{(jk)})^{2}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\leq(C^{\prime}\sqrt{p}\sigma)^{p}n,

where
C^{\prime}
is another universal constant, and we used
Equation

26
again.
Note that

\|\boldsymbol{G}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}\|^{2}_{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}^{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}[|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}|\geq 1]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}^{2}\cdot|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}|^{p}=\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{p+2}^{p+2},

regardless of
p
.
We choose
p
so that
\sqrt{p+2}=(eC^{\prime}\sigma)^{-1}
(so we need to also choose
\sigma\leq(\sqrt{3}eC^{\prime})^{-1}
so that
p\geq 1
).
With this choice, we get

\mathop{\bf E\/}[\|\boldsymbol{G}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}\|^{2}_{2}]\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}[\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{p+2}^{p+2}]\leq\exp\left\lparen-\frac{1}{C^{\prime\prime}\sigma^{2}}\right\rparen n.

Employing Markov again, and combining with
Equation

28
, we get

\textbf{err}\leq C_{1}\sigma\exp\left\lparen-\frac{1}{C_{2}\sigma^{2}}\right\rparen n\text{ except with probability at most }.002.

(29)

Finally, from
Equations

25
and
27
we have shown that the algorithm constructs Gaussian state
\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
satisfying

\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}h)\geq\left\lparen\sigma^{2}\cdot\epsilon-C_{1}\sigma\exp\left\lparen-\frac{1}{C_{2}\sigma^{2}}\right\rparen\right\rparen n

with probability at least
.002
.

\|P^{\prime}_{0}(\boldsymbol{G})\|_{n}\leq\frac{C_{10}}{n}\left\lparen\sqrt{n}\|A\|_{\{1,2,3\}}+n\|A\|_{\{1,2\},\{3\}}+n\|A\|_{\{1,3\},\{2\}}+n\|A\|_{\{2,3\},\{1\}}+n^{3/2}\|A\|_{\{1\},\{2\},\{3\}}\right\rparen,

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\{1,2,3\}}=n,\qquad\|A\|_{\{1,2\},\{3\}}=\|A\|_{\{2,3\},\{1\}}=\|\mathbb{1}_{n\times n}\otimes\underbrace{(1,\dots,1)}_{\text{$n$}}\|_{\text{op}}=\sqrt{n},

Theorem 8.8

For a universal constant

To prove

Proposition 8.10
.

Theorem

8.8
holds with
\boldsymbol{h}\sim\mathrm{SYK}^{\textnormal{2col}}_{4}(\frac{3}{4}n,\frac{1}{4}n)
in place of
\boldsymbol{h}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{4}(n)
.

Proof.

The input
h
to the algorithm resides in an algebra with
\frac{3}{4}n+\frac{1}{4}n
anticommuting indeterminates, which we will simply call
\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{n}
(rather than
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{\frac{3}{4}n},\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{\frac{1}{4}n}
).
These are represented by the
\gamma
-matrices from
Equation

6
, over an
n/2
-qubit Hilbert space.

Now the target variational state
\rho_{\theta}
defined in
Equation

32
(with
\theta
chosen as in
Equation

39
) in fact resides in
\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma)
for
\Gamma=K_{n+\frac{1}{4}n}
, since it involves the additional indeterminates
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n/4}
.
To accommodate this the algorithm will actually work in the enlarged Hilbert space with
n/8
additional qubits.
But since the input
h
does not depend on the
\sigma_{j}
’s, it’s easy to see that if the algorithm manages to construct the quantum state
\rho_{\theta}
, it may as its final step simply discard (trace out) its last
n/8
qubits, leaving a quantum state
\rho
that has
\operatorname{tr}(\rho h)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{\theta}h)
.

Thus we only need to show the algorithm can construct
\rho_{\theta}
.
Indeed, by slightly adjusting the constant
c_{0}
in the statement of
Theorem

8.8
, it suffices for it to construct
\rho_{\theta}
up to sufficiently small
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
error in trace distance.
The first step of the algorithm is to construct the Gaussian state
\rho_{0}
.
This may be done very efficiently; this state is maximally mixed on the first
3n/8
qubits, and on the remaining
n/4
qubits it is a stabilizer state
[
Got97
]
with a simple description.
(Indeed, had the last
n/2
indeterminates been ordered as
\chi_{3n/4},\sigma_{1},\chi_{3n/4+1},\sigma_{2},\dots
, then
\rho_{0}
would simply be
2^{n/8}\ket{00\cdots 0}\!\bra{00\cdots 0}
on these qubits, as in
7.2
.)

It remains for the algorithm to rotate this state by
\exp(-\theta\zeta)
(up to polynomially small error).
This rotation can be accomplished efficiently: it is the same as Hamiltonian evolution under the Hamiltonian
-\mathrm{i}\zeta
for time
\theta
.
This Hamiltonian evolution can be done in polynomial time to any desired inverse polynomial error, for example by Trotter–Suzuki evolution, since the “Hamiltonian”
-i\zeta
is the sum of polynomially many terms (namely, the monomials in the Majorana operators), and unitary evolution under a given term can be done efficiently as each term is a product of Pauli operators.
More efficient algorithms exist which can provide a polynomial speedup in
n
and exponential speedup in error
[
BBN19
]
.
∎

It now remains to move from the
2

\mathrm{SYK}

For this, let us partition
\binom{[n]}{4}=A_{\text{in}}\sqcup A_{\text{out}}
, where

A_{\text{in}}=\{\{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}\}:j_{1}<j_{2}<j_{3}\leq\tfrac{3}{4}n<j_{4}\},\quad A_{\text{out}}=\binom{[n]}{4}\setminus A_{\text{in}},

and let us also write any homogeneous degree-
4
polynomial

as

, where

(respectively,

) contains the monomials of

corresponding to indices

(respectively,
A_{\text{out}}
).
It is then easy to see that for
\boldsymbol{h}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{4}(n)
it holds that
\boldsymbol{h}_{\text{in}}
is distributed as
\mathrm{SYK}^{\textnormal{2col}}_{4}(\frac{3}{4}n,\frac{1}{4}n)
up to a scaling factor; specifically, we have

c\cdot\boldsymbol{h}_{\text{in}}\sim\mathrm{SYK}^{\textnormal{2col}}_{4}(\tfrac{3}{4}n,\tfrac{1}{4}n)\quad\text{for }c\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{\binom{n}{4}}{\binom{3n/4}{3}(n/4)}}\leq\frac{8}{3\sqrt{3}}.

(44)

We may now establish
Theorem

.

Theorem

8.8
.

Let
W
be the quantum algorithm that, on input

homogeneous of degree
4
, applies the algorithm from

8.10
to

, where

is as in
Equation

.
Suppose now that

\boldsymbol{h}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{4}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[n]\\
|S|=4\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{J}_{S}\chi^{S}\sim\mathrm{SYK}_{4}(n).

Then as discussed,

is distributed as
\mathrm{SYK}^{\textnormal{2col}}_{4}(\tfrac{3}{4}n,\tfrac{1}{4}n)

’s algorithm satisfies

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\boldsymbol{J}_{S}:S\in A_{\text{in}}}[\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\rho}\boldsymbol{h}_{\text{in}})\geq\tfrac{c^{\prime}_{0}}{c}\sqrt{n}]\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(n))

(45)

(for some constant

On the other hand, for

Appendix A
Derivatives of
\mathrm{Opt}(\ell)
for
\ell
supported on an independent set

In
Section

4
, we have shown the bound

\alpha(\Gamma)\leq\Psi(\Gamma)\leq\vartheta(\Gamma)

for the
\Psi
function.

Here we further consider the question of whether there is some graph
\Gamma
such that

\Psi(\Gamma)>\alpha(\Gamma)
. As partial evidence that these two functions might coincide, we prove the following.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
S
be a maximal independent set in
\Gamma
. Regard

\mathrm{Opt}(\sum_{j}a_{j}\chi_{j})
as a function of the vector
\vec{a}
on the sphere
|\vec{a}|=1
.
Let
\vec{a}_{0}
be the vector with
(\vec{a}_{0})_{j}=0
for
j\not\in S
and

(\vec{a}_{0})_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|S|}}\lambda_{j}
for
j\in S
, where the signs
\lambda_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}

are chosen so that some common eigenvector of the
\chi_{j}

has associated eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
. Hence,
\ell_{0}=\sum_{j}(\vec{a}_{0})_{j}\chi_{j}
is the
\ell
considered in
Proposition

4.7
.

Then, the first derivative of this function with respect to
\vec{a}
vanishes at
\vec{a}=\vec{a}_{0}
, and the Hessian matrix (i.e., matrix of second derivatives w.r.t. some coordinates on the sphere
|J|=1
) is negative semidefinite.

Remark: we say that the matrix is negative semidefinite rather than that we are at a local optimum as the matrix may have a zero eigenvalue. Indeed, consider the case of two operators,
X,Z
which anticommute. Then
\ell=\cos(\theta)X+\sin(\theta)Z
has largest eigenvalue equal to
1
for all
\theta
.

Proof.

A convenient set of coordinates on the sphere in a neighborhood of a given
\vec{a}=\vec{a}_{0}
is to consider vectors
{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}
normal to
\vec{a}_{0}
and let

\vec{a}=(\vec{a}_{0}+{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a})/|\vec{a}_{0}+{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}|
.
Let

E=\mathrm{Opt}(\sum_{\alpha}((\vec{a}_{0})_{j}+{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}_{j})O_{j}).

Note that the argument of the optimum in
E
is not normalized to have
\ell_{2}
norm equal to
1
.
We have

\mathrm{Opt}\Bigl(\frac{\vec{a}_{0}+{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}}{|\vec{a}_{0}+{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}|}\Bigr)=\frac{E}{|\vec{a}_{0}+{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}|}.

The vanishing of the first derivative is immediate: operators
\chi_{k}
for
k\not\in S
anticommute with at least one operator
\chi_{j}
for some
j\in S
and so the expectation value of
\chi_{k}
vanishes in any eigenstate of
\ell_{0}
of maximal eigenvalue.

After some calculus we find to second order, for
{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}
normal to
\vec{a}_{0}
, that

\frac{E}{|\vec{a}_{0}+{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}|}=\sqrt{|S|}+\sum_{j,k}M_{j,k}{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}_{j}{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}_{k}-\sum_{j,k}\frac{E}{2}\delta_{j,k}{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}_{j}{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}_{k}

with

M_{j,k}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{j}\partial_{j}E,

where we write
\partial_{j}
for brevity for
\partial_{{\mathrm{d}}\vec{a}_{j}}
.

So, we must show that the largest eigenvalue of
M
in the given subspace (i.e., for vectors normal to
\vec{a}_{0}
) is bounded by
E/2=|\sqrt{S}|/2
.

We have, for
j,k\not\in S
that

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}E=\langle\phi,\chi_{j}(E-\ell_{0})^{-1}\chi_{k}\phi\rangle,

(47)

where
\phi
is an eigenstate
\ell_{0}
with maximal eigenvalue.
This follows from standard second-order perturbation theory results.

We claim that
M
is block-diagonal. Each block corresponds to a choice of
T\subset S
and
k
is in a given block iff
\chi_{k}
anticommutes with
\chi_{m}
for all
m\in T
and commutes with
\chi_{m}
for all
m\in S\setminus T
.
Indeed, the state
\chi_{k}\phi

is an eigenstate of
\chi_{m}
for each
m\in S
with eigenvalue
\pm 1
(the sign is
-1
if
m\in T
and
+1
otherwise), and
E-\ell_{0}
commutes with all such
\chi_{l}
, so

\partial_{j}\partial_{k}E
vanishes between blocks.

Consider a given block for given
T
. The operator
E-\ell_{0}
equals

\frac{2|T|}{\sqrt{|S|}}

in that block. Hence, the largest eigenvalue of
M
in that block is bounded by
\frac{\sqrt{|S|}}{2|T|}
times the cardinality of that block. Hence it is less than
\sqrt{|S|}/2
unless the cardinality of that block is larger than
|T|
.
However, in that case, the independent set was not maximal: we may remove all elements of
T
from
S
and add all elements of the given block to increase the independent set.
∎

Appendix B
Low-rank and Gaussian states

Suppose

p=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\lambda_{\alpha}Q_{\alpha}^{2},

(48)

where
\alpha
is some index,
k
is an integer called the
rank
,
\lambda_{\alpha}

is a real scalar and

Q_{\alpha}
is a self-adjoint quadratic operator.

Let
Q_{\alpha}=\sum_{jk}\chi_{j}(K_{\alpha})_{jk}\chi_{k},

where
K_{jk}
is a self-adjoint anti-symmetric matrix (hence,
K
is pure imaginary).
Let us fix

|K_{\alpha}|_{2}=1
. Hence,
\|Q_{\alpha}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=O(n^{1/2})
.

Here we consider the low rank case, where
k=O(1)
.
Assume also for normalization that
\sum_{\alpha}|\lambda_{\alpha}|=O(1)
.

We have

[Q_{\alpha},Q_{\beta}]=4\sum_{jk}\Bigl([K_{\alpha},K_{\beta}]\Bigr)_{jk}\chi_{j}\chi_{k}.

The
\ell_{2}
norm of
[K_{\alpha},K_{\beta}]
is bounded (by a triangle inequality and Cauchy-Schwarz) by
2|K_{\alpha}|_{2}|K_{\beta}|_{2}\leq 2
, though probably tighter bounds are possible.
Hence, by results before,
\|[Q_{\alpha},Q_{\beta}]\|_{\mathrm{op}}=O(n^{1/2})
.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\|[Q_{\alpha},p]\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\beta=1}^{k}|\lambda_{\beta}|\|[Q_{\beta}^{2},Q_{\alpha}]\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\beta=1}^{k}|\lambda_{\beta}|\cdot O(n).

Consider the re-scaled operators
n^{-1/2}Q_{\alpha}
and
n^{-1}p
. These operators all have operator norm
O(1)
and the commutator of any two such operators is
O(n^{-1/2})
.

By section VII of
[
Has09
]
, we can define a POVM (positive operator-valued measure) that approximately measures the
K
different operators
n^{-1/2}Q_{\alpha}
and
n^{-1}p
on some arbitrary quantum state
\rho
. In particular (dropping the re-scaling by
n^{-1/2}
and
n^{-1}
from here on), the measurement returns scalars
q_{\alpha}
such that the average over outcomes of the expectation value
(Q_{\alpha}-q_{\alpha})^{2}
in the resulting state is
o(n)
.
Further, for any
\alpha
, the expectation value
\operatorname{tr}(p\rho)
is within
o(n)
of the average of
\sum\lambda_{\alpha}q_{\alpha}^{2}
over measurement outcomes.
Let
\rho
then be the state that maximizes
\operatorname{tr}(p\rho)
. Apply this POVM. The expectation value of
p
in the resulting state (called
\rho_{outcome}
), averaged over outcomes, is within

o(n)
of
\operatorname{tr}(p\rho)
.
Hence, the average over measurement outcomes of
\sum_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{outcome}Q_{\alpha})^{2}
is within
o(n)
of
\operatorname{tr}(p\rho)
.

So, there is some state
\sigma
for which
\sum_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}\operatorname{tr}(\sigma Q_{\alpha})^{2}
is within
o(n)
of
\operatorname{tr}(p\rho)
.
Let us optimize
\sum_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}\operatorname{tr}(\sigma Q_{\alpha})^{2}
over states
\sigma
.
An argument with Lagrange multipliers shows that the maximum is attained on a Gaussian state. Further, on this Gaussian state, one may verify that
\operatorname{tr}(p\sigma)
is
within
o(n)
of
\sum_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}\operatorname{tr}(\sigma Q_{\alpha})^{2}
and hence within
o(n)
of
\operatorname{tr}(p\rho)
.
