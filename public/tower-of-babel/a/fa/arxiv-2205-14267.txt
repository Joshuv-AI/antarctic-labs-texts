An algorithm for finding weakly reversible deficiency zero realizations of polynomial dynamical systems
Gheorghe Craciun, Jiaxin Jin, Polly Y. Yu

Abstract
Systems of differential equations with polynomial right-hand sides are very common in applications. On the other hand, their mathematical analysis is very challenging in general, due to the possibility of complex dynamics: multiple basins of attraction, oscillations, and even chaotic dynamics. Even if we restrict our attention to mass-action systems, all of these complex dynamical behaviours are still possible. On the other hand, if a polynomial dynamical system has a weakly reversible deficiency zero (WR 0 ) realization , then its dynamics is known to be remarkably simple: oscillations and chaotic dynamics are ruled out and, up to linear conservation laws, there exists a single positive steady state , which is asymptotically stable. Here we describe an algorithm for finding WR 0 realizations of polynomial dynamical systems, whenever such realizations exist.

Introduction

By a
polynomial dynamical system
we mean a system of ODEs with polynomial right-hand side, of the form

\begin{split}\frac{dx_{1}}{dt}&=p_{1}(x_{1},...,x_{n}),\\
\frac{dx_{2}}{dt}&=p_{2}(x_{1},...,x_{n}),\\
&\qquad\quad\vdots\\
\frac{dx_{n}}{dt}&=p_{n}(x_{1},...,x_{n}),\\
\end{split}

(1)

where
p_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. In general, such systems are very difficult to analyze due to nonlinearities and feedback that may give rise to bifurcations, multiple basins of attraction, oscillations, and even chaotic dynamics. The second part of Hilbert’s 16th problem (about the number of limit cycles of polynomial dynamical systems in the plane) is still essentially unsolved, even for
quadratic
polynomials
[
25
]
. Even the simplest object associated to (
1

In terms of applications, polynomial dynamical systems often show up in, for example, chemistry, biology, and population dynamics. In these models, the variable
x_{i}
typically represents concentration, population, or another quantity that is strictly positive, so the domain of (
1
) is restricted to the positive orthant. For example, in an infectious disease model, an infectious individual might covert a susceptible individual; this would contribute a “
{}+bxy
” term to
\frac{dx}{dt}
, where
x
is the population of susceptible individuals,
y
the infectious population, and
b>0
a parameter measuring the contact rate. Collecting all contributing terms results in an interaction network. An active area of research is to relate the structure of the interaction network to the dynamics generated by it
[

,

,

,

,

]
.

Conversely, one may start with (
1
) from experimental data, with little or no information on the generating interaction network. One may try to elucidate the underlying interaction network; however, without additional assumptions, a polynomial dynamical system is not uniquely generated by one interaction network, but infinitely many
[
14
]
. This lack of identifiability of the underlying network can actually be leveraged to analyze the dynamics: if a network with certain properties can be found to generate (
1
), then we may be able to immediately infer its dynamical behaviour.

A class of systems whose dynamics is very well understood is the family of
complex-balanced systems

[
21
]
, which are also called
toric dynamical systems

[
9
]
. They can never exhibit oscillations or chaotic dynamics, and, up to linear conservation laws, there exists a

[

]
. Moreover, this steady state is conjectured to be a global attractor
[

]
.

Not only are the dynamical properties of complex-balanced systems well understood, but also the network and parameter structures that characterize them
[
23
]
. While in general, there are algebraic conditions on the parameters necessary for complex-balancing, the exception to this rule is the case of weakly reversible and deficiency zero (WR

) networks – these systems are complex-balanced

In this paper we describe an efficient algorithm for determining whether a given polynomial dynamical system admits a WR

2.1
Dynamical systems and Euclidean embedded graphs

In this section, we introduce the Euclidean embedded graph (E-graph), a directed graph in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and explain how a system of differential equations with polynomial right-hand side (a polynomial dynamical system) is defined by it.

Definition 2.1
.

A
Euclidean embedded graph
(
E-graph
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a directed graph
(V,E)
, where
V
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq}^{n}
, and there are neither self-loops nor isolated vertices. Denote by
V_{s}
the set of source vertices.

Let
V=\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{m}\}
. An edge
({\boldsymbol{y}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j})
, or
(i,j)\in E
, is also denoted
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
. Since vertices are points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, an edge can be regarded as a bona fide vector between vertices. An
edge vector

{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}
is associated to the edge
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
.

For the purpose of using E-graphs to study polynomial dynamical systems, we assume
V_{s}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\geq}^{n}
, even though most results stated in this paper hold for
V\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq}^{n}
.

The set of vertices
V
of
(V,E)
is partitioned by its connected components, which we identify by the subset of vertices that belong to that connected component. If every connected component is strongly connected, i.e., every edge is part of a cycle, then
(V,E)
is said to be
weakly reversible
.

Two geometric properties of the E-graph will become important to our analysis of polynomial dynamical system. The first is a notion of affine independence within each connected component; the second is a notion of linear independence between connected components.

Definition 2.2
.

An E-graph
(V,E)
has
affinely independent connected components
if the vertices in each connected component are affinely independent, i.e., if
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{r}\}\subseteq V
is a connected component, then the set
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}\colon j=1,2,\ldots,r\}
is linearly independent.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(V,E)
be an E-graph. For any
U\subseteq V
, the
associated linear subspace of
U
is
S(U)=\Span\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\colon{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i},\,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\in U\}
.
The
associated linear space
1
1

1

In reaction network theory literature, the associated linear space is called the
stoichiometric subspace
of the network.
of
(V,E)
is

\displaystyle S=\Span\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\colon{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\in E\}.

If
U
defines a connected component of
(V,E)
, then
S(U)\subseteq S
. Indeed, if
V_{1}
,
V_{2},\ldots,V_{\ell}
are the connected components, then
S=S(V_{1})+S(V_{2})+\cdots+S(V_{\ell})
.

Thus far, we have defined an E-graph, and introduced several objects and properties associated to it. We now turn our attention to how such a graph is canonically associated to dynamics, by assigning a positive weight to each edge.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(V,E)
be an E-graph. For each
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\in E
, let
\kappa_{ij}>0
be its weight, and let
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=(\kappa_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}_{>}^{E}
. The
associated dynamical system
on
\mathbb{R}_{>}^{n}
of the weighted E-graph
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
is

\displaystyle\frac{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\kappa_{ij}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}).

(2)

It is sometimes convenient to refer to
\kappa_{ij}
even though
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
may not be an edge in the network. In such cases, set
\kappa_{ij}=0
.

Remark 2.5
.

We defined the domain of (

\mathbb{R}_{>}^{n}
. Systems of ODEs with polynomial right-hand side do not in general leave

It is clear that the right-hand side of (

A

2.2
Complex-balanced systems and WR
0
systems

General polynomial dynamical systems can display a wide range of dynamical behaviours, ranging from stable or unstable steady states, limit cycles, and even chaos. In this work, we are interested in the family of
complex-balanced systems
, which enjoy various algebraic and stability properties.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
be a weighted E-graph in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
\dot{{\boldsymbol{x}}}={\boldsymbol{f}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
be its associated dynamical system. A state
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{>}^{n}
is said to be a
positive steady state
if
{\boldsymbol{f}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})={\boldsymbol{0}}
. Let
V_{>}({\boldsymbol{f}})
be the set of positive steady states. A state
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}>{\boldsymbol{0}}
is a
complex-balanced steady state
if at every
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\in V
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\kappa_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}=\sum_{(j,i)\in E}\kappa_{ji}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}.

The equations above can be interpreted as balancing the fluxes flowing across the vertex
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}
. If a weighted E-graph
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
admits one complex-balanced steady state, then every positive steady state is complex-balanced
[
21
]
; such a
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
is called a
complex-balanced system
.

These systems first arose from the study of chemical systems under mass-action kinetics, as a generalization of thermodynamic equilibrium. The following theorem lists some of the most important results about complex-balanced systems. For more details, see
[
38
,
20
,
17
]
.

Theorem 2.12

(
[
21
]
)
.

Let
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
be a complex-balanced system, with steady state
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{>}^{n}
, and associated linear space
S
. Then the following are true:

(i)

All positive steady states are complex-balanced, and there is exactly one steady state within each invariant polyhedron.

(ii)

Any complex-balanced steady state
{\boldsymbol{x}}
satisfies
\ln{\boldsymbol{x}}-\ln{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\in S^{\perp}
.

(iii)

The function

\displaystyle L({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}(\ln x_{i}-\ln x^{*}_{i}-1),

defined on
\mathbb{R}_{>}^{n}
, is a strict Lyapunov function within each invariant polyhedron
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)\cap\mathbb{R}_{>}^{n}
, with a global minimum at the corresponding complex-balanced steady state.

(iv)

Every complex-balanced steady state is asymptotically stable with respect to its invariant polyhedron.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
(V,E)
be an E-graph with
\ell

is the integer

The notion of deficiency can also be applied to the connected components. Suppose

Lemma 3.1
.

3.2
The set of positive steady states of a WR
0
realization

Algorithm

1
determines whether a given polynomial dynamical system admits a WR
0
realization. If it does, its steady state set is in fact log-linear. In this section, we write down a system of linear equations whose solution set is in bijection with the set of positive steady states; this provides an explicit parametrization of the set of positive steady states.

For any
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>}^{n}
, define the component-wise operations
\exp{\boldsymbol{z}}=(e^{z_{1}},e^{z_{2}},\ldots,e^{z_{n}})^{\top}
and
\log({\boldsymbol{x}})=(\log x_{1},\log x_{2},\ldots,\log x_{n})^{\top}
. We extend these operations to sets. If
Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\exp(Z)=\{\exp{\boldsymbol{z}}\colon{\boldsymbol{z}}\in Z\}
, and if
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>}^{n}
, then
\log(X)=\{\log{\boldsymbol{x}}\colon{\boldsymbol{x}}\in X\}
.

Assume that the polynomial dynamical system

\displaystyle\frac{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i},

(10)

with distinct
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq}^{n}
and
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
, passes
Algorithm

1
, i.e., it admits a WR
0
realization
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
. Without loss of generality, assume the vertices are ordered according to connected components in
(V,E)
, i.e., the first
m_{1}
vertices belong to the connected component
(V_{1},E_{1})
, the next
m_{2}
vertices belong to the connected component
(V_{2},E_{2})
, and so forth. Let
\{{\boldsymbol{c}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{c}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\ell}\}
be a minimal set of generators of
\ker\mathbf{W}\cap\mathbb{R}_{\geq}^{m}
, ordered in an analogous way. From
Algorithm

1
, we know that the supports of the vectors
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\ell}
correspond to the connected components of
(V,E)
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{1}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m_{1}},0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
. Define matrix
\mathbf{D}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m_{1}-1)\times n}
whose rows are the affine vectors from
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}
to the remaining vertices of
V_{1}
, and define vector
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}-1}
using the log-differences of the components of
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{1}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}\\
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{3}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{m_{1}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad{\boldsymbol{J}}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\log(\alpha_{2}/\alpha_{1})\\
\log(\alpha_{3}/\alpha_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\log(\alpha_{m_{1}}/\alpha_{1})\end{pmatrix}.

For the connected component
(V_{p},E_{p})
, define
\mathbf{D}_{p}
and
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{p}
in a similar fashion. Define

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{D}_{1}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{D}_{2}\\
\hline\cr\vdots\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{D}_{\ell}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m-\ell)\times n}\quad\text{and}\quad{\boldsymbol{J}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{J}}_{1}\\
\hline\cr{\boldsymbol{J}}_{2}\\
\hline\cr\vdots\\
\hline\cr{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\ell}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m-\ell}.

(11)

Theorem 3.12
.

Suppose the system of differential equation (
10
) admits a WR
0
realization
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
, and let
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m-\ell)\times n}
and
{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>}^{m-\ell}
be defined as in (
11
). Then the system
\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{z}}={\boldsymbol{J}}
is solvable. Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}+\ker\mathbf{D}
be its solution set. Then the set of positive steady states of (
10
) is
\exp({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}+\ker\mathbf{D})
.

Proof.

First we prove that the linear system
\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{z}}={\boldsymbol{J}}
is solvable. Consider
\mathbf{D}_{1}
. The vertices
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{m_{1}}
in the first connected component are affinely independent, so the rows of
\mathbf{D}_{1}
are linearly independent. Moreover, as noted in
3.6
the row-space of
\mathbf{D}_{1}
is the associated linear subspace
S(V_{1})
. Therefore
\rank\mathbf{D}_{1}=m_{1}-1
, and the matrix
\mathbf{D}_{1}
is surjective onto
\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}-1}
.

Similarly, for each
p=2,\ldots,\ell
, the row-space of the matrix
\mathbf{D}_{p}
is
S(V_{p})
, and the matrix
\mathbf{D}_{p}
is surjective. In addition, because the realization
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
has deficiency zero,
S(V_{1})
,
S(V_{2}),\ldots,S(V_{\ell})
are linearly independent; in other words, the
m-\ell
rows of the matrix
\mathbf{D}
are linearly independent. Consequently,
\mathbf{D}
is surjective, and the system
\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{z}}={\boldsymbol{J}}
is solvable.

Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}+\ker\mathbf{D}
be the set of solution to
\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{z}}={\boldsymbol{J}}
. We next show that each solution can be related to a positive steady state of (
10
), which by definition satisfies

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}.

In other words,
({\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{m}})^{\top}
lies in the steady state flux cone
\ker\mathbf{W}\cap\mathbb{R}_{>}^{m}
. Decomposing this vector with respect to the generators of the cone allows us to focus on one connected component at a time.

For simplicity of notation, consider the first connected component. At steady state, for some constant
\lambda>0
, we have
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}=\lambda\alpha_{j}
for
j=1
,
2,\ldots,m_{1}
. Thus

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}}=\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{1}}

for
j=2,3,\ldots,m_{1}
. Taking the logarithm of both sides, we obtain the system
\mathbf{D}_{1}{\boldsymbol{z}}={\boldsymbol{J}}_{1}
with
{\boldsymbol{z}}=\log{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Repeating this computation for each connected component, we conclude that
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a positive steady state for (
10
) if and only if
{\boldsymbol{x}}
solves
\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{z}}={\boldsymbol{J}}
with
{\boldsymbol{z}}=\log{\boldsymbol{x}}
. This leads us to the characterization of the set of positive steady states for (
10
) as
\exp({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}+\ker\mathbf{D})
, where
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}+\ker\mathbf{D}
is the set of solutions to
\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{z}}={\boldsymbol{J}}
.
∎

3.3
Extension to polynomial systems with unspecified coefficients

If instead of (
10
), we need to analyze

\displaystyle\frac{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}

(12)

for some unknown
a_{i}>0
, it turns out that the answer as to whether a WR
0
realization exists is the same:

Theorem 3.13
.

For any
a_{i}>0
, the system (
12
) admits a WR
0
realization
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
if and only if the system (
10
) admits a WR
0
realization
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{*})
. Moreover,
\kappa_{ij}=a_{i}\kappa^{*}_{ij}
.

Proof.

The forward implication is trivial. We focus our attention on the other direction.
For any
i
,
j
, let
\kappa_{ij}=a_{i}\kappa^{*}_{ij}
, so
\kappa_{ij}>0
if and only if
\kappa^{*}_{ij}>0
. In other words, the weighted E-graph
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
shares the same set of edges as
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{*})
. Because the deficiency is characterized by affine and linear independence of the connected components, and the two graphs share the same structure,
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
is weakly reversible and deficiency zero if and only if
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{*})
is.

Suppose
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{*})
is a realization of (
10
). Then in
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
, the net direction vector from
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}
can be expanded using the realization
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{*})
, since

\displaystyle a_{i}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}=a_{i}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\kappa^{*}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\kappa_{ij}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}).

Therefore,
(V,E,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
realizes (
12
).
∎

3.4
Deficiency zero realizations that are not weakly reversible

If a polynomial dynamical system admits a deficiency zero realization that is
not
weakly reversible, then its dynamics is also greatly restricted: it can have no positive steady states, no oscillations, and no chaotic dynamics
[
23
,
16
,
18
]
. Actually, such realizations are special examples of mass-action system that are
not consistent

[
1
]
. An E-graph
(V,E)
is said to be
consistent
if there exist real numbers
\alpha_{ij}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\alpha_{ij}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})={\boldsymbol{0}}.

It is easy to see that a polynomial dynamical system of the form (
10
) has a realization that is not consistent if and only if

\ker\mathbf{W}\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>}=\emptyset.

(13)

If a polynomial dynamical system has a realization that is not consistent, then it cannot have realization that is weakly reversible, because weakly reversible systems must have at least one positive steady state
[
7
]
.
Therefore, if
Algorithm

1
is accompanied by a preprocessing step that checks condition (
13
), then that step will decide whether our given system (
10
) has a realization that is not consistent; in particular, this step will also find all cases where our given system has a deficiency zero realization that is
not
weakly reversible.
