Area-law entanglement from quantum geometry
Nisarga Paul

Abstract
Quantum geometry, which encompasses both Berry curvature and the quantum metric, plays a key role in multi-band interacting electron systems. We study the entanglement entropy of a region of linear size ℓ \ell in fermion systems with nontrivial quantum geometry, i.e. whose Bloch states have nontrivial k k dependence. We show that the entanglement entropy scales as S = α ​ ℓ d − 1 ​ ln ⁡ ℓ + β ​ ℓ d − 1 + ⋯ S=\alpha\ell^{d-1}\ln\ell+\beta\ell^{d-1}+\cdots where the first term is the well-known area-law violating term for fermions and β \beta contains the leading contribution from quantum geometry. We compute this for the case of uniform quantum geometry and cubic domains and provide numerical results for the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model, 2D massive Dirac cone, and 2D Chern bands. An experimental probe of the quantum geometric entanglement entropy is proposed using particle number fluctuations. We offer an intuitive account of the area-law entanglement related to the spread of maximally localized Wannier functions.



I
Introduction

A central topic in current condensed matter physics is the study of strongly correlated phases in partially filled Chern bands, such as unconventional superconductivity, Mott insulators, and fractional Chern insulators
Cao et al. 2018a
;
Cao et al. 2018b
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Spanton et al. 2018
. For non-topological bands, the study of strongly correlated phases often begins with the Hubbard model, since one can always find a basis of exponentially localized Wannier orbitals. In contrast, one cannot find such a basis for topological bands in greater than one dimension, a fact known as the Wannier obstruction
Brouder et al. 2007
. For example, in a Landau level in a disordered potential, most states are localized while a narrow band of states must be delocalized– which is crucial for the integer quantum Hall effect.

The inability to find exponentially localized Wannier orbitals in topological bands should imply longer-range entanglement. Entanglement has proven a valuable conceptual tool and diagnostic in topological condensed matter physics
Kitaev and Preskill 2006
;
Zeng et al. 2015
;
Li and Haldane 2008
, but there has not been a detailed study of real-space entanglement in partially filled Chern bands.

We find that an essential quantity is the quantum geometry of the partially filled band. Quantum geometry is simply the structure of Bloch state overlaps, and it encompasses both Berry curvature and the quantum metric
Provost and Vallee 1980
. Recent works have highlighted the importance of the full quantum geometry, and not simply Berry curvature, in the study of interactions in Chern bands, for instance in twisted bilayer graphene
Parameswaran et al. 2013
;
Ledwith et al. 2020
;
Parker et al. 2021
.

In particular we show that nontrivial quantum geometry contributes additional area-law entanglement; that is, the von Neumann entropy of a region of linear size
\ell
in
d
dimensional systems goes as

S=\alpha\ell^{d-1}\ln\ell+\beta\ell^{d-1}+\cdots

(1)

where the quantum geometry contributes at order
\ell^{d-1}
. The leading term is a log-enhanced area law originating from the
O(\ell^{d-1})
number of independent 1d chiral modes on the Fermi surface, each of which contributes
\ln\ell

Swingle 2010
.
\alpha
can be computed using Widom’s conjecture and depends only on the entangling region and Fermi surface, not on the quantum geometry
Gioev and Klich 2006
.

We find it useful to define the
quantum geometric entanglement entropy

S_{QG}
by subtracting from
S
the entropy obtained by setting all Bloch overlaps
\left\langle u_{\boldsymbol{k}}|u_{\boldsymbol{q}}\right\rangle
to unity. In other words,

S_{QG}=S-S|_{\left\langle u_{\boldsymbol{k}}|u_{\boldsymbol{q}}\right\rangle\to 1}.

(2)

S_{QG}
measures the additional entropy coming from intrinsically multi-band effects, omitting contributions shared with continuum fermions with an identical Fermi surface.

Our main results can then be expressed as follows:

1.

We show
S_{QG}=\beta\ell^{d-1}+\cdots
for the case of uniform quantum geometry (i.e.
\left\langle u_{\boldsymbol{k}}|u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}\right\rangle=g({\boldsymbol{q}})
) and provide a closed form for
\beta

2.

We show numerically that
S_{QG}
is area law for the 1D Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model, the 2D massive Dirac fermion, and 2D Chern bands

3.

We establish that particle number variance receives a similar area-law contribution from quantum geometry and propose an experimental measure of
S_{QG}

As a corollary to 3, we find a protocol to measure the quantum metric at the band bottom using particle number fluctuations.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we define fermion entanglement entropy and other preliminaries. In Sec.
III
we provide a simple two-particle example which captures general features of quantum geometric entanglement entropy. In Sections
III
,
V
, and
VI
we calculate
S_{QG}
for the cases of uniform quantum geometry in
d
dimensions, the 1d Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model, and 2d models, respectively. In Sec.
VII
we establish similar results for particle number fluctuations and suggest an experimental protocol for measuring
S_{QG}
. In Sec.
VIII
we provide an intuitive explanation for the area-law behavior of
S_{QG}
and its relation to quantum geometry using Wannier orbital spread, and in Sec.
IX
we conclude.

II
Preliminaries

In this section we review how entanglement entropy can be computed for fermions, although we note for the reader that only Eq. (
15
) is needed in the sections that follow. We start by considering a system of
N
noninteracting fermions with a discrete one-particle spectrum. The many-body wavefunction takes the form
\Psi({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N})=\det[\phi_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})]/\sqrt{N!}
where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
includes both spatial and internal coordinates (e.g. spin / sublattice) and the
\phi_{n}
are the normalized wavefunctions of the occupied states. The partial density matrix of a spatial region
A
is

\rho_{A}=\Tr_{\bar{A}}\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}

(3)

and the
\alpha
’th Rényi entropy is

S^{(\alpha)}(A)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\ln\Tr\rho_{A}^{\alpha},

(4)

with
\alpha\to 1
the von Neumann entropy. We have the useful relation
Peschel 2003

\rho_{A}=(\det\mathbb{C})\exp\left[\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in A}\ln\left(\mathbb{C}^{-1}-\mathbb{I}\right)_{{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}}c_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\dagger}c_{\boldsymbol{y}}\right]

(5)

where

\mathbb{C}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\left\langle c^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{x}})c({\boldsymbol{y}})\right\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\phi_{n}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})\phi_{n}({\boldsymbol{y}})

(6)

is the two-point correlator in
\Psi
restricted to
A
. This allows us to express the Rényi entropies as

S^{(\alpha)}(A)=\sum_{\ell}e_{\alpha}(\lambda_{\ell})

(7)

where

e_{\alpha}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\ln(\lambda^\alpha+(1-\lambda)^\alpha)

(8)

and
\lambda_{\ell}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{C}
. Note that the limit
\alpha\to 1
gives

e_{1}(\lambda)=-\lambda\ln\lambda-(1-\lambda)\ln(1-\lambda).

(9)

Since
\mathbb{C}
grows in size with
A
, computing its spectrum can be a nontrivial task even on a lattice. To simplify things, we use the Fredholm determinant

D_{A}(\lambda)=\det[\lambda\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{C}]

(10)

to rewrite
S^{(\alpha)}(A)
as
Jin and Korepin 2004
;
Calabrese et al. 2011

S^{(\alpha)}(A)=\oint\frac{d\lambda}{2\pi i}e_{\alpha}(\lambda)\derivative{\ln D_A(\lambda)}{\lambda}

(11)

where the integration contour encircles the segment
[0,1]
. Next we introduce the overlap matrix
Klich 2006
;
Calabrese et al. 2011

\mathbb{A}_{nm}=\int_{A}d{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\,\phi_{n}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})\phi_{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}).

(12)

Importantly, by
\int_{A}d{\boldsymbol{x}}
we mean an integral over the spatial region
A

and
a sum over internal indices. This matrix enjoys the property

\displaystyle\Tr\mathbb{A}^{k}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}=1}^{N}\int_{A}d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\phi_{n_{1}}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})\phi_{n_{2}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})\cdots\int_{A}d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\phi_{n_{k}}^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})\phi_{n_{1}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{A}d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\cdots\int_{A}d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\mathbb{C}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})\mathbb{C}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k-1})\cdots\mathbb{C}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})

\displaystyle\quad\qquad=\Tr\mathbb{C}^{k}

(13)

and therefore
Calabrese et al. 2011

\displaystyle\ln D_{A}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\ln\lambda-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Tr\mathbb{C}^{k}}{k\lambda^{k}}=\ln\lambda-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Tr\mathbb{A}^{k}}{k\lambda^{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\ln(\lambda-a_m)

(14)

where the
a_{m}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{A}
. We apply the residue theorem to Eq. (
11
) to obtain

S^{(\alpha)}(A)=\sum_{m}e_{\alpha}(a_{m}),

(15)

which lends itself well to numerical calculations since
\mathbb{A}
is an
N\times N
matrix, independent of the entangling region.

III
Simple two-particle example

Some implications of quantum geometry for entanglement entropy can be seen in a simple two-particle example. Consider a Slater determinant of two occupied single-particle states in a translationally invariant
m
-band system,

\phi_{i}({\boldsymbol{r}})=L^{-d/2}e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}_{i}\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}}\ket{ u_{\k_i}},\quad i=1,2,

(16)

where
L^{d}
is the system size and
\ket{ u_{\k_i}}
is an
m
-component vector. We will parameterize the Bloch overlap as

\left\langle u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1}}|u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{2}}\right\rangle=ue^{i\phi}.

(17)

When
u=1
, the quantum geometry is trivial (
\phi
can be gauged away). Therefore the deviation of
u
from
1
is a measure of quantum geometry, in a way we make precise. Let the entangling region be
A
. The overlap matrix is

\mathbb{A}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{0}&I_{d{\boldsymbol{k}}}ue^{i\phi}\\
I_{-d{\boldsymbol{k}}}ue^{-i\phi}&I_{0}\end{pmatrix}

(18)

where
d{\boldsymbol{k}}\equiv{\boldsymbol{k}}_{2}-{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1}
and

I_{\boldsymbol{k}}=L^{-d}\int_{A}d^{d}{\boldsymbol{r}}\,e^{-i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}}.

(19)

The eigenvalues of the overlap matrix are

a_{1,2}=I_{0}\pm|I_{d{\boldsymbol{k}}}|u

(20)

and the entropy is
S^{(\alpha)}=\sum_{i}e_{\alpha}(a_{i})
. It is easy to check that
\left.\partial S^{(\alpha)}/\partial u\right|_{u=1}<0
, so that
S^{(\alpha)}
increases as
u
decreases from
1
. In fact, since
e_{\alpha}
is Schur-concave,
S^{(\alpha)}
increases monotonically with decreasing
u
. The entropy is maximized when
u=0
, i.e. when the Bloch states are orthogonal.

In this example, the entanglement entropy “increases with quantum geometry.” We can make this more precise if
d{\boldsymbol{k}}\equiv{\boldsymbol{k}}_{2}-{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1}
is small, in which case

1-u^{2}\approx g_{\mu\nu}({\boldsymbol{k}}_{1})dk_{\mu}dk_{\nu}

(21)

where
g_{\mu\nu}({\boldsymbol{k}})
is the quantum metric. Then the entanglement entropy increases with the quantum metric.

In general, nontrivial quantum geometry has the effect of decreasing the magnitudes of off-diagonals of
\mathbb{A}
. Since the trace is unaffected, this drives the eigenvalues towards their average value, increasing the entropy. While the phase
\phi
of the Bloch overlap played no role in this simple two-state example, phases play a role as soon as three or more states are involved, and in general the phase structure is captured by Berry curvature.

IV
Uniform quantum geometry

Before generalizing to
d
dimensions, let us first consider a two-band model in a 1d system of size
L
with periodic boundary conditions. As a simple model with quantum geometry, we take the wavefunctions

\displaystyle\phi_{1,k}(x)

\displaystyle=L^{-1/2}e^{ikx}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\beta k)\\
\sin(\beta k)\end{pmatrix}

(22)

\displaystyle\phi_{2,k}(x)

\displaystyle=L^{-1/2}e^{ikx}\begin{pmatrix}-\sin(\beta k)\\
\cos(\beta k)\end{pmatrix}

(23)

for the 1st and 2nd band, respectively, and
k\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}/L
. Let
A=[-\ell/2,\ell/2]
be the entangling region and assume the 1st band is filled in the region
\Gamma=[-k_{F},k_{F}]
. The overlap matrix is

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{k,k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int_{A}dx\,\phi^{*}_{1,k}(x)\cdot\phi_{1,k^{\prime}}(x)

\displaystyle=L^{-1}\int_{A}dx\,e^{-i(k-k^{\prime})x}\cos(\beta(k-k'))

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sin((k-k')\ell/2)}{L(k-k^{\prime})}\cos(\beta(k-k'))

(24)

\displaystyle\equiv f(k-k^{\prime})

Since the matrix elements depend only on
k-k^{\prime}
,
\mathbb{A}
is a Toeplitz matrix. Its spectrum converges at large
N
to the image of its “symbol”

\displaystyle f(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}f(k)e^{ikL\theta/2\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=\pm}\sum_{k}\frac{2\sin k\ell/2}{kL}e^{ik(\frac{L}{2\pi}\theta+s\beta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=\pm}(1-\chi_{[\frac{\ell}{2L},1-\frac{\ell}{2L}]}(\theta/2\pi+s\beta/L))

(25)

where
\chi_{[a,b]}(x)=1
if
x\in[a,b]
and
0
otherwise. This is plotted in Fig.
1
, restricted to
0\leq\theta<2\pi
. Next, we use the classical Szegő theorem to compute
Grenander and Szegő 1958

.

\displaystyle S_{QG}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e_{1}(f(\theta))\,d\theta+\cdots

\displaystyle=2(k_{F}/\pi)|\beta|\ln 2+\cdots

(26)

where we’ve used
2k_{F}=2\pi N/L
and the
\cdots
are subleading terms going as
\ell^{-1}\ln\ell
which can be calculated analytically using the Fisher-Hartwig theorem. Each boundary point contributes

, resulting in an area-law contribution to entanglement entropy.

To be concrete, we could provide a lattice model with the eigenfunctions in Eq.
22
even though the particulars of the model aren’t needed to compute the entanglement entropy. Let’s choose band energies
\epsilon_{1,2}(k)=\mp(t_{0}+t_{1}\cos ka)
for spinless fermions on a linear chain with two sublattices
A

H=\sum_{j}\left[\sum_{\delta}\frac{t_{\delta}}{2}(c_{Aj}^{\dagger}c_{A,j+\delta}-c_{Bj}^{\dagger}c_{B,j+\delta})\right.\\
\left.+i\sum_{\delta^{\prime}}t_{\delta^{\prime}}c_{Aj}^{\dagger}c_{B,j+\delta^{\prime}}-i\sum_{\delta^{\prime\prime}}t_{\delta^{\prime\prime}}c_{Aj}^{\dagger}c_{B,j+\delta^{\prime\prime}}+\text{h.c.}\right]

(27)

where
\delta=\pm 2\beta/a,\pm 1\pm 2\beta/a
,
\delta^{\prime}=+2\beta/a,\pm 1+2\beta/a
,
\delta^{\prime\prime}=-2\beta/a,\pm 1-2\beta/a
,
t_{\pm 2\beta/a}=-t_{0}/2
and
t_{\pm 1\pm 2\beta/a}=-t_{1}/4
, and
2\beta/a\in\mathbb{Z}
.

This explicit lattice model suggests a natural interpretation for
S_{QG}
in this 1D example. The lattice model involves hopping by a range proportional to
\beta
, which results in an area-law increase in entanglement entropy proportional to
\beta
. We return to this point in Sec.
VIII
.

Next we show that the entanglement entropy is area-law in
d
dimensions. In this case we work with a multilevel Toeplitz matrix, and the classical Szegő theorem generalizes straightforwardly
Tyrtyshnikov and Zamarashkin 1998
. We will work with cubic regions
A=[-\ell/2,\ell/2]^{d}
for convenience, though the leading behavior at large
\ell
should depend only weakly on this choice.

The symbol is now a function of a vector
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of
d
angles. We can write

f({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{2\sin k_{i}\ell/2}{k_{i}L}\right)\cos(\vec\beta\cdot\k)e^{i(L/2\pi){\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}

(28)

where we’ve generalized to a vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and the sum is over
(\frac{2\pi}{L}\mathbb{Z})^{d}
. This straightforwardly gives

f({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=\pm}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-\chi_{[\frac{\ell}{2L},1-\frac{\ell}{2L}]}(\theta_{i}/2\pi+s\beta_{i}/L)).

(29)

The entropy is given by

\displaystyle S_{QG}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{N}{2\pi}\right)^{d}\int e_{1}(f({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\,d^{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\cdots

\displaystyle=2(k_{F}/\pi)^{d}\ell^{d-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\beta_{i}|\ln 2+\cdots

(30)

where the
\cdots
are subleading in
\ell
.

Next we treat the general case with uniform quantum geometry. By this we mean

\left\langle u_{\boldsymbol{q}}|u_{{\boldsymbol{q}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right\rangle=g({\boldsymbol{k}}),

(31)

i.e. Bloch overlaps depend only on the momentum difference
{\boldsymbol{k}}
. Since
g(-{\boldsymbol{k}})=g({\boldsymbol{k}})^{*}
, we may write this in the continuum setting as

g({\boldsymbol{k}})=\int d^{d}\boldsymbol{\beta}\,\tilde{g}(\boldsymbol{\beta})e^{i\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot{\boldsymbol{k}}}

(32)

where
\tilde{g}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is a real-valued density integrating to 1. It is useful to define the marginal density of
\beta_{i}
as

\tilde{g}_{i}(\gamma)=\int(\prod_{j\neq i}d\beta_{j})\,\tilde{g}(\boldsymbol{\beta})|_{\beta_{i}=\gamma}

(33)

and the cumulative marginal density as

\tilde{G}_{i}(\gamma)=\int_{-\infty}^{\gamma}d\gamma^{\prime}\,\tilde{g}_{i}(\gamma^{\prime}).

(34)

Applying the classical Szegő theorem then gives

S_{QG}=\left(\frac{k_{F}}{\pi}\right)^{d}\ell^{d-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int d\beta\,e_{1}(\tilde{G}_{i}(\beta))

(35)

to leading order in
\ell
, which recovers Eq. (
30
) when
\tilde{g}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\frac{1}{2}(\delta^{d}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\beta})+\delta^{d}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\beta}))
.

In this section, we study the role of quantum geometry on entanglement in the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) model
Su et al. 1979
at partial fillings. The SSH model describes electrons hopping on a chain with staggered hopping amplitudes without interactions. Denoting the sublattices
A
and
B
, the Hamiltonian is

H=\sum_{j}vc_{Aj}^{\dagger}c_{Bj}+wc_{Bj}^{\dagger}c_{A,j+1}+h.c.

(36)

which can be written in a momentum basis as
H=\sum_{k}c_{k}^{\dagger}H_{k}c_{k}
with

H_{k}=\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\quad\boldsymbol{d}_{k}=\left(v+w\cos k,w\sin k,0\right).

(37)

The two bands have energies
\pm d_{k}
. The winding number of
\boldsymbol{d}_{k}/d_{k}
around the origin separates two phases. For
v<w
the winding number is 0 and electrons are in a topologically trivial phase. For
v>w
, the winding number is 1 and electrons are in a topological phase, indicated by edge modes on a finite chain and a nonzero Zak phase.

Being one dimensional, the SSH chain has no Berry curvature and a single-component quantum metric. We recall that the quantum metric can be obtained from

1-|\left\langle u_{\boldsymbol{k}}|u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+d{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right\rangle|^{2}=g_{\mu\nu}({\boldsymbol{k}})dk_{\mu}dk_{\nu}+O(dk^{4}).

(38)

The metric in the SSH model for both bands is

g(k)=\frac{(\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\times\boldsymbol{d}_{k}^{\prime})^{2}}{4d_{k}^{4}}.

(39)

We plot it in Fig.
2
a. The Bloch overlaps are

\left\langle u_{k}|u_{q}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{(d^{1}_{k}-id^{2}_{k})(d^{1}_{q}+id^{2}_{q})}{d_{k}d_{q}}\right).

(40)

Since
S_{QG}
depends on the structure of wavefunction overlaps and not directly on the band energies, we do not restrict our choice of Fermi seas to only those allowed by energetics. Rather, we simply use
\Gamma=[0,k_{F}]
in this case. Alternatively, we could imagine deforming the energy bands such that
\Gamma
is the true Fermi sea, while keeping the wavefunctions the same.

We compute
S_{QG}
by explicitly diagonalizing the overlap matrices. In Fig.
2
b, we see that
S_{QG}
saturates to a constant for a variety of Fermi sea sizes. For a filled lower band,
S_{QG}
approaches
\ln 2
per boundary
Ryu and Hatsugai 2006
. This is a manifestation of bulk-edge correspondence
Swingle and Senthil 2012
, which relates the entanglement cut to a physical cut; the edge modes of a physical cut correspond to modes in the entanglement spectrum which contribute
\ln 2
to the entropy.

We plot the dependence of
S_{QG}
on
w/v
in Fig.
2
c. When the first band is filled,
S_{QG}
and
\int g(k)
both increase monotonically up to their maximum at
w/v=1
and then decrease monotonically, as shown in Fig.
2
c (inset). A partial understanding of this can come from the fact that the localization length of the maximally localized Wannier orbital in 1 dimension is

\Omega=\int_{\text{BZ}}g(k)\,dk.

(41)

Since entanglement entropy is invariant under the unitary transformation from momentum eigenstates to Wannier orbitals, one is free to choose the basis of maximally localized Wannier orbitals in computing entanglement entropy across
A
. This choice suggests that
S_{QG}
is roughly proportional to the number of Wannier orbitals straddling the boundary of
A
, implying area-law growth proportional to
\Omega
. We discuss this point of view further in Sec.
VIII
.

Next, we study the quantum geometric entanglement entropy in two dimensional models. The simplest starting point is the massive Dirac fermion,

H=\boldsymbol{d}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\quad\boldsymbol{d}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=(k_{x},k_{y},m).

(43)

and the quantum metric satisfies

{\boldsymbol{k}}=(0,\pi)
and
(\pi,0)
. In order to avoid effects due to specific Dirac points, we choose the Fermi sea to be the entire lower band. As expected for a 2D model,
S_{QG}\sim\ell
for large
\ell
. In Fig.
4
we plot the dependence of
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}S_{QG}/\ell
on
u
. In contrast with the
m=0
behavior of
S_{QG}
for the massive Dirac fermion, the entropy for the QWZ model does not show sharp features across the band-touching points. This is also in contrast with the integrals of
\sqrt{\det g(k)}
and
\Tr g(k)
over the lower band (insets); the former shows cusp-like transitions while the latter diverges at
u=\pm 2,0
.
S_{QG}
is not directly proportional to either of these quantities, suggesting again that
S_{QG}
is a quantum geometric probe which goes beyond leading order in
dk
.

VII
Experimental measures

Although entanglement entropy has proven quite useful conceptually in condensed matter physics, it is difficult to measure directly. This is in part because it is nonlinear in
\rho
and highly nonlocal. In the context of optical lattices, Refs.
Pichler et al. 2013
and
Daley et al. 2012
proposed schemes for measuring the entanglement entropy using quantum interference of two copies of the many-body state. Using this method, Ref.
Islam et al. 2015
measured the 2nd Rényi entropy in the ground state of the four-site Bose-Hubbard model in both the Mott insulating and superfluid phases. In general, one can interfere
n
copies of the state to measure an
n
th-order polynomial in
\rho

Ekert et al. 2002
.

Such a protocol is generally impractical for solid state devices. Along different lines, Ref.
Klich and Levitov 2009
suggested particle number fluctuations as an effective measure of many-body entanglement. This is based on the fact that fermion entanglement entropy has an exact expansion in terms of even cumulants
V^{(2j)}_{A}
of the particle number distribution which rapidly converges at large
N

Calabrese et al. 2012
. In particular,

S=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}V^{(2)}+\frac{\pi^{4}}{45}V^{(4)}+\frac{2\pi^{6}}{945}V^{(6)}+\cdots

(46)

where
V^{(2)}=\Tr\mathbb{A}(1-\mathbb{A})
and similar expressions exist for the other cumulants. Therefore the fluctuations of current flowing through a quantum point contact serve as a good probe of the many-body entanglement, and thus of quantum geometry.

Let us work out the dependence of the leading term on quantum geometry. We write

\mathbb{A}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}=I_{{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\left\langle u_{\boldsymbol{k}}|u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle

(47)

where
I_{{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}=L^{-d}\int_{A}d{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\,e^{-i({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime})\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}}
. As before, we use a cubic region
A
with volume
\ell^{d}
. The particle number variance can be written as

\displaystyle V^{(2)}

\displaystyle=N(\ell/L)^{d}-\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}|A_{{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=N(\ell/L)^{d}-2^{-d}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}|I_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}|^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}|\left\langle u_{\boldsymbol{k}}|u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{p}}}\right\rangle|^{2}

(48)

letting
{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{p}}
. When
\left\langle u_{\boldsymbol{k}}|u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle=1
, an explicit calculation using the Widom conjecture gives leading
\ell^{d-1}\ln\ell
behavior
Calabrese et al. 2012
. As with entropy, we define the
quantum geometric cumulant

V_{QG}^{(m)}
by subtracting the result when
\left\langle u_{\boldsymbol{k}}|u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle=1
.

|I_{\boldsymbol{p}}|^{2}
is strongly peaked near
{\boldsymbol{p}}=0
, so this motivates an expansion

1-|\left\langle u_{\boldsymbol{k}}|u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{p}}}\right\rangle|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{2j}}^{(2j)}({\boldsymbol{k}})\,p_{\mu_{1}}\cdots p_{\mu_{2j}}

(49)

in even powers of momentum, where
\Omega^{(2j)}_{\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{2j}}
is a symmetric rank-
2j
tensor. For instance,
\Omega^{(2)}_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}=g_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
, the quantum metric. Let us study the contribution at order
2j
:

\delta_{2j}V_{QG}^{(2)}=2^{-d}\sum_{\boldsymbol{p}}|I_{\boldsymbol{p}}|^{2}p_{\mu_{1}}\cdots p_{\mu_{2j}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\Omega^{(2j)}_{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{2j}}({\boldsymbol{k}}).

(50)

As before, let’s assume a Fermi sea
\Gamma=[-k_{F},k_{F}]^{d}
; the main results are unaffected by this choice. The sum over
{\boldsymbol{p}}
vanishes if an odd power of
p_{i}
occurs. Moreover, the contribution which is leading in
\ell
comes from
\mu_{1}=\cdots=\mu_{2j}
. Letting

\Omega^{(2j)}({\boldsymbol{k}})\equiv\sum_{\mu}\Omega_{\mu\cdots\mu}^{(2j)}({\boldsymbol{k}})

(51)

and converting the sums to integrals, we may write

\displaystyle\delta_{2j}V_{QG}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{L^{2d}}{2^{d}d}\int_{2\Gamma}\frac{d^{d}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,|I_{\boldsymbol{p}}|^{2}p^{2j}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{d^{d}{\boldsymbol{k}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\Omega^{(2j)}({\boldsymbol{k}})+O(\ell^{d-2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{2j-d}}{(2j-1)\pi}k_{F}^{2j-1}\ell^{d-1}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{d^{d}{\boldsymbol{k}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\Omega^{(2j)}({\boldsymbol{k}})+O(\ell^{d-2}).

(52)

Altogether,

V_{QG}^{(2)}=\bar{\Omega}\ell^{d-1}+O(\ell^{d-2})

(53)

where

\bar{\Omega}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{2j-d}k_{F}^{2j-1}}{(2j-1)\pi}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{d^{d}{\boldsymbol{k}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\Omega^{(2j)}({\boldsymbol{k}}).

(54)

Like the quantum geometric entanglement entropy,
V_{QG}^{(2)}
follows an area law. Moreover, if
\Gamma
is small compared to the Brillouin zone, the quantum metric term dominates. In other words

V_{QG}^{(2)}\approx 2^{2-d}(k_{F}/\pi)\ell^{d-1}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{d^{d}{\boldsymbol{k}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\Tr g({\boldsymbol{k}})

(55)

for small
k_{F}
and large
\ell
. This allows for a direct, practical probe of the quantum metric: by slightly doping a band and measuring the particle number variance at large
\ell
, one directly measures
\Tr g({\boldsymbol{k}})
near the band bottom.

To conclude, particle number fluctuations give an experimental measure of the entanglement entropy via Eq. (
46
), which converges rapidly at large
N
. In an experiment, the leading
\ell^{d-1}\ln\ell
behavior must be subtracted to extract the quantum geometric contribution. We confirmed that the quantum geometric contribution to particle number variance follows an area law (like
S_{QG}
) and increases with quantum geometry, and we arrived at a simple probe of the quantum metric near the band bottom, Eq. (
55
).

VIII
Relation to Wannier spread

A partial understanding of the relation between quantum geometric entanglement entropy and quantum geometric quantities such as
\int\Tr g({\boldsymbol{k}})
can come from considering maximally localized Wannier orbitals. The Wannier spread (taken to mean the real-space variance of a Wannier orbital) for a single isolated non-topological band can be written as

\Omega=\Omega_{I}+\tilde{\Omega}

(56)

where we’ve adopted the notation of Ref.
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
. Here

\Omega_{I}=V\int_{\text{BZ}}\frac{d^{d}{\boldsymbol{k}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\Tr g({\boldsymbol{k}})

(57)

is a gauge-invariant piece depending on the quantum metric and
\tilde{\Omega}
depends on the Berry connection:

\tilde{\Omega}=V\int_{\text{BZ}}\frac{d^{d}{\boldsymbol{k}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}|\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{r}}|^{2}

(58)

where
{\boldsymbol{r}}=V\int_{BZ}\frac{d^{d}{\boldsymbol{k}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. The gauge choice which minimizes
\tilde{\Omega}
(and thus
\Omega
) is the transverse gauge
\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=0

Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
. For
d=2
or
3
, this is equivalent to choosing the Berry connection by solving the Laplace equation

\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

(59)

where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\times\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is the Berry curvature, with suitable generalizations for other
d
. We emphasize that while Eq. (
58
) is gauge-dependent, its
minimum
is gauge-invariant and depends directly on the Berry curvature.

Entanglement entropy across a region
A
can be thought of as a measure of the number of modes straddling the boundary of
A
. It is gauge-invariant and invariant under unitary rotations in the Hilbert subspace of occupied states. For a filled non-topological band, one is free to consider the basis of maximally localized Wannier orbitals, in which case one may speculate that
S_{QG}
should be area law and roughly proportional to
\Omega
for the maximally localized Wannier orbitals, which is defined entirely in terms of gauge-invariant quantum geometric data.

We remark, however, that in the previous sections we have provided several counterexamples to an exact
S_{QG}\sim\Omega\ell^{d-1}
scaling relation. The exact expression of
S_{QG}
in terms of quantum geometric data is an interesting problem for future research.

The picture discussed above breaks down for topological bands and fractional band filling. For topological bands, there is no basis of exponentially localized Wannier orbitals, and such a basis generally mixes states of all momenta. However, the previous sections have shown that an area law scaling of
S_{QG}
holds in these cases as well.

IX
Discussion

To summarize, we have introduced the
quantum geometric entanglement entropy

S_{QG}
which captures the contribution of quantum geometry to real-space von Neumann entanglement entropy for noninteracting fermion systems. For the case of uniform quantum geometry an explicit form was given, and shown to be area-law, asymptotically in the linear size of the entangling region. We also demonstrated the area-law behavior for the SSH model, the 2D massive Dirac cone, and the QWZ model.
S_{QG}
was shown to resemble, but not precisely match, various quantum geometric quantities such as
\int\Tr g(k)
, which in turn is related to the minimal Wannier orbital spread for a non-topological band. Evidently
S_{QG}
is a refined measure of quantum geometry which is sensitive to all orders in momentum separation, unlike the quantum metric and Berry curvature, which are second-order in derivatives. Finally, we related
S_{QG}
to particle number fluctuations, which are measurable using a quantum point contact. A corollary was a direct way to measure the quantum metric at the bottom of a band using the particle number variance.

One interesting problem for future work is the explicit formula for the leading behavior of
S_{QG}
as a functional of
g({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{q}})\equiv\left\langle u_{\boldsymbol{k}}|u_{\boldsymbol{q}}\right\rangle
(we have completed the case
g({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{q}})=g({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{q}})
). The general case would amount to an analytical calculation of
S_{QG}
for all models where the Bloch states are known.

The interplay of weak interactions and quantum geometry from an entanglement viewpoint is also an interesting topic for future study. For example, what are the effects of underlying quantum geometry on the entanglement properties of a Fermi liquid? In this case, both real or momentum-space entanglement cuts could give nontrivial information
Flynn et al. 2022
.
