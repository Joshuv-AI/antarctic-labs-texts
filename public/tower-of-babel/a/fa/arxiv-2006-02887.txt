Foundations of regular coinduction
Francesco Dagnino

Abstract
. Inference systems are a widespread framework used to define possibly recursive predicates by means of inference rules. They allow both inductive and coinductive interpretations that are fairly well-studied. In this paper, we consider a middle way interpretation, called regular , which combines advantages of both approaches: it allows non-well-founded reasoning while being finite. We show that the natural proof-theoretic definition of the regular interpretation, based on regular trees, coincides with a rational fixed point. Then, we provide an equivalent inductive characterization, which leads to an algorithm which looks for a regular derivation of a judgment. Relying on these results, we define proof techniques for regular reasoning: the regular coinduction principle , to prove completeness, and an inductive technique to prove soundness, based on the inductive characterization of the regular interpretation. Finally, we show the regular approach can be smoothly extended to inference systems with corules , a recently introduced, generalised framework, which allows one to refine the coinductive interpretation, proving that also this flexible regular interpretation admits an equivalent inductive characterisation.

1.
Introduction

Non-well-founded structures, such as graphs and streams, are ubiquitous in computer science.
Defining and proving properties of this kind of structures in a natural way is a challenging problem.
Indeed, standard inductive techniques are not adequate, because they require to reach a base case in finitely many steps, and this is clearly not guaranteed, since non-well-founded structures are conceptually infinite.
The natural way to deal with such structures is by
coinduction
, the dual of induction, which allows non-well-founded reasoning.

A widespread approach to structure formal reasoning is by
inference rules
, which define the steps we can do to prove judgements we are interested in.
They support both inductive and coinductive reasoning in a pretty natural way:
in inductive reasoning we are only allowed to use finite derivations, while in the coinductive one we can prove judgements by arbitrary, finite or infinite, derivations,
hence coinductive reasoning can properly handle non-well-founded structures.

Coinductive reasoning is very powerful: it allows to derive judgements which require the proof of infinitely many different judgements.
For instance, consider the following inference rule used to prove that a stream contains only positive elements:

\ \displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{allPos}(s)}{\mathsf{allPos}(x\colon s)}\ \,x>0

To prove that the stream of all odd natural numbers contains only positive elements,
we can use the following infinite derivation:

\ \displaystyle\frac{\ \displaystyle\frac{\ \displaystyle\frac{\vdots}{\mathsf{allPos}(5:7:9:\ldots)}\ }{\mathsf{allPos}(3:5:7:\ldots)}\ }{\mathsf{allPos}(1:3:5\ldots)}\

which is valid in coinductive reasoning and contains infinitely many different judgements.

However, there are cases where, even though we need an infinite derivation, this derivation requires only the proof of
finitely many
different judgements.
This is often the case when dealing with cyclic structures, such as graphs or cyclic streams, since they are non-well-founded, but finitely representable.
For instance, if we want to prove that the stream of all 1’s contains only positive elements, we can use the following derivation:

\ \displaystyle\frac{\ \displaystyle\frac{\ \displaystyle\frac{\vdots}{\mathsf{allPos}(1:1:1:\ldots)}\ }{\mathsf{allPos}(1:1:1:\ldots)}\ }{\mathsf{allPos}(1:1:1:\ldots)}\

which is infinite, but requires only the proof of
\mathsf{allPos}(1:1:1:\ldots)
.

Borrowing the terminology from trees
[
Cou83
]
, we call a derivation requiring the proof of finitely many different judgments
regular
(a.k.a.
rational
1
1

1

The terms regular and rational are synonyms. However we will mainly use the second one for the model-theoretic approach, see Sect.
3
.
),
and we call
regular coinduction
(or
regular reasoning
) the approach that allows only regular derivations.

Whereas inductive and coinductive reasoning have well-known semantic foundations and proof principles, to our knowledge regular reasoning by means of inference rules has never been explored at the same extent. The aim of this paper is to fill this gap, by providing solid foundations also to the regular approach. Indeed, we believe that
the regular approach provides a very interesting middle way between induction and coinduction.

Indeed, inductive reasoning is restricted to finite derivations, but, in return, we implicitly get an (abstract) algorithm, which looks for a derivation of a judgement.
Such an algorithm is sound and complete with respect to derivable judgements. That is, it may not terminate for judgements that do not have a finite derivation, but it is guaranteed to successfully terminate, finding a finite derivation, for all and only derivable judgments.
Instead, coinductive reasoning allows also infinite derivations, but there is no hope, in general, to find an algorithm which succesfully terminates for derivable judgments, because, as we have seen, a derivation may require infinitely many different judgements to be proved
2
2

2

This is just an intuitive explaination. This fact has been proved for logic programs in
[
AD15
]
, which are a particular, syntactic, instance of general rule-based definitions considered in this paper.
.

Regular reasoning combines advantages of the two approaches:
on one hand, it is not restricted to finite derivations, going beyond limits of induction, but, on the other hand, it still has, like induction, a finite nature,
hence it is possible to design an algorithm which finds a derivation for all and only derivable judgments, as we will show in the following.

In detail, the contribution of this paper is the following.

•

An equivalent
model-theoretic
characterization of judgements derivable by a regular proof tree, showing it is an instance of the
rational fixed point

[
AMV06
]
.
This is important since it provides a purely semantic view of regular coinduction. Moreover, from
this we get a proof principle, the
regular coinduction principle
, which can be used to prove completeness of a set of inference rules against a set of valid judgements, that is, that all valid judgement are derivable by a regular proof tree.

•

An equivalent
inductive
characterization of judgements derivable by a regular proof tree.
Essentially, following the structure of the operational model of coinductive logic programming
[
SMBG06
,
AD15
]
,
but in the more abstract setting of rule-based definitions,
we enrich judgements by a finite set of
circular hypotheses
, used to keep track of already encountered judgements so that, when the same judgement is found again, it can be used as an axiom.
This nicely formalizes, by an abstract construction in the general setting of rule-based definitions and a correcteness proof given once and for all, techniques used in different specific cases for dealing with cyclic structures inductively, by detecting cycles to ensure termination.
Furthermore, this provides us with a sound and complete algorithm to find a regular derivation for a judgment, if any.
Finally, relying on the inductive characterization, we define a proof technique to show soundness of a set of inference rules against a set of valid judgements, that is, that all derivable judgements are valid.

Moreover, we show that all these results can be smoothly extended to a recently introduced generalisation of coinductive reasoning, namely
flexible coinduction

[
Dag17
,
ADZ17b
,
Dag19
]
.
Beside standard rules, this generalised framework allows also
corules
,
which are special rules used to validate infinite derivations.
As a result, using corules, we are able to filter out some undesired infinite derivations, having a much finer control on the set of derivable judgements.
Flexible coinduction smoothly extends standard coinduction, subsuming it, that is, standard coinduction can be recovered by a specific choice of corules.
We will show this is the case also in the regular case, that is, flexible regular coinduction subsumes standard regular coinduction.

2.
Inference systems and regular derivations

In this section, we recall basic definitions about inference systems
[
Acz77
,
LG09
,
San11
]
and their standard semantics,
and define their regular interpretation.

Let us assume a
universe

\mathcal{U}
, which is a set, whose elements
\mathit{j}
are called
judgements
.
An
inference system

\mathcal{I}
is a set of
(inference) rules
, which are pairs
{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr},\,\mathit{c}}\right\rangle}
, also written
\ \displaystyle\frac{\mathit{Pr}}{\mathit{c}}\
, where
\mathit{Pr}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
is the set of
premises
, while
\mathit{c}\in\mathcal{U}
is the
conclusion
.
A
proof tree
(a.k.a.
derivation
) in
\mathcal{I}
is a tree with nodes (labelled) in
\mathcal{U}
and such that, for each node
\mathit{j}
with set of children
\mathit{Pr}
, there is a rule
{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}
in
\mathcal{I}
.
The
inference operator

{\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}}:{\wp(\mathcal{U})}\rightarrow{\wp(\mathcal{U})}
is defined as follows:

\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(X)=\{\mathit{j}\in\mathcal{U}\mid\exists\mathit{Pr}\subseteq X.\ {\left\langle{\mathit{Pr},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}\}

A subset
X\subseteq\mathcal{U}
is
closed
if, for all rules
{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
, if
\mathit{Pr}\subseteq X
then,
\mathit{j}\in X
, that is,
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(X)\subseteq X
,
it is
consistent
if, for all
\mathit{j}\in X
, there is a rule
{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
, that is,
X\subseteq\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(X)
, and
it is an
interpretation
, if it is both closed and consistent, namely,
a fixed point
X=\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(X)
.

There are two main approaches to define interpretations of an inference system: the model-theoretic and the proof-theoretic one.
The two standard interpretations, the inductive and the coinductive one, can be equivalently defined in proof-theoretic and in model-theoretic style:

•

the
inductive interpretation

\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
is the set of judgements having a well-founded proof tree, and also the least fixed point of
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
, and

•

the
coinductive interpretation

\nu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
is the set of judgements having an arbitrary (well-founded or not) proof tree, and also the greatest fixed point of
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
.

In the following, we will write
\mathcal{I}\vdash_{\mu}\mathit{j}
for
\mathit{j}\in\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
and
\mathcal{I}\vdash_{\nu}\mathit{j}
for
\mathit{j}\in\nu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
.

In this paper, we assume inference systems to be
finitary
, that is, all rules have a finite set of premises.
Under this assumption, well-founded proof trees are always finite and infinite proof trees are always non-well-founded
3
3

3

This is an immediate consequence of the König’s lemma.
, hence we will use this simpler terminology.

In the coinductive interpretation, since we allow arbitrary proof trees, we can derive judgements requiring infinitely many different judgements to be proved.
However, there are cases where we still need infinite derivations, but only of finitely many judgements.
This idea of an infinite proof tree containing only finitely many different judgements nicely corresponds to a well-known class of trees:
regular trees

[
Cou83
]
.
We say that a tree is
regular
if it has a finite number of different subtrees.
Then, we can define another set of judgements:

{defi}

[Regular interpretation]
The
regular interpretation
of an inference system
\mathcal{I}
is the set
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
of judgements having a regular proof tree.

In the following we will write
\mathcal{I}\vdash_{\rho}\mathit{j}
for
\mathit{j}\in\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
.
To ensure that the regular interpretation is well-defined, we have to check it is indeed an interpretation, namely, it is a fixed point of
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
. We refer to Sect.
4
for this proof.

Let us illustrate regular proof trees by a couple of examples.
Consider
\lambda
-terms, ranged over by
M,N
, with the usual full
\beta
-reduction
\to
.
Denote by
R(M)
the set of terms reachable from
M
, that is,
R(M)=\{N\mid M\to^{\star}N\}
, where
\to^{\star}
is the reflexive and transitive closure of
\to
, and by
S(M)
the subset of
R(M)
of those terms reachable in one step, that is,
S(M)=\{N\mid M\to N\}
.
Note that
S(M)
is necessarily finite as the set of redexes in any
\lambda
-term is finite, while
R(M)
may in general be infinite.
We say that a term
M
is
regularly reducible
if the set
R(M)
is finite.
In other words, this means that its evaluation graph of
M
is finite or, alternatively, its evaluation tree is regular, because the evaluation tree of
M
has one different subtree for any term reachable from
M
.
Obviously, all strongly normalising terms are regularly reducible, but also some non-normalising terms are regularly reducible,
for instance, so is the term
\Omega=\Delta\,\Delta
, where
\Delta=\lambda x.x\,x
, as it always reduces to itself.
Instead, a term like
\Omega_{1}=\Delta_{1}\,\Delta_{1}
, with
\Delta_{1}=\lambda x.x\,x\,x
, is not regularly reducible
as it reduces to larger and larger terms
(
\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{1}\,\Delta_{1}\to\Omega_{1}\,\Delta_{1}\,\Delta_{1}\to\ldots
).

We can define a judgement
\textsf{reg-red}(M)
characterising regularly reducible terms as the regular interpretation of the following rule:

\scriptstyle{\textsc{({step})}}\ \displaystyle\frac{\textsf{reg-red}(N_{1})\hskip 6.99997pt\ldots\hskip 6.99997pt\textsf{reg-red}(N_{k})}{\textsf{reg-red}(M)}\ \,\begin{array}[]{l}S(M)=\{N_{1},\ldots,N_{k}\}\end{array}

Indeed, the proof tree for
\textsf{reg-red}(M)
coincides with the evaluation tree of
M
, hence judgements derivable by regular proof trees coincides with regularly reducible terms.

As another example,
assume that we want to define the judgement
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{u},\delta)
, where
\mathit{G}
is a graph,
\mathit{v}
and
\mathit{u}
are nodes in
\mathit{G}
and

\mathit{v}

\mathit{u}

We represent a graph by its accessibility function

\mathit{V}

The judgement is defined by the following (meta-)rules, where we assume

\scriptstyle{\textsc{({empty})}}\ \displaystyle\frac{}{\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{v},0)}\ \,\begin{array}[]{l}\end{array}\hskip 15.00002pt\scriptstyle{\textsc{({adj})}}\ \displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v}_{1},\mathit{u},\delta_{1})\hskip 6.99997pt\ldots\hskip 6.99997pt\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v}_{n},\mathit{u},\delta_{n})}{\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{u},1+\min\{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n}\})}\ \,\begin{array}[]{l}\mathit{v}\neq\mathit{u}\\
\mathit{G}(\mathit{v})=\{\mathit{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{v}_{n}\}\end{array}

Hence, we need infinite derivations to handle cycles, and,

3.
The rational fixed point

In this section we define the rational fixed point in a lattice-theoretic setting, which will be the basis for the fixed point characterisation of the regular interpretation.
The construction we present in Def.
3
and Theorem
1
is an instance of analogous constructions
[
AMV06
,
MPW16
,
MPW19
]
developed in a more general category-theoretic setting.
We work in the lattice-theoretic setting, since it is enough for the aim of this paper and definitions and proofs are simpler and understandable by a wider audience.

First, we report some basic definitions on lattices, for details we refer to
[
DP02
]
.
A
complete lattice
is a partially ordered set
{\left\langle{\mathit{L},\,\sqsubseteq}\right\rangle}
where all
A\subseteq\mathit{L}
have a least upper bound (a.k.a.
join
), denoted by
\bigsqcup A
.
In particular, in
\mathit{L}
there are both a top element
\top=\bigsqcup\mathit{L}
and a bottom element
\bot=\bigsqcup\emptyset
.
Furthermore, it can be proved that all
A\subseteq\mathit{L}
have also a greatest lower bound (a.k.a.
meet
), denoted by
\bigsqcap A
.
In the following, for all
x,y\in\mathit{L}
, we will write
x\sqcup y
for the binary join and
x\sqcap y
for the binary meet.
The paradigmatic example of complete lattice is the power-set
\wp(X)
of a set
X
, ordered by set inclusion, where least upper bounds are given by unions.

An element
x\in\mathit{L}
is
compact
if, for all
A\subseteq\mathit{L}
such that
x\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup A
, there is a finite subset
B\subseteq A
such that
x\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup B
.
We denote by
\mathcal{C}(\mathit{L})
the set of compact elements in
\mathit{L}
.
It is easy to check that
\mathcal{C}(\mathit{L})
is closed under binary joins, that is, if
x,y\in\mathit{L}
are compact, then
x\sqcup y
is compact as well.
In the power-set lattice, compact elements are finite subsets.

An
algebraic lattice
is a complete lattice
{\left\langle{\mathit{L},\,\sqsubseteq}\right\rangle}
where each
x\in\mathit{L}
is the join of all compact elements below it, that is,

x=\bigsqcup\{y\in\mathcal{C}(\mathit{L})\mid y\sqsubseteq x\}
.
In other words, an algebraic lattice is generated by the set of its compact elements, since each element can be decomposed as a (possibly infinite) join of compact elements.
The power-set lattice is algebraic, since each element can be decomposed as a union of singletons, which are obviously compact.

Given a function
{\mathit{F}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
and an element
x\in\mathit{L}
, we say that

x
is a
pre-fixed point
if
\mathit{F}(x)\sqsubseteq x
,
a
post-fixed point
if
x\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(x)
, and
a
fixed point
if
x=\mathit{F}(x)
.
We are interested in a special class of functions, called finitary functions (a.k.a. Scott-continuous functions), defined below.
A subset
A\subseteq\mathit{L}
is
directed
if, for all
x,y\in A
, there is
z\in A
such that
x\sqsubseteq z
and
y\sqsubseteq z
, then
a
finitary
function
{\mathit{F}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
is a function preserving the joins of all directed subsets of
\mathit{L}
, that is,
for each directed subset
A\subseteq\mathit{L}
,
\mathit{F}(\bigsqcup A)=\bigsqcup\mathit{F}_{!}(A)
.
A finitary function is also monotone: if
x\sqsubseteq y
then the set
\{x,y\}
is directed and its join is
y
, hence we get
\mathit{F}(y)=\mathit{F}(x)\sqcup\mathit{F}(y)
, that is,
\mathit{F}(x)\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(y)
.
Monotone functions over a complete lattice have an important property: thanks to the Knaster-Tarski theorem
[
Tar55
]
, we know that they have both least and greatest fixed points, that we denote by
\mu\mathit{F}
and
\nu\mathit{F}
, respectively.
We will show that for a finitary function over an algebraic lattice we can construct another fixed point lying between the least and the greatest one.
In the following we assume an algebraic lattice
{\left\langle{\mathit{L},\,\sqsubseteq}\right\rangle}
.

{defi}

Let
{\mathit{F}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
be a finitary function.
The
rational fixed point
of
\mathit{F}
, denoted by
\rho\mathit{F}
, is the join of all compact post-fixed points of
\mathit{F}
, that is,
if
R_{\mathit{F}}=\{x\in\mathcal{C}(\mathit{L})\mid x\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(x)\}
,

\rho\mathit{F}=\bigsqcup R_{\mathit{F}}

Note that, since both compact elements and post-fixed points are closed under binary joins, we have that, for all
x,y\in R_{\mathit{F}}
,
x\sqcup y\in R_{\mathit{F}}
, but, in general,
\rho\mathit{F}
is not compact, because it is the join of an infinite set.

The following theorem ensures that the rational fixed point is well-defined, that is, it is indeed a fixed point.
Such result is a consequence of a general category-theoretic analysis
[
AMV06
]
, we rephrase the proof in our more specific setting as it is much simpler.

Theorem 1
.

Let
{\mathit{F}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
be a finitary function, then
\rho\mathit{F}
is a fixed point of
\mathit{F}
.

Proof 3.1
.

Since
\rho\mathit{F}
is defined as the least upper bound of a set of post-fixed points, it is post-fixed as well.
Hence, we have only to check that
\mathit{F}(\rho\mathit{F})\sqsubseteq\rho\mathit{F}
.

First, since
\mathit{L}
is algebraic we have
\mathit{F}(\rho\mathit{F})=\bigsqcup\{x\in\mathcal{C}(\mathit{L})\mid x\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(\rho\mathit{F})\}
, hence it is enough to prove that, for all
x\in\mathcal{C}(\mathit{L})
such that
x\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(\rho\mathit{F})
, we have
x\sqsubseteq\rho\mathit{F}
.
Consider
x\in\mathcal{C}(\mathit{L})
such that
x\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(\rho\mathit{F})
.
Note that
R_{\mathit{F}}
is a directed set, indeed, if
X\subseteq R_{\mathit{F}}
is finite, then
\bigsqcup X\in R_{\mathit{F}}
, hence, since
\mathit{F}
is finitary, we have
\mathit{F}(\rho\mathit{F})=\mathit{F}(\bigsqcup R_{\mathit{F}})=\bigsqcup\mathit{F}_{!}(R_{\mathit{F}})
.
Therefore,
x\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup\mathit{F}_{!}(R_{\mathit{F}})
and, since
x
is compact, there is a finite subset
W\subseteq R_{\mathit{F}}
such that
x\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup\mathit{F}_{!}(W)
.
Set
w=\bigsqcup W
, since
\mathit{F}
is monotone, we get
x\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup\mathit{F}_{!}(W)\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(\bigsqcup W)=\mathit{F}(w)
.
By definition,
w\in R_{\mathit{F}}
, namely, it is compact and post-fixed, hence we get
x\sqcup w\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(w)\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(x\sqcup w)
, since
\mathit{F}
is monotone.
Finally,
x\sqcup w
is compact, as it is the join of compact elements, hence
x\sqcup w\in R_{\mathit{F}}
, and this implies
x\sqsubseteq x\sqcup w\sqsubseteq\rho\mathit{F}
, as needed.

As for least and greatest fixed points, as an immediate consequence of Def.
3
, we get a proof principle to show that an element is below
\rho\mathit{F}
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
{\mathit{F}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
be a finitary function and
z\in\mathit{L}
, then,
if there is a set
X\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\mathit{L})
such that

•

for all
x\in X
,
x\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(x)
, and

•

z\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup X
,

then
z\sqsubseteq\rho\mathit{F}
.

Proof 3.2
.

If these conditions hold, then we have
X\subseteq R_{\mathit{F}}
, hence
z\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup X\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup R_{\mathit{F}}=\rho\mathit{F}
.

4.
Fixed point semantics for regular coinduction

In this section, we prove that the regular interpretation
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
of a (finitary) inference system
\mathcal{I}
(Def.
2
) coincides with the rational fixed point of the inference operator
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
.
Rather than giving an ad-hoc proof, we present a general framework where to express in a uniform and systematic way the equivalence between proof-theoretic and model-theoretic semantics, and then prove such equivalence for the regular case.
To do this, we need a more formal account of proof trees.
These definitions work for any inference system, even non-finitary ones.

A formal account of proof trees

To carry out the proof, we need a more formal account of proof trees (see
[
Dag19
]
for details).

A
tree language
on a set
A
is a non-empty and prefix-closed subset
L\subseteq A^{\star}
, that is, such that, for all
\alpha\in A^{\star}
and
x\in A
, if
\alpha x\in L
then
\alpha\in L
, hence, in particular, the empty sequence belongs to any tree language.

A
tree

\tau
on a set
A
is a pair
{\left\langle{r,\,L}\right\rangle}
where
L
is a tree language on
A
and
r\in A
is the
root
of the tree.
We set
\mathsf{N}(\tau)=L
and
\mathsf{r}(\tau)=r
.
Intuitively, a sequence
\alpha\in L
represents a node of the tree labelled by
\tau(\alpha)
, defined as the last element of the sequence
\mathsf{r}(\tau)\alpha
.
Therefore, a tree
\tau
on
A
induces a partial function from
A^{\star}
to
A
whose domain is a tree language.
Differently from the literature
[
Cou83
,
AAMV03
]
, our definition forces trees to be unordered and, more importantly, it ensures there cannot be two sibling nodes with the same label, we refer to
[
Dag19
]
, where these trees are called children injective, for a detailed comparison.
These two additional requirements will be essential to prove a crucial
result of this section, Theorem
9
, and are reasonable to define proof trees as we will see below.

Given a tree
\tau
and a node
\alpha\in\mathsf{N}(\tau)
, we denote by
\tau_{\mid_{\alpha}}
the
subtree of
\tau
rooted at
\alpha
, defined as the pair
{\left\langle{\tau(\alpha),\,\{\beta\in A^{\star}\mid\alpha\beta\in L\}}\right\rangle}
.
Hence
\tau
is
regular
iff the set
\mathsf{SubTr}(\tau)=\{\tau_{\mid_{\alpha}}\mid\alpha\in\mathsf{N}(\tau)\}
is finite.
We also define
\mathsf{chl}_{\tau}(\alpha)=\{\tau_{\mid_{\beta}}\mid\exists x\in A.\beta=\alpha x,\beta\in\mathsf{N}(\tau)\}
the set of
children
of
\alpha
in
\tau
and

\mathsf{dst}(\tau)=\mathsf{chl}_{\tau}(\varepsilon)
the set of
direct subtrees
of
\tau
, which are the children of the root of
\tau
.
Note that, for all
\alpha\in\mathsf{N}(\tau)
, we have
\tau(\alpha)=\mathsf{r}(\tau_{\mid_{\alpha}})
and
\mathsf{chl}_{\tau}(\alpha)=\mathsf{dst}(\tau_{\mid_{\alpha}})
.

Having these notations, we say that a tree
\tau
on the universe
\mathcal{U}
is a
proof tree
in
\mathcal{I}
iff, for all nodes
\alpha\in\mathsf{N}(\tau)
, we have

{\left\langle{\mathsf{r}_{!}(\mathsf{chl}_{\tau}(\alpha)),\,\tau(\alpha)}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
.
In the following, as it is customary, we often represent proof trees using stacks of rules, that is, if
{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr},\,\mathit{c}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
and
T=\{\tau_{i}\mid i\in I\}
is a collection of trees such that
\mathsf{r}_{!}(T)=\mathit{Pr}
and
\mathsf{r}(\tau_{i})=\mathsf{r}(\tau_{j})
implies
i=j
, we denote by

\ \displaystyle\frac{T}{\mathit{c}}\
the proof tree
\tau={\left\langle{\mathit{c},\,\mathsf{N}(\tau)}\right\rangle}
where

\mathsf{N}(\tau)=\{\varepsilon\}\cup\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathsf{r}(\tau_{i})\mathsf{N}(\tau_{i})

Finally, we say that a tree
\tau
is a
proof tree for a judgement

\mathit{j}\in\mathcal{U}
if it is a proof tree rooted in
\mathit{j}
, that is,
\mathsf{r}(\tau)=\mathit{j}
.

Relating proof-theoretic and model-theoretic semantics

We now present a systematic approach to define and relate proof-theoretic and model-theoretic semantics of inference systems.
Let
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}}
be the set of all (well-founded or not) proof trees in
\mathcal{I}
and, abusing a bit the notation, let
{\mathsf{r}}:{\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{U}}
be the function that maps a proof tree to its root.
Then the direct image and the inverse image along
\mathsf{r}
are
{\mathsf{r}_{!}}:{\wp(\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}})}\rightarrow{\wp(\mathcal{U})}
and
{\mathsf{r}^{\ast}}:{\wp(\mathcal{U})}\rightarrow{\wp(\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}})}
, respectively.
There is an adjunction
\mathsf{r}_{!}\dashv\mathsf{r}^{\ast}
, that is, for all
X\subseteq\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}}
and
Y\subseteq\mathcal{U}
,
\mathsf{r}_{!}(X)\subseteq Y
iff
X\subseteq\mathsf{r}^{\ast}(Y)
.
In other words,
\mathsf{r}_{!}
behaves as an abstraction function
[
CC77
]
.
We define the
tree inference operator

{\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}}:{\wp(\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}})}\rightarrow{\wp(\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}})}
as follows:

\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}(X)=\{\tau\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}}\mid\mathsf{dst}(\tau)\subseteq X\mbox{ and }{\left\langle{\mathsf{r}_{!}(\mathsf{dst}(\tau)),\,\mathsf{r}(\tau)}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}\}

It is easy to check that
\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}
is monotone.
This function behaves the same way as
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
, but keeps track of the trees used to derive the premises of the rule.
More formally,
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
and
\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}
are related by the following proposition, which is an immediate consequence of their definitions.

Proposition 3
.

\mathsf{r}_{!}\circ\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}=\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}\circ\mathsf{r}_{!}
and

\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}\circ\mathsf{r}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathsf{r}^{\ast}\circ\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
.

Corollary 4
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}}
and
Y\subseteq\mathcal{U}
, then

•

if
X\subseteq\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}(X)
then
\mathsf{r}_{!}(X)\subseteq\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(\mathsf{r}_{!}(X))
,

•

if
\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}(X)\subseteq X
then
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(\mathsf{r}_{!}(X))\subseteq\mathsf{r}_{!}(X)
, and

•

if
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(Y)\subseteq Y
then
\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathsf{r}^{\ast}(Y))\subseteq\mathsf{r}^{\ast}(Y)
.

It can be proved
4
4

4

We omit the proofs because the focus of the paper is on regular trees.
that
\mu\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}
is the set of well-founded proof trees, while
\nu\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}
is the set of all proof trees.
Then, the correspondence between proof-theoretic and model-theoretic approaches in the standard inductive and coinductive cases can be succinctly expressed by the equalities:

\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket=\mathsf{r}_{!}(\mu\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}})=\mu\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
and
\nu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket=\mathsf{r}_{!}(\nu\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}})=\nu\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
.

The regular case

First of all, we state a fundamental property of finitary inference systems:

Proposition 5
.

If
\mathcal{I}
is finitary, then
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
and
\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}
are finitary.

Proof 4.1
.

We do the proof for
\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}
.
Let
X\subseteq\wp(\mathcal{U})
be a directed subset.
Since
\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}
is monotone, we have
\bigcup{\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}}_{!}(X)\subseteq\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}(\bigcup X)
, hence we have only to check the other inclusion.
If
\tau\in\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}(\bigcup X)

\mathsf{dst}(\tau)\subseteq\bigcup X

is finitary, there is a finite subset

Then, since

Let us denote by
\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{I}}
the set of regular proof trees in
\mathcal{I}
.
Thanks to Prop.

\rho\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}

\rho\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}

Then, the theorem we have to prove is the following:

Theorem 6

\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket=\mathsf{r}_{!}(\rho\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}})=\rho\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
.

The first step of the proof is to show that the set of regular proof trees coincides with the rational fixed point of
\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}
.
To this end, we have the following characterization of post-fixed points of

Lemma 7

Let

Proof 4.2

We start from the left-to-right implication.
The inclusion

\tau

To prove the other inclusion, set
\tau\in X

The proof is by induction on

Base:

If

Induction:

If

The other implication is trivial: if

To prove

Thanks to Lemma

To prove the second equality of Theorem

To this end, assume a set

Note that functions
{\mathsf{dst}}:{\mathcal{T}_{A}}\rightarrow{\wp(\mathcal{T}_{A})}

Basically, we show that, starting from a graph structure on a subset of

In this context a graph is a function

Let

X
to
\wp(X)\times A
, but it is not.
Indeed, such a functor cannot have a final coalgebra, because this would imply the existence of a bijection between a set
Z
, the carrier of such a final coalgebra, and
\wp(Z)\times A
, which is not possible for cardinality reasons.
Here we manage to have a unique function making the diagram commute,
because we additionally require the function
{v}:{X}\rightarrow{A}
to be injective.

We can now prove Theorem
6
.

Proof 4.5

(Proof of Theorem
6
)
.

By Lemma
8
we get
\mathsf{r}_{!}(\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{I}})=\mathsf{r}_{!}(\rho\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}})
.
Recall that in
\wp(\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}})
, compact elements are finite subsets, hence the set of all compact elements is
\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}})
.
Then, by definition of the rational fixed point and since
\mathsf{r}_{!}
preserves arbitrary unions (it is a left adjoint), we get

\mathsf{r}_{!}(\rho\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}})=\bigcup\{\mathsf{r}_{!}(X)\mid X\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}})\mbox{ and }X\subseteq\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}(X)\}
.
Hence, if
X\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{I}})
and
X\subseteq\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}}(X)
,
\mathsf{r}_{!}(X)
is obviously finite and, by Corollary
4
, it is also post-fixed.
Therefore, by definition of the rational fixed point, we get
\mathsf{r}_{!}(X)\subseteq\rho\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
, and this proves
\mathsf{r}_{!}(\rho\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{I}})\subseteq\rho\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
.

To conclude the proof, we show that
\rho\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\mathsf{r}_{!}(\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{I}})
.
To this end, we just have to prove that, given a finite set
X\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{U})
such that
X\subseteq\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(X)
, each judgement
\mathit{j}\in X
has a regular proof tree.
Since
X\subseteq\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(X)
, for each
\mathit{j}\in X
, there is
\mathit{Pr}_{\mathit{j}}\subseteq X
such that
{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr}_{\mathit{j}},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
.
Hence, applying Theorem
9
, where
g
maps
\mathit{j}
to
\mathit{Pr}_{\mathit{j}}
and
v
is the restriction of the identity on
\mathcal{U}
to
X
, we get an injective function
{p}:{X}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}}
.
We have still to prove that
p(\mathit{j})
is a proof tree.
It is easy to prove by induction on
\alpha
that, for all
\alpha\in\mathsf{N}(p(\mathit{j}))
, there is
\mathit{j}^{\prime}\in X
such that
p(\mathit{j})_{\mid_{\alpha}}=p(\mathit{j}^{\prime})
.
Therefore,

{\left\langle{\mathsf{r}_{!}(\mathsf{dst}(p(\mathit{j})_{\mid_{\alpha}})),\,\mathsf{r}(p(\mathit{j})_{\mid_{\alpha}})}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{\mathsf{r}_{!}(\mathsf{dst}(p(\mathit{j}^{\prime}))),\,\mathsf{r}(p(\mathit{j}^{\prime}))}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{\mathit{Pr}_{\mathit{j}^{\prime}},\,\mathit{j}^{\prime}}\right\rangle}
, because
\mathsf{dst}\circ p=p_{!}\circ g
and
\mathsf{r}\circ p=v
, by Theorem
9
.
This proves that
p(\mathit{j})
is a proof tree in
\mathcal{I}
, as
{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr}_{\mathit{j}^{\prime}},\,\mathit{j}^{\prime}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
by hypothesis.

5.
An inductive characterization

Although the regular interpretation is essentially coinductive, as it allows non-well-founded derivations,
it has an intrinsic finite nature, because it requires proof trees to be regular, that is, to have only finitely many subtrees.
Given this finiteness, a natural question is the following:
is it possible to find a finite presentation of derivations for judgements belonging to the regular interpretation?
f a

In this section we show this is the case, by providing an inductive characterization of the regular interpretation.

The idea behind such an inductive characterisation is simple.
Regular trees are basically cyclic structures.
Usually, to deal with cyclic structures inductively, we need to use auxiliary structures to detect cycles, to ensure termination.
For instance, in order to perform a visit of a graph, we detect cycles by marking already encountered nodes.
The inductive characterization described below models such cycle detection mechanism in an abstract and canonical way, in the general setting of inference systems.
The idea is the following:
during the proof, we keep track of already encountered judgements and, if we find again the same judgement, we can use it as an axiom.

This approach is intuitively correct, since in a regular proof tree there are only finitely many subtrees, hence infinite paths
must
contain repeated judgements, and this mechanism is designed precisely to detect such repetitions.

We now formally define the construction and prove its correctness.
Let
\mathcal{I}
be a finitary inference system on the universe
\mathcal{U}
.
We consider judgements of shape
\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}
where
\mathit{H}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
is a finite set of judgements, called
circular hypotheses
, and
\mathit{j}\in\mathcal{U}
is a judgement.
Then, we have the following definition.

{defi}

The inference system
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}
consists of the following rules:

\scriptstyle{\textsc{({hp})}}\ \displaystyle\frac{}{\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}}\ \,\begin{array}[]{l}\mathit{j}\in\mathit{H}\end{array}\hskip 15.00002pt\scriptstyle{\textsc{({unfold})}}\ \displaystyle\frac{\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}\triangleright\mathit{j}_{1}\hskip 6.99997pt\ldots\hskip 6.99997pt\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}\triangleright\mathit{j}_{n}}{\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}}\ \,\begin{array}[]{l}{\left\langle{\{\mathit{j}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{j}_{n}\},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}\end{array}

Therefore, in the system
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}
, we have the same rules as in
\mathcal{I}
, that, however, extend the set of circular hypotheses by adding the conclusion of the rule as an hypothesis in the premises.
Furthermore,
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}
has also an additional axiom that allows to apply circular hypotheses.

The correctness of the construction in Def.
5
is expressed by the fact that, a judgement
\mathit{j}
has a regular proof tree in
\mathcal{I}
if and only if it has a finite derivation in
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}
without circular hypotheses, as formally stated by the next theorem.

Theorem 10
.

\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}\vdash_{\mu}\emptyset\triangleright\mathit{j}
iff
\mathcal{I}\vdash_{\rho}\mathit{j}
.

We prove a more general version of the theorem.
First of all, if
X\subseteq\mathcal{U}
, we denote by
\mathcal{I}_{\oplus X}
the system obtained from
\mathcal{I}
by adding an axiom for each element of
X
, hence we have

\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\oplus X}}(Y)=\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(Y)\cup X
, for all
Y\subseteq\mathcal{U}
.
Then, the left-to-right implication of Theorem
10
is an immediate consequence of the following lemma:

Lemma 11
.

If
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}\vdash_{\mu}\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}
then
\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}\vdash_{\rho}\mathit{j}
.

Proof 5.1
.

The proof is by induction on rules in
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}
.
There are two types of rules, hence we distinguish two cases:

(hp)
:

we have
\mathit{j}\in\mathit{H}
, hence
{\left\langle{\emptyset,\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}
, thus
\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}\vdash_{\rho}\mathit{j}
.

(unfold)
:

we have a rule
{\left\langle{\{\mathit{j}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{j}_{n}\},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
and, by induction hypothesis, we get
\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}}\vdash_{\rho}\mathit{j}_{i}
, for all
i\in 1..n
.
Since
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}}}\rrbracket
is a rational fixed point (Theorem
6
), for all
i\in 1..n
, there is a finite set
X_{i}
such that

\mathit{j}_{i}\in X_{i}\subseteq\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}}}(X_{i})=\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}}(X_{i})\cup\{\mathit{j}\}
.
Set
X=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, then
X
is finite,
X\subseteq\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}}(X)\cup\{\mathit{j}\}
, as
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}}
is monotone, and
\mathit{j}\in\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(X)
, because, by construction,
\{\mathit{j}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{j}_{n}\}\subseteq X
.
Thus, we get
X\cup\{\mathit{j}\}\subseteq\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}}(X)\cup\{\mathit{j}\}=\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}}(X)
, because
\mathit{j}\in\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(X)\subseteq\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}}(X)
, hence
X\cup\{\mathit{j}\}
is a post-fixed point of
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}}
, since it is monotone.
Therefore, since
X\cup\{\mathit{j}\}
is a finite post-fixed point,
by Prop.
2
and Theorem
6
, we get
\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}\vdash_{\rho}\mathit{j}
.

The proof of the other implication relies on an auxiliary family of functions indexed over
finite subsets of
\mathcal{U}
and finite graphs
{g}:{X}\rightarrow{\wp(X)}
, with
X\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{U})
, mapping
a judgement
\mathit{j}\in X
and a subset
S\subseteq X
to a tree whose nodes are judgements of shape
\mathit{H}^{\prime}\triangleright\mathit{j}^{\prime}
.
This function is recursively defined as follows:

\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}(\mathit{j},S)=\begin{cases}\ \displaystyle\frac{}{\mathit{H}\cup S\triangleright\mathit{j}}\ &\mathit{j}\in\mathit{H}\cup S\\[12.91663pt]
\ \displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}(\mathit{j}_{1},S\cup\{\mathit{j}\})\hskip 6.99997pt\ldots\hskip 6.99997pt\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}(\mathit{j}_{n},S\cup\{\mathit{j}\})}{\mathit{H}\cup S\triangleright\mathit{j}}\ &\begin{array}[]{l}\mathit{j}\notin\mathit{H}\cup S\\
g(\mathit{j})=\{\mathit{j}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{j}_{n}\}\end{array}\end{cases}

The function
\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}
enjoys the following properties:

Proposition 12
.

For all
\mathit{H}\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{U})
,
{g}:{X}\rightarrow{\wp(X)}
with
X\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{U})
,
\mathit{j}\in X
and
S\subseteq X
,

\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}(\mathit{j},S)
is defined.

Proof 5.2
.

Denote by
c(S)
the cardinality of the set
X\setminus(\mathit{H}\cup S)
.
We prove that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
S\subseteq X
, if
c(S)=n
then
\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}(\mathit{j},S)
is defined.
The proof is by induction on
n
.

Base:

If
c(S)=n=0
, then
X\subseteq\mathit{H}\cup S
, hence
\mathit{j}\in\mathit{H}\cup S
hence
\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}(\mathit{j},S)=\ \displaystyle\frac{}{\mathit{H}\cup S\triangleright\mathit{j}}\
.

Induction:

If
c(S)=n+1
, if
\mathit{j}\in\mathit{H}\cup S
then
\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}(\mathit{j},S)
is defined as before;
otherwise, we have
\mathit{j}\notin\mathit{H}\cup S
and, if
g(\mathit{j})=\{\mathit{j}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{j}_{k}\}
, then, for all
i\in 1..n
,
\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}(\mathit{j}_{i},S\cup\{\mathit{j}\})=\tau_{i}
by induction hypothesis, as
c(S\cup\{\mathit{j}\})=n
since
\mathit{j}\notin S
,
hence
\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}(\mathit{j},S)=\ \displaystyle\frac{\tau_{1}\hskip 6.99997pt\ldots\hskip 6.99997pt\tau_{n}}{\mathit{H}\cup S\triangleright\mathit{j}}\
, as needed.

Proposition 13
.

For all
\mathit{H}\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{U})
,
{g}:{X}\rightarrow{\wp(X)}
with
X\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{U})
,
\mathit{j}\in X
and
S\subseteq X
,
if
{\left\langle{g(\mathit{j}^{\prime}),\,\mathit{j}^{\prime}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
, for all
\mathit{j}^{\prime}\in X\setminus\mathit{H}
, then

\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}(\mathit{j},S)
is a finite proof tree for
\mathit{H}\cup S\triangleright\mathit{j}
in
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}
.

Proof 5.3
.

The proof is a straightforward induction on the definition of
\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}
.

We can now prove the following lemma, which concludes the proof of Theorem
10
.

Lemma 14
.

If
\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}\vdash_{\rho}\mathit{j}
then
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}\vdash_{\mu}\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}
.

Proof 5.4
.

If
\mathit{j}\in\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}}\rrbracket
, since
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}}\rrbracket=\rho\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}}
(Theorem
6
), we have that there exists a finite set
X\subseteq\mathcal{U}
such that
\mathit{j}\in X\subseteq\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\oplus\mathit{H}}}(X)=\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(X)\cup\mathit{H}
.
Then, for each
\mathit{j}^{\prime}\in X\setminus\mathit{H}
, there is
\mathit{Pr}_{\mathit{j}^{\prime}}\subseteq X
such that
{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr}_{\mathit{j}^{\prime}},\,\mathit{j}^{\prime}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
.
Define
{g}:{X}\rightarrow{\wp(X)}
by
g(\mathit{j}^{\prime})=\mathit{Pr}_{\mathit{j}^{\prime}}
, if
\mathit{j}^{\prime}\in X\setminus\mathit{H}
, and
g(\mathit{j}^{\prime})=\emptyset
otherwise.
Therefore, by Prop.
12
,
\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}(\mathit{j},\emptyset)
is defined and, by Prop.
13
, it is a finite proof tree for
\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}
in
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}
, hence we get
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}\vdash_{\mu}\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}
, as needed.

We conclude the section by discussing a more operational aspect of Def.
5
.
In this definition, we aimed at being as liberal as possible,
hence, the two types of rules are not mutually exclusive: for a judgement
\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}
with
\mathit{j}\in\mathit{H}
we can either apply the circular hypothesis or use a rule from
\mathcal{I}
.
Since here we are only interested in derivability, this aspect is not that relevant, however, it becomes more interesting from an algorithmic perspective.
Indeed, we can consider an alternative definition, where
we allow the second type of rule only if
\mathit{j}\notin\mathit{H}
,
in other words, we apply circular hypotheses as soon as we can.

In this way we would have less valid proof trees in
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}
, but the set of derivable judgements remains the same.
Indeed, Lemma
11
ensures soundness also of the deterministic version, because any proof tree in the deterministic version is also a proof tree in the non-deterministic one.
On the other hand, Lemma
14
ensures completeness of the deterministic version, because the functions
\mathsf{tr}^{\mathit{H}}_{{\left\langle{X,\,g}\right\rangle}}
build a proof tree in the deterministic version, since they perform the additional check
\mathit{j}\notin\mathit{H}
to apply rules from
\mathcal{I}
.

6.
Regular reasoning

In this section we discuss proof techniques for regular reasoning, which can be defined thanks to the results proved in Sect.
4
and Sect.
5
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a finitary inference system on the universe
\mathcal{U}
.
We are typically interested in comparing the regular interpretation of
\mathcal{I}
to a set of judgements
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
(
specification
).
In particular, we focus on two properties:

soundness:

all derivable judgements belong to
\mathcal{S}
, that is,
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket\subseteq\mathcal{S}
,

completeness:

all judgements in
\mathcal{S}
are derivable, that is,
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
.

For completeness proofs, we rely on the fixed point characterization of
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
(Theorem
6
).
Indeed, since
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket=\rho\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
, from Prop.
2
we get a proof principle, which we call the
regular coinduction principle
, expressed by the following proposition:

Proposition 15

(Regular coinduction)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
be a set of judgements, then
if, for all
\mathit{j}\in\mathcal{S}
, there is a finite set
X\subseteq\mathcal{U}
such that

•

X\subseteq\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(X)
, and

•

\mathit{j}\in X
,

then,
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
.

Proof 6.1
.

Immediate from Prop.
2
.

This is very much like the usual coinduction principle, but it additionally requires
X
to be finite.
The condition
X\subseteq\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(X)
can be equivalently expressed as follows:
for all
\mathit{j}\in X
, there is a rule
{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\mathit{Pr}\subseteq X
.

{exa}

To show how proofs by regular coinduction work, as first example we consider the introductory one: the definition of the judgement
\mathsf{allPos}(s)
, where
s
is a stream of natural numbers, which, intuitively, should hold when
s
is positive, that is, it contains only positive elements.
We report here the inference system
\mathcal{I}^{>0}
defining this predicate:

\ \displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{allPos}(s)}{\mathsf{allPos}(x{:}s)}\ \ x>0

The specification
\mathcal{S}^{>0}
is the set of judgements
\mathsf{allPos}(s)
, where
s
is rational, meaning that it has finitely many different substreams, and positive.
Then, the completeness statement is the following:

If
s
is rational and positive, then
\mathcal{I}^{>0}\vdash_{\rho}\mathsf{allPos}(s)
.

To prove the result, let
s
be a rational stream containing only positive elements and set
X_{s}=\{\mathsf{allPos}(s^{\prime})\mid s=x_{1}{:}\ldots{:}x_{n}{:}s^{\prime}\}
.
Clearly,
X_{s}
is finite, because
s
is rational, and
\mathsf{allPos}(s)\in X_{s}
, hence we have only to prove that it is consistent.
Let
\mathsf{allPos}(s^{\prime})\in X_{s}
, then
s^{\prime}=x{:}s^{\prime\prime}
, thus
s=x_{1}{:}\ldots{:}x_{n}{:}x{:}s^{\prime\prime}
, and so
x>0
, because it is an element of
s
, and
\mathsf{allPos}(s^{\prime\prime})\in X_{s}
, by definition of
X_{s}
, and this proves that
X_{s}
is a post-fixed point.
Therefore, by the regular coinduction principle we get the thesis.

Let us now focus on the soundness property.
If we interpreted
\mathcal{I}
inductively, we would prove soundness by induction on rules, but in the regular case this technique is not available, since it is unsound.
However, in Theorem
10
, we proved that
\mathit{j}\in\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
if and only if
\emptyset\triangleright\mathit{j}
is derivable in
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}
, which is interpreted inductively.
Therefore, we can exploit the induction principle associated with
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}
to prove soundness, as the following proposition states:

Proposition 16
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
be a set of judgements, then,
if there is a family
(\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{H}})_{\mathit{H}\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{U})}
such that
\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{H}}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\emptyset}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, and,
for all
\mathit{H}\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{U})
,

•

\mathit{H}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{H}}
, and

•

for all rules
{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
, if
\mathit{Pr}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}}
then
\mathit{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{H}}
,

then
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket\subseteq\mathcal{S}
.

Proof 6.2
.

By induction on
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}
, we immediately get that
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}\vdash_{\mu}\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}
implies
\mathit{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{H}}
.
Therefore, if
\mathit{j}\in\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
, by Theorem
10
, we have
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft}\vdash_{\mu}\emptyset\triangleright\mathit{j}
, hence
\mathit{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{\emptyset}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
.

In other words, given a specification
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
, to prove soundness we have first to generalize the specification to a family of specifications,
indexed over finite sets of judgements, in order to take into account circular hypotheses.
Then, we reason by induction on rules in the equivalent inductive system (see Def.
5
) and, since
\mathcal{S}_{\emptyset}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, we get soundness.

{exa}

We illustrate the technique again on the definition of
\mathsf{allPos}(s)
.
The soundness statement is the following:

If
\mathcal{I}^{>0}\vdash_{\rho}\mathsf{allPos}(s)
, then
s
is rational and positive.

The first step is to generalize the specification to a family of sets
\mathcal{S}^{>0}_{\mathit{H}}
, indexed over finite subsets of judgements
\mathit{H}
.

\mathsf{allPos}(s)\in\mathcal{S}^{>0}_{\mathit{H}}
iff
either
s
is rational and positive,
or
s=x_{1}{:}\ldots{:}x_{n}{:}s^{\prime}
with
x_{i}>0
, for all
i\in 1..n
, and
\mathsf{allPos}(s^{\prime})\in\mathit{H}
.

It is easy to see that
\mathcal{S}^{>0}_{\emptyset}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{>0}
and, for all
\mathit{H}\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{U})
,
\mathit{H}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{>0}_{\mathit{H}}
, by definition of
\mathcal{S}^{>0}_{\mathit{H}}
.
Hence, we have only to check that the sets
\mathcal{S}^{>0}_{\mathit{H}}
are closed with respect to the rule, as formulated in Prop.
16
.

Let us assume
\mathsf{allPos}(s)\in\mathcal{S}^{>0}_{\mathit{H}^{\prime}}
, with
\mathit{H}^{\prime}=\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathsf{allPos}(x{:}s)\}
and
x>0
.
We have the following cases:

•

If
s
is rational and positive, this is true for
x{:}s
as well, because
x>0
by hypothesis.

•

If
s=x_{1}{:}\ldots{:}x_{n}{:}s^{\prime}
with
x_{i}>0
, for all
i\in 1..n
, and
\mathsf{allPos}(s^{\prime})\in\mathit{H}^{\prime}
, then,
if
\mathsf{allPos}(s^{\prime})\in\mathit{H}
, since
x{:}s=x{:}x_{1}{:}\ldots{:}x_{n}{:}s^{\prime}
and
x>0
, we have the thesis;
if
s^{\prime}=x{:}s
then
x{:}s=x{:}x_{1}{:}\ldots{:}x_{n}{:}x{:}s
, thus it is rational and positive, as
x>0
.

We now consider a more complex example: the definition of the distance in a graph (see page
2
), proving it is sound and complete with respect to the expected meaning.

{exa}

For the reader’s convenience, we report here the rules defining this judgement:

\scriptstyle{\textsc{({empty})}}\ \displaystyle\frac{}{\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{v},0)}\ \,\begin{array}[]{l}\end{array}\hskip 13.87491pt\scriptstyle{\textsc{({adj})}}\ \displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v}_{1},\mathit{u},\delta_{1})\hskip 6.47491pt\ldots\hskip 6.47491pt\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v}_{n},\mathit{u},\delta_{n})}{\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{u},1+\min\{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n}\})}\ \,\begin{array}[]{l}\mathit{v}\neq\mathit{u}\\
\mathit{G}(\mathit{v})=\{\mathit{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{v}_{n}\}\end{array}

We denote by
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{dist}}
the above inference system.
We recall for the reader’s convenicence a few definitions we need in the proof.
Let us assume a graph
{\mathit{G}}:{\mathit{V}}\rightarrow{\wp(\mathit{V})}
.
An edge in
\mathit{G}
is a pair
{\left\langle{\mathit{v},\,\mathit{u}}\right\rangle}
such that
\mathit{u}\in\mathit{G}(\mathit{v})
, often written
\mathit{v}\mathit{u}
. We denote by
\mathit{E}
the set of edges in
\mathit{G}
.
A path from
\mathit{v}_{0}
to
\mathit{u}_{n}
in
\mathit{G}
is a non-empty finite sequence of nodes
\alpha=\mathit{v}_{0}\ldots\mathit{v}_{n}
with
n\geq 0
, such that, for all
i\in 1..n
,
\mathit{v}_{i-1}\mathit{v}_{i}\in\mathit{E}
.
The empty path starting from the node
\mathit{v}
to itself is the sequence
\mathit{v}
.
If
\alpha
is a path in
\mathit{G}
, then we denote by
\|{\alpha}\|
the length of
\alpha
, that is, the number of edges in
\alpha
, and we write
\mathit{v}\in\alpha
when the node
\mathit{v}
occurs in
\alpha
, that is, the path
\alpha
traverses
\mathit{v}
.
The distance from a node
\mathit{v}
to a node
\mathit{u}
, denoted by
\delta(\mathit{v},\mathit{u})
, is the least length of a path from
\mathit{v}
to
\mathit{u}
, that is,

\delta(\mathit{v},\mathit{u})=\min\{\|{\alpha}\|\mid\alpha\mbox{ is a path from $\mathit{v}$ to $\mathit{u}$}\}
,
hence, if there is no path from
\mathit{v}
to
\mathit{u}
,
\delta(\mathit{v},\mathit{u})=\min\emptyset=\infty
.
We say a path
\alpha=\mathit{v}_{0}\ldots\mathit{v}_{n}
is
simple
if it visits every node at most once, that is,
\mathit{v}_{i}=\mathit{v}_{j}
implies
i=j
, for all
i,j\in 0..n
.
Note that the empty path is trivially simple.
It is also important to note that
\delta(\mathit{v},\mathit{u})
is the least length of a simple path from
\mathit{v}
to
\mathit{u}
.
Then, the specification
\mathcal{D}
is the set of judgements
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{u},\delta)
with
\delta=\delta(\mathit{v},\mathit{u})
.

We can now state that the definition of
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{u},\delta)
is sound and complete with respect to the specification
\mathcal{D}
.

\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{dist}}\vdash_{\rho}\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{u},\delta)
iff
\delta=\delta(\mathit{v},\mathit{u})
.

Completeness proof

The proof is by regular coinduction.
Let us consider a judgement
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{u},\delta(\mathit{v},\mathit{u}))
.
Let
R_{\mathit{v}}\subseteq\mathit{V}
be the set of nodes reachable from
\mathit{v}
and let us define
X_{\mathit{v}}=\{\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v}^{\prime},\mathit{u},\delta(\mathit{v}^{\prime},\mathit{u}))\mid\mathit{v}^{\prime}\in R_{\mathit{v}}\}
.
This set is clearly finite and, moreover,
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{u},\delta(\mathit{v},\mathit{u}))\in X_{\mathit{v}}
, because
\mathit{v}
is reachable from itself.
Hence, we have only to prove that
X_{\mathit{v}}
is a post-fixed point.
Let
\mathit{v}^{\prime}\in R_{\mathit{v}}
, then we have to find a rule with conclusion
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v}^{\prime},\mathit{u},\delta(\mathit{v}^{\prime},\mathit{u}))
and whose premises are in
X_{\mathit{v}}
.
We have two cases:

•

If
\mathit{v}^{\prime}=\mathit{u}
, then
\delta(\mathit{v}^{\prime},\mathit{u})=0
and so we have the thesis by rule
(empty)
.

•

If
\mathit{v}^{\prime}\neq\mathit{u}
, then
we have
\delta(\mathit{v}^{\prime},\mathit{u})=1+\min\{\delta(\mathit{v}^{\prime\prime},\mathit{u})\mid\mathit{v}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathit{G}(\mathit{v}^{\prime})\}
, hence,
since
\mathit{G}(\mathit{v}^{\prime})\subseteq R_{\mathit{v}}
, all the premises
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v}^{\prime\prime},\mathit{u},\delta(\mathit{v}^{\prime\prime},\mathit{u}))
, for
\mathit{v}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathit{G}(\mathit{v}^{\prime})
, belong to
X_{\mathit{v}}
, as needed.

Soundness proof

To apply Prop.
16
, we generalize the specification
\mathcal{D}
to a family of sets
\mathcal{D}_{\mathit{H}}
, indexed over finite sets of judgements, defined below.

(\star)
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{u},\delta)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathit{H}}
iff there is a set of paths
P
and a function
{f}:{P}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}}
such that

(1)

for all
\alpha\in P
,
either
\alpha
goes from
\mathit{v}
to
\mathit{u}
and
f(\alpha)=0
,
or
\alpha
goes from
\mathit{v}
to
\mathit{v}^{\prime}
and
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v}^{\prime},\mathit{u},f(\alpha))\in\mathit{H}
;

(2)

for each simple path
\beta
from
\mathit{v}
to
\mathit{u}
, there is
\alpha\in P
such that
\beta=\alpha\beta^{\prime}
;

(3)

\delta=\min\{\|{\alpha}\|+f(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in P\}
.

First, we have to check that
\mathcal{D}_{\emptyset}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
.
Let
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{u},\delta)\in\mathcal{D}_{\emptyset}
, then, by Item
3
of
(\star)
,
\delta=\min\{\|{\alpha}\|+f(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in P\}
, for some set of paths
P
and function
{f}:{P}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}}
.
Since
\mathit{H}
is empty, by Item
1
of
(\star)
, we have that, for all
\alpha\in P
,
\alpha
is a path from
\mathit{v}
to
\mathit{u}
and
f(\alpha)=0
, hence
\delta(\mathit{v},\mathit{u})\leq\|{\alpha}\|+f(\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in P
and so
\delta(\mathit{v},\mathit{u})\leq\delta
.
To prove the other inequality, let
\beta
be a simple path from
\mathit{v}
to
\mathit{u}
, then,
by Item
2
of
(\star)
, there is
\alpha\in P
such that
\beta=\alpha\beta^{\prime}
, but, by Item
1
of
(\star)
,
\alpha
goes from
\mathit{v}
to
\mathit{u}
and
f(\alpha)=0
, hence
\beta=\alpha
, because it cannot traverse twice
\mathit{u}
;
thus we have
\delta\leq\|{\beta}\|=\|{\alpha}\|+f(\alpha)
, for any simple path
\beta
, and so
\delta\leq\delta(\mathit{v},\mathit{u})
.

The fact that
\mathit{H}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\mathit{H}}
is immediate because, if
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{u},\delta)\in\mathit{H}
, then, to get the thesis, it is enough to take as
P
the set containing only the empty path with

, which trivially satisfies all conditions in
(\star)
.

Then, we have only to check that all sets
\mathcal{D}_{\mathit{H}}
are closed with respect to the rules
(empty)
and
(adj)
, as formulated in Prop.
16
.

(empty)
:

If
\mathit{v}=\mathit{u}
and
\delta=0
, then it is enough to take as
P
the set containing only the empty path, with
f(\mathit{v})=0
.

(adj)
:

We have
\mathit{v}\neq\mathit{u}
,
\mathit{G}(\mathit{v})=\{\mathit{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{v}_{n}\}
and, for all
i\in 1..n
,
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v}_{i},\mathit{u},\delta_{i})\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathit{H}^{\prime}}
with
\mathit{H}^{\prime}=\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v},\mathit{u},\delta)\}
.
If
n=0
, then
\mathit{G}(\mathit{v})
is empty,
\delta=\min\emptyset=\infty
and there is no path from
\mathit{v}
to
\mathit{u}
.
Hence, the thesis follows by taking
P=\emptyset
.

Then, let us assume
n\geq 1
.
By hypothesis,
\delta=1+\min\{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n}\}=1+\delta_{k}
,
for some
k\in 1..n
, since we are considering rule
(adj)
.
Since
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathit{v}_{i},\mathit{u},\delta_{i})\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathit{H}^{\prime}}
, for all
i\in 1..n
, there are
P_{i}
and
{f_{i}}:{P_{i}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}}
satisfying
(\star)
, in particular, by Item
3
, we have that
\delta_{i}=\min\{\|{\alpha}\|+f_{i}(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in P_{i}\}
.
We define
P
as the set of paths
\mathit{v}\alpha
with
\alpha\in P_{i}
such that, if
\alpha
ends in
\mathit{v}
, then
f_{i}(\alpha)\neq\delta
, and

{f}:{P}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}}
is defined by
f(\mathit{v}\alpha)=f_{i}(\alpha)
when
\alpha\in P_{i}
.
Clearly,
P
satisfies Item
1
of
(\star)
with respect to
\mathit{H}
.
To check that Item
2
holds, let
\beta
be a simple path from
\mathit{v}
to
\mathit{u}
, then
\beta=\mathit{v}\mathit{v}_{i}\beta^{\prime}
, for some
i\in 1..n
.
Hence,
\mathit{v}_{i}\beta^{\prime}
is a simple path from
\mathit{v}_{i}
to
\mathit{u}
and
\mathit{v}\notin\mathit{v}_{i}\beta^{\prime}
, thus, by Item
2
of
(\star)
applied to
P_{i}
, there is
\alpha^{\prime}\in P_{i}
such that
\mathit{v}_{i}\beta^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime}\gamma
,
and
\mathit{v}\notin\alpha^{\prime}
, because
\mathit{v}\notin\mathit{v}_{i}\beta^{\prime}
.
Therefore,
\mathit{v}\alpha^{\prime}\in P
and
\mathit{v}\alpha^{\prime}\gamma=\mathit{v}\mathit{v}_{i}\beta^{\prime}=\beta
, as needed.

We now prove Item
3
of
(\star)
, that is,
\delta=\min\{\|{\alpha}\|+f(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in P\}
.
Let
\alpha=\mathit{v}\mathit{v}_{i}\alpha^{\prime}\in P
, for some

, then
\mathit{v}_{i}\alpha^{\prime}\in P_{i}
, hence,
\delta_{k}\leq\delta_{i}\leq\|{\mathit{v}_{i}\alpha^{\prime}}\|+f_{i}(\mathit{v}_{i}\alpha^{\prime})
, thus

\delta=1+\delta_{k}\leq 1+\|{\mathit{v}_{i}\alpha^{\prime}}\|+f_{i}(\mathit{v}_{i}\alpha^{\prime})=\|{\alpha}\|+f(\alpha)

and this implies

.
To conclude, we have to prove the other inequality,
hence we distinguish the following cases:

•:

if

, then

and this proves the thesis, since

for all

;

•:

otherwise,
\delta_{k}=\|{\alpha^{\prime}}\|+f_{k}(\alpha^{\prime})
, for some

If

7.
Flexible regular coinduction

Infinite derivations are a very powerful tool, which make it possible to deal with a variety of situations that cannot be handled by only finite derivations.
However, in some cases, they have an unexpected behaviour, allowing the derivation of intuitively incorrect judgements.
The same issue affects also regular derivations.
Let us explain this by an example.
Consider the following rules, defining the judgement
\mathsf{min}(x,l)
, where
x
is an integer and
s
is a rational stream, stating that
x
is the minimum of the stream
s
.

\ \displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{min}(y,s)}{\mathsf{min}(z,x{:}s)}\ \,z=\min\{x,y\}

In Fig.
2
we report three infinite regular derivations, thus valid for the regular interpretation of the above rules,
where, however, only the first one is intuitively correct: judgements
\mathsf{min}(0,2{:}2{:}\ldots)
and
\mathsf{min}(1,2{:}2{:}\ldots)
should not be derivable, as
0
and
1
do not belong to the stream.

\ \displaystyle\frac{\ \displaystyle\frac{\ \displaystyle\frac{\vdots}{\mathsf{min}(2,2{:}2{:}2{:}\ldots)}\ }{\mathsf{min}(2,2{:}2{:}2{:}\ldots)}\ }{\mathsf{min}(2,2{:}2{:}2{:}\ldots)}\ \hskip 15.00002pt\ \displaystyle\frac{\ \displaystyle\frac{\ \displaystyle\frac{\vdots}{\mathsf{min}(1,2{:}2{:}2{:}\ldots)}\ }{\mathsf{min}(1,2{:}2{:}2{:}\ldots)}\ }{\mathsf{min}(1,2{:}2{:}2{:}\ldots)}\ \hskip 15.00002pt\ \displaystyle\frac{\ \displaystyle\frac{\ \displaystyle\frac{\vdots}{\mathsf{min}(0,2{:}2{:}2{:}\ldots)}\ }{\mathsf{min}(0,2{:}2{:}2{:}\ldots)}\ }{\mathsf{min}(0,2{:}2{:}2{:}\ldots)}\

Figure 2.
Some infinite regular derivation for the judgement
\mathsf{min}(x,s)
.

Inference systems with corules
[
ADZ17b
,
Dag19
]
have been recently designed precisely to address this issue for the coinductive interpretation, where arbitrary infinite derivations are allowed.
Beside standard inference rules, they introduce special rules, called
corules
, which allow one to refine the coinductive interpretation,
by filtering out some, undesired, infinite derivations.
More precisely, an
inference system with corules
, or
generalised inference system
, is a pair
{\left\langle{\mathcal{I},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\right\rangle}
where
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}
are inference systems, whose elements are called
rules
and
corules
, respectively.
A corule is also denoted by
\genfrac{}{}{1.5pt}{0}{\prem}{\conclu}
, very much like a rule, but with a thicker line.

The semantics of such a pair, denoted by
\nu\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
, is constructed in two steps:

•

first, we take the inductive interpretation of the union
\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}
, that is,
\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
,

•

then, we take the coinductive interpretation of
\mathcal{I}
restricted to
\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
.

In symbols, we have
\nu\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket=\nu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}}}\rrbracket
, where

\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{X}}
is the inference system obtained from
\mathcal{I}
by keeping only rules with conclusion in
X\subseteq\mathcal{U}
.

In terms of proof trees,
\nu\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
is the set of judgements with an arbitrary (finite or not) proof tree in
\mathcal{I}
, whose nodes all have a finite proof tree in
\mathcal{I}{\cup}\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}
.

In
[
Dag19
]
,
\nu\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
is proved to be an interpretation of
\mathcal{I}
, that is, a fixed point of
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
.

In this section, we show that the results previously given for regular coinduction smoothly extend to generalised inference systems.
The technical development in the following is partly repetitive; this could have been avoided by presenting the results in the generalized framework since the beginning.
However, to have separation of concerns, we preferred to first give a presentation using only standard notions, limiting to this section non-standard ones.

We start by defining the regular interpretation of an inference system with corules.

{defi}

Let
{\left\langle{\mathcal{I},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\right\rangle}
be an inference system with corules.
The
regular interpretation

\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
of
{\left\langle{\mathcal{I},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\right\rangle}
is defined by

\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket=\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}}}\rrbracket
.

As we will see later in this section (Corollary
21
), but it is not difficult to be convinced of it,
in proof-theoretic terms this is equivalent to say that
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
is the set of judgements with a regular proof tree in
\mathcal{I}
,
whose nodes all have a finite proof tree in
\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}
.
In this way, we can filter out some, undesired, regular derivations.
In the following, we will write
{\left\langle{\mathcal{I},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\right\rangle}\vdash_{\rho}\mathit{j}
for
\mathit{j}\in\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
.

Coming back to the example, using corules, we can provide a correct definition of the judgement
\mathsf{min}(x,s)
as follows:

\ \displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{min}(y,s)}{\mathsf{min}(z,x{:}s)}\ \,z=\min\{x,y\}\hskip 15.00002pt\genfrac{}{}{1.5pt}{0}{}{ \minElem{x}{x\cons s} }

The additional constraint, imposed by the coaxiom, allows us to build regular infinite derivation using only judgements
\mathsf{min}(x,s)
where
x
belongs to
s
;
thus filtering out the second and third incorrect proof trees in Fig.
2
, since they involve judgements with no finite derivation using also the coaxiom.

All the results discussed so far for the regular interpretation can be smoothly extended to the regular interpretation of an inference system with corules.
We will now develop all the tecnical machinery needed for this, adapting constructions in
[
ADZ17b
,
Dag19
]
to the regular case.

7.1.
Bounded rational fixed point

To construct such a fixed point, we come back to the lattice-theoretic setting of Sect.
3
.
Let us assume an algebraic lattice
{\left\langle{\mathit{L},\,\sqsubseteq}\right\rangle}
.

Let
{\mathit{F},\mathit{G}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
be two functions,
we write
\mathit{F}\sqcup\mathit{G}
for the pointwise join of
\mathit{F}
and
\mathit{G}
,
and, for all
z\in\mathit{L}
,
{\mathit{F}}_{{\sqcap}z}
for the function defined by
{\mathit{F}}_{{\sqcap}z}(x)=\mathit{F}(x)\sqcap z
.
It is easy to see that, if
\mathit{F}
and
\mathit{G}
are monotone, then
\mathit{F}\sqcup\mathit{G}
is monotone as well, hence, by the Tarski theorem, it has a least fixed point
\mu(\mathit{F}\sqcup\mathit{G})
.
It is also easy to check that if
z\in\mathit{L}
is a pre-fixed point of
\mathit{F}\sqcup\mathit{G}
, then it is a pre-fixed point of
\mathit{F}
as well, because
\mathit{F}(z)\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(z)\sqcup\mathit{G}(z)\sqsubseteq z
;
this will be crucial for the following construction.

We can now define the bounded rational fixed point:

{defi}

Let
{\mathit{F}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
be finitary and
{\mathit{G}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
be monotone.
The
rational fixed point bounded by
\mathit{G}
,
\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}]
is defined by

\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}]=\rho{\mathit{F}}_{{\sqcap}\mu(\mathit{F}\sqcup\mathit{G})}

In other words,
\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}]
is the least upper bound of all compact elements
below
the least fixed point of
\mathit{F}\sqcup\mathit{G}
, that is,

\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}]=\bigsqcup\{x\in\mathcal{C}(\mathit{L})\mid x\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(x),\,x\sqsubseteq\mu(\mathit{F}\sqcup\mathit{G})\}

To see that
\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}]
is well-defined, that is, it is indeed a fixed point of
\mathit{F}
, we have the following propositions:

Proposition 17
.

If
{\mathit{F}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
is finitary, then, for all
z\in\mathit{L}
,
{\mathit{F}}_{{\sqcap}z}
is finitary as well.

Proof 7.1
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathit{L}
be a directed set.
Since
\mathit{F}
is finitary, it is monotone, hence
{\mathit{F}}_{{\sqcap}z}
is monotone as well, therefore we get

\bigsqcup(\mathit{F}_{!}(D)\sqcap z)\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}\left(\bigsqcup D\right)\sqcap z
.
To prove the other inequality, it is enough to show that, for any compact element
y\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}\left(\bigsqcup D\right)\sqcap z
,
y\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup(\mathit{F}_{!}(D)\sqcap z)
, because the lattice is algebraic.
Since
\mathit{F}
is finitary, we have
\mathit{F}\left(\bigsqcup D\right)=\bigsqcup\mathit{F}_{!}(D)
.
We know that
y\sqsubseteq z
and
y\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}\left(\bigsqcup D\right)=\bigsqcup\mathit{F}_{!}(D)
and, since
y
is compact, there is a finite subset
W\subseteq D
such that
y\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup\mathit{F}_{!}(W)
.
Since
D
is directed and
W
is finite, there is
w\in D
such that
\bigsqcup W\sqsubseteq w
, hence
\bigsqcup\mathit{F}_{!}(W)\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(w)\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup\mathit{F}_{!}(D)
, because
\mathit{F}
is monotone and
w\in D
.
Therefore, we get
y\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(w)\sqcap z\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup(\mathit{F}_{!}(D)\sqcap z)
, as needed.

Proposition 18
.

Let
{\mathit{F}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
be finitary and
{\mathit{G}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
be monotone, then

\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}]
is a fixed point of
\mathit{F}
.

Proof 7.2
.

Set
z=\mu(\mathit{F}\sqcup\mathit{G})
and note that
\mathit{F}(z)\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(z)\sqcup\mathit{G}(z)=z
, as
z
is a fixed point of
\mathit{F}\sqcup\mathit{G}
.
By Prop.
17
,
{\mathit{F}}_{{\sqcap}z}
is finitary, hence, by Def.
7.1
and Theorem
1
we have
\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}]=\mathit{F}(\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}])\sqcap z
,
and from this we derive
\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}]\sqsubseteq z
and
\mathit{F}(\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}])\sqsubseteq\mathit{F}(z)\sqsubseteq z
.
Therefore, we get
\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}]=\mathit{F}(\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}])\sqcap z=\mathit{F}(\rho[\mathit{F},\mathit{G}])
, as needed.

In
[
ADZ17b
,
Dag19
]
the authors show that the least and the greatest fixed point are instances of the bounded fixed point.
Analogously, we show that the least and the rational fixed point are instances of the rational fixed point bounded by a function
\mathit{G}
, that is, they can be recovered for specific choices of
\mathit{G}
.
In the following, for all
z\in\mathit{L}
, we write
{K_{z}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
for the constant function, that is,
K_{z}(x)=z
, for all
x\in\mathit{L}
.

Proposition 19
.

Let
{\mathit{F}}:{\mathit{L}}\rightarrow{\mathit{L}}
be a finitary function, then the following hold:

(1)

\mu\mathit{F}=\rho[\mathit{F},K_{\bot}]
, and

(2)

\rho\mathit{F}=\rho[\mathit{F},K_{\top}]
.

Proof 7.3
.

To prove
1
, note that
\mu\mathit{F}\sqsubseteq\rho[\mathit{F},K_{\bot}]
, as
\rho[\mathit{F},K_{\bot}]
is a pre-fixed point, and

\rho[\mathit{F},K_{\bot}]\sqsubseteq\mu\mathit{F}
, as
\mu(\mathit{F}\sqcup K_{\bot})=\mu\mathit{F}
and
\rho[\mathit{F},K_{\bot}]=\mathit{F}(\rho[\mathit{F},K_{\bot}])\sqcap\mu\mathit{F}\sqsubseteq\mu\mathit{F}
.
To prove
2
, note that
\mu(\mathit{F}\sqcup K_{\top})=\top
, hence we have
{\mathit{F}}_{{\sqcap}\mu(\mathit{F}\sqcup K_{\top})}=\mathit{F}
, thus
\rho[\mathit{F},K_{\top}]=\rho\mathit{F}
, as needed.

7.2.
Fixed point semantics

Let
{\left\langle{\mathcal{I},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\right\rangle}
be an inference system with corules where
\mathcal{I}
is finitary.
We have two goals: first we want to justify the proof-theoretic characterisation provided at the beginning of this section and,
second, we want to prove that the rational interpretation generated by corules is indeed an interpretation of the first inference system.

To get the proof-theoretic characterisation, it is enough to observe the following property:

Proposition 20
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathcal{U}
, then

\tau\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{X}}}
iff
\tau\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{I}}
and, for all
\alpha\in\mathsf{N}(\tau)
,
\tau(\alpha)\in X
.

Proof 7.4
.

By definition we have
\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{X}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, hence
\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{X}}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{I}}
, and,
all rules in
\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{X}}
have conlcusion in
X
.
Then, the thesis is immediate since, by definition, all nodes of a proof tree are labelled by the conclusion of some rule.

Recall that we have described
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
in proof-theoretic terms as the set of judgements having a regular proof tree in
\mathcal{I}
, whose nodes all have a finite proof tree in
\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}
.
Formally, we have the following corollary:

Corollary 21
.

{\left\langle{\mathcal{I},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\right\rangle}\vdash_{\rho}\mathit{j}
iff there is
\tau\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{I}}
such that
\mathsf{r}(\tau)=\mathit{j}
and, for all
\alpha\in\mathsf{N}(\tau)
,
\tau(\alpha)\in\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
.

Proof 7.5
.

Set
X=\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
.
From Theorem
6
and Def.
7
, we get

\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket=\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{X}}}\rrbracket=\mathsf{r}_{!}(\rho\mathit{T}_{(\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{X}})})=\rho\mathit{F}_{\!(\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{X}})}=\mathsf{r}_{!}(\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{X}}})
.
Applying Prop.
20
with
X=\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
,
we get the thesis.

Towards the second goal, we show that the regular interpretation of
{\left\langle{\mathcal{I},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\right\rangle}
coincides with the rational fixed point of
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
bounded by
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}
(see Def.
7.1
), which is an immediate consequence of the following proposition:

Proposition 22
.

\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket=\rho[\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}},\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}]
.

Proof 7.6
.

By Def.
7
and Theorem
6
, we know that
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket=\rho\mathit{F}_{\!(\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}})}
.
By Def.
7.1
, we have
\rho[\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}},\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}]=\rho{(\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}})}_{{\sqcap}\mu(\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}\cup\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}})}
and, by definition of the inference operator, we have
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}=\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}\cup\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}
, hence
\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket=\mu(\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}\cup\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}})
and
{(\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}})}_{{\sqcap}\mu(\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}\cup\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}})}={(\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}})}_{{\sqcap}\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}
.
Therefore, as proved in
[
Dag19
]
, we have
\mathit{F}_{\!(\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}})}={(\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}})}_{{\sqcap}\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}
, which implies the thesis.

Then, this proposition, together with Prop.
18
, in particular ensures that
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
is indeed a fixed point of
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}
, that is, an interpretation of
\mathcal{I}
.

An important property of inference systems with corules is that standard interpretations (the inductive and the coinductive one) are particular cases.
Analogously, the inductive and the regular interpretations are particular cases of the regular interpretation generated by corules.
Let us denote by
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{U}}
the inference system containing one axiom for each
\mathit{j}\in\mathcal{U}
, that is,

{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{U}}
iff
\mathit{Pr}=\emptyset
.
We have the following proposition:

Proposition 23
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an inference system, then

\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket=\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\emptyset}\rrbracket
and
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket=\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{U}}}\rrbracket
.

Proof 7.7
.

It follows from Prop.
19
, because
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket=\rho[\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}},\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}]
, by Prop.
22
, and we have
\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{U}}}(X)=\mathcal{U}
and
\mathit{F}_{\!\emptyset}(X)=\emptyset
, for all
X\subseteq\mathcal{U}
.

In other words, when the set of corules is empty, we allow only rules with conclusion in
\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\emptyset}\rrbracket=\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
, hence we cannot derive anything outside
\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
,
and, on the other hand, when the set of corules is
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{U}}
, we do not remove any rule, because
\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{U}}}\rrbracket=\mathcal{U}
, thus we get exactly the regular interpretation of
\mathcal{I}
.

7.3.
Cycle detection for corules

As the standard regular interpretation, also the regular interpretation of an inference system with corules has a sound and complete algorithm to find a derivation for a judgment, if any, and may not terminate otherwise.

Let us assume an inference system with corules
{\left\langle{\mathcal{I},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\right\rangle}
.
Since its regular interpretation is defined as the regular interpretation of
\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}}
, which is the inference system obtained from
\mathcal{I}
by keeping only rules with conclusion in
\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
,
we could get an inductive characterisation of
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
by applying the construction in Def.
5
to the inference system
\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}}
.
This provides us with a sound and complete algorithm to find a derivation for a judgement which belongs to
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
, which works the same way as the one introduced in Sect.
5
, but, in addition,
each time we apply the rule
(unfold)
with
{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
, we have to check that
\mathit{j}\in\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
.
However, we will see that this additional check is necessary only to apply circular hypotheses, thus defining a cleaner procedure.

To this end we construct the inference system
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}
as follows:

{defi}

The inference system
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}
consists of the following rules:

\scriptstyle{\textsc{({b-hp})}}\ \displaystyle\frac{}{\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}}\ \,\begin{array}[]{l}\mathit{j}\in\mathit{H}\\
\mathit{j}\in\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket\end{array}\hskip 15.00002pt\scriptstyle{\textsc{({b-unfold})}}\ \displaystyle\frac{\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}\triangleright\mathit{j}_{1}\hskip 6.99997pt\ldots\hskip 6.99997pt\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}\triangleright\mathit{j}_{n}}{\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}}\ \,\begin{array}[]{l}{\left\langle{\{\mathit{j}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{j}_{n}\},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}\end{array}

This definition is basically the same as Def.
5
, except the additional side condition in rule
(b-hp)

\mathit{j}\in\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
, which enforces the additional check.
We have the following fundamental properties:

Proposition 24
.

If
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\vdash_{\mu}\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}
then
\mathit{j}\in\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
.

Proof 7.8
.

By induction on rules of
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}
:
the case for rule
(b-hp)
is trivial,
for the rule
(b-unfold)
, by Def.
7.3
, we have a rule
{\left\langle{\{\mathit{j}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{j}_{n}\},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
and, by induction hypothesis, we know that
\mathit{j}_{k}\in\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
, for all
k\in 1..n
, hence
\mathit{j}\in\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
, as
\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
is closed with respect to
\mathcal{I}
.

Proposition 25
.

If
\mathit{H}\subseteq\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
, then

\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\vdash_{\mu}\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}
iff
\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}}^{\circlearrowleft}\vdash_{\mu}\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}
.

Proof 7.9
.

The proof of the left-to-right implication is by induction on rules in
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}
.

(b-hp)
:

By hypothesis
\mathit{j}\in\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
, then the thesis follows by rule
(hp)
.

(b-unfold)
:

By Def.
7.3
, we have a rule
{\left\langle{\{\mathit{j}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{j}_{n}\},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
and by Prop.
24
we have
\mathit{j}\in\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
, hence
\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}\subseteq\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
and
{\left\langle{\{\mathit{j}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{j}_{n}\},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}}
.
Therefore, by induction hypothesis, we get
\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}}^{\circlearrowleft}\vdash_{\mu}\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}\triangleright\mathit{j}_{k}
, for all
k\in 1..n
, then the thesis follows by rule
(unfold)
.
Therefore, we get the thesis applying rule
(unfold)
.

The proof of the right-to-left implication is by induction on rules in
\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}}^{\circlearrowleft}
.

(hp)
:

Immediate by rule
(b-hp)
, as
\mathit{j}\in\mathit{H}\subseteq\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
.

(unfold)
:

By Def.
5
, we have a rule
{\left\langle{\{\mathit{j}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{j}_{n}\},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mid_{\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, hence
\mathit{j}\in\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
, and so
\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}\subseteq\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
.
Therefore, by induction hypothesis, we get
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\vdash_{\mu}\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}\triangleright\mathit{j}_{k}
, for all
k\in 1..n
, then the thesis follows by rule
(b-unfold)
.

Then, we get the following result, proving that the inductive characterisation is correct, that is, sound and complete, with respect to the regular interpretation of
{\left\langle{\mathcal{I},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\right\rangle}
.

Corollary 26
.

\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\vdash_{\mu}\emptyset\triangleright\mathit{j}
iff
{\left\langle{\mathcal{I},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\right\rangle}\vdash_{\rho}\mathit{j}
.

Proof 7.10
.

It is immediate by Prop.
25
and Theorem
10
.

The resulting algorithm behaves as follows:
we start from a judgement
\mathit{j}
with an empty set of circular hypotheses,
then we try to build a regular derivation for
\mathit{j}
using rules in
\mathcal{I}
, exactly the same way as for standard regular coinduction;
but, this time, when we find a cycle, say for a judgement
\mathit{j}^{\prime}
, we trigger another procedure, which looks for a finite derivation for
\mathit{j}^{\prime}
in
\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
.

7.4.
Flexible regular reasoning

We now adapt proof techniques presented in Sect.
6
to this generalised setting.
For completeness proofs, in
[
ADZ17b
,
Dag19
]
, the standard coinduction principle is extended to generalised inference systems, by adding an additional constraint, which takes into account corules.
The regular coinduction principle (Prop.
15
) can be smoothly extended to this generalised setting following the same strategy, as expressed in the next proposition.
We call the resulting principle the
bounded regular coinduction principle
.

Proposition 27

(Bounded regular coinduction)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
be a set of judgements, then
if, for all
\mathit{j}\in\mathcal{S}
, there is a finite set
X\subseteq\mathcal{U}
such that

•

X\subseteq\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
,

•

X\subseteq\mathit{F}_{\!\mathcal{I}}(X)
, and

•

\mathit{j}\in X
,

then,
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
.

This proposition immediately follows from Prop.
2
, as

\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
is a rational fixed point by Prop.
22
and Def.
7.1
.
The additional constraint
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
, named
boundedness
, reflects the fact that, using corules, we are only allowed to build proof trees using judgements in
\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket
.
Note that, when
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{U}}
, thus
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket=\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I}}\rrbracket
, the additional constraint is trivially true, because it requires
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
,
hence we recover the regular coinduction principle in Prop.
15
.

We illustrate this technique on our running example: the definition of
\mathsf{min}(x,s)
, which should hold when
x
is the minimum of the rational stream of integers
s
.
We denote by
\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{min}}
the set of judgements
\mathsf{min}(x,s)
where
x
is indeed the minimum of
s
.
We prove, using Prop.
27
, the following statement:

if
\mathsf{min}(x,s)\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{min}}
then it has a regular derivation with corules

Proof 7.11
.

Let
\mathsf{min}(x,s)\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{min}}
and define
X
as the set of judgements
\mathsf{min}(z,r)\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{min}}
such that
r
is a substream of
s
.
Trivially
\mathsf{min}(x,s)\in X
and, since
s
is rational, it has finitely many different substreams, hence
X
is finite.
The boundedness condition, that is, if
\mathsf{min}(z,r)\in X
then it has a finite proof tree using also the coaxioms, is easy to check, because,
if
y
is the minimum of
r
, then
y
occurs somewhere in
r
,
hence we can prove the thesis by induction on the least position of
y
in
r
.
In order to check that
X
is consistent, consider
\mathsf{min}(z,r)\in X
, with
r=y{:}r^{\prime}

Since
z
is the minimum of
r
and
r^{\prime}
is a substream of
r
,
z
is a lower bound of
r^{\prime}
, thus it has a minimum, say
y^{\prime}
, and so
\mathsf{min}(y^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in X
.
To conclude, we have to show that
z=\min\{y,y^{\prime}\}
.
The inequality
z\leq\min\{y,y^{\prime}\}
is trivial,
for the other inequality, since
z
belongs to
r
, we have two cases:
if
z=y
, then
\min\{y,y^{\prime}\}\leq z
,
otherwise
z
belongs to
r^{\prime}
and so
y^{\prime}\leq z
, thus
\min\{y,y^{\prime}\}\leq z
.

Differently from the standard coinductive interpretation, for the regular interpretation we have also defined a proof technique to show soundness (Prop.
16
).
Such a technique relies on the inductive characterisation of the regular interpretation.
As also the regular interpretation of an inference system with corules has an inductive characterisation (Corollary
26
), we can provide a proof technique to show soundness also in this generalised setting, which smoothly extends the one of standard regular coinduction.

Proposition 28
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
be a set of judgements, then,
if there is a family
(\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{H}})_{\mathit{H}\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{U})}
such that
\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{H}}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\emptyset}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, and,
for all
\mathit{H}\in\wp_{\omega}(\mathcal{U})
,

•

\mathit{H}\cap\mu\llbracket{\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{H}}
, and

•

for all rules
{\left\langle{\mathit{Pr},\,\mathit{j}}\right\rangle}\in\mathcal{I}
, if
\mathit{Pr}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathit{j}\}}
then
\mathit{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{H}}
,

then
\rho\llbracket{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\rrbracket\subseteq\mathcal{S}
.

Proof 7.12
.

By a straightforward induction on rules in
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}
, we get that if
\mathcal{I}^{\circlearrowleft\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{co}}}\vdash_{\mu}\mathit{H}\triangleright\mathit{j}
, then
\mathit{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{H}}
;
thus, the thesis follows from Corollary
26
.

Again, this proof principle is almost the same as Prop.
16
, but with an additional constraint, this time on sets of circular hypothesis, which takes into account corules.

We illustrate this technique proving that the definition of
\mathsf{min}(x,s)
is sound, that is,

if
\mathsf{min}(x,s)
has a regular derivation with corules then
x
is the minimum of
s
.

Proof 7.13
.

First of all, we note that, if
\mathsf{min}(x,s)
has a finite proof tree using also the coaxiom, then
x
belongs to
s
.
Then, we define
\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{min}}_{\mathit{H}}
as follows:

\mathsf{min}(x,s)\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{min}}_{\mathit{H}}
iff
x
is the minimum of
s
or
s=x_{1}{:}\ldots{:}x_{n}{:}r
,
\mathsf{min}(y,r)\in\mathit{H}
,
\mathsf{min}(y,r)
has a finite proof tree using also the coaxiom and
x=\min\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y\}
.
We have trivialy that
\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{min}}_{\emptyset}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{min}}
.

Assume a finite set of judgements
\mathit{H}
.
Clearly, if
\mathsf{min}(x,s)\in\mathit{H}
has a finite proof tree using also the coaxiom, then
\mathsf{min}(x,s)\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{min}}_{\mathit{H}}
.
Now, suppose
s=x{:}r
,
\mathit{H}^{\prime}=\mathit{H}\cup\{\mathsf{min}(z,s)\}
,
\mathsf{min}(y,r)\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{min}}_{\mathit{H}^{\prime}}
and
z=\min\{x,y\}
, then we have two cases:

•

if
y
is the minimum of
r
, then
z
is the minimum of
s=y{:}r
, hence
\mathsf{min}(z,s)\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{min}}_{\mathit{H}}
;

•

if
r=x_{1}{:}\ldots{:}x_{n}{:}r^{\prime}
,
\mathsf{min}(y^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in\mathit{H}^{\prime}
,
\mathsf{min}(y^{\prime},r^{\prime})
has a finite proof tree using also the coaxiom and
y=\min\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y^{\prime}\}
, then

s=x{:}r=x{:}x_{1}{:}\ldots{:}x_{n}{:}r^{\prime}
and
z=\min\{x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y^{\prime}\}
.
We distinguish two subcases:

–

if
\mathsf{min}(y^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in\mathit{H}
, then
\mathsf{min}(z,s)\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{min}}_{\mathit{H}}
by definition, and

–

if
y^{\prime}=z
and
r^{\prime}=s
, then
s=x{:}x_{1}{:}\ldots{:}x_{n}{:}s
and
\mathsf{min}(z,s)
has a finite proof tree using also the coaxiom, thus
z
belongs to
s
and
z=\min\{x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},z\}
, that is,
z
is the minimum of
s
.

We now consider a more involved example.

{exa}
[Addition of rational numbers]
It is well-known that real numbers in
[0,1]
can be represented as, not necessarily rational, streams of digits in some basis.
Let
\mathbb{N}_{>0}
be the set of positive natural numbers and assume a basis
b\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
.
A digit
d
is a natural number in
0..b-1
, then,
given a stream
r=(d_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}}
of digits, the series
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d_{i}b^{-i}
converges and its limit is the real number represented by
r
and denoted by
\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
.
It is also well-known that every real number
x\in[0,1]
has at most two different representations as a stream,
for instance, with
b=10
, the number
1/2
can be represented as either
5{:}\overline{0}
or
4{:}\overline{9}
, where, for any digit
d
,
\overline{d}
is the stream
d{:}d{:}d{:}\ldots
.

Consider the following inference system with corules, defining the judgement
\mathsf{add}(r_{1},r_{2},r,c)
, where
c
is an integer representing the carry, and which should hold when
\llbracket{r_{1}}\rrbracket+\llbracket{r_{2}}\rrbracket=\llbracket{r}\rrbracket+c
.

\ \displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{add}(r_{1},r_{2},r,c)}{\mathsf{add}(d_{1}{:}r_{1},d_{2}{:}r_{2},(x\mod b){:}r,x\div b)}\ \,x=d_{1}+d_{2}+c\hskip 15.00002pt\genfrac{}{}{1.5pt}{0}{}{ \addNum{r_1}{r_2}{r}{c} }\,c\in-1..2

In
[
ADZ17b
,
Dag19
]
it is proved that this definition is correct.
It is also well-known that rational streams of digits represent rational numbers, that is, if
r
is a rational stream of digits, then
\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
is a rational number.
We show here that the regular interpretation of the above inference system with corules is correct with respect to the addition of rational numbers.

Define the set
\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{add}}
of correct judgements as follows:

\mathsf{add}(r_{1},r_{2},r,c)\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{add}}
iff
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
and
r
are rational and
\llbracket{r_{1}}\rrbracket+\llbracket{r_{2}}\rrbracket=\llbracket{r}\rrbracket+c
.
We start by proving completeness, stated below:

for rational streams
r_{1},r_{2},r
, if
\llbracket{r_{1}}\rrbracket+\llbracket{r_{2}}\rrbracket=\llbracket{r}\rrbracket+c
then
\mathsf{add}(r_{1},r_{2},r,c)
has a regular derivation with corules.

We use the bounded regular coinduction principle.
First of all, note that, since
\llbracket{r}\rrbracket\in[0,1]
for any stream
r
, if
\mathsf{add}(r_{1},r_{2},r,c)\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{add}}
, then

c=\llbracket{r_{1}}\rrbracket+\llbracket{r_{2}}\rrbracket-\llbracket{r}\rrbracket
, hence
c\geq-1
and
c\leq 2
.
Therefore, we immediately have that all judgements in
\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{add}}
have a finite proof tree using also the coaxiom.

Assume
\mathsf{add}(r_{1},r_{2},r,c)\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{add}}
and define
X
as follows:

\mathsf{add}(s_{1},s_{2},s,c^{\prime})\in X
iff
\llbracket{s_{1}}\rrbracket+\llbracket{s_{2}}\rrbracket=\llbracket{s}\rrbracket+c^{\prime}
and
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are substreams of
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, respectively.
Trivially,
\mathsf{add}(r_{1},r_{2},r,c)\in X
and
X
is finite because,
since
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are rational, they have finitely many different substreams, and
c^{\prime}
can assume only four values, hence
\llbracket{s}\rrbracket=\llbracket{s_{1}}\rrbracket+\llbracket{s_{2}}\rrbracket-c^{\prime}
can assume only finitely many values, and so there are finitely many
s
satisfying that equation.
Now we have to check that
X
is consistent.
Assume
\mathsf{add}(d_{1}{:}s_{1},d_{2}{:}s_{2},d{:}s,c^{\prime})\in X
, then we have

\llbracket{d_{1}{:}s_{1}}\rrbracket+\llbracket{d_{2}{:}s_{2}}\rrbracket=\llbracket{d{:}s}\rrbracket+c^{\prime}
.
It is easy to check that, for any stream
t
and digit
d
,
b\llbracket{d{:}t}\rrbracket=d+\llbracket{t}\rrbracket
.
Hence, we get
\llbracket{s_{1}}\rrbracket+\llbracket{s_{2}}\rrbracket=\llbracket{s}\rrbracket+c^{\prime\prime}
, with
c^{\prime\prime}=bc^{\prime}+d-d_{1}-d_{2}
.
Since
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are still substream of
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, respectively, we get
\mathsf{add}(s_{1},s_{2},s,c^{\prime\prime})\in X
, as needed.

We now prove soundness, as stated below:

if
\mathsf{add}(r_{1},r_{2},r,c)
has a regular derivation with corules, then
\llbracket{r_{1}}\rrbracket+\llbracket{r_{2}}\rrbracket=\llbracket{r}\rrbracket+c
and
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
and
r
are rational stream.

For any finite set
\mathit{H}
, we define
\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{add}}_{\mathit{H}}
as follows:

\mathsf{add}(r_{1},r_{2},r,c_{0})\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{add}}_{\mathit{H}}
iff
r_{1}=d_{11}{:}\ldots{:}d_{1n}{:}s_{1}
,

r_{2}=d_{21}{:}\ldots{:}d_{2n}{:}s_{2}
,

r=d_{1}{:}\ldots{:}d_{n}{:}s
and,
there are
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in-1..2
such that,
for all
i\in 1..n
,
d_{1i}+d_{2i}+c_{i}=bc_{i-1}+d_{i}
, and
either
\mathsf{add}(s_{1},s_{2},s,c_{n})\in\mathit{H}
or
s_{1}=r_{1}
,
s_{2}=r_{2}
,
s=r
and
c_{0}=c_{n}
.
The two closure properties in Prop.
28
are easy to check.
Hence, to conclude it is enough we show that
\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{add}}_{\emptyset}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{add}}
.
To this end, assume
\mathsf{add}(r_{1},r_{2},r,c_{0})\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{add}}_{\emptyset}
, then, by definition, we have

r_{1}=d_{11}{:}\ldots{:}d_{1n}{:}r_{1}
,

r_{2}=d_{21}{:}\ldots{:}d_{2n}{:}r_{2}
,

r=d_{1}{:}\ldots{:}d_{n}{:}r

there are

for all

.
This implies that

r_{1}=(d_{1_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}}

r_{2}=(d_{2i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}}

for all

d_{1i}+d_{2i}+c_{j+1}=bc_{j}+d_{i}

8.
Related work

The regular approach has been adopted in many different contexts, notably to define proof systems for several kinds of logics, and to define operational models of programming languages supporting cyclic structures.

Concerning proof systems allowing regular proofs, usually called
circlar proofs
,
we find proposals in
[
San02
,
FS13
,
Dou17
]
for logics with fixed point operators, and in
[
Bro05
,
BS11
]
for classical first order logic with inductive definitions.
In both cases, regular proofs allow to naturally handle the unfolding of fixed point and recursive definitions, respectively.
However, regular proofs allow the derivation of wrong sequents, such as the empty one;
hence, to solve this issue, they have to impose additional constraints on regular proofs, such as parity conditions in
[
FS13
]
, thus filtering out undesired derivations.
These additional requirements on regular proofs are expressed at the meta-level and typically require some condition to hold infinitely often in the regular proof.
As inference systems with corules have been designed precisely to filter out undesired infinite derivations,
and they seem pretty good at capturing requirements that should hold infinitely often in the proof,
it would be interesting to investigate whether these additional constraints can be enforced by an appropriate set of corules.

In
[
BKL19
]
, the authors present a proof system supporting coinductive reasoning with Horn clauses.
Such a system allows only finite derivations, enabling coinduction thanks to a cycle detection mechanism similar to the one we use in the context of inference systems.
However, in their setting, they have to perform an additional ‘productivity” check, that is, they have to ensure that a cycle is closed only after the application of a clause, otherwise they would fall in inconsistency.
This check is not required in our setting, as our derivations are built only using rules.

Even though all these proof systems are tightly related to our work, there is an important difference:
we study regular reasoning by itself, without fixing a specific syntax.
In this way, we can work abstractly, focusing only on the essential feature of regularity, thus providing a common abstract and simple background to all these proof systems.

The other context where we can find applications of regular coinduction is in programming languages supporting cyclic structures.
In this case, we use the term
regular corecursion
for a semantics of recursive definitions which detects cycles, analogously to the inductive characterization of the regular interpretation in Sect.
5
.

We can find proposals of language constructs for regular corecursion in all common programming paradigms: logic
[
SMBG06
,
SBMG07
,
AD15
]
, functional
[
JKS13
,
JKS17
]
and object-oriented
[
AZ12
]
.
There are also proposals, inspired by corules, supporting a flexible form of regular corecursion in the logic
[
ADZ17a
,
DAZ20
]
and object-oriented
[
ABDZ20
]
paradigms.

The approach proposed in the logic paradigm is particularly interesting.
Indeed, logic programs can be regarded as particular, syntactic instances of inference systems: judgments are ground atoms, and inference rules are ground instances of clauses.

The declarative semantics of logic programs turns out to be defined exactly the same way as that of the underlying inference systems, as fixed point of the associated inference operator.
Moreover,
in coinductive logic programming, the resolution procedure, called
coSLD resolution

[
SMBG06
,
SBMG07
,
AD15
]
, keeps track of already encountered goals, so that,
if it finds again the same goal, up-to unification, it can accept it.
This mechanism looks very similar to our inductive characterization of the regular interpretation, hence, the analogy with inference systems holds also at the operational level. The same analogies hold between generalised inference systems and
logic programs with coclauses
introduced in
[
DAZ20
]
.

Basing on this correspondence, in
[
DAZ20
]
soundness and completeness of the resolution procedure with respect to the regular declarative semantics are proved, rather than in an ad-hoc way, by relying on the inductive characterisation of the regular interpretation given in this paper (Theorem
10
and Corollary
26
). That is, it is enough to show that the resolution procedure is equivalent to the inductive characterisation of the regular interpretation.

9.
Conclusion

Inference systems
[
Acz77
,
San11
]
are a widely used framework to define and reason on several kinds of judgements (small-step and big-step operational semantics, type systems, proof systems, etc.) by means of inference rules.
They naturally support both inductive and coinductive reasoning: in the former approach only finite derivations are allowed, while in the latter one, arbitrary derivations (finite or not) can be used.

In this paper, we have considered regular reasoning, an interesting middle way between induction and coinduction, combining advantages of both approaches:
it is not restricted to finite derivations, thus overcoming limitations of the inductive approach, but it still has a finite nature, as a regular derivation can only contain finitely many judgements.
We started from a natural proof-theoretic definition of the regular interpretation of an inference system, as the set of judgements derivable by a regular proof tree.
After presenting the construction of the rational fixed point in a lattice-theoretic setting, we proved that the regular interpretation coincides with the rational fixed point of the inference operator associated with the inference system.
Then, we showed that the regular interpretation has an equivalent inductive characterization, which provides us with an algorithm to find a derivation for a judgment, if any.
Relying on these results, we discussed proof techniques for regular reasoning: from the fact that the regular interpretation is a rational fixed point, we got the regular coinduction principle, which allows us to prove completeness,
while, from the inductive characterization, we derived a proof technique to show soundness.

Finally, we focused on inference systems with corules
[
ADZ17b
,
Dag19
]
, a recently introduced generalisation of inference systems, allowing refinements of the coinductive interpretation.
We showed that all results presented for regular coinduction can be smoothly extended to this generalised framework, thus providing a flexible approach also to regular reasoning.

Concerning future work,
an interesting direction is the development of more sophisticated proof techniques for regular reasoning.
Indeed, several enhanced coinductive techniques have been proposed, such as
parametrized coinduction

[
HNDV13
]
and
coinduction up-to

[
BPPR17
]
, which have been proved to be effective in several contexts.
Adapting such techniques to the (flexible) regular case would provide us with powerful tools to support regular reasoning.
A further development in this direction would be to provide support to regular reasoning in proof assistants, which usually provide primitives only for plain induction and coinduction.
To this end, we could start from existing approaches
[
Spa16
,
UV17
]
to implement regular terms in proof assistants.
Finally, it would be interesting to apply results in this paper to build, in a principled way, abstract and operational models of languages supporting regular corecursion, going beyond the logic paradigm.
