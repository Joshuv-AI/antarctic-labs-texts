Reflected backward stochastic differential equations with rough drivers
Hanwu Li, Huilin Zhang, Kuan Zhang

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate reflected backward stochastic differential equations driven by rough paths (rough RBSDEs), which can be viewed as probabilistic representations of nonlinear rough partial differential equations (rough PDEs) or stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) with obstacles. Furthermore, we demonstrate that solutions to rough RBSDEs solve the corresponding optimal stopping problems within a rough framework. This development provides effective and practical tools for pricing American options in the context of the rough volatility model, thus playing a crucial role in advancing the understanding and application of option pricing in complex market regimes. The well-posedness of rough RBSDEs is established using a variant of the Doss-Sussman transformation. Moreover, we show that rough RBSDEs can be approximated by a sequence of penalized BSDEs with rough drivers. For applications, we first develop the viscosity solution theory for rough PDEs with obstacles via rough RBSDEs. Second, we solve the corresponding optimal stopping problem and establish its connection with an American option pricing problem in the rough path setting.

Introduction

1.1.
Background and motivation

In this paper we focus on the reflected backward stochastic differential equations (RBSDEs) involving an extra rough integral
d\mathbf{X}_{t}.
Specifically, for
p\geq 2,
we are interested in the equation

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &Y_{t}=\xi+\int_{t}^{T}f(r,S_{r},Y_{r},Z_{r})dr+\int_{t}^{T}H(S_{r},Y_{r})d\mathbf{X}_{r}-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{r}dW_{r}+K_{T}-K_{t},\\
&Y_{t}\geq L_{t},\ t\in[0,T],\textrm{ and }\int_{0}^{T}(Y_{r}-L_{r})dK_{r}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where

\int_{t}^{T}H(S_{r},Y_{r})d\mathbf{X}_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\int_{t}^{T}H_{i}(S_{r},Y_{r})d\mathbf{X}^{i}_{r},

the path
\mathbf{X}
is a so called
p
-variation
geometric rough path
, and the diffusion process
S
satisfies

S_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}b(r,S_{r})dr+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(r,S_{r})dW_{r},\quad t\in[0,T].

In addition, we focus on the case where
f
exhibits quadratic growth in
z.
When
H\equiv 0,
Eq. (
1.1
) degenerates to RBSDEs with quadratic growth, as studied by, e.g.,
[
7
,
31
,
33
,
41
]
.

The nonlinear backward stochastic differential equation (BSDE), introduced by Pardoux-Peng
[
38
]
, has been a famous area of research in stochastic analysis, with significant applications in various fields such as finance, control theory, and mathematical physics.
The BSDE has the following form:

(1.2)

Y_{t}=\xi+\int_{t}^{T}f(r,Y_{r},Z_{r})dr-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{r}dW_{r},\quad t\in[0,T],

where
\xi
is
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable, and the solution of (
1.2
) is a pair of adapted processes
(Y,Z),
with
Z
term (vaguely speaking) designed to cancel the anticipating part of
\xi+\int f(...)dr
so that
Y
is adapted. The concept of RBSDEs, first introduced by El Karoui et al.
[
20
]
, has extended this theory to incorporate constraints represented by the reflection term. Compared with classical BSDEs, the RBSDE requires an additional adapted increasing process
K_{t}
such that

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &Y_{t}=\xi+\int_{t}^{T}f(r,Y_{r},Z_{r})dr-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{r}dW_{r}+K_{T}-K_{t},\\
&Y_{t}\geq L_{t},\ t\in[0,T],\textrm{ and }\int_{0}^{T}(Y_{r}-L_{r})dK_{r}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where the obstacle
L
is continuous, adapted and
L_{T}\leq\xi.

Our interest in RBSDE (
1.1
) is motivated by the following two related problems.

(a) The first motivation is to solve the following obstacle problem for semilinear rough partial differential equations (rough PDEs):

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &du(t,x)+\mathcal{L}u(t,x)dt+H(x,u(t,x))d\mathbf{X}_{t}=0,\\
&u(T,x)=g(x),\quad u(t,x)\geq l(t,x),\quad t\in[0,T],\end{aligned}\right.

where

\mathcal{L}u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left\{\sigma\sigma^{\top}(t,x)D^{2}_{xx}u(t,x)\right\}+b^{\top}(t,x)D_{x}u(t,x)+f\big(t,x,u(t,x),\sigma^{\top}(t,x)D_{x}u(t,x)\big).

Rough PDEs, which can be viewed both as an extension of classical PDEs with singular drivers and as a pathwise interpretation of SPDEs, have been studied by various authors in different forms; see, e.g.,
[
8
,
14
,
15
,
18
,
28
]
. However, with reflections or obstacles, research on such rough differential equations is very limited. Some related topics can be found in Aida
[
1
]
and Deya et al.
[
13
]
, who study the reflected rough differential equation in the deterministic case. Nevertheless, rough PDEs with reflections in the infinite-dimensional case remain a widely open research area. If the driver
\mathbf{X}
is a smooth path, it is well understood that the solution

to (
1.4
) can be represented as a solution to an RBSDE via the so-called nonlinear Feynman-Kac formula. In the same spirit, when
\mathbf{X}
is rough, one expects an equivalent relationship between (
1.4
) and rough RBSDEs (see Section 5.1). On the other hand, when the
d\mathbf{X}_{t}
term is a pathwise realization of a Brownian integral, (
1.4
) corresponds to an obstacle problem for SPDEs, which has been well studied by Matoussi-Stoica
[
36
]
. Thus, the well-posedness of our rough RBSDE, and consequently of (
1.4

[
25

).

(b) The second motivation originates from the American option pricing under rough volatility. Consider a financial market model with two assets
P^{i},i=0,1
.
P^{0}
is the price of a riskless bond satisfying

where

where

and

the Riemann-Liouville fractional Brownian motion (fBm) derived from

[

[

Now we consider a slightly more general case where

According to the recent work

by Bank et al, one may

and thus the above stock price is equivalent to

The main advantage of the rough path framework is that it overcomes the problem arising from the non-Markovian nature of the volatility

When

is smooth (say, with bounded variation), El Karoui-Quenez

Then, in the rough case, by extending this argument, the American option pricing problem is naturally related to the rough RBSDE (

The theory of rough paths originates from Lyons’ seminal work
[

[

]

[

extend the result of

[
1
,
13
]
mentioned in our motivation, we may refer to
[

,

]
and the references therein for more recent works. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first research on RBSDEs driven by rough paths and, consequently, rough PDEs with obstacles.

The contributions of this paper are threefold. First, we systematically explore the characteristics and properties of rough RBSDEs, including the stability, the comparison theorem, and the construction and approximation of solutions via penalization. This penalization argument can be viewed as a numerical approximation for nonlinear rough PDEs or SPDEs with obstacles. Second, as applications, we relate rough RBSDEs to the obstacle problem within the rough path framework, and then we derive a nonlinear Feynman-Kac formula for the corresponding rough PDEs. Third, we delve into an optimal stopping problem that involves RBSDEs and rough BSDEs, and we relate this problem to an American option pricing problem.

The main idea for the well-posedness part, developed from
[
16
]
, is to approximate the rough path via
lifted
smooth paths. Then we solve the approximate RBSDE via a modified Doss-Sussman transformation. However, by following this idea, we will encounter fundamental problems when dealing with the penalization approximation and applications for rough RBSDEs, since the convergence of the solution via the prescribed approximation is rather weak. More precisely, the modified Doss-Sussman transformation fails to provide the continuous dependence of reflections
K_{t}
on the rough driver, and thus the convergence of
K_{t}
does not follow from the convergence of
(Y_{t},Z_{t}).
In addition, it is unclear if the continuity obtained by the approximation argument is consistent with the penalized approximation or the viscosity solution theory with obstacles. These two problems stem from the fact that the rough integral
\int_{0}^{t}H(...)d\mathbf{X}_{r}
exists solely in a formal sense, while RBSDE theory relies heavily on Itô’s theory to derive the convergence of
\int_{0}^{t}H(S_{r},Y^{m}_{r})d\mathbf{X}_{r}
when
Y^{m}\rightarrow Y
uniformly. Moreover, for the obstacle problem involving rough PDEs, the mere pointwise convergence of the approximated solution is not sufficient for the existence of solutions to such rough PDEs. Indeed, the locally uniform convergence of viscosity solutions to the limit should be considered.

We handle these problems separately. For the trouble caused by the definition of solutions, we rely on the monotonic property of the diffeomorphism induced by the Doss-Sussman transformation, as well as the continuous property of the obstacle. Indeed, these two properties make the convergence of reflections equivalent to the weak convergence of corresponding measures by analyzing the uniform convergence of cumulative distribution functions. For the similar trouble encountered in the penalization argument, inspired by Zhang
[
42
, Section 6.3.3]
, we transform the problem for convergence of penalization sequences into the study of continuous dependence of rough RBSDEs on varying obstacles, and we demonstrate the convergence of these obstacles using the Doss-Sussman transformation. That is, we treat the sequence of penalized rough BSDEs as a new kind of rough RBSDEs, whose obstacle is the smaller value between the original obstacle and the penalization solution. Then, we employ the continuity and differentiability of the Doss-Sussman transformation to study the trajectory semi-continuity of the limits of penalization solutions. This semi-continuity provides us with the a.s. uniform convergence of the new obstacles. Consequently, the desired convergence of the penalization sequence follows directly from the stability of rough RBSDEs. For the well-posedness of rough PDEs with obstacles, motivated by
[
16
]
, to avoid the lack of uniform convergence, we employ a so-called “semi-relaxed limits” technique, which is usually used to establish the invariance of viscosity solutions under limits. This technique offers a tool to establish a Dini-type argument, thereby allowing us to derive the uniform convergence of viscosity solutions from the equivalence of the relaxed upper and lower limits. Furthermore, the equivalence of relaxed limits is a consequence of the comparison theorem for viscosity solutions of transformed PDEs, as outlined in
[
10
, Section 6]
.

Lemma 2.4

Remark 3.1
.

Regarding the above conditions, we provide the following explanations.

(i).

Conditions (A1) and (A3) are slightly more general compared to the corresponding ones in

In the following we will present a helpful property of increasing functions, which implies that the pointwise convergence of continuous increasing functions can be strengthened to uniform convergence. This property will play an essential role in the proof of the well-posedness of rough RBSDEs.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

,
n\geq 1,
and

Assume that

for every

Let

be a positive function. Then it follows that

\int_{0}^{t}y_{s}dK^{n}_{s}\rightarrow\int_{0}^{t}y_{s}dK_{s}\text{ uniformly in }t\in[0,T].

Proof.

Denote
t_{0}:=\sup\{t\in[0,T];K_{t}=0\}.

Since the equality
K_{t_{0}}-K_{0}=0
leads to that

\left|\int_{0}^{t}y_{s}d(K^{n}-K)_{s}\right|=\left|\int_{0}^{t}y_{s}dK^{n}_{s}\right|\leq\|y\|_{\infty;[0,t_{0}]}|K^{n}_{t_{0}}-K^{n}_{0}|,

we have as
n\rightarrow\infty

(3.7)

\int_{0}^{t}y_{s}dK^{n}_{s}\rightarrow\int_{0}^{t}y_{s}dK_{s}\text{ uniformly in }t\in[0,t_{0}].

For
t\in(t_{0},T],
from the convergence
\lim_{n}K^{n}_{\cdot}=K_{\cdot},
it follows that for all
r\in[t_{0},t],

(K^{n}_{r}-K^{n}_{t_{0}})\rightarrow(K_{r}-K_{t_{0}})>0\text{ and }\int_{t_{0}}^{r}\frac{dK^{n}_{s}}{K^{n}_{t}-K^{n}_{t_{0}}}\rightarrow\int_{t_{0}}^{r}\frac{dK_{s}}{K_{t}-K_{t_{0}}}.

Thus, we have that

(3.8)

dK^{n}_{s}/(K^{n}_{t}-K^{n}_{t_{0}})\rightarrow dK_{s}/(K_{t}-K_{t_{0}}),\ \text{weakly in probability measure on }[t_{0},t].

Since
y
is continuous, by the definition of weak convergence in measure we have

(3.9)

\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{y_{s}dK^{n}_{s}}{K^{n}_{t}-K^{n}_{t_{0}}}\rightarrow\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{y_{s}dK_{s}}{K_{t}-K_{t_{0}}},\text{ for every }t\in(t_{0},T].

Let

According to the convergence
\lim_{n}(K^{n}_{t}-K^{n}_{t_{0}})=K_{t}-K_{t_{0}}
and (
3.9
), we have

(3.10)

\hat{K}^{n}_{t}\rightarrow\hat{K}_{t},\text{ for every }t\in(t_{0},T].

Furthermore, by the positivity of
y,

\hat{K}^{n}
and
\hat{K}
are positive increasing functions with
\hat{K}^{n}_{t_{0}}=\hat{K}_{t_{0}}=0.
And by the continuity of
K,

\hat{K}
is also continuous. Set
t_{0}^{\prime}:=\sup\{t\in[t_{0},T];\hat{K}_{t}=0\}.
Then by repeating the proof of (
3.7
) with
(K^{n},K)
replaced by
(\hat{K}^{n},\hat{K}),
and letting the integrand be
1,
we have that

(3.11)

\int_{t_{0}}^{t}y_{s}dK^{n}_{s}\rightarrow\int_{t_{0}}^{t}y_{s}dK_{s}\text{ uniformly in }t\in[t_{0},t^{\prime}_{0}].

By repeating the proof of (
3.8
) with
t=T,
we have

(3.12)

d\hat{K}^{n}_{s}/(\hat{K}^{n}_{T}-\hat{K}^{n}_{t^{\prime}_{0}})\rightarrow d\hat{K}_{s}/(\hat{K}_{T}-\hat{K}_{t^{\prime}_{0}}),\text{ weakly in probability measure on }[t^{\prime}_{0},T].

Note that the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of

is continuous. By applying

39

to (

(3.13)

\int_{t^{\prime}_{0}}^{t}\frac{y_{s}dK^{n}_{s}}{\hat{K}^{n}_{T}-\hat{K}^{n}_{t^{\prime}_{0}}}\rightarrow\int_{t^{\prime}_{0}}^{t}\frac{y_{s}dK_{s}}{\hat{K}_{T}-\hat{K}_{t^{\prime}_{0}}},\text{ uniformly in }t\in[t^{\prime}_{0},T].

According to (

),
\hat{K}^{n}_{T}-\hat{K}^{n}_{t_{0}^{\prime}}\rightarrow\hat{K}_{T}-\hat{K}_{t^{\prime}_{0}},
and then by using (

(3.14)

\int_{t^{\prime}_{0}}^{t}y_{s}dK^{n}_{s}\rightarrow\int_{t^{\prime}_{0}}^{t}y_{s}dK_{s},\text{ uniformly in }t\in[t^{\prime}_{0},T].

By (

-\theta_{2}:=\sup_{\tilde{y}\in[\underline{M}(T),\overline{M}(0)]}\frac{(-\lambda^{2}A^{4}+6C\lambda A^{4})e^{3\lambda A\tilde{y}}+2A^{4}e^{3\lambda A\tilde{y}}}{2|1+e^{\lambda A\tilde{y}}|^{4}\cdot|\varphi^{\prime}(\tilde{y})|^{2}}<0.

Thus, noting that
[\underline{M}(t),\overline{M}(t)]\subset[\underline{M}(T),\overline{M}(0)],

(
B.24
) implies that

(B.28)

D_{\tilde{y}}\tilde{f}(t,x,\tilde{y},\tilde{z})\leq-\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\tilde{z}|^{2},\ \text{ for all }\tilde{y}\in[\underline{M}(t),\overline{M}(t)].

In conclusion, we can choose the parameter
(A,K,\varepsilon,\lambda)
in the following way.
Firstly, choose
\lambda>0
such that (
B.27
) holds with

-\lambda^{2}+6C\lambda+2<0;

secondly, let

\varepsilon=e^{-4\lambda\tilde{M}e};

thirdly, find
K(\bar{M},C)>0
depends on
\bar{M}
and
C
such that (
B.26
) holds with

-K+2C<0
and
-K+C_{\varepsilon}<0;

finally, let

A:=e^{4\lambda\tilde{M}e^{KT}}.

Then the following conditions

D_{y}\tilde{f}(t,x,\tilde{y},\tilde{z})\leq\varepsilon|\tilde{z}|^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\ \text{ and }\ \varepsilon\leq A^{-1}\ \text{ are true whenever }\ T\leq K^{-1}\land h_{\varepsilon,\bar{M}}.

In addition, when
T\leq h\land h_{\varepsilon,\bar{M}}
with
h:=K^{-1},
(
B.28
), (
B.3
), and Assumption
(H1)
yield that there exists some
\bar{C}>0
such that for all
(t,x,\tilde{z})\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\tilde{y}\in[\underline{M}(t),\overline{M}(t)]

\displaystyle|\tilde{f}(t,x,\tilde{y},\tilde{z})|\leq\bar{C}(1+|\tilde{z}|^{2}),

\displaystyle|D_{x}\tilde{f}(t,x,\tilde{y},\tilde{z})|\leq\bar{C}(1+|z|^{2}),

\displaystyle D_{\tilde{y}}\tilde{f}(t,x,\tilde{y},\tilde{z})\leq-\bar{K}(1+|z^{2}|),

\displaystyle|D_{\tilde{z}}\tilde{f}(t,x,\tilde{y},\tilde{z})|\leq\bar{C}(1+|\tilde{z}|).

Let

\bar{f}(t,x,\tilde{y},\tilde{z}):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tilde{f}\big(t,x,\underline{M}(t),\tilde{z}\big)-\bar{K}(1+|\tilde{z}|^{2})(\tilde{y}-\underline{M}(t)),\ \tilde{y}<\underline{M}(t);\\
&\tilde{f}(t,x,\tilde{y},\tilde{z}),\ \tilde{y}\in[\underline{M}(t),\overline{M}(t)];\\
&\tilde{f}\big(t,x,\underline{M}(t),\tilde{z}\big)-\bar{K}(1+|\tilde{z}|^{2})(\tilde{y}-\overline{M}(t)),\ \tilde{y}>\overline{M}(t).\end{aligned}\right.

Then
\bar{f}
satisfies the condition (
B.4
).
By step 1, we have

u(t,x)\leq v(t,x),\ \text{ for all }(t,x)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For an arbitrary terminal time
T,
by taking the transition
\tilde{u}(\cdot,x):=u(\cdot+T-h\land h_{\varepsilon,\bar{M}},x),
then

u(t,x)\leq v(t,x),\ \text{ for all }(t,x)\in(T-h\land h_{\varepsilon,\bar{M}},T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

which completes the proof.
∎

In Section
4.1

T.
The following proposition is an adaption of
[
9
, Proposition 2]
in the obstacle setting.

Proposition B.5
.

Denote
(f^{0},l^{0},g^{0}):=(f,l,g).
Suppose that for
n\geq 0

f^{n}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

l^{n}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
and
g^{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are continuous. Assume
f^{n}(\cdot)\rightarrow f^{0}(\cdot),

l^{n}(\cdot)\rightarrow l^{0}(\cdot),
and
g^{n}(\cdot)\rightarrow g^{0}(\cdot)
locally uniformly, and assume
g^{n}(x)\geq l^{n}(T,x)
for all
n\geq 0.

In addition, suppose for
n\geq 1
,
v^{n}
is a viscosity solution of the obstacle problem for PDE (
B.1
) with parameter
(f^{n},l^{n},g^{n})
. Assume further
\|v^{n}\|_{\infty;(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}

is uniformly bounded in
n\geq 1.
Then
\overline{v}(T,x)=\underline{v}(T,x)=g^{0}(x),
where
\overline{v}=\limsup\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}{}^{*}v^{n}
and
\underline{v}=\limsup\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}{}_{*}v^{n}
are defined in (
4.5
).

Proof.

Clearly,
\overline{v}(T,x)\geq g^{0}(x).

Suppose that
\overline{v}(T,x)>g^{0}(x),

and thus there exist
\delta>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\overline{v}(T,x_{0})-g^{0}(x_{0})=\delta.

Assume without loss of generality that
g^{0}(x_{0})=0.
By the definition of
\overline{v},
there exists
n_{k}\rightarrow\infty
and
(s_{k},y_{k})\rightarrow(T,x_{0})
such that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}v^{n_{k}}(s_{k},y_{k})=\overline{v}(T,x_{0})=\delta.

Note that
(g^{n},l^{n})
converges to
(g^{0},l^{0})
locally uniformly, and
l^{0}(T,x_{0})\leq g^{0}(x_{0}).
Then there exists
\rho\in(0,T)
such that
g^{n}(x)<\frac{\delta}{2}
and
l^{n}(t,x)<\frac{\delta}{2}
whenever
|t-T|\vee|x-x_{0}|<\rho
and
n
is large enough. Define the test function

\phi(t,x):=\lambda(T-t)+K|x-x_{0}|^{2},

where
K:=\sup\limits_{n\geq 1}\|v^{n}\|_{\infty;(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}/\rho^{2}
and
\lambda>\sup\limits_{n\geq 1}\|v^{n}\|_{\infty;(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}/\rho
will be chosen later. Let

(\hat{t}^{n},\hat{x}^{n})\in\text{arg\ max}\left\{v^{n}(t,x)-\phi(t,x):(t,x)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}.

Denote
O_{\rho}(T,x_{0}):=\left\{(t,x)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};|t-T|\vee|x-x_{0}|\leq\rho\right\}.
First we claim that there exists
k^{\prime}\geq 1
such that
(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}})\in O_{\rho}
for all
k>k^{\prime}.
Actually, for
(t,x)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus O_{\rho}

and
n\geq 1,

v^{n}(t,x)-\phi(t,x)\leq\sup_{n\geq 1}\|v^{n}\|_{\infty;(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}-\lambda\rho\land K|\rho|^{2}\leq 0.

While

(B.29)

v^{n_{k}}(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}})-\phi(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}})\geq v^{n_{k}}(s_{k},y_{k})-\phi(s_{k},y_{k})\geq\frac{\delta}{2},\ \text{when $k$ is sufficiently large.}

In addition, we claim that
\hat{t}^{n_{k}}\neq T
when
k
is sufficiently large. Indeed, if
\hat{t}^{n_{k}}=T,

v^{n_{k}}(T,\hat{x}^{n_{k}})-\phi(T,\hat{x}^{n_{k}})=g^{n_{k}}(\hat{x}^{n_{k}})-\phi(T,\hat{x}^{n_{k}})\leq g^{n_{k}}(\hat{x}^{n_{k}})<\frac{\delta}{2}\ \text{ when $k$ is large,}

which leads to a contraction with (
B.29
) if
\hat{t}^{n_{k}}=T.
Therefore, we have
v^{n_{k}}(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}})>l^{n_{k}}(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}})
when
k
is large enough since
l^{n_{k}}(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}})<\frac{\delta}{2}
while (
B.29
) holds for sufficiently large
k.

Hence, by choosing
k
large enough, we have

\hat{t}^{n_{k}}<T\text{ and }v^{n_{k}}(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}})>l^{n_{k}}(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}}).

Recall that
I_{d}
is the unit matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.
Then by the viscosity subsolution property of
v^{n_{k}},
and by the fact that
(-\lambda,2K(\hat{x}^{n_{k}}-x_{0}),KI_{d})\in P^{2,+}v^{n_{k}}(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}}),
we have

(B.30)

0\leq-\lambda+G^{n_{k}}\big(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}},v^{n_{k}}(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}}),2K(\hat{x}^{n_{k}}-x_{0}),KI_{d}\big),

where
G^{n}(t,x,y,p,\Gamma):=\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left\{\sigma(t,x)\sigma^{\top}(t,x)\Gamma\right\}+p^{\top}b(t,x)+f^{n}\left(t,x,y,\sigma^{\top}(t,x)p\right).

Note that
(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}})
remains in the bounded set
O_{\rho}
,
\rho
is independent of
\lambda,
and
\|v^{n_{k}}\|_{\infty;(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
are uniformly bounded in
k\geq 1.
Then combined with the fact that
G^{n_{k}}(\cdot)
converges to
G^{0}(\cdot)
locally uniformly, we have that
G^{n_{k}}(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}},v^{n_{k}}(\hat{t}^{n_{k}},\hat{x}^{n_{k}}),2K(x_{0}-\hat{x}^{n_{k}}),KI_{d})
are uniformly bounded in
k\geq 1.
It follows that (
B.30
) leads to a contradiction by taking
\lambda
large enough. Thus we prove that
\overline{v}(T,x)=g^{0}(x),
and
\underline{v}(T,x)=g^{0}(x)
can be proved similarly.
∎

Appendix C.
Some elementary facts about increasing processes

Lemma C.1
.

Assume that
X,Y:[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are two optional processes. Suppose that for each stopping time
\tau\leq T
,

(C.1)

X_{\tau}\leq Y_{\tau}\text{ a.s..}

Then we have a.s.,
X_{t}\leq Y_{t}\text{ for all }t\in[0,T].

Proof.

Denote

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega;X_{t}>Y_{t}\right\}.

Since
X
and
Y
are optional, the random set
\mathcal{A}
is also optional. Thus by the optional section theorem (see Dellacherie-Meyer
[
12
, Chapter IV (no.83)]
), for every
\varepsilon>0,
there exists a stopping time
\tau^{\prime}
such that

(i)

\{\big(\tau^{\prime}(\omega),\omega\big);\omega\in\Omega,\ \tau^{\prime}(\omega)<\infty\}\subset\mathcal{A}
;

(ii)

\mathbb{P}\left\{\omega\in\Omega;\tau^{\prime}(\omega)<\infty\right\}\geq\mathbb{P}\left\{\omega\in\Omega;\text{ there exists $t<\infty$ such that }(t,\omega)\in\mathcal{A}\right\}-\varepsilon.

Assume that
\mathcal{A}
is not an evanescent random set, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}\left\{\omega\in\Omega;\text{ there exists $t<\infty$ such that }(t,\omega)\in\mathcal{A}\right\}>0.

Choosing
\varepsilon
small enough, it satisfies that

\mathbb{P}\left\{\omega\in\Omega;\tau^{\prime}(\omega)<\infty\right\}>0\text{ and }X_{\tau^{\prime}\land T}>Y_{\tau^{\prime}\land T}\text{ on }\left\{\omega\in\Omega;\tau^{\prime}(\omega)<\infty\right\},

which contradicts the condition (
C.1
).
∎

Now we apply the above lemma to prove that
K_{\cdot}
is an a.s. increasing process if it is increasing on every stopping times.

Lemma C.2
.

Assume
K:[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an optional process with
K_{0}=0.
Suppose that for every two stopping times
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
that
\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}\leq T,
it satisfies

(C.2)

K_{\tau_{1}}\leq K_{\tau_{2}}\text{ a.s..}

Then, we have a.s.,

K_{t_{1}}\leq K_{t_{2}},\text{ for all }0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq T.

Proof.

For simplicity of notations, we denote
K_{t}=0
if
t<0.
Denote by
\mathbb{Q}
be the set of rational numbers. Define

\widetilde{K}_{t}:=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{Q},s\leq t}K_{s}.

Clearly
\widetilde{K}_{t}
is also optional. For an arbitrary stopping time
\tau\leq T
, we have by (
C.2
)

\widetilde{K}_{\tau}=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{Q},s\leq\tau}K_{s}\leq\sup_{s\in\mathbb{Q}}K_{s\land\tau}\leq K_{\tau}\text{ a.s.. }

Thus by Lemma
C.1
we have a.s.

(C.3)

\widetilde{K}_{t}\leq K_{t},\text{ for all }t\in[0,T].

On the other hand, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, set

K^{n}_{t}:=K_{t-\frac{1}{n}}.

Clearly,
K^{n}_{t}
is optional. For an arbitrary stopping time
\tau\leq T
,

K^{n}_{\tau}=K_{\tau-\frac{1}{n}}\leq\sup_{s\in\mathbb{Q}}\left[K_{s}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau-\frac{1}{n}<s\leq\tau\}}\right]\leq\sup_{s\in\mathbb{Q},s\leq\tau}K_{s}=\widetilde{K}_{\tau}\text{ a.s.,}

where we have used the following fact in the first inequality

K_{\tau-\frac{1}{n}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau-\frac{1}{n}<s\leq\tau\}}\leq K_{s\vee(\tau-\frac{1}{n})}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau-\frac{1}{n}<s\leq\tau\}}=K_{s}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau-\frac{1}{n}<s\leq\tau\}}\text{ a.s..}

Thus, by Lemma
C.1
again, we have a.s.,

(C.4)

K^{n}_{t}\leq\widetilde{K}_{t},\text{ for all }t\in[0,T].

For every
n\in\mathbb{N},
it follows from (
C.3
) and (
C.4
) that a.s.,

K_{t_{1}}=K^{n}_{t_{1}+\frac{1}{n}}\leq\widetilde{K}_{t_{1}+\frac{1}{n}}\leq\widetilde{K}_{t_{2}}\leq K_{t_{2}}\text{ for all }0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T\text{ and }|t_{2}-t_{1}|>\frac{1}{n}.

By taking the intersection of the above cases in
n\in\mathbb{N},
we have a.s.

K_{t_{1}}\leq K_{t_{2}}\text{ for all }0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T,

which completes our proof.
∎
