Diameters of the Characteristic Imset Polytopes
Petter Restadh

Abstract
. It has been shown that the edge structure of the characteristic imset polytope is closely connected to the question of causal discovery. The diameter of a polytope is an indicator of how connected the polytope is and moreover gives us a hypothetical worst case scenario for an edge-walk over the polytope. We present low-degree polynomial bounds on the diameter of CIM n \mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n} and, for any given undirected graph G G , the face CIM G \mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G} .

52B05, 52B12, 62H22

1.
Introduction

Several algorithms within causal discovery were recently discovered to be edge-walks along convex polytopes.
A natural question becomes how efficient such an edge-walk can become.
To this end we study the diameters of these polytopes.

Let
[n]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\mathcal{G}=([n],E)
be a directed acyclic graph (DAG).
The characteristic imset of
\mathcal{G}
,
c_{\mathcal{G}}
, is a 0/1-vector, indexed by subsets of
[n]
, that in coordinate
S\in\{S\subseteq[n],|S|\geq 2\}
assumes the value

c_{\mathcal{G}}(S)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{ if there exists $i\in S$ such that, $S\subseteq\pa_{\mathcal{G}}(i)\cup\{i\}$},\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Then we define the
characteristic imset polytope
as

\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}\coloneqq\mathop{\rm conv}\nolimits\left(c_{\mathcal{G}}\colon\mathcal{G}=([n],E)\textrm{ a DAG}\right).

The polytope
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
is a full dimensional (
\dim\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}=\left|\{S\subseteq[n],|S|\geq 2\}\right|=2^{n}-n-1
) polytope whose vertices are precisely the characteristic imsets of DAGs.
The mapping
\mathcal{G}\mapsto c_{\mathcal{G}}
is not injective; we do however have a clear graphical understanding of when
c_{\mathcal{G}}=c_{\mathcal{H}}
(see
Theorem

1.4
).
It has been shown that
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
has many facets, at least one for each connected matroid on
[n]

[
17
]
, but a complete facet description is only available for
n\leq 4
.
Especially we are interested in explaining the geometry, such as the edges or facets, of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
in terms of the DAGs.

The motivation for these questions comes from the area of causal discovery where a well-studied question regards finding algorithms for inferring a DAG from data
[
4
,
15
,
21
]
.
To do this we interpret
i\to j
in
\mathcal{G}
to mean
i
being a direct cause of
j
.
Studený, Hemmecke, and Lindner transformed this question into a linear program over
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}

[
16
,
18
]
.
The authors of
[
9
]
showed that the edge structure of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
is of particular interest. In their paper we are given a geometric interpretation of several greedy algorithms as edge-walks over
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
and its faces.
In particular, for any undirected graph
G
, the face
[
9
]

\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}\coloneqq\mathop{\rm conv}\nolimits\left(c_{\mathcal{G}}\colon\mathcal{G}=([n],E)\textrm{ a DAG with skeleton }G\right),

(of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
) is studied.
A complete characterisation of the edges of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}
when
G
is a tree or a cycle was recently discovered
[
8
]
.
For general
G
, less is known and the only edges with a clear interpretation are the ones given in
[
9
, Proposition 3.2]
, namely that changing the direction of a single edge in a DAG, that does not create a directed cycle, gives us an edge in
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}
(see
Theorem

2.1
).

Given any polytope
P
, the vertex-edge graph of
P
,
G(P)
, is the graph with nodes corresponding to the vertices of
P
and an edge
v-u\in G(P)
if and only if
\mathop{\rm conv}\nolimits(v,u)
is an edge of
P
.
We define the distance in
G(P)
between
v
and
u
as the length of the shortest path between
v
and
u
in
G(P)
, and the diameter of
P
,
\diam(P)
, as the maximal distance between any two vertices in
G(P)
.
Polytope diameters have been studied extensively and show up in several different contexts
[
10
,
13
,
22
]
.
The original motivation was that the diameter of a polytope is a lower bound on the number of steps a simplex-type algorithm must take.
It also provides an indication of whether the graph
G(P)
is sparse or more densely connected.
Therefore, to better understand the polyhedral aspects of causal discovery knowledge of the diameter of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
and it’s faces is desired.

In this paper we will establish low-degree polynomial bounds on the diameters of the above-mentioned polytopes.
In particular, in
Section

2
we see that for a general undirected graph
G=([n],E)
we have
\diam\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}\leq|E|
.
If
G
is a tree we can, via the work of
[
8
]
, improve this bound to
\diam\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}\leq n-2
and give a lower bound in terms of the maximal path length of
G
.
This is done in
Section

2.1
.
Finally in
Section

2.2
we show, using a new type of edge, that
\diam\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}\leq 2n-2
.
Given this linear upper bound, we conjecture that we in fact have linear upper bounds (in
n
) on
\diam\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}
for any
G
.

The following classical result by Verma and Pearl gives us a graphical interpretation of Markov equivalence.

Theorem 1.2
.

[

Two DAGs are Markov equivalent if and only if they have the same skeleton and the same v-structures.

The previously mentioned work by Studený
[
16
]
outlines how to encode a model characterized via conditional independence statements, for example DAG models, via integer vectors.
One key idea being that a maximum likelihood estimation over all models is equivalent to maximizing a linear function over the set of vectors.
Developing this idea Studený, Hemmecke, and Lindner introduced the characteristic imset; a vector encoding where the MEC of

Lemma 1.3
.

[

Let

Moreover, Studený, Hemmecke, and Lindner showed that the characteristic imset is in fact a unique representation of the MEC.

.

[

Two DAGs

2.
Diameter of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits
polytopes

The diameter of the polytope gives us an upper bound on the number of steps any edge-walk needs to perform to get from one vertex to another, assuming we are walking optimally.
In
[
9
]
, it was shown that reversing an edge of a DAG
\mathcal{G}
either gives a Markov equivalent graph or an edge of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}
.
For any DAG
\mathcal{G}
with
i\to j\in\mathcal{G}
we define
\mathcal{G}_{i\leftarrow j}
to be the directed graph identical to
\mathcal{G}
but with the edge
i\to j
reversed.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
9
]

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a DAG with skeleton
G
and
i\to j\in\mathcal{G}
.
If
\mathcal{G}_{i\leftarrow j}
is a DAG not Markov equivalent to
\mathcal{G}
, then
\mathop{\rm conv}\nolimits(c_{\mathcal{G}},c_{\mathcal{G}_{i\leftarrow j}})
is an edge of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}
.

Using this we can show an upper bound on the diameter of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
G=([n],E)
be an undirected graph. Then
\diam(\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G})\leq|E|
.

Here we will use the same argument used in
[
9
, Proposition 3.5]
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
be two DAGs with skeleton
G
.
We claim that we can always reverse at least one edge
i\to j
in
\mathcal{G}
, such that
i\leftarrow j
is in
\mathcal{H}
, and reach another DAG.
As
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
share skeleton, and can thus at most differ on
|E|
edges there exists a sequence of at most
|E|
edges such that after reversing each we have a new DAG, and in the end we reach
\mathcal{H}
.
From
Theorem

2.1
we have that each of these moves either correspond to an edge or a vertex of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}
, and hence the result follows.

To this end we let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of edges that differ between
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
.
We impose a partial order on
\mathcal{C}
as
i^{\prime}\to j^{\prime}\preceq i\to j
if and only if
j^{\prime}\in\de_{\mathcal{G}}(j)
or, if
j^{\prime}=j
,
i\in\de(i^{\prime})
.
That is, we sort the children according to
\mathcal{G}
and the parents in reverse.
Let
i\to j
be a maximal edge in
\mathcal{C}
.
We claim that reversing
i\to j
does not create a cycle.

For the sake of contradiction, assume it does.
That is, we have another path
i\to\dots\to j
in
\mathcal{G}
.
As every edge in this path is greater than
i\to j
, according to our partial order, all of these edges must be present in
\mathcal{H}
.
However
i\leftarrow j
is in
\mathcal{H}
, contradicting that
\mathcal{H}
was a DAG.
Then the result follows.
∎

Proposition 2.13

Let

Proof.

Notice that since

We let

Let
\mathcal{D}

c_{\mathcal{G}}(S)=0

3.
Discussion

In this paper we have shown that we have, at worst, quadratic bounds for
\diam\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}
and linear bounds for
\diam\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
.
As the dimension of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
is
2^{n}-n-1
we get that the diameter of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
grows at most quadratically in the logarithm of the dimension, significantly slower than any general bounds (for example
[
10
]
).
In this sense we observe that
G(\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n})
is highly connected.

For an edge-walk on
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}
maximising an objective
W
, it is not always the case that the optimal path monotone in
W
.
As seen in
[
4
,
8
,
9
,
19
]
, not having access to all edges can still give us consistency guarantees with additional assumptions on
W
.
However, when dealing with a score function based on data, as often is the case with
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
, these additional assumptions are not guaranteed to hold for any finite sample size and, especially for smaller sample sizes, more edges can still improve performance
[
8
]
.
As we can expect there to be many edges in
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
and
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{G}
, the fundamental question becomes which edges are the most crucial for performance and which ones are easily checked.
For example, while the edges of
Proposition

2.13
give us a way to transverse the polytope in few steps, in practice it may be easier to work with only the edges of
Theorem

2.12
as a repeated use can indeed reach the same graph.
However, the question which specific class of edges to use cannot be properly discussed without a better understanding of the edge structure of
\mathop{\rm CIM}\nolimits_{n}
in general.
