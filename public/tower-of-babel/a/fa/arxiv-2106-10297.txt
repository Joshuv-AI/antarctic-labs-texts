Modes of a stellar system II: non-ergodic systems
Jun Yan Lau & James Binney

Abstract
An equation is derived for the energy of a small disturbance in a system that is generated by a distribution function (DF) of the form f ⁡ ( 𝐉 ) f({\bf J}) – most galaxies and star clusters can be closely approximated by such a DF. The theory of van Kampen modes is extended to such general systems. A bilinear form on the space of DFs is defined such that the energy of a disturbance is its norm under this form. It is shown that van Kampen modes that differ in frequency are then orthogonal, with the consequence that the energies of van Kampen modes are additive. Consequently, most of the insight into he dynamics of ergodic systems that was gained in a recent paper on the van Kampen modes of ergodic systems applies to real clusters and galaxies.



1
Introduction

Stellar systems have hitherto been modelled in mean-field limit of an infinite number
of constituent particles, when
fluctuations vanish. That fluctuations play an essential role in the
evolution of clusters was recognised over half a century ago (Henon, Spitzer,
Chandra), but even now observations are fitted to mean-field models such as
Michie-King models
(
Michie 1963
;
King 1966
;
McLaughlin et al. 2006
;
Piotto et al. 2015
;
Claydon et al. 2019
)
.

Some fluctuations are internally generated by the shot noise
inherent in a system with a finite number of particles, while other
fluctuations are externally stimulated by the gravitational fields of
neighbouring systems. As observational data become more precise, there must
come a point at which fluctuations of either type can be detected. Detection
of fluctuations would open the exciting possibility of using galaxies and
star clusters to detect the passage of dark-matter haloes because the tidal
fields of such haloes will excite the large-scale modes of globular clusters
and dwarf spheroidal galaxies.

Modelling the small-scale effects of shot noise is rather straightforward
because the system’s self-gravity is only important on the largest scales

(
Fouvry et al. 2021
, e.g)
. But following the derivation of the Balescu-Lenard equation
by
Heyvaerts 2010
and
Chavanis 2012
, it has become evident that
self-gravity has a big impact on system-scale fluctuations even when the
latter are stimulated by Poisson noise

(
Fouvry et al. 2015
;
Hamilton et al. 2018
;
Lau & Binney 2019
;
Lau & Binney 2021a
;
Heggie et al. 2020
)
. One expects
externally generated fluctuations to be predominantly large-scale, so a
viable theory of fluctuations must encompass self-gravity.

The standard approach to a theory of fluctuations is via normal modes:
By linearising the equations of motion one derives a set of harmonically
oscillating disturbances that is complete in the sense that any initial
condition can be expressed as a linear combination of modes. This
decomposition simultaneously characterises the initial condition in an
physically significant way, and provides a convenient way to compute the
system’s evolution by taking advantage of the almost trivial rule for
evolving a normal mode.

The stability of stellar systems has traditionally been investigated by
determining the frequencies of ‘Landau modes’ because the system is unstable
if any of these frequencies has a positive imaginary part. Landau modes,
however, lack the essential completeness property of normal modes. In the
case of a homogeneous electrostatic plasma,
van Kampen 1955
presented the true normal
modes, now known as van Kampen modes in his honour, and
Case 1959
proved that van Kampen’s modes
are complete. Specification of the distribution function (DF)
f(x,v)
of a
collisionless system requires much more information than is required to
specify the state of a fluid, and this fact is reflected in key differences
between van Kampen modes and the modes of a fluid system. Crucially, the spectrum of
van Kampen modes contains a continuum of real frequencies, and the DFs of individual modes
contains a Dirac
\delta
-function associated with its frequency. Expression of an
initial condition as a sum of normal modes involves an integral over
frequency that eliminates the
\delta
-function.

Recently in the first paper in this series
(
Lau & Binney 2021b
, hereafter Paper
I)
we derived the van Kampen modes of ergodic stellar systems,
that is ones with DFs
f(H)
, where
H={\textstyle{1\over 2}}v^{2}+\Phi({\bf x})
is the
Hamiltonian of an individual star. They showed that the energy of a
disturbance is the sum of the energies of its constituent van Kampen modes,
and that when the system is stable the energies of all modes are inherently
positive. In this paper we generalise these results to non-ergodic
stellar systems, that is, systems with DF
f({\bf J})
, where
{\bf J}
is the vector
of action integrals in the unperturbed system. This class of systems is
extremely broad and includes systems that are flattened or spherical and
exhibit velocity anisotropy and/or systematic rotation. Some our results will
be restricted to the subclass of systems that are unchanged by reversing all
velocities, which in practice
excludes systems with systematic rotation.

In Section
2
we introduce the notation and some results that
will be required in later sections. In Section
3
we extend to
systems with DF
f({\bf J})
the formula for the energy of a fluctuation that

Nelson & Tremaine 1999
derived for ergodic systems. In
Section
4

we obtain results for the van Kampen modes of systems with a DF
f({\bf J})

that are analogues of results derived for ergodic systems in Paper I. The
route taken to these results does, however, differ significantly to that used
in Paper I, being simpler but less powerful – the fourth paper in this series
will present the rather intricate generalisation to non-ergodic systems of
the approach used in Paper I, which exploits the Hermitian operator
introduced by
Antonov 1961
. In Section
5
we discuss
the differences between ergodic and non-ergodic systems and the relation
between
true modes and Landau modes.

2
Mathematical background

Here we introduce essential mathematical tools and establish our notation.
We focus on systems with integrable mean-field potentials,
so their DFs can be written in the form
f({\bf J})
.

2.1
Angle-action variables

The role that Cartesian variables play for
homogeneous systems is played for spheroidal systems by angle-action variables

({\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}},{\bf J})
. The actions
J_{i}
are constants of motion while their
conjugate variables, the angles
\theta_{i}
, increase linearly in time, so

{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}(t)={\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}(0)+\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}t
. The particles’ Hamiltonian
H({\bf x},{\bf v})
is a
function
H({\bf J})
of the actions only and the frequencies
\Omega_{i}
that
control the rates of increase of the angles are given by
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}=\partial H/\partial{\bf J}
. Angle-action variables are canonical, so the volume element of
phase space
{\rm d}^{6}{\bf w}={\rm d}^{3}{\bf x}{\rm d}^{3}{\bf v}={\rm d}^{3}{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}{\rm d}^{3}{\bf J}
and Poisson brackets can be computed
as

[f,g]=\sum_{i}\bigg({\partial f\over\partial\theta_{i}}{\partial g\over\partial J_{i}}-{\partial f\over\partial J_{i}}{\partial g\over\partial\theta_{i}}\bigg).

(1)

Functions on phase space can be expressed as Fourier series:

h({\bf w})=\sum_{\bf n}h_{\bf n}({\bf J})\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}}\ ;\ h_{\bf n}({\bf J})=\int{{\rm d}^{3}{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}\over(2\pi)^{3}}\mathrm{e}^{-{\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}}h({\bf w}).

(2)

Note that for real
h
,
h_{-{\bf n}}=h_{\bf n}^{*}
.

2.2
Potential-density pairs

Following

Kalnajs 1976
we solve Poisson’s equation by introducing a basis of
biorthogonal potential-density pairs. That is, a set of pairs

(\rho^{(\alpha)},\Phi^{(\alpha)})
such that

4\pi G\rho^{(\alpha)}=\nabla^{2}\Phi^{(\alpha)}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf x}\,\Phi^{(\alpha)*}\rho^{(\alpha^{\prime})}=-{\cal E}\delta_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}},

(3)

where
{\cal E}
is an arbitrary constant with the dimensions of energy.
Given a density
distribution
\rho({\bf x})
, we expand it in the basis

\rho({\bf x})=\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}\rho^{(\alpha)}({\bf x})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\Phi({\bf x})=\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}\Phi^{(\alpha)}({\bf x}),

(4)

where

A_{\alpha}=-{1\over{\cal E}}\int{\rm d}^{6}{\bf w}\,\Phi^{(\alpha)*}({\bf x})f({\bf w}).

(5)

If
\rho
and
\Phi
are time-dependent, the
A_{\alpha}
become
time-dependent. From equations (
3
) and (
4
) one
can obtain an expression for
\Phi
in terms of
\rho
. Comparison of this
relation with Poisson’s integral, yields

{G\over|{\bf x}^{\prime}-{\bf x}|}={1\over{\cal E}}\sum_{\alpha}\Phi^{(\alpha)}({\bf x})\Phi^{(\alpha)*}({\bf x}^{\prime}).

(6)

The system’s potential energy is

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle={\textstyle{1\over 2}}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf x}\,\Phi\rho={\textstyle{1\over 2}}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf x}\,\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}\Phi^{(\alpha)}\rho

(7)

\displaystyle=-{{\cal E}\over 2}\sum_{\alpha}|A_{\alpha}|^{2}.

(8)

A related calculation is the potential energy of one system when placed in
the potential of another. This is

P^{\prime}=-G\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf x}\,{\rho({\bf x})\rho^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime})\over|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}|}.

(9)

The symmetry of this expression establishes that the energy of system a in
the potential of system b is the same as that of system b placed in the
potential of system a. Trivial adaptation of the derivation of equation
(
7
) shows that

P^{\prime}=-{\cal E}\sum_{\alpha}A^{*}_{\alpha}A^{\prime}_{\alpha}.

(10)

Now

\displaystyle P^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}^{6}{\bf w}\,f\Phi^{\prime}=\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf J}\,{\rm d}^{3}{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}\,\sum_{{\bf m}{\bf n}}f_{\bf m}\Phi^{\prime}_{\bf n}\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}({\bf m}+{\bf n})\cdot{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}}

(11)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{3}\sum_{\bf n}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf J}\,f^{*}_{\bf n}\Phi^{\prime}_{\bf n}.

(12)

2.3
Linearised CBE

On dynamical timescales the DF of a stellar system satisfies the
collisionless Boltzmann equation

{\partial f\over\partial t}+[f,H]=0.

(13)

When we split
f({\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}},{\bf J},t)=f_{0}({\bf J})+f_{1}({\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}},{\bf J},t)
into its mean-field
and fluctuating components and neglect terms quadratic and higher in the
fluctuations, the CBE can be written

{\partial f_{1}\over\partial t}+[f_{1},H_{0}]+[f_{0},\Phi_{1}]=0.

(14)

When we use angle-action coordinates to evaluate the Poisson brackets and
write
f_{1}
and
\Phi_{1}
as Fourier series in angles, equation (
14
)
yields

{\partial f_{\bf n}\over\partial t}+{\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}f_{\bf n}-{\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot{\partial f_{0}\over\partial{\bf J}}\Phi_{1{\bf n}}=0,

(15)

where we have dropped the subscript 1 from
f_{1{\bf n}}
for brevity but
retained it on
\Phi_{1{\bf n}}
for reasons that will soon become apparent.

3
Energy of a perturbation

Following
Nelson & Tremaine 1999
we imagine using an externally applied
gravitational field to impose a real perturbation
f_{1}
on a mean-field model

f_{0}({\bf J})
. The
perturbing gravitational potential
\Phi_{1}
now has two components, the
potential
\Phi_{\mathrm{e}}
of the externally applied field and the potential

\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}
generated via Poisson’s equation by the perturbed density
distribution

\rho_{1}=\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf v}\,f_{1}.

(16)

The rate at which work is done by the external field is

\displaystyle{{\rm d}E\over{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=-\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf x}\,{\bf v}\cdot{\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{e}}\over\partial{\bf x}}\rho_{1}

(17)

\displaystyle=-\int{\rm d}^{6}{\bf w}\,f_{1}({\bf w}){\bf v}\cdot{\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{e}}\over\partial{\bf x}}.

(18)

Now

{\bf v}\cdot{\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{e}}\over\partial{\bf x}}=-[H_{0},\Phi_{\mathrm{e}}]=\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\cdot{\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{e}}\over\partial{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}},

(19)

so equation (
17
) can be written

\displaystyle{{\rm d}E\over{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=-\int{\rm d}^{6}{\bf w}\,f_{1}({\bf w})\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\cdot{\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{e}}\over\partial{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}}

(20)

\displaystyle=-(2\pi)^{3}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf J}\sum_{\bf n}\,f^{*}_{\bf n}({\bf w}){\rm i}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\cdot{\bf n}\,\Phi_{\mathrm{e}{\bf n}}.

(21)

With
\Phi_{1}
decomposed into its two components, the linearised CBE
(
15
) can be written

{\partial f_{\bf n}\over\partial t}+{\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}f_{\bf n}-{\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot{\partial f_{0}\over\partial{\bf J}}\Phi_{\mathrm{s}{\bf n}}={\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot{\partial f_{0}\over\partial{\bf J}}\Phi_{\mathrm{e}{\bf n}}.

(22)

Eliminating
\Phi_{\mathrm{e}{\bf n}}
between the last two equations

\displaystyle{{\rm d}E\over{\rm d}t}\equiv-(2\pi)^{3}

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf J}\sum_{\bf n}\,f^{*}_{\bf n}{{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\over{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}}\,

(23)

\displaystyle\times\bigg({\partial f_{\bf n}\over\partial t}+{\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}f_{\bf n}-{\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}\Phi_{\mathrm{s}{\bf n}}.\bigg)

(24)

The integrand contains three terms. The middle term is proportional to

{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}|f_{\bf n}|^{2}
and vanishes when summed over
{\bf n}
because the sum
includes both
{\bf n}
and
-{\bf n}
. The integral over the first term yields

{{\rm d}K\over{\rm d}t}=-{(2\pi)^{3}\over 2}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf J}\,\sum_{\bf n}{{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\over{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}}{\partial|f_{\bf n}|^{2}\over\partial t}.

(25)

In preparation for handling the third term, we note that

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}^{3}{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}\,f_{1}[\Phi_{\mathrm{s}},H_{0}]

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}^{3}{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}\,f_{1}{\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}\over\partial{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}

(26)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{3}\sum_{\bf n}f_{{\bf n}}^{*}{\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\Phi_{\mathrm{s}{\bf n}}.

(27)

Hence the third term in equation (
23
) is

\displaystyle{{\rm d}P\over{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle\equiv(2\pi)^{3}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf J}\sum_{\bf n}{\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}f_{\bf n}^{*}\Phi_{\mathrm{s}{\bf n}}

(28)

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}^{6}{\bf w}\,f_{1}[\Phi_{\mathrm{s}},H_{0}]=\int{\rm d}^{6}{\bf w}\,f_{1}{\bf v}\cdot{\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}\over\partial{\bf x}}

(29)

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf x}\,\Phi_{\bf s}{\partial\rho_{1}\over\partial t}=-{{\rm d}\over{\rm d}t}{\textstyle{1\over 2}}G\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf x}\,{\rm d}^{3}{\bf x}^{\prime}\,{\rho_{1}({\bf x})\rho_{1}({\bf x}^{\prime})\over|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}|}

(30)

where the penultimate equality uses integration by parts and the continuity
equation
\partial\rho/\partial t=-{\bf\nabla}_{\bf x}\cdot(\rho{\bf v})
.

Now that the two surviving contributions to the integral in equation
(
23
) have proved to be total time derivatives, we can immediately
integrate from
E=K=P=0
at
t=0
to obtain an equation for the energy of an arbitrary
fluctuation

\displaystyle E=-{(2\pi)^{3}\over 2}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf J}\,\sum_{\bf n}{{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\over{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}}|f_{\bf n}|^{2}-{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf x}\,{\rm d}^{3}{\bf x}^{\prime}\,{G\rho_{1}^{2}\over|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}|}.

(31)

This equation generalises to
three dimensions the quantity
Kalnajs 1971
shows to be
constant in an isolated razor-thin disc (Kalnajs’ eqn. 48), and which he
says he will
‘identify as energy’. Our derivation shows that
E
is the work
that must be done to establish a perturbation rather than just showing that

E
is constant when
\Phi_{\mathrm{e}}
vanishes. Equation (
31
)
differs from equation (5.130) in
Binney & Tremaine 2008
for the energy of a disturbed
ergodic system only by the replacement of
{\rm d}f_{0}/{\rm d}H
by

{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}/{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}
. When equation (
6
) is
used to eliminate
|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}|
from equation (
31
) we obtain

E=-{(2\pi)^{3}\over 2}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf J}\,\sum_{\bf n}{{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\over{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}}|f_{\bf n}|^{2}-{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\cal E}\sum_{\alpha}|A_{\alpha}|^{2}.

(32)

3.1
Restriction of the perturbed DF

For certain combinations of
{\bf n}
and
{\bf J}
,
{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}
will
vanish and one might worry that such combinations will make the integral in
equation (
31
) for
E
ill defined. However, from equation (
22
) it
follows that for these combinations
f_{\bf n}({\bf J})
remains zero as the
disturbance is excited, so the integrand in equation (
31
) vanishes
at the apparently problematic points. Thus the disturbances
f
that can be
induced in a system with equilibrium DF
f_{0}({\bf J})
by an external potential
are restricted in form.

Antonov 1961
broadened this result by showing
(e.g.
Binney & Tremaine 2008
, p. 429)
that the the perturbation

f_{1}
that is generated by applying
any
disturbing Hamiltonian
H_{1}
to

f_{0}
will be of the form

f_{1}=[h,f_{0}]={\partial h\over\partial{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}}\cdot{\partial f_{0}\over\partial{\bf J}},

(33)

where
h({\bf w})
is a function that depends on
H_{1}
. On expanding
f
and
h

in Fourier series we obtain

f_{\bf n}={\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot{\partial f_{0}\over\partial{\bf J}}h_{\bf n}

(34)

so when
{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}=0
,
f_{\bf n}
vanishes no matter how the system is
disturbed.

3.2
A bilinear form for DFs

Additivity of energies is a fundamental property of normal modes. It emerges
naturally if we can express the energy of a disturbance as the norm of the
disturbance defined by a bilinear form under which normal modes are mutually
orthogonal. Equation (
32
) suggests that the required form
is

\langle f|\widetilde{f}\rangle=-{(2\pi)^{3}\over 2}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf J}\,\sum_{\bf n}{{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\over{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}}f^{*}_{\bf n}\widetilde{f}_{\bf n}-{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\cal E}\sum_{\alpha}A^{*}_{\alpha}A^{\prime}_{\alpha},

(35)

where
A_{\alpha}[f]
is a functional of
f
while
A^{\prime}_{\alpha}
denotes the
corresponding functional of
\widetilde{f}
.

4
Normal modes of a non-ergodic system

We now look for disturbances with exponential time dependence, so

f_{\bf n}({\bf J},t)=f_{\bf n}({\bf J},\omega)\mathrm{e}^{-{\rm i}\omega t},\quad\Phi_{\bf n}({\bf J},t)=\Phi_{\bf n}({\bf J},\omega)\mathrm{e}^{-{\rm i}\omega t}

(36)

With this ansatz, the linearised CBE (
15
) becomes

({\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}-\omega)f_{\bf n}=({\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0})\Phi_{\bf n}({\bf J},\omega).

(37)

This equation yields a well defined value for
f_{\bf n}
in terms of

\Phi_{\bf n}
when
{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\neq\omega
, but on resonant tori (tori on
which
{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}=0
) it
does not constrain
f_{\bf n}
. Therefore, as
van Kampen 1955
pointed out,
the solution to this equation must be written

f_{\bf n}({\bf J},\omega)={\cal P}{{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}\over{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}-\omega}\Phi_{\bf n}({\bf J},\omega)+g_{\bf n}({\bf J})\delta({\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}-\omega),

(38)

where
{\cal P}
indicates that when integrated wrt
{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}
or

\omega
, the Cauchy principal
value around the singularity at
\omega={\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}
should be taken, and

g_{\bf n}({\bf J})
is an arbitrary function that specifies non-trivial
distributions of stars on the resonant tori. In Section

3.1
we saw that the DFs of disturbances are restricted such
than
f_{\bf n}=0
when
{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}=0
. Since a system’s normal
modes comprise possible disturbances,
g_{\bf n}
must be restricted in the
same way:

{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad g_{\bf n}=0.

(39)

In Paper I

\Phi_{\bf n}({\bf J},\omega)
in equation (
38
) is interpreted as the
potential generated by the the resonant stars and by the non-resonant stars
that they excite. It is the
dressed
potential of the resonant stars.
Consequently, Poisson’s equation imposes a relation between
g_{\bf n}
and

\Phi_{\bf n}
. We obtain this relation by multiplying equation (
38
) by

{\rm d}^{6}{\bf w}\,\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}{\bf n}\cdot{\mathchoice{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecten\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}{\hbox{{\gkvecseven\char 18\relax}}}}}\Phi^{(\alpha^{\prime})*}
and integrating through
phase space to obtain the self-consistency condition (cf Paper I eqn. 42)

\sum_{\alpha}M_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}A_{\alpha}=-B_{\alpha^{\prime}}

(40)

where

\displaystyle M_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}-{(2\pi)^{3}\over{\cal E}}{\cal P}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf J}\,\sum_{\bf n}{{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}f_{0}\over{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}-\omega}\Phi_{\bf n}^{(\alpha^{\prime})*}\Phi_{\bf n}^{(\alpha)}

(41)

\displaystyle B_{\alpha^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-{(2\pi)^{3}\over{\cal E}}\int{\rm d}^{3}{\bf J}\,\sum_{\bf n}g_{\bf n}({\bf J})\Phi_{\bf n}^{(\alpha^{\prime})*}({\bf J})\delta({\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}-\omega).

(42)

In any truly stable system
(
Mathur 1990
, so excluding marginally stable systems such
as those discussed by)
,
M(\omega)
has an inverse for all real

\omega
, so given
g({\bf w})
we can solve for the amplitudes
A_{\alpha}
of the
corresponding density and potential. The coefficients
B_{\alpha^{\prime}}
give the
density and potential generated by the resonant stars alone, that is, after
removing the contributions of stars driven by the gravitational field of the
resonant stars (Paper I eqn. 46).

In principle for given functions
g_{\bf n}
equation (
38
) yields a
mode for every frequency of the form
{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}
, but since vectors

{\bf n}
with any large component generate very small densities in real space,
the important modes are confined to frequencies that range from near zero
(
|\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}|\approx 0
at large
|{\bf J}|
) to about twice the system’s maximum
circular frequency.

B
necessarily vanishes for complex
\omega
because
{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}
is

5
Discussion

There are close parallels between formulae derived here for general systems
and ones presented in Paper I for ergodic systems. The essential difference
is the universal replacement of
{\rm d}f_{0}/{\rm d}H
in Paper I by

{\bf n}\cdot{\bf\nabla}_{\bf J}f_{0}/{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}
as here. The other differences are more superficial, being
caused by formulae in Paper I being derived from an operator that gives
\partial^{2}f/\partial t^{2}
rather than one that gives
\partial f/\partial t
. This change leads to

\delta\big(({\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$})^{2}-\omega^{2}\big)
in Paper I being replaced by

\delta({\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}-\omega)
. These symbols have different dimensions:

\delta\big(({\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$})^{2}-\omega^{2}\big)=\delta\big(({\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}-\omega)({\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}+\omega)\big)\simeq{\delta({\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}-\omega)\over 2\omega}.

(51)

As a consequence,
g_{\bf n}/2\omega
in Paper I is equivalent to
g_{\bf n}
here.

In Paper I we explored the consequences of reducing the self-gravity of a
system by reducing the masses of its particles by a factor
\xi<1
and
introducing a fraction
(1-\xi)
of the mean-field potential. This operation
modified the structure of the system’s van Kampen modes by suppressing the
dressing of the potential of resonant stars. The ensuing discussion applies equally to
the general equilibria treated here.

In Paper I we stressed that Landau ‘modes’ are not really modes but zeroes of
the function
|{\bf M}(\omega)|
. When such a zero lies just below the real axis
(a ‘weakly damped mode’) the van Kampen modes on the adjacent stretch of the
real axis are heavily dressed and will make a large contribution to the
system’s evolution. The time required for these modes to drift out of phase,
and the associated disturbance to fade in real space, is inversely
proportional to extent of the heavily dressed section of the real line, and
therefore inversely proportional to the distance of the zero from the real
line. This interpretation of ‘Landau damping’ applies equally to the general
systems discussed here.

Although
Mathur 1990
did not display an explicit example, he
demonstrated the logical possibility of marginally stable systems, that is
ones that can oscillate for ever. This possibility arises when frequencies of
the form
{\bf n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}({\bf J})
do not extend from a maximum frequency down
to zero. The easiest way to engineer a gap in the frequency coverage is to
impose a limit on the spatial extent of the system, so there is a minimum
orbital frequency
\Omega_{\rm min}
, and to make the system effectively
one-dimensional so small frequencies cannot be constructed by differencing
frequencies greater than
\Omega_{\rm min}
. When these conditions are
satisfied,
Mathur 1990
shows that
f_{0}
can be devised such that the
matrix
{\bf M}
of equation (
40
) has vanishing determinant in a gap.
Consequently, non-zero
{\bf A}
can then be found even though
{\bf B}
vanishes because we
are in a gap. In these exceptional circumstances, a complete set of modes
comprises the van Kampen modes plus any stable/unstable pairs of modes and
any marginally stable modes in gaps.

6
Conclusions

We have extended results presented in Paper I from ergodic systems to
systems with DFs of the form
f({\bf J})
. This is a major extension because
ergodic systems probably do not occur in Nature while many galaxies and star
clusters will have DFs that can be closely approximated by
f({\bf J})
.

We derived equation (
31
) for the energy of a disturbance to a
general system, and motivated by this result defined a bilinear form on the
space of DFs such that the energy of a disturbance is the norm of its DF.

Next we extended the concept of van Kampen modes to general systems. They
exist for essentially the same range of real frequencies, and have the same
physical interpretation and energy-additivity as in the ergodic case. There
is again a sharp distinction between van Kampen modes, which have frequencies
that lie in a real continuum, and classical modes, which have isolated, and
generally
complex, frequencies. When a system is time-reversible (lacks rotation) and
stable, it can only have van Kampen modes.

The mathematical apparatus used here is simpler and less powerful than that
deployed in Paper I. On the plus side, the formulae presented here are
somewhat simpler than the corresponding formulae in Paper I. The downsides
are (i) that modes do not
emerge as eigenfunctions of a Hermitian operator; (ii) we have not shown that all van Kampen modes have positive
energy.
The lack of a connection to a Hermitian operator deprives us of the ability
both to argue for the completeness of the modes and
to confine the normal-mode frequencies to the
axes of the complex plane as we could in the case of ergodic systems.
Nonetheless, at least in the case of systems with
f_{0}({\bf J})
that are
unchanged by reversing all velocities, it seems likely that the frequencies
are confined in the same way. This topic will be a major theme of Paper IV in
this series.

The existence of modes with negative energy would not have important
consequences on the dynamical timescale, because modes evolve independently
of one another so long as the linear approximation holds. On the longer
timescales associated with terms quadratic in the disturbance (the ‘two-body’
timescale), a system is likely to be rendered secularly unstable by the existence of
negative-energy modes because non-linear terms could transfer energy from
negative-energy modes to positive-energy terms and thus causing the
amplitudes of both types of mode to increase.
