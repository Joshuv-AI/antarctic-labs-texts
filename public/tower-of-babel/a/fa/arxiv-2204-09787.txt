Reinforcement Learning from Partial Observation: Linear Function Approximation with Provable Sample Efficiency
Qi Cai, Zhuoran Yang, Zhaoran Wang

Abstract
We study reinforcement learning for partially observed Markov decision processes (POMDPs) with infinite observation and state spaces, which remains less investigated theoretically. To this end, we make the first attempt at bridging partial observability and function approximation for a class of POMDPs with a linear structure. In detail, we propose a reinforcement learning algorithm ( Op timistic Explora t ion via Adv e rsarial I n tegral Equa t ion or OP-TENET ) that attains an ϵ \epsilon -optimal policy within O ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ 2 ) O(1/\epsilon^{2}) episodes. In particular, the sample complexity scales polynomially in the intrinsic dimension of the linear structure and is independent of the size of the observation and state spaces. The sample efficiency of OP-TENET is enabled by a sequence of ingredients: (i) a Bellman operator with finite memory, which represents the value function in a recursive manner, (ii) the identification and estimation of such an operator via an adversarial integral equation, which features a smoothed discriminator tailored to the linear structure, and (iii) the exploration of the observation and state spaces via optimism, which is based on quantifying the uncertainty in the adversarial integral equation.



1
Introduction

Partial observability poses significant challenges for reinforcement learning, especially when the observation and state spaces are infinite. Given full observability, reinforcement learning is well studied empirically
(
Mnih et al. 2015
;
Silver et al. 2016
;
Silver et al. 2017
)
and theoretically
(
Auer et al. 2008
;
Osband et al. 2016
;
Azar et al. 2017
;
Jin et al. 2018
;
Yang and Wang 2020
;
Jin et al. 2020b
;
Ayoub et al. 2020
;
Kakade et al. 2020
;
Du et al. 2021
)
. In particular, for infinite state spaces, neural function approximators achieve remarkable successes empirically
(
Mnih et al. 2015
;
Berner et al. 2019
;
Arulkumaran et al. 2019
)
, while linear function approximators become better understood theoretically
(
Yang and Wang 2020
;
Jin et al. 2020b
;
Ayoub et al. 2020
;
Kakade et al. 2020
;
Du et al. 2021
)
. In contrast, reinforcement learning in partially observed Markov decision processes (POMDPs) is less investigated theoretically despite its prevalence in practice
(
Cassandra et al. 1996
;
Hauskrecht and Fraser 2000
;
Brown and Sandholm 2018
;
Rafferty et al. 2011
)
.

More specifically, partial observability poses both statistical and computational challenges. From a statistical perspective, it is challenging to predict future rewards, observations, or states due to a lack of the Markov property. In particular, predicting the future often involves inferring the distribution of the state (also known as the belief state) or its functionals as a summary of the history, which is already challenging even assuming the (observation) emission and (state) transition kernels are known
(
Vlassis et al. 2012
;
Golowich et al. 2022
)
. Meanwhile, learning the emission and transition kernels faces various issues commonly encountered in causal inference
(
Zhang and Bareinboim 2016
)
. For example, they are generally nonidentifiable
(
Kallus et al. 2021
)
. Even assuming they are identifiable, their estimation possibly requires a sample size that scales exponentially in the horizon and dimension
(
Jin et al. 2020a
)
. Such statistical challenges are already prohibitive even for the evaluation of a policy
(
Nair and Jiang 2021
;
Kallus et al. 2021
;
Bennett and Kallus 2021
)
, which forms the basis of policy optimization. From a computational perspective, it is known that policy optimization is generally intractable
(
Vlassis et al. 2012
;
Golowich et al. 2022
)
. Moreover, infinite observation and state spaces amplify both statistical and computational challenges. On the other hand, most existing results are restricted to the tabular setting
(
Azizzadenesheli et al. 2016
;
Guo et al. 2016
;
Jin et al. 2020a
;
Xiong et al. 2021
)
, where the observation and state spaces are finite.

In this paper, we study linear function approximation in POMDPs to address the statistical challenges amplified by infinite observation and state spaces. In particular, our contribution is fourfold. First, we define a class of POMDPs with a linear structure and identify an ill conditioning measure for sample-efficient reinforcement learning. Such an ill conditioning measure corresponds to the undercompleteness in the tabular setting
(
Jin et al. 2020a
)
. Second, we propose a reinforcement learning algorithm (
OP-TENET
), which applies to any POMDP admitting the aforementioned linear structure. Moreover, we use a minimax optimization formulation in
OP-TENET
such that the algorithm can be implemented in a computation-efficient manor even if the dataset is large. Third, we prove in theory that
OP-TENET
attains an
\epsilon
-optimal policy within
O(1/\epsilon^{2})
episodes. In particular, the sample complexity scales polynomially in the intrinsic dimension of the linear structure and is independent of the size of the observation and state spaces. Fourth, our algorithm and analysis are based on new tools. In particular, the sample efficiency of
OP-TENET
is enabled by a sequence of ingredients: (i) a Bellman operator with finite memory, which represents the value function in a recursive manner, (ii) the identification and estimation of such an operator via an adversarial integral equation, which features a smoothed discriminator tailored to the linear structure, and (iii) the exploration of the observation and state spaces via optimism, which is based on quantifying the uncertainty in the adversarial integral equation.

1.1
Related Work

Our work is related to a line of recent work on the sample efficiency of reinforcement learning for POMDPs. In detail,
Azizzadenesheli et al. 2016
;
Guo et al. 2016
;
Xiong et al. 2021
establish sample complexity guarantees for searching the optimal policy in POMDPs whose models are identifiable and can be estimated by spectral methods. However,
Azizzadenesheli et al. 2016
and
Guo et al. 2016
add extra assumptions such that efficient exploration of the POMDP can always be achieved by running arbitrary policies. In contrast, the upper bound confidence (UCB) method is used in
Xiong et al. 2021
for adaptive exploration. However, they require strictly positive state transition and observation emission kernels to ensure fast convergence to the stationary distribution. The more related work is
Jin et al. 2020a
, which considers undercomplete POMDPs, in other words, the observations are more than the latent states. Their proposed algorithm can attain the optimal policy without estimating the exact model, but an observable component
(
Jaeger 2000
;
Hsu et al. 2012
)
, which is the same for our algorithm design, while only applies to tabular POMDPs.

In a broader context of reinforcement learning with partial observability, our work is related to several recent works on POMDPs with special structures. For example,
Kwon et al. 2021
considers latent POMDPs, where each process has only one latent state, and the proposed algorithm efficiently infers the latent state using a short trajectory.
Kozuno et al. 2021
considers POMDPs having tree-structured states with their positions in certain partitions being the observations. Compared with general POMDPs, these specially structures reduce the complexity of finding the optimal actions, and the corresponding algorithms use techniques closer to those for MDPs. Also, the aforementioned literature only consider tabular POMDPs.

In the contexture of reinforcement learning with function approximations, our work is related to a vast body of recent progress
(
Yang and Wang 2020
;
Jin et al. 2020b
;
Cai et al. 2020
;
Du et al. 2021
;
Kakade et al. 2020
;
Agarwal et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2021
;
Ayoub et al. 2020
)
on the sample efficiency of reinforcement learning for MDPs with linear function approximations. These works characterize the uncertainty in the regression for estimating either the model or value function of an MDP and use the uncertainty as a bonus on the rewards to encourage exploration. However, none of these approaches directly apply to POMDPs due to the latency of the states.

. Then, the agent interacts with the environment as follows. At the
h

\bm{o}_{h}\sim\mathcal{E}_{h}(\cdot\,|\,\bm{s}_{h})

\bm{a}_{h}

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}_{h}=(\bm{o}_{1},\ldots,\bm{o}_{h}),

and receives the reward

. Any mapping
\pi
from the observation history to the action is called a (deterministic) policy. We denote by

the set of all such mappings. Note that the policy does not use the action history as an input. Such a restriction does not exclude the optimal policy, as the action history can be decoded from the observation history. Subsequently, the agent receives the next state
\bm{s}_{h+1}
following

and observation emission kernel

and

\mathcal{E}_{1}

are known, which only account for the initialization. The goal is to find a policy that maximizes the expected total reward, that is,

\displaystyle\pi^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\pi\in\Pi}J(\theta^{*},\pi),

\displaystyle\text{where}~J(\theta,\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta,\pi}\Bigl[\sum_{h=1}^{H}\bm{r}_{h}\Bigr]~\text{for any $(\theta,\pi)\in\Theta\times\Pi$.}

(2.2)

3.1
Finite-Memory Bellman Operator

To cast a POMDP as an MDP, it is necessary to aggregate the observation history and action history as the “state” in an MDP to retrieve the Markov property. In detail, for any
(h,\theta,\pi)\in[H]\times\Theta\times\Pi

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}f)({\overline{\tau}}_{h})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta,\pi}[f({\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h+1})\,|\,{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h}={\overline{\tau}}_{h}]

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{O}}f\bigl({\overline{\tau}}_{h},\pi(\tau_{h}),o_{h+1}\bigr)\cdot p_{\theta}\bigl(\bm{o}_{h+1}=o_{h+1}\,|\,{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h}={\overline{\tau}}_{h},\bm{a}_{h}=\pi(\tau_{h})\bigr){\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h+1},

for any

and
{\overline{\tau}}_{h}\in\overline{\Gamma}_{h}
. Here, the second equality follows from

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta}_{h,a}(o_{h},\widetilde{o}_{h},\widetilde{o}_{h+1})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}}p_{\theta}(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h}=\widetilde{o}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1}=\widetilde{o}_{h+1}\,|\,\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{h}=\widetilde{s}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{a}}_{h}=a)\cdot\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(\widetilde{s}_{h},o_{h}){\,\mathrm{d}}\widetilde{s}_{h},

(3.6)

for any
o_{h},\widetilde{o}_{h},\widetilde{o}_{h+1}\in\mathcal{O}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
, where the function
\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}
is defined in Assumption
2.2
. Figure
2
illustrates the (random) variables in (
3.4
) and (
3.6
). In detail,
\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{h}
is an independent replicate of
\bm{s}_{h}
, that is, they are independent and identically distributed conditioning on
\bm{s}_{h-1}
and
\bm{a}_{h-1}
. Note that
\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{h}
is constructed for ease of presentation, and does not exist in practice. Then, the action
\widetilde{\bm{a}}_{h}
, state
\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{h+1}
, and observations
\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1}
are similarly defined. In other words, their distribution conditioning on
\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{h}
and
{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h-1}
mirrors the distribution of the action
\bm{a}_{h}
, state
\bm{s}_{h+1}
, and observations
\bm{o}_{h},\bm{o}_{h+1}
conditioning on
\bm{s}_{h}
and
{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h-1}
. When the state transition kernel and observation emission kernel have a specific parametrization, the function
\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}
has a corresponding parametrization by Assumption
2.2
, which allows us to parametrize the function
{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta}_{h,a}
in (
3.6
). See Section
A
for an example where the state transition kernel and observation emission kernel admit a linear structure. Compared with the full-memory Bellman operator
\mathbb{P}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}
, the finite-memory Bellman operator
{\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}
does not involve the distribution of
\bm{o}_{h+1}
conditioning on
{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h}
and
\bm{a}_{h}
. Instead, it involves the distribution of
\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h}
and
\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1}
conditioning on
\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{h}
, where the distribution of
\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{h}
is implied by the distribution of the single observation
\bm{o}_{h}
via the function
\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}
. See the following paragraph for more discussion. Moreover, estimating
{\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}
for each
h\in[H]
only involves the distribution of
\bm{o}_{h-1}
,
\bm{o}_{h}
, and
\bm{o}_{h+1}
, which is low-dimensional. See Section
3.2
for more discussion.

How Finite Memory Works:

For notational simplicity, we denote by
\sigma_{h-1}
the event

\displaystyle{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h-1}={\overline{\tau}}_{h-1},\quad\bm{a}_{h-1}=a_{h-1}

(3.7)

for any
h\in[H+1]
. The following lemma implies that the finite-memory Bellman operator
{\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}
is identical to the full-memory Bellman operator
\mathbb{P}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}
in expectation conditioning on
\sigma_{h-1}
, which allows us to use
{\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}
as a surrogate of
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}_{h,a}
.

Lemma 3.1

(Operators Equivalence)
.

For any
(h,\theta,\pi,\overline{\tau}_{h-1},a_{h-1})\in[H]\times\Theta\times\Pi\times\overline{\Gamma}_{h-1}\times\mathcal{A}
and
f\in L^{\infty}(\overline{\Gamma}_{h+1})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[({\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}f)({\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h})-(\mathbb{P}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}f)({\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h})\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}]=0.

Proof.

See Section
C.1
for a detailed proof.
∎

To see the intuition behind Lemma
3.1
, note that by the definition of
\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}
in Assumption
2.2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(\widetilde{s}_{h},\bm{o}_{h})\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}]=p_{\theta}(\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{h}=\widetilde{s}_{h}\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}),

for any
(\widetilde{s}_{h},{\overline{\tau}}_{h-1},a_{h-1})\in{\mathcal{S}}\times\overline{\Gamma}_{h-1}\times\mathcal{A}
. See Section
C.1
for a derivation. In other words,
\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}
serves as the bridge function in causal inference
(
Shi et al. 2020
)
, which recovers the conditional distribution of
\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{h}
from the conditional distribution of
\bm{o}_{h}
. Then, by taking the same conditional expectation on both sides of (
3.6
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta}_{h,a}(\bm{o}_{h},\widetilde{o}_{h},\widetilde{o}_{h+1})\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}]

\displaystyle=p_{\theta}(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h}=\widetilde{o}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1}=\widetilde{o}_{h+1}\,|\,\sigma_{h-1},\widetilde{\bm{a}}_{h}=a)

(3.8)

\displaystyle=p_{\theta}(\bm{o}_{h}=\widetilde{o}_{h},\bm{o}_{h+1}=\widetilde{o}_{h+1}\,|\,\sigma_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h}=a),

which is connected to the integral kernel
p_{\theta}(\bm{o}_{h+1}=o_{h+1}\,|\,{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h}={\overline{\tau}}_{h},\bm{a}_{h}=a)
on the right-hand side of (
3.1
) via the same conditional expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[p_{\theta}(\bm{o}_{h+1}=o_{h+1}\,|\,{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h},\bm{a}_{h}=a)\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}]

(3.9)

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[p_{\theta}(\bm{o}_{h+1}=o_{h+1}\,|\,\sigma_{h-1},\bm{o}_{h},\bm{a}_{h}=a)\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}]

\displaystyle\quad=p_{\theta}(\bm{o}_{h+1}=o_{h+1}\,|\,\sigma_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h}=a)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathcal{O}}p_{\theta}(\bm{o}_{h}=o_{h},\bm{o}_{h+1}=o_{h+1}\,|\,\sigma_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h}=a){\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h}.

Here the second equality in (
3.8
) follows from the fact that
(\widetilde{\bm{a}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{a}_{h})
is an independent replicate of
(\bm{a}_{h},\bm{o}_{h},\bm{o}_{h+1})
, that is, they follow the same distribution conditioning on
\sigma_{h-1}
.

Backward Bellman Recursion:

For any
(h,\theta,\pi)\in[H+1]\times\mathcal{A}\times\Pi
, we define the value function
V^{\theta,\pi}_{h}\in L^{\infty}(\overline{\Gamma}_{h})
by

\displaystyle V^{\theta,\pi}_{h}({\overline{\tau}}_{h})=({\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}\cdots{\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{H}R)({\overline{\tau}}_{h}),\quad\text{for any ${\overline{\tau}}_{h}\in\overline{\Gamma}_{h}$,}

(3.10)

which gives the backward Bellman recursion

\displaystyle V^{\theta,\pi}_{h}({\overline{\tau}}_{h})=({\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}V^{\theta,\pi}_{h+1})({\overline{\tau}}_{h}),\quad\text{for any ${\overline{\tau}}_{h}\in\overline{\Gamma}_{h}$.}

(3.11)

The following corollary is implied by Lemma
3.1
, which relates the value function
V^{\theta,\pi}_{h}
to the expected total reward. Note that
V^{\theta,\pi}_{h}
does not correspond to the “reward-to-go” in the usual value function definition in MDPs since it involves all rewards across the
H
steps.

Corollary 3.2
.

For any
(h,\theta,\pi)\in[H+1]\times\Theta\times\Pi
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta,\pi}\Bigl[V^{\theta,\pi}_{h}({\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h})-\sum_{i=1}^{H}\bm{r}_{i}\,\Big|\,\sigma_{h-1}\Bigr]=0,

(3.12)

for any
(\overline{\tau}_{h-1},a_{h-1})\in\overline{\Gamma}_{h-1}\times\mathcal{A}
. For
h=1
, we have
J(\theta,\pi)=\mathbb{E}[V^{\theta,\pi}_{1}(\bm{o}_{1})]
since
{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{1}=\bm{o}_{1}
and
\sigma_{0}=\varnothing
, which follows from the definition of
{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h}
in (
2.3
).

Proof.

See Section
C.2
for a detailed proof.
∎

Corollary
3.2
allows us to evaluate a policy
\pi
by estimating
\{{\mathbb{B}}^{\theta^{*},\pi}_{h}\}_{h=1}^{H}
instead of
\{\mathbb{P}^{\theta^{*},\pi}_{h}\}_{h=1}^{H}
. Meanwhile,
\{V^{\theta,\pi}_{h}\}_{h=1}^{H}
play a critical role in analyzing the sample complexity.

3.2
Operator Estimation via Minimax Optimization

Although the finite-memory Bellman operator
{\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}
defined in (
3.4
) does not involve the observation distribution conditioning on the history, that is, the distribution of
\bm{o}_{h+1}
conditioning on
{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h}
and
\bm{o}_{h}
, it remains unclear how to estimate
{\mathbb{B}}^{\theta^{*},\pi}_{h}
in a sample-efficient manner. Note that, by the definition of
{\mathbb{B}}^{\theta^{*},\pi}_{h}
, it suffices to estimate functions
\{{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta^{*}}_{h,a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
. To this end, we define the operator
\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a}:L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}^{3})\rightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}^{3})
for any
(h,a,\theta)\in\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}\times\Theta
by

\displaystyle(\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a}f)(o_{h-1},o_{h},o_{h+1})=\int_{\mathcal{O}^{2}}f(o_{h-1},\widetilde{o}_{h},\widetilde{o}_{h+1})\cdot{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta}_{h,a}(o_{h},\widetilde{o}_{h},\widetilde{o}_{h+1}){\,\mathrm{d}}\widetilde{o}_{h}{\,\mathrm{d}}\widetilde{o}_{h+1},

(3.13)

for any
f\in L^{1}(\mathcal{O}^{3})
and
o_{h-1}
,
o_{h}\in\mathcal{O}
. Note that
\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a}
is a truncated version of
{\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}
, which drops a few variables that are redundant for operator estimation. The following lemma motivates the estimator of
{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta^{*}}_{h,a}
, which uses the definition of
\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
(h,a,a^{\prime},\pi)\in\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}^{2}\times\overline{\Pi}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\rho^{\pi}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}[(\mathbb{F}^{\theta^{*}}_{h,a^{\prime}}f-f)(X)]=0,\quad\text{for any $f\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}^{3})$}.

Here, the distribution
\rho^{\pi}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{O}^{3})
is defined by

\displaystyle\rho^{\pi}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(o_{h-1},o_{h},o_{h+1})=p_{\theta^{*},\pi}\bigl(\bm{o}_{h-1}=o_{h-1},\bm{o}_{h}=o_{h},\bm{o}_{h+1}=o_{h+1}\,|\,\bm{a}_{h-1}=a,\bm{a}_{h}=a^{\prime}\bigr),

for any
o_{h-1}
,
o_{h}
,
o_{h+1}\in\mathcal{O}
. Also, we have
\|\mathbb{F}^{\theta^{*}}_{h,a^{\prime}}\|_{\infty\rightarrow\infty}\leq\gamma
.

Proof.

See Section
C.3
for a detailed proof.
∎

Minimax Optimization:

For any
(h,a^{\prime},\pi)\in\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}\times\overline{\Pi}
, Lemma
3.3
allows us to estimate
{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta^{*}}_{h,a^{\prime}}
based on a dataset
\{{\mathcal{D}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
, where the data points in
{\mathcal{D}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
are collected from the distribution
{\rho}^{\pi}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
. In other words, each episode involves three steps: (a) we execute the exploration policy
\pi
, which takes the actions
\bm{a}_{1},\ldots,\bm{a}_{h-2}
, (b) we take the actions
\bm{a}_{h-1}=a
and
\bm{a}_{h}=a^{\prime}
regardless of the observations, and (c) we add the observation tuple
(\bm{o}_{h-1},\bm{o}_{h},\bm{o}_{h+1})
to
{\mathcal{D}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
. Based on
\{{\mathcal{D}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\}_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}^{2}}
, we estimate
\{{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta^{*}}_{h,a^{\prime}}\}_{(h,a^{\prime})\in\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}}
by solving the following minimax optimization problem,

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}~\max_{f\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}^{3}):\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1}~\max_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}^{2}}~\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}[(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f-\mathbb{S}f)(X)].

(3.14)

Here,
\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
is the empirical distribution induced by the dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
. Also, the projection operator
\mathbb{S}:L^{1}(\mathcal{O}^{3})\rightarrow L^{1}(\mathcal{O}^{3})
satisfies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p}[(\mathbb{S}f)(X)]=\int_{\mathcal{O}^{3}}f(x)\cdot p^{\dagger}(x){\,\mathrm{d}}x,

(3.15)

for any
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}^{3})
and
p\in\Delta(\mathcal{O}^{3})
. Here,
p^{\dagger}\in L^{1}(\mathcal{O}^{3})
is the projection of
p
onto
{\rm linspan}(\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{\rm o}})
. See the definition of
\mathbb{S}
in the next paragraph. The minimax optimization problem in (
3.14
) is motivated by generative adversarial networks. To see the intuition behind (
3.14
), note that
f
serves as the discriminator and
F^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}
serves as the generator. In detail, note that the function
\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a}f
in (
3.13
) is constant with respect to the variable
o_{h+1}
. Thus, Lemma
3.3
implies that the true generator
F^{\theta^{*}}_{h,a^{\prime}}
recovers the distribution of
(\bm{o}_{h-1},\bm{o}_{h},\bm{o}_{h+1})\sim d^{\pi}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
(corresponding to the true parameter
\theta^{*}
) from the marginal distribution of
(\bm{o}_{h-1},\bm{o}_{h})
. In this case, the true distribution and the (fake) distribution recovered by the generator can not be distinguished by any discriminator in
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}^{3})
. When we train the generator and discriminator on a dataset, the discriminator class
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}^{3})
has a too large capacity. Therefore, we employ the projection operator
\mathbb{S}
to enforce the finite-dimensional linear structure of
d^{\pi}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
, which reduces the capacity of the discriminator class. Such a projection operator guarantees the generalization power of the solution to (
3.14
).

Projection Operator via RKHS:

In the following, we define the projection operator
\mathbb{S}
. To this end, we consider an RKHS
\mathcal{H}
induced by a kernel function
{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{O}^{3}\times\mathcal{O}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We define the corresponding RKHS embedding
\mathbb{K}:L^{1}(\mathcal{O}^{3})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
by

\displaystyle(\mathbb{K}p)(x)=\int_{\mathcal{O}^{3}}{\mathcal{K}}(x,y)p(y){\,\mathrm{d}}y,

(3.16)

for any
p\in L^{1}(\mathcal{O}^{3})
and
x\in\mathcal{O}^{3}
. Moreover, we define the matrix
G\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm o}\times d_{\rm o}}
by

\displaystyle[G]_{i,j}=\langle\mathbb{K}\phi_{i},\mathbb{K}\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\phi_{i},X^{\prime}\sim\phi_{j}}[{\mathcal{K}}(X,X^{\prime})],\quad\text{for any $i,j\in[d_{\rm o}]$.}

(3.17)

Recall that the distribution functions
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{\rm o}}
are defined in Assumption
2.2
. The following assumption specifies the regularity condition on
{\mathcal{K}}
and
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{\rm o}}
.

Assumption 3.4
.

The kernel function
{\mathcal{K}}
is bounded and continuous. In particular, we have
|{\mathcal{K}}(x,y)|\leq 1
for any
x,y\in\mathcal{O}^{3}
. Also, we have
\alpha=\lambda_{\min}(G)>0
, where we denote by
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
the minimum eigenvalue of a matrix and the matrix
G
is defined in (
3.17
).

Here, the continuity of
{\mathcal{K}}
is defined with respect to the topology space
\mathcal{O}^{3}
. For example,
\mathcal{O}^{3}
is (embedded as) a subset of some Euclidean space and the continuity of
{\mathcal{K}}
is defined with respect to the corresponding Euclidean distance. The boundedness of
{\mathcal{K}}
is satisfied by many kernel functions, for example, the radial basis function (RBF) kernel
(
Smola and Schölkopf 1998
)
. For the positive definiteness of the matrix
G
, note that, for any
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d_{\rm o}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm o}}
, we have

\displaystyle v^{\top}Gv=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\rm o}}v_{i}v_{j}\cdot\langle\mathbb{K}\phi_{i},\mathbb{K}\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\Bigl\|\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm o}}v_{i}\cdot\mathbb{K}\phi_{i}\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

Therefore, to make
G
positive definite, it suffices to require
\mathbb{K}\phi_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{K}\phi_{d_{\rm o}}
to be linearly independent in
\mathcal{H}
. With the the kernel function
{\mathcal{K}}
and matrix
G
defined above, we can verify that (
3.15
) holds for the operator
\mathbb{S}
defined by

\displaystyle(\mathbb{S}f)(x)=\sum_{i,j\in[d_{\rm o}]}[G^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\phi_{i},Y^{\prime}\sim\phi_{j}}\bigl[{\mathcal{K}}(x,Y)\cdot f(Y^{\prime})\bigr],

(3.18)

for any
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}^{3})
and
x\in\mathcal{O}^{3}
. Here, the distance in the projection from
p
to
p^{\dagger}
in (
3.15
) is defined by

\displaystyle d(p_{1},p_{2})=\|\mathbb{K}p_{1}-\mathbb{K}p_{2}\|_{\mathcal{H}},\quad\text{for any $p_{1},p_{2}\in L^{1}(\mathcal{O}^{3})$.}

(3.19)

See Section
F.1
for a derivation.

We present the

Exploration Phase:

(3.21)

We apply the operator estimation method defined in Section

\displaystyle\Theta_{k}=\Bigl\{\theta\in\Theta:L(\theta)\leq\beta\cdot k^{-1/2}\Bigr\},

Given the confidence set defined in (

\displaystyle\pi_{k}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\pi\in\Pi}\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{k}}~J(\theta,\pi),

(3.24)

\theta

is defined in (

\displaystyle\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\theta\in\Theta_{k}}J\bigl(\theta,\widehat{\pi}(\theta)\bigr),\quad\pi_{k}=\widehat{\pi}(\theta_{k}),

\widehat{\pi}(\cdot)

B

\pi_{k}
to collect data, which serves as the next exploration phase. We present

4.1
Sample Efficiency

The following theorem characterizes the sample complexity of

Theorem 4.1
.

Under Assumptions

,
2.2
, and
3.4
, for any

, if we choose a confidence level

in Algorithm

\displaystyle\beta\geq d^{3/2}_{\rm o}(\gamma+1)/\alpha\cdot\sqrt{8\log(2KHA^{2}/\delta)},

(4.1)

then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(J(\theta^{*},\pi^{*})-J(\theta^{*},\pi_{k})\bigr)\leq\frac{4d_{\rm s}\gamma^{2}\beta H^{2}A^{2}\cdot\log K}{K^{1/2}}+\frac{4d_{\rm s}\gamma H^{2}}{K}.

(4.2)

Recall that
d_{\rm s}
and
d_{\rm o}
are defined in Assumption
2.1
,
\gamma
is defined in Assumption
2.2
, and
\alpha
is defined in Assumption
3.4
.

Note that the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.2
) is the leading term for a sufficiently large number of iterations
K
. Recall that the state distribution dimension
d_{\rm s}
and observation distribution dimension
d_{\rm o}
are defined in Assumption
2.1
. Also, quantities
\gamma
and
\alpha
are defined in Assumptions
2.2
and
3.4
, respectively. By Theorem
4.1
, if we run
OP-TENET
for
K
iterations and sample a policy from
\{\pi_{1},\pi_{K}\}
uniformly at random, the expected suboptimality of such a policy converges to zero with high probability at the rate of
K^{-1/2}
up to logarithmic factors. Meanwhile, such a rate depends on
H
,
\mathcal{A}
,
d_{\rm s}
,
d_{\rm o}
,
\gamma
, and
1/\alpha
polynomially. In other words, to obtain an
\varepsilon
-optimal policy for any suboptimality
\varepsilon>0
, it suffices to run

\displaystyle K={\rm poly}(H,A,d_{\rm s},d_{\rm o},\gamma,1/\alpha)\cdot\widetilde{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2})

(4.3)

iterations in
OP-TENET
to collect the data set. Note that the total number of episodes in
K
iterations is
(H-1)A^{2}K
. To our best knowledge, Theorem
4.1
is the first polynomial sample complexity upper bound for reinforcement learning in POMDPs that is independent of the number of states and observations. Moreover, the order of
\varepsilon
is optimal even in the MDP setting
(
Ayoub et al. 2020
)
, which is a special case of POMDPs. In contrast to the sample complexity results in MDPs, a key difference of Theorem
4.1
is that it involves the (upper bound of the) operator norm
\gamma
of the bridge operator
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\theta}_{h}
, which is the left inverse of the observation operator
{\mathbb{O}}^{\theta}_{h}
. Recall that such a left inverse is defined with respect to the finite-dimensional subspace
{\rm linspan}(\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{\rm o}})
of
L^{1}(\mathcal{O}^{3})
in Assumption
2.2
. The (upper bound of the) operator norm
\gamma
is a measure of ill conditioning, which quantifies the fundamental difficulty, in terms of the information-theoretic limit, of reinforcement learning in the POMDP. In the degenerate case where
{\mathbb{O}}^{\theta}_{h}
is not invertible, Theorem
4.1
provides a trivial upper bound since we have
\gamma=\infty
. On the other hand, such a case contains examples that are fundamentally impossible to solve in a sample-efficient manner, which is implied by information theory
(
Jin et al. 2020a
)
.

Proof Sketch

In this section, we sketch the proof of Theorem

. In detail, we prove the theorem by three key lemmas. The following lemma provides a decomposition of the difference of expected total rewards in two POMDPs when the parameters are different and the policies are identical. Recall that the function

is defined in (

).

Lemma 4.2

(Value Decomposition)
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
, we have

\displaystyle J(\theta,\pi)-J(\theta^{\prime},\pi)=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\prime},\pi}[({\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}V^{\theta,\pi}_{h+1})({\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h})-({\mathbb{B}}^{\theta^{\prime},\pi}_{h}V^{\theta,\pi}_{h+1})({\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h})],

for any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
and
\pi\in\Pi
. Here, the function
V^{\theta,\pi}_{h+1}
is defined in (
3.10
).

Proof.

See Section
E.1
for a detailed proof.
∎

For any
k\in[K]
, we denote by
\theta_{k}\in\Theta
the model parameter that is selected in the planning phase of the
k
-th iteration
OP-TENET
(Algorithm
1
), which is defined in (
3.24
), that is,

\displaystyle(\theta_{k},\pi_{k})=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{(\theta,\pi)\in\Theta_{k}\times\Pi}J(\theta,\pi).

(4.4)

We define the state-dependent error
e^{k}_{h}:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle e^{k}_{h}(s_{h-1})=\bigl|\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{*},\pi_{k}}[({\mathbb{B}}^{\theta_{k},\pi_{k}}_{h}V^{\theta_{k},\pi_{k}}_{h+1})({\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h})-({\mathbb{B}}^{\theta^{*},\pi_{k}}_{h}V^{\theta_{k},\pi_{k}}_{h+1})({\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h})\,|\,\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1}]\bigr|,

(4.5)

for any
(k,h,s_{h-1})\in[K]\times[H]\times{\mathcal{S}}
. Conditioning on the event
\theta^{*}\in\Theta_{k}
, which is shown to occur with high probability in the following lemma, we have

\displaystyle J(\theta^{*},\pi^{*})-J(\theta^{*},\pi_{k})\leq J(\theta_{k},\pi_{k})-J(\theta^{*},\pi_{k})\leq\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{*},\pi_{k}}[e^{k}_{h}(\bm{s}_{h-1})],

(4.6)

which follows from Lemma
4.2
. Also, the following lemma characterizes the right-hand side of (
4.6
) when we replace the policy
\pi_{k}
by the mixing policy
\overline{\pi}_{k}
defined in (
3.21
).

Lemma 4.3

(Statistical Guarantee)
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
, and
3.4
, for any
\delta>0
, by choosing the confidence level
\beta
in
OP-TENET
(Algorithm
1
) such that it satisfies (
4.1
), with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

•

\theta^{*}\in\Theta_{k}
,

•

\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{*},\overline{\pi}_{k}}[e^{k}_{h}(\bm{s}_{h-1})]\leq 2HA^{2}\gamma^{2}\beta\cdot k^{-1/2}
,

for any
(k,h)\in[K]\times[H]
.

Proof.

See Section
E.2
for a detailed proof.
∎

To characterize the right-hand side of (
4.6
), it remains to connect
\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{*},\pi_{k}}[e^{k}_{h}(\bm{s}_{h-1})]
with
\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{*},\overline{\pi}_{k}}[e^{k}_{h}(\bm{s}_{h-1})]
, which involve different state distributions. The connection is established in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4

(Telescope of Error)
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
, for any

, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\theta,\pi_{k}}[e^{k}_{h}(\bm{s}_{h-1})]\leq 4\gamma d_{\rm s}H+2d_{\rm s}\log K\cdot\max_{k\in[K]}\bigl(k\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\theta,\overline{\pi}_{k}}[e^{k}_{h}(\bm{s}_{h-1})]\bigr).

See Section
E.3

for a detailed proof.

Definition A.1

We say that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}=\{({\mathcal{S}},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},H,{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta},\mathcal{E}^{\theta},\mu,r):{\theta\in\Theta}\}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{h}(s^{\prime}\,|\,s,a)

\displaystyle=u(s^{\prime})^{\top}M^{\theta}_{h,a}v(s),\quad\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{h}(o\,|\,s)=q(o)^{\top}g^{\theta}_{h}(s),

(A.1)

for any
(h,\theta,s,s^{\prime},a,o)\in[H]\times\Theta\times{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{O}

,

,

,
d_{v}
,
d_{q}
, and
d_{q}

has non-negative entries. Moreover, we have
\mu\in{\rm conh}(\{[u(\cdot)]_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{u}})
and

\displaystyle[u(\cdot)]_{i}\in\Delta({\mathcal{S}}),\quad[q(\cdot)]_{\ell}\in\Delta(\mathcal{O}),\quad\text{for any $(i,j)\in[d_{u}]\times[d_{q}]$.}

The following lemma shows that tabular POMDPs are linear kernel POMDPs.

\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle[u(\cdot)]_{s}=\ind\{s=\cdot\},\quad[v(\cdot)]_{s}=\ind\{s=\cdot\},\quad[q(\cdot)]_{o}=\ind\{o=\cdot\},

A.1
Verification of Assumption
2.1

Recall that in reinforcement learning for a POMDP, the state transition kernel and observation emission kernel are unknown elements of the POMDP. We say that the candidate class of the POMDP is a linear kernel POMDP set
{\mathcal{L}}=\{({\mathcal{S}},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},H,{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta},\mathcal{E}^{\theta},\mu,r):{\theta\in\Theta}\}
when the candidate class of the state transition kernel and observation emission kernel is
\{({\mathcal{T}}^{\theta},\mathcal{E}^{\theta}):\theta\in\Theta\}
and other elements of the POMDP are determined as
({\mathcal{S}},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},H,\mu,r)
. The following lemma shows that any linear kernel POMDP set satisfies the linear function approximation assumption (Assumption
2.1
).

Lemma A.3
.

When the candidate class of the POMDP is a linear kernel POMDP set
{\mathcal{L}}
as defined in Definition
A.1
, we have that Assumption
2.1
holds with

\displaystyle d_{\rm s}\leq d_{u}(d_{v}+1)\quad\text{and}\quad d_{\rm o}\leq d_{q}^{3}.

Recall that
d_{u}
,
d_{v}
, and
d_{q}
are the vector-valued function dimensions in the definition of
{\mathcal{L}}

d_{\rm s},d_{\rm o}
are the number of basis distribution functions in Assumption
2.1

Proof.

We prove the lemma by constructing the basis distribution functions
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{\rm s}}
and
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{\rm o}}

2.1
.

State Distribution:
Note that to make

\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{\rm s}}

\displaystyle p_{\theta,\pi}(\bm{s}_{h}=s_{h},\bm{o}_{h+1}=o_{h+1}\,|\,\bm{a}_{h}=a_{h})

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{h+1}(o_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h+1})\cdot{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{h}(s_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h},a_{h}){\,\mathrm{d}}s_{h+1}\cdot p_{\theta,\pi}(\bm{s}_{h}=s_{h})

(A.2)

where the second equality is by the form of
{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{h}
in Definition
A.1

\displaystyle p_{\theta,\pi}(\bm{s}_{h}=s_{h})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta,\pi}[{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{h-1}\bigl(s_{h}\,\big|\,\bm{s}_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h-1})]

\displaystyle=u(s_{h})^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{\theta,\pi}[M^{\theta}_{h,\bm{a}_{h-1}}v_{h-1}(\bm{s}_{h-1})].

(A.3)

For notational simplicity, we define the vectors

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{h+1}(o_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h+1})\cdot u(s_{h+1})^{\top}M^{\theta}_{h,a_{h}}{\,\mathrm{d}}s_{h+1}\Bigr)^{\top},

(A.4)

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta,\pi}[M^{\theta}_{h,\bm{a}_{h-1}}v_{h-1}(\bm{s}_{h-1})].

\displaystyle p_{\theta,\pi}(\bm{s}_{h}=s_{h}\,|\,\bm{o}_{h+1}=o_{h+1},\bm{a}_{h}=a_{h})

\displaystyle p_{\theta,\pi}(\bm{s}_{h}=\cdot\,|\,\bm{o}_{h+1}=o_{h+1},\bm{a}_{h}=a_{h})=\Bigl\langle v(\cdot)u(\cdot)^{\top},\frac{\zeta_{2}\zeta_{1}^{\top}}{p_{\theta,\pi}(\bm{o}_{h+1}=o_{h+1}\,|\,\bm{a}_{h}=a_{h})}\Bigr\rangle_{\rm tr},

\displaystyle d_{\rm s}=d_{u}(d_{v}+1),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}_{\rm s}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}_{\rm s}^{\prime}\subset{\rm conh}(\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{\rm s}}).

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}^{3}}p_{\theta,\pi}(\bm{s}_{h}=s_{h})\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{h}(o_{h}\,|\,s_{h},a_{h})\cdot{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{h}(s_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h},a_{h})\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{h+1}(o_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h+1})

f=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\psi_{i}\cdot c_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{h}(o^{\prime}\,|\,s^{\prime})\cdot f(s^{\prime}){\,\mathrm{d}}s^{\prime}=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{h}(o^{\prime}\,|\,s^{\prime})\cdot\psi_{\ell}(s^{\prime})\cdot c_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\nu_{h,\ell}(o^{\prime})\cdot c_{\ell},

(A.13)

where the last equality is by the definition of
\nu_{h,\ell}
in (
A.9
). Plugging (
A.13
) into the right-hand side of (
A.12
), we obtain

\displaystyle({\mathbb{Z}}^{\theta}_{h}{\mathbb{O}}^{\theta}_{h}f)(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\int_{\mathcal{O}^{2}}\psi_{i}(s)\cdot[(\Lambda_{h})^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot\nu_{h,j}(o^{\prime})\cdot\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}(o^{\prime},o)\cdot\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\nu_{h,\ell}(o^{\prime})\cdot c_{\ell}{\,\mathrm{d}}o{\,\mathrm{d}}o^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,\ell=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\psi_{i}(s)\cdot[(\Lambda_{h})^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot\Bigl(\int_{\mathcal{O}^{2}}\nu_{h,j}(o^{\prime})\cdot\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}(o^{\prime},o)\cdot\nu_{h,\ell}(o^{\prime}){\,\mathrm{d}}o{\,\mathrm{d}}o^{\prime}\Bigr)\cdot c_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,\ell=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\psi_{i}(s)\cdot[(\Lambda_{h})^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot[\Lambda_{h}]_{j,\ell}\cdot c_{\ell}.

Here, the last equality uses the definition of the matrix
\Lambda_{h}
in (
A.10
). By the definition of the inverse matrix, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\rm s}}[(\Lambda_{h})^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot[\Lambda_{h}]_{j,\ell}=\ind\{i=\ell\},

which implies

\displaystyle({\mathbb{Z}}^{\theta}_{h}{\mathbb{O}}^{\theta}_{h}f)(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\psi_{i}(s)\cdot c_{i}=f(s),\quad\text{for any $s\in{\mathcal{S}}$.}

In the following, we characterize the operator norm
\|\cdot\|_{1\rightarrow 1}
of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\theta}_{h}
. For any
(h,\theta,o)\in[H]\times\Theta\times\mathcal{O}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{S}}}|\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(s,o)|{\,\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Bigl|\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\psi_{i}(s)\cdot[(\Lambda_{h})^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot\int_{\mathcal{O}}\nu_{h,j}(o^{\prime})\cdot\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}(o^{\prime},o){\,\mathrm{d}}o^{\prime}\Bigr|{\,\mathrm{d}}s

(A.14)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\psi_{i}(s)\cdot\Bigl|\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\rm s}}[(\Lambda_{h})^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot\int_{\mathcal{O}}\nu_{h,j}(o^{\prime})\cdot\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}(o^{\prime},o){\,\mathrm{d}}o^{\prime}\Bigr|{\,\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\Bigl|\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\rm s}}[(\Lambda_{h})^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot\int_{\mathcal{O}}\nu_{h,j}(o^{\prime})\cdot\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}(o^{\prime},o){\,\mathrm{d}}o^{\prime}\Bigr|,

where the last equality is by the fact that
\psi_{i}
is a distribution function over
{\mathcal{S}}
for any
i\in[d_{\rm s}]
. The right-hand side of (
A.14
) is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\Bigl|\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\rm s}}[(\Lambda_{h})^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot\int_{\mathcal{O}}\nu_{h,j}(o^{\prime})\cdot\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}(o^{\prime},o){\,\mathrm{d}}o^{\prime}\Bigr|

(A.15)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\bigl|[(\Lambda_{h})^{-1}]_{i,j}\bigr|\cdot\Bigl|\int_{\mathcal{O}}\nu_{h,j}(o^{\prime})\cdot\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}(o^{\prime},o){\,\mathrm{d}}o^{\prime}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\Bigl(\max_{j\in[d_{\rm s}]}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\bigl|[(\Lambda_{h})^{-1}]_{i,j}\bigr|\Bigr)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\Bigl|\int_{\mathcal{O}}\nu_{h,j}(o^{\prime})\cdot\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}(o^{\prime},o){\,\mathrm{d}}o^{\prime}\Bigr|.

For any
(h,j)\in[H]\times[d_{\rm s}]
, because the kernel function
\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}
is uniformly bounded by
1
, following Assumption
A.4
, and
\nu_{h,j}
is a distribution function over
\mathcal{O}
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{\mathcal{O}}\nu_{h,j}(o^{\prime})\cdot\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}(o^{\prime},o){\,\mathrm{d}}o^{\prime}\Bigr|\leq 1.

(A.16)

Combining (
A.14
)-(
A.16
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{S}}}|\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(s,o)|{\,\mathrm{d}}s\leq\Bigl(\max_{j\in[d_{\rm s}]}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\bigl|[(\Lambda_{h})^{-1}]_{i,j}\bigr|\Bigr)\cdot d,

(A.17)

for any
(h,\theta,o)\in[H]\times\Theta\times\mathcal{O}
. Note that for any
f\in L^{1}(\mathcal{O})
, we have

\displaystyle\|{\mathbb{Z}}^{\theta}_{h}f\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Bigl|\int_{\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(s,o)\cdot f(o){\,\mathrm{d}}o\Bigr|{\,\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\mathcal{S}}\times\mathcal{O}}|\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(s,o)|\cdot|f(o)|{\,\mathrm{d}}o{\,\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Bigl(\int_{\mathcal{S}}|\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(s,o)|{\,\mathrm{d}}s\Bigr)\cdot|f(o)|{\,\mathrm{d}}o,

combining which with (
A.17
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|{\mathbb{Z}}^{\theta}_{h}f\|_{1}\leq\Bigl(\max_{j\in[d_{\rm s}]}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\rm s}}\bigl|[(\Lambda_{h})^{-1}]_{i,j}\bigr|\Bigr)\cdot d\cdot\|f\|_{1}.

Therefore, we conclude the proof of Lemma
A.5
.
∎

Appendix B
Minimax Optimization in
OP-TENET

In this section, we discuss the details on how to implement the computation of
OP-TENET
in practice. Recall that in the planning phase (introduced in Section
3.2
) of each iteration of
OP-TENET
, we only consider the parameter
\theta
such that

\displaystyle L(\theta)=\max_{f\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}^{3}):\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1}~\max_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}^{2}}~\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}[(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f-\mathbb{S}f)(X)]

is sufficiently small. Here,
\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}
are operators defined in (
3.18
) and (
3.13
), respectively. Also,
\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
is the empirical distribution induced by
{\mathcal{D}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
, which consists of
k
observation tuples with
k
being the iteration index. For ease of presentation, we assume that we have access to the following planning oracle.

Oracle B.1
.

We denote by
\widehat{\pi}
a planning oracle for any given POMDP. In other word, the mapping
\widehat{\pi}:\Theta\rightarrow\Pi
satisfies
\widehat{\pi}(\theta)\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\pi\in\Pi}J(\theta,\pi)
for any
\theta\in\Theta
.

With the planning oracle defined above, we select the parameter
\theta_{k}
by solving the following constrained optimization problem,

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}J\bigl(\theta,\widehat{\pi}(\theta)\bigr)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad L(\theta)\leq\beta\cdot k^{-1/2}.

(B.1)

Then, we select the policy
\pi_{k}=\widehat{\pi}(\theta_{k})
.

B.1
Lagrangian Relaxation

In the sequel, we handle the constraint in (
B.1
) via the Lagrangian relaxation. In detail, solving (
B.1
) is equivalent to solving the minimax optimization problem,

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\max_{\lambda\geq 0}~-J\bigl(\theta,\widehat{\pi}(\theta)\bigr)+\sum_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\cdot\bigl(\widetilde{L}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\theta)-\beta\cdot k^{-1/2}\bigr),

(B.2)

where
\lambda=(\lambda_{h,a,a^{\prime}})_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{J}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(H-1)A^{2}}
and

\widetilde{L}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\theta)
is defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{L}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\theta)=\max_{f\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}^{3}):\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}[(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f-\mathbb{S}f)(X)].

(B.3)

Here, for notational simplicity, we denote by
\mathcal{I}
the set
\mathcal{I}=\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}^{2}
. Note that for each
(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}
, we need to search a function within a ball in
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}^{3})
. To this end, we propose to search functions within a large function approximator class, for example, a sufficiently large neural network. In detail, we denote by
f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
the parametrization of the function approximator, where
w
is the parameter with a candidate set
\mathcal{W}
. For example, we can build a neural network whose input space is
\mathcal{O}^{3}
and output space is
\mathbb{R}^{(H-1)A^{2}}
. Then,
w
represents the weights of all layers and

\displaystyle\bigl(f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(x)\bigr)_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(H-1)A^{2}}

is the output of the neural network corresponding any input
x\in\mathcal{O}^{3}
. Moreover,
by properly choosing the activation function of the output layer, we are able to make
\|f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
for any
w\in\mathcal{W}
and
(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}
. Then, we approximately compute
\widetilde{L}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\theta)
in (
B.3
) by computing
\max_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\widehat{L}^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\theta)
, where

\displaystyle\widehat{L}^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}[(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-\mathbb{S}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}})(X)],

(B.4)

for any
w\in\mathcal{W}
. Combining (
B.2
)-(
B.4
), we approximately solve the constrained optimization problem in (
B.1
) by solving

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\max_{\lambda\geq 0,w\in\mathcal{W}}~-J\bigl(\theta,\widehat{\pi}(\theta)\bigr)+\sum_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\cdot\bigl(\widehat{L}^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\theta)-\beta\cdot k^{-1/2}\bigr).

(B.5)

B.2
Stochastic Gradient Method

We denote by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\lambda,w)=-J\bigl(\theta,\widehat{\pi}(\theta)\bigr)+\sum_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\cdot\bigl(\widehat{L}^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\theta)-\beta\cdot k^{-1/2}\bigr)

the minimax objective in (
B.5
). In the sequel, we consider the stochastic gradient method for solving the minimax optimization problem in (
B.5
). In detail, suppose that we have unbiased stochastic gradient estimators
g_{\theta}
,
g_{\lambda}
, and
g_{w}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g_{\theta}(\theta,\lambda,w)]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\lambda,w),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g_{\lambda}(\theta,\lambda,w)]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\lambda,w),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g_{w}(\theta,\lambda,w)]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\lambda,w),

for any
(\theta,\lambda,w)\in\Theta\times\mathbb{R}^{(H-1)A^{2}}\times\mathcal{W}
. Also, computing
g_{\theta}
,
g_{\lambda}
, and
g_{w}
does not require access to the full data set
{\mathcal{D}}=\{{\mathcal{D}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\}_{h\in\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}^{2}}
and thus has a low computation cost. In each iteration, starting from any
(\theta,\lambda,w)
within the candidate set, we first update
\lambda
and
w
by running

\displaystyle\lambda\leftarrow\lambda+\eta_{\lambda}\cdot g_{\lambda}(\theta,\lambda,w),\quad w\leftarrow w+\eta_{w}\cdot g_{w}(\theta,\lambda,w)

for
N_{\rm dual}
steps. Then, we update
\theta
by running

\displaystyle\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta_{\theta}\cdot g_{\theta}(\theta,\lambda,w).

for
N_{\rm primal}
steps. Here,
\eta_{\theta}
,
\eta_{\lambda}
,
\eta_{w}
are constant stepsizes. Note that after each update, if the updated parameter is not in the candidate set, we need to run an extra projection step, which replaces the updated parameter by its closest neighbor within the candidate set. Because we need to call the planning oracle
\widehat{\pi}
after updating
\theta
, which has a relatively high computation cost, it is better to set
N_{\rm primal}=1
and set
N_{\rm dual}
as a large number.

In the sequel, we construct unbiased gradient estimators for the objective
{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\lambda,w)
.

Construction of
g_{\lambda}
:

To construct
g_{\lambda}(\theta,\lambda,x)
, note that we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\lambda,w)=\widehat{L}^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\theta)-\beta\cdot k^{-1/2}

for any
(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}^{2}
. Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be a batch of data sampled from
{\mathcal{D}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
uniformly at random. Then, by computing the batch average, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{B}}|}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{B}}}(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-\mathbb{S}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}})(x)-\beta\cdot k^{-1/2}\Bigr]=\partial_{\lambda_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\lambda,w).

(B.6)

For any
(i,j,x)\in[d_{\rm o}]\times[d_{\rm o}]\times\mathcal{O}^{3}
, let
Y_{i,j,x}
and
Y^{\prime}_{i,j,x}
be independent random variables in
\mathcal{O}^{3}
sampled from the distributions
\phi_{i}
and
\phi_{j}
, respectively. Then, by the definition of the operator
\mathbb{S}
in (
3.18
), we have

\displaystyle(\mathbb{S}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}})(x)=\sum_{i,j\in[d_{\rm o}]}\mathbb{E}\bigl[[G^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(x,Y_{i,j,x})\cdot f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(Y^{\prime}_{i,j,x})\bigr].

(B.7)

Similarly, applying the definition of the operator
\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}
in (
3.13
), we have

\displaystyle(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}})(x)

(B.8)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i,j\in[d_{\rm o}]}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[[G^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(x,Y_{i,j,x})\cdot\int_{\mathcal{O}^{2}}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\bm{o}_{h-1},\widetilde{o}_{h},\widetilde{o}_{h+1})\cdot{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta}_{h,a}(\bm{o}_{h},\widetilde{o}_{h},\widetilde{o}_{h+1}){\,\mathrm{d}}\widetilde{o}_{h}{\,\mathrm{d}}\widetilde{o}_{h+1}\Bigr],

where we denote
Y^{\prime}_{i,j,x}=(\bm{o}_{h-1},\bm{o}_{h},\bm{o}_{h+1})
. Moreover, let
\phi_{\rm ip}
be a distribution supported on
\mathcal{O}^{2}
and
\widetilde{Y}_{i,j,x}=(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})
be a random variable in
\mathcal{O}^{2}
sampled from
\phi_{\rm ip}
. Then, following the idea of importance sampling, we can rewrite (
B.8
) as

\displaystyle(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}})(x)

(B.9)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i,j\in[d_{\rm o}]}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[[G^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(x,Y_{i,j,x})\cdot\frac{f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\bm{o}_{h-1},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})\cdot{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta}_{h,a}(\bm{o}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})}{\phi_{\rm ip}(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})}\Bigr].

Combining (
B.6
)-(
B.9
), we construct
g_{\lambda}(\theta,\lambda,x)
by

\displaystyle[g_{\lambda}(\theta,\lambda,x)]_{h,a,a^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{B}}|}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{B}}}\bigl(\widehat{\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}(x)-\widehat{\mathbb{S}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}(x)\bigr)-\beta\cdot k^{-1/2},

for any
(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}^{2}
, where

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[d_{\rm o}]}[G^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(x,Y_{i,j,x})\cdot\frac{f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\bm{o}_{h-1},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})\cdot{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta}_{h,a}(\bm{o}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})}{\phi_{\rm ip}(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{S}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[d_{\rm o}]}[G^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(x,Y_{i,j,x})\cdot f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(Y^{\prime}_{i,j,x}).

Construction of
g_{w}
:
To construct
g_{w}(\theta,\lambda,x)
, note that we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\lambda,w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\cdot\nabla_{w}\widehat{L}^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}[(\nabla_{w}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-\nabla_{w}\mathbb{S}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}})(X)].

Thus, following the similar argument as in (
B.7
)-(
B.9
), we construct
g_{w}(\theta,\lambda,w)
as

\displaystyle g_{w}(\theta,\lambda,w)=\sum_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{B}}|}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{B}}}\bigl(\widehat{\nabla_{w}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}(x)-\widehat{\nabla_{w}\mathbb{S}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}(x)\bigr)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla_{w}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[d_{\rm o}]}[G^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(x,Y_{i,j,x})\cdot\frac{\nabla_{w}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\bm{o}_{h-1},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})\cdot{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta}_{h,a}(\bm{o}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})}{\phi_{\rm ip}(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})},

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla_{w}\mathbb{S}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[d_{\rm o}]}[G^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(x,Y_{i,j,x})\cdot\nabla_{w}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(Y^{\prime}_{i,j,x}).

Here,
Y_{i,j,x}
,
\bm{o}_{h-1}
,
\bm{o}_{h}
,
\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h}
, and
\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1}
are random variables defined the same as in the construction of
g_{\lambda}
.

Construction of
g_{\theta}
:

To construct
g_{\theta}(\theta,\lambda,x)
, note that we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\lambda,w)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\theta}J\bigl(\theta,\widehat{\pi}(\theta)\bigr)+\sum_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{L}^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\theta}J\bigl(\theta,\widehat{\pi}(\theta)\bigr)+\sum_{(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}[(\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}})(X)].

(B.10)

Following the similar argument as in (
B.8
)-(
B.9
), we have the following unbiased estimator of the expectation in the second term on the right-hand side of (
B.2
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{B}}|}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{B}}}\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{F}^{\theta}_{h,a^{\prime}}f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}(x)

(B.11)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{B}}|}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{B}}}\sum_{i,j\in[d_{\rm o}]}[G^{-1}]_{i,j}\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(x,Y_{i,j,x})\cdot\frac{f^{w}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\bm{o}_{h-1},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})\cdot\nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{B}}^{\theta}_{h,a}(\bm{o}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})}{\phi_{\rm ip}(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h},\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{h+1})}.

It remains to construct an unbiased estimator of the first term on the right-hand side of (
B.2
). Note that
\widehat{\pi}(\theta)
is the maximizer of
J(\theta,\cdot)
. Thus, by the envelop theorem, it suffices to estimate

\displaystyle\bigl(\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta,\pi)\bigr)\big|_{\pi=\widehat{\pi}(\theta)}.

(B.12)

Note that for any
(\theta,\pi)\in\Theta\times\Pi
, we have

\displaystyle J(\theta,\pi)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}^{H}\times\mathcal{O}^{H}}R\bigl(o_{1},\pi(\tau_{1}),\ldots,o_{H},\pi(\tau_{H})\bigr)\cdot\mu(s_{1})\cdot\mathcal{E}_{1}(o_{1}\,|\,s_{1})

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\cdot\Bigl(\prod_{h=1}^{H-1}{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{h}\bigl(s_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h},\pi(\tau_{h})\bigr)\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{h+1}(o_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h+1})\Bigr){\,\mathrm{d}}o_{1}\cdots{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{H}{\,\mathrm{d}}s_{1}\cdots{\,\mathrm{d}}s_{H}.

Then, using the chain rule of the derivative, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta,\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{H-1}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}^{H}\times\mathcal{O}^{H}}R\bigl(o_{1},\pi(\tau_{1}),\ldots,o_{H},\pi(\tau_{H})\bigr)\cdot\mu(s_{1})\cdot\mathcal{E}_{1}(o_{1}\,|\,s_{1})

(B.13)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\cdot\Bigl(\nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{h}\bigl(s_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h},\pi(\tau_{h})\bigr)\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{h+1}(o_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h+1})

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad+{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{h}\bigl(s_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h},\pi(\tau_{h})\bigr)\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{h+1}(o_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h+1})\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\cdot\Bigl(\prod_{h=1,h\neq i}^{H-1}{\mathcal{T}}^{\theta}_{h}\bigl(s_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h},\pi(\tau_{h})\bigr)\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{h+1}(o_{h+1}\,|\,s_{h+1})\Bigr){\,\mathrm{d}}o_{1}\cdots{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{H}{\,\mathrm{d}}s_{1}\cdots{\,\mathrm{d}}s_{H}.

Using the relation
\nabla\ln f=\nabla f/f
, we can rewrite the right-hand side of (
B.13
) to obtain

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta,\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{H-1}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}^{H}\times\mathcal{O}^{H}}R\bigl(o_{1},\pi(\tau_{1}),\ldots,o_{H},\pi(\tau_{H})\bigr)\cdot\mu(s_{1})\cdot\mathcal{E}_{1}(o_{1}\,|\,s_{1})

\displaystyle[b^{k}_{h}]_{i}\cdot[c^{k}_{h}]_{i}

\phi=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d_{\rm o}})
and write
\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
in short for
\rho^{\overline{\pi}_{k}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
. Then, we can rewrite (
F.5
) and (
F.6
) as

\displaystyle\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\cdot)=\phi(\cdot)^{\top}w^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}},\quad\widehat{\rho}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(\cdot)=\phi(\cdot)^{\top}\widehat{w}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}.

Following the above definitions, we have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\rho}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{1}

(F.7)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathcal{O}^{3}}|\phi(o_{h-1},o_{h},o_{h+1})^{\top}(\widehat{w}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-w^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}})|{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h-1}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h+1}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{\mathcal{O}^{3}}\|\phi(o_{h-1},o_{h},o_{h+1})\|_{2}\cdot\|\widehat{w}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-w^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h-1}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h+1}

\displaystyle\quad\leq d_{\rm o}\cdot\|\widehat{w}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-w^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{2},

where the first inequality is by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the last inequality uses

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}^{3}}\|\phi(o_{h-1},o_{h},o_{h+1})\|_{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h-1}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h+1}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{\mathcal{O}^{3}}\|\phi(o_{h-1},o_{h},o_{h+1})\|_{1}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h-1}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h+1}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\rm o}}\int_{\mathcal{O}^{3}}\phi_{j}(o_{h-1},o_{h},o_{h+1}){\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h-1}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h+1}=d_{\rm o},

as
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{\rm o}}
are distribution functions over
\mathcal{O}^{3}
. Thus, to upper bound
\|\widehat{\rho}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{1}
, it suffices to upper bound
\|\widehat{w}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-w^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{2}
.

To this end, we define the vector
U^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm o}}
by

\displaystyle[U^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}]_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\phi_{i},Y\sim\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}[{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)],

(F.8)

where
\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
is the empirical distribution over
\mathcal{O}^{3}
induced by the dataset
{\mathcal{D}}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
. Then, we can rewrite the definition of
\widehat{w}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
in (
E.5
) as

\displaystyle\widehat{w}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}=G^{-1}U^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}},

where the matrix
G\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm o}\times d_{\rm o}}
is defined in (
3.17
). Then, we can write

\displaystyle\|\widehat{w}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-w^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{2}=\|G^{-1}U^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-G^{-1}Gw^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{2}\leq 1/\alpha\cdot\|U^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-Gw^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{2}.

(F.9)

Moreover, by the definition of
G
in (
3.17
), we have

\displaystyle[Gw^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}]_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[d_{\rm o}]}[G]_{i,j}\cdot[w^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}]_{j}

(F.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[d_{\rm o}]}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\phi_{i},Y\sim\phi_{j}}[{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)]\cdot[w^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}]_{j}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{O}^{3}\times\mathcal{O}^{3}}\phi_{i}(x)\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(x,y)\cdot\sum_{j\in[d_{\rm o}]}\phi_{j}(y)\cdot[w^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}]_{j}{\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y,

By the definition of
w^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
in (
F.5
), we can write (
F.10
) as

\displaystyle[Gw^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}]_{i}=\int_{\mathcal{O}^{3}\times\mathcal{O}^{3}}\phi_{i}(x)\cdot{\mathcal{K}}(x,y)\cdot\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}(y){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\phi_{i},Y\sim\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}[{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)]

(F.11)

Using the notation of the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
, we can further rewrite (
F.8
) and (
F.11
) as

\displaystyle[U^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}]_{i}=\langle\mathbb{K}\phi_{i},\mathbb{K}\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\quad[Gw^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}]_{i}=\langle\mathbb{K}\phi_{i},\mathbb{K}\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

Recall that
\mathbb{K}
is defined in (
3.16
). Therefore, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the inner product in
\mathcal{H}
, we have

\displaystyle\|U^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-Gw^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[d_{\rm o}]}\bigl(\langle\mathbb{K}\phi_{i},\mathbb{K}\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-\mathbb{K}\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\bigr)^{2}

(F.12)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[d_{\rm o}]}\|\mathbb{K}\phi_{i}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\cdot\|\mathbb{K}\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-\mathbb{K}\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}.

Since
{\mathcal{K}}
is uniformly bounded by 1 as specified in Assumption
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{K}\phi_{i}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\phi_{i},Y\sim\phi_{i}}[{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)]\leq 1.

(F.13)

In the sequel, we characterize
\|\mathbb{K}\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-\mathbb{K}\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
on the right-hand side of (
F.12
) for any fixed
(h,a,a^{\prime})\in\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}^{2}
. For notational simplicity, we denote by
Y_{j}
the data point that is added to
{\mathcal{D}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
in the
j
-th iteration of Algorithm
1
. In other words, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}=\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}\},\quad\text{for any $k\in[K]$.}

Then, the random function process
\{\mathcal{M}_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{j}(\cdot)=(1/k)\cdot\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{\min\{j,k\}}{\mathcal{K}}(Y_{i},\cdot)-\sum_{i=1}^{\min\{j,k\}}(\mathbb{K}\rho^{\pi_{i-1}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}})(\cdot)\Bigr)

(F.14)

is a martingale in
\mathcal{H}
adapted to the data filtration
\{\mathcal{U}_{j}\}_{j\geq 0}
of Algorithm
1
. In detail, for any
j\in[K]
, we have that
\mathcal{U}_{j}
contains the information of all data collected in the first
j
iterations of Algorithm
1
. Note that the data point
Y_{j}
follows from the distribution
\rho^{\pi_{j-1}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
conditioning on
\mathcal{U}_{j-1}
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{K}}(Y_{j},x)-(\mathbb{K}\rho^{\pi_{j-1}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}})(x)\,|\,\mathcal{U}_{j-1}]=\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{K}}(Y_{j},x)\,|\,\mathcal{U}_{j-1}]-\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\rho^{\pi_{j-1}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}[{\mathcal{K}}(Y,x)]=0,

for any fixed
x\in\mathcal{O}^{3}
, which implies that
\{\mathcal{M}_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
defined above is a martingale. Moreover, we have that the total quadratic variation of
\{\mathcal{M}_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}(1/k^{2})\cdot\|{\mathcal{K}}(Y_{i},\cdot)-(\mathbb{K}\rho^{\pi_{i}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}})(\cdot)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}(1/k^{2})\cdot 4=4/k,

where the inequality uses the fact

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{K}}(Y_{i},\cdot)-(\mathbb{K}\rho^{\pi_{i}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}})(\cdot)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\quad={\mathcal{K}}(Y_{i},Y_{i})+\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\rho^{\pi_{i}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}},Y^{\prime}\sim\rho^{\pi_{i}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}[{\mathcal{K}}(Y,Y^{\prime})]-2\cdot\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\rho^{\pi_{i}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}}[{\mathcal{K}}(Y_{i},Y)]\leq 4

following the same argument of (
F.13
). Then, invoking Lemma
F.7
with

\displaystyle c^{2}=4/k\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon=\sqrt{8\log(2KHA^{2}/\delta)}\cdot k^{-1/2}

for any
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta/(KHA^{2})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}_{k}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\|\mathbb{K}\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-\mathbb{K}\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\sqrt{8\log(2KHA^{2}/\delta)}\cdot k^{-1/2}.

(F.15)

Here, the equality uses the definition of
\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}=\rho^{\overline{\pi}_{k}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}
. Recall that
\overline{\pi}_{k}
is the mixing policy that uniformly selects a policy from
\{\pi_{0},\ldots,\pi_{k-1}\}
at random, which implies

\displaystyle\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\rho^{\pi_{i}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{K}\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{K}\rho^{\pi_{i}}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}.

Then, by further applying the union bound, we have that, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the inequality in (
F.15
) holds for any
(k,h,a,a^{\prime})\in[K]\times\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}^{2}
. Combining such an upper bound with (
F.9
), (
F.12
) and (
F.13
), we have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{w}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-w^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{2}\leq d^{1/2}_{\rm o}/\alpha\cdot\sqrt{8\log(2KHA^{2}/\delta)}\cdot k^{-1/2}.

(F.16)

Combining (
F.7
) and (
F.16
), we know that, for any
\delta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\rho}^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}-\rho^{k}_{h,a,a^{\prime}}\|_{1}\leq d_{\rm o}^{3/2}/\alpha\cdot\sqrt{8\log(2KHA^{2}/\delta)}\cdot k^{-1/2},

for any
(k,h,a,a^{\prime})\in[K]\times\{2,\ldots,H\}\times\mathcal{A}^{2}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
. Therefore, we conclude the proof of Lemma
E.2
.
∎

F.4
Property of the Bridge Operator

Lemma F.1

(Bridge Property)
.

Recall that we denote by
\sigma_{h-1}
the event

\displaystyle{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h-1}={\overline{\tau}}_{h-1},\quad\bm{a}_{h-1}=a_{h-1}.

For any
(h,\theta,{\overline{\tau}}_{h-1},a_{h-1})\in[H]\times\Theta\times\overline{\Gamma}_{h-1}\times\mathcal{A}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(\widetilde{s}_{h},\bm{o}_{h})\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}]=p_{\theta}(\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{h}=\widetilde{s}_{h}\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}).

Proof.

By the tower property of the expectation and the Markov property of the POMDP, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(\widetilde{s}_{h},\bm{o}_{h})\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigl[\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(\widetilde{s}_{h},\bm{o}_{h})\,|\,\bm{s}_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h-1}=a_{h-1}]\,\big|\,\sigma_{h-1}\bigr].

(F.19)

Note that, for any
s_{h-1}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(\widetilde{s}_{h},\bm{o}_{h})\,|\,\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h-1}=a_{h-1}]

(F.20)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(\widetilde{s}_{h},o_{h})\cdot p_{\theta}(\bm{o}_{h}=o_{h}\,|\,\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h-1}=a_{h-1}){\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathcal{O}\times{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(\widetilde{s}_{h},o_{h})\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{h}(o_{h}\,|\,s_{h})\cdot p_{\theta}(\bm{s}_{h}=s_{h}\,|\,\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h-1}=a_{h-1}){\,\mathrm{d}}s_{h}{\,\mathrm{d}}o_{h}.

We define the function
f:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle f(s_{h})=p_{\theta}(\bm{s}_{h}=s_{h}\,|\,\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h-1}=a_{h-1}),\quad\text{for any $s_{h}\in{\mathcal{S}}$.}

Then, we have
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\rm s}
and we can write the right-hand side of (
F.20
) as

\displaystyle(\mathbb{Z}^{\theta}_{h}\mathbb{O}^{\theta}_{h}f)(\widetilde{s}_{h})=f(\widetilde{s}_{h})=p_{\theta}(\bm{s}_{h}=\widetilde{s}_{h}\,|\,\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h-1}=a_{h-1})

following Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
. In other words, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(\widetilde{s}_{h},\bm{o}_{h})\,|\,\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h-1}=a_{h-1}]

(F.21)

\displaystyle\quad=p_{\theta}(\bm{s}_{h}=\widetilde{s}_{h}\,|\,\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h-1}=a_{h-1}).

Combining (
F.19
) and (
F.21
) and using the Markov property of the POMDP, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(\widetilde{s}_{h},\bm{o}_{h})\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}]

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathcal{S}}p_{\theta}(\bm{s}_{h}=\widetilde{s}_{h}\,|\,\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h-1}=a_{h-1})\cdot p_{\theta}(\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1}\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}){\,\mathrm{d}}s_{h-1}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathcal{S}}p_{\theta}(\bm{s}_{h}=\widetilde{s}_{h}\,|\,\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1},\sigma_{h-1})\cdot p_{\theta}(\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1}\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}){\,\mathrm{d}}s_{h-1}

\displaystyle\quad=p_{\theta}(\bm{s}_{h}=\widetilde{s}_{h}\,|\,\sigma_{h-1}),

which concludes the proof of lemma
F.1
.
∎

3.1
, which adds the state information to the expectation condition.

Lemma F.2

(Variant of Lemma
3.1
)
.

For any
(h,\theta,\pi,s_{h-1},\overline{\tau}_{h-1},a_{h-1})\in[H]\times\Theta\times\Pi\times{\mathcal{S}}\times\overline{\Gamma}_{h-1}\times\mathcal{A}
and
f\in L^{\infty}(\overline{\Gamma}_{h+1})

3.1
. Following the notation in Lemma
3.1
, by the definition of
{\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}
in (

), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[({\mathbb{B}}^{\theta,\pi}_{h}f)({\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h})\,|\,\bm{s}_{h-1}=s_{h-1},\sigma_{h-1}]

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}\times\mathcal{O}^{3}}f\bigl({\overline{\tau}}_{h}^{\dagger},\pi(\tau_{h}^{\dagger}),{\widetilde{o}}_{h+1}\bigr)\cdot p_{\theta}\bigl(\bm{o}_{h}=\widetilde{o}_{h},\bm{o}_{h+1}=\widetilde{o}_{h+1}\,|\,\bm{s}_{h}=\widetilde{s}_{h},\bm{a}_{h}=\pi(\tau_{h}^{\dagger})\bigr)

.

F.5
Properties of the Value Functions

Lemma F.3
.

For any
(h,\pi,\theta,{\overline{\tau}}_{h})\in[H]\times\Pi\times\Theta\times\overline{\Gamma}_{h}
, it holds that

\displaystyle V^{\theta,\pi}_{h}({\overline{\tau}}_{h})=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta,\pi}\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{H}\bm{r}_{i}\,\Big|\,\bm{s}_{h}=s_{h},{\overline{\bm{\tau}}}_{h-1}={\overline{\tau}}_{h-1},\bm{a}_{h-1}=a_{h-1}\Bigr]\cdot\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{h}(s_{h},o_{h}){\,\mathrm{d}}s_{h}.

(F.25)

Here, we denote
{\overline{\tau}}_{h}=({\overline{\tau}}_{h-1},a_{h-1},o_{h})
following the definition in (
2.3
).

Proof.

Lemma F.4
.

Therefore, we conclude the proof of Lemma
F.6
.
∎

F.7
Concentration Inequality

Lemma F.7
.

Suppose that
\{\mathcal{M}_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
is a martingale defined on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. For any
c>0
, if we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|\mathcal{M}_{j+1}-\mathcal{M}_{j}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\leq c^{2},

(F.45)

then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\sup_{j\geq 1}\|\mathcal{M}_{j}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\varepsilon\Bigr)\leq 2\exp\Bigl\{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2c^{2}}\Bigr\}.

Proof.

The lemma is a special case of Theorem 3.5 in
Pinelis 1994
(see also, Theorem 3 in
Pinelis 1992
), which is a more general result for martingales in Banach spaces.
∎
