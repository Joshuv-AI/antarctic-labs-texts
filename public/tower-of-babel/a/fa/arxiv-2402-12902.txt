Lipschitz stability for an inverse source problem of the wave equation with kinetic boundary conditions
Salah-Eddine Chorfi 1, Ghita El Guermai 1, Lahcen Maniar 1, Walid Zouhair 2

Abstract
. In this paper, we present a refined approach to establish a global Lipschitz stability for an inverse source problem concerning the determination of forcing terms in the wave equation with mixed boundary conditions. It consists of boundary conditions incorporating a dynamic boundary condition and Dirichlet boundary condition on disjoint subsets of the boundary. The primary contribution of this article is the rigorous derivation of a sharp Carleman estimate for the wave system with a dynamic boundary condition. In particular, our findings complete and drastically improve the earlier results established by Gal and Tebou [SIAM J. Control Optim., 55 (2017), 324–364]. This is achieved by using a different weight function to overcome some relevant difficulties. As for the stability proof, we extend to dynamic boundary conditions a recent argument avoiding cut-off functions. Finally, we also show that our developed Carleman estimate yields a sharp boundary controllability result. Keywords. Inverse problem, wave equation, dynamic boundary condition, stability estimate, Carleman estimate. MSC (2020). 35R30, 74J25, 35L05, 93B05, 93B07.



1.
Introduction

The wave equation
\partial^{2}_{t}y(x,t)-d\Delta y(x,t)=0
is a hyperbolic Partial Differential Equation (PDE) that models the propagation of waves in a medium
\Omega
and their positions in space
x
over time
t
, where
d>0
represents the wave velocity. This mathematical model is employed across diverse disciplines to represent various wave phenomena, including vibrating structures, electromagnetic waves, acoustic waves, etc. Solving the wave equation involves specifying initial and boundary conditions to accurately depict the behavior of the wave over space and time. The equations of motion for a given wave can be derived from the principle of stationary action. Classical boundary conditions (Dirichlet, Neumann, or Robin) neglect the momentum of the wave on the boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
of the physical domain. Taking into account this momentum and the bulk-surface interaction in the medium gives rise to dynamic boundary conditions of the form

\partial_{t}^{2}y_{\mid_{\Gamma}}-\Delta_{\Gamma}y_{\mid_{\Gamma}}=-d\partial_{\nu}y,

where
y_{\mid_{\Gamma}}
is the trace of
y
on
\Gamma
. These boundary conditions are also known as nonlocally reacting kinetic conditions, as they arise from a kinetic energy function with terms on the boundary
\Gamma
(potential energy could also be considered). The conormal derivative term
-d\partial_{\nu}y
acts as a source on
\Gamma
. For more details, we refer to
[
21
]
.

Let
T>0
be a fixed time and
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
a bounded domain (
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
) with smooth boundary
\Gamma
such that
\Gamma=\Gamma^{0}\cup\Gamma^{1}
, with
\Gamma^{0}
and
\Gamma^{1}
are two closed subsets and
\Gamma^{0}\cap\Gamma^{1}=\varnothing
. We consider the wave equation with dynamic/Dirichlet (kinetic) boundary conditions

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}y-d\Delta y+q_{\Omega}(x)y=f(x,t),&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{t}^{2}y_{\Gamma}-\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}+d\partial_{\nu}y+q_{\Gamma}(x)y_{\Gamma}=g(x,t),\quad y_{\Gamma}=y_{\mid_{\Gamma}},&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
y=0,&\text{ on }\Gamma^{0}\times(0,T),\\
(y(\cdot,0),y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0))=(0,0),\quad(\partial_{t}y(\cdot,0),\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0))=(0,0),&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1}.\end{cases}

The above system models, for instance, vibrations of the membrane of a bass drum
[
39
]
, where the quantity
(y(x,t),y_{\Gamma}(x,t))
describe the displacements of the membrane at point
x
and time
t
. The constants
d>0
and
\delta>0
represent the wave velocities in
\Omega
and
\Gamma
, respectively. Moreover,
f
and
g
designate the forcing terms. The potentials in system (
1.1
) are assumed to be bounded, i.e.,
q_{\Omega}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
q_{\Gamma}\in L^{\infty}\left(\Gamma^{1}\right)
. We denote by
\Delta_{\Gamma}
the Laplace-Beltrami operator and by
\partial_{\nu}y
the normal derivative with the outer unit normal vector field
\nu
.

We aim to establish a global Lipschitz stability for the source terms in system (
1.1
). Our purpose is to determine a couple of unknown forcing terms
\mathbf{F}:=(f,g)
in (
1.1
) that belong to an admissible set from a single partial measurement of the boundary flux
\partial_{\nu}y
on a subset of
\Gamma^{0}
(see Theorem
4.1
).

Hyperbolic evolution equations with dynamic-type boundary conditions have attracted considerable attention in the last years and have found many applications in various situations where wave propagation occurs along the boundary of a physical domain. Examples include vibrating membranes, equations of motion, structural acoustics, and oscillatory phenomena. The study of wave equations with kinetic boundary conditions of dynamic type requires careful consideration of both the PDE describing the evolution of the system inside the bulk and the evolution on its surface. Taking into account the coupled nature of the wave system, recent research has been done for the physical derivation

]
, well-posedness and regularity of such equations, see
[

,

,

]
and

,

,

,

,
41
,
42
,
43
]
. However, in terms of inverse problems and controllability, the existing literature is quite limited compared to the static case (Dirichlet, Neumann, and Robin), despite their importance in applications. Interested readers can be directed to
[

,

The so-called Carleman estimates provide a powerful tool for studying inverse problems and controllability. Recently, Gal and Tebou
[
16
]
have proposed a Carleman estimate in the context of boundary controllability of a nonconservative version of (
1.1
) at any
T>T_{0}
(

is given explicitly). The main result of the aforementioned work has been established using the classical weight function

(1.2)

\zeta(x,t)=|x-x_{0}|^{2}-\beta t^{2}+C_{0},\qquad(x,t)\in\Omega\times(-T,T)

for some
x_{0}\notin\overline{\Omega}
and constants
\beta,C_{0}>0
(
|\cdot|
is the Euclidean norm). The authors have used the following formula for the Hessian in
\Gamma
(as a Riemannian manifold) of
\zeta
:

(1.3)

\nabla_{\Gamma}^{2}\zeta=2\mathbb{I}_{(n-1)\times(n-1)},

where
\mathbb{I}
denotes the identity matrix, see
[
16
, p. 338]
. It turned out that the formula (
1.3
) played a crucial role in proving the Carleman estimate in
[

, Theorem 2.2]
, as it implies the pseudo-convexity on the boundary (see Hörmander
[

In the present paper, our initial step consists of proving a global Carleman estimate for the system (
1.1
) that corrects and improves the previous one from
[
16
]

Furthermore, we shall consider a new application of our Carleman estimate to an inverse problem by proving a global Lipschitz stability for the simultaneous recovery of two forcing terms from a single boundary measurement. As for parabolic equations with dynamic boundary conditions, we refer to

2.
General setting

2.1.
Well-posedness

We briefly outline the well-posedness of system (
1.1
). We will use the following real space

\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}\left(\Gamma^{1}\right),

which is a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product given by

\displaystyle\langle(u,u_{\Gamma}),(v,v_{\Gamma})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}:=\langle(u,u_{\Gamma}),(v,v_{\Gamma})\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle u_{\Gamma},v_{\Gamma}\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\Gamma^{1}\right)},

where the Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
is denoted by
\mathrm{d}x
and the surface measure on
\Gamma
by
\mathrm{d}S
. We also consider the following spaces:

\displaystyle H_{0,\Gamma^{0}}^{k}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\left\{y\in H^{k}(\Omega):y=0\text{ on }\Gamma^{0}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\left(y,y_{\Gamma}\right)\in H_{0,\Gamma^{0}}^{k}(\Omega)\times H^{k}\left(\Gamma^{1}\right):y_{\Gamma}=y_{\mid_{\Gamma^{1}}}\right\},\qquad k=1,2.

For simplicity, we set
\mathcal{E}^{1}=\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}^{-1}
for its dual space with respect to the pivot
\mathcal{H}
. Let
\mathbf{Y}:=(y,y_{\Gamma})
, then system (
1.1
) can be written as an abstract Cauchy problem

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\Psi=\mathbf{A}\Psi+\mathbf{F},\quad 0<t\leqslant T,\\
\Psi(0)=0,\end{cases}

where
\Psi:=(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y}_{t})
and
\mathbf{F}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f\\
g\end{array}\right)\in L^{2}\left(\Omega\times(0,T)\right)\times L^{2}\left(\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T)\right)
.
We define the linear operator
\mathbf{A}\colon D(\mathbf{A})\subset\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{I}_{2\times 2}\\
\mathbf{B}&\mathbb{O}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{with}\quad D(\mathbf{A})=D(\mathbf{B})\times\mathcal{H},

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Y}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}d\Delta y-q_{\Omega}y\\
\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}-d\partial_{\nu}y-q_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}\end{pmatrix},

where
D(\mathbf{B})=\left\{\left(y,y_{\Gamma}\right)\in\mathcal{E}:\Delta y\in L^{2}(\Omega),\,\,\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}-d\partial_{\nu}y\in L^{2}\left(\Gamma^{1}\right)\right\}
. The well-posedness result for system (
2.1
) can be derived following the approach outlined in
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
, leveraging the theory of semigroups.

2.2.
Weight functions

We start with the following remark that presents a counterexample to the identity (
1.3
).

Remark 2.1
.

The weight function defined
\zeta
by (
1.2
) does not satisfy the identity (
1.3
). Indeed, for
\Omega=\{(x,y,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3},\,x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}<1\}
, the boundary
\Gamma:=\mathbb{S}^{2}
is given by the unit
2
-sphere. Let
x_{0}=(0,0,2)\notin\overline{\Omega}
and consider the following parameterization of the
2
-sphere

\displaystyle\Phi:[0,2\pi)\times[0,\pi)\,\,

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbb{S}^{2}

\displaystyle(\theta,\varphi)\,\,

\displaystyle\mapsto(\cos(\theta)\sin(\varphi),\sin(\theta)\sin(\varphi),\cos(\varphi)).

We have
\zeta(x,y,z,t)=\zeta(\Phi(\theta,\varphi),t)=5-4\cos(\varphi)-\beta t^{2}+C_{0}
on
\Gamma
. Using formula (4.3.53) in
[
30
]
, a simple calculation yields

\nabla_{\Gamma}^{2}\zeta=\begin{pmatrix}4\sin^{2}(\varphi)\cos(\varphi)&0&\\
\\
0&4\cos(\varphi)&\end{pmatrix}\neq 2\mathbb{I}_{2\times 2}.

To define appropriate weight functions, we consider a specific type of convex set with some smoothness property of its boundary. We refer to
[
36
]
for the details.

Definition 2.2
.

An open, bounded, and convex set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be strongly convex if
\partial U
is of class
C^{2}
and all the principal curvatures are strictly positive functions on
\partial U.

Henceforth, we assume that
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}
, where
\Omega_{0}
is an open bounded set with
C^{2}
-boundary and
\Omega_{1}
is an open strongly convex set. Additionally, we may assume that
0\in\Omega_{1}
(up to a translation) so that
0\notin\overline{\Omega}
. A standard example is given by the annulus defined by
\Omega=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,r_{1}<|x|<r_{2}\}=B_{r_{2}}\setminus\overline{B_{r_{1}}}
, with
0<r_{1}<r_{2}
and
B_{r}
denotes the open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of center
0
and radius
r
. Furthermore, we set
\Gamma^{k}=\partial\Omega_{k}
for
k=0,1
.

Following
[
36
]
, we consider the Minkowski (gauge) function of the set
\Omega_{1}
defined by

(2.2)

\mu(x)=\inf\{\lambda\,\colon\lambda>0\,\text{ and }\,x\in\lambda\Omega_{1}\},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

For example, if
\Omega_{1}=B_{1}
, then
\mu(\cdot)=|\cdot|
. We refer to
[
24
]
for the main properties of
\mu
:

•

\mu(0)=0
and
\mu(x)>0
for all
x\neq 0
;

•

\mu(\lambda x)=\lambda\mu(x)
for all
\lambda>0
and

;

•

\Omega_{1}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\mu(x)<1\right\}
;

•

\Gamma^{1}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\mu(x)=1\right\}
.

To ensure sufficient regularity of the function

, we assume that
\Gamma^{1}
is of class

. Then by the following parametrization of
\Gamma^{1}
:

\displaystyle\Phi\colon\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Gamma^{1}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{x}{\mu(x)},

we see that

is of class

in
\overline{\Omega}
. The map
\Phi
is well-defined and bijective by the convexity of

and the fact that

. Note that
\Phi
is the inverse function of the parametrization considered in
[
36
]
. We also consider

(2.3)

\psi_{0}(x)=\mu^{2}(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

We shall use the following properties of the function
\psi_{0}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Under the above assumptions, the function
\psi_{0}
defined by (
2.3
) satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\psi_{0}\in C^{4}(\overline{\Omega})
,

(2)

\psi_{0}=1
on
\Gamma^{1}
,

(3)

\nabla\psi_{0}\neq 0

,

(4)

There exists

such that
\nabla^{2}\psi_{0}(\xi,\xi)\geqslant 2\rho|\xi|^{2}

Proof.

Since
\psi_{0}=\mu^{2}
, the assertions

and
2
follow directly from their counterparts for

, see
[

, Proposition 3.1]
. The assertions

and the fact that

for some constant

(since

is continuous in

). We use the properties

and

from
[

∎

Then, we define the Carleman weight functions as follows

(2.4)

\displaystyle\psi(x,t)=\psi_{0}(x)-\beta t^{2}+C_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(x,t)=e^{\lambda\psi(x,t)},\qquad(x,t)\in\overline{\Omega}\times[-T,T],

where

,

, and

Remark 2.4

By the convexity assumption on

. This implies that

The weight function defined in (

) has been recently employed to prove a Carleman estimate for a Schrödinger equation with dynamic boundary conditions, leading to an exact controllability result in

. We also refer to

]

3.
Carleman estimate

This section presents our key result on the Carleman estimate for (
1.1
). First, we introduce the following notations:

\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times(-T,T),\quad\gamma_{T}=\gamma\times(-T,T)\quad\text{ and }\qquad\Gamma^{k}_{T}=\Gamma^{k}\times(-T,T)\quad\text{ for }k=0,1,

where

(3.1)

\displaystyle\gamma:=\left\{x\in\Gamma\colon\;\partial_{\nu}\psi_{0}(x)\geqslant 0\right\}\subseteq\Gamma^{0}.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exist positive constants
C
,
s_{1}
and
\lambda_{1}
such that for all
\lambda\geqslant\lambda_{1}
and
s\geqslant s_{1}
, the following Carleman estimate holds

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{T}}{e^{2s\varphi}\left(s^{3}\lambda^{3}\varphi^{3}|y|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|\nabla y|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|\partial_{t}y|^{2}\right)}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{\Gamma_{T}^{1}}{e^{2s\varphi}\left(s^{3}\lambda^{3}\varphi^{3}|y_{\Gamma}|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|\partial_{\nu}y|^{2}+s\lambda\varphi|\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}|^{2}+[d(\delta-d)-8\beta\delta]s\lambda\varphi|\nabla_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}|^{2}\right)}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t

(3.2)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C\int_{\Omega_{T}}{e^{2s\varphi}|f|^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+C\int_{\Gamma_{T}^{1}}{e^{2s\varphi}|g|^{2}}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t+Cs\lambda\int_{\gamma_{T}}{e^{2s\varphi}\varphi|\partial_{\nu}y|^{2}}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt+Cs\lambda\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{2s\varphi(x,T)}\varphi(x,T)(|\partial_{t}y(x,T)|^{2}+|\nabla y(x,T)|^{2})\mathrm{d}x+Cs^{3}\lambda^{3}\int_{\Omega}e^{2s\varphi(x,T)}\varphi^{3}(x,T)|y(x,T)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt+Cs\lambda\int_{\Omega}e^{2s\varphi(x,-T)}\varphi(x,-T)\left(|\partial_{t}y(x,-T)|^{2}+|\nabla y(x,-T)|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt+Cs^{3}\lambda^{3}\int_{\Omega}e^{2s\varphi(x,-T)}\varphi^{3}(x,-T)|y(x,-T)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+Cs\lambda\int_{\Gamma^{1}}e^{2s\varphi(x,T)}\varphi(x,T)|\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}(x,T)|^{2}\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt+Cs\lambda\int_{\Gamma^{1}}e^{2s\varphi(x,-T)}\varphi(x,-T)|\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}(x,-T)|^{2}\mathrm{d}S+Cs^{3}\lambda^{3}\int_{\Gamma^{1}}e^{2s\varphi(x,T)}\varphi^{3}(x,T)|y_{\Gamma}(x,T)|^{2}\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt+Cs^{3}\lambda^{3}\int_{\Gamma^{1}}e^{2s\varphi(x,-T)}\varphi^{3}(x,-T)|y_{\Gamma}(x,-T)|^{2}\mathrm{d}S,

where

(y,y_{\Gamma})\in H^{2}\left(-T,T;\mathcal{H}\right)\cap L^{2}(-T,T;\mathcal{E})
satisfies

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle:=\partial_{t}^{2}y-d\Delta y+q_{\Omega}y\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T}),

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle:=\partial_{t}^{2}y_{\Gamma}-\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}+d\partial_{\nu}y+q_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}\in L^{2}\left(\Gamma_{T}^{1}\right).

Some relevant remarks are in order.

Remark 3.2
.

The Carleman estimate (
3.1
) corrects and drastically improves
[
16
, Theorem 2.2]
. Firstly, by the modified weight function (
2.4
), we use the fact that
\nabla_{\Gamma}^{2}\psi=0
on
\Gamma^{1}
instead of the incorrect identity (
1.3
). Secondly, the term with
|\nabla_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}|^{2}
can be kept only on the left-hand side of the Carleman estimate as long as
\delta>d
by choosing
\beta\leqslant\frac{d(\delta-d)}{8\delta}
, while in
[
16
, Theorem 2.2]
it appears on the right-hand side. Moreover, we have been able to absorb the term with
|\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}|^{2}
into the left-hand side, in contrast to
[
16
]
. Finally, being different from
[
16
, Section 2.3]
, we do not assume that
(y,y_{\Gamma})
and its derivatives are null at

to avoid cut-off functions in the applications (see Sections
4
and
5
).

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Here, we prove the Carleman estimate stated in Theorem
3.1
by adopting a refined strategy compared to the one used in
[
16
, Theorem 2.2]
. For the reader’s convenience, the proof will be detailed in several steps. To simplify, we will often write
y
instead of
y_{\Gamma}
for functions on the boundary
\Gamma
. We also neglect zeroth-order terms as they do not influence the Carleman estimate.

\bullet

Step 1: Change of variable.

Set
z=e^{s\varphi}y
and

Pz=e^{s\varphi}(\partial_{t}^{2}-d\Delta)(e^{-s\varphi}z)\;\text{in}\;\Omega_{T}\quad\text{and}\quad P_{\Gamma}z=e^{s\varphi}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}+d\partial_{\nu})(e^{-s\varphi}z)\;\text{on}\;\Gamma_{T}^{1}.

Firstly, we compute
Pz
and
P_{\Gamma}z
, we have

\partial_{t}(e^{-s\varphi}z)=(-s\partial_{t}\varphi z+\partial_{t}z)e^{-s\varphi},\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla(e^{-s\varphi}z)=(-sz\nabla\varphi+\nabla z)e^{-s\varphi},

then

\partial_{t}^{2}(e^{-s\varphi}z)=(-s\partial_{t}^{2}\varphi z+s^{2}|\partial_{t}\varphi|^{2}z-2s\partial_{t}\varphi\partial_{t}z+\partial_{t}^{2}z)e^{-s\varphi},

and

\Delta(e^{-s\varphi}z)=(-s\Delta\varphi z+s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}z-2s\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla z+\Delta z)e^{-s\varphi}.

Therefore, the operator
P
can be written as follows

(3.3)

\displaystyle Pz=P_{1}z+P_{2}z\quad\text{ in }\Omega_{T},

where the operators
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are given by

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}&P_{1}z=\partial_{t}^{2}z-d\Delta z+s^{2}(|\partial_{t}\varphi|^{2}-d|\nabla\varphi|^{2})z,\\
&P_{2}z=-2s(\partial_{t}\varphi\partial_{t}z-d\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla z)-s(\partial_{t}^{2}\varphi-d\Delta\varphi)z.\end{array}

Applying similar reasoning, we express
P_{\Gamma}
as follows

(3.4)

\displaystyle P_{\Gamma}z=P_{1\Gamma}z+P_{2\Gamma}z\quad\text{ on }\Gamma_{T}^{1},

where
P_{1\Gamma}
and
P_{2\Gamma}
are given by

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}&P_{1\Gamma}z=\partial_{t}^{2}z-\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}z+d\partial_{\nu}z+s^{2}(|\partial_{t}\varphi|^{2}-\delta|\nabla_{\Gamma}\varphi|^{2})z,\\
&P_{2\Gamma}z=-2s\left(\partial_{t}\varphi\partial_{t}z-\delta\left<\nabla_{\Gamma}\varphi,\nabla_{\Gamma}z\right>_{\Gamma}\right)-s\left(\partial_{t}^{2}\varphi-\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}\varphi+d\partial_{\nu}\varphi\right)z.\end{array}

Now, by applying the
L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
-norm to (
3.3
) and the
L^{2}(\Gamma^{1}_{T})
-norm to (

&=\int_{\Omega}|f(x,0)|^{2}e^{2se^{\lambda(\psi_{0}(x)+C_{1})}}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma^{1}}|g(x,0)|^{2}e^{2se^{\lambda(\psi_{0}(x)+C_{1})}}\mathrm{d}S\\
&\geqslant e^{2se^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}}\left(\|f(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|g(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}\left(\Gamma^{1}\right)}^{2}\right),\end{split}

where
d_{0}:=\underset{x\in\overline{\Omega}}{\min}\,\mu(x)
. By (
4.23
) and (
4.24
), we obtain

(4.25)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|f(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|g(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}\left(\Gamma^{1}\right)}^{2}\\
\leqslant&Cs\lambda e^{-2se^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\gamma}\varphi|\partial_{t}\partial_{\nu}y|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t\\
&\hskip-11.38092pt+Cs^{3}\lambda^{3}e^{3\lambda(d_{1}^{2}-\beta T^{2}+C_{1})}e^{2s\left(e^{\lambda(d_{1}^{2}-\beta T^{2}+C_{1})}-e^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}\right)}\left(\|f(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|g(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}\left(\Gamma^{1}\right)}^{2}\right).\end{split}

On one hand, we have

Cs\lambda e^{-2se^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\gamma}\varphi|\partial_{t}\partial_{\nu}y|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t\leqslant Cs\lambda e^{\lambda(d_{1}^{2}+C_{1})}e^{-2se^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\gamma}|\partial_{t}\partial_{\nu}y|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t.

Since
\lambda e^{\lambda(d_{1}^{2}+C_{1})}e^{-2se^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}}\rightarrow 0
as
\lambda\to\infty
, then for a fixed large
\lambda
, we obtain

\lambda e^{\lambda(d_{1}^{2}+C_{1})}e^{-2se^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}}\leqslant C.

Therefore

(4.26)

\displaystyle Cs\lambda e^{-2se^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\gamma}\varphi|\partial_{t}\partial_{\nu}y|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t\leqslant Ce^{Cs}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\gamma}|\partial_{t}\partial_{\nu}y|^{2}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t.

We also have

e^{\lambda(d_{1}^{2}-\beta T^{2}+C_{1})}-e^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}<0.

Indeed,

e^{\lambda(d_{1}^{2}-\beta T^{2}+C_{1})}-e^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}=e^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}\left(e^{\lambda(d_{1}^{2}-d_{0}^{2}-\beta T^{2})}-1\right).

Since
T>\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho d}}\sqrt{d_{1}^{2}-d_{0}^{2}}=:T_{*}
, we can choose
\beta\in\left(\frac{d_{1}^{2}-d_{0}^{2}}{T^{2}},\rho d\right)
so that
d_{1}^{2}-d_{0}^{2}-\beta T^{2}<0
. Thus

e^{\lambda(d_{1}^{2}-d_{0}^{2}-\beta T^{2})}<1.

Then, for a fixed
\lambda
, we obtain

\underset{s\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}s^{3}\lambda^{3}e^{3\lambda(d_{1}^{2}-\beta T^{2}+C_{1})}e^{2s\left(e^{\lambda(d_{1}^{2}-\beta T^{2}+C_{1})}-e^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}\right)}=0.

By taking
s
sufficiently large, we obtain

(4.27)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&Cs^{3}\lambda^{3}e^{3\lambda(d_{1}^{2}-\beta T^{2}+C_{1})}e^{2s\left(e^{\lambda(d_{1}^{2}-\beta T^{2}+C_{1})}-e^{\lambda(d_{0}^{2}+C_{1})}\right)}\left(\|f(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|g(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}\left(\Gamma^{1}\right)}^{2}\right)\\
\leqslant&\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\|f(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|g(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}\left(\Gamma^{1}\right)}^{2}\right).\end{split}

Consequently (
4.25
)-(
4.27
) yield

(4.28)

\|f(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|g(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}\left(\Gamma^{1}\right)}^{2}\leqslant Ce^{Cs}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\gamma}|\partial_{t}\partial_{\nu}y|^{2}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t.

For
t\in(0,T)
, we have

f(x,t)=f(x,0)+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{\tau}f(x,\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\quad\text{and}\quad g(x,t)=g(x,0)+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{\tau}g(x,\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau.

Since
(f,g)\in\mathcal{S}(C_{0})
, then

(4.29)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&|f(x,t)|\leqslant|f(x,0)|+\int_{0}^{T}|\partial_{\tau}f(x,\tau)|\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant(1+C_{0}T)|f(x,0)|,\\
&|g(x,t)|\leqslant|g(x,0)|+\int_{0}^{T}|\partial_{\tau}g(x,\tau)|\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant(1+C_{0}T)|g(x,0)|.\end{split}

Combining (
4.28
) and (
4.29
), we obtain

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))}^{2}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T))}^{2}\leqslant CT(1+C_{0}T)e^{Cs}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\gamma}|\partial_{t}\partial_{\nu}y|^{2}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t.

Thus, by fixing
s\geqslant s_{1}
, the proof is achieved.
∎

5.
Boundary controllability of the wave system

In this section, we briefly discuss a second application of our Carleman estimate (Theorem
3.1
) to the exact boundary controllability of the system

(5.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}y-d\Delta y+q_{\Omega}(x,t)y=0,&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{t}^{2}y_{\Gamma}-\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}+d\partial_{\nu}y+q_{\Gamma}(x,t)y_{\Gamma}=0,\quad y_{\Gamma}=y_{\mid_{\Gamma}},&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
y=\mathds{1}_{\gamma}v,&\text{ on }\Gamma^{0}\times(0,T),\\
(y(\cdot,0),y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0))=(y_{0},y_{0,\Gamma}),\quad(\partial_{t}y(\cdot,0),\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}(\cdot,0))=(y_{1},y_{1,\Gamma}),&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1},\end{cases}

with one control force
v\in L^{2}(\gamma\times(0,T))
, where
q_{\Omega}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))
,
q_{\Gamma}\in L^{\infty}\left(\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T)\right)
, and
\gamma
is the control region defined in (
3.1
). The solution of (
5.1
) with less regular initial data should be understood in the transposition sense.

Simultaneously with the system (
5.1
), we consider its adjoint backward system

(5.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}z-d\Delta z+q_{\Omega}(x,t)z=0,&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{t}^{2}z_{\Gamma}-\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}z_{\Gamma}+d\partial_{\nu}z+q_{\Gamma}(x,t)z_{\Gamma}=0,\quad z_{\Gamma}=z_{\mid_{\Gamma}},&\text{ on }\Gamma^{1}\times(0,T),\\
z=0,&\text{ on }\Gamma^{0}\times(0,T),\\
(z(\cdot,T),z_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T))=(z_{0},z_{0,\Gamma}),\quad(\partial_{t}z(\cdot,T),\partial_{t}z_{\Gamma}(\cdot,T))=(z_{1},z_{1,\Gamma}),&\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1}.\end{cases}

By a standard duality argument, the exact controllability of the system (
5.1
) is equivalent to the observability inequality for the solution of the adjoint system (
5.2
) given by the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

We assume that
T>2T_{*}
and
\delta>d
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
(z_{0},z_{0,\Gamma},z_{1},z_{1,\Gamma})\in\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{H}
, the solution of (
5.2
) satisfies the observability inequality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\left|z_{1}(x)\right|^{2}+d|\nabla z_{0}(x)|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma^{1}}\left(\left|z_{1,\Gamma}(x)\right|^{2}+\delta\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}z_{0,\Gamma}(x)\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t

(5.3)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\int_{\gamma\times(0,T)}|\partial_{\nu}z|^{2}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t.

The proof of the above theorem follows from our Carleman estimate (Theorem
3.1
) by adopting the same strategy of
[
25
, Proposition 1]
, so we omit the details.

Remark 5.2
.

Theorem
5.1
extends and improves
[
4
, Theorem 2.2]
by giving a sharp lower bound for the time required for observability (see
[
4
, Section 5.2]
). For instance, if
\Omega_{1}=B_{1}
, then we can choose
\rho=1
. Hence, we obtain the observability with an explicit minimal time

2T_{*}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\underset{x\in\overline{\Omega}}{\max}\,|x|^{2}-\underset{x\in\overline{\Omega}}{\min}\,|x|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Remark 5.3
.

In the case when
\delta<d
, it has been shown in
[
4
, Theorem 2.4]
that the corresponding observability inequality fails at any time
T>0
.

Remark 5.4
.

It should be emphasized that the minimal time
2T_{*}
needed for observability is more sharp compared to the classical one
T_{1}=2\,\underset{x\in\overline{\Omega}}{\max}\,\mu(x)
(for
d=1
) obtained by the multiplier method. We refer to
[
14
]
for recent results in this context.

The main result of this section reads as follows.

Theorem 5.5
.

We assume that
T>2T_{*}
and
\delta>d
. Then the system (
5.1
) is exactly controllable in time
T
with a control acting on
\gamma
, i.e., for all
(y_{0},y_{0,\Gamma},y_{1},y_{1,\Gamma})\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{E}^{-1}
, there exists a control
v\in L^{2}(\gamma\times(0,T))
such that the solution of (
5.1
) satisfies

(y(T),y_{\Gamma}(T))=(0,0)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left(\partial_{t}y(T),\partial_{t}y_{\Gamma}(T)\right)=(0,0)\quad\text{ in }\Omega\times\Gamma^{1}.

The above controllability result is sharp in the sense that only one control force on the Dirichlet portion is used, in contrast to
[
16
, Section 4]
, where two controls are used. This fact has already been observed in a similar situation
[
9
]
when
\Omega
is a multi-dimensional interval. We also refer to
[
35
]
when
\Omega
is a polygon (or polyhedron).

6.
Open problems

This section is devoted to some relevant open problems that deserve further investigation.

Open problem 6.1

In Theorems
3.1
and
4.1
, we could absorb the term

(6.1)

s\lambda\int_{\Gamma^{1}_{T}}\varphi|\nabla_{\Gamma}y_{\Gamma}|^{2}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t

thanks to the assumption
\delta>d
, i.e., when the velocity of the wave on the surface
\Gamma^{1}
dominates the velocity of the wave in the bulk
\Omega
(see Remark
4.2
). Although the results in
[
16
]
treat both cases
\delta>d
and
\delta\leqslant d
, the term (
6.1
) is not absorbed into the left-hand side of the Carleman estimate, so the results need to be sharpened. Moreover, given the results of
[
4
, Section 2.2]
, the assumption
\delta>d
is more involved in such problems. Indeed, by Remark
5.3
, a sharp Carleman estimate for the case
\delta<d
is impossible. However, in the case
\delta=d
, a sharp observability inequality is still an open question.

Open problem 6.2

All previous Carleman estimates for the wave equations with dynamic boundary conditions incorporate a Dirichlet boundary condition on one portion of the boundary. Besides the possible applications, such a setting was considered with the purpose of avoiding substantial difficulty in the Carleman estimate. Notably, a Carleman estimate for the wave equation with dynamic boundary conditions on the whole boundary
\Gamma
is still an open problem to be resolved. Indeed, the classical weight functions that are employed for static boundary conditions are not well-adapted for dynamic boundary conditions as can be seen in Remark
2.1
. Intuitively, one needs a more appropriate weight function
\psi
satisfying the pseudo-convexity property with respect to both differential operators in
\Omega
and on
\Gamma
, i.e., there exists
\rho>0
such that

(6.2)

\nabla^{2}\psi(\xi,\xi)(x)\geqslant 2\rho|\xi|^{2}\text{ in }\overline{\Omega}\quad\text{ and }\quad\nabla^{2}_{\Gamma}\psi(\xi,\xi)(x)\geqslant 2\rho|\xi|^{2}\text{ on }\Gamma\quad\text{ for all }\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We refer to
[
8
, Chapter 6]
for more details. Moreover, the designed weight function should absorb the tricky term

s\lambda\int_{\Gamma_{T}}{e^{2s\varphi}\varphi\partial_{\nu}\psi|\partial_{\nu}y|^{2}}\mathrm{d}S\mathrm{d}t,

which causes a main difficulty in such problems. Such a weight function has not been considered before, up to our knowledge. This issue has recently been resolved in the parabolic case
[
10
]
by regularity estimates. Thus, we believe that this open question deserves more attention, in contrast to
[
16
, Remark 3]
.
