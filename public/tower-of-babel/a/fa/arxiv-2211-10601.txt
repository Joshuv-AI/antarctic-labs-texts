Index Theory of Chiral Unitaries and Split-Step Quantum Walks
Chris BOURNE ab

Abstract
Building from work by Cedzich et al. and Suzuki et al., we consider topological and index-theoretic properties of chiral unitaries, which are an abstraction of the time evolution of a chiral-symmetric self-adjoint operator. Split-step quantum walks provide a rich class of examples. We use the index of a pair of projections and the Cayley transform to define topological indices for chiral unitaries on both Hilbert spaces and Hilbert C ∗ C^{*} -modules. In the case of the discrete time evolution of a Hamiltonian-like operator, we relate the index for chiral unitaries to the index of the Hamiltonian. We also prove a double-sided winding number formula for anisotropic split-step quantum walks on Hilbert C ∗ C^{*} -modules, extending a result by Matsuzawa.

Introduction

Quantum walks are discrete analogues of random walks and bring together elements of probability
theory, quantum information theory and mathematical physics.
Such quantum walks are often
realised via a unitary operator, considered as a discrete time step,
constructed from the discrete shift and multiplication/coin operators on the
Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

Adapting ideas from topological phases
of matter, Kitagawa examined topological properties of
split-step
quantum walks, which are a
sub-class of quantum walks whose spectrum is symmetric about the real axis
[
21
]
.
This was then extended by Cedzich et al., who gave a mathematical classification of unitaries
U

on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with
\pm 1
not in the essential spectrum and obeying
symmetry relations adapted from free-fermionic topological insulators and superconductors
[
13
,
14
]
.
The case
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}
where
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
are self-adjoint unitaries
was further examined by Suzuki, who studied the index by Cedzich et al.
from the perspective of supersymmetry and the Fredholm index
[
34
]
.
Throughout this paper, we will call unitaries with a decomposition as a product of self-adjoint unitaries

chiral unitaries
.
Our terminology comes from the property
\Gamma_{0}U\Gamma_{0}=U^{*}
, which can be considered
as a chiral symmetry of the unitary
U
.

The total symmetry index for chiral unitaries on a Hilbert space studied by Cedzich et al. and Suzuki possesses two important properties.
First, it is
realised via the index of a supersymmetric (odd) Fredholm operator. That is, for a given
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}
,
the self-adjoint Fredholm operator

\tfrac{1}{2\rm{i}}(U-U^{*})

anti-commutes with the self-adjoint unitary
\Gamma_{0}
, which can be considered as a grading operator of the Hilbert space.
Second, and more importantly, the index of this Fredholm operator is closely connected to invariant
and quasi-invariant states of the discrete time step
U
. Namely, a non-trivial index guarantees the existence
of eigenvectors in either
\Ker(U-{\bf 1})
or
\Ker(U+{\bf 1})
.

Analogues of the symmetry index for chiral unitaries on Hilbert spaces have been generalised in a variety of areas, including
non-unitary split-step quantum walks
[
1
]
and situations where the Fredholm condition
fails, where the Witten index is employed instead
[
26
]
. The purpose of this paper is to
give a new generalisation using tools from
K
-theory and (noncommutative) index theory. Namely, we
broaden our definition of chiral unitaries to include adjointable operators on Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules.
In this generalised setting, we obtain topological indices taking value in the

K
-theory group of a
C^{*}
-algebra. The Hilbert space setting can be recovered by considering
Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules over
\mathbb{C}
.

The condition we require in order to define topological indices for chiral unitaries is purely spectral
(or a related condition in the
C^{*}
-module setting using the generalised Calkin algebra) and so is independent from the setting
of quantum walks. However, the class of split-step quantum walks provides us with a rich class of examples that are of relevance
in a variety of fields. So while our constructions are general, they are done with
concrete applications in mind.

Let us provide some motivations to study chiral unitaries on Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules. Suppose that
we have a compact Hausdorff parameter space
X
for which we can consider a chiral unitary

U_{x}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})
for any
x\in X
. If the map
X\ni x\mapsto U_{x}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

is strongly continuous, then the family
u=\{U_{x}\}_{x\in X}
defines a unitary operator on the Hilbert

C^{*}
-module
(\mathcal{H}\otimes C(X))_{C(X)}
(see Section
3.1
for precise
definitions). Therefore, we can consider index-theoretic
properties of the family
u=\{U_{x}\}_{x\in X}
that will also be sensitive to the topology of the parameter space
X
.
Another motivation is that topological indices that take value in the
K
-theory group of a
C^{*}
-algebra
are in general much richer than the setting of operators on Hilbert spaces. Hence, we gain access to a
wider variety of possible invariants that can not be accessed by working solely on Hilbert spaces.
Indeed, the Fredholm-like condition required to define
K
-theoretic indices is substantially weaker than
the case of
Hilbert spaces. As an example from topological phases of matter, suppose
H=H^{*}
is a Hamiltonian acting on

\ell^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{Z}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{n}\bigr)
,
d\geq 2
, and constructed from matrices of polynomials of the discrete shift operators
on
\ell^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{Z}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{n}\bigr)
such that
0\notin\sigma(H)
. Then the restriction of
H
to the half-space

\ell^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{N},\mathbb{C}^{n}\bigr)
will define
a Fredholm operator on the Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
\bigl(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{C}^{n})\otimes M_{n}\bigl(C^{*}\bigl(\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}\bigr)\bigr)\bigr)_{M_{n}(C^{*}(\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}))}

with
C^{*}\bigl(\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}\bigr)
the group
C^{*}
-algebra of
\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}
. Hence, Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules also give us a mathematical
framework to study topological properties of edge/boundary phenomena, which may be useful for applications
in physics.

The main tools we use to define topological indices are the Cayley transform of unitary
operators and the index of a pair of projections. That is, given a chiral unitary

U
, we may consider the index of the (generally unbounded) self-adjoint operator

{\mathcal{C}(U)={\rm i}({\bf 1}+U)({\bf 1}-U)^{-1}}

or
\mathcal{C}(-U)
. Similarly, writing
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}=(2P_{0}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})
with

P_{0}
and
P_{1}
self-adjoint projections, we may consider the index of a pair of projections

\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
or
\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
. If these indices are well defined, their sum

\Index(\mathcal{C}(U))+\Index(\mathcal{C}(-U))
or
\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})+\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
recovers
the previously studied total symmetry index for chiral unitaries,
though the individual indices are defined more generally. Indeed,

\Index(\mathcal{C}(U))
and
\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
examine
\Ker(U+{\bf 1})
while

\Index(\mathcal{C}(-U))
and
\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
examine
\Ker(U-{\bf 1})
. The relation between
the index for chiral unitaries and the sum of an index of a pair of projections was already noted in
[
34
]
.
One advantage of working with the Cayley transform is that
\Index(\mathcal{C}(U))

still comes from the index of a self-adjoint odd Fredholm
operator as
\mathcal{C}(\pm U)
anti-commutes with
\Gamma_{0}
. The cost is that we need to work
with unbounded operators. We make the above statements
precise for unitaries acting on general Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules over an auxiliary

C^{*}
-algebra.
1
1

1

Some care is needed to discuss the index of a pair of projections in a Hilbert

C^{*}
-module as closed subspaces need not be complemented.

Outside of the quantum walk setting, our constructions provide a new presentation
of the
K
-theory group
K_{0}(A)
of a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
in terms of equivalence classes of chiral unitaries acting on
Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules over
A
, which may be of independent interest.

An important class of chiral unitaries arise from the discrete time evolution of a Hamiltonian-like
operator
{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi H}
, where
H
possesses a so-called chiral symmetry,
\Gamma_{0}H\Gamma_{0}=-H

with
\Gamma_{0}
a self-adjoint unitary.
2
2

2

Indeed, the example of the time-evolution of a chiral-symmetric
Hamiltonian is the main motivation for our use of the term ‘chiral unitaries’.

If
H
is Fredholm, then we can consider the symmetry index of
H
directly. Up to a regularisation of

H
, we show that this index can be computed by the index of
\mathcal{C}\bigl(-{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi H}\bigr)
or

\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
, which we also
extend to the setting of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules and
K
-theoretic indices.

As a brief application, we consider split-step quantum walk-like chiral unitaries on the
Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},B)
, where
B
is a unital
C^{*}
-algebra with a trace. Restricting to
operators constructed from the shift on
\mathbb{Z}
and functions
\mathbb{Z}\to B
with limits at
\pm\infty
,
a short exact sequence of
C^{*}
-algebras can be constructed that is closely related to the Toeplitz extension of the
crossed product
B\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
. Making this connection precise,
we can then use a semifinite analogue of the Noether–Toeplitz index theorem to relate the trace of the topological index of
a chiral unitary to the difference of winding numbers of a related unitary at the limit points
\pm\infty
.
Such a result was previously shown by Matsuzawa in the Hilbert space setting
[
24
]
.

Another advantage of working with unitaries on Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules is that non-trivial
K
-theoretic indices
can also be defined for regular (non-split-step) quantum walks. While the lack of chiral symmetry means there
is no
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading,
the Cayley transform of generic unitaries can still be used
to obtain a self-adjoint operator. If the Cayley transform is Fredholm, topological indices can be constructed
in odd
K
-theory.

We have previously studied the Cayley transform as a convenient tool to pass between self-adjoint and unitary
operators that is compatible with
K
-theory
[
6
,
9
]
. Indeed, our results will not be surprising to
those familiar with noncommutative index theory. Rather, we hope that this paper can be used as a starting
point to further probe the connections of index theory with quantum walks.

We note that while the works
[
18
,
23
,
33
]
also adopt techniques from the study
of topological phases to quantum walks, the setting of the current paper is rather different. In particular, we do
not consider bulk-edge phenomena, leaving this question for future work.

During the writing of this paper, we became aware of similar work by Moriyoshi and Natsume, who also
prove a Noether-like index theorem for split-step quantum walks
[
28
]
.

1.1
Outline and main results

Sections
2
and
3
serve as a summary of previous
results and, while some proofs are provided for completeness, no originality is claimed for any results in this part of the paper.

Section
2
considers
\mathbb{Z}
-valued indices defined for chiral unitaries on
Hilbert spaces. This section is mostly self-contained and independent from the rest of the paper.
We give a survey of the various topological indices for chiral unitaries on Hilbert spaces that have appeared
in the mathematical physics literature and show their connections to one another.
We then give another presentation of these indices via the index of a pair of projections and show
some basic stability properties and trace formulas. The connection to the generator of the
unitary time step and the Cayley transform is also briefly considered.

Section
3
is an executive summary of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules and
K
-theory
via Fredholm operators, mostly following
[
37
]
.
In particular we use a picture of
K
-theory that comes
about via the isomorphisms
K_{0}(A)\cong KK(\mathbb{C},A)
and
K_{1}(A)\cong KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)
for a
\sigma
-unital

C^{*}
-algebra
A
. We also consider an analogue of the index of a pair of projections in Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules,
where due to the lack of guaranteed complementability of subspaces, we instead construct an index via a concrete Kasparov module and equivalence class in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
. We show some basic properties of
this index and an
addition formula, which helps justify our terminology.
Lastly in the case of a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
B
with trace
\tau
, we review how to extract a numerical index via the map
\tau_{\ast}\colon KK(\mathbb{C},B)\to\mathbb{R}
.

In Section
4
, we return to chiral unitaries now defined on Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules.
Our first result is a precise condition on chiral unitaries that ensures the Cayley transform is a Fredholm
operator on a Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
E_{A}
and defines
an even
K
-theory class for the algebra
A
.
We can also work with arbitrary chiral unitaries in
\operatorname{Mult}(A)
, the multiplier algebra
of
A
, by considering
A
as a
C^{*}
-module over itself.
We also introduce the Cayley transform and
K
-theoretic index for a pair of projections that are
sufficiently close in the generalised Calkin algebra. We show that our indices for chiral unitaries and
pairs of projections are compatible with
each other as well as more standard presentations of the
K_{0}
-group of a
C^{*}
-algebra.
Properties of our
K
-theoretic index analogous to those in
Section
2
are also shown, though the proofs require more operator algebraic
machinery. In particular, we are able to relate the
K
-theoretic index of a self-adjoint and odd Fredholm operator
H

with
\|H\|\leq 1
to the index defined from the chiral unitary
{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi H}
. As previously mentioned in this
introduction, this example is of particular relevance for the case of a
H
a Hamiltonian on a system with
boundary.
Interestingly, many of our results in this section also hold true for non-chiral unitaries up to a

K
-theoretic degree shift
and so may be applicable to a wider class of quantum walk
systems.

Finally in Section
5
, we prove a noncommutative version of the Noether–Toeplitz index theorem
for the index of chiral unitaries
u
acting on the Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},B)
with an anisotropic condition
on the element

. Applying the trace map to

gives us a numerical quantity and our index

that are defined at the limits
\pm\infty
of the
space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},B)
. While still more abstract than the winding number on Hilbert spaces, the noncommutative
winding number is generally much easier to compute than
K
-theoretic Fredholm indices.

Our work deals extensively with projections and unitaries acting on both Hilbert spaces and
Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules. To help distinguish between these settings, we will use capital letters

U
and
P
for unitaries and projection on Hilbert spaces and small letters

and

for
unitaries and projections in more general
C^{*}
-algebras.

2.1
Basic definitions

We denote by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
the algebra of bounded and compact operators on

\mathcal{H}
, respectively. The quotient
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})/\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
is the Calkin algebra.
Given

\pi\colon\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})

We first note an elementary result.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
B
be a unital
C^{*}
-algebra,
u\in B
a unitary element and
\gamma_{0}\in B
a self-adjoint unitary.
Then
\gamma_{0}u\gamma_{0}=u^{*}
if and only if there is a self-adjoint unitary
\gamma_{1}\in B
such that

u=\gamma_{0}\gamma_{1}
.

Proof.

The right-to-left implication is a simple check. Similarly, if
\gamma_{0}u\gamma_{0}=u^{*}
, we define
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{0}u

and check that
\gamma_{1}^{*}=u^{*}\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{0}u
.
∎

Definition 2.2
.

We say that a unitary operator
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a chiral unitary
if
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}
, where
\Gamma_{j}
are self-adjoint unitaries on
\mathcal{H}
for
j\in\{0,1\}

Because
\Gamma_{0}U\Gamma_{0}=U^{*}
, the spectrum of the chiral unitaries is symmetric about the real axis.
This observation along with the example of a discrete time evolution of a chiral-symmetric Hamiltonian
(see Section

Example 2.3

(split-step quantum walks)
.

Recall that, because

Definition 2.4
.

\operatorname{si}(U)=\operatorname{si}_{+}(U)+\operatorname{si}_{-}(U)

In the definition of

2.3
The symmetry indices of Suzuki et al.

Let us now review alternative descriptions of the symmetry indices of Cedzich et al. that have appeared in the
mathematical physics literature. As previously emphasised, these topological indices are not new, but provide
a different presentation of the previously-defined indices that may be useful depending on the example under study.

Let
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}
be a chiral unitary on
\mathcal{H}
and suppose that both
+1
and
-1
are
not contained in the essential spectrum.
The index of self-adjoint operators from equation (
2.1
) was further studied by Suzuki
[
34
]
, who in particular emphasised the role of
supersymmetry.
3
3

3

Our use of the term ‘supersymmetry’ is to align our terminology with
[
34
]

and denotes the setting of a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded Hilbert space with operators that change the parity of vectors.
Such a setting need not be related to any symmetry between bosons and fermions.

Namely, given
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}
, the operator
Q=\operatorname{Im}(U)
anti-commutes with the
self-adjoint unitary
\Gamma_{0}
.
Hence there is a off-diagonal representation of
Q
with respect to the spectral decomposition
of
\Gamma_{0}
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}=(\Gamma_{0}\pm{\bf 1})\mathcal{H}=\Ker(\Gamma_{0}\mp{\bf 1})
. Then we may write

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{+}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{-},\qquad Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&Q_{+}^{*}\\
Q_{+}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Q_{+}\colon\ \mathcal{H}_{+}\to\mathcal{H}_{-}.

Definition 2.5

(
[
13
, Section 4]
and
[
34
]
)
.

We say that the chiral unitary
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}
is of supersymmetric Fredholm type if
Q_{+}
is a Fredholm operator. In such a case,
we define the supersymmetric index of
U
as the Fredholm index,

\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U)=\Index(Q_{+})=\dim\Ker(Q_{+})-\dim\Ker(Q_{+}^{*})\in\mathbb{Z}.

Note that if
\Ker(Q)
is finite dimensional, then it is well known (see, for example,
[
4
, p. 124]
) that

\Index(Q_{+})=\Tr\bigl(\Gamma_{0}|_{\Ker(Q)}\bigr).

Therefore, we immediately have an equivalence

\operatorname{si}(U)=\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U)=\operatorname{si}(\operatorname{Im}(U)).

(2.2)

Much like the decomposition of
\operatorname{si}(U)
into a sum of
\operatorname{si}_{\pm}(U)
, a decomposition of

\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U)
into a sum of two indices was given by Matsuzawa et al.
[
25
,
27
]
,
which we now review.

We first let
P_{0}
be the projection onto the
+1
eigenspace of
\Gamma_{0}
, i.e.,
\Gamma_{0}=2P_{0}-{\bf 1}
.
Then using the decomposition of
\mathcal{H}
into even and odd subspaces,

\mathcal{H}=({\bf 1}+\Gamma_{0})\mathcal{H}\oplus({\bf 1}-\Gamma_{0})\mathcal{H}=P_{0}\mathcal{H}\oplus({\bf 1}-P_{0})\mathcal{H}
, one
defines
R_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}P_{0}(U+U^{*})P_{0}
and
R_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}({\bf 1}-P_{0})(U+U^{*})({\bf 1}-P_{0})
as the restriction of

\operatorname{Re}(Q)=\tfrac{1}{2}(U+U^{*})
to the even and odd subspaces.

Definition 2.6
.

If
\Ker(R_{1}\pm{\bf 1})
and
\Ker(R_{2}\pm{\bf 1})
are finite dimensional, we define

\Ind_{\pm}(\Gamma_{0},U)=\dim\Ker(R_{1}\mp{\bf 1})-\dim\Ker(R_{2}\mp{\bf 1}).

It is already shown in
[
25
, Section 2]
that
\Ind_{\pm}(\Gamma_{0},U)
is well defined if

\pm 1\notin\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(U)
as well as the relations

\Ind_{\pm}(\Gamma_{0},U)=\operatorname{si}_{\pm}(U),\qquad\Ind_{+}(\Gamma_{0},U)+\Ind_{-}(\Gamma_{0},U)=\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U).

(2.3)

Example 2.7
.

Let us return to Example
2.3
with
n=1
,

\Gamma_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}c&\overline{d}S^{*}\\
dS&-c\end{pmatrix},\qquad\Gamma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b^{*}\\
b&-a\end{pmatrix},\qquad U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1},

where the operators
a
and
b
act as multiplication operators,
(a\psi)(x)=a(x)\psi(x)
, and

(c,d)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}
are scalars such that
c^{2}+|d|^{2}=1
.
The unitary
\Gamma_{0}
does not act locally, as is often demanded of a chiral symmetry operator, but can
be diagonalised to the more standard form
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{\bf 1}&\hphantom{-}0\\
0&-{\bf 1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
by the unitary transformation
in
[
35
, Theorem 8]
.
4
4

4

Ensuring that
the symmetry operators act locally is particularly important for studying the bulk-boundary correspondence, which we do not
consider in this paper.

The index
\operatorname{si}(U)=\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U)
has been explicitly computed in the case
that the functions
a
and
b
have limits at
\pm\infty

[
35
]
. The refined indices
\operatorname{si}_{\pm}(U)=\Ind_{\pm}(\Gamma_{0},U)

are also computed for explicit models under similar assumptions in
[
25
,
27
]
.
Briefly, the results in
[
25
,
27
,
35
]
use the fact that the essential spectrum of
U
is characterised
by
U
at the limit points
\pm\infty
, which allows for precise conditions to determine whether
\pm 1\notin\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(U)
.
Taking compact perturbations of
U
to a simpler unitary then allow the authors to compute the symmetry indices.
In
[
13
, Section 5.5]
, split-step quantum walks are constructed from shift operators and
rotation matrices, where the
angle of rotation may vary as a function of
\mathbb{Z}
. By constraining the possible range of the angle of rotation, Cedzich et al. show that the essential spectrum of the split-step quantum walk is gapped at
\pm 1
.

2.4
Connection to the index of a pair of projections

Our next task to connect the indices
\operatorname{si}_{\pm}(U)=\Ind_{\pm}(\Gamma_{0},U)
for the chiral unitary

U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}
to the index of a pair of projections
[
2
]
.
Let us first recall the following result.

Lemma 2.8

(
[
20
, Section 6, Lemma 4]
)
.

Let
B
be a unital
C^{*}
-algebra and
p_{0},p_{1}\in B
projections such that
\|p_{0}-p_{1}\|_{B}<1
. Then
there exists a unitary
v\in B
such that
p_{1}=vp_{0}v^{*}
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
be self-adjoint projections on
\mathcal{H}
.
We say that
(P_{0},P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair if

\|P_{0}-P_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<1
.

If
(P_{0},P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair, then the operator
P_{1}|_{\Ran(P_{0})}\colon\Ran(P_{0})\to\Ran(P_{1})
is a Fredholm operator and we define

\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})=\Index\bigl(P_{1}|_{\Ran(P_{0})}\bigr).

See
[
3
, Lemma 4.1]
for a proof that
P_{1}|_{\Ran(P_{0})}
is Fredholm if and only if
\|P_{0}-P_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<1
.
The index of a pair of projections is a generalisation of the essential codimension of self-adjoint
projections.

Lemma 2.10

(
[
7
, Lemma 4.10]
)
.

If
(P_{0},P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair of projections, then

\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})=\dim\bigl(\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\bigr)-\dim\bigl(\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\bigr).

We list some basic properties of
\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
.

Lemma 2.11

(cf.
[
2
, Section 3]
and
[
3
, Lemma 4.3]
)
.

Let
P_{0},P_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be self-adjoint projections.

(i)

If
(P_{0},P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair, then
\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})=-\Ind(P_{1},P_{0})=-\Ind\bigl(({\bf 1}-P_{0}),({\bf 1}-P_{1})\bigr)
.

(ii)

If
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair, then
\Ind(P_{0},({\bf 1}-P_{1}))=\Ind(P_{1},({\bf 1}-P_{0}))
.

(iii)

If
P_{0}
,
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
are projections such that
\|P_{0}-P_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\|P_{1}-P_{2}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<\tfrac{1}{2}
, then

\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})+\Ind(P_{1},P_{2})=\Ind(P_{0},P_{2})
.

Parts (i) and (ii) of Lemma
2.11
can be proved quite easily using Lemma
2.10
. Part (iii)
is less trivial.

Let us now use
\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
to study chiral unitaries
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.
Because
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
are self-adjoint unitaries, there are self-adjoint
projections
P_{0},P_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\Gamma_{j}=2P_{j}-{\bf 1}
,
j\in\{0,1\}
.
We first show the relation of the index of a pair of projections to the total symmetry
index
\operatorname{si}(U)=\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U)
.

Proposition 2.12

(cf.
[
34
, equation (1.6)]
)
.

Let
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a chiral unitary with

\Gamma_{j}=2P_{j}-{\bf 1}
,
j\in\{0,1\}
. Then
Q=\operatorname{Im}(U)=\frac{1}{2\rm{i}}(U-U^{*})
is Fredholm
if and only if
(P_{0},P_{1})
and
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
are Fredholm pairs and

\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})+\Ind(P_{0},({\bf 1}-P_{1}))=\operatorname{si}(Q)=\operatorname{si}(U)=\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U).

Proof.

Given
U=(2P_{0}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})
, a simple computation gives that

Q=\frac{1}{2\rm{i}}(U-U^{*})=\frac{1}{2\rm{i}}(\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{0})=\frac{2}{\rm i}(P_{0}P_{1}-P_{1}P_{0}).

Hence we have

\displaystyle Q_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}({\bf 1}-\Gamma_{0})Q\frac{1}{2}({\bf 1}+\Gamma_{0})=({\bf 1}-P_{0})QP_{0}

\displaystyle=-2{\rm i}({\bf 1}-P_{0})(P_{0}P_{1}-P_{1}P_{0})P_{0}=2{\rm i}({\bf 1}-P_{0})P_{1}P_{0}

and similarly
Q_{+}^{*}=-2{\rm i}P_{0}P_{1}({\bf 1}-P_{0})
. Therefore,
\Ker(Q)
is finite dimensional and

Q_{+}
is Fredholm if and only if
\Ker\bigl(({\bf 1}-P_{0})P_{1}P_{0}\bigr)
and
\Ker\bigl(P_{0}P_{1}({\bf 1}-P_{0})\bigr)

are finite dimensional. Recalling that
Q_{+}\colon P_{0}\mathcal{H}\to({\bf 1}-P_{0})\mathcal{H}
and
Q_{+}^{*}\colon({\bf 1}-P_{0})\mathcal{H}\to P_{0}\mathcal{H}
, we
furthermore find that

\displaystyle\Ker(Q_{+})

\displaystyle=\Ker(({\bf 1}-P_{0})P_{1}P_{0})=\Ker(({\bf 1}-P_{0})P_{1})\cap\Ran(P_{0})

\displaystyle=\bigl(\Ker(P_{1})\oplus\Ran(P_{1})\bigr)\cap\Ran(P_{0}),

and similarly

\displaystyle\Ker(Q_{+}^{*})

\displaystyle=\Ker(P_{0}P_{1}({\bf 1}-P_{0}))=\Ker(P_{0}P_{1})\cap\Ran({\bf 1}-P_{0})

\displaystyle=\bigl(\Ker(P_{1})\oplus\Ran(P_{1})\bigr)\cap\Ker(P_{0}).

That is,

\displaystyle\Index(Q_{+})={}

\displaystyle\dim\bigl(\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\bigr)+\dim\bigl(\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\bigr)

\displaystyle-\dim\bigl(\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\bigr)-\dim\bigl(\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\bigr).

Using Lemma
2.10
, if
(P_{0},P_{1})
and
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
are Fredholm pairs, then

\displaystyle\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})=\dim\bigl(\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\bigr)-\dim\bigl(\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\bigr),

\displaystyle\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})=\dim\bigl(\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\bigr)-\dim\bigl(\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\bigr)

and the index equality
\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}((2P_{0}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1}))=\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})+\Ind(P_{0},({\bf 1}-P_{1}))
follows.
The connection to
\operatorname{si}(U)=\operatorname{si}(\operatorname{Im}(U))
then also follows from equation (
2.2
).
We note that
\Ker(Q)
is finite dimensional if and only if the spaces

\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})
,
\Ker(P_{1})\cap\Ran(P_{0}),

\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})
and
\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})
are finite dimensional,
which is equivalent to
(P_{0},P_{1})
and
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
being Fredholm pairs.
∎

We can similarly relate
\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
and
\Ind(P_{0},({\bf 1}-P_{1}))
to the refined indices

\operatorname{si}_{\pm}(U)
and
\Ind_{\pm}(\Gamma_{0},U)
. We first prove some preparatory results.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}=(2P_{0}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})
be a chiral unitary on
\mathcal{H}
. If

(P_{0},P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair, then

\Ker(U+{\bf 1})=\Ker(\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1})=\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\oplus\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1}).

If
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair, then

\Ker(U-{\bf 1})=\Ker(\Gamma_{0}-\Gamma_{1})=\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\oplus\Ker(P_{0})\oplus\Ker(P_{1}).

Proof.

If
(P_{0},P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair of projections if and only if the subspace

\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\oplus\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})
is finite dimensional. We next note that

\Ker(\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1})=\Ker(P_{0}+P_{1}-{\bf 1})=\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\oplus\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1}),

where the last equality comes from
[
7
, Lemma 4.10]
(which can be proven easily).
We then see that

\Ker(\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1})=\Ker\bigl((\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1})\Gamma_{1}\bigr)=\Ker(U+{\bf 1})

as
\Gamma_{1}
is unitary.
The case of
\Ker(U-{\bf 1})
follows the same argument using that
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair. We omit the details.
∎

Lemma 2.14

(
[
15
, Lemma 3.7]
)
.

Let
U=(2P_{0}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})
be a chiral unitary on
\mathcal{H}
. The projections
(P_{0},P_{1})

(
respectively
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1}))
are a Fredholm pair if and only if
-1\notin\sigma_{\rm ess}(U)

(
respectively
1\notin\sigma_{\rm ess}(U))
. Consequently
\operatorname{si}(U)=\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U)

is well defined if and only if

\{\pm 1\}\cap\sigma_{\rm ess}(U)=\varnothing
.

Proof.

The result immediately follows from Lemma
2.13
as
\pm 1\notin\sigma_{\rm ess}(U)

if and only if
{\Ker(U\mp{\bf 1})}
is finite dimensional.
∎

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}=(2P_{0}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})
be a chiral unitary on
\mathcal{H}
.
If

(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair, then

\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})=\Ind_{+}(\Gamma_{0},U)=\operatorname{si}_{+}(U).

If
(P_{0},P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair, then

\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})=\Ind_{-}(\Gamma_{0},U)=\operatorname{si}_{-}(U).

Proof.

Recall the operators
R_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}P_{0}(U+U^{*})P_{0}
and
R_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}({\bf 1}-P_{0})(U+U^{*})({\bf 1}-P_{0})
that
appear in the definition of
\Ind_{\pm}(\Gamma_{0},U)
.
Elementary computations give that

R_{1}=P_{0}(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})P_{0}
and
R_{2}=-({\bf 1}-P_{0})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})({\bf 1}-P_{0})
, therefore

\displaystyle\Ker(R_{1}-{\bf 1})

\displaystyle=\Ker\bigl(P_{0}(2P_{1}-{\bf 1}-{\bf 1})P_{0}\bigr)=\Ker\bigl(P_{0}({\bf 1}-P_{1})P_{0}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker({\bf 1}-P_{1})=\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})

and similarly

\displaystyle\Ker(R_{2}-{\bf 1})

\displaystyle=\Ker\bigl(({\bf 1}-P_{0})(-2P_{1})({\bf 1}-P_{0})\bigr)=\Ran({\bf 1}-P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})

\displaystyle=\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\Ind_{+}(\Gamma_{0},U)

\displaystyle=\dim\Ker(R_{1}-{\bf 1})-\dim\Ker(R_{2}-{\bf 1})

\displaystyle=\dim\bigl(\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\bigr)-\dim\bigl(\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\bigr)=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})

and the equality with
\operatorname{si}_{+}(U)
follows from (
2.3
). An analogous argument also shows that

\operatorname{si}_{-}(U)=\Ind_{-}(\Gamma_{0},U)=\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
.
∎

We can also prove the connection between the index of a pair of projections and
\operatorname{si}_{\pm}(U)
without
first showing equality with
\Ind_{\pm}(\Gamma_{0},U)
. Namely, if
(P_{0},P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair, then
because
P_{0}
is the
+1
eigenspace projection of
\Gamma_{0}
and

\Ker(U+{\bf 1})=\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\oplus\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})
from Lemma
2.13
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{si}_{-}(U)

\displaystyle=\Tr\bigl(\Gamma_{0}|_{\Ker(U+{\bf 1})}\bigr)=\dim\big[\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(U+{\bf 1})\big]-\dim\big[\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ker(U+{\bf 1})\big]

\displaystyle=\dim\bigl(\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\bigr)-\dim\bigl(\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\bigr)=\Ind(P_{0},P_{1}).

The equality
\operatorname{si}_{+}(U)=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
follows the same argument.

2.5
Further properties

We now use properties of the index of a pair of projections to further study the symmetry indices of chiral unitaries.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
U=(2P_{0}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})
be a chiral unitary on
\mathcal{H}
.
If
(P_{0},P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair, then
\dim\Ker(U+{\bf 1})\geq|\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})|
.
If
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair, then
\dim\Ker(U-{\bf 1})\geq|\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})|
.

Proof.

Using that

\displaystyle\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})=\dim\bigl(\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\bigr)-\dim\bigl(\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\bigr),

\displaystyle\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})=\dim\bigl(\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\bigr)-\dim\bigl(\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\bigr),

the result follows from Lemma
2.13
.
∎

The index
\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
is well defined if
\|P_{0}-P_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<1
. In particular, we can define

\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
if
P_{0}-P_{1}
or equivalently
\Gamma_{0}-\Gamma_{1}
is a compact operator.
If we are in the special case that
(\Gamma_{0}\pm\Gamma_{1})^{2m+1}
is trace-class for some odd integer
2m+1
,
we can write another trace formula to compute
\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
and
\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
.

Proposition 2.17

(
[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
)
.

If
(\Gamma_{0}-\Gamma_{1})^{2m+1}
is trace-class, then

\operatorname{si}_{-}(U)=\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})=\Tr\bigl((P_{0}-P_{1})^{2m+1}\bigr)=\frac{1}{2^{2m+1}}\Tr\bigl((\Gamma_{0}-\Gamma_{1})^{2m+1}\bigr).

If
(\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1})^{2m+1}
is trace-class, then

\operatorname{si}_{+}(U)=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})=\Tr\bigl((P_{0}+P_{1}-{\bf 1})^{2m+1}\bigr)=\frac{1}{2^{2m+1}}\Tr\bigl((\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1})^{2m+1}\bigr).

We can use Proposition
2.17
to write many non-trivial examples of

\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
or
\Ind(P_{0},\allowbreak{\bf 1}-P_{1})
by taking
trace-class perturbations of projections, for example.
More general trace formulas for
\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
can be found in
[
11
, Section 3]
.

Let us briefly consider the case where
\mathcal{H}
is finite dimensional.

Proposition 2.18

(cf.
[
13
, Lemma 2.5]
)
.

Let
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}
be a chiral unitary on a finite-dimensional
Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Then
\operatorname{si}(U)=\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U)=\Tr(\Gamma_{0})=\operatorname{Sig}(\Gamma_{0})
.

Proposition
2.18
follows from Propositions
2.12

and
2.17
with
m=0
. We also give a short proof using properties of
the index of a pair of projections.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{H}
is finite dimensional, then
\sigma_{\rm ess}(U)=\varnothing
and all
projections are Fredholm pairs.
We use Lemma
2.11
and compute

\displaystyle\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U)

\displaystyle=\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})+\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})

\displaystyle=\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})+\Ind(P_{1},{\bf 1}-P_{0})

\displaystyle=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{0})

\displaystyle=\dim\bigl(\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker({\bf 1}-P_{0})\bigr)-\dim\bigl(\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran({\bf 1}-P_{0})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\dim\Ran(P_{0})-\dim\Ker(P_{0})=\Tr(\Gamma_{0})=\operatorname{Sig}(\Gamma_{0})

as
\Ran(P_{0})
and
\Ker(P_{0})
are the
+1
and
-1
eigenspaces of
\Gamma_{0}
, respectively.
∎

Remark 2.19
.

The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded nature of
\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U)
allows us to obtain non-trivial indices even in finite
dimensions, where the ungraded Fredholm index will be zero. Indeed, considering
\mathbb{C}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}
as
a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded Hilbert space, the operator

\mathbf{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{m,m}&0_{n,m}\\
0_{m,n}&0_{n,n}\end{pmatrix},\qquad 0_{j,k}\colon\ \mathbb{C}^{j}\to\mathbb{C}^{k}

is such that
Q_{+}:=0_{m,n}
is Fredholm with

\Index(Q_{+})=\dim\Ker(0_{m,n})-\dim\Ker(0_{n,m})=m-n.

Clearly the argument used in the proof of Proposition
2.18
fails
in infinite dimensions. Indeed, if
\mathcal{H}
is infinite dimensional, then
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{0})
can not be a
Fredholm pair as any self-adjoint unitary
\Gamma_{0}
on
\mathcal{H}
can not be trace class.
This simple observation leads to the following non-obvious result.

Proposition 2.20
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}
is infinite dimensional and
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}=(2P_{0}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})
is a chiral unitary
on
\mathcal{H}
. Then
\|\Gamma_{0}-\Gamma_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}\geq 1
or
\|\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}\geq 1
.

Proof.

We have that
\Gamma_{0}-\Gamma_{1}=2(P_{0}-P_{1})
and
\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1}=2(P_{0}+P_{1}-{\bf 1})
.
Now suppose the result is false. Then
\|P_{0}-P_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}
and
\|P_{0}+P_{1}-{\bf 1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}
are
strictly less than
\tfrac{1}{2}
. Hence we can estimate

\|P_{0}-({\bf 1}-P_{0})\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}\leq\|P_{0}-P_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}+\|P_{0}+P_{1}-{\bf 1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<1

and
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{0})
is a Fredholm pair. But this is only possible if
\mathcal{H}
is finite dimensional,
a contradiction of our assumptions.
∎

Corollary 2.21
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is infinite dimensional and
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}=(2P_{0}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})
is a chiral unitary
with
\{\pm 1\}\cap\sigma_{\rm ess}(U)=\varnothing
, then
1\leq\|\Gamma_{0}-\Gamma_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<2
or

1\leq\|\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<2
.

Proof.

The bound
1\leq\|\Gamma_{0}\pm\Gamma_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}
is Proposition
2.20
. If

U
is a chiral unitary with
\{\pm 1\}\cap\sigma_{\rm ess}(U)=\varnothing
, then both
(P_{0},P_{1})
and
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
are
Fredholm pairs,
\|P_{0}-P_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<1
and
\|P_{0}+P_{1}-{\bf 1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<1
,
which then gives the strict upper bound on
\|\Gamma_{0}\pm\Gamma_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}
.
∎

Proposition
2.20
shows that a tracial formula for
\operatorname{si}(U)=\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U)

of the form considered in Proposition
2.17
is impossible if
\mathcal{H}
is
infinite dimensional as
(\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1})^{2m+1}
and
(\Gamma_{0}-\Gamma_{1})^{2n+1}

cannot simultaneously be trace-class.

Finally, let us note some basic stability properties of the indices for chiral unitaries we have considered.

Proposition 2.22
.

Let
[0,1]\ni t\mapsto U(t)=\Gamma_{0}(t)\Gamma_{1}(t)=(2P_{0}(t)-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}(t)-{\bf 1})
be a strongly continuous
path of chiral unitaries on
\mathcal{H}
.

(i)

If
-1\notin\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(U(t))
for all
t\in[0,1]
, then
\Ind(P_{0}(t),P_{1}(t))
is constant.

(ii)

If
+1\notin\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(U(t))
for all
t\in[0,1]
, then
\Ind(P_{0}(t),{\bf 1}-P_{1}(t))
is constant.

This result is proved in a more general context in Proposition
4.7
, where for
Hilbert spaces the inequality
\|\pm U(t)-{\bf 1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<1
is equivalent to the condition that
\mp 1\notin\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(U(t))
.
In the Hilbert space setting, the result is relatively intuitive and we present a heuristic argument. Suppose
-1\notin\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(U(t))

for all
t\in[0,1]
. Then there is a
\delta>0
such that
range of the spectral projection
\chi_{({\rm e}^{{\rm i}(\pi+\delta)},{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(\pi-\delta)})}(U(t))
will be finite dimensional for all
t\in[0,1]
.
Therefore, any change in the dimension of
\Ker(U(t)+{\bf 1})
comes only from
eigenvalues of finite multiplicity. However, because the spectrum of
U(t)
is symmetric about the real axis,
eigenvalues of
U(t)
outside of
\pm 1
will come in pairs with respect to the spectral decomposition of
\Gamma_{0}(t)
.
So any change of dimension between
\Ker(U(t)+{\bf 1})
and
\Ker(U(t+\epsilon)+{\bf 1})
will always be even dimensional and such that

\Tr\bigl(\Gamma_{0}(t)|_{\Ker(U(t)+{\bf 1})}\bigr)=\Tr\bigl(\Gamma_{0}(t+\epsilon)|_{\Ker(U(t+\epsilon)+{\bf 1})}\bigr).

Hence,
\operatorname{si}_{-}(U(t))=\operatorname{si}_{-}(U(t+\epsilon))
and therefore the index of a pair of projections will be constant.

Corollary 2.23
.

The total symmetry index
\operatorname{si}(U)=\Ind_{\Gamma_{0}}(U)
is invariant under strongly continuous paths of chiral unitaries

\{U(t)\}_{t\in[0,1]}
such that
\{\pm 1\}\cap\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(U(t))=\varnothing
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Because
\operatorname{si}_{-}(U)=\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
and
\operatorname{si}_{+}(U)=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
can be written as a Fredholm index,
these quantities are locally constant in the norm-topology. We give a concrete realisation of this result below. A similar result can be
found in
[
13
, Proposition 2.6]
.

Proposition 2.24
.

Let
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}
and
U^{\prime}=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
be two chiral unitaries with the same chiral symmetry operator
\Gamma_{0}

and
\pi\colon\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})
the quotient map onto the Calkin algebra.
Suppose that
\|\pi(\Gamma_{0})\pm\pi(\Gamma_{1})\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<1
and
\|U-U^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}<1
. Then

\operatorname{si}_{\pm}(U)=\operatorname{si}_{\pm}(U^{\prime})
.

We do not need to assume that
\operatorname{si}_{\pm}(U^{\prime})
is well defined as this is implied by the hypothesis of the proposition.
Because the statement uses norm-bounds bounds in both
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and in
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})
, we will explicitly
write
\|\pi(S)\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}
(unlike the rest of this section).

Proof.

We consider the case that
\|\pi(\Gamma_{0})+\pi(\Gamma_{1})\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<1
and
\|U-U^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}<1

(the case
\|\pi(\Gamma_{0})-\pi(\Gamma_{1})\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<1
is analogous). Writing

\Gamma_{0}=2P_{0}-{\bf 1}
,
\Gamma_{1}=2P_{1}-{\bf 1}
and
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}=2P_{1}^{\prime}-{\bf 1}
, our assumptions imply that

\big\|\pi(P_{0})+\pi(P_{1})-{\bf 1}\big\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}=\frac{1}{2}\big\|\pi(\Gamma_{0})+\pi(\Gamma_{1})\big\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}<\frac{1}{2}

and

1>\big\|U-U^{\prime}\big\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}=\big\|\Gamma_{0}(\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{1}^{\prime})\big\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}=2\big\|P_{1}-P_{1}^{\prime}\big\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})},

so
\|\pi(P_{1})-\pi(P_{1}^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H})}\leq\|P_{1}-P_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}<\tfrac{1}{2}
. Therefore,
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
and
(P_{1},P_{1}^{\prime})
are Fredholm pairs. Applying
Lemma
2.11
,
({\bf 1}-P_{1},{\bf 1}-P_{1}^{\prime})
and
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1}^{\prime})
are also Fredholm pairs with

\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1}^{\prime})=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})+\Ind({\bf 1}-P_{1},{\bf 1}-P_{1}^{\prime})=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})-\Ind(P_{1},P_{1}^{\prime}).

Because
\|P_{1}-P_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}<1
, by Lemma
2.8

there is a unitary
V\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})
such that

VP_{1}V^{*}=P_{1}^{\prime}
. In particular, unitarily equivalent Fredholm pairs of projections are such that
\Ind(P_{1},P_{1}^{\prime})=0

[
2
, Theorem 3.3]
.
Putting these results together,

\operatorname{si}_{+}(U)=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1}^{\prime})-\Ind(P_{1},P_{1}^{\prime})=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1}^{\prime})=\operatorname{si}_{+}(U^{\prime}).

2.6
Connection to the generator/Hamiltonian

Quantum walks are often regarded as a discretisation of a time evolution, so it is natural to
consider the case
U_{H}={\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi H}
for some self-adjoint operator
H
on
\mathcal{H}
.
Recalling the symmetries of free-fermionic Hamiltonians,

H
is chiral-symmetric as a self-adjoint operator with respect to the self-adjoint unitary
\Gamma_{0}

if

\Gamma_{0}\cdot\Dom(H)\subset\Dom(H)
and
\Gamma_{0}H\Gamma_{0}=-H
.
For such chiral-symmetric
H
, we note that

\Gamma_{0}U_{H}\Gamma_{0}=U_{H}^{*}
and the discrete time evolution is a chiral unitary.
As in Section
2.3
, we can
decompose
H
as an off-diagonal operator under the spectral decomposition of
\Gamma_{0}
.
If
H
is Fredholm, we can consider the index problem

\Index(H_{+})
with
H_{+}=({\bf 1}-P_{0})HP_{0}
and
\Gamma_{0}=2P_{0}-{\bf 1}
.
We note that
\Index(H_{+})
is the same as the symmetry index
\operatorname{si}(H)
of the self-adjoint
and chiral-symmetric operator
H
introduced in equation (
2.1
) and
considered more generally in
[
13
, Section 2.6]
.

Proposition 2.25
.

Let
H\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be self-adjoint with
\Gamma_{0}H\Gamma_{0}=-H
,
\|H\|\leq 1
and

U_{H}={\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi H}=(2P_{0}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})
. If
H
is Fredholm, then

\operatorname{si}(H)=\Index(H_{+})=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})=\operatorname{si}_{+}(U_{H}).

The condition
\|H\|\leq 1
can always be guaranteed by considering
H\bigl({\bf 1}+H^{2}\bigr)^{-1/2}
for bounded or unbounded
H
.
We will prove an analogous result in the more general context of chiral unitaries on Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules in
Section
4.4
. For completeness, we give a short and independent proof.

Proof.

If
H_{+}
is Fredholm, then the index can be computed by the restricted trace of
\Gamma_{0}
,

\Index(H_{+})=\Tr\bigl(\Gamma_{0}|_{\Ker(H)}\bigr)
. Because
\Ker(H)
is finite dimensional and

\|H\|\leq 1
, we have that
\Ker(H)=\Ker(U_{H}-{\bf 1})
and so
(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{0})
is a Fredholm pair of
projections by Lemma
2.13
. We now use
that
\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})=\operatorname{si}_{+}(U_{H})
and so

\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})=\Tr\bigl(\Gamma_{0}|_{\Ker(U_{H}-{\bf 1})}\bigr)=\Tr\bigl(\Gamma_{0}|_{\Ker(H)}\bigr)=\Index(H_{+}).

Remark 2.26

(a warning with homotopies)
.

We have previously investigated the topological stability of
\mathbb{Z}
-valued indices for
chiral unitaries
U=(2P_{0}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})
via the index of a pair of projections.
Proposition
2.25
shows
that in the case that
U
comes from the discrete time evolution of a self-adjoint,
chiral-symmetric and bounded Fredholm operator
H
, then the index theoretic information of

H
can be recovered by considering
\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
.

A natural question to consider is whether the index of
H
is also connected to
\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
.
This is not the case. Furthermore, some care is required as homotopies
of the generator
H
that leave
\Index(H_{+})
invariant need not leave
\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
invariant.
As a simple example, let
H\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be Fredholm and consider the homotopy
[0,1]\ni t\mapsto H_{t}=(2\|H\|)^{-t}H
,
a continuous path from
H
to
\frac{1}{2\|H\|}H
. Clearly this path
is such that
\Ker(H_{t})
and
\Index((H_{t})_{+})
is constant for all
t\in[0,1]
.
However,
\|H_{1}\|=\tfrac{1}{2}
so
by the spectral mapping theorem
U_{H_{1}}={\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi H_{1}}={\rm e}^{\frac{{\rm i}\pi}{2\|H\|}H}
will have a spectral gap at
-1
.
In particular,
\Ker(U_{H_{1}}+{\bf 1})=\{0\}
. Therefore, for
P_{0}^{\prime}
and
P_{1}^{\prime}
such that
{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi H_{1}}=(2P_{0}^{\prime}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}^{\prime}-{\bf 1})
,

\big|\Ind(P_{0}^{\prime},P_{1}^{\prime})\big|\leq\dim\Ker(U_{H_{1}}+{\bf 1})=0
and so

\Ind(P_{0}^{\prime},P_{1}^{\prime})=0
. If, on the other hand,
H=H_{0}
has a unique eigenvalue at
+1
, then

\operatorname{si}_{-}\bigl({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi H_{0}}\bigr)=\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})=\pm 1
with
{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi H_{0}}=(2P_{0}-{\bf 1})(2P_{1}-{\bf 1})
.

2.7
Connection to the Cayley transform

Let us now study topological properties of the chiral unitary
U=\Gamma_{0}\Gamma_{1}
on
\mathcal{H}
via
the Cayley transform of
U
. Because we will study the Cayley transform more thoroughly in Section
4
,
we only provide a brief summary here. Recall that

\mathcal{C}(U)={\rm i}({\bf 1}+U)({\bf 1}-U)^{-1},\qquad\Dom(\mathcal{C}(U))=\Ran({\bf 1}-U)

and
\mathcal{C}(U)
is self-adjoint on the Hilbert subspace
\overline{\Ran({\bf 1}-U)}
, see, for example,
[
30
, Section X.1]
.

Let us first note that

\Gamma_{0}({\bf 1}-U)=({\bf 1}-U^{*})\Gamma_{0}=(U-{\bf 1})U^{*}\Gamma_{0},

so
\Gamma_{0}
preserves
\Ran({\bf 1}-U)=\Dom(\mathcal{C}(U))
and furthermore

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}\mathcal{C}(U)\Gamma_{0}

\displaystyle=({\bf 1}+U^{*})({\bf 1}-U^{*})^{-1}=\bigl(({\bf 1}+U^{*})U\bigr)\bigl(({\bf 1}-U^{*})U\bigr)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(U+{\bf 1})(U-{\bf 1})^{-1}=-\mathcal{C}(U).

Therefore,
\mathcal{C}(U)
anti-commutes with
\Gamma_{0}
and is chiral-symmetric as a self-adjoint operator.
We define

\mathcal{C}(U)_{+}=({\bf 1}-P_{0})\mathcal{C}(U)P_{0}
with

\Gamma_{0}=2P_{0}-{\bf 1}
.

Proposition 2.27
.

If
-1\notin\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(U)
, then
\mathcal{C}(U)
is Fredholm and

\operatorname{si}(\mathcal{C}(U))=\Index(\mathcal{C}(U)_{+})=\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})=\operatorname{si}_{-}(U).

If
+1\notin\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(U)
, then

\mathcal{C}(-U)
is Fredholm and

\operatorname{si}(\mathcal{C}(-U))=\Index(\mathcal{C}(-U)_{+})=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})=\operatorname{si}_{+}(U).

Proof.

If
-1\notin\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(U)
, then
\Ker(U+{\bf 1})
is finite dimensional and
(P_{0},P_{1})
is a Fredholm pair
by Lemma
2.14
.
Therefore,
\Ker(\mathcal{C}(U))=\Ker(U+{\bf 1})\cap\Ran({\bf 1}-U)
is also finite dimensional
and so
\mathcal{C}(U)
is Fredholm. Using
\Ran(U-{\bf 1})=\Ran(P_{0}-P_{1})
and Lemma
2.13
,

\Ker(\mathcal{C}(U))=\bigl(\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\oplus\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\bigr)\cap\Ran(P_{0}-P_{1}).

If
x\in\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})
, then
(P_{0}-P_{1})x=P_{0}x=x
and so
x\in\Ran(P_{0}-P_{1})
.
Similarly,
\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\subset\Ran(P_{0}-P_{1})
and so

\Ker(\mathcal{C}(U))=\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\oplus\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1}).

Now,
\Index(\mathcal{C}(U)_{+})
measures the spectral asymmetry of
\Gamma_{0}=2P_{0}-{\bf 1}
on
\Ker(\mathcal{C}(U))
. Hence,

\displaystyle\Index(\mathcal{C}(U)_{+})

\displaystyle=\dim\bigl(\Ran(P_{0})\cap\Ker(P_{1})\bigr)-\dim\bigl(\Ker(P_{0})\cap\Ran(P_{1})\bigr)=\Ind(P_{0},P_{1}).

The case of
\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
and
\mathcal{C}(-U)
is entirely analogous.
∎

Hence, all the previous results concerning
\operatorname{si}_{-}(U)=\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
and
\operatorname{si}_{+}(U)=\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
can be expressed
in terms of the Fredholm index of
\mathcal{C}(U)_{+}
and
\mathcal{C}(-U)_{+}
.

3
Resumé on Hilbert
\boldsymbol{C^{*}}
-modules and index theory

As a preliminary to considering chiral unitaries on Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules, we first review the basics of
Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules and noncommutative index theory.

3.1
Hilbert
\boldsymbol{C^{*}}
-modules

We refer the reader to
[
22
]
for a more detailed introduction and proofs.
Throughout this section,
A
is a
\sigma
-unital
C^{*}
-algebra. If
A
is separable, this condition
is equivalent to the existence of a countable approximate unit.

Definition 3.1
.

A Hilbert
C^{*}
-module is a right
A
-module
E_{A}
with a map

(\cdot\mid\cdot)_{A}\colon E_{A}\times E_{A}\to A
that is linear in the second variable such that
for all
e_{1},e_{2}\in E_{A}
and
a\in A
,

(e_{1}\mid e_{2}\cdot a)_{A}=(e_{1}\mid e_{2})_{A}a,\qquad(e_{1}\mid e_{2})_{A}=(e_{2}\mid e_{1})_{A}^{*},\qquad(e_{1}\mid e_{1})_{A}\geq 0

and
(e_{1}\mid e_{1})_{A}=0
if and only if
e_{1}=0
. Furthermore,
E_{A}
is complete with
respect to the norm
\|e\|=\big\|(e\mid e)_{A}\big\|_{A}^{1/2}
,
e\in E_{A}
.

Examples 3.2
.

(i)

A complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert
C^{*}
-module over
\mathbb{C}
with
(\cdot\mid\cdot)_{\mathbb{C}}
given by the
usual Hilbert space inner product.

(ii)

The algebra
A
can be seen as a Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
A_{A}
with the structure

a_{1}\cdot a_{2}=a_{1}a_{2},\qquad(a_{1}\mid a_{2})_{A}=a_{1}^{*}a_{2},\qquad a_{1},a_{2}\in A.

(iii)

The standard Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},A)
is defined as follows:

\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},A)=\bigg\{(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},a_{n}\in A\colon\ \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}^{*}a_{n}\text{ converges in }A\bigg\}

and has
C^{*}
-module structure

(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\cdot b=(a_{n}b)_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\quad a_{n},b\in A,\qquad\bigl((a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mid(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigr)_{A}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}^{*}b_{n}.

A Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
E_{A}
is full if the closure in the norm of
A
of the span of
\{(e_{1}\mid e_{2})_{A}
:
e_{1},e_{2}\in E_{A}\}

recovers
A
.
Both
A_{A}
and
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},A)
are full Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules.
Kasparov’s stabilisation theorem implies that for any countably generated Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
E_{A}
, there is an isometric embedding

E_{A}\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},A)

[
19
]
.

Remark 3.3
.

While Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules share many similarities to Hilbert spaces, one important difference is that if
F_{A}
is a closed submodule
of
E_{A}
with

F^{\perp}_{A}=\big\{e\in E_{A}\colon(e\mid f)_{A}=0\text{ for all }f\in F_{A}\big\},

then it is not guaranteed that
E_{A}=F_{A}\oplus F^{\perp}_{A}
in general.

Given a map
T\colon E_{A}\to E_{A}
, we say that
T
is adjointable if there is a map
T^{*}\colon E_{A}\to E_{A}
that acts as the adjoint
with respect to the
A
-valued inner product on
E_{A}
. Such maps are right
A
-linear and bounded in the operator norm.
The set of adjointable operators on
E_{A}
is denoted
\End_{A}(E)
and is a
C^{*}
-algebra with respect to the operator norm.
In analogy to the case of Hilbert spaces, where the compact operators arise as a norm-closure of finite-rank operators,
for any
e_{1},e_{2}\in E_{A}
, we define the operator
\Theta_{e_{1},e_{2}}(e_{3})=e_{1}\cdot(e_{2}\mid e_{3})_{A}
,
e_{3}\in E_{A}
.
One finds that
\Theta_{e_{1},e_{2}}^{*}=\Theta_{e_{2},e_{1}}
, so
\Theta_{e_{1},e_{2}}
is adjointable, and the
compact operators
\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
on
E_{A}
are defined such that

\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)=\overline{\operatorname{span}\big\{\Theta_{e_{1},e_{2}}\colon e_{1},e_{2}\in E_{A}\big\}}.

The compact operators are a
2
-sided ideal in
\End_{A}(E)
and we denote the

C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)=\End_{A}(E)/\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)

with
\pi\colon\End_{A}(E)\to\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)
the quotient map. Note that
\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)
is a
generalisation of the Calkin algebra to the setting of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules.

Examples 3.4
.

(i)

Considering a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
as a Hilbert
C^{*}
-module over
\mathbb{C}
, the compact and adjointable operators are
precisely the compact and bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
, respectively.

(ii)

Considering
A
as a Hilbert
C^{*}
-module over itself,
\mathbb{K}_{A}(A)=A
as multiplication is dense in
A
.
The adjointable operators are isomorphic to the multiplier algebra of
A
,
\Mult(A)\cong\End_{A}(A)
.

(iii)

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space and
\mathcal{H}\otimes A
the Hilbert
C^{*}
-module with structure

(\psi_{1}\otimes a_{1})\cdot a_{2}=\psi_{1}\otimes a_{1}a_{2},\qquad(\psi_{1}\otimes a_{1}\mid\psi_{2}\otimes a_{2})_{A}=\langle\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}a_{1}^{*}a_{2}

for
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
,
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
. Note that this Hilbert
C^{*}
-module is isomorphic to the standard Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},A)
.
In this case,
\mathbb{K}_{A}(\mathcal{H}\otimes A)=\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})\otimes A
and

\End_{A}(\mathcal{H}\otimes A)\cong\Mult(\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})\otimes A)
.

3.2
Fredholm operators on Hilbert
\boldsymbol{C^{*}}
-modules and
\boldsymbol{K}
-theory

We fix a
\sigma
-unital
C^{*}
-algebra
A
and countably generated
Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
E_{A}
. Our exposition closely follows
[
37
, Section 2]
.

We will occasionally need to deal with unbounded operators on Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules, though we will only
consider densely defined, closed and right
A
-linear operators
{D\colon\Dom(D)\!\!\subset\!E_{A}\!\to\!\!E_{A}}
.
We say that
D

is
regular
if
D^{*}
is densely defined and the operator
{\bf 1}+D^{*}D\colon\Dom(D^{*}D)\to E_{A}
has dense range.
Self-adjoint and regular operators admit a continuous functional calculus. We let

\Dom(D)_{A}
be the Hilbert
C^{*}
-module that comes from the completion of
\Dom(D)
in the graph
norm of
D
.
If
D
is regular,
{\bf 1}+D^{*}D
has a bounded and positive inverse in
\End_{A}(E)
with

\Ran\bigl(({\bf 1}+D^{*}D)^{-1}\bigr)\subset\Dom(D)
and dense in
E_{A}

[
22
, Lemma 9.2]
.
A short computation then shows that the
square root

({\bf 1}+D^{*}D)^{-1/2}\colon E_{A}\to\Dom(D)_{A}
is a unitary map.
The operator
F_{D}:=D({\bf 1}+D^{*}D)^{-1/2}\in\End_{A}(E)
is called the bounded transform of
D
.

Definition 3.5
.

We say that an operator
T\in\End_{A}(E)
is Fredholm on
E_{A}
if
\pi(T)\in\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)
is invertible.
We say that a regular operator
D\colon\Dom(D)\to E_{A}
is Fredholm on
E_{A}
if

F_{D}=D({\bf 1}+D^{*}D)^{-1/2}\in\End_{A}(E)
is Fredholm on
E_{A}
.

If
D\colon\Dom(D)\to E_{A}
is a regular operator, then
D
is Fredholm on
E_{A}
if and only if

D
is Fredholm as a bounded operator
\Dom(D)_{A}\to E_{A}
.

Proposition 3.6

(
[
37
, Proposition 2.1, Corollary 2.2]
)
.

Let
D
be a regular self-adjoint operator on
E_{A}
. The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

D
is Fredholm,

(ii)

There is an
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
\varphi\in C_{c}(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
,
\varphi(D)\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
.

(iii)

There is an
\varepsilon\!>\!0
such that for any continuous function
\chi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
{\chi|_{(-\infty,-\varepsilon]}=-1}

and
\chi|_{[\varepsilon,\infty)}=+1
,
\chi(D)^{2}-{\bf 1}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
D
be self-adjoint and regular on
E_{A}
.
We say that a smooth odd non-decreasing function
\chi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a normalising function for

D
if
\chi^{\prime}(0)>0
,
\lim_{x\to\infty}\chi(x)=1
and
\chi(D)^{2}-{\bf 1}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
.

We note that if
D
is self-adjoint, regular and
D
has compact resolvent,
(i+D)^{-1}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
, then
the bounded transform

x\mapsto x\bigl(1+x^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}
is a normalising function for
D
.

If
S\in\End_{A}(E)
, we will (again)
abuse notation and write
\|S\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}
to mean
\|\pi(S)\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}
with

\pi\colon\End_{A}(E)\to\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)
the quotient map.

Lemma 3.8

(
[
37
, Lemma 2.7]
)
.

(i)

A self-adjoint element
T\in\End_{A}(E)
with
\|T\|\leq 1
is Fredholm if and only if
{\big\|{\bf 1}-T^{2}\big\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}<1}
.

(ii)

A self-adjoint regular operator
T
is Fredholm if and only if
\big\|({\rm i}+T)^{-1}\big\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}<1
.

Proof.

The inequality
\big\|{\bf 1}-T^{2}\big\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}<c<1
holds if and only if
\pi(T)^{2}
is bounded from below by
{\bf 1}-c
.
Taking a
continuous function
\chi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\chi|_{(-\infty,-(1-c)]}=-1
,

\chi|_{[1-c,\infty)}=+1
, we see that
\chi(T)^{2}-{\bf 1}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
.
Part (i) then follows by Proposition
3.6
.

For (ii), we write
F_{T}=T\bigl({\bf 1}+T^{2}\bigr)^{-1/2}=T((T+{\rm i})^{*}(T+{\rm i}))^{-1/2}
and see that

\displaystyle{\bf 1}-F_{T}^{2}

\displaystyle={\bf 1}-T^{2}\bigl((T+{\rm i})^{*}(T+{\rm i})\bigr)^{-1}=\bigl((T+{\rm i})^{*}(T-{\rm i})-T^{2}\bigr)\bigl((T+{\rm i})^{*}(T+{\rm i})\bigr)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bigl((T+{\rm i})^{*}(T+{\rm i})\bigr)^{-1}.

Therefore,
\big\|{\bf 1}-\ F_{T}^{2}\big\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}=\big\|(T+{\rm i})^{-1}\big\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}^{2}
and by part (i)

\pi(F_{T})
is invertible if and only if
\big\|(T+{\rm i})^{-1}\big\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}<1
.
∎

Self-adjoint Fredholm operators on Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules yield topological information of

C^{*}
-algebras via Kasparov’s

KK
-theory
[
20
]
, which we briefly introduce in a simpler context.
If there is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading
E_{A}\cong\bigl(E^{0}\oplus E^{1}\bigr)_{A}
, we say that a densely
defined operator

D
is even (respectively odd) if
D\cdot\bigl(E^{i}\cap\Dom(D)\bigr)\subset E^{i}

(respectively
D\cdot\bigl(E^{i}\cap\Dom(D)\bigr)\subset E^{i+1}
) for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
E_{A}
be a countably generated Hilbert
C^{*}
-module and
F\in\End_{A}(E)
a self-adjoint operator such that

{\bf 1}-F^{2}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
.
If there is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading
E_{A}\cong\bigl(E^{0}\oplus E^{1}\bigr)_{A}
such that
F
acts as an odd operator, we
call the triple
(\mathbb{C},E_{A},F)
an even Kasparov module. Otherwise we call
(\mathbb{C},E_{A},F)
an odd Kasparov
module.

Two Kasparov modules
\bigl(\mathbb{C},E^{(0)}_{A},F_{0}\bigr)
and
\bigl(\mathbb{C},E^{(1)}_{A},F_{1}\bigr)
are unitarily equivalent if there is an
even unitary
U\colon E^{(1)}_{A}\to E^{(2)}

such that
UF_{1}U^{*}=F_{2}
.
We say that even/odd Kasparov modules are homotopic if there is an even/odd Kasparov module

\bigl(\mathbb{C},\tilde{E}_{A\otimes C([0,1])},F\bigr)
such that the evaluating the fibre at
0
and
1
yields Kasparov modules
that are unitarily equivalent to
\bigl(\mathbb{C},E^{(0)}_{A},F_{0}\bigr)
and
\bigl(\mathbb{C},E^{(1)}_{A},F_{1}\bigr)
, respectively.

Homotopy equivalence classes of even and odd Kasparov modules yields the abelian groups
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
and
KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)
, respectively,
where the group operation is by direct sum
[
20
, Section 4]
.

Remarks 3.10
.

(1)

If
F\in\End_{A}(E)
is a self-adjoint Fredholm operator such that
F^{2}={\bf 1}
, then the Kasparov module

\bigl(\mathbb{C},E_{A},F\bigr)
is called degenerate. Degenerate Kasparov modules represent the group identity in

KK(\mathbb{C},A)
or
KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)
.

(2)

Suppose that
\bigl(\mathbb{C},\bigl(E^{0}\oplus E^{1}\bigr)_{A},F\bigr)
is an even Kasparov module. We can ignore the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading
of
E_{A}
and instead consider this triple as an odd Kasparov module. However, this odd Kasparov module will be trivial
in
KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)
. To see this, let
\gamma=\gamma^{*}=\gamma^{-1}
denote the grading operator of
E_{A}

(
that is,
\gamma e^{i}=(-1)^{i}e^{i}
for
e^{i}\in E^{i}
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
)
. Then
by assumption
F\gamma+\gamma F=0
as
F
is odd. One can then check that the path

[0,1]\ni t\mapsto F_{t}=\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2}t)F+\sin(\tfrac{\pi}{2}t)\gamma\in\End_{A}(E)
is a homotopy of Fredholm operators on
E_{A}
and
we can define the odd Kasparov module

\bigl(\mathbb{C},C([0,1],E)_{A\otimes C([0,1])},F_{\bullet}\bigr),\qquad(F_{\bullet}e)(t)=F_{t}e(t).

Evaluating at the point
t=1
, the Kasparov module
(\mathbb{C},E_{A},\gamma)
is a degenerate odd Kasparov module and
so represents the identity in
KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)
.

Proposition 3.11

(
[
36
, Proposition 2.14]
)
.

Let
D
be a regular, self-adjoint and Fredholm operator on
E_{A}
and
\chi
a
normalising function for
D
. Then the triple
\bigl(\mathbb{C},E_{A},\chi(D)\bigr)
is an odd
Kasparov module. If there is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading
\bigl(E^{0}\oplus E^{1}\bigr)_{A}
such that
D
is an
odd operator, then the triple
\bigl(\mathbb{C},E_{A},\chi(D)\bigr)
is an even Kasparov module.
The equivalence class of this Kasparov module in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
or
KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)
is independent
of the choice of normalising function.

Definition 3.12
.

Given a self-adjoint, regular and Fredholm operator
D
on
E_{A}
, we denote by
[D]\in KK(\mathbb{C},A)
or
KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)
the equivalence class of the Kasparov
module from Proposition
3.11
.

Remark 3.13
.

If
F_{0},F_{1}\in\End_{A}(E)
are self-adjoint Fredholm operators such that
F_{0}-F_{1}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
, then

[F_{0}]=[F_{1}]\in KK(\mathbb{C},A)
or
KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)
, which can be shown by the simple homotopy

[0,1]\ni t\mapsto F_{t}=F_{0}+t(F_{1}-F_{0})
.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
A
be a
C^{*}
-algebra and
E_{A}
a countably generated Hilbert
C^{*}
-module.
Suppose that
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
are self-adjoint, regular and Fredholm on
E_{A}
,

\Dom(T_{0}T_{1})=\Dom(T_{1}T_{0})
is dense and
T_{1}T_{0}+T_{0}T_{1}=0
on this domain.
Then
[T_{0}]=[T_{1}]\in KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)
.
If
E_{A}
is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded and
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
are odd operators, then
[T_{0}]=[T_{1}]\in KK(\mathbb{C},A)
.

Proof.

We first fix normalising functions
\chi_{0}
and
\chi_{1}
for
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
, respectively.
Then
\chi_{0}(T_{0})
and
\chi_{1}(T_{1})\in\End_{A}(E)
also anti-commute. We can now
argue analogously to part 2 of Remarks
3.10
, where

\bigl(\mathbb{C},C([0,1],E)_{A\otimes C([0,1])},F_{\bullet}\bigr),\qquad(F_{\bullet}e)(t)=\bigl(\cos\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}t\big)\chi_{0}(T_{0})+\sin\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}t\big)\chi_{1}(T_{1})\bigr)e(t)

defines a Kasparov module and
so gives a homotopy in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
or
KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)
.
∎

There are isomorphisms of
KK
-theory to operator algebraic
K
-theory for trivially graded
C^{*}
-algebras,

KK(\mathbb{C},A)\xrightarrow{\simeq}K_{0}(A)
and
KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)\xrightarrow{\simeq}K_{1}(A)
,
see
[
20
, Section 6]
or
[
9
, Section 3]
. We will further examine the
isomorphism
KK(\mathbb{C},A)\cong K_{0}(A)
in Section
3.3
.

Remark 3.15
.

Given separable
C^{*}
-algebras
A
and
B
, the more general group
KK(A,B)
is constructed
from equivalence classes of triples

\bigl(A,{}_{\phi}E_{B},F\bigr)
, where
E_{B}
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded Hilbert
C^{*}
-module,

F
is an odd self-adjoint operator and there is a representation
\phi\colon A\to\End_{B}(E)

such that
\phi(a)\bigl({\bf 1}-F^{2}\bigr)
and
[\phi(a),F]_{\pm}
are compact for all
a\in A
with

[\cdot,\cdot]_{\pm}
the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded commutator.
The notation
{}_{\phi}E_{B}
is used to denote both the (right) Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
E_{B}
and the (left)
representation
\phi\colon A\to\End_{B}(E)
.
We note that

KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)\cong KK(\mathbb{C}\ell_{1},A)
with
\mathbb{C}\ell_{1}
the complex Clifford algebra with one generator.
While we will mostly work with
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
and
KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)
, we will occasionally take advantage
of the existence of the Kasparov product, a map

KK(A,B)\times KK(B,C)\to KK(A,C)

for separable
C^{*}
-algebras
A
,
B
and
C
. The Kasparov product also equips

KK(A,A)
with the structure of a ring.

3.3
The index of a pair of projections

In this section we review an analogue of the index of a pair of projections in the Hilbert
C^{*}
-module setting.
Most of the content can be extracted from
[
20
, Section 6]
. See also
[
37
, Section 3.2]
.
We again fix a
\sigma
-unital
C^{*}
-algebra
A
and countably generated Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
E_{A}
.

Definition 3.16
.

We say that the projections
p_{0},p_{1}\in\End_{A}(E)
are a Fredholm pair if

\|p_{0}-p_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}<1
.

Recalling Lemma
2.8
, any Fredholm pair of projections
p_{0},p_{1}\in\End_{A}(E)

will be unitarily equivalent in the quotient algebra
\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)
.
Constructing a Kasparov module from a Fredholm pair of projections is well known,
but for completeness we recall the details.

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
(p_{0},p_{1})
be a Fredholm pair of projections. Then for any
lift
V
of a unitary
v\in\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)
such that
v\pi(p_{0})v^{*}=\pi(p_{1})
, the triple

\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}\\
p_{1}Vp_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr)

is a Kasparov module. If
V^{\prime}
is another lift of
v
, then the two Kasparov modules represent the same class in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
.

Proof.

Using that
Vp_{0}V^{*}=p_{1}
modulo compacts, we check that

\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}\\
p_{1}Vp_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}^{2}-{\bf 1}=\begin{pmatrix}p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}Vp_{0}-p_{0}&0\\
0&p_{1}Vp_{0}V^{*}p_{1}-p_{1}\end{pmatrix}=0\quad{\rm mod}\ \mathbb{K}_{A}(E).

Finally, any other lift
V^{\prime}
of
v
is such that
V-V^{\prime}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
. Therefore

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&p_{0}V^{\prime*}p_{1}\\
p_{1}V^{\prime}p_{0}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is also a compact perturbation of

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}\\
p_{1}Vp_{0}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Hence their classes coincide in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
.
∎

Definition 3.18
.

Let
(p_{0},p_{1})
be a Fredholm pair of projections in
\End_{A}(E)
. We denote by

[(p_{0},p_{1})]\in KK(\mathbb{C},A)
the equivalence class of the Kasparov module from Proposition
3.17
.

The element
[(p_{0},p_{1})]
can be considered as a
K
-theoretic analogue of the index of a pair of projections in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.
Indeed, when
A=\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
, the isomorphism
KK(\mathbb{C},\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H}))\xrightarrow{\simeq}\mathbb{Z}
maps

[(p_{0},p_{1})]
to
\Ind(p_{0},p_{1})
introduced in Section
2.4
.

Remark 3.19
.

Let
(p_{0},p_{1})
be a Fredholm pair of projections in
\End_{A}(E)
. Using the same proof as
Proposition
3.17
, we can also construct the Kasparov module

\bigg(\mathbb{C},p_{0}\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)\oplus p_{1}\mathbb{K}_{A}(E),\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}\\
p_{1}Vp_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\bigg),\qquad\pi(Vp_{0}V^{*})=\pi(p_{1}),

where
\pi(V)\in\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)
is unitary and
\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
is the Hilbert
C^{*}
-module over itself.
We therefore obtain
an element
\big[(p_{0},p_{1})_{\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)}\big]\in KK(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{K}_{A}(E))
.
When
E_{A}
is a full Hilbert
C^{*}
-module,
\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
is Morita equivalent to
A
, which
gives an isomorphism
KK(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{K}_{A}(E))\xrightarrow{\simeq}KK(\mathbb{C},A)
that maps

\big[(p_{0},p_{1})_{\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)}\big]
to
[(p_{0},p_{1})]
.

We also note that any class in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
can be represented by an even Kasparov module

\bigl(\mathbb{C},\tilde{E}_{A},F\bigr)
such that
\tilde{E}_{A}
is a full Hilbert
C^{*}
-module.

If
A
is a unital
C^{*}
-algebra with ideal
J
and quotient
q\colon A\to A/J
, then the relative
K
-theory group
K_{0}(A,J)
can be
constructed via projections
p_{0},p_{1}\in A
such that
q(p_{0})
and
q(p_{1})
are
unitarily equivalent in
A/J
, see, for example,
[
5
, Section 5.4]
. There is also
an excision isomorphism
K_{0}(A,J)\cong K_{0}(J)
.
Therefore, if
(p_{0},p_{1})
is a Fredholm pair of projections in
\End_{A}(E)
, then by Lemma

2.8
we obtain a relative
K
-theory class

[p_{0}]-[p_{1}]\in K_{0}(\End_{A}(E),\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)).

Theorem 3.20

(
[
20
, Section 6, Theorem 3]
)
.

Let
(p_{0},p_{1})
be a Fredholm pair of projections in
\End_{A}(E)
. Then the map

K_{0}(\End_{A}(E),\mathbb{K}_{A}(E))\to KK(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{K}_{A}(E))
given by

[p_{0}]-[p_{1}]\mapsto\bigg[\bigg(\mathbb{C},p_{0}\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)\oplus p_{1}\mathbb{K}_{A}(E),\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}\\
p_{1}Vp_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\bigg)\bigg],\qquad\pi(Vp_{0}V^{*})=\pi(p_{1}),

is well defined and an isomorphism of groups.

We now consider some additional properties of
[(p_{0},p_{1})]
that help justify our terminology as
the index of a pair of projections.

Lemma 3.21
.

(i)

If
(p_{0},p_{1})
is a Fredholm pair of projections in
\End_{A}(E)
, then
[(p_{0},p_{1})]^{-1}=[(p_{1},p_{0})]\in KK(\mathbb{C},A)
.

(ii)

If
(p_{0},{\bf 1}-p_{1})
is a Fredholm pair of projections in
\End_{A}(E)
, so is
(p_{1},{\bf 1}-p_{0})

and
[(p_{0},{\bf 1}-p_{1})]=[(p_{1},{\bf 1}-p_{0})]\in KK(\mathbb{C},A)
.

Proof.

For part (i) we will show that
[(p_{0},p_{1})]\oplus[(p_{1},p_{0})]
is the group identity in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
. Taking a unitary

v\in\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)
such that
v\pi(p_{0})v^{*}=\pi(p_{1})
with lift
V\in\End_{A}(E)
, we have the sum of Kasparov modules

\displaystyle\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}\\
p_{1}Vp_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr)\oplus\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{1}E_{A}\oplus p_{0}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{1}Vp_{0}\\
p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr)

\displaystyle\quad=\biggl(\mathbb{C},\bigl(p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A}\bigr)\oplus\bigl(p_{1}E_{A}\oplus p_{0}E_{A},\bigr),\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}\\
p_{1}Vp_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\oplus\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{1}Vp_{0}\\
p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr).

We decompose the direct sum of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules into its even and odd parts and write the sum as the Kasparov module

\left(\mathbb{C},\bigl(p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A}\bigr)\oplus\bigl(p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A},\bigr),\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}\\
0&0&p_{1}Vp_{0}&0\\
0&p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}&0&0\\
p_{1}Vp_{0}&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\right).

The Fredholm operator of this Kasparov module anti-commutes with the operator
\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{3}

with
\sigma_{1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&{\bf 1}\\
{\bf 1}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
\sigma_{3}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{\bf 1}&\hphantom{-}0\\
0&-{\bf 1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
Therefore, by Proposition
3.14
the sum

[(p_{0},p_{1})]\oplus[(p_{1},p_{0})]
can be represented by the Kasparov module

(\mathbb{C},\bigl(p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A}\bigr)\oplus\bigl(p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A},\bigr),\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{3}),

which is degenerate and so represents the group identity in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
.

For part (ii), it is immediate that
(p_{0},{\bf 1}-p_{1})
is a Fredholm pair if and only if

(p_{1},{\bf 1}-p_{0})
is a Fredholm pair. We also note that if
Vp_{0}V^{*}={\bf 1}-p_{1}
modulo
\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
, then

V({\bf 1}-p_{0})V^{*}=p_{1}
modulo
\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
.
We will show that
[(p_{0},{\bf 1}-p_{1})]\oplus[(p_{1},{\bf 1}-p_{0})]^{-1}=[(p_{0},{\bf 1}-p_{1})]\oplus[({\bf 1}-p_{0},p_{1})]
is the
identity in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
. The sum can be represented by the Kasparov module

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{C},\bigl(p_{0}E_{A}\oplus({\bf 1}-p_{0})E_{A}\bigr)\oplus\bigl(p_{1}E_{A}\oplus({\bf 1}-p_{1})E_{A}\bigr),\vphantom{\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&p_{0}V^{*}({\bf 1}-p_{1})\\
0&0&({\bf 1}-p_{0})V^{*}p_{1}&0\\
0&p_{1}V({\bf 1}-p_{0})&0&0\\
({\bf 1}-p_{1})Vp_{0}\end{pmatrix}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&p_{0}V^{*}({\bf 1}-p_{1})\\
0&0&({\bf 1}-p_{0})V^{*}p_{1}&0\\
0&p_{1}V({\bf 1}-p_{0})&0&0\\
({\bf 1}-p_{1})Vp_{0}\end{pmatrix}\right).

We make the identifications

p_{0}E_{A}\oplus({\bf 1}-p_{0})E_{A}\cong E_{A},\qquad p_{1}E_{A}\oplus({\bf 1}-p_{1})E_{A}\cong E_{A},

which simplifies the Kasparov module to

\biggl(\mathbb{C},E_{A}\oplus E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&V^{*}\\
V&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr).

But this Kasparov module represents the index
[({\bf 1},{\bf 1})]\in KK(\mathbb{C},A)
as indeed
\pi(V{\bf 1}V^{*})=\pi({\bf 1})
in
\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)
.
Hence by part (i), the sum
[(p_{0},{\bf 1}-p_{1})]\oplus[({\bf 1}-p_{0},p_{1})]
is the group identity.
∎

Lastly, we prove an addition formula for Fredholm pairs of projections using the group structure in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
.

Proposition 3.22
.

Suppose that
(p_{0},p_{1})
and
(p_{1},p_{2})
are Fredholm pairs of projections in
\End_{A}(E)

with
v,w\in\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)
unitaries such that
v\pi(p_{0})v^{*}=\pi(p_{1})
and
w\pi(p_{1})w^{*}=\pi(p_{2})
.
Then

\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{2}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&V^{*}p_{1}W^{*}\\
Wp_{1}V&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr),\qquad\pi(V)=v,\qquad\pi(W)=w,

(3.1)

is a Kasparov module whose equivalence class
[(p_{0},p_{2})]=[(p_{0},p_{1})]\oplus[(p_{1},p_{2})]\in KK(\mathbb{C},A)
.

According to our definition,
(p_{0},p_{2})
need not be a Fredholm pair of projections. However, this is not required in order to build
a Kasparov module and class
[(p_{0},p_{2})]\in KK(\mathbb{C},A)
.

Proof.

Given the unitaries
v,w\in\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)
,
it’s then easy to see that
wv\in\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)
is also unitary and

wv\pi(p_{0})v^{*}w^{*}=\pi(p_{2})
.
Therefore, we can build the Kasparov module

\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{2}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{0}V^{*}W^{*}p_{2}\\
p_{2}WVp_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr).

Taking compact perturbations, this Kasparov module is equivalent in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
to

\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{2}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&V^{*}p_{1}W^{*}\\
Wp_{1}V&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr).

We now take the sum of the two Kasparov modules

\displaystyle\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}\\
p_{1}Vp_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr)\oplus\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{1}E_{A}\oplus p_{2}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{1}W^{*}p_{2}\\
p_{2}Wp_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr)

\displaystyle\quad=\biggl(\mathbb{C},\bigl(p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A}\bigr)\oplus\bigl(p_{1}E_{A}\oplus p_{2}E_{A}\bigr),\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{0}V^{*}p_{1}\\
p_{1}Vp_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\oplus\begin{pmatrix}0&p_{1}W^{*}p_{2}\\
p_{2}Wp_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr).

On the summed Kasparov module, we first rearrange the direct sum

\bigl(p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A}\bigr)\oplus\bigl(p_{1}E_{A}\oplus p_{2}E_{A}\bigr)\to\bigl(p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{2}E_{A}\bigr)\oplus\bigl(p_{1}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A}\bigr).

Applying this rearranging, we split the Kasparov module into a sum,

\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{2}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&V^{*}p_{1}W^{*}\\
Wp_{1}V&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr)\oplus\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{1}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&Vp_{0}V^{*}\\
W^{*}p_{2}W&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr),

where we have freely taken compact perturbations of the operators (which will not change the
KK
-class).
Finally,
Vp_{0}V^{*}
and
W^{*}p_{2}W
equal
p_{1}
modulo
\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
. Hence the second term in the sum is equivalent
to the Kasparov module

\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{1}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&{\bf 1}\\
{\bf 1}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr),

which is degenerate and represents the group identity in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
. Therefore

\displaystyle\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{2}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&V^{*}p_{1}W^{*}\\
Wp_{1}V&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr)\oplus\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{1}E_{A}\oplus p_{1}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&Vp_{0}V^{*}\\
W^{*}p_{2}W&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr)

\displaystyle\qquad\sim\biggl(\mathbb{C},p_{0}E_{A}\oplus p_{2}E_{A},\begin{pmatrix}0&V^{*}p_{1}W^{*}\\
Wp_{1}V&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr),

which is the same Kasparov module as equation (
3.1
).
∎

3.4
The map
\boldsymbol{(\tau_{B})_{\ast}\colon KK(\mathbb{C},B)\to\mathbb{R}}

Finally, we recall the induced map on
KK(\mathbb{C},B)
from a faithful norm-lower semicontinuous
trace
\tau_{B}
on a
C^{*}
-algebra
B
. In the context of this paper, we only consider the case where

B
is unital and
\tau_{B}({\bf 1})=1
. Further details and
proofs can be found in
[
17
, Section 4]
.

We first recall the induced trace on operator algebraic
K
-theory. An element

[p_{0}]-[p_{1}]\in K_{0}(B)
is represented by a pair of projections
p_{0},p_{1}\in M_{N}(B)

for some sufficiently large
N
. Writing these matrices as

p_{\bullet}=\bigl((p_{\bullet})_{j,k}\bigr)_{j,k=1}^{N}
,
(p_{\bullet})_{jk}\in B
,
\bullet\in\{0,1\}
,
we can then easily define a map

(\tau_{B})_{\ast}([p_{0}]-[p_{1}])=(\Tr\otimes\tau_{B})(p_{0}-p_{1})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\tau_{B}((p_{0})_{jj})-\tau_{B}((p_{1})_{jj}))\in\mathbb{R}

that respects the equivalence relation on
K_{0}(B)
.

Our task is to translate this map to the setting of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules and Fredholm operators.

Definition 3.23
.

We say that a Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
E_{B}
is finitely generated and projective if there is a
set
\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset E_{B}
such that
{\bf 1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Theta_{y_{j},y_{j}}
.

If
E_{B}
is finitely generated and projective, we call
\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
a finite frame for

E_{B}
. We note that for finitely generated and projective Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules,

{\bf 1}
is a compact endomorphism and so
\End_{B}(E)=\mathbb{K}_{B}(E)
. In particular,
the triple
\bigl(\mathbb{C},E_{B},0\bigr)
is a Kasparov module.

Using the finite frame
\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
,
we can define a projection

p=\bigl(p_{jk}\bigr)_{j,k=1}^{n}\in M_{n}(B),\qquad p_{jk}=(y_{j}\mid y_{k})_{B},

and there are maps

\displaystyle S\colon\

\displaystyle E_{B}\to(B_{B})^{n},\qquad

\displaystyle R\colon\

\displaystyle(B_{B})^{n}\to E_{B},

\displaystyle S(e)=\bigl((y_{j}\mid e)_{B}\bigr)_{j=1}^{n},\qquad

\displaystyle R\bigl((b_{j})_{j=1}^{n}\bigr)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}\cdot b_{j},

that restrict to isomorphisms
S\colon E_{B}\to p(B_{B})^{n}
and
R\colon p(B_{B})^{n}\to E_{B}
.
We now note the following crucial result.

Proposition 3.24

(
[
17
, Section 4.2]
)
.

Let
B
be a unital
C^{*}
-algebra,
E_{B}=(E^{0}\oplus E^{1})_{B}
a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded Hilbert

C^{*}
-module and
T=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&F^{*}\\
F&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\End_{B}(E)

a self-adjoint Fredholm operator. If
F\colon E^{0}_{B}\to E^{1}_{B}
has closed range, then the Hilbert

C^{*}
-submodules
\Ker(F)
and
\Ker(F^{*})
are finitely generated and projective.

The condition that
F
has closed range is equivalent to the existence of a Moore–Penrose inverse
[
39
, Theorem 2.2]
,
an adjointable operator
G:E^{1}_{B}\to E^{0}_{B}
such that

GFG=G,\qquad FGF=F,\qquad(FG)^{*}=FG,\qquad(GF)^{*}=GF.

Once we obtain finitely generated and projective Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules
\Ker(F)
and
\Ker(F^{*})
, we
can construct projections
p_{0}\in M_{n}(B)
and
p_{1}\in M_{m}(B)
from the finite frames
and take the induced trace. We can extend this to a map on
KK(\mathbb{C},B)
via the following
result.

Proposition 3.25

(
[
17
, Sections 4.3–4.4]
)
.

Let
T\in\End_{B}(E)
be an odd self-adjoint Fredholm operator on the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded
Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
E_{B}
. Then
[T]=\big[\tilde{T}\big]\in KK(\mathbb{C},B)
with

\tilde{T}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&\tilde{F}^{*}\\
\tilde{F}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\End_{B}\bigl(\tilde{E}\bigr)

an odd self-adjoint Fredholm operator on
\tilde{E}_{B}
such that

\tilde{F}
has closed range. Furthermore,
[T]
can be represented by the
even Kasparov module

\bigl(\mathbb{C},\Ker\bigl(\tilde{F}\bigr)_{B}\oplus\Ker\bigl(\tilde{F}^{*}\bigr)_{B},0\bigr),

with grading operator
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{\bf 1}&\hphantom{-}0\\
0&-{\bf 1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

We will write the map
(\tau_{B})_{\ast}\colon KK(\mathbb{C},B)\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle(\tau_{B})_{\ast}([T])={}

\displaystyle\tau_{B}\bigl(\Ker\bigl(\tilde{F}\bigr)\bigr)-\tau_{B}\bigl(\Ker\bigl(\tilde{F}^{*}\bigr)\bigr)

\displaystyle:={}

\displaystyle(\Tr\otimes\tau_{B})\big(p_{\Ker(\tilde{F})}\big)-(\Tr\otimes\tau_{B})\big(p_{\Ker(\tilde{F}^{*})}\big),

where
p_{\Ker(\tilde{F})}
and
p_{\Ker(\tilde{F}^{*})}
are the projections constructed from the
finite frames on
\Ker\bigl(\tilde{F}\bigr)
and
\Ker\bigl(\tilde{F}^{*}\bigr)
, respectively.

4
Chiral unitaries on Hilbert
\boldsymbol{C^{*}}
-modules and
\boldsymbol{K}
-theory

In this section, we extend our definition of chiral unitary to adjointable operators acting on
Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules. As Hilbert spaces are an example of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules, the contents of
Section
2

can be recovered as a special case.

4.1
The Cayley transform for chiral unitaries

We will freely use definitions and results from Section
3
.
Throughout this section, we will fix a complex
\sigma
-unital
C^{*}
-algebra
A
and a
countably generated Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
E_{A}
.
The adjointable and
compact operators on this Hilbert
C^{*}
-module will be denoted by
\End_{A}(E)
and
\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)

respectively. We also denote the quotient algebra
\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)=\End_{A}(E)/\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that a unitary operator
u\in\End_{A}(E)
is a chiral unitary with
respect to a self-adjoint unitary
\gamma_{0}\in\End_{A}(E)
if
\gamma_{0}u\gamma_{0}=u^{*}
.

Applying Lemma
2.1
,
u\in\End_{A}(E)
is a chiral unitary with
respect to a self-adjoint unitary
\gamma_{0}\in\End_{A}(E)
if and only if there is a
self-adjoint unitary
\gamma_{1}\in\End_{A}(E)
such that
u=\gamma_{0}\gamma_{1}
.

The Cayley transform of chiral unitaries considered in Section
2.7
can
also be employed in the Hilbert
C^{*}
-module setting.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
u\in\End_{A}(E)
be a chiral unitary with respect to
\gamma_{0}\in\End_{A}(E)
.
The Cayley transform of
u
is the operator

\mathcal{C}(u)={\rm i}({\bf 1}+u)({\bf 1}-u)^{-1},\qquad\Dom(\mathcal{C}(u))=({\bf 1}-u)E_{A}.

Lemma 4.3

(
[
22
, Chapter 10]
)
.

Let
u\in\End_{A}(E)
be a chiral unitary with respect to
\gamma_{0}\in\End_{A}\!(E)
. Then

\mathcal{C}(u)
is a self-adjoint and regular operator on
\overline{({\bf 1}\!-\!u)E}_{\!A}
, the closure of
\Dom(\mathcal{C}(u)\!)
in the
module norm of
E_{A}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{C}(u)
anti-commutes with
\gamma_{0}
.

Proof.

It is proved in
[
22
, Chapter 10]
that
\mathcal{C}(u)
is self-adjoint and regular.
We next note that
\gamma_{0}
preserves the domain of
\mathcal{C}(u)
,

\gamma_{0}({\bf 1}-u)E_{A}=({\bf 1}-u^{*})\gamma_{0}E_{A}=(u-{\bf 1})u^{*}\gamma_{0}E_{A}\subset({\bf 1}-u)E_{A}

and furthermore

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}\mathcal{C}(u)\gamma_{0}

\displaystyle=({\bf 1}+u^{*})({\bf 1}-u^{*})^{-1}=(u+{\bf 1})(u-{\bf 1})^{-1}=-\mathcal{C}(u).

∎

Definition 4.4
.

We say that a chiral unitary
u\in\End_{A}(E)
with respect to
\gamma_{0}\in\End_{A}(E)
is
of Fredholm type if
\|{\bf 1}-u\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}<2
.

Our terminology is justified by the following result.

Proposition 4.5
.

If
u\in\End_{A}(E)
is a chiral unitary with respect to
\gamma_{0}\in\End_{A}(E)
of Fredholm type,
then
\mathcal{C}(u)
is Fredholm on
\overline{({\bf 1}-u)E}_{A}
, the closure of
\Dom(\mathcal{C}(u))
in the
module norm of
E_{A}
.

Proof.

Recalling Lemma
3.8
, it suffices to show that
\big\|({\rm i}+\mathcal{C}(u))^{-1}\big\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(\overline{({\bf 1}-u)E})}<1
.
We can then easily compute that on
\Dom(\mathcal{C}(u))

{\rm i}+\mathcal{C}(u)={\rm i}\bigl(({\bf 1}-u)+({\bf 1}+u)\bigr)({\bf 1}-u)^{-1}=2{\rm i}({\bf 1}-u)^{-1},

hence
({\rm i}+\mathcal{C}(u))^{-1}=-\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2}({\bf 1}-u)
and extends to a map
E_{A}\to\Dom(\mathcal{C}(u))
. We therefore have that

\big\|({\rm i}+\mathcal{C}(u))^{-1}\big\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(\overline{({\bf 1}-u)E})}=\frac{1}{2}\|u-{\bf 1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}<1.

The self-adjoint unitary
\gamma_{0}
gives a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading of the Hilbert
C^{*}
-module

\overline{({\bf 1}-u)E}_{A}
with
\mathcal{C}(u)
an odd operator. Therefore, if
u\in\End_{A}(E)
is a chiral
unitary with respect to

\gamma_{0}\in\End_{A}(E)
of Fredholm type, we obtain an element

\big[\mathcal{C}(u)\big]\in KK(\mathbb{C},A)\cong K_{0}(A)
by Proposition
3.11
.

Remark 4.6
.

Suppose that
u\in\End_{A}(E)
is a
self-adjoint
chiral unitary,

u=u^{*}=\gamma_{0}u\gamma_{0}
. Hence
u
commutes with
\gamma_{0}
. Using
that
u^{-1}=u
, one can show that
\mathcal{C}(u)=-\mathcal{C}(u)
and so is the zero map.
Similarly the Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
\overline{({\bf 1}-u)E}_{A}=pE_{A}
with
p=\tfrac{1}{2}({\bf 1}-u)
. Hence, if

\|u-1\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}<2
, then the class
\big[\mathcal{C}(u)\big]
is determined by
the
-1
eigenspace projection
\tfrac{1}{2}({\bf 1}-u)
.

For completeness, let us also study the bounded transform

F_{\mathcal{C}(u)}=\mathcal{C}(u)\bigl({\bf 1}+\mathcal{C}(u)^{2}\bigr)^{-1/2}
on
\overline{({\bf 1}-u)E}_{A}
.
We first compute on the appropriate domain using the normality of
u
,

\displaystyle{\bf 1}+\mathcal{C}(u)^{*}\mathcal{C}(u)

\displaystyle={\bf 1}+\gamma_{0}({\bf 1}-u^{*})^{-1}({\bf 1}+u^{*})({\bf 1}+u)({\bf 1}-u)^{-1}\gamma_{0}

\displaystyle={\bf 1}+(2+u+u^{*})(2-u-u^{*})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bigl((2-u-u^{*})+(2+u+u^{*})\bigr)(2-u-u^{*})^{-1}

\displaystyle=4(2-u-u^{*})^{-1}=4\bigl(({\bf 1}-u)^{*}({\bf 1}-u)\bigr)^{-1}.

Therefore,
\bigl({\bf 1}+\mathcal{C}(u)^{2}\bigr)^{-1/2}=\tfrac{1}{2}(2-u-u^{*})^{1/2}=\tfrac{1}{2}|{\bf 1}-u|
.
We define
V
to be the (unitary) completion of the operator

({\bf 1}-u)e\mapsto|{\bf 1}-u|e
on the dense subspace
({\bf 1}-u)E_{A}
(cf.
[
22
, Proposition 3.8]
).
Then we find that

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{C}(u)}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm i}}{2}({\bf 1}+u)({\bf 1}-u)^{-1}(2-u-u^{*})^{1/2}=\frac{{\rm i}}{2}({\bf 1}+u)({\bf 1}-u)^{-1}|{\bf 1}-u|=\frac{{\rm i}}{2}({\bf 1}+u)V

(4.1)

and

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{C}(u)}^{*}F_{\mathcal{C}(u)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}|{\bf 1}-u|({\bf 1}-u^{*})^{-1}({\bf 1}+u^{*})({\bf 1}+u)({\bf 1}-u)|{\bf 1}-u|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}({\bf 1}+u^{*})({\bf 1}+u)=\frac{1}{4}(2+u+u^{*}),

which then implies

{\bf 1}-F_{\mathcal{C}(u)}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}(u+u^{*})=\frac{1}{4}(2-u-u^{*})=\frac{1}{4}({\bf 1}-u)^{*}({\bf 1}-u).

If
{\bf 1}-u\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)
, then
{\bf 1}-F_{\mathcal{C}(u)}^{2}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(\overline{({\bf 1}-u)E})

and
\mathcal{C}(u)
has compact resolvent,
({\rm i}+\mathcal{C}(u))^{-1}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(\overline{({\bf 1}-u)E})
.

We now prove a key stability result of our
K
-theoretic index for chiral unitaries.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\{u(t)\}_{t\in[0,1]}\subset\End_{A}(E)
and
\{\gamma_{0}(t)\}_{t\in[0,1]}\subset\End_{A}(E)
be
strongly continuous paths of unitaries
such that
u(t)
is a chiral unitary with respect to
\gamma_{0}(t)
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
If
\|u(t)-{\bf 1}\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}<2
for all
t\in[0,1]
, then

\big[\mathcal{C}(u(t))\big]
is constant in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
.

Proof.

Our assumptions are such that for all
t\in[0,1]
,
\mathcal{C}(u(t))
is an odd, self-adjoint
and Fredholm operator on
\overline{({\bf 1}-u(t))E}
and so has a normalising function
\chi_{t}
.
Let us therefore fix a function
\chi
such that
\chi
is a normalising function of

\mathcal{C}(u(t))
for all
t\in[0,1]
. (This can be done via
Proposition
3.6
and taking a function
\chi
such that

\operatorname{supp}(\chi)\subset\operatorname{supp}(\chi_{t})
for all
t\in[0,1]
.)
Then by
[
38
, Lemma 1.1]
,
the path

[0,1]\ni t\mapsto\chi(\mathcal{C}(u(t)))\in\End_{A}(\overline{({\bf 1}-u(t))E})
is strongly continuous.
In particular, the function

[0,1]\ni t\mapsto{\bf 1}-\chi(\mathcal{C}(u(t)))^{2}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(\overline{({\bf 1}-u(t))E})
and is
norm
continuous.
Therefore, we can construct an even Kasparov module

\Big(\mathbb{C},C([0,1],\overline{({\bf 1}-u(\cdot))E})_{C[0,1]\otimes A},\chi\big[\mathcal{C}(u(\cdot))\big]\Big),\qquad\big[\chi(\mathcal{C}(u(\cdot)))g\big](t)=\chi\big[\mathcal{C}(u(t))\big]g(t),

where
g\in C([0,1],\overline{({\bf 1}-u(\cdot))E})_{C[0,1]\otimes A}
is such that
g(t)\in\overline{({\bf 1}-u(t))E}_{A}
.
We therefore have obtained a homotopy of even
Kasparov modules in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
and so
\big[\mathcal{C}(u(t))\big]
is constant.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

If
u_{0}
is homotopic to
{\bf 1}
in
\End_{A}(E)
via a path
\{u_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
of chiral unitaries
with respect to
\gamma_{0}
of Fredholm type,
then
\big[\mathcal{C}(u_{0})\big]
is
trivial in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
.

Remark 4.9
.

If
u\in\End_{A}(E)
is a chiral unitary with respect to
\gamma_{0}\in\End_{A}(E)
, then so is
u^{*}
and
-u
.
Furthermore,
u
is of Fredholm type if and only if
u^{*}
is of Fredholm type and
\big[\mathcal{C}(u^{*})\big]
represents the inverse of
\big[\mathcal{C}(u)\big]
in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)
.
In contrast,
there is no relation between
\big[\mathcal{C}(u)\big]
and
\big[\mathcal{C}(-u)\big]
in
general. Indeed, there is no guarantee that
\mathcal{C}(-u)
is Fredholm if
\mathcal{C}(u)
is Fredholm.
This is analogous to the indices
\Ind(P_{0},P_{1})
and
\Ind(P_{0},{\bf 1}-P_{1})
considered in Section
2
.

To summarise the contents of this subsection, we have defined an index taking values in
KK(\mathbb{C},A)\cong K_{0}(A)

for chiral unitaries
u=\gamma_{0}\gamma_{1}
acting on the Hilbert
C^{*}
-module
E_{A}
that are of Fredholm type
(see Definition
4.4
). Proposition
4.7
shows that this index is
indeed a homotopy invariant of chiral unitaries on
E_{A}
of Fredholm type.
Recalling Proposition
2.27
, the index
\big[\mathcal{C}(u)\big]\in KK(\mathbb{C},A)
,
is a direct generalisation of the indices defined for chiral unitaries on Hilbert spaces.

4.2
The Cayley transform of a pair of projections

Because any chiral unitary
u\in\End_{A}(E)
has a decomposition

u=(2p_{0}-{\bf 1})(2p_{1}-{\bf 1})
with
p_{0},p_{1}\in\End_{A}(E)
projections, we can rewrite many of
our results concerning the Cayley transform in terms of
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
directly.
This Cayley transform can be seen as an equivalent formulation of the
index of a pair of projections
considered in Section
3.3
.
We first note the following, which can be proved with basic algebra.

Lemma 4.10

(cf.
[
2
, Section 2]
)
.

Let
p_{0},p_{1}\in\End_{A}(E)
be projections.

(i)

Then the operators
(p_{1}+p_{0}-{\bf 1})
and
(p_{1}-p_{0})
anti-commute and
(p_{1}-p_{0})^{2}+(p_{1}+p_{0}-{\bf 1})^{2}={\bf 1}
.

(ii)

The operator
(p_{1}-p_{0})^{2}
commutes with
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
.

Definition 4.11
.

Let
p_{0},p_{1}\in\End_{A}(E)
be projections. We define the Cayley transform of a pair of projections as the operator

\mathcal{C}(p_{1},p_{0})={\rm i}(p_{1}+p_{0}-{\bf 1})(p_{1}-p_{0})^{-1},\qquad\Dom\bigl(\mathcal{C}(p_{1},p_{0})\bigr)=(p_{1}-p_{0})E_{A}.

Lemma 4.12
.

The operator
\mathcal{C}(p_{1},p_{0})
is self-adjoint and regular operator on
\overline{(p_{1}-p_{0})E}_{A}
and anti-commutes with

2p_{0}-{\bf 1}
.

Proof.

Defining
u=(2p_{0}-{\bf 1})(2p_{1}-{\bf 1})
where
(2p_{0}-{\bf 1})u(2p_{0}-{\bf 1})=u^{*}
, this is just a
restatement of Lemma
4.3
after noting that

\displaystyle({\bf 1}+u)=\bigl(2p_{1}-{\bf 1}+2p_{0}-{\bf 1})(2p_{1}-{\bf 1})=2(p_{1}+p_{0}-{\bf 1})(2p_{1}-{\bf 1}),

\displaystyle({\bf 1}-u)^{-1}=\bigl((2p_{1}-{\bf 1}-(2p_{0}-{\bf 1}))(2p_{1}-{\bf 1})\bigr)^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}(2p_{1}-{\bf 1})^{-1}(p_{1}-p_{0})^{-1}.

∎

Applying the results of

To better connect our indices on Hilbert

Proposition 4.18

Let

5

5

Because

We are particularly interested in the unitary

Theorem 4.21

anti-commuting with

\gamma_{0}\in\End_{A}(E)

We will prove this result in a few steps.

Lemma 4.22

Define the operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)=(H-{\rm i})(H+{\rm i})^{-1}
. Then

\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)
is a unitary operator in
\End_{A}(E)
such that

\gamma_{0}\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)\gamma_{0}=\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)^{*}

\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)^{\sim}
, the minimal unitisation of
\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)

Proof.

Because
H
is self-adjoint and regular, its Cayley transform
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)\in\End_{A}(E)
is unitary
[
22
, Theorem 10.5]
.
Using that
\gamma_{0}\cdot\Dom(H)\subset\Dom(H)
, we compute

\gamma_{0}\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{0}(H-{\rm i})\gamma_{0}\bigl(\gamma_{0}(H+{\rm i})\gamma_{0}\bigr)^{-1}=(H+{\rm i})(H-{\rm i})^{-1}=\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)^{*}.

If
(H+{\rm i})^{-1}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)

{\bf 1}-\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)=\bigl(H+{\rm i}-(H-{\rm i})\bigr)(H+{\rm i})^{-1}=2{\rm i}(H+{\rm i})^{-1}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E).

Remark 4.23
.

If
H
is bounded,
-1
is not in the spectrum of

. If

is not in the spectrum of

.

Lemma 4.24
.

A self-adjoint regular operator

if and only if

Proof.

Recalling Lemma

if and only if

\big\|{\bf 1}-\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)\big\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}<2

\big\|(H+{\rm i})^{-1}\big\|_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}(E)}<1

∎

Lemma 4.25
.

If

is Fredholm, then

is a chiral unitary of Fredholm type.

Proof.

By the definition of
\eta(H)

with

such that

∎

If the self-adjoint operator

(H+{\rm i})^{-1}\in\mathbb{K}_{A}(E)

Lemma 4.26
.

The unitaries

∎

Proof of Theorem
4.21
.

By Lemma
4.24
,
H
is Fredholm on
E_{A}
if and only if

\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)
is a chiral unitary of Fredholm type, which in turn implies that

\mathcal{C}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)\bigr)
is a self-adjoint Fredholm operator on

\overline{\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)-1\bigr)E}_{A}

anti-commuting with
\gamma_{0}
.
We first note that the domain of

is

\bigl({\bf 1}-\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)\bigr)E_{A}=2{\rm i}(H+{\rm i})^{-1}E_{A}=\Dom(H)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)\bigr)={\rm i}\bigl({\bf 1}+\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)\bigr)\bigl({\bf 1}-\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)\bigr)^{-1}={\rm i}(H+{\rm i}+(H-{\rm i}))(H+{\rm i}-(H-{\rm i}))^{-1}=H.

.
Finally, by Lemma

,

via

Therefore, by Proposition

[H]=\big[\mathcal{C}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(H)\bigr)\big]=\big[\mathcal{C}(U_{H})\big]

∎

Remark 4.27
.

Indices of the form

often appear in the study of higher index theory or systems with
a boundary. Theorem

shows that we can recast this
index problem in terms of homotopy equivalence classes of unitary operators. Depending on the situation
under study, it may be more tractable to work with unitary operators rather than (possibly unbounded) self-adjoint
operators. Hence our result may offer additional insight into the class

4.5
Some brief remarks on non-chiral unitaries and quantum walks

Split-step quantum walks represent a special subclass of more general quantum walks, unitary operators

on
\mathcal{H}
that have a decomposition into shift and coin operators.

6

6

See
[
16

However, index theoretic properties of generic quantum walk unitaries are seldom studied as

By expanding our domain of definition of quantum walks to include unitaries on Hilbert

Let us fix a

Our results in Sections

Definition 4.28
.

We say that a unitary

is of Fredholm type if

Proposition 4.29
.

Let

be a unitary of Fredholm type. Then the operator

Theorem 4.30
.

Let

Let us consider a simpler setting, where the unitary

unitisation of

The following result reconciles these two approaches.

Theorem 4.31

(
[

)

The map

K_{1}(A)\ni[u]\mapsto\big[\mathcal{C}(u)\big]\in KK^{1}(\mathbb{C},A)

Example 4.32

(quantum walks with weighted shifts)
.

Let us consider the following simple quantum walk unitary on

U_{m,n}=S_{m,n}C,\qquad S_{m,n}=\begin{pmatrix}S^{m}&0\\
0&(S^{*})^{n}\end{pmatrix},\qquad C\in\mathcal{U}\bigl(\mathbb{C}^{2}\bigr),\qquad m,n\in\mathbb{Z},

where

\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

with

\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

Hence, the isomorphism

.

5

In this section, we provide an extension of the winding number formula for split-step quantum walks by

[
12
]

We have previously defined a

Preliminaries and setting

Equation (

5.2
The index formula

To state our index formula, we now consider the setting where
B
has a faithful norm-lower semicontinuous
trace
\tau_{B}\colon B\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\tau({\bf 1})=1
and
\tau_{B}\bigl(\alpha(b)\bigr)=\tau_{B}(b)
for all
b\in B
.
If
B
is a subalgebra of
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and we are in the setting of regular quantum walks,
\tau_{B}
is the normalised
matrix trace.

Because
\tau_{B}
is invariant under the automorphism

\alpha\in\Aut(B)
, we can define the semifinite dual trace on the crossed product

\Tr_{\tau}\colon\ \Dom(\Tr_{\tau})\subset B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C},\qquad\Tr_{\tau}(S^{n}b)=\delta_{n,0}\tau_{B}(b).

Let us recall the noncommutative calculus and winding number.
The algebra
B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
possesses a derivation
\delta\colon\Dom(\delta)\to B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
, where

\Dom(\delta)
is dense,
S^{n}b\in\Dom(\delta)
and
\delta(S^{n}b)=nS^{n}b
. It can be easily checked that

\delta(a_{1}a_{2})=\delta(a_{1})a_{2}+a_{1}\delta(a_{2}),\qquad\delta(a_{1}^{*})=-\delta(a_{1})^{*},\qquad\Tr_{\tau}(\delta(a_{1}))=0

for
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
in the dense
\ast
-subalgebra
\mathcal{A}\subset B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
of elements
\sum_{n}S^{n}b_{n}
such that the
function
n\mapsto\|b_{n}\|_{B}
has superpolynomial decay in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark 5.3
.

The noncommutative calculus has been highly successful in characterising the topological phase of systems in condensed
matter physics, which often admit a description via crossed product
C^{*}
-algebras, see
[
29
]
for example.

For invertible elements in a dense subspace of
B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
, we define the noncommutative winding number

\operatorname{Wind}\colon\ \Dom(\operatorname{Wind})\subset GL(B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{C},\qquad\operatorname{Wind}(a)=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}\pi}\Tr_{\tau}\bigl(a^{-1}\delta(a)\bigr).

If
B
is a subalgebra of
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then

B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
is isomorphic to a subalgebra of
M_{n}(C(\mathbb{T}))
and
\operatorname{Wind}
is the usual
winding number of invertible matrix-valued functions of
\mathbb{T}
.

The noncommutative winding number of
B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
is closely connected to the Toeplitz extension

\displaystyle 0\to\mathcal{K}\otimes B\to\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}\to B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}\to 0,

(5.3)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
is generated by
B
and an isometry
\widehat{S}
such that

\widehat{S}b=\alpha(b)\widehat{S},\qquad\widehat{S}^{*}b=\alpha^{-1}(b)\widehat{S}^{*},\qquad\widehat{S}^{*}\widehat{S}={\bf 1},\qquad\widehat{S}\widehat{S}^{*}={\bf 1}-p,

with
p=p^{*}=p^{2}
a projection. The surjection
\sigma\colon\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}\to B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
is uniquely
determined by
\sigma\big(\widehat{S}b\big)=Sb
.

The short exact sequence in equation (
5.2
) and the Toeplitz extension of equation (
5.3
) give boundary maps in
K
-theory,
\partial_{L/R}
and

\partial_{{\rm Toep}}
, respectively. That is, there are homomorphisms

\partial_{L/R}\colon\ K_{\ast}(B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})^{\oplus 2}\to K_{\ast-1}(B),\qquad\partial_{{\rm Toep}}\colon\ K_{\ast}(B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})\to K_{\ast-1}(B).

The following result relates these two maps.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
[(w_{L},w_{R})]\in K_{\ast}(B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})^{\oplus 2}
, then

\partial_{L/R}\big[(w_{L},w_{R})\big]=\partial_{{\rm Toep}}[w_{L}]-\partial_{{\rm Toep}}[w_{R}],

where
-[v]
denotes the addition of
[v]^{-1}
.

Proof.

The fixed point
-\infty
of the translation action of
\mathbb{Z}
on
C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\},B)
gives a completely positive map

B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}\to C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}
that is equivalent to the
completely positive map
Sb\mapsto\widehat{S}b
from the Toeplitz extension. That is, the Toeplitz extension is equivalent to the short exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{K}\otimes B\to C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{{\rm ev}_{L}}B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}\to 0.

The limit
+\infty
reverses the orientation and so represents the inverse of the Toeplitz extension.
∎

Suppose that
u\in C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}
is a chiral unitary on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},B)
with respect to some

\gamma_{0}\in C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}
. If
u
is of Fredholm type, then it determines
a class
\big[\mathcal{C}(u)\big]\in KK(\mathbb{C},B)
. Because
B
possesses a unital trace
\tau_{B}
, we can also consider the numerical
index
(\tau_{B})_{\ast}\bigl([\mathcal{C}(u)]\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}
(cf. Section
3.4
). As the following result shows,
the noncommutative calculus on

can be used to give a concrete formula for this index.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\bullet\in\{L,R\}
and

be any element such that

is unitary and is contained in
\Dom(\operatorname{Wind})
.
Then

defines a class
[F]\in KK(\mathbb{C},B)
and

(\tau_{B})_{\ast}\bigl([F]\bigr)=\operatorname{Wind}(F_{R})-\operatorname{Wind}(F_{L})=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}\pi}(\Tr_{\tau}(F_{R}^{*}\delta(F_{R}))-\Tr_{\tau}(F_{L}^{*}\delta(F_{L}))).

Proof.

If
F
is such that
F_{L}
and
F_{R}
are unitary, then recalling that
\mathbb{K}_{B}\bigl(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},B)\bigr)\cong\mathcal{K}\otimes B
,
we have that
F\in C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}\subset\End_{B}\bigl(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},B)\bigr)

is unitary modulo compact operators. Hence, the triple

\biggl(\mathbb{C},\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},B)\oplus\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},B),\begin{pmatrix}0&F^{*}\\
F&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr)

is an even Kasparov module and gives a class
[F]\in KK(\mathbb{C},B)\cong K_{0}(B)
.
Because
F_{L}
and
F_{R}
are unitary elements in
B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
, we can consider the
class
[(F_{L},F_{R})]\in K_{1}(B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})^{\oplus 2}
.
Recalling the
index map in
K
-theory (see, for example,
[
5
, Section 8.3]
),
[F]
represents

\partial_{L/R}[(F_{L},F_{R})]\in K_{0}(B)\cong KK(\mathbb{C},B)

by construction.
Therefore,
(\tau_{B})_{\ast}([F])=\bigl((\tau_{B})_{\ast}\circ\partial_{L/R}\bigr)[(F_{L},F_{R})]

and we consider the composition

K_{1}(B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})^{\oplus 2}\xrightarrow{\partial_{L/R}}K_{0}(B)\xrightarrow{(\tau_{B})_{\ast}}\mathbb{C}.

Using Lemma
5.4
, we can write this map as

(\tau_{B})_{\ast}\bigl(\partial_{{\rm Toep}}([F_{L}])\bigr)-(\tau_{B})_{\ast}\bigl(\partial_{{\rm Toep}}([F_{R}])\bigr).

Now, using
[
8
, Proposition 3.3]
, the boundary map
\partial_{{\rm Toep}}
can be represented as
the Kasparov product with the unbounded Kasparov module
\big[\hat{X}\big]\in KK^{1}(B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z},B)
constructed in
[
8
, Section 3]
.
So we can equivalently consider the product

K_{1}(B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})\times KK^{1}(B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z},B)\to K_{0}(B)\xrightarrow{(\tau_{B})_{\ast}}\mathbb{C}.

(5.4)

If
F_{L},F_{R}\in\Dom(\operatorname{Wind})
,
we can use the index formula
[
10
, Theorem 6]
, which says that (
5.4
) applied
to a unitary
F_{\bullet}\in\Dom(\operatorname{Wind})
is given by
-\operatorname{Wind}(F_{\bullet})
.
The result now follows.
∎

Remarks 5.6
.

(1)

Given the setting of Theorem
5.5
, if in addition the operator

F\in C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}

has closed range, then
\Ker(F)
and
\Ker(F^{*})
are finitely generated and projective modules over the unital
C^{*}
-algebra
B
.
In such a setting, the map
KK(\mathbb{C},B)\xrightarrow{(\tau_{B})_{*}}\mathbb{C}
can be explicitly characterised and our index formula can be
written as

\tau_{B}\bigl(\Ker(F)\bigr)-\tau_{B}\bigl(\Ker(F^{*})\bigr)=\operatorname{Wind}(F_{R})-\operatorname{Wind}(F_{L}).

(2)

There is a dense
\ast
-subalgebra
\mathcal{A}\subset B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
that is Fréchet, stable under the
holomorphic functional calculus and
GL(\mathcal{A})\subset\Dom(\operatorname{Wind})
. The noncommutative
winding number is a homotopy invariant and gives a well-defined map
K_{1}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathbb{C}
.
Because
K_{1}(\mathcal{A})\cong K_{1}(B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})
,
we can define
\operatorname{Wind}(v)
for any invertible
v\in B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
via

\operatorname{Wind}(v)=\operatorname{Wind}(\widetilde{v})
, where
[v]=[\widetilde{v}]\in K_{1}(B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})

and
\widetilde{v}\in\mathcal{A}
. Hence we can remove the assumption that

F_{L},F_{R}\in\Dom(\operatorname{Wind})
from Theorem
5.5
, though the existence
of elements
\widetilde{F}_{L},\widetilde{F}_{R}\in\mathcal{A}
is non-constructive in general.

(3)

Given Lemma
5.4
, Theorem
5.5

is not so surprising to those familiar with the Noether–Toeplitz index theorem. However, the use of double-sided limits
is an interesting variation, something that also appears in the work of Matsuzawa
[
24
]
. We expect further
variants and generalisations of such an index formula to hold that may also be of relevance for topological properties of chiral
unitaries and split-step quantum walks. We leave this question to another place.

Let us now examine the hypotheses of Theorem
5.5
for the case of a
chiral unitary
u\in C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}
with respect to

\gamma_{0}\in C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}
and acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},B)
.
If
\|{\bf 1}-(u_{L},u_{R})\|_{B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}}<2
, then
u
is of Fredholm type and
we obtain a Fredholm operator
\chi(\mathcal{C}(u))
with
\chi
a normalising function for

\mathcal{C}(u)
. Recalling that
({\rm i}+\mathcal{C}(u))^{-1}=-\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2}({\bf 1}-u)\in C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}
,
we also have that
\chi(\mathcal{C}(u))\in C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}
.

Because
\gamma_{0}\in C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}
, so is

p_{0}=\tfrac{1}{2}({\bf 1}+\gamma_{0})
and
{\bf 1}-p_{0}
. In particular the operator

F=({\bf 1}-p_{0})\chi(\mathcal{C}(u))p_{0}\in C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}

is such that
F_{L},F_{R}\in B\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
is unitary.
If
F_{L}
and
F_{R}
are contained in
\Dom(\operatorname{Wind})
, then we can say that

(\tau_{B})_{\ast}\bigl([\mathcal{C}(u)]\bigr)=\operatorname{Wind}(F_{R})-\operatorname{Wind}(F_{L}).

The more difficult question is whether
F_{L}
and
F_{R}
are contained in
\Dom(\operatorname{Wind})
.
Certainly if
F
is of the form

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\widetilde{S}^{n}f_{n},\qquad f_{n}\in C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\},B),

(5.5)

where
f_{n}
is such that the function
n\mapsto\|f_{n}(\pm\infty)\|_{B}
has superpolynomial decay in
n
,
then
F_{L}
and
F_{R}
will be in
\Dom(\operatorname{Wind})
.
However, a concrete expression for
F
is challenging in general.

Let us consider the case that
{\bf 1}-u\in C_{0}(\mathbb{Z},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}
, i.e.,

u
is in the minimal unitisation
\mathbb{K}_{B}\bigl(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},B)\bigr)^{\sim}
.
Then
\mathcal{C}(u)
has compact resolvent we can use the normalising function

\chi(x)=x\bigl(1+x^{2}\bigr)^{-1/2}
. Recalling (
4.1
) on p.
4.1
,

\displaystyle F=({\bf 1}-p_{0})F_{\mathcal{C}(u)}p_{0}=\frac{{\rm i}}{2}({\bf 1}-p_{0})({\bf 1}+u)Vp_{0},

where
V
is the extension of the operator on
(u-{\bf 1})\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},B)
,

V(u-{\bf 1})e=|u-{\bf 1}|e
for
e\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},B)
. Supposing that
u
and

\gamma_{0}
are of the form described in (
5.5
), then
the only obstruction is the operator
V
. Writing

V=|{\bf 1}-u|({\bf 1}-u)^{-1}=(2-u-u^{*})^{1/2}({\bf 1}-u)^{-1},

we have that
V\in C(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\},B)\rtimes_{\widetilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{Z}
,
but a condition on
u
that implies sufficient regularity of
V_{L}
and
V_{R}
with respect
to
\delta
and
\Tr_{\tau}
is difficult to state
in general. We leave a more comprehensive analysis of this question to future work.
