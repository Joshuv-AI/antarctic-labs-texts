Optimal Control of McKean-Vlasov equations with controlled stochasticity
Luca Di Persio, Peter Kuchling

Abstract
In this article, we analyse the existence of an optimal feedback controller of stochastic optimal control problems governed by SDEs which have the control in the diffusion part. To this end, we consider the underlying Fokker-Planck equation to transform the stochastic optimal control problem into a deterministic problem with open-loop controller.



1
Introduction

The present paper aims at considering the optimal control problem

\text{minimize }\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}g(X(t))+h(u(t,X(t)))dt\Big]+\mathbb{E}g_{0}(X(T))

(1.1)

for some functions
g,h
and
g_{0}
, subject to either

\begin{split}dX(t)&=f(X(t))dt+\sqrt{u(t,X(t))}\sigma(X(t))dW(t)\\
X(0)&=x_{0}\end{split}

(1.2)

where
\sigma=((2a_{ij})_{i,j}^{d})^{\frac{1}{2}}
, or by the McKean-Vlasov SDEs

\begin{split}dX(t)&=b\Bigg(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{X(t)}}{dx}(X(t))\Bigg)D(X(t))dt+\Bigg(\frac{2u(t,X(t))\beta\big(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{X(t)}}{dx}(X(t))\big)}{\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{X(t)}}{dx}(X(t))}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}dW(t)\\
X(0)&=X_{0}.\end{split}

(1.3)

In both situations, the controller
u
appears in the stochasticity part and is taken from a set

\mathcal{U}=\{u\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon\gamma_{1}\leq u\leq\gamma_{2},\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}D_{ij}^{2}(a_{ij}(x)u(t,x))\leq\gamma_{3}\text{ a.e. }(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\}.

where
(a_{ij})
is an elliptic matrix for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the case (
1.2
), while it is represented by
a_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
for (
1.3
). The assumptions on all the other coefficients will be later specified.
Functions
u\in\mathcal{U}
are also called quasi-concave.

Let us recall that the above equations have been analysed in various contexts related to mean-field games
[
25
,
31
]
as well as statistical Physics as in
[
21
,
24
]
. In general, the McKean-Vlasov equations have been introduced by the seminal work of McKean
[
28
]
. The specific form of marginal dependence of the coefficients is also known as
Nemytskii-type
, which was recently studied in works such as
[
12
,
23
]
.

The specific shape of the volatility term in (
1.3
) gives rise to the Nemytskii-type or singular McKean-Vlasov equation. Such equations appear as microscopic descriptions of various physical processes, such as the Bose-Einstein statistic (with
\beta(r)=a\log(1+r)
). Solutions of such equations were recently studied in
[
14
]
and the references therein, as well as
[
7
,
10
,
11
]
in the context of the corresponding SDE and
[
8
,
9
,
33
]
.

Let us put our optimal control approach into context. Other works, e.g.,
[
5
,
29
,
17
,
15
,
26
,
30
]
, rely on the classical approach, i.e., using the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation, see also
[
19
,
20
]
. This approach poses certain technical issues. Indeed, in these cases, the solution of the HJB equation turns out to be only a viscosity solution, which is not differentiable and hence makes it difficult to analyse the dynamic programming equation to obtain the optimal controller. Furthermore, the dynamic programming approach needs the technical notion of differentiability concerning measures using the Wasserstein distance.

Recently, a different path has been considered, mainly based on transferring the stochastic optimal control problem with feedback inputs to a deterministic problem, which yields an open-loop controller serving as the solution to Problem (
1.1
). Some successful attempts have been made by employing the corresponding Kolmogorov (backward) equation in
[
2
,
6
,
13
]
. Also, in
[
22
]
, a direct approach using BSDEs via the Kolmogorov approach yields the existence of an optimal controller in the drift term. However, the existence of an optimal controller follows if the optimal value function, which is the solution of the corresponding dynamic equation, is sufficiently smooth.

An alternative solution is proposed in this work.
In particular, we consider the Fokker-Planck equation (or forward Kolmogorov equation) associated with the above SDEs. So, we rewrite the problem as an optimal control problem governed by a deterministic parabolic equation (the Fokker-Planck equation). This approach has been used in
[
1
]
in case the controller appears in the drift of the SDE. It has also been mentioned in
[
26
]
as a possible alternative to the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman approach.

Since we wish to model a controller in the diffusive term, the approach used in
[
1
]
must be slightly modified. Indeed, since it is generally to be avoided to impose too strong regularity assumptions on the controller, we consider the Fokker-Planck equations corresponding to (
1.2
) or (
1.3
) in a variational setting using the triplet
L^{2}\subset H^{-1}\subset(L^{2})^{\ast}
. In both cases, the first goal is to show the existence of a solution
\rho^{u}
to the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation for any controller
u\in\mathcal{U}
. This is done in Proposition
2.2
and Theorem
3.2
, respectively. Next, we show that the sequence of controllers minimising the cost functional
I
has a limit
u^{\ast}\in\mathcal{U}
corresponding to its solution
\rho^{u^{\ast}}
, our optimal controller. This is proven in Theorem
2.5
and Theorem
3.3
, respectively. A question that will be answered in future work is the representation of the controller via the maximum principle.

While using
H^{-1}
as pivot space seems counterintuitive initially, this space appears naturally in studying the regularity of solutions whose initial conditions have finite first and second moments. As explained in
[
14
]
, solutions with
\rho_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}
are differentiable in
H^{-1}
, while such a statement fails concerning the semigroup approach on
L^{1}
.

The rest of the article is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we show the existence of an optimal controller for the linear problem. Section
3
is devoted to the analogous results in the case of a nonlinear McKean-Vlasov equation. For the sake of completeness, we included, within the Appendix section, some classical results regarding the existence of solutions to our PDEs due to Lions.

Notation

We denote by
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the Banach spaces of
p
-Lebesgue integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Furthermore,
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon\nabla u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
, and
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
its dual. Denote the corresponding dual spaces by
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
etc.. Similarly, we write
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon\nabla u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
.

By
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we denote all infinitely differentiable functions with compact support. The symbols
C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
represent the space of continuous functions and the space of bounded continuous functions, respectively. By
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we denote the space of all continuously differentiable functions. Partial derivatives in the
i
-th variable are denoted by
D_{i}
.

For a real Banach space
\mathcal{X}
and
0<T<\infty
, we denote by
L^{p}(0,T;\mathcal{X})
the space of Bochner
p
-integrable functions
u\colon(0,T)\to\mathcal{X}
and by
C([0,T];\mathcal{X})
the space of
\mathcal{X}
-valued continuous functions on
[0,T]
.

2
Stochastic Optimal Control: The SDEs (
1.2
)

2.1
Setup

We consider the following optimal control problem:

\text{minimize }\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}g(X(t))+h(u(t,X(t)))dt\Big]+\mathbb{E}g_{0}(X(T))

(
1.1
)

subject to SDEs

\displaystyle dX

\displaystyle=f(X)dt+\sqrt{u}\sigma(X)dW

\displaystyle X(0)

\displaystyle=x_{0}

and
u=u(t,x)\in\mathcal{U}
, where
\mathcal{U}\subset L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a closed convex set to be made precise below. Furthermore, we assume that
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
while
h\geq 0
is convex and continuous. We assume also that

g\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),g\geq 0\text{ and }g_{0}\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),g_{0}\geq 0.

(2.1)

If we set
\rho:=\rho(t,x)
to be the density of
X
, i.e. for
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\mathbb{P}(X(t)\in A)=\int_{A}\rho(t,x)dx,\ \forall\ t\geq 0,

then
\rho
solves (in the sense of distributions) the Fokker-Planck equation (see
[
8
,
9
,
33
]
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho(t,x)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}D_{ij}^{2}\left[u(t,x)a_{ij}(x)\rho(t,x)\right]+\div(f(x)\rho(t,x))=0,\ (t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x),\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\end{cases}

(2.2)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
a_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sigma_{ik}\sigma_{kj}
. Note that in particular, we consider the situation where
\rho(t)\geq 0
and
\rho(t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
t
, as the function
\rho
models a probability distribution. While this assumption is superfluous for the existence of a solution to (
2.2
), we need to assume
\rho_{0}\geq 0
and
\rho_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for the existence of an optimal controller. By the results from e.g.
[
8
]
, we then obtain
\rho(t)\geq 0
and
\rho(t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
t
.

Assume that
a_{ij}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and there exists
\gamma_{0}>0
such that

\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\geq\gamma_{0}|\xi|^{2},\ \forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.3)

By taking
\gamma_{0}>0
, we assume here that the volatility is non-degenerate. In other words, the covariance of the Brownian term is positive definite. It should be possible to generalise the equation by approximation to degenerate coefficients, i.e., a positive semidefinite correlation. This will be the subject of future work.

Denote the solution to (
2.2
) by
\rho^{u}
. Then, we may rewrite the optimal control problem as the following minimisation problem:

\text{minimize }I(u)=\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[g(x)+h(u(s,x))]\rho^{u}(s,x)dsdx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g_{0}(x)\rho^{u}(T,x)dx\right]

(2.4)

subject to (
2.2
) and
u\in\mathcal{U}
, where

\mathcal{U}=\{u\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon\gamma_{1}\leq u\leq\gamma_{2},\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}D_{ij}^{2}(a_{ij}u(t,x))\leq\gamma_{3}\}

and
0<\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}<\infty
.

Remark 2.1
.

In Problem (
1.1
),
u=u(t,X(t))
is a Markov stochastic feedback control, while in (
2.4
) it is an open distributed controller.

The difference to the optimal control problem of
[
1
]
consists in the fact that while the author applied the control function to the deterministic part, our control problem considers the control function on the stochastic part. This way, the control appears in the second-order derivative instead of the divergence.

2.2
Well-posedness of the state system (
2.2
)

In view of Theorem
A.1
, we consider (
2.2
) in the variational setting w.r.t. the following spaces:

\displaystyle V=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),H=H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Thus, we have
V^{\ast}=(L^{2})^{\ast}=(I+A_{0})V
, where the dual is taken with respect to the
H^{-1}
-duality functional

{{}_{V^{\ast}}}\langle u,v\rangle_{V}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[(I+A_{0})^{-1}u]vdx=((I+A_{0})^{-1}u,v)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=(u,v)_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(2.5)

for
u\in V^{\ast},v\in V
, where
A_{0}
is the operator

A_{0}\rho=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}D_{i}(a_{ij}D_{j}\rho)\ \forall\rho\in V

considered in the sense of distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We note that by (
2.3
), it follows that
(I+A_{0})
is an isomorphism of
H^{1}
onto
H^{-1}
and thus, expression (
2.5
) is also used to define a scalar product on
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
We have

V=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset H=H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset V^{\ast}=(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{\ast},

with dense and continuous embeddings. The operator
A(t)\colon V\to V^{\ast}
is defined for
\rho\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as

A(t)\rho=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}D_{ij}^{2}(u(t,x)a_{ij}(x)\rho)+\div(f(x)\rho)\text{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Therefore, the bilinear form in question is defined as follows:

\displaystyle a(t;\rho,\psi)

\displaystyle={{}_{V^{\ast}}}\langle A\rho,\psi\rangle_{V}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}D_{ij}^{2}(ua_{ij}\rho)\psi dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\div f\rho\cdot\psi dx,\ \rho,\psi\in V.

We have:

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume that there exist constants
0<\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}
and
\gamma_{0}>0
such that
\gamma_{1}\leq u\leq\gamma_{2}
and (
2.3
). Furthermore, assume that
a_{ij}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, that is,

\overline{a}:=\max\{\max_{i,j}\|D_{i}a_{ij}\|_{\infty},\|a\|_{\infty}\}<\infty.

Let
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\rho_{0}\in H=H^{-1}
. Then there exists a unique
H^{-1}
-weak solution
\rho
of (
2.2
), that is,

\rho\in L^{2}(0,T;V)\cap C([0,T];H)\text{ and }\frac{d}{dt}\rho\in L^{2}(0,T;V^{\ast}).

Taking into account Theorem
A.1
, we must check the following two conditions:

•

|a(t,\rho,\varphi)|\leq M\|\rho\|_{V}\|\varphi\|_{V}

•

a(t,\rho,\rho)\geq\alpha\|\rho\|_{V}^{2}-C\|\rho\|_{H}^{2}

for
\rho,\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Since
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset V
is dense, there exists a unique continuous extension of
a(t;\cdot,\cdot)\colon\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
which is given by our operator. The next subsections are devoted to each of the estimates.

2.2.1
Upper bound

This section is devoted to Condition 2 of Theorem
A.1
. By density, it suffices to show the upper bound for
\rho,\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. To this end, we need to rewrite the differential operator in a more suitable form.

Lemma 2.3
.

For all test functions
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}D_{ij}^{2}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi=-\psi+(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi.

Proof.

We have by the product rule (denote
D_{ij}^{2}=D_{i}D_{j}=D_{j}D_{i}
)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}D_{ij}^{2}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}D_{ij}^{2}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi+\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}D_{i}[a_{ij}D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi]-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi

\displaystyle=-A_{0}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi

\displaystyle=-(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi-A_{0}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi+(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi

\displaystyle=-\psi+(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi

∎

Note that with this approach, we circumvent the regularity assumption on
u
, as the lemma can easily see.

Let now
\rho,\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset V
. Using Lemma
2.3
, we may proceed in the estimation required for Theorem
A.1
:

\displaystyle a(t;\rho,\psi)

\displaystyle={{}_{V^{\ast}}}\langle A\rho,\psi\rangle_{V}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}D_{ij}^{2}(ua_{ij}\rho)\psi dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\div(f\rho)\psi dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}D_{ij}^{2}(ua_{ij}\rho)(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\div(f\rho)\psi dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\rho a_{ij}D_{ij}^{2}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\div(f\rho)\psi dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\rho\psi dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\rho(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi dx

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\rho\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\div(f\rho)\psi dx

We may estimate the four terms separately (Recall that
V=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
). Note that

•

Differential operators
D_{i}\colon H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are continuous with
\|D_{i}\|\leq 1
.

•

The mapping
(I+A_{0})^{-1}\colon(L^{2})^{\ast}\to L^{2}
is continuous. Denote its operator norm by
\|(I+A_{0})^{-1}\|=\alpha
.

•

Also,
(I+A_{0})^{-1}\colon H^{-1}\to H^{1}
, i.e. for elements from
H^{-1}
, we have higher regularity.

Using these facts, we obtain

1.

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\rho\psi dx\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\rho\psi|dx\leq\gamma_{2}\|\rho\|_{V}\|\psi\|_{V}

2.

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\rho(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi dx\Big|\leq\gamma_{2}\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\rho\psi|dx\leq\gamma_{2}\alpha\|\rho\|_{V}\|\psi\|_{V}

3.

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\rho\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi dx\Big|

\displaystyle\leq d\overline{a}\gamma_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\rho\sum_{j=1}^{d}D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi|dx

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}\overline{a}\gamma_{2}\alpha\|\rho\|_{V}\|\psi\|_{V}

4.

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\div(f\rho)\psi dx\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f\rho\nabla(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi|dx

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\rho\sum_{j=1}^{d}D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\psi|dx

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\alpha d\|\rho\|_{V}\|\psi\|_{V}

In total, we have

|a(t,\rho,\psi)|\leq\Big(\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}+\frac{\gamma_{2}\alpha}{2}+\frac{d^{2}\overline{a}\gamma_{2}\alpha}{2}+\|f\|_{\infty}d\alpha\Big)\|\rho\|_{V}\|\psi\|_{V}

which is the desired upper bound. Using that
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset V
is dense, we obtain the upper bound for all
\rho,\varphi\in V
.

2.2.2
Lower bound

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Again using Lemma
2.3
, we obtain the following:

\displaystyle a(t;\rho,\rho)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}D_{ij}^{2}(ua_{ij}\rho)\rho dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\div(f\rho)\rho dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}ua_{ij}\rho D_{ij}^{2}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\rho dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\div(f\rho)\rho dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\rho^{2}dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\rho(I+A_{0})^{-1}\rho dx

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\rho(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\rho dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\div(f\rho)\rho dx

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\|\rho\|_{V}^{2}-\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}\|\rho\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\rho(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\rho dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\div(f\rho)\rho dx}_{(\ast)}

For the remaining term
(\ast)
, we proceed in a similar fashion as
[
1
]
. Consider the two terms separately:

1.

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\rho(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\rho dx\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u\rho(D_{i}a_{ij})D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\rho|dx

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{2}\overline{a}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\rho D_{j}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\rho|dx

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{2}\overline{a}\|\rho\|_{L^{2}}\|(I+A_{0})^{-1}\rho\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{2}\overline{a}\alpha\|\rho\|_{V}\|\rho\|_{H}

2.

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I+A_{0})^{-1}\div(f\rho)\rho dx\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\|(I+A_{0})^{-1}\div(f\rho)\|_{H^{1}}\|\rho\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|f\rho\|_{L^{2}}\|\rho\|_{H^{-1}}\leq\alpha\|f\|_{\infty}\|\rho\|_{L^{2}}\|\rho\|_{H^{-1}}

Hence, in total, we have

|(\ast)|\leq\underbrace{\Big(\frac{d^{2}\gamma_{2}\overline{a}\alpha}{2}+\alpha\|f\|_{\infty}\Big)}_{=:C_{2}}\|\rho\|_{V}\|\rho\|_{H}\leq\frac{C_{2}\beta}{2}\|\rho\|_{V}^{2}+\frac{C_{2}}{2\beta}\|\rho\|_{H}^{2}

where the last inequality is an application of Young’s inequality, which is valid for any
\beta>0
. Turning this inequality around, we obtain

(\ast)\geq-\frac{C_{2}\beta}{2}\|\rho\|_{V}^{2}-\frac{C_{2}}{2\beta}\|\rho\|_{H}^{2}

Putting everything together, we are left with

a(t;\rho,\rho)\geq\frac{\gamma_{1}-C_{2}\beta}{2}\|\rho\|_{V}^{2}-\Big(\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}+\frac{C_{2}}{2\beta}\Big)\|\rho\|_{H}^{2}.

Choosing
\beta
small enough such that
\gamma_{1}-C_{2}\beta>0
, we obtain the desired estimate for Theorem
A.1
for
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Using the density of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset V
, the bound also holds for all
\rho\in V
.

Remark 2.4
.

The existence result of Proposition
2.2
can be shown without the additional requirement on the second derivatives of
u
.

2.3
Existence of an optimal controller

The goal is to show the existence of a minimizer for Problem (
2.4
), i.e.,

3
Optimal Control of McKean-Vlasov-equation: The nonlinear diffusion case

The goal of this section is to prove the existence of an optimal controller for the McKean-Vlasov-Equation, i.e. solve the minimising problem

\text{minimise }\mathbb{E}(J(X,u))=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}g(X)+h(u(X))dt+g_{0}(X(T))\Big]

subject to

u\in\mathcal{U}:=\{u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon 0<\gamma_{1}\leq u\leq\gamma_{2},\Delta u(x)\leq\gamma_{3}\text{ a.e. }(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

(3.1)

and

\begin{split}dX(t)&=b\Bigg(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{X(t)}}{dx}(X(t))\Bigg)D(X(t))dt+\Bigg(\frac{2u(t,X(t))\beta\big(\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{X(t)}}{dx}(X(t))\big)}{\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{X(t)}}{dx}(X(t))}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}dW(t)\\
X(0)&=X_{0}\end{split}

(
1.3
)

where
\mathcal{L}_{X(t)}
is the law of
X(t)
and
\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{X(t)}}{dx}
is its density. To stay in the spirit of Section
2.3
, we focus here on a solution in
H^{-1}
.

The McKean-Vlasov equation (
1.3
) is equivalent to the Fokker-Planck equation

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\rho&-\Delta(u\beta(\rho))+\div(D(x)b(\rho)\rho)=0\text{ in }(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
\rho(0)&=\rho_{0}\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\end{split}

(3.2)

where
\rho_{0}dx=\mathcal{L}_{X_{0}}
. As explained in
[
8
]
, by using
[
33
, Theorem 2.5]
, the existence of a solution to (
3.2
) yields a probability measure on the canonical space
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
solving the corresponding martingale problem. This implies the existence of a weak solution to (
1.3
) using e.g.
[
32
, Theorem 4.5.2]
. The other direction from (
1.3
) to (
3.2
) can be seen using Itô’s formula.

Hence, the optimal control problem reduces to

\text{minimize }I(u)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(g(x)+h(u(x)))\rho(t,x)dtdx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g_{0}(x)\rho(T,x)dx

(3.3)

subject to (
3.1
) and (
3.2
).

While it is desirable from a modelling perspective to have solutions in
L^{1}
, the appearance of the controller within the diffusion term forces us to rely on
H^{-1}
-methods for the variational approach in the construction of a solution and controller.

To show the existence of an optimal controller, we proceed as in Section
2.3
. Namely, we use a nonlinear version of Lions’ theorem to show the existence of an
H^{-1}
-solution to (
3.2
) for any
u\in\mathcal{U}
. Then we consider an approximating sequence
\{u_{s}\}_{s\in\mathbb{N}}
and show that its limit
u^{\ast}
attains the infimum of the cost functional.

Let us now formulate the standing assumptions for this section.

Assumption 1
.

We assume the following conditions on the coefficients:

1.

\beta\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
0<\alpha_{0}\leq\beta^{\prime}(r)
for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
\beta(0)=0
.

2.

b\in C(\mathbb{R}),|b|_{\infty}<\infty
,
D\in L^{\infty}
.

3.

The function
\beta
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
|\beta|_{\mathrm{Lip}}
.

4.

There exists
\alpha_{2}\geq 0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

|b(x)x-b(y)y|\leq\alpha_{2}|\beta(x)-\beta(y)|.

Remark 3.1
.

Actually, the condition
|b|_{\infty}<\infty
is redundant: If everything else is assumed, for any
x\neq 0
,

\displaystyle|b(x)|

\displaystyle=\frac{|b(x)x|}{|x|}=\frac{|b(x)x-b(0)0|}{|x|}\leq\alpha_{2}\frac{|\beta(x)-\beta(0)|}{|x|}\leq\alpha_{2}|\beta|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\frac{|x|}{|x|}=\alpha_{2}|\beta|_{\mathrm{Lip}}.

By continuity, this bound also holds for
x=0
and we get
|b|_{\infty}\leq\alpha_{2}|\beta|_{\mathrm{Lip}}
.

Furthermore, it follows directly that the function
y\mapsto b(y)y
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
\alpha_{2}|\beta|_{\mathrm{Lip}}
.

3.1
Well-posedness of the state system (
3.2
)

To show existence of an
H^{-1}
-solution to (
3.2
), we wish to apply Theorem
A.2
, again in the case of
V=L^{2},H=H^{-1},V^{\ast}=(L^{2})^{\ast}
. We consider the nonlinear operator
A\colon V\to V^{\ast}
, defined by

{{}_{V^{\ast}}}(Ay,z)_{V}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I-\Delta)^{-1}(-\Delta(u\beta(y)))z\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(I-\Delta)^{-1}\div(D(b(y)y))z\,dx

for
y,z\in V
. This leads to the following existence result:

Theorem 3.2
.

Under Assumption
1
For any
\rho_{0}\in H
, there exists a solution
\rho\colon[0,T]\to V^{\ast}
to (
3.2
) satisfying

\displaystyle\rho\in C([0,T];H^{-1})\cap L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}),\ \frac{d\rho}{dt}\in L^{2}(0,T;(L^{2})^{\ast})

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}(t)+Ay(t)=0\text{ a.e. }t\in(0,T),\ \rho(0)=\rho_{0}.

Proof.

We show that the conditions of Theorem
A.2
for
p=2
are fulfilled for the operator

A(y)=-\Delta(u\beta(y))+\div(Db(y)y),\ y\in V.

We start by showing that
A
is demicontinuous, i.e., for any sequence
\{y_{n}\}\subset V=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with
y_{n}\to y
in
V
and any
z\in V
, we show that

{{}_{V^{\ast}}}(A(y_{n})-A(y),z)_{V}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

Hence, let
z\in V
. Integration by parts and Cauchy-Schwarz yields

\displaystyle|{{}_{V^{\ast}}}(Ay_{n}-Ay,z)_{V}|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\int(I-\Delta)^{-1}z(-\Delta(u\beta(y_{n})-u\beta(y)))dx\Big|

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt+\Big|\int(I-\Delta)^{-1}z\div(Db(y_{n})y_{n}-Db(y)y)dx\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int|\Delta(I-\Delta)^{-1}z||u(\beta(y_{n})-\beta(y))|dx

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt+\int|\nabla(I-\Delta)^{-1}zD(x)(b(y_{n})y_{n}-b(y)y)|dx

\displaystyle\leq\|\Delta(I-\Delta)^{-1}z\|_{L^{2}}\gamma_{2}|\beta|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\|y_{n}-y\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt+|D|_{\infty}\|\nabla(I-\Delta)^{-1}z\|_{L^{2}}\|b(y_{n})y_{n}-b(y)y\|_{L^{2}}

Since
y\mapsto b(y)y
is Lipschitz continuous, both terms converge to zero due to the
L^{2}
-convergence of
\{y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Hence, the demicontinuity is shown.

Let us underline that, for the demicontinuity, the Lipschitz condition on
y\mapsto b(y)y
is unnecessary. We wish to apply the continuous mapping theorem for the second term, which only holds on finite measure spaces. Note that we have

by assumption, implying that

locally in measure; hence, by the continuous mapping theorem, it holds that

locally in measure, which also means that

locally in measure. Since

, we obtain by Pratt’s theorem (see e.g.

b(y_{n})y\to b(y)y\text{ in }L^{2}.

We need to show that there exists some

such that

{{}_{V^{\ast}}}(A(y_{1})-A(y_{2}),y_{1}-y_{2})_{V}\geq-\gamma|y_{1}-y_{2}|_{H}^{2}

for all

. First of all, we have

\displaystyle{{}_{V^{\ast}}}(A(y_{1})-A(y_{2}),y_{1}-y_{2})_{V}

\displaystyle=\int(I-\Delta)^{-1}(A(y_{1})-A(y_{2}))(y_{1}-y_{2})dx

\displaystyle=\int(I-\Delta)^{-1}(-\Delta(u\beta(y_{1}))+\Delta(u\beta(y_{2}))(y_{1}-y_{2})dx

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+\int(I-\Delta)^{-1}[\div(Db(y_{1})y_{1}-\div(Db(y_{2})y_{2}](y_{1}-y_{2})dx

\displaystyle=-\int(I-\Delta)^{-1}\Delta[u\beta(y_{1})-u\beta(y_{2})](y_{1}-y_{2})dx

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+\int(I-\Delta)^{-1}[\div(Db(y_{1})y_{1})-\div(Db(y_{2})y_{2})](y_{1}-y_{2})dx

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int u(\beta(y_{1})-\beta(y_{2}))(y_{1}-y_{2})dx}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt-\int(I-\Delta)^{-1}(u\beta(y_{1})-u\beta(y_{2}))(y_{1}-y_{2})dx

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+\int(I-\Delta)^{-1}[\div(Db(y_{1})y_{1})-\div(Db(y_{2})y_{2})](y_{1}-y_{2})dx

Since the first term is

, we only need to estimate the other two. For the second term, We have

\displaystyle-\int

\displaystyle(I-\Delta)^{-1}((u\beta(y_{1})-u\beta(y_{2}))(y_{1}-y_{2})dx

\displaystyle=-\int(u\beta(y_{1})-u\beta(y_{2}))(I-\Delta)^{-1}(y_{1}-y_{2})dx

\displaystyle\geq-\gamma_{2}\int(\beta(y_{1})-\beta(y_{2}))(I-\Delta)^{-1}(y_{1}-y_{2})dx

\displaystyle\geq-\gamma_{2}|\beta|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\int(y_{1}-y_{2})(I-\Delta)^{-1}(y_{1}-y_{2})dx

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{2}|\beta|_{\mathrm{Lip}}|y_{1}-y_{2}|_{H^{-1}}^{2}.

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle(I-\Delta)^{-1}\div[(Db(y_{1})y_{1})-(Db(y_{2})y_{2})](y_{1}-y_{2})dx

In total, we have
