Homotopic classification of band structures: Stable, fragile, delicate, and stable representation-protected topology
Piet W. Brouwer, Vatsal Dwivedi

Abstract
The topological classification of gapped band structures depends on the particular definition of topological equivalence. For translation-invariant systems, stable equivalence is defined by a lack of restrictions on the numbers of occupied and unoccupied bands, while imposing restrictions on one or both leads to “fragile” and “delicate” topology, respectively. In this article, we describe a homotopic classification of band structures — which captures the topology beyond the stable equivalence — in the presence of additional lattice symmetries. As examples, we present complete homotopic classifications for spinless band structures with twofold rotation, fourfold rotation and fourfold dihedral symmetries, both in presence and absence of time-reversal symmetry. Whereas the rules of delicate and fragile topology do not admit a bulk-boundary correspondence, we identify a version of stable topology, which restricts the representations of bands, but not their numbers, which does allow for anomalous states at symmetry-preserving boundaries, which are associated with nontrivial bulk topology.

Introduction

The concept of topological equivalence, combined with symmetry constraints, has been an important tool to classify gapped band structures of non-interacting fermions

;
Qi and Zhang 2011
;
Bernevig and Hughes 2013
;

Topological gapped band structures include the quantized Hall effect, the quantum spin-Hall effect, topological insulators, and, if crystalline symmetries are imposed, topological crystalline insulators. A nontrivial topology of the band structure implies the existence of anomalous, gapless states on the sample boundary and vice versa, provided the boundary respects the symmetry constraints that are imposed on the bulk.

Various notions of topological equivalence of band structures have been employed in the literature, which differ in the possible transformations that are allowed to deform two topologically equivalent Hamiltonians into each other. The standard definition in condensed matter physics has been the “stable equivalence”
Kitaev 2009

This definition ensures that the topology realized in mathematical models, often consisting of only a few bands, is realized in physical systems consisting of atoms with infinitely many orbitals. II
Classification strategy

In this section, we describe the homoptopic classification for band structures with arbitrary lattice symmetries, which is based on well-established methods of algebraic topology for CW complexes. Our classification procedure is applicable to all ten Altland-Zirnbuaer classes
Altland and Zirnbauer 1997
; however, to avoid overburdenening the present exposition, we restrict the explicit discussion to Bloch Hamiltonians without antisymmetry requirements, corresponding to the Altland-Zirnbauer classes A, AI, and AII. These classes include all topological crystalline insulators, but not topological superconductors or lattice models with a sublattice symmetry. In the examples of Secs.
III
-
VIII
we further restrict to symmetry classes A and AI, describing insulators with spin-rotation symmetry with and without time-reversal symmetry.

II.1
Stable, fragile, delicate, and representation protected topologies

We begin with some general remarks on various notions of topological equivalence for translation invariant systems, which are completely described by a finite-dimensional Bloch Hamiltonian
H(\mathbf{k})
. Two Bloch Hamiltonians
H_{1,2}
are considered
homotopically
equivalent,

H_{1}\sim H_{2},

(1)

if
H_{1}
can be continuously deformed to
H_{2}
without closing the gap or violating the symmetry constraints imposed on
H_{1,2}
. A looser notion is that of
stable
equivalence, whereby two Bloch Hamiltonians
H_{1,2}
are considered topologically equivalent if there exists a gapped Bloch Hamiltonian
H
, subject to the same symmetry constraints as
H_{1,2}
, so that

H_{1}\oplus H\sim H_{2}\oplus H

(2)

in the homotopic sense
3
3

3

This definition requires that
H_{1,2}
have equal number of conduction and valence bands. However, if the symmetries allow for the definition of a canonical “trivial” band, then this definition can be further relaxed to allow comparison of systems with different number of bands by adding the trivial bands.

.
Finally,
fragile
equivalence is defined by Eq. (
2
) with the further constraint on
H
that it consist only of unoccupied bands. In mathematical terms, the problem of fragile and stable classification can be expressed as the computation of equivalence classes of vector bundles, with the equivalence being isomorphism
Nittis and Gomi 2014
;
Nittis and Gomi 2015
;
Kennedy and Guggenheim 2015
for the former case and isomorphism
up to Whitney sums
for the latter, which falls under the purview of topological K-theory
Kitaev 2009
. Given a choice of a reference band structure
H_{\text{ref}}
, a Bloch Hamiltonian
H
is said to have “stable topology” if it is not equivalent to
H_{\text{ref}}
under stable equivalence. If
H
and
H_{\text{ref}}
are topologically equivalent under stable equivalence, but not under homotopic equivalence or vector bundle isomorphism, then
H
is said to possess ‘‘unstable’’ topology. The space of stably equivalent band structures can be endowed with a group structure, the group operation corresponding to the direct sum of the Bloch Hamiltonians
4
4

4

Strictly speaking, defining the trivial element for the group operation does not require the identification of a trivial band structure, but, instead, proceeds via the Grothendieck construction, which considers topological equivalence of
pairs

(H,H^{\prime})
of gapped band structures. Two pairs
(H_{1},H_{1}^{\prime})
and
(H_{2},H_{2}^{\prime})
are then considered topologically equivalent if
H_{1}\oplus H_{2}^{\prime}
can be smoothly deformed into
H_{2}\oplus H_{1}^{\prime}
, without closing the excitation gap and without violating the symmetry constraints.

.
As this group operation is incompatible with the restrictions imposed by fragile and delicate topology, they do not have a similar group structure.

The rules of topological classification can be generalized by
a priori
limiting the types of orbitals and Wyckoff positions allowed,
i.e.
, restricting the band representations. Such
representation-protected
phases have been previously investigated in Refs.
Fu 2011
;
Song et al. 2020b
;
Kobayashi and Furusaki 2021
, whereby
{\mathrm{C}}_{4}
-symmetric band structures were shown to exhibit a topological phase for arbitrary number of bands, provided one only allows
p
-orbitals. As in the unrestricted case, one may have delicate, fragile, and stable versions of representation-protected topological classifications. All of these are sometimes referred to in the literature as “fragile”
Song et al. 2020b
;
Kobayashi and Furusaki 2021
; however, stable representation-protected topology exhibits many of the characteristics of conventional stable topology, such as a group structure and the existence of anomalous states at boundaries that respect the symmetry restrictions imposed on the bulk.

Our classification procedure starts by fixing the numbers
n_{\mathrm{o}}
and
n_{\mathrm{e}}
of occupied and unoccupied bands and thus naturally identifies all possible delicate topological phases. The fragile classification is obtained by taking the limit
n_{\mathrm{e}}\to\infty
for a fixed
n_{\mathrm{o}}
, while the stable classification is obtained when we take
n_{\mathrm{o},\mathrm{e}}\to\infty
. As our goal is to obtain a full homotopic classification, we respect symmetry-imposed obstructions between atomic-limit phases and do not follow the sometimes-used custom
Bradlyn et al. 2017
;
Po et al. 2017
;
Song et al. 2018
to refer to all band structures that are homotopically equivalent to an atomic-limit as “trivial”. To infer whether a certain homotopic equivalence class corresponds to an atomic-limit phase, one has to compare the topological invariants to the topological invariants of atomic limit phases. An exhaustive listing of atomic-limit phases for all crystalline symmetry groups has been obtained in Ref.
Bradlyn et al. 2017
in the framework of “topological quantum chemistry”.

II.2
Lattice symmetries and the Bloch Hamiltonian

We consider a
n\times n
Bloch Hamiltonian

in the basis of Bloch states

. An arbitrary band representation can be decomposed into a direct sum of

(EBRs), which can be enumerated by considering all possible orbital types localized at high-symmetry points of the lattice. A systematic study of possible band representations has been central to the partial classifications based on “symmetry-based indicators”

;
Po et al. 2017
;
Tang et al. 2019a
;
Tang et al. 2019b
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Tang et al. 2019c
and “topological quantum chemistry”
Bradlyn et al. 2017
;
Cano et al. 2018a
;
Cano and Bradlyn 2021
;
Vergniory et al. 2019
. The full homotopic classification is thus an extension of these partial classifications.

II.3.2
Topology at levels
p>0

We now turn to topology associated with the
p
-cells for
p>0
. We start with two observations:

•

The topological class of
H
is always determined relative to a reference
H_{\rm ref}
. The classification thus addresses
pairs
of Bloch Hamiltonians
(H,H_{\text{ref}})
.

•

If
H
and
H_{\rm ref}
have different invariants on level-
q
for any
q<p
, then they are topologicaly inequivalent. Thus, for classification on
p
-cells, we may assume that
H
and
H_{\text{ref}}
have identical invariants up to level
p-1
.

The latter observation implies that for a
p
-cell
\alpha
, the restrictions of
H
and
H_{\text{ref}}
to
\partial\alpha
can be smoothly deformed into each other, and, furthermore, that this deformation can be done simultaneously for all
p
-cells. Choosing such a smooth deformation, the “difference” between
H
and
H_{\text{ref}}
then defines a map from the
p
-sphere
S^{p}
to
\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}
for each
p
-cell
\alpha
. This is illustrated in Fig.
2
for the cases
p=1
and
p=2
. Such maps are classified by the homotopy spaces
K_{\alpha}\equiv\pi_{p}[\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}]
, which we term the
parent classification set
of the
p
-cell
\alpha
. The full level-
p
parent classification set then reads

K_{p}=\prod_{\alpha}K_{\alpha};\quad K_{\alpha}=\pi_{p}\left[\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}\right].

(8)

The parent classification set
K_{p}
is, however, not the actual classifying set for equivalence classes of Bloch Hamiltonians
H
, because a given pair
(H,H_{\text{ref}})
can yield different elements in
K_{p}
, depending on the deformation used to achieve equality of
H
and
H_{\text{ref}}
on
\partial\mathbb{D}_{p}
. Such elements of
K_{p}
must be identified to get a true descriptor of homotopic equivalence classes. Explicitly, any deformation used to achieve equality of
H
and
H_{\rm ref}
on
\partial\mathbb{D}_{p}
can be written as a
p
-dimensional “Bloch Hamiltonian”
H_{\partial}(\mathbf{k}_{\partial},t)
, where
\mathbf{k}_{\partial}\in\partial\mathbb{D}_{p}
,
t\in[0,1]
, and
H_{\partial}
satisfies

H_{\partial}(\mathbf{k}_{\partial},0)=H(\mathbf{k}_{\partial}),\quad H_{\partial}(\mathbf{k}_{\partial},1)=H_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{k}_{\partial}),

(9)

as well as symmetry constraints for each
q
-cell in
\partial\mathbb{D}_{p}
for any
t
. The “difference” of two boundary deformations transforming
H
to
H_{\rm ref}
on
\partial\mathbb{D}_{p}
can be viewed as a boundary deformation that connects
H_{\rm ref}
to itself,
i.e.
, with

H_{\partial}(\mathbf{k}_{\partial},0)=H_{\partial}(\mathbf{k}_{\partial},1)=H_{\rm ref}(\mathbf{k}_{\partial}).

(10)

These “boundary deformations” can be visualized as in Fig.
3
, whereby one imagines that
H_{\partial}(\mathbf{k}_{\partial},t)
is continuously “grown” onto the band structure defined by
H
on
\alpha
for each
p
-cell
\alpha\in\mathbb{D}_{p}
. The level-
p
topological invariants are then obtained from
K_{p}
by identification of elements related by deformations of this type.

H(\mathbf{k})

,

, is continously added onto

coincides with the reference band structure

for

and

and

satisfies all symmetry constraints at each point in the transformation.

To determine the action of boundary deformations
H_{\partial}
on the elements of
K_{p}
, it is sufficient to consider at least one boundary deformation from each topological equivalence class of boundary deformations. Considering multiple boundary deformations from the same topological class is unproblematic, because they have the same action on
K_{p}
and therefore lead to identification of the same elements. Since the domain
\partial\mathbb{D}_{p}\times[0,1]
of a boundary deformation is again a CW complex, these topological equivalence classes can be determined in the same manner as described above. It is, in fact, sufficient to only determine the corresponding parent classification set

K_{p}^{\partial}=\prod_{\alpha\in\partial\mathbb{D}_{p}}\pi_{r_{\alpha}}[\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}],

(11)

where
r_{\alpha}=\dim\alpha+1
. This is because the parent classification set
K_{p}^{\partial}
is “overcomplete”, so that considering one boundary deformation from each class in
K_{p}^{\partial}
ensures that
at least
one boundary transformation from each (true) topological class is considered. Only having to determine
K_{p}^{\partial}
leads to a substantial simplification by avoiding having to deal with “boundary transformations of boundary transformations”. For the reader that is nevertheless interested in obtaining the exact boundary classification set, we review a recursive procedure in Appendix
C
.

There are additional constraints on the (parent) topological invariants, which arise from the fact that the individual cells are contractible. For any
(p+1)
-cell
\alpha
with
1\leq p\leq d-1
, the boundary
\partial\alpha
can thus be continuously deformed to a point within
\alpha
. Consequently, the topology of any Bloch Hamiltonian
H
restricted to
\partial\alpha
, evaluated with respect the (larger) target space
\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}\supseteq\mathcal{X}_{\beta}
for
\beta\in\partial\alpha
, must be trivial. This implies that the “sum” of level-
p
invariants for
p
-cells
\beta\in\partial\alpha
must vanish. We term these conditions — one for each
(p+1)
-cell — the
level-
p
compatibility conditions
. Since this sum over level-
p
invariants may also depend on topological invariants at levels
q<p
, the level-
p
compatibility conditions may affect invariants at all levels
q\leq p
. The compatibility condition equally apply to the boundary deformations and their parent classification
K_{p}^{\partial}
.

To summarize, the topological invariants at all levels
0\leq p\leq d
, modulo the ambiguities associated with boundary deformations, and constrained by the compatibility relations at all levels, constitute the complete classification of the
d
-dimensional systems. The compatibility relations ensure that for each set of topological invariants there is a gapped band structure with these invariants, while the robustness to boundary deformations ensures that the topological invariants can be uniquely associated with a gapped band structure. The classification naturally has a hierarchical reciprocal-space structure
Kennedy and Guggenheim 2015
, since the classification at level-
p
depends on the invariants at all lower level, starting with the symmetry based indicators at level-0.

II.4
Target spaces

We now discuss the various target spaces
\mathcal{X}
that arise in the classification for symmetry classess A, AI, and AII and describe their homotopy groups.

II.4.1
Grassmannians and their topology

We are interested in the space of
n\times n
Hermitian matrices with a fixed number of occupied and empty bands (
i.e.
, negative and positive eigenvalues)
n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}
satisfying

n=n_{\mathrm{o}}+n_{\mathrm{e}}.

(12)

For a topological classification,
H(\mathbf{k})
may be replaced by a Hermitian matrix with a “flattened spectrum” by deforming all positive/negative eigenvalues to
\pm 1
, so that
H(k)
is unitarily equivalent to
\text{diag}\{\openone_{n_{\mathrm{e}}},-\openone_{n_{\mathrm{o}}}\}
. The space of such Hamiltonians is the
complex Grassmannian

\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(n_{\mathrm{o}},n_{\mathrm{e}})
, which is a manifold that parametrizes
n_{\mathrm{o}}
-dimensional (or equivalently,
n_{\mathrm{e}}
-dimensional) complex planes in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(see App.
D
for more details). We also encounter Bloch Hamiltonians
H(\mathbf{k})
with real
(\mathbb{R})
or quaternion
(\mathbb{H}
) entries, for which the target space is the corresponding Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{R}}(n_{\mathrm{o}},n_{\mathrm{e}})
or
\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{H}}(n_{\mathrm{o}},n_{\mathrm{e}})
, respectively. These three spaces constitute the top-level target spaces
\mathcal{X}
for the symmetries considered here.

For a
p
-cell
\alpha
with a nontrivial little group
G_{\alpha}
, the target spaces
\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
can be written as a product of Grassmannians. This is because the eigenstates of
H
restricted to
\alpha
can be labeled with irreps
\rho
of
G_{\alpha}
, so that after a spectral flattening, we get

\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}=\prod_{\rho}\mathrm{Gr}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{\rho}}\left(n_{\mathrm{o},\rho}^{\alpha},n_{\mathrm{e},\rho}^{\alpha}\right),

(13)

where
\rho
runs over the irreps of
G_{\alpha}
and
\mathbb{F}_{\rho}
is a number field, which, depending on
\rho
, may be
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{R}
, or
\mathbb{H}
. The latter two occur only if
G_{\alpha}
contains antiunitary symmetries. Since we are interested in
\pi_{p}[\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}]
and the homotopy of a product of spaces satisfies

\pi_{k}\Big[\prod_{\alpha}X_{\alpha}\Big]=\prod_{\alpha}\pi_{k}\left[X_{\alpha}\right],

(14)

we only need to compute
\pi_{p}[\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{F}}(n_{\mathrm{o}},n_{\mathrm{e}})]
.

Homotopy groups of Grassmannians have been extensively studied in the mathematical literature. Their computation is briefly outlined in Appendix
D
. We, however, make two general observations here:

•

\pi_{p}[\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{F}}(n_{\mathrm{o}},n_{\mathrm{e}})]=0
for all
p>0
if
n_{\mathrm{o}}=0
or
n_{\mathrm{e}}=0
, since in that case,
\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{F}}(n_{\mathrm{o}},n_{\mathrm{e}})
degenerates to a single point.

•

\pi_{p}[\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{F}}(n_{\mathrm{o}},n_{\mathrm{e}})]
becomes independent of
n_{\mathrm{o}}
and
n_{\mathrm{e}}
(“stabilizes”) once
n_{\mathrm{o}}
and
n_{\mathrm{e}}
are large enough, which typically means
n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}\gtrsim p
.

As to the former observation, while
n_{\mathrm{o},\mathrm{e}}>0
for gapped band structure,
\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{F}}(n_{\mathrm{o}},n_{\mathrm{e}})
with
n_{\mathrm{o}}=0
or
n_{\mathrm{e}}=0
may occur in individual symmetry sectors at high-symmetry manifolds. The latter observation forms the basis for the classification of “stable” topology.

The homotopy invariants of Grassmannians can often be expressed in terms of Wilson lines or loops
Alexandradinata et al. 2014b
;
Bouhon et al. 2019
. Given a path
\gamma
in the reciprocal space, the Wilson line
{W}^{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}(\gamma)\in\mathrm{U}(n)
can be associated with the occupied/empty bands and encodes the evolution of the corresponding subspaces under parallel transport along
\gamma
. Wilson lines are not gauge invariant, since they depend on the choice of basis at the end points of
\gamma
. They are unambiguously defined only if there are additional constraints on the end points of
\gamma
that fix the basis or if the path
\gamma
is a closed loop. A local-in-
\mathbf{k}
symmetry on
H(\mathbf{k})
implies a restriction on Wilson lines/loops; in particular, if the Hamiltonian is restricted by symmetries to be real- or quaternion-valued, then they are restricted to
\mathrm{O}(n),\,\mathrm{Sp}(n)\subset\mathrm{U}(n)
, respectively.

II.5
Topology and bulk-boundary correspondence

Band structures with nontrivial stable topology in
d
dimensions exhibit anomalous gapless boundary states on
(d-1)
-dimensional boundaries that respect all symmetries — a phenomenon known as the “bulk-boudary correspondence”
Hasan and Kane 2010
;
Qi and Zhang 2011
;
Bernevig and Hughes 2013
;
Ando and Fu 2015
. In this context, a boundary mode is called “anomalous” if it cannot be realized by any
(d-1)
-dimensional lattice model that respects all the symmetries. Of the three types of topological classification — based on delicate, fragile, and stable equivalence — only the first allows for anomalous boundary states as a signature of the bulk topology. This, however, also includes the representation protected topological phases (defined in Sec.
II.1
), provided the boundary respects the symmetry as well as the representation constraints.

Our classification procedure can also be used to classify anomalous gapless states at a symmetry-preserving boundary. To this end, we first enumerate the possible gapless phases consistent with given symmetries by looking for all possible violations of the compatibility conditions in
d-1
dimensions. Such violations indicate gapless phases, since a level-
p
compatibility condition follows from the contractibility of a
(p+1)
-cell
\alpha
, so that its a violation implies that
H_{\alpha}:\alpha\to\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}
is defined only for a noncontractible subset of
\alpha
,
i.e.
, the Bloch Hamiltonian is gapless somewhere on
\alpha
. A gapless (
d-1
)-dimensional Bloch Hamiltonian
H
is anomalous if it necessarily violates a constraint imposed on the topological invariants by a gluing condition or by symmetry. We illustrate this classification scheme for anomalous boundary states by deriving some well known examples in the next section.

We first apply our classification strategy to band structures without additional crystalline symmetries. Since these classification results are well known, this section mainly serves as an illustration of our classification scheme following the more abstract discussion of Sec.
II
.

Classification without time-reversal symmetry

In the absence of time-reversal symmetry (symmetry class A), the fundamental domain is the entire Brillouin zone, which can be decomposed into cells as shown in the left column of Fig.

in two and three dimensions, respectively. The topological invariants for the unique 0-cell are the numbers of occupied and unoccupied bands
n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}

(n_{\mathrm{o}},n_{\mathrm{e}})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
. The target space
\mathcal{X}
for all cells is

\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(n_{\mathrm{o}},n_{\mathrm{e}}).

(20)

We can now read off the parent topological invariants from Tab.
1
.

There are no level-1 invariants, since the first homotopy group of complex Grassmannians is always trivial. At level-2, the parent classification set is
K_{1}=\pi_{2}[\mathcal{X}]\cong\mathbb{Z}
and the corresponding topological invariant is the
\mathbb{Z}
-valued Chern number
\mathrm{C}
. To get the true topological invariant, however, we need to check if it is robust to deformations at the boundary of a 2-cell. As discussed in Sec.
II.3
, the parent classification set of boundary deformations
K_{2}^{\partial}
is generated by deformations along the 0-cells and the 1-cells, whose equivalence classes are parametrized by
\pi_{1}[\mathcal{X}]=0
and
\pi_{2}[\mathcal{X}]\cong K_{2}\cong\mathbb{Z}
, respectively. Thus, boundary deformations may carry a nonzero Chern number along a 1-cell on

III.3
Anomalous boundary states

In the symmetry class A, anomalous boundary states are associated with a nontrivial value of the only stable invariant,
viz
, the Chern number. In two dimensions, these are the chiral edge states of the quantum Hall effect, while in three dimensions, these are chiral surface states of a weak Chern insulator. We now derive these invariants from a boundary perspective.

Consider a 1d Bloch Hamiltonian in symmetry class A, whose fundamental domain is the entire 1d Brillouin zone, as shown in Fig.
6
(left). We assume that
H
is gapped at the 0-cells, so that the level-0 invariants
n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}^{\bar{S}}
are well defined for
\bar{S}\in\{{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}},{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}^{\prime}\}
. The presence of a chiral state crossing the Fermi level implies that the level-0 invariants
n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}^{{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}}\neq n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}^{{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}^{\prime}}
, which violates both the compatibility condition and the gluing condition. The former violation signifies that a band structure with a chiral state crossing the Fermi level is necessarilty gapless, while the latter violation signals that such a band structure is anomalous,
i.e.
, it can not be realized in a one-dimensional lattice model

If

has a zero energy mode at
{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}

.
An analogous argument holds for a chiral band crossing the Fermi energy in 2d.

In symmetry class AI, there is no nontrivial bulk topology and, hence, there are no associated anomalous boundary states. The same conclusion can be obtained from a boundary perspective. To wit, the fundamental domain of a 1d Bloch Hamiltonian in symmetry class AI is half the 1d Brillouin zone, as shown in Fig.

(right). A band crossing between
{\overline{\Gamma}}
amd
{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}

for
\bar{S}\in\{{\overline{\Gamma}},{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}\}
— signalling a gapless phase — but it does not imply the violation of a gluing condition, so that such a band crossing is not anomalous. Instead, time-reveral symmetry imposes the presence of two opposite crossings of the Fermi energy in the full boundary Brillouin zone, which can be realized in a 1d lattice model and which may deformed to a gapped Hamiltonian. Thus, there are no anomalous boundary modes in symmetry class AI.

In symmetry class AII, anomalous boundary modes are associated with the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-valued stable invariant. In two dimensions, these are the helical edge states of the quantum spin Hall effect, while in three dimensions, these are the surface Dirac cones of a strong topological insulator. In this case, the fundamental domain is identical to that of symmetry class AI (Fig.

are
half
the number of occupied/unoccupied bands. Thus, if a chiral mode crosses the Fermi level between
{\overline{\Gamma}}
and
{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}

Application of a similar arguments to any pair of high-symmetry points in the surface Brillouin zone indicates the existence of anomalous surface states for 3d topological insulators

;
Moore and Balents 2007

Roy 2009

IV
Classification in three dimensions with crystalline symmetries: General remarks

In the next sections, we apply the classification strategy of Sec.
II
to band structures with
{\mathrm{C}}_{2}{\mathrm{T}}
,
{\mathrm{C}}_{2}
,
{\mathrm{C}}_{4}
, and
{\mathrm{D}}_{4}
symmetries. Since these symmetries are all two-dimensional and include a rotation, in two dimensions they do not exhibit a boundary that is invariant under the symmetries; however, they all exhibit invariant planes in three dimensions, which may host anomalous boundary states. We thus consider a simple-cubic lattice with unit lattice constant, with the rotation axis (and mirror planes for
{\mathrm{D}}_{4}
) parallel to the
z
-axis. For the crystalline symmetries considered in this article, this simplification does not affect our conclusions. Since
C_{2}
is an element of all of these groups, for the time-reversal-symmetric case, we choose a basis such that
{\mathrm{C}}_{2}{\mathrm{T}}
is represented by the identity matrix, so that

H(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=H^{*}(k_{x},k_{y},-k_{z}).

(26)

For
k_{z}=0,\pi
(and for 2d systems), this reduces to a reality condition on
H(\mathbf{k})
.

The fundamental domain in all such cases is a prism with the two-dimensional fundamental domain as the base and
k_{z}\in(-\pi,\pi]
without and
k_{z}\in[0,\pi]
with time-reversal symmetry. (See Fig.
1
for the fundamental domains without crystalline symmetries.) The “vertical” faces are formed by extruding the 1-cells of the fundamental domain
\mathbb{D}_{2}
along
z
, so that the vertical faces and edges inherit the little groups of the 1- and 0-cells of which they are an extrusion. The “horizontal” faces at
k_{z}=\pm\pi
for the time-reversal broken case and
k_{z}=0,\pi
for the time-reversal-symmetric case are copies of
\mathbb{D}_{2}
, and thus satisfy the corresponding constraints.

The classification on the horizontal faces depends on the details of the symmetry in question, but that on the vertical faces can be described more generally. Since time-reversal (if present) does not impose any additional constraints on the side faces for a two-dimensional symmetry, the target space for the vertical 1- and 2-cells is always the complex Grassmannian. Thus, there are no level-1 invariants for the vertical 1-cells, while the level-2 parent invariants are given by a set of Chern numbers, one for each irrep on the 1-cells of
\mathbb{D}_{2}
. The various Chern numbers satisfy a compatibility constraint arising from the fact that taken together, the 2-cells form the boundary of the 3-cell.

The parent classification set on the 3-cell is identical to that obtained without additional symmetries,
i.e.
, the
\mathbb{Z}
-valued Hopf invariant
\mathrm{H}
only if
n_{\mathrm{o}}=n_{\mathrm{e}}=1
. Interestingly, it is robust even if the vertical 2-cells possess nonzero Chern numbers, as shown in App.
E
. This can alternatively be seen by extending the calculation of the Hopf invariant to the full Brillouin zone, for which the
{\mathrm{C}}_{2}
or
{\mathrm{C}}_{4}
symmetry rules out a nonzero Chern number associated with the vertical faces of the full Brillouin zone. However, if a cross section of the fundamental domain
\mathbb{D}
at constant
k_{z}
has a nonzero Chern number
\mathrm{C}
, then the Hopf invariant reduces to a
\mathbb{Z}_{2\mathrm{C}}
-valued invariant, owing to a deformation along the vertical faces that changes
\mathrm{H}
by an even multiple of
\mathrm{C}
(see App.
E
).

n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}
. The target space for all cells is again the real Grassmannian
\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{R}}(n_{\mathrm{o}},n_{\mathrm{e}})
. The level-1 classification is similar to the one-dimensional case discussed above: each of the two 1-cells contribute the
\mathbb{Z}
-valued first Euler classes
\mathrm{Eu}_{1,x/y}
if
n_{\mathrm{o}}=n_{\mathrm{e}}=1
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-valued Stiefel-Whitney invariants
\mathrm{SW}_{1,x/y}
otherwise. The latter are robust to boundary deformations, while the former are robust only if both orbitals lie at Wyckoff position with the same
x/y
coordinate, and reduce to a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant otherwise.

The level-1 invariants are further constrained by compatibility relations. Since the boundary of the 2-cell consists of two copies of each 1-cell, the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-valued
\mathrm{SW}_{1}
always add up to
0
, so that there are no additional restrictions on
\mathrm{SW}_{1,x/y}
. There are nontrivial compatibility constraints on
\mathrm{Eu}_{1,x/y}
, however, that depend on the reciprocal-space translation operators
\Lambda_{x/y}
. If the two orbitals are at the same Wyckoff position, such that
\Lambda_{x/y}\propto\sigma^{0}
, then there are no additional constraints on
\mathrm{Eu}_{1,x/y}
, so that the level-1 classification set is
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. If only one of the translation operators (
\Lambda_{x}
, say) is proportional to
\sigma^{3}
, then
\mathrm{Eu}_{1,x}
is only defined modulo 2, while
\mathrm{Eu}_{1,y}
adds up for the two copies of the 1-cell along
y
, so that the compatibility constraint requires
2\,\mathrm{Eu}_{1,y}=0
. This leaves only one
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-valued invariant,
viz
,
\mathrm{SW}_{1}\equiv\mathrm{Eu}_{1,x}\mod 2
. On the other hand, if both
\Lambda_{x/y}\propto\sigma^{3}
(such as when the two orbitals are at Wyckoff positions “a” and “d”), then the compatibility condition reads

2\,\mathrm{Eu}_{1,x}-2\,\mathrm{Eu}_{1,y}=0\implies\mathrm{Eu}_{1,x}=\mathrm{Eu}_{1,y}\equiv\mathrm{Eu}_{1}.

Furthermore, the boundary deformations (described for the 1d case) simultaneously changes both
\mathrm{Eu}_{1,x}
and
\mathrm{Eu}_{1,y}
by an even integer. Thus, we are again left with only one
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-valued invariant
\mathrm{Eu}_{1}
.

At level-2,
K_{2}=\pi_{2}[\mathcal{X}]
depends on
n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}
(see Tab.

\mathrm{Eu}_{2}^{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}

\mathbb{Z}

2
) when
n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}=2

n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}>2

\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-valued second Stiefel-Whitney invariant
\mathrm{SW}_{2}\equiv\mathrm{Eu}_{2}^{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}\mod 2

K_{2}^{\partial}
consists of a copy of
\pi_{1}[\mathcal{X}]
for the 0-cell and
\pi_{2}[\mathcal{X}]\cong K_{2}
for each of the 1-cells. We now discuss their effect on the Euler and Stiefel-Whitney invariant separately.

Second Euler invariant —

Since these invariants are defined only when
n_{\mathrm{o}}=2
or
n_{\mathrm{e}}=2

K_{2}^{\partial}
is
\pi_{1}[\mathcal{X}]=\mathbb{Z}_{2}

\mathrm{SO}(2)
(see Sec.

\partial\mathbb{D}

This change of orientation can originate from either a reciprocal space translation operator or a nontrivial first Stiefel-Whitney invariant. Explicitly, denoting the 1-cells along
x
and
y

\alpha_{x}
and
\alpha_{y}

\mathrm{Eu}_{2}

(36)

for

\mathrm{SW}_{1,a}

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

A boundary deformation that is nontrivial at a 0-cell changes the sign of
\mathrm{Eu}_{2}^{\mathrm{o}}
and
\mathrm{Eu}_{2}^{\mathrm{e}}

Second Stiefel-Whitney invariant —

These are robust to boundary deformations, since the effect of the latter always cancels out modulo 2.

{\mathrm{C}}_{2}{\mathrm{T}}

In three dimensions,

V.3.1
Homotopic classification

The level-1 invariants are two independent copies of the invariants obtained for the 2d classification. As discussed in Sec.

The level-2 invariants for the

\mathrm{C}_{x/y}

The

A classification of

;

VI.1
Wyckoff positions, basis orbitals, and symmetries

With twofold rotation symmetry and in two dimensions, there are four special Wyckoff positions “a”, “b”, “c”, and “d” (shown in Fig.
8
) and one pair of generic Wyckoff positions “g”. At each special Wyckoff position, electron orbitals are even (
+
) or odd (

+

\pm

\displaystyle n=

\displaystyle\,n_{\mathrm{a}}+n_{\mathrm{b}}+n_{\mathrm{c}}+n_{\mathrm{d}}+2n_{\mathrm{g}}=n_{+}+n_{-}.

(37)

The reciprocal-space group
G_{\Lambda}
is generated by twofold rotation
g_{2}
and two reciprocal-space translations
g_{x}
and
g_{y}

4

\Lambda_{x}

\Lambda_{y}

\displaystyle H(k_{x},k_{y})=C_{2}H(-k_{x},-k_{y})C_{2}^{\dagger}

(38)

5
) for the reciprocal-space translations
g_{x,y}

\Lambda_{x}

\Lambda_{y}

C_{2}\Lambda_{x,y}=\Lambda_{x,y}^{\dagger}C_{2}^{\phantom{\dagger}}.

(39)

We choose the over-all phase factor of the twofold rotation operator

C_{2}=\text{diag}\{\openone_{n_{+}},-\openone_{n_{-}}\}.

(40)

Explicit expressions for

B.1

\displaystyle C_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle C_{{\mathrm{X}},{\mathrm{Y}}}

VI.2.1
Without time-reversal (symmetry class A)

The level-
0

n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e},\pm}^{S}
for each 0-cell
S

(44)

42

\displaystyle\sum_{S}\left(n_{\mathrm{o},-}^{S}+n_{\mathrm{e},-}^{S}\right)=0\mod 2.

This is sufficient to ensure that the level-0 invariants, summed over occupied and unoccupied bands, constitute a linear combination of the EBRs listed in Tab.
15

For fixed level-0 invariants, the target spaces are

\mathcal{X}_{S}=\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(n_{\mathrm{o},+}^{S},n_{\mathrm{e},+}^{S})\times\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(n_{\mathrm{o},-}^{S},n_{\mathrm{e},-}^{S}).

(46)

for the 0-cells and

\mathbb{Z}

With time-reversal (symmetry class AI)

representation-protected stable topology

{\cal Q}_{2}^{\land}

Po et al. 2017

VI.3.3
Anomalous boundary states

In the absence of time-reversal symmetry, a nontrivial Chern number
\mathrm{C}_{z}
for the horizontal 2-cell indicates a weak Chern-insulator phase, which is a stable topological phase with topologically protected chiral surface states on surfaces parallel to the rotation axis.

(right). At levels 0, 1, and 2, we get two copies of the 2d classification problem discussed in Sec.
VIII.2
. At level-2, we additionally get Chern numbers
C_{\pm}^{SS^{\prime}}
for the vertical 2-cells. The conditions for their robustness and the level-2 compatibility condition are identical to the time-reversal broken case. At level-3, we get the
\mathbb{Z}
-valued Hopf invariant if
n_{\mathrm{o}}=n_{\mathrm{e}}=1
.

\mathbb{D}

VIII.3.3
Representation-protected stable topology

A restriction to the two dimensional irrep
B
leads to representation-protected stable topology with nontrivial level-2 invariants. In the absence of time-reversal symmetry, the parent invariants are the six Chern numbers
\mathrm{C}_{\pm}^{SS^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{Z}
computed on the vertical faces. In the presence of time-reversal symmetry,
\mathrm{C}_{\pm}^{SS^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{Z}
can be combined with the difference between the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-valued invariants
\mathrm{SW}_{1,\pm}^{SS^{\prime}}
computed at
k_{z}=0,\pi
to yield a
\mathbb{Z}
-valued Chern number computed for
k_{z}\in(-\pi,\pi]
. (The situation is analogous to the case of
{\mathrm{C}}_{2}{\mathrm{T}}
-symmetry, see Sec.
V.3
.) Time reversal symmetry thus plays no role in this case. In the following, we use
{\mathrm{C}}^{SS^{\prime}}_{\pm}
to refer to the Chern number computed for
k_{z}\in(-\pi,\pi]
both without and with time-reversal symmetry.

The effect of boundary deformations depends on whether one or both Wyckoff positions are allowed, as in the case of the 2d representation protected phase (see Sec.
VIII.2.3
). Thus, if a single Wyckoff position is allowed, then we are left with two independent
\mathbb{Z}
-valued invariants. For the Wyckoff position “a”, these can be written as (
cf.
Eq. (
98
))

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}^{\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathrm{C}_{+}^{\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}}-\mathrm{C}_{-}^{\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}^{+}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathrm{C}_{+}^{\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}}+\mathrm{C}_{+}^{{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}}+\mathrm{C}_{+}^{{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma}.

(101)

For only the Wyckoff position “b”, the latter is instead defined as

\mathrm{C}^{+}\equiv\mathrm{C}_{+}^{\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}}+\mathrm{C}_{+}^{{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}}+\mathrm{C}_{+}^{{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma}.

(102)

If both Wyckoff positions are allowed, then only
\mathrm{C}^{\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}}
remains well-defined.

VIII.4
Anomalous boundary states

For the band structures with representation-protected topology, there are anomalous boundary modes at the
{\mathrm{D}}_{4}
-symmetric surfaces at constant
z
. For the case that only orbitals at Wyckoff position “a” are allowed, we find it convenient to replace the integer invariant
\mathrm{C}^{+}
with

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}^{{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma}=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{C}^{{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}}_{+}+\mathrm{C}^{{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma}_{+}-\mathrm{C}^{{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}}_{-}-\mathrm{C}^{{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma}_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,2\mathrm{C}^{+}-\mathrm{C}^{\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}},

(103)

which, by definition, has the same parity as
\mathrm{C}^{\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}}
. The case that only orbitals at ”b” are allowed proceeds analogously, whereas
\mathrm{C}^{{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma}
is not defined if both Wyckoff positions are allowed. To relate these invariants to boundary states, we note that, since
n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e},+}^{SS^{\prime}}=n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e},-}^{SS^{\prime}}
for each 1-cell, we can glue the two parity sectors to define a Chern insulator on
SS^{\prime}
. The Chern number
\mathrm{C}^{\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}}
then equals the difference of the number of even-parity and odd-parity bands crossing the Fermi surface along
\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}
, while
\mathrm{C}^{{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma}
(if defined) counts this difference along
{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}
and
{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma
. An odd parity of
\mathrm{C}^{\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}}
(and also of
\mathrm{C}^{{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma}
, if defined) implies protected band touchings at
\Gamma
and
{\mathrm{M}}
, similar to the case of a
{\mathrm{C}}_{4}
-symmetric band structure
Fu 2011
;
Song et al. 2020a
;
Kobayashi and Furusaki 2021
. Even contributions to
\mathrm{C}^{\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}}
and
\mathrm{C}^{{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma}
can be traced back to equal numbers of even-parity and odd-parity bands crossing the Fermi level in opposite directions. Since such crossings can be gapped out away from the high-symmetry lines, they imply Dirac cones protected by mirror symmetry along
\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}
and, if applicable, along
{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma
. If
\mathrm{C}^{\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}}
and
\mathrm{C}^{{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma}
are both defined, the numbers of such Dirac crossings along along
\Gamma{\mathrm{M}}
and
{\mathrm{M}}{\mathrm{X}}\Gamma
, counted according parity, are independent signatures of the representation-protected topology.

From the boundary perspective, the presence of only
B
irreps at
{\overline{\Gamma}}
and
{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}
implies that the number of states in the occupied/empty subspaces is even, and furthermore, on each high-symmetry lines,
n_{\mathrm{o},+}^{\overline{SS^{\prime}}}=n_{\mathrm{o},-}^{\overline{SS^{\prime}}}
. In this setup, we can get anomalous gapless modes in two ways:

•

A single surface band crosses the Fermi level between
{\overline{\Gamma}}
and
{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}
, so that so that
n^{\overline{\Gamma}}_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}\neq n^{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}\mod 2
. This is incompatible with having only the irrep
B
at both the
{\overline{\Gamma}}
and
{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}
points. Since
n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}
is defined for any path connecting
{\overline{\Gamma}}
to
{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}
, all such paths must have a level crossing the Fermi level, so that we get a loop of zero crossings.

•

A pair of surface bands with opposite parities cross the Fermi level on
{\overline{\Gamma}}{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}
in opposite directions. This is compatible with having only irrep
B
at
{\overline{\Gamma}}
and
{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}
; however, the level-0 compatibility conditions at
{\overline{\Gamma}}
and
{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}
cannot be satisfied simultaneously by only
B
irreps. Since
n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}
stay unchanged under this crossing, it can be gapped away from
{\overline{\Gamma}}{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}
, leading to a Dirac cone on
{\overline{\Gamma}}{\overline{{\mathrm{M}}}}
protected by a mirror symmetry.

These are precisely the two kinds of anomalous boundary states obtained from the bulk perspective.

IX
Discussion

In this article, we presented a homotopic classification of band-structures with crystalline symmetries. The homotopic classification describes the delicate topology of gapped Bloch Hamiltonians with a fixed number of occupied/empty bands
n_{\mathrm{o}}/n_{\mathrm{e}}
and fixed numbers of orbitals for each orbital type at each Wyckoff position. A fragile classification, in which only the number of occupied bands is fixed and unoccupied bands may be added
ad libitum
, and a stable classification, in which both occupied and unoccupied bands may be added, can be obtained from the homotopic classification by taking the limits
n_{\mathrm{e}}\to\infty
and
n_{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}\to\infty
, respectively.

The central object in our scheme is the interpretation as a
CW-complex
of the fundamental domain of the point group, which is the minimal region in reciprocal space required to define the full Bloch Hamiltonian. A CW complex, fundamental in homotopy theory, is a topological space constructed from a collection of “cells” of dimension
p
with
p=0
, …,
d
using a set of “gluing conditions.” We classify the Bloch Hamiltonians restricted to the
p
-cells, starting with
p=0
and building up to higher
p
, imposing the constraints demanded from the CW-complex at each step. The structure of the classification over
p
-cells then depends on the concrete topological invariants over
q
-cells with
q<p
, thereby endowing the classification with a hierarchical structure. For a homotopic classification, the classifying space does not have a group structure. However, in the stable limit, the invariants at different levels factorize, leading to the well-known result that the full classifying spaces are products of groups, with the group operation deriving from the direct sum of bands.

Two basic elements of our approach — bootstrapping the full classification from that of restrictions of the Hamiltonian to individual cells, and, for each cell, the reduction of a larger (but easier to compute) “parent” classification group to the actual classification set by removing ambiguity from boundary deformations — have previously been applied to the classification of gapped band structures. The former element is shared with the partial classifications based on topological quantum chemistry
Fu and Kane 2007
;
Fang et al. 2012
;
Kruthoff et al. 2017
;
Bradlyn et al. 2017
;
Po et al. 2017
;
Song et al. 2018
;
Tang et al. 2019a
;
Tang et al. 2019b
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Tang et al. 2019c
, which consider topological invariants of 0-cells only. The latter was used previously for symmetry class AII by Moore and Balents
Moore and Balents 2007
as well as for the classification of inversion-symmetric insulators in the stable limit
Turner et al. 2012
(see also Ref.
Trifunovic and Brouwer 2017
).

We have illustrated the homotopic classification scheme on point groups of increasing complexity, for both time-reversal-invariant and time-reversal-broken cases. Applications to band structure without crystalline symmetry in symmetry classes A, AI, AII make a connection to existing homotopic classification approaches for gapped band structures without crystalline symmetries
Moore and Balents 2007
;
Kennedy and Zirnbauer 2016
. The other examples we consider elucidate various aspects of the homotopic classification and illustrate that it can be performed systematically. However, this process is involved enough that we have not been able to automate the generation of delicate classification tables, which would allow us to compute the topological invariants at all levels for all space groups, as it is done for the level-0 invariants in the framework of topological quantum chemistry and symmetry indicators
Bradlyn et al. 2017
;
Po et al. 2017
;
Song et al. 2018
;
Tang et al. 2019a
;
Tang et al. 2019b
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Tang et al. 2019c
. A quick look at the complexity of arguments required to produce the classification tables for a single crystalline symmetry in Secs.
VI
,
VII
, and
VIII
explains why such an automatized procedure may be too much to expect.

We find that a crucial aspect of a delicate classification is that restricting only the number of occupied/empty bands does not determine the classification uniquely. Instead, it is essential to also fix the (total) number of orbitals of each type at each Wyckoff position. Typically, the Wyckoff positions determine the level-0 invariants, and all subsequent restrictions on higher-level invariants can be expressed in terms of these, so that the Wyckoff positions and the representations of orbitals do no appear explicitly in the classification tables. For the examples we consider, this is the case for the point groups
{\mathrm{C}}_{2}
,
{\mathrm{C}}_{4}
, and
{\mathrm{D}}_{4}
. However, the Wyckoff positions may also enter the homotopic classification directly, as is the case for
{\mathrm{C}}_{2}{\mathrm{T}}
-symmetric band structures.

The existence of boundary states is a hallmark of stable topology — a topologically nontrivial insulator protected by a lattice symmetry exhibits anomalous boundary states at a boundary that respect the requisite symmetries. In the examples that we consider here, the lattice symmetries include a rotation, so that there are no invariant boundaries in two dimensions. However, in three dimensions, there is an invariant boundary normal to the rotation axis. We establish that such a boundary indeed hosts anomalous boundary modes if the stable topology is nontrivial. Anomalous boundary modes also exist for stable representation-protected topology, whereby one must not only constrain the types (but not the number) of orbitals in the bulk, but also at the at the invariant boundary.

In real systems, the anomalous boundary states protected by representation-protected stable topology may disappear if there is a surface reconstruction that violates the constraint on orbital types. Nevertheless, if this violation is sufficiently weak, non-anomalous boundary states may exist that can be traced back to the anomalous boundary states of representation-protected topology. A similar reasoning relating non-anomalous boundary states to anomalous boundary states protected by additional symmetries is used in the case of,
e.g.
, simplified models with a sublattice symmetry, which have zero-energy flat bands on edges or on surfaces (such as the “drumhead states” of nodal-line semimetals
Burkov et al. 2011
and the flat bands at zig-zag edges of graphene
Klein 1994
;
Fujita et al. 1996
), which do not remain flat and at zero energy once sublattice-symmetry-breaking perturbations are taken into account.

Even in absence of
individual
invariant boundaries, stable topology can lead to boundary signatures if the overall crystal is invariant under the symmetry. Such boundary signatures may be of higher-order type
Schindler et al. 2018
;
Langbehn et al. 2017
;
Song et al. 2017
;
Benalcazar et al. 2017a
;
Benalcazar et al. 2017b
;
Fang and Fu 2019
— such as anomalous gapless states at crystal hinges — and they are guaranteed to exist if the band structure is not of atomic limit type
Trifunovic and Brouwer 2019
;
Trifunovic and Brouwer 2020
. For band structures of atomic-limit type, more subtle boundary signatures, such as fractional corner charges or “twisted boundary signatures” may exist
Lau et al. 2016
;
Benalcazar et al. 2017a
;
Benalcazar et al. 2017b
;
van Miert and Ortix 2018
;
Schindler et al. 2019
;
Ono et al. 2019
;
Song et al. 2020a
;
Peri et al. 2020
;
Trifunovic 2020
;
Watanabe and Ono 2020
;
Ren et al. 2021
;
Watanabe and Po 2021
;
Kooi et al. 2021
;
Fang and Cano 2021
;
Takahashi et al. 2021
;
Naito et al. 2022
, but this is not guaranteed. We expect no such higher-order boundary signatures for representation-protected topology, because it is not possible to organize orbitals according to their representation under the crystalline symmetry operations on a surface that is not itself invariant.

In conclusion, we have presented analytical machinery that can be used to classify band structures with crystalline symmetry at the highest possible level of granularity. Whether the application of this methodology to other symmetry classes and more complicated point groups might yield further examples of representation-protected topology, remains a topic of future study.

Appendix A
Symmetries of the Bloch Hamiltonian

We briefly recapitulate the symmetry analysis of the Bloch Hamiltonians for general crystalline symmetries. (For a detailed analysis, see, e.g., the Supplementary Material of Ref.
Alexandradinata et al. 2014a
.)

A.1
Crystalline symmetries

We consider a symmetry of the lattice, derived from an isometry of the underlying space
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The physical systems of interest consist of a set of
n
orbitals
\alpha
located at positions
\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}
within the unit cell, whose origin is labeled by
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Under the action of
g
, an arbitrary lattice point transforms as

g(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})=g\mathbf{R}+{\delta\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{r}_{\alpha^{\prime}}\equiv\mathbf{R}^{\prime}+\mathbf{r}_{\alpha^{\prime}},

(104)

where, since
g\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}
may not lie within the unit cell, we subtract off a lattice translation
{\delta\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}
to move it back within the unit cell at the new position
\mathbf{r}_{\alpha^{\prime}}
. The symmetry generically maps a basis orbital at
\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}
to a linear combination of basis orbitals at
\mathbf{r}_{\alpha^{\prime}}
, so that its action on the position space basis state
\ket{\mathbf{R},\alpha}
can be written as

C(g)\ket{\mathbf{R},\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{n}C_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ast}(g)\ket{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}.

(105)

The unitary matrices
C(g)
for various
g\in G
then constitute a representation of the point group
G
.

In Sec.
II
, we define the Bloch states as

\ket{\mathbf{k},\alpha}=\sum_{\mathbf{R}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})}\ket{\mathbf{R},\alpha}.

(106)

Under the lattice symmetry
g
, these transform as

\displaystyle C(g)\ket{\mathbf{k},\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{R}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})}C(g)\ket{\mathbf{R},\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{R}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})}C_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ast}(g)\ket{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{n}C_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ast}(g)\left(\sum_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(g\mathbf{k})\cdot(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}+\mathbf{r}_{\alpha^{\prime}})}\ket{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{n}C_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ast}(g)\ket{g\mathbf{k},\alpha^{\prime}},

(107)

where we have used Eq. (
104
) and
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}=(g\mathbf{k})\cdot(g\mathbf{R})
. The symmetry operators are independent of
\mathbf{R}^{\prime}
, so that we do not need to account for the change in unit cell that a site might undergo under the symmetry operation. On the other hand, under translation by a reciprocal lattice vector
\mathbf{G}
, these Bloch states pick up a phase
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}}
, which can be written in form identical to Eq. (
107
) as

C(g_{\mathbf{G}})\ket{\mathbf{k},\alpha}\equiv\ket{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G},\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}}\Lambda_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ast}(\mathbf{G})\ket{\mathbf{k},\alpha^{\prime}},

(108)

where
\Lambda_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k})=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}}\delta_{\alpha\beta}
. We thus consider the point group symmetries and reciprocal lattice translations together as elements of the reciprocal space group
G_{\Lambda}
, whose action on the Bloch states is given by Eq. (
107
). The Bloch Hamiltonian then satisfies the constraint

H(g\mathbf{k})=C^{\dagger}(g)H(\mathbf{k})C(g)

(109)

for all
g\in G_{\Lambda}
.

We contrast this with the conventional definition of Bloch states (which does not contain the position of orbitals within the unit cell)

\ket{\mathbf{k},\alpha}_{\text{c}}=\sum_{\mathbf{R}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\ket{\mathbf{R},\alpha}_{\text{c}}.

(110)

These states are periodic under reciprocal lattice transformations, while under a lattice symmetry, they transform as

\displaystyle C(g)\ket{\mathbf{k},\alpha}_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{R}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}C(g)\ket{\mathbf{R},\alpha}_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{R}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}C_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ast}(g)\ket{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{n}C_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ast}(g)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(g\mathbf{k})\cdot{\delta\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}}\ket{g\mathbf{k},\alpha^{\prime}}_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widetilde{C}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ast}(g,\mathbf{k})\ket{g\mathbf{k},\alpha^{\prime}}_{\text{c}},

(111)

so that the unitary matrix
\widetilde{C}(g,\mathbf{k})
now depends on
\mathbf{k}
. The matrices
\widetilde{C}(g,\mathbf{k})
can be related to the
\mathbf{k}
-independent representation matrices
C(g)
as
\widetilde{C}(g,\mathbf{k})=\Lambda(g\mathbf{k})C(g)\Lambda(\mathbf{k})^{\dagger}
.
The two definitions of Bloch states are related by

\ket{\mathbf{k},\alpha}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}}\ket{\mathbf{k},\alpha}_{\text{c}}=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}}\Lambda_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}^{\ast}(\mathbf{k})\ket{\mathbf{k},\alpha^{\prime}}_{\text{c}},

(112)

so that the corrresponding Bloch Hamiltonians are related by

H(\mathbf{k})=\Lambda(\mathbf{k})\widetilde{H}(\mathbf{k})\Lambda^{\dagger}(\mathbf{k}).

(113)

In the following sections, we work exclusively in the Bloch basis of Eq. (
106
).

A.2
Time reversal symmetry

With the exception of Sec.
III.2.2
, we consider a time-reversal symmetry with
{\mathrm{T}}^{2}=+1
, and choose a basis of orbitals at lattice sites such that the Bloch Hamiltonian satisfies

H(\mathbf{k})=H^{*}(-\mathbf{k}).

(114)

This is equivalent to
\widetilde{H}^{*}(-\mathbf{k})=\widetilde{H}(\mathbf{k})
in the conventional Bloch basis, since the unitary matrix relating the two bases (Eq.
113
) satisfies

\Lambda_{\alpha\beta}^{*}(\mathbf{k})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}}\delta_{\alpha\beta}=\Lambda_{\alpha\beta}(-\mathbf{k}),

(115)

so that

\displaystyle H^{*}(-\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{\ast}(-\mathbf{k})\widetilde{H}^{*}(-\mathbf{k})\Lambda^{*}(-\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{\dagger}(\mathbf{k})\widetilde{H}(\mathbf{k})\Lambda(\mathbf{k})=H(\mathbf{k}).

(116)

Let the symmetries of the lattice include twofold rotation (in 2d) or inversion (in 3d) that squares to
+1
, so that the Hamiltonian satisfies

H(\mathbf{k})=C^{\dagger}H(-\mathbf{k})C

(117)

for some unitary matrix
C
satisfying

C^{2}=\openone=CC^{\dagger}\implies C=C^{\dagger}.

(118)

Thus, the time-reversed version of Eq. (
117
) becomes

H^{*}(\mathbf{k})=CH(\mathbf{k})C,

(119)

Complex conjugating both sides, we get

H(\mathbf{k})=C^{*}H^{*}(\mathbf{k})C^{*}=(C^{\ast}C)H(\mathbf{k})(C^{*}C)^{\dagger},

(120)

so that
H(\mathbf{k})
must commute with
C^{*}C
.
This is trivially satisfied if
C^{*}C=\pm\openone
, i.e., if
C
is purely real or purely imaginary.

The local-in-
\mathbf{k}
condition of Eq. (
119
) can be reduced to a reality condition on the Bloch Hamiltonian by defining a unitarily equivalent Hamiltonian

H^{\prime}(\mathbf{k})\equiv BH(\mathbf{k})B^{\dagger},

(121)

where
B
is unitary.
Eq. (
119
) then becomes

\displaystyle H^{\prime*}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=B^{\ast}\left(CH(\mathbf{k})C\right)B^{T}

\displaystyle=\left(B^{*}CB^{\dagger}\right)H^{\prime}(\mathbf{k})\left(B^{*}CB^{\dagger}\right)^{\dagger}.

(122)

Thus,
H^{\prime}(\mathbf{k})
is real if

B^{*}CB^{\dagger}=\openone\implies C=B^{T}B,

(123)

i.e.
, if
B
is a “square root” of
C
. We can readily define such a matrix
B
by working in the eigenbasis of
C
, where
B
is simply a diagonal matrix whose entries are square roots of the diagonal entries of
C
.

Appendix C
Homotopic classification of maps defined on CW complexes

In this Section, we describe the classification procedure outlined in Sec.
II.3
more rigorously.

CW-complex —

The fundamental domain of the
d
-dimensional Brillouin zone has the structure of a CW-complex
\mathbb{D}
. Formally,
\mathbb{D}
is a collection of “
p
-cells” with
0\leq p\leq d
, each of which is homotopic to
(0,1)^{p}
, or, equivalently, to a
p
-ball. The CW-complex structure further includes a gluing map, which, given a
p
-cell
\alpha
, assigns to its boundary
\partial\alpha
— homotopically equivalent to the
(p-1)
-sphere
S^{p-1}
— a collection of
q
-cells with
q<p
. Note that a given
q
-cell
\beta
may be glued multiple times to the boundary
\partial\alpha
. We define the boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}
of the CW complex as the union of boundaries of individual
d
-cells. Note that for a
d
-dimensional CW-complex
\mathbb{D}
with multiple
d
-cells, lower-dimensional cells at the boundary between two
d
-cells are also considered to be part of
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

Bloch Hamiltonians and loop spaces —

A Bloch Hamiltonian is defined as a map
h\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathcal{X}
, which constitutes of a set of continuous maps
\alpha\to\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}
for each
p
-cell
\alpha
, so that
h
is compatible with the CW-complex structure of
\mathbb{D}
. We denote the space of such maps by
\Omega^{0}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
.

For the classification procedure, we also need
m
-parameter families or “
m
-loops” of Bloch Hamiltonians. We recursively define such an
m
-loop
h^{m}
as a map
h^{m}:\mathbb{D}\times[0,1]^{m}\to\mathcal{X}
that is compatible with the CW-complex structure of
\mathbb{D}
for each fixed
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in[0,1]^{m}
and which for
m\geq 1
satisfies the condition

h^{m}(\mathbf{k};t_{1},\ldots,t_{m-1},t_{m})=h^{m-1}_{\rm ref}(\mathbf{k};t_{1},\ldots,t_{m-1}),

(152)

with a
h^{m-1}_{\rm ref}
a reference
(m-1)
-loop if
m>1
and a reference Bloch Hamiltonian
h^{0}_{\rm ref}\equiv h_{\rm ref}
if
m=1
. With this notation, a Bloch Hamiltonian can be considered a “
0
-loop”. The space of such
m
-loops is denoted
\Omega^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
. We can similarly define the loop spaces for
\partial\mathbb{D}
and any other subcomplex of
\mathbb{D}
. Our end goal is the computation of the homotopy set
\pi_{0}[\Omega^{0}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})]
enumerating homotopy classes of Bloch Hamiltonians. However, we will need homotopy sets of
m
-loops as an intermediate step.

Classification on
\partial\mathbb{D}
—

The computation of
\pi_{0}[\Omega^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})]
is simplified by the observation that two maps
g^{m},h^{m}\in\Omega^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
cannot be topologically equivalent if the restrictions
g^{m}\rvert_{\partial\mathbb{D}},h^{m}\rvert_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\in\Omega^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
are topologically inequivalent. Furthermore, not all
h^{m}_{\partial}\in\Omega^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
can occur as the boundary restriction of some
h^{m}\in\Omega^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
. To see this, consider a
d
-cell
\alpha
. Since
\alpha
is homotopically isomorphic to the
d
-ball,
\partial\alpha
is homtopically equivalent to
S^{d-1}
, so that the restriction
h^{m}\rvert_{\partial\alpha}
defines a map
S^{d-1}\to\Omega^{m}(\mathcal{X}_{\alpha})
. Since
\alpha
is contractible,
h^{m}\rvert_{\partial\alpha}
must be trivial in
\pi_{d-1}[\Omega^{m}(\mathcal{X}_{\alpha})]
if there exists a
h^{m}\in\Omega^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
such that
h^{m}_{\partial}
is the boundary restriction
h^{m}
. We refer to this restriction as a
compatibility condition
. We denote the subspace of
\Omega^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
that satisfies the compatibility condition for all
d
-cells by
\Omega^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\rvert_{0}
.

Being in
\Omega^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\rvert_{0}
is not only
necessary
, but also a
sufficient
condition for a
h^{m}_{\partial}\in\Omega^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
to be the boundary restriction of some
h^{m}\in\Omega^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
. This is because satisfying the compatibility condition implies that for each
d
-cell
\alpha\in\mathbb{D}
there exists a homotopy
H_{\partial}^{m}:\partial\alpha\times[0,1]\to\mathcal{X}
that interpolates between
h_{\partial}^{m}
and the trivial (
i.e.
, constant) map. Using the homotopic equivalence of
\alpha
and the
p
-ball, we can interpret
H_{\partial}^{m}
as an extension of
h_{\partial}^{m}
to the full
d
-cell
\alpha
. Continuing this for each
\alpha
, we can thus define an extension of
h_{\partial}^{m}
to
\Omega^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
.

We next compute the equivalence classes of
\Omega^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
for a fixed homotopic class on
\partial\mathbb{D}
,
i.e.
, for a fixed element of
\pi_{0}\!\left[\,\Omega^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\rvert_{0}\,\right]
. We denote the corresponding subspaces of
\Omega^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
and
\Omega^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\rvert_{0}
by
\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
and
\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
, respectively. Hence, by construction
\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
is a connected space. The remaining classification problem amounts to the computation of
\pi_{0}[\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})]
, which enumerates the connected components of
\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
.

Classification on
\mathbb{D}
—

We choose a reference
m
-loop
h^{m}_{\partial,{\rm ref}}\in\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
and define
\overline{\Omega}_{\rm ref}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
as the set of maps
h^{m}
satisfying
h^{m}\rvert_{\partial\mathbb{D}}=h^{m}_{\partial,{\rm ref}}\forall(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in[0,1]^{m}
. If we further fix a reference
m
-loop
h^{m}_{\text{ref}}\in\overline{\Omega}_{\rm ref}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
, then for any
h^{m}\in\overline{\Omega}_{\rm ref}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
and any
d
-cell
\alpha
, the pair
(h^{m},h^{m}_{\text{ref}})
defines a map from
(\alpha\times\alpha)\times[0,1]^{m}\to\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}
. Since
h^{m}
and
h^{m}_{\text{ref}}
agree on the boundary of
\alpha
, we can identify them on
\partial\alpha
, thereby forming an
m
-loop defined on the
d
-sphere
S^{d}
. Such
m
-loops can be classified by

\pi_{d}[\Omega^{m}(\mathcal{X}_{\alpha})]\cong\pi_{m+d}[\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}],

(153)

where we have used the Freudenthal suspension theorem
(
Hatcher 2002
, Corollary 4.24)
. Combining classification spaces from all
d
-cells in
\mathbb{D}
, we get

\pi_{0}[\overline{\Omega}_{\rm ref}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})]=\prod_{d-{\rm cells}\,\alpha\in\mathbb{D}}\pi_{m+d}[\mathcal{X}_{\alpha}].

(154)

To compute
\pi_{0}[\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})]
, we use that the natural inclusion
i\colon\overline{\Omega}^{m}_{\rm ref}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\hookrightarrow\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
induces a map

\pi_{0}\!\left[\overline{\Omega}^{m}_{\rm ref}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\right]\xrightarrow{\;\;\;i_{*}\;}\pi_{0}\!\left[\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\right].

(155)

This map is surjective, because, by construction, the restriction
h^{m}|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}
of any
m
-loop
h^{m}\in\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
can always be continuously deformed to
h^{m}_{\partial,{\rm ref}}
and ths deformation can be extended to a homotopy between the full
m
-loop
h^{m}
and an
m
-loop in
\overline{\Omega}_{\rm ref}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
using the construction of Fig.
3
. Since

is surjective, it follows that

\pi_{0}\!\left[\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\right]\cong\pi_{0}\!\left[\overline{\Omega}^{m}_{\rm ref}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\right]/\sim,

(156)

where
\sim
denotes the equivalence classes of elements of
\pi_{0}\![\overline{\Omega}^{m}_{\rm ref}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})]
whose images under

are identical.

on

Quotienting out the boundary deformations —

To characterize the equivalence classes defined in Eq. (
156
), we first consider a pointed loop
\gamma^{m}
in
\overline{\Omega}^{m+1}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
with the reference
h^{m}_{\partial,{\rm ref}}
,
i.e.
, a family of
m
-loops
\gamma^{m}(t)\in\Omega^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
parameterized by

, such that
\gamma^{m}(0)=\gamma^{m}(1)=h^{m}_{\partial,{\rm ref}}
. Given

, we can “attach”

to

as shown graphically in Fig.

for one- and two-dimensional CW-complexes. We term this process a

of

.

More formally, boundary deformation corresponding to the pointed loop

induces the map

\overline{\Omega}^{m}_{\rm ref}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\xrightarrow{\;\;\;b_{\gamma}\;}\overline{\Omega}^{m}_{\rm ref}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X}).

(157)

This further induces a map between the homotopy sets

\pi_{0}\!\left[\overline{\Omega}^{m}_{\text{ref}}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\right]\xrightarrow{\;\;\;b_{\gamma\ast}\;}\pi_{0}\!\left[\overline{\Omega}^{m}_{\text{ref}}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\right].

(158)

This map is well defined, since if

are homotopically equivalent, they remain so after identical boundary deformations.
Since any loop
\gamma^{m}
has an inverse obtained by traversing it in the opposite direction,

is a bijection.

We now show that for
g^{m}
,
h^{m}\in\overline{\Omega}_{\rm ref}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
, the topological equivalence classes
[g^{m}]\sim[h^{m}]
under the equivalence relation of Eq. (
156
)
if and only if

\gamma^{m}\in\overline{\Omega}^{m+1}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
such that

. To this end, we first note that attaching the loop
\gamma^{m}
to

naturally provides a homotopy between

and

in
\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
(but not necessarily in
\overline{\Omega}^{m}_{\rm ref}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
), so that the images in
\pi_{0}\![\overline{\Omega}^{m}_{\text{ref}}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})]
of the topological equivalence classes
[h^{m}]
and
b_{\gamma\ast}[h^{m}]
under

are identical. To show that the converse is also true, we consider
g^{m}
,
h^{m}\in\overline{\Omega}^{m}_{\text{ref}}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
such that
i_{\ast}[g^{m}]=i_{\ast}[h^{m}]
,

,

and
h^{m}
are homotopically equivalent in
\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
. Thus, there exists a homotopy

such that

Since the boundary restrictions of

and

both equal

, the restriction

defines a boundary deformation in
\overline{\Omega}^{m+1}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
. Setting
\gamma^{m}\equiv I^{m}\rvert_{\partial\mathbb{D}}
, the existence of

implies that

can be homotopically deformed into

,
i.e.
,
b_{\gamma\ast}\colon[g^{m}]\mapsto[h^{m}]
.

From the definition of

we note that

depends only on the homotopic equivalence class of

. These homotopic equivalence classes are classified by

\pi_{1}\!\left[\overline{\Omega}^{m}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\right]\cong\pi_{0}\!\left[\overline{\Omega}^{m+1}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\right].

(159)

Hence, to compute the equivalence class under
\sim
, it is sufficient to consider the induced map

for one boundary deformation
\gamma^{m}
of each equivalence class in
\pi_{0}\![\overline{\Omega}^{m+1}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})]
.

The full classification —

We can complete the classification by computing
\pi_{0}[\overline{\Omega}^{0}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})]
using Eqns. (
154
) and (
156
) for each element of
\pi_{0}\![\,\Omega^{0}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\rvert_{0}\,]
,
i.e.
, for each possible lower-dimensional invariant. This requires knowledge of
\pi_{0}\!\left[\,\Omega^{0}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\rvert_{0}\,\right]
,
i.e.
., of the homotopic classification on boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}
. Further, as discussed above, to evaluate the quotient in Eq. (
156
), we need to know
\pi_{0}\!\left[\Omega^{1}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\right]
. Since
\partial\mathbb{D}
is itself a
(d-1)
-dimensional CW complex, both
\pi_{0}\![\,\Omega^{0}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\rvert_{0}\,]
and
\pi_{0}\![\Omega^{1}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})]
can be calculated using the procedure described above. Proceeding recursively, we get classification sets with increasing loop order
m
, while lowering the dimension of the CW-complexes. We eventually end up with a 0-dimensional CW-complex,
i.e.
, a disjoint set of points, the classification on which can be performed by elementary means.

This recursive procedure is greatly simplified by noting that it is not necessary to precisely determine
\pi_{0}\!\left[\Omega^{m+1}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})\right]
in order to obtain

from Eq. (

, thereby terminating the recursive procedure after one step. This simplification is possible because

is surjective and it is sufficient to consider a collection of boundary deformations
\gamma^{m}\in\overline{\Omega}^{m+1}(\partial\mathbb{D},\mathcal{X})
that is large enough to include
at least one
boundary deformation in each topological equivalence class.

Appendix D
Homotopy groups of Grassmannians

The Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{F}}(m,n)
is a manifold that parameterize the
n
-dimenional linear subspaces of a
(m+n)
-dimensional vector space over the field
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{H}
. They can be explicitly written as a quotient of groups as

\mathrm{Gr}_{F}(m,n)=\frac{G(m+n)}{G(m)\times G(n)},

(160)

where
G
denotes the orthogonal, unitary or symplectic group for
F=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{H}
, respectively. The homotopy spaces of the Grassmannians can thus be computed by starting with the short exact sequence of groups

\displaystyle 1\to G(m)\times G(n)\to G(m+n)\to\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{F}}(m,n)\to 1.

This defines the long exact sequence of homotopy spaces
(
Hatcher 2002
, Sec 4.2)

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle\to\pi_{p}[G(m)]\times\pi_{p}[G(n)]\to\pi_{p}[G(m+n)]

\displaystyle\to\pi_{p}[\mathrm{Gr}_{F}(m,n)]\to\pi_{p-1}[G(m)]\times\pi_{p-1}[G(n)]\to\dots

The homotopy groups of
G(n)
for
G=\mathrm{O,U,}\text{ and }\mathrm{Sp}
are well-known. They can all be computed in terms of the known homotopy groups of spheres using the short exact sequence
(
Hatcher 2002
, Sec 4.2, Example 4.55)

1\to G(n)\to G(n+1)\to S^{r(n)}\to 1,

(161)

where
r(n)=n,2n+1,
and
4n+3
for
G=\mathrm{O,U,}\text{ and }\mathrm{Sp}
, respectively. This leads to a long-exact sequence of homotopies

\displaystyle\dots\to\pi_{p}[G(n)]\to\pi_{p}[G(n+1)]\to\pi_{p}[S^{r(n)}]\to\dots

But since
\pi_{p}[S^{k}]=0
for
k>p
, this long exact sequence splits into

0\to\pi_{p}[G(n)]\to\pi_{p}[G(n+1)]\to 0.

for
p>r(n)
. Thus,
\pi_{p}[G(n)]\cong\pi_{p}[G(n+1)]
for
p>r(n)
, which is the stabilization of the homotopy groups of
G(n)
for
n\to\infty
.

Appendix E
The Hopf invariant

The continuous maps from the 3-sphere
S^{3}
to the complex Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(1,1)\cong\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}\cong S^{2}
are classified by the third homotopy group

\pi_{3}[\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(1,1)]=\pi_{3}[S^{2}]=\mathbb{Z}.

(162)

The corresponding topological invariant is termed the
Hopf invariant

\mathrm{H}
. For a given map
h\colon S^{3}\to S^{2}
, it can be calculated as the linking number of the preimages under
h
of two distinct points in
S^{2}
.

More explicitly, given a point
x\in S^{2}
, the preimage
\gamma=h^{-1}(x)
defines a (single or multiple) closed loop(s). We take two distinct points
x^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime\prime}
infinitesimally close to
x
and denote their preimages under
h
by
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
, respectively. We then assign a directionality to
\gamma
for each point on it by computing the cross product of the vectors pointing towards
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
. The same direction may be assigned to
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
, as these curves are (infinitesimally) close to
\gamma
. The Hopf number
\mathrm{H}
is then given by the linking number of the directed curves
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
. Note that
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
only serves to define the direction along
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
, but is otherwise not involved in the the calculation of the linking number.

An example of a map with
\mathrm{H}=1
can be constructed by taking
S^{3}
as the 3-ball
B^{3}=\{\mathbf{r}|\,|\mathbf{r}|\leq 1\}
with its surface identified to a single point. On
B^{3}
, we then consider the “band structure”

h(\mathbf{r})=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi\mathbf{r}\cdot\bm{\tau}}h_{0}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\mathbf{r}\cdot\bm{\tau}},

(163)

where the
\tau_{\alpha}
,
\alpha=1,2,3
are the Pauli matrices and
h_{0}=\mathbf{h}_{0}\cdot\bm{\tau}
is a fixed gapped Hamiltonian. More explicitly,

h(\mathbf{r})=\cos(2\pi r)h_{0}-\sin(2\pi r)\mathbf{h}_{0}\times\mathbf{r}

(164)

Setting
h_{0}=\tau_{3}
in the following, the preimages
\gamma
,
\gamma^{\prime}
, and
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
of
h=-\tau_{3}
,
h^{\prime}=-\tau_{3}+\pi^{2}\eta\tau_{2}
, and
h^{\prime\prime}=-\tau_{3}+\pi^{2}\eta\tau_{1}
, with
\eta\downarrow 0
a positive infinitesimal are the curves parameterized by

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}(\phi)=

\displaystyle\,(1/2)(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0),

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(\phi)=

\displaystyle\,(1+\eta\cos\phi)\mathbf{r}(\phi)+\eta\sin\phi(0,0,1),

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime}(\phi)=

\displaystyle\,(1-\eta\sin\phi)\,\mathbf{r}(\phi)+\eta\cos\phi(0,0,1),

(165)

with
0\leq\phi<2\pi
.
The curves are directed in the positive
\phi
direction. The Hopf invariant
\mathrm{H}=1
is the linking number of the curves
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
.

The Chern number

The

In the following, we consider the Chern number over the 2-cell for a two-dimensional system with spinless time-reversal symmetry (symmetry class AI). Consider a general boundary deformation

H_{\partial}(\mathbf{k}_{\partial},t)=\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}H^{\ast}_{\partial}(-\mathbf{k}_{\partial},t)\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\dagger}

{W}_{\partial}(\mathbf{k}_{\partial})

{W}_{\partial}(\mathbf{k}_{\partial})={W}_{\partial}^{*}(-\mathbf{k}_{\partial})

(166)

Thus, the windings from boundary segments related by time-reversal
add up
. This observation immediately allows one to construct boundary deformations with even Chern numbers.

To construct a boundary deformation with odd Chern numbers, we choose a basis where
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{T}}=\openone
, so that the Hamiltonian is real at the high-symmetry points
S
. The corresponding Wilson loops are orthogonal, so that
\det{W}_{\partial}(\mathbf{k}_{S})\in\{\pm 1\}
. If
\det{W}(\mathbf{k}_{S})=-1
at an odd number of 0-cells, then
{W}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp})
must have an odd winding number. This is illustrated in Fig.
16
for a minimal boundary transformation, which has winding number
1
.

The existence of such a deformation can be traced back to nontrivial topology at one of the 0-cells, since
\pi_{1}[\mathcal{X}_{S}]=\pi_{1}[\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{R}}(n_{\mathrm{o}},n_{\mathrm{e}})]\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
in the stable limit. Thus, we can construct a boundary deformation carrying a nonzero Chern number that is constant everywhere except in a neighborhood of a single high-symmetry point
S
. We denote the 1d coordinate along the boundary near
S
by
\theta
such that
S
lies at
\theta=0
, and define a deformation
H_{\partial}(\theta,t)
as
\tau_{3}
(independent of
t
) for
\left|\theta\right|\geq\pi
and

\displaystyle H_{\partial}(\theta,t)=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi t\Gamma\left(\theta/2\right)}\tau_{3}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi t\Gamma\left(\theta/2\right)},

(167)

for
\theta\in(-\pi,\pi)
, where
\Gamma(\theta)=\tau_{2}\cos\theta-\tau_{3}\sin\theta
. We can verify that

H_{\partial}(\pm\pi,t)=H_{\partial}(\theta,0)=H_{\partial}(\theta,1)=\tau_{3}

and
H^{\ast}_{\partial}(\theta,t)=H_{\partial}(-\theta,t)
, so that the boundary constraint and the time-reversal symmetry are satisfied. The wavefunction for the occupied band is given by

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{o}}(\theta,t)=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi t\Gamma\left(\theta/2\right)}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi t\sin\left(\theta/2\right)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi t\cos\left(\theta/2\right)\tau_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

The corresponding Wilson loop is

{W}^{\mathrm{o}}(\theta)=-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\sin\left(\theta/2\right)}.

(168)

Thus, at the high symmetry point
\theta=0
, we have
{W}^{\mathrm{o}}(0)=-1
. Upon taking
\theta
over the interval
[-\pi,\pi]
, the phase of
{W}^{o}(\theta)
winds once, corresponding to a Chern number
+1
.

F.2
The second Euler/Stiefel-Whitney number

The
\mathbb{Z}
-valued second Euler invariants
\mathrm{Eu}_{2}^{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}
are the level-2 parent invariants when the Hamiltonian is real-valued and the occupied/empty subspace consists of exactly two bands. In the following, we construct a boundary deformation that is nontrivial only in the neighborhood a high-symmetry point and carries a nonzero second Euler invariant. Such a deformation thus adds integers of the same parity to
\mathrm{Eu}_{2}^{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}
. This boundary deformation is relevant for
{\mathrm{C}}_{2}
and
{\mathrm{C}}_{4}
symmetric band structure, whereby the rotation symmetry relates the two 1-cells adjacent to the high-symmetry point.

Explicitly, we assume that the rotation operator at the high symmetry point
C_{S}
, restricted to the subspace of interest
C_{S}^{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}
, contains one positive and one negative eigenvalue. We work in the eigenbasis of the rotation operator, so that
C_{S}=\rho_{3}\otimes\tau_{0}
, where
\rho_{\mu}
and
\tau_{\mu}
are Pauli matrices. We again choose the 1d coordinate along the 1-cells near
S
by
\theta
, with
S
located at
\theta=0
. We then consider a boundary deformation

\displaystyle H^{\pm}_{t}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\pi/4)\sin\left(\theta/2\right)\Sigma_{12}}\,h^{\pm}_{t}(\theta)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\pi/4)\sin\left(\theta/2\right)\Sigma_{12}},

(169)

where
\Sigma_{12}=\rho_{1}\otimes\tau_{2}-\rho_{2}\otimes\tau_{1}
and

h^{\prime\pm}_{t}(\theta)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi t\Gamma_{\pm}(\theta)}(\rho_{0}\otimes\tau_{3})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi t\Gamma_{\pm}(\theta)}

(170)

with

\displaystyle\Gamma_{-}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\,\rho_{2}\otimes\tau_{0}\sin(\theta/2)+\rho_{3}\otimes\tau_{2}\cos(\theta/2),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{+}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\,\rho_{2}\otimes\tau_{3}\sin(\theta/2)+\rho_{0}\otimes\tau_{2}\cos(\theta/2).

(171)

One verifies that
H^{\pm}_{t}(\theta)=\rho_{3}H_{t}(-\theta)\rho_{3}
and that
H^{\pm}_{0}(\theta)=H^{\pm}_{1}(\theta)
. At the high-symmetry point
\theta=0
,
H^{\pm}_{t}(\theta)
satisfies the symmetry constraints and has one occupied and one unoccupied band of each symmetry type. At
\theta=\pm\pi
, one has
H^{\pm}_{t}(\pi)=\rho_{3}\otimes\tau_{0}
, independent of
t
. As a function of
t
, the two blocks of
H^{\pm}
at
\theta=0
have winding numbers 1 and
\pm 1
. To find the Wilson loops at fixed
\theta
, we note that they are the same for
H^{\pm}
and
h^{\pm}
, since
H^{\pm}
and
h^{\pm}
differ only by a
t
-independent basis transformation. Hence,

\displaystyle{W}_{\pm}^{\mathrm{o}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{e}^{\mp i\pi\rho_{2}\sin(\theta/2)},

\displaystyle{W}_{\pm}^{\mathrm{e}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\rho_{2}\sin(\theta/2)}.

(172)

At
\theta=0
, all Wilson loops are equal to
-\rho_{0}
, which is topologically nontrivial and consistent with the winding numbers of
h^{\pm}
being odd. Looking at the full family of Wilson loops
{W}^{\mathrm{o}/\mathrm{e}}(\theta)
for
-\pi\leq\theta\leq\pi
, we see that
H^{\pm}
generates winding number pairs
(1,1)
and
(-1,1)
for occupied and unoccupied orbitals, thereby yielding the desired result.

F.3
The Hopf invariant

The
\mathbb{Z}
-valued Hopf invariant is the parent invariant at level-3 when the Hamiltonian is complex-valued on the 3-cell and
n_{\mathrm{o}}=n_{\mathrm{e}}=1
. In the following, we describe boundary deformations that may affect
\mathrm{H}
.

No additional symmetries —

In this case, the effect of boundary deformations on
\mathrm{H}
depends on the level-2 invariants,
i.e.
, the Chern numbers
{\mathrm{C}}_{x,y,z}
. To describe a general deformations, we first note that while the preimages of points for the Hopf map
S^{3}\to S^{2}
are closed curves in
S^{3}
, those for a 3-cell may start and end at the boundaries of
\mathbb{D}
. The difference between the number of times a component of a preimage
\gamma
starts and ends at the 2-cell perpendicular to the coordinate direction
\alpha=x
,
y
,
z
is equal to the Chern number
\mathrm{C}_{\alpha}
. Since
\mathrm{H}
is given by the linking number of a pair of preimages, we must construct a deformation that changes this linking number.

In Fig.
17
, we depict such a deformation for the case of
\mathrm{C}_{x}=\mathrm{C}_{y}=0
,
\mathrm{C}_{z}=2
, which implies the existence of two pairs of directed segments along
z
. We can then define a boundary deformation of the vertical faces, so that the deformation of each 1-cell carries a Chern number
+1
. This necessitates the existence of a pair of closed preimage curves, along the boundary. These curves encircle the two curve pairs running along
z
, thereby changing the linking number by
2\mathrm{C}_{z}
. A similar deformation can be defined for Chern numbers in the other directions, so that the Hopf invariant can be changed by
n=2\mbox{gcd}(\mathrm{C}_{x},\mathrm{C}_{y},\mathrm{C}_{z})
.

Figure 17:

C_{z}=2
. The square represents a fixed-
k_{z}
cut of the 3-cell. The Chern number implies the existence of two directed segment pairs (shown red and blue) along the

.

With time-reversal symmetry —

We explicitly construct a boundary deformation that changes
\mathrm{H}
by one and is nontrivial only in the vicinity of a high symmetry point
S
at
\mathbf{k}_{S}=(0,0,\pi)
. We introduce polar coordinates
(\rho,\phi)
in the
k_{x}
-
k_{y}
plane with the origin at
S
and define a deformation
H_{t}(\rho,\phi)
that is independent of
t
for
\rho>1
. Explicitly, we take

H_{t}(\rho,\phi)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\Gamma(t,\rho,\phi)}\tau_{3}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi\Gamma(t,\rho,\phi)},

(173)

with

\Gamma(t,\rho,\phi)=\cos(\pi t)\tau_{2}+\rho\sin(\pi t)(\tau_{1}\cos\phi+\tau_{3}\sin\phi),

(174)

This deformation then meets all required constraints,
H_{t}(\rho,\phi)=\tau_{3}
for
t=0
, 1 and for
\rho=1
and
H_{t}(\rho,\phi)=H_{t}(\rho,\phi+\pi)^{*}
. The boundary deformation
H_{t}(\rho,\phi)
is a continuous deformation of the band structure
h
of Eq. (
163
). Hence, it has unit Hopf number.
This can be seen by noting that
H_{t}(\rho,\phi)
is a deformation of the matrix-valued function
h(\mathbf{r})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\mathbf{r}\cdot\bm{\tau}}\tau_{3}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi\mathbf{r}\cdot\bm{\tau}}
, which is defined on the 3-ball
|\mathbf{r}|\leq 1
and which the paradigmatic example of a map with unit Hopf number.

With
{\mathrm{C}}_{2}{\mathrm{T}}
symmetry —

In this case, the Hamiltonian is real for
k_{z}=0
and
k_{z}=\pi
. On the one hand, this implies that the Chern number
C_{z}=0
, so that boundary deformations along the
xz
and
yz
faces of the fundamental domain do not change the Hopf number. On the other hand, this prohibits a boundary deformation along the
xy
and
xz
faces or along the
xy
and
yz
faces to nucleate a closed curve pair that encloses the fundamental domain. Hence, neither boundary deformations can change the Hopf number, so that Hopf number is a robust topological index if
n_{\mathrm{o}}=n_{\mathrm{e}}=1
.

With
{\mathrm{C}}_{2}
or
{\mathrm{C}}_{4}
symmetry —

In this case, there can be a curve pair penetrating the fundamental domain in the
k_{z}
direction only, so that boundary deformations associated with faces parallel to the
k_{z}
axis cannot change the parent Hopf number. Hence, with broken time-reversal symmetry the Hopf number is defined modulo
2\mathrm{C}
,
\mathrm{C}
being the Chern number associated with a plane at constant
k_{z}
.
With time-reversal symmetry, the Hamiltonian is real for
k_{z}=0
and
k_{z}=\pi
, which also rules out curve pairs penetrating the fundamental domain in the
k_{z}
direction. Hence, in this case the Hopf invariant is an integer.
