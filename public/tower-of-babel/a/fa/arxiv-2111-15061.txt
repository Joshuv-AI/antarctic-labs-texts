Phase transition of an anisotropic Ginzburg–Landau equation
Yuning Liu

Abstract
. We study the effective geometric motions of an anisotropic Ginzburg–Landau equation with a small parameter ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 which characterizes the width of the transition layer. For well-prepared initial datum, we show that as ε \varepsilon tends to zero the solutions will develop a sharp interface limit which evolves under mean curvature flow. The bulk limits of the solutions correspond to a vector field 𝐮 ⁡ ( x , t ) \mathbf{u}(x,t) which is of unit length on one side of the interface, and is zero on the other side. The proof combines the modulated energy method and weak convergence methods. In particular, by a (boundary) blow-up argument we show that 𝐮 \mathbf{u} must be tangent to the sharp interface. Moreover, it solves a geometric evolution equation for the Oseen–Frank model in liquid crystals. Keywords: modulated energy methods, weak convergence methods, blow-up analysis, mean curvature flow, phase-transition. Mathematical Subject Classification : 53E10, 35R35, 35K58 35K57.



1.
Introduction

In the study of liquid crystals one often encounters elastic energies with anisotropy, i.e. energies with distinct coefficients multiplying the square of the divergence and
the curl of the order parameters. Typical examples involve the Oseen–Frank model
[
30
]
, Ericksen’s model
[
44
,
43
]
and the Landau–De Gennes model
[
5
]
. From a microscopic point of view, the anisotropy of these models can be interpreted as excluded volume potential of molecular interaction, cf.
[
29
]
. Anisotropic models also arise in the theory of superconductivity, cf.
[
10
]
.
The anisotropy brings various new challenges to the studies of both variational problems and their gradient flows of the aforementioned models.
In contrast to the convergence analysis of isotropic models, i.e. the (scalar) Allen–Cahn equations (cf.
[
19
,
34
,
6
,
53
,
54
,
48
,
50
,
47
]
), the powerful analytic tools such as maximum principle and monotonicity formula are not readily established for anisotropic ones.

The attempt of
this work is to study an anisotropic system modeling the isotropic-nematic phase transition of a liquid crystal droplet. Let
d\in\{2,3\}
be the dimension of the physical domain
\Omega
with
C^{3}
boundary
\partial\Omega
.
We consider the anisotropic Ginzburg–Landau type energy

(1.1)

A_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mu|\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{u}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\mathbf{u})\right)\,dx.

Here
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}):\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
is the order parameter describing the state of the system. The function
F(\mathbf{u})
is a double equal-well potential which permits the isotropic-nematic phase transition. More precisely, it attains its
global minimum value
0
at
\{0\}\cup\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
An example of
F
is the Chern–Simons-Higgs model

F(\mathbf{u})=|\mathbf{u}|^{2}(1-|\mathbf{u}|^{2})^{2}
. See for instance
[
31
,
36
]
for the physics and

[
9
,
27
,
28
]
for the mathematical analysis of related variational problems.
The parameter
\varepsilon>0
denotes the relative intensity of elastic and bulk energy, which is usually quite small. The parameter
\mu>0
is material dependent which measures the degree of anisotropy.

The energy (
1.1
) is a simplified case of the full Landau–De Gennes energy (cf.
[
35
,
45
]
).
The variational investigations of
the isotropic-nematic phase transition involving (
1.1
) were first done by Golovaty, Novack, Sternberg and Venkatraman
[
27
,
28
]
in the static case in 2D. The present paper is concerned with the
L^{2}
-gradient flow of (
1.1
), i.e. the following system.

(1.2a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\mu\nabla(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}DF(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle~\text{in}~\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.2b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,0)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{in}(x)

\displaystyle~\text{in}~\Omega,

(1.2c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle~\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),

where
DF(\mathbf{u})
is the gradient of
F(\mathbf{u})
with respect to
\mathbf{u}
.
We shall study the small
\varepsilon
-asymptotics of this system with well-prepared initial datum
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{in}
that undergoes a sharp transition across a co-dimensional one interface
I_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We shall show that the energy density
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})
will be concentrated on a mean curvature flow
I:=\bigcup_{t\geqslant 0}I_{t}\times\{t\}
starting from
I_{0}
, namely

(1.3)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})\right)\,dx=\sigma\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(I_{t}),

where
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
is the
(d-1)
dimensional Hausdorff measure, and
\sigma
is a positive constant depending on
F
.
Moreover, we shall derive bulk limit
\mathbf{u}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}
away from
I_{t}
and its boundary condition on
I_{t}
.

I_{t}
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Omega_{t}^{+}}
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Omega_{t}^{-}}
\partial\Omega

Figure 1.
I_{t}
is the interface,
\Omega_{t}^{+}
is the nematic phase and
\Omega_{t}^{-}
is the isotropic phase.

System (
1.2a
) is a vectorial and anisotropic generalization of the scalar parabolic Allen–Cahn equation. In the scalar case, there have been many developments on its co-dimensional one limit to the (two-phase) mean curvature flow during the last two decades. Here we mention two classes of results and postpone the discussions of some others in the sequel. One is the convergence to a Brakke’s flow by Ilmanen
[
34
]
using a version of Huisken’s monotonicity formula
[
32
]
and tools from geometric measure theory. See also
[
11
,
33
,
54
,
48
,
47
,
50
]
and the references therein for further renovations. Despite of its energetic nature, a major difficulty of such an approach is the control of the so-called
discrepancy measure
, and almost all existing literatures using this approach rely crucially on a version of Modica’s maximum principle
[
46
]
.
However, it is not clear whether Modica’s maximum principle holds for elliptic/parabolic systems.
Another approach, which relies more on parabolic comparison principle, is the global in time convergence towards the viscosity solution built by Chen–Giga-Goto
[
13
]
and independently by Evans–Spruck
[
20
]
. Such an approach has been implemented by Evans–Soner–Souganidis
[
19
]
. One can also refer to
[
6
,
53
]
and the references therein for further discussions. These two approaches both give global in time (weak) convergences to weakly defined solutions of the mean curvature flow (up to their extinction times). However, as their technics involve parabolic maximum principle in one way or another, it is not clear how to use them to attack vectorial models in general. It is worth mentioning that for radially symmetric initial datum, Bronsard–Stoth
[
8
]
have obtained global in time convergence to the mean curvature flow of planar circles.

To the best of our knowledge, there are mainly two approaches to rigorously justify the convergence of the vectorial Allen–Cahn equations, both assuming that the limiting interface motion has a (local in time) classical solution. Compared with the aforementioned methods, which lead to global in time (weak) convergence, they have quite different natures.
The first approach is the asymptotic expansion technics developed by De Mottoni–Schatzman
[
15
]
and by Alikakos–Bates–Chen
[
1
]
. It has been used recently by Fei–Wang–Zhang–Zhang
[
22
]
to study the isotropic-nematic phase transition in liquid crystals, and by Fei–Lin–Wang–Zhang
[
21
]
to study matrix-valued Allen–Cahn equations.
The second approach, which also assumes a classical solution of the limiting interface motion (but not the limiting flows in the bulk regions), is the modulated energy method developed by Fischer–Laux–Simon
[
24
]
. Such a method is motivated by Jerrard–Smets
[
37
]
and Fischer–Hensel
[
23
]
, and has been generalized to a matrix–valued model by Laux–Liu
[
40
]
.

In the present work, we shall use the methods employed in
[
24
,
40
]
to derive the energy convergence (
1.3
) and the bulk limit
\mathbf{u}=\lim_{\varepsilon_{k}\to 0}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon_{k}}
by establishing two modulated energy inequalities. Moreover, the derivation of the anchoring boundary condition of
\mathbf{u}
(see (
1.18c
) below) uses a blow-up argument, which is inspired by a recent work of Lin–Wang
[
43
]
.
There the authors have studied isotropic-nematic phase transitions in the static case based on an anisotropic Ericksen’s model.

To state the main result, we assume that

(1.4)

\begin{split}I=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}I_{t}\times\{t\}~\text{ is a smoothly evolving}\\
(d-1)\text{-dimensional submanifold in}~\Omega,\end{split}

starting from a
(d-1)
-dimensional submanifold
I_{0}\subset\Omega
.
Here a
(d-1)
-submanifold refers to an embedded closed smooth surface when
d=3
and curve when
d=2
.

Let
\Omega^{+}_{t}
be the domain enclosed by
I_{t}
, and
d_{I}(x,t)
be the signed-distance from
x
to
I_{t}
which takes negative values in
\Omega^{-}_{t}
, and positive values in
\Omega^{+}_{t}=\Omega\backslash\overline{\Omega^{-}_{t}}
. Equivalently,

(1.5)

\Omega^{\pm}_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega\mid d_{I}(x,t)\gtrless 0\}.

For
\delta>0
, the (open)
\delta
-neighborhood of
I_{t}
is denoted by

(1.6)

B_{\delta}(I_{t}):=\{x\in\Omega\mid|d_{I}(x,t)|<\delta\}.

Let
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
be a sufficiently small number so that the nearest point projection

P_{I}(\cdot,t):B_{4\delta_{0}}(I_{t})\rightarrow I_{t}

is smooth for any
t\in[0,T]
, and that the interface (
1.4
) stays at least
4\delta_{0}
distant away from the physical boundary
\partial\Omega
. A further description of the geometry can be found in Subsection
2.2
or in
[
12
]
.

The first step to study the singular limit of (
1.2
) is to construct a modulated energy which encodes a distance between the energy in (
1.1
) and an energy corresponding to the moving interface
I_{t}
in (
1.4
).
Following
[
37
,
23
,
24
]
, we define an extension of the inward normal vector
\mathbf{n}(\cdot,t)
of
I_{t}
by

\boldsymbol{\xi}(x,t):=\phi\left(\frac{d_{I}(x,t)}{\delta_{0}}\right)\nabla d_{I}(x,t)\quad\text{ for }x\in\Omega,

where
\phi\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R};[0,1])
is an appropriate cut-off function (see (
2.11
) below for its precise definition).
Now we introduce

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|I](t):=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mu|\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)|^{2}\,dx

(1.7)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}{F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t))}-\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\right)\,dx,

where

\psi_{\varepsilon}
is defined by

(1.8)

\displaystyle\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,t):=\int_{0}^{|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)|}g(s)\,ds.

We shall work with a class of potentials
F(\mathbf{u})
under standard assumptions (see e.g.
[
34
,
11
]
). That is,

(1.9)

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{u})=f(|\mathbf{u}|)=g^{2}(|\mathbf{u}|)/2,

where
f
is a double equal-well potential, namely,

(1.10a)

\displaystyle f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}),\quad f(s)>0\text{ for }s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\backslash\{0,1\},

(1.10b)

\displaystyle g\geqslant 0\text{ and is {locally} Lipschitz continuous},~g(0)=g(1)=0.

Moreover, the following structural assumptions on
f
are made:

(1.11a)

\displaystyle\exists s_{0}\in(0,1)\text{ s.t. }f^{\prime}(s)>0\text{ on }(0,s_{0})~\text{ and }~f^{\prime}(s)<0\text{ on }(s_{0},1);

(1.11b)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0)=f^{\prime}(1)=0,\qquad f^{\prime\prime}(0),f^{\prime\prime}(1)>0;

(1.11c)

\displaystyle\exists c_{0}\in(0,1)\text{ s.t. }2c_{0}^{2}s^{2}\leqslant f(s)\leqslant 2c_{0}^{-2}s^{2}\text{ for any }s\geqslant 100.

After an appropriate modification for large
|s|
, the function
g(s)=|s||s^{2}-1|
, which corresponds to the Chern–Simons–Higgs potential, satisfies (
1.11
).

To control the bulk errors, we need
another modulated energy:

(1.12)

B[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|I](t):=\int_{\Omega}\Big(\sigma\chi-\sigma+2(\psi_{\varepsilon}-\sigma)^{-}\Big)\eta\circ d_{I}\,\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\left(\psi_{\varepsilon}-\sigma\right)^{+}|\eta\circ d_{I}|\,dx.

Here
\chi(\cdot,t):=\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{t}^{+}}-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{t}^{-}}
,
h^{\pm}
denote the positive/negative parts of a function
h
respectively, and
\eta
is a truncation of the identity function defined by

(1.13)

\eta(z):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}z\qquad\text{when }&z\in[-\delta_{0},\delta_{0}],\\
\delta_{0}\qquad\text{when }&z\geqslant\delta_{0},\\
-\delta_{0}\qquad\text{when }&z\leqslant-\delta_{0}.\end{array}\right.

Note that
(\eta\circ d_{I})~\chi\geqslant 0
in
\Omega
due to our convention on the signed-distance function, and thus the two integrands in (
1.12
) are both non-negative. We refer the readers to the proof of Theorem
4.1
below for more details on the positivity of (
1.12
).

Now we state the main result of this work:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\in\{2,3\}
, and the assumptions (

) and (

Assume that the moving interface
I
in (

(1.14a)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{in}\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)},

(1.14b)

\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{in})\leqslant c_{1},

(1.14c)

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{in}|I_{0}]+B[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{in}|I_{0}]\leqslant c_{1}\varepsilon,

where
c_{1}>0

Then there exists
C_{1}>0

\varepsilon
such that

(1.15)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|I](t)+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}B[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|I](t)\leqslant C_{1}\varepsilon,

(1.16)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\Omega}|\psi_{\varepsilon}-\sigma\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{t}^{+}}|\,dx\leqslant C_{1}\varepsilon^{1/4}.

Moreover, up to extraction of a subsequence

(1.17)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon_{k}}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{t}^{+}}\mathbf{u}~

\displaystyle\text{ in }~C([0,T];L^{2}_{loc}{(\Omega\backslash I_{t})}),

where
\mathbf{u}
satisfies the following properties:

(1.18a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,6/5}(\Omega^{+}_{t};\mathbb{S}^{2})),~\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{6/5}(\Omega^{+}_{t})),

(1.18b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(x,t)=0~\text{ for every }~t\in[0,T]\text{ and for a.e. }~x\in\Omega_{t}^{-},

(1.18c)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n})(x,t)=0\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,T]\text{ and for }\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\text{-a.e. }x\in I_{t}.

and in Theorem

To construct an initial datum satisfying (
1.14

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
I_{0}\subset\Omega
be a
(d-1)
-dimensional submanifold. For any vector field

(1.19)

\mathbf{u}^{in}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{2})~\text{ with }~\mathbf{u}^{in}|_{I_{0}}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{I_{0}}=0~a.e.~\text{ on }~I_{0},

there exists
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{in}\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that

(1.20)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{in}=\mathbf{u}^{in}&\quad\text{in}~\Omega^{+}_{0}\backslash B_{2\delta_{0}}(I_{0}),\\
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{in}=0&\quad\text{in }\Omega^{-}_{0}\backslash B_{2\delta_{0}}(I_{0}),\end{cases}

and (

) holds for a constant

which only depends on

and

We comment on the conditions in (
1.19
). When

,

is a smooth closed surface in
\Omega
. Due to topological obstructions, a vector field satisfying (
1.19
) is usually not smooth. For instance, when

is diffeomorphic to a 2-sphere, due to the hairy ball theorem,
\mathbf{u}^{in}|_{I_{0}}
must have (at least) one pole.
One example of such a pole, which is often encountered in the theory of liquid crystal, is given by the hedgehog profile. Locally the tangent vector field near such a pole is
C^{1}
-equivalent to the mapping
\mathbf{h}(x)=x/|x|:B_{1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Note that
\mathbf{h}\in W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(B_{1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{2})
but
\mathbf{h}\notin W^{1,2}(B_{1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
When
d=2
, there are fewer constraints to arrange a vector field
\mathbf{f}:I_{0}\mapsto\mathbb{S}^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
that is orthogonal to the planar curve

.
In general, using the extension lemma of Hardt–Lin (cf.
[
42

), any tangent vector field
\mathbf{f}\in W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(I_{0};\mathbb{S}^{2})
has an extension
\mathbf{u}^{in}
satisfying (
1.19
).

An immediate consequence of Theorem
1.1
is the convergence in (
1.3
). Indeed, it follows from (

) below that

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mu|\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}|^{2}\,dx\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0
,
and thus such an energy does not contribute to the surface energy in the limit. However, it forces

Now applying integration by parts to the last term of (
1
), and then using (

) and

, we find

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}-\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\xi})\psi_{\varepsilon}\,dx=-\sigma\int_{\Omega_{t}^{+}}(\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\xi})\,dx=\sigma\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(I_{t}).

Note that the last step is due to the Green’s formula.

Under additional assumptions, we can show that the limit
\mathbf{u}
in (
1.17
) solves a geometric evolution equation in the bulk region
\Omega^{+}:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Omega^{+}_{t}\times\{t\}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
d=2
and the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
be in place. Assume further that

(1.22)

\begin{split}&f(s)=s^{2}\text{ for }s\leqslant 1/4;f(s)=(s-1)^{2}\text{ for }s\geqslant 3/4;\\
&f(s)\geqslant 1/{16}\text{ for }s\in[1/4,3/4];\\
&\sup_{s\in[1/4,3/4]}|f^{\prime}(s)|\leqslant 4.\end{split}

Then there exists a sufficiently small
\mu>0
(independent of
\varepsilon
) such that the vector field
\mathbf{u}
in (
1.17
) satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}\wedge\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Psi}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\mathbf{u}\wedge\mathbf{u})\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\Psi}\,dx

(1.23)

\displaystyle=\mu\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u})\Big((\operatorname{rot}\boldsymbol{\Psi})\cdot\mathbf{u}-(\operatorname{rot}\mathbf{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{\Psi}\Big)\,dx

for almost every
t\in(0,T)
and for every
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega_{t}^{+};\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

In the above equation
\wedge
is the wedge product in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and

\operatorname{rot}
is the curl operator.
The equation (
1.23
) is the weak formulation of an Oseen–Frank flow, written as

(1.24)

\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}=\Delta\mathbf{u}+\mu({\mathbb{I}_{3}}-\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})\nabla(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u})+|\nabla\mathbf{u}|^{2}\mathbf{u},\qquad\text{ for }t\in(0,T],x\in\Omega_{t}^{+}.

It can be verified that when
\mathbf{u}
is sufficiently regular, then (
1.23
) implies (
1.24
).
It is worth mentioning that equation of the form (
1.24
) is the
L^{2}
-gradient flow of the variational problem

(1.25)

\inf\int_{U}\left(\mu|\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}|^{2}+|\nabla\mathbf{u}|^{2}\right)\,dx,

where the infimum is taken among mappings
\mathbf{u}\in W^{1,2}(U;\mathbb{S}^{2})
fulfilling certain boundary conditions on
\partial U
. Note that (
1.25
) is a special case of the full Oseen–Frank model (cf.
[
30
]
).

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation and Conventions

We shall adopt the following conventions throughout the paper.
Unless specified otherwise,
C>0
is a generic constant whose value might change from line to line, and will depend on the geometry of the interface (
1.4
) but not on
\varepsilon
or
t\in[0,T]
.
For two square matrices
A
and
B
, their Frobenius inner product is defined by
A:B:=\operatorname{tr}A^{\mathsf{T}}B
, which induces the norm
|A|:=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}A^{\mathsf{T}}A}
.
We shall also use the following notation for a vector-valued function
\mathbf{u}(x,t)=(u_{1}(x,t),u_{2}(x,t),u_{3}(x,t))
where

(2.1)

x=\begin{cases}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})&\text{ when }d=3,\\
(x_{1},x_{2},0)&\text{ when }d=2.\end{cases}

(2.2)

\begin{split}\partial_{0}&=\partial_{t},\quad\partial_{i}=\partial_{x_{i}}\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant 3,\\
\nabla u_{1}&=(\partial_{1}u_{1},\partial_{2}u_{1},\partial_{3}u_{1}),\quad\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\partial_{i}u_{i},\\
\operatorname{rot}\mathbf{u}&=(\partial_{2}u_{3}-\partial_{3}u_{2},\partial_{3}u_{1}-\partial_{1}u_{3},\partial_{1}u_{2}-\partial_{2}u_{1}).\end{split}

To ease computations when
d=2
,
\nabla\mathbf{u}
will be understood as the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{1}u_{1}&\partial_{2}u_{1}&0\\
\partial_{1}u_{2}&\partial_{2}u_{2}&0\\
\partial_{1}u_{3}&\partial_{2}u_{3}&0\end{pmatrix}

\begin{split}&\text{any planar vector field is understood as a}\\
&\text{3D vector field with vanishing 3rd component.}\end{split}

In particular, the latter applies to the normal and the mean curvature vector fields (cf. (

) and (

For a function of

, like

, its gradient will be denoted by

Finally we can normalize

\sigma:=\int_{0}^{1}g(s)\,ds=1.

As the

-gradient flow of (

), the system
(
1.2
) enjoys the following energy dissipation law:

(2.6)

A_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\hat{T}))+\int_{0}^{\hat{T}}\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon|\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dxdt=A_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{in}(\cdot))

for arbitrarily large time

Combining this with the theory of gradient flow and the regularity theory for elliptic system (cf.

Under the assumption (

), the nonlinearity of (

) has a linear growth. So considering the system with initial datum

\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\text{ is a classical solution of }\eqref{Ginzburg-Landau}\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,\hat{T}].

For initial datum undergoing phase transitions near the initial interface

will be singular near

For any

with

Differentiating this identity with respect to

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists a constant

(2.15a)

\displaystyle|\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}+\mathbf{H}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}|\leqslant C

\displaystyle~|d_{I}|\quad\text{in}~B_{\delta_{0}}(I_{t}),

(2.15b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}d_{I}+(\mathbf{H}\cdot\nabla)d_{I}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in}~B_{\delta_{0}}(I_{t}),

(2.15c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\xi}+\left(\mathbf{H}\cdot\nabla\right)\boldsymbol{\xi}+\left(\nabla\mathbf{H}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in}~B_{\delta_{0}}(I_{t}),

where

is a matrix with

Proof.

By introducing

, we can rewrite (

. Since

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=|\nabla d_{I}|^{2}\phi_{0}^{\prime}(d_{I})+\phi_{0}(d_{I})\Delta d_{I}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\qquad\quad O(d_{I})+\phi_{0}(d_{I})\Delta d_{I}(P_{I}(x,t),t).

).

) and (

), we can write (

), which leads to the following identities in

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\nabla d_{I}+(\mathbf{H}\cdot\nabla)\nabla d_{I}+(\nabla\mathbf{H})^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla d_{I}=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{0}(d_{I})+(\mathbf{H}\cdot\nabla)\phi_{0}(d_{I})=0.

These two equations together imply (

It will be convenient to introduce

(2.16)

\psi_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{d}^{F}\circ\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{ where }\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{v}):=\int_{0}^{|\mathbf{v}|}g(s)\,ds.

It can be verified using (

) that

(2.17)

\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{v})\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\quad\text{ and }\quad D\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{v})=0~\text{ iff }~\mathbf{v}\in\{0,\mathbb{S}^{2}\}.

By (

(2.18)

(2.19a)

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\cdot D\mathrm{d}^{F}\left(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\right)\quad\text{ for any }(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T],

(2.19b)

\displaystyle\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\nabla|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)|~g(|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)|)\qquad\text{ if }~\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\neq 0.

Now we define the phase-field analogues of the normal vector and the mean curvature vector respectively by

(2.20a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\frac{\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}}{|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|}(x,t)&\text{ if }\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\neq 0,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2.20b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}-\left(\varepsilon\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}DF(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})\right)\cdot\frac{\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}{\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|}&\text{ if }\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\neq 0,\\

(2.21)

\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}=|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{ for any }(x,t).

\Pi_{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}
by

(2.22)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{i}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}:=\begin{cases}\left(\partial_{i}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}{|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|}\right)\frac{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}{|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|}&~\text{if}~\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\neq 0,\\
0,&~\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Lemma 2.2
.

The following equations hold:

(2.23a)

\displaystyle|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|=|\Pi_{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}||D\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})|\quad\text{ for any }(x,t),

(2.23b)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|}{|D\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})|^{2}}D\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{ on }\{x\mid|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|\notin\{0,1\}\}.

Proof.

Concerning (
2.23a
), it suffices to work with the set
\{x\mid|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|\notin\{0,1\}\}
where

(cf. (
1.10

) and (
2.19a
).
On this set we deduce from (

) that

, and we can rewrite (
2.19a
) as

(2.24)

\partial_{i}\psi_{\varepsilon}=\partial_{i}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{D\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})}{|D\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})|}|D\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})|=\partial_{i}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}{|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|}|D\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})|.

This combined with (
2.22
) implies (
2.23a
).

Now we turn to the proof of (
2.23b
). On the set
\{x\mid|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|\notin\{0,1\}\}
, we have

(2.25)

\displaystyle\frac{|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|}{|D\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})|^{2}}D\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\overset{\eqref{normal diff2}}{=}\frac{D\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})}{|D\mathrm{d}^{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})|^{2}}\otimes\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}\overset{\eqref{ADM chain rule1}}{=}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}{|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|}\otimes\nabla|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|,

and this implies (
2.23b
) in view of (
2.22
).

Lemma 2.3
.

The following estimates hold for every

(2.26a)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})-|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|I],

(2.26b)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\left(\mu|\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\Pi_{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right)\,dx

(2.26c)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|I],

(2.26d)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left({\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})+\left|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}\right|\right)\left(1-\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant{4E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|I]},

(2.26e)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})+|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|\right)\min\left(d^{2}_{I},1\right)\,dx

where

.

Proof.

The case when
\mu=0
has been done in

]
, and the proof carries over to the present case. First, it follows from (

) that

(2.27)

\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\Pi_{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\left|\Pi_{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}=\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}.

Combining this with (

), we can write

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})-\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})-|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|+|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|(1-\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\Pi_{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\Pi_{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})-|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|\right)

\displaystyle+|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|(1-\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}).

Observe that the second term on the left-hand side of (

.

) relies on the following lemma.

.

) and (

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\,{|\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}-\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}}\left|\Pi_{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\,2(1-\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon})\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}.

On the set

where

\displaystyle~|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{n}|^{2}=|\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\otimes(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{n})|^{2}
