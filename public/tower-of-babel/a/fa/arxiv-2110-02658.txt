Rank-2 Toric Code in Two Dimensions
Yun-Tak Oh, Jintae Kim, Eun-Gook Moon, Jung Hoon Han

Abstract
We study a two-dimensional spin model obtained by “Higgsing” the rank-2 U(1) lattice gauge theory (LGT) with scalar or vector charges on the L x × L y L_{x}\times L_{y} square lattice under the periodic boundary condition (PBC). There are p p degrees of freedom per orbital and three orbitals per unit cell in the spin model. The resulting spin model is a stabilizer code consisting of three mutually commuting projectors that are, in turn, obtained by Higgsing the mutually commuting Gauss’s law operators and the magnetic field operators in the underlying LGT. The spin model thus obtained is exactly solvable, with the ground state degeneracy (GSD) D D given by log p ​ D = 2 + ( 1 + δ L x ​ mod ​ p , 0 ) ​ ( 1 + δ L y ​ mod ​ p , 0 ) \text{log}_{p}D=2+(1+\delta_{L_{x}~\text{mod}~p,0})(1+\delta_{L_{y}~\text{mod}~p,0}) when p p is a prime number. Two types of dipole excitations, pristine and emergent, are identified. Both the monopoles and the dipoles are free to move, with restrictions on monopoles to hop only by p p lattice spacing along with certain directions. The monopole-monopole braiding phase depends on the separation of the x x or y y coordinates of the initial monopole positions, making it distinct from the ordinary anyon braiding statistics. The monopole-dipole braiding obeys the usual anyonic statistics. Despite the oddity, the monopole-monopole braiding phase can be understood as the Aharonov-Bohm phase of some emergent vector potentials.



I
Introduction

Fractons have come to embody excitations in lattice models that are immobile on their own
Haah 2011
;
Vijay et al. 2015
;
Chamon 2005
;
Nandkishore and Hermele 2019
. Many variations of the fracton model such as Haah’s cubic code and X-cube model have been proposed so far, and new ones continue to show up
Haah 2011
;
Vijay et al. 2015
. A defining property common to all fracton models is, besides its restricted mobility of quasiparticles, the sub-extensive GSD, although recently a different kind of fracton model with extensive GSD has been suggested
Kim and Han 2021
. Field-theoretic interpretations of the fracton physics have been in development for some time
Slagle and Kim 2017
;
Ma et al. 2018
;
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2018
;
You et al. 2020
;
Fontana et al. 2021
employing various modifications of the Chern-Simons and/or BF theories. Another line of thinking was initiated by Pretko
Pretko 2017a
;
Pretko 2017b
, who pointed out that the rank-2 U(1) gauge theory developed by Xu and collaborators
Xu 2006
;
Rasmussen et al. 2016
offers an intuitive understanding of the sub-dimensional motion of quasiparticle excitations in fracton models.

In ordinary electrodynamics, charges are created as a charge-neutral dipole and subsequently, each charge executes a free motion. Such processes are allowed within the constraints of the rank-1 U(1) gauge theory and the charge conservation (Gauss’s) law. A rank-2 U(1) gauge theory
Pretko 2017a
;
Pretko 2017b
typically imposes both charge and dipole conservations, permitting the creation of a dipole-antidipole pair but not the individual dipole. Subsequently, eaach dipole can move freely but not the individual charge that comprise it. By “Higgsing” the rank-2 U(1) LGT as was done in Refs.
Ma et al. 2018
;
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2018
, the constraints become somewhat relaxed and the free motion of the charge can take place as well.

In this paper, we re-visit the Higgsing procedure and write down an exactly solvable, stabilizer spin Hamiltonian in two dimensions. The idea is, similar to previous works
Ma et al. 2018
;
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2018
and in particular Ref.
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2018
, to identify some operators in the parent rank-2 U(1) LGT that commute with one another and then elevate them to mutually commuting spin operators through the exponentiation procedure commonly known as “Higgsing”. In short, the Higgsing procedure converts the rank-2 U(1) LGT, well known to be unstable to the proliferation of instanton in two dimensions
Rasmussen et al. 2016
;
Pretko 2017a
;
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2018
into a gapped
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
spin model with
p
chosen to be some prime number. The case of
p=2
and even-by-even lattice dimensions
L_{x}\times L_{y}
was explored in Ref.
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2018
. The spin model obtained in this manner will be dubbed ‘rank-2 toric code (R2TC)’ as it is obtained from Higgsing the rank-2 U(1) gauge theory. The well-known toric code is obtained by Higgsing the rank-1 U(1) LGT instead and warrants the classification as the rank-1 toric code (R1TC).

The nature of elementary quasiparticles in the R2TC model consisting of monopoles and dipoles and their mutual statistics are analyzed in detail. Whereas the charge excitations in the rank-2 LGT are completely immobile due to the constraint of dipole moment conservation, monopole excitations in the R2TC can move in any direction due to the relaxation of the constraint. The quasiparticles of R2TC are not as free as the anyonic quasiparticles of the R1TC, though, in that they are bound to hop only by
p
lattice spacings in certain directions. Such restriction is however lifted when the two monopoles combine to form a dipole, whose motion is completely free. Most surprisingly, the braiding statistics between a pair of monopoles in our R2TC model is not that of ordinary abelian anyons. Rather,
the braiding phase depends on the separation of
x
or
y
coordinates of the initial monopole positions
. Such feature is distinct from path-independent anyonic braiding statistics in R1TC. The braiding of a dipole round a monopole, on the other hand, does exhibit the familiar anyonic statistics. The dipole-dipole braiding statistics is bosonic. In addition, we show that the GSD of the R2TC depends on the (mod
p
) linear system sizes
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
.

We begin by reviewing how the Higgsing procedure transforms the rank-1 U(1) LGT into the well-known toric code in Sec.
II
. Afterward, we review the rank-2 U(1) LGT in Sec.
III
and construct the rank-2 toric code by applying the Higgsing procedure in Sec.
IV
. Although the Higgsing recipe itself works for both two and three dimensions, detailed analyses are performed for two-dimensional models only due to the difficulty of analyzing three-dimensional models to the same depth. We work out GSD of the R2TC in Sec.
V
and all the related logical operators in Sec.
VI
, both of which turn out to depend on the mod
p
parity of the linear dimensions
L_{x},~L_{y}
of the square lattice under the PBC. After identifying all the monopole as well as dipole quasiparticle excitations of the model in Sec.
VII
, we work out their mutual braiding statistics in Sec.
VIII
. In Sec.
IX
, we show that the position-dependent phase of the mutual statistics of the monopoles can be described by the integral of the magnetic field. Summary of our work is given in Sec.
X
.

II
Toric code from Higgsing the Rank-1 U(1) LGT

In this section, we review the rank-1 U(1) LGT in two-dimensional square lattice and show how the ‘Higgsing’ procedure transforms it to the well-known R1TC. The rank-1 U(1) LGT is defined in terms of a pair of canonical variables - the compact gauge field
A_{i}^{a}
and the electric field
E_{i}^{a}
(
a=x,~y
) assigned on the links (labeled by
i
) of the square lattice. The two fields obey the canonical commutation relation,

\displaystyle\left[A_{i}^{a},E_{j}^{b}\right]=i\delta_{i,j}\delta_{a,b}.

(1)

The compactness comes from the gauge field being an angular variable
A_{i}^{a}\in[0,2\pi)
. Consequently,
E_{i}^{a}\sim-i\partial/\partial A_{i}^{a}
takes on integer eigenvalues.

On two-dimensional square lattice, the magnetic field
B_{i}
of an elementary plaquette in the rank-1 U(1) LGT is defined by

\displaystyle B_{i}=A_{i+\hat{x}}^{y}-A_{i}^{y}-A_{i+\hat{y}}^{x}-A_{i}^{x}

(2)

as illustrated in Fig.
1
(a). The local change of the gauge field is implemented as the unitary transformation

\displaystyle A_{i}^{a}

\displaystyle\rightarrow e^{-i\sum_{j}f_{j}({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bm{E}})_{j}}A_{i}^{a}e^{i\sum_{j}f_{j}({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bm{E}})_{j}}

\displaystyle=A_{i}^{a}+f_{i+\hat{a}}-f_{i},

(3)

where
f_{i}
is an arbitrary scalar field at the link
i
. The generator for the gauge transformation is

\displaystyle\left({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bm{E}}\right)_{i}\equiv E^{x}_{i}-E^{x}_{i-\hat{x}}+E^{y}_{i}-E^{y}_{i-\hat{y}},

(4)

which is often known as the Gauss’s law.

Note that the Gauss’s law
\left({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf\bm{E}}\right)_{i}
and the magnetic field
B_{j}
commute:

\displaystyle\left[\left({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bm{E}}\right)_{i},B_{j}\right]=0.

(5)

Such commutativity of the magnetic field and the Gauss’s law is general in gauge theories, both discrete and continuous, as it comes from the fact that the Gauss’s law is the generator of the relevant gauge transformation and the magnetic field is a quantity which, by definition, is invariant under such gauge transformation. A simple recipe emerges for constructing a family of exactly solvable spin models based on the commuting elements of the parent LGTs. The idea is to exploit the commutative nature of the Gauss’s laws and the magnetic fields of the parent LGT to construct mutually commuting spin operators, and from there, some exactly solvable models in the form of the stabilizer Hamiltonian
Gottesman 1996
;
Gottesman 1997
;
Kitaev 2003
.

Figure 1:

(a) Magnetic field
B_{i}
and the Gauss’s law

The first step in the construction is to define the local
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
Hilbert space consisting of states:
|g\rangle=|0\rangle,~|1\rangle,~\cdots,~|p-1\rangle
. Next, one exponentiates the two fields
(A,E)
in the rank-1 U(1) LGT as

\displaystyle X=e^{iA},~~~Z=e^{2\pi iE/p}.

(6)

The canonical commutation
[A,E]=i
gives rise to the algebra

\displaystyle X|g\rangle=|g+1\rangle,~Z|g\rangle=\omega^{g}|g\rangle,~ZX=\omega XZ,

(7)

where
\omega=e^{i2\pi/p}
and all additions are mod
p
. The basis states
|g\rangle
are naturally given as eigenstates of
Z
.

The next step is the exponentiation of the Gauss’s law and the magnetic field themselves according to the recipe in Eq. (
6
):

\displaystyle a_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{p}({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bm{E}})_{i}\right)=Z_{1,i}Z_{1,i-\hat{x}}^{-1}Z_{2,i}Z_{2,i-\hat{y}}^{-1}

\displaystyle b_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\exp(iB_{i})=X_{1,i}X_{2,i+\hat{x}}X^{-1}_{1,i+\hat{y}}X^{-1}_{2,i}.

(8)

The connection between the gauge theory operators and the spin operators through the exponentiation procedure is illustrated in Fig.
1
. Note that we are using subscript 1 (2) for the spin operators at the horizontal (vertical) links. The Gauss’s law in Eq. (
4
) is the directed sum of the
E
field operators at the four links emanating from a vertex. When exponentiated, it becomes the
a_{i}
operator, a product of
Z
’s and
Z^{-1}
’s on the four links as shown in Fig.
1
. This is one of the operators one can use to construct the stabilizer. The magnetic field, on the other hand, is the directed sum of the gauge field operators
A
around an elementary plaquette which, upon exponentiation and applying Eq. (
6
), becomes the
b_{i}
operator consisting of the product of
X
’s and
X^{-1}
’s. This becomes the second set of stabilizers. The Hamiltonian is then given as the sum of the two types of stabilizers. We refer to the mapping of the Gauss’s laws and the magnetic field into corresponding spin operators as the “Higgsing” of the lattice gauge theory in accordance with the nomenclature in the recent literature
Fradkin and Shenker 1979
;
Ma et al. 2018
;
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2018
. Once the Higgsing procedure is complete, one can do away with the parent LGT and focus on the analysis of various interesting properties of the resulting spin model.

The commutativity
\left[\left({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bm{E}}\right)_{i},B_{j}\right]=0
of the parent LGT is inherited as that of the spin operators,
[a_{i},b_{j}]=0
.
Mutually commuting projectors are then constructed,

\mathbb{A}_{i}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(a_{i})^{j},~~\mathbb{B}_{i}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(b_{i})^{j}.

(9)

One can prove their projector properties,
\mathbb{A}_{i}^{2}=\mathbb{A}_{i}
, etc. The Hamiltonian
H=-\sum_{i}(\mathbb{A}_{i}+\mathbb{B}_{i})
obtained in this way is none other than the
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
toric code. The Higgsing scheme makes transparent the fact that the exact solvability of the toric code originates from the commuting relation of the Gauss’s law and the magnetic field in the parent LGT. It is also clear that the scheme will generalize to other LGTs to generate new, exactly solvable spin models.

Rank-2 U(1) LGT in two dimensions

The rank-2 U(1) LGT
Xu 2006
can be defined on a two-dimensional square lattice with compact gauge fields
A_{i}^{ab}

(a,b=x,y)
assigned on vertices (
A^{xx}_{i}

One can write down two kinds of rank-2 U(1) LGTs depending on whether the scalar or the vector charge is assumed

Pretko 2017a
;
Ma et al. 2018

Bulmash and Barkeshli 2018

For the rank-2 U(1) LGT with scalar charge
\rho_{i}

\displaystyle B_{i}^{x}

\displaystyle\equiv m(A_{i}^{xy}-A_{i-\hat{x}}^{xy})-n(A_{i-\hat{x}+\hat{y}}^{xx}-A_{i-\hat{x}}^{xx}),

\displaystyle\equiv n(A_{i+\hat{x}-\hat{y}}^{yy}-A_{i-\hat{y}}^{yy})-m(A_{i}^{xy}-A_{i-\hat{y}}^{xy}),

\displaystyle A_{i}^{ab}

\displaystyle\rightarrow e^{-i\sum_{j}f_{j}({\bf D}E)_{j}}A_{i}^{ab}e^{i\sum_{j}f_{j}({\bf D}E)_{j}}

\displaystyle=A_{i}^{aa}-m(f_{i+2\hat{a}}-2f_{i+\hat{a}}+f_{i})

\displaystyle(a

\displaystyle=b)

\displaystyle=A_{i}^{ab}-n(f_{i+\hat{a}+\hat{b}}-f_{i+\hat{a}}-f_{i+\hat{b}}+f_{i})

\displaystyle(a

\displaystyle\neq b).

(13)

One can readily check that the magnetic fields (

For the rank-2 U(1) LGT with vector charge, there is one component of the magnetic field

\displaystyle B_{i}\equiv n(A_{i-\hat{x}-\hat{y}}^{xx}-2A_{i-\hat{x}}^{xx}+A_{i-\hat{x}+\hat{y}}^{xx}+A_{i-\hat{x}-\hat{y}}^{yy}-2A_{i-\hat{y}}^{yy}+A_{i+\hat{x}-\hat{y}}^{yy})-m(A_{i}^{xy}-A_{i-\hat{y}}^{xy}-A_{i-\hat{x}}^{xy}+A_{i-\hat{x}-\hat{y}}^{xy}),

\displaystyle({\bf D}E)_{i}^{x}

\displaystyle=m(E_{i+x}^{xx}-E_{i}^{xx})+n(E_{i+y}^{xy}-E_{i}^{xy})\equiv\rho_{i}^{x},

\displaystyle({\bf D}E)_{i}^{y}

\displaystyle=m(E_{i+y}^{yy}-E_{i}^{yy})+n(E_{i+x}^{yx}-E_{i}^{yx})\equiv\rho_{i}^{y},

with commutation

\displaystyle A_{i}^{ab}

\displaystyle\rightarrow e^{-i\sum_{j,c}[f^{c}_{j}({\bf D}E)^{c}_{j}]}A_{i}^{ab}e^{i\sum_{j,c}[f^{c}_{j}({\bf D}E)^{c}_{j}]}

\displaystyle=A_{i}^{aa}-m(f^{a}_{i+\hat{a}}-f^{a}_{i})

\displaystyle(a=b)

\displaystyle=A_{i}^{ab}-n(f^{b}_{i+\hat{a}}-f^{b}_{i}+f^{a}_{i+\hat{b}}-f^{a}_{i})

\displaystyle(a\neq b),

IV
Rank-2 Toric Code

We exploit the Higgsing procedure detailed in Sec. II to construct the stabilizer spin Hamiltonian out of the Gauss’s law and the magnetic field of the parent rank-2 U(1) LGT with vector charge given in Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
). The final result is the rank-2 analogue of the toric code, which we call the rank-2 toric code, or R2TC for short.

Following Eq. (
6
), we convert the canonical variables
A_{i}^{ab}
and
E_{i}^{ab}
into spin operators as

\displaystyle X_{0,i}=e^{iA_{i}^{xy}},

\displaystyle~~Z_{0,i}=e^{2\pi iE_{i}^{xy}/p}

\displaystyle X_{1,i}=e^{iA_{i}^{xx}},

\displaystyle~~Z_{1,i}=e^{2\pi iE_{i}^{xx}/p},

\displaystyle X_{2,i}=e^{iA_{i}^{yy}},

\displaystyle~~Z_{2,i}=e^{2\pi iE_{i}^{yy}/p}.

(18)

The two indices
1
and
2
represent the two orbitals assigned on the vertices and the index
0
the orbital on the plaquette center. The second step is the exponentiation of the Gauss’s law and the magnetic field in Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) according to the recipe in Eq. (
6
):

\displaystyle a_{i}^{x}

\displaystyle\equiv\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{p}\rho_{i}^{x}\right)=Z_{1,i}^{-m}Z_{1,i+\hat{x}}^{m}Z_{0,i}^{n}Z_{0,i-\hat{y}}^{-n},

\displaystyle a_{i}^{y}

\displaystyle\equiv\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{p}\rho_{i}^{y}\right)=Z_{2,i}^{-m}Z_{2,i+\hat{x}}^{m}Z_{0,i}^{n}Z_{0,i-\hat{x}}^{-n},

\displaystyle b_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\exp(iB_{i})

\displaystyle=X_{2,i-\hat{x}}^{n}X_{2,i}^{-2n}X_{2,i+\hat{x}}^{n}X_{1,i-\hat{y}}^{n}X_{1,i}^{-2n}X_{1,i+\hat{y}}^{n}

\displaystyle~~~\times X_{0,i}^{m}X_{0,i-\hat{x}}^{-m}X_{0,i-\hat{y}}^{-m}X_{0,i-\hat{x}-\hat{y}}^{m}.

(19)

They are illustrated in Fig.
2
(b). The commutativity among
(\rho^{x}_{i},\rho^{y}_{i},B_{i})
is inherited now as that of spin operators
(a_{i}^{x},a_{i}^{y},b_{i})
. This property allows us in turn to construct a stabilizer Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{i}^{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(a_{i}^{x})^{j},

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{i}^{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(a_{i}^{y})^{j},

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(b_{i})^{j},

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}(\mathbb{A}^{x}_{i}+\mathbb{A}^{y}_{i}+\mathbb{B}_{i}).

(20)

This completes the construction of the R2TC - the exactly solvable stabilizer model from the underlying rank-2 U(1) LGT. The ground state(s) is characterized by
\mathbb{A}_{i}^{x}|{\rm GS}\rangle=\mathbb{A}_{i}^{y}|{\rm GS}\rangle=\mathbb{B}_{i}|{\rm GS}\rangle=|{\rm GS}\rangle
.

V
Ground State Degeneracy

Calculation of the GSD for exactly solvable spin models is based on the general formula
Gottesman 1996
;
Calderbank et al. 1997
;
Kim and Han 2021
,

\displaystyle\log_{p}D=N_{o}-N_{s}=N_{lo},

(21)

where
D
,
p
,
N_{o}
,
N_{s}
, and
N_{lo}
represent the GSD, the local Hilbert space dimension, the number of orbitals, the number of independent stabilizers, and the number of independent logical operators, respectively. There are three orbital degrees of freedom, two at the site and one at the dual lattice site, for
N_{o}=3L_{x}L_{y}
on a
L_{x}\times L_{y}
square lattice with PBC.

We consider the case of
p
being a prime number in this work due to the extra complication caused by non-prime integer
p
. For example, if
p~{\rm mod}~m=0
in the
(m,n)
vector charge theory, we have the identity

\displaystyle\left(a_{i}^{x}(a_{i}^{y})^{-1}(a_{i-\hat{x}}^{x})^{-1}a_{i-\hat{y}}^{y}\right)^{p/m}=1.

(22)

This identity, which holds for every site
i
of the lattice, creates constraints that will have to be taken into account when we later try to count the number of independent stabilizers. Furthermore, operators such as
Z_{0,i}^{p/m}
,
X_{1,i}^{p/m}
, and
X_{2,i}^{p/m}
commute with the Hamiltonian and act as local symmetry generators, which vastly affect the ground state structure. For these reasons,
p
is a prime number in the remainder of the paper.

The integers
(m,n)
are, of course, only meaningful mod
p
, since the
p
-th power of either
X
or
Z
operator is an identity. This is also the meaning of “Higgsing”, by which the strict charge conservation of the underlying rank-2 U(1) LGT relaxes to the mod
p
conservation. So far, the pair of integers
(m,n)
were kept general. Keeping them was helpful in identifying the duality relation between scalar and vector charge theories in Sec.
III
. In the following, however, we restrict ourselves to the case
(m,n)=(1,1)
in enumerating the GSD and for other analyses of the model. We believe the essence of the subsequent arguments and analyses remain insensitive to the choice of
(m,n)
.

The GSD of the R2TC calculated for the
L_{x}\times L_{y}
square lattice under the PBC turns out to depend on whether
L_{x}
or
L_{y}
is a multiple of
p
. The following products of operators prove useful in counting the GSD:

\displaystyle H_{j_{y}}(O)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{L_{x}}O_{j_{x}\hat{x}+j_{y}\hat{y}},

\displaystyle V_{j_{x}}(O)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{L_{y}}O_{j_{x}\hat{x}+j_{y}\hat{y}},

\displaystyle M(O)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{L_{x}}\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{L_{y}}O_{j_{x}\hat{x}+j_{y}\hat{y}}.

(23)

Here,
O
is an operator in the R2TC model. As one can see,
H_{j_{y}}(O)
is a product of
O
’s along the
j_{y}
-th row, and
V_{j_{x}}(O)
the product of
O
’s along the
j_{x}
-th column.
M(O)
is referred to as a membrane operation since it is the product of
O
’s over the entire lattice.

In addition to the horizontal and the vertical operators defined above, some diagonal operators can be defined by

\displaystyle D_{i}^{xy}(O)

\displaystyle\equiv\prod^{L_{l}}_{j=1}O_{i+j(\hat{x}+\hat{y})},

\displaystyle D_{i}^{x\bar{y}}(O)

\displaystyle\equiv\prod^{L_{l}}_{j=1}O_{i+j(\hat{x}-\hat{y})}.

(24)

They are the products of
O
’s along the
(1,1)
or
(1,-1)
direction starting from the site
i
. The site coordinates are labeled modulo
(L_{x},L_{y})
under the PBC. The product over
j
in the above diagonal operators spans
1\leq j\leq L_{l}
where
L_{l}={\rm lcm}(L_{x},L_{y})
is the least common multiple of
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
. Note that
L_{l}
is the smallest number for which
O_{i+j(\hat{x}+\hat{y})}=O_{i}
. There are
L_{g}
distinct diagonal operators, where
L_{g}={\rm gcd}(L_{x},L_{y})
stands for the greatest common divisor. Specifically, we have
D_{i}^{xy}(O),~D_{i+\hat{y}}^{xy}(O),\cdots,~D_{i+(L_{g}-1)\hat{y}}^{xy}(O)
in the (1,1) direction. In total, due to
L_{x}L_{y}=L_{g}L_{l}
, every site appears exactly once in the diagonal operator of a given direction.

At first there seem to be
3L_{x}L_{y}
stabilizers,
a_{i}^{x}
,
a_{i}^{y}
, and
b_{i}
, for all
i
, on a
L_{x}\times L_{y}
under the PBC. Some constraints readily emerge amongst them:

\displaystyle\prod_{i}a_{i}^{x}=1,~~~\prod_{i}a_{i}^{y}=1,~~~\prod_{i}b_{i}=1.

(25)

The product
\prod_{i}
runs over all the sites, with the identity remaining valid regardless of the lattice size or the degree of freedom
p
, suggesting that the number of independent stabilizers would be
N_{s}=3L_{x}L_{y}-3
. Interestingly, several more identities arise among the stabilizers if
L_{x}
or
L_{y}
is a multiple of the local Hilbert space dimension
p
.

To figure out what the extra identities are, first consider

\displaystyle H_{j_{y}}(a^{x})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{L_{x}}Z_{0,j_{x}\hat{x}+j_{y}\hat{y}}Z_{0,j_{x}\hat{x}+(j_{y}-1)\hat{y}}^{-1},

\displaystyle V_{j_{x}}(a^{y})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{L_{y}}Z_{0,j_{x}\hat{x}+j_{y}\hat{y}}Z_{0,(j_{x}-1)\hat{x}+j_{y}\hat{y}}^{-1}.

(26)

It is straightforward to show

\displaystyle H_{j_{y}}(a^{x})[H_{j_{y}-1}(a^{x})]^{2}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{L_{x}}Z_{0,j_{x}\hat{x}+j_{y}\hat{y}}Z_{0,j_{x}\hat{x}+(j_{y}-1)\hat{y}}Z_{0,j_{x}\hat{x}+(j_{y}-2)\hat{y}}^{-2},

and in general,

\displaystyle\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{p}\left[H_{-j_{y}}(a^{x})\right]^{j_{y}}=\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{p}\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{L_{x}}Z_{0,j_{x}\hat{x}-j_{y}\hat{y}}

(27)

because the last factor
Z_{0}^{p}=1
vanishes from the product. In the same way we can show

\displaystyle\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{p}\left[V_{-j_{x}}(a^{y})\right]^{j_{x}}=\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{p}\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{L_{y}}Z_{0,-j_{x}\hat{x}+j_{y}\hat{y}}.

(28)

When
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
are both multiples of
p
, we can extend both of these products to cover the entire lattice and get

\displaystyle\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{L_{y}}\left[H_{-j_{y}}(a^{x})\right]^{j_{y}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{L_{x}}\left[V_{-j_{x}}(a^{y})\right]^{j_{x}}

(29)

This is the first of the extra identities as it relates the product of
a^{x}
stabilizers with those of the
a^{y}
stabilizers. This identity applies only when both
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
are multiples of
p
.

Additional extra identities arise from considering a product of
b_{i}
stabilizers:

\displaystyle\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{p}\left[H_{j_{y}}(b)\right]^{j_{y}}=\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{L_{x}}X_{1,j_{x}\hat{x}}X_{1,j_{x}\hat{x}+p\hat{y}}^{-1}.

If
L_{y}
is a multiple of
p
, extending the product over
j_{y}
over the entire lattice gives the second identity:

\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{L_{y}}\left[H_{j_{y}}(b)\right]^{j_{y}}=1.

(30)

This is the second extra identity, which holds only for
L_{y}~{\rm mod}~p=0
. Arguing along an analogous line gives

\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{L_{x}}\left[V_{j_{x}}(b)\right]^{j_{x}}=1,

(31)

when
L_{x}
is a multiple of
p
. All told, the number of independent stabilizers is
3L_{x}L_{y}-2-\left(1+\delta_{0,L_{x}\mod p}\right)

\left(1+\delta_{0,L_{y}{\rm mod}~p}\right)
, leading to the GSD

\displaystyle\log_{p}D

\displaystyle=2+\left(1+\delta_{0,L_{x}~{\rm mod}~p}\right)\left(1+\delta_{0,L_{y}~{\rm mod}~p}\right)

(32)

according to the general formula, Eq. (
21
). The
\log_{p}D
varies from 3 to 6, depending on the mod
p
parity of
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
. The
2^{6}
GSD for
p=2
in Ref.
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2018
misses the subtle variation taking place with the system size.

VI
Logical operators

In the previous section, we successfully counted the GSD by utilizing Eq. (

and hence

Z

H_{j_{y}}(Z_{0})

\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{L_{x}}\left[V_{-j_{x}}(a^{y})\right]^{j_{x}}=M(Z_{0})

For counting the number of independent logical operators amongst

\displaystyle H_{j_{y}}(a^{x})

\displaystyle=\left[H_{j_{y}-1}(Z_{0})\right]^{-1}H_{j_{y}}(Z_{0}),

{\mathbb{D}}_{i}^{x\bar{y}}
operators are truly independent. For that, we start with the identity

\displaystyle\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{p}\left[D^{xy}_{i+j_{y}\hat{y}}(b)\right]^{j_{y}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{D}_{i}^{xy}\left[\mathbb{D}_{i+p\hat{y}}^{xy}\right]^{-1},

\displaystyle\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{p}\left[D^{x\bar{y}}_{i+j_{y}\hat{y}}(b)\right]^{j_{y}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{D}_{i}^{x\bar{y}}\left[\mathbb{D}_{i+p\hat{y}}^{x\bar{y}}\right]^{-1},

(43)

similar to Eq. (
40
) for the horizontal logical operators. The first identity shows that
{\mathbb{D}}_{i}^{xy}|{\rm GS}\rangle={\mathbb{D}}_{i+p\hat{y}}^{xy}|{\rm GS}\rangle
. For
L_{g}~{\rm mod}~p\neq 0
, then, every diagonal operator
{\mathbb{D}}_{i}^{xy}
becomes equivalent, leaving only one independent logical operator
\mathbb{D}_{i}^{xy}
. Similarly, there is only one independent
\mathbb{D}_{i}^{x\bar{y}}
when
L_{g}~{\rm mod}~p\neq 0
.

The argument breaks down for
L_{g}~{\rm mod}~p=0
, and one must consider another kind of identities

\displaystyle D_{i}^{xy}(b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{D}_{i-\hat{y}}^{xy}\left[\mathbb{D}_{i}^{xy}\right]^{-2}\mathbb{D}_{i+\hat{y}}^{xy},

\displaystyle D_{i}^{x\bar{y}}(b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{D}_{i-\hat{y}}^{x\bar{y}}\left[\mathbb{D}_{i}^{x\bar{y}}\right]^{-2}\mathbb{D}_{i+\hat{y}}^{x\bar{y}}.

(44)

Since
D_{i}^{xy}(b)|{\rm GS}\rangle=D_{i}^{x\bar{y}}(b)|{\rm GS}\rangle=|{\rm GS}\rangle
, one can express one of the diagonal operators on the r.h.s. of Eq. (
44
) in terms of the other two. There are thus only two independent logical operators among
\mathbb{D}_{i}^{xy}
’s and also among
\mathbb{D}_{i}^{x\bar{y}}
’s when
L_{g}~{\rm mod}~p=0
.

Since
p
is a prime number, the condition
L_{g}~{\rm mod}~p=0
is equivalent to
L_{x}~{\rm mod}~p=L_{y}~{\rm mod}~p=0
. It then seems the number of independent
\mathbb{D}_{i}^{xy}
and
\mathbb{D}_{i}^{x\bar{y}}
operators are
2+2\delta_{0,L_{x}~{\rm mod}~p}\delta_{0,L_{y}~{\rm mod}~p}
. However, there exist additional identities constraining the independence of logical operators:

\displaystyle M(X_{0})\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{L_{y}}H_{j_{y}}(X_{1})\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{L_{x}}V_{j_{x}}(X_{2})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{L_{g}}\mathbb{D}_{j_{y}\hat{y}}^{xy},

\displaystyle M(X_{0}^{-1})\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{L_{y}}H_{j_{y}}(X_{1})\prod_{j_{x}=1}^{L_{x}}V_{j_{x}}(X_{2})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j_{y}=1}^{L_{g}}\mathbb{D}_{j_{y}\hat{y}}^{x\bar{y}}.

(45)

These identities apply irrespective of
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
.
H_{j_{y}}(X_{1})
and
V_{j_{x}}(X_{2})
are valid logical operators for all values of
L_{x},L_{y}
. According to the argument given at the beginning of the subsection,
M(X_{0})
is a logical operator when
L_{x}
or
L_{y}
is incommensurate with
p
, but acts as an identity operator when both
L_{x},L_{y}
are commensurate with
p
. In either case, the two relations given in Eq. (
45
) place constraints on
\mathbb{D}_{i}^{xy}
and
\mathbb{D}_{i}^{x\bar{y}}
and reduce their degrees of freedom each by one, making the total number of diagonal operators equal to
2\delta_{0,L_{x}~{\rm mod}~p}\delta_{0,L_{y}~{\rm mod}~p}
.

Gathering all the statements, we once again arrive at the number of logical operators given by
N_{lo}=2+\left(1+\delta_{0,L_{x}~{\rm mod}~p}\right)

\left(1+\delta_{0,L_{y}~{\rm mod}~p}\right)
.

IX
Aharonov-Bohm phase

It must be admitted that the phase factors coming from the
(q,p_{x})
and
(q,p_{y})
braiding are highly unusual. In this section, we present some ways to understand these factors in the framework of the conventional Aharonov-Bohm (AB) phase. It is well known that the statistical phase can be understood as an AB phase due to the magnetic flux tightly attached to the quasiparticles. We can view the monopole-monopole braiding phases as also arising from the magnetic flux localized at the position of the monopole, which is experienced by the test monopole. We regard the monopole
q
at
{\bm{r}}_{q}=(x_{q},y_{q})
as the one with the magnetic flux attached and the
p_{x}
or
p_{y}
monopole as the test charge. The situation corresponds to
p_{x}
braiding round
q
.

The magnetic flux attached to the monopole
q
is given by Eq. (
14
) with
(m,n)=(1,1)
which becomes, in the continuum limit,

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta_{b,d})\epsilon^{ab}\epsilon^{cd}\partial_{a}\partial_{c}A^{bd}

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}^{2}A^{yy}+\partial_{y}^{2}A^{xx}-\partial_{x}\partial_{y}A^{xy}.

(53)

The localized magnetic field
B({\bm{r}})=(2\pi q/p)\delta^{2}({\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}_{q})
in the rank-2 U(1) gauge theory comes from the tensor potential such as

\displaystyle A^{xx}({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi q}{p}(y-y_{q})\delta(x-x_{q})\theta(y-y_{q})

\displaystyle A^{xy}({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle=A^{yy}({\bm{r}})=0.

(54)

One can indeed show that a localized magnetic flux is generated by this choice of the tensor potential using Eq. (
53
). Other choices of tensor potential for the same magnetic field are discussed in the Appendix.

It is argued that the
(q,p_{x})
and
(q,p_{y})
braiding phases
\phi_{x}
and
\phi_{y}
obtained in the previous section are captured as area integrals

\displaystyle\phi_{x}

\displaystyle=p_{x}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}dx\int_{y_{-}}^{y_{+}}dy\left[\int_{y_{0}}^{y}B(x,y^{\prime})dy^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle\phi_{y}

\displaystyle=-p_{y}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}dx\int_{y_{-}}^{y_{+}}dy\left[\int_{x_{0}}^{x}B(x^{\prime},y)dx^{\prime}\right].

(55)

The integration area is assumed to be a square
[x_{-},x_{+}]\times[y_{-},y_{+}]
, which reflects the braiding path used in the previous section. The key difference from the conventional AB phase integral is that the
x
or
y
integral of the
B
-field is being used as an integrand, instead of the
B
field itself. Performing the integrals assuming the localized magnetic field
B({\bm{r}})=(2\pi q/p)\delta^{2}({\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}_{q})
gives

\displaystyle\phi_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi qp_{x}}{p}[(y_{+}\!-\!y_{q})-(y_{+}\!-\!y_{-})\theta(y_{0}\!-\!y_{q})],

\displaystyle\phi_{y}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\pi qp_{y}}{p}[(x_{+}\!-\!x_{q})+(x_{+}\!-\!x_{-})\theta(x_{0}\!-\!x_{q})].

(56)

Depending on the choice of the integration constant
y_{0}
, one gets
\theta(y_{0}-y_{q})=1
and
\phi_{x}=(2\pi qp_{x}/p)(y_{-}-y_{q})
, or
\theta(y_{0}-y_{q})=0
and
(2\pi qp_{x}/p)(y_{+}-y_{q})
. Recall further that the
p_{x}
monopole can only hop by
p
lattice spacing along the
y
direction, so that both
y_{+}
and
y_{-}
are equal to
y_{x}
, the initial
y
-coordinate of the
p_{x}
monopole, up to multiples of
p
. As a result,

\displaystyle\phi_{x}=\frac{2\pi qp_{x}}{p}(y_{x}-y_{q})+2\pi n

(57)

where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, which matches the phase derived from the lattice model (see Table
3
). The same consideration also gives
\phi_{y}=(2\pi qp_{y}/p)(x_{q}-x_{y})
mod
2\pi
. Cast as integrals of a gauge-invariant magnetic field, the two expressions in Eq. (
55
) are manifestly gauge-invariant. In the Appendix we show how to convert the area integrals in Eq. (
55
) to line integrals of some emergent vector potentials.

X
Discussion

We have carefully revisited the Higgsing process proposed in Refs.
Ma et al. 2018
;
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2018
to come up with an easy-to-implement recipe for constructing a stabilizer spin model from a given LGT. The Higgsing procedure is applied to the rank-2 U(1) LGT defined on a two-dimensional square lattice to obtain the rank-2
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
toric code. The monopole and dipole quatiparticles are the elementary excitations of the R2TC. Allowed motions of both monopoles and dipoles in the R2TC model were analyzed in detail.

The GSD of R2TC model varies from
p^{3}
to
p^{6}
depending on the commensurability of the lattice size with
p
, the local Hilbert space dimension. The counting of GSD was performed by examining the number of independent stabilizers as well as the number of independent logical operators. Our interpretation of the logical operators in terms of monopole-pair and dipole-pair creation and re-annihilation processes may shed further light on previous reports on the system size dependence of the GSD
Hsieh and Halász 2017
;
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2018
;
Ma et al. 2018
.

The braiding statistics between a pair of monopoles and a monopole-dipole pair was worked out. We propose a new integral formula capturing the braiding statistics, which invites further field-theoretical investigation of the emergent quasiparticle dynamics. Checking the stability of our spin model remains as a future work, which can be done by perturbative continuous unitary transformation method previously exploited in the study of the phase diagram of the toric code or fracton models
Vidal et al. 2009
;
Mühlhauser et al. 2020
.

The following integral expressions for the statistical phases were introduced in Sec.

\displaystyle\phi_{x}

\displaystyle=p_{x}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}dx\int_{y_{-}}^{y_{+}}dy\left[\int_{y_{0}}^{y}B(x,y^{\prime})dy^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle\phi_{y}

\displaystyle=-p_{y}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}dx\int_{y_{-}}^{y_{+}}dy\left[\int_{x_{0}}^{x}B(x^{\prime},y)dx^{\prime}\right].

(58)

It is possible to apply Stokes’ theorem and express the above surface integrals as line integrals of some effective vector potentials. First one writes the magnetic field
B(x,y)
in the rank-2 U(1) gauge theory as the curl

\displaystyle B(x,y)=\partial_{x}C^{y}(x,y)-\partial_{y}C^{x}(x,y),

(59)

where

is given by

\displaystyle C^{x}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\partial_{x}A^{xy}(x,y)-\partial_{y}A^{xx}(x,y),

\displaystyle C^{y}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}A^{yy}(x,y)-\gamma\partial_{y}A^{xy}(x,y)

(60)

for some arbitrary constant
\gamma
. Accordingly, one can write

\displaystyle\int^{y}_{y_{0}}B(x,y^{\prime})dy^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}\alpha_{x}^{y}-\partial_{y}\alpha_{x}^{x},

\displaystyle\int^{x}_{x_{0}}B(x^{\prime},y)dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}\alpha_{y}^{y}-\partial_{x}\alpha_{y}^{x}

(61)

where

\displaystyle\alpha^{x}_{x}

\displaystyle=\int^{y}_{y_{0}}dy^{\prime}\left[C^{x}(x,y^{\prime})-C^{x}(x,y_{0})\right],

\displaystyle\alpha^{y}_{x}

\displaystyle=\int^{y}_{y_{0}}dy^{\prime}C^{y}(x,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\alpha^{x}_{y}

\displaystyle=\int^{x}_{x_{0}}dx^{\prime}C^{x}(x^{\prime},y)

\displaystyle\alpha^{y}_{y}

\displaystyle=\int^{x}_{x_{0}}dx^{\prime}\left[C^{y}(x^{\prime},y)-C^{y}(x_{0},y)\right].

(62)

Thus we obtain a pair of emergent vector potentials
{\bm{\alpha}}_{x}=(\alpha_{x}^{x},\alpha_{x}^{y})
and
{\bm{\alpha}}_{y}=(\alpha_{y}^{x},\alpha_{y}^{y})
with which to express the AB phases as line integrals

\displaystyle\phi_{x}

\displaystyle=p_{x}\oint d{\bm{r}}\cdot{\bm{\alpha}}_{x},~~~\phi_{y}=-p_{y}\oint d{\bm{r}}\cdot{\bm{\alpha}}_{y}.

(63)

The gauge invariance of
\phi_{x},\phi_{y}
under the transformation

\displaystyle A^{ab}

\displaystyle\rightarrow A^{ab}+\partial_{a}f^{a}

\displaystyle(a=b)

\displaystyle\rightarrow A^{ab}+\partial_{a}f^{b}+\partial_{b}f^{a}

\displaystyle(a\neq b)

(64)

follows readily.
