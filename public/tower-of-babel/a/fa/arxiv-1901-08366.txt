Notes: An assessment of some closed-form expressions for the Voigt function III: Combinations of the Lorentz and Gauss functions Journal: J. Quant. Spectros. & Rad. Transfer, received December 2018, accepted January 2019; doi: 10.1016/j.jqsrt.2019.01.017
Franz Schreier

Abstract
A variety of “pseudo-Voigt” functions, i.e. a linear combination of the Lorentz and Gauss function (occasionally augmented with a correction term), have been proposed as a closed-form approximation for the convolution of the Lorentz and Gauss function known as the Voigt function. First, a compact review of several approximations using a consistent notation is presented. The comparison with accurate reference values indicates relative errors as large as some percent.

1
Introduction

Rapid yet accurate computation of the Voigt function
[
1
]
is a challenge in many fields of the physical sciences.
Because the convolution integral of a Lorentz and Gauss function does not have an analytical solution, approximations have been discussed in numerous papers.
Whereas many modern “state-of-the art” algorithms evaluate the closely related complex error function (also known as complex probability function, plasma dispersion function, or Fadde(y)eva function, cf. e.g.,
2
,
3
,
4
) utilizing sophisticated numerical techniques,
“simple” closed-form expressions still appear to be attractive.

Linear combinations of the Lorentz and/or Gauss functions have been suggested by several authors.

Flores-Llamas et al. 1991
proposed a sum of the Lorentzian and its derivatives and
Melcher and Gerth 1977
fitted the Voigt functions by “generalized Lorentz functions”.
McLean et al. 1994
have further developed an approximation originally suggested by
Martin and Puerta 1981a
,
Martin and Puerta 1981b
,
Puerta and Martin 1981
,
Puerta and Martin 1983
and proposed a
superposition of four Lorentzians
[
12
, for a recent assessment see]
.
Linear combinations of a Lorentz and Gauss function (sometimes called “pseudo-Voigt” function and occasionally including a correction term)
have been suggested or used by
Whiting 1968
,
Matveev 1972
,
Peyre and Principi 1972
,
Kielkopf 1973
,
Wertheim et al. 1974
,
Thompson et al. 1987
,
Teodorescu et al. 1994
,
Ida et al. 2000
and
Liu et al. 2001
.

In this note we present an assessment of closed-form expressions for the Voigt function using a combination of Lorentz and Gauss functions.
After a short review of the basic definitions in the next subsection, we describe several combinations using a consistent notation (in chronological order).
The results of our tests using an accurate Voigt function code as reference are presented in Section
3
.
The codes have been implemented in Python and a Scientific Python (
scipy.org
) implementation has been used as a reference.
In the final Section
4
we provide a summary and some conclusions.

2
Theory

2.1
The Voigt function

The Voigt function (normalized to
\sqrt{\pi}
) is defined by

K(x,y)~=~{y\over\pi}~\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}}\over(x-t)^{2}+y^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t

(1)

where
x
is a measure of the distance to the center peak, and
y
is essentially the ratio of the Lorentzian and Gaussian width,
y=\sqrt{\ln 2}\gamma_{\text{L}}/\gamma_{\text{G}}
.
At the line center
x=0
the Voigt function can be expressed as the exponentially scaled complementary error function

\displaystyle K(0,y)~=~\exp(y^{2})\bigl(1-\mathop{\rm erf}(y)\bigr)~=~\exp(y^{2})\mathop{\rm erfc}(y)~.

(2)

The Voigt function is symmetric, i.e.
K(-x,y)=K(x,y)
and essentially reduces to the Lorentz function for large
|x+\mathrm{i}y|
.
The Voigt function is identical to the real part of the complex function

\displaystyle w(z)~

\displaystyle\equiv~K(x,y)\,+\,\mathrm{i}L(x,y)~=~{\mathrm{i}\over\pi}~\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}~{e^{-t^{2}}\over z-t}~\mathrm{d}t

(3)

\displaystyle=~\exp(-z^{2})\mathop{\rm erfc}(-\mathrm{i}z)\qquad\hbox{ with }\quad z=x+\mathrm{i}y.

(4)

2.2
The
Whiting 1968
approximation

To our knowledge the first approximation of the Voigt function using a combination of Lorentz and Gauss functions

K(x,y)~=~K(0,y)\left[\bigr(1-\eta(y)\bigl)G(x)~+~\eta(y)L(x)\right]\\

(5)

with

\displaystyle L(x)~

\displaystyle=~\left[1+\left({x\over x_{\text{h}}}\right)^{2}\right]^{-1}

(6)

\displaystyle G(x)~

\displaystyle=~\exp{\left(-\ln 2(x/x_{\text{h}})^{2}\right)}

(7)

is due to
Whiting 1968
.
The weight factor is given by the ratio of the widths of the Lorentz and Voigt profiles or

\displaystyle\eta~

\displaystyle=~y/x_{\text{h}}

(8)

with the half width

\displaystyle x_{\text{h}}~

\displaystyle=~\tfrac{1}{2}\left(y+\sqrt{y^{2}+4\ln 2}\right)~.

(9)

An improved approximation is obtained by adding a correction term (i.e.
K(x,y)\longrightarrow K(x,y)+C(x,y)
) that is given by “kind of Lorentzians and Gaussians”,

\displaystyle C(x,y)=0.016\,\eta(1-\eta)\,\Biggl[

\displaystyle\exp{\left(-0.4(x/x_{\text{h}})^{(9/4)}\right)}

(10)

\displaystyle-{10\over 10+(x/x_{\text{h}})^{(9/4)}}\Biggr]

By definition, the approximation (
5
) is exact in the center at
x=0
.
Furthermore, both approximations are exact for the limiting cases of pure Lorentz and Gauss functions.
According to the author, “this approximation matches the Voigt profile within 5 per cent at worst and is generally within 3 per cent or less.”

2.3
The
Matveev 1972
approximation

The approximation is given by

K(x,y)~=~{\sqrt{\ln 2}\over x_{\text{h}}}\>\left[(1-\eta)G(x)~+~{\eta\over\sqrt{\pi\ln 2}}L(x)\right]

(11)

with a correction term

\displaystyle C(x,y)~=

\displaystyle~{\eta(1-\eta)\over\sqrt{\pi\ln 2}}\left({1.5\over\ln 2}+1+\eta\right)

(12)

\displaystyle~~~\times\Biggl[0.066\,\exp{\left(-0.4\left({x\over x_{\text{h}}}\right)^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~-{1\over 40-5.5\,\left({x\over x_{\text{h}}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{x}{x_{\text{h}}}\right)^{4}}\Biggr]~.

The weight
\eta
is defined as in (
8
) and the half width is given by a refinement of Whiting’s approximation (
9
)

2.4
The
Kielkopf 1973
approximation

Without correction term this approximation is identical to (
5
), but with the weight and half width defined as

\displaystyle\eta~

\displaystyle=~{yx_{\text{h}}\over 1+yx_{\text{h}}}

(15)

\displaystyle x_{\text{h}}~

\displaystyle=~\tfrac{1}{2}y\,\left(1+k_{\text{e}}\ln 2+\sqrt{(1-k_{\text{e}}\ln 2)^{2}+{4\ln 2\over y^{2}}}\right)

(16)

The correction contains the difference of a Gaussian and Lorentzian multiplied with a rational function of
x
(see Online
B
for numerical values)

C(x,y)~=~\eta(1-\eta)\bigl(G(x)-L(x)\bigr){k_{1}+k_{2}x^{2}\over 1+k_{3}x^{2}+k_{4}x^{4}}~.

(17)

This approximation “is accurate to the order of 0.0001 of the peak value of the function”.

2.5
The
Thompson et al. 1987
approximation

In contrast to the three approximations discussed so far the weight factors of the
Thompson et al. 1987
(and
Liu et al. 2001
, next subsection) approaches are defined by power series of
y
.
The pseudo-Voigt function is written as

K(x,y)~=~{\sqrt{\ln 2}\over x_{\text{h}}}\;\left[(1-\eta)G(x)+{\eta\over\sqrt{\pi\ln 2}}L(x)\right]

(18)

with the Voigt half width and weight (see Online
B
)

\displaystyle x_{\text{h}}~

\displaystyle=~\Bigl(t_{0}+t_{1}y+t_{2}y^{2}+t_{3}y^{3}+t_{4}y^{4}+y^{5}\Bigr)^{(1/5)}

(19)

\displaystyle\eta~

\displaystyle=~\tau_{1}{y\over x_{\text{h}}}+\tau_{2}\left({y\over x_{\text{h}}}\right)^{2}+\tau_{3}\left({y\over x_{\text{h}}}\right)^{3}

(20)

According to
Ida et al. 2000
the maximum deviation of about
1.2\%
is found at
y/(y+\sqrt{\ln 2})\approx 0.5
.

2.6
The
Liu et al. 2001
approximation

Introducing a dimensionless parameter
d=(y-\sqrt{\ln 2})/(y+\sqrt{\ln 2})
and approximating the weights (see Eq. (
34
) in the Online Appendix) as

\displaystyle c_{\text{L}}~

\displaystyle=~l_{0}+l_{1}d+l_{2}d^{2}+l_{3}d^{3}

(21)

\displaystyle c_{\text{G}}~

\displaystyle=~g_{0}+g_{1}d+g_{2}d^{2}+g_{3}d^{3}

(22)

the pseudo-Voigt function is written as

K(x,y)~=~{c_{\text{L}}\over x_{\text{h}}\sqrt{\pi}}L(x)~+~c_{\text{G}}{\sqrt{\ln 2}\over x_{\text{h}}}\,G(x)

(23)

with the half width given by the
Olivero and Longbothum 1977
approximation

\displaystyle x_{\text{h}}(y)

\displaystyle=~\bigl(y+\sqrt{\ln 2}\bigr)\;\bigl(1-0.18121(1-d^{2})-\beta\sin\pi d\bigr)

(24)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=~0.023665\exp{(0.6d)}+0.00418\exp{(-1.9d)}

According to the abstract
[
21
]
“the maximum errors of width, area, and peak … are 0.01%, 0.2%, and 0.55%, respectively.”

3
Results

Ignoring the correction terms Eqs. (
10
), (
12
), and (
17
), all algorithms use a weighted sum of the Lorentz and Gauss function,
where the Lorentz weight tends to one for
y\gg 1
and zero for
y\ll 1
.
Fig.
1
compares the weights of the Lorentz function for all five approximations.
The weights of
Whiting 1968
and
Matveev 1972
are identical by construction, Eq. (
8
), and the
Thompson et al. 1987
and
Liu et al. 2001
weights appear to be very similar (with differences for small and large
y
only).
For
y\approx 1
(i.e. equal width of the Lorentz and Gauss function) all weights are approximately 0.7.
Note that except for
Liu et al. 2001
the Gaussian weight is simply given by
1-\eta
.

As discussed in
Schreier 2011
and in our previous assessments of simple closed-form approximations of the Voigt function
[
25
,
26
,
12
]
,
the range of
y
values encountered in molecular spectroscopy and atmospheric and astrophysical applications spans many orders of magnitude.
In Fig.
2
(left) we compare the pseudo-Voigt approximations with reference values for
w(z)
, Eq. (
3
), obtained with the
wofz
code
(algorithm originally based on
Poppe and Wijers 1990a
,
Poppe and Wijers 1990b
and later refined with ideas from
Zaghloul and Ali 2011
;
Scientific Python (
http://scipy.org
) implementation
scipy.special.wofz
with at least 13 significant digits according to the documentation).

The Voigt function (left) and the relative error (right) for
y=10
(top),
y=1.0
,
y=0.1
, and
y=0.01
(bottom).
Note the different range of
x
values in the four rows and the linear

The function values shown on the left appear to be in reasonably good agreement with the reference.
However, significant problems show up in the relative errors
|K-K_{\text{wofz}}|/K_{\text{wofz}}
(right side)
and for all approximations the maximum error is larger than 1 percent.

In the line center (
x=0
) the Whiting and Kielkopf approximations are exact by definition (assuming that the exponentially scaled complementary error function
K(0,y)=\mathop{\rm erfce}(y)
, Eq. (
2
), is evaluated exactly).
The other approximations have errors of some percent for
y=1
, but smaller errors for smaller and larger
y
.
Note that in the line center the Whiting and Kielkopf corrections vanish, i.e.
C(0,y)=0
, in contrast to the Matveev correction.

In the line wings only Matveev’s approximation (with and without correction) has relative errors decreasing with increasing
|x|
.
Evaluating the Voigt function for very large
x
(
x\leq 2000
for
y=10
and
x\leq 100
for
y=1
) indicates that
for all other approximations the errors become constant for large
x
.
For Kielkopf this asymptotic error is about
10^{-4}
for
y=10
and
0.02
for
y=0.1
and
0.01
.

For small
y\leq 0.1
Matveev’s approximation appears to be superior (with errors less than a few percent), however, for
y>1
the correction term worsens the accuracy,
and the results shown in the top of Fig.
2
have been obtained without the correction (
12
).
For Kielkopf and Matveev, the largest errors occur for intermediate values of
x
,
whereas the other approximations always fail for large
x
.

The contour plots of relative errors shown in Fig.

Except for the

(see

for a discussion of the

For Whiting and Kielkopf a relative high accuracy is achieved near the origin for small and large

The maximum relative error indicated in the title of all subplots identifies Kielkopf’s code (with correction) as the most accurate approximation.
However, this maximum error is relatively large for modern standards (about six percent in the wings),
and for small

Despite the significant accuracy problems of all pseudo-Voigt approximations it is nevertheless instructive to test the numerical performance of the codes.
Simple tests within the IPython interpreter
[
30
]
indicate that the Kielkopf and Matveev approximations are somewhat slower than the optimized combination of
the
Humlíček 1982
and
Weideman 1994

[

.
However, evaluation of HNO
3
cross sections in the microwave for a series of pressures and temperatures corresponding to Earth’s atmosphere in the
0\,\text{--}\,120\rm\,km

range is about a factor two slower with these two pseudo-Voigt approximations compared to the
Humlíček 1982
–
Weideman 1994
combination.
For details see the online appendix
D
.

4
Summary and Conclusions

Closed-form expressions for the Voigt function based on combinations of Lorentz- and Gauss-type functions show significant accuracy problems,
with relative errors in the percent range.
Note that in this study we have only considered “pseudo-Voigt” approximations based on an analytical, closed-form expression for the weight of the Lorentz and Gauss components
(as a function of
y
, the ratio of the Lorentz and Gauss width).
In several studies pseudo-Voigt approximations have been used for analysis of experimental data, where the weight has been estimated by least squares fitting
[
17
,
33
, e.g.]
.

Our conclusions now are therefore similar to those given in
Schreier 2016
,
Schreier 2017
:
Closed-form expressions as presented here might be desirable for certain applications, but their quality is limited.
In general approximations based on modern state-of-the-art numerical methods, e.g. rational approximations as discussed in
Humlíček 1979
,
Humlíček 1982
,
Weideman 1994
and
Schreier 2011
,
Schreier 2018b
are recommended.

\displaystyle~{1\over\gamma_{\text{G}}}\left({\ln 2\over\pi}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\exp\left[-\ln 2\left({\nu-\hat{\nu}\over\gamma_{\text{G}}}\right)^{2}\right]~.

(26)

\displaystyle g_{\text{V}}(\nu-\hat{\nu},\gamma_{\text{L}},\gamma_{\text{G}})~=

\displaystyle~{\sqrt{\ln 2/\pi}\over\gamma_{\text{G}}}\,K(x,y)
