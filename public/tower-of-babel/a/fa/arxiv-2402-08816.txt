Abstract
We design a randomized data structure that, for a fully dynamic graph G G updated by edge insertions and deletions and integers k , d k,d fixed upon initialization, maintains the answer to the Split Completion problem: whether one can add k k edges to G G to obtain a split graph. The data structure can be initialized on an edgeless n n -vertex graph in time n ⋅ ( k ​ d ⋅ log ⁡ n ) 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) n\cdot(kd\cdot\log n)^{\mathcal{O}(1)} , and the amortized time complexity of an update is 5 k ⋅ ( k ​ d ⋅ log ⁡ n ) 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) 5^{k}\cdot(kd\cdot\log n)^{\mathcal{O}(1)} . The answer provided by the data structure is correct with probability 1 − 𝒪 ⁡ ( n − d ) 1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-d}) .

Introduction

In the field of
parameterized algorithms
, one measures resources used by an algorithm not only in terms of the total size of the considered instance, but also in terms of auxiliary quantitative measures associated with the instance, called
parameters
. Recently there is a growing interest in applying this principle to the area of
dynamic data structures
. In this context, we typically consider an instance
I
of a fixed problem of interest, with an associated parameter
k
. The instance is dynamic, in the sense that it is updated over time by problem-specific update operations, while for simplicity we assume that the parameter stays intact. The goal is to design a data structure that would maintain whether
I
is a yes-instance of the problem under the updates to
I
. Here we allow the update time to depend in any computable way on the parameter and sublinearly on the instance size; for instance, we are insterested in (possibly amortized) update times of the form
f(k)
,
f(k)\cdot(\log n)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, or
f(k)\cdot n^{o(1)}
, where
f
is some computable function and
n
is the size of
I
. Note that we allow
f
to be superpolynomial, so this framework may be applied even to
\mathsf{NP}
-hard problems, as long as their static parameterized variants are fixed-parameter tractable.

Parameterized dynamic data structures were first systematically investigated by Iwata and Oka
[
23
]
, followed by
Alman et al.
[
1
]
. These works provided data structures with update times
f(k)
or
f(k)\cdot(\log n)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
for several classic problems such as
Vertex Cover
,
Cluster Vertex Deletion
,
Hitting Set
,
Feedback Vertex Set
, or
Longest Path
. Other recent advances include data structures for maintaining various graph decompositions together with runs of dynamic programming procedures
[
8
,
13
,
14
,
26
,
28
]
, treatment of parameterized string problems from the dynamic perspective
[
32
]
, and even an application of the framework in the context of timed automata
[
19
]
.

A topic within parameterized algorithms that could be particularly productive from the point of view of dynamic data structures is that of
graph modification problems
. In this context, we fix a graph class
\mathcal{C}
and a set of graph operations
\Pi
(e.g. vertex deletion, edge deletion, edge insertion), and consider the following parameterized problem: given a graph
G
and a parameter
k
, decide whether one can apply at most
k
operations from
\Pi
to
G
in order to obtain a graph belonging to
\mathcal{C}
. By instantiating different graph classes
\mathcal{C}
and sets of operations
\Pi
, we obtain a wealth of parameterized problems with vastly different complexities, highly dependent on the combinatorics of the class
\mathcal{C}
in question. The most widely studied are
vertex deletion problems
(only vertex deletions are allowed),
edge deletion
and
completion problems
(only edge deletions, respectively insertions, are allowed), and
editing problems
(both edge deletions and edge insertions are allowed).

Note that
Vertex Cover
,
Feedback Vertex Set
, and
Cluster Vertex Deletion
can be understood as vertex deletion problems, for the classes of edgeless, acyclic, and cluster graphs, respectively. Thus, the results of Iwata and Oka
[
23
]
and of Alman et al.
[
1
]
already give parameterized dynamic data structures for some basic graph modification problems, but the impressive volume of work on static parameterized algorithms for such problems suggests that there is much more to be explored.

In this work we focus on the case of
split graphs
; recall that a graph
G
is a split graph if the vertices of
G
can be partitioned into sets
C
and
I
so that
C
is a clique and
I
is an independent set. As proved by Földes and Hammer
[
17
]
, split graphs are exactly graphs that exclude
2K_{2}
,
C_{4}
, and
C_{5}
as induced subgraphs. Hence, a standard branching strategy solves
Split Vertex Deletion
in time
5^{k}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
and
Split Completion
in time
5^{k}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
(note that
Split Edge Deletion
is equivalent to
Split Completion
in the complement of the given graph). In fact, faster algorithms are known:
Split Vertex Deletion
can be solved in time
1.2738^{k}\cdot k^{\mathcal{O}(\log k)}+n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

[
10
]
, while
Split Completion
can be solved in subexponential parameterized time, more precisely in time
k^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k})}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, and admits a kernel with
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
vertices
[
18
]
. Somewhat surprisingly,
Split Editing
is polynomial-time solvable
[
20
]
, while both
Split Vertex Deletion
and
Split Completion
remain
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
27
,
31
]
. This makes graph modification problems related to split graphs very well understood in the static setting, and of remarkably low complexity, which suggests that they may serve as a suitable testbed for considerations from the point of view of dynamic data structures.

In fact, split graphs have already been considered in the dynamic setting. Ibarra
[
22
]
gave a dynamic data structure with constant update time for the membership problem: the data structure maintains whether the graph in question is a split graph. This can be understood as the treatment of (any) modification problem for parameter
k
equal to
0
. Further aspects of dynamic maintenance of split graphs were investigated by Heggernes and Mancini
[
21
]
.

Our contribution.

In this work we propose a randomized dynamic data structure for the
Split Completion
problem whose amortized update time depends polylogarithmically on the size of the graph. Formally, we prove the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

There is a randomized data structure that for a fully dynamic graph
G
, updated by edge deletions and edge insertions, and a parameter
k
fixed upon initialization, maintains the answer to the following question: can one add at most
k
edges to
G
to obtain a split graph. The data structure can be initialized on an edgeless
n
-vertex graph and an accuracy parameter
d\in\mathbb{N}
in time
k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot d^{2}\cdot n\cdot(\log n)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, and the amortized time complexity of updates is
5^{k}\cdot k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot d^{2}\cdot(\log n)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
. At all times, the answer provided by the data structure is correct with probability at least
1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-d})
.

We note that by a tiny modification of the algorithm, we can also provide an analogous data structure for the dynamic
Split Edge Deletion
problem, with same complexity guarantees.

Overview.

Let us now discuss the main ideas behind the proof of
Theorem

1.1
. The basic approach is to try to dynamize the standard
5^{k}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
-time branching algorithm, which works as follows: recursively find an obstruction — an induced subgraph belonging to
\{2K_{2},C_{4},C_{5}\}
— and branch into at most
5
different ways on how to destroy this obstruction by adding an edge. To implement this strategy in the dynamic setting, we need to have an efficient way of finding an obstruction in the current graph, or concluding that it is split. Indeed, if we get such a localization procedure, then the branching algorithm can be implemented using data structure’s own methods for inserting and removing edges.

The next observation is that if
(G,k)
is a
Yes
-instance of the
Split Completion
problem, then in particular it is also a
Yes
-instance of the
Split Editing
problem: one can obtain a split graph from
G
by adding or removing at most
k
edges. This is equivalent to the following statement: there is a partition
(A,B)
of the vertex set of
G
such that the number of edges with both endpoints in
B
plus the number of non-edges with both endpoints in
A
is at most
k
. For a partition
(A,B)
, this number is called the
splittance
of
(A,B)
. As observed by Hammer and Simeone
[
20
]
, a partition with optimal splittance can be computed in polynomial time using a simple greedy argument. Ibarra
[
22
]
dynamized this observation and showed that a partition
(A,B)
with optimum splittance can be maintained with
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
worst-case update time. Moreover, upon every edge update, the data structure of Ibarra moves only
\mathcal{O}(1)
vertices between the parts
A
and
B
.

Therefore, thanks to the result of Ibarra we may assume that a partition
(A,B)
with optimum splittance is available to us; this gives us a basic structural understanding of
G
. If the splittance of
(A,B)
is larger than
k
, then we are certain that
(G,k)
is a
No
-instance of
Split Completion
. Hence, from now on we will work in the
promise model
: We will assume that the maintained partition
(A,B)
has splittance bounded by
k
at all times. Lifting the result from the promise model to the general setting can be done by making a wrapper data structure that works as follows: while the splittance is larger than
k
, the new updates are put on a queue instead of directly implemented, and when the splittance becomes at most
k
again, all the enqued updates are implemented in one large batch. We remark that this is the only source of amortization in our data structure: in the promise model, the claimed running time guarantees are worst-case.

The wrapper data structure is presented in
Section

5
. We note that together with the partition
(A,B)
, the wrapper data structure also maintains sets
\mathtt{nonEdgesA}
and
\mathtt{edgesB}
consisting of non-edges with both endpoints in
A
and edges with both endpoints in
B
, with a guarantee that they are correct whenever the splittance of
(A,B)
is at most
k
. Note that then
|\mathtt{nonEdgesA}|+|\mathtt{edgesB}|\leqslant k
.

The next step is to implement efficient access to edges and non-edges crossing the partition
(A,B)
. More precisely, we need
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k^{\mathcal{O}(1)})
-time queries that given
\ell\in\mathcal{O}(k)
, are able to list:

•

for a given
a\in A
, any set of
\ell
neighbors of
a
in
B
; and

•

for a given
b\in B
, any set of
\ell
non-neighbors of
b
in
A
.

If
a
has fewer than
\ell
neighbors in
B
, then the query should list all of them; similarly for
b
. As the second query is symmetric to the first one, we focus on the latter.

First, consider the case when
a
has exactly one neighbor in
B
. Then to quickly recover this neighbor, we can maintain in the data structure the following information:

•

the sum
s_{A}
of the identifiers of all the vertices in
A
; and

•

for every vertex
u
, the sum
s_{u}
of the identifiers of all the neighbors of
u
.

Then given
a
, the identifier of the sole neighbor of
a
in
B
can be obtained by taking
s_{a}-s_{A}
and adding the identifiers of all non-neighbors of
a
in
A
, which can be listed in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k)
using the set
\mathtt{nonEdgesA}
. This trick can be lifted to listing all neighbors of
a
in
B
assuming that that their number is
\mathcal{O}(k)
using the technique of
color coding
of Alon, Yuster, and Zwick
[
2
]
. Finally, to allow listing
\ell=\mathcal{O}(k)
neighbors from possibly much larger neighborhoods, we sample in advance a polylogarithmically-sized family
\mathcal{S}
of vertex subsets of varying sizes so that for any possible neighborhood
N
, with high probability among the sampled sets there will be some
S\in\mathcal{S}
such that
\ell\leqslant|N\cap S|\leqslant\mathcal{O}(\ell)
. Then we apply the ideas presented above to list the members of
N\cap S
, whose number is already suitably bounded. We remark that this final trick is the only element of the reasoning that we do not know how to derandomize.
Implementation of this part of the proof is in
Section

4
.

The last step is to implement the procedure for finding of an obstruction. Here, the main idea is that every obstruction present in
G
must contain a non-edge with both endpoints in
A
or an edge with both endpoints in
B
; for otherwise it would be a split graph. For these (non-)edges, we have at most
k
candidates contained in the lists
\mathtt{nonEdgesA}
and
\mathtt{edgesB}
. Therefore, while searching for an obstruction, we can immediately “anchor” two of its vertices by guessing a member of
\mathtt{nonEdgesA}\cup\mathtt{edgesB}
that is contained in the obstruction. Localizing the other two or three vertices of the obstruction requires a skillful juggle of the functionality specified above, in particular the methods for enumeration of (non-)neighbors across the partition
(A,B)
. This part of the argument can be found in
Section

6
.

Preliminaries

For a positive integer
n
, we define
[n]\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
If
x
is a positive real number, we write
[x]
as a shorthand for
[\left\lceil x\right\rceil]=\{1,2,\ldots,\left\lceil x\right\rceil\}
.
For two sets
A
and
B
, we denote their symmetric difference by

A\triangle B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
.
Given any sets
A,B,C
we write
C=A\uplus B
if
C=A\cup B
, and
A\cap B=\emptyset
, that is, if
(A,B)
is a partition of
C
.
For a boolean condition
b
, we denote by
\mathbbm{1}_{b}
the indicator function of
b
, that is,
\mathbbm{1}_{b}=1
if
b
is true, and
\mathbbm{1}_{b}=0
otherwise.

We use the
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
notation to hide polylogarithmic factors; that is,
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(f(n))
is the class of all functions
g(n)
that are upper-bounded by
c\cdot f(n)\cdot(\log n)^{c}
for some constant
c
.

Graphs.

All graphs considered in this paper are undirected and simple, that is, contain no loops or parallel edges. We often assume that the vertex set of a graph on
n
vertices is
[n]
.

Let
G
be a graph.
We denote the sets of vertices and of edges of
G
by
V(G)
and
E(G)
, respectively.
We refer to an edge
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
as
uv
, for every pair of vertices
u
and
v
.
For a vertex
v\in V(G)
, we denote the degree of
v
by
d_{G}(v)

and the set of all neighbors of
v
by
N_{G}(v)

(we may skip index
G
if it is clear from the context).
Additionally, we define
\overline{N}_{G}(v)
as the set of non-neighbors of
v
, that is,
\overline{N}_{G}(v)=V(G)\setminus(N_{G}(v)\cup\{v\})
.

For a subset
A\subseteq V(G)
, we denote by
G[A]
the subgraph of
G
induced by
A
.
For a graph
F
, we say that
G
is
F
-free if it does not contain an induced subgraph isomorphic to
F
.
Similarly, for a family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
, a graph
G
is
\mathcal{F}
-free if it is
F
-free for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
.

Split graphs.

Let
G
be a graph.
We say that
G
is
split
if its vertices can be partitioned into two sets
A
and
B
such that
G[A]
is a clique, and
G[B]
is an independent set.
For any partition
(A,B)
of
V(G)
, we denote

\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B)\coloneqq\binom{|A|}{2}-|E(G[A])|+|E(G[B])|,

that is,
\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B)
counts the number of non-edges of
G
within
A
and edges of
G
within
B
.
For the whole graph
G
, we write

\mathsf{splittance}(G)\coloneqq\min_{(A,B)\colon V(G)=A\uplus B}\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B).

One can observe that
\mathsf{splittance}(G)
counts the minimal number of edge updates (insertions or deletions) that need to be done on
G
in order to make the graph
G
split.
In particular,
G
is split if and only if
\mathsf{splittance}(G)=0
. It turns out that a partition with optimum splittance can be computed greedily by taking a prefix of the vertex set ordered by decreasing degrees, as stated formally in the following observation of Hammer and Simeone
[
20
]
.

Fact 2.1

(
[
20
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices, and let
d_{1}\geqslant d_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant d_{n}
be a sorted sequence of the vertex degrees of
G
.
Define
m\coloneqq\max\{i\in[n]\mid d_{i}\geqslant i-1\}
.
Then,

\mathsf{splittance}(G)=m(m-1)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{i}+\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}d_{i}=0,

and a certifying partition
(A,B)
of
V(G)
can be obtained by taking the vertices of
G
corresponding to the degrees
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{m})
and
(d_{m+1},\ldots,d_{n})
, respectively.

We will also make use of another characterization of split graphs, namely via a finite family of forbidden induced subgraphs.

Fact 2.2

(
[
17
]
)
.

A graph
G
is split if and only if
G
is
\mathcal{F}
-free, where
\mathcal{F}=\{2K_{2},C_{4},C_{5}\}
.

Computation model and basic data structures.

We assume the standard word RAM model of computation with machine words of length
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
. As the most basic data structures, we use
sets
(representing a set of objects that can be updated by insertions and deletions, and queried for membership) and
dictionaries
(representing a set of key-value pairs that can be updated by insertions and deletions, and queried for the value associated with a given key). For both these data structures, we use standard implementations using any kind of self-balancing binary search trees, which offer all operations in worst-case time
\mathcal{O}(\log n)\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
. In particular, graphs are represented as dictionaries mapping vertices to the sets of their neighbors. We refer to this representation as
adjacency list representation
. It allows verifying the adjacency of a pair of vertices in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
.

Most of our data structures are randomized. We would like to stress that the random choices happen only during the initialization, and therefore the events of incorrectness of further queries are
not
independent — they all depend on the initial randomness.

3
Dynamic splittance

In this section, we discuss how to maintain the value of
\mathsf{splittance}(G)
with a witnessing partition
(A,B)
of
V(G)
for a dynamic graph
G
.
The data structure is a simple adaptation of the data structure of Ibarra
[
22
]
who showed how to maintain whether a dynamic graph
G
is split.
We include the proof for the sake of self-containedness.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a data structure
\mathsf{DSplit}[n]
that runs on a dynamic graph
G=(V,E)
on
n
vertices, and maintains a partition
(A,B)
of its vertices satisfying
\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B)=\mathsf{splittance}(G)
.
The data structure supports the following operations:

•

\mathtt{initialize}(n)
: fixes the set of vertices

V\coloneqq[n]
for the entire run, sets the initial graph
G

to an edgeless graph
G\coloneqq(V,\emptyset)
and
sets the initial partition
(A,B)
to

(A,B)\coloneqq(\emptyset,V)
. Runs in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n)
.

•

\mathtt{update}(uv)
: inserts edge
uv

if
uv\not\in E(G)
or removes edge
uv
if
uv\in E(G)
.
Let
G^{\prime}\coloneqq(V,E(G)\triangle\{uv\})
be the updated graph
and
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
be the updated partition for
G^{\prime}
.
The method returns a set
{V}^{\textrm{moved}}\subseteq V
of size

|{V}^{\textrm{moved}}|\leqslant\mathcal{O}(1)

such that

(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=(A\triangle{V}^{\textrm{moved}},B\triangle{V}^{\textrm{moved}})
.
The running time is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
.

•

\mathtt{splittance}()
: returns the current value of
\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B)=\mathsf{splittance}(G)
in time
\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Proof.

The algorithm maintains the following data:

•

the standard representation of the dynamic graph
G
(i.e., for each vertex
v\in V(G)
, we store a dictionary of all the vertices adjacent to
v
);

•

a sorted list
\mathcal{L}
of all the vertex degrees of
G
:
d_{1}\geqslant d_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant d_{n}
;

•

an index
m\in[n]
equal to
\max\{i\in[n]\mid d_{i}\geqslant i-1\}
and a pointer to an element of
\mathcal{L}
corresponding to
d_{m}
;

•

the value of
\mathsf{splittance}(G)
computed according to
2.1
;

•

the sets
A
and
B
partitioning
V(G)
defined as in
2.1
;

•

a dictionary
\mathtt{degree}
mapping each vertex
v
of
G
a pointer to an element of
\mathcal{L}
equal to
d_{G}(v)
, where all the pointers are assumed to be pairwise different, and a dictionary
\mathtt{vertex}\colon\mathcal{L}\to V(G)
storing the inverse mapping
\mathtt{degree}^{-1}
;

•

dictionaries
\mathtt{firstD},\mathtt{lastD}
which, for every
i\in[n]
, store the pointers to the first and the last element of
\mathcal{L}
equal to
i
, respectively (or, null pointers if there are no such elements in
\mathcal{L}
).

Clearly, given an integer
n
, all the data above can be initialized in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n)
.

Now, consider an update of an edge
e=\{u,v\}
.
Having the graph
G
, we can verify whether
e
should be inserted or removed in time
\mathcal{O}(1)
.
We can easily update the graph
G
to
G^{\prime}\coloneqq(V,E\triangle\{e\})
, and update the list
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\coloneqq(d_{1}^{\prime}\geqslant d_{2}^{\prime}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant d_{n}^{\prime})
with the use of dictionaries
\mathtt{degree}
,
\mathtt{firstD}
and
\mathtt{lastD}
.
All these computations can be done in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
.

Next, we need to compute the value of
m^{\prime}\coloneqq\max\{i\in[n]\mid d^{\prime}_{i}\geqslant i-1\}
.
Ibarra (
[
22
, Section 7]
) observed that after a single edge update, it holds that
|m^{\prime}-m|\leqslant\mathcal{O}(1)
.
Hence, we can find the desired index
m^{\prime}
in time
\mathcal{O}(1)
, by inspecting the
\mathcal{O}(1)
-sized neighborhood on
\mathcal{L}
of the list element corresponding to
d_{m}
.
Consequently, using the formulas from
2.1
the value of
\mathsf{splittance}(G^{\prime})
can be computed from
\mathsf{splittance}(G)
in time
\mathcal{O}(1)
, and only a constant number of vertices need to be moved between
A
and
B
in the new partition
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
.
Again, it is easy to verify that all the remaining data can be updated in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
, and the correctness is guaranteed by
2.1
.

∎

4
Listing (non-)edges across the partition

Let
G=(A\uplus B,E)
be a dynamic graph whose vertices are partitioned into two sets
A
and
B
which might change over time.
In this section, we devise two auxiliary data structures that are responsible for listing all neighbors of a given
a\in A
in the set
B
, provided this neighborhood is small, or sampling a sufficient number of neighbors of
a
in
B
.
Symmetric queries can be also given for non-neighborhoods in
A
of vertices
b\in B
.

Both data structures work under the promise that
\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B)\leqslant k
. This promise will be lifted in the next section, where we design a wrapper data structure that works also when
\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B)>k
. To facilitate the design of this wrapper data structure, both data structures presented in this section need to be able to process updates in batches: instead of being given only single edge updates, they will be given sets of edge modifications such that after applying all of them the condition
\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B)\leqslant k
is preserved.

Also, both data structures are assumed to have access to sets
\mathtt{nonEdgesA},\mathtt{edgesB}
consisting of non-edges with both endpoints in
A
and edges with both endpoints in
B
, respectively. This access will be provided by the wrapper data structure.

We now give the first data structure, called
\mathsf{PromiseNL}
(standing for neighbors listing).

Lemma 4.1
.

There is a data structure
\mathsf{PromiseNL}[n,k,\ell,d,S]
,
parametrized by integers
k,\ell,d\in\mathbb{N}
and a subset

S\subseteq V(G)
,
that runs on a dynamic graph
G=(V,E)
with
V=[n]
,
and maintains a partition
(A,B)
of vertices of
G

by supporting
the following operations:

•

\mathtt{initialize}(n,k,\ell,d,S)
: sets

V\coloneqq[n]
, fixes the values of
k
,
\ell
,
d

and
S
for the entire run, initializes

G\coloneqq(V,\emptyset)
and

(A,B)\coloneqq(\emptyset,V)
.
Runs in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\ell d\cdot n)

•

\mathtt{update}({V}^{\textrm{moved}},{E}^{\textrm{mod}},\mathtt{nonEdgesA},\mathtt{edgesB})
:
modifies the edge set of the underlying graph to

(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\coloneqq(A\triangle{V}^{\textrm{moved}},B\triangle{V}^{\textrm{moved}})

after the update
\mathsf{splittance}_{G^{\prime}}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\leqslant k
for the
modified graph

Requires two lists of edges

and all edges of

, respectively.
Note that both sets are of size at most

The running time is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k+\ell d\cdot(|{V}^{\textrm{moved}}|+|{E}^{\textrm{mod}}|))

Moreover, the data structure can answer the following queries:

•

\mathtt{listNeighborsBS}(a)
: given a vertex

N\coloneqq N_{G}(a)\cap B\cap S

provided

•

\mathtt{listNonNeighborsAS}(b)
: given a vertex

, either returns the set

provided
|N|\leqslant\ell
, or reports
TooMany
if
|N|>\ell
.

Both queries run in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k\ell d)
and return correct answers with probability
1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-d})
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be some large enough constant whose value will be set later.
We assume that
\ell\leqslant n
, for otherwise we might set
\ell\coloneqq n
, and this would not affect answers to the queries.
We also denote the sum of the vertex indices in a set

X\subseteq V
as
\texttt{sum}(X)=\sum_{v\in X}v
.
The data structure
\mathsf{PromiseNL}[n,k,\ell,d,S]
stores:

•

the adjacency list representation of
G
and
representation of
A
and
B
as sets;

•

the integer values of
k,\ell,d
and
S\subseteq V(G)
represented as a set;

•

the sets of edges

\mathtt{nonEdgesA},\mathtt{edgesB}\subseteq\binom{V(G)}{2}

provided in the updates, as lists;

•

for every
i\in[\gamma d\log n]
, a vertex
\ell
-coloring
\chi_{i}\colon V(G)\to[\ell]
.
Define
S_{i,c}\coloneqq S\cap\chi_{i}^{-1}(c)
, for every
i\in[\gamma d\log n]
and
c\in[\ell]
; these sets are also stored in the data structure;

•

the following integer values:

\displaystyle\mathtt{countS}(v)

\displaystyle=|N_{G}(v)\cap S|\quad\text{for every }v\in V(G);

\displaystyle\mathtt{countAS}

\displaystyle=|A\cap S|;\qquad\mathtt{countBS}=|B\cap S|;

\displaystyle\mathtt{idSumS}_{i,c}(v)

\displaystyle=\texttt{sum}(N_{G}(v)\cap S_{i,c})\quad\text{for every }i\in[\gamma d\log n],c\in[\ell]\text{ and }v\in V(G);

\displaystyle\mathtt{idSumAS}_{i,c}

\displaystyle=\texttt{sum}(A\cap S_{i,c})\quad\text{for every }i\in[\gamma d\log n]\text{ and }c\in[\ell];

\displaystyle\mathtt{idSumBS}_{i,c}

\displaystyle=\texttt{sum}(B\cap S_{i,c})\quad\text{for every }i\in[\gamma d\log n]\text{ and }c\in[\ell].

Before proceeding with the description of operations, let us give a brief idea how these variables are going to be used.
Consider a vertex
a\in A
.
Using the variables
\mathtt{count}
- and the set
\mathtt{nonEdgesA}
, one can obtain the number of neighbors of
a
in the set
B\cap S
by applying a simple inclusion-exclusion formula.
If this neighborhood
N
is of size at most
\ell
, we would like to list all the vertices of
N
.
To this end, we use the vertex colorings
\chi_{i}
and the corresponding variables
\mathtt{idSum}
-.
Each coloring
\chi_{i}
will be initialized randomly.
Therefore, with high probability, for every vertex
v\in N
, there will be a coloring
\chi_{i}
such that under
\chi_{i}
,
v
has a different color than all the other vertices of
N\setminus\{v\}
.
By applying a similar inclusion-exclusion principle on the variables
\mathtt{idSum}
- we will be able to retrieve an identifier of
v
.

Initialization.

•

We initialize the adjacency representation of
G

to store an edgless graph on vertices
[n]
.
We initialize
A=\emptyset
and
B=V
.
Also, we set the lists

\mathtt{nonEdgesA},\mathtt{edgesB}
to be empty.

•

We store the values of
k,\ell,d
and
S
as a set.

•

All colorings
\chi_{i}
are initialized randomly, that is, for every
i\in[\gamma d\log n]
and
v\in V(G)
, we choose a color for
\chi_{i}(v)
uniformly at random from the set
[\ell]
.
The colorings are fixed for the entire run as well, and so are sets
S_{i,c}
.

•

Since the initial graph is empty, all the values
\mathtt{count}
- and
\mathtt{idSum}
- can be computed according to their definitions in total time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\ell d\cdot n)
.

Update.

Consider an update
\mathtt{update}({V}^{\textrm{moved}},{E}^{\textrm{mod}},\mathtt{nonEdgesA},\mathtt{edgesB})
.
First, we save the new values of
\mathtt{nonEdgesA}
and
\mathtt{edgesB}
.
Observe that the functions
\mathtt{countS}(\cdot)
and
\mathtt{idSumS}_{i,c}(\cdot)
need to be changed only for the endpoints of the edges of
{E}^{\textrm{mod}}
, and the remaining
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\ell d)
stored variables are affected only by vertices moved between
A
and
B
.
Therefore, the entire update takes
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k+\ell d\cdot(|{V}^{\textrm{moved}}|+|{E}^{\textrm{mod}}|))
time.

Queries.

We show how to answer the query
\mathtt{listNeighborsBS}(a,\ \mathtt{nonEdgesA})

for a vertex
a\in A
.
Recall that since
\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B)\leqslant k
, there are at most
k
non-edges within
A
, i.e.,
|\mathtt{nonEdgesA}|\leqslant k
.
Let
N\coloneqq N_{G}(a)\cap B\cap S
.
We start with the following observation.

Claim 4.2
.

One can compute the value of
|N|
in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k)
.

Proof of the claim.
The following equalities hold:

\displaystyle|N|=|N_{G}(a)\cap B\cap S|

\displaystyle=|N_{G}(a)\cap S|-|N_{G}(a)\cap A\cap S|=\mathtt{countS}(a)-|N_{G}(a)\cap A\cap S|

\displaystyle=\mathtt{countS}(a)-(|A\cap S|-\mathbbm{1}_{a\in S}-|\overline{N}_{G}(a)\cap A\cap S|)

\displaystyle=\mathtt{countS}(a)-(\mathtt{countAS}-\mathbbm{1}_{a\in S}-|\overline{N}_{G}(a)\cap A\cap S|).

Observe that
\overline{N}_{G}(a)\cap A\cap S\subseteq\overline{N}_{G}(a)\cap A
, and we can obtain all the non-neighborhood of
a
in
A
by iterating over the given set
\mathtt{nonEdgesA}
of size at most
k
.
Thus, we can compute the value of
|\overline{N}_{G}(a)\cap A\cap S|
in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k)
.

\lhd

Now, as a first step to answer

\mathtt{listNeighborsBS}(a,\ \mathtt{nonEdgesA})
query, we compute
|N|
.
If
|N|>\ell
, we report
TooMany
.
So assume that
|N|\leqslant\ell
.
For a vertex
b\in N
and a coloring
\chi_{i}
,
let us say that
b
is
exposed
by the coloring
\chi_{i}

if
\chi_{i}(b)\neq\chi_{i}(b^{\prime})
for every
b^{\prime}\in N\setminus\{b\}
. First, we note that every member of
N
is exposed with high probability.

Claim 4.3
.

With probability at least
1-n^{-d}
, the following event holds: For every
b\in N
, there is an index

i\in[\gamma d\log n]
such that
b
is exposed by
\chi_{i}
.

Proof of the claim.
If
\ell=1
, the claim is trivial, so assume that
\ell\geqslant 2
.
After fixing a vertex
b\in N
and a coloring
\chi_{i}
, we obtain that

\mathbb{P}\left(\text{$b$ is exposed by $\chi_{i}$}\right)=\frac{\ell\cdot(\ell-1)^{|N|-1}}{\ell^{|N|}}=\left(\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\right)^{|N|-1}\geqslant\left(\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\right)^{\ell-1}>\frac{1}{10e}.

Therefore, by applying the union bound we can upper bound the
probability of the event complementary to the one in the claim as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{b\in N}\ \forall_{i\in[\gamma d\log n]}\ \text{ $b$ is not exposed by $\chi_{i}$}\right)\leqslant\sum_{b\in N}\mathbb{P}\left(\forall_{i\in[\gamma d\log n]}\ \text{ $b$ is not exposed by $\chi_{i}$}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{b\in N}\left(1-\frac{1}{10e}\right)^{\gamma d\log n}\leqslant n\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{10e}\right)^{\gamma d\log n}\leqslant\frac{1}{n^{d}},

where the last inequality holds for all large enough values of
\gamma
.

\lhd

Next, we note that once a vertex of
N
is exposed, its identifier can be easily computed using a similar inclusion-exclusion principle as in the proof of
4.2
.

Claim 4.4
.

Let
i\in[\gamma d\log n]
be an index and

c\in[\ell]
be a color such that there is a vertex
b

exposed by
\chi_{i}
with color
\chi_{i}(b)=c
.
Then, given
c
and
i
, one can retrieve the identifier of
b
in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k)
.

Proof of the claim.
Since
b
is exposed by
\chi_{i}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle b=\texttt{sum}(N\cap\chi^{-1}(c))=\texttt{sum}(N_{G}(a)\cap B\cap S\cap\chi^{-1}(c))=\texttt{sum}(N_{G}(a)\cap B\cap S_{i,c}).

Now, we apply a similar inclusion-exclusion formula as in the proof of
4.2
:

\displaystyle\texttt{sum}(N_{G}(a)

\displaystyle\cap B\cap S_{i,c})=\texttt{sum}(N_{G}(a)\cap S_{i,c})-\texttt{sum}(N_{G}(a)\cap A\cap S_{i,c})

\displaystyle=\mathtt{idSumS}_{i,c}(a)-\texttt{sum}(N_{G}(a)\cap A\cap S_{i,c})

\displaystyle=\mathtt{idSumS}_{i,c}(a)-\left(\texttt{sum}(A\cap S_{i,c})-a\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{a\in S_{i,c}}-\texttt{sum}(\overline{N}_{G}(a)\cap A\cap S_{i,c})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathtt{idSumS}_{i,c}(a)-\left(\mathtt{idSumAS}_{i,c}-a\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{a\in S_{i,c}}-\texttt{sum}(\overline{N}_{G}(a)\cap A\cap S_{i,c})\right).

Again, we observe that
\overline{N}_{G}(a)\cap A\cap S_{i,c}\subseteq\overline{N}_{G}(a)\cap A
, and the latter set can be easily enumerated using the set
\mathtt{nonEdgesA}
in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k)
.

\lhd

We already have all the tools to finish the implementation of the query
\mathtt{listNeighborsBS}(a)
.
To list all the elements of
N
,
it is enough to iterate over all indices

i\in[\gamma d\log n]
and colors
c\in[\ell]
,
and for each pair
(i,c)
retrieve the
value that is described in
4.4

and check whether the returned value is a vertex that belongs to

N
.
By
4.3
, this procedure will retrieve the entire
set
N
with probability at least
1-n^{-d}
and the total
running time is bounded by
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k\ell d)
.

To answer the queries
\mathtt{listNonNeighborsAS}(b,\ \mathtt{edgesB})
for
b\in B
we proceed analogously.
To this end, we need additionally the values of:

•

|\overline{N}_{G}(b)\cap S|
which can be obtained from the values of
|N_{G}(b)\cap S|
and
|S|
; and

•

\texttt{sum}(\overline{N}_{G}(b)\cap S_{i,c})
which we can
obtain from
\texttt{sum}(N_{G}(b)\cap S_{i,c})
and

\texttt{sum}(S_{i,c})
, where the latter sums can be precomputed during the initialization.∎

We note that the application of color coding presented in
4.3
and
4.4
can be derandomized in a standard way, by replacing sampling the sets
S_{i,c}
with drawing them from a suitable universal hash family; see e.g.
[
9
, Section 5.6]
. However, as we will use randomization again in our second data structure (
Lemma

4.5
below), we prefer to present color coding here in a randomized, and therefore simpler manner.

So far, we can list
\ell
neighbors of a vertex
a\in A
in the set
B\cap S
only under an assumption that this neighborhood
N
is small.
However, this condition does not need to hold in general, and in
the next sections we will also need to find several neighbors of
a
in
B
(more precisely, a subset of
N
of size
\Theta(k)
) in the case when the set
N
is large.
This is the responsibility of our next data structure, named
\mathsf{PromiseNS}
(standing for neighbors sampling).
Roughly speaking, this data structure will store a set of layers
of the vertex set, where the
i
th layer contains
roughly
n/2^{i}
random vertices of
G
.
Then, provided
|N|>\ell
,
with high probability one of the layers will contain
\Theta(\ell)
vertices of
N
. By running an instance of
\mathsf{PromiseNL}
on each layer, we will be able to retrieve those vertices.

Lemma 4.5
.

There exists a data structure
\mathsf{PromiseNS}[n,k,\ell,d]
,
parametrized by integers
k,\ell,d\in\mathbb{N}
,
that runs on a dynamic graph
G=(V,E)
with
V=[n]
,
and maintains a dynamic partition
(A,B)
of vertices of
G

by supporting
the following operations:

•

\mathtt{initialize}(n,k,\ell,d)
: sets

V\coloneqq[n]
, fixes the values of
k
,
\ell
and
d

for the entire run, initializes

G\coloneqq(V,\emptyset)
and

(A,B)\coloneqq(\emptyset,V)
.
Runs in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\ell d^{2}\cdot n)
.

•

\mathtt{update}({V}^{\textrm{moved}},{E}^{\textrm{mod}},\mathtt{nonEdgesA},\mathtt{edgesB})
:
modifies the edge set of the underlying graph to

E^{\prime}\coloneqq E\triangle{E}^{\textrm{mod}}
and modifes the partition to

(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\coloneqq(A\triangle{V}^{\textrm{moved}},B\triangle{V}^{\textrm{moved}})
, provided that
after the update
\mathsf{splittance}_{G^{\prime}}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\leqslant k
for the
modified graph
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
.
Requires two lists of edges

\mathtt{nonEdgesA},\mathtt{edgesB}\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
storing the sets of
all non-edges of
G^{\prime}[A^{\prime}]
and all edges of
G^{\prime}[B^{\prime}]
, respectively.
Note that both sets are of size at most
k
since

\mathsf{splittance}_{G^{\prime}}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\leqslant k
.
The running time is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(kd+\ell d^{2}\cdot(|{V}^{\textrm{moved}}|+|{E}^{\textrm{mod}}|))
.

Moreover, the data structure can answer the following queries:

•

\mathtt{sampleEdges}(a)
: given a vertex
a\in A
, returns a subset

{N}^{\textrm{sample}}_{B}(a)\subseteq N_{G}(a)\cap B

of size
\min(\ell,|N_{G}(a)\cap B|)
;

•

\mathtt{sampleNonEdges}(b)
: given a vertex
b\in B
, returns a subset

{\overline{N}}^{\textrm{sample}}_{A}(b)\subseteq\overline{N}_{G}(b)\cap A

of size
\min(\ell,|\overline{N}_{G}(b)\cap A|)
.

Both queries run in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k\ell d^{2})
and
return correct answers with probability

1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-d})
.

Proof.

The data structure
\mathsf{PromiseNS}[n,k,\ell,d]
stores:

•

the adjacency lists representation of
G

and representation of
A
and
B
as sets;

•

the values of the parameters
k
,
\ell
, and
d
;

•

the sets

\mathtt{nonEdgesA},\mathtt{edgesB}\subseteq\binom{V(G)}{2}

given in the updates, as lists;

•

a single instance
\mathsf{PromiseNL}[n,k,\ell^{\prime},d,S]
of the data structure from
Lemma

4.1
running on the same dynamic graph
G
, where
\ell^{\prime}\coloneqq 72\ell
and
S\coloneqq V(G)
;

•

for every
i\in[\log n]
and
j\in[d\log n]
, a subset of vertices
V_{i,j}\subseteq V(G)
, fixed upon initialization and not modified later on; and

•

for every
i\in[\log n]
and
j\in[d\log n]
, an instance
\mathsf{PromiseNL}_{i,j}[n,k,\ell^{\prime},d,S_{i,j}]
of the data structure from
Lemma

4.1
running on the same dynamic graph
G
, where
S_{i,j}\coloneqq V_{i,j}
.

Initialization.

Intuitively, we want the set
V_{i,j}
to be a random subset containing roughly
n/2^{i}
vertices of
G
.
To achieve this, for every
i\in[\log n]
and
j\in[d\log n]
, and every vertex
v\in V(G)
, we put
v
into the set
V_{i,j}
with probability
2^{-i}
, independently.
Then, we initialize all the data structures
\mathsf{PromiseNL}
and
\mathsf{PromiseNL}_{i,j}
.
The total running time of this phase is bounded by

\log n\cdot d\log n\cdot\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\ell^{\prime}d\cdot n)\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\ell d^{2}\cdot n).

Update.

Given an update with arguments
({V}^{\textrm{moved}},{E}^{\textrm{mod}},\mathtt{nonEdgesA},\mathtt{edgesB})
, we simply pass it to all the stored data structures.
The running time is thus bounded by

\log n\cdot d\log n\cdot\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k+\ell^{\prime}d\cdot(|{V}^{\textrm{moved}}|+|{E}^{\textrm{mod}}|))\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(kd+\ell d^{2}\cdot(|{V}^{\textrm{moved}}|+|{E}^{\textrm{mod}}|)).

Sampling.

Fix a vertex
a\in A
.
We show how to answer the query
\mathtt{sampleEdges}(a)
.
The implementation of queries
\mathtt{sampleNonEdges}(b)
, for
b\in B
, is symmetric.
Let
N\coloneqq N_{G}(a)\cap B
.
Our goal is to sample a subset of the set
N
of size
\min(\ell,|N|)
.

First, consider the case when
|N|\leqslant\ell^{\prime}
.
Then, by querying the structure
\mathsf{PromiseNL}
about the list of
neighbors of
a
in the set
B\cap V(G)=B

(i.e., we run
\mathtt{listNeighborsBS}(a)
), we obtain all the
vertices of
N
with probability at least
1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-d})
.
Otherwise, the call returns
TooMany
, and we can proceed as follows.

From now on, assume that
|N|>\ell^{\prime}=72\ell
.
Then, we query all the data structures
\mathsf{PromiseNL}_{i,j}
about the list of neighbors of
a
in the corresponding sets
B\cap V_{i,j}
(i.e., we run
\mathtt{listNeighborsBS}(a)
on each
\mathsf{PromiseNL}_{i,j}
).
We prove that with probability
at least
1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-d})
one of such queries will correctly return at least
\ell
elements of
N
.
Define
N_{i,j}\coloneqq N\cap V_{i,j}
, for every
i\in[\log n]
,
j\in[d\log n]
.

Claim 4.6
.

There is
i\in[\log n]
so that

\mathbb{P}\left(|N_{i,j}|\not\in(12\ell,72\ell)\textrm{ for all }j\in[d\log n]\right)\leqslant n^{-d\ell}
.

Proof of the claim.
Recall that
|N|>72\ell
and each set of the form
N,N_{1,j}
,
N_{2,j}
,
N_{3,j},\ldots,
(for every
j\in[d\log n]
) contains half as many vertices in expectation as the previous one. Hence, there exists an index
i\in[\log n]
such that

\mathbb{E}\,|N_{i,j}|\in[24\ell,48\ell]\quad\text{ for every }j\in[d\log n].

Fix such an index
i
and let
\mu\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\,|N_{i,j}|=n/2^{i}
.
Observe that for every
j
,
|N_{i,j}|
is the sum of
n

random variables
X_{v}
taking values
0
or
1
, indicating
whether a vertex
v
belongs to
N_{G}(a)\cap B\cap V_{i,j}
.
Hence, we can apply the following Chernoff’s inequality:

\mathbb{P}\left(|N_{i,j}|\not\in\left(\frac{\mu}{2},\frac{3\mu}{2}\right)\right)\leqslant 2e^{-\mu/12}\quad\text{ for every }j\in[d\log n].

By plugging in the bounds on
\mu
(i.e.,
\mu\in[24\ell,48\ell]
), we obtain that

\mathbb{P}\left(|N_{i,j}|\not\in(12\ell,72\ell)\right)\leqslant 2e^{-24\ell/12}<2\cdot 2^{-2\ell}<2^{-\ell}\quad\text{ for every }j\in[d\log n].

Finally, since the random variables
|N_{i,j}|
are
independent over the choices of
j\in[d\log n]
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(|N_{i,j}|\not\in(12\ell,72\ell)\text{ for every }j\in[d\log n]\right)\leqslant 2^{-\ell\cdot d\log n}=n^{-d\ell}.

This finishes the proof of the claim.

\lhd

Take an index
i\in[\log n]
satisfying
4.6
.
We know that with probability at least

1-n^{-d\ell}
there exists
j\in[d\log n]
such that the
set
N_{i,j}
contains more than
12\ell>\ell
but less than
72\ell=\ell^{\prime}
vertices.
The corresponding query to the data structure
\mathsf{PromiseNL}_{i,j}
will return at least
\ell
elements of
N_{i,j}\subseteq N
with probability at least
1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-d})
.
Hence, by the union bound, the probability of incorrectly answering the query
\mathtt{sampleEdges}(a)
is upper bounded by
n^{-d\ell}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-d})\subseteq\mathcal{O}(n^{-d})
.
The running time is dominated by the total running time of queries on all structures
\mathsf{PromiseNL}_{i,j}
which is bounded by

\log n\cdot d\log n\cdot\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k\ell^{\prime}\cdot d)\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k\ell\cdot d^{2})
.
∎

5
Lifting the promise

In the previous section we presented data structures that work under the promise that
\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B)\leqslant k
.
Now, we focus on the general case when no such condition is guaranteed to hold.
The main responsibility of our next data structure,
\mathsf{Wrapper}[n,k,d]
, is to implement the same functionality as data structures
\mathsf{PromiseNL}
and
\mathsf{PromiseNS}
, but to work without the assumption that
\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B)\leqslant k
and, additionally, to offer methods for listing non-edges with both endpoints in
A
and edges with both endpoints in
B
(these were needed for queries in the data structures
\mathsf{PromiseNL}
and
\mathsf{PromiseNS}
).
All those methods are required to give correct outputs only when the inequality
\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B)\leqslant k
holds, but the data structure should persist also when
\mathsf{splittance}_{G}(A,B)>k
, and return to giving correct answers again once the splittance decreases to at most
k
.

Lemma 5.1
.

There exists a data structure
\mathsf{Wrapper}[n,k,d]
parametrized by integers

k,d\in\mathbb{N}
that runs on a
dynamic graph

7
Branching

We are ready to prove our main result,
Theorem

1.1
, which we recall for convenience.

Theorem

1.1
.

There is a randomized data structure that for a fully dynamic graph
G
, updated by edge deletions and edge insertions, and a parameter
k
fixed upon initialization, maintains the answer to the following question: can one add at most
k
edges to
G
to obtain a split graph. The data structure can be initialized on an edgeless
n
-vertex graph and an accuracy parameter
d\in\mathbb{N}
in time
k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot d^{2}\cdot n\cdot(\log n)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, and the amortized time complexity of updates is
5^{k}\cdot k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot d^{2}\cdot(\log n)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
. At all times, the answer provided by the data structure is correct with probability at least
1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-d})
.

Proof.

Our main data structure stores:

•

the values of
k
and
d
;

•

an instance
\mathsf{Wrapper}[n,k,d^{\prime}]
of the data structure
from
Lemma

5.1
, extended with a method
from
Lemma

6.1
, for
d^{\prime}\coloneqq d+k+c
, where
c
is
the constant from
Lemma

6.1
.

Initialization.

We set the values of
k
and
d
for the entire run, and initialize the inner data structure
\mathsf{Wrapper}
with corresponding values.
The total running time of this phase is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k\cdot d^{\prime 2}\cdot n)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot d^{2}\cdot n).

Update.

Consider an edge update
e=uv
(that is, we either insert
e
to the graph
G
, or remove it).
First, we pass this to the structure
\mathsf{Wrapper}
.
Let
G^{\prime}
denote the newly obtained graph.
Now, we should update the answer to our query: we need to determine whether one can insert at most
k
edges to
G^{\prime}
so that the resulting graph is split.

If
\mathsf{splittance}(G^{\prime})>k
, then we know that
(G^{\prime},k)
is a
No
-instance of the
Split Completion
problem.
Assume then that
\mathsf{splittance}(G^{\prime})\leqslant k
.
Now we make a query
\mathtt{findObstruction}()
on
\mathsf{Wrapper}
.
If
\mathtt{findObstruction}()
reports the answer
Split
, then
G^{\prime}
is a split graph, and in particular,
(G^{\prime},k)
is a
Yes
-instance of the
Split Completion
problem.
Otherwise,
\mathtt{findObstruction}()
returns a subset
U\subseteq V(G^{\prime})
such that
G^{\prime}[U]
is an obstruction.

Since in the
Split Completion
problem we are only allowed to insert new edges, we know that in order to make the graph
G^{\prime}
split, we need to insert to it at least one non-edge of
G^{\prime}[U]
. Note that
G^{\prime}[U]
has at most
5
non-edges (in the case of
C_{5}
).
We branch into all these possibilities, and when considering an insertion of a non-edge
e\in\overline{E}(G^{\prime}[U])
, we temporarily insert it to the graph
G^{\prime}
(by doing appropriate call to the structure
\mathsf{Wrapper}
), and proceed recursively, where at each step either we obtain that the current graph is split, or
\mathsf{Wrapper}
localizes an obstruction which needs to be eliminated from the graph by subsequent insertions.
Since we are allowed to insert at most
k
edges to
G^{\prime}
, we can exit branches of depth larger than
k
, and thus the total number of nodes in the search tree (and also the number of calls to
\mathsf{Wrapper}
) is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(5^{k})
.

The correctness of this recursive procedure is straightforward under an assumption that all the calls to
\mathsf{Wrapper}
return correct answers.
The amortized running time of the entire update is bounded by

\mathcal{O}(5^{k})\cdot\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot d^{\prime 2})=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(5^{k}\cdot k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot d^{2})

By the union bound, the probability of reporting an incorrect answer is at most

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{5^{k}}{n^{d^{\prime}-c}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{5^{k}}{n^{k+d+c-c}}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{d}}\right).

Finally, let us remark that this is in fact a Monte Carlo algorithm: once our procedure finds a set of edges
F\subseteq\binom{V(G)}{2}
to be inserted, we can verify deterministically whether
G^{\prime\prime}=(V(G^{\prime}),E(G^{\prime})\cup F)
is a split graph by temporarily inserting edges of
F
to
\mathsf{Wrapper}
, checking whether
\mathsf{splittance}(G^{\prime\prime})=0
, and removing edges of
F
from
\mathsf{Wrapper}
afterwards.
∎

We remark that the proof of
Theorem

1.1
can be trivially adjusted to handle the
Split Edge Deletion
problem instead: once an obstruction is located we branch into at most five ways of breaking it by an edge deletion instead of an edge insertion. The running time guarantees remain the same.

8
Conclusions

In this work we gave a dynamic data structure for the
Split Completion
problem with amortized update time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\cdot(d\log n)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, where the error probability is bounded by
1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-d})
. Two natural questions that one may ask are the following:

•

Can the data structure be derandomized? The usage of color coding in
Lemma

4.1
can be derandomized in a standard way using universal hash families. However, we also apply randomization in a non-trivial way in the proof of
Lemma

4.5
, and this application we do not know how to derandomize.

•

A careful analysis of our arguments reveals that the polylogarithmic factor in the obtained update time is
(\log n)^{4}
.
Can this be improved? Could one expect update time independent of
n
, that is, of the form
f(k)
for some computable function
f
?

As for amortization, the only place where amortization comes into play is the proof of
Lemma

5.1
: the updates are queued during the time when the splittance is above
k
, and then they get executed all at once only when the splittance becomes bounded by
k
. Consequently, our data structure actually executes updates in
worst-case time

2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\cdot(d\log n)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
under the assumption that the splittance is never larger than
k
. We believe that relying on amortization to handle the general setting without any promises is a rather small hindrance. Consequently, while the question about removing amortization is definitely relevant, we consider it less interesting than the two questions stated above.

In a wider context, our result together with the previous work of Iwata and Oka
[
23
]
and of Alman et al.
[
1
]
suggest that investigating parameterized dynamic data structures for graph modification problems might turn out to be a particularly fruitful direction. Concrete problems that we suspect to be amenable to dynamization are
Planarization

[
30
,
24
]
and various graph modification problems connected to chordal graphs and their subclasses such as interval, proper interval, trivially perfect, or threshold graphs; these were studied extensively, see e.g.
[
3
,

,

,
