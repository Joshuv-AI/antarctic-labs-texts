On the unique solvability of the simultaneous Pell equations x 2 − a ​ y 2 = 1 x^{2}-a\,y^{2}=1 and z 2 − b ​ x 2 = 1 z^{2}-b\,x^{2}=1 .
Tobias Hilgart, Volker Ziegler

Abstract
. We consider the simultaneous Pell equations x 2 − a ​ y 2 = 1 , z 2 − b ​ x 2 = 1 , x^{2}-a\,y^{2}=1,\qquad z^{2}-b\,x^{2}=1, where a > b ≥ 2 a>b\geq 2 are positive integers. We describe a procedure which, for any fixed b b , either confirms that the simultaneous Pell equations have at most one solution in positive integers, or finds all exceptions for which we have proved that there are at most finitely many.

11D09, 11Y50

1.
Introduction

A natural question to ask when considering simultaneous Pell equations

a\,x^{2}-b\,z^{2}=\delta_{1},\qquad c\,y^{2}-d\,x^{2}=\delta_{2}

is, how many solutions (in positive integers) there are. Ineffective results from Thue
[
13
]
and Siegel
[
12
]
imply that for
\gcd(ab,\delta_{1})=\gcd(cd,\delta_{2})=1
and
b\delta_{1}\neq c\delta_{1}
there are only finitely many.

If neither
ab
nor
bd
is a perfect square, and
\gcd(abd,\delta)=1
, then Yuan
[
15
]
proved that if each equation is solvable, the simultaneous equations

a\,x^{2}-b\,z^{2}=\delta,\qquad c\,y^{2}-d\,z^{2}=\delta

have a solution (even an infinite number of them) if and only if
ad
is a perfect square.

Masser and Rickert
[
9
]
proved that, given the flexibility to freely choose both
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
, simultaneous Pell equations can be constructed with arbitrarily many solutions.

That is, it makes sense to narrow the scope when considering simultaneous Pell equations; in this paper, we consider

(1)

x^{2}-a\,y^{2}=1,\qquad z^{2}-b\,x^{2}=1.

Bennett
[
4
]
noted that an analogous version of his proof shows that the above equations have at most three solutions. Cipu and Mignotte
[
5
]
improved this bound to two solutions, and reformulated a conjecture of Yuan
[
15
]
on the number of solutions,

Conjecture 1.1
.

If the system of simultaneous Pell equations

a\,x^{2}-b\,y^{2}=1,\qquad c\,y^{2}-d\,z^{2}=1

has at least two solutions in positive integers, then their coefficients are given by

(a,b,c,d)=(1,m^{2}-1,1,n(l,m)^{2}-1),\qquad(a,a-1,b(l,a),b(l,a)-1)

or equivalent forms thereof, where

n(l,m)=\cfrac{\left(m+\sqrt{m^{2}-1}\right)^{2l}-\left(m-\sqrt{m^{2}-1}\right)^{2l}}{4\sqrt{m^{2}-1}}

and

4b(l,a)-1=\cfrac{\left(\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{a-1}\right)^{l}-\left(\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{a-1}\right)^{l}}{2\sqrt{a-1}},\quad l\equiv 3\mod 4.

The conjecture states for the system (
1
) that there is at most one solution in positive integers, except maybe some special cases where
a
and
b
are almost squares.

For several classes of coefficients, the above conjecture was confirmed. Yuan himself
[
14
]
proved it for
b
of the form
4m(m+1)
, and Cipu
[
6
]
for
4m^{2}-1
. Zhang et al.
[
1
]
completely solved the case where
a
is prime and
b=24
, not only confirming the conjecture in this case, but finding that there are only the solutions
(99,70,485)
and
(10,3,49)
when
a=2
and
a=11
, respectively.

In the spirit of
[
1
]
, we consider
b
to be fixed – but now arbitrary. Without any further restrictions, we prove that the conjecture (that there is at most one solution) is eventually true, in the sense that when
a
is sufficiently large with respect to
b
. We formulate this in the following

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
a>b\geq 2
be two positive integers, where
b
is fixed. Then there exists an effectively computable constant
a_{0}
, dependent only on
b
, such that for
a\geq a_{0}
the simultaneous Pell equations

(2)

x^{2}-a\,y^{2}=1,\qquad z^{2}-b\,x^{2}=1

have at most one solution
(x,y,z)
in positive integers.

If we follow the proof of the above theorem and depart from it at the right time, we can use the theory of continued fractions and a well-known reduction method (or variant thereof) based on it to obtain an executable procedure that can confirm the above conjecture for any (reasonably large)
b
. The algorithm is described and analysed in Section
4
. Our implementation in Sagemath
[
7
]
has yielded the following results.

Theorem 1.3
.

For
1\leq b\leq 10000
, there are no two solutions to the simultaneous Pell equations

x^{2}-a\,y^{2}=1,\qquad z^{2}-b\,x^{2}=1,

for any
a\geq b
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

The pair
(z_{1},x_{1})
solving the equation
z^{2}-b\,x^{2}=1
and minimising
z
is given by some convergent of
\sqrt{b}
and is called the fundamental solution of the Pell equation. It has the well-known property that all solutions to the Pell equation can be constructed via

(z_{i}+\sqrt{b}x_{i})=(z_{1}+\sqrt{b}x_{1})^{k},\qquad k=1,2\ldots.

This is why the unit
z_{1}+\sqrt{b}\,x_{1}
is sometimes also called the fundamental solution. We call it
\epsilon
and take the unit such that
\epsilon>1
by changing the sign and/or taking the inverse if necessary. Similarly, we denote the fundamental solution greater than
1
of
x^{2}-a\,y^{2}=1
by
\delta_{a}
. In particular, this means that

(3)

\delta_{a}\geq 2\sqrt{a},\qquad\epsilon\geq 2\sqrt{b}.

Let
(x,y,z)
be a solution to Equation (
2
) in positive integers. Since

x^{2}-a\,y^{2}=\left(x+\sqrt{a}\,y\right)\left(x-\sqrt{a}\,y\right)=1,

there is a positive integer
m\geq 1
such that

x+\sqrt{a}\,y=\delta_{a}^{m},\qquad x-\sqrt{a}\,y=\delta_{a}^{-m},

and eliminating
y
by summing the two equations gives

x=\frac{\delta_{a}^{m}+\delta_{a}^{-m}}{2}.

We do the same thing for the second Pell equation and deduce that there exists a positive integer
n\geq 1
such that

(4)

x=\frac{\delta_{a}^{m}+\delta_{a}^{-m}}{2}=\frac{\epsilon^{n}-\epsilon^{-n}}{2\sqrt{b}},

and so we have

(5)

\frac{\epsilon^{n}}{\sqrt{b}}-\delta_{a}^{m}=\frac{\epsilon^{-n}}{\sqrt{b}}+\delta_{a}^{-m}>0.

But the right-hand side of the equation is small with respect to
m
and
n
, so the two terms on the left-hand side must be asymptotically equal with respect to
m
and
n
. We can specify this statement as

(6)

\delta_{a}^{m}<\frac{\epsilon^{n}}{\sqrt{b}}<c_{1}\,\delta_{a}^{m},

where
c_{1}
is an upper bound to the expression

1+\frac{1}{\delta_{a}^{2m}}+\frac{1}{\delta_{a}^{m}\epsilon^{n}\sqrt{b}};

since
\delta_{a}\geq 2\sqrt{a}>2\sqrt{b}
and
m,n\geq 1
, we can take

c_{1}=1+\frac{1}{4b}+\frac{1}{2b\epsilon}.

We then divide Equation (
5
) by
\delta_{a}^{m}
and use the first inequality in Equation (
6
), more precisely
\epsilon^{-n}<\delta_{a}^{-m}/\sqrt{b}
to get

0<\frac{\epsilon^{n}}{\sqrt{b}}\delta_{a}^{-m}-1<\left(1+\frac{1}{b}\right)\,\delta_{a}^{-2m},

which, since
0<\log\xi<\xi-1
for
\xi>1
, implies

(7)

0<-m\log\delta_{a}+n\log\epsilon-\log\sqrt{b}<\left(1+\frac{1}{b}\right)\delta_{a}^{-2m}.

We then apply Theorem
2.1
on this non-zero linear form, where we set

D\leq 4,\quad A_{1}=2\log\delta_{a},\quad A_{2}=2\log\epsilon,\quad A_{3}=4\log\sqrt{b}.

Using the second inequality in Equation (
6
), we get

n\frac{\log\epsilon}{\log\delta_{a}}<m+\frac{\log(c_{1}\sqrt{b})}{\log\delta_{a}},

and with Equation (
3
), we can set
B
in Theorem
2.1
as

m+\frac{\log(c_{1}\sqrt{b})}{\log\delta_{a}}<m+\frac{\log(c_{1}\sqrt{b})}{\log 2\sqrt{b}}<m+0.52=B.

Plugging everything into Theorem
2.1
, we compare its lower bound for the linear form with the upper bound from Equation (
7
), which gives

-c_{\text{Mat}}16^{2}\log\delta_{a}\log\epsilon\log\sqrt{b}\;\log\left(6e\log(6e)\,(m+0.52)\right)<-2m\log\delta_{a}+\log(1+1/b).

Again using Equation (
3
) and the worst case
b=2
, we can bound
\frac{\log(1+1/b)}{\log\delta_{a}}
by
\frac{\log(1+1/b)}{\log(2\sqrt{b})}<0.39
. So, if we divide the above inequality by
\log\delta_{a}
, we get

(8)

-c_{\text{Mat}}16^{2}\log\epsilon\log\sqrt{b}\;\log\left(6e\log(6e)\,(m+0.52)\right)<-2m+0.39,

and comparing both sides we find that there exists an effectively computable constant
c_{m}
such that
m<c_{m}
.

These arguments hold for every solution to (
2
), whenever there is at least one solution. From now on, we assume that there are two solutions
(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2})
with the corresponding exponents
(m_{1},n_{1})
and
(m_{2},n_{2})
that satisfy
n_{1}<n_{2}
. Equation
4
and
\delta_{a},\epsilon>1
imply that then
m_{1}<m_{2}
as well.

Again we divide Equation (
5
) by
\delta_{a}^{m_{1}}
and
\delta_{a}^{m_{2}}
, respectively, but this time we use the second inequality from Equation (
6
), i.e.
\delta_{a}^{-m}<c_{1}\sqrt{b}\,\epsilon^{-n}
, to get upper bounds in
\epsilon^{-2n_{1}}
and
\epsilon^{-2n_{2}}
:

(9)

\begin{split}0&<-m_{1}\log\delta_{a}+n_{1}\log\epsilon-\log\sqrt{b}<\left(c_{1}^{2}b+c_{1}\right)\epsilon^{-2n_{1}}\\
0&<-m_{2}\log\delta_{a}+n_{2}\log\epsilon-\log\sqrt{b}<\left(c_{1}^{2}b+c_{1}\right)\epsilon^{-2n_{2}}\end{split}

We take the first linear form
m_{2}
-times and subtract from it the
m_{1}
-th multiple of the second linear form. In this way, we arrive at the linear form

(10)

\left|\left(m_{2}n_{1}-m_{1}n_{2}\right)\log\epsilon-\left(m_{2}-m_{1}\right)\log\sqrt{b}\right|<c_{m}\left(c_{1}^{2}b+c_{1}\right)\epsilon^{-2n_{1}},

from which we have eliminated
\log\delta_{a}
. We know that this linear form still does not vanish, since the logarithms are linearly independent and at least
m_{2}-m_{1}
is non-zero. With only two logarithms, we can use the often numerically better bounds of Laurent, Theorem
2.2
; we set

D=2,\quad\log A_{1}=\frac{\log\epsilon}{2},\quad\log A_{2}=\log\sqrt{b},\quad b^{\prime}=\frac{\left|m_{2}n_{1}-m_{1}n_{2}\right|}{2\log A_{2}}+\frac{\left|m_{2}-m_{1}\right|}{2\log A_{1}}.

Using the fact that all
m_{i}
and
n_{i}
are positive and
m_{1},m_{2}<c_{m}
, we can bound
b^{\prime}
in terms of
n_{2}
. In addition, we can use Equtaion (
6
) twice in order to estimate
n_{2}
in terms of
n_{1}
,

\displaystyle n_{2}

\displaystyle<m_{2}\frac{\log\delta_{a}}{\log\epsilon}+\frac{\log\left(c_{1}\sqrt{b}\right)}{\log\epsilon}

\displaystyle<c_{m}\frac{\log\delta_{a}}{\log\epsilon}+\frac{\log\left(c_{1}\sqrt{b}\right)}{\log\epsilon}

(11)

\displaystyle<c_{m}\left(n_{1}+\frac{\log\sqrt{b}}{\log\epsilon}\right)+\frac{\log\left(c_{1}\sqrt{b}\right)}{\log\epsilon},

and can thus effectively bound
b^{\prime}
by
n_{1}
. We can even make this explicit by writing

b^{\prime}<\frac{c_{m}^{2}(n_{1}+\log\sqrt{b}/\log\epsilon)+c_{m}\log(c_{1}\sqrt{b})/\log\epsilon}{2\log\sqrt{b}}+\frac{c_{m}}{\log\epsilon}.

Applying Theorem
2.2
to the linear form in Equation (
10
) and comparing it to its upper bound gives

17.9\cdot 2^{3}\left(\log b^{\prime}+0.38\right)^{2}\log\epsilon\log\sqrt{b}>2n_{1}\log\epsilon-\log\left(c_{m}\left(c_{1}^{2}b+c_{1}\right)\right),

which in turn implies
n_{1}<c_{n_{1}}
for an effective constant
c_{n_{1}}
. It then follows from Equation (
6
) that

\log\delta_{a}<c_{n_{1}}\log\epsilon-\log\sqrt{b}<\log(2\sqrt{a_{0}}),

for an effective constant
a_{0}
. Finally, by the first inequality in Equation (
3
), this implies that
a<a_{0}
, which completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

4.
Implementation

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is effective in the sense that it describes a procedure that we can (theoretically) follow for any explicit b to eventually obtain an explicit
a_{0}
. We can then check for the remaining ”small”
a
whether any of the simultaneous Pell equations have more than one solution.

However, the problem is that we use lower bounds for linear forms in logarithms twice, to get the bounds
c_{m}
and
c_{n_{1}}
, respectively. For example, for
b=24
and compared with
[
1
]
, we know that the fundamental solution of
z^{2}-24\,x^{2}=1
is
5+\sqrt{24}
, which we can use for
\epsilon
. If we calculate the two constants, we would get
c_{m}\approx 3.345\times 10^{14}
and
c_{n_{1}}=720888
. This would lead to an absolutely staggering bound of
a_{0}\approx 10^{3.3\times 10^{6}}
for
a
.

So instead we depart from the theoretical proof after obtaining
c_{m}
. Furthermore, we do not calculate the fundamental solution of the Pell equation
z^{2}-b\,x^{2}=1
, but use the fundamental unit of the associated number field
\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{b}]
. There are lists of these fundamental units in most computer algebra systems, such as the one we used, Sagemath
[
7
]
.

So from now on let
\epsilon
be the fundamental unit of the number field
\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{b}]
. This doesn’t change the derivation of Inequality (
8
), and we can calculate the constant
c_{m}
by solving it numerically. Instead of using lower bounds for linear forms in logarithms, we apply Lemma
2.3
to Inequality (
10
). We set

c_{0}=\frac{c_{m}(c_{1}^{2}b+c_{1})}{\log\epsilon},\qquad\mu=\frac{\log\sqrt{b}}{\log\epsilon},

to rewrite Inequality (
10
) as

(12)

\left|(m_{2}n_{1}-m_{1}n_{2})-(m_{2}-m_{1})\,\mu\right|<c_{0}\epsilon^{-2n_{1}}.

For
\mu
we ensure that the logarithms are computed with a high enough precision so that the convergents are correct. Since by Lemma
2.3
we are looking for the first convergent greater than
q=(m_{2}-m_{1})<c_{m}
, it is sufficient to take a precision some order of magnitude greater than
c_{m}
. Applying Lemma
2.3
to the first convergent

\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}]

of
\mu=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots]
that satisfies
q_{k}>c_{m}
and comparing to the upper bound of Inequality (
12
) gives the bound

c_{n_{1}}=\left\lfloor\frac{\log q_{k}+\log c_{0}+\log(2+\max\left\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\right\})}{2\log\epsilon}\right\rfloor

for
n_{1}
. Note that we can expect
c_{n_{1}}
to be relatively small, somewhere in the order of
\log c_{m}/\log\epsilon
.

Assuming that there are two solutions, so that we can formulate Inequality (
10
), the smaller solution must be one of the candidates

x_{n}=\frac{\epsilon^{n}-\epsilon^{-n}}{2\sqrt{b}},\qquad 1\leq n\leq c_{n_{1}}.

We can iteratively compute the candidates in the number field
\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{b}]
quite efficiently, and loop over all of them. It may happen (e.g. for
b=2
) that not every
x_{n}
is an integer, and since
\epsilon
is the fundamental unit, not the fundamental solution,
x_{n}
does not necessarily have to be a solution to
z^{2}-b\,x^{2}=1
. Furthermore, if
x_{n}\leq\sqrt{1+b}<\sqrt{1+a}
then it cannot be a solution to
x^{2}-a\,y^{2}=1
. We can skip these candidates.

For the rest, we look at the quantity
\gamma_{n}=x_{n}+\sqrt{x_{n}^{2}-1}
. If
x_{n}
is also a solution to
x^{2}-a\,y^{2}=1
, then
\gamma_{n}=x_{n}+\sqrt{a}\,y
. By computing the prime factorisation

x_{n}^{2}-1=\prod_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}},

we can find all
a
and
y
such that
x_{n}^{2}-a\,y^{2}=1
. Furthermore,
a
is bounded from below by the square-free part of
x_{n}^{2}-1
,

\Pi=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
\alpha_{i}\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{s}p_{i}.

If
x_{n}^{2}-1
is square-free, then
a=x_{n}^{2}-1
is unique and
y
must be
1
. Since
y=1
is minimal we can even conclude that
\gamma_{n}=\delta_{a}
must be the fundamental solution in this case. Otherwise we have
\gamma_{n}=\delta_{a}^{l}
for some positive integer
l
which we can bound by

c_{l}=\left\lfloor\frac{\log\gamma_{n}}{2\sqrt{\Pi}}\right\rfloor,

since
\delta_{a}\geq 2\sqrt{a}\geq 2\sqrt{\Pi}
.

We can substitute
\delta_{a}
for
\gamma_{n}^{1/l}
in the second linear form of Equation (
7
) and get

(13)

0<-m_{2}\log\gamma_{n}+ln_{2}\log\epsilon-l\log\sqrt{b}<l(c_{1}^{2}b+c_{1})\epsilon^{-2n_{2}},

where all the logarithms are now explicit. Furthermore,
m_{2}
is explicitly bounded by
m_{2}
, and
ln_{2}
is explicitly bounded by

c_{n_{2}}(l)=\left\lfloor\frac{c_{m}\log\gamma_{n}+l\log(c_{1}\sqrt{b})}{\log\epsilon}\right\rfloor

according to the line above Inequality (
11
).

If we set

\mu=\frac{\log\epsilon}{\log\gamma_{n}},\quad\tau(l)=-\frac{l\log\sqrt{b}}{\log\gamma_{n}},\quad c_{2}(l)=\frac{l(c_{1}^{2}b+c_{1})}{\log\gamma_{n}},\quad c_{3}(l)=\frac{2\log\epsilon}{l},

then we can write Equation (
13
) as

\left|ln_{2}\,\mu+\tau(l)-m_{2}\right|<c_{3}(l)\,e^{-c_{2}(l)ln_{2}}.

We use Theorem
2.4
to reduce the bound
c_{n_{2}}(l)
for every possible
l=1,\ldots,c_{l}
. To do this, we first compute the continued fraction expansion
[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots]
of
\mu
and iterate over its convergents
p_{k}/q_{k}=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots a_{k}]
.

Setting
\kappa=1/(2lc_{n_{2}}(l)\left\{q_{k}\mu\right\})
ensures that the first condition in the theorem holds. And the
q_{k}
are denominators of convergents of
\mu
, so the fractional part
\left\{q_{k}\mu\right\}
quickly becomes smaller than
1/(2lc_{n_{2}}(l))
, or equivalently
\kappa>1
. We iterate until the second condition in the theorem,
\kappa\left\{q_{k}\tau(l)\right\}>1
, is also satisfied and then compute the reduced bound

\left\lfloor\frac{\log(2\kappa q_{k}c_{2}(l))}{lc_{3}(l)}\right\rfloor

for
n_{2}
. We then take
c_{n_{2}}
to be the largest of all these reduced bounds for
1\leq l\leq c_{l}
.

At this point we have a range
n+1\leq n^{\prime}\leq c_{n_{2}}
for the exponent of the second solution if the candidate
x_{n}
gives the first, and we can calculate the corresponding candidates
x_{n^{\prime}}
accordingly. If we indeed have two solutions, then the rational

\frac{x_{n^{\prime}}^{2}-1}{x_{n}^{2}-1}=\frac{a\,y^{\prime 2}}{a\,y^{2}}=\left(\frac{y^{\prime}}{y}\right)^{2}

must be a square. If it is, we have found a pair of solutions. Computing the prime factorisation of

-values for which

and

, respectively. It is enough to know

(and the prime factorisation of
x_{n}^{2}-1
, if we do not want to calculate it a second time) to construct the entire solution.

In conclusion, we have proved the correctness of the following algorithm.

Remark 4.2
.

There are a number of points worth noting in an implementation of the above procedure that are not readily apparent from the pseudocode alone.

First, computing the powers of the fundamental unit
\epsilon=\xi+\eta\sqrt{b}
to get the candidates for the solutions is best done in the number field
\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{b}]
.

Solving Inequality (
8
), or the inequality

\begin{split}-c_{\text{Mat}}16^{2}\log\delta_{a}\log\epsilon\log\sqrt{b}\log\left(6e\log(6e)\left(m+\frac{\log(c_{1}\sqrt{b})}{\log(2\sqrt{b})}\right)\right)<\\
-2m\log\delta_{a}+\log\left(1+\frac{1}{b}\right)\end{split}

to be more precise, numerically may fail or take too long. In this case, or as a general alternative, we can use the fact that
m<c\log m
implies
m<2c\log c
to compute an upper bound
c_{m}=2c\log c
(for the explicit
c
given by the terms in the inequality).

When considering the continued fractional expansion of
\mu=\frac{\log\sqrt{b}}{\log\epsilon}
, we must ensure that the convergents up to the one used in the calculation of
c_{n_{1}}
are the correct ones. Sagemath can compute infinite continued fraction expansions (up to a certain memory limit). Since the convergents
\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}
of any number always suffice
q_{k}\geq\Phi^{k}
for the golden ratio
\Phi
, we know that we have to compute no more than the first
\left\lfloor\frac{\log c_{m}}{\log\Phi}\right\rfloor
convergents to obtain
q_{k}\geq c_{m}
.

If we instead compute the finite continued fraction expansion of an approximation of
\mu
, we must also take into account the approximation error we have made. This could lead to an infinite loop if the approximation error always dominates the computed lower bound from Lemma
2.3
.

It is a priori possible that the condition
\kappa\left\{q_{i}\tau\right\}>1
will never be satisfied. The algorithm can be made terminable by stopping the iteration over the convergents of
\frac{\log\epsilon}{\log\gamma_{n}}
if the reduced bound would already be greater than the initial bound
\left\lfloor\frac{c_{m}\log\gamma_{n}+l\log(c_{1}\sqrt{b})}{\log\epsilon}\right\rfloor
, or if the reduced bound is too large for the (last) following step to be practically feasible. In practice, it had often been the first or second
\kappa>1
that also satisfied
\kappa\left\{q_{i}\tau\right\}>1
.
