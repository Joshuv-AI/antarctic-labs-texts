On the Measurability of Stochastic Fourier Integral Operators
Michael Oberguggenberger, Martin Schwarz

Abstract
This work deals with the measurability of Fourier integral operators (FIOs) with random phase and amplitude functions. The key ingredient is the proof that FIOs depend continuously on their phase and amplitude functions, taken from suitable classes. The results will be applied to the solution FIO of the transport equation with spatially random transport speed as well as to FIOs describing waves in random media.



1
Introduction

In the theory of hyperbolic partial differential equations (PDEs), Fourier integral operators (FIOs) have become an important tool to examine certain properties of the solution, e.g., the propagation of singularities
[
1
,
6
,
12
]
. When studying waves in random media, the coefficients of the underlying PDEs are random fields. This has become important in seismology
[
3
,
7
]
and in material science
[
5
,
9
]
. As the phase and amplitude functions of the FIOs producing a solution or a parametrix are functions of the coefficients of the underlying PDE, one has to ensure that the FIO, respectively its action, stays measurable. The question of measurability of a FIO arises also in its own right, when a deterministic phase or amplitude function is subjected to a stochastic perturbation
[
8
]
. This work is dedicated to providing a rigorous proof of various continuity and measurability properties.

Consider a FIO of the form

\displaystyle A_{\Phi,a}[u]({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int\int{\mathrm{e}}^{i\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}a({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})u({\boldsymbol{y}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

The first task will be to show that, for
\psi\in{\mathcal{D}}(Y)
, respectively
u\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(Y)
, the maps

(\Phi,a)\mapsto A_{\Phi,a}[\psi],\quad(\Phi,a)\mapsto A_{\Phi,a}[u]

(1)

are continuous with values in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(X)
, respectively
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(X)
, on suitable spaces of phase and amplitude functions. (Here
X
and
Y
are open subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$X$}}}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}}}
.) Equipping these spaces with their Borel
\sigma
-algebra, we consider random functions

\omega\to(\Phi_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),a_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}))

on a probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
. The second task will be to infer that the maps

\omega\mapsto A_{\Phi_{\omega},a_{\omega}}[\psi],\quad\omega\mapsto A_{\Phi_{\omega},a_{\omega}}[u]

are measurable as well. The applicability of these results will be demonstrated in three examples: the transport equation and the half wave equation, both with a spatially random propagation speed, and a random perturbation of the solution operator to the wave equation.

The plan of the paper is as follows. We start by recalling required facts from the classical theory of Fourier integral operators. In the subsequent section, we prove the continuity and measurability results. The final section addresses the announced applications. The paper is part of a larger program aiming at studying wave propagation in random media by means of stochastic Fourier integral operators
[
8
,
10
]
; it provides the probabilistic basis for this program.

2
Classical theory of oscillatory integrals and FIOs

2.1
Oscillatory integrals

Let
{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}},{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
Y\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}}}
be an open set. The subsequent short exposition follows
[
11
]
. Let

\displaystyle I_{\Phi}(au)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}}\int_{Y}{\mathrm{e}}^{i\Phi({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}a({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})u({\boldsymbol{y}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

(2)

Here
u\in{\mathcal{D}}(Y)
is a smooth function with compact support and
\Phi:Y\times{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}
is a
phase function
, which means that
\Phi\big|_{Y\times({\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}\backslash\boldsymbol{\left\{0\right\}})}
is smooth, real valued and positively homogeneous of degree
1
in
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. Furthermore,
\Phi
does not have any critical points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}\backslash{\left\{\boldsymbol{0}\right\}}
, i.e., for all
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in Y
and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}\backslash{\left\{\boldsymbol{0}\right\}}

[\partial_{y_{1}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\ldots,\partial_{y_{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}}}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\partial_{\xi_{1}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\ldots,\partial_{\xi_{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]^{{\mathsf{T}}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}.

The function
a:Y\times{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}
is a Hörmander symbol of class
S_{\varrho,\delta}^{d}(Y\times{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}})
,
d\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
0\leq\delta<1
and
0<\varrho\leq 1
. That means, it is smooth and for any given multi-indices
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}}},{\boldsymbol{l}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}
and any compact
K\subset Y
there exists a constant
C_{{\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{k}},K}
such that

\left|\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{{\boldsymbol{l}}}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\ a({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|\leq C_{{\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{k}},K}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\rangle^{d-\varrho\left|{\boldsymbol{l}}\right|+\delta\left|{\boldsymbol{k}}\right|},

where
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in K
and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}
. As usual, we write
\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\rangle=(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{2})^{1/2}
.

In general,
({\mathrm{e}}^{i\Phi({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}a({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})u({\boldsymbol{y}}))
is not absolutely integrable; the oscillatory integral (
2
) has to be regularized. We recall the usual procedure, as presented, e.g., in
[
11
]
.

Let
\chi\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}})
with
\chi({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\equiv 1
for
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|<1
and
\chi({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\equiv 0
for
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|>2
. Furthermore, let

r({\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\bigg(\sum_{l=1}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{2}\left|\partial_{\xi_{l}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n_{y}}\left|\partial_{y_{k}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|^{2}\bigg),

(3)

and

{\underline{\alpha}}_{l}=\frac{-i}{r}(1-\chi)\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{2}(\partial_{\xi_{l}}\Phi),\quad{\underline{\beta}}_{k}=\frac{-i}{r}(1-\chi)(\partial_{y_{k}}\Phi),\quad{\underline{\gamma}}=\chi.

(4)

Let
L
be the differential operator

\displaystyle Lf=-\sum_{l=1}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}{\partial_{\xi_{l}}}({\underline{\alpha}}_{l}f)-\sum_{k=1}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}}}{\partial_{y_{k}}}({\underline{\beta}}_{k}f)+{\underline{\gamma}}f.

(5)

Then, the formal adjoint operator
{}^{\mathsf{t}}\!{L}

{}^{\mathsf{t}}\!{L}=\bigg(\sum_{l=1}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}{\underline{\alpha}}_{l}{\partial_{\xi_{l}}}+\sum_{k=1}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}}}{\underline{\beta}}_{k}{\partial_{y_{k}}}+{\underline{\gamma}}\bigg),

satisfies

{}^{\mathsf{t}}\!{L}{\mathrm{e}}^{i\Phi}={\mathrm{e}}^{i\Phi}.

Furthermore,
{\underline{\alpha}}_{l}\in S^{0}_{1,0}(Y\times{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}),{\underline{\beta}}_{k}\in S^{-1}_{1,0}(Y\times{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}})
and
{\underline{\gamma}}\in S^{-1}_{1,0}(Y\times{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}})
.

Finally, one can choose a
\kappa\in{\mathbb{N}}
large enough and iteratively apply
L
to (
2
), and get a convergent integral by

I_{\Phi}(au)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}}\int_{Y}{\mathrm{e}}^{i\Phi({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}L^{\kappa}(a({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})u({\boldsymbol{y}}))\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

2.2
Classical theory of FIOs

Let
{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$X$}}},{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}},{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
X
resp.
Y
be an open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$X$}}}}
resp.
{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}}}
. A Fourier integral operator is an operator of the form

\displaystyle A_{\Phi,a}[u]({\boldsymbol{x}})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}}\int_{Y}{\mathrm{e}}^{i\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}a({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})u({\boldsymbol{y}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}\,\mathrm{d}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\raisebox{3.65973pt}{$\scriptstyle-\!$}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}},

(6)

where
\,\mathrm{d}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\raisebox{3.65973pt}{$\scriptstyle-\!$}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=(2\pi)^{-n}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. Let
\Phi:X\times Y\times{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
phase function
on
X\times Y\times{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}
and
a\in S^{d}_{\varrho,\delta}(X\times Y\times{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}})
an
amplitude function
. Furthermore, the following conditions for
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\neq 0
are assumed:

\displaystyle[\partial_{x_{1}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\ldots,\partial_{x_{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$X$}}}}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\partial_{\xi_{1}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\ldots,\partial_{\xi_{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]^{{\mathsf{T}}}

\displaystyle\neq\boldsymbol{0},

(7)

\displaystyle[\partial_{y_{1}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\ldots,\partial_{y_{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}}}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\partial_{\xi_{1}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\ldots,\partial_{\xi_{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})]^{{\mathsf{T}}}

\displaystyle\neq\boldsymbol{0},

(8)

The function
\Phi
is then called an
operator phase function
.

By classical theory, if condition (
8
) is satisfied, operator (
6
) continuously maps
{\mathcal{D}}(Y)
into
{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(X)
. Under condition (
7
) operator (
6
) can be extended to a continuous map from
{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(Y)
into
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(X)
by

\left\langle A[u],\phi\right\rangle=\left\langle u,{}^{\mathsf{t}}\!{A}[\phi]\right\rangle,

where

{}^{\mathsf{t}}\!{A}_{\Phi,a}[\phi]({\boldsymbol{y}})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}}\int_{X}{\mathrm{e}}^{i\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\ a({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\phi({\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\raisebox{3.65973pt}{$\scriptstyle-\!$}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

3
Stochastic Fourier integral operators

The set of operator phase functions and the space of amplitudes are equipped with a natural metrizable topology, which we now describe. The first task in this section will be to prove the continuity of the maps (
1
). In order to derive the required inequalities, it will be necessary to replace the conditions on nondegeneracy (
7
), (
8
) by strict bounds away from zero. It turns out that this is not a serious restriction in the applications. The resulting space is a closed subset of the space of operator phase functions.

To avoid technical difficulties we hereafter exclude
\boldsymbol{0}
from
{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}
and set
\Xi={\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}\backslash\left\{\boldsymbol{0}\right\}
.
The sets
X
and
Y
are open subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$X$}}}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}}}
, respectively.

If
Z
is an open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\in\{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$X$}}},{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}}\}
, we define a compact exhaustion
K_{Z,m}
of
Z
by

K_{Z,m}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in Z,\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|\leq m,\ \dist({\boldsymbol{x}},{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash Z)\geq 1/m\}.

Definition 3.1
.

The space of
positively homogeneous functions of degree one
is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{{hg}}(X,Y,\Xi)=\Big\{

\displaystyle\Phi:X\times Y\times\Xi\to{\mathbb{R}},\Phi\text{ smooth and }

\displaystyle\text{ positively homogeneous of degree $1$ w.r.t. ${\boldsymbol{\xi}}$}\Big\}.

Let
\alpha>0
. The subspace
{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}(X,Y,\Xi)
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}(X,Y,\Xi)=\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\Phi\in{\mathcal{M}}_{{hg}}(X,Y,\Xi)\text{ such that }\forall{\boldsymbol{x}}\in X,{\boldsymbol{y}}\in Y,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\Xi:

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{-1}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\\
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\end{pmatrix}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|^{2}\geq\alpha,\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{-1}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}\\
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\end{pmatrix}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|^{2}\geq\alpha\ \Bigg\}.

Let
{\boldsymbol{j}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$X$}}}},{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$Y$}}}},{\boldsymbol{l}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}
be multi-indices and let
(p_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence of seminorms with

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &p_{m}(\Phi):=\sup\bigg\{\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|^{\boldsymbol{l}-1}\left|\partial_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{{\boldsymbol{j}}}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{{\boldsymbol{l}}}\Phi\left({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\right)\right|,{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K_{X,m},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in K_{Y,m},\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\Xi,\left|{\boldsymbol{j}}\right|+\left|\boldsymbol{k}\right|+\left|\boldsymbol{l}\right|\leq m\bigg\}.\end{aligned}

(9)

The topology of
{\mathcal{M}}_{{hg}}(X,Y,\Xi)
is defined by the seminorms
(p_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Since
\Phi
is positively homogeneous of degree one with respect to
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, an equivalent sequence of seminorms is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\widetilde{p}_{m}(\Phi)=\sup\bigg\{\big|{\partial_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{{\boldsymbol{j}}}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}^{{\boldsymbol{l}}}\Phi\left({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}\right)\big|\Big|_{\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{\xi}/{\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|}}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K_{X,m},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in K_{Y,m},\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\Xi,\left|{\boldsymbol{j}}\right|+\left|\boldsymbol{k}\right|+\left|\boldsymbol{l}\right|\leq m\bigg\}.\end{aligned}

(10)

Remark 3.3
.

Note that any function in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}
automatically satisfies (
7
) and (
8
). However, there are functions satisfying (
7
) and (
8
), but there is no
\alpha>0
such that they are in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}
. For
X=Y={\mathbb{R}}
consider

\Phi(x,y,\xi)=({\mathrm{e}}^{-x^{2}}-y)\ \xi.

Then
\partial_{\xi}\Phi(x,y,\xi)={\mathrm{e}}^{-x^{2}}
and
(\left|\xi\right|^{-1}\partial_{x}\Phi(x,y,\xi))=-2{\sign{(\xi)}}x{\mathrm{e}}^{-x^{2}},
which can be arbitrarily small for
\left|x\right|

Now let
\partial B=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\Xi,\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|=1\right\}
be the unit sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}}
. The space
{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(X\times Y\times\partial B)
with its usual topology
\mathcal{T}
is a separable Fréchet space and thus metrizable and complete
[
2
, Chapter XVII, Section 2]
. We have the following result

Proposition 3.4
.

The space
\big({\mathcal{M}}_{{hg}}(X,Y,\Xi),(p_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}\big)
is isomorphic to the space
\big({\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(X\times Y\times\partial B),\mathcal{T}\big)
and hence
separable, metrizable, and complete.

Proof.

The isomorphism is explicitly given by

\displaystyle I:{\mathcal{M}}_{{hg}}(X,Y,\Xi)

\displaystyle\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(X\times Y\times\partial B)

\displaystyle\Big(({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mapsto f({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\Big)

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigg(({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mapsto f\bigg({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},\frac{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}{\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|}\bigg)\bigg).

The bicontinuity of
I
can be most easily seen by employing local spherical coordinates on
\partial B
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\alpha>0
, then
{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}(X,Y,\Xi)
is a closed subset of
{\mathcal{M}}_{{hg}}(X,Y,\Xi)
.

Proof.

We show that

\left\|\left(\begin{matrix}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{-1}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\\
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\end{matrix}\right)(\cdot)\right\|^{2}

continuously maps
{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}(X,Y,\Xi)
to
{\mathbb{R}}
: Fix
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\in(X\times Y\times\Xi)
, and choose
m\geq 1
large enough, such that
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K_{X,m}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in K_{Y,m}
. Then, by (
9
)

\displaystyle\left|\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{-1}\partial_{x_{j}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|\leq p_{m}(\Phi)

and

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{\xi_{l}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|\leq p_{m}(\Phi).

So in total

\left\|\left(\begin{matrix}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{-1}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\\
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\end{matrix}\right)\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|^{2}\leq({n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}+{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$X$}}})(p_{m}(\Phi))^{2}.

Since the
2
-norm is continuous as well, the limit of a convergent sequence
(\Phi_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
can be pulled out

\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{-1}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\\
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\end{pmatrix}\lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{-1}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\\
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\end{pmatrix}\Phi_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|^{2}\geq\alpha,

and the continuity is shown. Since the preimage of a closed set is closed,

\left\{\Phi\in{\mathcal{M}}_{{hg}}(X,Y,\Xi):\left\|\left(\begin{matrix}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{-1}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\\
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\end{matrix}\right)\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right\|^{2}\geq\alpha\right\}

is closed.
The proof works analogously for
\left\|\left(\begin{matrix}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{-1}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}\\
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\end{matrix}\right)(\cdot)\right\|^{2}
, and the intersection of closed sets is closed again.
∎

Definition 3.6
.

The space of amplitude functions on
X\times Y\times\Xi
is defined by

{\mathcal{S}}_{\varrho,\delta}^{d}(X,Y,\Xi)=\left\{a\big|_{X\times Y\times\Xi},\ a\in S_{\varrho,\delta}^{d}(X\times Y\times{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}})\right\}.

This space is equipped with the seminorms

\displaystyle q_{m}(a)=\sup\Big\{\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\rangle^{-d+\varrho\left|{\boldsymbol{l}}\right|-\delta(\left|{\boldsymbol{j}}\right|+\left|{\boldsymbol{k}}\right|)}

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{{\boldsymbol{j}}}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{{\boldsymbol{l}}}\ a({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|,\text{ where }

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K_{X,m},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in K_{Y,m},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\Xi,\left|{\boldsymbol{j}}\right|+\left|{\boldsymbol{k}}\right|+\left|{\boldsymbol{l}}\right|\leq m\big\}.

Note that by
[
4
, Chapter VII, Section 7.8]
the space
({\mathcal{S}}_{\varrho,\delta}^{d},(q_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}})
forms a Fréchet space, and thus it is complete and metrizable. Furthermore, one can check that it is separable. Actually,
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varrho,\delta}^{d}(X,Y,\Xi)
is isomorphic with

S_{\varrho,\delta}^{d}(X\times Y\times{\mathbb{R}}^{{n_{\scalebox{0.42}{$\Xi$}}}})
.

In any case, both the phase function space and the amplitude function space are closed subsets of separable, metrizable, complete spaces. For any further consideration we will deal with

{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,\varrho,\delta}^{d}(X,Y,\Xi)={\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}(X,Y,\Xi)\times{\mathcal{S}}_{\varrho,\delta}^{d}(X,Y,\Xi),

which is equipped with the product topology of the two spaces, induced by the seminorms
\boldsymbol{p_{m}}(\Phi,a):=p_{m}(\Phi)+q_{m}(a),\ m\in\mathbb{N}
.
We call
{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,\varrho,\delta}^{d}(X,Y,\Xi)
the space of
FIO operator functions
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\Phi\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}(X,Y,\Xi)
and let
{\underline{\alpha}}_{i}
resp.
{\underline{\beta}}_{i}
be the coefficients of the regularizing operator

(see (

such that for any

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\Xi

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{{\boldsymbol{j}}}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{{\boldsymbol{l}}}\ {\underline{\alpha}}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|\leq{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(p_{m}(\Phi))\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\rangle^{-\left|{\boldsymbol{l}}\right|}

(11)

for

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{{\boldsymbol{j}}}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{{\boldsymbol{l}}}\ {\underline{\beta}}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|\leq{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(p_{m}(\Phi))\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\rangle^{-1-\left|{\boldsymbol{l}}\right|}

(12)

for

\left|{\boldsymbol{j}}\right|+\left|{\boldsymbol{k}}\right|+\left|{\boldsymbol{l}}\right|+1\leq m

For this proof we will show the inequalities only for the zeroth and first derivative of (

Recall the notation from Equation (

Since

one has that

\alpha\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{2}\leq r({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

and therefore,

\displaystyle\left|{\underline{\alpha}}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{(1-\chi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}))\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|^{2}\partial_{\xi_{i}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{r({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1-\chi({\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{\alpha}\ \partial_{\xi_{i}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq{C_{m}^{0}p_{m}(\Phi)}{=:{\mathcal{P}}_{m}^{0}(p_{m}(\Phi))}

We note that for all multi-indices
{\boldsymbol{j}},{\boldsymbol{k}}
and
{\boldsymbol{l}}
with
\left|{\boldsymbol{j}}\right|+\left|{\boldsymbol{k}}\right|+\left|{\boldsymbol{l}}\right|+1\leq m
there exists a constant

\displaystyle\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(X)

\displaystyle(x\mapsto c(\omega,x))

\displaystyle\mapsto\big(x\mapsto\gamma(\omega,x,t;0)\big)

is continuous in the
(\pi_{X,\widetilde{m}})_{\widetilde{m}\in{\mathbb{N}}}
-sense.
Therefore, for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
fixed,

\displaystyle\Phi_{\omega}:\Omega

\displaystyle\to{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}(X,Y,\Xi)

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle\mapsto\big((x,y,\xi)\mapsto\xi\cdot(\gamma(\omega,x,t;0)-y)\big)

is measurable again. Furthermore, let
a(x,y,\xi)\equiv 1
and let
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}(X)
resp.
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(X)
. Then, for fixed
\omega
, the solution to (
14
) is given by
A_{\Phi_{\omega},a}[u_{0}]
, and the maps

\begin{aligned} \Omega&\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(X)\\
\omega&\mapsto A_{\Phi_{\omega},a}[u_{0}]\end{aligned}\quad\text{resp.}\quad\begin{aligned} \Omega&\to{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(X)\\
\omega&\mapsto A_{\Phi_{\omega},a}[u_{0}]\end{aligned}

are measurable.

Example 4.2
.

Consider as before
X=Y={\mathbb{R}}
,
\Xi={\mathbb{R}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}
. The half wave equation in one dimension is

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \big(\partial_{t}+ic(\omega,x)P(D_{x})\big)\,u(\omega,x,t)&=0\\
u(\omega,x,0&)=u_{0}(x),\end{aligned}\right.

(16)

where
P
is the pseudodifferential operator with symbol
P(\xi)=\left|\xi\right|(1-\chi(\xi)),
where
\chi\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathbb{R}})
,
\chi(\xi)\equiv 1
for
\left|\xi\right|<1/4
and
\chi(\xi)\equiv 0
for
\left|\xi\right|>1/2
. We assume that
c:\Omega\mapsto{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\alpha}(X)
is measurable and that
c(\omega,x)
and
\left|\partial_{x}c(\omega,x)\right|
are bounded from above by a constant
R
for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\omega\in\Omega
. Further, all seminorms
\pi_{X,{m}}(c(\omega,\cdot))
are assumed to be bounded independently of
\omega
.

We fix
\omega\in\Omega
for now and for notational simplicity we will drop it. A FIO parametrix for (
16
) can be constructed following
[
12
, Chapter VIII, §3]
. The phase function of the parametrix is of the form
\Phi(x,y,\xi)=\left|\xi\right|\phi\big(x,\frac{\xi}{\left|\xi\right|},t\big)-y\cdot\xi
, where
\phi
satisfies the eikonal equation,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\phi(x,t)+c(x)P(\partial_{x}\phi(x,t))&=0\\
\phi(x,0)&=x\cdot\xi.\end{aligned}\right.

(17)

This is a nonlinear differential equation of the form
Q(x,t,\phi_{x},\phi_{t})=0
. In
[
13
, Chapter II, Sect 19]
one can find an explicit representation of the solution to this problem, which we will write down in the following.
Let
F(t;x_{1},\xi_{1})
and
G(t;x_{1},\xi_{1})
satisfy the following equations

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\,\mathrm{d}F}{\,\mathrm{d}t}&=c(F)P^{\prime}(G)\\
F(0;x_{1},\xi_{1})&=x_{1}\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\,\mathrm{d}G}{\,\mathrm{d}t}&=-c^{\prime}(F)P(G)\\
G(0;x_{1},\xi_{1})&=\xi_{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

(18)

There exists a
T\in{\mathbb{R}}
, such that

P(G(t;x_{1},\xi_{1}))\equiv\left|\xi_{1}\right|\quad\text{and}\quad P^{\prime}(G(t;x_{1},\xi_{1}))\equiv\sign{\xi_{1}}

for
0\leq t\leq T
and for all
\xi_{1},x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\left|\xi_{1}\right|\geq 1
. In that case
F
does not depend on
G
and
x=F(t;x_{1},\xi_{1})
is just the flow from
(0,x_{1})
to
(t,x)
. The inverse flow is then
F(-t,x,\xi_{1})
, which goes from
(t,x)
to
(0,x_{1})
. Finally,
\phi
is given by

\displaystyle\phi(x,t,\xi)=x\xi-\int_{0}^{t}c(x)P(G(F(-s;x,\xi),s,\xi))\,\mathrm{d}s.

(19)

for
t\in[0,T]
. We observe that
F
depends only on the sign of
\xi_{1}
and
\lambda G(t;x_{1},\xi_{1})=G(t;x_{1},\lambda\xi_{1})
for
\lambda\geq 1
,
\left|\xi_{1}\right|>1
and all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
t\in[0,T]
. Thus,

\phi(x,t,\xi)-\left|\xi\right|\phi\bigg(x,t,\frac{\xi}{\left|\xi\right|}\bigg)

is compactly supported with respect to
\xi
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
t\in[0,T]
. Noting that
\phi(x,0,\xi)=x\xi
and by the representation (
19
) one can also see that for a sufficiently small time interval, there is an
\alpha
such that
\Phi\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}(X,Y,\Xi)
.

Finally, by classical ODE theory, one can show that
F
and
G
continuously depend on
c
and thus also
\Phi
(at least for a short time interval).

To obtain the amplitude one has to solve a cascade of transport equations of the form

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{t}-c(x)P^{\prime}(\xi)\partial_{x}+H(x,t,\xi)\big)a_{j}(x,t,\xi)=0,\qquad j\leq 0,

where
H
is a smooth function, depending on derivatives of
c
,
\phi
and
a_{k},k>j
(for details see again
[
12
, Chapter VIII, §3]
). Similarly to the previous example one can show that
a_{j}
continuously depends on
c
. In
[
11
]
one can find an explicit construction of a function
a
such that
(a-\sum_{j\leq 0}a_{j}(x,\xi,t))
is smoothing, namely

a(x,t,\xi)=\sum_{j\leq 0}(1-\chi(\xi/n_{j}))a_{j}(x,t,\xi),

where
\chi\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathbb{R}})
, as above,
\chi(\xi)\equiv 1
for
\left|\xi\right|<1/4
and
\chi(\xi)\equiv 0
for
\left|\xi\right|>1/2
, and
n_{j}\in{\mathbb{N}}
approaches
+\infty
quickly enough as
j
goes to
-\infty
. Since the
a_{j}
depend continuously on
c
in the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-topology, one can chose
n_{j}
such that the series converges uniformly together with all derivatives when
c
is taken from a bounded set in
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\infty}(X)
. Thus,
a
depends continuously on
c
as well in the corresponding topologies.

Consequently, the phase function
\Phi
and the amplitude function
a
of the parametrix
A_{\Phi,a}
depend continuously on
c
in the corresponding topologies.

Recall that
c:\Omega\to\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\infty}(X)
is a random function. At fixed
\omega\in\Omega
, one obtains the parametrix
A_{\Phi_{\omega},a_{\omega}}
. By Theorem
3.13
, if
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathbb{R}})
resp.
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}})
, then the map

\omega\to A_{\Phi_{\omega},a_{\omega}}[u_{0}](x,t)

is measurable.

Example 4.3
.

The standard bottom-up approach to modeling waves in random media would place the randomness in the coefficients of the underlying PDEs. However, the solution depends in a strongly nonlinear way on the coefficients of the equation, even if the PDEs are linear. This makes it hard to track or compute the stochastic features of the solution, such
as the expectation, the variance or the autocovariance function. Accordingly, a top-down approach has been proposed in
[
9
,
10
]
. Starting from the mean field equations as constant coefficient PDEs, the deterministic solution can readily be represented by FIOs. The stochastic properties of the medium are then modeled

a posteriori
by random perturbations of the phase and amplitude functions. In applications to material sciences, e.g., damage detection, these random perturbations can be calibrated to measurement data (just as in the bottom-up approach, where the coefficients are calibrated to measurement data). This program has been carried through in

[
10
]
in the case of three-dimensional linear elasticity.

The approach leads to stochastic FIOs, whose measurability has to be proven. We present a simplified example using the scalar wave equation.

Let
Y={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
X={\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}
and
\Xi={\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash\left\{\boldsymbol{0}\right\}
. One can solve the deterministic wave equation with constant wave speed
c_{0}

\left\{\begin{aligned} (\partial_{tt}-c_{0}\Delta)u({\boldsymbol{x}},t)&=0\\
u({\boldsymbol{x}},0)&=u_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})\\
\partial_{t}u({\boldsymbol{x}},0)&=0\end{aligned}\right.

using a linear combination of FIOs with phase functions

\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}},t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\rangle\pm c\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|t

and amplitude function

a({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\equiv\frac{1}{2}.

Then, instead of using the deterministic
\Phi
and
a
, randomly perturbed version are introduced. One has to make sure that the randomly perturbed phase function is still an operator phase function. Thus fix
\alpha>0
and let

\displaystyle c:\Omega

\displaystyle\to{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle\mapsto\big({\boldsymbol{x}}\mapsto c(\omega,{\boldsymbol{x}}))

be a measurable, smooth random field with
{\mathbb{E}}(c(x))\equiv c_{0}
. Let
\Phi_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{x}},t,{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\left\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\rangle\pm\left\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right\|c({\omega,\boldsymbol{x}})t
. Then,
\Phi_{\omega}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}(X,Y,\Xi)
. Note that this is guaranteed, since we included the time into the image space
X
and
\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|^{-1}\left|\partial_{t}\Phi_{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x},t,\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\xi})\right|\geq\alpha
.

Furthermore, let

\displaystyle a:\Omega

\displaystyle\to{\mathcal{S}}_{\varrho,\delta}^{d}(X,Y,\Xi)

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle\mapsto a_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{x}},t,{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

be random with
{\mathbb{E}}(a_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}))=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Then,
(\Phi_{\omega},a_{\omega})\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,\varrho,\delta}^{d}(X,Y,\Xi)
and, as above,
\omega\to A_{\Phi_{\omega},a_{\omega}}[u_{0}]
is measurable for a given
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}(X)
resp.
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(X)
in the corresponding topology. If
\Phi
and
a
are independent, the expectation, the variance and the autocovariance function of
A_{\Phi_{\omega},a_{\omega}}[u_{0}]({\boldsymbol{x}})
, resp.
\langle A_{\Phi_{\omega},a_{\omega}}[u_{0}],\psi\rangle
can be computed without much difficulty, observing that
{\mathbb{E}}\big(\exp(i\Phi_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{x}},t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{y}}))\big)
is just the characteristic function of the random variable
\Phi_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{x}},t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
, see
[
8
]
.
