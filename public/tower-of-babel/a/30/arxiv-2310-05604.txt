Photon-number resolution with microwave Josephson photomultipliers
E. V. Stolyarov, O. V. Kliushnichenko, V. S. Kovtoniuk, A. A. Semenov

Abstract
We study counting photons confined in a mode of a microwave resonator via repeated measurements by a Josephson photomultiplier (JPM). The considered JPM is essentially a flux-biased phase qubit operating as a single-photon detector. We identify optimal operational regimes that maximize photon-number resolution within a predetermined range. Two counting techniques are studied. The first is to count the total number of clicks in the measurement sequence. The second involves counting the number of clicks until the occurrence of either the first no-click event or the end of the measurement sequence. Our theoretical methods employ the derived positive operator-valued measures for the considered photocounting techniques and the introduced measure of the photon-number resolution. The results reveal that the resolution decrease in both cases is mainly caused by the JPM relaxation. As an example, we show how the obtained results can be used for practical testing nonclassical properties of electromagnetic radiation in a microwave resonator.

The JPM is an on-chip device compatible with other circuit QED components.
Technically, it is a phase qubit
Martinis 2009
that acts as a narrowband absorbing photodetector.
In general, there are two types of JPMs.
The first type
Chen et al. 2011
;
Poudel et al. 2012
;
Govia et al. 2012
;
Oelsner et al. 2013
;
Govia et al. 2014
;
Schöndorf et al. 2018
;
Oelsner et al. 2017
;
Golubev et al. 2021
;
Guarcello et al. 2021
is based on a single current-biased Josephson junction (JJ)
Martinis et al. 1985
.
While compact and simple, this type of JPM has an essential disadvantage.
When a photon is absorbed by such a JPM, it switches to the voltage state, resulting in an outburst of quasiparticles which strongly affect the next measurements.
The quasiparticles have relaxation times of milliseconds
Lenander et al. 2011
;
Wang et al. 2014
which decreases the measurement repetition rate for this JPM design to
\displaystyle\textstyle\sim 1\,\mathrm{kHz}

Ribeill 2016
limiting its practical use.

To remedy the limitations of current-biased JPMs, another design has been proposed and experimentally demonstrated; cf. Refs.
Shnyrkov et al. 2018
;
Opremcak et al. 2018
;
Opremcak et al. 2021
;
Shnyrkov et al. 2023
.
In this design, a JJ is shunted by an inductor, which adds the quadratic term to the JJ cosine potential.
By adjusting the external flux through the JPM loop, the JPM potential can be set to a two-well configuration.
This prevents the JPM from switching completely to the voltage state due to photon absorption and subsequent tunneling out of the local potential well.
Instead, photon absorption results in a single tunneling event.
By reading which well the particle is in, one can determine if the tunneling event has occurred.
The inductively-shunted design provides fast readout and reset.

The macroscopic response of JPMs indicates absorption of a single photon with high detection efficiency.
This feature sets them apart from most single-photon detectors operating in the optical domain, whose macroscopic response is indistinguishable for single- and multiphoton absorption.
This brings us to the idea of using JPMs for fast repetition measurements.
In this paper, we theoretically examine whether such a measurement sequence can give a result similar to that of ideal photon-number resolving (PNR) detectors.

We focus on two counting techniques.
In the first, we repeat the measurement a fixed number of times, regardless of the obtained outcomes.
The second approach consists in counting photons until either the first no-click event or the end of the sequence.
The number of photons is then associated with the number of clicks.

Our theoretical methods imply an accurate derivation of the positive operator-valued measures (POVMs), see e.g., Refs.
Nielsen and Chuang
;
van Enk 2017
, for both counting techniques.
This in fact means that we get photocounting formulas tailored to the considered scenarios.
We introduce a measure of photon-number resolution, based on a recent observation in Ref.
Len et al. 2022
, that the probability of registering
\displaystyle n
clicks given
\displaystyle n
photons is a relevant characteristic of photon-number resolution.
We optimize this measure with parameters of JPM and show that in practical situation such detectors can be considered as pseudo-PNR detectors, with high ability to resolve between adjacent photon numbers.

We use our theory to show how JPMs can be employed to test nonclassicality of electromagnetic radiation.
As widely accepted in quantum optics

;
Mandel 1986
;
Sperling and Walmsley 2018a

*sperling2018b
;

\displaystyle P

;

We utilize the methods recently developed in Refs.
Semenov and Klimov 2021
;
Kovtoniuk et al. 2023
, which enable us to test whether the photocounting statistics for the given quantum state can be reproduced with the classical electromagnetic radiation.

We consider a circuit QED setup consisting of a Josephson photomultiplier (JPM) coupled to a mode of a microwave resonator. The scheme of the considered system is shown in Fig.

The resonator is represented by either a coplanar waveguide resonator (CPW)

;

All these types of resonators are routinely employed in the state-of-the-art circuit QED systems.
In the considered setup, the coupling between the resonator mode and the JPM is mediated by a capacitor

The JPM design we consider is based on a flux-biased phase qubit

constituted by the JJ shunted by a gradiometric inductance
\displaystyle\textstyle L_{\mathrm{G}}
and a large capacitance
\displaystyle\textstyle C_{\mathrm{s}}\gg C_{\mathrm{J}}
, where
\displaystyle\textstyle C_{\mathrm{J}}
is the capacitance of the junction.
The JJ is characterized by the critical current
\displaystyle\textstyle I_{0}
and the Josephson energy
\displaystyle\textstyle E_{\mathrm{J}}=I_{0}\Phi_{0}/(2\pi)
, where
\displaystyle\textstyle\Phi_{0}=h/(2e)
denotes the magnetic flux quantum.
The JJ and the gradiometric inductance
\displaystyle\textstyle L_{\mathrm{G}}
comprise together an rf-SQUID loop threaded by the external flux
\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{b}}
generated by the external flux-bias line (FBL).
Such a JPM design was experimentally demonstrated by Opremcak
et al.

Opremcak et al. 2018
;
Opremcak et al. 2021
.
For gaining more control over the JPM operation frequency, one can replace the single JJ in the JPM circuit with a dc SQUID controlled via an individual FBL

.
However, for conciseness, here we consider a JPM design with a single JJ similar to that demonstrated in Refs.
Opremcak et al. 2018
;
Opremcak et al. 2021
.
The JPM schematics is demonstrated in Fig.
1
(b).

With the proper choice of the Josephson energy
\displaystyle\textstyle E_{\mathrm{J}}
and the gradiometric inductance
\displaystyle\textstyle L_{\mathrm{G}}
, the JPM potential can feature either one or two wells depending on the value of the external flux
\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{b}}
.
For probing the resonator field, one tunes the flux
\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{b}}
in such a way that the JPM potential has an asymmetric two-well configuration with a deep well, accommodating multiple energy states, and a shallow well hosting only two metastable states (denoted as
\displaystyle\textstyle|0\rangle
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|1\rangle
) as shown in Fig.
1
(c).
For ensuring the efficient excitation exchange between the resonator and the JPM, the resonator frequency and the frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
of
\displaystyle\textstyle|0\rangle\leftrightarrow|1\rangle
JPM transition are tuned to resonance.
The level
\displaystyle\textstyle|1\rangle
lies near the top of the potential barrier separating the wells.
This provides the rapid tunneling of the excitation to the deep well.
Once the excitation has tunneled to the deep well, it undergoes the fast cascaded relaxation via multiple levels accommodated in the deep well and ultimately relaxes to the global minimum of the JPM potential.
This process is schematically illustrated in Fig.
1
(c).
The tunneling and relaxation to the deep potential well results in the change of the JPM classical fluxoid state, which is interpreted as a “click”.

To ensure the rapid relaxation to the bottom of the potential well, we propose to couple the JPM to an additional “waste” mode.
The latter constitutes an engineered electromagnetic environment implemented by a long microwave transmission line terminated by an impedance-matched absorbing element

and supplemented by a side-coupled resonator with frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
.
This resonator serves as a band-stop Purcell filter

rejecting the photons with frequencies close to the JPM working frequency.
The scheme of this JPM supplement is shown in Fig.
1
(d).
Besides, such a “waste” mode coupled to the JPM efficiently channels the broadband cascade of microwave photons generated in the course of the JPM relaxation out of the resonator-JPM system.
This provides an additional suppression of the spurious population of the resonator by those photons.
The latter effect was observed in experiment in Ref.
Opremcak et al. 2018
.

Now, let us outline the procedure of photodetection [see diagram in Fig.
1
(e)] in the setup we consider.
Here, we mainly follow Refs.
Opremcak et al. 2018
;
Opremcak et al. 2021
, and the considered sequence is essentially a simplified version of a detection procedure presented therein.
On the initial stage, we prepare a quantum state of the resonator mode.
In particular, Fock states
Hofheinz et al. 2008
;
Premaratne et al. 2017
;
Pfaff et al. 2017
and their arbitrary superpositions
Hofheinz et al. 2009
, squeezed vacuum
Moon and Girvin 2005
;
Didier et al. 2014
;
Kono et al. 2017
;
Malnou et al. 2018
;
Dassonneville et al. 2021
;
Eickbusch et al. 2022
, and Schrödinger cat states
Leghtas et al. 2013
;
Pfaff et al. 2017
;
Ma et al. 2019
;
Girvin 2019
can be prepared for further analysis.
On the preparation stage, the JPM potential is set in a symmetric two-well configuration.
The frequencies of the JPM interlevel transitions are far detuned from the resonator frequency and there is no excitation exchange between the resonator mode and the JPM.

After the resonator field state being prepared, we need to efficiently capture the resonator photons by the JPM.
On the photon capture stage, the JPM potential is rapidly tilted in such a way, that the left well contains just two levels and the transition frequency between them is close to the resonator frequency, which enables excitation exchange between the resonator and the JPM.
As we mentioned earlier, in this regime, the resonator photon drives the JPM from the ground to the excited state.
The excitation then tunnels via the barrier and relaxes to the bottom of the right deep well.
We let the resonator and the JPM interact during time
\displaystyle\textstyle t_{\mathrm{cpt}}
long enough to ensure high probability of photon absorption and subsequent interwell tunneling.

Next, we need to perform the JPM readout to determine whether the photon absorption and tunneling have occurred and the JPM has changed its fluxoid state.
On the readout stage, the JPM potential is tilted back to a slightly asymmetric two-well configuration that each well is characterized by a different plasma frequency.
By probing the JPM with a weak microwave signal on one of the plasma resonances, one can determine the JPM fluxoid state with high (
\displaystyle\textstyle>99.9\%
) fidelity in
\displaystyle\textstyle\sim 250\,\mathrm{ns}

Opremcak et al. 2021
.

Besides the JPM readout approach of Refs.
Opremcak et al. 2018
;
Opremcak et al. 2021
outlined above, there are other techniques for the JPM readout.
For example, the interwell tunneling induced by the absorption of the resonator photon results in the change of the JPM fluxoid state which can be detected by the rf SQUID weakly coupled to the JPM
Shnyrkov et al. 2023
.
Alternatively, the JPM fluxoid state can be detected by the ballistic fluxons propagating in the underdamped Josephson transmission line interfaced with the single-flux quantum logic digital circuitry
Howington et al. 2019
.

Finally, after the readout the JPM is reset to the single-well configuration and the current photodetection procedure is over.
For the next measurement, the JPM potential is again set to the photon capture regime and the whole detection procedure is repeated.

The Model

III.1
The Hamiltonian

Let us start our analysis with a classical description of the considered system comprised by the resonator mode coupled to the JPM.
It is described by the classical Hamiltonian

H=H_{\mathrm{r}}+H_{\mathrm{p}}+H_{\mathrm{int}},

(1)

see Appendix
A
for details of its derivation.
Here
\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{r}}
,
\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{p}}
, and
\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{int}}
are terms describing the resonator mode, the JPM, and the interaction between them, respectively.

The first term in Eq. (
1
) is given by

H_{\mathrm{r}}=4E_{C,\mathrm{r}}n_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}+E_{L,\mathrm{r}}\frac{\phi_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}}{2},

(2)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle n_{\mathrm{r}}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\phi_{\mathrm{r}}
denote the resonator Cooper-pair number and phase variables, respectively, which are proportional to field quadratures of the resonator mode.
In Eq. (
2
), parameters
\displaystyle E_{C,\mathrm{r}}=e^{2}/(2C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{r}})
and
\displaystyle E_{L,\mathrm{r}}=(\Phi_{0}/2\pi)^{2}/L_{\mathrm{r}}
are the resonator capacitive and inductive energies, respectively.
The last term in Eq. (
2
) is the resonator potential energy.

The second term in Eq. (
1
) is the JPM Hamiltonian expressed as

H_{\mathrm{p}}=4E_{C,\mathrm{p}}n_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}+\overbrace{E_{L,\mathrm{p}}\frac{(\phi_{\mathrm{p}}-\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{b}})^{2}}{2}-E_{\mathrm{J}}\cos\phi_{\mathrm{p}}}^{U_{\mathrm{p}}(\phi_{\mathrm{p}})},

(3)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle n_{\mathrm{p}}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\phi_{\mathrm{p}}
denote the JPM Cooper-pair number and phase variables, respectively.
The quantities
\displaystyle\textstyle E_{C,\mathrm{p}}=e^{2}/(2C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{p}})
and
\displaystyle\textstyle E_{L,\mathrm{p}}=(\Phi_{0}/2\pi)^{2}/L_{\mathrm{G}}
stand for the capacitive and inductive energies of the photodetector, respectively.
The last two terms in Eq. (
3
) constitute the JPM potential energy
\displaystyle\textstyle U_{\mathrm{p}}(\phi_{\mathrm{p}})
, where
\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{J}}
is the Josephson energy.

The third term in Eq. (
1
), describing the interaction between the resonator mode and the JPM, reads as

H_{\mathrm{int}}=E_{C,\mathrm{c}}\,n_{\mathrm{r}}n_{\mathrm{p}},

(4)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle E_{C,\mathrm{c}}=e^{2}/(2C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{c}})
stands for the energy of the capacitive coupling between the resonator and the JPM.
The definitions of the renormalized (loaded) capacitances
\displaystyle\textstyle C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{r}}
,
\displaystyle\textstyle C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{p}}
, and
\displaystyle\textstyle C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{c}}
arising in Eqs. (
2
)–(
4
) are given in Eq. (
50
) in Appendix
A
.

In the phase qubit regime, one has
\displaystyle\textstyle E_{C,\mathrm{p}}\ll E_{\mathrm{J}},E_{L,\mathrm{p}}
.
This implies that the JPM phase “particle” is localized in the vicinity of the local minimum of the JPM potential resembling a behavior of a classical heavy particle oscillating near its equilibrium position in a potential well.
The positions of the local minima of the JPM potential
\displaystyle\textstyle U_{\mathrm{p}}(\phi)
are determined from the standard conditions

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\phi}U_{\mathrm{p}}(\phi)\big|_{\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{p}}}=0,\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\phi^{2}}U_{\mathrm{p}}(\phi)\big|_{\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{p}}}>0,

which yield

\begin{split}&E_{L,\mathrm{p}}(\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{p}}-\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{b}})+E_{\mathrm{J}}\sin\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{p}}=0,\\
&E_{L,\mathrm{p}}+E_{\mathrm{J}}\cos\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{p}}>0.\end{split}

(5)

Next, let us define
\displaystyle\textstyle\phi_{\mathrm{p}}=\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{p}}+\varphi_{\mathrm{p}}
with
\displaystyle\textstyle\varphi_{\mathrm{p}}
being a fluctuation from the equilibrium position of the JPM phase “particle”.
Similar arguments hold for the resonator phase “particle” as well, since
\displaystyle\textstyle E_{C,\mathrm{r}}\ll E_{L,\mathrm{r}}
.
Thus, we can formally write
\displaystyle\textstyle\phi_{\mathrm{r}}=\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{r}}+\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}
, where
\displaystyle\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{r}}
is the minimum of the resonator potential. However, since
\displaystyle\textstyle\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{r}}=0
, we just replace
\displaystyle\textstyle\phi_{\mathrm{r}}
with
\displaystyle\textstyle\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}
for consistency of notations.

To proceed, we expand the cosine term in the JPM potential
\displaystyle\textstyle U_{\mathrm{p}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{p}}+\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{p}})
up to the fourth order in
\displaystyle\textstyle\varphi_{\mathrm{p}}
.
Accounting for the first line in Eq. (
5
) and dropping the constant terms, one obtains

U_{\mathrm{p}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{p}})=E^{\prime}_{L,\mathrm{p}}\,\frac{\varphi_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}}{2}-E_{\mathrm{J}}\sin\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\frac{\varphi_{\mathrm{p}}^{3}}{6}-E_{\mathrm{J}}\cos\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\frac{\varphi_{\mathrm{p}}^{4}}{24}.

(6)

Here
\displaystyle\textstyle E^{\prime}_{L,\mathrm{p}}=E_{L,\mathrm{p}}+E_{\mathrm{J}}\cos\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{p}}
can be interpreted as the total inductive energy of the JPM.

Now, we proceed to a quantum description of the considered resonator mode–JPM system.
For obtaining the quantum version of the circuit Hamiltonian, we follow the canonical quantization approach

;

We promote the classical variables of the JPM and the resonator mode to the quantum operators obeying the commutation relations
\displaystyle\textstyle[\hat{\varphi}_{\mathrm{p}},\hat{n}_{\mathrm{p}}]=[\hat{\varphi}_{\mathrm{r}},\hat{n}_{\mathrm{r}}]=\mathrm{i}

Then, we express these operators in terms of the ladder operators.
For the resonator phase and Cooper-pair number variables
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\varphi}_{\mathrm{r}}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{n}_{\mathrm{r}}

\hat{\varphi}_{\mathrm{r}}=\varphi_{\mathrm{zpf},\mathrm{r}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}),\quad\hat{n}_{\mathrm{r}}=-\mathrm{i}\,n_{\mathrm{zpf},\mathrm{r}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{a}),

(7)

\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{a}
(

\displaystyle\textstyle\varphi_{\mathrm{zpf},\mathrm{r}}=(2E_{C,\mathrm{r}}/E_{L,\mathrm{r}})^{1/4}

\displaystyle\textstyle n_{\mathrm{zpf},\mathrm{r}}=[E_{L,\mathrm{r}}/(32E_{C,\mathrm{r}})]^{1/4}

Analogously, the JPM phase and the Cooper-pair number variables are expressed as

\hat{\varphi}_{\mathrm{p}}=\varphi_{\mathrm{zpf},\mathrm{p}}(\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\hat{b}),\quad\hat{n}_{\mathrm{p}}=-\mathrm{i}\,n_{\mathrm{zpf},\mathrm{p}}(\hat{b}^{\dagger}-\hat{b}).

(8)

\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{b}
(
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{b}^{\dagger}
) is an annihilation (creation) operator of an excitation in the JPM,
\displaystyle\textstyle\varphi_{\mathrm{zpf},\mathrm{p}}=(2E_{C,\mathrm{p}}/E^{\prime}_{L,\mathrm{p}})^{1/4}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle n_{\mathrm{zpf},\mathrm{p}}=[E^{\prime}_{L,\mathrm{p}}/(32E_{C,\mathrm{p}})]^{1/4}

) yields the quantum Hamiltonian of the resonator mode–JPM circuit,

\begin{split}\frac{\hat{\mathcal{H}}}{\hbar}=&\,\overbrace{\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}^{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}}}+\overbrace{\varpi_{\mathrm{p}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}-\varXi_{3}(\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\hat{b})^{3}-\varXi_{4}(\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\hat{b})^{4}}^{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{p}}}\\
&\,\underbrace{-g(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{a})(\hat{b}^{\dagger}-\hat{b})}_{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{int}}}.\end{split}

(9)

Here the first term is the Hamiltonian of the quantum harmonic oscillator (mode of the resonator) with frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{\mathrm{r}}=1/\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{r}}C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{r}}}
.
The second term in the above equation is the Hamiltonian of the JPM, where
\displaystyle\textstyle\varpi_{\mathrm{p}}
is the JPM plasma frequency determined as

\varpi_{\mathrm{p}}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\sqrt{8E_{C,\mathrm{p}}E^{\prime}_{L,\mathrm{p}}}.

(10)

Note that
\displaystyle\textstyle\varpi_{\mathrm{p}}
depends on the external flux
\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{b}}
.
Parameters
\displaystyle\textstyle\varXi_{3}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\varXi_{4}
in Eq. (
9
) are defined as

\varXi_{3}=\frac{E_{\mathrm{J}}}{6\hbar}\varphi_{\mathrm{zpf},\mathrm{p}}^{3}\sin\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{p}},\quad\varXi_{4}=\frac{E_{\mathrm{J}}}{24\hbar}\varphi_{\mathrm{zpf},\mathrm{p}}^{4}\cos\phi^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{p}}.

(11)

The last term in Eq. (
9
) describes the coupling between the resonator mode and the JPM, where

g=\frac{E_{C,\mathrm{c}}}{\hbar}\,n_{\mathrm{zpf},\mathrm{r}}n_{\mathrm{zpf},\mathrm{p}}

(12)

is the strength of this coupling.

The JPM Hamiltonian in Eq. (
9
) can be approximately diagonalized using the Schrieffer-Wolff (SW) unitary transformation
Bravyi et al. 2011
;
Klimov and Sanchez-Soto 2000
:

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{p}}\rightarrow\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\hat{\mathcal{S}}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\hat{\mathcal{S}}},

(13)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\lambda=\varXi_{3}/\varpi_{\mathrm{p}}
and the anti-Hermitian operator
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\mathcal{S}}
is given by

\hat{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{3}(\hat{b}^{{\dagger}3}-\hat{b}^{3})+3(\hat{b}^{{\dagger}2}\hat{b}-\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}^{2})+3(\hat{b}^{\dagger}-\hat{b}).

(14)

For the typical circuit parameters we work with, one has
\displaystyle\textstyle\lambda^{2}\ll 1
.
Applying the SW transformation to the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
9
), keeping the terms only up to the first order in
\displaystyle\textstyle\lambda
, and making the rotating-wave approximation by dropping the fast-oscillating terms, one arrives at the effective circuit Hamiltonian as follows

\begin{split}\frac{\hat{\mathcal{H}}}{\hbar}=&\,\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\omega_{\mathrm{p}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}-\frac{\xi}{2}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}2}\hat{b}^{2}\\
&\,+g(\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{b})-g_{2}(\hat{b}^{{\dagger}2}\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{b}^{2}),\end{split}

(15)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{\mathrm{p}}=\varpi_{\mathrm{p}}-\xi
is the JPM transition frequency and
\displaystyle\textstyle\xi=60\lambda\varXi_{3}+12\varXi_{4}
is the anharmonicity of the JPM levels.
Figure
2
demonstrates the dependence of the JPM transition frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
on the bias flux
\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{b}}
and the JJ critical current
\displaystyle\textstyle I_{0}
.
The terms in the bottom line in Eq. (
15
) describe couplings between the resonator mode and the JPM.
The first term in the bottom line describes the coupling between the resonator mode and the JPM giving rise to the coherent single-excitation exchange between them.
The second term in the bottom line in Eq. (
15
) describes the resonator mode-JPM coupling with strength
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{2}=4\lambda g
, which leads to conversion of a single excitation (photon) in the resonator into a pair of excitations in the JPM.
This nondipolar interaction arises due to the asymmetry of the shallow well of the JPM potential resulting in that the transitions between the JPM states with the same parity of their wavefunction become allowed.

Dependence of the transition frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
of the uncoupled (
\displaystyle\textstyle C_{\mathrm{c}}=0
) JPM on the external flux
\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{b}}
for various values of the JJ critical current
\displaystyle\textstyle I_{0}
(indicated near the corresponding lines).
The rest of the JPM parameters used for computations are
\displaystyle\textstyle L_{\mathrm{G}}=0.5\,\rm{nH}
and
\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{p}}=1.0\,\rm{pF}
.
Thick solid parts of lines indicate that the shallow well of the JPM potential hosts only two levels, while the thin dashed parts correspond to more than two levels existing in the shallow well.

On the photon capture stage, for providing the fastest excitation exchange, the JPM frequency is tuned close to the resonator frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle|\Delta_{\mathrm{p}}|\ll g
, where
\displaystyle\textstyle\Delta_{\mathrm{p}}=\omega_{\mathrm{p}}-\omega_{\mathrm{r}}
is the detuning between the frequencies of the JPM and the resonator.
Besides, for the circuit parameters we work with, one has
\displaystyle\textstyle\xi\gg g
.
Under these conditions, the JPM acts effectively as a two-level emitter (2LE) even when the shallow well accommodates more than two levels.
In this case, the nondipolar interaction described by the last term in Eq. (
15
) is strongly suppressed and this term can be neglected.
Thus, the Hamiltonian (
15
) reduces to the standard Hamiltonian of the Jaynes-Cummings model describing a mode of a resonator coupled to a single 2LE
Jaynes and Cummings 1963
;
Shore and Knight 1993
:

\frac{\hat{\mathcal{H}}}{\hbar}=\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}+\Delta_{\mathrm{p}})\hat{\sigma}_{11}+g(\hat{\sigma}_{10}\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{01}).

(16)

Here we introduced the 2LE operator
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\sigma}_{jj^{\prime}}\equiv|j\rangle\langle j^{\prime}|
, where
\displaystyle\textstyle j,j^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
, obeying the spin-
\displaystyle 1/2
commutator algebra
\displaystyle\textstyle\left[\hat{\sigma}_{jj^{\prime}},\hat{\sigma}_{ll^{\prime}}\right]=\hat{\sigma}_{jl^{\prime}}\delta_{j^{\prime}l}-\hat{\sigma}_{lj^{\prime}}\delta_{l^{\prime}j}
.

To proceed, it is convenient to move to a rotating frame via a transformation

\hat{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow\hat{R}(t)\hat{\mathcal{H}}\hat{R}^{\dagger}(t)+\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\partial\hat{R}(t)}{\partial t}\,\hat{R}^{\dagger}(t),

(17)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{R}(t)=\exp(-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}t\,\hat{N}_{\mathrm{ex}})
with
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{N}_{\mathrm{ex}}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\hat{\sigma}_{11}
being the operator of the total number of excitations in the system.
In the rotating frame, the Hamiltonian of the resonator mode-JPM system reads as

\frac{\hat{\mathcal{H}}}{\hbar}=\Delta_{\mathrm{p}}\hat{\sigma}_{11}+g(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{01}+\hat{\sigma}_{10}\hat{a}).

(18)

This is the Hamiltonian we use in what follows for describing the coupled resonator mode–JPM system on the photon capture stage.

Contrary to the resonant regime of the interaction between the JPM and the resonator mode on the capture stage, on the JPM readout stage the potential is tilted to a slightly asymmetric configuration
Opremcak et al. 2018
and the JPM transition
\displaystyle\textstyle\bar{\omega}_{\mathrm{p}}
frequency becomes strongly detuned from the resonator frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle|\bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{p}}|\gg g
, where
\displaystyle\textstyle\bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{p}}=\bar{\omega}_{\mathrm{p}}-\omega_{\mathrm{r}}
.
Thus, the excitation exchange between the resonator and the JPM is essentially suppressed on the readout stage.
The same holds also for the reset stage.
Thus, on these stages, we can treat the resonator mode and the JPM as decoupled subsystems.

III.2
Master equation

We describe the evolution of the system state
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\rho}(t)
by the master equation
Breuer and Petruccione 2002

\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}(t)=\hat{\mathcal{L}}(t)\hat{\rho}(t).

(19)

Here, assuming that the switching between the regimes of photon capture and the JPM readout occurs much faster than the durations of these regimes, we represent the time-dependent Liouvillian superoperator
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}(t)
as

\begin{split}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(t)=&\,\left[\Theta(t)-\Theta(t-t_{\mathrm{cpt}})\right]\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{cpt}}\\
&\,+\left[\Theta(t-t_{\mathrm{cpt}})-\Theta(t-t_{\mathrm{msr}})\right]\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{rdt}},\end{split}

(20)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle t_{\mathrm{msr}}=t_{\mathrm{cpt}}+t_{\mathrm{rdt}}+t_{\mathrm{rst}}
is the total duration of a single photodetection cycle comprised by the times of the photon capture
\displaystyle\textstyle t_{\mathrm{cpt}}
, readout
\displaystyle\textstyle t_{\mathrm{rdt}}
, and reset
\displaystyle\textstyle t_{\mathrm{rst}}
;
\displaystyle\textstyle\Theta(t)
denotes the Heaviside step-function.
The first term in the above expression describes the evolution of the system during the photon capture stage, while the second term corresponds to the JPM readout and reset stages.

The Liouvillian
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{cpt}}
has the Lindblad form
Manzano 2020
and reads, cf. Refs.
Govia et al. 2012
;
Sokolov and Wilhelm 2020
,

\begin{split}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{cpt}}\hat{\rho}=&\,-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\left[\hat{\mathcal{H}},\hat{\rho}\right]+\sum_{j=0,1}\gamma_{j}\hat{\mathcal{D}}\left[|\mathrm{c}\rangle\langle j|\right]\hat{\rho}\\
&\,+\Gamma_{10}\hat{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{\sigma}_{01}]\hat{\rho}+\Gamma_{11}\hat{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{\sigma}_{11}]\hat{\rho}+\kappa\hat{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{a}]\hat{\rho},\end{split}

(21)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{\bullet}]\hat{\rho}=\hat{\bullet}\hat{\rho}\hat{\bullet}^{\dagger}-(\hat{\bullet}^{\dagger}\hat{\bullet}\hat{\rho}+\hat{\rho}\hat{\bullet}^{\dagger}\hat{\bullet})/2
.
The first term in Eq. (
21
) describes the unitary quantum evolution during the photon capture stage described by the Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}
given by Eq. (
18
).
The second term describes the interwell tunneling of the JPM metastable states
\displaystyle\textstyle|0\rangle
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|1\rangle
from the shallow well to the deep well.
Here, similarly to Ref.
Govia et al. 2012
, we replaced the cascade of multiple states in the deep well with a single state
\displaystyle\textstyle|c\rangle
into which the metastable states
\displaystyle\textstyle|0\rangle
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|1\rangle
can irreversibly transit to.
We assume that the relaxation of the top levels in the deep well occurs much faster than the interwell tunneling.
That implies that once the particle tunneled from the shallow well, it does not tunnel back but rapidly dissipates its energy, eventually falling to the bottom level of the deep potential well.
Such a simplified description significantly reduces the Hilbert space of the JPM states, abating the numerical complexity of the problem while retaining the essential general features of quantum evolution of the considered system.
The third and fourth terms in Eq. (
21
) describe the processes of the JPM relaxation with rate
\displaystyle\textstyle\Gamma_{10}
and the pure dephasing with rate
\displaystyle\textstyle\Gamma_{11}
, respectively.
The last term corresponds to the resonator mode relaxation with rate
\displaystyle\textstyle\kappa
.

The tunneling rates of the metastable levels
\displaystyle\textstyle|0\rangle
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|1\rangle
are evaluated using the WKB approximation
Strauch 2004

\begin{split}\gamma_{j}=&\,\frac{\Omega_{j}}{j!\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{j+1/2}{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{j+1/2}\\
&\,\times\exp\left(-\frac{2}{\hbar}\int^{\phi_{2}}_{\phi_{1}}\mathrm{d}\phi\,\sqrt{2C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{p}}[E_{j}-U_{\mathrm{p}}(\phi)]}\right),\end{split}

(22)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\Omega_{j}=E_{j}/\hbar
with
\displaystyle\textstyle E_{j}
being the eigenenergy of the
\displaystyle j
-th level in the shallow well.
Figure
3
shows the dependence of the tunneling rates of the levels
\displaystyle|0\rangle
and
\displaystyle|1\rangle
on the external flux
\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{b}}
.
Calculations demonstrate that
\displaystyle\gamma_{1}/\gamma_{0}\gtrsim 10^{3}
for the typical JPM parameters we use.
A similar estimate was obtained, e.g., in Ref.
Shnyrkov et al. 2023
.

Figure 3:
Dependence of the tunneling rates
\displaystyle\textstyle\gamma_{1}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\gamma_{0}
on the external flux
\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{b}}
threading via the JPM.
Thick solid and thin dashed parts of lines have the same meaning as in Fig.

.
Computations are performed for
\displaystyle\textstyle I_{0}=1.2\,\rm{\mu A}
, while the rest of the circuit parameters are the same as in Fig.

.

As we mentioned earlier, on the readout and reset stages, the JPM transitions are effectively decoupled from the resonator mode due to strong detuning.
Since the JPM dynamics on these stages does not affect the resonator field and the detailed description of the JPM dynamics is not required, we can take partial trace with respect to the JPM states and consider the evolution of the resonator mode
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{r}}(t)
alone, where
\displaystyle\textstyle t_{\mathrm{cpt}}<t<t_{\mathrm{msr}}
.
Here
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{r}}(t)\equiv\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{p}}\left[\hat{\rho}(t)\right]
, where the subscript
\displaystyle\mathrm{p}
of the trace operator implies that only the JPM states are traced out.
The master equation governing the evolution of the state of the resonator mode reads as

\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{r}}(t)=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{rdt}}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{r}}(t),\quad t_{\mathrm{cpt}}<t<t_{\mathrm{msr}},

(23)

where the Lindbladian

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{rdt}}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{r}}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\left[\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}},\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{r}}\right]+\kappa\hat{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{a}]\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{r}}

(24)

describes the free evolution and the relaxation of the resonator mode.

The Lindblad master equation given by Eqs. (
19
)–(
21
), (
23
), and (
24
) is solved numerically using
QuantumOptics.jl
– Julia library offering versatile toolkit for simulating various quantum optics problems
Krämer et al. 2018
.
For simulations, we assume that at the beginning of the photodetection (we set this moment as
\displaystyle\textstyle t=0
) the state of the system
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\rho}(0)
is separable with the JPM residing in its lowest metastable state
\displaystyle|0\rangle
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\rho}(0)=\hat{\varrho}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|
with
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\varrho}
being the initial state of the resonator mode.

In our analysis, we neglect the effects of the thermal excitations on the system evolution.
The working temperatures of the circuit QED setups are usually lie in the range
\displaystyle\textstyle T_{\mathrm{sys}}\sim 10\textrm{---}30\,\rm{mK}
.
For the typical operational frequencies
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{\mathrm{sys}}/(2\pi)\sim 4\textrm{---}10\,\mathrm{GHz}
, assuming the Bose-Einstein distribution of the thermal excitations, one obtains the upper estimate for its average number
\displaystyle\textstyle n_{\mathrm{th}}\lesssim 10^{-2}
which justifies our approximation.

IV
Photocounting statistics

Having developed the model describing the resonator mode–JPM system, we proceed to considering the process of counting photons in the resonator mode.
Here we aim to determine the statistics of the photocounts obtained as a result of the repeated measurements of the resonator mode by the single JPM.
As mentioned in introduction, the photocounting statistics is a prominent example of quantum measurements.
Its outcome probabilities are described by Born’s rule
Born 1926
.
The photocounting distribution for the resonator mode in the state
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\varrho}
is given by

P(k)=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\varrho}\,\hat{\Pi}_{k}\},

(25)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\Pi}_{k}
is the POVM element corresponding to the outcome with
\displaystyle\textstyle k
clicks.
Thus, as it follows from Eq. (
25
), also known as the photocounting formula, calculation of photocounting statistics requires knowledge of the POVM.

The POVM of the JPM-based photocounter we study depends on the counting technique.
Here we consider two approaches to counting resonator photons.
The first approach is to count photons by performing a fixed number of measurement (photodetection) iterations,
\displaystyle\textstyle M
, regardless of the obtained results.
In the second approach, the measurement is iterated until either the first no-click event occurs or the maximum number of iterations,
\displaystyle\textstyle M
, is reached.
In both cases, the measurement outcome
\displaystyle k
is given by the total number of clicks.

The POVM is convenient to consider in the Fock-state basis,

\displaystyle\displaystyle P(k|n)=\vphantom{\langle}_{\mathrm{r}}\langle n|\hat{\Pi}_{k}|n\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}.

(26)

Here
\displaystyle P(k|n)
are diagonal matrix elements of the POVM elements, defining the probability of obtaining
\displaystyle\textstyle k
clicks given the
\displaystyle\textstyle n
-photon Fock state.
Importantly, since photocounting is a phase-insensitive measurement, the nondiagonal matrix elements of the POVM vanish,
\displaystyle\vphantom{\langle}{}_{\mathrm{r}}\langle n|\hat{\Pi}_{k}|m\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}=0
for
\displaystyle\textstyle n\neq m
.
On the other hand, the spectral decomposition theorem yields

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{k}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{+\infty}P(k|n)\left|n\right\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}\!\left\langle n\right|.

(27)

This implies that the POVM in Eq. (
25
) is uniquely defined by its diagonal matrix elements.

IV.1
Statistics of subsequent events

Our initial objective is to determine the probability of obtaining a series of measurement outcomes
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{M}=\{i_{M},\ldots,i_{1}\}
given an
\displaystyle n
-photon Fock state.
Each event within this sequence,
\displaystyle l=1,\ldots,M
, can have a binary result:
\displaystyle i_{l}=0
and
\displaystyle i_{l}=1
, corresponding to “no-click” and “click”, respectively.
The entire measurement procedure could be explained by Bernoulli’s process, if all events were independent and described by the same binary probability distribution.
In such a scenario, the two measurement techniques mentioned in the preamble of this section would be described by binomial and geometric distributions, respectively.
However, in our case, every event modifies the state of the resonator mode and, consequently, their probabilities are not independent.

Let us define the probability
\displaystyle\textstyle W(m,i|n)
to get
\displaystyle m
photons in the resonator mode and the measurement result
\displaystyle i=0,1
given
\displaystyle n
photons before the iteration event.
In the sequence of measurements, the result of the subsequent event depends solely on the number of photons that remain after the previous measurement event, and not on any prior states of the resonator mode.
This indicates a typical Markov process.
Thus, we can calculate the joint probabilities to obtain the outcome sequence
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
and the sequence of remaining photon numbers
\displaystyle\{m_{M},\ldots,m_{1}\}
.
Averaging this probability with respect to the latter results in an expression for the probability of obtaining a series of measurement outcomes,
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{M}
, from the
\displaystyle n
-photon Fock state at the beginning,

P(\mathcal{I}_{M}|n)=\sum_{m_{M}=0}^{+\infty}\cdots\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{+\infty}\prod_{l=1}^{M}W(m_{l},i_{l}|m_{l-1}),

(28)

where we set
\displaystyle m_{0}=n
.

Using Bayes’ theorem, we can decompose the probabilities in the right-hand side of Eq. (
28
),

W(m_{l},i_{l}|m_{l-1})=W(i_{l}|m_{l-1})W(m_{l}|i_{l},m_{l-1}).

(29)

Here
\displaystyle W(i_{l}|m_{l-1})
is the probability to get the measurement result
\displaystyle i_{l}
given
\displaystyle m_{l-1}
photons before the iteration event and
\displaystyle W(m_{l}|i_{l},m_{l-1})
is the probability that the state of the resonator mode contains
\displaystyle m_{l}
photons given the measurement result
\displaystyle i_{l}
and also
\displaystyle m_{l-1}
photons before the iteration event.
Both these probabilities can be calculated with the model considered above.

IV.2
Positive operator-valued measures

The probabilities
\displaystyle P(\mathcal{I}_{M}|n)
enable us to obtain the POVM in the Fock basis.
The first technique, which involves counting the total number of click events in the sequence
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
, yields a probability distribution of clicks that can be considered a generalization of the binomial distribution.
In this scenario, the POVM in the Fock basis is given by

P_{\mathrm{b}}(k|n)=\sum_{\mathcal{I}_{M}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{k}}P(\mathcal{I}_{M}|n).

(30)

Here

\displaystyle\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{k}=\left\{\mathcal{I}_{M}\left|\sum\limits_{l=1}^{M}i_{l}=k\right.\right\}.

(31)

is the set of all measurement sequences with
\displaystyle k
click events.
We refer to this counting technique as generalized binomial.

The second technique involves counting clicks until the first no-click event or reaching the end of the sequence.
This yields the probability distributions of clicks, which is a generalization of the geometric distribution.
Thus, the POVM in the Fock representation for this scenario reads

P_{\mathrm{g}}(k|n)=P(\mathcal{I}_{k}^{(1)}|n).

(32)

Here

\mathcal{I}_{k}^{(1)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{i_{k+1}=0,i_{k}=1,\ldots,i_{1}=1\},&k<M\\
\{i_{M}=1,\ldots,i_{1}=1\},&k=M\end{array}\right.

(33)

is the sequence of measurement outcomes with
\displaystyle k
consecutive click events.
This counting technique will be referred to as generalized geometric.

IV.3
Click probability

As mentioned, the probabilities
\displaystyle W(i|n)
in Eq. (
29
) can be calculated with the model considered above.
In this context, we first note that
\displaystyle W(0|n)=1-W(1|n)
.
Hence, it is sufficient to determine only the probability
\displaystyle W(1|n)
to get a click given
\displaystyle n
photons.

The probability
\displaystyle\textstyle W(1|n)
of the JPM delivering a click is given by

W(1|n)=\beta\mathcal{P}_{c}(n;t_{\mathrm{cpt}}).

(34)

Here
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{P}_{c}(n;t)=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}(n;t)|c\rangle\langle c|\right]
stands for the population of the state
\displaystyle\textstyle|c\rangle
at an instant
\displaystyle\textstyle t
for the resonator mode initially in the
\displaystyle n
-photon Fock state.
Here we introduced a notation
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\rho}(n;t)
for the system density operator at a moment of time
\displaystyle t
given that at the beginning of the photon capture stage the resonator mode is in an
\displaystyle n
-photon Fock state
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\varrho}=|n\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}\langle n|
.
The phenomenological parameter
\displaystyle\textstyle 0\leq\beta\leq 1
introduced in Eq. (
34
) models the efficiency of the JPM readout procedure outlined in Sec.
II
.
As we mentioned therein, the efficiency of the reflectometry-based JPM readout can exceed
\displaystyle 99.9\%
.

IV.4
Conditional photon-number distribution

Next, we determine the probability
\displaystyle W(m|i,n)
to get
\displaystyle m
photons given the measurement result
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle n
photons before the measurement iteration.
For this purpose, we need to determine the postmeasurement state
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\varrho}(i,n)
of the resonator mode, i.e., the state after obtaining the measurement result
\displaystyle i
under the Fock state with
\displaystyle n
photons.
This yields the probability to find
\displaystyle m
photons in the resonator mode given
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle n
,

W(m|i,n)=\vphantom{\langle}_{\mathrm{r}}\langle m|\hat{\varrho}(i,n)|m\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}.

(35)

Here partial averaging is taken over the Fock state of the resonator mode,
\displaystyle|m\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}
.

The resonator mode postmeasurement state
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\varrho}(i,n)
is determined as
Govia et al. 2012

\hat{\varrho}(i,n)=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{p}}[\hat{\rho}(n;t_{\mathrm{msr}})\,\hat{\pi}_{i}]}{W(i|n)},

(36)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\rho}(n;t_{\mathrm{msr}})
is the state of the system at the end of the measurement sequence given the initial
\displaystyle\textstyle n
-photon Fock state of the resonator mode.
As above, the subscript
p
of the trace operator indicates that the trace is taken over the JPM states only.
In Eq. (
36
)
\displaystyle,\textstyle\hat{\pi}_{i}
stands for operators in the space of the JPM states,

\hat{\pi}_{1}=\beta|c\rangle\langle c|,\quad\hat{\pi}_{0}=\hat{\mathbb{1}}-\hat{\pi}_{1},

(37)

where
\displaystyle i=0,1
denotes the measurement result.
In this case, the unity operator reads as
\displaystyle\textstyle\hat{\mathbb{1}}=|0\rangle\langle 0|+|1\rangle\langle 1|+|c\rangle\langle c|
.

V
Photon-number resolution

As mentioned above, our goal is to tune the parameters of the JPM detectors to make such counters as close as possible to the ideal PNR detectors.
Such an optimization requires a quantity (measure) that characterizes the ability of detectors to resolve between adjacent numbers of photons.
As discussed in Ref.
Len et al. 2022
, the probability
\displaystyle\textstyle P(n|n)
of obtaining
\displaystyle\textstyle n
clicks given
\displaystyle n
photons can be considered as a relevant characteristic of the photon-number resolution for a fixed photon number
\displaystyle n
.

We generalize this characteristic to define a measure of the photon-number resolution between
\displaystyle M
photons.
Let us assume that (i) the resonator mode does not contain more than
\displaystyle M
photons and (ii) our
a priori
information about the photon number is minimal for
\displaystyle n=0,\ldots,M
.
In other words, this means that the prior photon-number distribution is uniform for these
\displaystyle M+1
values.
Therefore, the quantity

\mathcal{R}_{M}=\frac{1}{M+1}\sum_{n=0}^{M}P(n|n).

(38)

can be considered as the probability to get the correct value for the number of photons in the aforementioned domain, given the minimal
a priori
information and assuming that the state of the resonator mode is restricted by
\displaystyle M
photons.
Obviously, this quantity can be considered as a suitable measure of the photon-number resolution, which we will refer to as the photocounter resolution.
This measure takes values in the interval
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{M}\in[0,1]
.
For
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{M}=1
we get the ideal resolution between
\displaystyle M
photons, for
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{M}=0
it vanishes at all.
Note that even for PNR detectors with losses this measure is less than one.

VII
Nonclassicality of photocounting statistics

The aim of this section is to analyze the principal ability of the JPMs to serve as a playground for testing nonclassical properties of electromagnetic radiation in a microwave resonator.
Based on the results of the previous section, we anticipate that the considered JPM detector will provide sufficiently high quality photocounting statistics to solve this task efficiently.
It is also important to realize how test results for JPM detectors differ from results for the ideal PNR detectors.

Any measurement conducted under the coherent states or their statistical mixtures can be explained by classical electrodynamics; see, e.g., Refs.
Titulaer and Glauber 1965
;
Mandel 1986
;
Sperling and Walmsley 2018a
;
*sperling2018b
;
Sperling and Vogel 2020
;
Mandel and Wolf 1995
;
Vogel and Welsch 2006
;
Agarwal 2013
.
The density operator of any quantum state can be expanded by coherent states as

\hat{\varrho}=\int_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\alpha\,P(\alpha)|\alpha\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}\langle\alpha|,

(41)

where
\displaystyle P(\alpha)
is the Glauber-Sudarshan
\displaystyle P
function
Glauber 1963
;
Sudarshan 1963
.
Hence, nonclassical states are characterized by
\displaystyle P(\alpha)\ngeq 0
, which cannot be interpreted as probability distributions.

Photocounting measurements, even if they are ideal, do not provide complete information about the quantum state.
Consequently, any test with such measurements represents only a sufficient condition for nonclassicality of quantum states.
On the other hand, such tests address another important issue: can the given photocounting statistics be replicated with a statistical mixture of classical electromagnetic fields?
For instance, this question is addressed in the test of the sub-Poissonian statistics of photocounting based on the Mandel
\displaystyle Q
parameter
Mandel 1979
, its higher-order generalization
Agarwal and Tara 1992
, the Klyshko test
Klyshko 1996
and its generalization
Innocenti et al. 2022
, etc.
An issue with these tests is that they are formulated for ideal PNR detectors.
When applied directly to JPM detectors, even with high values of the photocounter resolution
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{M}
, they may lead to incorrect conclusions.
Other tests adapted for arrays of on-off detectors (see, for example, Refs.
Sperling et al. 2012b
;
Bartley et al. 2013
;
Sperling et al. 2013
) are not applicable in our case since they are based on a significantly different POVM.

The nonclassicality test adapted to arbitrary POVMs has been proposed in Refs.
Semenov and Klimov 2021
;
Kovtoniuk et al. 2023
;
Rivas and Luis 2009
.
This states that the photocounting statistics can be explained classically if and only if, for any
\displaystyle\lambda(n)
, the inequality

\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}\lambda(n)P(n)\leq\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}}\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}\lambda(n)\Pi(n|\alpha)

(42)

holds.
Therefore, if there exists such
\displaystyle\lambda(n)
that this inequality is violated, then photocounting statistics is nonclassical.
In Eq. (
42
)

\Pi(n|\alpha)=\vphantom{\langle}_{\mathrm{r}}\langle\alpha|\hat{\Pi}_{n}|\alpha\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}=\mathrm{e}^{-|\alpha|^{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{|\alpha|^{2k}}{k!}P(n|k)

(43)

is the
\displaystyle Q
symbol of the POVM, describing the probability of obtaining
\displaystyle n
clicks of the photocounter given the resonator mode prepared in the coherent state
\displaystyle\textstyle|\alpha\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}
.
Both sides of this inequality can be estimated experimentally. The left-hand side can be estimated with the tested nonclassical state, while the right-hand side can be estimated with a set of coherent states.
Hence, in experiments one should not even trust the POVM model to make a conclusion about nonclassicality of photocounting statistics.

The squeezed vacuum states described in Eq. (
40
) serve as archetype examples of nonclassical states, and they can be prepared well beyond the 3 dB limit of squeezing in circuit QED
Malnou et al. 2018
;
Dassonneville et al. 2021
;
Eickbusch et al. 2022
.
These states are used here as a test case.
Interestingly, the Mandel
\displaystyle Q
parameter associated with these states is positive.
However, other tests show a nonclassical character of the photocounting statistics for squeezed vacuum states; see, e.g., Refs.
Semenov and Klimov 2021
;
Kovtoniuk et al. 2023
.

We have considered the case of
\displaystyle M=3
.
Inequality (
42
) has been optimized numerically with respect to
\displaystyle\textstyle\lambda(n)
to find its maximum violation.
We define the violation as the difference of the left- and right-hand sides of inequality (
42
).
For the numerical optimization, we used the adaptive differential evolution algorithm
Zhang and Sanderson 2009
.
This means that for each quantum state (the squeezing parameter
\displaystyle r
for the considered example) we search for such a function
\displaystyle\lambda(n)
that the violation is maximized.
This procedure has been performed for both generalized binomial and geometric counting techniques, as well as for different values of the detector parameters.
We also applied this procedure to the ideal PNR detectors, considering only
\displaystyle n=0,\ldots,M{-}1
.
The results are shown in Fig.
9
.
Evidently, counting techniques with the JPM detectors can be efficiently considered to test nonclassicality of squeezed vacuum states.
Moreover, for small squeezing (
\displaystyle\lesssim 2
dB) the results for the JPMs are almost the same as for the ideal PNR detectors.
Therefore, for small squeezing parameters, JPMs can be used for nonclassicality tests as proper PNR detectors.
It is worth noting that the described nonclassicality test does not necessarily require optimization of the detector parameters and can be applied even with state-of-the-art devices.

VIII
Summary

In this paper, we have theoretically investigated the possibility of using repeated measurements of microwave electromagnetic radiation confined in a resonator mode by a JPM detector.
Our goal was to understand if this method can be used to obtain the maximum possible resolution between adjacent numbers of photons—a key feature of photocounters required for various fundamental and applied problems.
We have considered two counting techniques referred to as generalized binomial and geometric.
The first involves counting the number of detector clicks in a given range of the predetermined measurement sequence.
The second assumes counting up to either the first no-click event or the end of the measurement sequence.

Our theoretical methods involve three steps.
First, we have developed a description of a JPM photocounter coupled to a resonator mode with the Linblad master equation, which we then solve numerically.
Second, this solution has been applied to derive the POVMs for both counting techniques.
Third, the POVM is used in the proposed measure of photon-number resolution, which is then optimized by detector parameters.

We conclude that the currently available or near-term circuit QED devices can be used to achieve a high degree of photon-number resolution up to about seven photons.
Interestingly, the generalized geometric technique shows better results compared with the generalized binomial technique.
That could be explained by the fact that in the latter technique, we perform a fixed number of measurement iterations. This leads to a higher probability of obtaining erroneous results compared with the generalized geometric technique, where we stop counting once a no-click result is obtained. The erroneous result can arise due to photon loss in the JPM or the resonator mode or the superfluous tunneling from the lower metastable level of the JPM.

We have applied our theory to show that the JPM detectors can be used to experimentally solve a typical problem in quantum optics—testing nonclassicality of photocounting statistics.
Using the example of the squeezed vacuum state, it is shown that for relatively small squeezing parameters, the results are indistinguishable from those obtained with ideal PNR detectors.
Therefore, nonclassical properties of low-intensity radiation analyzed by JPM detectors can be assumed to be analyzed by the ideal PNR detectors with a high degree of confidence.
We hope that our theoretical results will be useful in fundamental and applied research requiring detectors with high photon-number resolution.
