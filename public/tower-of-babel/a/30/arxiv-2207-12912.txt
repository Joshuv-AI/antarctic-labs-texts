Phase Transition of Parabolic Ginzburg–Landau Equation with Potentials of High-Dimensional Wells
Yuning Liu

Abstract
. In this work, we study the co-dimensional one interface limit and geometric motions of parabolic Ginzburg–Landau systems with potentials of high-dimensional wells. The main result generalizes the one by Lin et al. (Comm. Pure Appl. Math., 65(6):833-888, 2012) to a dynamical case. In particular combining modulated energy methods and weak convergence methods, we derive the limiting harmonic heat flows in the inner and outer bulk regions segregated by the sharp interface, and a non-standard boundary condition for them. These results are valid provided that the initial datum of the system is well-prepared under natural energy assumptions.



1.
Introduction

In the work of Keller–Rubinstein–Sternberg
[
32
,
33
]
, they created a general gradient flow theory in the descriptions of fast reaction and slow diffusion, and established its relations to the mean curvature flow (MCF) and the harmonic heat flows into manifolds. These works involve some formal statements associated with the multiple components phase transitions with higher dimensional wells. Such statements are also refereed to as the
Keller–Rubinstein–Sternberg problem
. More precisely they investigated the vectorial Allen–Cahn equation (also called Ginzburg–Landau equation)

\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}=\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon^{-2}\partial F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}),

(1.1)

where
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t):\Omega\times(0,T)\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a mapping depending on a small parameter
\varepsilon>0
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded domain with
C^{1}
boundary. Here
F(\mathbf{u})
is a double equal-well potential with ground state being the disjoint union of two smooth closed submanifolds
\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\partial F(\mathbf{u})
is the differential of
F
at
\mathbf{u}
. The
Keller–Rubinstein–Sternberg problem
is concerned with the limiting behavior of
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon
tends to zero.
In the work of Lin–Pan–Wang
[
25
]
, they set up an analytic program to
rigorously justify the formally asymptotic analysis given in the aforementioned works of Keller–Rubinstein–Sternberg. To be more precise
they considered the minimizers of the Ginzburg–Landau functional

A_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}):=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})\right)\,dx

(1.2)

that satisfy well-prepared boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
. They established the co-dimensional one interface limit of (
1.2
), which essentially generalizes the
\Gamma
-convergence of Modica–Mortola
[
29
]

to vectorial cases (though they did not state their main theorems in such an abstract manner). More importantly, they showed that the limits of
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}
in the bulk regions correspond to minimizing harmonic maps into
\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}
, and they derived a non-standard boundary condition (also called the minimal pair condition, cf. (
1.19
) below) which services as a constraint on the limiting harmonic maps when restricted on the interface. Such a boundary condition is a new feature that arises due to minimization of surface tensions in vectorial cases. Note that such a condition holds trivially in the case of scalar Allen-Cahn equation.

In this work we shall try to generalize Lin–Pan–Wang
[
25
]
to the parabolic system (
1.1
) by proving the following statements: Firstly, for well-prepared initial datum, as
\varepsilon
tends to
0
, the solution gradients of (
1.1
) will undergo phase transitions
across a moving interface
\Sigma_{t}
that propagates according to (two-phase) MCF. Secondly, in the two bulk regions
\Omega_{t}^{\pm}
segregated by the interface
\Sigma_{t}
, the solutions will converge to harmonic heat flows mapping into
\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}
respectively. Finally, the one-sided traces of the limiting harmonic heat flows on
\Sigma_{t}
must satisfy the minimal pair condition firstly derived in
[
25
]
.

Our first result is a vectorial analogy of the co-dimensional one scaling limit of scalar parabolic Allen–Cahn equation to the MCF, i.e. the special case of (
1.1
) when
\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}
are two distinct points
a^{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
. There have been major progresses in the scalar case over the last thirty years, made under different frameworks. Here we mention two classes of results and leave the discussions of some others in the sequel. One is the convergence to a Brakke’s flow by Ilmanen
[
19
]
using a version of Huisken’s monotonicity formula together with tools from geometric measure theory. See also
[
6
,
18
,
37
,
31
,
30
,
34
]
and the references therein for further renovations. Despite of its energetic nature, a major difficulty of such an approach is the control of the so called
discrepancy measure
, and in every existing literature in this direction the method relies crucially on a version of Modica’s maximum principle
[
28
]
.
There have been attempts to generalize such a method to vectorial cases. However, it is not clear whether Modica’s maximum principle holds for elliptic system.
Another approach, which relies more on the parabolic comparison principle, is the global in time convergences to the viscosity solution of MCF. These are weak solutions to the MCF built independently by Chen–Giga-Goto
[
7
]
and Evans–Spruck
[
12
]
. Concerning the convergence of scalar Allen–Cahn equation to such solutions, we refer the readers to the work of Evans–Soner–Souganidis
[
11
]
, the work of Soner
[
35
]
and the references therein. These two approaches both give global in time (weak) convergences to weakly defined solutions of MCFs up to their life spans. However, as their technics involve parabolic maximum principle and comparison principle in one way or another, it is not clear how to use them to attack vectorial cases in general. It is worth mentioning that for radially symmetric initial datum and when
\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}
are two concentric circles, Bronsard–Stoth
[
4
]
obtain global in time convergences to MCF of planar circles.

To the best of our knowledge, there are mainly two approaches to rigorously justify the convergences of the vectorial Allen–Cahn equations, both assuming that the limiting interface propagation problem has a (local in time) classical solution. Compared with the aforementioned methods which lead to global in time (weak) convergences, they have quite different natures.
One of these methods is the asymptotical expansion technics developed by De Mottoni–Schatzman
[
9
]
and later by Alikakos–Bates–Chen
[
1
]
, which has been used recently in
[
13
,
14
]
for matrix-valued cases of (
1.1
).
In particular, Fei–Lin–Wang–Zhang
[
13
]
studied the case when
\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}=O^{\pm}(n)
, the
n
-dimensional orthogonal group. By inner-outer expansions together with a gluing procedure, such an approach reduces the convergence problem to a linear stability problem given that the limiting system (not merely the limiting interface motion) is strongly well-posed.
The major challenge of this approach is the analysis of the spectrum of the linearized operator at the
minimal orbits
(also called optimal profile) or their variants.
Indeed, one of the novelties of
[
13
]
is to devise the so called
quasi-minimal orbits
, overcoming the lack of
minimal orbits
in the bulk regions, and to derive the spectrum stability of the linearized operator at such orbits making use of the minimal pair condition.
Finally we refer Lin–Wang
[
26
]
for a general theory for the strong well-posedness of the limiting system.

Another approach, which also assumes a regular solution of the limiting interface motion but not the limiting harmonic heat flows, is the relative entropy method developed by Fischer–Laux–Simon
[
16
]
, motivated by Jerrard–Smets
[
20
]
and Fischer–Hensel
[
15
]
. A generalization to matrix–valued case has been done by Laux-Liu
[
22
]
to study the isotropic–nematic transition in Landau–De Gennes model of liquid crystals. More recently, in
[
27
]
the author used these methods, together with those developed by Lin–Wang
[
24
]
, to investigate the convergence problem of an anisotropic 2D Ginzburg–Landau model.

Now we introduce a minimum amount of terminologies necessary for stating the main result of this work.
Let

\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}\text{ be two disjoint smooth, closed, connected submanifold in }\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(1.3)

For technical purposes we assume
0\in\mathfrak{m}_{-}
.
Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be a smooth function with
\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}
being its double equal-wells:

\operatorname{Arg~min}F=\mathfrak{m}:=\mathfrak{m}_{+}\sqcup\mathfrak{m}_{-}.

(1.4)

We assume that
F(\mathbf{u})
only depends on the distance from
\mathbf{u}
to
\mathfrak{m}
. That is,

F(\mathbf{u})=f(\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}(\mathbf{u})),

(1.5)

where
\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u})
is the distance (see (
2.14
) below for the full definition), and
f(s)\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R}^{+})
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{cases}f(s)=s&\text{ if }0\leqslant s\leqslant\delta_{0}^{2},\\
f(s)=2\delta_{0}^{2}&\text{ if }s\geqslant 2\delta_{0}^{2}.\end{cases}

(1.6)

In (
1.6
)
\delta_{0}>0
is a small number so that the nearest-point projection
P_{\mathfrak{m}}
from
B_{2\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m})
, the
2\delta_{0}
-tubular neighborhood of
\mathfrak{m}
, to
\mathfrak{m}
is smooth.

We consider the following initial boundary value problems on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
C^{1}
boundary:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon^{-2}\partial F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle~\text{in}~\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.7a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{in}

\displaystyle~\text{in}~\Omega\times\{0\},

(1.7b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle~\text{on}~\partial\Omega\times(0,T).

(1.7c)

Here
\partial F(\mathbf{u})
is the gradient of
F(\mathbf{u})
, and
\mathbf{g}:\overline{\Omega}\mapsto\mathfrak{m}_{-}
is a given smooth mapping.
Our main result is concerned with the asymptotical behaviors of solutions to (
1.7
) for well-prepared initial datum. To give an analytic characterization of such initial datum, we need to set up the geometry of the interface motion. To this end, we assume that

\Sigma=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Sigma_{t}\times\{t\}~\text{is a smoothly evolving closed hypersurface in}~\Omega,

(1.8)

starting from a closed smooth surface
\Sigma_{0}\subset\Omega
. We denote by
\Omega^{\pm}_{t}
the domain segregated by
\Sigma_{t}
, and by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\Sigma}(x,t)\text{ the signed-distance from }x\text{ to the set }\Sigma_{t}\text{ taking positive values in }\Omega^{+}_{t},

(1.9)

and taking negative values in
\Omega^{-}_{t}=\Omega\backslash\overline{\Omega^{+}_{t}}
. In other words,

\Omega^{\pm}_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega\mid\mathrm{d}_{\Sigma}(x,t)\gtrless 0\}.

(1.10)

To avoid contact angle problems, we assume that
\Sigma
stays at least
4\delta_{0}
distant away from
\partial\Omega
.

Following
[
20
,
15
,
16
]
, we define the modulated energy (also called the relative entropy energy) by

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|\Sigma](t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}{F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t))}-\bm{\xi}\cdot\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\right)\,dx.

(1.11)

Here
\bm{\xi}
is an appropriate extension of the unit normal vector field of
\Sigma
(see (
2.27
) below), and
\psi_{\varepsilon}
is the
scalar function

\displaystyle\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,t):=\mathrm{d}_{F}\circ\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

(1.12)

with
\mathrm{d}_{F}
defined by (
2.17
) below. As we shall see later on, the integrand of (
1.11
) is non-negative, and enjoys several coercivity estimates including controls of
discrepancy
and calibration of the Ginzburg–Landau energy (
1.2
).
We also need the surface tension coefficient

c_{F}:=2\int_{0}^{\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2}}\sqrt{2f(\lambda^{2})}d\lambda,

(1.13)

where

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}

is the Euclidean distance between
\mathfrak{m}_{+}
and
\mathfrak{m}_{-}
,
and another modulated energy controlling the bulk errors:

B[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|\Sigma](t):=\int_{\Omega}\Big(c_{F}\chi-c_{F}+2(\psi_{\varepsilon}-c_{F})^{-}\Big)\eta\circ\mathrm{d}_{\Sigma}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\left(\psi_{\varepsilon}-c_{F}\right)^{+}|\eta\circ\mathrm{d}_{\Sigma}|\,dx.

(1.14)

In (
1.14
)
\chi(\cdot,t)=\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{t}^{+}}-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{t}^{-}}
and
g^{\pm}
denotes the positive/negative parts of a function
g
respectively, and
\eta
is an appropriate truncation of the identity function (cf. (
3.8
)). In particular,
(\eta\circ\mathrm{d}_{\Sigma})\chi\geqslant 0
holds in
\Omega
due to our convention on the signed-distance function, and thus the two integrands in (
1.14
) are both non-negative. We refer the readers to the proof of Theorem
3.2
below for more details on the positivity of (
1.14
).

The main result of this work is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that the family of hypersurfaces
\Sigma
(
1.8
) evolves by mean curvature flow during
[0,T]
. If the initial datum of (
1.7
) is well-prepared in the sense that

\varepsilon\|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{\infty}}+B[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|\Sigma](0)+E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|\Sigma](0)\leqslant C_{1}\varepsilon

(1.15)

for some constant
C_{1}
that is independent of
\varepsilon
, then there exists
C_{2}
independent of
\varepsilon
so that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|\Sigma](t)\leqslant C_{2}\varepsilon,

(1.16a)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}B[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|\Sigma](t)\leqslant C_{2}\varepsilon,

(1.16b)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\Omega}|\psi_{\varepsilon}-c_{F}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{t}^{+}}|\,dx\leqslant C_{2}\varepsilon^{1/2}.

(1.16c)

Moreover, for some subsequence
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
there holds

\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon_{k}}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\mathbf{u}^{\pm}~\text{weakly in}~L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{loc}(\Omega^{\pm}_{t})),

(1.17)

where
\mathbf{u}^{\pm}
are weak solutions to the harmonic heat flows into
\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}
respectively and

\mathbf{u}^{\pm}\in L^{\infty}\left(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega^{\pm}_{t};\mathfrak{m}_{\pm})\right),\quad\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}^{\pm}\in L^{2}\left(0,T;L^{2}_{loc}(\Omega^{\pm}_{t})\right).

(1.18)

Furthermore, for every
t\in(0,T)
,

|\mathbf{u}^{+}-\mathbf{u}^{-}|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(x,t)=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}\quad\text{ for }\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\text{- a.e. }x\in\Sigma_{t}.

(1.19)

A few comments are in order. Firstly, in (
1.15
) the
L^{\infty}
bound of the initial datum is used (together with (
1.6
)) to obtain an uniform in space-time
L^{\infty}
-bound of
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}
, i.e.

\|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))}\leqslant c_{0}

(1.20)

for some
\varepsilon
-independent constant
c_{0}
. Such an estimate, derived by applying the maximum principle to (
1.7a
), enables us to avoid several technical complications in the passage of the limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. Indeed, even in the case when
d=2
, severe difficulties arise in the anisotropic model considered in
[
27
]
where an estimate like (
1.20
) is not available.
Secondly, if we denote the second fundamental forms of
\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}
at points
\mathbf{p}^{\pm}
by
A^{\pm}(\mathbf{p}^{\pm})(\cdot,\cdot)
, respectively,
then the theorem above claims that the pair of mappings

\mathbf{u}^{\pm}(\cdot,t):\Omega^{\pm}_{t}\mapsto\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

(1.21)

satisfy the following system in the weak sense:

\left\{\begin{split}\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}^{\pm}-\Delta\mathbf{u}^{\pm}&=A^{\pm}(\mathbf{u}^{\pm})(\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\pm},\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\pm})&&~\text{in}~\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Omega^{\pm}_{t}\times\{t\},\\

\mathbf{u}^{-}&=\mathbf{g},&&~\text{on}~\partial\Omega.\end{split}\right.

(1.22)

The first equation in (
1.22
) says that
\mathbf{u}^{\pm}
are (weak) harmonic map heat flows from the moving domains

to the target manifolds
\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}
respectively. The second equation in (

) is referred to as the minimal pair boundary condition (cf.
[
25

26
]
), and in the last equation

is a prescribed smooth mapping.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Special function of bounded variation

Definition 2.1
.

We say that
\mathbf{u}\in BV(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a special function with bounded variation and we write
\mathbf{u}\in SBV(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, if the Cantor part of its distributional derivative
\nabla^{c}u
vanishes, i.e.

\nabla\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{a}\mathbf{u}\,\mathcal{L}^{d}+\left(\mathbf{u}^{+}-\mathbf{u}^{-}\right)\otimes\nu_{\mathbf{u}}~\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}J_{\mathbf{u}}

(2.1)

where
\nabla^{a}
denotes the absolutely continuous part of the distributional derivative (with respect to Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{d}
) and
J_{\mathbf{u}}
is the jump set of
\mathbf{u}
with measure theoretical outer normal vector
\nu_{\mathbf{u}}
.

The following two results will be used to obtain convergences up to the free boundary. We refer the readers to the monograph of Ambrosio–Fusco–Pallara
[
3
]
for the proofs.

Proposition 2.2
.

(Closure of

) Let

Let

be open and bounded, and let
\{\mathbf{u}_{k}\}\subset SBV(\Omega)
such that

\sup_{k}\int_{\Omega}\varphi\left(\left|\nabla^{a}\mathbf{u}_{k}\right|\right)\,dx+\sup_{k}\int_{J_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}}\left|\mathbf{u}_{k}^{+}-\mathbf{u}_{k}^{-}\right|\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}<\infty.

(2.2)

where

is the absolute continuous part of the distributional gradient

, and
(\mathbf{u}_{k}^{+},\mathbf{u}_{k}^{-})
are the approximate one-sided limits on the jump set

. If

weakly-star converges in

•

\mathbf{u}\in SBV(\Omega)
.

•

•

•

For any convex function

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varphi(|\nabla^{a}\mathbf{u}|)\,dx\leqslant\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}\varphi\left(\left|\nabla^{a}\mathbf{u}_{k}\right|\right)\,dx.

Proposition 2.3
.

(Compactness of
SBV
) Let
\varphi,\Omega

\left\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

2.2.
Minimal connections

We shall briefly describe some basic properties of minimal orbits.
For any
\mathbf{p}^{\pm}\in\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}
, we define their minimal connection

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{F}(\mathbf{p}^{+},\mathbf{p}^{-})

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2}|\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+F(\bm{\gamma}(t))\,dt\,\Big|\,\bm{\gamma}\in H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{n}),\bm{\gamma}(\pm\infty)=\mathbf{p}^{\pm}\in\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}\right\}.

(2.5)

\widetilde{F}(\lambda):=\begin{cases}f\left(\left(\tfrac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2}+\lambda\right)^{2}\right)&\text{ if }\lambda\leqslant 0,\\

c_{\widetilde{F}}:=\min\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|\gamma^{\prime}(s)\right|^{2}+\widetilde{F}(\gamma(s))\right)dt~\Big|~\gamma\in H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}),\gamma(\pm\infty)=\pm\tfrac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2}\right\}.

(2.7)

The following result was obtained in

,

]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

It holds that

c_{\widetilde{F}}:=2\int_{0}^{\tfrac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2}}\sqrt{2\widetilde{F}(\lambda)}d\lambda\quad=c_{F}\left(=2\int_{0}^{\tfrac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2}}\sqrt{2f(\lambda^{2})}d\lambda\right).

(2.8)

Moreover, there exists a minimizer
\alpha(s)

(2.9b)

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}(s)=\sqrt{2\widetilde{F}(\alpha(s))},\quad\forall~s\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.9c)

\displaystyle\left|\alpha^{\prime}(s)\right|+\left|\alpha(s)\mp\tfrac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2}\right|\leqslant Ce^{-C|s|}\quad\text{ as }s\rightarrow\pm\infty.

(2.10b)

2.3.
Quasi-distance function

We define the distance from
\mathbf{u}
to
\mathfrak{m}
according to its relative distance to its two components:

\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u})=\left\{\begin{split}\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{+}}(\mathbf{u})&\qquad\text{ when }\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{+}}(\mathbf{u})\leqslant\tfrac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2},\\
\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{-}}(\mathbf{u})&\qquad\text{ when }\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{-}}(\mathbf{u})\leqslant\tfrac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2}.\end{split}\right.

(2.14)

In general such a function is not
C^{1}
unless
\mathbf{u}
is close to but not in
\mathfrak{m}
(cf.
[
21
, Section 4.4]
). We assume that the nearest point projection
P_{\mathfrak{m}}:B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m})\mapsto\mathfrak{m}
is smooth. Using this, we have

\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u}):=|\mathbf{u}-P_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u})|,\quad\forall~\mathbf{u}\in B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m}).

(2.15)

Such a function is
C^{1}
in
B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m})\backslash\mathfrak{m}
, and this motivates the following unit vector field:

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u}):=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{\mathbf{u}-P_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u})}{|\mathbf{u}-P_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u})|},\quad&\forall~\mathbf{u}\in B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m})\backslash\mathfrak{m},\\
0\quad&\forall~\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{m}.\end{cases}

(2.16)

Note that such a normal vector field is in general not continuous up to
\mathfrak{m}
unless
\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}
are hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

With these preparations, we introduce the quasi-distance function

\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u}):=\left\{\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u})}\sqrt{2f(\lambda^{2})}\,d\lambda\quad&\text{ if }\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{-}}(\mathbf{u})\leqslant\tfrac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2},\\
\tfrac{1}{2}c_{F}\quad&\text{ if }\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u})>\tfrac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2},\\
c_{F}-\int_{0}^{\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u})}\sqrt{2f(\lambda^{2})}\,d\lambda\quad&\text{ if }\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{+}}(\mathbf{u})\leqslant\tfrac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2},\end{split}\right.

(2.17)

where
c_{F}=2\int_{0}^{\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2}}\sqrt{2f(\lambda^{2})}d\lambda
is the surface tension coefficient (cf. (
1.13
)).
Such a function is a modification of the one used in
[
36
,
17
]
. We list some of its important properties here.

Lemma 2.6
.

The function
\mathrm{d}_{F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto[0,c_{F}]
is Lipschitz continuous in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and satisfies

\displaystyle|\partial\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u})|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{2F(\mathbf{u})}\quad a.e.~\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}0\qquad\text{if and only if}&~\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{m}_{-},\\
c_{F}\qquad\text{if and only if}&~\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{m}_{+}.\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{F}

\displaystyle\in C^{1}(B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m})),\text{ and }\partial\mathrm{d}_{F}|_{B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m})}(\mathbf{u})=0~\text{if and only if }~\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{m}.

(2.21)

Moreover,
\frac{\partial\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u})}{|\partial\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u})|}
is a continuous unit vector field in
B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m})\backslash\mathfrak{m}
so that

\frac{\partial\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u})}{|\partial\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u})|}=\bm{\nu}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u})\qquad\forall\mathbf{u}\in B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m})\backslash\mathfrak{m}.

(2.22)

Proof.

It is obvious that
\mathrm{d}_{F}
is continuous in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and is Lipschitz in each subdomain where it is defined. It suffices to check the Lipschitz condition across adjacent regions. If
\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})>\tfrac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2}
and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{-}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})\leqslant\tfrac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2}
, then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u}_{1})-\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u}_{2})=\int_{\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{-}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})}^{\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathfrak{m}}}{2}}\sqrt{2f(\lambda^{2})}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{-}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})}^{\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{-}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})}\sqrt{2f(\lambda^{2})}\,d\lambda\leqslant C|\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{-}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})-\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{-}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})|\leqslant C|\mathbf{u}_{2}-\mathbf{u}_{1}|.

Other cases can be treated in a similar way. The inequality (
2.18
) and the formula (
2.6
) follows directly from the definition (
2.17
).

To show
\mathrm{d}_{F}\in C^{1}(B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m}))
, it suffices to write it as a function of
\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}
which is smooth up to
\mathfrak{m}
. Indeed, by (
1.6
) one can verify that

h(s)=\int_{0}^{s^{1/2}}\sqrt{2f(\lambda^{2})}\,d\lambda\in C^{1}[0,\infty).

(2.23)

As a result,
\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u})=h(\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}^{2})
in
B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m}_{-})
, and a similar argument applies to
B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m}_{+})
. The rest assertions are due to
\mathrm{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}\in C^{1}(B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathfrak{m})\backslash\mathfrak{m})
.

∎

2.5.
Modulated energy method

As the gradient flow of the Ginzburg–Landau energy (
1.2
), the system
(
1.7a
) has the following energy dissipation law

A_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,T))+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon|\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx\,dt=A_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,0)),~\text{for all}~T>0.

(2.35)

For initial datum undergoing a phase transition across the initial interface
\Sigma_{0}
, due to concentrations of
\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}
on
\Sigma_{t}
, the dissipation law (
2.35
) is not sufficient to derive quantitative convergences of
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}
, not even away from
\Sigma_{t}
. Following a recent work of Fisher–Laux-Simon
[
16
]
we shall derive an inequality which modulates the concentrations and leads to compactness of
\{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\}
in Sobolev spaces.

We shall start by discussing the differentiability of
\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\mathrm{d}_{F}\circ\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
(cf. (
1.12
)).
It follows from Lemma
2.6
that
\mathrm{d}_{F}(\cdot)
is a Lipschitz function in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathrm{d}_{F}(0)=0
under the assumption that
0\in\mathfrak{m}^{-}
. Following Laux–Simon
[
23
]
, for every
(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]
, we consider the restriction of
\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t))
to the affine space

\displaystyle T^{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}_{x,t}:=\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)+{\rm span}\{\partial_{0}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t),\cdots,\partial_{d}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\},

denoted by
\mathrm{d}_{F}|_{T^{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}_{x,t}}
. By the generalized chain rule of Ambrosio–Dal Maso
[
2
]
,
\mathrm{d}_{F}|_{T^{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}_{x,t}}

is differentiable at
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
. Now we denote the orthogonal projection from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the subspace
T^{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}_{x,t}-\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
by
\Pi_{x,t}^{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}
, and define the generalized differential by

\partial\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}):=\partial\left(\mathrm{d}_{F}|_{T^{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}_{x,t}}\right)\Big|_{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)}\circ\Pi^{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}_{x,t}.

(2.36)

Then we have for
0\leqslant i\leqslant d
that

\partial\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})\cdot\partial_{i}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}=\partial\left(\mathrm{d}_{F}|_{T^{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}_{x,t}}\right)\Big|_{\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)}\cdot\partial_{i}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}=\partial_{i}(\mathrm{d}_{F}\circ\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})

(2.37)

where the second equality is due to the directional derivative at
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
pointing to
\partial_{i}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
.
This proves the generalized chain rule

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\partial_{i}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\cdot\partial\mathrm{d}_{F}\left(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\right)\quad\text{for }0\leqslant i\leqslant d\text{ and a.e. }(x,t).

(2.38)

Moreover, the point-wise differential inequality (
2.18
) is valid for the generalized differential (
2.36
):

|\partial\mathrm{d}_{F}(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})|\leqslant\sqrt{2F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})}.

(2.39)

Note that the above inequality, rather than its classical version (
2.18
), will be used to prove the modulated energy inequalities.

To proceed, we define the phase-field analogues of the normal vector and the mean curvature vector respectively by

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\frac{\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}}{|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|}(x,t)&\text{ if }\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\neq 0,\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2.40a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}-\left(\varepsilon\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\partial F(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})\right)\cdot\frac{\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}{\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|}&\text{ if }\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\neq 0,\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2.40b)

Note that in (
2.40b
), the inner product is made with the column vectors of

.

(\mathbf{u}_{+},\mathbf{u}_{-})

\displaystyle~+\mathrm{d}_{F}^{*}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{u}_{+},\tfrac{1}{2}c_{F}),\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{u}_{-},\tfrac{1}{2}c_{F})\right)
