Linear Quivers at Large- N N
Carlos Nunez, Leonardo Santilli, Konstantin Zarembo

Abstract
Quiver theories constitute an important class of supersymmetric gauge theories with well-defined holographic duals. Motivated by holographic duality, we use localisation on 𝕊 d \mathbb{S}^{d} to study long linear quivers at large- N N . The large- N N solution shows a remarkable degree of universality across dimensions, including d = 4 d=4 where quivers are genuinely superconformal. In that case we upgrade the solution of long quivers to quivers of any length.

Four-dimensional supersymmetric gauge theories with eight supercharges (
\mathcal{N}=2

The amount of supersymmetry allows to extract a wealth of exact results, without forcing a rigid structure and thus including a wide variety of phenomena. Renown examples of these powerful features are Seiberg–Witten theory

Embedding these quantum field theories in M-theory or Type II string theory yields a top-down perspective on them

Building on

Supersymmetric localisation

Long quiver theories are not exclusive to four dimensions: SCFTs with eight supercharges that correspond to conformal fixed points of linear quiver gauge theories exist in all possible dimensions (

The gauge theory is conveniently encoded in a quiver. More precisely, the gauge and matter content of a theory with eight supercharges is encoded in the representation of a framed doubled quiver, in which the nodes indicate gauge groups, the framing (drawn as square nodes) indicate the flavour groups, and unoriented edges (which correspond to pairs of edges with opposite orientation) indicate hypermultiplet in the bifundamental representation.

Definition 2.1
.

A quiver gauge theory with eight supercharges is said to be

There exist many more SCFTs which do not have a Lagrangian description, and in particular they are not quiver gauge theories. In this work we only consider Lagrangian theories.

Statement 2.4
.

Let

. The long quiver limit of a balanced linear quiver is governed by a function

\varrho\ :\ [0,1]\times\R\ \to\ \R_{\geq 0}

that solves the Poisson equation

\frac{1}{4}\partial_{x}^{2}\varrho(z,x)+\partial_{z}^{2}\varrho(z,x)-\partial_{z}^{2}\nu(z)\delta(x)=0,

for
0<z<1
and

.

To prepare the stage, we introduce the eigenvalue densities

\rho_{j}(\phi):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{j}}\delta\left(\phi-\phi^{(j)}_{a}\right),\qquad\phi\in\R.

Note that they carry an overall factor
1/N
, as opposed to the customary

. Hence, they are not canonically normalised to 1. We can collect all the eigenvalue densities for each gauge factor in the function of two variables
\rho(z,\phi)
with
\rho\left(\frac{j}{P},\phi\right)=\rho_{j}(\phi)
. This new eigenvalue density is normalised as

\int_{\R}\mathrm{d}\phi\rho(z,\phi)=\nu(z)\qquad\forall\penalty\ 0<z<1.

In the latter expressions
\phi\in\R
is a one-dimensional integration variable, related to but different from the
\mathrm{rk}(G)
-dimensional vector
\vec{\phi}
.

With these definitions, the matrix model effective action in (
2.5
) is written as a functional of
\rho

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{eff}}[\rho]=N^{2}P\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}z\int\mathrm{d}\phi\rho(z,\phi)

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{u_{d}}{2t_{d}(z)}\phi^{2}+\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\mathrm{d}\sigma\rho(z,\sigma)f_{\mathrm{v}}(\phi-\sigma)\right.

(2.15)

\displaystyle\left.+\zeta(z)f_{\mathrm{h}}(\phi)+\int\mathrm{d}\sigma\rho(z+\delta z,\sigma)f_{\mathrm{h}}(\phi-\sigma)\right\}+\mathfrak{B}.

Replacing each sum over

with an integral over

yields an overall factor of

comes from replacing the sum over

with an integral over

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int

We use the trivial identity
\delta_{j,P-1}=\delta_{j+1,P}

3.1

Before delving into the study of the partition function of the gauge theory on

In these expressions,

Statement 3.1

Proof.

In the linear quivers we are considering, the number of vector multiplet and hypermultiplet modes are given by

\sum_{j=1}^{P-2}N_{j}N_{j+1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}N_{j}\left(N_{j+1}+N_{j-1}\right),\qquad N_{0}:=0=:N_{P}.

Imposing the balancing condition (

n_{\rm v}=\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}\left(N_{j}^{2}-1\right),\qquad n_{\rm h}=\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}N_{j}\left(2N_{j}-\frac{1}{2}N_{j-1}-\frac{1}{2}N_{j+1}\right).

\displaystyle 48a

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}\left[12N_{j}^{2}-N_{j}\left(N_{j+1}-2N_{j}+N_{j-1}\right)\right]-10(P-1),

\displaystyle 48c

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}\left[12N_{j}^{2}-2N_{j}\left(N_{j+1}-2N_{j}+N_{j-1}\right)\right]-8(P-1).

We now take the long quiver limit of these expressions, following the notation of Section
2

\displaystyle 48a

\displaystyle=N^{2}P\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}z\penalty\ \left[12\nu(z)^{2}-\frac{1}{P^{2}}\nu(z)\partial_{z}^{2}\nu(z)\right],

\displaystyle 48c

\displaystyle=N^{2}P\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}z\penalty\ \left[12\nu(z)^{2}-\frac{2}{P^{2}}\nu(z)\partial_{z}^{2}\nu(z)\right].

Construct the matrix of derivatives:

3.6
Solution at strong coupling

We wish to go beyond the computation of the coefficient
a
and extract more interesting behaviour from the sphere free energy. To do so, we consider the strong ’t Hooft coupling limit
t_{4}(z)\to\infty
. This is the regime in which we expect a more precise match with supergravity, beyond the purely kinematical anomaly coefficient
a
.

In the strong coupling limit, (
3.5
) trivialises. This indicates that the ansatz that
\phi
does not scale with
P
misses all the information at strong ’t Hooft coupling. We therefore abandon the no-scaling ansatz, and plug the long quiver scaling ansatz (
2.19
) into (
3.4
).

The saddle point equation at strong coupling reads

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\mathrm{d}\sigma\frac{\rho(z,\sigma)}{\phi-\sigma}+\frac{1}{2P^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathscr{K}(\phi-\sigma)\partial_{z}^{2}\rho(z,\sigma)+\frac{1}{2P^{2}}[P^{2}\zeta(z)]\mathscr{K}(\phi)=0.

(3.10)

For large argument,

\mathscr{K}(\phi)\approx 2\phi\ln\lvert\phi\rvert-2\phi+\frac{1}{6\phi},

and therefore, replacing
\phi
with
2P^{\chi}x
as prescribed by (
2.19
), we have

\frac{1}{2P^{2}}\mathscr{K}(\phi)\approx 2\chi P^{\chi-2}\ln(P)x+2P^{\chi-2}\left(x\ln\lvert 2x\rvert-x\right)+\frac{1}{24x}P^{-\chi-2}.

(3.11)

With the substitutions (
2.19
)-(
3.11
), eq. (
3.10
) becomes

\displaystyle 0=2\chi P^{\chi-2}\ln(P)

\displaystyle\left[\int\mathrm{d}y\partial_{z}^{2}\varrho(z,y)(x-y)+[P^{2}\zeta(z)]x\right]

\displaystyle+2P^{\chi-2}

\displaystyle\left[\int\mathrm{d}y\partial_{z}^{2}\varrho(z,y)(x-y)\left(\ln\lvert 2x-2y\rvert-1\right)+[P^{2}\zeta(z)]x\left(\ln\lvert 2x\rvert-1\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}P^{-\chi}

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\mathrm{d}y\frac{\varrho(z,y)}{x-y}+\mathcal{O}(P^{-\chi-2}).

The first line vanishes as a consequence of the balancing condition (
2.10
). The shifts by
\ln(2)-1
in the second line cancel for the same reason. To have competition between the terms in the second and third line, so to reach a non-trivial saddle point configuration, the matrix model requires
\chi-2=-\chi
. We thus recover in
d=4
the value
\chi=1
, that we have found in Section
2

\forall\penalty\ d\in\R_{\geq 3}\setminus 2\Z
.

We arrive at

\frac{1}{4}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\mathrm{d}y\frac{\varrho(z,y)}{x-y}+\int\mathrm{d}y(x-y)\ln\lvert x-y\rvert\partial_{z}^{2}\varrho(z,y)+[P^{2}\zeta(z)]x\ln\lvert x\rvert=0.

We now use the trick of writing

\frac{1}{x-y}=\partial_{y}^{2}\left((x-y)\ln\lvert x-y\rvert\right)

and integrate twice by parts,

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\mathrm{d}y\frac{\varrho(z,y)}{x-y}=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\mathrm{d}y(x-y)\ln\lvert x-y\rvert\partial_{y}^{2}\varrho(z,y).

Besides, we use the trivial equalities

\displaystyle x\ln\lvert x\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\mathrm{d}y(x-y)\ln\lvert x-y\rvert\delta(y),

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}y(x-y)\ln\lvert x-y\rvert f(y)

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\mathrm{d}y(x-y)\ln\lvert x-y\rvert f(y),

with the second identity valid for every smooth function
f:\R\to\R
. The saddle point equation is thus recast in the form

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\mathrm{d}y(x-y)\ln\lvert x-y\rvert\penalty\ \left[\frac{1}{4}\partial_{y}^{2}\varrho(z,y)+\partial_{z}^{2}\varrho(z,y)+[P^{2}\zeta(z)]\delta(y)\right]=0,

(3.12)

that must hold
\forall\penalty\ x\in\R
and
0<z<1
. Therefore, the solution to (
3.12
) satisfies the Poisson equation

\frac{1}{4}\partial_{x}^{2}\varrho(z,x)+\partial_{z}^{2}\varrho(z,x)+P^{2}\zeta(z)\delta(x)=0.

This exact same equation was already found in (
2.24
) for

, and we have derived it directly in

at strong coupling.

Digression.

The effect for which, at large-

\mathcal{N}=4

The partition function of
\mathcal{N}=4

is the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble. In the planar limit with ’t Hooft coupling

(3.13)

However, one may also take the limit with fixed gauge coupling

∎

3.7
Free energy in four-dimensional long quivers

Statement 3.4
.

In the long quiver limit and at infinite ’t Hooft coupling,

-\ln\mathcal{Z}_{\cs^{4}}\simeq\frac{1}{2}P\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}R_{k}^{2}\,\ln\frac{\pi k\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{\text{\tiny EM}}}}{P}+\mathcal{O}(\ln P).

(3.14)

Proof.

We compute

-\ln\mathcal{Z}_{\cs^{4}}\simeq\left.S_{\mathrm{eff}}\right\rvert_{\text{on-shell}}.

The on-shell effective action is given by

\displaystyle\left.S_{\mathrm{eff}}\right\rvert_{\text{on-shell}}=\frac{1}{2}PN^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}z\int\mathrm{d}x\varrho(z,x)

\displaystyle\left[\zeta(z)\ln H(2Px)-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\mathrm{d}y\varrho(z,y)\ln\lvert 2P(x-y)\rvert\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{1}{8P^{2}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\mathrm{d}y\varrho(z,y)\partial_{y}^{2}\left(\ln\lvert H(2P(x-y))\rvert\right)\right],

where we have used the fact that
\varrho(z,y)
solves the saddle point equation to manipulate the expression.
At large values of the argument,

\ln H(\phi)\simeq-\frac{\phi^{2}}{2}\left(\ln\phi^{2}-3\right),

and
\partial^{2}_{\phi}\ln H(\phi)\simeq-\ln\phi^{2}
. We thus find cancellations between the double integrals in
S_{\mathrm{eff}}
.
The remaining single integral can be computed with the help of the Fourier series (
2.13
) and (
2.25
):

\left.S_{\mathrm{eff}}\right\rvert_{\text{on-shell}}=-\frac{\pi^{3}P}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{3}R_{k}^{2}\int_{\R}\mathrm{d}x\,x^{2}\left[\ln\left(2Px\right)^{2}-3\right]\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi k|x|}.

An integration over
x
gives (
3.14
).

Before concluding, let us stress that, using
a\propto P\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}R_{k}^{2}
, the
\mathcal{O}(P)
constant in
\ln\mathcal{Z}_{\cs^{4}}
is scheme-dependent.
Recall from (
2.28
) that the partition function on
\cs^{4}
contains a UV divergence controlled by the central charge:
\ln\mathcal{Z}_{\cs^{4}}=-4a\ln(\Lambda r)+{\rm finite}
, for
r
the radius of
\cs^{4}
and
\Lambda
the UV cutoff. Changing renormalisation scheme shifts the free energy by
\,{\rm const}\,P\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}R_{k}^{2}
, effectively multiplying the the argument of the logarithm in (
3.14
) by a constant. The constant we computed (
\pi\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{\text{\tiny EM}}}
) corresponds to a particular scheme used in
[
22
]
and in practice has no particular meaning. On the other hand, the dependence on
P
and on the mode number
k
, does not change with the scheme and can be faithfully compared to supergravity, which we will do later on in Subsection
3.9
.

∎

3.8
Four-dimensional linear quivers of arbitrary length

It is possible to solve the planar limit of any linear
d=4

\mathcal{N}=2
quiver for arbitrary
P
. We omit the details of the derivation of the saddle point equation, since they are conceptually analogous to what we have done so far, and only highlight the differences.

•

The discrete index
j
labelling the nodes of the quiver is not replaced by a parameter
z=j/P
.

•

The gauge ranks
N_{j}
scaled into rational numbers
\left\{\nu_{j}\right\}_{j},

\nu_{j}=N_{j}/N
are not collected into a function
\nu(z)
.

•

Likewise, the Veneziano parameters
\left\{\zeta_{j}\right\}
are not collected into a function
\zeta(z)
. In the planar limit,
\zeta_{j}
are
\mathcal{O}(1)
.

•

The eigenvalue densities are normalised as

\int\mathrm{d}\phi\rho_{j}(\phi)=\nu_{j},\qquad\forall j=1,\dots,P-1.

•

Related to the above, being
P
finite, the contribution from the bifundamental hypermultiplets is a difference

\frac{1}{2}\left[\rho_{j+1}-2\rho_{j}+\rho_{j-1}\right],

and does not produce a differential
\partial_{z}^{2}
.

•

To encode the above fact, we define the
(P-1)\times(P-1)
matrix

\mathsf{M}:=\left(\begin{matrix}2&-1&&&&&\\
-1&2&-1&&&&\\
&-1&2&-1&&&&\\
&&&&\ddots&&&\\
&&&&-1&2&-1\\
&&&&&-1&2\\
\end{matrix}\right)

That is,
\mathsf{M}
is the Cartan matrix of the
A_{P-1}
Lie algebra, which equals twice the unit matrix minus the adjacency matrix of the linear quiver.

•

The balancing condition is written in matrix form as
K_{j}=\mathsf{M}_{ji}N_{i}
, or more compactly

\vec{K}=\mathsf{M}\vec{N}.

•

As a notational reminder, the indices
i,j\in\left\{1,\dots,P-1\right\}
will indicate the nodes of the quiver. Moreover, sum over repeated indices will be understood to lighten the formulae. The imaginary unit is denoted
\mathtt{i}=\sqrt{-1}
to avoid confusion with the index
i
.

To diagonalise the Cartan matrix we introduce its eigenvectors
\vec{v}^{(p)}=(v_{1}^{(p)},\cdots,v_{P-1}^{(p)})
,
p=1,\dots,P-1
. To be more explicit, the components are

v_{j}^{(p)}=\sin\frac{\pi pj}{P},\qquad j=1,\dots,P-1,

(3.15)

and, after elementary trigonometric manipulations, the corresponding eigenvalues are

\mathsf{M}_{ji}v_{i}^{(p)}=\left(2\sin\frac{\pi p}{2P}\right)^{2}v_{j}^{(p)}

or, in matrix notation,

\mathsf{M}\vec{v}^{(p)}=\left(2\sin\frac{\pi p}{2P}\right)^{2}\vec{v}^{(p)},\qquad\forall p=1,\dots,P-1.

These vectors form a basis that satisfies orthogonality and completeness conditions

\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}v_{j}^{(p)}v_{j}^{(p^{\prime})}=\frac{P}{2}\,\delta^{pp^{\prime}},\qquad\sum_{p=1}^{P-1}v_{j}^{(p)}v_{i}^{(p)}=\frac{P}{2}\,\delta_{ji}.

(3.16)

We can express the vector of gauge ranks
\vec{N}
in this basis:

\vec{N}=\sum_{p=1}^{P-1}\tilde{N}_{p}\vec{v}^{(p)},

which in components reads

N_{j}=\sum_{p=1}^{P-1}\tilde{N}_{p}\sin\frac{\pi pj}{P}\,.

That is to say,
\vec{N}
has components
N_{i}
in the canonical basis
\left\{\vec{e}^{(i)}\right\}_{i=1,\dots,P-1}
with
e^{(i)}_{j}=\delta_{ji}
, and it has components
\tilde{N}_{p}
in the eigenbasis of
\mathsf{M}

\left\{\vec{v}^{(p)}\right\}_{p=1,\dots,P-1}
. The balancing condition (
2.4
) results in

K_{j}=\sum_{p=1}^{P-1}\tilde{N}_{p}\left(2\sin\frac{\pi p}{2P}\right)^{2}\sin\frac{\pi pj}{P}.

(3.17)

Statement 3.5

Consider a balanced linear quiver in
d=4

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int\mathrm{d}\sigma\frac{\rho_{j}(\sigma)}{\phi-\sigma}-\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{M}_{ji}\int\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathscr{K}(\phi-\sigma)\rho_{i}(\sigma)+\frac{\zeta_{j}}{2}\mathscr{K}(\phi)=0,

We take the Fourier transform of (

\tilde{\rho}_{j}(\omega)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\phi\rho_{j}(\phi)\zeta_{j}\ln H(\phi).

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\ln\mathcal{Z}_{\cs^{4}}

3.9
Towards comparison with supergravity: Simplified holography

The supergravity dual of a 4d quiver CFT is the Gaiotto–Maldacena background
[
16
]
, with the supergravity approximation valid at infinite ’t Hooft coupling and in the long quiver limit.

9

The sphere free energy is holographic dual to the on-shell Einstein–Hilbert action, evaluated on the classical solution and properly regularised. The regularisation unavoidably introduces scheme dependence, and both in CFT and supergravity the free energy is defined up to a finite counterterm proportional to the central charge:

S_{\text{\rm counterterm}}=\text{\rm const}\cdot a.

The supergravity action and the free energy of the matrix model thus agree possibly up to a counterterm of this form. Since
a
is a fairly simple function of the parameters, this ambiguity is easy to factor out and faithfully compare localisation to supergravity.

We now quickly review the generalities of the supergravity solution, and then propose a heuristic approach to evaluate the on-shell action, finding agreement with the computation in the long quiver SCFT.

The starting point is eleven-dimensional supergravity on AdS
{}_{5}\times M_{6}

Statement 3.9
.

The solution to (

V(\sigma,\eta)=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}R_{k}\,K_{0}\left(\frac{\pi k\sigma}{P}\right)\sin\frac{\pi k\eta}{P}\,.

Proof.

The Fourier expansion of

in the compact

V(\sigma,\eta)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}V_{k}(\sigma)\sin\frac{\pi k\eta}{P},

where we have already imposed the third condition in (

\left[\left(\sigma\partial_{\sigma}\right)^{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}k^{2}}{P^{2}}\sigma^{2}\right]V_{k}(\sigma)=0.

The solution to this equation is:

V_{k}(\sigma)=V_{k}^{(0)}\cdot K_{0}\left(\frac{\pi k\sigma}{P}\right),

where

is the modified Bessel function of the second kind, and

It remains to substitute this into the action of Type IIA supergravity.

The 10d supergravity fields depend on

Statement 3.10
.

Let

denote the finite part of (

and after holographic renormalisation. With a suitable choice of renormalisation scheme, we find

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\cos\left(\frac{\pi k\eta}{P}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi\ell\eta}{P}\right)K_{0}\left(\frac{\pi k\sigma}{P}\right)K_{0}\left(\frac{\pi\ell\sigma}{P}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\penalty\ \sin\left(\frac{\pi k\eta}{P}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi\ell\eta}{P}\right)K_{1}\left(\frac{\pi k\sigma}{P}\right)K_{1}\left(\frac{\pi\ell\sigma}{P}\right)\right].

Summary and outlook

In the present work we have exhaustively investigated linear quiver theories with eight supercharges.

The long quiver limit has been solved in arbitrary dimension
d\geq 3
(Section
2
). The central ingredient in our method is the piece-wise linear rank function
\mathcal{R}(\eta)
, which encodes the specifics of the conformal linear quiver under consideration. This rank function is defined independently of the dimension
d
and serves as input for the holographic dual problem in supergravity when
d
is integer.

The function
\tilde{F}_{d}
given in (
2.31
) interpolates between the free energies in
d=3
and
d=5
and matches the Weyl anomaly coefficient
a
in
d=4
. Besides, not only (
2.31
) reproduces the previously known answers when evaluated at
d\in\left\{3,5\right\}
, but it extends them to finite ’t Hooft couplings.

The interpolation across dimensions successfully extends to defect one-point functions, including expectation values of half-BPS Wilson loops. We have computed the expectation value of one-, two-, and three-dimensional defects in
d\geq 3
, finding a universal behaviour.

Considering the different large-

A take-home summary of this work is:

Long linear quivers at large-

show a universal behaviour, are dealt with uniformly as functions of

Superconformal gauge theories in four dimensions can be also modelled by circular quivers. A linear quiver is a circular quiver with one node erased, by setting one coupling to zero, but that necessarily takes the theory out of the strong-coupling regime. The circular quivers indeed have a very different holographic description, they are dual to orbifolds of AdS
{}_{5}\times\mathbb{S}^{5}
at some point in their parameter space, and thus naturally belong to type IIB strings, while linear quivers have type IIA holographic duals. The difference is well refelcted in the matrix model. The large-

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{j}}\left[K_{j}f^{\prime}(\phi_{a}^{(j)})+\sum_{b=1}^{N_{j+1}}f^{\prime}(\phi_{a}^{(j)}-\phi_{b}^{(j+1)})\right].

Using these expressions we arrive at the formulae used in Section
2
.

A.2
Lerch’s transcendent

In this appendix we collect the main properties of Lerch’s transcendent function
[
79
]

\Phi(z,d,a):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{(k+a)^{d}},

defined for
a>0
and
\lvert z\rvert<1
, or
\lvert z\rvert=1
and
\Re(d)>1
. The latter case is the one realised by our gauge theories. It follows from the infinite series definition that Lerch’s transcendent generalises both the Hurwitz
\zeta
-function and the polylogarithm:

\Phi(1,d,a)=\zeta(d,a),\qquad z\Phi(z,d,1)=\mathrm{Li}_{d}(z).

It admits various integral representations, including

\Phi(z,d,a)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(d)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\penalty\ \frac{u^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-au}}{1-z\mathrm{e}^{-u}},

which provides a definition valid outside the disk
\lvert z\rvert\leq 1
. Another useful integral representation is

\Phi(z,d,a)=-\Gamma(1-d)\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{2\pi\mathtt{i}}\penalty\ \frac{(-u)^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-au}}{1-z\mathrm{e}^{-u}},

with the integration contour
\mathcal{C}
being a Hankel contour (encircles the positive real semi-axis counterclockwise) that leaves outside all the poles of the integrand at
u=\ln(z)+2\pi\mathtt{i}\Z
.

We need the following result.

Lemma A.2
.

For
d\in\N
and
\lvert z\rvert\leq 1
,

z^{a}\Phi(z,d,a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\neq d-1\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta(d-k,a)}{k!}(\ln z)^{k}+\left[\psi(d)-\psi(a)-\ln(-\ln(z))\right]\frac{(\ln z)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!},

where
\zeta(\cdot,\cdot)
is Hurwitz’s
\zeta
-function and
\psi(\cdot)
is the digamma function.

Appendix B
Three perspectives on the saddle point equation

We discuss three different approaches to derive the saddle point equation (
2.18
). The content of this appendix will certainly be known to most readers, but we wish to emphasise the physical significance of certain aspects of the computation.

The canonical way to derive the saddle point equation, reviewed for example in
[
52
,
91
]
, goes as follows. One keeps
S_{\mathrm{eff}}
written in terms of the eigenvalues
\phi^{(j)}_{a}
of the matrix model, and keeps the sums instead of the integrals. The saddle point configuration is a point in
\R^{\mathrm{rk}(G)}
, where

\mathrm{rk}(G)=\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}N_{j}.

It is determined by solving the system

\frac{\partial S_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\partial\phi_{a}^{(j)}}=0,\qquad\forall\penalty\ a=1,\dots,N_{j},\ \forall\penalty\ j=1,\dots,P-1.

It is at this stage that one introduces the eigenvalue densities
\rho_{j}(\phi)
, which reduce the system of
\mathrm{rk}(G)
equations to a system of
(P-1)
integral equations for the
(P-1)
measures
\rho_{j}(\phi)\mathrm{d}\phi
on
\R
, for
j=1,\dots,P-1
. In the large-
P
limit, we can collect all of them into a single measure
\rho(z,\phi)\mathrm{d}\phi\mathrm{d}z
on the strip
[0,1]\times\R
. We thus reduce the problem to a unique saddle point equation. Performing the steps explicitly, one finds (
2.18
).

There are at least two alternative methods. In both cases, one starts by approximating the sums
\sum_{a=1}^{N_{j}}
with integrals over the variable

\mathsf{a}=\frac{a}{N_{j}},\qquad 0<\mathsf{a}\leq\nu(z).

The analogous substitution is made to replace
\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}
with an integral over the variable
z=j/P
,
0<z<1
. The eigenvalues
\phi_{a}^{(j)}
become functions

\phi:[0,1]\times[0,\nu(z)]\to\R

(B.1)

and, for every function
f(\phi)
,

\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{j}}f\left(\phi_{a}^{(j)}\right)\simeq P\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}z\penalty\ N\int_{0}^{\nu(z)}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{a}\penalty\ f\left(\phi(z,\mathsf{a})\right).

The approximation becomes exact as
N,P\to\infty
. The
\mathrm{rk}(G)
-fold integral over the eigenvalues in the matrix model is replaced by a functional integral over continuous functions (
B.1
). One then implicitly inverts the relation
\phi=\phi(z,\mathsf{a})
to write a function
\mathsf{a}(z,\phi)
. The density of eigenvalues is defined as the inverse of the derivative
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\mathsf{a}}
, namely

\rho(z,\phi):=\frac{\partial\mathsf{a}(z,\phi)}{\partial\phi}.

This definition agrees with the definition using
\delta
-functions. Writing the latter relation as an equality of measures, one replaces the integrals

\int_{0}^{\nu(z)}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{a}\penalty\ f\left(\phi(z,\mathsf{a})\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho(z,\phi)\mathrm{d}\phi\penalty\ f\left(z,\phi\right).

This is the presentation given in the main text.

One way to obtain the saddle point equation from there is to take the functional derivative

\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\delta\rho}=0.

(B.2)

However, the effective action
S_{\mathrm{eff}}
can always be redefined by a (scheme-dependent) term of the form

S_{\mathrm{c.t.}}=c\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}z\int\mathrm{d}\phi\rho(z,\phi),

for a constant
c
. This action is a local counterterm that multiplies the partition function by a complex number,

\mathcal{Z}_{\cs^{d}}\ \mapsto\ \mathrm{e}^{-S_{\mathrm{c.t.}}}\mathcal{Z}_{\cs^{d}},

without altering the field content and interactions in the gauge theory. As usual, we are interested in studying the theory up to these counterterms.

By the above discussion, shifting
S_{\mathrm{eff}}\mapsto S_{\mathrm{eff}}+S_{\mathrm{c.t.}}
produces a shift of the functional derivative by a constant
c
. We are therefore interested in (
B.2
) up to constant shifts, which leads us to solve

\partial_{\phi}\left(\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\delta\rho}\right)=0.

(B.3)

Computing the latter explicitly indeed yields (
2.18
).

The third approach is a variation of the above. One establishes a variational problem on the functional space of continuous functions
\mathsf{a}(z,\phi)
. One first (implicitly) inverts the relation
\phi=\phi(z,\mathsf{a})
from (
B.1
), introduces the density of eigenvalues, and writes
S_{\mathrm{eff}}
as we have done in the main text. This procedure sets up the variational problem with respect to the function
\mathsf{a}(z,\phi)
.
S_{\mathrm{eff}}
does not depend explicitly on
\mathsf{a}
, but depends on
\rho=\partial_{\phi}\mathsf{a}
. The functional Euler–Lagrange equation yields (
B.3
).
