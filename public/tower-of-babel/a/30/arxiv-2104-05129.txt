A Complex Borel-Bernstein Theorem
Gerardo González Robert

Abstract
. Zero-one laws are a central topic in metric Diophantine approximation. A classical example of such laws is the Borel-Bernstein theorem. In this note, we prove a complex analogue of the Borel-Bernstein theorem for complex Hurwitz continued fractions. As a corollary, we obtain a complex version of Khinchin’s theorem on Diophantine approximation.



1.
Introduction

For over a century, number theory and probability have had a fruitful relationship. A particularly fecund environment for this alliance is the theory of regular continued fractions, which we shall denote by

[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]_{\mathbb{R}}=a_{0}+\frac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots}}}.

The Borel-Bernstein Theorem is a classical result in this area, its proof can be found in
[
3
]
(Theorem 1.11.).

Theorem 1

(Borel-Bernstein)
.

Let
\mathbf{u}=(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of positive real numbers and define

E_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{u}):=\left\{z=[0;a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]_{\mathbb{R}}\in[0,1]:a_{n}\geq u_{n}\;\text{ for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

If
\mathfrak{m}_{1}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1)
, then

\mathfrak{m}_{1}(E_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{u}))=\begin{cases}0,\text{ if }\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}u_{n}^{-1}<+\infty,\\
1,\text{ if }\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}u_{n}^{-1}=+\infty.\end{cases}

In this note, we show a complex analogue of the Borel-Bernstein theorem using Hurwitz continued fractions (defined on Section 2). Hurwitz continued fractions allow us to represent every complex number as a finite or infinite continued fraction of the form

[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots]:=a_{0}+\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots}}},

where each
a_{n}
is a Gaussian integer. In this context, a unit square
\mathfrak{F}
centered at the origin (defined on Section 2) will play the role the interval
[0,1)
plays within the regular continued fraction theory.

Theorem 2

(Main result)
.

Let
\mathbf{u}=(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of positive real numbers and define

E(\mathbf{u})=\left\{z=[0;a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]\in\mathfrak{F}:|a_{n}|\geq u_{n}\;\text{ for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

If
\mathfrak{m}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathfrak{F}
, then

\mathfrak{m}(E(\mathbf{u}))=\begin{cases}0,\text{ if }\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}u_{n}^{-2}<+\infty,\\
1,\text{ if }\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}u_{n}^{-2}=+\infty.\end{cases}

We obtain a complex version of Khinchin’s theorem on Diophantine approximation (Theorem 1.10 in
[
3
]
) as a consequence of Theorem
2
.

Corollary 3
.

Given
\psi:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, define the set

K(\psi)=\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{C}:\left|\xi-\frac{p}{q}\right|\leq\psi(|q|)\;\text{ for infinitely many }p,q\in\mathbb{Z}[i],q\neq 0\right\}.

If
x\mapsto x^{2}\psi(x)
is non-increasing, then

\mathfrak{m}(K(\psi))=\begin{cases}0,\text{ if }\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{3}\psi(n)^{2}<+\infty,\\
\text{full},\text{ if }\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{3}\psi(n)^{2}=+\infty.\end{cases}

A. Nogueira studied a Borel-Bernstein theorem for some multidimensional continued fractions algorithms, such as the Perron-Jacobi and Brun algorithm
[
15
]
. Some results resembling Corollary
3
are well known. In 1952, W. J. Leveque introduced a complex continued fraction expansion and he used it to show a Khinchin-type result (Theorem 5 in
[
13
]
). In 1982, with the aid of disjoint spheres and Bianchi groups, D. Sullivan obtained a result stronger than Corollary
3
(see Theorem 5 in
[
16
]
). In 2006, V. Beresnevich, D. Dickinson, and S. Velani strengthened Sullivan’s result by applying their work on ubiquitous systems. They proved that Corollary
3
still holds if we replace “
x\mapsto x^{2}\psi(x)
is non-increasing” by “
\psi
is non-increasing” (Theorem 7 in
[
1
]
). Moreover, in the recent paper
[
7
]
, Y. He and Y. Xiong used Hurwitz continued fractions to compute the Hausdorff dimension of
K(\psi)
for non-increasing functions
\psi
such that
\psi(x)
tends to
0
when
x
tends to
+\infty
. We can combine Theorem 8 in
[
1
]
and Theorem 1.2 in
[
7
]
to obtain the next result:

Theorem 4
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be a non-increasing function such that
\psi(x)\to 0
when
x\to+\infty
. Define

\lambda:=\lambda(\psi)=\liminf_{x\to+\infty}\frac{-\log\psi(x)}{\log(x)}

and let
\HD(\psi)
be the Hausdorff dimension of
K(\psi)
. If
\lambda>2
, then
\HD(\psi)=\tfrac{4}{\lambda}
and
\HD(\psi)=2
whenever
\lambda\leq 2
. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{H}^{\HD(\psi)}
denotes the
\HD(\psi)
-Hausdorff measure on
\mathbb{C}
, we have

\mathcal{H}^{\HD(\psi)}(K(\psi))=\begin{cases}0,\text{ if }\sum_{n\geq 1}\psi(n)^{\HD({\psi})}n^{3}<+\infty,\\
\text{full, if }\sum_{n\geq 1}\psi(n)^{\HD({\psi})}n^{3}=+\infty.\end{cases}

When we choose
\varepsilon>0
and
\psi(x)=x^{-(1+\varepsilon)}
, Theorem
4
is precisely the complex Jarník-Besicovitch Theorem (Theorem 6.1 in
[
5
]
). A nice discussion of Khinchin-type results and a simple proof of the complex Jarník-Besicovitch Theorem can be found in
[
5
]
. A related multidimensional theorem was proven by M. Hussain in
[
10
]
. None of these papers use on any Borel-Bernstein-type result. However, they do rely on the convergence part of the Borel-Cantelli Lemma, whose proof can be found in almost every measure theory book.

Lemma 5

(Borel-Cantelli lemma, convergence part)
.

Let
(X,\mathscr{A},\mu)
be a measure space and
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
a sequence in
\mathscr{A}
. If
\sum_{n}\mu(A_{n})<+\infty
, then
\mu(\limsup_{n}A_{n})=0
.

The organization of this note is as follows: in the next section, we define Hurwitz continued fractions and prove some useful lemmas; afterwards, we prove Theorem
2
and Corollary
3
.

Notation.
The natural numbers,
\mathbb{N}
, are the positive integers and
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. If
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(y_{n})_{n\geq 1}

such that
x_{n}\leq\kappa y_{n}

. We write

if
x_{n}\ll y_{n}
and

are true. Whenever the constant implied by
\ll
depends on some object
\alpha
, we shall write
\ll_{\alpha}
. For any
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}
,
A^{*}:=A\setminus\{0\}
,
\Cl(A)
is the closure of
A
,
\inte A
is the interior of
A
,
zA:=\{za:a\in A\}
for a given

, and, if

is the number of elements contained in

2.
Hurwitz Continued Fractions

Let us define Hurwitz continued fraction. Denote by
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}
the usual floor function, that is,
\lfloor x\rfloor
is the largest integer less than or equal to
x
for any real number
x
. Define the function
[\cdot]:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{Z}[i]
by

\forall z\in\mathbb{C}\quad[z]=\left\lfloor\Re(z)+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor+i\left\lfloor\Im(z)+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor,

and the sets

\mathfrak{F}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:[z]=0\},\quad\mathfrak{F}^{*}:=\mathfrak{F}\setminus\{0\},\quad\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}:=\mathfrak{F}\setminus\mathbb{Q}(i).

Let
a_{1}:\mathfrak{F}^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}[i]
be given by
a_{1}(z)=[z^{-1}]
and
T:\mathfrak{F}\to\mathfrak{F}
by

\forall z\in\mathfrak{F}\quad T(z)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{z}-\left[\frac{1}{z}\right],\text{ if }z\neq 0,\\
0,\text{ if }z=0.\end{cases}

As usual,
T^{0}:\mathfrak{F}\to\mathfrak{F}
is the identity map and
T^{n}=T^{n-1}\circ T
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The function
T
is a complex analogue of the usual Gauss map.

Let
z
be any complex number. Define the possibly finite sequence of Gaussian integers
(a_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
as follows. First,
a_{0}(z):=[z]
and, as long as
T^{n-1}(z-a_{0}(z))\neq 0
, put

a_{n}(z)=a_{1}\left(T^{n-1}(z-a_{0}(z))\right).

The
Hurwitz continued fraction
(HCF) of
z
is

[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]:=a_{0}+\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots}}}.

We shall refer to the sequence
(a_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
as the
Hurwitz elements
of
z
and we will omit the dependence on
z
if there is no risk of ambiguity.

Several properties of regular continued fractions hold too in the complex case, although their known proofs might be much more complicated (for example, the third point of proposition
6
below). Among the properties that remain true, we have that the HCF of any
z\in\mathbb{Q}(i)
is finite and equals
z
. Also, when
z
is an irrational complex number, that is
z\in\mathbb{C}^{\prime}:=\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Q}(i)
, the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is infinite and

\lim_{n\to\infty}[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}]=z

(see
[
9
]
for a proof).

Proposition 6
.

Let
z
belong to
\mathbb{C}^{\prime}
, let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be its Hurwitz elements and define the sequences of Gaussian integers
(p_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(q_{n})_{n\geq 0}
by

\begin{pmatrix}p_{-1}&p_{-2}\\
q_{-1}&q_{-2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\quad\begin{pmatrix}p_{n}\\
q_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}p_{n-1}&p_{n-2}\\
q_{n-1}&q_{n-2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{n}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

The following statements hold for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
:

1.

q_{n}p_{n-1}-q_{n-1}p_{n}=(-1)^{n}
,

2.

If
z_{n}=T^{n-1}(z-a_{0})
, then
z=\displaystyle\frac{p_{n-2}z_{n}+p_{n-1}}{q_{n-2}z_{n}+q_{n-1}}
,

3.

|q_{n}|<|q_{n+1}|
.

4.

\tfrac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}]
.

The proofs of
3
and
4
can be found in
[
9
]
. The rest of the theorem is proven in a broader context as Propositions 3.3 and 3.7 in
[
4
]
. Proposition
6
also holds for finite HCF and any integer
n
such that the corresponding objects are defined.

We shall now explore the geometry of
T
. Let
\iota:\mathbb{C}^{*}\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
be the complex inversion,
\iota(z)=z^{-1}
, then

(1)

\iota[\mathfrak{F}]:=\overline{\mathbb{E}}(-1)\cap\overline{\mathbb{E}}(i)\cap\mathbb{E}(1)\cap{\mathbb{E}(-i)},

(see the paragraph on notation by then end of the introduction) and thus

(2)

\forall z\in\mathbb{C}^{\prime}\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\quad|a_{n}|\geq\sqrt{2}.

Hence, if for any
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}
we define the
cylinder

\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a}):=\{z\in\mathfrak{F}:a_{1}(z)=a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}(z)=a_{n}\},

we obtain from (
2
) that
\mathcal{C}_{1}(a)=\varnothing
for
a\in\{0,1,-1,i,-i\}
. Equation (
1
) along with some direct computations show that
T[\mathcal{C}_{1}(a)]=\mathfrak{F}
when
|a|\geq\sqrt{8}
. In general, given
a\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
with
|a|\in\{\sqrt{2},2,\sqrt{5}\}
, the set
T[\mathcal{C}_{1}(a)]
may assume fourteen different forms and, if we overlook the boundaries, it may assume only four different forms or any of their rotations by right angles (see Section 2 in
[
6
]
). Moreover, if for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}
we write

\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a}):=T^{n}[\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a})],

an inductive argument shows that the set
\{\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a}):n\in\mathbb{N},\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}\}\setminus\{\varnothing\}
is finite and we may conclude that

\{\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\right):n\in\mathbb{N},\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}\}\setminus\{0\}

is non-empty and finite (cfr. Section 2 in
[
6
]
).

It may happen that
\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\neq\varnothing
but
\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a}))=0
. Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we say that
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}
is
regular
if
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a})
has non-empty interior and
irregular
otherwise. An infinite sequence in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
is
regular
if all its prefixes are regular and irregular otherwise. Finally, a complex number is
regular
if the sequence of its HCF elements is regular and it is
irregular
in other case. Some properties follow directly from the definitions, inductive arguments, and our previous discussions. For example, if
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is irregular and
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\neq\varnothing
, then
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a})
is contained in some arc or in some line segment. Therefore, the set of irregular complex numbers is null relative to the Lebesgue measure. Also, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any regular
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}
we have

(3)

{\text{for some }}k\in\{0,1,2,3\}\quad i^{k}\inte\mathfrak{F}_{1}({1-i})\subseteq\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\subseteq\mathfrak{F}

(cfr. Lemma 1 in
[
6
]
). In what follows, we ignore irregular numbers, for they form a
\mathfrak{m}
-null set. However, for completeness sake, we exhibit an irregular sequence. If
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, then the set

\iota\left[\mathfrak{F}_{1}(-1+i)\right]\cap\{z+1-in:z\in\mathfrak{F}\}\neq\varnothing

is a segment of the line determined by
\Re(z)=\frac{1}{2}
, so
(-1+i,1-in)
is irregular.

We will not delve into the structure of the shift space associated to the dynamical system
(\mathfrak{F},T)
. We content ourselves with pointing out that it is rather complicated (see section 5.3 in
[
8
]
).

For any
z\in\mathbb{C}
, write
\|z\|:=\max\{|\Re(z)|,|\Im(z)|\}
. Hence, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
we have

\displaystyle\#\left(\{b\in\mathbb{Z}[i]:\|b\|=m\}\cap\iota\left[\mathfrak{F}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=8m,

\displaystyle\#\left(\{b\in\mathbb{Z}[i]:\|b\|=m\}\cap\iota\left[\inte\mathfrak{F}_{1}({1-i})\right]\right)

\displaystyle=2m-1.

For
m=2
, the first set contains fourteen elements while the second set has three elements. In view of (
3
), we can conclude the next estimate:

Lemma 7
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}
be such that
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a})
is regular, then

(4)

\forall m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}\qquad\#\;\left\{b\in\mathbb{Z}[i]:\|b\|=m\right\}\cap\iota\left[\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\right]\asymp m.

Lemma 8
.

Every
z=[0;a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]\in\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}
satisfies

(5)

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\qquad\left|z-\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right|\leq\frac{4+2\sqrt{2}}{|a_{n+1}||q_{n}|^{2}}.

Proof.

Take
z=[0;a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]\in\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Write
z_{j}=[0;a_{j},a_{j+1},a_{j+2},\ldots]
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
and let
(p_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(q_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be as in Proposition
6
, then

\left|z-\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right|=\left|\frac{p_{n}z_{n+1}^{-1}+p_{n-1}}{q_{n}z_{n+1}^{-1}+q_{n-1}}-\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right|=\frac{1}{|q_{n}|^{2}\left|z_{n+1}^{-1}+\frac{q_{n-1}}{q_{n}}\right|}.

(see Proposition
6
). Thus, we can conclude (
5
) if we obtain an absolute constant
\kappa_{1}>0
such that

(6)

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\quad\left|z_{n+1}^{-1}+\frac{q_{n-1}}{q_{n}}\right|\geq\kappa_{1}|a_{n+1}|.

Take
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that
|a_{n+1}|\geq 2
. Put

{\kappa_{1}}=1-\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{4}.

Since
|q_{n-1}|<|q_{n}|
and
|z_{n+2}|\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
, we have

\left|{z_{n+1}^{-1}}+\frac{q_{n-1}}{q_{n}}\right|=\left|a_{n+1}+{z_{n+2}}+\frac{q_{n-1}}{q_{n}}\right|\geq|a_{n+1}|-\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\geq{\kappa_{1}}|a_{n+1}|.

Now, assume that
|a_{n+1}|=\sqrt{2}
. Since
\Cl(\mathfrak{F})\subseteq\mathbb{D}(0;1)
, the closed set
\iota[\Cl(\mathfrak{F})]
is entirely contained in the complement of the compact set
\overline{\mathbb{D}}(0;1)
, so

\kappa_{2}:=\inf\left\{|w-w^{\prime}|:w\in\iota[\Cl(\mathfrak{F})],\;w^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}(0;1)\right\}>0,

and hence

\left|{z_{n+1}^{-1}}+\frac{q_{n-1}}{q_{n}}\right|\geq\kappa_{2}=\frac{\kappa_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}|a_{n+1}|.

It is not hard to show that
\tfrac{\kappa_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}>\kappa_{1}
, so (
6
) also holds in this case. Because
\kappa_{1}^{-1}=4+2\sqrt{2}
, the lemma is proven.
∎

Once again, let us introduce some notation. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{m}
, write
\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}:=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})
, and define the sets
\Omega_{R}(n):=\{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n}:\mathbf{c}\text{ is regular}\}
and

R(\mathbf{a};m):=\left\{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{m}:\mathbf{a}\mathbf{c}\in\Omega_{R}(n+m)\right\}.

Lemma 9
.

If
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{a}\in\Omega_{R}(n)
, and
b\in R(\mathbf{a};1)
, then

\frac{\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n+1}(\mathbf{a}b)\right)}{\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\right)}\asymp\frac{1}{|b|^{4}}.

Lemma
9
is the complex version of a well known estimate for regular continued fractions (see Equation (57) in
[
11
]
for a precise statement and its proof).

Proof.

Let
n,\mathbf{a},b
be as in the statement and take
(p_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(q_{n})_{n\geq 0}
as in Proposition
6
. The restriction of
T^{n}
to
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a})
is a bijection onto
\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})
with inverse
t_{\mathbf{a},n}:\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\to\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a})

\forall z\in\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\quad t_{\mathbf{a},n}(z)=\frac{p_{n-1}z+p_{n}}{q_{n-1}z+q_{n}}=\frac{p_{n-1}}{q_{n-1}}+\frac{{(-1)^{n-1}}}{q_{n-1}(q_{n-1}z+q_{n})}.

Since
|q_{n-1}|<|q_{n}|
and
\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\subseteq\mathbb{D}\left(0;1/\sqrt{2}\right)
,
t_{\mathbf{a},n}
is holomorphic and

\forall z\in\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\quad t_{\mathbf{a},n}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{{(-1)^{n}}}{(q_{n-1}z+q_{n})^{2}}.

When we identify
\mathbb{C}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the function
t_{\mathbf{a},n}
is differentiable on
\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})
and, if
Dt_{\mathbf{a},n}(x,y)
denotes its real derivative at the point
(x,y)
, we get for all
z=x+iy
in
\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})

|\det Dt_{\mathbf{a},n}(x,y)|={|t_{\mathbf{a},n}^{\prime}(z)|}=\frac{1}{|q_{n-1}z+q_{n}|^{4}}=\frac{1}{|q_{n}|^{4}\left|\frac{q_{n-1}}{q_{n}}z+1\right|^{4}}

(see p. 33 in
[
12
]
) and, since every
z\in\mathfrak{F}
satisfies

1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\leq 1-|z|\leq 1-\left|\frac{q_{n-1}}{q_{n}}\right||z|\leq\left|\frac{q_{n-1}}{q_{n}}z+1\right|\leq\left|\frac{q_{n-1}}{q_{n}}z\right|+1<2,

we may conclude that
|\det Dt_{\mathbf{a},n}|\asymp|q_{n}|^{-4}
. Therefore, the Theorem of Change of Variable (Theorem 3.7.1 in
[
2
]
, Vol. 1., p. 194) implies that every Borel set
B\subseteq\mathbb{C}
satisfies

\mathfrak{m}\left(t_{\mathbf{a},n}[B\cap\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})]\right)\asymp\frac{\mathfrak{m}\left(B\cap\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\right)}{|q_{n}|^{4}}.

In particular, choosing
B=\mathfrak{F}
and using (
3
), we get
\mathfrak{m}(\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a}))\asymp|q_{n}|^{-4}
. If we apply a similar argument on
\mathcal{C}_{n+1}(\mathbf{a}b)
, we arrive at

\frac{\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n+1}(\mathbf{a}b)\right)}{\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\right)}\asymp\frac{|q_{n}|^{4}}{|bq_{n}+q_{n-1}|^{4}}=\frac{1}{\left|b+\frac{q_{n-1}}{q_{n}}\right|^{4}}.

Finally, by
|q_{n-1}|<|q_{n}|
and
\sqrt{2}\leq|b|
(see (
2
)), the constant
{\kappa_{3}}=4(\sqrt{2}-1)^{-4}
verifies

\frac{1}{2^{4}|b|^{4}}\leq\frac{1}{(|b|+1)^{4}}\ll\frac{\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n+1}(\mathbf{a}b)\right)}{\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\right)}\ll\frac{1}{(|b|-1)^{4}}\leq\frac{{\kappa_{3}}}{|b|^{4}}.\qed

3.
Proof of the Main Theorem

Let
\mathbf{u}=(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Instead of working with
E(\mathbf{u})
, we will use more manageable sets. For any
\kappa>0
define

E^{\infty}(\kappa\mathbf{u}):=\left\{z=[0;a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]\in\mathfrak{F}:\kappa u_{n}\leq\|a_{n}\|\quad\text{for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

and
E^{\infty}(\mathbf{u}):=E^{\infty}(1\mathbf{u})

E^{\infty}(\mathbf{u})\subseteq E(\mathbf{u})\subseteq E^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbf{u}\right),

Lemma 10
.

If
\sum_{n}u_{n}^{-2}<+\infty
, then
\mathfrak{m}(E^{\infty}(\kappa\mathbf{u}))=0
for every
\kappa>0
.

Proof.

Define the sequence of sets
(E_{n}^{\infty})_{n\geq 1}
by

(8)

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\qquad E_{n}^{\infty}:=\left\{[0;a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]\in\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}:u_{n}\leq\|a_{n}\|\right\}

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{a}\in\Omega_{R}(k)
, Lemmas
7
and
9
give

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}\left(E_{k+1}^{\infty}\cap\mathcal{C}_{k}(\mathbf{a})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\|b\|\geq u_{k+1}}\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{k+1}(\mathbf{a}b)\right)

\displaystyle\asymp\sum_{\|b\|\geq u_{k+1}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{k}(\mathbf{a})\right)}{|b|^{4}}

\displaystyle\ll\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{k}(\mathbf{a})\right)\sum_{n\geq u_{k+1}}n^{-3}{\leq\frac{\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{k}(\mathbf{a})\right)}{(u_{k+1}-1)^{2}},}

Note that there is no loss of generality in assuming that
u_{k+1}>1
. Letting
\mathbf{a}
run along
\mathbb{Z}[i]^{k}
, we arrive at
\mathfrak{m}(E_{k+1}^{\infty}){\ll(u_{k+1}-1)^{-2}}
. Hence, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma,
\mathfrak{m}\left(E^{\infty}(\mathbf{u})\right)=0
. Finally, for any
\kappa>0
, the convergence of
\sum_{k}u_{k}^{-2}
implies that of
\sum_{k}(\kappa u_{k})^{-2}
and the previous argument shows
\mathfrak{m}\left(E^{\infty}(\kappa\mathbf{u})\right)=0
.
∎

Lemma 11
.

If
\sum_{n\geq 1}u_{n}^{-2}=+\infty
, then
\mathfrak{m}\left(E^{\infty}(\kappa\mathbf{u})\right)=1
for every
\kappa>0
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
10
, it is enough to show the result for
\kappa=1
. Let
(E_{n}^{\infty})_{n\geq 1}
be as in (
8
) and define

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\qquad B_{n}:=\bigcap_{j\geq n}\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}\setminus E_{j}^{\infty}.

Note that
(B_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is increasing and

\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}\setminus E^{\infty}(\mathbf{u})=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}B_{n}.

Take
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{a}\in\Omega_{R}(k)
and let
0<c_{1}<1
be a constant (independent of
k
and
\mathbf{a}
) such that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\mathbf{a}\in\Omega_{R}(k)
we have

\forall b\in R(\mathbf{a};1)\qquad\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{k+1}(\mathbf{a}b)\right)\geq\frac{c_{1}}{|b|^{4}}\mathfrak{m}\left({\mathcal{C}_{k}}(\mathbf{a})\right)

(cfr. Lemma
9
). Let

be such that for every

\sum_{j\geq M}\frac{1}{j^{3}}>c_{2}\frac{1}{(M+1)^{2}}.

Therefore, after choosing a suitable constant

7
and writing

, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|b\|\geq M\\

and thus

(9)

\sum_{\|b\|\leq M}\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{k+1}(\mathbf{a}b)\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{c}{(M+1)^{2}}\right)\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathcal{C}_{k}(\mathbf{a})\right)

Define for each

{F(\mathbf{a},k,n)=\bigcup_{\mathbf{b}}\mathcal{C}_{k+n}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}),}

where

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}\quad\mathfrak{m}\left(F(\mathbf{a},k,n)\right)\leq\mathfrak{m}\left(F(\mathbf{a},k,1)\right)\prod_{j=2}^{n}\left(1-\frac{c}{(1+u_{k+j})^{2}}\right).

\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}\setminus E^{\infty}(\mathbf{u})\right)=\mathfrak{m}\left(\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}B_{k}\right)=0.\quad\qed
