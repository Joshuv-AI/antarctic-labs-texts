Universality of outliers in weakly confined Coulomb-type systems
Raphael Butez, David García-Zelada, Alon Nishry, Aron Wennman

Abstract
. This work concerns weakly confined particle systems in the plane, characterized by a large number of outliers away from a droplet where the bulk of the particles accumulate in the many-particle limit. We are interested in the asymptotic behavior of outliers for two classes of point processes: Coulomb gases at determinantal inverse temperature confined by a regular background, and a class of random polynomials. We observe that the limiting outlier process only depends on the shape of the uncharged region containing them, and the global net excess charge. In particular, for a determinantal Coulomb gas confined by a sufficiently regular background measure, the outliers in a simply connected uncharged region converge to the corresponding Bergman point process. For a finitely connected uncharged region Ω \Omega , a family of limiting outlier processes arises, indexed by the (Pontryagin) dual of the fundamental group of Ω \Omega . Moreover, the outliers in different uncharged regions are asymptotically independent, even if the regions have common boundary points. The latter result is a manifestation of screening properties of the particle system.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Weakly confined Coulomb gases

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on the complex plane
{\mathbb{C}}
.
We are interested in
a system of
N
particles
of unit negative charge
attracted to the (positive)
charge distribution

\kappa_{N}\mu
,
where
\kappa_{N}
is such that

N<\kappa_{N}\leq N+1
.
More precisely,
we consider the
point process on
\mathbb{C}

induced by the Boltzmann-Gibbs measure

(1.1)

{\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}_{N}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}e^{-2{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(z)}{\mathrm{d}}{m}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}(z),\qquad(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}

where the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
is given by

{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})=-\sum_{i<j}\log|z_{i}-z_{j}|+\kappa_{N}\sum_{j}U^{\mu}(z_{j}),\qquad(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N},

and where
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
is a normalizing constant.
Throughout,
{m}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}

denotes the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
and
U^{\mu}
is
the logarithmic potential of the measure
\mu

defined as

U^{\mu}(z)=\int\log|z-w|{\mathrm{d}}\mu(w),\qquad z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

We denote by
\Theta_{N}
a random variable
with law

\mathbb{P}_{N}
.
As the particles are interchangeable
and almost surely distinct, we may abuse
notation and think of the random vector
as a simple point
process
\Theta_{N}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\}

(see Appendix
A
).

The point process

\Theta_{N}
is sometimes called
a
weakly confined
Coulomb gas
or a
jellium
associated with
\mu
.
The idealized total background charge

\kappa_{N}=N
would correspond to
global charge neutrality of the system,
but we ask that
\kappa_{N}>N
for
the law
\mathbb{P}_{N}
to be well-defined
without spatial truncation.
This choice is discussed by Jancovici in

[
20
, Section 2.1]

for
\kappa_{N}=N+1
and also by Chafaï, García-Zelada and Jung in

[
8
]
.
The terminology

weakly confining

is related to the growth of the potential
at infinity

U^{\mu}(z)=\log|z|+\mathrm{O}(1)

(cf. Hardy
[
12
]
).
If the total background charge would instead be
so large that
\liminf_{N}N^{-1}\kappa_{N}>1

and if
U^{\mu}
is regular enough
then the gas would be strongly confined to a
compact set
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathrm{supp}(\mu)

(the
droplet
), i.e.,
with high probability all particles
would be found within a distance

N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\log N
from that set, see Ameur’s
paper
[
1
]
.

For both the weakly and strongly confined models, as
the number
N
of particles tends to infinity,
the system arranges itself in equilibrium at
the macroscopic level. For the weakly confined gas this amounts to the
convergence in law of the empirical measure

(1.2)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{z\in\Theta_{N}}\delta_{z}\longrightarrow\mu

which reflects the fact that the particles are attracted
by the charge distribution
\mu
and, to leading order,
are able to neutralize it in the limit.

The factor
2
appearing in front of the Hamiltonian
in (
1.1
) plays
a crucial role in our analysis. Indeed,
the more general Coulomb gas obtained by replacing

2
by an “inverse temperature”
\beta>0
is also well-studied, but
only for
\beta=2
is
the resulting point process
a determinantal point process.
Specifically, the Coulomb gas (
1.1
) is a
determinantal point process with correlation kernel

(1.3)

\mathcal{K}_{N}(z,w)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}P_{k,N}(z)\overline{P_{k,N}(w)}e^{-\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}(z)}e^{-\kappa_{N}\overline{U^{\mu}(w)}},\quad z,w\in\mathbb{C}

where
(P_{k,N})_{k\leq N-1}
is any orthonormal basis of

L^{2}({\mathbb{C}},e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}})\cap{\mathbb{C}}_{N-1}[X]
,
and background measure given by Lebesgue measure
on the plane, see Appendix
A
.

1.4

and the the component
\Omega=\Omega_{1}

z_{1}

1.2.
Bergman universality and independence.

Informally, we are interested in the

outlier point processes

for the Coulomb gas
\Theta_{N}
.
Specifically, if
\Omega

is an
uncharged region
, i.e.,
a connected component of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,(\mu)
,
we want to determine
the asymptotic distribution of the point process

\Theta_{N}

restricted to
\Omega
, namely

(1.4)

\Phi_{N}=\Theta_{N}\cap\Omega,\qquad N\geq 1.

We say that
\Phi_{N}
converges to a point
process
\Psi

in law
, if for
any
f\in C_{c}(\Omega)
, we have the convergence

(1.5)

\sum_{z\in\Phi_{N}}f(z)\to\sum_{z\in\Psi}f(z)

in distribution. We call
\Psi
the

limiting macroscopic outlier process in
\Omega
.
Notice that we do not normalize the linear statistics in
(
1.5
). For the usual (strongly confined)
Coulomb gases, there is no such limiting process.
However, in the setting of weak confinement,
there tends to be a relatively
large number of particles at positive distance from
\mathrm{supp}\,(\mu)
.
In the recent work
[
7
]
, the first two authors
studied these systems under the assumption that the measure

\mu
is radial, and it was found that
the outliers are governed by the so-called

Bergman point process
of the uncharged region.

We will use methods from potential theory
that require some regularity of
U^{\mu}
.
This is most sensitive near the boundary

\partial\Omega
, but to avoid technicalities
we require regularity also elsewhere.
The main example we have in mind
is the
case where
\mathrm{d}\mu=u\,{\mathrm{d}}m_{D}

for some nice enough density
u>0
and domain
D

but we allow for charges
situated on curves as well.
Specifically, we require that
the support of
\mu

is a disjoint union

\overline{D}\cup\Gamma
,
where
D
is a domain with analytic boundary
and where
\Gamma\cup\partial D
is a finite disjoint
union of closed analytic Jordan curves
(see Figure
1.1
),
\Gamma\cap D=\emptyset
.

We use the standard notation
C^{\infty}
and
C^{\omega}
for the classes of
infinitely differentiable and real-analytic functions,
respectively, and denote the arc length
measure on a disjoint union of curves
\gamma
by
\sigma_{\gamma}

and the restriction of the Lebesgue measure to
D
by
m_{D}
.

Figure 1.2
.
Illustration of the the weakly confined jellium.
The outliers are marked by white squares.

Definition 1.1

(Admissible probability measure)
.

A probability measure
\mu
is said to be
admissible

if its support equals
\overline{D}\cup\Gamma

for some
D
and
\Gamma
as above,
and

{\mathrm{d}}\mu=u\,{\mathrm{d}}{m}_{D}+v\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma_{\partial D\cup\Gamma},

where
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{D})

and
v\in C^{\omega}(\partial D\cup\Gamma)
,

u
is real-analytic
and strictly positive in a neighborhood of

\partial D
, and where
v

is either strictly positive or vanish
identically on each component of

\partial\Omega\cup\Gamma
.

Notice that, since
\partial D\cup\Gamma

is a finite union of closed curves in the plane,
either
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
or its complement
is bounded. By taking
D
to be the empty set, we allow
the measure to be supported solely on a system of disjoint
Jordan curves.

We begin with a simple version of our main result, where

\Omega
is assumed to be a simply
connected domain in the Riemann sphere

\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
.
For the formulation,
we recall that the Bergman kernel of a domain
\Omega

is the reproducing kernel
for the (closed) subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathrm{d}{m})
consisting of
holomorphic functions.

Theorem 1.2

(Outliers in a simply connected region)
.

Suppose
\mu
is an admissible probability
measure and
let
\Omega

be a simply connected component of

\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,(\mu)
.
If

\kappa_{N}=N+1
we have the convergence

\Phi_{N}\xrightarrow{\phantom{aa}}\Pi_{\Omega}\quad\text{in law}

as
N\to\infty
, where the limiting
process
\Pi_{\Omega}
in
\Omega
is
the determinantal point process with correlation kernel
given by the Bergman kernel
of
\Omega
.

The convergence
\Phi_{N}\to\Pi_{\Omega}
amounts to locally uniform
convergence of the correlation kernels for the respective processes.
The kernel and the limiting process, called
the
Bergman point process
,
are described in Section
2.1
.
In the case where
\Omega
is the unit disk, the Bergman point process
coincides with the zeros of the hyperbolic GAF studied by Peres and Virág

[
24
]
.
For a general simply connected domain
\Omega
, if
\phi
is a conformal
map from the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
to
\Omega
, then, in law,

\Pi_{\Omega}=\phi(\Pi_{\mathbb{D}})
, which is an immediate
consequence of the conformal equivariance of Bergman kernels.

Remark 1.3
.

The choice
\kappa_{N}=N+1
is a natural from
a geometric point of view. The reason is that, with this choice,
the Coulomb gas can naturally be viewed as a random process
on the sphere with respect to a continuous potential,
while any other choice of
\kappa_{N}
will add an extraneous charge at infinity.

Theorem
1.2
is a particular case of the next theorem.
If
\Omega
is multiply-connected,
the outlier processes do not converge in general.
Instead, there is a whole family of possible limiting processes.
We identify these and characterize subsequential convergence.

Theorem 1.4

(Outliers in general regions)
.

Suppose
\mu
is an admissible probability
measure and
let
\Omega

be a connected component of

\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,(\mu)
.
Let us fix
z_{1},\dots,z_{l}
in
the interior of each of the holes of
\Omega
, i.e.,
in the bounded connected components
of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, and
let
q_{1},\dots,q_{l}
be the
\mu
-mass of each
(closed) hole.
If, along some subsequence,

(1.6)

(e^{2\pi i\kappa_{N}q_{1}},\dots,e^{2\pi i\kappa_{N}q_{l}})\xrightarrow{\phantom{aaa}}(e^{2\pi iQ_{1}},\dots,e^{2\pi iQ_{l}})

for some

\mathbf{Q}=(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{l})\in\mathbb{(}{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})^{l}
,
then, along the same subsequence

\Phi_{N}\xrightarrow{\phantom{aa}}\Pi_{\Omega,\mathbf{Q}}\mbox{ in law},

where
\Pi_{{\mathbf{Q}},\Omega}
is the weighted Bergman
point process on
\Omega
associated with the weight

(1.7)

\omega_{{\bf Q}}(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{l}|z-z_{i}|^{-2Q_{i}}.

The weighted Bergman kernel and its associated point
process will be described in Section
2.1
.
Theorem
1.2

is a particular case of this result with
l=1
and
\kappa_{N}=N+1
.
Notice that by the uniqueness of the limit, the limiting
point process does not depend on the particular
choice of the points
(z_{i})_{i=1}^{l}

(for a direct argument, see
Proposition
2.2

and Proposition
2.5
).
Notice also that if
{\bf Q}\neq{\bf Q}^{\prime}

as elements of the torus
({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})^{l}
,
then the limiting processes are distinct
(see Proposition
2.5
).

When there are several uncharged components, especially if these have common
boundary points, it is natural to ask if the limiting processes interact
with each other.
The following theorem answers this question.

Theorem 1.5

(Independence between components)
.

Suppose
\mu
is an admissible probability
measure.
Let
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
be any two connected
components of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,(\mu)
and denote
by
\Phi_{1,N}
and

\Phi_{2,N}
the associated outlier processes.
Along a subsequence for which (
1.6
)
holds for both components, with some
{\bf Q}_{1}

and
{\bf Q}_{2}
,
we have that

(\Phi_{1,N},\Phi_{2,N})\xrightarrow{\phantom{law}}(\Pi_{{\bf Q}_{1},\Omega_{1}},\Pi_{{\bf Q}_{2},\Omega_{2}})\quad\text{ in law},

where

\Pi_{{\bf Q}_{1},\Omega_{1}}
and
\Pi_{{\bf Q}_{2},\Omega_{2}}

are independent.

1.3.
Outliers for random zeros

The Bergman universality of the outliers processes
goes beyond the weakly confined Coulomb gas model.
In support of this claim, we will show that the zero
set for one model of random polynomials exhibits the same feature.
We focus on this particular model for simplicity
but the result should hold
in larger generality, for instance,
for Weyl-type Gaussian polynomials
defined with respect to smooth exponentially varying weights.
In addition, the proof of
Theorem
1.6
below
should be possible to adapt to the setting of
a finitely connected uncharged
region.
This is related to the paper by Katori and Shirai
[
21
]
,
which deals with zeros of random
Laurent series with i.i.d. coefficients.

The model we consider
belongs to a family of random polynomials
introduced by Zeitouni and Zelditch
[
32
]
.
Denote by
\nu
a compactly supported probability measure on
{\mathbb{C}}
, and
fix an orthonormal basis
(Q_{k,N})_{k=0}^{N}
for the space

{\mathbb{C}}_{N}[z]
of polynomials
of degree at most
N
, endowed with the inner product
of
L^{2}({\mathbb{C}},e^{-2NU^{\nu}}{\mathrm{d}}\nu)
.
The random polynomial of degree
N
associated with
\nu
is the
linear combination of basis elements

(1.8)

P_{N}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\xi_{k}Q_{k,N},

where
(\xi_{k})_{k}
is a sequence of i.i.d. standard complex
Gaussians,
\xi_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)
.

The law of
P_{N}
does not depend
on the specific orthonormal basis

(Q_{k,N})_{k=0}^{N}
we choose.
The zero set
\Psi_{N}
of
P_{N}
forms
a point process in the plane, which shares many features
with a weakly confined Coulomb gas whose background measure is
\nu
.
This was noticed in
[
32
]
and
is discussed more thoroughly in
[
7
]
.
In particular, as
N
tends to infinity, the points of

\Psi_{N}
distribute according to the measure

\nu
and there is a large number of outliers.

We consider the class of measures

{\mathrm{d}}\nu=v\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma_{\Gamma}

where
v
is a strictly positive and real-analytic
density
with respect to the arc length measure
\sigma_{\Gamma}
on
a simple closed analytic curve
\Gamma
.
Let
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
denote the components of

\hat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Gamma
, and
\Xi_{j,N}=\Psi_{N}\cap\Omega_{j}
the point process of
outliers in
\Omega_{j}
.

Theorem 1.6
.

As
N\to\infty
, we have the convergence

(\Xi_{1,N},\Xi_{2,N})\xrightarrow{\phantom{law}}(\Pi_{\Omega_{1}},\Pi_{\Omega_{2}})\quad\text{ in law},

where the Bergman point processes

\Pi_{\Omega_{1}}
and

\Pi_{\Omega_{2}}
are independent.

We believe that Bergman universality for outliers should hold
also for more general measures
\nu
which are
not supported on a curve. However,
we will not pursue this here.

1.4.
Description of the approach

Our proofs are based on showing convergence of kernels: the correlation
kernels of the determinantal point processes for the jellium, and the
covariance kernels for the random polynomials. In the first case,
locally uniform convergence towards the limiting Bergman kernel implies
the convergence of the corresponding point process. In the second case,
locally uniform convergence of the covariance kernels to the Szegő
kernel implies the convergence of the (analytic) Gaussian fields,
which in turn implies the convergence of the zero processes.
Here we discuss only the first case, and moreover assume for the
sake of simplicity that
{\bf Q}=0
.

For a description of the kernel
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}(z,w)
corresponding to
the weakly confined jellium with
N
particles, see
Proposition
A.2
. However, we will work with a
modification of it
\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,w)
which is analytic in
(z,\bar{w})
.
Our goal is to show the locally uniform convergence

\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,w)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}{\mathcal{B}}_{\Omega}(z,w),\qquad z,w\in\Omega,

where
{\mathcal{B}}_{\Omega}
is the Bergman kernel of
\Omega
.
In fact, the analytic nature of the kernels implies it is
sufficient to prove convergence on the diagonal
w=z
.

An upper bound
\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,z)={\mathcal{K}}_{N}(z,z)\leq{\mathcal{B}}_{\Omega}(z,z)

on the diagonal follows from the extremal representation of
the kernels (Lemma
2.6
), and Montel’s theorem
reduces the task to proving the
pointwise
lower bound

(1.9)

\liminf_{N\to\infty}{\mathcal{K}}_{N}(z,z)\geq{\mathcal{B}}_{\Omega}(z,z),\qquad z\in\Omega.

We recall that

{\mathcal{B}}_{\Omega}(z,z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\psi_{k}(z)\right|^{2},\qquad z\in\Omega,

where
\left\{\psi_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 1}
is (any) orthonormal
basis for the Bergman space
A^{2}(\Omega)
, and similarly
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}(z,z)

can be expressed in terms of an appropriate sum of polynomials.
In order to obtain the lower bound (
1.9
),
we show that for a proper choice of
\psi_{k}
-s one can find,
for every
k_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
fixed, an orthonormal set

\left\{P_{k,N}\right\}_{k\leq k_{0}}
in

L^{2}_{N}({\mathbb{C}},e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}}{\mathrm{d}}m_{\mathbb{C}})

consisting of polynomials in
{\mathbb{C}}_{N-1}[z]
, such that,

\left|P_{k,N}(z)\right|^{2}e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}(z)}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\left|\psi_{k}(z)\right|^{2},\qquad z\in\Omega,\quad k\leq k_{0}.

Taking the limit
k_{0}\to\infty
we obtain
(
1.9
).
We emphasize these polynomials are not the
standard orthogonal polynomials
(ordered by degree), but a family adapted to
the basis
\left\{\psi_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 1}
.

The method we use to obtain the polynomials

P_{k,N}
is based on the approach developed in
by Hedenmalm and Wennman
[
14
]
(see also
[
16
]
for a less
technical exposition), which loosely
goes back to e.g. Berman’s paper
[
6
]
and the work
[
3
]

of Ameur, Hedenmalm and Makarov, respectively,
and even to Hörmander’s paper

[
19
]
. This uses a polynomial approximation
approach using Hörmander
\bar{\partial}
-estimates and
certain harmonic continuation properties
of the potential (Proposition
2.7
and Lemmas

3.10
and

3.11
). These technical steps
are responsible for our regularity assumptions, but we believe
this is excessive.
We should stress that, due to the weak confinement,
there are considerable simplifications in the
construction of approximately orthonormal
holomorphic functions when compared to
[
14
]
.
On the other hand, to allow us to consider bounded (cf.
[
15
]
)
and multiply connected uncharged domains,
we have to work with more flexible bases
than the one given by standard orthogonal polynomials.

1.5.
Remarks and related work

1.5.1.
Previous work

If we specialize to the case when
\mu
is
the equilibrium measure of a compact set
K
,
then Theorem
1.2
is
essentially contained in Sinclair
and Yattselev’s work
[
30
]
. In this work, they also
obtain detailed near-boundary blow-up asymptotics
for the correlation kernel. For the case of
outliers in an annulus, one can obtain
Theorem
1.4

by applying methods similar to

[
7
,
11
]
.
Coulomb gases subject to a different scale of boundary confinement
is analyzed in the paper
[
2
]
by Ameur, Kang and Seo.

Random polynomials similar to (
1.8
)
have appeared in the literature
previously, see, e.g., the work

[
27
]
of Shiffman-Zelditch.
However, in their work the weight
function is not varying with
N
, which simplifies matters.
As a consequence, deriving Theorem
1.6

can be done more directly using
general considerations on the Szegő kernel,
and in this context Shiffman and Zelditch
obtained very precise results on the covariance
kernels after a blowup. Note that in the special
case when
\nu
is the equilibrium
measure of a Jordan curve, the potential
U^{\nu}
is constant,
so this particular
instance becomes a special case of
[
27
]
.

1.5.2.
Some natural extensions

For bounded uncharged regions
\Omega
,
the assumption in Theorem
1.4

that
\mu
is a probability measure is not necessary,
and we make it only for convenience.
Indeed, for an external field
V
of
sufficient growth at infinity, we let
\mu_{V}

denote the associated equilibrium measure
\mu_{V}
in the sense of
Saff and Totik
[
26
]
. Assume that
\Omega

is a bounded finitely connected component of

{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{V})
such that

\Delta V=2\pi\mu_{V}
in a neighborhood of
\overline{\Omega}
,
so that in particular
\Delta V=0
in
\Omega
. Then, the proof of
Theorem
1.4

goes through word for word, provided that

\mu_{V}
satisfies an analogous regularity
condition to Definition
1.1
.

Finally, we would like to point out that
Theorem
1.4

could be generalized
to deal with line bundles over
Riemann surfaces as in the framework described by Berman

[
6
]
. In this geometric setting,
the non-negativity condition of
\mu

becomes a non-negativity condition
on the curvature of the Hermitian line bundle and the
limiting point processes
obtained are similar to
the ones described in Section
2.2
.

1.5.3.
Further questions

In this paper, we have only analyzed limiting
outlier processes as
N\to\infty
.
It is natural to also ask if it is possible to define
outliers also for finite
N
, but we do not
attempt to do this here. Other interesting
questions that we do no pursue here concern the blow-up behavior of

\Theta_{N}
near
\partial\Omega
, and possible limiting outlier
processes for weakly confined Coulomb gases at other inverse temperatures.
Finally, Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.4

should hold under considerably less restrictive regularity conditions
on the measure
\mu
. We have made no attempt
at optimizing our argument in this direction.

1.6.
Notation

We write
{\mathbb{T}},{\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\mathbb{C}}
for the unit circle, unit disk,
real line and complex plane, respectively.
The extended complex plane
(or Riemann sphere) is denoted by
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
.
We recall the definition
of the Wirtinger derivatives,

\partial=\partial_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\partial_{x}-{i}\partial_{y}\Big),\qquad\bar{\partial}=\partial_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\partial_{x}+{i}\partial_{y}\Big),\qquad z=x+{i}y.

The Cauchy-Riemann equations are encoded in the operator
\bar{\partial}

so that a complex differentiable function
f

on a complex domain is holomorphic if
\bar{\partial}f=0
, and
its complex derivative is given by
f^{\prime}(z)=\partial f(z)
.

We will make frequent use of standard
notation to compare asymptotic quantities.
Specifically
f=\mathrm{O}(g)
and

a\lesssim b
mean that
f/g
and
a/b
are bounded
under the limit procedure in question. The notation
f=\mathrm{o}(g)
means that

f/g\to 0
, while
a\asymp b
means that
a\lesssim b
and
b\lesssim a
holds
simultaneously. If, for two functions
f
and
g
,
the quotient
f/g
is a non-zero
constant, we write
f\propto g
.

We denote the
class of
x
in
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
as

\{x\}=x\,\mathrm{mod}\,1\in{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}.

If we have a sequence

as a real number.
In other words, we

.

We will use the notation

for the

2.
Background on Bergman spaces and potential theory

In this section we have collected preliminary
material on Bergman spaces and potential theory.
We begin in Section
2.1
by
recalling the standard notion
of Bergman spaces and Bergman kernels
in the weighted setting. Then,
in Section
2.2
,
we consider
log-harmonic weights and define
the notion of a Bergman point process that appear in
our theorems. Section
2.4

is about an harmonic extension of the potential while
Section
2.5

deals with finding an equivalent kernel.

2.1.
Bergman spaces and kernels

Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{C}

be an open set and let
\rho
be a continuous
positive
weight function
on

\mathcal{D}
. The Bergman space
A^{2}_{\rho}(\mathcal{D})
, also denoted
by
A^{2}(\mathcal{D},\rho)
, is
the collection of all holomorphic functions
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{D},\rho)
. It is well-known
that
A^{2}_{\rho}(\mathcal{D})
is a reproducing kernel
Hilbert space, and we denote its reproducing kernel by

K_{\rho,\mathcal{D}}(z,w)
.
More precisely,
K_{\rho,\mathcal{D}}
is the
kernel of
the orthogonal projection of

L^{2}(\mathcal{D},\rho)
onto
A^{2}_{\rho}(\mathcal{D})

and if
(\varphi_{k})_{k\geq 1}

is an orthonormal basis of
A^{2}_{\rho}(\mathcal{D})
then

K_{\rho,\mathcal{D}}(z,w)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\varphi_{k}(z)\overline{\varphi_{k}(w)},

where the convergence in the series is locally
uniform.
We denote by
{\mathcal{K}}_{\rho,\mathcal{D}}
the weighted

correlation kernel
, which is given by

{\mathcal{K}}_{\rho,\mathcal{D}}(z,w)=K_{\rho,\mathcal{D}}(z,w)\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}(z)\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}(w).

When the weight
\rho
involves a parameter
N
, we will
write
K_{N}
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}
for the kernels,
and
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{N}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{N}

for the
L^{2}
-norm and inner product,
respectively, when no confusion should occur.

For weakly confining weights
e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}}
, the
Bergman space
A^{2}({\mathbb{C}},e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}})

coincides with the
N
-dimensional space
{\mathbb{C}}_{N-1}[z]
of polynomials
of degree at most
N-1
(see (
3.1
) below).
If
\mathcal{D}
is not the whole space, then
there may of course be non-polynomial elements
of
A^{2}(\mathcal{D},e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}})
,
and we instead introduce the polynomial subspaces

A^{2}_{N}(\mathcal{D},e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}}):=A^{2}(\mathcal{D},e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}})\cap{\mathbb{C}}_{N-1}[z].

2.2.
Bergman point processes on an uncharged background

Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{C}
be an open set
and fix a positive weight function
\rho
on
\mathcal{D}
.

Definition 2.1
.

The
weighted Bergman point process

\Pi_{\rho,\mathcal{D}}

associated with the weight
\rho
is
the determinantal point process on
\mathcal{D}

associated with the kernel

\mathcal{K}_{\rho,\mathcal{D}}
and
the Lebesgue measure restricted
to
\mathcal{D}
.

When the weight is constant
\rho\equiv 1
, we write simply

\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}
for the

(unweighted) Bergman point process
.
For weights
\omega_{\mathbf{Q}}
of the form
(
1.7
) on a domain
\Omega
,
we denote the weighted Bergman point process by

\Pi_{\mathbf{Q},\Omega}
. As the notation
suggests,
\Pi_{\mathbf{Q},\Omega}

only depends on the class
\mathbf{Q}
of
the weight
\omega_{\mathbf{Q}}
.
The remainder of this section is devoted to
analyzing such processes and the
associated kernels.

The following proposition
shows that the law of
\Pi_{\rho}=\Pi_{\rho,{\mathcal{D}}}
is stable under a
wide class of perturbations of the weight.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{C}

be an open set and let
\rho
be a continuous strictly positive
function on
\mathcal{D}
.
Fix a zero-free holomorphic function
f

on
\mathcal{D}
and let

\rho_{f}=|f|^{2}\rho
. Then,

\Pi_{\rho}=\Pi_{\rho_{f}}\quad\text{ in law}.

In particular,

\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(z,z)=\mathcal{K}_{\rho_{f}}(z,z)
for every

z\in\mathcal{D}
. Moreover, if
\rho

and
\widetilde{\rho}
are two strictly positive
continuous weights on
\mathcal{D}
such that

\Pi_{\rho}=\Pi_{\widetilde{\rho}}
in law and if we suppose that

\Pi_{\rho}
is not the empty point process, then

\widetilde{\rho}=|f|^{2}\rho

for some zero-free holomorphic function

f
on
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

Let us first prove the first part.
Suppose that
\rho
is continuous and strictly positive
and that
f
is zero-free and holomorphic.
By the definition of determinantal point processes,
we need to show that

\det\left(\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(x_{i},x_{j})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\right)=\det\left(\mathcal{K}_{\rho_{f}}(x_{i},x_{j})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\right)

for every
k\geq 1
and any points

x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in\mathcal{D}
.
But, since
\rho_{f}=|f|^{2}\rho
,
the map given by

g\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D},\rho_{f})\mapsto fg\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D},\rho)

is a well-defined isometry
that sends

A^{2}_{\rho_{f}}(\mathcal{D})
onto

A^{2}_{\rho}(\mathcal{D})

so that the corresponding orthogonal projections
are conjugates of each other,
which is written as

K_{\rho}(z,w)=f(z)K_{\rho_{f}}(z,w)f(w)^{-1}

for every
z,w\in\mathcal{D}
. By the definition of

\mathcal{K}
, this implies that

\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(z,w)=\frac{f(z)}{|f(z)|}\mathcal{K}_{\rho_{f}}(z,w)\frac{\overline{f(w)}}{|f(w)|},

for every
z,w\in\mathcal{D}
, which
implies that the laws of

\Pi_{\rho}
and
\Pi_{\rho_{f}}
are the same
and that

\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(z,z)=\mathcal{K}_{\rho_{f}}(z,z)
for every
z\in\mathcal{D}
.

For the second part, let

\rho
and
\widetilde{\rho}
be two
continuous and strictly positive weights on
\mathcal{D}

such that
\Pi_{\rho}=\Pi_{\rho_{f}}
in law.
For simplification, we use the notation

K=K_{\rho}
and

\widetilde{K}=K_{\widetilde{\rho}}
for the kernels, which
we recall are
holomorphic in the first argument
and anti-holomorphic in the second.
We also use the notation

\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\rho}

and
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}={\mathcal{K}}_{\widetilde{\rho}}
for the weighted (correlation) kernels.
The equality in law
\Pi_{\rho}=\Pi_{\widetilde{\rho}}
implies that

\det\left(\mathcal{K}(z_{i},z_{j})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\right)=\det\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(z_{i},z_{j})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\right).

for every
k\geq 1
and

z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\in\mathcal{D}
.
In particular, we have that

\mathcal{K}(z,z)=\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(z,z)

while

\mathcal{K}(z,z)\mathcal{K}(w,w)-\mathcal{K}(z,w)\mathcal{K}(w,z)=\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(z,z)\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(w,w)-\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(z,w)\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(w,z)

for every
z,w\in\mathcal{D}
, which are the
k=1

and
k=2
cases.
Since the kernels are Hermitian, these two
cases imply
that
|\mathcal{K}(z,w)|=|\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(z,w)|

for every
z,w\in\mathcal{D}
. Then,

(2.1)

|K(z,w)|\rho(z)\rho(w)=|\widetilde{K}(z,w)|\widetilde{\rho}(z)\widetilde{\rho}(w)

for every
z,w\in\mathcal{D}
. Now, either

K
and
\widetilde{K}

are zero everywhere
or there is some

w_{0}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
the maps

z\mapsto K(z,w_{0})
and

z\mapsto\widetilde{K}(z,w_{0})

are non-zero holomorphic functions whose
zero sets coincide (and are discrete).
The first case is discarded because
\Pi_{\rho}

is not the empty process so that we can
consider the meromorphic function
f
defined by

f(z)=K(z,w_{0})\rho(w_{0})/(\widetilde{K}(z,w_{0})\widetilde{\rho}(w_{0}))
.
Then, (
2.1
) implies that

|f(z)|=\frac{\widetilde{\rho}(z)}{\rho(z)}

for every
z\in\mathcal{D}
such that

\widetilde{K}(z,w_{0})\neq 0
.
Since
\widetilde{\rho}(z)/\rho(z)
is locally bounded,
f

has only removable singularities
so that it can be extended holomorphically to
\mathcal{D}

and, by continuity,
|f|=\widetilde{\rho}/\rho
on
\mathcal{D}
.
Taking logarithms completes the proof.
∎

Write
\rho=e^{-2V}
for
some continuous function

V
on
\mathcal{D}
. We say that
\rho

is log-harmonic if
V
is harmonic, i.e.,
\Delta V=0
.
In view of Gauss’s law of electrostatics,

V
can be thought of as a possible potential
for an uncharged domain

which makes
it plausible that the
weights
\rho=e^{-2V}
are the relevant ones
in the context of outliers.

Remark 2.3

(Flat line bundles)
.

The notion of a log-harmonic measure
\rho

makes sense on any open Riemann surface
M

since any holomorphic change of coordinates
preserves the log-harmonicity.
In this way, Definition
2.1

can be used to define an analogous family of
Bergman point processes on
M

and
Proposition
2.2
also holds in this case.
Moreover,
the freedom described in
Proposition
2.2

is closely related
to the classification of flat Hermitian line bundles
on
M
. This is not a coincidence
since, by noticing that
every line bundle is trivial on the open Riemann surface
M
,
we may rephrase
our setting by considering Hermitian line bundles
on
M
similarly to
[
6
]
and the log-harmonic measures
would essentially play the role of
flat Hermitian metrics.
In particular, the set of equivalence
classes obtained by
making two log-harmonic measures

\mu
and
\widetilde{\mu}
equivalent if
and only if

for some
zero-free holomorphic function

on
M

is in one-to-one correspondence with the set
of morphisms from the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(M)

to the unit circle
U(1)

(the Pontryagin dual of
\pi_{1}(M)
).
This is stated
for a particular case
in Proposition
2.5
.

Below, we will consider domains appearing as uncharged
regions, and weights of the following form. If
\Omega
is
an
l
-connected domain,
we let

z_{1},\dots,z_{l}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
be a collection of points,
one in each hole of
\Omega
, and let

V(z)={\bf Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)
for some

\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
,
where we have defined

\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)=\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}_{z_{1},\dots,z_{l}}(z):=\Big(\log|z-z_{1}|,\dots,\log|z-z_{l}|\Big).

This gives us a

),

Suppose, for simplicity,
that the holes of
\Omega

have non-empty interior. Then,
by Axler
[
5
, p. 248]
and
the previous proposition, it follows that
for every harmonic function

\Pi_{e^{-2V}}=\Pi_{e^{-2\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}}}
.
Moreover, for general

\Pi_{\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)}

(e^{2\pi iQ_{1}},\dots,e^{2\pi iQ_{l}})

.
For this reason, there is no harm
in thinking about

.
To keep the notation short, we will denote the Bergman process

\Pi_{e^{-2\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)}}

\mathcal{K}_{\omega_{\mathbf{Q}},\Omega}

.
We notice that although the kernel

Remark 2.4

(Conformal equivariance of weighted Bergman processes)
.

Suppose that
\phi
is a conformal mapping of a domain

\Omega_{1}
onto another domain
\Omega_{2}
.
Then, for any weight
\omega
on
\Omega_{1}
,
we have
\phi(\Pi_{\omega,\Omega_{1}})=\Pi_{\omega\circ\phi,\Omega_{2}}
.
In particular, if
\Omega_{1}
is
l
-connected, then
for any
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{(}{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})^{l}
, there exists

\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{(}{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})^{l}

(directly determined by
\bf Q
and

)
such that
\phi(\Pi_{\Omega_{1},\mathbf{Q}})=\Pi_{\Omega_{2},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}}
in law.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}
denote the space
of point configurations on
\Omega

(see Section

) and let

Proposition 2.5

(Continuous injective

.

The function defined by

is continuous. Moreover,
the map from

that associates

The injectivity
is a consequence of

is not the modulus

.

.
∎

2.3.
An upper bound on one-point functions

Next, we state a lemma
indicating a relation between Bergman kernels
with log-harmonic weights
and the kernel for the outlier process.
We use the notations of
Section
1
.
Take an

l
-connected component

\Omega

of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,(\mu)
, where
\mu

is admissible, and
points
z_{1},\dots,z_{l}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega

in each of the holes of
\Omega
.
Denote
the
\mu
-mass of the
i
-th hole of
\Omega

by
q_{i}
and put
{\bf q}=(q_{i})_{i}
.

Lemma 2.6

(Diagonal domination)
.

For every
N\geq 1

and
z\in\Omega

{\mathcal{K}}_{N}(z,z)\leq{\mathcal{B}}_{\Omega,{\kappa_{\vskip 2.84526ptN}}{\bf q}}(z,z).

Proof.

We start by noticing that, for weakly confining
weights
\omega_{N}=e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}}
, the
Bergman space
A^{2}({\mathbb{C}},\omega_{N})

coincides with the
N
-dimensional space
{\mathbb{C}}_{N-1}[z]
of polynomials
of degree at most
N-1
(see (
3.1
) below
combined with Liouville’s theorem), hence
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}={\mathcal{K}}_{\omega_{N},{\mathbb{C}}}
.
In addition, we notice that

{\mathcal{B}}_{\Omega,\kappa_{N}{\bf q}}(z,z)={\mathcal{K}}_{{\omega_{N}}|_{\Omega},\Omega}(z,z)
. This is a consequence
of Lemma
2.9

together with
Proposition
2.2
.
If we denote by

\|f\|_{N}
the norm of

f
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\omega_{N})

and by

\lVert f\rVert_{N,\Omega}

the norm of

f|_{\Omega}
in

L^{2}(\Omega,\omega_{N}|_{\Omega})
,
we can write the extremal representation of the kernels

{\mathcal{K}}_{N}(z,z)=\sup_{f\in A^{2}_{\omega_{N}}({\mathbb{C}})}\frac{|f(z)|^{2}\omega_{N}(z)}{\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{N}}\quad\text{ and }\quad{\mathcal{K}}_{{\omega_{N}}|_{\Omega},\Omega}(z,z)=\hskip-8.53581pt\sup_{f\in A^{2}(\Omega,\omega_{N}|_{\Omega})}\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{|f(z)|^{2}\omega_{N}(z)}{\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{N,\Omega}}.

Then, since

and

,
we find the sought inequality.
∎

2.4.
Harmonic extension of potentials.

Assume that
f
is a holomorphic function on a domain
\Omega
, and that
for some
\varepsilon>0
and
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
,

I:=\partial\Omega\cap{\mathbb{D}}(z_{0},\varepsilon)

is a real-analytic arc. By Carathéodory’s theorem,

f
extends continuously to
I
. Suppose further that

f
maps
\Omega\cap{\mathbb{D}}(z_{0},\varepsilon)

univalently onto a domain in the unit disk,
such that
f(I)
is a sub-arc of the circle.
Then
f
extends to a univalent function of a neighborhood
of
I
(see, for instance, Pommerenke’s book
[
25
, Theorem 6.2]
).
In fact, similar extension properties hold for
any holomorphic or harmonic function which is
defined on one side of an analytic curve with
real-analytic extension to the boundary.
We will routinely apply such extensions,
and will usually not make any distinction between
the original function and the extended one in terms of notation.

The only function we will deal with for which the extension property
is not entirely obvious is the potential
U^{\mu}
.

Proposition 2.7

(Harmonic extension)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.4
,
the harmonic function
U^{\mu}|_{\Omega}
extends
past
\partial\Omega
to a harmonic function
{\mathcal{U}}={\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}

on an open set
\Omega^{\prime}
containing
\overline{\Omega}
.

This result should be absolutely standard, but since we did not locate
an appropriate reference we supply a proof in
Appendix
B
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\Gamma
be a boundary component of

\partial\Omega
and let
z_{0}\in\Gamma
.
Denote by
{\rm n}_{0}
the outward unit
normal to
\Omega
at
z_{0}
.
Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.4
,
we have

(2.2)

\big(U^{\mu}-\mathcal{U}^{\mu}\big)(z_{0}+t{\rm n}_{z_{0}})=\begin{cases}c_{1}t+\mathrm{O}(t^{2}),&\mu(\Gamma)>0\\
c_{2}t^{2}+\mathrm{O}(t^{3}),&\mu(\Gamma)=0,\end{cases}

for
0\leq t\leq t_{0}
, where
c_{i}>0
.
In addition, in each case the implicit constant
is uniform for
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, and
c_{i}=c_{i}(z_{0})

depends smoothly on
z_{0}
.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
z_{0}=0
and that
the normal points in the imaginary direction. We denote the relevant
connected component of
\partial\Omega
by
\Gamma
.
We split
U^{\mu}
as
U^{\mu_{1}}+U^{\mu_{2}}
where

\mu_{1}
has a density with respect
to
1_{\Omega}dm
locally near
0
, and where
\mu_{2}
is supported on

\Gamma
. Similarly, we let

\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{1}+\mathcal{U}_{2}
, where
\mathcal{U}_{i}
are harmonic
in
\Omega
and
U^{\mu_{i}}-\mathcal{U}_{i}=0
on
\Omega
(that this splitting
of the harmonic extensions is possible follows from the proof of
Proposition
2.7
). We perform a Taylor expansion
at
\Gamma
of each contribution
U^{\mu_{i}}-\mathcal{U}_{i}
separately.
We first find that
(U^{\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{U}_{1})
vanishes on
\Omega

and is
C^{1,1}
–smooth,
so its gradient also vanishes along
\Gamma
.
Since
\Gamma
is tangent to the real line at
0
, a Taylor expansion
shows that
\partial_{x}^{2}(U^{\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{U}_{1})(0)
equals the
second order tangential derivative of
U^{\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{U}_{1}
at
0
,
which implies that
\partial_{x}^{2}(U^{\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{U}_{1})(0)=0
.
This observation allows us to rewrite the second
derivative

\partial_{y}^{2}(U^{\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{U}_{1})(0)=\partial_{x}^{2}(U^{\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{U}_{1})(0)+\partial_{y}^{2}(U^{\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{U}_{1})(0)=\Delta U^{\mu_{1}}(0),

and hence, we obtain

(U^{\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{U}_{1})({i}y)=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{y}(U^{\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{U}_{1})(0)y^{2}+\mathrm{O}(y^{3})=\frac{1}{2}\Delta U^{\mu_{1}}(0)y^{2}+\mathrm{O}(y^{3}),\qquad y>0.

If
v\equiv 0
on
\Gamma
, we are done.
If not, we have
v(0)>0
,
in which case we have

(2.3)

(U^{\mu_{2}}-\mathcal{U}_{2})(x+{i}y)=(2\pi)v(0)y+\mathrm{O}(y^{2}),\qquad y>0.

Hence, we have established the desired formula also in this case.

By compactness of
\Gamma
, the error terms are uniform provided
that
U^{\mu_{i}}-\mathcal{U}_{i}|_{\Omega^{c}}
are
C^{3}
-smooth in a
one-sided neighborhood of
\Gamma
. But in fact
these functions extend real-analytically to a full
neighborhood of
\Gamma
, so the uniformity claim follows. That
c_{i}

depend smoothly on
z_{0}\in\Gamma
is likewise clear.
∎

2.5.
A transformation of the kernel

We recall that
for an
l
-connected component

\Omega

of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,(\mu)
, where
\mu

is admissible, we fixed
points
z_{1},\dots,z_{l}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega

in each of the holes of
\Omega
.
The mass of the
i
-th hole of
\Omega

is denoted by
q_{i}
, and we put
{\bf q}=(q_{i})
.
We let
\Omega^{\prime}\supset\overline{\Omega}

be as in Section
2.4
, so that the
harmonic extension
{\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}
of
U^{\mu}|_{\Omega}

is harmonic on the domain
\Omega^{\prime}
.

The following result is a direct consequence
of the
logarithmic
conjugation theorem
from Axler

[
5
, p. 248]
.

Lemma 2.9
.

There exists a holomorphic function
\mathfrak{h}
on
\Omega^{\prime}
such that

{\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}(z)-\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)=\operatorname{Re}\mathfrak{h}(z),

so that we have

(2.4)

e^{-\kappa_{N}{\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}(z)}=e^{-\{\kappa_{N}{\bf q}\}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)}\,\Big|e^{-\kappa_{N}\mathfrak{h}(z)}\prod_{i=1}^{l}(z-z_{i})^{-(\kappa_{N}q_{i}-\{\kappa_{N}q_{i}\})}\Big|.

We introduce the
transformed correlation kernel

(2.5)

\widehat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{N}(z,w)=e^{-{i}\kappa_{N}\operatorname{Im}(\mathfrak{h}(z))}{\mathcal{K}}_{N}(z,w)e^{{i}\kappa_{N}\operatorname{Im}(\mathfrak{h}(w))},\qquad(z,w)\in\Omega^{\prime}.

In the appendix we recall some basic properties of determinantal processes,
including the fact (
A.1
)
that
if
c:\Omega\to R
is a continuous function, then
the kernels
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}(z,w)
and

\widehat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{N}(z,w):=e^{{i}c(z)}{\mathcal{K}}_{N}(z,w)e^{-{i}c(w)},

determine the same determinantal point process.
Hence, the restrictions of the kernels
\widehat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{N}
and

{\mathcal{K}}_{N}
to
\Omega\times\Omega

induce the same process.

We obtain a holomorphic kernel after division
by
(\omega_{\{\kappa_{N}{\bf q}\}}(z)\omega_{\{\kappa_{N}{\bf q}\}}(w))^{1/2}
, which
is locally uniformly bounded in
N
. So, we put

(2.6)

\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,w)=\widehat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{N}(z,w)\omega_{\{\kappa_{N}{\bf q}\}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(z)\omega_{\{\kappa_{N}{\bf q}\}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(w).

This allows us to use a normal family argument
to deduce precompactness of the family of transformed kernels
whenever
(\{\kappa_{N}{\bf q}\})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges.

3.
Convergence of weighted polynomial bases

3.1.
Construction of basis functions

Fix an admissible measure
\mu
and let

\Omega
be an
l
-connected uncharged
domain
let
q_{1},\dots,q_{l}
be the
\mu
-mass of each hole in

\Omega
and let
z_{1},\dots,z_{l}
be fixed points in each of the holes.
We recall from Section
1.6
that
the number
\{\kappa_{N}q_{j}\}\in{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}

is represented by the unique real number
(also denoted
\{\kappa_{N}q_{j}\}
)
such that
\kappa_{N}q_{j}-\{\kappa_{N}q_{j}\}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and

\{\kappa_{N}q_{j}\}\in[q-\frac{1}{2},q+\frac{1}{2})
,
where
q
is a representative of the limit.
To simplify matters, we abuse notation
and write that
(\{\kappa_{N}{\bf q}\})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}

converges towards
{\bf Q}\in[0,1)^{l}
,
which may only be true along a subsequence
\mathcal{L}
.

The point process
\Phi_{N}
is the determinantal process
associated with the correlation kernel
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}

described in Section
2.1
. The outlier process
is given by the restricted kernel
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}|_{\Omega\times\Omega}
,
which determines the same process as the restriction of the modified
kernel
\widehat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{N}(z,w)
.
To prove the convergence of the point process

\Psi_{N}
towards the Bergman point process

{\mathcal{B}}_{\Omega,{\bf Q}}
, due to Proposition
A.3
, it is sufficient to
show that the kernels
\widehat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{N}
converge
uniformly on any compact set of
\Omega\times\Omega
towards

{\mathcal{B}}_{\Omega,{\bf Q}}
. This is equivalent to asking that the modified
holomorphic kernels
\mathscr{K}_{N}
defined in (
2.6
)
converge locally uniformly
towards the holomorphic Bergman kernel
B_{\Omega,{\bf Q}}
.
The next normal families lemma takes advantage of the
holomorphicity of
\mathscr{K}_{N}
to obtain precompactness.
Once that is established,
we only need to show that there is only one possible limit point.

Lemma 3.1

(Normal families)
.

The sequence
(\mathscr{K}_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of holomorphic
kernels is precompact in the
compact-open topology.

Proof.

The kernels in the sequence
(\mathscr{K}_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}

are analytic in
(z,\bar{w})
.
By Montel’s theorem for holomorphic functions in several variables
(see e.g
[
22
, Ch. 1, Proposition 6]
), it is
enough to show that
(\mathscr{K}_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}

is uniformly bounded on any compact
subset of
\Omega\times\Omega
. By the reproducing kernel property and
the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for any
(z,w)\in\Omega\times\Omega
,

|\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,w)|^{2}\leq\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,z)\mathscr{K}_{N}(w,w).

So, it is sufficient to prove that the
kernels are uniformly bounded on the diagonal.
Combining Lemma
2.6
and Proposition
2.5

gives, on any compact set
K

\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,z)\leq{\mathcal{B}}_{\Omega,\{\kappa_{N}{\bf q}\}}(z,z)\leq\sup_{(z,\mathbf{Q})\in(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{l}\times K}{\mathcal{B}}_{\Omega,\mathbf{Q}}(z,z)<\infty.

∎

To obtain the main result, it is enough to show that
if
\{\kappa_{N}{\bf q}\}
converges to
{\bf Q}
,
then we have convergence of holomorphic kernels along the diagonals

\forall z\in\Omega,\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,z)=B_{\Omega,{\bf Q}}(z,z).

Indeed, if
\mathscr{K}
is the limit of
\mathscr{K}_{N}
:

\mathscr{K}(z,w)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,w).

then
\mathscr{K}
coincides with the kernel

B_{\Omega,{\bf Q}}
on the diagonal of
\Omega\times\Omega
,
and the next lemma shows that this is enough to complete the proof.

Lemma 3.2

(Lemma 2.5.1 from
[
18
]
, p.30)
.

Let
L(z,w)
be analytic in
z
and anti-analytic in
w

(i.e. analytic in
\bar{w}
) for
(z,w)\in\Omega\times\Omega
.
If
L(z,z)=0

\forall z\in\Omega
,
then
L(z,w)=0

\forall z,w\in\Omega
.

In the remaining part of the proof, we show the
pointwise convergence of the diagonal restrictions of
the holomorphic kernels
\mathscr{K}_{N}
towards
B_{\Omega,\bf Q}
.
In the next lemma, we construct a sequence of
orthonormal bases in which we will express
the weighted Bergman kernels
{\mathcal{B}}_{\Omega,\{\kappa_{N}{\bf q}\}}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

There exists an orthonormal basis

(\psi_{k})_{k}
of
A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\bf Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})
, such that
moreover
\psi_{k}\in A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\mathbf{Q}_{N}}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})
.
If
(\psi_{k,N})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is the orthonormal set obtained
from
(\psi_{k})_{k}
by
the Gram-Schmidt procedure in

A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2\mathbf{Q}_{N}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})
, we have

(i)

For any
z\in\Omega
,
\psi_{k,N}(z)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\psi_{k}(z)
;

(ii)

All the functions

\psi_{k,N}
and
\psi_{k}
for
k\leq k_{0}
extend holomorphically to
some domain
\Omega^{\prime}_{k_{0}}\supset\overline{\Omega}
.

(iii)

The functions
\psi_{k,N}
are locally uniformly bounded in
N
.

Remark 3.4
.

When working with a fixed
k_{0}
, we replace

\Omega^{\prime}
with
\Omega^{\prime}\cap\Omega^{\prime}_{k_{0}}
to simplify notation.

Remark 3.5
.

In the above construction, we only claim to obtain an orthonormal set

(\psi_{k,N})_{k}\subset A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\mathbf{Q}_{N}}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})
,
as opposed to a full orthonormal basis.
Indeed, the space
A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\mathbf{Q}_{N}}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})

can be larger in general. However, this is all we need for our proof.

We start a lemma which ensures that there exists an orthonormal
basis
(\phi_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
A^{2}(\Omega)

consisting of certain rational functions.
This is essentially an
L^{2}
-version of the
Mergelyan theorem in a smooth setting,
of which related versions have appeared previously
(see e.g. Biard, Fornæss and Wu

[
10
]
and the references therein).

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Omega
be an
l
-connected planar domain
with analytic boundary and let
z_{1},\dots,z_{l}
be fixed
points in each hole of
\Omega
. Then rational functions with poles at

z_{1},\dots,z_{l}
form a dense subspace of

A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\bf Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})
.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

Suppose first that
\Omega
is bounded.
We denote by
\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{l}

the holes of
\Omega
, i.e.,
the connected components of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
.
We use the notation
\Omega_{j}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{j}

for
j\in\{1,\dots,l\}

and define also
\Omega_{l+1}=\Omega\cup\mathcal{T}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{T}_{l}
.
The domains
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{l+1}

are simply connected (on the sphere),
they have non-empty intersection with
\Omega
, and each
share a single boundary component with
\Omega
and,
in fact,
\partial\Omega
is the disjoint union

\bigcup_{j=1}^{l+1}\partial\Omega_{j}
.

Denote by
f
an arbitrary element of

A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\bf Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})
.
By Cauchy’s integral formula,

f
can be decomposed as

f(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{l+1}\frac{1}{2\pi{i}}\int_{C_{j}}\frac{f(w)}{w-z}{\mathrm{d}}w=:\sum_{j=1}^{l+1}g_{j}(z).

where for
1\leq j\leq l+1
, the
C_{j}
are small inward
perturbations towards
\Omega
of the connected
components of
\partial\Omega
, taken with the standard orientation.
By deforming the contours
C_{j}
, the functions
g_{j}
are seen to be holomorphic
on
\Omega_{j}
.

We will show that
g_{j}
can be approximated by certain rational functions.
We treat the cases
1\leq j\leq l
and
j=l+1
separately.

Case 1.

We fix an arbitrary index
j
with
1\leq j\leq l
.
Since for all
k\neq j
, the function
g_{k}
is locally bounded in a neighborhood of

\partial\Omega_{j}
,
it follows that
g_{j}=f-\sum_{k\neq j}g_{k}
lies
in
A^{2}(\Omega_{j},|z-z_{j}|^{-2\alpha})
where

\alpha
is some arbitrary fixed strictly positive number.
Indeed, near
\partial\Omega_{j}
, we use the uniform boundedness of
g_{k}

for
k\neq j
along with the fact that the
f
lies in the
space
A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\bf Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})
,
and near infinity we use the facts that
|g_{j}|

decays like
1/|z|
and that
\alpha
is
strictly positive.

There exists a conformal mapping
\phi_{j}:\Omega_{j}\to{\mathbb{D}}

which extends holomorphically past the boundary,
where we choose the normalization
\phi_{j}(\infty)=0
.
Below we will prove that
g_{j}
can be arbitrarily well approximated in

A^{2}(\Omega_{j},|z-z_{j}|^{-2\alpha})

by functions of the form
\phi_{j}^{\prime}\phi_{j}^{k}
for appropriate powers
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
But then by Runge’s theorem, since
\phi_{j}
is holomorphic in
a neighborhood of
\Omega_{j}
, the function
\phi_{j}
can be approximated in the
uniform norm on a neighborhood of
\overline{\Omega}_{j}
by rational
functions with poles at
z_{j}
.
Finally, since
\Omega
is bounded, the same
statement holds in the norm in the space

A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\bf Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})
.

It remains to show that
\{\phi^{\prime}\phi_{j}^{k}:k\geq k_{0}\}
spans the space

A^{2}(\Omega_{j},|z-z_{j}|^{-2\alpha})
.
By a change of variables with the conformal map,
this follows from the fact that polynomials are dense in
A^{2}({\mathbb{D}},\omega)

for weights which are smooth and positive in a
neighborhood of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, which
is equivalent to the classical statement that
polynomials are dense in
A^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
(see e.g. the book
[
13
]

by Hedenmalm-Korenblum-Zhu).

Case 2.

This case is similar,
but we approximate
g
by polynomials.

It only remains to treat the case when
\Omega
is unbounded.
However, essentially the same proof works in that setting.
The only difference is that
there is no curve
C_{l+1}
around the point at infinity, and hence no function

g_{l+1}
to approximate.
∎

Below, we need means to control
basis elements of
A^{2}(\Omega,\rho)
at infinity, in the case
when
\Omega
is unbounded and
\rho

is a positive weight with
\rho(z)\asymp|z|^{\alpha}
as
z\to\infty
,
for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
We use the following well-known fact on removable singularities.

Every
g:{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{C}
holomorphic such that

\int_{\mathbb{D}}|g(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}m(z)<\infty

admits a holomorphic extension to
\mathbb{D}
.

In particular, for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
if
\int_{\mathbb{D}}|z|^{\gamma}|g(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}m(z)<\infty
then

|z|^{\gamma}|g(z)|^{2}\leq C/|z|^{2-\varepsilon}

for some constant
C>0
and near
z=0
.
Indeed, we may write

\gamma=2k+\xi
with
\xi\in(-2,0]
and

k\in\mathbb{Z}
so that

\int_{\mathbb{D}}|z^{k}g(z)|^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{D}}|z|^{\gamma}|g(z)|^{2}<\infty
.
The above claim on removability of singularities
implies that

|z^{k}g(z)|^{2}
is bounded around zero
which tells us that

|z|^{\gamma}|g(z)|^{2}=|z|^{\xi}|z^{k}g(z)|^{2}\leq C|z|^{\xi}=\frac{C}{|z|^{2-\varepsilon}}

for some constants
C,\varepsilon>0
and near
z=0
.

From the previous argument,
for a positive weight
\rho

satisfying
\rho(z)\asymp|z|^{\alpha}
as
z\to\infty
,
there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\alpha)>0
such that
if
f\in A^{2}(\Omega,\rho)
then

(3.1)

|f(z)|^{2}\rho(z)\lesssim|z|^{-2-\varepsilon}.

Indeed, for
g(z)=f(1/z)
we would have

\int|g(z)|^{2}|z|^{-\alpha-4}\mathrm{d}m(z)<\infty

which would imply that

|g(z)|^{2}|z|^{-\alpha-4}\leq C/|z|^{2+\varepsilon}
.
By going back to
f
,
this is precisely (
3.1
).

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Let
{\bf Q}_{N}
be the sequence of elements of

({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})^{l}
which converge towards

{\bf Q}\in[0,1)^{l}
.
We first notice that for
N
large enough

A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\bf Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})\subset A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\bf Q}_{N}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)}).

Indeed, if
\Omega
is bounded, this is simply the fact that
\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)

is bounded on
\Omega
. If
instead
\Omega
contains a neighborhood of infinity,
(
3.1
)
implies that for any
f\in A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\bf Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})
,

|f(z)|^{2}e^{-2{\bf Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)}\leq C|z|^{-2-\varepsilon},

for a fixed
\varepsilon
,
so that since
\|{\bf Q}-{\bf Q}_{N}\|_{1}<\varepsilon
for
N
large enough,

f
belongs to the space
A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\bf Q}_{N}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})
as well.

Denote by
(\psi_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
an orthonormal basis of

A^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\bf Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}z})

consisting of rational functions with poles at the
z_{j}
,
the existence of which is
guaranteed by Lemma
3.6
.
We define
(\psi_{k,N})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
as the orthonormal set
obtained from the Gram-Schmidt process applied to
(\psi_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}

in the topology of
L^{2}(\Omega,e^{-2{\bf Q}_{N}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)})
.
All these functions are defined on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{z_{1},\dots,z_{l}\}
.

It only remains to establish the convergence

\psi_{k,N}\to\psi_{k}

as
N\to\infty
and the locally uniform boundedness in
N
of
\psi_{k,N}
.
But this would be immediate if we can show that the Gram
matrix
G_{N}=(\langle\psi_{k},\psi_{m}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega,\omega_{{\bf Q}_{N}})})_{1\leq j,k\leq k_{0}}

satisfies
G_{N}=I+\mathrm{o}(1)
as
N\to\infty
, using that
the functions
\psi_{k}
are locally uniformly bounded for the last point.
But the statement about the Gram matrices, in turn, follows from the bound
(
3.1
), the convergence
{\bf Q}_{N}\to{\bf Q}
and the
dominated convergence theorem.
The proof is complete.
∎

3.2.
Quasipolynomials and
\bar{\partial}
-surgery

Let
\psi_{k,N}

be the sequence
from Proposition
3.3

for
{\bf Q}_{N}=\{\kappa_{N}{\bf q}\}
.
We use it to construct an orthonormal basis

\big(P_{k,N}\big)_{0\leq k\leq N-1}

of the polynomial Bergman space

A^{2}_{N}(\Omega,e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}})
which satisfy, for any fixed
k

\forall z\in\Omega,\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}|P_{k,N}(z)|e^{-\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}(z)}=|\psi_{k}(z)|e^{-{\bf Q}\cdot\mathbf{\mathrm{Log}}(z)}.

Denote by
\chi

in a neighborhood of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}
, and
is identically one in a neighborhood of
\overline{\Omega}
.
See Figure
3.1
.

Definition 3.7

(Approximately orthogonal holomorphic functions)
.

For any
N,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define
the function

(3.2)

.

In addition, they are close to polynomials in

in the suitable

-sense
(see Proposition

In the case when
\Omega

behave like elements of
{\mathbb{C}}_{N-1}[z]

Lemma 3.8
.

For any
N,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the function

belongs to
L^{2}({\mathbb{C}},e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}})
. Moreover,
for any fixed
k,m\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

\langle F_{k,N},F_{m,N}\rangle_{N}=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}F_{k,N}(z)\bar{F}_{m,N}(z)e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}(z)}\mathrm{d}{m}(z)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\delta_{k,m}.

Figure 3.1
.
The cut-off function
\chi
for
\Omega=\Omega_{1}

used to define the function
F_{k,N}
.

Proof.

By the definition of
\psi_{k,N}
we find that

\int_{\Omega}F_{k,N}(z)\bar{F}_{m,N}(z)e^{-2\kappa_{N}U^{\mu}(z)}{\mathrm{d}}{m}_{\mathbb{C}}(z)=\delta_{k,m}.

On the set
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
,

∎

Turning to the

\Phi_{2,N}
(called
here

{\mathcal{K}}_{1,N}
and

{\mathcal{K}}_{2,N}
)
converge uniformly on compact
subsets of
\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{1}
and

\Omega_{2}\times\Omega_{2}
towards two limiting Bergman kernels,
which we denote by
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
, respectively.
If we show that the kernel of the determinantal point process

\Phi_{N,\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}}=\Theta_{N}\cap\big(\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}\big)

converges uniformly on compact subsets of

(\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2})\times(\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2})

towards the kernel

{\mathcal{B}}(z,w)\overset{\rm def}{=}\begin{cases}{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(z,w)&\text{ if }(z,w)\in\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{1},\\
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}(z,w)&\text{ if }(z,w)\in\Omega_{2}\times\Omega_{2},\\
0&\text{ if }(z,w)\in(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})\cup(\Omega_{2}\times\Omega_{1}),\end{cases}

then the asymptotic independence of the point processes
follows (see Proposition
A.3
) since

{\mathcal{B}}
corresponds to the kernel of the
union of independent determinantal point
processes associated with
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}

(see Proposition
A.4
of the Appendix).

Here, as in Section
2.1
,
the kernel
\mathscr{K}_{N}
associated with

\Phi_{N,\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}}
is
holomorphic
in the first coordinate
and anti-holomorphic
in the second coordinate.
Thanks to the reproducing kernel property
and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain that

\forall z\in\Omega_{1},\forall w\in\Omega_{2},\quad|\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,w)|^{2}\leq\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,z)\mathscr{K}_{N}(w,w).

This allows us to apply Montel’s Theorem,
and to show that any subsequence of the sequence

\mathscr{K}_{N}
has at least one further subsequence
converging uniformly on compact subsets of

(\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2})\times(\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2})
.
On
\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}\times\Omega_{2}
,
we have already established locally uniform convergence of the kernels.
The proof will be complete if we can show
that for any
z\in\Omega_{1}
, we have

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Omega_{1}}|\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,w)|^{2}\mathrm{d}{m}(w)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0.

To see why this convergence holds, let
S
be a
compact subset of
\Omega_{1}
. Then

\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,z)=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}|\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,w)|^{2}\mathrm{d}{m}(w)\geq\int_{S}|\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,w)|^{2}\mathrm{d}{m}(w)+\int_{\Omega_{1}^{c}}|\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,w)|^{2}\mathrm{d}{m}(w).

Since we know that
\mathscr{K}_{N}
converges uniformly
towards
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
on the compact
S
, we get

{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(z,z)\geq\int_{S}|{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(z,w)|^{2}\mathrm{d}{m}(w)+\limsup_{N\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Omega_{1}}|\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,w)|^{2}\mathrm{d}{m}(w).

Now, let
S
exhaust
\Omega_{1}
,
and notice that by the monotone convergence theorem we get

{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(z,z)\geq\int_{\Omega_{1}}|{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(z,w)|^{2}\mathrm{d}{m}(w)+\limsup_{N\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Omega_{1}}|\mathscr{K}_{N}(z,w)|^{2}\mathrm{d}{m}(w)

which, since
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
is a reproducing kernel on
\Omega_{1}
yields

{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(z,z)=\int_{\Omega_{1}}|{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(z,w)|^{2}\mathrm{d}{m}(w),

which completes the proof.

5.
Outliers for random polynomials

This section is devoted to proving Theorem
1.6
.
We begin with a discussion of some preliminary material.

5.1.
Hardy spaces

Denote by
\Omega
a Jordan domain with
analytic boundary (by convention,
\Omega
is bounded), and let
\eta

be a measure on
\partial\Omega

with a continuous
and strictly positive
density with respect
to the arc length measure.
We define the Hardy space
H^{2}(\Omega,\eta)
as the
closure of analytic polynomials in the norm

\lVert f\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega,\eta)}^{2}:=\int_{\partial\Omega}|f|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\eta.

Analogously, if instead
\Omega
is the complement of the closure of
a Jordan domain with analytic boundary, we define the Hardy space

H^{2}(\Omega,\eta)
on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}

as the closure of rational functions (with respect to the same norm)
with a fixed pole at a given
point
z_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}^{c}
.

The Hardy space
H^{2}(\Omega,\eta)
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, with
a Hermitian holomorphic reproducing kernel
S_{\Omega,\eta}(z,w)

defined on
\Omega\times\Omega
, known as the
Szegő
kernel of

(\Omega,\eta)
.
If the measure is simply the arc length measure
\sigma_{\partial\Omega}
,
we write
H^{2}(\Omega)
and
S_{\Omega}

for the space and the kernel, respectively.
We denote by
H^{2}_{N}(\Omega,\eta)
the
N+1
-dimensional
polynomial Hardy space

{\mathbb{C}}_{N}[z]\cap H^{2}(\Omega,\eta)
.

Remark 5.1
.

Let
\eta=v\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma
, where

v
is strictly positive
and real-analytic.
Let
\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{s}_{v}
be a bounded holomorphic function on
\Omega

whose real part equals
\frac{1}{2}\log v

on the boundary
\partial\Omega
. Such a function is often referred to
as a
Szegő
function of
v
. The function
\mathfrak{s}
allows us to
extend
v
to a log-harmonic function on
\Omega

by putting
v(z)=\big|e^{2\mathfrak{s}(z)}\big|=e^{2\operatorname{Re}\mathfrak{s}(z)}
.
This implies that the multiplication operator
f\mapsto fe^{-\mathfrak{s}}

induces an isometric isomorphism between the spaces

H^{2}(\Omega)
and
H^{2}(\Omega,v\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma)
.
In particular the weighted Szegő kernels satisfy the relation:

\forall z,w\in\Omega,\quad S_{\Omega}(z,w)=S_{\Omega,v}(z,w)e^{\mathfrak{s}(z)+\overline{\mathfrak{s}(w)}}.

In our main theorem on random polynomials (Theorem
1.6
),
we work with the reproducing kernel
for polynomial subspaces of
L^{2}(e^{-2NU^{\nu}}{\mathrm{d}}\nu)
,
where
\nu
is supported on a regular Jordan curve.
The above equality for the Szegő kernel turns
out to be a crucial ingredient in our proof.
This type of equality would not be true for the corresponding Bergman kernels
if the measure
\nu
was supported on, e.g., a domain.
This fact is what prevents us from treating more general
measures
\nu
in the theorem.

5.2.
Random polynomials

Without loss of generality we may consider
the case when
\Omega
is bounded.
The unbounded case
then follows by applying an appropriate inversion
(see, for instance,
[
7
, Theorem 1.14]
).

Notice that the Szegő kernel
S_{\Omega}

is the covariance kernel for the Gaussian analytic function

P(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\xi_{k}\psi_{k}(z),\qquad\xi_{k}\text{ i.i.d.},\;\xi_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}_{{\mathbb{C}}}(0,1),

where
(\psi_{k})_{k\geq 0}

is any orthonormal basis
of
H^{2}(\Omega)
.
Since by Remark
5.1

\psi_{k,v}\overset{\rm def}{=}(\psi_{k}e^{-\mathfrak{s}})_{k}

is an orthonormal basis for
H^{2}(\Omega,v\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma)
,
it follows that the zero set of
P
has the same
distribution as the zero set of the random analytic function

P_{v}
, obtained similarly as a random linear combination
of the basis elements
\psi_{k,v}
.

To prove Theorem
1.6
it is enough to show
that the covariance kernel for the random polynomial (
1.8
),
converges locally uniformly towards the
Szegő kernel
S_{\Omega,v}

(see, for instance, Section 2 in Shirai’s paper
[
28
]
).
Strictly speaking, this is not the case for our model,
but it can be arranged by multiplying (
1.8
) by an
appropriate zero-free holomorphic function.
Concretely, we introduce a holomorphic
function
\mathfrak{h}
on
\Omega
whose real part equals
U^{\nu}

on
\partial\Omega
, and consider the random analytic function

f_{N}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\xi_{k}Q_{k,N}(z)e^{-N\mathfrak{h}(z)},\qquad\xi_{k}\text{ i.i.d.},\;\xi_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}_{{\mathbb{C}}}(0,1).

Notice that
f_{N}
is a random linear
combination of an orthonormal system in
the Hardy space
H^{2}(\Omega,v\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma)
,
where we recall that
\nu=v\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma
.
The covariance kernel
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}(z,w)
of
f_{N}
is given by

\mathcal{S}_{N}(z,w)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}Q_{k,N}(z)\overline{Q_{k,N}(w)}e^{-N\big(\mathfrak{h}(z)+\overline{\mathfrak{h}(w)}\big)}.

Proof of Theorem

Structure of the proof

The proof is very similar to the proof of

Independence

The proof of the independence assertion in the

In actuality this proves that the Gaussian fields are independent in

We omit the details.

\mu_{2}
on
U_{2}
.
Both kernels may be
extended by zero to the rest of

(U_{1}\cup U_{2})\times(U_{1}\cup U_{2})
so that
it makes sense to write

K_{1}+K_{2}
. Similarly,
we may extend both measures by zero
to the rest of
U_{1}\cup U_{2}
so that
it makes sense to write
\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}
.

Proposition A.4
.

Let
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
be disjoint open
subsets of
\mathbb{C}
.
Suppose that for
j=1,2
,
\mathcal{X}_{j}

is a determinantal point process on
U_{j}

associated with
K_{j}

with respect to
\mu_{j}
.
Then, if
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}

are independent, the union
\mathcal{X}_{1}\cup\mathcal{X}_{2}

is a determinantal point process
on
U_{1}\cup U_{2}

associated with

K=K_{1}+K_{2}
with respect to

\mu=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}
.

Proof.

Recall that, with the above convention, we have
K=K_{j}

on
U_{j}\times U_{j}
.
Fix
k\geq 1
and let
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
be
k
measurable
subsets of
U_{1}\cup U_{2}
.
Introduce the sets
A_{i}^{(1)}=A_{i}\cap U_{1}

and
A_{i}^{(2)}=A_{i}\cap U_{2}

so that

A_{i}=A_{i}^{(1)}\uplus A_{i}^{(2)}\quad\text{ with }\quad A_{i}^{(1)}\subset U_{1}\text{ and }A_{i}^{(2)}\subset U_{2}.

We want to show that

\mathbb{E}\left[\#(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{X})\dots\#(A_{k}\cap\mathcal{X})\right]=\int_{A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{k}}\hskip-17.07164pt\det\big(K(x_{i},x_{j})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\big)\mathrm{d}\mu^{\otimes_{k}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}).

By linearity of the expectation, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\#(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{X})\dots\#(A_{k}\cap\mathcal{X})\right]=\hskip-8.53581pt\sum_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}\in\{1,2\}}\mathbb{E}\big[\#(A_{1}^{(\sigma_{1})}\cap\mathcal{X}_{\sigma_{1}})\dots\#(A_{k}^{(\sigma_{k})}\cap\mathcal{X}_{\sigma_{k}})\big].

On the other hand,
if
F_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\det\big(K(x_{i},x_{j})_{i,j\in\{1,\dots,k\}}\big)
,
we also have

\int_{A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{k}}F_{k}\mathrm{d}\mu^{\otimes_{k}}=\sum_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}\in\{1,2\}}\int_{A_{1}^{(\sigma_{1})}\dots\times A_{k}^{(\sigma_{k})}}F_{k}\mathrm{d}(\mu_{\sigma_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mu_{\sigma_{k}}).

Then, we only need to show that

\mathbb{E}\big[\#(A_{1}^{(\sigma_{1})}\cap\mathcal{X})\dots\#(A_{k}^{(\sigma_{k})}\cap\mathcal{X})\big]=\int_{A_{1}^{(\sigma_{1})}\dots\times A_{k}^{(\sigma_{k})}}F_{k}\mathrm{d}(\mu_{\sigma_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mu_{\sigma_{k}}).

Since
F_{k}
is symmetric, we may suppose that
there is
m\in\{0,\dots,k\}
such that

\sigma_{1}=\dots=\sigma_{m}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{m+1}=\dots=\sigma_{k}=2.

By the independence of
\mathcal{X}_{1}

and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\big[\#(A_{1}^{(\sigma_{1})}\cap\mathcal{X}_{\sigma_{1}})\dots\#(A_{k}^{(\sigma_{k})}\cap\mathcal{X}_{\sigma_{k}})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\#(A_{1}^{(1)}\cap\mathcal{X}_{1})\dots\#(A_{m}^{(1)}\cap\mathcal{X}_{1})\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\#(A_{m+1}^{(2)}\cap\mathcal{X}_{2})\dots\#(A_{k}^{(2)}\cap\mathcal{X}_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{A_{1}^{(1)}\times\dots\times A_{m}^{(1)}}F_{m}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}^{\otimes_{m}}\int_{A_{m+1}^{(2)}\times\dots\times A_{k}^{(2)}}F_{k-m}\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}^{\otimes_{k-m}}.

Moreover, by the calculation
of the determinant of a diagonal block matrix,

F_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})F_{k-m}(x_{m+1},\dots,x_{k})=F_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})

whenever
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in U_{1}

and
x_{m+1},\dots,x_{k}\in U_{2}

which, by Fubini’s theorem,
allows us to conclude the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Harmonic extension of potentials from uncharged regions

In this section we prove Proposition
2.7

i.e. that under the admissibility assumptions on
\mu
of Definition
1.1
,
the harmonic function
U^{\mu}|_{\Omega}
extends
harmonically past
\partial\Omega
to a
function
{\mathcal{U}}={\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}
on an open set

\Omega^{\prime}
, containing
\overline{\Omega}
.

Proof of Proposition
2.7
.

First of all, notice that
the statement
is local. Indeed,
suppose we show that
for every
z\in\partial\Omega

there exists a small open disk

D
centered at

z
and a harmonic function
on
D
that coincides with

U^{\mu}
on
\Omega\cap D
.
Then, we may cover
\partial\Omega
by a family of open sets

\{D_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}

that satisfy that intersections are connected, that
if
D_{\lambda_{1}}\cap D_{\lambda_{2}}\neq\emptyset
then

D_{\lambda_{1}}\cap D_{\lambda_{2}}\cap\Omega\neq\emptyset
,
and such that for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
there exists
a harmonic function

f_{\lambda}:D_{\lambda}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

f_{\lambda}|_{\Omega\cap D_{\lambda}}=U^{\mu}|_{\Omega\cap D_{\lambda}}
.
We may define a function
f
on
\mathcal{N}:=\cup_{\lambda}D_{\lambda}

by
f(z)=f_{\lambda}(z)
if
z\in U_{\lambda}
. Notice that if

z\in D_{\lambda_{1}}\cap D_{\lambda_{2}}
then

D_{\lambda_{1}}\cap D_{\lambda_{2}}\neq\emptyset
so that

D_{\lambda_{1}}\cap D_{\lambda_{2}}\cap\Omega\neq\emptyset
, i.e.,

f_{\lambda_{1}}=f_{\lambda_{2}}
on a nonempty open set which implies that

f_{\lambda_{1}}=f_{\lambda_{2}}
on
D_{\lambda_{1}}\cap D_{\lambda_{2}}

since
D_{\lambda_{1}}\cap D_{\lambda_{2}}
is connected.

Now fix
z\in\partial\Omega

and
choose a parametrization
of
\partial\Omega
near
z
, i.e.,
a holomorphic function

\varphi:(-1,1)\times(-1,1)\to\mathbb{C}

such that

\varphi|_{(-1,1)\times\{0\}}

is a usual parametrization
of
\partial\Omega

around
z
with

\varphi(0)=z

and
\varphi^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
.
By taking
a restriction of
\varphi

if necessary
and reparameterizing,
we have that

\varphi\big((-1,1)\times(-1,0]\big)\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\Omega}

and

\varphi\big((-1,1)\times(0,1)\big)\subset\Omega
.
Let us use the notation

A=\varphi\big((-1,1)\times(-1,0]\big)

and write

U^{\mu}=U^{\mu|_{A}}+U^{\mu|_{A^{c}}}.

Notice that

U^{\mu|_{A^{c}}}

is already harmonic on

\varphi\big((-1,1)\times(-1,1)\big)

so that we only need to
extend harmonically

U^{\mu|_{A}}

to
A
.

Now, the new setting is the following.
We define the square

\mathcal{S}=(-1,1)\times(-1,1)

and
\mu=\varphi^{*}(\mu|_{A})

which is a measure on (the redefined)

A=(-1,1)\times(-1,0]
.
We have a function

U:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}

such that
\Delta U=2\pi\mu

and we wish to find a harmonic extension of

U|_{(-1,1)\times(0,1)}

to the open square

\mathcal{S}
. The first
fact to notice is that
being able to extend
U

is independent of
U

as soon as

\Delta U=2\pi\mu
.
Indeed, if
V

were another function
such that
\Delta V=2\pi\mu

and if
\mathcal{U}

were a harmonic extension
of
U|_{(-1,1)\times(0,1)}
,
the function

\mathcal{U}-U+V

would be a harmonic extension of

V|_{(-1,1)\times(0,1)}
.
So, we have the freedom
to choose
U

and we will use the nice choice

U(z)=U^{\mu}(z)=\int\log|z-w|\mathrm{d}\mu(w).

Recall that

\mathrm{d}\mu=u\mathrm{d}m_{\mathcal{S}}+v\mathrm{d}m_{(-1,1)}

for some functions

u:(-1,1)\times(-1,0]\to\mathbb{R}
and

v:(-1,1)\to\mathbb{R}
which are real-analytic.
Denote

u_{y}(x)=u(x,y)

and, for any
y\in(-1,0]
, define the measure

\mathrm{d}\mu_{y}=u_{y}\mathrm{d}m_{(-1,1)\times\{y\}}
.
Denote by
\eta
the
measure on
(-1,1)
such that

\mathrm{d}\eta=v\mathrm{d}m_{(-1,1)}
. Then,
since
\mu=\int_{-1}^{0}\mu_{y}\mathrm{d}y+\eta
, we have

U(z)=\int_{-1}^{0}\left(\int\log|z-w|\mathrm{d}\mu_{s}(w)\right)\mathrm{d}s+\int\log|z-w|\mathrm{d}\eta(w).

We assume, without loss of generality,
that
u(x,y)=\sum_{m,n\geq 0}a_{m,n}x^{m}y^{n}

for every
(x,y)\in(-1,1)\times(-1,0]

and we extend it to
u:\mathcal{S}\times(-1,1)\to\mathbb{C}

by assuming, without loss of generality, that

u(z,y)=\sum_{m,n\geq 0}a_{m,n}(z-iy)^{m}y^{n}

is convergent.
Moreover, we can assume that the series
defining
u
converges
in
(2\mathcal{S})\times(-1,1)

and that
v
can be extended to a holomorphic function
on
2\mathcal{S}
.
Fix
y\in(-1,0]
and take a path
\gamma^{(y)}
in the lower half-plane
that connects

(-1,y)
and
(1,y)
avoiding

\mathcal{S}
.
Define
\mathcal{U}
by

\mathcal{U}(z)=\int_{-1}^{0}\left(\mathrm{Re}\int_{\gamma^{(y)}}\log(z-w)u_{y}(w)\mathrm{d}w\right)\mathrm{d}y+\mathrm{Re}\int_{\gamma^{(0)}}\log(z-w)v(w)\mathrm{d}w,

where

\log:\mathbb{C}\setminus i\mathbb{R}_{-}\to\mathbb{C}

is any branch of
the logarithm. Note that, for
w\in\gamma^{(y)}
and

z\in\mathcal{S}
,
z-w
belongs to
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus i{\mathbb{R}}_{-}
.
Since
u_{y}
and
v
are
holomorphic on
\mathcal{S}
,

U(z)=\mathcal{U}(z)
for
z\in\mathcal{S}\setminus A

and, since
\mathcal{U}
is harmonic
on
\mathcal{S}
, the proof is complete.
∎
