No bounded geometry wandering domains for sufficiently regular automorphisms Thanks: Supported by NSF grant DMS-1800180.
Sergei Merenkov

Abstract
. A question whether sufficiently regular manifold automorphisms may have wandering domains with controlled geometry is answered in the negative for quasiconformal or smooth homeomorphisms of n n -tori, n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , and hyperbolic surfaces. Besides control on geometry of wandering domains, the assumptions are either analytic, e.g., minimal sets having measure zero or supporting invariant conformal structures, or geometric, such as uniform relative separation of wandering domains.

37C05, 52C25

1.
Introduction

A classical result of A. Denjoy
[
De32
]
states that the action of an orientation preserving
\mathcal{C}^{1+bv}
-diffeomorphism
f
of the circle (i.e.,
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-diffeomorphism with
Df
of bounded variation) without periodic points is minimal, i.e., points have dense orbits on the circle. In particular, any such
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-diffeomorphism is topologically conjugate to an irrational rotation. The condition
\mathcal{C}^{2}
is Hölder-sharp in the sense that for each
\alpha\in[0,1)
there are
\mathcal{C}^{1+\alpha}
counterexamples
[
He79
]
.

Naturally, it is interesting to find out whether similar phenomena occur in higher dimensions.
A particular question of whether a diffeomorphism of 2-torus that is semiconjugate (but not conjugate) to a minimal translation can have uniform conformal geometry originates from work of A. Norton and D. Sullivan
[
NS96
]
.

1.1.
Denjoy type automorphisms

In the terminology of
[
NS96
]
, extended to higher dimensions, an orientation preserving homeomorphism
f
of
n
-torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{n},\ n\geq 2
, has
Denjoy type
if there exists a continuous map
h
of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
to itself, such that

–
h
is homotopic to the identity,

–the set
V_{h}
of
non-trivial
values of
h
(i.e., elements
p\in{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
with
\#\{h^{-1}(p)\}>1
) is non-empty and countable,

–there is a
minimal
translation
R
of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}

(i.e., every point has a dense orbit under the action of
R
)
such that
f
and
R
are semiconjugate, i.e.,

h\circ f=R\circ h,

–
f
is not conjugate to a translation.

Since
R
is a translation and
h
is homotopic to the identity, the functional equation above implies that the map
f
is isotopic to the identity.
The
minimal set
of
f
is

\Lambda={\mathbb{T}}^{n}\setminus\cup_{p\in V_{h}}\left\{(h^{-1}(p))^{o}\right\},

where
X^{o}
denotes the interior of a set
X
.
From this definition we conclude that if the interior of
h^{-1}(p)
is non-empty for at least one
p
, then
\Lambda
is a closed nowhere dense subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
that is
completely
invariant
, i.e., forward and backward invariant, under
f
. Finally, each non-empty
D_{p}=(h^{-1}(p))^{o}
is a
wandering domain
of
f
, i.e.,
f^{k}(D_{p})=f^{l}(D_{p}),\ k,l\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, implies
k=l
. Here and in what follows
f^{k}
denotes the
k
’th iterate of
f
.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
NS96
, Theorem 2]

Let a diffeomorphism
f\in{\rm Diff}^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
have Denjoy type, and suppose
f
preserves a
\mathcal{C}^{1+Z}
conformal structure on
\Lambda
. Then
f
cannot be in
{\rm Diff}^{2+Z}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
.

Here, superscript
k+Z,\ k=1,2
, means that the
k
th derivative satisfies the Zygmund condition.

Corollary 1.2
.

[
NS96
, Corollary 1]

No Denjoy type
f\in{\rm Diff}^{3}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
exists such that the wandering domains of
f
are non-degenerate geometric disks.

This corollary was generalized by A. Navas
[
Na18
]
to
\mathcal{C}^{n+1}
-diffeomorphisms of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n},\ n\geq 2
, and by the author
[
Me19
]
to
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-diffeomorphisms of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n},\ n\geq 2
.

A natural relaxation of the geometric disks assumption as in Corollary
1.2
is requirement of bounded geometry.
A collection of domains
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in a metric space
is said to have
bounded geometry
if there exist
C\geq 1,p_{i}\in D_{i}
, and
0<r_{i}\leq R_{i},\ i\in I
, with

(1)

B(p_{i},r_{i})\subseteq D_{i}\subseteq B(p_{i},R_{i}),\quad{\rm and}\quad{R_{i}}/{r_{i}}\leq C,

where
B(p,r)
denotes the ball centered at
p
of radius
r
.
The following is the first main result; see Section
2
for a definition of quasiconformality.

Theorem 1.3
.

If
f
is a Denjoy type homeomorphism of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n},n\geq 2
, whose minimal set
\Lambda
has measure zero, then either
f
cannot be quasiconformal or wandering domains of
f
do not have bounded geometry.

Theorem
1.1
also motivated the following questions.

Question 1.4

(A. Norton, D. Sullivan
[
NS96
]
)
.

Can
f\in{\rm Diff}^{2+Z}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
have Denjoy type?
Can Denjoy type
f\in{\rm Diff}^{2+Z}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
preserve a measurable bounded conformal structure on
\Lambda
?

In higher dimensions we allow
\Lambda
to have positive measure and give a partial answer to the second question. We say that a set
X
in a metric space is
locally rectifiably connected
if for each
x\in X
and a neighborhood
V
of
x
there is a neighborhood
U\subseteq V
of
x
such that any two points of
U\cap X
can be joined by a rectifiable path in
U\cap X
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
n\geq 3
, and
f
be a Denjoy type homeomorphism of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
such that the minimal set
\Lambda
of
f
is not all of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
, is connected and locally rectifiably connected. Assume further that the Lebesgue density points of
\Lambda

are dense in
\Lambda
.
Then
f
cannot be in
{\rm Diff}^{3}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})
and preserve the standard conformal structure on
\Lambda
.

1.2.
Surface automorphisms

More generally, let
(\mathcal{M},g)
be a
closed
, i.e., compact and with no boundary, Riemannian
n
-manifold.
Following terminology of F. Kwakkel and V. Markovic
[
KM10
]
, we say that a homeomorphism
f
of
\mathcal{M}

permutes
a dense collection of domains if there exists a connected, completely invariant, and nowhere dense compact subset
\Lambda\subset\mathcal{M}
such that the collection
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of connected components of the complement
\mathcal{M}\setminus\Lambda
has the following properties: for each
i\in I
, the domain
D_{i}
is the interior of its closure
\overline{D_{i}}
, the closures
\overline{D_{i}},\ i\in I
, are pairwise disjoint, and for each
i\in I,k\in{\mathbb{Z}},\ f^{k}(D_{i})\cap D_{i}\neq\emptyset
implies
k=0
. Abusing terminology, we refer to
\Lambda
as a
minimal set
of
f
and to the domains
D_{i},\ i\in I
, as
wandering domains
, as in the case of Denjoy type homeomorphisms. Note that neither do we assume that the orbit of a single
\overline{D_{i}}
is dense in
\Lambda
, nor that there is a unique orbit of wandering domains under
f
.

In the spirit of lowering regularity, in
[
KM10
]
, F. Kwakkel and V. Markovic posed the following question.

Question 1.6

(F. Kwakkel, V. Markovic
[
KM10
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a closed Riemann surface
equipped with the canonical metric induced from the standard conformal metric of the universal cover, do there exist diffeomorphisms
f\in{\rm Diff}^{1}(\mathcal{M})
with positive topological entropy that permute a dense collection of domains with bounded geometry?

F. Kwakkel and V. Markovic proved that the answer to Question
1.6
is negative under a stronger assumption that
f\in{\rm Diff}^{1+\alpha}(\mathcal{M})
, where
\alpha>0
;
[
KM10
, Theorem A]
.
Theorem
1.3
answers Question
1.6
in the negative for Denjoy type quasiconformal homeomorphisms of
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
, assuming that minimal sets have measure zero.
Our next result states that, under additional geometric conditions on the wandering domains, rather than the entropy condition on
f
, the answer to Question
1.6
is also negative; see Section
2
for the uniform quasidisks and uniform relative separation conditions.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a closed Riemann surface that is either hyperbolic or flat 2-torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
. Then there is no orientation preserving
f\in{\rm Diff}^{1}(\mathcal{M})
that permutes a dense collection of uniform quasidisks that are uniformly relatively separated.

Note that here we do not assume that the minimal set
\Lambda
of
f
has measure zero.
Theorem
1.7
is false in the case when
\mathcal{M}={\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
, as can be seen by taking
f(z)=\lambda z,\ \lambda>1
. One needs to “fill in” the fundamental annulus
\{z\colon 1\leq|z|<\lambda\}
by uniformly relatively separated disks
and spread them out using the dynamics of
f
. The simple details are left to the reader. We require the uniform quasidisks and uniformly relatively separated conditions in Theorem
1.7
since we use M. Bonk’s round quasiconformal uniformization result from
[
Bo11
]
. The assumption that the collection of permuted domains consists of uniform quasidisks is stronger than these domains having bounded geometry, but is in the same spirit nevertheless. The necessity of the assumption of uniform relative separation, as far as quasiconformal uniformization is concerned, is illustrated by
[
Nt20
, Proposition 3.6]
.
Finally, an interesting feature of Theorem
1.7
is that a regularity requirement on
f
, which is an analytic condition, along with geometric assumptions on the minimal set yield a dynamical conclusion. This is in contrast to
[
KM10
, Theorem A]
, where the assumption on topological entropy is dynamical.
A generalization of Theorem
1.7

is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a closed Riemann surface that is either hyperbolic or flat 2-torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
. Then there is no quasiconformal homeomorphism
f
of
\mathcal{M}
that permutes a dense collection of uniformly relatively separated uniform quasidisks, and such that
f
preserves a measurable bounded conformal structure on the minimal set
\Lambda
.

This theorem gives a negative answer to the second question in
1.4
for more general surfaces and under a much weaker topological and regularity assumptions on the automorphism
f
, albeit with uniform geometric assumptions on the minimal set
\Lambda
of
f
.

The proofs of the main results, Theorem
1.3
, Theorem
1.5
, Theorem
1.7
, and Theorem
1.8
require some preparation as well as invocation of previously known results, notably the quasiconformal uniformizaton by M. Bonk
[
Bo11
, Theorem 1.1]
.
The main tool used in the proof of Theorem
1.3
and in establishing a non-example of Proposition
10.1
is a certain version of transboundary modulus discussed in Section
3
. The transboundary modulus or extremal length in the classical setting of multiply connected domains was introduced by O. Schramm
[
Sch95
]
, and a certain discrete modification of it, called carpet modulus, was used successfully in the fractal setting in
[
Bo11
]
and
[
BM13
]
. The novelty in the present paper is that the transboundary modulus is used for fractals where we allow for continuous parts of mass distributions to account for the case when minimal sets have positive measure. This is crucial for Proposition
10.1
.
Other results employed, even though well known for maps between open regions in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
or the sphere
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
, are used here in less traditional setups of maps between fractals. An example is Proposition
7.3
, used in the proof of Theorem
1.7
. It is well known for quasiconformal groups on open sets since the late 1970s or early 1980s from the works of D. Sullivan
[
Su78
]
and P. Tukia
[
Tu80
]
, but it has not been presented in the fractal setting addressed here. Also, the proof of Theorem
1.5
uses essentially the smooth Liouville Theorem on conformal maps in higher dimensions, see, e.g.,
[
IM01
]
, but for positive measure fractals rather than for domains in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
While the proofs in the new settings often follow similar lines, the author felt it necessary and justified to include them or at least provide details where the two settings differ. However, if the reader is well familiar with the results in more established circumstances, there is not much harm in skipping some of the proofs here, e.g., the proofs of Proposition
3.1
, as well as the auxiliary lemmas leading to its proof, and Proposition
7.3
. Also, even though Lemma
7.1
appeared in the work of F. Kwakkel and V. Markovic
[
KM10
, Lemma 2.3]
, a proof is included here since it is important and short.

2.
Quasiconformal and related maps

Throughout, we denote by
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
the Riemann sphere,
{\mathbb{C}}
the complex plane, and
{\mathbb{D}}
the unit disk in
{\mathbb{C}}
. Also, by
{\mathbb{T}}
we denote the unit circle.
Whenever convenient, we identify
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
with
{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
via the stereographic projection.
In what follows, we typically assume that a given Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
is either
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with the Euclidean metric,
n
-torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{n},\ n\geq 2
, endowed with the metric induced by the Euclidean metric of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\ n\geq 2
, referred to as a
flat

n
-torus, or a Riemann surface endowed with the metric of constant curvature
1,0
, or
-1
, coming from the corresponding metric of the universal cover
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}},{\mathbb{C}}
, or
{\mathbb{D}}
, respectively. As is standard, we refer to metric of constant curvature 1 as
spherical
, constant curvature 0 as
flat
, and constant curvature
-1
as
hyperbolic
.

A non-constant orientation preserving homeomorphism
f\colon U\to V

between open subsets
U
and
V
of a Riemannian
n
-manifold
\mathcal{M}
is called
quasiconformal
if the map
f
is in the Sobolev space
W_{\rm loc}^{1,n}

and there exists a positive constant
K
such that the formal differential matrix
Df=(\partial f_{j}/\partial x_{i})

satisfies

(2)

\sup\left\{\|Df(p)(u)\|^{n}\colon\ u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\ \|u\|\leq 1\right\}\leq K\cdot\det\left(Df(p)\right)

for almost every
p\in U
.
The assumption
f\in W_{\rm loc}^{1,n}
means that
f
and the first distributional partial derivatives of
f
are locally in the Lebesgue space
L^{n}
. One also refers to
f
as
K
-
quasiconformal
, and
K
is called a
dilatation
of
f
. If
K
can be chosen to be 1 in Equation (
2
), the map
f
is called
conformal
. In the case
f
is conformal at a point
p
, we denote
Df(p)
by
f^{\prime}(p)
.
It is well known that compositions and inverses of quasiconformal maps are quasiconformal, and the same holds for conformal maps.
If
X\subseteq U
is a measurable subset and
H
is a collection of quasiconformal maps
h\colon U\to V
such that there exists a positive constant
K
with each
h\in H
satisfying Equation (
2
) at almost every point of
X
, then we say that the elements of
H
are
uniformly quasiconformal along

X
. If
K=1
, elements of
H
are said to be
conformal along

X
.

A homeomorphism
f\colon X\to\widetilde{X}
between metric spaces
(X,d_{X})
and
(\widetilde{X},d_{\widetilde{X}})
is called
quasisymmetric
if there exists a
distortion homeomorphism

\eta\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
, such that

\frac{d_{\widetilde{X}}(f(o),f(p))}{d_{\widetilde{X}}(f(o),f(q))}\leq\eta\bigg(\frac{d_{X}(o,p)}{d_{X}(o,q)}\bigg),

for every triple of distinct points
o,p
, and
q
in
X
. We also call
f
an
\eta
-
quasisymmetric
map in this case. Geometrically, for a map

f
to be quasisymmetric it means that the image under
f
of every ball
B
in
X
centered at any point
p
contains a ball in
\widetilde{X}
of radius
r
and is contained in a ball of radius
R
, both centered at
f(p)
, and such that
R/r\leq C
, where
C
does not depend on
B
. Quasisymmetric maps are global analogues of conformal maps in the context of arbitrary metric spaces, in particular those spaces that do not possess any meaningful infinitesimal structures. Quasisymmetric homeomorphisms between open sets in
{\mathbb{C}}
or
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
are quasiconformal. As a partial converse, global quasiconformal maps of
{\mathbb{C}}
or
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
are quasisymmetric. See
[
He01
]
for background on quasisymmetric maps.

Recall, a domain
D
in the plane
{\mathbb{C}}
or the sphere
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
is a
quasidisk
, or a
K
-
quasidisk
, if it is the image of the unit disk
{\mathbb{D}}
under a
K
-quasiconformal map of
{\mathbb{C}}
or
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
, respectively. Likewise, a
quasicircle
, or a
K
-
quasicircle
, is the image of the unit circle
{\mathbb{T}}
under a
K
-quasiconformal map of
{\mathbb{C}}
or
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
, respectively. The boundary curve of a quasidisk is thus a quasicircle.

More generally, a
quasicircle
in a metric space
X
is a Jordan curve
J
in
X
that is the image of
{\mathbb{T}}
under a quasisymmetric map.
In a Riemannian
n
-manifold
\mathcal{M}
(or, more generally, in a doubling metric space), a Jordan curve
J
is a quasicircle if and only if the following
two point condition
is satisfied: there exists
C>0
such that

(3)

{\rm diam}(J_{pq})\leq C\cdot d_{\mathcal{M}}(p,q),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ p,q\in J,

where
J_{pq}
is the smaller diameter subarc of
J
with endpoints
p
and
q
, and
{\rm diam}(J)
stands for the diameter of
J
; see
[
TV80
]
.
We say that a family
\{J_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of Jordan curves in a metric space
X
consists of
uniform quasicircles
if there exists a distortion function
\eta
such that each curve
J_{i}
is the image of
{\mathbb{T}}
under an
\eta
-quasisymmetric map
f_{i},\ i\in I
.
In a
n
-manifold
\mathcal{M}
, a collection
\{J_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of Jordan curves consists of uniform quasicircles if and only if (
3
) holds for all
J_{i},\ i\in I
, with the same
C>0
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a Riemann surface endowed with metric of constant curvature
1,0
, or
-1
. A
quasidisk

D
in
\mathcal{M}
is an open topological disk
with boundary
J=\partial D
being a quasicircle.
A collection of domains
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in
\mathcal{M}
is said to consist of
uniform quasidisks
if each
D_{i},\ i\in I
, is a quasidisk and the boundaries
\{J_{i}\}_{i\in I},\ J_{i}=\partial D_{i},\ i\in I
, are uniform quasicircles. A collection of continua (i.e., compact connected sets that consist of more than one point)
\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in
\mathcal{M}
is said to be
uniformly relatively separated
if there exists
\delta>0
such that

\Delta(K_{i},K_{j})=\frac{{\rm dist}(K_{i},K_{j})}{\min\{{\rm diam}(K_{i}),{\rm diam}(K_{j})\}}\geq\delta,\quad{\rm for\ all}\ i,j,\ i\neq j.

Here, the distance
{\rm dist}(K_{i},K_{j})=\inf\{d_{\mathcal{M}}(p,q)\colon p\in K_{i},q\in K_{j}\}
.
Quasisymmetric maps clearly take collections of uniform quasidisks to collections of uniform quasidisks, quantitatively. Also, quasisymmetric maps quantitatively preserve the uniform relative separation property, since, if
f
is an
\eta
-quasisymmetric map, then

\Delta(f(A),f(B))\leq\eta\left(2\Delta(A,B)\right),

where
A
and
B
are disjoint continua; see, e.g.,
[
He01
]
.

3.
Transboundary modulus

The following notion of modulus is inspired by O. Schramm’s transboundary extremal length, introduced in
[
Sch95
]
.

We fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}},\ n>1
, and let
\mathcal{M}
be a Riemannian
n
-manifold, and
\sigma
the Riemannian volume measure on
\mathcal{M}
.
Recall, if
\Gamma
is a path (or curve) family in
\mathcal{M}
, the
n
-
modulus
of
\Gamma
is defined as

{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma)=\inf\left\{\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho^{n}d\sigma\right\},

where the infimum is taken over all non-negative measurable functions
\rho
on
\mathcal{M}
, called
mass densities
, that are
admissible
for
\Gamma
, i.e.,

\int_{\gamma}\rho\,ds\geq 1,\quad{\rm for\ all}\ \gamma\in\Gamma,

where
ds
denotes the arc-length element. More generally, the notion of modulus for a curve family can be defined on arbitrary metric measure spaces.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathcal{M}
be a compact set with
\mathcal{M}\setminus\Lambda=\cup_{i\in I}D_{i}
, where
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a non-empty collection of disjoint complementary components of
\Lambda
in
\mathcal{M}
.
If

\Gamma
is a family of paths in

\mathcal{M}
, then we define the
transboundary modulus
of
\Gamma
with respect to
\Lambda
, denoted by
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
, as follows. Let
\rho
be a
mass distribution
, i.e., a function
\rho\colon\Lambda\cup\{D_{i}\}_{i\in I}\to[0,+\infty]
, where
the restriction
\rho|_{\Lambda}
is a measurable non-negative function, and
\rho(D_{i})
is a non-negative number for each
i\in I
. We refer to
\rho|_{\Lambda}
as the
continuous part
of a mass distribution
\rho
and to
\{\rho(D_{i})\}_{i\in I}
as the
discrete part
.
A mass distribution
\rho
is
admissible
for

{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
if
there exists a family
\Gamma_{0}\subseteq\Gamma
of
n
-modulus zero, such that

(4)

\int_{\gamma\cap\Lambda}\rho\,ds+\sum_{\gamma\cap D_{i}\neq\emptyset}\rho(D_{i})\geq 1,

for every path

\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{0}
. We call
\Gamma_{0}
an
exceptional family
for
\rho
. Now we set

(5)

{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)=\inf\left\{{\rm mass}(\rho)\right\},

where the infimum is taken over all mass distributions
\rho
that
are admissible for
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
, and

(6)

{\rm mass}(\rho)=\int_{\Lambda}\rho^{n}d\sigma+\sum_{i\in I}\rho(D_{i})^{n}

is the
total mass
of
\rho
.
Excluding an exceptional curve family
\Gamma_{0}
ensures that in many interesting cases
and for some relevant path families
\Gamma

an admissible mass distribution exists and
0<{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)<\infty
. We think of a mass distribution
\rho
as an element of the direct sum Banach space
L^{n}\oplus l^{n}
, where
L^{n}=L^{n}(\Lambda)
, and
l^{n}
consists of all sequences
(\rho_{i})_{i\in I}
with
\sum_{i}|\rho_{i}|^{n}<\infty
. The norm of
\rho\in L^{n}\oplus l^{n}
is given by

\|\rho\|_{L^{n}\oplus l^{n}}=\left(\int_{\Lambda}|\rho|^{n}d\sigma+\sum_{i\in I}|\rho(D_{i})|^{n}\right)^{1/n}.

If the set
\Lambda
has measure zero, then we ignore the continuous part of distribution
\rho
in (
4
) and (
6
).

Transboundary modulus has some familiar properties of an
n
-modulus, with almost identical proofs, e.g., monotonicity and subadditivity. Namely,
monotonicity
means that if
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
, then

{\rm mod}(\Gamma^{\prime})\leq{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma).

Subadditivity
means that if
\Gamma=\cup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Gamma_{k}
, then

{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)\leq\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\rm mod}(\Gamma_{k}).

The following proposition is the main result of this section.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
\Gamma
is a curve family with
{\rm mod_{\Lambda}}(\Gamma)<\infty
, and if

\{D_{i}\}_{i\in I}
have bounded geometry, then an extremal distribution
\rho
, i.e., an admissible distribution for which the infimum in the definition (
5
) is achieved, exists and is unique as an element of
L^{n}\oplus l^{n}
.

The proof of this proposition below mimics that of
[
BM13
, Proposition 2.4]
and employs the uncentered maximal function on Riemannian manifolds.

3.1.
Auxiliary lemmas

Let
(\mathcal{M},g)
be a compact Riemannian
n

[

[

Let

Lemma 3.4
.

If domains
D_{i},\ i\in I
, in a Riemannian
n
-manifold

have bounded geometry and
\Gamma

\mathcal{M}
, then
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)=0

{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma)=0
.

Proof.

Since
\Gamma
cannot contain constant curves,
we have

\Gamma=\cup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Gamma_{k},\quad{\rm where}\quad\Gamma_{k}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\ {\rm diam}(\gamma)\geq 1/k\}.

From subadditivity of
{\rm mod}_{n}
it follows that it is enough to show that
{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma_{k})=0
for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We fix
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Monotonicity of
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}
gives that
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma_{k})=0
. Thus, for each
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a mass distribution
\rho_{k,l}
with
{\rm mass}(\rho_{k,l})<1/2^{l}
, and an exceptional curve family
\Gamma_{k,l}
such that

\int_{\gamma\cap\Lambda}\rho_{k,l}\,ds+\sum_{i\colon\gamma\cap D_{i}\neq\emptyset}\rho_{k,l}(D_{i})\geq 1,

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{k}\setminus\Gamma_{k,l}
.

Let
\rho_{k}=\sum_{l\in{\mathbb{N}}}\rho_{k,l}
and
\Gamma_{k,\infty}=\cup_{l\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Gamma_{k,l}
. Then,
{\rm mass}(\rho_{k})<\infty
and subadditivity of
{\rm mod}_{n}
gives
{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma_{k,\infty})=0
. Moreover,

(9)

\int_{\gamma\cap\Lambda}\rho_{k}\,ds+\sum_{i\colon\gamma\cap D_{i}\neq\emptyset}\rho_{k}(D_{i})=\infty

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{k}\setminus\Gamma_{k,\infty}
.

Recall, the bounded geometry assumption gives the existence of balls
B(p_{i},r_{i}),B(p_{i},R_{i})
such that

B(p_{i},r_{i})\subseteq D_{i}\subseteq B(p_{i},R_{i}),\quad{\rm with}\quad{R_{i}}/{r_{i}}\leq C.

Now we define a mass density function
\rho
on
\mathcal{M}
by the formula

\rho=\rho_{k}+\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\rho_{k}(D_{i})}{R_{i}}\chi_{B(p_{i},2R_{i})},

where the first summand is extended to the complement of
\Lambda
as 0.
We first claim that for each
\gamma\in\Gamma_{k}\setminus\Gamma_{k,\infty}
one has

(10)

\int_{\gamma}\rho\,ds=\infty.

Indeed, for all but finitely many

, since
{\rm diam}(\gamma)\geq 1/k
, if
\gamma

, it intersects both complementary components of

\int_{\gamma}\chi_{B(p_{i},2R_{i})}ds\geq R_{i},

and the claim follows from Equation (
9
). Next, from Lemma
3.3
we conclude

(11)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}\rho^{n}d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n-1}\left(\int_{\Lambda}\rho_{k}^{n}d\sigma+C^{n}\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\rho_{k}(D_{i})^{n}}{R_{i}^{n}}\sigma\left(B(p_{i},r_{i})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(\mathcal{M})\cdot{\rm mass}(\rho_{k})<\infty,

where

is a constant that depends on the manifold

. Then, (

{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma_{k}\setminus\Gamma_{k,\infty})=0

{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma_{k})\leq{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma_{k,\infty})+{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma_{k}\setminus\Gamma_{k,\infty})=0.

∎

3.2.
Proof of Proposition
3.1

Let
(\rho_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a minimizing sequence of admissible mass distributions for
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
, i.e.,

{\rm mass}(\rho_{k})\to{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma),\quad k\to\infty.

The condition
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)<\infty
allows us to assume that
{\rm mass}(\rho_{k})\leq C
for some constant
C>0
and all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
By the Banach–Alaoglu Theorem, there exists a subsequence of
(\rho_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
that weakly converges to a mass distribution
\rho\in L^{n}\oplus l^{n}
.
In particular, after possibly passing to a subsequence, we may assume that the limits

\rho(D_{i})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\rho_{k}(D_{i})

exist for all
i\in I
. We claim that the mass distribution
\rho
is extremal.

One inequality, namely
{\rm mass}(\rho)\leq{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
follows from the weak lower semicontinuity of norms.
To show the reverse inequality one needs to demonstrate that
\rho
is admissible for
\Gamma
.
Since the sequence
(\rho_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges weakly to
\rho\in L^{n}\oplus l^{n}
, Mazur’s Lemma
[
Yo80
, Th. 2, p. 120]
gives that there is a sequence
(\tilde{\rho}_{K})_{K\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of convex combinations,

\tilde{\rho}_{K}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{K,k}\rho_{k},

that strongly converges to
\rho
in
L^{n}\oplus l^{n}
.
Therefore,
(\tilde{\rho}_{K})_{K\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is also a minimizing sequence for
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
. Moreover, each
\tilde{\rho}_{K},\ K\in{\mathbb{N}}
, is trivially admissible for
\Gamma
.
An exceptional curve family
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{K}
for
\tilde{\rho}_{K}
is the union of exceptional curve families for
\rho_{k},\ k=1,2,\dots,K
.
By possibly passing to a subsequence, we may assume that

\left\|\tilde{\rho}_{K}-\rho\right\|_{L^{n}\oplus l^{n}}\leq\frac{1}{2^{K}},\quad K\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Let

\Gamma_{K}^{\prime}=\left\{\gamma\in\Gamma\colon\int_{\gamma\cap\Lambda}|\tilde{\rho}_{K}-\rho|\,ds+\sum_{i\colon\gamma\cap D_{i}\neq\emptyset}|\tilde{\rho}_{K}(D_{i})-\rho(D_{i})|\geq\frac{1}{K}\right\},

and

\Gamma^{\prime}=\cap_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\cup_{K\geq k}\Gamma^{\prime}_{K}.

From this definition we have that if
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma^{\prime}
, then

\limsup_{K\to\infty}\left(\int_{\gamma\cap\Lambda}|\tilde{\rho}_{K}-\rho|\,ds+\sum_{i\colon\gamma\cap D_{i}\neq\emptyset}|\tilde{\rho}_{K}(D_{i})-\rho(D_{i})|\right)=0.

The mass distributions

\rho_{k}^{\prime}=\sum_{K=k}^{\infty}K|\tilde{\rho}_{K}-\rho|,\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}},

are admissible for
\Gamma^{\prime}
, and since
\left\|\tilde{\rho}_{K}-\rho\right\|_{L^{n}\oplus l^{n}}\leq{1}/{2^{K}}
, we have that
{\rm mass}(\rho_{k}^{\prime})\to 0,\ k\to\infty
. Thus
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma^{\prime})=0
. Lemma
3.4
then gives
{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma^{\prime})=0
. We conclude that
\Gamma_{\infty}=\Gamma^{\prime}\cup\left(\cup_{K\in{\mathbb{N}}}\widetilde{\Gamma}_{K}\right)
is an exceptional family for
\rho
. Indeed, by subadditivity of
{\rm mod}_{n}
it has modulus 0, and for each
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{\infty}
, since each
\tilde{\rho}_{K}
is admissible,

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma\cap\Lambda}\rho\,ds+\sum_{i\colon\gamma\cap D_{i}\neq\emptyset}\rho(D_{i})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\limsup_{K\to\infty}\left(\int_{\gamma\cap\Lambda}|\tilde{\rho}_{K}-\rho|\,ds+\sum_{i\colon\gamma\cap C_{i}\neq\emptyset}|\tilde{\rho}_{K}(C_{i})-\rho(C_{i})|\right)=1.

This completes the proof of existence of an extremal mass distribution for
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
.

A simple convexity argument implies that an extremal distribution
\rho
is also unique. Indeed, if there exists another, different, extremal mass distribution
\rho^{\prime}
, then
the average
(\rho+\rho^{\prime})/2
is also admissible for
\Gamma
. The strict convexity of
L^{n}\oplus l^{n},\ 1<n<\infty
, now gives that the average has a strictly smaller total mass, a contradiction.
∎

To finish this section, we prove the following conformal invariance lemma that makes transboundary modulus particularly useful. If
f
is a homeomorphism of
\mathcal{M}
that is conformal along its minimal set
\Lambda=\mathcal{M}\setminus\cup_{i\in I}D_{i}
, and
\rho
is a mass distribution on
\Lambda\cup\{D_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, we define the
pullback distribution

f^{*}(\rho)
to be
\rho(f)\|f^{\prime}\|
on
\Lambda
and
f^{*}(\rho)(D_{i})=\rho(f(D_{i})),\ i\in I
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f
be quasiconformal homeomorphism of
\mathcal{M}
that is conformal along its minimal set
\Lambda
. Let
\Gamma
be an arbitrary curve family in
\mathcal{M}
. If
\rho
is the extremal mass distribution for
f(\Gamma)
, where
f(\Gamma)=\{f(\gamma)\colon\gamma\in\Gamma\}
, then the pullback distribution
f^{*}(\rho)
is the extremal mass distribution for
\Gamma
.
Moreover,

{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(f(\Gamma))={\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma).

Proof.

To make the desired conclusion, we use the assumption that
f
is quasiconformal. Indeed, let
\Gamma_{0}\subseteq f(\Gamma)
be an exceptional curve family for
\rho
, and let
\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}=f^{-1}(\Gamma_{0})
be the preimage family. Then we have
{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma_{0}^{\prime})=0
because quasiconformal maps preserve vanishing of
n
-modulus. The latter is a simple consequence of the geometric definition of a quasiconformal map in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
; see
[
He01
]
. In addition, let
\Gamma_{0}^{\prime\prime}
be the family of paths on which
f
is not absolutely continuous. Again, since
f
is quasiconformal, we have
{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma_{0}^{\prime\prime})=0
, and thus
{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}\cup\Gamma_{0}^{\prime\prime})=0
. If
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus(\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}\cup\Gamma_{0}^{\prime\prime})
, then, in particular,
f(\gamma)\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{0}
, and hence

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma\cap\Lambda}\rho(f)\|f^{\prime}\|\,ds+\sum_{\gamma\cap D_{i}\neq\emptyset}\rho(f(D_{i}))

\displaystyle=\int_{f(\gamma)\cap\Lambda}\rho\,ds+\sum_{f(\gamma)\cap f(D_{i})\neq\emptyset}\rho(f(D_{i}))

\displaystyle=\int_{f(\gamma)\cap\Lambda}\rho\,ds+\sum_{f(\gamma)\cap D_{i}\neq\emptyset}\rho(D_{i})\geq 1,

i.e., the pullback mass distribution
f^{*}(\rho)
is admissible for
\Gamma
. The total mass of
f^{*}(\rho)
is

\int_{\Lambda}\rho(f)^{n}\|f^{\prime}\|^{n}d\sigma+\sum_{i\in I}\rho(f(D_{i}))^{n}=\int_{\Lambda}\rho^{n}d\sigma+\sum_{i\in I}\rho(D_{i})^{n},

i.e., it is equal to
{\rm mass}(\rho)
. Therefore,

{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)\leq{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(f(\Gamma)).

The converse inequality and indeed the full lemma follow from the facts that the inverse of a quasiconformal map is quasiconformal, and the inverse of a conformal map is conformal.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Assume for contradiction that there exists a Denjoy type quasiconformal homeomorphism
f
whose minimal set
\Lambda
has measure zero and wandering domains have bounded geometry.
Consider
\Gamma_{h}
, a path family on
n
-torus that consists of all
horizontal
paths.
I.e., if we write
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}=({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})\times({\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{n-1})
, then these are simple closed curves isotopic to the circle

C_{1}=\{(x_{1},0,\dots,0)\colon x_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\}\subset{\mathbb{T}}^{n}.

There is a natural
orthogonal projection

\pi_{1}
of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
onto
C_{1}
, namely,
\pi_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{1},0,\dots,0),\ x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}},\ i=1,2,\dots,n
.

The assumption of bounded geometry implies the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
f
and
\Lambda
are as above, for the horizontal path family
\Gamma_{h}
one has
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma_{h})<\infty
.

Proof.

Define a mass distribution
\rho
to be 0 on
\Lambda
and
\rho(D_{i})={\rm diam}(D_{i})
, the diameter value of each complementary component
D_{i},\ i\in I
, of
\Lambda
. Such
\rho
is
admissible for
\Gamma_{h}
. Indeed, let
\Gamma_{0}
be the subfamily of
\Gamma_{h}
whose curves spend positive or infinite length in
\Lambda
. Since
\Lambda
is assumed to have measure zero, we have
{\rm mod}_{n}(\Gamma_{0})=0
. Now, if
\gamma\in\Gamma_{h}\setminus\Gamma_{0}
, let
\{D_{i}\colon\ i\in I_{\gamma}\}
be the collection of all complementary components of
\Lambda
in
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
that intersect
\gamma
. Since
\gamma
spends zero length in
\Lambda
, the orthogonal projections
\pi_{1}(D_{i}),\ i\in I_{\gamma}
, cover
C_{1}
with a possible exception of a set of length 0. Therefore,
\sum_{i\in I_{\gamma}}{\rm diam}(D_{i})\geq\sum_{i\in I_{\gamma}}{\rm diam}(\pi_{1}(D_{i}))\geq 1
, i.e.,
\rho
is admissible. The assumption of bounded geometry for
D_{i},\ i\in I
, implies that the total mass of
\rho
is bounded above by
C(n)
, where
C(n)
is a constant that depends on the dimension
n
and the constant
C
from (
1
).
∎

Proposition
3.1
now implies that the unique extremal mass distribution
\rho_{h}
for
\Gamma_{h}
exists. Since
f
is isotopic to the identity, the path family
f(\Gamma_{h})=\{f(\gamma)\colon\gamma\in\Gamma_{h}\}
coincides with the horizontal path family
\Gamma_{h}
. In particular,

{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(f(\Gamma_{h}))={\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma_{h}).

The last equality holds for any homeomorphism of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
isotopic to the identity. Since
f
is quasiconformal, Lemma
3.5
along with the uniqueness of the extremal mass distribution give more, namely that

\rho_{h}(f(D_{i}))=\rho_{h}(D_{i}),\ i\in I.

But
D_{i},\ i\in I
, are wandering domains, and thus we must have that
\rho(D_{i})
is a constant
C
for each of infinitely many domains
D_{i}
in the same orbit under the map
f
. However
{\rm mass}(\rho_{h})<\infty
, and thus
C=0
for each such orbit, and therefore
\rho
is identically zero. This is impossible because a mass distribution that is identically zero cannot be admissible. The contradiction finishes the proof.
∎

5.
The Liouville Theorem and proof of Theorem
1.5

The main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.5
is a version of
[
IM01
, Theorem 2.3.1]
in the context of fractals.
Below, for a matrix
A
we denote its transpose by
A^{t}
. Also, in what follows, by a
Möbius map
or
transformation
in
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
or
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\cup\{\infty\}
we mean a finite composition of reflections in spheres. Note that a Möbius transformation may be orientation preserving or reversing.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a closed subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
that is connected and locally rectifiably connected. In addition, assume that the Lebesgue density points of
\Lambda

are dense in
\Lambda
.
Let
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{3}
-diffeomorphism such that
f
preserves the standard conformal structure on
\Lambda
.
Then
f
is a Möbius transformation on
\Lambda
.

Proof.

The proof is
almost verbatim the proof as presented in
[
IM01
]
. Because of this, we do not provide all the details below and only highlight the main steps and differences.

For
f
to preserve the standard conformal structure on
\Lambda
it means it
satisfies the Cauchy–Riemann system

(Df(x))^{t}\cdot Df(x)=|\det Df(x)|^{2/n}\operatorname{id},\quad{\rm at\ each}\ x\in\Lambda.

To simplify notations, we consider a more general system

(12)

(Df(x))^{t}\cdot Df(x)=G(x),

where
G(x)=[g_{ij}(x)]
is a positive definite symmetric
n\times n
matrix with entries that are twice continuously differentiable. Let
G^{-1}(x)=[g^{ij}]
denote the inverse of
G(x)
. We can rewrite Equation (
12
) as

(13)

Df(x)\cdot G^{-1}(x)\cdot(Df(x))^{t}=\operatorname{id}.

Denoting the coordinate functions of
f
by
f^{1},f^{2},\dots,f^{n}
and the partials of
f^{i}
by
f^{i}_{j}=\partial f^{i}/\partial x_{j},\ i,j=1,2,\dots,n
, we can write Equations (
12
), (
13
) as

(14)

f^{\alpha}_{i}f^{\alpha}_{j}=g_{ij},\quad g^{\nu\mu}f^{i}_{\nu}f^{j}_{\mu}=\delta_{ij},

respectively,
where we use Einstein’s summation convention, i.e., we sum over repeated indices, and
\delta_{ij}
denotes the Kronecker symbol. After differentiating the first system of equations in (
14
) with respect to
x_{k}
and manipulating algebraically, we obtain

(15)

f_{jk}=\Gamma^{\nu}_{jk}f_{\nu},

where
f^{i}_{jk}=\partial^{2}f^{i}/(\partial x_{j}\partial x_{k})
, and

\Gamma^{\nu}_{jk}=\frac{1}{2}g^{i\nu}\left(\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x_{k}}+\frac{\partial g_{ik}}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{\partial g_{jk}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)

are the Christoffel symbols of
G
.

A consequence of (
15
) is that if
G
is constant on
\Lambda
, then
f
is a linear transformation when restricted to
\Lambda
. This observation would be trivial if
\Lambda
were a domain. However, for
\Lambda
as in the statement of Theorem
5.1
one needs to exercise some care. Indeed, first of all, the components
g_{ij},\ i,j=1,2,\dots,n
, of
G
being constants on a closed set does not in general imply that all the partial derivatives
\partial g_{ij}/\partial x_{k}
are zero on that set. This is implied however at each Lebesgue density point of
\Lambda
.
The assumption that such points are dense in
\Lambda
along with the
\mathcal{C}^{3}
-regularity assumption give that all
\partial g_{ij}/\partial x_{k}
are zero at each point of
\Lambda
.
Hence all
f^{i}_{jk}
are identically zero in
\Lambda
.
The claim that the restriction of
f
to
\Lambda
is linear now follows from the Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals because
\Lambda
is assumed to be closed, connected, and locally rectifiably connected.

In particular, if
f
satisfies the Cauchy–Riemann system in
\Lambda
with constant Jacobian determinant, then, when restricted to
\Lambda
, the map
f
has the form

(16)

f(x)=b+\alpha Ax,

where
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
\alpha
is a positive constant, and
A
is an orthogonal matrix.

Differentiating (
15
) with respect to
x_{l}
and using (
14
), one obtains

(17)

f_{jkl}=\left(\frac{\partial\Gamma^{\nu}_{jk}}{\partial x_{l}}+\Gamma^{\mu}_{jk}\Gamma^{\nu}_{\mu l}\right)f_{\nu},

where
f_{jkl}
denotes
\partial^{3}f/(\partial x_{j}\partial x_{k}\partial x_{l})
. Permuting
k
and
l
and using the assumption that
f
is in
\mathcal{C}^{3}
, we obtain

(18)

R^{\nu}_{jlk}f_{\nu}=0,

where

R^{\nu}_{jlk}=\frac{\partial\Gamma^{\nu}_{jk}}{\partial x_{l}}-\frac{\partial\Gamma^{\nu}_{jl}}{\partial x_{k}}+\Gamma^{\mu}_{jk}\Gamma^{\nu}_{\mu l}-\Gamma^{\mu}_{jl}\Gamma^{\nu}_{\mu k}

is the Riemannian curvature tensor. The map
f
being a diffeomorphism implies that the vectors
f_{\nu},\ \nu=1,2\dots,n
, are linearly independent, and thus (
18
) gives

R^{\nu}_{jlk}=0.

The next claim is that for
f
satisfying the Cauchy–Riemann system on
\Lambda
, either
J(x,f)
is constant or

J(x,f)=r^{2n}\|x-a\|^{-2n},\quad x\in\Lambda,

for some constant
r>0
and a constant vector
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Indeed, suppose that
J(x,f)
is non-constant. Since

G(x)=\left(\det Df(x)\right)^{2/n}\operatorname{id},\quad x\in\Lambda,

we have that the Ricci curvature tensor
R_{ij}=R^{\nu}_{i\nu j}
vanishes on
\Lambda
. For the function

P(x)=J(x,f)^{-1/n},

vanishing of the Ricci curvature tensor can be written on
\Lambda
as

(19)

2P\,P_{ij}=\|\nabla P\|^{2}\delta_{ij},

where, as above, the lower indices in
P_{ij}
denote the corresponding partial derivatives. Differentiating (
19
) with respect to
x_{k}
and manipulating algebraically again one arrives at

P_{ijk}(x)=0,\quad x\in\Lambda,

for all indices
i,j,k=1,2,\dots,n
. This combined with the assumption that
\Lambda
is closed, connected, and locally rectifiably connected shows that the second partial derivatives
P_{ij}
are constant in
\Lambda
, and, in view of (
19
), one must have

P_{ij}(x)=c_{ij}=c\,\delta_{ij},\quad x\in\Lambda,\ i,j=1,2,\dots,n,

for some constant
c\geq 0
. If
c=0
, Equation (
19
) gives that
P
, and hence the Jacobian determinant, are constant in
\Lambda
. Thus we may assume that
c>0
and denote
c=2r^{-2}
, where
r>0
. Integrating along rectifiable paths in
\Lambda
one then has

\nabla P=2r^{-2}(x-a),\quad x\in\Lambda,

for some
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and hence

J(x,f)=P(x)^{-n}=r^{2n}\|x-a\|^{-2n},\quad x\in\Lambda.

If
J(x,f)
is a positive constant, we know from the above that
f
is a linear map of the form (
16
), where
A
is an orthogonal matrix. In the case

J(x,f)=r^{2n}\|x-a\|^{-2n},

if one writes
f=F\circ g
, where
g
is the reflection in the sphere centered at
a
with radius
r
, then a simple computation shows that
F
is a solution to the Cauchy–Riemann system in
g(\Lambda)
and has a constant Jacobian there. Hence
F
has the form (
16
), and therefore

f(x)=b+\frac{\alpha A(x-a)}{\|x-a\|^{2}},\quad x\in\Lambda,

a Möbius transformation.
∎

We are now ready to finish the proof of Theorem
1.5
. Assume for contradiction that
f\in{\rm Diff}^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})
as in Theorem
1.5
exists. Let
\widetilde{\Lambda}
denote the preimage of
\Lambda
under the covering map from
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
, and let
\tilde{f}
denote a lift of the map
f
under the covering map. It is immediate that the conditions of Theorem
5.1
are satisfied for
\widetilde{\Lambda}
and
\tilde{f}
. Therefore, the restriction of
\tilde{f}
to
\widetilde{\Lambda}
must be a Möbius transformation. Since
\tilde{f}
preserves the infinity, the map
\tilde{f}
must be linear, i.e.,

\tilde{f}(x)=b+\alpha Ax,\quad x\in\Lambda.

Compactness of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
and connectedness assumptions of
\Lambda
imply that the lifts of wandering domains of
f
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
have uniformly bounded diameters. One therefore must have
\alpha=1
, and hence
\tilde{f}
is an isometry. In particular,
f
cannot change the diameters of the wandering domains. This is impossible, i.e., no map
f
as in Theorem
1.5
exists.
∎

We finish this section with two comments. First, it should be possible to relax the
\mathcal{C}^{3}
-regularity assumption in Theorem
1.5
to Sobolev regularity
W_{\rm loc}^{1,n}
. In that case one would require the equation
(Df(x))^{t}\cdot Df(x)=|\det Df(x)|^{2/n}\operatorname{id}
to be satisfied only almost everywhere in
\Lambda
. The proof of the main tool, namely the Liouville Theorem, is very involved though even in the case of a domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Second, the current proof does not yield itself to replacing the standard conformal structure by an arbitrary measurable bounded conformal structure because in dimension 3 and higher the Measurable Riemann Mapping Theorem is not available. In particular, it is not sufficient to only require that the wandering domains have bounded geometry. Indeed, under
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-regularity one can show that the quasiconformal dilatations of all iterates of
f
are uniformly bounded; cf. Lemma
7.1
below. One can then argue
[
IM01
, Corollary 21.5.1]
that in this case there exists an invariant conformal structure under
f
; cf. Proposition
7.3
below. But, again, the lack of the Riemann Mapping Theorem does not allow to proceed.

6.
Schottky sets and groups

Here we recall some notions and facts on Schottky sets and Schottky groups; see
[
BKM09
]
for background and more details. A
Schottky set

S
is a closed subset of 2-sphere that can be written in the form

(20)

S={\hat{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i},

where the sets
B_{i},\ i\in I
, are pairwise disjoint
open disks in
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
. It is assumed that
I
consists of at least three elements, and, in fact, is infinite in what follows. The boundary circles
\partial B_{i},\ i\in I
, are referred to as
peripheral circles
. They are characterized topologically by the fact that their removal does not separate
S
. In particular, any homeomorphism between Schottky sets must take peripheral circles to peripheral circles. It is immediate from the definition that Möbius transformations take Schottky sets to Schottky sets.

The following is a quasiconformal uniformization result from
[
Bo11
]
that will be used in our proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Theorem 6.1
.

[
Bo11
, Theorem 1.1]

Suppose that
\Lambda
is a closed subset of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
whose complement is a union of uniformly relatively separated uniform quasidisks. Then there exists a quasiconformal homeomorphism
\beta
of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
such that
S=\beta(\Lambda)
is a Schottky set.

This is a quasiconformal analogue of P. Koebe’s (see, e.g.,
[
Co95
]
), and Z.-X. He–O. Schramm’s
[
HS93
]
conformal uniformizations of finitely connected, respectively countably connected, domains. The proof of Theorem
6.1
uses Koebe’s theorem and a delicate analysis that allows to control the quasiconformal dilatations of countably many quasiconformal maps using given geometry of
\Lambda
.

If
S\subseteq{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
is a Schottky set written as in (
20
), then for each
i\in I
, let
R_{i}\colon{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}\to{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
be the reflection in the
peripheral circle
\partial B_{i}
.
The subgroup of the group of all Möbius transformations on
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}

generated by the reflections
R_{i}
,
i\in I
, is the

Schottky group
associated to
S
and denoted by
G_{S}
. The group
G_{S}
is a discrete group of Möbius transformations with a presentation
given by the generators
R_{i}
,
i\in I
, and the relations
R_{i}^{2}=\operatorname{id}
,
i\in I
.

We set

(21)

S_{\infty}=\bigcup_{g\in G_{S}}g(S).

This is the
dissipative part
of
G_{S}
in the terminology of
[
Su78
]
, and the sets
g(S),\ g\in G_{S}
, form a measurable partition of
S_{\infty}
.
The
conservative part
of
G_{S}
consists of all points that are nested in infinitely many disks that are components of the complements of
g(S),\ g\in G_{S}
.
The sphere
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
is then decomposed into the dissipative and conservative parts.

If
\mathcal{M}={\mathbb{C}}
or
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
, a
Beltrami coefficient
is a complex measurable function
\mu
with
\|\mu\|_{\infty}<1
. If
h\colon U\to V
is a quasiconformal map,
the pullback of the trivial Beltrami coefficient
\mu=0
, i.e., the standard complex structure, is the
Beltrami coefficient

\mu_{h}

associated to

h
and given by

\mu_{h}=\begin{cases}{h_{\bar{z}}}/{h_{z}},\quad{\rm at\ points\ of\ differentiability\ of}\ h\ {\rm in}\ U,\\
0,\quad{\rm elsewhere}.\end{cases}

The quantity

K=\frac{1+\|\mu_{h}\|_{\infty}}{1-\|\mu_{h}\|_{\infty}}.

is a
dilatation
of
h
.
Conversely, the Measurable Riemann Mapping Theorem states that given any Beltrami coefficient
\mu
on
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
, there exists a quasiconformal map
h
of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
such that
\mu_{h}=\mu
almost everywhere. Moreover, the map
h
is uniquely determined by a post-composition by an orientation preserving Möbius transformation.

If
S
is a Schottky set, the group
G_{S}
is the associated Schottky group, and
\mu
is a Beltrami coefficient on
S
, i.e., a Beltrami coefficient that vanishes on
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus S
, we can define almost everywhere a new Beltrami coefficient

\mu_{S}(z)=\begin{cases}g^{*}(\mu)(z),\quad z\in g(S),\ g\in G_{S},\\
0,\quad{\rm otherwise}.\end{cases}

If
g
is a Möbius map, the pullback
g^{*}(\mu)
of a Beltrami coefficient
\mu
by
g
is given by

g^{*}(\mu)=\mu(g)\cdot\overline{g_{z}}/g_{z},\quad{\rm or}\quad g^{*}(\mu)=\overline{\mu(g)}\cdot g_{\bar{z}}/\overline{g_{\bar{z}}},

depending on whether
g
is orientation preserving or reversing, respectively.
It is immediate from the definition that
\mu_{S}
agrees with
\mu
almost everywhere on
S
, and, moreover, the coefficient
\mu_{S}
is
G_{S}
-invariant, i.e., almost everywhere in
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}

g^{*}(\mu_{S})=\mu_{S},\quad{\rm for\ all}\ g\in G_{S}.

Applying the Measurable Riemann Mapping Theorem we obtain a quasiconformal map
h_{S}
with

\mu_{h_{S}}=\mu_{S}.

The following lemmas are needed in the proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Lemma 6.2
.

[
BKM09
, Lemma 7.2]

If
S
is a Schottky set and
h
is a quasiconformal homeomorphism of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
with a
G_{S}
-invariant Beltrami coefficient, then
S^{\prime}=h(S)
is a Schottky set.

The proof is straightforward and uses the following observation. The
G_{S}
-invariance of
\mu_{h}
implies that an orientation reversing homeomorphism of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
that is conjugate by
h
to the reflection in a peripheral circle of
S
must preserve the standard complex structure of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
, and thus is a Möbius transformation. Since the fixed set of an orientation reversing Möbius transformation is a circle in
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
, the claim follows.

A related result is the following.

Lemma 6.3
.

[
BKM09
, Proposition 5.5]

Let
f\colon S\to S
be a quasisymmetric map of a Schottky set
S
.
Then
f
has a
G_{S}
-equivariant quasiconformal extension
f_{S}
to
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
. Namely, there exists a quasiconformal homeomorphism
f_{S}
of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
such that the restriction
f_{S}|_{S}
coincides with
f
, and for each
g\in G_{S}
there exists
g^{\prime}\in G_{S}
with
f_{S}\circ g=g^{\prime}\circ f_{S}
in
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
.

The idea of the proof is to use reflections to inductively double across peripheral circles to produce a
G_{S}
-equivariant extension
f_{S}
of
f
defined on the dissipative part of
G_{S}
, a dense subset of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
.
Such a map
f_{S}
then extends to a homeomorphism of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
. The fact that
f_{S}
is quasiconformal follows from application of the Ahlfors–Beurling extension as well as compactness arguments for families of normalized quasiconformal maps with uniformly bounded dilatation.

The following lemma, also needed in the proof of Theorem
1.7
below, is contained in
[
BKM09
]
and is elementary.

Lemma 6.4
.

[
BKM09
, Lemma 6.1]

Suppose that
f
is a continuous map of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, to itself that is differentiable at the origin
0
. Suppose further that there is a sequence of open geometric balls
(B_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
that contain 0, such that
{\rm diam}(B_{k})\to 0,\ k\to\infty
, and for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the set
f(B_{k})
is a geometric ball. Then the derivative
Df(0)
is a (possibly degenerate or orientation reversing) conformal linear map, i.e.,
Df(0)=\lambda T
, where
\lambda\geq 0
and
T
is a linear isometry.

7.
Uniformly bounded dilatation

In this and the following sections, unless explicitly stated,
\mathcal{M}
is assumed to be a closed Riemann surface equipped with the canonical metric induced from the standard conformal metric of the universal cover. We denote by
\psi\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\to\mathcal{M}
the covering map. This is a local isometry, and hence a conformal map.

Lemma 7.1
.

[
KM10
, Lemma 2.3]

Let
f\in{\rm Diff}^{1}(\mathcal{M})
permute a dense collection
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of domains with bounded geometry, and let
\Lambda=\mathcal{M}\setminus\cup_{i\in I}D_{i}
be the associated minimal set.
Then the map
f
as well as all its forward and backward iterates are uniformly quasiconformal along
\Lambda
.

Proof.

We assume that the minimal set
\Lambda
has positive measure, for otherwise there is nothing to prove. Also, since the covering map
\psi
is a local isometry, below we make no distinction between small disks in
\mathcal{M}
and in
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
and, whenever convenient, identify the map
f
with its lift
\tilde{f}
to
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
under the covering map
\psi
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
be fixed.
Since
\Lambda
is nowhere dense and the closures
\overline{D_{i}},\ i\in I
, are pairwise disjoint, for each
p\in\Lambda
there exists a sequence of complementary domains
(D_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
\Lambda
that accumulate at
p
, i.e.,

{\rm diam}\,D_{i}\to 0\quad{\rm and}\quad d_{\rm Hausd}(\overline{D_{i}},\{p\})\to 0\ {\rm as}\ i\to\infty,

where
d_{\rm Hausd}
denotes the Hausdorff distance.
Also, since
f
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-differentiable on a compact surface
\mathcal{M}
, we have

(22)

f^{k}(q+x)-f^{k}(q)-Df^{k}(q)(x)=o(x),\quad x\to 0,

where
|o(x)|/|x|\to 0
as
|x|\to 0
, uniformly in
q
.

The domains
D_{i},\ i\in I
, having bounded geometry means that there exist
C\geq 1,p_{i}\in D_{i}
, and
0<r_{i}\leq R_{i},\ i\in I
, with

B(p_{i},r_{i})\subseteq D_{i}\subseteq B(p_{i},R_{i}),\quad{\rm and}\quad{R_{i}}/{r_{i}}\leq C.

The Hausdorff limit of a subsequence of the rescaled domains

\left((D_{i_{m}}-p_{i_{m}})/r_{i_{m}}\right)_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}

is thus a closed set
\Omega
such that

(23)

B(0,1)\subseteq\Omega\subseteq\overline{B(0,C)}.

Let
D_{i_{m}}^{\prime},\ m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, denote the image of
D_{i_{m}},\ m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, under the map
f^{k}
. The domains
D_{i_{m}}^{\prime},\ m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, also satisfy

(24)

B(p_{i_{m}}^{\prime},r_{i_{m}}^{\prime})\subseteq D_{i_{m}}^{\prime}\subseteq B(p_{i_{m}}^{\prime},R_{i_{m}}^{\prime}),\quad{\rm and}\quad{R_{i_{m}}^{\prime}}/{r_{i_{m}}^{\prime}}\leq C,

for some
p_{i_{m}}^{\prime}\in D_{i_{m}}^{\prime},\ 0<r_{i_{m}}^{\prime}\leq R_{i_{m}}^{\prime}
, all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and with the same constant
C\geq 1
as above.
After possibly passing to a further subsequence, we may assume that the sequence

\left((D_{i_{m}}^{\prime}-f^{k}(p_{i_{m}}))/r_{i_{m}}\right)_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}

Hausdorff converges to a closed set
\Omega^{\prime}
. Here, we may choose the same sequence of scales
(r_{i_{m}})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
as for
(D_{i_{m}})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
because
f^{k}
is in
{\rm Diff}^{1}(\mathcal{M})
, and hence is locally bi-Lipschitz. From (
24
) we know that there exists
p^{\prime}\in\Omega^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime}>0
such that

(25)

B(p^{\prime},r^{\prime})\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\overline{B(p^{\prime},Cr^{\prime})},

where, again, the constant
C\geq 1
is the same as above.
Since Equation (
22
) is uniform in
q
, in particular holds for
q=p_{i_{m}},\ q+x\in D_{i_{m}},\ m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, one concludes that

(26)

\Omega^{\prime}=Df^{k}(p)(\Omega).

Combining (
23
), (
25
), and (
26
), and using the fact that the constant
C
does not depend on
p\in\Lambda
or
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we conclude that the map
Df^{k}(p)
has uniformly bounded dilatation, as claimed.
∎

We say that domains in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
have the
same shape
if they are homothetic to each other, and a domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
has a
generic shape
if the only elements of
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
preserving it are the elements of
SO(2,{\mathbb{R}})
. Note that most domains, including geometric disks and squares, have generic shape. However, non-circular ellipses and non-square rectangles do not have generic shape.

Corollary 7.2
.

If
f\in{\rm Diff}^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
permutes a dense collection of domains
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, and the lifts of
D_{i},\ i\in I
, to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
have the same generic shape,
then the map
f
is conformal along the minimal set
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Since the lifts of the domains
D_{i},\ i\in I
, have the same shape, it implies that
D_{i},\ i\in I
, have bounded geometry.
Also, a simple limiting argument gives that
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
as in the proof of Lemma
7.1
are homothetic to each other.
Moreover,
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}
have generic shape as it is a closed condition.
Hence, Equation (
26
) with
k=1
implies that for each
p\in\Lambda
we must have
Df(p)=\lambda(p)T(p)
, where
\lambda(p)\neq 0
is a scalar and
T(p)
is an element of
SO(2)
, i.e.,
f
is conformal along
\Lambda
.
∎

In the proof of Theorem
1.7
we use a modification, Proposition
7.3
below, of a result by P. Tukia
[
Tu80
]
(see also
[
Su78
]
).
Recall, a
Beltrami form
on a Riemann surface
\mathcal{M}
is a measurable
(-1,1)
-form
\mu=\mu(z)d\bar{z}/dz
, where we assume that
\|\mu\|_{\infty}<1
; see, e.g.,
[
BF14
]
for background on Beltrami forms. For a measurable subset
X
of
\mathcal{M}
, a
Beltrami form on

X
is a Beltrami form on
\mathcal{M}
that is equal to 0 outside
X
.
If
h\colon U\to V
is a quasiconformal map between open subsets
U
and
V
of
\mathcal{M}
and
\mu
is a Beltrami form on
V
(extended by 0 elsewhere), the
pullback
of
\mu
under
h
is the Beltrami form given by

h^{*}(\mu)=\left(\frac{h_{\bar{z}}+\mu(h)\overline{h_{z}}}{h_{z}+\mu(h)\overline{h_{\bar{z}}}}\right)\frac{d\bar{z}}{dz}.

If
h
is a quasiconformal map of
\mathcal{M}
, we say that a Beltrami form
\mu
is
invariant
under
h
, or that the map
h

preserves
a measurable bounded conformal structure, if

\mu=h^{*}(\mu).

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
\Lambda
be a measurable subset of a Riemann surface
\mathcal{M}
. Let
H
be a countable group of quasiconformal maps
h
of
\mathcal{M}
that leave
\Lambda
invariant and that are uniformly quasiconformal along
\Lambda
. Then there exists a Beltrami form
\mu
on
\Lambda
that is invariant under each map
h\in H
.

As a consequence, under the above assumptions and if
\mathcal{M}={\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
, there exists a quasiconformal map
\gamma
of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
that is conformal in
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus\Lambda
and that conjugates each
h\in H
to a map conformal along
\gamma(\Lambda)
. Conversely, if
H
is a group of quasiconformal maps of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
each element of which leaves a fixed Beltrami form
\mu
on a measurable set
\Lambda
invariant, then
H
consists of uniformly quasiconformal maps along
\Lambda
.

Proof.

As in Lemma
7.1
, we may assume that
\Lambda
has positive measure for otherwise there is nothing to prove.

In local charts of the universal cover, for arbitrary quasiconformal maps
f,g
, expressing
\mu_{g\circ f}
in terms of
\mu_{g}
and
\mu_{f}
, we have

(27)

\mu_{g\circ f}=f^{*}(\mu_{g})=T_{f}(\mu_{g}(f)),

where

T_{f}(w)=\frac{\mu_{f}+\frac{\overline{f_{z}}}{f_{z}}w}{1+\overline{\mu_{f}}\frac{\overline{f_{z}}}{f_{z}}w}

is an isometry of the disk model hyperbolic plane
{\mathbb{D}}
.

We consider

X(p)=\{\mu_{h}(p)\colon\ h\in H\},

a subset of
{\mathbb{D}}
defined for almost every
p\in\Lambda
up to a rotation, which depends on a local chart for
p
and is an isometry of the hyperbolic plane
{\mathbb{D}}
. Since
H
is a group, Equation (
27
) gives

(28)

\displaystyle T_{h}(X(h(p)))

\displaystyle=\left\{T_{h}(\mu_{h^{\prime}}(h(p)))\colon h^{\prime}\in H\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mu_{h^{\prime}\circ h}(p)\colon h^{\prime}\in H\right\}=X(p),

for each
h\in H
, almost every
p
in
\Lambda
, and an appropriate choice of local charts for
p
and
h(p)
.
If
X
is a non-empty bounded subset of
{\mathbb{D}}
, we define by
P(X)
to be the center of the smallest closed hyperbolic disk that contains
X
. Elementary hyperbolic geometry shows that
P(X)
is well defined.
Moreover,
P
satisfies the following properties (see
[
Tu80
]
for details):

1) if
X
is bounded above by
\delta
in the hyperbolic metric of
{\mathbb{D}}
, then
P(X)
is at most hyperbolic distance
\delta
from the origin in
{\mathbb{D}}
;

2) the map
P
commutes with each hyperbolic isometry of
{\mathbb{D}}
, i.e.,

P(T(X))=T(P(X))

for any hyperbolic isometry
T
of
{\mathbb{D}}
and arbitrary non-empty bounded subset
X
of
{\mathbb{D}}
.

We now define

\mu(p)=\begin{cases}P(X(p))\frac{d\bar{z}}{dz},\quad{\rm for\ almost\ every}\ p\in\Lambda,\\
0,\quad{\rm elsewhere\ in}\ \mathcal{M}.\end{cases}

The assumption that the elements of
H
are
K
-quasiconformal along
\Lambda
with the same constant
K
and property 1) above give that
\mu
is an essentially bounded Beltrami form.
Equation (
28
) combined with the commutative property 2) give, in appropriate local charts,

\mu(p)=P(T_{h}(X(h(p))))=T_{h}(P(X(h(p))))=T_{h}(\mu(h(p))),

for each
h\in H
and almost every
p
in
\Lambda
.
This is equivalent to

(29)

\mu=h^{*}(\mu),

on
\Lambda
for each
h\in H
, i.e.,
\mu
is invariant under each
h\in H
.

Now, if
\mathcal{M}={\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
, by the Measurable Riemann Mapping Theorem there exists a quasiconformal map
\gamma
of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
with
\mu_{\gamma}=\mu
. Since
\mu=0
outside
\Lambda
, the map
\gamma
is conformal in
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus\Lambda
. It remains to check that

\mu_{\gamma\circ h\circ\gamma^{-1}}(p)=0

for each
h\in H
and at almost every point
p
of
\gamma(\Lambda)
. This is equivalent to

(30)

\mu_{\gamma}(p)=\mu_{\gamma\circ h}(p),

for each
h\in H
and at almost every point
p
of
\Lambda
. But
\mu_{\gamma\circ h}=h^{*}(\mu_{\gamma})
, and since
\mu_{\gamma}=\mu
, Equation (
30
) is the same as (
29
), i.e., each map
\gamma\circ h\circ\gamma^{-1},\ h\in H
, is conformal along
\gamma(\Lambda)
.

Finally, to prove the converse statement, we just observe that, if
\gamma
is a quasiconformal map of
{\hat{\mathbb{C}}}
with
\mu_{\gamma}=\mu
, each
\gamma\circ h\circ\gamma^{-1},\ h\in H
, is conformal along
\gamma(\Lambda)
, and hence the elements
h\in H
are uniformly quasiconformal along
\Lambda
.
∎

]
.

.

Let

If
\{\widetilde{D}_{i}\}_{i\in\tilde{I}}

\{\widetilde{D}_{i}\}_{i\in\tilde{I}}

{\mathbb{D}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
, respectively.

Proof.

First, the lifts of the closures
\overline{D_{i}},\ i\in I
, to the universal cover are closed topological disks. Indeed, this follows from the facts that each
\overline{D_{i}}
is embedded in
\mathcal{M}
and that the covering map
\psi
is a local homeomorphism.

We use the two point condition (
3
) for quasicircles to verify that the lifts
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, of
D_{i},\ i\in I
, are uniform quasidisks in the intrinsic metric of the universal cover
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
, which is either
{\mathbb{D}}
with the hyperbolic metric or
{\mathbb{C}}
with the Euclidean metric. First, since
\mathcal{M}
is compact and
D_{i},\ i\in I
, are uniform quasidisks, we must have
{\rm diam}(D_{i})\to 0
, i.e., given any
\delta>0
, only finitely many of
D_{i},\ i\in I
, have diameters at least
\delta
. For, suppose not. Then there exists a sequence
(i_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, of distinct elements such that

{\rm diam}(D_{i_{k}})\geq\delta
for some
\delta>0
. Let
p^{1}_{k}
,
p^{2}_{k}\in\partial D_{i_{k}}
be two points with

{\rm dist}(p^{1}_{k},p^{2}_{k})={\rm diam}(D_{i_{k}}),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

By passing to subsequences, we may assume that
(p^{1}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,
(p^{2}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converge to points
p^{1},p^{2}
, respectively, necessarily with
{\rm dist}(p^{1},p^{2})\geq\delta
. Now, if
0<\epsilon<\delta/2
is such that in the disk
B(p^{1},2\epsilon)
an inverse branch of the covering map
\psi
is an isometry, we get a contradiction with the two point condition for
k
large enough, for pairs of points in the intersection of
\partial D_{i_{k}},\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, with the boundary circle of
B(p^{1},\epsilon)
.

Next, the intrinsic diameters of
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, are uniformly bounded above. Indeed, this follows from the facts that each
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, projects to some
D_{i},\ i\in I
, the diameters of the latter go to 0, and the covering map
\psi
is a local isometry.

To show that
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, are uniform quasidisks, assume that there exists a (possibly constant) sequence
(i_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and two sequences of distinct points
(\tilde{p}^{1}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}},(\tilde{p}^{2}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, with
\tilde{p}^{1}_{k},\tilde{p}^{2}_{k}\in\partial\widetilde{D}_{i_{k}}
, such that

(31)

{\rm dist}(\tilde{p}^{1}_{k},\tilde{p}^{2}_{k})/\min\{{\rm diam}(\tilde{J}^{1}_{k}),{\rm diam}(\tilde{J}^{2}_{k})\}\to 0,\quad k\to\infty,

where
\tilde{J}^{1}_{k},\tilde{J}^{2}_{k}
are two complimentary arcs of
\partial\widetilde{D}_{i_{k}}\setminus\{\tilde{p}^{1}_{k},\tilde{p}^{2}_{k}\}
. Here, the distances and diameters are in the intrinsic metric of the universal cover
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
.
Since
{\rm diam}(\tilde{J}^{1}_{k}),{\rm diam}(\tilde{J}^{2}_{k})
are uniformly bounded above and
\mathcal{M}
is compact, we must have
{\rm dist}(\tilde{p}^{1}_{k},\tilde{p}^{2}_{k})\to 0,\ k\to\infty
, and for
p^{1}_{k}=\psi(\tilde{p}^{1}_{k}),p^{2}_{k}=\psi(\tilde{p}^{2}_{k}),\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, by possibly passing to subsequences, we may assume that
p^{1}_{k},p^{2}_{k}\to p,\ k\to\infty
.
Using the fact that the covering map is a local isometry, let
\Omega
and
\widetilde{\Omega}
be open disks in
\mathcal{M}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
, respectively, such that
\Omega
contains
p
and
\psi\colon\widetilde{\Omega}\to\Omega
is an isometry.
By applying deck transformations, i.e., by acting by elements of the fundamental group, which are isometries, we may assume that for all
k
large enough
\tilde{p}^{1}_{k},\tilde{p}^{2}_{k}\in\widetilde{\Omega}
. If
J_{k}^{1}=\psi(\tilde{J}_{k}^{1}),J_{k}^{2}=\psi(\tilde{J}_{k}^{2})
, the uniform two point condition for
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in I}
gives
\min\{{\rm diam}(J^{1}_{k}),{\rm diam}(J^{2}_{k})\}\to 0,\ k\to\infty
. But that implies that for
k
large enough, one of the arcs
J^{1}_{k},J^{2}_{k}
with the smaller diameter is contained in
\Omega
.
Convergence (
31
) thus descends under
\psi
to
\mathcal{M}
, namely it holds for
D_{i_{k}},p^{1}_{k},p^{2}_{k}
in place of
\widetilde{D}_{i_{k}},\tilde{p}^{1}_{k},\tilde{p}^{2}_{k}
, respectively.
The domains
D_{i},\ i\in I
, are assumed to be uniform quasidisks however, and thus we arrive at a contradiction and conclude that
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, are uniform quasidisks in the intrinsic metric of the universal cover
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
.

We next argue that
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, are uniform quasidisks in the spherical metric.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a fixed fundamental domain in
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
under the action of the fundamental group of
\mathcal{M}
.
Since
\mathcal{M}
is compact, we may assume that the closure
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
of
\mathcal{F}
is compact in
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
. It was proved above that all
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, have uniformly bounded diameters. Since in any compact subset of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
the intrinsic and spherical metrics are bi-Lipschitz equivalent, all
\widetilde{D}_{i}
that intersect

are uniform quasidisks in the spherical metric.
For an arbitrary
\widetilde{D}_{i}
, i.e., one that does not intersect

, we can use the Koebe Distortion Theorem (see, e.g.,
[
Du83
, p. 32]
, or the Egg Yolk Principle
[
He01
, Theorem 11.14]
), because deck transformations are conformal automorphisms of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
.
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a closed Riemann surface that is either hyperbolic or flat 2-torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
.
Let
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of domains in
\mathcal{M}
that consists of uniform quasidisks that are uniformly relatively separated.
If
\{\widetilde{D}_{i}\}_{i\in\tilde{I}}
is the collection of lifts of
D_{i},\ i\in I
, to the universal cover, then
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, are uniformly relatively separated in the spherical metric of
{\mathbb{D}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
, respectively. Also, in the case when
\mathcal{M}
is hyperbolic,
{\mathbb{T}},\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, are uniformly relatively separated in the spherical metric of
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
.

Proof.

The argument is similar to establishing that all
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, are uniform quasidisks, and we first show that uniform relative separation holds in the intrinsic metric. Namely, assume that for two sequences
(\widetilde{D}_{i_{k}})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}},(\widetilde{D}_{j_{k}})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of domains we have

\frac{{\rm dist}(\widetilde{D}_{i_{k}},\widetilde{D}_{j_{k}})}{\min\{{\rm diam}(\widetilde{D}_{i_{k}}),{\rm diam}(\widetilde{D}_{j_{k}})\}}\to 0,\quad k\to\infty.

By applying elements of the fundamental group of
\mathcal{M}
, we may assume that one of the domains, say
\widetilde{D}_{i_{k}}
, intersects the closure
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
of a fixed fundamental domain.
Since, by the proof of Lemma
8.1
the intrinsic diameters of
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, are uniformly bounded above,
we must have
{\rm dist}(\widetilde{D}_{i_{k}},\widetilde{D}_{j_{k}})\to 0,\ k\to\infty
.
Also, since the fundamental group of
\mathcal{M}
is finitely generated, we may assume that for the projections
D_{i_{k}}=\psi(\widetilde{D}_{i_{k}}),D_{j_{k}}=\psi(\widetilde{D}_{j_{k}})
under the covering map one has
D_{i_{k}}\neq D_{j_{k}}
for all
k
, and
{\rm dist}(D_{i_{k}},D_{j_{k}})\to 0,\ k\to\infty
. Hence, the uniform relative separation assumption on
D_{i},\ i\in I
, gives
\min\{{\rm diam}(D_{i_{k}}),{\rm diam}(D_{j_{k}})\}\to 0,\ k\to\infty
. Compactness of
\mathcal{M}
gives a point
p\in\mathcal{M}
such that, after possibly passing to subsequences, one of the sequences, say
(D_{i_{k}})
, converges to
p
. Local isometry property of the covering map
\psi
near
p
along with the assumption of uniform relative separation of
D_{i},\ i\in I
, produce a contradiction, and thus
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, are uniformly relatively separated in the intrinsic metric. Now, in any given compact subset of
{\mathbb{D}}
, the intrinsic and spherical metrics are bi-Lipschitz equivalent to each other, and thus we conclude uniform relative separation in the spherical metric for domains
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, intersecting
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
. Again, one uses the Koebe Distortion Theorem to conclude that uniform relative separation in the Euclidean metric spreads around in
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
.

The assertion that, in the hyperbolic surface case,
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, are uniformly relatively separated in the spherical metric from the unit circle boundary

follows from the earlier conclusions that
\widetilde{D}_{i},\ i\in\tilde{I}
, have uniformly bounded diameters in the hyperbolic metric and are uniform quasidisks in the spherical metric, as well as elementary hyperbolic geometry. Namely, the fact that if a hyperbolic radius of a disk
D
in
{\mathbb{D}}
is fixed, its spherical radius is roughly proportional to the spherical distance of
D
to the boundary circle
{\mathbb{T}}
.
∎

Lemma 9.1
.

Each
h_{G}
is a Möbius transformation.

Proof.

Since

We consider the subfamily

y,\ 0\leq y<1
, where
\gamma_{y}(0)
and
\gamma_{y}(1)
are identified.
Since
\rho
is extremal for
\Gamma_{h}
, it is admissible for
\Gamma_{sh}
, and thus

1\leq\int_{\gamma_{y}\cap\Lambda}\rho\,dt+\sum_{\gamma_{y}\cap D_{i}\neq\emptyset}\rho(D_{i}),

for almost every
y\in[0,1)
, where
D_{i},\ i\in I
, are the square wandering domains. Integrating this inequality over
y\in[0,1)
, we obtain

1\leq\int_{\Lambda}\rho\,dtdy+\sum_{i\in I}\rho(D_{i})s_{i},

where
s_{i}
is the side length of the square
D_{i},\ i\in I
.
Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we get

1\leq\left(\int_{\Lambda}\rho^{2}dtdy+\sum_{i\in I}\rho(D_{i})^{2}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(|\Lambda|+\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where
|\Lambda|
denotes the area of
\Lambda
. Since the second factor of the right-hand side equals 1, we conclude that
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma_{h})\geq 1
. But
{\rm mass}(\rho_{h})=1
, and thus
{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma_{h})=1
, and the mass distribution
\rho_{h}
is extremal.

The map
f
being isotopic to the identity preserves the family
\Gamma_{h}
, and, in particular,

{\rm mod}_{\Lambda}(f(\Gamma_{h}))={\rm mod}(\Gamma_{h}).

By Lemma
3.5
, the pullback
f^{*}(\rho_{h})
is also an extremal mass distribution.
The uniqueness of the extremal mass distribution claimed in Proposition
3.1
implies that
\rho_{h}=f^{*}(\rho_{h})
. In particular, the map
f
must preserve the side lengths of the squares
D_{i},\ i\in I
. The contradiction comes from the fact that one cannot have infinitely many disjoint squares in
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
of the same side length.
∎
