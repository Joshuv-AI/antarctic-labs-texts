Polynuclear growth and the Toda lattice
Konstantin Matetski, Jeremy Quastel, Daniel Remenik

Abstract
. It is shown that the polynuclear growth model is a completely integrable Markov process in the sense that its transition probabilities are given by Fredholm determinants of kernels produced by a scattering transform based on the invariant measures modulo the absolute height, continuous time simple random walks. From the linear evolution of the kernels, it is shown that the n n -point distributions are determinants of n × n n\times n matrices evolving according to the two dimensional non-Abelian Toda lattice .



1.
Introduction

One-dimensional polynuclear growth (PNG) is a model for randomly growing interfaces which has been extensively studied as a solvable model in the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang universality class.
The KPZ class is characterized by non-standard fluctuations, which are universal for models in the class, but do remember the initial data. Most previous work on PNG has considered the
droplet
or
narrow wedge
initial condition.
For this special initial condition, the one-point distributions of the model can be identified with a Poissonized version of the longest increasing subsequence of a random permutation.
Ulam’s problem of determining the law of large numbers can be understood through the connection with the Hammersley process
[
Ham72
,
AD95
,
Sep96
,
AD99
]
, and a representation as a Toeplitz, then later a Fredholm, determinant led to the identification of the fluctuations around this limit as coinciding with those of the top eigenvalue of a matrix from the Gaussian unitary ensemble
[
Ges90
,
Joh98
,
TW99
,
BDJ99
,
Joh00
,
Joh01
]
.
Multipoint distributions in this geometry can be computed based on a multilayer extension, through which the Airy process was discovered
[
PS02
,
Joh03
]
.
Flat and stationary initial distributions were also considered in
[
BR00
,
SI04
,
IS04
,
PS04
,
Fer04
,
FS11
]
.
Recently
[
JR21
,
JR22
]
were able to produce certain multitime formulas for some of these special initial conditions.
One thing all such results had in common, for PNG as well as other related models, was the reliance on tricks particular to a few special initial conditions in order to obtain Fredholm determinant formulas suitable for asymptotics.

In
[
MQR21
,
QR22
]
we introduced the
KPZ fixed point
, a scaling invariant Markov process expected to be the universal limit of 1:2:3 scaled height functions, or their analogues, within the entire KPZ class.
The transition probabilities are given by Fredholm determinants of certain kernels based on a Brownian scattering transform of the initial data.
Furthermore, the
n
-point distributions, starting from an essentially arbitrary function, come from matrix Kadomtsev-Petviashvilli (KP) equations: In the one-point case, they simply satisfy the KP-II equation; for higher
n
, they are given as traces of matrix solutions of such equations.

A natural question is whether such a structure can be seen in one of the discrete or semi-discrete models in the KPZ class.
The question is particularly relevant as it may shed light on the physical basis of these equations.
The KPZ fixed point formulas were derived through asymptotic analysis of similar determinantal formulas for the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process (TASEP).
However, the TASEP transition probabilities appear not to satisfy any standard completely integrable equation.

In this article we consider PNG as a Markov process, with general initial data.
Its transition probabilities, again from essentially arbitrary initial data, are given by Fredholm determinants of kernels produced by a scattering transform based on continuous time symmetric random walks—the invariant measures of the process modulo the absolute height.
For PNG, the
n
-point distributions turn out to be associated to the most appealing discretization of the matrix KP: In the one-point case, they are solutions of the
two-dimensional Toda lattice
; for higher
n
, they are given as determinants of
n\times n
matrices solving the
non-Abelian Toda lattice
.
All of these are well-known
[
UT84
]
completely integrable extensions of the Toda lattice.
In this sense, PNG is shown to be a
completely integrable Markov process.

2.
Main results

2.1.
Model and setting

The
polynuclear growth model
(PNG) is a Markov process whose state space
\UC
consists of upper semi-continuous height functions
h
mapping
\mathbb{R}
into the integers
\mathbb{Z}
compactified at
\{-\infty\}
.
The topology on
\UC
is that of local Hausdorff convergence of hypographs, which is natural for models of random growth (see Sec.
4.1
for a precise definition).
The dynamics consists of two parts; a continuous, deterministic dynamics
h\mapsto\mathop{\mathop{\text{{sup}}}}\displaylimits_{|y-x|\leq t}h(y)
and a discontinuous, stochastic dynamics.
In the stochastic part of the dynamics, there is a Poisson point process in space-time with rate
2
(this choice of rate is arbitrary, but convenient in formulas).
If
(t^{*},x^{*})
is such a point, then
h(t^{*},x)=h((t^{*})^{-},x)+\mathbf{1}(x=x^{*})
.
In other words, in each interval
[x,x+\mathrm{d}x]
, with probability
2\mathrm{d}x\hskip 0.50003pt\mathrm{d}t
, a
nucleation
appears in
[t,t+\mathrm{d}t]
and the height function is increased by
1
at
x
.
Both dynamics are running simultaneously
1

1

Note that the dynamics makes sense starting from
any
function in
\UC

\mathop{\mathop{\text{{sup}}}}\displaylimits_{|y-x|\leq t}h(y)
is finite for any function in
\UC
, at any
x
, since the supremum is achieved, and
h
is not allowed to take the value
+\infty
at any point.
, so the continuous part of the dynamics implies that the nucleations spread in both directions deterministically at speed
1
.

Almost equivalently, the model can be thought of as a collection of one-dimensional ‘kinks’ (down steps) and ‘antikinks’ (up steps) moving on
\mathbb{R}
. They move ballistically at speed one: kinks to the right, antikinks to the left. They annihilate upon collision
[
Ben+81
]
, and are also produced, in pairs, a kink at
x^{+}
, an antikink at
x^{-}
, according to a rate
2
space-time Poisson process. The height
h(t,0)
is just its value
h(0,0)
at time
0
, plus the number of kinks and antikinks which have crossed the spatial point
0
up to time
t

0

0
.

.
Note however, that this transformation does not take our initial data into the standard initial data for the Hammersley process.

We will denote the PNG height function at time

(2.1)

\mathfrak{d}_{y}(y)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathfrak{d}_{y}(x)=-\infty\text{{ for $x\neq y$}}.

If we start PNG with a narrow wedge at

, we could call the resulting solution at time

,
{A}_{t}(x,y)
. The PNG dynamics (under the natural coupling where one starts with two or more different initial conditions and runs them using the same Poisson noise) preserve the max operation,

(2.2)

h(t,x;\mathop{\mathop{\text{{max}}}}\displaylimits\{h_{1},h_{2}\})=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{max}}}}\displaylimits\{h(t,x;h_{1}),h(t,x;h_{2})\},

and hence one has the variational formula

h(t,x;h_{0})=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{sup}}}}\displaylimits_{y}\{{A}_{t}(x,y)+h_{0}(y)\}.

We record a few other key properties. Typically for KPZ class models,
PNG is statistically invariant under spatial shifts

(2.4)

T_{-y}h(t,x;T_{y}h_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{dist}}}}{{=}}h(t,x;h_{0}),\qquad T_{y}f(x)=f(x-y),

and under reflections

(2.5)

Rh(t,x;Rh_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{dist}}}}{{=}}h(t,x;h_{0}),\qquad Rf(x)=f(-x).

A more special property, presumably connected to solvability, is

,

(2.6)

\mathbb{P}(h(t,x;g)\leq-f)=\mathbb{P}(h(t,x;f)\leq-g).

Unusually among continuous time random growth models, PNG has a

: Given everything up to time
t
, for
s>0
,
h(t+s,x)
only depends on
h(t,y)
,
|y-x|\leq s
, and on the points in the background Poisson process inside the space-time region

}.

Modulo a non-trivial upward height shift, the invariant measures for the process consist of the following one-parameter family of two-sided continuous time random walks.
Let
h^{+}(x)
,
h^{-}(x)
be independent Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}
with rates
\rho

\rho^{-1}
.
Then
h(x)=h^{+}(x)-h^{-}(x)
is invariant modulo height shifts, in the sense that, starting PNG from such an initial state, with
h(0)=0
, at time
t>0
,
h(t,x)-h(t,0)
will have the same distribution as the process in
x
. This is proved in Sec.
4.3
.
A concrete choice is the symmetric invariant state with
\rho=1
, which we denote by
\mathsf{N}(x)
.
In the hitting probabilities below, the determinant turns out to be unaffected by the choice of
\rho

The transition probabilities of a Markovian growth model such as PNG are completely determined by the finite dimensional distributions

(2.7)

F(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})=\mathbb{P}_{h_{0}}(h(t,x_{i})\leq r_{i},i=1,\ldots,n),

for an initial condition
h_{0}\in\UC
,
t>0
,
x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}
, and
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The PNG dynamics is surprisingly similar to
ballistic aggregation
(sometimes called

) where the height functions live over
\mathbb{Z}
instead of
\mathbb{R}
and

jumps to
\mathop{\mathop{\text{{max}}}}\displaylimits\{h(t,x-1),h(t,x)+1,h(t,x+1)\}
at rate one.
One of the challenges in the field of random growth is that we know almost nothing about many natural and basic models such as ballistic aggregation (in this case, only that it grows at some rate
Ct

[
Sep00
]
and plenty of computer simulations).
In contrast, for PNG we are now going to explain how the entire family (
2.7
) comes from known integrable non-linear wave equations.

2.2.
Toda

We need to introduce some notation.
For
a\leq b
consider the
hit operator
, with kernel

(2.8)

P^{\hit(h_{0})}_{a,b}(u,v)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{N}(a)=u}(\tau\in[a,b],\,\mathsf{N}(b)=v),\qquad\tau=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{inf}}}}\displaylimits\{x\geq a:\mathsf{N}(x)\in\hypo(h_{0})\},

where
\hypo(h_{0})=\{(x,g)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}\!:g\leq h_{0}(x)\}
is the
hypograph
of
h_{0}
, closed since
h_{0}\in\UC
, and
\mathsf{N}
is the continuous time random walk invariant measure introduced in the paragraph before (
2.7
).
If
a>b
we set
P^{\hit(h_{0})}_{a,b}\equiv 0
.
We need the symmetric first and second order discrete difference operators

(2.9)

\displaystyle\nabla f(u)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(f(u+1)-f(u-1)),\qquad\Delta f(u)=f(u+1)+f(u-1)-2f(u).

The latter is the infinitesimal generator of the random walk
\mathsf{N}
, that is, for
x\geq 0
and
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

(2.10)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{N}(0)=u}(\mathsf{N}(x)=v)=e^{x\Delta}(u,v).

The following should be thought of as a
scattering transform
of
h_{0}
by random walks,

(2.11)

{T}^{h_{0}}_{a,b}=e^{a\Delta}P^{\hit(h_{0})}_{a,b}e^{-b\Delta}.

Out of it, and for a fixed choice of
t\geq 0
and
x_{1}<\dotsm<x_{n}
, we build an
extended kernel
acting on
\ell^{2}(\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}\}\times\mathbb{Z})
:

(2.12)

\displaystyle K^{\text{{ext}}}(x_{i},\cdot;x_{j},\cdot)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}<x_{j}}e^{(x_{j}-x_{i})\Delta}+e^{-2t\nabla-x_{i}\Delta}{T}^{h_{0}}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}e^{2t\nabla+x_{j}\Delta}.

Now given
r=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we use the extended kernel
K^{\text{{ext}}}
to build an
n\times n
matrix kernel
K=K(t;x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n};r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n};h_{0})
acting on
\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})\coloneqq\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})\oplus\dotsm\oplus\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
, the
n
-fold direct sum of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
:

(2.13)

(K_{r})_{ij}(u,v)=K^{\text{{ext}}}(x_{i},u+r_{i};x_{j},v+r_{j}).

We stress that, although we only include the vector
r
in the notation,
K_{r}
depends on
t
,
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}
and
h_{0}
as well.
Let

(2.14)

r_{0}(t,x)=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{sup}}}}\displaylimits_{|y-x|\leq t}h_{0}(y),

which is simply the level reached at
x
by the deterministic part of the dynamics, starting with
h_{0}
, so

F(t,x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n},r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})=0\quad\text{{if $r_{i}<r_{0}(t,x_{i})~~$ for some $i\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}$}}.

We will show that if
r_{i}\geq r_{0}(t,x_{i})
for each

, then
I-K_{r}
is invertible; we will write

(2.15)

R_{r}=(I-K_{r})^{-1}

for this inverse.
Introduce also the variables

(2.16)

\zeta=\tfrac{1}{2}(t-\overline{x})\quad\text{and}\quad\eta=\tfrac{1}{2}(t+\overline{x}),\qquad\overline{x}=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n},

write

r\pm 1=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})\pm(1,\dotsc,1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\partial_{\overline{x}}=\partial_{x_{1}}+\dotsm+\partial_{x_{n}},

and consider the following element of

\zeta,\eta
, and

:

(2.17)

\displaystyle Q_{r}=R_{r}(1,1).

2.2.1.
Non-Abelian 2D Toda equation

We can now state the main result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
h_{0}\in\UC
be deterministic.
For
r=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})
such that
r_{i}>r_{0}(t,x_{i})
for each
i
, the matrix function
Q_{r}

satisfies the non-Abelian 2D Toda equation

(2.18)

\partial_{\zeta}(\partial_{\eta}Q_{r}Q_{r}^{-1})+Q_{r}Q_{r-1}^{-1}-Q_{r+1}Q_{r}^{-1}=0,

while

(2.19)

\frac{F(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},r_{1}+1,\ldots,r_{n}+1)}{F(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits Q_{r}.

The non-Abelian 2D Toda equation (
2.18
) arises from the special dependence of the kernel
K_{r}
on
r
,
\eta
and
\zeta
(or, more precisely, the
r_{i}
’s,
t
and the
x_{i}
’s).
The theorem is proved in Sec.
3
. It was inspired by
[
BP87
]
, where a special case is considered in an abstract setting (see Rem.
3.2
).

The non-Abelian 2D Toda equation forms a well-known integrable system
[
Mik81
]
.
Lax and Zakharov-Shabat (zero-curvature) forms of the equation can be found in Sec. 3.1 of
[
UT84
]
(see also
[
Tak18
]
).
They arise from compatibility of the equations for
vectors
\Phi_{r}

[
NW97
]

(2.20)

\partial_{\eta}\Phi_{r}=V_{r}\Phi_{r}+\lambda_{r}\Phi_{r-1},\qquad\partial_{t}\Phi_{r}=U_{r}\Phi_{r+1}

with

(2.21)

U_{r}=Q_{r}Q_{r-1}^{-1},\qquad V_{r}=-\partial_{\eta}Q_{r}Q_{r}^{-1}.

The non-Abelian 2D Toda equation is sometimes written in terms of
U_{r}
and
V_{r}
as

(2.22)

\partial_{\eta}U_{r}+V_{r}U_{r}-U_{r}V_{r-1}=0,\qquad\partial_{\zeta}V_{r}+U_{r+1}-U_{r}=0

(the first equation is an elementary consequence of the definition (
2.21
) of

and

, while the second one is equivalent to (

When

, there is no need to take the determinant in (

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
h_{0}\in\UC
be deterministic.
Then

(2.23)

F_{r}(t,x)=\mathbb{P}_{h_{0}}(h(t,x)\leq r)

satisfies the scalar 2D Toda equation

(2.24)

\tfrac{1}{4}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})\mathop{\mathop{\text{{log}}}}\nolimits F_{r}=\frac{F_{r+1}F_{r-1}}{F_{r}^{2}}-1

for

and

given by (

To see how the corollary follows from Thm.

, note that from (

) and (

U_{r}=\frac{F_{r+1}F_{r-1}}{F_{r}^{2}}
. On the other hand, since in this case

and

are scalars, the first equation in (

\partial_{\eta}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{log}}}}\nolimits U_{r}+V_{r}-V_{r-1}=0

Taking the

But

2.2.2.
1D Toda lattice

In the case of flat initial data,
h_{0}\equiv 0
,
F_{r}
is independent of
x
, the
x
derivative drops out of (
2.24
), and taking
g_{r}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{log}}}}\nolimits F_{r}-\mathop{\mathop{\text{{log}}}}\nolimits F_{r-1}
and

(2.25)

\ddot{g}_{r}=e^{g_{r+1}-g_{r}}-e^{g_{r}-g_{r-1}}.

2.2.3.
Boundary conditions

One needs to supplement (
2.24
) with boundary conditions at

t=0
and
r=r_{0}(t,x)
. At
t=0
we clearly have from the definition (
2.23
) of
F_{r}(t,x)
that

(2.26)

F_{r}(0,x)=\mathbf{1}_{r\geq h_{0}(x)}.

Since we are dealing with a wave equation, we require an extra piece of data at
t=0
, which in simple cases is given by

(2.27)

\partial_{t}F_{r}(0,x)=-\textstyle\sum_{y}\!\left((h_{0}(y)-h_{0}(y^{-}))\mathbf{1}_{h_{0}(y^{-})\leq r<h_{0}(y)}+(h_{0}(y)-h_{0}(y^{+}))\mathbf{1}_{h_{0}(y^{+})\leq r<h_{0}(y)}\right)\hskip-1.00006pt\delta_{y}(x).

This comes from the deterministic part of the dynamics: The random part does not contribute to the derivative because a jump at
x
coming from a nucleation in a time interval of length
\varepsilon
has probability of order
\varepsilon^{2}
.
From the dynamics of the process,
h(t,x)
can never be smaller than
r_{0}(t,x)
. Hence
F_{r}(t,x)=0
for
r<r_{0}(t,x)
. This leaves open the fate of
F_{r_{0}(t,x)}(t,x)
. We clearly have
\frac{F_{r_{0}+1}F_{r_{0}-1}}{F_{r_{0}}^{2}}=0
, since
F_{r_{0}-1}=0
, and
F_{r_{0}}>0
, since if there are no nucleations in the backward light cone of
(t,x)
, then

h(t,x)=r_{0}(t,x)
, and this has positive probability (note however, that other scenarios can also lead to
h(t,x)=r_{0}(t,x)
so
F_{r_{0}}
is not trivial to compute.)
This would appear to give
\frac{1}{4}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})\mathop{\mathop{\text{{log}}}}\nolimits F_{r}(t,x)\mid_{r=r_{0}(t,x)}=-1
, but it is not quite true because
F_{r_{0}}
has jumps when
r_{0}
has jumps. These can be worked out in the following way.
First of all,
r_{0}(t,x)
is computed in an elementary way from the initial data
h_{0}(x)
. It has discontinuities of the first kind (jumps) on (annihilating) lines of slope either
1
or
-1
emerging from the initial jumps of
h_{0}
and at each discontinuity point
(t,x)
we have
r_{0}(t^{-},x)<r_{0}(t^{+},x)
. It is not hard to see that
F_{r_{0}(t,x)}(t,x)
must jump from
F_{r_{0}(t^{-},x)}(t^{-},x)
to
F_{r_{0}(t^{+},x)}(t^{-},x)
as we cross the discontinuity line. In the interior of the regions bounded by the discontinuity lines, we do have
\frac{1}{4}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})\mathop{\mathop{\text{{log}}}}\nolimits F_{r}(t,x)\mid_{r=r_{0}(t,x)}=-1
. Because the jump is actually computed using the value of
F
at
t^{-}
,
F_{r_{0}(t,x)}(t,x)
can now be computed everywhere.
Again one requires an extra boundary condition which is provided by
\mathop{\mathop{\text{{lim}}}}\displaylimits_{r\to\infty}F_{r}(t,x)=1
.
We believe that under these conditions the 2D Toda equation is well posed, but this is very far from anything available in the literature, and we leave it for future work.
In the special narrow wedge and flat cases, these boundary conditions are very easy to state:

Narrow wedge
.

F_{r}(0,x)=\mathbf{1}_{x\neq 0}+\mathbf{1}_{x=0,\,r\geq 0}
,
\partial_{t}F_{r}(0,x)=-2\delta_{0}(x)
. For
|x|\leq t
,
r_{0}(t,x)=0
, while
r_{0}=-\infty
otherwise, and
F_{0}(t,x)=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{exp}}}}\nolimits\{-(t^{2}-x^{2})\}
,
F_{k}(t,x)=0
,
k<0
,
|x|\leq t
.

Flat
.

F_{r}(0,x)=\mathbf{1}_{r\geq 0}
,
\partial_{t}F_{r}(0,x)=0
and
r_{0}\equiv 0
with
F_{0}(t,x)=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{exp}}}}\nolimits\{-2t^{2}\}
and
F_{k}(t,x)=0
,
k<0
.

The boundary terms for the non-Abelian Toda equation are complicated to state and will be partially addressed in Appdx.
A
.

). It is far from clear however, if, or how, such methods could apply to general initial data. Because of this prevailing paradigm, the fact that general initial data lead to Toda actually comes as something of a surprise. There is a point of view that all these integrable models lead to
\tau
-functions. But this is refuted by the key integrable model which led to the discovery of the KPZ fixed point, TASEP, which does not appear to fit this mold so nicely.

Very recently
[
CR22
]
studied a finite temperature version of the narrow wedge solution, with a Fermi factor analogous to the deformation of the Airy kernel which gives the narrow wedge solution of KPZ
[
ACQ11
]
. Note that it is far from clear what a positive temperature deformation of PNG should look like; an interesting suggestion is studied in
[
ABW22
]
.
Using Riemann-Hilbert methods,
[
CR22
]
show that the Fredholm determinant satisfies the same equation as ours in the one-point narrow wedge case. They also obtain
t\searrow 0
asymptotics which refine what we have in the general case (see (
4.34
)). We note that the works were done completely independently, and theirs was posted to the arXiv after we had announced ours in several talks.

The scalar 2D Toda lattice also comes up in generating functions for Hurwitz numbers
[
Oko00
]
(see also
[
GPH15
]
), “moments” of conformal maps of simply connected domains
[
WZ00
]
, as well as in the intriguingly related situation of the multilayer heat equation
[
OW16
]
(see also
[
O’C12
]
, where the partition function of a Brownian directed polymer model is characterized in terms of a diffusion process associated with the quantum Toda lattice).
The 1D non-Abelian case appeared earlier, governing spin correlations in the inhomogeneous XY model
[
PC78
]
.
A mean field analysis of PNG in
[
BN+98
]
somewhat surprisingly leads to a directed version of the Toda lattice.

2.3.
Fredholm determinant formula

The proof of Thm.
2.1
and Cor.
2.2
is based on the following explicit Fredholm determinant formula for the multipoint distribution of the PNG height function, given in terms of the kernel
K_{r}
from (
2.13
):

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
h_{0}\in\UC
be deterministic and fix any
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
and
r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
r_{i}\geq r_{0}(t,x_{i})
(see (
2.14
)) for each
i=1,\dotsc,n
.
The finite dimensional distributions (
2.7
) are given by the Fredholm determinant

(2.28)

F(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K_{r})_{\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})}.

Note that the determinant in (
2.28
) is the Fredholm determinant of an operator acting on the
n
-fold direct sum of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
, while (
2.19
) is that of an
n\times n
matrix.

The way we originally derived this formula was as a limit of an analogous formula for parallel TASEP with general initial data obtained in
[
MR22
]
.
In the regime when each particle in parallel TASEP attempts to move with probability
p
close to
1
, the height function of that model can be thought of as a discrete time version of PNG
[
GW95
]
.
For parallel TASEP there is a biorthogonal ensemble representation, derived in
[
BFS08
]
, from which a Fredholm determinant formula for its multipoint distributions can be derived based on a generalization of the method introduced in
[
MQR21
]
in the context of continuous time TASEP
[
MR22
]
.
We will pursue this route to (
2.28
) in an upcoming paper, where we will also show that in fact parallel TASEP converges to PNG, after appropriate rescaling, as
p\to 1
.
A potential alternate approach could be to start from the formula in Thm. 2 of
[
JR22
]
, which is an analogue of Schutz’s formula for TASEP in the PNG context.
In principle, one could hope to pass from this to a Fredholm determinant formula.
But it has only been accomplished for a few special initial conditions (see
[
JR22
]
).
In this paper, we will prove Thm.
2.3
by directly checking that the right hand side of (
2.28
) solves the Kolmogorov backward equation for PNG.

Remark 2.4
.

We feel that the proof of (
2.28
) using the backward equation has considerable explanatory power. Although it turns out to be somewhat technical, the key fact that makes it work, Prop.
4.8
, is transparent and basic, explaining why hit kernels will produce transition probabilities for such models, and, we hope, opening the way to stochastic analysis of the KPZ fixed point itself. In
[
NQR20a
]
, the backward equation was also proved for the TASEP Fredholm determinant formulas. However, the key mechanism is not apparent there. The identification of this mechanism is one of the most important contributions of this article. It is also important to note that a proof like this, via the backward equation, can only be achieved once one has a formula for general initial data.

Remark 2.5
.

The finite propagation speed of the PNG dynamics implies that if
|x_{1}-x_{2}|>2t
then
h(t,x_{1})
and
h(t,x_{2})
are independent.
This fact can actually be seen from the Fredholm determinant formula (
2.28
): restricting to the two-point case for simplicity, in such a situation we have
P^{\hit(h_{0})}_{x_{2}-t,x_{1}+t}=0
, so
(K_{r})_{2,1}=0
, and thus the kernel is upper-triangular, which means that its Fredholm determinant equals the product of the Fredholm determinants of its diagonal entries, i.e.
\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-(K_{r})_{1,1})_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-(K_{r})_{2,2})_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})}
.
Note that this means, in particular, that there is no dependence on the
(K_{r})_{1,2}
entry of the kernel, which is the only one depending on
h_{0}
outside of
[x_{1}-t,x_{1}+t]\cup[x_{2}-t,x_{2}+t]
(which cannot affect the distribution of
h(t,x_{1})
and
h(t,x_{2})
).
The same argument works when there are several clusters of
x_{i}
’s which are separated by distance at least
2t
.

The non-Abelian Toda equations for PNG follow from (
2.28
) and the structure of the kernel
K_{r}
.
A key relation to this effect which is satisfied by the kernel is

(2.29)

\partial_{\eta}K_{r}(u,v)=K_{r-1}(u+1,v)-K_{r}(u+1,v),\quad\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}(u,v)=K_{r-1}(u,v+1)-K_{r}(u,v+1),

provided
r_{i}\geq r_{0}(t,x_{i})
for each
i
.
The two identities together yield
\partial_{\eta}\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}=K_{r+1}-2K_{r}+K_{r-1}
, which effectively provides a linear evolution for
K_{r}
from which the PNG multipoint distributions can be recovered through the Fredholm determinant, thus presenting the model as a stochastic integrable system.
The identities in (
2.29
) follow directly (and for all
r
) from the definition (
2.12
)/(
2.13
) if one ignores the dependence of the scattering transform
{T}^{h_{0}}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}
on the
x_{i}
’s and
t
.
Showing that this factor does not contribute to
\partial_{\eta}K_{r}
and
\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}
requires a separate argument, see Sec.
3.3
.

The formula (
2.28
) for the PNG finite dimensional distributions can be expressed equivalently in terms of the kernel
K^{\text{{ext}}}
from (
2.13
), which is how formulas of this type have often been written in the literature.
This way of writing the formula is convenient for some computations.
To state it, for a fixed vector
r\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and indices
x_{1}<\dotsm<x_{n}
let

(2.30)

\chi_{r}(x_{j},u)=\mathbf{1}_{u>r_{j}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bar{\chi}_{r}(x_{j},u)=\mathbf{1}_{u\leq r_{j}},

which are regarded as multiplication operators acting on the space
\ell^{2}(\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m}\}\times\mathbb{Z})
. We will also use this notation when
n=1
and
r\in\mathbb{Z}
, writing
\chi_{r}(u)=1-\bar{\chi}_{r}(u)=\mathbf{1}_{u>r}
.
Then we have, for
r_{i}\geq r_{0}(t,x_{i})
,
i=1,\dotsc,n
,

(2.31)

F(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-\chi_{r}K^{\text{{ext}}}\chi_{r})_{\ell^{2}(\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}\}\times\mathbb{Z}}.

On the other hand, the basic operators which make up the kernels appearing in (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) can be expressed as integral operators with kernels having an explicit contour integral formula:

(2.32)

e^{2t\nabla+x\Delta}(u_{1},u_{2})=e^{-2x}\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\oint_{\gamma_{0}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z^{u_{2}-u_{1}+1}}e^{t(z-z^{-1})+x(z+z^{-1})},

where
\gamma_{0}
is any simple, positively oriented contour around the origin.
When
t>|x|
this kernel can be expressed in terms of the Bessel function of the first kind,
J_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\oint_{\gamma_{0}}\mathrm{d}z\,e^{x(z-z^{-1})/2}/z^{n+1}
:

(2.33)

e^{2t\nabla+x\Delta}(u_{1},u_{2})=e^{-2x}\left(\tfrac{t-x}{t+x}\right)^{(u_{2}-u_{1})/2}\!J_{u_{2}-u_{1}}\bigl(2\sqrt{t^{2}-x^{2}}\bigr).

When
t=0
there is another Bessel function expression for the kernel of
e^{x\Delta}
for
x\geq 0
(i.e., for the semigroup of the walk
\mathsf{N}
):

(2.34)

e^{x\Delta}(u_{1},u_{2})=e^{-2x}I_{|u_{2}-u_{1}|}(2x),

where
I_{n}(2x)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\oint_{\gamma_{0}}\mathrm{d}z\,e^{x(z+z^{-1})}/z^{n+1}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind.
Formula (
2.32
) can be proved by differentiation in
t
and
x
, while (
2.33
) and (
2.34
) follow from the standard contour integral formulas for the Bessel functions
[
Nis
]
.

2.4.
KPZ universality

The
strong KPZ universality conjecture
states that for any model in the class there is an analogue of the height function and if
\varepsilon^{1/2}h_{0}(\varepsilon^{-1}x)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}_{0}(x)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
then there are (non-universal)

c
and
C_{\varepsilon}
such that

(2.35)

\varepsilon^{1/2}h(c\hskip 0.50003pt\varepsilon^{-3/2}t,\varepsilon^{-1}x)-C_{\varepsilon}t\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\mathfrak{h}(t,x;\mathfrak{h}_{0}),

where
\mathfrak{h}(t,x;\mathfrak{h}_{0})
is
the KPZ fixed point starting from
\mathfrak{h}_{0}

[
MQR21
]
. This can alternatively be interpreted as the definition of the KPZ class.
KPZ universality has been achieved in only a few cases, usually by showing that Fredholm determinant formulas for the transition probabilities converge to those of the KPZ fixed point.
There are two steps: 1. Pointwise convergence of the operator kernels; 2. Upgrade to trace class convergence, or analogous bounds.
The latter tends to be tedious and difficult, and is sometimes skipped in the literature. In the case of PNG, the pointwise convergence of the kernels can be checked by observation. It will be upgraded to trace class in a future article.
But for PNG, there is anyway a more direct route using the variational formula (
2.3
). Implicit in the article
[
DV21
]
is the statement that under the 1:2:3 scaling, (
2.35
),
{A}_{t}(x,y)
converges to the rescaled Airy sheet
t^{1/3}{A}(t^{-2/3}x,t^{-2/3}y)
and therefore that if one has uniform convergence on compact sets of the rescaled initial data to a compactly supported
\mathfrak{h}_{0}
, then at time
t>0
one has

\varepsilon^{1/2}h(\varepsilon^{-3/2}t,\varepsilon^{-1}x)-2\varepsilon^{-1}t\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{sup}}}}\displaylimits_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\big\{t^{1/3}{A}(t^{-2/3}x,t^{-2/3}y)+\mathfrak{h}_{0}(y)\}

in the same sense.
The right hand side is the variational description of the KPZ fixed point
\mathfrak{h}(t,x;\mathfrak{h}_{0})
(see
[
MQR21
,
DOV18
,
NQR20
]
).

For the KPZ fixed point
\mathfrak{h}(t,x)
, we showed in
[
QR22
]
that

\mathfrak{q}=\partial_{r}^{2}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{log}}}}\nolimits\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(t,x)\leq r)

satisfies the scalar Kadomtsev–Petviashvili (KP-II) equation

(2.36)

\partial_{t}\mathfrak{q}+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{r}\mathfrak{q}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{12}\partial_{r}^{3}\mathfrak{q}+\tfrac{1}{4}\partial_{r}^{-1}\partial_{x}^{2}\mathfrak{q}=0.

An equation, with the flavor of (
2.18
)/(
2.19
), was derived in that case also for the multipoint case.
In order to write it down, we let
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}(t;x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n};r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n};\mathfrak{h}_{0})
denote the
Brownian scattering operator
introduced in
[
MQR21
]
, which plays the role of the PNG kernel
K_{r}
for the KPZ fixed point, so that

\mathfrak{F}(t;x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n};r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n};\mathfrak{h}_{0})\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(t,x_{i})\leq r_{i},\,i=1,\dotsc,n)=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K})_{\oplus_{n}L^{2}([0,\infty))}.

Consider also the variables
\bar{r}=r_{1}+\dotsm+r_{n}
,
\overline{x}=x_{1}+\dotsm+x_{n}
, and write as before
\partial_{\bar{r}}=\partial_{r_{1}}+\dotsm+\partial_{r_{n}}
and
\partial_{\overline{x}}=\partial_{x_{1}}+\dotsm+\partial_{x_{n}}
.
Finally let

\mathfrak{Q}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{K}(0,0),

which is in
GL(n)
.
Then the result of
[
QR22
]
is that

\partial_{r}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{log}}}}\nolimits\mathfrak{F}=\tr(\mathfrak{Q})

while
\mathfrak{Q}
and its derivative
\mathfrak{q}=\partial_{r}\mathfrak{Q}
solve the matrix KP equation

(2.37)

\partial_{t}\mathfrak{q}+\tfrac{1}{2}\hskip 0.50003pt\partial_{r}\mathfrak{q}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{12}\partial_{r}^{3}\mathfrak{q}+\tfrac{1}{4}\partial_{x}^{2}\mathfrak{Q}+\tfrac{1}{2}[\mathfrak{q},\hskip 0.50003pt\partial_{x}\mathfrak{Q}]=0,

where
[A,B]=AB-BA
.

The following gives a twist on the convergence of PNG to the KPZ fixed point: Instead of convergence of kernels, one can check convergence of equations.
The argument which we will present is far from giving a complete proof, which would require, in addition to controlling the expansions, a uniqueness proof at the level of the KP equations for the KPZ fixed point.
This will be the subject of future research.
At any rate, the route of
[
DV21
]
is preferable for a rigorous proof here.
However, we believe that convergence of equations has considerable explanatory value as a direct and intuitive route to the universal fluctuations.
Well-known behaviours, such as the Tracy-Widom GUE and GOE one-point asymptotic fluctuations in KPZ models, are explained by their appearance as special self-similar solutions of (
2.36
), so such a derivation helps us understand the emergence of non-standard fluctuations in the KPZ class.
The idea is that their appearance is through such
normal form
equations.

In the flat case
\mathfrak{h}_{0}\equiv 0
, the
n=1

\mathfrak{q}
in (
2.36
) does not depend on
x
and the equation simplifies to the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV).
The derivation of the KdV equation from the Toda lattice (
2.25
) is a folklore result with a long history.
However, examination of the literature actually reveals a consensus that it does not actually hold unless one reduces the problem, often by considering waves traveling in only one direction.
For example,
[
BP87
]
states that, “it cannot be literally true” that KdV is the limit of the Toda lattice “since KdV is first order in time and antisymmetric” (in
r
), but instead has to be derived from first order versions, the Langmuir, or Kac-van Moerbeke lattice.
This approach goes back to considerable work on the problem of convergence to KdV by Toda himself.
In
[
TW73
]
it is the Boussinesq equation that is derived from Toda, which is then reduced to KdV by considering only one-way waves.

The following informal derivation (in the more general setting of non-Abelian 2D Toda and matrix KP) shows that the misconception was because the unexpected, highly refined 1:2:3 scaling was missed. It is our hope that it inspires further work.

2.4.1.
Scalar KP equation

We start with the scalar equation (
2.24
), where the derivation is easier to follow.
The 1:2:3 rescaling (
2.35
) means we are interested in
\mathfrak{F}_{\varepsilon}
defined by

(2.38)

F(t,x,r)=\mathfrak{F}_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{3/2}t,\varepsilon x,\varepsilon^{1/2}(r-ct)).

For now we let the linear shift in the third argument depend on a constant
c>0
, to be determined.
For the derivation, we have to expand
\mathfrak{F}_{\varepsilon}
in Taylor series.
We do not attempt a justification here.
Furthermore, in the
r
variable,
\mathfrak{F}_{\varepsilon}
really lives on
\varepsilon^{1/2}\mathbb{Z}
, yet we will pretend it is defined on
\mathbb{R}
and still expand in Taylor series. This type of argument is standard in such derivations, though, again, considerable work would be required to justify it.

Under this scaling, (
2.24
) becomes

(2.39)

\tfrac{1}{4}(\varepsilon^{3}\partial_{t}^{2}-2\varepsilon^{2}c\partial^{2}_{tr}+\varepsilon c^{2}\partial^{2}_{r}-\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{x}^{2})\mathop{\mathop{\text{{log}}}}\nolimits\mathfrak{F}=\tfrac{\mathfrak{F}(\cdot+\varepsilon^{1/2})\mathfrak{F}(\cdot-\varepsilon^{1/2})}{\mathfrak{F}^{2}}-1,

where for notational simplicity we removed the
\varepsilon
from the subscript in
\mathfrak{F}_{\varepsilon}
.
Expanding (
2.39
) out and multiplying by
\mathfrak{F}^{2}
, we get

(2.40)

\tfrac{\varepsilon c^{2}}{4}\mathfrak{F}\hskip 1.00006pt\partial_{r}^{2}\mathfrak{F}-\tfrac{\varepsilon c^{2}}{4}(\partial_{r}\mathfrak{F})^{2}-\tfrac{\varepsilon^{2}c}{2}\mathfrak{F}\hskip 1.00006pt\partial_{tr}^{2}\mathfrak{F}+\tfrac{\varepsilon^{2}c}{2}\partial_{t}\mathfrak{F}\hskip 1.00006pt\partial_{r}\mathfrak{F}-\tfrac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\mathfrak{F}\hskip 1.00006pt\partial_{x}^{2}\mathfrak{F}+\tfrac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}(\partial_{x}\mathfrak{F})^{2}\\
=\varepsilon\mathfrak{F}\hskip 1.00006pt\partial_{r}^{2}\mathfrak{F}-\varepsilon(\partial_{r}\mathfrak{F})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\big(\tfrac{1}{12}\mathfrak{F}\hskip 1.00006pt\partial_{r}^{4}\mathfrak{F}-\tfrac{1}{3}\partial_{r}\mathfrak{F}\hskip 1.00006pt\partial_{r}^{3}\mathfrak{F}+\tfrac{1}{4}(\partial^{2}_{r}\mathfrak{F})^{2}\big)+o(\varepsilon^{2}).

Clearly we should choose average growth rate
c=2
in order for the
O(\varepsilon)
terms to cancel. We are left with an equation at order
\varepsilon^{2}
which says that the associated bilinear form should vanish:

(2.41)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}\partial_{tr}^{2}\mathfrak{F}-\partial_{t}\mathfrak{F}\hskip 1.00006pt\partial_{r}\mathfrak{F}+\tfrac{1}{4}\mathfrak{F}\hskip 1.00006pt\partial_{x}^{2}\mathfrak{F}-\tfrac{1}{4}(\partial_{x}\mathfrak{F})^{2}+\tfrac{1}{12}\mathfrak{F}\hskip 1.00006pt\partial_{r}^{4}\mathfrak{F}-\tfrac{1}{3}\partial_{r}\mathfrak{F}\hskip 1.00006pt\partial_{r}^{3}\mathfrak{F}+\tfrac{1}{4}(\partial^{2}_{r}\mathfrak{F})^{2}=0.

This is the Hirota form of the scalar KP equation, and a simple computation shows that it means that
\mathfrak{q}=\partial_{r}^{2}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{log}}}}\nolimits\mathfrak{F}
satisfies (
2.36
).

Since we already learned the average growth rate

(2.43)

\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{2}+\Omega_{3}=0

and where, as before, we have removed the subscript

\displaystyle I+\varepsilon^{1/2}\mathfrak{Q}(r+\varepsilon^{1/2})

\displaystyle(I+\varepsilon^{1/2}\mathfrak{Q}(r))^{-1}

\displaystyle(I+\varepsilon^{1/2}\mathfrak{Q}(r-\varepsilon^{1/2}))^{-1}

(2.48)

\displaystyle\hskip-104.79128pt=I-\varepsilon^{1/2}\mathfrak{Q}+\varepsilon(\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}+\mathfrak{Q}^{2})-\varepsilon^{3/2}(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime\prime}+\mathfrak{Q}\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}+\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}\mathfrak{Q}+\mathfrak{Q}^{3})

(2.49)

\displaystyle\hskip-101.17755pt+\varepsilon^{2}(\tfrac{1}{6}\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime\prime\prime}+(\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime})^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathfrak{Q}\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime\prime}\mathfrak{Q}+\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}\mathfrak{Q}^{2}+\mathfrak{Q}\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}\mathfrak{Q}+\mathfrak{Q}^{2}\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}+\mathfrak{Q}^{4})

(2.50)

Using these expansions in the definition of

and collecting terms carefully, one sees that only terms of order

and higher remain, and after simplification one gets

(2.52)

\displaystyle\Omega_{3}=-\varepsilon^{3/2}\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon^{2}((\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime})^{2}+\mathfrak{Q}\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime\prime})-\varepsilon^{5/2}\Big((\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime})^{2}\mathfrak{Q}+\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}\mathfrak{Q}\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}+\mathfrak{Q}(\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime})^{2}+\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime\prime}\mathfrak{Q}^{2}

(2.53)

\displaystyle\hskip 231.26378pt+\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime\prime}\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}+\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime\prime}\mathfrak{Q})+\tfrac{1}{12}\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\Big)+o(\varepsilon^{5/2}).

Now we note that the only term of order
\varepsilon^{3/2}
and lower in the first three lines of (
2.44
) is
\partial_{r}^{2}\mathfrak{Q}
, which cancels the first term in (
2.52
).
We also have cancellation at order
\varepsilon^{2}
.
Thus the equation appears as the vanishing of terms of order
\varepsilon^{5/2}
.
From (
2.44
) we have

(2.54)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{5/2}~~\text{{coeff. of }}\Omega_{1}=

\displaystyle-\partial_{tr}^{2}\mathfrak{Q}-\tfrac{1}{4}\partial_{x}^{2}\mathfrak{Q}+\partial_{r}^{2}\mathfrak{Q}\mathfrak{Q}^{2},

(2.55)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{5/2}~~\text{{coeff. of }}\Omega_{2}=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{r}\mathfrak{Q}\partial_{x}\mathfrak{Q}+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\mathfrak{Q}\partial_{r}\mathfrak{Q}+\partial_{r}\mathfrak{Q}\mathfrak{Q}\partial_{r}\mathfrak{Q}+(\partial_{r}\mathfrak{Q})^{2}\mathfrak{Q}.

Adding them together with the
\varepsilon^{5/2}
coefficient of
\Omega_{3}
we get

(2.56)

-\partial_{tr}^{2}\mathfrak{Q}-\tfrac{1}{4}\partial_{x}^{2}\mathfrak{Q}-\tfrac{1}{2}[\partial_{r}\mathfrak{Q},\partial_{x}\mathfrak{Q}]-\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{r}^{2}\mathfrak{Q}\hskip 0.50003pt\partial_{r}\mathfrak{Q}-\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{r}\mathfrak{Q}\hskip 0.50003pt\partial_{r}^{2}\mathfrak{Q}-\tfrac{1}{12}\partial_{r}^{4}\mathfrak{Q}=0,

which is the matrix KP equation.

2.5.
Narrow wedge and flat examples, discrete Painlevé II

2.5.1.
Narrow wedge initial data

Consider PNG with narrow wedge initial condition
\mathfrak{d}_{0}
, defined in (
2.1
).
The scaling limit of the height function
h(t,x)
for fixed
t=1
was first derived in
[
PS02
]
, where the limiting Airy
2
process was first described, using the transfer matrix method for the Fermi field associated to a multilayer version of the process constructed using dynamics based on the Robinson-Schensted-Knuth (RSK) correspondence.
RSK is also behind the methods employed in
[
Joh03
]
to study a discrete version of PNG, for which uniform convergence on compacts to the same Airy
2
process was obtained.

We begin by showing how the formulas obtained earlier in the literature can be recovered from Thm.
2.3
.
Note that for narrow wedge initial data one has
h(t,x)=-\infty
for all
|x|>t
.
This can be thought of alternatively as restricting the model (and, in particular, the nucleations) to the domain
\{(t,x)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\!:|x|\leq t\}
, which is how it has been often defined in the literature in this case (see e.g.
[
PS02
]
).
The random walk
\mathsf{N}
can only hit
\hypo(\mathfrak{d}_{0})
at the origin, so

P^{\hit(\mathfrak{d}_{0})}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}=\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}-t\leq 0\leq x_{j}+t}e^{(t-x_{i})\Delta}\bar{\chi}_{0}e^{(x_{j}-t)\Delta}
,
and thus
{T}^{\mathfrak{d}_{0}}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}=\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}-t\leq 0\leq x_{j}+t}\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{\chi}_{0}
and the kernel in (
2.12
) equals

(2.57)

K^{{\text{{ext}}}}(x_{i},\cdot;x_{j},\cdot)=-\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}<x_{j}}e^{(x_{j}-x_{i})\Delta}+\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}-t\leq 0\leq x_{j}+t}e^{-2t\nabla-x_{i}\Delta}\bar{\chi}_{0}e^{2t\nabla+x_{j}\Delta}.

The indicators
\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}\leq t}
and
\mathbf{1}_{x_{j}\geq-t}
are natural because the model is essentially restricted to the domain
|x|\leq t
.
In particular, if
x_{\ell}>t
for some
\ell
and the
x_{j}
’s are ordered, then
x_{j}>t
for all
\ell\leq j\leq m
so
K^{\text{{ext}}}(x_{i},\cdot;x_{j},\cdot)=-\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}<x_{j}}e^{(x_{j}-x_{i})\Delta}
for
i\geq j\geq\ell
, and thus
K^{\text{{ext}}}
can be decomposed as

\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}(K^{\text{{ext}}})(x_{i},\cdot;x_{j},\cdot))_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell-1}&\star\\
\hline\cr 0&{\sf U}\end{array}\right]
,
where the value of the
\star
is irrelevant and
{\sf U}
is strictly upper triangular.
Thus in this case we have
F(t,x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n},r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits\bigl(I-\chi_{r}K^{\PNG}_{t,{\text{{ext}}}}\chi_{a}\bigr)_{\ell^{2}(\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\ell-1}\}\times\mathbb{Z})}
, which equals
F(t,x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\ell},r_{1},\dotsc,r_{\ell})
.
An analogous statement holds in the case
x_{\ell}<-t
for some
\ell
.

We focus now on (
2.57
) in the case
|x_{i}|<t
for all
i
(we throw away the case when some
x_{i}=\pm t
in order to simplify the presentation; at any rate this case is physically uninteresting because by definition of the process we know that
h(t,\pm t)=0
almost surely).
Assume first that
x_{i}\geq x_{j}
.
Using (
2.33
), the kernel (
2.57
) turns into

(2.58)

K^{\text{{ext}}}(x_{i},u_{i};x_{j},u_{j})=e^{2(x_{i}-x_{j})}\sum_{\ell\leq 0}\left(\tfrac{t+x_{i}}{t-x_{i}}\right)^{\frac{u_{i}-\ell}{2}}\!\!J_{u_{i}-\ell}\bigl({\scriptstyle{2\sqrt{t^{2}-x_{i}^{2}}}}\bigr)\left(\tfrac{t-x_{j}}{t+x_{j}}\right)^{\frac{u_{j}-\ell}{2}}J_{u_{j}-\ell}\bigl({\scriptstyle{2\sqrt{t^{2}-x_{j}^{2}}}}\bigr).

If instead we have
x_{i}<x_{j}
, we write
e^{(x_{j}-x_{i})\Delta}=e^{-2t\nabla-x_{i}\Delta}e^{2t\nabla+x_{j}\Delta}
, and obtain (
2.58
) with a minus sign in front.
This shows that
K^{\text{{ext}}}(x_{j},u_{j};x_{i},u_{i})=e^{2(x_{i}-x_{j})}B^{*}_{t}(u_{i},-x_{i};u_{j},-x_{j})
, where
B_{t}
is the
extended discrete Bessel kernel
derived in
[
PS02
, Eq. 3.52]
3
3

3

After correcting a minor typo in that paper:
\ell+1/2
inside the Heaviside function in (3.52) there should be
\ell-1/2
.
.
Having the adjoint of
B_{t}
does not change the value of the Fredholm determinant, nor does the conjugation of the kernel by
e^{2(x_{i}-x_{j})}
, while flipping the sign of the

’s corresponds to reflecting the model about the

-axis, which again does not change anything because the dynamics and the initial condition are symmetric.
Hence, after setting

, this recovers the formula derived in
[
PS02
]
.

Consider now the one-point case.
Using the above formula we have

K^{{\text{{ext}}}}(x,u;x,v)=\left(\tfrac{t+x}{t-x}\right)^{(u-v)/2}B_{s}(u,v),\qquad B_{s}(u,v)=\sum_{\ell\leq 0}J_{u-\ell}\bigl(2s\bigr)J_{v-\ell}\bigl(2s\bigr)

with

is the usual

[
BOO00
,
Joh01
]

in the sense of
[

]

and
B_{s}(u,u)=s(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}J_{u-1}(2s)J_{u}(2s)-J_{u-1}(2s)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}J_{u}(2s))

From
[
BO00
]

from

, we can rewrite

(2.59)

F_{r}(s)\coloneqq F(t,x,r)=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-\chi_{r}B_{s}\chi_{r})_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}=e^{-s^{2}}D_{r-1}(\varphi_{s}),

where

is the

(setting

F_{r}(s)=e^{-s^{2}}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I_{i-j}(2s))_{i,j=0,\ldots,r-1},

where the

are modified Bessel functions of the first kind (see (

This formula goes back to
[

]
, and was the starting point of the analysis in
[
BDJ99
]
.

The Toeplitz determinants

are intimately related to the orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle associated with the weight
\varphi_{s}
.
In the Szegő three term recurrence for such orthogonal polynomials on the circle with respect to a general weight
[

]
, only one coefficient
\alpha_{n}
depends on the choice of weight.
Thus the

, “encode” the weight, and can be thought of as the analogue of reflection coefficients in this context.
In the case of our weight

they are real, and related to

through

\alpha_{r}^{2}=1-F_{r}F_{r+2}/{F_{r-1}^{2}}.

They are known (see e.g.
[

, Sec. 3]
) to satisfy the discrete Painlevé II equation

(2.62)

-s(1-\alpha_{r}^{2})(\alpha_{r+1}+\alpha_{r-1})=(r+1)\alpha_{r}

(see also
[
Bor03
]
) as well as the Ablowitz-Ladik lattice

(2.63)

\partial_{s}\alpha_{r}=(1-\alpha_{r}^{2})(\alpha_{r+1}-\alpha_{r-1})

(see also
[
AM01
]
).
The Ablowitz-Ladik hierarchy is a reduction of the 2D Toda hierarchy
[
Tak18
]
. However, it is not clear if (

(2.64)

\tfrac{1}{4}(\partial_{s}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{s}\partial_{s})\mathop{\mathop{\text{{log}}}}\nolimits F_{r}=\tfrac{F_{r+1}F_{r-1}}{F_{r}^{2}}-1.

We mention also that in Thm. 7.12 of
[
BDS16
]
it is shown, using the Riemann-Hilbert problem for the Toeplitz determinant

, that the functions

2.5.2.
Flat initial data

Consider now PNG with flat initial condition
h_{0}\equiv 0
.
For fixed
t=1
, this case was analyzed in
[
BFS08
]
.
Clearly it is enough to consider
r_{i}\geq 0
for each
i
.
In view of (
2.31
), this means that we are interested in the kernel evaluated at values of
u_{i}>0
for each
i
.
In that region, the scattering kernel
e^{-2t\nabla-x_{i}\Delta}{T}^{0}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}e^{2t\nabla+x_{j}\Delta}=e^{-2t\nabla+t\Delta}P^{\hit(0)}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}e^{2t\nabla-t\Delta}

(2.65)

\mathbf{1}_{2t\geq x_{i}-x_{j}}\big(e^{-2t\nabla+t\Delta}\bar{\chi}_{0}e^{2t\nabla+(x_{j}-x_{i}+t)\Delta}+e^{-2t\nabla+t\Delta}\chi_{0}P^{\hit(0)}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}e^{2t\nabla-t\Delta}\big).

A simple computation using (
2.32
) shows that the first term inside the parenthesis can be represented by the contour integral

\frac{e^{2(x_{i}-x_{j})}}{(2\pi{\rm i})^{2}}\oint_{\hskip 0.81949pt\gamma_{0}}\mathrm{d}z\oint_{\hskip 0.81949pt\gamma_{0}}\hskip-1.00006pt\mathrm{d}w\frac{z^{u_{i}-1}}{w^{u_{j}}(w-z)}e^{2t(w-z)+(x_{j}-x_{i})(w+w^{-1})}

with the contours chosen so that
|w|>|z|
. But in the case of interest,
u_{i}>0
, the integrand is analytic in
z
and hence this term vanishes.
The second term on the right hand side of (
2.65
) is computed using the reflection principle for
\mathsf{N}
: assuming
x_{j}-x_{i}+2t\geq 0
and
u>0
, and writing
\underline{x}=x_{i}-t
,
\overline{x}=x_{j}+t

P^{\hit(0)}_{\underline{x},\overline{x}}(u,v)=e^{(\overline{x}-\underline{x})\Delta}(u,-v)\mathbf{1}_{v>0}+e^{(\overline{x}-\underline{x})\Delta}(u,v)\mathbf{1}_{v\leq 0}.

P^{\hit(0)}_{\underline{x},\overline{x}}e^{2t\nabla-t\Delta}(u,v)
equals the sum of two integrals,

\frac{e^{2(\overline{x}-\underline{x})}}{(2\pi{\rm i})^{2}}\hskip-0.50003pt\oint_{\hskip 0.81949pt\gamma_{0}}\hskip-1.00006pt\mathrm{d}z\hskip-1.00006pt\oint_{\hskip 0.81949pt\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}\hskip-1.00006pt\mathrm{d}w\,\frac{z^{u}}{w^{v}(1-wz)}e^{(\overline{x}-\underline{x})(z+\frac{1}{z})-\frac{2t}{w}}

|wz|<1

\frac{e^{2(\overline{x}-\underline{x})}}{(2\pi{\rm i})^{2}}\oint_{\hskip 0.81949pt\gamma_{0}}\mathrm{d}z\oint_{\hskip 0.81949pt\gamma_{0}}^{\prime}\hskip-1.00006pt\mathrm{d}w\frac{z^{u-1}}{w^{v}(w-z)}e^{(\overline{x}-\underline{x})(z+\frac{1}{z})-\frac{2t}{w}}

with contours chosen so that
|w|>|z|
.
The two integrals can be brought together if we choose contours with
|z|<1
and
|z|<|w|<1/|z|
, and simplifying we get

(2.66)

\textstyle P^{\hit(0)}_{\underline{x},\overline{x}}e^{2t\nabla-t\Delta}(u,v)=\frac{e^{2(\overline{x}-\underline{x})}}{(2\pi{\rm i})^{2}}\oint_{\hskip 0.81949pt\gamma_{0}}\mathrm{d}z\,\frac{1-z^{2}}{z^{1-u}}e^{(\overline{x}-\underline{x})(z+z^{-1})}\oint_{\hskip 0.81949pt\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}\hskip-1.00006pt\mathrm{d}w\,\frac{1}{w^{v}(w-z)(1-zw)}e^{-2tw^{-1}}.

\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\oint_{\gamma_{0}^{\prime\prime}}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{w^{v}(w-z)(1-zw)}e^{-2tw^{-1}}-\frac{z^{v}}{z^{2}-1}e^{-2tz}

\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\oint_{\gamma_{0}^{\prime\prime\prime}}\mathrm{d}w\,\frac{w^{v}}{(w-z)(1-zw)}e^{-2tw}-\frac{z^{v}}{z^{2}-1}e^{-2tz}
,
where the contour includes only the singularity at
0
.
We are interested only in the case
v>0
, for which the
w
integral vanishes, and thus using this for such
v

e^{2(\overline{x}-\underline{x})}\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\oint_{\gamma_{0}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z^{u+v+1}}e^{2tz+(x_{j}-x_{i})(z+z^{-1})}

where in the last integral we changed

to
z^{-1}

Continuing the computation in the same way we get, for

|z|>|w|
.
Now we enlarge the

.

as

.

(2.67)

\displaystyle\textstyle K^{\text{{ext}}}(x_{i},u_{i};x_{j},u_{j})

\displaystyle\textstyle=-e^{2(x_{i}-x_{j})}I_{|u_{i}-u_{j}|}(2(x_{j}-x_{i}))\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}<x_{j}}

\displaystyle\textstyle\qquad+\mathbf{1}_{2t\geq x_{i}-x_{j}}e^{2(x_{i}-x_{j})}\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\oint_{\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{w^{u_{i}+u_{j}+1}}e^{(x_{j}-x_{i})(w+w^{-1})}e^{2t(w-w^{-1})}.

3.
Derivation of the non-Abelian Toda equation

Our goal in this section is to prove Thm.
2.2
.
The proof is divided in three parts.
In Sec.
3.1
, we abstract away details particular to PNG and prove the non-Abelian Toda equations for a general class of matrix kernels
K_{r}
satisfying certain structural conditions.
In Sec.
3.2
we explain how the general result is used to derive the non-Abelian Toda equations for a simplified version of the PNG kernel where the dependence on the interval
[x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t]
in the scattering transform
{T}^{h_{0}}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}
in (
2.12
) is dropped.
Finally in Sec.
3.3
we show that dropping this dependence does not matter in the region above
r_{0}(t,x)
.

3.1.
General kernel

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix two integers
\underaccent{\bar}{r}<\bar{r}-1
and some open domain
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Let
K_{\eta,\zeta,r}
be a family of kernels defined for
r\in\{\underaccent{\bar}{r},\dotsc,\bar{r}\}
and
(\eta,\zeta)\in O
, which acts on
\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
, the
n
-fold direct sum of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
.
Write
K_{r}=K_{\eta,\zeta,r}
and suppose that its matrix kernel
K_{r}(u,v)
satisfies, for all
u,v>0
,

(1)

K_{r+1}(u,v)=K_{r}(u+1,v+1)
,
r\in\{\underaccent{\bar}{r},\dotsc,\bar{r}-1\}
,

(2)

\partial_{\eta}K_{r}(u,v)=K_{r-1}(u+1,v)-K_{r}(u+1,v)
,
r\in\{\underaccent{\bar}{r}+1,\dotsc,\bar{r}\}
,

(3)

\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}(u,v)=K_{r-1}(u,v+1)-K_{r}(u,v+1)
,
r\in\{\underaccent{\bar}{r}+1,\dotsc,\bar{r}\}
.

Assume that, for all
r\in\{\underaccent{\bar}{r},\dotsc,\bar{r}\}
and fixed
(\eta,\zeta)\in O
,
K_{r}
is trace class as an operator acting on
\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
, and that for such
r
the derivatives in (2) and (3) above exist in trace norm.
Assume furthermore that
I-K_{r}
is invertible for all
r\in\{\underaccent{\bar}{r},\dotsc,\bar{r}\}
and define the invertible
n\times n
matrix

(3.1)

\displaystyle Q_{r}=(I-K_{r})^{-1}(1,1).

Then for
r\in\{\underaccent{\bar}{r}+1,\dotsc,\bar{r}-1\}
and
(\eta,\zeta)\in O
,
Q_{r}

satisfies the non-Abelian 2D Toda equation

(3.2)

\partial_{\zeta}(\partial_{\eta}Q_{r}Q_{r}^{-1})+Q_{r}Q_{r-1}^{-1}-Q_{r+1}Q_{r}^{-1}=0.

We stress that, although we used the same notation,
K_{r}
and
Q_{r}
in this result are general and not necessarily related to PNG (in particular, the index
r
in these objects denotes an integer rather than element of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
).

Before starting the proof, we need to introduce some notations.
For
r\in\{\underaccent{\bar}{r},\dotsc,\bar{r}\}
let

R_{r}=(I-K_{r})^{-1},\qquad\text{{so}}\quad Q_{r}=R_{r}(1,1),

and define, for
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
,

\sigma f(u)=f(u+1),\qquad\sigma^{*}f(u)=f(u-1)\mathbf{1}_{u>1},

which have explicit kernels given by
\sigma(u,v)=\mathbf{1}_{v=u+1}
and
\sigma^{*}(u,v)=\mathbf{1}_{v=u-1>0}
.

Remark 3.2
.

In
[
BP87
]
it is shown, in this language, that if one has a scalar kernel
K_{r}(t)
satisfying
K_{r+1}=\sigma K_{r}=K_{r}\sigma^{*}
and
\partial_{t}K_{r}=K_{r+1}-K_{r-1}
, then
p_{r}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I+K_{r})
satisfies the one-sided bilinear Toda lattice equation
\dot{p}_{r+1}p_{r}-p_{r+1}\dot{p}_{r}=p_{r+2}p_{r-1}-p_{r+1}p_{r}
, which can be transformed into the classic Toda lattice (
2.25
) (see the introduction of
[
BP87
]
).

We will use the same notation
\sigma
for the diagonal matrix kernel with
\sigma
in each diagonal entry, which acts on
f\in\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
as above.
Then (1) in the assumptions of the theorem becomes

(3.3)

K_{r+1}=\sigma K_{r}\sigma^{*},

while

\sigma\sigma^{*}=I\qquad\text{and}\qquad P\coloneqq I-\sigma^{*}\sigma\quad\text{{is rank $n$}}.

P
is a diagonal matrix kernel with diagonal entries
\Pi
which have an explicit kernel given by

(3.4)

\Pi(u,v)=\mathbf{1}_{u=v=1}.

On the other hand, (2) and (3) in the assumptions of the theorem can be expressed in terms of
\sigma
and
\sigma^{*}
as

(3.5)

\partial_{\eta}K=\sigma(K_{r-1}-K_{r}),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}=(K_{r-1}-K_{r})\sigma^{*}.

By taking the
\eta
derivative of the first or the
\zeta
derivative of the second, we also have

(3.6)

\partial_{\eta}\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}=K_{r+1}-2K_{r}+K_{r-1}.

The proof of Thm.
3.1
is based on two identities which we collect in the next result:

Lemma 3.3
.

(i)

(I+PR_{r}K_{r}P)^{-1}=(I-P)+(I-K_{r})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})P
.

(ii)

(I+PR_{r}K_{r}P)(I+PR_{r-1}K_{r-1}P)^{-1}=I+PR_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma K_{r-1})P
.

Proof.

The first step of the proof is to show the following two simple identities:

(3.7)

\displaystyle(I+PR_{r}K_{r})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(I-K_{r})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r}),

(3.8)

\displaystyle(I+PR_{r}K_{r}P)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(I-P)+(I+PR_{r}K_{r})^{-1}P.

For the first one, use the fact that
R_{r}K_{r}=K_{r}R_{r}=R_{r}-I

to write
I+PR_{r}K_{r}=I-P+PR_{r}=(I-\sigma^{*}\sigma K_{r})R_{r}
, and then multiply by
(I-K_{r})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})
to get

(3.9)

\displaystyle(I+PR_{r}K_{r})(I-K_{r})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})=(I-\sigma^{*}\sigma K_{r})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})

(3.10)

\displaystyle\hskip 57.81621pt=I-\sigma^{*}\sigma K_{r}+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r}-\sigma^{*}\sigma K_{r}\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r}=I,

where in the last equality we used
\sigma K_{r}\sigma^{*}=K_{r+1}
together with
R_{r}K_{r}=K_{r}R_{r}=R_{r}-I
again.

To get (
3.8
), decompose
I+PR_{r}K_{r}P
as
(I+PR_{r}K_{r})-PR_{r}K_{r}(I-P)
and then multiply by
(I-P)+(I+PR_{r}K_{r})^{-1}P
on the right.
The result can be simplified using the fact that
P
is a projection and we get

I-PR_{r}K_{r}(I-P)(I+PR_{r}K_{r})^{-1}P=I,

the last equality following from the easily checked fact that
(I+PR_{r}K_{r})^{-1}P=P(I+R_{r}K_{r}P)^{-1}
.

The identity in (i) now follows directly from (
3.7
) and (
3.8
).
For (ii), write the left hand side as

I+P(R_{r}K_{r}-R_{r-1}K_{r-1})P(I+PR_{r-1}K_{r-1}P)^{-1}

and then use (i) to rewrite this as

(3.11)

I+P(R_{r}K_{r}-R_{r-1}K_{r-1})P(I-K_{r-1})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma K_{r-1})P,

where we used again the fact that
P
is a projection.
Now use (
3.3
) to write

(3.12)

\displaystyle(I-P)(I-K_{r-1})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma K_{r-1})P

\displaystyle=\sigma^{*}\sigma(I-K_{r-1})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma K_{r-1})P

(3.13)

\displaystyle=(\sigma^{*}\sigma(I-K_{r-1})+\sigma^{*}\sigma K_{r-1})P=\sigma^{*}\sigma P=0,

and use this to rewrite (
3.11
) further as

(3.14)

I+P(R_{r}K_{r}-R_{r-1}K_{r-1})(I-K_{r-1})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma K_{r-1})P.

Since
R_{r-1}K_{r-1}=K_{r-1}R_{r-1}
and
R_{r}K_{r}=R_{r}-I
we have

(R_{r}K_{r}-R_{r-1}K_{r-1})(I-K_{r-1})=R_{r}K_{r}(I-K_{r-1})-K_{r-1}=R_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})

and using this in (
3.14
) shows that it is equal to the desired right hand side.
∎

Proof of Thm.
3.1
.

Let

(3.15)

U_{r}=Q_{r}Q_{r-1}^{-1},\qquad V_{r}=-\partial_{\eta}Q_{r}Q_{r}^{-1}.

Then (
3.2
) can be rewritten as

(3.16)

\partial_{\zeta}V_{r}+U_{r+1}-U_{r}=0.

Our goal then will be to prove this last equation.

In order to achieve this, it is convenient to lift (
3.16
) to an equivalent equation for operators acting on
\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
.
To this end, we introduce the operators

\mathcal{X}_{r}=PR_{r}K_{r}P,\qquad\mathcal{Y}_{r}=I+\mathcal{X}_{r}=I+PR_{r}K_{r}P.

From the previous lemma,
\mathcal{Y}_{r}
is invertible.
On the other hand, since
K_{r}
is differentiable in
\eta
and
\zeta
in trace norm, its inverse
R_{r}
is also differentiable, and since
P
is finite rank then
\mathcal{X}_{r}
, and thus also
\mathcal{Y}_{r}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{-1}
, are differentiable in
\eta
and
\zeta
in trace norm.
In particular, we may introduce the following analogs of
U_{r}
and
V_{r}
in terms of the operator
\mathcal{Y}_{r}
:

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{r}=\mathcal{Y}_{r}\mathcal{Y}_{r-1}^{-1},\qquad\mathcal{V}_{r}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{Y}_{r}\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{-1}.

We claim that (
3.16
) is equivalent to the same equation at the level of
\mathcal{U}_{r}
and
\mathcal{V}_{r}
:

(3.18)

\partial_{\zeta}\mathcal{V}_{r}+\mathcal{U}_{r+1}-\mathcal{U}_{r}=0.

To see this note first that, in view of (
3.4
),
P
is a projection onto an
n
-dimensional subspace of
\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
, call it

Then we may think of

as acting on

as a block matrix operator

\mathcal{Y}_{r}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}I_{Z}+\mathcal{X}_{r}&0\\
0&I_{Z^{\perp}}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
,
and we have
\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{-1}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}(I_{Z}+\mathcal{X}_{r})^{-1}&0\\
0&I_{Z^{\perp}}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
.
In this way we thus have

\mathcal{U}_{r}=\begin{bmatrix}(I_{Z}+\mathcal{X}_{r})(I_{Z}+\mathcal{X}_{r-1})^{-1}&0\\

0&I_{Z^{\perp}}\end{bmatrix}.

In particular, (
3.18
) is completely trivial for the second row in this block representation.
For the first row we simply note that

is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the matrix of the linear transformation

, so the equation is equivalent to (

Hence our goal is to show (

).
Using

(3.19)

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}\hskip 0.50003pt\mathcal{Y}_{r}=P\partial_{\eta}R_{r}P=PR_{r}\partial_{\eta}K_{r}R_{r}P,\qquad\partial_{\zeta}\hskip 0.50003pt\mathcal{Y}_{r}=P\partial_{\zeta}R_{r}P=PR_{r}\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}R_{r}P,

(3.20)

\displaystyle\partial_{\zeta}\partial_{\eta}\hskip 0.50003pt\mathcal{Y}_{r}=P\partial_{\zeta}\partial_{\eta}R_{r}P=PR_{r}(\partial_{\zeta}\partial_{\eta}K_{r}+\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}R_{r}\partial_{\eta}K_{r}+\partial_{\eta}K_{r}R_{r}\partial_{\zeta}K_{r})R_{r}P,

so

(3.21)

\displaystyle\partial_{\zeta}\mathcal{V}_{r}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\zeta}\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{Y}_{r}\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{-1}+\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{Y}_{r}\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{-1}\partial_{\zeta}\mathcal{Y}_{r}\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{-1}

(3.22)

\displaystyle=-PR_{r}\Big[\partial_{\zeta}\partial_{\eta}K_{r}+\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}R_{r}\partial_{\eta}K_{r}+\partial_{\eta}K_{r}R_{r}\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}-\partial_{\eta}K_{r}R_{r}P\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{-1}PR_{r}\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}\Big]R_{r}P\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{-1}.

Now from (i) of Lem.
3.3
we have
R_{r}P\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{-1}=R_{r}P(I-K_{r})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})P
, while

(3.23)

\displaystyle R_{r}P(I-K_{r})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})

\displaystyle=I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r}-R_{r}\sigma^{*}\sigma(I-K_{r})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})

(3.24)

\displaystyle\hskip-40.0pt=I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r}-R_{r}(\sigma^{*}\sigma(I-K_{r})+\sigma^{*}(I-K_{r+1})R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})

\displaystyle\hskip-40.0pt=I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r}-R_{r}\sigma^{*}\sigma=I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r}-R_{r}(I-P),

where in the second equality we simplified using (

), and therefore

.
Using this in (

) gives

(3.26)

\partial_{\zeta}\mathcal{V}_{r}=-PR_{r}\Omega(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})P,

where

equals (using again the formula for

)

(3.27)

\displaystyle\partial_{\zeta}\partial_{\eta}K_{r}+\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}R_{r}\partial_{\eta}K_{r}+\partial_{\eta}K_{r}R_{r}\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}-\partial_{\eta}K_{r}R_{r}P\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{-1}PR_{r}\partial_{\zeta}K_{r}

(3.28)

\displaystyle\;\;=(K_{r+1}-2K_{r}+K_{r-1})+(K_{r}-K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma(K_{r}-K_{r-1})

(3.29)

\displaystyle\quad+\sigma(K_{r}-K_{r-1})R_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}-\sigma(K_{r}-K_{r-1})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})PR_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}

(3.30)

\displaystyle\;\;=(K_{r+1}-2K_{r}+K_{r-1})+(K_{r}-K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma(K_{r}-K_{r-1})

(3.31)

\displaystyle\quad-\sigma(K_{r}-K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r}PR_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}+\sigma(K_{r}-K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}\sigma R_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}

(3.32)

\displaystyle\;\;=(K_{r+1}\!-\!2K_{r}\!+\!K_{r-1})+(K_{r}\!-\!K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma(K_{r}\!-\!K_{r-1})+(K_{r+1}\!-\!K_{r})R_{r+1}(K_{r+1}\!-\!K_{r}),

and where we used (
3.3
) again together with the identity
R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r}P-\sigma=R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r}(I-\sigma^{*}\sigma)-\sigma=-R_{r+1}\sigma(I-K_{r})
.
On the other hand, (ii) of Lem.

\mathcal{U}_{r+1}-\mathcal{U}_{r}=PR_{r+1}(K_{r+1}-K_{r})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})P-PR_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma K_{r-1})P,

and using

R_{r+1}(K_{r+1}-K_{r})=R_{r}(K_{r+1}-K_{r})(I+R_{r+1}(K_{r+1}-K_{r}))
.

\mathcal{U}_{r+1}-\mathcal{U}_{r}=PR_{r}(K_{r+1}-K_{r})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})PPR_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma K_{r-1})P\\
\qquad+PR_{r}(K_{r+1}-K_{r})R_{r+1}(K_{r+1}-K_{r})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})P.

Comparing with (
3.26
), the last term on the right hand cancels with the piece coming from the third term in (

),
so we get

(3.34)

\displaystyle\partial_{\zeta}\mathcal{V}_{r}+\mathcal{U}_{r+1}-\mathcal{U}_{r}

\displaystyle=PR_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})P

(3.35)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad-PR_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma(K_{r}-K_{r-1})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})P

(3.36)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad-PR_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma K_{r-1})P

(3.37)

\displaystyle=PR_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}(R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r}-R_{r}\sigma K_{r-1})P

(3.38)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad-PR_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}R_{r}\sigma(K_{r}-K_{r-1})(I+\sigma^{*}R_{r+1}\sigma K_{r})P

\displaystyle=PR_{r}(K_{r}-K_{r-1})\sigma^{*}[R_{r+1}-R_{r}-R_{r}K_{r+1}R_{r+1}+R_{r}K_{r}R_{r+1}]\sigma K_{r}P.

The term in brackets vanishes as

3.18
) and completing the proof.
∎

3.2.
PNG kernel

Recall our definition
(K_{r})_{ij}(u,v)=K^{\text{{ext}}}(u+r_{i},v+r_{j})
.
In Sec.
2
we used
r
to denote the vector
(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Here it will be more convenient to regard
r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n}
as fixed parameters and
r
as an auxiliary variable taking values in
\mathbb{Z}
, and redefine

(3.40)

(K_{r})_{ij}(u,v)=K^{\text{{ext}}}(u+r_{i}+r,v+r_{j}+r).

This notation is consistent with our usage of
K_{r\pm 1}
in Sec.
2
, and then (
2.18
) coincides with (
3.16
) with
r=0
.
Therefore, to show that (
2.18
) holds at the given choice of parameters
(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})
satisfying
r_{i}>r_{0}(t,x_{i})
for each
i
, it is enough to show that
K_{r}
satisfies the assumptions of Thm.
3.1
with
\underaccent{\bar}{r}=-1
and
\bar{r}=1
.

That
K_{r}
satisfies (1) of Thm.
3.1
is straightforward by definition, so we turn to assumptions (2) and (3) of that result.
We fix
i
and
j
and focus on the
i,j
entry of
K_{r}
, which we denote by
K^{ij}_{r}
.
We need to check that (2) and (3) hold for
r\in\{\underaccent{\bar}{r}+1,\dotsc,\bar{r}\}
, which in our setting means
r\in\{0,1\}
.
From (
2.12
) we have that

\partial_{t}K^{ij}_{r}(u,v)
equals
K^{ij}_{r}(u-1,v)-K^{ij}_{r}(u+1,v)+K^{ij}_{r}(u,v-1)-K^{ij}_{r}(u,v+1)
plus a term coming from differentiating the scattering operator, and
\partial_{\overline{x}}K^{ij}_{r}(u,v)
equals
-K^{ij}_{r}(u-1,v)-K^{ij}_{r}(u+1,v)+K^{ij}_{r}(u,v-1)+K^{ij}_{r}(u,v+1)
plus a similar term.
Using this we get, making now the scattering terms explicit,

(3.41)

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}K^{ij}_{r}(u,v)

\displaystyle=K^{ij}_{r}(u,v-1)-K^{ij}_{r}(u+1,v)+W^{(\eta)}_{t,x_{i},x_{j}}(u+r_{i}+r,v+r_{j}+r),

\displaystyle\partial_{\zeta}K^{ij}_{r}(u,v)

\displaystyle=K^{ij}_{r}(u,v+1)-K^{ij}_{r}(u-1,v)+W^{(\zeta)}_{t,x_{i},x_{j}}(u+r_{i}+r,v+r_{j}+r)

with

(3.42)

W^{(\zeta)}_{t,x,x^{\prime}}=e^{-2t\nabla-x\Delta}\Big(\partial_{a}{T}^{h_{0}}_{a,b}\big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=x-t\\
b=x^{\prime}+t\end{subarray}}\Big)e^{2t\nabla+x^{\prime}\Delta},\quad W^{(\eta)}_{t,x,x^{\prime}}=e^{-2t\nabla-x\Delta}\Big(\partial_{b}{T}^{h_{0}}_{a,b}\big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=x-t\\
b=x^{\prime}+t\end{subarray}}\Big)e^{2t\nabla+x^{\prime}\Delta}.

Hence (2) and (3) will follow (since
K_{r}(u,v-1)=K_{r-1}(u,v)
,
K_{r}(u-1,v)=K_{r-1}(u,v+1)
) if we show that the
W^{(\eta)}_{t,x_{i},x_{j}}
and
W^{(\zeta)}_{t,x_{i},x_{j}}
terms on the right hand side of (
3.41
) vanish for
u,v>0
or, what is the same (since we are assuming
r_{i}>r_{0}(t,x_{i})
,
r_{j}>r_{0}(t,x_{j})
, and
r\in\{0,1\}
), that

(3.43)

W^{(\eta)}_{t,x_{i},x_{j}}(u,v)=W^{(\zeta)}_{t,x_{i},x_{j}}(u,v)=0\quad\forall u>r_{0}(t,x_{i})+1,\,v>r_{0}(t,x_{j})+1.

We do this in the next subsection.

K_{r}
itself is not trace class in general, but there is a multiplication operator
\vartheta
(independent of
t
, the
x_{i}
’s and the
r_{i}
’s) such that the conjugated kernel
\vartheta K_{r}\vartheta^{-1}
is trace class.
This is proved in Prop.
B.2
, and since the above argument remains valid if we replace
K_{r}
by this conjugation, and since both (
2.18
) and (
2.19
) also do not change under this conjugation, we may perform this replacement to prove Thm.
2.1
.
The necessary differentiability in trace norm of the conjugated kernel appears in Appdx.
B.3
.

It only remains to show that
I-K_{r}
is invertible for
r\in\{-1,0,1\}
.
In fact, this is true for all
r\geq-1
, which is equivalent (by (
3.40
)) to showing that
K^{\text{{ext}}}
is invertible whenever
r_{i}\geq r_{0}(t,x_{i})
for each
i
.
We prove this by appealing to Thm.
2.3
and a simple probabilistic argument.
Consider the event that there are no nucleations inside the region
C_{t,x_{1},x_{m}}=\{(s,y)\!:s\in[0,t],\,x_{1}-s\leq y\leq x_{m}+s\}
, which obviously has positive probability.
By definition of the PNG dynamics we necessarily have in that case that
h(t,x_{i})=r_{0}(t,x_{i})
for each
i
, and then since we are taking
r_{i}\geq r_{0}(t,x_{i})
we have

\mathbb{P}_{h_{0}}(h(t,x_{i})\leq r_{i})\geq\mathbb{P}_{h_{0}}(\text{{no nucl. in }}C_{t,x_{1},x_{m}})>0.

But by (
2.31
) the left hand side equals the Fredholm determinant of the identity minus the (trace class, after conjugation) kernel
K^{\text{{ext}}}
, so
I-K^{\text{{ext}}}
is invertible.

To finish the proof of the theorem we need to prove (
2.19
).
By (
2.28
) we have
F(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},r_{1}+1,\ldots,r_{n}+1)=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K_{r+1})_{\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})}
, where we keep thinking of
r
as a scalar variable.
The kernel
K_{r}
satisfies the hypotheses of Thm.
2.1
, so in particular the formalism employed there also applies here.
In particular, by (
3.3
) and the cyclic property of the Fredholm determinant we have

(3.44)

\displaystyle F(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},r_{1}\hskip-1.00006pt+\hskip-1.00006pt1,\ldots,r_{n}\hskip-1.00006pt+\hskip-1.00006pt1)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-\sigma K_{r}\sigma^{*})_{\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-(I-P)K_{r})_{\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})}

(3.45)

\displaystyle\hskip-43.36243pt=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I+PR_{r}K_{r})_{\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K_{r})_{\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})}

(3.46)

\displaystyle\hskip-43.36243pt=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I+PR_{r}K_{r})_{\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})}F(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}).

Now
P
is a projection onto an
n
-dimensional subspace of
\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
, and the matrix of the linear transformation
P+PR_{r}K_{r}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by
\big(\mathbf{1}_{i=j}+(R_{r}K_{r})_{ij}(1,1)\big)_{i,j=1}^{n}=\big((R_{r})_{ij}(1,1)\big)_{i,j=1}^{n}
(since
R_{r}K_{r}=R_{r}-I
).
Hence

\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I+PR_{r}K_{r})_{\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits((I-P)+(P+PR_{r}K_{r}))_{\oplus_{n}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(R_{r}(1,1)),

the last being a determinant of an
n\times n
matrix.
Using this and the definition
Q_{r}=R_{r}(1,1)
in (
3.45
) yields (
2.19
).

3.3.
The forcing term

Our goal now is to prove (
3.43
).
In fact, we will prove a more general result, which will also play a crucial role when we prove in Sec.
4
that our Fredholm determinant satisfies the Kolmogorov backward equation for PNG.
To state it we introduce the following notational convention: when an object
A_{a}
depends on a parameter
a\in\mathbb{R}
, then the notations
A_{a^{-}}
and
A_{a+}
will always mean

A_{a^{+}}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{lim}}}}\displaylimits_{s\searrow a}A_{s}\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{a^{-}}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{lim}}}}\displaylimits_{s\nearrow a}A_{s},

assuming these limits are well defined.
We also define

4.
PNG as a Markov process

We will need to develop some basic Markov machinery for PNG in order to show that the Fredholm determinant expressions (
2.28
) uniquely solve the backward (Kolmogorov) equations. To our surprise, there does not seem to be any previous reference, except
[
Ben+81
]
where the equation is written down and invariant measures computed from a physics viewpoint.

Let us briefly describe the plan. First of all, PNG is constructed directly as a Markov process, with a generator
\hat{}{L}
coming from general Markov process theory. Continuous time simple random walks are shown to be invariant modulo the height shift and the adjoint
\hat{}{L}^{*}
is computed. It is exactly the generator of the backward dynamics for PNG. Note that one can also think of this as the forward process “upside down”, i.e.
h\mapsto-h
.

The transition probabilities should satisfy the backward equation, but there is a technical issue which permeates the proof:
\mathbf{1}_{h(x_{i})\leq r_{i},i=1,\ldots,n}
is
not
a continuous function on
\UC
(for example, if
h_{\varepsilon}(x)=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{log}}}}\nolimits\mathbf{1}_{x\geq\varepsilon}
then

h_{\varepsilon}\longrightarrow h_{0}
in
\UC
, but
\mathbf{1}_{h_{\varepsilon}(0)\leq-1}=1
for
\varepsilon>0
and
0
for
\varepsilon=0
). But the exact formulas are only for
\mathbb{P}_{h}(h(t,x_{i})\leq r_{i},i=1,\ldots,n)
. In fact, the backward equations only hold in a weak sense and one cannot just appeal to general theory. For the Fredholm determinants the key behind the backward equations is the identity (
4.37
), which shows that the time evolution of the scattering kernels corresponds to the action of the generator.

Once one knows that the determinantal formulas satisfy the backward equation in an appropriate sense, uniqueness is provided by the existence of a large enough class of solutions of the adjoint equation (see Prop.
4.7
). This is now automatic, since the Fredholm determinant formulas can be “flipped on their head” to produce solutions of the adjoint equation, which is just the backward equation for the solution upside down.

Remark 4.1

From this and the above computation we get

\int\!{L}F(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma};u)\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{a,b,m}_{\rho}(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma})=\eqref{eq:Bdet}.

Although we did not introduce all the necessary notation for the periodic case, it should be clear from the computations that there we would have

It only remains to rewrite (

Consider the sum corresponding to the first term in the brackets on the right hand side of (

(with up jumps at rate

.

Proof of Prop.
4.2
.

First of all, because of the explicit construction described at the beginning of this section, the martingale problem for
{L}_{L}
is well-posed (Thms. 4.1 and 2.6 of
[
EK86
]
) and therefore by Echeverria’s theorem (Prop. 9.2 of
[
EK86
]
), a measure is invariant for the process if for all

in a core for
({L}_{L},{D}({L}_{L}))
,

\int{L}_{L}f\mathrm{d}\mu=0
.
This is the second statement of Prop.
4.3
, so we know that each
\mu_{L,\rho}
is invariant for the process on
[-L,L)
with periodic boundary conditions. Now start the full line
(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma})
process with measure
\mu_{\rho}
and let

be any local function and
t>0
. We run the periodic process on

using the same noise as the full line process and using the same initial data, all restricted to
[-L,L)
.
If

is chosen large enough depending on the range of

and

, then the configuration of the two processes at time

coincides within the range of

, because of the finite speed of propagation.
But we have shown that the distribution of the periodic process is

.
Therefore for the full line process
\mathbb{E}[F(\vec{y}(t),\vec{\sigma}(t))]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\rho}}[F]

Since local functions

are enough to determine the measure

, we have proven that

∎

4.4.
Adjoint generator

Our next goal is to compute the adjoint of
{L}
with respect to the
L^{2}(E^{a,b},\mu_{\rho})
inner product (for some fixed
\rho>0
).
Recall that we are interpreting elements of
E^{a,b}
as encoding the jumps of an upper semi-continuous function, and endowing it with the topology that it inherits from
\UC
.
Let us now interpret instead the elements of
E^{a,b}
as the jumps of a lower semi-continuous function
g\in\LC\coloneqq\{f\!:-f\in\UC\}
,
with the topology of local Hausdorff convergence of epigraphs.
A function
G\!:E^{a,b}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is therefore continuous if its restrictions to

satisfy the
dual matching conditions

(4.23)

\mathop{\mathop{\text{{lim}}}}\displaylimits_{y_{i}-y_{i-1}\searrow 0}G_{n}(\ldots,(y_{i-1},+1),(y_{i},-1),\ldots)=G_{n-2}(\ldots,(y_{i-2},\sigma_{i-2}),(y_{i+1},\sigma_{i+1}),\ldots).

Define

(4.24)

{L}^{*}_{\gcr}G_{n}(\vec{x},\vec{\sigma})=2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl(G_{n+2}(C^{*}_{x}(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma}))-G_{n}(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma})\bigr)\mathrm{d}\vec{y},

where the configuration
C^{*}_{z}(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma})
is obtained by inserting
(z,-1)
and
(z,+1)
into
(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma})
.
We will also use
\nu_{\rho}
to denote the product measure of counting measure on the height
h(0)\in\mathbb{Z}
at the origin with the invariant measure
\mu_{\rho}
,
\rho>0
.

Proposition 4.4
.

(Skew time-reversal invariance)

1.

In the above setting, the adjoint
{L}^{*}
of
{L}
on
L^{2}(\mu_{\rho})
is given by

(4.25)

{L}^{*}=-{L}_{\gdet}+{L}^{*}_{\gcr}.

2.

Let
\hat{{L}}^{*}
be the adjoint of
\hat{{L}}
on
L^{2}(\nu_{\rho})
. Then
\hat{{L}}^{*}
is the generator of
-h(t,x)
or, what is the same,
h(-t,x)
, where
h(t,x)
is the PNG process.

Proof.

The second statement is direct, so we focus on the first one.
Assume
F
and
G
have common support
(a,b)
.
Integration by parts shows that
\int({L}_{\gdet}F)G\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\rho}+\int F({L}_{\gdet}G)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\rho}
equals

(4.26)

\textstyle\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{\sigma}\in\{-1,1\}^{k+\ell}}\upsilon_{k,\ell}\left[\sum_{i=2}^{k+\ell}\sigma_{i}\int_{y_{i-1}=y_{i}}F_{n}G_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{k+\ell-1}\sigma_{i}\int_{y_{i}=y_{i+1}}F_{k+\ell}G_{k+\ell}\right]\\
\textstyle=\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{\sigma}\in\{-1,1\}^{k+\ell}}\upsilon_{k,\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{k+\ell-1}(\sigma_{i+1}-\sigma_{i})\int_{y_{i}=y_{i+1}}F_{k+\ell}G_{k+\ell}.

The terms on the right hand side with
\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i+1}
vanish, while those with
\sigma_{i}\neq\sigma_{i+1}
correspond to a
(\sigma_{i},-\sigma_{i})
pair at
y_{i}
, and thus using (
4.10
) and (
4.23
) we get that the above equals

(4.27)

\textstyle\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{\sigma}\in\{-1,1\}^{k+\ell}}\upsilon_{k,\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{k+\ell-1}\!\int_{y_{i}=y_{i+1}}\!\big(F^{\wedge i}_{k+\ell-2}G_{k+\ell}\hskip 0.50003pt\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{i+1}=-\sigma_{i}=1}-F_{k+\ell}G^{\wedge i}_{k+\ell-2}\hskip 0.50003pt\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{i}=-\sigma_{i+1}=1}\big),

where we are using the notation from the proof of Prop.
4.3
.
We have restricted the sum in
k
and
\ell
to start from
1
, because the indicator functions inside the integral imply that there has to be at least one up jump and at least one down jump.
Now we may reinterpret the integral
\sum_{i=1}^{k+\ell-1}\int_{y_{i}=y_{i+1}}F^{\wedge i}_{k+\ell-2}G_{k+\ell}\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{i+1}=-\sigma_{i}=1}
as
\int_{a}^{b}\left(\int F_{k+\ell-2}G_{k+\ell}(C_{z}(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma}))\right)\hskip-1.00006pt\mathrm{d}z
, where the inner integral is over
k+\ell-2
variables.
The analogous thing can be done for the other term in the integral and then, after changing
(k,\ell)\longmapsto(k-1,\ell-1)
and using that the change of measure
r^{2}p(1-p)=1
, we get that the above expression equals

\textstyle 2\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{\sigma}\in\{-1,1\}^{k+\ell}}\upsilon_{k,\ell}\int\left(\int\big(F_{k+\ell}G_{k+\ell+2}(C_{z}(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma}))-F_{k+\ell+2}(C_{z}(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma}))G_{k+\ell})\right)\mathrm{d}z.

From (
4.13
) and (

), the right hand side is exactly

-\int\hskip-1.00006pt\big(({L}_{\gcr}F)G-F({L}_{\gcr}^{*}G)\big)\mathrm{d}\mu_{\rho}
.
∎

4.5.
Backward equation

It would be convenient if we could conclude from (
4.8
) that our transition probabilities
F(t,h)
, defined by

(4.28)

F(t,h;\vec{x},\vec{r})=\mathbb{P}_{h}(h(t,x_{i})\leq r_{i},i=1,\ldots,n)={P}_{t}f(h),\quad f=\mathbf{1}_{h(x_{i})\leq r_{i},i=1,\ldots,n},

satisfy the backward equation

\partial_{t}F=\hat{}{L}F
.
Unfortunately, this function
f
is
not
continuous on
\UC
, and it does not follow. In fact, even for
t>0
,
F(t,h)\not\in{D}(\hat{}{L})
and the backward equation cannot hold in the classical sense.
However, we are forced to deal with them as it is only this particular class of functions for which we have exact formulas.
We now show that the backward equation is satisfied in a weak sense.

It is not too hard to see that if
h
corresponds to
(y_{i},\sigma_{i})
then the transition probabilities (
4.28
), for given
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}
, can have step discontinuities on the
shock set
,

(4.29)

\mathcal{S}(\vec{x})=\{(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma},t)\!:\,y_{i}-\sigma_{i}t=x_{j}\text{{ for some }}i,j\}.

Note that this set depends on
\vec{x}
.
It is essentially just a finite collection of lines of slope
\pm 1
and its complement is open. We will refer to any such object below, for some finite
\vec{x}
, as “a shock set”.
One can also think of it in terms of the height function
h
.
It is the collection of
h
with an up jump at some point
x_{k}+t
or a down jump at some point
x_{k}-t
, for some
k
.
At such points,
\partial_{t}F
and
\partial_{y_{i}}F
are ill-defined for particular values of the
r_{i}
’s and it is impossible for
\partial_{t}F=\hat{{L}}F
to hold in the classical sense.
On the other hand, it is always true at such points that the Dirac delta functions in
(\partial_{t}-\partial_{y_{i}})F
cancel, leaving at most a bounded singularity.
In other words,
F
satisfies
(\partial_{t}-\hat{{L}})F=0
off a co-dimension
1
set
\mathcal{S}(\vec{x})
where it is still well behaved, and this can be used to give meaning to the backward equation for
F
.
For our purposes, the following weak version of the backward equation is the most convenient:

Proposition 4.5
.

F
given by (
4.28
) is a weak solution of the backward equation in the following sense:
for any
\varphi=\varphi(s,h)
satisfying
1.
\varphi
is continuously differentiable off a shock set,
2.
\varphi(s,h)=0
for all

if
\mathop{\mathop{\text{{max}}}}\displaylimits_{i}r_{0}(t,x_{i})\leq-K

,

and

and
h
in the support of
\nu_{\rho}
,
we have

(4.30)

\int_{0}^{t}\int(-\partial_{s}-\hat{{L}}^{*})\varphi F\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{d}\nu_{\rho}\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{d}s=-\int\varphi(t)F(t)\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{d}\nu_{\rho}+\int\varphi(0)F(0)\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{d}\nu_{\rho}.

Proof.

Fix
\vec{x},\vec{r}
and recall
\nu_{\rho}
is the product of counting measure on
h(0)\in\mathbb{Z}
with the invariant measure
\mu_{\rho}
. The function
f_{\varepsilon}(h)=(2\varepsilon)^{-n}\int_{|z_{i}-x_{i}|<\varepsilon,\hskip 0.40974pti=1,\dotsc,n}\mathbf{1}_{h(z_{i})\leq r_{i},\hskip 0.40974pti=1,\ldots,n}\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{d}\vec{z}
is in
{C}_{0}
and so, by the general theory described at the beginning of Sec.
4

{P}_{t}f_{\varepsilon}=\mathbb{E}_{h}[f_{\varepsilon}(h(t))]
does satisfy
(\partial_{t}-\hat{}{L}){P}_{t}f=0
. Integrating by parts, (
4.30
) holds with

replaced by
{P}_{t}f_{\varepsilon}
. Furthermore, from the explicit description of the process at the beginning of Sec.
4
, it is clear that
{P}_{t}f_{\varepsilon}(h)\longrightarrow F(t,h)=F(t,h;\vec{x},\vec{r})
as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
uniformly in

and bounded sets of

.
Therefore, taking
\varepsilon\searrow 0
in the (weak) backward equation (
4.30
) for

, we deduce that the same equation holds for

provided that we can pass the limit through the integration.

In order to justify this, the only real issue is the sum over

implicit in
\nu_{\rho}
.
Let

be the smallest (finite) interval containing the origin, each
x_{i}
,
and every point at distance at most
t+1
from any of these points. Let
\overline{h}
and
\underline{h}
be the supremum and infimum of
h
over this interval.
The event
A_{k}=\{\overline{h}-\underline{h}=k\}
satisfies
\nu_{\rho}(A_{k})\leq\tilde{C}^{k}/k!
with a
\tilde{C}
depending on
\rho
,
t
and the

’s.
On the other hand, on

\varphi(s,h)=0
for all

and

with

, since
\overline{h}\geq\mathop{\mathop{\text{{max}}}}\displaylimits_{i}r_{0}(t,x_{i})
.
Similarly, on
A_{k}
we have

is bounded below by
h(0)-k
for each

and each

.
This last fact is also valid for

Now we consider the left hand side of (
4.30
) with
{P}_{t}f_{\varepsilon}(h)

Splitting the
\nu_{\rho}

\textstyle C\hskip 0.50003ptt\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{h(0)=-K-k}^{\underline{r}+k}\frac{1}{k!}\tilde{C}^{k}<\infty.

The same holds for the right hand side of (

) as needed.
∎

The next theorem says that (a truncated version of) the Fredholm determinant satisfies the backward equation in the same sense.

Theorem 4.6
.

Fix
r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
,

x_{i}
’s) and
q_{1},\dotsc,q_{m}
implies the theorem.
∎

Proof of Thm.
2.3
.

The previous lemma shows that (
2.28
) holds whenever
h
is in the support of
\nu_{\rho}
.
To go to general
h\in\UC
we will approximate by functions in this class.
The probabilities (
4.28
) are not continuous with respect to the
\UC
topology, but
\UC
has a partial order,
h_{1}\prec h_{2}
if
h_{1}(x)\leq h_{2}(x)
, and both sides of (
2.28
) are continuous with respect to local hypograph convergence with decreasing limits (for the Fredholm determinant on the right hand side of (
2.28
) this is proved in Prop.
B.2
).
Since every
\UC
function is the decreasing limit of functions in the support of
\nu_{\rho}
, the result follows.
∎

4.6.
Why hit kernels solve the Kolmogorov equation

The key to the proof of Thm.
4.6
is the following result, which is a consequence of time reversibility and preservation of order together with our invariant measure computations from Sec.
4.3
:

Proposition 4.8
.

Fix
h\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\UC
.
For any
a\leq b
and any
u>h(a)+1
,
v>h(b)+1
,

(4.37)

\hat{{L}}P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,v)=2[P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b},\nabla](u,v),

where the bracket denotes the commutator, i.e.
[A,B]=AB-BA
.

Recall that
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
, defined in (
4.9
), is the restricted state space on which we are working.
The condition
u>h(a)+1
,
v>h(b)+1
in the result is necessary: when there is a jump of
h
at
a
, (
4.37
) fails for
u\leq h(a)+1
, and similarly at the other edge of the interval.
This is already apparent in Lem.
3.4
, and will play a role later in the proof of Thm.
4.6
.

Here is the idea of how the proposition is used. From the structure of
K
(see (
2.11
) and (
2.12
), and also Lem.
3.4
) differentiating in
t
produces a commutator with
2\nabla
, so (
4.37
) shows
(\partial_{t}-\hat{}{L})K=0
. Modulo an unfortunate number of technicalities, we will show
(\partial_{t}-\hat{}{L})\hat{F}=\hat{F}\tr((I-K)^{-1}(\partial_{t}-\hat{}{L})K)
, which therefore vanishes.

Before we start the proof we introduce some notation and make some comments. Let
\Phi(g)
be the indicator of the event that a path
g
ever goes below
0
,

(4.38)

\Phi(g)=1-\mathbf{1}_{g>0}.

Consider a random path
g
in
[a,b]
which has the same law as
\mathsf{N}
conditioned on
\mathsf{N}(a)=u
and
\mathsf{N}(b)=v
.
We choose
g
to be lower semi-continuous, and extend it as
\infty
outside the interval
[a,b]
.
Then

(4.39)

P_{a,b}^{\hit(h)}(u,v)=\mathbf{E}_{a,u;b,v}(\Phi(g-h))e^{(b-a)\Delta}(u,v),

where
\mathbf{E}_{a,u;b,v}
denotes the expectation with respect to the law of the random path
g
.

The key idea of the proof is to attempt to use the backward PNG dynamics to rewrite the left hand side of (
4.37
) as
\mathbf{E}_{a,u;b,v}(\hat{}{L}^{*}\Phi(g-h))e^{(b-a)\Delta}(u,v)
where
\hat{}{L}^{*}
acts on
g
. If the expecatation were over a full invariant measure, this would vanish (
\hat{}{L}^{*}
has the same invariant measures as
\hat{}{L}
.) But since the expectation is over the conditioned measure on a finite box, we pick up boundary terms, given by the right hand side of (
4.37
). That these boundary terms are exactly what one gets from the full kernel (
2.12
) when differentiating in
t
is the mechanism which makes the backward equation work.

There is a technical issue that
\Phi(\cdot-h)
is not in the domain of
\hat{}{L}^{*}
(it is not even continuous.)
The solution is to perform a small average in
h
, to regularize, then remove the regularization.
Such a regularization has to be done carefully, and in fact will not work for all
u,v
.

Proof of Prop.
4.8
.

We divide the proof in several steps.

Step 1.

For
u
and
v
as in the proposition,
P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,v)\in{D}(\hat{{L}})
.
Now define
h_{\alpha}
to be the
\UC
function obtained from
h
by shifting each
y_{i}
of
h
in a slighly larger box, say,
[a-1,b+1]
by
\alpha_{i}
, and let
A_{\varepsilon}
be the set of such
\alpha
where
|\alpha_{i}|\leq\varepsilon
for each
i
.
Then we claim that for all
u>h(a)
and
v>h(b)
,

(4.40)

\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,v)\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,v),~\quad\hat{{L}}\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,v)\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\hat{{L}}P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,v),

where
\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}
denotes average over
\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}
.

Consider the first limit in (
4.40
) and assume first that there is no
y_{i}
at the boundary of
[a,b]
.
Then we may choose
\varepsilon
to be small enough so that this property is conserved as we perform the average, and then
it is not hard to see that we can control

\big|\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,v)-P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,v)\big|
by the probability that
\mathsf{N}
has a jump in
[y_{i}-\varepsilon,y_{i}+\varepsilon]
for some
y_{i}\in(a,b)
(for if the walk stays constant on each
[y_{i}-\varepsilon,y_{i}+\varepsilon]
interval, then it will hit
\hypo(h)
if and only if it hits
\hypo(h_{\alpha})
).
But
h\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}
so there is a fixed, finite number
M
of
y_{i}
’s inside
(a,b)
in the
(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma})
configuration associated to
h
, and thus the probability is of order
\varepsilon
.
If there is a jump at
a
or at
b
then the above argument does not work in general, because a fixed random walk path may or may not hit
\hypo(h_{\alpha})
as a down jump at the left edge or an up jump at the right edge enters or leaves the interval.
But since we are taking
u>h(a)
,
v>h(b)
, it is easy to see that the relevant event also has a probability of order at most
\varepsilon
by upper semi-continuity of
h
.

Now consider the second limit.
We claim first that
\hat{{L}}P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,v)={L}P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,v)
.
The reason is that
P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,v)
only depends on
h
through its value at
a
and the configuration of jumps inside, say,
[a-1,b+1]
.
Therefore, when computing the action of the generator on
P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,v)
, the kernel can be regarded as a function only of the jump configuration, the value of
h
at
a
being fixed, and thus the effect of
\hat{{L}}
and of
{L}
on
\hat{{L}}P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,v)
is the same.
Essentially the same argument shows that
\hat{{L}}\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,v)={L}\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,v)
.
Therefore it is enough to prove the second limit in (
4.40
) with
\hat{{L}}
replaced by
{L}={L}_{\gdet}+{L}_{\gcr}
.

Focus first on the creation part.
It is easy to see that the integral coming from the definition (
4.13
) of
{L}_{\gcr}
can be interchanged with the average, and then essentially the same argument used above proves the convergence.
In that argument we used the assumption
u>h(a)
,
v>h(b)
, which could be destroyed by a creation event at the boundary of
[a,b]
, but creations at a specific, given point can be thrown out from the integral in (
4.13
).
For
{L}_{\gdet}
one can similarly interchange derivatives and the average, and the convergence follows again from Lem.
3.6
, the assumptions on
u
and
v
and the above argument.

Step 2.

Our next goal is to prove that, for
u>h(a)
and
v>h(b)
,

(4.41)

\hat{{L}}\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,v)=2[\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b},\nabla](u,v).

For the proposition we only need to consider
u>h(a)+1
,
v>h(b)+1
, but the extension to
u=h(a)+1
and
v=h(b)+1
will be useful later in this section.
From the Kolmogorov backward equation,

(4.42)

\hat{{L}}\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,v)=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{lim}}}}\displaylimits_{\delta\searrow 0}\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\Big(\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h(\delta,\cdot;h_{\alpha}))}_{a,b}(u,v)-\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,v)\Big),

where
\mathbb{E}
denotes the expectation with respect to the PNG dynamics, and the PNG height function
h(\delta,\cdot;h_{\alpha})
is taken to start at
h(0,\cdot)=h_{\alpha}
.
From (
4.39
),

(4.43)

P_{a,b}^{\hit(h(\delta,\cdot;h_{\alpha}))}(u,v)=\mathbf{E}_{a,u;b,v}(\Phi(g(0,\cdot)-h(\delta,\cdot;h_{\alpha})))e^{(b-a)\Delta}(u,v)

where
\mathbf{E}_{a,u;b,v}
denotes the expectation with respect to the law of the random path
g(0,\cdot)
.

Run the PNG dynamics backwards in time, starting at time
\delta
and down to time
0
, using the same space-time Poisson process used to evolve
h(\delta,\cdot)
from
h(0,\cdot)
, and apply it to each of
h(\delta,\cdot)
and
g(0,\cdot)
separately.
The first of the two functions becomes
h(0,\cdot)=h_{\alpha}
, while the second one yields a new curve, call it
g(-\delta,\cdot)
.
The PNG dynamics preserves order, so almost surely we have
h_{\alpha}(x)\leq g(-\delta,x)
for some
x
if and only if
h(\delta,x)\leq g(0,x)
for some
x
.
Hence

(4.44)

\mathbb{E}\big(\mathbf{E}_{a,u;b,v}(\Phi(g(0,\cdot)-h(\delta,\cdot;h_{\alpha})))\big)=\mathbb{E}\big(\mathbf{E}_{a,u;b,v}(\Phi(g(-\delta,\cdot)-h_{\alpha}))\big).

Thus (
4.42
) can be rewritten as

(4.45)

\mathbf{E}_{a,u;b,v}\hskip-1.00006pt\Big(\mathop{\mathop{\text{{lim}}}}\displaylimits_{\delta\searrow 0}\frac{1}{\delta}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(g(-\delta,\cdot))\big)-\Phi_{\varepsilon}(g(0,\cdot))\Big)\Big)e^{(b-a)\Delta}(u,v),

where
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(g)=1-\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{1}_{g-h_{\alpha}>0}
.
Here we first used Fubini to change the order of expectations, justified by the definite sign of the integrands. Then the
\delta
limit was taken inside the expectation over the path
g
, justified by the average over
A_{\varepsilon}
, which keeps all relevant derivatives of the function
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
bounded by
C\varepsilon^{-1}
, and the dominated convergence theorem.
Inside the
\mathbb{E}
expectation the PNG dynamics is run, backwards, on
g
, and thus from the Kolmogorov backward equation and Prop.
4.4
we get that the above limit equals

(4.46)

\mathbf{E}_{a,u;b,v}\Big(\hat{}{L}^{*}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(g(0,\cdot))\Big)e^{(b-a)\Delta}(u,v),

where
\hat{}{L}^{*}
is applied on
g=g(0)
. This is justified by the fact that
\Phi_{\varepsilon}\in{D}(\hat{}{L}^{*})
.
The law of
\mathsf{N}
is invariant for the backwards PNG dynamics, but in (
4.46
) (in our choice of
g(0,\cdot)
) we are conditioning on the values at the endpoints, so we are in the setting of Prop.
4.3
, albeit with
{L}^{*}
instead of
{L}
.
Slightly abusing notation, and writing
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma};u)
for
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(g(0,\cdot))
, Prop.
4.3
tells us that (
4.46
) is given by

(4.47)

-d_{a,b,v-u-1}\int(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma};u)+\Phi_{\varepsilon}(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma};u+1))\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\rho}^{a,b,v-u-1}\\
\quad+d_{a,b,v-u+1}\int(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma};u)+\Phi_{\varepsilon}(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma};u-1))\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\rho}^{a,b,v-u+1}

(the sign difference coming from the fact that we are dealing with
{L}^{*}=-{L}_{\gdet}+{L}^{*}_{\gcr}
now).
In view of our choice of
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
, and since
\mu_{\rho}^{a,v,v-u}
corresponds to a conditioned measure while
P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,v)
is unconditioned, the above expression equals

\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}\Big[-P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,v-1)-P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u+1,v)+P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,v+1)+P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u-1,v)\Big],

which is exactly the right hand side of (
4.41
).

Step 3.

We finish the proof by taking
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
4.41
).
The left hand side converges to
\hat{{L}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,v)
by (
4.40
).
For the right hand side, the commutator involves evaluating
\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}
at
(u\pm 1,v)
and
(u,v\pm 1)
, and in all cases (
4.40
) ensures the necessary convergence, so the limit of the the right hand side is
2[P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b},\nabla](u,v)
as needed.
∎

Before turning to the proof of the backward equation we need three additional results.
The first one will be useful in handling the creation part of the generator:

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
h\in\UC
,
t\geq 0
, and
x_{1}<\dotsm<x_{n}
, and let
K(h)
denote the PNG kernel (
2.12
) defined using the given
x_{i}
’s,
t
, and
h_{0}=h
.
Then for any fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}
,
K(h+\mathbf{1}_{z})-K(h)
is rank one.

Proof.

In order to compare the two kernels we need to look at the hit probabilities involving
h
and
h+\mathbf{1}_{z}
.
We have

P^{\hit(h+\mathbf{1}_{z})}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}=P^{\hit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}+P^{\nohit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,z}\delta_{h(z)+1}P^{\nohit(h)}_{z,x_{j}+t},

where for convenience we set
P^{\nohit(h)}_{a,b}=0
if
a>b
(so that this covers both the case when
z
is in
[x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t]
and when it is not).
Then
K(h+\mathbf{1}_{z})(x_{i},\cdot;x_{j},\cdot)-K(h)(x_{i},\cdot;x_{j},\cdot)
equals

(4.48)

\displaystyle e^{-2t\nabla-x\Delta}\big({T}^{h+\mathbf{1}_{z}}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}-{T}^{h}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}\big)e^{2t\nabla+x^{\prime}\Delta}=e^{-2t\nabla-t\Delta}\big(P^{\hit(h+\mathbf{1}_{z})}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}-P^{\hit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}\big)e^{2t\nabla-t\Delta}

(4.49)

\displaystyle\hskip 115.63243pt=e^{-2t\nabla-t\Delta}P^{\nohit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,z}\delta_{h(z)+1}P^{\nohit(h)}_{z,x_{j}+t}e^{2t\nabla-t\Delta}=f_{i}\otimes g_{j}

with
f_{i}(u)=e^{-2t\nabla-t\Delta}P^{\nohit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,z}(u,h(z)+1)
and
g_{j}(v)=P^{\nohit(h)}_{z,x_{j}+t}e^{2t\nabla-t\Delta}(h(z)+1,v)
.
∎

The second result we need is an extension of (
4.40
) up to the curve
h
.
We state it only for evaluation at
h
at the right edge of the interval
[a,b]
, but of course an analog result holds at the left edge.

Lemma 4.10
.

For all
a\leq b
,
h\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}
and
u>h(a)+1
we have

(4.50)

\mathop{\mathop{\text{{lim}}}}\displaylimits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,h(b))=P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,h(b))-\tfrac{1}{2}P^{\nohit(h)}_{a,b^{-}}(u,h(b)).

Note that the second term on the right hand side of (
4.50
) vanishes unless
h
has an up jump at
b
.
This extra boundary term will be important in the proof of Thm.
4.6
below, as it will match another boundary term which will arise from (
3.48
).

Proof.

From the argument used to prove (
4.40
) in step 1 of the proof of Prop.
4.8
we see that the limit equals
P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,h(b))
unless
h
has an up jump at
b
.
Now suppose there is such a jump, and take
\varepsilon
to be small enough so that there are no other jumps of
h
in
[b-\varepsilon,b+\varepsilon]
.
The average over shifting the jumps of
h
other than the one at
b
has the same effect as in the proof of Prop.
4.8
.
Now write
P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,h(b))\big|_{b\hskip 0.81949pt\mapsto s}
for value of the kernel obtained when the jump of
h
at
b
is moved to
s
.
We have
P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,h(b))\big|_{b\hskip 0.81949pt\mapsto s}=P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,h(b))+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
for
s\in[b-\varepsilon,b)
(since the difference between the two hit probabilities involves a jump of
\mathsf{N}
inside the interval
[b-\varepsilon,b]
), but

P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,h(b))\big|_{b\hskip 0.81949pt\mapsto s}=P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b^{-}}(u,h(b))

for
s\in(b,b+\varepsilon]
.
Using this it is not hard to see that
\mathop{\mathop{\text{{lim}}}}\displaylimits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(u,h(b))
equals

(4.51)

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,h(b))+\tfrac{1}{2}P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b^{-}}(u,h(b))

\displaystyle=P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,h(b))+\tfrac{1}{2}\big(P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b^{-}}(u,h(b))-P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,h(b))\big)

(4.52)

\displaystyle=P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(u,h(b))-\tfrac{1}{2}P^{\nohit(h)}_{a,b^{-}}(u,h(b)).\qed

The last thing we need before proving Thm.
4.6
is an abstract result about Fredholm determinants.
To motivate it, consider the case
r_{i}>r_{0}(t,x)
for each
i
and suppose for a moment that we knew that the kernel
K=\chi_{r}K^{{\text{{ext}}}}\chi_{r}
is such that
I-K
is invertible; this fact will actually follow from Thm.
2.3
, because its Fredholm determinant equals
F(t,x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n},r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})
, which is positive under our assumption on the
r_{i}
’s (see the argument in Sec.
3.2
), but of course our proof of Thm.
2.3
depends on Thm.
4.6
.
Assuming that
I-K
is invertible we have the standard identity

\partial_{t}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K)=-\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K)\tr((I-K)^{-1}\partial_{t}K),

provided
\partial_{t}K
exists in trace class (in our setting this fails at the shock set, but we ignore this issue in this explanation).
Similarly
{L}_{\gdet}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K)=-\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K)\tr((I-K)^{-1}{L}_{\gdet}K)
, because
{L}_{\gdet}
is a first order differential operator.
The key is that the same holds for the creation part thanks to the last lemma and another standard identity: if
A
is rank one and
I-K
is invertible, then

(4.53)

\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K+A)-\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K)=-\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K)\tr((I-K)^{-1}A).

What is crucial here is that
\partial_{t}
,
{L}_{\gdet}
and
{L}_{\gcr}
all give rise to expressions of the same form, leading to

(\partial_{t}-{L})\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K)=-\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K)\tr((I-K)^{-1}(\partial_{t}-{L})K),

which has the advantage of reducing the proof of
(\partial_{t}-{L})F
to the kernel identity
(\partial_{t}-{L})K=0
.

In order to achieve something similar in our situation, where we do not known a priori that
I-K
is invertible, we proceed as follows.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space and let
\mathcal{B}_{1}
be the space of trace class operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
Define
\mathsf{D}\!:\mathcal{B}_{1}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
through

(4.54)

\mathsf{D}(K)=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K),

where the Fredholm determinant is computed on
\mathcal{H}
.

Lemma 4.11
.

\mathsf{D}
is Fréchet differentiable with Fréchet derivative
\mathsf{D}^{\prime}(K)
.
In particular, if
K_{a}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
depends on a parameter
a\in\mathbb{R}
and the derivative
\partial_{a}K_{a}
exists in trace norm, then

(4.55)

\partial_{a}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(K_{a})=\mathsf{D}^{\prime}(K_{a})(\partial_{a}K_{a}).

On the other hand, if
K\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
A
is a rank one operator acting on
\mathcal{H}
, then

(4.56)

\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K-A)-\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K)=\mathsf{D}^{\prime}(K)(A).

The main point of the result (which is simple, but which we did not find stated in the literature) is that (
4.56
) holds without any assumption on
K
.

Proof.

That
\mathsf{D}
is Frèchet differentiable is standard, see e.g.
[
Sim05
, Cor. 5.2]
, while (
4.55
) follows from that and the chain rule.
The same result in
[
Sim05
]
shows that, in the special case when
I-K
is invertible, the Fréchet derivative of
\mathsf{D}
has the following form: for each
L\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
,

(4.57)

\mathsf{D}^{\prime}(K)(L)=-\mathsf{D}(K)\tr((I-K)^{-1}L).

Now fix
K,A\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
with
A
rank one and, for
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
, define

\mathsf{R}(\mu)=\mathsf{D}(\mu K+A)-\mathsf{D}(\mu K)-\mathsf{D}^{\prime}(\mu K)(A).

The three objects on the right hand side are analytic in
\mu
(for
\mathsf{D}(\mu K)
this is standard, see e.g.
[
Sim05
, Lem. 3.3]
, while for the other two it is implicit in Cor. 5.2 of the same book), so
\mu\longmapsto\mathsf{R}(\mu)
is an analytic function.
On the other hand, if
\mu^{-1}
is not in the spectrum of
K
then, by (
4.53
) and (
4.57
),

\mathsf{D}(\mu K+A)-\mathsf{D}(\mu K)=-\mathsf{D}(\mu K)\tr((I-\mu K)^{-1}A)=\mathsf{D^{\prime}}(\mu K)(A).

Hence
\mathsf{R}(\mu)=0
whenever
\mu^{-1}
is not in the spectrum of
K
.

K
is compact, so its spectrum is discrete.
Hence we deduce that
\mathsf{R}(\mu)=0
for all
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and thus, in particular, for
\mu=1
.
∎

Proof of Thm.
4.6
.

The initial condition (i) was already verified.
The second point of (ii) is straightforward from the definition (
4.31
) of
\hat{F}
, while (iii) follows directly from 1 in (ii) and integration by parts.
To get the estimate on
|\hat{F}(s,h)|
in the third point of (ii) we use Prop.
B.2
together with the bound
|\hskip-1.00006pt\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-A)|\leq e^{\|A\|_{1}}
for any trace class operator
A
on given (separable) Hilbert space, with
\|A\|_{1}
its trace norm
[
Sim05
, Lem. 3.3]
.
They give
|\hat{F}(s,h)|\leq C(1+e^{c\hskip 0.40974pt\mathop{\mathop{\text{{max}}}}\displaylimits_{i}(r_{0}(s,x_{i})-r_{i})})\mathbf{1}_{r_{i}\geq r_{0}(s,x_{i}),\hskip 0.81949pti=1,\dotsc,n}
with constants
c,C>0
valid for all
s\in[0,t]
, which is clearly bounded uniformly as needed (note the key role played here by the truncation).
The estimate on
|(\partial_{s}-\hat{}{L})\hat{F}|(s,h)
follows from 1 of (ii) off the shock set, and will be proved in Case 3b of this proof on
\mathcal{S}(\vec{x})
.

The rest of the proof will be devoted to showing the two remaining conditions: that
(\partial_{t}-\hat{}{L})\hat{F}=0
off
\mathcal{S}(\vec{x})
and that
|(\partial_{t}-\hat{}{L})\hat{F}(t,h)|
is bounded uniformly on
\mathcal{S}(\vec{x})
for
t
in a compact interval and
h\in\UC
.
We divide the proof in several cases, according to the position of the parameters
r_{i}
and whether we are not at a shock.
Throughout the proof all determinants and traces are computed on
\ell^{2}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\times\mathbb{Z})
.

Case 1
: Suppose
r_{k}>r_{0}(t,x_{k})
for each
k
and write

K=\chi_{r}K^{\text{{ext}}}\chi_{r},\qquad\text{{so that}}\quad\hat{F}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K).

K
only depends on the jumps of
h
inside
[\mathop{\mathop{\text{{min}}}}\displaylimits\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}\}-t,\mathop{\mathop{\text{{max}}}}\displaylimits\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}\}+t]

{L}_{\gdet}
acts as a sum of finitely many derivatives.

\partial_{t}-{L}_{\gdet}
thus acts as a first order differential operator, and from the arguments in Appdx.
B.3
it follows that (the limit which defines)
(\partial_{t}-{L}_{\gdet})K

Then by (
4.55
) we have

(4.58)

(\partial_{t}-{L}_{\gdet})\hat{F}=\mathsf{D}^{\prime}(K)((\partial_{t}-{L}_{\gdet})K).

Now we consider
{{L}}_{\gcr}
.
Using (
4.13
) and the notation from Lem.
4.9

\textstyle{{L}}_{\gcr}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K(h))=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-1.00006pt\big(\hskip-1.00006pt\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K(h+\mathbf{1}_{z}))-\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K(h))\big)\hskip 0.50003pt\mathrm{d}z,

and since

4.11

This gives

\textstyle{{L}}_{\gcr}\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K)=\mathsf{D}^{\prime}(K)\!\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(K(h+\mathbf{1}_{z})-K(h))\,\mathrm{d}z\right)=\mathsf{D}^{\prime}(K)({L}_{\gcr}K).

From this and (

Therefore (recalling

(\partial_{t}-\hat{{L}})K_{ij}(u,v)=0\qquad\forall\,u>h(x_{i}-t)+1,~v>h(x_{j}+t)+1.

\partial_{t}K_{ij}(u,v)=e^{-2t\nabla-x_{i}\Delta}\Bigl(2[{T}^{h}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t},\nabla]+\partial_{t}{T}^{h}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}\Bigr)e^{2t\nabla+x_{j}\Delta}(u,v)

Lem.

,

Since, by Lem.
3.7

Case 2
: Suppose now that
r_{k}<r_{0}(t,x_{k})
for some
k
and that we are not at a shock in the

variable (i.e., that
h
does not have a jump at
x_{k}+t

Then
r_{k}<r_{0}(s,x_{k})
for all

in a neighborhood of

, and for all such

we then have
\hat{F}(s,h)=0
, so
\partial_{t}\hat{F}(t,h)=0
.
Now consider
\hat{{L}}\hat{F}
.
The deterministic part of the generator only moves the positions of the jumps of
h
, which lie away from
x_{k}\pm t
, while the creation part only moves
h
up, which means that the inequality
r_{k}<r_{0}(t,x_{k})
(and hence the fact that
\hat{F}=0
) is preserved through the action of the generator.
Hence
\hat{{L}}\hat{F}=0
, which gives us what we want.

Case 3
: Next suppose that
r_{k}<r_{0}(t,x_{k})
for some
k
and we are at a shock in the
x_{k}
variable.
We split in three subcases.

Case 3a
: Suppose
r_{j}<r_{0}(t,x_{j})
for some
j\neq k
for which we are not at a shock in the
x_{j}
variable.
Then the argument of case 2 applies, and we get again
(\partial_{t}-\hat{{L}})\hat{F}=0
.

Case 3b
: Suppose now that
r_{j}\geq r_{0}(t,x_{j})
for all
j\neq k
.
We will only consider the case when the
x_{k}
shock comes from a jump of
h
at
x_{k}+t
; a jump at the other edge, or at both edges, are handled similarly.
Additionally, we assume first for simplicity that there is only one jump at
x_{k}+t
, and explain at the end the extension to the case when there is a
(+1,-1)
pair at that location.
Let
(\vec{y},\vec{\sigma})
be the configuration of jump locations and signs associated to
h
.
By relabeling, we may assume that the (unique) jump at
x_{k}+t
has label
0
, i.e.
y_{0}=x_{k}+t
.
If
\sigma_{0}=-1
, i.e. there is a down jump at
x_{k}+t
, then by upper semi-continuity of
h
we have that
r_{0}(s,z)
is constant for
s\in[t-\varepsilon,t+\varepsilon]
,

and small

, so
\partial_{t}F=0
as above, and for the same reason
\hat{{L}}\hat{F}=0
(note here we need to consider the movement of the
y_{0}
jump, but it also does not affect
r_{0}(t,x_{k})
).

We turn now to the case
\sigma_{0}=1
.
In particular, in this case we have
r_{k}<r_{0}(t+\varepsilon,x_{k})
, and hence
\hat{F}(t+\varepsilon,h)=0
, for small

.
Note that, since we are assuming that the jump of
h
at
x_{k}+t
is up by one,
\mathop{\mathop{\text{{lim}}}}\displaylimits_{s\nearrow t}r_{0}(s,x_{k})=r_{k}(t,x_{k})-1
.
If
r_{k}<r_{0}(t,x_{k})-1
then we also have
r_{k}<r_{0}(t-\varepsilon,x_{k})
for small
\varepsilon>0
, and as in the previous cases we have
\partial_{t}\hat{F}=\hat{{L}}\hat{F}=0
.

The interesting situation is thus when
\sigma_{0}=1
and
r_{k}=r_{0}(t,x_{k})-1
.
This is the case for which
(\partial_{t}-\hat{{L}})\hat{F}
is not

; we will show that is is bounded as needed.
In this case we have as above
\hat{F}(t+\varepsilon,h)=0
, so

\hat{F}(t+\varepsilon,h)-\hat{F}(t-\varepsilon,h)=-\hat{F}(t-\varepsilon,h).

Now we study
\hat{{L}}\hat{F}
.
Once again, since the creation part of the generator only moves
h
up (and we are assuming
r_{k}<r_{0}(t,x_{k})
), we have
{L}_{\gcr}\hat{F}=0
.
On the other hand, it is clear that the deterministic part of the generator restricted to the jumps of
h
other than the one at
y_{0}=x_{k}+t
cannot change the inequality
r_{k}<r_{0}(t,x)
, so that part of the action of the generator on
F
vanishes.
This tells us that the only non-zero contribution to
\hat{{L}}F
comes from the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
of
(2\varepsilon)^{-1}
times

\hat{F}(t,h)|_{y_{0}=x_{k}+t-\varepsilon}-\hat{F}(t,h)|_{y_{0}=x_{k}+t+\varepsilon},

i.e. the difference between computing
\hat{F}
with the
y_{0}
jump moved to
x_{k}+t-\varepsilon
and to
x_{k}+t+\varepsilon
(recall that up steps move to the left).
We may assume that
\varepsilon
is small enough so that the only jump of
h
in
[x_{k}+t-\varepsilon,x_{k}+t+\varepsilon]

x_{k}+t
.
When the
y_{0}
jump is moved to
x_{k}+t-\varepsilon

remains (since

does not change) but, under our assumptions, when it is moved to
x_{k}+t+\varepsilon
,
r_{0}(t,x_{k})
changes to
r_{0}(t,x_{k})-1=r_{k}
, which means that the above difference equals
-\hat{F}(t,h)|_{y_{0}=x_{k}+t+\varepsilon}
.
The conclusion from all this is that, in the current situation,

(\partial_{t}-\hat{{L}})\hat{F}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{lim}}}}\displaylimits_{\varepsilon\to 0}(2\varepsilon)^{-1}\big(-\hat{F}(t-\varepsilon,h)+\hat{F}(t,h)|_{y_{0}=x_{k}+t+\varepsilon}\big).

Under our assumptions, when evaluating
\hat{F}
at
t-\varepsilon
with
y_{0}=x_{k}+t
or at
t
with
y_{0}=x_{k}+t+\varepsilon
, we are in the regime where
r_{i}\geq r_{0}(t,x_{i})
for each
i
, so that the
\hat{F}
’s on the right hand side are given by the corresponding Fredholm determinants, i.e.

(4.64)

(\partial_{t}-\hat{{L}})\hat{F}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{lim}}}}\displaylimits_{\varepsilon\to 0}(2\varepsilon)^{-1}\big(\hskip-1.00006pt\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K(t,h)|_{y_{0}=x_{k}+t+\varepsilon})-\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K(t-\varepsilon,h))\big),

where we are including the dependence of
K
on
t
,
h
and
y_{0}
in the same way as for
F
.
Now we claim that there is a constant
C<\infty
which is independent of
\varepsilon
and
h
in the support of
\nu_{\rho}
and bounded uniformly for

in a compact interval such that

(4.65)

\big|\hskip-1.00006pt\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K(t,h)|_{y_{0}=x_{k}+t+\varepsilon})-\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K(t-\varepsilon,h))\big|\leq C\hskip 0.50003pt\varepsilon.

We prove this in Appdx.
B.4
.
It shows that

|(\partial_{t}-\hat{{L}})\hat{F}|\leq C

uniformly in
t
in a finite set and

, which is what we wanted.

The only situation left to consider in this case is when there is a
(+1,-1)
pair at
x_{k}+t
, but it is easy to see that the above argument still works in this case: when the generator is applied to
\hat{F}
, the up jump moves left while the down jump moves right, but all the necessary inequalities as well as the estimates in Appdx.
B.4
remain valid.

Case 3c
: The last possibility is that there are several indices
j_{1},\dotsc,j_{\ell}
(including
k
) for which
r_{j_{i}}<r_{0}(t,x_{j_{i}})
and we are at a shock in each of those variables.
The proof in this case is basically the same.
When computing the limit defining
(\partial_{t}-\hat{{L}})\hat{F}
there will be terms as above coming from the
t
derivative for each of the
j_{i}
’s, and matching terms coming from
\hat{{L}}\hat{F}
coming from the deterministic movement of the jumps of
h
at the corresponding points.
Most of these terms will vanish as above, and those which do not will lead a sum of at most
\ell
differences as in (
4.64
), which can be estimated separately.

Case 4
: All that is left is to consider the case when
r_{k}=r_{0}(t,x_{k})
for some
k
.
As in case 3, we will focus on the case where this happens for a single index
k

Also, if
r_{j}<r_{0}(t,x_{j})
for some
j\neq k
then we fall back to the situations covered in cases 2 and 3, so we assume
r_{j}>r_{0}(t,x_{j})
for all
j\neq k
.
In this case we will show that
(\partial_{t}-\hat{{L}})\hat{F}=0

We are again in the situation of case 1: with the same notation as in that case,
\hat{F}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{det}}}}\nolimits(I-K)
and the argument leading to (
4.60
) applies in the same way, so that what we need is to show that

(4.66)

(\partial_{t}-\hat{{L}})K_{ij}(u,v)=0\qquad\forall\,u>r_{i},~v>r_{j}

for each
i,j
.
We begin by noting that, due to our assumption on the
r_{i}
’s, (
4.66
) is covered by (
4.61
) in all cases except when
i
or
j
equal
k
, and we evaluate at

.
So all we need to show is that

(4.67)

(\partial_{t}-\hat{{L}})K_{ik}(u,r_{k}+1)=0\quad\forall\,u>r_{i},\qquad(\partial_{t}-\hat{{L}})K_{kj}(r_{k}+1,v)=0\quad\forall\,v>r_{j}.

We will only prove the first identity; the second one can be proved in the same way (and, in fact, it follows from the first one by symmetry).
Note that the identity is also covered by (
4.61
) if
r_{0}(t,x_{k})>h(x_{k}+t)
, so we may assume
r_{k}=r_{0}(t,x_{k})=h(x_{k}+t)

Consider the case
i\neq k
; the case

is similar, and we comment on it at the end.
In (
4.67

u>r_{i}>r_{0}(t,x_{i})
, so we may assume

In view of (

\partial_{t}K_{ik}(u,r_{k}+1)=2[K_{ik},\nabla](u,r_{k}+1)-\tfrac{1}{2}e^{2t-2t\nabla-t\Delta}P^{\nohit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,(x_{k}+t)^{-}}(u,h(x_{k}+t)).

3.4

We have (as in (

\hat{{L}}K_{ik}(u,r_{k}+1)=e^{-2t\nabla-t\Delta}\big(\hat{{L}}P^{\hit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,x_{k}+t}\big)e^{2t\nabla-t\Delta}(u,r_{k}+1).

\hat{{L}}P^{\hit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,x_{k}+t}(\eta,\xi)

4.8
shows that this kernel equals

, so all that it left to compute is

4.10

\hat{{L}}\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b}(\eta,h(b)+1)=2[\text{{Av}}_{\alpha\in A_{\varepsilon}}P^{\hit(h_{\alpha})}_{a,b},\nabla](\eta,h(b)+1).

We have shown that

(4.71)

\hat{{L}}P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b}(\eta,\xi)=2[P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b},\nabla](\eta,\xi)-\tfrac{1}{2}P^{\nohit(h)}_{a,b^{-}}(\eta,h(b))\mathbf{1}_{\xi=h(b)+1}

,
\xi>h(b)
.
Using this in (
4.69

(4.72)

\displaystyle\hat{{L}}K_{ik}(u,r_{k}+1)

\displaystyle=2e^{-2t\nabla-t\Delta}[P^{\hit(h)}_{a,b},\nabla]e^{2t\nabla-t\Delta}(u,r_{k}+1)

(4.73)

\displaystyle\hskip 32.52127pt-\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-2t\nabla-t\Delta}P^{\nohit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,(x_{k}+t)^{-}}(u,h(x_{k}+t))e^{-2t\nabla-t\Delta}(h(x_{k}+t)+1,r_{k}+1)

(4.74)

\displaystyle=2[K_{ik},\nabla](u,r_{k}+1)-\tfrac{1}{2}e^{2t-2t\nabla-t\Delta}P^{\nohit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,(x_{k}+t)^{-}}(u,h(x_{k}+t)),

where the last equality follows as in previous computations.
Comparing with (

4.67

Finally consider the case
i=k
.
The only new kernel entry which we need to consider is

We can compute the

\partial_{t}K_{kk}(r_{k}+1,r_{k}+1)=2[K_{kk},\nabla](r_{k}+1,r_{k}+1)-\tfrac{1}{2}P^{\nohit(h)}_{(x_{k}-t)^{+},x_{k}+t}e^{2t-2t\nabla+t\Delta}(r_{k},r_{k}+1)\\
-\tfrac{1}{2}e^{2t-2t\nabla-t\Delta}P^{\nohit(h)}_{x_{k}-t,(x_{k}+t)^{-}}(r_{k}+1,r_{k}).

On the other hand, repeating the above argument but now using both Lem.

[a,b]
, we see that in order to compute

This finishes case 4, and the proof.

Appendix A
Initial data for non-Abelian Toda

In this appendix we compute the initial condition for the non-Abelian 2D Toda equation for PNG.
We focus on the equation (
2.22
) in terms of
U_{r}
and
V_{r}
, and give expressions for these two matrices at
t=0
.
We stress that this is far from providing the whole boundary data for the equation, which we do not attempt here.
In everything that follows we work under the assumption
r_{i}\geq r_{0}(t,x_{i})
, which for
t=0
means we take, for each
i
,

(A.1)

r_{i}\geq h_{0}(x_{i}).

The formula (
4.33
) for
K^{\text{{ext}}}\big|_{t=0}
has the same structure as the (slightly formal, in that case) formula derived in
[
QR22
]
, in the setting of the KPZ fixed point, for the Brownian scattering operator at
t=0
.
Thus the same basic argument applies in our case, and it will lead to a formula for
Q_{r}\big|_{t=0}
in (
A.8
) below, providing a discrete analog of the formula derived in
[
QR22
]
.
However, in our setting we need to calculate
Q^{-1}_{r}\big|_{t=0}
and
\partial_{\eta}Q_{r}\big|_{t=0}
as well.
This will require an additional computation, leading to more complicated formulas.
To express them we will need some:

Notation
.

For
i<j
and
i\leq k\leq m\leq j
, let

(A.2)

P_{i,j}^{\geq h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k,m}}(u,v)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{N}(x_{i})=u}\big(\mathsf{N}(y)\geq h_{0}(y)~\forall\,y\in[x_{i},x_{j}],\\
\mathsf{N}(x_{\ell})\leq r_{\ell}\text{{ for each }}x_{\ell}\in[x_{k},x_{m}]\cap(x_{i},x_{j}),\,\mathsf{N}(x_{j})=v\big).

Note that in the probability we ask
\mathsf{N}
to stay above
h_{0}
inside the whole interval
[x_{i},x_{j}]
, and to stay below the heights
r_{\ell}
only at those points
x_{\ell}
among the
x_{1}<\dotsm<x_{n}
with indices between
k
and
m
but excluding, if necessary, the two endpoints
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
.
The notation
\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k,m}
represents an upside-down, multiple narrow wedge, defined as
\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k,m}(x)=r_{\ell}
if
x=x_{\ell}
for some
x_{\ell}\in\{x_{k},\dotsc,x_{m}\}
and
\infty
otherwise (so the event with upper bound inside the probability is the same as asking for
\mathsf{N}(y)\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k,m}(y)
for
y\in[x_{i},x_{j}]
).
We extend this notation in several ways:

(i) If
k=m
then we write
\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k}
instead of
\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k,k}
.
(ii) If we change, remove or add inequalities in the superscript on the left hand side, then all we do is change, remove or add the corresponding inequalities in the probability on the right hand side.
(iii) When
u=r_{i}
and
v=r_{j}
, we simply write

(A.3)

\bar{P}_{i,j}^{\geq h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i,j}}=P_{i,j}^{\geq h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i,j}}(r_{i},r_{j}),

and similarly if any of the inequalities are modified.
(iv) We turn the last definition into an
n\times n
matrix
\bar{P}^{\geq h_{0},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}}_{\vec{r}}
defined through (here for convenience we make the dependence on
\vec{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
explicit)

(A.4)

(\bar{P}_{\vec{r}}^{\geq h_{0},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}})_{ij}=\mathbf{1}_{i<j}\bar{P}^{\geq h_{0},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i,j}}_{i,j}

for each
i,j
(note that this is using implicitly the convention that each
\bar{P}_{ij}=0
for
i\geq j
).
(v) We let
\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 1.00006pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}
denote the event that
\mathsf{N}
stays
\geq h_{0}
but hits exactly
h_{0}
along the way, i.e.

(A.5)

P^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}}_{i,j}=P^{\geq h_{0}}_{i,j}-P^{>h_{0}}_{i,j}.

We extend this notation as before if we need to add further restrictions in the paths of
\mathsf{N}
, and also to the case
\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\,\,\hskip 1.00006pt\cdot$}\hss}$\leq$}\check{\mathfrak{d}}
.
(vi) Finally, we use the notation
\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 1.00006pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}\hskip 0.50003pt\scalebox{0.65}{$\curvearrowright$}\hskip 0.50003pt\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\,\,\hskip 1.00006pt\cdot$}\hss}$\leq$}\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i,j}
if we require the
\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i,j}
touching to occur after the first
h_{0}
touching, and we also write

(A.6)

\big(\bar{P}^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}}_{+,\vec{r}}\big)_{ij}=P^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i,j}}_{i,j}(r_{i}+1,r_{j})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\big(\bar{P}^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}\hskip 0.40974pt\scalebox{0.65}{$\curvearrowright$}\hskip 0.40974pt\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\,\,\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\leq$}\check{\mathfrak{d}}}_{+,\vec{r}}\big)_{ij}=P^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}\hskip 0.40974pt\scalebox{0.65}{$\curvearrowright$}\hskip 0.40974pt\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\,\,\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\leq$}\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i,j}}_{i,j}(r_{i}+1,r_{j}).

Additionally, we introduce the shift operators

\theta_{r}f(u)=f(u+r),\qquad\theta^{*}_{r}f(u)=f(u-r).

The action of
\theta_{r}
is meant as taking place in all of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
(which is why we are distinguishing
\theta_{1}
and
\sigma
).
In terms of our formulas, where all (scalar) kernel products take place in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
, it is better to think of
\theta_{r}
/
\theta^{*}_{r}
simply as a convenient notation, which modifies the kernel to its right/left, rather than as the action of an operator.
The point is that for a given kernel
A
we have
\theta_{r}A\theta^{*}_{r}(u,v)=A(u+r,v+r)
so we can, for instance, rewrite (
4.33
) (using also (
A.1
)) as

(A.7)

(K_{r})_{ij}=-\mathbf{1}_{i<j}\theta_{r_{i}}\chi_{r_{i}}P^{>h_{0}}_{i,j}\chi_{r_{j}}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}}=-\mathbf{1}_{i<j}\theta_{r_{i}}P^{>h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}},

since
\theta_{r}\chi_{r}=\chi_{0}\theta_{r}
,
\chi_{r}\theta_{r}^{*}=\theta_{r}^{*}\chi_{0}
and our kernels act on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
so the
\chi_{0}
factors may be omitted.

We turn now to the computation.
We begin with a formula for
Q_{r}\big|_{t=0}
:

Proposition A.1
.

Under the condition (
A.1
) we have

(A.8)

Q_{r}\big|_{t=0}=I-\bar{P}_{\vec{r}}^{\geq h_{0},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Q^{-1}_{r}\big|_{t=0}=I+\bar{P}_{\vec{r}}^{\geq h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}}.

Proof.

In view of (
A.7
),
K_{r}
is strictly upper triangular, so
(R_{r})_{ij}=\sum_{m=0}^{j-i}(K^{m})_{ij}
and we have
(R_{r})_{ii}=I
and
(R_{r})_{ij}=0
for
i>j
.
For
i<j
, on the other hand, we decompose
(K^{m}_{r})_{ij}
as
\sum_{\pi}\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}(K_{r})_{\pi(\ell)\pi(\ell+1)}
, where the sum is over strictly increasing paths
\pi=(\pi(1),\dotsc,\pi(m+1))
going from
i
to
j
along indices
\{1,\dotsc,n\}
.
Applying the inclusion-exclusion principle and using the fact that we are acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
and
\theta^{*}_{r}\chi_{0}\theta_{r}=\chi_{r}
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, we deduce that

(A.9)

(R_{r})_{ij}=\mathbf{1}_{i=j}-\mathbf{1}_{i<j}\theta_{r_{i}}P^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i,j}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}}

(see
[
QR22
, Eqn. (A.6)]
for a similar argument), and then evaluating at
(1,1)
gives

Q_{ij}=\mathbf{1}_{i=j}I-\mathbf{1}_{i<j}P^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i,j}}(r_{i}+1,r_{j}+1)=\mathbf{1}_{i=j}I-\mathbf{1}_{i<j}P^{\geq h_{0},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i,j}}(r_{i},r_{j}),

where in the second equality we used the translation invariance of
\mathsf{N}
.
This is the first identity in (
A.8
).

For
Q^{-1}_{r}\big|_{t=0}
it is now enough to check that
(I-\bar{P}_{\vec{r}}^{\geq h_{0},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}})(I+\bar{P}_{\vec{r}}^{\geq h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}})=I
, which is equivalent to
\bar{P}_{\vec{r}}^{\geq h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}}-\bar{P}_{\vec{r}}^{\geq h_{0},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}}=\bar{P}_{\vec{r}}^{\geq h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}}\bar{P}_{\vec{r}}^{\geq h_{0},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}}
.
The left hand side equals
\bar{P}_{\vec{r}}^{\geq h_{0},\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\,\,\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\leq$}\check{\mathfrak{d}}}
, using the notation (v) introduced above.
The right hand side is trivially equal to this at or below the diagonal, while a simple computation using the upper triangularity of each factor shows that, above the diagonal, the product provides a decomposition of
\bar{P}_{\vec{r}}^{\geq h_{0},\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\,\,\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\leq$}\check{\mathfrak{d}}}
according to the last time the walk hits
\check{\mathfrak{d}}
.
∎

Next we need to compute
\partial_{\eta}Q_{r}|_{t=0}
.

Proposition A.2
.

With the notation introduced above, and under condition (
A.1
), we have

(A.10)

\partial_{\eta}Q_{r}\big|_{t=0}=\bar{P}^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}}_{+,\vec{r}}-\bar{P}^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}\hskip 0.40974pt\scalebox{0.65}{$\curvearrowright$}\hskip 0.40974pt\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\,\,\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\leq$}\check{\mathfrak{d}}}_{+,\vec{r}}.

Proof.

In all that follows we set
t=0
.
We have

(A.11)

\partial_{\eta}Q_{r}=\partial_{\eta}R_{r}(1,1)=R_{r}\partial_{\eta}K_{r}R_{r}(1,1).

R_{r}
is upper triangular while
\partial_{\eta}K_{r}
is strictly upper triangular, so the same holds for
\partial_{\eta}K_{r}R_{r}
and
R_{r}\partial_{\eta}K_{r}R_{r}
.
In view of this, in the computation that follows it is enough to consider
i<j
.

Recall
\partial_{\eta}K_{r}(u,v)=-K_{r}(u+1,v)+K_{r}(u,v-1)
, so using (
A.7
) we have

(\partial_{\eta}K_{r})_{ij}=\theta_{r_{i}+1}P^{>h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}}-\theta_{r_{i}}P^{>h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1}=\theta_{r_{i}}P^{\geq h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1}-\theta_{r_{i}}P^{>h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1}=\theta_{r_{i}}P^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1},

where we used translation invariance and the notation introduced in (
A.5
).
Using now (
A.9
) we get that

(A.12)

\textstyle(R_{r}\partial_{\eta}K_{r})_{ij}=\theta_{r_{i}}P^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1}-\sum_{i<k<j}\theta_{r_{i}}P^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i+1,k-1},>\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k},\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1},

where in the second
P_{i,j}
operator the sequence of inequalities means in particular that
\mathsf{N}>h_{0}
in
[x_{i},x_{k}]
and
\mathsf{N}\;\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 1.00006pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}\;h_{0}
in
[x_{k}+1,x_{j}]
(there is no ambiguity at
x_{k}
since, under (
A.1
), the condition

implies
\mathsf{N}(x_{k})>h_{0}(x_{k})
anyway), and we used again that
\theta^{*}_{r}\chi_{0}\theta_{r}=\chi_{r}
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Next we compute
(R_{r}\partial_{\eta}K_{r}R_{r})_{ij}
.
Using the last formula and (
A.9
), it is given by

(A.13)

\textstyle\theta_{r_{i}}P^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1}-\sum_{i<k<j}\theta_{r_{i}}P^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i+1,k-1},>\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k},\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1}-\sum_{i<k<j}\theta_{r_{i}}P^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}}_{i,k}\theta^{*}_{r_{k}-1}\theta_{r_{k}}P^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k,j}}_{k,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}}\\
\textstyle+\sum_{i<k<\ell<j}\theta_{r_{i}}P^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i+1,k-1},>\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k},\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}}_{i,\ell}\theta^{*}_{r_{\ell}-1}\theta_{r_{\ell}}P^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{\ell,j}}_{\ell,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}}.

Consider the factor
\theta^{*}_{r_{k}-1}\theta_{r_{k}}P^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k,j}}_{k,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}}
.
If we make explicit the fact that everything acts on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
except for the
\theta_{r}
’s to their right and the
\theta^{*}_{r}
’s to their left, we can write this as

(A.14)

\theta^{*}_{r_{k}-1}\chi_{0}\theta_{r_{k}}P^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k,j}}_{k,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}}=\chi_{r_{k}-1}\theta_{1}P^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k,j}}_{k,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}}=\chi_{r_{k}-1}P^{\geq h_{0},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k,j}}_{k,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1},

where once again we used translation invariance.
Let us for a moment replace the projection
\chi_{r_{k}-1}
on the left by
\chi_{r_{k}}
, and use this in the last two terms of the above formula.
We get

(A.15)

\textstyle\theta_{r_{i}}P^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1}-\sum_{i<k<j}\theta_{r_{i}}P^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i+1,k-1},>\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k},\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1}-\sum_{i<k<j}\theta_{r_{i}}P^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0},>\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k+1,j-1},\geq h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1}\\
\textstyle+\sum_{i<k<\ell<j}\theta_{r_{i}}P^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i+1,k-1},>\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k},\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0},>\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{\ell},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{\ell-1,j},\geq h_{0}}_{i,j}\theta^{*}_{r_{j}-1}.

The second and third terms correspond to
\mathsf{N}
paths which stay

and which at some point touch

and at some other point go strictly above one of the
r_{k}
’s; the second sum corresponds to a decomposition of the event that the path goes above some
r_{k}
before it first touches
h_{0}
, while the third sum corresponds to the event that the path goes above some
r_{k}
after it last touches
h_{0}
.
Now we note that the sum in the last term is precisely a decomposition of the probability of the intersection of these two events.
Thus the last three terms above correspond to minus the probability that
\mathsf{N}\;\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 1.00006pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}\;h_{0}
and it goes strictly above one of the
r_{k}
’s.

P^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i,j}}_{i,j}(r_{i}+1,r_{j})

What remains is to account for the difference between
\chi_{r_{k}-1}

\chi_{r_{k}}
in (

This difference is just
\mathbf{1}_{\{r_{k}\}}
, which corresponds to evaluation at
r_{k}
.
So, upon evaluating at
(1,1)
, the remaining terms we get from (
A.13
)/(
A.14
) amount to

(A.16)

\textstyle-\sum_{i<k<j}P^{\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}}_{i,k}(r_{i}+1,r_{k})P^{\geq h_{0},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k,j}}_{k,j}(r_{k},r_{j})\\
\textstyle+\sum_{i<k<\ell<j}P_{i,\ell}^{>h_{0},\leq\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{i+1,k-1},>\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{k},\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 0.81949pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}h_{0}}(r_{i}+1,r_{\ell})P_{\ell,j}^{\geq h_{0},<\check{\mathfrak{d}}_{\ell,j}}(r_{\ell},r_{j}).

\mathsf{N}\;\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 1.00006pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}\;h_{0}
and it touches some of the

’s at some point after it first touches
h_{0}

\check{\mathfrak{d}}
after that.

\mathsf{N}
to go strictly above some of the

, before touching

.

\mathsf{N}\;\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\hskip 1.00006pt\cdot$}\hss}$\geq$}\;h_{0}

\mathsf{N}\;\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\,\,\hskip 1.00006pt\cdot$}\hss}$\leq$}\;\check{\mathfrak{d}}

Appendix B
Trace class estimates

B.1.
Continuity

The PNG kernel and transition probabilities are not continuous in the initial data
h_{0}
in
\UC
.
Consider, for simplicity, the one-point case.
The issue arises at the shock set
\mathcal{S}(x)
.
As an example, take
h_{\varepsilon}(z)=\mathbf{1}_{z\geq t+\varepsilon}
, converging to
h_{0}(z)=\mathbf{1}_{z\geq t}
.
But
P^{\hit(h_{0})}_{-t,t}=e^{2t\Delta}\bar{\chi}_{0}
while
P^{\hit(h_{0}^{\varepsilon})}_{-t,t}=0
. Writing
K_{h}
for
K^{\text{{ext}}}_{h}(0,\cdot;0,\cdot)
we get

K_{h_{0}}=e^{-2t\nabla+t\Delta}\bar{\chi}_{0}e^{2t\nabla-t\Delta}
and
K_{h_{\varepsilon}}=0
.
Moreover
\mathbb{P}_{h_{0}}(h(t,0)\leq 0)=0
while
\mathbb{P}_{h_{\varepsilon}}(h(t,0)\leq 0)=1
.

In the above example,
h_{\varepsilon}\leq h_{0}
.
We will see that if we restrict instead to approximations of
h_{0}
from above, continuity does hold.
To express this more precisely, we say that
h_{n}\longrightarrow h
from above
in
\UC
if
h_{n}\longrightarrow h
in
\UC
and
h_{n}(x)\geq h(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. A function
F\!:\UC\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, is
continuous from above
if
F(h_{n})\longrightarrow F(h)
whenever
h_{n}\longrightarrow h
from above.

Proposition B.1
.

(i)

For each
a<b
in
\mathbb{R}
and
u,v
in
\mathbb{Z}
,
P_{a,b}^{\hit(h)}(u,v)
is continuous from above as a function of
h\in\UC
.

(ii)

For each
h\in\UC
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}
,
P^{\hit(h)}_{x-t,x+t}(u,v)
is right continuous in
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

(i) It is not hard to see that if
h_{n}
are converging to
h
from above in
\UC
, then the Lebesgue measure of the subset
A_{n}
of
[a,b]
on which
h
is strictly less than
h_{n}
is going to zero.
Since
P_{a,b}^{\hit(h_{n})}(u,v)-P_{a,b}^{\hit(h)}(u,v)\geq 0
and it is bounded above by the probability that the walk
\mathsf{N}
has a downward jump in
A_{n}
, which goes to
0
, we conclude that the desired property holds. (ii) Let
t_{\varepsilon}\searrow t
. Since

, for

∎

Proof.

(i) It is enough to show that each entry

Consider the first term on the right hand side of (

for fixed

\textstyle\|\chi_{r_{i}}\vartheta_{i}e^{\frac{1}{2}(x_{j}-x_{i})\Delta}\vartheta^{-1/2}_{i+j}\|_{2}^{2}\leq C^{2}\hskip 0.50003pte^{c(x_{j}-x_{i})}\sum_{u>r_{i}}(1+u^{2})^{2i}\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}}\lambda^{2|u-v|}(1+v^{2})^{-i-j}.

Now we turn to the second term in (

\textstyle m_{0}=\mathop{\mathop{\text{{max}}}}\displaylimits_{z\in[\underline{x},\mathop{\mathop{\text{{min}}}}\displaylimits\{x_{i}+t,\overline{x}\}]}h_{0}(z),\qquad x_{0}=\argmax_{z\in[\underline{x},\mathop{\mathop{\text{{min}}}}\displaylimits\{x_{i}+t,\overline{x}\}]}h_{0}(z),

where in the argmax we take, say, the rightmost point in the interval where the maximum is attained.
Write

h_{0}
through

This dependence will come from estimates on the factors involving

As we will see, the factors

Note that if

\textstyle\int_{\underline{x}}^{x_{0}}\int_{x_{0}}^{\overline{x}}\sum_{\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha(\eta_{1},\mathrm{d}s_{1},\eta_{2},\mathrm{d}s_{2})A_{s_{1}}(u,\eta_{1})B_{s_{2}}(\eta_{2},v)

The trace norm of the average can be bounded by the average of the trace norms of the rank one operators.

and

times

\textstyle\sum_{\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\xi}\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{N}(\underline{x})=\xi}(\hat{\tau}\in[\underline{x},x_{0}],\mathsf{N}(\hat{\tau})=\eta_{1})\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{N}(x_{0})=\xi}(\tau\in[x_{0},\overline{x}],\mathsf{N}(\tau)=\eta_{2})\\

y_{0}=x_{k}+t
moved to
x_{k}+t+\varepsilon
.
The difference on the right hand side of the first line can be estimated in trace norm, after conjugating and projecting, using the arguments of Sec.
B.2
, and it is not hard to obtain a bound of the form
C\hskip 0.50003pt\varepsilon
; we omit the details.
Hence, in order to finish the proof we need to show that the same holds for the second line of (
B.12
), i.e. that

(B.13)

\big\|\vartheta_{i}\chi_{r_{i}}e^{-2t\nabla-t\Delta}\big(P^{\hit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}-P^{\hit(h^{\varepsilon})}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}\big)e^{2t\nabla-t\Delta}\vartheta_{j}^{-1}\chi_{r_{j}}\big\|_{1}\leq C\hskip 0.50003pt\varepsilon.

To prove (
B.13
) we need to separate cases according to where
x_{k}+t
lies with respect to
x_{i}-t
and
x_{j}+t
.
The simplest situation is
x_{k}+t\notin[x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t]
; in this case
h=h^{\varepsilon}
inside
[x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t]
(for small enough
\varepsilon
) so the left hand side of (
B.13
) vanishes.
If
k=j
then
\big(P^{\hit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}-P^{\hit(h^{\varepsilon})}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}\big)(u,\xi)=P^{\hit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}(u,h(x_{k}+t)-1)\mathbf{1}_{\xi=h(x_{k}+t)-1}
, which gives
\big(P^{\hit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}-P^{\hit(h^{\varepsilon})}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}\big)\vartheta_{j}^{-1}\chi_{r_{j}}(u,v)=P^{\hit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}(u,h(x_{k}+t)-1)e^{2t\nabla-t\Delta}(h(x_{k}+t)-1,v)\vartheta_{j}(v)\mathbf{1}_{v>r_{j}}
, but in this case
r_{j}=r_{k}=r_{0}(t,x_{k})-1\geq h(x_{k}+t)-1
, so by Lem.
3.7
the kernel vanishes again.

Finally we consider (
B.13
) when
x_{k}+t\in[x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t)
.
In particular, this implies that the interval is non-empty, and we may assume that it also includes
x_{k}+t+\varepsilon
.
Note that the event inside the probabilities
P^{\hit(h)}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}
and
P^{\hit(h^{\varepsilon})}_{x_{i}-t,x_{j}+t}
is the same if we restrict to paths where the random walk
\mathsf{N}
stays put between time
x_{k}+t
and
x_{k}+t+\varepsilon
, and event whose complement has probability
1-e^{-2\varepsilon}\leq 2\varepsilon
.
The same argument as in the proof of Prop.
B.2
can now be employed to turn this into the desired bound (
B.13
) (note that,
h
and
h^{\varepsilon}
are, in fact, at Hausdorff distance
\varepsilon
).
