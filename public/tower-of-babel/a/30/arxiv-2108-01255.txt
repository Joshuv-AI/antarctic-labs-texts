Abstract
Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) is a popular method for estimating the average treatment effect (ATE). However, empirical studies show that the IPTW estimators can be sensitive to the misspecification of the propensity score model. To address this problem, researchers have proposed to estimate propensity score by directly optimizing the balance of pre-treatment covariates. While these methods appear to empirically perform well, little is known about how the choice of balancing conditions affects their theoretical properties. To fill this gap, we first characterize the asymptotic bias and efficiency of the IPTW estimator based on the Covariate Balancing Propensity Score (CBPS) methodology under local model misspecification. Based on this analysis, we show how to optimally choose the covariate balancing functions and propose an optimal CBPS-based IPTW estimator. This estimator is doubly robust; it is consistent for the ATE if either the propensity score model or the outcome model is correct. In addition, the proposed estimator is locally semiparametric efficient when both models are correctly specified. To further relax the parametric assumptions, we extend our method by using a sieve estimation approach. We show that the resulting estimator is globally efficient under a set of much weaker assumptions and has a smaller asymptotic bias than the existing estimators. Finally, we evaluate the finite sample performance of the proposed estimators via simulation and empirical studies. An open-source software package is available for implementing the proposed methods. Key words: Average treatment effect, causal inference, double robustness, model misspecification, semiparametric efficiency, sieve estimation

Introduction

Suppose that we have a random sample of
n
units from a population of
interest. For each unit
i
, we observe
(T_{i},Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
, where

\bm{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
d
-dimensional vector of pre-treatment
covariates,
T_{i}
is a binary treatment variable, and
Y_{i}
is an
outcome variable. In particular,
T_{i}
takes
1
if unit
i
receives
the treatment and is equal to
0
if unit
i
belongs to the control
group. The observed outcome can be written as

Y_{i}=Y_{i}(1)T_{i}+Y_{i}(0)(1-T_{i})
, where
Y_{i}(1)
and
Y_{i}(0)
are the
potential outcomes under the treatment and control conditions,
respectively. This notation implicitly requires the stable unit
treatment value assumption
(
Rubin 1990
)
. In addition, throughout
this paper, we assume the strong ignorability of the treatment
assignment
(
Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983
)
,

\{Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0)\}\ \mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\ T_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}\quad{\rm and}\quad 0\ <\ \mathbb{P}(T_{i}=1\mid\bm{X}_{i})\ <\ 1.

(1.1)

Next, we assume that the conditional mean functions of potential
outcomes exist and denote them by,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(0)\mid\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K(\bm{X}_{i})\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(1)\mid\bm{X}_{i})\ =\ K(\bm{X}_{i})+L(\bm{X}_{i}),

(1.2)

for some functions
K(\cdot)
and
L(\cdot)
, which represent the
conditional mean of the potential outcome under the control condition and
the conditional average treatment effect, respectively. Under this
setting, we are interested in estimating the average treatment effect
(ATE),

\mu\ =\ \mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0))\ =\ \mathbb{E}(L(\bm{X}_{i})).

(1.3)

The propensity score is defined as the conditional probability of
treatment assignment
(
Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983
)
,

\displaystyle\pi(\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=1\mid\bm{X}_{i}).

(1.4)

In practice, since
\bm{X}_{i}
can be high dimensional, the propensity
score is usually parameterized by a model

\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})
where
\bm{\beta}
is a
q
-dimensional vector of
parameters. A popular choice is the logistic regression model, i.e.,

\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})=\exp(\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta})/\{1+\exp(\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta})\}
.
Once the parameter
\bm{\beta}
is estimated (e.g., by the maximum
likelihood estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
), the Horvitz-Thompson estimator
(
Horvitz and Thompson 1952
)
,
which is based on the inverse probability of treatment weighting
(IPTW), can be used to obtain an estimate of the ATE,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{T_{i}Y_{i}}{\pi_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-\frac{(1-T_{i})Y_{i}}{1-\pi_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right).

(1.5)

However, it has been shown that the IPTW estimator with the known
propensity score does not attain the semiparametric efficiency bound

(
Hahn 1998
)
. A variety of efficient ATE estimators have been
proposed
(see e.g.,
Robins et al. 1994
;
Bang and Robins 2005
;
Tan 2006
;
Qin and Zhang 2007
;
Robins et al. 2007
;
Cao et al. 2009
;
Tan 2010
;
van der Laan 2010
;
Rotnitzky et al. 2012
;
Han and Wang 2013
;
Vermeulen and Vansteelandt 2015
, among many others)
.
Despite the popularity of these methods, researchers have found that
in practice the estimators can be sensitive to the misspecification of
the propensity score model and the outcome model

(
Kang and Schafer 2007
, e.g.,)
. To overcome this problem, several
researchers have recently considered the estimation of the propensity
score by optimizing covariate balance rather than maximizing the
accuracy of predicting treatment assignment

(
Hainmueller 2012
;
Graham et al. 2012
;
Imai and Ratkovic 2014
;
Chan et al. 2016
;
Zubizarreta 2015
;
Zhao and Percival 2017
;
Zhao 2019
, e.g.,)
.
In this paper, we focus on the Covariate Balancing Propensity Score
(CBPS) methodology
(
Imai and Ratkovic 2014
)
. In spite of its simplicity,
several scholars independently found that the CBPS performs well in
practice
(
Wyss et al. 2014
;
Frölich et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
. The method
can also be extended for the analysis of longitudinal data

(
Imai and Ratkovic 2015
)
, general treatment regimes

(
Fong et al. 2018a
)
and high-dimensional propensity score

(
Ning et al. 2018
)
. In this paper, we conduct a theoretical
investigation of the CBPS. Given the similarity between the CBPS and
some other methods, our theoretical analysis may also provide new
insights for understanding other covariate balancing methods.

The CBPS method estimates the parameters of the propensity score
model,
\bm{\beta}
, by solving the following
m
-dimensional estimating equation,

\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})\ =\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\ =\ 0\quad{\rm where}\quad\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\ =\ \left(\frac{T_{i}}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right)\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i}),

(1.6)

for some covariate balancing function

\mathbf{f}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
when the number of equations
m

is equal to the number of parameters
q
.
Imai and Ratkovic 2014
point
out that the common practice of fitting a logistic model is equivalent
to balancing the score function with

\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})=\pi_{\bm{\beta}}^{{}^{\prime}}(\bm{X}_{i})=\partial\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})/\partial\bm{\beta}
. They find that choosing

\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{X}_{i}
, which balances the first moment between the
treatment and control groups, significantly reduces the bias of the
estimated ATE. Some researchers also include higher moments and/or
interactions, e.g.,
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})=(\bm{X}_{i}\ \bm{X}_{i}^{2})
, in their
applications. This guarantees that the treatment and control groups
have an identical sample mean of
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})
after weighting by the
estimated propensity score.

When
m>q
, then
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
can be estimated by optimizing the
covariate balance by the generalized method of moments (GMM) method

(
Hansen 1982
)
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}\ \bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})^{\top}\ \widehat{\mathbf{W}}\ \bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X}),

(1.7)

where
\Theta
is the parameter space for
\bm{\beta}
in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
and

\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
is an
(m\times m)
positive definite weighting matrix,
which we assume in this paper does not depend on
\bm{\beta}
.
Alternatively, the empirical likelihood method can be used

(
Owen 2001
)
. Once the estimate of
\bm{\beta}
is obtained, we can
estimate the ATE using the IPTW estimator in equation (
1.5
).

The main idea of the CBPS and other related methods is to directly
optimize the balance of covariates between the treatment and control
groups so that even when the propensity score model is misspecified we
still obtain a reasonable balance of the covariates between the
treatment and control groups. However, one open question remains in
this literature: How shall we choose the covariate balancing function

\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})
? In particular, if the propensity score model is
misspecified, this problem becomes even more important.

This paper makes two main contributions. First, we conduct a thorough
theoretical study of the CBPS-based IPTW estimator with an arbitrary
covariate balancing function
\mathbf{f}(\cdot)
. We characterize the
asymptotic bias and efficiency of this estimator under locally
misspecified propensity score models. Based on these findings, we
show how to optimally choose the covariate balancing function

\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})
for the CBPS methodology
(Section
2
).

However, the optimal choice of
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})
requires some initial
estimators for the unknown propensity score model and the outcome models. This limits the application of
the CBPS method with the optimal
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})
in practice. Our second contribution is to
overcome this problem by developing an optimal CBPS method that does
not require an initial estimator. We show that the IPTW estimator
based on the optimal CBPS (oCBPS) method retains the double
robustness property. The proposed estimator is
semiparametrically efficient when both the propensity score and
outcome models are correctly specified. More importantly, we show that the rate of convergence of the
proposed oCBPS estimator is faster than the augmented inverse probability weighted (AIPW) estimator
(
Robins et al. 1994
)
under locally misspecified models. (Section
3
).

To relax the parametric assumptions on the propensity score model and
the outcome model, we further extend the proposed oCBPS method to the
nonparametric settings, by using a sieve estimation
approach
(
Newey 1997
;
Chen 2007
)
. In
Section
4
, we establish the semiparametric efficiency
result for the IPTW estimator under the nonparametric setting. Compared to the existing nonparametric
propensity score methods

(
Hirano et al. 2003
;
Chan et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
, our theoretical
results require weaker smoothness assumptions. For instance, the theories in
Hirano et al. 2003
,

Imbens et al. 2007
and
Chan et al. 2016
require
s/d>7
,

s/d>9
and
s/d>13
, respectively, where
s
is the smoothness
parameter of the corresponding function class and
d=\dim(\bm{X}_{i})
. In
comparison, we only require
s/d>3/4
, which is significantly weaker
than the existing conditions. To prove this result, we exploit the
matrix Bernstein’s concentration inequalities

(
Tropp 2015
)
and a Bernstein-type concentration
inequality for U-statistics
(
Arcones 1995
)
.
Moreover, we show that our estimator has smaller asymptotic bias than
the usual nonparametric method

(
Hirano et al. 2003
, e.g.,)
. Therefore, the asymptotic
normality result is expected to be more accurate in practice (Section
4
). The proof of the theoretical results are deferred to the supplementary material.

An open-source
R
software package
CBPS
is available for
implementing the proposed estimators
(
Fong et al. 2018b
)
. In Section
5
, we conduct
simulation studies to evaluate the performance of the proposed
methodology and show that the oCBPS methodology indeed performs
better than the standard CBPS methodology in a variety of
settings. Finally, we conduct an empirical study using a canonical
application in labor economics. We show that the oCBPS method is
able to yield estimates closer to the experimental benchmark when
compared to the standard CBPS method.

2
CBPS under Locally Misspecified Propensity Score Models

Our theoretical investigation starts by examining the consequences of
model misspecification for the CBPS-based IPTW estimator. While
researchers can avoid gross model misspecification through careful
model fitting, in practice it is often difficult to nail down the
exact specification. The prominent simulation study of

Kang and Schafer 2007
, for example, is designed to illustrate this
phenomenon. We therefore consider the consequences of local
misspecification of propensity score model in the general framework of

Copas and Eguchi 2005
. In particular, we assume that the true
propensity score
\pi(\bm{X}_{i})
is related to the working model

\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})
through the exponential tilt for some

\bm{\beta}^{*}
,

\displaystyle\pi(\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})\exp(\xi\ u(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})),

(2.1)

where
u(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})
is a function determining the direction of
misspecification and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
represents the magnitude of
misspecification. We assume
\xi=o(1)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
so that
the true propensity score
\pi(\bm{X}_{i})
is in a local neighborhood of
the working model
\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})
. Intuitively, since

\pi(\bm{X}_{i})\approx\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})
holds, we can interpret

\bm{\beta}^{*}
as the approximate true value of
\bm{\beta}
. The main advantage of this exponential tilt approach is that
\pi(\bm{X})
is always nonnegative, while it does not guarantee
\pi(\bm{X})\leq 1
. However, with
\xi=o(1)
and Assumption
B.1
(i.e.,
|u(\bm{X};\bm{\beta}^{*})|\leq C
almost surely for some constant
C>0
), we can show that
\pi(\bm{X})\leq 1
holds with probability tending to 1. Finally, we note that under suitable regularity conditions model (
2.1
) can be approximated by
\pi(\bm{X})=\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X})+\xi\bar{u}(\bm{X};\bm{\beta}^{*})+O_{p}(\xi^{2})
, for some
\bar{u}(\bm{X};\bm{\beta}^{*})
. This provides an asymptotically equivalent specification of the locally missepecified model. To keep our presentation focused, in this section we assume model (
2.1
) holds.

In the following, we will establish the asymptotic normality of the
CBPS-based IPTW estimator in (
1.5
) under this local model
misspecification framework.

(2.2)

\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt\left.+\bm{H}_{y}^{*}(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*})^{-1}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{u(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right)\right\},

where
K(\bm{X}_{i})
and
L(\bm{X}_{i})
are defined in (
1.2
),
\mathbf{W}^{*}
is the limiting value of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
in (
1.7
), and

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{y}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))L(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))}\cdot\frac{\partial\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))}\left(\frac{\partial\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\right)^{\top}\right).

Furthermore, denote

,

\bar{\bm{H}}^{*}=(1,\bm{H}_{y}^{*\top})~~\textrm{and}~~\bm{\Sigma}=\left(\begin{array}[]{clcr}\Sigma_{\mu}&\bm{\Sigma}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\\
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}&\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\beta}}\end{array}\right),

(2.3)

where

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mu}=\Var\bigl(\mu_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\bigr)\ =\ \mathbb{E}\biggl(\frac{Y_{i}(1)^{2}}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}+\frac{Y_{i}(0)^{2}}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{X}_{i})}-(\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(1))-\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(0)))^{2}\biggr),

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\beta}}=(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*})^{-1}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\Var(\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i}))\mathbf{W}^{*}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*}(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*})^{-1},

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}=-(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*})^{-1}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\Cov(\mu_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i}),\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})),

in which
\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
is defined in (
1.6
).
Under the model in equation (

\displaystyle\Var(\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i}))=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))}\right),

\displaystyle\Cov(\mu_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i}),\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i}))=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\{K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))L(\bm{X}_{i})\}\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))}\right].

The following theorem establishes the asymptotic normality of the
CBPS-based IPTW estimator under the local misspecification of the
propensity score model.

Theorem 2.1

(
Asymptotic Distribution under Local
Misspecification of the Propensity Score Model)
.

If the propensity score model is locally misspecified as
in (
2.1
) with
\xi=n^{-1/2}
and Assumption
B.1

in Appendix
B
holds, the estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
in (
1.5
),
where
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
is obtained by GMM (
1.7
), has the following
asymptotic distribution

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}-\mu)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle{N}(B,\ \bar{\bm{H}}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bar{\bm{H}}^{*}),

(2.4)

where
B
is the asymptotic bias given in equation (
2.2
) and the asymptotic variance
\bar{\bm{H}}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bar{\bm{H}}^{*}
is obtained from (
2.3
).

The theorem shows that the first order asymptotic bias of

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
is given by
B
under local model
misspecification. In particular, this bias term implicitly depends on
the covariate balancing function
\mathbf{f}(\cdot)
. Thus, we consider how
to choose
\mathbf{f}(\cdot)
such that the first order bias
|B|
is
minimized. While at the first glance the expression of
B
appears to
be mathematically intractable, the next corollary shows that any

\mathbf{f}(\bm{X})
satisfying (
2.5
) can eliminate the first order
bias,
B=0
.

Corollary 2.1
.

Suppose that the covariate balancing function
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X})
satisfies the following condition: there exits some
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})=\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(0)\mid\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(1)\mid\bm{X}_{i}).

(2.5)

In addition, assume that the dimension of
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})
is equal to the
number of parameters, i.e.,
m=q
. Then, under the conditions in
Theorem
2.1
, the asymptotic bias of the IPTW
estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
is 0, i.e.,
B=0
.

Intuitively, the above result can be viewed as a “local” version of robustness of IPTW with
respect to the misspecification of the propensity score model. The form of
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})
in (
2.5
) implies that when
balancing covariates, for any given unit we should give a greater
weight to the determinants of the mean potential outcome that is less
likely to be realized. For example, if a unit is less likely to be
treated, then it is more important to balance the covariates that
influence the mean potential outcome under the treatment condition.
In the following, we focus on the asymptotic variance of

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
in
Theorem
2.1
. Interestingly, we can show that the
same choice of
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})
in (
2.5
) minimizes the
asymptotic variance.

Corollary 2.2
.

Under the same conditions in Corollary
2.1
, the
asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
is minimized by any
covariate balancing function
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})
which satisfies
(
2.5
). In this case, the CBPS-based IPTW estimator

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
attains the semiparametric asymptotic variance
bound in Theorem 1 of
Hahn 1998
, i.e.,

V_{\textrm{opt}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\Var(Y_{i}(1)\mid\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}+\frac{\Var(Y_{i}(0)\mid\bm{X}_{i})}{1-\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}+\{L(\bm{X}_{i})-\mu\}^{2}\right].

(2.6)

Based on Theorem
2.1
, we can define the asymptotic
mean squared error (AMSE) of
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
as

AMSE=B^{2}+\bar{\bm{H}}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bar{\bm{H}}^{*}
. Corollaries
2.1
and
2.2

together imply that
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
with
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X})
satisfying
(
2.5
) attains the minimum AMSE over all possible covariate
balancing estimators. Thus, we refer to (
2.5
) as the
optimality condition for the covariate balancing function. We note
that there may exist many choices of
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X})
which satisfy
(
2.5
). For instance, we can choose

f_{1}(\bm{X})=\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(0)\mid\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(1)\mid\bm{X}_{i})
and
f_{2},...,f_{m}

in an arbitrary way, as long as the estimating equation

\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})\ =\ 0
is not degenerate. In this case,
to implement
f_{1}(\bm{X})
, we need to further estimate
\bm{\beta}^{*}
by
some initial estimator, e.g., the maximum likelihood estimator, and
estimate the conditional mean
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(0)\mid\bm{X}_{i})
and

\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(1)\mid\bm{X}_{i})
by some parametric/nonparametric
models. While Corollaries
2.1
and
2.2

hold with this choice of
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X})
, the empirical performance of the
resulting estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
is often unstable due to
the estimation error of the initial estimators. To overcome this
problem, we will next construct the optimal CBPS estimator that does
not require any initial estimator.

3
The Optimal CBPS Methodology

Recall that the optimal covariate balancing function
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X})
is
given by (
2.5
). Plugging
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X})
into the estimating
function
\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
in (
1.6
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{T_{i}}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right)\Big[\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))(K(\bm{X}_{i})+L(\bm{X}_{i}))\Big]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{T_{i}}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right)K(\bm{X}_{i})+\left(\frac{T_{i}}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-1\right)L(\bm{X}_{i}).

(3.1)

In other words, the optimality condition (
2.5
) holds if
and only if some linear combination of estimating function

\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
satisfies (
3.1
). Motivated by this
observation, we construct the following set of estimating functions,

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{\bm{g}}_{1\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})\\
\bar{\bm{g}}_{2\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})\end{array}\right),

where
\bar{\bm{g}}_{1\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})={n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{g}_{1\bm{\beta}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
and
\bar{\bm{g}}_{2\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})={n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{g}_{2\bm{\beta}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
with

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{1\bm{\beta}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{T_{i}}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right)\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i}),\bm{g}_{2\bm{\beta}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\ =\ \left(\frac{T_{i}}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-1\right)\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i}),~~

(3.5)

for some pre-specified functions

\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
and

\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}
with
m_{1}+m_{2}=m
. It is
easy to see that if the functions
K(\cdot)
and
L(\cdot)
lie in the
linear space spanned by the functions
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and

\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
respectively, then there exists a vector

\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that (
3.1
) holds for

(\bm{g}_{1\bm{\beta}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i}),\bm{g}_{2\bm{\beta}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i}))
, further
implying that the optimality condition (
2.5
) is met.

As
discussed in Section
2
, the choice of
the optimal covariate balancing function is not unique. Unlike the one mentioned after Corollary
2.2
, the
estimating function in (
3
) does not require any initial
estimators for
\bm{\beta}
or the conditional mean models, and is more
convenient for implementation. Given the estimating functions in
(
3
), we can estimate
\bm{\beta}
by the GMM estimator

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
in (
1.7
). We call this method as the optimal
CBPS method (oCBPS). Similarly, the ATE is estimated by the IPTW
estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
in (
1.5
). The
implementation of the proposed oCBPS method (e.g., the choice of

\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
) will be discussed in later
sections.

It is worthwhile to note that
\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})
has the
following interpretation. The first set of functions

\bar{\bm{g}}_{1\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})
is the same as the existing covariate
balancing moment function in (
1.6
), which balances the
covariates
\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})
between the treatment and control
groups. However, unlike the original CBPS method, we introduce another
set of functions
\bar{\bm{g}}_{2\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})
which matches the
weighted covariates
\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})
in the treatment group to the
unweighted covariates
\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})
in the control group, because

\bar{\bm{g}}_{2\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})=0
can be rewritten as

\sum_{T_{i}=1}\frac{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})\ =\ \sum_{T_{i}=0}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i}).

As seen in the derivation of (
3.1
), the auxiliary estimating
function
\bar{\bm{g}}_{2\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})
is required in order to satisfy
the optimality condition.

3.1
Theoretical Properties

We now derive the theoretical properties of the IPTW estimator (
1.5
) based on the proposed oCBPS method. In
particular, we will show that the proposed estimator is doubly robust
and locally efficient. The following set of assumptions
are imposed for the establishment of double robustness.

Assumption 3.1
.

The following regularity
conditions are assumed.

1.

There exists a positive definite matrix
\mathbf{W}^{*}
such that

\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbf{W}^{*}
.

2.

For any
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
in (
3.5
), the minimizer

\bm{\beta}^{o}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}(\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X}))^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\mathbb{E}(\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X}))
is unique.

3.

\bm{\beta}^{o}\in\textrm{int}(\Theta)
, where
\Theta
is a
compact set.

4.

\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X})
is continuous in
\bm{\beta}
.

5.

There exists a constant
0<c_{0}<1/2
such that with
probability tending to one,
c_{0}\leq\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X})\leq 1-c_{0}
,
for any
\bm{\beta}\in\textrm{int}(\Theta)
.

6.

\mathbb{E}|Y(1)|^{2}<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}|Y(0)|^{2}<\infty
.

7.

For any
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
in (
3.5
) and
\mathbf{W}^{*}
in part 1,
\mathbf{G}^{*}:=\mathbb{E}(\partial\bm{g}({\bm{\beta}^{o}})/\partial\bm{\beta})

exists where
\bm{g}(\bm{\beta})=(\bm{g}_{1\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})^{\top},\bm{g}_{2\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})^{\top})^{\top}
and there is a
q
-dimensional function
C(\bm{X})
and a
small constant
r>0
such that

\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(\bm{\beta}^{o})}|\partial\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X})/\partial\beta_{k}|\leq C_{k}(\bm{X})
for
1\leq k\leq q
,
and
\mathbb{E}(|h_{1j}(\bm{X})|C_{k}(\bm{X}))<\infty
for
1\leq j\leq m_{1}
,
1\leq k\leq q
and

\mathbb{E}(|h_{2j}(\bm{X})|C_{k}(\bm{X}))<\infty
for
1\leq j\leq m_{2}
,
1\leq k\leq q
, where

\mathbb{B}_{r}(\bm{\beta}^{o})
is a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
with radius
r
and
center
\bm{\beta}^{o}
.

Conditions 1–4 of Assumption
3.1
are the standard conditions
for consistency of the GMM estimator
(
Newey and McFadden 1994
)
. Note that
we allow the propensity score model to be misspecified, so that we use
the notation
\bm{\beta}^{o}
in Condition 2 to distinguish it from

\bm{\beta}^{*}
used in the previous section. Condition 5 is the
positivity assumption commonly used in the causal inference literature

(
Robins et al. 1994
;
Robins et al. 1995
)
. Conditions 6 and 7
are technical conditions that enable us to apply the dominated
convergence theorem. Note that,

\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(\bm{\beta}^{o})}|\partial\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X})/\partial\beta_{k}|\leq C_{k}(\bm{X})
in Condition 7 is a local condition in the sense that it
only requires the existence of an envelop function
C_{k}(\bm{X})
around a
small neighborhood of
\bm{\beta}^{o}
.

We now establish the double robustness of the proposed estimator under
Assumption
3.1
.

Theorem 3.1

(
Double Robustness)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, the proposed oCBPS-based IPTW estimator

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\beta}}
is doubly robust. That is,

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\beta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu
if at least
one of the following two conditions holds:

1.

The propensity score model is correctly specified, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=1\mid\bm{X}_{i})=\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})
;

2.

The functions
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
in (
3.5
) and
\mathbf{W}^{*}
in Assumption
3.1
satisfy the following condition. There exist some vectors

\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
such that
K(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{1}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})
and

L(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{\alpha}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{2}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})
, where
\mathbf{M}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m_{1}}
and
\mathbf{M}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m_{2}}
are the partitions of
\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}=(\mathbf{M}_{1},\mathbf{M}_{2})
.

Next, we establish the asymptotic normality of the proposed estimator
if either the propensity score model (Condition 1 in Theorem
3.1
) or the outcome model is correctly
specified (Condition 2 in Theorem
3.1
) . For this result, we require an additional set of
regularity conditions.

Assumption 3.2
.

The following regularity conditions are
assumed.

1.

For any
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
in (
3.5
) and
\mathbf{W}^{*}
in Assumption
3.1
,
\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\mathbf{G}^{*}
and

\bm{\Omega}=\mathbb{E}(\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})^{\top})

are nonsingular.

2.

The function
C_{k}(\bm{X})
defined in Condition 7 of
Assumption
3.1
satisfies
\mathbb{E}(|Y(0)|C_{k}(\bm{X}))<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}(|Y(1)|C_{k}(\bm{X}))<\infty
for
1\leq k\leq q
.

Condition 1 of Assumption
3.2
ensures the non-singularity of
the asymptotic variance matrix and Condition 2 is a mild technical
condition required for the dominated convergence
theorem.

Theorem 3.2

(
Asymptotic Normality)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
hold.

1.

If Condition 1 of Theorem
3.1
holds, then the proposed
oCBPS-based IPTW estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
has the following
asymptotic distribution:

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}-\mu)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N\left(0,\ \bar{\mathbf{H}}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{*}\right),

(3.6)

where
\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{*}=(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{H}^{*\top})^{\top}
,

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\beta}}=(\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\mathbf{G}^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{W}^{*}\mathbf{G}^{*}(\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\mathbf{G}^{*})^{-1}

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i}))L(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i}))}\cdot\frac{\partial\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma_{\mu}&\bm{\Sigma}^{\top}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}\\
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}&\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\beta}}\end{array}\right),~~\textrm{with}~~\Sigma_{\mu}=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{Y_{i}^{2}(1)}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})}+\frac{Y_{i}^{2}(0)}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right)-\mu^{2}.

In addition,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}

\displaystyle=-(\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\mathbf{G}^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\Big\{\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{i}^{o})L(\bm{X}_{i})}{(1-\pi_{i}^{o})\pi_{i}^{o}}\bm{h}^{\top}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})\Big),

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{i}^{o})L(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{i}^{o}}\bm{h}^{\top}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})\Big)\Big\}^{\top}.

2.

If Condition 2 of Theorem
3.1
holds, then the proposed
oCBPS-based IPTW estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
has the following
asymptotic distribution:

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}-\mu)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N\left(0,\ \widetilde{\mathbf{H}}^{*\top}\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}^{*}\right),

(3.10)

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}^{*}=(1,\check{\mathbf{H}}^{*\top})^{\top}
,

\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{H}}^{*}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\left\{\frac{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})(K(\bm{X}_{i})+L(\bm{X}_{i}))}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})^{2}}+\frac{(1-\pi(\bm{X}_{i}))K(\bm{X}_{i})}{(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i}))^{2}}\right\}\frac{\partial{\pi}_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\bm{\beta}^{o}}\right],

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\mu}&\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}\\
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}&\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\beta}}\end{array}\right)~~\textrm{with}~~\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\mu}\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})Y_{i}^{2}(1)}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})^{2}}+\frac{(1-\pi(\bm{X}_{i}))Y_{i}^{2}(0)}{(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i}))^{2}}\right)-\mu^{2}.

In addition,
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}
is given by

\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}\ =\ -(\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\mathbf{G}^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\bm{S},

where
\bm{S}=(\bm{S}_{1}^{\top},\bm{S}_{2}^{\top})^{\top}
and

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\{\frac{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})(K(\bm{X}_{i})+L(\bm{X}_{i})-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})\mu)}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})^{2}}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 126.47249pt\left.\left.+\frac{(1-\pi(\bm{X}_{i}))(K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i}))\mu)}{(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i}))^{2}}\right\}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})\right],

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\{\frac{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})[(K(\bm{X}_{i})+L(\bm{X}_{i}))(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i}))-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})\mu]}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})^{2}}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 126.47249pt\left.\left.+\frac{(1-\pi(\bm{X}_{i}))K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i}))\mu}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right\}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})\right].

3.

If both Conditions 1 and 2 of Theorem
3.1
hold and

\mathbf{W}^{*}=\bm{\Omega}^{-1}
, then the proposed
oCBPS-based IPTW estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
has the following
asymptotic distribution:

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}-\mu)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,V),

where

V=\Sigma_{\mu}-(\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{2})\mathbf{G}^{*}(\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\bm{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{G}^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{M}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\alpha}_{1}\\
\mathbf{M}_{2}^{\top}\bm{\alpha}_{2}\end{array}\right)

(3.11)

and
\Sigma_{\mu}
is defined in (
1
).

The asymptotic variance
V
in (
3.11
) contains two terms.
The first term
\Sigma_{\mu}
represents the variance of each summand
in the estimator defined in equation (
1.5
) with
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}

replaced by
\bm{\beta}^{o}
. The second term can be
interpreted as the effect of estimating
\bm{\beta}
via covariate
balance conditions. Since this second term is nonnegative, the
proposed estimator is more efficient than the standard IPTW estimator
with the true propensity score model, i.e.,
V\leq\Sigma_{\mu}
. In
particular,
Henmi and Eguchi 2004
offered a theoretical analysis of
such efficiency gain due to the estimation of nuisance parameters
under a general estimating equation framework.

Since the choice of
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
can be arbitrary, it
might be tempting to incorporate more covariate balancing
conditions into
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
. However, the following corollary shows that under Conditions 1 and 2 of
Theorem
3.1
one cannot improve
the efficiency of the proposed estimator by increasing the number of functions

\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
or equivalently, the dimensionality
of covariate balance conditions, i.e.,
\bar{\bm{g}}_{1\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})

and
\bar{\bm{g}}_{2\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Define
\bar{\bm{h}}_{1}(\bm{X})=(\bm{h}^{\top}_{1}(\bm{X}),\bm{a}^{\top}_{1}(\bm{X}))^{\top}
and

\bar{\bm{h}}_{2}(\bm{X})=(\bm{h}^{\top}_{2}(\bm{X}),\bm{a}^{\top}_{2}(\bm{X}))^{\top}
, where

\bm{a}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{a}_{2}(\cdot)
are some additional covariate
balancing functions. Similarly, let
\bar{\bm{g}}_{1}(\bm{X})
and

\bar{\bm{g}}_{2}(\bm{X})
denote the corresponding estimating equations
defined by
\bar{\bm{h}}_{1}(\bm{X})
and
\bar{\bm{h}}_{2}(\bm{X})
. The resulting
oCBPS-based IPTW estimator is denoted by
\bar{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}

where
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
is in (
1.7
) and its
asymptotic variance is denoted by
\bar{V}
. Under Conditions 1 and 2 of
Theorem
3.1
, we have
V\leq\bar{V}
, where
V
is defined in (
3.11
).

The above corollary shows a potential trade-off between robustness and efficiency
when choosing
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
. Recall that Condition 2 of
Theorem
3.1
implies
K(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{1}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})
and

L(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{\alpha}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{2}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})
. Therefore, we can make the
proposed estimator more robust by incorporating more
basis functions into
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
, such that this condition is more likely to hold. However,
Corollary
3.1
shows that doing so may inflate the variance of the proposed estimator.

In the following, we focus on the efficiency of the estimator.
Using the notations in this section, we can rewrite the semiparametric asymptotic variance bound
V_{\textrm{opt}}
in (
2.6
) as

V_{\textrm{opt}}\ =\ \Sigma_{\mu}-(\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{2})\bm{\Omega}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{M}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\alpha}_{1}\\
\mathbf{M}_{2}^{\top}\bm{\alpha}_{2}\end{array}\right).

(3.12)

Comparing this expression with (
3.11
), we see
that the proposed estimator is semiparametrically efficient if
\mathbf{G}^{*}

is a square matrix (i.e.,
m=q
) and invertible.
This important result is summarized as the following corollary.

Corollary 3.2
.

Assume
m=q
and
\mathbf{G}^{*}
is invertible. Under
Assumption
3.1
, the proposed estimator

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
in (
1.5
) is doubly
robust in the sense that

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu
if
either of the following conditions holds:

1.

The propensity score model is correctly specified. That is

\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=1\mid\bm{X}_{i})=\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})
.

2.

There exist some vectors

\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
such that
K(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})
and

L(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{\alpha}_{2}^{\top}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})
.

In addition, under Assumption
3.2
, if both conditions hold, then the proposed estimator has the asymptotic variance given in (
3.12
). Thus, our estimator is a locally semiparametric efficient estimator in the sense of
Robins et al. 1994
.

The corollary shows that the proposed oCBPS method has two advantages
over the original CBPS method
(
Imai and Ratkovic 2014
)
with balancing
first and second moments of
\bm{X}_{i}
and/or the score function of the
propensity score model. First, the proposed estimator

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
is robust to model misspecification, whereas
the original CBPS estimator does not have that property. Second, the
proposed oCBPS estimator can be more efficient than the original CBPS
estimator.

Corollary
3.2
also implies that the asymptotic variance of

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
is identical to the semiparametric variance
bound
V_{\textrm{opt}}
, even if we incorporate additional covariate
balancing functions into
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
. Namely,
under the conditions in Corollary
3.2
, we have

V=\bar{V}=V_{\textrm{opt}}
in the context of Corollary

3.1
. Thus, in this setting, we can improve the
robustness of the estimator without sacrificing the efficiency by
increasing the number of functions
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and

\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
. Meanwhile, this also makes the propensity score model
more flexible, since we need to increase the number of parameters

\bm{\beta}
to match
m=q
as required in Corollary
3.2
. This
observation further motivates us to consider a sieve estimation
approach to improve the oCBPS method, as shown in Section

4
.

Remark 3.1

(
Implementation of the oCBPS method).

Based on Corollary
3.2
,
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
serves as the basis
functions for the baseline conditional mean function
K(\cdot)
,
while
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
represents the basis functions for the
conditional average treatment effect function
L(\cdot)
. Thus, in
practice, researchers can choose a set of basis functions for the
baseline conditional mean function and the conditional average
treatment effect function when determining the specification for

\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
. Once these functions are
selected, they can over-parameterize the propensity score model by
including some higher order terms or interactions such that
m=q

holds. The resulting oCBPS-based IPTW estimator may reduce bias
under model misspecification and attain high
efficiency.

Remark 3.2
.

We also extend the oCBPS method to the estimation of the average
treatment effect for the treated (ATT). Given the space limitation,
we defer the details to the supplementary material.

3.2
Comparison with Related Estimators

Next, we compare the proposed estimator with some related estimators
from the literature. We begin with the following standard AIPW
estimator of
Robins et al. 1994
,

\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}}\ =\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{T_{i}Y_{i}}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-\frac{(1-T_{i})Y_{i}}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-(T_{i}-\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i}))\left(\frac{K(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\alpha})+L(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\gamma})}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})}+\frac{K(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\alpha})}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right)\right\},

where
K(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\alpha})
and
L(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\gamma})
are the conditional
mean models indexed by finite dimensional parameters
\bm{\alpha}
and

\bm{\gamma}
.
Assume the linear outcome models:

K(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\alpha})=\bm{\alpha}^{T}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})
and

L(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\gamma})=\bm{\gamma}^{T}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})
. It is interesting to note
that our IPTW estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
in
Corollary
3.2
can be rewritten as the AIPW estimator

\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}}
(for any
\bm{\alpha}
and

\bm{\gamma}
), since we have,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(T_{i}-\pi_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}(\bm{X}_{i}))\left(\frac{K(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\alpha})+L(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\gamma})}{\pi_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}(\bm{X}_{i})}+\frac{K(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\alpha})}{1-\pi_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right)\ =\ 0,

by the definition of the covariate balancing estimating equations in
(
3
).

It is well known that the AIPW estimator is consistent provided that
either the propensity score model or the outcome model is correctly
specified. Since both the AIPW estimator and our estimator are doubly
robust and locally efficient, in the following we conduct a
theoretical investigation of these two estimators under the scenario
that
both
propensity score and outcome models are
misspecified. Indeed, this scenario corresponds to the simulation
settings used in the influential study of
Kang and Schafer 2007
.

To make the comparison mathematically tractable, we focus on the case
that both of these two models are locally misspecified. Similar to
Section
2
, we assume that the true
treatment assignment satisfies,

\pi(\bm{X}_{i})=\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})\exp(\xi\ u(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*}))
in
(
2.1
), while the true regression functions
K(\bm{X}_{i})
and

L(\bm{X}_{i})
in (
1.2
) satisfy

K(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{\alpha}^{*\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})+\delta r_{1}(\bm{X}_{i}),~~~L(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{\gamma}^{*\top}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})+\delta r_{2}(\bm{X}_{i}),

(3.13)

where
\bm{\alpha}^{*}
and
\bm{\gamma}^{*}
can be viewed as the approximate
true values of
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\gamma}
, the functions
r_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})
and

r_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})
determine the direction of misspecification, and

\delta\in\mathbb{R}
represents the magnitude of misspecification.

Assume further that the models are locally misspecified, i.e.,

\xi,\delta=o(1)
. Under regularity conditions similar to
Section
2
, we can show that the
proposed estimator satisfies,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}-\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\frac{T_{i}}{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}\{Y_{i}(1)-K(\bm{X}_{i})-L(\bm{X}_{i})\}-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}\{Y_{i}(0)-K(\bm{X}_{i})\}+L(\bm{X}_{i})-\mu\Big]

\displaystyle~~~~+O_{p}(\xi^{2}\delta+\delta n^{-1/2}+\xi n^{-1/2}),

(3.14)

whereas the AIPW estimator satisfies,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}},\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}},\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}}-\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\frac{T_{i}}{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}\{Y_{i}(1)-K(\bm{X}_{i})-L(\bm{X}_{i})\}-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}\{Y_{i}(0)-K(\bm{X}_{i})\}+L(\bm{X}_{i})-\mu\Big]

\displaystyle~~~~+O_{p}(\xi\delta+\delta n^{-1/2}+\xi n^{-1/2}),

(3.15)

where
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}},\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}
are the
corresponding maximum likelihood and least square estimators. The
derivation of (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) is shown in
Appendix
I
.

The leading terms in the asymptotic expansions of

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}-\mu
and

\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}},\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}},\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}}-\mu
are
identical and are known as the efficient influence function for

\mu
. However, the remainder terms in (
3.14
) and
(
3.15
) may have different order. Consider the following
two scenarios. First, if
\xi\delta\gg n^{-1/2}
, then we have

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}-\mu=O_{p}(\xi^{2}\delta+n^{-1/2})
and

\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}},\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}},\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}}-\mu=O_{p}(\xi\delta)
. Thus, the proposed
estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
converges in probability to the ATE at a faster
rate than
\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}},\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}},\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}}
. Second, if
\xi\delta=o(n^{-1/2})
, the two estimators have the
same limiting distribution, i.e.,

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mu}-\mu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,V_{\textrm{opt}})
, where
\widehat{\mu}
can be either

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
or

\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}},\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}},\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}}
. However,
the rates of convergence of the Gaussian approximation determined by
the remainder terms in (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) are
different. For instance, assume that
\xi=\delta=n^{-(1/4+\epsilon)}

for some small positive
\epsilon<1/4
. We observe that the remainder
term in (
3.14
) is of order
O_{p}(n^{-(3/4+\epsilon)})
and
is smaller in magnitude than the corresponding term in
(
3.15
), which is of order
O_{p}(n^{-(1/2+2\epsilon)})
. As
a result, the proposed estimator converges in distribution to

N(0,V_{\textrm{opt}})
at a faster rate than the AIPW estimator. The
above analysis justifies the theoretical advantage of the proposed
oCBPS estimator over the standard AIPW estimator.

Furthermore, the proposed estimator is related to the class of
bias-reduced doubly robust estimators
(
Vermeulen and Vansteelandt 2015
)
, see also
Robins et al. 2007
. To see
this, we consider the derivative of

\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}}
with respect to the nuisance
parameters
\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}
. In particular, under the linear
outcome models, it is easily shown that

\partial\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}}/\partial\bm{\alpha}=\bar{\bm{g}}_{1\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})
and

\partial\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}}/\partial\bm{\gamma}=\bar{\bm{g}}_{2\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})
, where

\bar{\bm{g}}_{1\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})
and
\bar{\bm{g}}_{2\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})
are
our covariate balancing functions in (
3
). This provides an
alternative justification for the proposed method: the oCBPS estimator

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
, which satisfies
\bar{\bm{g}}_{2\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})=0
and

\bar{\bm{g}}_{1\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})=0
, removes the local effect of the
estimated nuisance parameters, i.e.,

\partial\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}}/\partial\bm{\alpha}=0
and

\partial\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}}/\partial\bm{\gamma}=0
. This property would not hold if we replace
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
by
the maximum likelihood estimator or other convenient estimators of

\bm{\beta}
.
Vermeulen and Vansteelandt 2015
defined the class of bias-reduced
doubly robust estimator as

\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\bar{\bm{\beta}},\bar{\bm{\alpha}},\bar{\bm{\gamma}}}
, where

(\bar{\bm{\beta}},\bar{\bm{\alpha}},\bar{\bm{\gamma}})
are the estimators
corresponding to the estimating equations

\partial\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}}/\partial\bm{\alpha}=0,\partial\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}}/\partial\bm{\gamma}=0,\partial\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}}/\partial\bm{\beta}=0
. The first two sets of estimating equations are identical to
the covariate balancing estimating equations in (
3
),
whereas the last set of estimating equations

\partial\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}}/\partial\bm{\beta}=0

(leading to the estimators
\bar{\bm{\alpha}},\bar{\bm{\gamma}}
) is unnecessary
in our setting because

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}=\widehat{\mu}^{AIPW}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\gamma}}

does not rely on how
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\gamma}
are estimated. As
expected, all the theoretical properties of the bias-reduced doubly
robust estimator in Section 3 of
Vermeulen and Vansteelandt 2015
hold for our
estimator.

Recently, a variety of empirical likelihood based estimators are
proposed to match the moment of covariates in treatment and control
groups

(
Tan 2006
;
Tan 2010
;
Hainmueller 2012
;
Graham et al. 2012
;
Han and Wang 2013
;
Chan et al. 2016
;
Zubizarreta 2015
;
Zhao and Percival 2017
, e.g.,)
. Usually,
these methods aim to estimate
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(1))
and
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(0))
(or

\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(1)\mid T_{i}=0)
and
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(0)\mid T_{i}=1)
) separately and
combine then to estimate the ATE. Our approach directly estimates the
propensity score and ATE by jointly solving the potentially
over-identified estimating functions (
3
). In addition, our
asymptotic results and the discussion rely on the GMM theory for
over-identified estimating functions which is different from these
methods. Another recent paper by
Zhao 2019
studied the
robustness of a general class of loss function based covariate
balancing methods. When the goal is to estimate the ATE, his score
function reduces to our first set of estimating functions

\bar{\bm{g}}_{1\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})
in (
3
). In this case, his
estimator is robust to the misspecification of the propensity score
model under the constant treatment effect model, i.e.,
L(\bm{X})=\tau^{*}

for some constant
\tau^{*}
. In comparison, our methodology and
theoretical results cover a broader case that allows for heterogeneous
treatment effects.

4
Nonparametric oCBPS Methodology

In this section, we extend our theoretical results of the oCBPS methodology to
nonparametric estimation. As seen in Corollary

3.2
, the proposed estimator is efficient if both the
propensity score
\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=1\mid\bm{X}_{i})
and the conditional mean
functions
K(\cdot)
and
L(\cdot)
are correctly specified. To avoid
model misspecification, we can choose a large number of basis
functions
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)
, such that the conditional
mean functions
K(\cdot)
and
L(\cdot)
satisfy the condition 2 in Corollary
3.2
.

However, the parametric assumption for the propensity score model

\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=1\mid\bm{X}_{i})=\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})
imposed in
Corollary
3.2
may be too restrictive. Once the propensity
score model is misspecified, the proposed oCBPS-based IPTW estimator

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
is inefficient and could even become
inconsistent. To relax the strong parametric assumptions imposed in
the previous sections, we propose a flexible
nonparametric approach for modeling the propensity
score and the conditional mean functions. The main advantage of this
nonparametric approach is that, the resulting oCBPS-based IPTW
estimator is semiparametrically efficient under a much broader class
of propensity score models and the conditional mean models than those
of Corollary
3.2
.

Specifically, we assume
\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=1\mid\bm{X}_{i})=J(\psi^{*}(\bm{X}_{i}))
, where

J(\cdot)
is a known monotonic link function (e.g.,

J(\cdot)=\exp(\cdot)/(1+\exp(\cdot))
), and
\psi^{*}(\cdot)
is an
unknown smooth function. One practical way to estimate
\psi^{*}(\cdot)
is
to approximate it by the linear combination of
\kappa
basis functions,
where
\kappa
is allowed to grow with
n
. This approach is known as the
sieve estimation

(
Andrews 1991
;
Newey 1997
)
. In detail, let

\bm{B}(\bm{x})=\{b_{1}(\bm{x}),...,b_{\kappa}(\bm{x})\}
denote a collection of
\kappa
basis
functions, whose mathematical requirement is given in Assumption

E.1
. Intuitively, we would like to approximate
\psi^{*}(\bm{x})
by

\bm{\beta}^{*\top}\bm{B}(\bm{x})
, for some coefficient
\bm{\beta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}
.

To estimate
\bm{\beta}^{*}
, similar to the parametric case, we define

\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T}_{i},\bm{X}_{i})/n
,
where

\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T}_{i},\bm{X}_{i})=(\bm{g}^{\top}_{1\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T}_{i},\bm{X}_{i}),\bm{g}^{\top}_{2\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T}_{i},\bm{X}_{i}))^{\top}
with,

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{1\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T}_{i},\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{T_{i}}{J(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{B}(\bm{X}_{i}))}-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-J(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{B}(\bm{X}_{i}))}\right)\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i}),

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{2\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T}_{i},\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{T_{i}}{J(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{B}(\bm{X}_{i}))}-1\right)\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i}).

Recall that
\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}
are interpreted as the basis functions for
K(\bm{X})
and
L(\bm{X})
.
Let
m_{1}+m_{2}=m
and
\bm{h}(\bm{X})=(\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X})^{\top},\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X})^{\top})^{\top}
. Here, we assume
m=\kappa
, so that the number of
equations in
\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})
is identical to the
dimension of the parameter
\bm{\beta}
. Then define

\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}=\arg\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})\|_{2}^{2}
, where
\Theta
is the parameter space for
\bm{\beta}
and

\|\bm{v}\|_{2}
represents the
L_{2}
norm of the vector
\bm{v}
. The
resulting IPTW estimator is,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{T_{i}Y_{i}}{J(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\bm{B}(\bm{X}_{i}))}-\frac{(1-T_{i})Y_{i}}{1-J(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\bm{B}(\bm{X}_{i}))}\right).

To establish the large sample properties of
\widetilde{\mu}_{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}}
, we require a few regularity conditions. Due to the space constraint, we defer the regularity conditions to the supplementary material. The following
theorem establishes the asymptotic normality and semiparametric
efficiency of the estimator
\widetilde{\mu}_{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}}
.

Theorem 4.1

(
Efficiency under nonparametric models)
.

Assume that Assumption
E.1
in the supplementary material holds, and there exist
r_{b},r_{h}>1/2
,
\bm{\beta}^{*}
and
\bm{\alpha}^{*}=(\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{*\top},\bm{\alpha}_{2}^{*\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}
, such that the propensity score model satisfies

\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}|\psi^{*}(\bm{x})-\bm{\beta}^{*\top}\bm{B}(\bm{x})|=O(\kappa^{-r_{b}}),

(4.1)

and the outcome models
K(\cdot)
and
L(\cdot)
satisfy

\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}|K(\bm{x})-\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{*\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{x})|=O(\kappa^{-r_{h}}),~~\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}|L(\bm{x})-\bm{\alpha}_{2}^{*\top}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{x})|=O(\kappa^{-r_{h}}).

(4.2)

Assume
\kappa=o(n^{1/3})
and
n^{\frac{1}{2(r_{b}+r_{h})}}=o(\kappa)
. Then

n^{1/2}(\widetilde{\mu}_{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}}-\mu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,V_{\textrm{opt}}),

where
V_{\textrm{opt}}
is the asymptotic variance bound in (
2.6
). Thus,
\widetilde{\mu}_{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}}
is semiparametrically efficient.

This theorem can be viewed as a nonparametric version of
Corollary
3.2
. It shows that one can construct a globally
efficient estimator of the treatment effect without imposing strong
parametric assumptions on the propensity score model and the outcome
model. Since the estimator is asymptotically equivalent to the sample
average of the efficient influence function, it is also adaptive in
the sense of
Bickel et al. 1998
.

In the following, we comment on the technical assumptions of
Theorem
4.1
. We assume
\psi^{*}(\bm{x})
and
K(\bm{x})
(also

L(\bm{x})
) can be uniformly approximated by the basis functions

\bm{B}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{x})
(also
\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{x})
) in (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), respectively. It is
well known that the uniform rate of convergence is related to the
smoothness of the functions
\psi^{*}(\bm{x})
and
K(\bm{x})
(also
L(\bm{x})
)
and the dimension of
\bm{X}
. For instance, if the function class

\mathcal{M}
for
\psi^{*}(\bm{x})
and
\mathcal{H}
for
K(\bm{x})
(also

L(\bm{x})
) correspond to the Hölder class with smoothness parameter

s
on the domain
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]^{d}
, under the assumption that
m_{1}\asymp m_{2}\asymp\kappa
, (
4.1
) and (
4.2
)
hold for the spline basis and wavelet basis with
r_{b}=r_{h}=s/d
; see

Newey 1997
;
Chen 2007
for details. In the same setting,

Hirano et al. 2003
considered a nonparametric IPTW estimator,
which is globally efficient under the condition
s/d>7
.

Imbens et al. 2007
established the asymptotic equivalence between a
regression based estimator and
Hirano et al. 2003
’s estimator
under
s/d>9
. Recently,
Chan et al. 2016
proposed a sieve
based calibration estimator under the condition
s/d>13
. Compared to
these existing results, our theorem needs a much weaker condition,
i.e.,

s/d>3/4
. We refer to the supplementary material for further technical discussion of our nonparametric estimator.

5.2
An Empirical Application

We next apply the oCBPS methodology to a well-known study where the
experimental benchmark estimate is available. Specifically,

LaLonde 1986
conducted a study, in which after the
randomized evaluation study was implemented, the experimental control
group is replaced with a set of untreated individuals taken from the
Panel Study of Income Dynamics. This created an artificial
observational study with 297 treated observations and 2,490
control observations. Ever since the original study, this data set
has been used for evaluating whether a new statistical methodology can
recover the experimental benchmark estimate
(
Dehejia and Wahba 1999
;
Smith and Todd 2005
, see
e.g.,)
. In the original CBPS
article,
Imai and Ratkovic 2014
use this data set to show that the
propensity score matching estimator based on the CBPS method
outperforms the matching estimator based on the standard logistic
regression. In the following, we evaluate whether the proposed oCBPS
method can further improve the CBPS methodology.

We begin by replicating the original results of
Imai and Ratkovic 2014

and then compare those results with those of the proposed oCBPS
methodology. To do this, we focus on the estimation of the average
treatment effect for the treated (ATT). The response of interest is earnings in 1978 and the treatment variable is whether or not the individual participates the job training program.
The original randomized
experiment yields the ATT estimate $886, which is used as a benchmark
for the later comparison.
Imai and Ratkovic 2014
consider the propensity
score estimation based on the standard logistic regression (GLM), the
just-identified CBPS with moment balance condition only (CBPS1) and
the over-identified CBPS with score equation and moment balance
condition (CBPS2). Based on each set of these estimated propensity
scores, we estimate the ATT using the 1-to-1 nearest neighbor matching
with replacement. The estimates of standard errors are based on the
results in
Abadie and Imbens 2006
. We then add the estimated
propensity score based on the proposed oCBPS methodology. Since the
quantity of interest is the ATT, we use a slightly modified oCBPS
estimator described in Appendix
H
.

We follow the propensity score model specifications examined in

Imai and Ratkovic 2014
. The covariates we adjust include age, education,
race (white, black or Hispanic), marriage status, high school degree,
earnings in 1974 and earnings in 1975 as pretreatment variables. We
consider three different specification of balance conditions: the
first moment of covariates (Linear), the first and second moment of
covariates (Quadratic), and the Quadratic specification with some
interactions selected by
Smith and Todd 2005
(Smith & Todd). We
compare the performance of each methodology across these three
specifications.

6
Conclusion

This paper presents a theoretical investigation of the covariate
balancing propensity score methodology that others have found work
well in practice
(
Wyss et al. 2014
;
Frölich et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
. We
derive the optimal choice of the covariate balancing function so that
the resulting IPTW estimator is first order unbiased under local
misspecification of the propensity score model. Furthermore, it turns
out that the CBPS-based IPTW estimator with the same covariate
balancing function attains the semiparametric efficiency bound.

Given these theoretical insights, we propose an optimal CBPS
methodology by carefully choosing the covariate balancing estimating
functions. We prove that the proposed oCBPS-based IPTW estimator is
doubly robust and locally
efficient. More importantly, we show that the rate of convergence of the proposed
estimator is faster than the standard AIPW estimator under locally
misspecified models. To relax the parametric assumptions and improve
the double robustness property, we further extend the oCBPS method to
the nonparametric setting. We show that the proposed
estimator can achieve the semiparametric efficiency bound without
imposing parametric assumptions on the propensity score and outcome
models. The theoretical results require weaker technical conditions
than existing methods and the estimator has smaller asymptotic
bias. Our simulation and empirical studies confirm the theoretical
results, demonstrating the advantages of the proposed oCBPS
methodology.

In this work, we mainly focus on the theoretical development of the IPTW estimator with the propensity score estimated by the optimal CBPS approach. It is a very interesting research problem to establish the theoretical results for the matching estimators combined with the optimal CBPS approach or some variants. While the asymptotic theory (i.e., consistency and asymptotic normality) for the estimated propensity score via the optimal CBPS approach can be derived from the current results (by the Delta method), the full development is beyond the scope of this work. We leave it for a future study.

Appendix A
Locally Semiparametric Efficient Estimator

For clarification, we reproduce the following definition of
locally semiparametric efficient estimator given in

Robins et al. 1994
,

Definition A.1
.

Given a semiparametric model, say
A
, and an additional restriction
R
on the joint distribution of the data not imposed by the model, we say that an estimator
\widehat{\alpha}
is locally semiparametric efficient in model
A
at
R
if
\widehat{\alpha}
is a semiparametric estimator in model
A
whose asymptotic variance attains the semiparametric variance bound for model
A
when
R
is true.

In our setting, the semiparametric model
A
corresponds to the joint
distribution of the observed data
(T_{i},Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
subject to the
strong ignorability of the treatment assignment

\{Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0)\}\perp T_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}
; see

Hahn 1998
. The semiparametric variance bound for model
A

is
V_{opt}
. The restriction
R
is the intersection of
R_{1}
and

R_{2}
(denoted by
R_{1}\cap R_{2}
), where
R_{1}
is the model that
satisfies the first condition in Theorem 3.1 (i.e., the propensity
score is correctly specified) and
R_{2}
is the model that satisfies
the second condition in Theorem 3.1 (i..e,

K(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{1}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})
and

L(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{\alpha}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{2}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})
). In Corollary 3.2, we show
that the asymptotic variance of our estimator of ATE

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
is
V_{opt}
when
R_{1}\cap R_{2}
is true. From
the above definition of locally semiparametric efficient estimator, we
can claim that
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
is locally semiparametric
efficient at
R_{1}\cap R_{2}
.

Proof.

First, we derive the bias of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
. By the arguments in the proof of Lemma
B.3
, we can show that
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}=\bm{\beta}^{o}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
, where
\bm{\beta}^{o}
satisfies
\bm{\beta}^{o}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\beta}}\mathbb{E}(\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X}))^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\mathbb{E}(\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X}))
. Let
u_{i}^{*}=u(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})
.
By the propensity score model and the fact that
|u(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})|
is a bounded random variable and
\mathbb{E}|f_{j}(\bm{X}_{i})|<\infty
, we can show that

\mathbb{E}(\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}^{o}})=\mathbb{E}\Big\{\frac{\pi_{i}^{*}(1+\xi u_{i}^{*})\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{i}^{o}}-\frac{(1-\pi_{i}^{*}-\xi\pi_{i}^{*}u_{i}^{*})\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})}{1-\pi_{i}^{o}}\Big\}+O(\xi^{2}).

In addition, following the similar calculation, we have
\mathbb{E}(\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}})=O(\xi)
. Therefore,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{T},\bm{X}))^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\mathbb{E}(\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{T},\bm{X}))=0.

Clearly, this quadratic form
\mathbb{E}(\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X}))^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\mathbb{E}(\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{T},\bm{X}))
must be nonnegative for any
\bm{\beta}
. By the uniqueness of
\bm{\beta}^{o}
, we have
\bm{\beta}^{o}-\bm{\beta}^{*}=o(1)
. Therefore, we can expand
\pi_{i}^{o}
around
\pi_{i}^{*}
, which yields

\mathbb{E}(\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}^{o}})=\mathbb{E}\Big\{\xi\Big(\frac{u_{i}^{*}}{1-\pi_{i}^{*}}\Big)\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})+\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*}(\bm{\beta}^{o}-\bm{\beta}^{*})\Big\}+O(\xi^{2}+\|\bm{\beta}^{o}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}).

This implies that the bias of
\bm{\beta}^{o}
is

\bm{\beta}^{o}-\bm{\beta}^{*}=-\xi(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*})^{-1}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\mathbb{E}\Big\{\Big(\frac{u_{i}^{*}}{1-\pi_{i}^{*}}\Big)\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})\Big\}+O(\xi^{2}).

(C.1)

Our next step is to derive the bias of
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}
. Similar to the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we have

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}-\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}+\mathbf{H}_{y}^{*\top}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{o})+o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

where

D_{i}=\frac{T_{i}Y_{i}(1)}{\pi_{i}^{o}}-\frac{(1-T_{i})Y_{i}(0)}{1-\pi_{i}^{o}}-\mu,

and

n^{1/2}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{o})=-(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*})^{-1}n^{1/2}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})+o_{p}(1).

In addition, following the similar steps, we can show that
\mathbb{E}(D_{i})=Bn^{-1/2}+o(n^{-1/2})

\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}-\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{D_{i}-\mathbb{E}(D_{i})\}+\mathbf{H}_{y}^{*\top}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{o})+Bn^{-1/2}+o_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

Then the asymptotic normality of

\displaystyle B\ =\ \left[\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{u(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})(K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))L(\bm{X}_{i}))}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right\}+\bm{H}_{y}^{*}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*-1}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{u(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\beta}^{*})\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right)\right],

C.3
Proof of Corollary
2.2

Proof.

Recall that even if the propensity score mode is known or pre-specified, the minimum asymptotic variance over the class of regular estimators is given by
V_{\textrm{opt}}
. In the following, we will verify that with the optimal choice of
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X})
our estimator has asymptotic variance
V_{\textrm{opt}}
.

The asymptotic variance bound
V_{\textrm{opt}}
can be written as,

V_{\textrm{opt}}=\Sigma_{\mu}-\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}\bm{\alpha}
, where

\bm{\Omega}=\mathbb{E}(\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{o}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})^{\top})=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}}{\pi_{i}^{*}(1-\pi_{i}^{*})}\right).

We can write the asymptotic variance of our estimator as

\displaystyle V=\Sigma_{\mu}+2\bm{H}_{y}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}+\bm{H}_{y}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\beta}}\bm{H}_{y}^{*},

where

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{y}^{*}=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\partial\mu_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\right)=-\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{i}^{*})L(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{i}^{*}(1-\pi_{i}^{*})}\frac{\partial\pi_{i}^{*}}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}=-(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*})^{-1}\Cov(\mu_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i}),\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})),

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*}=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\right)=-\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{i}^{*}(1-\pi_{i}^{*})}\left(\frac{\partial\pi_{i}^{*}}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\right)^{\top}\right),

\displaystyle\Cov(\mu_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i}),\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i}))=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{K(\bm{X})+(1-\pi_{i}^{*})L(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{i}^{*}(1-\pi_{i}^{*})}\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\beta}}=(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*})^{-1}\Var(\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i}))(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top})^{-1},

\displaystyle\Var(\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}^{*}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i}))=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}}{\pi_{i}^{*}(1-\pi_{i}^{*})}\right).

If
K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{i}^{*})L(\bm{X}_{i})
lies in the linear space spanned by
\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})
, that is,
K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{i}^{*})L(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{y}^{*}=-\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{i}^{*}(1-\pi_{i}^{*})}\frac{\partial\pi_{i}^{*}}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\right)=(\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*})^{\top}.

So

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{y}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}=-\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*}(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*})^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{i}^{*}(1-\pi_{i}^{*})}\right)=-\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}}{\pi_{i}^{*}(1-\pi_{i}^{*})}\right)\bm{\alpha},

and

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{y}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\beta}}\bm{H}_{y}^{*}=\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*}(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*})^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}}{\pi_{i}^{*}(1-\pi_{i}^{*})}\right)(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*\top})^{-1}(\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{*})^{\top}=\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}}{\pi_{i}^{*}(1-\pi_{i}^{*})}\right)\bm{\alpha}.

It is seen that
\bm{H}_{y}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mu\bm{\beta}}=-\bm{H}_{y}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\beta}}\bm{H}_{y}^{*}
. Then we have

\displaystyle V=\Sigma_{\mu}-\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}\bm{\alpha},

which corresponds to the minimum asymptotic variance
V_{\textrm{opt}}
.
∎

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{G}}^{*\top}\bar{\bm{\Omega}}^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{G}}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{G}^{*\top},\mathbf{A}^{\top})\Big(\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{\Omega}&\bm{\Omega}_{1}\\
\bm{\Omega}_{1}&\bm{\Omega}_{2}\end{array}\Big)^{-1}\Big(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{G}^{*}\\
\mathbf{A}\end{array}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(\mathbf{G}^{*\top},\mathbf{A}^{\top})\Big(\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{\Omega}^{-1}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\Big)\Big(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{G}^{*}\\
\mathbf{A}\end{array}\Big)=\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\bm{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{G}^{*}.

This completes the proof of (
D.5
), and therefore the corollary holds.

Proof of Corollary

Proof of Corollary
3.2
.

The proof of the double robustness property mainly follows from Theorem
3.1
. In this case, we only need to verify that
\textrm{span}\{\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)\}=\textrm{span}\{\mathbf{M}_{1}\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)\}
and
\textrm{span}\{\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)\}=\textrm{span}\{\mathbf{M}_{2}\bm{h}_{2}(\cdot)\}
, where
\mathbf{M}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m_{1}}
and
\mathbf{M}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m_{2}}
are the partitions of
\mathbf{G}^{*\top}\mathbf{W}^{*}=(\mathbf{M}_{1},\mathbf{M}_{2})
. Apparently, we have
\textrm{span}\{\mathbf{M}_{1}\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)\}\subseteq\textrm{span}\{\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)\}
, since the former can always be written as a linear combination of
\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)

\textrm{span}\{\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)\}\subseteq\textrm{span}\{\mathbf{M}_{1}\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)\}
, note that the
m_{1}\times m_{1}
principal submatrix
\mathbf{M}_{11}

\textrm{span}\{\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)\}=\textrm{span}\{\mathbf{M}_{11}\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)\}\subseteq\textrm{span}\{\mathbf{M}_{1}\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)\}
. This is because the
m_{1}
dimensional functions
\mathbf{M}_{11}\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)
are identical to the first
m_{1}
coordinates of
\mathbf{M}_{1}\bm{h}_{1}(\cdot)

Lemma F.1

(Bernstein’s inequality for

(

Given i.i.d. random variables

Suppose that

Lemma F.2
.

Under the conditions in Theorem
4.1
, it holds that

\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}\Big|Q_{n}(\bm{\beta})-Q(\bm{\beta})\Big|=O_{p}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{K^{2}\log K}{n}}\Big).

Proof of Lemma
F.2
.

Let
\bm{\xi}(\bm{\beta})=(\xi_{1}(\bm{\beta}),...,\xi_{n}(\bm{\beta}))^{\top}
and
\bm{\phi}(\bm{\beta})=(\phi_{1}(\bm{\beta}),...,\phi_{n}(\bm{\beta}))^{\top}
, where

\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta})=\frac{T_{i}}{J(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{B}(\bm{X}_{i}))}-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-J(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{B}(\bm{X}_{i}))},~~\phi_{i}(\bm{\beta})=\frac{T_{i}}{J(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{B}(\bm{X}_{i}))}-1.

Then we have

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=n^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big[\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta})\xi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})+\phi_{i}(\bm{\beta})\phi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{j})\big]

\displaystyle=n^{-2}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\big[\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta})\xi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})+\phi_{i}(\bm{\beta})\phi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{j})\big]+A_{n}(\bm{\beta}),

where
A_{n}(\bm{\beta})=n^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta})^{2}\|\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})\|^{2}_{2}+\phi_{i}(\bm{\beta})^{2}\|\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\big]
. Since there exists a constant
c_{0}>0
such that
c_{0}\leq|J(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{B}(\bm{x}))|\leq 1-c_{0}
for any
\bm{\beta}\in\Theta
and
T_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, it implies that

\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta})|\leq C
and
\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\phi_{i}(\bm{\beta})|\leq C
for some constant
C>0
. Then we can show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}|A_{n}(\bm{\beta})|\Big)\leq\frac{C}{n}\mathbb{E}(\|\bm{h}(\bm{X}_{i})\|^{2}_{2})=O(K/n).

By the Markov inequality, we have
\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}|A_{n}(\bm{\beta})|=O_{p}(K/n)=o_{p}(1)
. Following the similar arguments, it can be easily shown that
\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}|Q(\bm{\beta})|/n=O(K/n)
. Thus, it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}|Q_{n}(\bm{\beta})-Q(\bm{\beta})|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}\Big|\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}u_{ij}(\bm{\beta})\Big|+O_{p}(K/n),

(F.1)

where
u_{ij}(\bm{\beta})=u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta})+u_{2ij}(\bm{\beta})
is a kernel function of a U-statistic with

\displaystyle u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta})\xi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})-\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta})\xi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})],

\displaystyle u_{2ij}(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}(\bm{\beta})\phi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{j})-\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(\bm{\beta})\phi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{j})].

Since
\Theta
is a compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
, by the covering number theory, there exists a constant
C
such that
M=(C/r)^{K}
balls with the radius
r
can cover
\Theta
. Namely,
\Theta\subseteq\cup_{1\leq m\leq M}\Theta_{m}
, where
\Theta_{m}=\{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}:\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}_{m}\|_{2}\leq r\}
for some
\bm{\beta}_{1},...,\bm{\beta}_{M}
. Thus, for any given
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta})\Big|>\epsilon\Big)\leq\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta_{m}}\Big|\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta})\Big|>\epsilon\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Big[\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta}_{m})\Big|>\epsilon/2\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta_{m}}\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Big|u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta})-u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta}_{m})\Big|>\epsilon/2\Big)\Big].

(F.2)

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
|\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})|\leq\|\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})\|_{2}\|\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})\|_{2}\leq CK
, and thus
|u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta}_{m})|\leq CK
. In addition, for any
\bm{\beta}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\{\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})]-\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta})\xi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})]\big\}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big\{\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})]\big\}^{2}\leq\|\mathbb{E}\xi^{2}_{i}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\|_{2}\cdot\|\mathbb{E}\xi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})\|_{2}^{2}\leq CK,

for some constant
C>0
. Here, in the last step we use that fact that

\|\mathbb{E}\xi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\|\xi_{j}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\cdot\mathbb{E}\|\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})\|_{2}^{2}\leq CK,

and
\|\mathbb{E}\xi^{2}_{i}(\bm{\beta})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\|_{2}
is bounded because
\|\mathbb{E}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})^{\top}\|_{2}
is bounded by assumption. Thus, we can apply the Bernstein’s inequality in Lemma
F.1
to the U-statistic with kernel function
u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta}_{m})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta}_{m})\Big|>\epsilon/2\Big)\leq 2\exp\big(-Cn\epsilon^{2}/[K+K\epsilon]\big),

(F.3)

for some constant
C>0
. Since
|\partial J(v)/\partial v|
is upper bounded by a constant for any
v=\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{B}(\bm{x})
, it is easily seen that for any
\bm{\beta}\in\Theta_{m}
,
|\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta})-\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{m})|\leq C|(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}_{m})^{\top}\bm{B}(\bm{X}_{i})|\leq CrK^{1/2}
, where the last step follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequalty. This further implies
|\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta})\xi_{j}(\bm{\beta})-\xi_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{m})\xi_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{m})|\leq CrK^{1/2}
for some constant
C>0
by performing a standard perturbation analysis. Thus,

\displaystyle|u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta})-u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta}_{m})|

\displaystyle\leq CrK^{1/2}|\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})|\leq CrK^{3/2},

and note that with
r=K^{-2}
, then
CrK^{1/2}\mathbb{E}|\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})|\leq\epsilon/4
for
n
large enough. Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta_{m}}\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Big|u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta})-u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta}_{m})\Big|>\epsilon/2\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\frac{2CrK^{1/2}}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})|>\epsilon/2\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\frac{2CrK^{1/2}}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\big[|\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})|-\mathbb{E}|\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{j})|\big]>\epsilon/4\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(-CnK\epsilon^{2}),

(F.4)

where the last step follows from the Hoeffding inequality for U-statistic. Thus, combining (
F.2
), (
F.3
) and (
F.4
), we have for some constants

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}u_{1ij}(\bm{\beta})\Big|>\epsilon\Big)

where we take

Lemma F.6
.

Under the conditions in Theorem
4.1
, it holds that

\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1).

Proof of Lemma
F.6
.

Recall that
\bm{\beta}^{o}
is the minimizer of
Q(\bm{\beta})
. We now decompose
Q(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})-Q(\bm{\beta}^{o})
as

Q(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})-Q(\bm{\beta}^{o})=\underbrace{[Q(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})-Q_{n}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})]}_{I}+\underbrace{[Q_{n}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})-Q_{n}(\bm{\beta}^{o})]}_{II}+\underbrace{[Q_{n}(\bm{\beta}^{o})-Q(\bm{\beta}^{o})]}_{III}.

(F.8)

In the following, we study the terms I, II and III one by one. For the term I, Lemma
F.2
implies

|Q(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})-Q_{n}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})|\leq\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\Theta}\Big|Q_{n}(\bm{\beta})-Q(\bm{\beta})\Big|=o_{p}(1)
.
This shows that
|I|=o_{p}(1)
and the same argument yields
|III|=o_{p}(1)
. For the term II, by the definition of
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
, it is easy to see that
II\leq 0
. Thus, combining with (
F.8
), we have for any constant
\eta>0
to be chosen later,
Q(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})-Q(\bm{\beta}^{o})<\eta
with probability tending to one. For any
\epsilon>0
, define
E_{\epsilon}=\Theta\cap\{\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{o}\|_{2}\geq\epsilon\}
. By the uniqueness of
\bm{\beta}^{o}
, for any
\bm{\beta}\in E_{\epsilon}
, we have
Q(\bm{\beta})>Q(\bm{\beta}^{o})
. Since
E_{\epsilon}
is a compact set, we have
\inf_{\bm{\beta}\in E_{\epsilon}}Q(\bm{\beta})>Q(\bm{\beta}^{o})
. This implies that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\eta^{\prime}>0
such that
Q(\bm{\beta})>Q(\bm{\beta}^{o})+\eta^{\prime}
for any
\bm{\beta}\in E_{\epsilon}
. If
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}\in E_{\epsilon}
, then
Q(\bm{\beta}^{o})+\eta>Q(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})>Q(\bm{\beta}^{o})+\eta^{\prime}
with probability tending to one. Apparently, this does not holds if we take
\eta<\eta^{\prime}
. Thus, we have proved that
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}\notin E_{\epsilon}
, that is
\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{o}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
for any
\epsilon>0
. Thus, we have
\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{o}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)
.

Next, we shall show that
\|\bm{\beta}^{o}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)
. It is easily seen that these together lead to the desired consistency result

\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{\beta}^{o}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}+\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{o}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1).

To show
\|\bm{\beta}^{o}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)
, we use the similar strategy. That is we want to show that for any constant
\eta>0
,
Q(\bm{\beta}^{*})-Q(\bm{\beta}^{o})<\eta
. In the following, we prove that
Q(\bm{\beta}^{*})=O(K^{1-2r_{b}})
. Note that

Q(\bm{\beta}^{*})\leq C^{2}K^{-2r_{b}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}|\bm{h}_{j}(\bm{X})|^{2}=O(K^{1-2r_{b}}),

where the first inequality follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the last step uses the assumption that
\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\|\bm{h}(\bm{x})\|_{2}=O(K^{1/2})
.
In addition, it holds that
Q(\bm{\beta}^{o})\leq Q(\bm{\beta}^{*})=O(K^{1-2r_{b}})
. As
K\rightarrow\infty
, it yields
Q(\bm{\beta}^{*})-Q(\bm{\beta}^{o})<\eta
, for any constant
\eta>0
. The same arguments yield
\|\bm{\beta}^{o}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)
. This completes the proof of the consistency result.
∎

is bounded and the last step follows from Lemma

and the maximum eigenvalue of

.

Next, we consider

\displaystyle\|\bm{\Delta}_{n2}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{G}^{*-1}\|_{2}\cdot\Big\|\Big(\frac{\partial\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\Big)^{-1}\Big\|_{2}\cdot\Big\|\mathbf{G}^{*}-\frac{\partial\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}(\bm{T},\bm{X})}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\Big\|_{2}\cdot\|\bar{\bm{U}}\|_{2}

Appendix G
Discussion on the Results in Section
4

Under the conditions in Theorem
4.1
, it is well known that the convergence rate for estimating
K(\bm{x})
(and also
L(\bm{x})
,
\psi^{*}(\bm{x})
) in the
L_{2}(P)
norm (i.e,
\int(\widehat{K}(\bm{x})-K(\bm{x}))^{2}P(d\bm{x})
) is
O_{p}(\kappa^{-2r_{h}}+\kappa/n)
; see
Newey 1997
. Thus, the optimal choice of
\kappa
that minimizes the rate is
\kappa\asymp n^{1/(2r_{h}+1)}
. Assume that
r_{b}=r_{h}
. With
\kappa\asymp n^{1/(2r_{h}+1)}
, the conditions
\kappa=o(n^{1/3})
and
n^{\frac{1}{2(r_{b}+r_{h})}}=o(\kappa)
always hold as long as
r_{h}>1
. Recall that from the previous discussion
r_{h}=s/d
, where
s
is the smoothness parameter and
d
is the dimension of
\bm{X}
. Thus under very mild conditions
s>d
, we do not need to under-smooth the estimator.

Remark G.1
.

By the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we find that when

\kappa=o(n^{1/(2r_{b}+1)})
and
\kappa=o(n^{1/(2r_{h}+1)})
hold, the asymptotic
bias of the estimator
\widetilde{\mu}_{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}}
is of order

O_{p}(K^{-(r_{b}+r_{h})})
, which is the product of the approximation
errors for
\psi^{*}(\bm{x})
and
K(\bm{x})
(also
L(\bm{x})
). Thus, to make
the bias of the estimator
\widetilde{\mu}_{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}}

asymptotically ignorable, we can require either
r_{b}
or
r_{h}

sufficiently large (not necessarily both). This phenomenon can be
viewed as the double robustness property in the nonparametric
context, which holds for the kernel based doubly robust estimator

(
Rothe and Firpo 2013
)
and the targeted maximum likelihood
estimator
(
Benkeser et al. 2017
)
. In addition, our estimator
has smaller asymptotic bias than the usual nonparametric method. For
simplicity, assume
r_{b}=r_{h}=r
. The asymptotic bias of the IPTW
estimator in
Hirano et al. 2003
is generally of order

O_{p}(\kappa^{-r})
, whereas our estimator has a smaller bias of order

O_{p}(\kappa^{-2r})
.

I_{1}
. Note that

I_{1}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\frac{T_{i}}{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}\{Y_{i}(1)-K(\bm{X}_{i})-L(\bm{X}_{i})\}-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}\{Y_{i}(0)-K(\bm{X}_{i})\}+L(\bm{X}_{i})-\mu\Big]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i},

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\frac{T_{i}}{\pi_{\beta^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-\frac{T_{i}}{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}\Big\}\{Y_{i}(1)-K^{o}(\bm{X}_{i})-L^{o}(\bm{X}_{i})\}

\displaystyle-\Big\{\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\pi_{\beta^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}\Big\}\{Y_{i}(0)-K^{o}(\bm{X}_{i})\}

\displaystyle-\frac{T_{i}}{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}\{K^{o}(\bm{X}_{i})+L^{o}(\bm{X}_{i})-K(\bm{X}_{i})-L(\bm{X}_{i})\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}\{K^{o}(\bm{X}_{i})-K(\bm{X}_{i})\}+L^{o}(\bm{X}_{i})-L(\bm{X}_{i}).

The central limit theorem implies
n^{1/2}(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}-\mathbb{E}\Delta_{i})/sd(\Delta_{i})\rightarrow N(0,1)
. In order to derive the order of
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}
, it suffices to compute the
\mathbb{E}(\Delta_{i})
and
sd(\Delta_{i})
. As in the derivation of (
C.1
), after some algebra, we similarly obtain

\beta^{o}-\beta^{*}=\xi\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{M}+O(\xi^{2}),

where

\mathbf{M}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbb{E}(\frac{1}{1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}u_{i}^{*}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i}))\\
\mathbb{E}(u_{i}^{*}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})\end{array}\right)

and
\mathbf{T}=[\mathbb{E}(\frac{1}{\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})(1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))}\frac{\partial\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\beta}\bm{h}_{1}^{T}(\bm{X}_{i})),\mathbb{E}(\frac{1}{\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\frac{\partial\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\beta}\bm{h}_{2}^{T}(\bm{X}_{i}))]^{T}
.

Denote
\widetilde{r}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})=r_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})-\bm{A}_{1}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})
and
\widetilde{r}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})=r_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})-\bm{A}_{2}\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\Delta_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big\{\frac{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{\beta^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\delta(\widetilde{r}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})+\widetilde{r}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i}))-\frac{1-\pi(\bm{X}_{i})}{1-\pi_{\beta^{o}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\delta\widetilde{r}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})-\delta\widetilde{r}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})\Big\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big\{\{1+\xi u_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\frac{\partial\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\beta}(\beta^{o}-\beta^{*})\}\delta(\widetilde{r}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})+\widetilde{r}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i}))

\displaystyle-\{1-\frac{\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\xi u_{i}^{*}+\frac{1}{1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\frac{\partial\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\beta}(\beta^{o}-\beta^{*})\}\delta\widetilde{r}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})-\delta\widetilde{r}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i}))\Big\}+O(\xi^{2}\delta)

\displaystyle=\xi\delta\mathbb{E}\Big[\{\frac{1}{1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}u_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{(1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\frac{\partial\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\beta}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\}\widetilde{r}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle+\xi\delta\mathbb{E}\Big[\{u_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\frac{\partial\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\beta}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\}\widetilde{r}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})\Big]+O(\xi^{2}\delta).

Assume that at least one entry of
\mathbb{E}\Big[\{\frac{1}{1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}u_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{(1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\frac{\partial\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\beta}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})\Big]
is nonzero. Then, there exists
\bm{A}_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\{\frac{1}{1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}u_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{(1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\frac{\partial\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\beta}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\}\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\{\frac{1}{1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}u_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{(1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\frac{\partial\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\beta}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\}r_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})\Big],

which implies

\mathbb{E}\Big[\{\frac{1}{1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}u_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{(1-\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i}))\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\frac{\partial\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\beta}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\}\widetilde{r}_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})\Big]=0.

Similarly, by choosing a proper
\bm{A}_{2}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[\{u_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\frac{\partial\pi_{\beta^{*}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\beta}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\}\widetilde{r}_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})\Big]=0.

As a result, we obtain
\mathbb{E}(\Delta_{i})=O(\xi^{2}\delta)
. Finally, after some tedious calculation, we can show that
sd(\Delta_{i})=O(\xi+\delta)
. This implies
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}=O_{p}(\xi^{2}\delta+\xi n^{-1/2}+\delta n^{-1/2})
. This completes the proof of (
3.14
). The proof of (
3.15
) follows from the similar argument and we omit the details.

Appendix J
Asymptotic Variance Formulas Used for Simulations

In this appendix, we present the asymptotic variance formulas used for constructing the 95% confidence intervals for calculating the coverage probabilities in the simulations in Section
5.1
. In particular, for a generic estimator
\widehat{\mu}
, the 95% confidence interval is
(\widehat{\mu}-1.96*\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\mu}+1.96*\widehat{\sigma})
, where
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
is the estimate of the asymptotic variance of
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mu}-\mu)
.

For the
True
estimator, the asymptotic variance formula is similar to the one given in Section
2
and is as follows:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mu_{0}}=\Var\bigl(\mu_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(T_{i},Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\bigr)\ =\ \mathbb{E}\biggl(\frac{Y_{i}(1)^{2}}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})}+\frac{Y_{i}(0)^{2}}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-(\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(1))-\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(0)))^{2}\biggr).

For the
GLM
estimator, the asymptotic variance formula is as follows:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\textrm{GLM}}=\Sigma_{\mu_{0}}-\bm{H}_{y}^{\top}\bm{I}^{-1}\bm{H}_{y}

where
\Sigma_{\mu_{0}}
is defined like before,
\bm{I}
is the Fisher Information Matrix, and

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{K(\bm{X}_{i})+(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i}))L(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i}))}\cdot\frac{\partial\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\right).

Since the second term is positive definite,
\Sigma_{GLM}<\Sigma_{\mu}
and thus the variance decreases.

The
GAM
estimator achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound
(
Hirano et al. 2003
)
and so we can use
V_{\textrm{opt}}
given in (
2.6
) as the asymptotic variance formula. The
CBPS
estimator has the following asymptotic variance formula:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\textrm{CBPS}}~~=~~\Sigma_{\mu_{0}}~~+~~

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{y}^{\top}(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}^{-1}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}})^{-1}\bm{H}_{y}

\displaystyle-~~

\displaystyle 2\bm{H}_{y}^{\top}(\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}^{-1}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}})^{-1}\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}^{-1}\Cov(\mu_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(T_{i},Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i}),\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i}))

where
\Sigma_{\mu_{0}}
and
\bm{H}_{y}
are defined like before, and we have:

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{\mathbf{f}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})(1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i}))}\left(\frac{\partial\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})}{\partial\bm{\beta}}\right)^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Var(\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i}))

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(T_{i},\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \left(\frac{T_{i}}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})}\right)\mathbf{f}(\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle\mu_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(T_{i},Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{T_{i}Y_{i}}{\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})}-\frac{(1-T_{i})Y_{i}}{1-\pi_{\bm{\beta}_{0}}(\bm{X}_{i})}.

The asymptotic variance for the
DR
estimator is automatically computed in the R package
drtmle

and the confidence interval was constructed accordingly.

Finally, we note that when we estimate the asymptotic variances, we simply replace the quantities
\pi_{\beta_{0}}
and
K(\bm{X})
and
L(\bm{X})
with their estimates and replace the expectation with the sample average. To save space, we do not repeat the formulas of the estimated variances.
