A geometric framework for asymptotic inference of principal subspaces in PCA
Dimbihery Rabenoro, Xavier Pennec

Abstract
Consider data assumed to be iid samples from a multivariate Gaussian distribution whose covariance matrix has repeated eigenvalues. In our model, these eigenvalues are unknown but their multiplicities are supposed to be known. In this paper, we develop an asymptotic method to infer the collection of all principal subspaces together, i.e. the eigenspaces of this covariance matrix. Our approach is based on the geometry of the flag manifold to which the collection of all principal subspaces and our estimators of it belong.



1
Introduction

This article deals with the asymptotic study of principal component analysis (PCA), in the light of methods from Riemannian geometry. Throughout this paper, we assume that the data are iid samples from a
Gaussian
random vector
X
defined on a probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{A}},\Pr)
and valued in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, whose covariance matrix
\Sigma
has possibly
repeated
eigenvalues. In PCA, the principal subspaces (PS’s) are the eigenspaces of
\Sigma
associated respectively to eigenvalues in decreasing order. The sequence of multiplicities of the latter is called the
type
of
\Sigma
. Then, a classical problem is to estimate the PS’s by deriving Central Limit Theorems (CLT’s) involving the eigenvectors of sample covariance matrices. This question is addressed notably in the Anderson’s celebrated paper
Anderson 1963 Anderson, 1963
. The matrix
\Sigma
may have repeated eigenvalues, although the set of symmetric matrices having multiple eigenvalues is negligible. In fact, it is implicit in
Anderson 1963 Anderson, 1963
that
\Sigma
is obtained by equalizing beforehand nearly equal but
distinct eigenvalues of a sample covariance matrix. In
Szwagier and Pennec 2024 Szwagier and Pennec, 2024
, a principled procedure is developed to equalize such eigenvalues and thus to select the type of
\Sigma
.

The method of
Anderson 1963 Anderson, 1963
yields only an estimation of each PS separately and when its dimension is equal to
1
. This result is improved in
Tyler 1981 Tyler, 1981
where other CLT’s, issued from Perturbation Theory
Kato 1995 Kato, 1995
, provide an estimation of each PS of any dimension. However, each PS remains to be addressed separately.
In contrast, our geometric method allows us notably to estimate the
collection
of all PS together, regardless of their dimensions. Thus, the main novelty of our paper is to build statistics based on the Riemannian geometry of manifolds of linear subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Namely, each PS belongs to a Grassmannian, while the collection of them lies in a
flag manifold
. Here, a flag is viewed as a collection of mutually orthogonal subspaces spanning
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, whose sequence of dimensions is also called the
type
of the flag. This incremental subspaces representation of a flag is equivalent to the more usual one as nested sequence of linear subspaces. In our PCA setting, we denote by
\lambda_{1}>...>\lambda_{r}
the distinct eigenvalues of
\Sigma
, of respective multiplicities
q_{1},...,q_{r}
. Thus, the collection of all PS’s forms a flag of type
{\mathrm{I}}=(q_{i})_{1\leq i\leq r}
, denoted by
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
. Let
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
be the set of all flags of type
{\mathrm{I}}=(q_{i})_{i}
, so that
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)\in{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
. It is well-known that
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
is endowed with a structure of homogeneous space for the orthogonal group
O(d)
: see
Rabenoro and Pennec 2024 Rabenoro and Pennec, 2024
. The introduction of such manifolds in a PCA setting is notably inspired by
Pennec 2018 Pennec, 2018
, where it is proved that PCA can be rephrased as an optimization on a flag manifold. We also refer to
Mankovich Camps-Valls and Birdal 2024 Mankovich Camps-Valls and Birdal, 2024
for the use of flag manifolds for studying PCA.

A first idea to estimate
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
could be to exploit the structure of Riemannian manifold on
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
introduced in
Rabenoro and Pennec 2024 Rabenoro and Pennec, 2024
and, following
Bhattacharya and Patrangenaru 2005 Bhattacharya and Patrangenaru, 2005
, to establish a CLT in
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
in normal coordinates i.e. defined from the Riemannian Logarithm on
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
. However, the latter is
unknown
in closed form. In fact, there is no known metric on
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
for which an explicit expression of the Riemannian Logarithm is available. Here, the incremental subspaces representation of a flag allows us to overcome this lack, since it provides an embedding of
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
within a product of Grassmanians. Indeed, the Riemannian Logarithm is available in closed form in any Grassmannian.

In fact, our method is a wide geometric extension of that of
Anderson 1963 Anderson, 1963
. For
n\geq 1
, let
{\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}}
be the sample covariance matrix from an iid sample of size
n
of
X
. Then, in
Anderson 1963 Anderson, 1963
, a matrix
E_{n}\in O(d)
of the form
E_{n}=\Gamma^{\prime}C_{n}
is considered, where
\Gamma
and
C_{n}
are respectively matrices of eigenvectors of
\Sigma
and
{\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}}
, such that all diagonal entries of
E_{n}
are positive (See Section
2
for precise definitions). So,
E_{n}
measures the deviation between the PS’s and the eigenvectors of
{\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}}
. Then, the asymptotic distribution of
E_{n}
is described in
Anderson 1963 Anderson, 1963
. Thus, the diagonal blocks of size
q_{i}\times q_{i}
of
E_{n}
converge in distribution to a Haar measure on the set of orthogonal
q_{i}\times q_{i}
matrices whose diagonal entries are positive. The other blocks of
E_{n}
converge to Gaussian distributions, which provide Central Limit Theorems (CLT’s) that allow to infer the PS’s.

Instead of considering the usual coordinates of
E_{n}
, we introduce a
geometric splitting
of
E_{n}
into two parts, for which we recover respectively Gaussian distributions and Haar measures as
n{\rightarrow}\infty
. The first part yields CLT’s from which we deduce our estimation of
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
. The second part provides statistics that are valued, for all
n\geq 1
, in the orthogonal groups
(O(q_{i}))_{i}
, contrarily to the diagonal blocks of
E_{n}
. We derive such a geometric splitting of any orthogonal matrix from the structure of
Riemannian submersion
of the map which associates to a
q
-frame the
q
-linear subspace spanned by it, for any
1\leq q\leq d
.

We describe briefly hereafter our method to infer
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
. Thanks to the explicit expression of the Riemannian Logarithm in any Grassmannian, we establish a CLT in a Grassmannian for each PS separately. Then, contrarily to the CLT’s for each PS in
Tyler 1981 Tyler, 1981
, some geometric properties of Grassmannians allow us to
concatenate
our CLT’s to obtain a pivotal statistic for
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
, whose limiting distribution is a
\chi^{2}
one. Namely, recall that a.s., for all
n\geq 1
, the eigenvalues of
{\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}}
are all distinct. Then, one can construct a flag of type
{\mathrm{I}}
from the eigenvectors of
{\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}}
as follows: those associated to the
q_{1}
largest eigenvalues are grouped together, then those to the next
q_{2}
ones, …, and finally those to the
q_{r}
smallest ones. Then, the linear subspaces generated by each group form a flag
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}({\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}})\in{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
which may be compared to
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
. Thus, we prove a result of the form

\frac{n}{4}\left[\widetilde{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{\widehat{{\mathbb{K}}}_{n}}\left(F^{{\mathrm{I}}}\left(\Sigma\right),F^{{\mathrm{I}}}({\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}})\right)\right]^{2}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{d}\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{D}^{{\mathrm{I}}}},

(1.1)

where
\widetilde{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{\widehat{{\mathbb{K}}}_{n}}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
is a discrepancy function on
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
, indexed by a statistic
\widehat{{\mathbb{K}}}_{n}
which is a function of the spectrum of

{\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}}
, and
\mathrm{D}^{{\mathrm{I}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(d^{2}-\sum_{i}q_{i}^{2}\right)
. The convergence in Eq.(
1.1
) is illustrated numerically on synthetic experiments. Finally, we derive, from Eq.(
1.1
), confidence regions for
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}\left(\Sigma\right)
that are interpreted as confidence ellipsoids in
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
, centered at
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}({\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}})
.

Thus, our confidence regions are defined directly in the manifold
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
. In contrast, the confidence regions usually met in the literature on estimation on manifolds are defined in a chart, as in
Bhattacharya and Patrangenaru 2005 Bhattacharya and Patrangenaru, 2005
. Furthermore, in
Tyler 1981 Tyler, 1981
, for fixed
1\leq i\leq r
, the confidence regions for the principal subspace
P_{i}(\Sigma)
are based on a
\chi^{2}_{q_{i}(d-q_{i})}
distribution. Then, the sum of their degrees of freedom is
\sum_{i}q_{i}(d-q_{i})=2\mathrm{D}^{{\mathrm{I}}}
, so that we obtain a gain of a factor of
2
.

This article is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present Anderson’s results and a brief sketch of their proofs. In Section
3
, we develop the background of geometry needed for the rest of the paper and we present notably the aforementioned geometric splitting of
E_{n}
. Then, in Section
4
, we state our main result in Theorem
5
, which provides the limiting distribution of the statistics issued from our splitting of
E_{n}
and we deduce the estimation of the flag
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}\left(\Sigma\right)
. Finally, in Section
5
, we summarize our results and propose some open questions raised by them. The proof of Theorem
5
is deferred to the Appendix.

3.1.4
Background of Riemannian geometry

We introduce Riemannian structures on these manifolds, in order to measure intrinsic distances and perform estimation on them.

More generally, let
{\mathbb{M}}
be a connected smooth manifold. A
metric
on
{\mathbb{M}}
is a collection
g=(g_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{M}}}
of inner products on the tangent spaces
T_{x}{\mathbb{M}}
, varying smoothly wrt
x
. Then,
g
defines the
geodesic distance

\rho_{g}
on
{\mathbb{M}}
, where for
x,y\in{\mathbb{M}}
,
\rho_{g}(x,y)
is the infimum of lengths (wrt
g
) of all
C^{1}
curves between
x
and
y
. A
geodesic
is a smooth curve on
{\mathbb{M}}
that realizes locally this infimum. For
x\in{\mathbb{M}}
and
v\in T_{x}{\mathbb{M}}
, there exists a unique geodesic
\gamma_{x,v}
such that
\gamma_{x,v}(0)=x
and
\dot{\gamma_{x,v}}(0)=v
. By the Hopf-Rinow theorem, the metric space
({\mathbb{M}},\rho_{g})
is complete iff all geodesics are defined on
{\mathbb{R}}
, which we assume in the sequel. In that case, for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{M}}
, there exists at least one geodesic of minimal length between
x
and
y
. For
x\in{\mathbb{M}}
, the
exponential map
at
x
is the map
\mathrm{Exp}_{x}^{{\mathbb{M}}}:T_{x}{\mathbb{M}}{\longrightarrow}{\mathbb{M}}
such that
{\mathrm{Exp}}_{x}^{{\mathbb{M}}}(v)=\gamma_{x,v}(1)
.

We recall that, for all
x\in{\mathbb{M}}
, any geodesic starting at
x
is locally length-minimizing. The
cut locus
of
x\in{\mathbb{M}}
is the set
{\mathrm{Cut}}(x)
of points
y
such that the geodesics starting at
x
cease to be length-minimizing beyond
y
. Thus, on a sphere, the geodesics are the great circles, so that two antipodal points are each other’s cut locus. Let

x\in{\mathbb{M}}
and
y\in{\mathbb{M}}\setminus{\mathrm{Cut}}(x)
. Then, there exists a
unique
geodesic of minimal length between
x
and
y
, whose initial velocity is thus the smallest tangent vector
v\in T_{x}{\mathbb{M}}
such that
{\mathrm{Exp}}_{x}^{{\mathbb{M}}}\left(v\right)=y
. This vector is called the
Riemannian Logarithm
of
y
at
x
, denoted by
\mathrm{Log}_{x}^{{\mathbb{M}}}(y)
, related to the geodesic distance by:

\rho_{g}(x,y)=\left\|{\mathrm{Log}}_{x}^{{\mathbb{M}}}(y)\right\|_{x}.

(3.9)

For any
x\in{\mathbb{M}}
, there exists an open set
{\mathrm{ID}}_{x}\subset T_{x}{\mathbb{M}}
, called the
injectivity domain
at
x
, such that the restriction of
{\mathrm{Exp}}_{x}^{{\mathbb{M}}}
to
{\mathrm{ID}}_{x}
is a diffeomorphism onto
{\mathbb{M}}\setminus{\mathrm{Cut}}(x)
, whose inverse is a restriction of the map
{\mathrm{Log}}_{x}^{{\mathbb{M}}}(\cdot)
. Finally, we present results on
Riemannian submersions
.

involves the maps
{\mathrm{Log}}^{G^{i}}_{{P_{0}^{i}}}
, for
1\leq i\leq r
, each valued in the tangent space
T_{{P_{0}^{i}}}G^{i}
. Thus, we describe hereafter the structure of the latter.

Lemma 6
.

For
1\leq i\leq r
, set
{{\mathbb{T}}_{0}^{i}}:=T_{{P_{0}^{i}}}G^{i}
. Then,

{\mathbb{T}}_{0}^{1}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0_{q_{1}}&A^{1}_{>}\\
\left(A^{1}_{>}\right)^{\prime}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}:A^{1}_{>}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q_{1}\times(q_{2}+...+q_{r})}\right\}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ {\mathbb{T}}_{0}^{r}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&\left(A^{r}_{<}\right)^{\prime}\\
A^{r}_{<}&0_{q_{r}}\\
\end{pmatrix}:A^{r}_{<}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q_{r}\times(q_{1}+...+q_{r-1})}\right\}

{{\mathbb{T}}_{0}^{i}}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&\left(A^{i}_{<}\right)^{\prime}&0\\
A^{i}_{<}&0_{q_{i}}&A^{i}_{>}\\
0&\left(A^{i}_{>}\right)^{\prime}&0\end{pmatrix}:A^{i}_{<}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q_{i}\times(q_{1}+...+q_{i-1})}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ A^{i}_{>}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q_{i}\times(q_{i+1}+...+q_{r})}\right\},\quad i\notin\left\{1,r\right\}.

Proof.

By
(\ref{tangentSpaceGrass})
, for any
P\in G(q,d)
,
T_{P}G(q,d)=\left\{\Delta\in\mathrm{Sym}_{d}:\Delta P+P\Delta=\Delta\right\}
. So,

\Delta\in{{\mathbb{T}}_{0}^{i}}\iff\Delta\in{\mathrm{Sym}_{d}}\textrm{ and }\Delta{P_{0}^{i}}+{P_{0}^{i}}\Delta=\Delta.

(3.19)

We readily check that the sets described in the rhs’ in the statement of this Lemma satisfy
(\ref{conditionToi})
. Thus, each of these sets is a linear subspace of
{{\mathbb{T}}_{0}^{i}}
, and clearly, its dimension is equal to that of
{{\mathbb{T}}_{0}^{i}}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3
.

For any
d\times d
matrix
A=(a_{k\ell})_{1\leq k\leq\ell\leq d}
, its Frobenius norm is

\left\|A\right\|_{F}:={\mathrm{tr}}(A^{\prime}A)=\sqrt{\sum(a_{k\ell})^{2}}.

(3.20)

Let
1\leq i\leq r
. Then, for all
{\mathcal{S}}\in{{\mathbb{T}}_{0}^{i}}
,

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\left\|{\mathcal{S}}\right\|_{F}^{2}=4\sum\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq r}\left\|{\mathcal{S}}^{(i,j)}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(3.21)

Proof.

This identity is an easy consequence of Lemma
6
and
(\ref{defFrobNorm})
.
∎

3.3
Preliminaries for estimating the flag of PS’s

The flag
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
of PS’s is estimated by the
flag of eigenprojections

F^{{\mathrm{I}}}\left({\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}}\right)
, of type
{\mathrm{I}}
, introduced in paragraph
3.3.1
below. Thus, we need a distance on
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
to measure the deviation between flags of type
{\mathrm{I}}
. However, there is no known metric on
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
for which the geodesic distance on
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
is available in closed form. To overcome this lack, we introduce in
(\ref{defDex})
below, an
extrinsic distance

{\mathfrak{D}}_{{\mathrm{ex}}}
on
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
i.e. that is the restriction to
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
of a distance on
{\mathrm{G}^{\mathrm{I}}}
, which has an explicit expression.

3.3.1
Flag of eigenprojections

Let
\Sigma\in{\mathrm{Sym}_{d}}
, whose flag of eigenspaces, denoted by
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
, is of type
{\mathrm{I}}:=(q_{i})_{1\leq i\leq r}
. For
S\in{\mathrm{Sym}_{d}^{\neq}}
, let
\Gamma
and
C
be respective orthogonal matrices of eigenvectors of
\Sigma
and
S
, associated to eigenvalues in decreasing order.

For all
1\leq i\leq r
,
\pi^{i}\left(C^{(i)}\right)
is the projector onto a
q_{i}
-linear subspace spanned by eigenvectors of
C
. This projector is independent of the choice of
C
i.e. depends only on
S
. Thus it is called the
i
-th
eigenprojection
of
S
wrt
{\mathrm{I}}
, denoted by
P_{i}(S)
. Then, the collection
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(S):=\left(P_{i}(S)\right)_{i}
form a flag, called the
flag of eigenprojections
of
S
wrt
{\mathrm{I}}
. By
(\ref{linkPiSproof})
,

F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(S)=\left(P_{i}(S)\right)_{i}=\left(\pi^{i}\left(C^{(i)}\right)\right)_{i}={\pi^{\mathrm{I}}}(C).

(3.22)

Assume that
C\in{\mathbb{W}}_{F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)}
. Set
E:=\Gamma^{\prime}C
. Since
\Gamma
spans
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
, by Lemma
5
, the geodesic paramatrization of
C
wrt
\left(F^{{\mathrm{I}}}\left(\Sigma\right),\Gamma\right)
is fully determined by that of
E
wrt
\left({\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}},I_{d}\right)
, for which

\pi^{i}\left(E^{(i)}\right)=\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot\pi^{i}\left(C^{(i)}\right)=\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot P_{i}(S),\quad 1\leq i\leq r.

(3.23)

3.3.2
Extrinsic distance

Let
{\mathcal{R}}=(R_{i})_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
and
{\mathbb{W}}_{{\mathcal{R}}}
defined in
(\ref{defWR})
. Then,

{\pi^{\mathrm{I}}}\left({\mathbb{W}}_{{\mathcal{R}}}\right)=\left\{{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{i})_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}:\forall 1\leq i\leq r,\penalty\ P_{i}\notin{\mathrm{Cut}}(R_{i})\right\}.

Now, for
{\mathcal{P}}\in{\pi^{\mathrm{I}}}\left({\mathbb{W}}_{{\mathcal{R}}}\right)
, set

{\mathfrak{D}}_{{\mathrm{ex}}}({\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{R}}):=\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\delta^{i}(P_{i},R_{i})^{2}\right)^{1/2}\enskip\textrm{where}\enskip\delta^{i}(P_{i},R_{i}):=\left\|{\mathrm{Log}}_{P_{i}}^{G^{i}}\left(R_{i}\right)\right\|_{F}.

(3.24)

Thus,
\delta^{i}
is the geodesic distance on
G^{i}
. The distance
{\mathfrak{D}}_{{\mathrm{ex}}}
is
extrinsic
, i.e. it is the restriction to
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
of a distance on
{\mathrm{G}^{\mathrm{I}}}
, and has an
explicit expression
, provided by that of
{\mathrm{Log}}_{P_{i}}^{G^{i}}
. Clearly, the action of
O(d)
on
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
preserves
{\mathfrak{D}}_{ex}
, i.e. for all
Q\in O(d)
,

{\mathfrak{D}}_{{\mathrm{ex}}}(Q*{\mathcal{P}},Q*{\mathcal{R}})={\mathfrak{D}}_{{\mathrm{ex}}}({\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{R}}).

(3.25)

In particular, with the notations of paragraph
3.2.4
, we deduce from
(\ref{OdPreservesDex})
that for
E:=\Gamma^{\prime}C
,

{\mathfrak{D}}_{ex}\left({\pi^{\mathrm{I}}}(E),{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}}\right)={\mathfrak{D}}_{ex}\left(\Gamma^{\prime}*{\pi^{\mathrm{I}}}(C),\Gamma^{\prime}*F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)\right)={\mathfrak{D}}_{ex}\left(F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(S),F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)\right).

3.3.3
A discrepancy between flags

In Section
4
, to obtain a pivotal statistic for the flag
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
of PS’s, we need to standardize some Gaussian rv’s. Due to such renormalizations, the resulting confidence regions for
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
are expressed in terms of a
discrepancy
between
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
and
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}({\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}})
, instead of the extrinsic distance
{\mathfrak{D}}_{ex}
between them. In fact, this discrepancy, defined in
(\ref{discrepancyQGamma})
below, is a
deformation
of
{\mathfrak{D}}_{ex}
. This means that, without these renormalizations, this discrepancy is equal to
{\mathfrak{D}}_{ex}
. Let
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathrm{I}})
be the set of all block-diagonal matrices whose structure agrees with the type
{\mathrm{I}}
, i.e.

M\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathrm{I}})\iff M={\mathrm{Diag}}\left(M^{(1,1)},...,M^{(i,i)},...,M^{(r,r)}\right).

In this paragraph,
\Gamma
denotes a variable matrix in
O(d)
. Let
{\mathbb{K}}=\left(K^{i}\right)_{1\leq i\leq r}
be a collection of matrices such that for all
1\leq i\leq r
,
K^{i}\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathrm{I}})
. Let
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{i})_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
. By Lemma
4
,

\Gamma\in{\mathbb{W}}_{{\mathcal{P}}}\iff\forall\penalty\ 1\leq i\leq r,\penalty\ \Gamma^{\prime}P_{i}\Gamma\notin{\mathrm{Cut}}({P_{0}^{i}}).

(3.26)

Then, for
\Gamma\in{\mathbb{W}}_{{\mathcal{P}}}
, set

{\mathfrak{D}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}\left(\Gamma,{\mathcal{P}}\right):=\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\left(\delta_{K^{i}}^{i}\left(\Gamma,P_{i}\right)\right)^{2}}\enskip\textrm{where}\enskip\delta_{K^{i}}^{i}(\Gamma,P_{i}):=\left\|K^{i}{\mathrm{Log}_{P_{0}^{i}}^{G^{i}}}\left(\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot P_{i}\right)K^{i}\right\|_{F}.

(3.27)

Lemma 7
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{i})_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
and
\Gamma\in{\mathbb{W}}_{{\mathcal{P}}}
. Then,
{\mathfrak{D}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}\left(\Gamma,{\mathcal{P}}\right)
is independent of the class of
\Gamma
modulo
O({\mathrm{I}})

\Gamma H\in{\mathbb{W}}_{{\mathcal{P}}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad{\mathfrak{D}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}\left(\Gamma H,{\mathcal{P}}\right)={\mathfrak{D}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}\left(\Gamma,{\mathcal{P}}\right).

(3.28)

Proof.

Recall that
O({\mathrm{I}})
is the isotropy group of
{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathrm{I}}}
under the action of
O(d)
on
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
, i.e. for all
H\in O({\mathrm{I}})
and
1\leq i\leq r
,
H\cdot{P_{0}^{i}}={P_{0}^{i}}
. So, by
(\ref{GammaInWP})
, for all

,

\Gamma\in{\mathbb{W}}_{{\mathcal{P}}}\iff\Gamma H\in{\mathbb{W}}_{{\mathcal{P}}}.

(3.29)

Then, by Corollary
1
, for any
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{i})_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
,
\Gamma\in{\mathbb{W}}_{{\mathcal{P}}}
and
H\in O({\mathrm{I}})
,

{\mathrm{Log}_{P_{0}^{i}}^{G^{i}}}\left((\Gamma H)^{\prime}\cdot R_{i}\right)=H^{\prime}\left({\mathrm{Log}_{P_{0}^{i}}^{G^{i}}}\left(\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot R_{i}\right)\right)H.

This, combined to Remark
7
below, implies that for all
1\leq i\leq r
,

\delta_{K^{i}}^{i}(\Gamma H,P_{i})=\left\|K^{i}\left(H^{\prime}{\mathrm{Log}}_{{P_{0}^{i}}}^{G^{i}}\left(\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot P_{i}\right)H\right)K^{i}\right\|_{F}=\left\|H^{\prime}\left(K^{i}{\mathrm{Log}}_{{P_{0}^{i}}}^{G^{i}}\left(\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot P_{i}\right)K^{i}\right)H\right\|_{F}.

Finally, by the properties of
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{F}
, the rhs hereabove is equal to
\delta_{K^{i}}^{i}(\Gamma,P_{i})
.
∎

Remark 7
.

For all
H\in O({\mathrm{I}})
and
K\in\mathcal{D}({\mathrm{I}})
,
H
and
K
commute, i.e.

.

Now, we may introduce a discrepancy between flags of type
{\mathrm{I}}
as follows.

Definition 10

Let
{\mathbb{K}}=\left(K^{i}\right)_{i}\in\left({\mathcal{D}}({\mathrm{I}})\right)^{r}

such that

. Then, by Lemma

, we may define the

-discrepancy between
{\mathcal{R}}
and

as follows.

\widetilde{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}\left({\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{P}}\right):={\mathfrak{D}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}\left(\Gamma,{\mathcal{P}}\right),

(3.30)

for any

such that

, which is defined modulo

. In particular, it is easy to check that, for

-discrepancy

Remark 8

In general,

is not a distance on
{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}
. However, we readily prove that

(i)

(3.31)

(ii)
The
{\mathbb{K}}
-discrepancy preserves the action of

, i.e. for all

\widetilde{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}\left(Q*{\mathcal{R}},Q*{\mathcal{P}}\right)=\widetilde{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}\left({\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{P}}\right),\quad{\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{P}}\in{\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{I}}}.

Conclusion

Given a Gaussian random vector
X
whose covariance matrix
\Sigma
has possibly repeated eigenvalues, we develop a geometric method to estimate the flag
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
of its PS’s, for which we provide
confidence regions
and implementable tests. These results open many questions, among which the following ones, which we present with their motivations.

(i)
Are our geometric CLT’s of Theorem
5
valid when the distribution of
X
is elliptic?

If so, then, for such a distribution, the estimation of
F^{{\mathrm{I}}}(\Sigma)
would be derived as in paragraphs
4.2
and
4.3
, which are only based on such CLT’s. This question is motivated by
Tyler 1981 Tyler, 1981
. Therein, when
X
is elliptic, a CLT for each
P_{i}({\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}})
is obtained, but not of the form of
Eq.(
4.1
)

(ii)
Does
H_{n}^{i}
converge in
O(q_{i})
to
E^{i,i}
wrt the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence?

By Remark
5
, among all
q_{i}
-frames generating
{\mathrm{rg}}({P_{0}^{i}})
, we have that
{\mathcal{H}}^{q_{i}}_{\left(\Gamma^{\prime}P_{i}({\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}})\Gamma,P_{0}^{i}\right)}\left(E_{n}^{(i)}\right)
minimizes the geodesic distance in
{\mathrm{St}}_{q_{i}}
to
E_{n}^{(i)}
. We conjecture that this geometric optimality should imply a stronger mode of convergence of
H_{n}^{i}
to
E^{i,i}
, i.e. wrt the KL divergence in the compact group
O(q_{i})
. Our conjecture is motivated by the case of iid convolutions on compact groups, for which the convergence in distribution to the Haar measure has been first obtained: See
Johnson 2004 Johnson, 2004
and references therein. Later, it was proved in
Harremoes 2009 Harremoes, 2009
or
Johnson and Suhov 2000 Johnson and Suhov, 2000
