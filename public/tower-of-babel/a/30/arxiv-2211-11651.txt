Semiclassical resonance asymptotics for systems with degenerate crossings of classical trajectories
Marouane Assal, Setsuro Fujiie, Kenta Higuchi

Abstract
. This paper is concerned with the asymptotics of resonances in the semiclassical limit h → 0 + h\to 0^{+} for 2 × 2 2\times 2 matrix Schrödinger operators in one dimension. We study the case where the two underlying classical Hamiltonian trajectories cross tangentially in the phase space. In the setting that one of the classical trajectories is a simple closed curve whereas the other one is non-trapping, we show that the imaginary part of the resonances is of order h ( m 0 + 3 ) / ( m 0 + 1 ) h^{(m_{0}+3)/(m_{0}+1)} , where m 0 m_{0} is the maximal contact order of the crossings. This principal order comes from the subprincipal terms of the transfer matrix at crossing points which describe the propagation of microlocal solutions from one trajectory to the other. In addition, we compute explicitly the leading coefficient of the resonance widths in terms of the probability amplitudes associated with all the generalized classical trajectories escaping to infinity from the closed trajectory.



1.
Introduction

In this work, we are interested in the asymptotic distribution of quantum resonances in the semiclassical limit for matrix Schrödinger operators, especially in the effect of crossings of the underlying classical trajectories.
We study the one-dimensional model of Schrödinger systems arising in the context of the Born-Oppenheimer approximation of diatomic molecular Hamiltonians:

(1.1)

P=P(h):=\begin{pmatrix}P_{1}&hU\\
hU^{*}&P_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

The diagonal part consists of scalar Schrödinger operators

P_{j}=P_{j}(h):=(hD_{x})^{2}+V_{j}(x)\quad(j=1,2),\;\;\;\;D_{x}:=-i\frac{d}{dx},

with smooth real-valued potentials
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}
, and the anti-diagonal part describing the interaction between
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
consists of (
h
times) a first order semiclassical differential operator
U
and its adjoint
U^{*}
with smooth real-valued coefficients on
\mathbb{R}
.
We consider the situation where, at an energy-level
E_{0}
, the classical trajectories of
p_{1}
are trapping while the ones of
p_{2}
are non-trapping.
Here
p_{j}(x,\xi):=\xi^{2}+V_{j}(x)
is the classical Hamiltonian associated with
P_{j}

(j=1,2)
, and its classical trajectories are the integral curves of the corresponding Hamiltonian vector field
H_{p_{j}}=\partial_{\xi}p_{j}\partial_{x}-\partial_{x}p_{j}\partial_{\xi}
. In our one-dimensional framework, the classical trajectories of
p_{j}
coincide with the characteristic set
\Gamma_{j}(E_{0}):=p_{j}^{-1}(E_{0})
.
The interaction between
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
may shift the real eigenvalues near
E_{0}
of
P_{1}
to the lower half complex plane as resonances of
P
(Fermi’s golden rule).
The semiclassical asymptotics of the imaginary parts (widths) of the resonances has been studied in this setting in
[
17
,
18
,
3
,
1
]
and
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
Under a simple well condition on
P_{1}
, the first-mentioned series of works studied the exponential decay rate in crossing free cases while the second one gave polynomial asymptotics in the presence of crossings.

The aim of this paper is to generalize
[
10
]
. In that paper, a simplest model where the classical trajectories of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
cross transversally in the phase space
T^{*}\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{R}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}_{\xi}
is considered. The asymptotic of the resonance widths is explicitly computed at the principal level. In particular, its polynomial power in
h
is 2, which is determined only by the transversality of the crossings.
In the present paper, we relax the transversality condition on crossings, and give a precise asymptotics of the resonance widths for degenerate crossings. In this case, we prove that the polynomial power of the principal asymptotics of the resonance width is
(m+3)/(m+1)
(Theorem
1
), where
m
stands for the contact order (
2.4
) of the crossings (including the transversal case
m=1
). The number of crossing points is allowed to be finitely many. In that case,
m
should be the maximum contact order among them.

The power
(m+3)/(m+1)
is determined by the microlocal transfer matrix at each crossing point. The transfer matrix is a
2\times 2
matrix describing the microlocal outgoing data in terms of the incoming data, and we show in Theorem
3
that it is approximated by
I_{2}+h^{1/(m+1)}T_{\operatorname{sub}}
with an anti-diagonal matrix
T_{\operatorname{sub}}
. In the transversal case, this asymptotic formula was computed in
[
10
]
for matrix Schrödinger operators and generalized in
[
2
]
for general pseudodifferential systems by applying the normal form reduction due to Helffer-Sjöstrand
[
11
]
(see also
[
20
,
5
]
).
In our degenerate case, such a reduction is not valid and we develop a new approach which brings the study of microlocal solutions to the system near a given crossing point
\rho=(\rho_{x},\rho_{\xi})\in\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2}
to that of exact local solutions near
\rho_{x}
constructed by the method established in
[
8
]
.
Microlocally near each classical trajectory, incoming to or outgoing from a crossing point, we compute the asymptotic behavior of these solutions. The principal term of the anti-diagonal entries is expressed as the inner product of the WKB solutions to
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, and its asymptotic expansion is computed by the stationary phase method. The critical points of this integral are the crossing points, and the degeneracy of the critical point coincides with the contact order of the crossing point. From this, we obtain the exponent
1/(m+1)
of the anti-diagonal entries.
Here we only consider the generic case, where the energy level is different from the crossing level.
Otherwise, a crossing point is also a turning point, and the WKB approximation is not valid there. In such a case the analysis would be different.

A Bohr-Sommerfeld type “microlocal quantization condition” for resonances is given using the above microlocal transfer matrices. Resonances will be approximated by energies for which the “monodromy matrix” has one as eigenvalue (we call them pseudo-resonances).
This is an extension of the simple well problem to our system, where a non-trapping trajectory
\Gamma_{2}
exists in addition to the closed curve
\Gamma_{1}
. The usual Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization condition for the scalar operator
P_{1}
is the condition that the multiplication factor
-\exp(2i{\mathcal{A}}(E)/h)
after a continuation along
\Gamma_{1}
of a microlocal solution equals one, where
2{\mathcal{A}}(E)
is the action of
\Gamma_{1}
.
We justify this approximation by pseudo-resonances in the following way. The non-existence of resonance outside an
{\mathcal{O}}(h^{\infty})
-neighborhood of the pseudo-resonances is proved by applying the argument in
[
4
]
. The one-to-one correspondence between the pseudo-resonances and the resonances is shown using the Kato-Rellich theorem. This microlocal method enables us to consider not only a simple model but also general cases with finitely many crossings.

To obtain the principal term of the widths of pseudo-resonances, one needs to compute the asymptotic expansion of the microlocal transfer matrix up to
{\mathcal{O}}(h^{2/(m+1)})
. Instead, we employ the following additional argument.
The width of a resonance can be expressed as
h
times the square of the amplitude of the corresponding suitably normalized resonant state near infinity. The asymptotic behavior of the resonant state coincide with that of the microlocal WKB asymptotics on the outgoing trajectories tending to infinity. We will see in Lemma
4.12
that, for each outgoing trajectory tending to infinity, this microlocal behavior is given as the sum of “probability amplitude” over all “paths” from
\Gamma_{1}
to the trajectory. Here, paths are defined by crossing points and classical trajectories as in the graph theory (see Section
4
). The probability amplitude associated with a path is defined in Definition
4.1
in terms of the actions of the classical trajectories and entries of the microlocal transfer matrices at the crossing points.

The paper is organized as follows. The next section is devoted to the precise assumptions and the statement of our main result. In section
3
, we establish a microlocal connection formula at a crossing point in the phase space (Theorems
2
and
3
).
In the last section, we define the monodromy matrix and pseudo-resonances by applying the transfer matrix given in the previous section, and show the asymptotic distribution of resonances (Theorem
1
) in relation with that of pseudo-resonances.

2.
Assumptions and main result

2.1.
Precise assumptions

Let
p_{j}(x,\xi):=\xi^{2}+V_{j}(x)
,
(x,\xi)\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}
, be the classical Hamiltonian associated with
P_{j}
, and let
H_{p_{j}}:=2\xi\partial_{x}-V_{j}^{\prime}(x)\partial_{\xi}
be the corresponding Hamiltonian vector-field,
j=1,2
. For an energy
E\in\mathbb{R}
, denote by
\Gamma_{j}(E)
the characteristic set associated with
P_{j}
given by

\Gamma_{j}(E):=p_{j}^{-1}(E)=\{(x,\xi)\in T^{*}\mathbb{R};\,p_{j}(x,\xi)=E\}\quad(j=1,2),

and set

\Gamma(E):=\Gamma_{1}(E)\cup\Gamma_{2}(E).

Introduce the interaction operator

U=U(x,hD_{x}):=r_{0}(x)+ir_{1}(x)hD_{x}.

Throughout the paper, we assume the following condition.

Assumption 1
.

V_{1},V_{2},r_{0}
and
r_{1}
are smooth real-valued functions on
\mathbb{R}
. They can be extended to bounded holomorphic functions on an angular complex domain near infinity of the form

\Sigma=\Sigma(\theta_{0},R_{0}):=\{x\in\mathbb{C};\,\left|{\rm Im}\hskip 1.0ptx\right|<(\tan\theta_{0})\left|{\rm Re}\hskip 1.0ptx\right|,\ \left|{\rm Re}\hskip 1.0ptx\right|>R_{0}\}

for some constants
0<\theta_{0}<\pi/2
and
R_{0}\geq 0
.
Moreover, for each
j=1,2
,
V_{j}

admits limits
V_{j}^{\pm}
as
{\rm Re}\hskip 1.0ptx\to\pm\infty
in
\Sigma
.

In the sequel we fix a reference energy-level
E_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
E_{0}\neq V_{j}^{\pm}
,
j=1,2
. For
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
possibly
h
-dependent, one introduces the complex box

(2.1)

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{E_{0}}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2}):=[E_{0}-\delta_{1},E_{0}+\delta_{1}]+i[-\delta_{2},\delta_{2}].

Under Assumption
1
, the operator
P
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{2})
with domain
H^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{2})
. One can define the resonances of
P
in
\mathcal{R}
, for sufficiently small
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
, e.g.,
as the values
E\in\mathbb{C}
such that the equation
Pw=Ew
has a non-trivial outgoing solution
w
, that is, a non-identically vanishing solution such that for some
\theta>0
sufficiently small, the function
x\mapsto w(\zeta_{\theta}(x))
is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{2})
(see e.g.
[
15
]
), where
\zeta_{\theta}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C})
is such that
\zeta_{\theta}(x)=x
for
\left|x\right|\leq R_{0}
and
\zeta_{\theta}(x)=xe^{i\theta}
for
|x|\geq 2R_{0}
.
Equivalently, the resonances can be defined as the eigenvalues of a non-self adjoint operator
P_{\theta}
obtained from
P
by the method of analytic distortion (see Section
4
).
In particular, the imaginary part of each resonance is negative. We denote
\operatorname{Res}(P)
the set of resonances of
P
.

We now make some assumptions on the geometry of the characteristic set
\Gamma(E_{0})
and on the set of crossing points

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}(E_{0}):=\Gamma_{1}(E_{0})\cap\Gamma_{2}(E_{0}).

Assumption 2
.

(i)

There exist real numbers
a_{0}<b_{0}
such that

\frac{V_{1}(x)-E_{0}}{(x-a_{0})(x-b_{0})}>0\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}.

(ii)

The classically allowed region
\{x\in\mathbb{R};\,V_{2}(x)\leq E_{0}\}
consists of unbounded intervals, and
V_{2}^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in\{x\in\mathbb{R};V_{2}(x)=E_{0}\}
.

Assumption
2
(i) means that
V_{1}
admits a simple well at
E_{0}
and hence
\Gamma_{1}(E_{0})
is a simple closed curve symmetric with respect to the
x
-axis, while (ii) implies that
E_{0}
is a non-trapping energy for
p_{2}
.

For
E
near
E_{0}
, we define the action integral

{\mathcal{A}}(E):=\int_{\Gamma_{1}(E)}\xi dx.

This is a smooth function near
E_{0}
. Under Assumption
2
(i), it is well known that the spectrum of the scalar Schrödinger operator
P_{1}
in a small neighborhood of
E_{0}
consists of eigenvalues approximated by the roots of the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization rule (see e.g.,
[
16
,
22
]
)

(2.2)

\cos\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}(E)}{2h}\right)=0.

For
\delta_{1},h>0
small enough, we define an
h
-dependent discrete set
\mathfrak{B}_{h}\subset[E_{0}-\delta_{1},E_{0}+\delta_{1}]
by

(2.3)

\mathfrak{B}_{h}=\mathfrak{B}_{h}(\delta_{1}):=\left\{E\in[E_{0}-\delta_{1},E_{0}+\delta_{1}];\,E\ \text{satisfies}\,\eqref{BSR}\right\}.

The distance of two successive points in
\mathfrak{B}_{h}
is of order
\mathcal{O}(h)
, more precisely, if
\delta_{1}=\mathcal{O}(h)
, then for any
E\in\mathfrak{B}_{h}
one has

\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{B}_{h}\setminus\{E\},E)=\frac{2\pi h}{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(E_{0})}+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{2}).

Assumption 3
.

The set of crossing points
\mathcal{V}
is non-empty, and
\mathcal{V}\cap\{\xi=0\}=\emptyset
. Moreover, the contact order of
\Gamma_{1}(E_{0})
and
\Gamma_{2}(E_{0})
at each crossing point
\rho\in\mathcal{V}
defined by

(2.4)

m_{\rho}:=\min\left\{m\in\mathbb{N};\,H_{p_{1}}^{m}p_{2}(\rho)\neq 0\right\}=\min\left\{m\in\mathbb{N};\,H_{p_{2}}^{m}p_{1}(\rho)\neq 0\right\}

is finite.

Notice that since
\Gamma_{1}(E_{0})
is compact, the number of crossing points is finite under the above assumption. The condition
\mathcal{V}\cap\{\xi=0\}=\emptyset
means the non-existence of a crossing-turning point.
It is equivalent to
V_{2}(a_{0})\neq E_{0}
and
V_{2}(b_{0})\neq E_{0}
. Since
\rho=(\rho_{x},\rho_{\xi})\in\mathcal{V}
implies
\breve{\rho}:=(\rho_{x},-\rho_{\xi})\in\mathcal{V}
, the crossing points appear in pairs.
The contact order
m_{\rho}
at a crossing point
\rho=(\rho_{x},\rho_{\xi})\in\mathcal{V}
coincides with the contact order of the potentials
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
at
\rho_{x}
, more precisely one has

(2.5)

m_{\rho}=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N};V_{1}^{(k)}(\rho_{x})\neq V_{2}^{(k)}(\rho_{x})\}.

In fact, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, one has

H_{p_{1}}^{m}p_{2}(x,\xi)=2^{m}(V_{2}^{(m)}(x)-V_{1}^{(m)}(x))\xi^{m}+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\alpha_{k,m}(x)\xi^{k},

with
\alpha_{k,m}
of the form
\alpha_{k,m}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(V_{2}^{(j)}(x)-V_{1}^{(j)}(x))\beta_{j,k,m}(x)
, where
\beta_{j,k,m}
are smooth functions on
\mathbb{R}
.

2.2.
Main result

In the following, we use the same letter
U
for the principal symbol of the operator
U
, that is,
U(x,\xi):=r_{0}(x)+ir_{1}(x)\xi
,
(x,\xi)\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}
. Set

m_{0}:=\max\left\{m_{\rho};\rho\in\mathcal{V}\right\}.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
hold, and let
\mathfrak{B}_{h}
and
\mathcal{R}
be given by (
2.3
) and (
2.1
) with
\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}=Lh
for an arbitrarily fixed
L>0
. Then there exist a bijective map

z_{h}:\mathfrak{B}_{h}\to{\rm Res}(P)\cap{\mathcal{R}}

and a non-negative smooth function
\mathcal{D}(E)=\mathcal{D}(E;h)
of
E
near
E_{0}
uniformly bounded with respect to
h>0
small enough (given explicitly by (
4.29
)) such that for any
E\in\mathfrak{B}_{h}
one has

(2.6)

|z_{h}(E)-E|=\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{m_{0}+3}{m_{0}+1}}),

(2.7)

{\rm Im}\,z_{h}(E)=-\mathcal{D}(E)h^{\frac{m_{0}+3}{m_{0}+1}}+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{\frac{m_{0}+3}{m_{0}+1}+\kappa}),

uniformly as
h\to 0^{+}
, where
\kappa=\min\left\{1/3,\,1/(m_{0}+1)\right\}
.

The leading coefficient
\mathcal{D}(E)
in the asymptotics (
2.7
) may vanish at some energies
E\in\mathfrak{B}_{h}
for each small
h
.
This means that, in such a case, there exist resonances closer to the real axis than those with imaginary part of order
h^{(m_{0}+3)/(m_{0}+1)}
.
This phenomenon of double lines of resonances occurs due to the existence of a

directed cycle
of the characteristic set
\Gamma(E_{0})

created by crossings. We illustrate this in the next paragraph on a simple model. We conclude this section with the definition of
directed cycle.

Definition 2.1
.

We call a curve
\gamma:[0,T]\to\Gamma
a
generalized classical trajectory
if it is parametrized by the time variable of Hamiltonian flow on each
\Gamma_{j}

(j=1,2)
.
In particular, we call it
directed cycle
if it is a simple closed curve.

In other words, a generalized classical trajectory is a usual classical trajectory of
p_{1}
on
\Gamma_{1}\setminus\Gamma_{2}
and of
p_{2}
on
\Gamma_{2}\setminus\Gamma_{1}
, but it is allowed to change from one to the other at a crossing point.
One may find an analogous terminology in
[
21
]
.
In
[
13
]
, one of the authors showed that a directed cycle made by two non-trapping classical trajectories creates resonances with width of order
h\log(1/h)
.

2.3.
Example

We restate the above result with the explicit value of
{\mathcal{D}}(E)
for a simplest model including the one studied in
[

]
which corresponds to the case

.

Suppose that the set

E_{0}>0,\quad\quad\{x\in\mathbb{R};\,V_{1}(x)=V_{2}(x)\leq E_{0}\}=\{0\}\quad\text{and}\quad V_{1}(0)=V_{2}(0)=0.

Suppose moreover that

satisfies the simple well condition given by Assumption

, and there exists

Under these conditions, the set of crossing points

in the phase space consists of two points

, and there exists a unique directed cycle

. The associated action integral defined by

can be explicitly written as

and

near

(in particular,

and

). Finally assume that

.

3.
Microlocal connection at a crossing point

3.1.
Definition and asymptotics for the microlocal transfer matrix

We recall briefly some basic notions of semiclassical and microlocal analysis, referring to the textbooks
[
6
,
17
,
23
]
for more details.
For
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\mathbb{R}
, we introduce the class
S_{N}^{m}
of matrix-valued symbols

S_{N}^{m}:=\left\{a\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{N\times N})\,;\,\|\partial_{x}^{k}\partial_{\xi}^{l}a(x,\xi)\|_{N\times N}\leq C_{k,l}(1+\xi^{2})^{m/2},\ \forall k,l\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

For a symbol
a\in S_{N}^{m}
, the corresponding
h
-pseudodifferential operator, denoted
a^{w}(x,hD_{x})
, is defined as an unbounded operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{N})
via the
h
-Weyl quantization

(3.1)

\displaystyle a^{w}(x,hD_{x})u(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi h}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{i(x-y)\xi/h}a\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\right)u(y)dyd\xi.

This is a bounded operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{N})
if
m\leq 0
according to the Calderón-Vaillancourt Theorem, see e.g.
[
6
, Theorem 7.11]
.

Let
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
f=f(x;h)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{N})
with
\|f\|_{L^{2}}\leq 1
.
We say that
f
is microlocally
0
near
(x_{0},\xi_{0})
and we write

f(x;h)\equiv 0\quad\text{near }\ (x_{0},\xi_{0}),

if there exists a symbol
\chi=\chi(x,\xi)\in S_{N}^{0}
with
\det\,\chi(x_{0},\xi_{0})\neq 0
such that

\|\chi^{w}(x,hD_{x})f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}={\mathcal{O}}(h^{\infty}).

We also say that
f
is microlocally
0
near a set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
if it is microlocally
0
near each point of
\Omega
.
We define
\operatorname{WF}_{h}(f)
, the semiclassical wave front set of
f
, as the set of all points of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
where
f
is not microlocally
0
.
The following results for scalar pseudo-differential operators are well-known (see e.g.
[
23
, Theorem 12.5]
). Let
a\in S^{0}_{1}
. If
f
satisfies
a^{w}(x,hD)f\equiv 0
microlocally in
U
, we have
\operatorname{WF}_{h}(f)\cap U\subset\{a=0\}
.
Moreover, if
\partial a\neq 0
on
\{a=0\}
, the semiclassical wave front set
\operatorname{WF}_{h}(f)
is invariant under the Hamiltonian flow of
a
.

Throughout this section, we fix
E_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
as in the previous section and consider
E
belonging to our reference box
\mathcal{R}

\operatorname{WF}_{h}(g_{1,2})\subset\Gamma\cap\{\xi>0\}\quad\text{for }\ g_{1,2}:=g_{1,1}+\widetilde{K}_{1,L}g_{2,1}-c_{1,2}\breve{u}_{1}.

By repeating this process, we obtain such functions
g_{1,k}
,
g_{2,k}
and constants
c_{j,k}={\mathcal{O}}(h^{2k+j-3})

(j=1,2)
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
that we have

(3.57)

\displaystyle\operatorname{WF}_{h}(g_{1,k})\subset\Gamma\cap\{\xi>0\}\quad\text{for }\ g_{1,k}:=g_{1,k-1}+\widetilde{K}_{1,L}g_{2,k-1}-c_{1,k}\breve{u}_{1},

\displaystyle\operatorname{WF}_{h}(g_{2,k})\subset\Gamma\cap\{\xi>0\}\quad\text{for }\ g_{2,k}:=g_{2,k-1}+\widetilde{K}_{2,L}g_{1,k}-c_{2,k}\breve{u}_{2}.

Then these functions satisfy

(P(h)-E)g_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}hU_{1}r_{k}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{with }\ g_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}g_{1,k}\\
g_{2,k}\end{pmatrix},\quad r_{k}=\widetilde{K}_{2,L}g_{1,k}-c_{2,k}\breve{u}_{2}.

The estimates on the resolvent operators given by Proposition
3.8
implies
r_{k}={\mathcal{O}}(h^{(2k-1)/(m+1)})
.
∎

4.
Resonance asymptotics

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1
.

4.1.
Probability amplitude

We call a complex number
E\in\mathcal{R}
a
pseudo-resonance
of
P
if there exists an outgoing microlocal solution to the system
(P-E)w=0
near an enough large bounded subset of
\Gamma
. We make this definition precise by introducing the notions of
probability amplitude
and
monodromy matrix
. We will see that the pseudo-resonances approximate the resonances in the semiclassical limit.

On the characteristic set
\Gamma
, we define what we call
probability amplitude
. The probability amplitude is a complex number associated with each
generalized classical trajectory
(see Definition
2.1
).
We recall that
S_{\gamma}=\int_{\gamma}\xi dx
is the action along the trajectory
\gamma
.

Recall first from the Maslov theory that the microlocal WKB solutions change their asymptotic behavior when continued beyond a turning point (see
[
10
]
in our system case).
More precisely,
let
\gamma_{\rm{cl}}
be a classical trajectory for
p_{j}
starting from
\rho_{-}=(x_{-},\xi_{-})\in\Gamma_{j}\setminus(\mathcal{V}\cup\{\xi=0\})
and ending at
\rho_{+}=(x_{+},\xi_{+})\in\Gamma_{j}\setminus(\mathcal{V}\cup\{\xi=0\})
without passing through any crossing point:
\gamma_{\rm{cl}}\cap\mathcal{V}=\emptyset
. Then there exists

\nu_{\gamma_{\rm{cl}}}=\nu_{\gamma_{\rm{cl}}}(h)
which coincides with the number of turning points in
\gamma_{\rm{cl}}
modulo
{\mathcal{O}}(h)

such that the two microlocal solutions
f_{\rho_{-}}(x,h;x_{-})
and

f_{\rho_{+}}(x,h;x_{+})
to the system near
\rho_{-}
and
\rho_{+}
, respectively (see Proposition
3.2
), are related with the formula:

(4.1)

f_{\rho_{-}}(x,h;x_{-})=\exp\left(\frac{i}{h}S_{\gamma_{\rm{cl}}}-\frac{i\pi}{2}\nu_{\gamma_{\rm{cl}}}\right)f_{\rho_{+}}(x,h;x_{+}).

Let
\gamma:[0,T]\to\Gamma
be a generalized classical trajectory whose extremities are supposed not to be a crossing point nor a turning point. Thanks to Assumption
3
which guarantees that a turning point cannot be a crossing point, one can take a finite partition of the time interval

(4.2)

[0,T]=\bigsqcup_{l\in L}I_{l}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{n\in N}I_{n}

such that, for each
l\in L
,
\gamma_{l}:=\gamma(I_{l})
passes through a single turning point and no crossing point, and for
n\in N
,
\gamma_{n}:=\gamma(I_{n})
passes through a single crossing point
\rho_{n}\in\mathcal{V}
and no turning point.
Let
\nu_{\gamma_{l}}=1+\mathcal{O}(h)
,
l\in L
, be defined by (
4.1
). For each
n\in N
, let
T_{\rho_{n}}
be the transfer matrix at
\rho_{n}
defined with the microlocal WKB solutions at the extremities of
\gamma_{n}
, given by Theorem
2
. We denote
\tau_{\gamma_{n}}
the entry of
T_{\rho_{n}}
corresponding to the trajectory
\gamma_{n}
, that is, if the incoming part of
\gamma_{n}
belongs to
\Gamma_{j}
and the outgoing part belongs to
\Gamma_{k}
, then
\tau_{\gamma_{n}}=(T_{\rho_{n}})_{k,j}
is the
(k,j)
-entry of
T_{\rho_{n}}
.
The following definition of the probability amplitude does not depend of the choice of the partition of the time interval (
4.2
).

Definition 4.1
.

For a generalized classical trajectory
\gamma
whose extremities are neither a turning point nor a crossing point, we define the
probability amplitude

\mathscr{P}(\gamma)
by

(4.3)

\mathscr{P}(\gamma):=\exp\left(\frac{i}{h}S_{\gamma}-\frac{i\pi}{2}\sum_{l\in L}\nu_{\gamma_{l}}\right)\prod_{n\in N}\tau_{\gamma_{n}}.

Remark 4.2
.

The probability amplitude maps the addition of generalized classical trajectories to the multiplication. More precisely, if
\gamma_{1}:[0,T_{1}]\to\Gamma
and
\gamma_{2}:[0,T_{2}]\to\Gamma
are two consecutive generalized classical trajectories, that is, the terminal point
\gamma_{1}(T_{1})
coincides with the initial point
\gamma_{2}(0)
, then one has

(4.4)

\mathscr{P}(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})=\mathscr{P}(\gamma_{1})\mathscr{P}(\gamma_{2}).

Here
\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2}:[0,T_{1}+T_{2}]\to\Gamma
stands for the usual composition:

(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})(t)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\gamma_{1}(t)&&0\leq t<T_{1},\\
&\gamma_{2}(t-T_{1})&&T_{1}\leq t\leq T_{1}+T_{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

Using the terminology of the graph theory, we call
vertex
a crossing point and
edge
a classical trajectory joining two adjacent vertices (i.e. an edge does not contain any vertex except at the extremities), and write
\mathcal{E}
the set of all edges (we already use the notation
\mathcal{V}
for the set of vertices). For an edge
{\bm{e}}
, we denote
{\bm{e}}^{-}
its starting point and
{\bm{e}}^{+}
its endpoint.

4.2.
Monodromy matrix

We take a base point
\rho_{\bm{e}}
on each edge
{\bm{e}}\in\mathcal{E}
, and define a matrix
M(E,h)
the size of which is the total number of edges:

(4.5)

M(E,h)=(m_{{\bm{e}},{\bm{e}}^{\prime}}(E,h))_{{\bm{e}},{\bm{e}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}}.

The
({\bm{e}},{\bm{e}}^{\prime})
-entry is 0 if
{\bm{e}}^{-}\neq({\bm{e}}^{\prime})^{+}
and otherwise it is given by the probability amplitude
\mathscr{P}({\gamma})
of the generalized classical trajectory
\gamma
from
\rho_{{\bm{e}}^{\prime}}
to
\rho_{\bm{e}}
passing through exactly one crossing point
{\bm{e}}^{-}=({\bm{e}}^{\prime})^{+}
.

Definition 4.3
.

We call zeros in
\mathcal{R}
of
\det(I-M(E,h))
pseudo-resonances of
P
, and denote the set of pseudo-resonances by
\operatorname{Res}_{0}(P)
.

Remark 4.4
.

The pseudo-resonances are independent of the choice of the base points
\rho_{\bm{e}}
.

Remark 4.5
.

If
E\in\mathcal{R}
and if
w
is an outgoing microlocal solution to the equation
(P-E)w=0
near some neighborhood of
\mathcal{E}
, then
E
is a pseudo-resonance, and there exists an eigenvector
\alpha=(\alpha_{\bm{e}})_{{\bm{e}}\in\mathcal{E}}
of the eigenvalue 1 of
M(E,h)
such that

(4.6)

w\equiv\alpha_{\bm{e}}f_{\rho_{\bm{e}}}\quad\text{ near }\ {\bm{e}}

for any
{\bm{e}}\in\mathcal{E}
. Here
f_{\rho_{\bm{e}}}
is the WKB microlocal solution to the system
(P-E)w=0
near
\rho_{\bm{e}}
given by Proposition
3.2
.

We will see in the next subsection that the pseudo-resonances are a good approximation of the resonances in
\mathcal{R}
.
Here we compute the asymptotic behavior of the determinant

\det(I-M(E,h))
and observe that the condition of the pseudo-resonances coincide with the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization condition at principal level.

Proposition 4.6
.

As
h\to 0^{+}
, we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle\det(I-M(E,h))=1+e^{i\mathcal{A}(E)/h}+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{\frac{2}{m_{0}+1}}),

\displaystyle h\partial_{E}\det(I-M(E,h))=i{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(E)e^{i{\mathcal{A}}(E)/h}+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{\frac{2}{m_{0}+1}}),

uniformly for
E\in\mathcal{R}
.

As a consequence of the above asymptotics one immediately gets the following result:

Corollary 4.7
.

The pseudo-resonances are simple zeros of
\det(I-M(E,h))
and there exists a bijection
\widetilde{z}_{h}:\mathfrak{B}_{h}\to\operatorname{Res}_{0}(P)
such that as
h\to 0^{+}
one has,

(4.8)

\widetilde{z}_{h}(E)=E+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{\frac{m_{0}+3}{m_{0}+1}}),

uniformly for any
E\in\mathfrak{B}_{h}
.

As in the standard graph theory, we call a consecutive sequence of edges a
path
, and a path whose initial and terminal point coincide a
cycle
(we identify two cycles by ignoring their initial and terminal point). A cycle is said to be
primitive
if it does not contain any sub-cycles. Let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of primitive cycles.

Proof of Proposition
4.6
.

By definition, each non-zero entry of
M(E,h)
stands for the probability amplitude. According to (
4.4
), the multiplication of probability amplitudes corresponds to the composition of consecutive generalized classical trajectories.
Consequently, the determinant
\det(I-M)
can be written in terms of the probability amplitudes associated with the cycles as follows:

(4.9)

\det(I-M)=1+\sum_{K}\prod_{{\gamma}\in K}(-\mathscr{P}({\gamma})),

where the summation is taken over the nonempty subsets
K
of
\mathcal{C}
such that
{\gamma}\cap{\gamma}^{\prime}=\emptyset
holds for any
{\gamma},

{\gamma}^{\prime}\in K
with
{\gamma}\neq{\gamma}^{\prime}
.
According to Theorem
3
, for each crossing point
\rho\in\mathcal{V}
, the
(j,k)
-entry of
T_{\rho}
is
1+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{2/(m_{\rho}+1)})
if
j=k
and is
{\mathcal{O}}(h^{1/(m_{\rho}+1)})
if
j\neq k
. Then by the definition of the probability amplitude,

(4.10)

\mathscr{P}({\gamma})={\mathcal{O}}(h^{2/(m_{0}+1)})

holds for any primitive directed cycle
{\gamma}
containing some edges of
\mathcal{E}_{2}
. We also have

(4.11)

\mathscr{P}(\Gamma_{1})=(-1+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{2/(m_{0}+1)}))e^{i{\mathcal{A}}(E)/h}.

The representation (
4.9
) of
\det(I-M)
with Formulae (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) imply the asymptotic formula for
\det(I-M)
. For the derivative, we apply the fact that multiplying by
h
the derivative with respect to
E
of each entry of the transfer matrix
T_{\rho}
keeps the same order as itself.
∎

4.3.
Bijection between resonances and pseudo-resonances

In this subsection, we prove a one to one correspondence between the set of resonances and the set of pseudo-resonances in the domain
\mathcal{R}
and estimate the distance between them.

Proposition 4.8
.

For any
h
-independent
C>0
and for
h
small enough, there is no resonance outside a
Ch^{2}
-neighborhood of pseudo-resonances.

Remark 4.9
.

In fact, for any real number
n
, there is no resonance outside a
Ch^{n}
-neighborhood of pseudo-resonances for
h
small enough.

Proof.

We proceed by a contradiction argument.
Suppose that the proposition is not true. Then there would exist a subset
\mathfrak{h}\subset(0,1]
and maps
E
and
w
defined on
\mathfrak{h}
with values in
\{z\in\mathcal{R};\,\operatorname{dist}(z,\operatorname{Res}_{0}(P(h)))>Ch^{2}\}
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{2})
respectively, such that
0\in\bar{\mathfrak{h}}
,
\|w(h)\|_{L^{2}}=1
and

(4.14)

(P_{\theta}(h)-E(h))w(h)=0\quad\text{for }\ h\in\mathfrak{h}.

Then the equality (
4.6
) for
w
holds with a vector
\alpha=(\alpha_{\bm{e}})_{{\bm{e}}\in\mathcal{E}}
such that
(I-M(E,h))\alpha={\mathcal{O}}(h^{\infty})
. According to Proposition
4.6
, the inverse
(I-M(E,h))^{-1}
exists and is polynomially bounded with respect to
h
for any
E\in\mathcal{R}
with
\operatorname{dist}(E,\operatorname{Res}_{0}(P(h)))>Ch^{2}
, and consequently
w\equiv 0
near
\Gamma(E_{0})
.
This with Formula (
4.14
) implies that
\|w\|_{L^{2}}={\mathcal{O}}(h^{\infty})
which contradicts with
\|w\|_{L^{2}}=1
.
∎

Now, using Proposition
4.8
and the Kato-Rellich theorem we prove the unique existence of a resonance in
{\mathcal{O}}(h^{2})
-neighborhood of each pseudo-resonance:

Proposition 4.10
.

For each
E\in\mathfrak{B}_{h}
, there uniquely exists a resonance
z_{h}(E)
verifying

(4.15)

\left|z_{h}(E)-\widetilde{z}_{h}(E)\right|\leq h^{2},

for small
h>0
.

To prove this proposition, we introduce the family
P_{\theta}(s,h)
,
s\in\mathbb{C},\,\left|s\right|<2
of operators defined by

(4.16)

\displaystyle P_{\theta}(s,h):=P_{\theta}^{\operatorname{diag}}(h)+shQ_{\theta},\quad P_{\theta}^{\operatorname{diag}}(h):=\mathcal{U}_{\theta}P^{\operatorname{diag}}(h)\mathcal{U}_{\theta}^{-1},\quad Q_{\theta}:=\mathcal{U}_{\theta}Q\mathcal{U}_{\theta}^{-1},

\displaystyle P^{\operatorname{diag}}(h):=\begin{pmatrix}P_{1}(h)&0\\
0&P_{2}(h)\end{pmatrix},\quad Q:=\begin{pmatrix}0&U_{1}\\
U_{2}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{U}_{\theta}w:=\left|\zeta_{\theta}^{\prime}(x)\right|^{1/2}w\circ\zeta_{\theta},

where
\zeta_{\theta}
is defined in Section
2
.
This is first defined for analytic functions in
\Sigma
, and then extended to an operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{2})
by using the analyticity of the coefficients (Assumption
1
).
The operator
P_{\theta}
is non-self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{2})
, and its spectrum is discrete in
\mathcal{R}
due to the assumption
E_{0}\neq V_{j}^{\pm}
,
j=1,2
(see
[
12
, Appendix B]
), and is independent of
\theta
. The resonances in
\mathcal{R}
of
P
are equivalently defined as the eigenvalues of
P_{\theta}
.

For each fixed small
h>0
, this is an analytic family in the sense of Kato, that is,
P_{\theta}(s)=P_{\theta}(s,h)
satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

P_{\theta}(s)
is closed and the resolvent set of
P_{\theta}(s)
is not empty for each
\left|s\right|<2
.

(2)

For any
\left|s_{0}\right|<2
, there exists an element
\lambda_{0}
of the resolvent set of
P_{\theta}(s_{0})
such that
(P_{\theta}(s)-\lambda_{0})^{-1}
is an analytic operator-valued function of
s
near
s_{0}
.

In fact, for fixed small
h
, we can take
C_{0}>0
large enough such that

(4.17)

\left\|\left(P_{\theta}^{\operatorname{diag}}-(E_{0}-iC_{0}h)\right)^{-1}\right\|_{L^{2}\to H^{2}}\leq(h\|Q\|_{H^{1}\to L^{2}})^{-1},

since we know that the spectrum near
E_{0}
of
P_{\theta}(0)
consists only of the eigenvalues of
P_{1}
on the real line,
the essential spectrum and the resonances of
P_{2}
are far at least of order
h\log(1/h)
.
Then the resolvent of
P_{\theta}(s)
is given by the Neumann series which depends analytically on
s
:

(P_{\theta}(s)-\lambda_{0})^{-1}=\left(I+sh(P_{\theta}^{\operatorname{diag}}-\lambda_{0})^{-1}Q_{\theta}\right)^{-1}(P_{\theta}^{\operatorname{diag}}-\lambda_{0})^{-1},\quad(\lambda_{0}=E_{0}-iC_{0}h).

Our previous arguments for
P(h)
and
P_{\theta}(h)
remain true for

P(s,h):=P^{\operatorname{diag}}+shQ=\begin{pmatrix}P_{1}&shU_{1}\\
shU_{2}&P_{2}\end{pmatrix}\quad(s\in[0,1])

and
P_{\theta}(s,h)

(s\in[0,1])
(we may replace
U_{1},

r_{k}
by
sU_{1}
,
sr_{k}

(k=0,1)
). Note that our considering operator and the reference diagonal operator are
P(h)=P(1,h)
and
P^{\operatorname{diag}}=P(0)
, respectively.
Then we conclude by applying the Kato-Rellich theorem that for each
E=E(h)\in\mathfrak{B}_{h}
, there uniquely exists an analytic function
z_{h}(s,E(h))
of
s
such that
z_{h}(0,E(h))=E(h)+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{2})
is an eigenvalue of
P_{1}
and
z_{h}(s,E(h))
is an eigenvalue of
P_{\theta}(s,h)
, that is, a resonance of
P(s,h)
.

Proof of Proposition
4.10
.

Proposition
4.6
shows that each pseudo-resonance is away of order
h
from the others.
Then the complement in
\mathcal{R}
of the resonance free region (shown by Proposition
4.8
with
C=1
)

\mathcal{R}\setminus\{\operatorname{dist}(z,\operatorname{Res}_{0}(P(h)))<h^{2}\}

is written in the disjoint sum

\{\operatorname{dist}(z,\operatorname{Res}_{0}(P(h)))<h^{2}\}\bigsqcup_{E\in\mathfrak{B}_{h}}\{\left|z-\widetilde{z}_{h}(s,E)\right|<h^{2}\}.

In particular for
s=0
, each eigenvalue
z_{h}(0,E(h))=E(h)+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{2})
of
P_{1}
has to satisfy
\left|z_{h}(0,E(h))-\widetilde{z}_{h}(0,E)\right|<h^{2}
.
We also know that there is no other eigenvalue of the diagonal operator
P_{\theta}(0,h)
in
\mathcal{R}
. Then by the analyticity of
z_{h}(s,E)
, it is the unique resonance with
\left|z_{h}(s,E)-\widetilde{z}_{h}(s,E)\right|<h^{2}
for any

.
∎

4.4.
Precise width of resonances

Proposition
4.10
with Corollary
4.7
immediately implies that each resonance
z_{h}(E)
corresponding to
E\in\mathfrak{B}_{h}
admits the asymptotic formula

(4.18)

z_{h}(E)=E+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{\frac{m_{0}+3}{m_{0}+1}}).

The imaginary part of the subprincipal term of
{\mathcal{O}}(h^{(m_{0}+3)/(m_{0}+1)})
of this formula would give the width of the resonances.
To obtain this subprincipal term, we need to compute the subpricipal term of
{\mathcal{O}}(h^{2/(m_{0}+1)})
of the diagonal entries of the transfer matrices.

However we here employ an alternative method used in
[
10
]
.
It consists in the expression of the width of resonances in terms of the resonant state
w
near infinity. Due to the outgoing property of the resonant state, it is enough to compute the microlocal asymptotics of the resonant state on the outgoing tails of
\Gamma
. We restate this fact in the following lemma.
Let
\rho_{1}=(x_{1},\xi_{1})
[resp.
\rho_{2}=(x_{2},\xi_{2})
] be a point on the outgoing tail tending to
-\infty
[resp.
+\infty
], if it exists, such that
x_{1}<a_{0}<b_{0}<x_{2}
.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
f_{k}
be the microlocal WKB solution defined in Proposition
3.2
near
\rho_{k}
, whose phase is based on
\rho_{k}
, and
\alpha_{k}
defined by

(4.19)

w\equiv\alpha_{k}f_{k}\text{ near }\rho_{k},\quad k=1,2.

Then we have

(4.20)

{\rm Im}\hskip 1.0ptz=-\frac{h(1+{\mathcal{O}}(h))}{\|w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(x_{1},x_{2})}}(|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2}),

with the convention that
\alpha_{k}=0
when
\Gamma_{2}\cap\{x=x_{k}\}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

We have, by integration by parts from
{\rm Im}\hskip 1.0pt\langle(P(h)-z)w,w\rangle_{L^{2}(x_{1},x_{2})}=0
,

(4.21)

({\rm Im}\hskip 1.0ptz)\|w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(x_{1},x_{2})}=h^{2}{\rm Im}\hskip 1.0pt\left[-v_{1}^{\prime}\bar{v}_{1}-v_{2}^{\prime}\bar{v}_{2}+r_{1}v_{2}\bar{v}_{1}\right]^{x_{2}}_{x_{1}},\quad w=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix},

where
[g]_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}=g(x_{2})-g(x_{1})
.

For each
k=1,2
,
w={\mathcal{O}}(h^{\infty})
near
x_{k}
if
\Gamma_{2}\cap\{x=x_{k}\}=\emptyset
.
Otherwise, the line
\{x=x_{k}\}
intersects with
\Gamma_{2}
at two points (since
\Gamma_{2}
is symmetric with respect to
\{\xi=0\}
), where one is on an outgoing trajectory corresponding to
p_{2}
while the other is on an incoming trajectory.
We denote by
\rho_{k}=(x_{k},\xi_{k})
,
k=1,2
, the one on the outgoing trajectory.

Remark that in the subset
\omega_{k}\times\mathbb{R}_{\xi}
of the phase space, where
\omega
is a small neighborhood of
x_{k}
,
w
has its semiclassical wave front set only on the outgoing tail.
This fact together with
the microlocal expression of
w
on the outgoing tail implies that
w
admits the local expression in
\omega_{k}

(4.22)

w=\alpha_{k}\chi^{w}f_{k}+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{\infty})

where
\chi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(T^{*}\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
is identically 1 and supported only near
\rho_{k}
.
Since
v_{1}
is
{\mathcal{O}}(h)
on
\Gamma_{2}
, we have

-v_{1}^{\prime}\bar{v}_{1}+r_{1}v_{2}\bar{v}_{1}\Big|_{x=x_{k}}={\mathcal{O}}(h),\quad v_{2}^{\prime}\bar{v}_{2}\Big|_{x=x_{k}}=(-1)^{k}ih^{-1}\left|\alpha_{k}\right|^{2}(1+{\mathcal{O}}(h)).

Thus we obtain the lemma.
∎

Thanks to this lemma, it is enough to compute the asymptotics of

\|w\|_{L^{2}(x_{1},x_{2})}
and
\alpha_{k}
.

Let
{\bm{e}}_{0}
be the edge on
\Gamma_{1}
such that its endpoint
{\bm{e}}_{0}^{+}
coincides with the starting point of the outgoing tail containing
\rho_{1}
on it. We normalize
w
in such a way that

(4.23)

w\equiv f_{\rho_{0}}\,\,\,\text{ near }\rho_{0},

where
\rho_{0}
is the base point of
{\bm{e}}_{0}
.

The following Proposition asserts that
the microlocal behavior (
4.23
) of
w
on
{\bm{e}}_{0}
uniquely determines that on all other edges, if we “cut” the periodic trajectory
\Gamma_{1}
at the point
\rho_{0}
:
Let
\operatorname{Path}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
be the set of all generalized classical trajectories starting from
\rho_{1}
and ending at
\rho_{2}
without passing through
\rho_{0}
. This is an infinite set in general.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
w
be a resonant state satisfying (
4.23
).
Then at any point
\rho
on
\Gamma\setminus\mathcal{V}
, different from
\rho_{0}
, we have the following microlocal expression of
w
:

(4.24)

w\equiv\alpha_{\rho}f_{\rho}\quad\text{ near }\ \rho\quad\text{with }\ \alpha_{\rho}=\sum_{{\gamma}\in\operatorname{Path}(\rho_{0},\rho)}\mathscr{P}({\gamma}).

The sum on the right hand side of (
4.24
)
absolutely converges for
h
small enough.

Proof.

Put
\widetilde{M}:=(I-E_{{\bm{e}}_{0},{\bm{e}}_{0}})M
where
E_{{\bm{e}}_{0},{\bm{e}}_{0}}
stands for the matrix unit.
Then for
k\geq 1
, each
({\bm{e}},{\bm{e}}^{\prime})
-entry of
\widetilde{M}^{k}
stands for the sum of the probability amplitude associated with generalized classical trajectories from
\rho_{{\bm{e}}^{\prime}}
to
\rho_{{\bm{e}}}
passing through
k
-crossing points without coming back to
\rho_{0}
:

(4.25)

(\widetilde{M}^{k})_{{\bm{e}},{\bm{e}}^{\prime}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\operatorname{Path}(\rho_{{\bm{e}}^{\prime}},\rho_{\bm{e}}),\\
\#\{t;\,\gamma(t)\in\mathcal{V}\}=k\end{subarray}}\mathscr{P}(\gamma).

For
k
large enough, such a generalized classical trajectory always intersects with both
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
since
\rho_{0}\in\Gamma_{1}
. This implies that the absolute value of each eigenvalue of
\widetilde{M}
is smaller than one, and
(I-\widetilde{M})^{-1}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\widetilde{M}^{k}
. Then for any
{\bm{e}}
,

(4.26)

w\equiv\alpha_{\bm{e}}f_{\rho_{\bm{e}}}\quad\text{ near }\ \rho_{\bm{e}}

holds with
\alpha=(\alpha_{\bm{e}})_{{\bm{e}}\in\mathcal{E}}
given by

\alpha:=(I-\widetilde{M})^{-1}\delta_{{\bm{e}}_{0}},\quad\delta_{{\bm{e}}_{0}}=(\delta_{{\bm{e}}_{0}}({\bm{e}}))_{{\bm{e}}\in\mathcal{E}}\quad\text{(Kronecker's delta). }\

According to (
4.25
), we have

\alpha_{\bm{e}}=\sum_{\gamma\in\operatorname{Path}(\rho_{0},\rho_{\bm{e}})}\mathscr{P}(\gamma),

for any
{\bm{e}}\in\mathcal{E}
. This with (
4.26
) shows the required formula (
4.24
) when
\rho
is a point on an edge since the base point can be chosen arbitrarily. Apply (
4.4
) for otherwise.
∎

In the same way as in
[
10
]
, for a resonant state
w
satisfying (
4.23
), we have

(4.27)

\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}(x_{1},x_{2})}^{2}=2{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(E)+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{1/3}+h^{1/(m_{0}+1)}),

where the error
{\mathcal{O}}(h^{1/3})
comes from the turning point while
{\mathcal{O}}(h^{1/(m_{0}+1)})
comes from the crossing points (in
[
10
]
, the later is
{\mathcal{O}}(h)

(m_{0}=1)
and contained in the former).
We finally obtain the formula

(4.28)

{\rm Im}\hskip 1.0ptz_{h}(E)=-\frac{h}{2\left|{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(E_{0})\right|}\sum_{k=1,2}\Bigl|\sum_{\gamma\in\operatorname{Path}(\rho_{0},\rho_{k})}\mathscr{P}(\gamma)\Bigr|^{2}+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{\frac{m_{0}+3}{m_{0}+1}+\kappa}),

where
\kappa=\min\{1/3,1/(m_{0}+1)\}
.
This is of
{\mathcal{O}}(h^{\frac{m_{0}+3}{m_{0}+1}})
since each generalized classical trajectory changes between
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
at least one time.

The principal terms in the sum of the right hand side of (
4.28
) come from the generalized classical trajectories
which change trajectory once from
\Gamma_{1}
to
\Gamma_{2}
.
Therefore we have the following proposition.

Proposition 4.13
.

The coefficient
\mathcal{D}
of the principal term of the resonance width admits the formula

(4.29)

\mathcal{D}(E,h)=\frac{h^{-2/(m_{0}+1)}}{2\left|{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(E_{0})\right|}\sum_{k=1,2}\left|\sum_{\gamma\in\operatorname{Path}^{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{k})}\mathscr{P}(\gamma)\right|^{2},

where
\operatorname{Path}^{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{k})
is the set of
\gamma\in\operatorname{Path}(\rho_{0},\rho_{k})
(see before Lemma
4.12
for the definition) which changes trajectory from
\Gamma_{1}
to
\Gamma_{2}
exactly once.
Here the sum over
k
is for such a
k
that the corresponding outgoing tail exists.

Remark 4.14
.

The constant
\mathcal{D}(E,h)
defined by (
4.29
)
is obviously independent of the choice of
\rho_{0}
,
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
because of the absolute value taken for the sum over the general classical trajectories with the same starting and ending points. This absolute value is of order
h^{1/(m_{0}+1)}
because they change characteristic set once, and hence
\mathcal{D}(E,h)
is bounded as
h\to 0^{+}
.
