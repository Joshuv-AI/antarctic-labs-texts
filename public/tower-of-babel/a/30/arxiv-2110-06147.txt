Abstract
We provide general lower and upper bounds for Laplace Dirichlet heat kernel of convex 𝒞 1 , 1 \mathcal{C}^{1,1} domains. The obtained estimates precisely describe the exponential behaviour of the kernels, which has been known only in a few special cases so far. Furthermore, we characterize a class of sets for which the estimates are sharp, i.e. the upper and lower bounds coincide up to a multiplicative constant. In particular, this includes sets of the form { x ∈ ℝ n : x n > a ​ | ( x 1 , … , x n − 1 ) | p } \{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n}>a|(x_{1},...,x_{n-1})|^{p}\} where p ⩾ 2 p\geqslant 2 , n ⩾ 2 n\geqslant 2 and a > 0 a>0 .

Introduction

Heat kernels are basic objects in mathematical analysis, as fundamental solutions to parabolic differential equation (heat equations), as well as in the theory of stochastic processes, as their transition probability densities.
They are also, or maybe primarily, important from the point of view of Physics, since they describe evolution of particles, temperature and other phenomena. Despite of a very long and rich history of research on heat kernels in various settings, it turns out that there are still many open question even in the the most classical case, i.e. the one involving the Laplace operator
\Delta
(or, equivalently, the Brownian motion) in Euclidean space, which is the subject of this article.

Let
p(t,x,y)=(4\pi t)^{-n/2}e^{-|x-y|^{2}/4t}
be the global Laplace heat kernel in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For a domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we denote by
p_{D}(t,x,y)
the Dirichlet heat kernel of
D
, which is the fundamental solution to the heat equation with the Dirichlet condition at the boundary. From probabilistic point of view,
p_{D}(t,x,y)
is the transition probability density of Brownian motion killed when exiting the set
D
. Since applicable explicit formulae for
p_{D}(t,x,y)
are known only in a few cases (half-lines, intervals and their products), estimates are strongly desired. They have been intensively studied for more than half a century and led to numerous significant results (see, among others,
[
7
,
9
,
11
,
13
,
18
,
29
,
30
,
23
,
25
,
26
]
). In particular, it follows from general theory (
[
9
,
10
]
) that for a bounded domain
D
with boundary smooth enough the heat kernel
p_{D}(t,x,y)
is comparable for large
t
with
\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)e^{-\lambda_{1}t}
,
x,y\in D
,
where
\lambda_{1}
stands for the first eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in
D
,
\delta_{D}(x)
for distance of
x
to the boundary of
D
. For this reason, we will focus on small times (but will be considering unbounded domains as well).

Most of the general heat kernel estimates in the literature share one common weakness: lower and upper bounds are not comparable and their ratio is usually of the form
e^{|x-y|^{2}/ct}
for some constant
c>0
. Such estimates are therefore very imprecise. Let us recall Zhang’s result
[
29
]
, which provides the sharpest known bounds for the heat kernel of any set
D
which is a bounded
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
domain or a complement of a closure of a bounded
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
set.
Namely, there are constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}>0
such that for
x,y\in D
and
t<T
it holds

\displaystyle c_{1}\left(\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\wedge 1\right)\frac{e^{-c_{2}|x-y|^{2}/t}}{t^{n/2}}\leqslant p_{D}(t,x,y)\leqslant c_{3}\left(\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\wedge 1\right)\frac{e^{-c_{4}|x-y|^{2}/t}}{t^{n/2}}.

(1)

One may observe that incomparability of above-given bounds is caused by two different constants in exponents. Estimates with such a property are known as quantitatively sharp estimates. In fact, there are some results with correct exponents, but they completely fail in describing the boundary hehaviour (see e.g.
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
). For instance, the main result of
[
25
]
(combined with
[
18
]
), for simplicity restricted to convex domains, states that

p_{D}(t,x,y)\geqslant c\left(1\wedge\frac{\left(\delta_{D}(x)\wedge\delta_{D}(y)\right)^{2}}{t}\right)\frac{e^{-\lambda t/\left(\delta_{D}(x)\wedge\delta_{D}(y)\right)^{2}}}{\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\left(\delta_{D}(x)\wedge\delta_{D}(y)\right)^{2}\right)^{(n+2)/2}}p(t,x,y),

for some
c>0
, where
\lambda
stands for the first eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in the unit ball. However, the article was focused on asymptotics of the heat kernels with fixed space arguments, where the boundary behaviour plays marginal role. Until recently, precise two-sided estimates for Dirichlet heat kernels have been known only in such basic cases as a half-line and an interval (and their multidimensional extensions) as they are given by simple explicit formulae.
Even the case of such classical set as a ball turned out to require a more subtle approach and has been solved in
[
18
]
. Precisely, for a unit ball
B=B(0,1)
centered at the origin and for every
T>0
there exists a constant
C=C(n,T)>1
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\,h(t,x,y)p(t,x,y)\leqslant p_{B}(t,x,y)\leqslant C\,h(t,x,y)p(t,x,y)

(2)

for every
x,y\in B
and
t<T
, where

\displaystyle h(t,x,y)=\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{B}(x)\delta_{B}(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{B}(x)|x-y|^{2}}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{B}(y)|x-y|^{2}}{t}\right)\/.

(3)

Note that proper description of the exponential behaviour imposed the appearance of a new non-exponential factor
h(t,x,y)
. Above estimates have been complemented with asymptotics in
[
22
]
, which revealed that the behaviour of
p_{B}(t,x,y)
is in fact driven by the expression
\delta\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)/\sqrt{t}
. A similar property will be observed in general lower bound (
5
). We refer the reader to
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
12
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
for some other recent articles focused on sharp estimates of heat kernels in other settings.

The goal of this paper is to derive heat kernel estimates with correct exponential behaviour for general
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
convex domains
D
. The first main result is the following upper bound (see Theorem
3.4
)

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\leqslant Cp(t,x,y)\left[\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}\left(y\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\delta_{H_{y}}\left(x\right)}{t}\right)\right],

(4)

where
x,y\in D
,
0<t<T
,
C=C(D,T)
and
H_{x}
is any half-space such that
D\subset H_{x}
and
\delta_{D}(x)=\delta_{H_{x}}(x)
. Next, we provide lower bounds of the form (see Theorem
4.3
)

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\geqslant Cp(t,x,y)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right)}{t}\right),

(5)

\displaystyle\approx p(t,x,y)\left[\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)\right],

(6)

where
\approx
means that the ratio of both sides is uniformly bounded and bounded away from zero.
The exponential behaviour is indeed treated well in each of the above bounds, as expected. Let us now focus on the non-exponential factors. They are similar, but not identical. The reason is that they strongly depend on the shape of the boundary of
D
(see Example
5.1
). Note that in one dimensional case, where any convex set is just an interval with (at most) two-point boundary, all of the above bounds are equivalent. If there exist elementary sharp estimates in other dimensions, they are presumably more complex and involving more detailed geometrical features of the set
D
. The main advantages of bounds (
4
) – (
6
) are therefore not only the proper exponential behaviour but also a relatively simple form. Furthermore, they follow sharp estimates for a large class of domains. Indeed, we introduce a class
\mathcal{S}_{R}
such that the heat kernel of any
D\in\mathcal{S}_{R}
admits two-sided estimates of the form (
4
). As an example, we show that
\mathcal{S}_{R}
contains sets of the form

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n}>a|(x_{1},...,x_{n-1})|^{p}\}
, where
p\geqslant 2
,
n\geqslant 2
and
a>0
. Such sets are usually difficult to study, as neither they are bounded nor their complements are bounded; see
[
1
,
17
]
for some result concerning the first exit time of such sets and
[
13
]
for quantitatively sharp heat kernel estimates. Then, we fully characterize a class
\mathcal{S}_{Q}\subset\mathcal{S}_{R}
of sets whose heat kernels satisfy two-sided estimates of the forms (
5
) and (
6
). In particular,
\mathcal{S}_{Q}
contains balls (cf. (
2
)). Finally, let us note that all the obtained bounds immediately imply estimates for the first exit time and place density
q_{D}^{x}(t,z)
of Brownian motion from a domain
D
. Precisely, the well known representation
q_{D}^{x}(t,z)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial n_{z}}p_{D}(t,x,z)

and Dirichlet boundary condition give us

q_{D}^{x}(t,z)=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}D\ni y\rightarrow z\\
(y-z)||n_{z}\end{subarray}}\frac{p_{D}(t,x,y)}{\delta_{D}(y)},

where
n_{z}
is is the inward normal direction at
z\in\partial D
, which allows us easily transform estimates of
p_{D}(t,x,y)
into estimates of
q_{D}^{x}(t,z)
.

The assumption of smoothness of the boundary of
D
in the main results is very natural and common in the topic. On the other hand, convexity of the set
D
is necessary to obtain exponential behaviour of the same order as in the global heat kernel
p(t,x,y)
.
Namely,
S. R. S. Varadhan
showed (Corollary 4.7 in
[
27
]
) that

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}t\ln\left(p_{D}(t,x,y)\right)=\frac{1}{4}d_{D}^{2}(x,y),

where
d_{D}(x,y)
is the infimum of lengths of arcs included in
D
and connecting
x
and
y
. If
D
was concave, there would be
x,y\in D
such that
d_{D}(x,y)>|x-y|
and consequently
e^{-d^{2}_{D}(x,y)/4t}<<e^{-|x-y|^{2}/4t}
for
t
small enough. One could naturally try to obtain estimates with the term
-d^{2}_{D}(x,y)/4t
in the exponent, but this seem to be a much more challenging task and rather a material for further research, as there are expected some additional exponential terms related to Buslaev conjecture
[
6
,
15
]
. The first attempts of describing heat kernels behavior at points satisfying convexity property, i.e. such that the whole interval
\overline{xy}
connecting
x
and
y
is contained in
D
, are associated with the
property of not feeling the boundary
, introduced by M. Kac in
[
16
]
, which says that for such points
x
and
y
it holds

\frac{p_{D}(t,x,y)}{p(t,x,y)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\rightarrow 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}1
. In
[
24
]
, the following rate of convergence was derived

\displaystyle p(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\geqslant p_{D}(t,x,y)\geqslant p(t,x,y)\left(1-e^{-\rho^{2}/t}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{2^{k}}{(k-1)!}\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{t}\right)^{k-1}\right),

(7)

where
\rho=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\partial D\\
z\in\overline{xy}\end{subarray}}|w-z|
denotes the distance of the interval
\overline{xy}
to the boundary
of
D
.

A simple
observation is that for
\rho<c\sqrt{t}
the bound (
7
) induces sharp estimates of
p_{D}(t,x,y)
. As mentioned before, all the general results in the literature fail to describe the proper exponential behaviour in the remaining case
\rho<c\sqrt{t}<C
, and consequently provide bounds with a substantial error. For this reason, the results presented in the paper are first of their kind. One way to explain such enhancement is application of mixture of probabilistic and analytical methods, as purely analytical methods usually lead to estimates with different constants in exponents. Such approach has been already successfully adopted in e.g.
[
5
,
18
,
19
]
. Since also many arguments are geometrical, the methods presented in the paper seem to be adoptable in wider generality.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we gather notational details as well as preliminary information about the Brownian motion, including some inequalities related to semi-group property of
p_{D}(t,x,y)
. Sections 3 and 4 are devoted to general lower and upper bounds, respectively, of the examined heat kernels. Section 5 deals with two-sided estimates and contains some supporting examples.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

In this paper we work on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, equipped with the standard inner product
x\cdot y
and corresponding Euclidean metric
|x-y|
,
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By
B_{k}(a,r)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote the
k
-dimensional ball of radius
r>0
and center
a\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. If
k=n
, we simply write
B_{n}(a,r)=B(a,r)
. A set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a domain, if it is open and connected. The
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
sets are sets whose boundary is locally a graph of a
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. It is well known that
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
sets satisfy the inner and outer ball condition, which means that for any point
z
from the boundary
\partial D
of the set
D
there are two balls tangent to
D
at
z
such that one of them is completely included in
D
, and the other one in
D^{c}
. Furthermore, if
D
is bounded, then there exists a radius
r>0
such that for any
z\in\partial D
the condition is satisfied with balls of radius at least
r
. We will denote the class of sets with such property
by
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

For a domain
D
and
x\in D
we denote the distance of
x
to the boundary
\partial D
of
D
by
\delta_{D}(x)
. For a convex domain
D
and
z\in\partial D
from its boundary,
P_{z}
stands for a hyperplane such that
P_{z}\subset D^{c}
and
\{z\}\in P_{z}
. Note that
P_{z}
might be not unique, but if
D
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
domain, then there is only one such hyperplane and it is tangent to
D
at
z
. Any hyperplane
P_{z}
divides the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into two half-spaces. The one including
D
will be denoted by
H_{z}
. In that case we have
\partial H_{z}=P_{z}
. For
x\in D
and
z\in\partial D
realising the distance of
x
to the boundary of
D
, i.e.
\delta_{D}(x)=|x-z|
, we put
P_{x}=P_{z}
and
H_{x}=H_{z}
. Again, there might be more than one such point
z
, but this is irrelevant from our point of view, as all the results presented in the paper are valid for any choice of
z
. For two half-spaces
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
we define the angle
\angle(H_{1},H_{2})
between them as the angle inside
H_{1}\cap H_{2}
between lines
l_{1}\subset\partial H_{1}
and
l_{2}\subset\partial H_{2}
that are perpendicular to
\partial H_{1}\cap\partial H_{2}
. When
\angle(H_{1},H_{2})\leqslant\pi
then the angle is equal to
\pi-\angle(v_{1},v_{2})
, where
v_{1},v_{2}
are normal vectors of
\partial H_{1}
and
\partial H_{2}
, respectively, directed inside the set
H_{1}\cap H_{2}
.

For
x=(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 2
, we denote

\widetilde{x}=(x_{2},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1},\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ \utilde{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

If
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\widetilde{x}
and
\utilde{x}
will be treated as
0
in calculations. Analogously we define
\widetilde{\utilde{x}}
.

To compare two positive functions
f,g
we use notation
f\approx g
, which means that there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>1
, possibly depending on
n
, such that
c_{1}<f/g<c_{2}
for a given range of arguments. If the constants depend on other parameters
p_{1},...,p_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{1},...,p_{k}}}{{\approx}}g
.

2.2
Brownian motion

Let us consider
n
-dimensional Brownian motion
W=(W(t))_{t\geqslant 0}=(W_{1}(t),...,W_{n}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
starting
from
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The global heat kernel
p(t,x,y)
represents its transition probability density. By
\mathbb{P}^{x}
and
\mathbb{E}^{x}
we denote the corresponding probability law and the expected value, respectively.

For a domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we define the first exit time
\tau_{D}
of
W
from
D
by

\tau_{D}:=\inf\{t>0:W(t)\notin D\}.

Then by
W^{D}=(W^{D}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
we denote the Brownian motion killed upon leaving a set
D
, which is a process equal to
W_{t}
before time
\tau_{D}
and at that time it is moved to an additional state called
cemetery
. For sufficiently regular domains
D
(e.g. Lipschitz domains) the transition density function of
W^{D}
is given by the Dirichlet heat kernel
p_{D}(t,x,y)
. The relation between
p_{D}(t,x,y)
and
p(t,x,y)
is described by the Hunt formula

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)=p(t,x,y)-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial D}p(t-s,z,y)q^{x}_{D}(s,z)dsd\sigma(z)\/,

(8)

where
\sigma(z)
is the surface measure on
\partial D
and
q_{x}(t,z)
denotes the density function of the joint distribution
(\tau_{D},W_{\tau_{D}})
for the process
W
starting from
x\in D
. Note that the function
p_{D}(t,x,y)
is symmetric in space arguments and satisfies the Chapman-Kolmogorov identity, known also as the semi-group property, (see Theorem 2.4 in
[
8
]
)

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)=\int_{D}p_{D}(\alpha t,x,z)p_{D}((1-\alpha)t,z,y)\,dz,\ \ \ \ \ x,y\in D,\ t>0,\ \alpha\in(0,1).

(9)

For any half-space
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the Dirichlet heat kernel
p_{H}(t,x,y)
takes especially simple form. Precisely, reflection principle gives us

\displaystyle p_{H}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=p(t,x,y)-p(t,x,\bar{y})

\displaystyle=p(t,x,y)\left(1-e^{-\delta_{H}(x)\delta_{H}(y)}\right),

where
\bar{y}
is a symmetric reflection of
y
with respect to the boundary
\partial H
of
H
. This immediately implies

\displaystyle p_{H}(t,x,y)\approx\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H}(x)\delta_{H}(y)}{t}\right)p(t,x,y).

(10)

Another important property of Dirichlet heat kernels, which follows e.g. from the Hunt formula (
8
), is their monotonicity with respect to inclusion of domains. Namely, if
D_{1}\subset D_{2}
then

\displaystyle p_{D_{1}}(t,x,y)\leqslant p_{D_{2}}(t,x,y),\ \ \ \ x,y\in D_{1}.

(11)

To see this, let us observe that
\tau_{D_{1}}\leqslant\tau_{D_{2}}
and consequently
\{\tau_{D_{1}}>t\}\subset\{\tau_{D_{2}}>t\}
. Hence, for any borel
A\subset D_{1}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{A}

\displaystyle p_{D_{1}}(t,x,y)dy=\mathbb{P}^{x}\left[W_{t}\in A,\tau_{D_{1}}>t\right]\leqslant\mathbb{P}^{x}\left[W_{t}\in A,\tau_{D_{2}}>t\right]=\int_{A}p_{D_{2}}(t,x,y)dy.

2.3
Chapman-Kolmogorov-like inequalities

Below we collect some inequalities related to the Chapman-Kolmogorov identity (semi-group property) for the global heat kernel
p(t,x,y)
, that help us with dealing with analogous identities in case of the Dirichlet heat kernels and provide some intuitions about typical trajectories of Brownian motion. The first proposition may be interpreted by saying that Brownian motion going from
x
to
y
in time
t
is mostly at time
\alpha t
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, passing through a neighbourhood of the point
(1-\alpha)x+\alpha y
of a size comparable to
\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)t}
. In fact, we can move away from
(1-\alpha)x+\alpha y
by a distance comparable to
\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)t}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For every
r,d\geqslant 0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
we have

\displaystyle\int_{B(a,r)}p(\alpha t,x,z)p((1-\alpha)t,z,y)\,dz\geqslant\frac{e^{-\frac{d^{2}}{2\alpha(1-\alpha)t}-1}}{2^{n}\Gamma\left(\frac{n+2}{2}\right)}\left(1\wedge\frac{r^{2}}{\alpha(1-\alpha)t}\right)^{n/2}p(t,x,y),

where
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
t>0
and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
|a-((1-\alpha)x+\alpha y)|=d
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we assume
x=(-\alpha|x-y|,0,...,0)
,
y=((1-\alpha)|x-y|,0,...,0)
. This follows

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}|x-z|^{2}+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}|y-z|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|x-y|^{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}|z|^{2},\ \ \ \ \ \ z\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and consequently

\displaystyle p(\alpha t,x,z)p((1-\alpha)t,z,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{4t}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4\alpha(1-\alpha)t}\right)}{(4\pi t)^{n/2}(4\alpha(1-\alpha)\pi t)^{n/2}}

\displaystyle=p(t,x,y)\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4\alpha(1-\alpha)t}\right)}{(4\alpha(1-\alpha)\pi t)^{n/2}}\/.

(12)

Furthermore, the special form of
x
and
y
gives us
(1-\alpha)x+\alpha y=0
, which implies

|z|^{2}\leqslant\left(|z-a|+|a|\right)^{2}\leqslant 2\left(|z-a|^{2}+|a|^{2}\right)=2\left(|z-a|^{2}+d^{2}\right).

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B(a,r)}p(\alpha t,x,z)p((1-\alpha)t,z,y)dz

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{p(t,x,y)}{(4\alpha(1-\alpha)\pi t)^{n/2}}\int_{B(a,r)}\exp\left(-\frac{|z-a|^{2}+d^{2}}{2\alpha(1-\alpha)t}\right)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{p(t,x,y)}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}e^{-\frac{d^{2}}{2\alpha(1-\alpha)t}}\int_{B(0,r/\sqrt{2\alpha(1-\alpha)t})}\exp\left(-{|z|^{2}}\right)dz

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{p(t,x,y)}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}e^{-\frac{d^{2}}{2\alpha(1-\alpha)t}}e^{-1^{2}}\left|B\left(0,1\wedge\left(r/\sqrt{2\alpha(1-\alpha)t}\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{p(t,x,y)}{2^{n/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{n+2}{2}\right)}e^{-\frac{d^{2}}{2\alpha(1-\alpha)t}-1}\left(1\wedge\frac{r}{\sqrt{2\alpha(1-\alpha)t}}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{p(t,x,y)}{2^{n}\Gamma\left(\frac{n+2}{2}\right)}e^{-\frac{d^{2}}{2\alpha(1-\alpha)t}-1}\left(1\wedge\frac{r^{2}}{\alpha(1-\alpha)t}\right)^{n/2},

as required
∎

The next proposition deals with an integral over the intersection of two half-spaces, where integrands are somewhat related to the Dirichlet heat kernels of the half-spaces (cf. (
10
)).

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\geqslant 0
and
H_{1},H_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be two half-spaces. For
D=H_{1}\cap H_{2}
it holds

\displaystyle\int_{D}p(t/2,x,z)p(t/2,z,y)\left(\delta_{H_{1}}(z)\right)^{\alpha}\left(\delta_{H_{2}}(z)\right)^{\beta}dz

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha,\beta}}{{\lesssim}}\,p(t,x,y)\left(\sqrt{t}+{\delta_{H_{1}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\sqrt{t}+{\delta_{H_{2}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}\right)^{\beta}.

Proof.

Similarly as in the proof of Proposition
2.1
, we assume
x=(-\frac{1}{2}|x-y|,0,...,0)
,
y=(\frac{1}{2}|x-y|,0,...,0)
. Then we have
B((x+y)/2,r)=B(0,r)
and

\delta_{H_{1}}(z)\leqslant|z|+\delta_{H_{1}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right),\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \delta_{H_{2}}(z)\leqslant|z|+\delta_{H_{2}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right),

as well as

\displaystyle p(t/2,x,z)p(t/2,z,y)=p(t,x,y)\frac{e^{-|z|^{2}/t}}{(\pi t)^{n/2}}\/.

This follows

\displaystyle\int_{D}p(t/2,x,z)p(t/2,z,y)\left(\delta_{H_{1}}(z)\right)^{\alpha}\left(\delta_{H_{2}}(z)\right)^{\beta}dz

\displaystyle\leqslant p(t,x,y)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{e^{-|z|^{2}/t}}{(\pi t)^{n/2}}\left(|z|+\delta_{H_{1}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\right)^{\alpha}\left(|z|+\delta_{H_{2}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\right)^{\beta}dz

\displaystyle=p(t,x,y)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{e^{-|z|^{2}}}{\pi^{n/2}}\left(|z|\sqrt{t}+\delta_{H_{1}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\right)^{\alpha}\left(|z|\sqrt{t}+\delta_{H_{2}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\right)^{\beta}dz

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha,\beta}}{{\lesssim}}p(t,x,y)\left(\sqrt{t}+{\delta_{H_{1}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\sqrt{t}+{\delta_{H_{2}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}\right)^{\beta},

where the last estimate may be justified by the inequalities

\frac{1}{2}\left(a^{\gamma}+b^{\gamma}\right)\leqslant(a+b)^{\gamma}\leqslant 2^{\gamma}\left(a^{\gamma}+b^{\gamma}\right),

for
a,b,\gamma>0
.
∎

3
Upper bounds

Our general idea of finding upper bounds is to circumscribe sets of the form
H_{1}\cap H_{2}
, where
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are two half-spaces, on the domain
D
and to use the monotonicity property (
11
). Due to independence of coordinates of Brownian motion, the heat kernel of
H_{1}\cap H_{2}
is just a product of a heat kernel of a two-dimensional cone and the
n-2
-dimensional global heat kernel. However, in the literature there are neither estimates of heat kernels in cones that describe properly the exponential behaviour nor ones that are uniform with respect to the angle between the half-spaces (see
[
2
]
for some formulae and properties of Brownian motion in cones). For this reason we provide some upper bounds that are sufficient for applications to estimation of heat kernels in
C^{1,1}
domains.
We deal separately with cases when the angle between the half-spaces is
obtuse or acute.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be two half-spaces such that
\angle(H_{1},H_{2})\geqslant\pi/2
and denote
D=H_{1}\cap H_{2}
. Then there is an absolute constant
C>0
such that for
x,y\in D
and
t>0
it holds

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)\leqslant C

\displaystyle\,p(t,x,y)\times

\displaystyle\left[\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{1}}(x)\delta_{H_{1}}(y)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{2}}(x)\delta_{H_{2}}(y)}{t}\right)\right].

(13)

Proof.

Without loss of the generality we assume
\delta_{H_{1}}(x)\leqslant\delta_{H_{2}}(x)
. Since
\delta_{D}(x)=\delta_{H_{1}}(x)\wedge\delta_{H_{2}}(x)
, we have
\delta_{D}(x)=\delta_{H_{1}}(x)
.

If
\delta_{H_{1}}(y)\leqslant 2\delta_{H_{2}}(y)
, then
\delta_{D}(y)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\delta_{H_{1}}(y)
, and consequently

\delta_{H_{1}}(x)\delta_{H_{1}}(y)\leqslant 2\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y).

Hence, the inequality

p_{D}(t,x,y)\leqslant p_{H_{1}}(t,x,y)
together with the estimate (
10
) finish the proof in this case.

Consider now
\delta_{H_{1}}(y)>2\delta_{H_{2}}(y)
and let
\bar{x}
,
\bar{y}
be reflections of
x
,
y
with respect to hyperplanes
\partial H_{1}
,
\partial H_{2}
, respectively. Since
|y-\bar{y}|=2\delta_{H_{2}}(y)
, we have
\bar{y}\in H_{1}
. We may therefore repeat the argumentation of the formula (3.2) in
[
18
]
and get

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\leqslant p_{H_{1}\cap H_{2}}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\leqslant p(t,x,y)-p(t,\bar{x},y)-p(t,x,\bar{y})+p(t,\bar{x},\bar{y}).

Note that for
\angle(H_{1},H_{2})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\pi
the last inequality is expected to be opposite. Next, the right-hand side may be rewritten as

\displaystyle p(t,x,y)\left(1-\frac{p(t,\bar{x},y)}{p(t,x,y)}\right)\left(1-\frac{p(t,x,\bar{y})}{p(t,x,y)}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ +p(t,\bar{x},\bar{y})-\frac{p(t,\bar{x},y)p(t,x,\bar{y})}{p(t,x,y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{H_{1}}(t,x,y)p_{H_{2}}(t,x,y)}{p(t,x,y)}+\left(p(t,\bar{x},\bar{y})-\frac{p(t,\bar{x},y)p(t,x,\bar{y})}{p(t,x,y)}\right).

By (
10
), we clearly have

\frac{p_{H_{1}}(t,x,y)p_{H_{2}}(t,x,y)}{p(t,x,y)}\approx p(t,x,y)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{1}}(x)\delta_{H_{1}}(y)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{2}}(x)\delta_{H_{2}}(y)}{t}\right).

In order to deal with the other component, let us observe

\displaystyle|\bar{x}-y|^{2}

\displaystyle=|(x-y)+(\bar{x}-x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=|x-y|^{2}+\left(\bar{x}-x\right)\cdot\big((\bar{x}-x)+2(x-y)\big),

\displaystyle|\bar{x}-\bar{y}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|(x-y)+(\bar{x}-x)-(\bar{y}-y)|^{2}

\displaystyle=|x-y|^{2}-2\left(\bar{x}-x\right)\cdot\left(\bar{y}-y\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\left(\bar{x}-x\right)\cdot\big((\bar{x}-x)+2(x-y)\big)+\left(\bar{y}-y\right)\cdot\big((\bar{y}-y)+2(y-x)\big),

which follows

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle(t,\bar{x},\bar{y})-\frac{p(t,\bar{x},y)p(t,x,\bar{y})}{p(t,x,y)}=p(t,\bar{x},\bar{y})\left(1-e^{-(\bar{x}-x)\cdot(\bar{y}-y)/2t}\right).

Since, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

(\bar{x}-x)\cdot(\bar{y}-y)\leqslant|\bar{x}-x||\bar{y}-y|=4\delta_{H_{1}}(x)\delta_{H_{2}}(y)=4\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y),

we finally obtain

\displaystyle p(t,\bar{x},\bar{y})\left(1-e^{-(\bar{x}-x)(\bar{y}-y)/t}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant p(t,x,y)\left(1-e^{-2\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)/t}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim p(t,x,y)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\right),

as required. The proof is complete.
∎

In all the subsequent results
H_{x},H_{y}
,
x,y\in D
stand for two half-spaces such that
D\subset H_{x}\cap H_{y}
and
\delta_{H_{x}}(x)=\delta_{D}(x)
,
\delta_{H_{y}}(y)=\delta_{D}(y)
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be any convex domain. There is an absolute constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\leqslant Cp(t,x,y)\left[\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta(x)\delta(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}(y)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(x)\delta_{H_{y}}(y)}{t}\right)\right]

holds whenever
\angle(H_{x},H_{y})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\pi
.

Let us pass to the latter main result of this section.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}_{r}
,
r>0
, domain. For
0<t<T
we have

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1+\frac{T}{r^{2}}\right)p(t,x,y)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}(y)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(x)\delta_{H_{y}}(y)}{t}\right).

(14)

Proof.

Let
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\partial D
be tangent points of
D
to
H_{x}
and
H_{y}
, respectively, i.e. such that
\delta_{D}(x)=\delta_{H_{x}}(x)=|x-x^{\prime}|
and
\delta_{D}(y)=\delta_{H_{y}}(y)=|y-y^{\prime}|
. Since
D
is
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{1,1}
, there are balls of radius
r
inside
D
which are tangent at
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
to
D
(and consequently to
H_{x}
and
H_{y}
, respectively). If we combine this with the assumption
\angle(H_{x},H_{y})<\pi/2
, simple geometry shows that

\displaystyle\delta_{H_{x}}(y),\delta_{H_{y}}(x)\geqslant r.

(15)

Due to symmetry of
p_{D}(t,x,y)
, we may assume
\delta_{D}(x)\leqslant\delta_{D}(y)
.
If
\delta_{D}(y)=\delta_{H_{y}}(y)>r
, then it holds
\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(x)\delta_{H_{y}}(y)}{t}\right)\geqslant\left(1\wedge\frac{r^{2}}{T}\right)
, and consequently, by (
10
) and (
11
),

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\leqslant p_{H_{x}}(t,x,y)\lesssim p(t,x,y)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}(y)}{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(1+\frac{T}{r^{2}}\right)p(t,x,y)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}(y)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(x)\delta_{H_{y}}(y)}{t}\right),

as required.

Consider now
\delta_{D}(y)\leqslant r
. By the assumption
\delta_{D}(x)\leqslant\delta_{D}(y)
we also have
\delta_{D}(x)\leqslant r
. Combining this with (
15
) we get
\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\leqslant\delta_{H_{x}}(y)
and
\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\leqslant\delta_{H_{y}}(x)
. Thus, equality
\delta_{H_{x}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta_{H_{x}}(x)+\delta_{H_{x}}(y)\right)
gives us

\displaystyle\frac{r}{2}\leqslant\delta_{H_{x}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\leqslant\delta_{H_{x}}(y),\ \ \ \ \ \frac{r}{2}\leqslant\delta_{H_{y}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\leqslant\delta_{H_{y}}(x).

(16)

Next, using Chapman-Kolmogorov identity and estimating
p_{D}(t/2,x,z)\leqslant p_{H_{x}}(t/2,x,z)
,
p_{D}(t/2,z,y)\leqslant p_{H_{y}}(t/2,z,y)
we obtain for every
i,j\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{D}\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}\left(z\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\delta_{H_{y}}\left(z\right)}{t}\right)p(t/2,x,z)p(t/2,x,y)dz\lesssim I_{i,j},

where

\displaystyle I_{i,j}:=\int_{H_{x}\cap H_{y}}\left(\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}\left(z\right)}{t}\right)^{i}\left(\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\delta_{H_{y}}\left(z\right)}{t}\right)^{j}p(t/2,x,z)p(t/2,x,y)dz.

Applying Proposition
2.2
and the formula (
16
) we get

\displaystyle I_{i,j}

\displaystyle\lesssim p(t,x,y)\left(\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\left(\sqrt{T}+\delta_{H_{x}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\right)}{t}\right)^{i}\left(\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\left(\sqrt{T}+\delta_{H_{x}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\right)}{t}\right)^{j}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1+\frac{T}{r^{2}}\right)p(t,x,y)\left(\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}\left(y\right)}{t}\right)^{i}\left(\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\delta_{H_{y}}\left(x\right)}{t}\right)^{j},

which follows

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1+\frac{T}{r^{2}}\right)p(t,x,y)\min_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}\left\{\left(\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}\left(y\right)}{t}\right)^{i}\left(\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\delta_{H_{y}}\left(x\right)}{t}\right)^{j}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{T}{r^{2}}\right)p(t,x,y)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}\left(y\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\delta_{H_{y}}\left(x\right)}{t}\right).

This ends the proof.

∎

Theorem
3.4
follows now directly from Corollary
3.2
and Theorem
3.3
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a

,
r>0
, domain. There is a constant

such that

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\leqslant Cp(t,x,y)\left[\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta(x)\delta(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}\left(y\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\delta_{H_{y}}\left(x\right)}{t}\right)\right],

where
x,y\in D
,

.

Using the inequality
\delta_{H_{x}}(y)\leqslant\left(\delta_{H_{x}}\left(x\right)+\delta_{H_{x}}(y)\right)\leqslant 2\delta_{H_{x}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)

Let

.

4
Lower bounds

We start this section with a lower bound for heat kernels of a very specific set, which is so called half-capsule. Namely, for
L\geqslant R>0
we define

J_{R,L}=B_{n}(0,R)\cup\left((0,L)\times B_{n-1}(0,R)\right).

(17)

Roughly speaking,
J_{R,L}
is a cylinder of radius
R
and height
L
with a hemisphere of radius
R
attached to one on the bases of the cylinder.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
L>3\sqrt{t}
and
x=(L-\sqrt{t},0,0,...,0)
. There is a constant
C
depending only on
n
such that for
0<s\leqslant t
and
y\in J_{\sqrt{t},L}
such that
y_{1}\leqslant 0
we have

\displaystyle p_{J_{\sqrt{t},L}}(s,x,y)\geqslant C\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{j_{\sqrt{t},L}}(y)\sqrt{t}}{s}\right)p(s,x,y).

(18)

Proof.

The scaling property ofBrownian motion gives us

p_{J_{\sqrt{t},L}}(s,x,y)=p_{J_{1,L/\sqrt{t}}}\left(\frac{s}{t},\frac{x}{\sqrt{t}},\frac{y}{\sqrt{t}}\right),

which, together with the equality
\delta_{J_{\sqrt{t},L}}(y)=\sqrt{t}\delta_{J_{1,L/\sqrt{t}}}\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{t}}\right)
, allows us to consider only the case
t=1
. Without loss of generality we also assume
y=(y_{1},y_{2},0,...,0)
with
y_{1}\leqslant 0
and
y_{2}\in[0,1)
, which is justified by the rotational invariance of the set
J_{1,L}
with respect to the
Ox_{1}
axis.

Recall the notation

\mathbb{R}^{n}\ni x\longrightarrow\widetilde{x}=(x_{2},x_{3},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}

and let

\mathbb{T}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left(|\widetilde{y}|-\tfrac{3}{4}\right)^{2}+y_{1}^{2}<\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\right)^{2}\right\}\subset J_{1,L}

be a torus tangent to
J_{1,L}
at
\{0\}\times\partial B_{n-1}(0,1)
, and let
\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}:=\mathbb{T}\cup\left((-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})\times B_{n-1}(0,\frac{3}{4})\right)
be the set of all convex combinations of points from
\mathbb{T}
. Alternatively, we may define
\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}
as

\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}=\bigcup_{z\in\{0\}\times B_{n-1}(0,\frac{3}{4})}B_{n}\left(z,\tfrac{1}{4}\right),

which may better depict properties of this set.

The main step of the proof is to show that the assertion holds for
y\in\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}\cap\{y_{1}<0\}
. For
y\in\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}\backslash{\mathbb{T}}\subset(-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})\times B_{n-1}(0,\frac{3}{4})
it holds
\delta_{J_{1,L}}(y)>\sqrt{5/8}
. Combining this with
\delta_{J_{1,L}}(x)=1
and convexity of
J_{1,L}
, one may conclude (
18
) from (
7
).

For
y\in\mathbb{T}\cap\{y_{1}<0\}
the argument is more complicated.
Let
\tau_{1}
be the first time of hitting the hyperplane
\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:z_{1}=0\}
by the Brownian motion
W
starting from
x
, i.e.

\tau_{1}:=\inf\{u>0:W_{1}(u)=0\}.

Then, for any Borel set
A\subset\{z\in J_{1,L}:z_{1}\leqslant 0\}
the inclusion
\{W(s)\in A\}\subset\{s\geqslant\tau_{1}\}
,
s>0
, and Strong Markov property give us

\displaystyle\int_{A}p_{J_{1,L}}(s,x,z)dz

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{x}\left(W(s)\in A,s<\tau_{J_{1,L}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{x}\left(W(s)\in A,s<\tau_{J_{1,L}};s\geqslant\tau_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[s<\tau_{J_{1,L}};s\geqslant\tau_{1};\mathbb{E}^{W(u)}\left[s-u<\tau_{J_{1,L}};W(s-u)\in A\right]_{u=\tau_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[s<\tau_{J_{1,L}};s\geqslant\tau_{1};\int_{A}p_{J_{1,L}}(s-\tau_{1},W(\tau_{1}),z)dz\right],

(19)

which implies

\displaystyle p_{J_{1,L}}(s,x,y)=\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[s<\tau_{J_{1,L}};s\geqslant\tau_{1};p_{J_{1,L}}(s-\tau_{1},W(\tau_{1}),z)\right],

(20)

where
y\in\{z\in J_{1,L}:z_{1}\leqslant 0\}
. Let us now introduce another few sets. First, we denote

\displaystyle B_{0}

\displaystyle=B((0,\tfrac{1}{4},0,...,0),\tfrac{3}{4}),

\displaystyle I_{0}

\displaystyle=B_{n-1}((\tfrac{1}{4},0,...,0),\tfrac{3}{4}).

The relation between
B_{0}
and
I_{0}
is that
B_{0}\cap\{x_{1}=0\}=\{0\}\times I_{0}
. Furthermore, we define

\displaystyle R^{(1)}_{L}

\displaystyle:=(-\infty,L)\times B_{n-1}(0,1),

\displaystyle R^{(2)}_{L}

\displaystyle:=(-\infty,L)\times I_{0},

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{2}>1\}.

The set
R^{(1)}_{L}
is an extension of
J_{1,L}
into a half-infinite cylinder,
R^{(2)}_{L}
is another half-infinite cylinder which is contained in
R^{(1)}_{L}
and
H
is a half-space tangent to both of the cylinders at
(-\infty,L)\times\{(1,0,...,0)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The crucial properties of these sets are

\displaystyle\delta_{R^{(1)}_{L}}(x)\approx\delta_{R^{(2)}_{L}}(x)\approx\delta_{H}(x)\approx 1,

(21)

and

\displaystyle\delta_{B(0,1)}(z)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\delta_{R^{(1)}_{L}}(z)\approx\delta_{H}(z),\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ for }z\in B_{0}.

(22)

Bounds in (
21
) are clear while bounds in (
22
) follow from the below-given calculations:

\displaystyle\delta_{H}(z)

\displaystyle\geqslant\delta_{R^{(1)}_{L}}(z)\geqslant\delta_{B(0,1)}(y)=1-|z|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1-|z|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1-z_{2}\right)-\left(z_{1}^{2}+\left(z_{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}\right)^{2}+...+z_{n}^{2}-\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}(1-z_{2})+\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)^{2}-\left|z-\left(0,\tfrac{1}{4},0,...,0\right)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle>\tfrac{1}{4}(1-z_{2})=\tfrac{1}{4}\delta_{H}(z).

(23)

Our goal in this part of the proof is to show that
p_{J_{1,L}}(s,x,y)\gtrsim p_{R^{(2)}_{L}}(s,x,y)
holds for
y\in\mathbb{T}\cap\{y_{1}\leqslant 0\}
(keeping in mind the special form of
y=(y_{1},y_{2},0,...,0)
) and
0<s<1
. Since
J_{1,L}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{1}>0\}=R^{(1)}_{L}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{1}>0\}
, then
\{s<\tau_{J_{1,L}};s\geqslant\tau_{1}\}=\{s<\tau_{R^{(1)}_{L}};s\geqslant\tau_{1}\}
so we may change the condition
s<\tau_{J_{1,L}}
into
s<\tau_{R^{(1)}_{L}}
in (
20
). Furthermore, adding also the condition
W(\tau_{1})\in\{0\}\times I_{0}
under the expectation we arrive at

\displaystyle p_{J_{1,L}}(s,x,y)\geqslant\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[s<\tau_{R^{(1)}_{L}};s\geqslant\tau_{1};W(\tau_{1})\in\{0\}\times I_{0};p_{J_{1,L}}(s-\tau_{1},W(\tau_{1}),y)\right].

(24)

Observe now that the assumption
y=(y_{1},y_{2},0,...,0)\in{\mathbb{T}}
and the condition
W(\tau_{1})\in\{0\}\times I_{0}
imply
y,W(\tau_{1})\in B_{0}
.
Consequently, by (
11
), (
2
), (
22
) and (
10
) we get for
0<s\leqslant 1

\displaystyle p_{J_{1,L}}(s,W(\tau_{1}),y)

\displaystyle\geqslant p_{B(0,1)}(s,W(\tau_{1}),y)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H}(W(\tau_{1}))\delta_{H}(y)}{s}\right)p(s,W(\tau_{1}),y)

\displaystyle\approx p_{H}(s,W(\tau_{1}),y)\geqslant p_{R_{L}^{(1)}}(s,W(\tau_{1}),y).

Applying this to (
24
), we obtain

\displaystyle p_{J_{1,L}}(s,x,y)\gtrsim\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[s<\tau_{R^{(1)}_{L}};s\geqslant\tau_{1};W(\tau_{1})\in\{0\}\times I_{0};p_{R_{L}^{(1)}}(s-\tau_{1},W(\tau_{1}),y)\right].

The inclusion
R^{(2)}_{L}\subset R^{(1)}_{L}
imples
\{s<\tau_{R^{(2)}_{L}}\}\subset\{s<\tau_{R^{(1)}_{L}}\}
and
p_{R^{(1)}_{L}}(s-\tau_{1},W(\tau_{1}),y)\geqslant p_{R^{(2)}_{L}}(s-\tau_{1},W(\tau_{1}),y)
. Hence

\displaystyle p_{J_{1,L}}(s,x,y)\gtrsim\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[s<\tau_{R^{(2)}_{L}};s\geqslant\tau_{1};W(\tau_{1})\in\{0\}\times I_{0};p_{R^{(2)}_{L}}(s-\tau_{1},W(\tau_{1}),y)\right].

Furthermore, the condition
W(\tau_{1})\in\{0\}\times I_{0}
is always satisfied on the set
\{s<\tau_{R^{(2)}_{L}}\}
, so it may be removed. Thus, repeating argument from (
19
), we conclude

\displaystyle p_{J_{1,L}}(s,x,y)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[s<\tau_{R^{(2)}_{L}};s\geqslant\tau_{1};p_{R^{(2)}_{L}}(s-\tau_{1},W(\tau_{1}),y)\right]

\displaystyle=p_{R^{(2)}_{L}}(s,x,y),

as required. Next we will show that
p_{R^{(2)}_{L}}(s,x,y)
admits lower estimate from (
18
).
Cylindrical form of
R^{(2)}_{L}
combined with (
10
) and (
2
) give us

\displaystyle p_{R^{(2)}_{L}}(s,x,y)

\displaystyle=p_{(-\infty,L)}(s,x_{1},y_{1})p_{I_{0}}(s,\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left(1\wedge\frac{(L-x_{1})(L-y_{1})}{s}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{I_{0}}(\widetilde{x})\delta_{I_{0}}(\widetilde{y})}{s}\right)p(s,x,y)

\displaystyle=\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{I_{0}}(\widetilde{y})}{s}\right)p(s,x,y).

Since
y=(y_{1},y_{2},0,...,0)\in\mathbb{T}
, we have
y\in B_{n}((0,\frac{3}{4},0,...,0),\frac{1}{4})
, and, analogously as in (
23
) (or by rescaling), one can show that
\delta_{I_{0}}(\widetilde{y})\approx\delta_{J_{1,L}}(y)
, which completes the proof for
y\in\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}
.

Finally, let us consider any
y\in J_{1,L}
such that
y_{1}\leqslant 0
, as in the assertion. Denote by
m=(m_{1},...,m_{n})
the point on the interval
\overline{xy}
such that
m_{1}=0
. Due to the special form of
x
and
y
we have
m=(0,m_{2},0,...,0)
with
m_{2}\in[0,1)
. Next, we put
\alpha_{xy}=\frac{|m-y|}{|x-y|}
and denote

m^{\prime}=m-\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s}(0,1,0,...,0)=(0,m_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s},0,...,0).

Since
\alpha_{xy}<\frac{1}{L-1}<\frac{1}{2}
, we have
B(m^{\prime},\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s})\subset\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}
, and for all
z\in B(m^{\prime},\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s})
it holds

\displaystyle\delta_{J_{1,L}}(z)

\displaystyle\geqslant\delta_{J_{1,L}}(m^{\prime})-\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-(m^{\prime}_{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s})\right)\wedge\left((m^{\prime}_{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s})-(-1)\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-m_{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s}\right)\wedge\left(1-\tfrac{3}{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(\delta_{J_{1,L}}(m)+\tfrac{1}{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s}\right)\wedge\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{8}\left(\delta_{J_{1,L}}(m)+\tfrac{1}{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s}\right).

Furthermore, we clearly have
\delta_{J_{1,L}}(m)\geqslant\delta_{J_{1,L}}(y)
and, by intercept theorem,
\delta_{J_{1,L}}(m)\geqslant\alpha_{xy}\delta_{J_{1,L}}(x)=\alpha_{xy}
, which eventually gives us

\delta_{J_{1,L}}(z)\gtrsim w(x,y):=\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s}+\delta_{J_{1,L}}(y)+\alpha_{xy},\ \ \ \ \ z\in B(m^{\prime},\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s}).

Consequently, from previous case we have for
z\in\mathbb{\widetilde{T}}\cap\{z_{1}<0\}

\displaystyle p_{J_{1,L}}((1-\alpha_{xy})s,x,z)

\displaystyle\gtrsim p(t,x,z)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{J_{1,L}}(z)}{(1-\alpha_{xy})s}\right)\gtrsim p(t,x,z)\left(1\wedge\frac{w(x,y)}{s}\right).

Additionally, for the same range of
z
, (
2
) implies

\displaystyle p_{J_{1,L}}(\alpha_{xy}s,z,y)

\displaystyle\geqslant p_{B(0,1)}(\alpha_{xy}s,z,y)\gtrsim p(\alpha_{xy}s,z,y)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{J_{1,L}}(y)w(x,y)}{\alpha_{xy}s}\right).

Then, by Chapman-Kolmogorov identity and Corollary
2.1
, we get

\displaystyle p_{J_{1,L}}(s,x,y)

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{B(m^{\prime},\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s})\cap\{z_{1}<0\}}p_{J_{1,L}}((1-\alpha_{xy})s,x,z)p_{J_{1,L}}(\alpha_{xy}s,z,y)dz

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1\wedge\frac{w(x,y)}{s}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{J_{1,L}}(y)w(x,y)}{\alpha_{xy}s}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \int_{B(m^{\prime\prime},\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s})}p((1-\alpha_{xy})s,x,z)p(\alpha_{xy}s,z,y)dz

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left(1\wedge\frac{w(x,y)}{s}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{J_{1,L}}(y)w(x,y)}{\alpha_{xy}s}\right)p(s,x,y),

where

m^{\prime\prime}=m^{\prime}-\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s}(1,0,...,0)=(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s},m_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s},0,...,0).

In order to finish the proof, we need to show that the above product of two minima is greater (up to a constant factor) than
\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{J_{1,L}(y)}}{s}\right)
. If the right-hand side minimum is equal to
1
, then it is enough to use the bound
w(x,y)>\delta_{J_{1,L}}(y)
. If both of them are smaller than
1
, one can use
w(x,y)>\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}s}
. Finally, if only the right-hand side one is smaller than
1
, then we need to employ the bound
w(x,y)>\alpha_{xy}
. The proof is complete.
∎

The next theorem provides a general lower bound of the form as in (
1
) but with proper exponential behavior. Nevertheless, both results concern different classes of sets (with non-empty intersection).

Theorem 4.2
.

For any convex set
D\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}_{r}
and
T>0
there is
C=C(n,r,T)
such that

p_{D}(t,x,y)\geqslant C\,\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\right)p(t,x,y).

Proof.

Assume
t<1
. First, consider the case
\delta_{D}(x)>10\sqrt{t}
. Due to the bound (
7
), we may additionally assume
\delta_{D}(y)\leqslant\sqrt{t}
. Put
\alpha_{xy}=5\sqrt{t}/\delta_{D}(x)<1/2
and
m=\alpha_{xy}x+(1-\alpha_{xy})y
. It follows from intercept theorem that
\delta_{D}(m)>5\sqrt{t}
. Thus, for every
z\in B(m,\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}t})
we have
\delta_{D}(z)\geqslant 4\sqrt{t}
and
|z-y|\geqslant\delta_{D}(z)-\delta_{D}(y)\geqslant 3\sqrt{t}
, which lets us employ Lemma
4.1
. Precisely, for a given
z
one can transform isometrically the set
J_{\sqrt{t},L_{z}}
into
J^{\prime}_{\sqrt{t},L_{z}}
, for suitably chosen
L=L(z)>3\sqrt{t}
, such that
\delta_{D}(y)=\delta_{J^{\prime}_{\sqrt{t},L_{z}}}(y)
and the point
(L_{z}-\sqrt{t},0,0,...,0)
is transformed into
z
. Then, Lemma
4.1
gives us for
z\in B(m,\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}t})

\displaystyle p_{D}(\alpha_{xy}t,z,y)

\displaystyle\geqslant p_{J^{\prime}_{\sqrt{t},L_{z}}}(\alpha_{xy}t,z,y)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\sqrt{t}}{\alpha_{xy}t}\right)p(\alpha_{xy}t,z,y)

\displaystyle=\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\delta_{D}(x)}{6t}\right)p(\alpha_{xy}t,z,y),

while, by (
7
), we have

p_{D}((1-\alpha_{xy})t,x,z)\gtrsim p((1-\alpha_{xy})t,x,z).

Consequently, by Chapman-Kolmogorov identity and Corollary
2.1
we get

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{B(m,\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}t})}p_{D}((1-\alpha_{xy})t,x,z)p_{D}(\alpha_{xy}t,z,y)dz

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\delta_{D}(x)}{t}\right)\int_{B(m,\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}t})}p((1-\alpha_{xy})t,x,z)p(\alpha_{xy}t,z,y)dz

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\delta_{D}(x)}{t}\right)p(t,x,y).

Consider now
\delta_{D}(x),\delta_{D}(y)\leqslant 10\sqrt{t}
. Let
p_{xy}
be a point such that
|p_{xy}-\frac{x+y}{2}|=11\sqrt{t/2}
and
\delta_{D}(p_{xy})\geqslant 11\sqrt{t/2}
. Such point exists for sufficiently small
T
. Then, for
z\in B(p_{xy},\sqrt{t/2})
, we have
\delta_{D}(z)\geqslant 10\sqrt{t/2}
and, from the previous case, assertion is true for
p_{D}(t/2,x,z)
and
p_{D}(t/2,z,y)
.
Thus, by Corollary
2.1
we get

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{B(p_{xy},\sqrt{t/2})}p_{D}(t/2,x,z)p_{D}(t/2,z,y)dz

\displaystyle\gtrsim\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\sqrt{t}}{t}\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\sqrt{t}}{t}\int_{B(p_{xy},\sqrt{t/2})}p(t/2,x,z)p(t/2,z,y)dz

\displaystyle\approx\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\delta_{D}(x)}{t}\right)p(t,x,y),

where we used
\delta_{D}(x)\approx\delta_{D}(y)\approx\sqrt{t}
. Finally, let us observe that the range of
T
may be easily extended, but the cost we pay is decrease of the constant
C
. Indeed, for
T\leqslant t<2T
and
x,y\in D
there is a point
q_{xy}\in D
such that
\left|q_{xt}-\frac{x+y}{2}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{t}\frac{r}{2\sqrt{T}}
and
\delta_{D}(q_{xy})\geqslant\sqrt{t}\frac{r}{2\sqrt{T}}
. Then, repeating previous arguments, we obtain

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{B\left(q_{xy},\sqrt{t}\frac{r}{4\sqrt{T}}\right)}p_{D}(t/2,x,z)p_{D}(t/2,z,y)dz

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\sqrt{t}\frac{r}{2\sqrt{T}}}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\sqrt{t}\frac{r}{2\sqrt{T}}}{t}\right)\int_{B\left(q_{xy},\sqrt{t}\frac{r}{4\sqrt{T}}\right)}p(t/2,x,z)p(t/2,z,y)dz

\displaystyle\geqslant c\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\delta_{D}(x)}{t}\right)p(t,x,y),

for some constant
c=c(n,r,T)
.
∎

The lower bound from Theorem
4.2
may be improved by suitable application of Chapman-Kolmogorov identity, as presented below. In fact, the procedure could be iterated, however, further iterations lead to much more complicated forms, which do not seem to be a relevant enhancement in the general case.

Theorem 4.3
.

For any convex set
D\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}_{r}
and
T>0
there is
C=C(n,r,T)
such that

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\geqslant Cp(t,x,y)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right)}{t}\right)

(25)

\displaystyle\approx Cp(t,x,y)\left[\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)\right].

(26)

Proof.

For
\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\leqslant 2\sqrt{t}
the inequality (
25
) follows directly from Theorem
4.2
. We therefore assume
\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\geqslant 2\sqrt{t}
. Then, for
z\in B\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\sqrt{t}\right)\subset D
it holds

\delta_{D}(z)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{4}\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right).

Hence, by Chapman-Kolmogorov equality, Theorem
4.2
and Corollary
2.1
we get

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}p_{D}(t/2,x,z)p_{D}(t/2,z,y)dz

\displaystyle\geqslant C\int_{B\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\sqrt{t}\right)}p(t/2,x,z)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(z)}{t}\right)p(t/2,z,y)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(z)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\right)dz

\displaystyle\geqslant C\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right)}{t}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \times\int_{B\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\sqrt{t}\right)}p(t/2,x,z)p(t/2,z,y)dz

\displaystyle\gtrsim C\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right)}{t}\right)p(t,x,y),

as required. In order to show (
26
) we consider two cases as well. If
\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\geqslant\sqrt{t}
,

\displaystyle\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right)}{t}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\geqslant\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\right),

which means that the right-hand side term in brackets in (
26
) is dominating and comparable with the factor from (
25
). Similarly, for
\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\leqslant\sqrt{t}
we conclude
\delta_{D}(x),\delta_{D}(y)\leqslant 2\sqrt{t}
, and hence

\displaystyle\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\sqrt{t}\right)}{t}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\approx\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\right)\geqslant\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}{t^{2}}\approx\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right),

which ends the proof.
∎

5
Two-sided estimates

5.1
General results

For a strictly convex
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
domain
D
we define

\displaystyle Q_{D}

\displaystyle:=\inf_{w,z\in\partial D,w\neq z}\frac{\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)},

\displaystyle R_{D}

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}w,z\in\partial D,w\neq z\\
\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)\leqslant 1\end{subarray}}\frac{\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)},\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}w,z\in\partial D,w\neq z\\
\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)>1\end{subarray}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\overline{wz}\\
\delta_{D}(m)>1\end{subarray}}\frac{\delta_{D}\left(m\right)}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(m\right)}\right\}.

Note that since
D
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
domain, the half-space
H_{w}
is well defined for any
w\in\partial D
. It is clear that
0\leqslant Q_{D},R_{D}\leqslant 1
. Furthermore, since
\frac{w+z}{2}
is a possible value of
m
in the supremum in the definition of
R_{D}
, it holds
Q_{D}\leqslant R_{D}
. In general, we will be expecting
Q_{D},R_{D}>0
. The condition
Q_{D}>0
means that for any
w,z\in\partial D
the distance from the midpoint
\frac{w+z}{2}
to the boundary
\partial D
is comparable with the distances to
P_{w}
and
P_{z}
. In case
R_{D}>0
the condition is weaker whenever
\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)>1
, as we only require existence of a point at the interval
\overline{wz}
whose distance to
\partial D
is greater than
1
and comparable to distance to
P_{w}
. Let us introduce the following two class of sets corresponding to the characteristics
Q_{D}
and
R_{D}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{Q}

\displaystyle:=\left\{D\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):D\text{ is strictly convex},Q_{D}>0\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{R}

\displaystyle:=\left\{D\in\mathcal{C}_{r}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ for some }r>0:D\text{ is strictly convex},R_{D}>0\right\}.

In the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{Q}
we do not require the sets to be in
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, since it turns out that every
D\in\mathcal{S}_{Q}
is bounded (see Lemma
5.2
) and it is well known that every bounded
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
set belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for some
r>0
. Both of the classes
\mathcal{S}_{Q}
and
\mathcal{S}_{R}
contain nontrivial and important examples (see Propositions
5.6
and
5.7
). It seems also not easy to construct a strictly convex
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}_{r}
set which does not belong to
\mathcal{S}_{R}
.

The following monotonicity property will be needed in the sequel:

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
D
by a
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 2
, and let
w,z\in\partial D
. Then the function

\alpha\longrightarrow\frac{\delta_{D}\left((1-\alpha)w+\alpha z\right)}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left((1-\alpha)w+\alpha z\right)}

is non-increasing on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

Consider first
n=2
. Let
p,q\in\overline{wz}
be such that
0<|w-p|<|w-q|\leqslant|w-z|
and let
p^{\prime}\in\partial D
be a point realising the distance of
p
to
\partial D
. Then
P_{p}:=P_{p^{\prime}}
is a line tangent to
D
at
p^{\prime}
, and
H_{p}
is the related half-plane.
Thus, intercept theorem gives us

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{H_{w}}(p)}{|w-p|}=\frac{\delta_{H_{w}}(q)}{|w-q|},\ \ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \ \frac{\delta_{D}(p)}{|w-p|}\geqslant\frac{\delta_{H_{p}}(q)}{|w-q|}.

This implies

\frac{\delta_{D}(p)}{\delta_{H_{w}}(p)}\geqslant\frac{\delta_{H_{p}}(q)}{\delta_{H_{w}}(q)}\geqslant\frac{\delta_{D}(q)}{\delta_{H_{w}}(q)},

as required. For
n\geqslant 3
let
P
be the
2
-dimensional plane containing the interval
\overline{wz}
and the point
p^{\prime}\in\partial D
realising the distance of
p
to
\partial D
. Since
\delta_{D\cap P}(p)=\delta_{D}(p)
,
\delta_{D\cap P}(q)\geqslant\delta_{D}(q)
and, by intercept theorem,
\frac{\delta_{H_{w}\cap P}(p)}{\delta_{H_{w}\cap P}(q)}=\frac{\delta_{H_{w}}(p)}{\delta_{H_{w}}(q)}
, we may apply the result for
n=2
and get

\frac{\delta_{D}(p)}{\delta_{H_{w}}(p)}=\frac{\delta_{D\cap P}(p)}{\delta_{H_{w}}(p)}\geqslant\frac{\delta_{D\cap P}(q)\frac{\delta_{H_{w}\cap P}(p)}{\delta_{H_{w}\cap P}(q)}}{\delta_{H_{w}}(p)}\geqslant\frac{\delta_{D}(q)\frac{\delta_{H_{w}\cap P}(p)}{\delta_{H_{w}\cap P}(q)}}{\delta_{H_{w}}(p)}=\frac{\delta_{D}(q)}{\delta_{H_{w}}(q)},

which ends the proof.
∎

The next lemma shows that if
Q_{D}>0
, then
D
is bounded and the the infimum from the definition of
Q_{D}
taken over all
z,w\in\bar{D}
is positive as well, and consequently
\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)
and
\delta_{H_{z}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)
(for any choice of
H_{w}
) are comparable for any
z,w\in\bar{D}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{S}_{Q}
. Then

i)

D
is bounded,

ii)

for every
x,y\in D
we have

\displaystyle\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\leqslant\delta_{H_{x}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\leqslant\frac{3}{Q_{D}}\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right),

(27)

where
H_{x}
is any half-space such that
\delta_{D}(x)=\delta_{H_{x}}(x)
and
D\subset H_{x}
.

Proof.

i)
Assume
D
is an unbounded strictly convex
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
set with
Q_{D}>0
. There exists a half-line
l
starting at some point
w\in\partial D
and contained in
\overline{D}
. Let
P
be a (
2
-dimensional) plane containing
l
and equipped with coordinate system of axes
Ox
and
Oy
such that
l
is the nonnegative half-line of
Oy
. The intersection
P\cap\partial D
is then a graph of a strictly convex
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
function
f(x)
. Without loss of the generality we may assume that

is increasing on

. The distance of any point from

to the hyper-plane

is proportional to its distance to a line in

tangent to

at

(i.e. the point
w

is strictly convex and increasing on
[0,\infty)

\delta_{H_{w}}((x,f(x)))>c_{1}f(x)
for

\delta_{D}((x,y))\leqslant\delta_{D\cap P}\left((x,y)\right)\leqslant\left(y-f(x)\right)\wedge\left(f^{-1}(y)-x\right)
, where

and
(x,y)
is a point on
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
in coordinated introduced on

z=(x,f(x))\in\partial D
,
x>1

\frac{\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}\leqslant\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}f(x)-f\left(\frac{1}{2}x\right)\right)\wedge\left(\frac{1}{2}x\right)}{\frac{1}{2}c_{1}f(x)}=:g(x).

We will show that

. From strict convexity of

are increasing for

\frac{1}{2}f(x)-f\left(\frac{1}{2}x\right)
is bounded, then

clearly tends to zero. In the other case, it is when
\frac{1}{2}f(x)-f\left(\frac{1}{2}x\right)
tends to infinity, we employ L’Hôpital’s rule and obtain

\limsup_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}\leqslant\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\left(f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{2}x\right)\right)\wedge 1}{c_{1}f^{\prime}(x)}.

Since
f^{\prime}(x)
tends either to a constant or to infinity, the last limit equals zero, as required.

ii)
The first inequality follows simply from the inclusion

and

,
H_{y}=H_{y^{\prime}}

\delta_{H_{x}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)=\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)
and (

. In case

\displaystyle\left|\frac{x+y}{2}-\frac{x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}|x-x^{\prime}|+\frac{1}{2}|y-y^{\prime}|=\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta_{D}(x)+\delta_{D}(y)\right)\leqslant\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right),

where the last inequality follows from convexity of
D
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\delta_{H_{x}}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta_{H_{x}}\left(\frac{x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\left|\frac{x+y}{2}-\frac{x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{Q_{D}}\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{Q_{D}}\left(\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+\left|\frac{x+y}{2}-\frac{x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}}{2}\right|\right)+\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\frac{2}{Q_{D}}+1\right)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\leqslant\frac{3}{Q_{D}}\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right),

where we used the inequality
Q_{D}\leqslant 1
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
D
be a strictly convex
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
set. Then
D\in\mathcal{S}_{Q}
if and only if

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

(28)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D,T}}{{\approx}}p(t,x,y)\left[\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)\right].

holds for
x,y\in D
,
0<t<T
.

Proof.

If
Q_{D}>0
, the estimate (
28
) follows from Corollary
5.3
.

Let us assume that (
28
) holds for all
x,y\in D
and
0<t<T
for some
T>0
, and consider
w,z\in\partial D
such that
w\neq z
. Since
D
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
set, there is a ball of radius
r>0
contained in
D
and tangent to it at
w
, which ensures existence of a point
m
from the interval
\overline{wz}
such that
|m-w|<|w-z|/4
and

\displaystyle\delta_{D}\left(m\right)>\frac{1}{2}\delta_{H_{w}}(m).

(29)

Set

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}=\min\left\{r,\frac{1}{6}\delta_{D}\left(m\right),\sqrt{T}\right\},

(30)

and let
m^{\prime}
be a point at the interval
\overline{wm}
such that
\delta_{D}(m^{\prime})=6\sqrt{t}
. Then, from (
29
) and Proposition
5.1
we have

\displaystyle\delta_{D}\left(m^{\prime}\right)\approx\delta_{H_{w}}(m^{\prime})\approx\sqrt{t}.

(31)

Since we are going to apply estimates from (
28
) and
w\notin D
, we approximate
w
by a point from
D
. Precisely, let
x
be a point from
\overline{wm^{\prime}}
such that
|w-x|<\sqrt{t}
and let us put
\alpha:=\frac{|x-m^{\prime}|}{\left|x-\frac{w+z}{2}\right|}<\frac{1}{2}
.
Then, Chapman-Kolmogorov identity gives us

\displaystyle p_{D}\left(t,x,\frac{w+z}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{B(m^{\prime},\sqrt{\alpha t})}p_{D}(\alpha\,t,x,v)p_{D}\left((1-\alpha)t,v,\frac{w+z}{2}\right)dv.

(32)

For
v\in B(m^{\prime},\sqrt{\alpha t})
we have
|x-v|\geqslant 4\sqrt{t}
and
\delta_{D}(v)\geqslant\frac{5}{6}\delta_{D}(m^{\prime})\geqslant 5\sqrt{t}
, which allows us to employ Lemma
4.1
(in the same manner as in the proof of Theorem
4.2
) and get

p_{D}(\alpha t,x,v)\gtrsim\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\sqrt{t}}{\alpha t}\right)p(\alpha t,x,v)\approx\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(m^{\prime})}{\alpha t}\right)p(\alpha t,x,v).

Note that we could have not used Theorem
4.2
, since we assume
D
to be any strictly convex
C^{1,1}
set, so it may not belong to any
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}_{r}
,
r>0
. Next, intercept theorem and the inequality (
29
) give us

\displaystyle\delta_{D}(m^{\prime})

\displaystyle\gtrsim\delta_{H_{w}}(m^{\prime})=2\frac{|w-m^{\prime}|}{|w-z|}\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{|x-m^{\prime}|}{2\left|x-\frac{w+z}{2}\right|}\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\alpha\,\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right),

which follows

p_{D}(\alpha t,x,w)\gtrsim\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}{t}\right)p(\alpha t,x,w).

Furthermore, by (
7
), we have

\displaystyle p_{D}\left((1-\alpha)t,v,\frac{w+z}{2}\right)\gtrsim p\left((1-\alpha)t,v,\frac{w+z}{2}\right).

Applying the last two bounds to (
32
) and using Corollary
2.1
, we conclude

\displaystyle p_{D}\left(t,x,\frac{w+z}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant c_{1}\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}{t}\right)p\left(t,x,\frac{w+z}{2}\right),

(33)

for some
c_{1}>0
. On the other hand, convexity of

implies

\displaystyle\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\sup_{v\in\overline{wz}}\{\delta_{D}\left(v\right)\},

(34)

and consequently, by (
28
),

\displaystyle p_{D}\left(t,x,\frac{w+z}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{2}p\left(t,x,\frac{w+z}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times\left[\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{1}{2}x+\frac{1}{4}w+\frac{1}{4}z\right)}{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 3c_{2}\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}{t}\right)p\left(t,x,\frac{w+z}{2}\right),

(35)

for some
c_{2}(n,r,T)>0
. Finally, comparing (
33

\frac{\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}\geqslant\frac{c_{1}}{3c_{2}},

valid for any

Theorem 5.5
.

If
D\in\mathcal{S}_{R}

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r,T,R_{D}}}{{\approx}}p(t,x,y)\left[\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta(x)\delta(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}\left(y\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\delta_{H_{y}}\left(x\right)}{t}\right)\right].

Proof.

3.4

4.2

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r,T,R_{D}}}{{\gtrsim}}\,p(t,x,y)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}\left(y\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\delta_{H_{y}}\left(x\right)}{t}\right).

5.2

Proposition 5.6
.

Consider a domain
U=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n}>a|(x_{1},...,x_{n-1})|^{p}\}
, where
p\geqslant 2
,
n\geqslant 2
and
a>0
. Then
U\in\mathcal{S}_{R}
.

As a consequence, the heat kernel
p_{U}(t,x,y)
admits estimates from Theorem
5.5
with constants depending on
n,T,a,p
.

Proof.

Since changing
a
only rescales distances in the definition of
R_{D}
, we may assume
a=1
. Consider
w,z\in\partial U
and let us denote
\utilde{w}=(w_{1},...,w_{n-1})
. Additionally, we assume, without loss of the generality, that
w=(w_{1},0,...,0,w_{n})
with
w_{1},w_{n}\geqslant 0
. The hyperplane
P_{w}
tangent to
U
at
w
is then given by the equation

x_{n}=pw_{1}^{p-1}(x_{1}-w_{1})+w_{1}^{p}.

First, we will show that for
w,z
satisfying
|\utilde{w}-\utilde{z}|<\frac{1}{2}|\utilde{w}|
it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{U}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}>c_{p},

(38)

for some constant
c_{p}>0
. Let
\delta_{H_{w}}^{\downarrow}(z)
,
z\in\partial U
, denote the distance between
z
and its ”vertical” projection onto
P_{w}
, i.e. projection along the vector
(0,...,0,-1)
, given by

\delta_{H_{w}}^{\downarrow}(z)=z_{n}-\left(pw_{1}^{p-1}(z_{1}-w_{1})+w_{1}^{p}\right).

Using the elementary formula for the distance between a point and a hyperplane, we get

\delta_{H_{w}}\left(z\right)=\frac{|z_{n}-\left(pw_{1}^{p-1}(z_{1}-w_{1})+w_{1}^{p}\right)|}{\sqrt{\left(pw_{1}^{p-1}\right)^{2}+1}}=\frac{\delta^{\downarrow}_{H_{w}}\left(z\right)}{\sqrt{\left(pw_{1}^{p-1}\right)^{2}+1}},

which implies

\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{H_{w}}\left(z\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\approx}}\frac{\delta^{\downarrow}_{H_{w}}\left(z\right)}{w_{1}^{p-1}+1}.

Furthermore, by two-dimensional Taylor’s formula applied to
f(x,y)=((w_{1}+x)^{2}+y^{2})^{p/2}
we may write for
\utilde{v}=\utilde{z}-\utilde{w}

\displaystyle\delta_{H_{w}}^{\downarrow}(z)

\displaystyle=\left((w_{1}+v_{1})^{2}+|\widetilde{\utilde{v}}|^{2}\right)^{p/2}-\left(pw_{1}^{p-1}v_{1}+w_{1}^{p}\right)

\displaystyle=v_{1}^{2}\frac{p}{2}\left(((w_{1}+\xi_{1})^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}-1}+(p-2)(w_{1}+\xi_{1})^{2}((w_{1}+\xi_{1})^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}-2}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +|\widetilde{\utilde{v}}|^{2}\frac{p}{2}\left(((w_{1}+\xi_{1})^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}-1}+(p-2)\xi_{2}^{2}((w_{1}+\xi_{1})^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}-2}\right),

where
|\xi_{1}|,|\xi_{2}|\leqslant|\utilde{v}|
,
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence, for
|\utilde{v}|/|\utilde{w}|<\frac{1}{2}
we have
\delta_{H_{w}}^{\downarrow}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\approx}}w_{1}^{p-2}|\utilde{v}|^{2}
, and consequently

\displaystyle\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\approx}}\frac{w_{1}^{p-2}}{w_{1}^{p-1}+1}|\utilde{w}-\utilde{z}|^{2}.

Next we will estimate the distance from
\frac{w+z}{2}
to the boundary od
U
. One can see that if
\frac{w_{n}+z_{n}}{2}\leqslant 1
, then
\delta_{U}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)\approx\delta_{U}^{\downarrow}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)
, and if
\frac{w_{n}+z_{n}}{2}>1
, then
\delta_{U}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)\approx\delta_{U}^{\rightarrow}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)
, where, analogously as previously,
\delta_{U}^{\downarrow}(u)
denotes the distance between
u
and its projection onto
\partial U
along the vector
(0,...,0,-1)
and
\delta_{U}^{\rightarrow}(u)
denotes the distance between
z
and its projection onto
\partial U
along the vector
\utilde{u}
. In the case
\frac{w_{n}+z_{n}}{2}\leqslant 1
convexity of the function
\mathbb{R}\ni x\rightarrow x^{p/2}
and the estimate
a^{q}-b^{q}{\approx}q(a-b)a^{q-1}
valid for
0\leqslant b<a
and
q\geqslant 0
, give us for
\utilde{v}=\utilde{z}-\utilde{w}
and
|\utilde{v}|/|\utilde{w}|<\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\delta_{U}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\delta_{U}^{\downarrow}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(w_{n}+z_{n}\right)-\left|\frac{\utilde{w}+\utilde{z}}{2}\right|^{p}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}(w_{1}^{2})^{p/2}+\frac{1}{2}\big((w_{1}+v_{1})^{2}+|\widetilde{\utilde{v}}|^{2}\big)^{p/2}\right)-\left((w_{1}+\frac{1}{2}v_{1})^{2}+|\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\utilde{v}}|^{2}\right)^{p/2}

(39)

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(\frac{1}{2}w_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(w_{1}+v_{1})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\widetilde{\utilde{v}}|^{2}\right)^{p/2}-\left((w_{1}+\frac{1}{2}v_{1})^{2}+|\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\utilde{v}}|^{2}\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\approx}}\left(\frac{1}{4}v_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}|\widetilde{\utilde{v}}|^{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}w_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(w_{1}+v_{1})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\widetilde{\utilde{v}}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}-1}

\displaystyle\approx|\utilde{v}|^{2}w_{1}^{p-2}.

Furthermore, using the above bound, we get for
\frac{w_{n}+z_{n}}{2}>1

\displaystyle\delta_{U}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\delta_{U}^{\rightarrow}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)=\left(\frac{w_{n}+z_{n}}{2}\right)^{1/p}-\left|\frac{\utilde{w}+\utilde{z}}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\approx}}\left({\frac{1}{2}\left(w_{n}+z_{n}\right)-\left|\frac{\utilde{w}+\utilde{z}}{2}\right|^{p}}\right)\left(\frac{w_{n}+z_{n}}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}-1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\approx}}\delta_{U}^{\downarrow}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)w_{n}^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\gtrsim}}|\utilde{v}|^{2}w_{1}^{-1}.

This implies that for any value of
\frac{w_{n}+z_{n}}{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\approx}}w_{1}^{p}
we have

\delta_{U}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\gtrsim}}\frac{w_{1}^{p-2}}{w_{1}^{p-1}+1}|\utilde{w}-\utilde{z}|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\approx}}\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right),

which ends the proof of (
38
).

Let us pass to the main part of the proof. Assume that
U\notin\mathcal{S}_{R}
, i.e.
R_{U}=0
. Then, there is a sequence of pairs
(w^{(k)},z^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
from
\partial U\times\partial U
such that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f_{U}(w^{(k)},z^{(k)})=0
, where

f_{U}(w,z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\delta_{U}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)},&\text{ if }&\delta_{U}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)\leqslant 1,\\[10.0pt]
\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\overline{wz}\\
\delta_{U}(m)>1\end{subarray}}\frac{\delta_{U}\left(m\right)}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(m\right)},&\text{ if }&\delta_{U}\left(\frac{w+z}{2}\right)>1.\end{array}\right.

Furthermore, there is a subsequence
(w^{(k_{l})},z^{(k_{l})})
such that
|w^{(k_{l})}|\rightarrow\infty
or
w^{(k_{l})}\rightarrow w^{(0)}
for some
w^{(0)}\in\partial U
. Analogously, in each case there is a subsubsequence
(w^{(k_{l_{m}})},z^{(k_{l_{m}})})
such that
|z^{(k_{l_{m}})}|\rightarrow\infty
or
z^{(k_{l_{m}})}\rightarrow z^{(0)}
for some
z^{(0)}\in\partial U
. We will show that in any case
\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}f_{U}(w^{(k_{l_{m}})},z^{(k_{l_{m}})})>0
, which will finish the proof due to contradiction with the assumption
R_{U}=0
.

Assume first
w^{(k_{l})}\rightarrow w^{(0)}
. If
z^{(k_{l_{m}})}\rightarrow z^{(0)}\neq w^{(0)}
, then
\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}f_{U}(w^{(k_{l_{m}})},z^{(k_{l_{m}})})\geqslant{\delta_{U}\left(\frac{w^{(0)}+z^{(0)}}{2}\right)}/{\delta_{H_{w^{(0)}}}\left(\frac{w^{(0)}+z^{(0)}}{2}\right)}>0
. In case
z^{(k_{l_{m}})}\rightarrow z^{(0)}=w^{(0)}
there are two options: for
|\utilde{w}-\utilde{z}|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}|\utilde{w}|
the inequality (
38
) ensures that
f_{U}(w^{(k_{l_{m}})},z^{(k_{l_{m}})})>c_{p}
, while for
|\utilde{w}-\utilde{z}|>\frac{1}{2}|\utilde{w}|
, which may happen only if
w^{(0)}=z^{(0)}=0
, we have
{\delta_{U}\left(\frac{w^{(k_{l_{m}})}+z^{(k_{l_{m}})}}{2}\right)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\approx}}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w^{(k_{l_{m}})}+z^{(k_{l_{m}})}}{2}\right)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\approx}}\max\{|\utilde{w}|^{p},|\utilde{z}|^{p}\}
. Eventually, for
|z^{(k_{l_{m}})}|\rightarrow\infty
let us denote
m=(w^{(0)}_{1},...,w^{(0)}_{n-1},m_{n})
such that
m_{n}>w^{(0)}_{n}
and
\delta_{U}(m)=2
and let
m^{(k_{l_{m}})}\in\overline{w^{(k_{l_{m}})}z^{(k_{l_{m}})}}
be such that
m^{(k_{l_{m}})}_{n}=m_{n}
. We clearly have
m^{(k_{l_{m}})}\rightarrow m
and therefore
\delta_{U}\left(m^{(k_{l_{m}})}\right)\rightarrow 2
, which implies
\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}f_{U}(w^{(k_{l_{m}})},z^{(k_{l_{m}})})\geqslant\frac{2}{\delta_{H_{w^{(0)}}}(m)}>0
.

Assume now
|w^{(k_{l})}|\rightarrow\infty
. The bound (
38
) implies that if
|w^{(k_{l})}_{1}-z^{(k_{l})}_{1}|,|\widetilde{\utilde{w}}^{(k_{l})}-\widetilde{\utilde{z}}^{(k_{l})}|\leqslant\frac{1}{4}|{\utilde{w}}^{(k_{l})}|
then
f_{U}(w^{(k_{l_{m}})},z^{(k_{l_{m}})})>c_{p}
. Furthermore, one can verify that for
|\widetilde{\utilde{w}}^{(k_{l})}-\widetilde{\utilde{z}}^{(k_{l})}|>\frac{1}{4}|{\utilde{w}}^{(k_{l})}|
we have
{\delta_{U}\left(\frac{w^{(k_{l_{m}})}+z^{(k_{l_{m}})}}{2}\right)}{\approx}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w^{(k_{l_{m}})}+z^{(k_{l_{m}})}}{2}\right)}\approx\max\{|w^{(k_{l_{m}})}_{1}|,|z^{(k_{l_{m}})}_{1}|\}
, so the remaining case is
|w^{(k_{l})}_{1}-z^{(k_{l})}_{1}|>\frac{1}{4}|{\utilde{w}}^{(k_{l})}|=\frac{1}{4}|{w_{1}}^{(k_{l})}|
. Choosing
m^{(k_{l_{m}})}\in\overline{w^{(k_{l_{m}})}z^{(k_{l_{m}})}}
such that
|m^{(k_{l_{m}})}_{n}-w^{(k_{l_{m}})}_{n}|=\frac{1}{8}w^{(k_{l_{m}})}_{n}
we have
{\delta_{U}\left(m^{(k_{l_{m}})}\right)}{\approx}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(m^{(k_{l_{m}})}\right)}\approx w^{(k_{l_{m}})}_{1}\rightarrow\infty
, which may be observed, for instance, considering
z=\left(\frac{5}{4}w_{1},0,...,0,(\frac{5}{4}w_{1})^{p}\right)
and
z=\left(\frac{3}{4}w_{1},0,...,0,(\frac{3}{4}w_{1})^{p}\right)
. This ends the proof.

∎

Proposition 5.7
.

For
n=2
the class
\mathcal{S}_{Q}
contains strictly convex bounded domains with analytical boundary. As a consequence, the heat kernels of such sets admit estimates from both: Theorem
5.5
and Theorem
5.4
.

Proof.

The idea of the proof is similar as in the case of previous proposition. Let
D
be a strictly convex bounded domain with analytical boundary and suppose there is a sequence
(w^{(k)},z^{(k)})_{k\geqslant 1}
,
w^{(k)},z^{(k)}\in\partial D
, such that

\displaystyle\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w^{(k)}+z^{(k)}}{2}\right)}{\delta_{H_{w^{(k)}}}\left(\frac{w^{(k)}+z^{(k)}}{2}\right)}=0.

(40)

Compactness of
\partial D
allows us assume that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}w^{(k)}=w^{(0)}
and
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}z^{(k)}=z^{(0)}
for some
w^{(0)},z^{(0)}\in\partial D
. Since (
40
) is clearly not satisfied for
w^{(0)}\neq z^{(0)}
, we therefore assume also
w^{(0)}=z^{(0)}
. Next, we rotate and translate
D
such that
w^{(0)}=(0,0)
and
D\subset\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
. Since
\partial D
is analytical, there exists a neighbourhood
G
of
(0,0)
such that
\partial D\cap G
is a graph of a function
y=f(x)=\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{\infty}a_{i}x^{i}
where
i_{0}\geqslant 2
,
a_{i_{0}}>0
,
(x,y)\in G
. In particular, this implies
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\frac{f(x)}{a_{i_{0}}x^{i_{0}}}=1
.
Hence, for
|w_{1}^{(k)}-z_{1}^{(k)}|>\frac{1}{2}|w_{1}^{(k)}|
we have
{\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w^{(k)}+z^{(k)}}{2}\right)}{\approx}{\delta_{H_{w}}\left(\frac{w^{(k)}+z^{(k)}}{2}\right)}\approx\max\{|w^{(k)}_{1}|^{i_{0}},|z^{(k)}_{1}|^{i_{0}}\}
,
k\geqslant k_{0}
for some
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, denoting
u=w_{1}^{(k)}
and
v=z^{(k)}_{1}-w_{1}^{(k)}
, Taylor’s formula gives us for
|w_{1}^{(k)}-z_{1}^{(k)}|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}|w_{1}^{(k)}|

\displaystyle\delta_{H_{w^{(k)}}}\left(\frac{w^{(k)}+z^{(k)}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{H_{w^{(k)}}}\left(z^{(k)}\right)

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\arctan\left(f^{\prime}(u+v)\right)\right)\Big(f(u+v)-\left(f(u)+vf^{\prime}(u)\right)\Big)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{\infty}a_{i}\left((u+v)^{i}-\big(u^{i}+iu^{i-1}v\big)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{\infty}a_{i}\frac{i(i-1)}{2}\left(u+\xi_{i}\right)^{i-2}v^{2}\approx|u|^{i_{0}-2}v^{2},

(41)

for some
|\xi_{i}|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}|u|
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w^{(k)}+z^{(k)}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\left(f(w^{(k)}_{1})+f(z_{1}^{(k)})\right)-f\left(\frac{w_{1}^{(k)}+z_{1}^{(k)}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{\infty}a_{i}\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\left(u+v\right)^{i}-\left(u+\frac{1}{2}v\right)^{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant a_{i_{0}}\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{i_{0}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(u+v\right)^{i_{0}}-\left(u+\frac{1}{2}v\right)^{i_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ -\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{\infty}|a_{i}|\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\left(u+v\right)^{i}-\left(u+\frac{1}{2}v\right)^{i}\right).

Taking
|\widetilde{\utilde{v}}|=0
in (
39
), we get

a_{i_{0}}\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{i_{0}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(u+v\right)^{i_{0}}-\left(u+\frac{1}{2}v\right)^{i_{0}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i_{0}}}{{\gtrsim}}|u|^{i_{0}-2}v^{2}.

Furthermore, Taylor’s formula used twice implies

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{\infty}|a_{i}|\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\left(u+v\right)^{i}-\left(u+\frac{1}{2}v\right)^{i}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i_{0}}}{{\lesssim}}\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{\infty}|a_{i}|i(i-1)\left(|u|+|v|\right)^{i-2}v^{2}\approx|u|^{i_{0}-1}v^{2},

and we conclude
\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w^{(k)}+z^{(k)}}{2}\right)\gtrsim|w^{(k)}_{1}|^{i_{0}-2}v^{2}
for
|w^{(k)}|
small enough. Thus, in view of (
41
), for such
w^{(k)}
it holds

\delta_{H_{w^{(k)}}}\left(\frac{w^{(k)}+z^{(k)}}{2}\right)/\delta_{D}\left(\frac{w^{(k)}+z^{(k)}}{2}\right)\geqslant c(i_{0})>0
, which contradicts (
40
).

∎

Let us define the following set

S=\big(B_{2}((-1,0),1)\big)\cup\big((-1,1)\times(-1,1)\big)\cup\big(B_{2}((1,0),1)\big)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},

which is a square
(-1,1)\times(-1,1)
with two semicircles added to its left and right sides. It is known as a stadium. The next example shows that for some range of arguments the heat kernel
p_{S}(t,x,y)
is comparable neither to the bound from Theorem
3.4
nor to the one from Theorem
4.3
. Note that the space arguments realising the indicated behaviour of
p_{S}(t,x,y)
are located at opposite ends of the ’flat’ part of the boundary, which strongly suggests that non-strict convexity is indeed the property that impacts on the incomparability of the bounds.

Example 5.1
.

Let
x,y\in S
such that
x_{1}<-1
,
y_{1}>1
,
x_{2},y_{2}=1-t^{\gamma}
,
\gamma>0
, and
\delta_{S}(x),\delta_{S}(y)<t^{1+\gamma}
with
t<1
. For
0<\gamma\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
we have

\displaystyle p_{S}(t,x,y)\approx p(t,x,y)\left[\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta(x)\delta(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}\left(y\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\delta_{H_{y}}\left(x\right)}{t}\right)\right],

and for
\gamma\geqslant\frac{2}{3}
it holds

p_{S}(t,x,y)\approx p(t,x,y)\left[\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{S}(y)\delta_{S}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)\right].

However, for
\frac{1}{2}<\gamma<\frac{2}{3}
we have

\displaystyle p_{S}(t,x,y)\approx\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}(y)}{t^{3(1-\gamma)}}p(t,x,y),

(42)

while

\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{S}(y)\delta_{S}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{t}\right)\approx\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}(y)}{t}

and

\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta(x)\delta(y)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{x}}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}\left(y\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H_{y}}(y)\delta_{H_{y}}\left(x\right)}{t}\right)\approx\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}(y)}{t^{2-\gamma}}=\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}(y)}{t^{3(1-\gamma)+2\left(\gamma-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}}.

Proof.

First, let us observe

\displaystyle\delta_{S}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)=t^{\gamma},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \delta_{H_{x}}(y)\approx\delta_{H_{y}}(x)\approx t^{\gamma/2},

(43)

which immediately follows the last two approximations in the assertion.

Assume
\gamma\leqslant\frac{2}{3}
and put
\alpha=\frac{|x-(-1,x_{2})|}{|x-y|}
. We have
\alpha\approx t^{\gamma/2}
and therefore
\sqrt{\alpha t}<ct^{(2+\gamma)/4}\leqslant ct^{\gamma}
,
t<t_{0}
, for some
c,t_{0}>0
. Chapman-Kolmogorov identity, Theorem
4.2
and Proposition
2.1
give us for some
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle p_{S}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{B_{2}\left((-1,x_{2}),\frac{1}{2}t^{\gamma}\right)}\int_{B_{2}\left((1,x_{y}),\frac{1}{2}t^{\gamma}\right)}p_{S}(\alpha t,x,w)p_{S}((1-2\alpha)t,w,z)p_{S}(\alpha t,z,y)dzdw

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{S}(x)t^{\gamma}}{\alpha t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{t^{2\gamma}}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{S}(y)t^{\gamma}}{\alpha t}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \int_{B_{2}\left((-1,x_{2}),\frac{1}{2c}\sqrt{\alpha t}\right)}\int_{B_{2}\left((1,x_{y}),\frac{1}{2c}\sqrt{\alpha t}\right)}p(\alpha t,x,w)p((1-2\alpha)t,w,z)p(\alpha t,z,y)dzdw

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\approx}}\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{H_{x}}(y)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge{t^{2\gamma-1}}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{S}(y)\delta_{H_{y}}(x)}{t}\right)p(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}(y)}{t^{2-\gamma}}\left(1\wedge{t^{2\gamma-1}}\right)p(t,x,y).

This implies the lower bound in (
42
) and, together with Theorem
3.4
and (
43
), the first estimate in the example. Furthermore, the lower bound in the latter estimate follows directly from (
26
). In order to obtain remaining upper bounds for
\gamma\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
, we denote
H=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}<1\}
and estimate
\delta_{H_{x}}(z)\delta_{H_{y}}(z),\lesssim\delta_{H}(z)+t^{\gamma/2}
for
z\in S
. Consequently, by Theorem
3.4
,

\displaystyle p_{S}(t/2,x,z)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}(z)}{t}\right)+\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\left(\delta_{H}(z)+t^{\gamma/2}\right)}{t}\right)\left(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{H}(z)t^{\gamma}}{t}\right)\right)p(t/2,x,z)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{H}(z)}{t}\left(1+t^{\gamma-1}\left(\delta_{H}(z)+t^{\gamma/2}\right)\right)p(t/2,x,z).

Similarly,
p_{S}(t/2,z,y)\lesssim\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{H}(z)}{t}\left(1+t^{\gamma-1}\left(\delta_{H}(z)+t^{\gamma/2}\right)\right)p(t/2,z,y)
, hence

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\leqslant\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}(y)}{t^{2}}\int_{H}\left(\delta_{H}(z)\right)^{2}\left(1+t^{2\gamma-2}\left(\delta^{2}_{H}(z)+t^{\gamma}\right)\right)p(t/2,x,z)p(t/2,z,y)dz

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}(y)}{t}\left(1+t^{2\gamma-1}+t^{3\gamma-2}\right)p(t,x,y),

where the last inequality follows from Proposition
2.2
with
H_{1}=H_{2}=H
.
For
\gamma\geqslant\frac{2}{3}
, we get
p_{S}(t,x,t)\lesssim\frac{\delta_{S}(x)\delta_{S}(y)}{t}p(t,x,y)
, which, combined with (

,
which follows the upper bound in (

). The proof is complete.
