Twisted differential operators in several variables
Pierre Houédry

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce novel concepts and establish a formal framework for twisted differential operators in the context of several variables. The focus is on twisted coordinates within Huber rings, which facilitate the construction of diverse rings of twisted differential operators. We establish an equivalence between modules equipped with twisted connections and those endowed with an actions of twisted derivatives. Furthermore, we examine the convergence properties of twisted differential operators under specific conditions. This work aligns with the ongoing advancements, in p p -adic Hodge cohomology and prismatic cohomology.



Introduction

A
q
-difference equation is a relation of the form

E(x,f,\partial_{q}(f),\ldots,\partial_{q}^{n}(f))=0,

which involves an unknown function
f
and its iterates under the
q
-difference operator:

\partial_{q}(f)(x)=\frac{f(qx)-f(x)}{(q-1)x}.

The primary motivation for studying such equations is practical. The goal is to discretize a differential equation in order to facilitate its analysis, a process known as “deformation”. Conversely, recovering a differential equation from a
q
-difference equation as
q
tends towards 1 is referred to as “confluence”. From a practical standpoint, the key problem is to quantify the discrepancy between the solutions of the discretized equation obtained via deformation and those of the original differential equation.

This problem can be approached from an arithmetic perspective, specifically within the realm of
p
-adic numbers. In 2000, Lucia Di Vizio proved a
q
-analogue of Grothendieck’s
p
-curvature conjecture (see
[
DV02
]
). Shortly after, in 2004, Yves André and Lucia Di Vizio investigated the phenomenon of confluence (
cf.

[
ADV04
]
). They computed the Tannakian group of
q
-difference equations over the Robba ring, equipped with a Frobenius action, and demonstrated that it coincides with the Tannakian group of differential equations over the Robba ring with Frobenius action. Simultaneously, during his doctoral thesis, Andrea Pulita also explored these problems and established an equivalence between these categories by removing the assumptions on
q
from
[
ADV04
]
for equations on the affine line. Pulita introduced a principle called the
propagation principle
. Once verified, this principle suffices to prove the equivalence between confluence and deformation on rings of
p
-adic functions. Pulita extended his results to curves and to actions of operators more general than multiplication by
q
(
cf.

[
Pul08
]
).

Interest in this theory increased substantially when Bhargav Bhatt, Matthew Morrow, and Peter Scholze published their work on
p
-adic Hodge cohomology (see
[
BMS18
]
). Their construction enables specialization to both de Rham and étale cohomologies. They demonstrated that this cohomology can be computed locally using
q
-difference theory. Furthermore, Bhatt and Scholze introduced the concepts of
prism
and
prismatic cohomology
(
[
BS22
]
) to unify the various cohomologies they had previously defined. Remarkably, it seems that
q
-difference equations can once again be employed to compute prismatic cohomology.

In recent years, Bernard Le Stum, Adolfo Quirós, and Michel Gros have developed a more general theory (see
[
LSQ15
]
,
[
LSQ18a
]
,
[
LSQ18b
]
,
[
LSQ20
]
,
[
GLS14
]
, and
[
GLQ19
]
) that encompasses earlier results on
p
-adic
q
-difference equations (see
[
ADV04
]
,
[
Pul08
]
). They generalized the notion of differential operators and extended their work to affinoid algebras. Their theory draws an analogy with the formalism established in EGA-IV (see
[
Gro67
]
) and is guided by the classical framework of crystalline cohomology. However, their methods do not extend to multiple variables. As in classical calculus, transitioning from ordinary differential equations to partial differential equations is a highly non-trivial step.

Matthew Morrow and Takeshi Tsuji have made significant progress by developing a multi-variable theory in the context of
q
-difference equations (see
[
MT21
]
). The present article serves as foundational work, aiming to generalize prior theories by addressing the case of several variables and incorporating more general twists than multiplication by
q
. Additionally, the work of Claude Sabbah (see
[
Sab93
]
), though conducted over
\mathbb{C}
, bears a close connection to the research pursued here.

Proposition 1.8

The topology of a Huber ring can always be defined by a submultiplicative semi-norm. Furthermore, in the case of a Tate ring, the topology can be defined by a submultiplicative norm.

2
Topologically étale morphisms

We will define topologically étale morphisms. Within this class, we will focus on good étale morphisms. This is the type of étale morphisms along which we will be able to extend the coordinates that we will work with.

Definition 2.1
.

A morphism of Huber rings
\varphi:A\rightarrow B
is
topologically of finite type
(resp. of
finite presentation
) if it is adic and if there exists rings of definition
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
of
A
and
B
such that
\varphi(A_{0})\subset B_{0}
and

1.

for every ideal of definition
I\subset A_{0}
, the induced map

A_{0}/I\rightarrow B_{0}/\varphi(I)B_{0}

is of finite type (resp. finite presentation),

2.

the map
A\otimes_{A_{0}}\widehat{B_{0}}\rightarrow\widehat{B}
is of finite type (resp. finite presentation).

Remark
.

This definition is a variant of the definition 2.3.13 of
[
Abb10
]
that specifies only to formal schemes, which corresponds to adic rings in the affine case. The conditions here are weaker than in the algebraic version of the definition 1.8.18 of
[
Abb10
]
.

Examples
.

1.

For discrete rings, this definition reduces to the classical algebraic notion of finite type (respectively, finite presentation).

2.

Let
\varphi:A\rightarrow B
be a morphism of finite type (respectively, finite presentation) between two rings. For an ideal
I
of finite type in
A
, it is possible to equip
A
and
B
with the
I
-adic topology. Under this topology, the morphism
\varphi
is topologically of finite type (respectively, finite presentation).

3.

Let
A
be a Huber ring and
\underline{f}=(f_{0},\ldots,f_{r})
be elements of
A
that generate an open ideal, the morphism

A\rightarrow A\left(\underline{f}/f_{0}\right)

is topologically of finite presentation.
Indeed, by construction it is adic and for every couple of definition
(A_{0},I)
of
A
, the morphism

A_{0}/I\rightarrow\left(A_{0}/I\right)\left[\frac{f_{1}}{f_{0}},\ldots\frac{f_{r}}{f_{0}}\right]

is of finite presentation. The second condition is automatically satisfied.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
f:A\rightarrow B
and
g:B\rightarrow C
be two morphisms between adic rings.

1.

If both
f
and
g
are topologically of finite type (respectively, finite presentation), then
g\circ f
is also topologically of finite type (respectively, finite presentation).

2.

If
g\circ f
is topologically of finite type and
g
is adic, then
f
is topologically of finite type.

3.

If
g\circ f
is topologically of finite presentation and
g
is topologically of finite type, then
f
is topologically of finite presentation.

Proof.

This is the affine version of the proposition 2.3.18 of
[
Abb10
]
.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

A morphism
A\rightarrow B
between adic rings is topologically of finite presentation if and only if the induced completed morphism
\widehat{A}\rightarrow\widehat{B}
is topologically of finite presentation.

Proof.

Let us first consider the converse. Assume that
\widehat{A}\rightarrow\widehat{B}
is topologically of finite presentation. Then, for every ideal of definition
I
of
A
, the ideal
I\widehat{A}
is an ideal of definition of
\widehat{A}
, as established by Proposition
1.11
. The same proposition provides the following isomorphisms:

\widehat{A}/I\widehat{A}\simeq A/I\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{B}/I\widehat{B}\simeq B/IB.

This ensures that for every ideal of definition
I
of
A
, the morphism
A/I\rightarrow B/I
is of finite type.
Now, let us prove the direct implication. Suppose
I^{\prime}
is an ideal of definition for
\widehat{A}
. For every ideal of definition
I
of
A
, there exists an integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
I^{n}\widehat{A}\subset I^{\prime}
. We then consider the following commutative diagram:

\textstyle{\widehat{A}/I^{n}\widehat{A}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\widehat{B}/I^{n}\widehat{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\widehat{A}/I^{\prime}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\widehat{B}/I^{\prime}\widehat{B}}

Using Proposition
1.11
, we can deduce that the top morphism in this diagram is of finite presentation, which implies that the bottom morphism is also of finite presentation. The second condition can be easily verified.
∎

Example
.

For a non-archimedean field
K
, the canonical morphism
K\rightarrow K\left\{\underline{X}\right\}
is topologically of finite presentation.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\varphi:A\rightarrow B
be an adic morphism between adic rings, the following statements are equivalent

1.

The morphism
\varphi
is topologically of finite type (resp. finite presentation).

2.

For every ideal of definition
I
of
A
, the induced map

A/I\rightarrow B/\varphi(I)B

is of finite type (resp. finite presentation).

Proof.

The implication
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
is straightforward. It remains to prove
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Without loss of generality, we assume that the rings
A
and
B
are complete. By hypothesis, the morphism is adic. Let
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
be rings of definition for
A
and
B
that satisfy the conditions of the definition. For every ideal of definition
I\subset A_{0}
, the induced map

A_{0}/I\rightarrow B_{0}/\varphi(I)B_{0}

is of finite type. By extending scalars and using the second condition of the definition, we obtain that the map

A/I\simeq A\otimes_{A_{0}}A_{0}/I\rightarrow A\otimes_{A_{0}}B_{0}/\varphi(I)B_{0}\rightarrow B/\varphi(I)B

is of finite type.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A
,
B
, and
A^{\prime}
be adic rings, with
A\rightarrow B
a morphism that is topologically of finite presentation, and
A\rightarrow A^{\prime}
an adic morphism. Then, the morphism
A^{\prime}\rightarrow A^{\prime}\otimes_{A}B
is topologically of finite presentation.

Proof.

Since the morphism
A\rightarrow A^{\prime}
is adic, by Proposition
1.3
, the image of every ideal of definition
I
of
A
under this morphism generates an ideal of definition in
A^{\prime}
. Moreover, the induced map

A/I\rightarrow B/IB

is of finite presentation. This implies that the map

A^{\prime}/IA^{\prime}\simeq A^{\prime}/IA^{\prime}\otimes_{A/I}A/I\rightarrow A^{\prime}/IA^{\prime}\otimes_{A/I}B/I\simeq(A^{\prime}\otimes_{A}B)/I(A^{\prime}\otimes_{A}B)

is also of finite presentation.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
R
be an adic ring, and let
A
,
A^{\prime}
,
B
, and
B^{\prime}
be
R
-adic algebras. If the morphisms
A\rightarrow B
and
A^{\prime}\rightarrow B^{\prime}
are topologically of finite presentation, then the induced morphism
A\otimes_{R}A^{\prime}\rightarrow B\otimes_{R}B^{\prime}
is also topologically of finite presentation.

Proof.

By extending scalars and applying Lemma
2.5
, the morphisms

A\otimes_{R}A^{\prime}\rightarrow B\otimes_{R}A^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad B\otimes_{R}A^{\prime}\rightarrow B\otimes_{R}B^{\prime}

are topologically of finite presentation. Using Proposition
2.2
, we conclude that the morphism
A\otimes_{R}A^{\prime}\rightarrow B\otimes_{R}B^{\prime}
is topologically of finite presentation.
∎

Definition 2.7
.

A morphism of adic rings
A\rightarrow B
is
formally étale
if for every commutative diagram of adic rings

\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{B\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C/\mathfrak{c}}

where
C
is a complete adic ring and
\mathfrak{c}
is a nilpotent ideal, there exists a unique morphism
B\rightarrow C
making the diagram commute.
The morphism
A\rightarrow B
is called
topologically étale
if it is both formally étale and topologically of finite presentation.

Examples
.

1.

In the case of discrete rings, this definition reduces to the classical algebraic notion of étale morphisms.

2.

Let
\varphi:A\rightarrow B
be an étale morphism between rings. For an ideal
I
of finite type in
A
, one can equip
A
and
B
with the
I
-adic topology. In this case, the morphism
\varphi
is adic, topologically of finite presentation, and formally étale, making it topologically étale.

3.

For a non-archimedean field
K
, the canonical morphism
K_{0}[\underline{X}]\rightarrow K_{0}\left\{\underline{X}\right\}
is topologically étale.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
A
and
B
be two adic rings and
\varphi:A\rightarrow B
be an adic morphism. The following statements are equivalent

1.

The morphism
\varphi
is formally étale.

2.

For every diagram of adic rings

\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{B\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C/\mathfrak{c}}

where
C
is a complete adic ring and
\mathfrak{c}
is a topologically nilpotent ideal, there exists a unique morphism
B\rightarrow C
that makes the diagram commute.

Proof.

The implication
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
is clear, as a nilpotent ideal is topologically nilpotent. It remains to prove
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Consider a commutative diagram of adic rings

\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{B\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C/\mathfrak{c}}

where
C
is a complete adic ring and
\mathfrak{c}
is a topologically nilpotent ideal. For every ideal of definition
I
of
A
and every natural integer
k
, this diagram induces the following commutative diagram

\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C/I^{k}C\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{B\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C/I^{k}C/\mathfrak{c}.}

By Proposition
1.3
,
I^{k}C
is an ideal of definition of
C
, and thus
\mathfrak{c}
is nilpotent in
C/I^{k}C
. Furthermore,
C/I^{k}C
is complete for the discrete topology, so we can lift the morphism to obtain
B\rightarrow C/I^{k}C
. By passing to the completion, we obtain a morphism
B\rightarrow C
, and it can be easily verified that this morphism is adic.
∎

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
f:A\rightarrow B
and
g:B\rightarrow C
be two morphisms of adic rings. If both
f
and
g
are topologically étale, then the composition
g\circ f
is also topologically étale.

Proof.

Since the composition of two formally étale morphisms is itself formally étale, we can conclude that the morphism
g\circ f
is formally étale. By applying Proposition
2.2
, it follows that
g\circ f
is also topologically étale.
∎

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
R
be an adic ring, and let
A
,
B
,
C
, and
D
be
R
-adic algebras that are also adic rings. Suppose
\varphi:A\rightarrow C
and
\psi:B\rightarrow D
are topologically étale morphisms between adic rings. Then the induced morphism

\varphi\otimes_{R}\psi:A\otimes_{R}B\rightarrow C\otimes_{R}D

is also topologically étale.

Proof.

Consider a commutative diagram of adic rings:

\textstyle{A\otimes_{R}B\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{E\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C\otimes_{R}D\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{E/\mathfrak{e}}

where
E
is complete and
\mathfrak{e}
is a nilpotent ideal. This diagram induces the following commutative diagrams:

\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{E\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{E/\mathfrak{e}}

\textstyle{B\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{E\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{D\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{E/\mathfrak{e}}

By hypothesis, there exist unique liftings
C\rightarrow E
and
D\rightarrow E
that make these diagrams commute. By the universal property of the tensor product, there exists a unique map
C\otimes_{R}D\rightarrow E
that makes the original diagram commute. The fact that the morphism is topologically of finite presentation follows from Proposition
2.6
.
∎

Definition 2.11
.

A morphism
\varphi:A\rightarrow B
of Huber rings is said to be
good étale
if there exist rings of definition
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
of
A
and
B
satisfying
\varphi(A_{0})\subset B_{0}
such that the induced morphism
\varphi_{0}:A_{0}\rightarrow B_{0}
is topologically étale and
B=A\otimes_{A_{0}}B_{0}
. The triplet
(\varphi_{0},A_{0},B_{0})
is said to be a
good model
of
\varphi
.

Examples
.

1.

In the case of discrete rings, this reduces to the classical notion of an étale morphism.

2.

For a prime number
p\neq 2
, the morphism

\mathbb{Q}_{p}[T]\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}[X,Y]/(Y^{2}-X^{3}+X),\penalty\ T\mapsto X

is not good étale, where the topology on both sides is the
p
-adic topology.

3.

For a non-archimedean field
K
, the canonical morphism
K[\underline{X}]\rightarrow K\{\underline{X}\}
is good étale.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
A
and
B
be two adic rings and
\varphi:A\rightarrow B
a morphism. If
\varphi
is good étale, then it is formally étale.

Proof.

Let us consider a commutative diagram of adic rings

\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{B\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C/\mathfrak{c}}

with
C
complete and
\mathfrak{c}
nilpotent. For rings of definition
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
of
A
and
B
satisfying the requirements of the definition, we deduce the following diagram

\textstyle{A_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{B_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C/\mathfrak{c}.}

By hypothesis, the exists a unique lifting

.
∎

Definition 2.13
.

Let
A
be a Huber ring. An endomorphism

of

is said to be

over a subset
V
of

if there exists a topologically nilpotent subset

of

such that
(\sigma-\text{Id})(V)\subset U
and
\sigma(V)\subset V
.

Example
.

Let
A
be a Huber ring, and let

denote its submultiplicative semi-norm. Let

be a ring of definition. For an element

such that

is infinitesimal over

.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
B
be a complete Huber ring, and let

as a good model. If

, then

of

Let

. Since

is infinitesimal over

, it follows that

. The ideal

is topologically nilpotent in

. To obtain the desired extension, consider the following commutative diagram

\textstyle{A_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

is good étale, there exists a unique lifting

Let

Let

3
Reminders on filtered modules

In this section we recall some general results about filtered modules that will be useful for our work.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
A
be a ring. An
A
-module
M
is said to be
filtered
if it is equipped with an exhaustive decreasing filtration
\text{Fil}^{\bullet}M
, indexed by natural integers. For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the associated graduation is denoted by
\text{Gr}^{i}M=\penalty\ \text{Fil}^{i}M/\text{Fil}^{i+1}M
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
A
be a ring, and let
M
and
N
be two filtered
A
-modules. There exists a natural filtration on
M\otimes_{A}N
, defined for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
by

\emph{Fil}^{k}(M\otimes_{A}N)=\sum_{i+j=k}\emph{Im}\left(\emph{Fil}^{i}M\otimes\emph{Fil}^{j}N\rightarrow M\otimes_{A}N\right),

which induces a natural morphism

\bigoplus_{i+j=k}\emph{Gr}^{i}M\otimes\emph{Gr}^{j}N\rightarrow\text{Gr}^{k}(M\otimes_{A}N).

Moreover, if
M/\emph{Fil}^{i}M
and
N/\emph{Fil}^{j}N
are flat over
A
for every
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
, then this morphism is an isomorphism.

Proof.

By definition, for fixed
i+j=k
there is a map

\text{Fil}^{i}M\otimes\text{Fil}^{j}N\rightarrow M\otimes N\rightarrow\text{Gr}^{k}(M\otimes N).

The image of
\text{Fil}^{i+1}M\otimes\text{Fil}^{j}N
under this map is zero. Passing to the quotient, we obtain a morphism

\text{Gr}^{i}M\otimes\text{Fil}^{j}N\rightarrow\text{Gr}^{k}(M\otimes N).

Since the image of
\text{Gr}^{i}M\otimes\text{Fil}^{j+1}N
under this map is zero, we obtain, by passing to the quotient again, a morphism

\psi_{i,j}:\text{Gr}^{i}M\otimes\text{Gr}^{j}N\rightarrow\text{Gr}^{k}(M\otimes N).

Taking the direct sum, we have a morphism

\bigoplus_{i+j=k}\text{Gr}^{i}M\otimes\text{Gr}^{j}N\rightarrow\text{Gr}^{k}(M\otimes N)

which is the desired morphism. Now, assume that for every
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
,
M/\text{Fil}^{i}M
and
N/\text{Fil}^{j}N
are flat modules over
A
.
We aim to construct an inverse.
For fixed
i+j=k
, consider the composed morphism

\phi_{i,j}:\text{Fil}^{k}(M\otimes N)\hookrightarrow M\otimes N\rightarrow M/\text{Fil}^{i+1}M\otimes N/\text{Fil}^{j+1}N.

Next, compose with the surjection

\rho_{1}:M/\text{Fil}^{i+1}M\otimes N/\text{Fil}^{j+1}N\rightarrow M/\text{Fil}^{i}M\otimes N/\text{Fil}^{j+1}N.

This composition is the zero map. Indeed, given an element in the image of
\phi_{i,j}
it can be written under the form
\sum_{v+u=k}\overline{m_{u}}\otimes\overline{n_{v}}
with
m_{u}\in\text{Fil}^{u}M
and
n_{v}\in\text{Fil}^{v}N
, where either
u\geq i
or
v\geq j+1
. Such elements are mapped to zero by
\rho_{1}
. Therefore, the image of
\phi_{i,j}
lies in the kernel of
\rho_{1}
. Using the flatness of
N/\text{Fil}^{j+1}N
, we compute this kernel. By definition, the following sequence is exact

0\rightarrow\text{Gr}^{i}M\rightarrow M/\text{Fil}^{i+1}M\rightarrow M/\text{Fil}^{i}M\rightarrow 0.

By the flatness of
N/\text{Fil}^{j+1}N
, tensoring with
N/\text{Fil}^{j+1}N
yields an exact sequence

0\rightarrow\text{Gr}^{i}M\otimes N/\text{Fil}^{j+1}N\rightarrow M/\text{Fil}^{i+1}M\otimes N/\text{Fil}^{j+1}N\rightarrow M/\text{Fil}^{i}M\otimes N/\text{Fil}^{j+1}N\rightarrow 0.

Thus,
\text{ker}(\rho_{1})=\text{Gr}^{i}M\otimes N/\text{Fil}^{j+1}N
and we obtain a morphism

\phi_{i,j}^{\prime}:\text{Fil}^{k}(M\otimes N)\rightarrow\text{Gr}^{i}M\otimes N/\text{Fil}^{j+1}N.

Similarly, considering the surjection

\rho_{2}:\text{Gr}^{i}M\otimes N/\text{Fil}^{j+1}N\rightarrow\text{Gr}^{i}M\otimes N/\text{Fil}^{j}N,

we obtain a morphism

\phi_{i,j}^{\prime\prime}:\text{Fil}^{k}(M\otimes N)\rightarrow\text{Gr}^{i}M\otimes\text{Gr}^{j}N.

Proceeding in the same way for the indices, we obtain a morphism

\text{Gr}^{k}\left(M\otimes N\right)\rightarrow\text{Gr}^{i}M\otimes\text{Gr}^{j}N.

Taking again the direct sum, we obtain a morphism

\text{Gr}^{k}(M\otimes N)\rightarrow\bigoplus_{i+j=k}\text{Gr}^{i}M\otimes\text{Gr}^{j}N,

which serves as the inverse.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
M
and
N
be two
A
-modules, each equipped with exhaustive decreasing filtrations
\emph{Fil}^{\bullet}M
and
\emph{Fil}^{\bullet}N
, respectively. If
M\rightarrow N
is an isomorphism of filtered modules that induces, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, an isomorphism
\emph{Gr}^{i}M\rightarrow\emph{Gr}^{i}N
, then for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

\emph{Fil}^{i}M\simeq\emph{Fil}^{i}N.

Proof.

We proceed by induction. For
k=0
, the result is clearly true. Now, assume the statement is true for some
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
. We then have the following commutative diagram

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\text{Fil}^{k+1}M\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\text{Fil}^{k}M\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\text{Gr}^{k}M\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0}

\widehat{\text{D}}_{\underline{\sigma}}^{(\infty)}:=\left\{\sum_{\underline{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}z_{\underline{k}}\partial_{\underline{\sigma}}^{[\underline{k}]},\penalty\ z_{\underline{k}}\in A\right\}.

By duality, the commutative diagram

induces the following sequence of inclusions

\text{D}^{(\infty)}_{\underline{\sigma}}\rightarrow\text{D}^{(\eta)}_{\underline{\sigma}}\rightarrow\widehat{\text{D}}_{\underline{\sigma}}^{(\infty)}.

The right inclusion map is explicitly given by:

\varphi\mapsto\sum_{\underline{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\tilde{\varphi}_{\eta}\left(\underline{\xi}^{(\underline{k})_{\underline{\sigma}}}\right)\partial_{\underline{\sigma}}^{[\underline{k}]}.

Proposition 8.24
.

The injective map
\emph{D}^{(\eta)}_{A,\underline{\sigma}}\rightarrow\widehat{\emph{D}}^{(\infty)}_{A,\underline{\sigma}}
induces an isometric isomorphism of Banach
A
-modules

\emph{D}^{(\eta)}_{\underline{\sigma}}\rightarrow\left\{\sum_{\underline{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}z_{\underline{k}}\partial^{[\underline{k}]}_{\underline{\sigma}},\penalty\ \exists C>0,\penalty\ \forall\underline{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\penalty\ \lVert z_{\underline{k}}\rVert\leq C\eta^{|\underline{k}|}\right\}

for the sup norm

\left\lVert\sum_{\underline{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}z_{\underline{k}}\partial^{[\underline{k}]}_{\underline{\sigma}}\right\rVert_{\eta}=\sup\left\{\left\lVert z_{\underline{k}}\right\rVert/\eta^{|\underline{k}|}\right\}.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in\text{D}^{(\eta)}_{\underline{\sigma}}
. Since
\tilde{\varphi}_{\eta}
is continuous, for all
\underline{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, we have:

\left\lVert\tilde{\varphi}_{\eta}\left(\underline{\xi}^{(\underline{k})_{\underline{\sigma}}}\right)\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\tilde{\varphi}_{\eta}\right\rVert\left\lVert\underline{\xi}^{(\underline{k})_{\underline{\sigma}}}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\tilde{\varphi}_{\eta}\right\rVert\eta^{|\underline{k}|}.

The injection map
\text{D}^{(\eta)}_{\underline{\sigma}}\rightarrow\widehat{\text{D}}^{(\infty)}_{\underline{\sigma}}
has its image contained in the considered set, and its norm is at most 1.

Now, consider an element
\sum_{\underline{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}w_{\underline{k}}\partial_{\underline{\sigma}}^{[\underline{k}]}
with norm at most
C
on the right-hand side. By Proposition
8.17
, for every sequence
(z_{\underline{k}})_{\underline{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}
of elements of
A
satisfying
\lVert z_{\underline{k}}\rVert\eta^{|\underline{k}|}\rightarrow 0
, there exists a unique
\varphi\in\text{D}^{(\eta)}_{\underline{\sigma}}
such that:

\tilde{\varphi}_{\eta}\left(\sum_{\underline{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}z_{\underline{k}}\underline{\xi}^{(\underline{k})_{\underline{\sigma}}}\right)=\sum_{\underline{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}z_{\underline{k}}w_{\underline{k}}\in A,

which follows from the fact that
\lVert z_{\underline{k}}w_{\underline{k}}\rVert\leq C\lVert z_{\underline{k}}\rVert\eta^{|\underline{k}|}\rightarrow 0
. This also implies that
\lVert\varphi\rVert\leq C
. Therefore, this defines an inverse map, which is indeed an isometry.
∎
