Extended coupled SUSY, pseudo-bosons and weak squeezed states
F. Bagarello

Abstract
In this paper we consider squares of pseudo-bosonic ladder operators and we use them to produce explicit examples of eigenstates of certain operators satisfying a deformed 𝔰 ​ 𝔲 ​ ( 1 , 1 ) \mathfrak{su}(1,1) Lie algebra. We show how these eigenstates may, or may not, be square-integrable. In both cases, a notion of biorthonormality can be introduced and analyzed. Some examples are discussed in details. We also propose some preliminary results on bi-squeezed states arising from our operators.

I
Introduction

Finding eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian operator
H
of a given physical system
{\cal S}
is often the first step towards a full understanding of
{\cal S}
, and of its dynamical features. However, most of the times, this operation is not so easy. In fact, the eigensystem of a given operator can be exactly deduced only for some operators. Quite often, when
H
is
not so easy
, some approximation methods, or perturbation techniques, need to be adopted.

With the aim of finding more and more
solvable Hamiltonians
, many general strategies have been proposed along the years, studied in some details and applied to some specific physical systems. Intertwining operators, factorizable Hamiltonians, ladder operators of different nature, super-symmetric quantum mechanics (Susy qm), are just some of them. We refer to
[
1
]
-
[
14
]
for many details on these approaches, and for more references.

In this paper, we will focus on the use of ladder operators of a special kind, obeying pseudo-bosonic rules,
[
15
]
, which are essentially an extended version of the canonical commutation relations (CCR) typical of bosonic systems,
[
15
]
. These pseudo-bosonic ladder operators, often simply called
{\mathcal{D}}
-pseudo bosons
1
1

1

Here
{\mathcal{D}}
is some dense subspace of the Hilbert space where these ladder operators operate.
(
{\mathcal{D}}
-PBs), will be used in connection with a general strategy proposed in
[
16
]
, and which extends similar ideas first proposed in
[
17
]
, in which the ladder operators obey an
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
Lie algebra. The extension is useful in connection with non self-adjoint Hamiltonians of the kind considered, e.g., in
[
18

19

. Our analysis will show how to produce eigenstates of some
number-like
operators using suitable quadratic combinations of pseudo-bosonic operators. An interesting aspect of our construction is that some sort of extended biorthogonality can be established, not between functions of
{{\cal L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
, but between
compatible

. This is a feature which has already been observed in different systems, mostly connected to pseudo-bosonic operators,

An intriguing possibility that comes from the framework we shall introduce, is the derivation of a weak variant of the (bi-)squeezed states. These states naturally emerge as ground states of two pseudo-bosonic, non-self adjoint, Hamiltonians, constructed using the triplet of operators defining the extended

Remark:–
Before starting our review, it may be useful to stress that the operators involved in the rest of our discussion are unbounded. Because of that, they suffer of serious domain issues. It is well known that these issues can be usually solved in several ways. We will adopt here an implicit algebraic approach, proposed first in

[

for many more details, and to
[

]
for its explicit construction for our situation. We should also mention that another possible approach to deal with these operators is to assume that it exists a dense subspace of the Hilbert space

where the physical system is defined which is stable under the action of all the operators which, again, are relevant for us. This is indeed the point of view adopted, for instance, in

II.1
Extending CSusy

Definition 1

Let
d
,
c
,
r
and
s
be four elements of
{\cal L}^{\dagger}({\mathcal{D}})
, and let
\gamma,\delta
be two real numbers with
\delta>\gamma
. We say that
(d,c,r,s;\delta,\gamma)
define an extended coupled Susy (ECSusy), if the following equalities are satisfied:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}dc=rs+\gamma 1\!\!1,\\
cd=sr+\delta 1\!\!1,\\
\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

Here
1\!\!1
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
, and the formulas above are well defined since
{\cal L}^{\dagger}({\mathcal{D}})
is an algebra. Alternatively, see the last part of our previous remark, equation (
2.1
)
could be understood as follows:
d(cf)=r(sf)+\gamma f
and
c(df)=s(rf)+\delta f
, for all
f\in{\mathcal{D}}
. These equalities are both well defined since, if
f\in{\mathcal{D}}
, then
cf,df,sf,rf\in{\mathcal{D}}
as well, and, therefore, we also have
c(df)\in{\mathcal{D}}
, and so on.

Let us define the following operators, which are still in
{\cal L}^{\dagger}({\mathcal{D}})
:

k_{+}=\frac{1}{\delta-\gamma}ds,\qquad k_{-}=\frac{1}{\delta-\gamma}rc,\qquad k_{0}=\frac{1}{\delta-\gamma}\left(dc-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,1\!\!1\right),

(2.2)

and

l_{+}=\frac{1}{\delta-\gamma}sd,\qquad l_{-}=\frac{1}{\delta-\gamma}cr,\qquad l_{0}=\frac{1}{\delta-\gamma}\left(sr+\frac{\delta}{2}\,1\!\!1\right).

(2.3)

Using (
2.1
) we find that

[k_{0},k_{\pm}]=\pm k_{\pm},\qquad[k_{+},k_{-}]=-2k_{0}.

(2.4)

Similarly:

[l_{0},l_{\pm}]=\pm l_{\pm},\qquad[l_{+},l_{-}]=-2l_{0}.

(2.5)

Notice that
k_{+}
and
l_{+}
are not the adjoint of
k_{-}
and
l_{-}
, and
k_{0}
and
l_{0}
are not self-adjoint, as it happens for the
ordinary

\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
Lie algebra. This gives us the possibility to introduce two, in general, different families of operators,
p_{\alpha}
and
q_{\alpha}
,
\alpha=0,\pm
:

p_{0}=k_{0}^{\dagger},\qquad p_{\pm}=k_{\mp}^{\dagger};\qquad\qquad q_{0}=l_{0}^{\dagger},\qquad q_{\pm}=l_{\mp}^{\dagger}.

(2.6)

They satisfy the same commutators in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
):

[p_{0},p_{\pm}]=\pm p_{\pm},\qquad[p_{+},p_{-}]=-2p_{0};\qquad[q_{0},q_{\pm}]=\pm q_{\pm},\qquad[q_{+},q_{-}]=-2q_{0}.

(2.7)

Hence we conclude that (
2.1
) implies the existence of four (again, in general) different triples of operators obeying the same commutators of an
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
Lie algebra, but with different relations under the adjoint operation.

II.1.1
The eigenstates of a deformed
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
Lie algebra

As in
[
16
]
, let us consider here three operators,
x_{\pm}
and
x_{0}
, in
{\cal L}^{\dagger}({\mathcal{D}})
, satisfying
[x_{0},x_{\pm}]=\pm x_{\pm}
, and
[x_{+},x_{-}]=-2x_{0}
, but with
x_{+}^{\dagger}\neq x_{-}
and
x_{0}^{\dagger}\neq x_{0}
.

First we put, with a slight abuse of notation,

x^{2}=x_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(x_{+}x_{-}+x_{-}x_{+})=x_{0}^{2}+x_{0}-x_{-}x_{+}=x_{0}^{2}-x_{0}-x_{+}x_{-}.

(2.8)

We call it
an abuse
since
x^{2}
is not really the square of an operator
x
(to be identified). Also,
x^{2}
in (
2.8
) is not even positive. Nevertheless, we use this notation since it is the one usually adopted in the literature for the
ordinary

\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
Lie algebra. The operator
x^{2}
commutes with each
x_{\alpha}
:
[x^{2},x_{\alpha}]=0
, for
\alpha=0,\pm
. Now, since in particular
x^{2}
and
x_{0}
commute, we can look for common eigenstates of these two operators. Using again the same notation adopted for ordinary
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
, we assume the following: there exists a non zero vector
\Phi_{j,q_{0}}\in{\mathcal{D}}
satisfying the following eigenvalue equations:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x^{2}\Phi_{j,q_{0}}=j(j+1)\Phi_{j,q_{0}},\\
x_{0}\Phi_{j,q_{0}}=q_{0}\Phi_{j,q_{0}},\\
\end{array}\right.

(2.9)

for some
j
and
q_{0}
. We should stress that, in principle, there is no reason a priori to assume here that
j
and
q_{0}
are real or positive. This is because, as already observed,
x^{2}
is not positive or self-adjoint, and
x_{0}
is not self-adjoint. This makes in general much more complicated to describe the set of possible values of
j
and
q_{0}
in (
2.9
). However, some useful result can still be found, as we will see. In particular,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x^{2}(x_{\pm}\Phi_{j,q_{0}})=j(j+1)(x_{\pm}\Phi_{j,q_{0}}),\\
x_{0}(x_{\pm}\Phi_{j,q_{0}})=(q_{0}\pm 1)(x_{\pm}\Phi_{j,q_{0}}),\\
\end{array}\right.

(2.10)

at least if
\Phi_{j,q_{0}}\notin\ker(x_{\pm})
. This means that
x_{\pm}
are ladder operators and, in particular, that
x_{+}
is a raising while
x_{-}
is a lowering operator. Using the same standard arguments for
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
, we can also deduce that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{+}\Phi_{j,q_{0}}=(q_{0}-j)\Phi_{j,q_{0}+1},\\
x_{-}\Phi_{j,q_{0}}=(q_{0}+j)\Phi_{j,q_{0}-1}.\\
\end{array}\right.

(2.11)

These equations are in agreement with the fact that, as it is easy to check,

[x_{0},x_{-}x_{+}]=[x_{0},x_{+}x_{-}]=0.

In fact, from (
2.11
) we see that
x_{0}
and
x_{-}x_{+}
have the same eigenvectors. The same is true for
x_{0}
and
x_{+}x_{-}
.

As we have already observed in
[
16
]
, we have several possibilities:

Case 1:–
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
we have
x_{-}^{m-1}\Phi_{j,q_{0}}\neq 0
and
x_{-}^{m}\Phi_{j,q_{0}}=0
. In this case the set of eigenvalues of
x_{0}
,
\sigma(x_{0})
, is bounded below:
\sigma(x_{0})=\{q_{0}-m+1,q_{0}-m+2,q_{0}-m+3,\ldots\}
.

Case 2:–
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
we have
x_{+}^{k-1}\Phi_{j,q_{0}}\neq 0
and
x_{+}^{k}\Phi_{j,q_{0}}=0
. In this case
\sigma(x_{0})
, is bounded above:
\sigma(x_{0})=\{\ldots,q_{0}+k-3,q_{0}+k-2,q_{0}+k-1\}
.

Case 3:–
both conditions above are true. In this case, of course,
\sigma(x_{0})
, is bounded above and below:
\sigma(x_{0})=\{q_{0}-m+1,q_{0}-m+2,\ldots,q_{0}+k-2,q_{0}+k-1\}
.

Case 4:–
neither Case 1, nor Case 2, hold. Then
\sigma(x_{0})
has no bound below and above.

II.1.2
Back to ECSusy

We can use now these general results in the analysis of the operators introduced in Section
II.1
. However, this will not be the only ingredient of the procedure we are going to propose. In fact, as we will see, the natural biorthonormality connected to the appearance of non self-adjoint number-like operators will play a relevant role. We first consider the operators
k_{\alpha}
,
\alpha=0,\pm

2.9

\varphi_{j,q}\in{\mathcal{D}}
exists,
j,q\in\mathbb{C}

k^{2}\varphi_{j,q}=j(j+1)\varphi_{j,q},\qquad k_{0}\varphi_{j,q}=q\varphi_{j,q}.

Here, as in (
2.8

k^{2}=k_{0}^{2}+k_{0}-k_{-}k_{+}
, for instance. The operators

k_{+}\varphi_{j,q}=(q-j)\varphi_{j,q+1},\qquad k_{-}\varphi_{j,q}=(q+j)\varphi_{j,q-1},

for all

the set of all the

k_{-}

or
\varphi_{j,q}\notin\ker(k_{-})

{\cal F}_{\varphi}(j):=\{\varphi_{j,q},\,\forall q\in I_{j}\}
. Let then introduce
{\cal E}_{j}=l.s.\{\varphi_{j,q},\,q\in I_{j}\}
, the linear span of the vectors in
{\cal F}_{\varphi}(j)
, and
\mathcal{H}_{j}
the closure of
{\cal E}_{j}
, with respect to the norm of
\mathcal{H}
. Of course,
\mathcal{H}_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, for each fixed
j
. By construction,
{\cal F}_{\varphi}(j)
is a basis for
\mathcal{H}_{j}
. Let
{\cal F}_{\psi}(j):=\{\psi_{j,q},\,\forall q\in I_{j}\}
be its unique biorthogonal basis,
[
27
]
. Then

\langle\varphi_{j,q},\psi_{j,r}\rangle=\delta_{q,r},

(2.14)

for all
q,r\in I_{j}
, and
l.s.\{\psi_{j,q},\,q\in I_{j}\}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}_{j}
. Using (
2.6
), it is possible to check the following eigenvalue and ladder equalities:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p_{0}\psi_{j,q}=\overline{q}\psi_{j,q},\\
p_{+}\psi_{j,q}=\overline{(q+1+j)}\psi_{j,q+1},\\
p_{-}\psi_{j,q}=\overline{(q-1-j)}\psi_{j,q+1},\\
\end{array}\right.

(2.15)

at least if
\psi_{j,q}\notin\ker(p_{\pm})
. We refer to
[
16
]
for the reasons why these formulas look slightly different from those in
(
2.11
). Here we just observe that the vectors
\varphi_{j,q}
and the
\psi_{j,q}
are in fact introduced in the game in a different way: while
\varphi_{j,q}
are those vectors satisfying (
2.12
),
\{\psi_{j,q}\}
is the unique set of vectors which is biorthonormal to
\{\varphi_{j,q}\}
. This is, in fact, the reason of the difference we observe in the ladder equations.

It is useful to anticipate that, in the examples discussed in Section

{{\cal L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

A detailed example:
{\mathcal{D}}
-PBs

In
[
16
]
we have shown how

-PBs can be used to generate examples of the above framework. Some of these results will be briefly outlined in this section. In Section

First we recall what
{\mathcal{D}}
-PBs are: let
\mathcal{H}
be a given Hilbert space with scalar product
\left<.,.\right>
and related norm
\|.\|
. Let
a
and
b
be two operators
on
\mathcal{H}
, with domains
D(a)\subset\mathcal{H}
and

respectively,
a^{\dagger}
and

their adjoint, and let
{\mathcal{D}}
be a dense subspace of
\mathcal{H}

such that
a^{\sharp}{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq{\mathcal{D}}
and

. Here with

we indicate
x
or

. Of course,
{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq D(a^{\sharp})

and
{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq D(b^{\sharp})

Definition 2

The operators
(a,b)
are
{\mathcal{D}}
-pseudo bosonic if, for all

a\,b\,f-b\,a\,f=f.

(2.17)

We further require,
[
15
]

\varphi_{0},\Psi_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}
such that
a\,\varphi_{0}=b^{\dagger}\Psi_{0}=0

The invariance of

under the action of

implies
that the vectors

\varphi_{n}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\,b^{n}\varphi_{0},\qquad\Psi_{n}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\,{a^{\dagger}}^{n}\Psi_{0},

(2.18)

n\geq 0

and, as a consequence, to the domain of
a^{\sharp}
,
b^{\sharp}
and
N^{\sharp}
, where
N=ba
. Let us put
{\cal F}_{\Psi}=\{\Psi_{n},\,n\geq 0\}
and

{\cal F}_{\varphi}=\{\varphi_{n},\,n\geq 0\}

It is simple to deduce the following lowering and raising relations:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}b\,\varphi_{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\varphi_{n+1},\qquad\qquad\quad\,\,n\geq 0,\\

a^{\dagger}\Psi_{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\Psi_{n+1},\qquad\qquad\quad\,n\geq 0,\\
b^{\dagger}\Psi_{0}=0,\quad b^{\dagger}\Psi_{n}=\sqrt{n}\,\Psi_{n-1},\qquad n\geq 1,\\
\end{array}\right.

as well as the eigenvalue equations

\left<f,g\right>=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left<f,\varphi_{n}\right>\left<\Psi_{n},g\right>=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left<f,\Psi_{n}\right>\left<\varphi_{n},g\right>.

(2.21)

and we define

\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}}:=\varphi_{0}.

(2.25)

Hence we are in Case 1 of Section
II.1.1
, with
m=1
. In fact, since the spectrum of
N
is the set
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,
\sigma(k_{0})
is bounded below.

If we act
m
times with
k_{+}
on
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}}
,
m=1,2,3,\ldots
, formula (
2.13
) produces

\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=\frac{\sqrt{(2m)!}}{(2m-1)!!}\varphi_{2m},

(2.26)

where
\varphi_{2m}
are those in (
2.18
) and, with standard notation,
(2m-1)!!=1\cdot 3\cdots(2m-3)\cdot(2m-1)
, with
0!!=(-1)!!=1
. Using (
2.12
) and (
2.13
), or with a direct check, we find

k_{0}\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=\left(m+\frac{1}{4}\right)\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}},

(2.27)

and

k_{+}\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{5}{4}},\qquad k_{-}\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=m\,\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m-\frac{3}{4}}.

(2.28)

In particular, this last equality is true only if
m\geq 1
. If
m=0
we have
k_{-}\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}}=k_{-}\varphi_{0}=0
, as already noticed.

According to Section
II.1.2
, we can now define the set of linearly independent vectors
{\cal F}_{\varphi}^{(e)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)=\{\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}},\,m=0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
, and the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{-\frac{1}{4}}^{(e)}
, constructed by taking the closure of the linear span of these vectors. Here the suffix
e
stands for
even
, since only the vectors
\varphi_{2m}
belong to
{\cal F}_{\varphi}^{(e)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)
. It is clear that
\mathcal{H}_{-\frac{1}{4}}^{(e)}\subset\mathcal{H}
, since all the vectors with odd index,
\varphi_{2m+1}
, which belong to
\mathcal{H}
, do not belong to
\mathcal{H}_{-\frac{1}{4}}^{(e)}
. Hence, the set

{\cal F}_{\varphi}^{(e)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)
cannot be complete in
\mathcal{H}
, and, as a consequence, cannot be a basis for
\mathcal{H}
. Nevertheless, by construction,
\mathcal{H}_{-\frac{1}{4}}^{(e)}
is an Hilbert space as well, and
{\cal F}_{\varphi}^{(e)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)
is a basis for it. Then, see
[
27
]
, an unique biorthonormal basis
{\cal F}_{\psi}^{(e)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)=\{\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}},\,m=0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
exists, such that

\langle\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}},\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},l+\frac{1}{4}}\rangle=\delta_{m,l},

(2.29)

where the scalar product is the one in
\mathcal{H}
, and, for each
f\in\mathcal{H}_{-\frac{1}{4}}^{(e)}
,

f=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\langle\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}},f\rangle\,\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\langle\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}},f\rangle\,\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}.

(2.30)

From (
2.26
) and (
2.20
) it is clear that the vectors of this biorthonormal basis are the following:

\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=\frac{(2m-1)!!}{\sqrt{(2m)!}}\psi_{2m}.

(2.31)

Formulas (
2.15
) can now be explicitly checked, and we get

p^{2}\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=-\frac{3}{16}\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}},\qquad p_{0}\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=\left(m+\frac{1}{4}\right)\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}},

(2.32)

together with

p_{+}\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=\left(m+1\right)\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{5}{4}},\qquad p_{-}\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m-\frac{3}{4}}.

(2.33)

Once more, we stress that the difference between these ladder equations and those in (
2.28
) arises because, while the
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}
’s are introduced using directly the deformed
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
algebra, the
\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}
’s are just the unique basis which is biorthonormal to
{\cal F}_{\varphi}^{(e)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)
. However, as (
2.32
) and (
2.33
) show, these vectors are still eigenstates of
p^{2}
and
p_{0}
, and obey interesting ladder equations with respect to
p_{\pm}
, which are slightly different from those in (
2.11
).

Let us now consider the intertwining relations in (
2.16
). In the case of
{\mathcal{D}}
-PBs, these correspond to the following two equalities:

k_{0}b=b\left(k_{0}+\frac{1}{2}1\!\!1\right),\qquad k_{0}a=a\left(k_{0}-\frac{1}{2}1\!\!1\right).

(2.34)

The consequence of this kind of equalities is well known: if
\rho
is an eigenstate of
k_{0}
with eigenvalue
E
,
k_{0}\rho=E\rho
, and if
a\rho
and
b\rho
are both non zero, then

k_{0}(a\rho)=\left(E-\frac{1}{2}\right)(a\rho),\qquad k_{0}(b\rho)=\left(E+\frac{1}{2}\right)(b\rho),

which means that
a\rho
and
b\rho
are both eigenstates of
k_{0}
, but with two shifted (and different) eigenvalues,
E\pm\frac{1}{2}
. Now, since (
2.27
) shows that the eigenvalues related to different vectors
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}
and
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},l+\frac{1}{4}}
differ for integer quantities, we conclude that neither
a\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}
, nor
b\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}
, can still be of the same form
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},l+\frac{1}{4}}
, for any
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. And, in fact, this can be explicitly checked, since

a\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=\sqrt{2m}\frac{\sqrt{(2m)!}}{(2m-1)!!}\,\varphi_{2m-1},\qquad b\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=\frac{\sqrt{(2m+1)!}}{(2m-1)!!}\,\varphi_{2m+1},

(2.35)

with the agreement that
\varphi_{-1}=0
. Let us now define

\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}}:=b\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}=\frac{\sqrt{(2m+1)!}}{(2m-1)!!}\,\varphi_{2m+1},

(2.36)

for all
m\geq 0
. The reason for calling this vector in this way is because
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}}
is an eigenstate of
k_{0}
with eigenvalue
m+\frac{3}{4}
, as expected because of our previous analysis on
b\rho
:

k_{0}\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}}=\left(m+\frac{3}{4}\right)\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}},

(2.37)

We further deduce the following raising and lowering relations:

k_{+}\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}}=\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{7}{4}},\qquad k_{-}\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}}=m\,\frac{2m+1}{2m-1}\,\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m-\frac{1}{4}},

(2.38)

with the agreement that
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},-\frac{1}{4}}=0
.

It is clear that, in the same way in which
a
and
b
map
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}
into some
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},l+\frac{3}{4}}
, they also map these last vectors into the previous ones. More explicitly, we have

a\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}}=\sqrt{2m+1}\,\frac{\sqrt{(2m+1)!}}{(2m-1)!!}\,\varphi_{2m},\qquad b\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}}=\frac{\sqrt{(2m+2)!}}{(2m-1)!!}\,\varphi_{2m+2},

(2.39)

for all
m\geq 0
. Notice that the vectors in the RHS of these equalities are proportional to
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}
and to
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{5}{4}}
, see (
2.5
).

In analogy with what we have done before, we introduce now the set
{\cal F}_{\varphi}^{(o)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)=\{\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}},\,m=0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
, where
o
stands for
odd
, and the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{-\frac{1}{4}}^{(o)}
, constructed by taking the closure of the linear span of its vectors. It is clear that
\mathcal{H}_{-\frac{1}{4}}^{(e)}\cap\mathcal{H}_{-\frac{1}{4}}^{(o)}=\emptyset
, and that, together,
{\cal F}_{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right):={\cal F}_{\varphi}^{(e)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)\cup{\cal F}_{\varphi}^{(o)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)
is complete in
\mathcal{H}
, at least if the set
{\cal F}_{\varphi}
is complete, which is always the case in all the concrete examples in the literature, in our knowledge. In particular, if the
{\mathcal{D}}
-PBs are
regular
, see
[
28
]
,
{\cal F}_{\varphi}
and
{\cal F}_{\psi}
are biorthonormal Riesz bases. Hence
{\cal F}_{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)
is a Riesz basis as well.

Now, since
{\cal F}_{\varphi}^{(o)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)
is a basis for
\mathcal{H}_{-\frac{1}{4}}^{(o)}
, we can introduce an unique biorthonormal basis
{\cal F}_{\psi}^{(o)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)=\{\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}},\,m=0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
, whose vectors can be easily identified using (
2.36
) and (
2.20
). We have

\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}}=\frac{(2m-1)!!}{\sqrt{(2m+1)!}}\,\psi_{2m+1}=\frac{1}{2m+1}a^{\dagger}\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{1}{4}}.

(2.40)

It may be interesting to notice the difference in the normalization between
\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}}
and
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}}
, in terms of their
m+\frac{1}{4}
counterparts, see (
2.36
) and (
2.40
). This difference arises because we want to maintain biorthonormality of the vectors. In fact, with the choice in (
2.40
) we get

\langle\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}},\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},l+\frac{3}{4}}\rangle=\delta_{m,l},

(2.41)

where the scalar product is the one in
\mathcal{H}
, and, for each
f\in\mathcal{H}_{-\frac{1}{4}}^{(o)}
,

f=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\langle\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}},f\rangle\,\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\langle\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}},f\rangle\,\varphi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}}.

(2.42)

Repeating then what we have done for
\mathcal{H}^{(e)}
, we can consider the set
{\cal F}_{\psi}^{(o)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)=\{\psi_{-\frac{1}{4},m+\frac{3}{4}},\,m=0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
, and observe that

{\cal F}_{\psi}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right):={\cal F}_{\psi}^{(e)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)\cup{\cal F}_{\psi}^{(o)}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)
is complete in
\mathcal{H}
, or it is even a Riesz basis for
\mathcal{H}
, depending on the nature of the
{\mathcal{D}}
-PBs we are considering.

III
A general class of pseudo-bosonic operators

The main aim of this paper is to consider what happens, and what must be modified of the general settings described before, if
a
and
b
are first order differential operators of the form

a=\alpha_{a}(x)\,\frac{d}{dx}+\beta_{a}(x),\qquad b=-\frac{d}{dx}\,\alpha_{b}(x)+\beta_{b}(x),

(3.1)

for some suitable, sufficiently regular, functions
\alpha_{j}(x)
and
\beta_{j}(x)
,
j=a,b

First of all, for those
f(x)
for which
[a,b]f(x)
does make sense,
[a,b]f(x)=f(x)
if we have,
[
20
]
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha_{a}(x)\alpha_{b}^{\prime}(x)=\alpha_{a}^{\prime}(x)\alpha_{b}(x),\\
\alpha_{a}(x)\beta_{b}^{\prime}(x)+\alpha_{b}(x)\beta_{a}^{\prime}(x)=1+\alpha_{a}(x)\alpha_{b}^{\prime\prime}(x).\\
\end{array}\right.

(3.2)

In what follows, we will rewrite what has been originally deduced in
[
20
]
, implementing the following simple remark: if we restrict to functions
\alpha_{j}(x)
which are never zero, then the first condition in (
3.2
) can be rewritten as
\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\alpha_{a}(x)}{\alpha_{b}(x)}=0
, which implies that
\alpha_{a}(x)
and
\alpha_{b}(x)
must be proportional. For this reason, from now on and to simplify a little bit the notation, we will write

\alpha_{b}(x)=\alpha(x),\qquad\alpha_{a}(x)=k\alpha_{b}(x)=k\alpha(x),

(3.3)

where
k
is the proportionality constant which will always be assumed to be real and positive, from now on (
k>0
), to fix ideas. In this way we can rewrite the second equation in (
3.2
) as follows:

(\beta_{a}(x)+k\beta_{b}(x))^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\alpha(x)}+k\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x).

(3.4)

In the rest of this paper we will identify
\beta_{a}(x)
and
\beta_{b}(x)
as follows:

\beta_{a}(x)=\int\frac{dx}{\alpha(x)},\qquad\beta_{b}(x)=\alpha^{\prime}(x).

(3.5)

Of course, many other possible choices exist. The easiest alternative is when the role of
\beta_{a}(x)
and
\beta_{b}(x)
are exchanged. But we could also consider
\beta_{a}(x)=\int\frac{dx}{\alpha(x)}+\Phi(x)
and
\beta_{b}(x)=\alpha^{\prime}(x)-\Phi(x)
, for any possible choices of (sufficiently regular)
\Phi(x)
. It is clear that the freedom in choosing
\Phi(x)
makes this settings rather rich, and open the way to many possible interesting situations.

Remark:–
Notice that, fixing
\Phi(x)=0
, the operators in (
3.1
) can be rewritten as

a=k\,\alpha(x)\,\frac{d}{dx}+\beta_{a}(x),\qquad b=-\alpha(x)\,\frac{d}{dx}.

(3.6)

In what follows, we will restrict to (
3.5
), postponing our analysis on different choices of
\Phi(x)
to a future paper. However, we will keep the more general expressions in (
3.1
) for
a
and
b
, rather than (
3.6
), since this will be useful when extending our results to
\Phi(x)\neq 0
.

As in
[
20
]
we introduce now the (formal) adjoints of
a
and
b
as

a^{\dagger}=-k\frac{d}{dx}\,\overline{\alpha(x)}+\overline{\beta_{a}(x)},\qquad b^{\dagger}=\overline{\alpha(x)}\,\frac{d}{dx}+\overline{\beta_{b}(x)}.

(3.7)

The vacua of
a
and
b^{\dagger}
are the solutions of
a\varphi_{0}(x)=0
and
b^{\dagger}\psi_{0}(x)=0
, which are easily found:

\varphi_{0}(x)=N_{\varphi}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{k}\,\int\frac{\beta_{a}(x)}{\alpha(x)}\,dx\right\},\qquad\psi_{0}(x)=N_{\psi}\exp\left\{-\int\frac{\overline{\beta_{b}(x)}}{\overline{\alpha(x)}}\,dx\right\},

(3.8)

and are well defined under our assumptions on
\alpha(x)
and
\beta_{j}(x)
. Of course, these formulas simplify using (
3.5
):

\varphi_{0}(x)=N_{\varphi}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\,k}\,(\beta_{a}(x))^{2}\right\}\qquad\psi_{0}(x)=N_{\psi}\frac{1}{\overline{\alpha(x)}},

(3.9)

Here
N_{\varphi}
and
N_{\psi}
are normalization constants which will be fixed later. If we now introduce
\varphi_{n}(x)
and
\psi_{n}(x)
as usual,

\varphi_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\,b^{n}\varphi_{0}(x),\qquad\psi_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\,{a^{\dagger}}^{n}\psi_{0}(x),

(3.10)

n\geq 0
, in analogy with
[
20
]
we can prove that these functions can be rewritten as:

\varphi_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\,\pi_{n}(x)\varphi_{0}(x),\qquad\psi_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\,\sigma_{n}(x){\psi_{0}(x)},

(3.11)

n\geq 0
, where
\pi_{n}(x)
and
\sigma_{n}(x)
are defined recursively as follows:

\pi_{0}(x)=\sigma_{0}(x)=1,

(3.12)

and

\pi_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{k}\beta_{a}(x)\pi_{n-1}(x)-\frac{1}{\beta_{a}^{\prime}(x)}\,\pi_{n-1}^{\prime}(x),

(3.13)

\sigma_{n}(x)=\overline{\beta_{a}(x)}\,\sigma_{n-1}(x)-\frac{k}{\overline{\beta_{a}^{\prime}(x)}}\,\sigma_{n-1}^{\prime}(x),

(3.14)

n\geq 1
.
Is it further possible to check that these recursive formulas produce the following results, which extend those found in
[
20
]
:

\pi_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{(2k)^{n/2}}\,H_{n}\left(\frac{\beta_{a}(x)}{\sqrt{2k}}\right),\qquad\sigma_{n}(x)=\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)^{n/2}\,H_{n}\left(\frac{\overline{\beta_{a}(x)}}{\sqrt{2k}}\right),

(3.15)

being
H_{n}
the
n
-th Hermite polynomial.
If we now restrict, for simplicity, to the case of
\alpha(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the functions in (
3.11
) can be rewritten as

\varphi_{n}(x)=\frac{N_{\varphi}}{\sqrt{n!(2k)^{n}}}H_{n}\left(\frac{\beta_{a}(x)}{\sqrt{2k}}\right)e^{-\left(\frac{\beta_{a}(x)}{\sqrt{2k}}\right)^{2}},

(3.16)

and

\psi_{n}(x)=N_{\psi}\sqrt{\,\frac{1}{n!}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)^{n}}\,H_{n}\left(\frac{{\beta_{a}(x)}}{\sqrt{2k}}\right)\frac{1}{\alpha(x)}.

(3.17)

We observe that we have used here the fact that
\beta_{a}(x)
is real, with a clever choice of the integration constant in (
3.5
). Incidentally we also observe that
\beta_{a}(x)
is always increasing, since
\beta_{a}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha(x)}>0
, and
\beta_{b}(x)
is also real. The monotone behaviour of
\beta_{a}(x)
will play a role later on.

It is clear that, while
\varphi_{n}(x)
can easily be square integrable (as in all the examples considered in the remaining part of this section),
\psi_{n}(x)
is not expected to be in
{{\cal L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
except than for very special forms of
\alpha(x)
. However,
\psi_{n}(x)
and
\varphi_{m}(x)
are compatible:
\overline{\psi_{n}(x)}\varphi_{m}(x)\in{\cal L}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
for all
n,m=0,1,2,3,\ldots
, and biorthogonal. In particular, if we fix

\overline{N_{\psi}}N_{\varphi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\,k}},

(3.18)

we have

\langle\psi_{n},\varphi_{m}\rangle=\delta_{n,m},

(3.19)

so that the two families
{\cal F}_{\varphi}=\{\varphi_{n}(x)\}
and
{\cal F}_{\psi}=\{\psi_{n}(x)\}
are biorthonormal, as in (
2.20
). They also satisfy the ladder equations given in (
2.19
), and are (generalized) eigenstates of
N=ba
and
N^{\dagger}=a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}
, with eigenvalue
n
:
N\varphi_{n}(x)=n\varphi_{n}(x)
and
N^{\dagger}\psi_{n}(x)=n\psi_{n}(x)
.

After this analysis, we can now deduce the explicit form of the operators
k_{\alpha}
and
p_{\alpha}
in (
2.22
) and (
2.23
). We will not consider the operators
l_{\alpha}
and
q_{\alpha}
, since these coincide with the others. Long but straightforward computations produce
the following:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}k_{+}=\frac{1}{2}\alpha(x)\left(\alpha(x)\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\alpha^{\prime}(x)\frac{d}{dx}\right),\\
k_{-}=\frac{1}{2}\left[k^{2}\alpha^{2}(x)\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+k\alpha(x)\left(k\alpha^{\prime}(x)+2\beta_{a}(x)\right)\frac{d}{dx}+\beta_{a}^{2}(x)+k\right],\\
k_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\left[k\alpha^{2}(x)\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\alpha(x)\left(\beta_{a}(x)+k\alpha^{\prime}(x)\right)\frac{d}{dx}+\frac{1}{2}\right],\end{array}\right.

(3.20)

while

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p_{+}=\frac{1}{2}[k^{2}\alpha^{2}(x)\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+k\alpha(x)\left(3k\alpha^{\prime}(x)-2\beta_{a}(x)\right)\frac{d}{dx}+\beta_{a}^{2}(x)-k+\\
\hskip 28.45274pt+k^{2}\alpha^{\prime\,2}(x)+k^{2}\alpha(x)\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x)-2k\alpha^{\prime}(x)\beta_{a}(x)],\\
p_{-}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\alpha^{2}(x)\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+3\alpha(x)\alpha^{\prime}(x)\frac{d}{dx}+\alpha(x)\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x)+\alpha^{\prime\,2}(x)\right],\\
p_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\left[-k\alpha^{2}(x)\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\alpha(x)\left(\beta_{a}(x)-3k\alpha^{\prime}(x)\right)\frac{d}{dx}+\beta_{a}(x)\alpha^{\prime}(x)-k\alpha^{\prime\,2}(x)-k\alpha(x)\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\right],\end{array}\right.

(3.21)

with also

k^{2}=p^{2}=-\frac{3}{16}1\!\!1.

(3.22)

In what follows we will consider three different special choices of
\alpha(x)
, and we will deduce what our framework produces in these cases.

III.1
A special case: constant
\alpha(x)

We will first consider the case of a constant
\alpha(x)
:
\alpha(x)=\alpha
, a real positive constant. This is a particularly simple, but still absolutely non trivial, situation, as we will show here. First of all, using (
3.5
) and the simplest integration constant for
\beta_{a}(x)
, we have
\beta_{a}(x)=\frac{x}{\alpha}
while
\beta_{b}(x)=0
. Hence the pseudo-bosonic operators in (
3.1
) are

a=k\alpha\,\frac{d}{dx}+\frac{x}{\alpha},\qquad b=-\alpha\,\frac{d}{dx},

The operators
k_{\alpha}
and
p_{\alpha}
turn out to be

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}k_{+}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}},\qquad k_{-}=\frac{1}{2}\left[k^{2}\alpha^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+2kx\frac{d}{dx}+\left(\frac{x^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}+k\right)\right],\qquad k_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\left[k\alpha^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+x\frac{d}{dx}+\frac{1}{2}\right],\\
p_{+}=\frac{1}{2}\left[k^{2}\alpha^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}-2kx\frac{d}{dx}+\left(\frac{x^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}-k\right)\right],\qquad p_{-}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}},\qquad p_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\left[-k\alpha^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+x\frac{d}{dx}+\frac{1}{2}\right],\end{array}\right.

(3.23)

with
k^{2}
and
p^{2}
as in (
3.22
). The functions in (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) become now

\varphi_{n}(x)=\frac{N_{\varphi}}{\sqrt{n!(2k)^{n}}}H_{n}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2k}\,\alpha}\right)e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2k\alpha^{2}}},

(3.24)

and

\psi_{n}(x)=\frac{N_{\psi}}{\alpha}\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{n!}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)^{n}}\,H_{n}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2k}\,\alpha}\right).

(3.25)

It is evident that, since

IV
Some results on squeezed states

In this section, we explore the possibility of defining a weak formulation for squeezed states, which are states of great significance in physics.
As widely known, squeezed states in Quantum Mechanics can be obtained via the action of the unitary squeezing operator on the ground of the harmonic oscillator. Another way of defining a squeezed state is through the definition of a Hamiltonian operator that naturally arises, in our context, when working with the triplet of operators
k_{0},k_{-},k_{+}
defined in (
2.22
). We can in fact define the following, not self-adjoint, Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H=2\mu(z)\,k_{0}+2\lambda(z)\,k_{-}+2\lambda(\bar{z})\,k_{+}+\left(\sinh(r)-\frac{\mu(z)}{2}\right)1\!\!1,

(4.1)

where
z=re^{i\theta},\,\mu(z)=\cosh(2r),\,\lambda(z)=e^{-i\theta}\cosh(r)\sinh(r)
.

Next, if we introduce the operators

The relationship between

and a generalization of squeezed states becomes evident through the following arguments. Let us assume, for a moment, that
b=a^{\dagger}
. By performing standard computations similar to those presented in
[

, where
\mathcal{S}(z)=e^{\frac{1}{2}zb^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{z}a^{2}},\,z\in\mathbb{C}

\tau(z)
of

[
25

29
]

Refocusing on the case where
a
and
b
are defined as in equation (

), it is now natural to define a squeezed state as the ground state of
H
or, more in general, as the eigenstate relative to the null eigenvalue. We observe that the factorization
H=BA
implies that this ground state, whether it exists or can be defined in a weak sense, can be found by requiring that it is annihilated by
A

H^{\dagger}=A^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}
and it is annihilated by
B^{\dagger}
.
It is evident that the only possibility for the existence of this pair of squeezed states is to define them in a distributional sense, treating them as proper functionals on a suitable set of functions. The reason for this is that, due to the critical behavior of the two sets
{\cal F}_{\Psi}
and
{\cal F}_{\varphi}

{{\cal L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
for all

(or in some domain of convergence), of the form

\tau(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\nu_{\varphi}(z,n)\varphi_{n},\quad\kappa(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\nu_{\psi}(z,n)\psi_{n},

for some suitable choice of
\nu_{\varphi}(z,n),\nu_{\psi}(z,n)

Also, the squeezing operator becomes unbounded, and we should pay attention to, just to cite one problem, its domain. Given that, let us introduce the candidate squeezed states
\tau(z)
and
\kappa(z)
as functionals

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{c}=\left\{h(x)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}):h_{-}(x):=h\left(\beta_{a}^{-1}(\sqrt{2}x)\right)e^{x^{2}/2}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\right\}.

where

V
Conclusions

In this paper we have used the general theory of weak pseudo-bosons in the context of ECSusy. We have shown that
{\mathcal{D}}
-PBs provide examples of ladder operators obeying an extended version of
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
. In particular we have shown that ours is a
doubled extended
settings: the first extension is based on the lost of self-adjointness in going from
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
to the extended
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
considered in Section
II
. The second extension consists in leaving
{{\cal L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
, while keeping
compatibility
between eigenstates of different number-like operators connected by an adjoint operation.

Some preliminary results on squeezed states have also been discussed. In particular, we have proven that these states exist, in a weak sense, working in the compatibility setting we have introduced. This result is an intriguing aspect of the weak formulation connected with the pseudo-bosonic structures, although several key aspects still require further exploration. Primarily, we want to address the definition of
coherent squeezed
states, which, in standard quantum mechanics, are typically obtained through the combined action of squeezing and displacement operators over the ground state. Furthermore, a challenging problem regards the proper definition of these operators in a distributional settings when not dealing with the conventional
{{\cal L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
space. Notably, the recent work
[
24
]
introduced a displacement-like operator that acts on the ground states
\varphi_{0}
and
\psi_{0}
of the families
{\cal F}_{\varphi}
and
{\cal F}_{\psi}
, leaving open the problem of extending its definition to encompass the entire sets. We aim in some future works to address these open problems to improve our understanding of squeezed states in the distributional frameworks. We also plan to look for possible physical appearances of our squeezed states in (possibly) gain-loss systems, or in other systems driven by non self-adjoint Hamiltonians.
