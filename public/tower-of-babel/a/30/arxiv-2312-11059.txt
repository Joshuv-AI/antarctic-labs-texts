Tensor term logic for categorial grammars: simple unification of commutative and noncommutative structure
Sergey Slavnov

Abstract
A prototypical example of categorial grammars are those based on Lambek calculus, i.e. noncommutative intuitionistic linear logic. However, it has been noted that purely noncommutative operations are often not sufficient for modeling even very simple natural language phenomena. Therefore a number of alternative formalisms are considered in the literature: those using purely “commutative” linear logic as well as combining (to some level) commutative and non-commutative operations. The logic of tensor terms that we propose is a variant of such a combination. This logical calculus was designed specially for defining categorial grammars. It contains Lambek calculus and multiplicative linear logic as conservative fragments, yet the syntax is very simple, not departing much from that of multiplicative linear logic. The system is cut-free and decidable, it has both intuitionistic and classical versions, besides it is equipped with a simple intuitive semantics, which is sound and complete.

Lambek calculus and its variations

A prototypical example of categorial grammars are those based on Lambek calculus (

An alternative, or rather, complementary approach is to use ordinary, “commutative” multiplicative linear logic (

behave very poorly, see
[
10
]

For those reasons, it might be interesting to have formalisms
that combine (to some level) commutative and noncommutative structures. One of the options considered in the literature is to allow connectives of

Among examples of this sort one could mention

non-commutative logic

,

, as well as more recent

A somewhat different approach is taken in

[

, which is the logic of ordered string tuples (in the same sense as

First order linear logic and categorial grammars

A remarkable observation

]
is that first order (commutative) multiplicative linear logic (

or

for the intuitionistic version) can be used as a kind of grammatical formalism. In particular, both

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
. (A similar encoding for

This
suggests another approach to combining commutative and noncommutative structure, which avoids syntactic complications, staying in the limits of familiar commutative linear logic and usual sequent calculus.

The encodings are based on interpreting first order variables as denoting

. Noncommutative constructions, such as string concatenation, are represented by means of quantifiers binding variables. Typically, an

formula

has the

translation
||F||^{x,y}
parameterized by the free variables

defined by induction as

\begin{array}[]{rl}||p||^{x,y}=p(x,y),&|||A\bullet B||^{x,y}=\exists t(||A||^{x,t}\otimes||B||^{t,y}),\\
||A\backslash B||^{x,y}=\forall t(||A||^{t,x}\multimap||B||^{t,y}),&||B/A||^{x,y}=\forall t(||A||^{x,t}\multimap||B||^{y,t}),\end{array}

(1)

where

However, one might argue that the full system of

is quite redundant for the above linguistic interpretations.
Actually, only a tiny fragment of the system is used in these constructions, and the “linguistic” meaning of general
MILL1
formulas and sequents seems,
at least, obscure. Moreover,
the fragment of interest is not closed either under subformulas or proof-search. Typically, when deriving in

the translation of an

In
[

]
(elaborating on
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
) we proposed to isolate the relevant fragment of

, calling it the

, and then gave an alternative formulation for the latter. In a greater detail, we proposed
so called
extended tensor type calculus
(

a certain calculus of typed terms, called

, and is
equipped with an intrinsic deductive system and a transparent linguistic interpretation.
Then we established strict equivalence of the proposed calculus and the linguistic fragment.

In the current work, which can be seen as an upgrade of the above, we propose the

of tensor terms.
Tensor term logic
(
TTL
), is very similar to the previously defined

, but has much simpler syntax and sequent calculus, not too different from ordinary propositional

. Besides,

is equipped with a sound and complete

, similar to phase semantics of linear logic.

(On the other hand, the direct translation to the linguistic fragment of
MILL1
in the case of

is lost. Connections of
TTL
to first order linear logic remain to be understood; we make several side remarks on this subject throughout the text.)

1.3
Tensor term logic

Tensor terms

[
16
]
,
[
17
]
are, basically, tuples of words (written multiplicatively, as formal products) with labeled endpoints. We write words in square brackets and represent the endpoints as lower and upper indices, lower indices standing for right endpoints and upper indices for left ones. Thus, tensor terms can have the form

[\mbox{a}]_{i}^{j},\quad[\mbox{a}]_{i}^{j}\cdot[\mbox{b}]_{k}^{l}\cdot[\mbox{c}]_{r}^{s},\quad[\epsilon]_{i}^{j}

(where
\epsilon
stands for the empty word) and so on.

An index in a term can be repeated at most twice, once as an upper, and once as a lower one. A repeated index is called
bound
; it means that the corresponding words are concatenated along matching endpoints. For example, we have the term equality

[\mbox{a}]_{j}^{i}\cdot[\mbox{b}]_{l}^{k}\cdot[\mbox{c}]_{k}^{j}=[\mbox{acb}]_{l}^{i}

(the product is commutative). Naturally, indices that are not bound are called
free
.

In the logic of tensor terms (
TTL
), formulas or
types
denote sets of terms, and sequents denote relations between those (just as in
LC
formulas denote sets of strings and sequents denote relations between strings). The syntax of
TTL
is just that of
MLL
with the addition that formulas are decorated with indices and the sequent calculus is supplemented with a couple of rules for index manipulation. Typically, an atomic
TTL
formula
p^{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}
denotes a set of tensor terms all whose elements have
as their set of upper, respectively lower free indices the collection

i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}
, respectively
{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}
. Tensor product of types corresponds to product of terms (just as product in
LC
corresponds to concatenation of strings), and implication is the adjoint operation.

TTL
formulas can also have bound indices; these may occur in compound formulas. For example, the formula
a^{i}_{j}\otimes b^{j}_{k}
denotes the product of term sets corresponding to
a^{i}_{j}
and
b^{j}_{k}
. The latter sets have elements of the form
[u]^{i}_{j}
and
[v]^{j}_{k}
respectively; these are just strings with marked endpoints. Since the endpoints match, the product becomes concatenation, and the denotation of
a^{i}_{j}\otimes b^{j}_{k}

consists of elements of the form
[uv]^{i}_{k}
, with only two free indices. We say that the index
j
in
a^{i}_{j}\otimes b^{j}_{k}
is
bound
.

The above sketchy analysis suggests how to encode
LC
to
TTL
. In fact, the encoding is almost the same as the translation to
MILL1
in (
1
), with the only difference that we do not need cumbersome quantifiers.
The translation of an
LC
formula
F
is parameterized by two free indices according to the rules

\begin{array}[]{rl}||p||^{i}_{j}=p^{i}_{j},&|||A\bullet B||^{i}_{j}=||A||^{i}_{k}\otimes||B||^{k}_{j},\\
||A\backslash B||^{i}_{j}=||A||^{k}_{i}\multimap||B||^{k}_{j},&||B/A||^{i}_{j}=||A||^{j}_{k}\multimap||B||^{i}_{k}).\end{array}

On the other hand, when the indices in subformulas are disjoint, we obtain discontinuous operations typical for “commutative” grammars such as
ACG
. Tensor corresponds to “disjoint union” and implication produces a “hole”in a string. In this way we get both “commutative” and “noncommutative” operations available, without departing much from the simple syntax of propositional
MLL
. Cut elimination, decidability and a simple, intuitive semantics are also at hand.

{[}a_{1}\ldots a_{n}]\equiv[a_{n}a_{1}\ldots a_{n-1}]\mbox{ for }a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in T.\end{array}

(3)

Definition 2.2
.

For a tensor term or a well-formed tensor pseudo-term

, respectively

The meaning of relations (

) is as follows. Terms stand for string tuples, indices denote string endpoints. Repeated, i.e. bound, indices correspond to matching endpoints and strings are glued along them. The unit

corresponds to the empty tuple. As for singular terms, such as

Singular terms are pathological, but we need them for consistency of definitions.

One can take a more geometric view and represent elementary regular terms as directed string-labeled edges with indices denoting vertices and
elementary singular terms as closed loops labeled with cyclic words.
Edges are glued along matching vertices. General terms become graphs constructed from elementary ones by taking disjoint unions and
gluing edges along matching vertices. The title “boundary” is inspired precisely by such a view.

.

For a well-formed pseudo-term

and

Remark 2.6
.

In

[\epsilon]\equiv 1.

It should be emphasized that multiplication of terms is only partially defined: the product

{\bf TTL}^{\prime}

We define the
size of a derivation
in
{\bf TTL}^{\prime}
as follows. If the derivation
\pi
is an axiom then the size
\mathit{size}(\pi)
of
\pi
is
1
. If
\pi
is obtained from a derivation
\pi^{\prime}
by a single-premise rule then
\mathit{size}(\pi)=\mathit{size}(\pi^{\prime})+1
. If
\pi
is obtained from derivations
\pi_{1}
,
\pi_{2}
by a two-premise rule then

\mathit{size}(\pi)=\mathit{size}(\pi_{1})+\mathit{size}(\pi_{2})+1
.

Given a
{\bf TTL}^{\prime}
derivation with two subderivations
\pi_{1}
,
\pi_{2}
of the sequents

\vdash\Gamma,A
and
\vdash\overline{A},\Delta
respectively followed by the Cut rule, we say that this application of the Cut rule is a
principal cut
if

A=F\otimes G
and

\pi_{1}
, respectively
\pi_{2}
, ends with the
(\otimes_{\equiv\alpha})
, respectively the
(\invamp_{\equiv\alpha})
rule introducing formula
A
, respectively
\overline{A}
. An application of the Cut rule that is not principal is a
side cut
.

Lemma 5.4
.

There is an algorithm transforming a
{\bf TTL}^{\prime}
derivation
\pi
of a sequent
\vdash\Gamma
to a derivation
\pi^{\prime}
of some sequent
\vdash\Gamma^{\prime}
such that
\vdash\Gamma
and
\vdash\Gamma^{\prime}
are
\alpha
-equivalent,
\mathit{size}(\pi^{\prime})=\mathit{size}(\pi)
and all cuts in
\pi^{\prime}
principal.

Let
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}
be all subderivations of
\pi
ending with side cuts and
N=\sum\mathit{size}(\pi_{i})
. We will use
N
as the induction parameter for proving termination of the algorithm.

The algorithm consists in permuting a side cut with the preceding rule, which may be accompanied with tours of index renaming. There are two types of side cuts. First type is when the last rule in each of the two subderivations does not involve the cut-formula at all. The second type is when the last rule in one of the two subderivations is the
(\bot^{\to})
or the
(\bot_{\leftarrow})
rule modifying the cut-formula.

A generic example for the first type is shown in Figure
, where we have an application of the
(\invamp_{\equiv\alpha})
rule to the sequent
\vdash\Gamma,F,G,A
followed by a side cut with the sequent
\vdash\overline{A},\Delta

F,G
and
\Delta
have common free indices. We cannot cut the premise
\vdash\Gamma,F,G,A
of the
(\invamp_{\equiv_{\alpha}})

\vdash\overline{A},\Delta
of the side cut, because this would introduce forbidden repetitions of indices. Therefore we use Proposition
5.3
and replace the derivation of
\vdash\overline{A},\Delta

\alpha

\vdash\overline{A},\Delta^{\prime}

\alpha

The induction parameter
N

A generic example for the second type is shown in Figure

(\bot_{\leftarrow})

\vdash\overline{A},\bot^{j}_{i},\Delta

\vdash\Gamma,A^{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}

Again, after the transformation we obtain a derivation whose conclusion is
\alpha

6
Decidability

Although
TTL
is cut-free, it does not satisfy the subformula property in the direct sense, unless we agree
that the counit
\bot^{j}_{i}
is a subformula of everything, which seems rather exotic. The reason is in the reduction rule
(\bot_{\leftarrow})
. This rule also makes decidability of the system rather non-obvious. Our current goal is to show that applications of this rule can be heavily restricted, which makes proof-search finite and the system decidable.

Definition 6.1
.

An application

\cfrac{\vdash\Gamma,A,\bot^{j}_{i}\quad j\in FI_{\bullet}(A)}{\vdash\Gamma,A_{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}}

of the
(\bot_{\leftarrow})
rule in a
TTL
proof is
essential
if there is a bound occurrence of the index
j
in
\Gamma
. Otherwise it is
inessential
.

Our first goal is to show that inessential applications of the rule can be eliminated from the derivation.

Proposition 6.2
.

Assume that
\vdash\Gamma
is a sequent
derivable in
{\bf TTL}
with a cut-free derivation
\pi
and the index
j
occurs in
\Gamma
freely and has no bound occurrences. Let
i
be a fresh index.
The sequent
\vdash\Gamma^{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}_{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}
is derivable in
TTL
with a derivation
\pi^{\prime}
, which is obtained from
\pi
by replacing the index
j
with
i
.

Proof
Induction on
\pi
. The essential step is the
(\otimes)
rule.

Assume that
\pi
is obtained from derivations
\pi_{1}
,
\pi_{2}
with conclusions
\vdash\Gamma_{1}
,
\vdash\Gamma_{2}
respectively by the
(\otimes)
rule. Every occurrence of
j
in
\vdash\Gamma
comes from one of the premises
\vdash\Gamma_{1}
,
\vdash\Gamma_{2}
. Assume for definiteness that the upper free occurrence of
j
in
\vdash\Gamma
comes from
\vdash\Gamma_{1}
. There is also a lower free occurrence of
j
in
\vdash\Gamma_{1}
, and since there are no bound occurrences of
j
in
\vdash\Gamma
the latter occurrence remains free in
\vdash\Gamma
. It follows that both occurrences of
j
in
\vdash\Gamma
come from
\vdash\Gamma_{1}
. Also, there is no bound occurrence of
j
in
\vdash\Gamma_{1}
, because otherwise it would be inherited by
\Gamma
. Thus the induction hypothesis can be applied to
\Gamma_{1}
and
\pi_{1}
, and we have a derivation
\pi_{1}^{\prime}
of
\vdash(\Gamma_{1})^{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}_{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}
obtained from
\pi_{1}
by replacing
j
with
i
. The desired derivation
\pi^{\prime}
of
\vdash\Gamma^{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}_{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}
is obtained from
\pi_{1}^{\prime}
and
\pi_{2}
by the
(\otimes)
rule.
\Box

Proposition 6.3
.

Let a sequent
\vdash\Gamma,A,\bot^{j}_{i}
, where
j\in FI_{\bullet}(A)
,
i\not\in FI^{\bullet}(A)\cup FI_{\bullet}(A)
and there is no bound occurrence of
j
in
\Gamma
, be derivable in
TTL
with a cut-free derivation
\pi
.
The sequent
\vdash\Gamma,A_{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}

is derivable in
{\bf TTL}
with a cut-free derivation of the size smaller than that of
\pi
.

Proof
Induction on the size of
\pi
.

Assume that
\pi
is obtained from a derivation
\pi^{\prime}
by the
(\bot^{\to})
introducing the formula
\bot^{j}_{i}
.
This means
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\prime},B^{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}
, and
\pi^{\prime}
has the conclusion

\vdash\Gamma^{\prime},B,A
, where
j\in FI^{\bullet}(B)
,
i\not\in FI^{\bullet}(B)\cup FI_{\bullet}(B)
.
Also,
i
does not occur in
\Gamma^{\prime}
, otherwise the sequent
\vdash\Gamma,A,\bot^{j}_{i}
is not well-formed.
By the preceding proposition
we have a derivation
\pi^{\prime\prime}
of
\vdash\Gamma^{\prime},B^{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]},A_{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}
obtained from
\pi^{\prime}
by replacing
j
with
i
. But the conclusion of
\pi^{\prime\prime}
is precisely the desired sequent
\vdash\Gamma,A_{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}
and the size of
\pi^{\prime\prime}
equals the size of
\pi^{\prime}
, which is strictly smaller than the size of
\pi
.

Assume that the last rule in
\pi
is
(\bot_{\leftarrow})
modifying one of the formulas
\bot^{j}_{i}
or
A
. Assume for definiteness that the

(\bot_{\leftarrow})
rule modifies the formula
A
(the other case being very similar).
Then the premise of the
(\bot_{\leftarrow})
rule is a sequent
\vdash\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
of the form
\vdash\Gamma^{\prime},A_{[\nicefrac{{k}}{{j}}]},\bot^{k}_{j}
, where
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma,\bot^{j}_{i}
, and the derivation
\pi^{\prime}
of
\vdash\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
has the size
\mathit{size}(\pi^{\prime})=\mathit{size}(\pi)-1
. Then, by the induction hypothesis
the sequent
\vdash\Gamma^{\prime},A
,
is derivable with a cut-free derivation
\pi^{\prime\prime}
with the size

\mathit{size}(\pi^{\prime\prime})<\mathit{size}(\pi^{\prime})<\mathit{size}(\pi)
, and, applying the induction hypothesis once again, the desired sequent
\vdash\Gamma,A_{[\nicefrac{{i}}{{j}}]}
is derivable with a cut-free derivation
\rho
with
\mathit{size}(\rho)<\mathit{size}(\pi^{\prime\prime})<\mathit{size}(\pi)
.

The case of the
(\otimes)
rule is very similar to that of the
(\otimes)
rule in the proof of the preceding proposition.
Other cases are very easy.
\Box

Corollary 6.4
.

Any
TTL
derivable sequent is derivable with a cut-free derivation where all applications of the
(\bot_{\leftarrow})
rule are essential.
\Box

Thus, when searching for a
TTL
derivation of a tensor sequent we may restrict to cut-free derivations where all applications of the
(\bot_{\leftarrow})
rule are essential. In such a derivation premises of any rule are built from subformulas of the conclusion and, possibly, counits
\bot^{j}_{i}
, where the indices
i,j
occur in the conclusion. In particular there are only finitely many possible rule applications with a given conclusion. In order to guarantee that the proof-search stops we introduce a specific complexity measure of the sequent.

Let the
complexity

c(A)
of a formula
A
be defined by

c(A)=1\mbox{ for }A\mbox{ atomic},~c(A_{1}\otimes A_{2})=c(A_{1}\invamp A_{2})=c(A_{1})+c(A_{2})+1.

Let us say that an index
j
is
suspicious
for the sequent
\vdash\Gamma
if there is a bound occurrence of
j
in
\Gamma
, but the formula
\bot^{j}_{i}
does not occur in
\Gamma
for any
i
. Let
d(\Gamma)
be the number of indices suspicious for
\vdash\Gamma
. Finally, let the
complexity

c(\Gamma)
of
\vdash\Gamma
be defined as
c(\Gamma)=\sum\limits_{A\in\Gamma}c(A)+2d(\Gamma)
. Then if
\vdash\Gamma
is a conclusion of any
TTL
rule that is not a cut or an inessential application of the
(\bot_{\leftarrow})
rule, the premises of the rule have complexity strictly less than
\vdash\Gamma
. This provides us with a decision procedure for
TTL
and, by conservativity, for
ITTL
as well.

Corollary 6.5
.

The systems
TTL
and
ITTL
are decidable.
\Box

7
Semantics

We have already sketched the “naive” semantics for
ITTL
, which is sound, but whose completeness is under question. In this section we slightly refine the above and define semantics for both
TTL
and
ITTL
, which is definitely complete when the language is balanced. (Completeness will be proven in the end of the paper.)

7.1
Semantic types

Definition 7.1
.

Given a terminal alphabet
T
, a
semantic pretype

A
over
T
is a set
A

of tensor terms (over
T
) having common boundary, which is called the
boundary
of
A
. We denote the boundary of
A
as
FI(A)
and its two components as
FI^{\bullet}(A)
and
FI_{\bullet}(A)
.

In order to define a system of semantic
types
that will interpret tensor formulas we need to choose a special pretype
\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot
with empty boundary.

Definition 7.2
.

A
semantic type system
consists of a terminal alphabet
T
and a pretype
\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot
over
T
, called the
dualizing type
, satisfying
FI^{\bullet}(\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot)=FI_{\bullet}(\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot)=\emptyset
.

Given a semantic type system
(T,\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot)
and a pretype
A
over
T
, the
dual

\overline{A}
of
A
is the set of terms (over
T
) defined by

\overline{A}=\{s|~\forall t\in A~ts\in\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot\}
.

The pretype
A
is a
semantic type
if
A=\overline{\overline{A}}
.

In the following we assume that a semantic type system is given. We will omit the prefix “semantic”, unless it leads to a confusion.

Proposition 7.3
.

(i)

For any pretype
A
we have
FI(\overline{A})=(FI_{\bullet}(A),FI^{\bullet}(A))
.

(ii)

If
A,B
are pretypes and
A\subseteq B
then
\overline{B}\subseteq\overline{A}
.

(iii)

A pretype
A
is a type iff
A=\overline{B}
for some pretype
B
.

(iv)

The dualizing type
\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot=\overline{\{1\}}
is indeed a type.
\Box

7.1.1
Multiplicative operations

Definition 7.4
.

Let
A
,
B
be types such that
FI^{\bullet}(A)\cap FI_{\bullet}(B)=FI_{\bullet}(A)\cap FI^{\bullet}(B)=\emptyset
. The
internal hom type

A\multimap B
is the set

A\multimap B=\{t|~\forall s\in A~ts\in B\}.

Definition 7.5
.

Let
A
,
B
be types such that
FI^{\bullet}(A)\cap FI^{\bullet}(B)=FI_{\bullet}(A)\cap FI_{\bullet}(B)=\emptyset
. The
product pretype

A\cdot B
, the
tensor product type

A\otimes B
and the
cotensor product type
are given by

\begin{array}[]{c}A\cdot B=\{ts|~t\in A,s\in B\},~A\invamp B=\overline{\overline{A}\cdot\overline{B}},~A\otimes B=\overline{\overline{A\cdot B}},\end{array}

Proposition
7.3
, clause (iii) guarantees that tensor and cotensor products of types return types (and not just pretypes).
That the the internal hom is also a type, follows from the simple proposition below.

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
A,B
be types with
FI^{\bullet}(A)\cap FI^{\bullet}(B)=FI_{\bullet}(A)\cap FI_{\bullet}(B)=\emptyset
. We have

(i)

FI(A\otimes B)=FI(A\invamp B)=((FI^{\bullet}(A)\cup FI^{\bullet}(B))\setminus X,(FI_{\bullet}(A)\cup FI_{\bullet}(B))\setminus X)
,
where
X=(FI^{\bullet}(A)\cap FI_{\bullet}(B))\cup(FI_{\bullet}(A)\cap FI^{\bullet}(B))
;

(ii)

A\otimes B=\overline{\overline{B}\invamp\overline{A}}
,
A\invamp B=\overline{\overline{B}\otimes\overline{A}}
;

(iii)

A\multimap B=\overline{A\cdot\overline{B}}=\overline{A}\invamp B
.
\Box

Note that tensor and cotensor products of types are partial operations. In addition, they are, generally speaking,
non-associative
. If we have types
A,B,C
with
FI^{\bullet}(A)\cap FI_{\bullet}(B)\cap FI^{\bullet}(C)
nonempty, then, as is seen from the preceding proposition, clause (i), the types
(A\otimes B)\otimes C
and
A\otimes(B\otimes C)
(assuming that they are well-defined) even have different boundaries. We will assume the convention
similar to the one assumed for iterated product of terms (in Definition
2.7
).

Definition 7.7
.

The expression
X_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes X_{n}
denotes the iterated tensor product of types satisfying the condition

FI^{\bullet}(X_{i})\cap FI^{\bullet}(X_{j})=FI_{\bullet}(X_{i})\cap FI_{\bullet}(X_{j})=\emptyset

for all
i\not=j
. Similarly for cotensor product.

In the sequel, when writing tensor and cotensor products of types we will implicitly assume that the operations are well-defined and, in the case of iterated products, the convention from Definition
7.7
applies. This makes expressions with iterated products unambiguous.

Proposition 7.8
.

(
i
)

If
A,B,C
are types and the tensor/cotensor products below are defined then

\begin{array}[]{c}A\invamp(B\invamp C)=\overline{\overline{A}\cdot\overline{B}\cdot\overline{C}},\quad A\otimes(B\otimes C)=\overline{\overline{A\cdot B\cdot C}}.\end{array}

(
ii
)

Tensor and cotensor product of types are associative whenever defined and the convention from Definition
7.7
applies.

(
iii
)

A term
t
is in

Definition 7.9
.

Given two indices

,

, the

and the

Given a type

, we define the

.

Similarly, if

Proposition 7.10
.

Given a type
A
and indices
x\in FI^{\bullet}(A)
,
y\not\in FI^{\bullet}(A)\cup FI_{\bullet}(A)
, we have

(i)

A^{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}=\overline{\overline{A}_{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}}
, in particular,
A^{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}
is a type;

(ii)

A^{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}=A\otimes{\bf 1}^{y}_{x}=\bot^{y}_{x}\invamp A
.

Similarly, if
x\in FI_{\bullet}(A)
,
y\not\in FI^{\bullet}(A)\cup FI_{\bullet}(A)
, we have

A_{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}=\overline{\overline{A}^{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}}
and

A_{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}=A\otimes{\bf 1}_{y}^{x}=\bot_{y}^{x}\invamp A
.

Proof

(i)

Let
t\in\overline{\overline{A}_{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}}
. Pick
k\in\overline{A}
and let

k^{\prime}=k_{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}
.
Then
k^{\prime}\in\overline{A}_{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}
and
tk^{\prime}\in\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot
.
Let
t^{\prime}=t^{[\nicefrac{{x}}{{y}}]}
.
From term congruence relations (
3
) we have that
t^{\prime}k=t^{\prime}k^{\prime}_{[\nicefrac{{x}}{{y}}]}=t^{[\nicefrac{{x}}{{y}}]}k^{\prime}_{[\nicefrac{{x}}{{y}}]}=tk^{\prime}
, hence
t^{\prime}k\in\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot
. Since
k\in\overline{A}
was arbitrary, we get that
t^{\prime}\in\overline{\overline{A}}=A
. But
t=(t^{\prime})^{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}
, so
t\in A^{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}
. Since
t\in\overline{\overline{A}_{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}}
was arbitrary we get
\overline{\overline{A}_{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}}\subseteq A^{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}
. The opposite inclusion is proven analogously.

(ii)

If
x\in FI^{\bullet}(t)
and
y
does not occur in
t
then
t^{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}=t\delta^{y}_{x}
. Hence
A^{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}\subseteq A\cdot{\bf 1}^{y}_{x}\subseteq A\otimes{\bf 1}^{y}_{x}
.

If
t\in\bot^{y}_{x}\invamp A
then by Proposition
7.6
, clause (iii) we have that
\delta_{y}^{x}t\in A
. But

\delta_{y}^{x}t=t^{[\nicefrac{{x}}{{y}}]}
, hence
t^{[\nicefrac{{x}}{{y}}]}\in A
. It follows that
t\in A^{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}
. Thus
\bot^{y}_{x}\invamp A\subseteq A^{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}\subseteq A\otimes{\bf 1}^{y}_{x}
.

By the identical reasoning we get
\overline{A}\invamp\bot^{x}_{y}\subseteq\overline{A}_{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}\subseteq{\bf 1}^{x}_{y}\otimes\overline{A}
, which yields by duality (i.e. by Proposition
7.3
, clause (ii)) that
A\otimes{\bf 1}^{y}_{x}\subseteq A^{[\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}]}\subseteq\bot^{y}_{x}\invamp A
.
\Box

8.3

8.3.1
MLL
-like fragment

Definition 8.15
.

A tensor formula is

{\bf 1}^{i}_{j}

and has no bound indices. The
MLL
-like fragment

Proposition 8.16
.

If an
MLL

(\bot_{\leftarrow})
and the

Proof
By Corollary

, if
\vdash\Gamma
has a derivation in
TTL
then it has a cut-free derivation where all applications of the
(\bot_{\leftarrow})
rule are essential. But a cut-free derivation of

\Gamma

does not contain counits, and since the derivation is cut-free and does not use the

rule, it does not use the
(\bot^{\to})
rule, which introduces counits, either.
\Box

We see that the
MLL
-like fragment uses only rules of

, which suggests that the above fragment and the system of
MLL
should be translatable to each other. And indeed there exists a translation from
MLL
to the
MLL
-like fragment, however the input of the translation is not a provable

sequent, but a pair: an
MLL
sequent together with its derivation. “Morally” the situation is as follows. Given an
MLL

\vdash\Gamma

MLL
-like fragment. Different successful decorations of

9.1

Definition 9.1
.

A classical, respectively intuitionistic

is an expression of the form

, where

is a classical, respectively intuitionistic formula with the same boundary:

is regular, respectively lexical.
A typing judgement of the form

is a

. The set of all typing axioms is denoted as

.

The intended semantics of the typing judgement

is that

. With such a semantics, typing axioms are always valid, and this explains the title “axiom”.

Definition 9.2

A classical, respectively intuitionistic,

is a pair

, where

\mathit{Lex}
, the

or

,
and

, the

, is a positive literal of valency
(1,1)
.

is

, notation

t_{(1)}\rhd A_{(1)},\ldots,t_{(n)}\rhd A_{(n)}
of
\mathit{Lex}\cup\mathit{TpAx}
such that

and

(i.e., in the classical case,

)
is derivable in

, respectively
ITTL
. (The number

may equal zero, which means that

and the sequent
\vdash A
is derivable in
TTL
,
respectively
ITTL
.
)

The
language

generated by

or, simply, the

of

.

Typing judgements can be understood as a special kind of sequents and grammars as systems of nonlogical axioms (this will be discussed shortly below). In particular, we can define

-equivalence of typing judgements.

\vdash\overline{\bf t},A

{\bf t}\vdash A

The above definition is, strictly speaking, ambiguous, because the sequent
\vdash\overline{\bf t},A

9.3
Derivations of typing judgements

Definition 9.11
.

A
lexical
sequent is a sequent in the (classical) extended language of the form
\vdash\overline{\bf t},A
, where
A
is a formula in the base language,
t
is a lexical term and the representative of
\overline{\bf t}
is a context consisting of formulas of the form

, where
a\in T
(i.e. it does not contain counits
\bot^{i}_{j}

A
sequent lexicon
is a set of lexical sequents closed under
\alpha
-equivalence.

Obviously, lexical sequents represent lexical typing judgements and the lexicon of a tensor grammar gives rise to a sequent lexicon. (Remember our convention that lexicons of tensor grammars are closed under
\alpha
-equivalence.)

Proposition 9.12
.

Let
\Xi
be a sequent lexicon and assume that the sequent
\vdash\Gamma
is

derivable from elements of
\Xi

\pi

\vdash\Gamma
from elements of
\Xi
such that for any application of the Cut rule in

one of the two premises
is an element
\vdash\overline{\bf t},A
of
\Xi
and the cut-formula is

.

Proof

This follows directly from Lemma
5.9
.
\Box

Lemma 9.13
.

Let
\Xi
be a sequent lexicon. A sequent of the form
\vdash\overline{\bf t},\Gamma
, where

\vdash\overline{\bf t_{(1)}},A_{(1)},\ldots,\vdash\overline{\bf t_{(n)}},A_{(n)}
of
\Xi

Proof
For the “if” direction use the Cut rule, for the “only if” direction use induction on
a derivation of
\vdash\overline{\bf t},\Gamma
from Proposition
9.12

The most important part is the base of induction. Let the sequent
\vdash\Pi

\Pi=\overline{\bf t},\Gamma

\Xi

\Pi
has a representation
\Pi=\overline{{\bf t}_{(1)}},A_{(1)}

t_{(1)}
and does not contain counits, while

is a formula in the base language. There are two possibilities. The first one is that
\overline{\bf t}=\overline{{\bf t}_{(1)}}

Induction steps are straightforward.

\Box

Corollary 9.14

Given a classical, respectively intuitionistic, tensor grammar

We consider the classical case.

If

Assume that
\vdash\overline{\bf t},A

Lex

Since derivability

or in a tensor grammar

and

are closed under
\alpha
-equivalence of sequents, respectively typing judgements. By Lemma

\vdash\overline{\bf t_{(1)}},A_{(1)},\ldots,\vdash\overline{\bf t_{(n)}},A_{(n)}

{\bf Lex}
and indices

10
Completeness of tensor term semantics

In this section we establish completeness of
TTL
and, consequently, of
ITTL
in a balanced language for the tensor term semantics. We will discuss the classical system, the intuitionistic case will follow by conservativity.
We assume that we are given a classical tensor language generated by a finite set of balanced positive literals. We also assume that we have an
infinite
alphabet
T
of terminal symbols. We will extensively use the
extended language
as discussed in Sections
9.2
,
9.3
.

10.1
Canonical lexicon

Definition 10.1
.

A set
\mathbf{Lex}
of lexical sequents in the (classical) extended language is a
canonical lexicon
if the following hold:

(i)

for any formula
A
of the base language there are infinitely many elements of the form
\vdash\overline{\bf t},A
in
\mathbf{Lex}
,

(ii)

if
\vdash\overline{\bf t},A
,
\vdash\overline{\bf s},A^{\prime}
are elements of
\mathbf{Lex}
that are not
\alpha
-equivalent, then for any two terminal symbols
a,b\in T
such that
\overline{\bf a}^{i}_{j}\in\overline{\bf t}
,
\overline{\bf b}^{k}_{l}\in\overline{\bf s}
it holds that
a\not=b
;

(iii)

the set
\mathbf{Lex}
is closed under
\alpha
-equivalence of sequents.

The elements of
\mathbf{Lex}
will serve as “axioms” describing the model.

Proposition 10.2
.

Canonical lexicons exist.

Proof

A canonical lexicon can be constructed in countably many steps as follows. Choose an enumeration of the set
\mathit{Fm}(\mathit{Lit}_{+})\times\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\mathbf{Lex}_{0}=\emptyset
.
On the
i
th step consider the
i
th element
(A,n)
. Let
FI(A)=(\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\},\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\})
. If
k>0
, choose pairwise distinct terminal symbols
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\in T

not occurring in
\mathbf{Lex}_{i-1}
and define
\mathbf{Lex}_{i}
as
\mathbf{Lex}_{i-1}
plus the sequent

\vdash\overline{\bf a_{1}}^{i_{1}}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\overline{\bf a_{k}}^{i_{k}}_{j_{k}},A
. If
k=0
, choose
a,b\in T
not occurring in
\mathbf{Lex}_{i-1}
and define

\mathbf{Lex}_{i}
as
\mathbf{Lex}_{i-1}
plus the sequent

\vdash\overline{\bf a}^{i}_{j},\overline{\bf b}^{j}_{i},A
.
Finally, define
\mathbf{Lex}
as the closure of
\bigcup\limits_{i}\mathbf{Lex}_{i}
under
\alpha
-equivalence of sequents.

\Box

Proposition 10.3
.

If
\vdash\Gamma
is a sequent in the base language
TTL
derivable from elements of a canonical lexicon
\mathbf{Lex}
then
\vdash\Gamma
is derivable in
TTL
.

Proof
The statement is a trivial case of Lemma
9.13
.
\Box

.

Let
\vdash\Gamma
be a sequent in the extended language

derivable from elements of a canonical lexicon
\mathbf{Lex}
. The formula
\overline{\bf a}^{i}_{j}
, where

\vdash{\overline{\bf t}},A
of
\mathbf{Lex}

\Box

.

If
\vdash\overline{\bf t},A

\mathbf{Lex}
, where

10.2
Canonical model

Definition 10.6
.

Given a canonical lexicon
\mathbf{Lex}
, the
canonical type system
is defined by the dualizing type

\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot=\{t|~\vdash\overline{\bf t}\mbox{ is derivable from }\mathbf{Lex}\}

(9)

The
canonical model
of

is defined by the valuation

||A||=\{t|~\vdash\overline{\bf t},A\mbox{ is derivable from }\mathbf{Lex}\},

(10)

where

A=p^{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{k}}
,
p\in\mathit{Lit}_{+}
,
v(p)=(k,k)
.

It is easy to check that valuation (

TTL
(Definition
7.11
).

Lemma 10.7
.

In the setting of the canonical model, formula (
10
) holds for any formula
A
of the base language.

Proof
Induction on
A
, where we use that any formula of the base language can be constructed from atomic ones of the form

A=p^{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{k}}
, where
p\in\mathit{Lit}_{+}
, and
A=\bot^{i}_{j}
by means of the operations
\overline{(.)}
and
\invamp
.

Base: we only need to consider the case
A=\bot^{i}_{j}
. If the sequent
\vdash\overline{\bf t},\bot^{i}_{j}
is derivable from
\mathbf{Lex}
then, by definition (
9
), we have that

t\delta^{j}_{i}\in\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot
, hence
t\in\overline{\{\delta^{j}_{i}\}}=\bot^{i}_{j}=||\bot^{i}_{j}||
. If, on the other hand,
t\in||\bot^{i}_{j}||=\bot^{i}_{j}
then
t\delta^{j}_{i}\in\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot
, which, again by definition (
9
), means precisely that the sequent
\vdash\overline{\bf t},\bot^{i}_{j}
is derivable from
\mathbf{Lex}
. The base of induction is proven.

Let
A=\overline{B}
, and assume that the statement holds for
B
. Assume that the sequent
\vdash\overline{\bf t},A
is derivable from
\mathbf{Lex}
. Pick any

s\in||B||
. By the induction hypothesis, we have that the sequent
\vdash\overline{\bf s},B
is derivable from
\mathbf{Lex}
.
Using the Cut rule we get that the sequent
\vdash\overline{\bf t},\overline{\bf s}
, i.e.
\vdash\overline{\bf t\cdot s}
, is derivable from
\mathbf{Lex}
as well, hence
ts\in\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot
. Since

was arbitrary we conclude that
t\in\overline{\overline{||B||}}=||A||
. Now assume that
t\in||A||
.
It follows from Definition
10.1
that there exists an element

\vdash\overline{\bf s},B
in
\mathbf{Lex}
such that
t
and
s
have no common literals. By the induction hypothesis
\overline{\bf s}\in||B||
and it follows that
ts\in\bot\!\!\!\!\!\!\bot
. This means that the sequent
\vdash\overline{\bf t\cdot s}
, i.e.
\vdash\overline{\bf t},\overline{\bf s}
, is derivable from

. Then by Proposition
10.5
the sequent
\vdash\overline{\bf t},\overline{B}

The step

\Box

Corollary 10.8
.

The sequent
\vdash\Gamma
in the base language is valid in a canonical model if it is derivable in
TTL
.

Proof
Let
\Gamma=A_{(1)},\ldots,A_{(n)}
. The sequent
\vdash\Gamma
is valid in the canonical model iff

\vdash A_{(1)}\invamp\ldots\invamp A_{(n)}

Corollary 10.9

(Completeness Theorem)
.

A sequent in a balanced classical, respectively intuitionistic tensor language is derivable in

, respectively

iff it is valid in any tensor term model.
\Box

11
Conclusion and future work

We proposed a simple logical system,
TTL
, which combines commutative and non-commutative operations of linear logic, and
is designed specially for defining categorial grammars.

TTL
contains cyclic logic and Lambek calculus (allowing empty antecedents) as conservative fragments. It also allows encoding derivations of ordinary (commutative) multiplicative linear logic. The logic is cut-free and decidable, it has intuitionistic and classical versions and is equipped with a simple intuitive semantics, which is sound and complete. We also defined categorial grammars based on
TTL
.

A number of topics are left for future research. Among them are complexity of the calculus, algorithms for proof-search and grammar parsing, proof-nets. We plan to study representation of known grammatical formalism, such as
ACG
and
HTLCG
, in
TTL
. Also, connections with first order linear logic remain to be understood. We also think that there are some interesting extensions and modifications of the calculus. Can we add additive connectives? Can we add some structure that will allow representing
ordered
tuples of strings and obtain a conservative representation of displacement calculus? We think that other research questions might arise as well, because the setting seems to us rather rich.
