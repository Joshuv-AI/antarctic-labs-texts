Collisional corrections to spin polarization from quantum kinetic theory using Chapman-Enskog expansion
Shuo Fang, Shi Pu

Abstract
We have investigated the collisional corrections to the spin polarization pseudo-vector, δ ​ 𝒫 μ \delta\mathcal{P}^{\mu} , using quantum kinetic theory in Chapman-Enskog expansion. We derive the spin Boltzmann equation incorporating Møller scattering process. We further consider two distinct scenarios using hard thermal loop approximations for simplification. In scenario (I), the vector charge distribution function is treated as off-equilibrium under the validity domain of gradient expansion. Remarkably, the polarization induced by thermal vorticity and shear viscous tensors are modified, but δ 𝒫 μ \delta\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{ }}^{\mu} in this scenario does not depend on the coupling constant. In scenario (II), the vector charge distribution function is assumed to be in local thermal equilibrium. Then collisional corrections δ 𝒫 μ \delta\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{ }}^{\mu} in this scenario are at 𝒪 ( ℏ 2 ∂ 2 ) \mathcal{O}(\hbar^{2}\partial^{2}) . Additionally, we evaluate the δ ​ 𝒫 μ \delta\mathcal{P}^{\mu} using relaxation time approach for comparative analysis. Our results establish the theoretical framework necessary for the future numerical investigations on the interaction corrections to spin polarization.

II.1
Brief review of quantum kinetic theory for massless fermions

In this work, we consider a dilute fermionic gas with fermion elastic scattering. We focus on the quantum
electrodynamics (QED) interactions rather than the quantum chromodynamics (QCD) interactions. The reason is as follows.
First, the QED interaction shares some basic properties of the gauge theories
[
126
]
, e.g. the logarithm-type infrared divergence of the damping rate
[
127
]
, same coupling dependence of the relaxation time for the transport coefficients (e.g. see Ref.
[
128

for the calculations on the shear viscosity and charge conductivity) and others.
Second, full QCD calculations require incorporating quantum corrections to the gluon propagator within the QKT framework for vector fields. While the quantum Wigner function for the photon field has been studied in the Coulomb gauge
[
106
,
107
]
, even its equilibrium form remains incompletely understood. This lack of understanding further complicates the investigation of quark spin polarization due to collisions in a QCD medium. Consequently, the spin transport involved with gauge interaction is still challenging to perform computation within the framework of QKT. Therefore, we focus on the QED type interactions for simplicity.

II.3
Matching conditions from QKT to relativistic hydrodynamics

Before considering the higher order corrections from QKT, we need
to establish the matching conditions to match the macroscopic effective
theory, e.g. the relativistic hydrodynamics, to our microscopic theory.
The purpose of these matching conditions is to close the equations
of variables by defining the off-equilibrium energy density, particle
number density, and spacelike currents. For example, by integrating
the momentum, one can align the moments of microscopic distributions
with macroscopic quantities
[
124
,
125
]
.
This matching process eliminates any extra degrees of freedom that
originate from the symmetry of the theory
3
3

3

It reminds us the gauge “symmetry” is also a redundancy and needs
a constraint (the gauge fixing).

According to the Nöether theorem, the canonical energy-momentum
and spin angular momentum current tensor, and conserved U(1) current

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\overline{\psi}i\hbar(\gamma^{\mu}\overrightarrow{\partial}^{\nu}-\overleftarrow{\partial}^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu})\psi,

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{\mu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar}{2}\epsilon^{\alpha\mu\rho\sigma}\overline{\psi}\gamma_{\alpha}\gamma^{5}\psi,

(65)

\displaystyle\hat{j}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\psi.

(66)

tensor in Eq. (

\displaystyle S_{(u)}^{\mu\nu}(p)\beta_{0}\left[g_{1}(E_{\mathbf{p}})\nabla_{\nu}\alpha_{0}+g_{2}(E_{\mathbf{p}})\sigma_{\nu\alpha}p_{\perp}^{\alpha}\right],

(133)

where

\displaystyle g_{1}(E_{\mathbf{p}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}(p)f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{>}(p)c_{1}\left[\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}|}\frac{9\zeta(3)}{\beta_{0}^{2}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{3\beta_{0}}+\left(\frac{21\zeta(3)}{4\beta_{0}}-|\mathbf{p}|\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}\right)\left(1-2f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{>}(p)\right)\right],

(134)

\displaystyle g_{2}(E_{\mathbf{p}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}(p)f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{>}(p)c_{2}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}|}\left[\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}|}\frac{6\zeta(3)}{\beta_{0}^{2}}-\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3\beta_{0}}+\left(\frac{15\zeta(3)}{2\beta_{0}}-|\mathbf{p}|\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\right)\left(1-2f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{>}(p)\right)\right].

(135)

We eventually obtain the
\delta\mathcal{A}^{<,\mu}(p)
in two scenarios.
In scenario (I), inserting Eq. (
133
)
into Eq. (
97
) yields,

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{(I)}}^{<,\mu}(X,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\hbar\delta(p^{2})\beta_{0}\left[-g_{1}(E_{\mathbf{p}})\frac{\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_{\rho}u_{\sigma}}{2E_{\mathbf{p}}}\nabla_{\nu}\alpha_{0}-g_{2}(E_{\mathbf{p}})\frac{\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_{\rho}u_{\sigma}}{2E_{\mathbf{p}}}\sigma_{\nu\alpha}p^{\alpha}\right].

(136)

We comment on Eqs. (

,
136
).
Remarkably,
\delta\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{(I)}}^{<,\mu}(p)
appears
to be independent of the coupling constant
e
and persists even
as

. When setting
e\rightarrow 0
, i.e. considering
the free-streaming scenario where the particles’ mean free path
\lambda

is comparable to the characteristic macroscopic length scale of system

, the gradient expansion becomes invalid in such limits.

In scenario (II), we observe that

in Eq.
(

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{e^{4}\ln e^{-1}}\right)
,
and consequently, its correction to the axial Wigner function
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}

in Eq. (

We summarize the expression for
\delta\mathcal{A}^{<,\mu}(X,p)

in scenario (II) as follows,

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{(II)}}^{<,\mu}(X,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\hbar\pi p^{\mu}\delta(p^{2})f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}(p)f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{>}(p)\frac{\beta^{2}}{e^{4}\ln e^{-1}}\mathcal{F},

(137)

where
\mathcal{F}
is given by Eq. (
127
). On the
other hand, when numerically solving the SBE in Eq. (

),
the evolution of

effectively incorporates all orders

may

.

Similar effects related to the collisional corrections found in Eqs.(

[

,
123
,
153
]
.

)

\mathcal{A}^{\mu}

of

IV.1
Modified Cooper-Frye formula and spin polarization pseudo-vector
in local equilibrium

We briefly review the derivation of the Pauli-Lubanski pseudo-vector
and modified Cooper-Frye formula from QKT following Refs.
[
32
,
154
,
44
]
.
The Pauli-Lubanski vector density in phase space is defined as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mu}(x,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\mathcal{J}_{\nu\rho}(x,p)p_{\sigma},

(138)

where
\mathcal{J}_{\nu\rho}(x,p)=u^{\lambda}J_{\lambda\nu\rho}(x,p)

is the total angular momentum density in phase space with
J_{\lambda\nu\rho}(x,p)

being the angular momentum current.
J_{\lambda\nu\rho}(x,p)
can be decomposed as the orbital and spin
parts,

\displaystyle J_{\lambda\nu\rho}(x,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{\nu}T_{\lambda\rho}(x,p)-x_{\rho}T_{\lambda\nu}(x,p)+S_{\lambda\nu\rho}(x,p).

(139)

Using Eq. (
67
) within the canonical pseudo-gauge,
the energy momentum and spin tensors in phase space are,

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}(x,p)=4p_{\nu}\mathcal{V}_{\mu}^{<}(x,p)

\displaystyle,\quad

\displaystyle S_{\lambda\nu\rho}(x,p)=2\hbar\epsilon_{\alpha\lambda\nu\rho}\mathcal{A}^{<,\alpha}(x,p),

(140)

where the factor
2
for spin current tensor,
S_{\lambda\nu\rho}
,
comes from the degeneracy of spin. Now Eq.(
138
)
becomes,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mu}(x,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\hbar\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\epsilon_{\alpha\lambda\nu\rho}u^{\lambda}\mathcal{A}^{<,\alpha}(x,p)p_{\sigma}=2\hbar p_{0}\mathcal{A}^{<,\mu}(x,p),

(141)

where we have used
p\cdot\mathcal{A}^{<}(x,p)=0
from Eq.(
27
).
We emphasize that Eq.(
141
) is, by definition,
pseudo-gauge dependent, also see e.g. Refs.
[
155
,
70
]
for related discussions.

Assuming the fermions with mass
m
reach the local equilibrium in
the freeze-out hypersurface
\Sigma^{\mu}
, the particle number element
in phase space is given by,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(t;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{(2\pi)^{3}}\theta(p_{0})\delta(p^{2}-m^{2})f(x,p)p_{\mu}\mathrm{d}\Sigma^{\mu}\mathrm{d}p_{0}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p},

(142)

where
f(x,p)=2f_{\mathrm{FD}}(x,p)
with
f_{\mathrm{FD}}
defined
in Eq.(
37
) and factor
2
denoting
the degeneracy coefficient,
\mathrm{d}\Sigma^{\mu}
denotes the
time-like hypersurface element. For simplicity, we only consider the
particles and exclude the anti-particles. In terms of the Wigner function,
Eq.(
142
)
can be expressed using Eq. (
26
) as
follows,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(t;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}4\mathcal{V}_{\mu,\mathrm{leq}}^{<}(x,p)\mathrm{d}\Sigma^{\mu}\mathrm{d}p_{0}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}4\mathcal{V}_{\mu,\mathrm{leq}}^{<}(x,p)\hat{t}^{\mu}\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathrm{d}p_{0}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}

(143)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}4\theta(p_{0})2\pi\delta(p^{2}-m^{2})f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}(x,p)p_{0}\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathrm{d}p_{0}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p},

where
\hat{t}^{\mu}=(1,\mathbf{0})
is a the timelike normal vector
of the freeze-out hypersurface and we have adopt
\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{\mu}=(\mathrm{d}\sigma,\mathbf{0})=(\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{0})

temporarily. Then the Lorentz-invariant momentum spectrum is given
by,

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}p_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(t;\mathbf{p})}{\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}f(x,p)p_{\mu}\mathrm{d}\Sigma^{\mu}=4p_{0}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\Sigma^{\mu}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{0}}{2\pi}\mathcal{V}_{\mu,\mathrm{leq}}^{<}(x,p).

(144)

This procedure can usually be generalized to derive the Lorentz-covariant
Cooper-Frye formula for spin near the freeze-out hypersurface.
The
Pauli-Lubanski pseudo-vector element can be written as,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathcal{W}^{\mu}(t;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}2\hbar p_{0}\mathcal{A}^{<,\mu}(x,p)\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathrm{d}p_{0}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}2\hbar\mathcal{A}^{<,\mu}(x,p)p_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}\Sigma^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}p_{0}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}.

(145)

The Lorentz-covariant Cooper-Frye formula for spin is then given by,

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}p_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{W}^{\mu}(t;\mathbf{p})}{\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2p_{0}\hbar\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\Sigma^{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{0}}{2\pi}\mathcal{A}^{<,\mu}(x,p).

(146)

From now on, we introduce the
on-shell energy for fermions,

E_{\mathbf{p}}\equiv u^{\mu}p_{\mu}
defined outside the
p_{0}
integrals. We proceed to define the normalized Lorentz-covariant spin
polarization pseudo-vector for massive fermions,

\mathcal{P}^{\mu}(t;\mathbf{p})=\frac{1}{m}\frac{(2\pi)^{3}E_{\mathbf{p}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{W}^{\mu}(t;\mathbf{p})}{\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}E_{\mathbf{p}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(t;\mathbf{p})}{\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}}}=\hbar\frac{\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\Sigma\cdot p\int\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{0}}{2\pi}\mathcal{A}^{<,\mu}(x,p)}{2m\int_{\Sigma}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{0}}{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\Sigma\cdot\mathcal{V}^{<}(x,p)}.

(147)

Here, given the Pauli-Lubanski vector
\mathcal{W}^{\mu}(t;\mathbf{p})

has the dimension of mass, we introduce an additional fermion mass

m
, which serves as eigenvalue of Poincare group Casimir operator

\hat{P}^{2}
. This mass is included in the denominator on the right-hand
side of Eq. (
147
), ensuring the spin polarization
pseudo-vector
\mathcal{P}^{\mu}(t;\mathbf{p})
is dimensionless. This formulation is referred to as the modified Cooper-Frye formula

[
31
,
32
,
156
]
.

The above discussions hold for general massive fermions, including our interested
\Lambda
hyperons. In our previous calculations, we worked out the spin polarization of light fermions (quarks) in a QED medium by solving (spin) Boltzmann equations. These light fermions finally freeze out in a timelike hypersurface and the hyperons directly inherit the polarization of
s
-quarks
[
3
]
, namely, following the
s
-quark scenario
[
40
]
. So we might directly use the axial Wigner function of the in-medium light fermions in Eq.(
147
). Strictly speaking, we should use the axial Wigner functions of valence
s
quarks which are massive.

Next, we compute
\mathcal{P}^{\mu}(t;\mathbf{p})
in local equilibrium.
Inserting Eqs.(
37
,
38
)
into Eqs.(
26
,
27
)
and using the tensor decomposition (
10
)
yields,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{leq}}^{<,\mu}(x,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\delta(p^{2})\hbar f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}(x,p)f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{>}(x,p)\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}p_{\nu}\partial_{\alpha}(\beta_{0}u_{\beta})-\frac{1}{E_{\mathbf{p}}}\beta_{0}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_{\rho}u_{\sigma}p^{\alpha}\sigma_{\nu\alpha}\right.

(148)

\displaystyle\quad\left.-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_{\rho}u_{\sigma}(\beta_{0}Du_{\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\beta_{0})+\frac{\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_{\rho}u_{\sigma}}{E_{\mathbf{p}}}\nabla_{\nu}\alpha_{0}\right].

The polarization pseudo-vector in Eq. (
147
)
becomes
[
44
]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{leq}}^{\mu}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbar\frac{\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\Sigma\cdot p\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{leq}}^{<,\mu}(x,\mathbf{p})}{2\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\Sigma\cdot\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{leq}}^{<}(x,\mathbf{p})}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{leq},\mathrm{thermal}}^{\mu}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{leq},\mathrm{shear}}^{\mu}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{leq},\mathrm{accT}}^{\mu}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{leq},\mathrm{chem}}^{\mu}.

(149)

The polarization induced by thermal vorticity, shear tensor, fluid
acceleration and
\nabla\alpha_{0}
, are

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{leq},\mathrm{thermal}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}}{8N}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\Sigma\cdot pf_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}(p)f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{>}(p)\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}p_{\nu}\partial_{\alpha}(\beta_{0}u_{\beta}),

(150)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{leq},\mathrm{shear}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4N}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\Sigma\cdot pf_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}(p)f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{>}(p)\frac{\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_{\rho}u_{\sigma}}{E_{\mathbf{p}}}\beta_{0}p^{\alpha}\sigma_{\nu\alpha},

(151)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{leq},\mathrm{accT}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{8N}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\Sigma\cdot pf_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}(p)f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{>}(p)\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_{\rho}u_{\sigma}(\beta_{0}Du_{\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\beta_{0}),

(152)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{leq},\mathrm{chem}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4N}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\Sigma\cdot pf_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}(p)f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{>}(p)\frac{\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_{\rho}u_{\sigma}}{E_{\mathbf{p}}}\nabla_{\nu}\alpha_{0},

(153)

respectively, where

N\equiv m\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{\mu}p^{\mu}f_{\mathrm{V},\mathrm{leq}}^{<}(x,p).

(154)

We emphasize that we have extended the discussion to massive fermions by simply substituting the
\mathcal{W}^{\mu}
and
\mathcal{A}^{<,\mu}
for massless fermions into the massive case. The
\mathcal{P}^{\mu}
in Eqs. (
150
-
153
) are perpendicular to
p^{\mu}
after integrating over the freeze-out hypersurface. These results are consistent with those derived from linear response theory
[
39
]
for massive fermions, suggesting that our simple extension is effective. However, a very recent study on non-interacting massless fermions
[
157
]
suggests that
\mathcal{P}^{\mu}
is exclusively proportional to
p^{\mu}
. Currently, we do not have a clear understanding of the discrepancies between our findings and those in Ref.
[
157
]
. This question will be addressed in future studies.

IV.2
Collisional corrections

Now, we compute the collisional corrections to the spin polarization

136

into Eq. (
147

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{(I)}}^{\mu}(\mathbf{p})

polarization
[

41

further discussion of similar findings, refer to the recent work in
Ref.
[

In scenario (II), using Eq. (

pseudo-vector in Eq. (

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{(II)}}^{\mu}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar}{2N}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\Sigma\cdot pp^{\mu}\delta f_{\mathrm{A}}^{<}(x,p)+\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{2}\partial^{3})

(158)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\textrm{(II)}},\omega-\nabla T}^{\mu}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\textrm{(II)}},\nabla\omega}^{\mu}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\textrm{(II)},\omega-\mathrm{chem}}}^{\mu}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\textrm{(II)},\omega-\mathrm{shear}}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\textrm{(II)},chem-\nabla T}}^{\mu}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\textrm{(II)},shear-\nabla T}}^{\mu}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\textrm{(II)},shear-chem}}^{\mu},

\displaystyle\hbar^{2}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\Sigma\cdot pa_{\textrm{(II)}}^{\mu}\left[\left(E_{\mathbf{p}}-d_{1}\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}\right)d_{2}\beta_{0}\nabla^{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}+d_{6}\frac{1}{2}\beta_{0}^{2}\nabla^{\alpha}\omega^{\rho}p_{\langle\alpha}p_{\rho\rangle}\right],

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\textrm{(II)},\omega-\mathrm{chem}}}^{\mu}

with

.
Here the

V
Relaxation time approximation to SBE

In this section, we apply
the RTA to SBE (
50
) with
f_{\mathrm{V}}=f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}
,

\displaystyle p^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}f_{\mathrm{A}}^{<}(p)+\hbar\partial_{\mu}S_{(u)}^{\mu\alpha}(p)\partial_{\alpha}f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-E_{\mathbf{p}}\frac{f_{\mathrm{A}}^{<}-f_{\mathrm{A,leq}}^{<}}{\tau_{\mathrm{A}}},

(160)

where we parameterize the collision kernel
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{B}}[f_{\mathrm{V,leq}},\delta f_{\mathrm{A}}]

by using the Anderson-Witting relaxation time approximation
[
158
]

and
\tau_{\mathrm{A}}
is the relaxation time of axial distribution
function. We introduce the effective Green function
G(x,p)
for

f_{\mathrm{A}}^{<}(x,p)
in Eq. (
160
),

\displaystyle\left(p^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}+\frac{E_{\mathbf{p}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)f_{\mathrm{A}}^{<}(x,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{\mathbf{p}}\frac{f_{\mathrm{A,leq}}^{<}(x,p)}{\tau_{\mathrm{A}}}-\hbar\partial_{\mu}S_{(u)}^{\mu\alpha}(x,p)\partial_{\alpha}f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}(x,p)\equiv G(x,p).

(161)

We take the Fourier transform to both sides of Eq. (
161
)
and obtain,

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{A}}^{<}(x,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x^{\prime}e^{-ik\cdot(x-x^{\prime})}\frac{iG(x^{\prime},p)}{k_{\mu}p^{\mu}+iE_{\mathbf{p}}\tau_{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}}.

(162)

In this work, we consider the system expands as a Bjorken flow
[
159
]
,
i.e. the system is homogeneous in transverse plane and boost-invariant
in longitudinal direction. We refer to Refs.
[
160
]
and

[
161
]
for the discussion on the conventional and
chiral kinetic theory in a Bjorken flow. In the current work, we take

p^{\mu}=(E_{\mathbf{p}},0,0,p^{z})
in Eq. (
162
)
and assume that
G(x,p)
is independent on
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{T}}=(x,y)
.
Then, Eq. (
162
) reduces to,

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{A}}^{<}(x,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E_{\mathbf{p}}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}k_{0}\mathrm{d}k^{z}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{ie^{-ik^{0}(t-t^{\prime})+ik^{z}(z-z^{\prime})}}{k^{0}-k^{z}p^{z}E_{\mathbf{p}}^{-1}+i\tau_{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}}G(x^{\prime},p)\Big|_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{T}}}

(163)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E_{\mathbf{p}}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\theta(t-t^{\prime})\exp\left(-\frac{t-t^{\prime}}{\tau_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)G(x^{\prime},p)\Big|_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{T}},z-z^{\prime}=p^{z}E_{\mathbf{p}}^{-1}(t-t^{\prime})}.

which has the same structure as that in the solution of
\widetilde{a}^{\mu}

in Ref.
[
18
]
. Here,
\theta(x)
is the unit-step
function.

As mentioned in Sec.
IV
, to
apply the modified Cooper-Frye formula, it is essential to know the
axial distribution function
f_{\mathrm{A}}^{<}(x,p)
at the (chemical)
freeze out hypersurface. We assume that the freeze out occurs at
t_{f}

and focus on the interactions corrections near this hypersurface.
For analytical convenience, we further assume that
G(x,p)
defined
in Eq. (
161
) is nonzero for
t>t_{i}
, and negligible
for

For simplicity, we assume that

is very close to

and neglect the variation of

during
this time interval, as these are of

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathrm{A}}f_{\mathrm{A,leq}}^{<}(x,p)+\mathcal{O}\left(\hbar\partial^{2}\right),\quad\Gamma_{\mathrm{A}}\equiv\frac{t_{f}-t_{i}}{\tau_{\mathrm{A}}},

,
97
]
.

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we have computed
the collisional corrections to the SBE using Chapman-Enskog expansion
and derived the collisional corrections to spin polarization pseudo-vector.
We first derive the SBE for
f_{{\rm A}}
in Eq. (
50
),
considering the Møller scattering process in both
u
- and
t
-channels.
For simplification, we consider two distinct scenarios.

In scenario (I), we consider the vector distribution function is off-equilibrium,

f_{{\rm V}}\neq f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}
, and assume the applicability
of gradient expansion, i.e.
\mathcal{O}(\partial)\ll\mathcal{O}(Te^{4})
.
We revisit the matching condition in Eq. (
86
)
in Sec.
III.1
and derive
\delta\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{(I)}}^{<,\mu}(X,p)

shown in Eq. (
136
). Remarkably, we find that collisional
corrections to
\delta\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{(I)}}^{<,\mu}(p)
in this
scenario do not explicitly depend on the coupling constant, similar
to the side-jump mechanism in the conductivities of anomalous and
spin Hall effects in condensed matter physics
[
122
,
123
]
.
Consequently, the collisional corrections to the spin polarization
pseudo-vector
\delta\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{(I)}}^{\mu}(\mathbf{p})

as shown in Eq. (
155
) , exhibit a similar property.

In scenario (II), we consider vector distribution function is in local
equilibrium
f_{{\rm V}}=f_{\mathrm{V,leq}}^{<}
. In such case, the
axial collision kernel in spin Boltzmann equation is similar to the
scalar-type Boltzmann equation as shown in Eq.(
93
).
The collisional correction to the axial Wigner function,
\delta\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{(II)}}^{<,\mu}(X,p)
,
and the spin polarization pseudo-vector,
\delta\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{(II)}}^{\mu}(\mathbf{p})
,
are detailed in Eqs. (
136
) and (
158
),
respectively. Interestingly, we observe that there are many new dissipative
corrections to
\delta\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{(II)}}^{\mu}(\mathbf{p})

at
\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{2}\partial^{2})
proportional to the spin
relaxation time
\tau_{{\rm A}}
.

As a comparison, we also studied the SBE using RTA and derive
\delta\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{RTA}}^{<,\mu}(x,p)

and
\delta\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{RTA}}^{\mu}
, shown in Eqs. (
169
)
and (
169
), respectively.
In this case, we observe that the collisional corrections are solely
induced by kinetic vorticity affecting the spin polarization.

Before concluding this work, we would like to address the limitations
of our current study. First, we emphasize that the local equilibrium
condition outlined in Eq. (
36
) may
be influenced by the specifics of interactions and approximation schemes.
Secondly, our formalism for fermion elastic scattering in the HTL
approximation is limited to the leading-logarithm order. A complete
leading-order correction, including radiative corrections
[
141
]

and contributions from the photon Wigner function up to
\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{1})

[
106
,
107
]
, are also crucial. Furthermore,
it is necessary to extend our techniques to real quantum chromodynamical
processes. Thirdly, future numerical simulations based on our results
are essential to quantitatively study the collisional corrections
to spin polarization.
