Resonant conversion of axion dark radiation into terahertz electromagnetic radiation in a neutron star magnetosphere
Andrew J. Long, Enrico D. Schiappacasse

Abstract
In the strong magnetic field of a neutron star’s magnetosphere, axions coupled to electromagnetism develop a nonzero probability to convert into photons. Past studies have revealed that the axion-photon conversion can be resonantly enhanced. We recognize that the axion-photon resonance admits two parametrically distinct resonant solutions, which we call the mass-matched resonance and the Euler-Heisenberg assisted resonance. The mass-matched resonance occurs at a point in the magnetosphere where the radially-varying plasma frequency crosses the axion mass ω pl ≈ m a \omega_{\mathrm{pl}}\approx m_{a} . The Euler-Heisenberg assisted resonance occurs where the axion energy satisfies ω ≈ ( 2 ​ ω pl 2 / 7 ​ g γ ​ γ ​ γ ​ γ ​ B ¯ 2 ) 1 / 2 \omega\approx(2\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}/7g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}\bar{B}^{2})^{1/2} . This second resonance is made possible though the strong background magnetic field B ¯ \bar{B} as well as the nonzero Euler-Heisenberg four-photon self interaction, which has the coupling g γ ​ γ ​ γ ​ γ = 8 ​ α 2 / 45 ​ m e 4 g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}=8\alpha^{2}/45m_{e}^{4} . We study the resonant conversion of relativistic axion dark radiation into photons via the Euler-Heisenberg assisted resonance, and we calculate the expected electromagnetic radiation assuming different values for the axion-photon coupling g a ​ γ ​ γ g_{a\gamma\gamma} and different amplitudes for the axion flux onto the neutron star Φ a \Phi_{a} . We briefly discuss several possible sources of axion dark radiation. Achieving a sufficiently strong axion flux to induce a detectable electromagnetic signal seems unlikely.

If the axion couples to electromagnetism though the interaction

The axion-photon interconversion may be resonantly enhanced, depending on the model parameters and the system under consideration.
In a neutron star magnetosphere, it is necessary to account for the presence of an electron-proton plasma, which modifies the electric constitutive relation in a way that depends on the plasma frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}

Additionally, due to the star’s strong magnetic field, it is necessary to account for the Euler-Heisenberg four-photon self-interaction, which arises in the low-energy limit of quantum electrodynamics.
Both effects play a role in the axion-photon resonance

II
Resonant conversion of relativistic axions

In this section we study the resonant conversion of relativistic axions into photons.
The resonance is made possible by the presence of a plasma,
i.e.
nonzero plasma frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}
, and a strong background magnetic field.
Systems that exhibit both of these properties include the early universe and the magnetosphere of a neutron star.
In this article, we focus on the neutron star system.

II.1
Modeling a neutron star magnetosphere

We adopt the Goldreich-Julian (GJ) Model
Goldreich and Julian 1969
of the neutron star magnetosphere.
This simple model is expected to provide a reliable approximation for quiet and stable neutron stars
Haensel et al. 2007
, and it has been adopted in previous studies of axion dark matter such as Ref.
Pshirkov and Popov 2009
.
For older neutron stars that have crossed their death line, a combination of analytical modeling and numerical simulation
Krause-Polstorff and Michel 1985
;
Spitkovsky and Arons 2002
;
Cerutti and Beloborodov 2017
reveal that the magnetosphere is more reliably modeled as a vacuum spinning dipole (electrosphere), which we do not consider in this work.

The neutron star itself is modeled as a ball with radius
R_{\text{\sc ns}}
that rotates with angular velocity
{\bm{\Omega}}
about an axis through its center.
We also define the angular speed
\Omega=|{\bm{\Omega}}|
, the unit vector
\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}={\bm{\Omega}}/\Omega
, and the rotation period
P=2\pi/\Omega
.
Outside of the neutron star, the magnetic field
\bar{{\bm{B}}}({\bm{r}},t)
is modeled as a dipole, with magnetic dipole moment
{\bm{m}}(t)
having constant magnitude
m=|{\bm{m}}(t)|
and varying orientation
\hat{{\bm{m}}}(t)={\bm{m}}(t)/m
in a frame where
{\bm{\Omega}}
is fixed.
The field outside the star is written as

\begin{split}&\bar{{\bm{B}}}({\bm{r}},t)=B_{0}\,{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{r}}},t)\,\biggl(\frac{r}{R_{\text{\sc ns}}}\biggr)^{\!\!-3}\\
&\quad\text{where}\quad{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{r}}},t)=\tfrac{3}{2}\bigl(\hat{{\bm{m}}}(t)\cdot\hat{{\bm{r}}}\bigr)\,\hat{{\bm{r}}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{{\bm{m}}}(t)\;,\end{split}

(1)

.
At the north magnetic pole (
{\bm{r}}=R_{\text{\sc ns}}\hat{{\bm{m}}}
) the magnetic field is
\bar{{\bm{B}}}=2{\bm{m}}/R_{\text{\sc ns}}^{3}
; along the magnetic equator (
{\bm{r}}\perp\hat{{\bm{m}}}
) it is
\bar{{\bm{B}}}=-{\bm{m}}/R_{\text{\sc ns}}^{3}
; and at the south magnetic pole (
{\bm{r}}=-R_{\text{\sc ns}}\hat{{\bm{m}}}
) it is again
\bar{{\bm{B}}}=2{\bm{m}}/R_{\text{\sc ns}}^{3}
.
We define
B_{0}=2m/R_{\text{\sc ns}}^{3}
to denote the polar surface magnetic field strength.
For a typical pulsar,
R_{\text{\sc ns}}\approx 10\;\mathrm{km}
,
P\approx 0.1

10\;\mathrm{sec}

B_{0}\approx 10^{11\text{--}13}\;\mathrm{G}

;
Harding and Lai 2006
;
Olausen and Kaspi 2014
;
Kaspi and Beloborodov 2017

The GJ model rests on the assumption that the magnetic field lines co-rotate with the star, and a consistent solution of Maxwell’s equations is obtained by assuming a charged plasma to be present in the magnetosphere.
The density of electric charge
n_{c}({\bm{r}},t)
is required to be
n_{c}({\bm{r}},t)=[2{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\bar{{\bm{B}}}({\bm{r}},t)/e]/(1-|{\bm{\Omega}}\times{\bm{r}}|^{2})
where
e\approx 0.303
is the unit of electromagnetic charge and
\alpha=e^{2}/4\pi\approx 1/137
is the electromagnetic fine structure constant.
Assuming a nonrelativistic electron-proton plasma, the plasma frequency is approximately
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}({\bm{r}},t)\approx\sqrt{4\pi\alpha n_{e}({\bm{r}},t)/m_{e}}
where
m_{e}\approx 0.511\;\mathrm{MeV}

We take the density of charge as an estimate of the electron number density

The plasma frequency is

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}({\bm{r}},t)=\omega_{\mathrm{pl},0}\ \psi_{\omega}(\hat{{\bm{r}}},t)\ \biggl(\frac{r}{R_{\text{\sc ns}}}\biggr)^{\!\!-3/2}\quad\text{where}\quad

(2)

\displaystyle\ \psi_{\omega}(\hat{{\bm{r}}},t)=\bigl|2\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}\cdot{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{r}}},t)\bigr|^{1/2}\quad\text{and where}\quad

\displaystyle\ \omega_{\mathrm{pl},0}\approx\bigl(70\;\mu\mathrm{eV}\bigr)\biggl(\frac{B_{0}}{10^{14}\;\mathrm{G}}\biggr)^{\!\!1/2}\biggl(\frac{P}{1\;\mathrm{sec}}\biggr)^{\!\!-1/2}\biggl(\frac{n_{e}}{|n_{c}|}\biggr)^{\!\!1/2}/

In general

We expect the GJ model to be a reliable description of the neutron star magnetosphere in the equatorial regions near the star.
In the polar regions, acceleration of charged particles in the strong field leads to the formation of an electron-positron plasma

Far from the star’s surface, the plasma cannot co-rotate with the star, since it would need to travel at greater than the speed of light.

(3)

II.2
Modeling axion and photon interactions

To model the axion’s interactions with electromagnetism, we assume the usual
aF\tilde{F}
operator, which is expected to be present for the QCD axion in the KSVZ
Kim 1979
;
Shifman et al. 1980
and DFSZ
Dine et al. 1981
;
Zhitnitsky 1980
models as well as for axion-like particles that arise in the string axiverse
Gendler et al. 2023
.
The Lagrangian for the axion-photon system in vacuum is taken to be

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}a\partial^{\mu}a-\tfrac{1}{2}m_{a}^{2}a^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}g_{a\gamma\gamma}aF_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\qquad+\tfrac{1}{16}g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}\Bigl[\bigl(F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\bigr)^{2}+\tfrac{7}{4}\bigl(F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}\bigr)^{2}\Bigr]

(4)

where
a(x)
is the axion field,
A_{\mu}(x)
is the photon field,
F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
is the electromagnetic field strength tensor, and
\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}=\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}/2
is the dual tensor.
The axion-photon interaction is parameterized by the coupling
g_{a\gamma\gamma}
.
A strong axion-photon interaction would lead to undetected emission of axions from astrophysical bodies.
Searches for axion emission from our Sun by the CAST helioscope lead to a constraint
|g_{a\gamma\gamma}|<6.6\times 10^{-11}\;\mathrm{GeV}^{-1}
at
95\%
confidence for
m_{a}<0.02\;\mathrm{eV}

Anastassopoulos et al. 2017
.
For much lighter axions, X-ray and
\gamma
-ray observations of NGC 1275
Reynolds et al. 2020
, super star clusters
Dessert et al. 2020
, and SN-1987A
Payez et al. 2015
constrain the axion-photon coupling at the level for
g_{a\gamma\gamma}<\mathrm{few}\times 10^{-12}\;\mathrm{GeV}^{-1}
for various mass ranges up to
m_{a}<10^{-10}\;\mathrm{eV}
.
See also the Particle Data Group’s review on axions
Workman et al. 2022
for a comprehensive summary of constraints.

The
(FF)^{2}
and
(F\tilde{F})^{2}
terms in Eq. (
II.2

Heisenberg and Euler 1936
;
Weisskopf 1936
;
Adler 1971
.
These interactions arise in the low energy effective theory obtained from QED upon integrating out the electron/positron
Dunne 2004
, leading to
g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}=8\alpha^{2}/45m_{e}^{4}=2\alpha/(45\pi B_{\mathrm{crit}}^{2})
where
B_{\mathrm{crit}}=m_{e}^{2}/e\approx 4.4\times 10^{13}\;\mathrm{G}
.
In formulas and plots we generally fiducialize the magnetic field strength to
B_{0}=10^{14}\;\mathrm{G}
although the higher-order terms in the Euler-Heisenberg effective action will become significant for
|\bar{{\bm{B}}}|\gtrsim B_{\mathrm{crit}}
; one is free to scale
B_{0}
down in the formulas.
For reference, note that

\begin{split}g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}B_{0}^{2}\approx\bigl(5.3\times 10^{-4}\bigr)\biggl(\frac{B_{0}}{10^{14}\;\mathrm{G}}\biggr)^{2}\;,\end{split}

(5)

and we can generally neglect
g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}B_{0}^{2}
(or
g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}|\bar{{\bm{B}}}|^{2}
or
g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}\bar{B}^{2}
) when it appears in comparison with an order
1

In the background of a strong magnetic field, these interactions modify the photon’s dispersion relation and impact axion-photon mixing
Raffelt and Stodolsky 1988
.

In a neutron star magnetosphere, the electromagnetic field obeys the in-medium form of Maxwell’s equations (augmented by the axion-photon interaction and the Euler-Heisenberg self-interaction).
We model the medium with a linear constitutive relation
{\bm{D}}=\epsilon{\bm{E}}
where
\epsilon({\bm{r}},t)
is the dielectric tensor.
Due to the strong magnetic field, electrons perform orbits around field lines, and consequently electromagnetic waves propagating either longitudinal or transverse to the field lines experience different indices of refraction.
We model this effect by writing the dielectric tensor as
\epsilon_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-(\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}/\omega^{2})\hat{B}_{i}\hat{B}_{j}

Gurevich et al. 1993
where
\hat{\bm{B}}
is the unit vector that locally points in the direction of the magnetic field and
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}
is the plasma frequency.

II.3
Resonant axion-photon conversion

Several previous studies
Raffelt and Stodolsky 1988
;
Lai and Heyl 2006
contain derivations of the equations of motion for the axion-photon system, including effects of the axion-photon coupling, the Euler-Heisenberg photon self-interaction, and the ambient magnetized plasma.
For the sake of completeness, we present a detailed derivation in Appendix
A
.
For convenience we make two simplifying assumptions.
First we focus on aligned neutron stars for which the magnetic dipole moment aligns with the star’s rotation axis,
\hat{{\bm{m}}}(t)=\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}
.
An aligned neutron star has a static magnetic field
\bar{{\bm{B}}}({\bm{r}})=B_{0}\,{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{r}}})\,(r/R_{\text{\sc ns}})^{3}
and plasma frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}({\bm{r}})=\omega_{\mathrm{pl},0}\,\psi_{\omega}(\hat{{\bm{r}}})\,(r/R_{\text{\sc ns}})^{-3/2}
.
Second we focus on axions that move along radial trajectories out from the center of the star.
If the unit vector
\hat{{\bm{n}}}
points in the direction of the axion’s motion, then this assumption corresponds to choosing
\hat{{\bm{n}}}=\hat{{\bm{r}}}
.
We identify the magnetic field’s longitudinal and transverse components as

\displaystyle\bar{B}_{L}=\hat{{\bm{n}}}\cdot\bar{{\bm{B}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{B}_{T}=|\bar{{\bm{B}}}-\bar{B}_{L}\,\hat{{\bm{n}}}|

(6)

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad\beta_{L}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})=\tfrac{\bar{B}_{L}({\bm{r}},\hat{{\bm{n}}})}{\bar{B}({\bm{r}})}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{T}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})=\tfrac{\bar{B}_{T}({\bm{r}},\hat{{\bm{n}}})}{\bar{B}({\bm{r}})}/

Along a radial trajectory
\hat{{\bm{n}}}=\hat{{\bm{r}}}
, the ratios
\beta_{L}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})
and
\beta_{T}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})
are independent of
r
, and they satisfy
\beta_{L}^{2}+\beta_{T}^{2}=1

Resonance condition

Subject to the simplifying assumptions above, the equations of motion for the axion-photon system reduce to a set of coupled first-order differential equations

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\begin{pmatrix}a_{\omega,\hat{{\bm{n}}}}\\

where the top case corresponds to nonrelativistic axions with
\omega\approx m_{a}\gg k_{a}=(\omega^{2}-m_{a}^{2})^{1/2}
and the bottom case corresponds to relativistic axions with
\omega\approx k_{a}\gg m_{a}\,\beta_{L}
.
See Appendix
A.10
for additional details.
The orientation-dependent factors
|{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|
and
\psi_{\omega}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})
are discussed in Appendix
B
.

II.3.3
Euler-Heisenberg assisted resonance

To identify the second resonance, we set
m_{a}=0
in the resonance condition (
9
), which reduces to

\begin{split}\omega=\sqrt{\frac{2}{7}\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}(r_{\mathrm{res}}\,\hat{{\bm{n}}})}{g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}\bar{B}^{2}(r_{\mathrm{res}}\,\hat{{\bm{n}}})}}/\end{split}

(12)

For
g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}\bar{B}^{2}\ll 1
as in Eq. (
5
), it follows that
\omega\gg\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}(r_{\mathrm{res}}\,\hat{{\bm{n}}})
.
Since this solution relies upon the Euler-Heisenberg photon self interaction (
g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}\neq 0
), we call it the “Euler Heisenberg assisted” resonance.
It has also been called “axion-photon resonance” (collectively with the
MMR
) by
Lai and Heyl 2006
, “double lens effect” by
Bondarenko et al. 2023
, and “plasma-vacuum resonance” by
Song et al. 2024
.

For a given axion energy
\omega
and neutron star profile functions,
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}(r)
and
\bar{B}(r)
, one can solve the resonance condition (
12
) for
r=r_{\mathrm{res}}
to obtain the resonance radius

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{res}}(\omega,\hat{{\bm{n}}})=\frac{7^{1/3}}{2^{1/3}}g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}^{1/3}B_{0}^{2/3}\frac{\omega^{2/3}}{\omega_{\mathrm{pl},0}^{2/3}}\frac{|{\bm{\psi}}_{B}|^{2/3}}{|\psi_{\omega}|^{2/3}}\,R_{\text{\sc ns}}

(13)

\displaystyle\quad\approx\bigl(3.4R_{\text{\sc ns}}\bigr)\biggl(\frac{B_{0}}{10^{14}\;\mathrm{G}}\biggr)^{\!\!1/3}\biggl(\frac{P}{1\;\mathrm{sec}}\biggr)^{\!\!1/3}\biggl(\frac{n_{e}}{|n_{c}|}\biggr)^{\!\!-1/3}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\biggl(\frac{\omega}{10\;\mathrm{meV}}\biggr)^{\!\!2/3}\,|{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{2/3}\,|\psi_{\omega}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{-2/3}/

We require
r_{\mathrm{res}}>R_{\text{\sc ns}}
such that the conversion occurs outside the star, since otherwise the photon would likely scatter on the neutron star matter, rather than escaping from the system as radiation.
The width of the resonance region, denoted by
d_{\mathrm{res}}
, is related to how quickly
\Delta_{a}(r)-\Delta_{\parallel}(r)
varies at the resonance radius.
We find

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{res}}(\omega,\hat{{\bm{n}}})=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3}}\frac{\sqrt{k_{a}r_{\mathrm{res}}}}{\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}}\,|\beta_{T}|^{-1}

(14)

\displaystyle\quad\approx\bigl(4.5\times 10^{-2}\,r_{\mathrm{res}}\bigr)\biggl(\frac{B_{0}}{10^{14}\;\mathrm{G}}\biggr)^{\!\!-1/6}\biggl(\frac{P}{1\;\mathrm{sec}}\biggr)^{\!\!5/6}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\biggl(\frac{R_{\text{\sc ns}}}{10\;\mathrm{km}}\biggr)^{\!\!-1/2}\biggl(\frac{n_{e}}{|n_{c}|}\biggr)^{\!\!-5/6}\,\biggl(\frac{\omega}{10\;\mathrm{meV}}\biggr)^{\!\!2/3}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\biggl(\frac{k_{a}}{10\;\mathrm{meV}}\biggr)^{\!\!1/2}|\beta_{T}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{-1}\,|{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{2/3}\,|\psi_{\omega}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{-5/3}\;,

and one can find additional details in Appendix
A
.
Note that
d_{\mathrm{res}}
grows in relation to
r_{\mathrm{res}}
as the axion energy
\omega\approx k_{a}
is increased.
Provided that
d_{\mathrm{res}}\ll r_{\mathrm{res}}
, the axion-photon conversion probability may be calculated by employing the stationary phase approximation.
To leading order in the axion-photon coupling
g_{a\gamma\gamma}
, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}(\omega,\hat{{\bm{n}}})=\frac{2\pi}{21}\frac{g_{a\gamma\gamma}^{2}r_{\mathrm{res}}}{g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}k_{a}}=\frac{1}{4}\frac{\omega^{2}}{k_{a}^{2}}g_{a\gamma\gamma}^{2}\bar{B}_{T}^{2}d_{\mathrm{res}}^{2}

(15)

\displaystyle\quad\approx\bigl(3.7\times 10^{-2}\bigr)\biggl(\frac{g_{a\gamma\gamma}}{10^{-12}\;\mathrm{GeV}^{-1}}\biggr)^{\!\!2}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\biggl(\frac{B_{0}}{10^{14}\;\mathrm{G}}\biggr)^{\!\!1/3}\biggl(\frac{P}{1\;\mathrm{sec}}\biggr)^{\!\!1/3}\biggl(\frac{R_{\text{\sc ns}}}{10\;\mathrm{km}}\biggr)\biggl(\frac{n_{e}}{|n_{c}|}\biggr)^{\!\!-1/3}\,

\displaystyle\qquad\times\biggl(\frac{\omega}{10\;\mathrm{meV}}\biggr)^{\!\!2/3}\biggl(\frac{k_{a}}{10\;\mathrm{meV}}\biggr)^{\!\!-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\times|{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{2/3}\,|\psi_{\omega}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{-2/3}/

For relativistic axions
k_{a}\approx\omega
and
\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}\propto\omega^{2/3}k_{a}^{-1}\approx\omega^{-1/3}
, implying that conversion via the
EHR
is less likely for higher energy axions.
For comparison, the
MMR
has instead
\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}\propto\omega^{2}k_{a}^{-1}\approx\omega^{1}
such that higher-energy axions are more likely to convert into photons.

In order for this calculation to be self-consistent it is necessary to impose two conditions.
First, the resonance must occur outside of the neutron star; this condition requires
R_{\text{\sc ns}}<r_{\mathrm{res}}
.
Second, the resonance region must be narrow (since we calculate
\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}
using the stationary phase approximation); this condition requires
d_{\mathrm{res}}<r_{\mathrm{res}}
.
Together these two conditions bracket a range of axion energies
\omega_{\mathrm{min}}<\omega<\omega_{\mathrm{max}}
where

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{min}}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{7}}\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{pl},0}}{\sqrt{g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}}B_{0}}\frac{|\psi_{\omega}|}{|{\bm{\psi}}_{B}|}

(16)

\displaystyle\quad\approx\bigl(1.6\;\mathrm{meV}\bigr)\biggl(\frac{B_{0}}{10^{14}\;\mathrm{G}}\biggr)^{\!\!-1/2}\biggl(\frac{P}{1\;\mathrm{sec}}\biggr)^{\!\!-1/2}\biggl(\frac{n_{e}}{|n_{c}|}\biggr)^{\!\!1/2}\,

\displaystyle\qquad\times|\psi_{\omega}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|\,|{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{-1}

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{max}}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})=\frac{3^{3/7}}{2^{4/7}7^{2/7}\pi^{3/7}}\frac{R_{\text{\sc ns}}^{3/7}\omega_{\mathrm{pl},0}^{10/7}}{g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}^{2/7}B_{0}^{4/7}}\frac{|\beta_{T}|^{6/7}\,|\psi_{\omega}|^{10/7}}{|{\bm{\psi}}_{B}|^{4/7}}

\displaystyle\quad\approx\bigl(140\;\mathrm{meV}\bigr)\biggl(\frac{B_{0}}{10^{14}\;\mathrm{G}}\biggr)^{\!\!1/7}\biggl(\frac{R_{\text{\sc ns}}}{10\;\mathrm{km}}\biggr)^{\!\!3/7}\biggl(\frac{P}{1\;\mathrm{sec}}\biggr)^{\!\!-5/7}\biggl(\frac{n_{e}}{|n_{c}|}\biggr)^{\!\!5/7}\,

\displaystyle\qquad\times|\beta_{T}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{6/7}\,|\psi_{\omega}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{10/7}\,|{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{-4/7}/

For typical parameters, the
EHR
is relevant for axion energies from
1
to
100\;\mathrm{meV}
.
This is illustrated in Fig.
2
.
The window widens for larger
B_{0}
, and it closes off entirely for
B_{0}\lesssim(10^{11}\;\mathrm{G})(P/1\;\mathrm{sec})^{1/3}
.
Consequently, the
EHR
is only expected to occur in the strong-field environment of a neutron star magnetosphere.
As the neutron star period decreases, the window of axion energies shifts to smaller values.
For a millisecond pulsar (
P\sim 1-10\;\mathrm{ms}
)
Pétri 2016
the window would be in the microwave band; however, the typical magnetic field strength (
B\lesssim 10^{10}\;\mathrm{G}
) would make the
EHR
inaccessible for millisecond pulsars.

II.3.4
Nonresonant conversion

It is also possible that the resonance condition (
9
) does not admit any real solutions with
r_{\mathrm{res}}>R_{\text{\sc ns}}
.
For instance if the axion energy
\omega
is large compared to the axion mass
m_{a}
and the plasma frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}
, such that
g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}\bar{B}^{2}\omega^{2}\gg m_{a}^{2}
and
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}
, then the first three terms in Eq. (
9
) can be neglected with respect to the fourth, and the resonance condition has no real solution.
In this situation, the axion-photon interconversion is said to be nonresonant.
Several studies
Morris 1986
;
Raffelt and Stodolsky 1988
;
Lai and Ho 2002
;
Gill and Heyl 2011
;
Fortin and Sinha 2019
;
Dessert et al. 2019
;
Buschmann et al. 2021
;
Dessert et al. 2022a
;
Fortin et al. 2021b
;
Dessert et al. 2022b
have noted that nonresonant axion-photon conversion can play an important role in generating X-ray signals of axion emission from neutrons stars (including magnetars) and white dwarf stars, while other work
Dobrynina et al. 2015
has studied the conversion of TeV gamma rays into axions as they propagate across the galaxy.
If the axion mass and plasma frequency can be neglected, then a simple formula is available for the nonresonant conversion probability
Fortin and Sinha 2019

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}(\omega,\hat{{\bm{n}}})\approx\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{2}{5})^{2}}{2^{2/5}5^{6/5}7^{4/5}}\frac{g_{a\gamma\gamma}^{2}}{g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}^{4/5}}\bar{B}_{T,0}^{2/5}R_{\text{\sc ns}}^{6/5}\frac{\omega^{2/5}}{k_{a}^{6/5}}

(17)

\displaystyle\quad\approx\bigl(5.1\times 10^{-6}\bigr)\biggl(\frac{g_{a\gamma\gamma}}{10^{-12}\;\mathrm{GeV}^{-1}}\biggr)^{\!\!2}\biggl(\frac{B_{0}}{10^{14}\;\mathrm{G}}\biggr)^{\!\!2/5}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\biggl(\frac{R_{\text{\sc ns}}}{10\;\mathrm{km}}\biggr)^{\!\!6/5}\biggl(\frac{\omega}{1\;\mathrm{keV}}\biggr)^{\!\!2/5}\biggl(\frac{k_{a}}{1\;\mathrm{keV}}\biggr)^{\!\!-6/5}

\displaystyle\qquad\times|\beta_{T}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{2/5}\,|{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|^{2/5}\;,

where
\bar{B}_{T,0}=B_{0}\,|\beta_{T}|\,|{\bm{\psi}}_{B}|
.
For relativistic axions
k_{a}=(\omega^{2}-m_{a}^{2})^{1/2}\approx\omega
and
\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}\propto\omega^{2/5}k_{a}^{-6/5}\approx\omega^{-4/5}
.
For comparison the
EHR
has
\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}\propto\omega^{-1/3}
, which decreases less quickly toward large axion energy.
See Appendix
A.12
for additional details and generalizations.

II.3.5
Comparison of the two resonances

Figure

\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}

\omega

To produce this figure, we solved the coupled axion-photon system (
7

The curves shown in the figure combine numerical results at intermediate
\omega
with analytical results at small and large
\omega
.
The figure illustrates how the
MMR
dominates at small
\omega
, the
EHR
dominates at intermediate
\omega
, and the nonresonant conversion dominates at large
\omega
.

Toward small axion energy, the axion-photon interconversion is controlled by the

\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}\propto k_{a}^{-1}
.
For relativistic axions, which have
\omega\approx k_{a}\gg m_{a}
, the probability grows linearly, since
\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}\propto\omega^{2}k_{a}^{-1}\approx\omega
.
This behavior is partly because the probability increases as the width of the resonance region increases:
\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}\propto d_{\mathrm{res}}^{2}\omega^{2}/k_{a}^{2}
and

.
For the curve with
m_{a}=10^{-9}\;\mathrm{eV}

MMR
in which
\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}\propto m_{a}^{4/3}\omega^{2}
.
Lowering the axion mass reduces the probability, because the resonance radius obeys
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}(r_{\mathrm{res}})\approx m_{a}
, and a smaller
m_{a}
implies a larger
r_{\mathrm{res}}
where the magnetic field is weaker, leading to a smaller probability.

At intermediate axion energy, the axion-photon conversion is controlled by the
EHR
, and the parametric behavior can be understood from Eq. (
15
) in which
\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}\propto m_{a}^{0}\omega^{2/3}k_{a}^{-1}
.
For the parameters that were chosen to generate this figure, the probability is insensitive to the axion mass as long as
m_{a}\lesssim\mu\mathrm{eV}
.
Since
m_{a}\ll\omega\approx k_{a}

\omega\approx 10^{-3}\;\mathrm{eV}

r_{\mathrm{res}}>R_{\text{\sc ns}}

dominates over the
MMR
.

\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}\propto\omega^{-1/3}

EHR
.
This is mostly because
r_{\mathrm{res}}\propto\omega^{2/3}
so a higher energy axion converts further from the star, where the magnetic field is weaker, leading to a smaller probability.
For
\omega\gtrsim 0.1\;\mathrm{eV}

\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}\propto m_{a}^{0}\omega^{2/5}k_{a}^{-6/5}

EHR

\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}\propto g_{a\gamma\gamma}^{2}B_{0}^{1/3}R_{\text{\sc ns}}

\hat{{\bm{n}}}=\hat{{\bm{r}}}
via the factors
|{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})|

\psi_{\omega}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})

\beta_{T}(\hat{{\bm{n}}})

.
We explore the angular dependence further in Appendix

.

II.3.6
EFT considerations

Since the Wilson coefficient
g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}
of the dimension-8 Euler-Heisenberg operator appears in the denominator of the resonance condition (
12
), one might worry that the effective field theory (EFT) breaks down at the
EHR
.
However, through the following power-counting argument, we argue that this is not the case.

At the Lagrangian level, we compare the various terms that impact the electromagnetic field.
There is a dim-2 effective mass term due to medium effects, schematically written as
\mathscr{L}_{2}\sim\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}A^{2}
, and there is the dim-8 EH term, schematically written as
\mathscr{L}_{8}\sim g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}F^{4}\sim(e^{4}/m_{e}^{4})\omega^{2}\bar{B}^{2}A^{2}
where
g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}\sim e^{4}/m_{e}^{4}
.
We focus on only the terms that contain two photon field operators (
i.e.
,
\propto A^{2}
); there are also interaction operators (
i.e.
,
\propto A^{4}
), but these do not impact axion-photon mixing.
Going beyond mass dimension-8 operators, one would next encounter terms like
\mathscr{L}_{12}\sim(e^{6}/m_{e}^{8})F^{6}\sim(e^{6}/m_{e}^{8})\omega^{6}\bar{B}^{2}A^{2}
at mass dimension-12
Dunne 2004
.
At the
EHR
, the dim-2 and dim-8 operators are comparable by definition
\mathscr{L}_{8}/\mathscr{L}_{2}=O(1)
; note that Eq. (
12
) gives
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\sim(e^{4}/m_{e}^{4})\omega^{2}\bar{B}^{2}
.
The dim-12 operator goes as
\mathscr{L}_{12}/\mathscr{L}_{2}\propto r^{6}
, which is small at the star’s surface and grows with increasing distance from the star.
At the resonance radius
r_{\mathrm{res}}
it reaches a value of
\mathscr{L}_{12}/\mathscr{L}_{2}\sim e^{2}\omega^{4}/m_{e}^{4}\sim 10^{-30}(\omega/10\;\mathrm{meV})^{4}
.
However, provided that
\omega\ll m_{e}/\sqrt{e}\approx 1\;\mathrm{MeV}
, the dim-12 operator is tiny in comparison to the dim-2 and dim-8 operators at the resonance radius.
In short, although the resonance condition (
12
) needs
\omega
to be much larger than the plasma frequency, it still allows
\omega
to be much smaller than the electron mass, which sets the UV cutoff of the EFT.
Therefore, for the parameters of interest, we conclude that the EFT is under control at the
EHR
.

III
Terahertz emission due to axion dark radiation

Here we consider the electromagnetic radiation that may arise when axion dark radiation,
i.e.
a population of relativistic axions, is incident on a neutron star’s magnetosphere.
We assess the conditions under which resonant axion-photon conversion is expected to occur, and we calculate the resultant electromagnetic radiation spectrum.

III.1
Modeling axion dark radiation

We assume that relativistic axions are isotropically incident on the magnetosphere.
This is a reasonable expectation for all of the sources of axion dark radiation discussed in Sec.
IV
.
To take advantage of the system’s approximate spherical symmetry, it is convenient to imagine that the axions are being emitted from the center of the neutron star at
{\bm{r}}=0
.
We model the incident axion dark radiation with an energy flux spectrum,
\Phi_{a}(\omega)=\mathrm{d}E_{a}/\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{{\bm{n}}}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\omega
, which has the units of axion energy per unit solid angle per unit time per unit angular frequency.
We assume the emission is isotropic and static, meaning that
\Phi_{a}
doesn’t depend on either
\hat{{\bm{n}}}
or
t
.
For the sake of simplicity, we assume that the flux of axion radiation onto the neutron star can be modeled as a power-law across the observable frequency band.
This assumption lets us write

\begin{split}\Phi_{a}(\omega)&=\Phi_{a,0}\,\bigl(\omega/\omega_{0}\bigr)^{n}\;,\end{split}

(18)

where the exponent
n
is a (possibly non-integer) index.

III.2
Predicted electromagnetic radiation

If an axion with angular frequency
\omega
that propagates in the direction
\hat{{\bm{n}}}
(such that its momentum is
{\bm{k}}_{a}=(\omega^{2}-m_{a}^{2})^{1/2}\hat{{\bm{n}}}
) experiences resonant axion-photon conversion with probability
\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}(\omega,\hat{{\bm{n}}})
, then the average resultant energy flux spectrum of the emitted electromagnetic radiation is given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{\gamma}(\omega,\hat{{\bm{n}}})=\Phi_{a}(\omega)\,\mathbb{P}_{a\to\gamma}(\omega,\hat{{\bm{n}}})\;,

(19)

where
\Phi_{\gamma}(\omega,\hat{{\bm{n}}})=\mathrm{d}E_{\gamma}/\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{{\bm{n}}}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\omega
has the units of photon energy per unit solid angle per unit time per unit angular frequency.
Note that the probability depends on the orientation of the axion’s momentum
\hat{{\bm{n}}}
, because of the anisotropy of the dipolar magnetic field and plasma frequency.
Here we’ve also used the fact that an axion with energy
\omega
converts into a photon with the same energy.
The electromagnetic luminosity spectrum is obtained by integrating the flux over solid angle:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}(\omega)=\int_{4\pi}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{{\bm{n}}}\ \Phi_{\gamma}(\omega,\hat{{\bm{n}}})\;,

(20)

where

\displaystyle L_{\gamma}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\ \mathcal{L}_{\gamma}(\omega)=\int_{4\pi}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{{\bm{n}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\ \Phi_{\gamma}(\omega,\hat{{\bm{n}}})\;,

This calculation assumes that the neutron star is a distance

Additionally, this calculation neglects absorption of the radiation along the path of propagation, which effectively sets the optical depth

IV
Sources of axion dark radiation

The phrase ‘axion dark radiation’ refers to a population of relativistic axions, which we assume to be incident upon a neutron star’s magnetosphere with flux
\Phi_{a}(\omega)
.
Depending on their mass and interactions, relativistic axions may be produced in various ways.
Here we briefly mention several possibilities and estimate
\Phi_{a}
.

IV.1
Thermal relic axions

Axions may have reached thermal equilibrium with the primordial plasma in the early universe if their interactions with the Standard Model particles are sufficiently strong.
Later as the universe expanded and the plasma cooled, the axions’ interactions would have become inadequate to maintain thermal equilibrium, and they would have frozen out of equilibrium
Berezhiani et al. 1992
;
Salvio et al. 2014
;
Chacko et al. 2015
;
Baumann et al. 2016
;
Arias-Aragón et al. 2021
;
Arias et al. 2023
.
In this scenario, the Universe today is expected to contain a thermal cosmic axion background (CaB)
Conlon and Marsh 2013a
;
Dror et al. 2021
, similar to the thermal photon background (CMB) and the thermal neutrino background (C
\nu
B).

The predicted CaB energy per unit volume per unit angular frequency is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{a}}{\mathrm{d}\omega}=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{\omega^{3}}{e^{\omega/T_{a}}-1}\;,

(25)

assuming that the axion mass
m_{a}
is small compared to the CaB temperature today
T_{a}
.
If the CaB froze out when the photon temperature was
T_{d}
, then the conservation of comoving entropy implies that the CaB temperature today is
T_{a}\simeq[g_{\ast,S}(T_{0})/g_{\ast,S}(T_{d})]^{1/3}\,T_{\gamma}
where
T_{\gamma}\approx 0.234\;\mathrm{meV}
is the CMB temperature today.
We estimate the corresponding axion flux at the conversion radius
r_{\mathrm{res}}
as
\Phi_{a}(\omega)=\mathrm{d}E_{a}/\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{{\bm{n}}}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\omega\sim(\mathrm{d}E_{a}/\mathrm{d}V\mathrm{d}\omega)(cr_{\mathrm{res}}^{2}/\mathrm{sr})=(\mathrm{d}\rho_{a}/\mathrm{d}\omega)\,(cr_{\mathrm{res}}^{2}/\mathrm{sr})
.
For
T_{a}=0.1\;\mathrm{meV}
,
r_{\mathrm{res}}=100\;\mathrm{km}\approx 10R_{\text{\sc ns}}
, and
\omega=1\;\mathrm{meV}
this estimate gives
\Phi_{a}\sim 1\times 10^{9}\;\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{sr}/\mathrm{sec}/\mathrm{meV}
, which is much smaller than the fiducial value used in the estimate of
\omega\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}(\omega)
from Eq. (
24
).
Consequently, the thermal CaB is not expected to induce a detectable electromagnetic signal from resonant axion-photon conversion in a neutron star magnetosphere.

IV.2
Stellar axion emission

Axion dark radiation may be created in the universe today by scattering processes taking place within stars
Raffelt 1996
.
In particular, if axions couple to the constituents of a neutron star, and if the axion is lighter than the neutron star core temperature
T\sim 10\;\mathrm{keV}
, then neutron stars create their own flux of relativistic axions.
The dominant channels, typically nucleon bremstrahlung
NN\to NNa
(where
N=p,n
) or Cooper pair breaking and formation, are controlled by the axion-nucleon coupling
g_{aNN}
.
Since the star must not lose too much energy via axion emission, one can derive an upper limit on
g_{aNN}
from luminosity and age measurements of isolated neutron stars.
For example,
Buschmann et al. 2022
study axion emission from a set of five isolated neutron stars and derive limits on
g_{aNN}
, which are weakly model dependent, and generally at level of
|g_{aNN}|<1\times 10^{-9}
at
95\%
confidence.
When this inequality is saturated, the corresponding axion luminosity is on the order of
L_{a}\sim 10^{32}-10^{33}\;\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{sec}
where, for reference, the photon luminosity of the Sun is
L_{\odot}\approx 3.8\times 10^{33}\;\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{sec}
.
Assuming a thermal spectrum at
T\sim 10\;\mathrm{keV}
, the axion flux at angular frequencies around
\omega_{0}=10\;\mathrm{meV}
is
\Phi_{a}(\omega)\approx\Phi_{a,0}\,(\omega/\omega_{0})^{2}
with
\Phi_{a,0}\sim 10^{12}\;\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{sr}/\mathrm{sec}/\mathrm{meV}
.
This flux is much smaller than the fiducial value of
\Phi_{a,0}
used to estimate
\omega\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
at Eq. (
24
), indicating a negligible terrahertz signal.
There is a larger flux at higher energies (
\omega\sim T
), which may lead to X-ray emission via nonresonant conversion
Fortin and Sinha 2018
;
Buschmann et al. 2021
;
Fortin et al. 2021b
, but these energies are outside the window (
16
) that experience resonant conversion via the EHR.
Additional channels for axion emission include gap production in the polar cap regions
Prabhu 2021
;
Noordhuis et al. 2023a
;
Prabhu 2023
, electrobaryonic axion hair
Bai and de Lima 2024
, and axion stellar basin
Van Tilburg 2021
.
However, for each of these channels the axion spectrum is peaked far away from
\omega\approx\mathrm{meV}
and the flux in the
EHR
energy window is suppressed.
Consequently, the axion radiation produced by the neutron star itself is not expected to induce a detectable electromagnetic signal after resonant axion-photon conversion in the magnetosphere via the EHR.

IV.3
From dark matter decay or annihilation

Relativistic axions may also be created through the decay or annihilation of a heavier cosmological relic
Cicoli et al. 2013
;
Higaki and Takahashi 2012
;
Conlon and Marsh 2013a
;
Conlon and Marsh 2013b
;
Higaki et al. 2013b
;
Hebecker et al. 2014
;
Cicoli et al. 2014
;
Cui et al. 2018
;
Moroi and Yin 2021
;
Jaeckel and Yin 2022
.
For instance, the dark matter may be unstable but sufficiently long lived to survive in the Universe today.
If dark matter particles have a decay channel into much lighter axions, then dark matter decays would produce axion dark radiation
Dror et al. 2021
.
The spectrum of the axion dark radiation depends on the nature of the decay.
If the dark matter decays to a two-body final state, such as
\chi\to aa
, then the axion spectrum would have two components: a line centered at approximately half of the dark matter mass,
\omega\approx m_{\chi}/2
, associated with dark matter decaying today, and a low-frequency power-law tail associated with earlier decays into axions that experienced cosmological redshift.
Generating axions with energies around
1-100\;\mathrm{meV}
would require a dark matter particle with its mass around this scale.
Alternatively if the dark matter decays to a three-body (or higher) final state, then a broader spectrum of axion emission would result.
Similarly, if dark matter particles become trapped inside a neutron star and annihilate with one another
Bell et al. 2024
, then axion dark radiation could also be produced in this way.

To estimate the associated energy flux spectrum
\Phi_{a}(\omega)
we make the (wildly overly-generous) assumption that all of the dark matter energy is transformed into axion dark radiation at
\omega\approx 10\;\mathrm{meV}
.
In other words, we take
\mathrm{d}\rho_{a}/\mathrm{d}\omega\sim\rho_{\mathrm{dm}}/\omega
and assume that the dark matter energy density in the vicinity of the neutron star is comparable to the value in the Milky Way nearby to our solar system,
\rho_{\mathrm{dm}}\sim 0.3\;\mathrm{GeV}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}
.
By adapting the estimates used in Sec.
IV.1
, we obtain
\Phi_{a}\sim 1\times 10^{18}\;\mathrm{erg}/\mathrm{sr}/\mathrm{sec}/\mathrm{meV}
.
Comparing with the fiducial axion flux used in the estimate of Eq. (
24
), we do not anticipate a detectable signal due to axion dark radiation produced from dark matter decay.
It is worth mentioning that the dark matter density is much larger than the local value near the galactic center, but taking
d\approx 8\;\mathrm{kpc}
in Eq. (
24
) would incur a suppression of approximately
10^{-6}
, and moreover attenuation during propagation would become increasingly important.

IV.4
Topological defect production

Axion-like particles often arise in field theories as the pseudo-Goldstone boson of a spontaneously broken global symmetry.
If the symmetry breaking proceeded through a cosmological phase transition in the early universe, causality arguments
Kibble 1976
imply that a cosmological network of topological defects, typically strings and domain walls, should have formed.
A combination of analytical arguments and numerical simulations indicate that these defect networks evolve by exhausting energy into axions
Yamaguchi et al. 1999
;
Hagmann et al. 1999
;
Yamaguchi and Yokoyama 2003
;
Hiramatsu et al. 2011
;
Hiramatsu et al. 2012
;
Kawasaki et al. 2015
;
Lopez-Eiguren et al. 2017
;
Gorghetto et al. 2018
;
Kawasaki et al. 2018
;
Vaquero et al. 2019
;
Martins 2019
;
Buschmann et al. 2020
;
Hindmarsh et al. 2020
;
Klaer and Moore 2020
;
Gorghetto et al. 2021
;
Hindmarsh et al. 2021
.
If the axion is sufficiently light, those particles constitute a component of the axion dark radiation today
Harari and Sikivie 1987
;
Long 2018
.
Assuming that strings with tension
\mu
lose an order one fraction of their energy in each Hubble time into relativistic axions, then the accumulated axion dark radiation in the universe today can be estimated roughly as
\rho_{a}\sim\mu H_{0}^{2}\log(t_{\mathrm{eq}}/t_{\text{\sc pq}})
where logarithmic factor accounts for the build up of axion dark radiation from the time of string formation
t_{\text{\sc pq}}
until the time of radiation-matter equality
t_{\mathrm{eq}}
.
If the string tension were
\mu\sim(10^{14}\;\mathrm{GeV})^{2}
and the logarithimic factor were
\log\sim 10^{2}
, then the corresponding cosmological energy fraction in these axions is expected to be quite small
\rho_{a}/3M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}H_{0}^{2}\sim 10^{-6}
.
The corresponding axion flux
\Phi_{a}(\omega)
onto a neutron star magnetosphere would also be quite small, even in comparison with the estimates for thermal relic axions from Sec.
IV.1
.

V
Summary and conclusion

Let us first summarize the key elements of this work.

1.

We are interested in the resonant conversion of axions into photons in a neutron star magnetosphere. We identify two parametrically-distinct classes of resonant solutions; see Fig.
1
. The first, which we call the mass-matched resonance (
MMR
), can occur if there is a region outside the star where the plasma frequency crosses the axion mass,
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}\approx m_{a}
. The second, which we call the Euler-Heisenberg assisted resonance (
EHR
), can occur at a location where the axion energy satisfies
\omega=[2\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}/7g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}\bar{B}^{2}]^{1/2}
. This second resonance relies upon the Euler-Heisenberg four-photon self-interaction (
g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma}=8\alpha^{2}/45m_{e}^{4}
). Both resonances collectively have been called ‘axion-photon resonance’ by
Lai and Heyl 2006
.

2.

We focus our study on the
EHR
. We find that it is generally only accessible for relativistic axions with energy
\omega
in the range of
1\;\mathrm{meV}
to
100\;\mathrm{meV}
; see Fig.
2
. Additionally, for relativistic axions, we find that the
EHR
tends to induce a larger axion-photon conversion than either the MMR or nonresonant conversion; see Fig.
3
.

3.

We suppose that a population of relativistic axions is incident on a strongly-magnetized neutron star. We calculate the resultant electromagnetic radiation that would arise via the
EHR
as the axions propagate through the neutron star’s magnetosphere. We estimate the spectrum of radiation that would reach a detector on Earth; see Eq. (
24
). Obtaining a signal that is strong enough to detect would require a very large flux of axions. Moreover, the signal typically falls into the ‘terahertz gap’ between the microwave and infrared bands, which is attenuated by the atmosphere, making detection additionally challenging.

4.

We briefly survey a few possible sources of axion dark radiation. These include a primordial population of thermal relic axions, axions produced in stars, axions produced from the decay of dark matter (or other long-lived moduli), and axions produced from topological defect networks. These sources of axion dark radiation generally do not provide the requisite flux of axions to induce a detectable electromagnetic signal due to axion-photon conversion via the EHR.

Our main conclusions are that the
EHR
can provide the dominant channel for axion-photon conversion in a neutron star magnetosphere for axions with energy from
\omega\sim 1
to
100\;\mathrm{meV}
, provided that the magnetic field is sufficiently strong
B_{0}\gtrsim 10^{11}\;\mathrm{G}
and the axion is sufficiently light
m_{a}\lesssim\mu\mathrm{eV}
.
For typical values of the polar magnetic field strength
B\gtrsim 10^{11}\;\mathrm{G}
and the neutron star rotation period
P\sim 1-10\;\mathrm{sec}
, the resultant electromagnetic radiation would fall into the terahertz band, allowing for synergy with laboratory efforts to detect axions at terahertz masses
Barry, Pete and Chang, Clarence and Dona, Kristin
and Khatiwada, Rakshya and Knirck, Stefan and Kurinsky, Noah and Liu, Jesse
and Marsh, David and Miller, David and Noroozian, Omid and Sonnenschein,
Andrew 2021
;
Liu et al. 2022
.
However, given reasonable restrictions on the incident axion flux, the associated electromagnetic signal is not expected to be large enough to detect.

In our study we have adopted several simplifying assumptions in order to arrive at simple analytical relations for the properties of the
EHR
.
Of course, it is possible to relax these assumptions and revisit the analysis with a more accurate modeling of the axion-photon system.
For example, we assume axions propagate on radial trajectories out from the center of the star, but one can perform three-dimensional ray tracing as in Refs.
Safdi et al. 2019
;
Dessert et al. 2019
;
McDonald and Witte 2023
.
Moreover, we assume that any electromagnetic radiation produced by axion-photon conversion is able to escape from the neutron star environment and reach Earth, but one can also consider photon-plasma interactions and propagation effects as in Refs.
Witte et al. 2021
;
Tjemsland et al. 2024
.
However, we do not expect a more refined analysis to alter the broader conclusions about the detectability of axion-induced terahertz radiation from the
EHR
.

In this work we have focused on the conversion of axions into photons.
Although the
EHR
allows for a large conversion probability, the strength of the signal is limited by the tiny incident flux of axion dark radiation.
It may also be interesting to explore the conversion of photons into axions, which could lead to potentially detectable features in the spectrum or polarization of the background radiation.
For magnetic white dwarf stars, in which the weaker magnetic field makes the
EHR
inaccessible and the axion-photon conversion is nonresonant, similar studies have led to constraints on the axion-photon coupling from observations of polarized optical emission
Gill and Heyl 2011
;
Dessert et al. 2022b
.
As for neutron stars, a pair of recent studies have already investigated the role of the
EHR
to modulate the star’s spectrum and polarization.

Bondarenko et al. 2023
uses measurements of the high-frequency radio spectrum of a radio-loud magnetar to search for a kink-like feature (corresponding in our notation to
\omega\approx\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
where the
EHR
becomes accessible).
They conclude that if such a feature could be detected with
O(5-20\%)
sensitivity, it would provide a strong probe of axion-like particles at
g_{a\gamma\gamma}\gtrsim(0.5-2)\times 10^{-12}\;\mathrm{GeV}^{-1}
and
m_{a}\lesssim\mu\mathrm{eV}
.

Song et al. 2024
uses optical linear polarization measurements for a set of three neutron stars to search for an enhancement in polarized emission due to the
EHR
.
Using upper limits on the polarization degree for these stars, they obtain powerful constraints on the axion-photon coupling that can be as strong as
g_{a\gamma\gamma}<\mathrm{few}\times 10^{-12}\;\mathrm{GeV}^{-1}
for small
m_{a}
.
In our view these studies demonstrate the potentially important role of the
EHR
to probe axions in neutron star environments.

A.3

A set of constitutive relations determine

{\bm{E}}

{\bm{B}}

The constitutive relations may depend on
\omega

For a linear medium the constitutive relations take the form
{\bm{D}}=\epsilon\,{\bm{E}}

{\bm{H}}=\mu^{-1}\,{\bm{B}}

\epsilon({\bm{r}},t)

\mu^{-1}({\bm{r}},t)

If the medium were also isotropic and homogeneous, then the tensors would be independent of

\epsilon_{ij}=(1-\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}/\omega^{2})\,\delta_{ij}

\mu_{ij}^{-1}=\delta_{ij}

\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}

.
The squared plasma frequency is calculated by summing the contributions from each particle species in the system:

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}=\sum_{\psi}\omega_{\mathrm{pl},s}^{2}\qquad\text{where}\qquad\omega_{\mathrm{pl},s}^{2}=\frac{q_{s}^{2}e^{2}n_{s}}{m_{s}}\;,

(28)

where
q_{s}
is the electric charge,
n_{s}
is the number density, and
m_{s}
is the mass of particle species
s
.
Note
e=\sqrt{4\pi\alpha}\approx 0.303
.
In a nonrelativistic electron-ion plasma the plasma frequency contributions are
\omega_{\mathrm{pl},e}^{2}=e^{2}n_{e}/m_{e}
for the electrons and
\omega_{\mathrm{pl},\mathrm{ion}}^{2}=Z^{2}e^{2}n_{\mathrm{ion}}/m_{\mathrm{ion}}
for the ions with atomic number
Z
and mass number
A
.
Since
m_{e}\ll m_{\mathrm{ion}}/Z^{2}\approx Am_{p}/Z^{2}
even for reasonably large
Z
, it follows that
\omega_{\mathrm{pl},\mathrm{ion}}^{2}\ll\omega_{\mathrm{pl},e}^{2}
if the system is approximately charge neutral
n_{e}\approx n_{\mathrm{ion}}
.

For a neutron star magnetosphere, the strong dipolar magnetic field
{\bm{B}}
breaks the spatial isotropy of the system.
Charged particles tend to move up and down the field lines while orbiting in the transverse direction at their cyclotron frequency.
If we write
B=|{\bm{B}}|
and
\hat{{\bm{B}}}={\bm{B}}/B
then the cyclotron frequency for particles of species
s
is
\omega_{B,s}=|q_{s}|eB/m_{s}
.
Consequently the dielectric permittivity in the direction of
{\bm{B}}
is different from the permittivity in the directions normal to
{\bm{B}}
.
Since the particles can only move down the field lines, the conductivity in the transverse directions is approximately zero.
In the high magnetization limit,
i.e.

\omega_{B}\gg\omega,\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}
, the appropriate modification of the constitutive relations is
Gurevich et al. 1993

\begin{split}{\bm{D}}({\bm{r}},t)&=\epsilon({\bm{r}},t)\,{\bm{E}}({\bm{r}},t)\qquad\text{where}\qquad\epsilon_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\tfrac{\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\,\hat{B}_{i}\hat{B}_{j}\\
{\bm{H}}({\bm{r}},t)&=\mu^{-1}({\bm{r}},t)\,{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}},t)\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mu_{ij}^{-1}=\delta_{ij}/\end{split}

(29)

In other words, the electric permittivity is
1-\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}/\omega^{2}
in the direction of the background magnetic field
{\bm{B}}
, and it is
1
in the orthogonal directions.
Note that
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}({\bm{r}},t)
varies throughout the neutron star magnetosphere, and we treat it as a part of the background solution.
In the equatorial regions, the neutron star magnetosphere primarily consists of a nonrelativistic electron-proton plasma
Hook et al. 2018
, and the plasma frequency is well approximated by just the electron contribution
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}=e^{2}n_{e}/m_{e}
.
On the other hand, in the polar regions, a relativistic electron-positron plasma is present, and the calculation of axion-photon conversion near the poles requires a different analysis, which we do not consider here; see for example Refs.
Safdi et al. 2019
;
Noordhuis et al. 2023a
;
Caputo et al. 2023
;
Noordhuis et al. 2023b
.

A.4

A neutron star magnetosphere possesses a strong electromagnetic field associated with the star’s large magnetic dipole moment.
Additionally the axion dark radiation corresponds to an ambient axion field.
However, we are interested in the much weaker electromagnetic and axion fields associated with radiation propagating through this environment.

A.5
Goldreich-Julian background solution

The background fields are required to solve

\begin{split}&\ddot{\bar{a}}-\nabla^{2}\bar{a}+m_{a}^{2}\bar{a}=0\ ,\qquad{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\bar{{\bm{D}}}=\bar{\rho}_{f}\ ,\qquad{\bm{\nabla}}\times\bar{{\bm{H}}}=\bar{{\bm{j}}}_{f}+\dot{\bar{{\bm{D}}}}\\
&\quad{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\bar{{\bm{B}}}=0\ ,\qquad{\bm{\nabla}}\times\bar{{\bm{E}}}=-\dot{\bar{{\bm{B}}}}\ ,\qquad\bar{{\bm{D}}}=\bar{{\bm{E}}}\ ,\qquad\bar{{\bm{H}}}=\bar{{\bm{B}}}\;,\end{split}

(32)

where we have neglected the
O(g_{a\gamma\gamma})
and
O(g_{\gamma\gamma\gamma\gamma})
terms.
We take
\bar{a}({\bm{r}},t)=0
, since the background axion field associated with axion dark radiation is not expected to be very large.
In the polar regions where
\bar{{\bm{E}}}\cdot\bar{{\bm{B}}}
can be significant, a nonzero
\bar{a}
can be induced by the axion-photon interaction (
g_{a\gamma\gamma}
terms)
Prabhu 2021
;
Noordhuis et al. 2023a
;
Prabhu 2023
, but we neglect this effect since it is not expected to significantly impact resonant axion-photon conversion.

We model the background magnetic field in the region outside of the neutron star as a magnetic dipole that rotates at the same speed as the star.
Here we use a Cartesian coordinate system and take the origin to be the center of the star such that the position vector is
{\bm{r}}=x\,\hat{{\bm{x}}}+y\,\hat{{\bm{y}}}+z\,\hat{{\bm{z}}}
with
r=|{\bm{r}}|=(x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2})^{1/2}
and
\hat{{\bm{r}}}={\bm{r}}/r
.
We write the star’s angular velocity as
{\bm{\Omega}}=\Omega\,\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}
where
\Omega=2\pi/P
and
P
is the rotation period of the star and
\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}=\hat{{\bm{z}}}
is the orientation of the angular velocity.
We further suppose that the magnetic dipole moment is

\displaystyle{\bm{m}}(t)=m\,\sin\theta_{m}\cos(\phi_{m}+\Omega t)\,\hat{{\bm{x}}}+m\,\sin\theta_{m}\sin(\phi_{m}+\Omega t)\,\hat{{\bm{y}}}+m\,\cos\theta_{m}\hat{{\bm{z}}}\;,

(33)

which remains constant in magnitude
m=|{\bm{m}}(t)|
, and which varies in orientation
\hat{{\bm{m}}}(t)={\bm{m}}(t)/m
while maintaining a constant the angle with respect to the star’s rotation axis:
\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}\cdot\hat{{\bm{m}}}(t)=\cos\theta_{m}
.
This idealized magnetic dipole moment is a contribution to the free current density
\bar{{\bm{j}}}_{f}({\bm{r}},t)
that is only nonzero at the origin.
This approximation is reliable in the magnetosphere outside the star
(r\geq R_{\text{\sc ns}})
, but it would break down inside the star.
The background
\bar{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{r}},t)
field must obey Ampere’s Law
{\bm{\nabla}}\times\bar{{\bm{H}}}=\bar{{\bm{j}}}_{f}+\dot{\bar{{\bm{D}}}}
, which implies

\begin{split}&\bar{\bm{H}}({\bm{r}},t)=\frac{3{\bm{m}}(t)\cdot{\bm{r}}}{r^{5}}\,{\bm{r}}-\frac{{\bm{m}}(t)}{r^{3}}\qquad\text{for $r\geq R_{\text{\sc ns}}$}/\end{split}

(34)

Note that
{\bm{\nabla}}\times\bar{{\bm{H}}}=0
everywhere except at the origin, which is the location of the magnetic dipole.
In order for Ampere’s Law to be satisfied, we will need to adjust the free current such that
\bar{{\bm{j}}}_{f}+\dot{\bar{{\bm{D}}}}=0
.
The background magnetic field
\bar{{\bm{B}}}({\bm{r}},t)
must obey the constitutive relation
\bar{{\bm{H}}}=\bar{{\bm{B}}}
since
\mu^{-1}=1
, which implies

\begin{split}&\bar{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}},t)=B_{0}\,{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{r}}},t)\,\biggl(\frac{r}{R_{\text{\sc ns}}}\biggr)^{\!\!-3}\qquad\text{where}\qquad{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{r}}},t)=\tfrac{3}{2}\bigl(\hat{{\bm{m}}}(t)\cdot\hat{{\bm{r}}}\bigr)\,\hat{{\bm{r}}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{{\bm{m}}}(t)\qquad\text{for $r\geq R_{\text{\sc ns}}$}/\end{split}

(35)

Note that the magnetic field strength at the star’s surface in the direction of
{\bm{m}}
is
B_{0}=\bar{{\bm{B}}}(R_{\text{\sc ns}}\,\hat{{\bm{m}}},t)=2m/R_{\text{\sc ns}}^{3}
, and it is useful to treat
B_{0}
as the free parameter rather than
m
.
Note that this expression for the background magnetic field obeys the magnetic divergence condition
{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\bar{{\bm{B}}}=0
.
The background electric field
\bar{{\bm{E}}}({\bm{r}},t)
must obey Faraday’s Law
{\bm{\nabla}}\times\bar{{\bm{E}}}=-\dot{\bar{{\bm{B}}}}
.
One solution is

\displaystyle\bar{{\bm{E}}}({\bm{r}},t)=-\bigl({\bm{\Omega}}\times{\bm{r}}\bigr)\times\bar{{\bm{B}}}({\bm{r}},t)\;,

(36)

and other solutions may be obtained by adding the gradient of a scalar field, since the additional term would have zero curl.
Note that
\bar{{\bm{E}}}\cdot\bar{{\bm{B}}}=0
.
The associated electric displacement field
\bar{{\bm{D}}}({\bm{r}},t)
is given by the constitutive relation
\bar{{\bm{D}}}=\bar{{\bm{E}}}
, and thereby implies

\displaystyle\bar{{\bm{D}}}({\bm{r}},t)=-\bigl({\bm{\Omega}}\times{\bm{r}}\bigr)\times\bar{{\bm{B}}}({\bm{r}},t)/

(37)

The free charge density
\bar{\rho}_{f}({\bm{r}},t)
is given by the in-medium Gauss’s Law,
{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\bar{{\bm{D}}}=\bar{\rho}_{f}
, which implies

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}_{f}({\bm{r}},t)=-2{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\bar{{\bm{B}}}({\bm{r}},t)=-2\Omega B_{0}\ \hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}\cdot{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{r}}},t)\ \biggl(\frac{r}{R_{\text{\sc ns}}}\biggr)^{\!\!-3}\qquad\text{for $r\geq R_{\text{\sc ns}}$}/

(38)

If the star rotates so rapidly that relativistic corrections near its surface become significant, then an additional factor of
[1-|{\bm{\Omega}}\times{\bm{r}}|^{2}]^{-1}
is required.
Similarly the free current density
\bar{{\bm{j}}}_{f}({\bm{r}},t)
is given by the condition
\bar{{\bm{j}}}_{f}+\dot{\bar{{\bm{D}}}}=0
, implying

\displaystyle\bar{{\bm{j}}}_{f}({\bm{r}},t)=\bigl({\bm{\Omega}}\times{\bm{r}}\bigr)\times\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bar{{\bm{B}}}({\bm{r}},t)/

(39)

Note that if the dipole were aligned with the rotation axis of the star, corresponding to
\hat{{\bm{m}}}=\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}
, then the current would vanish
\bar{{\bm{j}}}_{f}=0
.
The free charge and current densities arise from the motion of the plasma’s charged constituents: nonrelativistic electrons and protons.
In particular we can write
\bar{\rho}_{f}({\bm{r}},t)=(-e)\bar{n}_{e}({\bm{r}},t)+(+e)\bar{n}_{p}({\bm{r}},t)
where
\bar{n}_{e}
and
\bar{n}_{p}
are the number densities of electrons and protons, respectively.
The squared plasma frequency arises primarily from the lighter electrons, and we have
\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}({\bm{r}},t)=e^{2}\bar{n}_{e}({\bm{r}},t)/m_{e}
.
If we estimate
\bar{n}_{e}\approx|n_{c}|
where
n_{c}\equiv\bar{\rho}_{f}/(-e)
is the effective number density of electric charge then the plasma frequency is given by

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{pl}}({\bm{r}},t)

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathrm{pl},0}\ \psi_{\omega}(\hat{{\bm{r}}},t)\ \biggl(\frac{r}{R_{\text{\sc ns}}}\biggr)^{\!\!-3/2}\quad\text{where}\quad\omega_{\mathrm{pl},0}=\sqrt{\frac{e\Omega B_{0}}{m_{e}}\frac{n_{e}}{|n_{c}|}}

(40)

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374pt\quad\text{and}\quad\psi_{\omega}(\hat{{\bm{r}}},t)=\Bigl|2\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}\cdot{\bm{\psi}}_{B}(\hat{{\bm{r}}},t)\Bigr|^{1/2}\qquad\text{for $r\geq R_{\text{\sc ns}}$}/

We estimate

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{pl},0}\approx\bigl(70\;\mu\mathrm{eV}\bigr)\biggl(\frac{B_{0}}{10^{14}\;\mathrm{G}}\biggr)^{\!\!1/2}\biggl(\frac{P}{1\;\mathrm{sec}}\biggr)^{\!\!-1/2}\biggl(\frac{n_{e}}{|n_{c}|}\biggr)^{\!\!1/2}\,\;,

(41)

using a fiducial set of parameters that are appropriate for a magnetar.

Note that the effective fluid speed
v\sim\bar{j}_{f}/\bar{\rho}_{f}\sim r\Omega
grows with increasing distance from the star.
The approximation of a dipolar magnetic field with a frozen-in plasma breaks down at the light cylinder where
r\approx\Omega^{-1}\equiv R_{\text{\sc lc}}
.
Our analysis focuses on
r\ll R_{\text{\sc lc}}
where speeds are much smaller and the Goldreich-Julian dipole model is reliable.

For the purposes of our calculation, it is a good approximation to neglect the background electric field
\bar{{\bm{E}}}
.
One is led to this conclusion from the following argument.
Inspecting Eq. (
36
), on sees the parametric relationship
\bar{E}\sim\Omega r\bar{B}
.
At the star’s surface
r\sim R_{\text{\sc ns}}
and this estimate gives
\bar{E}\sim\Omega R_{\text{\sc ns}}\bar{B}
where
\Omega R_{\text{\sc ns}}=2\pi R_{\text{\sc ns}}/P
is the speed of a point on the surface of the star that rotates with period
P
.
Different types of neutron stars have different rotation periods, ranging from millisecond pulsars with
P\approx 0.001\ \mathrm{sec}
to magnetars with
P\approx 10\ \mathrm{sec}

Lorimer et al. 2006
;
Kaspi and Beloborodov 2017
;
Nättilä and Kajava 2022
.
For a magnetar with
P=1\ \mathrm{sec}
and
R_{\text{\sc ns}}=10\ \mathrm{km}
, the speed is
\Omega R_{\text{\sc ns}}\approx 2\times 10^{-4}
, much smaller than the speed of light, and consequently
\bar{E}\ll\bar{B}
.
Since one also expects solutions with
|\delta\hskip-0.56917pt{\bm{E}}|\sim|\delta\hskip-0.56917pt{\bm{B}}|
, then it is a good approximation to neglect terms that contain factors of
\bar{{\bm{E}}}
.

For the fixed background, the plane wave solutions do not mix, but if the background varies slowly, then a plane wave in the axion field induces a plane wave in the electromagnetic field, or vice versa.

A.7
Propagation on a slowly-varying background

Whereas the previous discussion was restricted to an idealized system with axion and electromagnetic waves propagating on a homogeneous and static background, we now turn our attention a more accurate modeling of the relevant physical system.
As these waves propagate in a neutron star magnetosphere, the background fields can be modeled as a rotating magnetic dipole.
Under this assumption, one can solve the linearized equations of motion using numerical methods and calculate the probability of axion-photon interconversion.
However, if one seeks to derive analytical expressions, this background, which varies in both space and time, leads to equations that are intractable.
Moreover, for the regime of interest, the calculation naturally simplifies in several respects, which we now enumerate.

•

To study the conversion of relativistic axions into photons, it is a good approximation to treat the background as static. Since these waves travel at nearly the speed of light, the time it takes for them to traverse the neutron star’s magnetosphere is short compared to the rotation period of the star, and on these times scales the background is effectively static. Moreover, if the conversion is resonantly enhanced, then it typically occurs in a small region of space, and the light-crossing time for this region is generally much smaller than the rotation period of the star, which again justifies treating the background as static.

•

Additionally, it is a good approximation to treat the background fields as homogeneous in the direction transverse to the axis of propagation. In the framework of quantum mechanics, the incident axion can be viewed as a wavepacket. The spatial extent of the wavepacket is on the order of the de Broglie wavelength for the particle, which set by its momentum
{\bm{p}}
via
\lambda_{p}=2\pi/|{\bm{p}}|
. If the momentum is sufficiently large, then the wavepacket is tiny, and the background fields are approximately homogeneous on the scale of the wavepacket,
i.e.

\lambda_{p}\ll|\bar{{\bm{B}}}|/|\nabla\bar{{\bm{B}}}|\sim R_{\text{\sc ns}}
. This justifies neglecting variations in the background fields transverse to the direction of motion. See also
Carenza and Marsh 2023
for a discussion of when this approximation breaks down.

•

It is a good approximation to neglect the background axion field. The presence of axion dark radiation does not lead to a very large amplitude for the axion field.

•

Finally we make one assumption for convenience. The direction of the magnetic field is expected to vary along the path of the axion. This is the case for any ray passing through a magnetic dipole, except ones passing through the origin. In our study of propagation on a fixed background, we saw that the mode polarized normal to the plane spanned by
\bar{{\bm{B}}}
and
{\bm{k}}
decouples from the other two modes. If the background magnetic field changes orientation, all three modes will couple during the propagation, which leads to a more challenging analysis. As a matter of convenience, we assume that the orientation of the background magnetic field remains fixed along the axion’s path of propagation. This is accomplished by restricting
{\bm{r}}(t)
to be a radial path that passes through the center of the star. One can study the more general field configuration by solving the equations of motion numerically, and when this was done in earlier work the numerical and analytical results were found to agree with order one factors
Dessert et al. 2019
, and we expect a similar error here.

A.8
Axion-photon interconversion probability

The equations of motion in Eq. (
55

In particular, to calculate the probability that an axion is converted into a photon as it passes through the neutron star’s magnetosphere, we set a boundary condition at the star’s surface where

We are especially interested in the behavior as

For pedagogical purposes, let us first suppose that the mixing matrix is homogeneous.

,
\Delta_{\parallel}

With this assumption, Eq. (

(58)

However, if

\Delta_{\parallel}(r)

\Delta_{B}(r)

then the mixing angle is near maximal

This resonant contribution to the probability can be calculated analytically, as we demonstrate in the following section.

A.9

In the regime where the axion mass and plasma frequency can be neglected, then the conversion probability admits a particularly simple expression.

m_{a}=0
