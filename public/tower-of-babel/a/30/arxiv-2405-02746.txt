Sharp Spectral Projection Estimates for the Torus at p = 2 ​ ( n + 1 ) n − 1 p=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
Daniel Pezzi

Abstract
. We prove sharp spectral projection estimates for general tori in all dimensions at the exponent p c = 2 ​ ( n + 1 ) n − 1 p_{c}=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1} for shrinking windows of width 1 1 down to windows of length λ − 1 + κ \lambda^{-1+\kappa} for fixed κ > 0 \kappa>0 . This improves and generalizes the work of Blair–Huang–Sogge which proved sharp results for windows of width λ − 1 n + 3 \lambda^{-\frac{1}{n+3}} in [ 1 ] , and the work of Hickman [ 8 ] , Germain–Myerson [ 7 ] , and Demeter–Germain [ 6 ] which proved results for windows of all widths but incurred a sub-polynomial loss. Our work uses the approaches of these two groups of authors, combining the bilinear decomposition and microlocal techniques of Blair–Huang–Sogge with the decoupling theory and explicit lattice point lemmas used by Hickman, Germain–Myerson, and Demeter–Germain to remove these losses.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Stating the problem

The goal of this paper is to improve on spectral projection estimates for general tori. Let
\mathbb{T}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}/(e_{1}^{*}\mathbb{Z}+...+e_{n}^{*}\mathbb{Z})
where
\{e_{i}^{*}\}
is a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We will denote the corresponding lattice
L^{*}
, and all implicit constants will be allowed to depend on this lattice. For ease of computation we will also assume a scaling such that the injectivity radius is larger than
\frac{1}{2}
. Let
k_{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}e_{i}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
where
e_{i}
is the dual basis vector to
e_{i}^{*}
. Let
L
denote the dual lattice to
L^{*}
. An orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
is given by

\{e_{k_{L}}(x)\coloneq c_{k_{L}}e^{2\pi i\langle k_{L},x\rangle}:{k_{L}}\in L\}.

Where the
c_{k_{L}}
are normalization constants such that
\left\lVert e_{k_{L}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}=1
. Each of these vectors is an eigenvector of the Laplacian such that

-\Delta e_{k_{L}}(x)=4\pi^{2}|k_{L}|^{2}e_{k_{L}}(x)\coloneq\lambda^{2}e_{k_{L}}(x).

We adopt the convection that
\mathbb{T}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}/L^{*}
as we will primarily be working with the lattice dual to
L^{*}
. We then have the following representation of
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
in terms of this basis.

f(x)=\sum_{{k_{L}}\in L}\hat{f}(k_{L})e_{k_{L}}(x),\hskip 7.22743pt\hat{f}(k_{L})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f(x)e_{k_{L}}(-x)dx.

We shall also normalize so that
||{f}||_{2}=1
. We define at the outset the following spectral projection operators for the operator
\sqrt{-\Delta}
,

\displaystyle P_{\lambda,\delta}f\coloneq\sum_{{k_{L}}\in A_{\lambda,\delta}}\hat{f}(k_{L})e_{k_{L}}(x),

where
A_{\lambda,\delta}
is the annulus of radius

\lambda
and width
2\delta
. Because of our normalization for Fourier series, we have abbreviated
2\pi k_{L}\in A_{\lambda,\delta}
as
k_{L}\in A_{\lambda,\delta}
. Our goal is to prove bounds on these spectral projection operators in terms of
\lambda
and
\delta
. The case of
\delta=1
was settled by Sogge for all compact boundaryless manifolds, see
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
. That is to say width 1 projectors satisfy the universal estimates

\left\lVert P_{\lambda,1}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\leq C\lambda^{\mu(p)}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})},

where
\mu(p)=\max\{\frac{n-1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}),\frac{n-1}{2}-\frac{n}{p}\}
. Here,
C
is a constant that does not depend on the spectral parameter
\lambda
but may depend on other parameters such as
n,p,
and
L^{*}
. The transition between these two regimes occurs at the critical exponent
p_{c}=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
.

To gain improvements over these universal estimates we must consider shrinking windows, that is
\delta\rightarrow 0
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. The case
\delta=1
is settled and the smallest width that will be considered is
\sim\lambda^{-1}
as any smaller window results in the projection operator just being a projection onto an eigenspace for rational tori. Shrinking windows do not give improvements for all manifolds as
S^{n-1}
follows the same bounds as the universal case
(\delta\sim 1)
for all values of
\delta
. In the negative and non-positive sectional curvature cases gains are expected, see
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
, and
[
9
]
for results at this level of generality. In that setting, there is a natural barrier at windows of width
\sim(\log\lambda)^{-1}
. The fact that the universal covers of general manifolds have a number of Dirichlet domains that grows exponentially with respect to the radius of a ball centered at the origin make it difficult to analyze smaller windows with known methods. The torus case is different as its universal cover is a tiling of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which clearly has a number of Dirichlet domains that grows polynomially with the radius of a ball centered at the origin. This allows us to access the conjectured bounds for the torus all the way down to the smallest reasonable window for rational tori
\sim\lambda^{-1}
.

The conjecture we are interested in is the following, originally stated by Germain–Myerson and partially proved in
[
7
]
. We state it in its full level of generality for reference.

Conjecture 1.1

(The Germain–Myerson conjecture, spectral projection bounds for the tours)
.

Let
\delta>\lambda^{-1+\kappa}
for some fixed
\kappa\in(0,1]
. Then for some constant
C=C(n,p,\kappa,L^{*})
the spectral projection operators for
\mathbb{T}^{n}
obey the following estimates

(1)

\left\lVert P_{\lambda,\delta}\right\rVert_{2\rightarrow p}\leq\begin{cases}C(n,p,\kappa,L^{*})(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}=C(\lambda\delta)^{\mu_{1}(p)}&p\leq p_{c}\\
C(n,p,\kappa,L^{*})(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}=C(\lambda\delta)^{\mu_{1}(p)}&p_{c}\leq p\leq p^{*}\text{ and }\delta\leq\lambda^{e(p)}\\
C(n,p,\kappa,L^{*})\lambda^{(\frac{n-1}{2}-\frac{n}{p})}\delta^{1/2}=C\lambda^{\mu_{2}(p)}\delta^{1/2}&p^{*}\leq p\text{ and }\delta\geq\lambda^{e(p)}.\\
\end{cases}

Where
p_{c}=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
is the critical exponent,
p^{*}=\frac{2n}{n-2}
, and
e(p)=\frac{n+1}{n-1}(\frac{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p_{c}}}{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{n-3}{2(n-1)}})
. We have labeled

\mu_{1}(p)=\frac{n-1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}),\hskip 5.69046pt\mu_{2}(p)=\frac{n-1}{2}-\frac{n}{p}.

We are not concerned with the optimal value of
C
, only in obtaining the correct dependence on
\lambda
and
\delta
. When the torus in question is
\mathbb{T}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, one conjectures analogous bounds up to
\delta\sim\lambda^{-1}
. This is the discrete restriction conjecture studied extensively by Bourgain, Demeter, and others. The above conjecture can be viewed as a generalization of the universal estimates of Sogge and the discrete restriction conjecture.

Constructions of the examples that saturate the conjectured bounds and an explanation of the relevant constants are provided by Germain–Myerson in Section 3 of
[
7
]
. They also demonstrate the conjectured bounds would be sharp if true.

Partial progress has been made on Conjecture
1.1
. In particular, Germain–Myerson proved the following

Theorem 1.2

(Spectral projection bounds for the torus with
\epsilon
-loss,
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{T}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}/L^{*}
. Let
\delta>\lambda^{-1+\kappa}
for some fixed
\kappa\in(0,1]
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
and some
C(\epsilon)=C(n,p_{c},\kappa,L^{*},\epsilon)
we have

(2)

\left\lVert P_{\lambda,\delta}\right\rVert_{2\rightarrow p_{c}}\leq C(n,p_{c},\kappa,L^{*},\epsilon)\lambda^{\epsilon}(\lambda\delta)^{\mu_{1}(p_{c})}=C(\epsilon)\lambda^{\epsilon}(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{1}{p_{c}}},

which after interpolation with the trivial
L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}
estimates yields

(3)

\left\lVert P_{\lambda,\delta}\right\rVert_{2\rightarrow p}\leq C(n,p,\kappa,L^{*},\epsilon)\lambda^{\epsilon}(\lambda\delta)^{\mu_{1}(p)}\hskip 8.5359pt2\leq p\leq p_{c},

which verifies Conjecture
1.1
for
2\leq p\leq p_{c}
“with
\epsilon
-loss”.

The removal of these sub-polynomial losses in this setting is the subject of this paper. In the case
\mathbb{T}^{2}=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, Demeter–Germain in
[
6
]
removed these
\epsilon
-losses to prove sharp results when
2\leq p<p_{c}
.

Progress was also made when
p>p_{c}
for some region of
(p,\delta)
by Germain–Myerson in all dimensions in
[
7
]
. These results took inspiration from and improved over the results of Hickman
[
8
]
. We defer to those papers for a full account.

All of these approaches critically use the decoupling theorem of Bourgain and Demeter which incurs a
O_{\epsilon}(\lambda^{\epsilon})
loss at the critical exponent
p_{c}
. This loss is necessary when the decoupling theorem is applied as was proven by Bourgain in
[
3
]
, and so a sharp version of Theorem
1.2
cannot come from an abstract sharpening of the decoupling theorem. The conjecture at
p=p_{c}
without epsilon loss was proved for windows of width
\delta>\lambda^{-\frac{1}{n+3}}
by Blair–Huang–Sogge in
[
1
]
using methods applicable to more general manifolds that do not include technology such as decoupling. The general strategy of this paper will be to combine the techniques of Blair–Huang–Sogge with the techniques of Hickman, Germain–Myerson, and Demeter–Germain. This leads to the following sharp result.

Theorem 1.3

(Spectral projection bounds for the torus)
.

Let
\mathbb{T}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}/L^{*}
. Let
\delta>\lambda^{-1+\kappa}
for some fixed
\kappa\in(0,1]
. Then for some
C=C(n,p_{c},\kappa,L^{*})
we have

(4)

\left\lVert P_{\lambda,\delta}\right\rVert_{2\rightarrow p_{c}}\leq C(n,p_{c},\kappa,L^{*})(\lambda\delta)^{\mu_{1}(p_{c})}=C(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{1}{p_{c}}},

which after interpolation with the trivial
L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}
estimates yields

(5)

\left\lVert P_{\lambda,\delta}\right\rVert_{2\rightarrow p}\leq C(n,p,\kappa,L^{*})(\lambda\delta)^{\mu_{1}(p)},\hskip 8.5359pt2\leq p\leq p_{c}.

This verifies Conjecture
1.1
for
2\leq p\leq p_{c}
(without an
\epsilon
-loss).

Remark 1.4
.

p_{c}=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
is referred to as the critical exponent in both decoupling theory and in the Euclidean analog of this problem, but it is not strictly speaking a critical exponent for the Germain–Myerson conjecture as the full result does not follow from the result at
p_{c}
. Instead we have a critical curve in the
(\delta,p)
plane. When
\delta=1
the corresponding critical value is
p_{c}
but when
\delta\sim\lambda^{-1}
the corresponding critical value is
p^{*}=\frac{2n}{n-2}
. Still, we will call
p_{c}
the critical exponent as it bears this name in other problems. We note that this value of
p
is also called the Stein-Tomas exponent in the context of restriction theory.

1.2.
Notation

We will write
A\lesssim B
to mean that
A\leq CB
where
C
is a constant. For this paper, we will always allow the implicit constant to depend on
n,p,\kappa
and the lattice
L^{*}
. For this problem
\kappa
is fixed and our final estimates will not be defined as
\kappa
approaches
0
. In particular, this approach cannot give sharp eigenfunction bounds for
\mathbb{T}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{Z}^{n}
which corresponds to
\kappa=0
. It is important that all implicit constants do not depend on the spectral parameter
\lambda
. For example, we may rewrite the main result as

\left\lVert P_{\lambda,\delta}\right\rVert_{2\rightarrow p}\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\mu_{1}(p)},\hskip 8.5359pt2\leq p\leq p_{c}.

If we wish to highlight dependence we will write
A\lesssim_{\epsilon}B
to mean that
A\leq C(\epsilon)B
. The statement “with
\epsilon
loss” will refer to
A\lesssim_{\epsilon}\lambda^{\epsilon}B
for arbitrarily small but fixed choice of
\epsilon>0
. The smaller the choice of
\epsilon
, the larger the multiplied constant that depends on
\epsilon
. These are also referred to as “sub-polynomial losses” as the dependence in
\lambda
must grow smaller than any polynomial. The expression
A\leq\ln(\lambda)B
is an example of a bound that exhibits sub-polynomial losses. We will not worry about explicit bounds on the sub-polynomial factor. It is only relevant that such a factor grows slower than any fixed power of
\lambda
. We write
A\sim B
to mean
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
for the same list of parameters. Writing a quantity is
\sim A
means equality up to a choice of multiplied constant.

We use the notation
2^{j}\ll 1
to indicate that the value of the dyadic sequence
\{2^{j}\}
should not exceed some fixed absolute constant that depends on the dimension
n
.

We say that an object
A
has rapid decay if
A\leq C(N)\lambda^{-N}
for each natural number
N
. Such terms in expressions are often unimportant error terms as by selecting
N
large the object
A
will overpower any polynomial powers of
\lambda
.

When we state that a parameter or amount is
O(1)
, we always do so with respect to the spectral parameter
\lambda
. Such constants are allowed to depend on the parameters we hide with
\lesssim
. Throughout this paper,
C
will be used to denote fixed constants whose specific values we wish to suppress. The value of
C
will be allowed change from line to line but will never depend on
\lambda
.

1.3.
An overview of the proof

This paper largely follows the work of Blair–Huang–Sogge
[
1
]
which began by approximating the spectral projection operator with a smoothed out version. This argument is standard. Microlocal cutoffs
Q_{\nu}
were then introduced. The phase support of the kernels
Q_{\nu}(x,y)
were contained in the intersection of an annulus of radius and width
\sim\lambda
and a cone with aperture
\delta
centered at the origin pointing in the direction
\nu
, where
\nu
comes from a
\delta
separated set in
S^{n-1}
. These operators satisfy estimates that align with the conjecture when composed with
P_{\lambda,1}
and can be inserted into the problem with acceptable losses.

Next, a bilinear decomposition in terms of the
Q_{\nu}
was deployed. Terms in this decomposition were sorted into two operators: one collected the diagonal terms where the supports of the kernels were close, and the other collected the far terms where the supports of the kernels were sufficiently separated. This can be encoded in the separation of the
\nu
directions. Techniques developed in that paper were able to handle both the diagonal terms and the far terms in the context of general manifolds of non-positive sectional curvature, and sharp results were obtained.

There are two major departures in this paper from the work of Blair–Huang–Sogge needed to get sharp results in the torus case. First, we employ a bilinear decomposition of the discrete projection operator and only introduce the microlocal cutoffs afterwards. This sidesteps the use of estimates in the original paper that did not present issues with the larger windows under consideration, but are problematic with our target width of
\lambda^{-1+\kappa}
. Second, we will handle the off diagonal terms using decoupling, a cap counting result, and an explicit computation using Fourier series, tools which are not available in the more general case and require our projection operator to be written as a Fourier multiplier. This is another motivation for doing the bilinear decomposition before introducing microlocal cutoffs.

The diagonal terms are handled much in the same way as
[
1
]
, but are easier in this setting because of the added flexibility of working with torus multipliers. Whereas the estimate proved in that paper needed to be an
l^{p_{c}}
norm, we can instead use the larger
l^{2}
norm and orthogonality.

2.
A decomposition

We follow the setup in
[
1
]
but instead partition the Fourier multiplier before any approximation. Our decomposition into multipliers will be analogous to our decomposition that defines the microlocal cutoffs
Q_{\nu}
that will be used later.

Let
\theta_{0}=\delta
. This will always be the value of
\theta_{0}
but we distinguish the two to track which contributions come from the angular decomposition and which come from the window, to maintain consistency with previous work, and to clarify matters when we eventually define
\theta_{k}=2^{k}\theta_{0}
. Let
\{\nu\}
be a maximal
\theta_{0}
-separated set in
S^{n-1}
such that every
\xi\in S^{n-1}
is within
\theta_{0}
of a
\nu
. This collection of points will also be used to define the
Q_{\nu}
. For a fixed
\nu
, let
a_{\nu}
be the set of
\xi\in S^{n-1}
such that
|\xi-\nu|<|\xi-\nu^{\prime}|
for all
\nu\neq\nu^{\prime}
. As written there may be a set of points such that
|\nu-\xi|=|\nu^{\prime}-\xi|
for
\nu\neq\nu^{\prime}
. We remove such a
\xi
from one of
a_{\nu},a_{\nu^{\prime}}
in this situation so that
\{a_{\nu}\}
is a partition of
S^{n-1}
. Define

A_{\lambda,\delta}^{\nu}\coloneq\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\xi|\in(\lambda-\delta,\lambda+\delta),\xi/|\xi|\in a_{\nu}\}.

It suffices to prove the results in this paper with the
\nu
restricted to a sufficiently small
O(1)
neighborhood of
(0,...,0,1)
. This follows as any rotated lattice is again a lattice, so the problem has rotational symmetry, and the triangle inequality. This reduction is to allow us to organize a sum based on the projection of
\nu
to
\{x:x_{n}=0\}
in Section 4.

Define

(6)

P_{\nu}f\coloneq\sum_{{k_{L}}\in A^{\nu}_{\lambda,\delta}}\hat{f}(k_{L})e_{k_{L}}(x),

which allows us to make the following organization

(7)

(P_{\lambda,\delta}f)^{2}=\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\in\Xi_{\theta_{0}}}P_{\nu}fP_{\nu^{\prime}}f+\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\notin\Xi_{\theta_{0}}}P_{\nu}fP_{\nu^{\prime}}f=\Upsilon^{\text{diag}}(f)+\Upsilon^{\text{far}}(f),

where
(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\in\Xi_{\theta_{0}}
if and only if
|\nu-\nu^{\prime}|<2^{r}\theta_{0}=C\theta_{0}
for some fixed small integer
r
. Here
r
defines to threshold between the diagonal and far terms. Its exact value is not relevant as long as it is fixed and larger than say 2. Our goal now is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1

(Spectral projection operators for the torus at
p_{c}
)
.

Let
\delta>\lambda^{-1+\kappa}
for some
\kappa\in(0,1]
. Let
\mathbb{T}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}/L^{*}
. Then

(8)

||{P_{\lambda,\delta}f}||_{L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\leq||{(\Upsilon^{diag}f)^{1/2}}||_{{L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}}+||{(\Upsilon^{\text{far}}f)^{1/2}}||_{{L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}}\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{1}{p_{c}}}||{f}||_{{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}}.

The first inequality follows from our previous discussion and the triangle inequality. Interpolation with the trivial estimates shows that this implies Theorem 1.3. The bound for the far terms is the content of Proposition
4.1
. Section
3
will establish some preparatory results to aid in that proof. The diagonal terms will be handled by Proposition
5.3
which relies on microlocal estimates which are proved in Section
6
.

3.
Cap Counting

To prove their result with
\epsilon
-loss, Germain–Myerson used the decoupling theorem of Bourgain–Demeter which necessitates covering the annulus
A_{\lambda,\delta}
with caps of a specific geometry. Decoupling will also be used in our proof. In this section we shall establish the relevant properties of the caps, present an abridged version of the proof with
\epsilon
-loss using decoupling, and discuss how decoupling will be utilized in our proof of sharp results.

3.1.
Understanding caps

In this context, a
cap
will refer to a box in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose dimensions are given by
c_{1}(\lambda\delta)^{1/2}\times...\times c_{n-1}(\lambda\delta)^{1/2}\times c_{n}\delta
. The constants
c_{i}
will not depend on
\lambda
, will have no bearing on our arguments, and are oftentimes suppressed. Caps are allowed to have arbitrary center and orientation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. More often in applications of decoupling caps are of the form
\sim R^{-1}\times...\times R^{-1}\times R^{-2}
for some large positive
R
. This discrepancy is a result of our scaling as we are considering
\lambda S^{n-1}
not
S^{n-1}
. A suitable change of variables aligns the two perspectives.

Let
\Omega=\{\omega\}
be a collection of finitely disjoint caps
\omega
that cover the annulus
A_{\lambda,\delta}
. That is,
A_{\lambda,\delta}\subset\cup_{\omega\in\Omega}\omega
. Also, define

\#\omega\coloneq\#(\omega\cap 2\pi L).

This is the number of lattice point contained in a cap
\omega
. The factor of
2\pi
is because of our normalization of Fourier series. When referring to lattice points in a cap we mean the lattice
2\pi L
. Later, we will relate the number of lattice points in a cap to the
L^{p}
norm of
P_{\omega}f
, the Fourier projection onto frequencies contained in the cap
\omega
. As written the caps possibly overlap. To deal with this we employ a partition of unity. Let
\chi_{\omega}
be smooth functions such that
0\leq\chi_{\omega}\leq 1
,
\text{supp}\,\chi_{\omega}\subset\omega,
and
\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\chi_{\omega}=1
on
A_{\lambda,\delta}
. Initially define

(9)

P_{\omega}^{\prime}f\coloneq\sum_{k_{L}\in\omega}\chi_{\omega}(2\pi k_{L})\hat{f}(k_{L})e_{k_{L}}(x),

and

(10)

P_{\omega}f\coloneq P_{\lambda,\delta}P_{\omega}^{\prime}f.

We clearly have
P_{\lambda,\delta}=\sum_{\omega\in\omega}P_{\omega}
.

While we include the projection
P_{\lambda,\delta}
in the definition of
P_{\omega}
to make this a true decomposition of our operator, it will often be more productive to think of
P_{\omega}f
as having Fourier support in the lattice points contained in
\omega
not
\omega\cap A_{\lambda,\delta}
. The reason for this is the convexity of the caps
\omega
and the easier geometric interpretation of it being a box in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In doing so we will discuss estimates for lattice points in the cap
\omega
which is clearly an upper bound for the number of lattice points in
\omega\cap A_{\lambda,\delta}
. As our estimates involving the
L^{p}
norm of
P_{\omega}f
will be bounded by the total number of lattice points in its Fourier support so bounding by the number of lattice points in
\omega
instead leads to worse results that will still be sufficient.

Returning to the properties of the caps, the lattice points in
\omega
have a dimension as a vector space (after shifting the cap to the origin, consider the span over
\mathbb{R}
of the lattice points contained in the cap). We will denote this dimension
d_{\omega}
. This is important as by the log convexity of
L^{p}
norms we have the following inequality for caps
\omega
.

(11)

\left\lVert P_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim(\#\omega)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\left\lVert P_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})},\,p\geq 2.

Here we used the trivial
L^{2}\rightarrow L^{\infty}
and
L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}
bounds. Recall that
\omega
has a short side of length
\delta
. This means that if
d_{\omega}=n
we have
\#\omega\lesssim\delta(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}
which is the volume of the cap. We expect this bound to hold for most caps.

If
d_{\omega}=n-1
, we may have the worst possible bound of
\#\omega\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}
. Additionally, for such an
\omega
, one may check that using (
11
) with this bound on the number of lattice points gives

(12)

\left\lVert P_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\left\lVert P_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})},\,p\geq 2.

Which aligns with the conjectured bound at
p_{c}
. The saturating example, the Knapp example in this context, is a function whose frequency support is contained in a single cap
\omega
that contains the maximum number of lattice points. This is the “geodesic focusing case” as in physical space, such a function is concentrated in the neighborhood of a line which is a geodesic on the (flat) torus.

The result with an extra
O_{\epsilon}(\lambda^{\epsilon})
factor follows from the bound in (
12
), which clearly holds for any cap, after an application of the decoupling theorem. We shall later show that there are not many caps with the maximum number of lattice points. Despite using the bound
\#\omega\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}
for all caps in the decoupling argument, the only loss comes from decoupling itself because such a bound is the saturating example and because of orthogonality. Let us see how decoupling “almost” proves sharp results.

3.2.
The almost proof using decoupling

We will now introduce the decoupling theorem and show how it was used to prove the result with
\epsilon
-loss.

Theorem 3.1

(Discrete decoupling for the sphere,
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\Omega
be a finitely disjoint covering of
\lambda S^{n-1}
by
c_{1}(\lambda\delta)^{1/2}\times...\times c_{n-1}(\lambda\delta)^{1/2}\times c_{n}\delta
caps. Then for any 1-separated set
\xi_{\alpha}\subset\lambda S^{n-1}
and sequence
\{a_{\alpha}\}
it follows that for any fixed
\epsilon>0
,

(13)

\Bigl|\Bigl|{\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}e(x\cdot\xi_{\alpha})}\Bigr|\Bigr|_{L^{p_{c}}(B_{\delta^{-1}})}\lesssim_{\epsilon}\lambda^{\epsilon}\Bigl(\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\Bigl|\Bigl|{\sum_{\alpha:\,\xi_{\alpha}\in\omega}a_{\alpha}e(x\cdot\xi_{\alpha})}\Bigr|\Bigr|_{L^{p_{c}}(w_{B_{\delta^{-1}}})}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2},

where
B_{\delta^{-1}}
is a spatial ball of radius
\delta^{-1}
and
L^{p_{c}}(w_{B_{\delta^{-1}}})
is a weighted norm.

It is known that there must be a
O_{\epsilon}(\lambda^{\epsilon})
loss in the above theorem, see
[
5
]
for a discussion of this and a precise definition of the weighted norm appearing on the right hand side. That the above theorem can be applied in our situation is known and was used by Hickman
[
8
]
and Germain–Myerson
[
7
]
to make progress on Conjecture
1.1
.

The outline for applying this result to our situation is as follows. We will always take
\xi_{\alpha}
to be coming from a lattice, which allows us to interpret the exponential sums as Fourier series. The localization at scale
\delta^{-1}
provides uncertainty at scale
\delta
in Fourier space, so we can consider points
\xi_{\alpha}
that are contained in the annulus
A_{\lambda,\delta}
. This is done rigorously in the proof of Corollary 9 in
[
8
]
. Fourier series are periodic, and so the spatial norms can be replaced by an integral over the fundamental domain of the lattice which can be interpreted as
\mathbb{T}^{n}
. The loss
\lambda^{\epsilon}
ostensibly depends on
\lambda
and
\delta
, but the exact power is subsumed by the
\epsilon
exponent and so this dependency is suppressed. This justifies the following statement which is what we shall apply in our proofs.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a collection of
c_{1}(\delta\lambda)^{1/2}\times...\times c_{n-1}(\delta\lambda)^{1/2}\times c_{n}\delta
finitely disjoint caps covering the annulus
A_{\lambda,\delta}
. Let
P_{\lambda,\delta}
be the Fourier projection operator onto
A_{\lambda,\delta}
and
P_{\omega}
be defined as in (
10
). We then have

(14)

\left\lVert P_{\lambda,\delta}f\right\rVert_{L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim_{\epsilon}\lambda^{\epsilon}\Bigl(\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\left\lVert P_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

This is essentially the form reached in Corollary 9 in
[
8
]
. Combined with (
11
) this immediately gives the result as

(15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\lVert P_{\lambda,\delta}f\right\rVert_{L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}&\lesssim_{\epsilon}\lambda^{\epsilon}\Bigl(\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\left\lVert P_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\\
&\lesssim_{\epsilon}\lambda^{\epsilon}\sup_{\omega}(\#\omega)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{c}}}\Bigl(\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\left\lVert P_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\\
&\lesssim_{\epsilon}\lambda^{\epsilon}(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{c}})}||{f}||_{2}.\end{split}

The above argument shows that while the majority of caps contain a number of lattice points less than
(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}
, the caps with a full number of lattice points dominate the norm. The above argument is also sharp up to the
O_{\epsilon}(\lambda^{\epsilon})
factor which comes from the application of decoupling
not
the lattice point bound for an
\omega
. The
O_{\epsilon}(\lambda^{\epsilon})
is mandatory in the sense that an abstract application of decoupling must involve at least a
\log(\lambda)^{c}
loss so the above argument cannot prove sharp bounds by an improved general decoupling estimate.

Decoupling, however, will be useful for us because of its ability to handle a large number of caps. We will label caps that contain near the maximum number of lattice points “bad” and ones that contain less “good”. Specifically, for a fixed
\eta>0
, define

(16)

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{\text{good}}=\{\omega:\#\omega\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}-\eta}\},

(17)

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{\text{bad}}=\{\omega:\#\omega\gtrsim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}-\eta}\}.

Define
P_{\text{good}}
and
P_{\text{bad}}
in an analogous way to
P_{\omega}
using the same partition of unity. Clearly

(18)

P_{\lambda,\delta}=P_{\text{good}}+P_{\text{bad}}.

This is motivated the above argument as all good caps can be handled by an application of decoupling. We have by (
15
)

(19)

\left\lVert P_{\text{good}}f\right\rVert_{L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim_{\epsilon}\lambda^{\epsilon}(\lambda\delta)^{(\frac{n-1}{2}-\eta)(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{c}})}||{f}||_{2}.

As
-\eta(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{c}})
is negative for any choice of positive
\eta
we can select
\epsilon
small enough so that

(20)

\left\lVert P_{\text{good}}f\right\rVert_{L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim_{\eta}(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{c}})}||{f}||_{2}.

And so decoupling can be used to show the good caps obey sharp bounds. This handles the vast majority of caps. The rest of our argument will be to bound the projection onto the bad caps. To do so we will need a quantitative bound on the number of such caps which is the subject of the next subsection and will the bad caps to be handled in Section
4
.

Remark 3.3
.

The above bound for the good caps is included for illustrative purposes. In our full argument we employ the decomposition described in Section 2. We will then only use the this section’s results involving caps on the far terms. The argument also works by first estimating the good caps as has been described, then using a bilinear decomposition only on the remaining bad caps. While this may lead to a cleaner exposition, it is also strictly weaker and so we have chosen to argue in this order.

3.3.
A result about bad caps

Our goal is to bound the number of bad caps. The threshold for a cap to be considered bad is controlled by the parameter
\eta
which we may select freely as long as it is positive and does not depend on
\lambda
. It is allowed to depend on other parameters in the problem such as
n
and
L^{*}
.

As the caps
\omega
have
n-1
long sides of length
(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}
and
1
short side of length
\delta
, the caps such that
d_{\omega}=n
cannot have near the maximum number of lattice points. The caps such that
d_{\omega}\leq n-2
obviously do not near the maximum number of lattice points as the separation distance between any two points is at least
O(1)
. Therefore, by a sufficiently small choice of
\eta
, every bad cap has
d_{\omega}=n-1
.

To count these caps we will use Theorem 4.1 from
[
7
]
. In that paper, Germain–Myerson chose to analyze the equivalent problem of projecting onto a
\delta
neighborhood of a quadratic form
Q(x)
with the lattice fixed to be
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. We fix our quadratic form to be given by the sphere and let the lattice
L
vary. This is necessitated by the use of the microlocal operators later in the paper. The following proposition states that the number of bad caps can be made small by taking
\eta
to be sufficiently small.

We will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
C_{\text{bad}}
be the collection of all caps such that
\#\omega\gtrsim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}-\eta}
with
\eta
chosen small enough such that
d_{\omega}=n-1
for every
\omega\in C_{\text{bad}}
. We then have

(21)

\#C_{\text{bad}}\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\eta n}.

This is essentially the same result as Germain–Myerson Theorem 4.1 and our proof assuming that theorem is largely a change of variables. We recall Theorem 4.1 now, with some slight alterations. In that paper different notation and conventions were used. We have translated their result to our notation but the content is the same. Additionally we will restrict to the range of
\eta
such that
d_{\omega}=n-1
for every bad
\omega
which allows for the following simpler version of Theorem 4.1.
1
1

1

Specifically,
[
7
]
indexed their collection of caps by
2^{j}
by gathering caps containing
\sim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\delta 2^{j}
lattice points. For the theorem to hold as stated we must take
2^{j}>K\delta^{-1}(\lambda\delta)^{-1/2}
where
K
is an absolute constant from that paper. For the same condition to be satisfied by our bad caps we need
\delta^{-1}(\lambda\delta)^{-\eta}>2^{j}>K\delta^{-1}(\lambda\delta)^{-1/2}
or
\eta<\frac{1}{2}
. This is our first condition on
\eta
.
We have

Lemma 3.5

(Theorem 4.1,
[
7

)
.

Let

be almost disjoint caps intersected with

such that

. Let

for a sufficiently small

. Then

(22)

C^{Q}_{\text{bad}}\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\eta n}.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
assuming Lemma
3.5
.

The
\theta
are not caps by our convention but caps intersected with
S_{\lambda\,\delta}
. Let
M:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the invertible linear transformation such that

. We apply the change of variables given by
M(x_{1},...,x_{n})=(s_{1},...,s_{n})
.

In the
s
-variables our lattice is given by
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
A_{\lambda,\delta}
is the
c_{M}\delta
neighborhood of
\sqrt{Q}
for some quadratic form
Q
. The constant

which of course depends only on
L
and not
\lambda
. Constants throughout this proof will share this dependence.

We have that
A_{\lambda,\delta}\subset\cup\omega

\omega
is not necessarily a cap in the
s

\omega
must fit in a cap in the
s

\omega
we will call
\theta^{\prime}(\omega)
an

is linear.

Clearly the collection of

4.
Handling the far terms

4.1.
A reduction

We first wish to prove a bound like (
5
) for
\Upsilon^{\text{far}}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

||{(\Upsilon^{\text{far}}f)^{1/2}}||_{p_{c}}=||{(\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\notin\Xi_{\theta_{0}}}P_{\nu}fP_{\nu^{\prime}}f)^{\frac{1}{2}}}||_{p_{c}}=||{(\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\notin\Xi_{\theta_{0}}}P_{\nu}fP_{\nu^{\prime}}f)}||_{p_{c}/2}^{1/2}\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{1}{p_{c}}}||{f}||_{2}.

The diagonal terms will be handled in the next section. We shall organize our sum using a Whitney decomposition as was done successfully in Section 4 of
[
1
]
. Here we use our reduction to directions close to
(0,...,0,1)
.

We organize based on the first
n-1
coordinates. Consider dyadic cubes in
\{x_{n}=0\}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
of sidelength
\theta_{k}=\theta_{0}2^{k}
. We will label
\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}
as the translations of
[0,\theta_{k})^{n-1}
by
\mu\in\theta_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
.

We do not have to worry about
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
that are close by the definition of
\Xi_{\theta_{0}}
, so we will instead worry about organizing based on distance. We will call two cubes close if they have sidelength
\theta_{k}
, are not adjacent, and are contained in adjacent cubes of sidelength
2\theta_{k}
. Clearly there are
O(1)
close pairs for any fixed cube, and all close cubes are separated by about
\theta_{k}
. We write
\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}\sim\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu^{\prime}}
if two cubes
\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu},\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu^{\prime}}
of sidelength
\theta_{k}
are close.

We will write
\nu\in\tau_{\mu}^{\theta_{k}}
if the projection of
\nu
onto the first
n-1
coordinates is within
\tau_{\mu}^{\theta_{k}}
. Given
(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\not\in\Xi_{\theta_{0}}
there is a unique set
(\theta_{k},\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu},\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu^{\prime}})
such that
\nu\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu},\nu^{\prime}\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu^{\prime}}
, and
\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}\sim\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu^{\prime}}
. Our reduction to directions near
(0,...,0,1)
ensures that projections of
(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
onto the first
n-1
coordinates being close implies that
(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
are close as points on
S^{n-1}
.

All of this allows for the organization

(24)

\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\notin\Xi_{\theta_{0}}}(P_{\nu}f)(P_{\nu^{\prime}}f)=\sum_{\{k\geq r:2^{k}\theta_{0}\ll 1\}}\sum_{(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}\sim\tau_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\theta_{k}}}\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\in\tau_{\mu}^{\theta_{k}}\times\tau_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\theta_{k}}}(P_{\nu}f)(P_{\nu^{\prime}}f).

Where
r
is such that
|\nu-\nu^{\prime}|<2^{r}\theta_{0}\implies(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\in\Xi_{\theta_{0}}
and the maximum value of
k
is the smallest such that
\sup_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}|\nu-\nu^{\prime}|<2^{k}\theta_{0}
. As
\nu,\nu^{\prime}
come from the sphere this quantity is
O(1)
. The advantage of this organization is that we have isolated the terms that contribute. The first sum contains
\sim\log(\lambda)
terms, while the second sum has the property that for a fixed
\mu
there exist only
O(1)
values of
\mu^{\prime}
such that the relation
\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}\sim\tau_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\theta_{k}}
holds. This allows us to reduce to the following proposition.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
P_{\gamma},P_{\gamma^{\prime}}
be projections onto disjoint angular sectors of
A_{\lambda,\delta}
that are subsets of a fixed neighborhood of
(0,...,0,1)
. Let each be defined by aperture
\sim\theta_{k}
and have angular separation
\sim\theta_{k}
from each other. There exists a fixed positive number
\sigma
such that the following holds.

(25)

||{(P_{\gamma}fP_{\gamma^{\prime}}f)^{1/2}}||_{p_{c}}\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{1}{p_{c}}-\sigma}\Bigl(||{P_{\gamma}f}||_{2}||{P_{\gamma^{\prime}}f}||_{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Before we prove this, let us show that it implies Proposition
4.1
.

Proof that Proposition
4.2
\implies
Proposition
4.1
.

Note that by our restriction using the triangle inequality and rotation, the following equality holds

(26)

P_{\gamma}\Bigl(\sum_{\nu\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}}P_{\nu}f\Bigr)=\sum_{\nu\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}}P_{\nu}f,

if we widen the definition of
P_{\gamma}
by a constant that does not depend on
\lambda
. This allows us to apply the following inequalities assuming Proposition
4.2
.

\displaystyle||{(\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\notin\Xi_{\theta_{0}}}P_{\nu}fP_{\nu^{\prime}}f)^{\frac{1}{2}}}||_{p_{c}}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{\{k\geq r:2^{k}\theta_{0}\ll 1\}}\sum_{(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}\sim\tau_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\theta_{k}}}||{\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\in\tau_{\mu}^{\theta_{k}}\times\tau_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\theta_{k}}}P_{\nu}fP_{\nu^{\prime}}f}||_{p_{c}/2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sum_{\{k\geq r:2^{k}\theta_{0}\ll 1\}}\sum_{(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}\sim\tau_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\theta_{k}}}||{P_{\gamma}\Bigl(\sum_{\nu\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}}P_{\nu}f\Bigr)P_{\gamma^{\prime}}\Bigl(\sum_{\nu^{\prime}\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu^{\prime}}}P_{\nu^{\prime}}f\Bigr)}||_{p_{c}/2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Bigl(\sum_{\{k\geq r:2^{k}\theta_{0}\ll 1\}}\sum_{(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}\sim\tau_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\theta_{k}}}(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{2}{p_{c}}-2\sigma}||{\sum_{\nu\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}}P_{\nu}f}||_{2}||{\sum_{\nu^{\prime}\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu^{\prime}}}P_{\nu^{\prime}}f}||_{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{1}{p_{c}}-\sigma}\Bigl(\sum_{\{k\geq r:2^{k}\theta_{0}\ll 1\}}\sum_{(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}\sim\tau_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\theta_{k}}}\Bigl(\sum_{\nu\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}}||{P_{\nu}f}||_{2}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{\nu^{\prime}\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu^{\prime}}}||{P_{\nu^{\prime}}f}||_{2}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{1}{p_{c}}-\sigma}\Bigl(\sum_{\{k\geq r:2^{k}\theta_{0}\ll 1\}}\Bigl(\sum_{(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}\sim\tau_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\theta_{k}}}\sum_{\nu\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}}||{P_{\nu}f}||_{2}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}\sim\tau_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\theta_{k}}}\sum_{\nu^{\prime}\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu^{\prime}}}||{P_{\nu^{\prime}}f}||_{2}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{1}{p_{c}}-\sigma}\Bigl(\sum_{\{k\geq r:2^{k}\theta_{0}\ll 1\}}\sum_{\mu}\sum_{\nu\in\tau^{\theta_{k}}_{\mu}}||{P_{\nu}f}||_{2}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

Here we have used the fact that the first sum only has

Using the relation

P_{\gamma}=P_{\gamma}P_{\lambda,\delta}=P_{\gamma}P_{\text{good}}+P_{\gamma}P_{\text{bad}},

naturally define the projections

(27)

4.2.
Estimating the bad caps

To estimate the bad caps, we will use an explicit computation of the
L^{4}
norm. We will then interpolate with the
L^{2}\rightarrow L^{\infty}
or
L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}
norm depending on
n
.

The following general result connects the geometry of lattice points in caps to
L^{4}
norms.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
A,B
be subsets of a lattice
L
. For
(a,b)\in A\times B
, define

S(a,b)\coloneq\{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in A\times B:a+b=a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}\},

and suppose
S
is such that
S(a,b)\leq S
for every
a,b\in A\times B
. Then

\left\lVert(P_{A}fP_{B}f)^{1/2}\right\rVert_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim S^{1/4}\Bigl(\left\lVert P_{A}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\left\lVert P_{B}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\Bigr)^{1/2}

where
P_{A}f
and
P_{B}f
are the Fourier projections of
f
onto the set
A,B
respectively.

Proof.

This is an explicit calculation. We have

\displaystyle||{(P_{A}fP_{B}f)^{1/2}}||_{4}^{4}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}|\sum_{(a,b)\in A\times B}\hat{f}(a)\hat{f}(b)e_{a+b}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}|\sum_{c\in A+B}\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a+b=c\\
(a,b)\in A\times B\end{subarray}}\hat{f}(a)\hat{f}(b)\Bigr)e_{c}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in A+B}|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a+b=c\\
(a,b)\in A\times B\end{subarray}}\hat{f}(a)\hat{f}(b)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(a,b)\in A\times B}|\hat{f}(a)\hat{f}(b)|\Bigr(\sum_{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in S(a,b)}|\hat{f}(a^{\prime})\hat{f}(b^{\prime})|\Bigl)

\displaystyle\leq S^{1/2}\sum_{(a,b)\in A\times B}|\hat{f}(a)\hat{f}(b)|\Bigr(\sum_{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in S(a,b)}|\hat{f}(a^{\prime})\hat{f}(b^{\prime})|^{2}\Bigl)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq S^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{(a,b)\in A\times B}|\hat{f}(a)\hat{f}(b)|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigr(\sum_{(a,b)\in A\times B}\sum_{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in S(a,b)}|\hat{f}(a^{\prime})\hat{f}(b^{\prime})|^{2}\Bigl)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=S\bigl(\sum_{a\in A}|\hat{f}(a)|^{2}\bigr)\bigl(\sum_{b\in B}|\hat{f}(b)|^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=S||{P_{A}f}||_{2}^{2}||{P_{B}f}||_{2}^{2}.

Where we used Plancharel and Cauchy-Schwarz twice. Taking

∎

5.
Handling the Diagonal Terms

5.1.
Bounding the diagonal terms assuming microlocal estimates

To bound the diagonal terms we will use microlocal operators
Q_{\nu}
that act on functions on the torus as was done in
[
1
]
, specifically Section 6 of that paper. While the operator here will be the same as the one defined in that paper up to a change of variables, the estimates we will use will look slightly different as we have not replaced our projection operators with a smoothed out approximation. In this section we will define the
Q_{\nu}
and assume three estimates involving it, then show how this allows us to finish the argument. Section
6
is then dedicated to proving these estimates.

Let us define the
Q_{\nu}
where
\nu
is taken from the same set that defined
P_{\nu}
. Let
\sum\psi(x)=1
be a smooth partition of unity of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
. We demand that the support of each
\psi
be small enough so that once
\mathbb{T}^{n}
is identified with the fundamental domain of
L^{*}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\psi f
has a periodic extension via
L^{*}
to all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for any
f
. This can be done by taking
\text{supp}\,\psi
to be contained in a ball of sufficiently small radius. This radius only depends on the chosen lattice
L^{*}
and dimension
n
. There are
O(1)
cutoffs
\psi
and its properties do not depend on the spectral parameter.

For each
\psi
in this partition define a
\tilde{\psi}
that is
1
on the support of
\psi
and whose support is small enough such that
\tilde{\psi}f
can be extended for every
f
in the manner previously described. We shall use the
\psi
to partition the torus. Technically, there exists a
Q_{\nu}
for each
\psi
but as we will always analyze after applying the triangle inequality we suppress this dependence.

Let
\sum_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}(\xi)=1
be a partition of unity on
S^{n-1}
such that
\beta_{\nu}
is supported in a
2\theta_{0}
cap centered at
\nu\in S^{n-1}
. Also write
\beta_{\nu}
for the degree zero homogeneous extension of these bump functions such that they serve as a partition of unity for all
\xi
away from the origin. Additionally, let
\tilde{\beta}(\xi)
be a bump function that is supported on
[1/4,4]
and identically 1 on
[1/2,2]
. Define the kernel

Q_{\nu}(x,y)\coloneq\tilde{\psi}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\langle x-y,\xi\rangle}\beta_{\nu}(\xi/|\xi|)\tilde{\beta}(2\pi|\xi/\lambda|)d\xi.

Where
x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
and
y
will be integrated over
\mathbb{T}^{n}
. It is important for our purposes that
\tilde{\psi}
is compactly supported in such a way that
Q_{\nu}f
is again a well defined function on the torus. In that light, if we identify
\mathbb{T}^{n}
with a tiling of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we initially define
Q_{\nu}f
on the fundamental domain then consider its periodic extension which is well defined because of the properties of
\tilde{\psi}(x)
.

Remark 5.1
.

Constructing the
Q_{\nu}
in this context obscures the subtlety that went into their construction. This is because of a property of the torus; the geodesic flow is given by straight lines whose direction does not depend on the value of
x
. In the case of general manifolds of non-positive curvature, the symbols of
Q_{\nu}
were constructed to be invariant under the geodesic flow and depended both on
x
and
\xi
. Additionally,
\psi(x)
selects out a coordinate chart of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
, but in the general case the partition of unity also selected out directions
\xi
as well. Working on the torus simplifies our analysis at the risk of making the definition feel mystical. One should confer
[
1
]
Section 4 for an in depth explanation of the construction as well as properties the
Q_{\nu}
that are not relevant for this work.

There are 3 properties of the
Q_{\nu}
that we shall use to conclude.

Proposition 5.2
.

Fix a
\psi
from the partition of unity and consider its associated mircrolocal cutoffs
Q_{\nu}
. Suppose
(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
are such that
|\nu-\nu^{\prime}|\geq C\theta_{0}
for some fixed

that can be taken sufficiently small. Then for every positive integer

\displaystyle\Bigl|\Bigl|{(I-\sum_{\nu}Q_{\nu})\psi P_{\lambda,1}f}\Bigr|\Bigr|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim_{N}\lambda^{-N}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}

(36)

\displaystyle\left\lVert Q_{\nu^{\prime}}\psi P_{\nu}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim_{N}\lambda^{-N}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}

(37)

\displaystyle\sup_{\nu}\left\lVert Q_{\nu}\psi P_{\lambda,\delta}f\right\rVert_{L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim_{\kappa}(\lambda\delta)^{\mu_{1}(p_{c})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}

The first two estimates follow from non-stationary phase and show that the given terms enjoys rapid decay in

[

after an application of duality and orthogonality. The proof of all three of these estimates will be given in Section

Proposition 5.3
.

Assume the results in Proposition
5.2
. Let

\left\lVert(\Upsilon^{\text{diag}}f)^{1/2}\right\rVert_{L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}=\left\lVert\Upsilon^{\text{diag}}f\right\rVert_{L^{p_{c}/2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}^{1/2}\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{\frac{1}{p_{c}}}||f||_{2}.

Proof.

Note that for a fixed
\nu
, there are only
O(1)

\nu^{\prime}

(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\in\Xi_{\theta_{0}}

\displaystyle||{\Upsilon^{diag}f}||_{p_{c}/2}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=||{\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\in\Xi_{\theta_{0}}}P_{\nu}fP_{\nu^{\prime}}f}||_{p_{c}/2}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\in\Xi_{\theta_{0}}}||{P_{\nu}fP_{\nu^{\prime}}f}||_{p_{c}/2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\in\Xi_{\theta_{0}}}||{P_{\nu}f}||_{p_{c}}||{P_{\nu^{\prime}}f}||_{p_{c}}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\in\Xi_{\theta_{0}}}||{P_{\nu}f}||_{p_{c}}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/4}\Bigl(\sum_{(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\in\Xi_{\theta_{0}}}||{P_{\nu^{\prime}}f}||_{p_{c}}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/4}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Bigl(\sum_{\nu}||{P_{\nu}f}||_{p_{c}}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

We now introduce the partition of unity
\psi
. By the triangle inequality it is sufficient to bound

\Bigl(\sum_{\nu}||{\psi P_{\nu}f}||_{p_{c}}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

By (
35
) and the relation

, we have

\Bigl(\sum_{\nu}||{\psi P_{\nu}f}||_{p_{c}}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\lesssim_{N}\Bigl(\sum_{\nu}||{\sum_{\nu^{\prime}}Q_{\nu^{\prime}}\psi P_{\nu}f}||_{p_{c}}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}+O(\lambda^{-N^{\prime}}).

Here

\nu

in the inner sum, the fact that

\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{\nu}||{\sum_{\nu^{\prime}:|\nu^{\prime}-\nu|<C\theta_{0}}Q_{\nu^{\prime}}\psi P_{\nu}f}||_{p_{c}}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\lesssim\Bigl(\sum_{\nu}\Bigl(\sum_{\nu^{\prime}:|\nu^{\prime}-\nu|<C\theta_{0}}||{Q_{\nu^{\prime}}\psi P_{\lambda,\delta}P_{\nu}f}||_{p_{c}}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

∎

Remark 5.4
.

That the bound for

6.
Proving the mircrolocal estimates

We have reduced matters to showing the three estimates in Proposition

After expanding the operators, an application of non-stationary phase yields rapid decay. Proving the last bound is more involved.

6.1.
Proving proposition
5.2
, estimates (

) and (
36

Our goal in this section is to prove the following two estimates.

\displaystyle\Bigl|\Bigl|{(I-\sum_{\nu}Q_{\nu})\psi P_{\lambda,1}f}\Bigr|\Bigr|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim_{N}\lambda^{-N}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}

\displaystyle\left\lVert Q_{\nu^{\prime}}\psi P_{\nu}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim_{N}\lambda^{-N}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}

Proof of Proposition
5.2
, estimates (

) and (

We will prove the first inequality in detail. The second follows by a similar argument. To begin let us expand
\sum_{\nu}Q_{\nu}\psi P_{\lambda,1}f

(39)

\tilde{\psi}(x)\sum_{k_{L}\in A_{\lambda,1}}\hat{f}(k_{L})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\langle x-y,\xi\rangle+2\pi i\langle k_{L},y\rangle}\sum_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}(\xi/|\xi|)\tilde{\beta}(2\pi|\xi/\lambda|)\psi(y)dyd\xi.

Ostensibly the integral in

is taken over
\mathbb{T}^{n}

\psi(y)
is of sufficiently small compact support, once we view the integral over
\mathbb{T}^{n}
as an integral over the fundamental domain in

we can trivially extend the integral to the full space. This will help with alignment with the other term
\psi P_{\lambda,1}f

by the definition of
\tilde{\psi}(x)
, we can expand
\psi P_{\lambda,1}f

(40)

\tilde{\psi}(x)\sum_{k_{L}\in A_{\lambda,1}}\hat{f}(k_{L})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\langle x-y,\xi\rangle+2\pi i\langle k_{L},y\rangle}\psi(y)dyd\xi.

\psi(y)
is compactly supported in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
so the integrals above obviously converge. The difference of these operators is then

(41)

\tilde{\psi}(x)\sum_{k_{L}\in A_{\lambda,1}}\hat{f}(k_{L})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\langle x-y,\xi\rangle+2\pi i\langle k_{L},y\rangle}(1-\tilde{\beta}(2\pi|\xi/\lambda|))\psi(y)dyd\xi.

Where we have used
\sum_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}=1
away from the origin to simplify. Let
\phi=2\pi i\langle x-y,\xi\rangle+2\pi i\langle k_{L},y\rangle
. For each integral in the sum we clearly have

\partial_{y}\phi=0\iff\xi=k_{L}.

Our cutoff
\tilde{\beta}
has been constructed such that
\xi\neq k_{L}
on the
\xi
support of the integral. The properties of

force

2\pi|\xi|\not\in[\lambda/2,2\lambda].

As

implies

we clearly have

|\xi-k_{L}|\geq\frac{1}{4}\lambda,

for

large enough. Repeated integration by parts in

(non-stationary phase) gives the desired bound. To prove the other inequality one repeats this proof except
\xi
and

will be separated because of the restricted angle not because of the restricted radius. The details are omitted.
∎

Proving Proposition

We need to prove

Using orthogonality and duality, this is an immediate consequence of (6.18) from
[
1
]

\rho(0)=1,\hskip 2.84544pt\text{supp}\,\hat{\rho}\subset\{t:|t|\in(c/2,c)\}.

Where

\rho_{\lambda}f=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\rho(\delta^{-1}(\lambda-\lambda_{m}))E_{m}f.

Here
E_{m}f
projections onto the
m

\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}}
. We decorate the operator with
\mathbb{T}^{n}
to clarify later expressions. By duality and orthogonality it suffices to prove

\sup_{\nu}\left\lVert Q_{\nu}\psi\rho_{\lambda}f\right\rVert_{L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim_{\kappa}(\lambda\delta)^{\mu_{1}(p_{c})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}.

The duality and orthogonality arguments we have been relying on are outlined in Theorem 3.2.1 of

]
. The above is the form proven in

To aid in the exposition we shall change variables so that

Q_{\nu}(x,y)=\tilde{\psi}(x)\lambda^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i\lambda\langle x-y,\xi\rangle}\beta_{\nu}(\xi/|\xi|)\tilde{\beta}(2\pi|\xi|)d\xi.

We will import the following two estimates from
[
1
]
.

\displaystyle||{Q_{\nu}}||_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\rightarrow L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim 1\text{ for }2\leq p\leq\infty,

\displaystyle||{Q_{\nu}^{*}}||_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\rightarrow L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\lesssim 1\text{ for }1\leq p\leq 2.

By
TT^{*}
and an application of the above bound for the adjoint, if
\Psi=\rho^{2}
it is sufficient to prove

(45)

||{Q_{\nu}\psi\Psi}||_{p_{c}^{\prime}\rightarrow p_{c}}\lesssim(\lambda\delta)^{2\mu_{1}(p_{c})}.

Let
\beta
be a smooth compactly supported bump function supported on
(1/2,2)
such that for
\beta_{k}\coloneq\beta(t/2^{k})
we have
\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\beta_{k}(t)=1
for
t>0
. Also define
\beta_{0}\coloneq 1-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\beta_{k}
.

By Fourier’s inversion formula, we have the decomposition

(46)

\Psi=L_{\lambda}+G_{\lambda},

where

(47)

L_{\lambda}=\delta\int e^{2\pi i\lambda t}e^{-2\pi it\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}}}\beta_{0}(2\pi|t|)\hat{\Psi}(\delta 2\pi t)dt,

and

(48)

G_{\lambda}=\delta\int e^{2\pi i\lambda t}e^{-2\pi it\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}}}(1-\beta_{0})(2\pi|t|)\hat{\Psi}(\delta 2\pi t)dt.

We interpret the operator
e^{-2\pi it\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}}}
by the spectral theorem, that is

e^{-2\pi it\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}}}f=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi it\lambda_{j}}E_{m}f.

By Euler’s formula we have that

e^{-2\pi it\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}}}=2\cos(2\pi t\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}})+e^{2\pi it\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}}}.

If we replace
e^{-2\pi it\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}}}
by
e^{2\pi it\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}}}
, the resulting operator has a kernel that enjoys rapid decay in
\lambda
(bounded by
\lesssim_{N}\lambda^{-N}
for any integer
N
). This is because the signs of the two exponentials agree and so there are no stationary points. As such, if

(49)

\tilde{G}_{\lambda}=2\delta\int e^{2\pi i\lambda t}\cos(2\pi t\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}})(1-\beta_{0})(2\pi|t|)\hat{\Psi}(\delta 2\pi t)dt,

it suffices to bound
Q_{\nu}\psi L_{\lambda}
and
Q_{\nu}\psi\tilde{G}_{\lambda}
by the bound in (
45
).

To bound
Q_{\nu}\psi L_{\lambda}
we do not need the microlocal cutoffs, and so we will just bound
L_{\lambda}
and use that
Q_{\nu}\psi
is
L^{p_{c}}\rightarrow L^{p_{c}}
bounded. After using the spectral theorem to expand we have

\displaystyle L_{\lambda}=\delta\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Bigr(\int e^{2\pi it(\lambda-\lambda_{m})}\beta_{0}(2\pi|t|)\hat{\Psi}(\delta 2\pi t)dt\Bigl)E_{m}

\displaystyle=\delta\sum_{m}s(\lambda;\lambda_{m})E_{m}.

Where
s(\lambda;\lambda_{m})
is the inverse Fourier transform of
\beta_{0}(2\pi t)\hat{\Psi}(\delta 2\pi t)
with respect to
t
. Because of the fixed compact support of
\beta_{0}(2\pi t)
the product
\beta_{0}(2\pi t)\hat{\Psi}(\delta 2\pi t)
has compact support independent of
\lambda
. Its inverse Fourier transform is therefore a Schwartz class function, and the multiplier
s(\lambda;\lambda_{m})
enjoys the bound

|s(\lambda;\lambda_{m})|\lesssim_{N}(1+|\lambda-\lambda_{m}|)^{-N},

for any choice of
N
which leads to
\sum_{m}s(\lambda;\lambda_{m})E_{m}
obeying the universal estimates. The universal estimate at
p_{c}
is the bound for spectral projectors of with 1 in the following expression

(50)

||{P_{\lambda,1}}||_{p_{c}^{\prime}\rightarrow p_{c}}\lesssim\lambda^{2\mu_{1}(p_{c})}.

See Theorem 2.1 in
[
10
]
. To see that the universal estimates apply we break up the multiplier based on
|\lambda-\lambda_{m}|
. In particular,

(51)

L_{\lambda}=\delta\sum_{m}s(\lambda;\lambda_{m})E_{m}=\delta\sum_{m:|\lambda-\lambda_{m}|<1}s(\lambda;\lambda_{m})E_{m}+\delta\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m:|\lambda-\lambda_{m}|\sim 2^{j}}s(\lambda;\lambda_{m})E_{m}.

As the multiplier
s(\lambda;\lambda_{m})
is bounded the first terms obeys the universal estimates. For the second terms, the set
|\lambda-\lambda_{m}|\sim 2^{s}
can be broken into
2^{s}
intervals of length
\sim 1
that obey the universal estimates. The rapid decay of
s(\lambda;\lambda_{m})
ensures the multiplier is bounded by
2^{-jN}
for any
N
of our choosing. Therefore, by the triangle inequality.

(52)

||{L_{\lambda}}||_{p_{c}^{\prime}\rightarrow p_{c}}\lesssim_{N}\delta\lambda^{2\mu_{1}(p_{c})}+\delta\lambda^{2\mu_{1}(p_{c})}\sum_{j}2^{-Nj}2^{j}\lesssim\delta\lambda^{2\mu_{1}(p_{c})}.

For
N
chosen sufficiently large in a manner that does not depend on
\lambda
. We then have by
TT^{*}
that

(53)

||{L_{\lambda}}||_{2\rightarrow p_{c}}\lesssim\delta^{1/2}\lambda^{\mu_{1}(p_{c})}.

To bound
G_{\lambda}
we will continue to use our time cutoff
\beta_{k}
. In particular, define

(54)

\tilde{G}_{\lambda,k}=2\delta\int e^{2\pi i\lambda t}\cos(2\pi t\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}})\beta_{k}(2\pi|t|)\hat{\Psi}(\delta 2\pi t)dt.

By Huygens’ principle we can restrict to
2^{k}\lesssim\delta^{-1}
. By summing in
k
, we have reduced to showing

(55)

||{Q_{\nu}\psi\tilde{G}_{\lambda,k}}||_{p_{c}^{\prime}\rightarrow p_{c}}\lesssim\delta\lambda^{2\mu_{1}(p_{c})}2^{\frac{2k}{n+1}}.

We will prove this by interpolation. By basic properties of the Fourier transform we have

(56)

||{Q_{\nu}\psi\tilde{G}_{\lambda,k}}||_{2\rightarrow 2}\lesssim\delta 2^{k}.

We will interpolate this with a
L^{1}\rightarrow L^{\infty}
norm which will be more difficult to prove. Our goal is

(57)

||{Q_{\nu}\psi\tilde{G}_{\lambda,k}}||_{1\rightarrow\infty}\lesssim\delta\lambda^{\frac{n-1}{2}}2^{k(1-\frac{n-1}{2})}.

This is the only estimate that requires the
Q_{\nu}
operators. To evaluate the kernel of
\cos(2\pi t\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}})
we will lift to the universal cover which is a tiling of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by the fundamental domain of
L^{*}
. That is,

(58)

\cos(2\pi t\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}})(x,y)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\cos(2\pi t\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}})(x-(y+j_{L^{*}})).

With
j_{L^{*}}=j_{1}l_{1}^{*}+...+j_{n}l_{n}^{*}
where

\mathbb{T}^{n}
. As

\mathbb{Z}^{n}

j_{L^{*}}
as iterating through the domains in the universal cover of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
. Let
K_{\lambda,k}(x,y)
be the kernel of
\tilde{G}_{\lambda,k}
. For clarity we will first analyze without the microlocal cutoffs. Applying the above formula gives

K_{\lambda,k}(x,y)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\tilde{K}_{\lambda,k}(x,(y+j_{L^{*}})),

where

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{\lambda,k}(x,y)=2\delta\int e^{2\pi i\lambda t}\cos(2\pi t\sqrt{-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}})(x,y)\beta_{k}(2\pi|t|)\hat{\Psi}(\delta 2\pi t)dt

\displaystyle=2\delta\int\int e^{2\pi i\lambda t}e^{2\pi i\langle x-y,\xi\rangle}\cos(2\pi|\xi|)\beta_{k}(2\pi|t|)\hat{\Psi}(\delta 2\pi t)dtd\xi.

Here,
x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
is the variable we are integrating over in the norm whereas

We will now compute a crude estimate that will motivate our particular definition of the

\tilde{K}_{\lambda,k}(x,y)=\sum_{\pm}\delta\lambda^{\frac{n-1}{2}}2^{-k\frac{n-1}{2}}e^{\pm 2\pi i\lambda|x-y|}a_{\pm}(\lambda,|x-y|)+R_{\lambda}.
