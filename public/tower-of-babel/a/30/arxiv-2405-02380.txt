Emergent gravity from the correlation of spin- 1 2 \displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2} systems coupled with a scalar field
Quentin Ansel

Abstract
This paper introduces several ideas of emergent gravity, which come from a system similar to an ensemble of quantum spin- 1 2 \displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2} particles. To derive a physically relevant theory, the model is constructed by quantizing a scalar field in curved space-time. The quantization is based on a classical discretization of the system, but contrary to famous approaches, like loop quantum gravity or causal triangulation, a Monte-Carlo based approach is used instead of a simplicial approximation of the space-time manifold. This avoids conceptual issues related to the choice of the lattice. Moreover, this allows us to easily encode the geometric structures of space, given by the geodesic length between points, into the mean value of a correlation operator between two spin-like systems. Numerical investigations show the relevance of the approach, and the presence of two regimes: a classical and a quantum regime. The latter is obtained when the density of points reaches a given threshold. Finally, a multi-scale analysis is given, where the classical model is recovered from the full quantum one. Each step of the classical limit is illustrated with numerical computations, showing the very good convergence towards the classical limit and the computational efficiency of the theory.

They postulate that the correlation, quantified by the quantum mutual information, gives us areas between adjacent region, and they are able to recover, at least partially, Einstein field equations. The relation between the mutual information and space-time areas is strongly motivated by different arguments, the main ones being that the entropy of the horizon is proportional to its area, and the mutual information follows an approximated area law

Moreover, the coupling with matter fields is not straightforward, and it seems difficult to recover quantum field theory in curved space-time with an adiabatic elimination

One drawback of these approaches is that the connectivity between these atoms of space is not imposed by fundamental principles, and one usually assumes 4 valent nodes (discretization with tetrahedrons) or 6-valent nodes (discretization with cuboids), but this is only motivated by a computational convenience. This discretization can be thought of as a quantum gravity analog of the nearest neighbors approximation of solid-state physique, but the ultimate theory must be free of the choice of a lattice.

Based on these observations, I propose a model of emergent gravity for which the main ingredients are the following: (i) the structure of space-time is encoded in the correlation of subparts of a quantum state, each subpart being tough as an "atom of space", a quantum region of space (ii) Correlations are related to the geodesic distance between two quantum regions, not the area (iii) Quantum field theory in curved space-time is recovered in the limit when gravitational degrees of freedom are eliminated adiabatically

2
From continuous to discontinuous field theory with a Monte-Carlo approximation

As outlined in the introduction, space-time is assumed fundamentally discrete, and the continuous theory is obtained at a large scale, without assumptions on a lattice structure at the Planck scale. Here, quantum regions are not connected with the nearest neighbors, they are all connected together with more or less weight. This is quite different from the typical situation encountered in lattice quantum field theory (such as in Wilson’s approach of path integrals

In the following, the points defining space are assumed
uniformly distributed
on a manifold
\displaystyle\Sigma
, such that for any function
\displaystyle f:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, and any
\displaystyle R\subset\Sigma
we have
[
29
,

]

\int_{R\in\Sigma}d^{3}x\sqrt{q(x)}f(x)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{V_{R}}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(x_{n})

(1)

where
\displaystyle V_{R}
is the volume of
\displaystyle R
, and
\displaystyle q
is the metric on
\displaystyle\Sigma
. In this paper, the Lebesgue measure is replaced by a probability distribution
\displaystyle p_{x}(y)
. Using the fact that
\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}d^{3}y\sqrt{q(y)}p_{x}(y)=1
, the normalization factor for an integration around the point
\displaystyle x
is deduced to be

\frac{V_{x}}{N}=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{x}(y_{n})\right)^{-1}.

(2)

This result is written for a distribution with support on
\displaystyle\Sigma
, but it can be easily restricted to
\displaystyle R\subset\Sigma
.
This leads to a simple formula for the calculation of integral without reference to a coordinate system:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[f]_{x}&=\int_{\Sigma}d^{3}y\sqrt{q(y)}p_{x}(y)f(y)\\
&\approx\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(y_{n})p_{x}(y_{n})}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{x}(y_{n})}.\end{split}

(3)

To be true,
\displaystyle p_{x}(y)
must be gauge invariant. A natural definition for such a quantity is based on the geodesic length between
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
, which can be computed without too much difficulty as soon as the points are not too far from each other. In the following, it is assumed that

p_{x}(y)=\frac{\mathcal{N}(R)}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\varepsilon)^{3}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma(x,y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)

(4)

where
\displaystyle\varepsilon
has the dimension of a length, and
\displaystyle\sigma
is Synge’s world function
[
31
]
, which is equal to half the square of the geodesic length, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(R)
is a normalization coefficient that depends on the space-time curvature. It is equal to 1 in a flat space.
In this setting,
\displaystyle\varepsilon
provides a natural notion of distance. It is not fixed by first principles, but it is assumed of the order of the Planck length, i.e.,
\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\sim\hbar G/c^{3}
. At a first sight, it is not obvious that Eq. (
4
) gives a valid probability distribution in a curved space-time. This aspect is explored in appendix
A
with a taylor expansion of Synge’s world function in Riemann normal coordinates.

The discretization procedure described here above can be easily generalized to the case when the distribution of points is not homogeneously distributed on
\displaystyle\Sigma
, but is distributed homogeneously with respect to a given coordinate system. In such a case, the previous formulas can be adapted using the replacement rule
\displaystyle p_{x}(y)\rightarrow\sqrt{q(y)}p_{x}(y)
.

3
Discrete quantum scalar field theory in curved space-time

3.1
Hamiltonian of a scalar field

Now that the general setting for the discretization of a field theory is made, a specific example can be studied. As a toy model, a real scalar field
\displaystyle\varphi
is considered. The starting point of the presentation is the Lagrangian density
[
32
,
33
]
:

\mathcal{L}=\frac{\sqrt{-g}}{2}\left[g^{\mu\nu}(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)(\partial_{\nu}\varphi)+m^{2}\varphi^{2}\right]

(5)

where
\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}
is the metric tensor of the space-time manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
,
\displaystyle\sqrt{-g}\equiv\sqrt{-\det g}
, and
\displaystyle m
is the mass. Both
\displaystyle g
and
\displaystyle\varphi
depend on the space-time position
\displaystyle x
. In the following, a 3+1 splitting is performed, to define a Hamiltonian
[
34
,
21
]
. With this splitting, the metric is rewritten as follows:
\displaystyle g_{00}=-\mathsf{N}+\mathsf{N}^{a}\mathsf{N}_{a}
,
\displaystyle g_{0a}=\mathsf{N}_{a}
, and
\displaystyle g_{ab}=q_{ab}
, where coordinate indices with Latin characters run over space coordinates only (i.e.,
\displaystyle a,b=1,2,3
), and
\displaystyle q
is the metric of a space sub-manifold
\displaystyle\Sigma\subset\mathcal{M}
. With this notation, the volume density is
\displaystyle\sqrt{-g}=\sqrt{q}\mathsf{N}
. The next step is to determine the conjugated momentum of
\displaystyle\varphi
. Using
\displaystyle\Pi=\tfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{0}\varphi)}
, one can determine

\Pi=\sqrt{q}\mathsf{N}g^{0\nu}\penalty\ \partial_{\nu}\varphi,

(6)

and the Hamiltonian reads

H=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}d^{3}x\penalty\ \left(\frac{\Pi^{2}}{\sqrt{q}\mathsf{N}}+\sqrt{q}\mathsf{N}\left[q^{ab}\partial_{a}\varphi\partial_{b}\varphi+m^{2}\varphi^{2}\right]\right).

(7)

The Klein-Gordon equation in curved space-time is then recovered using Hamilton’s equations
\displaystyle\tfrac{\delta H}{\delta\varphi}=-\partial_{0}\Pi
and
\displaystyle\tfrac{\delta H}{\delta\Pi}=\partial^{0}\varphi
. To simplify the analysis, the coordinate gauge choice
\displaystyle\mathsf{N}=1
and
\displaystyle\mathsf{N}_{a}=0
is made. This avoids the use of many subtleties of algebraic QFT in curved space-time
[
32
,
33
]
, and thus, the derivation of a quantum theory is significantly simplified. A first look at a covariant approach to the theory is presented in Appendix
C
. The natural next step is to introduce complexifed field variables. They are defined by

\displaystyle\displaystyle a(x)=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2}}\left(\varphi+\frac{i}{m\sqrt{q}}\Pi\right)

(8)

\displaystyle\displaystyle a^{\dagger}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2}}\left(\varphi-\frac{i}{m\sqrt{q}}\Pi\right).

(9)

With these new variables, the Hamiltonian takes the form

\begin{split}H=&\int_{\Sigma}d^{3}x\sqrt{q}\penalty\ \left(m\left(a^{\dagger}a+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right.\\
&\left.+\frac{1}{4m^{2}}q^{ab}\partial_{a}(a^{\dagger}+a)\partial_{b}(a^{\dagger}+a)\right).\end{split}

(10)

Contrary to the standard approach, the Hamiltonian is not diagonalized by the complexified variables
[
35
]
. This enables the development of a quantum theory where the gravitational field plays a role only in the coupling terms between different space-time positions (the term with derivatives).

3.2
Discretization procedure

So far, the theory is given by a continuous space-time background. This section is devoted to its discretization. The most subtle point concerns the discretization of the coupling Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\partial^{a}\varphi\partial_{a}\varphi
, which must return a term of the form
\displaystyle\nabla_{a}\nabla^{a}\varphi
in the equation of motion,
\displaystyle\nabla
being the covariant derivative
1
1

1

In a curved space-time, the covariant derivative
\displaystyle\nabla
replaces the usual derivative
\displaystyle\partial
, to take into account the modification of the tangent space
\displaystyle T_{x}\mathcal{M}
when a vector is moved in the manifold. For a scalar field,
\displaystyle\nabla
reduces to
\displaystyle\partial
, but
\displaystyle\partial_{a}\varphi=\nabla_{a}\varphi
is a vector and its derivative must be computed with
\displaystyle\nabla

[
34
]
.
. To apply the procedure of Sec.
2
, Stokes’s theorem
[
34
]
must be used and a field vanishing on the boundary of the domain must be assumed. Therefore,

\int d^{3}x\penalty\ \sqrt{q}\mathsf{N}\penalty\ \partial_{a}\varphi\partial^{a}\varphi\rightarrow-\int d^{3}x\penalty\ \sqrt{q}\mathsf{N}\penalty\ \varphi\penalty\ \nabla_{a}\nabla^{a}\varphi.

(11)

with this setting, the Laplacian is related to the average of the field around a given position. More precisely, one has

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\varphi]_{x}&=\int_{\Sigma}d^{3}y\sqrt{q}\penalty\ p_{x}(y)\varphi(y)\\
&=\varphi(x)+\int_{\Sigma}d^{3}y\sqrt{q}\penalty\ p_{x}(y)\penalty\ y^{a}\partial_{a}\varphi(x)\\
&\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ +\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}d^{3}y\sqrt{q}\penalty\ p_{x}(y)\penalty\ y^{a}y^{b}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\varphi(x)+...\\
&=\varphi(x)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\nabla_{a}\nabla^{a}\varphi(x)+O(\varepsilon^{3}),\end{split}

(12)

and as a consequence,

\nabla_{a}\nabla^{a}\varphi(x)=\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left[\mathbb{E}[\varphi]_{x}-\varphi(x)\right]+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

(13)

This result is easily obtained in a flat space, but a few more cautions must be taken in curved space. The validity of the
\displaystyle O(\varepsilon^{3})
approximation is explored in appendix
A
.

Next, the last equation is plugged into the integral over
\displaystyle\Sigma
, and a Monte-Carlo approximation is made. The result is

\int_{\Sigma}d^{3}y\sqrt{q}\mathsf{N}\varphi\nabla_{a}\nabla^{a}\varphi\approx\frac{2V}{\varepsilon^{2}N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathsf{N}_{n}\left[\varphi_{n}\mathbb{E}[\varphi]_{n}-\varphi^{2}_{n}\right].

(14)

The subscript
\displaystyle n
is used to specify that quantities are evaluated at the point
\displaystyle x_{n}
.

Complexified variables can be restored and Eq. (
14
) can be inserted into the Hamiltonian (
10
). The theory is quantized straightforwardly by promoting the complexified field variables into creation and annihilation operators acting on a Fock space. The quantum Hamiltonian is

\begin{split}\hat{H}=&\frac{V}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(m\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{a}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right.\\
&\left.-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}m}\left[(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}+\hat{a}_{n})\mathbb{E}[(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a})]_{x_{n}}-(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}+\hat{a}_{n})^{2}\right]\right),\end{split}

(15)

with
\displaystyle\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}
and
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{n}
the creation/annihilation operators at the position
\displaystyle x_{n}
. Despite the relatively simple expression of the Hamiltonian, the average
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}
hides a factor
\displaystyle V/N
, which plays the role of an unknown coupling constant between oscillators. To circumvent this issue, the Hamiltonian is factorized with a global factor
\displaystyle V/N
, and at each position
\displaystyle x_{n}
the volume is evaluated with the relation (
2
). The global factor is only responsible for a rescale of the time coordinate in the Schrödinger equation. The final result is:

\begin{split}\hat{H}=&\frac{V^{2}}{N^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{x_{n}}(x_{k})\right)\left(m\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{a}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right.\\
&\left.+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}m}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}+\hat{a}_{n})^{2}\right)\\
&-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}m}\left((\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}+\hat{a}_{n})\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{x_{n}}(x_{k})(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}+\hat{a}_{k})\right).\end{split}

(16)

This Hamiltonian shares very strong similarities with the Bose-Hubbard model
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
. In particular, this model was used to simulate quantum fields in curved space-times
[
41
]
. While the standard approach is to start from a given lattice configuration of the Bose-Hubbard model and to compute the resulting effective space-time, here the reverse approach is made. However, Eq. (
16
) is free from the nearest neighbor approximation, and it provides a simple mapping between the space-time geometry and the parameters of the Hamiltonian.

4
Quantum space-time

In the previous sections, a formalism that allows us to discretize a quantum scalar field in space was developed, where gravitational degrees of freedom have an influence only through an interaction Hamiltonian that couples the fields at different locations. With these materials at hand, a (partial) quantum theory for the gravitational field consistent with QFT in curved space-time can be constructed.

A closer look at Eq. (
16
) highlights that in the interaction Hamiltonian, the geometry of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
is hidden in
\displaystyle P_{x_{n}}(x_{k})
. These weights are the quantities that must be quantized.

The quantization relies on several assumptions. In particular, the geometry of the gravitational state is encoded in the correlation between different sites of an ensemble of spin-
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}
, a spin being associated with a point
\displaystyle x_{n}
. Then, a general quantum state
\displaystyle|\Psi_{G}\rangle
is a linear combination of vectors of
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{G}=\mathbb{C}^{2N}
.

Many different operators can be used to describe a two-point correlation function. Here, the following choice is made:

\hat{C}_{nk}=\hat{\sigma}_{+}(x_{n})\hat{\sigma}_{-}(x_{k})+\hat{\sigma}_{+}(x_{k})\hat{\sigma}_{-}(x_{n})

(17)

with
\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{-}(x_{n})
and
\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{+}(x_{n})
the spin creation/annihilation operators associated with the point
\displaystyle x_{n}
.
The mean value of this operator,
\displaystyle C_{nk}=\langle\Psi_{G}|\hat{C}_{nk}|\Psi_{G}\rangle
, gives us information on how much two regions are correlated, and more specifically how much two regions are entangled since a superposition of different states is necessary to get
\displaystyle C_{nk}\neq 0
.

To relate correlations and geometric data of the manifold, I propose to postulate the following relation between the correlation and the geodesic distance:

C_{nk}^{2}=\alpha\penalty\ \exp\left(-\frac{\sigma(x_{n},x_{k})}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right),

(18)

with
\displaystyle\alpha
a proportionality coefficient. This definition, motivated by a numerical investigations, requires the exclusion of the terms
\displaystyle n=k
in the sum. This is because
\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{+}(x_{n})\hat{\sigma}_{-}(x_{n})+\hat{\sigma}_{+}(x_{n})\hat{\sigma}_{-}(x_{n})=\textrm{1}\!\!\!\!1_{2}
, and thus, by taking the expectation value, it is not possible to recover the coefficient
\displaystyle\alpha
. As a consequence, the condition
\displaystyle p_{x_{n}}(x_{n})=0
must be imposed in Eq. (
16
). This is not a problem for the convergence of the Monte-Carlo approximation scheme since it concerns a modification of the probability distribution whose Lebesgue measure is equal to zero.

Eq. (
18
) has a very simple physical interpretation. The larger the distance between
\displaystyle x_{n}
and
\displaystyle x_{k}
, the smaller the correlation. In order to suppress the quantum fuzziness at large scales, the correlation must decay sufficiently fast. A normal law (see Eq. (
4
)) is assumed for convenience, but it is not excluded that another distribution can be more adapted to the problem. For example,
\displaystyle C_{nk}^{2}\propto\exp(-\sqrt{2\sigma_{nk}}/\varepsilon)
could be also a viable choice. The validity of this ansazt is explored numerically in Fig.
2
. The approach of the numerical investigation is the following. A cost function
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}|\alpha e^{-\sigma_{nk}/\varepsilon^{2}}-C_{nk}^{2}(\Psi_{G})|^{2}
is minimized with respect to the vector components of
\displaystyle|\Psi_{G}\rangle
. For a different number of points (i.e., a different number of spins), a quantum state is optimized numerically such that
\displaystyle C_{nk}^{2}
is as close as possible to
\displaystyle\alpha e^{-\sigma_{nk}/\varepsilon^{2}}
. The value of
\displaystyle\alpha
is fixed by hand, to simplify the optimization process. Specific details on these numerical computations are gathered in Appendix
B
. Different kinds of configurations and spaces are investigated, with flat and curved spaces, and with points organized randomly or regularly. In all situations, very low values of
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}
are obtained, except when the density of points is too large. For example, in the case of Fig.
2
b), the value of
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}
drops significantly when
\displaystyle L
reaches a given threshold (
\displaystyle L/\varepsilon\approx 1.5
for the cube and
\displaystyle L/\varepsilon\approx 1.2
for the 3-simplex). The threshold depends on the number of spins and it seems to correspond to
\displaystyle C_{nk}\approx 1/\sqrt{N}
. Further investigations may clarify this point, but this numerical result has an important physical consequence:
there is a natural minimum scale for which we can obtain a classical geometry
. The value of the threshold depends on
\displaystyle\alpha
, and thus it is not clear at this point in which exact circumstances it is possible to encounter a quantum space-time. This issue will be investigated elsewhere.

The Hamiltonian of the scalar field (
16
) can be reconstructed with the information given by
\displaystyle C_{nk}
. However, the square of
\displaystyle C_{nk}
is required, to ensure a positive quantity that can be assimilated with a probability. This cannot be realized as the mean value of a linear operator acting on
\displaystyle|\Psi_{G}\rangle
, the quadraticity can be recovered with a duplicate of the state and a tensor product. We introduce
\displaystyle|\tilde{\Psi}_{G}\rangle=|\Psi_{G}\rangle\otimes|\Psi_{G}\rangle
, and with this state,
\displaystyle C_{nk}^{2}
is obtained with the relation

\langle\tilde{\Psi}_{G}|\hat{C}_{nk}\otimes\hat{C}_{nk}|\tilde{\Psi}_{G}\rangle=C_{nk}^{2}.

(19)

The full quantum Hamiltonian is obtained by replacing
\displaystyle P_{x_{n}}(x_{k})
by
\displaystyle\hat{C}_{nk}\otimes\hat{C}_{nk}
in Eq. (
16
). The parameter
\displaystyle\alpha
is omitted since it can be factorized with the term
\displaystyle V^{2}/N^{2}
, which is already a global scaling factor of the Hamiltonian.

The dynamics of the full quantum system are given by the Schrödinger equation

\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi\rangle=-\frac{iN}{V}\left(\hat{H}+\hat{H}_{G}\right)|\Psi\rangle,

(20)

where
\displaystyle\hat{H}
is the Hamiltonian
[
34
,
21
]
given in Eq. (
16
), and the origin of factor
\displaystyle N/V
is explained in appendix
D
.
\displaystyle\hat{H}_{G}
is a gravitational Hamiltonian. Deriving the explicit expression of
\displaystyle\hat{H}_{G}
is a nontrivial key point of the theory. It is left unresolved in this article. Both
\displaystyle\hat{H}
and
\displaystyle\hat{H}_{G}
act on gravitational degrees of freedoms, but in the low coupling limit,
\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle
approximately take the form of a tensor product at any time, i.e.
\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle\approx|\tilde{\Psi}_{G}\rangle\otimes|\Psi_{S.F.}\rangle
(Born approximation). This limit is not obtained by
\displaystyle m\rightarrow 0
, but when
\displaystyle\|\hat{H}|\Psi\rangle\|\ll\|\hat{H}_{G}|\Psi\rangle\|
. By taking the trace over gravitational degrees of freedom, one gets:

\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi_{S.F.}\rangle=-\frac{iN}{V}\left(\langle\hat{H}\rangle_{\tilde{\Psi}_{G}}+\langle\hat{H}_{G}\rangle_{\tilde{\Psi}_{G}}\textrm{1}\!\!\!\!1\right)|\Psi_{S.F.}\rangle

(21)

The mean value of
\displaystyle\hat{H}_{G}
returns only an irrelevant phase from the point of view of the scalar field, and thus, the Schrödinger equation can be redefined as

\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi_{S.F.}\rangle=-\frac{iN}{V}\langle\hat{H}\rangle_{\tilde{\Psi}_{G}}|\Psi_{S.F.}\rangle.

(22)

It gives us the dynamics of the scalar field in the limit of quantum fields in curved space-time. This is a mean-field Schrödinger equation similar to the one encountered in quantum optics
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
. From the point of view of open quantum systems, this corresponds to a first-order adiabatic elimination
[
46
]
. The second-order expansion introduces non-unitary dynamics and an effective Lindblad equation must replace Eq. (
22
). The second-order correction goes beyond the standard quantum field theory in curved space-time and it incorporates the notion of gravitational decoherence
[
47
,
48
]
. The rest of this paper is focused on the first-order approximation and only Eq. (
22
) is considered.

5
Multi-scales analysis

In the previous sections, a quantum theory for the scalar field and the gravitational field was developed, such that quantum field theory in curved space-time is recovered when suitable limits are taken. Several aspects have been omitted and the goal of this section is to go deeper in the analysis, using numerical calculations.

A second interesting quantity is the purity of the reduced state associated with each spin.

However, they give us elements of comparison with the states describing a classical geometry. We observe that the IPR is usually quite high (between 30% and 50% of the total dimension of the Hilbert space) and the purity is low, close to the minimum value of 0.5. 6
Conclusion

In this paper, several ideas of quantum gravity have been explored, such as the possibility to encode space-time geometric distances in the correlation of a spin-like system, and to use a discretization scheme based on Monte-Carlo principles instead of a lattice-based discretization. These two ingredients offer interesting advantages compared to other common quantum gravity theories which are based on elementary areas (instead of distances) and/or simplicial lattices for the discretization scheme. The use of distances leads to a simpler connection with general relativity and our everyday world, for which elementary quantities are distances. The Monte Carlo method simplifies the discretization scheme of differential operators, and it avoids conceptual problems induced by the arbitrary choice of a lattice.

To construct a physically relevant theory, the starting point was to quantize a scalar field in curved space-time. Working with such a test system has led to a simple link between general ideas and a well-defined mathematical model. In this model, the gravitational field is described by a quantum system which is similar to an ensemble of spin-
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}
particles. At each spin corresponds a point of space. The geodesic length between two of these points is given by the quantum state through the relation
\displaystyle C_{nk}^{2}=\langle\hat{\sigma}_{+}(x_{n})\hat{\sigma}_{-}(x_{k})+\hat{\sigma}_{+}(x_{k})\rangle_{\Psi_{G}}^{2}\propto p_{x_{n}}(x_{k})
. This relation is a postulate of the theory, and its physical relevance has been investigated with numerical tests. Quantum states that describe various classical geometries have been found with a very high numerical precision. This is a first positive result supporting this very strong postulate. The numerical simulations have also shown the existence of two different regimes associated with two different length scales. Bellow a specific threshold, the classical description does not hold anymore and one obtains something that can be called "a quantum space-time". This is a nontrivial consequence of the model, which remotely resembles the quantization of areas and volumes in loop quantum gravity. In loop quantum gravity, the minimal scale is given by the lowest eigenvalues of area and volume operators
[
21
,
22
,
16
]
. This can be interpreted as the existence of minimal building blocks that can be used to construct the space-time. However, in the theory of this paper, the interpretation is slightly different. The classical space-time is observed only if the distance between two points is sufficiently far from each other. If the density of points becomes too short, there is a threshold where a classical picture is not possible anymore and the resulting object cannot be interpreted as a space-like sub-manifold. This effect may have interesting consequences in black hole physics and cosmology. The relevance of the model has also been investigated from another point of view, which has consisted of recovering, by means of successive approximation, the classical theory of a scalar field. Each step of the procedure has been illustrated by numerical examples. These examples show clearly the different regimes of the theory, but they also demonstrate its computational efficiency.

In these few pages, many issues have been glossed over. Here is a (non-exhaustive) list of the points that shall be investigated:

\displaystyle\bullet

One of the most important ones concerns the explicit construction of the gravitational Hamiltonian
\displaystyle H_{G}
, which is compatible with the Einstein-field equation (at least in some limit cases, compatible with current experimental observations). Another issue concerns the choice of the probability density
\displaystyle p_{x_{n}}(x_{k})
, which has been assumed to be a normal law. In fact, the form of the density may be fixed by the theory itself, by
\displaystyle\hat{H}_{G}
and its eigenvectors. The use of a normal Law is interesting because at small distances we have
\displaystyle C_{nk}^{2}\propto 1-q_{ab}dx^{a}dx^{b}/(2\varepsilon^{2})
, with
\displaystyle dx
a vector connecting
\displaystyle x_{n}
to
\displaystyle x_{k}
. The relation with the metric tensor is therefore very simple and quite natural, but many different distributions can have locally this form and very different behaviors at large scales.

\displaystyle\bullet

In this paper, it was sufficient to provide a quantum mechanical description of the 3-metric
\displaystyle q_{ab}
. However, this is not sufficient to describe the entire space-time manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
, the extrinsic curvature
\displaystyle k_{ab}
is also necessary, and it must be encoded somewhere in
\displaystyle|\tilde{\Psi}_{G}\rangle
.

\displaystyle\bullet

A similar study with a Dirac field instead of a scalar field could also be interesting to test the coupling operator
\displaystyle\hat{C}_{nk}
. This operator (see Eq. (
17
)) has been chosen because it provides interesting numerical results, but this choice is not unique. For a Dirac field, it could be more interesting to choose an operator of the form
\displaystyle\hat{C}_{nk}=\sum_{i,j\in\{x,y,z\}}w_{ij}\hat{\sigma}_{i}(x_{n})\hat{\sigma}(x_{k})
, with
\displaystyle w_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
, with
\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{i}(x_{n})
the Pauli matrices.

\displaystyle\bullet

Another point to clarify concerns the possibility of interpreting
\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{i}(x_{n})
as a quantized 3D reference frame. If such a link can be consistently made with the ideas of the paper, this may provide a very interesting connection with current theories of quantum reference frame.

\displaystyle\bullet

Finally, a last point to investigate with deeper details, together with the construction of
\displaystyle H_{G}
, is the physical consequence of the use of
\displaystyle|\tilde{\Psi}_{G}\rangle=|\Psi_{G}\rangle^{\otimes 2}
. This condition has a pure mathematical origin, it could be relaxed, such that
\displaystyle|\tilde{\Psi}_{G}\rangle=|\Psi_{1}\rangle\otimes|\Psi_{2}\rangle
. The two states would correspond only in the classical limit.

Appendix C
Covariant Monte-Carlo discretization

In this appendix, the ideas introduced in Sec.
3.2
are reused to define a covariant quantum theory of the scalar field. In such a framework, the action is discretized, and it can be inserted in a second step into a path-integral. The precise definition of the path integral is not discussed here, the following few lines focus on the discretization of the action.
The usual action for a scalar field is given by:

S=\int d^{4}x\frac{\sqrt{-g}}{2}\left[g^{\mu\nu}(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)(\partial_{\nu}\varphi)+m^{2})\varphi^{2}\right]

(34)

It can be discretized with a Monte-Carlo approach, as given in Sec.
2
, but using the space-time metric
\displaystyle g
instead of the metric
\displaystyle q
of a 3D hypersurface. The nontrivial point would be to estimate the term
\displaystyle g^{\mu\nu}(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)(\partial_{\nu}\varphi)
.

One can notice that:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{2}]_{x}&=\int d^{4}y\sqrt{-g}\penalty\ p_{x}(y)\varphi(x)^{2}\\
&\approx\varphi(x)^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\varphi(x)\nabla_{\mu}\nabla^{\mu}\varphi(x)+\varepsilon^{2}(\partial^{\mu}\varphi)(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)\end{split}

(35)

The computation steps are basically the same as in Eq. (
12
), but using the Taylor expansion of
\displaystyle\varphi(x)^{2}
over space-time. Using the relation of Eq. (
13
), generalized to 4D, it is possible to rewrite Eq. (
35
) into

\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{2}]_{x}\approx\varepsilon^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\varphi(x)\mathbb{E}[\varphi]_{x}+(\partial^{\mu}\varphi)(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)\right),

(36)

and thus,

(\partial^{\mu}\varphi)(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)\approx\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{2}]_{x}-\frac{1}{2}\varphi(x)\mathbb{E}[\varphi]_{x}.

(37)

As a consequence, with the Monte-Carlo approximation, the action is approximately

S\approx\frac{V}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}m^{2}\varphi_{n}^{2}+\frac{V}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{x_{n}}(x_{k})\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\varphi_{k}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{n}\varphi_{k}\right)

(38)

One can also factorize by
\displaystyle V/N
, as in Eq. (
16
), and the final result is

\begin{split}S\approx&\frac{V^{2}}{2N^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{x_{n}}(x_{k})\right)m^{2}\varphi_{n}^{2}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{x_{n}}(x_{k})\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\varphi_{k}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{n}\varphi_{k}\right).\end{split}

(39)

Once again, this formula is evaluated in 4D, which means that
\displaystyle p_{x_{n}}(x_{k})
is a function of the geodesic length in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
, not in
\displaystyle\Sigma
