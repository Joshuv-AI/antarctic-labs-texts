On the Conditions for a Quantum Violent Relaxation
Guido Giachetti 1, Nicolò Defenu 2

Abstract
In general, classical fully-connected systems are known to undergo violent relaxation. This phenomenon refers to the relaxation of observables to stationary, non-thermal, values on a finite timescale, despite their long-time dynamics being dominated by mean-field effects in the thermodynamic limit. Here, we analyze the “quantum" violent relaxation by studying the dynamics of generic many-body systems with two-body, all-to-all, interactions in the thermodynamic limit. We show that, in order for violent relaxation to occur very specific conditions on the spectrum of the mean-field effective Hamiltonian have to be met. These conditions are hardly met and “quantum" violent relaxation is observed rarely with respect to its classical counterpart. Our predictions are validated by the study of a spin model which, depending on the value of the coupling, shows a transition between violent-relaxation and a generic prethermal phase. We also analyze a spin version of the quantum Hamiltonian-Mean-Field model, which is shown not to exhibit violent-relaxation. Finally, we discuss how the violent-relaxation picture emerges back in the classical limit. Our results demonstrate how, even in the mean-field regime, quantum effects have a rather dramatic impact on the dynamics, paving the way to a better understanding of light-matter coupled systems.

The physics of long-range interacting systems

Violent relaxation is a characteristic feature of classical long-range systems out of equilibrium
Barré et al. 2012
;
Campa et al. 2009
. This phenomenon has been observed in fully-connected models, including the paradigmatic Hamiltonian-Mean-Field (HMF) model
Antoni and Ruffo 1995
;
Campa et al. 2009
, and it is believed to be a key ingredient to explain the formation of large-scale astrophysical structures

;

Chavanis and Sommeria 1998
;
Chavanis and Bouchet 2005

Chavanis 2006a
. The HMF dynamics, in the thermodynamic limit, becomes mean-field, thus preventing the loss of memory and the onset of the thermalization. In spite of this, on a finite (size-independent) time-scale, the system is usually observed to relax toward a so-called quasi-stationary state, in which large-scale observables take a constant (non-thermal) value. While the general mechanism behind the phenomenon is generally understood

;
Chavanis 2006a

In the quantum regime, fully connected Hamiltonians, akin to the HMF, can be engineered in cavity experiments

;
Mivehvar et al. 2021

Lahaye et al. 2009
;
Chomaz et al. 2022
or seen as a large-dimension approximation of short-range models
Metzner and Vollhardt 1989

, which has proven to be robust against external noise and competing short-range perturbations

;
Lerose et al. 2018
;
Defenu 2021
;
Piccitto et al. 2021
. As the mean-field description becomes exact

;
Pearce and Thompson 1978
;
Tindemans and Capel 1974
;
Kastner 2010
, according to the general wisdom, the dynamics of fully-connected systems can be reduced to a small number of classical macroscopic degrees of freedom in the thermodynamic limit

Sciolla and Biroli 2013

Žunkovič et al. 2016

In spite of the fundamentally classical nature of the mean-field dynamics, the resulting phenomenology lies outside the violent-relaxation paradigm, proper of classical model in mean-field regime, suggesting that the non-thermal relaxation is hindered by quantum effects. Quantum versions of the HMF model have been introduced both in the fermionic
Chavanis 2011a
and bosonic
Chavanis 2011b

Plestid et al. 2018
. In spite of these preliminary results, a general picture to explain the onset of violent-relaxation in the quantum world is still missing. In this Letter we address the matter in its generality, finding the conditions under which fully-connected models undergo violent relaxation. We find that such a possibility is closely linked to the property of the spectrum of the mean-field effective Hamiltonian. This result forms a bridge between the classical and the quantum realms and highlights how, already at the level of mean-field dynamics, single-particle quantum effects do play an important role and can deeply affect the out-of-equilibrium phenomena.

Results:
Let us consider a generic set of
N
quantum spins coupled by a fully-connected, two-body, interaction which can be described by the Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{j}H_{0}(\tau_{j})-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i,j}H_{1}(\tau_{i})H_{1}(\tau_{j})

(1)

where
\tau_{j}
denotes a set of quantum variable relative to the site
j
, such that
[\tau_{j},\tau_{l}]=0
for
i\neq l
, and the
1/N
factor is chosen in agreement with the so-called Kac scaling, which ensures an extensive energy
Campa et al. 2014
;
Kac et al. 1963
. The interaction is chosen to be ferromagnetic. The
\tau
variables can be chosen to be spins or rotor variables, but this is not essential for our results.

Many of the fully-connected models studied in the past can be seen as particular instances of Hamiltonian Eq. (
1
): for example the LMG model
Glick et al. 1965
;
Meshkov et al. 1965
;
Lipkin et al. 1965
is recovered with the choice
H_{0}(\mathbf{s})=-hs_{x}
and
H_{1}=s_{z}
. Since the interaction is fully invariant under any permutation, we assume the initial state
\ket{\Psi(0)}
at
t=0
to be fully symmetric as well: said
\ket{k_{j}}
,
j=1,\dots,N
a collection of single-site states, we can express it as

\ket{\Psi(0)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\sum_{P}\bigotimes_{j}\ket{k_{P(j)}}\ ,

(2)

where the sum runs all over the permutations. In the thermodynamics limit, the mean-field description of the system becomes exact
Pearce and Thompson 1975
;
Pearce and Thompson 1978
;
Tindemans and Capel 1974
;
Kastner 2010
;
Sciolla and Biroli 2010
;
Sciolla and Biroli 2013
. This means that, as long as we are interested in single-site operators, the dynamics of the system can be described (see
SM
for a physical argument) in terms of a single-site effective density operator
\rho(t)
, evolving through the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}(t)=H_{0}(\mathbf{s})-\mu(t)H_{1}(\mathbf{s}),

(3)

where
\mu(t)
is determined self consistently via

\mu(t)=\mathrm{tr}{\left(\rho(t)H_{1}\right)}\ .

(4)

At
t=0
we have
\rho(0)=1/N\sum_{j}\ket{k_{j}}\bra{k_{j}}
. For finite
N
, the above picture breaks down on timescales polynomial in
N
(see Ref.
Pappalardi et al. 2018
). If
\mu(t)=0
, however, the many-body fluctuations dominates over the expectation value of
H_{1}
regardless of the value of
N
, so that the mean-field approximation is no longer valid.

Let us notice that the dynamics described by Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
) conserves the quantity
\epsilon=\mathrm{tr}(\rho(t)H_{0})-\mu^{2}(t)/2
(see
SM
) which corresponds to a physical energy density. As the dynamics is effectively one-body, the long-time behavior of the observable is not expected to converge to the thermal average. In analogy with the classical case, violent relaxation shall occur if
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu(t)\rightarrow\mu_{\infty}
which will differ from the thermal expectation value
\mu_{\infty}\neq\mu_{\rm th}
. While violent relaxation is widespread in classical long-range physics, in the quantum realm other scenarios are possible: for example, in the LMG, the periodic oscillations of the magnetization survive at all times in the thermodynamic limit. Even more complicated, non periodic, behaviors are in principle possible. In this Letter we show how, in fact, the presence of violent relaxation in a quantum system can be seen as an exception, rather than as the rule. This is a consequence of the following property, which is the main result of our Letter:

Color plot of the of the magnetization

\ket{\Psi(0)}\propto 3\ket{L=0}+\ket{L=-1}+\ket{L=1}
. Although the quantitative features of the dynamics of

, it is clear from the figure that no violent relaxation actually occur for any value for

A necessary condition for the system described by mean-field dynamics to undergo violent relaxation is that the (dynamically accessible) point spectrum of the single-site Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(t)
in Eq. (
3
) contains, at most, a single element (possibly degenerate). In case some of the corresponding states are not accessible to the dynamics because of symmetry reason, those should not be included in the above analysis. If the discrete spectrum is empty, then
\mu_{\infty}=0
.

While a detailed proof of the above statement is provided in the Suppl. Mat.
SM
, its interpretation is straightforward: in case of violent relaxation
\mathcal{H}(t)
will become time-independent at large times. Then, the existence of more than one element in the dynamically accessible point spectrum, would result in infinitely-lived Rabi oscillations between the populations of the (two or more) levels in the point spectrum. As a consequence, any system with a finite Hilbert site per site (i.e., in our setting, for any finite
s
) do not display violent relaxation. This surprising outcome underlines the deep difference between classical and the quantum dynamics already for mean-field models.

Examples:
In this paragraph we are going to verify our statement by examining some particular instances of the Hamiltonian (
1
). First, we consider the two-component quantum rotor model
Sachdev 1999
;
Defenu et al. 2021
, whose Hamiltonian can be written as

H_{\rm QR}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}L^{2}_{j}-\frac{\lambda}{2N}\sum_{j,k}\mathbf{n}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{k}

(5)

where
n^{\alpha}_{j}
(
\alpha=1,2
) are the components of a unity vector (
\mathbf{n}_{j}^{2}=1
) and
L_{j}
the corresponding angular momenta:
[L_{k},n^{\alpha}_{j}]=i\delta_{jk}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}n^{\beta}_{k}
(we set
\hbar=1
). Let us notice that this model can be seen as a large
s
limit of an Hamiltonian of the form (
1
). Introducing the angular parametrization
\mathbf{n}_{j}=(\cos\theta_{j},\sin\theta_{j})
, with
[\theta_{j},L_{k}]=i\delta_{jk}
, we can write the single-site Hamiltonian (
3
) as

\mathcal{H}_{\rm QR}(t)=\frac{L^{2}}{2}-\lambda\mu_{1}(t)\cos\theta-\lambda\mu_{2}(t)\sin\theta

(6)

with
\mu_{1}(t)=\mathrm{tr}(\rho(t)\cos\theta)
,
\mu_{2}(t)=\mathrm{tr}(\rho(t)\sin\theta)
. In the following, we will assume a
\theta\rightarrow-\theta
symmetry in the initial state, so that
\mu_{2}(t)\equiv 0
. The main reason behind the choice of the Hamiltonian (
5
) is that, in the
\hbar\rightarrow 0
limit, it reduces to the HMF model
Antoni and Ruffo 1995
. Indeed, while the Hamiltonian Eq. (
6
) does not exactly coincide with the one of the quantum HMF model
Chavanis 2011b
, we notice that that, for uniform initial conditions,
\rho(t)
is a pure state and the evolution of the system can be expressed though the Gross-Pitajevskij formalism as in the bosonic HMF model
Chavanis et al. 1996
;
Plestid et al. 2018
.

In spite of the fact that the single-space Hilbert space is infinite dimensional, let us notice that the spectrum of an Hamiltonian of the form (
6
) has a completely discrete spectrum for any value of the
\mu
, so that we expect the quantum system not to undergo violent relaxation. This expectation is indeed verified by a numerical analysis of the dynamics of Eq. (
6
). As shown in Fig.
1
, indeed, contrary to the classical case, the evolution of
\mu_{1}(t)
does not converge to an asymptotic constant value.

In order to see an instance of violent relaxation in the quantum world we introduce the
w
-model Hamiltonian

H_{w}=\frac{h}{s}\sum_{j}s^{x}_{j}-\frac{\lambda}{2N}\sum_{j,k}\theta_{H}(w^{2}-s^{z2}_{j})\theta_{H}(w^{2}-s^{z2}_{k})

(7)

which is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetric (
s_{j}^{z}\rightarrow-s_{j}^{z}
). In this expression we denote with
\theta_{H}(x)
the Heaveside step function(with the convention
\theta_{H}(0)=1
) while
w
is a non-negative integer. Physically, the model is characterized by the competition between the single operator terms and the collective interaction, which tends to localize the system in a subspace with small magnetic quantum number
|m|\leq w
. The corresponding single-site Hamiltonian reads

\mathcal{H}_{w}(t)=\frac{h}{s}s_{x}-\lambda\mu(t)\theta_{H}(w^{2}-s_{z}^{2}),

(8)

where
\mu(t)=\mathrm{tr}{\left[\rho(t)\theta_{H}(w^{2}-s_{z}^{2})\right]}
. In the large spin limit
s\to\infty
and for constant
\mu(t)=\bar{\mu}>0
the Hamiltonian (
8
) has a continuum of eigenstates with energy
\mathcal{E}>-h
, delocalized with respect to the magnetic number
m
, and a finite number of non-degenerate bound states, localized around
m=0
, with a discrete set of energies
\mathcal{E}_{n}<-h
. The number
n_{\rm b}
of the bound states varies from
1
, for
\lambda\bar{\mu}\rightarrow 0^{+}
to a maximum of
2w+1
in the limit
\lambda\bar{\mu}\rightarrow+\infty
, their parity with respect to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
(
m\rightarrow-m
) being
(-1)^{n}
,
n=0,\cdots,n_{\rm b}
(see
SM
). In the following, we will restrict to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-even initial conditions, so that the maximum number of accessible bound states is
w+1
.

The
w
model is the ideal playground to check the link between the discrete spectrum and the presence of violent relaxation. For
w=0
the discrete spectrum has one element regardless of the value of the parameters. The numerical results shown in Fig.
2
(left panel) (see
SM
for the details) demonstrate how the presence of a single bound-state leads to a dynamical phase transition between an asymptotically thermal phase at small values of
\lambda
and a violent relaxation phase at
\lambda\gtrsim 1.2
. Indeed, at small
\lambda

\mu(t)
vanishes in the long-time limit and the thermal behaviour is recovered. On the contrary, for
\lambda\gtrsim 1.2
the system undergoes violent relaxation and the system reaches a quasi-stationary state, where
\mu(t)
attains a non-thermal stationary value
\mu_{\infty}
at large times. Our analysis does not rule out the presence of small, long-lived oscillations in the localized phase which, anyway, would be compatible with our exact statement.

For
w>1
a second even bound state emerges for
\lambda\mu>x_{c}(w)
. Its emergence, as shown in Fig. (
2
) (right panel), results into a new phase for large
\lambda

\mu(t)
no longer relaxes; rather it exhibits undamped oscillations. In agreement with our theoretical analysis, the phase in which the system exhibits violent-relaxation falls within the region of parameter space in which
\mathcal{H}_{w}
has only one accessible bound states, namely
\lambda\mu<x_{c}(1)\sim 1.51
(see
SM
).

Classical limit:
The above analysis suggests that violent relaxation should emerge in the classical limit, regardless of the single-particle spectrum of the quantum problem. To understand the mechanism behind this phenomenon let us consider a system with a well-defined classical limit, e.g. the two-component quantum rotor model of Eq. (
5
). In this case it is convenient to resort to the Wigner function formalism
Wigner 1932
;
Moyal 1949
;
SM
. Let us then introduce the Wigner quasi-probability distribution
W_{t}(x,p)
relative to the single-particle density operator
\rho(t)
, where
x
and
p
are a pair of canonical conjugate variables (e.g.
\theta
and
L
in Eq. (
6
)). The evolution equation

i\partial_{t}W_{t}(q,p)=\{\{\mathcal{H}_{W}(t),W_{t}(q,p)\}\}\equiv\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}(W_{t})

(9)

where
\{\{\cdot,\cdot\}\}
denotes the Moyal brackets
Moyal 1949
,
\mu(t)=\int dqdp\ H_{1,\rm cl}(q,p)W_{t}(q,p)
and
\mathcal{H}_{W}
,
H_{1,W}
denotes the Weyl transform of
\mathcal{H}
and
H_{1}
respectively. For any fixed value of
\mu
, the spectrum of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}
coincides with the one of the superoperator
[\mathcal{H},\cdot]
, namely
\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E^{\prime}}
, where
\mathcal{E}
,
\mathcal{E^{\prime}}
belong to the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}
. In the classical limit,
W_{t}(x,p)
becomes a probability distribution, while we can replace
\{\{\cdot,\cdot\}\}\rightarrow i\{\cdot,\cdot\}
, where
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denotes the Poisson brackets, so that Eq. (
9
) can be interpreted as a Vlasov equation
Vlasov 1968
;
Campa et al. 2014
;
Giachetti et al. 2020
for the mean-field evolution of a distribution of classical particles.

By looking at the classical limit of the operator
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}
, we are able to show that, under very general assumption on the form of
\mathcal{H}
, that the classical system behaves effectively as a quantum system with a single (infinitely degenerate) element in the discrete spectrum (see
SM
for the details). As a consequence, the necessary condition for the violent relaxation is generally met in the classical limit, thus explaining the ubiquitousness of the phenomenon in the classical world.

Conclusions:
In this Letter we have explored the possibility for a quantum model with attractive, fully-connected, interactions, to undergo violent-relaxation. While this is the standard behavior in the classical limit, we pointed out that is rather exceptional in the quantum regime due to the presence of discrete points in the spectrum of the effective, mean-field, Hamiltonian. If two or more discrete points are present, resonances between these states give rise to non-decaying oscillations, thus preventing the relaxation of the system. To test such prediction we introduced a new spin model (
7
), characterized, in the large-
s
limit, by a tunable number of bound states. We also checked that violent relaxation is absent in the two-component quantum rotor model
Sachdev 1999
(
5
) which, in the classical limit, reduces to the paradigmatic Hamiltonian-mean-field model
Antoni and Ruffo 1995
. Finally, we shown that the necessary conditions in order to have violent-relaxation in the quantum regime, are met under very mild hypothesis in the classical limit.

It is worth noting that Hamiltonian (
6
) has purely attractive interactions and our findings differ from the ones depicted in Ref.
Plestid et al. 2018
, where bi-clustering phenomena have been discussed. However, while our analysis is carried out in the formalism of commuting lattice variables, the fact that the effective Hamiltonian (
6
) reproduces the Gross-Pitajevskii formalism discussed in Refs.
Plestid et al. 2018
;
Chavanis 2011b
suggests that our picture can be extended to the bosonic case.

All together our results indicate that, regardless of the mean-field nature of such models, quantum effects play a crucial role in the dynamics, and can alter their behaviour already at a qualitative level. As cold atoms into cavity are known to give raise to all-to-all effective interactions, our prediction could be verified experimentally in atomic, molecular, optical setups. On the other hand, the possibility of observing violent-relaxation in quantum model could provide a new way for engineering stationary states in quantum technological applications.

I. Mean-field limit

In this section, we are going to derive Eq. (3) and Eq. (4) of the main text. Given a generic, single-site observable

A_{j}\equiv\mathbb{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\underbrace{A}_{j}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{I}

(10)

A^{S}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}A_{j}.

(11)

Since both the initial state and the Hamiltonian enjoy a fully permutational symmetry we have that, for every

\braket{A_{j}}=\braket{A^{S}}

\begin{split}i\frac{d}{dt}\braket{A^{S}}=-\braket{[H,A^{S}]}&=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\braket{[H_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{j}),A_{j}]}+\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{j^{\prime},j}\braket{H_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{j^{\prime}})[H_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{j}),A_{j}]+[H_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{j}),A_{j}]H_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{j^{\prime}})}\\
&=-\braket{[H_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{j}),A_{j}]^{S}}+\frac{1}{2}\braket{H_{1}^{S}[H_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{j}),A_{j}]^{S}+[H_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{j}),A_{j}]^{S}H_{1}^{S}}\end{split},

&=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{j}\sum_{P^{\prime}P}\text{tr}_{j}\bigotimes_{j^{\prime}}\ket{k_{P(j^{\prime})}}\bra{k_{P^{\prime}(j^{\prime})}}\\
&=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P^{\prime}P}\sum_{j}\sum_{\{k^{\prime}_{j}\}}\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\braket{k^{\prime}_{j}|k_{P(j^{\prime})}}\braket{k_{P^{\prime}(j^{\prime})}|k^{\prime}_{j}}\ket{k_{P(j)}}\bra{k_{P^{\prime}(j)}}\\
&=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P^{\prime}P}\sum_{j}\sum_{\{k^{\prime}_{j}\}}\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\delta_{k^{\prime}_{j},k_{P(j^{\prime})}}\delta_{k^{\prime}_{j},k_{P^{\prime}(j^{\prime})}}\ket{k_{P(j)}}\bra{k_{P^{\prime}(j)}}\\
&=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{j}\sum_{P^{\prime}P}\delta_{P,P^{\prime}}\ket{k_{P(j)}}\bra{k_{P^{\prime}(j)}}\\
&=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{j}\sum_{P}\ket{k_{P(j)}}\bra{k_{P(j)}}\\
&=\frac{(N-1)!}{N!}\sum_{j}\ket{k_{j}}\bra{k_{j}}\\
\,\end{split}

(18)

from which our result follows.

From this analysis it also follows that the energy density

\epsilon=\mathrm{tr}(\rho(t)H_{0})-\mu^{2}(t)/2

(19)

is conserved. Indeed

\dot{\epsilon}=\mathrm{tr}(\dot{\rho}H_{0})-\mu\mathrm{tr}(\dot{\rho}H_{0})=\mathrm{tr}(\dot{\rho}\mathcal{H})=\mathrm{tr}([\mathcal{H},\rho]\mathcal{H})=0

(20)

I.2
II. Proof of the necessary condition

In the following we will prove the necessary condition exposed in the main text.

Let us thus suppose that Violent Relaxation occurs, i.e. that in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
,
\mu(t)\rightarrow\mu_{\infty}
for some
\mu_{\infty}
. As a consequence, at large times the effective hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
in Eq. (3) of the main text can be considered a time-independent operator
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
, and the evolution of the effective state
\rho(t)
given by the Von Neuman equation

i\frac{d}{dt}\rho(t)=\left[\mathcal{H}_{\infty},\rho(t)\right]\equiv\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\rho(t)).

(21)

Let us now suppose the spectrum of
\mathcal{E}
is continuous except for a finite set of discrete points
\{E_{k}\}
,
k=1,\cdots,K
. The spectrum of the (time-independent) Von Neumann superoperator
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
is given by
\{\Omega\}=\{\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\}
, where
\mathcal{E}
,
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
belong to the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
. In particular, the
\Omega
will be continuous as well, but for a finite set of values of the form
\mathcal{E}_{k}-\mathcal{E}_{k^{\prime}}
, with
k,k^{\prime}=1,\cdots n
. We can thus write that, at large times

\rho(t)=\int d\Omega\,\rho(\Omega)\,d(\Omega)e^{i\Omega t}+\sum^{n}_{k,k^{\prime}=1}\rho_{k,k^{\prime}}e^{i(\mathcal{E}_{k}-\mathcal{E}_{k^{\prime}})t}

(22)

where
d(\Omega)
is the density of states of the continuous part of the spectrum,
\rho(\Omega)
,
\rho_{k,k^{\prime}}
are operator fixed by the initial conditions and the small-time dynamics. Finally,

\mu(t)=\int d\Omega\,\text{tr}(\rho(\Omega)H_{1})\,d(\Omega)e^{i\Omega t}+\sum^{n}_{k,k^{\prime}=1}\text{tr}(\rho_{k,k^{\prime}}H_{1})\,e^{i(\mathcal{E}_{k}-\mathcal{E}_{k^{\prime}})t}\ .

(23)

By the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma, the first term in the right-hand-side of Eq. (
23
) goes to zero in the
t\rightarrow\infty
limit. This means that, for
n=0
,
\mu(t)\rightarrow\infty
at later times. Let us consider now the
n>1
case: the sum in the right-hand-side will contain oscillating terms thus contradicting the hypothesis of a dumping. We conclude that we can have violent relaxation only for
n=0
or
n=1
, while for
n>1

\mu(t)
keeps oscillating. In particular, for
n=0
, we have that
\mu(t)\rightarrow 0
, while for
n=1
this argument does not fix the asymptotic value of
\mu(t)
.

Let us notice that, for
n=2
, only one harmonic is present in the right-hand side of Eq. (
23
). This, however, is not enough to conclude that for
n=2

\mu(t)
is periodic. Indeed, if
\mu(t)
were periodic at late times, one could not define a time-independent effective Hamiltonian analogous to
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
. For the same reason, the above argument cannot be used to prove that, when
n=1
or
n=0
for any time, then an asymptotic relaxation occurs. Indeed, even in presence of a single bound state in the instantaneous Hamiltonian, parametric resonances may occur.

I.4
IV. The classical limit

I.4.1
Wigner function formalism

In this paragraph we will address the classical limit of our problem, expanding the argument given in the main text. We thus consider a system with a well-defined classical limit, e.g. a system whose local variables can be expressed in terms of a canonical pair
x,p
,
[x,p]=i
. Let us notice that the two-component quantum rotor model introduced in the main text falls into this category, with
x=\theta
,
p=L
. In this case it is convenient to introduce the Wigner function associated to the state
\rho(t)

W_{t}(x,p)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int dy\ \bra{x-y}\rho(t)\ket{x+y}e^{2ipy}\ ,

(33)

and the corresponding Weyl transform of a generic observable

A_{W}(x,p)=2\int dy\ \bra{x-y}A\ket{x+y}e^{2ipy}\ .

(34)

In this formalism the evolution of
W_{t}(x,p)
is given by the equation

\partial_{t}W_{t}=\{\{\mathcal{H}_{W},W_{t}\}\}=\{\{\mathcal{H}_{0,W}+\mu(t)\mathcal{H}_{1,W},W_{t}\}\}=-i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}(W_{t})\

(35)

where

\mu(t)=\int dxdp\ W_{t}(x,p)\mathcal{H}_{1,W}(x,p)\ ,

(36)

and
\{\{\cdot,\cdot\}\}
denotes the Moyal brackets

\{\{A,B\}\}=2A(x,p)\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}(\overleftarrow{\partial_{x}}\overrightarrow{\partial_{p}}-\overleftarrow{\partial_{p}}\overrightarrow{\partial_{x}})\right)B(x,p)\ .

(37)

For any given (constant) value of
\mu
, the evolution operator
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}
introduced in Eq. (
35
), has the same spectrum of the Von Neumann super-operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{H}}=[\mathcal{H},\cdot]
(see Sec. II) , namely

\{\Omega\}=\{\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\},

(38)

with
\mathcal{E}
,
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
part of the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}
.

I.4.2
Taking the classical limit

In the classical limit,
W_{t}(x,p)
becomes a probability density in the phase space, while the Moyal brackets reduces to Poisson brackets

\{\{\cdot,\cdot\}\}\rightarrow\{\cdot,\cdot\}

(39)

so that the evolution equation (
35
) becomes a Vlasov equation for
W_{t}(x,p)

\partial_{t}W_{t}=\{\mathcal{H}_{W},W_{t}\}=-i\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{H}}(W_{t})\ ,

(40)

where
\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the classical equivalent of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}
. Remarkably, it is possible to derive a general expression for the spectrum of
\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{H}}
. Indeed, let us notice that Eq. (
40
) is covariant under canonical transformation of the canonical phase-space pair
x,p
. For any given
\mu
it is thus possible to write everything in terms of the action-angle pair
\phi,J
so that
\mathcal{H}_{W}(x,p)
becomes a function
\mathcal{H}_{W}(J)
of
J
alone. Eq. (
40
) becomes thus

\partial_{t}W_{t}(\phi,J)=-\omega(J)\partial_{\phi}W_{t}(\phi,J)\ ,

(41)

with
\omega(J)\equiv\mathcal{H}_{W}^{\prime}(J)
, and finally

\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{H}}=i\omega(J)\partial_{\phi}\ .

(42)

The eigenfunctions of
\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{H}}
are given that

F_{J^{\prime},n}(J,n)=e^{-in\phi}\delta(J-J^{\prime})\ ,

(43)

with
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, with corresponding eigenvalues

\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{H}}(F_{J,n})=n\omega(J)F_{J,n}\ .

(44)

As
\omega(J)
is, in general, a continuous function of
J
, the spectrum

\{\Omega\}=\{n\omega(J)|n\in\mathbb{Z}\}

(45)

exhibits a band structure for any
n\neq 0
, while for
n=0
the whole band collapses into
\Omega=0
. By comparing it with the general form of the spectrum of the quantum operator
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}}
, namely. By comparing it with the quantum version (
38
) of the spectrum, we see that
\{\Omega\}
has exactly the structure expected from a quantum system with a single discrete point, i.e. the necessary condition for Violent Relaxation proved in Sec. II.
