Abstract




Abstract.
For the classical
N
-body problem in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
, Maderna-Venturelli in their remarkable paper [Ann.
Math. 2020] proved the existence of hyperbolic motions with any positive energy constant, starting from any configuration and along any non-collision configuration. Their original proof relies on the long time behavior of solutions by Chazy 1922 and Marchal-Saari 1976, on the Hölder estimate for Mañé’s potential by Maderna 2012, and on the weak KAM theory.

We give a new and completely different proof for the above existence of hyperbolic motions. The central idea is that, via some geometric observation, we build up uniform estimates for Euclidean length and angle of geodesics of Mañé’s potential starting from a given configuration and ending at the ray along a given non-collision configuration.

Moreover, our geometric approach works for Hamiltonians
\frac{1}{2}\|p\|^{2}-F(x)
, where
F(x)\geq 0
is lower semicontinuous and decreases very slowly to
0
faraway from collisions. We therefore obtain the existence of hyperbolic motions to such Hamiltonians with any positive energy constant, starting from any admissible configuration and along any non-collision configuration. Consequently, for several important potentials
F\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, we get similar existence of hyperbolic motions to the generalized
N
-body system
\ddot{x}=\nabla_{x}F(x)
, which is an extension of Maderna-Venturelli [Ann. Math. 2020].

1
Introduction

The classical
N
-body problem in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
consists in studying the second order ordinary system

\ddot{x}=\nabla_{x}U(x),

(1.1)

where
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
, each body
x_{i}
has mass
m_{i}>0
,

U(x)
denotes the Newtonian potential, and
\nabla_{x}U=(\nabla_{x_{i}}U)_{1\leq i\leq N}
with

\nabla_{x_{i}}=\frac{1}{m_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}
being the gradient with respect to the mass scalar product.
In other words,

U(x)=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{|x_{i}-x_{j}|},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla_{x_{i}}U(x)=\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}\frac{m_{j}}{|x_{j}-x_{i}|^{3}}(x_{j}-x_{i}).

The Hamiltonian corresponding to the system (
1.1
) is
\frac{1}{2}\|p\|^{2}-U(x)
, where the weighted norm
\|p\|
is defined by
\|p\|:=(\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}|p_{i}|^{2})^{1/2}

for
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
.
Below, denote by
\Omega
the set of non-collision configurations and by
\Sigma
the set of configurations including some collision, that is,

\Omega:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}:x_{i}\neq x_{j},\,\forall\,1\leq i<j\leq N\}\quad\mbox{and $\Sigma:={\mathbb{R}}^{dN}\setminus\Omega$}.

It is well known that
\Omega
is a path connected open set (that is, a domain) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
.

The long-time behavior of solutions to the system (
1.1
) has been insistently concerned after the fundamental works by Chazy in 1918; see
[
13
,
14
,
32
,
23
,
29
,
18
,
6
,
21
]
and the references therein. Indeed, Chazy classified different asymptotic types of solutions for the three-body problem, and moreover, established the continuity of the limit shape; see
[
13
,
14
]
.
Later, these results were generalized to the
N
-body problem for any
N\geq 4
; see
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
40
,
44
,
23
]
. Following Chazy
[
14
]
, if
x:[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
is a solution to (
1.1
) and satisfies
x(t)=ta+o(t)
as
t\to+\infty
for some non-collision configuration
a\in\Omega
, we call it a
hyperbolic motion
to (
1.1
) along
a
. The following was proved in
[
13
,
14
,
23
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
x:[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
be a hyperbolic motion to (
1.1
) along
a\in\Omega
.
Then we have the following:

(i)

The solution
x
has asymptotic velocity
a
, that is,
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\dot{x}(t)=a;

(ii)

Given any
\epsilon>0
, there are constants

t^{\ast}>0
and
\delta>0
such that, for any maximal solution
y:[0,T)\to\Omega

satisfying
|y(0)-x(0)|<\delta
and
|\dot{y}(0)-\dot{x}(0)|<\delta
, we have

(ii)
1

T=+\infty
,
|y(t)-ta|<t\epsilon
for all
t>t^{\ast}
and

(ii)
2
there is
b\in\Omega
with
|b-a|<\epsilon
such that
y=tb+o(t).

In 1976, Marchal-Saari provided the following classification of long-time behavior for the
N
-body problem; see Theorem 1 and Section 10 in
[
32
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
x:[0,+\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
be a solution to (
1.1
). Then, as
t\to+\infty
, either

\frac{R(t)}{t}\to+\infty,\,\mbox{where $R(t):=\max\{|x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)|:1\leq i<j\leq N\}$,}

(1.2)

or

\mbox{ there exists $A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}$ such that}\ x(t)=At+o(t).

(1.3)

Note that if
x
satisfies (
1.3
) with
A\in\Omega
, then
x(t)
is exactly a hyperbolic motion. In general, (
1.3
) may not be true; and even when it is true,
the condition
A\in\Omega
does not necessarily hold. Thus Theorem
1.2
does not affirm the existence of hyperbolic motions.

In 2020, Maderna-Venturelli showed the existence of hyperbolic motions to (
1.1
) starting from any configuration and along any given non-collision configuration; see Theorem 1.1 in
[
29
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Given any initial configuration
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
, any non-collision configuration
a\in\Omega
with
\|a\|=1
, and any choice of the energy constant
\lambda>0
, there always exists a hyperbolic motion

x:[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
to (
1.1
) starting from
x_{0}
and along
\sqrt{2\lambda}a
, that is,

\mbox{$x(0)=x_{0}$ and }\ x(t)=\sqrt{2\lambda}at+o(t)\mbox{ as }t\to+\infty.

The original proof of Theorem
1.3
by Maderna-Venturelli relies on Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, and also uses the weak KAM theory and Hölder estimate for Mañé’s potential. Indeed,
starting from given
x_{0}
and along given
a
as in Theorem
1.3
,
Maderna-Venturelli
[
29
]

constructed unbounded calibrating curves of viscosity solutions to Hamilton-Jacobi equation involving the Newtonian potential,
where the possibility of bounded calibrating curves was ruled out by exploiting a classical result by Von Zeipel
[
45
]
(1908). Next, thanks to Marchal-Saari’s classification result in Theorem
1.2
,
such unbounded calibrating curves satisfy either (
1.2
) or (
1.3
). Via some Hölder estimate for Mañé’s potential
m_{\lambda}
by Maderna
[
28
]
(see also
[
29
, Theorem 2.11]
), the possibility of (
1.2
) is excluded.
Thus such unbounded calibrating curves must satisfy (
1.3
) for some
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
.
Finally, by using the asymptotic behavior in Theorem
1.1
by Chazy
[
14
]
and Gingold-Solomon
[
23
]
, they showed that
A
must be
\sqrt{2\lambda}a
as desired.

In this paper, we give a new and completely different proof for Theorem
1.3
, which is based on some geometric observation for geodesics of Mañé’s potential. The central point is that: we consider a solution
{\gamma}^{(n)}:[0,\sigma^{(n)}]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
to the system (
1.1
) joining
x_{0}
and
x_{0}+s^{\ast}a+2^{n}a
for all sufficiently large
n
, where
s^{\ast}
is a constant depending on
x_{0}
and
a
; indeed,
{\gamma}^{(n)}
is a geodesic of Mañé’s potential
m_{\lambda}
. Via some geometric observation and an induction argument, we are able to bound, uniformly in
n
, the Euclidean length of
{\gamma}^{(n)}|_{[0,t]}
and also the angle between
{\gamma}^{(n)}(t)-x_{0}-s^{\ast}a
and
a
whenever
t\in[\sigma_{0},\sigma^{(n)}]
for some constant
\sigma_{0}
depending on
x_{0},\lambda
and
a
. The desired hyperbolic motion is then given by the limit of
{\gamma}^{(n)}
as
n\to\infty
(up to some subsequence). See Section 2 for more detail.

Moreover, our geometric approach works for Hamiltonians
\frac{1}{2}\|p\|^{2}-F(x)
, where
F(x)\geq 0
is lower semicontinuous and decreases very slowly to
0
faraway from collisions.
We therefore obtain the existence of hyperbolic motions to such Hamiltonian with any positive energy constant, starting from any admissible configuration, and along any non-collision configuration. Consequently, we get a similar existence of hyperbolic motions to the generalized
N
-body problem
\ddot{x}=\nabla_{x}F(x)
for several important potentials
F\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, which is an extension of Maderna-Venturelli [Ann. Math. 2020]. See Theorem 1.4 and Corollary 1.5 below.

To be precise, we first recall several necessary notions and facts for the Hamiltonian
\frac{1}{2}\|p\|^{2}-F(x)
.
In this paper we always assume

\left\{\begin{split}&F(x):={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}F_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\quad\mbox{$\forall\ x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}$},\mbox{ where }\\
&\mbox{$F_{ij}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\Delta\to[0,\infty)$ is locally bounded, and is also lower semicontinuous, that is, }\\
&\mbox{for any $\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}$ the level set
$\{(x_{i},x_{j})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\Delta:F_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})>\delta\}$ is open.}\end{split}\right.

(1.4)

Here and below we write
\Delta=\{(z,z):z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\}
. In a natural way, one may extend
F_{ij}
to be a lower semicontinuous function in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with values in
[0,\infty]
, and hence extend
F
to be a lower semicontinuous function in
{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
with values in
[0,\infty]
. See Appendix for details.

For any energy constant
\lambda>0
, Mañé’s potential or the action potential
m_{\lambda}
is defined by

\displaystyle m_{\lambda}(x,y):=\inf\{A_{\lambda}({\gamma}):{\gamma}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma];{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})\,\,\mbox{for some $\sigma>0$}\},\qquad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN},

(1.5)

where

A_{\lambda}({\gamma}):=\int_{0}^{\sigma}[\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}(s)|^{2}+F\circ{\gamma}(s)+\lambda]\,ds,

and for any set
W\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{dN},

\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma];W):=\{{\gamma}:[0,\sigma]\to W\ \mbox{is absolutely continuous, and }{\gamma}(0)=x,{\gamma}(\sigma)=y\}.

Since
\Omega
is path-connected,
(\Omega,m_{\lambda})
is a metric space. Denote by
\widetilde{\Omega}
the set

\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}:\text{there exists $\lambda>0$ and $x\in\Omega$ such that $m_{\lambda}(x,y)<+\infty$}\}.

(1.6)

We remark that the set
\widetilde{\Omega}
is independent of the choice of
\lambda>0
and
x\in\Omega
; see Appendix. Configurations
x\in\widetilde{\Omega}
are also said to be admissible.
It turns out that
(\widetilde{\Omega},m_{\lambda})
is a geodesic space, and geodesics joining any pair of distinct configurations
x,y\in\widetilde{\Omega}
are given by the arc-length parametrization of minimizers of
A_{\lambda}
in the class

\cup_{\sigma>0}\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma];\widetilde{\Omega})
; see Appendix for more details.
Below, to
clarify the geometric nature, we call a minimizer of
A_{\lambda}

as an
m_{\lambda}
-geodesic with canonical parameter
. We also call a continuous curve
{\gamma}:[0,\infty)\to\widetilde{\Omega}
as an
m_{\lambda}
-geodesic ray with canonical parameter
if,
for any
\sigma>0
, the restriction

{\gamma}|_{[0,\sigma]}
is an
m_{\lambda}
-geodesic with canonical parameter.
Moreover, in the spirit of Chazy, if
{\gamma}
is an
m_{\lambda}
-geodesic ray with canonical parameter for some
\lambda>0
and satisfies
x(t)=at+o(t)
as
t\to+\infty
for some non-collision configuration
a
, we call
{\gamma}
as a
hyperbolic motion to the Hamiltonian
\frac{1}{2}\|p\|^{2}-F(x)
with energy constant
\lambda>0
and along
a
,
or for simplicity,
hyperbolic motion to
m_{\lambda}
along
a
.

Next, assuming in addition that
F_{ij}\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\Delta;{\mathbb{R}})
for all
1\leq i<j\leq N
(for simplicity we write
F\in C^{2}(\Omega)
below), we consider the generalized
N
-body system

\ddot{x}=\nabla_{x}F(x),

(1.7)

which corresponds to the Hamiltonian
\frac{1}{2}\|p\|^{2}-F(x)
.
Following Chazy, if a solution
x:[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
to (
1.7
) satisfies
x(t)=at+o(t)
as
t\to+\infty
for some non-collision configuration
a\in\Omega
, then we call it as a
hyperbolic motion to (
1.7
) along
a
.

Recall that if
{\gamma}:[0,\sigma]\to\widetilde{\Omega}
is an
m_{\lambda}
-geodesic with canonical parameter and if
{\gamma}
is collision free interiorly, that is,
{\gamma}|_{(0,\sigma)}\subset\Omega
, then it is a solution to the system (
1.7
) with energy constant
\lambda
,
see Lemma A.3 and Lemma A.4 in Appendix. Consequently, a hyperbolic motion to
m_{\lambda}
without collision in
(0,\infty)
is a hyperbolic motion to (
1.7
).

Below, we build up an existence result of hyperbolic motions to the Hamiltonian
\frac{1}{2}\|p\|^{2}-F(x)
, and hence to the corresponding N-body system
\ddot{x}=\nabla_{x}F(x)
when
F\in C^{2}(\Omega)
additionally,
provided that
F
satisfies the following growth assumption faraway from collisions:

\left\{\begin{split}&F_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq f(|x_{i}-x_{j}|),\ \mbox{ $\forall(x_{i},x_{j})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\Delta_{2}$ for all $1\leq i<j\leq N$,}\\
&\mbox{ where $f\in C^{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},{\mathbb{R}}_{+})$ satisfies}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(2^{-k}\int_{2^{k}}^{2^{k+1}}f(s)\,ds\right)^{1/2}<\infty.\end{split}\right.

(1.8)

Here and below
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}:=(0,\infty)
, and

\Delta_{\delta}:=\{(z,w)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:|z-w|<\delta\}.

Note that, if
f
is decreasing, then

\int_{4}^{\infty}\sqrt{f(s)}\frac{ds}{s}\leq\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\left(2^{-k}\int_{2^{k}}^{2^{k+1}}f(s)\,ds\right)^{1/2}\leq 2\int_{2}^{\infty}\sqrt{f(s)}\frac{ds}{s}.

Moreover, if

f(s)\leq C(\log s)^{-\beta}
,
\forall s\geq 2
, for some
\beta>2
, then

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(2^{-k}\int_{2^{k}}^{2^{k+1}}f(s)\,ds\right)^{1/2}\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{C^{1/2}}{(\log 2)^{\beta/2}}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{-\beta/2}<\infty.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
F
be as in (
1.4
) and satisfying (
1.8
).
Given any energy constant
\lambda>0
, any initial configuration
x\in\widetilde{\Omega}
and any non-collision configuration
a\in\Omega
with
\|a\|=1
, we have the following:

(i)

There exists a hyperbolic motion
{\gamma}:[0,+\infty)\to\widetilde{\Omega}

to the Hamiltonian
\frac{1}{2}\|p\|^{2}-F(x)
with the energy constant
\lambda>0
, along
\sqrt{2\lambda}a
and
starting from
x
, that is,

{\gamma}(0)=x
and

{\gamma}(t)=\sqrt{2\lambda}at+o(t)
as
t\to+\infty.

(ii)

There exists
t_{0}=t_{0}(x,a,\lambda)\geq 0
such that the restriction

{\gamma}|_{[t_{0},\infty)}
is collision-free, that is,

{\gamma}|_{[t_{0},\infty)}\subset\Omega
.

(iii)

If
F\in C^{2}(\Omega)
,
then the restriction
{\gamma}|_{[t_{0},\infty)}
is a hyperbolic motion to (
1.7
) along
\sqrt{2\lambda}a
.

(iv)

If
F\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, and suppose in addition
\gamma|_{(0,t_{0})}
is collision free,
then
{\gamma}
is a hyperbolic motion to (
1.7
)
starting from
x
and along
\sqrt{2\lambda}a
.

The study of (non-)collisions is one of the main issues in this field. To some extent, (non-)collisions are determined by the behavior of
F
near the set
\Sigma
of collisions. There do exist potentials
F
, for which one can find some
m_{\lambda}
-geodesic including interior collisions, see
[
8
,
9
,
34
]
for constructions. Therefore, in Theorem 1.4 (iv), since
no other assumption is made on
F
around
\Sigma
, we can not expect in general that the restriction
{\gamma}|_{(0,t_{0})}
is collision-free.

On the other hand, it is well known (see
[
22
,
5
]
) that all
m_{\lambda}
-geodesics are collision-free interiorly, and hence are solutions to the system (
1.7
), provided that
F(x)
satisfies any one of the following conditions near
\Sigma
:

(E1)

For all
1\leq i<j\leq N
,

F_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\geq Cm_{i}m_{j}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-2}
in
\Delta_{1/2}
,
where
C>0
.

(E2)

For all
1\leq i<j\leq N
,
F_{ij}
coincides with the homogeneous potential
m_{i}m_{j}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-\alpha}

in
\Delta_{1/2}
, where
0<\alpha<\infty
. When
\alpha=2
,
it was also called as the Jacobi-Banachiewitz potential; see
[
3
,
43
,
15
]
.

(E3)

For all
1\leq i<j\leq N
,

F_{ij}
is given by the quasi-homogeneous potential

m_{i}m_{j}\left(|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-\alpha}+\delta|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-\beta}\right)\mbox{in $\Delta_{1/2}$, where $0<\beta<\alpha<\infty$ and $\delta>0$.}

It was also called as the Manev potential when
\alpha=2
and
\beta=1
(see
[
31
,
17
,
27
]
),
and as
the Schwarzschild potential
when
\alpha=3
and
\beta=1
(see
[
2
]
).

(E4)

For all
1\leq i<j\leq N
,
F_{ij}
is given by the
Lennard-Jones potential (see
[
25
,
26
]
), that is,
A|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-12}-B|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-6}
in

\Delta_{\delta}
for some
\delta=\delta(A,B)>0
, where
A,B>0
.

(E5)

For all
1\leq i<j\leq N
,
F_{ij}

is gien by the Seeliger-Yukawa potential (see
[
41
,
42
]
), that is,
Ae^{-B|x_{i}-x_{j}|}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-1}
in
\Delta_{1/2}
, where
A,B>0
.

(E6)

For all
1\leq i<j\leq N
,

F_{ij}
is given by the Mücket-Treder potential (see
[
35
,
39
]
), that is,
|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-1}(A-B\log|x_{i}-x_{j}|)
in
\Delta_{1/2}
,
where
A,B>0
.

(E7)

For all
1\leq i<j\leq N
,
F_{ij}
coincides with the logarithmic potential
m_{i}m_{j}\log|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-1}

in
\Delta_{1/2}
.

Thus, as a direct consequence of Theorem 1.4 (iv), we have the following.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
F\in C^{2}(\Omega)
be as in (
1.4
) and satisfy (
1.8
).
Suppose that
F
satisfies any one of (E1)-(E7) near
\Sigma
.

Given any energy constant
\lambda>0
, any initial configuration
x\in\widetilde{\Omega}
and any non-collision configuration
a\in\Omega
with
\|a\|=1
, there is a hyperbolic motion to the system (
1.7
) with the energy constant
\lambda
, along
\sqrt{2\lambda}a
and starting from
x
.

In Section 3–Section 8, we prove Theorem
1.4
and hence Corollary 1.5;
in particular, when

with

For reader’s convenience, in Section 2 we sketch our geometric proof to Theorem

Remark 1.6
.

We remark that it seems possible to extend our geometric approach to general Hamiltonians

2
Sketch of the proof to Theorem
1.4

Note that we only prove Theorem
1.4
under the assumption that

the mass
m_{i}=1
for each
1\leq i\leq N
.

(2.1)

For general masses, by some necessary and standard modifications, our argument also works; we omit the details.

Before sketching the proof, we introduce several notations which are needed below. In the sequel of this paper, we always assume that (
2.1
) holds and hence the corresponding weighted norm
\|\cdot\|

(resp. weighted inner product)
is then the Euclidean norm (resp. inner product), that is,

\|x\|=(\sum_{i=1}^{N}|x_{i}|^{2})^{1/2}=|x|\quad\mbox{and hence }\quad\langle\langle x,y\rangle\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle x_{i},y_{i}\rangle\quad\mbox{ for $x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}$.}

(2.2)

To measure the distance of any configuration
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
to the set of collisions, we always set

y^{\flat}:=\min\{|y_{i}-y_{j}|:1\leq i<j\leq N\}.

(2.3)

Note that
y^{\flat}>0
if and only if
y\in\Omega
, that is,

\Omega=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}:y^{\flat}>0\}.

For any
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
,
write
[x,y]
(resp.
(x,y)
) as the closed (resp. open) line-segment between them, that is,

[x,y]=\{tx+(1-t)y\}_{t\in[0,1]}\ \mbox{and}\ (x,y)=[x,y]\setminus\{x,y\}=\{tx+(1-t)y\}_{t\in(0,1)}.

(2.4)

Write
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}=(0,\infty)
, the open half ray

x+{\mathbb{R}}_{+}y=\{x+ty:t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\}

and
\overline{x+{\mathbb{R}}_{+}y}
as the closure of
x+{\mathbb{R}}_{+}y
.
Given

and

the Euclidean distance from

For any absolutely continuous curve
{\gamma}:[0,\sigma]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
, denote by

its Euclidean length, that is,

(2.5)

For any two configurations

with
a,b\neq 0
, denote by

the angle between them, that is the unique element of
[0,\pi]
satisfying

\cos\angle(a,b)=\frac{\langle a,b\rangle}{|a||b|}.

Denote by
\mathbb{S}^{dN-1}
the set of all configuration
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
satisfying
|a|=1
.
Note that, for any two configurations
a,b\in\mathbb{S}^{dN-1}
, the smallness of

is equivalent to that of the angle

; indeed,

\frac{1}{2}|a-b|=\sin\frac{1}{2}\angle(a,b).

(2.6)

Now we are ready to sketch the proof to Theorem
1.4

and hence Corollary 1.5,
in particular, when
F=U
and

, we sketch a new proof of Theorem 1.3.

3
Some basic geometric properties of non-collision configurations

In what follows, we always assume the mass
m_{i}=1
for each
1\leq i\leq N
. Recall that
\Omega
is the set of non-collision configurations, that is, all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
with
x^{\flat}>0
. We refer to (
2.2
)-(
2.5
) and therein for several notations which will be used below.

In this section, we prove several basic geometric properties of non-collision configurations (see Lemma 2.1–Lemma 2.4), which will be used later.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
and
a\in{\mathbb{S}}^{dN-1}
with
a^{\flat}>0
.
If
|a-b|\leq\delta{a^{\flat}}
for some
0<\delta<\frac{1}{5}
, then

b^{\flat}>a^{\flat}-2|b-a|\geq(1-2\delta)a^{\flat},

(3.1)

and

\cos\angle(a_{i}-a_{j},b_{i}-b_{j})=\frac{\langle a_{i}-a_{j},b_{i}-b_{j}\rangle}{|a_{i}-a_{j}||b_{i}-b_{j}|}\geq 1-4\delta,\quad\forall 1\leq i<j\leq N.

If we further assume
b\in{\mathbb{S}}^{dN-1}
, then

\cos\angle(a,b)=\langle a,b\rangle\geq 1-2\delta^{2}.

(3.2)

Proof.

By the triangle inequality,

|b_{i}-b_{j}|\geq|a_{i}-a_{j}|-|b_{i}-a_{i}|-|b_{j}-a_{j}|>|a_{i}-a_{j}|-2|b-a|,\quad\forall 1\leq i<j\leq N.

From this, we conclude

b^{\flat}>a^{\flat}-2|b-a|.

Thus, by
|a-b|\leq\delta a^{\flat}
, it holds that

b^{\flat}\geq(1-2\delta)a^{\flat}.

Moreover, for all
1\leq i<j\leq N,
by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and the triangle inequality,

\langle a_{i}-a_{j},b_{i}-b_{j}\rangle=\langle a_{i}-a_{j},a_{i}-a_{j}\rangle+\langle a_{i}-a_{j},b_{i}-a_{i}-b_{j}+a_{j}\rangle\geq|a_{i}-a_{j}|[|a_{i}-a_{j}|-2|b-a|].

Note that

|b_{i}-b_{j}|\leq|a_{i}-a_{j}|+|b_{i}-a_{i}|+|b_{j}-a_{j}|\leq|a_{i}-a_{j}|+2|b-a|.

We then have

\frac{\langle a_{i}-a_{j},b_{i}-b_{j}\rangle}{|a_{i}-a_{j}||b_{i}-b_{j}|}\geq\frac{|a_{i}-a_{j}|-2|b-a|}{|a_{i}-a_{j}|+2|b-a|}=1-\frac{4|b-a|}{|a_{i}-a_{j}|+2|b-a|}\geq 1-\frac{4|b-a|}{a^{\flat}},

and hence, by
|a-b|\leq\delta a^{\flat}
, it holds that

\cos\angle(a_{i}-a_{j},b_{i}-b_{j})\geq 1-4\delta.

For the last inequality (
3.2
), note that
a^{\flat}<2
and

2-2\langle a,b\rangle=|a-b|^{2}\leq\delta^{2}(a^{\flat})^{2}.

It implies

\langle a,b\rangle=1-\frac{1}{2}|a-b|^{2}\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}(a^{\flat})^{2}\geq 1-2\delta^{2}

as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
and

a\in{\mathbb{S}}^{dN-1}
with
a^{\flat}>0
. For
s\geq 50\frac{1+|x|}{a^{\flat}}
, we have

|\frac{x+sa}{|x+sa|}-a|\leq\frac{a^{\flat}}{20}

and

(x+sa)^{\flat}\geq\frac{24}{25}sa^{\flat}>2.

Proof.

For
s>|x|
, write

|\frac{x+sa}{|x+sa|}-a|=\frac{1}{|x+sa|}\left|x+sa-|x+sa|a\right|.

By the triangle inequality, one has

\left|x+sa-|x+sa|a\right|\leq|x|+\left|s-|x+sa|\right|=|x|+\left||sa|-|x+sa|\right|\leq 2|x|,

and hence

|\frac{x+sa}{|x+sa|}-a|\leq\frac{1}{|x+sa|}2|x|\leq\frac{1}{s-|x|}{2|x|}.

By
s\geq 50\frac{1+|x|}{a^{\flat}}
and
a^{\flat}<2
,
we have
s-|x|\geq 40\frac{|x|}{a^{\flat}}
and hence

\frac{1}{s-|x|}{2|x|}\leq\frac{a^{\flat}}{20}

as desired.

Moreover, it is easy to see that

(x+sa)^{\flat}\geq sa^{\flat}-2|x|\geq\frac{24}{25}sa^{\flat}.

The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

If
z,b\in\Omega
satisfy

\angle(b_{i}-b_{j},z_{i}-z_{j})\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\mbox{ or equivalently}\ \cos\angle(b_{i}-b_{j},z_{i}-z_{j})\geq 0\quad\forall 1\leq i<j\leq N,

then
(z+tb)^{\flat}>z^{\flat}>0
, and hence
z+tb\subset\Omega
, for all
t\in[0,\infty)
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
for any
1\leq i<j\leq N
,

|z_{i}+tb_{i}-(z_{j}+tb_{j})|\geq|z_{i}-z_{j}|
for all
t>0
.

To see this, write

\mbox{$q=z_{i}-z_{j}$ and $e=b_{i}-b_{j}$, and hence, $|z_{i}+tb_{i}-(z_{j}+tb_{j})|=|q+te|$}.

Then

|e|\geq b^{\flat}>0
and
|q|\geq z^{\flat}>0
. The assumption
\angle(q,e)\leq\pi/2
implies
\langle q,e\rangle\geq 0.
Thus

|q+et|=\sqrt{\langle q+et,q+et\rangle}=\sqrt{|q|^{2}+2\langle q,e\rangle t+|e|^{2}t^{2}}\geq\sqrt{|q|^{2}+|e|^{2}t^{2}}\geq|q|\geq z^{\flat}>0,\quad\forall t\geq 0

as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
and

a\in{\mathbb{S}}^{dN-1}
with
a^{\flat}>0
.
If
b\in{\mathbb{S}}^{dN-1}
with
|a-b|\leq\frac{a^{\flat}}{20}
,
then for all
s\geq 50\frac{1+|x|}{a^{\flat}}
and
t\geq 0,
one has

(x+sa+tb)^{\flat}\geq(x+sa)^{\flat}\geq\frac{24}{25}sa^{\flat}\geq 2.

(3.3)

Therefore,
x+sa+tb\in\Omega
.

Proof.

For any fixed
s\geq 50\frac{1+|x|}{a^{\flat}}
, write
c=\frac{x+sa}{|x+sa|}
.
By Lemma
3.2
,
|c-a|\leq\frac{a^{\flat}}{20}
.
Since
|a-b|\leq\frac{a^{\flat}}{20}
, by (
3.1
) in Lemma
3.1
, we have

|c-b|\leq|c-a|+|a-b|\leq\frac{a^{\flat}}{10}\ \,\,\mbox{and}\ \,\,b^{\flat}\geq\frac{9}{10}a^{\flat}>0.

Thus

|c-b|\leq\frac{b^{\flat}}{9}
. By Lemma
3.1
, it implies that
\angle(c,b)\leq\arccos\frac{79}{81}<\pi/2
. Then for all
\delta\geq 0
, it holds that
\angle(c+\delta b,b)\leq\angle(c,b)<\pi/2
. Hence,

|\frac{c+\delta b}{|c+\delta b|}-b|=2\sin\frac{1}{2}\angle(c+\delta b,b)\leq 2\sin\frac{1}{2}\angle(c,b)=|c-b|\leq\frac{b^{\flat}}{9},\,\quad\forall\delta\geq 0.

(3.4)

Note that for any
t\geq 0
,

\frac{x+sa+tb}{|x+sa+tb|}=\frac{c+\frac{t}{|x+sa|}b}{|c+\frac{t}{|x+sa|}b|}.

By (
3.4
), it follows that

|\frac{x+sa+tb}{|x+sa+tb|}-b|=|\frac{c+\frac{t}{|x+sa|}b}{|c+\frac{t}{|x+sa|}b|}-b|\leq\frac{b^{\flat}}{9}.

By Lemma
3.1
, it holds that

\cos\angle(x_{i}+sa_{i}+tb_{i}-x_{j}-sa_{j}-tb_{j},b_{i}-b_{j})\geq\frac{5}{9}>\frac{1}{2},\quad\mbox{ $\forall$ $1\leq i<j\leq N$}.

From Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.2
, it follows that

(x+sa+tb)^{\flat}\geq(x+sa)^{\flat}\geq\frac{24}{25}sa^{\flat}.

We have completed the proof.
∎

4
An upper bound of Euclidean length of
m_{\lambda}
-geodesics

In this section, we build up the following upper bound of the Euclidean length of
m_{\lambda}
-geodesics with canonical parameter.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
x\in\widetilde{\Omega}
,
a\in\mathbb{S}^{dN-1}
with
a^{\flat}>0
and
\lambda>0
.
For any
s\geq 50\frac{1+|x|}{a^{\flat}}
,
b\in\mathbb{S}^{dN-1}
with
|a-b|\leq\frac{a^{\flat}}{20}
,
and any
T\geq 0
, if
{\gamma}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x+sa+Tb};[0,\sigma];\widetilde{\Omega})
is an
m_{\lambda}
-geodesic with canonical parameter, then we have

l({\gamma})\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}m_{\lambda}(x,x+sa+Tb)\leq T+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}m_{\lambda}(x,x+sa)+\frac{1}{\lambda}N^{2}\frac{1}{a^{\flat}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\int_{(x+sa)^{\flat}}^{2T+2|x+sa|}f(\tau)\,d\tau}.

(4.1)

To prove this we begin with the following trivial upper bound.

Lemma 4.2
.

If

{\gamma}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma];\widetilde{\Omega})
is an
m_{\lambda}
-geodesic with canonical parameter for some
\lambda>0
,
then one has

l({\gamma})\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}m_{\lambda}(x,y)\leq|y-x|+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{0}^{|y-x|}F(x+s\frac{y-x}{|y-x|})\,ds.

(4.2)

Proof.

Concerning the the first inequality in (
4.2
), by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality one has

\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}(s)|^{2}+F({\gamma}(s))+\lambda\geq\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}(s)|^{2}+\lambda\geq 2\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}(s)|^{2}}=\sqrt{2\lambda}|\dot{\gamma}(s)|,\quad a.\,e.

Thus

{m_{\lambda}(x,y)}=A_{\lambda}({\gamma})\geq\sqrt{2\lambda}l({\gamma}).

To see the second inequality in (
4.2
), we set
a=\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}
and
\eta(s)=x+\sqrt{2\lambda}sa
for
s\in[0,\frac{|y-x|}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}]
.
By definition,
{m_{\lambda}(x,y)}\leq A_{\lambda}(\eta)
. Since

\displaystyle A_{\lambda}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\frac{|y-x|}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}}[\lambda+F(x+\sqrt{2\lambda}sa)]\,ds+\lambda\frac{|y-x|}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}=\sqrt{2\lambda}|y-x|+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\int_{0}^{|y-x|}F(x+sa)\,ds,

we obtained the second inequality in (
4.2
) as desired.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Note that above if
[x,y]\subset\Omega
, then
\int_{0}^{|y-x|}F(x+sa)\,ds<\infty
; otherwise, it may happen that

\int_{0}^{|y-x|}F(x+sa)\,ds=\infty
. Recall that no assumption is made on the behavior of

in

essentially.
Below via some necessary geometric argument in Section 3, we could only meet the the integral of type
\int_{0}^{A}F(y+sb)\,ds
where

and the line-segment
[y,y+Ab]\Subset\Omega
.

Next we bound
\int_{0}^{T}F(z+sa)\,ds
for some
z
and
a
with
\overline{z+{\mathbb{R}}_{+}a}\subset\Omega
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
,

z\in\Omega
with
z^{\flat}\geq 2
and
a\in\mathbb{S}^{dN-1}
with
a^{\flat}>0
.
If

\sup_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\angle(a_{i}-a_{j},z_{i}-z_{j})\leq\theta,

then

\int_{0}^{T}F(z+sa)\,ds\leq\frac{N^{2}}{2\cos\theta}\frac{1}{a^{\flat}}\int_{z^{\flat}}^{2T+2|z|}f(s)\,ds

(4.3)

holds for any
T>0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
, one has

(z+sa)^{\flat}\geq z^{\flat}\geq 2
for each
s\geq 0
.
By (
1.4
) and (
1.8
), we then obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}F(z+sa)\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\int_{0}^{T}F_{ij}(z_{i}+sa_{i},z_{j}+sa_{j})\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\int_{0}^{T}f(|(z_{i}-z_{j})+s(a_{i}-a_{j})|)\,ds.

We claim that, for any
1\leq i<j\leq N
,

\int_{0}^{T}f(|(z_{i}-z_{j})+s(a_{i}-a_{j})|)\,ds\leq\frac{1}{\cos\theta}\frac{1}{|a_{i}-a_{j}|}\int_{|z_{i}-z_{j}|}^{|z_{i}-z_{j}+T(a_{i}-a_{j})|}f(t)\,dt.

(4.4)

Assume this claim holds for the moment. Note that

|z_{i}-z_{j}|\geq z^{\flat}\geq 2,\,\,|a_{i}-a_{j}|\geq a^{\flat}>0

and

|z_{i}-z_{j}+T(a_{i}-a_{j})|\leq 2|z|+T|a_{i}-a_{j}|\leq 2|z|+2T.

One has

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}F(z+sa)\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N^{2}}{2\cos\theta}\frac{1}{a^{\flat}}\int_{z^{\flat}}^{2T+2|z|}f(t)\,dt,

that is, (
4.3
) holds.

Finally, we prove the above claim (
4.4
).
For any fixed

1\leq i<j\leq N
, write
q=z_{i}-z_{j}
and
e=a_{i}-a_{j}
,
and hence

\int_{0}^{T}f(|(z_{i}-s_{j})+s(a_{i}-a_{j})|)\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}f(|q+se|)\,dt.

Write

for

. Simce the assumption

\langle q,e\rangle\geq|q||e|\cos\theta>0

and

\frac{dt}{ds}=\frac{\langle q+se,e\rangle}{|q+se|}\geq\frac{|e||q|\cos\theta+|e|^{2}s}{|q+se|}\geq|e|\cos\theta\frac{|q|+{|e|s}}{|q+se|}\geq|e|\cos\theta>0,\ \forall s\geq 0.

Thus

is a strictly increasing

\frac{ds}{dt}\leq\frac{1}{|e|\cos\theta}.

And hence, by the change of variable, we have

\int_{0}^{T}f(|q+se|)\,ds\leq\frac{1}{\cos\theta}\frac{1}{|e|}\int_{|q|}^{|q+Te|}f(t)\,dt

as desired.
∎

\int_{0}^{T}F(z+sb)\,ds\leq N^{2}\frac{2}{a^{\flat}}\int_{z^{\flat}}^{2T+2|z|}f(s)\,ds,\quad\,\forall T>0.

Proof.

Since

and

.
It follows that

Thus by applying Lemma

5
A key geometric observation for
m_{\lambda}
-geodesics

To state our geometric observation, we first introduce some conventions.

Given
x\in\widetilde{\Omega}
and

a\in{\mathbb{S}}^{dN-1}
with
a^{\flat}>0
, we always write

x^{\ast}:=x+(50\frac{1+|x|}{a^{\flat}})a.

By Lemma
3.4
, one has

(x^{\ast}+Tb)^{\flat}\geq 2
, and hence
x^{\ast}+Tb\in\Omega
,

\forall T\geq 0
and
b\in\mathbb{S}^{dN-1}
with
|a-b|\leq\frac{a^{\flat}}{20}
.

Given any
\lambda>0
, we always set

\Psi(T)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Psi_{\lambda,x,a}}(T):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}m_{\lambda}(x,x^{\ast})+\frac{1}{\lambda}N^{2}\frac{1}{a^{\flat}}\int_{(x^{\ast})^{\flat}}^{2T+2|x^{\ast}|}f(t)\,dt,\quad\forall T\geq 0.

(5.1)

Note that the assumption (
1.8
) guarantees

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{2^{-j}\Psi(2^{j})}<\infty.

(5.2)

Indeed, let
\xi\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
2^{\xi-1}\leq|x^{\ast}|<2^{\xi}
.
By (
5.1
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{2^{-j}\Psi(2^{j})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(2\lambda)^{1/4}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\sqrt{2^{-j}m_{\lambda}(x,x^{\ast})}+\frac{N}{(\lambda a^{\flat})^{1/2}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\sqrt{2^{-j}\int_{(x^{\ast})^{\flat}}^{2^{j+1}+2|x^{\ast}|}f(t)\,dt}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{(2\lambda)^{1/4}}\sqrt{m_{\lambda}(x,x^{\ast})}+\frac{N}{(\lambda a^{\flat})^{1/2}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\sqrt{2^{-j}\int_{1}^{2^{j+{\xi}+2}}f(t)\,dt}

where in the last inequality we use the facts that
|x^{\ast}|<2^{\xi}
and
2^{a}+2^{b}\leq 2^{a+b}
whenever
a\geq 1
and
b\geq 1
.
Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1}\sqrt{2^{-j}\int_{1}^{2^{j+{\xi}+2}}f(t)\,dt}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\geq 1}\sum_{k=0}^{j+{\xi}+1}2^{-j/2}\sqrt{\int_{2^{k}}^{2^{k+1}}f(t)\,dt}\leq{C(x^{\ast})}\sum_{k\geq 0}\sqrt{2^{-k}\int_{2^{k}}^{2^{k+1}}f(t)\,dt}

for some positive constant

. Thanks to (
1.8
), the right-hand side of the last inequality is
finite. Thus (
5.2
) holds.

We further write

\widetilde{\Psi}(t):=\sum_{j\geq\lfloor\log_{2}t\rfloor+1}\sqrt{2^{-j}\Psi(2^{j})}<\infty,\quad\forall\,t\geq 1.

Then
\widetilde{\Psi}(t)
is decreasing to

as

. Moreover,

\sqrt{t^{-1}\Psi(t)}\leq\widetilde{\Psi}(t),\quad\forall t\geq 1.

(5.3)

To see this, letting

for some

, we have

\widetilde{\Psi}(t)=\sum_{j\geq k+1}\sqrt{\frac{\Psi(2^{j})}{2^{j}}}\geq\sqrt{\Psi(2^{k+1})}\sum_{j\geq k+1}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{j}}}\geq\sqrt{\Psi(2^{k+1})}\frac{\sqrt{2^{-(k+1)}}}{1-\sqrt{2}^{-1}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\Psi(2^{k+1})}{2^{k}}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\Psi(t)}{t}},

where in the first and last “
\geq
” we use the fact that
\Psi(t)
is increasing with respect to
t
, and in the second last “
\geq
” we use the fact
(1-\sqrt{2}^{-1})^{-1}\geq\sqrt{2}
.

From (

),
it follows that

(5.4)

Obviously, one has

\displaystyle\Psi(T)\leq 2^{-20}(a^{\flat})^{2}T,\quad\mbox{for all $T\geq 2^{n_{0}}$}.

(5.5)

We remark that with the aid of

and

Thus
F
is also lower semicontinuous in whole
{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
.

For any
\lambda>0
, Mañé’s potential
m_{\lambda}
is defined in (
1.5
).
Observe that

m_{\lambda}(x,y)\geq\sqrt{2\lambda}|x-y|
for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
.

(A.1)

Indeed,
for any
{\gamma}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma],{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})
,
by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, its Euclidean length

l({\gamma})\leq(2\sigma)^{1/2}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma}\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}(s)|^{2}\,ds\right)^{1/2}\leq(2\sigma)^{1/2}\left(A_{\lambda}-\lambda\sigma\right)^{1/2},

and hence by
|x-y|\leq l({\gamma})
, one has

A_{\lambda}({\gamma})\geq\frac{1}{2\sigma}l({\gamma})^{2}+\lambda\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2\sigma}|x-y|^{2}+\lambda\sigma.

Since
\frac{1}{2\sigma}|x-y|^{2}+\lambda\sigma
reaches its minimal value at
\sigma=|x-y|/\sqrt{2\lambda}
, we have
A_{\lambda}({\gamma})\geq\sqrt{2\lambda}|x-y|
as desired.

On the other hand, we have

m_{\lambda}(x,y)<\infty,\,\,\quad\forall x,y\in\Omega.

Indeed, since
\Omega
is path connected, one can always find a smooth curve
{\gamma}_{x,y}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma];\Omega)
for some
\sigma>0
.
By the semicontinuity of
F
and
F<\infty
in
\Omega
, we know that

F
is bounded in the compact set
{\gamma}([0,\sigma])
and hence we know that
A_{\lambda}({\gamma})<\infty
.
But for
x\in\Omega
and
y\notin\Omega
, it is not clear whether
m_{\lambda}(x,y)
is finite or not.
This is indeed determined by the behaviour of
F
around
\Sigma
.

Set

\Omega_{\lambda}:=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}:m_{\lambda}(x,y)<\infty,\ \forall x\in\Omega\}.

Obviously,
\Omega\subset\Omega_{\lambda}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
.
We observe that
\Omega_{\lambda}=\Omega_{\mu}
for all
0<\lambda<\mu<\infty
. To see this, it suffices to show that

m_{\lambda}(x,y)<\infty
if and only if
m_{\mu}(x,y)<\infty
for any
x\in\Omega,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
.

Since
A_{\lambda}({\gamma})\leq A_{\mu}({\gamma})
for all possible
{\gamma}
, by definition, one always has
m_{\lambda}(x,y)\leq m_{\mu}(x,y)
.
On the other hand, if
m_{\lambda}(x,y)<\infty
, we have
A_{\lambda}({\gamma})<\infty
for some
{\gamma}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,t];{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})
. Thus

A_{\mu}({\gamma})\leq A_{\lambda}({\gamma})+(\mu-\lambda)t<\infty,

which implies
m_{\mu}(x,y)<\infty
as desired. Recalling (
1.6
), we write
\widetilde{\Omega}=\Omega_{\lambda}
for any
\lambda>0
.

One may directly check that
m_{\lambda}
is a distance in
\widetilde{\Omega}
, and hence
(\widetilde{\Omega},m_{\lambda})
is a metric space.
Observe that
(\widetilde{\Omega},m_{\lambda})
is always complete.
Indeed, let
\{y_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset\widetilde{\Omega}
be a Cauchy sequence with respect to
m_{\lambda}
. Recall that (
A.1
) gives
|z-w|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}m_{\lambda}(z,w)
for all
z,w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
.
It follows that
\{y_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is also a Cauchy sequence with respect to the Euclidean distance, and hence
|y_{n}-y|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for some
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
.
It then suffices to show that
y\in\widetilde{\Omega}
and
m_{\lambda}(y_{n},y)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
If there exists
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
y_{n}=y
for all
n\geq m
, then
y_{m}\in\widetilde{\Omega}
implies
y\in\widetilde{\Omega}
and
m_{\lambda}(y_{n},y)=0
for all
n\geq m
as desired.
Now we assume that there are infinitely many
k
such that
y_{k}\neq y
. Thanks to this, it is standard to find a subsequence
\{y_{n_{k}}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
\{y_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

such that
0<m_{\lambda}(y_{n_{k}},y_{n_{k+1}})\leq 2^{-k}
. For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
there is
{\gamma}_{k}\subset\mathcal{AC}(y_{n_{k}},y_{n_{k+1}};[0,\sigma_{k}];{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})
such that

A_{\lambda}({\gamma}_{k})\leq 2m_{\lambda}(y_{n_{k}},y_{n_{k+1}})\leq 2^{-k+1}
.
Fix an arbitrary
x\in\Omega
. There is

{\gamma}_{0}\subset\mathcal{AC}(x,y_{n_{1}};[0,\sigma_{0}];{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})
such that

A_{\lambda}({\gamma}_{0})\leq 2m_{\lambda}(x,y_{1})<\infty
.
Noting that
\sigma_{k}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}A_{\lambda}({\gamma}_{k})
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
, it implies that

\sigma:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{k}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A_{\lambda}({\gamma}_{k})\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k+1}<\infty.

Then the concatenation
{\gamma}
of these curves
{\gamma}_{k}
(that is,

{\gamma}=\ast_{k}{\gamma}_{k}
) belongs to
\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma];{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})
and satisfies

A_{\lambda}({\gamma})\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A_{\lambda}({\gamma}_{k})<\infty
. Thus
m_{\lambda}(x,y)<\infty
, that is,
y\in\widetilde{\Omega}
, and moreover

m_{\lambda}(y_{n_{k}},y)\leq\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{\lambda}({\gamma}_{j})\leq\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}2^{-j+2}=2^{-k+3},

which implies
m_{\lambda}(y_{n_{k}},y)\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.
Since
\{y_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a Cauchy sequence with respect to
m_{\lambda}
,
by a standard argument, one further gets
m_{\lambda}(y_{n},y)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
as desired.

Recall that
\Omega\subset\widetilde{\Omega}
, and
\Omega
is an open subset of
\widetilde{\Omega}
with respect to
m_{\lambda}
.
Thus
(\Omega,m_{\lambda})
is also a metric space as a subspace of
(\widetilde{\Omega},m_{\lambda})
.
Since there is no any other assumption made on the behaviour of
F
around
\Sigma
(except the lower semicontinuity),
\Omega
is not necessarily closed
or complete in general. It is interesting to determine the relation between
(\Omega,m_{\lambda})
, or its completion/closure, and
(\widetilde{\Omega},m_{\lambda})
.
In the following remark, we have some discussion about this issue.

Remark A.1
.

(i) It is easy to see that if

F_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\geq C|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-2}
when
|x_{i}-x_{j}|\leq\frac{1}{2}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq N
,

then
\widetilde{\Omega}=\Omega
, and hence,
(\Omega,m_{\lambda})
is complete. One may also check that if

|F_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})|\leq C|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-\alpha}
when
|x_{i}-x_{j}|\leq\frac{1}{2}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq N
and
\alpha\in(0,2)
,

then
\Omega\subsetneqq\widetilde{\Omega}={{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
.
But in general, it is difficult to determine the set
\widetilde{\Omega}
. For example, see
[
11
,
16
,
30
,
22
,
12
,
5
,
8
,
34
]
and references therein.

(ii) If
F=+\infty
in
\Sigma={\mathbb{R}}^{dN}\setminus\Omega
, then
\Omega
is dense in
(\widetilde{\Omega},m_{\lambda})
, equivalently,
the completion of
(\Omega,m_{\lambda})
is
(\widetilde{\Omega},m_{\lambda})
.
Indeed, if
\Omega\subsetneqq\widetilde{\Omega}
, given any
y\in\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\Omega
and

x\in\Omega
, there is
{\gamma}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma];{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})
with
A_{\lambda}({\gamma})<\infty
. Since
F=\infty
in
\Sigma
, we know that

{\gamma}(t)\subset\Omega
for almost all
t\in[0,\sigma]
. Therefore there exists a sequence
\{t_{n}\}\subset[0,\sigma]
so that
t_{n}\to\sigma
with
{\gamma}(t_{n})\in\Omega
, and
A_{\lambda}({\gamma}|_{[t_{n},\sigma]})\to 0
, which implies
m_{\lambda}({\gamma}(t_{n}),y)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, that is,
y
is the limit of the Cauchy sequence
\{{\gamma}(t_{n})\}\subset\Omega
with respect to
m_{\lambda}
.
This shows the closure
\overline{\Omega}^{m_{\lambda}}
of
\Omega
with respect to
m_{\lambda}
contains
\widetilde{\Omega}
. On the other hand, since
\Omega\subset\widetilde{\Omega}
and
\widetilde{\Omega}
is complete with respect to
m_{\lambda}
, it follows that
\overline{\Omega}^{m_{\lambda}}\subset\widetilde{\Omega}
. We conclude that

\overline{\Omega}^{m_{\lambda}}=\widetilde{\Omega}
.

(iii) Without the assumption
F=+\infty
in
\Sigma
, in general
one can not expect that
\Omega
is dense in
(\widetilde{\Omega},m_{\lambda})
, equivalently, the completion of
(\Omega,m_{\lambda})
is
(\widetilde{\Omega},m_{\lambda})
.
Indeed, we construct a potential
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}\to(0,\infty)

so that
\Omega
is not dense in
(\widetilde{\Omega},m_{\lambda})
; see the following 3 steps.

Step 1.
Constructions of
h_{\pm}:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
by modifying
r^{-2}
.

Let
h_{\pm}:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be two functions defined by

h_{+}(r)=\left\{\begin{split}1\ \quad\quad&\mbox{when}\ 0\leq r\leq 1;\\
\frac{1}{r^{2}}\quad\quad&\mbox{when}\ 1<r<\infty\end{split}\right.

and

h_{-}(r)=\left\{\begin{split}&1\quad&\mbox{when}\ r\in\Lambda:=\{0\}\cup(\cup_{k\geq 3}[2^{-k^{2}-1},2^{-k^{2}+1}]);\\
&\frac{1}{r^{2}}\quad&\mbox{ when }\ r\in(0,\infty)\setminus\Lambda.\end{split}\right.

Obviously,
h_{+}\in C^{0}([0,\infty))
, and

h_{-}
is lower semicontinuous in
[0,\infty)
with

\displaystyle h_{-}(r)=\liminf_{t\to r}h_{-}(t)\quad\forall\ 0\leq r<\infty.

moreover,
h_{+}(r)\leq h_{-}(r)\leq r^{-2}
for all
0<r<\infty
, and

h_{+}=h_{-}
in
\Lambda\cup[1,+\infty)
.

Step 2.
Construction of a potential
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}\to(0,\infty)
via
h_{\pm}
.

For any nonempty subset
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, recall the standard characteristic function
\chi_{E}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}\to\{0,1\}
, which maps the elements of
E
to
1
, and all other elements to
0
. Define

F(x):=F_{12}(x_{1},x_{2})\mbox{ for all $x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}$}

with

F_{12}(x_{1},x_{2}):=h_{-}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\chi_{\Gamma\times\Gamma}+h_{+}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\chi_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus[\Gamma\times\Gamma]},

where

\mbox{$x_{i}=(x_{i}^{1},\cdots,x_{i}^{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$ and }\Gamma:=\{x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:x_{i}^{j}<0,\ \forall 1\leq j\leq d\}.

Obviously,
F_{12}
is locally bounded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:x_{1}=x_{2},\,x_{1}\in\overline{\Gamma}\},

and
F_{12}(x_{1},x_{2})=1
whenever
|x_{1}-x_{2}|\in\Lambda
. Moreover,
F_{12}
is lower semicontinuous in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with

F_{12}(x_{1},x_{2})=\liminf_{(z_{1},z_{2})\to(x_{1},x_{2})}F_{12}(z_{1},z_{2})\mbox{ for all
$(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}$}.

In particular,
F
satisfies the condition (
1.4
).

Note that

\limsup_{(z_{1},z_{2})\to(x_{1},x_{2})}F_{12}(z_{1},z_{2})=+\infty\quad\mbox{whenever $x_{1}=x_{2}$ and $x_{1}\in\Gamma$.}

Below we write

\Sigma^{-}_{12}:=\{x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}:x_{1}=x_{2}\in\Gamma\}\subset\Gamma\times\Gamma\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d(N-2)}

and

\Sigma_{12}:=\{x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}:x_{1}=x_{2}\}.

Notice that the local boundedness of
F_{12}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:x_{1}=x_{2},\,x_{1}\in\overline{\Gamma}\}
implies the local boundedness of
F
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}\setminus\overline{\Sigma_{12}^{-}}
. In particular,
F
is locally bounded in
\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{12}
.

Step 3.
We show that

\widetilde{\Omega}={\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
and
\Sigma_{12}^{-}\subset\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\overline{\Omega}^{m_{\lambda}}
.

We first show that
\widetilde{\Omega}={\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
. It suffices to show that
\Sigma\subset\widetilde{\Omega}
.
We consider three cases:
x\in\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{12}
,
x\in\Sigma_{12}\setminus\overline{\Sigma_{12}^{-}}

and
x\in\overline{\Sigma_{12}^{-}}
.

Case
x\in\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{12}
.
By Step 2, we know that
F
is locally bounded at
x
. Since
{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}\setminus\Sigma_{12}
is a connected open subset, there exists an Euclidean ball
B(x,r_{x})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}\setminus\Sigma_{12}
for some
r_{x}>0
such that
F|_{B(x,r_{x})}\leq M_{x}
for some
M_{x}>0
.
Noting that
\Sigma={\mathbb{R}}^{dN}\setminus\Omega
is closed and does not have interior points, we can find a curve
{\gamma}_{x}\in C^{1}([0,t],B(x,r_{x}))
with end point
x
such that
{\gamma}_{x}\setminus\{x\}\subset\Omega
and
F|_{{\gamma}_{x}}\leq M_{x}
. Therefore, we see that

m_{\lambda}({\gamma}_{x}(0),x)\leq A_{\lambda}({\gamma}_{x})<\infty
, that is,
x\in\widetilde{\Omega}
.

Case
x\in\Sigma_{12}\setminus\overline{\Sigma_{12}^{-}}
.
By the definition of
F
, we know
F(x)=1
. Note that
{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}\setminus\overline{\Sigma_{12}^{-}}
is a connected open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
. Similar to the first case, there is an Euclidean ball
B(x,r_{x})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}\setminus\overline{\Sigma_{12}^{-}}
for some
r_{x}>0
such that
F|_{B(x,r_{x})}\leq 2
, and moreover,
we can find a curve
{\gamma}_{x}\in C^{1}([0,t],B(x,r_{x}))
with end point
x
such that
{\gamma}_{x}\setminus\{x\}\subset\Omega
and
F|_{{\gamma}_{x}}\leq 2
. Therefore, we
see that

m_{\lambda}({\gamma}_{x}(0),x)\leq A_{\lambda}({\gamma}_{x})<\infty
, that is,
x\in\widetilde{\Omega}
.

Case
x\in\overline{\Sigma_{12}^{-}}
.
Letting
y=(y_{i})_{1}^{N}\in\Sigma_{12}\setminus\overline{\Sigma_{12}^{-}}
such that
y_{1}=y_{2}=(1,\cdots,1)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
y_{i}=x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for
i=3,\cdots,N
, taking
{\gamma}
as the line-segment joining
y
and
x
, then
{\gamma}\subset\Sigma_{12}
and hence

F|_{{\gamma}}\equiv 1
.
We have
m_{\lambda}(x,y)<A_{\lambda}({\gamma})<\infty
. Since
y\in\Sigma_{12}\setminus\overline{\Sigma_{12}^{-}}\subset\widetilde{\Omega}
, there exists some
x_{0}\in\Omega
, such that
m_{\lambda}(x,x_{0})\leq m_{\lambda}(x,y)+m_{\lambda}(y,x_{0})<\infty
. Hence,
x\in\widetilde{\Omega}.

Finally, we show that

\Sigma_{12}^{-}\subset\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\overline{\Omega}^{m_{\lambda}}
.
Let
x\in\Sigma_{12}^{-}
be an arbitrary point.
It then suffices to show that

\inf_{z\in\Omega}m_{\lambda}(z,x)>0.

(A.2)

To prove this, we need some properties of
m_{\lambda}
-geodesics joining
z,x
which will be introduced/proved below.
So we postpone the proof of (
A.2
) to the end of this appendix.

Moreover, we know that
(\widetilde{\Omega},m_{\lambda})
is a geodesic space, that is, for any

x,y\in\widetilde{\Omega}
with
x\neq y
, there exists a

\eta\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,m_{\lambda}(x,y)];{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})
such that

\eta([0,m_{\lambda}(x,y)])\subset\widetilde{\Omega}
,
and

m_{\lambda}(\eta(s),\eta(t))=t-s
for all
0\leq s<t\leq m_{\lambda}(x,y)
.

Indeed, thanks to Lemma
A.2
below, the desired geodesic
{\gamma}
comes from the arc length (with respect to
m_{\lambda}
) parametrisation of the following minimizer of

A_{\lambda}
in the class of
\cup_{\sigma>0}\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma];\widetilde{\Omega})
.

Lemma A.2
.

Given
\lambda>0
and
x,y\in\widetilde{\Omega}
with
x\neq y
,
there is a curve
{\gamma}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma];\widetilde{\Omega})
such that
A_{\lambda}({\gamma})=m_{\lambda}(x,y)
.
Moreover, for any
{\gamma}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma];\widetilde{\Omega})
satisfying
A_{\lambda}({\gamma})=m_{\lambda}(x,y)
, we have

m_{\lambda}({\gamma}(s),{\gamma}(t))=A_{\lambda}({\gamma}|_{[s,t]}),\quad\forall 0\leq s<t\leq\sigma.

The proof of Lemma A.2 is standard, for readers’ convenience we give it later.

Considering the geodesic nature, as in the introduction we call a minimizer
{\gamma}:[0,\sigma]\to\widetilde{\Omega}
of
A_{\lambda}
with given endpoints
x,y
as an
m_{\lambda}
-geodesic with the canonical parameter
joining
x,y
.
We also call a ray
{\gamma}:[0,\infty)\to\widetilde{\Omega}
as an
m_{\lambda}
-geodesic ray with the canonical parameter
if

{\gamma}|_{[0,\sigma]}
is an
m_{\lambda}
-geodesic with the canonical parameter for any
\sigma>0
.

Next we prove the following result.

Lemma A.3
.

Given any
x,y\in\widetilde{\Omega}
and
\lambda>0
, let

{\gamma}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma],{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})
be any

m_{\lambda}
-geodesic with canonical parameter. Then
{\gamma}
has energy constant
\lambda
, that is,

|\dot{\gamma}(s)|=\sqrt{2(F({\gamma}(s))+\lambda)}\mbox{ almost all $s\in[0,\sigma]$. }

Moreover,

m_{\lambda}(x,y)=\widetilde{m}_{\lambda}(x,y)=\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}({\gamma}),

where

\widetilde{m}_{\lambda}(x,y):=\inf\{\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(\eta):\eta\in\cup_{\tau>0}\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\tau],{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})\}\quad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dN},

and

\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(\eta):=\int_{0}^{\tau}|\dot{\eta}(s)|\sqrt{2F\circ\eta+2\lambda}\,ds.

Here in the above inequality, we use the convention that
|\dot{\eta}(s)|\sqrt{2F\circ\eta+2\lambda}=0
when
|\dot{\eta}(s)|=0
and
F\circ\eta(s)=\infty
.

Consequently, we have the following.

Lemma A.4
.

Under additional assumption
F\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, for any
\lambda>0
,
if

{\gamma}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma],{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})
is an

m_{\lambda}
-geodesic with canonical parameter joining
x,y
and also is interiorly collision-free (that is,

{\gamma}|_{(0,\sigma)}\subset\Omega
), then
{\gamma}
is a
solution to
\ddot{x}=\nabla F
in
(0,\sigma)
starting from
x
and ending at
y
.

Consequently, if

{\gamma}:[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}
is an
m_{\lambda}
-geodesic ray with canonical parameter starting from
x
and also is interiorly collision-free (that is,

{\gamma}|_{(0,\infty)}\subset\Omega
), then
{\gamma}
is a
solution to
\ddot{x}=\nabla F
in
(0,\infty)
starting from
x
.

Below we prove Lemma A.2-Lemma A.4.

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.

Let
x,y\in\widetilde{\Omega}
be two given configurations, with

x\neq y
. Since
0<m_{\lambda}(x,y)<\infty
, there exist a sequence of curves
\{\delta_{n}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma_{n}];{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{\lambda}(\delta_{n})=m_{\lambda}(x,y).

Thus for
n
large enough we have
A_{\lambda}(\delta_{n})\leq m_{\lambda}(x,y)+1
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that for all
n
, this holds.
This also implies that
\delta_{n}\in\mathcal{AC}(x,y;[0,\sigma_{n}];\widetilde{\Omega})
; otherwise
\delta_{n}(t)\notin\widetilde{\Omega}
for some
0<t<\sigma_{n}
and hence

m_{\lambda}(x,\delta_{n}(t))<A_{\lambda}(\delta_{n}|_{[0,t]})<A_{\lambda}(\delta_{n})<\infty,

which is a contradiction with the definition of
\widetilde{\Omega}
.

Next, for every
n
, since

\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n}}|x-y|^{2}+\lambda\sigma_{n}\leq A_{\lambda}(\delta_{n})\leq 1+m_{\lambda}(x,y),

we know

0<\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{n}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{n}<+\infty.

and therefore up to considering subsequence, we
may assume that
\sigma_{n}\to\sigma_{0}
as
n\to\infty
.

Now, for each
n>0
, we parameterize
\delta_{n}
linearly as below.
Define
{\gamma}^{(n)}(t)=\delta_{n}(\sigma_{n}\sigma_{0}^{-1}t)
for
t\in[0,\sigma_{0}]
.
A direct calculation leads to

\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{\lambda}({\gamma}^{(n)})=\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{\lambda}(\delta_{n})=m_{\lambda}(x,y).

We may also assume that

A_{\lambda}({\gamma}^{(n)})\leq m_{\lambda}(x,y)+1,\quad\forall n\geq 1.

It is easy to see that
\{{\gamma}^{(n)}\}
is uniform bounded and equicontinuous. Indeed,
for any
s,t\in[0,\sigma_{0}]
with
s<t
, one has

|{\gamma}^{(n)}(s)-{\gamma}^{(n)}(t)|\leq\int_{s}^{t}|\dot{\gamma}^{(n)}(\tau)|\,d\tau\leq(\int_{0}^{\sigma_{0}}|\dot{\gamma}^{(n)}(\tau)|^{2}\,d\tau)^{1/2}|s-t|^{1/2}\leq[2(m_{\lambda}(x,y)+1)]^{1/2}|s-t|^{1/2}.

In particular,

|{\gamma}^{(n)}(0)-{\gamma}^{(n)}(t)|\leq[2(m_{\lambda}(x,y)+1)]^{1/2}|t|^{1/2}\leq[2(m_{\lambda}(x,y)+1)]^{1/2}|\sigma_{0}|^{1/2}.

By Arzela-Ascoli Theorem, up to some subsequence, we may assume
that the sequence
\{{\gamma}^{(n)}\}
converges uniformly to a curve
{\gamma}\in C^{0}([0,\sigma_{0}];{\mathbb{R}}^{dN})
with
{\gamma}(0)=x,{\gamma}(\sigma_{0})=y
.
Note that
{\gamma}
is absolutely continuous. Indeed, for any
\epsilon>0
and for any family
\{[s_{i},t_{i}]\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
of mutually disjoint intervals such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}|s_{i}-t_{i}|<\epsilon
, applying
the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}|{\gamma}^{(n)}(s_{i})-{\gamma}^{(n)}(t_{i})|

\displaystyle<2^{1/2}[m_{\lambda}(x,y)+1]^{1/2}\epsilon^{1/2}.

Thus

.
Apply Tonelli’s Theorem for convex Lagrangians to get

Therefore
A_{\lambda}({\gamma})\leq m_{\lambda}(x,y)
, which is only possible if the equality holds.
Note that

also implies that

.

Next for any

and for any

\displaystyle<A_{\lambda}({\gamma}|_{[0,s]})+A_{\lambda}({\gamma}|_{[s,t]})+A_{\lambda}({\gamma}|_{[t,\sigma]})

Then
F\circ{\gamma}=h_{-}\circ v.

Moreover, note that
v\in C^{0}([0,\sigma])
. By
x_{1}=x_{2}
,

v(\sigma)=|{\gamma}_{1}(\sigma)-{\gamma}_{2}(\sigma)|=|x_{1}-x_{2}|=0;

while by
z\in\Omega
, we have
z_{1}\neq z_{2}

and hence

v(0)=|{\gamma}_{1}(0)-{\gamma}_{2}(0)|=|z_{1}-z_{2}|>0.

Set

\kappa:=\min\{s\in[0,\sigma]\ |\ v(s)=0\}.

Then
0<\kappa\leq\sigma
. Below we will prove that

\int_{0}^{\kappa}|\dot{\gamma}|\sqrt{h_{-}\circ v}\,ds=\infty.

(A.10)

Before we give the proof of (
A.10
), we show that
(
A.10
) leads to a contradiction. Indeed,
Lemma A.2 and Lemma A.3 yield

m_{\lambda}(z,x)\geq m_{\lambda}(z,{\gamma}(\kappa))\geq\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}({\gamma}|_{[0,\kappa]}).

Write

\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}({\gamma}|_{[0,\kappa]})=\int_{0}^{\kappa}|\dot{\gamma}|\sqrt{2F\circ{\gamma}+2\lambda}\,ds=\int_{0}^{\kappa}|\dot{\gamma}|\sqrt{2h_{-}\circ v+2\lambda}\,ds\geq\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{\kappa}|\dot{\gamma}|\sqrt{h_{-}\circ v}\,ds.

We see that
(
A.10
) implies
\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}({\gamma}|_{[0,\kappa]})=\infty
and hence

m_{\lambda}(z,x)=\infty
. However, since

z\in\Omega
, we have
m_{\lambda}(z,x)<\infty
.
This is a contradiction.
Therefore, we conclude that the hypothesis
(
A.9
) is not correct, and hence, the claim (
A.8
) must hold as desired.

Finally, we prove
(
A.10
) via the following 4 substeps.

Substep 1.

From the definition of
\kappa
it follows that

v(\kappa)=0
,
v(t)>0
for
t\in[0,\kappa)
,
{\gamma}(\kappa)\in\Sigma^{-}_{12}
and

{\gamma}|_{[0,\kappa)}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{dN}\setminus\Sigma^{-}_{12}
.

Set

\tau=\sup_{s\in[0,\kappa]}v(s).

The choice of
\kappa
and the continuity of
v
give that
v([0,\kappa])=[0,\tau]
.
Moreover, we observe that
\tau\leq c_{\ast}(x)\leq 1
. Indeed,
by
x_{1}=x_{2}
and the triangle inequality, for any
s\in[0,\kappa]
we have

v(s)=|{\gamma}_{1}(s)-{\gamma}_{2}(s)|\leq|{\gamma}_{1}(s)-x_{1}(s)|+|x_{2}(s)-{\gamma}_{2}(s)|\leq 2|{\gamma}-x|

and hence, by the hypothesis
(
A.9
),
v(s)\leq c_{\ast}(x)
.

For any
s\in[0,\kappa]
, recall that

h_{-}\circ v(s)=\left\{\begin{split}&1\quad&\mbox{if }v(s)\in\Lambda=\{0\}\cup(\cup_{k\geq 3}[2^{-k^{2}-1},2^{-k^{2}+1}]);\\
&\frac{1}{v(s)^{2}}\ &\mbox{if}\quad v(s)\in[0,\tau]\setminus\Lambda.\end{split}\right.

Wrtie
E:=\{s\in[0,\kappa]|v(s)\in[0,\tau]\setminus\Lambda\}
.Then

h_{-}(v(s))=\frac{1}{v(s)^{2}}\quad\mbox{ for $s\in E$}

and

\chi_{E}(s)=\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus\Lambda}(v(s))=\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus\Lambda}\circ v(s)\quad\forall s\in[0,\kappa].

Thus

\int_{0}^{\kappa}|\dot{\gamma}|\sqrt{h_{-}\circ v}\,ds\geq\int_{0}^{\kappa}\chi_{E}(s)|\dot{\gamma}(s)|\frac{1}{v(s)}\,ds=\int_{0}^{\kappa}\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus\Lambda}\circ v(s)|\dot{\gamma}(s)|\frac{1}{v(s)}\,ds.

(A.11)

Substep 2.
Since both of
{\gamma}_{1}
and
{\gamma}_{2}
are absolutely continuous in
[0,\kappa]
, we know that
v
is also absolutely continuous in
[0,\kappa]
, and hence
\dot{v}\in L^{1}([0,\kappa])
.
One further has

|\dot{v}(t)|=\frac{|\langle(\dot{\gamma}_{1}-\dot{\gamma}_{2})(t),{\gamma}_{1}(t)-{\gamma}_{2}(t)\rangle|}{v(t)}\leq|(\dot{\gamma}_{1}-\dot{\gamma}_{2})(t)|\leq 2|\dot{\gamma}(t)|\quad\mbox{ for almost all $t\in[0,\kappa]$.}

(A.12)

Applying this in (
A.11
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{\kappa}|\dot{\gamma}|\sqrt{h_{-}\circ v}\,ds\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\kappa}\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus\Lambda}\circ v(s)|\dot{v}(s)|\frac{1}{v(s)}\,ds.

(A.13)

Substep 3.
We claim that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\kappa}\frac{1}{v}|\dot{v}|\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus\Lambda}\circ v\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{[0,\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\frac{1}{t}\,dt,

(A.14)

whose proof will be given in Substep 4.
Assume that the claim (
A.14
) holds for the moment. By (
A.13
)
and the claim (
A.14
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{\kappa}|\dot{\gamma}|\sqrt{h_{-}\circ v}\,ds>\int_{[0,\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\frac{1}{t}\,dt.

Let
k_{\tau}\geq 3
such that
2^{-k_{\tau}^{2}+1}\leq\tau
. Then

\int_{[0,\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\frac{1}{t}\,dt\geq\sum_{k\geq k_{\tau}}\int_{2^{-k^{2}+1}}^{2^{-(k-1)^{2}-1}}\frac{1}{t}\,dt=\sum_{k\geq k_{\tau}}\ln\frac{2^{-(k-1)^{2}-1}}{2^{-k^{2}+1}}\geq\sum_{k\geq k_{\tau}}\ln 2^{2k-3}\geq(\ln 2)\sum_{k\geq k_{\tau}}k=\infty.

Thus

\int_{0}^{\kappa}|\dot{\gamma}|\sqrt{h_{-}\circ v}\,ds=\infty,

which gives (
A.10
).

Substep 4.
We prove the claim (
A.14
) via Lemma
A.6
(i).
Since
v
is only defined in
[0,\sigma]
and also not necessarily Lipschitz in
[0,\kappa]
, we can not use
Lemma
A.6
(i) directly.

To overcome this difficulty, we show that the restriction of
v
in subintervals
[0,{\kappa}-\epsilon]
is Lipschitz for all sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, and extend them to
{\mathbb{R}}
via the McShane’s extension.
To be precise, by Lemma A.3 and
F\circ{\gamma}=h_{-}\circ v
, one has

|\dot{\gamma}|=\sqrt{2(F\circ{\gamma}+\lambda)}=\sqrt{2(h_{-}\circ v+\lambda)}\quad\text{ almost everywhere in }[0,\kappa].

Recall that
0<v\leq 1
in
[0,\kappa)
,
\lambda>0
, and
h_{-}(t)\leq\frac{1}{t^{2}}
by definition, we have

h_{-}\circ v+\lambda\leq(1+\lambda)\frac{1}{v^{2}}\quad\mbox{ in $[0,\kappa]$.}

Thus, by (
A.12
),

|\dot{v}|\leq 2|\dot{\gamma}|\leq 2\sqrt{2(1+\lambda)}\frac{1}{v}\quad\mbox{ almost everywhere in $[0,\kappa]$.}

For
0<\epsilon<{\kappa}
, set

\delta_{\epsilon}:=\min\{v(t),t\in[0,{\kappa}-\epsilon]\}.

Since
v
is continuous in
[0,{\kappa}]
and
v>0
in
[0,\kappa)
,
we know that
\delta_{\epsilon}>0
. Thus

|\dot{v}|\leq 2\sqrt{2(1+\lambda)}\frac{1}{\delta_{\epsilon}}\mbox{\ almost everywhere in $[0,\kappa-\epsilon]$.}

From this and the absolute continuity of
v
in
[0,\kappa]
,
we know that
v
is Lipschitz in
[0,\kappa-\epsilon]
with

|v(s)-v(t)|=|\int_{s}^{t}\dot{v}(\xi)\,d\xi|\leq 2\sqrt{2(1+\lambda)}\frac{1}{\delta_{\epsilon}}|s-t|,\quad\forall s,t\in[0,\kappa-\epsilon].

Denote by
\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}
the McShane’s extension of
v|_{[0,\kappa-\epsilon]}
into
{\mathbb{R}}
, that is,

\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(t)=\inf\left\{v(s)+2\sqrt{2(1+\lambda)}\frac{1}{\delta_{\epsilon}}|t-s|\ \bigg|\ s\in[0,\kappa-\epsilon]\right\},\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(A.15)

Then

|\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(s)-\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(t)|\leq 2\sqrt{2(1+\lambda)}\frac{1}{\delta_{\epsilon}}|s-t|,\quad\forall s,t\in{\mathbb{R}},

that is,
\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}
is Lipschitz in
{\mathbb{R}}
, and moreover,

\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}|_{[0,\kappa-\epsilon]}=v|_{[0,\kappa-\epsilon]}
;
see for example [23,Chapter 6].

Moreover,
write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\kappa}\frac{1}{v}|\dot{v}|\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus\Lambda}\circ v\,dt=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{{\kappa}-\epsilon}\frac{1}{v}|\dot{v}|\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ v\,ds.

Since
v
is continuous in
[0,{\kappa}]
,
v>0
in
[0,\sigma)
and
\varphi({\kappa})=0
,
we know that
\delta_{\epsilon}\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
.
When
\epsilon>0
is sufficiently small, one also has

v([0,\kappa-\epsilon])=[\delta_{\epsilon},\tau]
, that is,

\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ v(s)=\chi_{[\delta_{\epsilon},\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ v(s)\quad\quad\mbox{ for any }s\in[0,{\kappa}-\epsilon].

Therefore, for all sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, one has

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{{\kappa}-\epsilon}\frac{1}{v}|\dot{v}|\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ v\,ds=\int_{0}^{{\kappa}-\epsilon}\frac{1}{v}|\dot{v}|\chi_{[\delta_{\epsilon},\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ v\,ds.

Denote by
\widetilde{v}^{\epsilon}
the McShane’s extension of
v|_{[0,\kappa-\epsilon]}
into
{\mathbb{R}}
as in
(
A.15
).
Since
\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}|_{[0,\kappa-\epsilon]}=v|_{[0,\kappa-\epsilon]}
,
it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{{\kappa}-\epsilon}\frac{1}{v}|\dot{v}|\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ v\,ds=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{[0,\kappa-\epsilon]}(s)\chi_{[\delta_{\epsilon},\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(s)\frac{1}{\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(s)}|(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}})^{\prime}(s)|\,ds.

Since
\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}
is Lipschitz in
{\mathbb{R}}
and

\chi_{[0,\kappa-\epsilon]}\frac{1}{\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}}\chi_{[\delta_{\epsilon},\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
,
we are able to apply the change of variable formula in Lemma
A.6
(i) with
f=\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}
and
g=\chi_{[0,\kappa-\epsilon]}\frac{1}{\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}}\chi_{[\delta_{\epsilon},\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}
therein to get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{{\kappa}-\epsilon}\frac{1}{v}|\dot{v}|\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ v\,ds=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\sum_{s\in(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}})^{-1}(\{t\})}\left[\chi_{[0,\kappa-\epsilon]}(s)\chi_{[\delta_{\epsilon},\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(s)\frac{1}{\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(s)}\right]\,dt.

(A.16)

For any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
,
since
v([0,\kappa-\epsilon])=[\delta_{\epsilon},\tau]
, given any
t\in[\delta_{\epsilon},\tau]\setminus\Lambda
, one can find at least one
s\in[0,\kappa-\epsilon]

such that
\widetilde{v}^{\epsilon}(s)=v(s)=t
, and hence,

\sum_{s\in(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}})^{-1}(\{t\})}\left[\chi_{[0,\kappa-\epsilon]}(s)\chi_{[\delta_{\epsilon},\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(s)\frac{1}{\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(s)}\right]\geq\frac{1}{t}.

From this and (
A.16
) one deduces that

\int_{0}^{{\kappa}-\epsilon}\frac{1}{v}|\dot{v}|\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\circ v\,ds\geq\int_{[\delta_{\epsilon},\tau]\setminus\Lambda}\frac{1}{t}\,dt.

Sending

\epsilon\to 0
, by
\delta_{\epsilon}\to 0
one gets

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\kappa}\frac{1}{v}|\dot{v}|\chi_{[0,\tau]\setminus\Lambda}\circ v\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{[0,\tau]\setminus{\Lambda}}\frac{1}{t}\,dt,

which gives the claim (
A.14
)
as desired. The proof is complete.
