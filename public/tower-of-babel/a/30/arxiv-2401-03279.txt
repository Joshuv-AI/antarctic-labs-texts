Entropy and forecasting complexity of hidden Markov models, matrix product states, and observable operator models
Peter Grassberger

Abstract
In a series of three papers, Jurgens and Crutchfield recently proposed a supposedly novel method to compute entropies of hidden Markov models (HMMs), discussed in detail its relationship to iterated function systems, and applied it to compute their “ambiguity rates”, a concept supposedly introduced by Claude Shannon. We point out that the basic formalism is not new (it is the well known “forward algorithm” for HMMs), and that all three papers have also serious other faults.



Hidden Markov models (HMMs)
Bengio
;
Ephraim
;
Zucchini
are a standard tool in data analysis, with
a vast number of applications covering among others finance, medicine, chemistry, bioinformatics, speech
analysis, meteorology, and physics. It is repeatedly claimed in the literature that no good algorithms
exit for estimating their (Shannon) entropy
Jacquet
;
Ordentlich
, but this is not true: both the
“forward” and the Baum-Welch algorithms
Zucchini
do just this. It is true that in their treatments
entropy is often not mentioned, but likelihoods are, and entropy is just the average negative log-likelihood.

In
Jurgens1
, a supposedly new algorithm based on “mixed states” was proposed. But this
algorithm is exactly the forward algorithm, and the “mixed states” become precisely the causal states
introduced in
Grass86
, when their set is minimized. This minimization is, in general, anyhow necessary
for an efficient application. Indeed, the forward algorithm was independently (re-)discovered for a special
class of HMMs in
Zambella
, where it was used to present precise entropy estimates for a number
of non-trivial models,
and where it was also used to compute forecasting complexities (FCs
Grass86
; later
called ‘stochastic complexities’ in
Young
). The fact that the algorithm proposed in
Zambella

is much more general was stressed in
arXiv
, where also a number of other points were clarified.

More precisely, we consider a finite stationary ergodic irreducible first order Markov process
M
. Here,
“finite” means that it has a finite number
N
of states, in the following called
s_{t}
with values
numbered simply by
``i",\;i=1\ldots N
. The restriction to first order is trivial, as any higher order
Markov chain can be re-written as a first order process by re-labelling the states. Irreducibility is
essential since it implies (by the Frobenius-Perron-theorem) that there is a unique stationary
state. The probability to be in state
i
at (discrete) time
t
is denoted as
P_{i,t}
. The
stationary state is characterized by the probabilities
P_{i,\infty}
. The transition matrix is

T_{ji}={\rm prob}\{s_{t+1}=j|s_{t}=i\}
. The time evolution of the probabilities is

P_{j,t+1}=\sum_{i}T_{ji}P_{i,t}.

(1)

In a more compact vector notation, we denote the state at time
t
by a vector
{\bf P}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
so that the time evolution can be written as
{\bf P}_{t+1}={\bf T}{\bf P}_{t}
.

This Markov process is “hidden”, i.e. its states cannot be observed. What is observed instead
is the chain of discrete symbols
x_{t}
which are emitted one-by-one during each transition.
These emitted symbols are chosen from an alphabet
A
(with cardinality
|A|
), and

T^{(x)}_{ji}\equiv({\bf T}^{(x)})_{ji}={\rm prob}\{s_{t+1}=j,x_{t}=x|s_{t}=i\}.

(2)

For consistency, we have

{\bf T}=\sum_{x\in A}{\bf T}^{(x)}.

(3)

Notice that we consider thus “edge-emitting” HMMs. The more widely discussed “node-emitting” HMMs

Zucchini

are obtained simply by assuming that the emitted symbol does depend only on state
i
and not on
j
,
so that
T^{(x)}_{ji}=T_{ji}q(x|i)
.

We now assume that the process has been going on for a long time, but no emitted symbols have been
observed for any time
t\leq 0
, so that
{\bf P}_{0}={\bf P}_{\infty}
. Thus the observer/forecaster
has at time
t=1
no clue about the internal state of
M
, and cannot make any non-trivial
forecast beyond that based on stationarity. This changes, however, when observations are made
at times
t=1,2,\ldots
. From these, the observer can make inferences about the internal states,
which will then lead to improved forecasts. Comparing these forecasts with the actually
emitted symbols will lead to improved inferences, and so on. In general, this
‘observation
process’
is
not
stationary (even if the HMM is). It will in the limit
t\to\infty

lead to a situation where the non-predicted amount of information per symbol is a.s. the entropy.

The optimally inferred states of the hidden dynamics based on the observation process were called
‘mixed states’ in
Jurgens1
;
Jurgens2
;
Jurgens3
, and will be denoted here as

\hat{\bf P}_{t}(x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t})
. Based on them, the optimal forecast of the observed process is

\hat{p}_{t+1}(x|x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t})=\hat{p}_{t+1}(x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t},x)/\hat{p}_{t}(x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t})

with

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{t+1}(x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t},x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}T^{(x)}_{ji}\hat{P}_{i,t}(x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t})

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf 1}\cdot{\bf T}^{(x)}\hat{\bf P}_{t}(x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t})

with
{\bf 1}=(1\ldots 1)^{T}
. On the other hand, an unbiased hidden state inferred from a new
observation
x_{t+1}
is

\hat{\bf P}_{t+1}(x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t+1})=c\cdot{\bf T}^{(x_{t+1})}\hat{\bf P}_{t}(x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t}),

(5)

where the constant
c
is determined by the normalization

{\bf 1}\cdot\hat{\bf P}_{t+1}(x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t+1})=1
, which gives finally

\hat{\bf P}_{t+1}(x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t+1})=\frac{{\bf T}^{(x_{t+1})}\hat{\bf P}_{t}(x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t})}{\hat{p}_{t+1}(x_{t+1}|x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t})}.

(6)

The complete observation / forecasting process is thus a cyclic chain between inferences,
forecasts, and updates due to observations.

Equations (4) and (6) are essentially the same as Eq.(3.4) in
Zambella
, and are today well
known as ‘forward algorithm’
Zucchini
. They hold also for ‘observable operator models’ (OOMs)

Jaeger
;
Spanczer
(in which the positivity requirements made for HMMs
are dropped), and even for ‘matrix product states’ in statistical physics
Derrida
;
Klumper
)
with discrete time and discrete values.

While the Baum-Welch algorithm is often considered as superior to the forward algorithm, it is
shown in
Zucchini
that both have their advantages. Indeed, the entropy estimates of simple
but non-trivial models made in
Zambella
were of unprecedented accuracy, and no brute-force
estimates would have been possible at that time with the same accuracy. Using the estimator of

arXiv2003
, we now checked that all entropies quoted in Table 1 of
Zambella

agreed perfectly with brute-force estimations, with one single exception: For rules 25 and 61,
the correct value of the entropy is 0.88955 bits, while 0.88947 bits was quoted in
Zambella
.

As shown in
Zambella
(see also
arXiv
), the set of ‘mixed states’ becomes precisely the
set of causal states introduced in
Grass86
when minimized. Minimization means that two
vectors
\hat{\bf P}_{t}(x_{1}\ldots x_{t})
and
\hat{\bf P}_{t}(x^{\prime}_{1}\ldots x^{\prime}_{t})
are identified if they
lead, via Eq.(4), to the same
\hat{p}_{t+k}(x)\;\forall k\geq 1
. No such minimization was done in

Jurgens1
, which is why no FCs were estimated there and no precise estimates of entropy.
It was also shown in
Zambella
that causal states do in general
not
correspond
to elements of a partitioning of phase space (in contrast to what was claimed in
Young
), at
least when transient parts of the “
\epsilon
-machine” are not cut off. Cutting them off (as
proposed in most recent papers by Crutchfield
et al.
) is not a viable alternative,
as in some cases (called ‘
totally recurrent graphs’
in
arXiv
) none of
the nodes of the “
\epsilon
-machine” correspond to elements of a phase space partitioning

Zambella
;
arXiv
. An example of the latter is e.g. ‘rule 22’
Zambella
;
arXiv
.

A main point in
Jurgens1
;
Jurgens2
was that no such minimization can in general be expected
(and FCs are infinite), if the HMM depends on irrational real valued parameters, such that all
different observed sequences lead to different optimal forecasts. It was already pointed out in

Grass-Helv
;
Grass-Nat
that even rather simple systems can lead to infinite FC.
On the other hand, symmetries of the
{\bf T}^{(x)}
can lead to non-trivial minimizations,
even if the
{\bf T}^{(x)}
depend on irrational parameters (an instance where
some

optimal forecasts do not depend on the history at all, but FC is infinite nevertheless, will be
be discussed below).
No entropy estimates with precision close to these in
Zambella
were made in
Jurgens1
,
and no estimates of FCs at all. The main reason is that no minimizations of the
set of ‘mixed states’ were performed.

It is claimed in
Jurgens1
;
Jurgens2
;
Jurgens3
that the set of mixed states is in general not
countable, if irrational parameters are involved, but this is wrong. It is always countable by
construction. What is correct, however, is that the
closure
of the
set of mixed states is in such cases uncountable, just as the triadic Cantor set is countable, while
its closure is not (a non-countable set could be obtained
by using any arbitrary starting vector
{\bf P}_{0}
instead of
{\bf P}_{0}={\bf P}_{\infty}
, but the
resulting forecasts would not be optimal). This non-countability of the closure is, however, not
restricted to HMMs with real-valued parameters (as claimed in
Jurgens1
;
Jurgens2
;
Jurgens3
),
but holds also for many of the HMMs studied in
Zambella
. The difference between these two groups
of models is not in the cardinality of the sets of inferences, but in the Hausdorff dimension of the
invariant measure (the Blackwell measure
Blackwell
) supported by it. It was zero in all models
studied in
Zambella
, although the box-counting dimensions were non-zero in many cases.

The failure of distinguishing sets from their closures is also responsible for most of
Jurgens2

being obsolete. As remarked already in
Zambella
, the set of mixed (=causal) states is very
similar to an iterated function system (IFS), with a difference largely overlooked in

Jurgens1
;
Jurgens2
;
Jurgens3
: While the maps
{\bf T}^{(x)}
are chosen randomly (maybe with
non-uniform probabilities) in an IFS, their choice is in the present case constrained by the (in general
non-trivial) grammar of the sequence
\{x_{t}\}
. Thus theorems about IFSs cannot be taken over blindly

Non-trivial grammatical restrictions do not seem to be responsible for the ‘resetting strings’
found in some of the models studied in
Zambella
, which are finite strings
R=x_{1}\ldots x_{n}
of
observed symbols for which
{\bf T}^{(R)}\equiv{\bf T}^{(x_{n})}\ldots{\bf T}^{(x_{1})}
maps any

{\bf P}
onto
{\bf P}_{\infty}
. They correspond to catastrophic forgettings of the internal
state and are then responsible for the finite Hausdorff dimension of the Blackwell measure; in a
systematic search, we now found that this phenomenon occurs in 108 of the 256 models studied in

Zambella
. For the model called ‘rule 22’ in
Zambella
. the shortest resetting string is
e.g. ‘01000010000’ (the string given in
Zambella
is an extension of this).
. Nevertheless,
the
open set condition
(OSC)
Moran
;
Peres-Rams
should hold in many cases. But its
application is much less trivial than assumed in
Jurgens2
, where the (non-)overlapping of the sets
was confused with the (non-)overlapping of their closures. That such a confusion can be fatal is maybe
best illustrated by the ‘fat baker’s transformation’
Alexander
.

If minimization of the set of mixed states had been used, the study of the Hausdorff dimension of the
set of causal states in
Jurgens2
(called ‘statistical complexity dimension’ there), would have
been very useful. Both were obviously motivated by the calculations of Hausdorff dimensions and
by similar studies of the convergence of entropy and forecasting complexity in
Zambella
,
although that paper was not cited. But although this minimization was alluded to repeatedly in

Jurgens1
;
Jurgens2
;
Jurgens3
, it was never actually done.

But even more natural and interesting would seem anyhow what we might call the ‘
FC
divergence rate
’
\alpha
, in which we replace the dependence of the FC on the
precision of the hidden state inferences (as in the statistical complexity dimension
Jurgens2
)
by its dependence on the achieved values of the Shannon entropy. Assume we have a stochastic process
with finite entropy
h
and infinite true FC, and assume that we have an approximate
forecasting scheme with a finite FC
C
and an approximate entropy
h_{C}>h
. Then we might expect
generically

C\sim\delta h^{-\alpha}\qquad{\rm with}\;\;\;\delta h=h_{C}-h.

(7)

Indeed, this can be defined for general stationary ergodic processes, not only for HMM. All what is
needed is that one can define a sequence of
approximate
HMMs or OOMs, to which one can apply Eqs. (4,6).
Minimizing
h_{C}-h
can be used for model (or parameter) inference
Zucchini
, but minimizing
both
h_{C}-h
and
C
at fixed
\alpha
could be useful for finding models / parameters / approximations
that are both accurate and relatively easy to forecast.

Finally, Ref.
Jurgens3
is devoted to the calculation of the
‘ambiguity rate’

h_{a}
of a process,
a notion supposedly introduced by Shannon in
Shannon
as a quantity opposed to ‘equivocation’. In fact,
‘ambiguity’ is mentioned exactly once in
Shannon
, but as a
synonym
for equivocation. The
concept as defined in
Jurgens3
is the rate at which information about the past can be
discarded, and yet prediction of the future is optimal. If
h_{a}<h
, this would mean that less information
is dismissed than new information is obtained, i.e. that FC (the information stored in an optimal forecasting
scheme) diverges with time. In the opposite case, i.e. when
h_{a}=h
, it was claimed that “the associated

\epsilon
-machine’s internal causal-state process is stationary.”
Jurgens3
. This is wrong, as
demonstrated by the counter-examples given in
Zambella
. There it was shown that FC is finite in
all considered cases (i.e.,
h_{a}=h
), but yet the forecasting process is not stationary in cases like
‘rule 22’, because of the slow convergence of FC.

When each observed sequence leads to a different forecast, it is clear that no information can be
dismissed in an optimal forecast, and thus
h_{a}=0
. In some cases, the situation is a bit more subtle.
Consider, e.g., the example in Sec. 7B of

Jurgens3
for control parameter values
x=0.25,\alpha=0.5
, which was treated in detail in
appendix B of
Jurgens3
. In this model, there are three emitted symbols
\triangle,\Box,\circ
.
One finds that always
\hat{p}_{t}(\Box)=1/4
, while
\hat{p}_{t}(\triangle)
and
\hat{p}_{t}(\circ)

depend non-trivially on the observations at times
1,\ldots t-1
. Indeed, no two different histories lead
to the same
\hat{p}_{t}(\triangle)
and
\hat{p}_{t}(\circ)
(at least for
t<12
; for larger
t
one would
need extended precision arithmetic to avoid numerical problems), so that no information can be dismissed
if
all
forecasts are to be optimal, and thus
h_{a}=0
— while
h_{a}=0.4499
bits is quoted
in
Jurgens3
.
Obviously, this wrong value was obtained because
h_{a}
was calculated in
Jurgens3
using
the open set condition, and overlaps between sets were confused with overlaps between their closures.
Since this error was made in all calculations of
h_{a}
, all numerical results of

Jurgens3
are either trivial or obsolete.

Jurgens1
;
Jurgens2
;
Jurgens3
are either
wrong or were well known for a long time. In particular, many of them were first obtained in

Zambella
(which was not cited), a fact pointed out already several times to
Dr. Crutchfield (see also
arXiv
;
arXiv2
).
