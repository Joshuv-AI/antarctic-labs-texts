On Positivity Preservers with constant Coefficients and their Generators Journal: ArXiv
Philipp J. di Dio

Abstract
In this work we study positivity preservers T : ℝ ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x n ] → ℝ ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x n ] T:\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}] with constant coefficients and define their generators A A if they exist, i.e., exp ⁡ ( A ) = T \exp(A)=T . We use the theory of regular Fréchet Lie groups to show the first main result. A positivity preserver with constant coefficients has a generator if and only if it is represented by an infinitely divisible measure ( 4.37 ). In the second main result ( 4.41 ) we use the Lévy–Khinchin formula to fully characterize the generators of positivity preservers with constant coefficients.

Introduction

Non-negative polynomials are widely applied and studied.
A first step to investigate non-negative polynomials is to study the convex cone

\mathrm{Pos}(K):=\{p\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\,|\,p(x)\geq 0\ \text{for all}\ x\in K\}

of non-negative polynomials on some
K\subseteq\mathds{R}^{n}
.

The second logical step is to study (linear) maps

T:\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

between polynomials, especially when they preserve non-negativity, i.e.,

T\mathrm{Pos}(K)\subseteq\mathrm{Pos}(K),

see e.g.
[
9
,
16
,
4
]
.
With the multi-index notation
\partial^{\alpha}=\partial_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
for all
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
with
n\in\mathds{N}
the following is long known.
An explicit proof can e.g. be found in
[
16
]
.

Lemma 1.1

(folklore, see e.g.
[
16
, Lem. 2.3]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}_{0}
and let

T:\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

be linear.
Then for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
there exist unique
q_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
such that

T=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}q_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}.

The map
T:\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is called to have constant coefficients if
q_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R}
for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
.
The subset with
q_{0}=1
is denoted by

\mathfrak{D}:=\left\{\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}q_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}\,\middle|\,q_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R},\ q_{0}=1\right\}\quad\subsetneq\mathds{R}[[\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{n}]].

Positivity preservers are fully described by their polynomial coefficients. We have the following.

Theorem 1.2

(see e.g.
[
4
, Thm. 3.1]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
and let
T:\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be linear.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

T\mathrm{Pos}(\mathds{R}^{n})\subseteq\mathrm{Pos}(\mathds{R}^{n})
.

(ii)

(\alpha!\cdot q_{\alpha}(y))_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
is a moment sequence for all
y\in\mathds{R}^{n}
.

Example 1.3
.

Let
n=1
.
Then

\exp(t\cdot\partial_{x}^{2})=\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{0}}\frac{t^{k}\cdot\partial_{x}^{2k}}{k!}

is a positivity preserver for all
t\geq 0
.

\circ

The following is a linear operator
T
which is not a positivity preserver.

Example 1.4
.

Let
k\geq 3
and
a\in\mathds{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
Then

\exp(a\partial_{x}^{k}):=\sum_{j\in\mathds{N}_{0}}\frac{a^{j}\cdot\partial_{x}^{j\cdot k}}{j!}

is not a positivity preserver since
q_{2k}=a^{2k}\neq 0
but
q_{2k+2}=0
, i.e.,
(j!\cdot q_{j})_{j\in\mathds{N}_{0}}
is not a moment sequence.

\circ

From elementary calculations and measure theory we get the following additional properties of positivity preservers.
Let
T
be a positivity preserver with constant coefficients and
\mu
be a representing measure of the corresponding moment sequence
s=(s_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}=(\alpha!\cdot q_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}
, then

(Tf)(x)=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}f(x+y)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(y)

for all
f\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. For short we call
\mu
also a
representing measure of
T
.
If follows for two such operators
T
and
T^{\prime}
with representing measures
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
that

(TT^{\prime}f)(x)=\int f(x+y)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}(\mu*\mu^{\prime})(y)

(1)

where
\mu*\mu^{\prime}
is the
convolution of the two measures

\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
:

(\mu*\mu^{\prime})(A):=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathds{R}^{n}}\chi_{A}(x+y)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}(y)\\
=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\mu(A-y)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}(y)=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\mu^{\prime}(A-x)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x)

(2)

for any
A\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathds{R}^{n})
, see e.g.
[
3
, Sect. 3.9]
.
Here,
\chi_{A}
is the characteristic function of the Borel set
A
.
For the supports we have

\mathrm{supp}\,(\mu*\mu^{\prime})=\mathrm{supp}\,\mu+\mathrm{supp}\,\mu^{\prime}.

(3)

This can easily be proved from (
2
) or found in the literature, see e.g.
[
7
, Prop. 14.5 (ii)]
for a special case.
We abbreviate

\mu^{*k}:=\underbrace{\mu*\dots*\mu}_{k\text{-times}}

for all
k\in\mathds{N}
and set
\mu^{*0}:=\delta_{0}
with
\delta_{0}
the Dirac measure centered at
0
.

Previously we investigated the heat semi-group (see
1.3
for
n=1
) and its action on (non-negative) polynomials, see
[
5
,
6
]
.
For
n\in\mathds{N}
we have that
\exp(t\Delta)p_{0}
is the unique solution of the polynomial valued heat equation

\partial_{t}p=\Delta p

with initial values
p_{0}\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.
In
[
6
]
we observed the very strange behavior that several non-negative polynomials which are not sums of squares (e.g. the Motzkin and the Choi–Lam polynomial) become a sum of squares in finite time under the heat equation.
In
[
6
, Thm. 3.20]
we showed that every non-negative
p\in\mathds{R}[x,y,z]_{\leq 4}
becomes a sum of squares in finite time.
All these observations hold for the heat equation, i.e., the family
(\exp(t\Delta))_{t\geq 0}
of positivity preservers with constant coefficients with the generator
\Delta=\partial_{1}^{2}+\dots+\partial_{n}^{2}
.
So a natural question to further study these effects is to understand any family
(\exp(tA))_{t\geq 0}
of positivity preservers and hence to determine all possible generators
A
.
This is the main question of the current work for the constant coefficient case.
It is answered in
4.41
.

The paper is structured as follows.
To define and work with
\exp(A)
we repeat in the preliminaries for the readers convenience the notion of Lie groups, Lie algebras, and especially their lesser known infinite dimensional versions of regular Fréchet Lie groups.
We will see that
\mathfrak{D}
is a regular Fréchet Lie group with a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{d}
.
By
1.2
we transport this property to sequences in
Section

3
.

Section

4
contains the two main results.
In the first
4.37
we show that a positivity preserver with constant coefficients has a generator if and only if it is represented by an infinitely divisible measure.
Using the Lévy–Khinchin formula (which is also given in the preliminary, see
2.22
) we give in the second
4.41
the full description of all generators of positivity preservers with constant coefficients.
In
Section

5
we discuss a strange action on non-negative polynomials on
[0,\infty)
caused by the heat equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions.
We end this paper with a summary and an open question.

Preliminaries

Lie groups and Lie algebras are standard concepts in mathematics
[
28
]
.
However, this only applies to the finite dimensional cases.
For the readers convenience we give here the infinite dimensional definitions and examples we need to make the paper as self-contained as possible.
For the sake of completeness we also include the explicit statement of the Lévy–Khinchin formula in

2.1
Lie Groups and their Lie Algebras

Definition 2.5
.

A group
(G,\,\cdot\,)
is called a
Lie group
if
G
is also a
n
-dimensional smooth manifold,
n\in\mathds{N}
, such that
G\times G\to G:(A,B)\mapsto AB^{-1}
is smooth.
The
Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
G
is the tangent space
T_{e}G
at the identity
e\in G
.

The connection between the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and the Lie group
G
is given by the
exponential map

\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to G
.
For the special case
G=\mathrm{Gl}(n,\mathds{C})
the exponential mapping fulfills the following.

Lemma 2.6

(see e.g.
[
28
, p. 134, Ex. 15]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
.
The following hold:

(i)

The exponential map

\exp:\mathrm{Gl}(n,\mathds{C})\to\mathrm{Gl}(n,\mathds{C}),\quad A\mapsto\exp A:=\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{0}}\frac{A^{k}}{k!}

is surjective.

(ii)

Let
\mathrm{id}+N\in\mathrm{Gl}(n,\mathds{C})
be such that
N
is nilpotent.
Then

\log(\mathrm{id}+N)=-\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}}\frac{(-N)^{k}}{k}

is well-defined and

\exp(\log(\mathrm{id}+N))=\mathrm{id}+N

holds.

The proof of
2.6
(ii) follows from formal power series calculations.
For more on Lie groups and Lie algebras see e.g.
[
28
]
.

The charts
\varphi:U\subseteq\mathds{R}^{n}\to G
of the
n
-dimensional smooth manifold
G
induce the Euclidean topology on the group
G
and
(A,B)\mapsto AB^{-1}
is smooth with respect to this topology.
When extending this to infinite dimensions more than one topology is possible and the choice of topology on
G
is important.

2.2
Fréchet Spaces

Definition 2.7
.

A topological vector space
\mathcal{V}
is called a
Fréchet space
if the following three conditions are fulfilled:

(i)

\mathcal{V}
is metrizable (i.e.,
\mathcal{V}
is Hausdorff),

(ii)

\mathcal{V}
is complete, and

(iii)

\mathcal{V}
is locally convex.

It is clear that
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}
is a Fréchet space for all
d\in\mathds{N}_{0}
since they are finite dimensional.
Their Fréchet topology is unique.

Example 2.8

(see e.g.
[
27
, pp. 91–92, Ex. III]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
.
The vector space
\mathds{R}[[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]]
of formal power series

p=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}c_{\alpha}\cdot x^{\alpha}\qquad\text{with}\ c_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R}

equipped with the topology induced by the semi-norms

|p|_{d}:=\sup_{|\alpha|\leq d}|c_{\alpha}|\qquad\text{with}\ d\in\mathds{N}_{0}

is a Fréchet space.
In other words we have the convergence

p_{i}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}c_{i,\alpha}\cdot x^{\alpha}\quad\to\quad p=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}}c_{\alpha}\cdot x^{\alpha}\qquad\text{for}\ i\to\infty

if and only if
c_{i,\alpha}\xrightarrow{i\to\infty}c_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
.

\circ

For more on topological vector spaces see e.g.
[
27
,
26
]
.

We call

(ii)

2.4
The Lie Group
\mathfrak{D}_{d}

Definition 2.10
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
and
d\in\mathds{N}_{0}
. We define

\mathfrak{D}_{d}:=\mathfrak{D}|_{\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}}
.

Since

A\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}\subseteq\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}

holds for all
d\in\mathds{N}_{0}
and
A\in\mathfrak{D}
the
\mathfrak{D}_{d}
are well-defined.
From
2.10
we see that
\mathfrak{D}_{d}
consists only of operators of the form

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:|\alpha|\leq d}c_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}

with
c_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R}
and
c_{0}=1

since on
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}
every operator
\partial^{\beta}
with
|\beta|>d
fulfills
\partial^{\beta}p=0
for all
p\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}
, i.e.,
\partial^{\beta}=0
on
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}
.

Remark 2.11
.

We can also define
\mathfrak{D}_{d}
by
\mathfrak{D}/\langle\partial^{\alpha}\,|\,|\alpha|=d+1\rangle
.
Both definitions are almost identical.
However,
2.10
has the following advantage.
In
\mathfrak{D}/\langle\partial^{\alpha}\,|\,|\alpha|=d+1\rangle
we have the problem that we are working with equivalence classes and hence we can not calculate
A+B
for
A\in\mathfrak{D}_{d}
and
B\in\mathfrak{D}_{e}
for
d\neq e
.
With
2.10
we can calculate
A+B
for
A\in\mathfrak{D}_{d}
and
B\in\mathfrak{D}_{e}
with
d\neq e
since
A+B
is defined on

\mathrm{dom}\,(A+B)=\mathrm{dom}\,A\cap\mathrm{dom}\,B=\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq\min(d,e)}

as usual for (unbounded) operators
[
21
]
.

2.10
can then even be used to calculate
A+B
for
A
on
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}
and
B
on
\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]_{\leq e}
for
n\neq m
and
d\neq e
on

\mathrm{dom}\,(A+B)=\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{\min\{n,m\}}]_{\leq\min\{d,e\}}.

Example 2.12
.

Let
n=1
and
d=3
. Then

\mathfrak{D}_{3}=\left\{1+c_{1}\partial_{x}+c_{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+c_{3}\partial_{x}^{3}\;\middle|\;c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathds{R}\right\}\quad\text{on}\quad\mathds{R}[x]_{\leq 3}.

Let

A=1+a_{1}\partial_{x}+a_{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+a_{3}\partial_{x}^{3}\quad\text{and}\quad B=1+b_{1}\partial_{x}+b_{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+b_{3}\partial_{x}^{3}

be in
\mathfrak{D}_{3}
.
Then

\begin{split}AB&=BA=(1+a_{1}\partial_{x}+a_{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+a_{3}\partial_{x}^{3})\cdot(1+b_{1}\partial_{x}+b_{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+b_{3}\partial_{x}^{3})\\
&=1+(a_{1}+b_{1})\partial_{x}+(a_{2}+a_{1}b_{1}+b_{2})\partial_{x}^{2}+(a_{3}+a_{2}b_{1}+a_{1}b_{2}+b_{3})\partial_{x}^{3}\end{split}

(4)

since derivatives
\partial^{i}
with

are the zero operators on
\mathds{R}[x]_{\leq 3}
.
Hence,
(\mathfrak{D}_{3},\,\cdot\,)
is a commutative semi-group with neutral element
\mathds{1}=1
.

We will now see that
\mathfrak{D}_{3}
is even a commutative group.
For that it is sufficient to find for any
A\in\mathfrak{D}_{3}
a
B\in\mathfrak{D}_{3}
with

.
By (
4
)
AB=\mathds{1}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=a_{1}+b_{1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad b_{1}=-a_{1}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=a_{2}+a_{1}b_{1}+b_{2}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad b_{2}=-a_{2}+a_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=a_{3}+a_{2}b_{1}+a_{1}b_{2}+b_{3}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad b_{3}=-a_{3}+2a_{2}a_{1}-a_{1}^{3},

\circ

We have seen in the previous example that

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
and
d\in\mathds{N}_{0}
. Then
(\mathfrak{D}_{d},\,\cdot\,)
is a commutative group.

Proof.

Let
A=\sum_{\alpha:|\alpha|\leq d}a_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha},B=\sum_{\beta:|\beta|\leq d}b_{\beta}\partial^{\beta}\in\mathfrak{D}_{d}
, i.e.,
a_{0}=b_{0}=1
.
Then

AB=C=\sum_{\gamma:|\gamma|\leq d}c_{\gamma}\cdot\partial^{\gamma}

holds with

c_{\gamma}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:\alpha+\beta=\gamma}a_{\alpha}b_{\beta}.

(5)

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\succeq\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})
on
\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
if and only if
\alpha_{i}\geq\beta_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
.
Then (
5
) can be solved by induction on
|\gamma|
.
For
|\gamma|=0
we have

and
a_{0}=c_{0}=1
, i.e.,

.
So assume (

) is solved for all

with
|\gamma|\leq d-1
.
Then for any
\gamma\in\mathds{N}_{0}
with

we have

b_{\gamma}=a_{0}b_{\gamma}=-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}\setminus\{0\}:\gamma\succeq\alpha}a_{\alpha}\cdot b_{\gamma-\alpha},

(6)

i.e., the system (
5
) of equations has a unique solution gained by induction.
Hence, for every
A\in\mathfrak{D}_{d}
there exists a unique
B\in\mathfrak{D}_{d}
with
AB=BA=\mathds{1}
.
∎

) a diffeomorphism and it is a coordinate map for

.

2.6
The Regular Fréchet Lie Group
\mathfrak{D}
and its Lie Algebra

In

we have seen that
(\mathfrak{D}_{d},\,\cdot\,)
is a Lie group with Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{d}_{d},\,\cdot\,,+)

Hence, similar to

Definition 2.18
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}

\mathfrak{d}:=\left\{\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}d_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}\,\middle|\,d_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R}\ \text{for all}\ \alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\right\}.

For
\mathfrak{D}
and

Theorem 2.19
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
.
Then the following hold:

(i)

(\mathfrak{d},\,\cdot\,,+)
is a commutative algebra.

(ii)

(\mathfrak{D},\,\cdot\,)
is a commutative group.

(iii)

The map

\exp:\mathfrak{d}\to\mathfrak{D},\quad A\mapsto\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{0}}\frac{A^{k}}{k!}

(iv)

The map

\log:\mathfrak{D}\to\mathfrak{d},\quad A\mapsto-\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}}\frac{(\mathds{1}-A)^{k}}{k}

(v)

The maps
\exp:\mathfrak{d}\to\mathfrak{D}
and
\log:\mathfrak{D}\to\mathfrak{d}

Proof.

(i): That is clear.

(ii): That
A\cdot B=B\cdot A
holds for all

is clear.
The inverse of
A\in\mathfrak{D}
is uniquely determined by solving (
5
) to get (
6
) for all
\gamma\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
.
This is a formal power series argument with coordinate-wise convergence (as in the Fréchet topology,
2.8
).

(iii): At first we show that
\exp:\mathfrak{d}\to\mathfrak{D}
is well-defined.
To see this note that for any

we have

A^{k}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}:|\alpha|\geq k}c_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha},

i.e.,

contains no differential operators of order
\leq k-1
.
Hence, the sum

\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{A^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}c_{K,\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}

converges coefficient-wise to

\exp A=\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{0}}\frac{A^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}c_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha},

i.e., in the Fréchet topology of
\mathfrak{D}\subsetneq\mathds{R}[[\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{n}]]\cong\mathds{R}[[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]]
, see

.
In other words, the coefficients

for all

is well-defined.
With that we have
\exp\mathfrak{d}\subseteq\mathfrak{D}
.
For equality we give the inverse map in (v).

(iv): To show that

is well-defined the same argument as in (iii) holds for
(\mathds{1}-A)^{k}
with
A\in\mathfrak{D}
.
It shows that

for all

is well-defined and we have
\log\mathfrak{D}\subseteq\mathfrak{d}
.

(v): To prove that
\exp
and

are inverse to each other we remark

\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]=\bigcup_{d\in\mathds{N}_{0}}\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}.

Define

\exp_{d}A:=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\frac{A^{k}}{k!}\quad\text{and}\quad\log_{d}A:=-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{(\mathds{1}-A)^{k}}{k}.

Then for every

with

\exp(\log A)p=\exp_{d}(\log_{d}A)p=Ap

\exp(\log A)=A
for all

.
Similarly, we have

.
This also shows the remaining assertions

and
\log\mathfrak{D}=\mathfrak{d}
from (iii) and (iv).
∎

(iii): Follows like (ii) from

2.7
Lévy–Khinchin Formula

A measure
\mu
on
\mathds{R}^{n}
is called
divisible by
k\in\mathds{N}
if there exists a measure
\nu
such that
\mu=\nu^{*k}
.
A measure
\mu
on
\mathds{R}^{n}
is called
infinitely divisible
if it is divisible by any
k\in\mathds{N}
.
Infinitely divisible measures are fully characterized by the
Lévy–Khinchin formula
.

Theorem 2.22

(Lévy–Khinchin, see e.g.
[
13
, Cor. 15.8]
or
[
14
, Satz 16.17]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
and
\mu
be a measure on
\mathds{R}^{n}
.
The following are equivalent:

(i)

\mu
is infinitely divisible.

(ii)

There exist a vector
b\in\mathds{R}^{n}
, a symmetric matrix
\Sigma\in\mathds{R}^{n\times n}
with
\Sigma\succeq 0
, and a measure
\nu
on
\mathds{R}^{n}
such that

\log\int e^{itx}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x)=itb-\frac{1}{2}t^{T}\Sigma t+\int(e^{itx}-1-itx\cdot\chi_{\|x\|_{2}<1})\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x)

holds for the characteristic function of
\mu
.

The Regular Fréchet Lie Group Structure of Sequences

We define appropriate sets
\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{s}\subsetneq\mathds{R}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}

*

Definition 3.23
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
.
We define

\mathfrak{S}:=\left\{(s_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\in\mathds{R}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\,\middle|\,s_{0}=1\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{s}:=\left\{(s_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\in\mathds{R}^{\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\,\middle|\,s_{0}=0\right\}.

(13)

and on

the convolution

s^{0}=\mathds{1}:=(\delta_{0,\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad s^{*k}:=\underbrace{s*\cdots*s}_{k\text{-times}}

for all
k\in\mathds{N}
and

D
in (

) is of course a Fréchet space isomorphism.

and
\mathfrak{s}

and

, respectively.

.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
. Then the following hold:

holds for all

(iv)

.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
.
Then

Main Theorem 4.41
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
.
The following are equivalent:

(i)

A=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{a_{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{d}_{+}
.

(ii)

There exists a symmetric matrix
\Sigma=(\sigma_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{n}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
with
\Sigma\succeq 0
, a vector
b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})^{T}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
, and a measure
\nu
on
\mathds{R}^{n}
with

\int_{\|x\|_{2}\geq 1}|x_{i}|\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\nu(x)<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}|x^{\alpha}|\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\nu(x)<\infty

for all
i=1,\dots,n
and
\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}
with
|\alpha|\geq 2
such that

\displaystyle a_{e_{i}}

\displaystyle=b_{i}+\int_{\|x\|_{2}\geq 1}x_{i}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle\text{for all}\ i=1,\dots,n,

\displaystyle a_{e_{i}+e_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i,j}+\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}x^{e_{i}+e_{j}}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle\text{for all}\ i,j=1,\dots,n,

and

\displaystyle a_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}x^{\alpha}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle\text{for all}\ \alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}\ \text{with}\ |\alpha|\geq 3.

Proof.

By
4.37
“(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)” we have that (i)
A\in\mathfrak{d}_{+}
if and only if
\exp A
has an infinitely divisible representing measure
\mu
, i.e., by
1.2
we have

\exp A=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\frac{1}{\alpha!}\cdot\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}x^{\alpha}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x)\cdot\partial^{\alpha}.

(16)

By
2.21
we can take the logarithm and hence (
16
) is equivalent to

A=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{a_{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}=\log\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\frac{1}{\alpha!}\cdot\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}x^{\alpha}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x)\cdot\partial^{\alpha}\right).

(17)

With the isomorphism

\mathds{C}[[\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{n}]]\to\mathds{C}[[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]],\ \partial_{1}\mapsto it_{1},\ \dots,\ \partial_{n}\mapsto it_{n}

we have that (
17

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{a_{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\cdot(it)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}\frac{1}{\alpha!}\cdot\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}x^{\alpha}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x)\cdot(it)^{\alpha}\right).

(18)

But the right hand side of (

) is now the characteristic function

\displaystyle=\log\int e^{itx}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(x)

of the
\mu
.
Hence, by the Lévy–Khinchin formula (see
2.22
) we have

\displaystyle=ibt-\frac{1}{2}t^{T}\Sigma t+\int(e^{itx}-1-itx\cdot\chi_{\|x\|_{2}<1})\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\nu(x).

(19)

After a formal power series expansion of

in the Fréchet topology of

, see

, and a comparison of coefficients we have that (

) is equivalent to (ii) which ends the proof.
∎

Form the previous result we see that the difference between

and a moment sequence is that the representing (Lévy) measure

5
A Strange Action on
\mathrm{Pos}([0,\infty))

Before we end this work we want to discuss an example of a strange positivity action on
[0,\infty)
.
We take the following example.

Example 5.42
.

Let
\Delta=\partial_{x}^{2}
on
L^{2}([0,\infty),\mathds{R})
with Dirichlet boundary conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\Delta u

\displaystyle u(0,t)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for all}\ t\geq 0

\displaystyle u(\,\cdot\,,0)

\displaystyle=u_{0}\in L^{2}([0,\infty),\mathds{R}).

Then

u(x,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{t}(x,y)u_{0}(y)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}y

with

k_{t}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\cdot e^{-(x-y)^{2}/4}\cdot\left[1-e^{xy/t}\right]

for all
x,y\geq 0
and
t>0
, see
[
8
, Exm. 4.1.1]
.

For
u_{0}\in\mathcal{S}([0,\infty),\mathds{R})
a Schwartz function on
[0,\infty)
we have that
k_{t}*u_{0}\in\mathcal{S}([0,\infty),\mathds{R})
for all
t>0
and hence we look at the time-dependent moments

s_{j}(t)=\int_{K}x^{j}\cdot u(x,t)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}x=\int_{[0,\infty)^{2}}x^{j}\cdot k_{t}(x,y)\cdot u_{0}(y)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}x\penalty\ \mathrm{d}y

for all
j\in\mathds{N}_{0}
and
t>0
.

The action on
\mathds{R}[x]
is then given by
(T_{t}p)(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}p(y)\cdot k_{t}(x,y)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}y
. We find for
p=1
that

(T_{t}1)(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{t}(x,y)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}y

is a continuous, non-decreasing function in
x\in[0,\infty)
with
(T_{t}1)(0)=0
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}(T_{t}1)(x)=1
, i.e.,
T_{t}1\notin\mathds{R}[x]
for any
t\in(0,\infty)
.
While for
p=x^{j}
with
j\in\mathds{N}
we get for the time-dependent moments by partial integration

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{j}\cdot u(x,t)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{j}\cdot u(x,t)^{\prime\prime}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\underbrace{x^{j}\cdot u(x,t)^{\prime}\penalty\ \Big|_{x=0}^{\infty}}_{=0}-\int_{0}^{\infty}j\cdot x^{j-1}\cdot u(x,t)^{\prime}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-j\cdot x^{j-1}\cdot u(x,t)\penalty\ \Big|_{x=0}^{\infty}}_{=0}+\int_{0}^{\infty}j\cdot(j-1)\cdot x^{j-2}\cdot u(x,t)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=j\cdot(j-1)\cdot s_{j-2}(t).

These are the recursive relations we already encountered with the heat equation on
\mathds{R}
, see
[
5
,
6
]
.
Therefore, for
odd
polynomials
p\in\mathds{R}[x]
we have

(T_{t}p)(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}p(y)\cdot k_{t}(x,y)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}y=(e^{t\partial_{x}^{2}}p)(x)\in\mathds{R}[x]

with
T_{t}x^{2d+1}=\mathfrak{p}_{2d+1}(x,t)
for all
d\in\mathds{N}_{0}
in
[
6
, Dfn. 3.1]
and if additionally
p\geq 0
on
[0,\infty)
, then
T_{t}p\geq 0
on
[0,\infty)
.
On the other hand for
even
polynomials
p\in\mathds{R}[x]
we find that
T_{t}p\notin C^{\infty}([0,\infty))\setminus\mathds{R}[x]
but at least
T_{t}p\geq 0
on
[0,\infty)
.
The reason for this strange behavior is of course the Dirichlet boundary condition and the resulting reflection principle.
This effect needs further investigations.

\circ

This example shall also serve as a warning.
Just because the operator (symbol) is
\partial_{x}^{2}
does not mean that the positivity preservers are
\exp(t\partial_{x}^{2})
.
The domain and the corresponding boundary conditions have to be taken into account as is done and is well-known in the partial differential equation literature and semi-group theory.
Besides the
6.43
below this is another direction where further studies have to be done.

6
Summary and Open Question

If

A=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}q_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}\quad\text{with}\quad q_{\alpha}\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq|\alpha|}

(20)

then

A\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}\subseteq\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq d}\quad\text{for all}\quad d\in\mathds{N}_{0},

(21)

i.e.,
A
is called
degree preserving
.
In fact for any linear
A:\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
we have (
20
)
\Leftrightarrow
(
21
).
(
20
)
\Rightarrow
(
21
) is clear.
For (
21
)
\Rightarrow
(
20
) take in (
20
) the smallest
\alpha
with respect to the lex-order such that
\deg q_{\alpha}>|\alpha|
.
Then
\deg Ax^{\alpha}>|\alpha|
which is a contradiction to (
21
).

Similar to
4.36
we have that the partial differential equation

\partial_{t}p(x,t)=Ap(x,t)\quad\text{with}\quad p(x,0)=p_{0}(x)\in\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

and
A
as in (
20
)
is equivalent to a linear system of ordinary differential equations in the coefficients of
p
.
Hence, it has a unique solution
p(\,\cdot\,,t)=\exp(tA)p_{0}
for all
t\in\mathds{R}
, i.e.,
\exp(tA)
is well-defined for any
A
as in (
20
) and
t\in\mathds{R}
.
If additionally
A
is a positivity preserver then
\exp(tA)
is a degree preserving positivity preserver with polynomial coefficients for all
t\geq 0
.
In
4.41
we have already seen for the positivity preservers with constant coefficients that additional
A
appear.
The restriction that
A
is degree preserving is a natural restriction since otherwise
\exp(tA)\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\not\subseteq\mathds{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
for any
t>0
.
So we arrive at the following open problem.

Open Problem 6.43
.

What is the description of the set

\left\{A=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathds{N}_{0}^{n}}q_{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}\;\middle|\;\exp(tA)\ \text{is a positivity preserver for all}\ t\geq 0\right\}

of all positivity generators, i.e., not necessarily with constant coefficients?

The constant coefficient case is completely solved by
4.41
.
Part of the non-constant coefficient cases are covered by the previous case that
A
is a degree preserving positivity preserver.
For the non-constant coefficient cases note that we are then in the framework of a non-commutative infinite dimensional Lie group and its Lie algebra.
Here the theory is much richer, see e.g.
[
17
,
12
,
18
,
25
,
29
,
23
]
and references therein.
