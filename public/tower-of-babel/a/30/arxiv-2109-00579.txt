Moving intervals for packing and covering
Rain Jiang, Kai Jiang, Minghui Jiang

Abstract
We study several problems on geometric packing and covering with movement. Given a family ℐ \mathcal{I} of n n intervals of κ \kappa distinct lengths, and another interval B B , can we pack the intervals in ℐ \mathcal{I} inside B B (respectively, cover B B by the intervals in ℐ \mathcal{I} ) by moving τ \tau intervals and keeping the other σ = n − τ \sigma=n-\tau intervals unmoved? We show that both packing and covering are W[1]-hard with any one of κ \kappa , τ \tau , and σ \sigma as single parameter, but are FPT with combined parameters κ \kappa and τ \tau . We also obtain improved polynomial-time algorithms for packing and covering, including an O ⁡ ( n ​ log 2 ​ n ) O(n\log^{2}n) time algorithm for covering, when all intervals in ℐ \mathcal{I} have the same length.

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a family of
n
intervals of
\kappa
distinct lengths
and of total length
\ell_{\mathcal{I}}
.
Let
B
be another interval of length
\ell_{B}
,
in the same line as the intervals in
\mathcal{I}
.
We study the following problems of deciding,
for two parameters
\sigma
and
\tau
where
\sigma+\tau=n
,
whether we can move
\tau
intervals in
\mathcal{I}
,
and keep the other
\sigma
intervals in
\mathcal{I}
unmoved,
to achieve certain geometric configurations:

Pack

\displaystyle:\textrm{the $n$ intervals in $\mathcal{I}$ are pairwise-disjoint and are contained in $B$,}

Cover

\displaystyle:\textrm{the union of the $n$ intervals in $\mathcal{I}$ contains $B$,}

Join

\displaystyle:\textrm{the $n$ intervals in $\mathcal{I}$ are joined into a contiguous interval of length $\ell_{\mathcal{I}}$,}

J-Pack

\displaystyle:\textrm{the $n$ intervals in $\mathcal{I}$ are joined into a contiguous interval of length $\ell_{\mathcal{I}}$ that is contained in $B$,}

J-Cover

\displaystyle:\textrm{the $n$ intervals in $\mathcal{I}$ are joined into a contiguous interval of length $\ell_{\mathcal{I}}$ that contains $B$,}

Tile

\displaystyle:\textrm{the $n$ intervals in $\mathcal{I}$ are joined into a contiguous interval of length $\ell_{\mathcal{I}}$ that coincides with $B$.}

Without loss of generality, we assume that

\ell_{\mathcal{I}}\leq\ell_{B}
for

Pack
and

J-Pack
,

\ell_{\mathcal{I}}\geq\ell_{B}
for

Cover
and
J-Cover
,
and

\ell_{\mathcal{I}}=\ell_{B}
for

.
The problem

is a special case of the four problems

Pack
,
Cover
,
J-Pack
, and
J-Cover
,
which are equivalent when
\ell_{\mathcal{I}}=\ell_{B}
.
The problem

is similar to the problem

,
but does not have the constraint of the interval
B
.
Indeed

can be viewed as a special case of

where

B
contains all intervals in
\mathcal{I}
and has extra spaces of
\ell_{\mathcal{I}}

We represent all intervals in the form
[x,y)
,
which is a set closed at left endpoint

and open at right endpoint

.
To avoid unnecessary technicality,
we allow zero-distance moves. That is, an interval may be “moved”
without changing its position.
This ensures natural monotonicities of
\tau
and
\sigma
for these problems.
For example,
denote by

(\tau,\sigma)

the predicate whether the
n
intervals in
\mathcal{I}

can be packed inside the interval

by moving
\tau
intervals and keeping
\sigma=n-\tau

If

Let

∗
,
Cover
∗
,

Join
∗
,

J-Pack
∗
,
J-Cover
∗
,
and
Tile
∗

be the optimization versions of

\tau

,
and let

denote the optimal values,
where
\tau^{*}+\sigma^{*}=n
.

In slightly different formulations,

[
5
]
,
for the application of intrusion detection and barrier coverage by sensors.
When the number

of distinct lengths of intervals in

[

In particular,

already for

[

, Theorem 2]
,
and

, hence

,

The complexities of these problems for fixed

.

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

, and

When all intervals in

presented an

and

,

and

.

,

,

, and

2
Algorithms on intervals of the same length for

J-Pack
∗
and
J-Cover
∗

In this section we prove Proposition
2
on the four problems

Join
∗
,

J-Pack
∗
,
J-Cover
∗
,
and
Tile
∗
.
Recall that

Join
∗
is a special case of
J-Pack
∗
,
and

Tile
∗
is a special case of

J-Pack
∗
and
J-Cover
∗
.
Thus it suffices to present algorithms for

J-Pack
∗
and
J-Cover
∗
.

For any real number
x
,
denote by
\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor

the
fractional part
of
x
.
For any interval
[x,x+\ell)
of integer length
\ell
,
define its
fractional coordinate
as
\{x\}
.

Let
B=[0,\ell_{B})
,
and
\mathcal{I}=\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}\}
,
where
I_{i}=[x_{i},x_{i}+1)
.
Then
\ell_{\mathcal{I}}=n
.
For
\ddot{x}\in[0,1)
,
let
\mathcal{I}(\ddot{x})
be the subfamily of intervals in
\mathcal{I}

with fractional coordinate
\ddot{x}
.
As in
[
5
, Theorem 4 and Theorem 7]
,
the problem

J-Pack
∗
(respectively,
J-Cover
∗
)
reduces to computing,
for every distinct fractional coordinate
\ddot{x}\in[0,1)
of the intervals in
\mathcal{I}
,
the maximum number of pairwise-disjoint intervals in
\mathcal{I}(\ddot{x})

that are inside some interval
B_{\ddot{x}}

of fractional coordinate
\ddot{x}
and of length
\ell_{\mathcal{I}}=n
,
such that
B_{\ddot{x}}\subseteq B
(respectively,
B_{\ddot{x}}\supseteq B
).
Then the intervals in
\mathcal{I}
can be joined into a contiguous interval
that coincides with
B_{\ddot{x}}
.

The algorithm for
J-Pack
∗
(respectively,
J-Cover
∗
)
works as follows.
First sort the intervals in
\mathcal{I}
lexicographically,
by considering each interval
I_{i}
as a pair of numbers
(\{x_{i}\},x_{i})
.
Then the intervals in each subfamily
\mathcal{I}(\ddot{x})
appear consecutively
in the sorted list.
Next scan each subfamily
\mathcal{I}(\ddot{x})
independently.

For each
\ddot{x}
,
regardless of the intervals in
\mathcal{I}(\ddot{x})
,
there exists an interval
B_{\ddot{x}}\subseteq B
(respectively,
B_{\ddot{x}}\supseteq B
)
of fractional coordinate
\ddot{x}
and length
n

if and only if
either
\ddot{x}=0
or

[\ddot{x},\ddot{x}+n)\subseteq[0,\ell_{B})

(respectively,
[\ddot{x}-1,\ddot{x}-1+n)\supseteq[0,\ell_{B})
),
which can be checked in constant time.
If this condition is satisfied,
then proceed to find the maximum number of pairwise-disjoint intervals in
\mathcal{I}(\ddot{x})

that are contained in
[0,\ell_{B})
(respectively, in
[\ell_{B}-n,n)
)
such that the span
x_{j}-x_{i}+1

of the first interval
I_{i}
and the last interval
I_{j}

is at most
n
.
This can be done in linear time using the standard “two pointers” technique.
These intervals in
\mathcal{I}(\ddot{x})
, with span at most
n
,
are inside some interval
B_{\ddot{x}}
of length exactly
n

such that
B_{\ddot{x}}\subseteq[0,\ell_{B})

(respectively,
B_{\ddot{x}}\subseteq[\ell_{B}-n,n)
and hence
B_{\ddot{x}}\supseteq[0,\ell_{B})
).

The overall running time of the algorithm is
O(n\log n)
, which is dominated by the sorting.
This completes the proof of Proposition
2
.

Remark
.

We have used fractional numbers for the interval coordinates so that
the intervals in
\mathcal{I}
have a convenient unit length of
1
.
Alternatively, we could let
B
and the intervals in
\mathcal{I}
have integer coordinates,
and let the uniform length
\ell
of the intervals in
\mathcal{I}
be an integer greater than
1
.
In this formulation, we would define
\{x\}=x\bmod\ell
.
Then the same idea yields an algorithm of the same
O(n\log n)
running time.

Let

,

\mathcal{I}=\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}\}
,
where
I_{i}=[x_{i},x_{i}+1)
.
Then
\ell_{\mathcal{I}}=n

Add two dummy intervals

I_{n+1}=[x_{n+1},x_{n+1}+1)=[\ell_{B},\ell_{B}+1)

After relocating outside intervals and sorting,

-1=x_{0}\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\ldots\leq x_{n}\leq x_{n+1}=\ell_{B}.

When comparing pairs,
we use notations such as

,
and use terms such as

and

For example,

3.1
Algorithms for
Pack
∗

For
0\leq i<j\leq n+1
,
write
i\prec j
if
x_{j}-x_{i}\geq 1
.
For
0\leq j\leq n+1
,
let
l_{j}
be the largest index
i
such that
0\leq i\prec j
,
or
-1
if no such
i
exists.
With increasing
x_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq n+1
,

l_{i}
for all
i

can be computed in
O(n)
time using the standard “two pointers” technique.

Recall that

\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor
.
For
0\leq i<j\leq n+1
,
define

w(i,j)=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if $\{x_{i}\}>\{x_{j}\}$},\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is easy to check that
the
n
intervals in
\mathcal{I}
can be packed inside
B

by moving
n-\sigma
intervals and keeping
\sigma
intervals unmoved
if and only if
there is a sequence
\boldsymbol{s}
of
\sigma+2
indices

,
0\leq h\leq\sigma+1
,
where

0=s_{0}\prec s_{1}\prec\ldots\prec s_{\sigma+1}=n+1,

such that the following condition holds:

\sum_{h=0}^{\sigma}\left(\lfloor x_{s_{h+1}}\rfloor-\lfloor x_{s_{h}}\rfloor-w(s_{h},s_{h+1})\right)\geq n+1,

which simplifies to

\displaystyle\lfloor x_{n+1}\rfloor-\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor-\sum_{h=0}^{\sigma}w(s_{h},s_{h+1})

\displaystyle\geq n+1

\displaystyle\lfloor\ell_{B}\rfloor+1-\sum_{h=0}^{\sigma}w(s_{h},s_{h+1})

\displaystyle\geq n+1

\displaystyle\sum_{h=0}^{\sigma}w(s_{h},s_{h+1})

\displaystyle\leq\lfloor\ell_{B}\rfloor-n.

Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}
be a permutation of the
n+2
indices
0,1,\ldots,n,n+1

such that for
0\leq i<j\leq n+1
,

\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}
if and only if

\{x_{i}\}>\{x_{j}\}
,
which can be found by sorting in
O(n\log n)
time.
For
0\leq i<j\leq n+1
,
denote by
\mathrm{inv}(i,j)
the indicator variable which is
1
when
\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}

and is
0
otherwise.
Then
w(i,j)=\mathrm{inv}(i,j)
.

From this new perspective,
the problem of determining the maximum value
\sigma^{*}
,
such that
the
n
intervals in
\mathcal{I}
can be packed inside
B

by moving
n-\sigma^{*}
intervals and keeping
\sigma^{*}
intervals unmoved,
reduces to
the problem of
finding the maximum length
\sigma^{*}+2
of a sequence
\boldsymbol{s}
of indices

0=s_{0}\prec s_{1}\prec\ldots\prec s_{\sigma^{*}+1}=n+1
,
such that
the number of inversions
\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}
between consecutive indices
i<j
in
\boldsymbol{s}

(which we will call
drops
)
is at most
d^{*}=\lfloor\ell_{B}\rfloor-n
.

Dynamic programming

For
0\leq h\leq i\leq n
,
define
dp(h,i)
as the pair
(d(h,i),p(h,i))
,
where
d(h,i)
is the minimum number of drops
in any sequence
\boldsymbol{s}
of
h+1
indices

0=s_{0}\prec\ldots\prec s_{h}\leq i
,
and
p(h,i)
is the minimum value of
p=\pi_{s_{h}}

over all such sequences
\boldsymbol{s}
with exactly
d=d(h,i)
drops.
If there are no such sequences
\boldsymbol{s}
,
define
dp(h,i)=(\infty,0)
.
For convenience,
also define
dp(h,i)=(\infty,0)
for
h>i
.

The table
dp
can be computed by dynamic programming.
For the base case when
h=0
or
h=i
,
let
dp(0,i)=(0,\pi_{0})

for
0\leq i\leq n
,
and, for
1\leq i\leq n
, let

dp(i,i)=\begin{cases}dp(i-1,i-1)\to i&\textrm{if $i-1\prec i$},\\
(\infty,0)&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

where

(d,p)\to i=\begin{cases}(\infty,0)&\textrm{if $(d,p)=(\infty,0)$},\\
(d,\pi_{i})&\textrm{else if $p<\pi_{i}$},\\
(d+1,\pi_{i})&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then for
0<h<i\leq n
,
in particular, for
h=1,\ldots,n-1
and
i=h+1,\ldots,n
,
we can compute
dp(h,i)
using the recurrence

dp(h,i)=\min\{\,dp(h,i-1),\,dp(h-1,l_{i})\to i\,\}.

Note that
\sigma^{*}
is the maximum
h

such that the
d
-part of

dp(h,l_{n+1})\to n+1

is at most
d^{*}
,
which can be found in
O(n\sigma^{*})
time by a sequential search
for increasing values of
h
.

We can also find
\tau^{*}=n-\sigma^{*}
in
O(n\tau^{*})
time by another sequential search,
for increasing values of
t=i-h
instead of
h
.
First compute
dp(h,i)
for the base case when
h=0
or
h=i
as before,
then compute
dp(h,h+t)

for
t=1,\ldots,n-1
and
h=1,\ldots,n-t
.
Note that
\tau^{*}
is the minimum
t

such that the
d
-part of

dp(n-t,l_{n+1})\to n+1

is at most
d^{*}
.

Thus we have an algorithm for

∗

running in
O(n\log n+n\min\{\sigma^{*},\tau^{*}\})
time.

Algorithms for
Cover
∗

Recall that

\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor
.
For
0\leq i<j\leq n+1
,
define

w(i,j)=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if $\{x_{i}\}<\{x_{j}\}$ or $x_{i}=x_{j}$},\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is easy to check that

B
can be covered by the
n
intervals in
\mathcal{I}

by moving
n-\sigma
intervals and keeping
\sigma
intervals unmoved
if and only if
there is a sequence
\boldsymbol{s}
of
\sigma+2
indices
s_{h}
,
0\leq h\leq\sigma+1
,
where

0=s_{0}<s_{1}<\ldots<s_{\sigma+1}=n+1,

such that the following condition holds:

\sum_{h=0}^{\sigma}\left(\lfloor x_{s_{h+1}}\rfloor-\lfloor x_{s_{h}}\rfloor+w(s_{h},s_{h+1})\right)\leq n+1,

which simplifies to

\displaystyle\lfloor x_{n+1}\rfloor-\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor+\sum_{h=0}^{\sigma}w(s_{h},s_{h+1})

\displaystyle\leq n+1

\displaystyle\lfloor\ell_{B}\rfloor+1+\sum_{h=0}^{\sigma}w(s_{h},s_{h+1})

\displaystyle\leq n+1

\displaystyle\sum_{h=0}^{\sigma}w(s_{h},s_{h+1})

\displaystyle\leq n-\lfloor\ell_{B}\rfloor.

We prove a technical lemma in the following:

Lemma 1
.

For
0\leq i<j\leq n+1
,

(\{x_{i}\},-x_{i},i)<(\{x_{j}\},-x_{j},j)

if and only if

\{x_{i}\}<\{x_{j}\}
or
x_{i}=x_{j}
.

Proof.

We first prove the
only if
implication.
Suppose that

(\{x_{i}\},-x_{i},i)<(\{x_{j}\},-x_{j},j)
.
Then by the lexicographic order,
there are three cases:
either
\{x_{i}\}<\{x_{j}\}
,
or
\{x_{i}\}=\{x_{j}\}
and
x_{i}>x_{j}
,
or
\{x_{i}\}=\{x_{j}\}
and
x_{i}=x_{j}
and
i<j
.
Recall that
x_{i}\leq x_{j}
for
i<j
.
Thus the second case does not hold.
The third case simplifies to
x_{i}=x_{j}
.
In summary, we have
either
\{x_{i}\}<\{x_{j}\}

or
x_{i}=x_{j}
.

We next prove the
if
implication.
Suppose that
\{x_{i}\}<\{x_{j}\}
or
x_{i}=x_{j}
.
If
\{x_{i}\}<\{x_{j}\}
,
then clearly
(\{x_{i}\},-x_{i},i)<(\{x_{j}\},-x_{j},j)
.
If
x_{i}=x_{j}
,
then
\{x_{i}\}=\{x_{j}\}
and
-x_{i}=-x_{j}
,
but since
i<j
, we again have

(\{x_{i}\},-x_{i},i)<(\{x_{j}\},-x_{j},j)
.
∎

Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}
be a permutation of the
n+2
indices
0,1,\ldots,n,n+1

such that for
0\leq i<j\leq n+1
,

\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}
if and only if

(\{x_{i}\},-x_{i},i)<(\{x_{j}\},-x_{j},j)
,
which can be found by sorting in

For
0\leq i<j\leq n+1
,
denote by
\mathrm{inv}(i,j)
the indicator variable which is

when
\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}

and is
0
otherwise.
Then, by Lemma

.

the problem of determining the maximum value

,
such that

B
can be covered by the
n
intervals in
\mathcal{I}

by moving
n-\sigma^{*}
intervals and keeping
\sigma^{*}
intervals unmoved,
reduces to
the problem of
finding the maximum length
\sigma^{*}+2
of a sequence
\boldsymbol{s}
of indices
increasing from

to
n+1
,
such that
the number of inversions

between consecutive indices

in
\boldsymbol{s}

(which we will call

)
is at most
d^{*}=n-\lfloor\ell_{B}\rfloor
.

Dynamic programming

For
0\leq h\leq i\leq n
,
define
dp(h,i)
as the pair
(d(h,i),p(h,i))
,
where

is the minimum number of drops

\boldsymbol{s}
of
h+1

,

over all such sequences

The table

For the base case when

Note that

We can also find

First compute

Note that

is the minimum

such that the

Thus we have an algorithm for

Lagrangian relaxation

We next present an algorithm for
Cover
∗
running in
O(n\log^{2}n)
time.
To do this, we first redesign the dynamic programming algorithm,
then speed it up using Lagrangian relaxation.
The technique of Lagrangian relaxation is also known as
Aliens trick

in the competitive programming community.
Our use of this trick here is inspired
by a related problem
1
1

1

The problem can be translated to the following:
Given a permutation
\boldsymbol{\pi}
of
[1,n]
,
compute for each
0\leq d<n

the maximum length of a sequence
\boldsymbol{s}
of indices
such that the number of inversions
\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}

is at most

One of the solutions by the problem setters
uses Aliens trick and runs in

created by Compton and Qi

.

For
0\leq d\leq i\leq n+1
,
let
\sigma(d,i)
be the maximum

\boldsymbol{s}
of

d
drops.
Let

For convenience, also define
\sigma(d,i)=-\infty
for

.
Our goal is to get
\sigma^{*}=\sigma(d^{*},n+1)
,
where

We can compute

by dynamic programming.
For

\sigma(0,i)

is
2
less than the length of a longest increasing subsequence of
\pi_{0}\ldots\pi_{i}

starting at

and ending at

In particular,
\sigma(0,0)=-1

With the help of some data structures such as balanced search trees,

If

In the following, we assume that

For

we can compute

For the base case,

\sigma(d,0)=\sigma(d-1,0)

For

With balanced search trees, this takes

Thus we can get

We next speed up the algorithm using Aliens trick,
which depends on a property of

stated in the following lemma.
We say that a function

Lemma 2
.

\sigma(d,n+1)
is concave for
1\leq d\leq n+1
.

The trick is to assign a penalty

from

to

we will compute
\sigma(d,i)-d\lambda

and get

indirectly

\sigma(d^{*},n+1)-d^{*}\lambda+d^{*}\lambda

by a binary search for a suitable
\lambda
.

For any
\lambda\geq 0
, and for
1\leq i\leq n+1
,
let

\sigma_{\lambda}(i)=\max\{\,\sigma(d,i)-d\lambda\mid 1\leq d\leq i\,\},

and correspondingly,

be the minimum

,

such that

\sigma(d,i)-d\lambda=\sigma_{\lambda}(i)

Consider

and
\lambda

Geometrically,

(d,\sigma(d,n+1))

As

the vector

rotates from

direction to

For any fixed
\lambda

(d,\sigma(d,n+1))
onto the same vector
(-\lambda,1)
.

For each
d
,

,

and

\lambda

\sigma(d,n+1)-d\lambda=\sigma(d+1,n+1)-(d+1)\lambda,

which has an integer solution,

\lambda_{d}=\sigma(d+1,n+1)-\sigma(d,n+1).

By Lemma
2
,

.
Since any sequence with at most

.
Also, since

, and

,

.
Thus

Thus for each

,
1\leq d\leq n+1
,
there is a non-empty range of integer values for
\lambda
,
between

and

,

Specifically, these ranges are

for

\lambda_{d}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{d-1}
for

for

Correspondingly,

is decreasing for

for

to

,
and
to

\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda\leq n

Some of these ranges may be empty,

\lambda
increases from

to

But we always have

for

.
Recall our assumption that

.

Let

be

\lambda

.
Then
\lambda^{*}=\lambda_{d^{*}}
,

It remains to find

.
Recall that {

can be computed in

and subsequently

After these two preliminary steps,

For each such

Then, for

use the recurrence
