On the optimal rate of vortex stretching for axisymmetric Euler flows without swirl
Deokwoo Lim, In-Jee Jeong

Abstract
. For axisymmetric flows without swirl and compactly supported initial vorticity, we prove the upper bound of t 4 / 3 t^{4/3} for the growth of the vorticity maximum, which was conjectured by Childress [Phys. D, 2008] and supported by numerical computations from Childress–Gilbert–Valiant [J. Fluid Mech. 2016]. The key is to estimate the velocity maximum by the kinetic energy together with conserved quantities involving the vorticity.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study the optimal rate of
vortex stretching
for one of the simplest three-dimensional flows, namely axisymmetric flows without swirl. Let us first recall the 3D vorticity equation for incompressible and inviscid flows: for vorticity
\omega(t,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega=\omega\cdot\nabla u,\\
&u=\nabla\times(-\Delta)^{-1}\omega,\\
&\omega|_{t=0}=\omega_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

The term
\omega\cdot\nabla u
in the vorticity equation is referred to as the
vortex stretching
term, and the Biot–Savart law
u=\nabla\times(-\Delta)^{-1}\omega
is given explicitly in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by

(1.2)

\begin{split}u(t,x)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}K_{3}(x-y)\times\omega(t,y)dy,\quad K_{3}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{x}{|x|^{3}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.\end{split}

Let us reduce (
1.1
) by taking axisymmetric flows without swirl: this means to take the ansatz

(1.3)

u=u^{r}(r,z)e^{r}+u^{z}(r,z)e^{z},\qquad\omega=\omega^{\theta}(r,z)e^{\theta}=(-\partial_{z}u^{r}+\partial_{r}u^{z})e^{\theta}

in the cylindrical coordinates
(e^{r},e^{\theta},e^{z})
. Then, (
1.1
) simplifies to

(1.4)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\omega^{\theta}+(u^{r}\partial_{r}+u^{z}\partial_{z})\omega^{\theta}&=\frac{u^{r}}{r}\omega^{\theta},\end{split}

and note that the vortex stretching term has been reduced to
r^{-1}u^{r}\omega^{\theta}
in (
1.4
). Still, depending on the regularity of
\omega^{\theta}(t=0)
, this term can lead to either instantaneous, finite-time, or infinite-time
infinite growth
of the vorticity maximum
\|\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
(
[
18
,
19
,
28
,
10
,
21
,
15
]
).

1.1.
Main result

Our main result gives an upper bound for the growth rate of
\|\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
for smooth data, which is believed to be optimal, see the discussion below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
\omega^{\theta}_{0}
is compactly supported and
\|r^{-1}\omega^{\theta}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}
is bounded. Then the corresponding unique global-in-time solution
\omega(t,\cdot)
of (
1.1
) belonging to
L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R};L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
with the initial data
\omega_{0}=\omega^{\theta}_{0}e^{\theta}
satisfies, for some constant
A(\omega_{0}^{\theta})>0
depending only on
\omega_{0}^{\theta}
,

(1.5)

\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq A(\omega_{0}^{\theta})(1+|t|)^{4/3}\quad\text{for all}\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Remark 1.1
.

Any smooth and compactly supported initial vorticity satisfies the assumption of Theorem
1.1
; we have
\|r^{-1}\omega^{\theta}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}<\infty
once
\omega_{0}=\omega^{\theta}_{0}e^{\theta}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).
The constant
A(\omega_{0}^{\theta})
depends only on
\|\omega^{\theta}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}
,
\|r^{-1}\omega^{\theta}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}
,
\|r^{-1}\omega^{\theta}_{0}\|_{L^{1}}
, and
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}
. Since
\omega^{\theta}_{0}
is compactly supported,
\|r^{-1}\omega^{\theta}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}<\infty
implies that
\|\omega_{0}^{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}}
and
\|r^{-1}\omega^{\theta}_{0}\|_{L^{p}}
(for any
p<\infty
) are bounded. Furthermore, from the fast decay of axisymmetric Biot–Savart kernel (see §
2.2
below), the initial velocity belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

1.2.
Wellposedness for axisymmetric Euler without swirl

In this section, let us clarify the wellposedness issue for (
1.4
) as well as (
1.1
). A comprehensive survey of wellposedness results for (
1.1
) can be found in
[
3
,

17

To begin with, global regularity of axisymmetric Euler without swirl (
1.4
) goes back at least to the work of Ukhovskii–Yudovich
[
42
]
in 1968, where global in time existence, uniqueness, propagation of regularity was shown assuming that

\begin{split}\omega_{0}\in H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\qquad\frac{\omega_{0}}{r}\in(L^{2}\cap L^{\infty})(\mathbb{R}^{3})\end{split}

with
m\geq 2
. Such a regularity assumption on
r^{-1}\omega_{0}
was natural, since (
1.4
) could be written as

(1.6)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\,\frac{\omega^{\theta}}{r}+(u^{r}\partial_{r}+u^{z}\partial_{z})\,\frac{\omega^{\theta}}{r}=0.\end{split}

In particular, we have the a priori estimate

\begin{split}\left\|\frac{\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)}{r}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\left\|\frac{\omega^{\theta}_{0}}{r}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\end{split}

for any
0\leq p\leq\infty
by incompressibility.
1
1

1

In the case
p=0
, it means the conservation of the volume of the support of
\omega^{\theta}
.
Therefore, this work already clarified that once the initial datum is sufficiently regular, the corresponding solution to (

On the other hand, local in time existence and uniqueness for the full 3D Euler equations (

[

\omega_{0}\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

\alpha>0

\omega_{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

These local regularity results guarantee that once

In two dimensions, the (scalar) vorticity is simply being advected by the flow, and the celebrated Yudovich theorem

where

1.3.
Optimal vorticity growth and eroding dipole model by Childress

It is well known to experimental physicists that vortex stretching occurs when two anti-parallel vortex rings move towards each other. This so-called “head on collision” scenario has been investigated intensively (
[
29
,
33
,
37
,
41
,
14
]
), where it was observed that the maximal vortex strength increases together with the Reynolds number. A simple but illuminating mathematical model of this scenario can be given based on (
1.4
) (formally taking the Reynolds number to be infinite), by taking the initial data to be of the form

(1.7)

\begin{split}{\omega_{0}^{\theta}}(r,z)=-\varphi(r,z)+\varphi(r,-z),\end{split}

where
\varphi\geq 0
is a bump function supported near some point in
(r,z)
, say
(1,1)
. This models two anti-parallel vortex rings, and the key features are that the vorticity is odd symmetric in
z
and non-positive for
z\geq 0
. Under these two assumptions, it can be proved that (
[
10
,
21
]
)

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\iint_{\Pi}r^{2}|\omega^{\theta}(t,r,z)|drdz>0,\qquad\frac{d}{dt}\iint_{\Pi}|z\omega^{\theta}(t,r,z)|drdz<0\end{split}

where
\Pi:=\left\{(r,z):r\geq 0\right\}
. This shows that the vorticity moves southeast in the
(r,z)
-plane in average. Actually, direct numerical simulations of (
1.4
) reveals that for initial data of the type (
1.7
), the solution
\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)
rather quickly attaches to the plane
\{z=0\}
as it slides outwards in the
r
-axis (
[
5
,
39
,
38
]
). Furthermore, in all of the aforementioned vorticity growth results (
[
18
,
19
,
28
,
10
,
21
,
15
]
), the data indeed share the same symmetry and sign condition with (
1.7
): it is essential to have
u^{r}>0
for vorticity growth.

This is precisely the setup that Childress and his collaborators investigated in detail (
[
6
,
7
,
9
,
8
]
). In
[
6
, Theorem 1]
, Childress proves the upper bound
4
4

4

The initial data can be general and not restricted to the form given in (
1.7
).

(1.8)

\begin{split}\|\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq C(1+t^{2}),\end{split}

by solving the following variational optimization problem: at time
t
, assuming that
\omega^{\theta}
is supported near some radius
R\gg 1
, what is the optimal shape of
\omega^{\theta}
which maximizes its radial velocity
u^{r}
, under a constraint on the volume of the support of
\omega^{\theta}
(which is conserved in time)?

Following
[
6
,
7
]
,
t^{2}
can be easily explained as follows: assume that the vorticity at time
t
is localized near the point
(R(t),0)
with
L(t)
as its own length scale. Assuming that
L(t)\ll R(t)
, the velocity on the support of vorticity scales as
R(t)L(t)
: roughly speaking, this is because the velocity can be obtained by “integrating” the vorticity (which has size
\sim R(t)
) over a domain with length scale
\sim L(t)
. Then the volume conservation imposes the restriction
R(t)L^{2}(t)\lesssim 1
, or
L(t)\lesssim(R(t))^{-1/2}
. Then, from

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}R(t)\sim\frac{d}{dt}\|\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\|u^{r}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)))}\sim R(t)L(t)\lesssim(R(t))^{1/2},\end{split}

we arrive at (
1.8
).

Furthermore, in the same papers, Childress makes an important observation that this scenario contradicts the conservation of kinetic energy, namely
\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
. Indeed, using that the support volume scales like
R(t)L^{2}(t)
and the velocity
R(t)L(t)
, we have

\begin{split}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\gtrsim R^{3}(t)L^{4}(t),\end{split}

and the scaling
L(t)\sim(R(t))^{-1/2}
clearly makes the right hand side divergent as
R(t)\to\infty
. To fix this, we need to have
L(t)\lesssim(R(t))^{-3/4}
, which gives instead

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}R(t)\lesssim(R(t))^{1/4}.\end{split}

Based on these considerations, Childress conjectured in
[
6
,
7
]
that the maximal growth rate is given by
t^{4/3}
, which is confirmed in our Theorem
1.1
. Later, Childress–Gilbert–Valiant
[
9
]
presents numerical experiments which supports that the growth rate of
t^{4/3}
can be indeed achieved. They also report that the optimal growth is attained by rescaled Sadovskii vortex patch–see
[
13
,
23
]
for more discussion related to this point.

Finally, we observe that
L(t)\lesssim(R(t))^{-3/4}
implies that the volume of the dipole support in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which is
R(t)L^{2}(t)\lesssim(R(t))^{-1/2}
, goes to zero as
R(t)\to\infty.
Since the total volume of the vorticity support remains constant, it means that most of the volume filaments behind (this is what we have attempted to depict in Figure
1
). Hence the terminology “eroding” dipole in
[
6
,
7
]
is appropriate.

1.4.
Velocity bounds for axisymmetric flows: the proof strategy

Let us now review existing bounds on the velocity for axisymmetric flows and then discuss our proof strategy.

Exponential upper bound

To begin with, it is relatively straightforward to obtain the exponential in time upper bound for the vorticity. This can be done in a number of ways: in
[
16
]
, the following estimate was established:

\begin{split}\left\|\frac{u^{r}}{r}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\left\|\frac{\omega^{\theta}}{r}\right\|_{L^{3,1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.\end{split}

Using that the right hand side is conserved in time, one obtains the vorticity bound from (
1.4
)

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\|\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\left\|\frac{u^{r}(t,\cdot)}{r}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\|\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\|\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\end{split}

which results in an exponential in time upper bound, using Gronwall’s inequality. Another estimate (assuming compact support of
\omega^{\theta}
) from Majda–Bertozzi
[
35
]
is
\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
, which just uses the conservation of the volume of the support for
\omega^{\theta}
. This again results in an exponential upper bound for
\|\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
. To the best of our knowledge, before the work of Childress, the exponential bound was still the best one.

Feng–Sverak estimate and the
t^{2}
bound again

A very interesting inequality for axisymmetric flows by Feng–Sverak
[
20
]
in 2015 gives an alternative, simple proof of the
t^{2}
upper bound (although the authors had a completely different motivation). Their inequality reads

(1.9)

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\bigg\|\frac{\omega^{\theta}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\bigg\|\frac{\omega^{\theta}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}\|r\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}

(where
u=(u^{r},u^{z})
) and given this inequality, one can estimate

\begin{split}\|r\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}\leq(R(t))^{1/2}\bigg\|\frac{\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}\end{split}

and with conservation of
\|r^{-1}\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{p}}
, one obtains

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}R(t)\lesssim(R(t))^{1/2},\end{split}

which immediately gives the
t^{2}
upper bound.
5
5

5

Let us clarify that the quantity
\|r\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{1}}
is conserved only when
\omega^{\theta}
is
single-signed
, namely if it is non-negative or non-positive everywhere in
\Pi
. Indeed, for single-signed vorticity, one concludes from (
1.9
) that the velocity is uniformly bounded in space and time, which gives the linear bound
R(t)\lesssim 1+t
. Actually, in the single-signed case, the work of Maffei–Marchioro
[
34
]
in 2001 provides an upper bound of the form
R(t)\lesssim 1+t^{1/4}\log(e+t)
.

Actually there is a reason why a sharp inequality on
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}
just involving
L^{p}
norms of
\omega^{\theta}
(possibly with
r
weights) results in the same answer with Childress’ variational proof. This requires an understanding of how the exponents in the right hand side of (
1.9
) are determined. This is non-trivial since three norms appear, and for the inequality

(1.10)

\begin{split}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\bigg\|\frac{\omega^{\theta}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{\alpha}\bigg\|\frac{\omega^{\theta}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{\beta}\|r\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{\gamma}\end{split}

to hold, we clearly need

•

0\leq\alpha,\beta,\gamma
and
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1
(homogeneity)

•

2\alpha-\beta-3\gamma=0
(3D scaling law).

We need one more relation to determine the exponents, which comes from
two-dimensionalization
: for (
1.10
) to hold, it should be true in particular when
\omega^{\theta}
is sharply concentrated near a single point in
\Pi
, say
(1,0)
. In that case, (
1.10
) simply reduces to

(1.11)

\begin{split}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\|{\omega^{\theta}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{\alpha}\|\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{{\beta+\gamma}},\end{split}

and the punchline is that for such concentrated vortices, the 3D Biot–Savart kernel (
1.2
) is asymptotic to the 2D Biot–Savart law. Therefore, (
1.11
) must obey the 2D scaling law, which is

•

\alpha=1/2=\beta+\gamma
.

This last part was indeed the essential observation in the
t^{2}
proof of Childress: the velocity scales like
R(t)L(t)
, exactly as in 2D fluids.

Global velocity bound using kinetic energy and the
t^{3/2}
bound

Given that the kinetic energy conservation plays the key role in the
t^{4/3}
conjecture of Childress, it was very natural for us to attempt to get a Feng–Sverak type inequality which features
\|u\|_{L^{2}}
in the right hand side. After a lot of trial and error, we could finally prove the following inequality (see Proposition
A.1
below)

(1.12)

\|u^{r}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/3}\bigg\|\frac{\omega^{\theta}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\|r\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/6}

where the exponents on the right hand side can be explained as in the case of (
1.9
). Now that the exponent of
\|r\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{1}}
is lower, it gives a better bound which is
t^{3/2}
. Unfortunately, this is still worse than
t^{4/3}
, and we were unable to lower the exponent
1/6
in (
1.12
).
6
6

6

An elementary algebraic computation shows that
1/6
is the smallest power of
\|r\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{1}}
that one can use, under the assumption that on the right hand side, one is using
\|r\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{1}}
together with
\|u\|_{L^{2}}
and
\|r^{-1}\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{p}}
for all possible values of
p
.

Before we proceed to explaining the proof for
t^{4/3}
bound, we note that the
t^{3/2}
upper bound is the best we can obtain

the compact support assumption for the vorticity. We still need some decay, which is
r\omega_{0}^{\theta}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that
\omega_{0}^{\theta}
and
r^{-1}\omega_{0}^{\theta}
are in
(L^{1}\cap L^{\infty})(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and that
r\omega_{0}^{\theta}
is in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Then the corresponding unique global-in-time solution
\omega(t,\cdot)
of (
1.1
) belonging to
L_{loc}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};(L^{1}\cap L^{\infty})(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
with the initial data
\omega_{0}=\omega_{0}^{\theta}e^{\theta}
satisfies
r\omega\in L_{loc}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
and, for some constant
A(\omega_{0}^{\theta})>0
depending only on
\omega_{0}^{\theta}
,

(1.13)

\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq A(\omega_{0}^{\theta})(1+|t|)^{3/2}\quad\text{for all}\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

We shall prove this result in Appendix
A
after the proof of Proposition
A.1
.

Strategy and heuristic for the
t^{4/3}
bound

At a heuristic level, it is easy to see why (
1.12
) does not give the optimal upper bound: in the eroding dipole model of Childress, the quantity
\|r\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{1}}
measured
locally
on the leading dipole should decay in time due to filamentation. The question was how to make this observation precise and obtain the
t^{4/3}
bound.

To begin with, while the inequality (
1.12
) is interesting in its own, it is wasteful in the sense that for the growth of the vorticity maximum, we only need to obtain a bound on
u^{r}(t,\cdot)
at the maximal support radius of
\omega^{\theta}(t,\cdot)
, which we denote by
R(t)
.

The support of vorticity is contained in the “rectangle”
(r,z)\in[0,R(t)]\times\mathbb{R}
, and it turns out to be useful to divide the rectangle into two regions:
axial regime
(
(I_{1})
in Figure
2
) and
two-dimensional regime
(
(I_{21})\cup(I_{22})
in Figure
2
).

First, estimating the contribution to
u^{r}
coming from the axial regime is easier for two reasons: this region is relatively far away from the point
(R(t),0)
and the axisymmetric Biot–Savart kernel enjoys enhanced decay relative to the two-dimensional one (see Lemma
2.1
). Furthermore, we have bounds on
\|r^{-1}\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{p}}
-norms, which says that the vorticity is small for
r
small. Based on these, we were able to bound the contribution to
\|u^{r}\|_{L^{\infty}}
coming from the axial regime simply by

\begin{split}\bigg\|\frac{\omega^{\theta}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/3}\bigg\|\frac{\omega^{\theta}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2/3}\end{split}

which is conserved in time: this regime cannot contribute to any superlinear growth of
R(t)
.

Therefore, the key was to understand the two-dimensional regime in detail. For this purpose, it is actually very helpful to consider the two-dimensional Euler equations. Denoting
\omega_{2D}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
as the scalar vorticity on the plane and
u_{2D}
the corresponding velocity given by

\begin{split}u_{2D}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{(x-y)^{\perp}}{|x-y|^{2}}\omega_{2D}(y)dy,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\end{split}

as a warm up one can actually prove the following:

\begin{split}\|u_{2D}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim\|u_{2D}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\|\omega_{2D}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}.\end{split}

Believing in this inequality and two-dimensionalization of the axisymmetric Biot–Savart kernel, one may expect to have something like

\begin{split}\|u^{r}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\lesssim\|u\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{1/2}\|\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}^{1/2}\end{split}

where
U\subset\Pi
is a small ball centered at
(R(t),0)
. Now to turn the
L^{2}(U)
norm into the
L^{2}
norm measured in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we need to incorporate the metric factor:

\begin{split}\|u\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{1/2}\sim R^{-1/4}\|r^{1/2}u\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{1/2}\lesssim R^{-1/4}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}.\end{split}

Moreover, noting that
\|\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}^{1/2}\sim R^{1/2}\|r^{-1}\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}^{1/2}\lesssim R^{1/2}\|r^{-1}\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}
, one obtains

\begin{split}\|u\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{1/2}\|\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}^{1/2}\lesssim R^{1/4}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\|r^{-1}\omega^{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2},\end{split}

which says that the contribution coming from the two-dimensional regime is bounded by
R^{1/4}
.

Therefore, if one could justify all these heuristic discussions, one would obtain the inequality

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}R(t)\sim\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\,u^{r}(t,R(t),z)\lesssim 1+(R(t))^{1/4},\end{split}

which finally gives the
t^{4/3}
bound. The formal statement and proof for this key inequality are given in §
3.1
below.

Axisymmetric Biot–Savart law

We recall a very convenient form of the axisymmetric Biot–Savart law derived in Feng–Sverak

where we recall from §

that

\begin{split}u^{z}(r,z)=\iint_{\Pi}F^{z}(r,z,\overline{r},\overline{z})\omega^{\theta}(\overline{r},\overline{z})d\overline{z}d\overline{r}\end{split}

where

\begin{split}&F^{z}(r,z,\overline{r},\overline{z})=\frac{r-\overline{r}}{\pi r^{3/2}\overline{r}^{1/2}}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{D^{2}}{r\overline{r}}\bigg)+\frac{\overline{r}^{1/2}}{4\pi r^{3/2}}\bigg[\mathcal{F}\bigg(\frac{D^{2}}{r\overline{r}}\bigg)-\frac{2D^{2}}{r\overline{r}}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{D^{2}}{r\overline{r}}\bigg)\bigg].\end{split}

Later, we shall obtain bounds on the kernel

and its derivatives by the following result.

(
[

)
.

For any integer

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We denote the maximal radial support of the solution

of (

) with initial data

as

, with

. Then for any

using conservations of the kinetic energy

and the

. This gives us the bound

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We use the same notation of

) to obtain

\displaystyle{|(I_{12}^{\prime\prime})|}

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint_{\Pi}\frac{\overline{r}^{3/2}}{{D_{1,0}^{4}}}\chi^{\prime}(2{D_{1,0}})\frac{|\overline{z}|}{{D_{1,0}}}(\overline{r}^{1/2}|u^{r}|)\lesssim\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{1/4}(1,0)}}\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}^{4}}}(\overline{r}^{1/2}|u^{r}|).

The second inequality follows from the fact that the integrand above is supported on the region
{D_{1,0}}\sim 1
. Finishing this estimate, we have

(A.12)

\displaystyle{|(I_{12}^{\prime\prime})|}

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg(\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{1/4}(1,0)}}\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}^{8}}}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{1/4}(1,0)}}\overline{r}|u^{r}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

To estimate the term (
A.9
),
we use the inequality

|(\overline{r}-1)(\overline{r}+1)-\overline{z}^{2}|^{1/2}\lesssim D_{1,0},

which holds on the region
\Pi\setminus B_{1/4}(1,0)
.
We use this to estimate the term (
A.9
) as

(A.13)

\displaystyle|\partial_{\overline{r}}F^{r}(1,0,\overline{r},\overline{z})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{|\overline{z}|}{\overline{r}^{5/2}}\bigg[\overline{r}\frac{\overline{r}^{5/2}}{{D_{1,0}^{5}}}+|(\overline{r}-1)(\overline{r}+1)-\overline{z}^{2}|\frac{\overline{r}^{7/2}}{{D_{1,0}^{7}}}\bigg]\lesssim\frac{\overline{r}}{{D_{1,0}^{4}}},

where it holds on the region
\Pi\setminus B_{1/4}(1,0)
as well.
Using this, we get

(A.14)

\displaystyle{|(I_{13}^{\prime\prime})|}

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint_{{\Pi\setminus B_{1/4}(1,0)}}\frac{\overline{r}^{1/2}}{{D_{1,0}^{4}}}(\overline{r}^{1/2}|u^{{z}}|)\lesssim\iint_{{\Pi\setminus B_{1/4}(1,0)}}\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}^{7/2}}}(\overline{r}^{1/2}|u^{{z}}|)\lesssim{\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}}.

Lastly, we obtain

(A.15)

\displaystyle{|(I_{14}^{\prime\prime})|}

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint_{\Pi}\frac{\overline{r}^{3/2}}{{D_{1,0}^{4}}}\chi^{\prime}(2{D_{1,0}})\frac{|\overline{r}-1|}{{D_{1,0}}}(\overline{r}^{1/2}|u^{{z}}|)\lesssim\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{1/4}(1,0)}}\frac{\overline{r}^{1/2}|u^{{z}}|}{{D_{1,0}^{4}}}\lesssim{\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}}.

Now we may collect the estimates above to conclude

\displaystyle|(I_{1}^{\prime\prime})|

\displaystyle=|(I_{1}^{\prime\prime})|^{1/3}|(I_{1}^{\prime\prime})|^{2/3}\lesssim{\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/3}\bigg(\bigg\|\frac{\omega}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{3/4}\|r\omega\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}\bigg)^{2/3}}

(A.16)

\displaystyle=\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/3}\bigg\|\frac{\omega}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\|r\omega\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/6}.

Estimate of
(I_{2}^{\prime\prime})
: splitting further.
To estimate
(I_{2}^{\prime\prime})
, we define the ratio

(A.17)

k={\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{{2/3}}}{\bigg\|\frac{\omega}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{{-1/2}}}{\|r\omega\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{{-1/6}},}

and split
(I_{2}^{\prime\prime})
into

\begin{split}\underbrace{\iint_{\Pi}F^{r}(1-\phi_{k})\phi_{1/2}\omega}_{=:(I_{21}^{\prime\prime})}+\underbrace{\iint_{\Pi}F^{r}\phi_{k}\phi_{1/2}\omega}_{=:(I_{22}^{\prime\prime})}.\end{split}

Estimate of
(I_{22}^{\prime\prime})
.
We define
\bar{k}:=\min\{k,1\}
and estimate
(I_{22}^{\prime\prime})
as

(A.18)

\displaystyle|(I_{22}^{\prime\prime})|

\displaystyle{\leq}\iint_{{B_{\bar{k}}(1,0)}}|F^{r}\omega|\lesssim\iint_{{B_{\bar{k}}(1,0)}}\frac{\overline{r}^{1/2}}{{D_{1,0}}}|\omega|^{3/4}|\omega|^{1/4}\frac{\overline{r}^{3/4}}{\overline{r}^{3/4}}

(A.19)

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg\|\frac{\omega}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}({B_{\bar{k}}(1,0)})}^{3/4}\bigg(\iint_{{B_{\bar{k}}(1,0)}}\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}^{4/3}}}\bigg)^{3/4}\bigg(\iint_{{B_{\bar{k}}(1,0)}}{\overline{r}^{2}}|\omega|\bigg)^{1/4}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\bar{k}}\bigg\|\frac{\omega}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}({B_{\bar{k}}(1,0)})}^{3/4}\|r^{2}\omega\|_{L^{1}({B_{\bar{k}}(1,0)})}^{1/4}\lesssim\sqrt{\bar{k}}\bigg\|\frac{\omega}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{3/4}\|r\omega\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}

(A.20)

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/3}\bigg\|\frac{\omega}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\|r\omega\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/6}.

Estimate of
(I_{21}^{\prime\prime})
.
Let us assume
k<1
because
(I_{21}^{\prime\prime})
just vanishes otherwise. We use integration by parts to get

(A.21)

\begin{split}(I_{21}^{\prime\prime})=&\ \iint_{\Pi}\Big[\ \big[\partial_{\overline{z}}\big(F^{r}(1-\phi_{k})\phi_{1/2}\big)\big]u^{r}-\big[\partial_{\overline{r}}\big(F^{r}(1-\phi_{k})\phi_{1/2}\big)\big]u^{z}\Big]\\
=&\ {\underbrace{\iint_{\Pi}(\partial_{\overline{z}}F^{r})(1-\phi_{k})\phi_{1/2}u^{r}}_{=:(I_{211}^{\prime\prime})}-\underbrace{\iint_{\Pi}F^{r}(\partial_{\overline{z}}\phi_{k})\phi_{1/2}u^{r}}_{=:(I_{212}^{\prime\prime})}+\underbrace{\iint_{\Pi}F^{r}(1-\phi_{k})(\partial_{\overline{z}}\phi_{1/2})u^{r}}_{=:(I_{213}^{\prime\prime})}}\\
&{-\underbrace{\iint_{\Pi}(\partial_{\overline{r}}F^{r})(1-\phi_{k})\phi_{1/2}u^{z}}_{=:(I_{214}^{\prime\prime})}+\underbrace{\iint_{\Pi}F^{r}(\partial_{\overline{r}}\phi_{k})\phi_{1/2}u^{z}}_{=:(I_{215}^{\prime\prime})}-\underbrace{\iint_{\Pi}F^{r}(1-\phi_{k})(\partial_{\overline{r}}\phi_{1/2})u^{z}}_{=:(I_{216}^{\prime\prime})}.}\end{split}

Now let us estimate each term. First, we get

\displaystyle{|(I_{211}^{\prime\prime})|}

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{k/2}(1,0)}}\frac{\overline{r}^{1/2}}{{D_{1,0}^{2}}}|u^{r}|

(A.22)

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{k/2}(1,0)}}\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}^{4}}}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{k/2}(1,0)}}{\overline{r}}|u^{r}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim\frac{1}{k}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

Next, we have

\displaystyle{|(I_{212}^{\prime\prime})|}

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)}}\frac{\overline{r}^{1/2}}{{D_{1,0}}}\frac{1}{k}\chi^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{{D_{1,0}}}{k}\bigg)\frac{|\overline{z}|}{{D_{1,0}}}|u^{r}|\lesssim\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{k/2}(1,0)}}\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}^{2}}}{\overline{r}^{1/2}}|u^{r}|.

(A.23)

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{k/2}(1,0)}}\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}^{4}}}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{k/2}(1,0)}}{\overline{r}}|u^{r}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim{\frac{1}{k}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}}.

In the second inequality, we used the relation
{D_{1,0}}\sim k
, which holds because of the term
\partial_{\overline{z}}\phi_{k}
.
Also, we use the relation
D_{1,0}\sim 1
coming from the term
\partial_{\overline{z}}\phi_{1/2}
to get

(A.24)

\displaystyle{|(I_{213}^{\prime\prime})|}

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint_{{\Pi\setminus B_{k/2}(1,0)}}\frac{\overline{r}^{1/2}}{{D_{1,0}}}\chi^{\prime}(2{D_{1,0}})\frac{|\overline{z}|}{{D_{1,0}}}|u^{r}|\lesssim\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{k/2}(1,0)}}\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}^{2}}}{\overline{r}^{1/2}}|u^{r}|\lesssim{\frac{1}{k}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}}.

Now we can use the inequality
|(\overline{r}-1)(\overline{r}+1)-\overline{z}^{2}|\lesssim{D_{1,0}}
on the disc
B_{1/2}(1,0)
to get

(A.25)

\displaystyle|\partial_{\overline{r}}F^{r}(1,0,\overline{r},\overline{z})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{|\overline{z}|}{\overline{r}^{5/2}}\bigg[\overline{r}\frac{\overline{r}}{{D_{1,0}^{2}}}+|(\overline{r}-1)(\overline{r}+1)-\overline{z}^{2}|\frac{\overline{r}^{2}}{{D_{1,0}^{4}}}\bigg]\lesssim\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}}}+\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}^{2}}}\lesssim\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}^{2}}}.

Using this, we have

(A.26)

\displaystyle{|(I_{214}^{\prime\prime})|}

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{k/2}(1,0)}}\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}^{2}}}|u^{{z}}|\lesssim\iint_{{B_{1/2}(1,0)\setminus B_{k/2}(1,0)}}\frac{1}{{D_{1,0}^{2}}}{\overline{r}^{1/2}}|u^{{z}}|{\lesssim\frac{1}{k}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}}

and similarly, we get

(A.27)

{|(I_{215}^{\prime\prime})|+|(I_{216}^{\prime\prime})|}\lesssim{\frac{1}{k}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}}.

Thus, gathering all the estimates above, we obtain

(A.28)

\begin{split}|(I_{21}^{\prime\prime})|&{\lesssim\frac{1}{k}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/3}\bigg\|\frac{\omega}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\|r\omega\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/6}.}\end{split}

We used the definition of
k
in the last inequality.

Conclusion.
Summing up (
A.20
) and (
A.28
), we have

(A.29)

{|(I_{2}^{\prime\prime})|\lesssim\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/3}\bigg\|\frac{\omega}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\|r\omega\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/6}.}

Hence, adding (
A.16
) and (
A.29
), the proof is done.
∎

Now we prove Theorem
1.2
using Proposition
A.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We let
t\geq 0
, and we define the
length
function
L(t)
as

(A.30)

L(t):=1+\int_{0}^{t}\|u^{r}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}ds.

Claim
. We have the estimates

(A.31)

\displaystyle\|r\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\bigg\|\frac{\omega_{0}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|r\omega_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\bigg)L(t)^{2},

(A.32)

\displaystyle\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\bigg\|\frac{\omega_{0}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\bigg)L(t).

Once this
Claim
is shown, then (
A.31
) and conservation of
\|u\|_{L^{2}}
and the
L^{p}
-norms of
r^{-1}\omega
with
p\in[1,\infty]
imply that we have

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}L(t)&=\|u^{r}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\\
&\lesssim\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/3}\bigg\|\frac{\omega(t,\cdot)}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\|r\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/6}\\
&\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/3}\bigg\|\frac{\omega_{0}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\bigg(\bigg\|\frac{\omega_{0}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|r\omega_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\bigg)^{1/6}L(t)^{1/3}\lesssim_{\omega_{0}}L(t)^{1/3}.\end{split}

From this, we have

L(t)\lesssim_{\omega_{0}}(1+t)^{3/2},

where this and the second estimate (
A.32
) from our claim give us the desired estimate

(A.33)

\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim_{\omega_{0}}(1+t)^{3/2}.

It only remains to prove the
Claim
. To do this, we use the flow map
\Phi_{t}
which solves the ODE

\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{t}(a)=u(t,\Phi_{t}(a)),\quad\Phi_{0}(a)=a.

We let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and use notations
r_{x}:=(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{1/2}
and
z_{x}:=x_{3}
. In addition, we denote
\Phi_{t}^{r}
and
\Phi_{t}^{z}
as the radial and vertical component of
\Phi_{t}
, respectively. Also, we take
y=(\Phi_{t})^{-1}(x)
, where
(\Phi_{t})^{-1}
is the inverse map of
\Phi_{t}
. Then note that we have the following equality:

x=\Phi_{t}(y)=\Phi_{0}(y)+\int_{0}^{t}u(s,\Phi_{s}(y))ds=y+\int_{0}^{t}u(s,\Phi_{s}(y))ds,

where its radial component would be written as

(A.34)

r_{x}=\Phi_{t}^{r}(y)=r_{y}+\int_{0}^{t}u^{r}(s,\Phi_{s}(y))ds.

Additionally, we can use the fact that the quantity
r^{-1}\omega
is conserved in time along the flow map
\Phi_{t}
:

(A.35)

\frac{\omega(t,x)}{r_{x}}=\frac{\omega(t,\Phi_{t}(y))}{\Phi_{t}^{r}(y)}=\frac{\omega_{0}(y)}{r_{y}}.

To prove the first estimate (
A.31
), we define the set
\Omega(t):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:r_{x}\leq L(t)\}
and split the integral range of the
L^{1}
-norm of
r\omega(t,\cdot)
into

\|r\omega(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega(t)}r_{x}|\omega(t,x)|dx}_{=:(A)}+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega(t)^{c}}r_{x}|\omega(t,x)|dx}_{=:(B)}.

The term
(A)
is estimated as

(A.36)

\begin{split}(A)&=\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{|\omega(t,x)|}{r_{x}}r_{x}^{2}dx\leq\bigg(\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{|\omega(t,x)|}{r_{x}}dx\bigg)L(t)^{2}\\
&\leq\bigg\|\frac{\omega_{0}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}L(t)^{2}.\end{split}

To estimate the term
(B)
, we observe that we have the relation

(A.37)

\begin{split}(\Phi_{t})^{-1}(\Omega(t)^{c})&=\{(\Phi_{t})^{-1}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x\in\Omega(t)^{c}\}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:r_{x}>L(t)\}\\
&\subset\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:r_{y}>1\}.\end{split}

Also, in the region
\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:r_{y}>1\}
, we have the inequality

(A.38)

\frac{\Phi_{t}^{r}(y)}{r_{y}}=1+\frac{1}{r_{y}}\int_{0}^{t}u^{r}(s,\Phi_{s}(y))ds\leq 1+\int_{0}^{t}u^{r}(s,\Phi_{s}(y))ds\leq L(t).

Using these two observations, we can estimate the term
(B)
:

(A.39)

\begin{split}(B)&=\int_{\Omega(t)^{c}}\frac{|\omega(t,x)|}{r_{x}}r_{x}^{2}dx=\int_{(\Phi_{t})^{-1}(\Omega(t)^{c})}\frac{|\omega_{0}(y)|}{r_{y}}[\Phi_{t}^{r}(y)]^{2}dy\\
&\leq\int_{\{r_{y}>1\}}\frac{|\omega_{0}(y)|}{r_{y}}r_{y}^{2}\bigg[\frac{\Phi_{t}^{r}(y)}{r_{y}}\bigg]^{2}dy\leq\|r\omega_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}L(t)^{2}.\end{split}

Gathering estimates (
A.36
) and (
A.39
), we have shown the first estimate (
A.31
). Now to prove the second estimate (
A.32
), we consider the case
x\in\Omega(t)
and
x\in\Omega(t)^{c}
. For the first case
x\in\Omega(t)
, we use the conservation (
A.35
) to get

(A.40)

|\omega(t,x)|=\frac{|\omega(t,x)|}{r_{x}}r_{x}\leq\frac{|\omega_{0}(y)|}{r_{y}}L(t)\leq\bigg\|\frac{\omega_{0}}{r}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}L(t).

For the other case
x\in\Omega(t)^{c}
, we use the conservation (
A.35
) and the inequality (
A.38
) to get

(A.41)

\begin{split}|\omega(t,x)|&=\frac{|\omega(t,x)|}{r_{x}}r_{x}=|\omega_{0}(y)|\frac{\Phi_{t}^{r}(y)}{r_{y}}\leq\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}L(t).\end{split}

Summing up estimates (
A.40
) and (
A.41
), we obtain the second estimate (
A.32
). This finishes the proof.
∎
