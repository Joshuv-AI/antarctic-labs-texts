The set of ratios of derangements to permutations in digraphs is dense in [ 0 , 1 / 2 ] \displaystyle[0,1/2] .
Bethany Austhof, Patrick Bennett, Nick Christo

Abstract
A permutation in a digraph G = ( V , E ) \displaystyle G=(V,E) is a bijection f : V → V \displaystyle f:V\rightarrow V such that for all v ∈ V \displaystyle v\in V we either have that f \displaystyle f fixes v \displaystyle v or ( v , f ⁡ ( v ) ) ∈ E \displaystyle(v,f(v))\in E . A derangement in G \displaystyle G is a permutation that does not fix any vertex. In [ 1 ] it is proved that in any digraph, the ratio of derangements to permutations is at most 1 / 2 \displaystyle 1/2 . Answering a question posed in [ 1 ] , we show that the set of possible ratios of derangements to permutations in digraphs is dense in the interval [ 0 , 1 / 2 ] \displaystyle[0,1/2] .



1
Introduction

A
permutation
in a digraph
\displaystyle G=(V,E)
is a bijection
\displaystyle f:V\rightarrow V
such that for all
\displaystyle v\in V
we either have that
\displaystyle f
fixes
\displaystyle v
or
\displaystyle(v,f(v))\in E
. A
derangement
in
\displaystyle G
is a permutation that does not fix any vertex. We define the parameter
\displaystyle(d/p)_{G}
to be the ratio of derangements to permutations in
\displaystyle G
.

Bucic, Devlin, Hendon, Horne and Lund
[
1
]
showed that
\displaystyle(d/p)_{G}\leq 1/2
for all digraphs
\displaystyle G
, with equality if and only if
\displaystyle G
is a directed cycle. They also gave a construction (the blow-up of a directed cycle) that can achieve a ratio arbitrarily close to but not equal to
\displaystyle 1/2
. Let
\displaystyle S=\{(d/p)_{G}\;:\;G\;\;\text{is a digraph}\}
be a set of values arising as a ratio
\displaystyle(d/p).
In
[
1
]
they analyzed the ratio
\displaystyle(d/p)_{G}
for the random graph
\displaystyle G=G(n,m)
, and as a corollary of this analysis they showed that
\displaystyle S
is dense in
\displaystyle[0,1/e]
. This corollary follows from two facts:
\displaystyle(d/p)_{G}
is concentrated around its mean, and by choosing a suitable value of
\displaystyle m
one can make the expected ratio
\displaystyle(d/p)_{G}
close to any given value in
\displaystyle[0,1/e]
. At the end of the paper
[
1
]
they ask whether
\displaystyle S
is dense in
\displaystyle[0,1/2]
. Our main theorem, below, answers this question in the positive.

Theorem 1
.

The set of possible ratios of derangements to permutations in digraphs is dense in
\displaystyle[0,1/2]
.

The construction we use, described in more detail later, is a random subgraph of the blow-up of a directed cycle. The main part of the proof is an application of the second moment method (see
[
2
]
, for example, for an introduction to the method) to show that the number of derangements and permutations are concentrated around their expectations.

2
Proof of Theorem
1

2.1
Outline

First we outline the proof. Suppose we are given a fixed real number
\displaystyle r\in[0,1/2]
. We will show that there exists a sequence of digraphs
\displaystyle G_{k}
such that the ratio of derangements to permutations in
\displaystyle G_{k}
is
\displaystyle r+o(1)
as
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty
(which proves Theorem
1
). If
\displaystyle r=0
or
\displaystyle 1/2
this is trivial. Indeed, for
\displaystyle r=0
observe that a digraph with one vertex and no edges has no derangements and one permutation, and for
\displaystyle r=1/2
observe that any directed cycle has one derangement and two permutations. So we assume
\displaystyle 0<r<1/2
.

Our construction is as follows. As defined in
[
1
]
, let the digraph
\displaystyle D_{k,\ell}
where
\displaystyle k\geq 1
and
\displaystyle\ell\geq 2
have vertices
\displaystyle v_{ij}
for
\displaystyle i\in[k]
and
\displaystyle j\in[\ell]
such that
\displaystyle(v_{ij},v_{lm})\in E(D_{k,\ell})
if and only if
\displaystyle m=j+1\,\text{mod}\,l
. In other words,
\displaystyle D_{k,\ell}
is the blow-up of a directed
\displaystyle\ell
-cycle where each vertex is expanded to a set of
\displaystyle k
vertices. We let
\displaystyle V_{i}=\{v_{ij}:j\in[\ell]\}
.
As was shown in
[
1
]
, the number of derangements on
\displaystyle D_{k,\ell}
is
\displaystyle(k!)^{\ell}
and the number of permutations on
\displaystyle D_{k,\ell}
is
\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(\binom{k}{i}(k-i)!\right)^{\ell}
. Hence,
\displaystyle(d/p)_{k,\ell}=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{i!}\right)^{\ell}\right)^{-1}
can be made arbitrarily close to 1/2 by choosing
\displaystyle\ell
large enough (even for large
\displaystyle k
). This construction yields a graph for which the ratio of derangements to permutations is arbitrarily close to 1/2 but not exactly 1/2. We will also use this construction, but we will randomly remove some edges. By taking a random subraph we can “interpolate” between
\displaystyle D_{k,\ell}
(a dense digraph whose ratio of derangements to permutations is close to
\displaystyle 1/2
) and a sparse random digraph (whose ratio is
\displaystyle 0
).

In this paper all asymptotics are as
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty
.
\displaystyle\ell
is treated as fixed. We use standard big-O, little-o and
\displaystyle\Omega
notation. We write
\displaystyle x\sim y
if
\displaystyle x=(1+o(1))y
. All logarithms are base
\displaystyle e
.

2.2
Proof details

Let the random graph
\displaystyle G_{k,\ell}(m)
be chosen uniformly from among all subgraphs of
\displaystyle D_{k,\ell}
with
\displaystyle m
edges. We will fix some
\displaystyle p,\ell
and let
\displaystyle m=pk^{2}\ell
(so
\displaystyle p
is the probability that any particular edge of
\displaystyle D_{k,\ell}
becomes an edge of
\displaystyle G_{k,\ell}
). Let the random variables
\displaystyle X,Y
be the number of derangements and permutations in
\displaystyle G_{k,\ell}(m)
respectively. Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{D},\mathcal{P}
be the collection of all possible derangements and permutations on
\displaystyle D_{k,\ell}(m)
.

2.2.1
First moments of
\displaystyle X,Y

We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{D\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{P}[D\subseteq G_{k,\ell}]=(k!)^{\ell}\frac{\binom{k^{2}\ell-k\ell}{m-k\ell}}{\binom{k^{2}\ell}{m}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(k!)^{\ell}\left(\frac{m}{k^{2}\ell}\right)^{k\ell}\exp\left\{\frac{k^{2}\ell^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}\ell}-\frac{1}{m}\right)+O\left(\frac{k^{3}}{m^{2}}+\frac{k}{m}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sim(k!)^{\ell}p^{k\ell}\exp\left\{\frac{\ell}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\right\}

(2.1)

where on the second line we have used the following fact:

Fact 2.1
.

\frac{\binom{a-x}{b-x}}{\binom{a}{b}}=\frac{(b)_{x}}{(a)_{x}}=\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{x}\exp\left\{\frac{x^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{a}-\frac{1}{b}\right)+O\left(\frac{x^{3}}{b^{2}}+\frac{x}{b}\right)\right\}.

For completeness we include the proof although it is well-known.

Proof.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{(b)_{x}}{(a)_{x}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{x}\cdot\frac{1\left(1-\frac{1}{b}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{b}\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{x-1}{b}\right)}{1\left(1-\frac{1}{a}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{a}\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{x-1}{a}\right)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{x}\cdot\exp\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{x-1}\left[{\ln\left(1-\frac{i}{b}\right)-\ln\left(1-\frac{i}{a}\right)}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{x}\cdot\exp\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{x-1}\left[{-\frac{i}{b}+\frac{i}{a}+O\left(\frac{i^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{i^{2}}{b^{2}}\right)}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{x}\cdot\exp\left\{\frac{x(x-1)}{2}\left(\frac{1}{a}-\frac{1}{b}\right)+O\left(\frac{x^{3}}{b^{2}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{x}\exp\left\{\frac{x^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{a}-\frac{1}{b}\right)+O\left(\frac{x^{3}}{b^{2}}+\frac{x}{b}\right)\right\}.

∎

Before we calculate
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y]
we introduce a function
\displaystyle f_{\ell}(x)
. For any integer
\displaystyle\ell\geq 1
, let

f_{\ell}(x):=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{i\ell}}{(i!)^{\ell}}.

(2.2)

Note that the above power series for
\displaystyle f_{\ell}(x)
converges for all
\displaystyle x
and therefore in particular each
\displaystyle f_{\ell}
is continuous in
\displaystyle x
. We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{P}[P\subseteq G_{k,\ell}]=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(\binom{k}{i}(k-i)!\right)^{\ell}\frac{\binom{k^{2}\ell-(k-i)\ell}{m-(k-i)\ell}}{\binom{k^{2}\ell}{m}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(\frac{k!}{i!}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{m}{k^{2}\ell}\right)^{(k-i)\ell}\exp\left\{\frac{(k-i)^{2}\ell^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}\ell}-\frac{1}{m}\right)+O\left(\frac{k^{3}}{m^{2}}+\frac{k}{m}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(k!)^{\ell}p^{k\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{i!}\right)^{\ell}p^{-i\ell}\exp\left\{\frac{\ell}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)+O\left(\frac{i+1}{k}\right)\right\}.

(2.3)

We split the above sum into two ranges of
\displaystyle i
. Note that for
\displaystyle 0\leq i\leq\sqrt{k}
we have

\displaystyle\exp\left\{O\left(\frac{i+1}{k}\right)\right\}=1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)
, while for
\displaystyle\sqrt{k}\leq i\leq k
we have
\displaystyle\exp\left\{O\left(\frac{i+1}{k}\right)\right\}=O(1)
. Thus line (
2.3
) becomes

(k!)^{\ell}p^{k\ell}\left[{\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)\right)\exp\left\{\frac{\ell}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\right\}\sum_{0\leq i\leq\sqrt{k}}\left(\frac{1}{i!}\right)^{\ell}p^{-i\ell}+O(1)\sum_{\sqrt{k}<i\leq k}\left(\frac{1}{i!}\right)^{\ell}p^{-i\ell}}\right].

(2.4)

As
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty
we have

\sum_{0\leq i\leq\sqrt{k}}\left(\frac{1}{i!}\right)^{\ell}p^{-i\ell}\rightarrow f_{\ell}(1/p),

and

\sum_{\sqrt{k}<i\leq k}\left(\frac{1}{i!}\right)^{\ell}p^{-i\ell}\leq\sum_{i=\sqrt{k}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{i!}\right)^{\ell}p^{-i\ell}=o(1)

since the latter is the tail of a convergent series. Thus, returning to our estimate of
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y]
on line (
2.4
), we have

\mathbb{E}[Y]\sim(k!)^{\ell}p^{k\ell}\exp\left\{\frac{\ell}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\right\}f_{\ell}(1/p).

(2.5)

2.2.2
Choosing
\displaystyle p,\ell

Now that we know
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X],\mathbb{E}[Y]
we will choose
\displaystyle p,\ell
to make sure that the ratio of
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]
to
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y]
is close to
\displaystyle r
. Using lines (
2.1
) and (
2.5
) we have

\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{\mathbb{E}[Y]}\sim\frac{1}{f_{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)},

so we would like to choose
\displaystyle\ell
and
\displaystyle 0<p<1
so that
\displaystyle f_{\ell}(1/p)=1/r
. We have

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}f_{\ell}(x)=\infty,\qquad f_{\ell}(1)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{i!}\right)^{\ell}=1+1+\frac{1}{2^{\ell}}+\frac{1}{6^{\ell}}+\frac{1}{24^{\ell}}+\ldots.

Note that we can make
\displaystyle f_{\ell}(1)
arbitrarily close to 2 by taking
\displaystyle\ell
large. Indeed, we have
\displaystyle f_{\ell}(1)\geq 2
and

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{\ell}(1)=2+\sum_{i\geq 2}\left(\frac{1}{i!}\right)^{\ell}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2+\sum_{i\geq 2}\left(\frac{1}{2^{i-1}}\right)^{\ell}=2+\frac{1}{2^{\ell}-1}.

Since
\displaystyle r<1/2
, we can choose
\displaystyle\ell
so that
\displaystyle f_{\ell}(1)<1/r
. Then by the intermediate value theorem there is some
\displaystyle x\in(1,\infty)
such that
\displaystyle f_{\ell}(x)=1/r
. We choose
\displaystyle p
to be the value
\displaystyle 1/x
, so
\displaystyle 0<p<1
and
\displaystyle f_{\ell}(1/p)=1/r
. So we view
\displaystyle\ell
and
\displaystyle p
as constants determined entirely by
\displaystyle r
.

2.2.3
Second moments of
\displaystyle X,Y

In this section we show that
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\sim\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]\sim\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}
. This will complete the proof, since then by the second moment method we have that

\frac{X}{Y}\sim\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{\mathbb{E}[Y]}\sim\frac{1}{f_{\ell}(1/p)}=r.

with probability approaching 1 as
\displaystyle k
goes to infinity.

To help us estimate
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{2}],\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
we will find the function
\displaystyle h(a,b)
(defined below) useful. Suppose we have some fixed matching
\displaystyle B
of
\displaystyle b
many edges in the graph
\displaystyle K_{a,a}
. Then by inclusion-exclusion the number of perfect matchings that do not have any edges from
\displaystyle B
is

h(a,b):=\sum_{w=0}^{b}(-1)^{w}\binom{b}{w}(a-w)!.

Note that we always have
\displaystyle h(a,b)\leq a!
. We will now observe that, roughly speaking,
\displaystyle h(a,b)\approx\frac{a!}{e}
whenever
\displaystyle b\approx a\rightarrow\infty
. More formally we have the following

Fact 2.2
.

Suppose
\displaystyle a-a^{1/10}\leq b\leq a
. Then we have

h(a,b)=\left(1+O(a^{-4/5})\right)\frac{a!}{e}

as
\displaystyle a\rightarrow\infty

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle h(a,b)=\sum_{0\leq w\leq b}(-1)^{w}\binom{b}{w}(a-w)!=a!\sum_{0\leq w\leq b}\frac{(-1)^{w}}{w!}\frac{(b)_{w}}{(a)_{w}}.

(2.6)

Now, for
\displaystyle 0\leq w\leq a^{1/10}
we have by Fact
2.1
that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{(b)_{w}}{(a)_{w}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{w}\exp\left\{\frac{w^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{a}-\frac{1}{b}\right)+O\left(\frac{w^{3}}{b^{2}}+\frac{w}{b}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1+O\left(a^{-9/10}\right)\right)^{O\left(a^{1/10}\right)}\exp\left\{O(a^{-4/5}\right\}=1+O(a^{-4/5}).

Meanwhile for
\displaystyle w\geq a^{1/10}
we have that the corresponding term in line (
2.6
) has absolute value

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{w!}\frac{(b)_{w}}{(a)_{w}}\leq\frac{1}{(a^{1/10})!}=\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(a^{1/10}\log a\right)\right\}

by Stirling’s approximation. Thus, the sum of all such terms in line (
2.6
) is at most

b\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(a^{1/10}\log a\right)\right\}=O(a^{-4/5})

(this bound is quite comfortable). By the Alternating Series Test we have that

\sum_{0\leq w\leq a^{1/10}}\frac{(-1)^{w}}{w!}=\frac{1}{e}+O\left(\frac{1}{(a^{1/10})!}\right)=\frac{1}{e}+O(a^{-4/5}).

Breaking up the sum for
\displaystyle h(a,b)
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle h(a,b)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=a!\left[{\sum_{0\leq w\leq a^{1/10}}\frac{(-1)^{w}}{w!}\frac{(b)_{w}}{(a)_{w}}+\sum_{a^{1/10}<w\leq b}\frac{(-1)^{w}}{w!}\frac{(b)_{w}}{(a)_{w}}}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=a!\left[{\left(1+O(a^{-4/5})\right)\sum_{0\leq w\leq a^{1/10}}\frac{(-1)^{w}}{w!}+O(a^{-4/5})}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1+O(a^{-4/5})\right)\frac{a!}{e}.

∎

We find that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{D,D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{P}[D,D^{\prime}\subseteq G_{k\ell}]=(k!)^{\ell}\sum_{D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{P}[D_{0},D^{\prime}\subseteq M]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(k!)^{\ell}\sum_{b=0}^{k\ell}\left[\frac{\binom{k^{2}\ell-(2k\ell-b)}{m-(2k\ell-b)}}{\binom{k^{2}\ell}{m}}\sum_{\vec{b}\in S_{b}}\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}\binom{k}{b_{c}}h(k-b_{c},k-b_{c})\right].

(2.7)

where in the inner sum,
\displaystyle S_{b}
is the set of
\displaystyle\ell
-dimensional vectors
\displaystyle\vec{b}=(b_{1},\ldots b_{\ell})
whose components are nonnegative integers summing to
\displaystyle b
.

By
2.1
, if
\displaystyle b\leq k^{1/10}
then we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\binom{k^{2}\ell-(2k\ell-b)}{m-(2k\ell-b)}}{\binom{k^{2}\ell}{m}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=p^{2k\ell-b}\exp\left\{\frac{(2k\ell-b)^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}\ell}-\frac{1}{m}\right)+O\left(\frac{k^{3}}{m^{2}}+\frac{k}{m}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1+O(k^{-9/10})\right)p^{2k\ell-b}\exp\left\{2\ell\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\right\}

and by Fact
2.2
we have

\displaystyle h(k-b_{c},k-b_{c})=\left(1+O(k^{-4/5})\right)\frac{(k-b_{c})!}{e}
. Therefore the term corresponding to
\displaystyle b
in (
2.7
) is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\binom{k^{2}\ell-(2k\ell-b)}{m-(2k\ell-b)}}{\binom{k^{2}\ell}{m}}\sum_{\vec{b}\in S_{b}}\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\binom{k}{b_{c}}h(k-b_{c},k-b_{c})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1+O(k^{-4/5})\right)p^{2k\ell-b}\exp\left\{2\ell\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\right\}\sum_{\vec{b}\in S_{b}}\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}\binom{k}{b_{c}}\frac{(k-b_{c})!}{e}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1+O(k^{-4/5})\right)(k!)^{\ell}p^{2k\ell-b}\exp\left\{2\ell\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)-\ell\right\}\sum_{\vec{b}\in S_{b}}\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{b_{c}!}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1+O(k^{-4/5})\right)(k!)^{\ell}p^{2k\ell-b}\exp\left\{\ell\left(1-\frac{2}{p}\right)\right\}\frac{\ell^{b}}{b!}

where on the last line we used the multinomial formula. Meanwhile if
\displaystyle b\geq k^{1/10}
then the term corresponding to
\displaystyle b
in (
2.7
) is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\binom{k^{2}\ell-(2k\ell-b)}{m-(2k\ell-b)}}{\binom{k^{2}\ell}{m}}\sum_{\vec{b}\in S_{b}}\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\binom{k}{b_{c}}h(k-b_{c},k-b_{c})\leq\sum_{\vec{b}\in S_{b}}\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}\binom{k}{b_{c}}(k-b_{c})!

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(k!)^{\ell}\frac{\ell^{b}}{b!}=(k!)^{\ell}\cdot\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(k^{1/10}\log k\right)\right\}

and so the sum of all terms in (

) with
\displaystyle b\geq k^{1/10}
is at most

k\ell\cdot(k!)^{\ell}\cdot\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(k^{1/10}\log k\right)\right\}=(k!)^{\ell}\cdot O\left(k^{-4/5}\right).

Therefore

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(k!)^{\ell}\sum_{b=0}^{k\ell}\left[\frac{\binom{k^{2}\ell-(2k\ell-b)}{m-(2k\ell-b)}}{\binom{k^{2}\ell}{m}}\sum_{\vec{b}\in S_{b}}\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}\binom{k}{b_{c}}h(k-b_{c},k-b_{c})\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(k!)^{\ell}\left[{\sum_{0\leq b\leq k^{1/10}}\left(1+O(k^{-4/5})\right)(k!)^{\ell}p^{2k\ell-b}\exp\left\{\ell\left(1-\frac{2}{p}\right)\right\}\frac{\ell^{b}}{b!}+(k!)^{\ell}\cdot O\left(k^{-4/5}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1+O(k^{-4/5})\right)(k!)^{2\ell}p^{2k\ell}\exp\left\{\ell\left(1-\frac{2}{p}\right)\right\}\sum_{0\leq b\leq k^{1/10}}p^{-b}\frac{\ell^{b}}{b!}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1+O(k^{-4/5})\right)(k!)^{2\ell}p^{2k\ell}\exp\left\{\ell\left(1-\frac{2}{p}\right)\right\}\cdot\left(\exp\left\{\frac{\ell}{p}\right\}+O(k^{-4/5})\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1+O(k^{-4/5})\right)(k!)^{2\ell}p^{2k\ell}\exp\left\{\ell\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}

For
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
we find an exact expression to be cumbersome, but the following upper bound will suffice:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i,j\leq k\\
0\leq b\leq k\ell\\
\vec{b}\in S_{b}\end{subarray}}\frac{\binom{k^{2}\ell-(2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b)}{m-(2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b)}}{\binom{k^{2}\ell}{m}}\left(\frac{k!}{i!}\right)^{\ell}\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}\binom{k-i}{b_{c}}\binom{k-b_{c}}{j}h(k-j-b_{c},k-i-b_{c}-2j)

(2.8)

The term corresponding to a tuple
\displaystyle(i,j,b,\vec{b})
above is an upper bound on the contribution to
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
due to pairs of permutations
\displaystyle(P,P^{\prime})
such that
\displaystyle P
fixes
\displaystyle i
vertices per part,
\displaystyle P^{\prime}
fixes
\displaystyle j
vertices per part, and
\displaystyle P
and
\displaystyle P^{\prime}
share a total of
\displaystyle b
edges where
\displaystyle b_{c}
of the shared edges are between
\displaystyle V_{c}
and part
\displaystyle V_{c+1}
. The first factor is the edge probability, and the next factor is the number of choices for
\displaystyle P
. The next factor is an upper bound on the number of choices for
\displaystyle P^{\prime}
. Indeed, we choose the edges of
\displaystyle P^{\prime}
from
\displaystyle V_{c}
to
\displaystyle V_{c+1}
by first choosing
\displaystyle b_{c}
edges of
\displaystyle P
to be shared, then we choose
\displaystyle j
vertices in
\displaystyle V_{c}
to be fixed by
\displaystyle P^{\prime}
, and finally we choose a matching between the remaining vertices (the vertices of
\displaystyle V_{c}\cup V_{c+1}
that are not fixed by
\displaystyle P^{\prime}
and are not endpoints of the
\displaystyle b_{c}
shared edges already chosen). This matching must avoid any edges of
\displaystyle P
, and the vertices to be matched induce at least
\displaystyle k-i-b_{c}-2j
edges of
\displaystyle P
, explaining the last factor above.

We will now estimate the significant terms in (

). Assume
\displaystyle i,j,b\leq k^{1/10}
. Then by Fact

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\binom{k^{2}\ell-(2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b)}{m-(2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b)}}{\binom{k^{2}\ell}{m}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=p^{2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b}\exp\left\{\frac{(2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b)^{2}}{2k^{2}\ell}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=p^{2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b}\exp\left\{2\ell\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)+O\left(k^{-9/10}\right)\right\}.

Next we estimate

\displaystyle\displaystyle h(k-j-b_{c},k-i-b_{c}-2j)=\left(1+O\left(k^{-4/5}\right)\right)\frac{(k-j-b_{c})!}{e}

by Fact
2.2
. So the product in (
2.8
) is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}\binom{k-i}{b_{c}}\binom{k-b_{c}}{j}h(k-j-b_{c},k-i-b_{c}-2j)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1+O\left(k^{-4/5}\right)\right)\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}\frac{(k-i)_{b_{c}}}{b_{c}!}\frac{(k-b_{c})_{j}}{j!}\frac{(k-j-b_{c})!}{e}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left(1+O\left(k^{-4/5}\right)\right)\left(\frac{k!}{ej!}\right)^{\ell}\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{b_{c}!}.

(2.9)

The sum of terms in (
2.8
) corresponding to small
\displaystyle i,j,b
is at most

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(1+O\left(k^{-4/5}\right)\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i,j,b\leq k^{1/10}\\
\vec{b}\in S_{b}\end{subarray}}p^{2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b}\exp\left\{2\ell\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\right\}.\left(\frac{k!}{i!}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{k!}{ej!}\right)^{\ell}\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{b_{c}!}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1+O\left(k^{-4/5}\right)\right)\exp\left\{\ell\left(1-\frac{2}{p}\right)\right\}\sum_{0\leq i,j,b\leq k^{1/10}}p^{2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b}\left(\frac{k!}{i!}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{k!}{j!}\right)^{\ell}\frac{\ell^{b}}{b!}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left(1+O\left(k^{-4/5}\right)\right)(k!)^{2\ell}p^{2k\ell}\exp\left\{\ell\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\right\}\sum_{0\leq i,j,b}\frac{p^{-i\ell}}{(i!)^{\ell}}\cdot\frac{p^{-j\ell}}{(j!)^{\ell}}\cdot\frac{(\ell/p)^{b}}{b!}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1+O\left(k^{-4/5}\right)\right)(k!)^{2\ell}p^{2k\ell}\exp\left\{\ell\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\right\}f_{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}

where on the second-to-last line we have used

\sum_{0\leq i}\frac{p^{-i\ell}}{(i!)^{\ell}}=f_{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{p}\right),\qquad\qquad\sum_{0\leq b}\frac{(\ell/p)^{b}}{b!}=\exp\left\{\frac{\ell}{p}\right\}.

It remains to show that the sum of all other terms (i.e. terms where
\displaystyle i,j,
or
\displaystyle b
is at least
\displaystyle k^{1/10}
) is negligible compared to
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}
, which is of order
\displaystyle(k!)^{2\ell}p^{2k\ell}
. Note that by Fact

2.1

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\binom{k^{2}\ell-(2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b)}{m-(2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b)}}{\binom{k^{2}\ell}{m}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=p^{2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b}\exp\left\{\frac{(2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b)^{2}}{2k^{2}\ell}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=O\left(p^{2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b}\right).

Thus, the sum (over
\displaystyle\vec{b}
) of terms corresponding to a fixed triple
\displaystyle(i,j,b)
in line (
2.8
) big-O of

\displaystyle\displaystyle p^{2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b}\sum_{\vec{b}\in S_{b}}\left(\frac{k!}{i!}\right)^{\ell}\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}\binom{k-i}{b_{c}}\binom{k-b_{c}}{j}(k-j-b_{c})!

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq p^{2k\ell-(i+j)\ell-b}\left(\frac{k!}{i!}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{k!}{j!}\right)^{\ell}\sum_{\vec{b}\in S_{b}}\prod_{c=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{b_{c}!}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left((k!)^{2\ell}p^{2k\ell}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\ell^{b}}{p^{(i+j)\ell+b}(i!)^{\ell}(j!)^{\ell}b!}\right).

It is easy to see that if
\displaystyle i,j
or
\displaystyle b
is at least
\displaystyle k^{1/10}
then the second factor above is
\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(k^{1/10}\log k\right)\right\}
. Since the number of triples
\displaystyle(i,j,b)
is polynomial in
\displaystyle k
, the sum of all such terms (i.e. where
\displaystyle i,j
or
\displaystyle b
is at least
\displaystyle k^{1/10}
) is
\displaystyle o\left((k!)^{2\ell}p^{2k\ell}\right)
which is a negligible contribution to
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
. Therefore we have
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]\sim\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}.

3
Remarks and Open Problems

The reader should note that we did not use a “binomial” random construction (e.g. keep each edge of
\displaystyle D_{k,\ell}
with probability
\displaystyle p
independently) because such a model lacks the concentration we need here. Indeed, for example Janson (
[
3
]
) showed that the number of perfect matchings in
\displaystyle G(n,p)
is not concentrated even when it is quite large, while the number of perfect matchings of
\displaystyle G(n,m)
is concentrated. We tried to use a binomial random construction and found that the second moments were too large, which in light of Janson’s result makes sense (for example derangements in our graph are just a union of several perfect matchings on bipartite graphs).

There are still interesting open problems in
[
1
]
. In particular it is still open whether
\displaystyle S
, the set of possible ratios
\displaystyle(d/p)_{G}
, is equal to
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1/2]
. Here we would like to pose another open problem that is mostly unrelated to our result. In particular, we ask about stability for digraphs whose ratio
\displaystyle(d/p)_{G}
is close to
\displaystyle 1/2
: do such digraphs have to resemble the blow-up of a directed graph?
