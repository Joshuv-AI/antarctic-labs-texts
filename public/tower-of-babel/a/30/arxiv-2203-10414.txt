Unique continuation results for abstract quasi-linear evolution equations in Banach spaces igor.freire@ufscar.br and igor.leite.freire@gmail.com
Igor Leite Freire

Abstract
Unique continuation properties for a class of evolution equations defined on Banach spaces are considered from two different point of views: the first one is based on the existence of conserved quantities, which very often translates into the conservation of some norm of the solutions of the system in a suitable Banach space. The second one is regarded to well-posed problems. Our results are then applied to some equations, most of them describing physical processes like wave propagation, hydrodynamics, and integrable systems, such as the b − b- ; Fornberg-Whitham; potential and π − \pi- Camassa-Holm; generalised Boussinesq equations; and the modified Euler-Poisson system.

Introduction

In this paper we consider the problem of unique continuation of solutions for systems of evolution equations

u^{i}_{t}+A^{i}_{j}u^{j}=g^{i},\quad 1\leq i\leq m,

(1.1)

where
m
is a fixed positive integer;
{\bf u}=(u^{1},\cdots,u^{m})
is a vector function of real variables
t
and
x
, such that for each fixed
t
for which the solution exists,
{\bf u}(t,\cdot)
belongs to a certain Banach space;
A^{i}_{j}=A^{i}_{j}([{\bf u}])
are
m^{2}
linear operators;
g=g([{\bf u}])
is a member of some Banach space for each fixed
t
, where
g=(g^{1},\cdots,g^{m})
; and
[{\bf u}]
denotes dependence on
{\bf u}
and its (
x-
)derivatives up to a finite, but arbitrary, order. The Einstein summation convention over repeated indices is presupposed in (
1.1
). In case
m=1
, we simply denote
u
instead of
{\bf u}
.

System (
1.1
) can be conveniently rewritten in a shorter form

{\bf u}_{t}+A([{\bf u}]){\bf u}=g([{\bf u}]),

(1.2)

where
A([{\bf u}])=(A^{i}_{j}([{\bf u}]))
is a (matrix of) linear operator(s). We would like to point out that under mild conditions on
A(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
one can establish local well-posedness of the equation above subject to
{\bf u}(0,\cdot)={\bf u}_{0}(\cdot)
using Kato’s approach
[
22
]
.

Important equations describing waves propagating in a physical media fall into the class (
1.2
), or can be taken as such. To restrict ourselves to a single example, we mention the non-local form of the Camassa-Holm equation
[
2
]

u_{t}+uu_{x}=-\partial_{x}\Lambda^{-2}\Big(u^{2}+\frac{1}{2}u_{x}^{2}\Big),

(1.3)

which is a famous model in hydrodynamics.

In this work we present two different approaches for studying unique continuation of solutions of equations of the type (
1.2
):

•

The first one does not necessarily presume uniqueness of solutions, but requires the existence of a conserved quantity satisfying certain mild conditions;

•

The second one makes use of local well-posedness, but does not require any conserved quantity.

In some sense, our results could be seen as unique continuation counter-parts for some classes of abstract evolution equations considered by Kato
[
22
]
: while his theory can be applied to a large class of equations of the type (
1.2
), our findings require more conditions on the map
g(\cdot)
, stronger than those requested by Kato for proving local well-posedness for Cauchy problems involving (
1.2
). Despite being applicable to a class of problems smaller than that covered by Kato’s local well posedness approach, the tools introduced in the present paper can still be applied to a considerably wide class of equations describing physical processes in hydrodynamics, wave propagation and integrable systems.

Let us make an overview of the manuscript’s structure: in the next section we present our main results, namely, our approaches for dealing with unique continuation properties for the solutions of (
1.2
), which we call
conserved quantity approach
and
local well-posedness approach
. Then, in section
3
we illustrate our results by applying them to some equations, mostly of them coming from hydrodynamics and integrable systems theory.

The machinery developed in the present work enables us to treat problems of unique continuation of solutions for other classes of equations beyond (
1.2
) with some customisation, and depending on the situation, regardless the order of the derivative with respect to
t
. As an example, the tools introduced here give us condition to prove unique continuation results for the generalised Boussinesq equation

u_{tt}=\partial_{x}^{2}f(u)+u_{ttxx},

(1.4)

which was considered by Constantin and Molinet
[
6
]
. This is done in section
4
. Such an equation, for certain choices of the function
f(\cdot)
, can describe nonlinear waves in weakly dissipative media
[
3
]
.

Another example is given in section
5
, where we use the ideas given in section
2
to prove that if
(u,\rho)
is a conservative solution (this notion will be given in section
5
) of the modified Euler-Poisson system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\rho_{t}+(u\rho)_{x}=0,\\
\\
u_{t}-u_{txx}+uu_{x}-3u_{x}u_{xx}-uu_{xxx}+\rho_{x}=0,\end{array}\right.

(1.5)

vanishing on an open set
\emptyset\neq\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then
\rho\equiv 0
and
u
is necessarily a solution of the inviscid Burgers equation

u_{t}+uu_{x}=0.

Our results are discussed in section
6
, while our conclusions are presented in section
7
.

Main results

2.1
Notation and conventions

The set of continuous operators between two Banach spaces
{\cal B}_{1}
and
{\cal B}_{2}
is denoted by
{\cal L}({\cal B}_{1};{\cal B}_{2})
, while
{\cal B}_{1}\hookrightarrow{\cal B}_{2}
means that the Banach space
{\cal B}_{1}
is continuously embedded in the Banach space
{\cal B}_{2}
.

By
X
we denote the real line
\mathbb{R}
or the circle
\mathbb{S}
, that can be identified with the interval
[0,1)
. For a function
u
with two variables, partial derivatives with respect to the first argument will be denoted by
u_{t}
, whereas partial derivatives of
u
with respect to its second argument will be often denoted by
u_{x}
or, eventually, by
\partial_{x}u
.

The convolution between two functions
f
and
g
is denoted by
f\ast g
;
H^{s}(X)
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
, is the usual Sobolev space of order
s
;
\Lambda^{2}:=1-\partial_{x}^{2}
, and its inverse
\Lambda^{-2}f=g\ast f
is given by

g(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\frac{e^{-|x|}}{2}},&\text{if}\,\,X=\mathbb{R},\\
\\
\displaystyle{\frac{\cosh(x-\lfloor x\rfloor-1/2)}{2\sinh(1/2)}},&\text{if}\,\,X=\mathbb{S}.\end{array}\right.

Very often we make use of the identity
\partial_{x}^{2}\Lambda^{-2}=\Lambda^{-2}-1
whenever such operator appears and is defined. For
s\geq t
, then
H^{s}(X)\hookrightarrow H^{t}(X)
, and
H^{s}(X)
is an algebra as soon as
s>1/2
, that is, if
u,v\in H^{s}(X)
, then
uv\in H^{s}(X)
. Additionally, given
u,v\in H^{s}(X)
, with
s>1/2
, then
\|uv\|_{H^{s-1}(X)}\leq c\|u\|_{H^{s}(X)}\|v\|_{H^{s-1}(X)}
, for some constant
c>0
depending on
s
. For further details, see
[
29
, Chapter 4]
and
[
22
, Appendix]
.

2.2
General assumptions

Throughout the paper we assume the following global conditions on the system (
1.2
) and its solutions
{\bf u}
:

G0

{\bf u}
is defined on
[0,T)\times X
, for some
T>0
;

G1

There exist two Banach spaces
{\cal B}_{1}
and
{\cal B}_{2}
, such that
{\cal B}_{1}\hookrightarrow{\cal B}_{2}
, and
{\bf u}\in C^{0}([0,T);{\cal B}_{1})\cap C^{1}((0,T);{\cal B}_{2})
;

G2

A([{\bf u}])\in{\cal L}({\cal B}_{1};{\cal B}_{2})
, for each fixed
t
;

G3

g([{\bf u}])\in{\cal B}_{2}
, for each fixed
t
. Moreover,
{\bf u}\mapsto g([{\bf u}])
is continuous.

Henceforth, we simply write
{\bf u}
instead of
[{\bf u}]
in order to make the notation cleaner and more concise. Moreover, in some parts of the presentation we replace
[{\bf u}]
by
{\bf u}(t,x)
in order for the independent variables to be emphasised.

We would like to make a small digression and present some short, but useful, comments about the general conditions above.

Remark 2.1
.

Condition
G0
does not necessarily request, and nor imply, uniqueness of the solution, although
G1
may lead to such under mild conditions, see
[
22
, Theorem 6]
.

Remark 2.2
.

A solution
u
satisfying condition
G1
need not to be continuous, although it may imply that
u
is continuously differentiable provided that members of the space
{\cal B}_{2}
have enough regularity, e.g,
{\cal B}_{2}=H^{s}
, for
s
large enough.

Remark 2.3
.

For each
t
such that the solution exists, conditions
G2
and
G3
say that
A({\bf u}(t,\cdot))
is a bounded linear operator between the Banach spaces
{\cal B}_{1}
and
{\cal B}_{2}
, whereas
g({\bf u}(t,\cdot))\in{\cal B}_{2}
. Moreover, for
t>0
, we have
g({\bf u}(t,\cdot))={\bf u}_{t}(t,\cdot)+A({\bf u}(t,\cdot)){\bf u}(t,\cdot)
. This relation plays vital importance in the demonstration of our main results.

Remark 2.4
.

We observe that
g
must vanish at
0
for sake of consistency.

Below we present some examples illustrating the conditions above.

Example 2.1
.

Let
u\in C^{0}([0,T);H^{s}(X))\cap C^{1}((0,T);H^{s-1}(X))
,
A(u)=u\partial_{x}
,
{\cal B}_{1}=H^{s}(X)
,
{\cal B}_{2}=H^{s-1}(X)
, and
s>3/2
. Then conditions
G0
–
G2
are clearly satisfied and, in particular,
A(u)u=uu_{x}
.

In addition, the fact that the operator
\partial_{x}\Lambda^{-2}
applies
H^{s}(X)
into
H^{s+1}(X)
implies

g(u)=\partial_{x}\Lambda^{-2}\Big(au+bu^{2}+cu_{x}^{2}\Big)\in H^{s-1}(X),

for all
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
, since
H^{s}(X)\hookrightarrow H^{s-1}(X)
, and hence,
{\bf G3}
is satisfied.

Example 2.2
.

Let
A(u)=u_{x}\partial_{x}
and
g(u)=\Lambda^{-2}(u_{x}^{2}+u_{xx}^{2}/2)
. Taking
u\in C^{0}([0,T);H^{s}(\mathbb{R}))\cap C^{1}((0,T);H^{s-1}(\mathbb{R}))
, with
s>5/2
, we see that
G0
–
G3
are satisfied with
{\cal B}_{1}=H^{s}(X)
,
{\cal B}_{2}=H^{s-1}(X)
,
s>5/2
.

Let
{\bf u}
be a solution of (
1.2
). Fixed
t\in[0,T)
, we define

F_{t}(x):=g({\bf u}(t,x)),

(2.1)

and for this reason, in some parts of the paper we write
F_{t}=(F^{1}_{t},\cdots,F_{t}^{m})
. Observe that (
2.1
) defines a family of (vector) functions

(F_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}\subseteq{\cal B}_{2}
.

2.3
Conserved quantity approach

In addition to the global conditions
{\bf G0}-{\bf G3}
, throughout this subsection we also assume the following:

C0

There exists a continuous, non-negative or non-positive, real valued function
h
, depending on
u
and its derivatives of arbitrary, but finite order, which we simply write as
h=h({\bf u}(t,x))
, such that

{\cal H}(t)=\int_{X}h({\bf u}(t,x))dx

(2.2)

is constant. Additionally, we assume that
h(y)=0
if and only if
y=0
.

The quantity
{\cal H}(t)
and the function
h({\bf u}(t,x))
in (
2.2
) are called
conserved quantity
and
conserved density
, respectively, whereas in the jargon of analysis of PDEs, very often
{\cal H}(t)
is called
conservation law
. Condition
{\bf C0}
is nothing but the assumption of the existence of a conserved quantity for solutions of (
1.2
).

Remark 2.5
.

Even though we could include explicit dependence on the independent variables without changing the results, we prefer the restriction of the conserved densities to those only depending on
{\bf u}
and its derivatives of some finite order for sake of simplicity. In case these other variables are taken into account, we should assume that
h(t,x,[{\bf u}])
vanishes if and only if
{\bf u}=0
. Moreover, note that
[{\bf u}]\equiv 0
if and only if
{\bf u}\equiv 0
. Finally, it is worth mentioning that we identify a function
{\bf u}
with its zeroth-order derivative.

Some examples illustrating
C0
are:

•

Let
u=u(t,x)
be a real valued function and
h(u)=u
. If
u
is non-negative, then (
2.2
) implies that
\|u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
is a conserved quantity.

•

Still assuming that
u=u(t,x)
and
\rho=\rho(t,x)
are real valued functions, then
h(u)=u^{2}+u_{x}^{2}+\rho^{2}
is a density implying that the product norm
\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+\|\rho\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
is conserved.

Above we assume that all integrals involved are finite. More examples can be found and are discussed in detail in section
3
. Henceforth,
a
and
b
denote distinct real numbers satisfying
a<b
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
t_{0}\in(0,T)
,
a,b\in X
, and
{\bf u}
be a solution of (
1.2
). Suppose that
{\bf u}
and
g(\cdot)
satisfy
{\bf C0}
and the conditions

C1

{\bf u}(t_{0},x)=0
,
{\bf u}_{t}(t_{0},x)=0
, for
x\in[a,b]
;

C2

g({\bf u}(t_{0},x))=0
,
x\in[a,b]
, implies
{\bf u}(t_{0},\cdot)\equiv 0
.

Then
{\bf u}\equiv 0
.

Proof.

From (
1.2
) (see also Remark
2.3
), we have

g({\bf u}(t,x))={\bf u}_{t}(t,x)+A({\bf u}(t,x)){\bf u}(t,x).

(2.3)

As a consequence, restricting
x
to
[a,b]
, and taking
t=t_{0}
, from
{\bf C1}
we conclude that
g({\bf u}(t_{0},x))=0
, whereas
{\bf C2}
implies that
{\bf u}(t_{0},x)=0
,
x\in X
. The conserved quantity (
2.2
) then implies
{\cal H}(t)={\cal H}(t_{0})=0
, for any
t\in(0,T)
. Due to the fact that
h(\cdot)
is either non-negative or non-positive, the condition

\int_{X}h({\bf u}(t,x))dx=0,

implies that
h({\bf u}(t,\cdot))=0
, that is,
{\bf u}(t,\cdot)=0,\,\,t\in[0,T)
, since
h(y)=0
if and only if
y=0
.
∎

Corollary 2.1
.

If
{\bf u}
is a solution for (
1.2
) such that
{\bf C0}
and
{\bf C2}
hold, and there exists an open set
\Omega\subseteq[0,T)\times X
for which
{\bf u}\Big|_{\Omega}\equiv 0
, then
{\bf u}\equiv 0
.

Proof.

Let us take
t_{0}\in(0,T)
and
a,b\in X
such that
\{t_{0}\}\times[a,b]\subseteq\Omega
. Then condition
{\bf C1}
is satisfied and the result is a foregone conclusion of Theorem
2.1
.
∎

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
{\bf u}=(u^{1},\cdots,u^{m})
,
F_{t}=(F_{t}^{1},\cdots,F_{t}^{m})
,
t\in[0,T)
, where
F_{t}
is given by (
2.1
), and suppose that
{\bf C0}
holds. Assume that there exists
t_{0}\in(0,T)
,
a,b\in X
, and some
i\in\{1,\cdots,m\}
such that:

C3

{\bf u}(t_{0},x)=0
,
x\in[a,b]
;

C4

{\bf u}_{t}(t_{0},a)={\bf u}_{t}(t_{0},b)
;

C5

\partial_{x}F_{t_{0}}^{i}(\cdot)
is either non-positive or non-negative on
(a,b)
;

C6

\partial_{x}F^{i}_{t_{0}}(x)=0
, for
x\in(a,b)
, implies
{\bf u}(t_{0},\cdot)=0
.

Then
{\bf u}\equiv 0
.

Proof.

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we have

F_{t_{0}}^{i}(b)-F_{t_{0}}^{i}(a)=\int_{a}^{b}\partial_{x}F^{i}_{t_{0}}(s)ds.

(2.4)

Conditions
{\bf C3}
and
{\bf C4}
, jointly with (
2.3
), imply
g({\bf u}(t_{0},a))=g({\bf u}(t_{0},b))=0
. By (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) we conclude that
F_{t_{0}}(a)=F_{t_{0}}(b)
, which, jointly with (
2.4
) tell us that

\int_{a}^{b}\partial_{x}F_{t_{0}}^{i}(s)ds=0.

By
C5,

\partial_{x}F^{i}_{t_{0}}(\cdot)
is non-positive or non-negative. Hence, the equality above implies that
\partial_{x}F_{t_{0}}^{i}(x)=0
,
x\in(a,b)
, while
C6
tells that
{\bf u}(t_{0},x)=0
,
x\in X
. As consequence, from (
2.2
) we arrive at
{\cal H}(t_{0})=0
, which implies
{\bf u}(t,\cdot)=0
in view of the invariance of the conserved quantity.
∎

2.4
Local well-posedness approach

The unique continuation results proved before were based on the existence of a conserved quantity as stated by condition
C0
. We now look to the problem using the following different conditions.

C7

The Cauchy problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}{\bf u}_{t}+A({\bf u}){\bf u}&=&g({\bf u}(t,x)),\\
\\
{\bf u}(0,x)&=&{\bf u}_{0}(x),\end{array}\right.

(2.5)

for some
u_{0}\in{\cal B}_{1}
, is locally well-posed, so that we can at least assure the existence of a unique local solution
u
defined on
[0,T)\times X
, for some lifespan
T>0
.

It is worth mentioning that system (
1.2
) is invariant under translations in
t
, although its use may not be evident at first glance. Then we have the following crucial observation.

Remark 2.6
.

Condition
C7
has a powerful implication for the approach we are bound to present: if we know that a solution
{\bf u}
of (
2.5
) satisfies
{\bf u}(t_{0},x)=0
, for all
x\in X
and some
t_{0}\in(0,T)
, then
v={\bf u}(t+t_{0},x)
is a solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}v_{t}+A(v)v&=&g(v(t,x)),\\
\\
v(0,x)&=&0.\end{array}\right.

Clearly the function
v\equiv 0
is a solution of such a Cauchy problem. Since it has a unique solution, we are forced to conclude that
v(t,x)=0
(and it can be taken as a global solution) and, again by uniqueness, the same applies to
{\bf u}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
{\bf u}
be a solution of (
1.2
) satisfying
C7
. If, for some
t_{0}\in(0,T)
,
a,b\in X
,
C1
and
C2
as in Theorem

are satisfied, then
{\bf u}\equiv 0
.

Proof.

If
C1
is satisfied, then
g({\bf u}(t_{0},x))=0
,
x\in[a,b]
. By
C2
,
{\bf u}(t_{0},x)=0
,
x\in X
. The result is then a consequence of Remark
.
∎

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
{\bf u}
be a solution of (
1.2
) satisfying
C7
. If, for some
t_{0}\in(0,T)
,
a,b\in X
,
C3
–
C6
as in Theorem
2.2
are satisfied, then
{\bf u}\equiv 0
.

Proof.

The proof of theorem
2.2
shows that
u(t_{0},x)=0
. The result is again a consequence of Remark
2.6
.
∎

3
Applications

Here we illustrate our results by applying them to some equations. As a first application we specialise them to a general class of equations including relevant models in hydrodynamics and integrable systems, such as the Benjamin-Bona-Mahony (BBM) or the Camassa-Holm (CH) equations.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
g=g(u)
and
G=G(u)
be continuous functions and suppose that
u
is a solution of the equation

u_{t}+g(u)=\partial_{x}\Lambda^{-2}G(u).

(3.1)

Assume that
g=g(u)
and
G=G(u)
are functions depending on
u
and its
x
derivatives up to a finite, but arbitrary, order, such that
\partial_{x}\Lambda^{-2}G
is well defined; both vanishing when
u=0
;
G(\cdot)
is either non-positive or non-negative; and the condition
G(u(t_{0},x))=0
implies that
u(t_{0},x)=0
,
x\in X
.

If there exists an open set
\Omega
such that
u\big|_{\Omega}\equiv 0
and at least one of the conditions

•

{\bf C0}
, or

•

{\bf C7}
(replacing the equation in (
2.5
) by (
3.1
)),

are satisfied, then
u\equiv 0
. In particular, the result holds if
g(u)=a(u)u
, where
a=a(u)
is a linear operator.

Remark 3.1
.

Note that (
3.1
) belongs to the class (
1.2
) with
m=1
. Actually, taking
A(u)=0
and replacing
g(u)
by
\partial_{x}\Lambda^{-2}G(u)-g(u)
we obtain (
3.1
) from (
1.2
).

Proof.

Let
t_{0}
,
a
and
b
such that
\{t_{0}\}\times[a,b]\subseteq\Omega
, and define
F_{t}(x)=\partial_{x}\Lambda^{-2}G(t,x)
. By (
3.1
) we conclude that
F_{t_{0}}(a)=F_{t_{0}}(b)
. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we have

0=F_{t_{0}}(b)-F_{t_{0}}(a)=\int_{X}\Lambda^{-2}Gdx=\int_{X}(g\ast G)dx,

which implies that
G(u(t_{0},x))=0
, and thus
u(t_{0},x)=0
. The result then follows the same steps as theorem
2.1
or Remark
2.6
, depending on the condition taken.
∎

, see also

. We present it here to show the difference between the approaches introduced in the previous sections. The remaining examples of this section are original.

3.1
The
b-

Let us apply theorem

to the
b-

u_{t}-u_{txx}+(b+1)uu_{x}=bu_{x}u_{xx}+uu_{xxx},

, which for

]
. It can be rewritten as

u_{t}+uu_{x}=-\partial_{x}\Lambda^{-2}\Big(\frac{b}{2}u^{2}+\frac{3-b}{2}u_{x}^{2}\Big).

(3.2)

Example
2.1
implies that conditions
G0
–
G3
are satisfied whenever
u\in C^{0}([0,T);H^{s}(X))\cap C^{1}((0,T);H^{s-1}(X))
, with
s>3/2
. If we suppose that
b\in(0,3]
and there exists an open set
\Omega
for which
u\big|_{\Omega}\equiv 0
, we can then find
\{t_{0}\}\times[a,b]\subseteq[0,T)\times X
such that conditions
{\bf C3}
and
{\bf C4}
in Theorem

hold.

Defining

F_{t_{0}}(x)=-\partial_{x}\Lambda^{-2}\Big(\frac{b}{2}u^{2}+\frac{3-b}{2}u_{x}^{2}\Big)(t_{0},x),

(3.3)

we easily conclude that
F^{\prime}_{t}(x)
is non-positive. Moreover, as soon as
x\in[a,b]
, from (
3.3
), (

Remark 3.5

The study of unique continuation properties of the FW equation above assumes that the solution does not change its sign. 4
Application to the generalised Boussinesq equation

We now apply our machinery to equation (
1.4
), that is quite illustrative inasmuch as it shows how our ideas can be employed to a class of equations larger than that one might think at first glance. Our goal in this section is to prove the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
f
is a non-negative and smooth function, vanishing only at
0
, and
s\geq 0
. If a solution
u\in C^{2}([0,T);H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}))
of (
1.4
) vanishes on a non-empty and open set
\Omega\subseteq[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}
, then

.

Before proving the theorem above, let us first explore a bit more (
1.4
) and make some observations that will be useful for our purposes.

Let us assume that
u\in C^{2}([0,T);H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}))
, for some
T>0
, and
s\geq 0
. Apparently (
1.4
) does not seem to belong to the class (
1.2
). Let us define
v:=u_{t}
. From (
1.4
) we obtain
v_{t}=\partial_{x}^{2}f(u)+v_{txx}
, that is
\Lambda^{2}v_{t}=f(u)
. Inverting the operator
\Lambda^{-2}
, using the identity
\partial_{x}^{2}\Lambda^{-2}=\Lambda^{-2}-1
, and the constraint relating
u
and
v
, we can rewrite (
1.4
) as the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}v_{t}&=&\Lambda^{-2}f(u)+f(u),\\
\\
u_{t}&=&v.\end{array}\right.

(4.1)

Let us assume that
\Omega
is a non-empty open set in which
u
vanishes. Then we can find numbers
t^{\ast}
,
a
,
b
, and
\varepsilon>0
such that
{\cal R}=I_{\varepsilon}\times[a,b]\subseteq\Omega
, where
I_{\varepsilon}=[t^{\ast}-\varepsilon,t^{\ast}+\varepsilon]
. We note that for each
t\in I_{\varepsilon}
fixed, conditions
{\bf C3}
and
{\bf C4}
in theorem
2.2
are satisfied for the pair
(u,v)
.

We recall that
f
is a smooth, non-negative function, vanishing only at
0
. Defining
F_{t}(x)=\Lambda^{-2}f(u(t,x))
, we straightforwardly conclude that
F_{t}(x)\geq 0
for all
x
and it vanishes if and only if
u(t,\cdot)\equiv 0
.

As long as
(t,x)\in{\cal R}
, the first equation in (
4.1
) yields

0=v_{t}-f(u(t,x))=\Lambda^{-2}f(u(t,x))=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-|x-y|}f(u(t,y))dy,

which implies that
u(t,x)=0
, for all
t\in I_{\varepsilon}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let us fix
t_{0}\in(t^{\ast}-\varepsilon,t^{\ast}+\varepsilon)
and define
u_{0}(\cdot)=u(t_{0},\cdot)
and
u_{1}(\cdot)=u_{t}(t_{0},\cdot)
. Since
u\in C^{2}([0,T);H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}))
,
s\geq 0
, we then conclude that
u_{0},u_{1}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover,
(u_{0},u_{1})(x)=(0,0)
and then the solution of the problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}u_{tt}=\partial_{x}^{2}f(u)+u_{ttxx},\\
\\
u(t_{0},x)=u_{0}(x),\\
\\
u_{t}(t_{0},x)=v_{0}(x),\end{array}\right.

(4.2)

vanishes identically, since
[
6
, Theorem 1]
assures that (
4.2
) is well-posed and its solution depends continuously on the initial datum.

Let us now give a more elaborate proof of the same fact, but using conserved quantities. To this end, we introduce new functions and use a conserved quantity found in
[
6
]
to re-obtain our conclusion.

Substituting
z_{x}:=u_{t}
and
w_{x}:=u
, see
[
6
, page 1070]
, into (
1.4
) we obtain
z_{tx}=\partial_{x}^{2}F(w_{x})+z_{txxx}
and
w_{tx}=z_{x}
. Integrating these two equations and noticing that
(z,w)\rightarrow 0
at infinity (note that both belong to
H^{s+1}(\mathbb{R})
and
s\geq 0
), we conclude that
(z,w)
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}z_{t}&=&\partial_{x}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}F(w_{x}),\\
\\
w_{t}&=&z,\end{array}\right.

(4.3)

which is clearly of the type (
1.2
).

From
[
6
, Equation 4.7]
(see also
[
6
, Theorem 4]
), (
4.3
) has the conserved quantity

{\cal H}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\|z(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(u(t,x))dx,

(4.4)

where

(4.5)

Since

is non-negative and

if and only if

, we then conclude that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(u(t,x))dx\geq 0

and it vanishes if and only if

,

.

For

and the conditions on

, we conclude that

. On the other hand, we have already concluded that

, which means the quantity

Noting the presence of the Helmholtz operator

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\rho_{t}+(u\rho)_{x}=0,\\

Theorem 5.1

Let us show that system (

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}q_{t}(t,x)&=&u(t,q(t,x)),\\

), for each fixed

into itself.

Theorem 5.2
.

Assume that
(u_{0},\rho_{0})\in H^{3}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{2}(\mathbb{R})

is a non-negative function. Then the corresponding solution of (

Since

[

are both Lipschitz, bounded and Lipschitz with respect to

By letting

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\partial_{t}q_{x}(t,x)&=&u_{x}(t,q(t,x))q_{x},\\

\displaystyle{q_{x}(t,x)=e^{\int_{0}^{t}u_{x}(\tau,x)d\tau}>0.}

This shows that

is an increasing diffeomorphism from

into its image,

, for some

and

,

Now we evaluate

6
Discussion

The results reported in this paper have two different motivations: the one related to conserved quantities is based on the concepts of conservation of physical quantities, such as mass or energy, whereas the other, related to existence of solutions, is concerned with qualitative aspects of solutions for the dynamical system (
1.2
). In the first case the independent variables
t
and
x
have the meaning of time and space, respectively.

Equations of the type (
1.2
) coming from Physics usually
do have
conserved densities, say
h([{\bf u}])
, which gives rise to the conserved quantity

{\cal H}(t)=\int_{\Omega}h([{\bf u}])dx,

associated to a solution
{\bf u}
. These equations usually describe wave propagating in a media, such as water, and very often, a conserved density refers to mass or kinect energy densities, that are described by non-negative functions
h([{\bf u}])
. If a mass density vanishes, then such fact implies on the non-existence of a media in which a wave could propagate. On the other hand, if the density refers to kinect energy, its vanishing means that the there is no perturbation in a medium, that is, we do not have any event causing a phenomenon. These observations are the main ideas behind the results developed in the subsection
2.3
.

We note that while
C0
,
C1
,
C3
, and
C4
are properties of the
solution of the system
,
C2
,
C5
,
C6
, and
C7
are conditions arising from the
system/Cauchy problem
. Moreover, these attributes are used to build our results from the two perspectives aforementioned, that despite being conceptually rather different, both have a common crucial ingredient: the existence of a value
t_{0}
for which a solution
u
vanishes. If one is successful in finding such a value, one can then prove that the properties stated in conditions
C1
; or
C3
and
C4
, persist, since the solution necessarily vanishes and these conditions are satisfied for the identically zero solution. The main difference between the two approaches is the way we extend to all values of
t
the information we got to
t_{0}
. This can be done in the following forms:

•

From a physical point of view, in case we are able to find a time
t_{0}
for which the conserved quantity vanishes, we then conclude that it vanishes for each
t
such that the solution exists, in view of its time invariance. Physically speaking, usually the conserved density vanishes if and only if the field variable
{\bf u}
vanishes.

•

From a mathematical perspective the results developed in the subsection
2.4
are concerned with well-posed Cauchy problems. Remark
shows that if we can find a value of
t
for which the solution vanishes, it then vanishes everywhere in view of uniqueness.

The two different views above are connected in the following way: if the mass or kinect energy of a physical system vanishes, then we do not have any non-zero initial data provoking a modification in the system. Mathematically speaking, we have a zero initial data to the Cauchy problem (
2.5
). Still from mathematical eyes, the mass or kinect energy associated to conserved quantities very often corresponds to some norms in suitable Banach spaces.

Note that while the existence of a conserved quantity or the local well-posedness is a crucial ingredient to extend to any value of
t
the same property known to
t_{0}
, it is the map
g
in (
1.2
) that gives the information that the solution vanishes at some specific time, see conditions
{\bf C2}
or
{\bf C6}
in theorems
2.1
–
2.4
.

The aforementioned results have as an immediate corollary the following: given a solution
{\bf u}
of the system (
1.2
) satisfying certain conditions, if it vanishes on an open set
\Omega\subseteq[0,T)\times X
, then it necessarily vanishes identically. Hence, such a solution is global. This consequence is explicitly stated in the corollary of theorem
2.1
, but an analogous result could be proved as a corollary for theorem
2.3
. Moreover, theorem
3.1
is a manifestation of this fact for scalar equations.

In
[
24
]
was proved a unique continuations result for the
b-
equation (
b\in[0,3]
) and, more generally, for equations of the type (
3.1
), with functions
g
and
G
depending only on
u
or
u_{x}
, or both, and
G
is non-negative and vanishes only when
u=0
, see
[
24
, Theorem 1.6]
. Following our language, the results in
[
24
]
were established using local well-posedness. Our theorem
3.1
in particular, and the results presented in section
2
in general, generalise these results not only for equations depending on higher order derivatives, but also for systems of equations.

Recently, conserved quantities have been used for establishing unique continuation properties for solutions of the Dullin-Gottwald-Holm equation
[
13
]
(see also its correction
[
14
]
); the BBM equation
[
7
]
; 0-Holm-Staley equation
[
8
,
10
]
; a generalisation of the CH equation
[
12
]
; and a geometrically integrable equation
[
27
]
. All of these references are concerned to equations, while in
[
11
]
the conservation of norms of solutions of a bi-Hamiltonian system (that has the CH as a particular case) was used to establish unique continuation of its solutions, giving a strong indication that the results (and approaches) considered in
[
24
,
13
,
14
]
could be generalised and extended to systems of differential equations or for equations like (
3.1
) depending on a finite jet of the solution
{\bf u}
.

Most of the works mentioned above are concerned with, or motivated by, hydrodynamic equations, and some of our examples as well. Therefore, it is natural that one wonders if our results could be applied to the KdV equation, that is a quite famous hydrodynamic model. It can be written as

u_{t}=\alpha uu_{x}+u_{xxx}=:A_{1}u,

where
A_{1}=\alpha u\partial_{x}+\partial_{x}^{3}
, and
\alpha\neq 0
is constant that we do not impose any restriction. Comparing the KdV equation with (
1.2
) we see that
A=A_{1}
and
g(u)\equiv 0
. Therefore, none of the conditions
C2
or
C6
are satisfied and our results cannot be applied to it.

Last but not least, it is worth mentioning that our machinery enable us to establish unique continuation properties for the solutions of (
1.4
), which as far as the author knows, has not been proved yet.

7
Conclusion

In this paper we establish conditions for proving unique continuation results for the arbitrary system (
1.1
) using two different points of view. Our results are then applied to some important equations in hydrodynamics, wave propagation and integrable systems. Also, we proved a unique continuation result for the generalised Boussinesq equation (
1.4
) using the tools developed for tackling the class (
1.1
).
