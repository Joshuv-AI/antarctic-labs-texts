Effective Block Preconditioners for Fluid Dynamics Coupled to Reduced Models of a Non-Local Nature
Marc Hirschvogel, Mia Bonini, Maximilian Balmus, David Nordsletten

Abstract
Modeling cardiovascular blood flow is central to many applications in biomedical engineering. To accommodate the complexity of the cardiovascular system, in terms of boundary conditions and surrounding vascular tissue, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) often are coupled to reduced circuit and/or solid mechanics models. These allow for realistic simulations of hemodynamics in the heart or the aorta, but come at additional computational cost and complexity. In this contribution, we design a novel block preconditioner for the solution of the stabilized Navier-Stokes equations coupled to reduced-order models of a non-local nature. These models encompass lumped-parameter systems that impose flux-dependent boundary tractions, and Galerkin reduced-order models that can be used to account for outlying mechanical structures. Here we propose a 3×3 preconditioner derived from the block factorization and approximation to the Schur complement(s). The solver performance is demonstrated for a series of examples with increasing complexity, culminating in a reduced FSI simulation in a patient-specific contracting left heart model. For all test cases, we show that our proposed approach is superior to other frequently presented 2×2 schemes that merge stiffness contributions from reduced models into the fluid Jacobian or consolidate some variables for the purpose of efficiency—with an up to six times shorter overall computing time and/or only half as many linear iterations.

This necessitates efficient iterative solvers for the resulting linearized system of equations, with adequate block preconditioners that accelerate the convergence of stationary algorithms like GMRES

Block preconditioners for the (stabilized) Navier-Stokes equations, where a 2×2 system of velocity and pressure variables arises, are numerous, amongst them variants based on the Schur complement of the velocity block

Similar approaches also have shown to be effective for (single-field compressible) solid mechanics coupled to 0D models

However, introducing a 0D/RD model to Navier-Stokes comes with the addition of a new set of equations, expanding the 2×2 to a 3×3 coupled matrix system to solve.
Often, 0D systems in fluid dynamics are introduced as decoupled in-/outflow boundary conditions, whose stiffness contribution is condensed into the main fluid matrix
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29

However, these approaches introduce dense fill-ins into the Jacobian, which require an extra level of consideration to mitigate potential ill-conditioning of the momentum stiffness matrix
[
28
]

Other solution approaches for hemodynamics with so-called flow-rate “defective” boundary conditions, which are non-local constraints to impose a prescribed flux value at one or more fluid boundaries, similarly result in 3×3 systems, but may be adequately reduced to facilitate usage of 2×2 preconditioners
[
30
,
31
]

In a comparable way, reduced solid mechanics wall models, such as the recently introduced fluid-reduced-solid interaction (FrSI) technique
[
16
]
, may have a decisive effect on the performance of preconditioners.
While it may be feasible to treat these models as if they were part of the fluid, as it is done for the (non-reduced or reduced-dimensional) solid component in cardiovascular FSI
[
29
]
, the projection-based FrSI models will require an extra level of consideration due to the non-locality of their degrees of freedom as a result of the Galerkin projection of the boundary space.

In this contribution, we present a novel Schur complement-based block preconditioner for efficiently solving fluid mechanics coupled to reduced systems of a non-local nature.
This is achieved by separately considering the sub-systems that govern fluid velocity, pressure, and reduced variables. We derive a preconditioner based on the exact block factorization of the resulting 3×3 matrix system, where subsequent approximations to Schur complements are aligned with common approaches
[
19
]
but equivalently can be designed in a flexible way.
It therefore allows to use field-specific solution schemes and preconditioners respecting the individual physics, without compromising the sparsity patterns of the momentum or continuity/Schur complement-related Jacobians. The solver is exemplified on two types of models: fluid-0D systems of blood flow in an Eulerian reference frame, and ALE fluid-0D systems using FrSI to account for solid mechanical contributions.
For the latter type, we propose a monolithic nonlinear solution scheme for the coupled fluid-RD/0D and ALE domain motion problem, where the 3×3 Schur preconditioner is embedded into a (forward) Block Gauss-Seidel method
[
32
]
that effectively preconditions the 4×4 block matrix system of fluid velocities, pressures, reduced variables, and domain displacements.
For fluid-0D models, we show that the proposed solver is superior to 2×2 block preconditioners that operate on either a condensed system (eliminating 0D variables from the main system) or a merged system (consolidating 0D and fluid pressure variables), both in terms of run time and memory.
For ALE fluid-0D FrSI models, where the wall model lives in a POD space linking all boundary degrees of freedom to a few modes, we show that classical 2×2 block approaches fail in generating a feasible Schur complement operator in terms of memory needs due to the inherent link of reduced velocity variables to the fluid pressure space.
Our results indicate that the proposed preconditioners are effective, displaying the least amount of linear iterations and reduced computational demand compared to the alternative approaches.
Results are generated using two different solver frameworks: the multi-physics solver
\mathcal{C}\text{Heart}

[
33
]
as well as the FEniCSx-based
[
34
,
35
]
open-source multi-physics cardiovascular solver Ambit
[
36
]
.

{}_{\text{1}}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{vv}&\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{vp}&\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{v\mathit{\Lambda}}\\

\Delta\bm{\mathsf{p}}\\

\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{p}\\

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{vv}-\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{v\mathit{\Lambda}}\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathit{\Lambda}\mathit{\Lambda}})^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathit{\Lambda}v}&\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{vp}\\

(5)

2.2
Discretized Form for ALE Fluid Coupled to 0D and RD FrSI Models

Considering the problem of a discrete ALE fluid coupled to both 0D and RD FrSI models adds two key conceptual differences compared to the Eulerian fluid-0D system introduced in the previous section.
First, introduction of the Arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian (ALE) reference frame requires the solution of a domain motion problem, typically found using a harmonic extension or pseudo-mechanical formulation
[
39
]
.
This can be integrated by introducing the discrete domain motion problem
\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{d}
to the root-finding problem.
Secondly, the reduced-dimensional wall model representing the elasticity of the boundary is confined to a subspace spanned by POD modes that either have been precomputed from an external data source (another high-dimensional model, observations from real world) or are synthetically generated, depending on the application.
We define the operator that projects the full-dimensional model to a boundary-reduced model by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{v}\times(r_{v}+n_{v}^{\mathit{\Omega}})},

(6)

where
n_{v}
is the size of the full velocity space,
n_{v}^{\mathit{\Omega}}
the size of the space of bulk (non-boundary) velocities, and
r_{v}
the number of reduced boundary modes (degrees of freedom). This Galerkin projector, thereby, confines all velocity degrees of freedom of
\mathit{\Gamma}_{0}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax\tilde{\text{s}}}}
to the lower-dimensional subspace but provides an identity mapping for the velocity degrees of freedom associated to the bulk domain.
Details of the governing equations and discrete weak form systems are provided in the
Supplementary Material
, Sec.
9.1.2
and Sec.
9.2.2
.

Similar to the Eulerian fluid-0D system, these problems can be written as root-finding problem at the current time step indexed by
n+1
, where we seek to find the discrete velocity
\bm{\mathsf{v}}_{n+1}
, pressure
\bm{\mathsf{p}}_{n+1}
, 0D model variables
\bm{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{n+1}
, and domain displacements
\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{n+1}
satisfying

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{n+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{v}(\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{v}}},\bm{\mathsf{p}},\bm{\mathsf{\Lambda}},\bm{\mathsf{d}})\\
\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{p}(\bm{\mathsf{p}},\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{v}}},\bm{\mathsf{d}})\\
\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{\mathit{\Lambda}}(\bm{\mathsf{\Lambda}},\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{v}}},\bm{\mathsf{d}})\\
\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{d}(\bm{\mathsf{d}},\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{v}}})\end{bmatrix}_{n+1}=\bm{\mathsf{0}},

(7)

with the trial space projection

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{v}}=\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{v}}},

(8)

where
\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{v}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{v}+n_{v}^{\mathit{\Omega}}}
is the (partly) reduced-dimensional velocity vector.
Further details on the FrSI technique can be found in
[
16
]

\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{p}\\

\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{\mathit{\Lambda}}\\
\\
\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{d}\end{bmatrix}_{n+1}^{k},

where sub-block matrices of

Partitioned FrSI

As an alternative to the monolithic approach, the linearized fluid-0D system and ALE domain motion can be solved in a staggered way.
This approach allows to operate on reduced overall system sizes and re-use tailored solvers and preconditioners for fluid-0D systems.
In this case, the fluid-0D system simplifies to a matrix problem similar to the three-field approach (though incorporating potentially dense rows/columns due to POD reduction),
e.g.
,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{vv}\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}}&\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{vp}&\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{v\mathit{\Lambda}}\\
\\
\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{pv}\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}}&\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{pp}&{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bm{\mathsf{0}}}\\
\\
\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathit{\Lambda}v}\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}}&{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bm{\mathsf{0}}}&\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathit{\Lambda}\mathit{\Lambda}}\end{bmatrix}_{n+1}^{k}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{v}}}\\

\Delta\bm{\mathsf{p}}\\
\\
\Delta\bm{\mathsf{\Lambda}}\end{bmatrix}_{n+1}^{k+1}=-\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{V}}_{v}^{\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{T}}}\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{v}\\
\\
\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{p}\\
\\
\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{\mathit{\Lambda}}\end{bmatrix}_{n+1}^{k},

(10)

which is solved prior to the ALE domain motion,

\displaystyle[\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{dd}]_{n+1}^{k}\,[\Delta\bm{\mathsf{d}}]_{n+1}^{k+1}=-[\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{d}]_{n+1}^{k},

(11)

2.3
Preconditioning and Linear Solution

In the following, we present suitable preconditioning techniques to efficiently solve the linearized system of equations for fluid-0D and ALE fluid-0D FrSI problems.
We start by reviewing established preconditioning methods for the 2×2 systems of equations,
e.g.
, Eq. (
4
).
Next, we present a novel 3×3 block preconditioning algorithm that can deal with different types of modeling elements that introduce a non-local structure into the matrix system.

The solution of the linear system of equations (
e.g.
, each Newton-Raphson iteration for each time step) requires the solution to the linear system

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{K}}\Delta\bm{\mathsf{x}}=-\bm{\mathsf{r}},

(12)

with
\bm{\mathsf{K}}
being a (block) matrix as previously discussed,
\Delta\bm{\mathsf{x}}
the combined discrete update vector of all state variables (
e.g.
, velocities, pressures, multipliers), and
\bm{\mathsf{r}}
the block right-hand side residual vector. Note, for ease of notation, we have dropped iterative counters.

Solving Eq. (
12
) using direct methods (such as LU factorization or multi-frontal approaches
[
40
,
41
]
) becomes increasingly expensive for large systems as they typically scale
\sim\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{dof}}^{2})
.
Iterative approaches, such as the Generalized Minimal Residual method (GMRES)
[
18
]
, can significantly reduce computing costs; however, their efficiency depends significantly on the eigenspectrum of
\bm{\mathsf{K}}
.
The application of so-called
right
preconditioners seeks to alter the system in order to improve the eigenspectrum and thus the performance of many iterative solution techniques.
This is achieved by altering the solution to Eq. (
12
) into a two-step process whereby we

\displaystyle\textbf{solve: }\bm{\mathsf{K}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{y}}=-\bm{\mathsf{r}},\quad\textbf{solve: }\bm{\mathcal{P}}\Delta\bm{\mathsf{x}}=\bm{\mathsf{y}}.

(13)

In this case, the effectiveness of iterative techniques depends now on the eigenspectrum of
\bm{\mathsf{K}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}
.
Here we seek to construct an effective approximation where
\varrho(\bm{\mathsf{K}}\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{-1})\sim 1
while the action of
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}
can be applied using efficient iterative solution techniques (such as algebraic multigrid or additive Schwarz decomposition).
In this case, since
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}
is not analytically found, but its action approximated, we use the right preconditioned Flexible Generalized Minimal Residual method (FGMRES)
[
42
]
.

2.3.1
Schur Complement Preconditioner S2×2

A proposal to the solution of both systems introduced would be to utilize a Schur complement preconditioner (here denoted by S2×2), whereby the variables are lumped to construct a 2×2 matrix system with the general form

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{A}}&\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}\\
\\
\hat{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}&\bm{\mathsf{C}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{\mathsf{x}}_{A}\\
\\
\Delta\bm{\mathsf{x}}_{C}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{A}\\
\\
\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{C}\end{bmatrix},

(14)

where
\bm{\mathsf{A}},\ldots,\bm{\mathsf{C}}
denote sub-blocks related to different components of the physical system,
\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{A}
and
\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{C}
the equivalent discrete residual vector functions, and
\Delta\bm{\mathsf{x}}_{A}
and
\Delta\bm{\mathsf{x}}_{C}
the updates sought.
Typically, this form is found when solving the stabilized Navier-Stokes problems
[
3
]
; however, Eq. (
14
) may also be representative of the case where any contributions of reduced models are implicitly integrated (condensed) into the momentum residual,
\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{A}
, cf. Eq. (
4
), or in case some variables (
e.g.
, fluid pressures and 3D-0D multipliers) are consolidated from Eq. (
3
).
A well-known class of preconditioners for the Navier-Stokes system Eq. (
14
) are based on finding an approximation to the Schur complement of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{S}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}-\hat{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}\,\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathrm{T}},

(15)

and aim at providing an approximation to the block matrix inverse based on its block factorization, which can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{A}}&\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}\\
\\
\hat{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}&\bm{\mathsf{C}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{A}}&{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bm{\mathsf{0}}}\\
\\
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bm{\mathsf{0}}}&\bm{\mathsf{I}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{I}}&{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bm{\mathsf{0}}}\\
\\
\hat{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}&\bm{\mathsf{I}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}&{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bm{\mathsf{0}}}\\
\\
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bm{\mathsf{0}}}&\bm{\mathsf{S}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{I}}&\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}\\
\\
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bm{\mathsf{0}}}&\bm{\mathsf{I}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{A}}&{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bm{\mathsf{0}}}\\
\\
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bm{\mathsf{0}}}&\bm{\mathsf{I}}\end{bmatrix}.

2.3.3
Block Preconditioners for FrSI

Fluid-reduced-solid interaction (FrSI) involves the Galerkin projection of prior data to a lower-dimensional subspace spanned by Eq. (
6
), introducing a narrow-banded, dense sub-block into the fluid momentum and pressure coupling blocks.
Similar to non-local boundary conditions, this can amplify the number of non-zeros and limit capacity in the use of efficient solvers. Figure
3
A shows the native sparsity pattern of the 3×3 fluid sub-system with reduced solid, either Eq. (
10
) or Eq. (
9
) with omitted rows and columns from the ALE problem (for demonstrative purposes). Two main aspects that may deteriorate solver performance arise:

•

Narrow bands in the
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
block reach out to far off-diagonal locations due to the dependence of a few projected boundary residuals on many non-projected velocity degrees of freedom belonging to the bulk, and vice versa. This may cause suboptimal performance of preconditioned iterative methods that operate on this block.

•

Further dense narrow bands appear in the
\hat{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}
blocks due to the dependence of many continuity residuals on a few projected boundary velocities, and vice versa. These render dense fill-ins of the size of the whole boundary pressure space when computing the product
\hat{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}
which is involved in the Schur complement, cf. Eq. (
27
). These may either cause depletion of CPUs’ memory resources during matrix allocation, or may significantly slow down typical preconditioned iterative methods.

By restructuring into a 3×3 (see Fig.
3
B), these dense non-local sub-blocks can be efficiently clustered.
This enables blocks with large rank (with degrees of freedom in the velocity and pressure spaces) maintain efficient sparsity. Further, Schur complement matrices which must be formed and their inverses approximated either retain local sparsity inherent to the finite element scheme (in the case of
\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{S}}}
) or are dense but low rank (in the case of
\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{W}}}
) due to the reduced dimension of the Galerkin projection.

Remark:

However, our experiences have shown only very little improvement with respect to GMRES iterations, which do not compensate for the computational cost needed to do the extra solve.

Test Examples

To test the impact of the preconditioners discussed in this section, we consider four examples of increasing complexity (see Fig.
4
).
More details on the specifics of each model problem formulation can be found in the
Supplementary Material
, Sec.
9.3
.

Blocked Pipe Flow with Bypass (Fluid-0D)

First, we consider the academic example of Eulerian fluid flow through an idealized pipe (see Fig.
4
A), with a blockage that is bypassed via a 0D model interconnecting two boundaries of the 3D fluid region (Sec.
3
).
The example is simple enough for reproducibility but demonstrates the influence of more interconnected coupling between 3D and 0D models, as is common to more complex cardiovascular simulations.

Deformable Pipe Flow with Resistive BC (FrSI-0D)

The second example presented builds on the same pipe model, but with different boundary conditions, now accounting for the deformability of the wall by means of the fluid-reduced-solid interaction (FrSI)
[
16
]
method (fluid in ALE reference frame) (see Fig.
4
B).
The setup is kept equally simple in order to facilitate reproducibility, using only one synthetically generated deformation mode for the lateral wall and a resistance-only 0D model at the outflow (Sec.
4
).

Blood Flow in Patient-Specific Aortic Arch (Fluid-0D)

Increasing the problem complexity, we present a patient-specific example of Eulerian blood flow through a rigid aortic arch in Sec.
5
, coupled to a closed-loop circulation model that interlinks several in- and outflow boundaries of the 3D model (see Fig.
4
C).
It hence can be considered as a very general case of 3D-0D coupling using a complex geometry with boundary layer as well as delicate outflow structures like the coronary arteries.

Hemodynamics in the Left Heart (FrSI-0D)

Finally, we present a FrSI model of a patient-specific left heart and aortic root model in Sec.
6
, linked to a closed-loop model of systemic, pulmonary, and coronary circulation with several in- and outflows to and from the 3D domain, including immersed valve models (see Fig.
4
D). We further incorporate novel methods that deal with a POD space that is regionally local to atrium, ventricle, and aorta, hence allowing individual contraction behavior of the active chambers. This model is meant to demonstrate the performance of the solver even for computationally challenging large-deformation ALE fluid problems with sudden changes in the flow state (isovolumetric phases, ejection, filling).

For each example, the efficacy of the solvers is evaluated by considering three levels of spatial refinement (rf0, rf1, rf2), which are shown in Fig.
5
for all of the examples, along with the degree of freedom tables for each of the sub-systems (velocity, pressure, multipliers/reduced variables, grid displacement).
To examine solver performance, total run time is quantified along with the number of linear solves per time step (GMRES iterations) and number of nonlinear iterations.
Critically, solvers must demonstrate parallel scalability to enable effective use for large problems.
To assess scalability, both weak and strong scaling are evaluated.
For comparability, all nonlinear and linear solver parameters remain constant for the examples and results reported in this chapter. They are summarized in Tab.

7
Discussion

This paper introduces a novel block preconditioner tailored towards efficiently solving stabilized Navier-Stokes equations with strong coupling to reduced models of a non-local nature.
The preconditioner exploits the 3×3 block structure of the monolithic problem and allows for use of efficient field-specific solvers for the fluid momentum, continuity of mass, and constraint/reduced equations.
It may be seen as an extension to the concept of Schur complement preconditioners for 2×2 systems
[
19
]
, retaining the flexibility for different types of approximations to the Schur complement
[
23
]
.
The proposed methodology preserves the underlying 3×3 matrix structure without the need for updates to the Jacobian originating from integration of reduced models as is needed in other commonly used strategies
[
27
,
29
]
. We demonstrate that the proposed preconditioner is highly suitable and effective for solving computational fluid dynamics, both with strong coupling to 0D lumped-parameter models as well as projection-based reduced wall models of co-dimension 2 accommodating the elasticity of the fluid’s boundary.
The preconditioner is evaluated over four examples, testing efficacy in simple, repeatable tests as well as complex patient-specific cardiovascular applications.

7.1
Preconditioning of Fluid-0D Models

From Tab.
2
and Fig.
14
A&B, we see that for the two fluid-0D test problems (
e.g.
,
blocked pipe
,
aortic arch
), the introduced S3×3 solver compares competitively with S2×2(p) and superior to S2×2(c/c
⋆
).
For smaller problem sizes (rf0, rf1), S3×3 and S2×2(p) compare favorably with similar compute times and linear iterations per FGMRES solve.
However, as the system size increases (rf2), the S3×3 solver shows a 15—20 % reduction in compute time and a 30—50 % reduction in the number of linear iterations.
This separation in compute cost as the system size increases likely stems from inaccuracies of 0D model variable updates in the approximate Schur solve.
In contrast, S2×2(c) and S2×2(c
⋆
) perform significantly worse, increasing compute times by a factor 5—6 and 3—4 for the most refined systems, respectively.
This impact is likely caused by the introduction of dense sub-blocks into the primary fluid matrix.

From Fig.
15
A&B, we see that for the two fluid-0D tests, pure AMG is superior with respect to overall computing time when comparing to S3×3 with the original settings (using inner solves on the
A
- and
S
-block), whereas AMG linear iterations are increased (and growing significantly with problem size), limiting the effectiveness per iteration. Optimizing S3×3 for speed—compromising on linear iteration effectiveness—by using preconditioner sweeps (“pre-only”) instead of iterative solves on the sub-problems, run times can also be decreased, eventually being superior to AMG with the given settings (with more limited growth with problem size). This is likely due to the fact that AMG may be able to effectively construct coarse grids of the 3×3 system due to its overall sparse structure, however deteriorates in representing the strong coupling features of the system.

The approaches in
[
26
,
27
,
29
]
deal with preconditioning fluid-0D systems, where the 0D model contributes by rank 1 updates to the fluid Jacobian (matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
), retaining a 2×2 system of equations amenable to S2×2 preconditioners of the type Eq. (
18
) with minor modifications
[
26
]
.
While this approach has been shown efficient for a number of applications, challenges arise for applications that introduce constraints of a non-local nature (such as 0D models).
The 0D model’s inherent non-locality endorses rank 1 updates to the momentum stiffness matrix that may alter its standard sparsity pattern, particularly for in-/outflow boundaries of larger size (
e.g.
, increasing the rank of the non-local constraints).
This is considered even more challenging if the 0D model interlinks several boundaries of the 3D model, where a change of flow at surface
i
due to a change in pressure at surface
j
has to be accounted for. This coupling endorses further fill-ins at offdiagonal locations, cf. Fig.
2
A. However, for similar models, it was stated that these offdiagonal blocks may be neglected in the tangent matrix (resulting in a system sketched in Fig.
2
B), presumably improving preconditioner performance while not or only negligibly affecting nonlinear iterations
[
27
,
38
]
. Our results here confirm these findings, seeing no change in nonlinear solver performance and shorter run times for S2×2(c
⋆
) compared to S2×2(c).

The effects of preconditioning these 2×2 systems are exemplified in Tab.
2
and Fig.
14
A&B, where dense sub-blocks associated with the coupled boundaries are introduced into the fluid momentum block for S2×2(c/c
⋆
), leading to increased compute time. On the contrary, S2×2(p) presumably suffers from the offdiagonal multiplier-velocity coupling blocks collapsing inside the Schur complement, whose approximation is limited Eq. (
19
) and whose solve is only done up to a rough tolerance (cf. Tab.
1
), leading to increased linear iterations compared to using a direct (hence exact) solve on the reduced block like in S3×3.

Nonetheless, retaining 2×2 block systems for fluid-0D problems can be advantageous when access to the solver infrastructure is limited, providing increased modularity for coupling 0D models to fluid systems without the need for more invasive alterations of the equation/code structure. Like this, an ordinary fluid system is retained, which more easily can be interfaced to provide the needed residual and Jacobian updates in case of the statically condensed approach.

7.2
Preconditioning of ALE Fluid-0D/RD (FrSI) Models

Comparing the ALE fluid-0D/RD (FrSI) models (
e.g.
,
deformable pipe
,
left heart
),
3×3 solvers are shown to perform superior to their 2×2 counterparts (see Tab.
2
and Fig.
14
C&D).
Using S2×2(p) as a benchmark (which outperformed S2×2(c/c
⋆
) in the fluid-0D tests), the 3×3 preconditioners were approximately 5 to 14 times faster in the deformable pipe example (for rf0 and rf1, respectively).
For all other models, the size of the introduced dense sub-block into the Schur complement resulted in the solver running out of memory (“OOM”) or poor Schur complement approximations that prevented convergence of the Newton solver given the linear solver settings, cf. Tab.
1
(“DIV”).
In addition, we considered both S3×3, which performs a partitioned solve of the ALE domain motion sub-problem within the nonlinear fixed point, and BGS-S3×3, which introduces a Block Gauss-Seidel approximation to the monolithic problem including the domain motion.
Both S3×3 and BGS-S3×3 perform comparably for the pipe model, likely due to the simplicity of the solid motion (which is restricted to one POD mode).
However, in the more realistic
left heart
example, the impact of the grid motion yields an approximate 2-fold increase in compute time due to the increase in the number of nonlinear iterations required. Therefore, a monolithic approach preconditioned with BGS-S3×3 is considered the most stable and effective option here.

From Fig.
15
C&D, we see that for the two ALE fluid-0D/RD (FrSI) tests, pure AMG is inferior with respect to overall computing time to both BGS-S3×3 with the original settings (using inner solves on the
A
-,
S
-, and
G
-block) as well as to the speed-optimized version, where solves on the iterative sub-problems are replaced by preconditioner sweeps (“pre-only”). As for the fluid-0D tests, AMG experiences ineffectiveness per linear iteration, in the extreme case showing on average
>\!800
linear iterations per solve for the left heart model.

Preconditioners for Navier-Stokes equations that are coupled to solid mechanics models of reduced or 3D nature (fluid-solid interaction, FSI) have to deal with the added complexity of another type of field equations entering the system.
For monolithic FSI, Block Gauss-Seidel (BGS) methods with single-field AMG-preconditioned solvers have proven to be effective
[
49
,
32
]
, similar to more intrusive approaches using AMG on the fully coupled system and BGS on each multigrid level
[
32
]
. Alternative methods are based on Schwarz decomposition techniques
[
50
]
, and some combine the former and the latter to effectively reduce FSI interface errors
[
51
]
.
Other 3D FSI preconditioners, on the contrary, embed the contribution of the solid into the main fluid Jacobian in order to facilitate usage of 2×2 Schur complement-based block preconditioners for the FSI system
[
29
]
.
Therein, this avenue equally has been pursued for solid models of co-dimension 2, that is, for reduced models defined on the fluid’s boundary. While the effectiveness of these approaches is shown, in our contribution, we uniquely deal with a reduced solid model of co-dimension 2 which has a
non-local
structure.
This so-called fluid-reduced-solid interaction (FrSI) methodology
[
16
]
performs a Galerkin projection of the fluid-tissue boundary to confine it to a subspace spanned by POD modes.
As outlined in Sec.
2.3.3
, the non-locality of this model introduces Jacobian contributions that deteriorate the sparsity patterns of both the
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
block and the Schur complement when using standard 2×2 preconditioning methods, i.e. S2×2(p).
For the flexible pipe example, Sec.
4
, we observe that using S2×2(p) for discretization
\mathrm{rf2}
fails due to depletion of the CPUs’ memory resources.
This is explained by the need to perform the matrix product
\hat{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}
when computing the Schur complement, Eq. (
27
).
This becomes apparent in the sparsity sketch in Fig.
3
A, where the reduced boundary residuals depend on the size of the (non-reduced) fluid pressure space.
Vice versa, the non-reduced continuity residuals exhibit a dependence on the reduced set of boundary velocities.
Hence, the respective matrix product introduces a dense fill-in on the Schur complement of the size of the entire boundary pressure space, hampering a memory-optimized matrix allocation.
For the left heart hemodynamics model, Sec.
6
, the effectiveness of the provided approximate block inverses deteriorates.
We observe that nonlinear solver convergence even for the coarser meshes (
\mathrm{rf0}
and
\mathrm{rf1}
) cannot be achieved for the given solver settings, while the fine mesh (
\mathrm{rf2}
) similarly cannot be setup due to memory limits, cf. Tab.
2
.
Therefore, for our FSI models of non-local nature, S3×3 represents the only candidate (of considered preconditioners) that allows an effective and scalable preconditioning of the underlying coupled matrix systems.

7.3
Outlook

The presented solver enables the efficient solution of complex CFD and FrSI models and will pave the way for future research on large-scale patient-specific models of blood flow in arteries or the heart cavities. It will facilitate the integration of closed-circuit 0D models as well as reduced-dimensional Galerkin ROMs that provide boundary subspaces for large-strain solid models of co-dimension 2. In order to increase the flexibility of the proposed preconditioner, more sophisticated approximations for the Schur complement readily can be integrated into S3×3, providing robustness for strongly convection-dominated flows at high Reynolds numbers
[
23
,
19
,
26
]
. Approaches presented in
[
32
]
, switching the order of block decomposition and (multigrid-preconditioned) single-field solves, also may be suitable candidates for improving the Schur approximation. Furthermore, the solver readily may be used and tested for inf-sup stable approximation pairs of velocity and pressure,
e.g.
, Taylor-Hood finite elements, which have not been considered in this contribution.

8
Conclusion

We present a novel 3×3 block preconditioner that is designed for accelerating the convergence of solvers for the finite element-discretized 3D stabilized Navier-Stokes equations, subject to constraints of a non-local nature.
These include coupling to flux-dependent boundary conditions at in- or outflows as well as Galerkin projection-based reduced wall/FSI models of co-dimension 2.
The preconditioner leverages the intrinsic block matrix structure of the linearized three-equation system of fluid momentum, conservation of mass, and constraints/reduced momentum terms and extends the concept of 2×2 Schur complement solvers.
At the same time, it preserves the modularity of these concepts by allowing any suitable approximations for the Schur complements.
With the presented approach, all sparsity patterns of the original (non-constrained) fluid matrices are retained, and optimal field-specific solvers (AMG-preconditioned GMRES for fluid momentum and Schur complement, direct solve for reduced variable block) can be used.
Conclusively, superiority of this preconditioner compared to more classical 2×2 approaches, which need some block matrix manipulations deteriorating the native sparsity patterns, is shown, with an up to 50 % reduction in linear iterations and up to 80 % reduction in overall computing time, depending on the model and the solver.

In this section, we state the strong forms of 3D fluid dynamics coupled to reduced models of a non-local nature in a very general form. The equations governing Eulerian fluid flow with flux-dependent traction constraints are elaborated in Sec.
9.1.1
, and those for ALE fluid with a reduced-dimensional solid mechanics wall model of co-dimension 2 in Sec.
9.1.2
.

9.1.1
Eulerian Fluid Coupled to 0D Models

The general strong form of the governing equations for incompressible Navier-Stokes flow in an Eulerian reference frame with coupling to reduced 0D models is elaborated. The primary variables are the fluid velocity
\bm{v}
, fluid pressure
p
, and a set of multipliers that enforce flux constraints to a lumped-parameter model at
n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}
boundaries,
\{\mathit{\Lambda}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}=\left\{\mathit{\Lambda}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{\Lambda}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\right\}
. The initial boundary value problem is stated as follows:

\displaystyle\rho\left(\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}+(\nabla\bm{v})\bm{v}\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}(\bm{v},p)\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\mathit{\Omega}\times[0,T],

(33)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{v}

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\tilde{\mathit{\Omega}}\times[0,T],

(34)

\displaystyle\bm{t}_{i}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax 0d}}

\displaystyle=-\mathit{\Lambda}_{i}\,\bm{n}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\mathit{\Gamma}_{i}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax 0d}}\times[0,T],\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}\},

(35)

\displaystyle\bm{t}^{N}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{t}}_{N}(t,\bm{v})\quad

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\mathit{\Gamma}^{N}\times[0,T],

(36)

\displaystyle\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{v}}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\mathit{\Gamma}^{D}\times[0,T],

(37)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{j}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax 0d}}}\bm{v}\cdot\bm{n}\,\mathrm{d}A

\displaystyle=\alpha_{j}\,q_{j}(\{\mathit{\Lambda}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}})\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\;[0,T],\quad j\in\{1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}\},

(38)

where
\nabla
is the gradient operator with respect to physical space,
\nabla\bm{v}:=\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\bm{e}_{i}\otimes\bm{e}_{j}
,
\rho
is the fluid’s density,
\bm{n}
a unit outward normal vector, and
\bm{t}^{N}(t,\bm{v})
a generic, possibly time- or velocity-dependent Neumann or Robin-like traction term. The fluid is Newtonian (dynamic viscosity
\mu
), with the constitutive equation for the Cauchy stress

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(\bm{v},p)=-p\bm{I}+\mu\left(\nabla\bm{v}+(\nabla\bm{v})^{\mathrm{T}}\right).

(39)

The constraint Eq. (
38
) enforces consistency between the flux over a fluid boundary and a flux
q_{j}
emerging from the solution of the 0D model problem, possibly dependent on one or more multiplier variables. Scaling parameters
\alpha_{j}
account for the directionality of flow, i.e. should take the value
-1
if a 0D flux variable is imposed as inflow to the fluid domain, and
1
otherwise. Specific forms of possible 0D models are given in Sec.
9.4
and will be introduced along with the example-specific problems.

9.1.2
ALE Fluid Coupled to 0D and RD Models (Fluid-reduced-Solid Interaction, FrSI)

The strong form of the governing equations for incompressible Navier-Stokes flow in an ALE reference frame
[
4
,
5
]
with coupling to reduced 0D models as well as solid mechanics models of co-dimension 2 is elaborated. This constitutes the physics-related part of the FrSI method
[
16
]
. The primary variables here are the fluid velocity
\bm{v}
, fluid pressure
p
, a set of multipliers that enforce flux constraints to a lumped-parameter model at
n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}
boundaries,
\{\mathit{\Lambda}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}=\left\{\mathit{\Lambda}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{\Lambda}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\right\}
, as well as the domain displacement
\bm{d}
. In order to generate this arbitrary motion field, a model of the type

\displaystyle\nabla_{0}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{g}}(\bm{d})

\displaystyle=\bm{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\mathit{\Omega}_{0}\times[0,T],

(40)

\displaystyle\bm{d}

\displaystyle=\bm{u}_{\mathrm{f}}(\bm{v})\quad

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\mathit{\Gamma}_{0}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax\tilde{\text{s}}}}\times[0,T],

(41)

is employed. On the deformable boundary
\mathit{\Gamma}_{0}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax\tilde{\text{s}}}}
, the model is subject to a Dirichlet condition that takes the value of the fluid displacement

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{\mathrm{f}}(\bm{v})=\int\limits_{0}^{t}\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{t}.

(42)

A constitutive equation for
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{g}}(\bm{d})
may be chosen dependent on the problem and will be detailed later. We introduce the kinematics of the bijective mapping between the reference and the arbitrary domain deformation, namely the domain velocity, deformation gradient as well as its determinant, as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{w}}=\frac{\partial\bm{d}}{\partial t},\quad\widehat{\bm{F}}=\bm{I}+\nabla_{0}\bm{d}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{J}=\det\widehat{\bm{F}}.

(43)

The initial boundary value problem with the tractions from the 0D constraint and reduced solid model, Neumann as well as Dirichlet conditions reads

\displaystyle\rho\left(\left.\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}\right|_{\bm{x}_{0}}+(\nabla_{0}\bm{v}\,\widehat{\bm{F}}^{-1})(\bm{v}-\widehat{\bm{w}})\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{0}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{v},p,\bm{d}):\widehat{\bm{F}}^{-\mathrm{T}}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\mathit{\Omega}_{0}\times[0,T],

(44)

\displaystyle\nabla_{0}\bm{v}:\widehat{\bm{F}}^{-\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\tilde{\mathit{\Omega}}_{0}\times[0,T],

(45)

\displaystyle\bm{t}_{0,i}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax 0d}}

\displaystyle=-\mathit{\Lambda}_{i}\,\widehat{J}\widehat{\bm{F}}^{-\mathrm{T}}\bm{n}_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\mathit{\Gamma}_{0,i}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax 0d}}\times[0,T],

(46)

\displaystyle\qquad i\in\{1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}\},

\displaystyle\bm{t}_{0}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax\tilde{\text{s}}}}

\displaystyle=-h_{0}\left(\rho_{0,\text{s}}\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}-\tilde{\nabla}_{0}\cdot\tilde{\bm{P}}(\bm{u}_{\text{f}}(\bm{v}))\right)\quad

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\mathit{\Gamma}_{0}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax\tilde{\text{s}}}}\times[0,T],

(47)

\displaystyle\bm{t}_{0}^{N}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{t}}_{N}(t,\bm{v})\quad

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\mathit{\Gamma}_{0}^{N}\times[0,T],

(48)

\displaystyle\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{v}}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\mathit{\Gamma}_{0}^{D}\times[0,T],

(49)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{0,j}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax 0d}}}(\bm{v}-\widehat{\bm{w}})\cdot\widehat{J}\widehat{\bm{F}}^{-\mathrm{T}}\bm{n}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}A_{0}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{j}\,q_{j}(\{\mathit{\Lambda}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}})\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\;[0,T],

(50)

\displaystyle\qquad j\in\{1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}\},

where
\nabla_{0}
denotes the gradient operator with respect to reference space (
\nabla_{0}\bm{v}:=\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{0,j}}\bm{e}_{i}\otimes\bm{e}_{j}
or
\nabla_{0}\bm{\sigma}:=\frac{\partial\sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_{0,k}}\bm{e}_{i}\otimes\bm{e}_{j}\otimes\bm{e}_{k}
), and
\left.\frac{\partial(\bullet)}{\partial t}\right|_{\bm{x}_{0}}
is the time derivative in the ALE frame. Further,
\rho
and
\rho_{0,\text{s}}
are the fluid’s and reduced solid’s density, respectively,
\bm{n}_{0}
a unit outward normal vector in the reference frame, and
\bm{t}_{0}^{N}(t,\bm{v})
a generic, possibly time- or velocity-dependent Neumann or Robin-like traction vector. The fluid is Newtonian (dynamic viscosity
\mu
), with the ALE version of the constitutive equation for the Cauchy stress

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(\bm{v},p,\bm{d})=-p\bm{I}+\mu\left(\nabla_{0}\bm{v}\,\widehat{\bm{F}}(\bm{d})^{-1}+\widehat{\bm{F}}(\bm{d})^{-\mathrm{T}}(\nabla_{0}\bm{v})^{\mathrm{T}}\right).

(51)

The constraint Eq. (
50
) enforces consistency between the flux over a fluid boundary and a flux
q_{j}
emerging from the solution of the 0D model problem, possibly dependent on one or more multiplier variables. Parameters
\alpha_{j}
are introduced similar to the Eulerian case, cf. Eq. (
38
). Specific forms of possible 0D models are given in Sec.
9.4
and will be introduced along with the example-specific problems.

The physics-based reduction component of the FrSI method entails a finite strain solid dynamics model living on the 2-dimensional manifold of the fluid-solid interface
\mathit{\Gamma}_{0}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax\tilde{\text{s}}}}
. Its governing equation is the balance of linear momentum of nonlinear elastodynamics, eventually resulting in the boundary traction given by Eq. (
47
). Therein,
h_{0}
represents a wall thickness parameter, and
\tilde{\nabla}_{0}
is the gradient operator on the 2D manifold (with vanishing surface normal components)
[
14
,
16
]
. The first Piola-Kirchhoff stress is mapped from its material counterpart, the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress, as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{P}}=(\bm{F}_{\text{f}}-\bm{F}_{\text{f}}\,\bm{n}_{0}\otimes\bm{n}_{0})\tilde{\bm{S}},

(52)

with the fluid deformation gradient

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{\text{f}}=\bm{I}+\nabla_{0}\bm{u}_{\text{f}},

(53)

using Eq. (
42
). Making use of Eq. (
53
), eliminating the normal components, and re-defining the out-of-plane stretch on assumptions of incompressibility, the membrane right Cauchy-Green tensor
\tilde{\bm{C}}
of the 2-dimensional manifold as well as its time derivative can be defined
[
16
]
. The specific constitutive equation for the stress tensor
\tilde{\bm{S}}
in Eq. (
52
) will be introduced along with the examples presented later. Eventually, the projection-based component of FrSI is performed post spatio-temporal discretization and detailed in Sec.
2.2
.

9.2
Weak Forms and Time-Discrete Representations

Here, we state the generic time-discrete weak representations of the strong governing equations for Eulerian and ALE fluid dynamics coupled to 0D models, cf. Sec.
9.1.1
and Sec.
9.1.2
, respectively.
We use an implicit single-step time integration method based on the generalized midpoint scheme, where a quantity at time instance
t^{n+\theta}
is a linear combination of the value of the current time step at
t^{n+1}
and the one of the old step at
t^{n}
:

\displaystyle(\bullet)^{n+\theta}:=\theta(\bullet)^{n+1}+(1-\theta)(\bullet)^{n},

(54)

with
\theta\in(0,1]
. For the subsequent finite element approximation, let
\mathcal{T}^{h}(\mathit{\Omega}_{0})
denote the set of elements corresponding to the 3D discretization and let

\mathit{\Omega}^{h}_{0}=\bigcup\limits_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}^{h}(\mathit{\Omega}_{0})}\tau

(55)

indicate the meshed representation of the domain
\mathit{\Omega}_{0}
. All discrete function spaces are based on continuous piecewise polynomial spaces in the form of:

\displaystyle S^{k}(\mathit{\Omega}^{h}_{0})=\left\{f:\mathit{\Omega}^{h}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\,|\,f\in C^{0}({\bar{\mathit{\Omega}}^{h}_{0}}),\,\left.f\right|_{\tau}\in\mathbb{P}^{k}(\tau)\,\hskip 8.53581pt\forall\,\tau\in\mathcal{T}^{h}(\mathit{\Omega}_{0})\right\},

(56)

representing the general continuous
k
th-order piecewise polynomial spaces defined on
\mathit{\Omega}^{h}_{0}
. We define the velocity, domain displacement, and pressure spaces as follows:

\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{h}=\left[S^{1}\left(\mathit{\Omega}^{h}_{0}\right)\right]^{3}\text{, }\bm{\mathcal{D}}^{h}=\left[S^{1}\left(\mathit{\Omega}^{h}_{0}\right)\right]^{3},\text{ and }\mathcal{W}^{h}=S^{1}(\tilde{\mathit{\Omega}}^{h}_{0}).

The sub-spaces incorporating the Dirichlet and homogeneous boundary read:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{D,h}

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{v}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{h}\,|\,\bm{v}=\bm{v}^{d}\text{ on }\mathit{\Gamma}_{0}^{D,h}\right\},

(57)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{0,h}

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{v}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{h}\,|\,\bm{v}=\bm{0}\text{ on }\mathit{\Gamma}_{0}^{D,h}\right\},

(58)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{D}}^{D,h}

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{d}\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}^{h}\,|\,\bm{d}=\bm{d}^{d}\text{ on }\mathit{\Gamma}_{0}^{D,h}\right\},

(59)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{D}}^{0,h}

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{d}\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}^{h}\,|\,\bm{d}=\bm{0}\text{ on }\mathit{\Gamma}_{0}^{D,h}\right\}.

(60)

Here, we allow that the domain on which the pressure spaces are defined,
\tilde{\mathit{\Omega}}^{h}_{0}
, may differ from the one on which velocity (and domain displacement) live,
\mathit{\Omega}^{h}_{0}
. This allows a split pressure domain across valve planes (with joint interfaces, using a matching discretization) in order to facilitate modeling of a pressure jump.

9.2.1
Eulerian Fluid Coupled to 0D Models

The general Eulerian fluid problem with
n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}
coupling constraints to (one or multiple) 0D lumped-parameter network model(s), cf. Sec.
9.1.1
, at the current unknown time step indexed by
n+1
, can be stated as follows:

Find fluid velocity and pressure variables,
\left(\bm{v}^{n+1},\,p^{n+1}\right)\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{D,h}\times\mathcal{W}^{h}
, as well as multiplier variables
\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n+1}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}
such that conservation of linear momentum,

\displaystyle R_{\delta v}\left(\bm{v}^{n+1},p^{n+1},\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n+1}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}};\delta\bm{v}\right):=\int\limits_{\mathit{\Omega}^{h}}\rho

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\bm{v}^{n+1}-\bm{v}^{n}}{\Delta t}+(\nabla\bm{v}^{n+\theta})\,\bm{v}^{n+\theta}\right)\cdot\delta\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}V

(61)

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{\mathit{\Omega}^{h}}\bm{\sigma}\left(\bm{v}^{n+\theta},p^{n+\theta}\right):\nabla\delta\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}V

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\mathit{\Lambda}_{i}^{n+\theta}\int\limits_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{i}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax 0d},h}}\bm{n}\cdot\delta\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}A

\displaystyle+R_{\delta v}^{N}\left(t^{n+\theta};\delta\bm{v}\right)+R_{\delta v}^{R}\left(\bm{v}^{n+\theta};\delta\bm{v}\right)

\displaystyle+S_{\delta v}^{\mathrm{D}}\left(\bm{v}^{n+\theta},p^{n+\theta};\delta\bm{v})+S_{\delta v}^{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{v}^{n+\theta};\delta\bm{v}\right)=0,

conservation of mass,

\displaystyle R_{\delta p}(\bm{v}^{n+1},p^{n+1};\delta p):=\int\limits_{\tilde{\mathit{\Omega}}^{h}}(\nabla\cdot\bm{v}^{n+1})\,\delta p\,\mathrm{d}V+S_{\delta p}^{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{v}^{n+1},p^{n+1};\delta p)=0,

(62)

as well as constraints enforcing consistency between 0D models and the 3D domain,

\displaystyle R_{\delta\mathit{\Lambda}}\left(\bm{v}^{n+1},\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n+1}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}};\{\delta\mathit{\Lambda}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\right):=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\left(\int\limits_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{i}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax 0d},h}}\bm{v}^{n+1}\cdot\bm{n}\,\mathrm{d}A-\alpha_{i}\,\bm{\mathsf{y}}\left(\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n+1}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\right)\cdot\bm{\mathsf{e}}_{i}\right)\delta\mathit{\Lambda}_{i}=0,

(63)

hold true, for all fluid velocity and pressure test functions
\left(\delta\bm{v},\,\delta p\right)\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{0,h}\times\mathcal{W}^{h}
as well as multiplier test functions
\{\delta\mathit{\Lambda}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}
.

In order to fulfill Eq. (
63
), 0D variables
\bm{\mathsf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{e}}}
, governed by a system of
n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{e}}
algebraic and first-order differential equations, have to be found such that

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{0d}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{y}}^{n+1},\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n+1}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}};\delta\bm{\mathsf{y}}\right):=

(64)

\displaystyle\Biggl(\frac{\bm{\mathsf{g}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{y}}^{n+1},\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n+1}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\right)-\bm{\mathsf{g}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{y}}^{n},\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\right)}{\Delta t}\;+

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathrm{0d}}\,\bm{\mathsf{f}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{y}}^{n+1},\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n+1}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\right)+(1-\theta_{\mathrm{0d}})\,\bm{\mathsf{f}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{y}}^{n},\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\right)\Biggr)\cdot\delta\bm{\mathsf{y}}=0,

for all 0D test functions
\delta\bm{\mathsf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{e}}}
, where
\bm{\mathsf{g}}
is a linear (“left-hand side”) and
\bm{\mathsf{f}}
a possibly nonlinear (“right-hand side”) function in the variable vector
\bm{\mathsf{y}}
and/or multipliers
\{\mathit{\Lambda}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}
, with
\theta_{\mathrm{0d}}\in(0,1]
.

The constitutive law for the Cauchy stress tensor is given by Eq. (
39
). Generic Neumann and Robin terms may be introduced in Eq. (
61
), i.e.

\displaystyle R_{\delta v}^{N}(t;\delta\bm{v})=-\int\limits_{\mathit{\Gamma}^{N,h}}\bm{t}_{N}(t)\cdot\delta\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}A\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\delta v}^{R}(\bm{v};\delta\bm{v})=\int\limits_{\mathit{\Gamma}^{R,h}}c\,\bm{v}\cdot\delta\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}A,

(65)

where
\bm{t}_{N}
represents a traction vector and
c
a viscosity parameter.

Here, we use a variant of the G2 stabilization method
[
52
,
53
,
54
]
suitable for equal-order
\mathbb{P}_{1}\mathchar 45\relax\mathbb{P}_{1}
finite element approximations for the velocity and pressure space. The respective operators which are introduced in Eq. (
61
) and in Eq. (
62
) read

\displaystyle S_{\delta v}^{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{v},p;\delta\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathit{\Omega}^{h}}d_{1}(\nabla\bm{v})\,\bm{v}\cdot\mathrm{sym}(\nabla\delta\bm{v})\,\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}V+\int\limits_{\mathit{\Omega}^{h}}d_{2}\,(\nabla\cdot\bm{v})(\nabla\cdot\delta\bm{v})\,\mathrm{d}V

(66)

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{\mathit{\Omega}^{h}}d_{3}\,(\nabla p)\cdot\mathrm{sym}(\nabla\delta\bm{v})\,\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}V,

and

\displaystyle S_{\delta p}^{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{v},p;\delta p)=\frac{1}{\rho}\int\limits_{\tilde{\mathit{\Omega}}^{h}}d_{1}(\nabla\bm{v})\,\bm{v}\cdot(\nabla\delta p)\,\mathrm{d}V+\frac{1}{\rho}\int\limits_{\tilde{\mathit{\Omega}}^{h}}d_{3}\,(\nabla p)\cdot(\nabla\delta p)\,\mathrm{d}V,

(67)

with

\displaystyle d_{1}=\frac{h_{\mathrm{e}}}{v_{\mathrm{max}}},\quad d_{2}=h_{\mathrm{e}}v_{\mathrm{max}},\quad d_{3}=\frac{h_{\mathrm{e}}}{v_{\mathrm{max}}},

(68)

where
h_{\mathrm{e}}
is the cell diameter and
v_{\mathrm{max}}
a reference velocity. In order to prevent backflow-induced divergence, all Neumann boundaries will be subject to an outflow stabilization
[
55
]
:

\displaystyle S_{\delta v}^{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{v};\delta\bm{v})=-\int\limits_{\mathit{\Gamma}^{N,h}}\beta\min(\bm{v}\cdot\bm{n},0)\,\bm{v}\cdot\delta\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}A.

(69)

9.2.2
ALE Fluid Coupled to 0D and RD Models (Fluid-reduced-Solid Interaction, FrSI)

A general ALE fluid problem with deformable boundary treated with the FrSI method
[
16
]
and
n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}
coupling constraints to (one or multiple) 0D lumped-parameter network model(s), cf. Sec.
9.1.2
, at the current unknown time step indexed by
n+1
, can be stated as follows:

Find fluid velocity and pressure variables,
\left(\bm{v}^{n+1},\,p^{n+1}\right)\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{D,h}\times\mathcal{W}^{h}
, multiplier variables
\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n+1}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}
, as well as ALE domain motion variables
\bm{d}^{n+1}\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}^{D,h}
such that conservation of linear momentum,

\displaystyle R_{\delta v}\left(\bm{v}^{n+1},p^{n+1},\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n+1}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}},\bm{d}^{n+1};\delta\bm{v}\right):=

(70)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathit{\Omega}_{0}^{h}}\widehat{J}(\bm{d}^{n+\theta})\rho\left(\frac{\bm{v}^{n+1}-\bm{v}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\left(\nabla_{0}\bm{v}^{n+\theta}\,\widehat{\bm{F}}(\bm{d}^{n+\theta})^{-1}\right)\,\left(\bm{v}^{n+\theta}-\widehat{\bm{w}}^{n+\theta}\right)\right)\cdot\delta\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}V_{0}

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{\mathit{\Omega}_{0}^{h}}\widehat{J}(\bm{d}^{n+\theta})\,\bm{\sigma}(\bm{v}^{n+\theta},p^{n+\theta},\bm{d}^{n+\theta}):\nabla_{0}\delta\bm{v}\,\widehat{\bm{F}}(\bm{d}^{n+\theta})^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}V_{0}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\mathit{\Lambda}_{i}^{n+\theta}\int\limits_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{0,i}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax 0d},h}}\widehat{J}(\bm{d}^{n+\theta})\widehat{\bm{F}}(\bm{d}^{n+\theta})^{-\mathrm{T}}\bm{n}_{0}\cdot\delta\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}A_{0}

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{0}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax\tilde{\text{s}}},h}}h_{0}\left(rho_{0,\text{s}}\,\frac{\bm{v}^{n+1}-\bm{v}^{n}}{\Delta t}\cdot\delta\bm{v}+\tilde{\bm{P}}\left(\bm{u}_{\text{f}}^{n+\theta},\bm{v}^{n+\theta}\right):\tilde{\nabla}_{0}\delta\bm{v}\right)\,\mathrm{d}A_{0}

\displaystyle+R_{\delta v}^{N}(t^{n+\theta};\delta\bm{v})+R_{\delta v}^{R}(\bm{v}^{n+\theta},\widehat{\bm{w}}^{n+\theta};\delta\bm{v})

\displaystyle+S_{\delta v}^{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{v}^{n+\theta},p^{n+\theta},\bm{d}^{n+\theta};\delta\bm{v})+S_{\delta v}^{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{v}^{n+\theta},\bm{d}^{n+\theta};\delta\bm{v})=0,

conservation of mass,

\displaystyle R_{\delta p}(\bm{v}^{n+1},p^{n+1},\bm{d}^{n+1};\delta p):=

(71)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\tilde{\mathit{\Omega}}_{0}^{h}}\widehat{J}(\bm{d}^{n+1})\,\nabla_{0}\bm{v}^{n+1}:\widehat{\bm{F}}(\bm{d}^{n+1})^{-\mathrm{T}}\delta p\,\mathrm{d}V_{0}+S_{\delta p}^{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{v}^{n+1},p^{n+1},\bm{d}^{n+1};\delta p)=0,

constraints enforcing consistency between 0D models and the 3D realm,

\displaystyle R_{\delta\mathit{\Lambda}}\left(\bm{v}^{n+1},\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n+1}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}},\bm{d}^{n+1};\{\delta\mathit{\Lambda}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\right):=

(72)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\left(\int\limits_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{0,i}^{\mathrm{\text{f}\mathchar 45\relax 0d},h}}(\bm{v}^{n+1}-\widehat{\bm{w}}^{n+1})\cdot\widehat{J}(\bm{d}^{n+1})\widehat{\bm{F}}(\bm{d}^{n+1})^{-\mathrm{T}}\bm{n}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}A_{0}-\alpha_{i}\,\bm{\mathsf{y}}\left(\{\mathit{\Lambda}^{n+1}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\right)\cdot\bm{\mathsf{e}}_{i}\right)\delta\mathit{\Lambda}_{i}=0,

as well as ALE domain motion

\displaystyle R_{\delta d}(\bm{d}^{n+1},\bm{v}^{n+1};\delta\bm{d})

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{\mathit{\Omega}_{0}^{h}}\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{g}}(\bm{d}^{n+1}):\nabla_{0}\delta\bm{d}\,\mathrm{d}V_{0}=0,

(73)

hold true, for all fluid velocity and pressure test functions
\left(\delta\bm{v},\,\delta p\right)\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{0,h}\times\mathcal{W}^{h}
, ALE domain motion test functions
\delta\bm{d}\in\bm{\mathcal{D}}^{0,h}
, as well as multiplier test functions
\{\delta\mathit{\Lambda}\}_{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{0d}}^{\mathrm{b}}}
. The ALE problem is further subject to the essential boundary condition Eq. (
41

9.5
Valves Immersed in 3D Fluid Space

Immersed resistive valve models for computational fluid dynamics of the heart are numerous
[
7
,
70
,
71
,
72
,
73
]
, where most approaches have in common that natural or essential boundary conditions are imposed at an interface between the left ventricle and left atrium (mitral valve) or left ventricle and aortic outflow tract (aortic valve). There, a discontinuous representation of the fluid pressure field has to be accounted for, which either can be inherent to the numerical approximation method (discontinuous Galerkin methods
[
74
]
) or has to be considered by means of split domains across these interfaces (if continuous Lagrange finite element approximations are used). These boundary conditions may be time-controlled or driven by a pressure jump across that interface.

We denote the pressure jump across the mitral (“
\mathrm{mv}
”) or aortic valve (“
\mathrm{av}
”) plane as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta\bar{p}_{i}=\bar{p}_{i}-\bar{p}_{i,\mathrm{open}},\quad i\in\{\mathrm{mv},\mathrm{av}\},

(156)

with the surface-averaged pressures

\displaystyle\qquad\bar{p}_{i}=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{i}(\tilde{\mathit{\Omega}}_{u})}\mathrm{d}A}\;\int\limits_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{i}(\tilde{\mathit{\Omega}}_{u})}p\,\mathrm{d}A,\qquad\bar{p}_{i,\mathrm{open}}=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{i}(\tilde{\mathit{\Omega}}_{d})}\mathrm{d}A}\;\int\limits_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{i}(\tilde{\mathit{\Omega}}_{d})}p\,\mathrm{d}A,

(157)

where
u
and
d
refer to the up- and downstream compartments (mitral valve:
u=\mathrm{LA}
,
d=\mathrm{LV}
, aortic valve:
u=\mathrm{LV}
,
d=\mathrm{AO}
), respectively. Here, the downstream pressure is denoted by subscript “
\mathrm{open}
”, indicating the pressure threshold to be surpassed in order to open the valve.

The valves then are controlled by a Robin condition, Eq. (
65
)
{}_{\text{2}}
for Eulerian or Eq. (
77
) for ALE fluid dynamics, where the resistive coefficient depends on the pressure difference across the valve, Eq. (
156
), as follows:

\displaystyle c_{i}(\Delta\bar{p}_{i}):=\begin{cases}c_{i,\max},&\mathrm{if}\;\Delta\bar{p}_{i}>0,\\
c_{i,\min},&\mathrm{if}\;\Delta\bar{p}_{i}\leq 0.\end{cases}

(158)

For all valves and models reported, we use
c_{i,\max}=10^{3}\;\frac{\mathrm{kPa}\cdot\mathrm{s}^{2}}{\mathrm{mm}}
and
c_{i,\min}=10^{-3}\;\frac{\mathrm{kPa}\cdot\mathrm{s}^{2}}{\mathrm{mm}}
. In this study, for the sake of algorithmic efficiency, we discretize the immersed valve law in an explicit manner. Hence, the value of the previous, converged time step of Eq. (
156

9.6.1
Imaging Snapshots and POD

Modeling blood flow in the left heart using the FrSI method
[
16
]
described in Sec.
2.2
requires information about the motion of the endocardial heart wall in order to construct a suitable space for projection. Here, we use time-resolved data retrieved from CT imaging that provides a time series of 19 wall motion states over one cardiac cycle. These states subsequently are compressed and decomposed by means of Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD)
[
75
]
, yielding a set of
r_{v}
POD modes that are used for projecting the high-dimensional boundary model to a lower-dimensional space. Here, we consider the first
r_{v}=10
POD modes, shown in Fig.
16
sorted in descending order with respect to their relative energy content.

9.6.2
Region-wise Locality of POD Subspace

The global nature of the modes constructed as described in Sec.
9.6.1
may impose additional constraints on the model’s kinematics and may limit adaptive wall motion in response to a change in hemodynamic loads. Therefore, we propose a method to decompose the POD subspace such that the left atrium, the left ventricle, and the aortic outflow tract can experience independent dynamics. By means of a partition of unity approach, we provide regionally local POD spaces for each of the three wall regions, and additionally at their junctions (mitral and aortic valve rims) as well as at the rims of pulmonary veins and aortic outflows. These partitions are shown in Fig.
17
. Hence, here, we have
n_{\mathrm{loc}}=7
local POD subspaces, yielding a total of
n_{\mathrm{loc}}\cdot r_{v}=70
degrees of freedom for the reduced wall model.

Figure 17:
Partition of unity decomposition of POD subspace for LA-LV-AO model.

Furthermore, we use the partition of unity fields to smoothly translate between the different wall thicknesses of atrium, ventricle, and aortic arch, assuming that the wall is thicker around the rims of mitral and aortic valve:

\displaystyle h_{0}^{\mathrm{LA}}=h_{0,\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{LA}}(1-\psi_{\mathrm{mv}})+h_{0,\mathrm{v}}\psi_{\mathrm{mv}},

(159)

\displaystyle h_{0}^{\mathrm{LV}}=h_{0,\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{LV}}(1-\psi_{\mathrm{mv}})+h_{0,\mathrm{v}}\psi_{\mathrm{mv}}+h_{0,\mathrm{v}}\psi_{\mathrm{av}}-h_{0,\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{LV}}\psi_{\mathrm{av}},\quad\text{and}

(160)

\displaystyle h_{0}^{\mathrm{AO}}=h_{0,\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{AO}}(1-\psi_{\mathrm{av}})+h_{0,\mathrm{v}}\psi_{\mathrm{av}},

(161)

with the wall thickness at the valve rims
h_{0,\mathrm{v}}=20\,\mathrm{mm}
and the base thicknesses
h_{0,\mathrm{b}}^{i}
.

9.6.3
Boundary Conditions in Reduced Space

Suitable boundary conditions that mimic the mechanical tissue properties of adjacent portions of the vascular beds truncated from the model region of interest have to be accounted for. Here, these would be at the upstream locations of the pulmonary veins or at locations downstream, i.e. the aortic outflow tract or the coronary arteries. In order to localize the effect of the adjacent tissue, Robin conditions are imposed in the POD space to the modes associated to the pulmonary veins and aortic root rims. Hence, the following term is added to the first row of block vector Eq. (
7
):

\displaystyle\ldots+\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\mathrm{R}}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{v}}}+\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{R}}(\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}_{\text{f}}+\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}_{\mathrm{pre}}),

(162)

where
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\mathrm{R}},\bm{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(r_{v}+n_{v}^{\mathit{\Omega}})\times(r_{v}+n_{v}^{\mathit{\Omega}})}
are diagonal damping and stiffness matrices, with entries
C_{\mathrm{R},ii},K_{\mathrm{R},ii}\neq 0
at locations that link to the subset of modes belonging to the pulmonary vein and aortic outflow boundary partitions defined by
\psi_{\mathrm{b1}}
and
\psi_{\mathrm{b2}}
, respectively, cf. Fig.
17
. Vectors
\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}_{\text{f}}
and
\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{u}}}_{\mathrm{pre}}
represent the projected discrete fluid and prestress displacements, respectively, cf. the space-continuous time-discrete counterpart Eq. (

0.05\;\frac{\mathrm{mN}}{\mathrm{mm}}
