On the (dis)similarities between stationary imprecise and non-stationary precise uncertainty models in algorithmic randomness
Floris Persiau, Jasper De Bock, Gert de Cooman

Abstract




Abstract

The field of algorithmic randomness studies, amongst other things, what it means for infinite binary sequences to be random for some given uncertainty model.
Classically, martingale-theoretic notions of randomness involve precise uncertainty models, and it is only recently that imprecision has been introduced into this context.
As a consequence, the investigation into how imprecision alters our view on martingale-theoretic random sequences has only just begun.
In this contribution, where we allow for non-computable uncertainty models, we establish a close and surprising connection between precise and imprecise uncertainty models in this randomness context.
In particular, we show that there are stationary imprecise models and non-computable non-stationary precise models that have the exact same set of random sequences.
We also give a preliminary discussion of the possible implications of our result for a statistics based on imprecise probabilities, and shed some light on the practical (ir)relevance of both imprecise and non-computable precise uncertainty models in that context.

Keywords:
algorithmic randomness, imprecise probabilities, computability theory, probability intervals, supermartingales, non-stationarity

Introduction

What does it mean for an infinite binary sequence, such as
011001100\cdots
, to be random?
This is a highly non-trivial question that has led to numerous investigations.
First of all, it is important to realise that randomness is typically defined with respect to some uncertainty model.
So, our opening question only makes sense once such a model has been specified.
Uncertainty models can be stationary or non-stationary, computable or non-computable, as well as precise or imprecise
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
It is between the non-computable non-stationary precise and the stationary imprecise uncertainty models that we will reveal a remarkably close connection: we will show that for every stationary imprecise model there are non-computable non-stationary precise models that have the exact same set of random sequences.

The earliest notions of randomness only considered precise probability models that assign a probability
p\in[0,1]
to the outcome
1
.
For instance, in 1919 Von Mises suggested considering an infinite binary sequence to be random for
p
if the relative frequencies of ones along the sequence, and along all infinite subsequences selected by selection rules, converge to
p

[
9
]
.
In 1939, Wald proved that for any
p\in(0,1)
, such random sequences do exist if we restrict our attention to a countable set of selection rules
[
10
,
11
]
.
This result, however, left open which countable set to consider.
In 1940, based on Wald’s work, Church suggested adopting the countable set of computable selection rules
[
10
,
12
]
; an infinite binary sequence is then Church random for
p
if the relative frequency of ones along every computably selectable infinite subsequence converges to
p
, where ‘computably selectable’ essentially means that there is some finite algorithm that decides which elements to keep and which to discard.

However, there are infinite sequences that satisfy this requirement, but for which the running frequency of ones along the sequence converges to
p
from below.
Such sequences disobey the law of the iterated logarithm.
For this reason, Jean Ville criticised this type of randomness definition, and argued that besides the law of large numbers, a random sequence also ought to satisfy other statistical laws
[
10
]
.
Arguments of this kind led to the development of many other randomness notions.
Some of the most well-known and well-studied amongst these are Martin-Löf randomness, computable randomness and Schnorr randomness
[
13
,
14
]
.
The reason for this is twofold: they have an intuitive interpretation and they can be defined in several equivalent ways
[
15
,
8
]
.
From a measure-theoretic point of view, for example, an infinite binary sequence is random for a computable real
p\in[0,1]
if it passes all implementable statistical tests that are associated with
p
.
On the other hand, if we adopt the martingale-theoretic approach, then a sequence is random for a computable
p
if there is no implementable betting strategy for getting arbitrarily rich along this sequence without borrowing, where the bets that are allowed are determined by
p
, where the betting strategy specifies the possible accumulated capital in the betting game, and where the meaning of ‘implementable’ depends on the notion of randomness at hand; for instance, in the case of computable randomness, ‘implementable’ means that there is a finite algorithm that yields the strategy.

There is more to randomness, however, than the simple case of a single computable probability
p
.
As mentioned above, more general uncertainty models, such as non-stationary precise ones or imprecise ones, can also be used to define notions of randomness
[
4
,
7
,
5
,
6
]
; these models need not necessarily be computable either.

In a measure-theoretic context, most of the classical approaches impose computability
[
13
,
14
,
8
]
, but non-computable uncertainty models have been studied too. One approach, for example, is to consider the implementable statistical tests that are associated with a non-computable measure, but that do not access it as a resource; this notion of randomness is known as
Hippocratic
or
Blind randomness

[
16
,
17
,
7
]
.
Another measure-theoretic notion of randomness that allows for non-computable uncertainty models was put forward by Levin in 1973 and is nowadays known as
uniform randomness

[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.
This notion of uniform randomness also allows for imprecision by considering so-called ‘effectively compact classes of probability measures’; in particular, there are tests such that a sequence passes this test if and only if it is uniformly random with respect to some probability measure in the considered class.

In the context of this paper, however, we focus on the martingale-theoretic approach to randomness, for which imprecise-probabilistic uncertainty models have been considered only recently.
To be more precise, in the past few years, De Cooman and De Bock put forward a martingale-theoretic approach that associates (weak) Martin-Löf, computable and Schnorr randomness with imprecise rather than precise probability models
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
These imprecise models take the form of so-called forecasting systems, and need not be computable.

This recent work still leaves room for many open questions on how allowing for imprecision (and letting go of computable uncertainty models) changes our understanding of random sequences.
In the present paper, we try and contribute to this understanding by proving a remarkable relation between randomness for precise and imprecise probability models.
In particular, for every non-vanishing closed interval
I\subseteq[0,1]
and each of the above-mentioned four martingale-theoretic notions of randomness, we will show that there is a non-stationary precise but then necessarily non-computable uncertainty model for which the set of random paths is the same as for
I
.
So, for the results in this paper, allowing for non-computable uncertainty models is of crucial importance.
We leave aside whether using non-computable uncertainty models in algorithmic randomness is defensible on philosophical or theoretical grounds; we simply let go of the classical computability restriction on uncertainty models, and investigate what happens if we do so.
Nevertheless, in our conclusions, we do argue why computable uncertainty models are to be favoured on practical grounds.

Our contribution is structured as follows.

Sections
2
–
4
provide a short overview of relevant earlier work.

We start by introducing interval forecasts and the associated coherent upper expectations in Section
2
, where we also explain how their interpretation leads to a convex cone of gambles that a subject is willing to offer.
Section
3
explains how to bet on a single variable in a way that agrees with an interval forecast, and extends this idea to a betting game/protocol on an infinite sequence of variables by defining betting strategies—which are basically supermartingales—that again agree with such interval forecasts, and that avoid borrowing.
After clarifying in Section
4
when such betting strategies are implementable, we present in Section
5
the imprecise-probabilistic martingale-theoretic notions of (weak) Martin-Löf, computable and Schnorr randomness introduced in earlier work
[
2

18
]

At this point, in Section
6
, we are ready to formulate our central claim/result: for each of the above-mentioned martingale-theoretic notions of randomness, an infinite sequence is random for an interval forecast if and only if it is random for some specific related non-computable non-stationary precise uncertainty model.

We complement this result with a reflection on what it tells us about allowing for imprecision and non-computability in a martingale-theoretic approach to algorithmic randomness.
Moreover, we explain that our result has implications for the interpretation of imprecise randomness, and that it finds a reflection in results for the measure-theoretic approach.

In the subsequent three sections, we work towards the proof of our main claim.
In Section

In Section
8
we introduce, inspired by Wald’s work, a very general imprecise-probabilistic frequentist notion of randomness in terms of countable sets of selection processes, and highlight the particular imprecise-probabilistic frequentist notions of (weak) Church randomness that we introduced elsewhere

In Section

We conclude this paper with a discussion in Section

It will be useful to have an analytical condition that, for a subject with interval forecast
I\in\mathscr{I}
, characterises the gambles he is willing to offer to an opponent.
To this end, we introduce upper (and lower) expectation operators.
When
I=p\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e., when
I
reduces to a single number, we consider the
linear expectation

E_{p}
defined by

E_{p}(f)\coloneqq pf(1)+(1-p)f(0)\text{ for all }f\in\mathscr{L}(\{0,1\}).

(2)

This is a most informative—or least conservative—model for a subject’s uncertainty.
When
I=[\underline{p},\overline{p}]\notin\mathbb{R}

\overline{E}_{I}

\overline{E}_{I}(f)\coloneqq\max_{p\in I}E_{p}(f)=\max\{E_{\underline{p}}(f),E_{\overline{p}}(f)\}\text{ for all }f\in\mathscr{L}(\{0,1\}).

(3)

As a closely related operator, we consider the
lower expectation

\underline{E}_{I}\colon\mathscr{L}(\{0,1\})\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\underline{E}_{I}(f)\coloneqq\min_{p\in I}E_{p}(f)=\min\{E_{\underline{p}}(f),E_{\overline{p}}(f)\}\text{ for all~$f\in\mathscr{L}(\{0,1\})$.}

(4)

It is clear that lower and upper expectations are related to each other through the following conjugacy relationship:
\overline{E}_{I}(f)=-\underline{E}_{I}(-f)
for all
f\in\mathscr{L}(\{0,1\})

3
Forecasting systems and betting strategies

We can better describe the correspondence between a subject’s interval forecasts, which specify his beliefs about the unknown outcomes of binary variables, and actual outcome sequences, by taking him up on a specific betting game.

We first introduce the betting game for a single binary variable
X
.
There are three players involved:
Forecaster
(who will take up our subject’s part),
Sceptic
(who is his opponent) and
Reality
.
Forecaster initiates the game by providing an interval forecast
I\subseteq[0,1]
, which describes, as we explained in the previous section, his beliefs about—and betting commitments related to—the uncertain outcome
X\in\{0,1\}
.
Next, Sceptic, being Forecaster’s opponent, is allowed to pick any gamble
f\in\mathscr{L}(\{0,1\})
that Forecaster is willing to offer, in the specific sense that
\overline{E}_{I}(f)\leq 0
.
This leads to an uncertain (possibly negative) gain
f(X)
for Sceptic and
-f(X)
for Forecaster.
Finally, Reality reveals the outcome
x\in\{0,1\}
, which leads to an actual (possibly negative) gain
f(x)
for Sceptic and
-f(x)
for Forecaster.

In order to extend these ideas to an infinite betting game involving a sequence of successively revealed binary variables
X_{1}
, …,
X_{n}
, …, we require a bit more terminology.

In what follows,
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of natural numbers, and
{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
denotes the set of non-negative integers.
An infinite outcome sequence
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},\dots)
is called a
path
and is also denoted by
\omega
.
All such paths are collected in the set
\Omega\coloneqq\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, and for every path
\omega=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},\dots)\in\Omega
, we let
\omega_{1:n}\coloneqq(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
\omega_{n+1}\coloneqq x_{n+1}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
A finite outcome sequence
x_{1:n}\coloneqq(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is called a
situation
and is also denoted by
s
, with length
|s|\coloneqq n
, for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
All situations are collected in the set
\mathbb{S}\coloneqq\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}\{0,1\}^{n}
.
For any
s=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{S}
and
x\in\{0,1\}
, we write
sx
as a shorthand notation for
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x)
.
We call the empty sequence
\square\coloneqq x_{1:0}=()
the
initial situation
.
Note that for every path
\omega\in\Omega
, we have that
\omega_{1:0}=\square
.

It will also be useful to be able to deal with objects that depend on the situations.
Formally, we define a
process

F
as a map on the set
\mathbb{S}
of all situations.
In particular, a
real
process
F\colon\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is a map from situations to real numbers.
A real process
F
is called
non-negative
if
F(s)\geq 0
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
; it is called
positive
if
F(s)>0
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
.
We call a non-negative real process
F
a

if additionally
F(\square)=1
.
A zero-one valued process
S
—with
S(s)\in\{0,1\}
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
—is called a
selection process
.
If a process
F
depends only on the situations
s\in\mathbb{S}
through their length
|s|
, we call it
temporal
, and then also write
F(n)
instead of

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
s\in\mathbb{S}
with

.

Forecaster’s part in the game now consists in providing an interval-valued process.
That is, he provides an interval forecast
I_{s}\in\mathscr{I}
for every finite outcome sequence
s\in\mathbb{S}
, in order to describe his beliefs about the binary variable

The interval-valued process that summarises these interval forecasts, we call a forecasting system.

Definition 1

(Forecasting system)

A
forecasting system
is a map
\varphi\colon\mathbb{S}\to\mathscr{I}

\varphi(s)\in\mathscr{I}

A forecasting system

\varphi(s)\in\mathbb{R}

.
We denote the set
\smash{\mathscr{I}^{\mathbb{S}}}

\Phi

With every forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi

\underline{\varphi}

\overline{\varphi}

\underline{\varphi}(s)\coloneqq\min\varphi(s)

\overline{\varphi}(s)\coloneqq\max\varphi(s)

s\in\mathbb{S}
.
Clearly, a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi

\underline{\varphi}(s)=\overline{\varphi}(s)
for all

.
If a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
uses the very same interval forecast

in all situations, i.e.,
\varphi(s)=I

, then we call it

\varphi
.
If for two forecasting systems
\varphi,\varphi^{*}\in\Phi

\varphi(s)\subseteq\varphi^{*}(s)
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
, then we say that
\varphi^{*}
is less informative—or more conservative—than
\varphi
, and denote this by
\varphi\subseteq\varphi^{*}
.

In this case, if
\varphi
is precise, then we also say that
\varphi
is
compatible
with
\varphi^{*}

It is also worth noting that, through the Kolmogorov extension theorem, every precise forecasting system uniquely determines a corresponding probability measure on the algebra (of sets of paths) generated by the situations. In measure-theoretic notions of randomness, one will typically focus on this measure instead of its forecasting system. In our game-theoretic approach, however, the forecasting system takes the center stage.

Once Forecaster has specified a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
, Sceptic is allowed to adopt any betting strategy that, for every situation
s\in\mathbb{S}
, selects one of the gambles that Forecaster is bound to offer by his specification of the interval forecast

, i.e., some gamble

\overline{E}_{\varphi(s)}(f_{s})\leq 0
.
Afterwards, Reality reveals the successive outcomes
X_{n}=x_{n}
at each successive
time instant

, leading to the sequence
\omega=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},\dots)
.
At every time instant

, after Reality has revealed the outcome
x_{n}
, Sceptic uses the gamble
f_{x_{1:n}}
that corresponds to her betting strategy.
Next, Reality reveals the subsequent outcome
X_{n+1}=x_{n+1}\in\{0,1\}
and the reward
f_{x_{1:n}}(x_{n+1})
goes to Sceptic.
Moreover, we will prohibit Sceptic from borrowing.

In order to formalise these betting strategies for Sceptic, we define a
gamble process
as a map from situations to gambles.
In particular, we associate with every real process
F
a
process difference

\Delta F\colon\mathbb{S}\to\mathscr{L}(\{0,1\})
, which is the gamble process that maps any
s\in\mathbb{S}
to the gamble
\Delta F(s)\coloneqq F(s\,\bullet)-F(s)
, where we use
F(s\,\bullet)
to denote the gamble on
\{0,1\}
whose value, for any
x\in\{0,1\}
, is given by
F(sx)
.
Note that
F(x_{1:n})=F(\square)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Delta F(x_{1:k})(x_{k+1})
for all
x_{1:n}\in\mathbb{S}
, with
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

Given a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
, we call a real process
M
a
supermartingale
for
\varphi
if
\overline{E}_{\varphi(s)}(\Delta M(s))\leq 0
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
.
A real process
M
is called a
submartingale
for
\varphi
if
-M
is a supermartingale for
\varphi
, meaning that
\underline{E}_{\varphi(s)}(\Delta M(s))\geq 0
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
.
All supermartingales and submartingales for
\varphi
are respectively collected in the sets
\overline{\mathbb{M}}(\varphi)
and
\underline{\mathbb{M}}(\varphi)
.

Supermartingales correspond to Sceptic’s allowed betting strategies.
Indeed, assume that Forecaster adopts the forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
, consider a time instant
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, and consider the situation where Reality has revealed a finite outcome sequence
x_{1:n}\in\mathbb{S}
.
A supermartingale
M
for
\varphi
then specifies a gamble
\Delta M(x_{1:n})\in\mathscr{L}(\{0,1\})
that Sceptic is allowed to pick.
If she does, and Reality reveals the outcome
x_{n+1}\in\{0,1\}
, the (possibly negative) amount
\Delta M(x_{1:n})(x_{n+1})
goes to Sceptic and her total capital becomes

\displaystyle M(x_{1:n+1})=M(x_{1:n})+\Delta M(x_{1:n})(x_{n+1})=M(\square)+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Delta M(x_{1:k})(x_{k+1}),

with
M(\square)
her initial capital.
By focusing on non-negative supermartingales, we additionally prevent Sceptic from borrowing.

As an important special case, we consider
test supermartingales

T\colon\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
for
\varphi
.
These are non-negative supermartingales for
\varphi
for which
T(\square)\coloneqq 1
.
We collect all test supermartingales for
\varphi
in the set
\overline{\mathbb{T}}(\varphi)
.
In one of our notions of randomness, we will adopt a particular way of defining such test supermartingales by focusing on multiplicative rather than additive betting strategies.
For this reason, we introduce the notion of a
multiplier process
, which is a non-negative gamble process.
With every such multiplier process
D
, we associate a test process
D^{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\circledcirc$}}
, defined by the ‘initial condition’
D^{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\circledcirc$}}(\square)\coloneqq 1
and, for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
and
x\in\{0,1\}
, by the recursion equation
D^{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\circledcirc$}}(sx)\coloneqq D^{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\circledcirc$}}(s)D(s)(x)
, and we say that
D^{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\circledcirc$}}
is
generated by

D
.
In particular, we say that a test supermartingale
T\in\overline{\mathbb{T}}(\varphi)
is
generated by a multiplier process
if there is some non-negative gamble process
D_{T}
such that
T(x_{1:n})=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}D_{T}(x_{1:k})(x_{k+1})
for all
x_{1:n}\in\mathbb{S}
, with
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

4
Computable forecasting systems and implementable betting strategies

Sceptic will not be allowed to adopt just any non-negative supermartingale as a betting strategy.
We will also require that it should be implementable
[
10
,
21
]
.
Loosely speaking, this means that for each of her betting strategies, there is some finite description that specifies how to approximate it (to arbitrary precision).
In order to be able to formalise when a supermartingale is implementable, we turn to computability theory.

As a basic building block, this theory considers
recursive
natural functions
\phi\colon{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, which are maps that can be computed by a Turing machine
[
22
]
.
By the Church–Turing thesis, this is equivalent to the existence of a finite algorithm that, given the input
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, outputs
\phi(n)\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
For example, since a path
\omega\in\Omega
is a function from
\mathbb{N}
to
\{0,1\}
, we call it recursive if there is some finite algorithm that, given the input
n\in\mathbb{N}
, outputs
\omega_{n}\in\{0,1\}
.

Instead of
{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, we will also consider functions whose domain equals
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{S}
,
\mathbb{S}\times\{0,1\}
,
\mathbb{S}\times{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
or any other countably infinite set
\mathscr{D}
whose elements can be encoded by the natural numbers.
A function
\phi\colon\mathscr{D}\to{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is then called
recursive
if there is a Turing machine that, when given the natural-valued encoding of an element
d\in\mathscr{D},
3
3

3

The choice of encoding is not important provided different encodings can be translated into each other. The latter means that we can algorithmically decide whether a natural number is an encoding of an object and, if this is the case, that we can find an encoding of the same object with respect to the other encoding
[
23
]
.
outputs
\phi(d)
.
Also in this more general case, by the Church–Turing thesis, this is equivalent to the existence of a finite algorithm that, given the input
d\in\mathcal{D}
, outputs
\phi(d)\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. In fact, throughout this paper and its proofs, it is this alternative, more intuitive, characterisation that we will be using.

For example, we will call a selection process
S
recursive if there is a finite algorithm that, given the input
s\in\mathbb{S}
, outputs the binary digit
S(s)\in\{0,1\}
.

More generally, for any countable domain
\mathscr{D}

that can be encoded by the natural numbers
, a
rational
map
q\colon\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{Q}
is called
recursive
if there are three recursive natural maps
a,b,c\colon\mathscr{D}\to{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
such that

b(d)\neq 0\text{ and }q(d)=\smash{(-1)^{c(d)}\frac{a(d)}{b(d)}}\text{ for all }d\in\mathscr{D}.

Since a finite number of finite algorithms can always be combined into one finite algorithm
[
24
]
, this is equivalent to the existence of a finite algorithm that outputs
q(d)
when given
d\in\mathscr{D}
as input.
In particular, a rational process
F\colon\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{Q}
is called recursive if there is a recursive rational map
q\colon\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{Q}
such that
F(s)=q(s)
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
.

Computability theory not only considers recursive objects, but also uses them to introduce
lower semicomputable
ones.
The simplest case is that of a lower semicomputable real number: a real number
x\in\mathbb{R}
is called lower semicomputable if there is a recursive rational map
q\colon{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to\mathbb{Q}
such that
q(n)\leq q(n+1)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, and
x=\lim_{m\to\infty}q(m)
.
More generally, for any countable domain
\mathscr{D}

that can be encoded by the natural numbers
, a real map
r\colon\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{R}
is called lower semicomputable if there is a recursive rational map
q\colon\mathscr{D}\times{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to\mathbb{Q}
such that
q(d,n)\leq q(d,n+1)
and
r(d)=\lim_{m\to\infty}q(d,m)
for all
d\in\mathscr{D}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
A real map
r
is thus lower semicomputable if there is some finite algorithm that, for every element
d\in\mathscr{D}
of the domain, can provide an increasing sequence
(q(d,n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}
of rational numbers that approaches the real number
r(d)
from below—but without necessarily knowing how good the lower bounds
q(d,n)
are.
In particular, a real process
F
is called lower semicomputable if there is a recursive rational map
q\colon\mathbb{S}\times{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to\mathbb{Q}
such that
q(s,n)\leq q(s,n+1)
and
F(s)=\lim_{m\to\infty}q(s,m)
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
Similarly, a real multiplier process
D
is called lower semicomputable if there is a recursive rational map
q\colon\mathbb{S}\times\{0,1\}\times{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to\mathbb{Q}
such that
q(s,x,n)\leq q(s,x,n+1)
and
D(s)(x)=\lim_{m\to\infty}q(s,x,m)
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
,
x\in\{0,1\}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
Two types of lower semicomputable (gamble) processes that we will make frequent use of, are lower semicomputable test supermartingales and lower semicomputable multiplier processes that generate test supermartingales.

We call a real map
r\colon\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{R}

upper semicomputable
if
-r
is lower semicomputable.
If a real map
r
is both lower and upper semicomputable, then we call it
computable
.
Equivalently
[
1
]
, a real map
r\colon\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{R}
is computable if and only if there is a recursive rational map
q\colon\mathscr{D}\times{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to\mathbb{Q}
such that

|r(d)-q(d,n)|<2^{-n}\text{ for all }d\in\mathscr{D}\text{ and }n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}.

This means that there is a finite algorithm that, for every
d\in\mathscr{D}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, outputs a rational number
q(d,n)
that approximates the real number
r(d)
with a precision of at least
2^{-n}
.
In particular, a real number
x\in\mathbb{R}
is called computable if there is a recursive rational map
q\colon{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to\mathbb{Q}
such that
|x-q(n)|<2^{-n}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
An interval forecast
I\in\mathscr{I}
is called computable if the real numbers
\min I
and
\max I
are both computable.
Moreover, a real process
F\colon\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is called computable if there is a recursive rational map
q\colon\mathbb{S}\times{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to\mathbb{Q}
such that
|F(s)-q(s,n)|<2^{-n}
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
Finally, a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
is called computable if the real processes
\underline{\varphi}
and
\overline{\varphi}
are both computable.

So, a real process
F\colon\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
can be implementable by being recursive, lower semicomputable, upper semicomputable or computable, but also, if it is generated by a multiplier process
D
, by
D
being of one of these four types.
In what follows, it will be useful to have the following sets of implementable real processes at our notational disposal:

\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{ML}}

all lower semicomputable test processes;

\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{wML}}

all test processes generated by

lower semicomputable multiplier processes;

\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{C}}=\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{S}}

all recursive positive rational test processes.

From the discussion above, it is clear that if
F
is recursive, then it is computable and hence lower semicomputable as well; so
\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{S}}=\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{C}}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{ML}}
.
It follows from discussions elsewhere
[
2
, Section 5]
that all four sets are in fact nested:
\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{S}}=\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{C}}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{wML}}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{ML}}
.
As a result, for any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
, it also holds that the set
\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}
is countably infinite, because the lower semicomputable test processes are countable in number; see for example
[
4
, Lemma 13]
.

5
Several martingale-theoretic notions of randomness

Now that we know in what different ways Sceptic’s betting strategies can be implementable, we are ready to introduce four different martingale-theoretic notions of randomness: Martin-Löf (ML) randomness, weak Martin-Löf (wML) randomness, computable (C) randomness and Schnorr (S) randomness; except for wML-randomness, these are all generalisations to interval forecasts of classical notions of randomness.
We recall from the Introduction that, in the martingale-theoretic setting, a path
\omega\in\Omega
is random for a forecasting system
\varphi
if Sceptic has no implementable betting strategy that is allowed by
\varphi
and that makes her arbitrarily rich on
\omega
without borrowing.
This is true for each of the above-mentioned four notions of randomness.
The difference between them lies in the way the allowed betting strategies are implementable, and in the way Sceptic should not be able to become arbitrarily rich.
The allowed betting strategies for the notions of ML-, wML-, C- and S-randomness are gathered in the following sets:

\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{ML}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{ML}}\cap\overline{\mathbb{T}}(\varphi)

all lower semicomputable test supermartingales for
\varphi
;

\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{wML}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{wML}}\cap\overline{\mathbb{T}}(\varphi)

all test supermartingales for
\varphi
generated by

lower semicomputable multiplier processes;

\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{C}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{C}}\cap\overline{\mathbb{T}}(\varphi)

all recursive positive rational test supermartingales for
\varphi
;

\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{S}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{S}}\cap\overline{\mathbb{T}}(\varphi)

all recursive positive rational test supermartingales for
\varphi
.

By recalling that
\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{S}}=\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{C}}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{wML}}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{ML}}
, it readily follows that the above sets of betting strategies satisfy the following relations for any forecasting system
\varphi
:
\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{S}}(\varphi)=\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{C}}(\varphi)\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{wML}}(\varphi)\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{ML}}(\varphi)
.

Now, for any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C}\}
, we will consider a path
\omega\in\Omega
to be R-random for a forecasting system
\varphi
if Sceptic can adopt no betting strategy
T\in\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{R}}(\varphi)
that is
unbounded
on
\omega
, in the sense that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}T(\omega_{1:n})=\infty
.

Definition 2

(
[
2
, Definition 2]
[
18
, Definition 5 and Proposition 6]
)
.

For any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C}\}
, a path
\omega\in\Omega
is R-random for a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
if no test supermartingale
T\in\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{R}}(\varphi)
is unbounded on
\omega
.

We want to emphasise here that, contrary to what is typically done in the literature for algorithmic randomness associated with precise forecasts, we don’t necessarily impose computability requirements on the forecasting systems
\varphi
.

In the case of
C
-randomness, it may initially seem rather unintuitive that we consider recursive rational test supermartingales instead of computable real non-negative supermartingales, since the naming suggests the latter.
However, as we have discussed in
[
25
, Section 5]
, both sets of betting strategies result in the same notion of randomness; we use the former here for mathematical convenience.
In what follows, we will also implicitly use this equivalence when referring to results proven elsewhere.

To introduce S-randomness, we require the additional notion of a
real growth function

\tau\colon{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, which is a computable map from non-negative integers to non-negative reals that is non-decreasing—so
\tau(n+1)\geq\tau(n)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
—and unbounded—so
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau(n)=\infty
.
Now, a path
\omega\in\Omega
is considered to be S-random for a forecasting system
\varphi
if no test supermartingale
T\in\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{S}}(\varphi)
is
computably unbounded
on
\omega
, meaning that there is some real growth function
\tau
such that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}[T(\omega_{1:n})-\tau(n)]\geq 0
.

Intuitively, and analogously to computable randomness, this means that Sceptic should not be able to adopt a recursive (positive rational) betting strategy
T\in\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{C}}(\varphi)=\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{S}}(\varphi)
that allows her to get arbitrarily rich, but now at some computable rate.

Definition 3
.

A path
\omega\in\Omega
is S-random for a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
if no test supermartingale
T\in\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{S}}(\varphi)
is computably unbounded on
\omega
.

Interestingly, and similarly to
C
-randomness, we can replace the set of allowable betting strategies
\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{S}}(\varphi)
by the set of computable test supermartingales for
\varphi
, without changing the set of
S
-random paths for
\varphi
.
That is, a path
\omega\in\Omega
is
S
-random for a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
if and only if there is no computable betting strategy that starts with unit capital, is allowed by
\varphi
and makes Sceptic arbitrarily rich along
\omega
at some computable rate; here too, we will implicitly use this equivalence when referring to results proven elsewhere.

Proposition 3
.

A path
\omega\in\Omega
is
S
-random for a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
if and only if no computable non-negative supermartingale
M\in\overline{\mathbb{M}}(\varphi)
is computably unbounded on
\omega
.

Proof.

The reverse implication holds trivially since every recursive positive rational test supermartingale
T\in\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{S}}(\varphi)
is a computable non-negative supermartingale for
\varphi
as well.

For the direct implication, we assume
ex absurdo
that there is a computable non-negative supermartingale
M\in\overline{\mathbb{M}}(\varphi)
that is computably unbounded on
\omega
, meaning that there is a real growth function
\tau

\limsup_{n\to\infty}[M(\omega_{1:n})-\tau(n)]\geq 0
.
By
[

\nicefrac{{(M(s)-7)}}{{\alpha}}\leq T(s)

If we introduce the real growth function

.
Hence,
T\in\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{S}}(\varphi)
is computably unbounded on
\omega

\omega
is
S
-random for
\varphi
.
∎

\varphi

\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}

(

6
A remarkable equivalence: non-stationary precise forecasting systems vs interval forecasts

For didactic reasons, we will now put a temporal halt to our rather technical but necessary introduction of mathematical concepts.
After all, now that we have these four martingale-theoretic notions of randomness and some of their properties at our disposal, we can start to address our central question in this paper: How does allowing for imprecision change our understanding of random sequences?
Is it for example ‘easier’ to capture the randomness of some paths by imprecise forecasting systems?
Are there paths whose randomness can only be described by imprecise uncertainty models?

To start the discussion, we fix any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
and recall from Proposition
6
that if a path
\omega\in\Omega
is R-random for some precise forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
, then it is also R-random for any forecasting system that is less informative.
Hence, in particular, for any real numbers
p,q\in[0,1]
such that
p<q
, if
\omega
is R-random for the forecasting system
\varphi_{p,q}
, defined by

\displaystyle\varphi_{p,q}(s)\coloneqq\begin{cases}p&\text{if $|s|$ is odd}\\
q&\text{if $|s|$ is even}\end{cases}\text{ for all }s\in\mathbb{S},

then it is also R-random for the interval forecast
[p,q]
.
So we see that
[p,q]
can be used as a simpler—because stationary—yet imprecise alternative for
\varphi_{p,q}
.
In many cases, this procedure of replacing a precise non-stationary forecasting system by a stationary imprecise one will result in a larger set of R-random paths, and therefore lead to a less informative description of the
R
-randomness associated with
\omega
.
However, interval forecasts do not merely serve as an alternative for non-stationary precise forecasts.
Indeed, as was shown by De Cooman and De Bock
[
2
, Section 10]
, there are paths
\omega
that are R-random for
[p,q]
, but not R-random for any (more) precise (possibly non-stationary) computable forecasting system
\varphi
.

Theorem 8

(
[
2
, Theorem 37]
)
.

Consider any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
and any interval forecast
[p,q]\in\mathscr{I}
.
Then there is a path
\omega\in\Omega
that is
R
-random for the interval forecast
[p,q]
, but that is never
R
-random for any computable forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
whose highest imprecision is smaller than that of
[p,q]
, in the specific sense that
\sup_{s\in\mathbb{S}}[\overline{\varphi}(s)-\underline{\varphi}(s)]<q-p
.

Theorem
8
led them to claim that
R
-randomness is inherently imprecise, because the randomness of the paths
\omega
in Theorem
8
can only be captured by an imprecise forecasting system.
The following corollary, which is essentially a less technical formulation of our main result—Theorem
24
further on in Section
9
—shows that the assumption that
\varphi
is computable is crucial for this claim: indeed, Corollary
9
shows that there is a precise forecasting system
\varphi
—so with
\sup_{s\in\mathbb{S}}[\overline{\varphi}(s)-\underline{\varphi}(s)]=0
—such that
\omega
is R-random for
[p,q]
if and only if it is R-random for
\varphi
.
Hence, for this particular
\varphi
, there is no path
\omega\in\Omega
that is R-random for
[p,q]
but not for
\varphi
.
We postpone an exact formulation and proof of Theorem
24
to Section
9
, since that requires even more mathematical technicalities.

Corollary 9
.

Consider any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
and any interval forecast
[p,q]\in\mathscr{I}
with
p<q
.
Then there is a precise forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
, with
\varphi\subseteq[p,q]
, such that any path
\omega\in\Omega
is
R
-random for
\varphi
if and only if it is
R
-random for
[p,q]
.

Proof.

It is an immediate consequence of Theorem
24
that there is some precise forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
, with
\varphi\subseteq[p,q]
, such that a path
\omega\in\Omega
is R-random for
[p,q]
if and only if it is R-random for
\varphi
.
∎

By Theorem
8
, the precise forecasting system
\varphi
in Corollary
9
is then necessarily non-computable, as well as non-stationary.

Corollary 10
.

Consider any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
, any precise forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
and any interval forecast
[p,q]\in\mathscr{I}
with
p<q
.
If
\Omega_{\textnormal{R}}(\varphi)=\Omega_{\textnormal{R}}([p,q])
, then
\varphi
must be non-computable and non-stationary.

Proof.

Since
q-p>0
by assumption, and since
\sup_{s\in\mathbb{S}}[\overline{\varphi}(s)-\underline{\varphi}(s)]=0<q-p
due to the precision of
\varphi
, it follows from Theorem
8
that
\varphi
must be non-computable, because, otherwise, this theorem would guarantee that there is some some path
\omega\in\Omega
that is R-random for
[p,q]
but not R-random for
\varphi
.

To conclude, we prove that the precise forecasting system
\varphi
can’t be stationary either.
Indeed, assume
ex absurdo
that
\varphi(s)=r\in[0,1]
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
.
Theorem
8
guarantees that there is some path
\omega\in\Omega
that is R-random for
[p,q]
, but that is not R-random for any computable forecasting system whose highest imprecision is smaller than that of
[p,q]
.
By our assumption, however,
\omega
must also be R-random for
\varphi
, or in other words, for
r
.
Now, there always is some computable interval forecast
[\underline{r},\overline{r}]
such that
r\in[\underline{r},\overline{r}]
and
\overline{r}-\underline{r}<q-p
.
Since
\omega
is R-random for
r
by assumption, it is also R-random for
[\underline{r},\overline{r}]
by Proposition
6
, and this contradicts the assumption that
\omega
is not R-random for any computable forecasting system whose highest imprecision is smaller than that of
[p,q]
.
∎

We repeat that, in the martingale-theoretic setting, our main result complements Theorem
8
by showing the importance of the computability assumption on the precise forecasting systems.
It turns out that our main result is also interesting from a measure-theoretic randomness perspective, since we’ll see below it readily leads to corollaries that are reminiscent of existing measure-theoretic randomness results.
The measure-theoretic randomness notion that we consider for this purpose is
uniform randomness
, which, as mentioned in the Introduction, allows us to consider the randomness of a path with respect to an effectively closed—or compact—class of measures.
In particular, there is a test such that a path
\omega
passes this test if and only if it is uniformly random with respect to at least one member of the considered class of measures
[
7
, Theorem 5.23 and Remark 5.24]
.
Our next result shows that our martingale-theoretic notion of randomness satisfies a similar property: a path
\omega\in\Omega
is random for a stationary interval forecast if and only if it is random for at least one compatible precise forecasting system.

Corollary 11
.

Consider any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
and any stationary interval forecast
[p,q]\in\mathscr{I}
.
Then a path
\omega\in\Omega
is
R
-random for
[p,q]
if and only if it is
R
-random for at least one compatible precise forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
.

Proof.

By Proposition
6
, the ‘if’ part is straightforward, so we proceed to the ‘only if’ part.
Assume that
\omega
is R-random for the interval forecast
[p,q]
. If
p=q
, then
\omega
is clearly R-random for the compatible price forecasting system
p=[p,q]
. If
p<q
, then it follows from Corollary
9
that there is some precise compatible forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
for which
\omega
is R-random.
∎

Another measure-theoretic result that we can now show has a martingale-theoretic counterpart, is the existence of a so-called
neutral
measure for which all paths
\omega\in\Omega
are random. In a measure-theoretic context, this is true for uniform randomness
[
7
, Theorem 6.2]
. We here obtain a similar result for any of the four martingale-theoretic notions of randomness that we consider.

Corollary 12
.

Consider any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
.
Then there is a precise—but necessarily non-stationary and non-computable—forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
for which all paths
\omega\in\Omega
are
R
-random.

Proof.

From Corollary
9
, it immediately follows that there is some precise forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
such that
\Omega_{\textnormal{R}}(\varphi)=\Omega_{\textnormal{R}}([0,1])
, and hence, by Proposition
5
,
\Omega_{\textnormal{R}}(\varphi)=\Omega
.
Corollary
10
then implies that
\varphi
is necessarily non-stationary and non-computable.
∎

We find this result to be particularly intriguing.
Proposition
5
guarantees that every path
\omega\in\Omega
is random for the vacuous forecasting system.
But since all precise forecasting systems are compatible with the vacuous forecasting system, Corollary
11
then tells us that this amounts to every path
\omega\in\Omega
being random for at least one precise forecasting system.
The result above strengthens this, by showing that there is in fact one single precise forecasting system for wich all paths are random.

Where does this discussion leave us?
Corollaries
9
and
10
show that the randomness of a path
\omega\in\Omega
with respect to an interval forecast
[p,q]\in\mathscr{I}
with
p<q
, be it computable or not, can be equivalently described by a precise forecasting system that is then necessarily
non-computable
and non-stationary. This furthermore implied, as we have seen in Corollary
11
, that randomness with respect to a stationary interval forecast can be equivalently described in terms of the compatible precise forecasting systems.
It may therefore seem that, on purely theoretical grounds, stationary imprecise forecasting systems are not needed in the study of algorithmic randomness.
However, if we want to maintain our claim that randomness is inherently imprecise, Corollaries
10
and
12
tell us we need only explain why we believe that non-computable forecasting systems are non-satisfactory, and even fairly useless.
We will come to that in Section
10
, where we argue why the computability assumption on the forecasting system is justified on practical grounds.
But before getting to that, we now devote ourselves to the formulation and proof of Theorem
24
, and to introducing the requisite mathematical machinery.

7
Global uncertainty models and almost sure events

We will not only consider ‘local’ gambles on the sample space
\{0,1\}
, as we did in Section
2
, but also ‘global’ gambles
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, which are bounded maps from the set
\Omega
of all paths to the real numbers.
We collect all such gambles in the set
\mathscr{L}(\Omega)
.
With every subset
A\subseteq\Omega
, which we call an
event
, we associate the gamble
\mathbb{I}_{A}\in\mathscr{L}(\Omega)
which assumes the value
1
on
A
and
0
elsewhere, and call it the
indicator
of
A
; observe that, since
0\leq\mathbb{I}_{A}(\omega)\leq 1
for any
\omega\in\Omega
,
\mathbb{I}_{A}
is bounded and hence indeed a gamble.
The complement
\Omega\setminus A
of an event
A\in\Omega
is denoted by
A^{c}
and its indicator
\mathbb{I}_{A^{c}}
satisfies
\mathbb{I}_{A}=1-\mathbb{I}_{A^{c}}
.

Similarly to considering upper and lower expectations of local gambles with respect to interval forecasts in Section
2
, we can also associate global upper and lower expectations with global gambles
f\in\mathscr{L}(\Omega)
, but now with respect to forecasting systems
\varphi\in\Phi
.
We do so by adopting the so-called game-theoretic
[
2
, Equations (6) and (7)]
4
4

4

Several versions of these definitions exist, which differ only in the type of supermartingales that are used (real-valued, extended real-valued, unbounded, bounded, bounded below)
[
2
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
. For gambles, however, all these definitions are equivalent. We adopt the version in
[
2
]
for reasons of simplicity, as it allows us to use the same supermartingales we introduced in Section
3
.
upper expectation
\overline{E}^{\varphi}
and lower expectation
\underline{E}^{\varphi}
, which are defined by

\displaystyle\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf\big\{M(\square)\colon M\in\overline{\mathbb{M}}(\varphi)\text{ and }\liminf_{n\to\infty}M(\omega_{1:n})\geq f(\omega)\text{ for all~$\omega\in\Omega$}\big\}\text{ for all }f\in\mathscr{L}(\Omega),

and

\displaystyle\underline{E}^{\varphi}(f)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup\big\{M(\square)\colon M\in\underline{\mathbb{M}}(\varphi)\text{ and }\limsup_{n\to\infty}M(\omega_{1:n})\leq f(\omega)\text{ for all~$\omega\in\Omega$}\big\}\text{ for all }f\in\mathscr{L}(\Omega).

These global upper and lower expectations are related to each other through the following conjugacy relationship
[
2
, Equation (8)]
:
\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f)=-\underline{E}^{\varphi}(-f)
for all
f\in\mathscr{L}(\Omega)
.

The global upper expectation
\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f)
is the infimum initial capital for which Sceptic can adopt a betting strategy that guarantees her ending up with a higher capital than the reward that is associated with
f
, along all paths.
Moreover,
\overline{E}^{\varphi}
satisfies the following properties, which resemble
C1
–
C5
.

Proposition 13

(
[
2
, Proposition 2]
)
.

Consider any forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
.
Then for all gambles
f,g\in\mathscr{L}(\Omega)
and all
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
:

E1.

\inf f\leq\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f)\leq\sup f

[boundedness]

E2.

\overline{E}^{\varphi}(\lambda f)=\lambda\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f)

[non-negative homogeneity]

E3.

\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f+g)\leq\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f)+\overline{E}^{\varphi}(g)

[subadditivity]

E4.

\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f+\mu)=\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f)+\mu

[constant additivity]

E5.

if
f\leq g
then
\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f)\leq\overline{E}^{\varphi}(g)

[monotonicity]

For any event
A\subseteq\Omega
and any forecasting system
\varphi
, global upper and lower expectations allow us to also define their corresponding
lower and upper probabilities
:
\underline{P}^{\varphi}(A)\coloneqq\underline{E}^{\varphi}(\mathbb{I}_{A})
and
\overline{P}^{\varphi}(A)\coloneqq\overline{E}^{\varphi}(\mathbb{I}_{A})
.
We say that an event
A
is
almost sure
for a forecasting system
\varphi
if
\underline{P}^{\varphi}(A)=1
; if the forecasting system
\varphi
is not important, or clear from the context, we simply say that
A
is almost sure.
Observe that by
E4
and conjugacy,

\overline{P}^{\varphi}(A^{c})=\overline{E}^{\varphi}(\mathbb{I}_{A^{c}})=\overline{E}^{\varphi}(1-\mathbb{I}_{A})=1+\overline{E}^{\varphi}(-\mathbb{I}_{A})=1-\underline{E}^{\varphi}(\mathbb{I}_{A})=1-\underline{P}^{\varphi}(A)\text{ for all }A\subseteq\Omega.

Hence, an event
A
is almost sure if and only if
\overline{P}^{\varphi}(A^{c})=0
.
This alternative characterisation is often more convenient in proofs, and we will use it implicitly.

There are two features of almost sure events that will be useful to us. The first is that they are never empty.

Proposition 14
.

Any almost sure event
A\subseteq\Omega
is non-empty.

Proof.

Assume
ex absurdo
that
A
is empty.
This would imply that
A^{c}=\Omega
and therefore, since
A
is almost sure, that
\overline{P}^{\varphi}(\Omega)=0
. But it follows from property
E1
that, actually,
\overline{P}^{\varphi}(\Omega)=\overline{E}^{\varphi}(1)=1
.
∎

The second feature is that countable intersections of almost sure events are still almost sure.
We start with finite intersections.

Lemma 15
.

Consider two almost sure events
A,B\subseteq\Omega
, then
A\cap B
is almost sure as well.

Proof.

Since
A
and
B
are almost sure events, we know that
\overline{P}^{\varphi}(A^{c})=0
and
\overline{P}^{\varphi}(B^{c})=0
.
By invoking properties
E1
,
E3
and
E5
, it follows that

\displaystyle 0\overset{\text{\ref{prop:globalbound}}}{\leq}\overline{E}^{\varphi}(\mathbb{I}_{(A\cap B)^{c})})=\overline{E}^{\varphi}(\mathbb{I}_{A^{c}\cup B^{c}})\overset{\text{\ref{prop:increasingness}}}{\leq}\overline{E}^{\varphi}(\mathbb{I}_{A^{c}}+\mathbb{I}_{B^{c}})\overset{\text{\ref{prop:subadditivity}}}{\leq}\overline{E}^{\varphi}(\mathbb{I}_{A^{c}})+\overline{E}^{\varphi}(\mathbb{I}_{B^{c}})=\overline{P}^{\varphi}(A^{c})+\overline{P}^{\varphi}(B^{c})=0.

So
\overline{P}^{\varphi}((A\cap B)^{c})=0
and, therefore,
A\cap B
is almost sure.
∎

By combining this result with the following lemma, we obtain the version for countable intersections.

Lemma 16
.

Consider any non-decreasing sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}
in
\mathscr{L}(\Omega)
that converges pointwise to a gamble
f\in\mathscr{L}(\Omega)
.
Then
\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f_{n})
.

Proof.

From
[
27
, Equation (5) and Proposition 10]
, it follows that the global upper expectation
\overline{E}^{\varphi}(f)
, with
f\in\mathscr{L}(\Omega)
, can be equivalently defined in terms of bounded below supermartingales.
By
[
26
, Proposition 36]
, this equivalence continues to hold when considering extended real-valued bounded below supermartingales.
Consequently, this lemma follows from
[
26
, Theorem 23]
.
∎

Corollary 17
.

For any sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}
of almost sure events,
\bigcap_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}A_{n}
is almost sure.

Proof.

For any
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, we know from Lemma
15
that the event
\bigcap_{k=0}^{n}A_{k}
is almost sure and, therefore, that
0=\overline{P}^{\varphi}((\bigcap_{k=0}^{n}A_{k})^{c})=\overline{P}^{\varphi}(\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}A^{c}_{k})
.
Since the sequence
(\mathbb{I}_{\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}A^{c}_{k}})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}
in
\mathscr{L}(\Omega)
is non-decreasing and converges pointwise to the gamble
\mathbb{I}_{\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}A^{c}_{n}}\in\mathscr{L}(\Omega)
, it follows from Lemma
16
that

\displaystyle\overline{P}^{\varphi}((\bigcap_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}A_{n})^{c})=\overline{P}^{\varphi}(\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}A^{c}_{n})=\overline{E}^{\varphi}(\mathbb{I}_{\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}A^{c}_{n}})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\overline{E}^{\varphi}(\mathbb{I}_{\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}A^{c}_{k}})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\overline{P}^{\varphi}(\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}A^{c}_{k})=0,

so
\bigcap_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}A_{n}
is indeed almost sure.
∎

8
Several frequentist notions of randomness

Inspired by von Mises and Wald’s work
[
11
,
10
,
9
]
, which we mentioned in the Introduction, we will also consider a number of very general frequentist notions of randomness, in addition to the four martingale-theoretic randomness notions mentioned in Section
5
.
In particular, we will consider a path
\omega\in\Omega
to be random for a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
and a countable set of selection processes
\mathscr{S}
if the frequencies of ones along all infinite subsequences of
\omega
selected by these selection processes are bounded by
\varphi
, in the following sense.

Definition 4
.

Consider any countable set of selection processes
\mathscr{S}
.
Then a path
\omega\in\Omega
is
\mathscr{S}
-random for a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
if for every selection process
S\in\mathscr{S}
for which
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})=\infty
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})[\omega_{k+1}-\underline{\varphi}(\omega_{1:k})]}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})}\geq 0\text{ and }\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})[\omega_{k+1}-\overline{\varphi}(\omega_{1:k})]}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})}\leq 0.

For a stationary forecasting system
I\in\mathscr{I}
, the conditions in this definition simplify to the perhaps more intuitive requirement that

\min I\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})\omega_{k+1}}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})\omega_{k+1}}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})}\leq\max I.

If we restrict our attention to the set of all recursive selection processes—which, as mentioned in the Introduction
[
10
,
12
]
, is countable—then the above randomness notion coincides with the notion of Church (CH) randomness that we introduced elsewhere, and similarly for recursive temporal selection processes and weak Church (wCH) randomness
[
19
]
.

Definition 5

(
[
19
, Definition 6]
)
.

A path
\omega\in\Omega
is CH-random (wCH-random) for a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
if for every recursive (recursive temporal) selection process
S
for which
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})=\infty
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})[\omega_{k+1}-\underline{\varphi}(\omega_{1:k})]}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})}\geq 0\text{ and }\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})[\omega_{k+1}-\overline{\varphi}(\omega_{1:k})]}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})}\leq 0.

In order to establish that Definition
4
is meaningful, and therefore Definition
5
as well, we proceed as in Section
5
and prove that for every forecasting system
\varphi
and every countable set of selection processes
\mathscr{S}
, there is at least one
\mathscr{S}
-random path for
\varphi
.
Observe that this extends the work of Wald that we mentioned in the Introduction.
We will make use of the following lemma.

Lemma 18

(
[
2

)

For any forecasting system
\varphi
, any selection process
S
and any gamble

\displaystyle\bigg\{\omega\in\Omega\colon\text{if }\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})=\infty\text{ then }\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})\big[f(\omega_{k+1})-\underline{E}_{\varphi(\omega_{1:k})}(f)\big]}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})}\geq 0\bigg\}

\varphi
.

From Lemma

, it will readily follow that for any forecasting system
\varphi

\mathscr{S}

\mathscr{S}

\varphi

\varphi

\mathscr{S}

\displaystyle A^{+}_{S}

\displaystyle A^{-}_{S}

Corollary 20
.

For any forecasting system
\varphi

, there is at least one path
\omega\in\Omega

\mathscr{S}
-random for

.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Propositions

∎

If we restrict our attention to interval forecasts

Proposition 21
.

Consider any path
\omega\in\Omega

If

is
wCH
-random for
I
, then it is non-recursive.
The same is true if
\omega
is
\mathscr{S}
-random for
I
, with
\mathscr{S}
a countable superset of the recursive temporal selection processes,
or if
\omega
is
R
-random for
I
, for any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
.

Proof.

Assume
ex absurdo
that
\omega
is recursive.
Then the temporal selection processes
S_{0},S_{1}~\in~\smash{\mathscr{S}}
, defined by
S_{0}(n)\coloneqq 1-\smash{\omega_{n+1}}
and
S_{1}(n)\coloneqq\smash{\omega_{n+1}}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, are recursive.
Clearly, since
\omega
is a binary infinite sequence, it holds that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\smash{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}}S_{0}(\omega_{1:k})=\infty
or
\lim_{n\to\infty}\smash{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}}S_{1}(\omega_{1:k})=\infty
, and therefore that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{0}(\omega_{1:k})\omega_{k+1}}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{0}(\omega_{1:k})}=0\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{1}(\omega_{1:k})\omega_{k+1}}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{1}(\omega_{1:k})}=1.

However, due to our assumptions about
I
, we also know—
using Definition
5
if
\omega
is wCH-random, Definition
4
if
\omega
is
\mathscr{S}
-random, or
[
2
, Corollary 29]
if
\omega
is
R
-random
—that every recursive temporal selection process
S
for which
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})=+\infty
satisfies

\displaystyle 0<\min I\leq\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})\omega_{k+1}}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})}\quad\text{and}\quad\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})\omega_{k+1}}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\omega_{1:k})}\leq\max I<1,

a contradiction.
∎

In other words, if a path is recursive, meaning that it has a finite description, then it cannot be random—in any of the senses considered in the result—with respect to an interval forecast
I
that stays away from both zero and one. Consider for example the path that takes the value zero at even positions and one at the others. Due to the above result, this path cannot be random with respect to an interval
[p,q]
, unless either
p=0
or
q=1
. In fact, it is easy to see that this particular path is only random for the interval
[0,1]
.

In the next section, we will use an
\mathscr{S}
-random path
\omega\in\Omega
to finally craft the special precise forecasting systems we have been constantly talking about. Due to the above result, this path will necessarily be non-recursive, and hence not describable in any finite way.

9
Proof of the main result

At this point, we have introduced the mathematical apparatus that is necessary for proving the main result of this paper; we intend to show that for any interval forecast
[p,q]\in\mathscr{I}
and any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
, there is some (non-stationary non-computable) precise forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
that has the exact same set of R-random paths as
[p,q]
.

To this end, we consider a special type of precise forecasting system.
Fix any two real numbers
p,q\in[0,1]
and any path
\varpi\in\Omega
, and consider the associated temporal precise forecasting system
\varphi^{\varpi}_{p,q}\in\Phi
, defined by

\varphi^{\varpi}_{p,q}(n)\coloneqq\begin{cases}p&\text{if }\varpi_{n+1}=0\\
q&\text{if }\varpi_{n+1}=1\end{cases}\text{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}.

(5)

In our main result, Theorem
24
below, we will in particular use paths
\varpi
that are
\mathscr{S}
-random for an interval forecast
I\subseteq(0,1)
, where the countable set of selection processes
\mathscr{S}
is of a special type.
To make clear what such
\mathscr{S}
are like, we start by associating with every real process
F
and every real number
r\in[0,1]
a temporal selection process
S^{r}_{F}
, defined by

S^{r}_{F}(n)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if $E_{r}(\Delta F(s))>0$ for some $s\in\mathbb{S}$ with $|s|=n$}\\
0&\text{if $E_{r}(\Delta F(s))\leq 0$ for all $s\in\mathbb{S}$ with $|s|=n$}\end{cases}\text{ for all $n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}$.}

(6)

We use these temporal selection processes to associate with every countable set
\mathscr{F}
of real processes and every two real numbers
p,q\in[0,1]
the clearly countable set
\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}}
of temporal selection processes, defined by

\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}}\coloneqq\big\{S^{r}_{F}\colon F\in\mathscr{F}\text{ and }r\in\{p,q\}\big\}.

(7)

Since
\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}}
is countable, Corollary
20
guarantees that there is at least one path that is
\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}}
-random for a given interval forecast
I\subseteq(0,1)
.

In this construction of the sets
\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}}
, the specific countable sets
\mathscr{F}
of real processes that we will consider, are the sets
\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{S}}
,
\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{C}}
,
\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{wML}}
and
\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{ML}}
introduced in Section
4
.
If we recall that
\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{S}}=\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{C}}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{wML}}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{ML}}
, Equation (
7
) tells us that

\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{S}}}=\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{C}}}\subseteq\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{wML}}}\subseteq\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{ML}}}\text{ for all $p,q\in[0,1]$.}

(8)

The sets
\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}}
might look a bit artificial, but if we restrict our attention to
rational
numbers
p,q\in\mathbb{Q}
and to
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
, then as our next results shows, the corresponding
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}^{p,q}
are in fact the set of all recursive temporal selection processes.
Since this is exactly the set of selection processes used in Definition
5
to define wCH-randomness, we conclude from this that in those particular cases, for any forecasting system
\varphi
, a path
\varpi\in\Omega
is
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}^{p,q}
-random for
\varphi
if and only if it is
wCH
-random for
\varphi
.

Proposition 22
.

Consider any two rational numbers
p,q\in[0,1]
and any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
.
Then
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}^{p,q}
consists of all recursive temporal selection processes.

Proof.

We start by proving that every selection process
S\in\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}^{p,q}
is recursive and temporal.
By Equation (
7
), we know that
S=S^{r}_{F}
for some
F\in\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}
and
r\in\{p,q\}
.
Hence, by Equation (
6
),
S=S^{r}_{F}
is temporal.
Furthermore, since the rational process
F
is recursive, since
p,q\in\mathbb{Q}
and since there is a finite algorithm that, for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, can enumerate the finite number of situations
s\in\mathbb{S}
for which
|s|=k
, it immediately follows that there is a finite algorithm that can check the inequalities in Equation (
6
), so the temporal selection process
S=S^{r}_{F}
is also recursive.

That, conversely, every recursive temporal selection process belongs to
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}^{p,q}
,
follows directly from our next result, Proposition
23
.
∎

More generally, for any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
and any two
real
numbers
p,q\in[0,1]
, the set
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}^{p,q}
will actually include all recursive temporal selection processes.
Therefore, if a path
\varpi\in\Omega
is
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}^{p,q}
-random for a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
, it will in particular also be wCH-random for
\varphi
.
Further on, we will restrict our attention to stationary forecasting systems
\varphi\in\Phi
that stay away from zero and one, in the sense that
\varphi(s)=I\subseteq(0,1)
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
, and the
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}^{p,q}
-random paths for
I
will then necessarily be non-recursive, due to Proposition
21
.

Proposition 23
.

Consider any two real numbers
p,q\in[0,1]
and any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
.
Then every recursive temporal selection process belongs to
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}^{p,q}
.

Proof.

Fix any recursive temporal selection process
S
and consider the temporal rational test process
F
defined by
F(n)\coloneqq 1+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(k)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
Since
S
is recursive and non-negative, it readily follows that the rational test process
F
is recursive and positive, and therefore
F\in\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{S}}
.
Since
\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{S}}=\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{C}}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{wML}}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{ML}}
, this implies that
F\in\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}
, and therefore, that
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}^{p,q}
contains the temporal selection process
S^{p}_{F}
.

We conclude the argument by showing that
S^{p}_{F}=S
.
To do so, fix any
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
If
S(n)=1
, then for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
and
x\in\{0,1\}
with
|s|=n
, we have that
\Delta F(s)(x)=F(n+1)-F(n)=S(n)=1
, and hence, by
C1
,
E_{p}(\Delta F(s))=1>0
.
Consequently, by Equation (
6
),
S^{p}_{F}(n)=1=S(n)
.
Otherwise, if
S(n)=0
, then for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
and
x\in\{0,1\}
with
|s|=n
, we have that
\Delta F(s)(x)=F(n+1)-F(n)=S(n)=0
, and hence, by
C1
,
E_{p}(\Delta F(s))=0
. As a consequence, by Equation (
6
),
S^{p}_{F}(n)=0=S(n)
.
∎

Let us now move on to our main result, where we use the special countable sets of selection processes
\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}}
and the special forecasting systems
\varphi^{\varpi}_{p,q}
to reveal a surprisingly close connection between non-stationary precise forecasting systems and interval forecasts.

Theorem 24
.

Consider any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
, any two real numbers
p,q\in[0,1]
such that
p<q
, any interval forecast
I\subseteq(0,1)
, any countable set of selection processes
\mathscr{S}\supseteq\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}
, and any path
\varpi\in\Omega
that is
\mathscr{S}
-random for
I
.
Then a path
\omega\in\Omega
is
R
-random for
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
if and only if it is
R
-random for
[p,q]
.

In our proof for this result, we make use of the following two lemmas to prove the implementability of a number of real processes.

Lemma 25
.

Consider any lower semicomputable real process
F
and any two natural numbers
N,K\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then the real process
\tilde{F}
, defined by

\tilde{F}(s)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|\leq N\\
\frac{1}{K}F(s)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}\text{ for all }s\in\mathbb{S},

is lower semicomputable as well.

Proof.

Since the real process
F
is lower semicomputable, there is a recursive rational map
q\colon\mathbb{S}\times{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to\mathbb{Q}
such that
q(s,n+1)\geq q(s,n)
and
F(s)=\lim_{m\to\infty}q(s,m)
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
Consider now the recursive rational map
\tilde{q}\colon\mathbb{S}\times{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to\mathbb{Q}
defined by

\tilde{q}(s,n)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|\leq N\\
\frac{1}{K}q(s,n)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}\text{ for all }s\in\mathbb{S}\text{ and }n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}.

Then for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
,

\tilde{q}(s,n+1)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|\leq N\\
\frac{1}{K}q(s,n+1)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}\geq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|\leq N\\
\frac{1}{K}q(s,n)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}=\tilde{q}(s,n)

and

\lim_{m\to\infty}\tilde{q}(s,m)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|\leq N\\
\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{K}q(s,m)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|\leq N\\
\frac{1}{K}F(s)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}=\tilde{F}(s),

and therefore,
\tilde{F}
is lower semicomputable as well.
∎

Lemma 26
.

Consider any test process
F
that is generated by a lower semicomputable multiplier process, and any two natural numbers
N,K\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then the test process
\tilde{F}
, defined by

\tilde{F}(s)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|\leq N\\
\frac{1}{K}F(s)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}\text{ for all }s\in\mathbb{S},

is generated by a lower semicomputable multiplier process as well.

Proof.

Let
D
be the lower semicomputable multiplier process that generates
F
, meaning that
F=D^{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\circledcirc$}}
.

Since
D
is lower semicomputable, there is a recursive rational map
q\colon\mathbb{S}\times\{0,1\}\times{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to\mathbb{Q}
such that
q(s,x,n+1)\geq q(s,x,n)
and
D(s)(x)=\lim_{m\to\infty}q(s,x,m)
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
,
x\in\{0,1\}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
Since
D
is a multiplier process, it is non-negative, and hence, we can safely assume that the recursive rational map
q
is non-negative as well; otherwise, we just replace it by the recursive rational map
\max\{q,0\}
.
Moreover, since
F
is generated by the multiplier process
D
, it readily follows that
\tilde{F}
is generated by the multiplier process
\tilde{D}
defined by

\tilde{D}(s)(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|<N\\
\frac{1}{K}F(sx)&\text{if }|s|=N\\
D(s)(x)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}\text{ for all }s\in\mathbb{S}\text{ and }x\in\{0,1\}.

So it suffices to prove that
\tilde{D}
is lower semicomputable.
To that end, consider the recursive rational map
\tilde{q}\colon\mathbb{S}\times\{0,1\}\times{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to\mathbb{Q}
defined by

\tilde{q}(s,x,n)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|<N\\
\frac{1}{K}\Big(\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}q(x_{1:k},x_{k+1},n)\Big)q(s,x,n)&\text{if }|s|=N\\
q(s,x,n)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}\text{ for all $s=x_{1:m}\in\mathbb{S}$, $x\in\{0,1\}$ and $n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}$}.

Then for all
s=x_{1:m}\in\mathbb{S}
,
x\in\{0,1\}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(s,x,n+1)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|<N\\
\frac{1}{K}\Big(\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}q(x_{1:k},x_{k+1},n+1)\Big)q(s,x,n+1)&\text{if }|s|=N\\
q(s,x,n+1)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}

\displaystyle\geq\begin{cases}\mathrlap{1}\hphantom{\frac{1}{K}\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}q(x_{1:k},x_{k+1},n+1)q(s,x,n+1)}&\text{if }|s|<N\\
\frac{1}{K}\Big(\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}q(x_{1:k},x_{k+1},n)\Big)q(s,x,n)&\text{if }|s|=N\\
q(s,x,n)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\tilde{q}(s,x,n),

where the inequality holds because
K>0
and
q\geq 0
, and

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\tilde{q}(s,x,m)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathrlap{1}\hphantom{\frac{1}{K}\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}q(x_{1:k},x_{k+1},n+1)q(s,x,n+1)}&\text{if }|s|<N\\
\frac{1}{K}\lim_{m\to\infty}\Big(\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}q(x_{1:k},x_{k+1},m)\Big)q(s,x,m)&\text{if }|s|=N\\
\lim_{m\to\infty}q(s,x,m)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathrlap{1}\hphantom{\frac{1}{K}\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}q(x_{1:k},x_{k+1},n+1)q(s,x,n+1)}&\text{if }|s|<N\\
\frac{1}{K}\Big(\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}D(x_{1:k})(x_{k+1})\Big)D(s)(x)&\text{if }|s|=N\\
D(s)(x)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|<N\\
\frac{1}{K}F(sx)&\text{if }|s|=N\\
D(s)(x)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\tilde{D}(s)(x),

so we see that
\tilde{D}
is lower semicomputable, as needed.
∎

Proof of Theorem
24
.

We begin with the direct implication.
Assume that
\omega\in\Omega
is R-random for
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
.
Since
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}(n)\subseteq[p,q]
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, it follows from Proposition
6
that
\omega
is also R-random for
[p,q]
.

To prove the converse implication, assume that
\omega\in\Omega
is R-random for
[p,q]
.
Taking into account Definitions
2
and
3
, in order to prove that
\omega
is R-random for
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
, we consider any test supermartingale
T\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{R}}(\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi})}
and prove that it isn’t unbounded on
\omega
when
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C}\}
, and that it isn’t computably unbounded on
\omega
when
\textnormal{R}=\textnormal{S}
.

To this end, consider the two temporal selection processes
S_{T}^{p}
and
S_{T}^{q}
as defined by Equation (
6
).
We will take a closer look at the temporal selection process
S_{T}^{p}
and prove that there is only a finite number of non-negative integers
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
for which
S_{T}^{p}(n)=1
.
To this end, assume
ex absurdo
that there is an infinite number of them, and therefore that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{T}^{p}(k)=\infty
.
Consider any
k\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
such that
S_{T}^{p}(k)=1
, then it follows from Equation (
6
) that there is some
s\in\mathbb{S}
with
|s|=k
such that
E_{p}(\Delta T(s))>0
.
Since
E_{\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}(s)}(\Delta T(s))\leq 0
(because
\Delta T
is a supermartingale for
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
), this implies that necessarily
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}(s)=q
and therefore, since
|s|=k
, we infer from Equation (
5
) that
\varpi_{k+1}=1
.
Since this is true for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
such that
S_{T}^{p}(k)=1
, it follows that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{T}^{p}(k)\varpi_{k+1}}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{T}^{p}(k)}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{T}^{p}(k)}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{T}^{p}(k)}=1.

(9)

Since
T\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{R}}(\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi})}
, and therefore also
T\in\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}
, we can infer from Equation (

) that
S_{T}^{p}\in\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}\subseteq\mathscr{S}
.
Consequently, since
\varpi
is
\mathscr{S}
-random for

by assumption and since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{T}^{p}(k)=\infty
, we find [see Definition
4
] that

\max I\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{T}^{p}(k)\varpi_{k+1}}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{T}^{p}(k)}\overset{\eqref{eq:helpiewelpie}}{=}1,

contradicting the assumption that
I\subseteq(0,1)
.
We conclude that, indeed, there is only a finite number of non-negative integers
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
for which
S_{T}^{p}(n)=1
.

In a completely similar manner, it can be shown that there is only a finite number of non-negative integers
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
for which
S_{T}^{q}(n)=1
.
Indeed, assume
ex absurdo
that there is an infinite number of them.
Then, by adopting a similar argument, it follows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{T}^{q}(k)=\infty
, that
S_{T}^{q}\in\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}\subseteq\mathscr{S}
, and that for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, if
S_{T}^{q}(k)=1
, then
\varpi_{k+1}=0
.
That being so, it follows from Definition

, since
\varpi
is
\mathscr{S}
-random for
I
by assumption, that

\min I\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{T}^{q}(k)\varpi_{k+1}}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{T}^{q}(k)}=0,

contradicting the assumption that
I\subseteq(0,1)
.

Since there are only a finite number of non-negative integers
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
for which
S_{T}^{p}(n)=1
or
S_{T}^{q}(n)=1
, and since for each such
n

s\in\mathbb{S}
such that
|s|=n
, it follows from Equation (

) that there are only a finite number of situations
s\in\mathbb{S}
for which
E_{p}(\Delta T(s))>0
or
E_{q}(\Delta T(s))>0
.
Hence, there is some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\overline{E}_{[p,q]}(\Delta T(s))=\max\{E_{p}(\Delta T(s)),E_{q}(\Delta T(s))\}\leq 0
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
such that
|s|>N
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
be any positive natural number such that
K>T(s)
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
with
|s|=N+1
, and consider the test process
\tilde{T}\colon\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(s)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|s|\leq N\\
\frac{1}{K}T(s)&\text{if }|s|>N\end{cases}\text{ for all }s\in\mathbb{S}.

We intend to prove that
\tilde{T}\in\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{R}}([p,q])
. We will do so by consecutively showing that it is a supermartingale for
[p,q]
, that it is positive if
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
, that it is implementable in the same way as
T
is, and that
\tilde{T}(\square)=1
.

To prove that
\tilde{T}
is a supermartingale for
[p,q]
, we fix some
s\in\mathbb{S}
, and consider three mutually exclusive possibilities:
|s|<N
,
|s|=N
and
|s|>N
.
If
|s|<N
, then

\overline{E}_{[p,q]}\big(\Delta\tilde{T}(s)\big)=\overline{E}_{[p,q]}(0)\overset{\text{\ref{axiom:coherence:bounds}}}{=}0.

If
|s|=N
, then

\overline{E}_{[p,q]}(\Delta\tilde{T}(s))=\overline{E}_{[p,q]}\big(\tilde{T}(s\,\bullet)-\tilde{T}(s)\big)=\overline{E}_{[p,q]}\Big(\frac{1}{K}T(s\,\bullet)-1\Big)\overset{\text{\ref{axiom:coherence:increasingness}}}{\leq}\overline{E}_{[p,q]}(1-1)\overset{\text{\ref{axiom:coherence:bounds}}}{=}0,

where the inequality holds because
K>T(t)\geq 0
for all
t\in\mathbb{S}
with
|t|=N+1
.
Finally, if
|s|>N
, then

\overline{E}_{[p,q]}(\Delta\tilde{T}(s))=\overline{E}_{[p,q]}\Big(\frac{1}{K}\Delta T(s)\Big)\overset{\text{\ref{axiom:coherence:homogeneity}}}{=}\frac{1}{K}\overline{E}_{[p,q]}(\Delta T(s))\leq 0,

where in the second equality and final inequality, we also used the fact that
K>0
.

Observe that
\tilde{T}
is positive if
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
, because then
T\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{R}}(\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi})}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}
is positive and because
K>0
.

Let us now prove that
\tilde{T}
is implementable in the same way as
T
is.
If
\textnormal{R}=\textnormal{ML}
, then
T\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{ML}}(\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi})}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{ML}}
is lower semicomputable, so it follows from Lemma
25
that
\tilde{T}
is lower semicomputable as well.
If
\textnormal{R}=\textnormal{wML}
, then
T\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{wML}}(\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi})}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{wML}}
is generated by a lower semicomputable multiplier process, so it follows from Lemma
26
that
\tilde{T}
is generated by a lower semicomputable multiplier process as well.
And finally, if
\textnormal{R}=\textnormal{C}
or
\textnormal{R}=\textnormal{S}
, then
T\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{C}}(\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi})}=\smash{\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{S}}(\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi})}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{C}}=\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{S}}
is a rational and recursive process, and it is therefore obvious that this is true for
\tilde{T}
as well.

Since also
\tilde{T}(\square)=1
, we conclude that
\tilde{T}\in\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{R}}([p,q])
.
We now consider two possibilities.
If
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C}\}
, then since
\omega
is R-random for
[p,q]
by assumption,
\tilde{T}
can’t be unbounded on
\omega
by Definition
2
.
Since also

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tilde{T}(\omega_{1:n})<\infty\Rightarrow\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{T(\omega_{1:n})}{K}<\infty\overset{K>0}{\Rightarrow}\limsup_{n\to\infty}T(\omega_{1:n})<\infty,

it then follows that
T
does not become unbounded on
\omega
.

If
\textnormal{R}=\textnormal{S}
, then since
\omega
is R-random for
[p,q]
by assumption,
\tilde{T}
can’t be computably unbounded on
\omega
by Definition
3
.
Consider now any real growth function
\tau
and an associated real growth function
\tilde{\tau}
defined by
\tilde{\tau}(n)\coloneqq\nicefrac{{\tau(n)}}{{K}}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
It then holds that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}[\tilde{T}(\omega_{1:n})-\tilde{\tau}(n)]<0\Rightarrow\limsup_{n\to\infty}\bigg[\frac{T(\omega_{1:n})}{K}-\frac{\tau(n)}{K}\bigg]<0\overset{K>0}{\Rightarrow}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\bigg[T(\omega_{1:n})-\tau(n)\bigg]<0,

and hence, since
\tilde{T}
is not computably unbounded on
\omega
for the real growth function
\tilde{\tau}
,
T
does not become computably unbounded on
\omega
for
\tau
.
Since this holds for any real growth function
\tau
, we conclude that
T
does not become computably unbounded on
\omega
.
∎

According to Theorem
24
, for every choice of
R
in
\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
, there is some path
\varpi\in\Omega
such that the R-random paths for the interval forecast
[p,q]
and for the temporal precise forecasting system
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
coincide.
Interestingly, there is also a single path
\varpi\in\Omega
that does this job for all four notions of randomness that we consider here.
Basically, this is true because the weaker the notion of randomness, the weaker the conditions on
\varpi
that are required in Theorem
24
, in the sense that the minimally required countable set of selection processes
\mathscr{S}
becomes smaller.

Corollary 27
.

Consider any two real numbers
p,q\in[0,1]
such that
p<q
, any interval forecast
I\subseteq(0,1)
, any countable set of selection processes
\mathscr{S}\supseteq\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{ML}}}^{p,q}
, and any path
\varpi\in\Omega
that is
\mathscr{S}
-random for
I
.
Then, for any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
, a path
\omega\in\Omega
is
R
-random for
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
if and only if it is
R
-random for
[p,q]
.

Proof.

Since
\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{S}}}=\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{C}}}\subseteq\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{wML}}}\subseteq\mathscr{S}^{p,q}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{ML}}}
by Equation (
8
), this follows readily from Theorem
24
.
∎

If we restrict our attention to rational numbers
p,q\in\mathbb{Q}
and to
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
, then, as proved in Proposition
22
, the set
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{F}_{\textnormal{R}}}^{p,q}
consists of the recursive temporal selection processes, and hence, by comparing Definitions
4
and
5
, the conditions on
\varpi
that are required in Theorem
24
translate into
\varpi
being wCH-random for an interval forecast
I\subseteq(0,1)
.

.

Consider any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
, any two rational numbers
p,q\in[0,1]
such that
p<q
, any interval forecast

.
Then a path

is

10
Theoretical and practical necessity of interval forecasts in statistics

Let’s now zoom out and move away from the technicalities in the previous sections, in order to better understand the implications of Theorem
24
and its Corollary
9
.
In trying to come to a better understanding, we have found it useful to look at these results from the point of view of statistics, whose aim it is to learn an uncertainty model from data.
Regarding the data, we will consider a finite sequence
\omega_{1:n}
and assume that it is an initial segment of an idealised (and unobserved) path
\omega
that is (ML-, wML-, C- or S-)random; there are clearly a multitude of forecasting systems for which this is the case.
Under this assumption, we will examine what forecasting systems—that make the path
\omega
random—can be learned from the finite initial segment
\omega_{1:n}
.
Notice that, whilst doing so, we have changed our point of view: instead of focusing on the paths that are random for a forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
, as we have done before, we have a look at the forecasting systems that make a path
\omega\in\Omega
random.
Even though it is commonly assumed that the uncertainty model
\varphi
to be estimated or identified from the data
\omega_{1:n}
is precise, we have put forward elsewhere
[
2
]
a number of arguments that question the assumption that a path’s randomness should always be described by a precise forecasting system
\varphi
.
So, in the discussion below, we want to remain open about that possibility, and see what can be said if we don’t assume
a priori
that the sequence
\omega
is necessarily random for a precise forecasting system.

From Proposition
5
,
we know that there is at least one candidate (stationary) interval forecast that makes
\omega
random: all paths are random for the unit interval
[0,1]
.
In fact, interestingly, there is (almost always) a smallest (stationary) interval forecast
[p,q]
that makes
\omega
random
[
19
]
.
Meanwhile, it is not guaranteed that there is a stationary precise forecast
p
that makes
\omega
random; the smallest (stationary) interval forecast
[p,q]
that makes
\omega
random needn’t be a singleton
[
2
, Section 9.1]
.
Hence, generally speaking, imprecision is needed if we insist on a stationary uncertainty model to describe a path’s randomness.
If we also allow for non-stationary uncertainty models however, then Theorem
24
shows that we could replace
[p,q]
by a non-stationary precise forecasting system
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
, with
\varpi
chosen as in Theorem
24
.
In fact, there is an even more (theoretically) straightforward way to associate a non-stationary precise forecasting system with a path
\omega
: the temporal forecasting system
\varphi_{0,1}^{\omega}
that assigns probability
1
to the actual next value, and hence, makes a perfect prediction.

Proposition 29
.

Consider any
\textnormal{R}\in\{\textnormal{ML},\textnormal{wML},\textnormal{C},\textnormal{S}\}
, then any path
\omega\in\Omega
is
R
-random for the precise forecasting system
\varphi_{0,1}^{\omega}
.

Proof.

Consider any test supermartingale
T\in\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{R}}(\varphi_{0,1}^{\omega})
.
Since
T
is a supermartingale for
\varphi_{0,1}^{\omega}
, it holds for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
that

\displaystyle 0\geq E_{\varphi_{0,1}^{\omega}(\omega_{1:n})}(\Delta T(\omega_{1:n}))

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}E_{0}(\Delta T(\omega_{1:n}))&\text{if }\omega_{n+1}=0\\
E_{1}(\Delta T(\omega_{1:n}))&\text{if }\omega_{n+1}=1\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathrlap{\Delta T(\omega_{1:n})(0)}\hphantom{E_{0}(\Delta T(\omega_{1:n}))}&\text{if }\omega_{n+1}=0\\
\Delta T(\omega_{1:n})(1)&\text{if }\omega_{n+1}=1\end{cases}=\Delta T(\omega_{1:n})(\omega_{n+1}),

and therefore,

\displaystyle T(\omega_{1:n})=T(\square)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Delta T(\omega_{1:k})(\omega_{k+1})\leq T(\square)=1.

Consequently, all test supermartingales
T\in\overline{\mathbb{T}}_{\textnormal{R}}(\varphi_{0,1}^{\omega})
are bounded above by
1
along
\omega
.
It therefore holds [see Definitions
2
and
3
] that
\omega
is R-random for
\varphi_{0,1}^{\omega}
.
∎

Hence, if
\omega
is random for
[p,q]
, then it is also random for at least two non-stationary precise models.
We won’t risk getting bogged down into a discussion on what uncertainty models are best associated with a path
\omega
; that would require a paper on its own.
But we do want to point out that the uncertainty models that correspond with
\omega
typically do not contain the same information; that is, they do not share the same set of random paths.
Interestingly, however, as we know from Theorem
24
,
[p,q]
and
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
do have the same set of random paths and are, in that sense, equally expressive.
On that ground, theoretically, one might argue that the imprecision in
[p,q]
is not needed.

We believe that this story changes when moving to more practical grounds.
If we are given an initial finite segment
\omega_{1:n}
of a path
\omega\in\Omega
and want to learn a forecasting system
\varphi
for which
\omega
is random, we will have to do so by adopting a finite algorithm that, given the data
\omega_{1:n}
, outputs a forecasting system
\varphi^{\prime}
whose set of random paths is then believed to contain
\omega
.
A candidate for
\varphi^{\prime}
could be the forecasting system
\varphi_{0,1}^{\omega}
that is generated by
\omega
itself.
However, it is unfeasible to learn this forecasting system, or to even approximate it, as it basically requires us to know the entire path
\omega
itself.

Another candidate for
\varphi^{\prime}
could be the non-computable forecasting system
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
.
Here too, however, it seems impossible to learn or even approximate this model because it requires us to learn the path
\varpi
, which is non-recursive by Proposition
21
.
At the same time, learning a stationary interval forecast
[p,q]
—which is as expressive as
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
—seems a much less daunting, and practically more feasible, task, especially if
[p,q]
is computable.

In summary, it is one thing to associate precise uncertainty models with a path
\omega
that has no precise stationary forecast, but it is another thing to actually learn them.
When it comes to the latter, computable stationary interval forecasts seem more promising than non-computable non-stationary precise ones.

11
Conclusions and future work

We conclude that precision and computability are not always compatible when describing a path’s randomness.
Indeed, if you require computability, then Theorem
8
shows that you should allow for imprecision as there is at least one path
\omega\in\Omega
whose randomness can be described by a computable interval forecast
[p,q]
, but not by any computable precise forecasting system
\varphi\in\Phi
.
On the other hand, if you require precision, then Theorem
8
and
24
show that you should allow for non-computability since the path
\omega
is random for the non-computable precise forecasting system
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
, but not for any computable precise one.
We repeat that the above holds because interval forecasts
[p,q]
have the same sets of martingale-theoretically random paths as the related non-computable non-stationary precise forecasting systems
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
, while being simpler and stationary.
Moreover, our preliminary analysis suggests that the stationary character of interval forecasts will be of the utmost importance when moving to the field of statistics.
In particular, it seems neither possible nor opportune to try and learn—or even approximate—the non-computable non-stationary precise forecasting systems
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
, which—by definition—cannot be described by a finite algorithm, from a finite initial path segment
\omega_{1:n}
.

In our future work, we plan to further explore these preliminary ideas about a randomness-based approach to statistics, and try to develop new statistical methods based on them.

Moreover, we want to explore whether the theorems and ideas for martingale-theoretic randomness in this paper apply equally well to frequentist notions of randomness, like the ones in Section
8
.
Our preliminary investigation seems to indicate that, similarly to what Theorem
8
states, there are paths that are Church random for an interval forecast
I\in\mathscr{I}
, but not for any computable (more) precise forecasting system; it is an open question whether a similar property holds for weak Church randomness.
Meanwhile, and in contrast with Theorem
24
, we suspect that if there is a precise forecasting system that has the exact same set of (weak) Church random paths as a non-vanishing interval forecast, then it must be non-temporal, and therefore can’t be of the form
\varphi_{p,q}^{\varpi}
.

Lastly, we intend to explore whether the proof of Theorem
24
can be modified to allow for arbitrary forecasting systems
\varphi
rather than only stationary interval forecasts.
