Linear Discriminant Analysis with High-dimensional Mixed Variables
Binyan Jiang, Chenlei Leng, Cheng Wang, Zhongqing Yang, Xinyang Yu

Abstract
Datasets containing both categorical and continuous variables are frequently encountered in many areas. The dimensions of these variables can be very high especially in modern data analysis. Despite the recent progress made in modelling high-dimensional data for continuous variables, there is a scarcity of methods that can deal with a mixed set of variables. To fill this gap, this paper develops a novel approach for classifying high-dimensional observations with mixed variables. Our framework builds on a location model, in which the distributions of the continuous variables conditional on categorical ones are assumed Gaussian. We overcome the challenge of having to split data into exponentially many cells, or combinations of the categorical variables, by kernel smoothing, and provide new perspectives for its bandwidth choice to ensure an analogue of Bochner’s Lemma, which is different to the usual bias-variance tradeoff. We show that the two sets of parameters in our model can be separately estimated and provide penalized likelihood for their estimation. Results on the estimation accuracy and the misclassification rates are established, and the competitive performance of the proposed classifier is illustrated by extensive simulation and real data studies.



Keywords
:
Bayes risk, High dimensional mixed variable, Linear discriminant analysis, Location model, Semiparametric estimation

Introduction

Consider the problem of classifying very high-dimensional observations into categories. In a great many cases, the datasets can contain a mixed set of variables including discrete and continuous ones, both of which can be high-dimensional while the sample size is small. Examples include:

•

In clinical practice, it is common to collect data that come with continuous variables and discrete variables.
The dimension of these features can be high, while the number of patients is relatively small, especially for serious or rare diseases.
For example,
in the Hepatocellular Carcinoma dataset considered in the real data study, there are 165 patients with 22 continuous variables which are mainly from patients’ medical test results, and 118 binary variables which are mainly indicators of related symptoms and medical histories.

•

In integrative analysis, the main objective is to combine different datasets for a comprehensive study. One of the possible possibilities is to integrate continuous-type data with discrete-type data. For example, the
Breast Cancer Gene Expression Profiles data considered in our analysis consists of 489 mRNA Z-Scores (which are measurements of the relative expression
of patients’ genes to the reference population), and a set of indicators of mutation for 173 genes. A strong motivation for combining these two datasets is that together they may provide more information about the mortality risk, the main quantity of interest.

To deal with these mixed variables, a simple strategy is to treat the categorical variables as continuous ones and apply existing classification methods developed for continuous variables. Such a treatment ignores the nature of categorical variables and intuitively incurs loss of information. Here we present a simple example.
Consider a two-class classification problem where there are one continuous variable
X
and one binary categorical variable
U
. Assume that the prior for the class label
L
is balanced, i.e.,
P(L=1)=P(L=2)=0.5
, and that
P(U=0)=P(U=1)=0.5
for both classes. For Class 1, suppose the conditional distribution of
X
given
U
satisfies

X|U=0\sim N(-1,1),\quad X|U=1\sim N(1,1).

Likewise, for Class 2, assume that

X|U=0\sim N(1,1),\quad X|U=1\sim N(-1,1).

If we simply treat
U
as a continuous variable that takes value
0
or
1
, and seek for the best linear classifier, the misclassification rate for the optimal linear classifier
will be easily seen as
\frac{1}{2}\Big[\frac{1}{2}+\Phi(-1)\Big]
, which is more than twice of the optimal Bayes misclassification rate
\Phi(-1)
; see Section
6.1
in the Supplementary for more details.
An immediate message from this simple example is that, in order to obtain a sound classifier, we may have to handle the effects of categorical variables and continuous variable differently, and seek for ways of capturing their interactions.
In our setting, the challenge on the need to handle mixed variables is further exasperated by the high-dimensionality of the problem.

The setup
. Denote
({\bf Z},{\bf U})
as the mixed variable, where
{\bf Z}
is a
p
-dimensional continuous variable and
{\bf U}
is a
d
-dimensional discrete variable. Both
p
and
d
are very large. For simplicity, we shall assume that all variables in
{\bf U}
are binary. Note that
variables with more than two categories can be transformed to be binary by introducing a
set of dummy (binary) variables
[
18
]
.
Write the class label as

L\in\{1,2\}
and denote the probability function of
({\bf Z},{\bf U})
in class
i
as
f_{i}({\bf Z},{\bf U})
for
i=1,2
. The Bayes rule, which is the optimal rule achieving the smallest misclassification rate among all discriminant rules, classifies a data point to the first class if and only if

\pi_{1}f_{1}({\bf Z},{\bf U})>\pi_{2}f_{2}({\bf Z},{\bf U}),

where
P(L=i)=\pi_{i},i=1,2
, is the prior probability of an observation coming from class
i
. As an application of the Bayes rule, consider the case where there are no discrete variables. If we assume that observations from class
i
follow
N(\mu_{i},\Sigma)
, a multivariate Gaussian distribution with class-specific mean
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and a common covariance matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, an application of the Bayes rule gives rise to the familiar linear discriminant analysis (LDA) rule which assigns observation to class
1
if and only if

(1)

\displaystyle(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\left[{\bf Z}-\frac{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}{2}\right]+\log\frac{\pi_{1}}{\pi_{2}}>0.

The Gaussianity assumption of the observations with a common covariance matrix is particularly appealing since the resulting Bayes rule is a simple linear function of the variable with an index
\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})
and an intercept
\log\{{\pi_{1}}/{\pi_{2}}\}
. Indeed, a widely studied and popular approach in high-dimensional classification, as discussed in previous work below, is to assume a sparse index that gives rise to the so-called sparse LDA
[
6
,
9
,
28
,
26
]
.

A semiparametric location model
. The presence of the discrete variables
{\bf U}
clearly rules out the use of a joint Gaussian distribution for
({\bf Z},{\bf U})
. To continue to enjoy the attractive properties of the Gaussian distribution for classification, we employ an idea originated in
[
31
]
by assuming a location model. Specifically, in this model, we treat the discrete random vector
{\bf U}
as a location or cell, and assume that conditional on
{\bf U}
, the continuous random vector
{\bf Z}
follows a location-dependent multivariate normal distribution in that
{\bf Z}|{\bf U}\sim N(\mu_{i}({\bf U}),\Sigma({\bf U})),i=1,2
. The common discrete variable dependent covariance
\Sigma({\bf U})
is inspired by the assumption in LDA. Denote the probability that an observation from class
i
falls in cell
{\bf U}={\bf u}
as
p_{i}({\bf u})
and the posterior probability of
({\bf Z},{\bf U})
from class
i
as
P(i|{\bf Z},{\bf U}),i=1,2
. Under this location model,
the optimal Bayes rule classifies
({\bf Z},{{\bf U}})
into class
1
if

(2)

\displaystyle\log\frac{P(1|{\bf Z},{\bf U})}{P(2|{\bf Z},{\bf U})}=\log\frac{\pi_{1}f_{1}({\bf Z},{\bf U})}{\pi_{2}f_{2}({\bf Z},{\bf U})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\mu_{1}({\bf U})-\mu_{2}({\bf U})]^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}({\bf U})\left[{\bf Z}-\frac{\mu_{1}({{\bf U}})+\mu_{2}({\bf U})}{2}\right]+\log\frac{\pi_{1}p_{1}({\bf U})}{\pi_{2}p_{2}({\bf U})}>0,

and into class 2 otherwise. Denote
\beta({\bf U}):=\Sigma^{-1}({\bf U})[\mu_{1}({\bf U})-\mu_{2}({\bf U})]
, and
\eta({\bf U}):=\log\frac{\pi_{1}p_{1}({\bf U})}{\pi_{2}p_{2}({\bf U})}
. The classifier in (
2
) can be written as

(3)

\displaystyle\beta({\bf U})^{\top}\left[{\bf Z}-\frac{\mu_{1}({{\bf U}})+\mu_{2}({\bf U})}{2}\right]+\eta({\bf U})>0,

which is a functional linear classifier in
{\bf Z}
, with a location-dependent direction
\beta({\bf U})
and a location-dependent intercept
\eta({\bf U})
. In our setting,
\beta({\bf u})
is a
p
-dimensional vector for each fixed
{\bf u}
, and
{\bf u}
itself is also high-dimensional. As a result, the dimensionality of the model is extremely high. In the setting considered in this paper
where
{\bf U}
is a vector of binary variables,

•

For
\beta({\bf U})
, we have

2^{d}

p
-dimensional vectors to estimate;

•

For
\eta({\bf U})
, we have
2^{d}
different values to estimate.

Thus, even after assuming the location model, our estimation problem is much more challenging than that of the LDA in which only a scalar intercept and a
p
-dimensional vector need estimating. Given a relative small sample size, there is no hope that either
\beta({\bf U})
or
\eta({\bf U})
can be reasonably estimated unless some kind of structures are assumed. In this paper, we focus on a general scenario where
\beta({\bf U})
is treated as a function which varies smoothly over the location
{\bf U}
, and
\eta({\bf U})
is modelled by a parametric first order approximation. Some of the properties regarding the location model (e.g., Proposition
2.1
) and our theoretical framework can be adapted to cases where more stringent assumptions are imposed to further simplify the model complexity; see Section
5
for discussion.
Because of the dependence of
\beta({\bf U})
on
U
, we shall refer to our model as the semiparametric location model.

Estimation
. Denote the sample in population 1 as
({\bf U}_{1},{\bf X}_{1}),\ldots,({\bf U}_{n_{1}},{\bf X}_{n_{1}})
and in population 2 as
({\bf V}_{1},{\bf Y}_{1}),\ldots,({\bf V}_{{n_{2}}},{\bf Y}_{n_{2}})
.
We now summarize the main steps for estimating the parameters based on this sample. First, we observe that the estimation of
\beta({\bf U})
and that of
\eta({\bf U})
can be made separate, thanks to a key result provided in Proposition
2.1
.
Note that from Proposition
2.1
we have:

\eta({\bf u})=\log\frac{P(L=1|{\bf U}={\bf u})}{P(L=2|{\bf U}={\bf u})},

i.e.,
\eta({\bf u})
is the logit transformation of the
probability
P(L=1|{\bf U}={\bf u})
.
Consequently,
\eta({\bf U})
can be simply obtained by fitting a logistic regression with
{\bf U}
as covariates,
I(L=1)
as the response, and
\eta({\bf u})
as the discriminant function. Here
I(\cdot)
is the indicator function. Note that
\eta({\bf u})
as a function in
\{0,1\}^{d}
can be written as a polynomial of
{\bf u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}
.
In the context we consider in this paper where
d
(i.e., the dimension of
{\bf U}
) grows to infinity, one popular approach to fit this logistic regression is to focus on the lower order terms in
\eta({\bf U})
. Inspired by the LDA, we focus on their main effects by modelling
\eta({\bf U})
as

(4)

\displaystyle\eta({\bf u})=A_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{i}u_{i}.

We note that in addition to achieve parsimony of modelling, the approximation using low order terms for binary variables is more meaningful than that in the usual linear model.
In particular, (
4
) would hold when the higher order coefficients in the polynomial expansions of

\log\pi_{1}p_{1}({\bf u})
and
\log\pi_{2}p_{2}({\bf u})
are the same.
Further remarks and discussion on (
4
) can be found in the Supplementary, Section
6.2
.

Given this simplification, to estimate
\eta({\bf U})
, we only need to estimate
(A_{0},{\bf A})
with
{\bf A}=(A_{1},...,A_{d})^{\top}
and do it by minimizing the following penalized logistic loss

(5)

\displaystyle(\hat{A}_{0},\hat{{\bf A}})=\underset{A_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}},{\bf A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\argmin}\frac{1}{n}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\left[-(A_{0}+{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf U}_{i})+\log(1+\exp\{A_{0}+{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf U}_{i}\})\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\log(1+\exp\{A_{0}+{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf V}_{i}\})\Big\}+\lambda_{\eta}|{\bf A}|_{1},

where
\mathbb{R}=(-\infty,\infty)
,
|{\bf A}|_{1}
denotes the
\ell_{1}
norm of
{\bf A}
, and
\lambda_{\eta}>0
is a tuning parameter.

The estimation of
\beta({\bf U})=\Sigma^{-1}({\bf U})[\mu_{1}({\bf U})-\mu_{2}({\bf U})]
is more challenging. Our strategy is to estimate
\mu_{i}({\bf U})
and
\Sigma({\bf U})
first via kernel smoothing. Since
{\bf U}
is discrete, we employ Hamming distance to measure the closeness of two vectors with discrete values. Theoretically, we handle the diverging dimension of
{\bf U}
by analyzing normalized versions of the kernel weights, and analyze the interplay between the bandwidth and the dimensionality in the kernel smoothing via a novel analysis on the small ball probability to ensure suitable convergence. Different from classical kernel smoothing theory where the bandwidth only affects the estimation bias and variance, the bandwidth here is crucial for the integrated small ball probability that is the normalizing constant in our case. Interestingly, as indicated by Lemma
6.2
in the Supplementary, the bandwidth should be large enough to guarantee an analogue of Bochner’s Lemma
[
4
]
to hold. As a result,
we establish concentration inequalities for the normalized terms in the kernel estimators, and further show that the misclassification rate of our proposed classifier converges to the optimal Bayes risk under appropriate assumptions. This analysis can be of independent interest.

Denote the estimate of
\mu_{1}({\bf u}),\mu_{2}({\bf u})
and
\Sigma({\bf u})
as
\hat{\mu}_{1}({\bf u})
,
\hat{\mu}_{2}({\bf u})
and
\hat{\Sigma}({\bf u})
respectively.
By noting that given
{\bf u}
,
\beta({\bf u})
is the minimizer of the convex loss function
R(\beta({\bf u})):=\beta({\bf u})^{T}\Sigma({\bf u})\beta({\bf u})-2\beta({\bf u})^{T}[\mu_{1}({\bf u})-\mu_{2}({\bf u})]
,
we estimate
\beta({\bf u})
by minimizing the following penalized loss function

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}({\bf u}):=\argmin_{b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}~~b^{T}\hat{\Sigma}({\bf u})b-2b^{T}(\hat{\mu}_{1}({\bf u})-\hat{\mu}_{2}({\bf u}))+\lambda_{\beta}\left|b\right|_{1},

at each
{\bf u}
, where
\lambda_{\beta}>0
is a tuning parameter, and
\hat{\mu}_{1},\hat{\mu}_{2}
and
\hat{\Sigma}
are kernel estimators of
{\mu}_{1},{\mu}_{2}
and
{\Sigma}
discussed above and defined in Section
2.2
.
Because
\beta({\bf U})
and
\eta({\bf U})
can be estimated separately (c.f. Proposition
2.1
below),
\lambda_{\beta}
in (
5
) can be independently chosen without referencing to the estimation of
\eta({\bf u})
. In particular, Proposition
2.1
implies that the choice of
\lambda_{\beta}
can be determined by minimizing the misclassification rate when the intercept
\eta({\bf U})
is simply set to zero.

Our contributions
. Our methodological contribution is to propose a new semiparametric location model for classifying high-dimensional mixed variables in a unified framework. We show that the classification direction
\beta({\bf u})
and the intercept
\eta({\bf u})
can be estimated separately, and overcome the curse of dimensionality of estimating these two high-dimensional functions by penalized likelihood. Crucially for estimating
\beta({\bf u})
, we are the first to leverage traditional kernel smoothing with modern development on small ball probability under high dimensionality which can be of independent interest.
More specifically, although asymptotic properties of kernel smoothing estimators for regression functions of infinite order have been rigorously derived in

[
12
]
, the case considered in this paper is very different and more challenging in that high dimensionality appears in both the regression function and the binary covariates. In particular, we have
identified that unlike classical kernel smoothing where the bandwidth is usually chosen to balance the bias and variance of a kernel smoothing estimator, the bandwidth here must be chosen to be large enough to ensure an analogue of Bochner’s Lemma to hold for high dimensional discrete variables.
Built on this, we have further established concentration inequalities for the kernel smoothing estimators, which are essential for obtaining consistency under high dimensionality. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to establish a theoretical framework to evaluate the concentration behavior of kernel smoothing estimators for high dimensional regression functions with high dimensional independent covariates.
Lastly, we have integrated all the estimation errors and derived the asymptotic misclassification rate of the proposed semiparametric classifier, from which one gains useful insights on how the estimation errors of the classification direction and the intercept affect the classification accuracy.

Prior work
.
High-dimensional datasets containing both discrete and continuous variables are frequently encountered in the big data era.
For discriminant analysis, while there are early works in investigating how to model mixed variables under the traditional fixed dimensional setting, recent research has focused almost exclusively on datasets with high dimensional continuous variables. Methodologies that can effectively take into account both the high-dimensionality and the mixing nature of the datasets are scarce.

When the dimensionality is fixed, discriminant analysis with discrete and continuous data has been well studied. In addition to the simple strategy by transforming the two different types of variables into one type, as mentioned previously, another approach is to establish different discriminant rules for different types of variables and combine them to obtain a final classifier; see for examples
[
7
]
and
[

]
.
[

]
first proposed linear discriminant rules based on the location model (
2
).
Later, more comprehensive discriminant rules were proposed based on logistic discrimination, kernel estimation and the location model; see for example
[
1
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
17
]
and the references therein.
Variable selection for location model based discriminant rules and further extension to quadratic rules and a nonparametric smoothing version can be found in
[
8
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
,
[

]
,
[

and

. Clearly, these approaches developed under fixed dimensionality are not applicable any more to the modern data era where dimensions of the data are high. In particular, in terms of theoretical analysis, these discriminant rules are either algorithmic without theoretical justification, or statistically justified under the fixed dimensional assumption.

When the dimensionality is high with discrete variables absent, there is a large growing literature devoted to the study of classification.
[
3
]
first showed that using estimates developed under the fixed-dimensionality scenario for high-dimensional problems gives a classifier equivalent to random guessing in the worst case scenario. For the LDA in (

proposed to shrink the entries of their empirical estimates. By assuming that

further studied a multiclass extension of the LDA.
[

[

Despite these new developments in high dimensional LDA for model (

Semiparametric Location Model: Estimation

In our semiparametric location classifier in (
3
),
\eta({\bf u})
is a linear function of
{\bf u}
and
\beta({\bf u})
is a function of the location
{\bf u}
.
In this section we will first look at the classification problem from the perspective of minimizing the expected misclassification rate, from which it is found that although
\beta({\bf u})
and
\eta({\bf u})
both appear in the Bayes rule (
3
),
they can be independently estimated. We then focus on discussing the estimation of
\beta({\bf u})
, as the problem of estimating
\eta({\bf u})
has been discussed in the Introduction.

2.1
Separability of
\beta({\bf u})
and
\eta({\bf u})

For a given location
{\bf U}={\bf u}
, consider a general discriminant rule

(7)

\displaystyle D({\bf b}({\bf u}),b_{0}({\bf u})):={\bf b}({\bf u})^{\top}[{\bf Z}-\frac{\mu_{1}({\bf u})+\mu_{2}({\bf u})}{2}]+b_{0}({\bf u}),

which classifies a new observation
({\bf Z},{\bf u})
to class 1 if and only if
D({\bf b}({\bf u}),b_{0}({\bf u}))>0
.
Under the location model, the misclassification rate of the classifier
D({\bf b}({\bf u}),b_{0}({\bf u}))
over all locations can be seen as

(8)

\displaystyle R(D({\bf b}({\bf u}),{b}_{0}({\bf u}))=\sum_{{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}[\pi_{1}p_{1}({\bf u})P_{\bf u}\left(2|1\right)+\pi_{2}p_{2}({\bf u})P_{\bf u}\left(1|2\right)],

where
P_{\bf u}(i|j)
is the conditional misclassification probability of classifying
{\bf Z}
from class
i
to class
j
given the location
{\bf u}
. Let
\Phi(\cdot)
be the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution. Under the Gaussianity assumption we have

(9)

P_{\bf u}(i|j)=\Phi\left(\frac{b_{0}({\bf u})-{\bf b}({\bf u})^{\top}[\mu_{i}({\bf u})-\mu_{j}({\bf u})]/2}{\sqrt{{\bf b}({\bf u})^{\top}\Sigma({\bf u}){\bf b}({\bf u})}}\right).

The purpose of classification is to seek a classification direction
{\bf b}({\bf u})
and the corresponding intercept
b_{0}({\bf u})
such that the expected misclassification rate in (
8
) is minimized. Although the estimation of these two arguments are interrelated, we show in the following proposition that their estimation can be separated.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume the location model hold, and let
\beta({\bf u})
and
\eta({\bf u})
be the optimal classification direction and intercept in the Bayes classifier (
3
), respectively. Consider
D(b({\bf U}),0)
, a special case of the general discriminant rule (
7
) with a zero intercept:
b_{0}({\bf u})=0
. We have

\displaystyle\beta({\bf u})=\Sigma^{-1}({\bf u})[\mu_{1}({\bf u})-\mu_{2}({\bf u})]=\argmin_{{\bf b}({\bf u})\in{\cal D}}~~E\{R({\bf b}({\bf u}),0)\},

where
{\cal D}
is the set of all functions from
\{0,1\}^{d}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
, and
E
is the expectation. On the other hand, for the optimal intercept
\eta({\bf u})
we have:

\eta({\bf u})=\log\frac{P(L=1|{\bf U}={\bf u})}{P(L=2|{\bf U}={\bf u})}
.

This proposition ensures that the estimation
\beta({\bf u})
and
\eta(u)
can be conducted separately. In particular, it indicates that the estimation of
\beta({\bf u})
can be conducted by simply setting
\eta({\bf u})=0
.

2.2
Estimation of
\beta({\bf u})

To estimate
\beta({\bf u})
at any cell
{\bf u}
, we will resort to kernel smoothing. Recall that our sample consists of
({\bf U}_{1},{\bf X}_{1}),\ldots,({\bf U}_{n_{1}},{\bf X}_{n_{1}})
from population 1 and
({\bf V}_{1},{\bf Y}_{1}),\ldots,({\bf V}_{{n_{2}}},{\bf Y}_{n_{2}})
from population 2.
For any
{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, define the normalized Hamming distance between
{\bf u}_{1}
and
{\bf u}_{2}
as

<{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2}>=\frac{|{\bf u}_{1}-{\bf u}_{2}|_{1}}{{d}},

where
|\cdot|_{1}
is the
\ell_{1}
norm.
Our estimation of the mean and covariance matrix is based on the following Nadaraya-Watson type local smoothing.
For given bandwidths
h_{x}
and
h_{y}
, we estimate
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
as

\hat{\mu}_{1}({\bf u})=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\frac{\exp\{-(dh_{x})^{-1}|{\bf U}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}{\bf X}_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\exp\{-(dh_{x})^{-1}|{\bf U}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}},~~\hat{\mu}_{2}({\bf u})=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\frac{\exp\{-(dh_{y})^{-1}|{\bf V}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}{\bf Y}_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\exp\{-(dh_{y})^{-1}|{\bf V}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}}.

Based on these, we shall establish the theory for the kernel smoothing estimators.
Note that
\Sigma({\bf u})=E[{\bf X}({\bf u}){\bf X}({\bf u})^{\top}]-E{\bf X}({\bf u})(E{\bf X}({\bf u}))^{\top}=E[{\bf Y}({\bf u}){\bf Y}({\bf u})^{\top}]-E{\bf Y}({\bf u})(E{\bf Y}({\bf u}))^{\top}
. We estimate
\Sigma({\bf u})
as

\hat{\Sigma}({\bf u})=\frac{n_{1}}{n}\hat{\Sigma}_{1}({\bf u})+\frac{n_{2}}{n}\hat{\Sigma}_{2}({\bf u}),

where

\hat{\Sigma}_{1}({\bf u})=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\exp\{-(dh_{xx})^{-1}|{\bf U}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}{\bf X}_{j}{\bf X}_{j}^{T}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\exp\{-(dh_{xx})^{-1}|{\bf U}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}}-\hat{\mu}_{1}({\bf u})\hat{\mu}_{1}({\bf u})^{\top},

\hat{\Sigma}_{2}({\bf u})=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\exp\{-(dh_{yy})^{-1}|{\bf V}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}{\bf Y}_{j}{\bf Y}_{j}^{T}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\exp\{-(dh_{yy})^{-1}|{\bf V}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}}-\hat{\mu}_{2}({\bf u})\hat{\mu}_{2}({\bf u})^{\top},

with the bandwidth parameters
h_{xx}
and
h_{yy}
controlling the smoothness of the estimators. Then
\beta({\bf u})
is estimated via the minimization problem in (
6
).

Theoretical Results

The following notations will be used. For a
k\times p
matrix
{\bf M}=(M_{ij})_{k\times p}
we denote the vector

3.1
Concentration inequalities

To study the property of the proposed estimators, we make the following assumptions.

(C1).

There exists a constant
B\in(0,0.5]
such that
B\leq d^{-1}EN_{\bf u}\leq 1-B
holds for any
{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
.
There exist constants
C>0
and
0\leq\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
such that for any
{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
,
E(\sum_{i=1}^{d}W_{\bf u}^{(i)})^{k}\leq Ck!d^{1+\alpha(k-2)}
for any
k\geq 2
, where
{\bf W}_{\bf u}=(W^{(1)}_{\bf u},\ldots,W_{\bf u}^{(d)})^{\top}=\Delta_{\bf u}-E\Delta_{\bf u}
.

(C2).

Write
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
. We assume that
n_{1}\asymp n_{2}
,
\frac{\log(p+d)}{n}+\frac{\log(p+d)}{d\log^{2}n}\rightarrow 0
, and for

h=h_{x},h_{y},h_{xx}
or
h_{yy}
, there exists a
positive constant
C>0
such that
\frac{\log(d+n)}{h^{2}d}\leq C
.

(C3).

For any
{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
and
t>0
, denote

\kappa_{\bf u}(t):=\frac{E[m({\bf U})-m({\bf u})]\exp\{-(dt)^{-1}N_{\bf u}\}}{E\exp\{-(dt)^{-1}N_{\bf u}\}},

and let
\kappa(t)=\sup_{{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\kappa_{\bf u}(t)
.
We assume that
\kappa(t)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow 0
and
dt\rightarrow\infty
.

(C4).

There exists a positive constant
M
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{1\leq i\leq p,{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}EX^{4}_{1i}({\bf u})\leq M<\infty,\quad\sup_{1\leq i\leq p,{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}EY^{4}_{1i}({\bf u})\leq M<\infty

There exists a constant
M_{\Sigma}>1
such that

M_{\Sigma}^{-1}\leq\inf_{{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\lambda_{1}(\Sigma({\bf u}))\leq\sup_{{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\lambda_{p}(\Sigma({\bf u}))\leq M_{\Sigma},

where
\lambda_{1}(\Sigma({\bf u}))
and
\lambda_{p}(\Sigma({\bf u}))
are the smallest and largest eigenvalues of
\Sigma({\bf u})
, respectively.

Condition (C1) is a regularization condition on the discrete variable
{\bf U}
, and is generally true when the success probability of each element in
{\bf U}
is bounded away from zero and one.
Condition (C2) specifies the order of the bandwidth
h
. Unlike classical results in kernel smoothing where
h
is chosen to balance the bias and variance, our
h
here has to be large enough to ensure the small ball probability
E\exp\{-(hd)^{-1}|{\bf U}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}
is large enough. Specifically,
as a form of Bochner’s Lemma, a commonly applied result in classical kernel smoothing estimation is that
\frac{1}{h}EK\left(\frac{|{\bf U}-{\bf u}|_{1}}{dh}\right)
would tend to the density function of
{\bf u}
for a properly chosen kernel function under some regular conditions
[
4
]
. However, such a conclusion fails in our case. More specifically, suppose we use a continuous density to approximate the discrete probability mass function of
{\bf u}
as
d\rightarrow\infty
. With some abuse of notations, let
p_{d}({\bf u})
be the point mass probability of
{\bf u}
. The approximated density at location
{\bf u}
, following traditional arguments, is given as
f({\bf u})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum_{{\bf u}_{\epsilon}:d^{-1}|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}-{\bf u}|_{1}\leq\epsilon}p_{d}({\bf u}_{\epsilon})
. Similar to the small ball probability issue in the
[
12
]
, such a density relies on the choice of
\epsilon
and hence is not well defined. On the one hand, as
d
grows, the point mass function at
{\bf u}
converges to zero in an exponential rate. Such a point mass probability cannot be well estimated unless the sample size also grows exponentially in
d
. To tackle this issue, other than evaluating the denominator and numerator in the Nadaraya-Watson type estimators directly,
we establish concentration inequalities for their normalized versions such as
\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\frac{\exp\{-(dh_{x})^{-1}|{\bf U}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}}{E\exp\{-(dh_{x})^{-1}|{\bf U}_{1}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}}
and
\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\frac{\exp\{-(dh_{x})^{-1}|{\bf U}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}X_{ji}}{E\exp\{-(dh_{x})^{-1}|{\bf U}_{1}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}}
.
Similar to equation (6) of
[
12
]
, the normalizing coefficient
E\exp\{-(hd)^{-1}|{\bf U}-{\bf u}|_{1}\}
can be viewed as an integrated small ball probability.
Condition (C3) quantifies the smoothness of
m({\bf u})
. For better understanding, we provide some examples where (C3) is satisfied.

Example 1.

Smoothness on the expected different function on the contour

Note that for any integer
s
,
{\cal C}_{{\bf u},s}:=\{{\bf U}:N_{{\bf u}}=s\}
defines a contour with radius
s
from the center
{\bf u}
.
Let
G_{{\bf u}}(s):=E[m({\bf U})-m({\bf u})|N_{{\bf u}}=s]
be the expected difference of
m(\cdot)
over all the
{\bf U}
’s on the contour
{\cal C}_{{\bf u},s}
. (C3) is satisfied if
G_{{\bf u}}(s)
is smooth in the sense that
\frac{E[G_{{\bf u}}(N_{{\bf u}})\exp\{-(dt)^{-1}N_{\bf u}\}]}{E\exp\{-(dt)^{-1}N_{\bf u}\}}=\kappa_{{\bf u}}(t)\rightarrow 0
.
As an example, suppose for any
{\bf u}_{i}\in{\cal C}_{{\bf u},1}
, we have
m({\bf u}_{i})=m({\bf u})+s_{{\bf u},{\bf u}_{i}}
, where
s_{{\bf u},{\bf u}_{i}}
is the a “signal” generated from
s_{{\bf u},{\bf u}_{i}}=I\{\varepsilon_{\bf u}=0\}N(0,\sigma_{d}^{2})
for some constant
\tau>0
and variance
\sigma_{d}^{2}>0
, and some independent Bernoulli random innovation
\varepsilon_{\bf u}
such that
P(\varepsilon_{\bf u}=0)=P(1-\varepsilon_{\bf u}=0)=\pi_{\bf u}
. The variance
\sigma_{d}^{2}
captures the level of fluctuation of
m({\bf u})
near the neighborhood locations, and the point mass probability
\pi_{\bf u}
controls the proportion of neighbors that take the same value as
m({\bf u})
. Under this setting, we have, when
|{\bf u}_{i}-{\bf u}|_{1}=s
,
m({\bf u}_{i})=m({\bf u})+O_{p}(\sqrt{s\pi_{\bf u}}\sigma_{d})
. Condition (C3) is satisfied when
\sqrt{\pi_{\bf u}}\sigma_{d}\rightarrow 0
fast enough such that
G_{{\bf u}}(s)=o(1)
for all
s=1,\ldots,d
. This can be true when, for example,
\sqrt{\pi_{\bf u}}\sigma_{d}=O(d^{-1/2-\tau})
for some constant
\tau>0
. The term
\sqrt{\pi_{\bf u}}\sigma_{d}
in this case captures the order of the difference between
m({\bf u})
and the expectation
E[m({\bf U})|{\bf U}\in{\cal C}_{{\bf u},1}]
over the contour
{\cal C}_{{\bf u},1}
.

Example 2.

Lipschitz with exponential order

For any
{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, suppose there exists a constant
0\leq c\leq 1

such that,

|m({\bf u}_{1})-m({\bf u})|\leq\kappa_{1}\exp\left\{\frac{|{\bf u}_{1}-{\bf u}|_{1}^{c}-EN_{\bf u}^{c}}{(d\log d)^{\frac{c}{2}}}\right\},

for some
\kappa_{1}\rightarrow 0
.
Note that by Jensen inequality we have for any
c\in[0,1]
,
EN_{\bf u}^{c}\leq(EN_{\bf u})^{c}
. By Kimball’s inequality, Lemma
6.1
, Conditions (C1) and (C4), there exists a large enough constant
C_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{E[m({\bf U})-m({\bf u})]\exp\{-(dt)^{-1}N_{\bf u}\}}{E\exp\{-(dt)^{-1}N_{\bf u}\}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}E\exp\left\{\frac{N_{\bf u}^{c}-EN_{\bf u}^{c}}{(d\log d)^{\frac{c}{2}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}E\exp\left\{\frac{EN_{\bf u}^{c}}{(d\log d)^{\frac{c}{2}}}\cdot\left(\frac{N_{\bf u}^{c}}{EN_{\bf u}^{r}}-1\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\kappa_{1}\exp\left\{C_{1}\left(\frac{d}{\log d}\right)^{\frac{c}{2}}\cdot\left(\frac{\log d}{d}\right)^{\frac{c}{2}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(\kappa_{1}).

Example 3.

Centered Lipschitz with exponential order

Existing literature for estimating the distribution of high dimensional discrete variables sometimes models the probability mass as a function of the centered variable instead
[
10
]
. We hence consider the following Lipschitz condition centered at the mean of
N_{\bf u}
:
For any
{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, there exist constants
c\geq 0
,
C>0
such that,

|m({\bf u}_{1})-m({\bf u})|\leq C\kappa_{1}\exp\left\{\frac{(|{\bf u}_{1}-{\bf u}|_{1}-EN_{\bf u})^{c}}{(d\log d)^{\frac{c}{2}}}\right\},

for some
\kappa_{1}\rightarrow 0
.
By Kimball’s inequality and Lemma
6.1
we have,

\displaystyle\frac{E[m({\bf U})-m({\bf u})]\exp\{-(dt)^{-1}N_{\bf u}\}}{E\exp\{-(dt)^{-1}N_{\bf u}\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{E[m({\bf U})-m({\bf u})]\exp\{-(dt)^{-1}(N_{\bf u}-EN_{\bf u})\}}{E\exp\{-(dt)^{-1}(N_{\bf u}-EN_{\bf u})\}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\kappa_{1}E\exp\left\{\frac{(N_{\bf u}-EN_{\bf u})^{r}}{(d\log d)^{\frac{r}{2}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(\kappa_{1}).

Next we establish concentration inequalities for the weighted estimators of the mean and covariance matrix functions.
Note that in practice, the sample size in either the training dataset or the testing dataset can be much smaller than the total locations
2^{d}
.
For simplicity, we shall assume that the region of interest is the ball centered at
{\bf v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
with radius
r
:
B_{\bf v}(r):=\{{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}:|{\bf u}-{\bf v}|_{1}\leq r\}
. Let
\kappa(\cdot)
be defined as in Condition (C3).
The following theorems establish uniform consistency for any
{\bf u}
in the ball
B_{\bf v}(r)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under Conditions (C1)-(C4), when
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are large enough, there exist constants
C_{1}>0
,
C_{2}>0
such that for any
\epsilon_{n}>\kappa(h)
,

\displaystyle P\left(\underset{1\leq i\leq p,{\bf u}\in B_{\bf v}(r)}{\sup}\left|\hat{\mu}_{1i}({\bf u})-\mu_{1{i}}({\bf u})\right|>\epsilon_{n}\right)\leq C_{1}pd^{r}\exp\left\{-C_{2}n_{1}(\epsilon_{n}-\kappa(h))^{2}\right\},

and

\displaystyle P\left(\underset{1\leq i\leq p,{\bf u}\in B_{\bf v}(r)}{\sup}\left|\hat{\mu}_{2i}({\bf u})-\mu_{2{i}}({\bf u})\right|>\epsilon_{n}\right)\leq C_{1}pd^{r}\exp\left\{-C_{2}n_{2}(\epsilon_{n}-\kappa(h))^{2}\right\}.

Similarly, we can show the following.

Theorem 3.2
.

Under Conditions (C1)-(C4), when
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are large enough, there exist constant
C_{1}>0
,
C_{2}>0
such that for any
\epsilon_{n}>\kappa(h)
,

\displaystyle P\left(\underset{{\bf u}\in B_{\bf v}(r)}{\sup}\left\|\hat{\Sigma}({\bf u})-\Sigma({\bf u})\right\|_{\infty}>\epsilon_{n}\right)\leq C_{1}p^{2}d^{r}\exp\left\{-C_{2}n(\epsilon_{n}-\kappa(h))^{2}\right\}.

Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
above provide concentration results for
\hat{\mu}_{1}({\bf u})
and
\hat{\mu}_{2}({\bf u})
. In particular, the right hand side of the concentration inequalities will tend to 0 when we set
\epsilon_{n}=C\sqrt{\frac{\log(p+d)}{n}}+\kappa(h)
for some large enough constant
C>0
. The rate
\sqrt{\frac{\log(p+d)}{n}}
echoes a classical rate that quantifies its dependence on the dimension and the sample size, while the term
\kappa(h)
is a bias caused by local smoothing. It is generally hard to evaluate
\kappa(h)
unless some strong structural assumptions are imposed for
m({\bf u})
. Under the classical context, the bandwidth
h
is usually chosen to obtain a trade-off between the bias and the variance, and hence it is theoretically crucial to know the rate of the bias. However, under the setting that
m({\bf u})
is high dimensional, the
h
that provides the best bias-variance trade-off may not necessarily provide any guarantee for the uniform convergence of the estimator, which is an essential requirement for establishing consistency under high dimensionality. Alternatively, we suggest setting
h
to be large enough (as in Condition (C3)) to ensure an analogue of Bochner’s Lemma (i.e., Lemma
6.2
in the Supplementary) to hold, and as a result, concentration results in the above two theorems can be appropriately established. Practically, although it is common to select the bandwidth by minimizing the mean integrated squared error via cross validation, for classification with high dimensional mixed variables, we have found that it works better to choose
h
to minimize the misclassification rate, as described in Section 4.

\hat{\beta}({\bf u})

3.2.1
\ell_{1}
-penalized estimation

Before we introduce the main results for the estimation of
\beta({\bf u})
, we
study the theoretical properties of the solution of the following generic penalized quadratic loss. These results will be used to prove the consistency of
\hat{\beta}({\bf u})
later. For a given
{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}

\hat{{\bf\Omega}}({\bf u})

\hat{{\bf a}}
be consistent estimators of a

p

For a given tuning parameter

{\bf b}^{*}({\bf u}):={\bf\Omega}({\bf u})^{-1}{\bf a}({\bf u})

(10)

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf b}}({\bf u})=\argmin_{{\bf b}\in R^{p}}\frac{1}{2}{\bf b}^{\top}\hat{{\bf\Omega}}({\bf u}){\bf b}-\hat{{\bf a}}^{\top}({\bf u}){\bf b}+\lambda|{\bf b}|_{1}.

Denote the support of

in terms of the estimation accuracy of
\hat{{\bf a}}({\bf u})
and
\hat{{\bf\Omega}}({\bf u})
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Denote
\epsilon_{\bf u}=\|\hat{{\bf a}}({\bf u})-{\bf a}({\bf u})\|_{\infty}+\|[\hat{{\bf\Omega}}({\bf u})-{\bf\Omega}({\bf u})]{\bf b}^{*}({\bf u})\|_{\infty}
, and
e_{\bf u}=\|{\bf b}^{*}({\bf u})\|_{0}\|{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})^{-1}\|_{L}

\|\hat{{\bf\Omega}}({\bf u})-{\bf\Omega}({\bf u})\|_{\infty}

Assume the following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bf u}\in B_{\bf v}(r)}\{\|{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u}){\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})^{-1}\|_{L}+e_{\bf u}\}<1,

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bf u}\in B_{\bf v}(r)}2[1-\|{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u}){\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})^{-1}\|_{L}-2e_{\bf u}]^{-1}\epsilon_{\bf u}<\lambda.

We have, for any

(i)

\hat{\bf b}_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}({\bf u})={\bf 0}
;

(ii)

\|\hat{{\bf b}}({\bf u})-{\bf b}^{*}({\bf u})\|_{\infty}\leq 2(1-e_{\bf u})^{-1}\|{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})^{-1}\|_{L}\lambda.

Proposition
3.3
provides a general result for the oracle property of an estimator defined by minimizing the estimated quadratic loss (
10

3.3

In particular, we do not impose any assumption on the signal strength of the nonzero parameters.
Conditions in the above proposition rely on the magnitude of

3.4
Misclassification rate

Given
{\bf U}={\bf u}
, denote
D({\bf Z};{\bf u})=\beta({\bf u})^{\top}\Big[{\bf Z}-\frac{\mu_{1}({\bf u})+\mu_{2}({\bf u})}{2}\Big]+\eta({\bf u})
, and correspondingly denote
\hat{D}({\bf Z};{\bf u})=\hat{\beta}({\bf u})^{\top}\left[{\bf Z}-\frac{\hat{\mu}_{1}({\bf u})+\hat{\mu}_{2}({\bf u})}{2}\right]+\hat{\eta}({\bf u})
. The optimal Bayes’ risk is given as

R_{{\bf u}}=\pi_{1}R_{{\bf u}}(2|1)+\pi_{2}R_{{\bf u}}(1|2),

where
R_{{\bf u}}(2|1)=P({D}({\bf Z};{\bf u})\leq 0|{\bf Z}\in N(\mu_{1}({\bf u}),\Sigma({\bf u}))
and
R_{{\bf u}}(1|2)=P({D}({\bf Z};{\bf u})>0|{\bf Z}\in N(\mu_{2}({\bf u}),\Sigma({\bf u}))
. Correspondingly, the misclassification rate of our proposed method is

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{{\bf u}}=\pi_{1}\hat{R}_{{\bf u}}(2|1)+\pi_{2}\hat{R}_{{\bf u}}(1|2),

where
\hat{R}_{{\bf u}}(2|1)=P(\hat{D}({\bf Z};{\bf u})\leq 0|{\bf Z}\in N(\mu_{1}({\bf u}),\Sigma({\bf u}))
and
\hat{R}_{{\bf u}}(1|2)=P(\hat{D}({\bf Z},{\bf u})>0|{\bf Z}\in N(\mu_{2}({\bf u}),\Sigma({\bf u}))
.
Note that when
{\bf Z}\in N(\mu_{2}({\bf u}),\Sigma({\bf u}))
, we have,

D({\bf Z};{\bf u})\sim N\left({\beta({\bf u})^{\top}[\mu_{2}({\bf u})-\mu_{1}({\bf u})]}/{2}+\eta({\bf u}),{\beta({\bf u})^{\top}[\mu_{1}({\bf u})-\mu_{2}({\bf u})]}\right).

Denote
\Delta({\bf u}):=\beta({\bf u})^{\top}[\mu_{1}({\bf u})-\mu_{2}({\bf u})]=[\mu_{1}({\bf u})-\mu_{2}({\bf u})]^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}({\bf u})[\mu_{1}({\bf u})-\mu_{2}({\bf u})]
. We assume:

(C7).

\sup_{{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\Delta({\bf u})\geq\delta
for some constant
\delta>0
.

We remark that
\Delta({\bf u})
captures how far are the (normalized) centers of the two classes away from each other. Condition (C7) ensures that the center of the two class are separable. The following theorem indicates that the estimated semiparametric classification rule
I\{\hat{D}({\bf Z};{\bf u})>0\}
is asymptotically optimal.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let Conditions (C1)-(C7) hold. For a given
{\bf u}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
of interest, let
s=\underset{{\bf u}\in B_{\bf v}(r)}{\sup}\|\beta({\bf u})\|_{0}
. We have:

(11)

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf u}\in B_{\bf v}(r)}{\sup}|\hat{R}_{{\bf u}}-{R}_{{\bf u}}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{p}\left(\Big(s+\sup_{{\bf u}\in B_{\bf v}(r)}|\beta({\bf u})|_{1}\Big)M_{r}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(p+d)}{n}}+\kappa(h)\right)+M^{2}e^{M}\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}}\right).

There are two terms on the right hand side of (
11
). The first term is similar to those for other high dimensional LDA classifiers with continuous data only
[
6
,
13
]
, and is mainly introduced by the estimation of
\beta({\bf u})
. The second term is introduced by the estimation of
\eta({\bf u})
.
Although both
p
and
d
are allowed to grow exponentially in
n
, the sparsity requirement for the discrete variables seems to be more restricted as we require
M^{2}e^{M}
to be
o\big(\sqrt{\frac{n}{\log d}}\big)
.

Numerical Studies

4.1
Selection of tuning parameters

In what follows we will introduce the use of cross validation for determining the bandwidths and the tuning parameters
\lambda_{\beta}
and
\lambda_{\eta}
.

4.1.1
Selection of the bandwidth and
\lambda_{\beta}
for the estimation of
\beta({\bf u})

One approach to choose the bandwidths
h_{x},h_{y},h_{xx}
and
h_{yy}
is to use leave-one-out cross validation as is usually done in kernel smoothing. This bandwidth selection approach does not recognize the constraint in our theory that the bandwidths should be large enough (see Condition C2) to guarantee an analogue of Bochner’s Lemma (i.e., Lemma
6.2
) to hold. As an alternative, we propose to select the bandwidths and the tuning parameter
\lambda_{\beta}
together by minimizing classification error.

For simplicity, we shall use a common bandwidth parameter
h
for all the bandwidths
h_{x},h_{y},h_{xx}
and
h_{yy}
.
Suppose we reformulate the weights by
introducing
\theta=\frac{\exp\{-(dh)^{-1}\}}{1+\exp\{-(dh)^{-1}\}}
. That is, by writing
\exp\{-{(dh)^{-1}}{t}\}=\left(\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}\right)^{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{1}({\bf u})=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\frac{(\frac{\theta}{1-\theta})^{|{\bf U}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}}{\bf X}_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}(\frac{\theta}{1-\theta})^{|{\bf U}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}}},\qquad\hat{\mu}_{2}({\bf u})=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\frac{(\frac{\theta}{1-\theta})^{|{\bf V}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}}{\bf Y}_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}(\frac{\theta}{1-\theta})^{|{\bf V}_{j}-{\bf u}|_{1}}}.

Clearly
\theta\in[0,0.5]
.
When
\theta\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}
,
\hat{\mu}_{1i}({\bf u})
and
\hat{\mu}_{2i}({\bf u})
reduce to the means of all the samples, and when
\theta\rightarrow 0
,
\hat{\mu}_{1i}({\bf u})
and
\hat{\mu}_{2i}({\bf u})
reduce to the sample means in the cells only. Coincidentally, under this formulation, we found that subject to a normalizing term
(1-\theta_{x})^{d}
, the denominator is the same as the smoothing estimator for the distribution of a high dimensional and binary random vector in
[
1
]
and
[
10
]
. We shall be adopting this new formulation for tuning selection, as the parameter
\theta
is now bounded, which is practically more convenient for tuning.

Note that Proposition
2.1
implies that the estimation of
\beta({\bf u})

can be independently conducted by minimizes the expected misclassification rate over the class of zero-intercept classifiers. More specifically, given
(\theta,\lambda_{\beta})
, let
\hat{\beta}_{-i}({\bf U}_{i})
and
\hat{\beta}_{-i}({\bf V}_{i})
be the estimators obtained using (
6
) by leaving
({\bf X}_{i},{\bf U}_{i})
and
({\bf Y}_{i},{\bf V}_{i})
out, respectively. We choose
(\theta,\lambda_{\beta})
such that the following misclassification rate is minimized:

\displaystyle R_{0}(\lambda_{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}I\left\{\hat{\beta}_{-i}({\bf U}_{i})^{\top}\left({\bf X}_{i}-\frac{\hat{\mu}_{1,-i}({\bf U}_{i})+\hat{\mu}_{2}({\bf U}_{i})}{2}\right)\leq 0\right\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}I\left\{\hat{\beta}_{-j}({\bf V}_{j})^{\top}\left({\bf Y}_{j}-\frac{\hat{\mu}_{1}({\bf V}_{j})+\hat{\mu}_{2,-j}({\bf V}_{j})}{2}\right)\geq 0\right\}.

4.1.2
Selection of
\lambda_{\eta}
for the estimation of
\eta({\bf u})

Given the chosen
(\theta,\lambda_{\beta})
, we denote

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}:=\hat{\beta}_{-i}({\bf U}_{i})^{\top}\left({\bf X}_{i}-\frac{\hat{\mu}_{1,-i}({\bf U}_{i})+\hat{\mu}_{2}({\bf U}_{i})}{2}\right),\quad\zeta_{n_{1}+j}=\hat{\beta}_{-j}({\bf V}_{j})^{\top}\left({\bf Y}_{j}-\frac{\hat{\mu}_{1}({\bf V}_{j})+\hat{\mu}_{2,-j}({\bf V}_{j})}{2}\right)

for
i=1,\ldots,n_{1}
and
j=1,\ldots,n_{2}
. Note that these values have been computed when determining
\lambda_{\beta}
and hence it requires no extra computation burden. Let
(\hat{A}_{0,-i},\hat{{\bf A}}_{-i})
and
(\hat{A}_{0,-(n_{1}+j)},\hat{{\bf A}}_{-(n_{1}+j)})
be the estimator based on (
5
) by leaving
{\bf U}_{i}
and
{\bf V}_{j}
out, respectively. We then choose
\lambda_{\eta}
by minimizing the following misclassification rate:

\displaystyle R(\lambda_{\eta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}I\left\{\zeta_{i}+\hat{A}_{0,-i}+{\bf U}_{i}^{\top}\hat{{\bf A}}_{-i}\leq 0\right\}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}I\left\{\zeta_{n_{1}+j}+\hat{A}_{0,-(n_{1}+j)}+{\bf V}_{j}^{\top}\hat{{\bf A}}_{-(n_{1}+j)}\geq 0\right\}.

4.2
Alternative classifiers

For any
{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, we use
d({\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2})
to denote the distance between
{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2}
, where
d(\cdot,\cdot):\{0,1\}^{d}\times\{0,1\}^{d}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
is a distance metric. While the normalized Hamming distance:
d({\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2})=\frac{|{\bf u}_{1}-{\bf u}_{2}|_{1}}{{d}}
has been adopted (c.f. Section
2.2
) in our estimation of the semiparametric location model,
we can also assume that there exists an embedding of the categorical variables in
{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e,
\mathcal{E}(\cdot):\{0,1\}^{d}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
, such that
d({\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2})=|\mathcal{E}({\bf u}_{1})-\mathcal{E}({\bf u}_{2})|
. In particular, in this numerical study, we will consider the following two cases:

•

Linear Embedding:
\mathcal{E}({\bf u})=\eta({\bf u})=A_{0}+{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf u}
, where
\eta({\bf u})
is optimal linear classifier defined as in (
4
). As a consequence, we have
d({\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2})=|{\bf A}^{\top}({\bf u}_{1}-{\bf u}_{2})|
. We shall denote the semiparametric location model with such a linear embedding on the categorical variables as
{\rm SLM_{LE}}
.

•

Deep Embedding: Embedding of categorical variables via deep neural networks has been well studied in the literature of Natural Language Processing, and one of the most popular methods is the neural network-based model “Word2Vec”
[
30
]
. While “Word2Vec” was developed for word vectors,
[
37
]
proposed a new model “Cat2Vec” which extended the deep embedding framework of “Word2Vec” to deal with general categorical variables. We shall adopt the “Cat2Vec” approach and maps the high dimensional categorical variables onto the real space
{\mathbb{R}}
, and we denote the semiparametric location model with such a deep embedding on the categorical variables as
{\rm SLM_{DE}}
.

5
Discussion

In this paper we have proposed a semiparametric model based on the optimal Bayes rule for classifying data with high dimensional mixed variables. The oracle Bayes rule essentially relies on the joint distribution of
({\bf X},{\bf U})
in each class. In our approach, the joint distribution is formulated via the distributions of
{\bf X}|{\bf U}
and
{\bf U}
, where the discrete variable
{\bf U}
is viewed as a random “location” in
\{0,1\}^{d}
, and
{\bf X}|{\bf U}
is assumed to be Gaussian as in classical LDA, with the mean and covariance matrix defined as functions of the location. We have shown that under this location model, the classification direction and the the intercept are separable.
To address the curse of dimensionality, we consider a general formulation where the classification direction is assumed to be varying smoothly over the locations, and the intercept is approximated via a first order (linear) expansion.
The estimation methods we have adopted in this paper have covered (low order) parametric approximation, and nonparametric smoothing. Different combinations of the parametric and nonparametric approaches could result in different theories and performance in different applications. For example, for the illustrative example in the Supplementary, the optimal classifier can be achieved by adopting a first order linear form for both
\beta({\bf u})
and
\eta({\bf u})
.

Above all, the key message we hope to convey in this paper is that
categorical variables and continuous variables should be treated differently, and more dedicated modeling strategies should be considered in the presence of other complex structures such as high dimensionality. Apart from the semiparametric classifier we have developed, there are other possible variations that we can consider. First, we can impose different structures for
\beta({\bf u})
and
\eta({\bf u})
. For example, similar to
\eta({\bf u})
, we can also adopt a linear approximation for the classification direction
\beta({\bf u})
and the mean and covariance functions. The resulting classifier will reduce to a classifier with linear effects
{\bf X}
,
{\bf U}
and their interactions. Second, we can also consider relaxing the Gaussian assumption on
{\bf X}
to a copula model as in
[
14
]
and
[
27
]
. Third, it is interesting to apply the idea of the location model to develop ensemble classifiers using for example random subspace for high dimensional data with mixed variables
[
36
]
. Last but not least, the proposed idea can be extended to handle the case where
{\bf X}
admits a matrix or tensor structure
[
32
]
. We leave these for future exploration.

6.1
An illustrative example

Consider a two-class classification problem with one binary covariate
U
and one continuous covariate
X
. Assume that
the prior for the class label
L
is balanced, i.e.,
P(L=1)=P(L=2)=0.5
, and let
P(U=0)=P(U=1)=0.5
for both classes. For class 1, suppose
X\sim N(-1,1)
under category
U=0
and
X\sim N(1,1)
under category
U=1
. For class 2, assume that
X\sim N(1,1)
under category
U=0
and
X\sim N(-1,1)
under category
U=1
.
We have:

(1)

The optimal Bayes risk is
\Phi(-1)
where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

(2)

The misclassification rate for the optimal linear classifier is
\frac{1}{2}\left[\Phi(-1)+\frac{1}{2}\right]
.

Proof.

(1). With some simple calculations it can be shown that the optimal Bayes classifier is:
classify
(X,U)
into class 1 if
XU>0
, and into class 2 otherwise. The corresponding misclassification rate can be easily established.

(2). Without loss of generality, consider a classifier that classify
(X,U)
into class 1 if and only if
X+aU>b
for some
a
and
b
. Let
Z
be a random variable following the standard normal distribution. The misclassification rate of the linear classifier can be computed as:

\displaystyle R(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}P(X+aU>b|L=2,U=0)+\frac{1}{4}P(X+aU>b|L=2,U=1)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}P(X+aU<b|L=1,U=0)+\frac{1}{4}P(X+aU<b|L=1,U=1).

Note that

P(X+aU>b|L=2,U=0)=P(X>b|X\sim N(1,1))=P(Z>b-1)
. Similarly, we have

P(X+aU>b|L=2,U=1)=P(Z>b-a+1)
,
P(X+aU<b|L=1,U=0)=P(Z<b+1)
and
P(X+aU<b|L=1,U=1)=P(Z<b-a-1)
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle R(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}P(b-1<Z<b+1)-\frac{1}{4}P(b-a-1<Z<b-a+1)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}P(-1<Z<1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\Phi(-1),

where the equal sign in the second inequality is obtained when
a=b\rightarrow\infty
.

∎

6.2
Motivation for model (
4
)

Recall that
\eta({\bf u})=\frac{\pi_{1}p_{1}({\bf u})}{\pi_{2}p_{2}({\bf u})}
.
Note that for any injective function
G({\bf u}):\{0,1\}^{d}\mapsto R
, it can be easily shown by induction that
G({\bf u})
can be written as a polynomial of
{\bf u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}
.

Specifically, there exist constants
a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{p};a_{1,2},a_{1,3},\ldots,a_{p-1,p};\ldots;a_{1,2\ldots,d}
, such that

\displaystyle G({\bf u})=a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}u_{i}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}a_{i,j}u_{i}u_{j}+\cdots+a_{1,2\ldots,d}u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{d}.

Suppose

\pi_{1}\log p_{1}({\bf u})
and
\pi_{2}\log p_{2}({\bf u})
are injective functions such that

(12)

\displaystyle\log\pi_{j}p_{j}({\bf u})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{0}^{(j)}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}^{(j)}u_{i}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}a_{i,j}u_{i}u_{j}+\cdots+a_{1,2\ldots,d}u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{d},~~~j=1,2,

where the intercepts and first order coefficients between the two classes,
a_{0}^{(1)},a_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,a_{p}^{(1)}
and
a_{0}^{(2)},a_{1}^{(2)}

,\ldots,a_{p}^{(2)}
, are potentially different, and the higher order coefficients
a_{1,2},\ldots,

a_{1,2\ldots,d}
, are assumed to be the same between the two classes. Correspondingly, we have

(13)

\displaystyle\eta({\bf u})=\log\frac{\pi_{1}p_{1}({\bf u})}{\pi_{2}p_{2}({\bf u})}=A_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{i}u_{i},

with
A_{i}=a_{i}^{(1)}-a_{i}^{(2)},i=0,\ldots,d
.
The assumption of common higher order coefficients between the two classes in (
12
) is analogous to the common covariance assumption in the classical LDA setting.
Importantly, since all the
u_{i}
’s are binary variables, the probability of a higher order term
u_{i_{1}}u_{i_{2}}\cdots u_{i_{k}}
taking the value 1 tends to zero in a geometric rate. Hence the expansion (
6.2
) can in general be well approximated by the lower order terms in practice. This is in contrast to the unusual lower order approximation widely applied in linear models where the rational is to achieve certain amount of parsimony. In particular, (
13
) is true if we only consider model (
12
) with the first two terms only.

\displaystyle\|(\hat{\bf\Omega}({\bf u})\hat{{\bf b}}^{0}({\bf u})-\hat{{\bf a}}({\bf u}))_{\mathcal{S}}\|_{\infty}\leq\lambda,\textrm{ and }

(24)

\displaystyle\|(\hat{\bf\Omega}({\bf u})\hat{{\bf b}}^{0}({\bf u})-\hat{{\bf a}}({\bf u}))_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}\|_{\infty}<\lambda.

(
23
) is a direct result of (
20
). For (
24
), we have

\displaystyle(\hat{\bf\Omega}({\bf u})\hat{{\bf b}}^{0}({\bf u})-\hat{{\bf a}}({\bf u}))_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf\Omega}}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})\hat{{\bf b}}^{0}_{\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})-\hat{{\bf a}}_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}({\bf u})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf\Omega}}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})\hat{{\bf b}}^{0}_{\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})-{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u}){\bf b}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})+{\bf a}_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}({\bf u})-\hat{{\bf a}}_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}({\bf u})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf\Omega}}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})(\hat{{\bf b}}^{0}_{\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})-{\bf b}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{S}}({\bf u}))+[\hat{{\bf\Omega}}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})-{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})]{\bf b}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})+{\bf a}_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}({\bf u})-\hat{{\bf a}}_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}({\bf u})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\hat{{\bf\Omega}}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})-{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})](\hat{{\bf b}}^{0}_{\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})-{\bf b}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{S}}({\bf u}))+{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u}){\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})^{-1}\{{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})(\hat{{\bf b}}^{0}_{\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})-{\bf b}^{\ast}_{\mathcal{S}}({\bf u}))\}

\displaystyle\|(\hat{\bf\Omega}({\bf u})\hat{{\bf b}}^{0}({\bf u})-\hat{{\bf a}}({\bf u}))_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76228pt\times\left\{\frac{1+\|{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u}){\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})^{-1}\|_{L}}{1-\|{\bf b}^{\ast({\bf u})}\|_{0}\|{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})^{-1}\|_{L}\|\hat{{\bf\Omega}}({\bf u})-{\bf\Omega}({\bf u})\|_{\infty}}\right\}.

Under the Condition that

{2[{1-\|{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S}^{c},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u}){\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})^{-1}\|_{L}-2\|{\bf b}^{\ast}({\bf u})\|_{0}\|{\bf\Omega}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}}({\bf u})^{-1}\|_{L}\|\hat{{\bf\Omega}}({\bf u})-{\bf\Omega}({\bf u})\|_{\infty}}]^{-1}\epsilon_{\bf u}}<\lambda,

6.7
Proof of Theorem
3.5

Proof.

Denote the objective function on the right hand side of (
5
) as
L_{n}(\eta)
. The true linear function
\eta({\bf W})=A_{0}+{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf W}
is the minimizer of the expected risk:

\displaystyle El(\tilde{\eta};{\bf W},L))=E\{-(2-L)\tilde{\eta}({\bf W})+\log(1+\exp\{\tilde{\eta}({\bf W})\})\}.

Let
\hat{\cal M}:=\{j:\hat{A}_{j}\neq 0\}
.
Using similar arguments as in the proof of Proposition
3.3
, we can first show that under Conditions (C5) and (C6),
P(\hat{\cal M}={\cal M})\rightarrow 1
. For convenience we shall assume hereafter in this proof that
\hat{\cal M}={\cal M}
.

Let
{\cal B}_{\eta}(\delta)
be the set of linear functions
\bar{\eta}({\bf w})=\bar{A}_{0}+\bar{\bf A}_{1}^{\top}{\bf w}
, such that
\sum_{i=0}^{d}|\bar{A}_{i}-A_{i}|=\delta
for some
\delta>0
.
Here
\bar{A}_{i}
is the
i
-th element of
\bar{\bf A}_{1}
, and
\bar{A}_{i}=0
for
i\in{\cal M}^{c}
.
With some abuse of notations, let
W_{i}={\bf U}_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\binom{1}{W_{i}}
for
i=1\ldots,n_{1}
,
W_{n_{1}+i}={{\bf V}_{i}},\tilde{W}_{n_{1}+i}=\binom{1}{W_{n_{1}+i}}
for
i=1\ldots,n_{2}
, and denote
{\bf W}_{n}=(\tilde{W}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{W}_{n})
.
The Hessian matrix of
L_{n}
is then given as:

H_{n}({\eta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{W}_{i}\tilde{W}_{i}^{\top}\frac{\exp\{\eta(W_{i})\}}{(1+\exp\{\eta(W_{i})\})^{2}}.

By Taylor expansion we have, there exists an
\eta^{*}({\bf w})=A_{0}^{*}+{\bf w}^{\top}{\bf A}_{1}^{*}
, such that
A_{0}^{*}\in[\bar{A}_{0},A_{0}]
,
A_{i}^{*}\in[\bar{A}_{i},A_{i}]
for
i\in{\cal M}
,
A_{i}^{*}=0
for
i\in{\cal M}^{c}
, and

\displaystyle L_{n}(\bar{\eta})=L_{n}(\eta)+L^{\prime}_{n}(\eta)\binom{\bar{A}_{0}-A_{0}}{\bar{{\bf A}}_{1}-{\bf A}_{1}}+\binom{\bar{A}_{0}-A_{0}}{\bar{{\bf A}}_{1}-{\bf A}_{1}}^{\top}H_{n}({\eta}^{*})\binom{\bar{A}_{0}-A_{0}}{\bar{{\bf A}}_{1}-{\bf A}_{1}}.

Note that from Bernstein’s inequality
[
23
]
and the fact that
EL^{\prime}_{n}(\eta)={\bf 0}
, we have, there exists a constant
C>0
such that with probability larger than
1-O(d^{-1})
,

\left|L^{\prime}_{n}(\eta)\binom{\bar{A}_{0}-A_{0}}{\bar{{\bf A}}_{1}-{\bf A}_{1}}\right|\leq C\delta M\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}},

holds for all
\bar{\eta}\in{\cal B}_{\eta}(\delta)
. On the other hand, using Condition (C5) and the fact that

(\bar{A}_{0}-A_{0})^{2}+\|\bar{{\bf A}}_{1}-{\bf A}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\geq M^{-1}\delta^{2}
,
we have with probability larger than
1-O(d^{-1})
,

\binom{\bar{A}_{0}-A_{0}}{\bar{{\bf A}}_{1}-{\bf A}_{1}}^{\top}H_{n}({\eta}^{*})\binom{\bar{A}_{0}-A_{0}}{\bar{{\bf A}}_{1}-{\bf A}_{1}}\geq\frac{\delta^{2}}{Me^{M}},

holds for all
\bar{\eta}\in{\cal B}_{\eta}(\delta)
. Consequently, by choosing
\delta=C_{1}M^{2}e^{M}\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}}
for some large enough constant
C_{1}>0
, we have with probability larger than

,

L_{n}(\bar{\eta})<L_{n}(\eta)
holds for all
\bar{\eta}\in{\cal B}_{\eta}(\delta)

, we conclude that with probability tending to 1,

\hat{\eta}
of

must lie inside the

-ball with radius
\delta
, i.e.,
|\hat{\eta}({\bf w})-\eta({\bf w})|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{d}|\hat{A}_{i}-A_{i}|<\delta
. This proves the theorem.
∎
