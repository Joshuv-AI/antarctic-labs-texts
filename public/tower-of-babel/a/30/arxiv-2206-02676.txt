Abstract
This paper is concerned with the distance of a symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix T T to the manifold of similarly structured singular matrices, and with determining the closest matrix to T T in this manifold. Explicit formulas are presented, that exploit the analysis of the sensitivity of the spectrum of T T with respect to structure-preserving perturbations of its entries.

Introduction

The sensitivity of the solution of a linear algebra problem with respect to perturbations in the data has received considerable attention in the literature.
This interest can be traced back to the work of Golub and Wilkinson
[
13
]
, Wilkinson
[
38
,
39
]
, Demmel
[
4
,
5
]
, and numerous other authors; see, e.g.,
[
12
,
34
]
and the references therein. The effect of structure-preserving perturbations of the matrix entries has been analyzed in the context of linear systems (see, e.g.,
[
37
,
15
,
31
,
32
]
) as well as for structured eigenproblems. Measures of structured eigenvalue sensitivity include both structured pseudospectra (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
30
,
6
,
17
,
2
,
25
]
) and
structured condition numbers
, introduced by Tisseur
[
35
]
. The latter measures have been investigated, e.g., in
[
18
,
22
,
24
]
and, recently, applied to network analysis
[
28
,
9
]
.

Symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrices arise in several applications, including the numerical solution of ordinary and partial differential equations
[
33
,
1
]
, and as regularization matrices in Tikhonov regularization for the solution of discrete ill-posed problems
[
14
,
29
,
16
]
. It is therefore important to understand properties of such structured matrices relevant for computation.

By leveraging structured eigensensitivity analysis, this paper concerns with the distance of symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrices to the manifold of similarly structured singular matrices, and with determining the closest matrix in this manifold. The structured measures we deal with can be computed by endowing the considered subspace of matrices with the Frobenius norm. We remark that estimates on the distance to singularity in the spectral norm of symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrices, with respect to normwise structured perturbations, can be found in the work of Rump
[
31
]
, however the present paper develops a novel approach based on the structured eigensensitivity analysis that gives rise to an explicit formula for the
structured distance to singularity
in the Frobenius norm of such matrices and to upper bounds for their structured distance to singularity in the spectral norm, except for the definite case where one has an explicit formula also for the latter distance.

We denote symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
by

(1)

T=(n;\delta,\sigma)=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\delta&\sigma&&&&&\Large{O}\\
\sigma&\delta&\sigma&&&&\\
&\sigma&\cdot&\cdot&&&\\
&&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&&\\
&&&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\\
&&&&\cdot&\cdot&\sigma\\
\Large{O}&&&&&\sigma&\delta\end{array}\right].

It is well known that the eigenvalues of (
1
) are given by

(2)

\lambda_{h}=\delta+2\sigma\,\cos\frac{h\pi}{n+1},\quad h=1,\dots,n;

see, e.g.,
[
33
]
.
Throughout this paper we refer to
\lambda_{h}
in (
2
) as the
h
th eigenvalue of
T=(n;\delta,\sigma)
. Assume
\sigma\neq 0
. Then
T
has
n
simple real eigenvalues
allocated symmetrically with respect
to
\delta
. The components of the eigenvector

\mathbf{x}_{h}=[x_{h,1},x_{h,2},\ldots,x_{h,n}]^{T}

associated with the eigenvalue

\lambda_{h}
and normalized
to have unit Euclidean norm, are the following,

(3)

x_{h,k}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{n+1}}\sin\frac{hk\pi}{n+1},\quad k=1,\dots,n,\quad h=1,\dots,n;

see, e.g.,
[
24
]
. Let
\Lambda
and
X
denote the diagonal matrix with
\lambda_{h}
as
h
th diagonal entry
and the orthogonal matrix with
\mathbf{x}_{h}
as
h
th column, respectively.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the manifold of singular matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
The distance of
T
to
\mathcal{S}
in the Frobenius norm is given by

d_{F}(T)=\min_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left\|T-S\right\|_{F},

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
stands for the Frobenius norm.
As it is well known, if
\lambda_{k}
is an eigenvalue of
T
such that
|\lambda_{k}|<|\lambda_{h}|
, with
h=1,\dots,n,\,h\neq k
, then, thanks to the Eckart-Young theorem
[
7
]
, one has that

(4)

S_{*}:=\sum_{h=1,\dots,n,h\neq k}\lambda_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T}

is the unique matrix in
{\mathcal{S}}
such that

(5)

d_{F}(T)=\left\|T-S_{*}\right\|_{F}=\left\|\lambda_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\right\|_{F}=|\lambda_{k}|.

Throughout this paper the superscript
(\cdot)^{T}
stands for transposition and
(\cdot)^{H}

for transposition and complex conjugation. We explicitly observe that Equation (
5
) holds true if
\lambda_{k}
is such that
|\lambda_{k}|\leq|\lambda_{h}|
, for
h\neq k
. However, if not all the above inequalities are strict, then
S_{*}
in (
4
) is not the unique matrix that attains the minimum
\min_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left\|T-S\right\|_{F}
.

Let
{\cal T}
denote the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
formed by
symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrices. The structured distance to singularity
of
T
in the Frobenius norm is given by

d_{F}^{\cal T}(T)=\min_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}}\left\|T-S\right\|_{F}.

Notice that the singular matrix closest to
T
, i.e., the matrix
S_{*}
defined in (
4
),
is not a tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix, that is to say,
S_{*}\notin{\cal T}
. Hence, by (
5
), one has

d_{F}^{\cal T}(T)>\min_{h=1,\dots,n}|\lambda_{h}|\,.

The property of any
T=(n;\delta,\sigma)
of fixed order
n
, with
\sigma\neq 0
, to have the same eigenvectors (
3
),
as well as the knowledge of the worst-case
structured perturbation for the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
(see, e.g.,
[
26
]
)
are the tools used in this paper to shed light on the structured distance to singularity of a
symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix.

For any matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
let
A|{\cal T}
denote the matrix in the subspace

{\cal T}
closest to
A
with respect to the Frobenius norm. It is straightforward to verify that, in order to have such projection of
A
in
{\cal T}
, one takes the symmetric part of the tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix obtained in turn by replacing in each structure diagonal all the entries of
A
with their arithmetic mean.
Notice that the projection
S_{*}|{\cal T}\notin{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}
. In the present paper, we give an explicit formula for the structured distance to singularity of
T\in{\cal T}
, based on
the ratios

\frac{|\lambda_{h}|}{\left\|(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}\right\|_{F}}\,,\qquad h=1,\dots,n,

and we illustrate how to find the closest
matrix
S_{*}^{\cal T}\in{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}
to
T
.
Additionally, if
T\in{\cal T}
is positive or negative definite, we show that

\frac{d_{F}(T)}{d_{F}^{\cal T}(T)}\approx\sqrt{\frac{3}{n}}\,.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
is concerned with the sensitivity analysis of
the spectrum of a symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix
T
with respect to structure-preserving perturbations and
Section
3
exploits such analysis to discuss an upper bound for the structured distance to singularity of
T
in the Frobenius norm.
Section
4
is concerned with determining
S_{*}^{\cal T}
, i.e., the unique closest singular symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix to
T

in the Frobenius norm, as well as its distance
d_{F}^{\cal T}(T)
to
T
. Section
5
discusses the structured
distance to singularity of a symmetric positive definite tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix both in the Frobenius norm and in the spectral norm and shows monotonicity properties of the entries of its Cholesky factor. In Section
6
the cases relevant to indefinite matrices in
{\cal T}
are investigated.
Numerical examples are presented in
Section
7
and concluding remarks can be found in Section
8
.

2
Eigenvalue structured sensitivity

Let
\mathcal{C}
be any subspace of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
formed by matrices
with a given symmetry-structure, that is to say a structure that exhibits a kind of symmetry, like reflection or translation. Examples of structured matrices of such kind are complex symmetric, skew-symmetric, persymmetric, skew-persymmetric matrices, or, more specifically, Toeplitz and Hankel matrices; see, e.g.,
[
23
, Section 5]
.
Let
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
be a simple eigenvalue of a given matrix
C\in\mathcal{C}
,
with corresponding right and left eigenvectors

\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
of unit Euclidean norm.
A well-known indicator of the
sensitivity of
\lambda
to perturbations of the entries of
C
is given by analyzing the
coefficient of the first term in the expansion of powers of
\varepsilon
of
\lambda(\varepsilon)
,
where
\lambda(\varepsilon)
is the eigenvalue of
C+\varepsilon E
, with
\|E\|_{F}=1
, that tends toward
\lambda
when

\varepsilon
goes to zero:

\lambda(\varepsilon)=\lambda+\frac{\mathbf{y}^{H}E\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{y}^{H}\mathbf{x}}\,\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

In fact, the traditional condition number
\kappa(\lambda)
is the first-order measure of the worst-case effect on
\lambda
of perturbations in
C
, i.e.,

\kappa(\lambda)=\max_{E\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n},\,\,\|E\|_{F}=1}\left|\frac{\mathbf{y}^{H}E\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{y}^{H}\mathbf{x}}\right|=\frac{1}{\mathbf{y}^{H}\mathbf{x}},

and the
worst-case perturbation
is proven to be
E=\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{H}
; see
[
39
, Section 2]
.

We are interested in the sensitivity of the eigenvalues of
C\in{\cal C}
with respect to structure-preserving perturbations. Let
(\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{H})|{\cal C}
denote the matrix in the subspace
{\cal C}
closest to
\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{H}
with respect to the Frobenius norm. Following the approach above, and admitting only unit-norm perturbation matrices
E
that belong to
\mathcal{C}
,
leads to the
structured condition number

\kappa^{\cal C}(\lambda)
as the first-order measure of the worst-case effect on
\lambda
of perturbations of the same structure as
C
, that is to say,

\kappa^{\cal C}(\lambda)=\max_{E\in{\cal C},\,\,\|E\|_{F}=1}\left|\frac{\mathbf{y}^{H}E\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{y}^{H}\mathbf{x}}\right|=\frac{\|(\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{H})|{\cal C}\|_{F}}{\mathbf{y}^{H}\mathbf{x}};

see
[
18
,
23
]
. Thus

\frac{\kappa^{\cal C}(\lambda)}{\kappa(\lambda)}=\|(\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{H})|{\cal C}\|_{F}\leq 1.

Moreover, thanks to
[
23
, Lemma 3.2]
, which claims that

(6)

\mathbf{y}^{H}(\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{H})|{\cal C}\,\mathbf{x}=\|(\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{H})|{\cal C}\|^{2}_{F},

one has that the
worst-case structured perturbation
is given by

E=\frac{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{H}|{\cal C}}{\|\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{H}|{\cal C}\|_{F}}.

Consider now the symmetry-structured subspace
{\cal T}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Since
T\in{\cal T}
is
symmetric, then for any (real) eigenvalue
\lambda_{h}
in (
2
), one has

\mathbf{x}_{h}=\mathbf{y}_{h}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and

(7)

\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})=\|(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}\|_{F}.

Explicit formulas for the structured condition numbers
\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})
for
the eigenvalues
\lambda_{h}
, with
h=1,\dots,n
, of
T=(n;\delta,\sigma)
, with
\sigma\neq 0
,
are the following,

(8)

\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+\frac{2}{n-1}\cos^{2}\frac{h\pi}{n+1}},\;\;\;\;h=1,\dots,n;

see
[
26
, Proposition 4.11]
. As the eigenvectors (
3
), the structured
condition numbers (
8
) do not depend on
\delta
and
\sigma
.
Figure
1
shows the structured condition numbers of the eigenvalues of
a
100\times 100
symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix; see
[
26

.

3
An upper bound for the structured distance to singularity

The structured conditioning analysis in Section
2
can be exploited to obtain an upper bound for the structured distance to singularity of
T\in{\cal T}
in the Frobenius norm.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider
T=(n;\delta,\sigma)
. One has that

(11)

T-\lambda_{h}\frac{(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}}{\|(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}\|_{F}^{2}},

where
\mathbf{x}_{h}
is a unit-norm eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{h}
, is a symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix with null
h
th eigenvalue. Its distance to
T
in the Frobenius norm is
given by the ratio
|\lambda_{h}|/\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})
, where
\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})
is the structured condition number of
\lambda_{h}
.

Proof 3.2
.

By (
6
), one has

\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T}\,\left(T-\lambda_{h}\frac{(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}}{\|(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}\|_{F}^{2}}\right)\,\mathbf{x}_{h}=\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T}\,T\,\mathbf{x}_{h}-\frac{\lambda_{h}}{\|(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}\|_{F}^{2}}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T}\,(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}\,\mathbf{x}_{h}

=\lambda_{h}-\frac{\lambda_{h}}{\|(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}\|_{F}^{2}}\|(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}\|_{F}^{2}=0.

Thus, counting eigenvalues as in (
2
), the
h
th eigenvalue of the symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix in (
11
) is zero. By (
7
), one has

\left\|\lambda_{h}\frac{(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}}{\|(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}\|_{F}^{2}}\right\|_{F}=\frac{|\lambda_{h}|}{\|(\mathbf{x}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}^{T})|{\cal T}\|_{F}}=\frac{|\lambda_{h}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})},

which concludes the proof.

We observe that Theorem
11
implies an upper bound for the structured distance to singularity of
T
in the Frobenius norm, that is to say,

\min_{h=1,\dots,n}|\lambda_{h}|=d_{F}(T)\leq d_{F}^{\cal T}(T)\leq\min_{h=1,\dots,n}\frac{|\lambda_{h}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})}.

4
Structured distance to singularity

According to Theorem
3.1
, the matrix (
11
) belongs to
{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}
.
Is (
11
) the matrix in
{\mathcal{T}}
with null
h
th eigenvalue closest to
T
in the Frobenius norm? The following results address such issue.

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider
T=(n;\delta,\sigma)
. The unique symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix with null
h
th eigenvalue closest to
T
in the Frobenius norm is
S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}=(n;\delta_{*},\sigma_{*})
, where

(12)

\delta_{*}=\frac{2(nc^{2}\delta-(n-1)c\sigma)}{n-1+2nc^{2}},\;\;\;\;\sigma_{*}=\frac{(n-1)\sigma-nc\delta}{n-1+2nc^{2}},\;\;\;\;{\mbox{w}ith}\;\;c=\cos\frac{h\pi}{n+1}.

One has

(13)

\|T-S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}\|_{F}=\frac{|\delta+2c\sigma|}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+\frac{2c^{2}}{n-1}}}.

Proof 4.2
.

We seek to determine a matrix
S=(n;d,s)\in{\mathcal{T}}
such that its
h
th eigenvalue is
0
. By (
2
), one has

d=-2s\cos\frac{h\pi}{n+1}
. Thus,
S=(n;-2cs,s)
, with
c=\cos\frac{h\pi}{n+1}
, and the squared distance to
T
can
be expressed as

(14)

\|T-S\|_{F}^{2}=2(2nc^{2}+n-1)s^{2}+4(nc\delta+(1-n)\sigma)s+n\delta^{2}+2(n-1)\sigma^{2}.

Differentiating (
14
) with respect to
s
and equating to zero straightforwardly yields the quantity

\sigma_{*}
in (
12
). Computing
-2c\sigma_{*}
gives
\delta_{*}
in (
12
). Finally, replacing
s
in (
14
) by
\sigma_{*}
in (
12
), dividing both numerator and denominator by
n(n-1)
, and taking the square root, gives (
13
) and concludes the proof.

Corollary 4.3
.

Consider
T=(n;\delta,\sigma)
. The symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix (
11
) is the unique matrix in
{\mathcal{T}}
with null
h
th eigenvalue closest to
T
in the Frobenius norm.

Proof 4.4
.

One observes that the distance in (
13
) coincides with the ratio between the absolute value of the eigenvalue
\lambda_{h}
and its structured condition number
\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})
. Since Theorem
3.1
claims that the matrix in (
11
) is a symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix with null
h
th eigenvalue at such distance to
T
whereas Theorem
4.1
claims that
S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}=(n;\delta_{*},\sigma_{*})
is the unique matrix in
{\mathcal{T}}
with null
h
th eigenvalue closest to
T
in the Frobenius norm, the proof is concluded.

We are now in a position to determine
S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}=\arg\min_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}}\|T-S\|_{F}

and its distance to
T
, i.e.,
d_{F}^{\cal T}(T)=\|T-S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}\|_{F}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Consider
T=(n;\delta,\sigma)
and let
h\in\{1,\dots,n\}
be such that

\frac{|\lambda_{h}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})}\leq\frac{|\lambda_{k}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{k})},\quad\;\;k=1,\dots,n.

Then the symmetric tridiagonal matrix
S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}=(n;\delta_{*},\sigma_{*})
built as in Theorem
4.1
attains the minimum
\min_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}}\|T-S\|_{F}
. Hence, the structured distance to singularity of
T
is

(15)

d_{F}^{\cal T}(T)=\|T-S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}\|_{F}=\frac{|\lambda_{h}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})}.

Moreover, if

(16)

\frac{|\lambda_{h}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})}<\frac{|\lambda_{k}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{k})},\quad\;\;k=1,\dots,n,\;\;k\neq h,

then
S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}=(n;\delta_{*},\sigma_{*})
is the unique closest matrix in
{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}
to
T
.

Remark 4.6
.

Let
d_{2}(T)
denote the distance to singularity of
T=(n;\delta,\sigma)
in the spectral norm. If
\lambda_{k}
is such that
|\lambda_{k}|<|\lambda_{j}|
, with
j=1,\dots,n,j\neq k
, i.e., if
|\lambda_{k}|
is the unique smallest singular value of
T
, then
the matrix
S_{*}
defined in (
4
) is the unique matrix in
{\mathcal{S}}
such that

d_{2}(T)=\min_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left\|T-S\right\|_{2}=\left\|T-S_{*}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\lambda_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\right\|_{2}=|\lambda_{k}|,

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
stands for the spectral norm. Hence
d_{2}(T)=d_{F}(T)
.
It is now interesting to observe that Theorem
4.5
gives an upper bound for the structured distance to singularity of
T
in the spectral norm
d_{2}^{\cal T}(T)=\min_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}}\left\|T-S\right\|_{2}\,
.
In fact, if

\frac{|\lambda_{h}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})}\leq\frac{|\lambda_{j}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{j})},\quad\;\;j=1,\dots,n,

then
S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}=(n;\delta_{*},\sigma_{*})\in{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}
, with
\delta_{*}
,
\sigma_{*}
, and
c
in (
12
), is such that

d_{2}^{\cal T}(T)\leq\|T-S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}\|_{2}=\|X(\Lambda-\Lambda_{*})X^{T}\|_{2}=\|\Lambda-\Lambda_{*}\|_{2},

where we denote by
\Lambda_{*}
the diagonal matrix having as
j
th diagonal entry the
j
th eigenvalue of
S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}
, for
j=1,\dots,n
. Then, since

\|\Lambda-\Lambda_{*}\|_{2}=\frac{|\lambda_{h}|}{n-1+2nc^{2}}(n-1+2n|c|\cos\frac{\pi}{n+1}),

one has the following lower and upper bounds for
d_{2}^{\cal T}(T)
:

(17)

|\lambda_{k}|=d_{2}(T)\leq d_{2}^{\cal T}(T)\leq\frac{|\lambda_{h}|}{n-1+2n\cos^{2}\frac{h\pi}{n+1}}(n-1+2n\left|\cos\frac{h\pi}{n+1}\right|\cos\frac{\pi}{n+1}).

5
The definite case

If
T=(n;\delta,\sigma)
is positive or negative definite, then the smallest eigenvalue in magnitude is either
\lambda_{n}
or
\lambda_{1}
, according to the sign of
\sigma
.
Following our analysis on the eigenvalue structured sensitivity, cf. Section
2
, the two extremal eigenvalues in the spectrum of
T
have the largest structured condition numbers. Therefore, since there exists
k\in\{1,n\}
such that
|\lambda_{k}|<|\lambda_{h}|
, for
h=1,\dots,n
,
h\neq k
, then the same eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
satisfies the inequalities in (
16
). Hence, by Corollary
4.3
, the structured distance to singularity is given by
the ratio between the absolute value of the smallest eigenvalue in magnitude and its structured condition number.

Remark 5.1
.

Note that the approach here is the opposite of the one in
[
27
, Example 4.6]
, where the authors
were concerned with the structured distance to symmetric positive
semidefiniteness
\Delta_{F}^{\cal{T}}(T)
of (indefinite) matrices
T\in{\cal T}
. In more detail, by
[
27
, Theorem 4.5]
, one has

\Delta_{F}^{\mathcal{T}}(T)\leq\min\sqrt{2(n-1)\sigma^{2},n\max\left\{0,2|\sigma|\cos\frac{\pi}{n+1}-\delta\right\}^{2}},\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\rm{if}\;\;\delta>0,

\Delta_{F}^{\mathcal{T}}(T)\leq\min\sqrt{2(n-1)\sigma^{2}+n\delta^{2},n\left(2|\sigma|\cos\frac{\pi}{n+1}-\delta\right)^{2}},\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\rm{if}\;\;\delta\leq 0.

5.1
About the the structured distance to singularity in the spectral norm

Remark
4.6
shows lower and upper bounds for the structured distance to singularity in the spectral norm,
d_{2}^{\cal T}(T)
, of any symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix
T=(n;\delta,\sigma)
.
In the definite case, such structured distance equals the (unstructured) distance to singularity
d_{2}(T)
. Indeed, since the
k
th eigenvalue of
T
, with either
k=1
or
k=n
, is such that

|\lambda_{k}|<|\lambda_{h}|\quad\mbox{and}\;\;\frac{|\lambda_{k}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{k})}<\frac{|\lambda_{h}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})},\quad\mbox{with}\;\;h=1,\dots,n,\;\;h\neq k,

then by (
17
) one has

|\lambda_{k}|=d_{2}(T)\leq d_{2}^{\cal T}(T)\leq\frac{|\lambda_{k}|}{n-1+2n\cos^{2}\frac{\pi}{n+1}}(n-1+2n\cos^{2}\frac{\pi}{n+1})=|\lambda_{k}|,

so that

d_{2}(T)=d_{2}^{\cal T}(T)=|\lambda_{k}|=d_{F}(T)<d_{F}^{\cal T}(T)=\frac{|\lambda_{k}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{k})}.

We emphasize that we have shown that the equality

d_{2}^{\cal T}(T)=\frac{1}{\|T^{-1}\|_{2}}

holds true if and only if
T=(n;\delta,\sigma)
is positive or negative definite. Thus, following the analysis in the work of Rump
[
31
]
, in such a case one has that the reciprocal of the spectral condition number of
T
is equal to its relative structured distance to the closest singular matrix with respect to the the spectral norm:

\frac{1}{\kappa_{2}(T)}=\min_{\Delta T\in{\cal T}}\left\{\frac{\|\Delta T\|_{2}}{\|T\|_{2}}:T+\Delta T\in{\cal S}\right\}.

5.2
The discrete Laplacian

Eq. (
10
) gives the following estimate for the ratio between the unstructured
and the structured distances to singularity of a large definite matrix
T\in{\cal T}
,

\frac{d_{F}(T)}{d_{F}^{\cal T}(T)}\approx\sqrt{\frac{3}{n}}.

Consider as an example the discrete Laplacian
T(n)=(n;2,-1)
. One observes that

\lambda_{1}(n)
is the smallest eigenvalue of
T(n)
and, in a asymptotical perspective, one has

\lambda_{1}(n)=2-2\cos\frac{\pi}{n+1}=\pi^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(1+{\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\,,

whereas

\frac{\lambda_{1}(n)}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{1}(n))}=\frac{\lambda_{1}(n)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+\frac{2}{n-1}\cos^{2}\frac{\pi}{n+1}}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{\sqrt{3}}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{\frac{3}{2}}}\left(1+{\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\,.

Thus, as it is clear by measuring its structured (or unstructured) distance to singularity either in the spectral norm or in the Frobenius norm,
T(n)
is asymptotically singular. In more detail, since in Theorem
4.5
one has
h=1
, the closest matrix in
{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}
to
T(n)
in the Frobenius norm is
S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}(n)=(n;\delta_{*}(n),\sigma_{*}(n))
, with
\delta_{*}(n)
,
\sigma_{*}(n)
as in (
12
) and
c(n)=\cos\frac{\pi}{n+1}
, so that

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}c(n)=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\cos\frac{\pi}{n+1}=1.

Thus, the entries of
S_{*}^{\mathcal{T}}(n)
satisfy

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\delta_{*}(n)=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{2(2nc(n)^{2}+(n-1)c(n))}{2nc(n)^{2}+n-1}=2,

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{*}(n)=-\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{(n-1)+2nc(n)}{n-1+2nc(n)^{2}}=-1.

For the sake of completeness, we remark that an explicit formula for the inverse of
T(n)
is given in
[
21
, Theorem 2.8]
:

[T^{-1}(n)]_{i,j}=i\,\frac{n-j+1}{n+1}.

5.3
About the pattern of the Cholesky factor

In
[
19
, Lemma 1]
, Laudadio et al. showed that the diagonal entries of the upper triangular factor
R
of the Cholesky factorization of a symmetric positive definite Toeplitz matrix
A=R^{T}R
decrease monotonically with increasing row number. We are interested in further monotonicity properties of the entries of the Cholesky factor
R
of a symmetric positive definite tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix, which can be easily obtained and will be useful to illustrate the numerical tests regarding positive definite matrices in
{\cal T}
, cf. Example 1 in Section
7
. We collect such results in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
T=(n;\delta,\sigma)
,
\sigma\neq 0
, be positive definite and let
R=(r_{i,j})
,
i,j=1,\dots,n
be its Cholesky factor so that
T=R^{T}R
. Then,
R
is upper bidiagonal with the entries of the diagonal satisfying

(18)

r_{i-1,i-1}\geq r_{i,i}>0,\;\;i=2,\dots,n\,,

and the entries of the upper diagonal having the same sign of
\sigma
and satisfying

(19)

|r_{i-1,i}|\leq|r_{i,i+1}|,\;\;i=2,\dots,n-1\,.

Moreover, if
\delta\geq 2|\sigma|
, one has

(20)

r_{i-1,i-1}>|r_{i-1,i}|\;\;\;\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;\;\;\;r_{i,i}>|r_{i-1,i}|,\;\;\;\;\;\;i=2,\dots,n\,.

Proof 5.3
.

It is well known that the Cholesky factor of a symmetric positive definite tridiagonal matrix is upper bidiagonal. The inequalities in (
18
) are proved in
[
19
, Lemma 1]
. The entries of
T
satisfy

(21)

\delta=r_{i,i}^{2}+r_{i-1,i}^{2}\;\;\;\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;\;\;\;\sigma=r_{i-1,i}r_{i-1,i-1},\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;i=2,\dots,n\,.

The equalities satisfied by
\sigma
in (
21
) imply that all the entries of the upper diagonal of
R
have the same sign as
\sigma
and that

|r_{i-1,i}|=|r_{i,i+1}|\frac{r_{i,i}}{r_{i-1,i-1}}\leq|r_{i,i+1}|,\;\;i=2,\dots,n-1\,

where the inequality is due to (
18
). This concludes the first part of the proof.

Let
\delta
and
\sigma
satisfy
\delta\geq 2|\sigma|>0
. Firstly, we prove that

(22)

r_{i-1,i-1}>|r_{i-1,i}|\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;r_{i,i}>|r_{i-1,i}|,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;i=2,\dots,n\,.

Assume that
r_{i-1,i-1}>|r_{i-1,i}|
. Thanks to (
21
), one has

r_{i,i}^{2}=\delta-r_{i-1,i}^{2}\geq 2|\sigma|-r_{i-1,i}^{2}=2|r_{i-1,i}|r_{i-1,i-1}-r_{i-1,i}^{2}>2r_{i-1,i}^{2}-r_{i-1,i}^{2}=r_{i-1,i}^{2},

where in the last inequality we have used that
r_{i-1,i-1}>|r_{i-1,i}|
. This gives
r_{i,i}>|r_{i-1,i}|
. The viceversa is
assured by (
18
). Then, we prove

(23)

r_{i,i}>|r_{i-1,i}|\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;r_{i,i}>|r_{i,i+1}|,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;i=2,\dots,n-1\,.

Assume that
r_{i,i}>|r_{i-1,i}|
. By (
21
), one has

r_{i,i}^{2}=\frac{r_{i,i}^{2}+r_{i,i}^{2}}{2}>\frac{r_{i,i}^{2}+r_{i-1,i}^{2}}{2}=\frac{\delta}{2}\geq{|\sigma|}=|r_{i,i+1}|r_{i,i},

This gives
r_{i,i}>|r_{i,i+1}|
. The viceversa is assured by (
19
). In order to prove (
7.2
), we observe that
r_{1,1}\geq 2\,|r_{1,2}|
. Indeed,
r_{1,1}^{2}=\delta
, by construction, so that, by (
21
), one has
r_{1,1}^{2}\geq 2|\sigma|=2\,|r_{1,2}|r_{1,1}
, which implies that
r_{1,1}\geq 2|r_{1,2}|
. Now, the proof follows by recursively applying the
\Longrightarrow
implications in Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
), from
r_{1,1}>|r_{1,2}|\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;r_{2,2}>|r_{1,2}|
till
r_{n-1,n-1}>|r_{n-1,n}|\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;r_{n,n}>|r_{n-1,n}|
. This concludes the proof.

6
The indefinite case

We are interested in analyzing the cases when the closest matrix in
{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}
to T in the Frobenius norm is not unique. As we commented above, this can happen only in the indefinite case.

6.1
The case
\boldsymbol{\delta}\,\mathbf{=0}

We have to distinguish two cases.
If
\delta=0
and
n
is odd, then
T
is singular (
d_{F}(T)=d_{F}^{\cal T}(T)=0
), whereas
if
\delta=0
and
n
is even, then the eigenvalues
\lambda_{n/2}
and
\lambda_{(n+2)/2}
have opposite signs and the same smallest absolute value, because

\cos\frac{n\pi}{2(n+1)}=-\cos\frac{(n+2)\pi}{2(n+1)}
.
In the latter case,

d_{F}(T)=|\lambda_{n/2}|=|\lambda_{(n+2)/2}|

and, by the analysis in Section
2
, one proves that

d_{F}^{\cal T}(T)=\frac{|\lambda_{n/2}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{n/2})}=\frac{|\lambda_{(n+2)/2}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{(n+2)/2})}.

Indeed, if
\delta=0
, then
|\lambda_{k}|\leq|\lambda_{h}|

implies

|\lambda_{k}|/\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{k})\leq|\lambda_{h}|/\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h}),

and the equalities hold only for
\lambda_{n/2}
and
\lambda_{(n+2)/2}=-\lambda_{n/2}
.
Thus, there are two closest matrices in
{\mathcal{S}}
to
T
in the Frobenius norm, i.e.,

T\pm\lambda_{n/2}(\mathbf{x}_{n/2}\mathbf{x}_{n/2}^{T}),

and two closest matrices in
{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}
to
T
in the Frobenius norm, i.e.,

T\pm\lambda_{n/2}\frac{(\mathbf{x}_{n/2}\mathbf{x}_{n/2}^{T})|{\cal T}}{\|(\mathbf{x}_{n/2}\mathbf{x}_{n/2}^{T})|{\cal T}\|_{F}^{2}}.

Eq. (
9
) gives, when
n
is large, the following estimate for the ratio between the unstructured
and the structured distances to singularity of
T

\frac{d_{F}(T)}{d_{F}^{\cal T}(T)}\approx\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}.

Consider as an example
T(n)=(n;0,\sigma)\in{\cal T}
, with
\sigma>0
. One observes that, setting
c(n)=\cos\frac{n\pi}{2(n+1)}

for the smallest eigenvalue of
T(n)
, in a asymptotical perspective, one has

\lambda_{\frac{n}{2}}=-\lambda_{\frac{n+2}{2}}=2\sigma c(n)={\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),

whereas

\frac{|\lambda_{\frac{n}{2}}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{\frac{n}{2}})}=\frac{\lambda_{\frac{n}{2}}}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{\frac{n}{2}})}=-\frac{\lambda_{\frac{n+2}{2}}}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{\frac{n+2}{2}})}=\frac{2\sigma c(n)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+\frac{2}{n-1}c(n)^{2}}}={\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\,.

On the contrary, if one has
\sigma<0
, then the signs of
\lambda_{\frac{n}{2}}
and
\lambda_{\frac{n+2}{2}}
are the opposite, however the conclusion is the same:

T(n)
is asymptotically singular. Its (unstructured) distance to singularity is infinitesimal of order
\frac{1}{n}
whereas its structured distance to singularity is an infinitesimal of order
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
. Moreover, since

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\pm c(n)=0,

the entries of both the closest matrices in
{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}
to
T
in the Frobenius norm, satisfy

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\delta_{*}(n)^{\pm}=\mp\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{2(n-1)c(n)\sigma}{n-1+2nc(n)^{2}}=0,

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{*}(n)^{\pm}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{(n-1)\sigma}{n-1+2nc(n)^{2}}=\sigma.

6.2
The case
\boldsymbol{\delta}\,\mathbf{\neq 0}

If the diagonal entry of
T
in not trivial and there is not a unique eigenvalue with the smallest magnitude (i.e., if the property required by Eckart-Young
theorem is not satisfied), then on the contrary there exists a unique closest matrix in
{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}
to
T
in the Frobenius norm. This is good news. However, we are interested in analyzing the opposite case where there exists a unique closest matrix in
\mathcal{S}
to
T
in the Frobenius norm but (
16
) is not satisfied.

It is straightforward that if
\lambda_{k_{1}}
and
\lambda_{k_{2}}
are two eigenvalues with the smallest magnitude, then they are of opposite sign and
k_{1}=k_{2}\pm 1
. Moreover, one can observe that the same happens also for the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k_{1}}
and
\lambda_{k_{2}}
, with
|\lambda_{k_{1}}|\neq|\lambda_{k_{2}}|
, satisfying

(24)

\frac{|\lambda_{k_{1}}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{k_{1}})}=\frac{|\lambda_{k_{2}}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{k_{2}})}<\frac{|\lambda_{h}|}{\kappa^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})},\quad\mbox{with}\;\;h=1,\dots,n,\;\mbox{and}\;\;h\notin\{k_{1},k_{2}\}.

In more detail, since the sequence
\{|\lambda_{h}|\}_{h=1,\dots n}
first decreases and then increases, then:

– if
n
is odd, there can exist only two eigenvalues
\lambda_{k_{1}}
and
\lambda_{k_{2}}
of opposite sign, with consecutive indices both belonging either to
\{1,\dots n_{2}\}
or to
\{n_{2},\dots n\}
, where
n_{2}
denotes the smallest integer greater than
n/2
, that satisfy (
24
), because the sequence
\{1/\kappa_{F}^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})\}
increases for
h=1,\dots n_{2}
and decreases for
h=n_{2},\dots n
;

– if
n
is even, there can exist only two eigenvalues
\lambda_{k_{1}}
and
\lambda_{k_{2}}
of opposite sign, with consecutive indices both belonging either to
\{1,\dots n/2\}
or to
\{n/2+1,\dots n\}
, that satisfy (
24
), because the sequence
\{1/\kappa_{F}^{\cal T}(\lambda_{h})\}
increases for
h=1,\dots n/2
and decreases for
h=n/2+1,\dots n
.

Finally, it can happen that both the closest matrices in
{\mathcal{S}}
and in
{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}
are unique whereas the eigenvalue of smallest magnitude is not the same as the eigenvalue satisfying (
16
).

7
Numerical examples

This section provides four representative examples for the cases that have been analyzed throughout the paper and also reports remarks about the monotonicity properties of the entries of the Cholesky factor of positive definite matrices in
\cal T
that have been investigated in Subsection
5.3
(cf. Remark
7.1
) as well as a quantitative test designed to illustrate the cases relevant to indefinite matrices in
\cal T
that have been investigated in Subsection
6.2
(cf. Remark
7.2
). The four examples mentioned above address the case of positive definite test matrices (Example 1), the case where there is neither a unique closest matrix in
{\mathcal{S}}
nor in
{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}
to the test matrix (Example 2), the case where the smallest eigenvalue in magnitude is not unique but there is an eigenvalue that satisfies (
16
) (Example 3), and the case where the unique smallest eigenvalue in magnitude is not the same as the unique eigenvalue that satisfies (
16
) (Example 4).
Since the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrices are known in closed form, and all ingredients of our analysis are easily computable, assessing the theoretical results has been straightforward. All computations were carried out in MATLAB R2022b with about
16
significant decimal digits on an iMac with a 3,2 GHz Intel Core i7 6 core and equipped with 16 GB of RAM.

Example 1.

Consider

. This test matrix is, as it is well known, symmetric positive definite.

.

We report that in all tests with symmetric positive definite tridiagonal Toeplitz matrices with

8
Conclusions and future work

Given a real symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix
T
, this paper discusses the determination of its projection in the manifold of similarly structured singular matrices. While an unstructured analysis states both the same sensitivity to perturbations for each eigenvalue and the same distance to singularity of
T
if any of its eigenvalues is the closest one to the origin, on the contrary a structured analysis reveals that the more extreme the eigenvalue closest to the origin, the more unsafe the situation, in line with the relevant structured eigenvalue sensitivity. Future work concerns the analysis of the structured distance to singularity of symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz-type matrices that are obtained by suitably modifying the first and last diagonal entries of a symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix, which have eigenpairs known in closed form
[
20
]
and share with
T
the property of having eigenvectors that do not depend on the matrix entries; see
[
26
, Proposition 4.15]
.
