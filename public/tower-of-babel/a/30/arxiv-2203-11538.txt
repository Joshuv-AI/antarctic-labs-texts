Isoparametric singularity extraction technique for 3D potential problems in BEM
Tadej Kanduč

Abstract
To solve boundary integral equations for potential problems using collocation Boundary Element Method (BEM) on smooth curved 3D geometries, an analytical singularity extraction technique is employed. By adopting the isoparametric approach, curved geometries that are represented by mapped rectangles or triangles from the parametric domain are considered. The singularity extraction on the governing singular integrals can be performed either as an operation of subtraction or division, each having some advantages. A particular series expansion of a singular kernel about a source point is investigated. The series in the intrinsic coordinates consists of functions of a type R p ​ x q ​ y r R^{p}x^{q}y^{r} , where R R is a square root of a quadratic bivariate homogeneous polynomial, corresponding to the first fundamental form of a smooth surface, and p , q , r p,q,r are integers, satisfying p ≤ − 1 p\leq-1 and q , r ≥ 0 q,r\geq 0 . By extracting more terms from the series expansion of the singular kernel, the smoothness of the regularized kernel at the source point can be increased. Analytical formulae for integrals of such terms are obtained from antiderivatives of R p ​ x q ​ y r R^{p}x^{q}y^{r} , using recurrence formulae, and by evaluating them at the edges of rectangular or triangular parametric domains. Numerical tests demonstrate that the singularity extraction technique can be a useful prerequisite for a numerical quadrature scheme to obtain accurate evaluations of the governing singular integrals in 3D collocation BEM.

In this paper we study a singularity extraction for singular integrals for 3D potential problems. Focusing on isogeometric collocation BEM, curved boundary domains are represented as a set of rectangles or triangles in the parametric domain, mapped to the physical space using B-spline (or NURBS) functions. As a proof of concept, the main focus is on weakly and nearly singular integrals, appearing for Laplace problems, although the ideas could be applied to other types of integrals as well.
The singularity extraction utilizes a particular series expansion of the singular kernel about a source point in the parametric domain – to the best of author’s knowledge this type of series expansions have not been studied before in this form in BEM. No distortion of the integration domain occurs since the extraction is applied directly in the initial parametric domain; this is an important accuracy and efficiency feature since the same quadrature nodes can be used for regular and singular integrals and for several basis functions and source points. The expansion depends on the (higher) derivatives of the geometry parameterization, which are considered to be easily computable in IGA setting. For each summand in the series, multiplied with a polynomial basis function, recursive formulae to evaluate its double integral analytically are provided. The smoothness of the regularized kernel at the source point is controlled by the number of considered terms in the series. An additional constant cost to evaluate more terms can be outweighed by the higher accuracy of a quadrature for regularized integrals – this is especially a preferred trade-off for isogeometric methods with high convergence orders.
The developed singularity extraction has already been applied in BEM for Laplace problems
[

The structure of the paper is as follows. For completeness, we briefly outline the considered numerical integration schemes for the governing integrals in BIE in Section 2.3 and we provide some directions for implementation speedups for the analytical evaluation of the studied singular integrals in 2.4. In 2.5, smoothness of a more general regularized kernel is studied with respect to the two regularization techniques: subtraction and division. In Section 3 we perform tests of numerical integrations for the two common singular kernels for Laplace problems and for the two regularization techniques. In appendix we collect basic properties of functions

2
Singularity extraction

In this section we demonstrate how to apply a singularity extraction technique on weakly singular kernels for the single and double layer potential for the 3D Laplace problems (see
[
20
]
how to rewrite the kernels in Helmholtz equation). As a first step, a series expansion of the singular kernels about the source point in intrinsic coordinates is derived. A truncated series is an approximation of the singular kernel and it is used either in a singularity subtraction or in a singularity division to obtain regularized kernels. The remaining regular parts of the integrands can be locally approximated with a suitable polynomial. Therefore, the singular integrals in the underlying Boundary Integral Equations (BIE) are approximated as a finite sum of integrals, described in Section
A
. At the end of the section we also analyze the smoothness of the derived regularized kernels.

2.1
Integral formulation of the potential problem

To be concise, let us consider 3D potential problems described by the Laplace equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions on finite volumes
\Omega
with closed smooth boundary surfaces
\Gamma
,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u=0&{\rm in}\;\Omega,\\
u=u_{D}&{\rm on}\;\Gamma,\end{array}\right.

where the solution
u
belongs to the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\Omega)
and the Dirichlet boundary datum
u_{D}
is in
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
, the trace space of
H^{1}(\Omega)
. For more details we refer to
[
4
,
11
,
46
]
.

To solve (
2.1
) using the so-called direct approch we rewrite the problem into the following BIE (the Symm’s integral equation)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}{\mathcal{G}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\phi({\boldsymbol{y}})\mathop{d\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}}=\frac{1}{2}u_{D}({\boldsymbol{x}})+\int_{\Gamma}{\mathcal{H}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})u_{D}({\boldsymbol{y}})\mathop{d\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}},\qquad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Gamma,

(3)

where the function
\phi
denotes the unknown flux of
u
and belongs to
H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
, the dual space of
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
(duality is defined with respect to the usual
L^{2}(\Gamma)
-scalar product).
The weakly singular kernels for the single and the double layer potentials are

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{1}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{2}},\qquad{\mathcal{H}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\frac{\partial{\mathcal{G}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{n}}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\top}}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{2}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{n}}({\boldsymbol{y}}),

(4)

and
{\boldsymbol{n}}
denotes the outward unit normal vector.

Following the isoparametric approach, the boundary
\Gamma
is represented as a mapped domain
\hat{\Gamma}
in
{\mathop{{\rm I}\kern-1.99997pt{\rm R}}\nolimits}^{2}
,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{F}}:\hat{\Gamma}\to{\mathop{{\rm I}\kern-1.99997pt{\rm R}}\nolimits}^{3},\;{\boldsymbol{t}}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}}),

where the geometry mapping
{\boldsymbol{F}}
is described in terms of bivariate splines or NURBS
[
13
,
21
,
47
]
in parametric coordinates
{\boldsymbol{t}}:=(t_{1},t_{2})
.
By applying the collocation discretization, we search for the approximate solution
\phi_{h}
of (
3
) in the discretization space, spanned by basis functions
B_{j}:\hat{\Gamma}\to{\mathop{{\rm I}\kern-1.99997pt{\rm R}}\nolimits}
,
j=1,2,\dots,N_{\rm DOF}
,

\displaystyle\phi_{h}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm DOF}}\alpha_{j}(B_{j}\circ{\boldsymbol{F}}^{-1})({\boldsymbol{x}}).

By fixing suitable collocation sites
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
, for
i=1,2,\dots,N_{\rm DOF}
, the unknown entries in the vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N_{\rm DOF}})^{\top}
are obtained by solving a square linear system,

\displaystyle A\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\beta}.

The system matrix
A
is fully populated and its entries
a_{ij}
are

\displaystyle a_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{1}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{2}}(B_{j}\circ{\boldsymbol{F}}^{-1})({\boldsymbol{y}})\mathop{d\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\hat{\Gamma}}\frac{1}{\|{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}}_{i})-{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})\|_{2}}B_{j}({\boldsymbol{t}})J({\boldsymbol{t}})\mathop{d\boldsymbol{t}},

where
J:=\|D_{1}{\boldsymbol{F}}\times D_{2}{\boldsymbol{F}}\|_{2}

is the area of the infinitesimal surface element,
D_{1}:=\partial{\boldsymbol{F}}/{\partial t_{1}},D_{2}:=\partial{\boldsymbol{F}}/{\partial t_{2}}
, and
{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}}_{i})={\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
,
{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})={\boldsymbol{y}}
.
Entries of the right-hand side vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
are

\displaystyle\beta_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(u_{D}\circ{\boldsymbol{F}})({\boldsymbol{s}}_{i})+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\hat{\Gamma}}{\mathcal{H}}({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}),{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}}))\,J({\boldsymbol{t}})\,(u_{D}\circ{\boldsymbol{F}})({\boldsymbol{t}})\mathop{d\boldsymbol{t}}.

Integral in
a_{ij}
is classified singular if the source point
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}
is inside the support of
B_{j}
. If
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}
is outside the support but close to it, we declare the integral nearly singular. In the remaining case we say the integral is regular.

2.2
Series expansion of a singular kernel

In this subsection we derive a series expansion of kernels
{\mathcal{G}}
and
{\mathcal{H}}
for fixed source point
{\boldsymbol{s}}
. By restricting the kernel to a line segment, the singular part of the kernel is easily decoupled from the regular part – for the latter part we then apply the Taylor series expansion. The procedure shares some similarity to polar and Duffy transformation and to radial integration approach by Gao
[
25
]
, with the difference that in our case the final expression of the series is written again in the starting intrinsic coordinates. Each summand in the series is a function of a type, described in Section
A.2
, thus a closed form expression for its integral exists.

2.2.1
Series expansion of
{\mathcal{G}}

As a proof of concept we focus on the simplest kernel,
{\mathcal{G}}
in (
4
).
Let us write
{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})
,
{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})
and let us assume
{\boldsymbol{F}}
to be analytical (or sufficiently smooth) near fixed
{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})
. For a fixed
{\boldsymbol{s}}
we can express kernel
{\mathcal{G}}
,
{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{z}}):={\mathcal{G}}({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}}),{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}}))
, in local coordinates
{\boldsymbol{z}}:=(z_{1},z_{2}):={\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{t}}
. Taylor expansion of
{\boldsymbol{F}}
about
{\boldsymbol{s}}
can be compactly written as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})={\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\boldsymbol{D}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}}),\qquad{\boldsymbol{D}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}}):=\sum_{|\alpha|=i}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\alpha},\qquad{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\alpha}:=\frac{(-1)^{|\alpha|}}{\alpha!}D^{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}}).

Here we adopt the multi-index notation. Specifically,
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
,
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
,
\alpha!=\alpha_{1}!\alpha_{2}!
. The partial derivative operator
D^{\alpha}
acts on each component of a vector function
{\boldsymbol{F}}
separately and on both variables,

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}=D_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}D_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}=\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}}{\partial t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\partial t_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}}.

Also we compactly write the scalar
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\alpha}=z_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}z_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}
.

From the expression

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})-{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})=-\sum_{|\beta|\geq 1}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\beta}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\beta}

we can easily express the kernel

{\mathcal{G}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}
as a power series of
{\boldsymbol{z}}
about
(0,0)
,

\displaystyle 4\pi{\mathcal{G}}({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}}),{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})-{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})\|_{2}}=\left(\sum_{{|\alpha|\geq 2}}c_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\alpha}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(5)

where

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta+\gamma=\alpha\\
|\beta|\geq 1,|\gamma|\geq 1\end{subarray}}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\beta}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\gamma}

(6)

are the coefficients in the series expansion of
\|{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})-{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})\|_{2}^{2}
about
{\boldsymbol{s}}
in local variable
{\boldsymbol{z}}
. From (
6

is a linear combination of products of derivatives of
{\boldsymbol{F}}
, where the total degree of the derivatives is at most
|\alpha|-1
.

In an ideal world one would probably just truncate the series in (
5
) and integrate the obtained function, involving the Taylor polynomial. Unfortunately, closed forms for these types of integrals are not known in general, therefore a further transformation of the expression is needed. In the next step we derive a Taylor expansion of the regular part of (
5
), restricted to an arbitrary line
(z_{1},vz_{1})
,
v\in\mathbb{R}
. From the parameter

depended linear expansion it is relatively straightforward to derive a truncated series expansion of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
that can be integrated analytically.

Let
L_{v}:=\{(z_{1},vz_{1}),z_{1}\in(-\delta,\delta)\}
be a line segment passing the origin
(0,0)
, where
v\in\mathbb{R}
is a fixed parameter and
\delta
is sufficiently small.
With
{\mathcal{G}}_{v}
let us write the kernel
{\mathcal{G}}
, restricted on
L_{v}
and written as a function of
z_{1}
.
By multiplying the expression (
5

we obtain

\displaystyle r_{v}(z_{1}):=|z_{1}|\,4\pi{\mathcal{G}}_{v}(z_{1})=|z_{1}|\left(\sum_{|\alpha|\geq 2}c_{\alpha}z_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}(vz_{1})^{\alpha_{2}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\sum_{|\alpha|\geq 2}c_{\alpha}v^{\alpha_{2}}z_{1}^{|\alpha|-2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(7)

and the newly defined function
r_{v}
is regular (it holds
\sum_{|\alpha|=2}c_{\alpha}v^{\alpha_{2}}>0

That way we separate the singular part

of the kernel
{\mathcal{G}}_{v}
from the regular part

,

\displaystyle 4\pi{\mathcal{G}}_{v}(z_{1})=|z_{1}|^{-1}r_{v}(z_{1}).

(8)

Since

is a regular function in
z_{1}
, we can replace it with its Taylor series expansion in
z_{1}
about origin 0 using expression (
7

\displaystyle r_{v}(z_{1})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{r_{v}^{(\ell)}(0)}{\ell!}z_{1}^{\ell}=\big(P_{v,2}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\big(P_{v,2}\big)^{-\frac{3}{2}}P_{v,3}\,z_{1}+\bigg(-\big(P_{v,2}\big)^{-\frac{3}{2}}P_{v,4}+\frac{3}{4}\big(P_{v,2}\big)^{-\frac{5}{2}}\big(P_{v,3}\big)^{2}\bigg)\frac{z_{1}^{2}}{2}+\dots

(9)

where
P_{v,\ell}:=\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}c_{\alpha}v^{\alpha_{2}}
is a polynomial of degree
\ell
in
v
.

The obtained expression for
4\pi{\mathcal{G}}_{v}
is valid for any
v
, we insert back
\displaystyle{v=\frac{z_{2}}{z_{1}}}
into (
8
) using the expansion (
9
)
and get the sought series expansion of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[R({\boldsymbol{z}})^{-1}-\frac{1}{2}R({\boldsymbol{z}})^{-3}P_{3}({\boldsymbol{z}})+\left(-\frac{1}{2}R({\boldsymbol{z}})^{-3}P_{4}({\boldsymbol{z}})+\frac{3}{8}R({\boldsymbol{z}})^{-{5}}P_{3}({\boldsymbol{z}})^{2}\right)+\dots\right],

where
R({\boldsymbol{z}}):=R(c_{(2,0)},c_{(1,1)},c_{(0,2)},{\boldsymbol{z}})=(\sum_{|\alpha|=2}c_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\alpha})^{1/2}
,

is the bivariate homogeneous polynomial of degree

are defined in (
6

10

z_{1}=0
and

For the sake of easier notation and vocabulary, we consider an

, that satisfy
\zeta(R^{p}z_{1}^{q}z_{2}^{r})=\ell-2
. Indeed, compactly we could express (
10

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{\ell=1,2,\dots}R^{-2\ell+1}P_{3\ell-3}^{[\ell]},

(11)

where
P_{3\ell-3}^{[\ell]}
are appropriate homogeneous polynomials of degree

\ell

\zeta(R^{-2\ell+1}P_{3\ell-3}^{[\ell]})=\ell-2

With

we denote a kernel, obtained from

Remark 1
.

If the geometry is locally flat and parameterized by a linear map, then the cancellation of the singularity with the first term in the series expansion is exact,
\rho:={\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}-{\mathcal{G}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},1}\equiv 0

\rho:={\mathcal{G}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}/{\mathcal{G}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},1}\equiv 1

2.2.2
Series expansion of
{\mathcal{H}}

We proceed similarly as for the kernel
{\mathcal{G}}
. Since we want to express the kernel
{\mathcal{H}}
in the intrinsic coordinates from the information of the geometry mapping
{\boldsymbol{F}}
, it is more convenient to consider the kernel
\bar{\mathcal{H}}:={\mathcal{H}}J
, where
J({\boldsymbol{t}})=\|D_{1}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})\times D_{2}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})\|_{2}
,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{H}}({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}}),{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}}))={\mathcal{H}}({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}}),{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}}))\,J({\boldsymbol{t}})=\left(\frac{{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})-{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})}{\|{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})-{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})\|^{3}}\right)^{\top}(D_{1}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})\times D_{2}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})).

(12)

The inner product of the two vectors in (

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{H}}({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}}),{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}}))=\frac{\varepsilon_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\big({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})-{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})\big)_{\ell_{1}}\big(D_{1}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})\big)_{\ell_{2}}\big(D_{2}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})\big)_{\ell_{3}}}{\|{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})-{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})\|^{3}},

where we use Levi-Civita symbol

By fixing the source points

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})-{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{|\beta|\geq 1}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\beta}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\beta},

\displaystyle D_{1}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})

\displaystyle=D_{1}\bigg({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})+\sum_{|\gamma|\geq 1}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\gamma}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\gamma}\bigg)=-\sum_{|\gamma|\geq 0}(\gamma_{1}+1){\boldsymbol{a}}_{(\gamma_{1}+1,\gamma_{2})}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\gamma},

\displaystyle D_{2}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{t}})

\displaystyle=D_{2}\bigg({\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{s}})+\sum_{|\delta|\geq 1}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\delta}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\delta}\bigg)=-\sum_{|\delta|\geq 0}(\delta_{2}+1){\boldsymbol{a}}_{(\delta_{1},\delta_{2}+1)}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\delta},

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{{|\alpha|\geq 2}}d_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\alpha}}{\displaystyle\left(\sum_{{|\alpha|\geq 2}}c_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}},

\displaystyle d_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta+\gamma+\delta=\alpha\\
|\beta|\geq 1,|\gamma|\geq 0,|\delta|\geq 0\end{subarray}}\varepsilon_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\big({\boldsymbol{a}}_{\beta}\big)_{\ell_{1}}\big((\gamma_{1}+1){\boldsymbol{a}}_{(\gamma_{1}+1,\gamma_{2})}\big)_{\ell_{2}}\big((\delta_{2}+1){\boldsymbol{a}}_{(\delta_{1},\delta_{2}+1)}\big)_{\ell_{3}}.

Note that

\displaystyle d_{(1,0)}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\big({\boldsymbol{a}}_{(1,0)}\big)_{\ell_{1}}\big({\boldsymbol{a}}_{(1,0)}\big)_{\ell_{2}}\big({\boldsymbol{a}}_{(0,1)}\big)_{\ell_{3}}=\left|\begin{matrix}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{(1,0)}&{\boldsymbol{a}}_{(1,0)}&{\boldsymbol{a}}_{(0,1)}\end{matrix}\right|=0,

\displaystyle d_{(0,1)}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\big({\boldsymbol{a}}_{(0,1)}\big)_{\ell_{1}}\big({\boldsymbol{a}}_{(1,0)}\big)_{\ell_{2}}\big({\boldsymbol{a}}_{(0,1)}\big)_{\ell_{3}}=\left|\begin{matrix}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{(0,1)}&{\boldsymbol{a}}_{(1,0)}&{\boldsymbol{a}}_{(0,1)}\end{matrix}\right|=0,

since in both cases the involved three vectors are linearly dependent.

Again, let
L_{v}:=\{(z_{1},vz_{1}),z_{1}\in(-\delta,\delta)\}

,

\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{v}

Then

2.3
Numerical integration scheme

By utilizing the kernel expansion from the previous subsection we can now focus on numerical integration of integrals in the Symm’s integral equation (
3
).
In this section we consider a more general singular kernel
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
that can be written as a similar series expansion and two regularization approaches are introduced.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}={\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
be a singular kernel, expressed about a source point
{\boldsymbol{s}}
in local intrinsic coordinates
{\boldsymbol{z}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}=\sum_{\ell=1,2,\dots}R^{-2(\ell+m_{1})+1}P_{3\ell+m_{2}}^{[\ell]},

(19)

with regularity
m:=\zeta({\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}})=-2m_{1}+m_{2}+2
and integers
m_{2}\geq-3
,
m_{1}\geq 0
. We assume
m<0
and smaller
m
corresponds to a stronger type of singularity. Here
P_{3\ell+m_{2}}^{[\ell]}
are again suitable homogeneous polynomials of degree
3\ell+m_{2}
.
Additionally, let
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
be sufficiently smooth for
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in D_{\varepsilon}
, where

\displaystyle D_{\varepsilon}:=\{{\boldsymbol{z}}:0<\|{\boldsymbol{z}}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\}

is a punctured disk for some fixed
\varepsilon>0
. Let
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}
be an approximation of
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
by truncating the infinite series (
19
) after the
n
-th term,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}=\sum_{\ell=1,2,\dots,n}R^{-2(\ell+m_{1})+1}P_{3\ell+m_{2}}^{[\ell]}.

(20)

The singular kernel
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
is either regularized by the subtraction, i.e.,
\rho:={\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}-{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}
, or the division, i.e.,
\rho:={\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}/{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}
. Our goal is to numerically compute integrals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{I_{1}}\int_{I_{2}}{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{z}})B_{j}({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}})v({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}})\mathop{d\boldsymbol{z}},

(21)

where
B_{j}
is a polynomial basis function and
v
is an auxiliary smooth function that can include for example the area of the infinitesimal surface element
J
or a boundary datum.

Case 1:

If the integral (
21
) is regular, we can apply one of the common numerical integration schemes since the integrand is a smooth function.

Case 2:

If the integral (
21
) is singular or nearly singular, the following transformation needs to be applied first.

(a)

The first strategy, which is more common in literature, is the singularity subtraction. By writing
\rho:={\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}-{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}
, the integral (
21
) is expressed as a sum of two integrals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{I_{1}}\int_{I_{2}}\rho({\boldsymbol{z}})B_{j}({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}})v({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}})+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{I_{1}}\int_{I_{2}}{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}({\boldsymbol{z}})B_{j}({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}})v({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}})\mathop{d\boldsymbol{z}}.

(22)

The integrand in the first integral in (
22
) is sufficiently regular if the geometry parameterization (hence
v
) and
\rho
are sufficiently regular. The smoothness of
\rho
is studied in the next subsection. Thus, for this type of integrals we can apply a quadrature scheme for regular integrals. In the second integral in (
22
) the regular part
B_{j}({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}})v({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}})
can be approximated sufficiently well with an appropriate polynomial
P
via least square fitting, quasi-interpolation etc.

(b)

In the regularization process via division step we consider
\rho:={\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}/{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}
and express the integral (
21
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{I_{1}}\int_{I_{2}}{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}({\boldsymbol{z}})B_{j}({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}})v({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}})\rho({\boldsymbol{z}})\mathop{d\boldsymbol{z}}.

(23)

The regular part of the integrand,
B_{j}({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}})v({\boldsymbol{s}}-{\boldsymbol{z}})\rho({\boldsymbol{z}})
, is again approximated with a suitable polynomial
P
.

In both strategies (a), (b), the singular part of the integral (
21
) is approximated with

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{I_{1}}\int_{I_{2}}{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}({\boldsymbol{z}})P({\boldsymbol{z}})\mathop{d\boldsymbol{z}}

(24)

for a suitable polynomial
P
. The integral (
24
) can be expressed as a sum of fundamental integrals, presented in Section
A.2
.

Remark 2
.

In (
21
), instead of a polynomial basis we can proceed similarly if we consider
B_{j}
to play a role of a polynomial spline basis function. In that case we can decompose the basis function into a sum of polynomial contributions (for example using a Bézier extraction technique) and from thereon follow a similar procedure as before.

Alternatively, we can approximate the smooth part of the integrand of (
21
) with a spline function and write the product of the two splines as a new spline function, for example by using formulae in
[
41
]
for the tensor product basis functions. For a successful implementation of this idea to solve Laplace and Helmholtz boundary integral equations in BEM see
[
16
,
18
,
20
]
. The model combines a spline quasi-interpolation operator
[
38
,
39
]
, together with a spline recurrence relation formula
[
20
]
to compute the integrals (
24
), where
P
plays a role of a polynomial spline function.

2.4
Implementation speedup

The presented singularity extraction technique can only be considered useful in practice if it can be efficiently implemented in software. For example, to perform a simulation using BEM, the matrix formation can induce a computation of thousands or millions of integrals of the type (
24
) and it can represent the main bottleneck in the system matrix formation. However, since the evaluation of these integrals for different source points is embarrassingly parallelizable, the time complexity of this step can be partially alleviated. In this subsection we propose an additional reduction of the computational complexity by accessing specific precomputed integrals from lookup tables.

By exchanging the order of summation and integration in (
24
), and writing each polynomial
P
as a weighted sum of monomials (the weights depend on the local geometry around the point
{\boldsymbol{s}}
and can be efficiently computed in a preprocess step), we are left to analyze how to efficiently integrate functions
R^{p}x^{q}y^{r}
. Unfortunately, analytical formulae for the indefinite integrals (
32
) can have very long symbolic expressions that can result in undesired lengthy evaluation, especially for higher values of
-p+q+r
. To overcome this problem, we apply several simplifications of the expressions to reduce the number of parameters, influencing the integral values, and precompute values of particular simpler integrals. We can later on access these values in the stored lookup tables to compute the definite integrals.

2.5
Smoothness of the regularized kernels

The smoothness of the regularized kernel
\rho
at
{\boldsymbol{s}}
increases by one if
n
is increased by one. First we consider the more common singularity subtraction technique, where the analysis is very straightforward.

Theorem 3
.

For a singular kernel
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
, defined in (
19
), let
\rho={\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}-{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}
be the regularized kernel with
m+n\geq 1
. Then
\rho
is a
C^{m+n-1}
smooth function at
{\boldsymbol{z}}=0
and
D^{\alpha}\rho=0
for
0\leq|\alpha|\leq m+n-1
.

Proof.

Let us write
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}={\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}+\tau_{n}
, where
\tau_{n}
is the corresponding tail in the series expansion of
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
. Then
\rho=\tau_{n}
and from (
19
) it is clear that
\zeta(\rho)\geq m+n
. Apply
Proposition
12
and
13
and the proof is complete.

∎

For the regularization by the division step we need to additionally assume
P_{m_{2}+3}^{[1]}({\boldsymbol{z}})\neq 0
for
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in D_{\varepsilon}
. This condition is needed to not introduce new singularities in the regularized kernel
\rho
. In the next proposition we locally assume
P_{m_{2}+3}^{[1]}>0
but an analogous statement can be done for
P_{m_{2}+3}^{[1]}<0
.

Corollary 4
.

Let
P_{m_{2}+3}^{[1]}>0
for
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in D_{\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
such that
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}({\boldsymbol{z}})>0
for every
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in D_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Write

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}R^{-2(\ell+m_{1})+1}P_{3\ell+m_{2}}^{[\ell]}=R^{-2(n+m_{1})+1}\left(R^{2n-2}P_{m_{2}+3}^{[1]}+\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}R^{2(n-\ell)}P_{3\ell+m_{2}}^{[\ell]}\right).

(27)

Function
R^{-2(n+m_{1})+1}
is positive, thus it is enough to analyze the remaining factor. Homogeneous polynomial
R^{2n-2}P_{m_{2}+3}^{[1]}
is of degree
2n+m_{2}+1
and positive for
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in D_{\varepsilon}
. Each polynomial in the remaining sum is of degree greater than
2n+m_{2}+1
, thus it decays to zero faster than
R^{2n-2}P_{m_{2}+3}^{[1]}
, when
{\boldsymbol{z}}
goes to
\boldsymbol{0}
. We can deduce that the kernel
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}
is positive in
D_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
for sufficiently small
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
.

∎

Remark 5
.

The additional condition
P_{m_{2}+3}^{[1]}\neq 0
is clearly an notable drawback of the regularization via division, since not all kernels are necessary of the same sign near the source point
{\boldsymbol{s}}
, e.g., kernel
{\mathcal{H}}
. For these types of kernel the division approach cannot be directly applied in this simple form.

The case when
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{z}})\neq 0
for all
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in D_{\varepsilon}
but
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}({\boldsymbol{z}})=0
for some
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in D_{\varepsilon}
is far less problematic. For example, by adding a simple correction term
\eta\|{\boldsymbol{z}}\|_{2}^{2}
to
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
for a suitable
\eta
, we can enforce function
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}({\boldsymbol{z}})+\eta\|{\boldsymbol{z}}\|_{2}^{2}
to not vanish inside
D_{\varepsilon}
. Namely,
\eta>0
if
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}>0
, and
\eta<0
if
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}<0
.

Despite the mentioned drawbacks, probably the main advantage of the division technique is the improved smoothness of
\rho
when compared to the subtraction splitting for the same amount of regularization terms. Furthermore, the gap between the approaches is even bigger for kernels
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
with stronger singularity. Indeed, for the same number of terms
n
the smoothness of
\rho
is the same, regardless of the type of singularity, when the regularization via division step is used.

The following lemma is needed before proving the theorem for the smoothness of
\rho
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and let

\displaystyle p_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\sum_{|\alpha|=m}c_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\alpha}

be a bivariate homogeneous polynomial of degree
m
, such that
p_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})>0
for
\|{\boldsymbol{z}}\|_{2}<\varepsilon
. Then

1/({R^{\ell}p_{m}})

is a continuous function at
{\boldsymbol{z}}=\boldsymbol{0}
if
\zeta({R^{\ell}p_{m}})=\ell+m\leq-1
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Proposition
12
but considering a negative parameter
\zeta
instead of a positive one.

Fix a sufficiently small
\delta>0
. Let us show that there exists a small enough
\varepsilon
,
0<\varepsilon<1
, such that for
\|{\boldsymbol{z}}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
it follows
1/({R({\boldsymbol{z}})^{\ell}p_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})})\leq\delta
.

Let us again consider only the case
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in T_{1}
(see (
37
)), since the proof for the other subdomains follows similarly. We can write

z_{2}=\gamma z_{1}
for
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
.
From Lemma
10
we can infer that there exists a positive constant
C_{abc\ell,1}
such that

C_{abc\ell,1}\leq({a+b\gamma+c\gamma^{2}})^{\ell/2}
for all
\gamma
. From the assumptions we know that
p_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})=z_{1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|=m}c_{\alpha}\gamma^{\alpha_{2}}>0
for
z_{1}>0
, thus there exists a positive constant
C
such that
C\leq\sum_{|\alpha|=m}c_{\alpha}\gamma^{\alpha_{2}}
. By combining all the derived inequalities we can show that

\displaystyle R({\boldsymbol{z}})^{\ell}p_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})={(a+b\gamma+c\gamma^{2})}^{\ell/2}z_{1}^{\ell+m}\sum_{|\alpha|=m}c_{\alpha}\gamma^{\alpha_{2}}\geq C_{abc\ell,1}\,C\,z_{1}^{\ell+m}

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R({\boldsymbol{z}})^{\ell}p_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})}\leq C_{abc\ell,1}^{-1}\,C^{-1}\,z_{1}\leq C_{abc\ell,1}^{-1}\,C^{-1}\,\varepsilon.

By setting
\varepsilon\leq C_{abc\ell,1}\,C\,\delta
the proof is complete.

∎

Theorem 7
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},n}
be an approximation of
{\mathcal{K}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
, defined in (
19
), with

Proof.

Conclusion

The singularity extraction is studied for singular integrals in BIE for 3D potential problems on smooth geometries, focusing on the single and and double layer operator. Integrals of truncated series expansions of singular kernels are computed via recurrence formulae – to reduce the complexity of this process, a speedup via lookup tables that exploits correlated variables is provided.

Numerical tests demonstrate that the presented extraction technique can be a useful prerequisite for a numerical quadrature to recover the optimal order of convergence of the approximate solution in BEM with a small number of quadrature nodes. Since the extraction acts directly on the starting parametric domain, no modification of integration domain is needed – this is an advantageous property since the same quadrature nodes can be reused for several integrals involving neighbouring trial basis functions. The truncation error of the numerical integration rule can be attributed solely to the truncation error of approximating the regular part of the integrand; thus the accuracy of the rule can be effectively controlled by the number of terms in the series extraction and by the choice of the approximation operator for regular functions. Singularity subtraction and division give comparable results; the technique via division is arguably slightly more efficient since the governing integral does not need to be split into two integrals, however it cannot directly handle kernels that change signs.

Interesting topics for future work include a study of other kernels with stronger types of singularities and of non-stationary nature. Efficient implementation for computing singular integrals via lookup tables is beyond the scope of this paper, but it should be investigated in future work. Finding better ways to approximate kernels across non-smooth interfaces of the geometry, for examples on multipatch domains, could improve accuracy of the integration routines on more complex geometries.
