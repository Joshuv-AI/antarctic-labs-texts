Scouring Parrondo’s Paradox in Discrete-Time Quantum Walks
Gururaj Kadiri

Abstract
We propose a quantum game based on coin-based quantum walks. Given a quantum walk and a Hermitian operator on the coin-position composite space, winning this game involves choosing an initial coin state such that the given quantum walk leads to a composite state in which the expectation value of the given Hermitian operator is greater than a certain value. Parrondo’s paradox is a phenomenon where a combination of losing strategies becomes a winning strategy. We give a deterministic scheme for identifying Parrondo’s paradox in our game, in the sense that, given a collection of distinct quantum steps, we identify initial coin states which happen to be losing states for all quantum walks comprising solely of these steps individually, but turn out to be winning states for a quantum walk comprising of all the given steps taken in a sequence. Unlike traditional quantum steps that allow for equal magnitude forward and backward strides based on the outcome of the coin-toss, the steps of the quantum walks employed here, though still contingent upon coin-toss, permit the strides to be of unequal magnitude, and not necessarily in opposite directions. We believe the results presented here will contribute to a deeper understanding of evolution of expectation values of observables in quantum walks, and facilitate the development of novel quantum algorithms.



I
Introduction

Game theory is an interdisciplinary framework that analyzes strategic interactions among rational decision-makers. This mathematical discipline provides a systematic approach to understanding decision-making in competitive situations and provides a robust framework for optimizing individual actions
Owen 2013
;
Maschler et al. 2020
;
Kolokoltsov and Malafeyev 2020
. Parrondo’s paradox is a seemingly counter-intuitive phenomenon presented in this theory, wherein two games, both unfavorable when played individually, can become favorable when played together in a particular sequence
Parrondo et al. 2000
;
Harmer and Abbott 1999
;
Lai and Cheong 2020a
;
Abbott 2010
.
This captivating paradox has found applications in fields as diverse as dynamical systems
Canovas and Munoz 2013
;
Danca et al. 2014
;
Arena et al. 2003
, control theory
Allison and Abbott 2001
, sociology
Lai and Cheong 2020b
;
Lai and Cheong 2024
, biology
Cheong et al. 2019
;
Cheong et al. 2020
, financial markets
Bassi and Ferrari 2011
, to name a few. To elucidate the concept of this paradox in a straightforward manner, let’s examine the scenario of utilizing an escalator to ascend to the top floor, starting at the middle. Assume we have at our disposal two escalators A and B, which are such that escalator A always moves down one step at every unit of time, and escalator B moves two steps up and three steps down in alternate units of time. Evidently, both the escalators are unfavorable in the long run, in the sense that neither of them can lead us to the top of the floor in a finite amount of time. However, consider the choice of alternating the use of two escalators: that is, using the escalator B during the times in which it is moving up, and using escalator A during the time in which the escalator B is moving down. This switching strategy will lead to a net movement of one step forward in two units of time, eventually taking us to the top floor. This example provides a simple illustration of the Parrondo’s paradox which can be termed as “winning by alternating between two losing strategies”.

Quantum games are games where players have access to quantum resources like entanglement and superposition, which influence their strategies and payoffs. These games often played using qubits as players and quantum operations as moves. In this context, quantum game theory refers to the conceptual tool for exploring how the availability of quantum resources affect strategic decision-making and cooperative behavior
Flitney and Abbott 2002
;
Khan et al. 2018
;
Eisert et al. 1999
;
Du et al. 2002
. Parrondo’s paradox, already challenging classical rationality by demonstrating unexpected gains from seemingly losing strategies, takes on a new dimension within the quantum game regime, since these games enable interference between probability amplitudes across different game paths, as against classical Parrondo games, where probabilities simply add up.

Quantum walks refer to the theoretical framework for describing the movement of quantum particles through a discrete or continuous space based on the principles of quantum mechanics
Portugal 2013
. These walks are the quantum-mechanical counterpart to the ubiquitous classical walks, and are one of the most-suited platforms for demonstrating the essential quantum phenomena like superposition, post-measurement collapse etc
Mackay et al. 2002
;
Venegas-Andraca 2012
. Quantum walks have been shown to be a paradigm for quantum computation
Lovett et al. 2010
;
Singh et al. 2021
;
Asaka et al. 2023
, and they have proven to be a powerful tool in the quantum algorithm designer’s toolkit, enabling the creation of algorithms that can outperform classical ones for certain class of problems
Kendon 2006
;
Childs et al. 2003
;
Apers et al. 2022
;
Lovett et al. 2019
.
These walks have been experimentally demonstrated in different hardware platforms like circuit qed, linear optics, trapped ions etc
Zhou et al. 2019
;
Su et al. 2019
;
Giordani et al. 2019
;
Matjeschk et al. 2012
.

This paper explores the conditions necessary for the manifestation of Parrondo’s paradox in some single-player games designed within the context of a specific variant of quantum walks, called the discrete-time quantum walks (DTQWs). These walks play out on the tensor product of two vector spaces: a two-dimensional vector space called the coin space, and a
k
-dimensional space, called the position space. In a typical DTQW, the walker starts from the position zero in a definite coin state. At every step, she proceeds forward or backwards by one unit conditional to the outcome of a coin-toss operator, which can be modeled as an unitary operator on the coin-space. Since the coin-toss operation is not followed by a measurement, in one quantum step the quantum state evolves in both forward and backward directions coherently on the position space. A quantum walk corresponds to an application of multiple number of such quantum steps. The outcome of such a quantum walk is an entangled quantum state, with the probability amplitude spread over multiple positions.

One of the earliest references to the Parrondo’s paradox in DTQWs is in ref.
Flitney et al. 2004
, where a model of DTQW that depends on the outcomes of previous coin tosses is shown to display the Parrondo’s paradox. This paradox was demonstrated in discrete walks employing three-sided and four-sided, time-dependent coins and also aperodic sequence of coins
Walczak and Bauer 2022
;
Lai et al. 2020
;
Pires and Queirós 2020
;
Walczak and Bauer 2021
. The possibility of realizing this paradox in the Bose-Einstein condensate is examined in
Trautmann et al. 2022
. The relation of this paradox to the coin-position entanglement in DTQWs has been studied in
Panda et al. 2022
and
Fang et al. 2023
, and the ability of noise to induce Parrondo’s paradox in DTQWs was studied in ref.
Walczak and Bauer 2023
.
A proposal to deploy a quantum coin toss for encryption has been proposed in
Lai and Cheong 2021
, and an experimental demonstration of this paradox in DTQWs within the quantum optics scenario is presented in ref
Jan et al. 2020
.

Before discussing the contribution of this paper, it is important to briefly elucidate the general scheme that a majority of these papers have adopted for demonstrating this paradox. Owing to the translationally invariant action of a quantum walk, to every state on the coin-space one can associate a unique state on the composite space. This state is the outcome of initiating the quantum walk from the zero-position in that coin state. In the context of quantum games on DTQWs, a coin state was termed a winning state if in its associated composite state the probability of finding the walker at positive positions exceeds that at negative positions. The investigation into Parrondo’s paradox in discrete-time quantum walks commences with two distinct quantum steps. From these steps, two quantum walks having identical number of steps are constructed, each consisting of one kind of steps. Subsequently, a third quantum walk is built, having the same number of steps as the former two, but built out of both the steps applied in a specific sequence. Evidently, a coin state could be either a winning or losing state for each of the three walks separately. Now, a coin-state is said to display the “Parrondo effect” or “Parrondo paradox” if it is a losing state for the former two walks, but happens to be a winning state for the third walk. This is in line with the standard interpretation of Parrondo’s paradox, since for such coin states the former two walks lead to a loss, but the third walk, even though comprised of same steps as the former two, leads to a winning. It is, as it were, these coin states employ two losing quantum steps in a specific sequence and win the game.

In most of the papers demonstrating the Parrondo’s paradox in discrete time quantum walks, the paradox is illustrated for some choice of quantum step pairs and for some initial coin states, see for instance ref.
Lai and Cheong 2020c
;
Jan et al. 2023
;
Chandrashekar and Banerjee 2011
;
Rajendran and Benjamin 2018
. To the best of our knowledge, there isn’t any literature on how to identifying all the coin states that manifest the Parrondo paradox for a given pair of arbitrary quantum steps. In this backdrop, the contribution of this paper is the following: given a pair of quantum steps, we give a specific scheme for identifying all the coin-states which demonstrate the Parrondo’s paradox. Actually, here we go much further, as listed below:

1.

We employ a more generalized definition of quantum steps and quantum walks.

2.

We propose a generalization of winning quantum games, in the context of discrete time quantum walks.

3.

We define Parrondo’s paradox in the context of multiple such quantum steps rather than two as done currently in the literature.

While novel, it will be argued subsequently that we have expanded these definitions seamlessly from their conventional counterparts, staying true to the original.

In relation to the first point, in this paper we employ quantum steps which could be biased in the forward or backward directions. These quantum steps are in-spirit similar to these employed in the split-step quantum walks
Kitagawa et al. 2010
;
Narimatsu et al. 2021
;
Matsuzawa 2020
. Like the standard quantum steps, our quantum steps also act in a translationally invariant manner conditional to a coin-toss, except that their stride-lengths in forward and backward directions not identical in magnitude. Indeed, the definition of quantum walks employed here encompasses even such steps wherein the walker moves along the same direction (forward or backward), but with different stride lengths depending on the output of the coin toss.

With respect to the second point of defining a new winning criterion, we do it as follows. We begin by choosing an observable on the coin-position composite space. Given this observable as a Hermitian operator, to every coin we associate a real number called its “payoff”, which is defined as the expectation value of the Hermitian operator in its associated composite state. Evidently, the payoff of a coin-state depends both on the observable and the quantum walk. Now, a coin-state is considered a winning state if its payoff is greater than a certain real number, which we call as the “target payoff”. This definition of winning includes the traditional definition as well. This is due to the existence of an observable in the position space, the expected value of which equals the difference between the probabilities of occupying positive and negative positions in that state. Another example of an operator on the position space is the position operator, whose expectation value in a certain state gives the mean position. With this operator, and taking the target payoff as zero, the winning coin states will happen to be those which lead to a composite state where in the mean position is positive.

With respect to the third point, we shall demonstrate the Parrondo paradox over a collection of multiple number of quantum steps, rather than just two as is traditionally done.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section [
II
] we set-up the required theoretical and mathematical tools to be used for the rest of the section. We describe here the conventional quantum steps and quantum walks, and the notion of translational invariance of quantum states. we then introduce the novel quantum steps that we have conceived, and discuss their action. We also compare the contrast the two types of quantum steps. In Section [
III
] we give mathematical definition of the quantum games, winning them and the notion of Parrondo’s paradox in these games. Section [
IV
] constitutes the central contribution of this paper. Here, given a collection of quantum steps, we give a deterministic scheme for identifying the Parrondo states for the collection of walks for a given Hermitian operator on the composite space. These quantum steps could be standard quantum steps or the novel quantum steps introduced in this paper. In Section [
V
], we demonstrate a scheme for realizing Parrondo paradox explicitly using the biased quantum steps defined here. In Section [
VI
], we provide a few numerical illustrations of the proposals given in the earlier sections. We conclude the paper by summarizing the central results in Section [
VII
].

II
Theoretical Background

We shall indicate the coin and position spaces by the symbol
H_{c}
and
H_{p}
respectively, and the composite space by
H\equiv H_{c}\otimes H_{p}
.

II.1
Definition of the quantum states

An arbitrary pure state of the coin space
H_{c}
is given by:

\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle=s_{0}\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle+s_{1}\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle,

(1)

where
s_{0}
and
s_{1}
are complex numbers
\left|s_{0}\right|^{2}+\left|s_{1}\right|^{2}=1
, and
\{\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle,\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle\}
constitute an orthogonal basis of
H_{c}
. Given a pure state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
of the above form, we associate with it another pure state
\left|\bm{s}_{\perp}\right\rangle
, defined as:

\left|\bm{s}_{\perp}\right\rangle=\bar{s_{0}}\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle-\bar{s_{1}}\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle,

(2)

where the overbar indicates complex conjugation, as its orthogonal state. A mixed state of the coin space can be written as the density matrix

\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}=r\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{s}\right|+(1-r)\left|\bm{s}_{\perp}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{s}_{\perp}\right|

(3)

where
r
is a fraction,
0\leq r\leq 1
.
Given a density matrix of the form Eq. (
3
), we associate a vector in 3D, called here as the “qubit vector”, defined as

\vec{\bm{S}}(\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}})=(\textbf{tr}(\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}\hat{\sigma}_{x}),\textbf{tr}(\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}\hat{\sigma}_{y}),\textbf{tr}(\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}\hat{\sigma}_{z}))

(4)

where
\hat{\sigma_{i}},\,i=x,y,z
are the
2\times 2
operators defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma_{x}}

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{1}\right|+\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{0}\right|,

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma_{y}}

\displaystyle=i(\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{0}\right|-\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{1}\right|),\text{ and }

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma_{z}}

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{0}\right|-\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{1}\right|.

The definition of qubit vector in the case of a pure state gets simplified to:

\vec{\bm{S}}(\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle)=\left(\langle{\bm{s}}|{\hat{\sigma_{x}}|\bm{s}}\rangle,\langle{\bm{s}}|{\hat{\sigma_{y}}|\bm{s}}\rangle,\langle{\bm{s}}|{\hat{\sigma_{z}}|\bm{s}}\rangle\right)

(6)

When the quantum state is expressed without regard to the global phase, we represent the state by the symbol
\left|\bm{\theta,\phi}\right\rangle
:

\left|\bm{\theta,\phi}\right\rangle=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle+e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle

(7)

For the position space, we assume a privileged orthonormal basis
\{\left|m\right\rangle,m=-N,\cdots,N\}
, where
N
is potentially infinite, and in numerical simulations here we take it to be larger than any position we access in a quantum walks. Localized pure-states in the coin-position composite space are of the form

\left|\bm{s};m\right\rangle=\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle\otimes\left|m\right\rangle

(8)

A general composite pure-state is a superposition of these localized states as :

\left|\bm{S}\right\rangle\equiv\sum_{m=b}^{m=e}s_{m}\left|\bm{s}_{m};m\right\rangle

(9)

where
s_{m}
are real numbers such that
\sum_{m}s_{m}^{2}=1
, and
b
and
e\geq b
are integers, indicating the span of positions involved in the superposition. The states
\left|\bm{s}_{m};m\right\rangle
are localized states of the form Eq. (
8
). The sifted state and orthogonal states of
\left|\bm{S}\right\rangle
, indicated by
\left|\bm{S}_{+d}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{S}_{\perp}\right\rangle
respectively, are given by

\displaystyle\left|\bm{S}_{+d}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=b}^{m=e}s_{m}\left|\bm{s}_{m};m+d\right\rangle\text{ and }

(10)

\displaystyle\left|\bm{S}_{\perp}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=b}^{m=e}s_{m}\left|{(\bm{s}_{m})}_{\perp};-m\right\rangle

It is easy to see that
\left|\bm{S}_{\perp}\right\rangle
is orthogonal to the state
\left|\bm{S}\right\rangle
.
We define a composite state
\left|\bm{W}\right\rangle
of the form Eq. (
9
) as being translationally invariant
Kadiri 2023
if it satisfies the following orthogonality condition:

\langle{\bm{W}}|{\bm{W}_{+d}}\rangle=0,\,\forall d=1,\cdots,e-b.

(11)

A Hermitian operator
\hat{O}
on the composite space
H_{c}\otimes H_{p}
can be written as:

\hat{O}=\sum_{i=1}^{2(2N+1)}\lambda_{i}\left|\bm{U}_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{U}_{i}\right|,

(12)

where
\lambda_{i}
are real numbers, and
\{\left|\bm{U}_{i}\right\rangle,i=1,\cdots,2(2N+1)\}
is an orthogonal basis on the composite space, with each
\left|\bm{U}_{i}\right\rangle
being of the form Eq. (
9
). A density matrix is on the composite space is given by:

\hat{\rho}=\sum_{i=1}^{2(2N+1)}r_{i}\left|\bm{W}_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{W}_{i}\right|

(13)

where
r_{i}
are real numbers between
0
and
1
, such that
\sum_{n}r_{n}=1
, and
\{\left|\bm{W}_{i}\right\rangle,i=1,\cdots,2(2N+1)\}
is some orthogonal basis on the composite space.

II.2
Conventional quantum steps and quantum walks

We denote an arbitrary quantum step by the symbol
\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
, where
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma
are real numbers, with
0\leq\alpha,\gamma<2\pi
and
0\leq\beta\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
. This quantum step is defined in terms of two sub-steps as:

\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\hat{\mathcal{S}}(\hat{\mathit{c}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\otimes\hat{I}_{w}),

(14)

where
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
and
\hat{\mathit{c}}
are called the shift and coin-toss operator respectively, defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\left(\left|\bm{0};n-1\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{0};n\right|+\left|\bm{1};n+1\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{1};n\right|\right),

(15)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathit{c}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{i\alpha}\cos\beta&-e^{-i\gamma}\sin\beta\\
e^{i\gamma}\sin\beta&e^{-i\alpha}\cos\beta\end{array}\right).

Here
\hat{\mathit{c}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is an SU(2) operator
Chandrashekar et al. 2008
acting on the coin-space alone, while
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
acts on position-space alone, but conditional to the coin state. A collection of such steps is defined as a quantum walk.
These quantum walks however cannot generate all possible translationally invariant quantum states from home-states. That is, not all translationally invariant states can be generated by employing such quantum steps alone. A simplest illustration is the two position state
\left|\bm{S}\right\rangle=s_{m}\left|\bm{s}_{m};m\right\rangle+s_{n}\left|\bm{s}_{n};n\right\rangle
with
m\neq n
, the coin-state
\left|\bm{s}_{m}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{s}_{n}\right\rangle
being orthogonal:
\langle{\bm{s}_{m}}|{\bm{s}_{n}}\rangle=0
, and
s_{m}
and
s_{n}
are non-zero complex numbers such that
s_{m}^{2}+s_{n}^{2}=1
. This state is a composite state spread over two positions
\left|m\right\rangle
and
\left|n\right\rangle
. Now, it is easy to see that in spite of being a translationally-invariant state, if
m
and
n
are of opposite parities, no quantum walk comprising of steps of the form form Eq. (
14
) can generate it from some home-state.

A more general definition of unbiased quantum steps,
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\Delta}(\delta;\bm{c})
, a quantum walk of which can realize any translationally invariant quantum state, is given in terms of its action on orthogonal pair of product states:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\Delta}(\delta;\bm{c})\left|\bm{0};g\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\Delta}e^{i\delta}\left|\bm{c}_{\perp};g\right\rangle+\sqrt{\Delta}\left|\bm{c};g+1\right\rangle,

(16)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\Delta}(\delta;\bm{c})\left|\bm{1};g\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{1-\Delta}e^{-i\delta}\left|\bm{c};g\right\rangle+\sqrt{\Delta}\left|\bm{c}_{\perp};g-1\right\rangle.

This quantum step is such that only a fraction
\sqrt{\Delta}
of the amplitude participates in the walk. The remaining amplitude
\sqrt{1-\Delta}
does not see any change in the position, but undergoes only a change of the coin-state.
Any composite state of the form Eq. (
9
), if it is translationally invariant (that is, satisfies Eq. (
11
)), can be generated from some home-state using only these kind of steps
Kadiri 2023
.

II.3
Definition of novel quantum steps

As discussed in the earlier section, in this paper we shall employ a new class of quantum steps, instead of
\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
or
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\Delta}(\delta;\bm{c})
. We indicate these by the symbol
\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})
, where
p
and
q
are integers, and
\bm{s}
and
\bm{c}
indicate two states
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{c}\right\rangle
on the coin-space respectively. The action of this quantum step on the orthogonal home-states is as:

\displaystyle\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})\left|\bm{s};g\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{c};g+p\right\rangle,

(17)

\displaystyle\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})\left|\bm{s}_{\perp};g\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{c}_{\perp};g+q\right\rangle.

This quantum step moves the walker
p
units ahead from the current position if the coin state is
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
, and
q
units ahead from the current position if the coin state is
\left|\bm{s}_{\perp}\right\rangle
, while simultaneously transforming the coin state to
\left|\bm{c}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{c}_{\perp}\right\rangle
, respectively. We shall consider Eq. (
17
) itself as the definition of the quantum step
\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})
. The action of this step on any other product state
\left|\bm{w};g\right\rangle
can be obtained by expressing
\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle
in the {
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle,\left|\bm{s}_{\perp}\right\rangle
} basis, and acting
\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})
on the superposition. Likewise, the action of
\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})
on an arbitrary composite state
\left|\bm{S}\right\rangle
of the form Eq. (
9
) can be obtained by acting it linearly on each of the terms
\left|\bm{s}_{m};m\right\rangle
.
The step acts in a translationally invariant manner, since the definition of Eq. (
17
) is defined identically for all
\left|g\right\rangle
.
In the quantum step
\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})
, if one of
(p,q)
are zero, this would correspond to a unidrectional quantum step
Hoyer and Meyer 2009
;
Montero 2013
;
Innocenti et al. 2017
, where only one component participates in the walk. Here, however, we shall assume no relation between
p
and
q
. We have the following relation two quantum steps:

\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})\equiv\hat{T}(q,p;\bm{c}_{\perp},\bm{s}_{\perp})

(18)

We call the two states
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{c}\right\rangle
of a quantum step
\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})
as its coin and shift states respectively.

The quantum step
\hat{T}(p,q,\bm{c},\bm{s})
of Eq. (
17
) carries the same character as the conventional step
\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
of Eq. (
14
), in the sense that it can be cast in the conventional form of a shift operation followed by a coin toss operator as in Eq. (
14
):

\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})=\hat{S}(p,q;\bm{c})\left(\hat{\mathit{c}}(\bm{c};\bm{s})\otimes\hat{I}_{2N+1}\right)

(19)

where
\hat{S}(p,q;\bm{c})
is the shift operator that acts as in Eq. (
15
):

\displaystyle\hat{S}(p,q;\bm{c})\left|\bm{c};g\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{c};g+p\right\rangle,

(20)

\displaystyle\hat{S}(p,q;\bm{c})\left|\bm{c}_{\perp};g\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{c}_{\perp};g+q\right\rangle.

The operator
\hat{\mathit{c}}(\bm{c};\bm{s})
is the coin-toss operator which is just an SU(2) operator on the coin space, defined as:

\hat{\mathit{c}}(\bm{c};\bm{s})=\left|\bm{c}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{s}\right|+\left|\bm{c}_{\perp}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{s}_{\perp}\right|

(21)

We prove this by showing that the step
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\Delta}(\delta;\bm{c})
can itself be realized using a pair of
\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})
steps, as:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\Delta}(\delta;\bm{c})\equiv\hat{T}(1,0,\bm{c},\bm{b})\hat{T}(0,-1,\bm{0},\bm{0}),\text{ where }\left|\bm{b}\right\rangle=\sqrt{\Delta}\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle-\sqrt{1-\Delta}e^{i\delta}\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle.

(22)

This establishes that the quantum steps introduced here are powerful enough to generate any translationally invariant composite state from some home-state.

II.4
Novel quantum walks and their action on home-states

A quantum walk
\hat{W}
of
N
steps is a collection of above quantum steps:

\hat{W}=\hat{T}_{N}\hat{T}_{N-1}\cdots\hat{T}_{1},

(23)

with
\hat{T}_{i}
being the
i^{th}
quantum step of the form Eq. (
17
),
\hat{T}_{i}\equiv\hat{T}\left(p_{i},q_{i};\bm{c}_{i},\bm{s}_{i}\right)
. We shall call quantum walks where all the steps are identical as “homogeneous walks”, and the others as “homogeneous walks”.

Given a quantum walk as in Eq. (
23
), we associate an integer
d
to it, computed from
p
and
q
parameters of the individual steps, as:

d=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(p_{i}+q_{i}\right),

(24)

where
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
are
p
and
q
parameters of the
i^{th}
quantum step
\hat{T}_{i}
defined through Eq. (
17
).
Like in case of quantum steps, one could decide label quantum walk themselves as unbiased or biased forward or biased backward, depending or whether
d
is equal to or greater than or less than
0
respectively.
The action of a quantum walk
\hat{W}
on a product state
\left|\bm{w};0\right\rangle
is to transform it into a composite state
\left|\bm{W}\right\rangle
of the form Eq. (
9
):

\displaystyle\hat{W}\left|\bm{w};0\right\rangle=\left|\bm{W}\right\rangle,\,\text{ and }\hat{W}\left|\bm{w}_{\perp};0\right\rangle=\left|(\bm{W}_{\perp})_{+d}\right\rangle,

(25)

where
d
is given by Eq. (
24
), and
\left|\bm{W}\right\rangle
is a translationally-invariant state (defined in Eq. (
11
)).
Recall that any translationally invariant state can be generated from some home-state by using quantum steps of the kind
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\Delta}(\delta;\bm{c})
. This is true even for the quantum steps of
\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})
.
Actually, it is to be noted that the quantum steps
\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})
do not give access to new states on the composite space over and above what is possible with unbiased quantum steps
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\Delta}(\delta;\bm{c})
. To emphasize, consider the composite states
\left|\bm{W}\right\rangle
and
\left|(\bm{W}_{\perp})_{+d}\right\rangle
appearing on the RHS of Eq. (
25
). These orthogonal composite states result from quantum walks using steps of the form
\hat{T}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s})
starting from orthogonal home-states. These two composite states can also be obtained from quantum walks using steps of the form
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\Delta}(\delta;\bm{c})
, except that a single quantum walk cannot accomplish this task, unless
d=0
.

Moving forward, the following points can be readily derived about quantum walks of Eq. (
23
):

1.

If the steps of a quantum walk are such that the coin state of one is identical to the shift state of the subsequent step, the complete walk can be interpreted as a single step whose shift state is the shift state of the first and coin state is the coin state of the last step:

\prod_{i=1}^{m}\hat{T}(p_{i},q_{i},\bm{s}_{i+1},\bm{s}_{i})=\hat{T}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i},\bm{s}_{m+1},\bm{s}_{1}\right)

(26)

2.

A homogeneous quantum walk comprised of a quantum step with identical coin and shift states, is another quantum step by itself:

{\hat{T}(p,q,\bm{u},\bm{u})}^{N}=\hat{T}(Np,Nq,\bm{u},\bm{u})

(27)

3.

With quantum steps in which shift state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
and the coin state
\left|\bm{c}\right\rangle
are orthogonal, we have the relation on the homogeneous quantum walk comprising of an even number of them:

{\hat{T}(p,q,\bm{u}_{\perp},\bm{u})}^{N}=(-1)^{\frac{N}{2}}\hat{T}\left(\frac{N}{2}(p+q),\frac{N}{2}(p+q);\bm{u},\bm{u}\right)

(28)

As most generalized initial states of a quantum walk, we shall consider not product states of the form
\left|\bm{w};0\right\rangle
, but localized mixed states of the form
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|
,
where
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
is the density matrix of the coin state, Eq. (
3
).
We shall call the states of this form as the “home-states”.
The result of the walk
\hat{W}
on the home-states is the density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{res}
:

\hat{\rho}_{res}=\hat{W}(\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|)\hat{W}^{-1}

(29)

It is to be noted that
\hat{\rho}_{res}
is a density matrix on the composite space and
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
is a density matrix on the two-dimensional coin space alone.
If the home-state
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
is a pure-state density matrix (that is, if
r=1
), then the resulting quantum walk density matrix has the form
\hat{\rho}_{res}=\left|\bm{S}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{S}\right|
, where
\left|\bm{S}\right\rangle
is a composite pure state of the form Eq. (
9
).

III
Quantum games and Parrondo’s paradox in quantum walks

III.1
A quantum game on these quantum walks

Given a walk
\hat{W}
, and an arbitrary mixed coin state
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
of the form Eq. (
3
), we use the expression
\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}})
to indicate the expectation value
\hat{O}
in the density matrix resulting from the action of
\hat{W}
at the position
\left|0\right\rangle
, in a coin state whose density matrix is given by
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
. We call this quantity as the payoff.

Consider a tuple
(\hat{W},\hat{O},\omega)
where
\hat{W}
is a quantum walk defined in Eq. (
23
),
\hat{O}
is an Hermitian operator defined in Eq. (
12
), and
\omega
is a real number denoting the “target payoff”. Given these, we say a home-state
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
is a “winning state” if the payoff, that is
\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}})
defined in Eq. (
32
), is greater than
\omega
. Likewise, a “losing state” if the payoff is less than
\omega
, and a “tie state” if it is equal to
\omega
.

Given a walk, an operator on the composite space, and a target payoff, the game here is to identify the winning home-states, if any.
With this background, we are ready to define the Parrondo paradox.

III.2
Parrondo’s paradox in the above game

For realizing the Parrondo’s paradox in this game, we consider
m
quantum steps
\hat{T}_{1},\cdots\hat{T}_{m}
of the form Eq. (
17
), and using these quantum steps, we construct
m+1
quantum walks as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{1}=[\hat{T}_{1}]^{n\times m},\,\hat{W}_{2}=[\hat{T}_{2}]^{n\times m},\,\cdots,\hat{W}_{m}=[\hat{T}_{m}]^{n\times m},

(30)

\displaystyle\text{ and }\hat{W}_{m+1}=[\hat{T}_{m}\cdots\hat{T}_{2}\hat{T}_{1}]^{n}.

The first
m
of these walks are all homogeneous walks, while the last one is an inhomogenous walk comprising of all the steps in a sequence. Now, a home-state
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
may be a winning state for some of the
m+1
walks, a losing for some, and possibly a tie state for some other of these
m+1
walks.
For observing Parrondo’s paradox in the above setting, we seek a home-state
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
such that it is a losing state for all the homogeneous walks
\hat{W}_{i}
, for
i=1\cdots m
, but happens to be a winning state for the inhomogenous walk
\hat{W}_{m+1}
. That is

\displaystyle\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W}_{i},\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}})

\displaystyle<\omega,\text{ for }i=1,\cdots,m,\text{ and }

(31)

\displaystyle\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W}_{m+1},\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}})

\displaystyle>\omega.

We call the home-states
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
that satisfy the Eq. (
31
) as “Parrondo states”.
It is to be noted that the walks of Eq. (
30
) contain a positive integer parameter
n
. We call this
n
as the number of cycles. It is possible that a pure or mixed home-state is a Parrondo state for a certain
n=n_{0}
but not for any
n<n_{0}
and
n>n_{0}
. Now, we say a Parrondo state
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
to be a “persistent Parrondo state” if it satisfies Eq. (
31
) for all
n
greater than a certain
n_{0}
.

IV
Parrondo’s paradox in arbitrary quantum walks

By definition, the payoff
\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}})
can be computed as

\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}})\equiv\textbf{tr}(\hat{W}^{-1}\hat{O}\hat{W}(\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|)),

(32)

where
tr
stands for trace operator over the composite system.
If the home-state density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
is a pure state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{s}\right|
, then we indicate the expectation value on the LHS of Eq. (
32
) as
\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\bm{s})
, and it simplifies to:

\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\bm{s})\equiv\langle{\bm{s};0|\hat{W}^{-1}\hat{O}\hat{W}}|{\bm{s};0}\rangle

(33)

Now, it is straight-forward to see that a convex combination of Parrondo states will also be a Parrondo state. We will therefore search for Parrondo states only among the pure states.

We shall first try to compute the expression for the payoff
\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\bm{s})
for a given home-state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
.

IV.1
Home-states leading to the largest expectation values

Given a walk
\hat{W}
and an operator
\hat{O}
, we shall refer to the minimum and maximum payoffs possible by
o^{max}
and
o_{min}
, and the coin-states that lead to these payoffs by the symbols
\left|\bm{o}^{max}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{o}_{min}\right\rangle
respectively:

\displaystyle\left|\bm{o}_{max}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\textbf{max}_{\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\bm{s})}\left\{{\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle}\right\},

(34)

\displaystyle o^{max}

\displaystyle=\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\bm{o}^{max}),

\displaystyle\left|\bm{o}_{min}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\textbf{min}_{\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\bm{s})}\left\{\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle\right\},

\displaystyle o_{min}

\displaystyle=\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\bm{o}_{min}).

In other words, this coin-states
\left|\bm{o}^{max}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{o}_{min}\right\rangle
are such that
\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\bm{o}^{max})\geq\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}})
and
\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\bm{o}_{min})\leq\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}})
for all density matrices
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
of the coin-space. It is evident that the maximum and minimum payoffs can be expected only with pure states, not mixed states.

First we will try to identify these
\left|\bm{o}^{max}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{o}_{min}\right\rangle
coin states, which, for a given walk
\hat{W}
and operator
\hat{O}
, are unique up to a global phase.
These states are actually the eigenstates of a Hermitian operator
\hat{o}
, defined as:

\hat{o}=\textbf{tr}_{walk}\left((\hat{I}_{2}\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|)\hat{W}^{-1}\hat{O}\hat{W}\right),

(35)

where
\textbf{tr}_{walk}
refers to the tracing over the walk degree-of-freedom. The resulting operator
\hat{o}
is a
2\times 2
Hermitian operator on the coin space. Note that
\hat{o}
depends on the quantum walk
\hat{W}
, in addition to the operator
\hat{O}
.
In an arbitrary basis
\{\left|\bm{w};\bm{w}_{\perp}\right\rangle\}
of the coin space, the matrix representation of
\hat{o}
is given by:

\hat{o}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\langle{\bm{W}}|{\hat{O}|\bm{W}}\rangle&\langle{\bm{W}}|{\hat{O}|(\bm{W}_{\perp})_{+d}}\rangle\\
\langle{(\bm{W}_{\perp})_{+d}}|{\hat{O}|\bm{W}}\rangle&\langle{(\bm{W}_{\perp})_{+d}}|{\hat{O}|(\bm{W}_{\perp})_{+d}}\rangle\end{array}\right],

(36)

where
\left|\bm{W}\right\rangle
and
\left|(\bm{W}_{\perp})_{+d}\right\rangle
are the composite states obtained by action of the walk
\hat{W}
on home-states
\left|\bm{w};0\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{w}_{\perp};0\right\rangle
respectively. (see Eq. (
25
)).
The desired
o^{max}
and
o_{min}
are the larger and smaller eigenvalues of the matrix
\hat{o}
respectively, and the states
\left|\bm{o}^{max}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{o}_{min}\right\rangle
are the corresponding eigenvectors. We have
\langle{\bm{o}^{max}}|{\bm{o}_{min}}\rangle=0
, since
\hat{o}
is a Hermitian operator.

The payoff possible with any other home-state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
can be can be computed directly from these quantities as:

\displaystyle\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\bm{u})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left((o^{max}-o_{min})\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{o}^{max})\cdot\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{u})\right)

(37)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(o^{max}+o_{min}).

Here the
\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{o}^{max})
and
\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{u})
are the qubit vectors (defined in Eq. (
6
)) of the coin-states
\left|\bm{o}^{max}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
respectively. Likewise, the payoff in case of mixed-states can also be computed as:

\displaystyle\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left((2r-1)(o^{max}-o_{min})\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{o}^{max})\cdot\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{s})\right)

(38)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(o^{max}+o_{min}).

The maximally mixed state corresponds to
r=\frac{1}{2}
, and for this case the expectation value is
\frac{1}{2}(o^{max}+o_{min})
.

IV.2
Winning the quantum game

Recall that we say a home-state
\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle
is a winning state if its payoff
\textbf{exp}(\hat{O},\hat{W},\bm{u})
, is greater than the target payoff
\omega
. Now, for a given pair of
\hat{W}
and
\hat{O}
, it is possible that the minimum and maximum possible payoffs are equal, in which case it follows from Eq. (
37
) that every home-state yields the same payoff. This would be mean the whole Bloch sphere behaves identically, that is, the whole sphere is a winning or losing or tie region, depending on the target payoff. Otherwise, we define a quantity
\Omega
as follows:

\Omega=\frac{2\omega-(o^{max}+o_{min})}{o^{max}-o_{min}}

(39)

Therefore, from Eq. (
37
), it follows that a pure home-state
\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle
is a winning state if and only if the dot-product
\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{o}^{max})\cdot\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{u})>\Omega
, and it is a losing state if and only if
\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{o}^{max})\cdot\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{u})<\Omega
. Home-states with
\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{o}^{max})\cdot\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{u})=\Omega
, are the “tie-states”.
Since the winning criteria of a home-state
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
depends only on the dot product of its qubit vector and the qubit vector of
\left|\bm{o}^{max}\right\rangle
,
We shall now try to locate these Parrondo states in a unit-sphere called the Bloch sphere, where every state
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
is represented by its qubit vector
\vec{\bm{S}}(\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}})
. Pure states are located on surface of this sphere and the mixed states are placed within, with the maximally mixed state placed at centre of the sphere.
It follows that if the home-state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
is in the
\left|\bm{o}^{max}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{o}_{min}\right\rangle
basis as

\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle=\cos\frac{\nu}{2}\left|\bm{o}^{max}\right\rangle+e^{i\delta}\sin\frac{\nu}{2}\left|\bm{o}_{min}\right\rangle,

(40)

All home-states with the same latitude
\nu
have the same expectation values.
It follows therefore that the tuple
(\hat{W},\hat{O},\omega)
splits the Bloch sphere into two contiguous regions, such that the states in one region are all winning states and those in the other are all losing states. The states along the circle separating these spherical caps are the tie states. The normals to this circle are the qubit vectors
\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{o}^{max})
and
\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{o}_{min})
. If
\Omega=0
, that is if the target payoff
\omega
is taken to be equal to
\frac{1}{2}(o^{max}+o_{min})
, then the two spherical caps become two hemispheres, and the circle separating them will be a great-circle.

IV.3
Identifying the Parrondo states on the Bloch sphere

That was about single quantum walk. Now, for identifying Parrondo states, we shall have to deal with
m+1
walks of Eq. (
30
). A given state
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
is a winning state for the walk
\hat{W}_{i}
if the dot product
\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{o}^{max}_{i}).\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{u})
is greater than
\Omega_{i}
. Here
\Omega_{i}
is the
\Omega
of Eq. (
39
), computed for the walk
\hat{W}_{i}
. The surface of the Bloch sphere thus splits into utmost
2^{m+1}
disjoint slices, depending upon whether each state
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
on it is a winning state for
\hat{W}_{i}
or not. Whether all
2^{m+1}
distinct regions exist depend upon the choice of the target payoff
\omega
, and also on the
m+1
stokes vectors
\vec{\bm{S}}(\bm{o}^{max}_{i})
.
One slice among these, for instance, might be comprising of home-states which are winning for all
m+1
walks simultaneously, and likewise another region might be corresponding to home-states which are losing states simultaneously for all of them. One among these regions corresponds to all home-states that are losing states for the
m
walks
\hat{W}_{i},i=1,\cdots,m
but are winning states for the
\hat{W}_{m+1}
walk. These are the Parrondo states that we are after.

It must be understood that biased quantum walks or biased quantum steps are not essential for the above scheme of identifying the Parrondo’s state to work. In other words, the steps
\hat{T}_{i}
of Eq. (
30
) could be the steps
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\Delta}(\delta;\bm{c})
of Eq. (
16
) or
\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
of Eq. (
14
) as well.

IV.4
Persistence of Parrondo’s paradox

Consider the quantum walk
\hat{W}_{i}
, one of the
m+1
quantum walks of Eq. (
30
). Recall that these quantum walks are defined for a particular cycle-length
n
. We shall refer to the walk
\hat{W}_{i}
defined for a particular
n
by the symbol
\hat{W}_{i}(n)
. For a given operator
\hat{O}
and the quantum walk
\hat{W}_{i}(n)
, we shall indicate the coin-space operator
\hat{o}
of Eq. (
35
) by the symbol
\hat{o}_{i}(n)
. The Parrondo regions corresponding to the cycle-lengths
n
and
n^{\prime}
will be identical if the matrices
\hat{o}_{i}(n)
and
\hat{o}_{i}(n^{\prime})
commute for all
i=1,\cdots,m+1
.In the general case, however, the matrices do not commute. In that case, the intersection of the Parrondo regions correspond to
n
and
n^{\prime}
would lead to home-state that display Parrondo’s effect at both the cycle-lengths.
The intersection of Parrondo regions corresponding to all
n>n_{0}
for some
n_{0}
comprise of the persistent Parrondo states.

We shall give an illustration of this in Section
VI.2
.

V
Parrondo’s paradox with biased quantum steps

The above construction of identifying Parrondo’s paradox works for any kind of quantum steps, biased or unbiased. The composite states that result from these quantum walks are entangled, spanning multiple position states. The manifestation of Parrondo paradox in these quantum walks can be attributed to the interference affects of probability amplitudes. This raises an interesting question: are entanglement and delocalized probability amplitudes indispensable for the emergence of this paradox? In this section, we answer this question in the negative. We show that one could design quantum steps such that a desired home-state happens to be the Parrondo state for the mean-position operator. These quantum steps are such that the chosen home-state coherently hops on the position state, without being in the superposition of position states at any time. The collection of quantum steps can be thought of as displaying “classical Parrondo’s paradox”. The construction here however, works only with biased quantum steps, and for only certain operators.

V.1
Zero-position operator

In this illustration, we consider the Hermitian operator
\hat{O}
to be
\hat{0}=\hat{I}_{2}\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|
.
The expectation value of
\hat{0}
in a composite state
\left|\bm{S}\right\rangle
of the form Eq. (
9
), is
s_{0}^{2}
.
We can take the target payoff
\omega
to be any number strictly between 0 and 1:
0<\omega<1
. For a quantum walk
\hat{W}
, a home state
\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle
is a winning state if in the resulting composite state
\hat{W}\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle
, the probability at position zero is greater than
\omega
.
Here we demonstrate that two quantum steps, where a walk of
Here we consider two quantum steps of the form:

\hat{T}_{A}=\hat{T}(m,m,\bm{c}_{1},\bm{s}_{1}),\,\text{ and }\hat{T}_{B}=\hat{T}(-m,-m,\bm{c}_{2},\bm{s}_{2})

(41)

for some
m\neq 0
.
From these we construct four quantum walks as

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{A}=[\hat{T}_{A}]^{2n},\,\hat{W}_{B}=[\hat{T}_{B}]^{2n},

(42)

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{AB}=[\hat{T}_{B}\hat{T}_{A}]^{n},\text{ and }\hat{W}_{BA}=[\hat{T}_{A}\hat{T}_{B}]^{n}.

Using these two steps, we shall demonstrate the Parrondo paradox, simultaneously for two triplets of walks:
(\hat{W}_{A},\hat{W}_{B},\hat{W}_{AB})
and
(\hat{W}_{A},\hat{W}_{B},\hat{W}_{BA})
.

Consider a walker starting from a home-state
\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle
. The walks
\hat{W}_{A}
and
\hat{W}_{B}
place the walker at the locations
\left|2nm\right\rangle
and
\left|-2nm\right\rangle
respectively, in some coin-states.
The walks
\hat{W}_{AB}
and
\hat{W}_{BA}
, on the other hand, are just SU(2) transformations on the coin-space alone, and do not change the position of the walker. Therefore the walker remains at home,
\left|0\right\rangle
, albeit in a different coin state, when acted on either of them. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\textbf{exp}(\hat{I_{2}}\otimes\hat{0},\hat{W}_{A},\bm{s})

\displaystyle=\textbf{exp}(\hat{I_{2}}\otimes\hat{0},\hat{W}_{B},\bm{s})=0,

(43)

\displaystyle\textbf{exp}(\hat{I_{2}}\otimes\hat{0},\hat{W}_{AB},\bm{s})

\displaystyle=\textbf{exp}(\hat{I_{2}}\otimes\hat{0},\hat{W}_{BA},\bm{s})=1.

Therefore for the operator
\hat{0}
, together with any target payoff
0<\omega<1
, every state on the Bloch sphere is a losing state for the walks
\hat{W}_{A}
and
\hat{W}_{B}
, but is a winning state for both the walks,
\hat{W}_{AB}
and
\hat{W}_{BA}
.
The complete Bloch sphere is a Parrondo region. Furthermore, this result holds true for any
n
, leading to the whole Bloch sphere being a persistent Parrondo region.

V.2
Mean position operator

In this section, we shall consider the operator
\hat{O}
of Eq. (
32
) to be
\hat{I}_{2}\otimes\hat{\mu}
, where
\hat{\mu}
is the position operator as

\hat{\mu}=\sum_{m=-N}^{N}m\left|m\right\rangle\left\langle m\right|

(44)

The expectation value of
\hat{\mu}
in a composite state
\bm{S}
is given by

\langle{\bm{S}}|{\hat{I}_{2}\otimes\hat{\mu}|\bm{S}}\rangle=\sum_{m=b}^{e}ms_{m}^{2}.

(45)

We have the following points regarding this position operator, which we shall need further down the text.

1.

For a given quantum walk
\hat{W}
and two orthogonal pure states, we have the following relationship between the mean position they generate:

\textbf{exp}(\hat{I_{2}}\otimes\hat{\mu},\hat{W},\bm{s}_{\perp})=d-\textbf{exp}(\hat{I_{2}}\otimes\hat{\mu},\hat{W},\bm{s})
, with
d
of the walk
\hat{W}
defined is given by Eq. (
24
).

2.

If
p
and
q
parameters of a quantum step
\hat{T}
are both negative, then in a homogeneous walk of
N
such steps will never lead to a positive mean value for any home-state. That is,
\textbf{exp}\left(\hat{I}_{2}\otimes\hat{\mu},\hat{T}^{N}(p,q;\bm{c},\bm{s}),\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}\right)<0
if
p<0
and
q<0
.

We shall take the target payoff
\omega
to be equal to
0
. This means that a home state
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}
is a winning state if the walk
\hat{W}
transforms
\hat{\rho}_{r,\bm{s}}\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|
to a composite density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{res}
having a positive mean position.

In this subsection, we will explicitly create a set of quantum steps such that a given home state
\left|\bm{w};0\right\rangle
is a Parrondo state.
Consider a collection of even number
m
of quantum steps, defined as

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{1}

\displaystyle=\hat{T}(p_{1},q_{1},\bm{c}_{1},\bm{w}),

(46)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{2}

\displaystyle=\hat{T}(p_{2},q_{2},\bm{c}_{2},\bm{c}_{1}),

\displaystyle\cdots,

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{m-1}

\displaystyle=\hat{T}(p_{m-1},q_{m-1},\bm{w}_{\perp},\bm{c}_{m-2}),

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{m}

\displaystyle=\hat{T}(p_{m},q_{m},\bm{w},\bm{w}_{\perp}).

Here
\left|\bm{c}_{i}\right\rangle\text{ for }i=1,\cdots,m-2
and
\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle
are arbitrary coin-states. These quantum steps are “daisy-chained”, in the sense that the coin state of the
i^{th}
step is the shift state of
(i+1)^{th}
step. The step sizes
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
must satisfy the following conditions:

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle<0\,\forall i=1,\cdots,m-1,\,\text{ and }p_{m}>-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}p_{i},

(47)

\displaystyle q_{i}

\displaystyle<0\,\forall i=1,\cdots,m,

\displaystyle q_{m}

\displaystyle<\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}p_{i},\text{ and }\,p_{m}<-q_{m}.

Figure 1:
Constraints on the step sizes of the quantum steps. The step sizes
p_{i}\text{ and }q_{i},\text{ for }i=1,\cdots,m-1
are all negative. The step size
\hat{q}_{m}
should be more negative than
\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}p_{i}
. The step-size
p_{m}
is positive, and must lie between two the non-negative integers,
-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}p_{i}
and
-q_{m}
.

We shall now examine if the home-state
\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle
is a Parrondo state for the collection of walks of the form Eq. (
30
) constructed out of these steps. The state
\left|\bm{w};0\right\rangle
will be a losing state for the
m-1
walks
\hat{W}_{i}
for
i=1,\cdots,m-1
following the point (
2
) above. Consider now the
m^{th}
quantum walk,
\hat{W}_{m}
. Since
m
is taken to be an even number, we have from Eq. (
28
) the following action of the walk
\hat{W}_{m}
:

\hat{W}_{m}=\hat{T}\left(\frac{nm}{2}(p_{m}+q_{m}),\frac{nm}{2}(p_{m}+q_{m}),\bm{w},\bm{w}\right)

(48)

Since
p_{m}+q_{m}<0
(see Eq. (
47
)), the walk
\hat{W}_{m}
is also a losing walk for all
n
.

Consider now the walk
\hat{W}_{m+1}=[\prod_{i=m}^{1}\hat{T}_{i}]^{n}
. From Eq.(
26
) and Eq. (
27
), this walk is:

\hat{W}_{m+1}=\hat{T}\left(n\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i},n\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i};\bm{w},\bm{w}\right)

(49)

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}>0
, the home-state
\left|\bm{w};0\right\rangle
is a winning state for
\hat{W}_{m+1}
. This home-state therefore satisfies Eq. (
31
), and therefore is a Parrondo state.

Having demonstrated that
\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle
is a Parrondo state for this set of quantum walks, we shall now see if these quantum walks support any other Parrondo state.
Indeed the
m
quantum walks
\{\hat{W}_{1},\cdots,\hat{W}_{m}\}
are, by design, such that every state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
on the Bloch sphere is a losing state for all of them. Therefore a home-state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
is a Parrondo state if it happens to be a winning state for the walk
\hat{W}_{m+1}
. For identifying the winning states of the walk
\hat{W}_{m+1}
, consider its action on the home-state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
. We have from the definition of this walk, Eq. (
49
), the following:

\hat{W}_{m+1}\left|\bm{s};0\right\rangle=\langle{\bm{w}}|{\bm{s}}\rangle\left|\bm{w};n\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\right\rangle+\langle{\bm{w}_{\perp}}|{\bm{s}}\rangle\left|\bm{w}_{\perp};n\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}\right\rangle

(50)

The expectation value of the operator
\hat{\mu}
in the composite state of the RHS is given by:

\textbf{exp}(\hat{\mu},\hat{W}_{m+1},\bm{s})=n\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(q_{i}+\left|\langle{\bm{w}}|{\bm{s}}\rangle\right|^{2}(p_{i}-q_{i})\right)

(51)

This expectation value is positive if

\left|\langle{\bm{w}}|{\bm{s}}\rangle\right|^{2}>\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(q_{i}-p_{i})}.

(52)

Therefore all states
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
on the Bloch sphere, of the form
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle=\cos\frac{\nu}{2}\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle+e^{i\delta}\sin\frac{\nu}{2}\left|\bm{w}_{\perp}\right\rangle
, with
\nu<\nu_{max}
are Parrondo states, where
\nu_{max}=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(q_{i}-p_{i})}\right)
. Furthermore, all Parrondo states are persistent Parrondo states.

VI
Numerical illustrations

In this section we shall provide some illustrations of the schemes discussed above.
We shall define a few coin states:

\displaystyle\left|\bm{h}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle+\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle\right),\,\left|\bm{v}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle-\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle\right),

(53)

\displaystyle\left|\bm{d}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle+i\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle\right),\,\left|\bm{a}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(i\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle+\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle\right),

\displaystyle\left|\bm{f}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right)\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle+\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right)\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle.

We first provide two illustrations for the construction proposed in section
V.2
, and then give an illustration for the Parrondo paradox in arbitrary quantum walks, discussed in section
IV
.

VI.1
Quantum walks that accentuate the Parrondo’s Paradox

Here we shall demonstrate the construction of Section (
V.2
) for constructing the quantum steps such that a desired home-state is a Parrondo state for the mean-position operator
\hat{\mu}
.

VI.1.1
A two-step example

In this example, we intend to have the home-state
\left|\bm{h}\right\rangle
defined in Eq. (
53
) as the Parrondo state. We take
m
of Eq. (
30
) to be
2
, and take the two steps as:

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{1}=\hat{T}(-1,-1,\bm{v},\bm{h}),\,\text{ and }\hat{T}_{2}=\hat{T}(3,-4,\bm{h},\bm{v}),

(54)

The chosen steps are in the form listed in Eq. (
46
), and satisfy the conditions of Eq. (
47
).
Using these two quantum steps, we define three quantum walks:

\hat{W}_{1}\equiv[\hat{T}_{1}]^{2n},\,\hat{W}_{2}\equiv[\hat{T}_{2}]^{2n}\,\text{ and }\hat{W}_{3}\equiv[\hat{T}_{2}\hat{T}_{1}]^{n}.

(55)

The progression of the home state
\left|\bm{h};0\right\rangle
through these three walks is depicted (up to a global phase) in Eq. (
56
), for
n=1
.

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{1}:\;\left|\bm{h};0\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{1}\rightarrow\left|\bm{v};-1\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{1}\rightarrow\left|\bm{h};-2\right\rangle.

(56)

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{2}:\;\left|\bm{h};0\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{2}\rightarrow\left|\bm{v};-4\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{2}\rightarrow\left|\bm{h};-1\right\rangle.

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{3}:\;\left|\bm{h};0\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{1}\rightarrow\left|\bm{v};-1\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{2}\rightarrow\left|\bm{h};2\right\rangle.

Since
\left|\bm{h};0\right\rangle
is a losing state for both
\hat{W}_{1}
and
\hat{W}_{2}
while being a winning state for
\hat{W}_{3}
, it is a Parrondo state, as desired. Furthermore, it is also evident that
\left|\bm{h};0\right\rangle
is a persistent Parrondo state.

To see whether any other home-states are Parrondo states, we find the action of these quantum walks on an arbitrary home-state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
. We note the following:

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{1}\left|\bm{s};0\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{s};-2n\right\rangle,\,\hat{W}_{2}\left|\bm{s};0\right\rangle=\left|\bm{s};-n\right\rangle,\,

(57)

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{3}\left|\bm{s};0\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\bm{h}}|{\bm{s}}\rangle\left|\bm{h};2n\right\rangle+\langle{\bm{v}}|{\bm{s}}\rangle\left|\bm{v};-5n\right\rangle.

The expectation value of
\hat{\mu}
in the three composite states
\hat{W}_{i}\left|\bm{s};0\right\rangle
for the three walks are therefore:

\displaystyle\textbf{exp}(\hat{\mu},\hat{W}_{1},\bm{s})

\displaystyle=-2n,\,\textbf{exp}(\hat{\mu},\hat{W}_{2},\bm{s})=-n,

(58)

\displaystyle\textbf{exp}(\hat{\mu},\hat{W}_{3},\bm{s})

\displaystyle=(7\left|\langle{\bm{h}}|{\bm{s}}\rangle\right|^{2}-5)n.

Evidently, home-state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
is a losing state for
\hat{W}_{1}
and
\hat{W}_{2}
, while for
\hat{W}_{3}
it is a winning state if
\left|\langle{\bm{h}}|{\bm{s}}\rangle\right|^{2}>\frac{5}{7}
. The mean position varies proportional to
n
in all the three cases, and hence a Parrondo state will be a persistent Parrondo state.

VI.1.2
A four-step example

As a second example, we consider
m=4
, and the desired Parrondo state as
\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle
. We take the four steps as following:

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{1}

\displaystyle=\hat{T}(-1,-1,\bm{h},\bm{0}),\,\hat{T}_{2}=\hat{T}(-1,-1,\bm{d},\bm{h}),

(59)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{3}

\displaystyle=\hat{T}(-1,-1,\bm{1},\bm{d}),\text{ and }\hat{T}_{4}=\hat{T}(4,-5,\bm{0},\bm{1}).

As in the previous example, with these four quantum steps we define five walks:

\hat{W}_{i}=[{\hat{T}_{i}}]^{4n},\,i=1,2,3,4\text{ and }\hat{W}_{5}=[\hat{T}_{4}\hat{T}_{3}\hat{T}_{2}\hat{T}_{1}]^{n}.

(60)

The progression of home-state
\left|\bm{0};0\right\rangle
through each of the steps of these walks is shown (up to a global phase of the coin-state) in Eq. (
61
) for
n=1
.

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{1}:\;\left|\bm{0};0\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{1}\rightarrow\left|\bm{h};-1\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{1}\rightarrow\left|\bm{1};-2\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{1}\rightarrow\left|\bm{v};-3\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{1}\rightarrow\left|\bm{0};-4\right\rangle.

(61)

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{2}:\;\left|\bm{0};0\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{2}\rightarrow\left|\bm{v};-1\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{2}\rightarrow\left|\bm{a};-2\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{2}\rightarrow\left|\bm{0};-3\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{2}\rightarrow\left|\bm{v};-4\right\rangle.

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{3}:\;\left|\bm{0};0\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{3}\rightarrow\left|\bm{a};-1\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{3}\rightarrow\left|\bm{0};-2\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{3}\rightarrow\left|\bm{a};-3\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{3}\rightarrow\left|\bm{0};-4\right\rangle.

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{4}:\;\left|\bm{0};0\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{4}\rightarrow\left|\bm{1};-5\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{4}\rightarrow\left|\bm{0};-1\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{4}\rightarrow\left|\bm{1};-6\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{4}\rightarrow\left|\bm{0};-2\right\rangle.

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{5}:\;\left|\bm{0};0\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{1}\rightarrow\left|\bm{h};-1\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{2}\rightarrow\left|\bm{d};-2\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{3}\rightarrow\left|\bm{1};-3\right\rangle\rightarrow\hat{T}_{4}\rightarrow\left|\bm{0};1\right\rangle.

Evidently the home-state
\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle
is a losing state for the walks
\hat{W}_{i},\,i=1,\cdots,4
, but is a winning state for
\hat{W}_{5}
, and therefore is a Parrondo state. To examine if these walks support any other Parrondo state, we just need to examine the action of the fifth walk
\hat{W}_{5}
(of Eq. (
60
)) on an arbitrary home-state
\left|\bm{s};0\right\rangle
. The walk
\hat{W}_{5}
is actually equal to
\hat{T}(n,-8n;\bm{0},\bm{0})
, and therefore we have:

\hat{W}_{5}\left|\bm{s};0\right\rangle=\langle{\bm{0}}|{\bm{s}}\rangle\left|\bm{0};n\right\rangle+\langle{\bm{1}}|{\bm{s}}\rangle\left|\bm{1};-8n\right\rangle,

(62)

and therefore we have

\textbf{exp}(\hat{\mu},\hat{W}_{5},\bm{s})=n(9\left|\langle{\bm{0}}|{\bm{s}}\rangle\right|^{2}-8).

(63)

A home-state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
is a winning state for
\hat{W}_{5}
, and hence a Parrondo state for the quantum steps of Eq. (
59
), if
\left|\langle{\bm{0}}|{\bm{s}}\rangle\right|>\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}
.

VII
Conclusion

In discrete-time quantum walks, as a function of number of time-steps, the state of the walker evolves as a superposition of multiple terms of the coin-position composite space. As these quantum walks act, by design, in a translationally-invariant manner on the position-space, the properties of the resulting final states can be solely predicted by the initial coin-state. Parrondo’s paradox in this context refer to the phenomenon where, some initial coin-states are such that they are losing states for a walk comprising of two quantum steps which individually lead to losing states can collectively lead to a winning state when employed in a sequentially manner. Here winning states refer to those composite states in which the probability of occupying positive positions is greater than that of negative positions.

The aim of this paper has been to demonstrate the manifestation of Parrondo’s paradox in a generalization of discrete-time quantum walks, on a novel winning criterion. In this work, a composite state is termed as a winning state if the expectation value of a given observable in that state happens to be greater than a certain cutoff value.
Given a discrete time quantum walk and an observable as a Hermitian operator on the position-coin composite space, we have given an explicit procedure for identifying two orthogonal coin states that lead to the maximum and minimum expectation values of this observable. Furthermore, the expectation value corresponding to any other coin state can be obtained geometrically from these two coin states and the corresponding expectation values. With this, it is possible to explicitly identify coin states that lead to composite states wherein these expectation values are greater than a certain cutoff value. Armed with this, given a collection of quantum walks, and a Hermitian operator, we have given a scheme for identifying all the Parrondo states. These are coin-states which are losing states for all quantum walks composed of only one kinds of steps, but will be a winning state for a quantum walk comprised of applying all the given quantum steps in sequence. We have shown that these Parrondo states are not randomly scattered on the Bloch sphere, but occupy a contiguous convex region on it. One could therefore identify a wedge of the Bloch sphere, every pure or mixed state of which is a Parrondo state.

Another signification contribution of this paper has been the introduction of a novel generalization of quantum walks. In these quantum walks, the steps could be biased, in the sense that forward and backward step-sizes can be distinct. Indeed, a quantum step could be such that the walker move along the same direction but with different step-sizes, depending on the outcome of the coin-flip. All this, while being translationally-invariant.

This work also presented an innovative scheme for explicitly engineering such quantum steps so as to render a given coin state a Parrondo state.

We believe the novel quantum steps introduced here, and the procedure of identifying coin-state that lead to composite states having maximum expectation value of a given observable, would find applications extended beyond the realm of Parrondo paradox.
