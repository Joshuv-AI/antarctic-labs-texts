The stochastic Landau–Lifshitz–Baryakhtar equation: Global solution and invariant measure
Beniamin Goldys, Agus L. Soenjaya, Thanh Tran

Abstract
. The Landau–Lifshitz–Baryakhtar (LLBar) equation perturbed by both additive and multiplicative noises is a system of fourth-order stochastic PDEs which models the evolution of magnetic spin fields in ferromagnetic materials at elevated temperatures, taking into account longitudinal damping, long-range interactions, spin current, and noise-induced phenomena at high temperatures. In this paper, we show the existence of a unique pathwise solution (which is analytically strong) to the stochastic LLBar equation posed in a bounded domain 𝒟 ⊂ ℝ d \mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} , where d = 1 , 2 , 3 d=1,2,3 . We also prove the convergence of this pathwise solution to that of the stochastic Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch (LLB) equation in the limit of vanishing exchange relaxation parameter. Finally, we show the Feller property of the Markov semigroup associated with the strong solution, and prove the existence of nontrivial invariant measures. For temperatures above the Curie temperature, exponential stability of the solution and uniqueness of invariant measure are obtained under certain dissipativity conditions.



1.
Introduction

In recent years there has been growing interest in systems of stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs), especially systems with high order partial derivatives; see for example an important paper
[
42
]
. However, these high order systems are still little understood. On the other hand, well-posedness for some fourth-order scalar-valued SPDEs, such as the stochastic Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation
[
18
,
20
]
and the stochastic Swift–Hohenberg equation
[
21
,
49
]
have been analysed in the literature, but mainly for the case of one- or two-dimensional spatial domains.

In this paper, we are concerned with a particular fourth-order system of nonlinear SPDEs arising in the theory of ferromagnetism. This is a semi-classical continuum theory that aims to describe the magnetic properties of a ferromagnetic object. The problem takes the following form:
Given a magnetic body
\mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{1,2,3\}
, with sufficiently smooth boundary
\partial\mathscr{D}
, its magnetisation vector
\boldsymbol{u}(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
for any time
t>0
satisfies the following equations

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}=\big(\lambda_{r}\boldsymbol{H}-\lambda_{e}\Delta\boldsymbol{H}-\gamma\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{H}+S(\boldsymbol{u})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}W_{k}(t)\;

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}
,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}=\alpha\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\kappa_{1}\boldsymbol{u}-\kappa_{2}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\boldsymbol{u}\;

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}
,

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{for }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{D},

(1.1d)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=\boldsymbol{0},\;\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{H}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=\boldsymbol{0}}\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{for }(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\partial\mathscr{D},

where
\{W_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a family of independent real-valued Wiener processes and
S(\boldsymbol{u})
is a nonlinear function of
\boldsymbol{u}
to be specified later.
For physical reasons and concreteness we take

(1.2)

G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\boldsymbol{g}_{k}+\gamma\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{h}_{k},

where
\boldsymbol{g}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{h}_{k}
are given functions with certain regularity. For simplicity of presentation, we set
\alpha=\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=1
(corresponding to the regime below the Curie temperature), except in Theorem
2.6
and Theorem
8.1
where we set
\alpha=\kappa_{2}=1
and
\kappa_{1}=-1
(corresponding to the regime above the Curie point). This system is known as an initial-boundary value problem associated with the stochastic Landau–Lifshitz–Baryakhtar (LLBar) equation, the physical background of which (together with the physical meanings of all coefficients in the equations) and the literature review are deferred to a subsequent paragraph.

To the best of our knowledge, the existing general theory of SPDEs does not cover problem (
1.1
). The results in
[
42
]
would require the equations under consideration posed in the full space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and containing Lipschitz nonlinearities only. While an extension might be possible, we have chosen instead to use the classical arguments based on Galerkin approximations. We begin by proving the existence of an analytically strong global martingale solution. It is worth noting that the right-hand side of (
1.1a
) does not define a monotone operator over the full range of parameters, making uniform-in-time estimates nontrivial. Next, we establish pathwise uniqueness, which in turn yields the existence of a unique pathwise global strong solution to the problem. These solutions generate a Markov family on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, and we show that the corresponding transition semigroup
\{P_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is Feller, in the sense that
P_{t}C_{b}(\mathbb{H}^{2})\subset C_{b}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
. We remark that, for related nonlinear problems such as the Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch equation, the Feller property has been established only when the state space is endowed with the weak topology
[
10
]
. In
[
27
]
, the Feller property is proved for the strong topology but the domain is one-dimensional. The proof of the Feller property in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
endowed with the norm topology requires some delicate arguments. We introduce two sequences of stopping times: the first one bounds the
\mathbb{H}^{2}
norm of the solution, while the second one captures the instantaneous parabolic regularisation inherent in the equation. Together, these stopping times are essential in establishing a continuous dependence estimate, which in turn yields the Feller property of the transition semigroup on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. The existence of an invariant measure then follows from the classical Krylov–Bogoliubov theorem.

Problem (
1.1
) is not dissipative in general. In Theorem
2.6
, we consider the dissipative case under the assumption of the absence of additive noise in (
1.1
). In this case, the Dirac measure
\delta_{\boldsymbol{0}}
is trivially invariant. Theorem
2.6
further shows that
\delta_{\boldsymbol{0}}
is the unique invariant measure for the system, and that solutions converge exponentially fast to this stationary state, provided the dissipativity is sufficiently strong. This dissipativity assumption corresponds to the situation in which the temperature of the ferromagnetic material lies above the Curie point; see the discussion below.

Finally, in Theorem
8.1
, we show that as
\lambda_{e}\to 0^{+}
, the pathwise solution converges to that of the stochastic Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch (LLB) equation, given by (
1.1a
) with
\lambda_{e}=0
. In the deterministic setting (
\boldsymbol{g}_{k}=\boldsymbol{h}_{k}=\boldsymbol{0}
), this fact allows us to approximate the solution of the LLB equation by that of the LLBar equation, the latter can be solved numerically by a mixed finite element scheme efficiently
[
45
]
. In the stochastic setting, this result forms a basis for the mixed finite element method proposed in
[
26
]
, which further reduce the computational cost compared to the
C^{1}
-conforming method proposed in
[
24
]
.

We now provide some physical background of the problem under consideration. The standard model in the theory of ferromagnetism is the Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert (LLG) equation
[
33
]
. It is widely known, however, that this model is only valid at temperatures far below the Curie temperature of the material
[
5
,
12
]
. On the other hand, many modern magnetic devices, including the heat-assisted magnetic recording (HAMR)
[
54
]
and the thermally-assisted magnetic random access memory
[
43
]
, operate at temperatures very close to (or even exceeding) its Curie temperature. As such, it is essential to have an accurate physical model for magnetisation dynamics at elevated temperatures. Several micromagnetic models which are valid at high temperatures have been proposed in the literature, two of the earliest being the LLB equation
[
22
]
and the LLBar equation
[
3
,
4
]
.

Some comments on a version of the LLBar equation we study in this paper are in place now. In equations (
1.1a
) and (
1.1b
), the positive constants
\lambda_{r},\lambda_{e}
,
\alpha
, and
\gamma
are, respectively, the relativistic damping constant, exchange relaxation constant, exchange field constant, and electron gyromagnetic ratio. Physical considerations dictate that the constants
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are positive for temperatures below the Curie temperature, while above the Curie temperature
\kappa_{1}
is negative.
In (
1.1a
),

(1.3)

S(\boldsymbol{u}):=R(\boldsymbol{u})+L(\boldsymbol{u}),

where
L(\boldsymbol{u})
is a Lipschitz function of
\boldsymbol{u}
and
R(\boldsymbol{u})
is the spin-torque terms (see
[
2
,
35
,
44
,
48
,
52
,
53
]
) defined by

(1.4)

R(\boldsymbol{u}):=\beta_{1}(\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\beta_{2}\boldsymbol{u}\times(\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u},

with
\boldsymbol{\nu}(t):\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
being the spin current, and
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
being constants.
More precise assumptions on
L(\boldsymbol{u})
are elaborated in Section
2.2
.

To incorporate random perturbation into the dynamics, many approaches are available in the physics literature. A suggestion is made in
[
19
]
to perturb the precessional term and add a random torque, while another argument is put forth in
[
51
]
, by virtue of the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, to perturb only the precessional term. For the stochastic LLBar problem (
1.1
) considered in this paper, we generally add noise to the precessional term and append a random forcing term (cf. (
1.2
)), except in Theorem
2.6
and in Section
7
where noise enters only through the precessional term.

The deterministic version of (
1.1
) (corresponding to
G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u})\equiv 0
in (
1.1a
)) has been studied in
[
46
,
47
]
. Other fourth-order equations of Landau–Lifshitz-type are studied in
[
17
]
.
It is noted that systems of fourth-order equations also appear in certain areas of chemistry and biology.
In particular, if in (
1.1a
)
\gamma=0
and
\beta_{2}=0
, then we have a generalised reaction-diffusion-advection model with long range effects, which is prominently used in mathematical biology
[
38
, Chapter 11]
(see also
[
14
,
15
,
40
]
), and in chemistry to model anomalous bi-flux diffusion process
[
7
,
30
]
.

The stochastic versions of the LLG and LLB equations have been studied before, for instance in
[
1
,
10
,
11
,
25
]
. In particular, the well-posedness and long-time behaviour of the stochastic LLG equation, including large deviations results, have been studied in
[
9
,
28
]
. The existence of invariant measures for
d=1
has been established in
[
27
,
39
]
, while numerical simulations aimed at capturing the behaviour of invariant measures in two- or three-dimensional domains have been carried out in
[
6
]
. For the stochastic LLB equation, the existence of pathwise solutions and invariant measures for
d\leq 2
has been shown in
[
10
]
.
After the completion of this work, we become aware of the preprint
[
50
]
, which proves the unique existence of
analytically weak
pathwise global solutions for the stochastic LLBar equation for
d=1
. In contrast, our results establish the existence and uniqueness of
analytically strong
pathwise global solutions for dimensions
d\leq 3
. A key element of our analysis is a uniform
\mathbb{H}^{1}
estimate, obtained by exploiting the energy structure of the equation, which could also be employed to extend the results of
[
50
]
to higher dimensions. Furthermore, we show the existence of invariant measures supported on a more regular space and valid for
d\leq 3
, whereas
[
50
]
obtains such invariant measures only for
d=1
.

The main results of this paper are as follows:

(i)

existence and uniqueness of (probabilistically and analytically) strong solution (Theorem
2.3
),

(ii)

existence of a non-trivial invariant measure (Theorem
2.4
),

(iii)

exponential stability of the solution and uniqueness of invariant measure for temperatures above the Curie point, in the case where the noise enters only through the precessional term (Theorem
2.6
),

(iv)

convergence of the strong solution of the stochastic LLBar equation to that of the stochastic LLB equation in the limit of vanishing exchange relaxation parameter (Theorem
8.1
).

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
R
be the map defined in (

) and
\boldsymbol{\nu}
be given. Then for each
\epsilon>0

C_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\big|\left\langle R(\boldsymbol{v}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\big|

(2.16)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\epsilon}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D};\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\left(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}-\nabla\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Inequality (
2.15

2.16

\displaystyle\big|\left\langle R(\boldsymbol{v}),\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\left\langle\beta_{1}(\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}+\beta_{2}\boldsymbol{v}\times(\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\big|

we then have by Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle\big|\left\langle R(\boldsymbol{v})-R(\boldsymbol{w}),\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\big|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\epsilon}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D};\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}-\nabla\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{\epsilon}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D};\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

This completes the proof of the lemma.

3.
Faedo–Galerkin Approximation

Let
\{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
denote an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{L}^{2}

consisting of smooth eigenfunctions
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
of
-\Delta
such that

\displaystyle-\Delta\boldsymbol{e}_{i}=\mu_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\ \text{ in $\mathscr{D}$}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{e}_{i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \text{ on }\partial\mathscr{D},\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{N},

where
\mu_{i}\geq 0
are the eigenvalues of
-\Delta
, associated with

\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathbb{V}_{n}:=\text{span}\{\boldsymbol{e_{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e_{n}}\}
and
\Pi_{n}:\mathbb{L}^{2}\to\mathbb{V}_{n}
be the orthogonal projection defined by

(3.1)

\displaystyle\left\langle\Pi_{n}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{V}_{n},\;\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}.

Note that
\Pi_{n}
is self-adjoint and satisfies

(3.2)

\displaystyle\left\|{\Pi_{n}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{L}^{2},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla\Pi_{n}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{1}.

Moreover,

\left\langle\Pi_{n}\Delta\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=\left\langle\Delta\Pi_{n}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=-\left\langle\nabla\Pi_{n}\boldsymbol{v},\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{V}_{n},\;\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}.

The Faedo–Galerkin method seeks to approximate the solution
to (
1.1
) by
(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(t),\boldsymbol{H}_{n}(t))\in\mathbb{V}_{n}\times\mathbb{V}_{n}
satisfying

(3.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}=\big(\lambda_{r}\boldsymbol{H}_{n}-\lambda_{e}\Delta\boldsymbol{H}_{n}-\gamma\Pi_{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\times\boldsymbol{H}_{n})+\Pi_{n}S(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Pi_{n}G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\,\mathrm{d}W_{k}(t)&&\quad\text{in $(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}$,}\\
&\boldsymbol{H}_{n}=\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n}+\boldsymbol{u}_{n}-\Pi_{n}(|\boldsymbol{u}_{n}|^{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{n})&&\quad\text{in $(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}$,}\\
&\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0n}&&\quad\text{in $\mathscr{D}$,}\end{aligned}\right.

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{0n}=\Pi_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
. Note that
\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{H}_{n}\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
; thus in particular
\partial\boldsymbol{u}_{n}/\partial\boldsymbol{n}=\partial(\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n})/\partial\boldsymbol{n}=\partial\boldsymbol{H}_{n}/\partial\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\partial\mathscr{D}
.

Substituting the second equation in (
3.4
) into the first gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}

\displaystyle=\big((\lambda_{r}-\lambda_{e})\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n}-\lambda_{e}\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}+\lambda_{r}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}-\lambda_{r}\Pi_{n}(|\boldsymbol{u}_{n}|^{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\lambda_{e}\Delta\Pi_{n}(|\boldsymbol{u}_{n}|^{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{n})-\gamma\Pi_{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\times\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n})

(3.5)

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma\Pi_{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\times\Pi_{n}(|\boldsymbol{u}_{n}|^{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}))+\Pi_{n}S(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Pi_{n}G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\,\mathrm{d}W_{k}(t).

The existence of a unique local strong solution to (
3.5
), hence to (
3.4
), follows from Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
shown in the following and
[
13
, Theorem 10.6]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
, define

\displaystyle F_{n}^{1}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\Delta\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{v},

\displaystyle F_{n}^{2}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=-\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{v},

\displaystyle F_{n}^{3}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=-\Pi_{n}(|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\boldsymbol{v}),

\displaystyle F_{n}^{4}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\Pi_{n}\Delta(|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\boldsymbol{v}),

\displaystyle F_{n}^{5}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\Pi_{n}(\boldsymbol{v}\times\Delta\boldsymbol{v}),

\displaystyle F_{n}^{6}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\Pi_{n}(\boldsymbol{v}\times\Pi_{n}(|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\boldsymbol{v})),

\displaystyle F_{n}^{7}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\Pi_{n}S(\boldsymbol{v}),

\displaystyle G_{n}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\Pi_{n}G_{k}(\boldsymbol{v}).

Then
F_{n}^{j}
,
j=1
,
2,\ldots,7
, are well-defined mappings from
\mathbb{V}_{n}

into
\mathbb{V}_{n}
. Moreover,
F_{n}^{1}
,
F_{n}^{2}
, and
G_{n}
are globally Lipschitz while

F_{n}^{3}
,
F_{n}^{4}
,
F_{n}^{5}
,
F_{n}^{6}
, and
F_{n}^{7}
are locally Lipschitz.

Proof.

Global Lipschitz continuity for
F_{n}^{1}
and
F_{n}^{2}
, and local Lipschitz continuity for
F_{n}^{3}
,
F_{n}^{4}
, and
F_{n}^{5}
were shown in
[
46
, Lemma 3.2]
. Also,
G_{n}
is linear, so it is clearly globally Lipschitz. Next, for any
\boldsymbol{v}
,
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\left\|{F_{n}^{6}(\boldsymbol{v})-F_{n}^{6}(\boldsymbol{w})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}\times F_{n}^{3}(\boldsymbol{v})-\boldsymbol{w}\times F_{n}^{3}(\boldsymbol{w})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}\times\big(F_{n}^{3}(\boldsymbol{v})-F_{n}^{3}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{w})\times F_{n}^{3}(\boldsymbol{w})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{F_{n}^{3}(\boldsymbol{v})-F_{n}^{3}(\boldsymbol{w})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{3}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

Since all norms on
\mathbb{V}_{n}
are equivalent and
F_{n}^{3}
is locally Lipschitz,
F_{n}^{6}
is locally Lipschitz.

Finally,
F_{n}^{7}(\boldsymbol{v})=\Pi_{n}R(\boldsymbol{v})+\Pi_{n}L(\boldsymbol{v})
. The map
L
is globally Lipschitz by assumption. Moreover, for any
\boldsymbol{v}
,
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\left\|{\Pi_{n}R(\boldsymbol{v})-\Pi_{n}R(\boldsymbol{w})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{(\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla)(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{w})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\beta\left\|{(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{w})\times(\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\beta\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}\times(\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla)(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{w})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D};\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}+\beta\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D};\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}+\beta\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D};\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}.

Since all norms on
\mathbb{V}_{n}
are equivalent, this implies
F_{n}^{7}
is locally Lipschitz. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\sum_{j=1}^{7}F_{n}^{j}(\boldsymbol{v}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\|{\Pi_{n}G_{k}(\boldsymbol{v})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq C_{n}\left(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right),

where
C_{n}
is a constant, possibly depending on
n
.

Proof.

We will estimate each term on the left-hand side. Firstly, since
\boldsymbol{v},\Delta\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
, using integration by parts we have

\displaystyle\left\langle F_{n}^{1}(\boldsymbol{v})+F_{n}^{2}(\boldsymbol{v}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=-\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Moreover, noting (
3.1
) and (
2.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle F_{n}^{3}(\boldsymbol{v})+F_{n}^{4}(\boldsymbol{v}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=-\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}-\left\|{|\boldsymbol{v}|\,|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\nabla|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq-\left\|{|\boldsymbol{v}|\,|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Similarly, by (
3.1
) and the fact that
(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b})\cdot\boldsymbol{a}=0
for any
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle F_{n}^{5}(\boldsymbol{v})+F_{n}^{6}(\boldsymbol{v}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=0.

Also, by Young’s inequality and the assumptions on
\boldsymbol{\nu}
and
L
,

\displaystyle\left\langle F_{n}^{7}(\boldsymbol{v}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=\left\langle R(\boldsymbol{v})+L(\boldsymbol{v}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathscr{D};\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{|\boldsymbol{v}|\,|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+C\big(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\big)

for any
\epsilon>0
. Finally, by Hölder’s inequality and (
3.2
),

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\|{\Pi_{n}G_{k}(\boldsymbol{v})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq C\sigma_{g}\sigma_{h}\left(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right).

Adding all the above inequalities yields the required result.
∎

Before proceeding to prove uniform bounds for the approximate solutions
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
, to simplify notations we write (
3.4
) as

(3.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}=F_{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{H}_{n})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{k=1}^{n}G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\,\mathrm{d}W_{k}(t),&&\quad\text{in $(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}$,}\\
&\boldsymbol{H}_{n}=\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n}+\boldsymbol{u}_{n}-\Pi_{n}(|\boldsymbol{u}_{n}|^{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{n})&&\quad\text{in $(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}$,}\\
&\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0n}&&\quad\text{in $\mathscr{D}$,}\end{aligned}\right.

where

(3.7)

\displaystyle F_{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{H}_{n})

\displaystyle:=\lambda_{r}\boldsymbol{H}_{n}-\lambda_{e}\Delta\boldsymbol{H}_{n}-\gamma\Pi_{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\times\boldsymbol{H}_{n})-\Pi_{n}S(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}),

(3.8)

\displaystyle G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})

\displaystyle:=\Pi_{n}(\boldsymbol{g}_{k}+\gamma\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\times\boldsymbol{h}_{k}).

Now, we prove uniform bounds for the approximate solutions
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

For any
p\geq 1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
t\in(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(\tau)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2p}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)|\,|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p}\right]\leq C\left(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2p}\right)e^{Ct},

where
C
is a positive constant depending on
p
,
\sigma_{g}
, and
\sigma_{h}
(but is independent of
n
and
t
).

Proof.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{V}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function defined by
\boldsymbol{v}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}
. Then

\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{h})=\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{h}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{k})=\left\langle\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{h}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{V}_{n}.

By Itô’s Lemma,

(3.9)

\mathrm{d}\psi(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})=\left\langle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

Therefore, by (
3.9
) and (
3.6
), we deduce that

(3.10)

\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}=\left(\left\langle F_{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{H}_{n}),\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\|{G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\langle G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}),\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}W_{k}(t).

Note that by (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), we have

\displaystyle\left\langle F_{n}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{H}_{n}),\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{r}\left\langle\boldsymbol{H}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\lambda_{e}\left\langle\Delta\boldsymbol{H}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\langle S(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}),\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{r}\left\langle\boldsymbol{H}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\lambda_{e}\left\langle\Delta\boldsymbol{H}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\langle L(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}),\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{e}-\lambda_{r})\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\lambda_{e}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\lambda_{r}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\lambda_{r}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}-\lambda_{e}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}_{n}||\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{n}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

(3.11)

\displaystyle\quad-2\lambda_{e}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\langle L(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}),\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

and

(3.12)

\left\langle G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}),\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=\left\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{k},\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

Substituting (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) into (
3.10
) and applying Hölder’s inequality yield

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)|\,|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+2\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s),\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{k},\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}W_{k}(s),

Therefore, taking expectation on both sides of (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(\tau)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2p}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)|\,|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p}\right]

The required estimate then follows by the Gronwall inequality.
∎

\mathbb{P}

Next, for the term

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}:=\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{B}:=\boldsymbol{H}_{1}-\boldsymbol{H}_{2}
, where
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
are martingale solutions of (
1.1
) (on the same probability space and with the same Wiener process).
Then
\boldsymbol{v}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\big(\lambda_{r}\boldsymbol{B}-\lambda_{e}\Delta\boldsymbol{B}-\gamma(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{H}_{1}+\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\times\boldsymbol{B})+R(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-R(\boldsymbol{u}_{2})+L(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-L(\boldsymbol{u}_{2})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{2})\right)\mathrm{d}W_{k}(t)

with
\boldsymbol{v}(0)=\boldsymbol{0}

Moreover, for any
R>0
, define the stopping time

(5.1)

\tau_{R}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\vee\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}>R\}.

Note that
\tau_{R}\to\infty
as
R\to\infty
.
Now, by Itô’s lemma,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\lambda_{r}\left\langle\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\lambda_{e}\left\langle\nabla\boldsymbol{B},\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\gamma\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\times\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\langle R(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-R(\boldsymbol{u}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\langle L(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-L(\boldsymbol{u}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|{G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{2})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t.

We have

(5.3)

\displaystyle\lambda_{r}\left\langle\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{r}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\lambda_{r}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\lambda_{r}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}|\,|\boldsymbol{v}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\lambda_{r}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\lambda_{r}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

(5.4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{e}\left\langle\nabla\boldsymbol{B},\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{e}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\lambda_{e}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\lambda_{e}\left\langle|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}|^{2}\boldsymbol{v},\Delta\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\lambda_{e}\left\langle\big((\boldsymbol{u}_{1}+\boldsymbol{u}_{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\big)\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\Delta\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

and

(5.5)

\displaystyle-\gamma\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\times\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\times\Delta\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

Substituting (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+(\lambda_{r}-\lambda_{e})\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(s))\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s),\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)|^{2}\boldsymbol{v}(s),\Delta\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\big((\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)+\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s))\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(s)\big)\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s),\Delta\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s-\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s)\times\Delta\boldsymbol{v}(s),\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle R(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-R(\boldsymbol{u}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle L(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-L(\boldsymbol{u}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{2})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

(5.6)

\displaystyle=:I_{1}(t)+I_{2}(t)+I_{3}(t)+I_{4}(t)+I_{5}(t)+I_{6}(t)+I_{7}(t)+I_{8}(t).

With
\tau_{R}
as defined in (

\displaystyle I_{4}(t\wedge\tau_{R})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{e}}{8}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{e}}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)+\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{S}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0})<t)

(6.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\rho_{R}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0})<t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{R}(1+e^{Ct}),

where
C
depends on
\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.3
, Proposition
3.7
, and Markov’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{S}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0})<t)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(\tau;\boldsymbol{v}_{0})}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}>S\right)\leq\frac{1}{S}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(\tau)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C}{S}(1+e^{Ct}),

thus (
6.13
) is shown.
Similarly, inequality (
6.14
) follows from Lemma
6.2
and Markov’s inequality.
∎

Next, define

(6.15)

\displaystyle\sigma_{S}

\displaystyle:=\sigma_{S}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m})\wedge\sigma_{S}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}),

(6.16)

\displaystyle\rho_{R}

\displaystyle:=\rho_{R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m})\wedge\rho_{R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}).

We can now show the Feller property of the transition semigroup
P_{t}
.

Proposition 6.5
.

The semigroup
(P_{t})
is Feller on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, that is
P_{t}\left(C_{b}(\mathbb{H}^{2})\right)\subset C_{b}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
be given and fix
t>0
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
be such that
\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}<1
. With
\sigma_{S}
and
\rho_{R}
as defined in (
6.15
) and (
6.16
) respectively, we write

\displaystyle\left|{P_{t}\phi(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m})-P_{t}\phi(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m}))-\phi(\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))\right]}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m}))-\phi(\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))\right]\mathbb{I}_{\{\sigma_{S}<t\}}}\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|{\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m}))-\phi(\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))\right]\mathbb{I}_{\{\rho_{R}<t\}}}\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|{\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m}))-\phi(\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))\right]\mathbb{I}_{\{\sigma_{S}\geq t\}}\mathbb{I}_{\{\rho_{R}\geq t\}}}\right|=:E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3}.

We will estimate each term in the following. To this end, let
\left\|{\phi}\right\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left|{\phi(\boldsymbol{v})}\right|
. By Lemma
6.4
,

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\infty}\left(\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{S}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m})<t)+\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{S}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})<t)\right)\leq\frac{C}{S}\left\|{\phi}\right\|_{\infty}(1+e^{Ct}).

Similarly,

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{R}\left\|{\phi}\right\|_{\infty}(1+e^{Ct}),

where
C
depends on
\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
. By taking
S
and
R
sufficiently large, we can ensure that
E_{1}+E_{2}<\epsilon/2
.

Next, we will estimate
E_{3}
. Note that on the set
\{\rho_{R}\geq t\}
, we have
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m},\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\boldsymbol{B}:=\boldsymbol{B}_{3}\left((R/t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
, where
\boldsymbol{B}_{3}(r)
is the closed ball in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with radius
r
centred at
\boldsymbol{0}
. On this ball
\boldsymbol{B}
(which is compact in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
since the embedding
\mathbb{H}^{3}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2}
is compact), we can approximate the given
\phi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
by a Lipschitz function
\varphi
with Lipschitz constant
L_{\varphi}
such that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{B}}\left|{\phi(\boldsymbol{v})-\varphi(\boldsymbol{v})}\right|<\frac{\epsilon}{8}.

Then by the Kirszbraun theorem
[
31
]
,
\varphi
can be extended to a Lipschitz function on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.
Therefore, by the triangle inequality, Jensen’s inequality, and Lemma
6.3
, we have

\displaystyle E_{3}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{B}}\left|{\phi(\boldsymbol{v})-\varphi(\boldsymbol{v})}\right|+L_{\varphi}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{(\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m}))-\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))\mathbb{I}_{\{\sigma_{S}\geq t\}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{4}+L_{\varphi}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t\wedge\sigma_{S}]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m})-\boldsymbol{u}(s;\boldsymbol{u}_{0})}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{4}+C_{S}L_{\varphi}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}},

which can be made less than
\epsilon/2
whenever
\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}<\epsilon/(4C_{S}L_{\varphi})=:\delta
. Hence, we have shown that if
\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}<\delta
, we have
\left|{P_{t}\phi(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{m})-P_{t}\phi(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})}\right|<\epsilon
. This completes the proof of the statement.
∎

Theorem
2.4
is an immediate consequence of the above lemmas, which we prove below.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

We have shown in Proposition
6.5
that the transition semigroup
\{P_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is Feller in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Next, we show tightness on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
of the family of probability measures
\{\mu_{T}\}_{T\geq 1}
given by

\mu_{T}(\cdot):=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}P_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Let
\boldsymbol{B}_{3}(R)
denote the closed ball in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with radius
R
centred at
\boldsymbol{0}
. Then, by Markov’s inequality and Lemma
6.1
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{T\geq 1}\mu_{T}\left(\mathbb{H}^{2}\setminus\boldsymbol{B}_{3}(R)\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{T\geq 1}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{P}\big(\{\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s;\boldsymbol{u}_{0})}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{3}}\geq R\}\big)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{T\geq 1}\frac{1}{TR}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{3}}\right]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{T\geq 1}\frac{C(1+T)}{TR}\leq\frac{2C}{R}\to 0\;\text{ as }R\to\infty.

Since the embedding
\mathbb{H}^{3}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2}
is compact, the family of probability measures
\{\mu_{T}\}_{T\geq 1}
is tight on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.
This yields the existence of at least one invariant measure by the Krylov–Bogoliubov theorem.

We now show that such an invariant measure
\mu
is supported on
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. By the invariance property of
\mu
, for any
T>0
and
\phi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
we have

(6.17)

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\phi(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}P_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u})\phi(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}).

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
R>0
, and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
, let

\Psi_{n,R}(\boldsymbol{v}):=\left\|{\nabla\Delta\Pi_{n}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\wedge R,

where
\Pi_{n}
is the projection operator defined in (
3.1
).
Note that for any
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\boldsymbol{B}_{2}(\alpha)
, the ball of radius
\alpha
about the origin in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, by Lemma
6.1
we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}P_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u})\Psi_{n,R}(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}t\right|=\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Psi_{n,R}(\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\leq C\left(1+\frac{1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}}{T}\right)\leq C\left(1+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{T}\right).

Then by (
6.17
) and the above inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\Psi_{n,R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}P_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u})\Psi_{n,R}(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\mathrm{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{B}_{2}(\alpha)}\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Psi_{n,R}(\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\mathrm{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{H}^{2}\setminus\boldsymbol{B}_{2}(\alpha)}\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Psi_{n,R}(\boldsymbol{u}(t;\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\mathrm{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\alpha^{2}T^{-1})\,\mu(\boldsymbol{B}_{2}(\alpha))+R\,\mu(\mathbb{H}^{2}\setminus\boldsymbol{B}_{2}(\alpha)),

where
C
is a constant which does not depend on
T
,
\alpha
, and
R
. First, we take
\alpha
to be sufficiently large (depending on
R
) so that
R\,\mu(\mathbb{H}^{2}\setminus\boldsymbol{B}_{2}(\alpha))\leq 1
. Then we choose
T
sufficiently large (depending on
\alpha
), so that
\alpha^{2}T^{-1}\leq 1
. This implies

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\Psi_{n,R}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\leq C,

where
C
is independent of
n
and
R
. Letting
n\to\infty
, by Fatou’s lemma we have

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left(\left\|{\nabla\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\wedge R\right)\mathrm{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\leq C,

where
C
is independent of
R
. By the monotone convergence theorem, taking
R\uparrow\infty
, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\left\|{\nabla\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{u}_{0})<\infty.

In particular, this implies that
\mu
is supported on
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

The existence of an ergodic invariant measure follows by standard arguments. The set of all invariant measures
K
considered in the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is convex and compact, hence a convex hull of the set of its extremal points by the Krein-Milman theorem. Then, by Proposition 11.12 in
[
16
]
every extremal point of
K
is an ergodic invariant measure.
This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

7.
Proof of Theorem
2.6
: Exponential Stability above the Curie Temperature

We shall consider a special case of the stochastic LLBar equation (
1.1
), namely one perturbed by a random precession (multiplicative noise)
above
the Curie temperature in the absence of the spin current. In other words, we assume
\kappa_{1}=-1
,
\boldsymbol{g}_{k}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
, and
S(\boldsymbol{u})\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
in this section. We also assume that
\lambda_{r}>\lambda_{e}
. In this case, we have an exponential stability result, which implies the uniqueness of invariant measure under certain conditions. To this end, we start with the following lemmas.

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose that
\lambda_{r}>\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}
and let
\mu:=\lambda_{r}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}
. Let
\boldsymbol{u}
be the solution of (
1.1
) given by Theorem
2.2
, corresponding to the case
\kappa_{1}=-1
,
\kappa_{2}=1
,
\boldsymbol{g}_{k}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
, and
S(\boldsymbol{u})\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
. Then
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for all
t>0
,

(7.1)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq e^{-\mu t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

Furthermore,

(7.2)

\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}\mathrm{d}s\leq K_{0}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

where
K_{0}
is a constant independent of
t
.

Proof.

Applying Itô’s lemma to the process
\frac{1}{2}e^{\mu t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\left(e^{\mu t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mu e^{\mu t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e^{\mu t}\Big(-(\lambda_{r}+\lambda_{e})\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\lambda_{e}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\lambda_{r}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-\lambda_{r}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}

\displaystyle\quad-\lambda_{e}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}||\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}-2\lambda_{e}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{h}_{k}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Integrating and applying Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}e^{\mu t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{r}+\lambda_{e})\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mu s}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mu s}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mu s}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mu s}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mu s}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}(s)|\,|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}(s)|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mu s}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s)\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mu s}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Noting
\mu>0
, we obtain the required result after rearranging the terms.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

Under the same assumptions as in Lemma
7.1
, there exists a constant
K_{1}
such that for any
t>0
,

(7.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left(\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}(\tau)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(\tau)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{H}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\leq K_{1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{4}.

The constant
K_{1}
is independent of
t
.

Proof.

The proof follows the same argument as that of Proposition
3.5
, but with
\boldsymbol{g}_{k}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
and
S(\boldsymbol{u})\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
.
∎

Under a slightly stronger dissipativity condition, we have the following exponential moment bound.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\delta>0
and suppose that
\lambda_{r}\geq 3\gamma^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}
. Then for any
t>0
,

(7.4)

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\delta\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\delta\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}\right)\right]\leq\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\delta\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\delta\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}\right).

Proof.

Let
\psi(t):=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}
. Applying Itô’s lemma to the process
\exp(\delta\psi(t))
, we have

\displaystyle e^{\delta\psi(t)}+\lambda_{r}\delta\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s)}\left\|{\boldsymbol{H}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{e}\delta\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s)}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{H}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=e^{\delta\psi(0)}+\frac{\delta\gamma^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s)}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s)\times\boldsymbol{h}_{k}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\delta\gamma^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s)}\left\|{\nabla(\boldsymbol{u}(s)\times\boldsymbol{h}_{k})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{3\delta\gamma^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s)}\left\|{\left|{\boldsymbol{u}(s)}\right|\left|{\boldsymbol{u}(s)\times\boldsymbol{h}_{k}}\right|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\delta\gamma^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s)}\left|{\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}(s)\times\boldsymbol{h}_{k},\boldsymbol{H}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad-\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s)}\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}(s)\times\boldsymbol{h}_{k},\boldsymbol{H}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}W_{k}(s).

For any
R>0
, define the stopping time
\tau_{R}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\psi(t)\geq R\right\}
. Therefore, integrating over
[0,t\wedge\tau_{R}]
and taking the expectation, noting that
\boldsymbol{H}=\Delta\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}-\left|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right|^{2}\boldsymbol{u}
, following the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
3.5
and Lemma
6.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\delta\psi(t\wedge\tau_{R})}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\lambda_{r}\delta\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\left\|{\boldsymbol{H}(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\lambda_{e}\delta\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{H}(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\delta\psi(0)}+\frac{5}{2}\delta\gamma^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}\right)\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\delta\gamma^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\left\|{\left|{\boldsymbol{u}(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\right|\left|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\right|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right].

Now, note that

\left\|{\boldsymbol{H}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}=\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\left\|{\left|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right|\left|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}}\right|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+4\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{6}}^{6}.

Thus, applying Young’s inequality, we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\delta\psi(t\wedge\tau_{R})}\right]+\lambda_{r}\delta\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\left\|{\boldsymbol{H}(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\lambda_{e}\delta\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{H}(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\delta\psi(0)}+3\delta\gamma^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{\delta\psi(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\left\|{\boldsymbol{H}(s\wedge\tau_{R})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right].

If
\lambda_{r}\geq 3\gamma^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}
, then we can absorb the last term to the left-hand side, which implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\delta\psi(t\wedge\tau_{R})}\right]\leq e^{\delta\psi(0)}.

By Fatou’s lemma and continuity in time of
\psi
, taking
R\to\infty
, we have the required inequality.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
2.6
.

Proof of Theorem
2.6
.

Firstly, inequality (
2.7
) is shown in Lemma
7.1
.

Now, let
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
be solutions corresponding to initial data
\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
, respectively. We will show an exponential stability result, namely there are constants
C
and
\alpha
independent of
t
such that

(7.5)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right]\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

which implies the uniqueness of invariant measure.

To this end, let
\boldsymbol{v}:=\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
. By Itô’s lemma applied to
\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+(\lambda_{r}-\lambda_{e})\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)|\,|\boldsymbol{v}(s)|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s)|\,|\boldsymbol{v}(s)|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(s))\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s),\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)|^{2}\boldsymbol{v}(s),\Delta\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\big((\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)+\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s))\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(s)\big)\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s),\Delta\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s-\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s)\times\Delta\boldsymbol{v}(s),\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}_{2})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

(7.6)

\displaystyle=:\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}+J_{4}+J_{5}.

For the first term on the right-hand side, we have by Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle J_{1}\leq\frac{\lambda_{r}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)|\,|\boldsymbol{v}(s)|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\lambda_{r}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)|\,|\boldsymbol{v}(s)|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

For the remaining terms, similarly we have

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{4}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\lambda_{e}}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle J_{3}

\displaystyle\leq 8\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{4}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{4}\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\lambda_{e}}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle J_{4}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\lambda_{e}}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\lambda_{e}}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle J_{5}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{h}\gamma^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Thus, from (
7.6
) we obtain

(7.7)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\,\exp\left(-2\lambda_{r}t+R(t)\right),

where

(7.8)

R(t):=12\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{4}\mathrm{d}s+8\lambda_{e}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{4}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\lambda_{e}}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\sigma_{h}\gamma^{2}.

We will estimate
R(t)
defined above.

Case 1 (
d=1
)
: In this case, by Agmon’s inequality we have

R(t)\leq 12\lambda_{e}C_{\mathrm{A}}K_{0}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{4}+8\lambda_{e}C_{\mathrm{A}}K_{0}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{4}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\lambda_{e}}C_{\mathrm{S}}K_{0}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\sigma_{h}\gamma^{2}=:\Gamma_{1},

where
K_{0}
is the constant in (
7.2
),
C_{\mathrm{S}}
is the constant in the Sobolev embedding
\mathbb{H}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
, and
C_{\mathrm{A}}
is the constant in Agmon’s inequality
\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\leq C_{\mathrm{A}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}
.
Therefore, from (
7.7
) we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right]\leq e^{\Gamma_{1}}e^{-2\lambda_{r}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Here,
\Gamma_{1}
is independent of
t
.

Case 2 (
d=2
)
: Similarly, by Agmon’s inequality we have from (
7.8
),

R(t)\leq 12\lambda_{e}C_{\mathrm{A}}^{\prime}K_{0}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{4}+8\lambda_{e}C_{\mathrm{A}}^{\prime}K_{0}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{4}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\lambda_{e}}C_{\mathrm{S}}^{\prime}K_{0}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\sigma_{h}\gamma^{2}=:\Gamma_{2},

where
K_{0}
is the constant in (
7.3
),
C_{\mathrm{S}}^{\prime}
is the constant in the Sobolev embedding
\mathbb{H}^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
, and
C_{\mathrm{A}}^{\prime}
is the constant in Agmon’s inequality
\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\leq C_{\mathrm{A}}^{\prime}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
.
Therefore, from (
7.7
) we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right]\leq e^{\Gamma_{2}}e^{-2\lambda_{r}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Here,
\Gamma_{2}
is independent of
t
.

Case 3 (
d=3
)
: In this case, we cannot bound
R(t)
pathwise as in previous cases. Instead, taking expectation on both sides of (
7.7
), we have

(7.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right]\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\,e^{-2\lambda_{r}t}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(R(t))\right].

Note that by Agmon’s inequality and (
7.2
),

\displaystyle R(t)

\displaystyle\leq 12\lambda_{e}C_{\mathrm{A}}^{\prime\prime}K_{0}\left(\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(\tau)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+8\lambda_{e}C_{\mathrm{A}}^{\prime\prime}K_{0}\left(\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(\tau)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\lambda_{e}}C_{\mathrm{S}}^{\prime}K_{0}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\sigma_{h}\gamma^{2}.

Therefore, under the assumption that
\lambda_{r}\geq 3\gamma^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}
, applying Lemma
7.3
to (
7.9
) yields

(7.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right]\leq e^{\Gamma_{3}}e^{-2\lambda_{r}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

where
\Gamma_{3}:=24\lambda_{e}C_{\mathrm{A}}^{\prime\prime}K_{0}\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{6}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0,2}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{6}\right)
is obtained by applying (
7.4
). The constant
C_{\mathrm{A}}^{\prime\prime}
is the constant in Agmon’s inequality
\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\leq C_{\mathrm{A}}^{\prime\prime}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
. Thus, we have a bound of the form (
7.5
) when
\lambda_{r}\geq 3\gamma^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}
.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

8.
Relationship between the Stochastic LLBar and the Stochastic LLB Equations

Recall that the stochastic LLB equation is (
1.1
) with
\lambda_{e}=0
and
\kappa_{1}=-1
. The existence of a strong solution for the stochastic LLB equation is obtained in
[
10
]
for
d=1,2
. In the following theorem, we show the convergence of the strong solution of the stochastic LLBar equation (with
\kappa_{1}<0
) to that of the stochastic LLB equation.

Theorem 8.1
.

For
d\in\{1,2\}
, let
(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{H}^{\varepsilon})
be a strong solution of (
1.1
) with
\lambda_{e}=\varepsilon
(where
\varepsilon<\lambda_{r}
) and initial data
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Let
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{H})
be a strong solution of the stochastic LLB equation with the same initial data
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[0,t]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(r)-\boldsymbol{u}(r)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(s)-\boldsymbol{u}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]=0.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}:=\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}-\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{B}:=\boldsymbol{H}^{\varepsilon}-\boldsymbol{H}
. Then
\boldsymbol{v}
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\big(\lambda_{r}\boldsymbol{B}-\varepsilon\Delta\boldsymbol{H}^{\varepsilon}-\gamma(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}\times\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{H})+R(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon})-R(\boldsymbol{u})+L(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon})-L(\boldsymbol{u})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon})-G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u})\right)\mathrm{d}W_{k}(t)

with
\boldsymbol{v}(0)=\boldsymbol{0}
. Applying Itô’s lemma, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\lambda_{r}\left\langle\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\varepsilon\left\langle\nabla\boldsymbol{H}^{\varepsilon},\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}-\gamma\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}\times\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\langle R(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon})-R(\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\langle L(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon})-L(\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|{G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon})-G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Integrating this with respect to
t
, noting (
5.3
) and (
5.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(s)|\,|\boldsymbol{v}(s)|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\nabla\boldsymbol{H}^{\varepsilon}(s),\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s+\lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(s)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(s))\boldsymbol{u}(s),\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\boldsymbol{v}(s)\times\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(s),\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle R(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon})-R(\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle L(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon})-L(\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon})-G_{k}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

(8.1)

\displaystyle=:I_{1}(t)+I_{2}(t)+I_{3}(t)+I_{4}(t)+I_{5}(t)+I_{6}(t).

Now for any
R>0
, define the stopping time

(8.2)

\tau_{R}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}\vee\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{4}\mathrm{d}s>R\right\}.

We will derive bounds for
I_{j}(t\wedge\tau_{R})
, where
j=1,2,\ldots,6
, by using Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities and the Sobolev embedding as follows. For the terms besides
I_{3}
and
I_{4}
, we have

(8.3)

\displaystyle I_{1}(t\wedge\tau_{R})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\lambda_{r}}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{H}^{\varepsilon}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\lambda_{r}}{8}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

(8.4)

\displaystyle I_{2}(t\wedge\tau_{R})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{r}}{2}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(s)|\,|\boldsymbol{v}(s)|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\lambda_{r}}{2}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}(s)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

(8.5)

\displaystyle I_{5}(t\wedge\tau_{R})

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

(8.6)

\displaystyle I_{6}(t\wedge\tau_{R})

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{h}\gamma^{2}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

For the term
I_{3}
, we use Lemma
2.8
to obtain

(8.7)

\displaystyle I_{3}(t\wedge\tau_{R})\leq C\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\Phi\big(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(s)\big)\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\lambda_{r}}{4}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

where
\Phi
was defined in (
2.14
). For the term
I_{4}
, by (
2.17
) we have

(8.8)

\displaystyle I_{4}(t\wedge\tau_{R})\leq C\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D};\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\left(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\lambda_{r}}{8}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

The constant
C
is independent of
\varepsilon
. We now apply the estimates (
8.3
), (
8.4
), (
8.5
), (
8.6
), (
8.7
), (
8.8
), and the Gronwall inequality on (
8.1
). Noting (
8.2
) and (
3.5
), we have

\displaystyle\sup_{r\in[0,t\wedge\tau_{R}]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(r)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon\left(\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{H}^{\varepsilon}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)\cdot\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}+\Phi\big(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(s)\big)\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon e^{C(t+R^{2})}\left(\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{H}^{\varepsilon}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right).

Taking expectation and applying Proposition
3.5
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[0,t\wedge\tau_{R}]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(r)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\leq C\varepsilon e^{C(t+R^{2})},

where
C
is independent of
\varepsilon
by Remark
3.4
. Therefore, for any
R>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[0,t\wedge\tau_{R}]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(r)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}(s)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]=0.

Taking
R\uparrow\infty
and applying the monotone convergence theorem, we obtain the required result.
∎
