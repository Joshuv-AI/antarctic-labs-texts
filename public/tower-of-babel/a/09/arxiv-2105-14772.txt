Energy-Efficient and Federated Meta-Learning via Projected Stochastic Gradient Ascent
Anis Elgabli, Chaouki Ben Issaid, Amrit S. Bedi † , Mehdi Bennis, Vaneet Aggarwal ∗

Abstract
In this paper, we propose an energy-efficient federated meta-learning framework. The objective is to enable learning a meta-model that can be fine-tuned to a new task with a few number of samples in a distributed setting and at low computation and communication energy consumption. We assume that each task is owned by a separate agent, so a limited number of tasks is used to train a meta-model. Assuming each task was trained offline on the agent’s local data, we propose a lightweight algorithm that starts from the local models of all agents, and in a backward manner using projected stochastic gradient ascent (P-SGA) finds a meta-model. The proposed method avoids complex computations such as computing hessian, double looping, and matrix inversion, while achieving high performance at significantly less energy consumption compared to the state-of-the-art methods such as MAML and iMAML on conducted experiments for sinusoid regression and image classification tasks.



I
Introduction

In many artificial intelligence problems, the aim is to develop systems that use previous experience to acquire new skills more effectively. To fulfill this objective, meta-learning has been proposed as a framework that enables an agent to quickly learn a new task by leveraging an optimized initializer that is trained using similar tasks
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. One way to formulate the meta-learning problem is to cast it as a bi-level optimization problem
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
, involving two loops: (i) an inner loop optimization that represents adaptation to a given task, and (ii) an outer loop optimization with the objective to train a meta-model. In
[
5
]
, the authors proposed the model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML) formulation, which is shown to perform well in terms of convergence and fast adaptation to new task. However, despite its convergence and fast adaptation properties, solving MAML formulation requires backpropagation through the inner optimization process. Consequently, it requires higher-order derivative (Hessian’s computation) which imposes high computational cost and vanishing gradients. These issues limit the scalability of MAML especially when the local models are multiple SGD steps from the initializer. First-order MAML (FOMAML)
[
5
]
and Reptile
[
7
]
are two first-order MAML-type algorithms which avoid second-order derivatives. Though both algorithms reduce the computational cost, they both ignore the dependence of task-models on the meta-models.

In
[
6
]
, MAML with implicit gradients (iMAML) was proposed with the goal of reducing the computational cost of MAML by leveraging implicit differentiation. The authors derived an analytical expression for the meta-gradient that depends only on the solution to the inner optimization and not the path taken by the inner optimization algorithm. However, this expression involves matrix inversion that has been approximated by solving a problem that involves using conjugate gradient (CG) method. Though iMAML reduces the computation cost compared to MAML, it still involves high computation cost due to: (i) the two loop procedure, (ii) the hessian computation which could be computationally expensive for large models, and (iii) the matrix inversion procedure using CG. Due to these limitations, running iMAML in a distributed environment that involves local computation and communication using energy-limited IoT devices is not feasible. Moreover, iMAML assumes a large number of labeled tasks since at every iteration, it samples a batch of these tasks, which may not be practical in cases where there are only few tasks owned by different agents. Therefore, in this paper, we propose a distributed and energy-efficient meta-learning approach that overcomes these limitations and ensures efficient learning.

Standard federated learning solves the problem of
N
workers trying to learn a shared global model with the aid of a parameter server (PS). Each worker is exposed to limited data samples and is not willing to share its data due to privacy and/or communication constraints
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. The PS starts with an initial guess of the global model, shares it with all contributing workers where each worker performs a local computation step before sharing its output. The local computation step could be (i) calculating the gradient of the local function with respect to the global model using a randomly selected mini-batch
[
11
]
or (ii) performing a number of local stochastic gradient descent (SGD) steps using the global model as initializer
[
12
]
. Then the PS aggregates the outputs of all workers and performs one global step which is one SGD step using the aggregated gradients of all workers for (i), or model averaging for (ii). Under some assumptions, this leads to convergence to a global model shared across workers.

Similarly, in federated (distributed) meta-learning, every task is owned by an agent and each iteration involves communication with the PS. At each iteration, every agent updates its local model and uploads it to the PS where the meta-model is updated in a global step and pushed to all agents. Since this involves local computation and communication energy consumption, it is crucial to minimize the computation cost and save energy for communication. Motivated by this, we propose a novel formulation that can be solved very efficiently without involving double loop and Hessian computation. Particularly, at every iteration, we show that our inner problem can be solved in a simple and closed form expression that involves one SGD step and projection. Experiments using sinusoid regression and image classification tasks show that our proposed approach significantly outperforms iMAML in terms of energy consumption.

II
Review of MAML and IMAML

In this section, we will describe the concept and briefly highlight the key steps of MAML and iMAML.

II-A

MAML

Let
\{\mathcal{T}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be a set of meta-training tasks assumed to be drawn from
P(\mathcal{T})
. For each task
\mathcal{T}_{i}
, we can sample two disjoint datasets: a training dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{i}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{i}^{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
and a test dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{test}}_{i}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{j},\mathbf{y}_{i}^{j})\}_{j=1}^{J}
, where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
denote the inputs and outputs, respectively.

Introducing the loss function
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi},\mathcal{D})
where
\bm{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, each task
\mathcal{T}_{i}
aims to learn task-specific parameters
\bm{\phi}_{i}
using
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{i}
by minimizing its test loss,
\mathcal{L}({\bm{\phi}}_{i},\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{test}}_{i})
. MAML seeks to learn
\{\bm{\phi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
using a set of meta-parameters
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the training datasets from each task. The objective is to learn the meta-parameter that achieve good task specific parameters after adaptation. The meta-learning problem can be cast as the following bi-level optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{\theta}},\{\bm{\phi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ \mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\phi}_{i}({\bm{\theta}}),\ \mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{test}}_{i}\right).

(1)

Giving a new task
\mathcal{T}_{i}\sim P(\mathcal{T})
, MAML can achieve a good generalization performance by using the adaptation procedure with the learned parameters
{\bm{\phi}}_{i}
using
{\bm{\theta}}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{i}
. More specifically, in the inner level of MAML,
{\bm{\phi}}_{i}
can be updated using gradient descent initialized at
{\bm{\theta}}
.

\displaystyle{\bm{\phi}}_{i}={\bm{\theta}}-\alpha\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}},\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{i}),

(2)

where
\alpha
is the learning rate. Note that MAML also allows updating
{\bm{\phi}}_{i}
by running multiple gradient descent steps starting from
{\bm{\theta}}
. However, a major concern with MAML is that updating the meta-model according to

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}-\eta\ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{d\bm{\phi}_{i}({\bm{\theta}})}{d{\bm{\theta}}}\ \nabla_{\bm{\phi}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i}({\bm{\theta}}),\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{test}}_{i}),

(3)

when
\bm{\phi}_{i}({\bm{\theta}})
is
K
SGD steps from
{\bm{\theta}}
requires backpropagating through the dynamics of the
K
steps, which leads to a significant computation and memory burden.

II-B

MAML with Implicit Gradients (iMAML)

Due to requirements on memory and higher-order derivatives, MAML with Implicit Gradients (iMAML) was proposed in
[
6
]
to address the challenge of differentiating through the long optimization path in MAML. In addition to that, iMAML proposes to incorporate regularization into the problem formulation to enable appropriate leaning in the inner level, while preventing over-fitting. In this case, the inner-level problem is formulated as

\displaystyle\underset{{\bm{\phi}}}{\min}~\mathcal{L}({\bm{\phi}},\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}~||{\bm{\phi}}-{\bm{\theta}}||^{2},

(4)

where
\lambda
is a scalar hyperparameter that controls the regularization strength. The introduced regularization term in (
4
) enforces
{\bm{\phi}}_{i}
to remain close to
{\bm{\theta}}
. Ideally, the problem in (
4
) is solved using iterative algorithms and the optimal
\bm{\phi}
is determined. In this case,
the term
d\bm{\phi}_{i}({\bm{\theta}})/d{\bm{\theta}}
can be explicitly derived as

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\phi}_{i}({\bm{\theta}})}{d{\bm{\theta}}}=\left(\bm{I}+\frac{1}{\lambda}~\nabla_{\bm{\phi}}^{2}\mathcal{L}({\bm{\phi}}_{i},\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{tr}}_{i})\right)^{-1}.

(5)

According to iMAML, the problem in (
4
) is solved up to
\delta
-optimal solution, and the matrix inversion in (
5
) is determined by approximating a solution of an equivalent problem using a few conjugate gradient (CG) steps.

III
Problem Formulation

In the standard MAML problem defined over
N
different tasks, the goal is to find an initial point
\theta^{\star}
such that when the local task
i
runs its local
K
SGD steps, it provides a better personalization for it. Hence, mathematically, we can write the problem of MAML as

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta},\{\bm{\phi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i}^{K}(\bm{\theta}),\mathcal{D}_{i}^{test})

(6)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\ \ \bm{\phi}_{i}^{K}(\bm{\theta})=

\displaystyle SGD_{K}^{\alpha}\left(\bm{\theta},\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta}),\mathcal{D}_{i}^{tr})\right),\forall i

(7)

where

denotes that stochastic gradient descent is used for each task with

as the starting point, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{i}^{K}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\phi}_{i}^{0}-\alpha\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\nabla_{\bm{\phi}_{i}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i},\mathcal{D}_{i}^{tr})\Big|_{\bm{\phi}_{i}=\bm{\phi}_{i}^{k}(\bm{\theta})},\forall i,

(8)

where
\bm{\phi}_{i}^{0}=\bm{\theta}
and
\alpha
is the learning rate. Now, let us see the importance of the starting point
\bm{\theta}
for each task in more detail. The starting point actually controls the number of iterations required to converge to the local optima
\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\star}(\bm{\theta})
. If the local function is strongly convex, then the number of iterations required for
\epsilon
-optimal solution is given by
[
13
]

\displaystyle K_{i}\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\epsilon}\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},\mathcal{D}_{i}^{tr})-\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i}^{K_{i}}(\bm{\theta}),\mathcal{D}_{i}^{tr})}{\epsilon}\right)\right).

(9)

Hence for each task
i
, the number of steps
(K_{i})
required to achieve
\epsilon
-optimal depends upon the term
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},\mathcal{D}_{i}^{tr})-\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i}^{K_{i}}(\bm{\theta}),\mathcal{D}_{i}^{tr})
controlled by the initialization
\bm{\theta}
for each task
i
. The goal of standard meta learning is to learn a common initializer
\bm{\theta}
such that for each task
i
, after running a number of SGD steps using few data samples, we are able to obtain a good solution. So the placement of the initializer
\bm{\theta}
in the space of local solutions
\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\star}(\bm{\theta})
is the task.

We take a one step back and try to look into the problem in a backward manner. For instance, if we are given the local
\epsilon
-optimal solutions
\bm{\phi}_{i,\epsilon}^{\star}
(note that they are independent of
\bm{\theta}
) for each task
i
, then
what should be the initializer point
\bm{\theta}
so that each
\bm{\phi}_{i,\epsilon}^{\star}
is just
K_{i}
SGD steps away from

?
Since the final number of steps to converge to local optimal depends upon
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},\mathcal{D}_{i}^{tr})-\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i,\epsilon}^{\star},\mathcal{D}_{i}^{tr})
, the best initializer point can be obtained by solving the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta},\{\bm{\phi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\ \

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},D_{i}^{tr})-\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i,\epsilon}^{\star},\mathcal{D}_{i}^{tr})\right)^{2}

(10)

s.t.

\displaystyle SGD_{K_{i}}^{\alpha}\left(\bm{\theta},\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i}(\bm{\theta}),\mathcal{D}_{i}^{tr})\right)=\bm{\phi}_{i,\epsilon}^{\star},\forall i

(11)

where
\bm{\phi}_{i,\epsilon}^{\star}
is known. Solving (
10
)-(
11
) is as hard as solving the original MAML problem. Motivated by the fact that
\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\star}
is known and computed offline for every agent, we propose an approximate formulation for (

\displaystyle\min_{\{\bm{\phi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\ \

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i}^{k})-\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i}^{k+1}+\alpha\nabla_{\bm{\phi}_{i}^{k+1}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i},\mathcal{D}_{i}))\right)^{2}

(12)

s.t.

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\phi}_{i}^{k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{\phi}_{i}^{k+1}\right\|^{2}\leq\delta_{k},\forall i,k=0,\cdots,K-1

(13)

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{i}^{K}=\bm{\phi}_{i,\epsilon}^{\star},\forall i

(14)

Note that
\bm{\theta}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{\phi}_{i}^{0}
, and
\delta_{0}\leq\delta_{1}\leq\dots\leq\delta_{K-1}
. When

Let’s now intuitively explain the relation between the formulation in (

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{i}^{k}=\bm{\phi}_{i}^{k+1}+\alpha\nabla_{\bm{\phi}_{i}^{k}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\phi}_{i},\mathcal{D}_{i}).

\displaystyle\|\bm{\phi}_{i,\epsilon}^{\star}-\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\star}\|\leq\epsilon.

Communication time and energy:
We assume communication over Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) where the bandwidth is
B
(hz) per agent, the transmission power is
P
(W), and the noise power spectral density is
N_{o}
(W/hz). Each element in the model is transmitted using
32
bits. Hence, using Shannon formula, the transmission rate
R
is

R=B\text{log}_{2}\left(1+\frac{P}{N_{o}B}\right).

(33)

Then, we find the time (
\tau
) needed to send
32\cdot d
bits, where
d
is the model size. Knowing the transmission time and power, the communication energy per iteration and per agent is computed as
P\cdot\tau
. The total communication energy is then calculated by summing the per agent energy. The choices of
B

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, an energy-efficient meta-learning approach has been proposed. The proposed approach enables learning a meta-model in a distributed setting and is shown to achieve high accuracy (low loss) for a randomly generated new similar regression/classification task with very few samples, at significantly less energy consumption compared to iMAML. The practicality of the approach was demonstrated using linear regression and image classification. Future work will be focused on providing theoretical guarantees of the proposed approach in addition to investigating other use cases.
