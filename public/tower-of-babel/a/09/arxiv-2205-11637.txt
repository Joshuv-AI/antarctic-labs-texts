Optimal embedded and enclosing isosceles triangles
Áron Ambrus, Mónika Csikós, Gergely Kiss, János Pach, Gábor Somlai

Abstract
Given a triangle Δ \Delta , we study the problem of determining the smallest enclosing and largest embedded isosceles triangles of Δ \Delta with respect to area and perimeter. This problem was initially posed by Nandakumar [ 16 , 18 ] and was first studied by Kiss, Pach, and Somlai [ 12 ] , who showed that if Δ ′ \Delta^{\prime} is the smallest area isosceles triangle containing Δ \Delta , then Δ ′ \Delta^{\prime} and Δ \Delta share a side and an angle. In the present paper, we prove that for any triangle Δ \Delta , every maximum area isosceles triangle embedded in Δ \Delta and every maximum perimeter isosceles triangle embedded in Δ \Delta shares a side and an angle with Δ \Delta . Somewhat surprisingly, the case of minimum perimeter enclosing triangles is different: there are infinite families of triangles Δ \Delta whose minimum perimeter isosceles containers do not share a side and an angle with Δ \Delta .

Introduction

The following classical problem is the starting point of our investigation. Given two convex bodies,
C
and
C^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, decide whether
C
can be moved into a position where it covers
C^{\prime}
. One can easily list some necessary conditions, for instance, the volume, the surface area and the diameter of
C
has to be at least as large as the one of
C^{\prime}
. However, solving the decision problem can be rather challenging, even in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, or for special cases that might seem friendly at first sight.

For instance, consider the setup, where
C^{\prime}
is the ‘shadow’ of
C
, that is,
C
is embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
C^{\prime}
is the orthogonal projection of
C
onto a
2
-dimensional affine subspace.
The necessary conditions are clearly satisfied and it looks plausible that there is always a congruent copy of
C
which covers
C^{\prime}
. However, the proof of this fact is far from straightforward
[
4
,
13
]
, and rather surprisingly, the result does not generalize to higher dimensions: for
d\geq 3
, no convex
d
-polytope embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
can cover all of its shadows
[
4
]
.

Another special case is where both convex bodies are triangles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
: given two triangles
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
, find an efficient way to decide whether
\Delta
can be brought into a position where it covers
\Delta^{\prime}
. This is a classical problem posed by Steinhaus
[
25

in

and an algorithmic solution was proposed only
29
years later by Post
[
21
]
, who described a set of
18
polynomial inequalities of degree

such that a copy of

can cover
\Delta^{\prime}
if and only if at least one of these inequalities are satisfied.
The key geometric component of Post’s solution is the following.

Lemma 1.1

(Post
[
21
]
)
.

If a triangle
\Delta
can be moved to a position where it covers another triangle
\Delta^{\prime}
, then one can also find a covering position of
\Delta
with a side that contains one side of
\Delta^{\prime}
.

Results of this kind help us to reduce the number of configurations to consider, and are of both theoretical and practical interest.

Proof of
2.3

The areas of special embedded triangles of the first, the second and the third type are the following:

\mathrm{area}\left(A^{\prime}BC\right)=\frac{a^{2}\sin\gamma}{2},\ \ \mathrm{area}\left(AB^{\prime}C\right)=\frac{b^{2}\sin\alpha}{2},\ \ \mathrm{area}\left(A^{\prime\prime}BC\right)=\frac{a^{2}\sin\beta}{2}.

\mathrm{area}\left(A_{1}BC\right)=\frac{a^{2}\tan\beta}{4},\ \ \mathrm{area}\left(AB_{1}C\right)=\frac{b^{2}\tan\alpha}{4},\ \ \mathrm{area}\left(ABC_{1}\right)=\frac{c^{2}\tan\alpha}{4}.

\mathrm{area}\left(\overline{A}BC\right)=\frac{a^{2}\sin(2\beta)}{2},\ \ \mathrm{area}\left(\overline{\overline{A}}BC\right)=\frac{a^{2}\sin(2\gamma)}{2},\ \ \mathrm{area}\left(A\overline{B}C\right)=\frac{b^{2}\sin(2\gamma)}{2}.

1.

It follows easily from the previous equalities that

\mathrm{area}\left(A^{\prime\prime}BC\right)=\frac{a^{2}\sin\beta}{2}<\frac{a^{2}\sin\beta}{2}\frac{c}{b}=\frac{a^{2}\sin\beta}{2}\frac{\sin\gamma}{\sin\beta}=\mathrm{area}\left(A^{\prime}BC\right).

2.

We have seen that
\mathrm{area}\left(A_{1}BC\right)=\frac{a^{2}\tan\beta}{4}
and
\mathrm{area}\left(AB^{\prime}C\right)=\frac{b^{2}\sin\alpha}{2}
. Now
\frac{a^{2}\tan\beta}{4}<\frac{b^{2}\sin\alpha}{2}
is equivalent to
\frac{a^{2}}{2b^{2}}=\frac{\sin^{2}\alpha}{2\sin^{2}\beta}<\frac{\sin\alpha\cos\beta}{\sin\beta}
, by using the law of sines. Reformulating this we have
\sin\alpha<\sin(2\beta)
, which always holds as
\alpha<\min\{2\beta,180-2\beta\}
.

The inequality
\frac{b^{2}\tan\alpha}{4}<\frac{c^{2}\tan\alpha}{4}
implies that
\mathrm{area}\left(AB_{1}C\right)<\mathrm{area}\left(ABC_{1}\right)
.

3.

We first prove

\mathrm{area}\left(\overline{A}BC\right)<\mathrm{area}\left(ABC_{1}\right).

Using the equations given above this is equivalent to

\frac{a^{2}\sin(2\beta)}{2}<\frac{c^{2}\tan\alpha}{4}\Longleftrightarrow a^{2}\sin\beta\cos\beta<\frac{c^{2}\sin\alpha}{4\cos\alpha}.

By replacing
\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin\beta}
with
\frac{a}{b}
we obtain
\frac{4ab}{c^{2}}\cos\alpha\cos\beta<1.

Law of cosines gives

\frac{4ab}{c^{2}}\frac{2bc}{b^{2}+c^{2}-a^{2}}\frac{2ac}{a^{2}+c^{2}-b^{2}}<1.

After a simple rearrangement we obtain

(c^{2}+(a^{2}-b^{2}))(c^{2}-(a^{2}-b^{2}))<c^{4},

which holds by
a\neq b
.

The inequality
\mathrm{area}\left(A\overline{B}C\right)>\mathrm{area}\left(\overline{\overline{A}}BC\right)
follows from

\mathrm{area}\left(\overline{\overline{A}}BC\right)=\frac{a^{2}\sin(2\gamma)}{2}<\frac{b^{2}\sin(2\gamma)}{2}=\mathrm{area}\left(A\overline{B}C\right).

The length of the legs of the isosceles triangles
A\overline{B}C
and
AB^{\prime}C
are equal to
b
, but the apex angle is greater in the latter triangle. As both apex angles are upper bounded by
\alpha<90^{\circ}
, we get

\mathrm{area}\left(A\overline{B}C\right)<\mathrm{area}\left(AB^{\prime}C\right)
.

4.

If
ABC
is obtuse, then
0^{\circ}<\alpha<45^{\circ}
. The function
\sin(2\alpha)
is strictly monotonically increasing on the interval
[0^{\circ}45^{\circ}]
.
Since
\alpha<\beta
we have
\alpha<180^{\circ}-\gamma<90^{\circ}
. Thus

\sin(2\alpha)<\sin(180-\gamma)=\sin\gamma,

2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha<\sin\gamma

\sin\alpha<\frac{\sin\gamma}{2\cos\alpha}.

We multiply both sides of the inequality with the positive number
\frac{\sin\alpha\sin\gamma}{2}
:

\frac{\sin^{2}\alpha\sin\gamma}{2}<\frac{\sin\alpha\sin^{2}\gamma}{4\cos\alpha}.

By the law of sines this is equivalent to

\frac{a^{2}\sin\gamma}{2}<\frac{c^{2}\sin\alpha}{4\cos\alpha},

which implies

\mathrm{area}\left(A^{\prime}BC\right)=\frac{a^{2}\sin\gamma}{2}\leq\frac{c^{2}\tan\alpha}{4}=\mathrm{area}\left(ABC_{1}\right).

∎

Proof of
2.5

1.

\mathrm{per}\left(ABC^{\prime}\right)<\mathrm{per}\left(ABC^{\prime\prime}\right)
follows from the Hinge theorem since

|AB|=|AC^{\prime}|=|AC^{\prime\prime}|=c
and
\alpha=\sphericalangle C^{\prime}AB<\sphericalangle C^{\prime\prime}BA=\beta<90^{\circ}
.

2.

Notice that
|AB|=|AC_{1}|=|AC_{2}|=c
. On the other hand
\sphericalangle BAC^{\prime}=\alpha<\sphericalangle BAC_{2}=180^{\circ}-2\beta<180^{\circ}-2\alpha
since
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=180^{\circ}
and
\alpha<\beta<\gamma
. Thus the Hinge theorem gives
\mathrm{per}\left(ABC^{\prime}\right)<\mathrm{per}\left(ABC_{2}\right)<\mathrm{per}\left(ABC_{1}\right)
.

3.

Similarly to the previous cases, we have
|AC|=|CB^{\prime}|=|CB_{1}|=b
and
\sphericalangle ACB^{\prime}<\sphericalangle ACB_{1}
so the perimeter of
AB^{\prime}C
is smaller than that of

.

4.

First note that the triangles
\overline{A}BC
and

do exist if and only if

\gamma<90^{\circ}
. First we show that
\mathrm{per}\left(ABC^{\prime}\right)>\mathrm{per}\left(\overline{A}BC\right)

\mathrm{per}\left(ABC^{\prime}\right)=2c(1+\sin(\alpha/2))
and

\mathrm{per}\left(\overline{A}BC\right)=a(1+1/\cos\gamma)
, thus it is enough to show that

Using the law of sines we obtain

\frac{2(1+\sin(\alpha/2))}{1+1/\cos\gamma}<\frac{a}{c}=\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin\gamma}.

Equivalently,

\frac{2(1+\sin(\alpha/2))}{\sin\alpha}<\frac{1+1/\cos\gamma}{\sin\gamma}=\frac{2(1+\cos\gamma)}{\sin(2\gamma)}=\frac{2(1+\sin(90^{\circ}-\gamma))}{\sin(180^{\circ}-2\gamma)}.

Note that
(1+\sin x)/\sin(2x)
is strictly decreasing on the interval
(0^{\circ},60^{\circ})
. It is clear that

\gamma<90^{\circ}
.
Hence we get that
\mathrm{per}\left(ABC^{\prime}\right)<\mathrm{per}\left(\overline{A}BC\right)

The inequality
\mathrm{per}\left(\overline{A}BC\right)<\mathrm{per}\left(A\overline{B}C\right)
simply follows from that fact that
\overline{A}BC
and

are similar triangles such that the base of
\overline{A}BC
, is of length

so it shorter than the base of
