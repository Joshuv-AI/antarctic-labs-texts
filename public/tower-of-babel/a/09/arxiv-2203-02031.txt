Scaling Relations for Auxin Waves
Timothy E. Faver, Hermen Jan Hupkes, Roeland M. H. Merks, Jelle van der Voort

Abstract
. We analyze an ‘up-the-gradient’ model for the formation of transport channels of the phytohormone auxin, through auxin-mediated polarization of the PIN1 auxin transporter. We show that this model admits a family of travelling wave solutions that is parameterized by the height of the auxin-pulse. We uncover scaling relations for the speed and width of these waves and verify these rigorous results with numerical computations. In addition, we provide explicit expressions for the leading-order wave profiles, which allows the influence of the biological parameters in the problem to be readily identified. Our proofs are based on a generalization of the scaling principle developed by Friesecke and Pego to construct pulse solutions to the classic Fermi-Pasta-Ulam-Tsingou model, which describes a one-dimensional chain of coupled nonlinear springs.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Polar auxin transport

The phytohormone auxin is a central player in practically all aspects of the development and growth of plants, for example in phyllotaxis, root development and the initiation of lateral roots, the formation of vascular tissues in stems, the patterning of leaf veins, and flower development
[
49
]
. The pattern formation principles underlying these developmental mechanisms have been uncovered to a large part through an intensive cross-talk between experimental approaches and mathematical modeling
[
57
,
6
,
13
]
. Auxin is transported between cells and between cells and the cell walls both through diffusion and through transport proteins that are localized at the cell membrane of the cell. These transport proteins are distributed in a polarized manner inside the cells, and the polarization of adjacent cells is coordinated in plant tissue, leading to a directed transport of auxin through plant tissues in a mechanism called polar auxin transport (PAT)
[
1
]
. For example, in fully developed seed plants, auxin is synthesized in leaves, then is transported through the central tissues of the stem and the root towards the root tips, where it redirected along the superficial tissues of the root back to towards the stem and recycled towards the internal tissues of the root
[
1
]
.

Despite new details being uncovered incessantly (see e.g.
[
26
]
), it is still incompletely understood what mechanisms drive the polarization of auxin transporters inside cells and the coordinated polarization among adjacent cells. In a series of classical experiments, Sachs applied artificial auxin to bean plants, and observed that these become the source new vascular tissue that then joins the existing vasculature; see e.g.
[
54
]
and the review
[
27
]
. These initial observations, together with the discovery of auxin transporters including PIN1 suggested that auxin drives the polarization of its own transporters, and hence the direction of its own transport (reviewed in
[
44
,
27
]
). Initial models aimed to explain the formation of transport channels as observed in Sachs’ experiments. These models therefore assumed that the rate of auxin flux from cell to cell further polarised auxin transport. This positive feedback led to the self-organised formation of auxin transport channels in a process called auxin canalisation. When it was realised that auxin accumulations mark the formation of a new leaves at the shoot apex, an alternative model was proposed, in which cells polarised towards the locally increased concentrations of auxin, thus forming self-organised accumulation of auxin
[
51
]
. Mathematical models of the self-organisation of polar auxin transport therefore follow these two broad
categories. ‘With-the-gradient’ models formalise the canalisation hypothesis and assume that the rate of cell polarisation depends
on the auxin
flux
towards the relevant neighbour
[
46
,
45
,
53
,
52
]
. ’Up-the-gradient’ models assume that PIN polarizes in the direction of neighbouring cells
at a rate that positively depends on the auxin
concentration
in that neighbour
[
39
,
59
]
. Attempts to reconcile these two seemingly contradicting ideas have followed two broad approaches. The first approach proposed that with-the-gradient and up-the-gradient models act at different positions of the plant or at different stages during development. For example Bayer et al.
[
7
]
proposed that the up-the-gradient model act at superficial tissue layers of the shoot apical meristem where it forms auxin accumulation points leading to the initial of new leaves. The deeper tissue layers could follow the with-the-gradient model channeling auxin away from the auxin accumulation point towards the vascular tissues
[
7
]
. A similar approach was recently taken to explain the leaf venation patterning in combination with auxin convergence at the edge of the leaf primordium
[
34
]
. The second approach looked for variants of the with-the-gradient or up-the-gradient models that could explain both auxin canalisation and auxin canalisation depending on the parameter settings. In this line of reasoning Walker et al. have proposed a with-the-gradient hypothesis for phyllotaxis
[
62
]
, whereas one of us has proposed an up-the-gradient hypothesis for canalisation
[
44
]
.

1.2.
Mathematical motivation

In order to distinguish between the available phenomenological models of auxin-driven pattern formation and the general developmental principles that they represent, mathematical insight into the models’ structure and the models’ solutions will be crucial. This will help pinpoint key differences between the model structures and may uncover potential structural instabilities in the models upon which evolution may have acted, so as to produce new developmental patterning modules
[
9
]
.
From the mathematical side,
almost all previous studies have focused on the types of patterns that can be generated by different models once the transitory dynamics have died out. An important example is the study
by Van Berkel and coworkers
[
10
]
,
where a number of models for polar auxin transport are recast into a common mathematical framework that allows them to be compared.
A steady state analysis for a general class of
active transport models can be found in
[
15
]
,
using advanced tools such as snaking from the field of bifurcation theory. Both periodic and stationary patterns are examined in
[
2
]
, where the authors consider an extended
‘with-the-flux’ model. Haskovec and his coworkers derive local and global existence results together with an appropriate continuum limit
for their graph-based diffusion model in
[
28
]
.

Important qualitative examples of the with-the-gradient model are the formation of regularly spaced auxin maximums that lead to the growth of new leaves, as well as the formation of auxin channels that precede the formation of veins. Our goal here is to move beyond the well-studied equilibrium settings above and focus instead on understanding the dynamical behavior that leads to these patterns. In particular, we provide a rigorous framework to study a class of wave solutions that underpin the dynamical behaviour associated to with-the-gradient model. Ultimately, we hope that this analytic approach will provide an additional lens through which models of PAT can be examined and compared.

1.5.
Cross-diffusion

From a mathematical perspective, the problem
(
1.3.1
) is interesting due to its interpretation
as a so-called cross-diffusion problem, where the transport coefficient
of one component is influenced by one of the other components. Work in this
area was stimulated by developments in the modeling of bacterial cell membranes
[
58
]
and biofilms
[
16
]
,
where self-organization of biological molecules plays an important role.
In the continuum regime, such problems are tough to analyze on account
of potential degeneracies in the coefficients. The well-posedness of the underlying problem was analyzed in

[
60
]
, while a numerical method for such problems
was developed in
[
25
]
.

The key phenomenological assumption behind such models is that particles
behave differently when they are isolated compared to when they are
part of a cluster. A simplified agent-based approach to capture this
mechanism can be found in

[
37
]
, which reduces naturally to a scalar
PDE with nonlinear diffusion in the continuum limit. After adding
a small regularization term, it is possible to
use geometric singular perturbation theory
to show that this PDE
admits travelling wave solutions
[
42
]
.
In this setting, the steepness of the wavefronts provides
the necessary scale-separation required for rigorous results.

Our approach in this paper proceeds along entirely different lines,
using the amplitude of the auxin pulse as a small continuation parameter
to construct a family of travelling wave solutions
to (
1.3.1
). The key insight is that one can extract an effective limiting system by scaling the width and speed of the wave
in an appropriate fashion and sending the amplitude to zero.
By means of a fixed-point analysis one
can show in a rigorous fashion that solutions to this limiting system
can be continued to form a family of solutions to the full system.

1.6.
Relation to FPUT pulses

Our technique is a generalization of the approach developed
by Friesecke and Pego
[
20
]
to construct
small-amplitude travelling pulse solutions to the
Fermi-Pasta-Ulam-Tsingou (FPUT) problem

[
19
,
14
]

(1.6.1)

\ddot{x}_{j}=F(x_{j+1}-x_{j})-F(x_{j}-x_{j-1}),\qquad\qquad j\in\mathbb{Z}.

This models an infinite, one-dimensional chain of particles that
can only move horizontally and are connected to their nearest neighbours by springs. These springs transmit a force

(1.6.2)

F(r)=r+r^{2}

that hence depends nonlinearly on the relative distance
\displaystyle r

between neighbouring particles; see
[
20
,
31
,
48
]
for the impact of other choices.
The FPUT system is well-established as a fundamental model
to study the propagation of disturbances
through spatially discrete systems,
such as granular media, artificial metamaterials, DNA strands, and electrical transmission lines
[
11
,
41
]
.

Looking for a travelling wave in the relative displacement coordinates, one introduces an Ansatz of the form

(1.6.3)

x_{j+1}(t)-x_{j}(t)=\phi(j-\sigma t),

which leads to the scalar functional differential equation of mixed type (MFDE)

(1.6.4)

\sigma^{2}\phi^{\prime\prime}(\xi)=F\big(\phi(\xi+1)\big)-2F\big(\phi(\xi)\big)+F\big(\phi(\xi-1)\big).

Following the classic papers by Friesecke in combination with Wattis
[
24
]

and Pego
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
,
we introduce the scaling

(1.6.5)

\phi(\xi)=\epsilon^{2}\varphi_{\epsilon}(\epsilon\xi)

and write
\displaystyle\sigma=\sigma_{\epsilon}
,
which transforms (
1.6.4
) into the MFDE

(1.6.6)

\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\epsilon^{2}\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}=\big(S^{\epsilon}+S^{-\epsilon}-2\big)\big[\varphi_{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{2}\varphi_{\epsilon}^{2}\big].

Here the shift operator
\displaystyle S^{d}
acts as

(1.6.7)

(S^{d}f)(\xi)=f(\xi+d)

for any
\displaystyle d\in\mathbb{R}
. Since the symbol
\displaystyle S^{\epsilon}+S^{-\epsilon}-2
represents a discrete Laplacian, we can interpret (
1.6.6
) as a wave equation with a nonlinear diffusion term. To some extent, this clarifies the link with our original problem (
1.3.1
) and the discussion above.

Applying the Fourier transform to (
1.6.6
)
with
\displaystyle k
as the frequency variable, we arrive at

(1.6.8)

-\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\epsilon^{2}k^{2}\widehat{\varphi}_{\epsilon}(k)=2(\cos(\epsilon k)-1)\big[\widehat{\varphi_{\epsilon}}+\epsilon^{2}\widehat{\varphi_{\epsilon}^{2}}\big](k)=-4\sin^{2}(\epsilon k/2)\big[\widehat{\varphi_{\epsilon}}+\epsilon^{2}\widehat{\varphi_{\epsilon}^{2}}\big](k).

Upon introducing the symbol

(1.6.9)

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{FPUT}}^{(\epsilon)}(k)=\frac{4\epsilon^{2}\sin^{2}(\epsilon k/2)}{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\epsilon^{2}k^{2}-4\sin^{2}(\epsilon k/2)},

this can be recast into the compact form

(1.6.10)

\widehat{\varphi_{\epsilon}}(k)=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{FPUT}}^{(\epsilon)}(k)\widehat{\varphi_{\epsilon}^{2}}(k).

Upon choosing the speed

(1.6.11)

\sigma_{\epsilon}=1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3},

we can exploit the expansion

\displaystyle\sin^{2}(z/2)=\frac{1}{4}z^{2}-\frac{1}{48}z^{4}+O(z^{6})

to obtain the pointwise limit

(1.6.12)

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{FPUT}}^{(\epsilon)}(k)\to\frac{12}{8+k^{2}},\qquad\qquad\epsilon\to 0.

Using the fact that
\displaystyle(8+k^{2})
is the Fourier symbol for
\displaystyle 8-\partial^{2}_{\xi}
, this suggests that the
relevant
system for
\displaystyle\varphi_{\epsilon}
in the formal
\displaystyle\epsilon\to 0
limit is given by

(1.6.13)

8\varphi_{*}-\varphi_{*}^{\prime\prime}=12\varphi_{*}^{2},

which has the nontrivial even solution

(1.6.14)

\varphi_{*}(\xi)=\sech^{2}(\sqrt{2}\xi).

By casting the problem in an appropriate
functional analytic framework, one can show
that this explicit solution
\displaystyle\varphi_{*}
can be continued
to yield solutions
\displaystyle\varphi_{\epsilon}

to (
1.6.6
) for small
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
.
In this fashion, one establishes the existence of a family of pulse solutions
[
20
]

(1.6.15)

x_{j+1}(t)-x_{j}(t)=\epsilon^{2}\sech^{2}\Big(\sqrt{2}\epsilon(j-\sigma_{\epsilon}t)\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4}).

Roughly speaking, the main mathematical contribution in this paper is that
we show how this analysis can be generalized to the setting of
(
1.3.1
). The first main obstacle is that this
is a multi-component system, which requires us to
explicitly reduce the order before a tractable limit can be obtained.
The second main obstacle is that the analysis of our Fourier symbol is
considerably more delicate, since in our setting the wavespeed
\displaystyle c

converges to zero instead of one as
\displaystyle\epsilon\to 0
.
Indeed, the denominator
of
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{FPUT}}^{(\epsilon)}
above depends
only on the product
\displaystyle\epsilon k
, while in our case
there is a separate dependence on
\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}k
.
This introduces a quasi-periodicity into the problem that requires
our convergence analysis to carefully distinguish between ‘small’ values of
\displaystyle k
and several separate regions of ‘large’
\displaystyle k
.

The third main difference is that we cannot use formal spectral arguments to analyze the limiting linear operator, which in our case is related to the Bernoulli equation. Instead, we apply a direct solution technique using variation-of-constants formulas. On the one hand this is much more explicit, but on the other hand the resulting estimates are rather delicate
on account of the custom function spaces involved.

1.7.
Discussion

Due to the important organizing role that wave solutions often play in complex systems, scaling information such as (
1.4.3
) can be used as the starting point to uncover more general dynamical information concerning models
such as (
1.3.1
) and related models of polar auxin trasnport. As such, we hope that the ideas
we present here will provide a robust analytical tool to analyze
different types of models as well. The resulting insights and predictions could
help to prioritize competing models on the basis of
dynamical experimental observations. Indeed, scaling laws
appear to play a role in
many aspects of biological systems,
such as the structural properties of vascular systems
[
50
]
,
the mass dependence of metabolic rates
[
63
]

and the functional constraints imposed by size

[
56
]
.

Although we have included only right-polarizing PIN in our system,
we believe that our techniques can be adapted to cover the full
case where also left-polarizing PIN is included. However, the
computations rapidly become unwieldy and the limiting system
is expected to differ qualitatively. For this reason, we have
not chosen to pursue this level of generality in the present paper,
as it would only obscure the main ideas behind our framework.
One of the main generalizations that we intend to pursue
in the future is to study the model in two spatial dimensions.
This is motivated by recent
numerical observations concerning the formation of auxin channels
and their associated PIN walls
under the influence of travelling patterns that are localized in
both spatial dimensions
[
3
]
.

2.
The Travelling Wave Problem

2.1.
Rewriting the original problem (
1.3.1
)

We will reduce the problem (
1.3.1
) to a system of equations involving only
\displaystyle A_{j}
and
\displaystyle P_{j}
, and it will be this resulting system on which we make the long wave-scaled travelling wave Ansatz.

2.1.1.
Changes of notation

We begin by rewriting (
1.3.1
) in a slightly more compressed manner that also exposes more transparently the leading order terms in the nonlinearities.
Let
\displaystyle\delta^{\pm}
be the left and right difference operators that act on sequences
\displaystyle(x_{j})
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
via

\delta^{+}x_{j}:=x_{j+1}-x_{j}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\delta^{-}x_{j}:=x_{j}-x_{j-1}.

Next, for
\displaystyle k
,
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}
with
\displaystyle k+x\neq 0
we have

\frac{x}{k+x}=\frac{x}{k}-\frac{x^{2}}{k(k+x)}.

We put

(2.1.1)

\mathsf{Q}_{1}(x,y):=\frac{x^{2}y}{k_{a}+x}

and compress

(2.1.2)

\tau_{1}:=\frac{T_{\act}}{k_{a}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\tau_{2}:=T_{\diff}

to see that our equation for
\displaystyle A_{j}
now reads

\dot{A}_{j}=\tau_{2}\delta^{+}\delta^{-}A_{j}-\tau_{1}\delta^{-}(R_{j}A_{j})+\tau_{1}\delta^{-}\mathsf{Q}_{1}(A_{j},R_{j}).

Next, we abbreviate

(2.1.3)

\kappa:=\frac{k_{1}}{k_{r}k_{m}}

and put

(2.1.4)

\mathsf{Q}_{2}(x,y):=\kappa\left(\frac{k_{r}y+k_{m}x+xy}{(k_{r}+x)(k_{m}+y)}\right)xy

to see that, the equation for
\displaystyle P_{j}
is

\dot{P}_{j}=-\kappa{A}_{j+1}P_{j}+\alpha{A}_{j}+\mathsf{Q}_{2}(A_{j+1},P_{j}).

The equation for
\displaystyle R_{j}
is updated similarly, and so we have rewritten (
1.3.1
) as

(2.1.5)

\begin{cases}\dot{A}_{j}=\tau_{2}\delta^{+}\delta^{-}A_{j}-\tau_{1}\delta^{-}(R_{j}A_{j})+\tau_{1}\delta^{-}\mathsf{Q}_{1}(A_{j},R_{j}),\\
\\
\dot{P}_{j}=-\kappa{A}_{j+1}P_{j}+\alpha{A}_{j}+\mathsf{Q}_{2}(A_{j+1},P_{j}),\\
\\
\dot{R}_{j}=\kappa{A}_{j+1}P_{j}-\mathsf{Q}_{2}(A_{j+1},P_{j}).\end{cases}

We observe that the equation for
\displaystyle R_{j}
depends only on
\displaystyle A_{j+1}
and
\displaystyle P_{j}
and therefore can be solved by direct integration.
Before we do that, however, we rewrite the new equation for
\displaystyle P_{j}
using Duhamel’s formula.

2.1.2.
Rewriting the
\displaystyle P_{j}
equation

We can view the equation for
\displaystyle P_{j}
in (
2.1.5
) as a first-order linear differential equation forced by
\displaystyle\alpha{A}_{j}+\mathsf{Q}_{2}(A_{j+1},P_{j})
, and so we can solve it via the integrating factor method.
For
\displaystyle f
,
\displaystyle g\in L^{1}
and
\displaystyle h\in L^{\infty}
we introduce the operators

(2.1.6)

\mathsf{E}(f)(s,t):=\exp\left(-\kappa\int_{s}^{t}f(\xi)\ d\xi\right),\ s,t\in\mathbb{R},

(2.1.7)

\mathsf{P}_{1}(f,g)(t):=\alpha\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathsf{E}(f)(s,t)g(s)\ ds,

and

(2.1.8)

\mathsf{P}_{2}(f,h)(t):=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathsf{E}(f)(s,t)\mathsf{Q}_{2}(f(s),h(s))\ ds.

Recall from (
1.3.3
) that we want
\displaystyle P_{j}
to vanish at
\displaystyle-\infty
.
The unique solution for
\displaystyle P_{j}
in (
2.1.5
) that does vanish at
\displaystyle-\infty
must satisfy

P_{j}(t)=\mathsf{P}_{1}(A_{j+1},A_{j})(t)+\mathsf{P}_{2}(A_{j+1},P_{j})(t).

2.1.3.
Solving the
\displaystyle R_{j}
equation

Since, per (
1.3.3
), we want
\displaystyle R_{j}
to vanish at
\displaystyle-\infty
, and since we are assuming that each
\displaystyle A_{j}
vanishes sufficiently fast at both
\displaystyle\pm\infty
and
\displaystyle P_{j}
vanishes at
\displaystyle-\infty
and remains bounded at
\displaystyle+\infty
, we may solve for
\displaystyle R_{j}
by integrating the third equation in (
2.1.5
) from
\displaystyle-\infty
to
\displaystyle t
.
For
\displaystyle f
,
\displaystyle g\in L^{1}
and
\displaystyle h\in L^{\infty}
, we define more integral operators:

(2.1.9)

\mathsf{R}_{1}(f,g)(t):=\kappa\tau_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{t}f(s)\mathsf{P}_{1}(f,g)(s)\ ds,\ t\in\mathbb{R},

(2.1.10)

\mathsf{R}_{2}(f,g,h)(t):=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\big(\kappa{f}(s)\mathsf{P}_{2}(f,g)(s)-\mathsf{Q}_{2}(f(s),\mathsf{P}_{1}(f,g)(s)+\mathsf{P}_{2}(f,h)(s))\big)\ ds,

and

(2.1.11)

\mathsf{R}(f,g,h)(t):=\mathsf{R}_{1}(f,g)(t)+\mathsf{R}_{2}(f,g,h)(t).

We have defined
\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{1}
and
\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{2}
just above, respectively, in (
2.1.7
) and (
2.1.8
) and
\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{2}
earlier in (
2.1.4
).
Then the solution to the third equation in (
2.1.5
) that vanishes at
\displaystyle-\infty
is

(2.1.12)

R_{j}(t)=\mathsf{R}(A_{j+1},A_{j},P_{j})(t)=\mathsf{R}_{1}(A_{j+1},A_{j})(t)+\mathsf{R}_{2}(A_{j+1},A_{j},P_{j})(t).

2.1.4.
The final system for
\displaystyle A_{j}
and
\displaystyle P_{j}

We rewrite (part of) the
\displaystyle A_{j}
equation once more to incorporate the new expression for
\displaystyle R_{j}
.
For
\displaystyle f
,
\displaystyle g\in L^{1}
and
\displaystyle h\in L^{\infty}
and
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{R}
put

(2.1.13)

\mathsf{N}(f,g,h)(t):=\tau_{1}\mathsf{Q}_{1}(g(t),\mathsf{R}(f,g,h)(t))-\tau_{1}\mathsf{R}_{2}(f,g,h)(t)g(t),

where we defined
\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{1}
in (
2.1.1
).
Then
\displaystyle A_{j}
must satisfy

\dot{A}_{j}=\tau_{2}\delta^{+}\delta^{-}A_{j}-\delta^{-}\big(\mathsf{R}_{1}(A_{j+1},A_{j})A_{j}\big)+\delta^{-}\mathsf{N}(A_{j+1},A_{j},P_{j}),

and so our system for
\displaystyle A_{j}
and
\displaystyle P_{j}
is now

(2.1.14)

\begin{cases}\dot{A}_{j}=\tau_{2}\delta^{+}\delta^{-}A_{j}-\delta^{-}\big(\mathsf{R}_{1}(A_{j+1},A_{j})A_{j}\big)+\delta^{-}\mathsf{N}(A_{j+1},A_{j},P_{j}),\\
\\
P_{j}=\mathsf{P}_{1}(A_{j+1},A_{j})+\mathsf{P}_{2}(A_{j+1},P_{j}).\end{cases}

That is, using the formula (
2.1.12
) for
\displaystyle R_{j}
in terms of
\displaystyle A_{j}
and
\displaystyle P_{j}
, we can solve (
2.1.5
) if we can solve (
2.1.14
).

We will make two changes of variables on (
2.1.14
).
First, in Section
2.2
, we make a travelling wave Ansatz for
\displaystyle A_{j}
and
\displaystyle P_{j}
.
We reformulate (
2.1.14
) for the travelling wave profiles as the system (
2.3.2
) below.
Then, in Section
3.1
, we introduce our long wave scaling on these travelling wave profiles.
After numerous adjustments, we arrive at the final system (
3.1.14
) for the scaled travelling wave profiles, which we solve in Section
4
.
The reader uninterested in these intermediate stages may wish to proceed directly to Theorem
3.3.3
, which discusses the equivalence of the problem (
2.1.14
) for
\displaystyle A_{j}
and
\displaystyle P_{j}
and the ultimate long wave system (
3.3.12
).

Remark 2.1.1
.

The linearization of (

) at 0 yields

\dot{A}_{j}=\tau_{2}\delta^{+}\delta^{-}A_{j},\qquad P_{j}=R_{j}=0.

If we follow the discussion after
[

with

, we find the dispersion relation

(2.1.15)

-i\omega=2\tau_{2}(\cos(k)-1).

The only real solutions are
\displaystyle\omega=0
and
\displaystyle k\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
.
Previously, in
[

\displaystyle\omega=\omega(k)

2.2.
The travelling wave Ansatz

We now look for solutions
\displaystyle A_{j}
and
\displaystyle P_{j}
to (
2.1.14
) of the form

(2.2.1)

A_{j}=\phi_{1}(j-ct)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad P_{j}=\phi_{2}(j-ct).

The profiles
\displaystyle\phi_{1}
and
\displaystyle\phi_{2}
are real-valued functions of a single real variable and
\displaystyle c\in\mathbb{R}
.
The following manipulations will be justified if we assume
\displaystyle\phi_{1}\in H_{q}^{1}
and
\displaystyle\phi_{2}\in W^{1,\infty}
; we discuss the exponentially localized Sobolev space
\displaystyle H_{q}^{1}
in Appendix
A.3
.
Furthermore, since we want
\displaystyle P_{j}
to vanish at
\displaystyle-\infty
and be asymptotically constant at
\displaystyle+\infty
, per the limits (
1.3.3
) and the numerical predictions of Fig.
2
, we expect that
\displaystyle\phi_{2}
should vanish at
\displaystyle+\infty
and be asymptotically constant at
\displaystyle-\infty
.

We will convert the problem (
2.1.14
) for
\displaystyle A_{j}
and
\displaystyle P_{j}
into a nonlocal system for
\displaystyle\phi_{1}
and
\displaystyle\phi_{2}
, with
\displaystyle c
as a parameter.
Doing so amounts to little more than changing variables
many
times in the integral operators defined in Sections
2.1.2
and
2.1.3
and gives us a host of new integral operators that will constitute the problem for
\displaystyle\phi_{1}
and
\displaystyle\phi_{2}
.

In what follows we assume
\displaystyle f\in L^{1}
and
\displaystyle g\in L^{\infty}
, so that the operators below are defined in the special cases of
\displaystyle f=\phi_{1}\in H_{q}^{1}
and
\displaystyle g=\phi_{2}\in W^{1,\infty}
.
First, for
\displaystyle x
,
\displaystyle v\in\mathbb{R}
, put

(2.2.2)

\widetilde{\mathsf{E}}^{c}(f)(v,x):=\exp\left(\frac{\kappa}{c}\int_{v}^{x}f(u+1)\ du\right)

and

(2.2.3)

\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{c}(f)(x):=\frac{\alpha}{c}\int_{x}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathsf{E}}^{c}(f)(v,x)f(v)\ dv.

Then we use the Ansatz (
2.2.1
) and the definition of
\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{1}
in (
2.1.7
) to find

\mathsf{P}_{1}(A_{j+1},A_{j})(t)=\alpha\int_{-\infty}^{t}\exp\left(-\kappa\int_{s}^{t}\phi_{1}(j-c\xi+1)\ d\xi\right)\phi_{1}(j-cs)\ ds=\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{c}(\phi_{1})(j-ct).

Here we have substituted
\displaystyle u=j-c\xi
in the exponential’s integral and then
\displaystyle v=j-cs
throughout.

Similar substitutions, which we do not discuss, yield the following identities.
Put

(2.2.4)

\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{c}(f,g)(x):=\frac{1}{c}\int_{x}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathsf{E}}^{c}(f)(v,x)\mathsf{Q}_{2}(f(v+1),g(v))\ dv,

so that with
\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{2}
defined in (
2.1.8
) we have

\mathsf{P}_{2}(A_{j+1},P_{j})(t)=\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})(j-ct).

Thus
\displaystyle\phi_{2}
must satisfy

(2.2.5)

\phi_{2}=\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{c}(\phi_{1})+\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2}),

which indicates that, as expected,
\displaystyle\phi_{2}
should vanish at
\displaystyle+\infty
and be asymptotically constant at
\displaystyle-\infty
.

Now we reformulate the equation for
\displaystyle A_{j}
, equivalently, for
\displaystyle\phi_{1}
.
Put

(2.2.6)

\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{c}(f)(x):=\frac{\kappa\tau_{1}}{c}\int_{x}^{\infty}f(u+1)\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{c}(f)(u)\ du,

so that with
\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{1}
defined in (
2.1.9
) we have

\mathsf{R}_{1}(A_{j+1},A_{j})(t)=\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{c}(\phi_{1})(j-ct).

Put

(2.2.7)

\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{2}^{c}(f,g)(x):=\frac{1}{c}\int_{x}^{\infty}\big(\kappa{f}(u+1)\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{c}(f,g)(u)-\mathsf{Q}_{2}(f(u+1),g(u))\big)\ du

and

(2.2.8)

\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}^{c}(f,g):=\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{c}(f)+\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{2}^{c}(f,g),

so that with
\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{2}
defined in (
2.1.10
) and
\displaystyle\mathsf{R}
in (
2.1.11
) we have

\mathsf{R}_{2}(A_{j+1},A_{j},P_{j})(t)=\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{2}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})(j-ct)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathsf{R}(A_{j+1},A_{j},P_{j})(t)=\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})(j-ct).

Last, put

(2.2.9)

\widetilde{\mathsf{N}}^{c}(f,g)(x):=\tau_{1}\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{2}^{c}(f,g)(x)f(x)-\tau_{1}\mathsf{Q}_{1}(f(x),\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}^{c}(f,g)(x)),

so that with
\displaystyle\mathsf{N}
defined in (
2.1.13
) we have

\mathsf{N}(A_{j+1},A_{j},P_{j})(t)=\widetilde{\mathsf{N}}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})(j-ct).

For a function
\displaystyle f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle d\in\mathbb{R}
, define, as in (
1.6.7
), the shift operator
\displaystyle S^{d}
by

(2.2.10)

(S^{d}f)(x):=f(x+d).

This final piece of notation, along with the equation (
2.2.5
), allows us to convert the problem (
2.1.14
) for
\displaystyle A_{j}
and
\displaystyle P_{j}
into the following nonlocal system for
\displaystyle\phi_{1}
and
\displaystyle\phi_{2}
:

(2.2.11)

\begin{cases}-c\phi_{1}^{\prime}=\tau_{2}(S^{1}-2+S^{-1})\phi_{1}+(S^{-1}-1)\big(\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{c}(\phi_{1})\phi_{1}+\widetilde{\mathsf{N}}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\big),\\
\\
\phi_{2}=\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{c}(\phi_{1})+\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2}).\end{cases}

2.3.
The Fourier multiplier structure

We summarize our conventions and definitions for Fourier transforms and Fourier multipliers in Appendix
A
.
If we take the Fourier transform of the equation for
\displaystyle\phi_{1}
in (
2.2.11
), we find

\big(ick+2\tau_{2}(\cos(k)-1)\big)\widehat{\phi}_{1}(k)=(1-e^{-ik})\mathfrak{F}\big[\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{c}(\phi_{1})\phi_{1}+\widetilde{\mathsf{N}}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\big](k).

For
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
\displaystyle ick+2\tau_{2}(\cos(k)-1)=0
if and only if
\displaystyle k=0
.
Consequently, the function

(2.3.1)

\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{c}(k):=\frac{1-e^{-ik}}{ick+2\tau_{2}(\cos(k)-1)}

has a removable singularity at 0 and is in fact analytic on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
.
We therefore define
\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{c}
to be the Fourier multiplier with symbol
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{c}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{c}
satisfies

\widehat{\mathsf{M}_{c}f}(k)=\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{c}(k)\widehat{f}(k).

We discuss some further properties of Fourier multipliers in Appendix
A.2
.
Now the problem (
2.2.11
) is equivalent to

(2.3.2)

\begin{cases}\phi_{1}=\mathsf{M}_{c}\big(\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{c}(\phi_{1})\phi_{1}+\widetilde{\mathsf{N}}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\big)\\
\phi_{2}=\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{c}(\phi_{1})+\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2}).\end{cases}

3.
The Long Wave Problem

3.1.
The long wave scaling

We now make the long wave Ansatz

(3.1.1)

\phi_{1}(x)=\epsilon\psi_{1}(\epsilon^{\mu}x),\qquad\phi_{2}(x)=\epsilon^{\beta}\psi_{2}(\epsilon^{\mu}x),\qquad\text{ and }\qquad c=\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}.

We assume, as with
\displaystyle\phi_{1}
and
\displaystyle\phi_{2}
, that the scaled profiles satisfy
\displaystyle\psi_{1}\in H_{q}^{1}
and
\displaystyle\psi_{2}\in W^{1,\infty}
.
We think of
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
as small and keep the exponents
\displaystyle\beta
,
\displaystyle\gamma
,
\displaystyle\mu>0
arbitrary for now; eventually we will pick

\gamma=\mu=\frac{2}{5}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\beta=\frac{1}{5}.

The reasoning behind this choice is by no means obvious at this point and will not be for some time; leaving
\displaystyle\mu
,
\displaystyle\beta
, and
\displaystyle\gamma
arbitrary will allow this choice to appear more naturally (at the cost of temporarily more cumbersome notation).

As we intuited in Remark
2.1.1
, our wave speed is now close to 0, which is the auxin problem’s natural ‘speed of sound.’
The parameter
\displaystyle c_{0}
affords us some additional flexibility in choosing the wave speed.
A properly chosen value of
\displaystyle c_{0}
will cause the maximum of the leading-order term of
\displaystyle\phi_{1}
to be
\displaystyle\epsilon
, which will fulfill our promise in Section
1.4
that the auxin-height is, to leading order,
\displaystyle\epsilon
.
Friesecke and Pego introduce a similar auxiliary parameter into their
\displaystyle\epsilon
-dependent wave speed, see
[
20
, Eq. (2.5), (2.13)]
.
This parameter allows them to prove that the dependence of their travelling wave profile on wave speed is sufficiently regular in different function spaces, a result needed for their subsequent stability arguments in
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
.
We did not provide this extra parameter in our version (
1.6.11
) of the Friesecke-Pego wave speed, but rather we selected it so that the amplitude of the leading order
\displaystyle\sech^{2}
-profile term in (
1.6.14
) is 1.
Similarly, we will not pursue their depth of wave-speed analysis on our profiles’ dependence on
\displaystyle c_{0}
.

We convert (
2.3.2
) to another nonlocal system for
\displaystyle\psi_{1}
and
\displaystyle\psi_{2}
, which now depends heavily on the parameter
\displaystyle\epsilon
.
As before, this process mostly amounts to changing variables in many integrals.
For example, we use the definition of
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{c}
in (
2.2.3
) and the Ansatz (
3.1.1
) to find

(3.1.2)

\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{c}(\phi_{1})(x)=\frac{\alpha}{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}\int_{x}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathsf{E}}^{c}(\phi_{1})(v,x)\epsilon\psi_{1}(\epsilon^{\mu}v)\ dv,

where, using the definition of
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{E}}^{c}
in (
2.2.2
), we have

\widetilde{\mathsf{E}}^{c}(\phi_{1})(v,x)=\exp\left(\frac{\kappa}{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}\int_{v}^{x}\epsilon\psi_{1}(\epsilon^{\mu}u+\epsilon^{\mu})\ du\right)\\
=\exp\left(\frac{\kappa}{c_{0}}\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)}\int_{\epsilon^{\mu}v}^{\epsilon^{\mu}x}\psi_{1}(U+\epsilon^{\mu})\ dU\right).

Here we have substituted
\displaystyle U=\epsilon^{\mu}u
.

Now for
\displaystyle f\in L^{1}
we put

(3.1.3)

\mathcal{E}(f)(V,X):=\exp\left(\frac{\kappa}{c_{0}}\int_{V}^{X}f(U)\ dU\right),\ V,X\in\mathbb{R},

so that (
3.1.2
) becomes

\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{c}(\phi_{1})(x)=\frac{\alpha}{c_{0}}\epsilon^{1-\gamma}\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)}S^{\epsilon^{\mu}}\psi_{1})(\epsilon^{\mu}v,\epsilon^{\mu}x)\psi_{1}(\epsilon^{\mu}v)\ dv.

Here
\displaystyle S^{\epsilon^{\mu}}
is the shift operator defined in (
2.2.10
) with
\displaystyle d=\epsilon^{\mu}
.
We substitute again with
\displaystyle V=\epsilon^{\mu}v
and define

(3.1.4)

\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(f)(X):=\frac{\alpha}{c_{0}}\int_{X}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)}S^{\epsilon^{\mu}}f)(V,X)f(V)\ dV

to conclude that

\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{c}(\phi_{1})(x)=\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)}\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1})(\epsilon^{\mu}x).

Similar careful substitutions will allow us to reformulate the integral operators from Section
2.2
in terms of the long wave Ansatz.
First, however, we define

(3.1.5)

\breve{\mathsf{Q}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(X,Y):=\frac{X^{2}Y}{k_{a}+\epsilon{X}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\breve{\mathsf{Q}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(X,Y):=\kappa\frac{k_{r}Y+k_{m}\epsilon^{1-\beta}X+\epsilon{XY}}{(k_{r}+\epsilon{X})(k_{m}+\epsilon^{\beta}Y)}XY.

When
\displaystyle\epsilon\neq 0
, this definition permits the very convenient factorizations

\mathsf{Q}_{1}(\epsilon{X},\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)}Y)=\epsilon^{3-(\gamma+\mu)}\breve{\mathsf{Q}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(X,Y)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathsf{Q}_{2}(\epsilon{X},\epsilon^{\beta}Y)=\epsilon^{1+2\beta}\breve{\mathsf{Q}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(X,Y),

where
\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{1}
was defined in (
2.1.1
) and
\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{2}
in (
2.1.4
).

Now we work on the travelling wave integral operators.
Below we will assume
\displaystyle f\in L^{1}
and
\displaystyle g\in L^{\infty}
.
Put

(3.1.6)

\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(f,g)(X):=\frac{1}{c_{0}}\int_{X}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)}S^{\epsilon^{\mu}}f)(V,X)\breve{\mathsf{Q}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(f(V+\epsilon^{\mu}),g(V))\ dV,

so that with
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{c}
defined in (
2.2.4
) we have

\widetilde{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})(x)=\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)+2\beta}\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})(\epsilon^{\mu}x).

This converts the second equation in (
2.3.2
) for
\displaystyle\phi_{2}
to

\epsilon^{\beta}\psi_{2}(\epsilon^{\mu}x)=\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)}\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1})(\epsilon^{\mu}x)+\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)+2\beta}\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})(\epsilon^{\mu}x).

Passing to
\displaystyle X=\epsilon^{\mu}x
, we find that
\displaystyle\psi_{2}
must satisfy

(3.1.7)

\psi_{2}(X)=\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)-\beta}\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1})(X)+\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)+\beta}\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})(X).

Now put

(3.1.8)

\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(f)(X):=\frac{\kappa\tau_{1}}{c_{0}}\int_{X}^{\infty}\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(f)(V)f(V+\epsilon^{\mu})\ dV,

so that with
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{c}
defined in (
2.2.6
) we have

\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{c}(\phi_{1})(x)=\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))}\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1})(\epsilon^{\mu}x).

Put

(3.1.9)

\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(f,g)(X):=\frac{1}{c_{0}}\int_{X}^{\infty}\big(\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)}\kappa{f}(V+\epsilon^{\mu})\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(f,g)(V)-\breve{\mathsf{Q}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(f(V+\epsilon^{\mu}),g(V))\big)\ dV

and

(3.1.10)

\breve{\mathsf{R}}^{\epsilon}(f,g)(X):=\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)}\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(f)(X)+\epsilon^{2\beta}\mathsf{R}_{2}^{\epsilon}(f,g)(X),

so that with
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{2}^{c}
defined in (
2.2.7
) and
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}^{c}
defined in (
2.2.8
) we have

\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}_{2}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})(x)=\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)+2\beta}\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})(\epsilon^{\mu}x)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\widetilde{\mathsf{R}}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})(x)=\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)}\breve{\mathsf{R}}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})(\epsilon^{\mu}x).

Finally, put

(3.1.11)

\breve{\mathsf{N}}^{\epsilon}(f,g)(X):=\tau_{1}\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(f,g)(X)f(X)-\epsilon^{1-2\beta}\tau_{1}\breve{\mathsf{Q}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(f(X),\breve{\mathsf{R}}^{\epsilon}(f,g)(X)),

so that with
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{N}}^{c}
defined in (
2.2.9
) we have

\widetilde{\mathsf{N}}^{c}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})(x)=\epsilon^{2-(\gamma+\mu)+2\beta}\breve{\mathsf{N}}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})(\epsilon^{\mu}x).

The definition of scaled Fourier multipliers from (
A.2.3
) tells us that, for
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
,
\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}^{(\epsilon^{\mu})}
is the Fourier multiplier satisfying

\widehat{\mathsf{M}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}^{(\epsilon^{\mu})}f}(k)=\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)\widehat{f}(k),

where
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}
is defined by taking
\displaystyle c=\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}
in (
2.3.1
).
This converts the first equation in (
2.3.2
) for
\displaystyle\phi_{1}
to

\epsilon\psi_{1}(\epsilon^{\mu}x)=\mathsf{M}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}^{(\epsilon^{\mu})}[\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))}\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1})\epsilon\psi_{1}+\epsilon^{2-(\gamma+\mu)+2\beta}\breve{\mathsf{N}}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})](\epsilon^{\mu}x).

We factor this to reveal

(3.1.12)

\psi_{1}(X)=\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))}\mathsf{M}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}^{(\epsilon^{\mu})}\big[\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1})\psi_{1}+\epsilon^{-1+\gamma+\mu+2\beta}\mathsf{N}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\big](X).

We abbreviate

(3.1.13)

\breve{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon}:=\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))}\mathsf{M}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}^{(\epsilon^{\mu})}

to conclude from (
3.1.12
) and the prior equation (
3.1.7
) for
\displaystyle\psi_{2}
that the long wave profiles must satisfy

(3.1.14)

\begin{cases}\psi_{1}=\breve{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon}\big[\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1})\psi_{1}+\epsilon^{-1+\gamma+\mu+2\beta}\breve{\mathsf{N}}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\big]\\
\\
\psi_{2}=\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu+\beta)}\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1})+\epsilon^{1-(\gamma+\mu)+\beta}\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{2}^{\epsilon}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2}).\end{cases}

We have been tacitly assuming that all of the exponents on powers of
\displaystyle\epsilon
above are nonnegative so that the various
\displaystyle\epsilon
-dependent operators and prefactors are actually defined at
\displaystyle\epsilon=0
.
In particular, this demands

(3.1.15)

1-2\beta\geq 0,\qquad-1+\gamma+\mu+2\beta\geq 0,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad 1-(\gamma+\mu+\beta)\geq 0.

3.2.
The formal long wave limit and exponent selection

Our intention is now to take the limit
\displaystyle\epsilon\to 0
in the equations (
3.1.14
) for
\displaystyle\psi_{1}
and
\displaystyle\psi_{2}
.
Doing so in a way that the limit is both meaningful (i.e., defined and nontrivial) and reflective of what the numerics predict at
\displaystyle\epsilon=0
will teach us what the exponents
\displaystyle\mu
,
\displaystyle\gamma
, and
\displaystyle\beta
should be, beyond the requirements of (
3.1.15
).

3.2.1.
The formal limit on
\displaystyle\breve{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon}
and the selection of the exponents
\displaystyle\gamma
and
\displaystyle\mu

We want to assign a ‘natural’ definition to
\displaystyle\breve{\mathsf{M}}_{0}
, where
\displaystyle\breve{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon}
was defined, for
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
, in (
3.1.13
).
However, we relied above on having
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
to invoke the scaled Fourier multiplier identity (
A.2.3
) that gave us
\displaystyle\breve{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon}
, and naively setting
\displaystyle\epsilon=0
in that identity is meaningless.
Additionally, we should be careful that the prefactor
\displaystyle\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))}
in (
3.1.13
) does not lead us to define
\displaystyle\breve{\mathsf{M}}_{0}=0
; otherwise, we would have
\displaystyle\psi_{1}=0
when
\displaystyle\epsilon=0
, and that is not what the numerics in Fig.
2
predict.

A natural starting point, then, is to study
\displaystyle\breve{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon}
in the limit
\displaystyle\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, and this amounts to considering the limit of its symbol, whose definition we extract from the definition of
\displaystyle\breve{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon}
in (
3.1.13
) and the definition of the scaled Fourier multiplier in (
A.2.3
).
Thus, for each
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{R}
, we want the limit

(3.2.1)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))}\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)

to exist without being identically zero.
The function
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}
was defined in (
2.3.1
).

To calculate this limit, we first state the Taylor expansions

(3.2.2)

1-e^{-iz}=iz+iz^{2}N_{1}(z)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\cos(z)-1=-\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\frac{iz^{4}N_{2}(z)}{2\tau_{2}}

for
\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{C}
.
The functions
\displaystyle N_{1}
and
\displaystyle N_{2}
are analytic and uniformly bounded on strips in the sense that

(3.2.3)

C_{q}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|N_{1}(x\pm{iq})|+|N_{2}(x\pm{iq})|<\infty

for any
\displaystyle q>0
.
The choice of constants on
\displaystyle N_{1}
and
\displaystyle N_{2}
will permit some useful cancellations later.
Then

\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{c}(k)=\frac{ik+ik^{2}N_{1}(k)}{ick-\tau_{2}k^{2}+ik^{4}N_{2}(k)}=\frac{1+kN_{1}(k)}{c+i\tau_{2}k+k^{3}N_{2}(k)},

and so

(3.2.4)

\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))}\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)=\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))}\frac{1+\epsilon^{\mu}kN_{1}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)}{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}+i\tau_{2}\epsilon^{\mu}k+\epsilon^{3\mu}k^{3}N_{2}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)}.

At this point it does not make sense to set
\displaystyle\epsilon=0
, as then the denominator would be identically zero.
So, we would like to factor some power of
\displaystyle\epsilon
out of the denominator.
Since the first term in the denominator has a factor of
\displaystyle\epsilon^{\gamma}
and the second a factor of
\displaystyle\epsilon^{\mu}
, we assume
\displaystyle\gamma=\mu
and remove the power of
\displaystyle\epsilon
from both the first and the second terms.
We discuss the choice of
\displaystyle\gamma=\mu
further in Remark
3.2.2
.

Then

(3.2.5)

\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))}\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)=\epsilon^{2(1-2\gamma)}\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}(\epsilon^{\gamma}k)=\epsilon^{2(1-2\gamma)-\gamma}\frac{1+\epsilon^{\gamma}kN_{1}(\epsilon^{\gamma}k)}{c_{0}+i\tau_{2}k+\epsilon^{2\gamma}k^{3}N_{2}(\epsilon^{\gamma}k)}.

Pointwise in
\displaystyle k
we have

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1+\epsilon^{\gamma}kN_{1}(\epsilon^{\gamma}k)}{c_{0}+i\tau_{2}k+\epsilon^{2\gamma}k^{3}N_{2}(\epsilon^{\gamma}k)}=\frac{1}{c_{0}+i\tau_{2}k},

and so we want

2(1-2\gamma)-\gamma=0

so that the prefactor of
\displaystyle\epsilon^{2(1-2\gamma)-\gamma}
in (
3.2.5
) does not induce a trivial or undefined limit.
Thus we take

\gamma=\mu=\frac{2}{5}.

Certainly doing so does not contradict any of the inequalities in (
3.1.15
), provided that
\displaystyle\beta
is chosen appropriately.
Moreover, the power of
\displaystyle 2/5
agrees with the height-speed-width relations suggested in Fig.
3
.
And so

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))}\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\epsilon^{4/5}\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon^{2/5}c_{0}}(\epsilon^{2/5}k)=\frac{1}{c_{0}+\tau_{2}ik}.

Put

(3.2.6)

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(0)}(z):=\frac{1}{c_{0}+i\tau_{2}z},

so
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(0)}
is analytic on any strip
\displaystyle\!\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ \middle|\ |\im(z)|<q\right\}
for
\displaystyle q\in(0,\tau_{2}/c_{0})
.
Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(0)}
be the Fourier multiplier with symbol
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(0)}
.

Lemma
A.3.1
then gives the following properties of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(0)}
; the identities (
3.2.7
) are direct calculations with the Fourier transform.

Lemma 3.2.1
.

Fix
\displaystyle q\in(0,\tau_{2}/c_{0})
.
Then
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(0)}\in\mathbf{B}(H_{q}^{r},H_{q}^{r+1})
for all
\displaystyle r
.
More generally, if
\displaystyle f\in H^{1}
and
\displaystyle g\in L^{2}
, then

(3.2.7)

\mathcal{M}^{(0)}(c_{0}+\tau_{2}\partial_{X})f=f\qquad\text{ and }\qquad(c_{0}+\tau_{2}\partial_{X})\mathcal{M}^{(0)}g=g.

Because of the identities (
3.2.7
), we write
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(0)}=(c_{0}+\tau_{2}\partial_{X})^{-1}
.
The formal analysis above then leads us to expect

(3.2.8)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\breve{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{M}^{(0)}=(c_{0}+\tau_{2}\partial_{X})^{-1}.

However, we have not yet proved this rigorously by any means.

Remark 3.2.2
.

Here is why we take
\displaystyle\gamma=\mu
when factoring the power of
\displaystyle\epsilon
out of the denominator in (
3.2.4
).
First, taking
\displaystyle\gamma>\mu
produces

\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))}\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)=\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))-\mu}\frac{1+\epsilon^{\mu}kN_{1}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)}{\epsilon^{\gamma-\mu}c_{0}+i\tau_{2}k+\epsilon^{2\mu}k^{3}N_{2}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)}

instead of (
3.2.5
).
If
\displaystyle 2(1-(\gamma+\mu))-\mu>0
, then the right side above is identically zero at
\displaystyle\epsilon=0
, and so we demand
\displaystyle 2(1-(\gamma+\mu))-\mu=0
; there are many pairs of
\displaystyle\gamma
and
\displaystyle\mu
that work here.
But then

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1+\epsilon^{\mu}kN_{1}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)}{\epsilon^{\gamma-\mu}c_{0}+i\tau_{2}k+\epsilon^{2\mu}k^{3}N_{2}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)}=\frac{1}{i\tau_{2}k}.

This suggests that instead of (
3.2.8
), we have

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\breve{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon}=(\tau_{2}\partial_{X})^{-1}.

However, this is meaningless: differentiation is not invertible from
\displaystyle H_{q}^{r}
to
\displaystyle H_{q}^{r+1}
.

Taking
\displaystyle\gamma<\mu
also does not work.
In that case, instead of (
3.2.5
) we would have found

\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))}\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{\epsilon^{\gamma}c_{0}}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)=\epsilon^{2(1-(\gamma+\mu))-\gamma}\frac{1+\epsilon^{\mu}kN_{1}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)}{c_{0}+i\tau_{2}\epsilon^{\mu-\gamma}k+\epsilon^{3\mu-\gamma}k^{3}N_{2}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)}.

Since
\displaystyle\gamma<\mu
we find

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1+\epsilon^{\mu}kN_{1}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)}{c_{0}+i\tau_{2}\epsilon^{\mu-\gamma}k+\epsilon^{3\mu-\gamma}k^{3}N_{2}(\epsilon^{\mu}k)}=\frac{1}{c_{0}}.

We would then want
\displaystyle 2-3\gamma-2\mu=0
to prevent a nontrivial limit.

Choosing
\displaystyle\gamma
and
\displaystyle\mu
appropriately, we conclude that at
\displaystyle\epsilon=0
the equation for
\displaystyle\psi_{1}
from (
3.1.14
) formally reduces to

\psi_{1}=\frac{1}{c_{0}}\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{0}(\psi_{1})\psi_{1}.

Numerically we expect
\displaystyle\psi_{1}(X)>0
for all
\displaystyle X
when
\displaystyle\epsilon=0
, and so, using the definition of
\displaystyle\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{0}
from (
3.1.8
), we have

c_{0}=\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{0}(\psi_{1})(X)=\frac{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}}{c_{0}^{2}}\int_{X}^{\infty}\left(\int_{V}^{\infty}\psi_{1}(W)\ dW\right)\psi_{1}(V)\ dV.

Differentiating, we find

\left(\int_{X}^{\infty}\psi_{1}(W)\ dW\right)\psi_{1}(X)=0.

But since
\displaystyle\psi_{1}(W)>0
for all
\displaystyle W
, we cancel the integral factor to find
\displaystyle\psi_{1}(X)=0
, a contradiction to our numerical predictions.

3.2.2.
The formal leading order equation for
\displaystyle\psi_{1}

At
\displaystyle\epsilon=0
the equation for
\displaystyle\psi_{1}
in (
3.1.14
) becomes (again, formally)

\psi_{1}=\mathcal{M}^{(0)}\big(\breve{\mathsf{R}}^{0}(\psi_{1})\psi_{1}\big)=(c_{0}+\tau_{2}\partial_{X})^{-1}\big(\breve{\mathsf{R}}^{0}(\psi_{1})\psi_{1}\big).

This is equivalent to

(3.2.9)

c_{0}\psi_{1}+\tau_{2}\psi_{1}^{\prime}=\breve{\mathsf{R}}^{0}(\psi_{1})\psi_{1}.

We will rewrite this equation so that each term is a perfect derivative.

The definition of
\displaystyle\breve{\mathsf{R}}_{1}^{\epsilon}
in (
3.1.8
), valid for all
\displaystyle\epsilon
, gives

(3.2.10)

\breve{\mathsf{R}}^{0}(\psi_{1})(X)=\frac{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}}{c_{0}^{2}}\int_{X}^{\infty}\left(\int_{V}^{\infty}\psi_{1}(W)\ dW\right)\psi_{1}(V)\ dV.

Write

\Psi_{1}(X):=\int_{X}^{\infty}\psi_{1}(W)\ dW,

so that
\displaystyle\Psi_{1}^{\prime}=-\psi_{1}
.
The double integral from (
3.2.10
) is

\int_{X}^{\infty}\left(\int_{V}^{\infty}\psi_{1}(W)\ dW\right)\psi_{1}(V)\ dV=-\int_{X}^{\infty}\Psi_{1}(V)\Psi_{1}^{\prime}(V)\ dV=-\int_{X}^{\infty}\partial_{V}\left[\frac{\Psi_{1}^{2}(V)}{2}\right]\ dV\\
=\frac{\Psi_{1}(X)^{2}}{2}.

Here we are using the requirement that
\displaystyle\psi_{1}\in H_{q}^{1}
, which implies
\displaystyle\Psi_{1}(X)\to 0
as
\displaystyle X\to\infty
.
Thus

\breve{\mathsf{R}}^{0}(\psi_{1})\psi_{1}=-\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}}{2c_{0}^{2}}\right)\Psi_{1}^{2}\Psi_{1}^{\prime}=-\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}}{6c_{0}^{2}}\right)\partial_{X}[\Psi_{1}^{3}].

Then (
3.2.9
) is equivalent to

\tau_{2}\Psi_{1}^{\prime\prime}+c_{0}\Psi_{1}^{\prime}-\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}}{6c_{0}^{2}}\right)\partial_{X}[\Psi_{1}^{3}]=0.

We integrate both sides from 0 to
\displaystyle\infty
and use the aforementioned fact that
\displaystyle\Psi_{1}
and its derivatives are required to vanish at
\displaystyle\infty
to find

(3.2.11)

\tau_{2}\Psi_{1}^{\prime}+c_{0}\Psi_{1}-\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}}{6c_{0}^{2}}\right)\Psi_{1}^{3}=0.

This is a Bernoulli equation, and it has the solution

(3.2.12)

\Psi_{1}(X)=\Sigma(X):=\left(\frac{6c_{0}^{3}}{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}+6c_{0}^{2}\exp\big(2c_{0}X/\tau_{2}+\theta\big)}\right)^{1/2}.

Here
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{R}
is an arbitrary phase shift.
It follows that putting

(3.2.13)

\psi_{1}(X)=\sigma(X):=-\Sigma^{\prime}(X)=\frac{(6c_{0}^{3})^{3/2}\exp\big(2c_{0}X/\tau_{2}+\theta\big)}{\tau_{2}\big[\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}+6c_{0}^{2}\exp\big(2c_{0}X/\tau_{2}+\theta\big)\big]^{3/2}}

solves (
3.2.9
).

Friesecke and Pego
[
20
]
do not incorporate a phase shift like
\displaystyle\theta
into their leading order
\displaystyle\sech^{2}
-type KdV solution, since their broader existence result relies on working in spaces of even functions.
We will not need such symmetry in our subsequent arguments (nor could we achieve it, since no translation of
\displaystyle\sigma
is even or odd), and so we will leave
\displaystyle\theta
as an arbitrary free parameter and not specify its value.

3.2.3.
The formal leading order equation for
\displaystyle\psi_{2}
and the selection of the exponent
\displaystyle\beta

From our choice of
\displaystyle\gamma=\mu=2/5
and the inequalities in (
3.1.15
), we need, at the very least,

\frac{1}{10}\leq\beta\leq\frac{1}{5}.

If the strict inequality
\displaystyle\beta<1/5
holds, then at
\displaystyle\epsilon=0
the equation for
\displaystyle\psi_{2}
in (
3.1.14
) reduces to the trivial result
\displaystyle\psi_{2}=0
.
This is not at all what we expect numerically from Figure
2
; rather, we anticipate that
\displaystyle\psi_{2}
will asymptote to some nonzero constant at
\displaystyle\infty
.

However, if we instead take
\displaystyle\beta
so that

0=1-(\gamma+\mu+\beta)=\frac{1}{5}-\beta,

which is to say,

\beta=\frac{1}{5},

then the equation for
\displaystyle\psi_{2}
in (
3.1.14
) at
\displaystyle\epsilon=0
becomes

\psi_{2}=\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{0}(\psi_{1}).

Putting

(3.2.14)

\psi_{2}(X)=\zeta(X):=\breve{\mathsf{P}}_{1}^{0}(\sigma)(X)=\frac{\alpha}{c_{0}}\int_{X}^{\infty}\sigma(V)\ dV

therefore solves the leading order equation for
\displaystyle\psi_{2}
.
We really have

\zeta(X)=\frac{\alpha}{c_{0}}\Sigma(X)=\frac{\alpha}{c_{0}}\left(\frac{6c_{0}^{3}}{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}+6c_{0}^{2}e^{2c_{0}X/\tau_{2}+\theta}}\right)^{1/2},

where
\displaystyle\Sigma
was defined in (
3.2.12
).

3.3.
The final long wave system

With the choices of exponents
\displaystyle\gamma=\mu=2/5
and
\displaystyle\beta=1/5
, it becomes convenient to introduce the new small parameter

(3.3.1)

\nu:=\epsilon^{2/5}

into the problem (
3.1.14
) and then recast that problem more cleanly in terms of
\displaystyle\nu
.
First, the long wave Ansatz (
3.1.1
) becomes

(3.3.2)

\phi_{1}(x)=\nu^{5/2}\psi_{1}(\nu{x}),\qquad\phi_{2}(x)=\nu^{1/2}\psi_{2}(\nu{x}),\qquad\text{ and }\qquad c=\nu{c}_{0}.

Proceeding very much as in Section
3.1
, we then define

(3.3.3)

\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{\nu}(X,Y):=\frac{X^{2}Y}{k_{a}(k_{a}+\nu^{5/2}{X})}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{Q}_{2}^{\nu}(X,Y):=\kappa\frac{k_{r}Y+k_{m}\nu^{2}X+\nu^{5/2}{XY}}{(k_{r}+\nu^{5/2}{X})(k_{m}+\nu^{1/2}Y)}XY

for
\displaystyle X
,
\displaystyle Y\in\mathbb{R}
, while for
\displaystyle f\in L^{1}
and
\displaystyle g\in L^{\infty}
, we put

(3.3.4)

\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\nu}(f)(X):=\frac{\alpha}{c_{0}}\int_{X}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(\nu^{1/2}S^{\nu}f)(V,X)f(V)\ dV,

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}
was defined in (
3.1.3
), and

(3.3.5)

\mathcal{P}_{2}^{\nu}(f,g)(X):=\frac{1}{c_{0}}\int_{X}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(\nu^{1/2}S^{\nu}f)(V,X)\mathcal{Q}_{2}^{\nu}(f(V+\nu),g(V))\ dV,

Proposition 4.1.1

Let
\displaystyle q\in(0,c_{0}/\tau_{2})

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\in\mathbf{B}(H_{q}^{1})

18

A.2.
Fourier multipliers on Sobolev spaces

For integers
\displaystyle r\geq 0
, we denote by
\displaystyle H^{r}=H^{r}(\mathbb{R})
the usual Sobolev space of all
\displaystyle r
-times weakly differentiable functions whose weak derivatives are square-integrable.

Our fundamental operator on Sobolev spaces is the Fourier multiplier.
The following result above is standard; see, e.g.,
[
17
, Lem. D.2.1]
.

Lemma A.2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be measurable and suppose

N_{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}}(r,s):=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(k)|}{(1+k^{2})^{(r-s)/2}}<\infty.

Then the
Fourier multiplier
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
with symbol
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
defined by

(A.2.1)

\mathcal{M}{f}:=\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\big[\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\widehat{f}\big],

i.e., by
\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{M}{f}}(k)=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(k)\widehat{f}(k)
, is a bounded operator from
\displaystyle H^{r}
to
\displaystyle H^{s}
, and

(A.2.2)

\|\mathcal{M}\|_{\mathbf{B}(H^{r},H^{s})}=N_{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}}(r,s).

We also need a convenient expression for ‘scaled’ Fourier multipliers.
If
\displaystyle f
is a function on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle\nu\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, let
\displaystyle f(\nu\cdot)
be the ‘scaled’ map
\displaystyle x\mapsto f(\nu{x})
.
Now let
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
be the Fourier multiplier with symbol
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
and define
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}(k):=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(\nu{k})
.
Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(\nu)}
be the Fourier multiplier with symbol
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}
.
Then standard scaling properties of the Fourier transform imply that

(A.2.3)

\mathcal{M}[f(\nu\cdot)](x)=(\mathcal{M}^{(\nu)}f)(\nu{x}).

A.3.
Fourier multipliers on weighted Sobolev spaces

We frequently work with weighted Sobolev spaces.
For
\displaystyle q\in\mathbb{R}
, let

H_{q}^{r}:=\!\left\{f\in H_{q}^{r}\ \middle|\ e^{q|\cdot|}f\in H^{r}\right\}.

We norm this space by

\|f\|_{H_{q}^{r}}:=\|e^{q|\cdot|}f\|_{H^{r}},

and, see
[
17
, App.@ C]
, this norm is equivalent to

f\mapsto\sum_{j=0}^{r}\|e^{q|\cdot|}\partial_{x}^{j}[f]\|_{L^{2}}.

We put
\displaystyle L_{q}^{2}:=H_{q}^{0}
.
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality guarantees that
\displaystyle L_{q}^{2}
embeds into
\displaystyle L^{1}
:

\|f\|_{L^{1}}=\|e^{-q|\cdot|}(e^{q|\cdot|}f)\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|e^{-|q\cdot|}\|_{L^{2}}\|e^{q|\cdot|}f\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{q}\|f\|_{L_{q}^{2}}.

Finally, if
\displaystyle I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an interval, we sometimes denote by
\displaystyle L_{q}^{2}(I)
the set of all measurable functions
\displaystyle f\colon I\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\int_{I}e^{2q|X|}|f(X)|^{2}\ dX<\infty.

Since
\displaystyle H_{q}^{r}\subseteq H^{r}
, any Fourier multiplier defined on
\displaystyle H^{r}
is defined on
\displaystyle H_{q}^{r}
.
A variation on a result of Beale
[
8
, Lem. 5.1]
gives sufficient conditions for a Fourier multiplier on
\displaystyle H_{q}^{r}
to map into another weighted space
\displaystyle H_{q}^{s}
.

Lemma A.3.1

(Beale)
.

Fix
\displaystyle q>0
and let

\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{q}:=\!\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ \middle|\ |\im(z)|\leq q\right\}.

Let
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
be analytic on
\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{q}
.
Suppose there exist
\displaystyle s\geq 0
and
\displaystyle C
,
\displaystyle r_{0}>0
such that if
\displaystyle z\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{q}
and
\displaystyle r_{0}<|z|
, then

|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(z)|\leq\frac{C}{|\re(z)|^{(s-r)}}.

Then the Fourier multiplier
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
with symbol
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
, defined by (
A.2.1
), is a bounded operator from
\displaystyle H_{q}^{r}
to
\displaystyle H_{q}^{s}
and

\|\mathcal{M}\|_{\mathbf{B}(H_{q}^{r},H_{q}^{s})}\leq\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\big|(1+k^{2})^{(s-r)/2}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(k\pm{i}q)\big|.

\displaystyle z
is ‘far from’ 0.
Part of these estimates will involve bounding the denominator of
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}
away from zero; this will ensure the analyticity of
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}
, since it is the quotient of two analytic functions.

To quantify these regimes, we introduce two positive constants
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle m
; we say that
\displaystyle z
is ‘close’ to 0 if
\displaystyle|z|\leq\nu^{-p}
and ‘far from’ 0 if
\displaystyle|z|>\nu^{-p}
.
The constant
\displaystyle m
will later control how close the real part of
\displaystyle\nu{z}
is to an integer multiple of
\displaystyle 2\pi
, a bound that will be very useful in certain estimates to come.
All constants
\displaystyle C
in the work below are allowed to depend on
\displaystyle m
,
\displaystyle p
, and
\displaystyle q
, but they are always independent of
\displaystyle\nu
and
\displaystyle z
.

Our estimates will depend on the parameters
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle m
; once we have all the estimates together, we will choose useful values for
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle m
.
We feel that this approach allows the otherwise nonobvious final values for
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle m
to emerge very naturally.
This strategy of splitting the estimates over regions close to and far from 0 is modeled on the proofs of
[
18
, Lem. A.13]
and
[
61
, Lem. 3]
and the strategy in
[
36
, App. A.3]
.
Friesecke and Pego
[
20
, Sec. 3]
give a rather different proof of symbol convergence that relies on more knowledge of the poles of
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}
than we care to discover.

B.1.
Estimates for
\displaystyle z
‘close to’ 0

In this regime we fix
\displaystyle|z|\leq\nu^{-p}
.
We recall the Taylor expansions

1-e^{-iz}=iz+iz^{2}N_{1}(z)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\cos(z)-1=-\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\frac{iz^{4}N_{2}(z)}{2\tau_{2}}

from (
3.2.2
), as well as the estimate

C_{q}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|N_{1}(x\pm{iq})|+|N_{2}(x\pm{iq})|<\infty.

Now we can write

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}(z)=\frac{1+\nu{z}N_{1}(\nu{z})}{c_{0}+\tau_{2}iz+\nu^{2}z^{3}N_{2}(\nu{z})}.

With this expression we find the following equality

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}(z)-\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{0}(z)=I_{\nu}(z)+I\!I_{\nu}(z),

where

(B.1.1)

I_{\nu}(z):=\frac{c_{0}\nu{z}N_{1}(\nu{z})-\nu^{2}z^{3}N_{2}(\nu{z})}{(c_{0}+\tau_{2}iz+\nu^{2}z^{3}N_{2}(\nu z))(c_{0}+i\tau_{2}z)}

and

(B.1.2)

I\!I_{\nu}(z):=\frac{i\tau_{2}\nu{z}^{2}N_{1}(\nu{z})}{(c_{0}+\tau_{2}iz+\nu^{2}z^{3}N_{2}(\nu z))(c_{0}+i\tau_{2}z)}.

We work on the denominators.
We use the reverse triangle inequality to find

\big|\big(c_{0}+\tau_{2}iz-2\tau_{2}i\nu^{2}z^{3}N_{2}(\nu{z})\big)(c_{0}+\tau_{2}iz)\big|\geq\big|c_{0}-\tau_{2}q-2\tau_{2}\nu^{2}|z|^{3}|N_{2}(\nu{z})|\big||c_{0}-\tau_{2}q|.

As
\displaystyle q\in(0,c_{0}/\tau_{2})
, we have
\displaystyle|c_{0}-\tau_{2}q|>0
.
Also, since
\displaystyle|z|\leq\nu^{-p}
, we have
\displaystyle\nu^{2}|z|^{3}\leq\nu^{2-3p}
.
If we take

(B.1.3)

0<p<\frac{2}{3}

and assume
\displaystyle\nu\in(0,\nu_{1})
, where

(B.1.4)

\nu_{1}:=\min\left\{1,\left(\frac{|c_{0}-\tau_{2}q|}{4C_{q}\tau_{2}}\right)^{1/(2-3p)}\right\},

then

(B.1.5)

\big|c_{0}-\tau_{2}q-\nu^{2}|z|^{3}|N_{2}(\nu{z})|\big|\geq\frac{|c_{0}-\tau_{2}q|}{2}.

In particular,

(B.1.6)

\big|c_{0}-\tau_{2}q-2\tau_{2}\nu^{2}|z|^{3}|N_{2}(\nu{z})|\big||c_{0}-\tau_{2}q|\geq\frac{|c_{0}-\tau_{2}q|^{2}}{2},

and this inequality guarantees that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}
is defined (and analytic) for
\displaystyle|z|\leq\nu^{-p}
and
\displaystyle|\im(z)|<q
.
Then we use (
B.1.6
) to estimate
\displaystyle I_{\nu}(z)
from (
B.1.1
) as

|I_{\nu}(z)|\leq C\nu^{1-p}+C\nu^{2-3p}.

Next, we use (
B.1.5
) to estimate
\displaystyle I\!I_{\nu}(z)
from (
B.1.2
) as

|I\!I_{\nu}(z)|\leq C\nu^{1-p}\frac{|z|}{|c_{0}+i\tau_{2}z|}.

Setting
\displaystyle z=x\pm iq
we note

\frac{|z|^{2}}{|c_{0}+i\tau_{2}z|^{2}}=\frac{x^{2}+q^{2}}{(c_{0}\pm\tau_{2}q)^{2}+\tau_{2}^{2}x^{2}}\leq\frac{x^{2}+q^{2}}{(c_{0}-\tau_{2}q)^{2}+\tau_{2}^{2}x^{2}}.

We know

D:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{x^{2}+q^{2}}{(c_{0}-\tau_{2}q)^{2}+c_{0}^{2}x^{2}}<\infty,

and thus

|I\!I_{\nu}(z)|\leq C\nu^{1-p}.

We conclude

(B.1.7)

\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}(z)-\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(0)}(z)\big|\leq|I_{\nu}(z)|+|I\!I_{\nu}(z)|\leq C\big(\nu^{1-p}+\nu^{2-3p}\big).

As we required
\displaystyle p\in(0,2/3)
, the final estimate contains only positive powers of
\displaystyle\nu
.
Since we will always consider
\displaystyle 0<\nu<\nu_{1}
in the future, the definition of
\displaystyle\nu_{1}
in (
B.1.4
) ensures
\displaystyle 0<\nu<1
in the following regimes.

B.2.
Estimates for
\displaystyle z
‘far from’ 0

In this regime we assume
\displaystyle|z|>\nu^{p}
.
Take

(B.2.1)

\nu_{2}<\min\left\{\nu_{1},\left(\frac{\tau_{2}}{2c_{0}}\right)^{1/p}\right\},

with
\displaystyle\nu_{1}
defined in (
B.1.4
), so that if
\displaystyle 0<\nu<\nu_{2}
, then
\displaystyle|z|>c_{0}/\tau_{2}
.
With the reverse triangle inequality we find

(B.2.2)

|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(0)}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{\big||c_{0}|-|\tau_{2}z|\big|}<\frac{1}{\tau_{2}\nu^{-p}-c_{0}}<\frac{2}{\tau_{2}}\nu^{p}.

Consequently, it suffices in this regime to show that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}
is bounded by a multiple of some power of
\displaystyle\nu
.
It will be convenient now to rewrite
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}
as

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}(z)=\frac{\nu\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{(\nu)}(z)}{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{(\nu)}(z)},

where

(B.2.3)

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{(\nu)}(z):=1-e^{-i\nu{z}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{(\nu)}(z):=ic_{0}\nu^{2}z+2\tau_{2}(\cos(\nu{z})-1).

The analyticity of
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}
for
\displaystyle|z|>\nu^{p}
will follow if we bound
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{(\nu)}
away from zero here.

The presence of the factor
\displaystyle\cos(\nu{z})-1
in the denominator of
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}
suggests that the behavior of this function may be different when
\displaystyle\re(\nu{z})
is ‘close’ to an integer multiple of
\displaystyle 2\pi
and when it is not.
For this reason, we expand
\displaystyle z=x\pm iq
and let
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{Z}
be the unique integer such that
\displaystyle|\nu{x}-2\pi{n}|\leq\pi
.
We consider three cases on the behavior of
\displaystyle\nu{x}
and
\displaystyle n
.

B.2.1.
Estimates for
\displaystyle\re(\nu{z})
‘close to’ a nonzero integer multiple of
\displaystyle 2\pi

In this regime we assume
\displaystyle|\nu{x}-2\pi{n}|\leq\nu^{m}
with
\displaystyle n\neq 0
.

We first rewrite the numerator as

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{(\nu)}(z)=1-e^{-i(\nu{x}-2\pi{n)}}+e^{-i\nu{x}}(1-e^{\pm\nu{q}}).

Since the map
\displaystyle y\mapsto e^{-iy}
is uniformly Lipschitz on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
we have

|1-e^{-i(\nu{x}-2\pi{n})}|\leq|\nu{x}-2\pi{n}|\leq\nu^{m}.

Since the map
\displaystyle y\mapsto e^{-y}
is locally Lipschitz on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
we have, if we take
\displaystyle 0<\nu<\nu_{3}
with

(B.2.4)

\nu_{3}:=\min\left\{\nu_{2},\frac{1}{q}\right\}

and
\displaystyle\nu_{2}
defined in (
B.2.1
), the estimate

|1-e^{\pm\nu{q}}|\leq\nu{q}.

Then

(B.2.5)

\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{(\nu)}(z)\big|\leq\nu^{m}+\nu{q}\leq C(\nu^{m}+\nu).

We remark that we did not need
\displaystyle n\neq 0
here, although we will momentarily.

We now turn to the denominator,
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}^{(\nu)}(z)
.
Using the identity

(B.2.6)

\cos(a+bi)=\cos(a)\cosh(b)-i\sin(a)\sinh(b)

for
\displaystyle a,b\in\mathbb{R}
we find

\im\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{(\nu)}(z)\big)=c_{0}\nu^{2}x-2\tau_{2}\sin(\nu x)\sinh(\nu q).

We estimate

\big|\im\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{(\nu)}(z)\big)\big|\geq C\big(\nu|n|-\nu|\nu{x}-2n\pi|-|\sin(\nu{x}-2n\pi)||\sinh(\nu{q})|\big)

We control the three terms on the right as follows.
First,
\displaystyle|n|\geq 1
.
Next, we are in the regime
\displaystyle|\nu{x}-2n\pi|\leq\nu^{m}
.
Finally, we have

|\sin(\nu{x}-2n\pi)|\leq|\nu{x}-2n\pi|\leq\nu^{m}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad|\sinh(\nu{q})|\leq 2|\nu{q}|,

since
\displaystyle|\nu{q}|\leq 1
. We thus find

\big|\im\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{(\nu)}(z)\big)\big|\geq C\big(\nu-\nu^{m+1}).

Now that we have the numerator and the denominator bounded, we can conclude

(B.2.7)

\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}(z)\big|\leq C\nu\frac{\nu^{m}+\nu}{\nu-\nu^{m+1}}=C\frac{\nu^{m}+\nu}{1-\nu^{m}}\leq C\nu^{m}.

Here we need to assume

(B.2.8)

0<m<1.

B.2.2.
Estimates for
\displaystyle\re(\nu{z})
‘close to’ 0

In this regime we assume
\displaystyle|\nu{x}|\leq\nu^{m}
; in particular, we are taking
\displaystyle n=0
.
We will need the following bound on the cosine, which is a consequence of an elementary argument with Taylor’s theorem.

Lemma B.2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle Q\geq 0
.
There exist
\displaystyle C_{1,Q}
,
\displaystyle C_{2,Q}>0
such that if
\displaystyle Z\in\mathbb{C}
with
\displaystyle|Z|\leq C_{1,Q}
and
\displaystyle|\im(Z)|\leq Q
, then

|\cos(Z)-1|\geq C_{2,Q}|Z|^{2}.

In particular, if
\displaystyle Q=0
, then
\displaystyle C_{1,0}>\pi
.

We use the reverse triangle inequality on
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{(\nu)}
from (
B.2.3
) to find

(B.2.9)

\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{(\nu)}(z)\big|\geq 2\tau_{2}|\cos(\nu{z})-1|-c_{0}\nu^{2}q-c_{0}\nu^{2}|x|.

Take
\displaystyle 0<\nu<\nu_{\mathcal{M}}
, where

(B.2.10)

\nu_{\mathcal{M}}<\min\left\{\nu_{3},\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)^{1/(1-m)},\left(\frac{C_{1,q}}{2}\right)^{1/m}\right\},

with
\displaystyle\nu_{3}
defined in (
B.2.4
), to find

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\nu z|\leq\nu^{m}+\nu{z}\leq 2\nu^{m}<C_{1,q}.

Lemma
B.2.1
then guarantees

|\cos(\nu{z})-1|\geq C|\nu{z}|^{2}\geq C\nu^{2-2p}.

Finally, since
\displaystyle|x|\leq\nu^{m-1}
in this regime we use the bound (
B.2.9
) to conclude

\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{(\nu)}(z)\big|\geq C\big(\nu^{2-2p}-\nu^{2}-\nu^{m+1}\big).

We remark that the derivation of the estimate (
B.2.5
) only assumed
\displaystyle|\nu{x}-2\pi{n}|\leq\nu^{m}
and did not rely on having
\displaystyle n\neq 0
.
So it is still valid here, and we conclude

(B.2.11)

\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}(z)\big|\leq C\nu\frac{\nu^{m}+\nu}{\nu^{2-2p}-\nu^{2}-\nu^{m+1}}=C\frac{\nu^{m+2p-1}+\nu^{2p}}{1-\nu^{2p}-\nu^{m+2p-1}}\leq C\big(\nu^{m+2p-1}+\nu^{2p}\big).

Here we are assuming

(B.2.12)

0\leq 1-2p<\min\{1,m\}.

B.2.3.
Estimates for
\displaystyle\re(\nu{z})
‘far from’ a nonzero integer multiple of
\displaystyle 2\pi

In this regime we assume
\displaystyle|\nu{x}-2\pi{n}|>\nu^{m}
.
We do not perform separate work on
\displaystyle n=0
and
\displaystyle n\neq 0
.

Via (
B.2.6
) we find

\re\big(\mathcal{M}_{2}^{(\nu)}(z)\big)=-c_{0}\nu^{2}q+2\tau_{2}(\cos(\nu{x}-2n\pi)-1)+2\tau_{2}\cos(\nu{x})(\cosh(\nu{q})-1).

We estimate

\big|\re\big(\mathcal{M}_{2}^{(\nu)}(z)\big)\big|\geq C\big(|\cos(\nu{x}-2n\pi)-1|-|\cos(\nu{x})||\cosh(\nu{q})-1|-\nu^{2}\big).

Now we use Lemma
B.2.1
with
\displaystyle Q=0
to bound

|\cos(\nu{x}-2n\pi)-1|\geq C|\nu{z}-2n\pi|^{2}\geq C\nu^{2m}.

Also, a routine Lipschitz estimate on the hyperbolic cosine gives

|\cos(\nu{x})||\cosh(\nu{q})-1|\leq C\nu^{2}

since
\displaystyle|\nu{q}|\leq 1
.
We thus find

\big|\re\big(\mathcal{M}_{2}^{(\nu)}(z)\big)\big|\geq C(\nu^{2m}-\nu^{2}).

As we are assuming
\displaystyle 0<m<1
from (
B.2.8
), this is a positive lower bound.

Finally, we bound the numerator
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{\nu}(z)
crudely as
\displaystyle\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{(\nu)}(z)\big|\leq C
for all
\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{C}
with
\displaystyle|\im(z)|=q
.
This follows from the boundedness of
\displaystyle Z\mapsto e^{iZ}
on strips.
We conclude

(B.2.13)

\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}(z)\big|<C\frac{\nu}{\nu^{2m}-\nu^{2}}\leq C\nu^{1-2m}.

This is a positive bound if we now require

(B.2.14)

0<m<\frac{1}{2}.

B.3.
Overall estimates

Suppose
\displaystyle 0<\nu<\nu_{\mathcal{M}}
, where
\displaystyle\nu_{\mathcal{M}}
was specified in (
B.2.10
).
We conclude from (
B.1.7
) that

(B.3.1)

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\im(z)|=q\\
|z|\leq\nu^{-p}\end{subarray}}\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}(z)-\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(0)}(z)\big|\leq C\big(\nu^{1-p}+\nu^{2-3p}\big)

and, by combining (
B.2.2
), (
B.2.7
), (
B.2.11
), and (
B.2.13
), that

(B.3.2)

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\im(z)|=q\\
|z|>\nu^{-p}\end{subarray}}\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(\nu)}(z)-\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{(0)}(z)\big|\leq C\nu^{p}+C\max\big\{\nu^{m},\nu^{m+2p-1}+\nu^{2p},\nu^{1-2m}\big\}.

Additionally, we need, per (
B.1.3
), and (
B.2.12
), and (
B.2.14
), the exponents
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle m
to satisfy

(B.3.3)

0<p<\frac{2}{3},\qquad 0<m<\frac{1}{2},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad 0<1-2p<\min\{1,m\}.

There are many possible choices of
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle m
that will satisfy (
B.3.3
).
Purely for convenience, we elect to take
\displaystyle m=1/3
and then
\displaystyle p=1/2
.
We combine (
B.3.1
) and (
B.3.2
) to conclude the estimate (
B.0.1
).

Appendix C
The Proof of Proposition
4.1.1

Throughout this appendix we assume
\displaystyle q\in(0,c_{0}/\tau_{2})
.

C.1.
A formula for
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{-1}

Fix
\displaystyle g\in H_{q}^{1}
.
We want to find
\displaystyle f\in H_{q}^{1}
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}{f}=g
, and we want to do so in such a way that the mapping of
\displaystyle g
to this
\displaystyle f
is a bounded operator on
\displaystyle H_{q}^{1}
.
We recall that
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
was defined in (
4.1.3
).
The following steps are quite similar to the derivation of the Bernoulli solution
\displaystyle\sigma
in Section
3.2.2
.

We have
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}{f}=g
if and only if

(C.1.1)

(c_{0}+\tau_{2}\partial_{X})f-\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma)f+\big(D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma)f\big)\sigma=g,

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}
was defined in (
3.3.10
).
From this definition, we find

(C.1.2)

\big(D\mathcal{R}^{0}(\sigma)f\big)(X)=\int_{X}^{\infty}\left(\int_{W}^{\infty}\sigma(V)\ dV\right)f(W)\ dW+\int_{X}^{\infty}\left(\int_{W}^{\infty}f(V)\ dV\right)\sigma(W)\ dW.

Since
\displaystyle g
,
\displaystyle\sigma\in H_{q}^{1}
, and since we seek
\displaystyle f\in H_{q}^{1}
, we may define the antiderivatives

(C.1.3)

F(X):=\int_{X}^{\infty}f(W)\ dW,\qquad G(X):=\int_{X}^{\infty}g(W)\ dW\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Sigma(X):=\int_{X}^{\infty}\sigma(W)\ dW.

Then (
C.1.1
) is equivalent to

(C.1.4)

\tau_{2}F^{\prime\prime}(X)-c_{0}F^{\prime}(X)-\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}}{c_{0}^{2}}\right)F^{\prime}(X)\int_{X}^{\infty}\Sigma(W)\Sigma^{\prime}(W)\ dW\\
-\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}}{c_{0}^{2}}\right)\Sigma^{\prime}(X)\int_{X}^{\infty}\big(\Sigma(W)F^{\prime}(W)+F(W)\Sigma^{\prime}(W)\big)\ dW=-c_{0}G^{\prime}(X)-\tau_{2}G^{\prime\prime}(X).

Although it may not be apparent at first glance, every term in this equation is a perfect derivative.
First, since
\displaystyle\Sigma
and
\displaystyle F
must vanish at
\displaystyle+\infty
, we have

\int_{X}^{\infty}\Sigma(W)\Sigma^{\prime}(W)\ dW=-\frac{\Sigma(X)^{2}}{2}

and

\int_{X}^{\infty}\big(\Sigma(W)F^{\prime}(W)+F(W)\Sigma^{\prime}(W)\big)\ dW=-\Sigma(X)F(X).

Hence (
C.1.4
) really is

(C.1.5)

\tau_{2}F^{\prime\prime}-c_{0}F^{\prime}+\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}}{c_{0}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{F^{\prime}\Sigma^{2}}{2}+\Sigma^{\prime}\Sigma{F}\right)=-c_{0}G^{\prime}-\tau_{2}G^{\prime\prime},

where

\frac{F^{\prime}\Sigma^{2}}{2}+\Sigma^{\prime}\Sigma{F}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{X}[\Sigma^{2}F].

So, we deduce that
\displaystyle F
and
\displaystyle G
must satisfy

(C.1.6)

-\tau_{2}F^{\prime\prime}-c_{0}F^{\prime}+\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}}{c_{0}^{2}}\right)\partial_{X}\left[\frac{\Sigma^{2}F}{2}\right]=-c_{0}G^{\prime}-\tau_{2}G^{\prime\prime}.

Since both
\displaystyle F
and
\displaystyle G
must vanish at
\displaystyle+\infty
(though not necessarily at
\displaystyle-\infty
), we may integrate (
C.1.6
) to find

(C.1.7)

\tau_{2}F^{\prime}+c_{0}F-\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}}{c_{0}^{2}}\right)\frac{\Sigma^{2}F}{2}=c_{0}G+\tau_{2}G^{\prime}.

This is, of course, the linearization of the Bernoulli equation (
3.2.11
) at its solution
\displaystyle\Sigma
.

Moreover, (
C.1.7
) is really just a linear first-order ordinary differential equation, which we know how to solve with an integrating factor.
Put

(C.1.8)

\rho(X):=\frac{1}{\tau_{2}}\left(c_{0}-\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}}{c_{0}^{2}}\right)\frac{\Sigma(X)^{2}}{2}\right),

so that (
C.1.7
) is equivalent to

F^{\prime}+\rho{F}=rG+G^{\prime},\qquad r:=\frac{c_{0}}{\tau_{2}}.

Define an antiderivative
\displaystyle\Rho
of
\displaystyle\rho
by

(C.1.9)

\Rho(X):=\ln\left(\frac{[\alpha\kappa\tau_{1}+6c_{0}^{2}e^{2rX}]^{3/2}}{e^{2rX}}\right).

Then a solution to (
C.1.7
) is

(C.1.10)

F(X):=e^{-\Rho(X)}\int_{0}^{X}e^{\Rho(W)}\left(rG(W)+G^{\prime}(W)\right)\ dW.

This is, of course, not the most general solution to (
C.1.7
), as we have set the constant of integration that arises from the usual integrating factor method equal to 0.
But we know that if
\displaystyle F
is defined by (
C.1.10
), then we can put
\displaystyle f:=-F^{\prime}
to find that
\displaystyle f
solves (
C.1.1
).
After all, we just need to develop
one
solution to this problem.
It remains for us to check that we really do have
\displaystyle-F^{\prime}\in H_{q}^{1}
, to which we now turn.

We differentiate (
C.1.10
) to find

-F^{\prime}(X)=\rho(X)e^{-\Rho(X)}\int_{0}^{X}e^{\Rho(W)}\left(rG(W)+G^{\prime}(W)\right)\ dW-\big(rG(X)+G^{\prime}(X)\big).

We recall the definition of
\displaystyle G
from (
C.1.3
) to put

(C.1.11)

(\mathcal{S}{g})(X):=\rho(X)e^{-\Rho(X)}\int_{0}^{X}e^{\Rho(W)}\left(r\int_{W}^{\infty}g(V)\ dV-g(W)\right)\ dW-\left(r\int_{X}^{\infty}g(W)\ dW-g(X)\right).

Repackaging our work above, if we know that
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}{g}\in H_{q}^{1}
when
\displaystyle g\in H_{q}^{1}
, then we will have
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{S}{g})=g
.
This is indeed the case, and in the next section we will prove the following formal encapsulation of this result.

Proposition C.1.1
.

For
\displaystyle g\in H_{q}^{1}
, define
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}{g}
by (
C.1.11
).
Then
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\in\mathbf{B}(H_{q}^{1})
.

C.2.
The proof of Proposition
C.1.1

For
\displaystyle g\in H_{q}^{1}
put

(C.2.1)

(\mathcal{H}{g})(X):=r\int_{X}^{\infty}g(W)\ dW-g(X),

so that from (
C.1.11
) we have

(C.2.2)

(\mathcal{S}{g})(X)=\rho(X)e^{-\Rho(X)}\int_{0}^{X}e^{\Rho(W)}(\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW-(\mathcal{H}{g})(X)

and

(C.2.3)

(\mathcal{S}{g})^{\prime}(X)=\rho^{\prime}(X)e^{-\Rho(X)}\int_{0}^{X}e^{\Rho(W)}(\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW-\rho(X)(\mathcal{S}{g})(X)-(\mathcal{H}{g})^{\prime}(X).

We will show that
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}{g}
,
\displaystyle(\mathcal{S}{g})^{\prime}\in L_{q}^{2}
and that there exists
\displaystyle C>0
such that for any
\displaystyle g\in H_{q}^{1}
we have

\|\mathcal{S}{g}\|_{L_{q}^{2}}+\|(\mathcal{S}{g})^{\prime}\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}.

From this it will follow that
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
is a bounded operator on
\displaystyle H_{q}^{1}
.

Before proceeding, we record some convenient properties of
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle\Rho
that follow from their formulas in (
C.1.8
) and (
C.1.9
).

Lemma C.2.1
.

There exist
\displaystyle A
,
\displaystyle B
,
\displaystyle C_{\rho}>0
such that the following hold.

(i)

\displaystyle|\rho(X)-r|\leq C_{\rho}e^{-rX}
for
\displaystyle X>0
.

(ii)

\displaystyle|\rho(X)-(-2r)|\leq C_{\rho}e^{rX}
for
\displaystyle X<0
.

(iii)

\displaystyle|\rho^{\prime}(X)|\leq C_{\rho}e^{-r|X|}
.

(iv)

\displaystyle e^{-\Rho(X)}=\frac{e^{-rX}}{(Ae^{-2rX}+B)^{3/2}}=\frac{e^{2rX}}{(A+Be^{2rX})^{3/2}}
.

(v)

\displaystyle e^{\Rho(X)}=e^{rX}(Ae^{-2rX}+B)^{3/2}=e^{-2rX}(A+Be^{2rX})^{3/2}
.

Now we estimate with gusto.

C.2.1.
Estimates on
\displaystyle(\mathcal{S}{g})^{\prime}

The second and third terms of
\displaystyle(\mathcal{S}{g})^{\prime}
from (
C.2.3
) are easy to control if we have bounds on
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}{g}
.
We assume an estimate of the form
\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}{g}\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}
with
\displaystyle C
independent of
\displaystyle g
; we prove this in Section
C.2.2
below.
Since
\displaystyle\rho\in L^{\infty}
, we obtain at once

\|\rho(\mathcal{S}{g})\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\leq C\|\mathcal{S}{g}\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}.

Next, since
\displaystyle(\mathcal{H}{g})^{\prime}=-rg-g^{\prime}
by (
C.2.1
), we have

\|(\mathcal{H}{g})^{\prime}\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\leq C\|g\|_{L_{q}^{2}}+C\|g^{\prime}\|_{L_{q}^{2}}=C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}.

Estimating the first term in (
C.2.3
),

(\mathcal{I}{g})(X):=\rho^{\prime}(X)e^{-\Rho(X)}\int_{0}^{X}e^{\Rho(W)}(\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW,

requires slightly more work.
We first return to (
C.2.1
) to bound

(C.2.4)

\|\mathcal{H}{g}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|g\|_{L_{q}^{1}}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}

by the embedding of
\displaystyle L_{q}^{2}
into
\displaystyle L^{1}
, the Sobolev embedding of
\displaystyle H^{1}
into
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
, and the embedding of
\displaystyle H_{q}^{1}
into
\displaystyle H^{1}
.
Thus for any
\displaystyle X>0
we have

|(\mathcal{I}{g})(X)|\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}e^{-r|X|}e^{-\Rho(X)}\int_{0}^{X}e^{\Rho(W)}\ dW,

where we have used part
(iii)
of Lemma
C.2.1
to estimate
\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}
.
Our estimates on
\displaystyle e^{\pm\Rho}
are slightly different depending on whether
\displaystyle X
is positive or negative.

First suppose
\displaystyle X>0
.
Then part
(iv)
of Lemma
C.2.1
shows
\displaystyle e^{-\Rho(X)}\leq Ce^{-rX}
and
\displaystyle e^{\Rho(W)}\leq Ce^{rW}
for
\displaystyle 0\leq W\leq X
.
Hence

|(\mathcal{I}{g})(X)|\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}e^{-2rX}\int_{0}^{X}e^{rW}\ dW\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}e^{-rX},

and so

|e^{qX}(\mathcal{I}{g})(X)|\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}e^{(q-r)X},\ X>0.

The analysis when
\displaystyle X>0
is entirely similar, except we use the estimates
\displaystyle e^{-\Rho(X)}\leq e^{2rX}
and
\displaystyle e^{\Rho(W)}\leq e^{-2rW}
for
\displaystyle X\leq W\leq 0
.
We conclude

|e^{q|X|}(\mathcal{I}{g})(X)|\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}e^{-(r-q)|X|},

which implies
\displaystyle\|\mathcal{I}{g}\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}
.

C.2.2.
Estimates on
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}{g}

First suppose
\displaystyle X>0
and, using the definition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
in (
C.2.2
) and the formulas for
\displaystyle e^{\pm\Rho}
from parts
(iv)
and
(v)
of Lemma
C.2.1
, write

(\mathcal{S}{g})(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{4}(\mathcal{S}_{k}^{+}g)(X),

where

(\mathcal{S}_{1}^{+}g)(X):=(\mathcal{H}{g})(X)-re^{-rX}\int_{0}^{X}e^{rW}(\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW,

(\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}g)(X):=\frac{(r-\rho(X))e^{-rX}}{(Ae^{-2rX}+B)^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{X}e^{rW}(Ae^{-2rW}+B)^{3/2}(\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW,

(\mathcal{S}_{3}^{+}g)(X):=re^{-rX}\left(\frac{1}{(Ae^{-2rX}+B)^{3/2}}-\frac{1}{B^{3/2}}\right)\int_{0}^{X}e^{rW}(Ae^{-2rW}+B)^{3/2}(\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW,

and

(\mathcal{S}_{4}^{+}g)(X):=\frac{re^{-rX}}{B^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{X}e^{rW}\big[(Ae^{-2rW}+B)^{3/2}-B^{3/2}\big](\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW.

We claim

(C.2.5)

\sum_{k=2}^{4}|(\mathcal{S}_{k}^{+}g)(X)|\leq Ce^{-rX}\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}},

from which it follows that

(C.2.6)

\sum_{k=2}^{4}\|\mathcal{S}_{k}^{+}g\|_{L_{q}^{2}((0,\infty))}\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}.

We achieve (
C.2.5
) using the
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
-estimate (
C.2.4
) on
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}{g}
, the estimate on
\displaystyle|\rho(X)-r|
from part
(i)
of Lemma
C.2.1
, and (local) Lipschitz estimates on the two differences in
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{3}^{+}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{4}^{+}
.

To control
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}^{+}
we integrate by parts:

\int_{0}^{X}e^{rW}(\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW=\frac{e^{rX}(\mathcal{H}{g})(X)-(\mathcal{H}{g})(0)}{r}-\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{X}e^{rW}(\mathcal{H}{g})^{\prime}(W)\ dW.

By the definition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}
in (
C.2.1
) we have

(C.2.7)

\int_{0}^{X}e^{rW}(\mathcal{H}{g})^{\prime}(W)\ dW=-\int_{0}^{X}e^{rW}(rg(W)+g^{\prime}(W))\ dW=-\int_{0}^{X}\partial_{W}[e^{rW}g(W)]\ dW\\
=g(0)-e^{rX}g(X).

We conclude

(\mathcal{S}_{1}^{+}g)(X)=e^{-rX}(\mathcal{H}{g})(0)+e^{-rX}g(0)-g(X).

Use the
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
-estimate (
C.2.4
) on
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}{g}
and the Sobolev embedding on
\displaystyle g(0)
to conclude

(C.2.8)

|(\mathcal{S}_{1}^{+}g)(X)|\leq Ce^{-rX}\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}},

and so

(C.2.9)

\|\mathcal{S}_{1}^{+}g\|_{L_{q}^{2}((0,\infty))}\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}.

Our analysis for
\displaystyle X<0
starts out similarly.
Rewrite

(\mathcal{S}{g})(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{4}(\mathcal{S}_{k}^{-}g)(X),

where now

(\mathcal{S}_{1}^{-}g)(X):=(\mathcal{H}{g})(X)-2re^{2rX}\int_{X}^{0}e^{-2rW}(\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW,

(\mathcal{S}_{2}^{-}g)(X):=(\rho(X)-(-2r))\frac{e^{2rX}}{(A+Be^{2rX})^{3/2}}\int_{X}^{0}e^{-2rW}(A+Be^{2rW})^{3/2}(\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW,

(\mathcal{S}_{3}^{-}g)(X):=2r\left(\frac{1}{A^{3/2}}-\frac{1}{(A+Be^{2rX})^{3/2}}\right)e^{2rX}\int_{X}^{0}e^{-2rW}(A+Be^{2rW})^{3/2}(\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW,

and

(\mathcal{S}_{4}^{+}g)(X):=\frac{2r}{A^{3/2}}e^{2rX}\int_{X}^{0}e^{-2rW}[(A+Be^{2rW})^{3/2}-A^{3/2}](\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW.

As before, we obtain

(C.2.10)

\sum_{k=2}^{4}|e^{-qX}(\mathcal{S}_{k}^{-}g)(X)|\leq Ce^{(r-q)X}\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}},

and so

(C.2.11)

\sum_{k=2}^{4}\|\mathcal{S}_{k}^{-}g\|_{L_{q}^{2}((-\infty,0))}\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}.

We integrate by parts within
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}^{-}g
to find

\int_{X}^{0}e^{-2rW}(\mathcal{H}{g})(W)\ dW=\frac{e^{-2rX}(\mathcal{H}{g})(X)-(\mathcal{H}{g})(0)}{2r}-\frac{1}{2r}\int_{X}^{0}e^{-2rW}(rg(W)+g^{\prime}(W))\ dW.

The difference compared to (
C.2.7
) in our treatment of
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}^{+}g
is that we no longer have a perfect derivative as the integrand on the right; this is an artifact of the different asymptotic behavior of
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle e^{\Rho}
at
\displaystyle-\infty
compared to
\displaystyle+\infty
, as specified in Lemma
C.2.1
.
And so, at first glance, the best that we have is

(\mathcal{S}_{1}^{-}g)(X)=(\mathcal{H}{g})(0)e^{2rX}+re^{2rX}\int_{X}^{0}e^{-2rW}g(W)\ dW+e^{2rX}\int_{X}^{0}e^{-2rW}g^{\prime}(W)\ dW.

It suffices to show, of course, that each of the three terms above is a function in
\displaystyle L_{q}^{2}((-\infty,0))
with norm bounded by a constant multiple of
\displaystyle\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}
.
This is easy for the first term, since we can use the familiar
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
-estimate (
C.2.4
) on
\displaystyle(\mathcal{H}{g})(0)
.
For the integral terms, we want an estimate of the form

\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-2qX}\left|e^{2rX}\int_{X}^{0}e^{-2rW}g(W)\ dW\right|^{2}\ dX\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}

and similarly for
\displaystyle g^{\prime}
.
To obtain these estimates, we use the following lemma, whose proof we defer to Section
C.2.3
.

Lemma C.2.2
.

There exists
\displaystyle C>0
such that

(C.2.12)

\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{2X}\left|\int_{X}^{0}e^{-W}h(W)\ dW\right|^{2}\ dX\leq C\|h\|_{L^{2}}

for all
\displaystyle h\in L^{2}
.

We work out the estimate just for the integral term involving
\displaystyle g
.
Since
\displaystyle g\in L_{q}^{2}
, we can write
\displaystyle g(X)=e^{2q|X|}\widetilde{g}(X)
for some
\displaystyle\widetilde{g}\in L^{2}
.
Then, changing variables, we find

\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-2qX}\left|e^{2rX}\int_{X}^{0}e^{-2rW}g(W)\ dW\right|^{2}\ dX=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{2(2r-q)X}\left|\int_{X}^{0}e^{-(2r-q)W}\widetilde{g}(W)\ dW\right|^{2}\ dX\\
=\frac{1}{(2r-q)^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{2U}\left|\int_{U}^{0}e^{-V}g\left(\frac{V}{2r-q}\right)\ dV\right|^{2}\ dU.

Applying Lemma
C.2.2
, we obtain

\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-2qX}\left|e^{2rX}\int_{X}^{0}e^{-2rW}g(W)\ dW\right|^{2}\ dX\leq C\left\|\widetilde{g}\left(\frac{\cdot}{2r-q}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|e^{-q|\cdot|}(e^{q|\cdot|}\widetilde{g})\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}.}

After an identical analysis with
\displaystyle g^{\prime}
, we find

(C.2.13)

\|\mathcal{S}_{1}^{-}g\|_{L_{q}^{2}((-\infty,0))}\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}}.

Combine (
C.2.6
), (
C.2.9
), (
C.2.11
), and (
C.2.13
) to conclude

\|\mathcal{S}{g}\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\leq C\|g\|_{H_{q}^{1}},

as desired.

C.2.3.
The proof of Lemma
C.2.2

Put

\mathcal{W}:=\!\left\{(X,W,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ \middle|\ -\infty<X\leq 0,\ X\leq W\leq 0,\ X\leq Y\leq 0\right\},

so that, after using the triangle inequality, the integral in (
C.2.12
) is bounded by

\mathcal{I}:=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{2X}\left(\int_{X}^{0}e^{-W}|h(W)|\ dW\right)^{2}\ dX=\iiint_{\mathcal{W}}e^{2X}e^{-W}e^{-Y}|h(W)h(Y)|\ dY\ dW\ dX.

Next, put

\mathcal{W}_{1}:=\!\left\{(X,W,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ \middle|\ -\infty<X\leq W,\ W\leq Y\leq 0,\ -\infty<W\leq 0\right\}

and

\mathcal{W}_{2}:=\!\left\{(X,W,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ \middle|\ -\infty<X\leq Y,\ Y\leq W\leq 0,\ -\infty<Y\leq 0\right\},

so
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}_{1}\cup\mathcal{W}_{2}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\cap\mathcal{W}_{2}
has measure zero.
Then

\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{1}+\mathcal{I}_{2},

where

\mathcal{I}_{1}:=\iiint_{\mathcal{W}_{1}}e^{2X}e^{-W}e^{-Y}|h(W)h(Y)|\ dY\ dW\ dX

and

\mathcal{I}_{2}:=\iiint_{\mathcal{W}_{2}}e^{2X}e^{-W}e^{-Y}|h(W)h(Y)|\ dY\ dW\ dX.

Since the integrands are symmetric in
\displaystyle W
and
\displaystyle Y
, it suffices to show

\mathcal{I}_{1}\leq C\int_{-\infty}^{0}|h(X)|^{2}\ dX.

Change variables to obtain

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{W}^{0}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{W}e^{2X}\ dX\right)e^{-W}e^{-Y}|h(W)h(Y)|\ dY\ dW=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{W}^{0}e^{W}e^{-Y}|h(W)h(Y)|\ dY\ dW.

Now we estimate

(C.2.14)

4|\mathcal{I}_{1}|\leq\mathcal{I}_{12}+\mathcal{I}_{13},

where

\mathcal{I}_{12}:=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{W}^{0}e^{W}e^{-Y}|h(W)|^{2}\ dY\ dW\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{I}_{13}:=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{W}^{0}e^{W}e^{-Y}|h(Y)|^{2}\ dY\ dW.

We first evaluate

\mathcal{I}_{12}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left(\int_{W}^{0}e^{-Y}\ dY\right)e^{W}|h(W)|^{2}\ dW=\int_{-\infty}^{0}(1-e^{W})|h(W)|^{2}\ dW.

Since
\displaystyle W\leq 0
we have
\displaystyle|1-e^{W}|\leq 2
, and so

(C.2.15)

\mathcal{I}_{12}\leq 2\int_{-\infty}^{0}|h(W)|^{2}\ dW\leq C\|h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Next, we change variables in
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{13}
to find

(C.2.16)

\mathcal{I}_{13}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{Y}e^{W}\ dW\right)e^{-Y}|h(Y)|^{2}\ dY=\int_{-\infty}^{0}|h(Y)|^{2}\ dY\leq\|h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Combining the decomposition (
C.2.14
) and the estimates (
C.2.15
) and (
C.2.16
) gives

|\mathcal{I}_{1}|\leq C\|h\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

as desired.

As before, we stop when we have bounds in terms of

(i)
and
(ii)

I\!I\leq\|\mathcal{R}_{1}^{\nu}(\grave{\eta}_{1})-\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\grave{\eta}_{1})\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\eta_{1}-\grave{\eta}_{1}\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\leq C\nu^{1/2}\|\grave{\eta}_{1}\|_{H_{q}^{1}}\|\eta_{1}-\grave{\eta}_{1}\|_{H_{q}^{1}}

D.2.3.
Lipschitz estimates on
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{13}^{\nu}

We use again the smoothing property of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(0)}
to bound

\|\mathcal{V}_{13}^{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\eta})-\mathcal{V}_{13}^{\nu}(\grave{\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{H_{q}^{1}}\leq C\|\big(\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma+\eta_{1})-\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma)-D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma)\eta_{1}\big)\sigma\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\\
\leq C\|\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma+\eta_{1})-\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma)-D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma)\eta_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\sigma\|_{L_{q}^{2}}.

Next we will use the following ‘difference of squares’ estimate, which is proved using the fundamental theorem of calculus.
We thank J. Douglas Wright for pointing out this lemma to us.

Lemma D.2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
be Banach spaces with
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
open and convex and with
\displaystyle 0\in\mathcal{Z}
.
Let
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Y})
with
\displaystyle Df(0)=0
, and suppose

\Lip_{\mathcal{Z}}(Df):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\grave{x}\in\mathcal{Z}\\
x\neq\grave{x}\end{subarray}}\frac{\|Df(x)-Df(\grave{x})\|_{\mathbf{B}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}}{\|x-\grave{x}\|_{\mathcal{X}}}<\infty.

Then

\|f(x)-f(\grave{x})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\Lip_{\mathcal{Z}}(Df)\big(\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}}+\|\grave{x}\|_{\mathcal{X}}\big)\|x-\grave{x}\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

We apply this lemma to
\displaystyle f(\eta_{1}):=\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma)\eta_{1})-\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma)-D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma)\eta_{1}
, which is infinitely differentiable as a map from
\displaystyle H_{q}^{1}
to
\displaystyle W^{1,\infty}
by Remark
3.3.1
, to conclude

\|\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma+\eta_{1})-\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma)-D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma)\eta_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\big(\|\eta_{1}\|_{H_{q}^{1}}+\|\grave{\eta}_{1}\|_{H_{q}^{1}}\big)\|\eta_{1}-\grave{\eta}_{1}\|_{H_{q}^{1}}.

D.2.4.
Lipschitz estimates on
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{14}^{\nu}

We smooth with
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(0)}
once more, and then we use the fundamental theorem of calculus and the smoothness of
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}
to rewrite

\|\mathcal{V}_{14}^{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\eta})-\mathcal{V}_{14}^{\nu}(\grave{\boldsymbol{\eta}})\|_{H_{q}^{1}}\leq C\|I\|_{L_{q}^{2}}+C\|I\!I\|_{L_{q}^{2}},

where

I:=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\big(D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma+s\eta_{1})-D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma+s\grave{\eta}_{1})\big)\ ds\right)\eta_{1}^{2}

and

I\!I:=\left(\int_{0}^{1}D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma+s\grave{\eta}_{1})\ ds\right)(\eta_{1}+\grave{\eta}_{1})(\eta_{1}-\grave{\eta}_{1}).

Then

\|I\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\leq\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\big(D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma+s\eta_{1})-D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma+s\grave{\eta}_{1})\big)\ ds\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\eta_{1}\|_{L_{q}^{2}}

and

\|I\!I\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\leq\left\|\int_{0}^{1}D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}(\sigma+s\grave{\eta}_{1})\ ds\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\eta_{1}+\grave{\eta}_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\eta_{1}-\grave{\eta}_{1}\|_{L_{q}^{2}}.

We conclude

\|I\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\leq C\|\eta_{1}\|_{H_{q}^{1}}\|\eta_{1}-\grave{\eta}_{1}\|_{H_{q}^{1}}

via a Lipschitz estimate on
\displaystyle D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}
and

\|I\!I\|_{L_{q}^{2}}\leq C\big(\|\eta_{1}\|_{H_{q}^{1}}+\|\grave{\eta}_{1}\|_{H_{q}^{1}}\big)\|\eta_{1}-\grave{\eta}_{1}\|_{H_{q}^{1}}

via the boundedness of
\displaystyle D\mathcal{R}_{1}^{0}
.
