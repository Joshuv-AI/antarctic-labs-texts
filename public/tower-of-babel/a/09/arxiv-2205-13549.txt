Cosmological constraints on decaying axion-like particles: a global analysis
and Patrick Stöcker

Abstract
Axion-like particles (ALPs) decaying into photons are known to affect a wide range of astrophysical and cosmological observables. In this study we focus on ALPs with masses in the keV–MeV range and lifetimes between 10 4 10^{4} and 10 13 10^{13} seconds, corresponding to decays between the end of Big Bang Nucleosynthesis and the formation of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB). Using the CosmoBit module of the global fitting framework GAMBIT , we combine state-of-the-art calculations of the irreducible ALP freeze-in abundance, primordial element abundances (including photodisintegration through ALP decays), CMB spectral distortions and anisotropies, and constraints from supernovae and stellar cooling. This approach makes it possible for the first time to perform a global analysis of the ALP parameter space while varying the parameters of Λ \Lambda CDM as well as several nuisance parameters. We find a lower bound on the ALP mass of around m a > 300 ​ keV m_{a}>300\,\text{keV} , which can only be evaded if ALPs are stable on cosmological timescales. Future observations of CMB spectral distortions with a PIXIE-like mission are expected to improve this bound by two orders of magnitude.

Introduction

Cosmological observables such as the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) have now been measured with such precision that they not only constrain the properties of particles in the primordial thermal plasma, but also even probe out-of-equilibrium species with sub-dominant cosmological abundances. For example, unstable particles that decay around the time of recombination leave an observable imprint on the CMB temperature anisotropies, even if they change the energy density of the electron-photon plasma only at the level of one part per billion
[
Diamanti:2013bia
,
Slatyer:2016qyl
,
Poulin:2016anj
]
. Earlier decays are in turn tightly constrained by observations of CMB spectral distortions
[
1993_Hu
,
1910.04619
]
and the primordial element abundances produced in Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN)
[
Poulin:2015opa
,
Hufnagel:2018bjp
,
2006.14803
,
Depta:2020zbh
]
. This raises the exciting possibility of using cosmology to search for the effects of decaying exotic particles across many different cosmological eras, ranging from one second to billions of years
[
Chen:2003gz
]
.

A particularly well-motivated class of exotic particles that may be probed in this way are axion-like particles (ALPs)
[
Kim:1986ax
,
1002.0329
]
, which generically arise in theories with spontaneously broken global chiral symmetries and theories with compactified extra dimensions
[
Cicoli:2012sz
]
. In analogy with the QCD axion proposed to solve the strong CP problem
[
1978_weinberg_axion
,
1978_wilczek_axion
]
, ALPs would have an approximate shift symmetry that explains both the smallness of the ALP mass and the weakness of its couplings in terms of the large scale of symmetry breaking. Although ALPs may in general have a wide range of different couplings
[
Brivio:2017ije
,
Bauer:2020jbp
]
, the most phenomenologically interesting one is the coupling to photons. Indeed, there is a vast literature on how to constrain ALP-photon couplings using cosmological
[
hep-ph/9503293
,
hep-ph/9702275
,
1110.2895
,
1501.04097
]
, astrophysical
[
1201.5902
,
1302.1208
,
1512.08108
]
and laboratory data
[
Bauer:2017ris
,
Dolan:2017osp
]
. For more details, we refer the interested reader to reviews on QCD axion and ALP cosmology
[
1510.07633
]
and experimental search strategies
[
1801.08127
]
, as well as a continuously updated online repository for limits and sensitivity forecasts
[
zenodo_axionlimits
]
.

Most studies of cosmological ALP constraints make one of two assumptions on the ALP abundance. Either ALPs are assumed to be produced by the realignment mechanism
[
Preskill:1982cy
,
Abbott:1982af
,
Dine:1982ah
,
Turner:1983he
,
Turner:1985si
,
1201.5902
]
in such a way that their present-day abundance is consistent with the observed abundance of dark matter (DM) in the universe, or they are assumed to be in thermal equilibrium with the bath of Standard Model (SM) particles at some high temperature but then decay, depleting their abundance. Both of these scenarios turn out to be very tightly constrained, such that ALPs are often believed to be excluded unless they are either very light (with a mass at or below the keV-scale) or very heavy (with a mass at or above the GeV-scale)
[
1110.2895
]
. It has only recently been pointed out that a low reheating temperature can open up otherwise ruled-out parameter space for intermediate-mass ALPs, if realignment production is inefficient and their couplings are small enough to avoid attaining thermal equilibrium
[
2002.08370
]
. In this case the dominant contribution to ALP production arises from the freeze-in mechanism at temperatures close to the reheating temperature, and the resulting abundance may be small enough to evade cosmological constraints arising from their subsequent decays
[
Bolz:2000fu
,
1110.2895
]
.

In the present work we perform a detailed analysis of ALPs in the keV–GeV mass range with tiny couplings to SM photons, such that they never enter into thermal equilibrium in the early universe. While these ALPs may in principle be sufficiently long-lived to survive until the late universe, we focus on ALPs that decay between BBN and recombination. In this case the ALP decays affect a wide range of observables, such as

•

the primordial element abundances through photodisintegration of the nuclei formed during BBN,

•

the black-body spectrum of the CMB through the injection of electromagnetic energy into the plasma,

•

the CMB anisotropies through modifications of the recombination history,

•

the ratio of the photon and neutrino temperatures (encoded in the relativistic number of effective degrees of freedom) through a relative increase of the former,

•

auxiliary astrophysical observables, such as axion emission and decays from supernova SN1987A or stellar cooling.

[

]
, investigated the individual likelihoods instead. Compared to Ref.

[

and not all possible types of spectral distortions. In addition, our analysis improves upon the results of Ref.

Production and decay of axion-like particles coupled to photons

In this work we will focus on ALPs
a
with an effective coupling to photons given by

\mathcal{L}=\frac{g_{a\gamma}}{4}aF^{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}_{\mu\nu}\,,

(2.1)

where
F^{\mu\nu}
is the electromagnetic (EM) field strength tensor,
\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}=\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}
its dual, and the ALP-photon coupling
g_{a\gamma}
has mass dimension
-1

The ALP lifetime.

Axion-like particles are unstable and decay into two photons with a lifetime of
\tau_{a}=\Gamma_{a}^{-1}
, where

\Gamma_{a}=\frac{g_{a\gamma}^{2}\,m_{a}^{3}}{64\pi}=\frac{1}{$1.32\text{\times}{10}^{8}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$}\left(\frac{g_{a\gamma}}{$1\text{\times}{10}^{-12}\text{\,}\mathrm{GeV}$}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{m_{a}}{$10\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}$}\right)^{3}\,.

(2.2)

We can broadly distinguish three different lifetime regimes:

1.

ALPs decay before neutrino decoupling (
\tau_{a}\lesssim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
). In this case the decay photons will fully thermalise and not affect any cosmological observables.

2.

ALPs decay between neutrino decoupling and the present day (
$1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$\lesssim\tau_{a}\lesssim${10}^{17}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
). In this case ALPs may significantly affect various cosmological observables.

3.

ALPs survive until the present day (
\tau_{a}\gtrsim${10}^{17}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
). In this case ALPs contribute to the DM abundance; constraints in the mass range of interest arise from searches for mono-energetic X-ray or
\gamma
-ray photons.

The second case is particularly interesting because non-trivial constraints on the ALP parameter space can be expected. Indeed, the previous studies mentioned in
section

1
showed that there are very tight constraints on EM decays of DM particles during or after recombination, or during BBN.
Less attention has been given to the case of ALPs decaying between BBN and recombination, i.e.
${10}^{4}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$<\tau_{a}<${10}^{13}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
, which will be the focus of this work.

3.1.1
Modifications of

\eta_{b}

In the absence of any beyond-the-SM physics, the only particle species that are still relativistic after BBN are photons and neutrinos. Hence the radiation energy density is given by

\rho_{\rm rad}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})=\frac{\pi^{2}}{15}\left(T_{\gamma}^{4}+3\times\frac{7}{8}T_{\nu}^{4}\right)\,,

(3.1)

where the first term accounts for the contribution from photons and the second term accounts for the contribution from neutrinos.
As the two species are thermally decoupled, their respective temperatures
T_{\gamma}
and
T_{\nu}

However, because all SM processes that could modify either of the two heat baths are inefficient after neutrino decoupling, the respective temperatures are only subject to redshifting. Their ratio is therefore fixed at neutrino decoupling. In the instantaneous-decoupling approximation, the ratio’s value can be derived by assuming conservation of entropy in both heat baths separately, such that

(3.3)

For ALPs with a lifetime

Including ALP decays, the evolution of the photon temperature is given by

The first term of

3.2.1
Likelihood implementation and uncertainties

CosmoBit
uses
AlterBBN

[
Arbey:2011nf
,
Arbey:2018zfh
]
to compute an initial prediction of the primordial abundances of the light elements based on a given set of cosmological parameters. It then passes these abundances to
ACROPOLIS

[
Depta:2020mhj
]
. The latter includes the effects of photodisintegration by computing the photon spectrum in the post-BBN thermal bath and solving the Boltzmann equation for each of the primordial element abundances. Details of the
GAMBIT
interface to
ACROPOLIS
can be found in
section

A.3
.

Both
AlterBBN
and our interface to
ACROPOLIS
compute ratios of abundances to the hydrogen abundance
[{}{}\mathrm{Y}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{H}]
, from which we calculate
[\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He}/{}\mathrm{D}]=[\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He}/{}\mathrm{H}]/[{}\mathrm{D}/{}\mathrm{H}]
in order to match the measured value of
[\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He}/{}\mathrm{D}]
in
eq.

3.10
.
CosmoBit
then computes a multi-dimensional Gaussian likelihood for all abundances, taking into account correlated uncertainties (see Ref.
[
CosmoBit
]
for details):

-2\ln\mathcal{L}=f(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{O})\,\mathcal{C}^{-1}\,f(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{O})^{T}+(2\pi)^{n}\det(\mathcal{C}),

(3.11)

where
\mathcal{P}
is the vector of predicted abundances,
\mathcal{O}
the vector of observed abundances, and
\mathcal{C}
the covariance matrix. The function
f(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{O})
is introduced to differentiate elements for which the measurement is only an upper limit, such as
\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He}
. Therefore,
f(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{O})_{i}=\mathcal{P}_{i}-\mathcal{O}_{i}
for all elements except for
\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He}
, for which
f(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{O})_{\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He}}=\text{max}(\mathcal{P}_{\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He}}-\mathcal{O}_{\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He}},0)
.
This way, the likelihood becomes flat when the predicted
[\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He}/{D}]
is smaller than the observed value, in line with our desire to implement the
[\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He}/{D}]
measurement as an upper bound only.

We construct the covariance matrix
\mathcal{C}
by computing the uncertainties for each of the abundances independently, and then combining them with correlation information. We estimate the uncertainties on each abundance for each parameter point by taking the larger of the distances between the mean and the high/low operational modes of
AlterBBN
, i.e.
\sigma_{Y_{N}}=\max(Y_{N}^{\text{high}}-Y_{N}^{\text{mean}},Y_{N}^{\text{mean}}-Y_{N}^{\text{low}})
. A detailed computation of the correlations is possible in
AlterBBN
, but is computationally too expensive for our purposes. Fortunately, the production of primordial elements in our model proceeds as in
\Lambda
CDM. The correlations thus do not change significantly when varying the
\Lambda
CDM parameters within their typical ranges. We therefore employ a constant correlation matrix through our analysis that matches the one computed by
AlterBBN
with default settings. We then propagate the covariance matrix when computing the corrections after photodisintegration with
ACROPOLIS
, using the transfer matrix provided (see
section

A.3
for details). Finally, in
eq.

3.11
we use a corrected covariance matrix, which matches the change to the observed ratio of abundances for
[\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He}/{}\mathrm{D}]
.

Besides their obvious use as predictions to compare with observations, the primordial abundances are also relevant for cosmological probes after BBN. In particular the primordial helium abundance
Y_{\text{p}}
affects the physics of recombination and thus is an input to the computation of the CMB power spectrum with
CLASS
(see
section

3.1
). As recombination happens at temperatures much lower than the energy threshold for the photodisintegration reactions for
Y_{\text{p}}
, we make sure to include these effects on the predicted value of
Y_{\text{p}}
that we use for computing the spectrum of the CMB.

In addition to the BBN likelihood, we also include a nuisance likelihood for the neutron lifetime
\tau_{n}
, as the reaction rates that produce the primordial elements are strongly affected by uncertainties in
\tau_{n}
. In this analysis we use the laboratory measurement of
\tau_{n}
from ultra-cold neutron (“bottle”) experiments,
\tau_{n,\text{bottle}}=$879.4\pm 6\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$

\tau_{n}
on constraints from BBN, the CMB and other measurements were studied in Ref.
[

The
\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{7}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{Li}

The BBN predictions for the abundance of
\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{7}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{Li}

\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{7}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{Li}

Nevertheless, it is still interesting to consider physics beyond the SM as a possible solution of the
\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{7}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{Li}
problem. For example, Ref.
[

]
suggests that the tension between predictions and observations can be resolved via ALP decays. The idea in this work is to consider ALPs in the mass range
$3.17\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}$<m_{a}<$4.44\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}$
such that the photons produced in their decay have enough energy for the photodisintegration of
\hphantom{{}^{\text{7}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{7}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{Be}

3.3
Spectral distortions

While the CMB radiation closely follows a black-body spectrum with characteristic temperature
T_{\text{\tiny CMB}}\approx$2.725\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$

[
Fixsen1996
,
Fixsen2009
]
, spectral distortions (SDs) exist due to interactions taking place during the
entire
thermal history of the universe
[
Zeldovich:1969ff
,
Sunyaev:1970er
,
1972_Zeldovich
,
1975_Illarionov
,
1982_Danese
,
1991_Burigana
,
1993_Hu
,
1109.6552
]
. As the relevance of the underlying physical processes that induce SDs changes over time, their redshift dependence has to be taken into account. This means that SDs are – at least in theory – sensitive to any known or new physics that alters the energy budget of the expanding universe, thus making SDs a useful, complementary probe of e.g. inflation, dark matter, or new particles (see Refs.
[
1903.04218
,
1910.04619
]
for recent reviews).

Spectral distortions affect the photon spectrum
f(x,z)
, where
x=\omega/T_{\text{\tiny CMB}}
is the dimensionless frequency as observed today and
z
is the redshift. This can be expressed as a deviation from the perfect black-body shape, i.e.

f(x,z)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{x}+1}+\Delta f(x,z)\,.

(3.12)

Historically, SDs have been classified into different types, such as
y
-type distortions (from the Kompaneets or Sunyaev–Zeldovich parameter
y
) or chemical potential-type
\mu
distortions.
However, it is sufficient to specify the heating rates of the primordial plasma as a function of redshift, and integrate their spectral intensity
\Delta I\equiv(xT_{\text{\tiny CMB}})^{3}\Delta f(x,z)/2\pi^{2}
to compare to observations today.
Given the frequency-integrated heating rate
\dot{Q}
from the Boltzmann equation, we have (see Ref.
[
1910.04619
]
)

\Delta I\propto\frac{\Delta\rho_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}z\;\frac{\dot{Q}}{(1+z)H\rho_{\gamma}}\,.

(3.13)

We pass the default options to

The

[

After specifying a frequency binning for each experiment, the likelihood for the observed distortions of photon spectral intensities

\ln\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left(\frac{\Delta I_{\text{obs}}(\omega_{i})-\Delta I_{\text{pred}}(\omega_{i})}{\delta I(\omega_{i})}\right)^{2}\,,

(3.16)

where the
\Delta I_{\text{pred}}(\omega_{i})
are the theory predictions by
CLASS
and the
\delta I(\omega_{i})
are the expected sensitivities, which are the sum in quadrature of the experimental noise level and theory uncertainties.
This includes foreground maps, which were accounted for in Ref.
[
2010.07814
]
and largely based on Ref.
[
Abitbol:2017vwa
]

One of the strengths of implementing the likelihood with the computational framework described above is that we avoid the complications of using
eq.

3.15
for
\mu
and
y

[
1011.3694
,
1110.2895
]

3.5
Stellar evolution and cluster counts

Due to their weak interactions with matter, ALPs can carry away energy from stellar interiors, affecting the evolution of e.g. Horizontal Branch (HB) stars by reducing their lifetimes
[
Sato:1975vy
,
Raffelt:1990yz
,
book_raffelt_laboratories
]
. This allows one to place a limit on
g_{a\gamma}
by comparing the number of HB stars
N_{\text{HB}}
to the number of stars on the Red Giant Branch (RGB)
N_{\text{RGB}}
in globular clusters (GCs). The associated “
R
parameter” observable
R_{\text{GC}}\equiv N_{\text{HB}}/N_{\text{RGB}}

[
1983A&A...128...94B
]
, is directly connected to the lifetimes and energy loss rates of these systems.

The theoretically expected
R
parameter can be estimated from stellar evolution simulations that include the energy loss from ALPs
[
1512.08108
,
1983A&A...128...94B
,
Raffelt:1989xu
,
1406.6053
]
,

R_{\text{GC}}\approx 0.022-0.443\,(1+0.965\,|g_{a\gamma}|)^{1/2}+7.331\,Y_{\text{GC}}\,,

(3.19)

where
Y_{\text{GC}}=0.2515(17)

[
1503.08146
]
is an estimate for the Helium abundance in GCs. The observed value of the
R
parameter,
R_{\text{GC,obs}}=$1.39\pm 3$

[
1406.6053
]
, is consistent with the axion-free value from (
3.19
), which is
R_{\text{GC}}=$1.42\pm 1$
.

Equation

3.19
is only valid for ALP masses much smaller than the typical core temperature of an HB star,
m_{a}\ll T_{\text{HB}}\sim$10\text{\,}\mathrm{keV}$
. For greater ALP masses, the Primakoff interaction cross section, which leads to the energy loss in HB stars, should be multiplied by a correction factor
F(m_{a})
. Using Eq. (A.5) of Ref.
[
1110.2895
]
, we obtain
F(m_{a})
normalised such that
F(m_{a}\ll T_{\text{HB}})\simeq 1
. As the Primakoff cross section and the energy loss are proportional to
g_{a\gamma}^{2}
, it is sufficient to replace
g_{a\gamma}\mapsto g_{a\gamma}\times\sqrt{F(m_{a})}
in
eq.

3.19
in order to apply the correction.

The
R
parameter likelihood is part of the
DarkBit

[
darkbit
]
module of
GAMBIT
. It was first introduced in Ref.
[
Axions
]
, and the correction for larger masses was added in Ref.
[
2007.05517
]
. Also note that slightly stronger limits in the region around
m_{a}\sim$100\text{\,}\mathrm{keV}$
may be obtained by considering photon coalescence
[
2004.08399
]
. As the
R
parameter constraints only marginally affect our results, we do not include these slightly improved constraints.

4
Results

By combining all of the likelihoods introduced in
section

3
, we are now in the position to perform a global scan of ALP decays in the early universe. We first present the results from a frequentist analysis of the parameter space, which enables us to identify particularly interesting parameter regions, and then perform a Bayesian analysis, which provides complementary information on the preference between the ALP model and
\Lambda

4.1
Frequentist scans

The parameters included in our scans are summarised in

We emphasise that the parameter ranges for the ALP model are somewhat arbitrary and are chosen in such a way as to capture all relevant features.
12
12

12

We note that for the smallest ALP masses and the shortest lifetimes that we consider, the assumption of non-relativistic ALP decays is not a good approximation for ALPs produced via freeze-in, because it underestimates the energy density of ALPs. Nevertheless, we find that this parameter region is robustly excluded in our scans. A more accurate treatment would only strengthen this exclusion and is therefore unnecessary.

In other words, while viable parameter regions exists also beyond the scan ranges, they do not lead to any qualitative changes in our conclusions.
The scan ranges for the
\Lambda

Note that this statement is not in contradiction with our treatment of the reheating temperature, which we fix to

This choice is conservative since limits that depend on

Thanks to the various parameter degeneracies of the ALP abundance – schematically captured in

[

In terms of our numerical results, our choice of

[

The results shown below are based on a total of 33.5 million samples and took over 700,000 CPU hours on the Joliot-Curie HPC cluster at CEA (France).

In what follows, we use Wilks’ theorem to determine the exclusion regions of the profile likelihood. To do so we assume that the number of degrees of freedom corresponds to the number of free parameters – i.e. two degrees of freedom for the two-dimensional plots shown below. It is well known that the assumptions of Wilks’ theorem often do not hold exactly, and that care should be taken to ensure its validity when claiming a discovery
[
1911.10237
, e.g.]
. For the purpose of setting limits w.r.t the best-fitting point, however, we argue that Wilks’ theorem typically presents a good approximation and avoids costly Monte Carlo simulations that do not alter significantly the findings of our analysis (see e.g. Ref.
[
2007.05517
, appendix D]
for a demonstration in the context of global fits).

To conclude, we note that we have checked explicitly that all results presented in this section are robust against additional contributions to
\Delta N_{\text{eff}}
from further ultrarelativistic species. This is a direct consequence of the fact that the constraint on

5
Conclusions

Axion-like particles (ALPs) are well-motivated candidates for light new physics and arise generically in many extensions of the Standard Model. In the present work we studied ALPs with masses in the keV–MeV range. We focused on ALPs with couplings to photons small enough to be inaccessible to accelerator experiments, but large enough to ensure that the ALPs decay before recombination and therefore do not contribute to the observed dark matter abundance. In this coupling range, ALPs can affect various cosmological and astrophysical observables, which makes it possible to search for them and to constrain their properties.

A central aspect of our analysis is that we did not assume that ALPs enter into thermal equilibrium in the early universe, such that their abundance is essentially an independent parameter. Nevertheless, one can place a lower bound on the abundance from the irreducible freeze-in production, and we provided a detailed calculation of this abundance as a function of the ALP mass and reheating temperature. At the same time, BBN and CMB constraints (both from anisotropies and spectral distortions) place tight upper bounds on the ALP abundance, typically requiring that these particles constitute only a tiny fraction of the dark matter abundance even before they decay.

To perform a global analysis of the ALP parameter space, we interfaced the GAMBIT framework with a number of external codes in order to combine state-of-the-art likelihood calculations for all relevant data sets. Most notably, for the first time we combine detailed calculations of the effects of photodisintegration of light elements with constraints on CMB spectral distortions, both of which give highly relevant constraints on ALP models. Our setup makes it possible to scan simultaneously over the ALP model parameters, the
\Lambda
CDM parameters, and various nuisance parameters, in order to identify the most interesting regions of parameter space (see
fig.

5
).

In particular, the decays of ALPs into photons can modify both the abundances of light elements and the baryon-to-photon ratio
\eta_{b}
. As a result, we find that
[{}\mathrm{D}/{}\mathrm{H}]
and
\Omega_{b}h^{2}
are less tightly correlated in our model than in
\Lambda
CDM (see
fig.

6
). This makes it possible to achieve a slightly better fit to observations in our model than in
\Lambda
CDM, although the Bayesian evidence does not prefer the ALP model over standard
\Lambda
CDM. However, we did not find any allowed parameter regions in our model that would address the
\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{7}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{Li}
problem.

Compared to previous results, we note that leaving the nonthermal abundance as a free parameter opens up parameter space for higher ALP masses, which are otherwise excluded by cosmological constraints (see
fig.

1
). Nonetheless, bounds on the decays of ALPs produced in SN1987A still provide relevant constraints in that region. This emphasises the importance of an analysis framework that allows for the inclusion of all relevant parameters and complementary constraints from astrophysics – both of which can be achieved with global-fitting tools such as
GAMBIT
.

Our findings also highlight the fact that measurements of the spectrum of the CMB from over 20 years ago can provide some of the leading cosmological constraints for ALPs with masses around the MeV scale. In particular, these constraints are stronger than those from the photodisintegration of light elements after BBN, which only constrain ALP masses
\gtrsim$5\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}$
.
More accurate measurements from a future satellite mission such as PIXIE therefore promise substantially stronger constraints and the potential to discover weakly coupled MeV-scale ALPs via spectral distortions.

To conclude, we emphasise that our analysis was focused on a fairly restrictive set of ALP models, in which the ALP couplings to electrons and hadrons are assumed to be negligible.
While this is a common assumption in the literature, there has been substantial, recent progress in understanding the effects of ALP-matter couplings on cosmology
[
1604.08614
,
1808.07430
,
2007.01873
,
2109.12088
]
.
This research programme goes hand in hand with a detailed exploration of the importance of ALP-matter coupling for supernovae such as SN1987A
[
2008.04918
,
2008.11741
,
2109.03244
]
.
Moreover, ALPs with hadronic couplings have been proposed as a possible solution of the
\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{7}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{Li}
problem
[
Goudelis:2015wpa
]
.
It will therefore be very interesting to extend our setup and explore the interplay between such ALP models and observations, and the resulting constraints on more general ALP parameter spaces.

A few of the newly added capabilities involve computing properties of the ALPs that can be derived from the model parameters. The capability
lifetime
computes the ALP lifetime as a function of the mass and coupling. The
minimum
\
_abundance
capability computes the minimal freeze-in abundance of ALPs, produced via Primakoff processes, either analytically or numerically (by reading in tables pre-computed with
micrOMEGAs
), and
minimum
\
_fraction
computes the fraction of the total abundance of dark matter constituted by the minimal freeze-in abundance. The
total
\
_DM
\
_abundance
is the total abundance of ALPs, produced by realignment and freeze-in, and
DM
\
_fraction
provides the fraction of the total DM abundance constituted by ALPs (assuming no ALP decays).

In decaying DM models such as ours, the baryon-to-photon ratio at BBN
\eta_{\rm{BBN}}
, the neutrino-to-photon temperature ratio
r_{\nu}
, and the number of ultra-relativistic degrees of freedom at recombination
\Delta N_{\rm{eff}}
, are provided by the capability
external_dNeff_etaBBN
. These values are returned as a
std
::
map
, and are can be accessed individually using the capabilities
eta_ratio
for
\eta_{\rm{BBN}}
and
Neff_evolution
for both
r_{\nu}
and
\Delta N_{\rm{eff}}
. Details of these computations can be found in Appendix
.

As mentioned above in
A.1
, we have defined a re-parametrisation of the
\Lambda
CDM model in
GAMBIT
to more closely match the parametrisation in
CLASS
. Along with the new model, a few capabilities are defined to provide useful quantities.
tau_reio
and
z_reio
extract the values of the optical depth and redshift of reionisation from
CLASS
. The capabilities
H_at_z
and
time_at_z
return the Hubble parameter and time since the Big Bang from
CLASS
as
daFunk
functions of redshift (see
[
darkbit
]
for more information on the
daFunk
library). Lastly
age_universe
computes the age of the Universe at a given redshift with
CLASS
.

To take into account the effects of the photodisintegration of light elements due to the EM spectrum injected by the ALP decays, we have restructured the capabilities relating to BBN. The capability
primordial_abundances_BBN
replaces the old
BBN_abundances
capability, which computes the abundances after BBN using
AlterBBN
. The new capability
primordial_abundances
computes the abundances at the present time. Two module functions provide this capability,
primordial_abundances
, which simply returns the abundances unchanged from BBN, and
primordial_abundances_decayingDM
, which uses
ACROPOLIS
to modify the computed abundances at BBN in order to include the effects of photodisintegration.

Finally, the only new capability added to
DarkBit
is
PhotonFluence_SN1987A_Decay
, which computes the
\gamma
fluence from the supernova SN1987A as a result of the decay of ALPs into photons.

A.3
New and updated backend interfaces

GAMBIT
provides interfaces to a large collection of external tools, known as backends. The backend interfaces are documented along with the modules that use them
[
ColliderBit
,
CosmoBit
,
darkbit
,
FlavBit
,
RHN
,
SDPBit
]
. Backend interfaces can also be auto-generated
[
GUM
]
. For this analysis we have updated some of the backends reported in
[
CosmoBit
]
to their latest version, such as
CLASS 3.1

[
Blas:2011rf
]
and
MontePython 3.5

[
brinckmann2018montepython
]
. The updated version of
CLASS
brings along the possibility of including spectral distortions, as described in
section

3.3
, which also benefits from the new version of
MontePython
, as it comes with more likelihoods, including COBE/FIRAS and a mock PIXIE-like likelihood. Furthermore, we have modified the interface to
AlterBBN

[
Arbey:2011nf
,
Arbey:2018zfh
]
in order to allow the computation of abundances without uncertainties, as well as to return three values of the abundances, corresponding to the central, high and low values computed by
AlterBBN
.

The only absolutely new backend interface implemented for this study is to the external tool
ACROPOLIS 1.2.1

[
Depta:2020mhj
]
. Written in
Python
,
ACROPOLIS
computes the effects of photodisintegration of light elements on pre-computed abundances. In addition to the light element abundances,
ACROPOLIS
takes as input the mass of the decaying particle,
m_{a}
, its lifetime
\tau_{a}
, the ratio of the number density of the decaying particle today to that of photons,
N_{0,a}
, and the branching fractions to electrons and photons, respectively. From this set of input parameters, the backend convenience function
abundance_photodisintegration_decay
performs various transformations on the input and output abundances and covariance matrix, and returns the final abundances after photodisintegration, and their associated covariance matrix.

The light element abundances in
ACROPOLIS
are taken as absolute abundances, as opposed to the treatment in
GAMBIT
and
AlterBBN
, where they are treated as ratios over the hydrogen abundance,
r_{i}=Y_{i}/Y_{H}
. Hence before feeding the abundances to
ACROPOLIS
, they are modified accordingly. Furthermore, the abundances computed by
AlterBBN
include the late-time decays of
\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{H}
and
\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{7}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{Be}
. As these happen after photodisintegration, and are in fact automatically added by
ACROPOLIS
, we revert the
\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{H}
and
\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{7}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{7}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{Be}
decays when converting to absolute abundances for sending to
ACROPOLIS
. Conversely, before returning the final abundances, they are converted back to ratios over
Y_{H}
.

ACROPOLIS
takes the abundances as a fixed-size array ordered as
\{n,p,D,\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{3}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{3}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He},\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{4}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{4}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{4}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{4}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{4}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{He},\hphantom{{}^{\mathrm{6}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox and, along with the input parameters, computes a transfer matrix
\mathbf{M}
, which provides the abundances after photodisintegration as
\mathbf{Y}_{f}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Y}_{i}
. Besides the computation of the final abundances, this transfer matrix is used to propagate the covariance matrix as

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{f}=(\mathbf{J}_{2}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{J}_{1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}(\mathbf{J}_{2}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{J}_{1})^{T},

(A.18)

where
\mathbf{J}_{1}
is the Jacobian of the transformation from ratios over
Y_{H}
to absolute abundances, and
\mathbf{J}_{2}
the converse. These Jacobians are different because the former includes the subtraction of late-time decays, whereas these are not included in the latter, but rather in the transfer matrix
\mathbf{M}
.

ensures that the given options are passed to the relevant
