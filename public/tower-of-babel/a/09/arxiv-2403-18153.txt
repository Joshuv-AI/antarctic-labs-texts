Abstract
Consider a compact metric space S S and a pair ( j , k ) (j,k) with k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 and 1 ≤ j ≤ k 1\leq j\leq k . For any probability distribution θ ∈ 𝒫 ⁡ ( S ) \theta\in\mathcal{P}(S) , define a Markov chain on S S by: from state s s , take k k i.i.d. ( θ \theta ) samples, and jump to the j j ’th closest. A few results are proved in a companion article [ 1 ] ; this article, not intended for formal publication, records numerical studies and conjectures.

Introduction

We start with informal background that led to the formulation of the questions to be studied.
If I suggest a specific compact metric space
(S,d)
and ask you to define some “interesting” mapping
f:S\to S
then you can certainly do so.
However, if I challenge you to define a scheme which can be used in any “generic” such space
S
, without exploiting any structure of the specific space, then that seems very difficult.
For instance you could define

f(s):=\arg\max_{y}d(s,y)

that is the most distant point from
s
; this works for any space
S
with the property that the most distant point is always unique.
Are there any more interesting general schemes?

Instead let us write
\mathcal{P}(S)
for the space of probability distributions on
S
.
Consider the analogous challenge: define a scheme which can be used to specify a mapping
\mathcal{P}(S)\to\mathcal{P}(S)
for a generic
S
.
In contrast to the case of mappings
S\to S
, there are perhaps many ways to do so.
In particular, there are schemes involving Markov chains.
For instance, given
\theta\in\mathcal{P}(S)
you can define a transition kernel
K(s,\cdot)
for
s\in\mathrm{support}(\theta)
by conditioning
\theta

to the ball of radius
1
around
s
:

K(s,A)=\theta(A\cap\mathrm{ball}(s,1))/\theta(\mathrm{ball}(s,1)).

Under a simple connectivity condition
1
1

1

Essentially, that
\cup_{s\in\mathrm{support}(\theta)}\mathrm{ball}(s,1)
is connected.
on
\theta
, the corresponding Markov chain has a unique stationary distribution, which we can call
\pi(\theta)
.
So this scheme defines a map
\pi:\mathcal{P}_{0}(S)\to\mathcal{P}(S)
for every
S
but only for the subset
\mathcal{P}_{0}(S)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(S)
satisfying the connectivity condition.
One could view this as a continuous-space analog of random walk on a graph.

In this project we study a scheme which works for
all
compact metric spaces
S
and
all
of
\mathcal{P}(S)
.
Given
\theta\in\mathcal{P}(S)
, one can consider the Markov chain that from
point
s
makes the step to the nearer of
2
random points drawn i.i.d. from
\theta
, breaking ties uniformly at random.
This scheme naturally generalizes as follows: fix
k\geq 2
and
1\leq j\leq k
,
and step from
s
to the
j
’th nearest of
k
random points drawn i.i.d. from
\theta
, again breaking ties uniformly at random.
This defines a kernel
K^{\theta,j,k}
on
S
.
Write the associated chain as
\mathbf{X}^{\theta,j,k}=(X^{\theta,j,k}(t),t=0,1,2,\ldots)
.
Theorem
1
proves that this chain always has a unique stationary distribution, which we can call
\pi_{j,k}(\theta)
.
So now we have defined a mapping
\pi_{j,k}:\mathcal{P}(S)\to\mathcal{P}(S)
for every
S
.
Moreover Theorem
1
proves that the distributions
\theta
and
\pi_{j,k}(\theta)
are mutually absolutely continuous, so in particular
have the same support.

These maps
\pi_{j,k}
have apparently not been studied previously, even for special spaces
S
, so
the purpose of this project is to initiate their study.
There is a range of problems one might consider.
The proof of Theorem
1
, using the natural coupling, does not yield any helpful explicit expression for the stationary distribution.
So one can ask

On a given space
S
, is there any informative description of
\pi_{j,k}(\theta)
in terms of
\theta
?

In this project our focus is different.
Given a mapping
\pi
from a space to itself, it is mathematically natural to consider iterates

\pi^{n+1}(\theta)=\pi(\pi^{n}(\theta)),n\geq 1.

In our setting it seems plausible that (at least for typical initial
\theta
) the iterates should converge to some fixed point, that is we expect

\pi^{n}_{j,k}(\theta)\to_{w}\phi\mbox{ as }n\to\infty

(1)

and then we expect the limit to satisfy

\pi_{j,k}(\phi)=\phi.

(2)

We will call such a construction
(\pi^{n}_{j,k}(\theta),n\geq 0)
the
iterative procedure
.
Note that this does not have any simple stochastic process interpretation, in contrast to the mapping
\theta\to\pi_{j,k}(\theta)
derived from the Markov chain.

If
\phi
satisfies (
2
) we call it a
fixed point
or an
invariant distribution
for
\pi_{j,k}
.
One can view this property as a kind of “self-similarity under sampling” property.
The original motivation for this project was the hope that a typical compact metric space might have “interesting” invariant distributions.
Our first attempt at finding such distributions was via numerically implementing the iterative procedure.
But in doing so, with initial distributions lacking symmetry, we observed that typically there is a limit but it is supported on only one or two points.
This seemed counter-intuitive, and prompted the further study of fixed points in this article.

Let us emphasize the trivial observation:

On any
S
and for any
(j,k)
, two types of measures are always invariant:
we call these the
omnipresent
measures.

•

The distribution
\delta_{s}
degenerate at one point
s
;

•

The uniform two-point distribution

\delta_{s_{1},s_{2}}=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{s_{1}}+\delta_{s_{2}})
.

Are there others?

1.1
Formulating a program

There are some subtleties involved in formulating a precise program, as follows.

(i) Convergence in (
1
) is weak convergence.
Recall that a Markov chain is a
Feller
chain if its kernel
K
is weakly continuous:

K(s_{n},\cdot)\to_{w}K(s,\cdot)\mbox{ for all $s_{n}\to s$}.

(3)

It is straightforward and well known (
[
3
]
section 12.3) that a Feller chain on a compact
S
always has at least one stationary distribution.
Our chains
\mathbf{X}^{\theta,j,k}
are not necessarily Feller, the obstacle arising when the chain involves ties – see discussion in section
2.1
.
But it turns out that we do not need the Feller property for the foundational result, Theorem
1
.
On the other hand one
might hope that the mapping
\pi_{j,k}:\mathcal{P}(S)\to\mathcal{P}(S)
is continuous, but we can only prove that it is continuous at (roughly speaking) distributions
\theta
for which the Feller property holds –
see Proposition
2
.
So convergence (
1
) does not automatically imply that the limit is a fixed point (
2
), though we do not know a counter-example.

(ii)
For given
\theta
the iterates
\pi^{n}_{j,k}(\theta),n\geq 0
all have the same support but we will see that the limit usually
has much smaller support,
so we cannot insist on limits having full support on
S
.
On the other hand, any fixed point distribution over a space
S_{0}
is automatically a fixed point distribution over a superspace
S_{1}\supset S_{0}
, and to talk about
“fixed points for
S_{1}
” we want the fixed point to have some relation with
S_{1}
.
So an appropriate precise problem formulation is

Program.
(a) Given a compact metric space
(S,d)
and given
(j,k)
, find all the fixed points of
\pi_{j,k}

with full support on
S
.

(b) Given a compact metric space
(S,d)
and given
(j,k)
, determine which invariant measures (not necessarily with full support
on
S
)
arise as limits
(
1
) of the iterative procedure from some initial
\theta
with full support on
S
.

Existence and uniqueness of stationary distributions

Theorem 1
(
[
1
]
Theorem 1)

Consider a compact metric space
(S,d)
and a probability distribution
\theta\in\mathcal{P}(S)
.
For each pair
1\leq j\leq k,\ k\geq 2
, the Markov chain

\mathbf{X}^{\theta,j,k}=(X^{\theta,j,k}(t),t=0,1,2,\ldots)

has a unique stationary distribution
\pi_{j,k}(\theta)
.
From any initial point, the variation distance
D(t)
between
\pi_{j,k}(\theta)
and the distribution of
X^{\theta,j,k}(t)
satisfies

D(2t)\leq(1-1/k^{k-1})^{t},\quad 1\leq t<\infty

(4)

and so there is convergence to stationarity in variation distance.
Moreover, for
\pi=\pi_{j,k}(\theta)

\theta^{k}(A)\leq\pi(A)\leq k\theta(A),\ A\subseteq S

(5)

and so
\pi
and
\theta
are mutually absolutely continuous.

Remarks.

(a) Note that the bound on variation distance depends only on
k
.

(b) The variation distance bound (
4
) is exponentially decreasing in time, but it is more natural to consider

in the sense of
[
4
]
.
The example of the uniform distribution
\theta
on a 2-point space with

shows that the mixing time as a function of

can be order

.

(c) As mentioned earlier, the proof of Theorem
1
does not say anything about
\pi_{j,k}(\theta)
except (
5
).
We do not know if there are informative analytic descriptions of
\pi_{j,k}(\theta)

2.1
Concerning the Feller property

As an example to show that the Markov chains
\mathbf{X}^{\theta,j,k}
are not necessarily Feller,
consider
S=[0,1]
and
\theta=\frac{1}{3}(\delta_{0}+\delta_{1}+\lambda)

for Lebesgue measure
\lambda
.
Here the requirement (
3
) that
K(x_{i},\cdot)\to_{w}K(\frac{1}{2},\cdot)
as
x_{i}\to\frac{1}{2}
fails.

This difficulty occurs because there are ties in the distances to the points

(Y_{i},1\leq i\leq k)
sampled i.i.d. from
\theta
.
Suppose, for given
\theta\in\mathcal{P}(S)

and given

,

d(s,Y_{i})\mbox{ has non-atomic distribution}.

(6)

This of course cannot hold for finite
S
.
Property (
6

{\mathbb{P}}(d(s,Y_{i})=d(s,Y_{j}))=0\mbox{ for }j\neq i.

(7)

It is straightforward to show that, if (
7
) holds for all

,
then the Markov chain
\mathbf{X}^{\theta,j,k}
is Feller.
Let us consider the slightly weaker property: for given
\theta\in\mathcal{P}(S)

d(s,Y_{i})\mbox{ has non-atomic distribution for $\theta$-almost all }s\in S.

(8)

Note this is equivalent to

{\mathbb{P}}(d(Y_{1},Y_{2})=d(Y_{1},Y_{3}))=0.

(9)

We can now give a partial continuity property for the maps
\pi_{j,k}
.

Proposition 2

If
\theta\in\mathcal{P}(S)
has property (
8

\pi_{j,k}(\theta_{n})\to_{w}\pi_{j,k}(\theta)

for all
\theta_{n}\to_{w}\theta
.

Proof.

Consider
\theta_{n}\to_{w}\theta
.
Write
(X(t),t=0,1,2,\ldots)
for the chain
\mathbf{X}^{\theta,j,k}
started with distribution
\theta

and write
(X^{n}(t),t=0,1,2,\ldots)
for the chain
\mathbf{X}^{\theta_{n},j,k}
started with distribution
\theta_{n}
.
It is sufficient to prove that, for fixed

, we have convergence in distribution

\mathrm{dist}(X^{n}(t))\to_{w}\mathrm{dist}(X(t))\mbox{ as }n\to\infty

(10)

because then the result will follow from (
4
).
Proving this would make a nice exercise in a course on weak convergence.
We will write out the case
(j,k)=(1,2)
: the general case is similar.

We prove (
10
) by induction on

.
Fix

, assume (
10
) for that

, and recall that by construction

\mathrm{dist}(X(t))\leq k\theta.

(11)

There exist couplings
(Y_{i},Y_{i}^{n})
of
\theta

\theta_{n}
such that

a(n):=\mathbb{E}d(Y_{i},Y_{i}^{n})\to 0\mbox{ as }n\to\infty

\phi

\phi_{n}

We have

\displaystyle X(t+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y_{1}\mbox{ if }d(X(t),Y_{1})<d(X(t),Y_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y_{2}\mbox{ if }d(X(t),Y_{1})>d(X(t),Y_{2})

Combining with the analog for

\displaystyle d(X(t+1),X^{n}(t+1))

\displaystyle=

3
General remarks about fixed points of the mapping

On every compact metric space
S
we have an obvious “preservation of symmetry” result

Lemma 3

If
\theta\in\mathcal{P}(S)
is invariant under an isometry
\iota
of
S
then
\pi_{j,k}(\theta)
is also invariant under
\iota
.

This has several implications.

3.1
Fixed points existing by symmetry

In some cases there are distributions
\phi\in\mathcal{P}(S)
which are invariant (that is, fixed points) “by symmetry”.
In particular, the omnipresent examples mentioned earlier:

(i) The distribution
\delta_{s}
degenerate at one point
s
;

(ii) The uniform two-point distribution

\delta_{s_{1},s_{2}}=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{s_{1}}+\delta_{s_{2}})
;

But there are further examples:

(iii) The Haar probability measure on a compact group
S
with a metric invariant under the group action.

(iv) On a finite space
S
,
a sufficient condition for the uniform distribution to be invariant is that

S
is
transitive
, that is if for each pair
s,s^{\prime}
there is an isometry taking
s
to
s^{\prime}
.
This is equivalent to the finite case of Haar measure.
But for finite
S
a weaker condition suffices, because all that matters is the
rank matrix
– see section
5.1
.

Now in those cases the distribution is invariant for all
\pi_{j,k}
.
So the question becomes:
for a particular
(j,k)
, are there invariant distributions with full support, other than those “forced by symmetry” as above?

3.2
Symmetry in the limit

If
\theta\in\mathcal{P}(S)
is invariant under an isometry
\iota
, then by Lemma
3
each iterate
\pi_{j,k}^{n}(\theta)
is invariant
and so we expect (recall that we do not know that
\pi_{j,k}
is continuous) that a limit
\phi
will be invariant.
This observation will be relevant for the formulation of conjectures.

3.3
Fixed points for
\pi_{1,2}
and
\pi_{2,2}

Perhaps the basic example of a compact space is the unit interval
[0,1]
, and for that space we will give a detailed simulation study of the iterative process in section
7
.
Figure

shows the observed behavior of iterates of
\pi_{1,2}
and
\pi_{2,2}
from the initial uniform distribution.
As observed above, limits of such iterates should be invariant under the reflection isometry

.
We see the former converging to
\delta_{1/2}
and the latter to
\delta_{0,1}
.
We believe this qualitative difference will hold very generally — see the related Conjectures
5
and

.
So the following general result seems very counter-intuitive.

4
Two elements – the binomial case

One might suppose that the case of a 2-element set
S=\{a,b\}
would be trivial, but it is not.
Parametrizing a distribution
\theta
on
S
by
p:=\theta(a)
, we view the mapping
\pi_{j,k}:\mathcal{P}(S)\to\mathcal{P}(S)
as a mapping
\pi_{j,k}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
defined as follows.
In the associated 2-state Markov chain, the transition probabilities are

k_{a,b}={\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{Bin}(k,p)<j);\quad k_{b,a}={\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{Bin}(k,p)>k-j)

for Binomial random variables. From the stationary distribution we find

\pi_{j,k}(p)=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{Bin}(k,p)>k-j)}{{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{Bin}(k,p)>k-j)+{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{Bin}(k,p)<j)}.

So a fixed point is a solution of the equation

\pi_{j,k}(p)=p.

(12)

We know by symmetry that
p=0,p=1/2,p=1
are fixed points; are there others?
By symmetry it is enough to consider
0<p<1/2
.

We have not tried to find solutions analytically, but we will show results of numerical calculations of the iterates
\pi^{n}_{j,k}(p),n=1,2,3,\ldots
.
For a given
(k,j)
, we observe three possible types of qualitative behavior:

(i)
\pi^{n}_{j,k}(p)\to 0\mbox{ as }n\to\infty,\mbox{ for all }0<p<1/2
.

(ii)
\pi^{n}_{j,k}(p)\to 1/2\mbox{ as }n\to\infty,\mbox{ for all }0<p<1/2
.

(iii) There exists a critical value
p_{crit}\in(0,1/2)
such that

p_{crit}
is invariant

and
\pi^{n}_{j,k}(p)\to 0\mbox{ as }n\to\infty,\mbox{ for all }0<p<p_{crit}

and
\pi^{n}_{j,k}(p)\to 1/2\mbox{ as }n\to\infty,\mbox{ for all }p_{crit}<p<1/2
.

For us, (iii) is the interesting case.
It first arises with
k=5,j=4
, as shown in Figure
2
.

The previous sections stated “results” about
