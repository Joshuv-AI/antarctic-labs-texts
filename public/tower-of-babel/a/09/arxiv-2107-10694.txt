The Hilbert L L -matrix
František Štampach

Abstract
. We analyze spectral properties of the Hilbert L L -matrix ( 1 max ⁡ ( m , n ) + ν ) m , n = 0 ∞ \left(\frac{1}{\max(m,n)+\nu}\right)_{m,n=0}^{\infty} regarded as an operator L ν L_{\nu} acting on ℓ 2 ​ ( ℕ 0 ) \ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0}) , for ν ∈ ℝ \nu\in{\mathbb{R}} , ν ≠ 0 , − 1 , − 2 , … \nu\neq 0,-1,-2,\dots . In particular, we give answers to two open problems concerning the operator norm of L ν L_{\nu} published by L. Bouthat and J. Mashreghi in [ Oper. Matrices 15, No. 1 (2021), 47–58]. In addition, several general aspects concerning the definition of an L L -operator, its positivity, and Fredholm determinants are also discussed.

Introduction

In
[
7
]
, L. Bouthat and J. Mashreghi use the Schur test to deduce sufficient conditions under which the structured matrix

\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&a_{1}&a_{2}&\dots\\
a_{1}&a_{1}&a_{2}&\dots\\
a_{2}&a_{2}&a_{2}&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}

determines a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
and derived an upper bound on its operator norm. We adopt the terminology from
[
7
]
and refer to these matrices as the
L
-matrices.

Definition 1
.

A semi-infinite matrix
\mathcal{L}
is called
L
-matrix
if its matrix elements are of the form

\mathcal{L}_{m,n}=a_{\max(m,n)},\quad m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where
a=\{a_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a complex sequence. We call
a

the parameter sequence
of
\mathcal{L}
.

As an interesting example, the authors of
[
7
]
studied the norm of a bounded operator
L_{\nu}
determined by the
L
-matrix
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
whose parameter sequence reads

a_{n}(\nu)=\frac{1}{n+\nu},

for
\nu>0
. In an obvious analogy to the famous (generalized) Hilbert matrix, which is the Hankel matrix with the
(m,n)
-th entry equal to
a_{m+n}(\nu)
, matrix
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
is called the
Hilbert
L
-matrix
. It is proved in
[
7
]
that

\|L_{\nu}\|=4,

if
\nu\geq 1/2
. However, for
\nu\in(0,1/2)
, the value of
\|L_{\nu}\|
remained indeterminate. Since
\|L_{\nu}\|
has to dominate the first diagonal element of
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
, i.e.,
\|L_{\nu}\|\geq a_{0}(\nu)=1/\nu
, it is clear that, for
\nu\in(0,1/4)
,
\|L_{\nu}\|>4
. This let the authors of
[
7
]
to formulate two natural questions on the determination of numbers
\nu_{0}:=\inf\{\nu>0\mid\|L_{\nu}\|=4\}
and
\|L_{\nu}\|
, for
\nu<1/2
. In recent paper
[
8
]
, the authors deduced explicit upper and lower bounds on
\nu_{0}
.

The goal of this paper is a detailed spectral analysis of
L_{\nu}
for all admissible real values of the parameter
\nu
, i.e.,
\nu\neq 0,-1,-2,\dots
. In particular, we provide answers to both above questions. An important role is played by the unit argument hypergeometric
{}_{3}F_{2}
-functions; see definition (
15
) below. We show that
\nu_{0}
is the unique positive zero of the function

\nu\mapsto{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-1/2,1/2,3/2}{1,\nu+1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr),

which, if evaluated numerically, is approximately
\nu_{0}\approx 0.349086
. Further, we prove that, for
\nu\geq\nu_{0}
, the spectrum of
L_{\nu}
is purely absolutely continuous and fills the interval
[0,4]
. However, if
0<\nu<\nu_{0}
, a unique and simple eigenvalue greater than
4
appears in the spectrum of
L_{\nu}
. Clearly, this eigenvalue coincides with
\|L_{\nu}\|
. More concretely, we prove that

\|L_{\nu}\|=\frac{4}{1-4x_{0}^{2}(\nu)},

where
x_{0}(\nu)
is the unique zero of the function

x\mapsto{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{x-1/2,x+1/2,x+3/2}{2x+1,x+\nu+1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr)

located in
(0,1/2)
. In addition, we show that
\|L_{\nu}\|
is strictly decreasing in
(0,\nu_{0})
, deduce an asymptotic expansion of
\|L_{\nu}\|
for
\nu
small, and obtain bounds on
\|L_{\nu}\|
.

If
\nu<0
, spectral properties of
L_{\nu}
are more delicate. It turns out that
still
\sigma_{{\rm ac}}(L_{\nu})=[0,4]
and
\sigma_{{\rm p}}(L_{\nu})
can be identified with a set of roots of a transcendental equation. Moreover,
\sigma_{{\rm p}}(L_{\nu})
is shown to be always finite but, in contrast to the case
\nu>0
,
\sigma_{{\rm p}}(L_{\nu})
contains a negative eigenvalue, if
\nu<0
. By a numerical evidence, it seems that
\sigma_{\text{p}}(L_{\nu})
is actually at most a two-point set with a negative eigenvalue and possibly another eigenvalue greater than 4. This can be compared with the spectrum of the classical Hilbert matrix operator
H_{\nu}
, whose
(m,n)
-th matrix entry equals
a_{m+n}(\nu)
. Recall that
\sigma_{\text{ac}}(H_{\nu})=[0,\pi]
for all
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}_{0})
, while
\sigma_{\text{p}}(H_{\nu})
is empty, if
\nu\geq 1/2
, contains a unique eigenvalue greater than
\pi
, if
\nu\in(0,1/2)
, contains a negative eigenvalue, if
\nu<0
, and possibly another eigenvalue greater than
\pi
(which is always the case, if
\nu<-1/2
). On the other hand, the spectrum of
L_{\nu}
is always simple, which is not the case for
H_{\nu}
; see
[
26
, Thm. 5]
or
[
14
, Thm. 8]
for more details.

Notation:

\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
by using the capital calligraphic letters for matrices and standard capitals for operators. This is, of course, not needed when dealing with bounded operators only, however, necessary in general. Moreover, we use the following standard notation throughout the paper:
\mathbb{N}_{0}
is the set of non-negative integers;
\mathbb{N}
is the set of positive integers;
\{e_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
is the standard basis of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
;
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits A
,
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Ker}\nolimits A
, and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Ran}\nolimits A
is the domain, kernel, and range of an operator
A
, respectively;
\sigma(A)
,
\sigma_{{\rm p}}(A)
, and
\sigma_{{\rm ac}}(A)
is the spectrum, point spectrum, and absolutely continuous spectrum of
A
, respectively.

Other notation, mainly related to special functions, is defined by its first occurrence.

2.
L
-matrices

2.1.
An operator associated to a regular
L
-matrix

We first recall a general method of constructing densely defined operators on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
with a given matrix representation, see for example
[
4
, Sec. 2.1]
. A semi-infinite matrix
\mathcal{A}
determines a closed and densely defined operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
when its rows and columns can be identified with elements of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. In this case, we can define the so-called minimal operator
A_{\min}
and the maximal operator
A_{\max}
associated with the matrix
\mathcal{A}
.

The minimal operator
A_{\min}
associated with matrix
\mathcal{A}
is defined as the operator closure of the auxiliary operator
\dot{A}
acting on vectors
x
from the space of finitely supported sequences
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits\dot{A}:=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}\nolimits\{e_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
as the matrix multiplication
\dot{A}x:=\mathcal{A}\cdot x
, where
x
is regarded as an infinite column vector. The fact that
\dot{A}
is always closable can be easily checked.
On the other hand, the maximal operator
A_{\max}
acts again as the matrix multiplication
A_{\max}x:=\mathcal{A}\cdot x
but on vectors
x
from a maximal domain

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits A_{\max}:=\left\{x\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\mid\mathcal{A}\cdot x\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\right\}.

A_{\max}
is always closed.

One has
A_{\min}\subset A_{\max}
. If
B
is any operator which acts by the matrix multiplication
Bx=\mathcal{A}\cdot x
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}\nolimits\{e_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}\subset\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits B
, then
A_{\min}\subset B\subset A_{\max}
. In this sense, matrix
\mathcal{A}
determines a unique operator
A
acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
with
\langle e_{m},Ae_{n}\rangle=\mathcal{A}_{m,n}
, for all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, if and only if
A_{\min}=A_{\max}
. Such matrix
\mathcal{A}
is called
proper
.

Clearly, if
\mathcal{A}
is an
L
-matrix, then its rows and columns are square summable if and only if its parameter sequence
a\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
.

Definition 2
.

An
L
-matrix
\mathcal{L}
is called
regular
, if its parameter sequence fulfills
a_{n}\neq a_{n+1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
singular
, otherwise.

The following claim justifies the terminology used in Definition
2
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be an
L
-matrix with
a\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. Then

\mathcal{L}\mbox{ is regular }\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Ker}\nolimits L=\{0\},

for any operator
L
such that
L_{\min}\subset L\subset L_{\max}
.

Proof.

Fix
L
a linear operator with
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}\nolimits\{e_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}\subset\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits L
and
\mathcal{L}_{m,n}=\langle e_{m},Le_{n}\rangle
for all
m,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}
is singular. Then there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
a_{n}=a_{n+1}
which means that the
n
th and
(n+1)
th column of
\mathcal{L}
coincide. It follows that
e_{n}-e_{n+1}\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Ker}\nolimits L
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}
is regular and
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Ker}\nolimits L
. Then the equation
Lx=0
means that

a_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}x_{k}+\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}a_{k}x_{k}=0,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Shifting the index
n
by one and subtracting the resulting equation from the above one, we obtain

(a_{n+1}-a_{n})\sum_{k=0}^{n}x_{k}=0,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Since
a_{n}\neq a_{n+1}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, by assumption, the above equations imply that
x=0
.
∎

A particularly useful observation is that the formal inverse of a regular
L
-matrix is a tridiagonal matrix with a special structure. Recall that the matrix multiplication
\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}
of infinite matrices
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
is well defined, if at least one of the matrices
\mathcal{A}
or
\mathcal{B}
is banded. Provided that
a_{n}\neq a_{n+1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we put

b_{n}:=\frac{1}{a_{n}-a_{n+1}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(1)

and define the Jacobi matrix

\mathcal{J}:=\begin{pmatrix}b_{0}&-b_{0}\\
-b_{0}&b_{0}+b_{1}&-b_{1}\\
&-b_{1}&b_{1}+b_{2}&-b_{2}\\
&&-b_{2}&b_{2}+b_{3}&-b_{3}\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

(2)

Matrix
\mathcal{J}
turns out to be a formal inverse to regular
L
-matrix
\mathcal{L}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular
L
-matrix and
\mathcal{J}
the Jacobi matrix defined by (
1
) and (
2
), then we have:

(1)

\mathcal{L}\cdot\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}\cdot\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{I}
, where
\mathcal{I}
is the identity matrix.

(2)

If
\mathcal{J}\cdot x=0
, then
x_{n}=x_{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

(3)

\mathcal{J}
is proper.

Proof.

The proof of (i) is a matter of straightforward matrix multiplication. The verification of (ii) is also elementary. Thus, it suffices to prove claim (iii).

Let us denote by
p=\{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
and
q=\{q_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
the two solutions of the second order difference equation

-b_{n-1}\phi_{n-1}+(b_{n-1}+b_{n})\phi_{n}-b_{n}\phi_{n-1}=0,\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

determined by the initial conditions
p_{0}=0
,
p_{1}=1
and
q_{0}=1
,
q_{1}=0
. The matrix
\mathcal{J}
is proper, if and only if
p\notin\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
or
q\notin\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
; see
[
5
]
. Using (
1
), a straightforward computation shows that

p_{n}=\frac{a_{0}-a_{n}}{a_{0}-a_{1}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad q_{n}=\frac{a_{n}-a_{1}}{a_{0}-a_{1}},

for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Since
p_{n}+q_{n}=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p
and
q
cannot be both in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
.
∎

Observations from Lemma
4
can be used to define a unique closed operator associated to a regular
L
-matrix even when
a\notin\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, i.e, when the standard construction is not applicable. Indeed, since
\mathcal{J}
is proper by claim (iii) of Lemma
4
, it determines the unique operator
J
acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. Moreover,
J
is invertible since
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Ker}\nolimits J=\{0\}
by claim (ii) of Lemma
4
. This justifies the following definition.

Definition 5
.

To a given regular
L
-matrix
\mathcal{L}
, we associate the
L
-operator
L:=J^{-1}
, where
J
is the Jacobi operator given by (
2
).

Remark 6
.

We also call the
L
-operator from Definition
5

regular
, similarly as the
L
-matrix, to emphasize the (implicit) assumption
a_{n}\neq a_{n+1}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proposition 7
.

A regular
L
-operator is closed and densely defined.

Proof.

As an inverse of a closed operator
J
, the regular
L
-operator
L
is closed.
Further,
(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Ran}\nolimits J)^{\perp}=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Ker}\nolimits J^{*}
and, for
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Ker}\nolimits J^{*}
, one has
\mathcal{J}^{*}\cdot x=0
, which implies
x=0
by claim (ii) of Lemma
4
, where
\mathcal{J}^{*}
denotes the Jacobi matrix (
2
) with complex conjugated entries. It implies that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits L
is dense in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
.
∎

Of course, for an
L
-operator
L
associated to a regular
L
-matrix
\mathcal{L}
, the matrix
\mathcal{L}
is not the matrix representation of
L
in the usual sense, which means

\mathcal{L}_{m,n}=\langle e_{m},Le_{n}\rangle,\quad\forall m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

because the vectors
e_{n}
need not belong to
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits L
. This is guaranteed when we additionally have the parameter sequence
a\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
.

Even if
a\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, the
L
-operator need not be bounded. For example, if
a_{n}=1/(n+1)^{\alpha}
, for
1/2<\alpha<1
, then
L
is unbounded
[
7
, Ex. 2]
. A sufficient condition for boundedness of
L
is
a_{n}=O(1/n)
, as
n\to\infty
; see
[
7
]
. In view of Definition
5
,
L
is bounded if and only if
0\notin\sigma(J)
. Recall that
0\notin\sigma_{{\rm p}}(J)
as we know by claim (ii) of Lemma
4
.

Note also that the mapping
\mathcal{L}\mapsto L
between regular
L
-matrices and
L
-operators established by Definition
5
is not injective. Indeed, it suffices to note that the matrix elements of
\mathcal{J}
depend only on the
difference
between two consecutive elements of the parameter sequence
a
. Hence, an
L
-matrix with a parameter sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
gives rise to the same
L
-operator as the
L
-matrix with the parameter sequence
\{a_{n}+c\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, where
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
. This ambiguity is removed when the admissible parameter sequences are restricted to the square summable.

Example 8
.

Consider the parameter sequence
a_{n}=n
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then the
L
-operator
L
associated to the
L
-matrix

\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&2&3&\dots\\
1&1&2&3&\dots\\
2&2&2&3&\dots\\
3&3&3&3&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}

is the inverse of the Jacobi operator
J
given by the tridiagonal matrix

\mathcal{J}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\
1&-2&1\\
&1&-2&1\\
&&1&-2&1\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

Operator
J
is a discrete analogue to the Neumann Laplacian on the half-line and its spectral analysis is not very difficult
[
19
]
. It can be shown that
J
has simple spectrum

\sigma(J)=\sigma_{{\rm ac}}(J)=[-4,0]

and

\psi_{n}(\lambda):=U_{n}\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)-U_{n-1}\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

is the
n
th entry of the generalized eigenvector to
\lambda\in[-4,0]
, where
U_{n}
are Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind and
U_{-1}:=0
. In particular, it follows that the spectrum of
L
is simple and
\sigma(L)=\sigma_{{\rm ac}}(L)=(-\infty,-1/4]
.

2.2.
Factorization and positivity of regular
L
-matrices

First, it is useful to observe that, if
\mathcal{C}
is a bi-diagonal semi-infinite matrix of the form

\mathcal{C}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}&-c_{0}\\
&c_{1}&-c_{1}\\
&&c_{2}&-c_{2}\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

(3)

then

\mathcal{C}^{T}\cdot\mathcal{C}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}^{2}&-c_{0}^{2}\\
-c_{0}^{2}&c_{0}^{2}+c_{1}^{2}&-c_{1}^{2}\\
&-c_{1}^{2}&c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}&-c_{2}^{2}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

Comparing this observation with the structure of the Jacobi matrix
\mathcal{J}
from (
2
), it is clear that
\mathcal{J}
decomposes as

\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{B}^{T}\cdot\mathcal{B},

(4)

where

\mathcal{B}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{b_{0}}&-\sqrt{b_{0}}\\
&\sqrt{b_{1}}&-\sqrt{b_{1}}\\
&&\sqrt{b_{2}}&-\sqrt{b_{2}}\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

provided that
b_{n}>0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proposition 9
.

A real regular
L
-operator is positive semi-definite if and only if
a_{n}>a_{n+1}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proof.

Clearly, an
L
-operator
L=J^{-1}
is positive semi-definite if and only if the Jacobi operator
J
is positive semi-definite. Also
a_{n}>a_{n+1}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, if and only if
b_{n}>0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, which follows readily from (
1
). Consequently, we need to show that
J
given by (
2
) is positive semi-definite if and only if
b_{n}>0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Suppose that
J
is positive semi-definite, i.e.,
\langle x,Jx\rangle\geq 0
, for all
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits J
. It particularly follows that

\det J_{n}\geq 0,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

where
J_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n}
denotes the
n\times n
section of the matrix
\mathcal{J}
, i.e.,
J_{n}=P_{n}JP_{n}\upharpoonleft\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Ran}\nolimits P_{n}
, where
P_{n}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}\nolimits\{e_{0},\dots,e_{n-1}\}
. It is a matter of simple linear algebra to verify that

\det J_{n}=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Taking into account that
b_{n}\neq 0
by definition, the positivity of
b_{n}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, follows by induction.

Suppose, on the contrary, that
b_{n}>0
,
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Decomposition (
4
) implies that, for any
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}\nolimits\{e_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
, one has

\langle x,Jx\rangle=\|\mathcal{B}x\|^{2}\geq 0.

Since
\mathcal{J}
is proper by claim (iii) of Lemma
4
,
J=J_{\min}
. Therefore to any
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits J
, there exists a sequence of vectors
x_{n}\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}\nolimits\{e_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
such that
x_{n}\to x
and
Jx_{n}\to Jx
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, as
n\to\infty
. Then
\langle x_{n},Jx_{n}\rangle\to\langle x,Jx\rangle
, as
n\to\infty
, and the inequality

\langle x,Jx\rangle\geq 0
extends to all
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits J
.
∎

Remark 10
.

Note that, if
c_{n}\neq 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then matrix
\mathcal{C}
from (
3
) is formally invertible with the inverse

\mathcal{C}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{c_{0}}&\frac{1}{c_{1}}&\frac{1}{c_{2}}&\ldots\\
0&\frac{1}{c_{1}}&\frac{1}{c_{2}}&\ldots\\
0&0&\frac{1}{c_{2}}&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

This observation yields the following factorization of an
L
-matrix

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{A}^{T}\cdot\mathcal{A},

where

\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{a_{0}-a_{1}}&0&0&\ldots\\
\sqrt{a_{1}-a_{2}}&\sqrt{a_{1}-a_{2}}&0&\ldots\\
\sqrt{a_{2}-a_{3}}&\sqrt{a_{2}-a_{3}}&\sqrt{a_{2}-a_{3}}&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

provided that
a_{n}>a_{n+1}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
a_{n}\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
. Matrices of this structure appear in literature as terraced matrices
[
25
]
or
C
-matrices
[
8
]
. A decomposition of the Hilbert
L
-matrix into a product of Cesàro matrices is also mentioned in
[
9
]
.

2.3.
Schatten norms and the Fredholm determinant

First, we prove a simple-to-check sufficient condition for an
L
-operator to be in the trace class. For
p\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathscr{J}_{p}
the
p
-th Schatten class and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{J}_{p}}
the
p
-th Schatten norm. In particular,
\mathscr{J}_{1}
is the ideal of trace class operators and
\|A\|_{\mathscr{J}_{1}}=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Tr}\nolimits|A|
.

Theorem 11
.

If a complex sequence
a
fulfills the condition

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{k}|a_{k}|<\infty,

(5)

then the
L
-matrix given by the parameter sequence
a
is a matrix representation of a trace class operator
L
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. Moreover, for any
p\geq 1
, we have the following estimate:

\|L\|_{\mathscr{J}_{p}}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|a_{k}|\left((\mu_{+}(k))^{p/2}+(\mu_{-}(k))^{p/2}\right)^{1/p},

where

\mu_{\pm}(k)=k+\frac{1}{2}\pm\sqrt{k+\frac{1}{4}}.

Proof.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we denote by
F_{k}
the finite rank operator given by the
L
-matrix with parameter sequence
f_{k}=\{\delta_{k,n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, where
\delta_{k,n}=1
, if
k=n
, and
\delta_{k,n}=0
, if
k\neq n
. One readily computes that, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the only nonzero eigenvalues of
F_{k}^{2}
are
\mu_{\pm}(k)
and these are simple. Consequently, for any
p\geq 1
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, one has

\|F_{k}\|_{\mathscr{J}_{p}}=\left((\mu_{+}(k))^{p/2}+(\mu_{-}(k))^{p/2}\right)^{1/p}.

Since
\|F_{0}\|_{\mathscr{J}_{p}}=1
, the last equality remains true also for
k=0
.
Then the assumption (
5
) guarantees that the series

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}F_{k}

converges in the trace class norm and the limit is an operator
L
whose matrix representation coincides with the
L
-matrix with parameter sequence
a
. The second claim from the statement follows from the estimate

\|L\|_{\mathscr{J}_{p}}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|a_{k}|\|F_{k}\|_{\mathscr{J}_{p}}.

∎

Remark 12
.

Let us remark that condition (
5
) is not necessary. Notice that, if
L
is a regular
L
-operator with strictly decreasing parameter sequence
a
, then
L\geq 0
by Proposition
9
and hence

L\in\mathscr{J}_{1}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\|L\|_{\mathscr{J}_{1}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(a_{n}-a_{\infty}\right)<\infty,

where
a_{\infty}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}
. This means that, for example,
a_{n}=1/(n+1)^{\alpha}
is a parameter sequence of a trace class
L
-operator, for any
\alpha>1
, however, condition (
5
) is not fulfilled if
\alpha\in(1,3/2]
.

Recall that for a trace class operator
L
, the Fredholm determinant
\det(1-zL)
, also referred to as the
characteristic function
of
L
, is a well-defined entire function of
z
and its zeros coincides with the reciprocal values of non-zero eigenvalues of
L
together with their multiplicities, see
[
24
, Sec. XIII.17]
. Since there exist formulas for the characteristic function of certain Jacobi operators, see
[
30
,
31
]
, and one may also recall the Fredholm formula for the characteristic function of integral operators
[
27
, Thm. 3.10]
, it is likely that there is a formula for the characteristic function of a trace class
L
-operator expressed directly in terms of its parameter sequence. Such formula might be useful for spectral analysis of trace class
L
-operators, however, a more detailed investigation of these aspects should be a subject of a separate study. At this point, we provide only a limit formula for the characteristic function expressed in terms of the family of orthogonal polynomials corresponding to Jacobi matrix (
2
) and demonstrate its application on an interesting example below.

Let
\{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
denotes the sequence of monic orthogonal polynomials determined by (
2
), i.e.,

p_{n}(z):=\det(z-J_{n}),

(6)

where
J_{n}:=P_{n}JP_{n}\upharpoonleft\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Ran}\nolimits P_{n}
and
P_{n}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}\nolimits\{e_{0},\dots,e_{n-1}\}
. Alternatively, polynomials
p_{n}
are determined recursively by the recurrence

p_{n+1}(z)=(z-b_{n-1}-b_{n})p_{n}(z)-b_{n-1}^{2}p_{n-1}(z),\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

with the initial conditions
p_{0}(z)=1
and
p_{1}(z)=z-b_{0}
. Recall (
1
).

Theorem 13
.

Suppose
L
be a regular trace class
L
-operator with parameter sequence
a
. Then

\det(1-zL)=\lim_{n\to\infty}(-1)^{n}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(a_{j-1}-a_{j})\right]\!\left(a_{n-1}p_{n}(z)+a_{n}b_{n-1}p_{n-1}(z)\right)\!,

for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

First, recall that, if
L\in\mathscr{J}_{1}
, then

\det(1-zL)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\det(1-zP_{n}LP_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\det(1-zL_{n}),

where
L_{n}
denotes the
n\times n
section of the
L
-matrix
\mathcal{L}
, analogously as for
J_{n}
. The identity operator
1
acts either on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
or
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
depending on the context without being explicitly distinguished in the notation. Thus, in the remaining part of the proof, it suffices to verify the identity

\det(1-zL_{n})=(-1)^{n}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(a_{j-1}-a_{j})\right]\!\left(a_{n-1}p_{n}(z)+a_{n}b_{n-1}p_{n-1}(z)\right).

(7)

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose additionally that
a_{n-1}\neq 0
. This means that
L_{n}
is invertible since

\det L_{n}=a_{n-1}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(a_{j-1}-a_{j}).

(8)

It is straightforward to check that

L_{n}J_{n}=1+\frac{a_{n}b_{n-1}}{a_{n-1}}L_{n}e_{n}e_{n}^{T},

where we denote
e_{n}:=(0,\dots,0,1)^{T}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
with some abuse of notation. It follows that

L_{n}^{-1}=J_{n}-\frac{a_{n}b_{n-1}}{a_{n-1}}e_{n}e_{n}^{T}.

(9)

In view of (
8
) and (
9
), we have

\displaystyle\det(1-zL_{n})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}z^{n}\det(L_{n})\det\!\left(1-\frac{1}{z}L_{n}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}z^{n}a_{n-1}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(a_{j-1}-a_{j})\right]\det\!\left(1-\frac{1}{z}J_{n}+\frac{a_{n}b_{n-1}}{za_{n-1}}e_{n}e_{n}^{T}\right)

Next, by applying the identity

\det(A+cd^{T})=\left(1+d^{T}A^{-1}c\right)\det A,

(10)

which holds true for any
c,d\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and invertible matrix
A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n}
, we see that
\det(1-zL_{n})

equals

(-1)^{n}z^{n}a_{n-1}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(a_{j-1}-a_{j})\right]\left[1+\frac{a_{n}b_{n-1}}{za_{n-1}}e_{n}^{T}\left(1-\frac{1}{z}J_{n}\right)^{-1}e_{n}\right]\det\!\left(1-\frac{1}{z}J_{n}\right),

provided that
z^{-1}\notin\sigma(J_{n})
. If we also recall the well-know relation between the inverse of an invertible matrix
A
and the adjugate matrix of
A
, which particularly implies that

e_{n}^{T}Ae_{n}=\frac{\det A[n]}{\det A},

where
A[n]
denotes the matrix
A
whose last column and row are deleted, we obtain

\displaystyle\det(1-zL_{n})

\displaystyle=(-z)^{n}a_{n-1}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(a_{j-1}-a_{j})\right]\left[\det\!\left(1-\frac{1}{z}J_{n}\right)+\frac{a_{n}b_{n-1}}{za_{n-1}}\det\!\left(1-\frac{1}{z}J_{n-1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(a_{j-1}-a_{j})\right]\left[a_{n-1}\det\!\left(z-J_{n}\right)+a_{n}b_{n-1}\det\!\left(z-J_{n-1}\right)\right].

If the last expression is written in terms of the orthogonal polynomials (
6
), we arrive at (
7
). Clearly, the additional assumptions
a_{n-1}\neq 0
and
z^{-1}\notin\sigma(J_{n})
imposed during the derivation can be removed and formula (
7
) extends to all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
and arbitrary sequence
a
.
∎

We conclude this section by an interesting example of a trace class
L
-operator whose characteristic function can be computed with the aid of Theorem
13
in terms of well-known special functions.

Example 14
.

We investigate the
L
-operator
L
with exponential matrix entries:

\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}1&q&q^{2}&\dots\\
q&q&q^{2}&\dots\\
q^{2}&q^{2}&q^{2}&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

i.e.,
a_{n}=q^{n}
, where
q\in(0,1)
. By Proposition
9
and Theorem
11
,
L
is a positive semi-definite trace class operator. By (
1
), we have

b_{n}=\frac{1}{a_{n+1}-a_{n}}=\frac{q^{-n}}{1-q}

for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Define also
b_{-1}:=q/(1-q)
. Our goal is to make use of known asymptotic properties of the
q
-Chebyshev polynomials studied by Ismail and Mulla in
[
13
]
to deduce an asymptotic formula for the orthogonal polynomials
p_{n}(x)=\det(x-J_{n})
, for
n\to\infty
. Then an application of Theorem
13
provides us with the characteristic function of
L
. To this end, we introduce the rank-one perturbation matrix

\tilde{J}_{n}:=J_{n}+b_{-1}e_{0}e_{0}^{T},

the corresponding orthogonal polynomials
\tilde{p}_{n}(x):=\det(x-\tilde{J}_{n})
, and also the associated orthogonal polynomials
\tilde{q}_{n-1}(x):=\det(x-\tilde{J}_{n}[1])
, where
\tilde{J}_{n}[1]
is the matrix
\tilde{J}_{n}
with deleted first row and column. Recalling once more identity (
10
), we have

p_{n}(x)=\left(1+b_{-1}e_{0}^{T}(x-\tilde{J}_{n}e_{0})^{-1}e_{0}\right)\det\left(x-\tilde{J}_{n}\right)=\tilde{p}_{n}(x)+b_{-1}\tilde{q}_{n-1}(x),

(11)

where we have used the identity

e_{0}^{T}(x-\tilde{J}_{n}e_{0})^{-1}e_{0}=\frac{\tilde{q}_{n-1}(x)}{\tilde{p}_{n}(x)}.

By using the three-term recurrences for polynomials
\tilde{p}_{n}
and
\tilde{q}_{n}
, one can relate them to the
q
-Chebyshev polynomials defined by the recurrence

\theta^{(a)}_{n+1}(x;q)=(2x-aq^{n})\theta^{(a)}_{n}(x;q)-\theta^{(a)}_{n-1}(x;q),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

and initial conditions
\theta^{(a)}_{-1}(x;q)=0
and
\theta^{(a)}_{0}(x;q)=1
, where
a
is a real parameter. Namely, one arrives at formulas

\tilde{p}_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}q^{-n(n-2)/2}}{(1-q)^{n}}\theta_{n}^{\left((q^{-1/2}-q^{1/2})x\right)}\left(\frac{q^{1/2}+q^{-1/2}}{2};q\right)

(12)

and

\tilde{q}_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}q^{-n^{2}/2}}{(1-q)^{n}}\theta_{n}^{\left((q^{-1/2}-q^{1/2})qx\right)}\left(\frac{q^{1/2}+q^{-1/2}}{2};q\right).

(13)

In
[
13
, Thm. 2.1]
, it was proved that, for
x\notin[-1,1]
, one has the asymptotic formula

\theta^{(a)}_{n}(x;q)=A^{n}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k(k-1)/2}(-aB)^{k}}{(B/A;q)_{k+1}(q;q)_{k}}\right]\left(1+o(1)\right),\quad n\to\infty,

where
A
and
B
are the roots of the quadratic equation
1-2xt+t^{2}=0
such that
|A|\geq|B|
and

(\alpha;q)_{k}=(1-\alpha)(1-\alpha q)\dots(1-\alpha q^{k-1})
is the
q
-Pochhammer symbol.
If applied in (
12
) and (
13
) and using (
11
), we compute the asymptotic expansion

\displaystyle p_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n}q^{-n(n-1)/2}}{(1-q)^{n}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k(k-1)/2}}{(q;q)_{k}^{2}}(1-q)^{k}(-x)^{k}\right)\left[1+o(1)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n}q^{-n(n-1)/2}}{(1-q)^{n}}{}_{1}\phi_{1}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{0}{q}\biggl|\,q;\,(1-q)x\biggr)\left[1+o(1)\right],\quad n\to\infty,

where we have used the standard notation for the basic hypergeometric function, see
[
11
]
.
Now, an application of Theorem
13
and simple manipulations yield the desired formula:

\det(1-zL)={}_{1}\phi_{1}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{0}{q}\biggl|\,q;\,(1-q)z\biggr),\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

(14)

Characteristic function (
14
) can be identified with the third Jackson
q
-Bessel function, also known as the Hahn–Exton
q
-Bessel function, of order zero

J_{0}^{(3)}(z;q):={}_{1}\phi_{1}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{0}{q}\biggl|\,q;\,qz^{2}\biggr).

Properties of the third Jackson
q
-Bessel functions were intensively studied in literature
[
3
,
17
,
18
,
28
,
32
]
. Concerning their zeros, they are not known explicitly similarly to the case of classical Bessel functions. Nevertheless, properties of these zeros, that are readily related to the spectrum of
L
, were also investigated in separate works; see, for instance,
[
33
, Prop. A.3]
for an asymptotic formula for the zeros and
[
1
]
for explicit bounds on the zeros.

3.
Spectral analysis of the Hilbert
L
-operator

Our main goal is a spectral analysis of the Hilbert
L
-operator
L_{\nu}
determined by the parameter sequence

a_{n}(\nu)=\frac{1}{n+\nu},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

for
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}_{0})
. The inverse
J_{\nu}:=L_{\nu}^{-1}
is an unbounded Jacobi operator given by the Jacobi matrix (
2
), where

b_{n}=b_{n}(\nu)=\frac{1}{a_{n}(\nu)-a_{n+1}(\nu)}=(n+\nu)(n+\nu+1),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Operator
J_{\nu}
is self-adjoint which is a consequence of claim (iii) of Lemma
4
. Moreover,
J_{\nu}
is semi-bounded as shows the following lemma.

Lemma 15
.

For all
\phi\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}\nolimits\{e_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
, one has

\langle\phi,J_{\nu}\phi\rangle=\nu|\phi_{0}|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|(n+\nu)\phi_{n}-(n-1+\nu)\phi_{n-1}\right|^{2}.

In particular,
J_{\nu}\geq\min(0,\nu)
.

Proof.

Suppose
\phi\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}\nolimits\{e_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
. Then

\displaystyle\langle\phi,J_{\nu}\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\nu(\nu+1)|\phi_{0}|^{2}-\nu(\nu+1)\overline{\phi_{0}}\phi_{1}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.0pt+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\overline{\phi_{n}}\left[-(n-1+\nu)(n+\nu)\phi_{n-1}+2(n+\nu)^{2}\phi_{n}-(n+\nu)(n+1+\nu)\phi_{n+1}\right]

\displaystyle=\nu|\phi_{0}|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n+\nu)\overline{\phi_{n}}\left[(n+\nu)\phi_{n}-(n-1+\nu)\phi_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 144.0pt-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+\nu)\overline{\phi_{n}}\left[(n+1+\nu)\phi_{n+1}-(n+\nu)\phi_{n}\right].

By shifting the index in the last sum, we arrive at the equation from the statement.
∎

Further, we investigate spectral properties of
J_{\nu}
in more detail. In the analysis, the crucial role is played by the unit argument hypergeometric function

{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}}{b_{1},b_{2}}\biggl|\,1\biggr):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{n}(a_{2})_{n}(a_{3})_{n}}{n!\,(b_{1})_{n}(b_{2})_{n}},

(15)

where
(a)_{n}=a(a+1)\dots(a+n-1)
is the Pochhammer symbol. Function (
15
) is well defined, if
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits(b_{1}+b_{2}-a_{1}-a_{2}-a_{3})>0
and
b_{1},b_{2}\notin-\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The latter condition can be removed by introducing the regularized hypergeometric function

{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}}{b_{1},b_{2}}\biggl|\,1\biggr):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(b_{1})\Gamma(b_{2})}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}}{b_{1},b_{2}}\biggl|\,1\biggr),

which is analytic in each parameter
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},b_{1},b_{2}
, if
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits(b_{1}+b_{2}-a_{1}-a_{2}-a_{3})>0
.

At first, we show that, in the special case
\nu=1
, Jacobi operator
J_{1}
corresponds to a subfamily of the Continuous dual Hahn polynomials whose properties are well known. As a result, the spectral analysis of
J_{1}
readily follows. In the general case, however, one cannot make a direct use of existing results although orthogonal polynomials determined by
J_{\nu}
can be found as a particular case of the five-parameter family studied by Ismail, Letessier, and Valent in
[
12
]
within the context of birth and death processes with quadratic rates. However, in our particular setting, the parameters are out of range of the analysis made in
[
12
]
.

Remark 16
.

Let us explain the last comment in more detail. If we use the notation of
[
12
]
and set

a:=\nu,\quad b:=\nu+1,\quad\alpha:=\nu-1,\quad\beta:=\nu,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\eta:=0,

(16)

then polynomials
P_{n}(x)=P_{n}(x;a,b,\alpha,\beta,\eta)
studied in
[
12
]
coincide with orthogonal polynomials determined by
J_{\nu}
up to a multiplicative constant. However, the approach to derive an asymptotic formula for
P_{n}(x)
, as
n\to\infty
, used in
[
12
]
does not apply in the particular case of parameters (
16
), which is not immediately obvious from the paper. The asymptotic analysis relies on an application of the Darboux method to a convenient generating function of the sequence
P_{n}(x)
, which is, in its turn, found as a solution of a hypergeometric differential equation, see
[
12
, Eqs. (2.1) and (2.2)]
. In the exceptional case of parameters (
16
), however, functions
G_{1}^{\alpha,\beta}
and
G_{2}^{\alpha,\beta}
defined in
[
12
, p. 729]
do not form a fundamental system of the respective ODE, see
[
10
, § 15.10]
for more details. As a result, the generating function is not given by
[
12
, Eq. (2.6)]
. From this point of view, the spectral analysis of
J_{\nu}
is of independent interest since it complements the picture on the corresponding family of orthogonal polynomials.

3.1.
A warm up: Special case
\nu=1
and corollaries

Recall that the Continuous dual Hahn polynomials
S_{n}(x;a,b,c)
is a three-parameter family of hypergeometric orthogonal polynomials listed in the Askey scheme
[
16
, Sec. 9.3]
. They are defined by the formula
[
16
, Eq. 9.3.1]

S_{n}(x^{2};a,b,c):=(a+b)_{n}(a+c)_{n}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n,a+\ii x,a-\ii x}{a+b,a+c}\biggl|\,1\biggr).

In the particular case
a=b=1/2
,
c=3/2
, the sequence of polynomials

P_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{n!(n+1)!}\,S_{n}\!\left(x-\frac{1}{4};\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

fulfills the recurrence

\displaystyle(b_{0}(1)-x)P_{0}(x)-b_{0}(1)P_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-b_{n-1}(1)P_{n-1}(x)+(b_{n-1}(1)+b_{n}(1)-x)P_{n}(x)-b_{n}(1)P_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=0,\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

which follows from the general three-term recurrence for Continuous dual Hahn polynomials
[
16
, Eq. (9.3.4)]
. In other words, the column vector
P(x)=(P_{0}(x),P_{1}(x),\dots)^{T}
fulfills the generalized eigenvalue equation
J_{1}P(x)=xP(x)
. Moreover, we have the orthogonality relation

2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{m}\!\left(\frac{1}{4}+x^{2}\right)P_{n}\!\left(\frac{1}{4}+x^{2}\right)\frac{x\sinh(\pi x)}{\cosh^{2}(\pi x)}\!\left(\frac{1}{4}+x^{2}\right)\!{\rm d}x=\delta_{m,n},\quad m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(17)

which can be deduced from the general orthogonality relation
[
16
, Eq. (9.3.2)]
and well-known special values of the Gamma function, namely
[
10
, Eqs. 5.4.3, 5.4.4]
.

This means that
J_{1}
is unitarily equivalent to the operator of multiplication by the independent variable on
\mathscr{H}:=L^{2}([1/4,\infty),\rho(x){\rm d}x)
, where

\rho(x)=\pi x\frac{\sinh(\pi\sqrt{x-1/4})}{\cosh^{2}(\pi\sqrt{x-1/4})},

via the unitary mapping
U:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\to\mathscr{H}
unambiguously determined by correspondence
U:e_{n}\mapsto P_{n}
,
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Consequently,
\sigma(J_{1})=\sigma_{\text{ac}}(J_{1})=[1/4,\infty)
and the spectrum is simple (which is always the case for self-adjoint Jacobi operators). Since
L_{1}=J_{1}^{-1}
we immediately obtain the following statement.

Proposition 17
.

The spectrum of
L_{1}
is simple, purely absolutely continuous, and equal to the interval
[0,4]
.

Last observation gives us also an information about the absolutely continuous spectrum of
J_{\nu}
, for arbitrary
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}_{0})
. We will also arrive at the same result by direct means below.

Proposition 18
.

For all
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}_{0})
, the absolutely continuous spectrum of
L_{\nu}
is simple and fills the interval
[0,4]
.

Proof.

Let
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}_{0})
be fixed.
Since
L_{1}-L_{\nu}
is an
L
-operator with the parameter sequence

a_{n}(1)-a_{n}(\nu)=\frac{\nu-1}{(n+1)(n+\nu)},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

it is a trace class operator by Theorem
11
. The statement now follows from Proposition
17
and the Kato–Rosenblum Theorem, which implies that the absolutely continuous parts of
L_{1}
and
L_{\nu}
are unitarily equivalent, see
[
15
, Thm. 4.4, p. 540]
.
∎

3.2.
Solutions of the eigenvalue equation
J_{\nu}

At the start, we define a sequence of functions that will be of importance in the spectral analysis of
J_{\nu}
for general
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}_{0})
. Its definition for the full range of the involved parameters requires a certain cautiousness.

First, for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
n+\nu>0
, and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we define

\phi_{n}(z;\nu):=\Gamma(n+\nu+1)\,{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1/2+z, 1/2+z, 3/2+z}{1+2z,n+\nu+3/2+z}\biggl|\,1\biggr).

(18)

Clearly, the condition
n+\nu>0
is fulfilled for
n
sufficiently large and, if
\nu>0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. However, we wish to give meaning to the right hand side of (
18
) also if
n+\nu<0
. One way to do so, is to define
\phi_{n}
recursively for
n<-\nu
by using the following recurrence rule.

Lemma 19
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\nu>0
, and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, one has

-(n-1+\nu)(n+\nu)\phi_{n-1}(z;\nu)+\left[2(n+\nu)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}+z^{2}\right]\phi_{n}(z;\nu)-(n+\nu)(n+1+\nu)\phi_{n+1}(z;\nu)=0.

Proof.

We deduce the recurrence formula from a general identity for the
{}_{3}F_{2}
-functions. If we temporarily denote

F_{i}:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(b_{1}+i)}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}}{b_{1}+i,b_{2}}\biggl|\,w\biggr),

then the identity

(w-1)F_{-1}=(A_{1}+wB_{1})F_{0}+(A_{2}+wB_{2})F_{1}+wB_{3}F_{2},

(19)

holds true for

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=b_{2}-2b_{1},

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle=3b_{1}-a_{1}-a_{2}-a_{3},

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=b_{1}(b_{1}-b_{2}+1),

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle=(2b_{1}+1)(a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3})-3b_{1}^{2}-3b_{1}-1-a_{1}a_{2}-a_{1}a_{3}-a_{2}a_{3},

\displaystyle B_{3}

\displaystyle=(b_{1}-a_{1}+1)(b_{1}-a_{2}+1)(b_{1}-a_{3}+1).

This is one of several contiguous relations for
{}_{3}F_{2}
-functions. Recall that any four distinct contiguous functions
{}_{3}F_{2}
, i.e.,
{}_{3}F_{2}
-functions whose parameters differ by integers, are linearly related. We do not have an exact reference for the particular identity (
19
) but it can be verified straightforwardly by comparing coefficients of the same powers of
w
on both sides of (
19
); see also
[
23
, § 48]
.

To obtain the formula from the statement, it suffices to set
w=1
and

a_{1}=a_{2}=\frac{1}{2}+z,\quad a_{3}=\frac{3}{2}+z,\quad b_{1}=n+\nu+\frac{1}{2}+z,\quad b_{2}=1+2z

in (
19
) and do simple manipulations.
∎

With the aid of Lemma
19
, we can extend the definition of
\phi_{n}(z;\nu)
recursively for
n<-\nu
by equation

\phi_{n}(z;\nu)=\frac{2(n+1+\nu)^{2}-1/4+z^{2}}{(n+\nu)(n+1+\nu)}\phi_{n+1}(z;\nu)-\frac{n+2+\nu}{n+\nu}\phi_{n+2}(z;\nu).

This way,
\phi_{n}(z;\nu)
is defined for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and analytic in
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{-n,-n-1,\dots\}
.

It is useful to have a relatively simple hypergeometric formula like (
18
) for
\phi_{n}(z;\nu)
rather than the recursive definition. This is possible, however, the range of parameter
z
is to be restricted to a half-plane. We make use of the Thomae transformation
[
22
, Eq. 7.4.4.2]

{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a,b,c}{d,e}\biggl|\,1\biggr)=\frac{\Gamma(d+e-a-b-c)}{\Gamma(a)}\,{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{d-a,e-a,d+e-a-b-c}{d+e-a-b,d+e-a-c}\biggl|\,1\biggr),

(20)

which holds true if
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits(d+e-a-b-c)>0
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits a>0
. By putting

a=\frac{3}{2}+z,\quad b=c=\frac{1}{2}+z,\quad d=1+2z,\quad e=n+\nu+\frac{3}{2}+z,

we obtain from (
18
) the equality

\phi_{n}(z;\nu)=\frac{\Gamma(n+\nu)\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}{\Gamma(z+3/2)}\,{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{z-1/2,n+\nu,n+\nu}{n+\nu+z+1/2,n+\nu+z+1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr),

(21)

provided that
n+\nu>0
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>-3/2
. However, the condition
n+\nu>0
is only necessary for the hypergeometric function
{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}
present in definition (
18
) to be well defined. Note that, for
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>-3/2
fixed, the right-hand side of (
21
) is an analytic function in
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{-n,-n-1,\dots\}
. Therefore the right-hand side of (
21
) has to coincide with the recursively extended function
\phi_{n}(z;\nu)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{-n,-n-1,\dots\}
, and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>-3/2
.

One could observe already from Lemma
19
that functions
\phi_{n}(z;\nu)
are almost generalized eigenvectors of the Jacobi operator
J_{\nu}
, therefore they play a crucial role in the spectral analysis of
J_{\nu}
. Their selected properties are summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 20
.

Let
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}_{0})
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

(1)

The semi-infinite column vector
\phi(z;\nu)=(\phi_{0}(z;\nu),\phi_{1}(z;\nu),\dots)^{T}
fulfills the equation

J_{\nu}\phi(z;\nu)=\left(\frac{1}{4}-z^{2}\right)\phi(z;\nu)+\chi(z;\nu)e_{0},

where
\chi(z;\nu):=\nu(\nu-1)\left[\phi_{-1}(z;\nu)-\phi_{0}(z;\nu)\right]
. In addition, we have the hypergeometric representation

\displaystyle\chi(z;\nu)=\frac{\Gamma(\nu)\Gamma(\nu+1)}{\Gamma(z+3/2)}\bigg[

\displaystyle{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{z-1/2,\nu-1,\nu-1}{\nu+z-1/2,\nu+z-1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.0pt-\nu(\nu-1)\,{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{z-1/2,\nu,\nu}{\nu+z+1/2,\nu+z+1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr)\bigg],

(22)

provided that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>-3/2
.

(2)

For
n\to\infty
, the asymptotic expansion

\phi_{n}(z;\nu)=\frac{n^{-z-1/2}}{\Gamma(1+2z)}\left[1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right],

holds, provided that
-2z\notin\mathbb{N}
.

(3)

The Wronskian

W(\phi(z;\nu),\phi(-z;\nu)):=-b_{n}(\nu)\left[\phi_{n+1}(z;\nu)\phi_{n}(-z;\nu)-\phi_{n+1}(-z;\nu)\phi_{n}(z;\nu)\right]

of vectors
\phi(\pm z;\nu)
reads

W(\phi(z;\nu),\phi(-z;\nu))=\frac{\sin(2\pi z)}{\pi}.

Consequently,
\phi(z;\nu)
and
\phi(-z;\nu)
are linearly independent if and only if
2z\notin{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Remark 21
.

More expressions for
\chi(z;\nu)
, even simpler than (
22
), are given in Proposition
22
below.

Proof of Proposition
20
.

1) The first claim follows readily from Lemma
19
and formula (
22
) is obtained from (
21
).

2) For
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\left(-\mathbb{N}/2\right)
fixed and
n
large, we have formula (
18
), which is in a suitable form for the asymptotic analysis. First, from the very definition of the hypergeometric series (
15
), we have

{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1/2+z, 1/2+z, 3/2+z}{1+2z,n+\nu+3/2+z}\biggl|\,1\biggr)=1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),\quad n\to\infty.

Second, the Stirling formula yields

\frac{\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}{\Gamma\left(n+\nu+3/2+z\right)}=n^{-z-1/2}\left[1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right],\quad n\to\infty.

Applying the two asymptotic formulas in (
18
), we arrive at the statement.

3) Since both vectors
\phi(\pm z;\nu)
solve the second order difference equation from Lemma
19
their Wronskian
W(\phi(z;\nu),\phi(-z;\nu))
is an
n
-independent constant, which vanishes if and only if
\phi(z;\nu)
and
\phi(-z;\nu)
are linearly dependent. Therefore we can compute the Wronskian with the aid of the already proven claim 2). We have

\displaystyle W(\phi(z;\nu),\phi(-z;\nu))

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}(n+\nu)(n+\nu+1)\,\frac{n^{-z-1/2}(n+1)^{z-1/2}-n^{z-1/2}(n+1)^{-z-1/2}}{\Gamma(1+2z)\Gamma(1-2z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2z}{\Gamma(1+2z)\Gamma(1-2z)}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2z)\Gamma(1-2z)}=\frac{\sin(2\pi z)}{\pi},

for
2z\notin{\mathbb{Z}}
, where we have used the multiplication formula
[
10
, Eq. 5.5.3]

\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi z)}.

By definition, the function
z\mapsto W(\phi(z;\nu),\phi(-z;\nu))
is entire and hence the obtained formula extends to all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
∎

Function
\chi(z;\nu)
defined in claim 1) of Proposition
20
will play a role of the characteristic function of
J_{\nu}
. Three more hypergeometric expressions for
\chi(z;\nu)
, which will be needed below, are given in the next statement.

Proposition 22
.

The following hypergeometric representations hold:

(1)

For
\nu>0
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, one has

\chi(z;\nu)=\left(z+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma(\nu+1)\,{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{z-1/2,z+1/2,z+3/2}{2z+1,z+\nu+1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr).

(23)

(2)

For
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}_{0})
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>-1/2
, one has

\chi(z;\nu)=\frac{(z+1/2)\Gamma(\nu)\Gamma(\nu+1)}{\Gamma(z+1/2)}\,{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\nu-1,\nu+1,z+1/2}{z+\nu+1/2,z+\nu+1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr).

(24)

(3)

For
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}_{0})
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>-1/2
, one has

\chi(z;\nu)=\frac{(z+1/2)\Gamma(\nu)\Gamma(\nu+1)}{\Gamma(z+1/2)}\,{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\nu,\nu,z+1/2}{z+\nu-1/2,z+\nu+3/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr).

(25)

Proof.

1) First, applying the transformation
[
22
, Eq. 7.4.4.1]

{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a,b,c}{d,e}\biggl|\,1\biggr)=\frac{\Gamma(d+e-a-b-c)}{\Gamma(d-c)}\,{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{e-a,e-b,c}{d+e-a-b,e}\biggl|\,1\biggr),

(26)

which holds true if
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits(d+e-a-b-c)>0
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits(d-c)>0
, in (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\chi(z;\nu)

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\nu+1)\bigg[{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{z-1/2,z+1/2,z+1/2}{2z+1,z+\nu-1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr)

\displaystyle\hskip 139.0pt-(\nu-1)\,{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{z-1/2,z+1/2,z+1/2}{2z+1,z+\nu+1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\nu+1)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z-1/2)_{k}(z+1/2)_{k}^{2}}{k!\,\Gamma(2z+1+k)\Gamma(z+\nu+1/2+k)}\left[z+\nu-\frac{1}{2}+k-(\nu-1)\right]

for
\nu>0
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>-3/2
. This yields formula (

) for

and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>-3/2

and

. Since the right-hand side of (

2) If we apply (

∎

3.3.
Spectral analysis of
J_{\nu}

With the aid of Proposition
20
, we may determine the point spectrum of
J_{\nu}
.

Proposition 23
.

For all
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}_{0})
, we have

\sigma_{\text{p}}(J_{\nu})=\left\{\frac{1}{4}-x^{2}\;\bigg|\;\chi(x;\nu)=0,\;x>0\right\}.

In addition,
0\notin\sigma_{\text{p}}(J_{\nu})
.

Proof.

We write the spectral parameter
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
in the form
\lambda=1/4-z^{2}
, for
z\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup{\rm i}{\mathbb{R}}
. In fact, without loss of generality, it suffices to consider the following four cases:

i)\,z={\rm i}y,\,y>0,\qquad ii)\,z=x,\,x>0,\qquad iii)\,z=0,\qquad iv)\,z=\frac{1}{2}.

Ad i) By Proposition
20
, vectors
\phi(\pm{\rm i}y;\nu)
are linearly independent for all
y>0
and their linear combination

\psi(y;\nu):=\chi(-{\rm i}y;\nu)\phi({\rm i}y;\nu)-\chi({\rm i}y;\nu)\phi(-{\rm i}y;\nu)

is a solution of the eigenvalue equation
J_{\nu}\psi(y;\nu)=\left(1/4+y^{2}\right)\psi(y;\nu)
which is determined uniquely up to a multiplicative constant. Vector
\psi(y;\nu)
is nontrivial since

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(y;\nu)

\displaystyle=\nu(\nu-1)\left[\phi_{-1}(-{\rm i}y;\nu)\phi_{0}({\rm i}y;\nu)-\phi_{-1}({\rm i}y;\nu)\phi_{0}(-{\rm i}y;\nu)\right]=W\left(\phi(-{\rm i}y;\nu),\phi({\rm i}y;\nu)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sinh(2\pi y)}{{\rm i}\pi}\neq 0,

for
y>0
. In other words, the coefficients
\chi(\pm{\rm i}y;\nu)
cannot vanish simultaneously. Moreover, it follows from claim 2) of Proposition
20
that

\psi_{n}(y;\nu)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left[\frac{\chi(-{\rm i}y;\nu)}{\Gamma(1+2{\rm i}y)}\,n^{-{\rm i}y}-\frac{\chi({\rm i}y;\nu)}{\Gamma(1-2{\rm i}y)}\,n^{{\rm i}y}\right]\!\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\!,\quad n\to\infty.

Hence
\psi(y;\nu)\notin\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, for all
y>0
. Consequently,
(1/4,\infty)\cap\sigma_{{\rm p}}(J_{\nu})=\emptyset
.

Ad ii) By the asymptotic expansion from claim 2) of Proposition
20
,
\phi(x;\nu)
is non-trivial and square summable for all
x>0
. Moreover, we have
J_{\nu}\phi(x;\nu)=\left(1/4-x^{2}\right)\phi(x;\nu)
if and only if
\chi(x;\nu)=0
. Thus,
1/4-x^{2}\in\sigma_{{\rm p}}(J_{\nu})
, for
x>0
, if and only if
\chi(x;\nu)=0
.

Ad iii) Next, we verify that
1/4\notin\sigma_{{\rm p}}(J_{\nu})
. Note that Proposition
20
does not provide us with two linearly independent solutions of the difference equation

-(n-1+\nu)(n+\nu)\phi_{n-1}+\left[2(n+\nu)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right]\phi_{n}-(n+\nu)(n+1+\nu)\phi_{n+1}=0,

(27)

but only one
\phi=\phi(0;\nu)
. The second solution of (
27
) can be identified in terms of the hypergeometric functions, too, namely

\xi_{n}=\Gamma(-n-\nu)\Gamma(-n-\nu+1)\,{}_{3}\tilde{F}_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-1/2,-n-\nu,-n-\nu}{-n-\nu+1/2,-n-\nu+1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

In contrast to
\phi_{n}
, a direct asymptotic analysis of solution
\xi_{n}
, for
n\to\infty
, is a difficult task. However, in order to verify that
1/4\notin\sigma_{{\rm p}}(J_{\nu})
, an explicit asymptotic formula is not needed. At this point, it is more straightforward to apply the method of successive approximations to the difference equation (
27
); see
[
34
]
.

The method of successive approximations implies that there are two linearly independent solutions of (
27
) with the asymptotic behavior

\phi_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left[1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right]\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\xi_{n}=\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\left[1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right],

(28)

for
n\to\infty
; more details are given in Appendix
A.1
for reader’s convenience. Now, it is obvious from (
28
) that there is no nontrivial linear combination of
\phi
and
\xi
, which belongs to
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. It means that
1/4\notin\sigma_{\text{p}}(J_{\nu})
.

Ad iv) The claim
0\notin\sigma_{{\rm p}}(J_{\nu})
is a general fact, which follows from Lemma
4
. Nevertheless, we can also check that
\chi(1/2;\nu)\neq 0
by a direct computation. To this end, one uses (
22
) getting

\chi\left(\frac{1}{2};\nu\right)=\Gamma(\nu)\Gamma(\nu+1)\left[\frac{1}{\Gamma^{2}(\nu)}-\frac{\nu(\nu-1)}{\Gamma^{2}(\nu+1)}\right]=1.

∎

Our next goal is to compute the Weyl
m
-function of
J_{\nu}
,

m(\lambda;\nu):=\langle e_{0},(J_{\nu}-\lambda)^{-1}e_{0}\rangle,

for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
, and deduce the spectral measure
\mu_{\nu}:=\langle e_{0},E_{J_{\nu}}e_{0}\rangle
, where
E_{J_{\nu}}
is the projection-valued spectral measure of the self-adjoint operator
J_{\nu}
. We refer the reader to
[
2
,
29
]
for general theory of Jacobi operators. In order to deduce a formula for the Weyl
m
-function, we make use of the formula for a general formula for the Green kernel of a Jacobi operator, which, if applied to
J_{\nu}
, reads

\langle e_{m},(J_{\nu}-\lambda)^{-1}e_{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{W(\phi,\psi)}\begin{cases}\psi_{m}\phi_{n},&\quad\mbox{ if }m\leq n,\\
\psi_{n}\phi_{m},&\quad\mbox{ if }n\leq m,\end{cases}

(29)

where
\psi
is a generalized solution to the eigenvalue equation
J_{\nu}\psi=\lambda\psi
, and
\phi
is a square summable solution of the recurrence from Lemma
19
with
\lambda=1/4-z^{2}
.

Proposition 24
.

Suppose
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus(-\mathbb{N}_{0})
,
\lambda=1/4-z^{2}
, and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>0,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits z>0
. Then we have

m(\lambda;\nu)=\frac{\phi_{0}(z;\nu)}{\chi(z;\nu)}.

Proof.

Note that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda<0
for
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>0,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits z>0
.
It follows from Proposition
20
that the vector

\psi(z;\nu):=\chi(-z;\nu)\phi(z;\nu)-\chi(z;\nu)\phi(-z;\nu)

solves the eigenvalue equation

J_{\nu}\psi(z;\nu)=\lambda\psi(z;\nu)
and also that
\phi(z;\nu)
, the solution of the recurrence from Lemma
19
, belongs to
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
since we suppose
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>0

Proposition 26

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\to 1/4+x^{2}\\

Corollary 27
.

For any

3.4.

As our initial motivation is to determine

Lemma 28
.

The following properties hold:

(1)

The function

\nu\mapsto\frac{\Gamma(x+\nu+1/2)}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}\chi(x;\nu)

is strictly increasing on
(0,\infty)
, if

(0,\infty)

(2)

. In particular,
\chi(\,\cdot\,;\nu)
has no positive zero, if
\nu\geq 1/2

(3)

For all
\nu\in(0,1/2]

x\mapsto\frac{\Gamma(x+1/2)\Gamma^{2}(x+\nu+1/2)}{x+1/2}\,\chi(x;\nu)

is strictly increasing on
[0,1/2]

Proof.

i) According to (
23
), we have

\frac{\Gamma(x+\nu+1/2)}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}\chi(x;\nu)=\frac{x+1/2}{\Gamma(2x+1)}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{x-1/2,x+1/2,x+3/2}{2x+1,x+\nu+1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr),

for all
x\geq 0
and
\nu>0
. Hence it suffices to prove that the function

\nu\mapsto{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{x-1/2,x+1/2,x+3/2}{2x+1,x+\nu+1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr)

has the monotonicity property of claim 1).
By definition (
15
), we have the series representation

{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{x-1/2,x+1/2,x+3/2}{2x+1,x+\nu+1/2}\biggl|\,1\biggr)\!=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(x-1/2)_{k}(x+1/2)_{k}(x+3/2)_{k}}{k!\,(2x+1)_{k}}\frac{1}{(x+\nu+1/2)_{k}}.

Now, it suffices to note that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and all
x\geq 0
, the function

\nu\mapsto\frac{1}{(x+\nu+1/2)_{k}}

is strictly decreasing on
(0,\infty)
while, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the term

\frac{(x-1/2)_{k}(x+1/2)_{k}(x+3/2)_{k}}{k!\,(2x+1)_{k}}

is negative, if
x\in[0,1/2)
, and positive, if
x>1/2
.

ii) By inspection of the expression

\chi(x;\nu)=\frac{(x+1/2)\Gamma(\nu)\Gamma(\nu+1)}{\Gamma(x+1/2)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\nu)_{k}^{2}(x+1/2)_{k}}{k!\,\Gamma(x+\nu-1/2+k)\Gamma(x+\nu+3/2+k)},

which follows from (
25
), we see that each term is positive provided that
x\geq 0
,
\nu>0
and
x+\nu>1/2
. In the limiting case
x+\nu=1/2
, the first term of the sum vanishes while the remaining terms remain all positive.

iii) By making use of formula (
24
), we obtain

\frac{\Gamma(x+1/2)\Gamma^{2}(x+\nu+1/2)}{x+1/2}\,\chi(x;\nu)=\Gamma(\nu)\Gamma(\nu+1)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\nu-1)_{k}(\nu+1)_{k}}{k!}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}g_{j}(x;\nu),

where

g_{j}(x;\nu):=\frac{x+j+1/2}{(x+\nu+j+1/2)^{2}}.

Since, for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\nu\in(0,1/2]
, and
x>0
, we have

\frac{\partial g_{j}}{\partial x}(x;\nu)=-\frac{x-\nu+j+1/2}{(x+\nu+j+1/2)^{3}}\leq-\frac{x}{(x+\nu+j+1/2)^{3}}<0,

functions

are strictly decreasing for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Noting also that
(\nu-1)_{k}<0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\nu\in(0,1)
, we obtain the claim.
∎

A.1.
The method of successive approximations

The full description of the method of successive approximations is explained in

x_{0}

.

y_{0}
.

Then equation (
A.1
) has two linearly independent solutions
\phi
and
\xi
of asymptotic behavior:

\phi_{n}=x_{0}^{n}n^{y_{0}}\left[1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right]\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\xi_{n}=\log(n)\,x_{0}^{n}n^{y_{0}}\left[1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right],

(A.2)

as
n\to\infty
.

If we write equation (
