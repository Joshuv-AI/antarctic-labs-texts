Abstract
. For 1 < p < ∞ 1<p<\infty , we show that the Rosenthal X p , w X_{p,w} spaces and the Bourgain-Rosenthal-Schechtman R ω p R_{\omega}^{p} space have the factorization property and the primary factorization property.

1.
Introduction

This paper is motivated by the study of conditions that when placed on a bounded linear operator
T
on a Banach space
X
then
T
is a factor of the identity operator
I
on
X
, that is there exist bounded linear operators
L,R\colon X\to X
such that
I=LTR
. Factors of the identity have played a crucial role, e.g., in the study of decompositions of classical Banach spaces (i.e., prime and primary spaces) or the study of closed ideals in operator algebras
\mathcal{L}(X)
(if such an ideal contains a factor of the identity then it is trivial). We place our focus on complemented subspaces of
L_{p}
, for a fixed
1<p<\infty
. Specifically, we study operators on the Rosenthal spaces
X_{p,w}

[
14
]
and the first infinite dimensional Bourgain-Rosenthal-Schechtman
R_{\omega}^{p}
space
[
3
]
. Although all these spaces are isomorphic as Banach spaces (see
[
1
]
or Proposition
4.10
), they have non-equivalent natural Schauder bases. Studying
R_{\omega}^{p}
, that has a more complicated basis, is a starting point for a similar research program for higher order Bourgain-Rosenthal-Schechtman spaces
R_{\alpha}^{p}
,
\alpha<\omega_{1}
.

The Rosenthal
X_{p,w}
space is the first known complemented subspace of
L_{p}
, apart from
L_{p}
,
\ell_{2}
,
\ell_{p}
,
\ell_{2}\oplus\ell_{p}
and
\ell_{p}(\ell_{2})
. It is the closed linear span in
L_{p}
of an independent 3-valued symmetric sequence
(f_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that, if
p>2
,
(\|f_{n}\|_{2}/\|f_{n}\|_{p})_{n=1}^{\infty}
vanishes at a slow rate (when
p<2
, we consider its conjugate exponent). The spaces
R_{\alpha}^{p}
,
\alpha<\omega_{1}
, are defined by transfinite recursion and each of them has a Schauder basis given by a 3-valued symmetric martingale difference sequence. An appropriate cofinal subset of
\{R_{\alpha}^{p}:\alpha<\omega_{1}\}
, yields uncountably many pairwise non-isomorphic complemented subspaces of
L_{p}
. We focus on
R_{\omega}^{p}
, more accurately, on its isomorphic hyperplane of all mean-zero random variables,
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
. In all aforementioned cases, the complementation in
L_{p}
is witnessed via an orthogonal projection that is also well defined on
L_{p}
.

The first result we prove is the following.

Theorem
.

For
1<p<\infty
,
X_{p,w}
and
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
with their respective standard bases have the factorization property.

This means the following. If
X
is one of the above spaces and
(e_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
denotes its standard basis, then every bounded linear operator
T\colon X\to X
with large diagonal, i.e., such that
\inf_{n}|e_{n}^{\ast}(T(e_{n}))|=\delta>0
, is a factor of the identity.

The factorization property of spaces with bases was first studied by Andrew in 1979
[
2
]
, although it was defined slightly differently and called the perturbation property. There, he showed that
L_{p}
,
1<p<\infty
, with the Haar system has the factorization property. Several other classical spaces have been shown to have it, e.g.,
H_{p}(H_{q})
, for
1\leq p,q<\infty
with the biparameter Haar system in
[
7
]
and
L_{1}
with the Haar system in
[
9
]
.

The second result that we prove is as follows.

Theorem
.

For
1<p<\infty
, the spaces
X_{p,w}
and
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
have the primary factorization property.

The above means that for every bounded linear operator
T\colon X\to X
, where
X
is one of these spaces, either
T
or
I-T
is a factor of the identity. Note that the statement of this theorem is basis-free, and the spaces
X_{p,w}
and
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
are isomorphic. However, the proof relies on the basis. Specifically, we work with
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
and its basis as a starting point for proving the factorization property of the higher complexity spaces
R_{\alpha}^{p}
,
\alpha<\omega_{1}
.

It has long been known that Banach spaces satisfying the primary factorization property together with Pełczyński’s accordion property are primary. Thus, it has been implicitly or explicitly long studied for classical Banach spaces, often using their standard bases. Regarding the Haar system, a starting point for this can be found in the 1973 paper of Gamlen and Gaudet
[
5
]
, the famous Enflo-Starbird 1979 paper on
L_{1}

[
4
]
, and the Enflo-Maurey approach from 1975, for
1<p<\infty

[
12
]
. More recently, in 2022, in the paper
[
10
]
the biparameter Haar system of
L_{1}(L_{p})
was used to show primariness for this space.

In most of the above papers given a bounded linear operator
T\colon X\to X
, it is shown that there are
L,R\colon X\to X
and a “simpler” operator
S\colon X\to X
such that
LTR
is approximately
S
. This is achieved by using as
R
a conditional expectation (or very similar) operator
onto a subspace
Y
isomorphic to
X
via an
A\colon Y\to X
and
L=A^{-1}R
. This process is referred to as reducing
T
to
S
. After several successive reductions, usually,
T
is reduced to a scalar operator, i.e., an operator
\lambda I
, for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

The reason why the conditional expectation has been so fruitful is that in a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},P)
, if
(\tilde{h}_{I})_{I\in\mathcal{D}}
is a distributional copy of the Haar system, (for notation, see Section
2
) then
E(f)=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}}|I|^{-1}\langle\tilde{h}_{I},f\rangle\tilde{h}_{I}
is the conditional expectation onto the subspace of
\sigma(\tilde{h}_{I})_{I\in\mathcal{D}}
-measurable functions. Something similar is true for biparameter spaces
X(Y)
where the biparameter Haar system is a Schauder basis (see e.g.,
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
). The spaces
X_{p,w}
and
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
also each have a basis
(f_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that a similar approach works replacing the conditional expectation with the operator
P(f)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|f_{n}\|_{2}^{-2}\langle f_{n},f\rangle f_{n}
. Although this is not a conditional expectation, it is an orthogonal projection. For our approach, we develop a language of orthogonally complemented subspaces and orthogonal factors of operators. This was also done in
[
1
]
, but at a lesser depth.

The proof of the factorization property of
X_{p,w}
is the least involved part of our paper and it is thus presented first. That being said, it relies on some machinery developed in
[
9
]
, namely of strategical reproducibility of Schauder bases. This means that in a certain two-player game, one of the players has a winning strategy allowing them to reproduce the basis and its biorthogonal sequence in the dual. Using Rosenthal’s analysis of block sequences of the standard basis of
X_{p,w}
, we deduce that it is strategically reproducible and thus it satisfies the factorization property.

The study of
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
is much more involved and it requires repeated use of probabilistic and combinatorial arguments. The space
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
is the closed linear span of a collection
((h_{I}^{n})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
(h_{I}^{n})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}
is a distributional copy of the first
(n-1)
-levels of the Haar system and the collection
\sigma(h_{I}^{n})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is independent. With an appropriate enumeration, this basis is a martingale difference sequence, and thus, by Burkholder’s inequality, it is an unconditional basis of
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
. Because each part
(h_{I}^{n})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}
spans a distributional copy of
L_{p}^{n,0}
, the probabilistic techniques from
[
8
]
apply to this setting. We refine them to construct, for every
T\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
, an operator
D\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
that is diagonal with respect to the basis, and its eigenvalues are averages of the diagonal entries
((|I|^{-1}\langle h_{I}^{n},Th_{I}^{n}\rangle)_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
T
, and show that
T
is an approximate orthogonal factor of
D
(or
T
approximately orthogonally reduces to
D
). This means that, for a prechosen small
\epsilon
, there is a distributional embedding
j\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
such that
\|j^{-1}P_{j(R_{\omega}^{p,0})}Tj-D\|<\epsilon
, where
P_{j(R_{\omega}^{p,0})}
denotes the necessarily bounded orthogonal projection onto the image of
j
. With this, the diagonal factorization property follows relatively easily from the unconditionality of the basis. The proof of the primary factorization property of
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
is based on an orthogonal reduction of an arbitrary diagonal operator to a scalar multiple
\lambda I
of the identity, where
\lambda
is an average of eigenvalues of
T
. Because approximate orthogonal factorization is transitive, every
T\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
is an approximate orthogonal factor of a scalar operator
\lambda I
, where
\lambda
is in the convex hull of the diagonal entries of
T
. The definition of orthogonal factors easily then yields that
I-T
is an approximate orthogonal factor of
(1-\lambda)I
. The primary factorization property then follows easily. The orthogonal reduction of
D
to
\lambda I
is based on a probabilistic future-level stabilization argument from
[
11
]
that has its roots in
[
10
]
, together with some combinatorial stabilization.

The involvement of delicate probabilistic machinery is necessary for dealing with the structure of the basis
(h_{I}^{n})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1},n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Because
X_{p,w}
is isomorphic to
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
, it would have been far simpler to directly show the primary factorization property of
X_{p,w}
. However, our approach has set a path for attempting to show that all spaces
R_{\alpha}^{p}
,
\alpha<\omega_{1}
, satisfy the primary factorization property.

2.
Notation and Concepts

In this section, we introduce some language that will make the paper notationally easier. Moreover, we introduce the concepts of orthogonally complemented spaces (Definition
2.5
) and orthogonal factors (Definition
2.8
) and we give some properties related to these notions.

By operator we mean bounded linear operator and by projection, bounded linear projection. Moreover, by subspace we mean closed linear subspace. Given
A
a subset of a normed linear space, we denote
[A]
the closed linear span of
A
.

Given
L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
, where
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is a probability space and
1\leq p<\infty
, we denote
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}}\colon L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)\to L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
the conditional expectation operator with respect to a
\sigma
-subalgebra
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathcal{A}
and if
f_{1},...,f_{n}
are random variables on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
, we denote
\sigma(f_{1},...,f_{n})
the
\sigma
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
generated by
f_{1},...,f_{n}
. Furthermore, if
f
is a random variable on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
, we denote
\text{dist}(f)
the distribution of
f
. A space
L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is called a
L_{p}
-space. We denote
L_{p}
the space
L_{p}[0,1]
with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra and the Lebesque measure.

In this paper, we consider
1<p<\infty
and
p
and
q
are conjugate exponents. Moreover, denote
p^{\ast}=\max\{p,q\}
. For a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
we consider the standard duality pairing
L_{q}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)\times L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
, i.e, for
g\in L_{q}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
and
f\in L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
we let
\langle g,f\rangle=\int_{\Omega}gfd\mu
. Furthermore, if
A
is a set, we denote
\text{card}(A)
the cardinality of
A
and
\chi_{A}
the characteristic function of
A
. Finally, if
r
is a real number, we denote
[r]
the integer part of
r
.

Let us recall the notion of a factor of the identity that is used in the definitions of the factorization and primary factorization property.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and
T\colon X\to X
be an operator.

(a)

We say that
T
is a factor of the identity operator
I
on
X
if there are operators
A,B\colon X\to X
with
I=ATB
.

(b)

For
K>0
we say that
T
is a
K
-factor of the identity operator
I
on
X
if there are operators
A,B\colon X\to X
with

\displaystyle\|A\|\|B\|\leq K\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;I=ATB.

The following is from
[
7
]
. Recall that every operator on a space with a basis can be identified with an infinite scalar matrix. A basis is said to have the factorization property if every operator whose matrix representation has diagonal entries bounded away from zero is a factor of the identity.

Definition 2.2
.

Let X be a Banach space with a basis
(e_{n})
.

(a)

If an operator T on X satisfies
\inf_{n}|e_{n}^{\ast}(T(e_{n}))|>0
, then we say that T has large diagonal.

(b)

We say that the basis
(e_{n})
has the factorization property if whenever T is an operator on X with large diagonal, then
T
is a factor of the identity operator on X.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be subspaces of
L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
and
L_{p}(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
respectively, where
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
and
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
are probability spaces. We say that
X
and
Y
are distributionally isomorphic if there exists a linear isomorphism
T\colon X\to Y
with
\text{dist}(Tx)=\text{dist}(x)
, for all
x\in X
.

We will use the following remark later, in Remark
2.7

(a)
. After definining the notion of orthogonally complemented subspaces, this will be used to show that a distributional copy of such a space is also orthogonally complemented.

Remark 2.4
.

If
X
,
Y
and
T\colon X\to Y
are as in Definition
2.3
, then using the multivariable characteristic function we get that there exist
\sigma
-subalgebras
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
respectively, and a distributional onto isomorphism
j\colon L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{B},\mu)\to L_{p}(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{B}^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
such that
j(x)=T(x)
, for all
x\in X
.

In the following definition, we introduce the notion of an orthogonally complemented space. This concept was introduced for
p>2
in
[
1
]
, but not used significantly.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a probability space and
C>0
.

(a)

An orthogonal projection
P\colon L_{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)\to L_{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is called
p
-
C
-bounded if for every
f\in L_{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)\cap L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
we have

\displaystyle\|P(f)\|_{p}\leq C\|f\|_{p}.

(b)

A subspace
X
of
L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is called
C
-orthogonally complemented if there exists a
p
-
C
-bounded projection
P
such that
X
is the closure of
P(L_{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)\cap L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu))
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{p}
. In this case, we denote
P_{X}
the projection on
L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
onto
X
, which is given by extending
P\restriction_{L_{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)\cap L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)}
to
L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
. Note
\|P_{X}\|\leq C
.

Next we recall the definition of a martingale difference sequence. The basis of the Bourgain-Rosenthal-Schechtman
R_{\omega}^{p}
space is a martingale difference sequence.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
(f_{n})
be a sequence in
L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
, where
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is a probability space. The sequence
(f_{n})
is called a martingale difference sequence if
\mathbb{E}(f_{n}|f_{1},...,f_{n-1})=0
, for every
n\geq 2
.

Let us state some properties of orthogonally complemented spaces used in the rest of the paper.

Remark 2.7
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a probability space and
C>0
.

(a)

Every distributional copy of a
C
-orthogonally complemented space is
C
-orthogonally complemented. This follows from Remark
2.4
combined with a conditional expectation operator.

(b)

By duality and self-adjointness, if
P
is a
p
-
C
-bounded projection, then
P
is a
q
-
C
-bounded projection.

(c)

If
X
is a subspace of
L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
and it is

-orthogonally complemented, then the dual space
X^{\ast}
of

is
C
-isomorphic to the closure of
P(L_{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)\cap L_{q}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu))
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{q}
, which is
C
-orthogonally complemented, from
(b)
.

(d)

If
(f_{n})
is a

-valued symmetric martingale difference sequence and

in

is

-orthogonally complemented, then

\displaystyle P_{X}(f)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle\|f_{n}\|_{q}^{-1}f_{n},f\rangle\|f_{n}\|_{p}^{-1}f_{n}.

This is because
\|f_{n}\|_{p}\|f_{n}\|_{q}=\|f_{n}\|_{2}^{2}
(see

In the following definition, we introduce the notion of an approximate orthogonal factor. This is a special case of an operator being
T
an approximate factor of an operator
S
, and the factoring operators relate to an orthogonal projection.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
C>0
and
\epsilon>0
. Let
X
and
Y
be subspaces of
L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
and
L_{p}(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
respectively, where
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
and
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
are probability spaces, and assume that
Y
is
C
-orthogonally complemented. Moreover, let

and

be operators. We say that

is an orthogonal factor of

with error

if there exists a distributional embedding

such that
\|j^{-1}P_{j(Y)}Tj-S\|<\epsilon
. When the error is
\epsilon=0
, we will simply say that

is an orthogonal factor of

.

Remark 2.9
.

Let
T
and

be as in Definition

. If

is an orthogonal factor of

with error
\epsilon>0
, then

is an orthogonal factor of

with error

, where

and

are the identity operators on

respectively. This is because
P_{j(Y)}j=j
and therefore

\displaystyle\|j^{-1}P_{j(Y)}(I_{X}-T)j-(I_{Y}-S)\|<\epsilon.

The following explains the relation between orthogonal factors and projectional factors. The notion of projectional factor can be found in
[
10

, but we do not explicitly use it here.

Remark 2.10
.

Let
C>0
and
\epsilon>0
. Let
X
be a subspace of

, where
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is a probability space, and assume that

is

-orthogonally complemented. Moreover, let

be operators. If

is an orthogonal factor of

with error

, then

is a

. Note

.

In a certain sense, being an approximate orthogonal factor is a transitive property.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
T\colon X\to X
,
S\colon Y\to Y
and
R\colon Z\to Z
be operators, where
X
,
Y
and
Z
are subspaces of possibly different

-spaces, and assume that

is
C
-orthogonally complemented and

is
D
-orthogonally complemented. Furthermore, assume that

is an orthogonal factor of
S
with error
\epsilon_{1}
and
S
is an orthogonal factor of

with error

. Then

is an orthogonal factor of
R
with error
D\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}
.

Proof.

By the assumptions we have that

\displaystyle\|j_{1}^{-1}P_{j_{1}(Y)}Tj_{1}-S\|<\epsilon_{1}

and

\displaystyle\|j_{2}^{-1}P_{j_{2}(Z)}Sj_{2}-R\|<\epsilon_{2},

where
j_{1}\colon Y\to X
and
j_{2}\colon Z\to Y
are distributional embeddings.
Put

and observe that

\displaystyle j_{2}^{-1}P_{j_{2}(Z)}j_{1}^{-1}P_{j_{1}(Y)}Tj_{1}j_{2}=j^{-1}P_{j(Z)}Tj.

Hence,

\displaystyle\|j^{-1}P_{j(Z)}Tj-R\|\leq\|j^{-1}P_{j(Z)}Tj-j_{2}^{-1}P_{j_{2}(Z)}Sj_{2}\|+\|j_{2}^{-1}P_{j_{2}(Z)}Sj_{2}-R\|

\displaystyle\leq\|j_{2}^{-1}\|\|P_{j_{2}(Z)}\|\|j_{1}^{-1}P_{j_{1}(Y)}Tj_{1}-S\|\|j_{2}\|+\|j_{2}^{-1}P_{j_{2}(Z)}Sj_{2}-R\|

\displaystyle\leq D\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}.

Therefore,
T
is an orthogonal factor of
R
with error
D\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}
.

3.
The factorization property of the basis of the
X_{p,w}
space

In this section, we recall the definition of the
X_{p,w}
space (Definition
3.1
) and the Rosenthal
X_{p}
space (Definition
3.3
). In addition, we state some results related to these spaces that can be found in
[
14
]
, and we deduce that the Rosenthal
X_{p}
space is isomorphic to a
7.35(p/\log(p))
-orthogonally complemented subspace of
L_{p}
(Theorem
3.4
). Finally, using the notion of a strategically reproducible basis that can be found in
[
9
]
, we prove that the unit vector basis of the
X_{p,w}
space has the factorization property (Theorem
3.10
). Although all
X_{p,w}
spaces, for
w
satisfying the
(\ast)
property, are mutually isomorphic, their bases are not equivalent. But, we show that all of them have the factorization property. Note
X_{p}
does not have a standard basis.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
2<p<\infty
and
w=(w_{n})
be a sequence of positive reals satisfying the
(\ast)
property :

\displaystyle w_{n}\rightarrow 0\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w_{n}^{2p/(p-2)}=\infty.

Define

\displaystyle X_{p,w}=\{(x_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}:(x_{n})\in\ell_{p}\;\text{and}\;(x_{n}w_{n})\in\ell_{2}\}

and

\displaystyle\|(x_{n})\|_{X_{p,w}}=\max\{\|(x_{n})\|_{p},\|(x_{n}w_{n})\|_{2}\}.

The
(X_{p,w},\|\cdot\|_{X_{p,w}})
space is a Banach space with unconditional and shrinking basis the unit vector basis
(e_{n})
.

The following can be found in
[
14
, Theorem 13]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
2<p<\infty
and
w,w^{\prime}
be sequences of positive reals satisfying the
(\ast)
property. Then
X_{p,w}
is isomorphic to
X_{p,w^{\prime}}
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
2<p<\infty
. The Rosenthal
X_{p}
space is an
X_{p,w}
space, where
w=(w_{n})
is a sequence of positive reals satisfying the
(\ast)
property.

Recall that, for brevity, we denote

The condition in Definition

They repeat the moves infinitely many times, obtaining, for every

Let

\displaystyle b_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in E_{k}}w_{n}^{2/(p-2)}e_{n},

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{k}

\displaystyle=\|b_{k}\|_{p}^{-1}b_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in E_{k}}\|b_{n}\|_{p}\|b_{n}\|^{-2}_{2}w^{2/(p-2)}_{n}e^{\ast}_{n}.

\tilde{b}_{k}\in\langle e_{n}:n\geq n_{k}\rangle

Remark 4.7

.

We will use the following linearly ordered set to enumerate the basis of
R_{\omega}^{p}
.

Notation 4.11
.

Denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\omega}

\displaystyle=\{(n,I):n\in\mathbb{N},I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}\}

and define the linear order on
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
as follows:
(m,J)\prec(n,I)
if and only if
m<n
or if
m=n
, then
J<I
in the linear order of
\mathcal{D}_{m-1}
. The set
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
can be visualized as disjoint copies of the sets
\mathcal{D}_{n-1}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The subscript
\omega
for the set
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
indicates the space
R_{\omega}^{p}
. Similar sets can be used for the
R_{\alpha}^{p}
spaces.
We now define the basis of
R_{\omega}^{p}
and in Remark
4.13
we observe that it is a martingale difference sequence.

Notation 4.12
.

We fix a specific basis for
R_{\omega}^{p}
inside
L_{p}
as follows. For every
n
we fix
(h_{I}^{n})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}
a distributional copy of the basis
(h_{I})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}
of
L_{p}^{n,0}
in
L_{p}
such that the
\sigma
-subalgebras
\sigma(\{h_{I}^{n}:I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}\})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, are independent. Then, by Remark
4.7
,
R_{\omega}^{p}
is distributionally isomorphic to
[(\chi_{[0,1]})\cup(h_{I}^{n})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}]
in
L_{p}
and, by Remark
4.9
,
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
is distributionally isomorphic to
[(h_{I}^{n})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}]
in
L_{p}
. The sequence
(h_{I}^{n})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
consists of
\{-1,0,1\}
-valued symmetric random variables. For the rest of the paper, we will identify
R_{\omega}^{p}
and
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
with their aforementioned distributional copies in
L_{p}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle R_{\omega}^{p}=[(\chi_{[0,1]})\cup(h_{I}^{n})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}]

and

\displaystyle R_{\omega}^{p,0}=[(h_{I}^{n})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}].

Remark 4.13
.

The sequence
(h_{I}^{n})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
described in Notation
4.12
is a martingale difference sequence with respect to the linear order of
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
. This follows from the fact that
(h_{I}^{n})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, are independent and for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have that
(h_{I}^{n})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}
is a distributional copy of the martingale difference sequence basis
(h_{I})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}
of
L_{p}^{n,0}
. Therefore,
(h_{I}^{n})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
is an unconditional basis of
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
by the Burkholder inequality.

In the following remark we explain that
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
is orthogonally complemented.

Remark 4.14
.

By Theorem
4.6
the
R_{\omega}^{p}
space is
(p^{\ast}-1)^{2}(\frac{p^{\ast}}{2})^{3/2}
-orthogonally complemented in
L_{p}
via some projection
P
. Because the space
L_{p}^{0}
is 2-orthogonally completed via the projection
Qf=f-\mathbb{E}(f)
and
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
is a subspace of
L_{p}^{0}
, it follows that
PQ
is a
p
-
C
-bounded orthogonal projection onto
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
, for
C=2(p^{\ast}-1)^{2}(\frac{p^{\ast}}{2})^{3/2}
. Therefore,
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
is
2(p^{\ast}-1)^{2}(\frac{p^{\ast}}{2})^{3/2}
-orthogonally complemented.

We now give an explicit formula for the projection onto
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
.

Remark 4.15
.

By Remarks
4.13
,
4.14
and
2.7

(d)
we have that the projection
P_{R_{\omega}^{p,0}}\colon L_{p}\to L_{p}
onto
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
is

\displaystyle P_{R_{\omega}^{p,0}}(f)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}|I|^{-1}\langle h_{I}^{n},f\rangle h_{I}^{n}.

By Remarks
2.7

(a)
and
(d)
, if
(b_{I}^{n})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
is a distributional copy of
(h_{I}^{n})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
, then replacing
h_{I}^{n}
with
b_{I}^{n}
in the above formula yields the orthogonal projection onto
[(b_{I}^{n})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}]
.

5.
Orthogonal Reduction to a diagonal operator

In this section, we approximately orthogonally reduce an arbitrary operator on
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
to a diagonal operator on
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
(Theorem
5.7
) using Lemma
5.6
. This is based on probabilistic arguments first used by Lechner in
[
8
]
. Finally, using Theorem
5.7
we get that an arbitrary operator on
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
with large diagonal is a factor of the identity operator on
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
(Theorem
5.8
).

Notation 5.1
.

The following will be used inside the proof of Theorem
5.7
. We give this notation now to discuss the proof.

(a)

Let
(b^{n}_{I})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
be a distributional copy of
(h^{n}_{I})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
in
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
and let
Y
be the closed linear span of
(b^{n}_{I})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
. Define the distributional isomorphism
j\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to Y
with

\displaystyle j(h_{I}^{n})=b^{n}_{I}

and the projection
E\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
onto
Y
with

\displaystyle E(f)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}|I|^{-1}\langle b_{I}^{n},f\rangle b_{I}^{n},

which is bounded by Remark
4.15
.

(b)

Let
T\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
be an operator and denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(T)=\{|I|^{-1}\langle h_{I}^{n},Th_{I}^{n}\rangle:(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}\}

the diagonal elements of T with respect to the basis
(h^{n}_{I})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
of
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
. Define
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}(T))
to be the collection of all averages of finite subsets of
\mathcal{D}(T)
.

We describe now in broad strokes the general idea of the proof of Theorem
5.7
. Let
T\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
be an arbitrary operator. Using an induction on the linear order of
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we construct blocks
(b^{n}_{I})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}
of the basis
(h^{n}_{I})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}
of
L_{p}^{n,0}
in some
L_{p}^{N(n),0}
,
N(n)>n
, with
b_{I}^{n}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n}}\theta_{K}h_{K}^{N(n)}
, where
(\theta_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n}}\subset\{-1,1\}
, such that

\displaystyle\supp(b_{[0,1]}^{n})=[0,1]

\displaystyle\supp(b_{I^{+}}^{n})=[b_{I}^{n}=1]

\displaystyle\supp(b_{I^{-}}^{n})=[b_{I}^{n}=-1],

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. From this we obtain that
(b^{n}_{I})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
is a distributional copy of
(h^{n}_{I})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
. We construct the block sequence
(b^{n}_{I})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
such that

\displaystyle|\langle b_{J}^{m},Tb_{I}^{n}\rangle|<\epsilon_{J,I}^{m,n},

for every
(m,J)\neq(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
, and

\displaystyle|\langle b_{I}^{n},Tb_{I}^{n}\rangle-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n}}\langle h_{K}^{N(n)},Th_{K}^{N(n)}\rangle|<\epsilon_{I}^{n},

for every
(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
, where
\epsilon_{J,I}^{m,n}
and
\epsilon_{I}^{n}
are small errors. From this we obtain that
T
is an approximate orthogonal factor of a diagonal operator
R
, that is

\|j^{-1}ETj-R\|<\epsilon,

where
j
and
E
are as in Notation
5.1
and the diagonal operator
R
is defined by

R(h_{I}^{n})=\left(|I|^{-1}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n}}\langle h_{K}^{N(n)},Th_{K}^{N(n)}\rangle\right)h_{I}^{n}

and has diagonal element in
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}(T))
.

In each inductive step, the choice of the block vector
b^{n}_{I}
is performed probabilistically, and for this purpose, we give the following notation.

Notation 5.2
.

Let
T\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
be an operator,
f\in L_{q}^{0}
and
x\in R_{\omega}^{p,0}
. Moreover, let
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
with
M\geq N
and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{N}
. Consider the probability space
\{-1,1\}^{\mathcal{B}}
with the fair probability measure. Define the random vector

\displaystyle b_{\mathcal{B},M}(\theta)=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}\theta_{K}h_{K}^{M},

where
\theta=(\theta_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{B}}\in\{-1,1\}^{\mathcal{B}}
and the following random variables

\displaystyle Y_{\mathcal{B},M,f}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\langle f,b_{\mathcal{B},M}(\theta)\rangle,

\displaystyle W_{\mathcal{B},M,x}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\langle b_{\mathcal{B},M}(\theta),x\rangle

and

\displaystyle Z_{\mathcal{B},M,T}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\langle b_{\mathcal{B},M}(\theta),Tb_{\mathcal{B},M}(\theta)\rangle-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}\langle h_{K}^{M},Th_{K}^{M}\rangle.

The vector
b_{\mathcal{B},M}
is in
L_{q}
and
L_{p}
and thus can occupy either position in
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

In the above notation, the random vector
b_{\mathcal{B},M}
will be used in the inductive step of the previously described proof to choose an appropriate vector
b^{n}_{I}
. The random variables
Y_{\mathcal{B},M,f}
,
W_{\mathcal{B},M,x}
, and
Z_{\mathcal{B},M,T}
, represent the interaction of this vector with previously constructed vectors via
T
, and they are used to achieve the small errors
\epsilon_{J,I}^{m,n}
and
\epsilon_{I}^{n}
. Next, we find estimates for the variance of these random variables. We will use these estimates in the proof of Lemma
5.6
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
with
M\geq N
,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{N}
and
f\in L_{q}^{0}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y_{\mathcal{B},M,f})=0

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Y_{\mathcal{B},M,f})\leq\|f\|_{q}^{2}|\cup\mathcal{B}|^{1/p}2^{-N/p}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle Y_{\mathcal{B},M,f}(\theta)=\langle f,b_{\mathcal{B},M}(\theta)\rangle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}\theta_{K}\langle f,h_{K}^{M}\rangle.

So, since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y_{\mathcal{B},M,f})=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}(\theta_{K})\langle f,h_{K}^{M}\rangle=0,

we get that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Y_{\mathcal{B},M,f})=\mathbb{E}(Y^{2}_{\mathcal{B},M,f}).

But,

\displaystyle Y^{2}_{\mathcal{B},M,f}(\theta)

\displaystyle=(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}\theta_{K}\langle f,h_{K}^{M}\rangle)(\sum_{L\in\mathcal{B}}\theta_{L}\langle f,h_{L}^{M}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\sum_{K,L\in\mathcal{B}}\theta_{K}\theta_{L}\langle f,h_{K}^{M}\rangle\langle f,h_{L}^{M}\rangle.

Now, taking the expectation we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y^{2}_{\mathcal{B},M,f})=\sum_{K,L\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}(\theta_{K}\theta_{L})\langle f,h_{K}^{M}\rangle\langle f,h_{L}^{M}\rangle.

However,
\mathbb{E}(\theta_{K}\theta_{L})=1
if and only if
K=L
and, otherwise,
\mathbb{E}(\theta_{K}\theta_{L})=0
. Therefore,

\mathrm{Var}(Y_{\mathcal{B},M,f})=\sum_{K\in B}\langle f,h_{K}^{M}\rangle^{2}.

Set
a_{f,K}=\langle f,h_{K}^{M}\rangle
and we observe that
|a_{f,K}|\leq\|f\|_{q}|K|^{1/p}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Y_{\mathcal{B},M,f})=\langle f,\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}a_{f,K}h_{K}^{M}\rangle\leq\|f\|_{q}\|\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}a_{f,K}h_{K}^{M}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{q}\max_{K\in\mathcal{B}}|a_{f,K}|\|\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}h_{K}^{M}\|_{p}=\|f\|_{q}\max_{K\in\mathcal{B}}|a_{f,K}||\cup\mathcal{B}|^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{q}^{2}|\cup\mathcal{B}|^{1/p}\max_{K\in\mathcal{B}}|K|^{1/p}=\|f\|_{q}^{2}|\cup\mathcal{B}|^{1/p}2^{-N/p}.

∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
with
M\geq N
,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{N}
and
x\in R_{\omega}^{p,0}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(W_{\mathcal{B},M,x})=0

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(W_{\mathcal{B},M,x})\leq\|x\|_{p}^{2}|\cup\mathcal{B}|^{1/q}2^{-N/q}.

Proof.

The proof is almost identical to that of Lemma
5.3
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
T\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
be an operator,
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
with
M\geq N
and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{N}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z_{\mathcal{B},M,T})=0

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Z_{\mathcal{B},M,T})\leq 2\|T\|^{2}|\cup\mathcal{B}|^{(1/p)+1}2^{-N/q}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle Z_{\mathcal{B},M,T}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\langle b_{\mathcal{B},M}(\theta),Tb_{\mathcal{B},M}(\theta)\rangle-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}\langle h_{K}^{M},Th_{K}^{M}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\neq L\in\mathcal{B}}\theta_{K}\theta_{L}\langle h_{K}^{M},Th_{L}^{M}\rangle.

So,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z_{\mathcal{B},M,T})=\sum_{K\neq L\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}(\theta_{K}\theta_{L})\langle h_{K}^{M},Th_{L}^{M}\rangle=0

because
K\neq L
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Z_{\mathcal{B},M,T})=\mathbb{E}(Z^{2}_{\mathcal{B},M,T}).

But,

\displaystyle Z^{2}_{\mathcal{B},M,T}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{K_{1}\neq L_{1}\in\mathcal{B}}\theta_{K_{1}}\theta_{L_{1}}\langle h_{K_{1}}^{M},Th_{L_{1}}^{M}\rangle\right)\left(\sum_{K_{2}\neq L_{2}\in\mathcal{B}}\theta_{K_{2}}\theta_{L_{2}}\langle h_{K_{2}}^{M},Th_{L_{2}}^{M}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{K_{1}\neq L_{1}\in\mathcal{B},K_{2}\neq L_{2}\in\mathcal{B}}\theta_{K_{1}}\theta_{L_{1}}\theta_{K_{2}}\theta_{L_{2}}\langle h_{K_{1}}^{M},Th_{L_{1}}^{M}\rangle\langle h_{K_{2}}^{M},Th_{L_{2}}^{M}\rangle.

Now, taking the expectation we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z^{2}_{\mathcal{B},M,T})=\sum_{K_{1}\neq L_{1}\in\mathcal{B},K_{2}\neq L_{2}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}(\theta_{K_{1}}\theta_{L_{1}}\theta_{K_{2}}\theta_{L_{2}})\langle h_{K_{1}}^{M},Th_{L_{1}}^{M}\rangle\langle h_{K_{2}}^{M},Th_{L_{2}}^{M}\rangle.

However,
\mathbb{E}(\theta_{K_{1}}\theta_{L_{1}}\theta_{K_{2}}\theta_{L_{2}})=1
if and only if
K_{1}=K_{2},L_{1}=L_{2}
and
K_{1}=L_{2},K_{2}=L_{1}
, otherwise,
\mathbb{E}(\theta_{K_{1}}\theta_{L_{1}}\theta_{K_{2}}\theta_{L_{2}})=0.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Z_{\mathcal{B},M,T})=\sum_{K\neq L\in\mathcal{B}}\langle h_{K}^{M},Th_{L}^{M}\rangle\langle h_{L}^{M},Th_{K}^{M}\rangle+\sum_{K\neq L\in B}\langle h_{K}^{M},Th_{L}^{M}\rangle^{2}.

Set

\displaystyle A=\sum_{K\neq L\in\mathcal{B}}\langle h_{K}^{M},Th_{L}^{M}\rangle\langle h_{L}^{M},Th_{K}^{M}\rangle

and

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\neq L\in\mathcal{B}}\langle h_{K}^{M},Th_{L}^{M}\rangle^{2}.

We will find estimations for A and B. Indeed, set
a_{L,K}=\langle h_{L}^{M},Th_{K}^{M}\rangle
. We have
|a_{L,K}|\leq\|T\||L|^{1/q}|K|^{1/p}.
Hence,

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}\langle h_{K}^{M},\sum_{L\neq K\in\mathcal{B}}a_{L,K}Th_{L}^{M}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|T\|\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}|K|^{1/q}\|\sum_{L\neq K\in\mathcal{B}}a_{L,K}h_{L}^{M}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|T\|\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}|K|^{1/q}\max_{L\neq K\in\mathcal{B}}|a_{L,K}|\|\sum_{L\neq K\in\mathcal{B}}h_{L}^{M}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|T\|\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}|K|^{1/q}\max_{L\neq K\in\mathcal{B}}|a_{L,K}|\|\sum_{L\in\mathcal{B}}h_{L}^{M}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|T\|^{2}|\cup\mathcal{B}|^{1/p}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}|K|\max_{L\neq K\in\mathcal{B}}|L|^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\|T\|^{2}|\cup\mathcal{B}|^{(1/p)+1}2^{-N/q}.

The proof of the estimation for B is almost identical to that of A and we have
B\leq\|T\|^{2}|\cup\mathcal{B}|^{(1/p)+1}2^{-N/q}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Z_{\mathcal{B},M,T})\leq 2\|T\|^{2}|\cup\mathcal{B}|^{(1/p)+1}2^{-N/q}.

∎

Now, we use the previous probabilistic estimates to determine a value for a random vector. This will be used in the inductive step of the proof of Theorem
5.7
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
T\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
be an operator. Let
(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
,
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
with
M\geq N
and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{N}
with
|\cup\mathcal{B}|=|I|
. Let
x_{(m,J)}\in R_{\omega}^{p,0}
and
f_{(m,J)}\in L_{q}^{0}
, where
(m,J)\prec(n,I)
such that

\displaystyle\|x_{(m,J)}\|

\displaystyle\leq|J|^{1/p}

and

\displaystyle\|f_{(m,J)}\|

\displaystyle\leq|J|^{1/q}.

For
\theta=(\theta_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{B}}\in\{-1,1\}^{\mathcal{B}}
we define the random variables

\displaystyle Z(\theta)=Z_{\mathcal{B},M,T}(\theta)=\langle b_{\mathcal{B},M}(\theta),Tb_{\mathcal{B},M}(\theta)\rangle-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{B}}\langle h_{K}^{M},Th_{K}^{M}\rangle,

\displaystyle Y_{(m,J)}(\theta)=Y_{\mathcal{B},M,T^{\ast}f_{(m,J)}}(\theta)=\langle T^{\ast}f_{(m,J)},b_{\mathcal{B},M}(\theta)\rangle,\;\;\;(m,J)\prec(n,I)

and

\displaystyle W_{(m,J)}(\theta)=W_{\mathcal{B},M,Tx_{(m,J)}}(\theta)=\langle b_{\mathcal{B},M}(\theta),Tx_{(m,J)}\rangle,\;\;\;(m,J)\prec(n,I).

Let
\eta>0
and
\eta_{(m,J)}>0
, where
(m,J)\prec(n,I)
. If

(1)

N>p^{\ast}\left(2\log\|T\|+\log\left(\frac{2^{2n+3}}{\eta^{2}}+\sum_{(m,J)\prec(n,I)}\frac{1}{\eta^{2}_{(m,J)}}\right)\right)

then there exists
\theta^{0}=(\theta_{K}^{0})_{K\in\mathcal{B}}
such that

\displaystyle|Z(\theta^{0})|<\eta,

\displaystyle|Y_{(m,J)}(\theta^{0})|<\eta,\;\;\;(m,J)\prec(n,I)

and

\displaystyle|W_{(m,J)}(\theta^{0})|<\eta_{(m,J)},\;\;\;(m,J)\prec(n,I).

Proof.

To prove the existence of
\theta^{0}
, we define the following events:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}=\{\theta:|Z(\theta)|>\eta\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}(m,J)=\{\theta:|Y_{(m,J)}(\theta)|>\eta\},\qquad(m,J)\prec(n,I)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(m,J)=\{\theta:|W_{(m,J)}(\theta)|>\eta_{(m,J)}\},\qquad(m,J)\prec(n,I).

Let

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{Z}\cup\Big(\bigcup_{(m,J)\prec(n,I)}\mathcal{Y}(m,J)\Big)\cup\Big(\bigcup_{(m,J)\prec(n,I)}\mathcal{W}(m,J)\Big).

Taking the probability of the above events, we observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Z})+\sum_{(m,J)\prec(n,I)}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Y}(m,J))+\sum_{(m,J)\prec(n,I)}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}(m,J))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\mathrm{Var}(Z)+\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\sum_{(m,J)\prec(n,I)}\mathrm{Var}(Y_{(m,J)})+\sum_{(m,J)\prec(n,I)}\frac{1}{\eta^{2}_{(m,J)}}\mathrm{Var}(W_{(m,J)}),

by the Chebyshev inequality. Now, by Lemmas
5.3
,
5.4
and
5.5
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})\leq\frac{2}{\eta^{2}}\|T\|^{2}|I|^{(1/p)+1}2^{-N/q}+\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\sum_{(m,J)\prec(n,I)}\|T\|^{2}|J|^{2/q}|I|^{1/p}2^{-N/p}

\displaystyle+\sum_{(m,J)\prec(n,I)}\frac{1}{\eta^{2}_{(m,J)}}\|T\|^{2}|J|^{2/p}|I|^{1/q}2^{-N/q}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2^{2n+3}}{\eta^{2}}+\sum_{(m,J)\prec(n,I)}\frac{1}{\eta^{2}_{(m,J)}}\right)\|T\|^{2}2^{-N/p^{\ast}}.

So,
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})<1
by (
1
). Therefore, there exists
\theta^{0}=(\theta_{K}^{0})_{K\in\mathcal{B}}
such that

|Z(\theta^{0})|<\eta
,
|Y_{(m,J)}(\theta^{0})|<\eta
and

|W_{(m,J)}(\theta^{0})|<\eta_{(m,J)}
, for every
(m,J)\prec(n,I).

∎

This is the main theorem of this section where we reduce an arbitrary operator to a diagonal operator.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
T\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
be an operator. For every
\epsilon>0
there exist a diagonal operator
R\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
with diagonal elements in
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}(T))
such that
T
is an orthogonal factor of
R
with error
\epsilon
.

Proof.

Let an arbitrary
\epsilon>0
. Choose an increasing sequence of positive integers
k(n)
such that

\displaystyle k(n)>p^{\ast}\left(12n+13+2\log\left(\frac{\|T\|}{\epsilon}\right)\right)

and put
N(n)=k(n)+n
.

We will construct by induction on the linear order of
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
a distributional copy
(b^{n}_{I})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
of
(h^{n}_{I})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
consisting of blocks of the basis
(h^{n}_{I})_{(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
with

(2)

2\leq k\leq m-1
. So,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{2\leq k\leq m}\mathcal{Z}_{[0,\frac{1}{2}]}^{n_{k}}\cup\mathcal{Z}_{[\frac{1}{2},1]}^{n_{k}}\right)\leq\sum_{2\leq k\leq m}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Z}_{[0,\frac{1}{2}]}^{n_{k}})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Z}_{[\frac{1}{2},1]}^{n_{k}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{2\leq k\leq m}32m^{2}\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{2^{n_{1}}}\leq 2^{5}m^{3}\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{2^{n_{1}}}<1,

because
x\leq n_{1}
. Hence, there exist signs
\theta=(\theta_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{B}_{[0,1]}}
such that

\displaystyle|\lambda_{[0,\frac{1}{2}]}^{n_{k}}(\theta)-\lambda_{n_{k}}|<\frac{\epsilon}{4m}

and

\displaystyle|\lambda_{[\frac{1}{2},1]}^{n_{k}}(\theta)-\lambda_{n_{k}}|<\frac{\epsilon}{4m}

for every
2\leq k\leq m-1
.

Now, assume that for
I\in\mathcal{D}^{k-1}
we have found signs
\theta^{\prime}=(\theta^{\prime}_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{B}_{\pi(I)}}
, where without loss of generality assume that
I=\pi(I)^{+}
, sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n_{j}}=\{J\in\mathcal{D}^{n_{j}}:J\subset[b_{\pi(I)}^{\theta^{\prime}}=1]\}

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}=\frac{1}{\text{card}(\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n_{j}})}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n_{j}}}d_{J}

such that

\displaystyle|\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}-\lambda_{\pi(I)}^{n_{j}}|<\frac{\epsilon}{4m}

for every
k\leq j\leq m-1
. Now, for
I^{+},I^{-}\in\mathcal{D}^{k}
, we are looking for signs
\theta=(\theta_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n_{k}}}
, such that for the sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}=\{J\in\mathcal{D}^{n_{j}}:J\subset[b_{I}^{\theta}=1]\}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}=\{J\in\mathcal{D}^{n_{j}}:J\subset[b_{I}^{\theta}=-1]\},

we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}=\frac{1}{\text{card}(\mathcal{B}_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}})}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n_{j}}}d_{J}

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}=\frac{1}{\text{card}(\mathcal{B}_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}})}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n_{j}}}d_{J},

such that

\displaystyle|\lambda_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}-\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}|<\frac{\epsilon}{4m}

and

\displaystyle|\lambda_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}-\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}|<\frac{\epsilon}{4m}

for every
k+1\leq j\leq m-1
.
Indeed, let
\theta=(\theta_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n_{k}}}
. For the vector

\displaystyle b_{I}^{\theta}=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n_{k}}}\theta_{J}h_{J}^{M},

denote

\displaystyle\Gamma^{+}_{I}(\theta)=\{t\in[0,1]:b^{\theta}_{I}(t)=1\}

and

\displaystyle\Gamma^{-}_{I}(\theta)=\{t\in[0,1]:b^{\theta}_{I}(t)=-1\}.

Moreover, denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}(\theta)=\{J\in\mathcal{D}^{n_{j}}:J\subset\Gamma^{+}_{I}(\theta)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}(\theta)=\{J\in\mathcal{D}^{n_{j}}:J\subset\Gamma^{-}_{I}(\theta)\},

and define the random variables

\displaystyle\lambda_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}\colon\{-1,1\}^{\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n_{k}}}\to\mathbb{R}\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\lambda_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}\colon\{-1,1\}^{\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n_{k}}}\to\mathbb{R}

with

\displaystyle\lambda_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\text{card}(\mathcal{B}_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}(\theta))}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{B}_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}(\theta)}d_{J}

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\text{card}(\mathcal{B}_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}(\theta))}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{B}_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}(\theta)}d_{J}

We are looking for signs
\theta=(\theta_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n_{k}}}
such that

\displaystyle|\lambda_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}(\theta)-\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}|<\frac{\epsilon}{4m}

and

\displaystyle|\lambda_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}(\theta)-\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}|<\frac{\epsilon}{4m}

for every
k+1\leq j\leq m-1
. Indeed, define the events

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}=\{\theta:|\lambda_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}(\theta)-\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}|>\frac{\epsilon}{4m}\}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}=\{\theta:|\lambda_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}(\theta)-\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}|>\frac{\epsilon}{4m}\},

for every
k+1\leq j\leq m-1
. By Lemma
6.1
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\lambda_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}})=\mathbb{E}(\lambda_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}})=\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\lambda_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}})\leq\frac{2^{k-1}}{2^{n_{k}}}\Gamma^{2}

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\lambda_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}})\leq\frac{2^{k-1}}{2^{n_{k}}}\Gamma^{2},

for every
k+1\leq j\leq m-1
. But, by the Chebyshev inequality we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Z}_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}})\leq 16m^{2}\epsilon^{-2}\mathrm{Var}(\lambda_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}})

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Z}_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}})\leq 16m^{2}\epsilon^{-2}\mathrm{Var}(\lambda_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}),

for every
k+1\leq j\leq m-1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{j=k+1}^{m}\mathcal{Z}_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}\cup\mathcal{Z}_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}\right)\leq\sum_{j=k+1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Z}_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Z}_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=k+1}^{m}2^{5}\frac{m^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\frac{2^{k-1}}{2^{n_{k}}}\Gamma^{2}<2^{m+5}m^{3}\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{2^{n_{k}}}<1,

because
x\leq n_{1}<n_{2}<...<n_{m-1}
. Hence, there exist
\theta=(\theta_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{B}_{I}^{n_{k}}}
such that

\displaystyle|\lambda_{I^{+}}^{n_{j}}(\theta)-\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}|<\frac{\epsilon}{4m}

and

\displaystyle|\lambda_{I^{-}}^{n_{j}}(\theta)-\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}|<\frac{\epsilon}{4m},

for every
k+1\leq j\leq m-1
.

Now, take
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{n_{1}}
. For
I\in\mathcal{D}^{k}
and
k+1\leq j\leq m-1
, we observe that

\displaystyle|\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}-\lambda_{n_{j}}|\leq|\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{\pi(I)^{i}}^{n_{j}}-\lambda_{\pi(I)^{i+1}}^{n_{j}}|<\frac{\epsilon}{4},

for every
k+1\leq j\leq m-1
, where
\lambda_{\pi(I)^{0}}^{n_{j}}=\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}
and
\lambda_{\pi(I)^{k}}^{n_{j}}=\lambda_{n_{j}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle|\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}-\lambda_{0}|\leq|\lambda_{I}^{n_{j}}-\lambda_{n_{j}}|+|\lambda_{n_{j}}-\lambda_{0}|<\frac{\epsilon}{4}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}<\epsilon,

for every
k+1\leq j\leq m-1
. Now, for
I\in\mathcal{D}^{k}
, put
\lambda_{I}=\lambda_{I}^{n_{k+1}}
. We have that
|\lambda_{I}-\lambda_{0}|<\epsilon,
for every
I\in\mathcal{D}_{m-1}
. Finally, for an arbitrary
f\in L_{p}^{m,0}
with
f=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{m-1}}a_{I}h_{I}^{m}
, by the Paley-Burkholder inequality, we get that

\displaystyle\|(j^{-1}ETj-\lambda_{0}I)(f)\|=\|\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{m-1}}(\lambda_{I}-\lambda_{0})a_{I}h_{I}^{m}\|

\displaystyle\leq(p^{\ast}-1)\epsilon\|\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{m-1}}a_{I}h_{I}^{m}\|=(p^{\ast}-1)\epsilon\|f\|.

Therefore,
\|j^{-1}ETj-\lambda_{0}I\|<(p^{\ast}-1)\epsilon
.
∎

We now reduce an arbitrary diagonal operator to a scalar multiple of the identity operator using the above finite dimensional reduction.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
T\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
be a diagonal operator with diagonal elements
d_{I}^{n}=|I|^{-1}\langle h_{I}^{n},Th_{I}^{n}\rangle
, where
(n,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
. For every
\epsilon>0
there exists
\lambda_{0}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}(T))
such that
T
is an orthogonal factor of
\lambda_{0}I\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
with error
\epsilon
, where
I
is the identity operator on
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
.

Proof.

For
\frac{\epsilon}{4(p^{\ast}-1)}>0
, choose an increasing sequence of non-negative integers
N(m)
with

\displaystyle N(m)>m+6+m\frac{16(p^{\ast}-1)\|T\|}{\epsilon}+3\log(m)+2\log(\frac{4(p^{\ast}-1)\|T\|}{\epsilon}).

Then by Theorem
6.5
, there exist
\lambda_{0}^{m}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}(T))
, where

\displaystyle|\lambda_{I}^{m}-\lambda_{J}^{m}|<\frac{\epsilon}{4(p^{\ast}-1)},

for every
I,J\in\mathcal{D}_{m-1}
, and a distributional copy
(b_{I}^{m})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{m-1}}
of
(h^{m}_{I})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{m-1}}
consisting of blocks in the basis
(h^{N(m)}_{I})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{N(m)-1}}
of
L_{p}^{N(m),0}
that defines the operators
j_{m}
and
E_{m}
in Notation
6.3
such that

\displaystyle\|j_{m}^{-1}E_{m}Tj_{m}-\lambda_{0}^{m}I_{m}\|<\frac{\epsilon}{4}.

Since the sequence
(\lambda_{0}^{m})
is bounded there exists a subsequence
(\lambda_{0}^{m_{k}})
and
\lambda_{0}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}(T))
such that

\displaystyle|\lambda_{0}^{m_{k}}-\lambda_{0}|<\frac{\epsilon}{2(p^{\ast}-1)}

for every
k=1,2...
. Thus,

\displaystyle|\lambda_{I}^{m_{k}}-\lambda_{0}|<\frac{\epsilon}{(p^{\ast}-1)},

for every
k=1,2...
. Now, put

\displaystyle Y=[(b_{I}^{m_{k}})_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{k-1}}:k=1,2,...].

Define the distributional isomorphism
j\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to Y
with
j(h_{I}^{k})=b_{I}^{m_{k}}
, where
I\in\mathcal{D}_{k-1}
, and the projection
E\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
onto
Y
with

\displaystyle E(f)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{k-1}}|I|^{-1}\langle b_{I}^{m_{k}},f\rangle b_{I}^{m_{k}},

which is bounded by Remark
4.15
. Finally, because
j^{-1}ETj
is a diagonal operator with entries
(\lambda_{I}^{m_{k}})_{(k,I)\in\mathcal{D}_{\omega}}
we get that

\displaystyle\|j^{-1}ETj-\lambda_{0}I\|<\epsilon.

∎

In the following theorem, we reduce an arbitrary operator to a scalar multiple of the identity operator.

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
T\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
be an operator. For every
\epsilon>0
there exists
\lambda_{0}
in the convex hull of
\mathcal{D}(T)
such that
T
is an orthogonal factor of
\lambda_{0}I\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
with error
\epsilon
, where
I
is the identity operator on
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
.

Proof.

It follows immediately from Proposition
2.11
, Theorem
5.7
and Theorem
6.6
.
∎

Using the above theorem, we prove that
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
has the primary factorization property.

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
T\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
be an operator. For every
0<\epsilon<1
, either
T
or
I-T
is a
\frac{4}{1-\epsilon}(p^{\ast}-1)^{2}(\frac{p^{\ast}}{2})^{3/2}
-factor of the identity operator
I
on
R_{\omega}^{p,0}
.

Proof.

Let
0<\epsilon<1
. By Theorem
6.7
, there exists
\lambda_{0}
in the convex hull of
\mathcal{D}(T)
such that
T
is an orthogonal factor of
\lambda_{0}I\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
with error
\epsilon/2
, that is there exists a distributional embedding
j\colon R_{\omega}^{p,0}\to R_{\omega}^{p,0}
such that

\displaystyle\|j^{-1}P_{j(R_{\omega}^{p,0})}Tj-\lambda_{0}I\|<\epsilon/2.

If
|\lambda_{0}|\geq 1/2
, then

\displaystyle\|\lambda_{0}^{-1}j^{-1}P_{j(R_{\omega}^{p,0})}Tj-I\|<\epsilon.

So, the operator
A=\lambda_{0}^{-1}j^{-1}P_{j(R_{\omega}^{p,0})}Tj
is invertible with
\|A^{-1}\|\leq 1/(1-\epsilon)
. Moreover, if
B=A^{-1}\lambda_{0}^{-1}j^{-1}P_{j(R_{\omega}^{p,0})}
, then
BTj=I
and
\|B\|\|j\|\leq 2/(1-\epsilon)\|P_{j(R_{\omega}^{p,0})}\|\leq 4/(1-\epsilon)(p^{\ast}-1)^{2}(\frac{p^{\ast}}{2})^{3/2}
. Now, if
|\lambda_{0}|<1/2
, then, because

\displaystyle\|j^{-1}P_{j(R_{\omega}^{p,0})}(I-T)j-(1-\lambda_{0})I\|<\epsilon,

we achieve the same conclusion for
I-T
instead of
T
.
∎
