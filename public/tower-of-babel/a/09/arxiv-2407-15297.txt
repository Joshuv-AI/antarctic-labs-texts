Distributional limits of graph cuts on discretized grids
Housen Li, Axel Munk, Leo Suchan

Abstract
Graph cuts are among the most prominent tools for clustering and classification analysis. While intensively studied from geometric and algorithmic perspectives, graph cut-based statistical inference still remains elusive to a certain extent. Distributional limits are fundamental in understanding and designing such statistical procedures on randomly sampled data. We provide explicit limiting distributions for balanced graph cuts in general on a fixed but arbitrary discretization. In particular, we show that Minimum Cut, Ratio Cut and Normalized Cut behave asymptotically as the minimum of Gaussians as sample size increases. Interestingly, our results reveal a dichotomy for Cheeger Cut: The limiting distribution of the optimal objective value is the minimum of Gaussians only when the optimal partition yields two sets of unequal volumes, while otherwise the limiting distribution is the minimum of a random mixture of Gaussians. Further, we show the bootstrap consistency for all types of graph cuts by utilizing the directional differentiability of cut functionals. We validate these theoretical findings by Monte Carlo experiments, and examine differences between the cuts and the dependency on the underlying distribution. Additionally, we expand our theoretical findings to the Xist algorithm, a computational surrogate of graph cuts recently proposed in Suchan, Li and Munk (arXiv, 2023), thus demonstrating the practical applicability of our findings e.g. in statistical tests.

Introduction

Graph partitioning remains one of the most fundamental tasks in graph-based data analysis, with applications found in cluster analysis
(
Flake et al., 2004
;
Zabih and Kolmogorov, 2004
;
Long et al., 2022
)
, image segmentation
(
Shi and Malik, 2000
;
Tao et al., 2007
;
Paulhac et al., 2012
)
, machine learning
(
Zhou and Burges, 2007
;
Huang et al., 2009
;
On et al., 2020
)
, and it is relevant to a wide range of domain applications, including nuclear physics, cell biology, and the analysis of social networks, to mention a few
(
Yang et al., 2014
;
Xing and Karp, 2001
;
Rahimian et al., 2015
)
.

While a large body of work has been devoted to its mathematical understanding (e.g.
García Trillos et al., 2020
;
Mulas, 2021
) and algorithmic advances (e.g.
Chuzhoy et al., 2020
;
Saranurak and Wang, 2019
;
Chuzhoy and Khanna, 2019
;
Suchan et al., 2023
), the understanding of prominent balanced graph cuts such as Ratio Cut (RCut;
Hagen and Kahng, 1992
), Normalized Cut (NCut;
Shi and Malik, 2000
), Cheeger Cut (CCut;
Cheeger, 1971
) from a statistical perspective has been the subject of only a few papers.
Among these,
García Trillos et al. (2016)
established the
\Gamma
-convergence of the empirical cut towards a continuum cut on the underlying distribution for various balancing terms. More recently, as a first step in the asymptotic analysis of graph cuts,
García Trillos et al. (2022)
derived upper and lower bounds for the convergence rates for CCut in the same setting which, as the authors note, are in multiple ways not yet optimal. So while these results are valuable by assuring that through refinement of the graph structure the corresponding partitions stay valid asymptotically, they are still far from providing a limiting distribution, say, usable for statistical inference.

Although conceptually appealing, the original balanced graph cuts are of limited practical use as computing them is known to be NP-hard (e.g.
Shi and Malik, 2000
;
Šíma and Schaeffer, 2006
;
Mohar, 1989
;
Bui and Jones, 1992
). Hence, attention on graph cuts has largely focused on surrogates, most prominently on spectral clustering
(
von Luxburg, 2007
)
, a well-known convex relaxation of NCut (and, by extension, of RCut) which reduces the graph cut to an eigenvalue problem on the graph Laplacian. Here, the asymptotic perspective has, for instance, been taken by
von Luxburg et al. (2008)
who showed that under mild conditions the normalized graph Laplacian of a neighbourhood graph built from a sample converges toward a continuous operator depending on the underlying distribution.
Maier et al. (2013)
explored the impact of the choice of neighbourhood structure on the cut value and its asymptotic behaviour.

While spectral clustering seemingly enables fast computation even for large graphs, by design it merely computes an
imitation
of the true graph cut and can thus yield inaccurate results (see e.g.
Guattery and Miller (1998)
, in particular, their famous
“cockroach graph”
). In this paper we turn the spectral clustering idea on its head by utilizing discretization: Instead of relaxation of a cut functional of a graph built from a (large) point cloud, we discretize the points first, construct a graph out of this (much smaller) discretized sample and then cut it using the exact graph cut. Or, to rephrase: While spectral clustering computes an imitation of the graph cut at a high resolution, we consider the exact cut at a low resolution. This approach will allow us to derive asymptotic distributions which, to the best of our knowledge, are the first such results for graph cuts.

The impact of discretizing a sample on the statistical properties of the resulting graph cut has not been explored in much detail in the literature. Existing discretization approaches have been focused exclusively on utilizing random subsampling in order to approximate the graph. For instance,
Fetaya et al. (2015)
investigated the impact of vertex subsampling on the Laplacian for spectral clustering. Somewhat complimentary,
Tsay et al. (1999)
preserved the set of vertices and instead constructed a graph out of a random set of edges in order to approximate graph cuts. Both approaches, however, only considered the impact of subsampling on the sample graph, and not its relation to the underlying distribution. When one seeks to analyze the limiting behaviour of graph cuts, subsampling merely introduces another random component leading to more complications. In contrast, we take a completely different perspective on discretization by superimposing a fixed structure on top of any point cloud, taking into account the sample as a whole. Namely, we construct a new graph out of a predefined set of vertices and edges, where the sample itself merely influences the weighting of the edges. Utilizing this type of discretization has multiple distinct advantages:

1.

The resulting fixed, discrete structure avoids the need for any condition on the support of the underlying distribution such as boundary smoothness or certain manifold requirements.

2.

If chosen properly, our discretization provides a simple multinomial model without any requirement from the underlying distribution itself.

3.

The control over the discretization remains with the user, thus allowing for much flexibility depending on the context, e.g. including prior information about the underlying distribution.

4.

It often arises naturally in practical applications, e.g. as image pixels or voxels.

Informal statement of the main theorem

We consider a general setup of observing a sample from an arbitrary probability measure on a metric space. The asymptotic distributions of the objective values of the discretized empirical graph cuts are characterized as minima over normal variates indexed in all possible partitions.

(informal)
.

(i)

Then, for the empirical graph cut

\sqrt{n}\bigl(\XC(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\XC(\mathcal{G})\bigr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\longrightarrow}}\min_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{p}}}}Z_{S}^{\XC},

(1.1)

where

(ii)

Modelling and assumptions

By
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,{\bm{W}})
we denote a simple weighted undirected graph. Here
V
is the set of nodes,
E
the set of edges, and
{\bm{W}}=(w_{ij})_{i,j\in V}
the weight matrix with
w_{ij}
equal to the weight of edge
\{i,j\}
if
\{i,j\}\in E
and
w_{ij}=0
otherwise. Clearly,
{\bm{W}}
is symmetric and has zero diagonals since a simple graph disallows self-loops, i.e.
\{i,i\}\not\in E
. An edge
\{i,j\}\in E
is also denoted as
i\sim j
.

Definition 1
.

For a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,{\bm{W}})
, the
balanced graph cut

\XC
of the graph
\mathcal{G}
is

\XC(\mathcal{G})\coloneqq\min_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\XC_{S}(\mathcal{G})\quad\text{where}\quad\XC_{S}(\mathcal{G})\coloneqq\sum_{{i\in S,j\in{S}^{\mathsf{c}}}}\frac{w_{ij}}{\bal(S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}})}\quad\text{for}\,\,S\in\mathcal{S}.

Here
{S}^{\mathsf{c}}\coloneqq V\setminus S
denotes the complement of
S
,
\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\{S\subseteq V:V\neq S\neq\emptyset\}
is the set of partitions of
V
, and
\XC
and
\bal(S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}})
serve as placeholders for the graph cut and its corresponding balancing term, respectively.

Table

1
lists the balancing terms for MCut, RCut, NCut and CCut. In general, the balancing term can depend on the underlying graph structure, the partition
S
as well as the weights
w_{ij}
. Further, for any partition
S\in\mathcal{S}
define

|S|\coloneqq\#\{i\in S\}\quad\text{and}\quad\vol(S)\coloneqq\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{j\in V}w_{ij}.

To avoid some balancing terms becoming infinitely large we assume that
|V|<\infty
. This assumption has no further restriction on our model since it comes naturally by means of the discretization of the underlying space into
m
subsets (see
(M2)
below). This will become clear as this section progresses.
Note that while we use the balancing term
\bal(S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}})=\vol(S)\vol({S}^{\mathsf{c}})
for NCut, some authors use
\bal^{*}(S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}})\coloneqq(\vol(S)^{-1}+\vol({S}^{\mathsf{c}})^{-1})^{-1}
instead. This difference does not impact the resulting partition since
\bal^{*}(S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}})=\bal(S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}})/\vol(V)
, and
\vol(V)
does not depend on
S\in\mathcal{S}
. It does, however, influence the cut value as well as the limiting distribution that we present later in
Theorems

3.1
and
3.3
by a scaling factor of
\vol(V)
, see
Corollary

A.4
in the
Appendix A
Appendix
.

We now specify the assumptions which our model requires and then the discretization itself. Throughout this paper, we make the following assumptions:

(M1)

{\bm{X}}\coloneqq(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
is a vector of i.i.d. samples from a probability distribution
\nu
on a metric space
(\mathcal{D},\dist)
, equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra, with finite second moment.

(M2)

The support of
\nu
, denoted by
D
, can be divided into finitely many (fixed) disjoint measurable sets
D_{i}
, i.e.
D=\sum_{i=1}^{m}D_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

(M3)

The balancing terms
\bal(S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}})
are Hadmard directionally differentiable at the underlying discretized probability vector
{\bm{p}}\coloneqq\bigl(\nu(D_{i})\bigr)_{i=1,\ldots,m}
.

As a natural assumption,
(M1)
justifies treating the number of sample points that fall into each
D_{i}
as a sample from a multinomial distribution, which is the backbone of our model. The requirement on the second moment is necessary for a distributional limit, and the metric structure of the support
D
of
\nu
allows quantification of the distance between discretization districts. In
(M2)
we assume the existence of a discretization of
D
into finitely many
D_{i}
. Typical examples include a regular partition by equally spaced grids (e.g. image pixels) and a Voroni tessellation using a finite set of points.
In
(M3)
we require the Hadamard directional differentiability of the balancing terms at
{\bm{p}}
. Here we view
\bal(S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}})
as functions
\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
that depend on the discretization, see also the discussion at the end of this section. We stress that the Hadamard directional differentiability is a rather weak form of differentiability, see Appendix
A.1
. In fact,
(M3)
is satisfied for all graph cuts under consideration.

In the following we further specify our discretization model given data
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
. This will also shed more light on why we need certain assumptions.

Definition 2
.

Under
(M1)
–
(M2)
, we define the
discretized sample

{\bm{Y}}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m})
by

Y_{i}\coloneqq\bigl|\big\{j:X_{j}\in D_{i},\;j=1,\ldots,n\big\}\bigr|.

We define the corresponding
empirical discretized graph

\mathcal{G}_{n}\coloneqq(V,E,\widehat{{\bm{W}}})
as follows:

i.

The set of nodes is
V\coloneqq\{1\ldots,m\}
with
i\in V
denoting the index of
D_{i}
.

ii.

The edges are defined by a
t
-neighbourhood structure for some
t>0
. That is,
\{i,j\}\in E
if and only if
\mathrm{diss}(D_{i},D_{j})\leq t
. Here,
\mathrm{diss}(D_{i},D_{j})
is a certain measure of dissimilarity between
D_{i}
and
D_{j}
, for instance, the distance between two arbitrarily picked points (e.g. geometric centers) in
D_{i}
and
D_{j}
.

iii.

The weight matrix
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}=(\widehat{w}_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots,m}
consists of entries

\widehat{w}_{ij}\coloneqq\frac{Y_{i}}{n}\frac{Y_{j}}{n}\mathbbm{1}_{i\sim j}\qquad\text{ with }\quad\mathbbm{1}_{i\sim j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\{i,j\}\in E,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

As a discretization of
{\bm{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
onto the districts
D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}
,
{\bm{Y}}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m})
is a sample from
\mult(n,{\bm{p}})
for
{\bm{p}}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})
with
p_{i}=\nu(D_{i})
. Our choice of weights
\widehat{w}_{ij}=Y_{i}Y_{j}n^{-2}\mathbbm{1}_{i\sim j}
can be seen as an
“importance weighting”
, where the strength of a connecting edge is proportional to the
“importance”
(i.e. probability mass) of both vertices involved. To illustrate, consider the simple scenario that every observation in
D_{i}
connects with every observation in
D_{i}
if
\mathrm{dis}(D_{i},D_{j})<t
in the graph built from the original sample. Then, the weight
\widehat{w}_{ij}
is proportion to the total number of connections between observations in
D_{i}
and those in
D_{j}
, see
Figure

1
. The choice in scaling (in terms of
n
) serves as a standardization, noting that

\tfrac{n}{n-1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widehat{w}_{ij}\bigr]=p_{i}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}_{i\sim j}\quad\text{for all}\quad i,j\in V.

(2.1)

Moreover, a benefit of choosing
\widehat{w}_{ij}
in this way is that it connects
D_{i}
and
D_{j}
more strongly if
Y_{i}
and
Y_{j}
have similar values. More precisely, the function
f:(y_{i},y_{j})\mapsto y_{i}y_{j}
is minimized by
y_{i}=y_{j}
under the condition that
y_{i}+y_{j}
is constant. This means that if both districts contain, say,
10
observations in total, then
D_{i}
and
D_{j}
will be connected most closely if
Y_{i}=Y_{j}=5
(with
\widehat{w}_{ij}=25/n^{2}
). If, on the other hand,
Y_{i}=1
and
Y_{j}=9
,
\widehat{w}_{ij}=9/n^{2}
will result in a much weaker connection. As an analogy in a different context, the product structure in
\widehat{w}_{ij}
resembles the Physics laws that characterize interactions between objects, such as Coulomb’s law or Newton’s law of universal gravitation, where probability mass represents the charge or the mass of the objects involved. This formal similarity coincides with the intuition that
\widehat{w}_{ij}
measures the degree of connectivity between
D_{i}
and
D_{j}
.

Definition 3
.

We define the
discretized population graph

\mathcal{G}=(V,E,{\bm{W}})
with the same
V
and
E
as in the discretized graph
\mathcal{G}_{n}
from
Definition

2
, but a different weight matrix
{\bm{W}}=(w_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots,m}
, which is defined as
w_{ij}\coloneqq p_{i}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}_{i\sim j}
for the probability vector
{\bm{p}}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})\in[0,1]^{m}
and
p_{i}=\nu(D_{i})
.

We are mainly interested in the relation between the empirical graph cuts
\XC(\mathcal{G}_{n})
and its population counterpart
\XC(\mathcal{G})
, where the only difference lies in the associated weight matrices. The empirical graph
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is an empirical version of
\mathcal{G}
using the plug-in estimator
{\bm{Y}}/n
of
{\bm{p}}
. Note that the XCut objective function (in
Definition

1
) can be seen as a functional of the weight matrix. Thus, for every partition
S\in\mathcal{S}
, there exists a functional
g_{S}^{\XC}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

g_{S}^{\XC}\Bigl(\tfrac{{\bm{Y}}}{n}\Bigr)=\XC_{S}(\mathcal{G}_{n})\quad\text{and}\quad g_{S}^{\XC}({\bm{p}})=\XC_{S}(\mathcal{G}).

(2.2a)

More precisely, it takes the form of

g_{S}^{\XC}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\coloneqq\sum_{i\sim j,\,i\in S,\,j\in{S}^{\mathsf{c}}}\frac{x_{i}x_{j}}{\ball_{S}^{\XC}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})},

(2.2b)

where
\ball_{S}^{\XC}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
encodes the balance term
\bal(S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}})
. For instance, in case of NCut,

\ball_{S}^{\NC}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=\left(\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{j:j\sim i}x_{i}x_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{k\in{S}^{\mathsf{c}}}\sum_{l:l\sim k}x_{j}x_{l}\right).

Thus,
(M3)
actually requires the existence of the Hadamard directional derivative of the balance functional
\ball_{S}^{\XC}(\cdot)
at
{\bm{p}}
, which we denote by
\bigl(\ball_{S}^{\XC}\bigr)_{{\bm{p}}}^{\prime}
.

Main results

3.1
Limiting distributions for graph cuts

We start with the asymptotic distributions of graph cuts over all partitions.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under
(M1)
–
(M3)

, fix a distance threshold
t>0
. For
S\in\mathcal{S}
, define
{\bm{q}}_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
as

({\bm{q}}_{S})_{i}\coloneqq q_{i,S}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\sum_{j\in{S}^{\mathsf{c}},j\sim i}p_{j}&\text{ if }i\in S,\\
\sum_{j\in{S},j\sim i}p_{j}&\text{ if }i\in{S}^{\mathsf{c}},\end{cases}

(3.1)

and define the balance functional
\ball_{S}^{\XC}(\cdot)
as in
(
2.2
)
. Then:

(i)

As
n\to\infty
, it holds that

\Bigl(\sqrt{n}\left({\XC}_{S}(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\XC_{S}(\mathcal{G})\right)\Bigr)_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\longrightarrow}}\;\left(\frac{\langle{\bm{Z}},{\bm{q}}_{S}\rangle-\XC_{S}(\mathcal{G})\,\bigl(\ball_{S}^{\XC}\bigr)_{{\bm{p}}}^{\prime}({\bm{Z}})}{\bal(S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}})}\right)_{S\in\mathcal{S}},

where
{\bm{Z}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{m}({\bm{0}},{\bm{\Sigma}})
and

4
Extensions and variations

We now present several extensions of the asymptotic results of
Section

3
, and consider a computational surrogate (
Xist
, introduced in
Suchan et al., 2023
) for graph cuts that can be computed in polynomial time, more precisely in
\mathcal{O}(Nnm)
for MCut, RCut, NCut and CCut, with
N

(see
Definition

5
below), thus opening the door for a plethora of applications of our limit theorems.

4.1
A fast algorithm to approximate balanced graph cuts via
st
-min cuts

The disadvantage of balanced graph cuts is their computational complexity that is rooted in their combinatorial nature. It has been shown that the problem of computing NCut is NP-complete, see
(
Shi and Malik, 2000
, Appendix A, Proposition 1)
; the idea behind their proof can be adapted for the other balanced cuts in
Table

1
, namely, RCut and CCut (see
Šíma and Schaeffer (2006)
for an alternative proof of the latter). Several other balanced graph cuts are also known to be NP-complete to compute, for instance, the graph cut with the balancing term
\bal(S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}})=\min\{|S|,|{S}^{\mathsf{c}}|\}
, see
Mohar (1989)
. Thus, the balanced graph cuts become computationally infeasible even for problems of moderate sizes in practice. This remains the case even for relaxed versions of graph cuts
(
Wagner and Wagner, 1993
)
and also for approximations of graph cuts
(
Bui and Jones, 1992
)
. Moreover, the computation of balanced multiway graph cuts are also NP-complete
(
Mohar, 1989
)
, even if the graph is a grid or tree
(
Andreev and Räcke, 2004
)
, or if the graph cut value is approximated by a finite factor
(
Feldmann and Foschini, 2012
)
.

Spectral clustering is a popular method that circumvents this issue by relaxing the problem, but this approach can lead to qualitative drawbacks (e.g.
Guattery and Miller, 1998
;
Nadler and Galun, 2007
). A different approach was presented in
Suchan et al. (2023)
, where the
Xist algorithm
(
X
Cut
i
mitation through
\mathbf{st}
-MinCuts) was introduced. In essence, this algorithm computes the minimum XCut value over a certain subset of partitions, namely that of
st
-MinCuts, and it only considers vertices
s
and
t
that satisfy a local maximality property, i.e. their degree being larger than that of their neighbors. These restrictions allow a version of our limit results to still apply to the
Xist
algorithm, see
Theorem

4.1
later. Further, by
(
Suchan et al., 2023
, Theorem 3.2)
,
Xist
is polynomial in time: Its computational complexity is
\mathcal{O}(|\Vloc|\max\{|V||E|,\kappa\})
, where
\mathcal{O}(\kappa)
is set to be the complexity of computing
\XC_{S}(G)
for a fixed partition
S\in\mathcal{S}
and graph
G
, and
\Vloc\subseteq V
is defined below. Note that for graph cuts such as RCut, NCut or CCut,
\kappa=|V|\eqqcolon m
, meaning that in many scenarios,
Xist
would be able to run faster than spectral clustering (which has a worst-case complexity of
\mathcal{O}(|V|^{3})
, see
von Luxburg, 2007
). Note that the polynomial complexity of
Xist
does not contradict the NP-completeness of computing balanced graph cuts since the algorithm essentially minimizes the graph cut functional over a certain subset of partitions only (and not the full set of partitions
\mathcal{S}
).

In order to state the

(4.1)

The
Xist
algorithm consists of three parts: First, the computation of the set of local maxima
\Vloc
. The intuition behind restricting
Xist
to this subset of vertices is as follows: In the absence of a balancing term, an
st
-MinCut minimizes the sum of the weights of the cut edges in order to separate
s
and
t
. If
s
and
t
are local maxima belonging to different and, say, neighboring clusters, the
st
-MinCut is can be expected to separate
s
and
t
by cutting between the two clusters since there, connections are not as tight as within clusters. An additional benefit of the restriction to
\Vloc
is the reduction in computation time, see Appendix
A.6
for a brief discussion, or Section 4.2 in
Suchan et al. (2023)
for an empirical comparison with related algorithms.

Second,
Xist
computes
st
-MinCuts according to a selection procedure determined by
\tau

Gomory and Hu (1961)
, all
st
-MinCuts are computed by constructing (what is now called) a Gomory–Hu tree through selective vertex contraction, though it was later discovered to be usable without contractions entirely
(
Gusfield, 1990
)
. In short, through
\tau
the procedure ensures that for each distinct
st
-MinCut value,
s,t\in\Vloc
, at least one partition attaining this value is considered by the algorithm, while still only computing
|\Vloc|-1

st
-MinCuts. For more details regarding the cut pair selection procedure of
Xist
see
Suchan et al. (2023)
, in particular Theorem 3.1 and the corresponding proof.

Third,
Xist
outputs the minimum XCut among all
st
-MinCuts it computed. This gives rise to the intuition that
Xist
acts as a computational surrogate of the original graph cuts by retaining computation of the exact XCut values, only not over all partitions
S\in\mathcal{S}
but a certain subset.

Additionally, the design of the algorithm guarantees that the resulting partition is reasonable in the sense that it separates two vertices
s
and
t
through the
st
-MinCut partition
S_{st}
while also taking cluster size into account via the balancing term in the XCut value
\XC_{S_{st}}(\mathcal{G})
. This makes the NP-complete problem of computing balanced graph cuts approachable from a qualitative perspective since the output of
Xist
is guaranteed to be an
st
-MinCut for some
s,t\in\Vloc
.

In order to state a limit theorem for

-MinCuts. By design,

only requires one partition that attains the

-MinCut value for each pair

\mathcal{G}_{n}

Lemma

A.7
in the
Appendix A

st

\mathcal{G}_{n}
may still be unique, as demonstrated later in

To deal with the technical complexity caused by the nonuniqueness of

.

Under

4.2
Multiway cuts

Multiway cuts
are graph cuts that split the nodes of a graph into
k\in\mathbb{N}
pairwise disjoint subsets. The multiway MCut, known as the
k
-cut problem
, can be solved in polynomial time
Goldschmidt and Hochbaum (1994)
. If, however, one would prespecify
k
vertices to belong to disjoint subsets, i.e. construct a version of the
st
-MinCut for
k\geq 3
vertices, the resulting problem would be NP-complete in general
Dahlhaus et al. (1994)
. As previously stated, even the case of
k=2
is NP-complete if balancing terms are introduced. The literature on multiway cuts is rather limited and mostly consists of simple extensions of XCut results (
k=2
) to general
k\in\mathbb{N}
(see e.g.
von Luxburg (2007)
;
García Trillos et al. (2016)
), similar to the way we will proceed in the following.

Definition 6
.

For a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,{\bm{W}})
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, introduce the set of
k
-partitions of
V
as

\mathcal{S}^{k}\coloneqq\left\{S=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}:\,\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}S_{i}=V,\text{ and }S_{i}\cap S_{j}=\varnothing\text{ for all }i\neq j\right\}.

The
balanced multiway graph cut

\kXC
of the graph
\mathcal{G}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\kXC(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{S=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}\in\mathcal{S}^{k}}\kXC_{S}(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad\kXC_{S}(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{i\in S_{\ell},j\in{S_{\ell}}^{\mathsf{c}}}\frac{w_{ij}}{\bal(S_{\ell},{S_{\ell}}^{\mathsf{c}})}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\XC_{S_{\ell}}(\mathcal{G}).

The factor
1/2
in
\kXC
is included to avoid counting each edge twice. The case
k=1
is trivial. In case of
k=2
,
\XC_{S}(\mathcal{G})=\kXC_{\{S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}}\}}(\mathcal{G})
for any
S\in\mathcal{S}
or equivalently for any
\{S,{S}^{\mathsf{c}}\}\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
. Thus,
\kXC
is equivalent to the usual
\XC
for
k=2
. Further, as
\kXC
is merely a (scaled) sum of cuts, it is not difficult to adapt
Theorems

3.1
and
3.3
for general
k\geq 2
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume the same setup as in
Theorem

3.1
, and let
{\bm{Z}}^{\XC}=(Z_{S}^{\XC})_{S\in\mathcal{S}}
be the limiting distribution of
\bigl(\sqrt{n}\left(\XC_{S}(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\XC_{S}(\mathcal{G})\right)\bigr)_{S\in\mathcal{S}}
therein. Then:

(i)

As
n\to\infty
, it holds that

\Bigl(\sqrt{n}\bigl({\kXC}_{S}(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\kXC_{S}(\mathcal{G})\bigr)\Bigr)_{S\in\mathcal{S}^{k}}\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\longrightarrow}}\quad\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}Z_{S_{\ell}}^{\XC}\right)_{S=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}\in\mathcal{S}^{k}}.

(ii)

If the balance functional
\ball_{S}^{\XC}(\cdot)
is further differentiable at
{\bm{p}}
, then

\Bigl(\sqrt{n}\bigl(\kXC_{S}(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\kXC_{S}(\mathcal{G})\bigr)\Bigr)_{S\in\mathcal{S}^{k}}\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\longrightarrow}}\quad\mathcal{N}_{|\mathcal{S}^{k}|}\big({\bm{0}},{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\kXC}\big),

where the covariance matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\kXC}=({\bm{\Sigma}}_{T,S}^{\kXC})_{T,S\in\mathcal{S}^{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{k}|\times|\mathcal{S}^{k}|}
is given by

{\bm{\Sigma}}_{T,S}^{\kXC}\coloneqq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{T_{i},S_{j}}^{\XC}\qquad\text{for}\quad T=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}),\,S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})\in\mathcal{S}^{k},

with
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{T,S}^{\XC}
, for
T,S\in\mathcal{S}
, given in
Theorem

3.1
(ii).

(iii)

Let
\mathcal{S}_{*}^{k}\coloneqq\bigl\{S\in\mathcal{S}^{k}\,:\,\kXC_{S}(\mathcal{G})=\kXC(\mathcal{G})\bigr\}.
Then

\sqrt{n}\bigl({\kXC}(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\kXC(\mathcal{G})\bigr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{1}{2}\min_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{*}^{k}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}Z_{S_{\ell}}^{\XC}.

Note that
Theorems

3.1
and
3.3
can be seen as the particular case of
Theorem

4.2
when
k=2
. Further, one can derive the explicit limiting distributions for the multiway versions of MCut, RCut, NCut and CCut, by means of
Corollary

3.2
.

5.2
Influence of neighborhood distance

Consider the same two distributions as in
Figure

4

t
. The discrepancy between sample and limiting distribution will be measured by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance

D_{n,t}^{\XC}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{F}_{n,t}^{\XC}(x)-F^{\XC}_{t}(x)\bigr|,

where
\widehat{F}_{n,t}^{\XC}
is the distribution function of

\sqrt{n}\big(\XC(\mathcal{G}_{n,t})-\XC(\mathcal{G}_{t})\big)

with the subscript
t
indicated the dependence on the neighborhood distance
t
, and
F_{t}^{\XC}
is the distribution function of
\min_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{*}}Z_{S}^{\XC}
. We obtain
F_{t}^{\XC}
by Monte Carlo simulation (generating 50,000 samples). With
D_{n,t}^{\XC}
one can construct a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test against the null hypothesis
H_{0}
that
F_{t}^{\XC}
is the underlying distribution of
\widehat{F}_{n,t}^{\XC}
. This test will reject
H_{0}
if
D_{n,t}^{\XC}>q_{1-\alpha}
for the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile
q_{1-\alpha}
of the Kolmogorov distribution (which is the distribution of
\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|B(t)|
with
B(t)
the standard Brownian bridge).
We use a sample size of
n=2{,}000
and an (asymptotic) significance level
\alpha=0.05
, see
Figure

6
.
Overall, RCut converges fastest and most consistently across for all choices of
t
. Also, for all three cuts, a small neighborhood of
t=1
, i.e. connecting only directly adjacent nodes, apparently yield distributions that come closest to the limit (which is not surprising since choosing the smallest possible
t
is natural for only
m=9
nodes). Another interesting observation is that in the uniform case for
t\in[\sqrt{5},\sqrt{8})
, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance converges rather slowly for both RCut and CCut. Overall, CCut seems to perform worst out of all three cuts. Its curious behavior, especially in
Figure

, could be subject to a more thorough investigation in the future.

Another quantity of interest is the variance of the limiting distribution derived in

3.1
and how it behaves as the neighbourhood distance

5.4
Asymptotic testing

Asymptotic hypothesis tests are a prominent application for limit theorems, and graph cuts in particular offer interesting possibilities in this regard.
Consider an arbitrary probability vector
{\bm{p}}
, and let
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}_{n}
be the population and empirical graphs, respectively, constructed from (a sample from)
{\bm{p}}
. Further, let
Q_{{\bm{p}}}^{\XC}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be the quantile function of the limiting distribution of
\sqrt{n}\bigl(\XC(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\XC(\mathcal{G})\bigr)
according to
Theorem

3.3
. To simplify notation, let
Q^{\mathrm{unif}}
be the quantile function for the above limiting distribution for the uniform probability vector
{\bm{p}}^{\mathrm{unif}}\coloneqq(\tfrac{1}{m},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{m})^{\top}
, and let
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{unif}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{n}^{\mathrm{unif}}
be the corresponding population and empirical graphs.
We now seek to test

H_{0}:\XC(\mathcal{G})=\XC(\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{unif}})\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:\XC(\mathcal{G})\neq\XC(\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{unif}}),

(5.1)

i.e. whether cutting the distribution
{\bm{p}}
yields a different cut value than the uniform distribution. If the underlying distribution has two or more modes, we expect the resulting cut value to be small since its associated partition separates the modes that are assumed to be balanced (i.e. (close to) maximizing the graph cut balancing term), thus yielding a lower cut value than if the same partition were applied to the uniform distribution. In the case of one mode, however, the resulting partition is expected to either remove a small subset of vertices near the border (if the single cluster is very pronounced) or cut through the cluster, thus sacrificing a . In contrast, for the uniform distribution, one expects a markedly different (i.e. more balanced) graph cut partition and therefore also a different cut value. This means that the test problem
(
5.1
)
will reveal whether there is clustering structure in the underlying distribution.

Fix
\alpha\in[0,1]
as the asymptotic level of significance. We reject in the testing problem
(
5.1
)
if the underlying distribution satisfies
\XC(\mathcal{G}_{n})<\XC(\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{unif}})+Q^{\mathrm{unif}}(\alpha/2)/\sqrt{n}
or if
\XC(\mathcal{G}_{n})>\XC(\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{unif}})+Q^{\mathrm{unif}}(1-\alpha/2)/\sqrt{n}
. This is because that under
H_{0}
, by
Theorem

3.3
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\XC(\mathcal{G}_{n})\in\Bigl[\XC(\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{unif}})+\frac{Q^{\mathrm{unif}}(\alpha/2)}{\sqrt{n}},\XC(\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{unif}})+\frac{Q^{\mathrm{unif}}(1-\alpha/2)}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr]\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{p}}^{\mathrm{unif}}}\bigl(\sqrt{n}\bigl(\XC(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\XC(\mathcal{G})\bigr)\in\bigl[Q^{\mathrm{unif}}(\alpha/2),Q^{\mathrm{unif}}(1-\alpha/2)\bigr]\bigr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}1-\alpha,

i.e. under
H_{0}
the rejection probability is indeed asymptotically at most
\alpha
.

Consider now the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
in
(
5.1
)
. If
\XC(\mathcal{G})>\XC(\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{unif}})
, centering around
\XC(\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{unif}})
will not yield a limiting distribution; in fact,
Theorem

3.3
together with Slutsky’s lemma dictates that
\sqrt{n}\bigl(\XC(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\XC(\mathcal{G})\bigr)
will degenerate towards infinity in probability, so that the test above will reject with probability tending to 1. In contrast, if the sample stems from a distribution for which
\XC(\mathcal{G})<\XC(\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{unif}})
holds, the centering around
\XC(\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{unif}})
also does not yield a limiting distribution which degenerates towards negative infinity. Hence, as
n\to\infty
, the considered test will not reject with probability approaching 1. This demonstrates that the above testing procedure provides a means to decide whether the distribution underlying a given sample is predominantly uniform, or whether it exhibits some kind of clustering structure, be it uni- or multimodal.

6
Conclusion and discussion

We have obtained explicit limiting
distributions for balanced graph cuts in a general discretized setup that is particularly suited to be make graph cuts more applicable.
Theorem

3.3
determined the limiting distribution for the most prominent graph cuts such as RCut (Ratio Cut), NCut (Normalized Cut) and CCut (Cheeger Cut), thereby revealing several case distinctions that decide whether the limit is the minimum of Gaussians or that of a random mixture of Gaussians. We stress that the discretization approach we presented can be easily generalized in several directions. Firstly, we chose edge weights
\widehat{w}_{ij}=\smash{Y_{i}Y_{j}\mathbbm{1}_{i\sim j}}
to appropriately model the dependency between the discretized nodes. This notion can be extended to any Hadamard directionally differentiable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e. by considering
\widehat{w}_{ij}=f(Y_{i},Y_{j})\mathbbm{1}_{i\sim j}
. Then, deriving the limiting distributions for these new edge weights is analogous to the procedure we introduced in the preceding as will become apparent by the proofs as presented in the
Appendix A
Appendix
, although the resulting variances will obviously be different. What will not change, however, is the fact that the limit of
\sqrt{n}(\XC_{S}(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\XC_{S}(\mathcal{G}))
will still be Gaussian for MCut (Minimum Cut), RCut and NCut if
f
has a linear Hadamard derivative. Again, CCut will not abide by a normal limit due to the non-differentiability of its balancing term. It is possible to generalize further, however, by only requiring
f
to be Hadamard directionally differentiable. The appropriate approach then would be to apply the delta method for Hadamard directionally differentiable functions (
Theorem

A.3
) analogously to the steps we undertook to handle CCut, thus possibly resulting in non-Gaussian limits for the aforementioned statistic. Making changes to the neighbourhood structure, i.e. replacing the
t
-neighborhood that influences the weights by means of
\mathbbm{1}_{i\sim j}
also yields analogous results as long as the neighborhood does not depend on
{\bm{Y}}
. Otherwise, the neighborhood would need to be accounted for in the function
f
as well, with similar implications (i.e. requiring Fréchet or Hadamard directional differentiability in order to apply our results).

Another direction is changing the discretization itself. In order to obtain explicit limits with the proposed approach it was necessary to fix the discretization
D=\sum_{i=1}^{m}D_{i}
. Now, as the sample size
n
increases, it would be natural to increase the discretization resolution so that one is able to detect finer details, allowing for a more accurate partitioning of the resulting graph. Since this would violate the fixed structure of the underlying probability vector
{\bm{p}}\in[0,1]^{m}
and fixed number
m\in\mathbb{N}
of districts
D_{i}
, our multinomial model would not immediately apply. This scenario may be rather involved: Apart from apparent technical difficulties, it remains unclear whether there is still a reasonable limiting distribution for the cut values — this will be postponed to subsequent work.

Definition 8

\mathbb{E}_{Z_{n}\sim\widehat{L}_{n}}\bigl[f(Z_{n})\bigr]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}_{n}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim L}\bigl[f(Z)\bigr]\quad\text{for all }f\in C(\mathcal{D},[0,1]),

(A.2)

In this section we revisit

Lemma A.7
.

Let
{\bm{Y}}\sim\mult(n,{\bm{p}})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and some probability vector
{\bm{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
. Then, for any
i,j\in V
,
i\neq j
, with
p_{i},p_{j}\in[0,1]
(but not both zero), it holds that
\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}=Y_{j})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
i,j\in V
,
i\neq j
, be arbitrary, but fixed. If
p_{i}\neq p_{j}
, the claim holds simply by the law of large numbers, so w.l.o.g. let
p_{i}=p_{j}
. We apply the continuous mapping theorem on the function
\langle{\bm{e}}_{i}-{\bm{e}}_{j},\cdot\rangle
and obtain

\frac{Y_{i}-Y_{j}}{\sqrt{n}}=\left\langle{\bm{e}}_{i}-{\bm{e}}_{j},\sqrt{n}\bigl(\tfrac{{\bm{Y}}}{n}-{\bm{p}}\bigr)\right\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,({\bm{e}}_{i}-{\bm{e}}_{j})^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{e}}_{i}-{\bm{e}}_{j})\bigr).

Note that
({\bm{e}}_{i}-{\bm{e}}_{j})^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{e}}_{i}-{\bm{e}}_{j})=p_{i}(1-p_{i})+p_{j}(1-p_{j})+2p_{i}p_{j}>0

since
0<p_{i},p_{j}<1
(if
p_{i}
or
p_{j}
were equal to
1
, the claim would be trivial) and not both are equal to zero by assumption. This shows that the limiting distribution above is non-degenerate. Consequently, for any
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}=Y_{j})\leq\mathbb{P}\left(-\varepsilon<\frac{Y_{i}-Y_{j}}{\sqrt{n}}\leq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=F_{n}(\varepsilon)-F_{n}(-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}F(\varepsilon)-F(-\varepsilon)=\mathbb{P}(-\varepsilon<Z_{ij}\leq\varepsilon),

where
F_{n}
and
F
denote the cdf of
(Y_{i}-Y_{j})/\sqrt{n}
and its limiting distribution
Z_{ij}:\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,p_{i}+p_{j}-(p_{i}-p_{j})^{2}\bigr)
, respectively. Since the right hand side of the above equation becomes arbitrarily small as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we have
\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}=Y_{j})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for any
i,j\in V
,
i\neq j
.
∎

In the following we derive a limiting distribution for the

and proof a CLT-type result for it in

\XC(G)

The
basic Xvst algorithm
contains the essence of
Xist
, namely the principle of choosing the best XCut out of a subset of (or, in this case, all)
st
-MinCuts. In contrast to
Xist
, however, the
basic Xvst algorithm
does not restrict itself to
\Vloc
, and it does not contain any Gomory–Hu-type construction to improve its computational complexity. By
Suchan et al. (2023, Theorem 3.2)
, the
basic Xvst algorithm
runs in
\mathcal{O}(|V|^{3}|E|)
, so one order of magnitude slower than
Xist
if
|V|
and
|\Vloc|
are of the same order, e.g. if
p_{1}=\cdots=p_{m}
for
\mathcal{G}
. In practice, however, this case is not likely to happen, especially since
\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}=Y_{j})\to 0
for any
i\neq j
as
n\to\infty
by
Lemma

A.7
.

In order to gauge the computational impact that the restriction to
\Vloc
has on
Xist
compared to the
basic Xvst algorithm
, the expected number of local maxima is of interest, particularly if an underlying discretized distribution exhibits
p_{i}=p_{j}
for many connected vertex pairs
i,j\in V
. This quantity is difficult to compute exactly in general, however, due to
{\bm{\Sigma}}
being non-invertible, meaning that the limiting distribution of
\sqrt{n}({\bm{Y}}/n-{\bm{p}})
has no density. While this could be alleviated by restriction onto a subspace, it does not help with the second issue of having to compute a large number of integrals since the information whether vertex
i
is local maximum has a
“cascading effect”
, i.e. it introduces a dependency structure in the form of
Y_{i}
on all connected vertices. Consequently, the only feasible way to gauge the advantages of
Xist
is via Monte Carlo simulation. Consider a
\ell\times\ell
grid with
p_{1}=\cdots=p_{m}=1/m
for
m=\ell^{2}
— the law of large numbers allows us to ignore any non-uniform distributions — with edges only between directly horizontally and vertically adjacent vertices. Simulations show that the expected number of local maxima is approximately
4/3
for
\ell=2
,
8.6
for
\ell=5
,
22.5
for
\ell=10
and
49.4
for
\ell=15
, meaning that it seems to scale linearly with
m
, and, on average,
Xist
only considers around one fifth of all vertices, leading to a runtime that is 25 times faster than the
basic Xvst algorithm
, notably on a uniform distribution which is can be considered the
“worst-case scenario”
for the improved algorithm. Moreover, when the number of edges increases, the number of local maxima decreases, meaning
Xist
performs even better compared to the
basic Xvst algorithm
. Exemplified on the
\ell\times\ell
-grid with uniform distribution above, adding edges between directly diagonally adjacent vertices decreases the expected number of local maxima to around an eighth of all vertices. For a more thorough comparison of
Xist
and the
basic Xvst algorithm
, in particular regarding the impact of and explanation behind the
st
-MinCut selection procedure through
\tau
, see
Suchan et al. (2023)
.

Before deriving the limiting distribution of
basic Xvst algorithm
, we require a small auxiliary result that reformulates
Assumption
(A4)
to make it better usable in the following proofs.

Lemma A.8
.

Assumption
(A4)
holds if and only if the all components corresponding to elements in each
\mathcal{S}_{st}
,
s,t\in V
,
s\neq t
, of the MCut limiting distribution
{\bm{Z}}^{\MC}
are almost surely non-equal, i.e.

q_{i,S}=q_{i,T}\;\text{ for all }i\in V\iff\mathbb{P}(Z_{S}^{\MC}=Z_{T}^{\MC})=0.

Proof.

By
Theorem

4.2

(ii)
the MCut limit
{\bm{Z}}^{\MC}
is distributed according to the Gaussian
\mathcal{N}_{|\mathcal{S}|}({\bm{0}},{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\MC})
with
\Sigma_{S,T}^{\MC}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}q_{i,S}q_{i,T}-4\MC_{S}(\mathcal{G})\MC_{T}(\mathcal{G})
. Fix now
s,t\in V
,
s\neq t
as well as
S,T\in\mathcal{S}_{st}
(although the following holds for any two partitions
S
and
T
). Consider the vector
{\bm{a}}\coloneqq{\bm{e}}_{S}-{\bm{e}}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
, where
{\bm{e}}_{S}
and
{\bm{e}}_{T}
are unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
with one in the
S
-th and
T
-th component, respectively. Then,

{\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\MC}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\MC}{\bm{a}}).

This, however, means that
\mathbb{P}(Z_{S}^{\MC}=Z_{T}^{\MC})=\mathbb{P}(Z_{S}^{\MC}-Z_{T}^{\MC}=0)
is zero if and only if the variance of the Gaussian above is zero as well. We rewrite this as follows:

\displaystyle 0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}{\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\MC}{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{S,S}^{\MC}-2\Sigma_{S,T}^{\MC}+\Sigma_{T,T}^{\MC}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(q_{i,S}^{2}-2q_{i,S}q_{i,T}+q_{i,T}^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad-4\bigl(\MC_{S}(\mathcal{G})^{2}-2\MC_{S}(\mathcal{G})\MC_{T}(\mathcal{G})+\MC_{T}(\mathcal{G})^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(q_{i,S}-q_{i,T})^{2}\biggr)-\left(\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}q_{i,S}\biggr)-\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}q_{i,T}\biggr)\right)^{2},

where in the last step we used the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}q_{i,R}=2\MC(R)
for any partition
R\in\mathcal{S}
. As the
p_{i}
’s are all positive and sum up to 1, and
x\mapsto x^{2}
is convex, Jensen’s inequality dictates that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(q_{i,S}-q_{i,T})^{2}\geq\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(q_{i,S}-q_{i,T})\biggr)^{2}

with equality if and only if
q_{i,S}-q_{i,T}=q_{j,S}-q_{j,T}
for all
i,j\in V
. This new condition is now obviously satisfied if
q_{i,S}=q_{i,T}
, so it remains to show the converse.

To show this assume that
q_{i,S}-q_{i,T}=q_{j,S}-q_{j,T}
for all
i,j\in V
, and consider the fact that
by
Assumption
(A4)
both
S
and
T
attain the minimal
st
-MinCut. Define
\lambda\coloneqq q_{i,S}-q_{i,T}
, which is by assumption is independent of
i\in V
. Thus

\displaystyle 0=2\MC(S)-2\MC(T)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}q_{i,S}\bigr)-\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}q_{i,T}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(q_{i,S}-q_{i,T})=\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}=\lambda,

showing

for all
i\in V
, and finishing the proof.
∎

Proof of

.

We prove this using the same procedure we showed
Theorem

3.1
with. First, recall again that
\sqrt{n}({\bm{Y}}/n-{\bm{p}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}_{m}({\bm{0}},{\bm{\Sigma}})
, where
{\bm{\Sigma}}=(\Sigma_{i,j})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is the covariance matrix of the multinomial distribution
\mult(1,{\bm{p}})
. Now, for XCut and any partition
S\in\mathcal{S}
we define the function

\displaystyle{\bm{g}}^{\kXC}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|},\quad

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}\,\mapsto\,\left(g_{S}^{\kXC}({\bm{x}})\right)_{S\in\mathcal{S}}

for

\displaystyle g_{S}^{\kXC}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}\,\mapsto\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}g_{S_{\ell}}^{\XC}({\bm{x}}),

where
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
,
S=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}\in\mathcal{S}^{k}
, and
g_{S}^{\XC}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is the XCut functional we used throughout this paper.

(i)
: If
g_{S}^{\XC}
is Hadamard differentiable, we apply the delta method for Hadamard directionally differentiable functions (
Theorem

A.3
) to receive:

\sqrt{n}\Big(\kXC_{S}(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\kXC_{S}(\mathcal{G})\Big)=\sqrt{n}\left(g_{S}^{\kXC}\left(\tfrac{{\bm{Y}}}{n}\right)-g_{S}^{\kXC}({\bm{p}})\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\longrightarrow}}\left((g_{S}^{\kXC})_{{\bm{p}}}^{\prime}({\bm{Z}})\right)_{S\in\mathcal{S}},

where

. It is evident from
Definition

7

(g_{S}^{\kXC})_{{\bm{p}}}^{\prime}({\bm{Z}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}(g_{S_{\ell}}^{\XC})_{{\bm{p}}}^{\prime}({\bm{Z}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}Z_{S_{\ell}}^{\XC}\quad\text{for any}\quad S=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}\in\mathcal{S}^{k}.

Here

is the limiting distribution of
\sqrt{n}(\XC_{S_{\ell}}(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\XC_{S_{\ell}}(\mathcal{G}))
from
Theorem

3.1
.

(ii)
: If
g_{S}^{\XC}
is
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-differentiable, we may apply the standard delta method:

\displaystyle\Bigl(\sqrt{n}\bigl(\kXC_{R}(\mathcal{G}_{n})-\kXC_{R}(\mathcal{G})\bigr)\Bigr)_{R\in\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sqrt{n}\bigl(g_{R}^{\kXC}\bigl(\tfrac{{\bm{Y}}}{n}\bigr)-g_{R}^{\kXC}({\bm{p}})\bigr)\Bigr)_{R\in\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}_{|\mathcal{S}|}\bigl({\bm{0}},{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\kXC}\bigr),

so that for

\displaystyle\bigl({\bm{\Sigma}}^{\kXC}\bigr)_{T,S}
