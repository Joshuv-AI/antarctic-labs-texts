Optimal Photodetector Size for High-Speed Free-Space Optics Receivers
Muhammad Salman Bashir, Senior Member, IEEE, Qasim Zeeshan Ahmed, Member, IEEE, Mohamed-Slim Alouini, Fellow, IEEE

Abstract
The selection of an optimal photodetector area is closely linked to the attainment of higher data rates in optical wireless communication receivers. If the photodetector area is too large, the channel capacity degrades due to lower modulation bandwidth of the detector. A smaller photodetector maximizes the bandwidth, but minimizes the captured signal power and the subsequent signal-to-noise ratio. Therein lies an opportunity in this trade-off to maximize the channel rate by choosing the optimal photodetector area. In this study, we have optimized the photodetector area in order to maximize the channel capacity of a free-space optical link for a diverse set of communication scenarios. We believe that the study in this paper in general—and the closed-form solutions derived in this study in particular—will be helpful to maximize achievable data rates of a wide gamut of optical wireless communication systems: from long range deep space optical links to short range indoor visible light communication systems.

I-A

Trade-off Between Detector Bandwidth and SNR

Laser photodiodes are the basic detection elements in a modern optical receiver that are used not only for detection of optical symbols but also serve as position or angle-of-arrival sensors in a quad detector array configuration
[
6
,
7
]
. Photodiodes are classified as either P-N, P-I-N or avalanche photodiodes
[
8
]
. As the name implies, a P-N photodiode is made up of a P-N junction—a boundary between two types of semiconductor materials, the P-type semiconductor and N-type semiconductor. A P-I-N diode consists of a undoped intrinsic semiconductor region between the P-type and the N-type semiconductor materials. P-I-N photodiodes have faster switching capability compared to their P-N counterparts
[
9
]
. Avalanche photodiodes (APD) are either based on Silicon, Germanium or InGaAs semiconducting materials. A distinguishing feature of APD’s is that they are operated with a high reverse voltage that allows them to detect very low levels of signal energy. This feature endows them with high sensitivity and high signal-to-noise ratio characteristics compared to P-N and P-I-N photodiodes
[
10
,
11
]
.

The existence of P-N junction in a photodiode leads to capacitive
C
, and resistive
R
effects that can limit the switching rate—or the response speed—of a photodiode. This phenomenon is captured by
RC
time constant of a photodiode: a larger
RC
time constant leads to a reduced response time or modulation frequency (or cut-off frequency) of a photodiode and vice versa
[
12
]
. In order to be able to transmit high-data rates in optical wireless channels, a low
RC
time constant or a high modulation frequency is highly desirable. Since the capacitance of a diode is directly related to its active area, photodiodes with a smaller area lead to a higher modulation frequency or bandwidth. On the other hand, if the active area of the photodiode is too small, the signal energy captured by the photodiode diminishes. This leads to poor signal-to-noise ratio at the receiver photodiode and a poor signal detection performance. Thus, a trade-off exists between diode bandwidth and signal-to-noise ratio, and we ought to choose the optimal diode size or area in order to maximize the communication performance of the optical receiver.

I-B

Motivation of Current Study

The channel capacity is a useful metric that captures both the bandwidth as well as the signal-to-noise ratio of a communication channel. This metric was first employed in
[
13
]
to illustrate that the throughput (or capacity) of an pulse amplitude modulation optical wireless channel is maximized at a particular photodetector area. In order to demonstrate this concept, the channel capacity of a coherent Gaussian optical wireless channel—denoted by
\mathcal{C}
—is furnished by the well-known

[
14
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=W\mathcal{C}_{0},

\displaystyle=W\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\bar{P}}{N_{0}W}\right),

(1)

where
W
is the bandwidth in Hz,
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is channel capacity in bits per channel use,
\bar{P}
is the average received signal power in Watts and
N_{0}
is the two-sided power spectral density in Watts/Hz. Here, the quantity
\frac{\bar{P}}{N_{0}W}
is the signal-to-noise ratio. In (
1
), we observe that the Gaussian channel capacity (measured in bits per second) is the product of two terms—the bandwidth
W
and the channel capacity in terms of bits per channel use,
\mathcal{C}_{0}
. We also observe that the signal-to-noise ratio in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is itself inversely proportional to the bandwidth
W
. The bandwidth

is inversely proportional to the area

of the photodiode, whereas signal-to-noise ratio is directly proportional to the cube of photodiode active area:
A^{3}
. Thus, for a very small
A
, the available modulation bandwidth will be quite large, whereas the signal-to-noise ratio—and, therefore, the capacity
\mathcal{C}_{0}
—will diminish. On the other hand, a large photodiode area
A
leads to high capacity per channel use
\mathcal{C}_{0}
but negligible bandwidth
W
. This hints at the existence of an optimal trade-off—in terms of best or optimal area
A
—between bandwidth
W
and
\mathcal{C}_{0}
that leads to the maximization of the product (of these two terms): the channel capacity in bits per second. This phenomenon is illustrated in Fig.
1
where the bandwidth

A

(bits per channel use) is increasing monotonically with area

.

Even though the study
[
13
]
hints at the existence of an optimal photodetector area for maximization of channel capacity through a graphical illustration, they did not provide any closed-form analytical solutions for the optimal photodetector area. Moreover, the said study considers only one type of channel: the Gaussian laser channel that is not affected by signal fading. Since the optimization of photodetector area is an important problem in our estimation, there is a strong motivation to look for optimal (or even suboptimal) closed-form analytical solutions of the photodetector area for the maximization of Gaussian channel capacity. Additionally, signal-dependent shot noise appears in optical systems where the effect of background radiation can be quite large, and this requires optimization of photodetector area for signal dependent Gaussian noise channels. Finally, since detector arrays have been shown to provide significant performance improvement over a single detector for optical symbol detection
[
15
]
, maximization of capacity of channels involving detector array receivers—as a function of individual detector size—is an important problem for future optical wireless communication systems. All of the above mentioned considerations form the motivation of the current study that deals with the optimization of photodetector area for a diverse set of free-space optical communication scenarios.

I-D

Contributions of Our Study

With the exception of
[
13
]
and our understanding, there is no exiting research or literature that analyzes the relationship between channel capacity and the photodetector area of an optical wireless communication channel. Even though the said paper highlights the fact that the channel capacity peaks at a certain (optimal) photodetector area, a detailed analysis and derivations of (even approximate) closed-form solutions are not provided. Additionally, the focus of
[
13
]
is on just one type of channel: the signal independent thermal noise channel that is modeled by a Gaussian distribution. In this study, we carried out a comprehensive analysis on the optimization of photodetector area for channel capacity maximization. Specifically, we i) derived closed-form solution (in terms of Lambert W function) of optimal photodetector area for the signal independent Gaussian noise channel as furnished by (
23
), ii) provided analytical closed-form solutions of close-to-optimal area for intensity fading channels as given by (
42
) and (
44
), iii) analyzed channels based on a detector array receiver and provided closed-form optimal solutions for equal gain combining and maximal ratio combining arrays as supplied by (
64
) and (
70
), respectively, and iv) provided numerical solutions of optimal detector area for shot noise limited regime that is modeled by signal dependent Gaussian noise. A major conclusion of our study states that—for the signal independent Gaussian noise case—the optimal area is proportional to the factor
\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}
where the term
\frac{\alpha}{\beta}
is the inverse of the normalized signal-to-noise ratio of the optical receiver. The constant
m=3
for channels involving either the single photodetector or the maximal ratio combining array, and
m=2
for equal gain combining array. This implies that as the signal-to-noise ratio improves, the optimal detector area shrinks in order to exploit the extra bandwidth that comes with a smaller area. Based on this discussion, we propose an adaptive optical wireless communications receiver that
consists of a number of photodetectors with varying active areas. For such receivers, the “optimal” photodetector based on the value of estimated signal intensity is chosen at any given time in order to maximize the channel capacity. A conceptual example of such an adaptive receiver is shown in Fig.
2
which consists of a signal intensity estimation block, a selection block and three photodetectors of different areas. The intensity estimation block operates on pilot symbols to estimate signal intensity with the help of an array of detectors
[
34
]
. The intensity estimate is then fed into a selection block that selects the best photodetector to maximize channel capacity for incoming data symbols. This adaptive receiver can effectively mitigate the effect of slow intensity fading due to atmospheric turbulence or pointing error.

\displaystyle f_{\textsf{RC}}=\frac{1}{2\pi RC},

III-B

Channel Capacity

If we assume that the only major source of noise in the circuit is the
thermal noise
with variance
\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2}
, we have that
\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2}=N_{0}W
where
W
is the photodiode bandwidth and
N_{0}
is one-sided noise spectral density measured in W/Hz. Here, we assume that the photodiode resistance is negligible compared to circuit resistance, and therefore
\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2}
is generated mainly by the circuit resistive elements.

The Gaussian channel capacity of a coherent optical channel is reproduced below as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{W}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\mu_{s}^{2}}{\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2}}\right),

(13)

where the bandwidth

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\coloneqq f_{\textsf{RC}}=\frac{\alpha}{A}.

(14)

By substituting the expression for
\mu_{s}
and
\sigma_{\textsf{th}}
in (
13
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}A^{2}}{N_{0}\mathcal{W}}\right).

(15)

After substituting the expression of
\mathcal{W}
in (
15
), the final expression for channel capacity is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\frac{\alpha}{A}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right),

(16)

where

\displaystyle\beta_{0}\coloneqq\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{N_{0}}.

(17)

The factor
\dfrac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}
is the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the optical channel, and the term
\dfrac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}
is the normalized SNR value.

III-C

Optimal Photodetector Area

In order to find the optimal photodetector area that maximizes the channel capacity furnished by (
16
), we rewrite the capacity in terms of natural log function as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\frac{\alpha}{A\ln(2)}\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right).

(18)

Taking the partial derivative of (
18
) with respect to area
A
and setting it equal to zero, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{C}}{\partial A}=0

\displaystyle\implies\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)-3\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}=0

(19)

Subtracting three from both sides of (
19
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)-3\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}-3=-3

\displaystyle\implies\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)-3\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)=-3

\displaystyle\implies\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)\left(\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)-3\right)=-3.

(20)

Dividing both sides of (
20
) with
\exp(3)
and noting that
x=\exp(\ln(x))
, we have that

\displaystyle\left(\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)-3\right)\exp\left(\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)-3\right)=-3\exp(-3).

(21)

Now, using the fact that the solution of the equation of the form

\displaystyle x\exp(x)=y\implies x=W(y),

(22)

where
W(\cdot)
is the Lambert W function, we have that (
21
) can be solved as

\displaystyle\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)-3=W\left(-3\exp(-3)\right)

\displaystyle\implies 1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}=\exp\left(W\left(-3\exp(-3)\right)+3\right)

\displaystyle\implies A^{*}=\left(\exp\left(W\left(-3\exp(-3)\right)+3\right)-1\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}.

(23)

Let us define the factor
\gamma_{0}
as

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}\coloneqq\left(\exp\left(W\left(-3\exp(-3)\right)+3\right)-1\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}.

(24)

To show that the solution (
23
) is indeed a maximizer, we evaluate the second derivative of capacity
\mathcal{C}
at
A=A^{*}
. We have that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{C}}{\partial A^{2}}=\frac{1}{\ln(2)}\left(\frac{-9\beta_{0}^{2}A^{3}/\alpha}{\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)^{2}}+\frac{2\alpha}{A^{3}}\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)\right),

(25)

and

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{C}}{\partial A^{2}}\right|_{A=A^{*}}=\frac{\beta_{0}}{\ln(2)}\left(\frac{-9\gamma_{0}}{(1+\gamma_{0})^{2}}+\frac{2}{\gamma_{0}}\ln(1+\gamma_{0})\right)\approx-\frac{2.0947\beta_{0}}{\ln(2)}.

(26)

Since the second derivative is negative at
A^{*}
, we have that
A^{*}
represents global maximizer of channel capacity. Therefore, from (
23
), we note that the optimal area varies as the cube root of the factor
\frac{\alpha}{\beta_{0}}
which is the inverse of normalized SNR value.

Ergodic Capacity Maximization Under Intensity Fading in Free-Space Optical Channels

IV-A

Channel Capacity Under Intensity Fading

The erogodic capacity of a free-space optical channel under fading is described by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{W}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\mu_{s}^{2}h^{2}}{N_{0}\mathcal{W}}\right)f_{\mathbbm{h}}(h)\,dh,

(27)

where

is the distribution of random channel coefficient
\mathbbm{h}
, and
\mu_{s}
is the maximum signal power captured by the optical receiver under no fading. The signal does not experience fading when the channel coefficient
\mathbbm{h}=1
with probability one, i.e.,
\mathbbm{P}(\{\mathbbm{h}=1\})=1.

\mathcal{R}
is defined as

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{R}}(r)\coloneqq\dfrac{r}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{r^{2}}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(r),

(30)

where
\sigma_{p}
is the
scale parameter
of the pdf
f_{\mathcal{R}}(r)
. By noting that
h_{\mathcal{R}}
is the realization of
\mathbbm{h}_{\mathcal{R}}
, the ergodic capacity—under pointing error—is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{W}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\mu_{s}^{2}h_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}}{N_{0}\mathcal{W}}\right)f_{\mathcal{R}}(r)\,dr,

(31)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{W}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\mu_{s}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)}{N_{0}\mathcal{W}}\right)\dfrac{r}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{r^{2}}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right)\,dr

(32)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha}{A}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)}{N_{0}\alpha}A^{3}\right)\dfrac{r}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{r^{2}}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right)\,dr

(33)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha}{A}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\beta(r)}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)\dfrac{r}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{r^{2}}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right)\,dr,

(34)

where

\displaystyle\beta(r)\coloneqq\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)}{N_{0}},r>0.

(35)

Let us denote the optimal photodetector area
A^{*}
for a realization
r
of random variable
\mathcal{R}
as
A^{*}(r)
where

\displaystyle A^{*}(r)=\gamma_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta(r)}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}=\gamma_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha N_{0}}{\mu_{0}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}.

(36)

The (suboptimal) average area in this case is

\displaystyle A^{\star}=\int_{0}^{\infty}A^{*}(r)\dfrac{r}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{r^{2}}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right)\,dr

(37)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha N_{0}}{\mu_{0}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\dfrac{r}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{r^{2}}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right)\,dr

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha N_{0}}{\mu_{0}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\exp\left(-r^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}-\frac{1}{3\rho^{2}}\right)\right)\,dr,

(38)

where the integral in (
38
) converges if the factor

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sigma_{p}^{2}}-\frac{1}{3\rho^{2}}>0\implies\rho>\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\sigma_{p}.

(39)

This condition implies that the spot radius has to be at least
\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}
times the pointing error scale factor
\sigma_{p}
. This condition is almost always satisfied since the beam footprint is made large enough to accommodate any deviations due to misalignment or pointing error. In most scenarios, a suboptimal value of beam radius
\rho
(in relation to
\sigma_{p}
) is given by
\rho=\sqrt{2}\sigma_{p}

[
29
]
.

When the condition (
39
) holds, we have that

\displaystyle A^{\star}=\gamma_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha N_{0}}{\mu_{0}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\exp\left(-r^{2}\left(\frac{3\rho^{2}-2\sigma_{p}^{2}}{6\rho^{2}\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right)\right)\,dr

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha N_{0}}{\mu_{0}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2\left(\frac{3\rho^{2}\sigma_{p}^{2}}{3\rho^{2}-2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right)}\right)\right)\,dr.

(40)

Dividing and multiplying (
40
) by the factor
\dfrac{3\rho^{2}}{3\rho^{2}-2\sigma_{p}^{2}}
, we obtain the suboptimal photodetector area as

\displaystyle A^{\star}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha N_{0}}{\mu_{0}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(\frac{3\rho^{2}}{3\rho^{2}-2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r}{\frac{3\rho^{2}\sigma_{p}^{2}}{3\rho^{2}-2\sigma_{p}^{2}}}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2\left(\frac{3\rho^{2}\sigma_{p}^{2}}{3\rho^{2}-2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right)}\right)\right)\,dr

(41)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha N_{0}}{\mu_{0}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(\frac{3\rho^{2}}{3\rho^{2}-2\sigma_{p}^{2}}\right),

(42)

since the integral in (
41
) evaluates to unity.

IV-C

Suboptimal Detector Area Under Strong Atmospheric Turbulence

Assuming the case of strong atmospheric turbulence that is modeled by the negative exponential distribution, we note that the suboptimal average area in this case is

\displaystyle A^{\star}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}A^{*}(h)\frac{1}{\eta}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\eta}h\right)\,dh,

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta(h)}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\frac{1}{\eta}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\eta}h\right)\,dh,

(43)

where
\eta>0
is the mean of negative exponential distribution and
\beta(h)=\dfrac{(\mu_{0}h)^{2}}{N_{0}}
. This implies that

\displaystyle A^{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{0}\alpha^{\frac{1}{3}}N_{0}^{\frac{1}{3}}}{\eta\mu_{0}^{\frac{2}{3}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}h^{-\frac{2}{3}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\eta}h\right)\,dh

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{0}\alpha^{\frac{1}{3}}N_{0}^{\frac{1}{3}}}{\eta\mu_{0}^{\frac{2}{3}}}\eta^{\frac{1}{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\frac{2}{3}}\exp(-t)\,dt\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{0}\alpha^{\frac{1}{3}}N_{0}^{\frac{1}{3}}\Gamma(\frac{1}{3})}{\mu_{0}^{\frac{2}{3}}\eta^{\frac{2}{3}}},

(44)

where the gamma function
\Gamma(\cdot)
is defined as
\Gamma(z)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}\exp(-t)\,dt.

V
Channel Capacity Maximization of Detector Array Receivers

Fig. 5:
Gaussian beam projected onto a
4\times 4
detector array.

In this section, we consider the channel capacity of a detector array receiver in free-space optical communications. Detector arrays are commonly used for tracking the angle-of-arrival of laser beams by virtue of estimation of spot position on a detector array in focal plane
[
7
,
31
]
. However, detector arrays can also be used in a joint beam tracking/symbol detection configuration which simplifies receiver hardware by getting rid of a separate beam tracking assembly dedicated exclusively to beam tracking
[
15
]
.

As shown in Fig.
5
, the detector array considered in this study is of a square configuration where the individual elements or detectors are also of a square shape and are arranged in an
n\times n
arrangement for
n
a positive integer. Therefore, the total number of individual detectors in the array is
M=n^{2}
. We further assume that the area of the array is
\mathcal{A}
m
2
. Without any loss of generality, we assume that the array is centered at the origin
(0,0)
.

For thermal noise limited regime, the output
Y_{m}
of the
m
th element of the detector array is given by

\displaystyle Y_{m}=\Lambda_{m}+X_{m},\quad m=1,2,\dotsc,M,

(45)

In (
45
), the signal component
\Lambda_{m}
is defined as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{m}\coloneqq\iint_{A_{m}}\Lambda_{s}(x,y)\,dy\,dx,

(46)

where
\Lambda_{s}
is furnished by (
10
). The noise component
X_{m}
is distributed as a Gaussian random varaible:
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2}=N_{0}W)
. We note that
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
are independent and identically distributed random variables for integers
i
and
j
such that
i\neq j
and
1\leq i,j\leq M
.

In order to form the sufficient statistic with an array of detectors, we look at two signal combining schemes: the
equal gain combiner
(EGC) and the
maximal ratio combiner
(MRC). The EGC combines the output of each detector of the array with uniform weights, whereas the MRC weights the output of each detector with the optimal intensity value before combining
[
15
,
33
]
. It is important to note here that the intensity in each detector is not the same as the intensity in every other detector since the intensity of the laser spot in the focal plane varies according to Gaussian distribution as given by (
10
). In the presence of random fluctuation of angle-of-arrival, the spot location on the array will change randomly. In such a scenario, diversity schemes such as the MRC are quite effective in countering the effect of misalignment provided that the spot location on the array can be tracked effectively in real time
[
7
,
15
]
. Here, we also remind the reader that the MRC algorithm, discussed in this paper, does not take into account the phase of the optical signal since we are dealing with intensity modulated/direct detection (IM/DD) scheme in this study.

In the upcoming sections, we look at the ECG and MRC combining schemes for a detector array receiver. We will also consider the optimization of individual detector area of the array that maximizes the capacity that is achieved with these combining schemes.

V-A

Equal Gain Combining

An equal gain combiner simply adds output from each element of the array to form the sufficient statistic
Y
as follows:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m}=S+X,

(47)

where
S=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Lambda_{m}
is the signal component and
X=\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}
is the noise component. Since the noise in each detector of the array is assumed to be independent, the variance of
X
is
MN_{0}W
. The channel capacity of this scheme is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Lambda_{m}\right)^{2}}{MN_{0}\mathcal{W}}\right)

(48)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\mu_{s}^{2}}{MN_{0}\mathcal{W}}\right)

(49)

where
\mu_{s}\coloneqq\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Lambda_{m}
is the total energy captured by the detector array. The bandwidth
\mathcal{W}
in (
49
) is a function of number of detectors
M
in the array provided the array area
\mathcal{A}
is fixed. It is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=\frac{d}{2\pi\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{r}RA}=\frac{dM}{2\pi\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{r}R\mathcal{A}},

(50)

where
A
is the area of a single detector in the array.

V-B

Maximal Ratio Combining

The output
Y_{m}
of the
m
th element of the array is weighted by the signal component
\Lambda_{m}
before fusion takes place for the formation of the sufficient statistic. The resulting sufficient statistic,
Y
, is

\displaystyle Y=\sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m}\Lambda_{m}=S+X,

(51)

where the signal component
S=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Lambda_{m}^{2}
and the noise component
X=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Lambda_{m}X_{m}
. We note that
X
is a zero-mean Gaussian random variable with variance
\sigma_{X}^{2}=N_{0}W\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Lambda_{m}^{2}
. The channel capacity of this scheme is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{S^{2}}{\sigma_{X}^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{W}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Lambda_{m}^{2}\right)^{2}}{N_{0}\mathcal{W}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Lambda_{m}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Lambda_{m}^{2}}{N_{0}\mathcal{W}}\right),

(52)

where
\mathcal{W}
is given by (
50
).

V-C

Optimization Problem

For maximization of channel capacity with an array of detectors, we optimize the area
A
of the single detector element while we keep the total area of the array constant. Thus, we consider the optimization problem of the following type:

\displaystyle\underset{A}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

(53)

\displaystyle\textrm{subject to}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{0}.

In this optimization problem,
\mathcal{C}
is furnished by (
49
) for the equal gain combiner and (
52
) for maximal ratio combining. The factor
\mathcal{A}_{0}
is a positive constant.

V-C
1
Optimization of Photodetector Area for EGC Array Receiver

In order to obtain a closed-form solution to (
53
) for the EGC array, we rewrite (
49
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\mu_{s}^{2}}{MN_{0}\mathcal{W}}\right)

(54)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{A}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\mu_{s}^{2}}{\mathcal{A}N_{0}\alpha}A^{2}\right),

(55)

where we used the fact that the number of detectors
M=\frac{\mathcal{A}}{A}
, and that the bandwidth of the array is the same as the bandwidth of each individual element or detector of the array:
\mathcal{W}=\frac{\alpha}{A}
. Let us denote the factor

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\coloneqq\frac{\mu_{s}^{2}}{N_{0}}=\frac{\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Lambda_{m}\right)^{2}}{N_{0}}.

(56)

Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{A}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}\right)

(57)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{A\ln(2)}\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}\right).

(58)

The quantity
\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}
is the normalized SNR of the EGC array. Here, we note that when the individual detector area
A
is fixed, increasing array area
\mathcal{A}
implies increasing the number of detectors
M
in the array. Thus, for fixed
A
, increasing
\mathcal{A}
implies increasing the total noise in the array since noise power increases linearly with the number of elements
M
of the array.

In order to find the maximum of
\mathcal{C}
in (
58
) as a function of area
A
, we take the derivative of
\mathcal{C}
as a function of
A
and set it equal to zero to obtain

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}\right)\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}\right)-2\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}=0.

(59)

Subtracting two from both sides of (
59
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}\right)\left(\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}\right)-2\right)=-2.

(60)

Representing
\left(1+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}\right)
by
\exp\left(\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}\right)\right)

and dividing both sides of (
60
) by
\exp(2)
, we have that

\displaystyle\exp\left(\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}\right)-2\right)\left(\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}\right)-2\right)=-2\exp(-2)

(61)

\displaystyle\implies\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}\right)-2=W\left(-2\exp(-2)\right)

(62)

\displaystyle\implies 1+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\mathcal{A}\alpha}A^{2}=\exp\left(W\left(-2\exp(-2)\right)+2\right)

(63)

\displaystyle\implies A^{*}=\left(\exp\left(W\left(-2\exp(-2)\right)+2\right)-1\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\alpha\mathcal{A}}{\beta_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(64)

Let us define
\gamma_{1}\coloneqq\left(\exp\left(W\left(-2\exp(-2)\right)+2\right)-1\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
. In order to show that
A^{*}
is indeed a global maximizer, we evaluate the second partial of channel capacity at
A=A^{*}
:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{C}}{\partial A^{2}}\right|_{A=A^{*}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{1}^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{A}^{\frac{3}{2}}\ln(2)}\left(-\frac{4\gamma_{1}}{\left(1+\gamma_{1}^{2}\right)^{2}}-\frac{2}{\gamma_{1}\left(1+\gamma_{1}^{2}\right)}+\frac{2\ln\left(1+\gamma_{1}^{2}\right)}{\gamma_{1}^{3}}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{0.1218\beta_{1}^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{A}^{\frac{3}{2}}\ln(2)}

(66)

which is strictly negative at
A=A^{*}
. Hence,
A^{*}
in (
64
) is indeed a global maximizer of the channel capacity achieved with an EGC array.

From (
64
), we observe that the optimal area is proportional to the square root of the inverse of normalized SNR which is in contrast to the cube root relationship for a single-detector optical receiver (see (
23
)).

V-C
2
Optimization of Photodetector Area for MRC Array Receiver

In order to maximize capacity of an MRC array—given by (
52
)—as a function of photodetector area, we first define the average signal intensity
\Lambda_{s}^{(m)}
(measured in Watts/unit area) on the
m
th detector of the array:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{s}^{(m)}\coloneqq\frac{\Lambda_{m}}{A}.

(67)

We now rewrite (
52
) as a function of
\Lambda_{s}^{(m)}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{A^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{(m)}\right)^{2}}{N_{0}\mathcal{W}}\right)

(68)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{A}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha}A^{3}\right)

(69)

where
\beta_{2}\coloneqq\dfrac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\Lambda_{s}^{(m)}\right)^{2}}{N_{0}}
. We note that the channel capacity of an MRC array has the same form as the channel capacity of a single detector (given by (
16
)). Therefore, the optimum detector area for the MRC array is

\displaystyle A^{*}=\left(\exp\left(W\left(-3\exp(-3)\right)+3\right)-1\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}.

(70)

Here, the optimal photodetector area varies as cube root of normalized SNR as opposed to square root relationship for EGC array.

VI
Channel Capacity Under Signal Dependent Shot Noise

In this section, we consider the channel capacity maximization for the general case where the channel suffers both from shot noise and thermal noise. Let the number of received signal photons be equal to
N_{s}
. Then,
\mathbbm{E}[N_{s}]=\mu_{s}
where
\mathbbm{E}[\cdot]
is the ensemble average operator. The average background radiation intensity (giving rise to dark current) is denoted by
\lambda_{b}
Watts/m
2
. The total noise power due to background radiation is
\mu_{b}\coloneqq\lambda_{b}A
. Let the average number of noise photons be
N_{b}
. In this case,
\mathbbm{E}[N_{b}]=\mu_{b}
.

Here, for the sake of simplicity, let us consider the popular on-off keying (OOK) signaling. For the “on” state, let the total number of photons (signals plus noise ) be denoted by
N_{t}=N_{s}+N_{b}
. In low-to-medium photon rate regime, the random variable
N_{t}
is modeled by a Poisson distribution with parameter
(\mu_{s}+\mu_{b})
. In high photon rate regime,
N_{t}
converges (in distribution) to a Gaussian random variable:
N_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu=\mu_{s}+\mu_{b},\sigma_{\textsf{sh}}^{2}=\mu_{s}+\mu_{b})
when the signal pulse is present. When no signal is present,
N_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu=\mu_{b},\sigma_{\textsf{sh}}^{2}=\mu_{b})
. Here,
\sigma_{\textsf{sh}}^{2}
represents the (signal dependent) power in
shot noise
.

In the high photon rate regime, the signal-to-noise ratio (denoted by the symbol
SNR
) is given by

SNR

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{s}^{2}}{\sigma_{\textsf{sh}}^{2}+\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2}}=\frac{\mu_{s}^{2}}{\mu_{s}+\mu_{b}+\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{A\ll\pi\rho^{2}}{\approx}\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}A^{2}}{\mu_{0}A+\lambda_{b}A+\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2}}=\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}A}{\mu_{0}+\lambda_{b}+\frac{\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2}}{A}}.

(71)

Thus, the
\textsf{SNR}\to 0
as
A\to 0
.

For the binary OOK channel, the output
Y
is related to random input
X
as

\displaystyle Y=X+N_{\textsf{sh}}+N_{\textsf{th}},

(72)

where the random variable
N_{\textsf{sh}}
corresponds to signal dependent shot noise with distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\textsf{sh}}^{2})
, and
N_{\textsf{th}}
represents thermal noise with distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2})
. We assume that
N_{\textsf{sh}}\perp N_{\textsf{th}}
. For this channel, we obtain the capacity as the maximization of the difference of entropy
H(X)
and conditional entropy
H(X|Y)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{0}\coloneqq\max_{p(x)}\left(H(X)-H(X|Y)\right)

(73)

where
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is capacity in bits per channel use and
p(x)
is the distribution of input
X
. The capacity
\mathcal{C}
in bits per second is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=W\mathcal{C}_{0}.

(74)

In (
73
), the input
X
is a discrete random variable and the output
Y
is sampled from a continuous distribution. The quantity
H(X)\coloneqq-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x)\log_{2}p(x),
is the entropy of the input
X
where the set
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\{0,1\}
. The conditional entropy is

\displaystyle H(X|Y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\int_{y}p(x)p(y|x)\log_{2}\frac{p(y|x)p(x)}{p(y)}\,dy,

(75)

where
p(y)\coloneqq\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(y|x=i)p\left(x=i\right)
. Moreover,

\displaystyle p(y|x=1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(\mu_{0}A+\lambda_{b}A+\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2})}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y-\mu_{0}A-\lambda_{b}A)^{2}}{2(\mu_{0}A+\lambda_{b}A+\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2})}\right),

\displaystyle p(y|x=0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(\lambda_{b}A+\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2})}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y-\lambda_{b}A)^{2}}{2(\lambda_{b}A+\sigma_{\textsf{th}}^{2})}\right).

(76)

The closed-form solution of optimal detector area that maximizes channel capacity in (
74
) is not easy to achieve. Therefore, for signal dependent shot noise scenario, we resort to finding the peak of channel capacity numerically (graphically) in Section
VII
.

VIII
Conclusion

In this paper, we carried out a detailed analysis of the relationship between the photodetector area and the capacity of a free-space optical channel. For the signal independent Gaussian noise optical channel, we discovered that the analytical solutions for optimal area are functions of the term
\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}
where
\frac{\beta}{\alpha}
is the normalized SNR. This result indicates that the optimal photodetector area is inversely proportional to signal-to-noise ratio at the receiver. We also carried out channel capacity maximization for intensity fading channels and derived suboptimal solutions that provide quite a good approximation to optimal solutions, especially at high SNR. For the signal dependent shot noise channel that is dominated by noise from background radiation, we discovered that the optimal area increases with increasing SNR at the receiver, a fact that is opposite to the channel capacity behavior for signal independent Gaussian noise. The results of this study can be used to devise a novel optical wireless receiver that comprises a number of photodetectors of different areas (see Fig.
2
). For such a receiver, a photodetector of appropriate area can be chosen—based on the estimated value of signal intensity—in order to mitigate the effect of slow intensity fading either due to atmospheric turbulence or pointing error.
