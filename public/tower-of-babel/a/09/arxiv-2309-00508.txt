Geometry and Local Recovery of Global Minima of Two-layer Neural Networks at Overparameterization
Tao Luo luotao41@sjtu.edu.cn, Leyang Zhang leyangz_hawk@outlook.com, Yaoyu Zhang zhyy.sjtu@sjtu.edu.cn

Abstract
Under mild assumptions, we investigate the geometry of the loss landscape for two-layer neural networks in the vicinity of global minima. Utilizing novel techniques, we demonstrate: (i) how global minima with zero generalization error become geometrically separated from other global minima as the sample size grows; and (ii) the local convergence properties and rate of gradient flow dynamics. Our results indicate that two-layer neural networks can be locally recovered in the regime of overparameterization.



keywords: neural networks, global minima, analytic function theory, non-linear analysis, gradient flow, recovery stability

Introduction

Over the past decade, neural networks—a distinct class of nonlinear models—have revolutionized the field of artificial intelligence, including the theoretical studies and applications. However, the mathematical underpinnings that set them apart from other models are not well comprehended. Deciphering these structures poses a daunting but essential challenge for the field of mathematics. Among the various aspects of neural networks, the loss landscape is obviously important in shaping their training dynamics and generalization capabilities
E et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2020
. Of particular interest is the geometry of the global minima, which lies at the heart of a fundamental enigma: how neural networks are able to find well-generalizing solutions via global training from an ostensibly infinite pool of global minima—a majority of which do not generalize well—especially when the network is overparameterized
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Breiman 1995
.

In our research, we propose and address the geometric structure and local recovery problem for two-layer neural networks. To be precise, we uncover an important geometric structure of the perfect global minima, namely, set that recovers the target function: this set consists of different “branches” (subsets) which become separated from imperfect global minima at overparameterization. The separation property guarantees local recovery capability of the target function and surely reduces the difficulty in finding well-generalizing solutions globally via gradient dynamics. In fact, the separation of branches occur successively as training sample size increases. Beyond the separation property, we show that the geometry near different branches are vary significantly, which leads to the distinct limiting behavior of gradient flows nearby. Note that all these are inherent to the family of neural network models.

Our work demonstrates the profound impact of the fine geometry of global minima to the generalization of neural networks. In traditional machine learning problems, the global minima usually have trivial geometry, e.g., isolated points, rendering generalization a separate issue from loss landscape analysis
Sun et al. 2020
. However, for neural networks, in particular at overparameterization, we showcase the importance of analyzing the geometry of the well-generalizing set, e.g., the perfect global minima, amid the global minima of loss landscape for the understanding of generalization. Our view of the global minima that particularly highlights the structure of the perfect global minima as the backbone and its generalization consequence is a significant refinement over the view suggested by Cooper
Cooper 2021
which focuses on the overall geometry of the global minima. We hope our work could convince the audiences in mathematics that the global minima, or more broadly the critical points of the loss landscape, of neural networks possess rich geometric structures that are amenable to analysis, mathematically interesting, intrinsic to the model architecture, and undoubtedly plays a fundamental role to their exceptional training and generalization performance.

Technically speaking, the study of global minima in the loss landscape presents two fundamental issues. The first issue is the linear independence of neurons (and their derivatives). It determines geometric structures of the loss landscape, such as dimensions of global minima. Previous works address the linear independence of neurons for the analysis of critical points
Simsek et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2020
. In our work, to understand when perfect global minima are separated from the imperfect ones, we establish the linear independence of neurons
and their derivatives
. The second issue is that neural network architecture yields highly degenerate Hessian of loss at a global minimum. The loss may fail to be a Morse function, or even a generalized Morse–Bott function, thus beyond the reach of traditional methods. We devise methods for addressing gradient flow dynamics in the vicinity of degenerate critical points, which are indispensable to the analysis of neural networks. This outcome broadens the scope of most existing research that primarily focuses on gradient flow dynamics near critical points of Morse and generalized Morse–Bott functions. Furthermore, our investigations into the dynamics around global minima have yielded unprecedented results in terms of convergence rates and directions.

2.2
Local Recovery Problem

In this paper, we propose the concept of the local recovery problem in the context of neural networks at overparametrization. This problem refers to the challenge of ensuring that a neural network can perfectly recover the target function (the learned function has zero generalization error), in a local region of its parameter space. The local recovery problem arises due to the complex and non-convex nature of the loss landscape in neural networks, where infinitely many global minima exist and form complex patterns.

The key aspects of the local recovery problem include:

(a)

Geometric structure:
As we investigate the recovery of our target function, we are particularly interested in the geometry of global minima and the relation between sample size and global minima geometry.

(b)

Separation of minima:
In overparameterized neural networks, the possibility that perfect global minima, i.e., those that accurately recover the target function, can become separated from other global minima. This ensures our model to recover target function.

(c)

Convergent gradient dynamics:
The limiting properties of gradient-based optimization methods near these minima is affected by the local recovery problem. Understanding the dynamics in the vicinity of perfect global minima is essential for efficient training.

By proposing the local recovery problem, we aim to highlight how these geometric and dynamic properties impact the network’s ability to find these perfectly generalizing solutions throughout training process. Addressing this problem involves developing strategies to navigate the complex loss landscape and understand why the network can recover the target function effectively in local regions.

To illustrate the local recovery problem more concretely, we will use a specific example. Consider a two-neuron model with exponential activation function and with one-dimensional input, i.e.,
g(\theta,x)=a_{1}e^{w_{1}\cdot x}+a_{2}e^{w_{2}\cdot x}
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\theta=(a_{1},w_{1},a_{2},w_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
. Consider the training data
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and hence the loss

\displaystyle R(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g(\theta,x_{i})-y_{i}\right|^{2}.

By Cooper’s results
Cooper 2021
, up to an arbitrarily small perturbation of the
y_{i}
’s,
R^{-1}\{0\}
is a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{6}
with dimension
\max\{0,6-n\}
. In our paper, by considering the cases where
y_{i}
’s are sampled from a target function
f^{*}
expressible by the given two-layer NN, which allows for perfect generalization, we uncover more detailed structure of the global minima of
R
. For illustration, we consider a simple example with
f^{*}(x)=\bar{a}e^{\bar{w}\cdot x}
(
\bar{a}\neq 0
). The loss function writes

\displaystyle R(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g(\theta,x_{i})-\bar{a}e^{\bar{w}\cdot x_{i}}\right|^{2}.

First, note that
e^{w_{1}\cdot x}
and
e^{w_{2}\cdot x}
are linearly independent if and only if
w_{1}\neq w_{2}
. Thus, based on the number of distinct
w_{k}
’s, we have a partition of the perfect global minima as
Q^{*}=Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}\cup Q_{3}
independent of the training inputs, where

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=\{(a,\bar{w},\bar{a}-a,\bar{w}):a\in\mathbb{R}\}

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle=\{(\bar{a},\bar{w},0,w):w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{\bar{w}\}\}

\displaystyle Q_{3}

\displaystyle=\{(0,w,\bar{a},\bar{w}):w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{\bar{w}\}\}.

Geometrically,
Q_{1},Q_{2}
and
Q_{3}
look like three “branches” with different dimensions. See also the figure below.

Figure 2:
Illustration of
Q^{*}
. The closure of the branches
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}
are all affine subspaces in the parameter space. Moreover,
Q_{1},Q_{2}

Observe that
\overline{Q_{1}},\overline{Q_{2}},\overline{Q_{3}}
are all affine subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{6}
with different dimensions. In general, when sample size

, we expect no imperfect global minima, thus the NN achieves perfect generalization when it converges to zero loss. However, at overparameterization, i.e., when sample size
n<6
, imperfect global minimum generally exists, which sparkles the question of whether each branch of perfect global minima are enclosed by the imperfect global minima. If not, the training dynamics clearly has chance to converge to some perfect global minima, thus achieving perfect generalization.
Inspired by this example, we notice that in general, to understand the problem of achieving perfect generalization for a two-layer neural network (
m
,
m_{0}
and
n
are arbitrary), we must investigate the following questions:

(a)

Geometric structure:
How can we describe the perfect global minima
Q^{*}
geometrically? Does it consist of branches as for the example above?

(b)

Separation of minima:
How is
Q^{*}
related to
R^{-1}\{0\}
, in particular to the imperfect global minima
R^{-1}\{0\}\setminus Q^{*}
? Can it be “separated” from
R^{-1}\{0\}\setminus Q^{*}
? How does this depend on samples?

(c)

Convergent Gradient dynamics:
What are the convergence, convergence rate, convergence direction, etc., of gradient flows near
R^{-1}\{0\}
? Moreover, what can we say about the stability of these gradient flows?

In
Simsek et al. 2021
;
Fukumizu and Amari 2000
, Simsek and Fukumizu already have an answer to question (a), i.e., the geometry of
Q^{*}
. In the papers they show that
Q^{*}
is a set with lots of symmetry. Moreover, it is the union of finitely many branches of different dimensions, the closure of each branch being an affine subspace. See also Section
3.4
. Based on the geometry of
Q^{*}
, we provide detailed answers to questions (b) and (c) in Sections
4
and
5
. A summary of the results can be found in the following Section
2.3
, where the informal theorems (Theorem
2.1
to question (b) and Theorem
2.2
to question (c)) are presented. Then we apply these theorems to this example.

2.3
Main Results

The main results of this paper are theorems resolving the local recovery of two-layer neural networks. Let us summarize and discuss them informally as follows. For the separation of branches of
Q^{*}
we have the following.

Theorem 2.1

(separation of branches in
Q^{*}
)
.

Let
\{Q_{t}\}_{t=1}^{N}
be the branches of
Q^{*}
. Each branch
Q_{t}
corresponds to a sample size threshold
N_{t}\leq m(d+1)
(and if
m>m_{0}
, we have
N_{t}<m(d+1)
), such that when sample size
n\geq N_{t}
,
Q_{t}
is “separated” from the imperfect global minima. Moreover, by rearranging the indices of
Q_{t}
if necessary, there is a partition

Q^{*}:=\bigcup_{t=1}^{N}Q_{t}=\left(\bigcup_{t=1}^{N^{\prime}}Q_{t}\right)\bigcup\left(\bigcup_{t=N^{\prime}+1}^{N}Q_{t}\right)

such that whenever
t\leq N^{\prime}
and
n\geq N_{t}
,
R
is not Morse–Bott anywhere at
Q_{t}
, while for
t>N^{\prime}
and
n\geq N_{t}
,
R
is Morse–Bott a.e. at
Q_{t}
.

By saying that
Q_{t}
is separated we mean there is an open
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
such that
U\cap R^{-1}\{0\}=U\cap Q_{t}
. For definition of a Morse–Bott function
f
we mean the
\mathrm{Hess}\,f
is non-degenerate along the normal bundle of a manifold in
(\nabla f)^{-1}\{0\}
(Definition
4.1
). The details of Theorem
2.1
will be shown in Lemmas
14
and
16
in Section
4
, which relies on the theory of real analytic functions contained in Section
3.2
.

Finally, any gradient flow near
R^{-1}\{0\}
has the following properties.

Theorem 2.2

(gradient flow near global minima)
.

Following the hypotheses and notations in Theorems
2.1
, any gradient flow sufficiently close to
R^{-1}\{0\}
converges. On the other hand, any point in
R^{-1}\{0\}
is the limit of some gradient flow. The following results hold.

(a)

Whenever
t\leq N^{\prime}
and sample size
n\geq N_{t}
, a generic gradient flow sufficiently close to
Q_{t}
converges to a point
\theta^{*}\in Q_{t}
. The convergence does not have linear rate and the curve is “biased towards”
\ker\mathrm{Hess}R(\theta^{*})
. Moreover, any small perturbation of it still converges to
Q_{t}
.

(b)

Given
t>N^{\prime}
. When sample size
n\geq N_{t}
, any gradient flow sufficiently close to
Q_{t}
converges to points
Q_{t}
at linear rate. Similar to (a), any small perturbation of it still converges to
Q_{t}
.

In short, we characterize the convergence (Theorem
5.2
), limiting set (Proposition
5.1
), limiting direction and convergence rate of gradient flows near global minimum, especially near
Q^{*}
(Theorem
5.3
). Meanwhile we develop a concept called “generalization stability” which discusses how the limiting model (under gradient flow) changes at perturbation of gradient flow (see Definition
5.1
and the remark below).

The theorems above exhibit comprehensively the structural change of geometry and local dynamics of global minima at the overparameterized regime, with the perfect global minima
Q^{*}
as its backbone. In particular, the branch separation and convergence results guarantees the local recovery capability of two-layer neural networks, i.e., the target function will be recovered when initialized near separated branches. Furthermore, our results suggest the following mechanism of generalization at overparameteration that deserve further study: with proper generic initialization and hyperparameter tuning, the gradient dynamics can be globally guided to a neighbourhood of the separated branches of
Q^{*}
, thus recovering the target function at convergence. In the following, we illustrate above results in a simple example.

Let’s start with a lemma about power series.

Lemma 2

(characterization of
\varepsilon
-polynomial)
.

Let
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}\varepsilon^{j}
be a power series of real or complex coefficients
\{c_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
such that for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
there are some odd number
j_{\mathrm{odd}}>N
and even number
j_{\mathrm{even}}>N
with
c_{j_{\mathrm{odd}}}\neq 0,c_{j_{\mathrm{even}}}\neq 0
. Then for any
m,l\in\mathbb{N}
and any distinct
p_{1},...,p_{r}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, the power series in
\varepsilon
:

\sum_{j\geq l}c_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left[\alpha_{0k}+j\alpha_{1k}+j(j-1)\alpha_{2k}+...+\frac{j!}{(j-l)!}\alpha_{lk}\right]p_{k}^{j-l}\varepsilon^{j-l}

is a polynomial if and only if
\alpha_{tk}=0
for all
0\leq t\leq l
and
1\leq k\leq r
.

Proof.

First note that for any sufficiently small
\varepsilon
the power series in question converges. Without loss of generality, assume that
|p_{1}|\geq...\geq|p_{r}|
; in particular,
p_{1}
has the largest absolute value among the
|p_{k}|
’s. We consider two cases.

(a)

|p_{1}|>|p_{2}|
. Assume that the
\alpha_{t1}
’s are not all zero; otherwise we work on a smaller
r
. Thus, there is a largest
t_{1}\in\{0,...,l\}
with
\alpha_{t_{1}1}\neq 0
. Then

\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left[\alpha_{0k}+j\alpha_{1k}+...+\frac{j!}{(j-l)!}\alpha_{lk}\right]p_{k}^{j-l}\sim p_{1}^{j-l}

as
j\to\infty
. By hypothesis, there is a subsequence
\{c_{j_{s}}\}_{s=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathbb{R}(\mathbb{C})\setminus\{0\}
of
\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
. Therefore,

c_{j_{s}}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left[\alpha_{0k}+j_{s}\alpha_{1k}+...+\frac{j_{s}!}{(j_{s}-l)!}\alpha_{lk}\right]p_{k}^{j_{s}-l}\sim p_{1}^{j_{s}-l}

as
s\to\infty
, which shows that the power series has infinitely many non-zero coefficients, whence not a polynomial.

(b)

|p_{1}|=|p_{2}|
. Because the
p_{k}
’s are distinct, we must have
p_{1}+p_{2}=0
and
|p_{2}|>|p_{3}|
. Assume that
t_{1}\in\{0,...,l\}
is the largest number such that
\alpha_{t_{1}1}
or
\alpha_{t_{1}2}
is non-zero. Then, similar as (a) above, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left[\alpha_{0k}+j\alpha_{1k}+...+\frac{j!}{(j-l)!}\alpha_{lk}\right]p_{k}^{j-l}\sim\left(\alpha_{t_{1}1}+(-1)^{j-l}\alpha_{t_{1}2}\right)p_{1}^{j-l}

as
j\to+\infty
, provided that
\alpha_{t_{1}1}+(-1)^{j-l}\alpha_{t_{1}2}\neq 0
. By hypothesis, either i)
\alpha_{t_{1}1}=0
or
\alpha_{t_{2}1}=0
, or both are non-zero, there is an odd or even sequence
\{j_{s}\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha_{t_{1}1}+(-1)^{j_{s}-l}\alpha_{t_{1}2}\neq 0
and
c_{j_{s}}\neq 0
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}
. It follows that

c_{j_{s}}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left[\alpha_{0k}+j_{s}\alpha_{1k}+...+\frac{j_{s}!}{(j_{s}-l)!}\alpha_{lk}\right]p_{k}^{j_{s}-1}\sim p_{1}^{j_{s}-1}

as

, so the power series has infinitely many non-zero coefficients, whence not a polynomial.

In either case, we have shown that
\alpha_{01}=\alpha_{11}=...=\alpha_{l1}=0
must hold if
h
is a polynomial. By repeating this procedure for
r
times we can see that
\alpha_{tk}=0
for all
0\leq t\leq l
and
1\leq k\leq r
.
∎

3.1
Linear Independence of Neurons

Corollary 3.1

(linear independence of neurons)
.

Let
d
be any positive integer. Given a real analytic function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the following two statements about
\sigma
are equivalent.

(a)

\sigma
is a generic activation, namely, it satisfies Assumption
2.1
.

(b)

For any
r>0
and any distinct vectors
w_{1},...,w_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the functions
\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x),...,\sigma(w_{r}\cdot x)
are linearly independent.

Proof.

First suppose that (a) holds. Let
1\leq k<j\leq r
. The set

A_{k,j}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\langle x,w_{k}-w_{j}\rangle=0\}

(4)

is a subspace of dimension
d-1
, whence
\bigcup_{1\leq k<j\leq r}A_{k,j}
has
\lambda_{d}
-measure zero. This, together with linearity, implies that we can find some
e\in\partial B(0,1)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
p_{k}:=\langle w_{k},e\rangle\neq\langle w_{j},e\rangle=:p_{j}
whenever
k\neq j
. For any
|\varepsilon|<(\max_{j}|p_{j}|)^{-1}R
and any
k
we have

\sigma(w_{k}\cdot\varepsilon e)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}\sigma(w_{k}\cdot\varepsilon e)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(c_{j}p_{k}^{j})\varepsilon^{j}.

Now let
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{r}
be constants such that
x\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{r}\alpha_{k}\sigma(w_{k}\cdot x)
is a zero map. It follows that for
|\varepsilon|<(\max_{j}|p_{j}|)^{-1}R
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{r}\alpha_{k}\sigma(w_{k}\cdot\varepsilon e)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\alpha_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(c_{j}p_{k}^{j})\varepsilon^{j}

(5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{r}\alpha_{k}p_{k}^{j}\right)\varepsilon^{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{r}\alpha_{k}p_{k}^{j-1}\right)\varepsilon^{j-1}=0.

Since
\sigma
is a generic activation, the sequence
\{c_{j-1}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
satisfies the hypothesis of Lemma
2
. Since the
p_{k}
’s are also distinct, by Lemma
2
we have
\alpha_{1}=...=\alpha_{r}=0
. Therefore,
\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x),...,\sigma(w_{r}\cdot x)
are linearly independent.

Conversely, assume that (a) does not hold. First suppose there is some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any odd
j>N
we have
c_{j}=0
. Then
\sigma
is the sum of a polynomial and an even function. Let
\tilde{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
\tilde{\sigma}(x)=\sigma(x)-\sigma(-x)
, so
\tilde{\sigma}
is a polynomial of degree at most
N
. Thus, the dimension of
\text{span}\{\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{w}_{1}\cdot x),...,\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{w}_{r^{\prime}}\cdot x)\}
is bounded by
(N+1)^{d}
. This implies that for any
r>2(N+1)^{d}
the functions

\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x),\sigma(-w_{1}\cdot x),...,\sigma(w_{r}\cdot x),\sigma(-w_{r}\cdot x)

can never be linearly independent. Similarly, if there is some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any even
j>N
we have
c_{j}=0
. Then
\sigma
is the sum of a polynomial and an odd function. Let
\tilde{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
\tilde{\sigma}(x)=\sigma(x)+\sigma(-x)
, so
\tilde{\sigma}
is a polynomial. Thus, for sufficiently large
r
the functions

\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x),\sigma(-w_{1}\cdot x),...,\sigma(w_{r}\cdot x),\sigma(-w_{r}\cdot x)

can never be linearly independent.
∎

Remark 3
.

In fact, the proof of Corollary
3.1
shows that if
\sigma
is analytic and not a polynomial,
\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x),...,\sigma(w_{r}\cdot x)
are linearly independent for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
, whenever the distinct
w_{1},...,w_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy
w_{k}+w_{j}\neq 0
for all
1\leq k,j\leq r
. Similarly, Corollary
3.2
hold under similar requirements. In particular, these results hold for
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
,
\sigma(x)=\tanh(x)
or
\sigma(x)=\log(1+e^{-x})
. Interestingly, we also observe that current analysis of loss landscapes of neural network focus on polynomial and non-polynomial activations separately. For example, Venturi
Venturi et al. 2019
has shown that for sufficiently wide one-hidden-layer neural network with polynomial activation, the corresponding loss landscape has no spurious valley, while in
Simsek et al. 2021
the analysis of critical points are valid only for neural networks with certain non-polynomial activations.

Corollary
3.1
proves the (linear) neuron independence of analytic neurons, which is the main object we concern in this paper. For completeness, we also present a version of neuron independence result without requiring the neurons to be analytic. Instead, it considers other important properties of an activation function, which we hope could be of its own interest. More precisely, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 3.1
.

Assume that
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is an
s
-times continuously differentiable function (
s\geq 0
) with the following properties:

(a)

(rapid decreasing)
\frac{|\sigma^{(s)}(y)|}{|\sigma^{(s)}(x)|}\to+\infty
as
|x|,|y|\to+\infty
and
|x|-|y|\to+\infty
.

(b)

(non-asymptotic symmetry) There is some
c>1
such that either

\varliminf_{x\to+\infty}\frac{|\sigma^{(s)}(x)|}{|\sigma^{(s)}(-x)|}\geq c

or

\varliminf_{x\to-\infty}\frac{|\sigma^{(s)}(x)|}{|\sigma^{(s)}(-x)|}\geq c.

Remark 4
.

Notice that the rapid decreasing property of
\sigma^{(s)}
requires that
\sigma^{(s)}(x)\neq 0
for
|x|
sufficiently large. Moreover, this property implies that
\lim_{x\to+\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(x)=0
. To see this, let
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence with
\lim_{n\to\infty}|x_{n}|=+\infty
. By passing to a subsequence of
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, we can assume that
\lim_{n\to\infty}(|x_{n+1}|-|x_{n}|)=+\infty
as well. Given
A>1
, any sufficiently large
N\in\mathbb{N}
gives

\frac{|\sigma^{(s)}(x_{N})|}{|\sigma^{(s)}(x_{N+1})|}\geq A.

Thus,

|\sigma^{(s)}(x_{N})|\geq A|\sigma^{(s)}(x_{N+1})|\geq...\geq A^{n}|\sigma^{(s)}(x_{N+n})|.

Since
A>1
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}A^{n}=+\infty
, which shows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(x_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(x_{N+n})=0
.

We shall give some examples to illustrate the two properties in Assumption
3.1
. For the rapid decreasing property we have the following examples, which takes the commonly-seen activations into consideration.

(a)

\sigma(x)=\exp(-x^{2})
with
s=0
. Note that for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma(y)/\sigma(x)=\exp(x^{2}-y^{2})
, which obviously tends to infinity as
|x|-|y|
tends to infinity.

(b)

\sigma(x)=\exp(-|x|)
with
s=0
. For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma(y)/\sigma(x)=\exp(|x|-|y|)
, which obviously tends to infinity as
|x|-|y|
tends to infinity.

(c)

\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
with
s=1
. Recall that
\sigma^{\prime}(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{(1+e^{-x})^{2}}
. Thus, In other words,
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{\sigma^{\prime}(x)}{e^{-|x|}}=1
. Then by (b) we see that for any
|x|,|y|
sufficiently large,
\sigma^{\prime}(y)/\sigma^{\prime}(x)\approx\exp(|x|-|y|)
, which also tends to infinity as
|x|-|y|
tends to infinity.

(d)

\sigma(x)=\log(1+e^{x})
with
s=2
. This is because
\sigma^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
. Similarly,
\sigma(x)=\tanh(x)
also decreases rapidly.

It is easy to see that in any case above,
\sigma^{(s)}
does not satisfy the non-asymptotic symmetry property, because
\sigma^{(s)}
is an even function. However, in any case a horizontal shift of
\sigma^{(s)}
by
b\in\mathbb{R}
,
x\mapsto\sigma^{(s)}(x-b)
, which is just the
s
-th derivative of
\sigma(\cdot-b)
, satisfies both the rapid decreasing property and the non-asymptotic symmetry property.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
d
be a positive integer. Let
\sigma
satisfy Assumption
3.1
. For any
r>0
and any distinct vectors
w_{1},...,w_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, the functions
\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x),...,\sigma(w_{r}\cdot x)
are linearly independent.

Proof.

We prove in a similar way as that of Corollary
3.1
. As we have shown above, there is some
e\in\partial B(0,1)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
p_{k}:=\langle w_{k},e\rangle\neq\langle w_{j},e\rangle=:p_{j}\neq 0
whenever
k,j\in\{1,...,r\}
are different. By rearranging the indices if necessary, we can assume that
|p_{1}|\geq|p_{2}|\geq...\geq|p_{r}|>0
.

Let
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{r}
be constants such that
x\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{r}\alpha_{k}\sigma(w_{k}\cdot x)
is a zero map. In particular, this means

g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},g(\varepsilon)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\alpha_{k}\sigma((w_{k}\cdot e)\varepsilon)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\alpha_{k}\sigma(p_{k}\varepsilon)

is a zero map. Then the
s
-th derivative (when
s=0
, it is just
g
itself) of
g
in
\varepsilon
is given by

g^{(s)}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\alpha_{k}p_{k}^{s}\sigma^{(s)}(p_{k}\varepsilon)=0.

We start by showing that
\alpha_{1}=0
. When
r=1
this clearly holds. When
r\geq 2
, we consider two cases.

i)

|p_{1}|>|p_{k}|
for all
2\leq k\leq r
. Then
p_{1}^{s}\neq 0
. For
\sigma(p_{1}\varepsilon)\neq 0
(which holds when
|\varepsilon|
is sufficiently large), we can rewrite
g^{(s)}(\varepsilon)
as

\alpha_{1}=-\sum_{k=2}^{r}\alpha_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{p_{1}}\right)^{s}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{k}\varepsilon)}{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)}.

For any
2\leq k\leq r
,
|p_{1}|>|p_{k}|
, so
|p_{1}\varepsilon|,|p_{k}\varepsilon|
and
|p_{1}\varepsilon|-|p_{k}\varepsilon|
all tend to infinity as
\varepsilon\to\pm\infty
. Therefore, using the rapid decreasing property of
\sigma^{(s)}
we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to\pm\infty}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{k}\varepsilon)}{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)}.

In particular, it follows that as
\varepsilon\to+\infty
,

\alpha_{1}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to+\infty}\alpha_{1}=-\sum_{k=2}^{r}\alpha_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{p_{1}}\right)^{s}\lim_{\varepsilon\to+\infty}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{k}\varepsilon)}{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)}=0.

ii)

|p_{1}|=|p_{2}|
. Then we must have
p_{1}=-p_{2}\neq 0
and
|p_{1}|=|p_{2}|>|p_{k}|
for all
k\neq 1,2
. Again, for
\sigma(p_{1}\varepsilon)\neq 0
, we can rewrite
g^{(s)}(\varepsilon)
as

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{2}\left(\frac{p_{2}}{p_{1}}\right)^{s}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{2}\varepsilon)}{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)}-\sum_{k>2}\alpha_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{p_{1}}\right)^{s}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{k}\varepsilon)}{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{s+1}\alpha_{2}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(-p_{1}\varepsilon)}{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)}-\sum_{k>2}\alpha_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{p_{1}}\right)^{s}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{k}\varepsilon)}{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)},

where we use
p_{1}=-p_{2}
. Same as in i), letting
\varepsilon\to\pm\infty
, we still have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to\pm\infty}\sum_{k>2}\alpha_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{p_{1}}\right)^{s}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{k}\varepsilon)}{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)}=0.

For the other term, we use the non-asymptotic symmetry property of
\sigma^{(s)}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\varliminf_{x\to+\infty}\frac{|\sigma^{(s)}(-p_{1}\varepsilon)|}{|\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)|}\geq c
for some
c>1
. Then
\varlimsup_{x\to-\infty}\frac{|\sigma^{(s)}(-p_{1}\varepsilon)|}{|\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)|}\leq c^{-1}
. These two inequalities yield lower and upper bounds for
\alpha_{1}
:

\displaystyle|\alpha_{1}|

\displaystyle=\left|\varliminf_{x\to+\infty}(-1)^{s+1}\alpha_{2}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(-p_{1}\varepsilon)}{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)}-\varliminf_{x\to+\infty}\sum_{k>2}\alpha_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{p_{1}}\right)^{s}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{k}\varepsilon)}{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)}\right|

\displaystyle=|\alpha_{2}|\varliminf_{x\to+\infty}\frac{|\sigma^{(s)}(-p_{1}\varepsilon)|}{|\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)|}

\displaystyle\geq c|\alpha_{2}|

and similarly,

|\alpha_{1}|=\left|\varlimsup_{x\to-\infty}(-1)^{s+1}\alpha_{2}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(-p_{1}\varepsilon)}{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)}-\varlimsup_{x\to-\infty}\sum_{k>2}\alpha_{k}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{p_{1}}\right)^{s}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{k}\varepsilon)}{\sigma^{(s)}(p_{1}\varepsilon)}\right|\leq c^{-1}|\alpha_{2}|.\\

Unless
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=0
, we would get a contradiction.

In either case, we show that
\alpha_{1}=0
. By repeating this argument (at most)
r
times, we can show that
\alpha_{1}=...=\alpha_{r}=0
and thus the linear independence of
\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x),...,\sigma(w_{r}\cdot x)
follows.
∎

Note that the non-asymptotic symmetry property of
\sigma^{(s)}
is only used to deal with
p_{k}=-p_{j}
for some
k,j\in\{1,...,r\}
in the proof, which is unavoidable only when
w_{k}+w_{j}=0
for some
w_{k},w_{j}\neq 0
. Thus, if
w_{1},...,w_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
are distinct vectors satisfying
w_{k}+w_{j}\neq 0
for all
1\leq k,j\leq r
, any
\sigma\in C^{s}
which has the rapid decreasing property would give the linear independence result. By our examples above, this holds for lots of commonly seen neurons, including
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
,
\sigma(x)=\tanh(x)
,
\sigma(x)=\exp(-x^{2})
,
\sigma(x)=\exp(-|x|)
and
\sigma(x)=\log(1+e^{x})
.

Remark 5

(proof techniques)
.

In both cases (analytic and non-analytic), we use two important properties of neurons to show their linear independence. The first one is that for
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the mapping
\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni x\mapsto w\cdot x
reduces high-dimensional problems to 1-dimensional ones, as it induces a mapping
\mathbb{R}\ni\varepsilon\mapsto w\cdot(\varepsilon x)
. Therefore, many 1-dimensional results of neurons can be applied to higher-dimensional cases. Another technique is to use the fact that distinct weights of neurons, no matter how small the difference is, could distinguish them with significant different behaviors. In Proposition
3.1
, we observe this by taking higher derivative, while in Proposition
3.1
, we observe this by comparing their asymptotic behaviors at
\pm\infty
. The treatment of
w_{k}+w_{j}=0
for some
k,j
is more technical, and we make different assumptions in dealing with it: indeed, the function

\sigma(x)=e^{-x^{2}}+\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}

satisfies Assumption
2.1
and is rapidly decreasing, but
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\sigma(x)/\sigma(-x)=-1
.

We then study the rank of some special matrices related to
\sigma
. Recall the loss function
R(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sigma(w_{k}\cdot x_{i})-f(x_{i})|^{2}
. For each
1\leq i\leq n
, the second-order partial derivative of
|\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sigma(w_{k}\cdot x_{i})-f(x_{i})|^{2}
is given by the tensor product
v_{i,\sigma}^{\mathrm{T}}v_{i,\sigma}
where

\displaystyle v_{i,\sigma}:=(\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x_{i}),...,\sigma(w_{m}\cdot x_{i}),\sigma^{\prime}(w_{1}\cdot x_{i})x_{i}^{\mathrm{T}},...,\sigma^{\prime}(w_{m}\cdot x_{i})x_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}).

Since
R
is a sum of the
|\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sigma(w_{k}\cdot x_{i})-f(x_{i})|^{2}
’s, it motivates us to study the matrices whose rows are these vectors
v_{i,\sigma}
or parts of them.

Corollary 3.2

(separating inputs)
.

Let
\sigma
be a generic activation. For any
r\in\mathbb{N}
and any distinct
w_{1},...,w_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, there are
x_{1},...,x_{(d+1)r}
such that the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x_{1})&...&\sigma(w_{r}\cdot x_{1})&\sigma^{\prime}(w_{1}\cdot x_{1})x_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}&...&\sigma^{\prime}(w_{r}\cdot x_{1})x_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x_{(d+1)r})&...&\sigma(w_{r}\cdot x_{(d+1)r})&\sigma^{\prime}(w_{1}\cdot x_{(d+1)r})x_{(d+1)r}^{\mathrm{T}}&...&\sigma^{\prime}(w_{r}\cdot x_{(d+1)r})x_{(d+1)r}^{\mathrm{T}}\end{pmatrix}

has full rank.

Proof.

For simplicity, denote
v_{i,\sigma}
as the vector
(\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x_{i}),...,\sigma(w_{r}\cdot x_{i}),\sigma^{\prime}(w_{1}\cdot x_{i})x_{i}^{\mathrm{T}},...,\sigma^{\prime}(w_{r}\cdot x_{i})x_{i}^{\mathrm{T}})
, so
v_{i,\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{rd}
is the
i
-th row of the matrix in question. We shall prove the result by inductively showing that there are
x_{1},...,x_{(d+1)r}
such that for every
1\leq i\leq(d+1)r
, the matrix
(v_{1,\sigma}^{\mathrm{T}},...,v_{i,\sigma}^{\mathrm{T}})^{\mathrm{T}}
has rank
i
.

Since
\sigma(0)\neq 0
, we can select a sufficiently small
x_{1}
not orthogonal to
w_{1}
so that
\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x_{1})\neq 0
. This proves the desired result for
i=1
. Suppose the result holds for some
i-1
, where
2\leq i\leq(d+1)r
. Fix
x_{1},...,x_{i-1}
. To find
x_{i}
we do the following. First, choose
e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the three properties below:

(a)

p_{j}:=\langle w_{j},e\rangle\neq\langle w_{i},e\rangle=:p_{i}
for all distinct
i,j\in\{1,...,r\}
.

(b)

p_{1},...,p_{r}\neq 0
.

(c)

For any non-zero vector
b:=((b_{1}^{0},...,b_{r}^{0}),(b_{1}^{1},...,b_{r}^{1}))\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{rd}
in the orthogonal complement of
\{v_{j,\sigma}:1\leq j<i\}
, we have that
b_{1}^{0},...,b_{r}^{0},\langle b_{1}^{1},e\rangle,...,\langle b_{1}^{r},e\rangle
are not all zero.

Note that this holds for almost all
e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We claim that
\varepsilon\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{r}b_{k}^{0}\sigma(\varepsilon p_{j})+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\langle b_{j}^{1},e\rangle\sigma^{\prime}(\varepsilon p_{j})\varepsilon
is not constant zero. Thus, for almost all
\varepsilon
, by setting
x_{i}:=\varepsilon e
we would have
v_{i,\sigma}\notin\text{span}\{v_{j,\sigma}:1\leq j<i\}
, and thus the matrix
(v_{1,\sigma}^{\mathrm{T}},...,v_{i,\sigma}^{\mathrm{T}})^{\mathrm{T}}
has rank
i
. So suppose that this function is constant zero; equivalently,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{r}b_{k}^{0}\sigma(\varepsilon p_{k})+\sum_{k=1}^{r}\langle b_{k}^{1},e\rangle\sigma^{\prime}(\varepsilon p_{k})\varepsilon

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\,c_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{r}b_{k}^{0}+\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{r}[\alpha_{1k}+j\alpha_{2k}]p_{k}^{j-1}\varepsilon^{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,c_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{r}b_{k}^{0}+\varepsilon\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{r}[\alpha_{1k}+j\alpha_{2k}]p_{k}^{j-1}\varepsilon^{j-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,0,

where for each
1\leq k\leq m
,

\alpha_{1k}=b_{k}^{0}p_{k},\quad\alpha_{2k}=\langle b_{k}^{1},e\rangle.

Similar as in the proof of Corollary
3.1
, we may apply Lemma
2
to see that
\sum_{k=1}^{r}\beta_{k}=0
and
\alpha_{1k}=\alpha_{2k}=0
for all
1\leq k\leq m
. But this implies that
b_{k}^{0}=\langle b_{k}^{1},e\rangle=0
for all
k
, contradicting our construction of
e
. Thus, this function is not constant zero, completing the induction step.
∎

Inspired by the lemma above, we define separating inputs below. As we will see in Section

, separating inputs allows us to determine the rank of Hessian of

on

, and thus help us study the geometry of

(e.g., separation of branches, see also Definition

Definition 3.1

(separating inputs)
.

Let

. Given

(6)

has full rank. For any

with non-zero

Note that Corollary
3.2
shows the existence of separating inputs for

. Since

is analytic, this implies that for almost every

3.2
Theory of Real Analytic Functions

To prove such corollaries in this subsection, we introduce some properties of real analytic functions. such tools will also be used in the analysis of the geometry of the loss landscape of
R
near its global-min
R^{-1}\{0\}
.

Lemma 6

(analytic implicit function theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
be smooth manifolds of dimension
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, respectively, and each one has a coordinate representation given by an analytic diffeomorphism. Suppose that
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{M}_{1}\to\mathcal{M}_{2}
is a smooth map with constant rank
s
(
\text{rank}\,D\mathcal{F}(p)=s
for all
p
). For each
p\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
and any coordinate ball
U
around
p
, there exist coordinate maps
\varphi:U\to\mathbb{R}^{s_{1}}
,
\psi:\mathcal{F}(\varphi(U))\to\mathbb{R}^{s_{2}}
, such that

\psi\circ\mathcal{F}\circ\varphi^{-1}(\zeta_{1},...,\zeta_{s_{1}})=(\zeta_{1},...,\zeta_{s},0,...,0)

(7)

for
(\zeta_{1},...,\zeta_{s_{1}})\in\varphi(U)
.

Proof.

By
Krantz and Parks 2002
, the inverse of a (real) analytic diffeomorphism is analytic. Following the proof of the Rank theorem in
Lee 2013
, we can see that all the functions constructed can be made analytic, whence the desired result holds.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
be a connected open set and let
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a (real) analytic function. The following results hold.

(a)

Either
f=0
on
\Omega
or
f^{-1}\{0\}
has
\lambda_{(d+1)m}
-measure zero.

(b)

Suppose that
f
is not a zero map. For any
z_{0}\in f^{-1}\{0\}
and any compact
K\subseteq\Omega
,
K\cap f^{-1}\{0\}
is contained in a finite union of (analytic) smooth manifolds of dimension
(d+1)m-1
, each one having a coordinate representation by an analytic diffeomorphism.

(c)

Let
f^{-1}\{0\}
be locally contained in the union of connected embedded smooth manifolds
\mathcal{M}_{1},...,\mathcal{M}_{N}
of the same dimension, with
\mathcal{M}_{1}\subseteq f^{-1}\{0\}
. Then there is an open
U\subseteq\Omega
such that
U\cap f^{-1}\{0\}=U\cap\mathcal{M}_{1}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

(a)

We will show that given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any set
\Omega
open in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the zero set of any non-constant analytic function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
has
\lambda_{n}
-measure zero. Indeed, when
n=1
the zeros of
f
are (possibly empty isolated points), which means
f^{-1}\{0\}
has measure zero. Suppose that the result holds for
n-1
. Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be the projection of
\Omega
onto
\text{span}\{e_{1}\}
. For any
z_{1}\in E
,
f(z_{1},\cdot)
is an analytic function in
n-1
variables, whence by our assumption its zero set has
\lambda_{n-1}
-measure zero. It follows that

\lambda_{n}(f^{-1}\{0\})=\int_{\Omega}\chi_{f^{-1}\{0\}}=\int_{E}\lambda_{n-1}(f(z_{1},\cdot)^{-1}\{0\})dz_{1}=0,

where
\chi
is the characteristic function. This completes our induction and shows that
f^{-1}\{0\}
has measure zero.

(b)

It suffices to show that for any
y\in f^{-1}\{0\}
,
y
has a bounded neighborhood
U
such that
U\cap f^{-1}\{0\}
is contained in a finite union of (analytic) submanifolds of codimension
1
. Since
f
is not a zero map on
\Omega
, there is some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
D^{t}f(y)=0
for all multi-index
t
with
|t|\leq N
and there are some
j\in\{1,...,(d+1)m\}
, multi-index
\alpha
with
|\alpha|=N
and
\partial_{z_{j}}D^{\alpha}f(y)\neq 0
. Thus,

y\in\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}:D^{\alpha}f(z)=0\text{ and }\partial_{z_{j}}D^{\alpha}f(z)\neq 0\},

which shows that
y
is in a smooth manifold of dimension
(d+1)m-1
, and by Lemma
6
, it has a coordinate representation given by an analytic diffeomorphism. Let
U
be a bounded neighborhood of
y
on which
\partial_{z_{j}}D^{\alpha}f\neq 0
. It follows that
U\cap f^{-1}\{0\}
is contained in

\bigcup_{t,0\leq|t|\leq N}\bigcup_{i=1}^{(d+1)m}\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}:D^{t}f(z)=0\text{ and }\partial_{z_{i}}D^{t}f(z)\neq 0\}.

(8)

Just note that the right side is a finite union of (possibly empty) embedded, analytic smooth manifolds, as desired.

(c)

By shrinking the domain of
f
,
\Omega
if necessary, we may for simplicity assume that
f^{-1}\{0\}\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{M}_{i}
. First suppose that
N=2
. Since

\mathcal{M}_{1}=(\mathcal{M}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{2})\cup(\mathcal{M}_{1}\cap\mathcal{M}_{2}),

one of the sets on the right side of the equation must contain an open submanifold
E
of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
. Consider first the case in which
E\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{2}
and
x\in E
. If for any
\delta>0
the
(d+1)m
-dimensional ball
B(x,\delta)
intersects
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, then
x
is in the closure of
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, which implies that
x\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
, as
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
, a contradiction. Now suppose that
x\in E\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}\cap\mathcal{M}_{2}
. Since
\dim\mathcal{M}_{1}=\dim\mathcal{M}_{2}
,
E
is also an open submanifold of
\mathcal{M}_{2}
; furthermore, it is the intersection of an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
with
\mathcal{M}_{1}
(
\mathcal{M}_{2}
). In either case, there is some open
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
with
U\cap f^{-1}\{0\}=U\cap\mathcal{M}_{1}
. When
N\geq 2
, we argue by induction to obtain the same desired result.

∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta(\cdot-x_{i})
as in Assumption
2.2
. If
\sigma
is analytic, then
R
is analytic, in which case all the results in Lemma

Proof.

By our hypothesis,
R(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|g(\theta,x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})|^{2}
for some

∎

Corollary 3.4

(common zeros of parametrized analytic functions)
.

Let
f:E\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be analytic, where
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Suppose that for any
z\in E
,
f(z,\cdot)
is not constant-zero. Given
n>s
, for any prescribed
x_{1},...,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
\varepsilon>0
, we can perturb each of them with no more than
\varepsilon
-distance to obtain

, so that

\mathcal{A}_{E}:=\{z\in E:f(z,x_{1}^{\prime})=...=f(z,x_{n}^{\prime})=0\}

\mathcal{A}_{E}=\emptyset
for almost all
(x_{1}^{\prime},...,x_{n}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
.

Proof.

By hypothesis,
f
is not constant-zero, whence there is some
x_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|x_{1}^{\prime}-x_{1}|<\varepsilon
such that
f(\cdot,x_{1}^{\prime})|_{E}
is not constant-zero. By Lemma
7
(b), for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
and

with

not constant-zero,
f(\cdot,x)^{-1}\{0\}\cap(\overline{B(0,N)}\cap E)
is contained in a finite union of codimension-1 submanifolds of
\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Thus,
f(\cdot,x_{1}^{\prime})^{-1}\{0\}
is contained in a union of countably many submanifolds of
\mathbb{R}^{s}
, which we denote by
\{\mathcal{M}_{1n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

For the choice of
x_{2}^{\prime}
we consider two cases. Suppose first that there are (possibly finite)
\mathcal{M}_{1n_{1}},...,\mathcal{M}_{1n_{k}},...
such that

restricted to each of them is a zero map. Select points
\{z_{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{1n_{k}}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
. For each
k
, there is an open, dense

such that
\lambda_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus O_{k}^{\prime})=0

. Therefore

for all

, whenever

. Let
x_{2}^{\prime}\in\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}O_{k}^{\prime}
with
|x_{2}^{\prime}-x_{2}|<\varepsilon
. Otherwise,
f(z,x_{2}^{\prime})
is not constant-zero on

; in this case simply let

. By Lemma

, the set on which

Repeat this procedure to perturb

\mathcal{A}_{E,s^{\prime}}:=\{z\in E:f(z,x_{1}^{\prime})=...=f(z,x_{s^{\prime}}^{\prime})=0\}

\mathcal{A}_{E,s}
is a countable (possibly finite) set. For simplicity, denote
\mathcal{A}_{E,s}:=\{z_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}

O_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\lambda_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus O_{k})=0
and

for all

Suppose that Assumptions

3.4
Geometry of
Q^{*}

In this part, we investigate the geometry of
Q^{*}
under the assumption that
\sigma
is a generic activation (Assumption
2.1
). In this case, Refs.
Simsek et al. 2021
;
Fukumizu and Amari 2000
have shown that the global minima of the loss function is a union of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
whose closure are affine subspaces which we call “branches”. For completeness of our study for local recovery, we present this result and prove it with our notations (see Proposition
3.2
). Based on this,
Simsek et al. 2021
shows that
Q^{*}
is connected. We further investigate how different branches intersect with each other. First let’s recall partition and refinement.

Definition 3.2

(partition and refinement)
.

A partition of
\{1,...,m\}
is denoted by
P:=(q_{0},...,q_{r})
for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
, where
0=q_{0}<q_{1}<...<q_{r}=m
. Given another partition
P^{\prime}=(q_{0}^{\prime},...,q_{r^{\prime}}^{\prime})
, we say
P^{\prime}
is a refinement of
P
if
\{q_{0},...,q_{r}\}\subseteq\{q_{0}^{\prime},...,q_{r^{\prime}}^{\prime}\}
.

We then partition
Q^{*}
into symmetric subsets based on the number of distinct
w_{k}
’s.

Definition 3.3

(fragmentation/stratification of
Q^{*}
)
.

Given
m_{0}\leq r\leq m
, a partition
P=(q_{0},...,q_{r})
and a permutation
\pi
of
\{1,...,m\}
. We define the following subsets of
Q^{*}
.

(a)

Q^{r}:=\{\theta^{*}\in Q^{*}:\card\{w_{k}^{*}:1\leq k\leq m\}=r\}
, namely, it consists of points
(a_{k}^{*},w_{k}^{*})_{k=1}^{m}\in Q^{*}
which has precisely
r
distinct
w_{k}^{*}
’s.

(b)

Q_{P}^{r}
consists of
\theta^{*}=(a_{k}^{*},w_{k}^{*})_{k=1}^{m}\in Q^{*}
such that
w_{k}^{*}=w_{q_{t}}^{*}
for all
q_{t-1}<k\leq q_{t}
and
1\leq t\leq r
, and
w_{q_{t}}^{*}=\bar{w}_{t}
for all
1\leq t\leq m_{0}
. For example, if
m_{0}=2
,
r=3
,
m=6
and
P=(0,2,4,6)
then
\theta^{*}\in Q_{P}^{3}
if and only if it has the form

\theta^{*}=(a_{1}^{*},\bar{w}_{1},a_{2}^{*},\bar{w}_{1},a_{3}^{*},\bar{w}_{2},a_{4}^{*},\bar{w}_{2},a_{5}^{*},u,a_{6}^{*},u)

for some
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(c)

Q_{P,\pi}^{r}:=\{(a_{\pi(k)}^{*},w_{\pi(k)}^{*})_{k=1}^{m}:(a_{k}^{*},w_{k}^{*})_{k=1}^{m}\in Q_{P}^{r}\}
. We call each
Q_{P,\pi}^{r}
a brunch of
Q^{*}
.

The notations
Q_{P}^{r}
and
Q_{P,\pi}^{r}
make sense only when
P
partitions
\{1,...,m\}
into
r
subsets. Thus, when we write these notations we implicitly assume that
P
satisfies this requirement. Then, clearly,
Q_{P,\pi}^{r}
and
Q_{P}^{r}
are both subsets of
Q^{r}
. Also, notice that for any
(a_{k}^{*},w_{k}^{*})_{k=1}^{m}\in Q_{P}^{m_{0}}
(
Q_{P,\pi}^{m_{0}}
),
w_{k}^{*}\in\{\bar{w}_{j}:1\leq j\leq m\}
for each
k
, while for any
(a_{k}^{*},w_{k}^{*})_{k=1}^{m}\in Q_{P}^{m}
(
Q_{P,\pi}^{m}
),
w_{k}^{*}\neq w_{j}^{*}
whenever
k\neq j
.

An immediate observation is that for any permutation
\pi
,
Q_{P,\pi}^{r}
is the image of
Q_{P}^{r}
under a coordinate transformation, so they have the same geometric properties. To see this, let
\{e_{i}:1\leq i\leq(d+1)m\}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
. Clearly,
\pi
induces a permutation
\tau
on
\{1,...,(d+1)m\}
that maps
i
to
\tau(i)=(d+1)(\pi(i)-1)+k
if
i=k\bmod{(d+1)}
,
1\leq k\leq d+1
. Let
\rho
be the linear map on
\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
such that
\rho(e_{i})=e_{\tau(i)}
for all
1\leq i\leq(d+1)m
. Then
Q_{P,\pi}^{r}=\rho(Q_{P}^{r})
.

Since
Q_{P,\pi}^{r}
is isometric to
Q_{P}^{r}
,
Q^{*}
has a strong symmetry property. In particular, to study the structure of
Q_{P,\pi}^{r}
we need only study that of
Q_{P}^{r}
. Similarly, given
r^{\prime}
, partition
P^{\prime}
and permutation
\pi^{\prime}
, to investigate

Lemma 13

(

Let
\sigma

Recall the definition of Morse–Bott functions, which are summarized from
Feehan 2019
. They play an essential role in our study of the local convergence of gradient flow near each branch of
Q^{*}
.

Definition 4.1

(Morse–Bott function, rephrased from
Feehan 2019
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function and let
M\subseteq(\nabla f)^{-1}\{0\}
be a non-empty submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
. Let
NM\to M
be the normal bundle of
M
. Given
p\in M
, let
\operatorname{Hess}_{M}f(p):N_{x}M\times N_{x}M\to\mathbb{R}
be the restriction of
\operatorname{Hess}f(p)
to
N_{x}M
.
M
is called a non-degenerate critical manifold of
f
if
\operatorname{Hess}_{M}f(p)
is non-degenerate for each
p\in M
, and such
f
is called Morse–Bott (at
M
) if there is a neighborhood of
M
restricted to which
(\nabla f)^{-1}\{0\}=M
.

For our notation, we view
\mathrm{Hess}_{M}f(x)
as a map in Definition
4.1
. Since each bilinear map corresponds to a matrix, we may also view
\mathrm{Hess}_{M}f(x)
as a matrix, as we usually do for
\operatorname{Hess}f(x)
. In this case,
\mathrm{Hess}_{M}f(x)
is simply the restriction of
\operatorname{Hess}f(x)
to a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
.

Morse–Bott functions are not rare, the most trivial examples being
f(x,y)=x^{n}
,
n=2,3,4,...
. Another important example is analytic function whose set of critical points are submanifold(s) and whose Hessian are non-degenerate at the normal spaces of it. Below we give more examples of them with the focus on neural network type functions.

(a)

The linear regression model with
L^{2}
loss

R(\theta)=R(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g(w,x_{i})-y_{i}\right|^{2},\quad g(w,x_{i})=w\cdot x

is clearly a Morse–Bott function. This is because
\operatorname{Hess}R
is a constant matrix-valued function, which implies that
(\nabla R)^{-1}\{0\}
is a union of affine spaces which are all orthogonal to
\ker(\operatorname{Hess}R)
.

(b)

Consider
\sigma(x)=e^{x}
and
m=n=1
, namely,

R(\theta)=(g(\theta,x)-y)^{2},\quad g(\theta,x)=ae^{w\cdot x},

where

and

are chosen and fixed. Since

\frac{\partial{R}}{\partial{a}}=2\left(ae^{w\cdot x}-y\right)e^{w\cdot x},\quad\frac{\partial{R}}{\partial{w}}=2a(ae^{w\cdot x}-y)e^{w\cdot x}x

we can see that
\nabla R(\theta)=0
if and only if
\theta\in R^{-1}\{0\}
, if and only if

\operatorname{Hess}R(\theta)=2\begin{pmatrix}e^{2w\cdot x}&ae^{2w\cdot x}x_{1}&...&ae^{2w\cdot x}x_{d}\\
ae^{2w\cdot x}x_{1}&a^{2}e^{2w\cdot x}x_{1}^{2}&...&a^{2}e^{2w\cdot x}x_{1}x_{d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
ae^{2w\cdot x}x_{d}&a^{2}e^{2w\cdot x}x_{1}x_{d}&...&a^{2}e^{2w\cdot x}x_{d}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

To simplify, if
v

Obviously, (a) follows from (b), but to demonstrate different techniques we use two ways to prove them.

(a)

5
Dynamics of Gradient Flow Near
Q^{*}

Based on the geometry of
Q^{*}
and functional properties of
R
which we characterized above, we are now able to give the complete characterization of gradient flows near
Q^{*}
. In this section, we apply Lojasiewicz type inequalities to show the convergence of gradient flow near the critical points of a real analytic function
f
. Then we discuss whether each point in
(\nabla f)^{-1}\{0\}
is the limit of some gradient flow of
f
. By Assumption
2.2
and/or our discussion in Section
4
, all of them hold for
R
. Based on these results and Lemma
14
, we further characterize the convergence rate, limiting direction, and generalization stability (whether
g(\lim_{t\to+\infty}\gamma(t),\cdot)
is stable under perturbation of
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\gamma(t)
, see also Definition
5.1
) of gradient flow near
Q^{*}
. Thus we prove Theorem
2.2
, with a detailed understanding of the behavior of training dynamics near
R^{-1}\{0\}
.

5.1
Limiting Set of Gradient Flow

The following theorems, which can be seen as different types of Lojasiewicz inequality are summarized from
Feehan 2019
and
Absil 2005
. They show that for an analytic function, any gradient flow near a local minimum converges.

Theorem 5.1

(Theorem 1 of
Feehan 2019
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}\to\mathbb{R}
be a real analytic function. For any critical point
p
of
f
, there is a neighborhood
U
of
p
and constants
C>0
,
\mu\in[1/2,1)
such that

|\nabla f(q)|\geq C|f(q)-f(p)|^{\mu}

(10)

for any
q\in U
.

Theorem 5.2

(rephrased from Theorem 2.2 in
Absil 2005
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}\to\mathbb{R}
be a real analytic function. Suppose that
p
is a local minimum of
f
. Then
p
has a neighborhood
U
such that any non-constant gradient flow with initial value
x_{0}\in U
converges to
f^{-1}\{f(p)\}
. Moreover, any such gradient flow converges with some rate
0<\beta<1
depending only on
p
. Namely, the curve length

l(\gamma[t,+\infty))=O(|f(\gamma(t))-f(p)|^{\beta})

(11)

as
t\to+\infty
, where
l(\gamma[t,+\infty))
is the curve length of
\gamma[t,+\infty)
.

Proof.

By Theorem 2.2 in
Absil 2005
, there is some neighborhood
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
around
p
such that for any gradient flow
\gamma:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
with any
\gamma(0)=x_{0}\in U
,
\gamma
satisfies: there are some
c,\beta>0
such that for any
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<+\infty
,

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left|\dot{\gamma}(t)\right|dt\leq c|f(\gamma(t_{1}))-f(p)|^{\beta}.

Taking
t_{2}\to+\infty
, the monotonic convergence theorem gives
l(\gamma[t,+\infty))\leq c|f(\gamma(t_{1})-f(p)|^{\beta}
, as desired. This shows the length of
\gamma
is bounded, whence
\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma(t)
exists. In particular, there is some neighborhood
V\subseteq U
of
p
such that gradient flows with initial value in
V
lies in
U
eventually. Since
p
is a local minimum of
f
, the neighborhood
U
can be chosen so that
f(q)\geq f(p)
, and (by Theorem
5.1
)
|\nabla f(q)|\geq C|f(q)-f(p)|^{\mu}
. Thus,
\nabla f(q)=0
is possible only for
q\in f^{-1}\{f(p)\}
, which implies that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma(t)\in f^{-1}\{f(p)\}
whenever
\gamma
has initial value
\gamma(0)\in V
.
∎

Thus, if
f
is non-negative and
f^{-1}\{0\}
is non-empty, then there is some open
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
such that any gradient flow with initial value in
U
converges to
f^{-1}\{0\}
. We then show the converse of the previous result, i.e., if
f
is analytic near
N
, then any point in
N
is the limit of a gradient flow, in other words the limiting set of gradient flow contains
N
.

Proposition 5.1

(converse of Theorem
5.2
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}\to[0,+\infty)
be continuously differentiable. Suppose that each point
x^{*}\in f^{-1}\{0\}
has a neighborhood
U
satisfying

(a)

For any
p\in U
, the gradient flow starting at
p
,
\gamma_{p}
, converges to a point in
f^{-1}\{0\}
,

(b)

There are some
C,\alpha>0
such that for any
p\in U
, the curve length of
\gamma_{p}
is bounded above by
C\text{dist}\left({p},{f^{-1}\{0\}}\right)^{\alpha}
.

Then for any
x^{*}\in f^{-1}\{0\}
, there is a non-constant gradient flow converging to
x^{*}
as
t\to+\infty
. In particular, we result holds when
f
is analytic.

Proof.

By hypothesis, there is a sequence
\{x_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
and a sequence of non-constant gradient flow
\{\gamma_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\gamma_{j}(t)=x_{j}^{*}\in f^{-1}\{0\}
and
\lim_{j\to\infty}x_{j}^{*}=x^{*}\in f^{-1}\{0\}
(this can be proved in the same way as we do in the remark above). Choose a compact neighborhood
V\subseteq U
of
x^{*}
(so
\bar{V}\subseteq U
). For each
j
, there is a largest
t_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
p_{j}:=\gamma_{j}(t_{j})\in\partial V\cap\gamma_{j}
. Since
\partial V
is compact, the sequence
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
has an accumulation point
p
in
V
. Moreover, hypothesis (b) implies that
p\notin f^{-1}\{0\}
. Since
p\in U
, the gradient flow
\gamma_{p}:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
converges to a point in
f^{-1}\{0\}
and its curve length is bounded by
C\text{dist}\left({p},{f^{-1}\{0\}}\right)^{\alpha}
.

Let
l_{j}
be the curve length of
\gamma_{j}[t_{j},+\infty)
and
l
be the curve length of
\gamma_{p}
. For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, define
u_{j}:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
by

\displaystyle u_{j}(0)

\displaystyle=p_{j};

\displaystyle\dot{u}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\nabla f(u_{j}(t))}{|\nabla f(u_{j}(t))|},\quad 0<t<l_{j};

\displaystyle u_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=u_{j}(l_{j}),\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,t\geq l_{j}.

Similarly, define
u:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
by

\displaystyle u(0)

\displaystyle=p;

\displaystyle\dot{u}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\nabla f(u(t))}{|\nabla f(u(t))|},\quad 0<t<l;

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=u(l),\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,\,t\geq l.

Note that the
u_{j}
’s and
u
are exactly the trajectories of their corresponding gradient flows.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Choose any
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
|p-p_{k}|<\varepsilon
. There is some
T>0
such that for any
t>T
, we have
\text{dist}\left({u_{j}(t)},{f^{-1}\{0\}}\right)<\varepsilon^{1/\alpha}
an
\text{dist}\left({u(t)},{f^{-1}\{0\}}\right)<\varepsilon^{1/\alpha}
. Then the Grownwall’s inequality and hypothesis (b) yield

\displaystyle\left|\lim_{s\to+\infty}u(s)-\lim_{s\to+\infty}u_{k}(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\lim_{s\to+\infty}u(s)-u(t)|+|u(t)-u_{k}(t)|+|u_{k}(t)-p_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq C\text{dist}\left({u(t)},{f^{-1}\{0\}}\right)^{\alpha}+\exp(t)\varepsilon+C\text{dist}\left({u_{k}(t)},{f^{-1}\{0\}}\right)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq(2C+\exp(t))\varepsilon.

Since
\{p\}\cup\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a subset of the bounded
\partial V
, it follows that
\sup\{l,l_{1},l_{2},...\}<+\infty
. In particular, there is some
T^{\prime}>0
such that the
u_{j}
’s and
u
are all constant on
T^{\prime}
. Thus, we actually have

\left|\lim_{s\to+\infty}u(s)-\lim_{s\to+\infty}u_{k}(s)\right|\leq(2C+\exp(T^{\prime}))\varepsilon.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we see that
\lim_{s\to+\infty}u(s)=\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{k}^{*}=x^{*}
, which means
u
, and thus
\gamma_{p}
, converges to
x^{*}
. This shows the first part of the proposition.

Now suppose that
f
is analytic. Let
x^{*}\in f^{-1}\{0\}
. By Theorem
5.2
. there is a bounded neighborhood
U
of
x^{*}
and some
\beta>0
such that for any
p\in U
,
\gamma_{p}
converges at rate
\beta
. Since
U
is bounded and
f
is smooth,
f
is Lipschitz on
U
, so there is some
L>0
with
|f(z_{1})-f(z_{2})|\leq L|z_{1}-z_{2}|
for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in U
. It follows that the curve length

of any such
\gamma_{p}
can be estimated by

l(\gamma_{p})\leq\tilde{C}|f(p)|^{\beta}\leq\tilde{C}|f(p)-0|^{\beta}\leq\tilde{C}L^{\beta}|p-q|^{\beta},

where
\tilde{C}>0
is some constant, and
q\in f^{-1}\{0\}
is any point satisfying
|p-q|=\text{dist}\left({p},{f^{-1}\{0\}}\right)
(
q
exists because
f^{-1}\{0\}
closed). This shows

satisfies the hypotheses and the desired result follows.
∎

We now apply the results above to our loss function

. By Lemma

,

is analytic whenever

is a generic activation, whence Theorems
5.1
,
5.2
and Proposition

hold for

.

5.2
Convergence Rate and Limiting Direction of gradient flow

In this part we investigate the convergence rate and limiting direction of a gradient flow near any (fixed)
Q_{P,\pi}^{r}
for
r\in\{m_{0},...,m\}
, given that
Q_{P,\pi}^{r}
is separated. As we shall see, the behavior of gradient flows depends largely on
r
and partition
P
.

Let’s begin with two general lemmas about a “parameterized version of center manifold theory”. Consider a dynamical system of the form

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=f(x(t),y(t),z(t));

(12)

\displaystyle\dot{y}(t)

\displaystyle=H(z(t))y(t)+g(x(t),y(t),z(t));

\displaystyle\dot{z}(t)

\displaystyle=m(x(t),y(t),z(t)),

where
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
f,g,H,m
are
C^{3}
functions, and there is an open
U_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that for any
z\in U_{p}
, we have i)
H(z)
is negative definite, ii)
f(0,0,z)=g(0,0,z)=m(0,0,z)=0
, and iii) when
x,y\to 0
the maps
f,g,m
can be estimated as

\displaystyle f(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=O(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2});

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=O(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2});

\displaystyle m(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=O(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}).

Specifically, when
c=0
, we identify the space
\mathbb{R}^{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
, so that the system (
12
) becomes

\displaystyle\dot{y}(t)

\displaystyle=H(z(t))y(t)+g(y(t),z(t));

\displaystyle\dot{z}(t)

\displaystyle=m(y(t),z(t)).

Moreover, for each
z\in U_{p}
, We also consider the following simplified system

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=f(x(t),y(t),z);

(13)

\displaystyle\dot{y}(t)

\displaystyle=H(z)y(t)+g(x(t),y(t),z).

Lemma 18
.

Consider the system (
12
). Let
\gamma=(x,y,z):[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{c+s+l}
be a solution curve to (
12
) that converges to some
(0,0,z^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{c+s+l}
with
z^{*}\in U_{p}
.

(a)

If
c=0
then
|z(t)-z^{*}|=O(|y(t)|^{2})
as
t\to+\infty
.

(b)

If
c>0
and
\varliminf_{t\to+\infty,x(t)\neq 0}\frac{|y(t)|}{|x(t)|^{2}}<+\infty
, then
|y(t)|=O(|x(t)|^{2})
as
t\to+\infty
.

Proof.

(a)

Since
\dot{y}(t)=H(z(t))y(t)+O(|y(t)|^{2})
when
c=0
, for each
1\leq j\leq s
,
|y_{j}(t)|
decreases to 0 at exponential rate. Thus, there is some
T>0
such that for any
\zeta\geq t>T
and for any
1\leq j\leq s
, we have
|y_{j}(t)|<1
,
|\dot{y}_{j}(t)|\geq\frac{|\lambda_{j}|}{2}|y_{j}(t)|
, and

D_{1}|y_{j}(t)|e^{-\mu_{1}(\zeta-t)}\leq|y_{j}(\zeta)|\leq D_{2}|y_{j}(t)|e^{-\mu_{2}(\zeta-t)}

for some
D_{1},D_{2}\geq 0,\mu_{1},\mu_{2}>0
depending only on
T
. These assumptions imply that
y_{j}
is decreasing and
y_{j}=0
or the sign of
y_{j}
does not change on
(T,+\infty)
. Thus, for any
t>T
,

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}y_{j}^{2}(\zeta)d\zeta

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=\lfloor{t}\rfloor}^{\infty}y_{j}^{2}(k)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k=\lfloor{t}\rfloor}^{\infty}y_{j}(k)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\int_{\lfloor{t}\rfloor}^{\infty}\dot{y}_{j}(\zeta)d\zeta\right)^{2}=\frac{4}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}y_{j}^{2}(\lfloor{t}\rfloor),

where
\lfloor{t}\rfloor
denotes the largest integer smaller than
t
. But then

|y_{j}(t)|\geq D_{1}|y_{j}(\lfloor{t}\rfloor)|e^{-\mu_{1}(t-\lfloor{t}\rfloor)}\geq D_{1}|y_{j}(\lfloor{t}\rfloor)|e^{-\mu_{1}}.

This means there is some
C_{j}>0
with
y_{j}^{2}(\lfloor{t}\rfloor)\leq C_{j}y_{j}^{2}(t)
whenever
t>T
. Since
m(x,y,z)=O(|y|^{2})
for
z\in U_{p}
, there is some
C_{z}>0
with
|\dot{z}(t)|\leq C_{z}|y(t)|^{2}
for
t>T
. It follows that when
t>T
,

\displaystyle|z(t)-z^{*}|\leq\int_{t}^{\infty}|\dot{z}(\zeta)|d\zeta

\displaystyle\leq C_{z}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\int_{t}^{\infty}y_{j}^{2}(\zeta)d\zeta

\displaystyle\leq C_{z}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{4}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}C_{j}y_{j}^{2}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\left(C_{z}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{4}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}C_{j}\right)|y(t)|^{2},

which shows that
|z(t)-z^{*}|=O(|y(t)|^{2}
as
t\to+\infty
.

(b)

Without loss of generality, assume that
H(z^{*})
is diagonal, and its eigenvalues are
\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{s}
; denote
\lambda:=\frac{1}{2}\max_{1\leq j\leq s}\lambda_{j}
. Let
C_{y}>0
be a constant such that
|g(x,y,z)|\leq C_{y}(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})
. Consider the quotient

Q(t):=\frac{|y(t)|^{2}}{|x(t)|^{4}}

for
t\in[0,+\infty)
such that
x(t)\neq 0
. Suppose that (b) does not hold, then
\varlimsup_{t\to+\infty,x(t)\neq 0}Q(t)=+\infty
. By hypothesis,
\varliminf_{t\to+\infty,x(t)\neq 0}Q(t)<+\infty
, so by continuity of
Q
we can find a
k_{0}>0
and a sequence
\{t_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\to+\infty
such that
|\lambda k_{0}|>2C_{y}
, and
|y(t_{n})|=k_{0}|x(t_{n})|^{2}
,
\dot{Q}(t_{n})\geq 0
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We will show that this gives a contradiction. A straightforward computation yields

\dot{Q}(t)=\frac{2\sum_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}(t)\dot{y}_{j}(t)}{|x(t)|^{4}}-\frac{4|y(t)|^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{c}x_{j}(t)\dot{x}_{j}(t)}{|x(t)|^{6}}

when
x(t)\neq 0
. As
t_{n}\to+\infty
, both
x(t_{n}),y(t_{n})\to 0
by hypothesis, thus for sufficiently large
t_{n}
we have
|y(t_{n})|\leq|x(t_{n})|
and
|(H(z(t))y(t))_{j}|\geq\frac{|\lambda_{j}|}{2}|y_{j}(t)|
for all
j\in\{1,...,s\}
. Then for such
t_{n}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}(t_{n})\dot{y}_{j}(t_{n})

\displaystyle=\langle y(t_{n}),H(z(t))y(t_{n})\rangle+\langle y(t_{n}),g(x(t_{n}),y(t_{n}),z(t_{n}))\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\lambda|y(t_{n})|^{2}+|y(t_{n})|C_{y}(|x(t_{n})|^{2}+|y(t_{n})|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda|y(t_{n})|^{2}+2C_{y}|y(t_{n})||x(t_{n})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq k_{0}(\lambda k_{0}+2C_{y})|x(t_{n})|^{4}.

Since
\lambda<0
, by our assumption we have
k_{0}(\lambda k_{0}+2C_{y})<0
. On the other hand, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle|y(t_{n})|^{2}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{s}x_{j}(t_{n})\dot{x}_{j}(t_{n})\right|

\displaystyle\leq k_{0}^{2}|x(t_{n})|^{4}|x(t_{n})||\dot{x}(t_{n})|

\displaystyle\leq k_{0}^{2}|x(t_{n})|^{5}m(x(t_{n}),y(t_{n}),z(t_{n}))

\displaystyle=O(|x(t_{n})|^{7}).

Therefore,

\dot{Q}(t_{n})\leq 2k_{0}(\lambda k_{0}+2C_{y})-O(|x(t_{n})|)

from which we can see that if
N\in\mathbb{N}
is chosen so that for any
n>N
,
O(|x(t_{n})|)\leq k_{0}(-\lambda k_{0}-2C_{y})
, we would have
\dot{Q}(t_{n})\leq k_{0}(\lambda k_{0}+2C_{y})<0
, contradicting our assumption that
\dot{Q}(t_{n})>0
for each
n
.

∎

Remark 19
.

When
c>0
, we do not know much about the relationship between

and

. The best estimate we know is

\displaystyle|z(t)-z^{*}|=O\left(\int_{t}^{\infty}|\dot{z}(s)|ds\right)

Then we focus on systems with

Lemma 20

Consider the systems (

(a)

\displaystyle R(\theta)=R(\theta^{*})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\operatorname{Hess}R(\theta^{*})(\theta-\theta^{*}),\theta-\theta^{*}\rangle+O(|\theta-\theta^{*}|^{3}).

\displaystyle R(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\operatorname{Hess}R(\theta^{*})(\theta-\theta^{*}),\theta-\theta^{*}\rangle+O(|\theta-\theta^{*}|^{4})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\operatorname{Hess}R(\theta^{*})\pi_{s}(\theta-\theta^{*}),\pi_{s}(\theta-\theta^{*})\rangle+O(|\theta-\theta^{*}|^{4})

5.3
Local Recovery by Gradient Flow

We end this section with a discussion about whether the points in
Q^{*}
are stable under perturbation. Indeed, it is natural to ask by slightly perturbing a
\theta^{*}\in Q^{*}
to some
\theta_{0}
, do we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{\theta_{0}}(t)\in Q^{*}
? To study this problem, we first define “recovery stability” below which works for a more general case.

Definition 5.1

(recovery stability)
.

Let
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
. We say
\theta^{*}
is recovery stable if there is some
\delta>0
such that for any
\theta_{0}\in B(\theta^{*},\delta)
, the gradient flow
\gamma_{\theta_{0}}:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
with initial value
\theta_{0}
converges and satisfies

g(\theta^{*},x)=g\left(\lim_{t\to+\infty}\gamma_{\theta_{0}}(t),x\right)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If this is not true, we say
\theta^{*}
is recovery unstable. Given a subset
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
,
E
is called recovery stable if every
\theta^{*}\in E
is recovery stable; otherwise, we say
E
is recovery unstable.

The following result is an immediate corollary of Theorem
5.3
, Lemma
14
and Lemma
16
. It fully explains when a point in
Q^{*}
is recovery (un)stable.

Theorem 5.4

(recovery stability)
.

Given
m_{0}\leq r\leq m
, partition
P
and permutation
\pi
and separating inputs
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Then no point in
Q_{P,\pi}^{r}
is recovery stable when
n\leq r+(r-l)d
(
l
is the deficient number of
P
), and almost all points in
Q_{P,\pi}^{r}
are recovery stable when
n\geq r+(m+m_{0}-r)d
. Moreover, all points in
Q^{*}
are recovery stable when
n>(d+1)m
, namely,
Q^{*}
is recovery stable.

Proof.

The desired result follows from the observation that a point
\theta^{*}\in Q^{*}
is recovery stable if and only if it has a neighborhood
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m}
with
U\cap R^{-1}\{0\}=U\cap Q^{*}
.

So fix any
\theta^{*}\in Q^{*}
. First assume that this is not true, there is a sequence
\{\theta_{n}^{*}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
R^{-1}\{0\}\setminus Q^{*}
converging to
\theta^{*}
, whence by perturbing
\theta^{*}
to any
\theta_{n}^{*}
, the gradient flow
\gamma_{\theta_{n}^{*}}
starting at
\theta_{n}^{*}
clearly satisfies

\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{\theta_{n}}(t)=\theta_{n}^{*}\in R^{-1}\{0\}\setminus Q^{*}.

Conversely, if such a
U
exists, we can shrink it if necessary, so that for any
\theta_{0}\in U
,
\gamma_{\theta_{0}}
satisfies

\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{\theta_{0}}(t)\in Q^{*}.

Since any two point in
Q^{*}
represent the same model
f^{*}
, we clearly have

g(\theta^{*},x)=f^{*}(x)=g(\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{\theta_{0}}(t),x)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore,
\theta^{*}
is recovery stable.
∎

6
Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we analyzed the geometry and dynamics of the loss landscape for two-layer neural networks, focusing on the vicinity of global minima. We showed that the global minima with zero generalization error can be partitioned into distinct branches, which become geometrically separated from other global minima as the sample size increases. We identified the sample size thresholds for this separation and demonstrated that, for sufficiently large sample sizes, the loss function
R
is Morse–Bott at almost all points in certain branches, ensuring non-degenerate Hessians along their normal bundles. Our analysis revealed that gradient flows sufficiently close to the global minima converge to points within these minima, with linear convergence near Morse–Bott branches and sublinear convergence near others. We also introduced the concept of “recovery stability”, showing that almost all points in certain branches are recovery stable when the sample size is large enough.

Our results provide a detailed understanding of the loss landscape and training dynamics of two-layer neural networks, explaining their ability to find well-generalizing solutions even in the overparameterized regime. This work lays a foundation for further studies on the global recovery capabilities and generalization performance of neural networks. We demonstrated that two-layer neural networks can locally recover the target function in the overparameterization regime, guaranteed by the separation of branches of global minima and the convergence properties of gradient flows.

Finally, we point out several possible future works. First, the analysis in this paper could be extended to deeper neural networks with more than two layers to understand the increased complexity in the loss landscape. Second, we shall figure out how different activation functions, various initialization schemes and regularization techniques would impact generalization capabilities, training dynamics and performance. Furthermore, we expect empirical studies to validate the theoretical findings and explore their practical implications. Finally, future research on global recovery and generalization could provide a comprehensive understanding of neural networks’ ability to generalize from a global perspective.
