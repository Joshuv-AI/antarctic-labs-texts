Abstract
. In this paper, we study extremal problems of Steklov eigenvalues on combinatorial graphs by extending Friedman’s theory [Duke Math. J. 69 (1993), no. 3, 487–525] of nodal domains for Laplacian eigenfunctions to Steklov eigenfunctions, and solve an extremal problem for Steklov eigenvalues on combinatorial graphs that is an analogue of the extremal problem solved by Friedman [Duke Math. J. 83 (1996), no. 1, 1–18.] for Laplacian eigenvalues. More precisely, we mainly show that the minimum of the i th i^{\rm th} Steklov eigenvalue on a connected combinatorial graph with n n vertices is essentially attained by a star with each arm a minimal broom when i | n i\not|n , and attained by a regular comb with each tooth a minimal broom when i | n i|n .

1.
Introduction

For a compact Riemannian manifold with boundary, the Steklov operator or Dirichlet-to-Neumann map (DtN map for short) is defined to be the map sending the Dirichlet boundary data of a harmonic function on the manifold to its Neumann boundary data. The eigenvalues of the Steklov operator are called Steklov eigenvalues of the manifold. Such notions were originated in Steklov’s study of liquid sloshing more than a century ago (See
[
25
,
18
]
). Steklov operators or DtN maps are also original models for inverse problems such as detecting the inside of a body by boundary measurements. Escobar
[
5
]
also found relations between Steklov eigenvalues and the Yamabe problems on Riemannian manifolds with boundary. In recently years, Fraser and Scheon
[
6
]
found relations between Steklov eigenfunctions and free boundary minimal submanifolds in the Euclidean ball and made important progresses on extremal problems of Steklov eigenvalues (
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
).

The notions of Steklov operators and Steklov eigenvalues were recently extended to discrete settings independently by Hua, Huang and Wang in
[
16
]
and Hassannezhad and Miclo in
[
13
]
. Colbois and Girouard
[
3
]
also considered similar extensions of Steklov eigenvalues to the discrete settings when studying Steklov eigenvalues on manifolds constructed out of graphs (See also
[
4
]
). Although the notions are new, there have been a number of studies on exploring the properties of Steklov eigenvalues in the discrete setting. For example, in
[
13
,
16
,
17
,
12
,
14
,
20
,
26
]
, the authors considered isoperimetric control of Steklov eigenvalues. In
[
15
,
27
]
, the authors considered monotonicity of Steklov eigenvalues. In
[
19
]
, the author gave some interesting lower bounds. In
[
24
]
, the authors compared the Steklov eigenvalues with the Laplacian eigenvalues. In
[
23
]
, the authors obtained a Lichnerowicz-type estimate for Steklov eigenvalues. In
[
22
]
, the authors introduced higher degree versions of Steklove operators and Steklov eigenvalues.

In this paper, motivated by the works of Fraser and Schoen in the smooth setting and Friedman’s works
[
10
,
11
]
for Laplacian eigenvalues on combinatorial graphs, and also motivated by the works
[
14
,
15
]
of He and Hua, and our previous work
[
27
]
, we consider extremal problems on combinatorial graphs. Let’s first recall some preliminary notions for graphs.

Definition 1.1
.

(1)

A triple
(G,m,w)
is called a weighted graph where

(a)

G
is a simple graph with
V(G)
and
E(G)
the sets of vertices and edges of
G
respectively;

(b)

m:V(G)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the vertex-measure;

(c)

w:E(G)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the edge-weight.

When
m\equiv 1
and
w\equiv 1
, we say that
G
is equipped with the unit weight. A subgraph
G^{\prime}
of the weighted graph
G
is viewed as a weighted graph by restricting the vertex-measure and edge-weight of
G
to
G^{\prime}
. For convenience, the edge-weight
w
is also viewed as a symmetric function on
V(G)\times V(G)
by zero extension.

(2)

A pair
(G,B)
is called a graph with boundary where
G
is a simple graph and
B\subset V(G)
. The set
\Omega:=V(G)\setminus B
is called the interior of
G
. We will also denote
B
and
\Omega
as
B(G)
and
\Omega(G)
respectively if necessary.

(3)

A simple graph
G
is called a combinatorial graph with boundary (or is called a combinatorial graph for simplicity) if it is equipped with the unit weight and
B(G)=\{v\in V(G)\ |\ \deg v\leq 1\}
. For a nontrivial combinatorial tree
G
,
B(G)=L(G)
by definition, where
L(G)
is the collection of leaves in
G
.

On a weighted finite graph
(G,B,m,w)
with boundary, analogous to the smooth case, one can similarly define the Steklov operator
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}^{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{B}
(See Section
2
for details). The eigenvalues of
\Lambda
are called Steklov eigenvalues of the graph and denoted as

0=\sigma_{1}(G,B,m,w)\leq\sigma_{2}(G,B,m,w)\leq\cdots\leq\sigma_{|B|}(G,B,m,w).

For simplicity, we will also write
\sigma_{i}(G,B,m,w)
as
\sigma_{i}
,
\sigma_{i}(G)
or
\sigma_{i}(G,B)
if the ignored information is clear in context. When
i>|B|
, we conventionally take
\sigma_{i}=+\infty
. For the trivial graph
G
with only one vertex, the vertex is a boundary vertex by definition and hence
\sigma_{1}(G)=0
and
\sigma_{i}(G)=+\infty
for
i\geq 2
.

The extremal problem we considered in this paper is:

Let
G
be a connected combinatorial graph on
n
vertices. What is the minimum of
\sigma_{i}(G)
?
This problem is an analogue of the problem considered by Friedman
[
11
]
for Laplacian eigenvalues.

Note that the notion of graphs with boundary considered in this paper is more general than that in
[
19
]
. The main difference is that we don’t require that
E(B,B)=\emptyset
in (2) of Definition
1.1
. In fact, this kind of more general graphs with boundary was considered in
[
3
,
4
]
. Note that if all the edges in
E(B,B)
are deleted, we get a graph with boundary under the more special definition in
[
19
]
in general. Moreover, the process of deleting the edges in
E(B,B)
will reduce the Steklov eigenvalues by the simple monotonicity of Steklov eigenvalues in Theorem
1.2
below. Thus, there will be no difference in general when considering minimum problems on Steklov eigenvalues for the two kinds of graphs with boundary. The reason that we take this more general notion of graphs with boundary is twofold. One is that the notion is simpler and more general, and the other is that the path with two vertices can not be viewed as a combinatorial graph with boundary if we take the more special definition in
[
19
]
.

Our answer to the extremal problem for Steklov eigenvalues is similar to that of Friedman
[
11
]
for Laplacian eigenvalues. Follows is a summary of the main results of this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a connected finite combinatorial graph on
n\geq 2
vertices. Then,

(1)

\sigma_{2}(G)\geq\Lambda(\frac{n-1}{2})
. The equality holds if and only if

(a)

G={\rm DB}(m,2m,m)
when
n=4m+1
;

(b)

G={\rm DB}(m,2m+1,m)
when
n=4m+2
;

(c)

G={\rm DB}(m+1,2m,m+1),{\rm DB}(m,2m+2,m)\ \mbox{or}\ {\rm DB}(m,2m+1,m+1)
when
n=4m+3
;

(d)

G={\rm DB}(m+1,2m+1,m+1)
when
n=4m+4
;

(2)

for
i
with
2<i<n
and
i\not|\ n
,

\sigma_{i}(G)\geq\Lambda(m)

where
m=\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor
. Moreover, when
n=im+1
, the equality holds if and only if
G
is a star of degree
i
such that each arm is a minimal broom
{\rm Br}(m)
with the center of the star as the root.

(3)

for
i
with
2<i<n
and
i|n
,

\sigma_{i}(G)\geq\Lambda(m-1+\theta_{i})

where
m=\frac{n}{i}
and
\theta_{i}=\frac{1}{4\cos^{2}\frac{\pi}{2i}}
. The equality holds if and only if
G={\rm Comb}(P_{i};T)
when
i
is even, and
G={\rm Comb}(P_{i};T)\mbox{ or }{\rm Comb}(C_{i};T)
when
i
is odd. Here
P_{i}
and
C_{i}
are the path and the cycle on
i
vertices respectively. Moreover,
T
is the minimal broom
{\rm Br}(m-1+\theta_{i})
with the Dirichlet boundary vertex deleted and the vertex adjacent to the Dirichlet boundary vertex as the root.

Here,

\Lambda(l)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{l}&0<l<1\\
\frac{1}{1+\lfloor\frac{l+1}{2}\rfloor(l-\lfloor\frac{l+1}{2}\rfloor)}&l\geq 1.\end{array}\right.

For the definitions of the minimal broom
{\rm Br}(l)
, dumbbell
{\rm DB}(d_{1},i,d_{2})
and regular combs
{\rm Comb}(P_{i};T)
and
{\rm Comb}(C_{i};T)
, see Definition
2.4
, Definition
2.5
and Definition
2.7
in Section
2
respectively.

Note that when
m\geq 3
is an odd number, there are two different brooms in
{\rm Br}(m)
(See Lemma
2.3
). Thus, when
m
is an odd number not less than
3
, there are
i+1
different stars of degree
i
with all arms minimal brooms
{\rm Br}(m)
. So, in Theorem
1.1
, when
n=im+1
with
m
an odd number not less than
3
, there are
i+1
different graphs so that the equality
\sigma_{i}(G)=\Lambda(m)
holds. When
n=im+s
with
2\leq s\leq i-1
, the same as in
[
11
]
, we are not able to completely characterize the graphs such that the equality
\sigma_{i}(G)=\Lambda(m)
holds. However, the equality holds for example on the star with degree
i+s-1
such that
i
arms are minimal brooms
{\rm Br}(m)
and
s-1
arms are paths of length
1
.

Our strategy to solve the extremal problem for Steklov eigenvalues on combinatorial finite graphs is similar to the strategy by Friedman
[
11
]
solving the corresponding extremal problems for Laplacian eigenvalues. First by the following simple monotonicity of Steklov eigenvalues that is different with the monotonicity in
[
15
,
27
]
, we reduce the extremal problems to trees.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{B},m,w)
be a weighted finite graph and
(G,B)
be such that
G
is a subgraph of
\widetilde{G}
and
B\supset\widetilde{B}
. Then

(1.1)

\sigma_{i}(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{B})\geq\sigma_{i}(G,B)

for
i=1,2,\cdots,|\widetilde{B}|
. In particular, if
\widetilde{G}
is a finite combinatorial graph and
G
is a spanning subgraph of
\widetilde{G}
,
Then,

(1.2)

\sigma_{i}(\widetilde{G})\geq\sigma_{i}(G)

for all
i\geq 0
.

After reducing the extremal problem to trees, following the strategy by Friedman
[
11
]
, we then study the minimal problem of the first Steklov eigenvalues with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data on trees (See Section
5
for details). In
[
11
]
, Friedman showed that the minimal tree for the first Laplacian eigenvalues with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data is essentially a path. However, it is rather different for the Steklov case. The minimal tree for this case is essentially a minimal broom (See Theorem
5.1
for details). This makes the minimal graphs for Steklov eigenvalues become more complicated than those for Lapalcian eigenvalues in
[
11
]
.

Next, by developing a theory of nodal domains for Steklov eigenvalues that is similar to the theory of nodal domains for Laplacian eigenvalues established by Friedman
[
10
]
, we reduce the extremal problem for Steklov eigenvalues on trees to the minimum problem of the first Steklov eigenvalues with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data on its nodal domains which has been solved in the last step.

The organization of the rest of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce some preliminary notions and symbols on analysis of graphs; In Section
3
, we prove the monotonicity in Theorem
1.2
and discuss its rigidity; In Section
4
, we study nodal domains for Steklov eigenfunctions; In Section

, we obtain the minimum for the first Steklov eigenvalues with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data on trees which is an analogue of Lemma 3.1 in
[
11
]
and plays an important role in later applications; In Section
6
, we recall the notion of clump numbers of trees in
[
11
]
, explore some of its properties and recall the results in
[
11
]
about reducing clump numbers of trees by removing edges. In Section
7
, we prove Theorem
1.1
. A crucial step in the proof of Theorem
1.1
is to relate the first positive Steklov eigenvalue of a tree to its clump number (See Theorem
7.1
) which is an analogue of Lemma 4.1 in
[
11
]
for the first positive Laplacian eigenvalue.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some notions and symbols on spectral analysis of graphs.

Let
(G,m,w)
be a weighted finite graph. As in
[
1
, Definition 2.1]
, a skew symmetric function
\alpha:V(G)\times V(G)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\alpha(x,y)=0
when
x\not\sim y
is called a flow on the graph
G
. The collection of all flows on
G
is denoted as
A^{1}(G)
. Equipped with the spaces
\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
and
A^{1}(G)
the natural inner products

(2.1)

{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle}_{G}=\sum_{x\in V(G)}f(x)g(x)m_{x}

and

(2.2)

{\left\langle\alpha,\beta\right\rangle}_{G}=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E(G)}\alpha(x,y)\beta(x,y)w_{xy}

respectively. When
A\subset V(G)
and
S\subset E(G)
, we define

(2.3)

{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle}_{A}=\sum_{x\in A}f(x)g(x)m_{x}

and

(2.4)

{\left\langle\alpha,\beta\right\rangle}_{S}=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in S}\alpha(x,y)\beta(x,y)w_{xy}.

Let
d:\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}\to A^{1}(G)
be the map:

(2.5)

df(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f(y)-f(x)&x\sim y\\
0&x\not\sim y.\end{array}\right.

Let
d^{*}:A^{1}(G)\to\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
be the adjoint operator of
d
and define the Laplacian operator of
G
as

(2.6)

\Delta_{G}f=-d^{*}df.

By direct computation, it is not hard to check that

(2.7)

\Delta_{G}f(x)=\frac{1}{m_{x}}\sum_{y\in V(G)}(f(y)-f(x))w_{xy}

for any
x\in V(G)
and
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
. By the definition of
\Delta_{G}
, it is clear that

(2.8)

-{\left\langle\Delta_{G}f,g\right\rangle}_{G}={\left\langle df,dg\right\rangle}_{G}

for any
f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
. So
-\Delta_{G}
is a nonnegative self-adjoint operator on
\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
. Its eigenvalues are denoted as

0=\mu_{1}(G)\leq\mu_{2}(G)\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{|V(G)|}(G)

and are called the Laplacian eigenvalues of
G
.

Let
(G,B,m,w)
be a weight finite graph with boundary. For any
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
, define

(2.9)

\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}(x)=\frac{1}{m_{x}}\sum_{y\in V(G)}(f(x)-f(y))w_{xy}=-\Delta_{G}f(x)

for any
x\in B
. The operator is regarded as the discrete version of taking normal derivative on the boundary of a Riemannian manifold. By (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), we have the following Green’s identity:

(2.10)

{\left\langle df,dg\right\rangle}_{G}={\left\langle\frac{\partial f}{\partial n},g\right\rangle}_{B}-{\left\langle\Delta_{G}f,g\right\rangle}_{\Omega}.

This may be the reason that one defines
\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}
as
-\Delta_{G}f\big|_{B}
.

A real number
\sigma
is called a Steklov eigenvalue of
(G,B,m,w)
if the following boundary value problem:

(2.11)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{G}f(x)=0&x\in\Omega\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}(x)=\sigma f(x)&x\in B\end{array}\right.

has nonzero solutions. A nonzero solution
f
of the boundary value problem is called a Steklov eigenfunction for the eigenvalue
\sigma
. It is not hard the see that the Steklov eigenvalues are the eigenvalues of the so called Steklov operator or DtN map:

\Lambda:\mathbb{R}^{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{B},\Lambda(u)=\frac{\partial\widehat{u}}{\partial n}

where
\widehat{u}
means the harmonic extension of
u
. That is,
\widehat{u}
satisifies the following Dirichlet boundary value problem:

(2.12)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{G}\widehat{u}(x)=0&x\in\Omega\\
\widehat{u}(x)=u(x)&x\in B.\end{array}\right.

By (
2.10
), we know that

(2.13)

{\left\langle\Lambda(u),v\right\rangle}_{B}={\left\langle d\widehat{u},d\widehat{v}\right\rangle}_{G}

for any
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{B}
. So,
\Lambda
is a nonnegative self-adjoint operator on
\mathbb{R}^{B}
, and its eigenvalues are denoted as

0=\sigma_{1}(G,B,m,w)\leq\sigma_{2}(G,B,m,w)\leq\cdots\leq\sigma_{|B|}(G,B,m,w).

For simplicity, we also denote
\sigma_{i}(G,B,m,w)
as
\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i}(G),\sigma_{i}(G,B)
when the ignored information is clear in context.

To extend Friedman’s theory of nodal domains for Laplacian eigenfunction to Steklov eigenfunctions, we need to consider Steklov eigenvalues with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data as in
[
15
,
27
]
. So, we introduce the notion of graph with boundary and Dirichlet boundary for convenience.

Definition 2.1
.

A triple
(G,B,B_{D})
is called a graph with boundary
B
and Dirichlet boundary
B_{D}
if
G
is a simple graph,
B,B_{D}\subset V(G)
and
B\cap B_{D}=\emptyset
.
\Omega_{D}:=V(G)\setminus B_{D}
is called the Dirichlet interior of
G
and
\Omega=\Omega_{D}\setminus B
is called the interior of
G
. An edge in
E(B_{D},\Omega_{D})
is called a Dirichlet boundary edge. We will also denote
B,B_{D},\Omega
and
\Omega_{D}
as
B(G),B_{D}(G),\Omega(G)
and
\Omega_{D}(G)
respectively if necessary.

Let
(G,B,B_{D},m,w)
be a weighted finite graph with boundary
B
and Dirichlet boundary
B_{D}
. A real number
\lambda
is called a Steklov eigenvalue of
(G,B,B_{D},m,w)
with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data on
B_{D}
if the following boundary value problem:

(2.14)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{G}f(x)=0&x\in\Omega\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}(x)=\lambda f(x)&x\in B\\
f(x)=0&x\in B_{D}\end{array}\right.

has nonzero solutions. A nonzero solution
f
of the boundary value problem is called a Steklov eigenfunction with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data on
B_{D}
for the eigenvalue
\lambda
. The Steklov eigenvalues with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data on
B_{D}
are the eigenvalues of the following Steklov operator with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data on
B_{D}
:

\Lambda_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{B},\ \Lambda_{0}(u)=\frac{\partial\zeta_{u}}{\partial n}

where
\zeta_{u}
satisfies

(2.15)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{G}\zeta_{u}(x)=0&x\in\Omega\\
\zeta_{u}(x)=u(x)&x\in B\\
\zeta_{u}(x)=0&x\in B_{D}.\end{array}\right.

By (
2.10
),

(2.16)

{\left\langle\Lambda_{0}(u),v\right\rangle}_{B}={\left\langle d\zeta_{u},d\zeta_{v}\right\rangle}_{G}

for any
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{B}
. So,
\Lambda_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{B}
is a nonnegative self-adjoint operator on
\mathbb{R}^{B}
, and its eigenvalues are denoted as:

0\leq\lambda_{1}(G,B,B_{D},m,w)\leq\lambda_{2}(G,B,B_{D},m,w)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{|B|}(G,B,B_{D},m,w).

For simplicity, we also denote
\lambda_{i}(G,B,B_{D},m,w)
as
\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i}(G),\lambda_{i}(G,B,B_{D})
when the ignored information is clear in context. By the Dirichlet principle, it is clear that

\lambda_{1}(G,B,B_{D})=\min\left\{\frac{{\left\langle df,df\right\rangle}_{G}}{{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle}_{B}}\ \bigg|\ f\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}\ \mbox{with }f|_{B}\not\equiv 0\ \mbox{and }f|_{B_{D}}=0.\right\}.

Thus, if
G
is connected and
B_{D}
is nonempty, then
\lambda_{1}(G,B,B_{D})>0
. It is also clear that the measures of the vertices in
B_{D}
do not affect the map
\Lambda_{0}
and the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(G,B,B_{D})
.

Next, we introduce the notion of a broom.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
P
be the path of length
i
. The tree formed by adding a Dirichlet boundary edge to one of the end points of
P
with length
l
(i.e. with weight
\frac{1}{l}
) and adding
d
boundary vertices to the other end point of
P
is denoted as
{\rm Br}(l,i,d)
which is called a broom with parameters
(l,i,d)
. When
d=0
, the other end point of
P
is viewed as the boundary vertex. The Dirichlet boundary vertex is viewed as the root of the broom. All the vertices and edges of
{\rm Br}(l,i,d)
except the Dirichlet boundary edge are considered to be of unit weight.

Note that
{\rm Br}(l,i,1)={\rm Br}(l,i+1,0)
by definition. The Steklov eigenvalues with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data of a broom can be computed directly.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any real number
l>0
and nonnegative integers
i
and
d
,

\lambda_{1}({\rm Br}(l,i,d))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{l+i}&d=0\\
\frac{1}{1+(l+i)d}&d\geq 1.\end{array}\right.

Moreover, let
v_{0}\sim v_{1}\sim\cdots\sim v_{i}
be the path
P
and
o\sim v_{0}
in
{\rm Br}(l,i,d)
, and
u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{d}
be the boundary vertices of
{\rm Br}(l,i,d)
when
d>0
, where
o
is the root of
{\rm Br}(l,i,d)
. Then, the eigenfunction
f
for
\lambda_{1}
is given by

(2.17)

f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&x=o\\
\frac{l+j}{l+i}&x=v_{j}\mbox{ with }j=0,1,\cdots,i\end{array}\right.

when
d=0
, and

(2.18)

f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&x=o\\
\frac{(l+j)d}{1+(l+i)d}&x=v_{j}\mbox{ with }j=0,1,\cdots,i\\
1&x=u_{j}\mbox{ with }j=1,2,\cdots,d\\
\end{array}\right.

when
d>0
. Furthermore, when
d\geq 2
,

\lambda_{2}({\rm Br}(l,i,d))=\cdots=\lambda_{d}({\rm Br}(l,i,d))=1

with the eigenspace generated by

f_{j}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&x=u_{1}\\
-1&x=u_{j}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

for
j=2,3,\cdots,d
.

Proof.

The conclusion can be shown by direct verification.
∎

Next, we introduce the notion of minimal brooms. It plays an important role in Section
5
.

Definition 2.3
.

For any nonnegative integer
n
and positive real number
l
, we denote
{\rm Br}(l,n)
the brooms that have the minimal
\lambda_{1}
among all brooms
{\rm Br}(l,i,d)
with
i+d=n
. We call
{\rm Br}(l,n)
the minimal brooms with parameters
(l,n)
and denote
\lambda_{1}({\rm Br}(l,n))
as
\Lambda(l,n)
.

By Lemma
2.1
, we have the following conclusion.

Lemma 2.2
.

The function
\Lambda(l,n)
is strictly decreasing on
l
and
n
. More precisely, when
n=0,1
,
{\rm Br}(l,n)={\rm Br}(l,n,0)
, and when
n\geq 2,

\begin{split}{\rm Br}(l,n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm Br}(l,0,n)&l\geq n\\

Proposition 2.1

Definition 2.8
.

(1)

(2)

A weight on an abstract one dimensional simplicial complex is to assign each 0-simplex a measure and each 1-simplex a length.

(3)

Let
(G,m,w)
be a weighted graph. Assign to each 0-simplex

of

. We also simply denote
(K(G),m,\frac{1}{w})
as

.

(4)

Let
(G,m,w)
be a weighted graph and

be its geometric representation. We then identify each 1-simplex

in

with the interval

. Here

and

Conversely, from a connected open set in

Similarly as in

]
, we need the following two results when considering the case

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(G,m,w)
be a weighted connected bipartite finite graph. Then, the greatest Laplacian eigenvalue
\mu_{|V(G)|}(G)
is of multiplicity one and its eigenfunction
f
must have alternating signs which means that
f(x)f(y)<0
when
x\sim y
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be a function on
V(G)
taking values in
\{1,-1\}
which has alternating signs. The existence of
\varphi
is guaranteed by that
G
is bipartite. Let
f
be an eigenfunction for
\mu_{|V(G)|}
. Note that

(2.28)

\begin{split}\mu_{|V(G)|}\geq&\frac{{\left\langle d(\varphi|f|),d(\varphi|f|)\right\rangle}_{G}}{{\left\langle\varphi|f|,\varphi|f|\right\rangle}_{G}}\\
=&\frac{\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E(G)}(|f(x)|+|f(y)|)^{2}w_{xy}}{{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle}_{G}}\\
\geq&\frac{{\left\langle df,df\right\rangle}_{G}}{{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle}_{G}}\\
=&\mu_{|V(G)|}.\end{split}

So,
\varphi|f|
is an eigenfunction of
\mu_{|V(G)|}
.

Next, we want to show that
|f|>0
. Otherwise, there is a vertex
v
with
f(v)=0
and some
x\sim v
with
|f(x)|>0
since
G
is connected. Then,

(2.29)

\begin{split}0=&-\mu_{|V(G)|}\varphi(v)|f|(v)\\
=&\Delta_{G}(\varphi|f|)(v)\\
=&\frac{1}{m_{x}}\sum_{x\sim v}\varphi(x)|f(x)|w_{xv}\\
=&-\frac{\varphi(v)}{m_{x}}\sum_{x\sim v}|f|(x)w_{xv}\\
\neq&0\end{split}

which is a contradiction. So
|f|>0
. Moreover, by that the inequalities in (
2.28
) are equalities,

(2.30)

|f(x)|+|f(y)|=|f(x)-f(y)|

when
x\sim y
. So
f(x)f(y)<0
when
x\sim y
. This shows that
f
has alternating signs.

Suppose the multiplicity of
\mu_{|V(G)|}
is greater than one. Then, there are two eigenfunctions
f,g
of
\mu_{|V(G)|}
such that
{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle}_{G}=0
. However, this is impossible because both
f
and
g
have alternating signs. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
(G,m,w)
be a weighted connected bipartite finite graph and

be a Laplacian eigenfunction for

. Let

be the geometric representation of

in

be the zero point of

\frac{1}{m_{x}}\sum_{y\sim x}\frac{1}{\left|[xz_{xy}]\right|}=\mu_{|V(G)|}(G).

Proof.

Note that
f(x)\neq 0
and
w_{xy}=\frac{1}{|[xy]|}
for any edge
\{x,y\}
. So

(2.32)

\begin{split}\mu_{|V(G)|}(G)f(x)=&-(\Delta_{G}f)(x)\\
=&\frac{1}{m_{x}}\sum_{y\sim x}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{|[xy]|}\\
=&\frac{1}{m_{x}}\sum_{y\sim x}\frac{\widetilde{f}(x)-\widetilde{f}(z_{xy})}{|[xz_{xy}]|}\\
=&\frac{1}{m_{x}}\sum_{y\sim x}\frac{1}{|[xz_{xy}]|}f(x).\end{split}

Hence,
\frac{1}{m_{x}}\sum_{y\sim x}\frac{1}{|[xz_{xy}]|}=\mu_{|V|}(G)
.

4.
First Steklov eigenfunction with Dirichlet boundary data and Steklov nodal domains

In this section, we extend the Friedman’s theory of nodal domains for Laplacian eigenfunctions to Steklov eigenfunctions. We first show that the first Steklov eigenfunction with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data is positive and the first eigenvalue is of multiplicity one which is a discrete version of Courant’s result for Steklov eigenvalues.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(G,B,B_{D},m,w)
be a weighted connected finite graph with boundary
B
and Dirichlet boundary
B_{D}
. Suppose that the induced subgraph
G[\Omega_{D}]
of
G
on
\Omega_{D}
is connected. Then, the eigenfunctions of
\lambda_{1}(G,B,B_{D})
must be everywhere positive or everywhere negative in
\Omega_{D}
.

Proof.

Let
f_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
be an eigenfunction for
\lambda_{1}(G,B_{N},B_{D})
. Then,
f_{0}
is a minimizer of the Rayleigh quotient

(4.1)

R_{0}[f]=\frac{{\left\langle df,df\right\rangle}_{G}}{{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle}_{B}}

among all
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
with
f|_{B}\not\equiv 0
and
f|_{B_{D}}\equiv 0
. First, note that

(4.2)

\begin{split}{\left\langle d|f|,d|f|\right\rangle}_{G}=&\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E(G)}(|f|(x)-|f|(y))^{2}w_{xy}\\
\leq&\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E(G)}(f(x)-f(y))^{2}w_{xy}\\
=&{\left\langle df,df\right\rangle}_{G}\end{split}

with strictly inequality when for some edge
\{x,y\}
,
f(x)<0<f(y)
. Moreover,

(4.3)

{\left\langle f_{0},f_{0}\right\rangle}_{B}={\left\langle|f_{0}|,|f_{0}|\right\rangle}_{B}.

So,
|f_{0}|
is also a first eigenfunction and

(4.4)

{\left\langle df_{0},df_{0}\right\rangle}_{G}={\left\langle d|f_{0}|,d|f_{0}|\right\rangle}_{G}.

We then only need to show that
|f_{0}|>0
on
\Omega_{D}
. Otherwise, because
f_{0}\not\equiv 0
and
G[\Omega_{D}]
is connected, there is a vertex
v\in\Omega_{D}
, so that
|f_{0}|(v)=0
and
|f_{0}|(x)>0
for some
x\in\Omega_{D}
with
x\sim v
. If
v\in\Omega
, then

(4.5)

0=\Delta_{G}|f_{0}|(v)=\frac{1}{m_{v}}\sum_{y\sim v}(|f_{0}|(y)-|f_{0}|(v))w_{vy}=\frac{1}{m_{v}}\sum_{y\sim v}|f_{0}|(y)w_{vy}>0

which is a contradiction. If
v\in B
, then

(4.6)

0=\lambda_{1}|f_{0}|(v)=\frac{\partial|f_{0}|}{\partial n}(v)=-\frac{1}{m_{v}}\sum_{y\sim v}|f_{0}|(y)w_{vy}<0

which is also a contradiction. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

By Theorem
4.1
, we have the following straightforward consequence.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
(G,B,B_{D},m,w)
be a weighted connected finite graph with boundary
B
and Dirichlet boundary
B_{D}
. Suppose that the induced subgraph
G[\Omega_{D}]
is connected. Then,
\lambda_{1}(G,B,B_{D})
is of multiplicity one and any higher eigenfunctions must change signs on
B
.

Proof.

If
\lambda_{1}
is not of multiplicity one, then by Theorem
4.1
, there are two eigenfunctions
f_{1}|_{B}>0,f_{2}|_{B}>0
of
\lambda_{1}
such that
{\left\langle f_{1},f_{2}\right\rangle}_{B}=0
. This is ridiculous. So,
\lambda_{1}
is of multiplicity one. Let
f
be an eigenfunction for
\lambda_{1}
with
f|_{B}>0
, and
g
be an eigenfunction for
\lambda_{i}
with
i\geq 2
, then
{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle}_{B}=0
implies that
g
must change signs on
B
. This completes the proof of the corollary.
∎

The following result shows that the assumption on the connectivity of
G[\Omega_{D}]
is necessary for the conclusion of Theorem
4.1
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(G,B,B_{D},m,w)
be a weighted connected finite graph with boundary
B
and Dirichlet boundary
B_{D}
. Let
G_{1},G_{2},\cdots,G_{k}
be the connected components of
G[\Omega_{D}]
,
\widetilde{G}_{i}=G[V(G_{i})\cup B_{D}]
and
B_{i}=B\cap V(G_{i})
for
i=1,2,\cdots,k
. Then,

{\rm Spec}(G,B,B_{D})=\sqcup_{i=1}^{k}{\rm Spec}(\widetilde{G}_{i},B_{i},B_{D}).

Here
{\rm Spec}(G,B,B_{D})
is the collection of all the Steklov eigenvalues with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data on
(G,B,B_{D},m,w)
counting multiplicities and
\sqcup
means disjoint union.

Proof.

Note that any eigenfunction of
(\widetilde{G}_{i},B_{i},B_{D})
is an eigenfunction of
(G,B,B_{D})
with the same eigenvalue after zero extension. This proves the conclusion.
∎

Next, we give the definition of nodal domains for Steklov eigenfunctions.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(G,B,m,w)
be a weighted connected finite graph with boundary and
K(G)
be its geometric representation. Let
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
be a Steklov eigenfunction of
(G,B,m,w)
and
U
be a connected component of
|K(G)|\setminus\widetilde{f}^{-1}(0)
. Then,
U
and its induced graph
G_{U}
are both called a nodal domain of
f
.

We first confirm some elementary properties of nodal domains.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
(G,B,m,w)
be a weighted connected finite graph with boundary,
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
be a Steklov eigenfunction of
(G,B,m,w)
and
U
be a nodal domain of
f
. Then
\Omega_{D}(G_{U})\neq\emptyset
,
G_{U}[\Omega_{D}(G_{U})]
is connected, and
B(G_{U})\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

By definition of
U
, there is a vertex
v\in U
such that
f(v)\neq 0
. Then
v\in\Omega_{D}(G_{U})
. So
\Omega_{D}(G_{U})\neq\emptyset
. Moreover, because
U
is path connected,
G_{U}[\Omega_{D}(G_{U})]
is a connected graph.

Finally, if
B(G_{U})=\emptyset
, let
g=\widetilde{f}|_{V(G_{U})}
. Then, for any
x\in V(G_{U})\setminus B_{D}(G_{U})
,
x\in\Omega(G)
. Thus
\Delta_{G}f(x)=0
. Furthermore, because
f(x)\neq 0
, for any
y\in V(G)
adjacent to
x
, either
\{x,y\}\in E(G_{U})
or there is a point
z_{y}
in the edge
[xy]
such that
\widetilde{f}(z_{y})=0
and
\{x,z_{y}\}\in E(G_{U})
with weight
\frac{1}{|[xz_{y}]|}
. In the later case, we have

(4.7)

\frac{g(z_{y})-g(x)}{|[xz_{y}]|}=\frac{\widetilde{f}(z_{y})-\widetilde{f}(x)}{|[xz_{y}]|}=\frac{\widetilde{f}(y)-\widetilde{f}(x)}{|[xy]|}=(f(y)-f(x))w_{xy}.

Therefore,

(4.8)

\begin{split}\Delta_{G_{U}}g(x)=\Delta_{G}f(x)=0\end{split}

for any
x\in V(G_{U})\setminus B_{D}(G_{U})
. So
g
as the solution of the following boundary value problem:

(4.9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{G_{U}}g(x)=0&x\in V(G_{U})\setminus B_{D}(G_{U})\\
g(x)=0&x\in B_{D}(G_{U}),\end{array}\right.

must be vanished. This is a contradiction. So
B(G_{U})\neq\emptyset
. This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

At the end of this section, we extend Friedman’s nodal domain theorem in
[
10
]
for Laplacian eigenfunctions to the case of Steklov eigenfunctions. They are both discrete analogue of Courant’s classical result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(G,B,m,w)
be a weighted connected finite graph with boundary and
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
be a Steklov eigenfunction of
G
with eigenvalue
\sigma>0
. Then, for each nodal domain
U
of
f
,
\widetilde{f}|_{V(G_{U})}
is a Steklov eigenfunction for
G_{U}
with vanishing data on
B_{D}(G_{U})
for the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(G_{U})=\sigma
.

Proof.

By the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
4.2
using (
4.7
), we know that
\widetilde{f}|_{V(G_{U})}
is a Steklov eigenfunction for
G_{U}
with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data on
B_{D}(G_{U})
with respect to the eigenvalues
\sigma
. Moreover
\widetilde{f}|_{\Omega_{D}(G_{U})}
does not change signs because
U
is a nodal domain. Then, by Corollary
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
, we know that
\widetilde{f}|_{V(G_{U})}
is an eigenfunction for
\lambda_{1}(G_{U})
.
∎

5.
A lower bound for the first Steklov eigenvalue with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data

In this section, we first obtain a crucial lower bound for the first Steklov eigenvalues with vanishing Dirichlet boundary data on trees extending Lemma 3.1 in
[
11
]
to the case of Steklov eigenvalues. Then, as an application of the lower bound, we give a more explicit sufficient condition for the rigidity of the isodiametric estimates by He-Hua
[
14
]
and its extension by the authors
[
27
]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(G,B,B_{D},m,w)
be a weighted finite tree with boundary
B\neq\emptyset
and Dirichlet boundary
B_{D}\neq\emptyset
such that
L(G)=B\cup B_{D}
,
G[\Omega_{D}]
is a tree with unit weight and
|\Omega_{D}|=n+1
with
n\geq 1
. Then,

(5.1)

\lambda_{1}(G,B,B_{D})\geq\Lambda(l,n).

The equality of the inequality holds if and only if
G[\Omega]
is a path of length
I(l,n)
and all the Dirichlet boundary vertices are adjacent to the same end vertex of the path and the other boundary vertices are adjacent to the other end vertex of the path. Here

I(l,n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&l\geq n\\
\lfloor\frac{n-l}{2}\rfloor&l<n,\mbox{ and }\{\frac{n-l}{2}\}<\frac{1}{2}\\
\lceil\frac{n-l}{2}\rceil&l<n,\mbox{ and }\{\frac{n-l}{2}\}>\frac{1}{2}\\
\lfloor\frac{n-l}{2}\rfloor\mbox{ or }\lceil\frac{n-l}{2}\rceil&l<n,\mbox{ and }\{\frac{n-l}{2}\}=\frac{1}{2}.\end{array}\right.

In particular, when
|B_{D}|=1
, the equality holds if and only if
G
is a minimal broom
{\rm Br}(l,n)
. Here

l=\frac{1}{\sum_{x\in B_{D},y\in\Omega_{D}}w_{xy}}.

Proof.

We first consider the case
|B_{D}|=1
. Let
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
be an eigenfunction for
\lambda_{1}
. Then, by Theorem
4.1
, we can assume that
f>0
on
\Omega_{D}
.

Let
B_{D}=\{o\}
and take
o
as the root of the tree
G
. we first claim that

f
is increasing along the tree. More precisely, if
x
is the parent of
y
, then
f(x)\leq f(y)
.
Indeed, if this is not true, i.e.
f(x)>f(y)
, let

(5.2)

g(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f(z)+2(f(x)-f(y))&\mbox{$z=y$ or $z$ is a descendant of $y$ }\\
f(z)&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Then,

(5.3)

{\left\langle dg,dg\right\rangle}_{G}={\left\langle df,df\right\rangle}_{G}

and

(5.4)

{\left\langle g,g\right\rangle}_{B}>{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle}_{B}

and
g(o)=0
. So,

\frac{{\left\langle dg,dg\right\rangle}_{G}}{{\left\langle g,g\right\rangle}_{B}}<\frac{{\left\langle df,df\right\rangle}_{G}}{{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle}_{B}}

which contradicts that
f
is the eigenfunction for
\lambda_{1}
.

Next, we claim that
f
is strictly increasing.
In fact, when
x
is the parent of
y\in B
,

(5.5)

\begin{split}0<\lambda_{1}f(y)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}(y)=f(y)-f(x).\end{split}

So,
f(x)<f(y)
. Therefore, if the claim is not true, then there are three vertices
x,y,z
such that
x
is the parent of
y
and
y
is the parent of
z
with
f(x)=f(y)
and
f(y)<f(z)
. Note that
y\in\Omega
since
y
is not a leaf. Then

\begin{split}0=&\Delta_{G}f(y)\\
=&(f(x)-f(y))+(f(z)-f(y))+\sum_{v\neq x,z}(f(v)-f(y))w_{vy}\\
\geq&f(z)-f(y)>0\end{split}

since
f(v)\geq f(y)
by that
f
is increasing. This is a contradiction.

Let
G_{0}
be a tree satisfying the assumption in the statement of the theorem achieving the minimum of
\lambda_{1}
when
|\Omega_{D}|
and the weight of the Dirichlet boundary edge are fixed (
G_{0}
must exist because the number of such kind of trees is finite.). Let
f_{0}
be the eigenfunction for
\lambda_{1}
with
f_{0}>0
on
\Omega_{D}(G_{0})
. By the claims before, we know that
f_{0}
is strictly increasing when taking the Dirichlet boundary vertex
o
as the root.

We first claim that
G_{0}[\Omega(G_{0})]
must be a path and
o
is adjacent to one of the end vertices of the path.
Otherwise, there are
x,y,z\in\Omega(G_{0})
such that
x
is the parent of
y
and
z
. Suppose that
f_{0}(y)\geq f_{0}(z)
. Let
G^{\prime}
be the tree obtained by removing the edge
\{x,y\}
and adding a new edge
\{y,z\}
on
G
. Then,

\frac{{\left\langle df_{0},df_{0}\right\rangle}_{G^{\prime}}}{{\left\langle f_{0},f_{0}\right\rangle}_{B(G^{\prime})}}=\frac{{\left\langle df_{0},df_{0}\right\rangle}_{G_{0}}-(f_{0}(y)-f_{0}(x))^{2}+(f_{0}(y)-f_{0}(z))^{2}}{{\left\langle f_{0},f_{0}\right\rangle}_{B(G_{0})}}<\frac{{\left\langle df_{0},df_{0}\right\rangle}_{G_{0}}}{{\left\langle f_{0},f_{0}\right\rangle}_{B(G_{0})}}.

since
0<f_{0}(x)<f_{0}(z)\leq f_{0}(y)
. This contradicts that
G_{0}
achieves the minimum of
\lambda_{1}
.

We next claim that
all the leaves in
B(G_{0})
must be adjacent to the other end vertex of the path
G_{0}[\Omega(G_{0})]
(i.e. the end vertex of
G_{0}[\Omega(G_{0})]
not adjacent to
o
when
G_{0}[\Omega(G_{0})]
is a nontrivial path).
Otherwise, let
v
be the other end vertex of
G_{0}[\Omega(G_{0})]
, and suppose there is a leave
x\in B(G_{0})
adjacent to some vertex
y\in\Omega(G_{0})\setminus\{v\}
. By that
f_{0}
is strictly increasing, we know that

(5.6)

f_{0}(x)>f_{0}(y)\ \mbox{and}\ f_{0}(v)>f_{0}(y).

Let
G^{\prime}
be the graph formed by removing the edge
\{x,y\}
from
G_{0}
and adding the edge
\{x,v\}
and
f
be such that

f(x)=f_{0}(x)-f_{0}(y)+f_{0}(v)>f_{0}(x)

and
f(z)=f_{0}(z)
for
z\neq x
. Then,

(5.7)

\frac{{\left\langle df,df\right\rangle}_{G^{\prime}}}{{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle}_{B(G^{\prime})}}<\frac{{\left\langle df_{0},df_{0}\right\rangle}_{G}}{{\left\langle f_{0},f_{0}\right\rangle}_{B(G_{0})}}=\lambda_{1}(G_{0}).

This contradicts that
G_{0}
achieves the minimum of
\lambda_{1}
.

Thus,
G_{0}
must be a minimal broom
{\rm Br}(l,n)
. This completes the proof of the theorem when
|B_{D}|=1
.

When
|B_{D}|\geq 2
, let
G_{0}
be the tree achieving the minimal
\lambda_{1}
among all trees satisfying the assumptions in the statement of the theorem and with
|\Omega_{D}|
,
|B_{D}|
and the weights of Dirichlet boundary edges are fixed. Let
f_{0}
be a first Dirichlet eigenfunction such that
f_{0}|_{\Omega_{D}(G_{0})}>0
. Let
v\in\Omega(G_{0})
with

f(v)=\min_{x\in\Omega(G_{0})}f(x).

Let
G^{\prime}
be the tree by moving all the Dirichlet boundary edges of
G_{0}
to
v
. Then,

(5.8)

\frac{{\left\langle df_{0},df_{0}\right\rangle}_{G^{\prime}}}{{\left\langle f_{0},f_{0}\right\rangle}_{B(G^{\prime})}}\leq\frac{{\left\langle df_{0},df_{0}\right\rangle}_{G}}{{\left\langle f_{0},f_{0}\right\rangle}_{B(G_{0})}}=\lambda_{1}(G_{0}).

So,
G^{\prime}
is also a tree achieving the minimum of
\lambda_{1}
. Let
G^{\prime\prime}
be the tree by removing all the the Dirichlet boundary edges of
G^{\prime}
and adding a Dirichlet boundary edge to
v
with the weight equal to the total weight of all the removed Dirichlet boundary edges. Then, it is clear that
\lambda_{1}(G^{\prime\prime})=\lambda_{1}(G^{\prime})
. Now, we have reduced to the case that
|B_{D}|=1
and complete the proof of the theorem.
∎

6.
Clump numbers of trees

In this section, we recall the definition of the clump number of a tree in
[
11
]
and introduce some of its properties.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
G
be a finite combinatorial tree and
K(G)
be its geometric representation. For any
p\in|K(G)|
, the connected components
U_{1},U_{2},\cdots,U_{k}
(or their corresponding induced trees
G_{U_{1}},G_{U_{2}},\cdots,G_{U_{k}}
) of
|K(G)|\setminus\{p\}
are called the clumps of
G
with respect to
p
. The clump number of
G
with respect to
p
is defined as

{\rm Clump}(G,p)=\max\{{\rm Length}(U_{1}),{\rm Length}(U_{2}),\cdots,{\rm Length}(U_{k})\}

where
{\rm Length}(U_{i})
is the total length of
U_{i}
. Moreover, the clump number of
G
is defined to be

(6.1)

{\rm Clump}(G)=\inf_{p\in|K(G)|}{\rm Clump}(G,p).

We have the following properties for
{\rm Clump}(G,p)
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
G
be a finite combinatorial tree and
K(G)
be its geometric representation. Then,

(1)

{\rm Clump}(G,p)
is a lower semi-continuous function for
p\in|K(G)|
. Thus there is a point
p\in|K(G)|
such that
{\rm Clump}(G)={\rm Clump}(G,p)
;

(2)

the point
p\in|K(G)|
such that
{\rm Clump}(G)={\rm Clump}(G,p)
is either a vertex or a mid-point of an edge. Thus,
{\rm Clump}(G)
is either an integer or a half-integer.

(3)

if
p\in|K(G)|
is a mid-point of some edge such that
{\rm Clump}(G)={\rm Clump}(G,p)
, then the clumps of
G
with respect to
p
are two clumps of equal total length
\frac{|E(G)|}{2}
;

(4)

there is unique point
p\in|K(G)|
such that
{\rm Clump}(G)={\rm Clump}(G,p)
. We called the point
p
the equilibrium point of
G
.

Proof.

(1) When
p
is not a vertex of
K(G)
, it is clear that
{\rm Clump}(G,p)
is continuous at
p
by definition. When
p
is a vertex of
K(G)
, let
U_{1},U_{2},\cdots,U_{k}
be all the clumps of
p
and suppose that
U_{1}
has the maximal total length among the
k
clumps. Then, for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and
x\in B_{p}(\epsilon)\cap U_{i}
with
i=2,3,\cdots,k
,

(6.2)

{\rm Clump}(G,x)\geq{\rm Clump}(G,p)\geq{\rm Clump}(G,p)-\epsilon

because there is a clump of
x
containing
U_{1}
. Moreover, for any
x\in B_{p}(\epsilon)\cap U_{1}
, it is clear that

(6.3)

{\rm Clump}(G,x)\geq{\rm Clump}(G,p)-\epsilon

since
U_{1}\setminus[px]
is a clump of
x
. So,
{\rm Clump}(G,p)
is a lower semi-continuous function of
p\in|K(G)|
.

(2) Let
p\in|K(G)|
be a point such that
{\rm Clump}(G)={\rm Clump}(G,p)
and
p
is not a vertex of
K(G)
. Suppose that
p
is contained in the edge
\overline{xy}
. Let
U_{x}
and
U_{y}
be the clumps of
p
containing
x
and
y
respectively. Then

(6.4)

{\rm Length}(U_{x})=|[xp]|+n_{x}\ \mbox{and}\ {\rm Length}(U_{y})=|[yp]|+n_{y}

where
n_{x}+1
and
n_{y}+1
are numbers of vertices in
U_{x}
and
U_{y}
respectively.
We claim that
n_{x}=n_{y}
. Otherwise, suppose that
n_{x}>n_{y}
. Let
q
be the mid-point of
[xp]
. Then,

{\rm Clump}(G,q)=n_{x}+|[xq]|=n_{x}+\frac{1}{2}{|[xp]|}<n_{x}+|[xp]|={\rm Clump}(G,p)={\rm Clump}(G)

which is a contradiction. Now, let
z
be the mid-point of the edge
\overline{xy}
. Then,

{\rm Clump}(G)\leq{\rm Clump}(G,z)=n_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\leq{\rm Clump}(G,p)={\rm Clump}(G).

So
p=z
is the the mid-point of the edge
\overline{xy}
.

(3) It has been shown in the proof of (2).

(4) Let
p,q\in|K(G)|
be two different points such that

{\rm Clump}(G,p)={\rm Clump}(G,q)={\rm Clump}(G).

By (2) and (3), we know that
p
and
q
must be simultaneously vertices of
K(G)
or mid-points of some edges of
K(G)
.

For the first case:
p
and
q
are both vertices of
K(G)
, let
U_{1}
be the clump of
p
that contains
q
and
W_{1}
be the clump of
q
that contains
p
. Then,

(6.5)

{\rm Clump}(G)={\rm Clump}(G,p)\geq{\rm Length}(U_{1})

and similarly,

(6.6)

{\rm Clump}(G)={\rm Clump}(G,q)\geq{\rm Length}(W_{1}).

Let
v
be the vertex in
U_{1}
adjacent to
p
and
z
be the mid-point of
\overline{pv}
. Let
U_{p}
and
U_{q}
be the clumps of
z
containing
p
and
q
respectively. Then,
U_{p}\subsetneqq W_{1}
and
U_{q}\subsetneqq U_{1}
. So,

(6.7)

{\rm Length}(U_{p})<{\rm Length}(W_{1})\ \mbox{and}\ {\rm Length}(U_{q})<{\rm Length}(U_{1})

which implies that

(6.8)

{\rm Clump}(G,z)=\max\{{\rm Length}(U_{p}),{\rm Length}(U_{q})\}<{\rm Clump}(G).

This is a contradiction.

For the second case: both
p
and
q
are mid-points of some edges, it is clear that this is impossible by (3).
∎

Minimal Steklov eigenvalues

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
. The following conclusion is crucial in our solution of the extremal problems for Steklov eigenvalues.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
G
be a nontrivial finite combinatorial tree. Then,

(7.1)

\sigma_{2}(G)\geq\Lambda({\rm Clump}(G)).

The equality holds if and only if at least two of the clumps of the equilibrium point of
G
are the minimal brooms
{\rm Br}({\rm Clump}(G))
.

Proof.

Let
p\in|K(G)|
be the equilibrium point of
G
and
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
be an eigenfunction for
\sigma_{2}(G)
. Suppose that
\widetilde{f}(p)\leq 0
. Let
U
be a clump of
p
containing a leaf
v
of
G
such that
f(v)>0
and
N
be the nodal domain of
f
containing
v
. It is clearly that
N\subset U
. By Theorem
4.2
,
g=\widetilde{f}|_{V(G_{N})}
is an eigenfunction for
\lambda_{1}(G_{N})=\sigma_{2}(G)
. Let
\overline{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G_{U})}
be the zero extension of
g
. That is
g(x)=0
for any
x\in V(G_{U})\setminus V(G_{N})
. Then, by Theorem
4.2
and Theorem
5.1
,

(7.2)

\begin{split}\sigma_{2}(G)=\lambda_{1}(G_{N})=&\frac{{\left\langle dg,dg\right\rangle}_{G_{N}}}{{\left\langle g,g\right\rangle}_{B(G_{N})}}\geq\frac{{\left\langle d\overline{g},d\overline{g}\right\rangle}_{G_{U}}}{{\left\langle\overline{g},\overline{g}\right\rangle}_{B(G_{U})}}\\
\geq&\lambda_{1}(G_{U})\geq\Lambda({\rm Length}(U))\geq\Lambda({\rm Clump}(G)),\end{split}

where the first inequality comes from the fact that each edge of
N
is contained in some edge of
U
.

Moreover, if
\sigma_{2}(G)=\Lambda({\rm Clump}(G))
, then
U=N
and
\widetilde{f}(p)=0
. Furthermore, by Theorem
5.1
,
G_{U}
must be a minimal broom
{\rm Br}({\rm Clump}(G))
. Similarly, let
W
be a clump of
p
containing a leaf
w
of
G
such that
f(w)<0
. Then, for the same reason,
G_{W}
must be a minimal broom
{\rm Br}({\rm Clump}(G))
. This completes the necessary part for the equality to hold.

Conversely, if there are two clumps
U,W
of
p
that are minimal brooms
{\rm Br}({\rm Clump}(G))
. Let
f_{U}
be the eigenfunction and
f_{W}
be the eigenfunctions of
G_{U}
and
G_{W}
respectively such that

{\left\langle f_{U},1\right\rangle}_{B(G_{U})}+{\left\langle f_{W},1\right\rangle}_{B(G_{W})}=0.

Let
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
be such that

(7.3)

\psi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{U}(x)&x\in V(G_{U})\\
f_{W}(x)&x\in V(G_{W})\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Then,
\psi
is a Steklov eigenfunction of
G
with eigenvalue
\Lambda({\rm Clump}(G))
by Lemma
2.1
. By the estimate (
7.1
), we know that

(7.4)

\sigma_{2}(G)=\Lambda({\rm Clump}(G)).

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Given a positive integer

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
G

of

{\rm Clump}(G,v)\leq mk.

If there is a clump
T
of
