Abstract
. We study the sub-Finsler prescribed mean curvature equation, associated to a strictly convex body K 0 ⊆ ℝ 2 ​ n K_{0}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n} , for t t -graphs on a bounded domain Ω \Omega in the Heisenberg group ℍ n {\mathbb{H}}^{n} . When the prescribed datum H H is constant and strictly smaller than the Finsler mean curvature of ∂ Ω \partial\Omega , we prove the existence of a Lipschitz solution to the Dirichlet problem for the sub-Finsler CMC equation by means of a Finsler approximation scheme.

1.
Introduction

The aim of this work is to study the prescribed mean curvature equation for
t
-graphs in the Heisenberg group
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
with a sub-Finsler structure. In the Heisenberg group, which can be identified with
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
endowed with a suitable non-Euclidean group law, a sub-Finsler structure is defined by means of an asymmetric left-invariant norm
\|\cdot\|_{K_{0}}
on the horizontal distribution of
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
associated to a convex body
K_{0}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
containing the origin in its interior. Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
be a bounded open set,
H\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
F\in L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
and
u\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
. We consider the functional

(1.1)

\mathcal{I}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla u+F\|_{K_{0},*}\,dxdy+\int_{\Omega}Hu\,dxdy,

where
\|\cdot\|_{K_{0},*}
denotes the dual norm of
\|\cdot\|_{K_{0}}
. In particular, when
F(x,y)=(-y,x)
the first term in (
1.1
) coincides with the sub-Finsler area of the
t
-graph of
u
, see
[
50
,
22
]
. Moreover, if
K_{0}
is the Euclidean unit ball centered at the origin and
H=0
then (
1.1
) boils down to the classical area functional for
t
-graphs in Heisenberg group, see
[
11
,
35
]
and references therein.
We say that the graph of
u
has prescribed
K_{0}
-mean curvature
H
in
\Omega
if
u
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{I}
. Indeed, the Euler-Lagrange equation associated to
\mathcal{I}
out of the singular set
\Omega_{0}
, i.e. the set of points where
\nabla u+F
vanishes, is given by

(1.2)

\divv(\pi_{K_{0}}(\nabla u+F))=H,

where
\pi_{K_{0}}
is a suitable
0
-homogeneous function defined in (
2.5
).
When we fix a boundary datum
\varphi\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
, a solution to the
Dirichlet problem
for the prescribed
K_{0}
-mean curvature equation is a minimizer
u
of
\mathcal{I}
such that
u-\varphi
belongs to the Sobolev space
W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Our main result is Theorem
5.1
, where we prove, under suitable regularity assumptions on the data, that there exists a Lipschitz solution to the Dirichlet problem for the prescribed
K_{0}
-mean curvature equation when
H
is
constant
, it satisfies

(1.3)

|H|<H_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0})

for each
z_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})\in\partial\Omega
and

(1.4)

\left|\int_{\Omega}Hv\,dxdy\right|\leqslant(1-\delta)\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla v\|_{K_{0},*}\,dxdy

for each non-negative function
v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and a suitable
\delta=\delta(K_{0},\Omega,H)\in(0,1]
.
Here
H_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}
denotes the Finsler mean curvature of the boundary
\partial\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. Notice that the mean curvature of the graph of
u
is computed with respect to the downward pointing unit normal and the Finsler mean curvature of
\partial\Omega
is computed with respect to the inner unit normal. The upper bound (
1.3
) of
H
in terms of the Finsler mean curvature of the boundary is the Finsler analogous of the standard assumption for the solution to the Dirichlet problem for the classical mean curvature equation in the Euclidean setting as stated in
[
56
]
,
[
26
]
or
[
25
]
(see also
[
27
, Theorem 16.11]
).
On the other hand, (
1.4
) is a standard sufficient condition for the estimates of the supremum of
|u|
(see
[
25
]
or
[
27
]
). It is worth noting that, in the Euclidean setting (cf. e.g.
[
31
]
), the weaker condition

(1.5)

\left|\int_{\Omega}Hv\,dxdy\right|\leqslant\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla v\|_{K_{0},*}\,dxdy

for each
v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is actually a necessary condition for the existence of a solution to the Euclidean prescribed mean curvature equation. Moreover, the Euclidean version of (
1.5
) suffices to guarantee existence of solutions to the Euclidean prescribed mean curvature equation as long as no boundary conditions are imposed (cf.
[
31
,
38
]
).

Remarkably, as we will show in Section
4
and
6
, there are particular settings in which Theorem
5.1
continues to hold even without imposing (
1.4
), such as the
first sub-Finsler
Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{1}
(cf. Theorem
5.2
) and
any sub-Riemannian
Heisenberg group
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
(cf. Theorem
6.2
).
The Dirichlet problem for constant mean curvature in the first Riemannian Heisenberg group has been studied in
[
1
]
under the same condition on the mean curvature. It is worth mentioning that this is the first time that the existence of Lipschitz solutions to the sub-Finsler Dirichlet problem has been studied when
H\neq 0
, even in the particular case in which
K_{0}
is the unit disk centered at
0
, where the sub-Finsler and the sub-Riemannian frameworks coincide. Indeed, as far as we know, the sub-Riemannian Dirichlet problem has been studied in
[
47
,
12
,
11
,
10
,
19
,
49
]
only in the case of minimal surfaces under the bounded slope condition or the
p
-convexity assumption on
\Omega
, and in
[
44
]
when
H\neq 0
is small enough and in a weaker functional framework. In particular, we point out that when
n=1
our assumption (
1.3
) implies that
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is strictly convex, see Remark
4.9
. It is easy to check that our sub-Finsler functional
\mathcal{I}
for
H=0
satisfies the hypothesis of the area functional considered in
[
19
]
. Thus, assuming the bounded slope condition we directly obtain the existence of Euclidean Lipschitz minimizers for Plateau’s problem. The approach of the present paper, based on the Schauder fixed-point theory, follows the scheme developed in
[
12
]
and extends its results both to the case of prescribed constant mean curvature
H\neq 0
and to the sub-Finsler setting.
In Theorem
5.1
we cannot expect better regularity than Lipschitz. Indeed, even in the sub-Riemannian Heisenberg group
{\mathbb{H}}^{1}
there are
several examples of non-smooth area minimizers. For instance, S.D. Pauls
[
48
]
exhibited a solution of low
regularity for Plateau’s problem with smooth boundary datum,
while in
[
12
,
51
,
32
]

the authors provided solutions to the Bernstein problem in
{\mathbb{H}}^{1}
that are only Euclidean Lipschitz. These examples have been recently generalized to the sub-Finsler setting in
[
28
]
. We refer the interested reader to
[
30
]
for a positive result to the sub-Finsler Bernstein problem for
(X,Y)
-Lipschitz surfaces, which can be seen as a regularity result for global perimeter minimizers.

Since equation (
1.2
) is sub-elliptic degenerate and it is singular next to the singular set, inspired by
[
12
,
47
]
, we first introduce a family of desingularized approximating equations given by

(1.6)

\divv\left(\pi_{K_{0}}(\nabla u+F)\frac{\|\nabla u+F\|^{2}_{\ast}}{\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla u+F\|_{\ast}^{3}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)=H

for each
0<\varepsilon<1
. A similar approximation scheme was considered in the sub-Riemannian setting in
[
6
,
5
]
to study the Lipschitz regularity for non-characteristic minimal surfaces. For a detailed analysis of this approach, we refer to
[
4
]
. This family of equations can be obtained by considering a
(2n+1)
-dimensional convex body
K_{\varepsilon}
containing the origin in its interior, that converges in the Hausdorff sense to the
2n
-dimensional convex body
K_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. The choice of the convex body
K_{\varepsilon}
is not arbitrary. Indeed, we need a specific shape in order to obtain an approximating equation well-defined in the classical sense in the singular set. It is interesting to point out that the Riemannian approximation of
[
12
,
47
,
6
,
5
]
produces an approximation of the unit disk
D\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
by ellipsoids in the sub-Riemannian setting, and this approximation does not work in the greater sub-Finsler generality.
Indeed, if instead of (
1.6
) we were to consider the more natural equation

(1.7)

\divv\left(\pi_{K_{0}}(\nabla u+F)\frac{\|\nabla u+F\|_{*}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+\|\nabla u+F\|^{2}_{*}}}\right)=H,

reminiscent of the Riemannian approximation scheme of
[
12
]
(cf. Remark
2.9
), we would have to require certain assumptions on
K_{0}
for (
1.7
) to be well-defined in the classical sense. We refer to Section
6
for a more careful analysis in this regard. On the other hand, while (
1.6
) is always well-defined, it still tends to degenerate close to the singular set, so that it could fail to be elliptic. Therefore, we need to regularize (
1.6
) by perturbing it with an Euclidean curvature term. More precisely, we consider the family of equations given by

(1.8)

\divv\left(\pi(\nabla u+F)\frac{\|\nabla u+F\|^{2}_{\ast}}{\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla u+F\|_{\ast}^{3}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)+\eta\divv\left(\frac{\nabla u+F}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+F|^{2}}}\right)=H.

for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and any
\eta>0
sufficiently small, whose associated Finsler variational functional is given by

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla u+F\|_{K_{0},*}^{3}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\,dxdy+\eta\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+F|^{2}}\,dxdy+\int_{\Omega}Hu\,dxdy.

A direct computation (cf. Section
4
) will show that (
1.8
) is in fact a classical, quasi-linear second-order elliptic equation.
Therefore, given a boundary datum
\varphi\in C^{2,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
, the solvability of the Dirichlet problem associated to (
1.6
) is reduced by
[
27
, Theorem 13.8]
to
a priori
estimates in
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
of a related family of problems. As usual the
a priori
estimates in
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
consist of three parts: estimates of the supremum of
|u|
, boundary estimates of the gradient of
u
and interior estimates of the gradient of
u
. While the estimates of the supremum rely on assumption (
1.4
), the boundary estimates of the gradient are obtained by a barrier argument that depends on the Finsler distance from the boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Due to technical reasons in the construction of the barriers we need to assume the strict inequality in (
1.3
), avoiding the optimal case when
H
coincides with
H_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0})
at a given point
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
.
We emphasize that these results hold even if the prescribed curvature
H
is non-constant and Lipschitz. The only crucial step where we need
H
to be constant is the maximum principle for the gradient of the solution that allows us to reduce the interior estimates of the gradient to its boundary estimates. Finally, once we realize that
C^{1}
estimates are independent of the approximation parameters
\varepsilon
and
\eta
, passing to the limit as
\varepsilon,\eta\to 0
and using Arzelà-Ascoli Theorem we get the existence of a Lispchitz minimizer for the sub-Finsler Dirichlet problem.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

Unless otherwise specified, we let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
,
n,d\geqslant 1
. Given two open sets
A,B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we write
A\Subset B
whenever
\overline{A}\subseteq B
. We say that a set
K
is a convex body if it is convex, compact and has non-empty interior. We say that a convex body
K
is (in)
C^{k,\alpha}_{+}
, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
, if
\partial K
is of class
C^{k,\alpha}
with strictly positive principal curvatures.

2.2.
Minkowski norms

We follow the approach developed in
[
50
,
55
]
.
We say that
\|\cdot\|:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,+\infty)
is a
norm
if it verifies:

1.

\|v\|=0\Leftrightarrow v=0
,

2.

\|sv\|=s\|v\|
for any
s>0
,

3.

\|v+u\|\leqslant\|v\|+\|u\|

for any
u,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We stress the fact that we are not assuming the symmetry property
\|-v\|=\|v\|
. It is well known that any norm is equivalent to the Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
, that is, given a norm
\|\cdot\|
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
there exist constants
0<c<C
such that

(2.1)

c|\cdot|\leqslant\|\cdot\|\leqslant C|\cdot|.

Associated to a given a norm
\|\cdot\|
we have the set
F=\{u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:||u||\leqslant 1\}
, which, thanks to (
2.1
) and the properties of
\|\cdot\|
, is compact, convex and includes
0
in its interior. Reciprocally, given a convex body
K
with
0\in\intt(K)
, the function

||u||_{K}=\inf\{\lambda\geqslant 0:u\in\lambda K\}

defines a norm so that
K=\{u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:||u||_{K}\leqslant 1\}
. In the following we let

B_{K}(v,r):=\{w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,:\,\|w-v\|_{K}\leqslant r\}

for any
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
r>0
.
It is easy to check that

\|v\|_{K}=\|-v\|_{-K}
for any
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, so that

(2.2)

B_{-K}(v,r):=\{w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,:\,\|v-w\|_{K}\leqslant r\}

for any
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
r>0
.
Given a norm
\|\cdot\|
and a scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we consider the dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
of
\|\cdot\|
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, defined by

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{*}=\sup_{\|v\|\leqslant 1}\langle u,v\rangle.

The dual norm is the support function of the unit ball
F
with respect to the scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Moreover, thanks to the above definitions the following Cauchy-Schwarz formula holds:

(2.4)

\langle u,v\rangle\leqslant\|u\|_{*}\|v\|

for any
u,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
If in addition we assume
K
to be strictly convex and
u\neq 0
, then the compactness and strict convexity of
K
guarantee the existence of a unique vector
\pi_{K}(u)
in
\partial K
where the supremum in (
2.3
) is attained, i.e.

(2.5)

\|u\|_{K,*}=\langle u,\pi_{K}(u)\rangle.

It is easy to see that
\pi_{K}
is a positively
0
-homogeneous map, i.e.
\pi_{K}(\lambda u)=\pi_{K}(u)
for any
\lambda>0
and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, and that
\|\pi_{K}(u)\|_{K}=1
for any
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
. Moreover, if we assume that
K
is
C^{2}_{+}
, then
\pi_{K}|_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\longrightarrow\partial K
is a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism whose inverse is the Gauss map
\mathcal{N}_{K}
of
\partial K
with respect to the outer unit normal. In particular,

(2.6)

D\pi_{K}(u)\text{ is positive definite}

for any
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
.
Furthermore, we have that the norms
\|\cdot\|_{K}
and
\|\cdot\|_{K,*}
belong to
C^{k,\alpha}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\})
if and only if
\partial K
is
C^{k,\alpha}
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 1
. For further details see
[
55
, Section 2.5]
. The relation between the dual norm and the map
\pi_{K}
is given by

(2.7)

\nabla\|u\|_{K,\ast}=\pi_{K}(u).

Indeed, for any
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}

\nabla\|u\|_{K,\ast}=\nabla\langle u,\pi_{K}(u)\rangle=\pi_{K}(u)+u\cdot D\pi_{K}(u)=\pi_{K}(u),

where the last equality follows from the fact that
0
-homogeneous functions are radial.

2.3.
Finsler geometry of hypersurfaces in the Euclidean space

Let
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a convex body in
C^{2}_{+}
,
0\in\intt K
and
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded domain with boundary
\partial\Omega=\Sigma
of class
C^{2}
. Let
N
be the inner unit normal to
\Sigma
. Then the derivative map
(W_{K,\Sigma})_{p}=-d_{p}(\pi_{K}\circ N):T_{p}\Sigma\to T_{\pi_{K}(N(p))}\partial K
, being
\pi_{K}
as in (
2.5
), is called the
K
-Weingarten map
. Let
\gamma\subseteq\partial K
be a differentiable curve with
\gamma(0)=\pi_{K}(N(p))
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)\in T_{\pi_{K}(N(p))}\partial K
. By definition of
\pi_{K}
, the function

f(t)=\langle\gamma(t),N(p)\rangle

has a maximum at
0
and therefore
\langle\gamma^{\prime}(0),N(p)\rangle=f^{\prime}(0)=0
, which gives
T_{\pi_{K}(N(p))}\partial K=T_{N(p)}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Moreover it is well known that
(dN)_{q}

is an endomorphism of
T_{q}\Sigma
and therefore
(W_{K,\Sigma})_{p}
is an endomorphism of
T_{p}\Sigma
. We define the
K
-mean curvature
of
\Sigma
as

H_{K,\Sigma}=\text{Trace}(W_{K,\Sigma})=-\divv_{\Sigma}(\pi_{K}\circ N),

where
\divv_{\Sigma}
is the divergence in the tangent directions to
\Sigma
.
We remark that
W_{K,\Sigma}
is neither necessarily self-adjoint nor symmetric. Let us check that
W_{K,\Sigma}
is anyway diagonalizable. Indeed, given a parametrization
X
of
\Sigma
,
dN
has a symmetric matrix representation
S
in the basis
B=\{\partial_{x_{1}}X,\ldots,\partial_{x_{d-1}}X\}
. On the other hand,
\pi_{K}=\mathcal{N}_{K}^{-1}
and, since
K
is in
C^{2}_{+}
, the matrix
A
which represents
d(\mathcal{N}_{K}^{-1})
with respect to
B
is positive definite. Therefore, there exists an invertible matrix
P
such that
A=P^{t}P
. Notice that the matrices
P^{t}PS
and
PSP^{t}
have the same spectrum, and equal to the spectrum of
W_{K,\Sigma}
. Since
S
is symmetric we can apply Sylvester’s criterion
to obtain that all the eigenvalues of
PSP^{t}
are real. The eigenvalues of
W_{K,\Sigma}
are called
K
-principal curvatures
and the eigenvectors of
W_{K,\Sigma}
are called
K
-principal directions
.

2.3.1.
Finsler distance from the boundary and the Eikonal equation

In this and the following section we want to rely on some results by
[
40
,
39
]
, and so we assume that
K
is in
C^{\infty}_{+}
, i.e.
\partial K
is of class
C^{\infty}
with strictly positive principal curvatures. Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded domain with boundary
\partial\Omega=\Sigma
of class
C^{2,\alpha}
, for
0<\alpha\leqslant 1
, and inner unit normal
N
. We shall adapt Theorem 4.26 in
[
43
]
and the remarks at the end of Section 4.5 in
[
43
]
to prove existence of a tubular neighborhood of
\Sigma
and compute the
K
-mean curvature of parallel hypersurfaces. The
interior signed
K
-distance
to
\Sigma
is the function
d_{K,\Sigma}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
given by

d_{K,\Sigma}(p)=\begin{cases}\min\{\|p-q\|_{K}:q\in\Sigma\}&\text{ if }p\in\Omega\\
-\min\{\|p-q\|_{K}:q\in\Sigma\}&\text{ if }p\notin\Omega.\end{cases}

Consider the map
F:\Sigma\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
given by

F(q,t)=q+t(\pi_{K}\circ N)(q).

For any
v\in T_{q}\Sigma
, we have
(dF)_{(q,t)}(v,0)=v+td(\pi_{K}\circ N)(v)
and
(dF)_{(q,t)}(0,1)=(\pi_{K}\circ N)(q)
. Since
K
contains the origin,

\langle\pi_{K}(N),N\rangle>0

and
dF
is invertible at
t=0
. Thus
F
is locally a diffeomorphism and, being
\Sigma
a compact hypersurface,
F
is a diffeomorphism in a domain
\Sigma\times(-\delta,\delta)
. The set
F(\Sigma\times(-\delta,\delta))
is called a
tubular neighborhood
of
\Sigma
. Notice that if
p=F(q,t)
, then

(2.8)

p-q=t(\pi_{K}\circ N)(q)

and, taking the
K
-norm, we obtain that
d_{K,\Sigma}(p)=t
. We know (cf.
[
40
]
) that, under our assumptions, there exists
\bar{\delta}>0
such that

d_{K,\Sigma}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{F(\Sigma\times(-\delta,\delta))}).

for any
\delta<\bar{\delta}
.
Given
|t|<\delta
, we let

(2.9)

\Sigma_{t}=\{p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:p=F(q,t)\text{ for some }q\in\partial\Sigma\}.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded domain with boundary
\partial\Omega=\Sigma
of class
C^{2}
and let
F(\Sigma\times(-\delta,\delta))
be a tubular neighborhood of
\Sigma
. The
K
-mean curvature of
\Sigma_{t}
at
p\in\Sigma_{t}
is given by

(2.10)

H_{K,\Sigma_{t}}(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\frac{\kappa_{i}(q)}{1-t\kappa_{i}(q)},

where
q\in\Sigma
satisfies
p=F(q,t)
and
\kappa_{1}(q),\ldots,\kappa_{d-1}(q)
are the
K
-principal curvatures of
\Sigma
at
q
.

Proof.

Let
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d-1}\}
be a basis of
K
-principal directions of
\Sigma
. Then
(dF)_{(q,t)}(e_{i},0)=(1-t\kappa_{i})e_{i}
. Therefore a basis of principal directions in
\Sigma_{t}
is
\{\frac{e_{1}}{1-t\kappa_{1}},\ldots,\frac{e_{d-1}}{1-t\kappa_{d-1}}\}
. Since we have

-d(\pi_{K}\circ N)_{q}\left(\frac{e_{i}}{1-t\kappa_{i}}\right)=\frac{\kappa_{i}}{1-t\kappa_{i}}e_{i}

for each
i=1,\ldots,d-1
we get the conclusion.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

From (
2.10
), we obtain that the
K
-mean curvature is increasing in
t
. In particular, given
q\in\Sigma
and
p=F(q,t)
for
t>0
, it holds that

(2.11)

H_{K,\Sigma_{t}}(p)\geqslant H_{K,\Sigma}(q).

The following Eikonal equation can be deduced using classical arguments. We include the proof for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 2.3
.

It holds that

(2.12)

\|\nabla d_{K,\Sigma}(p)\|_{K,*}=1

for any
p
where
d_{K_{,}\partial\Omega}
is differentiable.

Proof.

It is clear that, for any
p,p^{\prime}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have

d_{K,\Sigma}(p^{\prime})\leqslant\|p^{\prime}-p\|_{K}+d_{K,\Sigma}(p).

Taking
p^{\prime}=p+tv
where
t>0
, we get

d_{K,\Sigma}(p+tv)-d_{K,\Sigma}(p)\leqslant\|tv\|_{K}.

Therefore,

(2.13)

\langle v,\nabla d_{K,\Sigma}(p)\rangle\leqslant\|v\|_{K}.

Taking
v=\pi_{K}(\nabla d_{K,\Sigma}(p))
in (
2.13
), we obtain

\|\nabla d_{K,\Sigma}(p)\|_{K,*}\leqslant 1.

On the other hand, let
\gamma(t)=F(q_{0},t)
. By (
2.8
) we have that

d_{K,\Sigma}(\gamma(t))=t.

Taking derivatives in the previous equation, we obtain

\langle\gamma^{\prime}(t),\nabla d_{K,\Sigma}(\gamma(t))\rangle=1.

Since
\gamma^{\prime}(t)=(\pi_{K}\circ N)(q_{0})
, we get that
\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|_{K}=1
. Using (
2.4
), we get

\|\nabla d_{K,\Sigma}(\gamma(t))\|_{K,*}\geqslant 1.\qed

Given a tubular neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
\partial\Omega
and
p=F(q,t)\in\Omega
, we denote by
N_{t}(p)
the inner unit normal to
\Sigma_{t}
at
p
.
Let us explicitly compute
\divv(\pi_{K}\circ N_{t})(p)
. Let us recall that, to the
0
-homogeneity of
\pi_{K}
, we get that

q\cdot D\pi_{K}(q)=0

for any
q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In particular, taking
q=N_{t}
, we obtain

N_{t}\cdot D(\pi_{K}\circ N_{t})=N_{t}\cdot D\pi_{K}(N_{t})\cdot DN_{t}=0,

which implies that

(2.14)

-\divv(\pi_{K}\circ N_{t})(p)=-\divv_{\Sigma}(\pi_{K}\circ N_{t})(p)=H_{K,\Sigma_{t}}(p)\geqslant H_{K,\partial\Omega}(q).

With the next result, we better understand the relationship between the Finsler mean curvature of
\Sigma
, the Euclidean curvature of
\Sigma
and the Euclidean principal curvatures of
K
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
K
be a convex body in
C^{2}_{+}
,
0\in\intt K
. Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded domain with
\partial\Omega=\Sigma
of class
C^{2}
and let
N_{q}
be the inner unit normal to
\Sigma
at
q
. Then we have

(2.15)

H_{K,\Sigma}(q)=-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\dfrac{\langle D_{e_{i}}N_{q},e_{i}\rangle}{k_{i}^{K}(\pi_{K}(N_{q}))}

where
k_{i}^{K}
are the Euclidean principal curvatures of
\partial K
and
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d-1}
is an orthonormal basis of Euclidean principal directions of
\partial K
.

Proof.

We shall drop the subscript for
\pi_{K}
. Let
q
in
\Sigma
and
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d-1}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}=T_{\pi(N_{q})}\partial K
such that

(d\mathcal{N}_{K})_{\pi(N_{q})}e_{i}=k_{i}^{K}(\pi(N_{q}))e_{i}.

By hypothesis,
k_{i}^{K}>0

for
i=1,\ldots,d-1
. Here
\mathcal{N}_{K}
denotes the Gauss map of
\partial K
. Then we have

\displaystyle H_{K,\Sigma}(q)=-\divv_{\Sigma}(\pi(N_{q}))=-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\langle D_{e_{i}}\pi(N_{q}),e_{i}\rangle,

where
D
is the Levi-Civita connection in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We claim that
D_{e_{i}}\pi(N_{q})=d\pi(D_{e_{i}}N_{q})
. Indeed, let
\gamma:(\epsilon,\epsilon)\to\Sigma
such that
\gamma(0)=q
and
\dot{\gamma}(0)=e_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,d-1
. Then we have

\displaystyle D_{e_{i}}\pi(N_{q})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{D}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\pi(N_{\gamma(s)})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\dfrac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\pi_{j}(N_{\gamma(s)})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\nabla\pi_{j}(N_{q})\dfrac{D}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}N_{\gamma(s)}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}=(d\pi)_{N_{q}}D_{e_{i}}N_{q}.

Moreover, since
d\pi
is a symmetric matrix we gain

(2.16)

H_{K,\Sigma}(q)=-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\langle(d\pi)_{N_{q}}D_{e_{i}}N_{q},e_{i}\rangle=-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\langle D_{e_{i}}N_{q},(d\pi)_{N_{q}}e_{i}\rangle.

Since
\pi=\mathcal{N}_{K}^{-1}
we obtain
d\pi=(d\mathcal{N}_{K})^{-1}
and

e_{i}=d\mathcal{N}_{K}^{-1}d\mathcal{N}_{K}(e_{i})=d\mathcal{N}_{K}^{-1}(k_{i}^{K}(\pi(N_{q}))e_{i})=k_{i}^{K}(\pi(N_{q}))d\pi(e_{i}),

by linearity. Therefore, we have
d\pi(e_{i})=(k_{i}^{K}(\pi(N_{q})))^{-1}e_{i}
. Hence, plugging this last equality in (
2.16
) we gain (
2.15
).
∎

2.3.2.
The Ridge of the Finsler distance

In the previous section we obtained some regularity and geometric properties of
d_{K_{,}\partial\Omega}
in a tubular neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
. We shall see that some of these properties persist outside a tubular neighborhood.
We fix a convex body
K\in C^{\infty}_{+}
and a bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
C^{2,1}
boundary.
For any
p\in\Omega
, we let
D(p):=\{q\in\partial\Omega\,:\,d_{K_{,}\partial\Omega}(p)=\|p-q\|_{K}\}
.
Since
d_{K_{,}\partial\Omega}
is continuous, then clearly
D(p)\neq\emptyset
for any
p\in\Omega
. Accordingly, we define the set

(2.17)

\Omega_{1}:=\{p\in\Omega\,:\,D(p)\text{ is a singleton}\},

and we define the
Ridge
of
\Omega
by

R:=\Omega\setminus\intt{\Omega_{1}}.

We know, again thanks to
[
40
]
, that, under our assumptions on
K
and
\Omega
,

(2.18)

d_{K_{,}\partial\Omega}\in C^{2,1}(\intt{\Omega_{1}}\cup\partial\Omega).

Moreover, in
[
39
, Corollary 1.6]
it is proven that the Hausdorff dimension of
R
is at most
d-1
. This fact implies that
R
has empty interior, so that

(2.19)

\partial(\intt{\Omega_{1}})=\partial\Omega\cup R.

The following result is inspired partially by
[
20
, Lemma 3.4]
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
p\in\Omega
, let
q\in D(p)

and let

(p,q):=\{tp+(1-t)q\,:\,t\in(0,1)\}.

Then
(p,q)\subseteq\intt{\Omega_{1}}
and

(2.20)

D(\gamma)=\{q\}

for any
\gamma\in(p,q)
.

Proof.

Let
p,q
be as in the statement, and fix
\gamma\in(p,q)
. We already know that
D(\gamma)\neq\emptyset
. On the other hand, assume that there exists
q^{\prime}\neq q
such that
q^{\prime}\in D(\gamma)
.
Let us notice that
p,q,q^{\prime}
cannot lie on the same line. Indeed, if by contradiction this was the case, then the only possibility is that
p
is a convex combination of
\gamma
and
q^{\prime}
. But then the strict convexity of
K
would imply that

\|\gamma-q^{\prime}\|_{K}\leqslant\|\gamma-q\|_{K}<\|p-q\|_{K}\leqslant\|p-q^{\prime}\|_{K}<\|\gamma-q^{\prime}\|_{K},

which is absurd.
This in particular implies that
p,\gamma,q^{\prime}
do not lie on the same line. Therefore, thanks again to the strict convexity of
K
, we get that

\|p-q^{\prime}\|_{K}<\|p-\gamma\|_{K}+\|\gamma-q^{\prime}\|_{K}\leqslant\|p-\gamma\|_{K}+\|\gamma-q\|_{K}=\|p-q\|_{K},

a contradiction to
q\in D(p)
. Hence (
2.20
) is proved. Assume by contradiction that
\gamma\in R
. By Corollary 4.11 in
[
39
]
, any point of the form
q+\lambda(\gamma-q)
with
\lambda>1
has a point in
\partial\Omega
closer than
q
. On the other hand, taking
w
the midpoint of
p
and
\gamma
, then by (
2.20
) it holds that
D(w)=\{q\}
, which is impossible.
∎

Let us take a point
p\in\intt{\Omega_{1}}
, and let
q\in D(p)
. Thanks to Proposition
2.5
, we know that

d_{K_{,}\partial\Omega}(z)=\|z-q\|_{K}

for any
z
in
(p,q)
. Recalling that
(p,q)\subseteq\intt{\Omega_{1}}
, together with (
2.18
), and Proposition
2.3
it is easy to see that

\nabla d_{K_{,}\partial\Omega}(z)\neq 0
. Thus, at least locally, the level set
\Sigma_{d_{K},\partial\Omega}(p)
is a well-defined
C^{2}
hypersurface. Reasoning as in Section
2.3.1
we conclude that

(2.21)

-\divv(\pi_{K}\circ N_{d_{K},\partial\Omega})(p)\geqslant H_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(q)

for any
p\in\intt{\Omega_{1}}
, where
q\in D(p)
.

2.4.
The Heisenberg group

Let
n\geqslant 1
. We denote by
\mathbb{H}^{n}
the Heisenberg group, defined as the
(2n+1)
-dimensional Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
endowed with the non-abelian group law
*
given by

(2.22)

(x,y,t)*(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{t})=\left(x+\bar{x},y+\bar{y},t+\bar{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\bar{x}_{i}y_{i}-x_{i}\bar{y}_{i}\right)\right),

where
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
\bar{x}=(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n})
,
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
and
\bar{y}=(\bar{y}_{1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{n})
.
A basis of left-invariant vector fields is given by

X_{i}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+y_{i}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t},\qquad Y_{i}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}-x_{i}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t},\qquad T=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t},

for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
For
p=(x,y,t)\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, the left translation by
p
is the diffeomorphism
L_{p}(q)=p*q
.
We denote by
\mathcal{H}
the horizontal distribution whose fiber at
p\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
is the
2n
-dimensional space

\mathcal{H}_{p}=\mathrm{span}\{X_{i}(p),\ Y_{i}(p)\ |\ i=1,\ldots,n\}.

From now on we will always identify
\mathcal{H}_{0}
with
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
.
We shall consider on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
the left-invariant Riemannian metric
g=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, so that the vector fields
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n},T\}
form an orthonormal basis at every point, and we let
D
be the Levi-Civita connection associated to the Riemannian metric
g
.
The Riemannian volume of a set
E
is, up to a constant, the Haar measure of the group and can be identified with the
(2n+1)-
dimensional Lebesgue measure. We denote it by
|E|
. The integral of a function
f
with respect to the Riemannian measure is denoted by
\int f\,d\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

2.5.
Sub-Finsler norms and perimeter

Let
K_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{0}\equiv{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
be a convex body in
C^{2}_{+}
(cf. Subsection
2.1
),
0\in\intt K_{0}
and let
\|\cdot\|_{K_{0}}
be the associated norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. In the following we shall write
\|\cdot\|,\|\cdot\|_{*}
and
\pi
instead of
\|\cdot\|_{K_{0}},\|\cdot\|_{K_{0},*}
and
\pi_{K_{0}}
respectively. For any
p\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, we define a left-invariant norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
on
\mathcal{H}_{p}
by means of the equality

\|v\|_{p}=\|dL_{p}^{-1}(v)\|\quad v\in\mathcal{H}_{p}

where
dL_{p}
denotes the differential of
L_{p}
.
In particular, for a horizontal vector field
\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}X_{i}+g_{i}Y_{i}
its norm at a point
p\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
is given by

\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(p)X_{i}(0)+g_{i}(p)Y_{i}(0)\Big\|=\|(f(p),g(p))\|,

where
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
and
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})
. Similarly, we extend the dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
and the projection
\pi
to each fiber of the horizontal bundle.
When
\|\cdot\|
is
C^{l}
with
l\geqslant 2
, all norms
\|\cdot\|_{p}
are
C^{l}
. Given a horizontal vector field
U
of class
C^{1}
, we define
\pi(U)
as the
C^{1}
horizontal vector field satisfying

\|U\|_{*}=\langle U,\pi(U)\rangle.

Proceeding as in § 2.3 of
[
50
]
, it is easy to see that the projection satisfies

\pi\left(\sum_{i}^{n}f_{i}X_{i}+g_{i}Y_{i}\right)=\mathcal{N}_{K_{0}}^{-1}\bigg(\frac{(f,g)}{\sqrt{|f|^{2}+|g|^{2}}}\bigg),

where
|f|^{2}=\langle f,f\rangle
.

Definition 2.6
.

Given a measurable set
E\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
we say that
E
has finite horizontal
K_{0}
-perimeter if

P_{K_{0},\mathcal{H}}(E)=\sup\left\{\int_{E}\divv(U)\ d{\mathbb{H}}^{n},U\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{H}}^{n}),\|U\|_{K_{0},\infty}\leqslant 1\right\}<+\infty,

where
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{H}}^{n})
is the space of
C^{1}
horizontal compactly supported vector fields in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
and
\|U\|_{K_{0},\infty}=\sup_{p\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}}\|U_{p}\|_{p}
.

Remark 2.7
.

The perimeter associated to the Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
is the sub-Riemannian perimeter as it is defined in
[
23
,
24
]
. A set has finite perimeter for a given norm if and only if it has finite perimeter for the standard sub-Riemannian perimeter. Hence all known results in the standard case apply to the sub-Finsler perimeter.
Moreover, if
E
has
C^{1}
boundary
\partial E
, then

P_{K_{0},\mathcal{H}}(E)=\int_{\partial E}\|N_{h}\|_{*}d\sigma=:A_{K_{0},\mathcal{H}}(\partial E),

where
N_{h}
is the projection on the horizontal distribution
\mathcal{H}
of the Riemannian normal
N
with respect to the metric
g
and
d\sigma
is the Riemannian measure of
\partial E
. For more details see § 2.4 in
[
50
]
when
n=1
.

As a significant example, we consider a bounded open set
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
and a
C^{1}
function
u:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
\text{Gr}(u)=\{(x,y,t)\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\,:\,u(x,y)-t=0\}
be the graph of
u
. Then we have

N_{h}=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(u_{x_{i}}-y)X_{i}+(u_{y_{i}}+x)Y_{i}}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+F|^{2}}}\quad\text{and}\quad d\sigma=\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+F|^{2}}\,dxdy,

where
\nabla u(x,y)
is the Euclidean gradient of
u(x,y)
and
F(x,y)=(-y,x)
.
Therefore we get

A_{K_{0},\mathcal{H}}(\text{Gr}(u))=\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla u+F\|_{*}\,dxdy.

2.6.
The Sub-Finsler prescribed mean curvature equation

Inspired by the previous computation and the sub-Riemannian problem studied by
[
12
]
we consider the following problem. Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
be a bounded open set and let
F\in L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
,
\varphi\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
and
H\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Then we set

(2.23)

\mathcal{I}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla u+F\|_{*}\,dxdy+\int_{\Omega}Hu\,dxdy

for each
u\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
such that
u-\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)
.
We say that
u\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
is a
minimizer
for
\mathcal{I}
if

\mathcal{I}(u)\leqslant\mathcal{I}(v)

for all
v\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
such that
v-\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)
.
In
[
12
, Section 3]
the authors investigate the first variation of the functional
\mathcal{I}
when
\|\cdot\|_{K_{0},*}
is the Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
, taking into account the bad beaviour of the singular set

(2.24)

\Omega_{0}=\{(x,y)\in\Omega\ :\ (\nabla u+F)(x,y)=0\}.

In the next result we derive the Euler-Lagrange equation associated to
\mathcal{I}
for
C^{2}
minimizers.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
K_{0}
be a
C_{+}^{2}
convex body such that
0\in\intt(K_{0})
. Let
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
be a minimizer for
\mathcal{I}
defined in (
2.23
). Assume that
F\in C^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
. Let
\Omega_{0}
be the singular set defined in (
2.24
). Then
u
satisfies

(2.25)

\displaystyle\divv(\pi(\nabla u+F))=H\qquad\text{ in $\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}$.}

Proof.

Given
v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0})
, by
[
50
, Lemma 3.2]
the first variation is given by

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\mathcal{I}(u+sv)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}}\dfrac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\|\nabla(u+sv)+F\|_{*}\ dxdy+\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}}Hv\ dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}}\dfrac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\|\nabla u+F+s\nabla v\|_{*}\ dxdy+\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}}Hv\ dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}}\langle\nabla v,\pi(\nabla u+F))\rangle\ dxdy+\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}}Hv\ dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}}v\,\left(H-\divv(\pi(\nabla u+F))\right)\ dxdy.\qed

Remark 2.9
.

When
K_{0}
is the unit disk
D_{0}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
centered at
0
of radius
1
we have

\pi_{D_{0}}(\nabla u+F)=\dfrac{\nabla u+F}{|\nabla u+F|}

and (
2.25
) is equivalent to

\divv\left(\frac{\nabla u+F}{|\nabla u+F|}\right)=H.

3.
The Finsler approximation problem

In this section we develop the Finsler approximation scheme in order to get rid of the singular nature of equation (
2.25
). To this aim, given
K_{0}
a convex body in
C^{2}_{+}
such that
0\in\intt{K_{0}}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we
denote by
K_{\varepsilon}
the set

(3.1)

K_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{(x,y,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}:\left(\frac{|t|}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}+\|(x,y)\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\leqslant 1\right\}.

Notice that
K_{\varepsilon}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}\equiv T_{0}{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
(here
T_{0}{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
denotes the tangent space of
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
at
p=0
) is a strictly convex body with
0\in\intt(K_{\varepsilon})
. Moreover
\partial K_{\varepsilon}
is of class
C^{1}
. Indeed it is a level set of the
C^{1}
function

g_{\varepsilon}(x,y,t):=\left(\frac{|t|}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}+\|(x,y)\|^{\frac{3}{2}},

whose gradient never vanishes on
\partial K_{\varepsilon}
. Hence, the projection
\pi_{K_{\varepsilon}}
is well-defined and continuous. We shall write
\|\cdot\|_{\varepsilon}
,
\|\cdot\|_{\varepsilon,*}
and
\pi_{\varepsilon}
instead of
\|\cdot\|_{K_{\varepsilon}}
,
\|\cdot\|_{K_{\varepsilon},*}
and
\pi_{K_{\varepsilon}}
respectively. The map
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}
is defined as the first
2n
components of
\pi_{\varepsilon}
. By abuse of notation, we write
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(x,y)=\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(x,y,-1)
when there is no confusion.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
K_{0}
be a convex body in
C^{2}_{+}
such that

, and
let
K_{\varepsilon}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
be the set defined in (

(i)

The map

\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(x,y)=\pi(x,y)\frac{\|(x,y)\|^{2}_{\ast}}{\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|(x,y)\|_{\ast}^{3}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}.

(ii)

The map
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}
can be extended to a

by setting

(iii)

\|(x,y,-1)\|_{K_{\varepsilon},*}=\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|(x,y)\|_{\ast}^{3}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}

Proof.

Let us prove that

(3.2)

\pi_{\varepsilon}(x,y,-1)=(\alpha\pi(x,y),-\varepsilon(1-\alpha^{3/2}))^{2/3}

for some

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\setminus\{0\}

the
(2n+1)
-th coordinate of
\pi_{\varepsilon}(x,y,-1)
and we let

be the convex set defined by

K_{t_{0}}:=\{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\ :\ (x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t_{0})\in K_{\varepsilon}\}.

Then we have

\begin{split}K_{t_{0}}\times\{t_{0}\}=\Bigl\{\Big(\frac{|t_{0}|}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{\frac{3}{2}}+\|(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\leqslant 1\Bigr\}=\left\{\|(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\|\leqslant\left(1-\left(\frac{|t_{0}|}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\right\}.\end{split}

Hence it follows that
\pi_{t_{0}}=(1-(\frac{|t_{0}|}{\varepsilon})^{\frac{3}{2}})^{\frac{2}{3}}\pi

\pi_{\varepsilon}
is the inverse of the Gauss map, we can see that

is normal to
\partial K_{\varepsilon}
at
\pi_{\varepsilon}(x,y,-1)
and so
(x,y)
is normal to

satisfies
\|\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(x,y)\|^{\frac{3}{2}}+(\frac{|t_{0}|}{\varepsilon})^{\frac{3}{2}}=1
. Since

is strictly convex, the projection is unique and
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(x,y)=\pi_{t_{0}}(x,y)
.
Hence (
3.2
) follows.
Taking the scalar product of
(x,y,-1)
with the curve

Notice that

\partial K_{\varepsilon}
and
\beta(\alpha)
is
\pi_{\varepsilon}

with an element of

is attained. Thus we can take derivatives in

and get

0=\|(x,y)\|_{\ast}-\varepsilon\frac{\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(1-\alpha^{3/2})^{\frac{1}{3}}}.

Then we obtain

\alpha=\frac{\|(x,y)\|_{\ast}^{2}}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|(x,y)\|_{\ast}^{3})^{2/3}}

and we get
(i)
. Since
\|(x,y,-1)\|_{K_{\varepsilon},*}=\langle(x,y,-1),\pi_{\varepsilon}(x,y,-1)\rangle

(iii)
.
Finally,
(ii)
follows from

and the

-homogeneity of the map
\pi(\cdot)\|\cdot\|^{2}_{*}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

and

(3.3)

\dfrac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\|u+sv\|_{\varepsilon,*}=\langle v,\pi_{\varepsilon}(u)\rangle.

Proof.

Let

and

∎

3.1.
The Finsler prescribed mean curvature equation

We are ready to derive the Finsler prescribed mean curvature equation, essentially in the same way as in the previous section. To this aim, let

\Omega\subseteq\{t=0\}
be a bounded open set and
u:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
C^{2}
function. Then we have

N=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(u_{x_{i}}-y)X_{i}+(u_{y_{i}}+x)Y_{i}-T}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+F|^{2}}}\quad\text{and}\quad d\sigma=\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+F|^{2}}\,dxdy,

where
F(x,y)=(-y,x).

Therefore we get

A_{K_{\varepsilon}}(\text{Gr}(u))=\int_{\Omega}\|(\nabla u+F,-1)\|_{\varepsilon,*}\,dxdy.

Hence, inspired by this computation and thanks to Proposition
3.1
, given
F\in L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
,
\varphi\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
and
H\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we define the approximating Finsler functional
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
by

(3.4)

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|(\nabla u+F)\|_{\ast}^{3}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\,dxdy+\int_{\Omega}Hu\,dxdy,

for any
u\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
such that
u-\varphi\in W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Arguing as in the previous section, and thanks to Lemma
3.2
, we are able to deduce the Euler-Lagrange equation associated to (
3.4
). Indeed, given
v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
, by Lemma
3.2
, the first variation is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(u+sv)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\|(\nabla(u+sv)+F,-1)\|_{\varepsilon,*}\ dxdy+\int_{\Omega}Hv\,dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\|(\nabla u+F,-1)+s(\nabla v,0)\|_{\varepsilon,*}\ dxdy+\int_{\Omega}Hv\,dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle(\nabla v,0),\pi_{\varepsilon}((\nabla u+F,-1))\rangle\ dxdy+\int_{\Omega}Hv\,dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla v,\pi^{h}_{\varepsilon}(\nabla u+F)\rangle\ dxdy+\int_{\Omega}Hv\,dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}v(H-\divv(\pi^{h}_{\varepsilon}(\nabla u+F)))\,dxdy.

Then the Finsler prescribed mean curvature equation for the graph of
u
is given by

(3.5)

\divv(\pi^{h}_{\varepsilon}(\nabla u+F))=H\quad\mbox{in}\ \Omega.

As already pointed out in the introduction, (
3.5
) is only degenerate elliptic in the singular set (cf. the computations of Section
4
). Therefore, in the next section, we will perturb (
3.5
) as in (
1.8
) in order to apply the aforementioned classical Schauder fixed-point theory for elliptic equations.

4.
A priori estimates for the Finsler Prescribed Mean Curvature Equation

In this section we want to find classical solutions to the regularized Finsler approximating Dirichlet problem associated to (
1.8
), that is

(4.1)

\begin{cases}\divv\left(\pi^{h}_{\varepsilon}(\nabla u+F)\right)+\eta\divv\left(\frac{\nabla u+F}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+F|^{2}}}\right)=H&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=\varphi&\text{ in }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\varepsilon,\eta\in(0,1)
,
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
is a bounded domain with
C^{2,\alpha}
boundary for
0<\alpha<1
,
K_{0}
is a convex body in
C^{2,\alpha}_{+}
with
0\in\intt{K_{0}}
,
H\in Lip(\overline{\Omega})
,
F=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{2n})\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
and
\varphi\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
. To this aim, let us fix some notation. It is easy to see that the map
G:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
defined by
G(p)=\pi(p)\|p\|_{*}^{2}
can be extended to a
2
-homogeneous and
C^{1}
map setting
G(0)=0
. Moreover, for any
i=1,\ldots,2n

D_{i}(\|\cdot\|_{*}^{3})=3G_{i}(\cdot),

where
G=(G_{1},\ldots,G_{2n})
.
Thanks to Proposition
3.1
, we can write the first equation of (
4.1
) in the form

(4.2)

\divv\left(\pi(\nabla u+F)\frac{\|\nabla u+F\|^{2}_{\ast}}{\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla u+F\|_{\ast}^{3}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)+\eta\divv\left(\frac{\nabla u+F}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+F|^{2}}}\right)=H.

An easy computation yields

\begin{split}\frac{1}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla u+F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{5}{3}}}\big((\varepsilon^{3}&+\|\nabla u+F\|_{*}^{3})\divv(G(\nabla u+F))\\
&-2G(\nabla u+F)(D^{2}u+DF)G(\nabla u+F)^{T}\big)\\
&+\frac{\eta}{(1+|\nabla u+F|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\left((1+|\nabla u+F|^{2})\divv\big(\nabla u+F\right)\\
&-(\nabla u+F)(D^{2}u+DF)(\nabla u+F)^{T}\big)=H.\end{split}

Therefore, we can write (
4.2
) in the familiar form

\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(z,\nabla u;F)D_{i,j}u+B^{\varepsilon,\eta}(z,\nabla u;F)=H,

where the coefficients
A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
and
B^{\varepsilon,\eta}
are defined by

(4.3)

\begin{split}A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(z,p;F)&:=\frac{1}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{2}{3}}}D_{j}G_{i}(p+F)-\frac{2}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{5}{3}}}G_{i}(p+F)G_{j}(p+F)\\
&\quad+\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{1+|p+F|^{2}}}\delta_{ij}-\frac{\eta}{(1+|p+F|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}(p_{i}+F_{i})(p_{j}+F_{j})\end{split}

and

\begin{split}B^{\varepsilon,\eta}(z,p;F)&:=\frac{1}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{2}{3}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}D_{j}G_{i}(p+F)D_{i}F_{j}\\
&\quad-\frac{2}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{5}{3}}}G(p+F)\,DF\,G(p+F)^{T}\\
&\quad+\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{1+|p+F|^{2}}}\divv F-\frac{\eta}{(1+|p+F|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}(p+F)DF(p+F)^{T}\end{split}

for any
z\in\Omega
and
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{2n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
.
Therefore (
4.2
) is a second-order quasi-linear equation. Moreover, thanks to the computations of the previous section and
(iii)
in Proposition
3.1
, we know that (
4.2
) is the Euler-Lagrange equation associated to the functional

u\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla u+F\|_{*}^{3}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}+\eta\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+F|^{2}}+uH\,dz.

Notice that the matrix
A^{\varepsilon,\eta}
is symmetric. Moreover, observing that

(4.4)

D_{j}(G_{i}(p))=\begin{cases}2\|p\|_{*}\pi_{j}(p)\pi_{i}(p)+\|p\|_{*}^{2}D_{i}\pi_{j}(p)&\text{if}\,p\neq 0\\
0&\text{if}\,p=0,\end{cases}

we infer that (
4.2
) is an elliptic equation. Indeed, assume first that
p+F=0
. Then, by (
4.3
) and (
4.4
)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(z,p;F)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}=\eta|\xi|^{2}

for any
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. From the other hand, when
p+F\neq 0,
(
2.6
), (
4.4
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality imply that

(4.5)

\begin{split}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}&(z,p;F)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}\frac{2\|p+F\|_{*}\pi_{i}(p+F)\pi_{j}(p+F)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}+\|p+F\|_{*}^{2}D_{i}\pi_{j}(p+F)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{2}{3}}}\\
&\quad-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}\frac{2\|p+F\|_{*}^{4}\pi_{i}(p+F)\pi_{j}(p+F)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{5}{3}}}+\eta\frac{(1+|p+F|^{2})|\xi|^{2}-\langle p+F,\xi\rangle^{2}}{(1+|p+F|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\\
&\geqslant\frac{\|p+F\|_{*}^{2}}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{2}{3}}}\left(\xi\,D\pi(p+F)\,\xi^{T}\right)+\eta\frac{|\xi|^{2}}{(1+|p+F|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\\
&>\eta\frac{|\xi|^{2}}{(1+|p+F|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\end{split}

for any
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, so that we conclude that

(4.6)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(z,p;F)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\geqslant\frac{\eta}{(1+|p+F|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}|\xi|^{2}

for any
z\in\overline{\Omega}
and any
p,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. We remark that, by (
4.5
), equation (
3.5
) is elliptic outside the singular set.
In view of (
4.6
), we are in position to apply the classical theory for quasi-linear elliptic equations of

27

Proposition 4.1

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}

Theorem 4.3
.

Let

\begin{split}Lw&=\divv(a_{i,j}D_{j}w)+c_{i}D_{i}w\end{split}

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

be a convex body in

. Moreover, the maps

\sigma\mapsto A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(\cdot,\cdot;\sigma F),\quad\sigma\mapsto B^{\varepsilon,\eta}(\cdot,\cdot;\sigma F)

are continuous as maps from
[0,1]
to
C^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})

Proof.

The second statement easily follows from the definition of the coefficients. Let us prove the first statement.
It is clear, thanks to our assumptions on
K_{0}
and

, that
A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(\cdot,\cdot,\sigma F)
and

belong to

for any
\sigma\in[0,1]
. Moreover, in view of (

is

for any

is

\lim_{p\to 0}\dfrac{|D_{j}G_{i}|(p)}{|p|^{\alpha}}=0.

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{|D_{j}G_{i}|(p)}{|p|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=2\dfrac{\|p\|_{*}}{|p|^{\alpha}}|\pi_{j}(p)\pi_{i}(p)+\|p\|_{*}^{2}D_{i}\pi_{j}(p)|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\dfrac{\|p\|_{*}^{\alpha}}{|p|^{\alpha}}\|p\|_{*}^{1-\alpha}\left(\left|\pi_{j}(p)\pi_{i}(p)\right|+\|p\|_{*}|D_{i}\pi_{j}(p)|\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|p\|_{*}^{1-\alpha}\to 0

as

, since

is bounded and the last factor in the previous inequality is

belongs to

∎

Lemma 4.5

), that

\begin{split}\langle p,\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(p+\sigma F)\rangle&=\langle p+\sigma F,\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(p+\sigma F)\rangle-\langle\sigma F,\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(p+\sigma F)\rangle\\
&\geqslant\frac{\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3}}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{2}{3}}}-\left(\frac{\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3}}{\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\|\sigma F\|_{*}\\
&\geqslant\frac{\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3}}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{2}{3}}}-\|F\|_{*}.\end{split}

Hence, noticing that

\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}\geqslant\|p\|_{*}-\|-\sigma F\|_{*}\geqslant\|p\|_{*}-\|-F\|_{*}

by the triangle inequality, it suffices to prove that

(4.12)

\frac{\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3}}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{2}{3}}}\geqslant\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}-1.

When
\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}\leqslant 1
(
4.12
) is trivial. Therefore let us assume
\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}>1
.
Notice that (
4.12
) is equivalent to

\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{\frac{9}{2}}\geqslant(\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}-1)^{\frac{3}{2}}(\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3}).

Since
a^{p}-b^{p}\geqslant(a-b)^{p}
when
0<b<a
and
p>1
, it is enough to check that

\begin{split}\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{9/2}&\geqslant(\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3/2}-1)(\varepsilon^{3}+\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})\\
&=\varepsilon^{3}\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3/2}+\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{9/2}-\varepsilon^{3}-\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3},\end{split}

which is clearly true since
\|p+\sigma F\|_{*}>1
and
\varepsilon<1
.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
K_{0}
be a convex body in
C^{2,\alpha}_{+}
with
0\in\intt{K_{0}}
. Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
be a bounded open set,
\varphi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
,
H\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
F\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
. If condition (
4.9
) is satisfied then there exist a constant
\eta_{0}=\eta_{0}(K_{0},\delta)\in(0,1)
and a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(n,K_{0},\Omega,\varphi,F,\delta)>0
, independent of
\sigma\in[0,1]
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
, such that, for any solution
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
to (
4.7
) with
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
it holds that

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant C_{1}.

Proof.

Let us notice that (
4.11
), the equivalence between
\|\cdot\|_{*}
and the Euclidean norm and the boundedness of
F
allow to find constants
a_{0},a_{2}>0
, independent of
\sigma\in[0,1]
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, such that

\langle p,\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(p+\sigma F)\rangle\geqslant a_{0}|p|-a_{2}

for any
z\in\Omega
and
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. This fact, together with the boundedness of
H
, suggests to rely on
[
27
, Lemma 10.8]
to limit ourselves to estimate
\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}
. Indeed, it is not difficult to show that
[
27
, Lemma 10.8]
remains true when condition
(10.23)
of
[
27
]
allows a positive coefficient multiplying
|p|
. Moreover, its proof can be easily adapted to achieve estimates from above of
\sup_{\Omega}-u
in terms of
\|u^{-}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}
for any solution of
Qu=0
where
Q
is defined in
(10.5)
of
[
27
]
. In the end it suffices to estimate
\|u^{+}\|_{{L^{1}(\Omega})}
and
\|u^{-}\|_{{L^{1}(\Omega})}
.
We only estimate
\|u^{+}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}
, being the other case analogous. Moreover, up to replacing
u
by
u-\|\varphi\|_{\infty,\partial\Omega}
, we can assume that
u\leqslant 0
in
\partial\Omega
.
Let us set
v=u^{+}
. Then it is clear that
v\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)
, and moreover
\nabla v
exists in the classical sense for almost every
z\in\Omega
. Therefore, since
u
is in particular a weak solution to

\divv(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(\nabla u+\sigma F))+\eta\divv\left(\frac{\nabla u+\sigma F}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+\sigma F|^{2}}}\right)=\sigma H,

it follows that

(4.13)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla v,\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(\nabla u+\sigma F)\rangle+\eta\left\langle\nabla v,\frac{\nabla u+\sigma F}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+\sigma F|^{2}}}\right\rangle\,dz=-\int_{\Omega}v\sigma Hdz.\end{split}

We claim that

(4.14)

\langle\nabla v,\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(\nabla u+\sigma F)\rangle\geqslant\|\nabla v\|_{*}-1-\|F\|_{*}-\|-F\|_{*}

holds in any point where
\nabla v
exists in the classical sense. Indeed, in such points
\nabla v
is either
0
or
\nabla u
. In the first case (
4.14
) is trivial, while in the second case it follows from Lemma
4.5
.
It is well known that, since
v\geqslant 0
and
v\in W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)
, there exists a sequence of non-negative functions
(v_{k})_{k}\subseteq C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
converging to
v
strongly in
W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Moreover, thanks to (
4.9
) it holds that

\left|\int_{\Omega}Hv_{k}dz\right|\leqslant(1-\delta)\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla v_{k}\|_{*}\,dz.

Hence, passing to the limit in the previous equation, and recalling that
\|\cdot\|_{*}
is equivalent to the Euclidean norm, we conclude that (
4.9
) holds for
v
.
Combining this information with (
4.13
) and (
4.14
) we get that

\begin{split}0&=\int_{\Omega}-\langle\nabla v,\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(\nabla u+\sigma F)\rangle-\eta\left\langle\nabla v,\frac{\nabla u+\sigma F}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+\sigma F|^{2}}}\right\rangle\,dz-\int_{\Omega}v\sigma H\,dz\\
&\leqslant\int_{\Omega}-\|\nabla v\|_{*}+1+\|F\|_{*}+\|-F\|_{*}+\eta|\nabla v|\,dz+\left|\int_{\Omega}vH\,dz\right|\\
&\leqslant\int_{\Omega}-\|\nabla v\|_{*}+1+\|F\|_{*}+\|-F\|_{*}+C\eta\|\nabla v\|_{*}+(1-\delta)\|\nabla v\|_{*}\,dz\\
&=\int_{\Omega}1+\|F\|_{*}+\|-F\|_{*}+(C\eta-\delta)\|\nabla v\|_{*}\,dz,\end{split}

where
C=C(K_{0})
is a positive constant as in (
2.1
). Hence, choosing
\eta_{0}\in(0,1)
such that
\delta-C\eta_{0}>0
, we conclude that

(\delta-C\eta_{0})\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla v\|_{*}\,dz\leqslant(\delta-C\eta)\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla v\|_{*}\,dz\leqslant\int_{\Omega}1+\|F\|_{*}+\|-F\|_{*}\,dz

for any
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
.
Thanks to the Poincaré inequality and the equivalence between
\|\cdot\|_{*}
and the Euclidean norm, we conclude that there exists a constant
c_{1}
, independent of
\sigma\in[0,1]
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
, such that

\int_{\Omega}u^{+}\,dz\leqslant c_{1}.

Since in the same way we can achieve an estimate for
u^{-}
, the thesis follows.
∎

The next step is to achieve gradient estimates, again in the
C^{0}
norm, for solutions to (
4.7
). As customary in this framework, we want to reduce ourselves to boundary gradient estimates via a suitable maximum principle. To this aim, arguing as in
[
12
]
, we need to assume the existence of scalar functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{2n}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
such that

(4.15)

D_{k}F_{i}=D_{i}f_{k}\quad\mbox{for any}\quad i,k=1,\ldots,2n.

We stress that interior gradient estimates usually depend on the bounds of the coefficients and the ellipticity nature of the equation (cf. e.g.
[
27
, Chapter 15]
). Consequently, since by (
4.6
) the ellipticity constant tends to vanish as
\eta\to 0
,
the right way to achieve estimates which are uniform in
\epsilon,\eta\in(0,1)
is to rely on a suitable maximum principle argument.
Indeed, thanks to (
4.15
), the following maximum principle, which is the Finsler counterpart of
[
12
, Proposition 4.3]
, holds.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
K_{0}
be a convex body in
C^{2,\alpha}_{+}
for
0<\alpha<1
with
0\in\intt{K_{0}}
. Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
be a bounded domain. Let
F\in C^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
be such that (
4.15
) holds. Let
H
be a constant.
Let
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
be a solution to (
4.7
). Then

(4.16)

\|\nabla u\|_{\infty,\Omega}\leqslant\|\nabla u\|_{\infty,\partial\Omega}+2\|f\|_{\infty,\Omega},

where
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{2n})
is as in (
4.15
).

Proof.

Fix
\sigma\in[0,1]
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\eta\in(0,1)
. Let
v\in C^{2}_{c}(\Omega)
and fix
k\in\{1,\ldots,2n\}
. Then, multiplying (
4.7
) by
D_{k}v
, using Proposition
3.1
, integrating over
\Omega
, integrating by parts and exploiting the properties of
F
, it holds that

\begin{split}0&=\int_{\Omega}\left(\divv\left(\pi(\nabla u+\sigma F)\frac{\|\nabla u+\sigma F\|^{2}_{\ast}}{\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla u+\sigma F\|_{\ast}^{3}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}+\eta\frac{\nabla u+\sigma F}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+\sigma F|^{2}}}\right)-\sigma H\right)D_{k}v\,dz\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\divv\left(\pi(\nabla u+\sigma F)\frac{\|\nabla u+\sigma F\|^{2}_{\ast}}{\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla u+\sigma F\|_{\ast}^{3}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}+\eta\frac{\nabla u+\sigma F}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+\sigma F|^{2}}}\right)D_{k}v\,dz\\
&=-\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\int_{\Omega}\left(\pi_{i}(\nabla u+\sigma F)\frac{\|\nabla u+\sigma F\|^{2}_{\ast}}{\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla u+\sigma F\|_{\ast}^{3}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}+\eta\frac{D_{i}u+\sigma F_{i}}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+\sigma F|^{2}}}\right)D_{i}D_{k}v\,dz\\
&=-\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\int_{\Omega}\left(\pi_{i}(\nabla u+\sigma F)\frac{\|\nabla u+\sigma F\|^{2}_{\ast}}{\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla u+\sigma F\|_{\ast}^{3}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}+\eta\frac{D_{i}u+\sigma F_{i}}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+\sigma F|^{2}}}\right)D_{k}D_{i}v\,dz\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\int_{\Omega}D_{k}\left(\pi_{i}(\nabla u+\sigma F)\frac{\|\nabla u+\sigma F\|^{2}_{\ast}}{\left(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla u+\sigma F\|_{\ast}^{3}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}+\eta\frac{D_{i}u+\sigma F_{i}}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+\sigma F|^{2}}}\right)D_{i}v\,dz\\
&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}\int_{\Omega}A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(z,\nabla u;\sigma F)D_{k}(D_{j}u+\sigma F_{j})D_{i}v\,dz\\
&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}\int_{\Omega}A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(z,\nabla u;\sigma F)D_{j}(D_{k}u+\sigma f_{k})D_{i}vdz,\end{split}

being
A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
as in (
4.3
).
Therefore we proved that

(4.17)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}\int_{\Omega}A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(z,\nabla u;\sigma F)D_{j}(D_{k}u+\sigma f_{k})D_{i}v\,dz=0

for any
v\in C^{2}_{c}(\Omega)
. Arguing as in
[
12
, Proposition 4.3]
it is easy to show that (
4.17
) actually holds for any
v\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega)
. Therefore, recalling (
4.6
), we proved that
D_{k}u+\sigma f_{k}
is a weak solution to the linear uniformly elliptic equation

\divv(a_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}D_{j}w)=0,

where

a_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(z):=A_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(z,\nabla u;\sigma F(z)).

Hence, being
a_{i,j}^{\varepsilon,\eta}(z)
bounded in
\Omega
, thanks to Theorem
4.3
with
b_{i},c_{i},d=0
we conclude that

\|\nabla u+\sigma f\|_{\infty,\Omega}\leqslant\|\nabla u+\sigma f\|_{\infty,\partial\Omega},

which in particular implies that

(4.18)

\|\nabla u\|_{\infty,\Omega}\leqslant\|\nabla u\|_{\infty,\partial\Omega}+2\|f\|_{\infty,\Omega}.\qed

Finally we are left to provide boundary gradient estimates for solutions to (
4.7
).
Therefore, inspired by
[
26
]
, we have to impose some constraints on the values of

depending on the Finsler mean curvature of

. More precisely, we require that

(4.19)

|H|(z_{0})<H_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0})

for any
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, where

is the

-mean curvature as defined in Subsection

. Here and in the rest of this section we assume that
K_{0}
is a convex body in
C^{\infty}_{+}
such that

, since we need to apply the results of Section
2.3.1
and Section
2.3.2
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
K_{0}
be a convex body in

. Let

First of all we notice that, being

H_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}

(
4.19
) implies the existence of a positive constant

|H(z_{0})|\leqslant H_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0})-3C_{3}

.

[
27

\mathcal{N}
of
z_{0}

\Omega

and

w^{+}(z_{0})=w^{-}(z_{0})=\sigma\varphi(z_{0}),

for any

.
In this proof we deal only with the upper barrier, being the other case analogous.
In order to find an upper barrier, we consider a tubular neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
\partial\Omega
and we let
\Gamma_{\mu}:=\{x\in\overline{\Omega}\,:\,d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(x)<\mu\}
, where

\mu\in(0,\mu_{0})
and

\Gamma_{\mu}\subseteq\Gamma_{\mu_{0}}\Subset\mathcal{O}
for any
\mu\in(0,\mu_{0})
. Let us denote by
H_{\Sigma_{d(z)}}(z)
the Euclidean mean curvature of
\Sigma_{d(z)}
at any

. Being
H_{\Sigma_{d(z)}}
continuous on
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mu_{0}}
, there exists a constant
C_{4}=C_{4}(\Omega,K_{0})>0
such that

(4.23)

|H_{\Sigma_{d(z)}}(z)|\leqslant C_{4}

for any
z\in\overline{\Gamma}_{\mu_{0}}
.
We fix
\mu\in(0,\mu_{0})
and we define
w^{+}:\Gamma_{\mu}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
by
w^{+}(z):=kd_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z)+\sigma\varphi(z)
, where
k>0
has to be chosen.
First, thanks to (
2.18
),
w^{+}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Gamma_{\mu}})
, and for any

there exists a unique

such that

. Moreover, it is clear that
w^{+}(z_{0})=\sigma\varphi(z_{0})
for any
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
. Thanks to (
4.20
), if we choose

k\geqslant\frac{\tilde{C_{1}}+\|\varphi\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\mu},

it follows that
w^{+}(z)\geqslant u(z)
for any
z\in\Omega
with
d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z)=\mu
, and so we conclude that
u(z)\leqslant w^{+}(z)
for any
z\in\partial\Gamma_{\mu}
.
We are left to show that
w^{+}
is a subsolution to (
4.7
). Therefore it suffices to show that

(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{5}{3}}\left(\divv(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F))+\eta\divv\Big(\frac{\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F|^{2}}}\Big)-\sigma H\right)<0

on
\Gamma_{\mu}
. Taking
k>\sup_{\Omega}\|-F\|_{*},
(
2.12
) ensures that
k\nabla d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z)+\sigma F(z)\neq 0
for any
z\in\Gamma_{\mu}
and
\sigma\in[0,1]
. Let us notice that Proposition
3.1
and a simple computation imply that

\begin{split}(\varepsilon^{3}+&\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{5}{3}}\divv(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F))\\
=&(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{5}{3}}\divv\left(\frac{\pi(\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F)\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{2}}{(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)\\
=&(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})\underbrace{\divv(\pi(\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F)\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{2})}_{A}\\
&+(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{5}{3}}\underbrace{\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{2}\langle\pi(\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F),\nabla\left((\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})^{-\frac{2}{3}}\right)\rangle}_{B}.\end{split}

We estimate separately
A
and
B
. In the following computations we let
d:=d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}
and
R_{\sigma}:=\sigma\nabla\varphi+\sigma F
. We are going to exploit the fact that, thanks to the homogeneity properties of the equation, the contribution of
R_{\sigma}
as

is negligible. Let us notice that by (
2.12
) and (
2.7

(4.24)

\pi(\nabla d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega})\cdot D^{2}d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}=0.

Hence, thanks to (
2.12
), (
4.24
), the
1
-homogeneity of
\|\cdot\|_{*}
, the
0
-homogeneity of
\pi
, the
-1
-homogeneity of
D\pi
and the properties of

, it holds that

\begin{split}A=&\|k\nabla d+R_{\sigma}\|^{2}_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}D_{i}\left(\pi_{i}(k\nabla d+R_{\sigma})\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\pi_{i}(k\nabla d+R_{\sigma})D_{i}\left(\|k\nabla d+R_{\sigma}\|^{2}_{*}\right)\\
=&\|k\nabla d+R_{\sigma}\|^{2}_{*}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}D_{i}\pi_{j}(k\nabla d+R_{\sigma})(kD_{ij}d+D_{i}R_{\sigma,j})\\

&+2k^{2}\left\|\nabla d+\frac{R_{\sigma}}{k}\right\|_{*}\pi\left(\nabla d+\frac{R_{\sigma}}{k}\right)\cdot\left(D^{2}d+\frac{DR_{\sigma}}{k}\right)\cdot\pi\left(\nabla d+\frac{R_{\sigma}}{k}\right)^{T}\\
=&k^{2}(1+o(1))(\divv(\pi(\nabla d))+o(1))+2k^{2}(1+o(1))(\pi(\nabla d)\cdot D^{2}d\cdot\pi(\nabla d)^{T}+o(1))\\
=&k^{2}\divv(\pi(\nabla d))+o(k^{2}),\end{split}

which allows to infer that

\begin{split}(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})A=k^{5}\divv(\pi(\nabla d))+o(k^{5})\end{split}

as

, where

is uniform with respect to

,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\sigma\in[0,1]
. Now, exploiting the same properties as above, we estimate

\begin{split}(\varepsilon^{3}+\|k\nabla d+R_{\sigma}\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{5}{3}}B&=-2\|k\nabla d+R_{\sigma}\|_{*}^{4}\langle\pi(k\nabla d+R_{\sigma}),\nabla(\|k\nabla d+R_{\sigma}\|_{*})\rangle\\
&=-2\|k\nabla d+R_{\sigma}\|_{*}^{4}\pi(k\nabla d+R_{\sigma})\cdot(kD^{2}d+DR_{\sigma})\cdot\pi(k\nabla d+R_{\sigma})^{T}\\
&=-2k^{5}\left\|\nabla d+\frac{R_{\sigma}}{k}\right\|_{*}^{4}\pi\left(\nabla d+\frac{R_{\sigma}}{k}\right)\cdot\left(D^{2}d+\frac{DR_{\sigma}}{k}\right)\cdot\pi\left(\nabla d+\frac{R_{\sigma}}{k}\right)^{T}\\
&=-2k^{5}(1+o(1))(\pi(\nabla d)\cdot D^{2}d\cdot\pi(\nabla d)^{T}+o(1))\\
&=-2k^{5}(1+o(1))o(1)\\
&=o(k^{5}).\end{split}

as
k\to\infty
and uniformly with respect to
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\sigma\in[0,1]
and
z\in\Gamma_{\mu}
.
From a similar computation, it follows that

\begin{split}\divv\left(\frac{\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F|^{2}}}\right)&=\frac{\divv(\nabla d)+\frac{\divv R_{\sigma}}{k}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{k^{2}}+\left|\nabla d+\frac{R_{\sigma}}{k}\right|^{2}}}-\frac{\left(\nabla d+\frac{R_{\sigma}}{k}\right)\cdot\left(D^{2}d+\frac{DR_{\sigma}}{k}\right)\cdot\left(\nabla d+\frac{R_{\sigma}}{k}\right)^{T}}{\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}}+\left|\nabla d+\frac{R_{\sigma}}{k}\right|^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\\
&=\frac{\divv(\nabla d)}{|\nabla d|}-\frac{\nabla d\cdot D^{2}d\cdot\nabla d^{T}}{|\nabla d|^{3}}+o(1)\\
&=\divv\left(\frac{\nabla d}{|\nabla d|}\right)+o(1)\end{split}

as
k\to\infty
and uniformly with respect to
\sigma\in[0,1]
and
z\in\Gamma_{\mu}
.
Finally, it is easy to see that

-(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{5}{3}}\sigma H\leqslant(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{5}{3}}|H|=k^{5}|H|+o(k^{5})

as
k\to\infty
and uniformly with respect to
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\sigma\in[0,1]
and
z\in\Gamma_{\mu}
.
In the end we get that

\begin{split}(\varepsilon^{3}+\|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{5}{3}}&\left(\divv(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F))+\eta\divv\left(\frac{\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla w^{+}+\sigma F|^{2}}}\right)-\sigma H\right)\\
&\leqslant k^{5}\left(\divv(\pi(\nabla d))+\eta\divv\left(\frac{\nabla d}{|\nabla d|}\right)+|H|\right)+o(k^{5})\end{split}

as
k\to\infty
and uniformly with respect to
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\sigma\in[0,1]
and
z\in\Gamma_{\mu}
.

Now, let
z\in\Gamma_{\mu}
and let
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
be such that
d(z)=\|z-z_{0}\|
. Thanks to the Lipschitz continuity of
H
and the equivalence between
\|\cdot\|
and the Euclidean norm, there exists a constant
C_{5}=C_{5}(K_{0})
such that

(4.25)

\begin{split}|H|(z)=|H|(z_{0})+|H|(z)-|H|(z_{0})\leqslant|H|(z_{0})+C_{5}d(z)\leqslant|H|(z_{0})+C_{5}\mu.\end{split}

Hence, thanks to (
2.10
), (
2.14
), (
4.22
) and (
4.23
), we conclude that

(4.26)

\begin{split}\divv(\pi(\nabla d))(z)+\eta\divv\left(\frac{\nabla d}{|\nabla d|}\right)+|H|(z_{0})+C_{5}\mu&=-H_{K_{0},\Sigma_{d(z)}}(z)-\eta H_{\Sigma_{d(z)}}(z)+|H|(z_{0})+C_{5}\mu\\
&\leqslant-H_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0})+\eta C_{4}+|H|(z_{0})+C_{5}\mu\\
&\leqslant-C_{3}<0,\end{split}

provided that
\mu\leqslant\frac{C_{3}}{C_{5}}
and
\eta\leqslant\frac{C_{3}}{C_{4}}
.
Hence we found an upper barrier, from which the thesis follows.
∎

Proposition 4.10
.

Assume that

. Let

. Let

, independent of
\sigma\in[0,1]
,

and

, such that, for any solution

to (

),
it holds that

\|u\|_{\infty,\Omega}\leqslant C_{1}.

Proof.

Let
k^{K_{0}}
be the geodesic curvature of
K_{0}
. Since

, then in particular

k^{K_{0}}(p)>0
for any
p\in\partial K_{0}
. Let
C_{3}=C_{3}(K_{0},\Omega,H)
be as in (

).
Let us define the function

by

(4.27)

v(z):=\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\varphi|+kd_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z)

for any

, where

has to be chosen and
\Omega_{1}
is the set defined in (

)) that
v\in C^{2}(\intt{\Omega_{1}})
. We repeat
verbatim
the computations of the proof of Proposition
4.8
up to (

constant, we can choose
C_{5}=0
in (
4.25

, we exploit Proposition

to infer that

(4.28)

\begin{split}\divv(\pi(\nabla d))(z)+\eta\divv\left(\frac{\nabla d}{|\nabla d|}\right)+|H|&=-H_{K_{0},\Sigma_{d(z)}}(z)-\eta H_{\Sigma_{d(z)}}(z)+|H|\\
&=-H_{K_{0},\Sigma_{d(z)}}(z)-\eta k^{K_{0}}\left(\pi_{K}(N_{z})\right)H_{K_{0},\Sigma_{d(z)}}(z)+|H|\\
&\leqslant-H_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0})+|H|\\
&\leqslant-3C_{3}<0.\end{split}

Hence there exists

, independent of
\varepsilon\in(0,1),

\eta\in(0,1)
,

) on

to a linear elliptic equation of the form

Hence, thanks to Theorem
4.3
and recalling (

), it follows that

\sup_{\Omega_{1}}(u-v)\leqslant\sup_{\partial\Omega\cup R}((u-v)^{+}).

Noticing that

on

and that

, we obtain that

\begin{split}u(z)-v(z)&\leqslant\sup_{\Omega}(u-v)=\sup_{\Omega_{1}}(u-v)\leqslant\sup_{\partial\Omega_{1}}((u-v)^{+})=\sup_{R}((u-v)^{+})\end{split}

for any

. We are left to show that

Moreover,

is a maximum point for

on
\overline{\Omega}
. Let us fix
y_{0}\in\partial\Omega
such that
d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0})=\|z_{0}-y_{0}\|
. Then, thanks to Proposition
2.5
, it is easy to see that

(4.29)

d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z)=\|z-y_{0}\|

for any

belonging to
(y_{0},z_{0})
, the segment connecting
y_{0}
and
z_{0}
. Let now
\nu:=\frac{y_{0}-z_{0}}{|y_{0}-z_{0}|}
. By (
4.29
) it holds that
v(z)<v(z_{0})
for any
z\in(y_{0},z_{0})
, and moreover

(4.30)

D_{\nu}^{+}v(z_{0}):=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{v(z_{0}+h\nu)-v(z_{0})}{h}<0.

Since
z_{0}
is a maximum point of
u-v
, it holds in particular that
D_{\nu}^{+}u(z_{0})\leqslant D_{\nu}^{+}v(z_{0})
, which implies, together with (
4.30
), that
D_{\nu}^{+}u(z_{0})=D_{\nu}u(z_{0})<0
. This proves that
Du(z_{0})\neq 0
. Since then
z_{0}
is a regular point for
u
, the level set
\{z\in\Omega\,:\,u(z)=u(z_{0})\}
is locally a
C^{2}
hypersurface. Therefore there exists a small Euclidean ball
B
such that
B
is tangent to the level set at
z_{0}
and moreover
B\subseteq\{z\in\Omega\,:\,u(z)\geqslant u(z_{0})\}
. Now, since by our assumptions the Finsler balls relative to
-K_{0}
are uniformly convex and
C^{2}
, there exists
\varrho>0
and
x_{0}\in\Omega
such that

(4.31)

\overline{B_{-K_{0}}(x_{0},\varrho)}\subseteq\{z\in\Omega\,:\,u(z)\geqslant u(z_{0})\}

and
B_{-K_{0}}(x_{0},\varrho)
is tangent to
B
at
z_{0}
. Indeed, fix a Finsler ball tangent to
B
at
z_{0}
relative to
-K_{0}
, say

. On one hand, the principal curvatures of
\partial B
at

are fixed. On the other hand, noticing that the principal curvatures of a

convex set admit a positive lower bound, we can dilate and translate
B_{F}
to make the curvature of

as big as we want to ensure that (
4.31
) holds. Notice that

(4.32)

d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z)\geqslant d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0})

for any

. Indeed, if by contradiction there exists
z\in\overline{B_{-K_{0}}(x_{0},\varrho)}
such that
d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z)<d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0})
, then (
4.31
) would imply

u(z)-kd_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z)\geqslant u(z_{0})-kd_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z)>u(z_{0})-kd_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0}),

a contradiction to the maximality of
z_{0}
. Let now
w_{0}\in\partial\Omega
be such that
d_{K_{,}\partial\Omega}(x_{0})=\|x_{0}-w_{0}\|
, and let

be the unique point of intersection between

and the segment joining

and
x_{0}
. Then by (
2.2
), (
4.29
), (
4.32
), the choice of

and the strict convexity of

, it holds that

\begin{split}d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(x_{0})=\|x_{0}-w_{0}\|=\|x_{0}-b_{0}\|+\|b_{0}-w_{0}\|=\varrho+d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(b_{0})\geqslant\varrho+d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0}).\end{split}

On the other hand, (
2.2
) and the triangle inequality imply

\begin{split}d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(x_{0})\leqslant\|x_{0}-y_{0}\|\leqslant\|x_{0}-z_{0}\|+\|z_{0}-y_{0}\|=\varrho+d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0}).\end{split}

Putting together the previous inequalities we get that

(4.33)

d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(x_{0})=\|x_{0}-y_{0}\|=\|x_{0}-z_{0}\|+\|z_{0}-y_{0}\|,

from which in particular we conclude, exploiting again the strict convexity of

, that

lies on
(y_{0},x_{0})
. Therefore, thanks to this fact, the first equality in (

) and Proposition

, we conclude that

, which is a contradiction. In the end we proved that

\sup_{\Omega}u\leqslant\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\varphi|+k\max_{\overline{\Omega}}d_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}.

Since the converse estimate can be obtained in a similar way, the thesis is proved.

5.
Existence of Lipschitz minimizers for the sub-Finsler functional
\mathcal{I}

Thanks to the
a priori
estimates of the previous section, together with Proposition
4.1
and the uniformity of the estimates with respect to
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
, we are in position
to pass to the limit and find a solution to the sub-Finsler Prescribed Mean Curvature equation.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
K_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{+}
be a convex body such that
0\in\intt K_{0}
. Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
be a bounded domain with
C^{2,1}
boundary. Let
\varphi\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, for
0<\alpha<1
, and let
F\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
be such that (
4.15
) is satisfied. Assume that
H
is a constant such that (
4.9
) and (
4.19
) hold. Then, there exists
\eta_{0}\in(0,1)
such that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and any
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
, there exists a function
u_{\varepsilon,\eta}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
which solves (
4.1
). Moreover, there exists a constant
M>0
, independent of
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
, such that any solution
u_{\varepsilon,\eta}
to (
4.1
) satisfies

(5.1)

\sup_{\Omega}|u_{\varepsilon,\eta}|+\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon,\eta}|\leqslant M.

Finally, there exists a Lipschitz continuous minimizer
u_{0}\in\text{Lip}(\overline{\Omega})
for the functional
\mathcal{I}
defined in (
2.23
) with
u_{0}=\varphi
on
\partial\Omega
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.6
, Proposition
4.7
and Proposition
4.8
, there exists a constant
M>0
such that, for any
\sigma\in[0,1]
, any
0<\varepsilon<1
and any
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
with
\eta_{0}>0
small enough, then any solution
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
to the problem (
4.7
) satisfies

\sup_{\Omega}|u|+\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla u|\leqslant M.

Then by Proposition
4.1
there exists a solution
u_{\varepsilon,\eta}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
to

\begin{cases}\divv(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{h}(\nabla u+F))+\eta\divv\left(\frac{\nabla u+\sigma F}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u+\sigma F|^{2}}}\right)=H&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=\varphi&\text{ in }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Again by Proposition
4.6
, Proposition
4.7
and Proposition
4.8
, we have that

(5.2)

\sup_{\Omega}|u_{\varepsilon,\eta}|+\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon,\eta}|\leqslant M,

where the constant
M>0
is uniform in
0<\varepsilon<1
and
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
. Let
\{\varepsilon_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subseteq(0,1)
and
\{\eta_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subseteq(0,\eta_{0})
be sequences such that
\varepsilon_{j}\to 0
and
\eta_{j}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
. Since
M
is uniform in
\varepsilon
and
\eta
by (
5.2
) we gain that
\sup_{\Omega}|u_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}|\leqslant M
and that for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in\Omega

(5.3)

|u_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}(z_{1})-u_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}(z_{2})|\leqslant M|z_{1}-z_{2}|.

Then, by Ascoli-Arzelà theorem there exists
u_{0}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
such that
u_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}\to u_{0}
uniformly in
\overline{\Omega}
. It is clear that
u=\varphi
on
\partial\Omega
. Moreover, taking the limit as
j\to 0
in (
5.3
), we gain that

\sup_{z_{1}\neq z_{2}}\frac{|u_{0}(z_{1})-u_{0}(z_{2})|}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|}\leqslant M,

thus
u_{0}
is Lipschitz. We claim that
u_{0}
is a minimizer for
\mathcal{I}
defined in (
2.23
). Indeed, we have that
\|u_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}\|_{W^{1,1}(\Omega)}\leqslant M|\Omega|
,
\|u_{0}\|_{W^{1,1}(\Omega)}\leqslant M|\Omega|
and
u_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}
converge to
u_{0}
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
. Moreover, the function
(p,(x,y))\to\|p+F(x,y)\|_{*}
is positive, continuous and convex in
p
. Therefore, by
[
45
, Theorem 4.1.2]
,
\mathcal{I}
is lower semicontinuous with respect to the strong
L^{1}
-topology, from which we have that

(5.4)

\mathcal{I}(u_{0})\leqslant\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathcal{I}(u_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}).

For each
v\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
such that
v-\varphi\in W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)
, it follows that

(5.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(u_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}+F\|_{*}\,dz+\int_{\Omega}Hu_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}\,dz

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{j}^{3}+\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}+F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{1}{3}}\,dz+\int_{\Omega}Hu_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}\,dz+\eta_{j}\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{1+|\nabla u_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}+F|^{2}}\,dz

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{j}^{3}+\|\nabla v+F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{1}{3}}\,dz+\int_{\Omega}Hv\,dz+\eta_{j}\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{1+|\nabla v+F|^{2}}\,dz

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon_{j}|\Omega|+\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla v+F\|_{*}\,dz+\int_{\Omega}Hv\,dz+\eta_{j}\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{1+|\nabla v+F|^{2}}\,dz,

where we have used the fact that the Dirichlet solution
u_{\varepsilon_{j},\eta_{j}}\in C^{2\,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
is a minimizer for the functional
v\to\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{j}^{3}+\|\nabla v+F\|_{*}^{3})^{\frac{1}{3}}+\int_{\Omega}Hv+\eta_{j}\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{1+|\nabla v+F|^{2}}\,dz
for each
v\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
s.t.
v-\varphi\in W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Passing to the liminf in (
5.5
) and taking into account (
5.4
), we obtain
\mathcal{I}(u_{0})\leqslant\mathcal{I}(v)
for each
v\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
s.t.
v-\varphi\in W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
∎

We now apply the same argument of the previous proof in
{\mathbb{H}}^{1}
, using the height estimate provided by Proposition
4.10
instead of the one given in Proposition
4.6
to avoid condition (
4.9
), to obtain the following sharp result in the first Heisenberg group.

.

and
K_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{+}
be a convex body such that

. Let

, for

\eta_{0}\in(0,1)

\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and any

u_{\varepsilon,\eta}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})

to (

\sup_{\Omega}|u_{\varepsilon,\eta}|+\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon,\eta}|\leqslant M.

To conclude this section, according to
[

we point out that the Dirichlet problem for the prescribed

6.
A sharp existence result of Lipschitz minimizers in the sub-Riemannian setting

As pointed out in the introduction, a Finsler approximation scheme for (
1.2
) cannot be arbitrarily chosen, since one needs to guarantee classical regularity of the resulting equations. Nevertheless, for a particular class of Finsler metrics, it is possible to choose a more natural approximation scheme. More precisely, let us consider the one-parameter family of differential equations defined formally by

(6.1)

\divv\left(\pi_{K_{0}}(\nabla u+F)\frac{\|\nabla u+F\|_{*}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+\|\nabla u+F\|^{2}_{*}}}\right)=H.

We point out that, when
K_{0}
is the Euclidean unit ball centered at the origin, (
6.1
) reduces to the well-known elliptic approximating equation considered for instance in
[
12
]
(cf. Remark
2.9
). In order to give to equation (
6.1
) a pointwise meaning, we must impose
a priori
that the function
\tilde{G}(p):=\|p\|_{*}\pi_{K_{0}}(p)
, which is
C^{1}
outside the origin, admits a
C^{1}
extension to the whole
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. This regularity hypothesis turns out to be equivalent to the fact that the left-invariant sub-Finsler structure induced by
K_{0}
comes from an underlying left-invariant sub-Riemannian metric on the distribution
\mathcal{H}
(cf.
[
57
]
), or equivalently that
K_{0}
is an ellipsoid centered at
0
. More precisely, it is easy to check that, if
\tilde{G}\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, then
D\tilde{G}
is necessarily a constant, symmetric and positive definite matrix, and moreover

(6.2)

\|p\|_{*}=\sqrt{p\cdot D\tilde{G}\cdot p^{T}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi_{K}(p)=\frac{D\tilde{G}\cdot p^{T}}{\|p\|_{*}}

for any
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. When (
6.2
) holds, a direct computation shows that (
6.1
) is a well-defined, quasi-linear elliptic equation, so that in this setting a Euclidean regularization term as in (
4.2
) is no longer needed. In order to solve the Dirichlet problem associated to (
6.1
) it is then possible to replicate almost word-by-word the computations of Section
4
, with the advantage that the absence of the Euclidean curvature term makes the process easier.
The main benefit of this new approximation is that, due to the absence of the Euclidean curvature term, a result analogous to Proposition
4.10
actually holds for any
n\geqslant 1
. We include the proof for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 6.1
.

Assume that
K_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{+}
induces a left-invariant sub-Riemannian metric on
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
be a bounded domain with
C^{2,1}
boundary, let
\varphi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
and let
H
be a constant which satisfies (
4.19
). There exists a
constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(K_{0},\Omega,\varphi,H,F)>0
, independent of
\sigma\in[0,1]
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, such that, for any solution
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
to

\begin{cases}\divv\left(\pi_{K_{0}}(\nabla u+\sigma F)\frac{\|\nabla u+\sigma F\|_{*}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+\|\nabla u+\sigma F\|^{2}_{*}}}\right)=\sigma H&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=\sigma\varphi&\text{ in }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

it holds that

\|u\|_{\infty,\Omega}\leqslant C_{1}.

Proof.

Let
C_{3}=C_{3}(K_{0},\Omega,H)
be as in (
4.22
).
Let us define the function
v:\intt{\Omega_{1}}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
as in (
4.27
), that is

v(z):=\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\varphi|+kd_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z)

for any
z\in\Omega_{1}
, where
k>0
has to be chosen and
\Omega_{1}
is the set defined in (
2.17
). Again we know (cf. (
2.18
)) that
v\in C^{2}(\intt{\Omega_{1}})
. We repeat again, with minor modifications, the computations of the proof of Proposition
4.8
up to (
4.26
). As in the proof of Proposition
4.10
, being
H
constant, we can choose
C_{5}=0
in (
4.25
). Moreover, since
\eta=0
, the analogous of (
4.26
) becomes

\begin{split}\divv(\pi(\nabla d))(z)+|H|(z_{0})&=-H_{K_{0},\Sigma_{d(z)}}(z)+|H|(z_{0})\\
&\leqslant-H_{K_{0},\partial\Omega}(z_{0})+|H|(z_{0})\\
&\leqslant-3C_{3}<0.\end{split}

Hence there exists
k>0
, independent of
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\sigma\in[0,1]
and
z\in\Omega_{1}
, such that
v
is a subsolution to (
6.1
) on
\intt{\Omega_{1}}
. The thesis then follows
verbatim
as in the proof of Proposition
4.10
.
∎

Therefore, in the sub-Riemannian setting, we can exploit Proposition
6.1
to avoid condition (
4.9
), so that the following sharper analogous to Theorem
5.1
holds.

Theorem 6.2
.

Assume that
K_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{+}
induces a left-invariant sub-Riemannian metric on
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
.
Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
be a bounded domain with
C^{2,1}
boundary. Let
\varphi\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, for
0<\alpha<1
, and let
F\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
be such that (
4.15
) is satisfied. Assume that
H
is a constant such that (
4.19
) holds. Then, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a function
u_{\varepsilon}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
which solves the Dirichlet problem associated to (
6.1
) with boundary datum
\varphi
.
Moreover, there exists a constant
M>0
, independent of
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, such that any solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to (
6.1
) satisfies

\sup_{\Omega}|u_{\varepsilon}|+\sup_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|\leqslant M.

Finally, there exists a Lipschitz continuous minimizer
u_{0}\in\text{Lip}(\overline{\Omega})
for the functional
\mathcal{I}
defined in (
2.23
) with
u_{0}=\varphi
on
\partial\Omega
.
