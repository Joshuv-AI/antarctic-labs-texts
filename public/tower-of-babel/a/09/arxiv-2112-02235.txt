Can non-local conductance spectra conclusively signal Majorana zero modes? Insights from von Neumann entropy
Abhishek Kejriwal, Bhaskaran Muralidharan

Abstract
The topological origin of the zero bias conductance signatures obtained via conductance spectroscopy in topological superconductor hybrid systems is a much contended issue. Recently, non-local conductance signatures that exploit the non-locality of the zero modes in three terminal hybrid setups have been proposed as means to ascertain the definitive presence of Majorana modes. The topological entanglement entropy, which is based on the von Neumann entropy, is yet another way to gauge the non-locality in connection with the bulk-boundary correspondence of a topological phase. We show that while both the entanglement entropy and the non-local conductance exhibit a clear topological phase transition signature for long enough pristine nanowires, non-local conductance fails to signal a topological phase transition for shorter disordered wires. While recent experiments have indeed shown premature gap-closure signatures in the non-local conductance spectra, we believe that the entanglement entropy can indeed signal a genuine transition, regardless of the constituent non-idealities in an experimental situation. Our results thereby point toward furthering the development of experimental techniques beyond conductance measurements to achieve a conclusive detection of Majorana zero modes.

Non-local correlations related to topological order and the resulting bulk-boundary correspondence can also be suitably quantified using the degree of entanglement
Ryu and Hatsugai 2006
;
Holzhey et al. 1994
;
Vidal et al. 2003
;
Koffel et al. 2012
, quantified by the von Neumann entropy
Boes et al. 2019
. The idea of using this construct is that entanglement in a higher dimensional Hilbert space is reflected via the mixedness of the density matrix in a reduced dimensional Hilbert space. By dividing the composite system into two subsystems, A and B, each of which having similar internal degrees of freedom, we calculate the mixedness of either subsystem using the von Neumann entropy using the partially traced density matrix :
S_{ent}=-\operatorname{Tr}\left(\rho_{A/B}\ln\rho_{A/B}\right)
. As pointed out in
Ryu and Hatsugai 2006
, away from the phase transition point, the entanglement entropy is directly related to the Berry phase of the ground state. Systems with a non-trivial Berry phase of
\pi\times
(odd integer), host topologically protected boundary modes and have a non-trivial value of entanglement entropy
>\ln 2
per boundary mode. However, for systems with a trivial Berry phase, the entanglement entropy vanishes. At the point of phase transition, the value of entanglement entropy is dictated by the correlation length, which leads to a spike in the magnitude of the entanglement entropy
Hegde et al. 2021
, making it an ideal metric for gauging the onset of a topological phase transition.

Entanglement Entropy :
We finally define the entanglement entropy metric for the TS chain. It is important to note that the entanglement entropy is calculated for the isolated TS chain. To calculate the entanglement entropy, we first evaluate the correlation matrix for the chain. The correlation matrix (
C
) is defined as:

C_{nm}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\left\langle c^{\dagger}c\right\rangle&\left\langle c^{\dagger}c^{\dagger}\right\rangle\\
\langle cc\rangle&\left\langle cc^{\dagger}\right\rangle\end{array}\right)_{nm},

(6)

where the matrix elements of the correlation matrix can be calculated by evaluating the expectation values of the operators quadratic in
c
,
c^{\dagger}
, in the BCS ground state. Once we evaluate the correlation matrix, we partition the chain into two subsystems, A and B, and assess the truncated correlation matrix for either of the subsystems. The matrix elements for the truncated correlation matrix (
\tilde{C}
) for subsystem
A
can be defined as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{nm}

\displaystyle=C_{(n,m)\in A}.

(7)

Following the calculation detailed in the supplementary material
Chung and Peschel 2001
, we can prove that the entanglement entropy and the eigenvalues of the truncated correlation matrix are related through the formula:

\displaystyle S_{ent}=-\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left[\xi_{k}\log\xi_{k}+\left(1-\xi_{k}\right)\log\left(1-\xi_{k}\right)\right],

(8)

where
\xi_{k}
denote the eigenvalues of the truncated correlation matrix.

Experimental non-local conductance measurements for disordered physical systems have signaled a gap-closure signature before the appearance of ZBCPs in the local conductance spectrum
Puglia et al. 2021
. This rules out the connection between gap-closure and ABSs. One of the possible reasons for the gap-closure can be related to the characteristic length scales of the disorder-induced subgap states being longer than the typical device lengths. For our device setup with local inhomogeneity, we observe a gap closure in the trivial regime for device lengths smaller than the characteristic localization length of the ABSs. However, the entanglement entropy signature remains robust to the device size and faithfully detects a topological phase transition.

The ideas presented here pose the challenge of looking beyond conductance spectra as means to ascertaining the presence of topological MZMs and establishing experimental measurement techniques for the entanglement entropy spectra. One of the possible ways is to figure out the exact ground state density matrix through quantum state tomography. However, quantum state tomography is resource-intensive, with the cost scaling exponentially with the subsystem size. Estimating the statistical correlations in the subsystem through the technique of random measurements provides an alternate route for assessing the entanglement entropy
Satzinger et al. 2021
;
Elben et al. 2018
;
van Enk and Beenakker 2012
;
Brydges et al. 2019
. The key idea behind this protocol is that the second-order Rényi entropies can be quantified using statistical correlations between the outcomes of measurements performed in random bases. Other works have presented the connection between entanglement entropy and the electrical shot noise generated by opening and closing a quantum point contact periodically in time
Klich and Levitov 2009
. It may also be possible to device setups based on quantum thermodynamics
Smirnov 2021a
;
Smirnov 2021b
to detect such entropic signatures.

Conclusion:
To summarize, we theoretically proposed the use of topological entanglement entropy as a metric to distinguish between the topological MZM and the trivial ABS. We clearly demonstrated that under various experimental conditions related to the setup, the non-local conductance measurements could yield false positives and premature gap closures. We showed that while both the entanglement entropy and the non-local conductance exhibit a clear topological phase transition signature for long enough pristine nanowires, non-local conductance fails to signal a topological phase transition for shorter disordered wires. While recent experiments have indeed shown premature gap-closure signatures in the non-local conductance spectra, we believe that the entanglement entropy can indeed signal a genuine transition, regardless of the constituent non-idealities in an experimental situation. Our results thereby point toward furthering the development of experimental techniques beyond conductance measurements to achieve a conclusive detection of Majorana zero modes.

\displaystyle\eta_{k}=\sum_{j}g_{kj}c_{j}+h_{kj}c_{j}^{\dagger},\mspace{30mu}\eta_{k}^{\dagger}=\sum_{j}g_{kj}c_{j}^{\dagger}+h_{kj}c_{j},

(S4)

such that,

\hat{H}=\sum_{k}\Lambda_{k}\eta_{k}^{\dagger}\eta_{k}+\mathrm{constant}.

(S5)

The matrices
g
and
h
can be chosen using the condition that
\eta_{k}
and
\eta_{k}^{\dagger}
anti-commute. The anti-commutation relation imposes the condition:

\left[\eta_{k},\hat{H}\right]=\Lambda_{k}\eta_{k}.

(S6)

Substituting (
S4
) and (
S5
) to (
S6
) imposes constraints on
g
and
h
that can be written as:

\begin{array}[]{l}\sum_{j}\left(g_{kj}A_{jn}-h_{kj}B_{jn}\right)=\Lambda_{k}g_{kn}\\
\sum_{j}\left(g_{kj}B_{jn}-h_{kj}A_{jn}\right)=\Lambda_{k}h_{kn}.\end{array}

(S7)

To solve for
g
and
h
, two new matrices are defined,

\displaystyle\Phi=g+h,\mspace{50mu}\Psi=g-h.

(S8)

This reduces (
S7
) to an eigenvalue problem in
\Phi
and
\Psi
.

\begin{array}[]{l}\Phi(A-B)(A+B)=\Lambda^{2}\Phi\\
\Psi(A+B)(A-B)=\Lambda^{2}\Psi.\end{array}

(S9)

The eigenvectors for the above eigenvalue problem give us
\Phi
and
\Psi
. We then use (
S8
) to get back the matrices
g
and
h
and thus we have successfully found a way to diagonalize
\hat{H}
. Since
\hat{H}
conserves parity, its ground state can either have odd or even number of fermions. Without loss of generality, we can assume the ground state to have even number of fermions. The general form of ground state for a Hamiltonian quadratic in fermionic operators and with definite parity is given by the BCS wavefunction which represents a superposition of either odd or even number of particles depending upon the parity of the system,

\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle=C\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}G_{ij}c_{j}^{\dagger}\right)|0\rangle,

(S10)

where
|0\rangle
is the vacuum state of the operators
c_{j}
. Since the ground state
|\Phi_{0}\rangle
is the vacuum state of opertors
\eta_{j}
,
\eta_{j}|\Phi_{0}\rangle=0
. This relation can be used to find the matrix
G
in terms of
g
and
h
and some tedious calculation leads us to the relation:
gG=h
.

Now that we have the ground state wavefunction for the system
|\Phi_{0}\rangle
, the density matrix is given by
\rho=|\Phi_{0}\rangle\langle\Phi_{0}|
which when substituted with (
S10
) yields:

\rho=|C|^{2}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}G_{ij}c_{j}^{\dagger}\right)|0\rangle\langle 0|\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}c_{i}G_{ij}c_{j}\right).

(S11)

The next step is to get the reduced density matrix for the sub-system A, using
\rho_{A}=\operatorname{Tr}_{B}(\rho)
. The final step is to directly diagonalize
\rho_{A}
to get its eigenvalues. These eigenvalues can be subsituted to (
S1
) to get the entanglement entropy. But this method requires calculating all the intermediate matrices defined above like
g
,
h
,
G
and can be quite tedious.

.1.2
Method 2

Another way to get the entanglement entropy is to use the correlation matrix for the system. A rigorous calculation using Grassman algebra
Chung and Peschel 2001
simplifies the reduced density matrix derived in (
S11
) to:

\rho_{A}=Kexp(-\mathcal{H}_{A}),

(S12)

where
K=|C|^{2}
and
\mathcal{H}_{A}
is the effective Hamiltonian for the sub-system
A
, which has the same general form as the original Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, the only difference being the matrices
A
and
B
. If we could figure out a way to get the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}_{A}
, then we can easily get the eigenvalues of
\rho_{A}
and thus the entanglement entropy.

Let’s assume that the subsystem
A
has
M
sites and the net system has
N
sites. The effective Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{A}
can be written as:

\mathcal{H}_{A}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2M}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{i}^{\dagger}H_{ij}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{j},

(S13)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{C^{\dagger}}}=(c_{1}^{\dagger},...,c_{M}^{\dagger},c_{1},...,c_{M})
.
\mathcal{H}_{A}
can now be diagonalized by defining
\Psi^{\dagger}=\tilde{\mathcal{C^{\dagger}}}S=(a_{M},...,a_{1},a_{1}^{\dagger},...,a_{M}^{\dagger})
with
S
such that
S^{\dagger}HS=D=diag(-\varepsilon_{M},...,-\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{1},...,\varepsilon_{M})
. This simple substitution yeilds:

\mathcal{H}_{A}=\sum_{ij=1}^{2M}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{i}^{\dagger}H_{ij}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{2M}\Psi_{j}^{\dagger}D_{jj}\Psi_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{j}^{\dagger}\varepsilon_{j}a_{j}-a_{j}\varepsilon_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}=2\sum_{j=1}^{M}\varepsilon_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}-E_{0},

(S14)

where
E_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\varepsilon_{j}
. Since the matrix
H
is not known, an alternate method is required to find the eigenvalues
\varepsilon
. This is where the concept of correlation matrix comes in. We’ll see later in this section how the eigenvalues of the truncated correlation matrix are related to the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}_{A}
. But first, we start by defining the correlation matrix and the truncated correlation matrix and describe how it’s calculated.

The correlation matrix for the entire system with size
N
is defined as:

C_{nm}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\left\langle c^{\dagger}c\right\rangle&\left\langle c^{\dagger}c^{\dagger}\right\rangle\\
\langle cc\rangle&\left\langle cc^{\dagger}\right\rangle\end{array}\right)_{nm}=\left\langle\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\dagger}\mathcal{C}_{m}\right\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\dagger}\mathcal{C}_{m}\right],

(S15)

where
\mathcal{C^{\dagger}}=(c_{1}^{\dagger},....,c_{N}^{\dagger},c_{1},....,c_{N})
. The elements of the correlation matrix have the form
\left\langle c_{n}^{\dagger}c_{m}\right\rangle=\left\langle\Phi_{0}\left|c_{n}^{\dagger}c_{m}\right|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle
. To evaluate these expectation values, we follow a procedure exactly similar to what we did in (
S14
). The Hamiltonian for the net system
\hat{H}
given by (
S2
) can be written as
\hat{H}=\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}H_{BdG}\mathcal{C}
where

H_{BdG}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\\
-B&-A\end{array}\right].

(S16)

Just as (
S13
), we can define
\Psi^{\dagger}=C^{\dagger}S=(a_{N},...,a_{1},a_{1}^{\dagger},...,a_{N}^{\dagger})
to diagonalize
H_{BdG}
, and we finally get:

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi_{0}\left|c_{n}^{\dagger}c_{m}\right|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{kl}\left\langle\Phi_{0}\left|\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}S_{kn}^{T}S_{ml}\Psi_{l}\right|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle

(S17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{kl\leq N}\left\langle\Phi_{0}\left|a_{N+1-k}S_{kn}^{T}S_{ml}a_{N+1-l}^{+}\right|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}S_{k,n}^{T}S_{m,k}.

Similarly we can get the other terms:

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi_{0}\left|c_{n}c_{m}\right|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle=\sum_{k>N,l\leq N}S_{n,k}S_{m,l}\left\langle\Phi_{0}\left|a_{k-N}a_{N+1-l}^{\dagger}\right|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}S_{2N+1-k,n}^{T}S_{m,k},

(S18)

and

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi_{0}\left|c_{n}^{\dagger}c_{m}^{\dagger}\right|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}S_{k,n}^{T}S_{m,2N+1-k}\mspace{50mu}\left\langle\Phi_{0}\left|c_{n}c_{m}^{\dagger}\right|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}S_{k+N,n}^{T}S_{m,k+N}.

(S19)

The above relations give us the entire correlation matrix
C
. The truncated correlation matrix
\tilde{C}
is a sub-matrix of the net correlation matrix
C
which is defined as follows:

\tilde{C}_{nm}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\left\langle c^{\dagger}c\right\rangle&\left\langle c^{\dagger}c^{\dagger}\right\rangle\\
\langle cc\rangle&\left\langle cc^{\dagger}\right\rangle\end{array}\right)_{n,m\in A}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho_{A}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{n}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{m}\right].

(S20)

Now that we have the truncated correlation matrix, our final task is to derive a relation between the eigenvalues of the reduced density matrix and the eigenvalues of the truncated correlation matrix. We start by substituting (
S12
) in (
S20
) to get:

\tilde{C}_{nm}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[K\exp\left(-\mathcal{H}_{A}\right)\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{n}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{m}\right].

(S21)

Substituting (
S14
) in (
S21
) gives:

\tilde{C}=K\operatorname{Tr}\left[\exp\left(-2\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}+E_{0}\right)\sum_{kl}\Psi_{k}^{+}S_{kn}^{T}S_{ml}\Psi_{l}\right].

(S22)

This can be simplified as:

\displaystyle\tilde{C}

\displaystyle=Ke^{E_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{2M}S_{kn}^{T}S_{mk}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\exp\left(-2\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}n_{j}\right)\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}\Psi_{k}\right]

(S23)

\displaystyle=Ke^{E_{0}}\sum_{k\leq M}S_{kn}^{T}S_{mk}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\exp\left(-2\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}n_{j}\right)\left(1-n_{k}\right)\right]+Ke^{E_{0}}\sum_{k>M}S_{kn}^{T}S_{mk}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\exp\left(-2\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}n_{j}\right)n_{k}\right],

where
n_{k}=a^{\dagger}_{k}a_{k}
. The value of K can be evaluated by imposing the condition
Tr[\rho_{A}]=1
, which is a fundamental property of density matrices. We can now uncover the following:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho_{A}\right]

\displaystyle=Ke^{E_{0}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\exp\left(-2\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}n_{j}\right)\right]=1

(S24)

\displaystyle=Ke^{E_{0}}\left(\prod_{l=1}^{M}\sum_{n_{l}=0,1}\right)\left\langle\Phi_{0}\left|\prod_{l=1}^{M}a_{l}^{n_{l}}e^{-2\varepsilon_{l}n_{l}}a_{l}^{\dagger n_{l}}\right|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle=Ke^{E_{0}}\prod_{l=1}^{M}\sum_{n_{l}=0,1}e^{-2\varepsilon_{l}n_{l}}=Ke^{E_{0}}\prod_{l=1}^{M}\left(1+e^{-2\varepsilon_{l}}\right)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow K=e^{-E_{0}}\prod_{l=1}^{M}\frac{1}{1+e^{-2\varepsilon_{l}}}.

Using the above two relations we get:

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{nm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\leq M}S_{kn}^{T}S_{mk}\left(1-\frac{e^{-2\varepsilon_{M+1-k}}}{1+e^{-2\varepsilon_{M+1-k}}}\right)+\sum_{k>M}S_{kn}^{T}S_{mk}\frac{e^{-2\varepsilon_{k-M}}}{1+e^{-2\varepsilon_{k-M}}}

(S25)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\leq M}S_{kn}^{T}S_{mk}\frac{1}{1+e^{-2\varepsilon_{M+1-k}}}+\sum_{k>M}S_{kn}^{T}S_{mk}\frac{1}{1+e^{2\varepsilon_{k-M}}}.

Comparing this with
H_{nm}
in (
S13
), which was,

\displaystyle H_{nm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}S_{nk}\lambda_{k}S_{km}^{T}=\sum_{k\leq M}S_{kn}^{T}S_{mk}\left(-\varepsilon_{M+1-k}\right)+\sum_{k>M}S_{kn}^{T}S_{mk}\varepsilon_{k-M},

(S26)

where
\lambda_{k}=D_{kk}
. Thus in terms of
\lambda_{k}
,

\displaystyle H_{nm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}S_{kn}^{T}S_{mk}

(S27)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{nm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{1}{1+e^{2\lambda_{k}}}S_{kn}^{T}S_{mk}=\sum_{k}\xi_{k}S_{kn}^{T}S_{mk}.

We can clearly see that
H
and
\tilde{C}
have the same set of eigenvectors and their eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
and
\varepsilon_{k}
are related as:

\begin{array}[]{l}\xi_{k}=\frac{1}{1+e^{2\lambda_{k}}},\mspace{50mu}\lambda_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1-\xi_{k}}{\xi_{k}}\right).\end{array}

(S28)

These relations finally lead us to the equation for entanglement entropy in terms of the eigenvalues of the truncated correlation matrix
\varepsilon_{k}
:

\displaystyle S_{ent}

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho_{A}\log\rho_{A}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left[\frac{2\varepsilon_{k}}{1+e^{2\varepsilon_{k}}}+\log\left(1+e^{-2\varepsilon_{k}}\right)\right]

(S29)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left[\xi_{k}\log\xi_{k}+\left(1-\xi_{k}\right)\log\left(1-\xi_{k}\right)\right].

.2
The Keldysh NEGF approach

.2.1
Current calculations

First, we start with the assumption that the leads are large enough so that they can be considered semi-infinite, and thus be effectively modelled by infinite dimensional matrices. This assumption allows using the NEGF approach by partitioning the channel and the leads and working with the channel
Green’s function and incorporating the leads via self energies.
The retarded channel Green’s function can now
be written as:

G^{r}(E)=\left[(E+i\eta)I-H_{BdG}-\Sigma_{L}^{r}-\Sigma_{R}^{r}\right]^{-1},

(B.1)

where E is the free variable energy, and I is the identity
matrix of the dimension of the Hamiltonian,
\eta
is a small positive damping parameter, and
\Sigma^{r}_{L}
and
\Sigma^{r}_{R}
represent the retarted self energies for the semi-infinite contacts.

Figure S1:
NEGF

The self energies can be represented in two possible choice of basis: (i) In the eigenbasis of the real-space Hamiltonian, (ii) the eigen basis with wide-band approximation, where the self energy is encapsulated via a parameter
\Gamma_{L/R}
. In the basis of the real-space hamiltonian, the self-energy matrices can be evaluated by a straightforward calculation of the matrix elements
\Sigma^{r}_{\alpha}\left(i,i\right)=\sigma_{\alpha\in L/R}
:

\sigma_{\alpha\in L/R}(E)=\sum_{k}\frac{\left|\tau_{\alpha,k}\right|^{2}}{\left(E-\epsilon_{k\alpha}+i\eta\right)}=P\int d\epsilon_{k\alpha}\frac{\left|\tau_{\alpha,k}\right|^{2}}{\left(E-\epsilon_{k\alpha}\right)}-i\pi\int d\epsilon_{k\alpha}\left|\tau_{\alpha,k}\right|^{2}\delta\left(E-\epsilon_{k\alpha}\right),

(B.2)

where P stands for the Cauchy principal value. Recasting this integral into a real and an imaginary part (
\sigma_{\alpha}=\epsilon_{\alpha}\left(E\right)-i\gamma_{\alpha}/2
) provides a better physical picture , with the imaginary part
\gamma_{\alpha}=i\left(\sigma^{r}_{\alpha}-\sigma^{a}_{\alpha}\right)
representing the level broadening, and the real part
\epsilon_{\alpha}
calculated from the principal value integral.

In this work, we stick to the wide-band approximation basis. Under this approximation,
\tau_{k\alpha,m}
is independent of energy and thus the principal value integral representing the real-part of the self energy vanishes and we are just left with the
broadening matrix
\Gamma_{\alpha}\left(E\right)
such that the self energies can be represented as
\Sigma^{r}_{\alpha}=-i\Gamma_{\alpha}/2
, which can be treated as
an input parameter. Once we are done calculating the self energies, we
need to evaluate the “lesser” Green’s function:

G^{<}(E)=G^{r}(E)\left(\Sigma_{L}^{<}(E)+\Sigma_{R}^{<}(E)\right)G^{a}(E),

(B.3)

where
G^{a}
is the advanced Green’s function (hermitian conjugate of the retarded Green’s function calculated
from (
B.1
)). Calculating the lesser Green’s function is equivalent to calculating the electron (hole) correlation function. Here,
\Sigma^{<}_{L,R}(E)
represent the
in-scattering functions from leads L and R respectively which can be evaluated using:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\alpha}^{<}(E)=-\left[\Sigma_{\alpha}^{r}(E)-\Sigma_{\alpha}^{a}(E)\right]f_{\alpha}(E)=i\Gamma_{\alpha}(E)f_{\alpha}(E),

(B.4)

where
f_{\alpha}=f(E-\mu_{\alpha})
represents the Fermi-Dirac distribution in either leads
\alpha=L(R)
with a chemical potential
\mu_{\alpha}
. In this formulation, all different components of the currents can then be deduced from the current operator formula. The elements of the current operator matrix can be deduced using the correlation function and the hopping elements of the Hamiltonian. A rigorous but straightforward calculation based on fundamental consideration gives the current operator matrix:

\displaystyle I_{L}^{op}(E)=\frac{e}{h}\left[G^{r}(E)\Sigma_{L}^{<}-\Sigma_{L}^{<}G^{a}(E)\right.\left.+G^{<}(E)\Sigma_{L}^{a}(E)-\Sigma_{L}^{r}(E)G^{<}(E)\right].

(B.5)

Taking the trace of the above equation gives the net charge current, which is given by the current formula:

I_{L}(E)=\frac{ie}{h}\text{ Trace }\left[\Gamma_{L}(E)f_{L}(E)\left(G^{r}(E)-G^{a}(E)\right)+\Gamma_{L}(E)G^{<}(E)\right].

(B.6)

The above formula can be further simplified by the notation
introduced in with the spectral function
A(E)=i(G^{r}-G^{a})
and the electron correlation matrix
G^{n}(E)=-iG^{<}(E)
as

I_{L}(E)=\frac{e}{h}\text{ Trace }\left[\Gamma_{L}(E)f_{L}(E)A(E)-\Gamma_{L}(E)G^{n}(E)\right].

(B.7)

Using simple manipulations as given below we get:

\displaystyle A(E)

\displaystyle=i\left(G^{r}-G^{a}\right)=iG^{r}\left[\left(G^{a}\right)^{-1}-\left(G^{r}\right)^{-1}\right]G^{a}=iG^{r}\left[\Sigma^{r}-\Sigma^{a}\right]G^{a}=G^{r}\Gamma G^{a},

(B.8)

where
\Gamma(E)=\Gamma_{L}(E)+\Gamma_{R}(E)
. Using the above in conjunction with (
B.3
) and (
B.4
), along with the properties
of the trace operation, yields the Landauer transmission
formula for the current as

I_{L}=\int dETrace\left[\Gamma_{L}G^{r}\Gamma_{R}G^{a}\right]\left(f_{L}(E)-f_{R}(E)\right),

(B.9)

where
f_{L(R)}(E)
represents the Fermi-Dirac distribution
in either lead. However, in the case involving superconductors, either functioning as a contact or as the central system or both,
the net current through the contact
\alpha
is the difference
between electron and hole currents in Nambu space. We
can now use the full fledged current operator in (
B.5
) to
evaluate the current from first principles as

I_{L}^{op}(E)=\frac{e}{h}\tau_{z}\left[G^{r}(E)\Sigma_{L}^{<}(E)-\Sigma_{L}^{<}(E)G^{a}(E)+G^{<}(E)\Sigma_{L}^{a}(E)-\Sigma_{L}^{r}(E)G^{<}(E)\right],

(B.10)

where
\tau_{z}=\sigma_{z}\otimes I_{N\times N}
, where is the Pauli-z matrix and is the identity matrix of dimension
N\times N
. The next step is to take a trace of the above equation,
which in general need not take the form of (
B.6
), typically
when the contacts are superconducting due to the non-diagonal
structure of
\Sigma_{L(R)}
, and may lead to erroneous results, specifically when evaluating the Josephson currents. However, in our case, as the contacts are normal, and hence diagonal, (
B.10
) indeed takes the form of (
B.6
) with the net current becoming

I^{\alpha}=\int dE\frac{(I_{\alpha}^{(e)}-I_{\alpha}^{(h)})}{2},

(B.11)

where each current
I^{e(h)}_{\alpha}
can be evaluated separately using
the form in (
B.6
) or (
B.7
). The factor of a half comes
since the original Hamiltonian been doubled while writing the BdG Hamiltonian in order to
write it consistently in the Nambu space. It is then instructive
in our context to note that various matrices defined within the approach will have a matrix structure due to the electron-hole Nambu space. In particular, the contact broadening matrices etc., can be written with a general diagonal structure (due to the diagonal structure of normal contacts in Nambu space) as
\Gamma_{\alpha}=\Gamma^{ee}_{\alpha}+\Gamma^{hh}_{\alpha}
, where the superscripts
ee(hh)
represent the electron (hole) diagonal part of the self energy or broadening matrix. Following this and using the above observations on the current operator
formula in (
B.7
), we obtain the electron (hole) current
across the TS as a sum of three components:

\displaystyle I_{L}^{e(h)}(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{h}\left(\text{ Trace }\left(\Gamma_{L}^{ee(hh)}G^{r}\Gamma_{R}^{ee(hh)}G^{a}\right)\left[f_{L}^{ee(hh)}(E)-f_{R}^{ee(hh)}(E)\right]\right)\longrightarrow(i)

(B.12)

\displaystyle+\frac{e}{h}\left(\text{ Trace }\left(\Gamma_{L}^{ee(hh)}G^{r}\Gamma_{L}^{hh(ee)}G^{a}\right)\left[f_{L}^{ee(hh)}(E)-f_{L}^{hh(ee)}(E)\right]\right)\longrightarrow(ii)

\displaystyle+\frac{e}{h}\left(\text{ Trace }\left(\Gamma_{L}^{ee(hh)}G^{r}\Gamma_{R}^{hh(ee)}G^{a}\right)\left[f_{L}^{ee(hh)}(E)-f_{R}^{hh(ee)}(E)\right]\right),\longrightarrow(iii)

where the term (i) represents the direct transmission process of
either the electron or the hole, (ii) represents the direct
Andreev transmission and (iii) represents the crossed
Andreev transmission. At this point it is worth noting
that
f^{ee}_{\alpha}=f(E-\mu_{\alpha})
,
f^{hh}_{\alpha}=f(E+\mu_{\alpha})
. The matrix structure of the broadening matrix
\Gamma
itself is diagonal such that
\Gamma^{ee}_{L}=\Gamma_{L}(1,1)=\gamma
,
\Gamma^{hh}_{L}=\Gamma_{L}(2,2)=\gamma
,
\Gamma^{ee}_{R}=\Gamma_{R}(2N-1,2N-1)=\gamma
,
\Gamma^{hh}_{R}=\Gamma_{R}(2N,2N)=\gamma
and zeros otherwise. The Andreev transmission can then be written as
T_{A}(E)=\gamma^{2}|G^{r,eh}_{11}(E)|^{2}
and the crossed Andreeev transmission as
T_{CA}(E)=\gamma^{2}|G^{r,eh}_{1N}(E)|^{2}

the direct transmission can be written as
T_{D}(E)=\gamma^{2}|G^{r,ee}_{1N}(E)|^{2}
. Here
G^{r,ee(hh)}_{ij}

represents the i, j element of the electron (hole) diagonal block of the retarded Green’s function in Nambu space and
G^{r,eh(he)}_{ij}
represents that of the off-diagonal block of the retarded Green’s function in Nambu space.

.3
Local and non-local conductances

.3.1
Floating superconductor configuration

We start with the current formula for a generic bias situation, (
V_{L}
,
V_{R}
) for the left and right contacts with
\mu_{L}=eV_{L}
and
\mu_{R}=eV_{R}

The current through one of the contacts has contributions from both electron and hole flow. The net current is given by:

I^{L}=\frac{I_{L}^{(e)}-I_{L}^{(h)}}{2}.

(B.13)

The individual electron and hole components can then be derived using:

\displaystyle I_{L}^{(e)}=-\frac{e}{h}

\displaystyle\left\{\int dET_{A}^{(e)}(E)\left[f\left(E-eV_{L}\right)-f\left(E+eV_{L}\right)\right]\right.

(B.14)

\displaystyle+\int dET_{CAR}^{(e)}(E)\left[f\left(E-eV_{L}\right)-f\left(E+eV_{R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\left.+\int dwET_{D}^{(e)}(E)\left[f\left(E-eV_{L}\right)-f\left(E-eV_{R}\right)\right]\right\},

\displaystyle I_{L}^{(h)}=-\frac{e}{h}

\displaystyle\left\{\int dET_{A}^{(h)}(E)\left[f\left(E+eV_{L}\right)-f\left(E-eV_{L}\right)\right]\right.

(B.15)

\displaystyle+\int dET_{CAR}^{(h)}(E)\left[f\left(E+eV_{L}\right)-f\left(E-eV_{R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\left.+\int dET_{D}^{(h)}(E)\left[f\left(E+eV_{L}\right)-f\left(E+eV_{R}\right)\right]\right\}.

Here,
I^{(e)}_{L}
and
I^{(h)}_{L}
can be shown to be equal and opposite using symmetry conditions and especially:

\displaystyle T_{CAR}^{(e)}(E)\equiv T^{(h)}_{CAR}(E),\mspace{50mu}T_{D}^{(e)}(E)\equiv T_{D}^{(h)}(-E).

(B.16)

It is thus sufficient to consider either one of
I^{(e)}_{L}
and
I^{(h)}_{L}
while calculating the net current through a contact. Let us now consider the conductance matrix:

\mathrm{G}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}G_{LL}&G_{LR}\\
G_{RL}&G_{RR}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\left.\frac{\partial I_{L}}{\partial V_{L}}\right|_{V_{R}=0}&\left.\frac{\partial I_{L}}{\partial V_{R}}\right|_{V_{L}=0}\\
\left.\frac{\partial I_{R}}{\partial V_{L}}\right|_{V_{R}=0}&\left.\frac{\partial I_{R}}{\partial V_{R}}\right|_{V_{L}=0}.\end{array}\right)

(B.17)

Now let us consider only the electron current for now since
I_{L}=I^{(e)}_{L}
. Using this fact we can evaluate the Local conductance as:

\displaystyle G_{LL}

\displaystyle\equiv\left.\frac{\partial I_{L}}{\partial V_{L}}\right|_{V_{R}\equiv 0}

(B.18)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{e}{h}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial V_{L}}\left[\int dET_{A}(E)[f(E-eV_{L})-f(E+eV_{L})]\right.\right.+\int dET_{CAR}(E)[f(E-eV_{L})-f(E)]

\displaystyle\mspace{450mu}+\left.\left.\int dET_{D}(E)[f(E-eV_{L})-f(E)]\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{e}{h}\left[\int dE\right.T_{A}(E)\left[\frac{\partial f\left(E-eV_{L}\right)}{\partial V_{L}}-\frac{\partial f(E+eV_{L})}{\partial V_{L}}\right]+\int dET_{CAR}(E)\left[\frac{\partial f(E-eV_{L})}{\partial V_{L}}\right]

\displaystyle\mspace{450mu}+\left.\int dET_{D}(E)\left[\frac{\partial f(E-eV_{L})}{\partial V_{L}}\right]\right].

At zero temperature, the
f\left(x\right)=\Theta\left(-x\right)
where
\Theta
is the Heaviside step function. This implies
f\left(E-eV\right)=\Theta\left(eV-E\right)
and similarly we can write the other terms. Since the derivative of the heaviside function is the Dirac Delta function, we get:

\displaystyle\left.G_{LL}(V)\right|_{T\rightarrow 0}\equiv\frac{e^{2}}{h}\left[T_{A}(E=eV)+T_{A}(E=-eV)\right.+\left.T_{CAR}(E=eV)+T_{D}(E=eV)\right].

(B.19)

Now we can similarly get the non local conductance.

\displaystyle G_{LR}=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial I_{L}}{\partial V_{R}}\right|_{V_{L}\equiv 0}

(B.20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e}{h}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial V_{R}}\left[\int dET_{CAR}(E)[f(E)-f(E+eV_{R})]\right.\right.\left.\left.+\int dET_{D}(E)\left[f\left(E\right)-f\left(E-eV_{R}\right)\right]\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e}{h}\left[\int dET_{CAR}(E)\left[\frac{-\partial f(E+eV_{R})}{\partial V_{R}}\right]\right.+\left.\int dET_{D}(E)\left[\frac{-\partial f(E-eV_{R})}{\partial V_{R}}\right].\right]

Once again, using the property of fermi dirac functions as
T\rightarrow 0
as
f(x)=\Theta(-x)
we get:

\displaystyle\left.G_{LR}(V)\right|_{T\rightarrow 0}\equiv\frac{e^{2}}{h}\left[T_{D}(E=eV)-T_{CAR}(E=-eV)\right].

(B.21)

.3.2
Grounded superconductor configuration

When the proximitizing superconductor is connected to the ground, current can flow into the superconducting terminal as well. This leads to an additional self-energy term (
\Sigma_{S}
) in the Green’s function. The Green’s function for the grounded setup is thus given by :

G^{r}(E)=\left[(E+i\eta)I-H_{BdG}-\Sigma_{L}^{r}-\Sigma_{R}^{r}-\Sigma_{S}\right]^{-1},

(B.22)

where
\Sigma_{S}
can be derived using a method similar (
B.2
) and is given by:

\Sigma_{S}(E)=-\gamma\frac{E+\Delta\tau_{x}}{\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-E^{2}}},

(B.23)

where
\gamma
represents the coupling strength between the Kitaev chain and the superconducting contact, and
\tau_{x}
represents the Pauli-sigma
x
matrix.

Due to the presence of the superconducting contact, as we’ll see below, an additional term gets added to the previously derived formula for local conductance (
B.19
), and no additional term gets added to the non-local conductance formula (
B.21
).

Starting with the general current operator for the left contact (
B.10
), we get the electron current for the left contact as:

\displaystyle I_{L}^{e}(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{h}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\Sigma_{L}^{<}(E)\left(G^{r}(E)-G^{a}(E)\right)\right.\left.-G^{<}(E)\left(\Sigma_{L}^{a}-\Sigma_{L}^{r}\right)\right]

(B.24)

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{h}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\Gamma_{L}f_{L}\left(G^{r}(E)\Gamma G^{a}(E)\right)\right.\left.+iG^{r}(E)\left(\Sigma_{L}^{<}+\Sigma_{R}^{<}+\Sigma_{S}^{<}\right)G^{a}(E)\Gamma_{L}\right],

where
\Sigma^{<}_{S}
is defined by the (
B.4
) with the Fermi energy of the superconducting contact given by
f_{S}
. The broadening matrix
\Gamma
is the sum of the broadening matrices for left,right and superconducting contacts:
\Gamma=\Gamma_{L}+\Gamma_{R}+\Gamma_{S}
. On further expanding the terms in (
B.24
) we get,

I_{L}^{e}(E)=\frac{e}{h}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\Gamma_{L}f_{L}G^{r}(E)\left(\Gamma_{L}+\Gamma_{R}+\Gamma_{S}\right)G^{a}(E)+iG^{r}(E)\left(i\Gamma_{L}f_{L}(E)+i\Gamma_{R}f_{R}(E)+i\Gamma_{S}f_{S}(E)\right)G^{a}(E)\Gamma_{L}\right].

(B.25)

The additional terms due to the presence of the superconducting contact are:

\frac{e}{h}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\underbrace{\Gamma_{L}f_{L}G^{r}(E)\Gamma_{S}G^{a}(E)}_{(\mathrm{i})}-\underbrace{G^{r}(E)\Gamma_{S}f_{S}(E)G^{a}(E)\Gamma_{L}}_{(\mathrm{ii})}\right].

(B.26)

The term (i) here has a
V_{L}
dependence since
f_{L}=f(E-eV_{L})
, whereas the term (ii) has neither
V_{L}
nor
V_{R}
dependence. Since the first term has a
V_{L}
dependence it adds an additional term to the local conductance formula (
B.19
):

G^{Gnd}_{LL}(V_{L})=\frac{e}{h}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\Gamma_{L}\frac{df_{L}}{dV_{L}}G^{r}(E)\Gamma_{S}G^{a}(E)\right].

(B.27)

Using the fact that the derivative of Fermi function at zero temperature is the Dirac-delta function, we get the additional term to local conductance as:

G^{Gnd}_{LL}(V)=\frac{e^{2}}{h}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\Gamma_{L}G^{r}(V)\Gamma_{S}G^{a}(V)\right].

(B.28)

Since neither of the terms in (
B.26
) have
V_{R}
dependence, no additional term gets added to the non-local conductance equation (
B.21
) on introducing the grounded superconductor.
