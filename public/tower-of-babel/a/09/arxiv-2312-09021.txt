Odd moments and adding fractions
Thomas F. Bloom and Vivian Kuperberg

Abstract
. We prove near-optimal upper bounds for the odd moments of the distribution of coprime residues in short intervals, confirming a conjecture of Montgomery and Vaughan. As an application we prove near-optimal upper bounds for the average of the refined singular series in the Hardy-Littlewood conjectures concerning the number of prime k k -tuples for k k odd. The main new ingredient is a near-optimal upper bound for the number of solutions to ∑ 1 ≤ i ≤ k a i q i ∈ ℤ \sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\tfrac{a_{i}}{q_{i}}\in\mathbb{Z} when k k is odd, with gcd ⁡ ( a i , q i ) = 1 \gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1 and restrictions on the size of the numerators and denominators, that is of independent interest.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we prove two new results related to odd moments in number theory, which have received much less attention than their even counterparts. Both of our upper bounds are within logarithmic factors of the conjectured truth. Our main new tool is a new, essentially best possible, upper bound for the number of solutions to a rational linear equation with an odd number of summands.

1.1.
Distribution of reduced residues

Let
q,h\geq 1
and, for any
k\geq 1
, let

M_{k}(q,h)=\sum_{1\leq n\leq q}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq m<n+h\\
\gcd(m,q)=1\end{subarray}}1-\frac{\phi(q)}{q}h\right)^{k}.

That is, if we choose an interval of length
h
uniformly at random, then
M_{k}(q,h)
is the
k
th central moment of the proportion of the interval which is coprime to
q
(which has expected value
\tfrac{\phi(q)}{q}h
).

Montgomery and Vaughan
[
15
]
showed that for any fixed
k\geq 1
1

f\ll g

f=O(g)

C

.

M_{k}(q,h)\ll\left(\frac{\phi(q)}{q}h+\left(\frac{\phi(q)}{q}h\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}\right)q.

This is sharp for even

, but not for odd

. For odd

Montgomery and Vaughan proved the stronger bound

M_{k}(q,h)\ll\left(\frac{\phi(q)}{q}h+\left(\frac{\phi(q)}{q}h\right)^{\frac{k}{2}-\frac{1}{7k}}\right)q,

but speculated that a much stronger bound should be true, with the exponent in the second summand replaced by
\frac{k-1}{2}
(when
k
is odd). In this paper we (almost) prove this conjecture, losing only a power of

.

Theorem 1
.

Let

. For any odd

In fact, the method of Montgomery and Vaughan allows for an asymptotic formula for
M_{k}
when
k
is even (this was sketched in
[
15
]
and proven in detail by Montgomery and Soundararajan
[

). We are unable to prove an asymptotic formula for
M_{k}
when
k
is odd, although we believe one should be possible. As an indication of the difficulty of this, we note that we do not even have a heuristic guess what the leading constant of the main term of such an asymptotic should be (unlike in the even case when there is a clearly dominant ‘diagonal term’). Nonetheless, we believe (and computational experimentation suggests) that there is a main term of order
(\frac{\phi(q)}{q}h+(\tfrac{\phi(q)}{q}h)^{\frac{k-1}{2}})q
, and hence we believe that Theorem

is essentially best possible, in that it is only improvable by a factor of
(\log h)^{O(1)}

1.2.
Distribution of prime
k
-tuples

For any
k
-tuple
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}
of distinct integers one can ask about the count of
n\leq N
such that
n+d_{i}
are prime for all
1\leq i\leq k
. Hardy and Littlewood
[
11
]
gave a convincing heuristic that produces a precise conjecture for the asymptotics of such a count. As usual when dealing with primes, the statements are simplified if we consider a weighted count instead, replacing
1_{\mathrm{prime}}
by the smoother von Mangoldt function
\Lambda
. In this form, the Hardy-Littlewood conjecture states that

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\leq N}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Lambda(n+d_{i})\sim\mathfrak{S}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})

(for any fixed
k
-tuple
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})
of distinct integers, as
N\to\infty
), where

\mathfrak{S}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-k}\left(1-\frac{\nu_{p}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})}{p}\right)

and for any prime
p
we write
\nu_{p}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})
for the number of residue classes modulo
p
occupied by the
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}
. Gallagher
[
9
]
proved the asymptotic that for any fixed
k\geq 1
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\leq h\\
d_{i}\textrm{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{S}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\sim h^{k}.

(That is, on average
\mathfrak{S}
tends to
1
as
h\to\infty
.)

This so-called ‘singular series’
\mathfrak{S}
is an object of great interest in the study of primes – aside from the obvious interest in counting prime tuples, the study of
\mathfrak{S}
is also important in understanding the distribution of primes in short intervals (see for example
[
14
]
) and in predicting the congruence classes of consecutive primes (see for example
[
13
]
).

Since, by the prime number theorem,
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\leq N}\Lambda(n)\sim 1
, for more precise information one should subtract the ‘main term’
1
from each
\Lambda
. The corresponding refined form of the Hardy-Littlewood conjecture then states that

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\leq N}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\Lambda(n+d_{i})-1)\sim\mathfrak{S}_{0}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})

where for
\mathcal{D}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}),

\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D})=\sum_{X\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}(-1)^{k-\left\lvert X\right\rvert}\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{X}).

Here we use the notational convention that
\mathcal{D}_{X}
denotes the tuple consisting only of those elements of
\mathcal{D}
with indices in
X
.
Using
\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D})=\sum_{X\subseteq\mathcal{D}}\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
one can recover information about
\mathfrak{S}
from asymptotics for
\mathfrak{S}_{0}
, but the latter yields more information, and the refined singular series
\mathfrak{S}_{0}
is more difficult to study. (In particular note that, unlike
\mathfrak{S}
, the refined series
\mathfrak{S}_{0}
can take negative values.)

Montgomery and Soundararajan
[
14
]
considered the sum

R_{k}(h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\leq h\\
d_{i}\textrm{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{S}_{0}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}).

When
k
is even
Montgomery and Soundararajan
[
14
]
proved an asymptotic for
R_{k}(h)
of the shape

R_{k}(h)\sim\mu_{k}(h\log h)^{k/2}

for some explicit constant
\mu_{k}>0
, which implies Gallagher’s estimate for the average of
\mathfrak{S}
. When
k
is odd they proved, for any
\epsilon>0
,

R_{k}(h)\ll_{k,\epsilon}h^{\frac{k}{2}-\frac{1}{7k}+\epsilon}.

It has been conjectured, by Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan in
[
13
]
and by the second author in
[
17
]
, that when
k\geq 3
is an odd integer and
h>1
we have

R_{k}(h)\asymp(\log h)^{\frac{k+1}{2}}h^{\frac{k-1}{2}}.

The second author proved in
[
17
]
that
R_{3}(h)\ll(\log h)^{O(1)}h
(and obtained bounds of comparable quality when
k=5
in the analogous function field setting). We extend this to all odd
k
.

Theorem 2
.

If
k\geq 3
is an odd integer and
h>1
then

R_{k}(h)\ll(\log h)^{O_{k}(1)}h^{\frac{k-1}{2}}.

As with Theorem
1
, and in keeping with the previous conjecture for the growth of
R_{k}(h)
, we believe (but cannot prove) that this upper bound is essentially the best possible, up to the exponent of
\log h
.

In Section
3.3
we calculate exact expressions for the expected main terms of
R_{k}(h)
in terms of
M_{j}(q,h)
(via an auxiliary expression
V_{j}(q,h)
). Unfortunately, as mentioned above we have no good heuristics for the asymptotics for
M_{j}(q,h)
, so these expressions are currently of limited value. The asymptotics for
M_{j}(q,h)
are, heuristically, closely related to the asymptotics for the number of primes in short intervals. The three problems of odd moments for primes in short intervals,
M_{k}(q,h)
for odd
k
, and
R_{k}(h)
for odd
k
, are strongly linked, in that a reasonable conjecture for the asymptotics of any of these would produce a conjectural asymptotic for the other two.

1.3.
Adding fractions

The main new idea is an improved upper bound for the number of solutions to a rational linear equation with an odd number of summands, of independent interest, which we expect will have other applications. The bound we prove is fairly technical, but the following simpler consequence is representative.

Theorem 3
.

Let
1\leq n\leq Q
, and let
k\geq 3
be an odd integer. The number of solutions to

\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{a_{k}}{q_{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\textrm{ where }\quad\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\textrm{ for }1\leq i\leq k,

with
\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert\leq n
and
q_{i}\leq Q
for
1\leq i\leq k
, is

\ll_{k}(\log Q)^{O_{k}(1)}n^{\frac{k+1}{2}}Q^{\frac{k-1}{2}}.

A bound of the shape of Theorem
3

17

2.
Adding an odd number of fractions

The new device which allows for our main results is the following upper bound on the number of solutions to a rational equation with an odd number of summands.

Theorem 4
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer. Let
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{2k+1}\geq 1
and
A_{1},\ldots,A_{2k+1}\subseteq[-1,1]
be intervals of lengths
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{2k+1}
respectively, where
\delta_{i}\geq 1/Q_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 2k+1
.

There exists some
X\subseteq\{1,\ldots,2k+1\}
with
\left\lvert X\right\rvert=k+1
such that the number of solutions to

\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{a_{2k+1}}{q_{2k+1}}\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\textrm{ where }\quad\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\textrm{ for }1\leq i\leq 2k+1,

with
a_{i}/q_{i}\in A_{i}
and
q_{i}\leq Q_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 2k+1
, is

\ll_{k}(\log(Q_{1}\cdots Q_{2k+1}))^{O_{k}(1)}\left(\prod_{i\in X}\delta_{i}\right)Q_{1}\cdots Q_{2k+1}.

Theorem
3
is an immediate corollary. The proof of Theorem
4
is quite technical, and so we will first give sketch proofs of three special cases: first when
k=3
and all the intervals
A_{i}
are identical and centred at the origin, secondly when
k=3
without any restriction on the intervals, and finally when
k=5
, the intervals are centred on the origin, and the denominators
q_{i}
have a particular arithmetic structure. These proofs offer representative illustrations of the main ideas, and so we hope will help the reader to follow the proof of Theorem
4
itself, which is a more technical and general elaboration of the same theme.

Our technique also proves the following slight variant of Theorem
4
:

Theorem 5
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer. Let
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{2k+1}\geq 1
and
B_{i}\subseteq[1,Q_{i}]
for
1\leq i\leq 2k+1
. There exists a partition
\{1,\ldots,2k+1\}=X\sqcup Y
with
|X|=k+1
and
|Y|=k
such that the number of solutions to

\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{a_{2k+1}}{q_{2k+1}}\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\textrm{ where }\quad\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\textrm{ for }1\leq i\leq 2k+1,

with
a_{i}\in B_{i}
and
q_{i}\leq Q_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 2k+1
, is

\ll_{k}(\log(Q_{1}\cdots Q_{2k+1}))^{O_{k}(1)}\left(\prod_{i\in X}|A_{i}|\right)\left(\prod_{j\in Y}Q_{j}\right).

We omit the proof of Theorem
5
, which follows along essentially identical lines to the proof of Theorem
4
. In fact, the special cases that we sketch when
k=3
or
k=5
and all intervals are centered at the origin are also special cases of Theorem
5
.

We make essential use of the notion of
relative greatest common divisors
: this is the observation that
k
-tuples
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}
of natural numbers can be parameterised by
2^{k}
-tuples of natural numbers
g_{I}
, where
I
ranges over all subsets of
\{1,\ldots,k\}
, where
g_{\emptyset}=1
, with the properties that

q_{i}=\prod_{I\ni i}g_{I}\textrm{ for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}

and

\gcd(g_{I},g_{J})=1\textrm{ unless }I\subseteq J\textrm{ or }J\subseteq I.

For example, when
k=2
this is just the fact that pairs of natural numbers
(a,b)
are parameterised by triples
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},d)
with
\gcd(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=1
, where
a=a^{\prime}d
and
b=b^{\prime}d
, by taking
d=\gcd(a,b)
.

The concept of relative greatest common divisors is an old one within number theory, although perhaps not as widely known as it deserves; we give a brief history, as well as details of the construction and proofs of the necessary properties, in Appendix
A
. We recommend the reader new to relative greatest common divisors peruse this appendix before continuing with the rest of this section.

2.1.
The case
k=3

As a warm-up, we first show that the number of solutions to

(1)

\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}+\frac{a_{2}}{q_{2}}+\frac{a_{3}}{q_{3}}=0\quad\textrm{ where }\quad\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\textrm{ for }1\leq i\leq 3,

with
\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert\leq n
, and
q_{i}\leq Q
, is
O_{\epsilon}(n^{2}Q^{1+\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Using relative greatest common divisors, there exist integers
g_{I}
for
I\subseteq\{1,2,3\}
such that
\gcd(g_{I},g_{J})=1
, unless either
I\subseteq J
or
J\subseteq I
, with

q_{1}=g_{123}g_{12}g_{13}g_{1}\quad q_{2}=g_{123}g_{12}g_{23}g_{2}\quad q_{3}=g_{123}g_{13}g_{23}g_{3}.

Without loss of generality, relabelling if necessary, we can assume that
g_{23}\leq g_{12}
. Multiplying both sides of (
1
) by
\prod_{I\subseteq\{1,2,3\}}g_{I}
we have

a_{1}g_{23}g_{2}g_{3}+a_{2}g_{13}g_{1}g_{3}+a_{3}g_{12}g_{1}g_{2}=0.

Note that
g_{1}
divides the second two summands, so
g_{1}\mid a_{1}g_{23}g_{2}g_{3}
. On the other hand,
\gcd(a_{1},g_{1})=\gcd(a_{1},q_{1})=1
, and
\gcd(g_{1},g_{23}g_{2}g_{3})=1
by properties of relative greatest common divisors, and hence this forces
g_{1}=1
. We can similarly deduce that
g_{2}=g_{3}=1
. The condition (
1
) has now been simplified to

a_{1}g_{23}+a_{2}g_{13}+a_{3}g_{12}=0.

We now choose, at a cost of
Q
, the value of
q_{1}=g_{123}g_{12}g_{13}
. By the divisor bound we can now also determine the individual values of
g_{123},g_{12},g_{13}
, inflating the number of choices made by an additional factor of
O_{\epsilon}(Q^{\epsilon})
.

We further fix the values of
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
, at an additional cost of
O(n^{2})
. The total number of choices made thus far is
O_{\epsilon}(n^{2}Q^{1+\epsilon})
. We have used up our budget, and therefore need to show that the remaining unknown values of
a_{3}
and
g_{23}
can be determined from the information already known. For this we note that

a_{1}g_{23}+a_{2}g_{13}\equiv 0\pmod{g_{12}}.

Since
\gcd(a_{1},g_{12})=1
(and the values of
a_{1},a_{2},g_{12},g_{13}
are all known) we can deduce the value of
g_{23}\pmod{g_{12}}
. Recalling our assumption that
g_{23}\leq g_{12}
it follows that
g_{23}
itself is known, and now
a_{3}
can be recovered, as the only remaining unknown. This concludes the proof.

We note that there is an alternative way to proceed: we could instead fix the values of
a_{1},a_{2},q_{3}
, and then recover
a_{3}
and
g_{12}
, which is accomplished by the divisor bound since

a_{3}g_{12}=-a_{1}g_{23}-a_{2}g_{13}

is known. This method is perhaps simpler (and does not require the assumption that
g_{23}\leq g_{12}
) but the generalisation to other odd
k>3
is (or at least seems to be) quantitatively poorer than the previous method.

2.2.
A harder case when
k=3

For our applications it is important to handle the case when the constraint
\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert\leq n
is replaced by
a_{i}/q_{i}\in I_{i}
, where
I_{i}\subseteq[-1,1]
is some fixed interval. We will now explain how this generalisation is handled when
k=3
.

Let
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}\geq 1
and
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\subseteq[-1,1]
be some fixed intervals of lengths
\delta_{i}\gg 1/Q_{i}
. We will show that the number of solutions to

\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}+\frac{a_{2}}{q_{2}}+\frac{a_{3}}{q_{3}}=0\quad\textrm{ where }\quad\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\textrm{ for }1\leq i\leq 3

with
\frac{a_{i}}{q_{i}}\in A_{i}
and
q_{i}\leq Q_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 3
is
O_{\epsilon}(\delta_{1}\delta_{2}(Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3})^{1+\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
, assuming
\delta_{1}\geq\delta_{2}\geq\delta_{3}
, say. (The previous subsection corresponds to the special case when all
Q_{i}=Q
and
A_{i}=A
, some interval of width
O(n/Q)
centred at
0

As above, we decompose the
q_{i}
into relative greatest common divisors
g_{I}
. At the cost of only
(\log Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3})^{O(1)}
we can assume that each
g_{I}\in[Q_{I},2Q_{I}]
for some known integers
Q_{I}\geq 1
. We can assume without loss of generality that
Q_{23}\geq Q_{12}
. We choose the values of
g_{123},g_{12},g_{13}
at a cost of
O_{\epsilon}(Q_{1}^{1+\epsilon})
. We further choose an interval of width
O(1/Q_{1})
which contains
a_{1}/q_{1}
and an interval of width
O(1/Q_{2})
which contains
a_{2}/q_{2}
, which costs a further
O(\delta_{1}\delta_{2}Q_{1}Q_{2})
, since
A_{i}
can be divided into
O(\delta_{i}Q_{i})
many sub-intervals of width
O(1/Q_{i})
. Note that then, since we know an interval of width
O(1/q_{1})
which contains
a_{1}/q_{1}
, and the denominator
q_{1}
is known, we also know
a_{1}
(after perhaps an additional cost of
O(1)
). We have spent a total of
O_{\epsilon}(\delta_{1}\delta_{2}Q_{1}^{2+\epsilon}Q_{2})
, and need to now argue that we can determine the remaining values of
g_{23},a_{2},a_{3}
at a cost of
O(Q_{23}/Q_{12})=O(Q_{3}/Q_{1})
.

As in the previous subsection, we have the modular constraint that

a_{1}g_{23}+a_{2}g_{13}\equiv 0\pmod{g_{12}}.

This means (since
a_{1},g_{12},g_{13}
are all known, and
a_{1}g_{13}
is coprime to
g_{12}
) that there is some known integer
1\leq r<g_{12}
such that
a_{2}\equiv rg_{23}\pmod{g_{12}}
. That is, there must exist some integer
k
such that
a_{2}=rg_{23}+kg_{12}
, whence

\frac{a_{2}}{q_{2}}=\frac{r}{g_{12}g_{123}}+\frac{k}{g_{23}g_{123}}.

(Note that since
\gcd(a_{2},g_{23})=1
we must have
\gcd(k,g_{23})=1
.) Since
a_{2}/q_{2}
lies inside some known interval of length
O(1/Q_{2})
we deduce that
k/g_{23}g_{123}
lies inside some known interval of length
O(1/Q_{2})
, and hence
k/g_{23}
lies inside some known interval of length
O(1/Q_{12}Q_{23})
. We now observe that any two distinct fractions
\frac{b_{1}}{r_{1}}\neq\frac{b_{2}}{r_{2}}
with denominators in
[Q_{23},2Q_{23}]
are separated by
\gg 1/Q_{23}^{2}
, since

\left\lvert\frac{b_{1}}{r_{1}}-\frac{b_{2}}{r_{2}}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\frac{b_{1}r_{2}-b_{2}r_{1}}{r_{1}r_{2}}\right\rvert\geq\frac{1}{r_{1}r_{2}}\gg\frac{1}{Q_{23}^{2}}.

Since
Q_{12}\leq Q_{23}
by assumption, therefore, there are only
O(Q_{23}/Q_{12})
many possible choices for
k/g_{23}
. Fixing such a choice fixes both
g_{23}
and
a_{2}
, and then
a_{3}
is known, and the solution is determined.

2.3.
A special case when
k=5

We now sketch a proof that the number of solutions to

(2)

\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}+\frac{a_{2}}{q_{2}}+\frac{a_{3}}{q_{3}}+\frac{a_{4}}{q_{4}}+\frac{a_{5}}{q_{5}}=0\quad\textrm{ where }\quad\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\textrm{ for }1\leq i\leq 5,

with
\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert\leq\min(n,q_{i})
and
q_{i}\in[Q,2Q]
, is
O_{\epsilon}(n^{3}Q^{2+\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
. As before, we will use relative greatest common divisors. Here we consider a special case: we restrict ourselves to bounding the number of solutions under the additional assumption that
g_{I}=1
unless
\left\lvert I\right\rvert=2
. Certainly not all solutions satisfy this assumption, but restricting the number of
g_{I}
that need to be considered allows for a clearer presentation of the essential ideas.

Clearing denominators in (
2
) we need to bound the number of
a_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 5
and
g_{I}
for
I\subseteq\{1,2,3,4,5\}
with
\left\lvert I\right\rvert=2
, such that

(3)

a_{1}\prod_{I\not\ni 1}g_{I}+a_{2}\prod_{I\not\ni 2}g_{I}+a_{3}\prod_{I\not\ni 3}g_{I}+a_{4}\prod_{I\not\ni 4}g_{I}+a_{5}\prod_{I\not\ni 5}g_{I}=0.

Losing only a factor of
(\log Q)^{O(1)}
in the final bound, it suffices to count those solutions where
g_{I}\in[Q_{I},2Q_{I}]
for some fixed integers
Q_{I}\geq 1
, for all
I
. (Notice that since
q_{i}=\prod_{I\ni i}g_{I}
we have
Q\asymp\prod_{I\ni i}Q_{I}
for all
i
.)

Let
X\sqcup Y=\{1,2,3,4,5\}
with
\left\lvert X\right\rvert=3
and
\left\lvert Y\right\rvert=2
such that

\prod_{I\subseteq X}Q_{I}\prod_{J\subseteq Y}Q_{J}

is minimised. Note that this implies that, for all
i\in X
,

(4)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in Y\end{subarray}}Q_{iy}\geq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in X\end{subarray}}Q_{ix},

or else the minimised product can be decreased by replacing
X
with
Y\cup\{i\}
and
Y
with
X\backslash\{i\}
. Relabelling if necessary, we can assume that
X=\{1,2,3\}
and
Y=\{4,5\}
.

We begin by fixing the values of
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
, and
g_{12},g_{13},g_{14},g_{15},g_{23},g_{45}
at a total cost of

\ll n^{3}Q_{12}Q_{13}Q_{14}Q_{15}Q_{23}Q_{45}\ll n^{3}QQ_{23}Q_{45}.

It remains to find the values of
a_{4},a_{5},g_{24},g_{25},g_{34},g_{35}
. We will first choose the values of
g_{24}
and
g_{25}
. Reducing (
3
) modulo
g_{12}
yields

g_{34}g_{35}g_{45}\left(a_{1}g_{23}g_{24}g_{25}+a_{2}g_{13}g_{14}g_{15}\right)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ g_{12}).

Since
\gcd(g_{34}g_{35}g_{45},g_{12})=\gcd(a_{1}g_{23},g_{12})=1
, and the values of
a_{1},a_{2},g_{23},g_{13},g_{14},g_{15}
are all known, it follows that the value of

g_{24}g_{25}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ g_{12})

is known. Since
g_{24}g_{25}\in[Q_{24}Q_{25},4Q_{24}Q_{25}]
, the value of
g_{24}g_{25}
is determined after paying a cost of

\ll 1+\frac{Q_{24}Q_{25}}{g_{12}}\ll 1+\frac{Q_{24}Q_{25}}{Q_{12}}\ll\frac{Q_{24}Q_{25}}{Q_{12}},

since, by (
4
),

Q_{24}Q_{25}\geq Q_{12}Q_{23}\geq Q_{12}.

The values of
g_{24}
and
g_{25}
can be recovered via the divisor bound, at an additional cost of
O_{\epsilon}(Q^{\epsilon})
.

Similarly, reducing (
3
) modulo
g_{13}g_{23}
,

g_{34}g_{35}\left(a_{1}g_{23}g_{24}g_{25}+a_{2}g_{13}g_{14}g_{15}\right)+a_{3}g_{12}g_{14}g_{15}g_{24}g_{25}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ g_{13}g_{23}).

Since

\gcd(a_{3}g_{12}g_{14}g_{15}g_{24}g_{25},g_{13}g_{23})=\gcd(g_{34}g_{35},g_{13}g_{23})=1,

(making crucial use of the facts that
\gcd(a_{3},q_{3})=0
and that
\gcd(g_{I},g_{J})=1
when
\left\lvert I\right\rvert=\left\lvert J\right\rvert=2
and
I\neq J
), the value of

g_{34}g_{35}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ g_{13}g_{23})

is known. As above, since by (
4
) we have
Q_{34}Q_{35}\geq Q_{13}Q_{23}
, the values of
g_{34}
and
g_{35}
can now be fixed after paying

\ll_{\epsilon}Q^{\epsilon}\frac{Q_{34}Q_{35}}{Q_{13}Q_{23}}.

The values of all
g_{I}
are now known, and it suffices to fix the values of
a_{4}
and
a_{5}
. The value of
a_{4}
is (again consulting (
3
)) known modulo
g_{14}g_{24}g_{34}
, and so since it suffices to fix the value of
a_{4}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q_{4})
we can recover
a_{4}
after paying

\ll\frac{Q}{Q_{14}Q_{24}Q_{34}}.

The value of
a_{5}
is now fixed, as it is the only remaining unknown in (
3
). All the variables are now chosen, and it remains to total up our costs. We have spent a total of

\ll_{\epsilon}Q^{\epsilon}n^{3}\left(QQ_{23}Q_{45}\right)\left(\frac{Q_{24}Q_{25}}{Q_{12}}\right)\left(\frac{Q_{34}Q_{35}}{Q_{13}Q_{23}}\right)\left(\frac{Q}{Q_{14}Q_{24}Q_{34}}\right)=n^{3}Q^{2+\epsilon}\frac{Q_{25}Q_{35}Q_{45}}{Q_{12}Q_{13}Q_{14}}.

Recalling that
Q_{25}Q_{35}Q_{45}\ll Q/Q_{15}
, and
Q\ll Q_{12}Q_{13}Q_{14}Q_{15}
, this bound is

\ll n^{3}Q^{3+\epsilon}\frac{1}{Q_{12}Q_{13}Q_{14}Q_{15}}\ll n^{3}Q^{2+\epsilon}

as required.

2.4.
Proof of Theorem
4

We will now prove Theorem
4
in full. The difficulties compared to the simplified sketches above are (i) to generalise that machinery to handle arbitrary odd
k\geq 3
, (ii) to handle the potentially non-trivial contribution from those
g_{I}
with
\lvert I\rvert\geq 2
, (iii) to take more care with the use of the divisor bound to produce factors of
(\log Q)^{O(1)}
rather than
Q^{\epsilon}
, and (iv) to allow for constraints of the shape
a_{i}/q_{i}\in I_{i}
as in Section
2.2
. It is a non-trivial task to handle these issues, but nonetheless we view them as largely technical, and believe that the presentation of the simplified cases above capture the essential spirit of the proof.

All implicit constants in this proof may depend on
k
in some unspecified fashion. For convenience we write
Q=Q_{1}\cdots Q_{2k+1}
. In this proof we use
\tau_{r}(n)
to denote the
r
-fold divisor function (that is, the number of representations
n=d_{1}\cdots d_{r}
), and recall the elementary estimate (used without further mention) that, for any constant
\ell>0
,

(5)

\sum_{n\leq N}\tau_{r}(n)^{\ell}\leq(\log N)^{O_{\ell,r}(1)}N.

It suffices to bound the number of solutions to

(6)

\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{a_{2k+1}}{q_{2k+1}}=n

for any fixed integer
n
(since there are trivially
O_{k}(1)
possible integers that need to be considered). Let
g_{I}
, as
I
ranges over subsets of
\{1,\ldots,2k+1\}
, be the relative greatest common divisor parameterisation of
q_{i}
. At the acceptable cost of an additional factor of
(\log Q)^{O(1)}
, it suffices to prove the target bound for the number of solutions to (
6
) under the additional assumption that
g_{I}\in[Q_{I},2Q_{I}]
for some fixed integers
Q_{I}
. Let
\{1,\ldots,2k+1\}=X\sqcup Y
be a partition with
\left\lvert X\right\rvert=k+1
and
\left\lvert Y\right\rvert=k
such that

(7)

\prod_{I\subseteq X}Q_{I}\prod_{J\subseteq Y}Q_{J}

is minimised. Relabelling if necessary, we will assume that
X=\{1,\ldots,k+1\}
and
Y=\{k+2,\ldots,2k+1\}
. (Note that in particular this choice of partition depends on the particular values of the
Q_{I}
, and hence perhaps different partitions
X\sqcup Y
may be used to bound different classes of solutions, but this clearly does not affect the validity of Theorem
4
, since we can simply take a maximum of the upper bound over all possible partitions.)

Before proceeding with the main line of the proof we pause to record the useful inequality that, for all
1\leq i\leq k+1
,

(8)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni i\\
I\subseteq\{i\}\cup Y\end{subarray}}Q_{I}\geq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}Q_{J}.

Indeed, (
8
) follows immediately from the stronger inequality

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni i\\
I\subseteq\{i\}\cup Y\end{subarray}}Q_{I}\geq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq X\end{subarray}}Q_{J},

which in turn follows by noting that if we replace
Y
by
X\backslash\{i\}
and
X
by
Y\cup\{i\}
then the quantity in (
7
) changes by a multiplicative factor of

\frac{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni i\\
I\subseteq\{i\}\cup Y\end{subarray}}Q_{I}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq X\end{subarray}}Q_{J}},

and so by the assumed minimality of (
7
) this quantity is
\geq 1
as required.

Upon multiplying both sides by
\prod_{I}g_{I}
, equation (
6
) is equivalent to

(9)

a_{1}\prod_{I\not\ni 1}g_{I}+\cdots+a_{2k+1}\prod_{I\not\ni 2k+1}g_{I}=n\prod_{I}g_{I}.

Note that
g_{\{i\}}
divides the right-hand side and every summand on the left-hand side except
a_{i}\prod_{I\not\ni i}g_{I}
, and therefore
g_{\{i\}}\mid a_{i}\prod_{I\not\ni i}g_{I}
. Since
\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1
and
\gcd(g_{\{i\}},g_{I})=1
for
i\not\in I
it follows that
g_{I}=1
whenever
\left\lvert I\right\rvert=1
.

Let

d_{I}=\gcd\left(g_{I},\prod_{J\subsetneq I\cap X}g_{J}\right)

and
\widetilde{g_{I}}=g_{I}/d_{I}
. At the cost of a further factor of
(\log Q)^{O(1)}
we may assume that
d_{I}\in[D_{I},2D_{I}]
, for all
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,2k+1\}
, for some integers
D_{I}
. Note that
\widetilde{g_{I}}\asymp Q_{I}/D_{I}
for all
I
.

The proof will proceed along the following steps:

•

Step one.
For each
i\in X
we fix
B_{i}
, a sub-interval of
A_{i}
of length
<1/Q_{i}
in which
\frac{a_{i}}{q_{i}}
will lie. We also fix
g_{J}
for all
J\subseteq Y
.
There are

\ll\prod_{i\in X}\delta_{i}Q_{i}\prod_{J\subseteq Y}Q_{J}

many possibilities for these choices.

•

Step two.
We will fix all the remaining
g_{I}
except those where
|I\cap X|=1
, i.e. all
g_{I}
with
|I\cap X|\geq 2
, together with all accompanying
d_{I}
. The cost to fix these terms, given that the terms from Step one have already been fixed, is (taking some care to use a bound for the divisor function only on average)

\ll(\log Q)^{O(1)}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
|I\cap X|\geq 2\end{subarray}}(Q_{I}/D_{I}).

•

Step three.
We now carefully fix (and count possibilities for) the values of the
g_{I}
with
|I\cap X|=1
; these values are heavily constrained by the values which have already been fixed (i.e. the constraints from Step one on
a_{i}/q_{i}
for
i\in X
, and
g_{I}
for all
I
with
|I\cap X|\neq 1
). This step is by far the most complicated, largely because in order to only lose a factor of
(\log Q)^{O(1)}
, we must be careful to only use the divisor bound on average. The additional cost to fix these terms is

\ll(\log Q)^{O(1)}\left(\prod_{J\subseteq X}D_{J}\right)\frac{\prod_{\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert=1}Q_{I}}{\prod_{J\subseteq X}Q_{J}}.

•

Step four.
All denominators have been fixed, which (since
a_{i}/q_{i}
is known to lie in some fixed interval of width
<1/Q_{i}\leq 1/q_{i}
for
i\in X
) also determines
a_{i}
for
i\in X
. It remains only to count the possibilities for
a_{i}
for all
i\in Y
. These values are almost fully determined by what has already been fixed; the number of possibilities for these
a_{i}
is, given the information known at this point, is

\ll\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
\left\lvert I\cap Y\right\rvert=1\end{subarray}}D_{I}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
\left\lvert I\cap Y\right\rvert\geq 2\end{subarray}}Q_{I}^{\left\lvert I\cap Y\right\rvert-1}.

Multiplying these four contributions together and simplifying, we get an upper bound of

\ll(\log Q)^{O(1)}\prod_{i\in X}\delta_{i}Q_{i}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
I\not\subseteq X\end{subarray}}Q_{I}^{|I\cap Y|}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
|I\cap Y|\leq 1\end{subarray}}D_{I}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
|I\cap X|\geq 2\end{subarray}}D_{I}^{-1}.

Note that if
\left\lvert I\cap Y\right\rvert\leq 1
then
\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert\geq 2
unless
\left\lvert I\right\rvert\leq 2
. It follows that, since
d_{I}=1
(and thus
D_{I}\ll 1
) whenever
|I|\leq 2
, the two products over the
D_{I}
are
\ll 1
.
Hence the total cost is

\ll(\log Q)^{O(1)}\prod_{i\in X}\delta_{i}Q_{i}\prod_{I}Q_{I}^{\left\lvert I\cap Y\right\rvert}.

The proof is complete after noting that
Q_{i}\asymp\prod_{I\ni i}Q_{I}
, and hence

\prod_{j\in Y}Q_{j}\asymp\prod_{I}Q_{I}^{\left\lvert I\cap Y\right\rvert}.

It remains to prove steps one through four.

2.4.1.
Step one:

This step is immediate by assumption on the
\frac{a_{i}}{q_{i}}
and
g_{J}
.

2.4.2.
Step two:

Our next goal is to determine all those
g_{I}
except those where
|I\cap X|=1
; this will involve making some additional choices. We first claim that, for any
I
such that
\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert\geq 2
, the corresponding
d_{I}
is a divisor of
(\prod_{J\subseteq X}\widetilde{g_{J}})^{O(1)}
. This is proved by induction on
\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert
: firstly, when
\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert=2
, if
J\subsetneq I\cap X
then
\left\lvert J\right\rvert\leq 1
and so
g_{J}=1
, and hence
d_{I}=1
for all such
I
. The general case follows immediately from

d_{I}\mid\prod_{J\subsetneq I\cap X}g_{J}=\left(\prod_{J\subsetneq I\cap X}\widetilde{g_{J}}\right)\left(\prod_{J\subsetneq I\cap X}d_{J}\right)

and the inductive hypothesis. It follows (recalling that
\widetilde{g_{J}}=1
for
\left\lvert J\right\rvert=1
) that the number of choices for
\widetilde{g_{I}}
and
d_{I}
for all
I
with
\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert\geq 2
is, using (
5
) and the fact that
\tau(ab)\leq\tau(a)\tau(b)
, at most

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert\geq 2\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{\widetilde{g_{I}}\ll Q_{I}/D_{I}}\tau(\widetilde{g_{I}})^{O(1)}\right)\ll(\log Q)^{O(1)}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert\geq 2\end{subarray}}(Q_{I}/D_{I}).

2.4.3.
Step three:

We have now determined an interval
B_{i}
of width
<1/Q_{i}
containing
a_{i}/q_{i}
for
i\in X
, as well as all
g_{I}
except those where
\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert=1
; our next goal is to fix the values of
g_{I}
with
\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert=1
.

For brevity, let

(10)

h_{i}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni i\\
I\subseteq\{i\}\cup Y\end{subarray}}g_{I}

for
i\in X
, and similarly
H_{i}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni i\\
I\subseteq\{i\}\cup Y\end{subarray}}Q_{I}
, so that
h_{i}\asymp H_{i}
. We need to both determine the value of each
h_{i}
and also fix a decomposition of
h_{i}
into
\leq 2^{k}
many factors, which will fix the value of each
g_{I}
in the product.

Let
P(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k+1},a_{1},\dots,a_{k+1})
be the event that there exists some
a_{k+2},\ldots,a_{2k+1}
and decomposition of each
h_{i}
into factors as in (
10
) which (in conjunction with the already fixed
g_{I}
for
\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert\neq 1
) forms a solution to (
9
) and satisfies the constraint that
a_{i}/q_{i}\in B_{i}
for all
i\in X
. The total cost to determine all remaining
g_{I}
can then be bounded above by

\sum_{h_{1},\ldots,h_{k+1},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k+1}}1_{P(h_{1},\ldots,a_{k+1})}\left(\prod_{i}1_{h_{i}\asymp H_{i}}\right)\tau_{2^{k}}(h_{1})\cdots\tau_{2^{k}}(h_{k+1}).

Using the elementary fact that
b_{1}\cdots b_{k+1}\leq b_{1}^{k+1}+\cdots+b_{k+1}^{k+1}
it therefore suffices to show that

(11)

\sum_{h_{1},\ldots,h_{k+1},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k+1}}1_{P(h_{1},\ldots,a_{k+1})}\left(\prod_{i}1_{h_{i}\asymp H_{i}}\right)\tau_{2^{k}}(h_{1})^{k+1}

is

\ll(\log Q)^{O(1)}\left(\prod_{J\subseteq X}D_{J}\right)\prod_{1\leq i\leq k+1}\frac{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni i\\
I\subseteq\{i\}\cup Y\end{subarray}}Q_{I}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}Q_{J}}.

We now note that the condition
P(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k+1},a_{1},\dots,a_{k+1})
implies a certain collection of cascading restrictions. Fix some
i\in X
. Since

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}g_{J}\mid\prod_{J\not\ni j}g_{J}

for all
j>i
, equation (
9
) implies that

\sum_{1\leq j\leq i}a_{j}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\not\ni j\\
I\not\subseteq\{i+1,\ldots,2k+1\}\end{subarray}}g_{I}\equiv 0\pmod{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}g_{J}},

where we have cancelled the common factor of
\prod_{I\subseteq\{i+1,\ldots,2k+1\}}g_{I}
since it is coprime to
\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}g_{J}
(using that
\gcd(g_{I},g_{J})=1
unless
I\subseteq J
or
J\subseteq I
). This implies that

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni i\\
I\subseteq\{i,\ldots,2k+1\}\end{subarray}}g_{I}\left(\sum_{1\leq j<i}a_{j}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\not\ni j\\
I\not\subseteq\{i,\ldots,2k+1\}\end{subarray}}g_{I}\right)\equiv-a_{i}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\not\ni i\\
I\not\subseteq\{i+1,\ldots,2k+1\}\end{subarray}}g_{I}\pmod{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}g_{J}}.

It is important to observe that the ‘unknown’
g_{I}
(those with
\left\lvert I\right\rvert\geq 2
and
\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert=1
) only appear in this expression as a component of some
h_{j}
, and hence this congruence condition can be expressed only in terms of ‘known’
g_{I}
, together with
a_{j}
and
h_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq i
. Note that (recalling
g_{I}=1
for
\left\lvert I\right\rvert=1
)

\gcd\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni i\\
I\subseteq\{i,\ldots,2k+1\}\end{subarray}}g_{I},\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}g_{J}\right)=1,

and hence if

e_{i}=\gcd\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}g_{J},\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\not\ni i\\
I\not\subseteq\{i+1,\ldots,2k+1\}\end{subarray}}g_{I}\right)

(which depends only on
h_{1},\ldots,h_{i-1}
and known values of
g_{I}
) then we have established the congruence conditions that, for
1\leq i\leq k+1
, there exists some
r_{i}
, depending only on the
g_{I}
already fixed together with
a_{j}
and
h_{j}
for
1\leq j<i
, such that, if
f_{i}=e_{i}^{-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}g_{J}
, we have

a_{i}\equiv r_{i}h_{i}\pmod{f_{i}}.

At the same time, for
1\leq i\leq k+1
, we have
q_{i}=h_{i}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni i\\
|I\cap X|\geq 2\end{subarray}}g_{I}
. Thus
\frac{a_{i}}{q_{i}}\in B_{i}
if and only if

\frac{a_{i}}{h_{i}}\in\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni i\\
|I\cap X|\geq 2\end{subarray}}g_{I}\right)B_{i}.

Let
C_{i}
denote the interval
\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni i\\
|I\cap X|\geq 2\end{subarray}}g_{I}\right)B_{i}
, and note that (recalling
B_{i}
is an interval of length
O(1/Q_{i})
), the interval
C_{i}
has length
\ll O(1/H_{i})
.
The left-hand side of (
11
) can therefore by bounded above by

\sum_{h_{1},\ldots,h_{k+1},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k+1}}\tau_{2^{k}}(h_{1})^{k+1}\prod_{1\leq i\leq k+1}1_{h_{i}\asymp H_{i}}1_{a_{i}\equiv r_{i}h_{i}\pmod{f_{i}}}1_{a_{i}/h_{i}\in C_{i}}1_{a_{i}/q_{i}\in B_{i}}

(where
r_{i},f_{i}
are integers that depend on
a_{j}
and
h_{j}
for
1\leq j<i
in the manner specified above).

We can now evaluate the sum over
a_{j},h_{j}
for
2\leq j\leq k+1
. We estimate these in reverse order, beginning with
j=k+1
, so that in our argument we can assume that the values of
a_{i}
and
h_{i}
are known for
1\leq i<j
, and hence
r_{j},f_{j}
are also known. Since
a_{j}\equiv r_{j}h_{j}\pmod{f_{j}}
, there exists some
\ell_{j}
such that

\frac{a_{j}}{h_{j}f_{j}}=\frac{r_{j}}{f_{j}}+\frac{\ell_{j}}{h_{j}}.

Since
\frac{a_{j}}{h_{j}}\in C_{j}
,
\frac{\ell_{j}}{h_{j}}
lies in a known interval of length
O(1/H_{j}f_{j})
. Since distinct fractions with denominators of size
\asymp H_{j}
are separated by at least
\gg 1/H_{j}^{2}
, there are at most
O(H_{j}/f_{j})
acceptable choices of
\frac{\ell_{j}}{h_{j}}
(and hence also of
h_{j}
), provided
f_{j}\ll H_{j}
. This is true by (
8
), since

f_{j}\ll\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni j\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,j\}\end{subarray}}Q_{J}\leq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni j\\
I\subseteq\{j\}\cup Y\end{subarray}}Q_{I}=H_{j}.

Note that once each
h_{j}
is fixed,
q_{j}
is fixed as well, which means that
a_{j}
is completely determined.

Recalling
d_{J}=\gcd(g_{J},\prod_{I\subsetneq J\cap X}g_{J})
and
\gcd(g_{I},g_{J})=1
unless
I\subseteq J
or
J\subseteq I
, and observing that if
i\in J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}
and
i\not\in I
and
I\subseteq J
then
I\subsetneq J\cap X
, note that

e_{i}\mid\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}\gcd\left(g_{J},\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\not\ni i\\
I\not\subseteq\{i+1,\ldots,2k+1\}\\
I\subseteq J\end{subarray}}g_{I}\right)\mid\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}d_{J},

and hence
e_{i}\ll\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}D_{J}
and

f_{j}\gg\frac{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni j\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,j\}\end{subarray}}Q_{J}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}D_{J}}.

It follows that the left-hand side of (
11
) is

\ll\prod_{2\leq j\leq k+1}\left(H_{j}\frac{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni i\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,i\}\end{subarray}}D_{J}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni j\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,j\}\end{subarray}}Q_{J}}\right)\sum_{h_{1}\asymp H_{1}}\tau_{2^{k}}(h_{1})^{k+1}.

The sum over
h_{1}
is
\ll(\log Q)^{O(1)}H_{1}
by (
5
) and hence the left-hand side of (
11
) is

\ll(\log Q)^{O(1)}\prod_{1\leq j\leq k+1}H_{j}\prod_{2\leq j\leq k+1}\left(\frac{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni j\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,j\}\end{subarray}}D_{J}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\ni j\\
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,j\}\end{subarray}}Q_{J}}\right).

Recalling
H_{j}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni j\\
I\subseteq\{j\}\cup Y\end{subarray}}Q_{I}
, this is

\ll(\log Q)^{O(1)}\left(\prod_{J\subseteq X}D_{J}\right)\frac{\prod_{\left\lvert I\cap X\right\rvert=1}Q_{I}}{\prod_{J\subseteq X}Q_{J}}

as required.

2.4.4.
Step four.

We have now fixed the values of all
a_{i}
with
i\in X
and all
g_{I}
.
It remains to fix the values of
a_{k+2},\ldots,a_{2k+1}
. For this we note that, with
k+2\leq j\leq 2k+1
,

\sum_{1\leq i<j}a_{i}\prod_{I\not\ni i}g_{I}=-a_{j}\prod_{I\not\ni j}g_{I}\pmod{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni j\\
I\cap\{j+1,\ldots,2k+1\}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}g_{I}},

and hence, provided we know the values of all
g_{I}
and all
a_{i}
with
i<j
, we know the value of
a_{j}
modulo

f_{j}^{-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni j\\
I\cap\{j+1,\ldots,2k+1\}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}g_{I},

where, for
k+2\leq j\leq 2k+1
,

f_{j}=\gcd\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni j\\
I\cap\{j+1,\ldots,2k+1\}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}g_{I},\prod_{J\not\ni j}g_{J}\right).

It follows that, once
a_{i}
are known for
i<j
, the cost to determine
a_{j}
is

\ll f_{j}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni j\\
I\cap\{j+1,\ldots,2k+1\}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}Q_{I}.

As above, we have

f_{j}\mid\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
I\cap Y=\{j\}\end{subarray}}\gcd\left(g_{I},\prod_{J\not\ni j}g_{J}\right)\mid\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
I\cap Y=\{j\}\end{subarray}}\gcd(g_{I},\prod_{J\subsetneq I\cap X}g_{J}),

and hence

f_{j}\ll\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
I\cap Y=\{j\}\end{subarray}}D_{I}.

Therefore the cost to determine
a_{j}
once
a_{i}
are known for
i<j
is

\ll\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
I\cap Y=\{j\}\end{subarray}}D_{I}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\ni j\\
I\cap\{j+1,\ldots,2k+1\}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}Q_{I}.

Multiplying this for all
j\in Y
, the total cost to determine all remaining
a_{i}
is therefore

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
\left\lvert I\cap Y\right\rvert=1\end{subarray}}D_{I}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
\left\lvert I\cap Y\right\rvert\geq 2\end{subarray}}Q_{I}^{\left\lvert I\cap Y\right\rvert-1}.

This concludes the proof of Step four, and hence the proof of Theorem
4
.

3.
Odd moments

We will now use Theorem
4
to prove Theorems
1
and
2
. These proofs follow along similar lines to those from
[
15
]
and
[
14
]
respectively, with several technical refinements; the significant difference is our use of Theorem
4
.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1

We introduce some definitions now to make the statements clearer. By
[
15
, Lemma 2]
we can write
M_{k}(q,h)=q(\frac{\phi(q)}{q})^{k}V_{k}(q,h)
, where

(12)

\displaystyle V_{k}(q,h)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\\
1<q_{i}|q\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu(q_{i})}{\phi(q_{i})}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\\
1\leq a_{i}\leq q_{i}\\
\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\\
\sum a_{i}/q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\sum_{1\leq d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\leq h}e\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{d_{i}a_{i}}{q_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\\
1<q_{i}|q\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu(q_{i})}{\phi(q_{i})}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\\
1\leq a_{i}\leq q_{i}\\
\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\\
\sum a_{i}/q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}E\left(\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}\right)\cdots E\left(\frac{a_{k}}{q_{k}}\right),

where
E(x):=\sum_{m=1}^{h}e(mx)
, with
e(u)=e^{2\pi iu}
. We note that
\lvert E(x)\rvert\leq F(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, with

F(x):=\min\{h,\|x\|^{-1}\}.

The overall strategy in proving Theorem
1
is as follows:

(1)

First note that
M_{k}(q,h)
is multiplicative in
q
, and hence we can write
q=q_{S}q_{R}
, where
q_{S}
is only divisible by primes
\leq h^{k}
and
q_{R}
is only divisible by primes
>h^{k}
, and use different methods to bound
M_{k}(q_{R},h)
and
M_{k}(q_{S},h)
separately.

(2)

The ‘smooth’ component
q_{S}
is controlled by Theorem
4
(after converting
M_{k}
to
V_{k}
as above and applying the triangle inequality), noting that the
F(a_{i}/q_{i})
is essentially
h
multiplied by an indicator function restricting
a_{i}/q_{i}
to an interval of width
\ll 1/h
. Some care must be taken to ensure that the logarithmic term is logarithmic only in
h
, however, since naively the denominators considered may range up to
\prod_{p\leq h^{k}}p\approx e^{h^{k}}
. To this end, we follow
[
15
]
and employ a further separation device to factorise each denominator into a small part and a large part, and apply Theorem
4
to only the contribution from small denominators.

(3)

The ‘rough’ component
q_{R}
is handled by a combinatorial device, in a similar fashion to
[
15
]
, although for our desired level of precision the argument is slightly more involved.

3.1.1.
The smooth component

We first explain how Theorem
4
is used to bound
M_{k}(q,h)
when
q
is ‘smooth’ (i.e. has only small prime divisors). Theorem
4
is employed via the following technical lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
h\geq 1
be an integer, let
k\geq 3
be an odd integer, and fix any rational
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\in[0,1)
. Suppose
2\leq y\leq h
. For each
i
, let
\ell_{i}=1
if
\alpha_{i}=0
and let
\ell_{i}=h
otherwise. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{1},\dots,s_{k}\\
\ell_{i}<s_{i}<y\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(s_{i})^{2}}{\phi(s_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\\
1\leq a_{i}\leq s_{i}\\
\gcd(a_{i},s_{i})=1\\
\sum_{i}a_{i}/s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}F\left(\frac{a_{i}}{s_{i}}+\alpha_{i}\right)\ll(\log y)^{O_{k}(1)}h^{\tfrac{k-1}{2}},

where the bound is independent of the choice of
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}
, with
F(x)=\min(h,\|x\|^{-1})
.

Proof.

The left-hand side can be bounded above by

(13)

\ll(\log y)^{k}\sum_{1<s_{1},\dots,s_{k}<y}(s_{1}\cdots s_{k})^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\\
1\leq a_{i}\leq s_{i}\\
\gcd(a_{i},s_{i})=1\\
\sum_{i}a_{i}/s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}F\left(\frac{a_{i}}{s_{i}}+\alpha_{i}\right),

since
\frac{s}{\phi(s)}\ll\log y
for any
s\leq y
. For
1\leq a_{i}\leq s_{i}
, we have

F\left(\frac{a_{i}}{s_{i}}+\alpha_{i}\right)=\min\left\{h,\left\|\frac{a_{i}}{s_{i}}+\alpha_{i}\right\|^{-1}\right\},

so that
\frac{1}{s_{i}}F\left(\frac{a_{i}}{s_{i}}+\alpha_{i}\right)\leq 1/n_{i}
, where

n_{i}=\mathrm{max}\left\{\frac{s_{i}}{h},\lfloor|\tilde{a_{i}}+\alpha_{i}s_{i}|\rfloor\right\},

and
\tilde{a_{i}}
is the representative of
a_{i}
mod
s_{i}
which minimises
|\tilde{a_{i}}+\alpha_{i}s_{i}|
(so that
|\tilde{a_{i}}+\alpha_{i}s_{i}|\leq s_{i}/2
).

The possible values of
n_{i}
are thus the set
\left\{\frac{s_{i}}{h}\right\}\cup\left(\mathbb{Z}\cap\left[\frac{s_{i}}{h},\frac{s_{i}}{2}\right]\right)
.
Note that if
s_{i}/h<1
, then
1<s_{i}<h
, so
\alpha_{i}=0
by assumption. In this case there is no contribution when
n_{i}=s_{i}/h
, since that implies
|\tilde{a_{i}}|\leq s_{i}/h<1
, and thus
\tilde{a_{i}}=0
, which contradicts the requirement that
\gcd(a_{i},s_{i})=1
. In particular, this means that
n_{i}\geq 1
always.

Notice that since
a_{i}\leq s_{i}
and
\lvert\tilde{a_{i}}+\alpha_{i}s_{i}\rvert\leq n_{i}+1
, if we assume that
s_{i}\in[S_{i},2S_{i}]
say, there must exist some interval
I_{i}
, independent of
a_{i}
and
s_{i}
, of length
O(n_{i}/S_{i})
, which contains
a_{i}/s_{i}
. It follows from the discussion thus far that (
13
) is bounded above by

(\log y)^{O_{k}(1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\\
1\leq n_{i}\leq y\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}^{-1}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\\
1\leq S_{i}\leq 2hn_{i}\end{subarray}}\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{1},\dots,s_{k}\\
s_{i}\in[S_{i},2S_{i}]\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\\
a_{i}/s_{i}\in I_{i}\\
\gcd(a_{i},s_{i})=1\\
\sum_{i}a_{i}/s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}1\Bigr).

Noting that the term inside the brackets depends only on the size of each
n_{i}
, we can dyadically pigeonhole this sum according to the size of
n_{i}
(at an additional cost of
(\log y)^{O(1)}
, and so it suffices to show that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}N_{1},\ldots,N_{k}\\
1\leq N_{i}\leq 2y\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}N_{i}^{-1}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\\
1\leq S_{i}\leq 4hN_{i}\end{subarray}}\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{1},\dots,s_{k}\\
s_{i}\in[S_{i},2S_{i}]\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\\
a_{i}/s_{i}\in I_{i}\\
\gcd(a_{i},s_{i})=1\\
\sum_{i}a_{i}/s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}1\Bigr)\ll(\log y)^{O(1)}h^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor},

where each
I_{i}
is an interval of length
O(N_{i}/S_{i})
centred at
\alpha_{i}
. By Theorem
4
the term inside the brackets is, for some
X\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}
of size
\left\lvert X\right\rvert=\lceil k/2\rceil
,

\ll(\log y)^{O(1)}\prod_{i\in X}N_{i}\prod_{j\not\in X}S_{j}\ll(\log y)^{O(1)}h^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}N_{1}\cdots N_{k},

since each
S_{i}\ll hN_{i}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

In certain regimes the following more direct bound is more effective.

Lemma 2
.

For a squarefree integer
q
and a fixed integer
k\geq 1
,

\sum_{r_{1},\dots,r_{k}|q}\frac{\mu(r_{i})^{2}}{\phi(r_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\\
1\leq b_{i}\leq r_{i}\\
\gcd(b_{i},r_{i})=1\\
\sum_{i}b_{i}/r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}1\leq\prod_{p|q}\left(1+\frac{2^{k}}{p-1}\right).

Proof.

The left hand side is multiplicative in
q
(since the constraints on the inside sum are independent for each prime
p|r_{1}\cdots r_{k}
), and hence it suffices to prove this when
q
is a prime, which we will call
p
. Then for all
i
, either
r_{i}=1
or
r_{i}=p
. Since
\sum_{i}\frac{b_{i}}{r_{i}}\in\mathbb{Z}
, either none or at least two of the
r_{i}
’s are equal to
p
. If no
r_{i}
is equal to
p
, then
b_{1}=\cdots=b_{k}=1
, and this term contributes
1
overall to the sum.

If
j
of the
r_{i}
’s are equal to
p
, for
j\geq 2
, then the contribution of these terms to the sum is

\leq\binom{k}{j}\frac{\phi(p)^{j-1}}{\phi(p)^{j}}=\binom{k}{j}\frac{1}{\phi(p)}.

To see this, note that there are
\binom{k}{j}
possibilities for which of the
r_{i}
’s are equal to
p
. If we reorder such that
r_{1}=\cdots=r_{j}=p
and
r_{j+1}=\cdots=r_{k}=1
, then
b_{j+1}=\cdots=b_{k}=1
. There are
\leq\phi(p)
options for each of
b_{1},\dots,b_{j-1}
, and then the value of
b_{j}
is determined by the constraint that
\sum_{i}b_{i}/r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. This is an inequality rather than an equality because if
\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}b_{i}/r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
b_{j}
cannot be relatively prime to
r_{j}
and also satisfy the sum constraint.

The left-hand side (when
q=p
is prime) is therefore at most

1+\frac{1}{\phi(p)}\sum_{j=2}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\leq 1+\frac{2^{k}}{\phi(p)},

and the proof is complete.
∎

Although our primary interest is
M_{k}(q,h)
, for the latter stages of the proof for general
q
, it is convenient to bound a more general quantity for smooth
q
: for any integers
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 0
let

M_{k_{1},k_{2}}(q,h)=\sum_{1\leq n\leq q}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq m<n+h\\
\gcd(m,q)=1\end{subarray}}1-\frac{\phi(q)}{q}h\right)^{k_{1}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq m<n+h\\
\gcd(m,q)=1\end{subarray}}1\right)^{k_{2}}.

Lemma 3
.

Let
h\geq 1
. Let
q\geq 1
be a squarefree integer, and let
y\geq h
be such that for any prime
p|q
,
p\leq y
. Then for any integers
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 0
and
\epsilon>0
,

M_{k_{1},k_{2}}(q,h)\ll(\log y)^{O_{k_{1},k_{2}}(1)}h^{\lfloor\frac{k_{1}}{2}\rfloor+k_{2}}q.

Proof.

Generalising the proof of
[
15
, Lemma 2]
we can write
M_{k}(q,h)=q(\frac{\phi(q)}{q})^{k}V_{k_{1},k_{2}}(q,h)
, where (writing
k=k_{1}+k_{2}
for brevity)

V_{k_{1},k_{2}}(q,h):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1},\dots,q_{k_{1}}\\
1<q_{i}|q\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{k_{1}+1},\dots,q_{k}\\
1\leq q_{i}|q\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu(q_{i})}{\phi(q_{i})}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\\
1\leq a_{i}\leq q_{i}\\
\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\\
\sum_{i}a_{i}/q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}E\left(\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}\right)\cdots E\left(\frac{a_{k}}{q_{k}}\right)

and
E(x):=\sum_{m=1}^{h}e(mx)
, with
e(u)=e^{2\pi iu}
. Since
\lvert E(x)\rvert\leq F(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, with

F(x):=\min\{h,\|x\|^{-1}\},

we immediately have by the triangle inequality that

|V_{k_{1},k_{2}}(q,h)|\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1},\dots,q_{k_{1}}\\
1<q_{i}|q\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{k_{1}+1},\dots,q_{k}\\
1\leq q_{i}|q\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu(q_{i})^{2}}{\phi(q_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\\
1\leq a_{i}\leq q_{i}\\
\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\\
\sum_{i}a_{i}/q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}F\left(\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}\right)\cdots F\left(\frac{a_{k}}{q_{k}}\right).

We claim that for each tuple
q_{1},\dots,q_{k}
in this sum there exist tuples
s_{1},\dots,s_{k}
and
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
such that

(1)

q_{i}=s_{i}r_{i}
for all
i
,

(2)

s_{i}\leq y^{2k}
for all
i
and
1<s_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k_{1}
,

(3)

\gcd\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}s_{i},\prod_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}\right)=1
, and

(4)

whenever
q_{i}>h
, then
s_{i}>h
as well, and
whenever
q_{i}\leq h
, we have
s_{i}=q_{i}
and
r_{i}=1
.

To prove this, we construct
s_{1},\dots,s_{k}
and
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
as follows. For each
i
with
q_{i}\leq h
, set
s_{i}=q_{i}
and
r_{i}=1
. For each
i
with
q_{i}>h
, we claim that
q_{i}
has a divisor
d_{i}
with
h<d_{i}\leq y^{2}
. Since each prime factor of
q_{i}
is less than
y
,
d_{i}
can be constructed greedily: if
d\leq h
and
d|q_{i}
, then there exists a prime factor
p|q_{i}
with
p\nmid d
, in which case
pd\leq y^{2}
. Iterating this process yields a divisor
d_{i}|q_{i}
with
h<d_{i}\leq y^{2}
. We then set

s_{i}=\gcd\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq k\\
q_{j}>h\end{subarray}}d_{j}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq k\\
q_{j}\leq h\end{subarray}}q_{j},q_{i}\right)\quad\textrm{and}\quad r_{i}=\frac{q_{i}}{s_{i}},

so that by definition of the
d_{i}
and since each
q_{i}
is squarefree,
h<d_{i}\leq s_{i}
, and
s_{i}\leq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq k\\
q_{j}>h\end{subarray}}d_{j}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq k\\
q_{j}\leq h\end{subarray}}q_{j}\leq y^{2k}
. The only remaining thing to check is that for all
i,j
we have
\gcd(s_{i},r_{j})=1
. By definition of
r_{j}
(and since
q_{j}
is squarefree) it suffices to note that by definition if
p\mid s_{i}
and
p\mid q_{j}
then
p\mid s_{j}
.

Note that any fraction of the shape
\frac{a_{i}}{q_{i}}
(with
\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1
) can be written uniquely as
\frac{b_{i}}{r_{i}}+\frac{c_{i}}{s_{i}}
with
\gcd(b_{i},r_{i})=\gcd(c_{i},s_{i})=1
. We thus replace the sum over
q_{1},\dots,q_{k}
by a sum over all such
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
and
s_{1},\dots,s_{k}
. This can only increase the value of the sum. In particular,

|V_{k_{1},k_{2}}(q,h)|\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\dots,r_{k}|q\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu(r_{i})^{2}}{\phi(r_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\\
1\leq b_{i}\leq r_{i}\\
\gcd(b_{i},r_{i})=1\\
\sum_{i}b_{i}/r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}W(\tfrac{b_{1}}{r_{1}},\ldots,\tfrac{b_{k}}{r_{k}})

where

W(\tfrac{b_{1}}{r_{1}},\ldots,\tfrac{b_{k}}{r_{k}})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{1},\dots,s_{k}\\
s_{i}\leq y^{2k}\\
r_{i}>1\Rightarrow s_{i}>h\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq k_{1}}1_{s_{i}>1}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu(s_{i})^{2}}{\phi(s_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\\
1\leq c_{i}\leq s_{i}\\
\gcd(c_{i},s_{i})=1\\
\sum_{i}c_{i}/s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}F\left(\frac{c_{i}}{s_{i}}+\frac{b_{i}}{r_{i}}\right).

By Lemma
1
, for any
k^{\prime}\geq k_{1}
, the contribution to
W(\tfrac{b_{1}}{r_{1}},\ldots,\tfrac{b_{k}}{r_{k}})
from those tuples with
k^{\prime}
of the
s_{i}
being
>1
is
\ll(\log y)^{O(1)}h^{\lfloor\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\rfloor+k-k^{\prime}}
(where we have used the trivial bound
F\leq h
for those
s_{i}=1
). Since
k=k_{1}+k_{2}
this is
\ll(\log y)^{O(1)}h^{\lfloor\frac{k_{1}}{2}\rfloor+k_{2}}
. Thus

\displaystyle|V_{k_{1},k_{2}}(q,h)|

\displaystyle\ll(\log y)^{O(1)}h^{\lfloor\frac{k_{1}}{2}\rfloor+k_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\dots,r_{k}|q\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu(r_{i})^{2}}{\phi(r_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\\
1\leq b_{i}\leq r_{i}\\
\gcd(b_{i},r_{i})=1\\
\sum b_{i}/r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\ll(\log y)^{O(1)}h^{\lfloor\frac{k_{1}}{2}\rfloor+k_{2}}\prod_{p|q}\left(1+\frac{2^{k}}{p-1}\right),

by Lemma
2
. Since
p|q
only if
p\leq y
, we have

\prod_{p|q}\left(1+\frac{2^{k}}{p-1}\right)\ll\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)^{2^{k}}\ll(\log y)^{2^{k}},

which completes the proof.
∎

3.1.2.
The rough component

To complete the proof of Theorem
1
we use an argument of Montgomery and Vaughan
[
15
]
to handle the ‘rough’ component of
q
, which is divisible by only large primes
>y
. To this end, set
y=h^{k}
, and for any squarefree
q
, let
q=q_{1}q_{2}
where
p\leq y
for all primes
p\mid q_{1}
and
p>y
for all primes
p\mid q_{2}
. Let
P_{1}=\phi(q_{1})/q_{1}
and
P_{2}=\phi(q_{2})/q_{2}
. As in
[
15
, Section 7]
we have

\displaystyle M_{k}(q,h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq n_{1}\leq q_{1}}\sum_{1\leq n_{2}\leq q_{2}}(P_{2}D_{1}(n_{1})+D_{2}(n_{1},n_{2}))^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=k}\binom{k}{k_{1}}P_{2}^{k_{1}}\sum_{1\leq n_{1}\leq q_{1}}D_{1}(n_{1})^{k_{1}}\sum_{1\leq n_{2}\leq q_{2}}D_{2}(n_{1},n_{2})^{k_{2}}

where

D_{1}(n_{1})=\sum_{1\leq m\leq h}1_{\gcd(m+n_{1},q_{1})=1}-hP_{1}

and

D_{2}(n_{1},n_{2})=\sum_{1\leq m\leq h}1_{\gcd(m+n_{1},q_{1})=1}1_{\gcd(m+n_{2},q_{2})=1}-P_{2}\sum_{1\leq m\leq h}1_{\gcd(m+n_{1},q_{1})=1}.

To conclude the proof of Theorem
1
therefore, it suffices to show that, for each
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 0
with
k_{1}+k_{2}=k
,

(14)

\sum_{1\leq n_{1}\leq q_{1}}D_{1}(n_{1})^{k_{1}}\sum_{1\leq n_{2}\leq q_{2}}D_{2}(n_{1},n_{2})^{k_{2}}\ll(\log h)^{O(1)}qP_{2}^{-k_{1}}\left((hP_{2})^{\frac{k-1}{2}}+hP_{2}\right).

(Here we have used
P_{1}\geq(\log h)^{-O(1)}
to simplify the right-hand side.) We henceforth fix such
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
. We require the following lemma to control the sum over
D_{2}
, which is a combination of
[
15
, Lemmas 9 and 10]
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
q\geq 1
and
P=\phi(q)/q
. Let
A
be a set of at most
h
integers, such that for each prime divisor
p\mid q
the integers in
A
are distinct modulo
p
, and
p\geq y
. For any
k\geq 1

\sum_{1\leq n\leq q}\left(\sum_{m\in A}1_{\gcd(m+n,q)=1}-\left\lvert A\right\rvert P\right)^{k}=q\sum_{1\leq s\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor}F_{k,s}(P)\left\lvert A\right\rvert^{s}+O\left(q\frac{(hP)^{k}+hP}{y}\right),

where
F_{k,s}(P)
are polynomials in
P
of degree at most
k
with
\left\lvert F_{k,s}(P)\right\rvert\ll P^{s}
.

Using Lemma
4
, if

A=A(n_{1})=\{1\leq m\leq h:\gcd(m+n_{1},q_{1})=1\},

the left-hand side of (
14
) is (using the trivial bound
\left\lvert D_{1}(n_{1})\right\rvert\ll h
for the error term)

q_{2}\sum_{1\leq s\leq\lfloor k_{2}/2\rfloor}F_{k_{2},s}(P_{2})\sum_{n_{1}\leq q_{1}}D_{1}(n_{1})^{k_{1}}\left\lvert A(n_{1})\right\rvert^{s}+O\left(qh^{k_{1}}\frac{(hP_{2})^{k_{2}}+hP_{2}}{y}\right).

Since
y=h^{k}
, this error term is acceptable, as (recalling
k=k_{1}+k_{2}
)

h^{k_{1}}\frac{(hP_{2})^{k_{2}}+hP_{2}}{y}=P_{2}^{k_{2}}+h^{1-k_{2}}P_{2}\ll hP_{2}.

Now note that, by Lemma
3
,

\sum_{n_{1}\leq q_{1}}D_{1}(n_{1})^{k_{1}}\left\lvert A(n_{1})\right\rvert^{s}=M_{k_{1},s}(q_{1},h)\ll(\log y)^{O(1)}h^{\lfloor\frac{k_{1}}{2}\rfloor+s}q_{1}.

It follows that the left-hand side of (
14
) is

\ll qhP_{2}+(\log h)^{O(1)}qh^{\lfloor\frac{k_{1}}{2}\rfloor}\sum_{1\leq s\leq\lfloor k_{2}/2\rfloor}F_{k_{2},s}(P_{2})h^{s}.

(Note that
P_{1}\leq(\log h)^{O(1)}
and hence the first error term is dominated by the second summand of the right-hand side of (
14
).) Since
F_{k_{2},s}(P)\ll P^{s}
the sum here is
\ll(hP_{2})^{\lfloor\frac{k_{2}}{2}\rfloor}+hP_{2}
. The required estimate (
14
) now follows since

h^{\lfloor\frac{k_{1}}{2}\rfloor}\left((hP_{2})^{\lfloor\frac{k_{2}}{2}\rfloor}+hP_{2}\right)\ll P_{2}^{-k_{1}}\left((hP_{2})^{\frac{k-1}{2}}+hP_{2}\right).

Indeed, if
h\leq P_{2}^{-1}
then this is equivalent to

h^{\lfloor\frac{k_{1}}{2}\rfloor}\ll P_{2}^{-k_{1}},

which holds since
\lfloor k_{1}/2\rfloor\leq k_{1}
for all
k_{1}\geq 0
, and if
h>P_{2}^{-1}
this is equivalent to

h^{\lfloor\frac{k_{1}}{2}\rfloor+\lfloor\frac{k_{2}}{2}\rfloor}P_{2}^{\lfloor\frac{k_{2}}{2}\rfloor}\ll h^{\frac{k-1}{2}}P_{2}^{\frac{k_{2}-k_{1}-1}{2}}.

This holds since
P_{2}\leq 1
and
\lfloor k_{2}/2\rfloor\geq(k_{2}-1)/2
, and (since
k
is an odd integer and
k_{1}+k_{2}=k
)

\lfloor\tfrac{k_{1}}{2}\rfloor+\lfloor\tfrac{k_{2}}{2}\rfloor=\frac{k-1}{2}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
2

This proof will follow along the same basic lines as Montgomery and Soundararajan
[
14
]
used to estimate
R_{k}(h)
when
k
is even. Our goal is to estimate
R_{k}(h)
; recall that
R_{k}(h)
is defined to be

R_{k}(h):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\leq h\\
d_{i}\textrm{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{S}_{0}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}).

Throughout this section,
\mathcal{D}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})
will denote an ordered
k
-tuple of integers. Recall our convention that, if
\mathcal{J}=\{j_{1}<\cdots<j_{t}\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}
, then
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{J}}=(d_{j_{1}},\ldots,d_{j_{t}})
is the
\left\lvert\mathcal{J}\right\rvert
-tuple restricted to
\mathcal{J}
. If the elements
d_{1},\dots,d_{k}
are distinct, we will say that
\mathcal{D}
is a
distinct
k
-tuple
. Note that in fact
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{0}
do not depend on the ordering of the tuple, although for technical reasons we find it convenient to work with ordered
k
-tuples throughout.

It is inconvenient that
\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D})
is convergent only if
\mathcal{D}
is a distinct
k
-tuple. To overcome this obstacle, we define the singular series at a
k
-tuple
\mathcal{D}
with respect to a modulus
q
, as defined in
[
17
]
, by

\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D};q):=\prod_{p|q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-k}\left(1-\frac{\nu_{p}(\mathcal{D})}{p}\right),

where as usual
\nu_{p}(\mathcal{D})
is the number of distinct residue classes mod
p
occupied by elements of
\mathcal{D}
. Note that this is a finite Euler product, so it is well-defined even if
\mathcal{D}
has repeated elements. More precisely, this definition has the following immediate consequence for repeated elements:

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{1}
be a
k_{1}
-tuple and let
\mathcal{D}_{2}
be a
k_{2}
-tuple such that
1\leq k_{1}<k_{2}
. Assume that
\mathcal{D}_{1}
has distinct elements and that every element of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
appears at least once in
\mathcal{D}_{2}
(that is,
\mathcal{D}_{1}
is a tuple whose elements are precisely the
distinct
elements of
\mathcal{D}_{2}
in some ordering). Then

\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{2};q)=\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)^{k_{2}-k_{1}}\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{1};q).

Just as for
\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D})
, we also define the refined singular series with respect to a modulus
q
, given by

\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D};q)=\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}(-1)^{k-\left\lvert\mathcal{Q}\right\rvert}\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Q}};q).

We will take the convention that
\mathfrak{S}(\emptyset)=\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\emptyset)=\mathfrak{S}(\emptyset;q)=\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\emptyset;q)=1
for all
q
. For appropriate choice of
q
, the sums
R_{k}(h)
and
V_{k}(q,h)
both have clean expansions in terms of the
\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D};q)
, which we summarise in the following lemma.

Lemma 6
.

Let
h,k\geq 1
. Set
y=h^{k+1}
and
q=\prod_{p\leq y}p
. Then

(15)

V_{k}(q,h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\leq h\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D};q),

and

(16)

R_{k}(h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\leq h\\
d_{i}\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D};q)+O(1).

Proof.

The proof of this lemma is routine, depending on known expansions of
\mathfrak{S}_{0}
and
V_{k}
. By
[
17
, equation (5) and Lemma 2.3]
,

(17)

\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D};q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\\
q_{i}>1\\
q_{i}|q\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu(q_{i})}{\phi(q_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\\
1\leq a_{i}\leq q_{i}\\
\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\\
\sum_{i}a_{i}/q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}e\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{d_{i}a_{i}}{q_{i}}\right).

Equation (
17
), along with the definition of
V_{k}(q,h)
in (
12
), immediately implies (
15
). We now prove equation (
16
). By
[
14
, equations (50) and (51)]
,

R_{k}(h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\leq h\\
d_{i}\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\\
q_{i}>1\\
q_{i}|q\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu(q_{i})}{\phi(q_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\\
1\leq a_{i}\leq q_{i}\\
\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\\
\sum_{i}a_{i}/q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}e\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{d_{i}a_{i}}{q_{i}}\right)+O(1),

when
q=\prod_{p\leq y}p
and
y=h^{k+1}
, which along with (
17
) immediately implies (
16
).
∎

Equations (
15
) and (
16
) differ only (yet crucially) in that the
d_{i}
in (
16
) are required to be distinct, unlike in (
15
). Our goal is to relate
R_{k}(h)
to a sum over
V_{j}(q,h)
for appropriate
j
and
q
, and then apply Theorem
1
. The argument to remove the distinctness condition is in essence a rather involved inclusion-exclusion argument; we will be able to express
R_{k}(h)
as a linear combination of
V_{j}(q,h)
, for different values of
j
.

Set
\delta_{ij}(\mathcal{D})=1
if
d_{i}=d_{j}
and
\delta_{ij}(\mathcal{D})=0
otherwise, so that

1_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\textrm{ are distinct}}=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(1-\delta_{ij}(\mathcal{D}))=\sum_{G\subseteq\binom{k}{2}}(-1)^{\left\lvert G\right\rvert}\prod_{(i,j)\in G}\delta_{ij}(\mathcal{D}).

Note that the product
\prod_{(i,j)\in G}\delta_{ij}(\mathcal{D})
in fact depends only on the connected components of the graph
G
, which is a partition of
\{1,\ldots,k\}
. If
\mathcal{P}
is a partition of
\{1,\ldots,k\}
then we write
G\sim\mathcal{P}
to mean that
\mathcal{P}
lists the connected components of
G
. Therefore, if

\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{D}):=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{ if }d_{i}=d_{j}\textrm{ whenever }i,j\textrm{ are in the same cell of the partition }\mathcal{P}\textrm{ and }\\
0&\textrm{otherwise,}\end{cases}

then whenever
G\sim\mathcal{P}
we have
\prod_{(i,j)\in G}\delta_{ij}(\mathcal{D})=\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{D})
. We have therefore shown that, if

w(\mathcal{P}):=\sum_{G\sim\mathcal{P}}(-1)^{\left\lvert G\right\rvert},

then

1_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\textrm{ are distinct}}=\sum_{\mathcal{P}}w(\mathcal{P})\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{D}).

In particular, inserting this into (
16
) yields

\displaystyle R_{k}(h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{P}}w(\mathcal{P})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\\
1\leq d_{i}\leq h\end{subarray}}\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{D})\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D};q)+O(1).

The tuples
\mathcal{D}
are now (for all except the trivial partition into singletons)
forced
to have repeated elements, but we can remove these repeated elements by applying Lemma
5
. However, Lemma
5
applies to
\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D};q)
and not
\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D};q)
, so in preparation for applying Lemma
5
, we recall the definition of
\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D};q)
to write

\displaystyle R_{k}(h)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\sum_{\mathcal{P}}w(\mathcal{P})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\\
1\leq d_{i}\leq h\end{subarray}}\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{D})\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}(-1)^{|\mathcal{Q}|}\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Q}};q)+O(1).

The task now is to rewrite this sum to decouple the dependence between the tuple
\mathcal{D}
and the partition
\mathcal{P}
.

For a fixed integer
\ell
, denote by
P_{\ell}(z)
the polynomial given by

(18)

P_{\ell}(z):=\frac{(1-z)^{\ell}-1}{z}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{j}(-1)^{j}z^{j-1},

so that for example
P_{1}(z)=-1
,
P_{2}(z)=z-2
,
P_{3}(z)=-z^{2}+3z-3
, and so on. The following lemma shows that
R_{k}(h)
can be written in terms of the polynomials
P_{\ell}(z)
.

Lemma 7
.

Fix a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
\{1,\dots,k\}
, and write
\mathcal{P}=\{S_{1},\dots,S_{M}\}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\\
1\leq d_{i}\leq h\end{subarray}}\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}(-1)^{|\mathcal{Q}|}\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Q}};q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{M}\\
1\leq d_{i}\leq h\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}\subseteq\{1,\dots,M\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{m\in\mathcal{J}}P_{|S_{m}|}\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{J}};q),

where
P_{\ell}(z)
is given in (
18
).

Proof.

By Lemma
5
, any multiple instance of any
d_{i}
can be removed and replaced by a factor of
\frac{q}{\phi(q)}
, so the left-hand side can be expressed as a sum of the form

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{M}\\
1\leq d_{i}\leq h\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}\subseteq\{1,\dots,M\}\end{subarray}}g_{\mathcal{J}}\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{J}};q),

for some polynomials
g_{\mathcal{J}}
. Our aim is to show that for all
\mathcal{J}
,
g_{\mathcal{J}}=\prod_{m\in\mathcal{J}}P_{|S_{m}|}
.

For a fixed
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\{1,\dots,M\}
, the terms contributing to
\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{J}};q)
are precisely those
\mathcal{Q}
such that
|\mathcal{Q}\cap S_{m}|\geq 1
if and only if
m\in\mathcal{J}
, and the contribution from
\mathcal{Q}
is
\prod_{m\in\mathcal{J}}\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)^{|\mathcal{Q}\cap S_{m}|-1}\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{J}};q)
. Thus, the coefficient of
\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{J}};q)
is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}\\
|\mathcal{Q}\cap S_{m}|\geq 1\Leftrightarrow m\in\mathcal{J}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\mathcal{Q}|}

\displaystyle\prod_{m\in\mathcal{J}}\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)^{|\mathcal{Q}\cap S_{m}|-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{m\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{t_{m}=1}^{|S_{m}|}\binom{|S_{m}|}{t_{m}}(-1)^{t_{m}}\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)^{t_{m}-1}.

This expression is precisely
\prod_{m\in\mathcal{J}}P_{|S_{m}|}\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right),
as desired.
∎

Applying Lemma
7
we thus have that
R_{k}(h)
is equal to

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\sum_{\mathcal{P}=\{S_{1},\dots,S_{M}\}}w(\mathcal{P})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{M}\\
1\leq d_{i}\leq h\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}\subseteq\{1,\dots,M\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{m\in\mathcal{J}}P_{|S_{m}|}\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{J}};q)+O(1).

We now note that

\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D};q)=\sum_{\mathcal{R}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{R}};q).

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{M}\\
1\leq d_{i}\leq h\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}\subseteq\{1,\dots,M\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{m\in\mathcal{J}}P_{|S_{m}|}\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{J}};q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{M}\\
1\leq d_{i}\leq h\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{R}\subseteq\{1,\dots,M\}\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,M\}\\
\mathcal{J}\supseteq\mathcal{R}\end{subarray}}\prod_{m\in\mathcal{J}}P_{|S_{m}|}\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)\right)\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{R}};q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{M}\\
1\leq d_{i}\leq h\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathcal{R}\subseteq\{1,\dots,M\}}f_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{P}}\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{R}};q),

say, where

f_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{P}}(z)=\prod_{m\in\mathcal{R}}P_{|S_{m}|}(z)\prod_{m\not\in\mathcal{R}}\left(1+P_{|S_{m}|}(z)\right)

is a polynomial of degree
k-M
, unless (recalling that
P_{1}(z)=-1
) there exists some
m\not\in\mathcal{R}
such that
|S_{m}|=1
, in which case
f_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{P}}(z)=0
.

We are now ready to return to the original computation, whence

\displaystyle R_{k}(h)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\sum_{\mathcal{P}}w(\mathcal{P})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{M}\\
1\leq d_{i}\leq h\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathcal{R}\subseteq\{1,\dots,M\}}f_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{P}}\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{R}};q)+O(1)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\sum_{\mathcal{P}}w(\mathcal{P})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{R}\subseteq\{1,\dots,M\}\end{subarray}}f_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{P}}\left(\frac{q}{\phi(q)}\right)h^{M-|\mathcal{R}|}V_{|\mathcal{R}|}(q,h)+O(1).

The second line follows by applying (
15
); if
|\mathcal{R}|=0
, the term
V_{0}(q,h)
does not appear and in its place is simply
1
(recalling that
\mathfrak{S}_{0}(\mathcal{D}_{\emptyset};q)=1
), but for simplicity we will write
V_{0}(q,h)=1
.

Recall that
q=\prod_{p\leq y}p
, and that in the notation of Theorem
1
,

V_{\mathcal{R}}(q,h)\ll(\log h)^{O(1)}h^{\lfloor\left\lvert\mathcal{R}\right\rvert/2\rfloor}

For a given partition

4.
Further remarks

In this section, we will discuss further directions and open problems, focusing mainly on our bound on fractions. For simplicity we will discuss Theorem
3
, although the same discussion could be applied to the more complicated Theorem
4
.

4.1.
Improving Theorem
3

The bound that we achieve in Theorem
3
is best possible up to the factors of
\log Q
: elementary number theory shows that, for any
1\leq n\leq Q
, the number of
1\leq q\leq Q
and
0\leq a_{1},a_{2}\leq n
such that

\gcd(a_{1},q)=\gcd(a_{2},q)=1

is
\gg n^{2}Q
. Using the trivial identity

\frac{a_{1}}{q}+\frac{a_{2}}{q}-\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}}{q}=0

therefore produces
\gg n^{2}Q
many solutions when
k=3
. (Of course it may not be true that
\gcd(a_{1}+a_{2},q)=1
, but clearly
\tfrac{a_{1}+a_{2}}{q}=\tfrac{a^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
for some
0\leq a^{\prime}\leq n
and
1\leq q^{\prime}\leq Q
with
\gcd(a^{\prime},q^{\prime})=1
.) A similar lower bound of
\gg n^{\frac{k+1}{2}}Q^{\frac{k-1}{2}}
for other odd
k>3
follows by adjoining such triples with diagonal pairs
(\tfrac{a}{q},-\tfrac{a}{q})
.

By a more delicate argument an improved lower bound can be obtained. For example, Blomer and Brüdern
[
1
]
prove that when
k=3
there are
\gg(\log Q)^{3}n^{2}Q
many solutions (and this exponent of
3
is optimal given their coprimality assumptions).

An asymptotic formula for the number of such solutions is not known for any odd
k
, even for
k=3
, although Blomer, Brüdern, and Salberger
[
2
]
proved such a formula in a similar situation (differing only in the coprimality condition) when
n=Q
and
k=3
, and Destagnol
[
7
]
has proved such a formula for arbitrary
k
, again when
n=Q
and under weaker coprimality conditions. It would be of interest to obtain even a reasonable conjecture for what the main term should be; the problem in this generality seems to be out of reach of Manin’s conjecture.

Just as with the case of even
k
, we expect the count for odd
k
to be dominated by ‘degenerate’ solutions (for example, when
k=5
the dominant contribution is likely from those where some subset of three fractions vanishes). It is perhaps then most natural to count only the ‘non-degenerate’ solutions.

More precisely, we say that a solution to

\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{a_{k}}{q_{k}}=0

with
\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1
,
0<|a_{i}|\leq n
, and
1\leq q_{i}\leq Q
is
degenerate
if there exists some proper subset
\emptyset\subsetneq I\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that

\sum_{i\in I}\frac{a_{i}}{q_{i}}=0.

For example, the lower bound described above for odd
k>3
is degenerate. Heuristically, one might expect the number of non-degenerate solutions to be of order
\approx n^{k-1}Q
(without worrying about powers of
\log
). To see this,
3
3

3

We thank Tim Browning for showing us this heuristic.
we begin with
n^{k}Q^{k}
total choices of
a_{i}
and
q_{i}
, and we require that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}q_{j}=0

(after clearing denominators). The left hand side is an integer of size
O(nQ^{k-1})
, so the probability that it is
0
is roughly
\frac{1}{nQ^{k-1}}
, leading to
\frac{n^{k}Q^{k}}{nQ^{k-1}}=n^{k-1}Q
solutions. This heuristic is of the same flavor as some of the reasoning behind Manin’s conjecture, and Heath–Brown’s result
[
12
]
on counting the error term in solutions when
k=4
and
n=1
is consistent with this heuristic.

We are thus led to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
1\leq n\leq Q
and
k\geq 3
be an odd integer. The number of non-degenerate solutions to

\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{a_{k}}{q_{k}}=0\quad\textrm{ where }\quad\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1\textrm{ for }1\leq i\leq k,

with
\left\lvert a_{i}\right\rvert\leq n
and
q_{i}\leq Q
for
1\leq i\leq k
, is

\ll_{k}(\log Q)^{O_{k}(1)}n^{k-1}Q.

A proof of such a result would help significantly in our asymptotic understanding of
M_{k}(q,h)
and
R_{k}(h)
.

4.2.
Improved error bounds for even moments

There is a strong connection between the study of odd moments and the study of lower-order terms in even moments; indeed, in the original work of Montgomery and Vaughan
[
15
]
both were treated essentially identically, resulting in the same bound (namely an exponent of
\frac{k}{2}-\frac{1}{7k}
) for both.

Surprisingly, the methods of this paper do not appear to help with the lower order terms for even moments. A variant strong enough to address Conjecture
1
would be helpful towards this end. Improved bounds for the error terms in even moments would have a number of applications, such as to our understanding of primes in short intervals (following Montgomery and Soundararajan
[
14
]
) and the distribution of squarefree numbers in short intervals (following Gorodetsky, Mangerel, and Rodgers
[
10
]
).

Appendix A
Relative greatest common divisors

In this appendix we give a precise definition for, and prove the necessary properties of, the notion of relative greatest common divisors. Recall that this is the parameterisation of an arbitrary
k
-tuple of natural numbers
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}
by some
2^{k}
integers
g_{I}
, as
I
ranges over subsets of
\{1,\ldots,k\}
, such that
q_{i}=\prod_{I\ni i}g_{I}
and
\gcd(g_{I},g_{J})=1
unless
I\subseteq J
or
J\subseteq I
. We note that we always have
g_{\emptyset}=1
for any
k
-tuple, but it is often convenient for technical reasons to allow
I
to range over all subsets.

This is a generalisation of the notion of greatest common divisors, which can be formulated as the parameterisation of arbitrary pairs of natural numbers
q_{1},q_{2}
by three integers
g_{1},g_{2},g_{12}
where
q_{1}=g_{1}g_{12}
and
q_{2}=g_{2}g_{12}
and
\gcd(g_{1},g_{2})=1
– indeed, we simply choose
g_{12}=\gcd(q_{1},q_{2})
.

The appropriate generalisation to arbitrary
k
-tuples was first given by Dedekind
[
6
]
in 1897, but was subsequently rediscovered by Bell, Schinzel, Gupta, Hall, and others. The theory was further developed, for applications to the study of Egyptian fractions, by Elsholtz, and we refer to the thesis of Elsholtz
[
8
]
for further historical remarks and discussion.

It is most common to define relative greatest common divisors recursively, beginning with
I=\{1,\ldots,k\}
, where

g_{\{1,\ldots,k\}}=\mathrm{gcd}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}),

and in general, for
\emptyset\neq I\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,k\}
, define

g_{I}=\frac{\gcd(q_{i}:i\in I)}{\prod_{J\supsetneq I}g_{J}},

so that

\prod_{J\supset I}g_{I}=\gcd(q_{i}:i\in I).

The following equivalent ‘local’ definition is more explicit and can be easier to manipulate in practice.

Definition 1
.

For any integers
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}
and non-empty
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}
we define the
relative greatest common divisor

g_{I}
as follows. It suffices to define
\nu_{p}(I)
for all primes
p
and all
\emptyset\neq I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}
, where
\nu_{p}(I)
denotes the greatest exponent such that
p^{\nu_{p}(I)}
divides
g_{I}
. For this, without loss of generality, we may assume that the
q_{i}
are ordered such that

\nu_{p}(q_{1})\leq\cdots\leq\nu_{p}(q_{k}).

With this ordering, we define
\nu_{p}(\{1,\ldots,k\})=\nu_{p}(q_{1})
and for all
2\leq i\leq k
set

\nu_{p}(\{i,\ldots,k\})=\nu_{p}(q_{i})-\nu_{p}(q_{i-1}),

and set
\nu_{p}(I)=0
for all other sets
I
. (Note that
\nu_{p}(I)\geq 0
for all
p
and
I
, and so the
g_{I}
are indeed integers.)

For example, if
q_{1}=6
and
q_{2}=9
and
q_{3}=12
then
2
-adically we have
\nu_{2}(\{1,2,3\})=0
and
\nu_{2}(\{1,3\})=\nu_{2}(\{3\})=1
(and otherwise
\nu_{2}(I)=0
), and
3
-adically we have
\nu_{3}(\{1,2,3\})=\nu_{3}(\{2\})=1
(and otherwise
\nu_{3}(I)=0
), and therefore

g_{1}=g_{12}=g_{23}=1\quad g_{3}=g_{13}=2\quad g_{2}=g_{123}=3.

The following lemma captures all the basic properties of relative greatest common divisors that we will use (in fact we will never use the third, but it is worth recording nonetheless). Note that the first property is equivalent to the relation for the recursive definition of relative gcds, when applied to
I=\{i\}
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}\geq 1
be any integers, and define the corresponding
g_{I}
for
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}
as above. We have:

(1)

for any
1\leq i\leq k

q_{i}=\prod_{i\in I}g_{I},

(2)

if
\gcd(g_{I},g_{J})>1
then either
I\subseteq J
or
J\subseteq I
, and

(3)

if all
q_{i}
are squarefree then
\gcd(g_{I},g_{J})=1
for all
I\neq J
.

Proof.

It suffices to give local proofs of each property, so fix some prime
p
and order the
q_{i}
such that

\nu_{p}(q_{1})\leq\cdots\leq\nu_{p}(q_{k}).

The first property follows from the fact that, for
1\leq i\leq k
,

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(q_{i})

\displaystyle=(\nu_{p}(q_{i})-\nu_{p}(q_{i-1}))+\cdots+(\nu_{p}(q_{2})-\nu_{p}(q_{1}))+\nu_{p}(q_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq j\leq i}\nu_{p}(\{j,j+1,\ldots,k\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{p}(I),

since
\nu_{p}(I)=0
if
I
is not of the shape
\{j,j+1,\ldots,k\}
for some
1\leq j\leq k
.

For the second property, note that if
\min(\nu_{p}(I),\nu_{p}(J))>0
then there must exist some
i,j
such that
I=\{i,\ldots,k\}
and
J=\{j,\ldots,k\}
, whence
J\subseteq I
or
I\subseteq J
as required.

Finally, the third property follows from the observation that if
\nu_{p}(q_{i})\in\{0,1\}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
then by definition there is exactly one
I
such that
\nu_{p}(I)\neq 0
(namely if
0=\nu_{p}(q_{1})=\cdots\nu_{p}(q_{i})
and
1=\nu_{p}(q_{i+1})=\cdots=\nu_{p}(q_{k})
then
I=\{i+1,\ldots,k\}
).
∎
