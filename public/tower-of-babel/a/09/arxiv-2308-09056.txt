When are permutation invariants Cohen-Macaulay?
H.E.A. Campbell 1 and David L. Wehlau 1,2

Abstract
. Over a field of characteristic 0, every ring of invariants of any finite group is Cohen-Macaulay. This is not true for fields of positive characteristic. We consider permutation representations and their invariant rings over fields 𝔽 p \mathbb{F}_{p} of prime order. We give an efficient algorithm which for any given permutation representation, determines those primes p p for which the invariant ring over 𝔽 p \mathbb{F}_{p} is Cohen-Macaulay. Extensions to subgroups of reflection groups other than the symmetric group are indicated.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 13A50; Secondary 20F55, 14M05

1.
Introduction

Let
R
be a ring and suppose the finite group
G
acts on the graded
polynomial ring
R[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
. The set of polynomials fixed pointwise by each element of
G
forms a subring known as the ring of
invariants and denoted
R[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]^{G}
. Here we consider whether
this ring of invariants is Cohen-Macaulay. We are mainly concerned with the case where
R
is a finite field of prime order and
G
acts by permuting
the variables
x_{i}
.

If
R=k
is a field with algebraic closure
\widehat{k}
then we may view
k[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
as a subring of
\widehat{k}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
which is the
coordinate ring of the
n
dimensional affine space

\widehat{V}=\mathbb{A}^{n}=\widehat{k}^{n}
.
Here
\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\}
is a basis for the dual space
\widehat{V}^{*}
and thus
the variables
x_{i}
are linear functions on
\widehat{V}
.
Let
\{e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n}\}
be the basis of
\widehat{V}
dual to
\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\}

and write
V
to denote the
k
-vector space spanned by
\{e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n}\}
.
Following the usual convention we write

\widehat{k}[\widehat{V}]=\widehat{k}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
.
More generally, we will write
R[V]
to denote
R[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
.
The action of
G
on
k[V]
corresponds to a
G
-action
on
V
such that
(g\cdot f)(v)=f(g^{-1}v)
for
g\in G
,
f\in k[V]
and
v\in V
. With this identification, the invariant polynomials are precisely the elements of
k[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]

which are constant on the
G
-orbits in
\widehat{V}
.

By Hochster and Eagon (
[
11
]
),
k[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay if
k
is any field of characteristic 0. However for fields of positive characteristic
this may fail.
Smith
[
14
]
showed in 1996 that
k[V]^{G}
is always Cohen-Macaulay if
k
is a field and the dimension of
V
is 3 or less.

The case where
G
acts by permuting the variables
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
is of particular interest. In this case
G
is a subgroup of the full group of
permutations on
n
letters,
S_{n}
. For
G\leqslant S_{n}
,
Blum-Smith and Marques
[
2
]
showed that
\mathbb{Z}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if the action of

G
on
V
is generated by reflections and bi-reflections.
Reflections are elements whose fixed point set is a codimension 1 subspace of
V
and bi-reflections are elements whose fixed point set is a codimension 2 subspace of
V
.
The reflections in
S_{n}
are the transpositions and the bi-reflections in
S_{n}

are the products of two different transpositions, which includes all 3-cycles.
Note that the identity element is neither a reflection nor a bi-reflection .

It is well-known that
\mathbb{Z}[V]^{S_{n}}
and
\mathbb{Q}[V]^{S_{n}}=\mathbb{Z}[V]^{S_{n}}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
are both
polynomial rings; the so-called rings of symmetric functions.
In fact
R[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]^{S_{n}}
is a polynomial ring for all rings
R
.

We can give a definition of Cohen-Macaulayness tailored for this setting. The ring of
G
-invariants
R[V]^{G}
forms a finitely generated module over
R[V]^{S_{n}}
and we have
R[V]^{G}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}R[V]^{S_{n}}\theta_{j}
for some
\theta_{j}\in R[V]^{G}
. It is known that
t\geqslant\ell:=[S_{n}:G]
,
[
7
, Thm 3.9.1, pg 113]
.
The ring
R[V]^{G}
is said to be
Cohen-Macaulay
if it is free as a
R[V]^{S_{n}}
-module.
It can be shown that
R[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
t=\ell
.
In this case
R[V]^{G}=\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell}R[V]^{S_{n}}\theta_{j}
and
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell}
is known as a set of
secondary invariants
for
G
. Generators for
R[V]^{S_{n}}
are known as primary invariants. In fact, any homogeneous system of parameters for
R[V]^{G}
forms a set of primary invariants, but for this article we will only use algebra generators for
R[V]^{S_{n}}
as primary invariants.
The above definition of Cohen-Macaulayness is sufficient for our purposes. For a definition valid in more general settings we refer the reader
to Section 3 of
[
16
]
or Section 2.1 of
[
2
]
.

By Hochster and Eagon
[
11
, Proposition 19]
,

\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay, i.e., it is free (of rank
\ell
) as a
\mathbb{Q}[V]^{S_{n}}
-module.
If
p\in\mathbb{Z}
is a prime then, reducing mod
p
, the representation of
G
descends naturally to a representation of
G

defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p}=\mathbb{Z}/(p)
. For primes
p
not dividing
|G|
the ring
\mathbb{F}[V]^{G}=\mathbb{F}_{p}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]^{G}
is always Cohen-Macaulay,
again by Hochster and Eagon
[
11
, Proposition 19]
.
However for those primes
p
which
do divide
|G|
, the ring
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
may or may not be Cohen-Macaulay.

2.
Summary of the Main Results

Suppose that
V
is a permutation representation of a group
G
and let

{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell})
be a sequence of elements of
\mathbb{Z}[V]^{G}
where
\ell
denotes the index of
G
in
S_{n}
.
Let
{\overline{\theta}}_{j}
denote the reduction modulo
p
of the integer polynomial
\theta_{j}
.
We construct an
\ell\times\ell
matrix
M({\bm{\theta}})
with entries in
\mathbb{Z}[V]^{G}
.

Then our main results may be summarized as follows.

(1)

\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay with
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}=\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{S_{n}}{\overline{\theta}}_{j}

if and only if
p
does not divide the content,
\mho({\bm{\theta}})
, of
\det M({\bm{\theta}})
.

(2)

There exists a set of invariants
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{Z}[V]^{G}
such that
for every prime
p
we have

\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
p
does not divide the
content of
\det M({\bm{\theta}})
.

(3)

We give an algorithm for constructing such a set of universal secondary invariants.

(4)

We give an efficient algorithm for finding the content of

\det M({\bm{\theta}})
.

Furthermore, the above results generalize to subgroups
G
of complex reflection groups

\Sigma
provided the relevant ring of integers is a PID.

(5)

For a subgroup,
G
of a complex reflection group
\Sigma
, we define
a natural semi-invariant, the
G
-discriminant
\Delta(G)
, depending of the generating reflections
of
G
and the action of
\Sigma
on
\Sigma/G
.

(6)

We show that

\Delta(G)
divides
\det M({\bm{\theta}})
, and obtain
\mho({\bm{\theta}})\Delta(G)=\det M({\bm{\theta}})
.

3.
Preliminaries.

For an introduction to the invariant theory of finite groups and descriptions of the important results we refer the reader to the wonderful survey article by Richard Stanley
[
16
]
.
Both computational and theoretical aspects of invariant theory are surveyed in
[
7
]
.
Facts about invariants of finite groups specifically in positive characteristic may be found in

[
6
]
.

Since the group action preserves degree, rings of invariants are graded rings:

R[V]^{G}=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}R[V]^{G}_{d}
. Over a field
k
we have
the Hilbert series of
k[V]^{G}
. This is the power series

\mathcal{H}(k[V]^{G},\lambda)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\dim_{k}(k[V]^{G}_{d})\,\lambda^{d}\ .

This is always a
rational function and for a subgroup
G\subseteq S_{n}
it may be written in the
form

\mathcal{H}(k[V]^{G},\lambda)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\lambda^{a_{j}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\lambda^{i})}

where
\ell=[S_{n}:G]
and the
a_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Furthermore we have
\deg\theta_{j}=a_{j}
for
j=1,2,\dots,\ell
for any set of secondaries
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell}
.
Molien’s Theorem (see
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
) provides a simple formula
for the Hilbert series of a ring of invariants when
|G|
is
invertible in
k
. Thus Molien’s Theorem may be used to find
the degrees of secondary invariants for
\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G}
.

M. Göbel proved a theorem
[
9
, Theorem 3.11]
which is usually described as asserting that for subgroups
G\leqslant S_{n}
the ring
\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G}
is always generated as an algebra by homogeneous invariant polynomials of degree at most
\max\{n,\binom{n}{2}\}
. In fact his proof demonstrates that for
G\leqslant S_{n}
there is always a set of homogeneous secondary invariants each of degree at most
\binom{n}{2}
.

For
G\leqslant S_{n}
there is a natural vector space basis for

(\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G})_{d}
given by orbit sums. Let
m=x_{1}^{i_{1}}x_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}
be a monomial of degree
d
. The
G
-orbit of

m
is a finite set of monomials
of degree
d
and the sum of these monomials is an invariant
which we denote by
\mathcal{Z}(m)
and call the orbit sum of
m
.
The set of orbit sums of monomials of degree
d
is clearly a basis for
(\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G})_{d}
. This shows that

\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G})_{d}
is the number of orbits of the
G
-action on the set of monomials of degree
d
.

Furthermore if we reduce mod
p
the reduced orbit sums
\overline{\mathcal{Z}(m)}
form a basis for
(\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G})_{d}
. Thus

\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G},\lambda)=\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G},\lambda)
.
In particular the multisets of the degrees of secondaries for

\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G}
and for
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
are equal if
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay.

4.
Good and Bad Primes

Definition 4.1
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{Z}
be a prime.
and let
\mathbb{F}_{p}
denote the field
\mathbb{F}_{p}=\mathbb{Z}/(p)
of order
and characteristic
p
.
Consider a set of secondary invariants

{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell})

for
\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G}
with each
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[V]^{G}
.
Thus
\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G}=\oplus_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{Q}[V]^{S_{n}}\theta_{i}
.
Reducing
\theta_{i}
modulo
p
yields
{\overline{\theta}}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
.
Following Blum-Smith and Marques
[
2
]
,
we say
p
is
good

if with
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}=\oplus_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}[V]^{S_{n}}{\overline{\theta}}_{i}
. Otherwise
p
is
bad
for
{\bm{\theta}}
.
Note that if
p
is good for
{\bm{\theta}}
then
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay but
the converse is false in general:
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
is not Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
p
is bad for
{\bm{\theta}}
for all
{\bm{\theta}}
.

We will use
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{\ell}
to denote a set of left
coset representatives of
G
in
S_{n}
.

Given a set of secondaries
{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell})
for

\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G}
with each
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[V]^{G}
we write
M({\bm{\theta}})
to
denote the
\ell\times\ell
matrix

M({\bm{\theta}})=[\gamma_{i}\theta_{j}]_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell\end{subarray}}
.
Fix a prime
p
. We write
M(\overline{{\bm{\theta}}})
to denote
the matrix
M(\overline{{\bm{\theta}}})=[\gamma_{i}{\overline{\theta}}_{j}]_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell\end{subarray}}
.
Thus
M(\overline{{\bm{\theta}}})
is the natural image
of
M({\bm{\theta}})
with entries in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]
obtained by reduction modulo
p
.

For an integer polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[V]
the
content
of
f

is the
\gcd
of the coefficients of
f
. The
primitive part
of such a polynomial is the quotient of the polynomial by its content.

Definition 4.2
.

We denote the absolute value of the content of the polynomial
\det M({\bm{\theta}})
by

\mho({\bm{\theta}})
.
We call the positive integer
\mho({\bm{\theta}})
the
deficiency
of the
sequence
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell}
.

4.1.
Bad Primes

Fix a set of secondaries
{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell})
for

\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G}
with each
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[V]^{G}
.
In this section we will show that a prime
p
is bad for

{\bm{\theta}}
if and only if
p
divides
\mho({\bm{\theta}})
.

For each degree
d
we have the map

\rho_{d}:\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{Z}[V]^{S_{n}}_{d-a_{j}}\to\mathbb{Z}[V]_{d}^{G}

given by

\rho_{d}(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{\ell})=f_{1}\theta_{1}+f_{2}\theta_{2}+\dots+f_{\ell}\theta_{\ell}
.

Reducing
\rho_{d}
modulo
p
gives the map

\overline{\rho}_{d}:\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{S_{n}}_{d-a_{j}}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]_{d}^{G}

given by

\overline{\rho}_{d}(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{\ell})=f_{1}{\overline{\theta}}_{1}+f_{2}{\overline{\theta}}_{2}+\dots+f_{\ell}{\overline{\theta}}_{\ell}
.

Now
p
is good for
{\bm{\theta}}
if and only if the
\ell
elements of
\overline{{\bm{\theta}}}
form a module basis for

\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{S_{n}}
if and only if
\overline{\rho}_{d}
is surjective for all
d
.
Since
\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay, the linear transformation

\rho_{d}\otimes\mathbb{Q}:\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{Q}[V]^{S_{n}}_{d-a_{j}}\to\mathbb{Q}[V]_{d}^{G}

is an isomorphism for each
d
.
From the equality of the Hilbert series we know that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}\left(\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{S_{n}}_{d-a_{j}}\right)=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}_{d}
. Hence

\overline{\rho}_{d}
is surjective if and only if it is
injective.

Lemma 4.3
.

The multi-homogeneous
\ell
-tuple
(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{\ell})\in(\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{S_{n}})^{\ell}
lies in the kernel of
\overline{\rho_{d}}
if and only it lies in the kernel of the matrix
M(\overline{{\bm{\theta}}})
.

Proof.

The sufficiency is obvious.

Suppose then that
f_{1}{\overline{\theta}}_{1}+f_{2}{\overline{\theta}}_{2}+\dots+f_{\ell}{\overline{\theta}}_{\ell}=0
.
Therefore

0=\gamma_{i}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}f_{j}{\overline{\theta}}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}(\gamma_{i}\cdot f_{j})(\gamma_{i}\cdot{\overline{\theta}}_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}(f_{j})(\gamma_{i}\cdot{\overline{\theta}}_{j})

for all
i
. Thus
(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{\ell})
lies in the kernel of
M(\overline{{\bm{\theta}}})
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

The prime
p\text{ divides }\mho({\bm{\theta}})
if and only if
p
is bad for
{\bm{\theta}}
.

Proof.

Any set of secondaries
\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell}\}
is linearly
independent.
It follows, since
\gamma_{1}=e
, that that the set of
\ell
vectors

\{(\gamma_{1}\theta_{j},\gamma_{2}\theta_{j},\dots,\gamma_{\ell}\theta_{j})\mid 1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell)\}
is a linearly independent subset of
\mathbb{Q}[V]^{\ell}
.
Thus
\det M({\bm{\theta}})\neq 0
.
Therefore
\det M(\overline{{\bm{\theta}}})=0
if and only if

p
divides
\mho({\bm{\theta}})
. Hence

\displaystyle p\text{ divides }\mho({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle\iff\det M(\overline{{\bm{\theta}}})=0

\displaystyle\iff\overline{\rho_{d}}\text{ is not injective for some }d

\displaystyle\iff\overline{\rho_{d}}\text{ is not surjective for some }d

\displaystyle\iff p\text{ is a bad prime for }{\bm{\theta}}\ .

∎

Example 4.5
.

The symmetric group
S_{5}
contains a unique (up to conjugacy) subgroup
G
of order 20. This group is generated by

\{(1,5,4,2,3),~(2,3,4,5)\}
and does not have a set of generators consisting of reflections and bi-reflections. Thus the theorem of Blum-Smith and Marques implies that there is at least one bad prime. Since this prime divides
|G|
we see that 2 and/or 5 are bad primes for this group. Using the algorithm of Section 10 with
\mathbb{Z}[V]=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}]
we get the following set of (universal) secondaries

\theta_{1}=1
,

\theta_{2}=\mathcal{Z}(x_{1}^{2}x_{2}x_{3})
,
\theta_{3}=\mathcal{Z}(x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}x_{3})
,
\theta_{4}=\mathcal{Z}(x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}x_{3})
,
\theta_{5}=\mathcal{Z}(x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}x_{3}x_{5})
, and

\theta_{6}=\mathcal{Z}(x_{1}^{4}x_{2}^{3}x_{4})
. This yields
\mho({\bm{\theta}})=2
and thus
\mho(G)=2
. Thus
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
p\neq 2
.

Since
\mathbb{Z}[V]^{G}
is not Cohen-Macaulay, we know there is a
G
-invariant of degree less than or equal to Goebel’s bound
\binom{5}{2}=10
that is not in
\mathcal{M}=\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{Z}[V]^{S_{n}}\theta_{j}
. Magma computation shows us that
f=\mathcal{Z}(x_{1}^{4}x_{2}^{3}x_{3}^{2}x_{4})
is not in
\mathcal{M}
but we have
2f\in\mathcal{M}
.

4.2.
Good Primes

In this section we illustrate, from another point of view, why
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay for primes not dividing
\mho({\bm{\theta}})
.
Suppose we have chosen secondary invariants
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}[V]

for
\mathbb{Q}[V]^{G}
which reduce to secondary invariants for
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
.

Let
\widehat{\mathbb{F}}
denote the algebraic closure of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
and write
\widehat{V}:=V\otimes\widehat{\mathbb{F}}
.
Dual to the inclusions of rings

\widehat{\mathbb{F}}[\widehat{V}]^{S_{n}}\hookrightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{F}}[\widehat{V}]^{G}\hookrightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{F}}[\widehat{V}]

are the morphisms of affine varieties

\widehat{V}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{G}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\widehat{V}/\!\!/G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\widehat{V}/\!\!/S_{n}}\ .

Then
\pi_{S_{n}}=\phi\circ\pi_{G}
is the quotient by
S_{n}
.

Consider
z\in\widehat{V}
such that the orbit
\{g\cdot z\mid g\in S_{n}\}
has cardinality

|S_{n}|
.
Then
\phi^{-1}(\pi_{S_{n}}(z))
is a set of
\ell
points in
{\widehat{V}/\!\!/G}
. Concretely, the

S_{n}
-orbit of
z
breaks up into
\ell
different
G
-orbits, and the
\ell
points of

\phi^{-1}(\pi_{S_{n}}(z))
are the points in
{\widehat{V}/\!\!/G}
corresponding to these orbits.
Write
X:=\phi^{-1}(\pi_{S_{n}}(z))=\{w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{\ell}\}
where we may assume
\gamma_{i}^{-1}z=w_{i}
.
Then
M(\overline{{\bm{\theta}}})_{|z}=[(\gamma_{i}\overline{\theta}_{j})(z)]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant\ell}=[\overline{\theta}_{j}(w_{i})]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant\ell}
.

For each
i=1,2,\dots,\ell
consider the vector
(\overline{\theta}_{1}(w_{i}),\overline{\theta}_{2}(w_{i}),\dots,\overline{\theta}_{\ell}(w_{i}))
.
Showing that

\det(\overline{M}({\bm{\theta}})_{|z})\neq 0
is equivalent to showing that these
\ell
vectors are linearly independent over

\widehat{\mathbb{F}}
.

The set
X
is a closed subset of
{\widehat{V}/\!\!/G}
. This implies the restriction map from

{\widehat{V}/\!\!/G}
to
X

corresponds to a surjection onto the ring of functions on
X
. But since
X
is a set of
\ell
points, this ring of functions is the ring

\widehat{\mathbb{F}}^{\ell}
(with componentwise multiplication). Since
p
is a good prime we have

\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}=\oplus_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{S_{n}}{\overline{\theta}}_{i}
and thus

\widehat{\mathbb{F}}[\widehat{V}]^{G}=\oplus_{i=1}^{\ell}\widehat{\mathbb{F}}[\widehat{V}]^{S_{n}}{\overline{\theta}}_{i}
. Now for
f\in\widehat{\mathbb{F}}[\widehat{V}]^{S_{n}}
, the restriction of
f
to
X
is just

(f(z),f(z),\dots,f(z))
. Hence the image of the restriction of
\widehat{\mathbb{F}}[\widehat{V}]^{S_{n}}
to
X
is the same as the constant functions
on
X
. This shows that the image of restricting
\widehat{\mathbb{F}}[\widehat{V}]^{G}
is spanned by the
\ell
functions

{{\overline{\theta}}_{1}}\rvert{{}_{X}},{{\overline{\theta}}_{2}}\rvert_{X},\dots,{{\overline{\theta}}_{\ell}}\rvert_{X}
. Since we know the ring of functions on
X
has
vector space dimension
\ell
it follows that these
\ell
restrictions must be linearly independent over
\widehat{\mathbb{F}}
.
Since these vectors are the columns of
M(\overline{{\bm{\theta}}})\rvert_{z}
, it follows that
\det M(\overline{{\bm{\theta}}})\rvert_{z}\neq 0
. This shows that
if
p
is a good prime for

\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell}
then
p
does not divide
\mho({\bm{\theta}})
.

Example 4.6
.

Consider the alternating group

G=A_{n}\leq S_{n}
. Since
A_{n}
is generated by 3-cycles which are bi-reflections, the theorem of Blum-Smith and Marques implies that
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{A_{n}}
is Cohen-Macaulay for all primes
p
. This result was first proved by Vic Reiner
[
12
]
. Here we show how it follows from our methods.

There are two natural choices for secondary invariants:

\{1,{\emph{disc}}\}
where

{\emph{disc}}=\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}(x_{i}-x_{j})
and

\{1,\mathcal{Z}(m)\}
where

m=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{n-i}
.

Take
\sigma\in S_{n}\setminus A_{n}
.
Then
\sigma\cdot{\emph{disc}}=-{\emph{disc}}
and
\sigma\cdot\mathcal{Z}(m)=\mathcal{Z}({m^{\prime}})
where

m^{\prime}=x_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n-2}x_{i}^{n-i}
.

Thus
M(1,{\emph{disc}})=\begin{pmatrix}1&{\emph{disc}}\\
1&-{\emph{disc}}\end{pmatrix}

and
\det(M)=-2\,{\emph{disc}}
and

\mho(1,{\emph{disc}})=2
.
Thus 2 is bad for
(1,{\emph{disc}})
and all other primes are good.

Conversely
M(1,\mathcal{Z}(m))=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathcal{Z}(m)\\
1&\mathcal{Z}({m^{\prime}})\end{pmatrix}

and
\det(M)=\mathcal{Z}({m^{\prime}})-\mathcal{Z}({m})=-{\emph{disc}}
and

\mho(1,\mathcal{Z}(m))=1
.
Hence all primes are good for
(1,\mathcal{Z}(m))
.

5.
Existence of Universal Secondaries

In the previous example, we saw that there was a choice of secondary invariants
which was good for all primes.
We want to show that in general there is a set of secondary invariants
which is good for all good primes
p
, i.e., a set

\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}[V]^{G}
for which

\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}=\oplus_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{S_{n}}{\overline{\theta}}_{i}
for all good primes.
We call such a set of secondaries a set of
universal secondaries
.

Let
S
be the (finite) set of bad primes and write
\mathbb{Z}_{S}
to denote the integers localized at the multiplicative set generated by
S
.

Recall that
\mathcal{H}(k[V]^{G},\lambda)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\lambda^{a_{j}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\lambda^{i})}
for
k=\mathbb{F}_{p}
and for
k=\mathbb{Q}
.
Since the Hilbert series is the same for all these
k
we see that the number of secondaries of degree
d
, is the same for all
k
for which
k[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay. We denote this number by
\tau(d):=\#\{j\mid a_{j}=d\}
.

Consider the exact sequence

\bigoplus_{d_{1}=1}^{d}(\mathbb{Z}_{S}[V]^{S_{n}}_{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{S}[V]^{G}_{d-d_{1}})\xrightarrow{~\nu_{d}~}\mathbb{Z}_{S}[V]^{G}_{d}\longrightarrow Q_{d}\longrightarrow 0

where
Q_{d}
is the co-kernel of
\nu_{d}
.

Tensoring with
\mathbb{F}_{p}=\mathbb{Z}/(p)
yields the exact sequence

\bigoplus_{d_{1}=1}^{d}(\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{S_{n}}_{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}_{d-d_{1}})\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}_{d}\longrightarrow Q_{d}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow 0\ .

The cokernel
Q_{d}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{p}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
vector space of dimension
\tau(d)
for all
good primes
p
.
Thus
Q_{d}
is a finitely generated torsion free
\mathbb{Z}_{S}
-module.
Every finitely generated torsion-free module over a PID (such as
\mathbb{Z}_{S}
) is free.

Thus
Q_{d}
is a free
\mathbb{Z}_{S}
-module and thus the map
\nu_{d}
splits.
Choosing a basis
f_{1},\dots,f_{\tau(d)}
for
Q_{d}
and a section

\psi_{d}:Q_{d}\to\mathbb{Z}_{S}[V]^{G}_{d}
of
\nu_{d}
we may use
\psi_{d}(f_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}_{S}[V]^{G}
for
i=1,2,\dots,\tau(d)
as the secondaries of degree
d
.
Clearing denominators, we may assume that each of these secondaries lies in
\mathbb{Z}[V]^{G}
.

Using such
\psi_{d}(f_{i})
as the secondaries
\theta_{j}
we get

\mathbb{Z}_{S}[V]^{G}=\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{Z}_{S}[V]^{S_{n}}\theta_{j}

and for all good primes
p
we have
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}=\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{S_{n}}{\overline{\theta}}_{j}
.

We write
\mho(G)
to denote
\mho({\bm{\theta}})
where

{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell})
is a set of universal secondaries.
Thus we have proved

Theorem 5.1
.

\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
p
does not divide
\mho(G)
.

Ben Blum-Smith has observed that the existence of a set of universal secondary invariants
can also be derived a consequence of Theorem 7 from the recent paper
[
1
]
of Areej Almuhaimeed.

In Sections
10
and
9
below, we will give an algorithm for finding a set of universal
secondaries and an efficient algorithm for determining
\mho(G)
from a universal set.

6.
Extension to subgroups of other complex reflection groups

We can extend the ideas discussed above to a more general setting.
The above results rely on the fact that
G\leqslant{S_{n}}
where
\mathbb{Q}[V]^{S_{n}}
is a polynomial ring. In general,
\mathbb{C}[V]^{\Sigma}
is a polynomial ring if and only
is
\Sigma
is a reflection group, i.e., is generated by reflections.

Suppose that
\Sigma
is a complex reflection group. Then
\Sigma
is defined over some number field
K
. Shephard and Todd
[
13
]
classified all irreducible complex reflection groups. The article
[
5
]
includes tables giving the
number field
K
for each of the groups in the list of Shephard and Todd.
Write
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
to denote the ring of integers of
K
and suppose

G\leqslant\Sigma\leqslant\text{GL}(n,{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}})
. Let
\wp
be a prime ideal in
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
.
Then
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}/\wp=\mathbb{F}_{q}
for some prime power
q
.
Then we may hope to apply the same techniques as used above to determine the primes
\wp\subset{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
for which
\mathbb{F}_{q}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay. Of course,
by
[
14
]
we are only concerned with representations
V
with
\dim V\geqslant 4
.

Some difficulties must be overcome. The proofs that sets of universal secondaries exist require the underlying ring of integers to be a PID. Thus we require
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
to have class number 1 if we want to be sure universal secondaries exist.

Another difficulty is that the generators of the ring
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}[V]^{\Sigma}
may have different degrees than those of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[V]^{\Sigma}
. This is one of the causes of the most serious difficulty: the rings
K[V]^{G}
and
\mathbb{F}_{q}[V]^{G}
often fail to have the same Hilbert series (for an example see Example
6.1
below).

Nevertheless, there are some subgroups of complex reflection
groups other than
S_{n}
for which none of the above difficulties occur and
we may directly use the above techniques to determine for which primes

\wp\subset{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
the ring
\mathbb{F}_{q}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay.

For example, consider the groups listed second on the list of Shephard and Todd.
These groups are
G(m,t,n)
where
t
divides
m
.
They are the irreducible imprimitive reflection groups.
Since
G(m,t,n)
is a subgroup (of index
t
) of
G(m,1,n)
we may restrict our
attention to
G\leqslant G(m,1,n)
. This group
is the set of
n\times n
matrices having only 1 non-zero entry in each row and column and with each of the non-zero entries being an
m^{\text{th}}
root of unity.

The group
G(m,1,n)
is defined over the ring of integers

{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{m}]
for the number field
\mathbb{Q}[\zeta_{m}]
where

\zeta_{m}
is a primitive
m^{\text{th}}
root of unity. The ring

\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{m}]
may fail to have class number 1.
Surprisingly all the other groups listed by Shephard and Todd are defined over
rings
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
having class number 1. However this list is the
list of irreducible complex reflection groups. Taking a direct sum of two or
more yields a reflection group and the corresponding ring of integers may
fail to have class number 1.

The invariants of the group
G=G(m,1,n)
are spanned by orbit sums meaning the Hilbert
series of
K[V]^{G}
and
\mathbb{F}_{q}[V]^{G}
are the same. Thus if

m
is such that
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{m}]
is a PID then we may apply our methods
to the invariants of subgroups of
G(m,1,n)
.

Example 6.1
.

As in Example
8.3
we consider the subgroup
G=G(2,2,n)
of the
Shephard-Todd group
\Sigma=G(2,1,n)
. To simplify our analysis we
suppose
n\geqslant 3
is odd.
The diagonal matrices in
\Sigma
form a normal subgroup

of order
2^{n}
.
It is easy to see that

\mathbb{Z}[V]^{H}=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2},\dots,x_{n}^{2}]

. Thus
\mathbb{Z}[V]^{\Sigma}=(\mathbb{Z}[V]^{H})^{\Sigma/H}=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2},\dots,x_{n}^{2}]^{S_{n}}
is the polynomial ring of
symmetric functions in the

.

The group
G
is of index 2 in
\Sigma
and fixes the monomial

whose square lies in
\mathbb{Z}[V]^{\Sigma}
.
Furthermore,

of odd degree

, is the only

-invariant (up to scalar multiples) in degree

.
Thus

is a set of (universal) secondary invariants and the matrix

1&-s_{n}\end{pmatrix}

has determinant
-2s_{n}
.

This shows that

is Cohen-Macaulay for all primes

.
For the prime

, reducing the entries of matrices in
\Sigma

does not yield a faithful representation of
\Sigma
.
Thus

\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{F}_{2}[V]^{\Sigma},\lambda)\neq\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{Q}[V]^{\Sigma},\lambda)
.

Consider a monomial

and its

-orbit sum
\mathcal{Z}(m)
.
If there exists

even and

.
If every

.
If every

is odd, then

\mathcal{Z}(m)=s_{n}\mathcal{Z}(m/s_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2},\dots,x_{n}^{2}]^{S_{n}}s_{n}=\mathbb{Z}[V]^{\Sigma}s_{n}
.
Therefore
\mathbb{Z}[V]^{G}=\mathbb{Z}[V]^{\Sigma}\oplus\mathbb{Z}[V]^{\Sigma}s_{n}

is the polynomial ring generated by

together with the first
n-1

elementary symmetric functions in
x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2},\dots,x_{n}^{2}
. In particular the
ring
\mathbb{Z}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay.

Another class of examples using more general reflection groups

is
given by considering Young subgroups of

.
These are groups of the form

.

Example 6.2
.

Consider the inclusion

\rho:S_{n-2}\hookrightarrow S_{n-2}\times S_{2}<S_{n}

defined by
\rho(\sigma)=(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
where
\sigma^{\prime}\in S_{2}
is such that

.
Then

has index
n(n-1)

.
Rather than working with the corresponding matrix

we

G
as a subgroup the Young subgroup

in which

has index 2 and so we only need two secondaries.
We may take these to be
\theta_{1}=1
and
\theta_{2}=\mathcal{Z}(m\,x_{n-1})
where

m
is the monomial

.
For

\gamma_{2}\cdot\theta_{2}=\gamma_{2}\cdot\mathcal{Z}(m\,x_{n-1})=\mathcal{Z}(m\,x_{n})
.

A permutation group
G\subseteq S_{n}
contains its so-called alternating group

.
Example

can be viewed as part of a chain of ideas from previous papers

Garsia and Stanton
[
8

Vic Reiner (see
[

]

Reiner’s result is generalized by Blum-Smith and Marques in
[

]
since alternating subgroups of Young subgroups are always generated by reflections and bi-reflections.

Since the results proved here may be independent interest,

Example 8.2

Example 8.3

Consider the Shephard-Todd group

There are

Theorem 8.4

We have

\deg(\Delta(G))=\frac{\ell}{2}(|\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)|-|\mathcal{R}(G)|)\ .

Proof.

We start with

\displaystyle e(G,P)

\displaystyle=\frac{|C_{P}|}{2}\sum_{i\geqslant 1}b_{i}(i-1)=\frac{|C_{P}|}{2}(\sum_{i\geqslant 1}ib_{i}-\sum_{i\geqslant 1}b_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{|C_{P}|}{2}(\ell-\operatorname{\#cycles}(g_{P}))=\frac{|C_{P}|\ell}{2}-\frac{|C_{P}|\dim(W^{C_{P}})}{2}\ .

Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}e(G,P)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{2}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}|C_{P}|-\frac{1}{2}(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}|C_{P}|\dim(W^{C_{P}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{2}(|\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)|+\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}1)-\frac{1}{2}(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}|C_{P}|\dim(W^{C_{P}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{2}(|\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)|)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}|C_{P}|\dim(W^{C_{P}})+\frac{\ell}{2}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}1

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{2}(|\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)|)-\frac{\ell}{2}(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}|C_{P}|\frac{\dim(W^{C_{P}})}{\dim(W)}-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{2}(|\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)|)-|\mathcal{R}(G)|)

∎

By the Theorem of Hochster-Eagon,
{K}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay.
Suppose
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{\ell}

is the multi-set of degrees associated to any collection of homogeneous
secondary invariants for
{K}[V]^{G}
as a free module over
{K}[V]^{\Sigma}
.
We have the following corollary.

Corollary 8.5
.

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}a_{i}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}e(G,P)\ .

In particular,
\deg M({\bm{\theta}})=\deg\Delta(G)
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from
[
15
, Corollary 4.3, Corollary 4.4]
which together imply that

\ell|\mathcal{R}(G)|+2\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}a_{i}=\ell|\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)|.

∎

9.
\Delta(G)
divides
\det(M)

In this section we will show that
\Delta(G)
is the primitive part of the
polynomial
\det M({\bm{\theta}})
.

Recall the semi-invariant

\lambda_{\mathcal{B}}:=\prod_{P\in\mathcal{B}}L_{P}
associated to the

\Sigma
orbit
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathcal{P}
.

Theorem 9.1
.

\lambda_{\mathcal{B}}^{e(G,\mathcal{B})}
divides
\det(M)
in
K[V]
.

Proof.

Note that
\Sigma
acts on the set of rows of
M
by permutations.
Let
W
denote the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space spanned by the rows of
M
.
This is a subspace of
\oplus_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{C}[V]_{a_{i}}
.
We examine
W
as a permutation representation of the cyclic group
C_{P}
of order
c
.
Since
C_{P}
is abelian there is a basis
\{r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{\ell}\}
of
W
consisting of eigenvectors for
g_{P}
,
i.e., working over
\mathbb{C}
, we may diagonalize the action of
g_{P}
on
W
. Then
g_{P}r_{i}=\mu_{i}(g_{P})r_{i}
for some character
\mu_{i}
of
C_{P}
.

Now the cycle structure of
g_{P}
acting on
\mathcal{S}
determines the permutation action of
C_{P}
on
W
.
A cycle of length
a
corresponds to a transitive
a
dimensional permutation
C_{P}
-subrepresentation of
W
.
We fix a choice of primitive
c^{\rm{th}}
root of unity
\zeta_{c}\in\mathbb{C}
by writing
\det(g_{P})=\zeta_{c}^{-1}
.
Note that since
g_{p}\in\text{GL}(n,{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}})
, this shows that
\zeta_{c}\in{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
.
Furthermore
g_{P}\cdot L_{P}=\zeta_{c}L_{P}
.
Diagonalizing the action of
g_{P}
on this
a
dimensional subrepresentation reveals eigenvectors for
g_{P}

with eigenvalues:
\zeta_{c}^{c/a},\zeta_{c}^{2c/a},\dots,\zeta_{c}^{ac/a}=1
.

A row
r_{s}
with eigenvalue
\zeta_{c}^{cj/a}
must be “divisible by”
L_{P}^{cj/a}
, i.e., each of the
\ell

entries in this row must be a polynomial divisible by
L_{P}^{cj/a}
.

A permutation subrepresentation of
W
of dimension
a
contributes the factor
L_{P}^{\sum_{j=1}^{a}cj/a}=L_{P}^{c\binom{a}{2}/a}=L_{P}^{c(a-1)/2}
to
\det(M)
.

Taking all the cycles into account we see that

L_{P}^{(c/2)\sum_{i\mathfrak{e}2}b_{i}(i-1)}
divides
\det(M)
.
Since for any two hyperplanes
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, the linear forms

L_{P_{1}}
and
L_{P_{2}}
are co-prime,

\lambda_{\mathcal{B}}^{e(G,\mathcal{B})}
divides
\det(M)
in
K[V]^{G}
for all orbits
\mathcal{B}
.
∎

Therefore

Corollary 9.2
.

\Delta(G)=\prod_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{P}}\lambda_{\mathcal{B}}^{e(G,\mathcal{B})}

divides
\det(M)
in
K[V]^{G}
.
Moreover, if
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
is a PID and
{\bm{\theta}}
is a set of secondaries
then
\det M({\bm{\theta}})=\mho({\bm{\theta}})\Delta(G)
.

Proof.

The first statement is clear since the
\lambda_{\mathcal{B}}
are pairwise coprime in the UFD
K[V]
. For, the second, if
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
is a PID then we may assume that
each
L_{P}
is primitive which ensures that
\Delta(G)
is also primitive.
∎

Example 9.3
.

For
G=G(2,2,n)
, with
{\bm{\theta}}=(1,s_{n})
we saw in Example
6.1
that

\mho({\bm{\theta}})=-2
. In Example
8.3
we saw that
\Delta(G)=s_{n}
.
These yield an explicit example of Corollary
9.2
:

\det M({\bm{\theta}})=-2s_{n}=\mho({\bm{\theta}})\Delta(G)
.

Remark 9.4
.

Note that for any choice of secondaries
{\bm{\theta}}
in
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}[V]^{G}
, we may compute
\mho({\bm{\theta}})
efficiently by evaluating at a point
z\in V
:

\mho({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{\det M({\bm{\theta}})\rvert_{z}}{\Delta(G)(z)}\quad\text{(up to associates)}

for any point
z
which does not lie on any reflecting hyperplane. This allows us to compute
in the ring
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
or
K
rather than in
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}[V]
or
K[V]
.
This makes the computation much simpler and faster.

Remark 9.5
.

Note that for any subgroup
G\leqslant S_{n}
we have

\det M({\bm{\theta}})=\mho({\bm{\theta}}){\emph{disc}}^{e}
where
e\in\mathbb{N}
and

\det M({\bm{\theta}})={\emph{disc}}^{e}
if
G
is generated by bi-reflections and
{\bm{\theta}}
is

G
a universal set of secondaries. This provides a family of matrix
factorizations for powers of the discriminant.

10.
Constructing Universal Secondaries

In Section
5
we gave a proof of the existence of a universal set of secondaries when
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
is a PID.
In this section we give an algorithm exploiting the theory of Smith Normal Forms to construct a set of universal secondaries when
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
is a PID.
Combined with Remark
9.4
, we obtain an algorithm which exactly determines all primes for which
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay when working over a UFD.

For a (possibly non-square) matrix
A
with entries in
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
we write
\mu(A)
denote the product of the elementary divisors of
A
, i.e.,
\mu(A)
is the gcd of all maximal minors of
A
.

Pick a point
z\in V
with entries from
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
and
whose
{S_{n}}
orbit has size
|{S_{n}}|=n!
, i.e.,
choose
z
defined over
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
and not lying in any of the reflecting hyperplanes. By Remark
9.4
, the value of

\frac{\det M({\bm{\theta}})\rvert_{z}}{\Delta(G)(z)}
(up to associates) is independent
of the choice of such
z
. Furthermore, having fixed
z
the
value of the denominator
\Delta(G)(z)
is also fixed.
It remains to choose secondaries which minimize the value of the numerator.
The algorithm proceeds by selecting
secondaries
\theta_{i}
iteratively in a manner which minimizes this numerator.
Write
w_{i}=\gamma_{i}^{-1}\cdot z
for
i=1,2,\dots,\ell
. We will iteratively choose optimal homogeneous secondary invariants
\theta_{k}\in{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}[V]^{G}
such that

\deg(\theta_{j})\leqslant\deg(\theta_{j+1})
. By optimal here, we mean that using these
\theta_{k}
will yield a submatrix
A
of
M
such that
\mu(A)
is the minimum possible (with respect to division) among all choices of secondary invariants.

We begin by choosing
\theta_{1}
to be the constant polynomial 1 and proceed as follows.
Suppose we have chosen
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{k}
and consider the corresponding
\ell\times k
matrix
A:=[\theta_{j}(w_{i})]_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant k\end{subarray}}
.

By Smith Normal Form theory, there exists an invertible
\ell\times\ell
matrix
P
and an invertible
k\times k
matrix
Q

both with entries in
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
such that
PAQ
is diagonal.
Here
PAQ=\operatorname{diag}(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{k})
where the
m_{i}
are the elementary divisors of
A
.
Then
\mu(A)=m_{1}m_{2}\cdots m_{k}
.

\begin{matrix}P\end{matrix}\begin{matrix}A\end{matrix}\begin{matrix}\,Q\end{matrix}=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&m_{k}\\
\hline\cr&&\\
&0&\\
&&\end{pmatrix}

Suppose
d
is the minimal degree for which
(\oplus_{j=1}^{k}K[V]^{S_{n}}\theta_{j})_{d}
is a proper subspace of
(K[V]^{G})_{d}
. Then we must choose
\theta_{k+1}\in({{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}[V]^{G})_{d}
.

Consider an
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
-basis of
({{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}[V]^{G})_{d}
and a subset

\{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\dots,\psi_{r}\}
of such a basis whose images form a basis of

(K[V]^{G})_{d}/(\oplus_{j=1}^{k}K[V]^{S_{n}}\theta_{j})_{d}
.

For
s=1,2,\dots,r
we write
A_{s}
to denote the matrix obtained by augmenting
A
by adjoining the single column

(\psi_{s}(w_{1}),\psi_{s}(w_{2}),\dots,\psi_{s}(w_{\ell}))^{T}
.
Write

P\cdot(\psi_{s}(w_{1}),\psi_{s}(w_{2}),\dots,\psi_{s}(w_{\ell}))^{T}=(v_{s1},v_{s2},\dots,v_{s\ell})\in{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}^{\ell}\ .

Then

\begin{matrix}PA_{s}\,\widetilde{Q}\end{matrix}=\begin{matrix}P\end{matrix}\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}A\end{matrix}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\begin{matrix}\psi_{s}(w_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{s}(w_{\ell})\end{matrix}\\
\end{pmatrix}\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}ccc|c@{}}&&&0\\
&Q&&\vdots\\
&&&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\ldots&0&1\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}ccc|c@{}}m_{1}&&&v_{s1}\\
&\ddots&&\vdots\\
&&m_{k}&v_{sk}\\
\hline\cr&&&v_{s(k+1)}\\
&0&&\vdots\\
&&&v_{s\ell}\end{array}\right)

From this it is clear that
\mu(A_{s})=\gcd\{\mu(A)v_{sj}:k+1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell\}=\mu(A)\gcd\{v_{sj}:k+1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell\}
.
Put
N:=\gcd\{\mu(A_{1}),\mu(A_{2}),\dots,\mu(A_{r})\}
.
We will choose
\theta_{k+1}\in({{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}[V]^{G})_{d}
and then
augment
A
with the column
(\theta_{k+1}(w_{1}),\theta_{k+1}(w_{2}),\dots,\theta_{k+1}(w_{\ell}))^{T}

obtaining an
\ell\times(k+1)
matrix
\widetilde{A}
.

Since
\theta_{k+1}
must be chosen in the
{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}
-span of
\{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\dots,\psi_{r}\}
,
every choice of
\theta_{k+1}
will yield
\widetilde{A}
with

N
dividing
\mu(\widetilde{A})
. We will choose
\theta_{k+1}
such that
\mu(\widetilde{A})=N
. It remains to show that such a choice is possible.

Consider the
(\ell-k)\times r
matrix
B
whose columns are

(\psi_{s}(w_{k+1}),\psi_{s}(w_{k+2}),\dots,\psi_{s}(w_{\ell}))^{T}
for
s=1,2,\dots,r
,
i.e.,
B:=[\psi_{s}(w_{i})]_{\begin{subarray}{c}k+1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell\\
1\leqslant s\leqslant k\end{subarray}}
.

The Smith Normal Form of the
B
is given by
P_{0}BQ_{0}=\operatorname{diag}(n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{c})
where

n_{1}=\gcd\{\psi_{s}(w_{i})\mid k+1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell,1\leqslant s\leqslant k\}=N/\mu(A)
.
Let
(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{r})^{T}
be the first column of
Q_{0}
. Let

(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{\ell-k})
be the first row of
P_{0}
. Then

(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{\ell-k})\cdot B\cdot(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{r})^{T}=N/\mu(A)
.
We take
\theta_{k+1}=\sum_{s=1}^{r}q_{s}\psi_{s}
so that

B\cdot(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{r})^{T}=(\theta_{k+1}(w_{1}),\theta_{k+1}(w_{2}),\dots,\theta_{k+1}(w_{\ell}))
.
Then

\begin{matrix}P\widetilde{A}\,\widetilde{Q}\end{matrix}=\begin{matrix}P\end{matrix}\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}A\end{matrix}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\begin{matrix}\theta_{k+1}(w_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\theta_{k+1}(w_{\ell})\end{matrix}\\
\end{pmatrix}\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}ccc|c@{}}&&&0\\
&Q&&\vdots\\
&&&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\ldots&0&1\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}ccc|c@{}}m_{1}&&&u_{1}\\
&\ddots&&\vdots\\
&&m_{k}&u_{k}\\
\hline\cr&&&u_{k+1}\\
&0&&\vdots\\
&&&u_{\ell}\end{array}\right)

where

P\cdot B\cdot(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{r})^{T}=(u_{k+1},u_{k+2},\dots,u_{\ell})
.
From this equation it is clear that
\mu(\widetilde{A})=\mu(A)\gcd\{u_{k+1},u_{k+2},\dots,u_{\ell}\}=N
as required.

Continuing we choose invariants
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell}\in{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}[V]^{G}
such that

\det\big([\gamma_{i}\theta_{j}(z)]_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell\end{subarray}}\big)
divides

\det\big([\gamma_{i}\phi_{j}(z)]_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell\end{subarray}}\big)
for all choices of secondaries

\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\dots,\phi_{\ell}
. In particular,

\mho(G)=\mho({\bm{\theta}})=\text{content of }\big(\det([\gamma_{i}\theta_{j}]_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant\ell\end{subarray}})\big)\ .

This implies that
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}=\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{\Sigma}\,{\overline{\theta}}_{j}
for all primes
p
not dividing
\mho({\bm{\theta}})
.

Example 10.1
.

Consider the subgroup
G
of
S_{6}
generated by
\{(2,3,6)
,
(1,3,4,6)(2,5)
,
(1,4)(3,6)
,
(1,5,4)(2,3,6)\}
. This group has order 36 and
\mho(G)=8
. There are two secondaries of degree 7.
Any set of universal secondaries here requires a secondary of degree 7 which is not an orbit sum. Applying the algorithm we find that every set of secondaries

{\bm{\theta}}
including two orbits sums of degree 7 has
\mho({\bm{\theta}})\geqslant 1800
.

11.
Conclusion

Any permutation representation of a finite group
G
is defined over every ring.
Over a field of characteristic zero the ring of invariants is always
Cohen-Macaulay. The invariants
K[V]^{G}
are closely related to

\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
where
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is the field of prime order
p
.
However, the latter ring of invariants may fail to be Cohen-Macaulay when

p
divides the order of
G
.
We defined a square integer matrix
M
associated to a set of secondary invariants
{\bm{\theta}}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell})\in{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}[V]^{G}
.
The determinant of
M
is an integer polynomial and
we denote the absolute value of its content by

\mho({\bm{\theta}})
.
The ring
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}=\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{S_{n}}\theta_{j}

if and only if
p
does not divide
\mho({\bm{\theta}})
.

We showed also that there always exists a set of universal secondary invariants

{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell})
such that

\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}=\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{S_{n}}\theta_{j}
for all primes
p
for
which
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay. Defining the deficiency of
G
to be
\mho(G)=\mho({\bm{\theta}})
for such
universal secondaries, we have
\mathbb{F}_{p}[V]^{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if

p
does not divide the deficiency of
G
. Using Smith Normal Form we gave an
algorithm to construct a set of universal secondaries.

We used the structure of
G
as a subgroup of the
reflection group
S_{n}
in order to define
a semi-invariant
\Delta(G)
. This semi-invariant was shown to be the primitive part of
\det(M)
for each
choice of the secondary invariants
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{\ell}
.
Thus
\det(M)=\pm\mho({\bm{\theta}})\Delta(G)
. Evaluating both sides of this
equation at a point provides an efficient method to find either

\mho({\bm{\theta}})
or
\mho(G)
the deficiency of
G
.

Finally we indicated some generalizations of these results for certain
subgroups of other Shephard-Todd reflection groups.
