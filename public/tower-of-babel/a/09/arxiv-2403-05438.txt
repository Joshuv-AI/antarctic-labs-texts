VideoElevator: Elevating Video Generation Quality with Versatile Text-to-Image Diffusion Models
Yabo Zhang, Yuxiang Wei, Xianhui Lin, Zheng Hui, Peiran Ren, Xuansong Xie, Xiangyang Ji, Wangmeng Zuo ( ✉ )

Abstract
Text-to-image diffusion models (T2I) have demonstrated unprecedented capabilities in creating realistic and aesthetic images. On the contrary, text-to-video diffusion models (T2V) still lag far behind in frame quality and text alignment, owing to insufficient quality and quantity of training videos. In this paper, we introduce VideoElevator, a training-free and plug-and-play method, which elevates the performance of T2V using superior capabilities of T2I. Different from conventional T2V sampling ( i.e . , temporal and spatial modeling), VideoElevator explicitly decomposes each sampling step into temporal motion refining and spatial quality elevating . Specifically, temporal motion refining uses encapsulated T2V to enhance temporal consistency, followed by inverting to the noise distribution required by T2I. Then, spatial quality elevating harnesses inflated T2I to directly predict less noisy latent, adding more photo-realistic details. We have conducted experiments in extensive prompts under the combination of various T2V and T2I. The results show that VideoElevator not only improves the performance of T2V baselines with foundational T2I, but also facilitates stylistic video synthesis with personalized T2I. Our code is available at https://github.com/YBYBZhang/VideoElevator

3
Preliminary

Latent diffusion model.

Instead of applying diffusion process in image space, latent diffusion model (LDM)
[
45
]
performs this in lower-dimensional latent space.
LDM mainly consists of pre-trained autoencoder and U-Net architecture
[
46
]
.
The autoencoder uses an encoder
\mathcal{E}
to compress an image
{\bm{x}}
into latent code
{\bm{z}}=\mathcal{E}({\bm{x}})
and a decoder
\mathcal{D}
to reconstruct it.
The U-Net is trained to learn the clean latent distribution
{\bm{z}}_{0}\sim p_{data}({\bm{z}}_{0})
within the DDPM framework
[
53
,
51
,
18
]
.
In specific, LDM performs a forward diffusion process by adding Gaussian noise to compute
{\bm{z}}_{t}
:

\displaystyle q({\bm{z}}_{t}|{\bm{z}}_{0})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{z}}_{t};\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}{\bm{z}}_{0},\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\mathbf{I}),

(1)

where
T
is the number of diffusion timesteps and
\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
control the noise schedules.
With the U-Net architecture, LDM learns to reverse the above diffusion process through predicting less noisy
{\bm{z}}_{t-1}
:

p_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t-1}|{\bm{z}}_{t})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{z}}_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t},t),\Sigma_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t},t)),

(2)

\mu_{\theta}
and
\Sigma_{\theta}
are the mean and variance parameterized with learnable
\theta
.
Using LDM (
e.g
.
, Stable Diffusion) as base model, T2V and personalized T2I usually freeze the autoencoder during finetuning, so they share clean latent distribution.

DDIM sampling and inversion.

According to Eqn.
1
, one can directly predict the clean latent
{\bm{z}}_{t\rightarrow 0}
with the noise latent
{\bm{z}}_{t}
:

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t\rightarrow 0}=\frac{{\bm{z}}_{t}-\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\epsilon_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t},t)}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}},

(3)

where
\epsilon_{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes “
\epsilon
-prediction” diffusion model.
When producing new samples from a random noise
{\bm{z}}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, DDIM sampling
[
52
]
denoises
{\bm{z}}_{t}
to
{\bm{z}}_{t-1}
of previous timestep:

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\alpha_{t-1}}{\bm{z}}_{t\rightarrow 0}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t-1}}\cdot\epsilon_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t},t),

(4)

Given a clean latent
{\bm{z}}_{0}
, DDIM inversion
[
52
]
deterministically inverts it into noise latent
{\bm{z}}_{T}
:

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}{\bm{z}}_{(t-1)\rightarrow 0}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\cdot\epsilon_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t-1},t),

(5)

The inverted
{\bm{z}}_{T}
can reconstruct
{\bm{z}}_{0}
by iterating Eqn.
4
.
Thanks to its capability in keeping structure, DDIM inversion has been widely used in image and video editing tasks
[
16
,
60
]
.

Noise schedules and latent distributions.

Noise schedules are parameterized by
\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and define the noise scales at different timesteps
[
18
,
53
]
.
In general, T2V and T2I adopt different noise schedules during training,
i.e
.
,
\{\alpha_{t}^{V}\}_{t=1}^{T}\neq\{\alpha_{t}^{I}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.
Therefore, their noise latent codes belong to different noise distributions, and cannot be used as input for each other.

4.1
Temporal motion refining

Temporal motion refining mainly involves in two aspects: (i) enhance temporal consistency of video latent
{\bm{z}}_{t\rightarrow 0}
using T2V generative priors, and (ii) convert it to the noise distribution required by T2I.
Firstly, the video decoded from
{\bm{z}}_{t\rightarrow 0}
lacks reasonable motion and contains visible flickers.
With the power of T2V generative priors
[
37
]
, it is possible to generate a video with natural motion, but challenging to remove its visible flickers (refer to Fig.
7
(a)).
Therefore, before using T2V generative priors, we first transform
{\bm{z}}_{t\rightarrow 0}
into frequency domain to reduce its high-frequency flickers.
Secondly, to preserve the motion integrity in (ii), we deterministically invert video latent into the corresponding noise distribution of T2I.

Specifically, in Fig.
3
, we first apply low-pass frequency filter
\texttt{LPFF}(\cdot)
in
{\bm{z}}_{t\rightarrow 0}
along the
temporal
dimension, which computes more stable
\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{t\rightarrow 0}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{{\bm{z}}}_{t\rightarrow 0})

\displaystyle=\texttt{FFT}_{Temp}(\bm{{\bm{z}}}_{t\rightarrow 0}),

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}(\bm{{\bm{z}}}_{t\rightarrow 0})

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{{\bm{z}}}_{t\rightarrow 0})\odot\bm{\mathcal{G}},

(7)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{{\bm{z}}}}_{t\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle=\texttt{IFFT}_{Temp}(\hat{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}(\bm{{\bm{z}}}_{t\rightarrow 0})),

(8)

where
\texttt{FFT}_{Temp}(\cdot)
is the fast fourier transformation to map
{\bm{z}}_{t\rightarrow 0}
to the frequency domain, while
\texttt{IFFT}_{Temp}(\cdot)
denotes inverse fast fourier transformation to map it back to the temporal domain.

\mathcal{G}
represents Gaussian low-pass filter mask to diminish high-frequency flickers.
Compared to
spatial-temporal

LPFF

[
61
]
, our
temporal

LPFF
not only effectively stabilizes the video, but also has less negative impact on spatial quality,
e.g
.
, more realistic details in Fig.

.

Albeit there are less high-frequency flickers in
\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{t\rightarrow 0}
, it is insufficient to achieve vivid and fine-grained motion without T2V generative priors.
Inspired by
[
37
]
, we adopt T2V-based SDEdit to portray natural motion to
\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{t\rightarrow 0}
.
In particular, with T2V noise schedule
\{\alpha_{t}^{V}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, we perform DDPM forward process in
\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{t\rightarrow 0}
to calculate
\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{t}
(with Eqn.
1
), and then iterates the T2V denoising process
N
times to achieve desirable motion (with Eqn.
4
),
i.e
.
,
\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{t-N}
or
\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{(t-N)\rightarrow{0}}
.
Notably, when
N
is very small (
e.g

,
N=1
), the synthesized video only contains coarse-grained motion, so we set

to
8\sim 10
to add fine-grained one (refer to Appendix
B

Finally, when inverting
\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{(t-N)\rightarrow{0}}
into the input noise distribution of T2I, we deterministically forward it to keep its motion.
Using the unconditional guidance
[
19
]
(
i.e
.
, null condition
\emptyset
), clean latent
\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{(t-N)\rightarrow{0}}
is inverted to noise latent distribution of T2I at timestep
t
:

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{z}}}_{t}=\texttt{Inversion}(\epsilon_{\phi}^{I};\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{(t-N)\rightarrow{0}},t)

(9)

where
\epsilon_{\phi}^{I}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
represents inflated T2I.
We use
\texttt{Inversion}(\cdot;\cdot,\cdot)
to denote a diffusion-based inversion algorithm, and choose the DDIM inversion
[
52
]
by default.
In contrast, DDPM-based strategies potentially impair the motion integrity of synthesized videos,
e.g
.
, leading to all frames being identical or inconsistent in Fig.

.

Empirically, applying temporal motion refining in just a few timesteps (
i.e
.
,
4\sim 5
steps) can ensure temporal consistency (refer to Appendix
B
).
To improve sampling efficiency, we perform both temporal motion refining and spatial quality elevating in selected timesteps, while performing T2I denoising only in other timesteps.

4.2
Spatial quality elevating

Given stablized latent
\tilde{{\bm{z}}}_{t}
from temporal motion refining, spatial quality elevating leverages T2I to directly add high-quality details.
However, individually denoising all frames with conventional T2I will lead to visible inconsistency in appearance.
Motivated by previous works
[
69
,
60
,
25
]
, we inflate T2I along the temporal axis so that all frames share the same content.

Particularly, we extend the U-Net of T2I along the temporal dimension, including convolution and self-attention layers.
The 2D convolution layers are converted to 3D counterpart by replacing
3\times 3
kernels with
1\times 3\times 3
kernels.
The self-attention layers are extended to first-only cross-frame attention layers by adding inter-frame interaction.
For first-only cross-frame interaction, frame latents
\tilde{{\bm{z}}}_{t}=\{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}_{t}[i]\}_{i=0}^{F-1}
(
F
is the number of frames) are updated as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Attention}({\bm{Q}},{\bm{K}},{\bm{V}})=\mathrm{Softmax}(\frac{{\bm{Q}}{\bm{K}}^{T}}{\sqrt{d}})\cdot{\bm{V}},

(10)

where
{\bm{Q}}={\bm{W}}^{Q}\tilde{{\bm{z}}}_{t}[i]
,
{\bm{K}}={\bm{W}}^{K}\tilde{{\bm{z}}}_{t}[0]
, and
{\bm{V}}={\bm{W}}^{V}\tilde{{\bm{z}}}_{t}[0]
.

Afterwards, we directly utilize
inflated
T2I to add photo-realistic details to
\tilde{{\bm{z}}}_{t}
, transitioning it to less noisy latent
{\bm{z}}_{t-1}
at timestep
t-1
:

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t-1}=\sqrt{\alpha_{t-1}^{I}}\cdot\tilde{{\bm{z}}}_{t\rightarrow 0}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t-1}^{I}}\cdot\epsilon_{\phi}^{I}(\tilde{{\bm{z}}}_{t},y,t)+\sigma_{t}^{I}\epsilon_{t}.

(11)

where
\epsilon_{t}
is random Gaussian noise and
\sigma_{t}^{I}
controls its scale.

At each sampling step, spatial quality elevating employs inflated T2I to provide realistic details to video latent.
Compared to T2V baselines, VideoElevator produces videos whose frames are closer to T2I generated images, from the perspectives of text alignment, quality, and aesthetic styles.

Low-pass frequency filter (LPFF) aims to reduce the high-frequency flickers in video latents.
We probe its effect under three settings: (i) without LPFF, (ii) temporal LPFF, and (iii) spatial-temporal LPFF

6
Conclusion

We introduce VideoElevator, a
training-free
and
play-and-plug
approach that boosts the performance of T2V baselines with versatile T2I.
VideoElevator explicitly decomposes each sampling step into two components, including temporal motion refining and spatial quality elevating.
Given noise latent at timestep
t
, temporal motion refining leverages a low-pass frequency filter to reduce its flickers, while applying T2V-based SDEdit to portray realistic motion.
When computing noise latent required by T2I, it performs deterministic inversion to preserve motion integrity.
Spatial quality elevating inflates the self-attention of T2I along the temporal axis, and directly harnesses inflated T2I to predict less noisy latent.
Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of VideoElevator under the combination of various T2V and T2I.
Even with foundational T2I, VideoElevator significantly improves T2V baselines in terms of text alignment, frame quality, and aesthetic score.
When integrating personalized T2I, it enables T2V baselines to faithfully produce various styles of videos.
