Random trees with local catastrophes: the Brownian case
Ariane Carrance, Jérôme Casse, and Nicolas Curien

Abstract
We introduce and study a model of plane random trees generalizing the famous Bienaymé–Galton–Watson model but where births and deaths are locally correlated. More precisely, given a random variable ( B , H ) (B,H) with values in { 1 , 2 , 3 , … } 2 \{1,2,3,...\}^{2} , given the state of the tree at some generation, the next generation is obtained (informally) by successively deleting B B individuals side-by-side and replacing them with H H new particles where the samplings are i.i.d. We prove that, in the critical case 𝔼 ⁡ [ B ] = 𝔼 ⁡ [ H ] \mathbb{E}[B]=\mathbb{E}[H] , and under a third moment condition on B B and H H , the random trees coding the genealogy of the population model converges towards the Brownian Continuum Random Tree. Interestingly, our proof does not use the classical height process or the Łukasiewicz exploration, but rather the stochastic flow point of view introduced by Bertoin & Le Gall [ BLG03 , BLG05 , BLG06 ] .

It might not be obvious, but this brick wall actually encodes a forest of random trees

Theorem 1

(Convergence towards the Brownian forest)
.

Suppose that the critical condition holds
\mathbb{E}[B]=\mathbb{E}[H]

\mathbb{E}[B^{3}],\mathbb{E}[H^{3}]<\infty

\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[B]}\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(B-H)^{2}\right]}.

\lambda\cdot\underline{F}^{\uparrow}\xrightarrow[\lambda\to 0]{(d)}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}.

Proof ideas and extensions.

The lack of independence properties in our model compared to the standard Bienaymé–Galton–Watson makes it more cumbersome to use standard tools to prove convergence of trees, such as the contour function or the Łukasiewicz exploration. We cope with this problem by describing our forest of random trees by their discrete
stochastic genealogical flows
. In a nutshell, this idea brought to light in the continuous setting by Bertoin & Le Gall
[
BLG03
,
BLG05
,
BLG06
]
boils down to controlling the evolution of a few subpopulations in our brick wall model (or equivalently by the subforest spanned by a few vertices in
T_{i}^{\downarrow}
). Indeed, despite the lack of independence in our model, the tools available in stochastic analysis are robust enough to prove that the offsprings of different subpopulations evolve, in the scaling limit, as
independent Feller diffusions
(see Proposition
12
). Combined by a tightness estimate (Proposition
21
) this enables us to control the large scale geometry of our forest of trees using just a few subpopulations’ evolution. We hope that the method developed in these pages could lead to other developments, in particular to the case of branching processes in varying environment, see
[
CKKM23
]
for a very recent result in that direction.

Plan of the paper.

In
Section

2
, we define in more detail our brick wall model and explain how Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees are recovered as a special case. We recall the well-known convergence of Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees with finite variance to the Brownian forest (see
2
). We then prove that, for critical brick wall models with finite second moments, subpopulations are martingales. In the rest of the paper, we restrict ourselves to critical models with finite third moments. To prove that the trees of these models also converge to a Brownian forest, we start by focusing on the evolution of subpopulations in
Section

3
. We first tackle the behavior of large populations after one time step, and we prove in particular variance estimates and a CLT. Then, we establish that the scaling limit of a finite tuple of large subpopulations is a tuple of independent Feller diffusions, and that this convergence holds jointly with that of the corresponding extinction times. This enables us to prove important uniform controls in
Section

3.3
, which are the key to the tightness estimates.
Section

4
is devoted to the proof of
1
. Finally, in
Section

5
, we discuss two directions in which to extend our model: one where we consider heavy-tailed brick distributions, and one where the events of birth and death occur at random times given by exponential clocks, rather than at integer times.

2.1
Definition of the model

Let
\rho
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
such that

Z:=\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}i\rho(i,j)<\infty.

We define its
bottom marginal law

\beta
and its
top marginal law

\eta
by

\beta(i)=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\rho(i,j),\ \ \eta(j)=\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\rho(i,j).

We also define the respective
bottom-biased
laws for
i,j\geqslant 1
by

\displaystyle\rho^{*}(i,j)=\frac{1}{Z}i\rho(i,j),\qquad\beta^{*}(i)=\frac{1}{Z}i\beta(i),\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\eta^{*}(j)=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i\geqslant 1}i\rho(i,j).

Definition 1

(Brick wall)
.

The
brick wall with distribution
\rho
is defined as follows. We construct the first row of bricks at height
k=0
, by first drawing a bi-infinite sequence of independent brick dimensions
(B_{i,0},H_{i,0})\sim\rho
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, and
(B_{0,0},H_{0,0})\sim\rho^{*}
. We think of these bricks as having rigid bottom and top lengths, but flexible vertical sides, so that we need some additional information to place them on the horizontal line. For that purpose, we fix the position of the root brick by requiring that the bottom-left corner of the root brick is
(-U_{0},0)
, where
U_{0}
is, conditionally on
(B_{i,0},H_{i,0})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
, uniform over
\{0,1,\dots,B_{0,0}-1\}
, and that its top-left corner of the root brick is at
(0,1)
. Once the root brick is fixed, we can place all the other bricks inductively, to its left and to its right.

We proceed similarly for the other rows of bricks that we stack on this first one: at each level
j\geqslant 1
, we pick, independently from the lower levels, a new bi-infinite sequence of brick dimensions,
(B_{i,j},H_{i,j})\sim\rho
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, and
(B_{0,j},H_{0,j})\sim\rho^{*}
. We then fix the position of the root brick at height
j
again by requiring that its bottom-left corner is at
(-U_{j},j)
, where
U_{j}
is uniform over
\{0,1,\dots,B_{0,j}-1\}
, and that its top-left corner is at
(0,j+1)
.

The horizontal sides of the bricks can be seen as edges so that the brick wall is a graph with vertex set
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
containing in particular all horizontal edges
(i,j)\leftrightarrow(i+1,j)
, see Figure
2
. This graph is rooted at the oriented edge
(0,0)\to(1,0)
. Its information is entirely encoded in the sequence

\displaystyle\big((B_{i,j},H_{i,j}:i\in\mathbb{Z}),U_{j}:j\geqslant 1\big)\quad\mbox{satisfying}\quad B_{i,j},H_{i,j}\geqslant 1\quad\mbox{ and }U_{j}\in\{0,1,...,B_{0,j}-1\}.

(1)

The reader may have already guessed that we use a size-biased law in our construction to get the following invariance property:

Proposition 1

(Invariance by translation)
.

The law of the brick wall is
invariant under the shift of the root edge

(0,0)\to(1,0)
one unit to the right (or to the left).

Proof.

The bottom right corners of the lowest bricks can be seen as a point process on
\mathbb{Z}
and since we use the size-biaised distribution for the root brick, a classical result in renewal theory (see e.g.
[
Kal02
, Proposition 9.18]
) shows that its distribution is the so-called "stationary" renewal process and its law is invariant under shift of the root edge one unit to the right. This shift may cause a relabeling of the vertices at height
1
, but an iterative application of the above shift invariance shows that the law of the entire brick wall is shift invariant. ∎

2.2
Special case: Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees

Consider the special case of a brick distribution where the top and bottom marginals are independent:
\rho=\beta\otimes\eta
. Let
\mu,\nu
be two probability distributions on
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
respectively, such that

\begin{cases}\eta=\mu\\
\beta\text{ is the law of }B=1+\inf\{i\geqslant 0\,\lvert\,\sum_{0\leqslant j\leqslant i}(N_{j}-1)<0\},\,N_{i}\text{ i.i.d.}\sim\nu\end{cases}

Then the evolution of the forest
F^{\uparrow}
can be described informally as a discrete genealogical process with “local catastrophes”, where, at each integer time
n\geqslant 1
:

•

each individual alive at time
n-1
is independently scheduled to give birth to a number of children according to
\mu

•

then, each individual alive at time
n-1
cancels all the impending offspring of a number of individuals of law
\nu
, starting (if the number is positive) with itself, and then going to the individuals on its right (that are not necessarily its siblings).

Informally,
B
is the time between two consecutive moments when the debt of deaths is settled. In the context of continuous branching processes in varying environment, processes with Poissonian “catastrophes” have been considered
[
BS15
]
, and the term local catastrophes, that we use here and in the title of this paper, is in analogy to those models. Note however that our discretes processes are not in general branching processes, due to the interdependence between the trees.

Bienaymé–Galton–Watson
processes can be seen as special cases of such genealogical process with local catastrophes, where the catastrophes are just the deaths of single individuals. Indeed, for any probability distribution
p
on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, the genealogical process obtained as above from

\begin{cases}\mu=(p\,\lvert\,p>0)\\
\nu=\text{Ber}(p_{0}),\end{cases}

is a Bienaymé–Galton–Watson process of offspring distribution
p
, started from
\mathbb{Z}
. This can also be directly expressed as a brick wall model with independent top and bottom distributions, given by

\begin{cases}\beta=\text{Geom}(1-p_{0})\\
\eta=(p\,\lvert\,p>0).\end{cases}

In this case, the random trees
T_{i}^{\uparrow}
are simply i.i.d. Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees with offspring distribution
p
: interaction between the trees disappear. In the case of a
critical
Bienaymé–Galton–Watson, if
X\sim p
has the law of the offspring distribution we have
\mathbb{E}[X]=1
and

\mathbb{E}[H]=\mathbb{E}[B]=\frac{1}{1-p_{0}},\quad\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]=\frac{1+p_{0}}{(1-p_{0})^{2}},\quad\mathbb{E}[H^{2}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]}{1-p_{0}},

so that

\sigma^{2}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(H-B)^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[B]}=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]-1=\mathrm{Var}{\left(X\right)}.

Let us take the opportunity given by this model to describe the Brownian forest in details. Recall from
[
DLG02
]
the classical coding of a real tree by a positive excursion: if
\mathbf{e}:[0,\ell]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a non-negative continuous function satisfying
\mathbf{e}(0)=\mathbf{e}(\ell)=0
; it can be seen as the contour function of a pointed real tree
(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{e}},\varrho)
. We can then consider the pushforward
\mathbf{n}
of Itô’s excursion measure for linear Brownian motion
[
RY05
, Chapter XII]
on pointed real trees by this coding. In particular
\mathbf{n}
is a sigma-finite measure on the set
\mathbb{T}
of compact pointed real trees (endowed with the pointed Gromov–Hausdorff distance) and we fix the normalization of
\mathbf{n}
by requiring that

\mathbf{n}(\{(\tau,\varrho\}:\mathrm{Height}(\tau)\geqslant x\})=\frac{2}{x},\quad\forall x>0.

For any
\sigma>0
, if
\Pi
is a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}
of intensity
\sigma^{-2}\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathbf{n}
, then we consider the pointed tree
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
obtained for each atom
(t,(\tau,\varrho))
of
\Pi
by grafting
(\tau,\varrho)
through
\varrho
at the point
t\in\mathbb{R}
. This operation is well-defined in the local pointed Gromov–Hausdorff topology since there is a finite number of trees of height larger than
\varepsilon
grafted on each compact set of
\mathbb{R}
– see
[
CKM22
, Section 6.1.2]
for a variant of this construction. The random tree
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
is pointed at
0\in\mathbb{R}
and has two ends. If
\underline{F}^{\uparrow}
is the infinite random tree obtained by grafting i.i.d. BGW
(p)
trees on the line
\mathbb{Z}
and pointed at
0
, then we have

Theorem 2

(Aldous
[
Ald91
]
, Le Gall
[
LG05
]
)
.

If
p
is critical and has finite non zero variance, then we have the following convergence for the local pointed Gromov–Hausdorff metric

\lambda\cdot\underline{F}^{\uparrow}\xrightarrow[\lambda\to 0]{}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}.

2.3
Population evolution and martingale property

We now prove that in a critical brick wall model, the population issued from a set of ancestors in
F^{\uparrow}
is a martingale. This is our first step in the understanding of the large scale properties of the trees
T_{i}^{\uparrow}
, since it implies in particular that all trees
T_{i}^{\uparrow}
are finite in the critical case.

Let
a<b\in\mathbb{Z}
. We denote by
M_{t}([a,b[)
the number of descendants at time
t
of the nodes
x
in
a\leqslant x<b
at time
0
, for the primal forest. Necessarily,
M_{0}([a,b[)=b-a
. We will also sometimes consider populations starting from individuals at time
t_{0}>0
, which we denote accordingly
M_{t}([(a,t_{0}),(b,t_{0})[)
, for
t\geqslant t_{0}
. Thus,
M_{t}([a,b[)=M_{t}([(a,0),(b,0)[)
.

Later on, we will also consider the extinction times of these populations, that is,

\tau_{a,b}:=\min\{t\geqslant 1:M_{t}([a,b[)=0\},\ \ \tau_{(a,t_{0}),(b,t_{0})}:=\min\{t\geqslant t_{0}+1:M_{t}([(a,t_{0}),(b,t_{0})[)=0\}.

Proposition 2
.

If
(H,B)\sim\rho
, we suppose that
\mathbb{E}[H]=\mathbb{E}[B]
(criticality) and furthermore that
\mathbb{E}[B^{2}],\mathbb{E}[H^{2}]<\infty
. Then for any
a<b\in\mathbb{Z}
, the sequence
\left(M_{t}([a,b[)\right)_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}
is a (positive) martingale and a homogeneous Markov chain.

Proof.

The fact that
\left(M_{t}([a,b[)\right)_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}
is a Markov chain is immediate from the definition of the model and by invariance by shift of the root edge (Proposition
1
). We now want to show that it is a martingale (the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{n}
being generated by the first
n
rows of the brick wall). Let us first check that
\mathbb{E}[M_{1}([0,n[)]<\infty
. To see this, we use the notation of
1
and the domination

\displaystyle M_{1}([0,n[)\leqslant H_{0,0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{i,0}.

(5)

While
H_{i,0}
for
i\geqslant 1
are i.i.d with a second moment, the first moment of
H_{0,0}
is also bounded by
\mathbb{E}[H_{0,0}]=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[B]}\mathbb{E}[H\cdot B]<\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[B]}\mathbb{E}[H^{2}+B^{2}]<\infty
. By the Markov property, it remains to show that
\mathbb{E}[M_{1}([a,b[)]=(b-a)
.
By invariance of the brick wall by shift of the root edge we have
\mathbb{E}[M_{1}([a,b[)]=\mathbb{E}[M_{1}([0,b-a[)]
, and so by linearity of expectation, there exists
c>0
so that for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
,

\displaystyle\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[M_{1}([0,n[)\right]}=c\,n.

(6)

One can prove that
c=1
by a direct tedious calculation (splitting the calculation according to the value of the last bottom-right corner of a brick before
n
), or using a limiting argument: putting the root brick of dimensions
(B_{0,0}^{*},H_{0,0})
aside, the law of large numbers immediately entails that almost surely we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{M_{1}([0,n[)}{n}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[H]}{\mathbb{E}[B]}=1.

On the other hand, it follows from (
5
) that
(\frac{M_{1}([0,n[)}{n}:n\geqslant 1)
is uniformly integrable. By dominated convergence, we deduce that
n^{-1}\cdot\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[M_{1}([0,n[)\right]}\to 1
, hence by (
6
) we must have
c=1
.
∎

\widetilde{{F}}_{[0,r]}^{\downarrow}[\rho]
, which is the same forest re-rooted at the random index

of the primal forest associated to

, and, highlighted in light blue, the set of vertices

To properly state this duality property, we need to introduce a bit of additional notation, which is illustrated in

explicit in the notation. Fix

. For a brick distribution
\rho

{F}^{\uparrow}_{[0,r]}[\rho]
(resp.
{F}_{[0,r]}^{\downarrow}[\rho]
) the “strip” of the associated primal (resp. dual) forest that lies in
\mathbb{Z}\times[0,r]
. For

, let us also denote by
T^{\downarrow}_{(i,r)}[\rho]
the tree of the dual forest descended from the vertex
(i,r)
(that has at most height

by our definition), and define
T^{\downarrow}_{(i,r)}[\rho](r)
as the set of vertices of
T^{\downarrow}_{(i,r)}[\rho]
at height
r
. Let
K_{r}
be the first index
i\geqslant 1
such that

. We also denote by
T_{(i,0)}^{\uparrow}[\rho]
the tree of the primal forest descended from the node
(i+1/2,0)
. We let
i_{r}>0
denote the smallest integer
i>0
such that

has height at least

T^{\uparrow}_{(i_{r},0)}[\rho](r)
denote the set of nodes of
T^{\uparrow}_{(i_{r},0)}[\rho]

Proposition 3
.

Let
\widetilde{{F}}_{[0,r]}^{\downarrow}[\rho]
be the infinite forest obtained by re-rooting

at the node
(J_{r},r)
, where the index

is chosen uniformly at random in
\{1,\dots,K_{r}\}
, turning it upside-down, and shifting its abscissae by

to the right (see

3
). Let
\mu^{\downarrow}_{r}[\rho]
be the measure on forests
(\dots,T_{-1},T_{0},T_{1},\dots)
of height

K_{r}
with respect to the law of
\widetilde{{F}}^{\downarrow}_{[0,r]}[\rho]
, and let
\mu^{\uparrow}_{r}[^{t}\rho]
be the measure on the same forests having density
\#T^{\uparrow}_{(i_{r},0)}[^{t}\rho](r)
with respect to the law of
{F}^{\uparrow}_{[0,r]}[^{t}\rho]
. Then

\mu^{\downarrow}_{r}[\rho]=\mu^{\uparrow}_{r}[^{t}\rho].

(7)

Proof.

Even though the trees we are studying are more complicated than the ones appearing in
[

]
(and, in particular, are not independent), we can prove this result similarly, since it is essentially combinatorial. Indeed, for any given brick wall, the two forests are determined in the exact same way from it (up to turning the wall upside-down and shifting abscissae by
1/2
).

Consider a configuration of bricks
\mathcal{C}
going from height 0 to
r
, with a distinguished root brick at each height level, such that the root brick at height
\ell
always shares an edge with the root brick at height
\ell+1
(so that it can be considered as a subset of a brick wall). We denote by
(i_{k,\ell},j_{k,\ell})
the bottom and top dimensions of the

\ell
(with the 0-th brick being the root brick, and the negative indices corresponding to the bricks to the left of the root brick).

We can construct a dual forest
{F}^{\mathcal{C}}=\dots,T^{\mathcal{C}}_{-1},T^{\mathcal{C}}_{0},T^{\mathcal{C}}_{1},\dots
from
\mathcal{C}
, where all trees have height less than or equal to

. We perform the re-rooting, rotation and shift as above to get a new rooted forest
\widetilde{{F}}^{\mathcal{C}}=\dots,\widetilde{T}^{\mathcal{C}}_{-1},\widetilde{T}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0},\widetilde{T}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1},\dots
. Let
k_{\mathcal{C}}
be the first index

such that
T^{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(r)\neq\varnothing
, and let
i_{r}
be the first index
i\geqslant 1
such that
\widetilde{T}^{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(r)\neq\varnothing

\displaystyle\mu_{r}^{\downarrow}[\rho]\left(\widetilde{{F}}^{\mathcal{C}}\right)

\displaystyle=k_{\mathcal{C}}\cdot\frac{i_{0,0}}{k_{\mathcal{C}}}\cdot\frac{1}{i_{0,0}}\frac{1}{Z}i_{0,0}\rho(i_{0,0},j_{0,0})\prod_{k\neq 0}\rho(i_{k,0},j_{k,0})\prod_{1\leqslant\ell\leqslant r-1}\frac{1}{i_{0,\ell}}\frac{1}{Z}i_{0,\ell}\rho(i_{0,\ell},j_{0,\ell}\left(\prod_{k\neq 0}\rho(i_{k,\ell},j_{k,\ell})\right)

\displaystyle=i_{0,0}\cdot\frac{1}{Z}\prod_{k}\rho(i_{k,0},j_{k,0})\prod_{1\leqslant\ell\leqslant r-1}\frac{1}{Z}\left(\prod_{k}\rho(i_{k,\ell},j_{k,\ell})\right)

\displaystyle=\mu^{\uparrow}_{r}[^{t}\rho]\left(\widetilde{{F}}^{\mathcal{C}}\right),

where the last equality comes from the fact that, by construction, we have
\#\widetilde{T}^{\mathcal{C}}_{i_{r}}(r)=i_{0,0}
.
∎

3
Scaling limits of subpopulations

In this section we prove that the evolution of large subpopulations converge in the scaling towards independent Feller diffusions. This relies on a combination of standard techniques in stochastic analysis. We start with variances estimates for the one-step transitions.

3.1
Behavior of subpopulations after one time step

In this section we establish variance estimates for one step of the Markov chain describing the population evolution which will be useful in
Section

3.2
when dealing with the full scaling limit of
(M_{k}([0,n[):k\geqslant 0)
.
For ease of notation, in this section we will write
M_{k}(n)
for
M_{k}([0,n[)
. We will first obtain an asymptotic estimate for the variance of
M_{1}(n)
, then prove a CLT for
M_{1}(n)
.

Variance.

In the terminology of
[
BA22
]
,
M_{1}(n)
is a
inhomogeneous compound renewal process
. The marginal laws
\beta
and
\eta
, as well as the bottom-biased laws
\beta^{*},\eta^{*}
all have finite variances – remember that for the root brick, we take the portion of the bottom that lies to the right of 0, whose law is uniform on
\{1,\dots,b\}
, conditionally on
B^{*}=b
, and the top of the root brick has the bottom-biased law
\eta^{*}
. Thus,
[
BA22
, Theorem 1.2.1]
applies and gives the following estimate.

Proposition 4
.

We have

\mathrm{Var}{\left(M_{1}(n)\right)}=n\sigma^{2}+\so{\hskip-1.49994pt\left(n\right)},

(8)

where

\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{Z}\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(H-B)^{2}\right]}=\frac{1}{Z}(\sigma^{2}_{\eta}+\sigma^{2}_{\beta}-2\textnormal{Cov}(B,H)),

(9)

for
(B,H)
a random variable of law
\rho
, with
Z=\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\beta\right]}=\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\eta\right]}
.

Remark 2
.

It is at this point that we really need to assume that the brick law
\rho
has finite third moments, to be able to get to Brownian scaling limits later on, because of the size-biasing of the root brick.

CLT after one time step.

We now prove a CLT, first for
M_{1}(n)
, then for tuples of subpopulations after one time step.

Proposition 5
.

We have the following convergence in distribution:

\frac{M_{1}(n)-n}{\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

(10)

where
\sigma
is defined by (
9
).

Proof.

Let us consider a sequence of independent variables
((B_{i},H_{i}))_{i\geqslant 0}
, where
(B_{0},H_{0})
has law
\rho^{*}
, and
(B_{i},H_{i})
has law
\rho
for
i\geqslant 1
, as well as the associated sums
\widehat{H}_{n}=\sum_{0\leqslant i\leqslant n}H_{i}
,
\widehat{B}_{n}=U_{B_{0}}+\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}B_{i}
, where, conditionally on
B_{0}
,
U_{B_{0}}
is uniform on
\{1,\dots,B_{0}\}
. Then, by the multidimensional FCLT, we have

\Bigg(\frac{\widehat{B}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-Znt}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\widehat{H}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-Znt}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigg)_{t\geqslant 0}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\Big(W^{(B)}(t),W^{(H)}(t)\Big)_{t\geqslant 0},

(11)

where
(W^{(B)}(t),W^{(H)}(t))
is a bidimensional Brownian motion with covariance matrix
C(\rho)
, the covariance matrix of
\rho
.

By Skorokhod’s representation theorem, let us assume that we are working on a probability space for which the convergence (
11
) holds in the almost sure sense rather than in distribution. Now, we have
M_{1}(n)=\widehat{H}_{t_{n}}
, where
t_{n}=\inf\{k\geqslant 0\lvert\widehat{B}_{k}>n\}-1
. Since we have assumed almost sure convergence, we have, uniformly in
t\geqslant 0
,

\widehat{B}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}=Znt+\sqrt{n}W^{(B)}(t)+\so{\hskip-1.49994pt\left(\sqrt{n}\right)},

so that with high probability
t_{n}=n/Z+\so{\hskip-1.49994pt\left(n\right)}
, and, using the continuity of
W^{(B)}
,

Zt_{n}=n-\sqrt{n}W^{(B)}\Big(\frac{1}{Z}\Big)+\so{\hskip-1.49994pt\left(\sqrt{n}\right)},

therefore

\displaystyle M_{1}(n)

\displaystyle=\widehat{H}_{t_{n}}=Z\tau_{n}+\sqrt{n}W^{(H)}\Big(\frac{t_{n}}{n}\Big)+\so{\hskip-1.49994pt\left(\sqrt{n}\right)}

\displaystyle=n+\sqrt{n}\Big(W^{(H)}\Big(\frac{1}{Z}\Big)-W^{(B)}\Big(\frac{1}{Z}\Big)\Big)+\so{\hskip-1.49994pt\left(\sqrt{n}\right)}.

Since
W^{(H)}-W^{(B)}
is a Brownian motion of variance
\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(H_{1}-B_{1})^{2}\right]}
, we obtain that
(M_{1}(n)-n)/\sqrt{n}
does converge in distribution to a centered Gaussian variable of variance

\frac{\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(H_{1}-B_{1})^{2}\right]}}{Z}=\sigma^{2}.\qed

The extension of the previous result to several populations is proved similarly:

Proposition 6
.

For
p
a positive integer, fix
s_{1}<\dots<s_{p+1}\in\mathbb{R}
, and consider the subpopulations
M_{1}(n,i)=M_{1}([\lfloor ns_{i}\rfloor,\lfloor ns_{i+1}\rfloor[)
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
. Then, we have the following convergence:

\left(\frac{M_{1}(n,i)-n(s_{i+1}-s_{i})}{\sqrt{n}}\right)_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\sigma^{2}\cdot\text{Diag}(\{s_{i+1}-s_{i}\})),

where
\text{Diag}(\{s_{i+1}-s_{i}\})
is the diagonal
p\times p
matrix with coefficients
s_{i+1}-s_{i},\,1\leqslant i\leqslant p
.

Covariance.

As a corollary of the previous results we have the following estimate for the covariance between different subpopulations after one time step.

Proposition 7
.

For any
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
with
0<C_{1}\leqslant C_{2}<C_{3}
, we have

\textnormal{Cov}\left(M_{1}([0,\lfloor C_{1}n\rfloor[),M_{1}([\lfloor C_{2}n\rfloor,\lfloor C_{3}n\rfloor[)\right)=o(n)\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

For ease of notation, let us assume
C_{1}=C_{2}=1
and
C_{3}=2
, and let us define

m(n):=\frac{M_{1}([0,n[)-n}{\sqrt{n}},\ \ m^{\prime}(n):=\frac{M_{1}([n,2n[)-n}{\sqrt{n}},\ \ v(n):=m(n)m^{\prime}(n).

From
6
we have

m(n)^{2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\sigma^{2}\cdot\chi^{2}_{1}

m^{\prime}(n)^{2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\sigma^{2}\cdot\chi^{2}_{1},

and furthermore (
8
) implies convergence of their means. It follows from the equality case in Fatou’s lemma (see
[
Kal02
, Lemma 4.11]
) that
(m(n)^{2}:n\geqslant 1)
and
(m^{\prime}(n)^{2}:n\geqslant 1)
are uniformly integrable. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\lvert v(n)\rvert\mathds{1}{{}_{\{v(n)>K\}}}\right]}\leqslant

\displaystyle\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\lvert m(n)m^{\prime}(n)\rvert\left(\mathds{1}{{}_{\{m(n)>K\}}}+\mathds{1}{{}_{\{m^{\prime}(n)>K\}}}\right)\right]}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[m(n)^{2}\right]}\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[m^{\prime}(n)^{2}\mathds{1}{{}_{\{m^{\prime}(n)>K\}}}\right]}+\frac{1}{2}\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[m(n)^{2}\mathds{1}{{}_{\{m(n)>K\}}}\right]}\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[m^{\prime}(n)^{2}\right]},

so that
v(n)
is uniformly integrable as well. Using
6
once again,
(m(n),m^{\prime}(n))
converges in distribution to
(Y_{1},Y_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\sigma^{2}\cdot I_{2})
, so that

\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{Cov}\left(M_{1}([0,n[)M_{1}([n,2n[)\right)=\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[v(n)\right]}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[Y_{1}Y_{2}\right]}=0.

∎

3.2
Feller diffusion limit for population evolution

Recall that we write
M_{k}(n)\equiv M_{k}([0,n[)
for
k\geqslant 1
for ease of notation. From what precedes, we expect that
(n^{-1}\cdot M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(n):t\geqslant 0)
converges in distribution to a Feller diffusion process. We will actually prove a stronger and more general result that applies to our case. This kind of statement (that might be of independent interest) is “classical”, but since we have not been able to locate a precise reference for it, we provide a complete proof.

Theorem 3

(Convergence to a Feller diffusion)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, let
(A_{k}^{(n)})_{k\geqslant 0}
be a discrete-time homogeneous,
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
-valued Markov chain, started at
A_{0}^{(n)}=n
. Assume that it is also a martingale, and that there exist constants
\sigma,s>0
such that the following conditions are satisfied:

\displaystyle\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(\Delta A_{1}^{(n)})^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\geqslant s\ \ \ \forall\,n\geqslant 1

(12)

\displaystyle\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(\Delta A_{1}^{(n)})^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\sigma^{2}n

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta A_{1}^{(n)}}{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}).

(14)

Let
\tau_{n}
be the extinction time of
(A_{k}^{(n)})_{t}
, i.e.
\tau_{n}=\min\{k\geqslant 1|A_{t}^{(n)}=0\}
. Then we have the following joint convergence in distribution:

\Bigg(\Bigg(\frac{A_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{(n)}}{n}\Bigg)_{t\geqslant 0},\frac{\tau_{n}}{n}\Bigg)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\Big(\big(X_{t}\big)_{t\geqslant 0},\theta^{0}\Big),

(15)

where
(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a Feller diffusion, i.e. the unique weak solution to

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma\sqrt{X_{t}}\mathrm{d}W_{t}

(16)

with initial condition
X_{0}=1
, for
(W_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
a standard Brownian motion, and where
\theta^{0}=\inf\{t\geqslant 0:X_{t}=0\}
is the extinction time of
X
.

Recall that the Feller diffusion
X
can equivalently be seen as a continuous-state branching process (CSBP) start gin from
X_{0}=1
and with branching mechanism
\psi(u)=(\sigma^{2}/2)u^{2}
. Thus, from classical properties of CSBPs (see for instance
[
LG99
, Chapter 2]
), the law of the extinction time
\theta^{0}
is explicit:

\displaystyle\Pb{\hskip-2.20001pt\left(\theta^{0}\leqslant t\right)}=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}t}}.

(17)

Notice that in the case of
A_{k}^{(n)}=M_{k}(n)
, the martingale property is granted by Proposition
2
, the variance assumption (
13
) by Proposition
4
, the CLT (
14
) by Proposition
5
and finally the non-degeneracy (
12
) is implied by (
4
). The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
3
.

3.2.1
Step 1: SDE approximation in a strip

To prove
3
, we first prove a weaker result for the process
\left(A_{k}^{(n)}\right)_{k}
, up to the time it leaves the strip with horizontal borders at levels
n\varepsilon
and
n/\varepsilon
, see
Figure

4
.

Figure 4:
In
8
, we prove the convergence of
\frac{A_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{(n,\varepsilon)}}{n}
, the rescaled discrete process up until it hits
[0,\varepsilon]\cup[1/\varepsilon,+\infty[
, to
(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})
, the Feller diffusion process up until it hits
[0,\varepsilon]\cup[1/\varepsilon,+\infty[
.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\big(\big(A_{k}^{(n)}\big)_{k}\big)_{n}
be like in
3
. For
\varepsilon>0
, let
\tau^{(n,\varepsilon)}
be the first time that
\big(A_{k}^{(n)}\big)_{k}
hits
[0,\varepsilon n]\cup[n/\varepsilon,+\infty[
. We denote by
\big(A_{k}^{(n,\varepsilon)}\big)_{k}
the process
A_{k}^{(n)}
stopped at
\tau^{(n,\varepsilon)}
:
A_{k}^{(n,\varepsilon)}=A_{k\wedge\tau^{(n,\varepsilon)}}
. Likewise, let
(X_{t})
be as in
3
, and define
\theta^{\varepsilon}
as the hitting time of
[0,\varepsilon]\cup[1/\varepsilon,+\infty[
for
(X_{t})
, and
(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})
as
(X_{t})
stopped at
\theta^{\varepsilon}
. Then we have the following convergence in distribution:

\Bigg(\frac{A_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{(n,\varepsilon)}}{n}\Bigg)_{t\geqslant 0}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\big(X_{t}^{\varepsilon}\big)_{t\geqslant 0}.

Proof.

We apply to
(A_{k}^{(n,\varepsilon)}/n)
the result
[
IP10
, Corollary 2.2]
(in our case, since we have a Markov process, the result of Ispány and Pap essentially boils down to a rephrasing of
[
JS03
, Theorem IX.4.21]
, with lighter notation). Following the notation of
[
IP10
]
, we define

U^{n}_{k+1}=\frac{1}{n}\Big(A^{(n,\varepsilon)}_{k+1}-A^{(n,\varepsilon)}_{k}\Big),\ \ U^{n}_{0}=1,\ \ \mathcal{U}^{n}_{t}=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}U^{n}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}A^{(n,\varepsilon)}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor},

and we take for
\big(\mathcal{F}^{n}_{k}\big)_{k}
the filtration associated to
\big(A^{(n,\varepsilon)}_{k}\big)_{k}
. Since
\big(\mathcal{U}^{n}_{t}\big)_{t}
is a martingale, condition (i) of
[

, Corollary 2.2]
is automatically satisfied with
\beta=0
. Moreover, from (

\mathrm{Var}{\left(\mathcal{U}^{n}_{k+1}\lvert\mathcal{F}^{n}_{k}\right)}\sim\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathcal{U}^{n}_{k},

so that condition (ii) is satisfied with
\gamma(s,u)=\sigma\sqrt{u}
.

It remains to check condition (iii) of
[
IP10
, Corollary 2.2]
on big jumps:

\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(U^{n}_{k})^{2}\mathds{1}{{}_{\{\lvert U^{n}_{k}\rvert>\kappa\}}}\lvert\mathcal{F}^{n}_{k-1}\right]}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0\quad\forall t,\kappa>0.

(18)

Note that, since
\big(A^{(n)}_{k}\big)
is homogeneous, for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, for any
n_{0}\in\{\lceil\varepsilon n\rceil,\dots,\lfloor n/\varepsilon\rfloor\}
, conditionally on
A^{(n,\varepsilon)}_{k}=n_{0}
, the variable
A^{(n,\varepsilon)}_{k+1}
has the same law as
A^{(n_{0},\varepsilon)}_{1}
. Now recall from (

) that we have

\frac{\Big(\Delta A^{(n_{0},\varepsilon)}_{1}\Big)^{2}}{n_{0}}\xrightarrow[n_{0}\to\infty]{(d)}\sigma^{2}\cdot\chi^{2}_{1},

and furthermore (

) implies convergence of their means. Like in the proof of

, it follows from
[

that the variables in the last display are uniformly integrable and in particular that

\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{n_{0}\geqslant 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\Big(\Delta A^{(n_{0},\varepsilon)}_{1}\Big)^{2}}{n_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{(\Delta A^{(n_{0},\varepsilon)}_{1})^{2}>K\cdot n_{0}}\right]=0,

so that in particular, for any fixed
\kappa>0
we have

\lim_{n_{0}\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\Big(\Delta A^{(n_{0},\varepsilon)}_{1}\Big)^{2}}{n_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{\Delta A^{(n_{0},\varepsilon)}_{1}>\kappa\cdot n_{0}}\right]=0,

and this easily implies (
18
).
∎

3.2.2
Step 2: control on extinction times

Let us denote
\widetilde{A}_{t}^{(n)}=A_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{(n)}/n
for ease of notation. To get the whole statement of
3
from
8
, we will use Skorokhod’s representation theorem, and assume that we are working on a probability space where the convergence of
8
occurs almost surely. On this probability space, we have the following result.

Proposition 9
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,n}
be the event where neither
\widetilde{A}^{(n)}
nor
X
:

(i)

Hit
1/\varepsilon
before
\varepsilon
;

(ii)

Go back up to level
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
after having hit
\varepsilon
;

(iii)

Survive for more than
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
after having hit
\varepsilon
.

Then, for any
\delta>0
, for
\varepsilon
small enough and
n
large enough, we have
\Pb{\hskip-2.20001pt\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,n}\right)}\geqslant 1-\delta
.

Figure 5:
A depiction of the event
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,n}
of
9
.

Before getting to the proof of
9
, let us explain how it yields
3
.

Proof of
3
.

Fix
\delta>0
. Suppose that
\widetilde{A}_{t}^{(n)}\to X
for the topology of uniform convergence on every compact of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Since the hitting time
\theta^{0}
of
0
by
X
is almost surely finite by (
17
), we can find
\varepsilon>0
small enough so that
\mathbb{P}(\theta^{0}\leqslant\varepsilon^{-1})\geqslant 1-\delta
. On this event intersected with the event
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon,n}
, we easily deduce that

\theta^{\varepsilon}\leqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\tau^{(n,\varepsilon)}}{n}\leqslant\theta^{0}\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{n}}{n}

which combined with

|\theta^{0}-\theta^{\varepsilon}|\leqslant\sqrt{\varepsilon}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\left|\frac{\tau^{(n,\varepsilon)}}{n}-\frac{\tau_{n}}{n}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{\varepsilon}

easily yields that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\tau_{n}/n-\theta^{0}|\leqslant 2\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. Similarly, the supremum norm between
\widetilde{A}_{t}^{(n)}
and
X
is upper bounded by the supremum norm over the time interval
[0,\varepsilon^{-1}]
plus
2\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. Up to decreasing
\varepsilon>0
, all of this happens on an event of probability at least
1-2\delta
,
3
follows.
∎

Let us now prove
9
. For point
(i)
, since
(X_{t})
is a martingale, and
\theta^{\varepsilon}
is an almost surely finite stopping time such that
X_{t\wedge\theta^{\varepsilon}}\leqslant 1/\varepsilon
a.s., by the optional stopping theorem we have
\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[X_{\theta^{\varepsilon}}\right]}=1
. Moreover,
\Pb{\hskip-2.20001pt\left(\{X_{\theta^{\varepsilon}}=\varepsilon\}\cup\{X_{\theta^{\varepsilon}}=1/\varepsilon\}\right)}=1
, so that

\Pb{\hskip-2.20001pt\left(X_{\theta^{\varepsilon}}=\varepsilon\right)}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon^{2}},

and the same arguments also apply to
\widetilde{A}^{(n)}
.

Point
(ii)
follows similarly from Doob’s (super)-martingale inequality, since for a positive super-martingale
(S_{t}:t\geqslant 0)
with continuous or discrete sample paths, we have

\Pb{\hskip-2.20001pt\left(\max_{t\geqslant 0}S_{t}\geqslant A\right)}\leqslant\frac{A}{S_{0}}.

This applies to both
X
and
\widetilde{A}^{(n)}
.

For point
(iii)
, we will use bounds on the extinction times of
\widetilde{A}^{(n)}
and
X
. For
X
these are explicitly given in (
17
). For
\widetilde{A}^{(n)}
we will use general bounds for extinction times of super-martingales with polynomial drift proved in Bertoin, Curien & Riera
[
BCR
]
which we restate here for the reader’s convenience:

Lemma 10

(
[
BCR
]
)
.

Let
(S_{k}:k\geqslant 0)
be a
(\mathcal{F}_{k}:k\geqslant 0)
-supermartingale with values in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
which is absorbed at 0. We suppose that there exist two constants
c>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that we have

\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[S_{k+1}-S_{k}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]}\leqslant-c\ S_{k}^{-\alpha}.

If
\tau=\inf\{k\geqslant 0:X_{k}=0\}
is the absorption time of the chain, then we have for
x>1

\sup_{x>1}\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\Pb{\hskip-2.20001pt\left(\tau\geqslant xn^{1+\alpha}\,\big|\,S_{0}=n\right)}\cdot x^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}<\infty.

The desired estimate will follow from the above lemma applied to
(A_{k}^{(n)})^{1/2}
(which obviously shares the same extinction time as
(\widetilde{A}_{k}^{(n)})
) and with
\alpha=1
, thanks to the following:

Lemma 11
.

The sequence
((A_{k}^{(n)})^{1/2}:k\geqslant 0)
is a super-martingale, and there exists
c>0
such that for all
n\geqslant 1
we have

\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(A_{1}^{(n)})^{1/2}-n^{1/2}\right]}\leqslant\frac{-c}{n^{1/2}}.

(19)

Proof.

The fact that it is a super-martingale follows directly from the conditional Jensen’s inequality. Now, to obtain (
19
), observe that

\big(A_{1}^{(n)}\big)^{1/2}=\big(n+\Delta A_{1}^{(n)}\big)^{1/2}=n^{1/2}\Bigg[1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Delta A_{1}^{(n)}}{n}-\frac{1}{8}\Big(\frac{\Delta A_{1}^{(n)}}{n}\Big)^{2}+\so{\hskip-1.49994pt\left(\Big(\frac{\Delta A_{1}^{(n)}}{n}\Big)^{2}\right)}\Bigg],

so that

\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(A_{1}^{(n)})^{1/2}-n^{1/2}\right]}=\frac{1}{2}\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\frac{\Delta A_{1}^{(n)}}{n^{1/2}}\right]}-\frac{1}{8}\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\frac{(\Delta A_{1}^{(n)})^{2}}{n^{3/2}}\right]}+\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\so{\hskip-1.49994pt\left(\frac{(\Delta A_{1}^{(n)})^{2}}{n^{3/2}}\right)}\right]}.

The first term in that sum is zero since
(A_{k}^{(n)})_{k}
is a martingale, and, by (
13
), the second behaves, as
n\to\infty
, like

-\frac{1}{8}\frac{\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(\Delta A_{1}^{(n)})^{2}\right]}}{n^{3/2}}\sim-\frac{1}{8}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{1/2}}.

Thus, there exists some integer
M>0
such that, for
n\geqslant M
, we have

\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(A_{1}^{(n)})^{1/2}-n^{1/2}\right]}\leqslant\frac{-\sigma^{2}}{16n^{1/2}}.

To handle smaller values of
n
, recall that
(A_{k}^{(n)})^{1/2}
is a supermartingale:

\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\big(A_{1}^{(n)}\big)^{1/2}\right]}<\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[A_{1}^{(n)}\right]}^{1/2}=n^{1/2},

and this inequality is strict since, by (
12
), the martingale is not degenerate. Thus we can indeed find
c>0
small enough so that the statement of the lemma holds for all
n\geqslant 1
.
∎

3.2.3
Convergence of several populations to independent Feller diffusions

We now prove an extension of
3
in the case of several subpopulations. Indeed, even if the discrete subpopulations are not independent themselves, their scaling limit is given by independent Feller diffusions.

Proposition 12
.

For
p\geqslant 1
a positive integer, fix
s_{1}<\dots<s_{p+1}\in\mathbb{R}
, and consider the subpopulations
M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{(n,i)}=M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}([\lfloor ns_{i}\rfloor,\lfloor ns_{i+1}\rfloor[)
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
and
0\leqslant t\leqslant 1
, along with their respective extinction times
\tau^{(n,i)}
. Then, we have the following joint convergence:

\Bigg(\Bigg(\frac{M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{(n,i)}}{n}\Bigg)_{t\geqslant 0},\frac{\tau^{(n,i)}}{n}\Bigg)_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\Big(\big(X_{t}^{(i)}\big)_{t\geqslant 0},\theta^{(i)}\Big)_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p},

where, the
(X^{(i)})
are independent Feller diffusion processes with respective initial conditions
X_{0}^{(i)}=s_{i+1}-s_{i}
, variances
(s_{i+1}-s_{i})\sigma^{2}
, and extinction times
\theta^{(i)}
.

Proof.

We will only detail the proof for
p=2
and
s_{1}=0,s_{2}=1,s_{3}=2
, as the general case can be obtained similarly but needs much heavier notation. In that case, we are interested in
(M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{(n,1)},M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{(n,2)})=(M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}([0,n[),M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}([n,2n[))
, and their extinction times
\tau^{(n,1)},\tau^{(n,2)}
.

Note that
(M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{(n,1)},M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{(n,2)})
is a bivariate martingale and a Markov chain. We will thus apply the same strategy as for a single subpopulation. We first define
(M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{(n,1,\varepsilon)},M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{(n,2,\varepsilon)})
as the couple of subpopulations, up to the first time one of them leaves the strip
(n\varepsilon,n/\varepsilon)
. Then, we apply
[
IP10
, Corollary 2.2]
, this time for to bivariate sequence
(U_{k}^{(n,1)},U_{k}^{(n,2)})
, defined as

U_{k+1}^{(n,i)}=\frac{1}{n}\left(M_{k+1}^{(n,i,\varepsilon)}-M_{k}^{(n,i,\varepsilon)}\right),\ \ U^{(n,i)}_{0}=1,\ \ \mathcal{U}^{(n,i)}_{t}=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}U^{(n,i)}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}M^{(n,i,\varepsilon)}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}.

Condition (i) of
[
IP10
, Corollary 2.2]
is once again satisfied with
\beta=0
since
(\mathcal{U}^{n,i}_{t})_{t}
is a martingale. Moreover, from (
8
) and
7
, condition (ii) is also satisfied with

\gamma(s,u)_{i,j}=\sigma\sqrt{u_{i}}\cdot\delta_{i,j}.

To check condition (iii), we can also proceed similarly to the case of a single subpopulation. Indeed, from
6
,
(M_{1}^{(n,1)},M_{1}^{(n,2)})
, rescaled by
\sqrt{n}
, converges to a couple of independent Gaussian variables, so that

\frac{1}{n}\lVert\Delta M_{1}^{n}\rVert^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\left((M_{1}^{(n,1)}-n)^{2}+(M_{1}^{(n,2)}-n)^{2}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\sigma^{2}\chi_{2}^{2}

and, from (
8
) we have convergence of their means

\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\frac{1}{n}\lVert\Delta M_{1}^{n}\rVert^{2}\right]}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}2\sigma^{2}=\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\sigma^{2}\chi_{2}^{2}\right]}.

Using once again
[
Kal02
, Lemma 4.11]
, we can show that this implies condition (iii) of
[
IP10
, Corollary 2.2]
.

We can then conclude with the same chain of arguments as for
3
, observing that the tightness of the rescaled extinction time
\frac{\tau_{2n}}{2n}
obtained above, implies the joint tightness of
(\frac{\tau^{(n,1)}}{n},\frac{\tau^{(n,2)}}{n})
, since we have
0\leqslant\tau^{(n,i)}\leqslant\tau_{2n}
.
∎

Corollary 14
.

There exists
0<c<1
such that for all

\frac{c}{n}\leqslant\mathbb{P}(M_{n}([0,1[)\neq 0)\leqslant\frac{c^{-1}}{n}.

Proof.

Let us write
M_{k}\equiv M_{k}([0,1[)

\tau\equiv\tau_{1}
denote the hitting time of

\mathbb{P}(\tau\geqslant n)
using the second moment method. Recall that by the martingale property we have
\mathbb{E}[M_{k}]=1

4

\mathbb{E}[(\Delta M_{k})^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leqslant\mathrm{Cst}\cdot M_{k}

Since the increments of a martingale are orthogonal in

4
Scaling limit of the trees

The goal of this section is to obtain the convergence of the whole rescaled genealogical forest to the Brownian forest, for the local pointed Gromov–Hausdorff topology. Recall from Definition
2
the definition of the trees
T^{\uparrow}_{i}
, and consider the pointed tree
\underline{F}^{\uparrow}
obtained by attaching those trees on the bi-infinite line
\mathbb{R}
pointed at
0
. For our discussion, it will be convenient to use the labeling of the nodes of
\underline{F}^{\uparrow}
by
\{(i,j):i\in\mathbb{Z},j\geqslant 0\}
.

Our first goal will be to define a good approximation of those random trees using finitely many points, see
Section

4.1
. Before that, let us briefly discuss the local Gromov-Hausdorff topology (for more details see Chapter 8.1 in
[
BBI01
]
, the reference
[
ADH13
]
or Section 2 of
[
CLG14
]
). First recall that a metric space
(E,d)

is called a length space if, for every
a,b\in E
, the distance
d(a,b)
coincides with the infimum
of the lengths of continuous curves connecting
a
to
b
. We also say that
(E,d)
is boundedly compact if all its closed balls are compact.
For
r>0
, and
(E,d,\rho)
a pointed metric space, let us denote by
\mathrm{Ball}_{r}(E)
the closed ball of radius
r
centered at
\rho
in
E
. A sequence
(E_{n},d_{n},\rho_{n})
of pointed boundedly compact length spaces is said to converge to
(E,d,\rho)
in the local Gromov-Hausdorff topology if, for every
r>0
,
\mathrm{Ball}_{r}(E_{n})
converges to
\mathrm{Ball}_{r}(E)
, for the
usual Gromov-Hausdorff distance between pointed compact metric spaces. The space of all pointed boundedly compact length spaces (modulo isometries) can be equipped with a metric
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}
which is compatible with the preceding notion of convergence, and is then separable and complete for this metric.

4.1
\varepsilon
-meshing approximation

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and suppose that
\varepsilon n
is an even integer to simplify our notation. An obvious proxy for
\underline{F}^{\uparrow}
is obtained by the subforest spanned by the nodes with coordinates
(i\varepsilon n+\frac{1}{2},j\varepsilon n)
for
i\in\mathbb{Z},j\geqslant 0
. Those nodes will be hereafter called the
\varepsilon n
-nodes
. We shall denote by
\underline{F}^{\uparrow,(\varepsilon n)}
the subtree of
\underline{F}^{\uparrow}
spanned by the
\varepsilon n
-nodes, similarly grafted on the bi-infinite line
\mathbb{R}
pointed at 0.

Figure 6:
Illustration of the
\varepsilon
-meshing approximation
\underline{F}^{\uparrow,(\varepsilon n)}
: the whole forest
\underline{F}^{\uparrow}
is in grey, and the meshing in blue.

The advantage of
\underline{F}^{\uparrow,(\varepsilon n)}
is that it can be described by “finitely many” random variables (as
n\to\infty
) and that it approximates
\underline{F}^{\uparrow}
very well in the following sense:

Proposition 15
.

The forest
\underline{F}^{\uparrow}
is close for the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff distance, to its
\varepsilon
-meshing
\underline{F}^{\uparrow,(\varepsilon n)}
, uniformly in
n
. More precisely, for every
\delta>0
we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pb{\hskip-2.20001pt\left(\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}\big(n^{-1}\cdot\underline{F}^{\uparrow},n^{-1}\cdot\underline{F}^{\uparrow,(\varepsilon n)}\big)\geqslant\delta\right)}=0.

(21)

Proof.

By definition of the local Gromov–Hausdorff topology, it is sufficient to prove the result once restricted to a ball centered at the root of arbitrary radius
A>0
in
\underline{F}^{\uparrow}
and
\underline{F}^{\uparrow,(\varepsilon n)}
and we take
A=1
to fix ideas. We first start by showing that all nodes of those trees lie in some large box (for their embedding in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) :

Lemma 16
.

For any
\delta>0
, there exists
C>0
such that for all
n
large enough, for their natural embedding in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
all nodes of
\mathrm{Ball}_{n}(\underline{F}^{\uparrow})
lie in the “box”

\mathrm{Box}_{C,n}:=\{(i+\frac{1}{2},j):-Cr\leqslant i\leqslant Cr,0\leqslant j\leqslant n\}

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

The nodes accessible in the ball of radius
n
centered at the root
(0,0)
of
\underline{F}^{\uparrow}
belong to the nodes of
T_{i}^{\uparrow}
at height less than
n
for some index
i
satisfying
-n\leqslant i\leqslant n
. The absolute value of the abscissae of those nodes are bounded from above by the quantity

\max_{0\leqslant k\leqslant n}M_{k}([-n,n[)+1

which by virtue of Proposition
12
converges in law once renormalized by
n^{-1}
. This proves the result. ∎

Fix
\delta>\varepsilon
and let us call a node
(i+\frac{1}{2},j)
a
(\delta,\varepsilon,n)
-
bad
node, if the tree descended from
(i+\frac{1}{2},j)
in
F^{\uparrow}
has height at least
\delta n
and contains no
\varepsilon n
-node. Clearly, if there is no
(\delta,\varepsilon,n)
-bad node within distance
n
from the root in
F^{\uparrow}
, then every node of
\mathrm{Ball}_{n}(\underline{F}^{\uparrow})
is within distance at most
\delta n
inside
\underline{F}^{\uparrow}
from an
\varepsilon n
-node, so that the Hausdorff distance between

\mathrm{Ball}_{n}(\underline{F}^{\uparrow})
and
\mathrm{Ball}_{n}(\underline{F}^{\uparrow,(\varepsilon n)})
is at most
2\delta n
. Given the previous lemma, the proof reduces to showing that:

Lemma 17
.

Fix
\delta>0
and
C>0
. Then we have

\inf_{n\geqslant 1}\mathbb{P}(\mbox{there are no $(\delta,\varepsilon,n)$-{bad} node in $\mathrm{Box}_{C,n}$})\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}1.

(22)

Proof.

To prove this, notice first that for a node to be
(\delta,\varepsilon,n)
-bad, its descendance needs first to survive for
\varepsilon n
unit of times, then be of size less than
\varepsilon n
every
\varepsilon n
unit of times (for otherwise it would contain a
\varepsilon n
-node) for an additional time
(\delta-\varepsilon)n
. Combining Corollaries
14
and
13
, the probability of such an event is bounded above by

\mathrm{Cst}\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon n}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\delta/\varepsilon}.

(23)

Notice also that if there is a
(\delta,\varepsilon,n)
-bad node in
\mathrm{Box}_{C,n}
whose descendance stays in
\mathrm{Box}_{C,n}
, then there must be an
(\delta-\varepsilon,\varepsilon,n)
-bad node among the set

\{(i+\frac{1}{2},j\varepsilon n):-Cn\leqslant i\leqslant Cn,0\leqslant j\leqslant\varepsilon^{-1}\}.

Moreover, similarly to
16
, we can suppose that all points in
\mathrm{Box}_{C,n}
have a descendance up to level
n
that stays within
\mathrm{Box}_{C^{\prime},n}
for some
C^{\prime}>C

1
), and using the bound (
23
), the probability in (

) is therefore bounded by

2C^{\prime}n\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathrm{Cst}\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon n}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\delta/\varepsilon}\leqslant\mathrm{Cst^{\prime}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\delta/\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

For fixed
\delta>0
, this goes to
0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
(uniformly in
n
) as desired.
∎

∎

4.2
Convergence of
\underline{F}^{\uparrow,(\varepsilon n)}

Proposition 18
.

For each
\varepsilon>0
, the random forest
n^{-1}\cdot\underline{F}^{\uparrow,(\varepsilon n)}

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0

\mathrm{Box}_{C,n}

By Proposition
12

Let us now consider the subforest
F^{\downarrow,(\zeta n)}
spanned by the
\zeta n

\mathrm{Box}_{C,n}
in the descending forest
F^{\downarrow}

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{t}([(x,y),(x^{\prime},y)[)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}([(\lfloor xn\rfloor,\lfloor yn\rfloor),(\lfloor x^{\prime}n\rfloor,\lfloor yn\rfloor)[),

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{(x,y),(x^{\prime},y)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\tau_{(\lfloor xn\rfloor,\lfloor yn\rfloor),(\lfloor x^{\prime}n\rfloor,\lfloor yn\rfloor)}.

(24)

We start at time
k=0
with
2\lfloor C/\zeta\rfloor+1
ancestral lines, at

\zeta

•

for
i=0

(0,k\zeta)
continues straight up to
(0,(k+1)\zeta)
;

•

for
i\geqslant 0

(i,k\zeta)
continues up to
(i^{\prime},(k+1)\zeta)

(j,k\zeta)
– where

(i^{\prime}\zeta+\hat{M}_{k\zeta}([(i,k\zeta),(j,k\zeta)[),(k+1)\zeta)

(i,k\zeta)

(j,k\zeta)

•

likewise for negative

•

for any integer

(which is possible since the values of

\zeta

(i\zeta,(k+1)\zeta)

We proceed until time

from a number

(\hat{\tau}_{v,v^{\prime}})

G^{(\zeta)}

F^{\uparrow,(\varepsilon n)}
inside
\mathrm{Box}_{C,n}
, but not enough to control its metric structure. The problem comes from the fact, although we know when two ancestral linages of some
\varepsilon n
-node are in the same “interval” (in pink in Figure
7
), we do not know whether those two lineages meet or not. However, it is easy to see that two such lineages must meet within distance
\delta n
as soon as there is no
(\delta,\zeta,n)
-bad node in the box. We can then use Lemma
22
to conclude that on the event when there is no
(\delta,\zeta,n)
-bad node in the box, the subforest
F^{\uparrow,(\varepsilon n)}
(restricted to the box) is known up to
\delta n
in Gromov–Hausdorff distance using
F^{\downarrow,(\zeta n)}
. Since the latter converges in distribution for any fixed
\zeta>0
, we get the result.
∎

4.3
Proof of Theorem
1

We can now prove Theorem
1
using a double limit procedure. Let us abstract the setting for clarity. We have random variable
X_{n}
(here
n^{-1}\cdot\underline{F}^{\uparrow}
) with values in a Polish space
(E,d)
(here the space of boundedly compact pointed length spaces). For each
\varepsilon>0
, we possess an
\varepsilon
-approximation
X_{n}^{{(\varepsilon)}}
(here
n^{-1}\cdot F^{\uparrow,(\varepsilon n)}
) which is controlled uniformly (Proposition
21
) in the sense that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\Delta\big(\mathcal{L}(X_{n}),\mathcal{L}(X_{n}^{{(\varepsilon)}})\big)=0,

where
\mathcal{L}(X)
is the law of
X
, thus a point in the Polish space
\mathcal{M}_{1}(E)
of probability measures on
E
, endowed with the Lévy-Prokhorov metric
\Delta
. Furthermore, each
\varepsilon
-approximation converges in law
X_{n}^{(\varepsilon)}\to\mathcal{X}^{(\varepsilon)}
as
n\to\infty
, or in terms of laws using the Lévy-Prokhorov metric:

\Delta\big(\mathcal{L}(X_{n}^{(\varepsilon)}),\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}^{(\varepsilon)})\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Coupling the previous two displays, we deduce that
(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}^{(\varepsilon)}):\varepsilon>0)
is a Cauchy family in
(\mathcal{M}_{1}(E),\Delta)
and so converges towards
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})
for some random variable
\mathcal{X}
. It is then an easy matter to see that
\Delta(\mathcal{L}(X_{n}),\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}))\to 0
or equivalently that
X_{n}\to\mathcal{X}
in distribution as
n\to\infty
. This proves the desired convergence in law.
In our case, in order to identify
\mathcal{X}
with the Brownian forest
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
, it suffices to notice the law of the limit
\mathcal{X}
only depends on
\sigma>0
, and that this is a very well-known result (see Theorem
2
) in the case when the brick distribution is associated with Galton–Watson trees with finite variance!

5
Extensions

In this section, we discuss extensions of the model that we have studied in this paper, in two different directions. The first one goes beyond the Brownian regime, in the case where the brick distribution
\rho
is heavy-tailed. The second one is a continuous-time model, where the bricks now have random, continuum heights.

5.1
Stable brick distributions

If we consider brick distributions with heavy tails, we might expect two different types of asymptotic behavior. Indeed, suppose that
\beta
and
\theta
are respectively in the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
- (resp.
\alpha^{\prime}
-) stable law, with
1<\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\leqslant 2
. Then, if, say
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
, we expect that the fluctuations of
\theta
prevail, so that, asympotically, the associated trees should essentially behave like independent
\alpha^{\prime}
-stable trees. Similarly, if
\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha
, then the dual trees should behave asymptotically like independent
\alpha
-stable trees. Now, if both
\beta
and
\theta
have
\alpha
-stable tails, then neither prevails on the other, and we expect to see a new type of limiting object, related to Lévy processes with both positive and negative jumps.

The study of either of these regimes requires more technical arguments than the Brownian regime. Indeed, in those cases, the lack of second moments for
\rho
entails that the size-biased bottom of the root brick has infinite expectation, and it also prevents us from using the arguments of the present paper to prove that subpopulations are martingales. In a work in progress, we are tackling those hurdles to prove the expected limit behaviors.

5.2
Continuous-time model

Let us consider a variant of the case where
\rho=\beta\otimes\eta
, that we describe informally, similarly to
Section

2.2
. Let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability distributions on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 2},\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 1}
respectively, with finite third moments, and with respective expectations
Z_{\mu},Z_{\nu}-1
. We start at time
t=0
with countably many individuals, represented by the nodes on
\mathbb{Z}
, and we equip them with independent pairs of independent exponential clocks
(X,X^{\prime})
of respective parameters 1 and
\lambda=Z_{\mu}/Z_{\nu}
. Then, when the first clock rings, if it is one of the first type, the corresponding individual gives birth to children according to
\mu
; if it is one the second type, a number of individuals on its right, starting with itself, die according to
\nu
. We continue this recursively on the descendants of the original individuals. (Note that a.s. no two clocks ring simultaneously.) This yields a bi-infinite forest
\mathscr{F}
, that we transform into a pointed tree
\underline{\mathscr{F}}
as before, by grafting it on the real line and pointing it at 0.

Let us now sketch the arguments to transfer the results that we have proven on discrete brick walls to this new variant. We can construct a coupling between
\underline{\mathscr{F}}
and a sequence
\big(\underline{F}^{\uparrow(N)}:N\geqslant 1\big)
of forests with discrete edge lengths, induced by brick walls, such that
\underline{\mathscr{F}}
is well approximated by
\underline{F}^{\uparrow(N)}
, as
N\to\infty
. Let
N
be a positive integer such that
1+\lambda<N
, and let
\ell_{N}:=\ln{\frac{N}{N-1}}
. For each individual
i
in
\mathscr{F}
, we define the following discrete approximations of the clocks
(X^{(i)},X^{\prime(i)})
associated to
i
:

Y^{(i)}:=\ell_{N}\Big\lfloor\frac{X^{(i)}}{\ell_{N}}\Big\rfloor,\ \ Y^{\prime(i)}:=\ell_{N}\Big\lfloor\frac{X^{\prime(i)}}{\ell_{N}}\Big\rfloor.

We then pick the exact same birth/death events after each discrete waiting time, as for their continuum counterparts: this yields a forest
{F}^{\uparrow(N)}
where edge lengths are multiples of
\ell_{N}
.

By construction,
Y^{(i)}/\ell_{N}
and
Y^{\prime(i)}/\ell_{N}
are geometric variables of parameters
1-1/N
and
1-\lambda/N
respectively. Thus, the forest
{F}^{\uparrow(N)}
has the same law (up to rescaling time by
\ell_{N}
) as the primal forest of a brick wall model whose brick distribution
\rho_{N}
is as follows:

•

\rho_{N}(1,k)=(1/N)\mu(k),\,\forall\,k\geqslant 2

•

\rho_{N}(k,1)=(\lambda/N)\nu(k-1),\,\forall\,k\geqslant 2

•

\rho_{N}(1,1)=1-1/N-\lambda/N
.

This model is critical, with both marginals
\eta_{N}
and
\beta_{N}
having finite third moments. We compute the associated variance
\sigma_{N}
given by (
9
):

\sigma_{N}^{2}=\frac{\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\mu^{2}\right]}-2\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\mu\right]}+\lambda(\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(\nu+1)^{2}\right]}-2\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\nu+1\right]})}{N+\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\mu\right]}-1-\lambda}.

By the results of the previous sections, if we take bricks with height 1 as before, for fixed
N
the discrete primal forest, rescaled by
1/n
, converges as
n\to\infty
to the Brownian forest of parameter
\sigma_{N}
. As the actual the discrete-time approximation
\underline{F}^{\uparrow(N)}
of
\underline{\mathscr{F}}
is rescaled in time by
\ell_{N}
, for fixed
N
,
\frac{1}{n}\underline{F}^{\uparrow(N)}
thus converges as
n\to\infty
to a Brownian forest of parameter
\sigma_{N}/\sqrt{\ell_{N}}
. We see that this scaling parameter behaves like
\sigma=\left[\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\mu^{2}\right]}-2\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[\mu\right]}+\lambda(\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(\nu+1)^{2}\right]}-2\Esp{\hskip-2.20001pt\left[(\nu+1)\right]})\right]^{1/2}
, as
N\to\infty
.

Therefore, we expect that
\frac{1}{n}\underline{\mathscr{F}}
itself converges to a Brownian forest of parameter
\sigma
. To prove this, we can establish a bound on the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between
\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Ball}_{n}(\underline{\mathscr{F}})
and
\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Ball}_{n}(\underline{F}^{\uparrow(N)})
, going to zero as
N\to\infty
, uniformly in
n
. We have a natural correspondence between
\underline{\mathscr{F}}
and
\underline{F}^{\uparrow(N)}
: from the definition of the coupling, they have the same exact genealogical structure, and differences in edge lengths of at most
\ell_{N}
(with full probability), which induces a correspondence
\mathcal{R}
where, for
x,y\in\underline{F}^{\uparrow(N)},x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\underline{\mathscr{F}}
, and
(x,x^{\prime}),(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}
:

d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\cdot(1-\ell_{N})\leqslant d(x,y)\leqslant d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

Thus, for any
n
, the distortion of this correspondence, restricted to balls of radius
n
, rescaled by
1/n
, is smaller than
\ell_{N}
. This suffices to conclude.
