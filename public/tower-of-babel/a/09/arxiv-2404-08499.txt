Generalized Hydrodynamics for the Volterra lattice: ballistic and non-ballistic behavior of correlation functions
Guido Mazzuca

Abstract
In recent years, a lot of effort has been put in describing the hydrodynamic behavior of integrable systems. In this paper, we describe such picture for the Volterra lattice. Specifically, we are able to explicitly compute the susceptibility matrix and the current-field correlation matrix in terms of the density of states of the Volterra lattice endowed with a Generalized Gibbs ensemble. Furthermore, we apply the theory of linear Generalized Hydrodynamics to describe the Euler scale behavior of the correlation functions. We anticipate that the solution to the Generalized Hydrodynamics equations develops shocks at ξ 0 = x t \xi_{0}=\frac{x}{t} ; so this linear approximation does not fully describe the behavior of correlation functions. Intrigued by this fact, we performed several numerical investigations which show that, exactly when the solution to the hydrodynamic equations develops shock, the correlation functions show an highly oscillatory behavior. In view of this empirical observation, we believe that at this point ξ 0 \xi_{0} the diffusive contribution are not sub-leading corrections to the ballistic transport, but they are of the same order.

Introduction

In recent years, a lot of effort has been put in describing the hydrodynamic behavior of integrable systems, i.e. dynamical system whose evolution can be explicitly computed in terms of the initial data. Specifically, it has been a big mathematical challenge to fully describe the correlation functions of such integrable models. Recently, physicists have introduced a new theory that aims to describe the behavior of such functions, the so-called
Generalized Hydrodynamics
(GHD)
[
2
,
7
,
3
]

, see also the recent review
[
12
]
and the reference therein
. The underline idea of this theory is to obtain a set of hydrodynamic equations describing the macroscopic evolution of the considered medium; those equations also describes the evolution of the correlation functions.

Despite not being fully mathematically rigorous, using this theory H. Spohn was able to describe the behavior of the correlation functions for the Toda lattice
[
56
,
54
,
53
]
, see also
[
11
,
5
]
. His results were confirmed by comparing the prediction of the generalized hydrodynamics with numerical simulations, see
[
43
]
.

H. Spohn was able to carry out this computation relaying on results from
Random Matrix theory
(RMT). In particular,
enforcing the relation between the Toda lattice and the classical Real Beta ensemble
[
14
,
41
]
,
he was able to describe the linear approximation of the correlation functions of the Toda lattice.
This relation between the Toda lattice and the Real beta ensemble is not unique.
Indeed, after Spohn breakthrough, several authors enforced this idea in order to describe statistical properties of the dynamical systems at hand. For example, in
[
29
]
, the authors were able to describe the density of states of the Ablowitz-Ladik lattice in terms of the one of the circular
\beta
ensemble
[
35
]
, independently Spohn obtained an analogous result
[
55
]
. In
[
30
,
44
]
, the authors obtained a large deviation principle linking the Toda lattice and the Ablowitz-Ladik lattice with the Real
\beta
ensemble and the Circular
\beta
ensemble respectively. Another interesting result in this direction is
[
26
]
, in this paper the authors established connections between the classical Gibbs ensemble for the Exponential Toda lattice and the Volterra lattice with the Laguerre ensemble
[
14
]
and the Antisymmetric
\beta
-ensemble
[
15
]
, respectively. We want to mention also of the work
[
27
]
, where the authors computed explicitly the correlation function for the short range harmonic chain; they were also able to describe the long time asymptotic of those correlations in great details.

GHD has a wide range of applications. For example, it can be applied to several
quantum integrable models
to compute their correlation functions and, in general, their thermodynamic behavior
[
3
,
52
,
1
,
6
]
. Furthermore, it is possible to fully describe the thermodynamics of several classical integrable models by means of semiclassical limit of their quantum counterpart, which can be analyzed through GHD
[
36
,
37
]
.
GHD has also been an important tool to describe the soliton gas picture for several integrable PDE models, see
[
16
,
18
,
19
,
13
,
25
,
4
]
. For a wider introduction to the topic, we refer to the recent reviews
[
57
,
17
]
and the references therein.

In this paper, we consider the
Volterra lattice

[
49
]
and we compute the
susceptibility matrix
and the
current-field correlation matrix
. Furthermore, we apply the theory of Generalized hydrodynamics to describe the Euler scale behavior of the correlation functions. We anticipate that the solution to the differential equations describing the Euler scale dynamics
develops shock
for some explicit value
\xi_{0}=\frac{x}{t}
.
The reason for this shock is that the effective velocity of the system
v_{\text{eff}}(\lambda)
has a minimum at
0
. Such behavior was also observed in other quantum systems, see
[
52
,
58
]
, and has also some analogy with the classical chain of couple oscillator, see
[
43
]
. In the later case, the correlation functions have different scaling properties around the minimum point. We mention that a new approach to describe such shock formation through Witham modulation theory was recently developed in
[
48
]
, and this approach might be extended to our case.

After the usual ballistic scaling, we notice that at the point
\xi_{0}

2
Model and Results

The
Volterra lattice
, also known as the
discrete KdV equation
, describes the motion of
2N
particles on the line with equations

\frac{d}{dt}a_{j}\equiv\dot{a_{j}}=a_{j}\left(a_{j+1}-a_{j-1}\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,2N.

(2.1)

It was originally introduced by Volterra to study
population evolution in a hierarchical system of competing species. It was first solved by Kac and Van Moerbeke in
[
34
]
using a discrete version of inverse scattering transform due to Flaschka
[
20
]
. Equations (
2.1
) can be considered as a finite-dimensional approximation of the Korteweg–de Vries equation.
The phase space is
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2N}
and we consider periodic boundary conditions
a_{j}=a_{j+2N}
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. The Volterra lattice is a reduction of the
second flow of the Toda lattice

[
34
]
. Indeed, the latter is described by the dynamical system

\displaystyle\dot{a_{j}}

\displaystyle=a_{j}\left(b_{j+1}^{2}-b_{j}^{2}+a_{j+1}-a_{j-1}\right),

\displaystyle\qquad j=1,\dots,2N,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\dot{b_{j}}

\displaystyle=a_{j}(b_{j+1}+b_{j})-a_{j-1}(b_{j}+b_{j-1}),

\displaystyle\qquad j=1,\dots,2N,

(2.3)

and equations (
2.1
) are recovered just by setting
b_{j}\equiv 0
. The Hamiltonian structure of the equations follows from the one of the Toda lattice. On the phase space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}_{+}
we introduce the Poisson bracket

\{a_{j},a_{i}\}_{\textrm{Volt}}=a_{j}a_{i}(\delta_{i,j+1}-\delta_{i,j-1})\,

(2.4)

and the Hamiltonian
H_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}a_{j}\,
so that the equations of motion (
2.1
) can be written in the Hamiltonian form

\dot{a}_{j}=\{a_{j},H_{1}\}_{\textrm{Volt}}\,.

(2.5)

An elementary constant of motion for the system is
H_{0}=\prod_{j=1}^{2N}a_{j}
which is independent of
H_{1}
.
The Volterra lattice is a completely integrable system, and it admits several equivalent
Lax representations
, see e.g.
[
34
,
49
]
. The classical one reads

\dot{L}_{1}=\left[A_{1},L_{1}\right],

(2.6)

where

\begin{split}L_{1}&=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}a_{j+1}E_{j+1,j}+E_{j,j+1},\\
A_{1}&=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}(a_{j}+a_{j+1})E_{j,j}+E_{j,j+2}\,,\end{split}

(2.7)

where we define the matrix
E_{r,s}
as
\left(E_{r,s}\right)_{ij}=\delta^{i}_{r}\delta^{j}_{s}
and
E_{j+2N,i}=E_{j,i+2N}=E_{j,i}
.
There exists also a
symmetric
formulation due to Moser
[
49
]
,

\begin{split}\dot{L}_{2}&=\left[A_{2},L_{2}\right]\\
L_{2}&=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\sqrt{a}_{j}(E_{j,j+1}+E_{j+1,j})\,,\\
A_{2}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\sqrt{a_{j}a_{j+1}}(E_{j,j+2}-E_{j+2,j})\,,\end{split}

(2.8)

which assumes that all
a_{j}>0
.
Furthermore, as it was noticed in
[
26
]
, there exists also an
antisymmetric
formulation for this Lax pair, indeed a straightforward computation yields

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
a_{j}>0
for all
j=1,\ldots,2N
. Then, the dynamical system (
2.1
) admits an antisymmetric Lax matrix
L_{3}
with companion matrix
A_{3}
, namely the equations of motion are equivalent to
\dot{L_{3}}=\left[A_{3},L_{3}\right]
with

\displaystyle L_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\sqrt{a_{j}}(E_{j,j+1}-E_{j+1,j}),

(2.9)

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\sqrt{a_{j}a_{j+1}}(E_{j+2,j}-E_{j,j+2}).

(2.10)

In view of the Lax representation
L_{3}\equiv L
, we deduce that
\{Q^{[n]}\}_{n=1}^{N}=\{(-1)^{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left(L^{2n}\right)\}_{n=1}^{N}
are constants of motion for the system, or
conserved field
, i.e.
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathrm{Tr}\left(L^{2n}\right)=0
. We notice that for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}

\mathrm{Tr}\left(L^{2k+1}\right)\equiv 0
in view of the antisymmetric property of the matrix
L
, and that
2H_{1}=Q^{[1]}
.

Since also
H_{0}
is conserved, we define

Q^{[0]}=\frac{1}{2}\ln(H_{0})\,,

(2.11)

and the
local conserved fields

Q^{[n]}_{j}

j=1,\ldots,N
as

Q^{[n]}_{j}=(-1)^{n}L^{2n}_{j,j}\quad n=1,\ldots,N\,,\quad Q^{[0]}_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\ln(a_{j})\,.

(2.12)

To compute the correlation functions, we must consider the
currents
related to the locally conserved field, specifically

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}Q^{[n]}_{j}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2}\left(L^{2n}_{j,j+2}\sqrt{a_{j}a_{j+1}}-L^{2n}_{j,j-2}\sqrt{a_{j-1}a_{j-2}}\right)\,,\quad n=1,\ldots,N

(2.13)

thus defining

J_{j}^{[n]}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2}\left(L^{2n}_{j,j+2}\sqrt{a_{j}a_{j+1}}+L^{2n}_{j-1,j+1}\sqrt{a_{j-1}a_{j}}\right)\,,\quad n=1,\ldots,N

(2.14)

we can rewrite (
2.13
) as

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}Q^{[n]}_{j}=J_{j}^{[n]}-J_{j-1}^{[n]}\,,\quad n=1,\ldots,N

(2.15)

we notice that
L^{2n}_{j-1,j+1}\sqrt{a_{j-1}a_{j}}
is a boundary term, that allows us to write (
2.13
) in a compact form.

For
n=0
we can define

J_{j}^{[0]}=\frac{1}{2}(a_{j+1}+a_{j})\,,

(2.16)

and we can cast the evolution for
Q_{j}^{[0]}
as

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}Q^{[0]}_{j}=J_{j}^{[0]}-J_{j-1}^{[0]}\,.

(2.17)

Remark 2.1
.

We notice that

J_{j}^{[0]}=-\frac{1}{2}Q_{j+1}^{[1]}\,,

(2.18)

thus also
J_{j}^{[0]}
is a locally conserved field.

Analogously to the conserved fields, we define the
total current

J^{[n]}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{j}^{[n]}
for
n=1,\ldots,N
.

2.1
Generalized Gibbs Ensemble

We introduce the generalized Gibbs ensemble for the Volterra lattice (
2.1
) following
[
26
,
45
]
as

{\rm d}\mu_{\textrm{Volt}}({\bf a})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{\textrm{Volt},1}(\beta,V)}\prod_{j=1}^{2N}a_{j}^{\beta/2-1}\mathbbm{1}_{a_{j}>0}e^{\mathrm{Tr}\left(V(L({\bf a})\right)}{\rm d}{\bf a},\quad\beta>0,

(2.19)

where
V\,:\,{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a polynomial of the form
V(x)=(-1)^{\ell+1}c_{\ell}x^{2\ell}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}c_{j}x^{2j}
,
\ell\geq 1
,
c_{\ell}>0
, and

Z_{N}^{\textrm{Volt},1}(\beta,V)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}_{+}}\prod_{j=1}^{2N}a_{j}^{\beta/2-1}\mathbbm{1}_{a_{j}>0}e^{\mathrm{Tr}\left(V(L)\right)}{\rm d}{\bf a}<\infty\,.

(2.20)

We recover the standard Gibbs ensemble setting
V(x)=x^{2}/2
, in this case the variables
a_{j}
are independent and identically distributed according to a random variables with probability density function
f_{\beta}(x)

f_{\beta}(x)=\frac{x^{\beta/2-1}e^{-x}}{\Gamma(\beta/2)}\,,

(2.21)

which is just a scaled
\chi^{2}
distribution with parameter
\beta
.
In this case, the partition function can be computed explicitly:

Z_{N}^{\textrm{Volt},1}(\beta,x^{2}/2)=\Gamma(\beta/2)^{2N}\,.

(2.22)

Remark 2.2
.

We recall that one can express the conserved fields as
Q^{[k]}=(-1)^{k}\mathrm{Tr}\left(L^{2k}\right)
for
k=1,\ldots,N
and that
Q^{[0]}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\ln(a_{j})
; therefore, one can rewrite the previous GGE in the form

{\rm d}\mu_{\textrm{Volt}}({\bf a})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{\textrm{Volt},1}(\beta,V)}\exp\left(\left(\beta-2\right)Q^{[0]}-c_{\ell}Q^{[\ell]}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}(-1)^{j}c_{j}Q^{[j]}\right){\rm d}{\bf a}.

We stress that the
-2
in the previous expression comes from the volume form related to the Poisson brackets (
2.4
)

For future computations, it is useful to represent the previous expressions in terms of the variables
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{2N}
defined as
x_{j}^{2}=a_{j}
, such that
x_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\,j=1,\dots 2N
. In this new set of variables, we can express the Gibbs measure (
2.19
) and its normalization (
2.20
) as

\begin{split}&{\rm d}\mu_{\textrm{Volt}}({\bf x})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{\textrm{Volt},2}(\beta,V)}\prod_{j=1}^{2N}x_{j}^{\beta-1}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{j}>0}e^{\mathrm{Tr}\left(V(L({\bf x}^{2}))\right)}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&Z_{N}^{\textrm{Volt},2}(\beta,V)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}_{+}}\prod_{j=1}^{2N}x_{j}^{\beta-1}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{j}>0}e^{\mathrm{Tr}\left(V(L({\bf x}^{2}))\right)}{\rm d}{\bf x}<\infty\end{split},\quad\beta>0,

(2.23)

where we defined
{\bf x}^{2}=(x_{1}^{2}.\ldots,x_{2N}^{2})
. Furthermore, we notice that in the case
V(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}
, the random variables
x_{j}
are distributed as
2N
independent
\chi
-distributions, i.e. their probability density function is of the form
g_{\beta}(x)

g_{\beta}(x)=\frac{x^{\beta-1}e^{-x^{2}}}{2^{-1}\Gamma\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)}\,.

(2.24)

In this case is possible to compute the partition function as

Z_{N}^{\textrm{Volt},2}(x^{2}/2,\beta)=2^{-2N}\Gamma\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)^{2N}\,.

(2.25)

In this new coordinates, the Lax matrix
L
(
2.9
) reads as

L=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}x_{j}(E_{j,j+1}-E_{j+1,j}),

(2.26)

The
main analytic result
of this paper is the explicit computation of the susceptibility matrix
C\in\textrm{Mat}({\mathbb{R}},N+1)
and the charge-current static correlation matrix
B\in\textrm{Mat}({\mathbb{R}},N+1)
in terms of the
density of states

\sigma_{\beta,V}
of the Volterra lattice endowed with the probability distribution
{\rm d}\mu_{\textrm{Volt}}
(
2.19
). The matrices
C,B
are defined as

C_{m,n}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\text{Cov}\left({Q^{[n]}};{Q^{[m]}}\right)\,,\quad B_{n,m}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\text{Cov}\left({Q^{[n]}};{J^{[m]}}\right)\,,\quad m,n=0,\ldots,N\,,

(2.27)

where the covariance

\text{Cov}\left({f};{g}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[{fg}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[{f}\right]\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[{g}\right]\,,

\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[{\cdot}\right]
is the expected value with respect to the GGE (
2.19
) and we adopt the convention that if the quantity is evaluated at time
t=0
, we omit the time dependence. We notice that in
[
45
]
, the authors showed how to compute the correlation matrix
C
(
2.27
) for the Volterra lattice in terms of the Free energy (
3.1
).

To get an explicit expression of
C,B
, we must obtain the
density of states
of the Volterra lattice.
The density of state
\sigma_{\beta,V}
is the probability distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}
defined as the weak limit of

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\delta_{w_{j}}(x)\rightharpoonup\sigma_{\beta,V}\,,

(2.28)

where
-iw_{j}
are the eigenvalues of the Lax matrix
L
(
2.9
), and
\delta_{y}(x)
is the delta function centered at
y
.

Given these definitions, we can state the main analytical result of our paper:

Theorem 2.2
.

Consider the Lax matrix
L
(
2.9
) endowed with the GGE (
2.19
). Define the susceptibility matrix
C
and the charge-current correlation matrix
B
as in (
2.27
). Then,

\begin{split}&C_{0,0}=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\langle\sigma_{\beta,V}(\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}})^{2}\rangle\,,\\
&C_{0,n}=C_{n,0}=\kappa\langle\sigma_{\beta,V}\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}}\left(\left[w^{2n}\right]^{\textrm{dr}}-q_{n}\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}}\right)\rangle\,,\quad n=1,\ldots,N\\
&C_{m,n}=2\langle\sigma_{\beta,V}\left(\left[{w^{2m}}\right]^{\textrm{dr}}-q_{m}[1]^{dr}\right)\left(\left[{w^{2n}}\right]^{\textrm{dr}}-q_{n}[1]^{dr}\right)\rangle\,\quad m,n=1,\ldots,N\,,\\
&B_{0,n}=B_{n,0}=-\frac{1}{2}C_{n,1}\,,\quad n=0,\ldots,N\\
&B_{m,n}=-\frac{2}{\kappa}\langle\sigma_{\beta,V}(v_{\textrm{eff}}-q_{1})\left[{w^{2m}}-q_{m}\right]^{\textrm{dr}}\left[{w^{2n}}-q_{n}\right]^{\textrm{dr}}\rangle\,\end{split}

(2.29)

Here
\langle\phi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x){\rm d}x
,

\sigma_{\beta,V}=\partial_{\beta}(\beta\rho_{\beta,V})
, where the derivative is understood in week sense, and

\rho_{\beta,V}
is the minimizer of the following functional

\begin{split}{\mathcal{F}}(\beta,V)[\rho]&=-\frac{\beta}{2}\int\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{2}}\ln(|x^{2}-y^{2}|)\rho(x)\rho(y){\rm d}x{\rm d}y-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left(V(ix)+V(-ix)-\ln(x)\right)\rho(x){\rm d}x\\
&+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\ln(\rho(x))\rho(x){\rm d}x\,,\end{split}

(2.30)

here,
V(x)
is a polynomial of the form
V(x)=(-1)^{\ell+1}c_{\ell}x^{2\ell}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}c_{j}x^{2j}
,
\ell\geq 1
,
c_{\ell}>0
.
The dressing operator
\left[\psi\right]^{\textrm{dr}}
is defined as

\left[\psi\right]^{\textrm{dr}}=(1-\beta T\rho_{\beta,V})^{-1}\psi,\quad T\psi(w)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\ln(|w^{2}-z^{2}|)\psi(z){\rm d}z\,,\quad w\in{\mathbb{R}}\,.

(2.31)

q_{m}
is the
2m^{th}
moment of
\sigma_{\beta,V}
, i.e.
q_{m}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\sigma_{\beta,V}(w)w^{2m}{\rm d}w
,
v_{\textrm{eff}}=\frac{\left[w^{2}\right]^{\textrm{dr}}}{\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}}}
and

\kappa=\partial_{\beta}2{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{Volt}}(\beta,V)\,,\quad{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{Volt}}(\beta,V)=-\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\ln\left(Z_{N}^{\textrm{Volt}}(\beta,V)\right)\,.

(2.32)

Remark 2.3
.

From the explicit expression of
C,B
the two matrices are symmetric, this is obvious for
C
due to its structure, but it is not for
B
.

The explicit computation of these two matrices allows us to apply the theory of Generalized Hydrodynamics and deduce the behavior of the space-time correlation functions at the Euler scale. Specifically, we argue that defining

S^{N}_{m,n}(j,t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\text{Cov}\left({Q^{[m]}_{j}(t)};{Q^{[n]}_{0}(0)}\right)\,,

(2.33)

its approximation at the Euler scale is

\lim_{N\to\infty}S^{N}_{m,n}(j,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x=\frac{j}{2N}}}{{\simeq}}{\mathtt{S}}_{m,n}(x,t)\,,

(2.34)

where

{\mathtt{S}}_{m,n}(x,t)=\begin{cases}\vskip 10.0pt\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\langle\sigma_{\beta,V}\delta(x+t(v_{\textrm{eff}}-q_{1})(\kappa)^{-1})\left((\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}})^{2}\right)\rangle\quad m,n=0\,,\\
\vskip 10.0pt\langle\kappa\sigma_{\beta,V}\delta(x+t(v_{\textrm{eff}}-q_{1})(\kappa)^{-1})\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}}\Xi[w^{n}]\rangle\quad m=0\,,\\
\vskip 10.0pt\langle\kappa\sigma_{\beta,V}\delta(x+t(v_{\textrm{eff}}-q_{1})(\kappa)^{-1})\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}}\Xi[w^{m}]\rangle\quad n=0\,,\\
2\langle\Xi[w^{m}]\Xi[w^{n}]\sigma_{\beta,V}\delta(x+t(v_{\textrm{eff}}-q_{1})(\kappa)^{-1})\rangle\quad m,n\neq 0\,,\end{cases}\,.

(2.35)

and
\Xi\phi=\left[\phi-\langle\sigma_{\beta,V}\phi\rangle\right]^{\textrm{dr}}
.

Remark 2.4
.

We notice that in
[
10
]
the Euler scale correlation functions have a Dirac delta function of the form
\delta(x-{\mathtt{v}}_{\text{eff}}t)
, one can recover such expression by the change of coordinates
{\mathtt{v}}_{\text{eff}}=-(v_{\textrm{eff}}-q_{1})(\kappa)^{-1}
. Indeed, the two effective velocities are linearly related.

As we already mentioned
, the function
{\mathtt{S}}_{m,n}(x,t)

is not continuous
for all
(x,t)
; the two main reasons are that the density
\sigma_{\beta,V}
has support just on the positive real axis,
and that the effective velocity
v_{\textrm{eff}}(w)
has a minimum at
0
, therefore it is not one-to-one form
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Intrigued by this fact, we performed several numerical investigation to compare the numerical correlation functions and the prediction obtained from the linearized Generalized Hydrodynamic (GHD). We extensively analyze them in the last section of our paper, here we summarize our findings

•

The GHD correctly predict the
ballistic scaling
of the correlation functions; i.e.

\lim_{N\to\infty}S^{N}_{m,n}(j,t)\sim\frac{1}{t}f\left(\frac{x-{\mathtt{v}}t}{{\mathtt{c}}t}\right)

for some function
f
and constant
{\mathtt{v}},{\mathtt{c}}
.

•

The approximation
{\mathtt{S}}_{m,n}(x,t)
is not continuous for
\frac{x}{t}=\xi_{0}=\frac{q_{1}-v_{\textrm{eff}}(0)}{\kappa}

•

In a space-time neighbor of the points
(x,t)
such that
\xi_{0}=\frac{x}{t}
, i.e. where
{\mathtt{S}}_{m,n}(x,t)
is not continuous, the numerical correlation functions display an
highly oscillatory behavior
.

The combination of these facts lead us to believe that, in order to obtain a more accurate prediction, one has to consider also some diffusive effect as described in
[
50
]
. Specifically, we believe that at the point
\xi_{0}
the diffusive effects are not a sub-leading correction to the transport dynamics, but they are of the same order.
We notice, that this is not the first time that such effect has been noticed, see
[
51
,
47
,
39
,
58
]
. Nevertheless, up to our knowledge, this is the first time that such behavior is present in a classical nonlinear integrable chain at equilibrium.
As we already mentioned, in
[
48
]
the authors proposed a new approach to study this shock formation through Whitham modulation theory; it is possible that such methodology could also be applied in this context.

2.2
Idea of the proof

The proof follows the same approach developed by Spohn in the series of papers
[
54
,
53
,
55
,
56
]
. The first step is to express both the average conserved fields and the average currents in terms of the free energy of the model. To do that, we use the results in
[
45
]
. Then, we must express these quantities in terms of the density of states of the model, and we have to characterize it. We do this by linking the Generalized Gibbs ensemble of the Volterra lattice with the classical Antisymmetric beta ensemble at high temperature
[
15
,
22
]
.
Using this connection, we are able to compute explicitly both the susceptibility matrix
C
and the charge-current correlation matrix
B
.
Given these two matrices, it is possible to apply GHD to obtain an approximation of the correlation functions of the model at hand.

3.3
Density of states

Another fundamental quantity to compute the linearized correlation functions is the so-called

of the matrix

In this particular regime, the partition function

Z^{AG}_{N}\left(\frac{\beta}{N},\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)=2^{-2N}\prod_{j=1}^{2N}\Gamma\left(\frac{\beta\left(1-\frac{j}{2N}\right)}{2}\right)\,.

4.1
Density of states

The density of states
\rho_{\beta,V}
for the Anti-symmetric
\beta

. For general polynomial potential, we can characterize the density of states for this ensemble using a

(LDP)
[
8

. This is not surprising, indeed for all the
\beta

[

In our case, the LDP is a corollary of

in combination with the result of Dumitriu–Forrester
[

]
, who were able to compute explicitly the joint eigenvalue density of the Anti-symmetric Gaussian
\beta
ensemble

Theorem 4.3
.

Consider the anti-symmetric
\beta
ensemble (
4.2
), and let
iw_{j}\,j=1,\ldots,N
be the first
N

(

then the probability density function (PDF) for

\frac{1}{\mathfrak{C}_{N,{\widetilde{\beta}},V}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}^{{\widetilde{\beta}}/2-1}e^{\sum_{j=1}^{N}V(w_{j})+V(-w_{j})}\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq N}\left(w_{j}^{2}-w_{i}^{2}\right)^{{\widetilde{\beta}}}d{\bf w}\,.

(4.10)

For

, one can explicitly evaluate

\mathfrak{C}_{N,{\widetilde{\beta}},x^{2}/2}=\frac{1}{N!}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{j{\widetilde{\beta}}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{(2j-1){\widetilde{\beta}}}{4}\right)}{2\Gamma\left(1+\frac{{\widetilde{\beta}}}{2}\right)}

(4.11)

Furthermore, let
q_{j}

be the (positive) first components of the independent eigenvector corresponding to

(q_{1},\ldots,q_{N})
has a Dirichlet distribution

(here

We notice that the previous theorem is stated in

satisfying condition (

One of the key steps of the proof of Dumitriu and Forrester is the explicit computation of the Jacobian of

\prod_{j=1}^{2N}2y_{j}^{{\widetilde{\beta}}j/2-1}d{\bf y}=\left(c_{q}^{{\widetilde{\beta}}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}q_{j}^{{\widetilde{\beta}}-1}d{\bf q}\right)\left(\zeta_{N}({\widetilde{\beta}})\prod_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}^{{\widetilde{\beta}}/2-1}\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq N}\left(w_{j}^{2}-w_{i}^{2}\right)^{{\widetilde{\beta}}}d{\bf w}\right)

Where,

which normalize to

\zeta_{N}({\widetilde{\beta}})=\mathfrak{C}_{N,{\widetilde{\beta}},x^{2}/2}^{-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2N}\Gamma\left(\frac{{\widetilde{\beta}}j}{2}\right)\frac{1}{N!}\,.

(4.13)

We are interested in the high temperature regime for this ensemble,

{\widetilde{\beta}}=\beta/N
. From the previous discussion,
we deduce the following

In the same hypotheses as before, let
{\widetilde{\beta}}=\beta/N

then the probability density function (PDF) for

\frac{1}{\mathfrak{C}_{N,\beta/N,V}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}^{\frac{\beta}{2N}-1}e^{\sum_{j=1}^{N}V(iw_{j})+V(-iw_{j})}\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq N}\left(w_{j}^{2}-w_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{N}}d{\bf w}\,.

(4.14)

Furthermore, for

\mathfrak{C}_{N,\beta/N,x^{2}/2}=\frac{1}{N!}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{j\beta}{2N}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{(2j-1)\beta}{4N}\right)}{2\Gamma\left(1+\frac{\beta}{2N}\right)}

Using the previous result combined with

Consider the functional

here

is an absolutely continuous measure with respect to the Lebesgue one, has support on the positive real line. The previous functional has a unique minimizer
\rho_{\beta,V}(x)
, which is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. In particular,

is the density of states of the Anti-symmetric

5
On the way to the matrix
C
and
B

In this section, we prove our main result, i.e. Theorem
2.2
. Therefore, we show how to compute the susceptibility matrix
C
and the charge-current correlation matrix
B
.

The main idea is to enforce Proposition
4.2
and Lemma
3.2
to obtain an explicit expression of
C,B
in terms of the free energy
{\mathfrak{F}}_{\text{AG}}
of the anti-symmetric Gaussian
\beta
ensemble at high temperature, and then use Theorem
4.5
to rewrite them in terms of the density of states
\rho_{\beta,V}
of the anti-symmetric ensemble. Finally, we use the relation between
\rho_{\beta,V}
and
\sigma_{\beta,V}
, the density of states of the Volterra lattice, to conclude the proof.

From Corollary
4.2
, we know that

\partial_{\beta}(\beta{\mathfrak{F}}_{\textrm{AG}}(\beta,V))={\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{Volt}}(\beta,V)\,,

(5.1)

which combined with Theorem
4.5
gives

{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{Volt}}(\beta,V)=\partial_{\beta}\left(\frac{\beta}{2}{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{AG}}(\beta,V)[\rho_{\beta,V}]\right)+\frac{\ln(\beta)}{2}-\ln(2)\,.

(5.2)

For the following computations, it is more convenient to absorb
\beta
into the measure
\rho
by setting
\varrho=\beta\rho
, in this way we get the modified functional from
\beta{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{AG}}(\beta,V)[\beta^{-1}\varrho]={\mathcal{F}}[\varrho]-\beta\ln\left(\beta\right)
, where

{\mathcal{F}}[\varrho]=-\frac{1}{2}\int\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}}\ln(|x^{2}-y^{2}|)\varrho(x)\varrho(y){\rm d}x{\rm d}y-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(V(ix)+V(-ix)-\ln(|x|))\varrho(x){\rm d}x+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\ln(\varrho(x))\varrho(x){\rm d}x\,,

(5.3)

which has to be minimized under the condition that

\varrho\geq 0\,,\qquad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\varrho(x){\rm d}x=\beta\,.

(5.4)

We define the unique minimizer
\varrho^{\star}
;
we notice that
\varrho^{\star}=\beta\rho_{\beta,V}
. Then

{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{Volt}}(\beta,V)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\beta}{\mathcal{F}}[{\widetilde{\varrho}}^{\star}]-\ln(2)-\frac{1}{2}\,.

(5.5)

The minimizer
\varrho^{\star}
is characterized by the Euler-Lagrange equation

-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\ln(|x^{2}-y^{2}|)\varrho^{\star}(y){\rm d}y-(V(ix)+V(-ix))+\ln(|x|)+\ln(\varrho^{\star})+1-\mu(\beta,V)=0\,,

(5.6)

where
\mu(\beta,V)
is a function depending on
\beta,V
,
but not on
x
.

To obtain the free energy of the Volterra lattice, we differentiate the functional as

\begin{split}\partial_{\beta}{\mathcal{F}}[\varrho^{\star}]&=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\ln(|x^{2}-y^{2}|)\partial_{\beta}\varrho^{\star}(x)\varrho^{\star}(y){\rm d}x{\rm d}y-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}(V(ix)+V(-ix)-\ln(|x|))\partial_{\beta}\varrho^{\star}(x){\rm d}x\\
&+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\ln(\varrho^{\star})\partial_{\beta}\varrho^{\star}(x)+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\partial_{\beta}\varrho^{\star}(x)\\
&=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\ln(|x^{2}-y^{2}|)\partial_{\beta}\varrho^{\star}(x)\varrho^{\star}(y){\rm d}x{\rm d}y-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}(V(ix)+V(-ix)-\ln(|x|))\partial_{\beta}\varrho^{\star}(x){\rm d}x\\
&+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\ln(\varrho^{\star})\partial_{\beta}\varrho^{\star}(x)+1\,.\end{split}

(5.7)

By testing (
5.6
) against
\partial_{\beta}\varrho^{\star}
we deduce that

\partial_{\beta}{\mathcal{F}}[\varrho^{\star}]=\mu(\beta,V)\,,

(5.8)

which implies that

{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{Volt}}(\beta,V)=\frac{\mu(\beta,V)}{2}-\ln(2)-\frac{1}{2}\,.

(5.9)

Consider now the following chain of equality

\partial_{\mu}\varrho^{\star}=\partial_{\mu}(\beta\rho_{\beta,V})=\partial_{\beta}(\beta\rho_{\beta,V})(\partial_{\beta}\mu)^{-1}=\sigma_{\beta,V}\kappa^{-1}\,,

(5.10)

where we defined

\kappa=2\partial_{\beta}{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{Volt}}(\beta,V)=-\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[{\ln(a_{1})}\right].

Following Spohn, we define a new measure
\sigma=\sigma_{\beta,V}\kappa^{-1}
, and we notice that
\left\langle\sigma\right\rangle=\kappa^{-1}
.

The measure
\sigma,\varrho^{\star}
play a crucial role in the computation of the matrices
B,C
. To simplify the notation we drop the upper index
\star
from
\varrho^{\star}
. Before proceeding with the computation of such matrices, we have to introduce the following operator

T\psi(w)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\ln(|w^{2}-z^{2}|)\psi(z){\rm d}z\,\quad w\in{\mathbb{R}}\,,

(5.11)

which is related to the scattering shift of the Volterra lattice.
Using this operator, we can introduce the
dressing of a function
\psi

\left[\psi\right]^{\textrm{dr}}=\psi+T\varrho\left[\psi\right]^{\textrm{dr}}\,,\quad\left[\psi\right]^{\textrm{dr}}=(1-T\varrho)^{-1}\psi\,,

(5.12)

here
\varrho
is just a multiplicative operator. We notice that the dressing of any real function according to (
5.12
) is
even
,
therefore if the function is differentiable at
0
, then the dressed function has a stationary point at
0
. Furthermore, by differentiating the Euler-Lagrange equation with respect to
\mu
we deduce the following chain of equality

\sigma=\left(1-\varrho T\right)^{-1}\varrho=\varrho\left(1-T\varrho\right)^{-1}[1]=\varrho\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}}\,,

(5.13)

where we used the fact that

5.1
The matrix

The aim of this section is to compute the correlation matrix

(5.21)

We start with
C_{0,0}

\begin{split}C_{0,0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Cor}\ref{cor:mean_cor_gibbs}}}{{=}}&-\partial_{\beta}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{Volt}}(\beta,V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:free_energy_rel}}}{{=}}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\beta}^{2}\mu(\beta,V)=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\beta}\kappa\\

Next we consider
C_{0,n}=C_{n,0}

3.1

\begin{split}C_{0,n}&=\partial_{\beta}\langle\kappa\sigma w^{2n}\rangle=(\partial_{\beta}\kappa)\langle\sigma w^{2n}\rangle+\kappa\partial_{\beta}\langle\sigma w^{2n}\rangle\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:c00}-\eqref{eq:prop4}}}{{=}}-\kappa^{3}\langle\sigma(\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}})^{2}\rangle\langle\sigma w^{2n}\rangle+\kappa^{2}\langle\sigma\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}}\left[w^{2n}\right]^{\textrm{dr}}\rangle\\

(5.23)

Finally, we have to compute

3.1

We have

\begin{split}{\mathcal{F}}[\varrho]&=-\frac{1}{2}\int\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{2}}\ln(|w^{2}-\lambda^{2}|)\varrho(w)\varrho(\lambda){\rm d}w{\rm d}\lambda+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(-V(iw)-V(-iw)+2it_{n}w^{2n})\varrho(w)\\

\begin{split}\dfrac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\varrho}&=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\ln(|w^{2}-\lambda^{2}|)\varrho(\lambda){\rm d}\lambda-(V(iw)+V(-iw))+2it_{n}w^{2n}+\ln(|w|)+\ln(\varrho)+1-\mu(\beta,V)\\

therefore

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t_{n}}\sigma(V+t_{n}w^{2n}){w^{2m}}\rangle

5.2
The matrix
B

The matrix
B
is the matrix of static covariance between the conserved fields and currents, which is defined as

B_{n,m}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\text{Cov}\left({Q^{[n]}};{J^{[m]}}\right)\,.

(5.32)

A priori, this matrix is not symmetric, but, as we show in this section, it is.

First, we start by computing
B_{n,0}=\lim_{N\to\infty}(2N)^{-1}\text{Cov}\left({Q^{[n]}};{J^{[0]}}\right)
. From Remark
2.1
, we deduce that

(5.33)

To compute the remaining part of the matrix

\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[{J_{0}^{[n]}}\right]
(
3.3
) using our new notation

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[{J_{0}^{[n]}}\right]&=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}\partial^{2}_{t_{1},t_{2}}{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{Volt}}(y,V-it_{1}x^{2}-it_{2}x^{2n}){\rm d}y\\
&=-\frac{1}{4}\beta\partial_{t_{1}}\partial_{t_{2}}{\mathfrak{F}}_{\textrm{AG}}(\beta,V-it_{1}x^{2}-it_{2}x^{2n})=-\frac{1}{2}i\partial_{t_{n}}\langle\varrho(V-it_{n}w^{2n}){w^{2}}\rangle\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:time_der}}}{{=}}\langle\varrho\left[{w^{2}}\right]^{\textrm{dr}}({w^{2n}}-q_{n})\rangle\end{split}\,.

We can now compute

\begin{split}B_{n,m}&=-i\partial_{t_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[{J_{0}^{[m]}}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:my_current}}}{{=}}-i\partial_{t_{n}}\langle\varrho(\left[{w^{2}}\right]^{\textrm{dr}}-q_{1}\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}})({w^{2m}}-q_{m})\rangle\\

(5.37)

6
Linearized Hydrodynamics

In this section, we compute the correlation functions of the Volterra lattice using the theory of Generalized Hydrodynamics. We start by computing the Euler equation for the density. We start from the continuity equation

\partial_{t}Q_{j}^{[n]}=-\partial_{x}J_{j}^{[n]}\,,

(6.1)

which, by averaging on a GGE with slowly varying parameters, become

\partial_{t}\langle Q_{j}^{[n]}\rangle_{\epsilon}=-\partial_{x}\langle J_{j}^{[n]}\rangle_{\epsilon}\,,

(6.2)

where by
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\epsilon}
we denote the parameter with slow variation, and by
\partial_{x}\cdot
the discrete spatial derivative.

After a normalization procedure, which still needs some rigorous justification, the previous equations imply that the density
\sigma
and the normalization
\kappa
evolve according to the following system of quasi-linear equations

\partial_{t}\kappa=\partial_{x}q_{1}\,,\qquad\partial_{t}(\kappa\sigma)+\partial_{x}((v_{\textrm{eff}}-q_{1})\sigma)=0\,,

(6.3)

at Euler scale.

As in the case of Toda lattice, the previous equations can be put in linear form by the following change of coordinates

\varrho=\sigma(1+T\sigma)^{-1}[1]\,,

(6.4)

in this new variable the equations read (
6.3
)

\kappa\partial_{t}\varrho+(v_{\textrm{eff}}-q_{1})\partial_{x}\varrho=0\,,

(6.5)

the proof is analogous to the one in
[
53
]
.
From the previous expression, we notice that the solution of this system of equations can develop some shock, indeed if the velocity
v_{\textrm{eff}}(w)-q_{1}<0
the corresponding wave will encouter some sort of obstacle at
x=0
, since the density is defined just for positive
x
.

We notice that this behavior is an effect of the linearization procedure. We expect that if we would to consider a more accurate description of the model by making a second order average approximation of equation (
6.1
), i.e. adding a damping term in the form of a Drude weight, the evolution would be smooth and the shock disappear. For a more general discussion, we refer to
[
56
, Chapter 12]
and
[
50
]
.

Despite that, we can still apply the theory of GHD (Landau-Lifshitz theory) to describe the correlation functions. For a general introduction see
[
56
, Chapter 7]
. The structure of the matrices
B,C
is the same as in
[
53
,
56
]
, thus following the exact same reasoning,
we can guess the linear correlation functions has the following operator form

{\mathtt{S}}(x,t)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\kappa^{3}}{2}\langle\sigma\delta(x+t(v_{\textrm{eff}}-q_{1})\kappa^{-1})\left((\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}})^{2}\right)\rangle&\kappa\langle\Xi^{*}\kappa\sigma\delta(x+t(v_{\textrm{eff}}-q_{1})\kappa^{-1})\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}}|\\
\kappa|\Xi\kappa\sigma\delta(x+t(v_{\textrm{eff}}-q_{1})\kappa^{-1})\left[1\right]^{\textrm{dr}}\rangle&2\Xi^{*}\sigma\kappa\delta(x+t(v_{\textrm{eff}}-q_{1})\kappa^{-1})\Xi\end{pmatrix}\,.

(6.6)

As we already noticed, these equations develops shock. Here this effect is clearer in view of the structure of the effective velocity
v_{\textrm{eff}}(w)
, see Figure
2
. Indeed, by looking at the collision rate ansatz (
5.41
), one immediately deduces that the effective velocity is an even function, and it is not singular, thus it is not a one to one transform of
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
into
{\mathbb{R}}
. So equations (
6.6
) are not continuous for some values of
\frac{x}{t}=\xi_{0}
that we can compute explicitly as

\xi_{0}=-\frac{v_{\textrm{eff}}(0)-q_{1}}{\kappa}\,.

Intrigued by this behavior, we performed several numerical simulations to understand to which extent the linear approximation captures the behavior of the correlation functions.

8
Conclusions and Outlooks

In this paper, we computed the GHD approximation of the correlation functions for the Volterra lattice. We were able to do so by connecting the GGE of the Volterra lattice with the classical Anti-symmetric Gaussian
\beta
ensemble. Given the explicit expression of the GHD prediction, we noticed that it has a discontinuity for
\frac{x}{t}=\xi_{0}
, see (
2.35
). This shock is due to the fact that the effective velocity
v_{\textrm{eff}}
defined by the collision rate ansatz, see Remark
5.1
, is an even smooth function, therefore it has a stationary point at
0
, which, in this case, is also a minimum. It would be interesting to understand if this point has some deep physical interpretation. This particular feature was already observed in
[
58
]
for quantum systems in the context of density ripples. We mention that in
[
48
]
, the authors describe a similar phenomena using Whitham modulation theory, and it might be possible that one can apply the same idea in this context.

Furthermore, we compared our findings with several numerical experiment, see Figure
1
. We notice that the numerical correlation functions and the GHD prediction line up away from the discontinuity point
\xi_{0}
, but around it the actual correlations are highly oscillatory. In order to be able to describe such region analytically, we think that it would be interesting to conduct some other experiment focusing on the
t
-dependence of the shock region.

Since the Volterra lattice is also the discretization of the KdV equation, it could be interesting to understand if one can recover the soliton gas description of the KdV obtained in
[
4
]
using the result in our paper.

A.1
Proof of Lemma
3.2

For the sake of the reader, we report the statement of the Lemma

Lemma A.1
.

Consider the Volterra lattice (
2.1
) endowed with the GGE (

To prove this lemma, we need a corollary of result from

Given a differentiable function

as the set

{\rm supp}\,F:=\left\{\ell\in\{1,\ldots,2N\}\colon\ \ \ \frac{\partial F}{\partial a_{\ell}}\nequiv 0\right\}

(A.2)

and its
diameter
as

{\rm diam}\left({\rm supp}\,F\right):=\sup_{i,j\in{\rm supp}\,F}{\mathtt{d}}_{2N}(i,j)+1,

(A.3)

where
{\mathtt{d}}_{k(i,j)}
is the
periodic distance

{\mathtt{d}}_{k(i,j)}:=\min\left(|i-j|,\ k-|i-j|\right).

(A.4)

Note that
0\leq{\mathtt{d}}_{2N}(i,j)\leq N
.

We say that a function
F
is
local
if
{\rm diam}\left({\rm supp}\,F\right)
is uniformly bounded in
N
, i.e. there exists a constant
{\mathtt{c}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
{\rm diam}\left({\rm supp}\,F\right)\leq{\mathtt{c}}
, and
{\mathtt{c}}
is independent of
N
.

Another important class of functions that we consider are the so-called
cyclic
functions, which are a class of function invariant under left or right shift of the variables. More specifically, for any
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, and
{\bf x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{2N})\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}_{+}
we define the
cyclic shift of order
\ell
as the map

S_{\ell}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2N},\qquad(S_{\ell}x)_{j}:=x_{((j+\ell-1)\mod 2N)+1}.

(A.5)

For example
S_{1}
and
S_{-1}
are the left respectively right shifts:

S_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{2N}):=(x_{2},\ldots,x_{2N},x_{1}),\qquad S_{-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{2N}):=(x_{2N},x_{1},\ldots,x_{2N-1}).

One can immediately check that for any
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
:

S_{\ell}\circ S_{\ell^{\prime}}=S_{\ell+\ell^{\prime}},\qquad S_{\ell}^{-1}=S_{-\ell},\qquad S_{0}=\mathds{1},\qquad S_{\ell+2N}=S_{\ell}.

(A.6)

Consider now a function
H\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}}
; we denote by
S_{\ell}H\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}}
the function

(S_{\ell}H)({\bf a}):=H(S_{\ell}{\bf a}),\qquad\forall{\bf a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}_{+}\,.

(A.7)

Clearly
S_{\ell}
is a linear operator.
We can now define cyclic functions:

Definition A.1

(Cyclic functions)
.

A function
H\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is called
cyclic
if
S_{1}H=H
.

It is easy to construct cyclic functions as follows: given a function
h\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}}
we define the new function
H
by

H({\bf a}):=\sum_{\ell=0}^{2N-1}(S_{\ell}h)({\bf a}).

(A.8)

H
is clearly cyclic and we say that
H
is
generated
by
h
, we remark that these definition were introduced in this context in
[
24
,
28
]
.

Remark A.1
.

According to the previous definition, the conserved field
Q^{[n]}
and the currents
J^{[n]}
of the Volterra lattice are cyclic; furthermore, their seed are local functions. Given these properties, we call these seeds
Q^{[n]}_{j}

local conserved fields
and
J^{[n]}_{j}
local currents
.

Given these definitions, we can state the following corollary:

Corollary A.2

(Decay of correlations)
.

Consider the Volterra lattice (
2.1
) endowed with the GGE (
2.19
), and let
I,J:{\mathbb{R}}^{2N}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be two local functions with the same support of diameter
k
. Assume that they are integrable with respect to the GGE (
2.19
). Write
2N=kM+\ell
, and let
j\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
. Then, there exists some
0<\mu<1
such that

{\mathbb{E}}_{1}\left[I({\bf a})S_{j}J({\bf a})\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{1}\left[I({\bf a})\right]{\mathbb{E}}_{1}\left[S_{j}J({\bf a})\right]=O(\mu^{{\mathtt{d}}_{M}(j,0)})\,.

With the previous corollary, we can prove Lemma
3.2

.

(A.9)

(A.11)

A.3
Proof of Theorem
4.5

For reader convenience, we report the statement of the Theorem.

Theorem A.4
.

Consider the functional
{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{AG}}(\beta,V)[\rho]
defined as

\begin{split}{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{AG}}(\beta,V)[\rho]&=-\frac{\beta}{2}\int\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{2}}\ln(|x^{2}-y^{2}|)\rho(x)\rho(y){\rm d}x{\rm d}y-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(V(ix)+V(-ix)+\ln(|x|))\rho(x){\rm d}x\\
&+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\ln(\rho(|x|))\rho(|x|){\rm d}x\end{split}

(A.31)

here
\rho(x)
is an absolutely continuous measure with respect to the Lebesgue one, has support on the positive real line. The previous functional has a unique minimizer
\rho_{\beta,V}(x)
, which is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. In particular,
\rho_{\beta,V}(x)
is the density of states of the Anti-symmetric
\beta
ensemble in the high temperature regime. Furthermore,

{\mathfrak{F}}_{\textrm{AG}}(\beta,V)=\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{AG}}(\beta,V)[\rho_{\beta,V}]+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\ln\left(\frac{\beta}{2}x\right)dx-\frac{\ln(2)}{2}\,.

(A.32)

Proof.

First from the definition of Free energy and (
4.12
) we deduce that

\begin{split}{\mathfrak{F}}_{\textrm{AG}}(\beta,V)&=-\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\ln\left(Z_{N}^{AG}(\beta/N,V)\right)\\
&=-\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\ln\left(\zeta_{N}\left(\frac{\beta}{N}\right)\right)\\
&-\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\ln\left(\int_{w_{1}<w_{2}<\ldots<w_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}^{\frac{\beta}{2N}-1}e^{\sum_{j=1}^{N}V(iw_{j})+V(-iw_{j})}\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq N}\left(w_{j}^{2}-w_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{N}}d{\bf w}\right)\,.\end{split}

(A.33)

The first term can be explicitly computed as

-\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\ln\left(\zeta_{N}\left(\frac{\beta}{N}\right)\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:zeta}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\ln\left(\frac{\beta}{2}x\right)dx-\ln(2)\right)\,.

(A.34)

For the second term, we can apply theorem
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
, to deduce that

\begin{split}-\lim_{N\to\infty}&\frac{1}{2N}\ln\left(\int_{w_{1}<w_{2}<\ldots<w_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}^{\frac{\beta}{2N}-1}e^{\sum_{j=1}^{N}V(iw_{j})+V(-iw_{j})}\prod_{1\leq j<i\leq N}\left(w_{j}^{2}-w_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{N}}d{\bf w}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\min_{{\widetilde{\rho}}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}{\mathcal{F}}_{\textrm{AG}}(\beta,V)[{\widetilde{\rho}}]\,,\end{split}

(A.35)

where
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
is the space of probability measure with support on the positive real line and

\begin{split}{\widetilde{{\mathcal{F}}}}_{\textrm{AG}}(\beta,V)[{\widetilde{\rho}}]&=-\frac{\beta}{2}\int\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{2}}\ln(|x^{2}-y^{2}|)\rho(x)\rho(y){\rm d}x{\rm d}y-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(V(ix)+V(-ix)-\ln(x))\rho(x){\rm d}x\\
&+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\ln(\rho(x))\rho(x){\rm d}x\,.\end{split}

(A.36)

Combining the previous expression with (
A.34
), we deduce the claim.
∎
