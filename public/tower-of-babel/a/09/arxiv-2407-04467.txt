Are Large Language Models Strategic Decision Makers? A Study of Performance and Bias in Two-Player Non-Zero-Sum Games
Nathan Herr, Fernando Acero, Roberta Raileanu, María Pérez-Ortiz, Zhibin Li

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have been increasingly used in real-world settings, yet their strategic decision-making abilities remain largely unexplored. To fully benefit from the potential of LLMs, it’s essential to understand their ability to function in complex social scenarios. Game theory, which is already used to understand real-world interactions, provides a good framework for assessing these abilities. This work investigates the performance and merits of LLMs in canonical game-theoretic two-player non-zero-sum games, Stag Hunt and Prisoner Dilemma. Our structured evaluation of GPT-3.5, GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-4o, and Llama-3-8B shows that these models, when making decisions in these games, are affected by at least one of the following systematic biases: positional bias, payoff bias, or behavioural bias. This indicates that LLMs do not fully rely on logical reasoning when making these strategic decisions. As a result, it was found that the LLMs’ performance drops when the game configuration is misaligned with the affecting biases. When misaligned, GPT-3.5, GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-4o, and Llama-3-8B show an average performance drop of 32%, 25%, 34%, and 29% respectively in Stag Hunt, and 28%, 16%, 34%, and 24% respectively in Prisoner’s Dilemma. Surprisingly, GPT-4o (a top-performing LLM across standard benchmarks) suffers the most substantial performance drop, suggesting that newer models are not addressing these issues. Interestingly, we found that a commonly used method of improving the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, reduces the biases in GPT-3.5, GPT-4o, and Llama-3-8B but increases the effect of the bias in GPT-4-Turbo, indicating that CoT alone cannot fully serve as a robust solution to this problem. We perform several additional experiments, which provide further insight into these observed behaviours.



1
Introduction

An LLM is prompted to select the best action in both
Configuration 1
and
Configuration 2
, where the order of the actions is reversed.
Despite the same underlying task in both configurations, the LLM’s reasoning differs, revealing that the model relies more on the order of the actions rather than their intrinsic meaning during the decision-making process. More examples can be found in Section
C
of the Technical Appendix.

The use of Large Language Models (LLMs) to solve everyday tasks has become increasingly widespread
(
Naveed et al. 2023
;
Hadi et al. 2023
;
Duan et al. 2024
)
. As LLMs are deployed in the real world, interacting with other humans or artificial agents, there is a need to understand their capabilities to operate in complex social scenarios. To this end, this study chose game theory, which is already applied to many existing real-world tasks
(
Martin 2017
)
in fields such as economics, psychology, and sociology
(
Fan et al. 2024
;
Duan et al. 2024
)
, to investigate the decision-making capabilities of LLMs.

Game theory
(
Roughgarden 2010
)
abstracts complex real-life scenarios as mathematical models designed to be easy to understand but require strategic decision-making abilities to be mastered. A sub-genre of game-theoretic games is two-player non-zero-sum games which have both competitive and cooperative elements. This provides a fair representation of agent interactions for many important real-world scenarios. LLMs have been shown to reach levels of reasoning comparable to humans, demonstrating their ability to solve such real-world tasks
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
. These accomplishments are primarily achieved using prompts that are specifically curated for the task at hand, referred to as prompt engineering
(
Sahoo et al. 2024
)
.

The majority of techniques make use of prompt engineering, which has shown promise but is often not robust
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
. This brittleness has been discovered and investigated in many state-of-the-art (SOTA) LLMs
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Papadatos and Freedman 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Zheng et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
such as LLama-2, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4, primarily in multiple-choice question answering and similar tasks. Specifically, these works show that LLMs suffer from what we refer to as systematic biases, which guide the LLMs’ decisions and not their strategic reasoning. Far fewer studies have examined systematic biases within the context of game theory, an important field where such investigations are necessary to fully benefit from the use of LLMs in real-world tasks.

To date, there is a lack of research on the systematic biases of LLMs when solving game-theoretic tasks, which has motivated this work. Our objectives are to (1) investigate the systematic biases in several SOTA LLMs during two-player zero-sum games and analyze how these biases vary between models, (2) demonstrate the statistical evaluations of these biases within each model and assess their impact on performance, and (3) conduct further study to investigate and understand these behaviours, including: (i) exploring the impact of fine-tuning on these biases, (ii) testing how prior knowledge affects LLM biases, (iii) evaluating how different prompt structures influence observed biases, and (iv) comparing the behaviours of humans and LLMs in a two-player zero-sum game.

2
Related Work

Having established the importance of investigating how systematic biases affect LLMs’ ability to make strategic decisions in two-player non-zero-sum games, we now explore existing related work.

LLMs and Game Theory.

In recent years, the use of LLMs as single-agent planners/decision makers has evolved into LLM-based multi-agent systems
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
where agents are required to solve strategic and logical reasoning problems. These capabilities are often evaluated through game-theoretic tasks
(
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Lorè and Heydari 2023
;
Gandhi, Sadigh, and Goodman 2023
)
. This shift has prompted many new benchmarks testing LLMs in game theoretic tasks with the aim of progressing the work within the field
(
Xu et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Duan et al. 2024
;
Li, Li, and Liu 2023
;
Aher, Arriaga, and Kalai 2023
)
. Furthermore, several existing works focus on game theoretic matrix games, such as Prisoner’s Dilemma, Stag Hunt, and Dictator Game, to name a few
(
Fan et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Lorè and Heydari 2023
;
Brookins and DeBacker 2023
;
Gandhi, Sadigh, and Goodman 2023
;
Phelps and Russell 2023
;
Guo 2023
)
, which are discussed below. In
Fan et al. 2024
’s work they show that LLMs, even when explicitly given the correct belief, from which they should reason to take correct action, tend to ignore or modify this belief. They also note that LLMs tend to select specific action labels more frequently than others (they note that GPT-3 prefers U to V), but do not investigate this any further.
Xu et al. 2023
and
Brookins and DeBacker 2023
show that LLMs tend to select the cooperative action more frequently than humans, despite it not being the optimal choice in most cases. Lastly, several works test how the LLMs’ behaviour changes as they modify the LLMs’ preferences or contextual frameworks, such as being selfish or cooperative
(
Fan et al. 2024
;
Phelps and Russell 2023
;
Guo 2023
;
Lorè and Heydari 2023
)
. They all note that LLMs are seemingly capable of following simple preferences, such as selecting the selfish action when prompted to be selfish. However, they do not investigate the effect the chosen prompt configuration has on the LLMs’ performance.

Bias in LLMs.

The presence of systemic biases (the term “bias” denotes the consistent deviation within LLMs
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
, such as token bias, which favours a specific token U over token V
(
Fan et al. 2024
)
) has recently become a topic of interest. Specifically, these biases are found and tested in multiple choice question evaluation
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
, multi-turn question answer evaluation
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
, response quality evaluation
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, and tasks such as text classification, fact retrieval, and information extraction
(
Zhao et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Berglund et al. 2023
;
Golovneva et al. 2024
)
. It was found that LLMs suffer from what is referred to as selection bias
(
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Zheng et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2021
)
, which is a combination of both token bias and positional bias. Prior works have also studied other types of biases when using LLMs to judge the quality of LLM generations. For example,
Zheng et al. 2024
discovers LLMs have a verbosity bias, favouring longer responses over shorter ones. Similarly,
Zheng et al. 2024
found that LLMs have a self-enhancement bias, favouring responses generated by the judge LLM relative to other LLMs. These are less relevant to our setting since we don’t focus on LLMs that judge other LLMs’ outputs.

3
Methodology

Two-Player Non-Zero-Sum games

Prisoner’s Dilemma is one of the most popular two-player non-zero-sum games used by researchers in both scientific literature and real-world settings to study complex social interactions
(
Gilbert Jr 1996
;
Pacheco et al. 2009
;
Mantas et al. 2022
)
. Stag Hunt is frequently compared to Prisoner’s Dilemma
(
Pacheco et al. 2009
)
because of its similar social dynamics and the differences in their Nash Equilibria
(
Chatterjee 2004
)
. This work, therefore, focuses on these canonical two-player non-zero-sum games. A brief description of each game is provided below with a detailed explanation of the games in Section
A
of the Technical Appendix.

Stag Hunt.

A scenario involving two hunters who can either hunt a stag together or each hunt a hare individually
(
Skyrms 2001
)
. They must decide their actions without communicating, based on their expectations of whether the other hunter will cooperate to hunt the stag or act independently to hunt the hare.

Prisoner’s Dilemma.

A scenario where two prisoners must choose to confess or remain silent
(
Tucker and Straffin Jr 1983
)
. If both confess, they each get 2 years in prison. If both remain silent, they each get 1 year. If one confesses and the other remains silent, the confessor goes free, and the silent one gets 3 years.

Game and Player Descriptions.

Both games are described using the same prompt skeleton (detailed in Section
B
of the Technical Appendix). The prompt makes use of action labels A and B to describe the payoff matrix for the specific games. For example, in Prisoner’s Dilemma, the text
“if you select action label A and they select action label B, you receive 0 points while they receive 3 points”
describes a scenario where you, the Acting Player (AP), selects to cooperate while they, the Fellow Player (FP), selects to defect. Note that neither the terms, cooperate nor defect are used in the prompt. The reason for this is to promote reasoning over the payoff matrix and not rely on the LLMs’ prior knowledge of the games. To this effect, the names of each game are not explicitly mentioned in the prompt and are only identifiable by their payoff matrices. In addition to the description of the game, we include
“player preferences”
, where the AP is prompted with the preferred behaviours (players either prioritise the common-gain or their self-gain) of both players, such that we may test how the LLMs’ behaviour changes as we modify the preferred behaviours, much like the work done in
(
Fan et al. 2024
;
Phelps and Russell 2023
;
Guo 2023
;
Lorè and Heydari 2023
)
.

LLM Biases

There are 3 ways in which one can change the prompt, used to describe the games, without changing the skeleton itself: (1) the order in which the action labels are presented in the prompt, (2) the payoffs associated with the action labels, and (3) the prompted preferred behaviours of the players. Given this, we focus our investigation on the biases related to these changes. The details of which are discussed below.

Positional Bias.

Changing the order in which the action labels are stated in the prompt affects the frequency of the selected action label. For example, Llama-3-8B, when prompted with action label A first and B second, tends to select the first action label A more frequently.

Payoff Bias.

Changing the payoffs associated with the different action labels, PayOff(label_1, label_2), affects the frequency of the selected action label. In particular, a model may be biased towards, (1) selecting the action that leads to the maximum possible self-gain or (2) selecting the action that leads to the maximum possible common-gain, rather than the action that maximizes the expected gain (which takes into account all possible actions the other agent can take). Note that in Stag Hunt these two actions are the same and for Prisoner’s Dilemma they are not (details on this can be found in Table
4
in Section
A
of the Technical Appendix). For example, GPT-4-Turbo in Prisoner’s Dilemma, when PayOff(A, A)=2, PayOff(A, B)=0, PayOff(B, A)=3, and PayOff(B, B)=1, tends to select action label A (the action associated with the maximum possible common-gain). Another example, Llama-3-8B in Prisoner’s Dilemma, with the same payoffs, tends to select action label B (the action associated with the maximum possible self-gain).

Behavioural Bias.

Changing the preferred behaviour of the Acting Player (AP) and Fellow Player (FP) affects the frequency of the selected action. For example, when GPT-3.5 (AP) is prompted to prioritise Common-Gain (CG) it tends to select action label A, irrespective of the FP’s preferred behaviour, and when prompted to prioritise Self-Gain (SG), tends to select action label B.

Experimental Setup

To perform this investigation, we methodically adjust the base prompt (detailed in Section
B
of the Technical Appendix) over all combinations of positions, payoffs, and behaviours, making up 16 different experimental setups. Additionally, each of these experimental setups is run with and without prompting the LLM to first reason over the problem. To do this, we use the following prompting schemes; (1) Answer-Only (AO) prompt, which requires the LLM to respond only with their answer without any reasoning, and (2) Zero-shot Chain-of-Though (CoT) prompt
(
Kojima et al. 2022
)
(the full prompts can be found in Section
B
of the Technical Appendix). All experiments discussed are applied to both Prisoner’s Dilemma and Stag Hunt.
We run all experiments on 4 LLMs (spanning both open-source and proprietary models of different sizes):
GPT-3.5
,
GPT-4-Turbo
,
GPT-4o
, and
Llama-3-8B
.

Statistical Analysis.

We test the statistical significance of all the biases. We run each experimental setup 100 times, from which we build the contingency tables required to perform the Fisher Exact Test
(
Kim 2017
)
(further details on the Fisher Exact Test can be found in Section
A
of the Technical Appendix).

Alignment Analysis.

Using the generated tables, we perform what we refer to as an alignment analysis. We compare the performance of the LLM when the experimental setup is aligned and misaligned with the LLMs’ bias. Performance is assessed based on the selection of the best action given the preferred behaviours of both the acting player and fellow player (the combinations of preferred behaviours and their respective correct actions can be found in Section
B
of the Technical Appendix).

Main Results

We tested a total of 4 LLMs under a total of 16 different prompt configurations (where we varied the position, payoff, and preferred behaviours in the prompt). Furthermore, we run each prompt configuration under 2 different prompting schemes, AO and CoT. The frequency tables for each bias are shown in Tables
1
,
2
, and
3
. The results of the statistical analysis can be found in Figure
1
and the alignment analyst results in Figure
2
. In the following paragraphs, we discuss these results in depth.

Positional Bias.

In Figure
1
, we can see that the positional bias is particularly
strong in GPT-3.5
when using the AO prompt. It becomes significantly weaker when asked to reason over the task first when using the CoT prompt.
GPT-4-Turbo
, on the other hand, shows an
overall much weaker
bias towards the position of the action labels, for both prompting methods. Interestingly, the newer
GPT-4o regresses
and shows a
strong
positional bias under both prompting methods. Lastly,
Llama-3-8B
, much like GPT-3.5, shows a
strong positional bias
under AO prompting and a much weaker bias under CoT prompting. More specifically, in Table
1
, we see that GPT-3.5 tends to select the action in the first position more frequently. Conversely, GPT-4-Turbo and GPT-4o tend to select the action in the second position more frequently. Llama-3-8B, under AO prompting, selects the first position more frequently and the second position under CoT prompting.

Payoff Bias.

In Figure
1
, we can see that both
GPT-3.5 and Llama-3-8B
show either a
very weak or insignificant
payoff bias for both prompting methods. Interestingly, both models tend to (1) maximise the common-gain in Stag Hunt and (2) maximise the self-gain in Prisoner’s Dilemma. This can be seen in Table
2
. Looking at Figure
1
again, we see that the payoff bias is
strong in both GPT-4-Turbo and GPT-4o
. It is the strongest bias in GPT-4-Turbo for both prompting methods in both games, whereas for GPT-4o the payoff bias is weaker under CoT prompting in Prisoner’s Dilemma. Presumably, the reason for this is that the maximum possible self-gain and common-gain in Prisoner’s Dilemma is less than in Stag Hunt and is, therefore, a weaker signal. In table
2
(and Table
8
in Section
C
of the Technical Appendix) we note that both GPT-4-Turbo and GPT-4o tend to maximise the common-gain more frequently. While in previous works, this behaviour has led to conclusions such as “LLMs have a propensity to be cooperative”
(
Xu et al. 2023
;
Brookins and DeBacker 2023
)
, our results suggest that this phenomenon is not indicative of “cooperative behaviour” and instead is a result of a skewed attention towards the action that leads to maximum possible gains, despite it not always being the optimal choice.

Behavioural Bias.

In Figure
1
, we can see that both
GPT-4-Turbo and GPT-4o
are
weakly affected
by the behavioural bias, whereas, both
GPT-3.5 and Llama-3-8B
are
strongly affected
. Specifically, GPT-3.5 is weakly biased when using the AO prompt and strongly biased when using the CoT prompt. Llama-3-8B shows a different pattern where it is strongly biased when using the AO prompt and weakly biased when using the CoT prompt. However, in Prisoner’s Dilemma, it is still the strongest bias under the CoT prompt. In Table
3
, we observed that GPT-3.5 tends to select action label A when prompted to prioritise Common-Gain and action label B when prompted to prioritise Self-Gain. Again, Llama-3-8B shows a different pattern, where it tends to select action label A when prompted to prioritise Self-Gain and action label B when prompted to prioritise Common-Gain.

To further understand the behaviour observed in the LLMs, we perform a set of additional experiments. The findings are briefly discussed below, while more details can be found in Section
D
of the Technical Appendix.

Effect of Fine-Tuning

We ran a set of experiments using LLama-3-8b (w/o Fine-Tuning) and LLama-3-8b-Instruct (w/ Fine-Tuning) to investigate the effect instruction-fine-tuning has on the identified biases. In Figure
3
we see that LLama-3-8b was primarily guided by the order in which the action labels were presented in the prompt, whereas LLama-3-8b-Instruct is equally guided by both the order and prompted preferred behaviours (this result can be observed in Figure
8
as well). However, the overall effect of the biases is weakened when the LLM is fine-tuned. Our results show that fine-tuning LLMs reduces the average significance of the biases, however, they also show that fine-tuning increases the behavioural bias in LLama-3-8b (where it was null to begin with), indicating fine-tuning may not be a sufficient remedy for this issue.

Effect of Prior Knowledge

To investigate the effect an LLM’s prior knowledge has on the identified biases, we alter the base prompt used in the main set of experiments to include the names of the games (referred to as the leaked prompt) i.e. Prisoner’s Dilemma and Stag Hunt. In Figure
4
we see that when prompting GPT-4o with the names of the games in the prompt, the effect of the biases weakens (since the performance drop when misaligned is less). This result can also be observed in Figure
7
as well. This drop is likely due to GPT-4o relying more on its prior knowledge about these games, rather than reasoning from scratch. Since GPT-4o is likely to have encountered these games and their solutions during training, it can solve the different instances with a higher degree of accuracy.

Effect of Prompt Variations

Here we aim to check whether these biases, which present themselves in the main set of experiments, are specific to the base prompt used or inherent in the LLM itself. To do this, we evaluate GPT-4o under different prompts, which we refer to as (1) bullet prompt and (2) code prompt (both found in Section
D
of the Technical Appendix). In Figure
4
we see that the biases present themselves under all these prompts (GPT-4o experiences a performance drop when misaligned for all prompts), albeit not equally. We also see in Figure
7
that the biases are still significant under all prompts. This shows that by changing the prompt, one can alter how the biases affect the LLM’s decision-making abilities, it also shows that one cannot simply expect to rid LLMs of their biases by changing the prompt alone, indicating such biases are inherent.

Humans vs AI

We compare the frequency at which humans and an LLM (GPT-4o) select specific actions in a Stag Hunt-like game. We found that humans (data provided by
Belloc et al. 2019
) select action label B (cooperative action) more frequently than action label A (selfish action). We also see that GPT-4o, under the same setting as the humans, selects action label B more frequently. We then test GPT-4o under a different setting (where B is selfish and A is cooperative) and note that it still selects action label B more frequently (see Figure
9
in Section
D
of the Technical Appendix for more details). This suggests that GPT-4o is greatly influenced by its biases, rather than using logical reasoning to solve the problem and that we should be careful when comparing the performance of LLMs and humans in such tasks.

6
Conclusion

We performed an in-depth analysis of SOTA LLMs, showing that they are significantly affected by at least one of the 3 identified biases (see Sections
3
and
4
for details) (1) positional, (2) payoff, and (3) behavioural, irrespective of the prompt used. These biases cause notable changes in the LLMs’ performances under different configurations, even though the underlying task remains unchanged. We show that models GPT-3.5, GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-4o, and Llama-3-8B exhibit an average performance drop of 31%, 21%, 34%, and 27%, respectively across the tested games. We highlight the importance of taking special care of how these biases affect performance when evaluating LLMs in game-theoretic tasks.

Further, we observe that, while GPT-4o outperforms all of the other tested models on the standard LLM evaluation metrics
(
OpenAI 2024
)
, it suffers the worst in terms of performance drop suggesting that these metrics of success are not sufficient indicators of how well LLMs will perform in game theoretic tasks, especially when considering the effects of these systematic biases. Using CoT prompting generally reduces the impact of biases in most models and helps mitigate performance drops, suggesting that further work in prompt engineering might address these biases to an extent.

However, this effect is not consistent across all models; for instance, GPT-4-Turbo exhibits stronger biases with CoT prompting, leading to greater performance drops. This indicates that the solutions effective for some models may not be a general one across all models. We believe that the root issue of these systematic biases is the inherently limited reasoning capabilities of LLMs. Further work into improving these abilities will resolve the current limitations of LLMs on their systematic biases to make these decisions.

Experiment Design

LLM Reasoning

The following blocks of text show common patterns of reasoning for GPT4-Turbo under the 4 different experimental configurations when both agents are prompted with a preference to prioritise their self-gain (similar patterns are observed for other combinations of preferred behaviours). Note how the LLM uses reasoning over the action it will select changes as the configurations change. This is because it is biased towards selecting a certain action and will therefore reason in such a way that the biased answer might make sense.

Stag Hunt:

Changes in reasoning across configs

Configuration 1

Given the game’s rules and the principles of both players, we need to consider the outcomes based on mutual self-interest. If both players choose A, each gains 5 points, which is the maximum possible for mutual choice. If one chooses A and the other B, the player choosing B gains 4 points while the other gains 0. If both choose B, each gains 2 points. Since both players prioritize their own points, choosing A could risk gaining 0 points if the other player chooses B, aiming for 4 points. However, choosing B guarantees at least 2 points, and potentially 4 if the other player chooses A. Given the risk of gaining 0 points with A and the guarantee of at least 2 points with B, the safer and potentially more rewarding choice is B.

Configuration 2

Given the game’s rules and the principles of both players, we need to consider the outcomes based on mutual decisions. If both choose A, each gains 2 points. If one chooses A and the other B, the one who chooses A gets 4 points, and the one who chooses B gets 0. If both choose B, each gains 5 points. If one chooses B and the other A, the one who chooses B gets 0 points, and the one who chooses A gets 4. Since both players prioritize their own points, choosing B is the safer and potentially more rewarding option because the worst-case scenario (choosing B while the opponent chooses A) results in 0 points, but the best-case scenario (both choosing B) results in 5 points each, which is the maximum possible gain for both players simultaneously.

Configuration 3

Given the game’s rules and the principles of both players, we can analyze the outcomes. If both players choose B, each gains 5 points, which is a good outcome. If one chooses B and the other A, the one choosing A gains 4 points while the other gets 0. If both choose A, each gains 2 points. Since both players prioritize their own points, choosing B could risk getting 0 points if the other player chooses A, aiming for 4 points. However, choosing A guarantees at least 2 points regardless of the other player’s choice, and potentially 4 points if the other chooses B. Therefore, choosing A maximizes the guaranteed points and also offers a chance for a higher reward if the opponent chooses B.

Configuration 4

Given the game’s rules and the principles of both players, we need to consider the outcomes based on each combination of choices. If both players choose B, each earns 2 points. If one chooses B and the other A, the B chooser gets 4 points and the A chooser gets 0. If both choose A, each earns 5 points. Since both players prioritize their own points, choosing A could be risky if the other player chooses B, resulting in 0 points. However, choosing B is safer as the worst outcome is 2 points (if both choose B) and the best is 4 points (if the opponent chooses A). Given that both players aim to maximize their own points, it’s likely the opponent might also consider choosing B to avoid the risk of earning 0 points. Therefore, choosing B seems to be the safer and potentially more rewarding strategy based on the game setup and player principles.

Prisoner’s Dilemma:

Changes in reasoning across configs

Configuration 1

Given the game’s rules and the principles of both players, we need to consider the outcomes of each combination of choices. If both choose A, each gains 2 points. If one chooses A and the other B, the one who chooses B gains 3 points while the other gains 0. If both choose B, each gains 1 point. Since both players prioritize their own points, choosing B is a safer option because it guarantees at least 1 point and potentially 3 points if the opponent chooses A. Choosing A risks gaining 0 points if the opponent chooses B.

Configuration 2

Given the game’s rules and the principles of both players, we need to consider the outcomes of each combination of choices. If both players choose A, each gains 1 point. If one chooses A and the other B, the one who chooses A gains 3 points while the other gets 0. If both choose B, each gains 2 points. If one chooses B and the other A, the one who chooses B gets 0 points and the other gets 3. Since both players prioritize their own points, choosing B is safer as it guarantees at least 2 points if both choose B, whereas choosing A risks getting 0 points if the opponent chooses B. Therefore, choosing B maximizes the minimum gain (minimax strategy).

Configuration 3

Given that both players prioritize their own points, choosing action B is risky unless both players choose B, which results in 2 points each. However, if one chooses B and the other chooses A, the player who chose A gets 3 points while the one who chose B gets 0. If both choose A, each player gets 1 point. Since both players aim to maximize their own points, itś likely that the opponent will choose A to avoid the risk of getting 0 points by choosing B. Therefore, to maximize your own points in anticipation of the opponentś likely choice of A, you should also choose A to ensure you get at least 1 point rather than risking getting 0 points.

Configuration 4

Given the game’s rules and the principles of both players, we need to consider the outcomes based on each possible combination of choices. If both players choose B, each earns 1 point. If one chooses B and the other A, the player choosing B earns 3 points while the other earns 0. If both choose A, each earns 2 points. If one chooses A and the other B, the player choosing A earns 0 points while the other earns 3. Since both players prioritize their own points, choosing A could risk earning 0 points if the other player chooses B. However, choosing B guarantees at least 1 point and can earn up to 3 points if the other player chooses A. Therefore, choosing B maximizes the potential gain while minimizing the risk of earning no points.

Effect of Fine-Tuning

We also run a set of experiments using LLama-3-8b (w/o Fine-Tuning) and LLama-3-8b-Instruct (w/ Fine-Tuning) to investigate the effect instruction fine-tuning has on the identified biases. We run the same experiment setup described in Section
3
, except we run it fewer times (20x). Looking at Figures
3
and
8
, we find that LLama-3-8b is primarily guided by the order in which the action labels are presented in the prompt, whereas LLama-3-8b-Instruct is equally guided by both the order and the preferred behaviours. However, the overall effect of the biases is weakened when the LLM is fine-tuned. This suggests that fine-tuning LLMs may have a positive effect on the model’s ability to overcome these systematic biases, however, it may also affect the models in an unforeseen manner, such as introducing a behavioural bias in LLama-3-8b.

present an experiment where a random sample of participants (88 people) play 4 games (352 games) of Stag Hunt under a time constraint (10 seconds), while a second set of participants (97) play 4 games (388 games) unconstrained. The constrained set of participants will be referred to as making a reflexive choice (without reasoning) and the unconstrained set make a reasoned choice. Processing the data made available by
Belloc et al. 2019
, we found that when humans are making a reflexive choice, they typically select label B 62% of the time and label A 38% of the time. When making a reasoned choice, they still prefer label B, choosing it 52.5% of the time, with label A being selected more often at 47.5%. These results suggest that humans have a weak bias towards selecting label B, be it because of order, payoff, or something else entirely. These results can be seen in Figure

.

We also prompted GPT-4o under a different setting (one which the humans are not tested on), where label A is Stag and B is Hare, changing the meaning of the labels but keeping their order the same. While human decision-making is based on both logical reasoning and potentially bias, GPT-4o is much more influenced by its biases. This suggests that there is considerable room for improvement in reducing the impact of these biases on the LLM’s decision-making process.
