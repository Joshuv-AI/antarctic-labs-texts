Optimal Sparse Estimation of High Dimensional Heavy-tailed Time Series
Sagnik Halder, George Michailidis

Abstract
Recently, high dimensional vector auto-regressive models (VAR), have attracted a lot of interest, due to novel applications in the health, engineering and social sciences. The presence of temporal dependence poses additional challenges to the theory of penalized estimation techniques widely used in the analysis of their iid counterparts. However, recent work (e.g., ( Basu and Michailidis 2015 ; Kock and Callot 2015 ) ) has established optimal consistency of ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -LASSO regularized estimates applied to models involving high dimensional stable Gaussian processes. The only price paid for temporal dependence is an extra multiplicative factor that equals 1 for independent and identically distributed (iid) data. Further, ( Wong et al. 2020 ) extended these results to heavy tailed VAR s that exhibit " β \beta -mixing" dependence, but the rates rates are sub -optimal, while the extra factor is intractable. This paper improves these results in two important directions: (i) We establish optimal consistency rates and corresponding finite sample bounds for the underlying model parameters that match those for iid data, modulo a price for temporal dependence, that is easy to interpret and equals 1 for iid data. (ii) We incorporate more general penalties in estimation (which are not decomposable unlike the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} norm) to induce general sparsity patterns. The key technical tool employed is a novel, easy-to-use concentration bound for heavy tailed linear processes, that do not rely on "mixing" notions and give tighter bounds.



Keywords:
Subweibull, High Dimensional Time Series, Vector Autoregressions, Structured Sparsity

We start by considering the stochastic regression model

(3)

Our focus concerns the high dimensional regime, wherein

vector

autoregressive

3.1
Subweibull Norms.

We start with the notion of an

(

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Psi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)

\Psi(0)=0

\displaystyle||Z||_{\Psi}:=\inf\{c>0:\mathbb{E}\left\{\Psi(|Z|/c)\leq 1\right\}\}.

(10)

Well known cases include

that corresponds t
\Psi(z)=z^{m}
,

, sub-Gaussian random variables given by
\Psi(z)=\exp(z^{2})-1
and sub-exponential random variables when
\Psi(z)=\exp(z)-1
. To generalize sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential norms, let us consider
\Psi(z)=\exp(z^{\gamma})-1
, for any
\gamma\in(0,2]
(called the
tail

index
). This is called the Subweibull norm (technically a
quasi-norm
for
\gamma\in(0,1)
), and random variables
Z
with finite Subweibull norm exhibit heavier tails than both sub-exponential and sub-Gaussian distributions. We shall rename the norm as
||\cdot||_{\psi_{\gamma}}
in this case, and refer to any random variable
Z
with finite Subweibull norm with tail index
\gamma
, as Subweibull(
\gamma
). Several equivalent characterizations and properties of Subweibull random variables exist in the literature (e.g. Lemma 5,
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
, Appendix A,
(
Götze et al. 2019
)
). We can then extend the notion of a Subweibull random variable to a Subweibull random

as follows.

Definition 3.2
.

A random vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is said to be Subweibull(
\gamma
) if
v^{\top}X
is Subweibull(
\gamma
) for all
v\in\mathbb{B}_{2}
, and its Subweibull norm is given by

\displaystyle||X||_{\psi_{\gamma}}:=\underset{v\in\mathbb{B}_{2}}{\sup}||v^{\top}X||_{\psi_{\gamma}}.

(11)

We will focus mainly on VAR models generated by a noise process with Subweibull tails, since its temporal dependence (see ((
12
))) is easy to quantify and the obtained rates can be compared and contrasted to the Gaussian case, by simply plugging the Subweibull tail-index
\gamma=2
. For more general results on regularized stochastic regression with Subweibull noise, under
mixing
, see Appendix (
D
).

Stationarity

Definition 3.3
.

A process

is

, the vector
(x_{m},...,x_{m+n})
has the same distribution as
(x_{m+r},...,x_{m+n+r})
. It is

Corr(x_{t},x_{t+l})

3.3
Role of temporal dependence: A Comparative Overview.

From a technical standpoint, temporal dependence factors arise while using concentration inequalities for dependent data, in the course of proving consistency of our penalized estimates. Our goal is to recover optimal consistency rates that have already been derived in the independence setting, modulo a multiplicative dependence factor. Ideally, this dependence factor should be easy to interpret, and reduce to 1 for iid data (thereby being a true extension of the latter). Unfortunately, there is no single, unified framework for temporal dependence that gives us tight concentration bounds for every family of time series. Hence, we require different, but related, notions of dependence that is suited to specific examples. These different notions of dependence do not imply one another, and may hence be seen as complementary to each other.

From the standpoint of consistency rates, the temporal dependence factor “inflates" the consistency bounds, and the finite, minimum sample size required to achieve the bound that holds for the independent case. Hence, the effect of dependence on the penalized estimates is clear.

3.3.1
Temporal Dependence Measure for Sub-Weibull Linear Processes.

We first define a measure of temporal dependence for a Subweibull linear process, that will be used when deriving subsequent consistency results. Consider a linear filter of an innovation process
\{\eta_{t}\}\sim\text{IID}(0,\Sigma_{\eta})
, given by
x_{t}=\sum_{i\geq 0}A_{i}\eta_{t-i}=\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{B})\eta_{t}
where
\mathfrak{B}
denotes the backshift operator, the matrix power series
\mathcal{A}(z)=\sum_{i\geq 0}A_{i}z^{i}
defined on the complex plane
z\in\mathbb{C}
, satisfy
\det(\mathcal{A}(z))\neq 0
on the unit disk
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1\}
(stationarity). We then define the following and assume it is finite:

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}(\mathcal{A}):=\sum_{i\geq 0}\sum_{j\geq 0}||A_{i+j}||_{2}||A_{j}||_{2}.

(12)

Note that
\mathsf{C}(\mathcal{A})<\infty
implies
\sum_{i\geq 0}||A_{i}||<\infty
, i.e. the process is stable. This definition is motivated by the fact that the dependence in the linear process
\{x_{t}\}
is due to the auto-correlation terms, i.e.
\mbox{Cov}(x_{0},x_{l})=\Sigma_{X}(l)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}A_{i}\Sigma_{\eta}A_{i+l}^{\top}
for all non-negative lags
l
. For a connection between this measure of dependence, and the one introduced in
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
)
, see Section (
3.4
). In particular, for a stationary VAR(1) process
x_{t}=Ax_{t-1}+\eta_{t}
, we have the causal representation
x_{t}=\sum_{i\geq 0}A^{i}\eta_{t-i}
. Assuming the spectral radius of the transition matrix
\rho(A)<1
(stability, see
(
Lütkepohl 2005
)
), it is easy to verify that the series
\sum_{i\geq 0}||A^{i}||_{2}<\infty
(using Gelfand’s formula:
\rho(A)=\underset{k\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}||A^{k}||_{2}^{1/k}
). Then,

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{i\geq 0}\sum_{j\geq 0}||A^{i+j}||_{2}||A^{j}||_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\geq 0}\sum_{j\geq 0}||A^{i}||_{2}||A^{j}||_{2}^{2}

(13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}||A^{i}||_{2}\sum_{j\geq 0}||A^{j}||_{2}^{2}\leq\left[\sum_{i\geq 0}||A^{i}||_{2}\right]^{3}<\infty.

(14)

We stress that the assumption:
\rho(A)<1
is mild and standard. Many authors (e.g.
(
Loh and Wainwright 2012
;
Bickel and Levina 2008
)
) work with the much stronger assumption that the spectral norm
||A||_{2}<1
. However, this fails for VAR(d) models of lag
d>1
(
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
, lemma E.1)
), and therefore can not be applied in general. Even with a VAR(1) process, we can take the transition matrix
A=\begin{bmatrix}a,b\\
0,a\end{bmatrix}
,
where
|a|<1,b\in\mathbb{R}
. The spectral radius is
\rho(A)=|a|<1
, while the spectral norm is
||A||_{2}^{2}=a^{2}+\frac{b^{2}}{2}+\frac{|b|}{2}\sqrt{4a^{2}+b^{2}}\rightarrow\infty
as
b\rightarrow\infty
, with
a
fixed. However, our dependence measure does not have this drawback. If we do have
||A||_{2}<1
, then
\mathsf{C}(\mathcal{A})\leq(1-||A||_{2})^{-2}
. Also, as the spectral radius
\rho(A)\rightarrow 1
, the process becomes highly unstable and the dependence factor
\mathsf{C}(A)
blows up. For a simple VAR(1) example, if we take
A=\begin{bmatrix}\rho,0\\
0,\rho\end{bmatrix}

where
|\rho|<1
, then, the spectral radius of A is
\rho(A)=\rho
while the dependence factor
\mathsf{C}(A)=(1-\rho)^{-1}(1-\rho^{2})^{-1}\rightarrow\infty
, as the spectral radius
\rho\rightarrow 1
.
Finally, we note that, in case of a VAR(d) process
Z_{t}=B_{1}^{\top}Z_{t-1}+\dots+B_{d}^{\top}Z_{t-d}+\epsilon_{t}
, we can write it as a VAR(1) process
\tilde{Z}_{t}=\tilde{B}^{\top}\tilde{Z}_{t-1}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{t}
, as in section (
2

\{Z_{t}\}
implies stability of
\{\tilde{Z}_{t}\}
(see e.g.
(
Lütkepohl 2005
, Ch 2.1)
), which means
\mathsf{C}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})<\infty
(note that
\mathsf{C}(\mathcal{B})
and

are not the same). From section (

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|u^{\top}(X^{\top}X/n-\Sigma_{X})u|>K^{2}\mathsf{C}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})t\right)\leq 6\exp\left[-c\min\left\{(nt)^{\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}},nt^{2})\right\}\right]

(15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|u^{\top}X^{\top}\eta/n|>K^{2}\mathsf{C}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})t\right)\leq 6dp\exp\left[-c\min\left\{(nt)^{\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}},nt^{2})\right\}\right].

(16)

Remark 3.1
.

When
\gamma_{2}=2
, Proposition (
3.1
) matches the deviation bounds (
A.1
) for the strictly Gaussian case in
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
)
, and moreover extends it to the Sub-gaussian case also (note that the simple trick of rotating a Gaussian vector to make its components independent, will not work outside Gaussianity). This closely mimics the bounds derived for the Sub-Gaussian case in
(
Zheng and Raskutti 2019
)
and extends those bounds to heavy tails.

Remark 3.2
.

Unlike Proposition (
A.4
) in
Wong et al. 2020
for the more general case that requires
mixing
conditions, these concentration bounds hold for all
t>0
,
n\geq 1
, so no preconditions are required. Further, they are used in the sequel to obtain clean expressions for the sample size
n
requirement, tuning parameter
\lambda_{n}
, as well as temporal dependence, that directly match those derived in
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
)
for the strictly Gaussian case.

Remark 3.3
.

These results are established by essentially a "truncation" argument analogous to that used for proving
(
Zheng and Raskutti 2019
, Lemma 5.2)
. This is possible because of the highly specific dependence structure of VAR — it is a causal (infinite) linear combination of
independent
noise/shocks. Hence, the trick is to truncate the linear series at some
finite
time point in the past and use a Hanson-Wright type inequality
(
Vershynin 2018
)
for this
finite
linear combination of
independent
noises. Finally, what’s "left-over" is controlled suitably by choosing this finite time-point carefully.

Remark 3.4
.

We have also compared this concentration bound to those found in the most relevant literature, namely
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
)
and
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
, as well as the different quantifying dependence factors, in Appendix (
A
).

3.4
Concepts Related to the Penalty Term.

As noted earlier, the focus in the literature has primarily been on sparse/group sparse penalties for the regression coefficient
\beta^{*}
in (
7
) and the transition matrix
\tilde{B}
in (
5
). Here, we review some concepts that would be used in the sequel to establish results for much more general penalty terms that are useful in practical settings.

Definition 3.4
.

Let G be a
p\times d
random matrix with iid
N(0,1)
entries. For a a set
{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
, the Gaussian width of
{T}
is defined as

\displaystyle w({T})=\underset{W\in{T}}{\sup}\text{tr}(W^{\top}G).

(17)

It measures the size of a (usually convex) set in the Euclidean space. A key challenge is to evaluate Gaussian widths of sets related to the regularizer
\mathcal{R}
.

Definition 3.5
.

Given a set
{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
, and a generic norm
\mathcal{R}()
on
\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
, the subspace norm compatibility constant is given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}({C})=\underset{W\in{C}-\{0\}}{\sup}\frac{\mathcal{R}(W)}{||W||_{F}}.

(18)

When
d=1
, the definition reduces to the one given in
(
Negahban et al. 2012
)
. It measures the relative price paid for switching between a generic norm
\mathcal{R}()
and the usual Euclidean norm
||\cdot||_{F}
. Also, the reverse norm compatibility is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}({C})=\underset{W\in{C}-\{0\}}{\sup}\frac{||W||_{F}}{\mathcal{R}(W)}.

(19)

In particular when
{C}=\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)
, we write
\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}({C})
as simply
\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}
. For example, we note that
||v||\leq\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{R}(v)\leq\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}
for all
v\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)
. We further assume that
\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}
is bounded above by an absolute constant (this holds in most cases under consideration).

4
Subweibull VAR under general sparsity.

In this section, we leverage Proposition (
3.1
) to obtain optimal consistency rates for different examples of sparse, SubWeibull VAR
s
. For model (
7
), we consider a general sparse structure on
\beta^{*}
, induced through the Dantzig selector (
8
) with an appropriate penalty
\mathcal{R}()
. To consistently estimate
\beta^{*}
, it is standard practice in high-dimensional literature (e.g.

(
Van de Geer et al. 2011
;
Bickel et al. 2009
)
) to verify a first order "deviation" condition, and a second order "Restricted Eigenvalue (RE)" condition. The deviation condition essentially restricts the cross product term
\frac{X^{\top}\eta}{n}
around zero with high probability, while the RE condition ensures the sample gram matrix
\frac{X^{\top}X}{n}
is uniformly bounded away from zero over a small cone, with high probability. We prove both under the general penalty
\mathcal{R}()
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Deviation Condition for Subweibull VAR.
Consider model (
7
) posited in section (
2
). Then, there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that, for
n\geq n_{dev}:=\left[cw^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))\right]^{4/\gamma_{2}-1}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{X^{\top}\eta}{n}\right)\geq 2\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}K^{2}\mathsf{C}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})\sqrt{\frac{cw^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))}{n}}\right]\leq 6\exp[-w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))+\log dp].

(20)

Proposition 4.2
.

RE condition for Subweibull VAR.
Consider model (
7
) posited in section (
2
). Assume
\Lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{X})>0
. Then, there is an absolute constant
c^{\prime}>0
such that, for a minimum sample size of

\displaystyle n\geq n_{RE}:=\left(c^{\prime}\max\left\{1,\frac{16\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}K^{4}\mathsf{C}^{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}{\Lambda_{\min}^{2}(\Sigma_{X})}\right\}w^{2}[\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})\right)^{2/\gamma_{2}}

(21)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\underset{v\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}}{\inf}\frac{v^{\top}X^{\top}Xv}{n}\geq\alpha_{RE}\right]\geq 1-6\exp[-w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})],

(22)

where the restricted eigenvalue is
\alpha_{RE}=\Lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{x})/2
.

Using these deviation and RE conditions, we can consistently estimate each of the
p
regressions that the Subweibull VAR model can be decomposed into. Subsequently, we can combine these estimates using a Bonferroni bound that leads to the following optimal consistency result.

Proposition 4.3
.

Consider the VAR(d) model posited in section (
2
). Further, suppose the minimum sample size and tuning parameter satisfies

\displaystyle n\geq\max\left\{n_{dev},n_{RE}\right\},\quad\lambda_{n}=2\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}K^{2}\mathsf{C}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})\sqrt{\frac{cw^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))}{n}}.

(23)

Then, denoting the columns of
B
(equivalently rows of
B^{\top}
) as
B_{1},\dots,B_{p}
, and assuming they share a common sparsity pattern, the augmented penalized estimate
\hat{B}
satisfies

\displaystyle\underset{1\leq j\leq p}{\max}||\hat{B}_{j}-B_{j}||\leq\frac{2\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})}{\alpha_{RE}},

(24)

\displaystyle\underset{1\leq j\leq p}{\max}\mathcal{R}(\hat{B}_{j}-B_{j})\leq\frac{2\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}(\mathcal{T})}{\alpha_{RE}},\quad\text{(Estimation error)},

(25)

\displaystyle\underset{1\leq j\leq p}{\max}(\hat{B}_{j}-B_{j})^{\top}\frac{X^{\top}X}{n}(\hat{B}_{j}-B_{j})\leq\frac{4\lambda_{n}^{2}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})}{\alpha_{RE}}\quad\text{(Prediction error)}.

(26)

with probability at least
1-6dp^{2}\exp[-w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))]-6p\exp[-w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})]
.

Noting that
x_{i}=\tilde{Z}_{i+d-2}
, for
1\leq i\leq n
, where
n=T-d+1
is the sample size, the design matrix
X
is given by

\displaystyle X=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{Z}_{d-1}^{\top}\\
\tilde{Z}_{d-2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{Z}_{n+d-2}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}_{(dp\times dp)}=\quad\begin{bmatrix}Z_{d-1}^{\top}&Z_{d-2}^{\top}&\cdots&Z_{0}^{\top}\\
Z_{d}^{\top}&Z_{d-1}^{\top}&\cdots&Z_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
Z_{T-1}^{\top}&Z_{T-2}^{\top}&\cdots&Z_{T-d}^{\top}\\
\end{bmatrix}_{(dp\times dp)}.

(27)

The restricted eigenvalue is given by
\alpha_{RE}=\Lambda_{min}(\Sigma_{X}(0))/2=\Lambda_{min}(\Sigma_{\tilde{Z}})/2
.

The key algebraic quantities of interest are the following:

•

The Gaussian width of the unit norm ball :
w(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))
.

•

The Gaussian width of the spherical cap of the tangent cone
\mathcal{T}
:
w(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})
.

•

The subspace compatibility constant
\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})
.

•

The reverse compatibility constant :
\bar{\Phi}
.

Next, we provide estimates for these quantities for different examples of the regularizer
\mathcal{R}
.

Example 4.1
.

that considers the
\ell_{1}
norm and serves for illustration purposes.

Suppose the parameter
\beta^{*}
is
s
-sparse, i.e.
||\beta^{*}||_{0}=s
. Then, the
\ell_{1}
norm is a convex relaxation of
||\cdot||_{0}
. Hence, for
\mathcal{R}()=||\cdot||_{1}
, (see Examples 1.1, 2.1, 3.1,
(
Banerjee et al. 2015
)
and Proposition 3.10,
(
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
)
), we have

\displaystyle w(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))\leq 2\sqrt{\log 2p},\quad w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})\leq 2s\log(p/s)+\frac{5}{4}s,\quad

(28)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})\leq 2\sqrt{s},\quad\bar{\Phi}\leq 1.

(29)

Hence, ignoring dependence factors, the
\ell_{2}
consistency rate is
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{s\log p/n})
, and the minimum sample size requirement is
n\succsim\max\left\{s(\log(p/s)+1),\log 2p\right\}
(which matches Proposition 3.3,
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
)
, which was derived for the
\ell_{1}
-LASSO).

Example 4.2
.

Sorted
\ell_{1}
norm.

The
\ell_{1}
norm has the drawback that it treats all coefficients in the
\beta
vector equally; this is a problem if there is there is cross-sectional dependence among the significant components of
\beta^{*}
. In that case, the
\ell_{1}
penalty basically selects an arbitrary subset of the significant components, whereas it is desirable to include
all
relevant variables in the analysis. Several authors have addressed this problem; the most popular solution being the elastic net. Another solution was proposed by
(
Bondell and Reich 2008
)
to deal with sparse regression with correlated variables, and generalized by
(
Bogdan et al. 2013
)
to a more general class of penalties characterized by the Ordered Weighted
\ell_{1}
or OWL norm (also called the SLOPE, e.g. see
(
Bogdan et al. 2015
;
Stucky and van de Geer 2018
)
). It is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathfrak{w}_{i}\beta_{i}^{\downarrow}

(30)

for some weights
\mathfrak{w}_{1}\geq...\geq\mathfrak{w}_{p}\geq 0
, wherein
\{\beta_{i}^{\downarrow},1\leq i\leq p\}
is a decreasing arrangement of
\{|\beta_{i}|,1\leq i\leq p\}
. Even though this norm is
not decomposable
, it is
atomic

(
Zeng and Figueiredo 2014
)
, and helps cluster significant variables when they exhibit strong cross-sectional dependence
(
Figueiredo and Nowak 2014
;
Figueiredo and Nowak 2016
)
. Let
\bar{\mathfrak{w}}_{i}=(\sum_{t=1}^{i}\mathfrak{w}_{t})/i
. Then
\mathfrak{w}_{1}\geq...\geq\mathfrak{w}_{p}\geq 0
implies
\bar{\mathfrak{w}}_{1}\geq...\geq\bar{\mathfrak{w}}_{p}\geq 0
. Denote
\tilde{\mathfrak{w}}_{s}
as the average of
\mathfrak{w}_{s+1},...,\mathfrak{w}_{p}
. Also,
\mathcal{R}(v)\geq\mathfrak{w}_{1}||v||_{1}\geq\mathfrak{w}_{1}||v||
. Then, for
s
-sparse
\beta^{*}
we get (see
(
Banerjee et al. 2015
)
)

\displaystyle w(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))\leq 2\underset{1\leq i\leq p}{\max}\left\{\frac{\sqrt{2+\log(2p/i)}}{\bar{\mathfrak{w}}_{i}}\right\}+2\sqrt{\log 2p}/\bar{\mathfrak{w}}_{p}\leq 2\sqrt{2+\log 2p}/\bar{\mathfrak{w}}_{p},

(31)

\displaystyle w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})\leq\frac{2\mathfrak{w}_{1}^{2}}{\tilde{\mathfrak{w}}_{s}}s\log(p/s)+\frac{3}{2}s,\quad

(32)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})\leq\frac{2\mathfrak{w}_{1}^{2}}{\tilde{\mathfrak{w}}_{s}}\sqrt{s},\quad\bar{\Phi}\leq\mathfrak{w}_{1}^{-1}.

(33)

Thus, the order of consistency is
\mathcal{O}\left(2\mathfrak{w}_{1}^{2}/(\bar{\mathfrak{w}}_{p}\tilde{\mathfrak{w}}_{s})\sqrt{(s\log p)/n}\right)
. The minimum sample size required is given by
n\succsim\max\left\{(\mathfrak{w}_{1}^{2}/\tilde{\mathfrak{w}}_{s})s(\log(p/s)+1),\log(2p)/\bar{\mathfrak{w}}_{p}^{2}\right\}
. When
\mathfrak{w}_{1}=...=\mathfrak{w}_{p}=1
, we get
\bar{\mathfrak{w}}_{p}=\tilde{\mathfrak{w}}_{s}=1
and
\mathcal{R}
reduces to the
\ell_{1}
norm, with the usual rate of consistency.

Example 4.3
.

Group sparsity.

Suppose, instead of element-wise sparsity, we assume a group structure on the parameter
\beta^{*}
. If the parameter space comprises of
M
(possibly overlapping) groups
\mathfrak{G}=\{G_{1},...,G_{M}\}
, with
G_{i}
being the set of parameter indices in the
i^{th}
group and
\cup_{i=1}^{M}G_{i}=\{1,..,p\}
, then it is appropriate to consider the
atomic
norm
\mathcal{R}
induced by this grouping
\mathfrak{G}
. When the groups
G_{i}
are disjoint, then
\mathcal{R}
is just the
\ell_{2,1}
norm given by
\mathcal{R}(\beta)=||\beta||_{2,1}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}||\beta_{G_{i}}||
. Let the maximum group size be denoted by
m
and suppose only
s
many groups are active. Then, by Lemma 2 in
(
Banerjee et al. 2015
)
, and
(
Rao et al. 2012
)
, we obtain

\displaystyle w(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))\leq\sqrt{m}+2\sqrt{\log M},\quad w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})\leq\left\{(\sqrt{2\log(M-s)}+\sqrt{m})^{2}+m\right\}s,\quad

(34)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})\leq s,\quad\text{(overlapping groups)}\quad\text{or}\quad\sqrt{s},\quad\text{(non-overlapping groups)}

(35)

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}\leq 1.

(36)

For non-overlapping groups, we then have the usual rate
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{s}(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{\log M})/\sqrt{n}\right)
and the sample size scales as
n\succsim s(m+\log M)
.

Example 4.4
.

The
k
-support norm.

The “elastic net" regularizer comprising of a combination of the
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
norms
(
Zou and Hastie 2005
)
is often advocated as a better alternative to the
\ell_{1}
LASSO, since it may be considered as a convex relaxation of
||\cdot||_{0}
with the scale set by the
\ell_{2}
norm. However, a tighter convex relaxation is possible, and captured by the so-called “k-support” norm introduced in
(
Argyriou et al. 2012
)
. It is an
atomic
norm, shown to recover sparse parameters
\beta^{*}
better than the elastic net empirically. We have, from
(
Banerjee et al. 2015
)
, Proposition 3.1,
(
Argyriou et al. 2012
)
,

\displaystyle w(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))\leq\sqrt{k}+2\sqrt{k\log(p/k)+k},\quad w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\beta_{\max}^{*}}{\beta_{\min}^{*}}s\log(p/s)+\frac{3}{2}s}

(37)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})\leq\sqrt{2}\left(1+\frac{2\beta_{\max}^{*}}{\beta_{\min}^{*}}\right),\quad\bar{\Phi}\leq 1.

(38)

Here,
\beta_{\max}^{*}=\underset{1\leq j\leq p}{\max}|\beta_{j}^{*}|
and
\beta_{\min}^{*}=\underset{1\leq j\leq p}{\min}|\beta_{j}^{*}|
.

4.1
Comparisons to Previous Work.

The result in Proposition (

) indicates how the sample size

and the tuning parameter
\lambda_{n}
scale by a factor
\mathsf{C}^{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
, the latter being

Comparison with
(

)
.

The special case of

, then
\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{I})

\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}
or, in terms of a number –
\Lambda_{min}(\Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}
or

even in this case. We recover this bound for the sub-Gaussian case also, simply by putting

)). Moreover,
(

)
considers the popular

Comparison with
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
.

Note that the obtained result is

than its counterpart presented in Appendix E in the aforementioned paper for the Subweibull regression framework under

penalty. The process
\{Z_{t}\}
can be shown to be geometrically

(
Wong et al. 2020
)
to this example, will lead to a sub-optimal sample size requirement
(

,

(and taking the

) and (

(see
(

), instead of the optimal bound

On the other hand, its generality precludes obtaining tight concentration inequalities without further assumptions. (

5
Subweibull VAR with a Low Rank plus Sparse Transition Matrix.

Next, we consider, for simplicity, a VAR(1) model where the transition matrix
B
is low-rank plus group sparse given by

\displaystyle Z_{t}=B^{\top}Z_{t-1}+\epsilon_{t},\quad B=L^{*}+R^{*},\quad\text{rank($L^{*}$)=r},

(39)

where
L^{*}
represents the low rank component and
R^{*}
represents either a sparse
S^{*}
, or group-sparse component

G^{*}
. We assume the number of non-zero elements in the sparse case is
\|S^{*}\|_{0}=s
, while in the group sparse case there are
g
non-zero groups out of
M
groups, with
r\ll p,s\ll p^{2}
and
g\ll p^{2}
. The matrix
L^{*}
captures a common dense persistence structure across
all

p
component series, specifically, strong cross-correlations between the component series, which a simple sparse VAR model cannot handle. Also, we assume the noise components
\{\epsilon_{tj}\}
to be independent SW(
\gamma_{2}
) with
||\epsilon_{tj}||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}\leq K,\forall i,j
. We want to estimate
L^{*}
and
R^{*}
accurately based on a sample
\{Z_{0},\dots,Z_{T}\}
, when
N\ll p^{2}
.

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Z_{T}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
Z_{1}^{\top}\end{array}\right]}_{\mathcal{Y}}=\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Z_{T-1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
Z_{0}^{\top}\end{array}\right]}_{\mathcal{X}}B+\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\epsilon_{T}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\epsilon_{1}^{\top}\end{array}\right]}_{E}.

This is a standard matrix regression problem with
n=T
samples and
p^{2}
parameters. However, we face an
identifiability
issue in the estimation of the low rank and sparse components
L^{*}
and
R^{*}
. For example, if the low-rank component
L^{*}
itself is
s
-sparse and the sparse component
R^{*}
is of rank
r
, then we cannot hope for a way to estimate
L^{*}
and
R^{*}
separately without further restrictions. Thus, we impose the restriction that condition that the low rank part should not be too sparse and the sparse or group-sparse part should not be low-rank (e.g.
(
Chandrasekaran et al. 2011
;
Agarwal et al. 2012
)
). We thus have the following estimation procedure:

\displaystyle(\hat{L},\hat{R})=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\begin{subarray}{c}L,R\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\\
L\in\Omega\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{Y}-\mathcal{X}(L+R)\right\|^{2}_{F}+\lambda_{n}\|L\|_{*}+\mu_{n}\|R\|_{\diamond},

(49)

where
\Omega=\{L\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}:\|L\|_{\max}\leq\alpha/p\}
(for sparse) or
\{L\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}:\|L\|_{2,\max}\leq\beta/\sqrt{M}\}
(for group sparse),
\|\cdot\|_{\diamond}
represents
\|\cdot\|_{1}
or
\|\cdot\|_{2,1}
depending on sparsity or group sparsity of
R
, and
\lambda_{n}
and
\mu_{n}
are tuning parameters. The parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
control for the degree of “non-identifiability” of the matrices allowed in the model. Specifically, large
\alpha
provide sparser estimates of
S
, while allowing simultaneous sparse and low-rank components to be absorbed in
\hat{L}
. On the other hand, smaller
\alpha
pushes the simultaneous low-rank and sparse components to be absorbed in
\hat{S}
. The problem under independence was studied by
(
Agarwal et al. 2012
)
, and extended to the dependent, Gaussian case by
(
Basu et al. 2019b
)

8
Discussion

In this paper, we consider the theoretical properties of penalized estimates in high-dimensional time series models when the data are generated from a (non-Gaussian) multivariate stationary process, under structured sparsity constraints. Specifically, we consider a sub-Weibull VAR
d
model, and derived optimal consistency rates under general sparsity, and gave applications of the obtained results.

A interesting direction for future research is to carry out inference for the regression (transition matrix) parameter for a sub-Weibull model under structured sparsity constraints. For iid data and the LASSO penalty, a number of papers have obtained normal approximations for the parameter estimates after a debiasing steps - see, e.g,
Zhang and Zhang 2014
;
Van de Geer et al. 2014
;
Javanmard and Montanari 2014
;
Ning and Liu 2017
. The literature is rather sparse for temporally dependent data, with
Basu et al. 2019a
and
(
Zheng and Raskutti 2019
)
being two recent works on the topic. We believe that analogous results can be extended, with some care, to the case of Sub-Weibull VAR
d
for debiased LASSO estimators. It would be of interest to extend such results to more general penalties
\mathcal{R}()
.

Finally, one potential future question which we briefly alluded to, is (non-asymptotic) inference (based on one-step or de-sparsified estimators) for high dimensional time series under general sparsity.

A.1
Comparison with Stability Factors in Basu et al.

There are several notions of dependence for a stochastic process
\{x_{t}\}
. We only mention a few here. Basu et al
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
;
Basu et al. 2015
)
quantify dependence in case of a strictly Gaussian time series by considering its spectral density. Formally, given a centered, stationary time series
\{x_{t}\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, with autocovariance matrix function
\Sigma_{X}(h)=\mbox{Cov}(x_{t},x_{t+h})
(we write
\Sigma_{X}(0)
as
\Sigma_{X}
interchangeably, depending on context), its spectral density
f_{X}
is a Hermitian matrix valued function given by:

\displaystyle f_{X}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{+\infty}\Sigma_{X}(l)e^{-il\theta},\quad\theta\in[-\pi,\pi].

(60)

If
\{(x_{t},y_{t})\}
is a jointly centered, stationary process with
\Sigma_{X,Y}(h)=\mbox{Cov}(x_{t},y_{t+h})
, then their cross-dependence is given by the cross-spectral term

\displaystyle f_{X,Y}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{+\infty}\Sigma_{X,Y}(l)e^{-il\theta},\quad\theta\in[\pi,\pi].

(61)

Definition A.1
.

Finally, assuming the existence of these densities, temporal dependence is quantified by the following stability factors:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(f_{X}):=\underset{\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]}{esssup}\quad\Lambda_{\max}(f_{X}(\theta))=\underset{\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]}{esssup}\quad||f_{X}(\theta)||_{2},

(62)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(f_{X}):=\underset{\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]}{essinf}\quad\Lambda_{\min}(f_{X}(\theta)),

(63)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(f_{X,Y}):=\underset{\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]}{esssup}\quad\sqrt{\Lambda_{\max}f_{X,Y}^{*}(\theta)f_{X,Y}(\theta)}=\underset{\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]}{esssup}\quad||f_{X,Y}(\theta)||_{2}.

(64)

Also, for
1\leq k\leq p
, the factors
\mathcal{M}(f_{X},k)=\underset{J\subseteq\{1,...,p\},card(J)\leq k}{max}\mathcal{M}(f_{X_{J}})
and similarly
\mathfrak{m}(f_{X},k)
, may be defined. A large class of time series (VAR models in particular) satisfies the assumption
0<\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})\leq\mathcal{M}(f_{X})<\infty
. The term
\mathcal{M}(f_{X})
measure the "spikes" of the spectrum which indicate greater dependence or "memory" in the process, while the term
\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})
measures the cross sectional dependence between the
p
component processes. To estimate stability factors, consider a linear filter of an innovation process
\{\eta_{t}\}\sim\text{IID}(0,\Sigma_{\eta})
, given by
x_{t}=\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{B})\eta_{t}
where
\mathfrak{B}
denotes the backshift operator, the matrix power series
\mathcal{A}(z)=\sum_{i\geq 0}A_{i}z^{i}
defined on the complex plane
z\in\mathbb{C}
, satisfy
\det(\mathcal{A}(z))\neq 0
on the unit disk
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1\}
(stationarity), and
\sum_{i\geq 0}||A_{i}||_{2}<\infty
(stability). Its spectral density is given by

\displaystyle f_{X}(\theta)=\mathcal{A}(e^{-i\theta})\frac{\Sigma_{\eta}}{2\pi}\mathcal{A}^{*}(e^{-i\theta}),\quad\theta\in[-\pi,\pi],

(65)

which leads to the following estimates

\displaystyle\frac{\Lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\eta})\mu_{min}(\mathcal{A})}{2\pi}\leq\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})\leq\mathcal{M}(f_{X})\leq\frac{\Lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\eta})\mu_{max}(\mathcal{A})}{2\pi},

(66)

where

\displaystyle\mu_{max}(\mathcal{A}):=\underset{|z|=1}{\max}\quad\Lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}(z)^{*}\mathcal{A}(z))=\underset{|z|=1}{max}||\mathcal{A}(z)||_{2}^{2},

(67)

\displaystyle\mu_{min}(\mathcal{A}):=\underset{|z|=1}{\min}\quad\Lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{A}(z)^{*}\mathcal{A}(z))=\underset{|z|=1}{\min}||\mathcal{A}(z)||_{2}^{-2}.

(68)

This can easily be extended to a stable rational filter of the form
\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{B}
in which case, we will get bounds matching (2.6) in
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
)
. The advantage of using these temporal dependence factors is that (i) they are easy to interpret and (ii) we can state tight concentration inequalities for dependent Gaussian processes, in terms of these factors, as stated below.

.

\displaystyle\beta(n)\leq 2\exp{(-n^{\gamma}\log\beta_{mix}^{-1})}\quad\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$ .}

(84)

We call this
\gamma
its geometric index, and
\beta_{mix}
the mixing rate (note that
\beta_{mix}
may depend on the true regression parameter
\beta^{*}
if
\{x_{t}\}
is endogenous). In applications, usually
\gamma\leq 1
. If
\gamma=1
,
\{x_{t}\}
is said to be
geometrically

\beta
-mixing.

Several more mixing notions exist in literature. For a connection between properties of spectral density and mixing, see
(
Bradley 2005
, sections 6,7)
. In particular, for a stationary, centered Gaussian sequence
\{(x_{t},y_{t})\}
, we can connect the stability factors to the mixing coefficients as follows:

Proposition A.3
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(f_{X})\leq||\Sigma_{x}||_{2}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{X}(l),\quad\mathcal{M}(f_{X,Y})\leq\sqrt{||\Sigma_{x}||||\Sigma_{y}||}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{X,Y}(l).

(85)

This follows from
(
Wong et al. 2020
, page 32)
and the equivalence of
\alpha
and
\rho
mixing for Gaussian processes
(
Kolmogorov and Rozanov 1960
, Theorem 2)
. In particular, if the mixing coefficients
\{\alpha_{X}(l)\}
(or equivalently
\{\rho_{X}(l)\}
) are summable, then
\mathcal{M}(f_{X})<\infty
. This is satisfied for many Gaussian processes. Further, denoting the mixing coefficients of the joint process
\{(x_{t},y_{t})\}
by
\alpha_{(X,Y)}
(not to be confused with
\alpha_{X,Y}
), it follows that
\alpha_{X},\alpha_{Y},\alpha_{X,Y}\leq\alpha_{(X,Y)}
.
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
, among others, prefers to work with the stronger assumption that the joint process
\{(x_{t},y_{t})\}
is mixing, hence relies on coefficients
\alpha_{(X,Y)}
. Note that, in the non Gaussian case,
\alpha
and
\rho
mixing are usually not equivalent and
\rho
mixing is often too restrictive for many time series. In those cases,
\beta
-mixing, though stronger than
\alpha
-mixing, is an acceptable compromise. It would be interesting to connect the stability factor
\mathcal{M}(f_{X})
with some decay condition on the mixing coefficients, in those cases. For multivariate processes (which is of interest in our case), theoretical results connecting spectral properties with the mixing properties of stationary processes are sparse in the literature

(
Cheng and Pourahmadi 1993
)
.

There exist concentration inequalities in terms of mixing coefficients in literature. For the Gaussian case, we can write a concentration bound similar to
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
, Proposition 2.4)
by replacing the stability factors by the mixing coefficients (this leads to
(
Wong et al. 2020
, Propositions 2 and 3)
). Going beyond Gaussianity poses significant problems to the mixing framework, since tight concentration bounds are not easily available. Using these bounds, in turn, leads to suboptimal consistency rates or suboptimal minimum sample size required to achieve that rate (with high probability). The key concentration inequality used in this case, holds for Subweibull random variables under
\beta
-mixing, and is given by
(
Wong et al. 2020
, lemma 13)
, which itself is a modification of a quite general result in
(
Merlevède et al. 2011
)
(such results are few and far in between).

Proposition A.4
.

Lemma 13,
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
.

Let
\{x_{t}\}
be a centered, strictly stationary, one dimensional, Subweibull(
\gamma_{2}
/2) process with Subweibull norm bounded by
K
. Further, assume that it is
sub
-geometrically
\beta
-mixing with mixing rate
\beta_{mix}
and sub-geometric index
\gamma_{1}
. Let
n\geq 4
and
t>1/n
. Also, let
1/\gamma_{1}+2/\gamma_{2}=1/\gamma
and suppose
\gamma<1
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\sum_{t=1}^{n}x_{t}|>Knt\right)\leq n\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}^{2}}\min\left\{(nt)^{\gamma},nt^{2}\right\}\right],

(86)

wherein
\mathsf{C}>0
is a constant that depends on
\beta_{mix}
,
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
.

Remark A.2
.

In Proposition (
A.4
), we specifically assumed the process
\{x_{t}\}
has a SubWeibull tail index
\gamma_{2}/2
, and not
\gamma_{2}
, in order to make a direct comparison to Proposition (
3.1
). In the latter case, the Subweibull terms are essentially squared, which leads to their tail index being halved
(
Wong et al. 2020
, Lemma 6)
.Hence the term
2/\gamma_{2}
appears in the right hand side of the concentration inequalities in (
3.1
). By comparison, in the right hand side of Proposition (
A.4
), we find the term
\gamma
instead of
\gamma_{2}/2
. Since this
\gamma<\gamma_{2}/2
, this concentration bound is suboptimal. Another drawback of Proposition (
A.4
) is that the dependence factor
\mathsf{C}^{-2}
is intractable compared to ours, even for the special case of SubWeibull VAR.

Remark A.3
.

As noted, this inequality does not hold for all
t>0
. However, the precondition
t>1/n
is extremely mild and boils down to the sample size
n
being bigger than a finite constant (that does not scale with dimension) in applications. Also, a factor of
n
still remains on the right hand side, which is sub-optimal. However, this is not a big price to pay. In fact, the exponential term usually dominates. For completeness, we derive consistency rates for SubWeibull stochastic regressions in Appendix (
D
).

Appendix B
Further Concepts Related to the Penalty Norm.

Here, we present some general concepts related to sparsity inducing norms, since many interesting norms in practice are
not
decomposable (unlike the
\ell_{1}
norm) and require additional technical machinery.

Definition B.1
.

A norm
\mathcal{R}
is called
decomposable
, if for any
S\subseteq\{1,...,p\}
, for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have
\mathcal{R}(v)=\mathcal{R}(v_{S})+\mathcal{R}(v_{S^{c}})
.

Definition B.2
.

Following
(
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
)
, let
\mathcal{A}
be a collection of
atoms
that is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Suppose no element
a\in\mathcal{A}
lies in the convex hull of the other elements
conv(A-\{a\})
, i.e., the elements of
\mathcal{A}
are the extreme points of
conv(A)
. Let
||x||_{\mathcal{A}}
denote the gauge of
\mathcal{A}
, i.e.
||x||_{\mathcal{A}}=\inf\{t>0:x\in t\cdot conv(\mathcal{A})\}
.The gauge is always a convex, extended-real valued function for any set
\mathcal{A}
. By convention this function evaluates to
+\infty
if
x
does not lie in the affine hull of
conv(\mathcal{A})
. We will assume without loss, that the centroid of
conv(\mathcal{A})
is at the origin, as this can be achieved by appropriate re-centering. With this assumption the gauge function may be recast as:

\displaystyle||x||_{\mathcal{A}}=\inf\{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}c_{a}:x=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}c_{a}a,c_{a}\geq 0,\forall a\in\mathcal{A}\},

(87)

with the sum being replaced by an integral when
A
is uncountable. If
A
is centrally symmetric about the origin (i.e.,
a\in\mathcal{A}
if and only if
-a\in\mathcal{A}
) we have that
||\cdot||_{\mathcal{A}}
is a norm, which we call
the
atomic
norm induced by
\mathcal{A}
. Henceforth, we assume that
||\cdot||_{\mathcal{A}}
is a norm. These norms are extremely useful because they need not be decomposable, but still cover a rich class of norms (see
(
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
;
Bhaskar et al. 2013
)
).

Remark B.1
.

We can also induce sparsity via non-convex penalties like the SCAD and MCP
(
Fan and Li 2001
;
Zhang 2010
)
. It is known that in certain high-dimensional regimes, the estimation error of nonconvex penalties like SCAD, MCP scales roughly in the same order as LASSO. Of course these results are established with iid data. However, they can easily be extended to our setup as well, since only the algebraic arguments need to be modified — the probabilistic arguments remain the same.

Appendix C
Stochastic Regression under Gaussianity.

In this section, we consider the stochastic regression model (
3
) under Gaussianity and the previously defined estimation procedures —the Dantzig selector and the LASSO— subject to generic regularizers
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
. First, we derive consistency results for the Dantzig selector — the results for LASSO are analogous.

C.1
The Dantzig selector

The Dantzig selector defined in (
8
) has been extensively studied in literature for
\mathcal{R}()=||\cdot||_{1}
and then extended at substantial depth
(
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
;
Chen and Banerjee 2015
;
Chen and Banerjee 2016
)
, to the case of general regularizers, for iid data. To ensure consistency, a first and a second order conditions need to hold with high probability
(
Wainwright 2019
)
. Specifically, the quantity
\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(X^{\top}\mathcal{E}/n\right)
needs to concentrate around zero (
deviation condition
), and also the minimum eigenvalue of the sample Gram matrix
X^{\top}X/n
restricted to a small set, needs to be bounded uniformly away from zero (
Restricted Eigenvalue
(RE) condition). These conditions are stated precisely below:

Proposition C.1
.

Deviation condition:

There is a constant
c_{0}>0
such that, for
n\geq c_{0}w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{X^{\top}\mathcal{E}}{n}\right)\geq 4\pi[\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}\mathcal{M}(f_{X},1)+\mathcal{M}(f_{\epsilon})+\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{M}(f_{X,\epsilon})]\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))}{n}}\right]

(88)

\displaystyle\leq 6\exp[-w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))],

(89)

where
w[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]
is the Gaussian width of the unit norm ball
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)
. The terms
\mathcal{M}(f_{X},1)
,
\mathcal{M}(f_{\epsilon})
and
\mathcal{M}(f_{X,\epsilon})
quantify the temporal dependence due to a one-dimensional projection of the covariate process
\{x_{t}\}
, the noise process
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
, and the cross-dependence between the two, respectively —see Appendix (
3.4
).

Proposition C.2
.

RE condition.

Suppose
\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})>0
and
c_{0}^{\prime}>0
is a constant. Let the tangent cone at
\beta^{*}
be denoted by
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{R}}(\beta^{*})=\mathrm{cone}\left(\{v:\mathcal{R}(v+\beta^{*})\leq\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})\}\right)
. Then, a sample size of
n\geq 16c_{0}^{\prime}\left\{\frac{\mathcal{M}(f_{X})}{\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})}\right\}^{2}w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})
is sufficient to guarantee that

\displaystyle\underset{v\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}}{inf}\frac{v^{\top}X^{\top}Xv}{n}\geq\alpha_{RE},

(90)

with probability at least
1-2\exp[-w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})]
, where the restricted eigenvalue is
\alpha_{RE}=\pi\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})
. Further,
\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})
may be replace by
\Lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{X})
/2, if required.

Remark C.1
.

The deviation condition implies that, under the scaling
w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))/n\rightarrow\infty
, the deviation term
\mathcal{R}^{*}(X^{\top}\mathcal{E}/n)
concentrates around zero. The RE condition, on the other hand requires the sample size
n
to scale with the size of the spherical cap of the tangent cone
\mathcal{T}
. These results are standard by now in case of iid data,
the only difference in our setting being the price paid for temporal dependence. Specifically, we consider the dependence factors appearing in the deviation and RE conditions. Ignoring the reverse compatibility factor
\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}
(it is usually bounded by an absolute constant), the dependence factor from the deviation condition is
\mathcal{M}(f_{X},1)+\mathcal{M}(f_{\epsilon})+\mathcal{M}(f_{X,\epsilon})
. This dependence factor appears in Proposition 3.2,
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
)
. It also matches the dependence factor in Proposition 2,
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
. To see this, note that, under the assumption the Gaussian process
\{(x_{t},y_{t})\}
(equivalently
\{(x_{t},\epsilon_{t})\}
by linearity) is mixing with coefficients
\{\alpha(l)\}
that sum to
\alpha
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(f_{X},1)+\mathcal{M}(f_{\epsilon})+\mathcal{M}(f_{X,\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left\{||\Sigma_{X}||_{2}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\sqrt{||\Sigma_{X}||_{2}\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}\right\}

(91)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\alpha}{2}\left(||\Sigma_{X}||_{2}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\right)\quad\text{(A.M.-G.M. inequality)}

(92)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\alpha}{2}\left(||\Sigma_{X}||_{2}+2\sigma_{y}^{2}+2\beta^{*\top}\Sigma_{X}\beta^{*}\right)

(93)

\displaystyle\leq 3\alpha\left(\sigma_{y}^{2}+(1+||\beta^{*}||)||\Sigma_{X}||_{2}\right).

(94)

However, the dependence factor from the RE condition is
\left\{\mathcal{M}(f_{X})/\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})\right\}^{2}
, which is worse than that appearing in Proposition 3.1,
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
)
, since
\mathcal{M}(f_{X},2k)
can be much smaller than
\mathcal{M}(f_{X})
, if
k
(which denotes the sparsity of
\beta^{*}
) is much smaller than
p
. However, this is due to the decomposability of the
\ell_{1}
norm, which gives rise to a cone set that can be
directly
approximated by (contained within) a
k
-sparse set (lemma F.1), whose Gaussian width is easy to compute. However, this is not the case for non-decomposable norms. In case of
endogenous
processes, such as VAR, the spectrum
f_{X}
depends on the sparse
\beta^{*}
; then, we can estimate the stability factors in terms of the low dimensional parameter
\beta^{*}
, and thus control them. Note that, in case of
\alpha
-mixing, we have (using the above notation),
\mathcal{M}(f_{X})\leq\alpha||\Sigma_{X}||_{2}=\alpha\Lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{X})
, and, as remarked in Proposition (
C.2
), replace
\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})
by
\Lambda_{\min}(f_{X})/2
. Hence, we can replace the factor
\left\{\mathcal{M}(f_{X})/\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})\right\}^{2}
by
\left\{\alpha\Lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{X})/\Lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{X})\right\}^{2}
, which matches the factor
``\eta^{-2}"
in Proposition 3,
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
.

Proposition C.3
.

Theoretical consistency of the Dantzig selector.

Suppose that the tuning parameter
\lambda_{n}
and the sample size
n
satisfy the following bounds:

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=4\pi[\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}\mathcal{M}(f_{X},1)+\mathcal{M}(f_{\epsilon})+\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{M}(f_{X,\epsilon})\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))}{n}},

(95)

\displaystyle n\geq\max\left\{c_{0}w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)),16c_{0}^{\prime}\left\{\frac{\mathcal{M}(f_{X})}{\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})}\right\}^{2}w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})\right\}

(96)

\displaystyle\succsim\left\{\frac{\mathcal{M}(f_{X})}{\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})}\right\}^{2}\max\left\{w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)),w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})\right\}.

(97)

Then, the penalized estimate
\hat{\beta}
satisfies:

\displaystyle||\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*}||\leq\frac{2\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})}{\alpha_{RE}},\quad\mathcal{R}(v)\leq\frac{2\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}(\mathcal{T})}{\alpha_{RE}},\quad\text{(Estimation error)},

(98)

\displaystyle\frac{v^{\top}X^{\top}Xv}{n}\leq\frac{4\lambda_{n}^{2}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})}{\alpha_{RE}}\quad\text{(Prediction error)}.

(99)

with probability at least
1-6\exp[-w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))]-2\exp[-w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})]
. Here
c_{0}
and
c_{0}^{\prime}
are constants that appear in the deviation and RE conditions respectively.

C.2
The LASSO

The LASSO type estimator is the solution to the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}=\underset{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}{\mathrm{\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}}}\quad\frac{1}{n}||Y-X\beta||^{2}+\lambda_{n}\mathcal{R}(\beta).

(100)

Here the penalty is given by a generic norm
\mathcal{R}()
which reflects our beliefs about the underlying structure about the parameter
\beta^{*}
in the model ((
3
)). Also,
\lambda_{n}
is a tuning parameter. This estimator has been shown to be consistent in the context of Gaussian stochastic regression, using
\ell_{1}
norm
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
)
. We extend the result from
\ell_{1}
to a general penalty
\mathcal{R}
. As we will see, the LASSO and Dantzig are virtually equivalent, with the same consistency rates, for any norm
\mathcal{R}
. The only difference is the cone of anti-concentration
\mathcal{T}
, given in Proposition (
C.2
), will be replaced by a larger cone
{C}
given by

\displaystyle{C}={C}(\beta^{*}):=\mathrm{cone}\{u:\mathcal{R}(u)/2+\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})-\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*}+u)\geq 0\}.

(101)

In case
\mathcal{R}
is decomposable,
\mathcal{T}\subseteq{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}(J,3)
, where
\mathfrak{C}(J,3)=\{v:\mathcal{R}(v_{J^{c}})\leq 3\mathcal{R}(v_{J})\}
is the standard cone introduced in
(
Bickel et al. 2009
)
. We may prove Proposition (
C.2
) with
\mathcal{T}
replaced by
{C}
. Thus, we first give deterministic recovery bounds for the LASSO.

Proposition C.4
.

Assume the following:

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\geq 4\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{X^{\top}\mathcal{E}}{n}\right)\quad\text{(Deviation Condition)},

(102)

and that, there exists and
\alpha_{RE}>0
such that

\displaystyle\underset{v\in{C}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}}{inf}\frac{1}{n}||Xv||^{2}\geq\alpha_{RE}\quad\text{(RE condition)}

(103)

Then, the penalized estimate
\hat{\beta}
satisfies:

\displaystyle||\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*}||\leq\frac{3\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}({C})}{2\alpha_{RE}},

(104)

\displaystyle\frac{||X(\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*})||^{2}}{n}\leq\frac{9\lambda_{n}^{2}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}({C})}{2\alpha_{RE}}.

(105)

Of course, the deviation and RE conditions can be shown to hold with high probability as in case of the Dantzig selector. The tuning parameter and sample size scales similarly.

Remark C.2
.

Of course, all our results carry through for a multi-response regression
y_{t}^{\top}=x_{t}^{\top}B+\epsilon_{t}^{\top}
where
\{x_{t}\}
and
\{y_{t}\}
are now random processes in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathbb{R}^{q}
respectively, and
B
is a
p\times q
matrix of regression parameters. We simply vectorize the process as follows:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}^{\top}\\
y_{2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
y_{n}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}}_{Y}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{\top}\\
x_{2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}}_{X}\cdot B+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon_{1}^{\top}\\
\epsilon_{2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\epsilon_{n}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}}_{E}

(106)

So that the matrix form of the process can be written in vectorized form:
vec(Y)=(I_{q}\otimes X)vec(B)+vec(E)
.

Appendix D
Stochastic regression with Subweibull tails and mixing.

The concentration (
A.4
) compares favorably with the Gaussian case
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
, Proposition 2.4)
,
(
Wong et al. 2020
, lemma 11)
), but for the factor
n
in the right hand side. Also, as it will be clear from the proofs that utilize this result, the precondition
t\geq 1/n
implies
n
is bigger than a finite constant (as opposed to scaling with dimension
p
, or quantities related to
p
). Hence, this will not be mentioned explicitly in statements giving finite sample bounds in terms of dimension

, to avoid clutter. For a more explicit derivation, see e.g
(
Wong et al. 2020
, Proposition 8)
.

Suppose now we have the regression model in Section (
2
) but instead of the processes

\{x_{t}\}
,
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
(and hence the response
\{y_{t}\}
) being Gaussian, we make the following probabilistic assumptions about the joint process
\{(x_{t},\epsilon_{t})\}
:

•

The process
\{(x_{t},\epsilon_{t})\}
is centered and strictly stationary. Also,
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{t}|x_{t}]=0
for each
t
.

•

The process
\{(x_{t},\epsilon_{t})\}
is Subweibull(
\gamma_{2}
) with
||(x,\epsilon)||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}\leq K
.

•

The process
\{(x_{t},\epsilon_{t})\}
is (sub)geometrically
\beta
-mixing with rate
\beta_{mix}
, and exponent
\gamma_{1}
.

•

We assume
\gamma<1
, where

\displaystyle\gamma:=\left[1/\gamma_{1}+2/\gamma_{2}\right]^{-1}.

(107)

Remark D.1
.

By
(
Wong et al. 2020
, Fact 1)
and linearity, assuming
\{(x_{t},\epsilon_{t})\}
is mixing is equivalent to assuming
\{(x_{t},y_{t})\}
is mixing.

Remark D.2
.

In applications, usually
\gamma_{2}\leq 2
. In that case,
\gamma<1
is immediate.

Remark D.3
.

Instead of assuming
\{(x_{t},\epsilon_{t})\}
is jointly Subweibull, it is sufficient to assume the marginals
\{x_{t}\}
and
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
are Subweibull (see
(
Wong et al. 2020
;
Kuchibhotla and Chakrabortty 2018
)
). Let the Subweibull norms of
\{x_{t}\}
and
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
be bounded above by
K_{x}
,
K_{\epsilon}
respectively. Let
v\in\mathbb{B}_{2}
be split as
v=(v_{1},v_{2})
with
v_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
. Then

\displaystyle||v^{\top}(x,\epsilon)||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}

\displaystyle=||v_{1}^{\top}x+v_{2}\epsilon||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}

(108)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}2^{1/\gamma_{2}}\left(||v_{1}^{\top}x||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}+||v_{2}\epsilon||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}\right)

(109)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}2^{1/\gamma_{2}}\left(||v_{1}||||x||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}+||v_{2}|||\epsilon||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}\right)

(110)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{1/\gamma_{2}}\left(||x||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}+||\epsilon||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}\right).

(111)

We used (a) Lemma A.3,
(
Götze et al. 2019
)
, and (b) lemma 12,
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
. Hence,
K\leq 2^{1/\gamma_{2}}(K_{x}+K_{\epsilon})
. Also, it is equivalent to assume the Subweibull norms of
\{x_{t}\}
and response
\{y_{t}\}
are bounded by
K_{x}
and
K_{y}
(say), since, by linearity,
||\epsilon||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}\leq||y||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}+||x^{\top}\beta^{*}||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}\leq K_{y}+||\beta^{*}||K_{x}
. Since we actually observe the samples
\{(x_{t},y_{t})\}
,
1\leq t\leq n
, we might as well make assumptions on the same. Note that, in this case the linear factor is
||\beta^{*}||=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{s})
, if
\beta^{*}
is
s
-sparse.

Remark D.4
.

The pair
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
encompasses a whole family of problems with the first coefficient measuring the strength of temporal dependence, and the second, measuring the heaviness of the tails of the stochastic processes. As noted in
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
, the challenging cases are the ones where
\gamma_{1}<1
signifying strong temporal dependence and
\gamma_{2}<2
signifying tails heavier than a Gaussian distribution. For the usual
\ell_{1}
LASSO with independent Subweibull elements (i.e.
\gamma_{1}=\infty
), see Theorem 4.5,
(
Kuchibhotla and Chakrabortty 2018
)
.

Remark D.5
.

The sub-geometric index
\gamma_{1}
and the the Subweibull tail index
\gamma_{2}
do not depend on underlying model parameters. However, the mixing rate
\beta_{mix}
of the joint process
\{(x_{t},\epsilon_{t})\}
often
does
(endogeneity). This is often an unavoidable feature of time series in general.

We can estimate the regression parameter
\beta^{*}
using either LASSO or the Dantzig selector as in the Gaussian case. We only need to show that the deviation and RE conditions still hold with high probability. However, unlike the Gaussian case, the temporal dependence factors that appear in the following propositions and proofs, will be a function of
\beta_{mix}
,
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, but the exact form is not explicit. This is clearly undesirable, but we feel it is unavoidable in the current setting, since we borrow our concentration results (Proposition (
A.4
)) from
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
, which suffers from the same drawback. On the other hand, to the best of our knowledge, no explicit forms can be derived in general, without further simplifying assumptions.

Proposition D.1
.

Deviation Condition for Heavy Tails.
There is an absolute constant
c>0
, and a constant
\mathsf{C}_{dev}>0
depending on
\beta_{mix}
,
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
such that, for
n\geq n_{dev}:=\left(\mathsf{C}_{dev}^{2}cw^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]\right)^{2/\gamma-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{u^{\top}X^{\top}\mathcal{E}}{n}\right)>\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}K^{2}\mathsf{C}_{dev}\sqrt{\frac{cw^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]}{n}}\right]

(112)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[-w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))+\log n\right].

(113)

Remark D.6
.

The results are remarkably similar to Proposition (
C.1
). The only major difference is the exponent term
2/\gamma-1
appearing in the lower bound for the sample size. In the best case scenario, when the dependence is negligible (
\gamma_{1}\rightarrow\infty
) and the tails are close to being sub-Gaussian (
\gamma_{2}\rightarrow 2
), we have
\gamma\rightarrow 1
, so that the exponent
2/\gamma-1\rightarrow 1
. Hence the sample size scales just as in the independent sub-Gaussian case. However, the situation gets worse when
\gamma\rightarrow 0
.

Remark D.7
.

The ‘
\log n
’ term appearing in the right hand side of the last inequality is a pretty reasonable price to pay; for example, with usual
\ell_{1}
norm, i.e.
\mathcal{R}()=||\cdot||_{1}
, the quantity
w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))
is
\mathcal{O}(\log p)
, so with large enough constants, we have that the RHS is
\mathcal{O}(p^{-c})
assuming
p\gg n
, which is the usual error bound for the
\ell_{1}
-LASSO.

Next, we have to verify that the RE condition holds with high probability. So, we have the following proposition. (Again, the proof is similar to that of Proposition (
C.2
). The only difference is showing Step 1 of that proof, i.e. a single concentration bound.) To fix ideas, let us consider the Dantzig selector— the anti-concentration cone for the RE condition is
\mathcal{T}
in this case (see Proposition (
C.2
)).

Proposition D.2
.

RE Condition for Heavy Tails.
Assume
\Lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{x})>0
. Then, there is a absolute constant
c^{\prime}>0
and a constant
\mathsf{C}_{RE}>0
depending on
\beta_{mix}
,
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
, such that, for a minimum sample size

\displaystyle n\geq n_{RE}:=\left(\mathsf{C}_{RE}^{2}c^{\prime}\max\left\{1,\frac{16\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}K^{4}}{\Lambda_{\min}^{2}(\Sigma_{X})}\right\}w^{2}[\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})\right)^{1/\gamma},

(114)

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\underset{v\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}}{\inf}\frac{v^{\top}X^{\top}Xv}{n}\geq\alpha_{RE}\right]\geq 1-\exp[-w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})+\log n],

(115)

where the restricted eigenvalue is
\alpha_{RE}=\Lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{x})/2
.

Remark D.8
.

Note that, similar to the deviation condition (
D.1
), the only change is the exponent
1/\gamma
appearing in the expression for the minimum sample size requirement. Again, when we approach independence (
\gamma_{1}\rightarrow\infty
) and sub-gaussian tails
\gamma_{2}\rightarrow 2
, so that the exponent
1/\gamma\rightarrow 1
, the minimum sample size for RE matches Proposition (
C.2
).

Proposition D.3
.

Theoretical consistency for Subweibull tails.
Suppose the sample size and tuning parameter satisfies

\displaystyle n\geq\max\left\{{n_{dev},n_{RE}}\right\}\quad\lambda_{n}=\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}K^{2}\mathsf{C}_{dev}\sqrt{\frac{cw^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]}{n}}.

(116)

Then, the penalized estimate
\hat{\beta}
satisfies:

\displaystyle||\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*}||\leq\frac{2\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})}{\alpha_{RE}},\quad\mathcal{R}(v)\leq\frac{2\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}(\mathcal{T})}{\alpha_{RE}},\quad\text{(Estimation error)},

(117)

\displaystyle\frac{v^{\top}X^{\top}Xv}{n}\leq\frac{4\lambda_{n}^{2}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})}{\alpha_{RE}}\quad\text{(Prediction error)}.

(118)

with probability at least
1-n\exp[-w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))]-n\exp[-w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})]
.

Remark D.9
.

Thus, the message is that, in presence of temporal dependence and heavy tails, the price we pay in terms of sample size
n
is an exponent of
\max\left\{2/\gamma-1,1/\gamma\right\}=2/\gamma-1
(since
\gamma<1
by assumption) which, in the best case scenario is close to 1 (as
\gamma\rightarrow 1
) and becomes arbitrarily large in the worst case scenario (when
\gamma\rightarrow 0
). The order of consistency is, however, same as the Gaussian case. One issue is that the temporal dependence factors
\mathsf{C}_{dev}
and
\mathsf{C}_{RE}
are hard to quantify unlike the Gaussian case, where they can be written in terms of the “spike”
\mathcal{M}(f_{X})
of the spectral density
f_{X}
. However, if we have a simple model like a linear time series, for example, then the dependence factor is easy to quantify, as we see in the case of Subweibull VAR models.

Appendix E
Proofs of propositions.

E.1
Proof of Proposition (
3.1
).

The proof is virtually identical to lemmas 5.1, 5.2 in
(
Zheng and Raskutti 2019
)
. The only change is that, instead of the Hanson-Wright inequality for the subgaussian case
(
Rudelson et al. 2013
)
, we use a version for the Subweibull case (
(
Götze et al. 2019
, Proposition 1.1)
). We reproduce the proof technique in
(
Zheng and Raskutti 2019
)
in its full generality.

Let
X_{t}=\sum_{j\geq 0}\Psi_{j}\epsilon_{t-j}
be a linear process in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
where the noise components
\epsilon_{tj}
are independent Subweibull random variables with
\|{\epsilon_{tj}}\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\leq\tau
. Suppose we have data
\{X_{0},\cdots,X_{T}\}
. Let
B
be a
p\times p
symmetric matrix (the symmetry is required)—we consider the chaos term
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X_{t}^{\top}BX_{t}
. In our example, we need a concentration bound for the term

\displaystyle u^{\top}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X_{t}X_{t}^{\top}\right]u=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X_{t}^{\top}uu^{\top}X_{t},

(119)

so that
B=uu^{\top}
in our example.

The chaos term
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X_{t}^{\top}BX_{t}
can be broken into 3 parts, as per lemma 5.2 in
(
Zheng and Raskutti 2019
)
. We have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X_{t}^{\top}BX_{t}=&\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Psi_{j}\epsilon_{t-j}\right)^{\top}B\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Psi_{j}\epsilon_{t-j}\right)\\
=&\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{t+m}\Psi_{j}\epsilon_{t-j}\right)^{\top}B\left(\sum_{j=0}^{t+m}\Psi_{j}\epsilon_{t-j}\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\sum_{j=t+m+1}^{\infty}\Psi_{j}\epsilon_{t-j}\right)^{\top}B\left(\sum_{j=t+m+1}^{\infty}\Psi_{j}\epsilon_{t-j}\right)\\
&+\frac{2}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{t+m}\Psi_{j}\epsilon_{t-j}\right)^{\top}B\left(\sum_{j=t+m+1}^{\infty}\Psi_{j}\epsilon_{t-j}\right)\\
\triangleq&E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3}.\end{split}

(120)

Note that, for the two cross product terms are equal because
B
is symmetric. Here,
m
is a positive integer to be chosen later. Then we can bound each
E_{i}
from its expectation separately, and
m
will be chosen to be sufficiently large later.

E.1.1
Bounding
E_{1}-\mathbb{E}(E_{1})
:

Fix an index
0\leq t\leq T-1
.
Let
\Theta^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(T+m)p}
and
{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{(T+m)p}
be defined as

\Theta^{(t)}=\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{t+m}^{(p)}&\cdots&\Psi_{0}^{(p)}&0&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix},{\epsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{-m}^{\top}&\cdots&\epsilon_{T-1}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}.

Then
E_{1}={\epsilon}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}\right){\epsilon}
, and by Proposition 1.1 in
(
Götze et al. 2019
)
, we only need to bound the operator norm and Frobenius norm of
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}
.

E.1.2
Bounding
\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}\right\|_{2}
:

For any unit vector
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{(T+m)p}
,

\begin{split}u^{\top}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}v=&\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{i,j=1}^{t+m+1}u^{(i)\top}\Psi_{t+m+1-i}^{\top}B\Psi_{t+m+1-j}v^{(j)}\\
=&\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i,j=1}^{T+m}u^{(i)\top}\left[\sum_{t=(i\vee j-m-1)\vee 0}^{T-1}\Psi_{t+m+1-i}^{\top}B\Psi_{t+m+1-j}\right]v^{(j)}\\
\leq&\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i,j=1}^{T+m}\|u^{(i)}\|_{2}\|v^{(j)}\|_{2}\|B\|_{2}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\left\|\Psi_{|i-j|+l}\right\|_{2}\left\|\Psi_{l}\right\|_{2},\end{split}

where
u^{(i)}=(u_{(i-1)p+1},\dots,u_{ip})
, for
1\leq i\leq(T+m)
etc. Let
{\alpha}_{i}=\left\|\Psi_{i}\right\|_{2}
, and
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{(T+m)\times(T+m)}
be defined as

\Gamma_{ij}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\alpha}_{|i-j|+k}{\alpha}_{k}
, then

\begin{split}u^{\top}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}v\leq\frac{\|B\|_{2}}{T}(\|u^{(1)}\|_{2},\dots,\|u^{(T+m)}\|_{2})\Gamma\begin{pmatrix}\|v^{(1)}\|_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\|v^{(T+m)}\|_{2}\end{pmatrix}\leq\frac{\|B\|_{2}\Lambda_{\max}(\Gamma)}{T}.\end{split}

Thus we only need to bound
\Lambda_{\max}(\Gamma)
. Applying Lemma C4 in
(
Zheng and Raskutti 2019
)
, the largest eigenvalue of Toeplitz matrix
\Gamma
can be bounded by

\begin{split}\Lambda_{\max}(\Gamma)\leq\text{ess}\sup_{\lambda}\left|\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{\alpha}_{|l|+j}{\alpha}_{j}e^{il\lambda}\right|\\
\leq 2\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{l+j}{\alpha}_{j}=2\mathsf{C}(\Psi).\end{split}

So we get,

\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{2\mathsf{C}(\Psi)\|B\|_{2}}{T}
.

E.1.3
Bounding
\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}\right\|_{F}^{2}
:

We have

\begin{split}\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{T^{2}}\sum_{s,t=0}^{T-1}\left|\text{tr}\left(\Theta^{(s)\top}B\Theta^{(s)}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}\right)\right|,\end{split}

Since
B
is symmetric using its spectral decomposition
B=P^{\top}\Lambda P
with orthogonal
P
and diagonal
\Lambda
, we get

\begin{split}\left|\text{tr}\left(\Theta^{(s)\top}B\Theta^{(s)}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}\right)\right|\\
\left|\text{tr}\left(P\Theta^{(s)}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}\Theta^{(s)\top}P^{\top}\Lambda\right)\right|\\
\leq\|B\|_{tr}\left\|\Theta^{(s)}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}\Theta^{(s)\top}\right\|_{2}\\
\leq\|B\|_{tr}\|B\|_{2}\left\|\Theta^{(s)}\Theta^{(t)\top}\right\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

Also,

\begin{split}\sum_{s,t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\Theta^{(s)}\Theta^{(t)\top}\right\|_{2}^{2}\\
=\sum_{s,t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{t\wedge s+m}\Psi_{t+m-i}\Psi_{s+m-i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\\
\leq\sum_{s,t=0}^{T-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t\wedge s+m}{\alpha}_{t+m-i}{\alpha}_{s+m-i}\right)^{2}\\
=\sum_{s,t=0}^{T-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t\wedge s+m-1}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{|t-s|+i}\right)^{2}\\
\leq\sum_{l=0}^{T-1}2(T-l)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{l+i}\right)^{2}\\
\leq 2T\left[\sum_{l=0}^{T-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{l+i}\right)\right]^{2}\leq 2T\mathsf{C}^{2}(\Psi)\end{split}

Hence we get
\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Theta^{(t)\top}B\Theta^{(t)}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{2\mathsf{C}^{2}(\Psi)\|B\|_{2}\|B\|_{tr}}{T}
. By Proposition 1.1 in
(
Götze et al. 2019
)
, we arrive at

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|E_{1}-\mathbb{E}(E_{1})\right|>\delta\right)\leq 2\exp\left[-c\min\left\{\left(\frac{T\delta}{\tau^{2}\mathsf{C}(\Psi)\|B\|_{2}}\right)^{\alpha/2},\frac{T\delta^{2}}{\tau^{4}\mathsf{C}^{2}(\Psi)\|B\|_{2}\|B\|_{tr}}\right\}\right].

E.1.4
Bounding
E_{2}-\mathbb{E}(E_{2})
:

We will show that
\left|E_{2}-\mathbb{E}(E_{2})\right|
is a Subweibull random variable whose Subweibull norm can be bounded above when
m
is large enough. First we bound
\|{E_{2}}\|_{\psi_{\alpha/2}}
. We have

\begin{split}\|{E_{2}}\|_{\psi_{\alpha/2}}\leq\frac{2^{1+\alpha/2}\|B\|_{2}}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\sum_{j=t+m+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\|{||\epsilon_{t-j}||_{2}}\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\right)^{2}\\
\leq\frac{2^{1+\alpha/2}||B||_{2}\|{||\epsilon_{0}||_{2}}\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\sum_{j=t+m+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\right)^{2}\\
\leq 2^{1+\alpha/2}||B||_{2}\|{||\epsilon_{0}||_{2}}\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\left(\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\right)^{2}.\end{split}

By corollary A.5 in
(
Götze et al. 2019
)
, we have, for some constant
c(\alpha)
that depends on
\alpha
, that

\|{E_{2}-\mathbb{E}(E_{2})}\|_{\psi_{\alpha/2}}\leq c(\alpha)\|{E_{2}}\|_{\psi_{\alpha/2}}\\
\leq c(\alpha)2^{1+\alpha/2}||B||_{2}\|{||\epsilon_{0}||_{2}}\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\left(\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{\tau^{2}\mathsf{C}(\Psi)||B||_{2}}{T}

for
m
large enough, since stability ensures
\sum_{j\geq 0}\alpha_{j}<\infty
(hence the tail of this series converges to 0). Note that
\|{||\epsilon_{0}||_{2}}\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}
may grow with the dimension
p
, however, in the non-asymptotic framework, the dimension
p
is large but finite, and
m
is completely in our control, so we will choose
m
to be correspondingly large enough. Finally by definition of a Subweibull random variable, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|E_{2}-\mathbb{E}(E_{2})\right|>\delta\right)\leq 2\exp\left[-c\left(\frac{\delta T}{\tau^{2}\mathsf{C}(\Psi)\|B\|_{2}}\right)^{\alpha/2}\right].

E.1.5
Bounding
E_{3}-\mathbb{E}(E_{3})
:

The term
E_{3}
is similar to
E_{2}
in that it is also a "cross-product" of two sums, the difference being that one of the sums is finite. Hence, one can bound
E_{3}
similarly by noting that
\mathbb{E}(E_{3})=0
and adapting Lemma 6 in
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
, that
\|{XY}\|_{\psi_{\alpha/2}}\leq 2^{2/\alpha}\|{X}\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\|{Y}\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}
for two Subweibull random variables
X
and
Y
(in order to deal with the cross product as before).

In conclusion, for any
\delta>0
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X_{t}^{\top}BX_{t}-\text{tr}(B\Sigma_{X}(0))\right|>\delta\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbb{P}\left(|E_{i}-\mathbb{E}(E_{i})|>\delta/3\right)\\
\leq 6\exp\left[-c\min\left\{\left(\frac{T\delta}{\tau^{2}\mathsf{C}(\Psi)||B||_{2}}\right)^{\alpha/2},\frac{T\delta^{2}}{\tau^{4}\mathsf{C}^{2}(\Psi)\|B\|_{2}\|B\|_{tr}}\right\}\right].\end{split}

The proof of the deviation condition, is similar, and mimics
(
Zheng and Raskutti 2019
, lemma 5.1)
.

We point out that there exists a “sketch” of proving concentration inequalities for linear Subweibull processes in
(
Lin and Michailidis 2020
, Appendix C)
. However, the authors appear to make a serious mistake in their reasoning: they assume that they can rotate a random vector to make its components independent: if a random vector
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}(x)=0
,
\mathbb{E}(xx^{\top})=\Sigma
, then
y=\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}x
satisfies
\mathbb{E}(y)=0
,
\mathbb{E}(yy^{\top})=I_{p}
. However,
this does not imply that the components of y are independent
. This works most notably when
x
is a Gaussian vector (this is just the trick used for proving
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
, Proposition 2.4)
, which the authors adapt). However, if
x
is
not
Gaussian, this trick fails, even for the subgaussian case (this was pointed out precisely in
(
Zheng and Raskutti 2019
)
). Hence, a standard Hanson-Wright inequality for independent random variables cannot be applied directly.

E.2
Proof of Proposition (
C.1
).

Proof.

We divide the proof into the following steps:

Step 1: Variational characterization of the norm : Note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{X^{\top}\mathcal{E}}{n}\right)=\underset{u\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)}{\sup}\frac{u^{\top}X^{\top}\mathcal{E}}{n}.

(121)

Step 2: Single deviation bound: Fix
u\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)
. Then
\{u^{\top}x_{t}\}
is a centered stationary Gaussian process with

f_{u^{\top}X}=u^{\top}f_{X}u
, and
f_{u^{\top}X,\epsilon}=u^{\top}f_{X,\epsilon}
, and, since we fixed
u\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)
, we have
\mathcal{M}(f_{u^{\top}X})\leq\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}\mathcal{M}(f_{X})
, and
\mathcal{M}(f_{u^{\top}X,\epsilon})\leq\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{M}(f_{X,\epsilon})
. By Proposition 2.4(b) in
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
)
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[u^{\top}X^{\top}\mathcal{E}/n>2\pi(\mathcal{M}(f_{u^{\top}X})+\mathcal{M}(f_{\epsilon})+\mathcal{M}(f_{u^{\top}X,\epsilon})t)]\leq 6\exp[-c_{1}n\min(t,t^{2})].

(122)

Step 3: Discretize : First,
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)\subseteq\mathbb{B}_{2}(0,\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}})=\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}\mathbb{B}_{2}
, so
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)
is totally bounded with respect to the usual Euclidean topology. In other words, it can be covered by finitely many
Euclidean
balls of any radius
\epsilon>0
of our choosing. Such a collection is called an
\epsilon
-net, and the
smallest
cardinality of such a collection is called the
\epsilon
-covering number. Choose a finite 1/4-net N of
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)
with the
smallest
cardinality (which corresponds to the 1/4-
covering
number of
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)
). We have,

\displaystyle\underset{u\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)}{\sup}\frac{u^{\top}X^{\top}\mathcal{E}}{n}\leq 2\underset{u\in N}{\sup}\frac{u^{\top}X^{\top}\mathcal{E}}{n}.

(123)

Step 4: Union bound: Since N is
finite
with cardinality
card(N)
(say), we have a finite union bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(X^{\top}\mathcal{E}/n\right)>4\pi(\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}\mathcal{M}(f_{X})+\mathcal{M}(f_{\epsilon})+\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{M}(f_{X,\epsilon}))t]

(124)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[\underset{u\in N}{\sup}\left(u^{\top}X^{\top}\mathcal{E}/n\right)>2\pi(\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}\mathcal{M}(f_{X})+\mathcal{M}(f_{\epsilon})+\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{M}(f_{X,\epsilon}))t]

(125)

\displaystyle\leq 6\exp[-c_{1}n\min(t,t^{2})+\log card(N)]

(126)

\displaystyle\leq 6\exp[-c_{1}n\min(t,t^{2})+c_{2}w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)],

(127)

where the last step follows from Sudakov’s minoration inequality (see e.g. Corollary 7.4.3,
(
Vershynin 2018
)
):
\log card(N)\leq c_{2}w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))
.

Finally, choose
n,t>0
so that

\displaystyle\min(t,t^{2})=t^{2},\quad-c_{1}n\min(t,t^{2})=(c_{2}+1)w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)).

(128)

Let
c_{0}=(c_{2}+1)/c_{1}
. Then
n\geq c_{0}w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))
and
t=\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1))}{n}}
. The proof is now complete.

∎

E.3
Proof of Proposition (
C.2
).

Proof.

Step 1: Single concentration bound: Fix
v\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}
. Then we may assume without loss, that
||v||=1
, since by definition of a cone,
v\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}
if and only if,
v/||v||\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}
. By Proposition 2.4(a) in
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|v^{\top}(X^{\top}X/n-\Sigma_{X}(0))v\right|\geq 2\pi\mathcal{M}(f_{X})t\right]\leq 2\exp[-c_{1}^{\prime}n\min(t,t^{2})].

(129)

Fix some
t_{0}>0
to be chosen later and note that
\min(t_{0},t_{0}^{2})\geq\min(1,t_{0}^{2})
.

Step 2 :Discretize the spherical cap
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}
. Note that
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{B}_{2}
, so it is totally bounded with respect to Euclidean topology. Using an
1/4
-net covering with the smallest cardinality, then taking a union bound, we get, as before

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\underset{v\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}}{\sup}\left|v^{\top}(X^{\top}X/n-\Sigma_{X}(0))v\right|\geq 4\pi\mathcal{M}(f_{X})t_{0}\right]

(131)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp[-c_{1}^{\prime}n\min(1,t_{0}^{2})+c_{2}^{\prime}w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})]\leq 2\exp[-w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})].

(132)

The last inequality holds whenever
c_{1}^{\prime}n\min(1,t_{0}^{2})\geq(c_{2}^{\prime}+1)w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})
, that is, the sample size
n
must satisfy
n\geq c_{0}^{\prime}\max(1,t_{0}^{-2})w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})
, where
c_{0}^{\prime}=(c_{2}^{\prime}+1)/c_{1}^{\prime}
.

Step 3: Decenter the quadratic form: By Proposition 2.3 in
(
Basu and Michailidis 2015
)
, we have
v^{\top}\Sigma_{X}(0)v\geq\Lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{X}(0))\geq 2\pi\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})
, for
||v||=1
. Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{v^{\top}X^{\top}Xv}{n}\geq 2\pi\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})-4\pi\mathcal{M}(f_{X})t_{0},\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2},

(133)

with probability at least
1-2\exp[-w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})]
. Finally, we set
t_{0}=\frac{\mathfrak{m}(f_{X})}{4\mathcal{M}(f_{X})}<1
and complete the proof.

∎

E.4
Proof of Proposition (
C.3
).

Proof.

Step 1: Since
\beta^{*}
is feasible, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{X^{\top}(y-X\beta^{*})}{n}\right)\leq\lambda_{n},

(134)

\displaystyle\text{i.e.}\quad\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{X^{\top}\mathcal{E}}{n}\right)\leq\lambda_{n}.

(135)

Step 2: Basic Inequality: Since
\hat{\beta}
is optimal and
\beta^{*}
is feasible,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\hat{\beta})\leq\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})

(136)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*}+v)\leq\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})\Rightarrow v\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{R}}(\beta^{*})

(137)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{R}}(\beta^{*})
is the tangent cone at
\beta^{*}
.

Step 3: RE condition : Now,
v\in\mathcal{T}
,if and only if
\frac{v}{||v||}\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\alpha_{RE}||v||^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}||Xv||^{2}=\frac{v^{\top}X^{\top}Xv}{n}\leq\mathcal{R}(v)\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{X^{\top}Xv}{n}\right).\quad\text{(H\"{o}lder's Inequality)}

(138)

Step 4: By triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{X^{\top}Xv}{n}\right)=\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{X^{\top}X(\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*})}{n}\right)

(139)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{X^{\top}(y-X\hat{\beta})}{n}\right)+\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{X^{\top}(y-X\beta^{*}))}{n}\right)\leq 2\lambda_{n},

(140)

which finally implies that

\displaystyle\alpha_{RE}||v||^{2}\leq\frac{v^{\top}X^{\top}Xv}{n}\leq 2\lambda_{n}\mathcal{R}(v)\Rightarrow||v||\leq\frac{2\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})}{\alpha_{RE}}.

(141)

It also follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(v)\leq\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})||v||\leq\frac{2\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}(\mathcal{T})}{\alpha_{RE}},

(142)

which in turn implies

\displaystyle\frac{v^{\top}X^{\top}Xv}{n}\leq 2\lambda_{n}\mathcal{R}(v)\leq\frac{4\lambda_{n}^{2}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})}{\alpha_{RE}}.

(143)

Using the probability bounds from the deviation and RE conditions now completes the proof.

∎

E.5
Proof of Proposition (
C.4
).

Proof.

We break up the proof in the following easy steps:

Step 1: Basic Inequality: We use the definition of
\hat{\beta}
and ((
3
)) to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}||y-X\hat{\beta}||^{2}+\lambda_{n}\mathcal{R}(\hat{\beta})\leq\frac{1}{n}||y-X\beta^{*}||^{2}+\lambda_{n}\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})

(144)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\frac{1}{n}||Xv||^{2}\leq\frac{2}{n}v^{\top}X^{\top}\mathcal{E}+\lambda_{n}[\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})-\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*}+v)]\quad(\text{where}\quad v=\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*})

(145)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\frac{1}{n}||Xv||^{2}\leq\mathcal{R}(v)\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{X^{\top}\mathcal{E}}{n}\right)+\lambda_{n}[\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})-\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*}+v)].\quad\text{(H\"{o}lder's Inequality)}

(146)

We note that a general version of Hölder’s inequality follows from the definition of the dual norm
\mathcal{R}^{*}
: for
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
we have
|u^{\top}v|\leq\mathcal{R}(u)\mathcal{R}^{*}(v)
.

Step 2 : Deviation bound: Using the deviation condition stated in the proposition, we get, using L.H.S and R.H.S to denote the left and right hand sides of the last inequality:

\displaystyle R.H.S\leq\lambda_{n}[\mathcal{R}(v)/2+\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})-\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*}+v)].

(148)

Step 3: Since the
L.H.S\geq 0
, the last step implies
v\in{C}
, where the cone
{C}
is described in the proposition.

Step 4: RE condition: From step 3, we see that that

\displaystyle\alpha_{RE}||v||^{2}\leq\frac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}||Xv||^{2}\leq\lambda_{n}\left[\frac{\mathcal{R}(v)}{2}+\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})-\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*}+v)\right]

(149)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\alpha_{RE}||v||^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}||Xv||^{2}\leq\lambda_{n}\frac{3\mathcal{R}(v)}{2}\quad\text{(Triangle Inequality)}.

(150)

The first inequality gives
||v||\leq\frac{3\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}({C})}{2\alpha_{RE}}
, where
\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}({C})
is the subspace compatibility constant. The second inequality therefore gives
||Xv||^{2}/n\leq\frac{9\lambda_{n}^{2}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}({C})}{2\alpha_{RE}}
. This completes the proof.

∎

E.6
Proof of Proposition (
D.1
).

Proof.

Step 1: Single concentration bound: Fix
u\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)
. Then
\{u^{\top}x_{t}\}
is a centered, one dimensional, strictly stationary, Subweibull(
\gamma_{2}
) process, with
||u^{\top}x||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}\leq\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}K
. Also,

||\epsilon||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}\leq K
. Then

\displaystyle||(u^{\top}x)\epsilon||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}/2}}\leq 2^{2/\gamma_{2}}||u^{\top}x||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}||\epsilon||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}\leq 2^{2/\gamma_{2}}\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}K^{2}.

(151)

Again,
\mathbb{E}[u^{\top}x_{t}\epsilon_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[u^{\top}x_{t}\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{t}|x_{t}]]=0
. In other words, the cross product sequence
\{u^{\top}x_{t}\epsilon_{t}\}
is a centered, strictly stationary, sub-geometrically
\beta
-mixing process with finite Subweibull(
\gamma_{2}/2
) norm. Further, by assumption,
1/\gamma=1/\gamma_{1}+1/(\gamma_{2}/2)=1/\gamma_{1}+2/\gamma_{2}
with
\gamma<1
. Hence, for
n\geq 4
,
t\geq 1/n
, we use Proposition (
A.4
) to get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{u^{\top}X^{\top}\mathcal{E}}{n}\right|>t\right]

(152)

\displaystyle\leq n\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}_{dev}^{2}}\min\left\{\left(\frac{nt}{K_{0}}\right)^{\gamma},\left(\frac{nt^{2}}{K_{0}^{2}}\right)\right\}\right].

(153)

Here
K_{0}=\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}K^{2}
and
\mathsf{C}_{dev}
depends on
\beta_{mix}
,
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
.

Step 2: Discretizing
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)
and taking union bounds as before, we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{R}^{*}\left(\frac{u^{\top}X^{\top}\mathcal{E}}{n}\right)>t\right]

(154)

\displaystyle\leq n\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}_{dev}^{2}}\min\left\{\left(\frac{nt}{K_{0}}\right)^{\gamma},\left(\frac{nt^{2}}{K_{0}^{2}}\right)\right\}+c_{0}w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]\right].

(155)

Note that
c_{0}
is an absolute constant.

Step 3: Choose
n
,
t
so that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{nt}{K_{0}}\right)^{\gamma}\geq\left(\frac{nt^{2}}{K_{0}^{2}}\right)=\mathsf{C}_{dev}^{2}(c_{0}+1)w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)].

(156)

Let
c_{1}=c_{0}+1
. We get

\displaystyle n\geq\left(\mathsf{C}_{dev}^{2}c_{1}w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]\right)^{2/\gamma-1},\quad t=K_{0}\mathsf{C}_{dev}\sqrt{\frac{c_{1}w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]}{n}}.

(157)

Note that
n
and
t
must also satisfy the precondition
t>1/n
. This gives

\displaystyle n\geq\frac{1}{K_{0}^{2}\mathsf{C}_{dev}^{2}c_{1}w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]}.

(158)

Note that the last inequality is extremely mild and satisfied easily as
w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]
grows with dimension
p
as
p\rightarrow\infty
, specifically as long as
w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]\succsim 1
. For example, when
\mathcal{R}
is the
\ell_{1}
norm,
w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]
is of exact order
\log p
. Hence the precondition is satisfied if
\log p\succsim 1
.

∎

E.7
Proof of Proposition (
D.2
).

Proof.

Step 1: Single concentration bound: Fix
v\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}
. Then, without loss,
||v||=1
. We have

\displaystyle||v^{\top}x||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}/2}}\leq 2^{2/\gamma_{2}}||v^{\top}x||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}^{2}\leq 2^{2/\gamma_{2}}\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}K^{2}.

(159)

\displaystyle||v^{\top}x-\mathbb{E}(v^{\top}x)||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}/2}}\leq 2||v^{\top}x||_{\psi_{\gamma_{2}}}^{2}\leq 2^{2/\gamma_{2}+1}\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}K^{2}.

(160)

Again, denoting
K_{0}=\bar{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}K^{2}
, we use Proposition (
A.4
) and get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|v^{\top}\left(\frac{X^{\top}X}{n}-\Sigma_{x}\right)v\right|\geq t\right]\leq n\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}_{RE}^{2}}\min\left\{\left(\frac{nt}{K_{0}}\right)^{\gamma},\left(\frac{nt^{2}}{K_{0}^{2}}\right)\right\}\right].

(161)

The constant
\mathsf{C}_{RE}>0
depends on
\beta_{mix}
,
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
.

Step 2: Discretizing the spherical cap
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}
and taking union bounds, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\underset{v\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}}{\sup}\left|v^{\top}\left(\frac{X^{\top}X}{n}-\Sigma_{x}\right)v\right|\geq 2K_{0}t\right]\leq n\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}_{RE}^{2}}\min\left\{(nt)^{\gamma},nt^{2}\right\}+c_{0}w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})\right]

. We used

,
\gamma<1
,
t>0
.

\displaystyle t=\Lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{X})/4K_{0}^{2},\quad\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}_{RE}^{2}}n^{\gamma}\min(1,t^{2})\geq(c_{0}+1)w^{2}(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}).

Let

(165)

∎

The precondition

boils down to
