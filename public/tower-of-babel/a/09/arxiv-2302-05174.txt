An Appropriate Probability Model for the Bell Experiment
Kees van Hee, Kees van Berkel, Jan de Graaf

Abstract
The Bell inequality constrains the outcomes of measurements on pairs of distant entangled particles. The Bell contradiction states that the Bell inequality is inconsistent with the calculated outcomes of these quantum experiments. This contradiction led many to question the underlying assumptions, viz. so-called realism and locality. This paper proposes an appropriate probability model for the Bell experiment. This model has only two simultaneously observable detector settings per measurement, and therefore does not assume realism. It is in full agreement with both quantum mechanics and experiments. In this model the expectation for a particular pair of observations is partial to the selected detector settings. This leads to a slightly different variant of the Bell inequality, one that is consistent with both quantum mechanics and measurements. In this model there is no Bell contradiction. Furthermore, the proposed probability model is statistically local, is not factorizable, and is not Bell-separable. The latter implies that either hidden variables must be ruled out, or that locality must be violated. Thus, our conclusion agrees with Bell’s conclusion.



Keywords:
Bell inequality, CHSH inequality, Bell experiment, Bell-paradox, Bell separability, hidden variables.

Introduction

J.S. Bell describes an experiment
[
1
]

in which an electron and a positron, formed somehow in a singlet spin state,
move freely in opposite directions. This experiment was inspired by the EPR paradox
[
2
]
.
He derives an inequality, labeled (15) in
[
1
]
,
that constrains the outcomes of the measurements of selected components of the spins of these two fermions.
Today this inequality is known as the
Bell inequality
.

In the body of this paper we focus on the more general CHSH version
[
3
]
of the Bell inequality.
(The original Bell inequality is revisited in Appendix
A
.)
The spins of the two prepared fermions are measured by detectors A and B.
Each detector has two different settings,
and the selection among the available detector settings is possibly random,
and possibly after the particle pair has been prepared.
Each individual measurement outcome has either value +1 (spin up) or -1 (spin down).
The corresponding stochastic variables for the detector settings of detector A
are denoted by
X_{0}
,
X_{1}
, and for detector B by
Y_{0}
, and
Y_{1}
.
The CHSH version
[
3
]
of the Bell inequality is:

\Big|\mathbb{E}[X_{0}Y_{0}]+\mathbb{E}[X_{1}Y_{0}]+\mathbb{E}[X_{1}Y_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{0}Y_{1}]\Big|\leq 2~,

(1.1)

where
\mathbb{E}[Z]
denotes the expected value (expectation) of random variable
Z
.

Next, Bell also calculates the outcomes of these experiments,
by applying the relevant observables (self-adjoint operators) to the quantum state of the particle pair.
He thus arrives at the
Bell contradiction
: these calculated outcomes contradict the inequality.
The Bell inequality also inspired numerous experiments, including
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
.
These experiments typically demonstrate “loophole-free Bell-inequality violation”.

The Bell contradiction led many to question the two assumptions made in the proof for
Inequality

, see a.i.
[

]
:

1.

The underlying physical properties for observations
X_{0}
,
X_{1}
,
Y_{0}
,
Y_{1}

exist independently of being observed or measured.
This assumption is known as
realism
or as
counterfactual definiteness
.

2.

The setting of detector A does not impact the outcome Y on the other detector
and vice versa.
This assumption is known as
(statistical) locality
.

The details of these assumptions are still being widely debated
among physicists, probability theorists, and philosophers

To date,

]
has been cited over 16 thousand times.
,
see for example

In this paper we offer a different and fresh perspective to the Bell contradiction.
Rather than questioning the underlying assumptions, we question the underlying probability model itself.
A probability model comprises an
outcome space
, an
event space
and a
probability measure
.
These notions will be reviewed in some detail in Section
2
.
Interestingly and significantly, an appropriate outcome space for the Bell experiment is generally left implicit.
The few exceptions include
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
,
where the experiment is modelled as a Bayesian network.
However, these Bayesian-network models are inherently factorizable with conditional probabilities,
and therefore disagree with quantum mechanics.
In Section
3
we review another, plausible outcome space and argue against it.
Next, in Section
4

we present an appropriate probability space for the Bell experiment,
including a probability measure, and calculate the expected values of the observables in the Bell inequality.
In this model there is no Bell contradiction.

Bell
[
1
]
derived his inequality in a different way.
He assumed that a so-called

could be introduced to account for the observed correlations
among measurement outcomes. Technically this means that the underlying probability measure
must be
separable
in a well-defined sense.
In Section
5
we analyze various properties of the proposed probability model,
and prove that the proposed probability measure is not Bell-separable.
The implication is that either local hidden-variable theories must be ruled out,
or that the underlying assumption of locality is violated

Preliminaries

In probability theory a
probability space

(see
[
14
]
and a.i.
[
15
,
16
]
)
is a 3-tuple
(\Omega,\cal F,\mathbb{P})
.
Here
\Omega
is an arbitrary set called the
outcome space
(or sample space),
and
\cal F
is the so-called the
event space
.
Event space
\cal F
is a set of subsets of
\Omega
such that

\emptyset\in\cal F
,
\Omega\in\cal F
,
and if
A\in\cal F
then
\Omega\backslash A\in\cal F
,
and
\cal F
is closed under countable unions.
If the outcome space is finite then
\cal F
is the power set of
\Omega
.
The last component
\mathbb{P}
is a
probability measure
on
\cal F
,
which is a function
\mathbb{P}\!:\cal F\rightarrow

[0,1]
such that
\mathbb{P}[\emptyset]\!=\!0,\mathbb{P}[\Omega]\!=\!1
and

\mathbb{P}[\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}]=\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{P}[A_{i}]
for some set
\{A_{i}|i\in I\}
of pairwise disjoint events of
\cal F
.

A
random variable
is defined as a real-valued function on a probability space.
For random variable
X\!:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

the probability
\mathbb{P}[\{\omega\in\Omega\}]\;|\;X(\omega)\!=\!x]
is abbreviated as
\mathbb{P}[X\!=\!x]
.
Given event
A\in\cal F
, the
conditional probability
of
X
given
A
is denoted by
\mathbb{P}[X|A]

and is defined by
\mathbb{P}[X|A]:=\mathbb{P}[X\cap A]/\mathbb{P}[A]
.

The Bell inequality is about expected values, or expectations for short.
The
expectation
of random variable
X
:

\mathbb{E}[X]:=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}X(\omega)\mathbb{P}[\{\omega\}]~.

(2.1)

The
conditional expectation
of
X
given event
A
is defined by

\mathbb{E}[X|A]:=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}X(\omega)\cdot\mathbb{P}[\{\omega\}\;\big|\;A]~.

(2.2)

The
partial expectation
of
X
given event
A
, less well-known, is defined by

\mathbb{E}_{A}[X]:=\sum_{\omega\in A}X(\omega)\cdot\mathbb{P}[\{\omega\}]=\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_{A}]~,

(2.3)

where
indicator function
(characteristic function)
\mathbf{1}_{A}(\omega)

returns
1
if
\omega\!\in\!A
and
0
otherwise.
2
2

2

Note that
\mathbf{1}_{A}
is also a random variable.

The two qualified expectations are related by

\mathbb{E}_{A}[X]~=~\mathbb{E}[X|A]\cdot\mathbb{P}[A]~.

(2.4)

Summing expectations looks like a simple and innocent operation.
After all, the expectation is linear and therefore
\mathbb{E}[X+Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]+\mathbb{E}[Y]~
.
Moreover, this linearity extends to summing conditional expectations:

\mathbb{E}[X+Y~|H]=\mathbb{E}[X~|H]+\mathbb{E}[Y~|H]
, given the same condition

for all terms involved.

(pairs of detector settings).

and

3
The Bell contradiction

Recall that the CHSH version
[
6
]
of the Bell inequality is

\big|\mathbb{E}[X_{0}Y_{0}]+\mathbb{E}[X_{1}Y_{0}]+\mathbb{E}[X_{1}Y_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{0}Y_{1}]\big|\leq 2~.

(3.1)

The quantum state of an entangled electron-positron pair can be described by

\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}({\bf{e}}_{0}\otimes{\bf{e}}_{1}-{\bf{e}}_{1}\otimes{\bf{e}}_{0})~\in~\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}~,

(3.2)

which alternatively can be written as
\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|01\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}-|10\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}\big)
.
A detector setting (observable) with angle
a
is modelled by a self-adjoint operator (Hermitean matrix)

F_{\!a}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
With well-chosen detector angles, that is, well-chosen observables for
X_{0},X_{1},Y_{0},Y_{1}
,
the average values for the compound observables are
[
6
]

\boldsymbol{\big\langle}X_{0}Y_{0}\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}=\boldsymbol{\big\langle}X_{1}Y_{0}\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}=\boldsymbol{\big\langle}X_{1}Y_{1}\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}=-\boldsymbol{\big\langle}X_{0}Y_{1}\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2}~,

(3.3)

resulting in

\big|\boldsymbol{\big\langle}X_{0}Y_{0}\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}+\boldsymbol{\big\langle}X_{1}Y_{0}\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}+\boldsymbol{\big\langle}X_{1}Y_{1}\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\big\langle}X_{0}Y_{1}\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}\big|=2\sqrt{2}~,

(3.4)

which is in contradiction with Inequality
3.1
.
Note that by comparing
3.4
to
3.1

we have overloaded the names
X_{0}
,
X_{1}
,
Y_{0}
, and
Y_{1}
:
in probability theory they denote random variables and
in quantum mechanics they denote observables (Hermitean operators).

The Bell contradiction apparently assumes that
\mathbb{E}[X_{0}Y_{0}]=\boldsymbol{\big\langle}X_{0}Y_{0}\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}
,
and likewise for observables
X_{1}Y_{0},X_{1}Y_{1}
, and
X_{0}Y_{1}
.
No proof has been given for this assumed equality.
A proper comparison of
\mathbb{E}[X_{0}Y_{0}]
with
\boldsymbol{\big\langle}X_{0}Y_{0}\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}
must be based on a well-defined outcome space.

Table

.

,

\Omega=\{-1,1\}^{4}
.
On this outcome space four
random variables
are defined:

,
the outcomes of passing the four detector settings

, and

4.2
The probability measure

Probability measure
p(x,y,a,b)
is calculated by performing the
four experiments, corresponding to the observables

~X_{0}\otimes Y_{0},~~X_{1}\otimes Y_{0},~~X_{1},\otimes Y_{1},~~X_{0}\otimes Y_{1}~

acting on the entangled state

\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}({\bf{e}}_{0}\otimes{\bf{e}}_{1}-{\bf{e}}_{1}\otimes{\bf{e}}_{0})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}~.

On each side the performing detector is modelled by a special type of self-adjoint operator (Hermitean matrix)

F_{\!a}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
, with
a\in[0,\pi)
, which is the difference of two orthogonal projections,

F_{\!a}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(2a)&\sin(2a)\\
\sin(2a)&-\cos(2a)\end{bmatrix}={\bf{f}}_{a}{\bf{f}}_{a}^{\top}-\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{a}\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{a}^{\top}\!,~~~{\bf{f}}_{a}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos a\\
\sin a\end{bmatrix},~~\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{a}={\bf{f}}_{a+\frac{\pi}{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}-\sin a\\
~\cos a\end{bmatrix}~.

(4.7)

The outcomes (eigenvalues) are
+1
and
-1

{\bf{f}}_{a}
and
\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{a}
.
Note that this covers all cases, because
{\bf{f}}_{a\,\pm\,\pi}=-{\bf{f}}_{a}

The considered experiment, which has a detector

F_{\!a}\otimes F_{\!b}

\boldsymbol{\Psi}

For the probability calculations, it is advantageous to orthogonally decompose
the entangled state
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
according to the spectral resolution of

\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\psi_{1,1}\,{\bf{f}}_{a}\otimes{\bf{f}}_{b}\;+\;\psi_{-1,1}\,\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{a}\otimes{\bf{f}}_{b}\;+\;\psi_{1,-1}\,{\bf{f}}_{a}\otimes\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{b}\;+\;\psi_{-1,-1}\,\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{a}\otimes\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{b}~.

(4.8)

The coefficients

are calculated by taking inner products. A specimen,

\psi_{-1,1}=\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}\boldsymbol{,}\;\tilde{{\bf{f}}}_{a}\otimes{\bf{f}}_{b}\big)_{\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}}~.

(4.9)

By Born’s rule,

of the outcome pair

A detailed specimen,

4.3
A Bell-like inequality

With the proposed probability space in place we now return to the Bell inequality and the expectations involved.
First note that, given the probability measure of Table
5
,

\begin{array}[]{llrlrl}\mathbb{E}[X\!\cdot\!Y\;\big|\;A=a_{i},B=b_{j}]&=&-\frac{1}{4}(\gamma^{2}-\beta^{2})&=&-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2}&\text{if}~i\!=\!0,j\!=\!1\\[1.42262pt]
&=&\frac{1}{4}(\gamma^{2}-\beta^{2})&=&\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2}&\text{otherwise}~.\end{array}

(4.14)

These conditional expectations correspond to the Born expectations
\boldsymbol{\big\langle}X_{i}\!\cdot\!Y_{j}\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}

as used in the Bell inequality.
In the proposed probability model, however, random variables
X_{i}
and
Y_{j}
do not occur.
In stead, we can define random variables that correspond to
X_{i}
and
Y_{j}
:

X_{i}~\leftrightarrow X\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}_{\{A=a_{i}\}}~~~~\text{and}~~~~Y_{j}~\leftrightarrow Y\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}_{\{B=b_{j}\}}~.

(4.15)

This correspondence leads to

X_{i}\!\cdot\!Y_{j}~\leftrightarrow~X\!\cdot\!Y\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}_{\{A=a_{i},B=b_{j}\}}~,

(4.16)

and, by Identity
2.3
, to

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}\!\cdot\!Y_{j}]~\leftrightarrow~\mathbb{E}_{\{A=a_{i},B=b_{j}\}}[X\!\cdot\!Y]~.

(4.17)

The values of
\mathbb{E}_{\{A=a_{i},B=b_{j}\}}[X\!\cdot\!Y]

can be read directly from column
a_{i}b_{j}
of Table
5
:

\begin{array}[]{llrl}\mathbb{E}_{\{A=a_{i},B=b_{j}\}}[X\!\cdot\!Y]~=~\sum_{x,y}x\!\cdot\!y\!\cdot\!\;p(x,y,a_{i},b_{j})&=&-\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{2}&\text{if}~i\!=\!0,j\!=\!1\\[1.42262pt]
&=&\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{2}&\text{otherwise}~.\end{array}

(4.18)

This correspondence results in the following Bell-like inequality

\Big|X\!\cdot\!Y\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}_{\{A=a_{0},B=b_{0}\}}+X\!\cdot\!Y\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}_{\{A=a_{1},B=b_{0}\}}+X\!\cdot\!Y\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}_{\{A=a_{1},B=b_{1}\}}-X\!\cdot\!Y\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}_{\{A=a_{0},B=b_{1}\}}\Big|~\leq 2~,

(4.19)

or, using shorthand
\mathbb{E}_{a_{i}b_{j}}[X\!\cdot\!Y]

for partial expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\{A=a_{i},B=b_{j}\}}[X\!\cdot\!Y]
,

\big|\mathbb{E}_{a_{0}b_{0}}[X\!\cdot\!Y]+\mathbb{E}_{a_{1}b_{0}}[X\!\cdot\!Y]+\mathbb{E}_{a_{1}b_{1}}[X\!\cdot\!Y]-\mathbb{E}_{a_{0}b_{1}}[X\!\cdot\!Y]\big|~=~\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2}~\leq 2~.

(4.20)

The contradiction with Inequality
1.1
is no longer.

In terms of
N
individual experiments,
consider random variables
X_{n}
,
Y_{n}
,
A_{n}
, and
B_{n}
, with

.
These variables are different for different values of

. Then

\begin{array}[]{lll}X_{i}^{(n)}&\leftrightarrow&X_{n}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}_{\{A_{n}=a_{i}\}}\\[1.42262pt]
Y_{j}^{(n)}&\leftrightarrow&Y_{n}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}_{\{B_{n}=b_{j}\}}~,\end{array}

(4.21)

and the partial expectation can also be written as

\mathbb{E}_{\{A=a_{i},B=b_{j}\}}[X\!\cdot\!Y]~=~\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{0}^{N-1}X_{n}\!\cdot\!Y_{n}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{1}_{\{A=a_{i},B=b_{j}\}}~.

(4.22)

5.1
p(x,y,a,b)
is statistically local

From Table

\mathbb{P}[X\!=\!1\;|\;A\!=\!a_{0},B\!=\!b_{0}]=(\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})/8=\frac{1}{8}

Likewise,

[

and also as

[

More precisely, this probability measure is statistically local if

(5.1)

and similarly for the other

or

This can be rewritten as

\frac{\mathbb{P}[X\!=\!1\;\wedge\;A\!=\!a_{0},B\!=\!b_{0}]}{\mathbb{P}[A\!=\!a_{0},B\!=\!b_{0}]}~=~\frac{\mathbb{P}[X\!=\!1\;\wedge\;A\!=\!a_{0}]}{\mathbb{P}[A\!=\!a_{0}]}~.

(5.2)

The left and right-hand sides can now be evaluated using the probabilities of
Table

.

\begin{array}[]{llll}\text{LHS}=&p_{00}\frac{1}{2}\big[\sin^{2}(a_{0}\!-\!b_{0})+\cos^{2}(a_{0}\!-\!b_{0})\big]\big/p_{00}=\frac{1}{2}\\[4.2679pt]
\text{RHS}=&\big(p_{00}\frac{1}{2}\big[\sin^{2}(a_{0}\!-\!b_{0})+\cos^{2}(a_{0}\!-\!b_{0})\big]+\\[1.42262pt]
&\;p_{01}\frac{1}{2}\big[\sin^{2}(a_{0}\!-\!b_{1})+\cos^{2}(a_{0}\!-\!b_{1})\big]\big)\big/\big[p_{00}+p_{01}\big]=\frac{1}{2}~.\end{array}

(5.3)

Clearly LHS=RHS, as it does for

This proves that for detector setting

, the outcome of detector A
does not depend on the setting of detector B.
The same holds for the other detector settings.
Probability measure

is (statistically) local.
This observed locality stems from the quantum-mechanical model of
the detectors (
F_{a}
and

) in Subsection

6
Conclusion

This paper proposes an appropriate probability model for the Bell experiment.
It is based on single outcome space for a single experiment that is repeated many times.
The outcome space is defined (Definition
4.1
)
as the cartesian product space of random variables
A
and
B

(detector settings) and
X
and
Y
(the corresponding measurement outcomes).
It has only two simultaneously observable detector settings per measurement
and therefore does not need the assumption of realism.
The probability measure
p(x,y,a,b)
is derived using quantum mechanics and described
by Identities
4.12
and by
Tables
4
and
5
.

From this probability measure it follows that
conditional expectations
\mathbb{E}[X\!\cdot\!Y\;\big|\;A=a_{i},B=b_{j}]
correspond to
Born expectations
\boldsymbol{\big\langle}X_{i}\!\cdot\!Y_{j}\boldsymbol{\big\rangle}
. From Correspondence
4.15

it follows that the terms in the Bell inequality become
partial expectations
\mathbb{E}_{\{A=a_{i},B=b_{j}\}}[X\!\cdot\!Y]
.
As a result the Bell contradiction disappears.
The proposed probability measure appears statistically local (Subsection
5.1
).

From the observed contradiction Bell
[
18
]
