Testing the Isotropic Cauchy Hypothesis
Jihad Fahs, Ibrahim Abou-Faycal and Ibrahim Issa

Abstract
Isotropic α \alpha -stable distributions are central in the theory of heavy-tailed distributions and play a role similar to that of the Gaussian density among finite second-moment laws. Given a sequence of n n observations, we are interested in characterizing the performance of Likelihood Ratio Tests where two hypotheses are plausible for the observed quantities: either isotropic Cauchy or isotropic Gaussian. Under various setups, we show that the probability of error of such detectors is not always exponentially decaying with n n with the leading term in the exponent shown to be logarithmic instead and we determine the constants in that leading term. Perhaps surprisingly, the optimal Bayesian probabilities of error are found to exhibit different asymptotic behaviors.



I
Introduction

Detecting the underlying sampling distribution of an empirical set of
n
-observations is a challenging and fundamental question in decision theory. It falls under the family of hypothesis testing problems whenever deciding on a possibility among a given number of choices. In the binary scenario (
p
vs.
q
), it is well known that Likelihood Ratio Tests (LRT) detectors are optimal for multiple setups. Under a Neyman-Pearson criterion or under a Bayesian risk formulation, the performance of such detectors is expected to be exponentially decaying with
n
, with the exponent being a Kullback-Leibler (KL) distance between pertinent laws. This is the case in a discrete scenario
[
1
, Th. 11.8.3 & Th. 11.9.1]
where a well-known proof is based on the method of types. Extensions to continuous setups do exist under certain conditions
[
2
,
3
]
.

One interesting quest is to analyze this decision problem while testing for

1.

the Cauchy distribution: representative of the family of “heavy-tailed”
\alpha
-stable distributions versus

2.

the Gaussian distribution: a central model among finite-variance probability laws.

Note that both models are limiting distributions of Independent and Identically Distributed (IID) sums of Random Variables (RV)s by the results of the generalized Central-Limit Theorem
[
4
]
and therefore supported by strong theoretical evidence. Additionally, isotropic
*
*

*

We will use “circular” interchangeably with “isotropic” in this document.

\alpha
-stable distributions are found to be accurate models for empirical data in a multitude of scenarios in which they are shown to provide a better fit to the data under consideration
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

In this work, we tackle the problem of testing the
\alpha
-stable hypothesis by considering the
n
-dimensional circularly-symmetric Cauchy distribution. More specifically, given a sample of
n
observations sampled from a rotationally invariant density, we want to characterize the error limits of the LRT devices detecting whether the observations are circular Gaussian –with Probability Density Function (PDF) denoted
p_{G}(\cdot)
, or circular Cauchy –with PDF
p_{C}(\cdot)
. We note that the proposed setup is substantially different from the standard one regarding two main aspects:

1.

First, circular Cauchy vectors are correlated and not independent as is the case for a Gaussian vector.

2.

Second, the relative entropy
D(p_{C}\|p_{G})
,
is infinite as shown in Appendix
A
.

Correspondingly, the results are found to deviate from the norm regarding two aspects:

\bullet

First, we show that the probability of error is not exponentially small with
n
. Namely we prove that, under a Neyman-Pearson formulation and in the first order of the exponent, the leading term is linear for the error of the first kind (
P_{\text{C}|\text{G}}
) but only logarithmic for that of the second kind (
P_{\text{G}|\text{C}}
). A similar logarithmic behavior of the exponent of the leading term is shown for the Bayesian error. Whenever possible, we determine the multiplicative constants of the provided asymptotic expressions.

\bullet

Second, we observe that the two types of Bayesian errors do not possess the same asymptotic rate which is at odds with the general assumption of a similar asymptotic behavior under IID statistics (see
[
1
, Th. 11.9.1]
for the discrete case). We postulate that this is due to the fact that the observations are correlated and we support our observation by providing in Appendix
B
illustrative examples in which one of the two hypotheses is correlated.

II
Problem Formulation

We consider the binary hypothesis testing problem where, given a (large) number
n>0

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle H_{0}\equiv H_{\text{G}}:&{\bf Y}^{n}\,\,\text{circular}\,\,\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),\\
\displaystyle H_{1}\equiv H_{\text{C}}:&{\bf Y}^{n}\,\,\text{circular}\,\,\mathcal{C}(0,\gamma).\end{array}\right.

Whether under a Bayesian risk formulation, or a Neyman-Pearson criterion, the optimal device is known to be a LRT:

\frac{p_{{\bf Y}|H}\left({\bf y}|H_{\text{G}}\right)}{p_{{\bf Y}|H}\left({\bf y}|H_{\text{C}}\right)}=\frac{p_{G}({\bf y})}{p_{C}({\bf y})}\begin{array}[]{c}{\scriptstyle H_{\text{G}}}\\
\geq\\
<\\
{\scriptstyle H_{\text{C}}}\end{array}\eta,

(2)

for some appropriately chosen
\eta>0
. Note that we identify the “Gaussian hypothesis”
H_{\text{G}}
with the null hypothesis
H_{0}
whenever we use the ”false alarm”, ”detection” and ”error types” terminology in this work. As such, the probability of false alarm for example
P_{F}
is equal to
P_{F}=P_{\text{C}|\text{G}}
and as
\eta
increases,
P_{F}
and
P_{D}
increase.

Our main objective is twofold. First we study the optimal Bayesian device and characterize its overall probability of error at large values of
n
via computing the asymptotic behavior of the probabilities of detection
P_{D}
and false alarm

. More specifically, if
\pi_{G}
and

denote the prior probabilities of

and

Second, we adopt the Neyman-Pearson criterion and answer the following question: let
P_{F}=P_{\text{C}|\text{G}}=\varepsilon
, for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)

We also answer the same question for the inverted problem: if

P_{F}=P_{\text{C}|\text{G}}=\frac{e^{-\Theta(n)}}{\sqrt{n}}.

For mathematical convenience and without loss of generality, we scale our observation vector

\displaystyle 1-P_{D}

\displaystyle P_{F}

V
Conclusion

It is well-known that, in a discrete setup and under IID observations, the error of the second kind
(1-P_{D})
goes to zero when
n\rightarrow\infty
according to
2^{-nD(p\|q)}
, where

p
and
q
are the probability laws under the two different hypotheses. In some cases, a similar observation can be inferred whenever
p
and
q
are continuous
[
2
]
. Though the results presented in this paper are for a specific problem on hypothesis testing between two isotropic probability laws, they are rather interesting on their own and are “non-standard” when compared to the IID “Gaussian”-centric case. Our findings suggest new insights on the hypothesis testing problem:

1.

As a generalization to the IID case, the leading term in the exponent might be possibly governed by the behavior of
D(p_{n}\|q_{n})
as a function of
n
whenever the relative entropy is finite. This extension is currently being investigated by the authors. Note that, under the IID case, we have
D(p_{n}\|q_{n})=nD(p\|q)
which recovers the standard result of the IID setup.

2.

In general
D(p_{n}\|q_{n})
is not linear in
n
. When
p_{n}
is
circular (IID)

\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and
q_{n}
is
\text{circular}\,\,\mathcal{C}(0,\gamma)
, we show in Appendix
A
that
D(p_{n}\|q_{n})=\Theta\left(\ln\sqrt{n}\right)
which corroborates the previous point as per the results of Theorem
2
for
(1-P_{D})
.

3.

Whenever
D(p_{n}\|q_{n})
is not finite, the term in the exponent is not linear: It was found in this setup to be at least
\Theta(n)+\frac{1}{2}\ln n
.

\displaystyle D(p_{G}\|p_{C})=-\frac{n}{2}\ln(\pi e\xi^{2})-\ln\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}+\frac{n+1}{2}\,I_{n}

\displaystyle\sim-\frac{n}{2}\ln(\pi e\xi^{2})-\frac{n-1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)+\frac{n-1}{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)+\frac{n+1}{2}\ln\pi+\frac{n+1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{n}{2}\,\xi^{2}\,\right)+o(\ln n)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{2}\ln n.

Appendix B
Correlated Hypotheses; Examples

B-A

Example 1

Let
0<\alpha<1
. Consider the following binary hypothesis testing problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle H_{0}:&\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\text{ IID }\sim\mathcal{B}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\\
\displaystyle H_{1}:&\begin{cases}X_{1}\sim\mathcal{B}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\,\,\&\,\,X_{1}=\cdots=X_{n}&\text{w.p.}\,\,\alpha\\
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\text{ IID }\sim\mathcal{B}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)&\text{w.p.}\,\,(1-\alpha),\end{cases}\end{array}\right.

where
\mathcal{B}(p)
is the Bernoulli variable with parameter
p
. It can be readily dervied that the Maximum Likelihood (ML) device –optimal in a Bayesian setting with equal a priori’s– decides on
H_{1}
whenever all
X_{i}
’s are equal and on
H_{0}
otherwise. Computing
P_{F}
:

\displaystyle P_{F}=\,

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left(\hat{H}=H_{1}|H_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left((\underset{n\,\,\text{times}}{\underbrace{0,0,\cdots,0}})\cup(\underset{n\,\,\text{times}}{\underbrace{1,1,\cdots,1}})|H_{0}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-1},

which is exponentially decaying in
n
. As for
P_{D}
:

\displaystyle P_{D}=\,

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left(\hat{H}=H_{1}|H_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left((\underset{n\,\,\text{times}}{\underbrace{0,0,\cdots,0}})\cup(\underset{n\,\,\text{times}}{\underbrace{1,1,\cdots,1}})|H_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}+(1-\alpha)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}\right),

which implies that

1-P_{D}=(1-\alpha)\left(1-\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-1}\right),

converging to a positive constant as
n
goes to infinity and is clearly different in behavior from
P_{F}
at large values of
n
.

B-B

Example 2: The Beta-Binomial Distribution

Let

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle H_{0}:&\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\text{ IID }\sim\mathcal{B}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\\
\displaystyle H_{1}:&\mathcal{BB}(n,1,1),\quad\text{correlated}\end{array}\right.

where
\mathcal{BB}(n,\alpha,\beta)
is the Beta-Binomial distribution with parameters
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\alpha,\beta>0

[
11
,
12
]
. Under the
\mathcal{BB}(n,\alpha,\beta)
distribution, the probability of having
i
zeros among length-
n
sequences is equal to:

b_{n}(i)=\begin{pmatrix}n\\
i\end{pmatrix}\frac{B(\alpha+i,n+\beta-i)}{B(\alpha,\beta)},

(49)

where
B(\alpha,\beta)
is the Beta function given in terms of the Gamma function as follows:

B(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}.

In out case
\alpha=\beta=1
, and
b_{n}(i)
evaluates to:

\displaystyle b_{n}(i)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}n\\
i\end{pmatrix}\frac{B(1+i,n+1-i)}{B(1,1)}=\begin{pmatrix}n\\
i\end{pmatrix}\frac{i!\,(n-i)!}{(n+1)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1},\quad 0\leq i\leq n.

(50)

Under equal apriori probabilities
p_{0}=p_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
, the MAP detector is a ML one and will choose hypothesis
H_{1}
whenever the length
n
sequence is more probable under
H_{1}
than under
H_{0}
. Let
i
be the number of zeros in a length-
n
sequence. Then,
\hat{H}=H_{1}
whenever:

\displaystyle\frac{B\left(1+i,n+1-i\right)}{B\left(1,1\right)}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\frac{i!\,(n-i)!}{(n+1)!}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}n\\
i\end{pmatrix}\leq\frac{2^{n}}{n+1}.

(51)

We note that whenever that there exists an index
i
satisfying equation (
51
), then
(n-i)
will also satisfy (
51
). For the range
\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil\leq i\leq n
, let
i_{0}(n)
be the smallest integer for which (
51
) is satisfied. For large
n
, we claim that:

\frac{i_{0}(n)-\frac{n}{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\sim c\,\sqrt{\ln n}

(52)

for
c=\frac{1}{2}
. Define
i^{*}(n)=\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}+c\sqrt{n\ln n}\right\rceil
. Since, for any
c>0
, we have
|\frac{n}{2}-i^{*}(n)|=o(n^{\frac{2}{3}})
, then using Stirling’s approximation for the binomial coefficient for large
n
and large
i

[
13
]
, we have:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}n\\
i^{*}(n)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2^{n}}{\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}\,n}}e^{-\frac{(n-2i^{*}(n))^{2}}{2n}}

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{2^{n}}{n^{\frac{1}{2}+2c^{2}}}.

(53)

Equation (
53
) violates condition (
51
) whenever
c<\frac{1}{2}
and satisfies it whenever
c\geq\frac{1}{2}
thus proving our claim. For large
n
, we have

\displaystyle P_{F}=\text{Pr}\left(\hat{H}=H_{1}|H_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}2\sum_{i_{0}(n)}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
i\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}\left(2^{n}-\sum_{n-i_{0}(n)+1}^{i_{0}(n)-1}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
i\end{pmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle\sim 1-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{n-i_{0}(n)+1}^{i_{0}(n)-1}e^{-\frac{(n-2i)^{2}}{2n}}

(54)

\displaystyle\sim 1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{n-i_{0}(n)+1}^{i_{0}(n)-1}e^{-\frac{(n-2t)^{2}}{2n}}\,dt

(55)

\displaystyle=1-\left[Q\left(\frac{2i_{0}(n)-n-2}{\sqrt{n}}\right)-Q\left(\frac{-2i_{0}(n)+n+2}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right]

(56)

\displaystyle=2Q\left(\frac{2i_{0}(n)-n-2}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{e^{-\frac{(2i_{0}(n)-n-2)^{2}}{2n}}}{\frac{2i_{0}(n)-n-2}{\sqrt{n}}}

(57)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\ln n}}

where we used Stirling’s approximation to write equation (
54
). Equation (
55
) is valid since
e^{-\frac{(n-2t)^{2}}{2n}}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
is convex for
n-i_{0}(n)+1\leq t\leq i_{0}(n)-1
, thus implying:

\displaystyle\int_{n-i_{0}(n)+1}^{i_{0}(n)-1}e^{-\frac{(n-2t)^{2}}{2n}}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n-i_{0}(n)+1}^{i_{0}(n)-1}e^{-\frac{(n-2i)^{2}}{2n}}\leq\int_{n-i_{0}(n)}^{i_{0}(n)-1}e^{-\frac{(n-2t)^{2}}{2n}}\,dt,

with both the upper and lower bounds having the same asymptotic behavior. Equation (
56
) is due to the change of variable
u=\frac{2t-n}{\sqrt{n}}
and equation (
57
) is justified by using the approximation in (
27
). The last equation is justified by equation (
52
). As for
1-P_{D}
, we have

\displaystyle 1-P_{D}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left(\hat{H}=H_{0}|H_{1}\right)

(58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n-i_{0}(n)+1}^{i_{0}(n)-1}\,b_{n}(i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\,(2i_{0}(n)-n-2),

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}}

where
b_{n}(i)
is given in equation (
50
) and where we used equation (
52
) to write the last equation. Once more,
P_{F}
and
1-P_{D}
exhibit different asymptotic behaviors.

B-C

Example 3: Continuous Laws

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq 3
, consider the following PDF over
[0,1]^{n}
:

\displaystyle p_{n}(x^{n})=\begin{cases}n-\sqrt{n},&\text{if }\|x^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq n^{-1/n},\\
\frac{\sqrt{n}}{n-1},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\|x^{n}\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}|x_{i}|
. It can be easily verified that
p_{n}
is a valid PDF by noting that the volume of the set
\{x^{n}:\|x^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq n^{-1/n}\}
is
\frac{1}{n}
.

Now, consider the following hypothesis testing problem:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle H_{0}:\{X\}_{i=1}^{n}\text{ IID }\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],\\
\displaystyle H_{1}:X^{n}\sim p_{n}(\cdot),\end{array}\right.

where
\mathcal{U}[0,1]
is the uniform law on the interval
[0,1]
.

Assuming a prior uniform over
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
, the optimal ML decision rule yields

\displaystyle\|x^{n}\|_{\infty}\underset{H_{0}}{\overset{H_{1}}{\lesseqgtr}}n^{-1/n}.

As such,

\displaystyle P_{F}

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}(\hat{H}=H_{1}|H_{0})=\text{Pr}\Big(\|X^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq n^{-1/n}|H_{0}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle 1-P_{D}

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}(\hat{H}=H_{0}|H_{1})

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}(\|X^{n}\|_{\infty}>n^{-1/n}|H_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{\{\|x^{n}\|_{\infty}>n^{-1/n}\}}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{n-1}\,dx^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{n-1}\left(1-\int_{\{\|x^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq n^{-1/n}\}}dx^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{n-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},

and the asymptotic behavior is clearly different from that of
P_{F}
.

Appendix C
(1-P_{D})

In this appendix, we show the following technical result. We have

\displaystyle 1-P_{D}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}\sqrt{\frac{2}{n+1}}\,\left(\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{n+1}{2r_{2}^{2}}\right)\hskip-0.08553pt-\hskip-0.08553pt\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{n+1}{2r_{1}^{2}}\right)\right).

(60)

Furthermore, under Regime (R-i), the following holds:

\displaystyle 1-P_{D}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}\sqrt{\frac{2}{n+1}}\,\left(\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{n+1}{2r_{2}^{2}}\right)\hskip-0.08553pt-\hskip-0.08553pt\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{n+1}{2r_{1}^{2}}\right)\right).

(61)

To show (
60
), we use the expression of
1-P_{D}
as given by equation (
13
) and the fact that that
\left(1+u\right)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}\geq e^{-\frac{n+1}{2}u}
for
u\geq 0
, and we write:

\displaystyle 1-P_{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}\int_{\frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}}^{\frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}}\left(1+u\right)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}u^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,du

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}\int_{\frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}}^{\frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}}e^{-\frac{n+1}{2}u}u^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}\sqrt{\frac{2}{n+1}}\,\left(\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{n+1}{2r_{2}^{2}}\right)-\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{n+1}{2r_{1}^{2}}\right)\right)

where the last equation is due to the definition of the upper incomplete Gamma function.
To further show (
61
), we note that under Regime (R-i) the following holds:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}\int_{\frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}}^{\frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}}\left(1+u\right)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}u^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,du}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}\int_{\frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}}^{\frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}}e^{-\frac{n+1}{2}u}u^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,du}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{\frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}}^{\frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}}e^{-\frac{n+1}{2}\left[\ln(1+u)-u\right]}e^{-\frac{n+1}{2}u}u^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,du}{\int_{\frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}}^{\frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}}e^{-\frac{n+1}{2}u}u^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,du}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}e^{-\frac{n+1}{2}\left[\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}\right]}

(62)

\displaystyle=\left[\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}e^{-\frac{n+1}{2}\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}\right)}\right]\left[\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}e^{\frac{n+1}{2}\frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}}e^{\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}}=1,

(63)

where in order to write equation (
63
), we used the fact that
r_{1}^{2}
behaves according to equation (
15
) under Regime (R-i). Equation (
62
) holds true since
\ln(1+u)-u
is non-increasing for
u\geq 0
.
