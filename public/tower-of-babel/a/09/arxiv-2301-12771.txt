1.
Introduction and plan of the paper

We let
k
always denote a commutative field, and
\kappa
its cardinality; in a moment we shall add the assumption that
k
is algebraically closed and has characteristic
0
.
Purpose of the paper is to study the following relation between subsets of
k
.

Definition 1
.

For
A,B\subseteq k
, let
A\leq_{p}B
if there is a polynomial function
P:k\to k
such that

(1)

A=P^{-1}(B).

We then say that
A

polynomially reduces
to
B
.
A polynomial
P
as in (
1
) is said to
reduce

A
to
B
.
Also, let
A\equiv_{p}B
denote
A\leq_{p}B\leq_{p}A
, in which case we say that
A,B
are
polinomially bireducible
.
If
A\leq_{p}B
but
A\not\equiv_{p}B
, we write
A<_{p}B
.

Relation
\leq_{p}
is an instance of a
reducibility relation
: if
\mathcal{F}
is a class of functions from a set
X
to itself, containing the identity function and closed under composition, setting

(2)

A\leq_{\mathcal{F}}B\Leftrightarrow\exists f\in\mathcal{F},A=f^{-1}(B),

for
A,B\subseteq X
, defines a preorder on
\mathcal{P}(X)
, the powerset of
X
.
This preorder is called
\mathcal{F}
-
reducibility
and the function
f
appearing in (
2
) is called a
reduction
of
A
to
B
.

The idea underlying the notion of reducibility is that, whenever the relation
A\leq_{\mathcal{F}}B
is witnessed by a reduction
f\in\mathcal{F}
, the complexity of the set
A
is bounded by the complexity of the set
B
since the problem of recognising membership in
A
is reduced to the same problem for
B
, as
x\in A\Leftrightarrow f(x)\in B
.
This notion of relative complexity depends on the chosen class of reductions
\mathcal{F}
, and the most notable example is when
X
is a topological space and
\mathcal{F}
is the collection of continuous functions on
X
.
Then
\leq_{\mathcal{F}}
is called
continuous reducibility
, or
Wadge reducibility
, since the first systematic study of it (for
X=\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
) was performed in Wadge’s thesis
[
11
]
.

In this paper we are interested in the case our set is an algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic
0
, and we choose as
\mathcal{F}
the class of polynomial functions on
k
, since these are the basic functions of interest in commutative algebra and algebraic geometry, being the morphisms of the affine variety
k
.
As
\equiv_{p}
is an equivalence relation, we still denote
\leq_{p}
the partial order induced on the equivalence classes, that is on
{\raisebox{1.99997pt}{$\mathcal{P}(k)\!$}\left/\raisebox{-1.99997pt}{$\equiv_{p}$}\right.}
.
The
\equiv_{p}
-equivalence classes
[A]=\{B\in\mathcal{P}(k)\mid B\equiv_{p}A\}
will be called
polynomial classes
.

We focus mainly on the study of
\leq_{p}
on finite subsets of
k
: this is the content of section
2
, which constitutes the core of the paper.
After presenting some general facts, in section
2.1
we prove that polinomial classes of sets of
n
elements, for any given
n
, form an affine variety
\Sigma_{n}
of dimension
n-2
.
The import of this fact is that it provides a framework to study
\leq_{p}
quantitatively, by using dimension, which is done in the subsequent sections.
In section
2.2
we describe
\equiv_{p}
on finite sets from a geometrical viewpoint and in section
2.3
we prove that
\Sigma_{3}
is an affine line.
Section
2.4
is devoted to those polynomial classes that have less representatives than the generic ones.
In this sense, we call them
exceptional classes
and we prove in proposition
36
and corollary
38
that they form a subvariety of
\Sigma_{n}
of dimension
\frac{n-2}{2}
or
\frac{n-3}{2}
, according to whether
n
is even or odd.
In section
2.5
we prove several results about
\leq_{p}
on finite sets.
Given a
n
-element subset
A
of
k
, we give conditions for a
m
-element set to be comparable with
A
and we prove that the family of polynomial classes of
m
-element sets comparable with
[A]
is a subvariety of
\Sigma_{m}
of which we compute the dimension (proposition
41
and theorems
42
and
44
).
In theorem
47
we show that most polynomial classes of finite sets are
\leq_{p}
-maximal below the class of
1
-element sets, thus providing a big antichain for
\leq_{p}
.

In section
3
we gather some results about
\leq_{p}
on sets that are both infinite and coinfinite.
We show that there are
2^{\kappa}
polynomial classes of sets of cardinality
\kappa
and whose complement has cardinality
\kappa
that are
\leq_{p}
-maximal (theorem
48
).
Moreover, if
k
is countable, most subsets of
k
are
\leq_{p}
-maximal from the Baire category point of view, in the sense that they form a comeagre subset of the powerset of
k
(theorem
49
).
Finally, when
k\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, we construct a chain of polynomial classes of order type
\zeta
—the order type of the integers— in proposition
51
.

To establish one of our main results, we need in lemma
43
that a specific matrix, which is an enrichment of the Vandermonde matrix, is non-singular.
This observation, which we could not find in the existing literature, might be interesting on its own and its proof is given in a separate section (section
5
).

Remark 1
.

(1)

Denote by
Pol
the monoid of polynomial functions on
k
under the operation of composition.
The preorder
\leq_{p}
is induced by the right action of
Pol
on
\mathcal{P}(k)
defined by
(A,P)\mapsto P^{-1}(A)
.

(2)

There are two incomparable polynomial classes that precede every other polynomial class:
[\emptyset]=\{\emptyset\}
and
[k]=\{k\}
.
Indeed, if
A
is a proper subset of
k
, then any constant polynomial with value outside
A
is a reduction of
\emptyset
to
A
; similarly,
k
is polynomially reducible to any non-empty set
A
.

Polynomial functions are continuous with respect to the Zariski topology on
k
(which is the cofinite topology), so it follows that

(3)

\forall A,B\in\mathcal{P}(k),A\leq_{p}B\Rightarrow A\leq_{W}B

where
\leq_{W}
is the relation of Wadge reducibility with respect to the Zariski topology.
The relation
\leq_{W}
for Zariski topologies has been studied in
[
1
]
: for example, it is noted there that non-empty, proper, closed subsets of
k
(when
k
is infinite these are exactly the finite non-empty sets) form a single class with respect to the equivalence relation associated to
\leq_{W}
.
Proposition
2
below shows that for some fields
k
the relation
\leq_{p}
can coincide with
\leq_{W}
on finite sets.
However, purpose of this note is to illustrate that in general the situation is quite different.

Proposition 2
.

Let
A
be a finite, non-empty subset of
k
.
Then
A\leq_{p}\{0\}
.

If
k
is an ordered field, then
\{0\}\leq_{p}A
holds as well.
Consequently, if
k
is an ordered field then
A\equiv_{p}B
for any finite, non-empty subsets
A,B
.

Proof.

The polynomial
P(X)=\prod_{a\in A}(X-a)
reduces
A
to
\{0\}
.
If
k
is an ordered field, let
a=\max A
; then
Q(X)=X^{2}+a
reduces
\{0\}
to
A
.
∎

From now on
k
is algebraically closed and has characteristic
0
.

Remark 3
.

The assumption on
k
implies in particular that:

(1)

k
is infinite, so this allows to identify polynomials and polynomial functions.

(2)

Every non-constant polynomial function is surjective; therefore if the non-constant polynomial
P
reduces
A
to
B
, it also follows that
P(A)=B
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{P}(k)\setminus\{\emptyset,k\}
.
If
A\leq_{p}B
, then either
A,B
are both finite, in which case
{\rm{card}}(A)\geq{\rm{card}}(B)
, or
{\rm{card}}(A)={\rm{card}}(B)
.
Consequently, for any
A,B
, if
A\equiv_{p}B
then
{\rm{card}}(A)={\rm{card}}(B)
.

Proof.

This holds since non-constant polynomials are surjective and every element has finitely many preimages.
∎

For
\lambda\leq\kappa
let

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}=\{A\in\mathcal{P}(k)\mid{\rm{card}}(A)=\lambda\},\qquad\check{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}=\{k\setminus A\}_{A\in\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}}.

By proposition
4
, every
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}
and
\check{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}
, as well as
\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}\cap\check{\mathcal{P}}_{\kappa}
, is invariant with respect to
\equiv_{p}
.
Recall that
{\rm{card}}(\mathcal{P}_{\lambda})={\rm{card}}(\check{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda})=\kappa^{\lambda}
(see
[
7
, exercise I.13.20]
) and that
{\rm{card}}(\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}\cap\check{\mathcal{P}}_{\kappa})=2^{\kappa}
.

We denote
\Sigma_{\lambda}=\{[A]\mid A\in\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\}
: this is the quotient of
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}
with respect to the restriction of
\equiv_{p}
.
Similarly, define
\check{\Sigma}_{\lambda}=\{[A]\mid A\in\check{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\}
.
The results on polynomial reducibility for
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda},\Sigma_{\lambda}
transfer to
\check{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda},\check{\Sigma}_{\lambda}
, respectively, via the bijection
A\mapsto k\setminus A
and the fact that
A\leq_{p}B\Leftrightarrow k\setminus A\leq_{p}k\setminus B
.

1.1.
Some terminology and notation

The terminology and notation that we employ throughout the paper is quite standard: main references are
[
5
,
4
,
9
,
2
]
.
We collect here a list of some common notions we use.

•

We denote
\deg(P)
the degree of a polynomial
P
.
A
linear polynomial
is a polynomial of degree
1
, that is, of the form
cX+c^{\prime}
where
c\neq 0
.
We denote
\mathcal{L}
the set of linear polynomials; this is a group under composition.
A
quadratic polynomial
is a polynomial of degree
2
, that is, of the form
cX^{2}+c^{\prime}X+c^{\prime\prime}
where
c\neq 0
.
If
P(X)=cX+c^{\prime}
is a linear polynomial, then repeated applications of
P
give

(4)

P^{j}(X)=c^{j}X+(c^{j}+\dots+c+1)c^{\prime};

consequently, if moreover
c\neq 1
, then

(5)

P^{j}(X)=c^{j}X+\frac{c^{j}-1}{c-1}c^{\prime}.

•

A polynomial
h\in k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
is
translation invariant
if

\forall a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},t\in k,h(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=h(a_{1}+t,\ldots,a_{n}+t).

•

If
\mathcal{S}\subseteq k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
, we denote
V(\mathcal{S})=\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in k^{n}\mid\forall f\in\mathcal{S},f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=0\}
; when
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
we also let
V(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})=V(\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\})
.
Sets isomorphic to some
V(\mathcal{S})
are
affine varieties
.

Note that by our definition an affine variety may be empty.

•

If
f\in k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
, the set
D(f)=k^{n}\setminus V(f)
is a
standard open set
: it consists of all elements of
k^{n}
where
f
does not vanish.
By
[
9
, proposition III.3.3]
,
D(f)
is an affine variety endowed with the restriction of the sheaf of regular functions of
k^{n}
.

•

If
V\subseteq k^{n}
is an affine variety, we denote
I(V)=\{f\in k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]\mid\forall(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in V,f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=0\}
the ideal of
V
.

•

We denote
Sym(A)
the symmetric group on a set
A
; we let also
Sym_{n}=Sym(\{1,\ldots,n\})
.

•

If
\alpha
is a group action and
a
is an element of the set acted upon, we denote

the stabiliser of

under action
\alpha
.

2.
Reducibility on finite sets

From (
3
) it follows that if
B
is finite, so closed in the Zariski topology, and
A\leq_{p}B
, then either
A=k
or
A
is finite.
Thus finite sets, together with
k
, constitute an initial segment of
\leq_{p}
.

Proposition 5
.

(1)

If
1\leq n<m,A\in\mathcal{P}_{n},B\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, then
A\nleq_{p}B
.

(2)

For every
n\geq 1
, every
A\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, and every
B\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
, it holds that
A\leq_{p}B
.

(3)

\forall A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{2},A\equiv_{p}B
.

In particular,
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
both consist of a single element.

Proof.

(1) This is proposition
4
.

(2) Let
B=\{b\}
.
Then the polynomial
\prod_{a\in A}(X-a)+b
reduces
A
to
B
.

(3) Let
A=\{a_{1},a_{2}\},B=\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
, in order to show the existence of a polynomial
P:k\to k
, in fact a linear one, such that
A=P^{-1}(B)
.
Let
P(X)=cX+c^{\prime}
.
The coefficients of such a polynomial can be found as the solutions of the linear system

(6)

\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1}&1\\
a_{2}&1\\
\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}c\\
c^{\prime}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\end{matrix}\right).

This shows that
A\subseteq P^{-1}(B)
; the fact that
P
cannot be constant implies that
P^{-1}(B)
has cardinality
2
and yields
A=P^{-1}(B)
.

As the polynomial
P^{-1}
reduces
B
to
A
, the assertion is proved.
∎

Proposition 6
.

Let
n\geq 2
.
If
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
P
is a polynomial such that
A=P^{-1}(B)
, then
P
is linear.

Proof.

Let
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\},B=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}
, where it can be assumed that
P(a_{j})=b_{j}
for every
j
.
Suppose, toward contradiction, that
P
has degree
\gamma>1
.
Then each equation
P(x)-b_{j}=0
has
a_{j}
as its unique solution, with multiplicity
\gamma
, so that
P(X)-b_{j}=c(X-a_{j})^{\gamma}
for some
c\neq 0
.
This means that for every
j,j^{\prime}
, the polynomial
c(X-a_{j})^{\gamma}-c(X-a_{j^{\prime}})^{\gamma}
is constant, which is not the case for
j\neq j^{\prime}
as the coefficient of degree
\gamma-1
is
c\gamma a_{j^{\prime}}-c\gamma a_{j}\neq 0
.
∎

Corollary 7
.

If
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
are such that
A\leq_{p}B
, then
A\equiv_{p}B
.
In other words, the elements of
\Sigma_{n}
are pairwise
\leq_{p}
-incomparable.

Proof.

By proposition
5
(2), it can be assumed that
n\geq 2
.
If
A\leq_{p}B
, by proposition
6
there is a linear polynomial
P
such that
A=P^{-1}(B)
.
Then
P^{-1}
is a polynomial, in fact a linear one, reducing
B
to
A
.
∎

Therefore, if

, the polynomial class
[A]
is the family of all images of
A
under some linear polynomial.
This leads to the following.

Definition 2
.

Let
\alpha
be the action of
\mathcal{L}
on
\mathcal{P}(k)
defined by
\alpha(P,A)=P(A)
.

We also denote
\alpha

.

.

The restriction

Corollary

.

Remark 9
.

From proposition
6

2.1.
The variety
\Sigma_{n}

Corollary
8
suggests the possibility of endowing the orbit space of action
\alpha
, that is
\Sigma_{n}
, with a geometric structure.
In fact, in this section we show that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and, more importantly,
\Sigma_{n}
can be given the structure of a quotient affine variety.
The import of this fact is that it provides a quantitative framework to study the relation
\leq_{p}
.

Let

f_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\prod_{j\neq j^{\prime}}(X_{j}-X_{j^{\prime}})\in k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}].

Then the standard open set
D(f_{n})=k^{n}\setminus V(f_{n})
consists of all elements of
k^{n}
having distinct coordinates.
In particular, the ring of regular functions on
D(f_{n})
is the localised ring
\Gamma(D(f_{n}))=k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]_{f_{n}}=\left\{\frac{g}{f_{n}^{r}}\mid g\in k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}],r\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
.

Remark 10
.

The polynomial
f_{n}
is not the least degree polynomial that identifies
D(f_{n})
: one could use the Vandermonde determinant
\prod_{j<j^{\prime}}(X_{j}-X_{j^{\prime}})
.
The polynomial
f_{n}
has the advantage of being symmetric, which is convenient in the next steps.
Note also that
f_{n}
is homogeneous of degree
n(n-1)
and translation invariant.

Next we describe the structure of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
as an affine variety.
We identify
\mathcal{P}_{n}
as the orbit space of the action of
Sym_{n}
on
D(f_{n})
by permutation of the coordinates.
Then
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is an affine variety by
[
4
, §10]
: it is the geometric quotient of
D(f_{n})
by this action.
Denote
\theta:D(f_{n})\to\mathcal{P}_{n}
the quotient projection.
The ring
\Gamma(\mathcal{P}_{n})
of regular functions on
\mathcal{P}_{n}
consists of those elements of
\Gamma(D(f_{n}))
that are invariant with respect to the action of
Sym_{n}
; this means that if
F\in\Gamma(\mathcal{P}_{n})
then there exist a symmetric
g\in k[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
one has

(8)

F(A)=\frac{g(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})}{(f_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))^{r}}.

By corollary
8
,
\Sigma_{n}
is the orbit space of the action
\alpha
on
\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
Let
\theta^{\prime}:\mathcal{P}_{n}\to\Sigma_{n}
be the quotient projection.
We claim that
\Sigma_{n}
is the geometric quotient of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
by
\alpha
.
For this, we first identify which is the
k
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
consisting of the elements of
\Gamma(\mathcal{P}_{n})
that are invariant under
\alpha
: if
\Sigma_{n}
is a geometric quotient, this is to be the ring of regular functions
\Gamma(\Sigma_{n})
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
F,g,r
be as in (
8
).
Then
F\in\mathcal{A}
if and only if
g
is homogeneous of degree
n(n-1)r
and translation invariant.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)

Assume
F\in\mathcal{A}
.

Let
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{n},s\in k\setminus\{0\}
.
Since
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\},\{sa_{1},\ldots,sa_{n}\}
belong to the same orbit, as witnessed by the linear polynomial
P(X)=sX
, from
F(\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\})=F(\{sa_{1},\ldots,sa_{n}\})
it follows that

\frac{g(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})}{(f_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))^{r}}=\frac{g(sa_{1},\ldots,sa_{n})}{(f_{n}(sa_{1},\ldots,sa_{n}))^{r}}=\frac{g(sa_{1},\ldots,sa_{n})}{s^{n(n-1)r}(f_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))^{r}}

whence
g(sa_{1},\ldots,sa_{n})=s^{n(n-1)r}g(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, showing that
g
is homogeneous of degree
n(n-1)r
.

Let now
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{n},t\in k
.
Since
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\},\{a_{1}+t,\ldots,a_{n}+t\}
belong to the same orbit, as witnessed by the linear polynomial
P(X)=X+t
, from
F(\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\})=F(\{a_{1}+t,\ldots,a_{n}+t\})
it follows that

\frac{g(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})}{(f_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))^{r}}=\frac{g(a_{1}+t,\ldots,a_{n}+t)}{(f_{n}(a_{1}+t,\ldots,a_{n}+t))^{r}}=\frac{g(a_{1}+t,\ldots,a_{n}+t)}{(f_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))^{r}}

whence
g(a_{1}+t,\ldots,a_{n}+t)=g(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, showing that
g
is translation invariant.

(\Leftarrow)

Assume
g
is homogeneous of degree
n(n-1)r
and translation invariant.
Let
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
be in the same orbit.
If
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
, then
B=\{sa_{1}+t,\ldots,sa_{n}+t\}
for some

, with
s\neq 0
.

W_{2}^{i_{2}}W_{3}^{i_{3}}\cdot\ldots\cdot W_{n}^{i_{n}}

where

.

We now apply this fact and the above discussion to answer question

for

Theorem 20
.

\Sigma_{3}
is isomorphic to an affine line.

Proof.

We show that the algebra
\Gamma(\Sigma_{3})
is isomorphic to the algebra of regular functions of an affine line.

By lemma
11

F\in\Gamma(\Sigma_{3})
then
F=\frac{g(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})}{[f_{3}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})]^{\gamma}}
where
g
is symmetric, homogeneous of degree
6\gamma
, and translation invariant.
By applying (
19
), we can write

F=\frac{\sum_{2i_{2}+3i_{3}=6\gamma}\beta_{i_{2}i_{3}}W_{2}^{i_{2}}W_{3}^{i_{3}}}{f_{3}^{\gamma}}

where
f_{3}=\sum_{2j_{2}+3j_{3}=6}\alpha_{j_{2}j_{3}}W_{2}^{j_{2}}W_{3}^{j_{3}}
; since
2j_{2}+3j_{3}=6
means that either
j_{2}=3,j_{3}=0
or
j_{2}=0,j_{3}=2
, we have

—which are not both null.

The relation

implies that
i_{3}
is even, so let

with

, whence

Setting

(20)

F=\frac{\sum_{i^{\prime}_{3}=0}^{\gamma}\beta_{i^{\prime}_{3}}T_{2}^{\gamma-i^{\prime}_{3}}T_{3}^{i^{\prime}_{3}}}{(\alpha_{0}T_{2}+\alpha_{1}T_{3})^{\gamma}}.

The algebra of all functions as in (

in the projective line

Definition 5

Let

So a polynomial class is an exceptional class if and only if there are distinct

such that the planes

described by the system (

\sigma

Exceptional classes have a peculiar role with respect to the relation

So the following questions appear to be natural.

•

What are the exceptional classes?

•

Given an exceptional class, what is its characteristic number?

The polynomial class
[B]
is an exceptional class if and only if given some (equivalently, any)
\pi\in\mathcal{F}_{B}
, the stabiliser of
\pi
under action
\beta
is not the trivial subgroup of
Sym_{n}
; equivalently, if and only if there exists a non-identity permutation
\sigma
such that
\pi_{\sigma}=\pi_{id}
.
Therefore, by (
15
), a polynomial class
[\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}]
is an exceptional class if and only if there exists a non-identity permutation
\sigma
such that, for every

(21)

\lambda_{j}=\frac{b_{\sigma(1)}-b_{\sigma(j)}}{b_{\sigma(1)}-b_{\sigma(2)}},

that is

(22)

\frac{b_{\sigma(1)}-b_{\sigma(2)}}{b_{1}-b_{2}}=\frac{b_{\sigma(1)}-b_{\sigma(j)}}{b_{1}-b_{j}}.

Notice that (

Note also that equations (

Any enumeration

induces an isomorphism (which depends on the chosen enumeration)

The images of a fixed

are conjugate.

If

, given distinct

The following gives a characterisation of exceptional classes in terms of the difference quotient (

Proposition 21

Let

(1)\Rightarrow(2)

(4)\Rightarrow(2)\Rightarrow(3)

(3)\Rightarrow(1)

(3)\Rightarrow(4)

Let

Let

-gon, and let

2.5.

Let
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{m},B=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, for some

Let

be a polynomial such that

and let
\gamma=\deg(P)
.
Since every equation

\gamma

m\leq n\gamma
, that is
\gamma\geq\frac{m}{n}
.

On the other hand, if

a
is a root of multiplicity
r_{a}-1
of the derivative
P^{\prime}
.
Let

for

Since

has degree

.
From the fact that

, it then follows that

, whence

.

Summing up,

(34)

\frac{m}{n}\leq\gamma\leq\frac{m-1}{n-1},

that is

(35)

\gamma(n-1)+1\leq m\leq\gamma n.

In particular, a necessary condition for a set in
\mathcal{P}_{m}
to reduce polynomially to a set in

is that:

(36)

\text{there exists a natural number in }\left[\frac{m}{n},\frac{m-1}{n-1}\right].

Let

, that is the biggest integer such that

.
Then condition (
36
) is equivalent to

\text{either }m=en,\quad\text{ or }m\geq(e+1)n-e.

Notice also that (

), which can be rewritten as

is certainly satisfied when

, since in the union displayed in (

is adjacent to the interval with

, and for bigger values of

By (

contains just one element; when
\gamma=2
use theorem
45
(1) and corollary
38
.
∎

3.
Sets that are infinite and coinfinite

We discuss now subsets of
k
that are infinite and coinfinite.
Recall indeed from proposition
4
that such sets are
\leq_{p}
-incomparable with sets that are finite or cofinite; moreover if the infinite sets
A,B
are
\leq_{p}
-comparable, then

(50)

{\rm{card}}(A)={\rm{card}}(B),\qquad{\rm{card}}(k\setminus A)={\rm{card}}(k\setminus B).

By the remark after proposition
2
, the class of singletons and the class of their complements are maximal elements with respect to
\leq_{p}
.
There are as many other maximal classes as there can be.

Theorem 48
.

There exist
2^{\kappa}
maximal elements in
\Sigma_{\kappa}\cap\check{\Sigma}_{\kappa}
.

Proof.

Recall that if
A<_{p}B
then there exists a polynomial
P
of degree at least
2
reducing
A
to
B
.
Using remark
3
(2), it is enough to build
2^{\kappa}
inequivalent sets
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}\cap\check{\mathcal{P}}_{\kappa}
, such that, for every polynomial
P
of degree at least
2
,
A\neq P^{-1}(P(A))
.

Let
\{P_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha<\kappa}
be an enumeration of all polynomials of degree at least
2
.
By recursion, let
\alpha<\kappa
and assume that elements
a_{\beta},b_{\beta},c_{\beta}\in k
, for every
\beta<\alpha
, have been defined such that all elements
a_{\beta},b_{\beta},c_{\beta}
for
\beta<\alpha
are distinct and with the property that
\forall\beta<\alpha\ P_{\beta}(a_{\beta})=P_{\beta}(b_{\beta})
.

Given any
d\in k
, the multiple roots of the polynomial
P_{\alpha}(X)-d
are also roots of
P_{\alpha}^{\prime}(X)
.
Since
{\rm{card}}(k\setminus P_{\alpha}(\{a_{\beta},b_{\beta},c_{\beta}\}_{\beta<\alpha}))=\kappa
, there must exist
d\in k\setminus P_{\alpha}(\{a_{\beta},b_{\beta},c_{\beta}\}_{\beta<\alpha})
such that
P_{\alpha}(X)-d
has no multiple roots, in particular
P_{\alpha}^{-1}(\{d\})
contains at least two elements: call them
a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha}
, fix also any
c_{\alpha}\in k\setminus(\{a_{\beta},b_{\beta},c_{\beta}\}_{\beta<\alpha}\cup\{a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha}\})
.

Let now
A
be any set containing
\{a_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha<\kappa}
and disjoint from
\{b_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha<\kappa}
.
By construction,
\forall\alpha<\kappa,b_{\alpha}\in P_{\alpha}^{-1}(P_{\alpha}(A))\setminus A
, so that
A
satisfies the requirement.

Finally, there are
2^{\kappa}
such sets that are pairwise inequivalent.
Indeed, there are
2^{\kappa}
sets of the form
\{a_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha<\kappa}\cup C
, where
C\subseteq\{c_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha<\kappa}
; since every polynomial class has at most
\kappa
elements, there is a subfamily of cardinality
2^{\kappa}
of inequivalent such sets.
∎

While theorem
48
states that many subsets of
k
are maximal with respect to
\leq_{p}
, the argument of the proof can be used to show in fact that in the countable case most subsets of
k
are maximal, from a topological viewpoint.
This can be viewed as an analogue for infinite sets of the fact that, geometrically, most polynomial classes of finite sets are
\leq_{p}
-maximal below the unique element of
\Sigma_{1}
, as established in the proof of theorem
47
.

Endow
\mathcal{P}(k)
with the topology whose basis is given by the sets

U_{u_{1}\ldots u_{n}}^{v_{1}\ldots v_{m}}=\{A\in\mathcal{P}(k)\mid u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\in A,v_{1}\ldots v_{m}\notin A\}

for any choice of distinct
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\in k
.
When
k
is countable, this topology is homeomorphic to the Cantor space.

Theorem 49
.

Assume that
k
is countable.
Then the family of
\leq_{p}
-maximal subsets of
k
is a comeagre subset of
\mathcal{P}(k)
.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathcal{P}(k)
.
Then
A
is
\leq_{p}
-maximal if and only if for every polynomial
P
of degree at least
2
:

(a)

either
P
is not a reduction of
A
to
P(A)

(b)

or there exists a polynomial
Q
such that
P(A)=Q^{-1}(A)

We show that the family of
\leq_{p}
-maximal sets contains a dense
G_{\delta}
set.
Since there are countably many polynomials, it is then enough to show that, for any given
P
, the family of sets
A
satisfying (a) is an open dense subset of
\mathcal{P}(k)
.

Condition (a) can be written as
A\neq P^{-1}[P(A)]
; since
A\subseteq P^{-1}[P(A)]
always holds, in turn (a) is equivalent to

(51)

\exists a\in k,(P(a)\in P(A)\land a\notin A)

For fixed
a,P
, both conditions
P(a)\in P(A)
and
a\notin A
are clopen in the argument
A
: the latter by definition of the topology of
\mathcal{P}(A)
; the former because it is equivalent to
a_{1}\in A\lor\ldots\lor a_{l}\in A
, where
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}\}=P^{-1}[P(\{a\})]
, and again by the definition of the topology.
Therefore, for any fixed
P
, condition (
51
) defines an open set.

To show density, fix distinct elements
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\in k
, in order to find some
A\in U_{u_{1}\ldots u_{n}}^{v_{1}\ldots v_{m}}
satisfying (
51
).
Let
b\in k\setminus\{P(u_{1}),\ldots,P(u_{n}),P(v_{1}),\ldots,P(v_{m})\}
such that
P^{-1}(\{b\})
contains at least two distinct elements, and let
u,v
be two of them.
Then any set
A
containing
\{u,u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}
and disjoint from
\{v,v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
, that is any element of
U_{uu_{1}\ldots u_{n}}^{vv_{1}\ldots v_{m}}
, does.
∎

Question 4
.

Do there exist
\leq_{p}
-minimal polynomial classes above
[\emptyset],[k]
?

The same ideas of the proof of theorem
49
show that, when
k
is countable, even if question
4
had a positive answer, from the topological point of view the family of
\leq_{p}
-minimal subsets of
k
above
\emptyset,k
is very small.

Proposition 50
.

Assume that
k
is countable.
Then the family of subsets of
k
that are
\leq_{p}
-minimal above
\emptyset,k
is a meagre subset of
\mathcal{P}(k)
.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathcal{P}(k)
.
Then
A
is minimal above
\emptyset,k
if and only if for every polynomial
P
of degree at least
2
there exists a polynomial
Q
such that
A=Q^{-1}[P^{-1}(A)]
.
In other words, such a subset of
\mathcal{P}(k)
can be written as
\bigcap_{P}\bigcup_{Q}\mathcal{A}_{PQ}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{PQ}=\{A\in\mathcal{P}(k)\mid A=Q^{-1}[P^{-1}(A)]\}
.
So it is enough to show that every
\mathcal{A}_{PQ}
is a closed set with empty interior.

The fact that each
\mathcal{A}_{PQ}
is closed holds since
A\in\mathcal{A}_{PQ}
if and only if

(52)

\forall a\in A,a\in A\Leftrightarrow PQ(a)\in A

To show that the complement is dense, fix distinct
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\in k
in order to show that there exists
A\in U_{u_{1}\ldots u_{n}}^{v_{1}\ldots v_{m}}
such that
A
does not satisfy (
52
).
As the polynomial
PQ
has degree at least
2
, pick any distinct
u,v\in k\setminus\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
such that
v\notin PQ(\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\})
and
PQ(u)=v
.
Then any set
A
containing
\{u,u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}
and disjoint from
\{v,v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
, that is any element of
U_{uu_{1}\ldots u_{n}}^{vv_{1}\ldots v_{m}}
, does.
∎

3.1.
Subfields of
\mathbb{C}

In this section we add the extra assumption
k\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, so that we can exploit some properties of the Euclidean topology.

Note that, in addition to satisfy (
50
), if
A,B\notin\{\emptyset,k\}
are
\leq_{p}
-comparable then if one of them is bounded, the other one is bounded as well; if the complement of one of them is bounded, then the complement of the other one is bounded as well.
The reason is that the preimage under a non-constant polynomial function of a bounded (respectively, unbounded) subset of
\mathbb{C}
is bounded (respectively, unbounded).

Proposition 51
.

There exists a
\leq_{p}
-chain of unbounded and counbounded subsets of
k
that has order type
\zeta
.

Proof.

For
n\in\mathbb{N},m\in\mathbb{Z}
denote
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{m}=\{x\in\mathbb{C}\mid n^{2^{m}}\leq|x|\leq(n+1)^{2^{m}}\}
.
For
m\in\mathbb{Z}
let

C_{m}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{C}_{2n}^{m},\qquad A_{m}=C_{m}\cap k,

thus, if
P(X)=X^{2}
then
\forall m\in\mathbb{Z},A_{m}=P^{-1}(A_{m+1})
.
Therefore it is enough to show that
\forall m\in\mathbb{Z},A_{m+1}\nleq_{p}A_{m}
.

Toward contradiction, suppose that
Q(X)=cX^{\gamma}+\ldots\in k[X]
is a polynomial of degree
\gamma
such that
A_{m+1}=Q^{-1}(A_{m})
, from which it follows also that
Q(A_{m+1})=A_{m}
.
Note that
\gamma\geq 2
.

Denote by
\hat{Q}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
the polynomial function extending
Q
to the complex field, which we endow with the Euclidean topology.
So
\hat{Q}
is surjective and continuous.
By the density of
k
in
\mathbb{C}
it follows that
\hat{Q}(C_{m+1})=C_{m}
.
Since every
\mathcal{C}_{h}^{m+1}
is connected, it follows that for every
h
there exists a unique
l
such that

(53)

\hat{Q}(\mathcal{C}_{h}^{m+1})\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{l}^{m}.

If
\varphi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is the function assigning to each
h
the unique
l
as is (
53
), by the continuity if
\hat{Q}
with respect to the Euclidean topology we get that
\forall h\in\mathbb{N},\varphi(h+1)=\varphi(h)\pm 1
.
Since there must exist
h
such that
\varphi(h)=0
, it follows that eventually
\varphi(h)\leq h
.
In particular, eventually

(54)

|\hat{Q}((h+1)^{2^{m+1}})|\leq(h+1)^{2^{m}}.

However

\forall\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{+},\exists M\in\mathbb{R}^{+},\forall x\in\mathbb{C},|x|>M\Rightarrow\left|\frac{|\hat{Q}(x)|}{|x|^{\gamma}}-|c|\right|<\varepsilon

where the last inequality can be rewritten as
(|c|-\varepsilon)|x|^{\gamma}<|\hat{Q}(x)|<(|c|+\varepsilon)|x|^{\gamma}
.
Therefore, for
h
big enough,
|\hat{Q}((h+1)^{2^{m+1}})|>\frac{|c|}{2}(h+1)^{2^{m+1}\gamma}
, contradicting (
54
).
∎

Remark 52
.

The fact that for finite sets polynomial bireducibility is witnessed by a linear polynomial (see proposition
6
) does not extends to sets that are infinite and coinfinite.

Indeed, using the notation of the proof of proposition
51
, let

A=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{C}_{2}^{2m}\cap k,\qquad B=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{C}_{2}^{2m+1}\cap k.

Letting
P(X)=X^{2}
, it follows that
A=P^{-1}(B),B=P^{-1}(A)
, whence
A\equiv_{p}B
.
However there is no linear polynomial
Q
such that
A=Q^{-1}(B)
, since otherwise, considering the polynomial function
\hat{Q}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
extending
Q
, by the density of
k
in
\mathbb{C}
it would follow that
\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{C}_{2}^{2m}=\hat{Q}^{-1}\left(\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{C}_{2}^{2m+1}\right)
, which is impossible.

4.
Further questions

We have restricted our study to the case of an algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic
0
.
While it is apparent that the hypothesis of algebraic closure has been widely used, we point out that the fact that
k
has characteristic
0
is needed in the the proof of proposition
6
and of lemma
29
, and therefore plays a central role both for the combinatorics of
\leq_{p}
and for the geometric structure of the polynomial classes.

These observations raise the following questions (see also proposition
2
).

Question 5
.

What can be said about
\leq_{p}
when
k
is not algebraically closed?

Question 6
.

What can be said about
\leq_{p}
when
k
has positive characteristic?

The polynomial functions
k\to k
, which we have chosen as the class of reductions inducing the preorder
\leq_{p}
we have studied in this paper, are the morphisms of the affine variety
k
.
Similarly, the polynomial functions
V\to V
are the morphisms of
V
, for any affine variety
V
.
It is then natural to try to extend our study, starting perhaps with the simplest case, namely
k^{n}
.

Question 7
.

Study the relation of polynomial reducibility on
k^{n}
and on other affine varieties.
To what extent the structure of polynomial reducibility is sensible to the geometric properties of the variety?

An answer to the last part of question
7
should be compared with the results of
[
1
]
, where it is shown that most of the geometric properties of the variety are forgotten by the relation of continuous reducibility
\leq_{W}
, whose structure turns out to depend only on few features of the variety, like the cardinality, the dimension, and the decomposition into irreducible components.

Appendix: a variation on Vandermonde

In this section we establish the claim in the proof of lemma
43
.

Given a matrix
M
, denote
R_{1}^{M},R_{2}^{M},\ldots
the rows of
M
and by
C_{1}^{M},C_{2}^{M},\ldots
the columns of
M
.
Let also
M[i,j]
be the entry of
M
on the
i
-th row and
j
-th column.

Definition 10
.

Let
\gamma,h
be natural numbers, with
h\geq 1
.
Fix a sequence
\vec{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{h})
of natural numbers and a sequence
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{h})
of distinct variables.
Define the sequence
\vec{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{h})
by
\begin{cases}r_{1}=1\\
r_{\ell+1}=r_{\ell}+s_{\ell}+1\text{ for }1\leq\ell<h\end{cases}
.
Assume that
R=\sum_{\ell=1}^{h}s_{\ell}+h\leq\gamma+1
.

We call
enriched Vandermonde matrix
, associated to the triple
(\gamma+1,\vec{s},\vec{a})
, the matrix
M
with
R
rows and
\gamma+1
columns such that

M[i,j]=\frac{d^{s}}{da_{\ell}^{s}}a_{\ell}^{j-1},

where
\ell,s
are unique with

(55)

i=r_{\ell}+s,\qquad 0\leq s\leq s_{\ell}.

In other words, an enriched Vandermonde matrix is a matrix, with a number of rows not exceeding the number of columns, consisting of blocks of rows of the form (
46
).
Notice that the matrix
M
of the claim in lemma
43
is an enriched Vandermonde matrix (though its blocks are one row shorter, as in its definition the last line of each block has been erased).

Theorem 53
.

Let
M
be an enriched Vandermonde matrix, with
R
rows and
\gamma+1
columns.
Then
rkM=R
.

Proof.

Use the notation of definition
10
.
Consider the homogeneous linear system associated to matrix
M
, in the unknowns
(c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{\gamma})
.
Then
(c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{\gamma})
is a solution to the system if and only if the polynomial
P(X)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\gamma}c_{\ell}X^{\ell}
has
a_{j}
as root of multiplicity at least
s_{j}+1
, since the equations in each block mean
P(a_{j})=P^{\prime}(a_{j})=\ldots=P^{(s_{j})}(a_{j})=0
.
Let
V
be the subvariety of
k^{\gamma+1}
of the solutions of the system.
Then
V
is a vector subspace of
k^{\gamma+1}
of dimension
\gamma+1-rkM
, so it is enough to prove that
\dim(V)=\gamma+1-R
.

Let
\varphi:k^{\gamma+1}\to k^{\gamma+1}
be the morphism defined by letting

\varphi(c,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\gamma})=\\
=((-1)^{\gamma}cE_{\gamma}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\gamma}),(-1)^{\gamma-1}cE_{\gamma-1}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\gamma}),\ldots,-cE_{1}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\gamma}),c),

where
E_{1},\ldots,E_{\gamma}
are the elementary symmetric functions, so that
\varphi(c,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\gamma})
is the sequence of coefficients of the polynomial
c(X-\alpha_{1})(X-\alpha_{2})\cdot\ldots\cdot(X-\alpha_{\gamma})
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{h}\}
be a collection of disjoint subsets of
\{1,\ldots,\gamma\}
with
{\rm{card}}(F_{j})=s_{j}+1
for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
, and let
W
be the subvariety of
k^{\gamma+1}
, in the coordinates
c,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\gamma}
, obtained by setting
\alpha_{r}=a_{j}
for every
r\in F_{j},j\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
.
Therefore
\dim(W)=\gamma+1-R
.
Now note that the fibers of
\varphi
are finite and that
\varphi(W)=V
, so the result follows from
[
9
, corollary IV.3.8(2)]
.
∎
