Degrees of the stretched Kostka quasi-polynomials
Shiliang Gao, Yibo Gao

Abstract
. We provide a type-uniform formula for the degree of the stretched Kostka quasi-polynomial K λ , μ ​ ( N ) K_{\lambda,\mu}(N) in all classical types, improving a previous result by McAllister in 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 r ​ ( ℂ ) \mathfrak{sl}_{r}(\mathbb{C}) . Our proof relies on a combinatorial model for the weight multiplicity by Berenstein and Zelevinsky.



1.
Introduction

The Kostka numbers
K_{\lambda,\mu}
have great importance in many branches of mathematics, including representation theory, algebraic geometry, symmetric function theory, polytope theory and so on. They can be defined as the dimension of the weight subspace of weight
\mu
in the irreducible representation
V_{\lambda}
with highest weight
\lambda
. In type
A
(i.e.
\mathfrak{sl}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
), they are also well-known as the coefficient of the expansion of Schur functions
s_{\lambda}
into monomial symmetric functions
m_{\mu}
, the number of semistandard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
and weight
\mu
, the number of lattice points in the Gelfand-Tsetlin polytope
\mathrm{GT}_{\lambda}
of weight
\mu
, and many others
[
7
,
14
]
.

One method to study a family of numbers is to organize them together. Define the
stretched Kostka quasi-polynomial
via
K_{\lambda,\mu}(N)=K_{N\lambda,N\mu}
(Definition
1.1
), which is known to be a quasi-polynomial. In type
A
, it is a polynomial and can be investigated via Ehrhart theory on the Gelfand-Tsetlin polytope, which enjoys rich combinatorial and polyhedral geometric properties including its face structures
[
1
,
5
,
10
]
, its connection to order polytopes
[
2
,
13
]
, its combinatorial diameter and automorphism group
[
8
]
. In particular, McAllister utilized the combinatorics of the Gelfand-Tsetlin polytope to compute the degree of the stretched Kostka polynomial in type
A

[
11
]
, resolving a conjecture by King, Tollu, and Toumazet
[
9
]
. However, although an analogue of Gelfand-Tsetlin polytopes is defined in other classical types
[
3
]
, which we call
Berenstein-Zelevinsky
polytope, its various properties are not so well-understood.

In this paper, we provide a type-uniform formula for the degree of the stretched Kostka quasi-polynomial
K_{\lambda,\mu}(N)
in all classical types (Theorem
1.2
), improving a result by McAllister
[
11
]
. Along the way, we study relevant properties of the corresponding Berenstein-Zelevinsky polytope, especially its interior points. In the remainder of the section, we state our main result (Theorem
1.2
). We provide further background in Section
2
and prove the main result in Section
3
.

We adopt the following notations throughout the paper. More background will be provided in Section
2
. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex semisimple Lie algebra with Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
and root system
\Phi\subset\mathfrak{h}^{*}
. Let
r=\dim\mathfrak{g}
, which is also the rank of
\Phi
, denoted
\mathrm{rk}(\Phi)
. Let
\Phi_{+}
be the positive roots,
\Pi=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}\}
be the simple roots and
\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{r}\}
be the corresponding fundamental weights. For dominant weights
\lambda
and
\mu
, let
V_{\lambda}
be the irreducible finite-dimensional representation of
\mathfrak{g}
with highest weight
\lambda
, and let
V_{\lambda}(\mu)\subset V_{\lambda}
be its subspace of weight
\mu
.

Definition 1.1
.

The
stretched Kostka quasi-polynomial
(in
N
) is defined as

K_{\lambda,\mu}(N):=\dim V_{N\lambda}(N\mu).

It is known that
K_{\lambda,\mu}(N)
is a quasi-polynomial in general, which is a polynomial in type
A
. It is also a classical result that
\dim V_{\lambda}(\mu)\neq 0
if and only if
\lambda-\mu
is a nonnegative integral linear combination of the simple roots
[
3
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex semisimple Lie algebra of classical type and dimension
r
. For dominant weights
\lambda,\mu\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
such that
\lambda-\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}\alpha_{i}
with
c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
for all
i
, write
\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\omega_{i}
where
d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
for all
i
. Let

\begin{cases}\Phi^{(1)}\subset\Phi\text{ be the root subsystem spanned by }\{\alpha_{i}\>|\>c_{i}\neq 0\},\\
\Phi^{(2)}\subset\Phi^{(1)}\text{ be the root subsystem spanned by }\{\alpha_{i}\>|\>c_{i}\neq 0,d_{i}=0.\}\end{cases}

Then the degree of the stretched Kostka quasi-polynomial equals

\deg K_{\lambda,\mu}(N)=|\Phi^{(1)}_{+}|-\mathrm{rk}(\Phi^{(1)})-|\Phi^{(2)}_{+}|.

Conjecture 1.3
.

Theorem
1.2
holds for all complex semisimple Lie algebras (of arbitrary types).

2.
Background

We discuss more background on root systems, and then talk about a model in
[
3
]
that allows us to compute
K_{\lambda,\mu}(N)
in classical types.

2.1.
Root system and Weyl groups

Following Section
1
, we continue with more background on root systems. Recall that the positive roots
\Pi=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}\}
form a basis of our ambient vector space
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, which is also called the
weight space
. It is sometimes more convenient to identify this space with its dual, by equipping it with an inner product
\langle-,-\rangle
. The
simple coroots
are defined as

\alpha_{i}^{\vee}=\frac{2\alpha_{i}}{\langle\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i}\rangle}

for
i=1,\ldots,r
. And the fundamental weights
\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{r}\}
are defined as the dual basis to the coroots, i.e.
\langle\alpha_{i}^{\vee},\omega_{j}\rangle=\delta_{i,j}
where
\delta
is the Kronecker delta. A weight
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
is an
integral weight
if
\lambda
is written as
\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\omega_{i}
where
d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
i
. All integral weights form the
weight lattice

Q
. We also say that a weight
\lambda=\sum_{i=1}d_{i}\omega_{i}
is an
dominant integral weight
if
d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
for all
i
. Let
\Lambda^{+}
be the set of all dominant integral weights.

We define a partial order, called the
root poset
, on the positive roots
\Phi_{+}
, where
\alpha\leq\beta
if
\beta-\alpha
is a nonnegative (integral) linear combination of the simple roots
\Pi
. Thus, the minimal elements of the root poset
\Phi_{+}
are precisely the simple roots. Define the
support
of a positive root
\alpha
to be

\Supp(\alpha):=\{\alpha_{i}\in\Pi\>|\>\alpha_{i}\leq\alpha\}\subset\Pi.

We also say that a positive root
\alpha
is
supported on

\alpha_{i}\in\Pi
if
\alpha_{i}\in\Supp(\alpha)
.

Similarly, for
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda^{+}
, we say that
\lambda

dominates

\mu
, if

(1)

\lambda-\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}\alpha_{i},

for some
c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
We say that a pair
(\lambda,\mu)
is
primitive
if
c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
for all
i
in (
1
). We call a primitive pair
simple
if the underlying Lie algebra is simple.

We adopt the following conventions on the root systems for classical types.

•

Type
A_{r-1}
(
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{r}
):
\Phi=\{e_{i}-e_{j}\>|\>1\leq i<j\leq r\}
,
\Phi_{+}=\{e_{i}-e_{j}\>|\>1\leq i<j\leq r\}
,
\alpha_{i}=e_{i}-e_{i+1}
and
\omega_{i}=e_{1}+\cdots+e_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,r-1
.

•

Type
B_{r}
(
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{so}_{2r+1}
):
\Phi=\{\pm e_{i}\pm e_{j}\>|\>1\leq i<j\leq r\}\cup\{\pm e_{i}\>|\>1\leq i\leq r\}
,
\Phi_{+}=\{e_{i}\pm e_{j}\>|\>1\leq i<j\leq r\}\cup\{e_{i}\>|\>1\leq i\leq r\}
,
\alpha_{i}=e_{i}-e_{i+1}
and
\omega_{i}=e_{1}+\cdots+e_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,r-1
, and
\alpha_{r}=e_{r}
and
\omega_{r}=\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+\cdots+e_{r})
.

•

Type
C_{r}
(
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sp}_{2r}
):
\Phi=\{\pm e_{i}\pm e_{j}\>|\>1\leq i<j\leq r\}\cup\{\pm 2e_{i}\>|\>1\leq i\leq r\}
,
\Phi_{+}=\{e_{i}\pm e_{j}\>|\>1\leq i<j\leq r\}\cup\{2e_{i}\>|\>1\leq i\leq r\}
,
\alpha_{i}=e_{i}-e_{i+1}
for
i=1,\ldots,r-1
and
\alpha_{r}=2e_{r}
, and
\omega_{i}=e_{1}+\cdots+e_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
.

•

Type
D_{r}
(
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{so}_{2r}
):
\Phi=\{\pm e_{i}\pm e_{j}\>|\>1\leq i<j\leq r\}
,
\Phi_{+}=\{e_{i}\pm e_{j}\>|\>1\leq i<j\leq r\}
,
\alpha_{i}=e_{i}-e_{i+1}
for
i=1,\ldots,r-1
,
\alpha_{r}=e_{r-1}+e_{r}
,
\omega_{i}=e_{1}+\cdots+e_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,r-2
,
\omega_{r-1}=\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+\cdots+e_{r-1}-e_{r})
and
\omega_{r}=\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+\cdots+e_{r})
.

2.2.
Kostka coefficients

Here we review some basic notions and results related to Kostka coefficients. The Kostka coefficients can be computed via the
Kostant multiplicity formula
, and can be expressed as tensor product multiplicities.

Definition 2.1
.

The
Kostant partition function
is defined by

(2)

P(\lambda-\mu):=\#\{(m_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}:m_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}m_{\alpha}\alpha=\lambda-\mu\}.

For a pair
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda^{+}
such that
\lambda
dominates
\mu
,
the
Kostant multiplicity formula
express
K_{\lambda,\mu}
as alternate sum of Kostant partition functions:

Theorem 2.2
.

K_{\lambda,\mu}=\sum_{w\in W}\det(w)P(w(\lambda+\rho)-\mu-\rho)
,
where
W
is the Weyl group and
\rho=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\alpha
.

Consider the root space decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}
:

(3)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

For
\alpha\in\Pi
, let
e_{\pm\alpha}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\pm\alpha}
and
h_{\alpha}=[e_{\alpha},e_{-\alpha}]\in\mathfrak{h}
be the standard generator.
For each weight space
V_{\lambda}(\beta)\subset V_{\lambda}
and highest weight
\nu=\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi}n_{\alpha}\alpha
, let
V_{\lambda}(\beta,\nu)\subseteq V_{\lambda}(\beta)
be the subspace defined by

(4)

V_{\lambda}(\beta,\nu):=\{v\in V_{\lambda}(\beta):e_{\alpha}^{n_{\alpha}+1}v=0\text{ for }\alpha\in\Pi\}.

Let
c_{\lambda,\nu}^{\mu}
be the multiplicity of
V_{\mu}
in the tensor product
V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\nu}
. It is known (see e.g.
[
15
]
) that
c^{\mu}_{\lambda,\nu}
can be realized as the dimension of certain subspace of
V_{\lambda}
:

(5)

c^{\mu}_{\lambda,\nu}=\dim(V_{\lambda}(\mu-\nu,\nu)).

Lemma 2.3
.

For
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda^{+}
such that
\lambda
dominates
\mu
, let
c_{i}
’s be as in (
1
) and set

\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}\omega_{i}\text{ and }\mu^{\prime}=\nu+\mu.

Then
K_{\lambda,\mu}=c^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\lambda,\nu}
.

Proof.

For any
v\in V_{\lambda}(\mu)
and any
i\in[r]
,

e_{\alpha_{i}}^{c_{i}+1}v\in V_{\lambda}(\mu+(c_{i}+1)\alpha_{i}).

Since
\lambda-\mu-(c_{i}+1)\alpha_{i}=(\sum_{j\neq i}c_{j}\alpha_{j})-\alpha_{i}
,

\lambda
does not dominate
\mu+(c_{i}+1)\alpha_{i}
for any
i\in[r]
. Therefore

\dim(V_{\lambda}(\mu+(c_{i}+1)\alpha_{i}))=0\text{ and }e_{\alpha_{i}}^{c_{i}+1}v=0\text{ for all }i\in[r].

Now by (
4
),

V_{\lambda}(\mu^{\prime}-\nu,\nu)=\{v\in V_{\lambda}(\mu):e_{\alpha_{i}}^{c_{i}+1}v=0\text{ for all }i\in[r]\}=V_{\lambda}(\mu).

We are then done by (
5
) as

K_{\lambda,\mu}=\dim(V_{\lambda}(\mu))=\dim(V_{\lambda}(\mu^{\prime}-\nu,\nu))=c^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\lambda,\nu}.\qed

2.3.
BZ-patterns

Definition 2.4
.

A real
BZ-pattern
in type
B_{r}/C_{r}
(where BZ stands for Berenstein-Zelevinsky) of highest weight
\lambda=\lambda_{11}e_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{1r}e_{r}
is an array of real numbers
\{\lambda_{i,j},\eta_{i,j}\}
with
1\leq i\leq j\leq r
, satisfying the inequalities

(6)

\min(\lambda_{i,j-1},\lambda_{i+1,j-1})\geq\eta_{i,j-1}\geq\max(\lambda_{i,j},\lambda_{i+1,j})

whenever the indices are defined, and
\eta_{i,r}\geq 0
for
1\leq i\leq r
. Denote
|\lambda_{i}|:=\sum_{j}\lambda_{i,j}
and
|\eta_{i}|:=\sum_{j}\eta_{i,j}
. We say that such a BZ-pattern has
weight

\mu=\mu_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+\mu_{r}e_{r}
where
\mu_{i}=|\lambda_{i}|+|\lambda_{i+1}|-2|\eta_{i}|
with the convention that
|\lambda_{r+1}|=0
.

A real BZ-pattern in type
D_{r}
of highest weight
\lambda=\lambda_{11}e_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{1r}e_{r}
is an array of real numbers
\{\lambda_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i\leq j\leq r}\cup\{\eta_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i\leq j<r}
, satisfying (
6
) and

(7)

\lambda_{i,r}+\lambda_{i+1,r}+\min(\lambda_{i,r-1},\lambda_{i+1,r-1})\geq\eta_{i,r-1}

for all
i
with the convention that
\lambda_{i+1,i}=+\infty
. The weight of a real type
D_{r}
BZ-pattern is defined in the same way as in type
B_{r}/C_{r}
.

It is visually more convenient to view BZ-patterns in the following way:

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{11}&&\lambda_{12}&\cdots&\cdots&\lambda_{1r}&\\
&\eta_{11}&&\eta_{12}&\cdots&\cdots&\eta_{1r}\\
&&\lambda_{22}&\cdots&\cdots&\lambda_{2r}&\vdots\\
&&&\ddots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
&&&&&\lambda_{rr}&\vdots\\
&&&&&&\eta_{rr}\end{pmatrix}

where the inequalities in (
6
) dictate that adjacent entries (in the
45^{\circ}
manner) increase from left to right.

Definition 2.5
.

For a dominant weight
\lambda=\lambda_{11}e_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{1r}e_{r}
, define
\BZ_{\lambda}
, the
BZ-polytope
of highest weight
\lambda
, to be the polytope cut off by the inequalities specified in Definition
2.4
. For another dominant weight
\mu
, let the BZ-polytope of highest weight
\lambda
and weight
\mu
,
\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
, be a section of
\BZ_{\lambda}
by imposing the equality
\mu_{i}=|\lambda_{i}|+|\lambda_{i+1}|-2|\eta_{i}|
for
i=1,\ldots,r
, as in Definition
2.4
.

The notion of
\BZ
-patterns allows us to work with classical types almost uniformly. Note that a
\BZ
-pattern of type
D
has different coordinates and additional inequalities comparing to type
B
and
C
.

Definition 2.6
.

A real BZ-pattern
\{\lambda_{i,j},\eta_{i,j}\}
is
integral
of

•

type
C_{r}
, if
\lambda_{i,j},\eta_{i,j}\in\mathbb{Z}
;

•

type
B_{r}
, if all of
\lambda_{i,j}
,
1\leq i\leq j\leq r
and
\eta_{i,j}
,
1\leq i\leq j<r
lie simultaneously in either
\mathbb{Z}
or
\frac{1}{2}+\mathbb{Z}
, and
\eta_{i,r}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
;

•

type
D_{r}
, if all of
\lambda_{i,j}
and
\eta_{i,j}
lie simultaneously in either
\mathbb{Z}
or
\frac{1}{2}+\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
3
]
)
.

In types
B_{r},C_{r},D_{r}
,
K_{\lambda,\mu}
equals the number of integral BZ-patterns of highest weight
\lambda
and weight
\mu
.

3.
Proof of the main theorem, Theorem
1.2

We first provide the general framework to out proof, before going into each subsection for the corresponding step:

(1)

Reduce to the case where the pair
(\lambda,\mu)
is primitive.

(2)

Compute the dimension of the polytope
\BZ_{\lambda}
.

(3)

Identify a point
P\in\BZ_{\lambda}^{\circ}\cap\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
to compute
\dim\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
.

(4)

Finish by establishing the equality
\deg K_{\lambda,\mu}=\dim\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
.

Throughout, consider a root system
\Phi
of rank
r
of classical type.

3.1.
Reduction to simple primitive pairs

For any
\Pi^{\prime}\subset\Pi
, let
\Phi^{\prime}\subset\Phi
be the root subsystem generated by
\Pi^{\prime}
. Let
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{g}
be the subalgebra defined by

(8)

\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi^{\prime}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},

where
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
is spanned by
\{h_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{h}:\alpha\in\Phi^{\prime}\}
. Let
p:\mathfrak{h}^{*}\rightarrow(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})^{*}
be the natural projection.

Lemma 3.1
.

p(\omega_{i})=0
if
\alpha_{i}\notin\Pi^{\prime}
and
p(\omega_{i})
is a fundamental weight of
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
if
\alpha_{i}\in\Pi^{\prime}
. In fact, all fundamental weights of
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
can be written as
p(\omega_{i})
for some
i
.

Proof.

For
\alpha_{i}\notin\Pi^{\prime}
,
\langle\omega_{i},\alpha\rangle=0
for all
\alpha\in\Pi^{\prime}
. Since
\{\alpha\in\Pi^{\prime}\}
span
(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})^{*}
,
\omega_{i}
is orthogonal to
(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})^{*}
and
p(\omega_{i})=0
. Now for
\alpha_{i}\in\Pi^{\prime}
, we have
\alpha_{i}\in(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})^{*}
. Therefore
\langle\omega_{i},\alpha_{i}\rangle=\langle p(\omega_{i}),\alpha_{i}\rangle=1
. For any
\alpha_{j}\in\Pi^{\prime}
such that
i\neq j
, since
\langle\omega_{i},\alpha_{j}\rangle=0
and
\alpha_{j}\in(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})^{*}
, we get
\langle p(\omega_{i}),\alpha_{j}\rangle=0
. Therefore
\langle p(\omega_{i}),\alpha_{j}\rangle=\delta_{i,j}
and
p(\omega_{i})
is a fundamental weight of
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
. Since the number of fundamental weights of
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
equals number of simple roots,
\{p(\omega_{i}):\alpha_{i}\in\Pi^{\prime}\}
are the fundamental weights of
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(
[
3
]
, Proposition 1.3)
.

Let
\lambda,\nu,\mu
be dominant integral weights of
\mathfrak{g}
such that
\lambda+\nu-\mu\in\Phi^{\prime}
. Denote
c_{p(\lambda),p(\nu)}^{p(\mu)}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})
the multiplicity of
V_{p(\mu)}\subset V_{p(\lambda)}\otimes V_{p(\nu)}
as
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
-module. Then

c^{\mu}_{\lambda,\nu}=c^{p(\mu)}_{p(\lambda),p(\nu)}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}).

Lemma 3.3
.

For
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda^{+}
such that
\lambda
dominates
\mu
and
\lambda-\mu\in\Phi^{\prime}
,

K_{\lambda,\mu}=K_{p(\lambda),p(\mu)}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}),

where
K_{p(\lambda),p(\mu)}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})
is the weight multiplicity of
p(\mu)
in the
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
-module
V_{p(\lambda)}
.

Proof.

Since
\lambda
dominates
\mu
and
\lambda-\mu\in\Phi^{\prime}
, we can write

\lambda-\mu=\sum_{\{i:\alpha_{i}\in\Pi^{\prime}\}}c_{i}\alpha_{i}.

By Lemma
2.3
,
K_{\lambda,\mu}=c^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\lambda,\nu}
, where
\nu=\sum_{\{i:\alpha_{i}\in\Pi^{\prime}\}}c_{i}\omega_{i}
and
\mu^{\prime}=\mu+\nu
. Since
\lambda+\nu-\mu^{\prime}=\lambda-\mu\in\Phi^{\prime}
, by Lemma
3.2
,
c^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\lambda,\nu}=c^{p(\mu^{\prime})}_{p(\lambda),p(\nu)}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})
. Since
\lambda-\mu\in(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})^{*}
,

p(\lambda)-p(\mu)=p(\lambda-\mu)=\lambda-\mu=\sum_{\{i:\alpha_{i}\in\Pi^{\prime}\}}c_{i}\alpha_{i}
. By Lemma
3.1
,
p(\nu)=\sum_{\{i:\alpha_{i}\in\Pi^{\prime}\}}c_{i}p(\omega_{i})
and each
p(\omega_{i})
is a fundamental weight of
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
that corresponds to
\alpha_{i}\in(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})^{*}
. Since
p(\mu^{\prime})=p(\mu+\nu)=p(\mu)+p(\nu)
, we can apply Lemma
2.3
again and get
K_{p(\lambda),p(\mu)}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})=c^{p(\mu^{\prime})}_{p(\lambda),p(\nu)}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})
. Therefore

K_{\lambda,\mu}=c^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\lambda,\nu}=c^{p(\mu^{\prime})}_{p(\lambda),p(\nu)}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})=K_{p(\lambda),p(\mu)}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}).\qed

We now return to the context of non-primitive pairs. For a pair
(\lambda,\mu)
that is not primitive, let
\Pi_{0}=\{\alpha_{i}:c_{i}\neq 0\}
be the subset of
\Pi
consisting of simple roots that appears in (
1
). Let
\Phi_{0}\subset\Phi,W_{0}\subset W
be the corresponding root subsystem and Weyl subgroup and
\mathfrak{g}_{0}\subset\mathfrak{g}
the Lie subalgebra as in (
8
). Write
r_{0}=rk(\Phi_{0})
and
\{\omega_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\Pi_{0}\}
the fundamental weights of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
. Since
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
is also semisimple, we can write
\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{m}\mathfrak{g}_{k}
where each
\mathfrak{g}_{k}
is a simple Lie algebra. Let
\mathfrak{h}_{k},\Pi_{k},\Phi_{k},W_{k}
be the Cartan subalgebra, simple roots, root system and Weyl group of
\mathfrak{g}_{k}
. In particular,
\Pi_{0}=\bigsqcup_{k=1}^{m}\Pi_{k}
and
W_{0}=W_{1}\times\ldots\times W_{m}
. Let
\rho_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}_{k}}\alpha
be the half sum of positive roots in
\Phi_{k}
.
Define
p_{k}:\mathfrak{h}^{*}\rightarrow(\mathfrak{h_{k}})^{*}
to be the natural projection.

Proposition 3.4
.

There exists pairs
(\lambda^{(k)},\mu^{(k)})_{k\in[m]}
such that each
(\lambda^{(k)},\mu^{(k)})
is a primitive pair of
\mathfrak{g}_{k}
and

K_{\lambda,\mu}=\prod_{k=1}^{m}K_{\lambda^{(k)},\mu^{(k)}}(\mathfrak{g}_{k}).

Proof.

Let
\lambda^{(i)}=p_{i}(\lambda)
and
\mu^{(i)}=p_{i}(\mu)
for all
i\in[0,m]
. By Lemma
3.3
,

(9)

K_{\lambda,\mu}=K_{\lambda^{(0)},\mu^{(0)}}(\mathfrak{g}_{0}).

For any
k\in[m]
, set

\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi_{k}}\langle\lambda^{(0)},\alpha\rangle\omega_{\alpha}\text{ and }\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi_{k}}\langle\mu^{(0)},\alpha\rangle\omega_{\alpha}.

Notice that
\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)},\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}\in(\mathfrak{h}_{k})^{*}\subset\mathfrak{h}^{*}
and
\lambda^{(0)}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)}
and
\mu^{(0)}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}
. By (
2.2
),

K_{\lambda^{0},\mu^{(0)}}(\mathfrak{g}_{0})=\sum_{w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})\in W_{0}}(\prod_{k=1}^{m}\det(w_{k}))P(w(\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)}+\rho_{k}))-\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}-\rho_{k}))

Since
\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)},\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}
are invariant under
W_{j}
for all
j\neq k
, for
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})\in W
,

P(w(\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)}+\rho_{k}))-\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}-\rho_{k}))=P(\sum_{k=1}^{m}(w_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)}+\rho_{k})-\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}-\rho_{k})).

Since
w_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)}+\rho_{k})-\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}-\rho_{k})\in(\mathfrak{h}_{k})^{*}
for all
k\in[m]
,

P(\sum_{k=1}^{m}(w_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)}+\rho_{k})-\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}-\rho_{k}))=\prod_{k=1}^{m}P(w_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)}+\rho_{k})-\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}-\rho_{k}).

Therefore

(10)

\displaystyle K_{\lambda^{0},\mu^{(0)}}(\mathfrak{g}_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})\in W}\prod_{k=1}^{m}(\det(w_{k})P(w_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)}+\rho_{k})-\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}-\rho_{k}))

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{w_{k}\in W_{k}}(\det(w_{k})P(w_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)}+\rho_{k})-\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}-\rho_{k}))

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{m}K_{\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)},\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}}(\mathfrak{g}_{0}).

Define
\lambda^{(k)}=p_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)})
and
\mu^{(k)}=p_{k}(\tilde{\mu}^{(k)})
. Since
\lambda^{(k)}-\mu^{(k)}=p_{k}(\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)}-\tilde{\mu}^{(k)})=p_{k}(\lambda^{(0)}-\mu^{(0)})
and every simple root of
\mathfrak{g}_{k}
appear in the expansion of
\lambda^{(0)}-\mu^{(0)}
,
(\lambda^{(k)},\mu^{(k)})
is a primitive pair of
\mathfrak{g}_{k}
for all
k\in[m]
. By Lemma
3.3
,
K_{\tilde{\lambda}^{(k)},\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}}(\mathfrak{g}_{0})=K_{\lambda^{(k)},\mu^{(k)}}(\mathfrak{g}_{k})
. We are then done by (
9
) and (
10
).
∎

An immediate consequence of Proposition
3.4
is the following:

Corollary 3.5
.

\deg K_{\lambda,\mu}(\mathfrak{g})(N)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\deg K_{\lambda^{(k)},\mu^{(k)}}(\mathfrak{g}_{k})(N)
.

By induction on
k
, it is now enough to prove Theorem
1.2
when
(\lambda,\mu)
is a simple primitive pair.

3.2.
Dimension of the BZ-polytope
\BZ_{\lambda}

Recall that the BZ-polytopes have coordinates indxed by

\begin{cases}\{\lambda_{i,j}\}_{1<i\leq j\leq r}\text{ and }\{\eta_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i\leq j\leq r}\text{ for type }B_{r}\text{ and }C_{r},\\
\{\lambda_{i,j}\}_{1<i\leq j\leq r}\text{ and }\{\eta_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i\leq j<r}\text{ for type }D_{r}.\end{cases}

The number of coordinates equals
|\Phi_{+}|
, where
\Phi
is the root system that we are working with.
Note that since
\lambda=\sum\lambda_{1i}e_{i}
is fixed, we choose not to consider
\lambda_{11},\ldots\lambda_{1r}
as coordinates for
\BZ_{\lambda}
and
\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\omega_{i}
be a dominant weight in a root system
\Phi
where
d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq}0
for all
i
. Let
\Phi^{\prime}\subset\Phi
be the root subsystem of
\Phi
spanned by
\{\alpha_{i}\>|\>d_{i}=0\}
. Then we have that

\dim\BZ_{\lambda}=|\Phi_{+}|-|\Phi^{\prime}_{+}|.

We prove Lemma
3.6
using two steps: upper bounding the dimension by figuring out conditions on the coordinates; and lower bounding the dimension by providing a certain neighborhood around an interior point.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
in type
B/C
.

We adopt the conventions in Section
2
. Let the simple roots of
\Phi^{\prime}
be
S^{\prime}
and let
S^{\prime}=Z_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup Z_{m}
be its partition into the connected components of the Dynkin diagram of
\Phi^{\prime}
where the indices are in order. Write
z_{i}=|Z_{i}|
for all
i
. Since
\omega_{j}=e_{1}+\cdots+e_{j}
for all
j
, if
j\in S^{\prime}
,
\lambda_{1,j}=\lambda_{1,j+1}
, with the convention that
\lambda_{1,r+1}=0
.

Case 1:

r\in S^{\prime}
. Here

are of type
A

is of type
B/C

|\Phi^{\prime}_{+}|=\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}{z_{i}+1\choose 2}+z_{m}^{2}.

For each

\lambda_{1,t+1}=\cdots=\lambda_{1,t+z_{i}+1}
, and thus all the

|\Phi^{\prime}_{+}|
fixed coordinates so
\dim\BZ_{\lambda}\leq|\Phi_{+}|-|\Phi^{\prime}_{+}|
.

, as follows. The coordinates
\lambda_{1,1},\ldots,\lambda_{1,r}
have been given. For all the other coordinates, take

and
\eta_{i,j}=(\lambda_{i,j}+\lambda_{i,j+1})/2

3.3.
Dimension of the BZ-polytope
\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}

Let
\lambda
be a dominant weight in a root system
\Phi
of rank
r
as above. Consider the linear projection
\wt:\BZ_{\lambda}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r}
which sends a BZ-pattern to its weight (See Definition
2.4
). Denote the image of
\wt
by
P_{\lambda}
, called the
weight polytope
in the literature. Recall that we have defined the
\BZ
-polytope
\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
to be
\wt^{-1}(\mu)
. The following theorem is well-known, see for example
[
4
]
.

Theorem 3.7
.

The image of
P_{\lambda}=\wt(\BZ_{\lambda})
, is a polytope defined by

P_{\lambda}=\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\>|\>\langle\lambda,\omega_{i}\rangle\geq\langle\mu,u\omega_{i}\rangle,\text{ for all }i=1,\ldots,r\text{ and }u\in W\},

or equivalently, by

P_{\lambda}=\mathrm{Conv}(\{u\lambda\>|\>u\in W\}),

where
W
is the Weyl group. Moreover,
V_{\lambda}(\mu)\neq 0
if and only if
\mu\in P_{\lambda}
and that
\lambda-\mu
is a nonnegative integral linear combination of the simple roots.

We need another lemma that is purely polytopal.

Lemma 3.8

(
[
11
]
, Lemma 3.1)
.

Given a polytope
P\in\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a linear map
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
(\pi(P))^{\circ}\subset\pi(P^{\circ})
, where
P^{\circ}
is the interior of
P
.

We are now ready to deduce the dimension of
\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
(\lambda,\mu)
be a primitive pair of dominant weights in a root system
\Phi
of rank
r
. Then
\dim\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}=\dim\BZ_{\lambda}-r
.

Proof.

Since
\mu
is dominant,
\langle\mu,u\omega_{i}\rangle\leq\langle\mu,\omega_{i}\rangle
for all
u\in W
and
i=1,\ldots,r
. Since
(\lambda,\mu)
is a primitive pair,
\lambda-\mu
is a positive linear combination of simple roots, meaning that
\langle\lambda-\mu,\omega_{i}\rangle>0
for all
i
. As a result,
\langle\lambda,\omega_{i}\rangle>\langle\mu,u\omega_{i}\rangle
for all
u\in W
and
i=1,\ldots,r
. All the defining inequalities of
P_{\lambda}
in Theorem
3.7
are strictly for
\mu
, so
\mu\in P_{\lambda}^{\circ}
. Recall
P_{\lambda}=\wt(\BZ_{\lambda})
. By Lemma
3.8
,
\mu\in(\wt(\BZ_{\lambda}))^{\circ}\subset\wt(\BZ_{\lambda}^{\circ})
. Thus, there exists a BZ-pattern
T\in\BZ_{\lambda}^{\circ}
such that
\wt(T)=\mu
. Let the coordinates of
T
be
\{\lambda_{i,j}\}\cup\{\eta_{i,j}\}
as before.

As a quick summary, we have a point
T
in the interior of
\BZ_{\lambda}
with weight
\mu
. Now the polytope
\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
can be viewed by intersecting
\BZ_{\lambda}
with
r
hyperplanes
H_{1},\ldots,H_{r}
which are the
r
defining equations (see Definition
2.4
) of the weight
\mu
that all pass through
T
. One directly sees that these defining equations are upper triangular with respect to the coordinates
\{\lambda_{i,j}\}\cup\{\eta_{i,j}\}
so the normals of
H_{1},\ldots,H_{r}
are linearly independent and
K:=H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{r}
has codimension
r
. As the linear subspace
K
passes through an interior point of
\BZ_{\lambda}
,
\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}=K\cap\BZ_{\lambda}
must have codimension
r
in
\BZ_{\lambda}
. Therefore,
\dim\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}=\dim\BZ_{\lambda}-r
as desired.
∎

3.4.
Finishing the proof

We use the following classical result in rational Ehrhart theory.

Theorem 3.10

(
[
6
,
12
]
)
.

Let
Q
be a rational polytope, i.e. the convex hull of finitely many points in
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
. Then
|NQ\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|
is a quasi-polynomial in
N
of degree
\dim Q
, for a positive integer
N
.

We are now ready for the final step of our main proof.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
(\lambda,\mu)
be a primitive pair of dominant weight in a root system of classical type. Then
\deg K_{\lambda,\mu}(N)=\dim\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
.

Proof.

From Definition
2.5
, we know that
\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
is bounded by equalities and inequalities with integral coefficients, and is thus a rational polytope. Since all the equalities and inequalities involved do not have constant terms,
\BZ_{N\lambda,N\mu}=N\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
for a positive integer
N
.

By Definition
2.6
and Theorem
2.7
, in type
C_{r}
,
K_{\lambda,\mu}(N)=K_{N\lambda,N\mu}=|\mathbb{Z}^{|\Phi_{+}|}\cap N\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}|
which is a quasi-polynomial in
N
of degree
\dim\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
. In type
D_{r}
,

K_{\lambda,\mu}(N)=K_{N\lambda,N\mu}=\left|\mathbb{Z}^{|\Phi_{+}|}\cap N\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}\right|+\left|(\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2})^{|\Phi_{+}|}\cap N\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}\right|

which is the sum of two quasi-polynomials in
N
of degree
\dim\BZ_{\lambda,\mu}
(by properly shifting the coordinate axis). As for type
B_{r}
, we use the same argument in type
D_{r}
after scaling the coordinates
\eta_{i,r}
by a factor of
2
, for
i=1,\ldots,r
.
∎

Finally, the proof of Theorem
1.2
follows from Corollary
3.5
, Lemma
3.6
, Lemma
3.9
and Lemma
3.11
.
