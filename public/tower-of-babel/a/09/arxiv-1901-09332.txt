Solution of an Open Problem about Two Families of Orthogonal Polynomials This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Orthogonal Polynomials, Special Functions and Applications (OPSFA14). The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/OPSFA2017.html
Walter VAN ASSCHE

Abstract
An open problem about two new families of orthogonal polynomials was posed by Alhaidari. Here we will identify one of them as Wilson polynomials. The other family seems to be new but we show that they are discrete orthogonal polynomials on a bounded countable set with one accumulation point at 0 0 and we give some asymptotics as the degree tends to infinity.

Introduction

Abdulaziz D. Alhaidari
[
4
]
submitted the following open problem for the proceedings of the OPSFA-14 conference.

Using an algebraic method for solving the wave equation in quantum mechanics, Alhaidari and co-authors
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
encountered two families of orthogonal polynomials on the real line. These polynomials are defined by their three-term recurrence relations and initial values. The weight functions (orthogonality measures), generating functions, orthogonality relations, etc. are yet to be derived analytically. See
[
3
]
for more physical background.

The first family is a four parameter family of orthogonal polynomials
H_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\alpha,\theta)
given by the recursion

\displaystyle\cos\theta H_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\alpha,\theta)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\left(z\sin\theta\left[\left(n+\frac{\mu+\nu+1}{2}\right)^{2}+\alpha\right]+\frac{\nu^{2}-\mu^{2}}{(2n+\mu+\nu)(2n+\mu+\nu+2)}\right)H_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\alpha,\theta)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}+\frac{2(n+\mu)(n+\nu)}{(2n+\mu+\nu)(2n+\mu+\nu+1)}H_{n-1}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\alpha,\theta)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}+\frac{2(n+1)(n+\mu+\nu+1)}{(2n+\mu+\nu+1)(2n+\mu+\nu+2)}H_{n+1}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\alpha,\theta),

(1.1)

with
0\leq\theta\leq\pi
,
\mu,\nu>-1
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. The initial values are
H_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\alpha,\theta)=1
and
H_{-1}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\alpha,\theta)=0
.

The second family is a three-parameter family of orthogonal polynomials
G_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\sigma)
which satisfies the recurrence

\displaystyle zG_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\sigma)=\left(\big(\sigma+B_{n}^{2}\big)\left[\frac{\mu^{2}-\nu^{2}}{(2n+\mu+\nu)(2n+\mu+\nu+2)}+1\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hphantom{zG_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\sigma)=}{}-\frac{2n(n+\nu)}{2n+\mu+\nu}-\frac{(\mu+1)^{2}}{2}\right)G_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\sigma)

\displaystyle\hphantom{z G_n^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\sigma) =}{}-\big(\sigma+B_{n-1}^{2}\big)\frac{2(n+\mu)(n+\nu)}{(2n+\mu+\nu)(2n+\mu+\nu+1)}G_{n-1}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\sigma)

\displaystyle\hphantom{z G_n^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\sigma) =}{}-\big(\sigma+B_{n}^{2}\big)\frac{2(n+1)(n+\mu+\nu+1)}{(2n+\mu+\nu+1)(2n+\mu+\nu+2)}G_{n+1}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\sigma),

(1.2)

with
B_{n}=n+1+\frac{\mu+\nu}{2}
,
\mu,\nu>-1
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and initial values
G_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\sigma)=1
,
G_{-1}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\sigma)=0
.

Open Problem

2
The first family of orthogonal polynomials

For the first family of orthogonal polynomials, the monic polynomials are given by
P_{n}(z)=H_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\alpha,\theta)/k_{n}
, where

\displaystyle\frac{k_{n+1}}{k_{n}}=-\frac{\sin\theta\bigl[\big(n+\frac{\mu+\nu+1}{2}\big)^{2}+\alpha\bigr](2n+\mu+\nu+1)(2n+\mu+\nu+2)}{2(n+1)(n+\mu+\nu+1)}.

The recurrence relation then becomes

\displaystyle zP_{n}(z)=P_{n+1}(z)+b_{n}P_{n}(z)+a_{n}^{2}P_{n-1}(z),

with recurrence coefficients

\displaystyle a_{n}^{2}=\frac{4n(n+\mu)(n+\nu)(n+\mu+\nu)}{\sin^{2}\theta\bigl[\big(n\!+\!\frac{\mu{+}\nu{+}1}{2}\big)^{2}\!+\!\alpha\bigr]\bigl[\big(n\!+\!\frac{\mu{+}\nu{-}1}{2}\big)^{2}\!+\!\alpha\bigr](2n\!+\!\mu\!+\!\nu\!+\!1)(2n\!+\!\mu\!+\!\nu)^{2}(2n\!+\!\mu\!+\!\nu\!-\!1)}

and

\displaystyle b_{n}=\frac{1}{\sin\theta\bigl[\big(n+\frac{\mu+\nu+1}{2}\big)^{2}+\alpha\bigr]}\left(\cos\theta+\frac{\mu^{2}-\nu^{2}}{(2n+\mu+\nu)(2n+\mu+\nu+2)}\right).

From this we can already deduce that for
\theta=\pi/2

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\alpha^{n}P_{n}(z/\alpha)=P_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z),

where
P_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}
are the monic Jacobi polynomials. This follows by taking the limit for
\alpha\to\infty
in the recurrence coefficients which, after appropriate scaling, gives the recurrence coefficients of the Jacobi polynomials.
Another useful observation is that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}^{2}=0,\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}=0,

and in fact
a_{n}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\big(1/n^{4}\big)
and
b_{n}=\mathcal{O}\big(1/n^{2}\big)
. This implies that the Jacobi matrix

\displaystyle J=\begin{pmatrix}b_{0}&a_{1}&0&0&0&\cdots\\
a_{1}&b_{1}&a_{2}&0&0&\cdots\\
0&a_{2}&b_{2}&a_{3}&0&\cdots\\
0&0&a_{3}&b_{3}&a_{4}&\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}

is a compact operator, and in fact it is a trace class operator. This implies that the spectrum of
J
is a countable set
\{x_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}\}
with one accumulation point at
0
, hence
\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}x_{k}=0
. The trace class condition implies that
\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}|x_{k}|
is finite. Consequently the orthogonality measure for the first family is a discrete measure supported on this countable set. See
[
16
]
for more information on compact operators and orthogonal polynomials.

The asymptotic behavior is given by

Theorem 2.1
.

There exists an entire function
Q
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}z^{n}P_{n}(1/z)=Q(z),

(2.1)

uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
. The function
Q
has infinitely many zeros at the points
\{1/x_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

Proof 2.2
.

We introduce the reversed polynomials
Q_{n}(z)=z^{n}P_{n}(1/z)
. We have
Q_{n}(0)=1
since the
P_{n}
are monic polynomials.
The three-term recurrence relation then becomes

\displaystyle Q_{n}(z)=Q_{n+1}(z)+b_{n}zQ_{n}(z)+a_{n}^{2}z^{2}Q_{n-1}(z),

(2.2)

from which we easily find

\displaystyle Q_{k+1}(z)-Q_{k}(z)=-b_{k}zQ_{k}(z)-a_{k}^{2}z^{2}Q_{k-1}(z).

Summing from
k=0
to
n-1
then gives

\displaystyle Q_{n}(z)=Q_{0}(z)-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k}zQ_{k}(z)-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}a_{k}^{2}z^{2}Q_{k-1}(z)

\displaystyle\hphantom{Q_n(z)}{}=1-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k}zQ_{k}(z)-\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}a_{k+1}^{2}z^{2}Q_{k}(z).

(2.3)

Let
K
be a compact set in
\mathbb{C}
, then there exists an
R>0
such that
|z|\leq R
for
z\in K
and from (
2.3
) we find

\displaystyle|Q_{n}(z)|\leq 1+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\big(|b_{k}|R+a_{k+1}^{2}R^{2}\big)|Q_{k}(z)|.

We can then use the discrete version of Gronwall’s inequality (see
[
15
, p. 440]
) to find that

\displaystyle|Q_{n}(z)|\leq\exp\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\big(|b_{k}|R+a_{k+1}^{2}R^{2}\big)\right),

so that uniformly for
z\in K

\displaystyle|Q_{n}(z)|\leq M=\exp\left(R\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|b_{k}|+R^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k+1}^{2}\right).

We can then use Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem and take the limit
n\to\infty
in (
2.3
) to find

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}(z)=1-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\big(b_{k}z+a_{k+1}^{2}z^{2}\big)Q_{k}(z):=Q(z),

(2.4)

and the sum converges uniformly for
z\in K
. The limit function
Q
is therefore an entire function, and its zeros are limits of the zeros of
Q_{n}
, which in turn are
\{1/x_{n,k},1\leq k\leq n\}
, where
x_{n,k}
are the zeros of
P_{n}
.

Theorem
2.1
gives the existence of an entire function
Q
for which (
2.1
) holds. The formula (
2.4
) is not very convenient to describe the limit function, since it is in terms of the polynomials
Q_{k}
that we are investigating. In order to find more information on
Q
, we can write

\displaystyle Q_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{n,k}z^{k},\qquad Q(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{k},

and then
c_{n,0}=1=c_{0}
and

\displaystyle c_{k}=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n,k}.

From the recurrence relation (
2.2
) we find

\displaystyle c_{n,k}=c_{n+1,k}+b_{n}c_{n,k-1}+a_{n}^{2}c_{n-1,k-2},

which, in principle, allows to compute the coefficients
c_{n,k}
recursively. The first few coefficients are

\displaystyle c_{n,1}=-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k},\quad c_{n,2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}b_{k}b_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}a_{k}^{2},

from which we find

\displaystyle c_{1}=-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k},\quad c_{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}b_{k}b_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}.

These sums are all absolutely convergent. Their explicit value depends on the parameters
\mu
,
\nu
,
\alpha
,
\theta

and involves the expression

\displaystyle\psi\left(\frac{\mu+\nu+1}{2}-\sqrt{-\alpha}\right)-\psi\left(\frac{\mu+\nu+1}{2}+\sqrt{-\alpha}\right),

where
\psi(z)=\Gamma^{\prime}(z)/\Gamma(z)
is the Psi function
[
12
,
13
, Section 5.2]
, so that a distinction between the cases
\alpha>0
and
\alpha<0
may be needed. No further analysis of the function
Q
has been done.

3
The second family of orthogonal polynomials

For the second family the monic polynomials are
P_{n}(z)=G_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z;\sigma)/k_{n}
, where

\displaystyle\frac{k_{n}}{k_{n+1}}=-\big(\sigma+B_{n}^{2}\big)\frac{2(n+1)(n+\mu+\nu+1)}{(2n+\mu+\nu+1)(2n+\mu+\nu+2)}.

The recurrence coefficients for the monic polynomials are then given by

\displaystyle a_{n}^{2}=\big(\sigma+B_{n-1}^{2}\big)^{2}\frac{4n(n+\mu)(n+\nu)(n+\nu+\mu)}{(2n+\mu+\nu-1)(2n+\mu+\nu)^{2}(2n+\mu+\nu+1)},

and

\displaystyle b_{n}=\big(\sigma+B_{n}^{2}\big)\left(\frac{\mu^{2}-\nu^{2}}{(2n+\mu+\nu)(2n+\mu+\nu+2)}+1\right)-\frac{2n(n+\nu)}{2n+\mu+\nu}-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mu+1)^{2}.

Again there is a limit transition to Jacobi polynomials:

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to\infty}\sigma^{-n}P_{n}(\sigma z)=P_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}(z-1),

where
P_{n}^{(\mu,\nu)}
are the monic Jacobi polynomials.
The recurrence coefficients have the asymptotic behavior

\displaystyle a_{n}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{4}{n^{4}}+\mathcal{O}\big(n^{3}\big),\qquad b_{n}=n^{2}+\mathcal{O}(n),

so that they are unbounded. The spectrum of the Jacobi matrix (and the support of the orthogonality measure for the polynomials) is therefore
unbounded. It was noted by Yutian Li
[
11
]
that the recurrence coefficients correspond to a special case of the Wilson polynomials

W_{n}(x;a,b,c,d)
, which are on top of the Askey table
[
9
, Section 9.1]
. The identification is

\displaystyle G_{n}^{(\mu;\nu)}(z,\sigma)=\frac{W_{n}(z/2;a,b,c,d)}{(a+b)_{n}(a+d)_{n}},

where the parameters
a
,
b
,
c
,
d
are given by

\displaystyle a=\tfrac{1}{2}{(\mu+1)}=c,\qquad b=\tfrac{1}{2}{(\mu+1)}+s,\qquad d=\tfrac{1}{2}{(\mu+1)}-s,

with
s=\sqrt{-\sigma}
.
