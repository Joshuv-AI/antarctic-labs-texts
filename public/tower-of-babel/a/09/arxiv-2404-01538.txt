An Infinite Family of Real Quadratic Fields with Three Classes of Perfect Unary Forms
Christian Porter

Abstract
In this paper, we revisit the theory of perfect unary forms over real quadratic fields. Specifically, we deduce an infinite family of real quadratic fields ℚ ⁡ ( d ) \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d}) when d = 2 d=2 or 3 3 mod 4 4 , such that there are three classes of perfect unary forms up to homothety and equivalence. This work, along with the work in [ 3 ] , seems to suggest that the number of classes of perfect unary forms is related to the fundamental unit of K K .



1
Introduction

Let
K
be a totally real number field over
\mathbb{Q}
of degree
n
. A quadratic form
f:K^{m}\to K
, defined by

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}f_{i,j}x_{i}x_{j}

is said to be positive definite if the matrix
\{\sigma(f_{i,j})\}_{i,j=1}^{m}
is positive-definite for all embeddings
\sigma:K\to\mathbb{R}
. If
f
is defined in a single variable, i.e.
m=1
, we call
f(x)=ax^{2}
a unary form, which is said to be a positive unary form if
\sigma(a)>0
for all embeddings
\sigma
, and we say that
a
is a totally positive element of
K
. We will denote by
K_{>>0}
the space of totally positive elements of
K
. From now on, when referring to unary forms we will always assume that the unary form is positive.
For a unary form
ax^{2}
of
K
, we will denote by

\displaystyle\mu(a)\triangleq\min_{x\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\setminus\{0\}}\text{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(ax^{2}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a)=\{x\in\mathcal{O}_{K}:\text{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(ax^{2})=\mu(a)\},

where
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is the ring of integers of
K
(i.e. the minimal nonzero value and minimal vectors of the corresponding rational quadratic form generated by the algebraic trace of the unary form, respectively). A unary form
ax^{2}
is said to be a perfect unary form if it is uniquely determined by
\mu(a),\mathcal{M}(a)
. Note that for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
,
\mu(\lambda a)=\lambda\mu(a),\mathcal{M}(\lambda a)=\mathcal{M}(a)
and so perfect unary forms can be considered by their homothety classes.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
denote the unit group of
K
. We say that two unary forms
ax^{2},a^{\prime}x^{2}
are equivalent if
a^{\prime}=au^{2}
for some
u\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
. We denote by
n_{K}
the number of
GL_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
-inequivalent homothety classes of perfect forms of a totally real number field
K
.

In
[
5
]
, if we let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
for
d
some square-free positive integer, the author computed
n_{K}
for
d<200000
. Moreover, in the same paper it was proven that there were infinitely many
K
such that
n_{K}=1
. In
[
3
]
, it was proven that
n_{K}=1
if and only if
d
was of one of four types:

•

T_{1}
:
d=n^{2}+1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n
odd,

•

T_{2}
:
d=n^{2}-1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n
even,

•

T_{3}
:
d=n^{2}+4
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n
odd,

•

T_{4}
:
d=n^{2}-4
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n>3
odd.

In the same paper, it was shown that there exists an infinite family of real quadratic fields with
n_{K}=2
(namely, when
K
is of Richaud-Degert type
[
1
]
). In this paper, we deduce an infinite family of real quadratic fields with
n_{K}=3
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
for some positive, square-free integer
d
, with
d\equiv 2
or
3\mod 4
. Write
d=n^{2}+r
for integers
n,r
satisfying
-n<r\neq\pm 1\leq n
. Let
\alpha+\beta\sqrt{d}
, and suppose
\beta=m(m+2)
for some odd integer
m\geq 3
. Then:

•

If
\alpha\equiv\pm 1\mod\beta^{2}
,
n_{K}=2
. All perfect forms are homothetically equivalent to, or equivalent via some
GL_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
transform to, either
a_{1}x^{2}
or
a_{2}x^{2}
, where

\displaystyle a_{1}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2n^{2}+r-1}{4(n^{2}+r)n}\sqrt{d},

\displaystyle a_{1}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2n^{2}+r-1}{4(n^{2}+r)n}\sqrt{d}.

•

If
\alpha\equiv\pm\left(\frac{m-1}{2}(m+2)^{2}+1\right)\mod\beta^{2}
,
n_{K}=3
. All perfect forms are homothetically equivalent to, or equivalent via some
GL_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
transform to, either
a_{1}x^{2},a_{2}x^{2}
or
a_{3}x^{2}
, where
a_{1},a_{2}
are defined as before, and

\displaystyle a_{3}=\alpha+\beta\sqrt{d}.

The first point in this theorem was proven in
[
3
]
, see Theorem 1.3 (this follows from the fact that such fields are of Richaud-Degert type). The case where
r=\pm 1
was also tackled in the same paper. Therefore, we will only prove the second point of this theorem in this paper.

2
Proof of Theorem
1

First, we will prove the following useful lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
with
d>0,d\equiv 2
or
3
mod
4
. Suppose that
d=n^{2}+r
for some integers
n,r
,
-n<r\neq\pm 1\leq n
. Then the unary forms
a_{1}x^{2},a_{2}x^{2}
are perfect, with

\displaystyle a_{1}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2n^{2}+r-1}{4n(n^{2}+r)}\sqrt{d},

\displaystyle a_{2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2n^{2}+r-1}{4n(n^{2}+r)}\sqrt{d}

Proof.

It suffices to show that
a_{1}x^{2}
has at least two minimal vectors (since
a_{2}
is a conjugate of
a_{1}
, if
a_{1}x^{2}
is perfect it immediately follows that
a_{2}x^{2}
is also perfect). Note that
\text{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(a_{1})=\text{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(a_{1}(n-\sqrt{d})^{2})=1
. We now want to show that
\mu(a)=1
.

Let
\gamma=\frac{2n^{2}+r-1}{2n(n^{2}+r)}
. Then

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(a_{1}(x_{0}+x_{1}\sqrt{d})^{2})=(x_{0}+x_{1}d\gamma)^{2}+(d-d^{2}\gamma^{2})x_{1}^{2},

(1)

for any
x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathbb{Q}
. We have

\displaystyle d-d^{2}\gamma^{2}=n^{2}+r-\frac{(2n^{2}+r-1)^{2}}{4n^{2}}=1-\frac{(r-1)^{2}}{4n^{2}}>1/4,

for all
n>0
,
-n<r\leq n
, so if
|x_{1}|\geq 2
,
x_{0}+x_{1}\sqrt{d}\not\in\mathcal{M}(a_{1})
. Obviously if
x_{0}+x_{1}\sqrt{d}\in\mathcal{M}(a_{1})
and
x_{1}=0
, then
x_{0}=1
. Moreover, we must have
x_{1}\leq 0
since
\gamma>0
, so we assume that
x_{1}=-1
.

The minimum of
\text{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(a_{1}(x_{0}-\sqrt{d})^{2})
is achieved for
|x_{0}-d\gamma|\leq 1/2
, by (
1
). Now

\displaystyle|x_{0}-d\gamma|=\left|\frac{2nx_{0}-(2n^{2}+r-1)}{2n}\right|\leq 1/2,

(2)

when
x_{0}=n
, so
\{\pm 1,\pm(n-\sqrt{d})\}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(a_{1})
, so
a_{1}x^{2}
has at least two minimal vectors, making it perfect.
∎

We will now prove Theorem
1
. Assume that
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
, with
d\equiv 2
or
3\mod 4
,
d>0
and
d=n^{2}+r
,
-n<r\neq 1\leq n
. Let
\alpha+\beta\sqrt{d}
denote the fundamental unit of
K
, and
\beta=m(m+2)
for some odd integer
m
, and
\alpha\equiv\pm\left(\frac{m-1}{2}(m+2)^{2}+1\right)\mod\beta^{2}
. By Lemma
2
, the forms
a_{1}x^{2},a_{2}x^{2}
are perfect unary forms, where

\displaystyle a_{1}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2n^{2}+r-1}{4n(n^{2}+r)}\sqrt{d},

\displaystyle a_{2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2n^{2}+r-1}{4n(n^{2}+r)}\sqrt{d}.

We have
\mathcal{M}(a_{1})=\{\pm 1,\pm(n-\sqrt{d})\}
and
\mathcal{M}(a_{2})=\{\pm 1,\pm(n+\sqrt{d})\}
if
r>-(n-1)
, or
\mathcal{M}(a_{1})=\{\pm 1,\pm(n-\sqrt{d}),\pm(n-1-\sqrt{d})\}
,
\mathcal{M}(a_{2})=\{\pm 1,\pm(n-\sqrt{d}),\pm(n-1+\sqrt{d})\}
if
r=-(n-1)
.

We say that two perfect forms
\alpha_{1}x^{2}
,
\alpha_{2}x^{2}
are neighbours if
\mathcal{M}(a_{1})\cap\mathcal{M}(a_{2})\neq\phi
. By the theory of perfect forms, the graph of neighbouring forms is connected. The perfect forms
a_{1}x^{2}
,
a_{2}x^{2}
are neighbours, so we want to look for a neighbouring form to
a_{1}x^{2}
.

We now consider the form
a_{3}x^{2}
, where
a_{3}=\alpha+\beta\sqrt{d}
. We note that this form is positive, which can be seen as follows: if
\alpha+\beta\sqrt{d}
were not totally positive, then

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}-d\beta^{2}=-1\iff\alpha^{2}\equiv-1\mod\beta^{2}.

However, since
\beta=m(m+2)
for some odd integer
m
, we have
\beta\equiv 3\mod 4
, so at least one prime dividing
m
must be equivalent to
3
mod
4
. The equation
x^{2}\equiv-1\mod N
has no solutions for
x\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
if any prime
p
equivalent to
3
mod
4
divides
N
, so we must instead have
\alpha^{2}-d\beta^{2}=1
.

We consider the positive rational form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(a_{3}(x_{0}+x_{1}\sqrt{d})^{2})=q(x_{0},x_{1})\triangleq\alpha x_{0}^{2}+d\alpha x_{1}^{2}+2d\beta x_{0}x_{1}.

We perform Gauss reduction on
q(x_{0},x_{1})
in order to ascertain the minimal vectors of
q
. Consider first
\alpha=k_{1}m^{2}(m+2)^{2}+\frac{m-1}{2}(m+2)^{2}+1
, for some integer
k_{1}\geq 0
. Setting
l=k_{1}m(m+2)+\frac{m+1}{2}
, we note that

\displaystyle\left|d\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-l\right|=\left|-\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1-\alpha^{-1}}{m(m+2)}\right|<\frac{1}{m}<1/2,

so
l=\lfloor d\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\rceil
. Then

\displaystyle q^{\prime}(x_{0},x_{1})\triangleq q(x_{0}-lx_{1},x_{1})=\alpha x_{0}^{2}+\left(d\alpha+l^{2}\alpha-2ld\beta\right)x_{1}^{2}+2\left(d\beta-l\alpha\right)x_{0}x_{1}.

We now consider the quantity

\displaystyle\gamma\triangleq\frac{d\beta-l\alpha}{d\alpha+l^{2}\alpha-2ld\beta}.

First, note that
d=l^{2}-2k_{1}(m+1)-1
, and so

\displaystyle d\alpha+l^{2}\alpha-2ld\beta

\displaystyle=(2l^{2}-2k_{1}(m+1)-1)\left(k_{1}m^{2}(m+2)^{2}+\frac{m-1}{2}(m+2)^{2}+1\right)

\displaystyle-2l\left(l^{2}-2k_{1}(m+1)-1\right)m(m+2)

\displaystyle=k_{1}((m+1)^{2}+1)+\frac{m+1}{2},

which is clearly less than
\alpha
, so
\pm 1\not\in\mathcal{M}(a_{3})
. Set
l^{\prime}=m+1
. Then

\displaystyle\gamma+l^{\prime}=\frac{2k_{1}(m+1)+1}{k_{1}((m+1)^{2}+1)+\frac{m+1}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2},

(3)

for all
m\geq 3
,
k_{1}\geq 0
, so
l^{\prime}=-\lfloor\gamma\rceil
. So we consider

\displaystyle q^{\prime\prime}(x_{0},x_{1})

\displaystyle\triangleq q^{\prime}(x_{0},x_{1}-l^{\prime}x_{0})=\left(\alpha+{l^{\prime}}^{2}\epsilon+2l^{\prime}\left(d\beta-l\alpha\right)\right)x_{0}^{2}+\epsilon x_{1}^{2}+2\epsilon(\gamma+l^{\prime})x_{0}x_{1},

where

\displaystyle\epsilon\triangleq d\alpha+l^{2}\alpha-2ld\beta.

A simple computation yields that

\displaystyle\epsilon=\alpha+{l^{\prime}}^{2}\epsilon+2l^{\prime}\left(d\beta-l\alpha\right),

and so we must have that
2\epsilon=\mu(a_{3})
, since the Gauss reduction algorithm must conclude here. Therefore,
k_{1}m(m+2)+\frac{m+1}{2}-\sqrt{d},(\alpha-\beta)\left(k_{1}m(m+2)+\frac{m+1}{2}+\sqrt{d}\right)\in\mathcal{M}(a_{3})
. Moreover, it is easily seen that
k_{1}m(m+2)+\frac{m+1}{2}-\sqrt{d}\neq\pm(\alpha-\beta)\left(k_{1}m(m+2)+\frac{m+1}{2}+\sqrt{d}\right)
, and so
a_{3}x^{2}
is a perfect unary form.

Suppose now
\alpha=k_{2}m^{2}(m+2)^{2}-\frac{m-1}{2}(m+2)^{2}-1
, for some integer
k_{2}\geq 1
. Again, we consider the positive rational form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(a_{3}(x_{0}+x_{1}\sqrt{d})^{2})=q(x_{0},x_{1})\triangleq\alpha x_{0}^{2}+d\alpha x_{1}^{2}+2d\beta x_{0}x_{1}.

As before, we perform Gauss reduction on
q(x_{0},x_{1})
. Setting
l=k_{2}m(m+2)-\frac{m+1}{2}
, we note that

\displaystyle\left|d\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-l\right|=\left|\frac{m+1-\alpha^{-1}}{m(m+2)}\right|<\frac{1}{m}<\frac{1}{2},

so
l=\lfloor d\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\rceil
. Then we set

\displaystyle q^{\prime}(x_{0},x_{1})\triangleq q(x_{0}-lx_{1},x_{1})=\alpha x_{0}^{2}+\left(d\alpha+l^{2}\alpha-2ld\beta\right)x_{1}^{2}+2\left(d\beta-l\alpha\right)x_{0}x_{1}.

Consider

\displaystyle\gamma\triangleq\gamma\triangleq\frac{d\beta-l\alpha}{d\alpha+l^{2}\alpha-2ld\beta}.

First, note that
d=l^{2}+2k_{2}(m+1)-1
, and so

\displaystyle d\alpha+l^{2}\alpha-2ld\beta

\displaystyle=(2l^{2}+2k_{2}(m+1)-1)\left(k_{2}m^{2}(m+2)^{2}-\frac{m-1}{2}(m+2)^{2}-1\right)

\displaystyle-2l\left(l^{2}+2k_{2}(m+1)-1\right)m(m+2)

\displaystyle=k_{2}\left((m+1)^{2}+1\right)-\frac{m+1}{2},

which is less than
\alpha
for all
k_{2}\geq 1
, and so
\pm 1\not\in\mathcal{M}(a_{3})
. Set
l^{\prime}=m+1
. Then

\displaystyle\gamma-l^{\prime}=-\frac{2k_{2}(m+1)+1}{k_{2}\left((m+1)^{2}+1\right)-\frac{m+1}{2}}>-1/2

for all
k_{2}\geq 1
,
m\geq 3
, so
l^{\prime}=\lfloor\gamma\rceil
. It follows again that the minimum of
q(x_{0},x_{1})
is
\epsilon
, where
\epsilon=d\alpha+l^{2}\alpha-2ld
, and so
\mu(a_{3})=2\epsilon
and
l-\sqrt{d},(\alpha-\beta\sqrt{d})(l+\sqrt{d})\in\mathcal{M}(a_{3})
, as before.

We now want to prove that
a_{1}x^{2},a_{2}x^{2},a_{3}x^{2}
are the only perfect unary forms, up to homothety and equivalence in
K
. We say that two perfect unary forms
b_{1}x^{2},b_{2}x^{2}
are neighbouring forms if
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
,
\mu(b_{1})=\mu(b_{2})
and
\mathcal{M}(b_{1})\cap\mathcal{M}(b_{2})\neq\phi
. It is known that the graph of neighbouring forms is connected
[
4
]
.

First, we want to show the following:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a_{1})=\{\pm 1,\pm(n-\sqrt{d})\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a_{2})=\{\pm 1,\pm(n+\sqrt{d})\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a_{3})=\{\pm(n-\sqrt{d}),\pm(\alpha-\beta\sqrt{d})(n+\sqrt{d})\}.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}(a_{1})
contains a minimal vector that is not
\pm 1,\pm(n-\sqrt{d})
. This can only happen when equality holds in (
2
), and equality here holds only if
r=-(n-1)
. Recall that if
\alpha=km^{2}(m+2)^{2}+\delta\left(\frac{m-1}{2}(m+2)^{2}+1\right)
where
\delta\in\{\pm 1\}
, then

\displaystyle d=\left(km(m+2)+\delta\frac{m+1}{2}\right)^{2}-2\delta k(m+1)-1.

Setting
r=-2\delta k(m+1)-1
,
n=km(m+2)+\delta\frac{m+1}{2}
, it is easily seen that
-(n-1)<r<n
, and so
\mathcal{M}(a_{1})=\{\pm 1,\pm(n-\sqrt{d})\}
. It can be similarly deduced that
\mathcal{M}(a_{2})=\{\pm 1,\pm(n+\sqrt{d})\}
, since
a_{2}
is conjugate to
a_{1}
.

If there was an element in
\mathcal{M}(a_{3})
that is not equal to
\pm(n-\sqrt{d}),\pm(\alpha-\beta\sqrt{d})(n+\sqrt{d})
, then (
3
) would have to hold with equality. This can happen only if
k_{1}=0,m=3
- however, this would mean that
d=3=4^{2}-1
, which contradicts the hypothesis that
d=n^{2}+r
with
r\neq\pm 1
. So,
\mathcal{M}(a_{3})=\{\pm(n-\sqrt{d}),\pm(\alpha-\beta\sqrt{d})(n+\sqrt{d})\}
.

A simple computation shows that
a_{1}x^{2},a_{2}x^{2},a_{3}x^{2}
do not lie in the same homothety or equivalence class, and so they must be distinct perfect unary forms up to homothety and equivalence, so
n_{K}\geq 3
. Recall that if
bx^{2}
is a perfect unary form, then
b^{\prime}x^{2}
is also a perfect unary form where
b^{\prime}
is a conjugate of
b
. Clearly
a_{1},a_{2}
are conjugates. If we denote by
a_{4}
the conjugate of
a_{3}
, it is easily seen that
a_{3}=a_{4}(\alpha-\beta\sqrt{d})^{2}
and so
a_{3}x^{2},a_{4}x^{2}
are equivalent perfect unary forms.

Since
\mathcal{M}(a_{1})\cap\mathcal{M}(a_{2})=\{\pm 1\}
,
a_{1}x^{2}
,
a_{2}x^{2}
are neighbouring perfect forms. Similarly,
a_{1}x^{2},a_{3}x^{2}
are neighbouring perfect forms. Suppose then
a_{3}x^{2}
has another neighbouring form that is not equivalent to any of the previously listed perfect forms, call this
a^{\prime}x^{2}
. Then
\pm(\alpha-\sqrt{d})(n+\sqrt{d})\subseteq\mathcal{M}(a^{\prime})
. This means that
\pm(n+\sqrt{d})\in\mathcal{M}(a^{\prime}(\alpha-\beta\sqrt{d})^{2})
, and
a^{\prime}(\alpha-\beta\sqrt{d})^{2}x^{2}
is also a perfect unary form - however,
\pm(n+\sqrt{d})\in\mathcal{M}(a_{2})
and
\pm(n+\sqrt{d})\in\mathcal{M}(a_{4})
where
a_{4}=\alpha-\beta\sqrt{d}
, and both
a_{2}x^{2},a_{4}x^{2}
are perfect unary forms, so
a^{\prime}x^{2}=\lambda a_{2}x^{2}
or
a^{\prime}x^{2}=\lambda a_{4}x^{2}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
, i.e.
a^{\prime}x^{2}
would lie in the same homothety and equivalence class as either
a_{2}x^{2}
or
a_{3}x^{2}
. We therefore conclude that
n_{K}=3
, with all perfect unary forms being in the same homothety and/or equivalence class as either
a_{1}x^{2},a_{2}x^{2},a_{3}x^{2}
.
