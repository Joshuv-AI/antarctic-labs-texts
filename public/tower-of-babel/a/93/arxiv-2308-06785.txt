Formal Verification of Intersection Safety for Automated Driving*
James Haydon, Martin Bondu, Clovis Eberhart, Jérémy Dubut, Ichiro Hasuo

Abstract
We build on our recent work on formalization of responsibility-sensitive safety (RSS) and present the first formal framework that enables mathematical proofs of the safety of control strategies in intersection scenarios. Intersection scenarios are challenging due to the complex interaction between vehicles; to cope with it, we extend the program logic dFHL \mathrm{dFHL} in the previous work and introduce a novel formalism of hybrid control flow graphs on which our algorithm can automatically discover an RSS condition that ensures safety. An RSS condition thus discovered is experimentally evaluated; we observe that it is safe (as our safety proof says) and is not overly conservative.



I
INTRODUCTION

Safety of automated driving vehicles (ADVs) is a problem of growing industrial and social interest. New technologies
are making ADV technologically feasible; but for the social acceptance, their safety should be guaranteed and explained.

In this paper, we pursue
formal verification
of ADV safety, that is, to provide its mathematical proofs. This
logical
approach, compared to
statistical
approaches such as accident statistics and scenario-based testing, has major advantages of
strong guarantees
(logical proofs never go wrong) and
explainability
(a proof records step-by-step safety arguments).

Specifically, we introduce a theoretical framework for formal verification of ADV safety in
intersection scenarios
. We derive an algorithm for computing assumptions (called
RSS conditions
) needed for safety proofs. We present its implementation, and we experimentally evaluate its output. We build our contributions
on top of the conceptual methodology called RSS
[
1
]
and its mathematical formalization
[
2
]
.

Below we describe our motivation and contributions. We introduce, at the
same time, the technical context of RSS that we rely on. The high-level overview is summarized in
Section

I-D
.

I-A

Responsibility-Sensitive Safety (RSS)

RSS is a rich concept with many aspects. The introduction below is focused on its implications on formal verification. See
[
3
]
for a more extensive introduction.

Formal verification of ADV safety is a natural idea with many important advantages. A big problem, however, is its feasibility. In order to write mathematically rigorous proofs in formal verification,
one needs rigorous
definitions
of all the concepts involved. Such definitions amount to mathematical
modeling
of target systems, which is hard for ADVs due to 1) the complexity of vehicles themselves and 2) complex interaction between traffic participants.

Responsibility-sensitive safety (RSS)

[
1
]
is a methodology for this challenge of modeling. Its central idea is to logically split the safety theorem into the following two, and to focus the proof effort to the conditional safety lemma only.

Lemma I.1

(conditional safety)
.

If all vehicles comply with so-called RSS rules, then there is no collision.

Assumption I.2

(rule compliance)
.

All vehicles do comply with RSS rules.

This way, most of the modeling difficulties can be left out of the scope of mathematical arguments—for example, the internal working of vehicles is confined to the rule compliance assumption. This makes rigorous proofs feasible.

One may wonder if
Section

I-A
may be too strong—it may be false and thus threaten the value of a safety proof. It turns out that RSS rules are
effective
rules, not only technically but also socially and industrially. Their granularity is suited for imposing them as contracts and standards—as shown below in
Section

I-A
—making
Section

I-A
a realistic assumption. It is reasonable that vehicles comply with RSS rules (the rules can be even
enforced
by safety architectures, see e.g.
[
2
,
4
]
).
Section

I-A
imposes rules also on other traffic participants such as pedestrians. In this case, those rules are chosen to be very mild commonsense rules (e.g. “no jumping to highways”), and they are enforced by social and legal means. See e.g.
[
3
]
.

An RSS rule
(A,\alpha)
is a pair of an
RSS condition

A
and a
proper response

\alpha
. A proper response is a specific control strategy; it can be thought of as a
minimum risk maneuver (MRM)
. Then
Section

I-A
states, to be precise, that the execution of
\alpha
, starting in a state where
A
is true, is collision-free.

Here is an example of an RSS rule from
[
1
]
.

Example I.3

(one-way traffic
[
1
]
)
.

Consider
Fig.

1
, where the subject vehicle
(
\mathsf{SV}
,
\mathrm{car}_{\mathrm{rear}}
) drives behind another car
(
\mathrm{car}_{\mathrm{front}}
).
The RSS condition

A\;=\;\bigl(y_{f}-y_{r}>\mathsf{dRSS}(v_{f},v_{r})\bigr),

(1)

where
\mathsf{dRSS}(v_{f},v_{r})
is the
RSS safety distance
defined by

\displaystyle\max\Bigl(\,0,\,v_{r}\rho+\frac{1}{2}a_{\mathrm{max}}\rho^{2}+\frac{(v_{r}+a_{\mathrm{max}}\rho)^{2}}{2b_{\mathrm{min}}}-\frac{v_{f}^{2}}{2b_{\mathrm{max}}}\,\Bigr).

(2)

Here
y_{f},y_{r}
are positions of the cars,

v_{f},v_{r}
are velocities,

\rho

is the
response time
for
\mathrm{car}_{\mathrm{rear}}
,

is the maximum
acceleration rate,

b_{\mathrm{min}}
is the maximum comfortable braking rate, and
b_{\mathrm{max}}
is the maximum emergency
braking rate.

The proper response
\alpha
dictates
\mathsf{SV}

(
\mathrm{car}_{\mathrm{rear}}
) to engage the maximum comfortable braking (at rate
b_{\mathrm{min}}
)
when condition
Eq.

For the RSS rule
(A,\alpha)
, proving the conditional safety lemma is not hard. See
[
1
,
3
]
(informal) and
[
2
]
(formal).

Fig. 1
:

We note that the above RSS rule is an a priori, rigorous yet simple rule, the compliance with which is externally checkable. It is independent of specific car models or manufacturers. It applies to all vehicles by changing the values of parameters such as
a_{\mathrm{max}}
.

RSS rules can be used for various purposes, such as attribution of liability, safety metrics, regulations and standards, and runtime safeguard mechanism. See e.g.
[
1
,
5
,
2
]
.

I-B

Formalization of RSS by the Program Logic
\mathrm{dFHL}

An RSS rule must be derived, and proved safe (
Section

I-A
), for each individual driving scenario. Towards broad application of RSS, we have to do such proofs for many scenarios.
Doing so informally (in a pen-and-paper manner) is not desirable for scalability, maintainability, and accountability.

This is why we pursued the formalization of RSS in
[
2
]
. We introduced a logic
\mathrm{dFHL}
—a symbolic framework to write proofs in—extending classic
Floyd–Hoare logic

[
6
]
with differential equations. The logic
\mathrm{dFHL}
derives
Hoare quadruples

\{A\}\alpha\{B\}:S
; it means that the execution of a
hybrid program

\alpha
, started when a
precondition

A
is true, terminates and makes a
postcondition

B
true. The
safety condition

S
specifies a property that should be true throughout the execution of
\alpha
.

Note that
Section

I-A
of RSS is naturally mapped to a Hoare quadruple: if we let
A
be an RSS condition and
\alpha
be a proper response, then
S
(expressing collision-freedom) is ensured throughout. Moreover, the postcondition
B
can express the
goal
of
\alpha
, such as to stop at a desired position. This extension of RSS, where RSS rules can guarantee not only safety but also goal achievement, is called
GA-RSS
in
[
2
]
.

Fig. 2
:
the intersection driving scenario, with the
subject vehicle

\mathsf{SV}
making a right turn and the
principal other vehicle

\mathsf{POV}{}
coming from the opposite

Fig. 3
:
pull over

Another benefit of formalization by
\mathrm{dFHL}
is
compositional
reasoning. In Floyd–Hoare logic, one can derive

\{A\}\,\alpha;\beta\,\{C\}
(a property of composition
\alpha;\beta
) from

\{A\}\,\alpha\,\{B\}
and

\{B\}\,\beta\,\{C\}
(properties of components).
Via similar compositionality in
\mathrm{dFHL}
, we devised in
[
2
]
a workflow in which a complex scenario is split into subscenarios, and RSS rules are derived in a divide-and-conquer manner.

As a case study, we derived an RSS rule for the
pull over
scenario (
Fig.

3
). It is a complex scenario with a goal that requires high-level maneuver planning. It is an important MRM, too, for an ADV exiting its ODD.

I-C

Automated Derivation of RSS Rules for Intersections

Our goal in this paper is a formal framework for deriving RSS rules for
intersection scenarios
such as
Fig.

3
. This class of driving scenarios exhibit unique challenges; to cope with them, we introduce a new theory that extends
\mathrm{dFHL}
and emphasizes automation, together with its implementation.

Intersection scenarios exhibit complex interaction between vehicles.
This makes modeling hard: there are many “discrete modes” due to the interaction, making manual modeling effort error-prone. Desired here is a
compositional modeling formalism
where one can model each vehicle separately, a feature absent in
[
2
]
. Another challenge is in verification: many discrete modes lead to complex case distinction and proof complexity; therefore
proof automation
is highly desired.

Our answer to these challenges is
hybrid control flow graphs (hCFGs)
as a modeling formalism (
Section

III
). They are to hybrid programs in
[
2
]
what control flow graphs are to usual imperative programs; we introduce a synchronization mechanism so that multiple hCFGs compose. For reasoning, we adapt
\mathrm{dFHL}
and introduce the notion of
Hoare annotation
(
Section

V
). We propose an automated reasoning algorithm, and present its implementation and experimental evaluation.

I-D

Contributions

We present the first framework for deriving RSS rules and proving their safety for intersection scenarios. Our technical contributions are as follows.
1)
We introduce the modeling formalism of hCFGs that enables compositional agent-wise modeling (
Section

). Their semantics is via translation to hybrid programs
[
2
]
.
2)
We introduce the reasoning formalism of Hoare annotations (
Section

V
), an automated reasoning algorithm, and its implementation (
Section

VI
).
3)
We apply the above framework to the intersection situation in
Section

IV
—it consists of the driving scenario
Fig.

3
and a specific proper response
\alpha
—and let our implementation automatically derive an RSS condition
A
.
4)
We experimentally evaluate the RSS rule
(A,\alpha)
.

I-F

Related and Future Work

Some RSS rules are implemented and offered as a library
[
7
]
. However, its coverage of various driving scenarios seems limited. For example,
[
8
]
reports that CARLA’s autopilot safeguarded by the RSS library exhibits dangerous behaviors in an intersection scenario. This is because the RSS rule used there is essentially the one in
Section

I-A
and does not address the intersection scenario.

There are some extensions of RSS. Besides our formalized, compositional and goal-achieving one
[
2
]
, they study 1) parameter selection for balancing safety and progress
[
9
]
, 2) an empirical (not logical) safeguard layer in addition to RSS
[
5
]
,
3) extension to unstructured traffic with vulnerable road users
[
10
]
, and 4) swerves as evasive manoeuvres
[
11
]
. The formalization in
[
2
]
has been extended to verification of safety architectures, too
[
4
]
.
All these extensions are orthogonal to the formalized approach of the current work, leaving their integration as future work.

Some recent works formalize traffic rules in temporal logic, so that they can be effectively monitored
[
12
,
13
]
. Rules are given externally in
[
12
,
13
]
, while our goal is to derive such rules and prove their safety.

An automated driving controller typically aims also at requirements other than safety (such as comfort). For this purpose, use of
hierarchically structured
human-interpretable
rules is advocated in
[
14
]
. In our previous work
[
4
]
we also investigated hierarchically structured RSS rules, this time for the purpose of graceful degradation of safety. Combination of
[
4
]
and the current work will not be hard.

Formal verification of ADV decision making in intersections is studied in
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
. They all take model-checking approaches, unlike the current work that emphasizes theorem proving. The work
[
15
]
employs statistical model checking, where correctness guarantees are by sampling and thus not absolute. The work
[
16
]
abstracts away continuous dynamics, unlike our explicit modeling by ODEs. Rigorous and fine-grained verification is pursued in
[
17
]
where high-level decision making and low-level continuous dynamics are holistically analyzed by
multi-model heterogeneous verification
. It is not clear how this method generalizes to numerous variations of intersection scenarios (while their
\mathrm{dFHL}
modeling will not be hard). More comparison is future work.

Rule-based decision making for intersection scenarios is proposed in
[
18
]
, but it does not come with safety proofs. From the RSS point of view, the work
is suggesting proper responses, for which safety-guaranteeing RSS conditions can be derived using our framework. Doing so is future work.

For intersection safety, statistical and learning-based approaches are actively pursued, e.g.
[
19
]
. They are different from our logical approach that allows rigorous safety proofs.

Formal verification of ADV safety is pursued in e.g.
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
. The works
[
20
,
21
]
adopt much more fine-grained modeling compared to the current work and
[
2
]
; a price for doing so seems to be scalability and flexibility. The work
[
22
]
makes discrete (and thus coarse-grained) modeling.

II
PRELIMINARIES: HYBRID PROGRAMS AND THE PROGRAM LOGIC
\mathrm{dFHL}

We review the logic
\mathrm{dFHL}
(differential Floyd–Hoare logic), introduced in
[
2
]
for the purpose of 1) modeling driving situations as imperative programs (called
hybrid programs
), and 2) to reason about their goal achievement and safety. For the framework later in §
III
–
V
,
\mathrm{dFHL}
is both a main inspiration and an important technical building block.

The following collects the definitions needed in this paper. Further details are found in
[
2
]
.

II-A

Hybrid Programs

Definition II.1

(assertion)
.

Let
V
be a fixed set of variables. A
term
over
V

is a rational polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Q}[V]
on
V
.

Assertions
over
V
are generated by the grammar

A,B\;::=\;\mathsf{true}\mid\mathsf{false}\mid e\sim f\mid A\land B\mid A\lor B\mid\lnot A\mid A\Rightarrow B

where
e
,
f
are terms and

\sim\ \in\left\{{=,\leq,<,\neq}\right\}
.

The
openness
and
closedness
of an assertion can be naturally defined:
x\leq 2
is closed while
1<x\land x\leq 2
is neither open nor closed, for example. See
[
2
]
.

Hybrid programs
are a combination of usual imperative programs
and differential equations for continuous dynamics.

Definition II.2
.

Hybrid programs

are defined by

\displaystyle\alpha,\beta\quad::=\quad

\displaystyle\mathsf{skip}{}\mid\alpha;\beta\mid x\mathop{{:}{=}}e\mid\mathsf{if}\,(A)\,\{\alpha\}\,\mathsf{else}{}\,\{\beta\}\mid

\displaystyle\mathsf{while}\,(A)\,\{\alpha\}\mid\mathsf{dwhile}\,(A)\,\{\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{f}\}.

Here
x\in V
is a variable,
e
is a term, and
A
is an assertion.
In
\mathsf{dwhile}\,(A)\,\{\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{f}\}
,
\mathbf{x}
and

\mathbf{f}
are lists of the same length, respectively of (distinct)
variables and terms, and we require that
A
be an open assertion.
We sometimes drop the braces in

\mathsf{dwhile}\,(A)\,\{\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{f}\}
for readability.

A

differential while
clause
\mathsf{dwhile}\,(A)\,\{\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{f}\}

encodes the continuous dynamics according to a system
\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{f}
of ODEs; it executes until the
guard
condition
A
is
falsified.
Openness of
A
ensures that, if
A
is falsified at some point, then
there is the smallest time
t_{0}
when it is falsified, at which time the command’s continuation (if any) starts to execute.

The syntax of hybrid programs is inspired by
\mathrm{dL}

[
23
]
, but comes with
significant changes. See
[
2
]
for comparison.

Definition II.3

(semantics of hybrid programs)
.

A
store

\rho\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
is a function from variables to reals.
The
value

\left\llbracket{e}\right\rrbracket_{\rho}
of a term
e
in a store

\rho
is a real defined as usual by induction on
e
(see for
example
[
24
, §2.2]
).
The
satisfaction
relation between stores
\rho
and
assertions
A
, denoted by
\rho\vDash A
, is also defined as usual
(see
[
24
, §2.3]
).

A
state
is a pair
\langle{\alpha},{\rho}\rangle
of a hybrid program
and a store.
The
reduction
relation
\to
on states is defined as usual, using several rules found in
[
2
]
(see
Section

II-A
later).
A state
s

reduces
to
s^{\prime}
if
s\to^{*}s^{\prime}
, where
\to^{*}
is the reflexive transitive closure of

\to
.
A state
s

converges
to a store
\rho
, denoted by

{s}\mathrel{\Downarrow}{\rho}
, if there exists a reduction sequence

s\to^{*}\langle{\mathsf{skip}},{\rho}\rangle
.

Example II.4
.

The state
\langle{\alpha},{\rho}\rangle
, where

\alpha\,\equiv\,\left(\,\mathsf{dwhile}\,(x>0)\,\{\dot{x}=-1\}\,;\;x\mathop{{:}{=}}x-1\right)

and
\rho(x)=2
, can reduce
1) to
\langle{\alpha},{{\rho}[{x}\to{v}]}\rangle
for any
v\in(0,2]
,
2) to

\langle{x\mathop{{:}{=}}x-1},{{\rho}[{x}\to{0}]}\rangle
,
and
3) to
\langle{\mathsf{skip}},{{\rho}[{x}\to{-1}]}\rangle
.
Only the last one corresponds to convergence (namely
{\langle{\alpha},{\rho}\rangle}\mathrel{\Downarrow}{{\rho}[{x}\to{-1}]}
).

II-B

The Program Logic
\mathrm{dFHL}

Definition II.5
.

A
Hoare quadruple
is a quadruple

\left\{A\right\}~\alpha~\left\{B\right\}\colon{}S
of three assertions

A
,
B
, and
S
, and a hybrid program

\alpha
.
It is
valid
if, for all stores
\rho
such that
\rho\vDash A
,

•

there exists
\rho^{\prime}
such that

{\langle{\alpha},{\rho}\rangle}\mathrel{\Downarrow}{\rho^{\prime}}
and
\rho^{\prime}\vDash B
, and

•

for all reduction sequences
\langle{\alpha},{\rho}\rangle\to^{*}\langle{\beta},{\rho^{\prime}}\rangle
,
\rho^{\prime}\vDash S
.

Hoare quadruples have safety conditions
S

in addition compared to
Hoare triples (see e.g.
[
24
]
). They specify safety properties which must hold
throughout
execution.

The semantics of Hoare quadruples (
Section

II-B
) has some notable features such as accommodation of continuous dynamics and
total correctness
semantics. See
[
2
]
for details.

The logic
\mathrm{dFHL}
has several
derivation rules
for Hoare quadruples. Many of them are standard Hoare logic rules (see e.g.
[
24
]
), extended in a natural manner to accommodate safety conditions. The rules for the
\mathsf{dwhile}
construct come in two versions. One uses explicit closed-form solutions; this is the one we will use. The other uses
differential (in)variants
—formulated using Lie derivatives—that correspond to barrier certificates and Lyapunov functions in control theory.
For space reasons, we refer to
[
2
]
for those derivation rules. They are
sound
, in that they only derive valid Hoare quadruples.

HYBRID CONTROL FLOW GRAPHS (hCFGs)

III-A

Formalization

Definition III.1
.

A
hybrid control flow graph (hCFG)
is a tuple
\mathcal{G}=(L,\Sigma,E,V,\mathsf{Flow},\mathsf{Guard},\mathsf{Assign},l_{\mathsf{Init}},\mathsf{Final})
where

•

L
is a finite set of
(control) locations
;

•

\Sigma
is a finite set of
event names
;

•

E\subseteq L\times\Sigma\times L
is a finite set of
edges
that represent discrete “jumps” (we write
l\xrightarrow{a}l^{\prime}
for
(l,a,l^{\prime})\in E
);

•

V
is a finite set of real-valued variables;

•

\mathsf{Flow}
is a function that associates, to each location
l\in L
, a system

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{f}

of ODEs, where 1)
\mathbf{x}
is the list (of length
|V|
) of all variables in
V
, and 2)
\mathbf{f}\in(\mathbb{Q}[V])^{|V|}
is a list (of the same length) of polynomials over
V
;

•

\mathsf{Guard}
is a function that associates, to each edge
e\in E
, its
guard

A
that is a closed assertion (
Section

II-A
);

•

\mathsf{Assign}
is a function that associates, to each edge
e\in E
and a special label
\mathsf{Init}
(designating initialization), a (possibly empty) list of
assignment commands
that are of the form
x\coloneqq f
where
x\in V
and
f\in\mathbb{Q}[V]
;

•

l_{\mathsf{Init}}\in L
is an
initial location
; and

•

\mathsf{Final}\subseteq L
is a set of
final locations
.

Definition III.2

.

Let

III-C

Networks of hCFGs

The following formalism enables compositional modeling. It is much like
networks of timed automata
(see e.g.
[
26
]
) although ours features richer interaction, as explained later.

Definition III.4

(network of hCFGs)
.

An
open hCFG
is defined in the same way as hCFGs (
Section

III-A
), with the difference that 1) the flow dynamics
\mathsf{Flow}(l)\equiv(\dot{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{f}})
may not cover all variables in
V
(that is, the length of the list
\mathbf{x}
may be
<|V|
), and 2) it has no final locations specified.

A
network of hCFGs
is a tuple
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{G}^{(1)},\dotsc,\mathcal{G}^{(k)})
of open hCFGs (these are called
component hCFGs
of
\mathcal{N}
) such that 1) their location sets
L^{(1)},\dotsc,L^{(k)}
are disjoint,
2) they share the same set
V
of variables,
and 3) they satisfy so-called
compatibility conditions
, deferred to

Section

-A
,
requiring that there are no conflicts in the descriptions of dynamics in different hCFGs.

Intuitively, a variable can be changed (by
\mathsf{Flow}
or
\mathsf{Assign}
) by only one component, but it can be seen (i.e. occur in guards) in multiple components.
This “can’t change but can see” interaction—essential in modeling driving situations—is not present in networks of timed automata
[
26
]
. A consequence of this feature is that a component CFG does not have its own semantics: its behavior depends on variables changed by other components. This does not matter for our purpose (compositional modeling, not compositional verification).

The semantics of a network of hCFGs is defined by the synchronized product of component hCFGs. There are two modes of synchronization: by shared variables (in guards), and by shared events (in edge labels). Below is an informal definition; a formal definition is in

Section

-B
.

Definition III.5
.

The
semantics
of a network
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{G}^{(1)},\dotsc,\mathcal{G}^{(k)})
of hCFGs is defined to be the semantics (
Section

III-B
) of its
synchronized product

\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{N},\mathsf{Final}}
with a given set
\mathsf{Final}
of final locations (
\mathsf{Final}
is specified separately). The hCFG
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{N},\mathsf{Final}}
is defined in a usual manner. Its location is a tuple
(l^{(1)},\dotsc,l^{(k)})
of locations of components. When an event name
a
is shared by multiple components, we require that
all
these components synchronize. The flow dynamics, guards, and assignment commands are defined by suitably joining those of components.

Definition V.1

(Hoare annotation)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an hCFG in
Section

III-A
. We adopt the notations in

Section

III-B

for edges, etc.

A
Hoare annotation

\gamma
for
\mathcal{G}
is a function that associates, to each location
l
of
\mathcal{G}
, an assertion
\gamma(l)
over
V
(cf.
Section

II-A
).

Let
S
be an assertion, and
\mathsf{Unsafe}
be a set of locations. A Hoare annotation
\gamma
for
\mathcal{G}
is
valid under a safety condition
S
and unsafe locations
\mathsf{Unsafe}
if the following holds.

If
l\in\mathsf{Final}
, then the implication
\gamma(l)\Rightarrow S
is valid.

If
l\in\mathsf{Unsafe}
, then
\gamma(l)\Rightarrow\mathsf{false}
is valid.

Otherwise, let
l\in L\setminus(\mathsf{Final}\cup\mathsf{Unsafe})
be a location that is not final nor unsafe; let its successors and guards denoted as in
Eq.

3
. Then there exist assertions
C_{l,1},\dotsc,C_{l,n_{l}}
such that

•

the Hoare quadruple

\begin{array}[]{r}\Bigl\{C_{l,i}\Bigr\}\;\left({\scriptsize\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{dwhile}\,(\lnot A_{l,i})\,\{\\
\hskip 8.19447pt\mathsf{Flow}(l)\\
\}\end{array}}\right)\,\Bigl\{\,\gamma(l^{\prime}_{l,i})\,\bigl[\,\mathsf{Assign}(e_{l,i})\,\bigr]\,\Bigr\}\\
\qquad\quad\;:\;S\land\bigwedge_{j\in[1,n_{l}]\setminus\{i\}}\lnot A_{l,j}\end{array}

(5)

is valid for each

In

The basic idea is that
\gamma
is an

\gamma(l)
is true when
l
is reached, then
\gamma(l^{\prime})
should be true at the “jump” moment that the successor
l^{\prime}
is reached. The definition is so that

and the avoidance of unsafe locations. The condition
S
is true throughout the flow dynamics, too, since
S
is in the quadruple
Eq.

5
.

Therefore, for
\gamma
, we have that 1) it is an invariant, and 2) its truth guarantees truth of
S
and avoidance of
\mathsf{Unsafe}

\gamma
holds initially,
then
S
is true and
\mathsf{Unsafe}
is avoided all the time. This is stated formally in
Theorem

V.2
.

Branching according to guards requires some care (cf.
Eq.

3
); this is why we split
\gamma(l)
into

.

C_{l,i}

5

•

The
i

•

Moreover, after taking the edge

\mathsf{Assign}(e_{l,i})

In particular,

x_{\mathsf{SV}},v_{\mathsf{SV}},x_{\mathsf{POV}{}},v_{\mathsf{POV}{}}
(and parameters such as
b
) that, if true initially, guarantees a safe execution of the max-brake proper response.

We used the following safety specifications, identifying a collision as a joint occupancy of
\mathsf{CZ}
:
S^{\mathsf{int}}:=\mathsf{true}
(no condition on the continuous dynamics);

\mathsf{Unsafe}^{\mathsf{int}}:=\bigl\{\,(l^{\mathsf{SVPos}},\dotsc,l^{\mathsf{POVTimer}})\,\big|\,l^{\mathsf{SVPos}}=\mathsf{SVInCZ}\land l^{\mathsf{POVPos}}=\mathsf{POVInCZ}\,\bigr\}
.
The automated derivation took a couple of minutes, most of which was spent for validity checking by Z3. The synthesis of
\gamma
was assisted by some user-provided assertions, although these assertions are straightforward (such as
v_{\mathsf{SV}}\geq 0\land x_{\mathsf{SV}}^{\mathsf{CZStart}}\leq x_{\mathsf{SV}}
for the location
\mathsf{SVInCZ}
in
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{SVPos}}
). Fully automatic synthesis (without assistance) should not be hard; we leave it as future work.

We conducted experiments to evaluate the RSS condition
A
we obtained for the intersection situation. We posed the following research questions.

RQ1

Does the RSS condition
A
indeed guarantee safety (
A
true
\Rightarrow
no collision)? This is theoretically guaranteed by
Theorem

V.2
, but we want to experimentally confirm.

RQ2

Is
A
not overly conservative? That is, when it is false (
A
says there is risk), is there a real risk of collision?

Another question is about the ease of modeling. We believe that the compositional modeling in
Section

IV
answers it positively.

To address the RQs, we implemented a simulator of the intersection situation (cf.

The experimental results yield the following answers to the research questions.

On
RQ1
, we confirmed experimentally that all RSS complying instances are safe. This is no surprise
given the theoretical guarantee in

.

On
RQ2
, we see only a small number of
false positive
instances, i.e. those which are RSS non-complying but experimentally safe. According to

Table

I
, the precision of the RSS condition is
90%
and its recall is 100%. This high precision indicates that the RSS condition
A
, synthesized by our algorithm, is not overly conservative.

In

5
, it is observed that the number of unsafe simulations vary greatly—depending on
\mathsf{POV}{}
’s behavior—and still the RSS condition
A
successfully spots all unsafe instances.

Let us consider, for comparison, scenario-based testing methods for detecting unsafe instances. For such methods, spotting an unsafe instance with fewer unsafe simulations will be a challenge, since they have to find a rare
\mathsf{POV}{}
behavior that causes a collision. In contrast,
\mathsf{POV}{}
behaviors are
universally quantified
in our formal verification approach, in the form of the two extreme behaviors in
Fig.

4
. Therefore our approach can spot unsafe instances regardless of the rarity of collisions.

We note that an instance that is RSS non-complying with respect to
A
may still be safe with respect to other proper responses than the max-brake one.
See
Section

IV-A
.

VIII
Conclusions

Building on RSS
[
1
]
and its formalization
[
2
]
, we presented the first framework for automated formal verification of safety of intersection situations. The modeling formalism of hCFGs is suited to the multi-agent character of intersection situations; the reasoning formalism of Hoare annotations demonstrates the power of program logic. We believe this is an important step forward towards RSS’s coverage of a wide variety of real-world traffic scenarios, and hence towards the public acceptance of automated driving where its safety is mathematically proved.

Compatibility Conditions in
Section

III-C

•

For each tuple
(l^{(1)},\dotsc,l^{(k)})
of locations of the components (where
l^{(i)}\in L^{(i)}
for each
i
), their flow dynamics has no conflicts, and moreover it is complete. That is, letting
\mathsf{Flow}(l^{(i)})\equiv(\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}=\mathbf{f}^{(i)})
,
1) if the same variable occurs in
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
and
\mathbf{x}^{(j)}
with
i\neq j
, then the polynomials on their right-hand side (in
\mathbf{f}^{(i)},\mathbf{f}^{(j)}
) coincide, and
2) the union of
\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\dotsc,\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
is the set
V
of variables.

•

Similarly, for each tuple
(e^{(1)},\dotsc,e^{(k)})
of edges of the component hCFGs, there is no conflict in their assignment commands, meaning that
\mathsf{Assign}(e^{(1)}),\dotsc,\mathsf{Assign}(e^{(k)})
have the same expressions on the right-hand side if the variable on the left-hand side is the same. We require the same compatibility for initial assignment commands, too.

-B

Semantics of Networks of hCFGs, Formalized

Definition .1
.

The
semantics
of a network
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{G}^{(1)},\dotsc,\mathcal{G}^{(k)})
of hCFGs is defined to be the semantics (
Section

III-B
) of its
synchronized product

\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{N},\mathsf{Final}}
with a given set
\mathsf{Final}
of final locations. The latter is the hCFG defined below.

•

Its locations are tuples
(l^{(1)},\dotsc,l^{(k)})
of locations of components. Here
l^{(i)}\in L^{(i)}
for each
i
.

•

Its set
E_{\mathcal{N},\mathsf{Final}}
of event names the union
E^{(1)}\cup\cdots\cup E^{(k)}
of those of the components.

•

Its set
V
of variables is the one of all components.

•

We have an edge

(l^{(1)},\dotsc,l^{(k)})\xrightarrow{a}(l^{\prime(1)},\dotsc,l^{\prime(k)})

if 1)
l^{(i)}\xrightarrow{a}l^{\prime(i)}
is an edge in
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
for each
i
such that
a\in E^{(i)}
, and 2)
l^{(i)}=l^{\prime(i)}
otherwise, i.e. for every
i
such that
a\not\in E^{(i)}
. In this case, we say that the component edges
l^{(i)}\xrightarrow{a}l^{\prime(i)}

enable
the edge in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{N},\mathsf{Final}}
. The intuition is that, if an event name
a
is shared by multiple components, then
all
these components must synchronize in the event.

•

Its flow dynamics at
(l^{(1)},\dotsc,l^{(k)})
is the union of the flow dynamics of
l^{(1)},\dotsc,l^{(k)}
. By the compatibility condition (
Section

III-C
), this union is well-defined and covers all variables in
V
, making
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{N},\mathsf{Final}}
a (proper) hCFG.

•

Its guard for an edge
(l^{(1)},\dotsc,l^{(k)})\xrightarrow{a}(l^{\prime(1)},\dotsc,l^{\prime(k)})

is the conjunction of those guards associated with the enabling component edges
l^{(i)}\xrightarrow{a}l^{\prime(i)}
.

•

Its assignment command for an edge in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{N},\mathsf{Final}}
is the concatenation of the assignment commands for the enabling component edges. There are no overriding assignments thanks to the compatibility condition (
Section

III-C
). The initial assignment commands are defined similarly.

•

Its initial location is the tuple
(l^{(1)}_{\mathsf{Init}},\dotsc,l^{(k)}_{\mathsf{Init}})
.

•

Its set
\mathsf{Final}
of final locations is specified separately. It is a set of locations, that is,
\mathsf{Final}\subseteq L^{(1)}\times\cdots\times L^{(k)}
.
