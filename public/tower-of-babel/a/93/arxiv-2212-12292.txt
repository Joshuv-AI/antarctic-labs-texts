Measurement-based Feedback Control of a Quantum System in a Harmonic Potential
Amy Rouillard, Anirudh Reddy, Humairah Bassa, Shamik Maharaj, Lajos Diósi, Thomas Konrad

Abstract
We present a formulation of measurement-based feedback control of a single quantum particle in one spatial dimension. An arbitrary linear combination of the position and momentum of the particle is continuously monitored, and feedback proportional to the measured signal is used to control the system. We derive a feedback master equation and discuss a general approach to computing the steady-state solutions for arbitrary potentials. For a quantum harmonic oscillator or a free particle, we show that it is possible to cool and confine the system using feedback that simultaneously damps the measured observable and its conjugate momentum, as well as compensates for noise introduced by the measurement. In addition, we demonstrate that appropriate feedback adds a quadratic term in the measured observable to the Hamiltonian of the system. For the particular case of the harmonic potential, we describe the exact dynamics of the system that can be cooled to the ground state. Moreover, we provide an argument for the possibility to cool systems with arbitrary potentials provided that the measurement is strong enough to localise the particle on an interval smaller than the characteristic length scale of the potential.



I
Introduction

Recent developments in the fields of quantum computing and quantum meteorology have resulted in a demand for protocols to control individual quantum systems
D’Alessandro 2008
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
;
d’Alessandro 2021
. One paradigm of quantum control is measurement-based feedback control in which the dynamics of a system are altered as information received from continuous measurements is fed back into the system. For example, in order to impede the movement of a particle a force proportional to the measured velocity can be applied in the opposite direction to that of the motion. Although a measurement inevitably changes the state of a quantum system, feedback control can be achieved with weak or unsharp measurements
Wiseman and Milburn 2010
, which allow to gain information about a system without much disturbance.

In this paper we present the theory that describes the dynamics of a system that is controlled by instantaneously feeding back the measurement signal at each moment, which corresponds to the Markovian limit. This is done by introducing a feedback Hamiltonian which depends on the stochastic measurement signal. The possibility of continuous feedback control was studied theoretically by Wiseman and Milburn
Wiseman and Milburn 2010
as well as experimentally in
Bushev et al. 2006
;
Steixner et al. 2005
;
Setter et al. 2018
. In particular, there are studies of the cooling of a single trapped ion
Bushev et al. 2006
;
Steixner et al. 2005
, the cooling of an optically levitated nano-particle
Setter et al. 2018
, and the cooling of trapped atoms probed by off-resonant light
Leibfried et al. 2003
.

Feedback presupposes a measurement step which necessarily introduces noise into the process even if all classical noise sources are eliminated. In our theory, this is manifest as a diffusion-like term in the master equation. The beauty of direct quantum feedback is that it might be used to cancel the noise which drives the mean values of the dynamical variables as shown in Section
V
. This is possible because the noise in the measurement signal is the same noise that drives the system. The result is that the steady-state of the feedback master equation can have the same variances as the conditioned states since all of the fluctuations in the mean values are overcome.

Here, we use the method developed by Diosi in
Diósi 1988
to express the state change in terms of generators and in the ”co-moving” frame, that is the reference frame in which the expectation values of both position and momentum are zero. We show that this method can greatly reduce the complexity of computations without additional approximation, as well as give insight into the type of feedback necessary to achieve the desired control.

In order to illustrate the power of this method we consider the case that the measured and feedback observables are an arbitrary linear combination of the position and momentum. We derive a stochastic feedback master equation in Section
II
and compute the corresponding steady-state in Section
IV
. In addition, we show that appropriate feedback can be used to compensate a part of the Hamiltonian of the system (Section
V
). In addition, for a harmonic potential, we determine how feedback can confine the particle to the centre of phase space in the laboratory frame, thereby reducing the energy of the particle. This phenomenon is also discussed for other potentials.

II
The Feedback Master Equation

Our feedback scheme relies on the use of information obtained from continuous observation to manipulate the motion of the system, e.g. to cool or confine it.
Assuming the measured observable is associated with the operator
\hat{M}
, the increment of the readout of the continuous measurement at a certain instance is

d\mathcal{M}=\langle\hat{M}\rangle dt+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}dW

(1)

where
\gamma
is the measurement strength which determines the rate at which information is extracted from the system. The first term is the expectation value of the observable,
\langle\hat{M}\rangle\equiv\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{M}\rho]
, defined with respect to the density operator
\rho
in the selective regime which represents the current state of the system for a given measurement record. The second term represents a contribution of Gaussian white noise (
dW
), modelling the deviation of the measurement result from the expectation value of the observable by a random Wiener process.

According to continuous measurement theory
Wiseman and Milburn 2010
;
Jacobs 2014
, the state change of the system, due to the measurement of the observable after a time step
dt
, is given by the Ito stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle d\rho=-

\displaystyle\tfrac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H},\rho]dt-\tfrac{\gamma}{8}\left[\hat{M},\left[\hat{M},\rho\right]\right]dt

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}\left\{\hat{M}-\langle\hat{M}\rangle,\rho\right\}\mathrm{d}W.

(2)

The first term corresponds to the unitary evolution related to the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
of the system. The second term refers to the dissipative dynamics due to measurement and tends to diagonalize the density operator in the eigenbasis of the measured observable,
\hat{M}
. The final term is proportional to the Wiener increment,
dW
, and indicates an update of the observer’s knowledge about the system according to a certain measurement result,
d\mathcal{M}
. The Ito differential
dW
satisfies the following algebra:

\displaystyle\langle dW\rangle_{st}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle dWdW

\displaystyle=dt,

\displaystyle dW^{n}

\displaystyle=0\text{ for }n>2\,.

(3)

The Wiener noise interment
dW
at a given time
t
, points to the future and is thus statistically independent of the state
\rho
and the measurement signal (
1
) at time
t
.

A simple Markovian feedback can be achieved by amplifying and feeding back the measurement signal, via the Hamiltonian
Combes and Wiseman 2011

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{fb}}(t)=\hat{F}\frac{d\mathcal{M}}{\delta t}\,,

(4)

where
\hat{F}
represents an hermitian operator, and
\delta t
is a sufficiently small time interval during which the feedback is implemented by means of the unitary time evolution

U=\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{F}d\mathcal{M}\right]\,.

(5)

Although Markovian feedback is not as powerful as Bayesian feedback
Audretsch et al. 2007
, which uses the best estimate of the system state, it is theoretically and experimentally simpler to implement
Combes and Wiseman 2011
;
Wiseman et al. 2002
. Realistic feedback is not Markovian, but has some time delay. For the Markovian approximation to be satisfied, the time delay between measurement and feedback should be small compared to the time scale on which the system evolves. We point out that it is possible to implement feedback without delay by incorporating it into the interaction with a probe system, that also realises the measurement
Konrad et al. 2021
.

A system undergoing continuous observation and Hamiltonian evolution changes as
\rho\rightarrow\rho+d\rho
, according to Eq. (
2
). Adding feedback, described by the unitary operator given in Eq. (
5
), causes the system to evolve as
\rho\rightarrow U(\rho+d\rho)U^{\dagger}
. The resulting stochastic master equation for the density operator of the system is thus given by

\displaystyle d\rho=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H},\rho\right]dt-\frac{\gamma}{8}\left[\hat{M},\left[\hat{M},\rho\right]\right]dt

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}\left[\hat{F},\left[\hat{F},\rho\right]\right]dt-\frac{i}{2\hbar}\left[\hat{F},\left\{\hat{M},\rho\right\}\right]dt

\displaystyle+\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}\left\{\hat{M}-\langle\hat{M}\rangle,\rho\right\}dW-\frac{i}{\hbar\sqrt{\gamma}}\left[\hat{F},\rho\right]dW\,,

(6)

and has a Lindblad structure. The complete derivation is given in Appendix
A
. This expression also appears in
Wiseman and Milburn 2010
;
Tilloy and Diósi 2016
. The first, second and fifth terms of this equation describes the free evolution and measurement back-action, while the remaining terms include the effects of the feedback loop. The fourth term causes the expected damping while the third term leads to diffusion in the observable conjugate to the generator of feedback
\hat{F}
. The diffusion occurs as a result of the noise in the measurement signal, which is also amplified and fed back to the system.

III
Determining the stationary state using the co-moving frame

Here we follow the method of Diosi
Diósi 1988
in order to derive the stationary-state solution. Accordingly, we use the coordinate free representation and assume the state of the system to be a pure state
\ket{\psi}
. We can thus write the feedback equation for the state vector in the equivalent form

\displaystyle d\ket{\psi}=

\displaystyle\Big\{\Big[-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}-\tfrac{\gamma}{8}\left(\hat{M}-\langle\hat{M}\rangle\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\tfrac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}\hat{F}^{2}-\tfrac{i}{2\hbar}\hat{F}\left(\hat{M}+\langle\hat{M}\rangle\right)\Big]dt

\displaystyle+\left[\tfrac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}(\hat{M}-\langle\hat{M}\rangle)-\tfrac{i}{\hbar\sqrt{\gamma}}\hat{F}\right]dW\Big\}\ket{\psi}\,.

(7)

It is possible to formulate this non-deterministic state change in terms of a generator as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}+d\ket{\psi}=\exp(\hat{G})\ket{\psi}\,,

(8)

where

\displaystyle\hat{G}:=

\displaystyle\Big[-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}-\tfrac{\gamma}{4}(\hat{M}-\langle\hat{M}\rangle)^{2}+\tfrac{i}{4\hbar}\left[\hat{M},\hat{F}\right]-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\langle\hat{M}\rangle\hat{F}\Big]dt

\displaystyle\,+\Big[\tfrac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}(\hat{M}-\langle\hat{M}\rangle)-\tfrac{i}{\hbar\sqrt{\gamma}}\hat{F}\Big]dW\,.

(9)

Up to this point we have not placed any restrictions on the Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}
, the measured observable,
\hat{M}
, or the generator of feedback,
\hat{F}
. Equation (
8
) is therefore valid for any system undergoing continuous measurement of an observable
\hat{M}
and feedback of the form (
5
) with hermitian feedback operator
\hat{F}
.

As outlined by Diosi in
Diósi 1988
, we now consider the dynamics of the system in the co-moving frame, i.e. the reference frame in which the expectation values of both position and momentum are zero. For this purpose, we transform the state
\ket{\psi}+d\ket{\psi}
at time
t+dt
into the co-moving frame as follows

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{\psi}}+d\ket{\tilde{\psi}}=e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}(\langle\hat{p}\rangle+d\langle\hat{p}\rangle)\hat{q}}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}(\langle\hat{q}\rangle+d\langle\hat{q}\rangle)\hat{p}}\left(\ket{\psi}+d\ket{\psi}\right)\,,

(10)

where
d\langle\hat{q}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{q}d\rho)
and
d\langle\hat{p}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{p}d\rho)
. Transformation (
10
) resembles feedback however, it is cannot be realised in a laboratory and therefore does not represent standard feedback, rather a purely mathematical operation. As a consequence of Eq. (
10
), the expectation value of position with respect to the transformed state at time a
t+dt
vanishes:

\displaystyle(\bra{\tilde{\psi}}+d\bra{\tilde{\psi}})\hat{q}(\ket{\tilde{\psi}}+d\ket{\tilde{\psi}})

\displaystyle=(\bra{\psi}+d\bra{\psi})e^{-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}(\langle\hat{q}\rangle+d\langle\hat{q}\rangle)\hat{p}}\hat{q}e^{\tfrac{i}{\hbar}(\langle\hat{q}\rangle+d\langle\hat{q}\rangle)\hat{p}}(\ket{\psi}+d\ket{\psi})

\displaystyle=(\bra{\psi}+d\bra{\psi})\left[\hat{q}-(\langle\hat{q}\rangle+d\langle\hat{q}\rangle)\right](\ket{\psi}+d\ket{\psi})

\displaystyle=0\,.

Similarly the expectation value of momentum,
(\bra{\tilde{\psi}}+d\bra{\tilde{\psi}})\hat{p}(\ket{\tilde{\psi}}+d\ket{\tilde{\psi}})
equals zero. The state
\ket{\tilde{\psi}}+d\ket{\tilde{\psi}}
therefore represents the state of the system viewed from the co-moving reference frame.

It is always possible to choose a special coordinate system where
\langle\hat{q}\rangle=0=\langle\hat{p}\rangle
at the given instant
t
when
\ket{\psi}
is considered. At this instant
\ket{\psi}
coincides with
\ket{\tilde{\psi}}
and it follows that the state change with respect to this coordinate system reads

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{\psi}}+d\ket{\tilde{\psi}}=

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}d\langle\hat{p}\rangle\hat{q}}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}d\langle\hat{q}\rangle\hat{p}}e^{\hat{G}}\ket{\tilde{\psi}}\,.

(11)

Note, that we have not placed any restrictions on the Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}
, measured observable,
\hat{M}
, or the generator of feedback,
\hat{F}
. Therefore, by replacing
q
and
p
in Eq. (
11
) with any pair of conjugate variables, we can shift the description of the system into a reference frame with respect to which the expectation values of these conjugate variables vanish simultaneously. Using the measured observable
M
and its conjugate momentum as the conjugate variables, greatly simplifies the calculation of the stationary states as we shall see in Section
IV
. This simplification is the essence of Diosi’s method, and the form invariance of Eq. (
11
) under canonical transformations makes it applicable to compute the stationary states for a large class of continuous measurements with feedback.

IV
Stationary state of a particle in a harmonic potential

In order to illustrate the power of this generalised approach we consider the specific case that the measured observable is an arbitrary linear combination of position and momentum,

\displaystyle\hat{M}=\alpha\hat{q}+\beta\hat{p}\,,

(12)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are real constants of units
1
and
{\mathrm{kg}}^{-1}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
, respectively. Furthermore, let the generator of feedback be

\displaystyle\hat{F}=\chi\hat{q}+\delta\hat{p}

(13)

where
\chi
and
\delta
are real gain factors of units
\mathrm{kg}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
and
{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
, respectively. In order to simplify the calculations that follow, we introduce the following operators

\displaystyle\hat{Q}

\displaystyle:=\hat{M}=\alpha\hat{q}+\beta\hat{p}

(14)

\displaystyle\hat{P}

\displaystyle:=-\beta^{\prime}\hat{q}+\alpha^{\prime}\hat{p}

(15)

that satisfy the canonical commutation relation,
[\hat{Q},\hat{P}]=i\hbar
, where for simplicity we assume that the factor
{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}+\beta\beta^{\prime}}=1
. This assumption also implies that
Q
and
P
are canonically conjugate. In addition, we can write

\displaystyle\hat{F}=u\hat{Q}+v\hat{P}\,,

(16)

where
u
and
v
are real factors of units
\mathrm{kg}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
and
{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
, respectively.
The transition to the co-moving frame can also be achieved by replacing variables
q
(
p
) in transformation (
10
) by the conjugate pair
Q
and
P
since the expectation values of position and momentum vanish if, and only if, those of the generalised variables
Q
and
P
vanish.

As an example, let us consider a particle in a harmonic potential with Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2m}\hat{p}^{2}+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}\hat{q}^{2}\,.

(17)

If
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha
and
\beta^{\prime}=m^{2}\omega^{2}\beta
, the Hamiltonian of a harmonic oscillator is form invariant under linear transformations of this type, so that

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2m}\hat{P}^{2}+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}\hat{Q}^{2}\,.

(18)

We now show that the transformation (
11
) for a particle in a harmonic potential leads to a unique solution in the stationary regime. Applying the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula and the Ito rule (
3
) to Eq. (
11
) results in a state change in the co-moving frame given by

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{\psi}}+d\ket{\tilde{\psi}}=

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{\left[-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\hat{{H}}-\tfrac{\gamma}{4}\left(\hat{Q}^{2}-\langle\hat{Q}^{2}\rangle\right)\right]dt

\displaystyle\hskip 18.0pt-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma}{2}}\Big[\left(i\hbar+\langle\{\hat{Q},\hat{P}\}\rangle\right)\hat{Q}-2\langle\hat{Q}^{2}\rangle\hat{P}\Big]dW\Big\}\ket{\tilde{\psi}},

(19)

where we ignored irrelevant phase factors and assumed Hamiltonian (
18
). The full derivation and the general state change for arbitrary Hamiltonian are provided in Appendix
B
.

We note that Eq. (
19
) no longer depends on feedback. This is to be expected as feedback of form (
5
) corresponds to a shift of the Wigner function
Olivares 2012
in phase space, and consequently its shape remains unchanged. Therefore, from the point of view of the co-moving frame, feedback has no impact on the state of the quantum particle. We note that if the generator of feedback
\hat{F}
contains terms of higher orders of position and momentum, for example angular momentum, then this is no longer true. However, this method still leads to a simplified state change in the co-moving frame since all first-order terms in Q and P are eliminated.

The stationary solutions are of the form
\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{\infty}}\exp(-iEt/\hbar)
, where
\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{\infty}}
is the time-independent part and
E
is a real number with units of energy. Substitution of this ansatz into Eq. (
19
) followed by a comparison of coefficients of the independent increments
dt
and
dW
yields

\displaystyle\left[\hat{{H}}-i\tfrac{\hbar\gamma}{4}\left(\hat{Q}^{2}-\langle\hat{Q}^{2}\rangle_{\infty}\right)\right]\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{\infty}}=E\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{\infty}}\,,

(20)

such that
E=\braket{\tilde{\psi}_{\infty}|\hat{H}|\tilde{\psi}_{\infty}}\equiv\langle\hat{H}\rangle_{\infty}
, and

\displaystyle\Big[\left(i\hbar+\langle\{\hat{Q},\hat{P}\}\rangle_{\infty}\right)\hat{Q}-2\langle\hat{Q}^{2}\rangle_{\infty}\hat{P}\Big]\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{\infty}}=0\,.

(21)

If we introduce the wave function in position representation
\tilde{\psi}(q)
, Eq. (
21
) becomes a first-order linear ordinary differential equation of the form
aq\tilde{\psi}(q)={d\tilde{\psi}(q)}/{dq}
, where
a
is a complex constant, namely

\displaystyle\big(i\hbar\alpha+\langle\{\hat{Q},\hat{p}\}\rangle_{\infty}

\displaystyle\big){q}\tilde{\psi}_{\infty}(q)

\displaystyle=i\hbar\big(i\hbar\beta-\langle\{\hat{Q},\hat{q}\}\rangle_{\infty}\big)\frac{d\tilde{\psi}_{\infty}(q)}{dq}\,.

(22)

This differential equation is known to have a Gaussian solution and it follows that the stationary solution in position representation is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}_{\infty}(q)=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\operatorname{Re}\{\sigma^{2}\}}{2\pi|\sigma^{2}|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\exp\left(-\frac{q^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}\right)\,,

(23)

where

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{i\hbar}{2}\frac{\langle\{\hat{q},\hat{Q}\}\rangle_{\infty}-i\hbar\beta}{\langle\{\hat{p},\hat{Q}\}\rangle_{\infty}+i\hbar\alpha}\,,

(24)

and similarly in momentum space

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}_{\infty}(p)=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2\operatorname{Re}\{\sigma^{2}\}}{\pi\hbar^{2}}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{4}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma^{2}p^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\right)\,.

(25)

The eigenvalue equation (
21
), and equivalently Eq. (
22
), holds for arbitrary Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, see Eq. (
82
). Therefore, one could ask the question whether arbitrary Hamiltonians allow the energy eigenvalue equation
and eigenvalue equation (
21
), that follow from Eq. (
82
), to be simultaneously satisfied. For the free particle it is possible to use the results of
Diósi 1988
to show that both eigenvalue equations hold. We briefly argue that this is also the case for a general Hamiltonian however, the analysis is be beyond the scope of this paper.

For the harmonic oscillator we can show explicitly that these Gaussian stationary wave functions also satisfy the energy eigenvalue equation (
20
) for all measurements strengths
\gamma
, see Appendix
C
. Proving this requires us to express the stationary value of the variance of the measured observable in the co-moving frame,
\langle\hat{Q}^{2}\rangle_{\infty}
, as a function of the expectation value of the anti-commutator
\langle\{\hat{Q},\hat{P}\}\rangle_{\infty}
and the variance
\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{\infty}
. The calculation of these terms is the subject of the next section. It turns out that under certain circumstances the quantities
\langle\hat{Q}^{2}\rangle
,
\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle
and
\langle\{\hat{Q},\hat{P}\}\rangle
obey a set of coupled first-order differential equations and thus evolve deterministically. Further, in Appendix
C
it is shown that for a particle in a harmonic potential the energy eigenvalue in Eq. (
20
) is given by

\displaystyle E=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m\langle\hat{Q}^{2}\rangle_{\infty}}\,.

(26)

It also turns out that in the weak measurement limit,
\gamma\rightarrow 0
, the energy with respect to the co-moving frame is minimal, i.e.,
\langle\hat{Q}^{2}\rangle_{\infty}\rightarrow\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}
. In addition, the product
\langle\hat{Q}^{2}\rangle_{\infty}\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{\infty}\rightarrow\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4}
so that
E\rightarrow\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{\infty}/m
, which is twice the expectation value of the kinetic energy in agreement with the Virial theorem.

IV.1
Calculation of stationary widths for a particle in a harmonic potential

From the expressions of second-order moments of position and momentum a system of differential equations can be derived that allows to calculate the width of the asymptotic wave function,

\displaystyle d\langle\Delta\hat{q}^{2}\rangle=

\displaystyle d\langle\hat{q}^{2}\rangle-2\langle\hat{q}\rangle d\langle\hat{q}\rangle-(d\langle\hat{q}\rangle)^{2}

(27)

\displaystyle d\langle\Delta\hat{p}^{2}\rangle=

\displaystyle d\langle\hat{p}^{2}\rangle-2\langle\hat{p}\rangle d\langle\hat{p}\rangle-(d\langle\hat{p}\rangle)^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle d\langle\{\Delta\hat{q},\Delta\hat{p}\}\rangle=

\displaystyle d\langle\{\hat{q},\hat{p}\}\rangle-2\langle\hat{q}\rangle d\langle\hat{p}\rangle-2\langle\hat{p}\rangle d\langle\hat{q}\rangle

\displaystyle-2d\langle\hat{q}\rangle d\langle\hat{p}\rangle\,.

(29)

Here we have introduced the notation
\Delta\hat{o}=\hat{o}-\langle\hat{o}\rangle
, such that the variance of
\hat{o}
is given by
\langle\Delta\hat{o}^{2}\rangle=\langle\hat{o}\rangle^{2}-\langle\hat{o}^{2}\rangle
. Note that the last terms in Eqs. (
27
)-(
29
) take into account the stochastic contribution
dW
in the increment of the expectation value, see equation below, the square of which leads to a contribution of order
dt
. The increment
d\langle\hat{o}\rangle
of an arbitrary operator
\hat{o}
can be calculated using the master equation (
II
),

\displaystyle d\langle\hat{o}\rangle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{o}d\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\langle[\hat{o},\hat{H}]\rangle-\tfrac{\gamma}{8}\langle[\hat{Q},[\hat{Q},\hat{o}]]\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\tfrac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}\langle[\hat{F},[\hat{F},\hat{o}]]\rangle-\tfrac{i}{2\hbar}\langle\{\hat{Q},[\hat{o},\hat{F}]\}\rangle\Big]dt

\displaystyle+\Big[\tfrac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}\langle\{\hat{o},\Delta\hat{Q}\}\rangle-\tfrac{i}{\hbar\sqrt{\gamma}}\langle[\hat{o},\hat{F}]\rangle\Big]dW\,.

(30)

Equations (
27
)-(
29
) are form invariant for any pair of conjugate variables. We can thus compute these total differentials for
\hat{Q}
and
\hat{P}
.

\displaystyle d\langle(\Delta\hat{Q})^{2}\rangle=

\displaystyle-\gamma\langle(\Delta\hat{Q})^{2}\rangle^{2}dt

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle\{\Delta\hat{Q},\Delta[\hat{Q},\hat{H}]\}\rangle dt

\displaystyle+{\sqrt{\gamma}}\langle(\Delta\hat{Q})^{3}\rangle dW

(31)

\displaystyle d\langle(\Delta\hat{P})^{2}\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}\gamma}{4}dt-\frac{\gamma}{4}\langle\{\Delta\hat{P},\Delta\hat{Q}\}\rangle^{2}dt

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle\{\Delta\hat{P},\Delta[\hat{P},\hat{H}]\}\rangle dt

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}\langle\{(\Delta\hat{P})^{2},\Delta\hat{Q}\}\rangle dW

(32)

\displaystyle d\langle\{\Delta\hat{Q},\Delta\hat{P}\}\rangle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle\{\Delta\hat{Q},\Delta[\hat{P},\hat{H}]\}\rangle dt

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle\{\Delta\hat{P},\Delta[\hat{Q},\hat{H}]\}\rangle dt

\displaystyle-\gamma\langle\{\Delta\hat{P},\Delta\hat{Q}\}\rangle\langle(\Delta\hat{Q})^{2}\rangle dt

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}\langle\{\{\Delta\hat{Q},\Delta\hat{P}\},\Delta\hat{Q}\}\rangle dW\,.

(33)

This set of coupled equations holds for all times
t
and for an arbitrary Hamiltonian,
H
.

For a particle in a harmonic potential and for large times
t>>\frac{m\omega}{\hbar\gamma}
, we can use the fact that the stationary states have a Gaussian form, see Section
IV
. This also applies for a free particle and it can also be assumed in good approximation whenever the particle is localised due to the measurement on length scales on which the potential does not vary much
Halliwell and Zoupas 1995
. In this case higher order moments are zero, in particular

\displaystyle\langle\Delta\hat{Q}^{3}\rangle_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\langle\{(\Delta\hat{P})^{2},\Delta\hat{Q}\}\rangle_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\langle\{\{\Delta\hat{Q},\Delta\hat{P}\},\Delta\hat{Q}\}\rangle_{\infty}=0\,.

(34)

Assuming that the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
is that of the harmonic oscillator (
18
), we note that
[\hat{Q},\hat{H}]=\tfrac{i\hbar}{m}\hat{P}
and
[\hat{P},\hat{H}]=-i\hbar m\omega^{2}\hat{Q}
so that Eqs. (
31
)-(
33
) are reduced to a system of linear first-order coupled differential equations which can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=-2\kappa x^{2}+z

(35)

\displaystyle\dot{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(1-z^{2}\right)-z

(36)

\displaystyle\dot{z}

\displaystyle=2\left(y-x\right)-2\kappa xz\,,

(37)

where

\displaystyle x:=

\displaystyle\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}\langle(\Delta\hat{Q})^{2}\rangle

(38)

\displaystyle y:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar m\omega}\langle(\Delta\hat{P})^{2}\rangle

(39)

\displaystyle z:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar}\langle\{\Delta\hat{Q},\Delta\hat{P}\}\rangle

(40)

are the unitless versions of the variances of interest and

\displaystyle\kappa

V
Feedback for cooling and confinement

The results of the previous section are dictated by measurement only, since the feedback vanishes from the equation of motion in the co-moving frame. Here we consider the effect of feedback on the motion of the quantum particle viewed from the lab frame and how it can be used to cool and confine the particle.

At each moment in time, feedback changes the position and momentum of the particle by an amount proportional to the measurement signal and according to coefficients
\chi
and
\delta
in Eq. (
13
). By tuning these coefficients, we can alter the motion of the particle in such a way as to mimic the action of a quadratic potential. In general, this allows for the Hamiltonian of the system to be altered by a term quadratic in the measured observable.

Feedback can also be used for the purpose of cooling and confinement. Intuitively, information gained from measurement can be used to gradually reduce the expectation value of the measured observable
Q
by shifting the system towards a smaller value of this observable. This is achieved by continuously applying feedback generated by the conjugate momentum
P
with a negative scaling. At the same time, it is possible to choose this scaling such that the random changes in
Q
introduced by the measurement diminish over time. The same can be achieved for the conjugate momentum
P
. This is realised by introducing friction generated by
Q
which simultaneously compensates for random changes of the conjugate momentum
P
generated by the measurement of
Q
. The combined feedback thus leads to damping of both the measured observable
Q
and its conjugate momentum
P
. To determine the values of the scaling coefficients
u
and
v
in Eq. (
16
) that lead to optimal cooling and confinement, it is necessary to consider the equations that govern the motion of the particle.

The stochastic master equation (
II
) in terms of the operators
\hat{Q}
and
\hat{P}
reads

\displaystyle d\rho=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H}+\tfrac{u}{2}\hat{Q}^{2},\rho\right]dt

\displaystyle-\tfrac{\gamma}{8}\left(1+\left(\tfrac{2u}{\hbar\gamma}\right)^{2}\right)\left[\hat{Q},\left[\hat{Q},\rho\right]\right]dt

\displaystyle-\tfrac{v^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}\left[\hat{P},\left[\hat{P},\rho\right]\right]dt-\tfrac{iv}{2\hbar}\left[\hat{P},\left\{\hat{Q},\rho\right\}\right]dt

\displaystyle-\tfrac{uv}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}\left(\left[\hat{Q},\left[\hat{P},\rho\right]\right]+\left[\hat{P},\left[\hat{Q},\rho\right]\right]\right)dt

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}\left\{\hat{Q}-\langle\hat{Q}\rangle,\rho\right\}dW-\tfrac{i}{\hbar\sqrt{\gamma}}\left[u\hat{Q}+v\hat{P},\rho\right]dW\,,

(47)

where we inserted
\hat{F}=u\hat{Q}+v\hat{P}
, and used the identity
[\hat{Q},\{\hat{Q},\rho\}]=[\hat{Q}^{2},\rho]
to obtain the first term in the this equation. Eq. (
V
) shows that by choosing

, the Hamiltonian of the system can be altered by a quadratic term in the measured observable
Q
. This is discussed in Section
V.1
.

Furthermore, according to Eq. (
IV.1
) the increments in the measured observable and its conjugate momentum are given by

\displaystyle d\langle\hat{Q}\rangle=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\langle[\hat{Q},\hat{H}]\rangle dt+v\langle\hat{Q}\rangle dt

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\tfrac{2\hbar\kappa}{m}}\left(\tfrac{m\omega}{\hbar}\langle(\Delta\hat{Q})^{2}\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2\kappa}\tfrac{v}{\omega}\right)dW

(48)

\displaystyle d\langle\hat{P}\rangle=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\langle[\hat{P},\hat{H}]\rangle dt-u\langle Q\rangle dt

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\tfrac{\hbar m\omega^{2}\kappa}{2}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\hbar}\langle\{\Delta\hat{Q},\Delta\hat{P}\}\rangle-\tfrac{1}{\kappa}\tfrac{u}{m\omega^{2}}\right)dW\,,

(49)

respectively. These equations indicate how to simultaneously eliminate the stochastic terms. As discussed in Section
V.2.2
, the corresponding choice of feedback turns out to be sufficient for cooling and confinement of a particle in a harmonic potential. Furthermore, it is possible to drive the particle into the ground state by combining this choice of feedback with weak measurement.

V.1
Manipulation of the Hamiltonian

First we consider the implications on the system dynamics if we choose feedback in order to alter the Hamiltonian of the system. If the generator of feedback is a linear combination of position and momentum, see Eq. (
13
) and Eq. (
16
), then feedback proportional to the measured observable
Q
allows us to simulate an additional quadratic potential in
Q
. This is implied by the form of the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
V
).

In the case of a harmonic potential (
18
), we consider how feedback might be used to compensate for a portion of the Hamiltonian. Let us assume that
\hat{F}=-m\omega^{2}\hat{Q}
, i.e.
u=-m\omega^{2}
and
v=0
. The master equation (
V

\displaystyle d\rho=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H}-\tfrac{m\omega^{2}}{2}\hat{Q}^{2},\rho\right]dt

\displaystyle-\tfrac{\gamma}{8}\left(1+\left(\tfrac{2m\omega^{2}}{\hbar\gamma}\right)^{2}\right)\left[\hat{Q},\left[\hat{Q},\rho\right]\right]dt

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{2}\left\{\hat{Q}-\langle\hat{Q}\rangle,\rho\right\}dW+\tfrac{im\omega^{2}}{\hbar\sqrt{\gamma}}\left[\hat{Q},\rho\right]dW\,.

(50)

Here feedback cancels a portion of the Hamiltonian so that the effective Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle:=\hat{H}-\tfrac{m\omega^{2}}{2}\hat{Q}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2m}\hat{P}^{2}

(51)

The master equation (
V.1

To take a particular example, let us continuously measure position, with
\hat{Q}=\hat{q}
and
\hat{P}=\hat{p}
(i.e.
\alpha=1
and
\beta=0
), so that

is the Hamiltonian of a free particle. According to Eq. (
V
) and in the non-selective regime of measurement, the expectation value of the position

grows linearly proportional to the expectation value of the conjugate momentum,
while the expectation value of the momentum
{p}
remains constant, according to Eq. (
V
). A method to continuously infer the position of the ion through spontaneous light scattering into mirror modes is provided in
Steixner et al. 2005

Alternatively, let us continuously monitor momentum, with
\hat{Q}=(m\omega)^{-1}\hat{p}
and
\hat{P}=-m\omega\hat{q}
, i.e.
\alpha=0
and
\beta=(m\omega)^{-1}
in order to satisfy the condition
\alpha^{2}+m^{2}\omega^{2}\beta^{2}=1

is given by

V
) and Eq. (

V.2
Cooling and confinement of a particle in a harmonic potential

In order to investigate the thermalising effect of the feedback, we first compare the master equation (
V
) with that of an optical harmonic oscillator coupled to a heat bath. We consider the prototypical example of an optical oscillator in the rotating wave approximation and in the non-selective regime of measurement, allowing us to compare our results with the typical equations obtained for such a system.

In the second part of this section we considering the equations governing the increment of the energy, as well as the expectation values of the measured observable and the conjugate momentum, outside of the limits of the aforementioned approximations. This allows us to derive a generalised approach to cooling that facilitates the simultaneous reduction of the expectation values of both position and momentum at an exponential rate.

V.2.1
Optical oscillator coupled to a heat bath

The position and momentum operators,
\hat{q}
and
\hat{p}
, can be expressed in terms of creation and annihilation operators as,

\hat{q}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;\hat{p}=i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar m\omega}{2}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{a}).

(52)

In Appendix
E
the master equation (
II
) written in terms of creation and annihilation operators is given in Eq. (
E
). We consider this result in the rotating wave approximation and in the non-selective regime of measurement, such that the state of an optical harmonic oscillator obeys

\displaystyle d\rho^{\prime}=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H},\rho\right]dt-\tfrac{iu}{4m\omega}[\left\{\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right\},\rho]dt

\displaystyle+\left(c-v\right)(\hat{a}\rho\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},\rho\})dt

\displaystyle+c(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\rho\hat{a}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger},\rho\})dt\,,

(53)

where the real constant

\displaystyle c=\tfrac{\kappa\omega}{4}+\tfrac{1}{4\kappa\omega}(\tfrac{u^{2}}{m^{2}\omega^{2}}+v^{2})+\tfrac{v}{2}\,.

(54)

For a particular class of feedback, namely
\hat{F}\propto\hat{P}
with
u=0

\displaystyle d\rho=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}[H,\rho]dt

\displaystyle+\gamma^{\prime}(N+1)(\hat{a}\rho\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},\rho\})dt

\displaystyle+\gamma^{\prime}\ N\ (\hat{a}^{\dagger}\rho\hat{a}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger},\rho\})dt\,,

(55)

where
\gamma^{\prime}

N=1/(\exp(\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{k_{B}T})-1)
is the mean excitation of the heat bath of harmonic oscillators, with
T

T=\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_{B}\ln((N+1)/N)}\,.

(56)

A system in thermal contact with a heat bath as described by Eq. (
V.2.1

V.2.1

V.2.1

V.2.1

From the difference between the coefficients of the pumping and the dissipation terms, we obtain the rate of thermalisation, i.e.
\gamma^{\prime}=-v
. If
\gamma^{\prime}
is greater than zero, then the system loses more energy via dissipation than it gains by pumping, which is equivalent to cooling. We can therefore conclude that if the proportionality constant
v
is negative then energy is reduced, the system is cooled.

That the generator of feedback should be chosen proportional to the conjugate momentum
P
with a negative scaling factor is intuitively correct. Doing so correspond to performing a shift of the measured observable towards a smaller value, based on information obtained from measuring the system, as discussed earlier. In Section
V.2.2
, we shall see that in the selective regime of measurement the situation is more subtle, none-the-less the results that follow will be useful for comparison.

The effective temperature of the system can be calculated using the ratio of the coefficients of the pumping and the dissipation terms in Eq. (
V.2.1
). By substituting this ratio into the Eq. (
56

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_{B}\ln\left(\frac{c-v}{c}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_{B}\ln\left(\frac{v-\kappa\omega}{v+\kappa\omega}\right)}\,.

(57)

Minimal temperature is therefore achieved if

, i.e.
\hat{F}=-\kappa\omega\hat{P}

\gamma^{\prime}=\kappa\omega=\tfrac{\hbar\gamma}{2m\omega}

\omega

10^{-7}K
. Whereas for an optical system of frequency of the order
10^{12}
, the system is cooled down to a temperature of the order
10^{-1}K
.

The dynamics of the mean excitation of the system can be calculated using
\tfrac{d\langle n\rangle}{dt}=-\gamma^{\prime}d\langle n\rangle+\gamma^{\prime}N

Wiseman and Milburn 2010

\langle n\rangle=N+(n_{i}-N)e^{-\gamma^{\prime}t},

(58)

where

is the initial mean excitation of the system and

is the asymptotic mean excitation of the system determined by

VI
Discussion

In this paper we investigated continuous measurement and feedback on a particle in a harmonic potential, where the observable and the generator of feedback are arbitrary linear combinations of position and momentum. We have shown that the asymptotic state, assumed by the particle after a certain convergence time determined by the measurement strength, is represented by a Gaussian wave function. Its width does not depend on the feedback but only on the product of the measurement strength, the variance of the ground state and the time period of the oscillator. On the other hand, the location of the Gaussian in phase space depends on the feedback. This is not surprising, since the feedback merely shifts the wave function in position and/or momentum space.

It is possible to localise the wave function in the centre of the harmonic potential with vanishing momentum expectation value. The harmonic oscillator wave function for the different scenarios (with and without measurement and feedback) is depicted in Figure
4
. Feedback enables localisation in the ground state, measurement allows the wave function to climb up the potential mountain without being squeezed as is the case in the absence of measurement. This surprising effect can be understood in the Heisenberg Picture where the observable is rotated periodically into its conjugate momentum, resulting in a homogeneous localisation of the particle.

Our analysis shows that, remarkably, for a free particle or a particle in a harmonic potential, the feedback can be employed to cancel the measurement noise completely, cp. Eqs. (

For arbitrary initial states the values are known in the asymptotic regime as a function of the relative measurement strength. They can also be eventually inferred from the measurement results (cp. state monitoring
Konrad et al. 2010
).
By combining this confining feedback with sufficiently weak measurement, it is possible to cool the system to the ground state.

We conjecture that cooling is also possible for arbitrary potentials. Feedback compensating the noise that contributes to the change of the expectation value of the measured observable simultaneously damps the motion of the particle. For sufficiently strong measurement, the asymptotic wave function can be localised on a length scale such that the potential can be approximated by a polynomial of second-order
Halliwell and Zoupas 1995
. Thus the dynamics is reduced to the case of the harmonic oscillator as discussed above.

On the other hand, it is also possible to control the motion of the particle by means of feedback which adds an extra term quadratic in the measured observable
Q
to the Hamiltonian. This allows for modifying or even compensating the kinetic or potential energy in the Hamiltonian. However, such dynamic control contributes in general to the generation of noise, since it is not compatible with the compensation of noise.
