Abstract
. We introduce Probabilistic Regular Expressions ( 𝖯𝖱𝖤 \mathsf{PRE} ), a probabilistic analogue of regular expressions denoting probabilistic languages in which every word is assigned a probability of being generated. We present and prove the completeness of an inference system for reasoning about probabilistic language equivalence of 𝖯𝖱𝖤 \mathsf{PRE} based on Salomaa’s axiomatisation of Kleene Algebra.



1.
Introduction

Kleene
(
Kleene 1951
)
introduced regular expressions and proved that these denote exactly the languages accepted by deterministic finite automata. In his seminal paper, Kleene left open a completeness question: are there a finite number of equations that enable reasoning about language equivalence of regular expressions? Since then, the pursuit of inference systems for equational reasoning about the equivalence of regular expressions has been subject of extensive study
(
Salomaa 1966
;
Krob 1990
;
Boffa 1990
;
Kozen 1994
)
. The first proposal is due to Salomaa
(
Salomaa 1966
)
, who introduced a non-algebraic axiomatisation of regular expressions and proved its completeness.

Deterministic automata are a particular type of transition system: simply put, an automaton is an object with a finite set of states and a
deterministic
transition function that assigns every state and every action of the input alphabet
exactly
one next state. By varying the type of transition function one gets different systems: e.g. if the function assigns to every state and every action of the input alphabet
a set
of the next states, the resulting system is said to be non-deterministic; if the transition function assigns the next state based on any sort of
probability distribution
then the system is said to be probabilistic. Probabilistic systems appear in a range of applications, including modelling randomised algorithms, cryptographic protocols, and probabilistic programs. In this paper, we focus on generative probabilistic transition systems (
\mathsf{GPTS}
) with explicit termination
(
van Glabbeek et al. 1995
)
, and study the questions that Kleene and Salomaa answered for deterministic automata.

Our motivation to look at probabilistic extensions of regular expressions and axiomatic reasoning is two-fold: first, regular expressions and extensions thereof have been used in the verification of uninterpreted imperative programs, including network policies
(
Kozen and Patron 2000
;
Kot and Kozen 2005
;
Anderson et al. 2014
)
; second, reasoning about exact behaviour of probabilistic imperative programs is subtle
(
Chen et al. 2022
)
, in particular in the presence of loops. By studying the semantics and axiomatisations of regular expressions featuring probabilistic primitives, we want to enable axiomatic reasoning for randomised programs and provide a basis to develop further verification techniques.

We start by introducing the syntax of Probabilistic Regular Expressions (
\mathsf{PRE}
), inspired by work from the probabilistic pattern matching literature
(
Ross 2000
)
.
\mathsf{PRE}
are formed through constants from an alphabet and
regular
operations of probabilistic choice, sequential composition, probabilistic Kleene star, identity and emptiness. We define the probabilistic analogue of
language semantics
of
\mathsf{PRE}
as exactly the behaviours of
\mathsf{GPTS}
. We achieve this by endowing
\mathsf{PRE}
with operational semantics in the form of
\mathsf{GPTS}
via a construction reminiscent of Antimirov derivatives of regular expressions
(
Antimirov 1996
)
. We also give a converse construction, allowing us to describe languages accepted by finite-state
\mathsf{GPTS}
in terms of
\mathsf{PRE}
, thus establishing an analogue of Kleene’s theorem.

The main contribution of this paper is presenting an inference system for reasoning about probabilistic language equivalence of
\mathsf{PRE}
and proving its completeness. The technical core of the paper is devoted to the completeness proof, which relies on technical tools convex algebra, arising from the rich structure of probabilistic languages. While being in the spirit of classic results from automata theory, our development relies on the more abstract approach enabled via the theory of universal coalgebra
(
Rutten 2000
)
. As much as a concrete completeness proof ought to be possible, our choice to use the coalgebraic approach was fueled by wanting to reuse recent abstract results on the algebraic structure of probabilistic languages. A concrete proof would have to deal with fixpoints of probabilistic languages and would therefore require highly combinatorial and syntactic proofs about these. Instead, we reuse a range of hard results on convex algebras and fixpoints that Milius
(
Milius 2018
)
, Sokolova and Woracek
(
Sokolova and Woracek 2015
;
Sokolova and Woracek 2018
)
proved in the last 5 years. In particular, we rely on the theory of rational fixpoints (for proper functors
(
Milius 2018
)
), which can be seen as a categorical generalisation of regular languages.

Our completeness proof provides further evidence that the use of coalgebras over proper functors provides a good abstraction for completeness theorems, where general steps can be abstracted away leaving as a domain-specific task to achieve completeness a construction to syntactically build solutions to systems of equations. Proving the uniqueness of such solutions is ultimately the most challenging step in the proof. By leveraging the theory of proper functors, our proof of completeness, which depends on establishing an abstract universal property, boils down to an argument that can be viewed as a natural extension of the work by Salomaa
(
Salomaa 1966
)
and Brzozowski
(
Brzozowski 1964
)
from the 1960s.

In a nutshell, the contributions of this paper are as follows.

•

We introduce Probabilistic Regular Expressions (
\mathsf{PRE}
), an analogue of Kleene’s regular expressions denoting probabilistic languages and propose an inference system for reasoning about language equivalence of
\mathsf{PRE}
(
Section

2
).

•

We provide a small-step semantics of
\mathsf{PRE}
through an analogue of Antimirov derivatives (
Section

3
) endowing expressions with a structure of Generative Probabilistic Transition Systems (
\mathsf{GPTS}
).

•

We give an overview of the coalgebraic setup for our main results (
Sections

4
and
5
) exposing which specific technical lemmas are needed for our concrete proof.

•

We prove soundness (
Section

6
) and completeness (
Section

7
) results for our axiomatisation. Due to our use of proper functors, the proof boils down to a generalisation of a known proof of Salomaa for regular expressions
(
Salomaa 1966
)
exposing the connection to a classical result. We also obtain an analogue of Kleene’s theorem allowing the conversion of finite-state
\mathsf{GPTS}
to expressions through an analogue of Brzozowski’s method
(
Brzozowski 1964
)
.

with a light blue background
.

2.
Probabilistic Regular Expressions

In this section, we will introduce the syntax and the language semantics of probabilistic regular expressions (
\mathsf{PRE}
), as well as a candidate inference system to reason about the equivalence of
\mathsf{PRE}
.

Syntax.
Given a finite alphabet
A
, the syntax of
\mathsf{PRE}
is given by:

e,f\in\mathsf{Exp}::={\mathsf{0}}\mid{\mathsf{1}}\mid a\in A\mid e\oplus_{p}f\mid e\mathbin{;}f\mid e^{[p]}\quad\quad\quad p\in[0,1]

We denote the expressions that immediately abort and successfully terminate by
{\mathsf{0}}
and
{\mathsf{1}}
respectively. For every letter
a\in A
in the alphabet, there is a corresponding expression representing an atomic action. Given two expressions
e,f\in{\mathsf{Exp}}
and
p\in[0,1]
, probabilistic choice
e\oplus_{p}f
denotes an expression that performs
e
with probability
p
and performs
f
with probability
1-p
. One can think of
\oplus_{p}
as the probabilistic analogue of the
plus operator (
e+f
) in Kleene’s regular expressions.
e\mathbin{;}f
represents sequential composition, while
e^{[p]}
is a probabilistic analogue of Kleene star: it successfully terminates with probability
1-p
or with probability
p
performs
e
and then iterates
e^{[p]}
again. In terms of the notational convention, the sequential composition
(\mathbin{;})
has higher precedence than the probabilistic choice
(\oplus_{p})
.

Example 2.1.

The expression
a\mathbin{;}a^{[\frac{1}{4}]}
first performs action
a
with probability
1
and then enters a loop which successfully terminates with probability
\frac{3}{4}
or performs action
a
with probability
\frac{1}{4}
and then repeats the loop again. Intuitively, if we think of the action
a
as observable, the expression above denotes a probability associated with a non-empty sequence of
a
’s. For example, the sequence
aaa
would be observed with probability
1\times(1/4)^{2}\times 3/4=3/64
.

Language semantics.

\mathsf{PRE}
denote probabilistic languages
A^{*}\to[0,1]
. For instance, the expression
{\mathsf{0}}
denotes a function that assigns
0
to every word, whereas
{\mathsf{1}}
and
a
respectively assign probability
1
to the empty word and the word containing a single letter
a
from the alphabet. The probabilistic choice
e\oplus_{p}f
denotes a language in which the probability of each word is the total sum of its probability in
e
scaled by
p
and its probability in
f
scaled by
1-p
. Describing the semantics of sequential composition and loops inductively is more involved. In particular, the semantics of loops would require a fixpoint calculation, which does not have as clear and straightforward (closed-form) formula, as the asterate of regular languages. Instead, we take an
operational approach
, and we formally define the language semantics of
\mathsf{PRE}
in
Section

5
through a small-step operational semantics, using a specific type of probabilistic transition system, which we introduce next.

Generative probabilistic transition systems.
A
\mathsf{GPTS}
consists of a set of states
Q
and a transition function that maps each state
q\in Q
to finitely many distinct outgoing arrows of the form:

•

successful termination
with probability
t
(denoted
q\xRightarrow{t}\checkmark
), or

•

to another state
r
, via an
a
-labelled transition
, with probability
s\in[0,1]
(denoted
q\xrightarrow{a\mid s}r
).

We require that, for each state, the total sum of probabilities appearing on outgoing arrows sums up to less or equal to one. The remaining probability mass is used to model unsuccessful termination, hence the state with no outgoing arrows can be thought of as exposing deadlock behaviour.

Given a word
w\in A^{*}
the probability of it being generated by a state

(denoted
\llbracket q\rrbracket(w)\in[0,1]
) is defined inductively:

(1)

\llbracket q\rrbracket(\epsilon)=t\quad\text{if }q\xRightarrow{t}\checkmark\qquad\qquad\llbracket q\rrbracket(av)=\sum_{q\xrightarrow{a\mid s}r}s\cdot\llbracket r\rrbracket(v)

We say that two states
q
and
q^{\prime}
are

if for all words

, we have that
\llbracket q\rrbracket(w)=\llbracket q^{\prime}\rrbracket(w)
.

E(e)=0

Operational Semantics of
\mathsf{PRE}

In this section, we recall the formal definition of
\mathsf{GPTS}

The definition of
\mathsf{GPTS}

Subdistributions—definitions and operations

A function
\nu:X\to[0,1]
is called a
subprobability distribution
or
subdistribution

\sum_{x\in X}\nu(x)\leq 1
. A subdistribution
\nu
is

if the set
\operatorname{supp}(\nu)=\{x\in X\mid\nu(x)>0\}
is finite. We use
\mathcal{D}{X}
to denote the set of finitely supported subprobability distributions on
X

Given
x\in X

\delta_{x}
which is given by
\delta_{x}(y)=1
only if
x=y

0
otherwise. We will moreover write

for a subdistribution with an empty support. It is defined as
0(x)=0
for all
x\in X
. When
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}:X\to[0,1]
are subprobability distributions and

p\nu_{1}+(1-p)\nu_{2}
for the convex combination of
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}

(p\nu_{1}+(1-p)\nu_{2})(x)=p\nu_{1}(x)+(1-p)\nu_{2}(x)

Given a map

4.
Roadmap to (Sound+Complete)ness

We want to show that the axioms in
Figure

1
are sound and complete to reason about probabilistic language equivalence of
\mathsf{PRE}
, that is:

e\equiv f\quad\begin{array}[]{c}{\text{\tiny Completeness}}\\
\Longleftarrow\\
\Longrightarrow\\
{\text{\tiny Soundness}}\end{array}\llbracket e\rrbracket=\llbracket f\rrbracket

We now sketch the roadmap on how we will prove these two results to ease the flow into the upcoming technical sections. Perhaps not surprisingly, the completeness direction is the most involved.

High-level overview.

The heart of both arguments will rely on arguing that the semantics map
\llbracket-\rrbracket:\mathsf{Exp}\to[0,1]^{A^{*}}
assigning a probabilistic language to each expression can be seen as the following composition of maps:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{Exp}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[0,1]^{A^{*}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces[-]}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dagger d}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket-\rrbracket}

In the picture above
[-]\colon\mathsf{Exp}\to{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
is a quotient map taking expressions to their equivalence class modulo the axioms of
\equiv
, while
\dagger d:{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}\to[0,1]^{A^{*}}
is a map taking each equivalence class to the corresponding probabilistic language. In such a case, soundness follows as a sequence of three steps:

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}e&\equiv f\\
\Rightarrow[e]&=[f]\\
\Rightarrow\dagger d([e])&=\dagger d([f])\\
\Rightarrow\llbracket e\rrbracket&=\llbracket f\rrbracket\end{split}

To obtain completeness we want to reverse all implications in
Equation

2
–and they all are easily reversible except
[e]=[f]\Rightarrow\dagger d([e])=\dagger d([f])
. To obtain this reverse implication we will need to show that
\dagger d
is
injective
.

Language semantics via determinisation.

Both arguments outlined above will crucially rely on the transition system structure on the set of expressions provided by the Antimirov construction and hence it will be convenient to study it through the theory of
universal coalgebra

(
Rutten 2000
;
Gumm 2000
)
. In general, coalgebras provide a uniform and abstract treatment of transition systems and their semantics. The theory allows us to derive the notion of coalgebra
homomorphisms
, which are transition-preserving maps between transition systems. Under mild conditions, one can also derive the notion of a
final coalgebra
, which provides a form of canonical domain allowing to assign meaning to states of transition systems, through unique coalgebra homomorphisms. Much of the theory can be illustrated through an example of deterministic automata, where the final coalgebra is given by the automaton structure over the set of all formal languages, namely
2^{A^{*}}
. In such a case, the unique final homomorphism is given by the language-assigning map taking each state of an automaton to its language.

Similarly to deterministic automata,
\mathsf{GPTS}
can be seen as coalgebras, which also admit a final coalgebra. Unfortunately, in this case, the final coalgebra is
not
carried by the set of probabilistic languages, that is
[0,1]^{A^{*}}
, because the canonical semantics of
\mathsf{GPTS}
happens to correspond to the more restrictive notion of probabilistic bisimilarity (also known as Larsen-Skou bisimilarity
(
Larsen and Skou 1991
)
). Probabilistic bisimilarity is a branching-time notion of equivalence, requiring observable behaviour of compared states to be equivalent at every step, while probabilistic language equivalence is a more liberal notion comparing sequences of observable behaviour. In general, if two states are bisimilar, then they are language equivalent, but the converse does not hold.

A similar situation happens when looking at nondeterministic automata through the lenses of universal coalgebra, where again the canonical notion of equivalence is the one of bisimilarity. A known remedy is the powerset construction from classic automata theory, which converts a nondeterministic automaton to a deterministic automaton, whose states are sets of states of the original nondeterministic automaton we have started from. Roadmap to Soundness.

The proof of soundness will crucially rely on viewing the language semantics of
\mathsf{PRE}
through generalised determinisation construction. Thanks to that point of view, the language
\llbracket e\rrbracket\in[0,1]^{A^{*}}
of an arbitrary expression
e\in\mathsf{Exp}
, can be alternatively seen as the language of the state
\delta_{e}
in the determinisation of the Antimirov transition system (after being seen as a more general
\mathsf{RPTS}
). We will show that the quotient
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
can be equipped with a structure of deterministic transition system, such that there is language preserving map (deterministic transition system homomorphism)
h:\mathcal{D}{\mathsf{Exp}}\to{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
from the determinisation of the Antimirov
\mathsf{GPTS}
, additionally satisfying that
h(\delta_{e})=[e]\in{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
. This will allow us to conclude that the language of the state
\delta_{e}
in the determinisation of the Antimirov
\mathsf{GPTS}
is the same as the one of the state
[e]
in the deterministic transition structure on
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
, thus establishing soundness. Equivalently, if we write
\dagger d:{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}\to[0,1]^{A^{*}}
for the unique map taking states of that deterministic transition system to corresponding probabilistic languages, then this will allow us to prove that
\llbracket-\rrbracket=\dagger d\circ[-]
, as outlined at the beginning of this section.

As much as our proof of soundness is not a straightforward inductive argument like in ordinary regular expressions, it immediately sets up the stage for the completeness argument. Essentially, we will need to show that
\dagger d
is injective, by showing that the deterministic transition system structure on
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
satisfies a certain universal property. In general, obtaining the appropriate transition system structure on
\mathsf{Exp}/_{\equiv}
needs a couple of intermediate steps, which then lead to soundness:

(1)

We first prove the soundness of a subset of the axioms of
Figure

1
: omitting
S0
and
D2
yields a sound inference system, which we call
\equiv_{0}
, wrt a finer equivalence–probabilistic bisimilarity as defined by Larsen and Skou
(
Larsen and Skou 1991
)
(
Lemma

6.1
). As a consequence, there exists a deterministic transition system structure on the set
\mathcal{D}{{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv_{0}}}
, such that
\mathcal{D}[-]_{\equiv_{0}}:\mathcal{D}\mathsf{Exp}\to\mathcal{D}{{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv_{0}}}
is a transition system homomorphism.

(2)

We then prove that the set of expressions modulo the bisimilarity axioms, that is
\mathsf{Exp}/_{\equiv_{0}}
, has the structure of a positive convex algebra
\alpha_{\equiv_{0}}:\mathcal{D}{{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv_{0}}}\to{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv_{0}}
. This allows us to flatten a distribution over equivalence classes into a single equivalence class. This proof makes use of a
fundamental theorem
decomposing expressions (
Theorem

6.2
).

(3)

With the above result, we equip the set
\mathsf{Exp}/{\equiv_{0}}
with a structure of deterministic transition system and show that the positive convex algebra structure map is also a transition system homomorphism from
\mathcal{D}{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv_{0}}
.

(4)

Through a simple inductive argument (this step encapsulates the key part of the soundness argument), we show that there exists a unique deterministic transition system structure on the coarser quotient, that is
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
, that makes further identification using axioms
S0
and
D2
(denoted
[-]_{\equiv}:{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv_{0}}\to{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
) into a homomorphism. We compose all homomorphisms into
h:\mathcal{D}{\mathsf{Exp}}\to{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
and show the correspondence of the probabilistic language of the state
[e]
in the above transition system with the one of
\delta_{e}
in the determinisation of Antimirov
\mathsf{GPTS}
, thus establishing soundness.

Roadmap to Completeness.

As mentioned above, we will show that the deterministic transition system on the quotient
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
possesses a certain universal property allowing us to conclude that the unique language-assigning homomorphism
\dagger d:{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}\to[0,1]^{A^{*}}
is injective. The universal property we care about is the one of
rational fixpoint

(
Milius 2010
)
, which is a coalgebraic generalisation of the concept of regular languages providing semantics to finite-state deterministic automata. Unfortunately, as we will see in
Section

7
, determinising a finite-state
\mathsf{GPTS}
can lead to an infinite state deterministic transition system. However, each determinisation of a finite-state
\mathsf{GPTS}
carries a structure of a positive convex algebra, that is
free finitely generated
. Thanks to the work of Milius
(
Milius 2018
)
and Sokolova & Woracek
(
Sokolova and Woracek 2018
)
, we will see that establishing that
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
is isomorphic to the rational fixpoint boils down to showing uniqueness of homomorphisms from determinisations of finite-state
\mathsf{GPTS}
. We will reduce this problem to converting
\mathsf{GPTS}
to language equivalent expressions through the means of axiomatic manipulation using a procedure reminiscent of Brzozowski’s equation solving method
(
Brzozowski 1964
)
for converting DFAs to regular expressions. As a corollary, we will obtain an analogue of (one direction of) Kleene’s theorem for
\mathsf{GPTS}
and
\mathsf{PRE}
. To sum up, the completeness result is obtained in
4
steps:

(1)

We show that the deterministic transition system on the quotient
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
has an additional algebraic structure, which allows us to rely on the theory of the rational fixpoint.

(2)

Following a traditional pattern in completeness proofs
(
Salomaa 1966
;
Backhouse 1976
;
Milner 1984
)
, we represent
\mathsf{GPTS}
as
left-affine
systems of equations within the calculus and show that these systems have
unique
solutions up to provable equivalence (
Theorem

7.6
).

(3)

We then show that these solutions are in 1-1 correspondence with well-behaved maps from algebraically structured transition systems obtained from finite-state
\mathsf{GPTS}
into the deterministic transition system on
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
(
Lemma

7.5
).

(4)

Finally, we use this correspondence together with an abstract categorical argument to show that a transition system structure on
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
has a universal property (
Corollary

7.11
) that implies injectivity of
\llbracket-\rrbracket
.

This section contains a number of technical definitions and results that we need to execute the roadmap to soundness described above, crucially relying on the theory of

. We assume the familiarity of the reader with the basic notions of category theory, such as endofunctors, monads, and natural transformations; for a detailed introduction, we refer to
(

)
. Below, we use the fact that subdistributions
\mathcal{D}
are in fact a functor on the category
{\mathsf{Set}}
of sets and functions:
\mathcal{D}
maps each set
X
to the set to
\mathcal{D}X
and maps each arrow
f:X\to Y
to the function
\mathcal{D}f:\mathcal{D}X\to\mathcal{D}Y
given by

\mathcal{D}f(\nu)(x)=\sum_{y\in f^{-1}(x)}\nu(y)

Moreover,
\mathcal{D}
also carries a monad structure with unit
\eta_{X}(x)=\delta_{x}
and multiplication given by
\mu_{X}(\Phi)(x)=\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{D}X}\Phi(\varphi)\varphi(x)
for
\Phi\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{D}X
.

Coalgebras—definitions and notation

A coalgebra for an endofunctor
F:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}
is a pair
(X,\beta)
, where
X
is an object in the category
\mathscr{A}
representing the state space of the coalgebra and
\beta:X\to FX
is a morphism representing the transition structure. A map
f:X\to Y
is a homomorphisms between coalgebras
(X,\beta)
and
(Y,\gamma)
if
\gamma\circ f=Ff\circ\beta
. We use
\mathop{\mathsf{Coalg}}{F}
to denote the category of
F
-coalgebras and their homomorphisms.

An
F
-coalgebra
(\nu F,t)
is final if for any
F
-coalgebra
(X,\beta)
there exists a unique homomorphism
(X,\beta)\to(\nu F,t)
. We write
\dagger c:X\to\nu F
for the unique homomorphism into the final coalgebra (if it exists). Sometimes, we will abuse the notation and write
\nu F
instead of
(\nu F,t)
.

\mathsf{GPTS}
can be viewed as coalgebras for the composite functor
\mathcal{D}F:{\mathsf{Set}}\to{\mathsf{Set}}
, where
F={1}+(-)\times A
and
\mathsf{RPTS}
can be modelled as
G\mathcal{D}
-coalgebras, where
G:{\mathsf{Set}}\to{\mathsf{Set}}
is the functor given by
G=[0,1]\times(-)^{A}
. The functor
G
has a final coalgebra with carrier set
[0,1]^{A^{*}}
– this characterises probabilistic languages as a canonical object and every
G
-coalgebra can be mapped into this final one (that is, every state in a
G
-coalgebra can be assigned a probabilistic language in a unique way).
\mathsf{RPTS}
are
G\mathcal{D}
-coalgebras and in order to exploit the finality of
G
we need to describe a transformation that maps
G\mathcal{D}
-coalgebras to
G
-coalgebras (with a richer state space in a category of algebras for a monad)–this will be an instance of a
generalised determinisation
construction
(
Silva et al. 2010
)
, which we describe below.

Algebras for a monad—definitions and notation

Let
(T,\eta^{T},\mu^{T})
be a monad on
{\mathsf{Set}}
. The category of Eilenberg-Moore algebras for
T
, denoted
{\mathsf{Set}}^{T}
, consists of
T
-algebras
(X,\alpha:TX\to X)
such that
\alpha\circ\eta^{T}_{X}=\mathsf{id}_{X}
and
\alpha\circ T\alpha=\alpha\circ\mu^{T}_{X}
and their homomorphisms. A
homomorphism between two Eilenberg-Moore algebras
(X,\alpha)
and
(Y,\beta)
is a map
f:X\to Y
satisfying
\beta\circ Tf=f\circ\alpha
.

For every set
X
, we call the algebra
(TX,\mu^{T}_{X})

free
. If
X
is finite, we call
(TX,\mu^{T}_{X})

free finitely generated
. Free algebras satisfy the universal property that given any map
f:X\to Y
, such that
(Y,\beta)
is an Eilenberg-Moore algebra, there is a unique
T
-algebra homomorphism
f^{\star}:(TX,\mu^{T}_{X})\to(Y,\beta)
satisfying
f=f^{\star}\circ\eta^{T}_{X}
.

For concrete monads, Eilenberg-Moore categories have familiar algebraic descriptions: for instance, for the powerset monad
\mathcal{P}
it is isomorphic to join semilattices and for the
\mathcal{D}
monad it is isomorphic to positive convex algebras.

Positive Convex Algebras—definitions and notation

A
positive convex algebra
is an algebra with an abstract convex sum operation
\bigboxplus_{i\in I}p_{i}\cdot(-)_{i}
for
I
finite,
p_{i}\in[0,1]
and
\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\leq 1
satisfying:

(1)

(Projection)
\bigboxplus_{i\in I}p_{i}\cdot x_{i}=x_{j}
if
p_{j}=1

(2)

(Barycenter)
\bigboxplus_{i\in I}p_{i}\cdot\left(\bigboxplus_{j\in J}{q_{i,j}}\cdot{x_{j}}\right)=\bigboxplus_{j\in J}\left(\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}q_{i,j}\right)\cdot x_{j}

Positive convex algebras and their homomorphisms form a category
{\mathsf{PCA}}
. This category is isomorphic to
{\mathsf{Set}}^{\mathcal{D}}

(
Jacobs 2010
;
Doberkat 2008
)
. In terms of notation, we denote the unary sum by
p_{0}\cdot x_{0}
. Throughout the paper we will we abuse the notation by writing
\bigboxplus_{i\in I}p_{i}\cdot e_{i}\boxplus\bigboxplus_{i\in J}q_{j}\cdot f_{j}
for a single convex sum
\bigboxplus_{k\in I+J}r_{k}\cdot g_{k}
, where
r_{k}=p_{k}
and
g_{k}=e_{k}
for
k\in I
and similarly
r_{k}=q_{k}
and
g_{k}=f_{k}
for
k\in J
. This is well-defined only if
\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}+\sum_{j\in J}r_{j}\leq 1
.

Generalised determinisation.
Language acceptance of nondeterministic automata (
\mathsf{NDA}
) can be captured via determinisation.
\mathsf{NDA}
can be viewed as coalgebras for the functor
N=2\times{\mathcal{P}_{f}(-)}^{A}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{f}
is the finite powerset monad. Determinisation converts a
\mathsf{NDA}

(X,\beta:X\to 2\times{\mathcal{P}_{f}X}^{A})
into a deterministic automaton
({\mathcal{P}_{f}X},\beta^{\star}:{\mathcal{P}_{f}X}\to 2\times{\mathcal{P}_{f}X}^{A})
, where
\beta^{\star}
is the unique extension of the map
\beta
. A language of the state
x\in X
of
\mathsf{NDA}
, is given by the language accepted by the state
\{x\}
in the determinised automaton
({\mathcal{P}_{f}X},\beta^{\star})
.

This construction can be generalised
(
Silva et al. 2010
)
to
HT
-coalgebras, where
T:{\mathsf{Set}}\to{\mathsf{Set}}
is a finitary monad and
H:{\mathsf{Set}}\to{\mathsf{Set}}
an endofunctor that admits a final coalgebra that can be lifted to the functor
\overline{H}:{\mathsf{Set}}^{T}\to{\mathsf{Set}}^{T}
. Generalised determinisation turns
HT
-coalgebras
(X,\beta:X\to HTX)
into
H
-coalgebras
(TX,\beta^{\star}:TX\to HTX)
. The language of a state
x\in X
is given by
\dagger\beta^{\star}\circ\eta^{T}_{X}:X\to\nu H
, where
\eta^{T}
is the unit of the monad
T
. Each determinisation
(TX,\beta^{\star})
can be viewed as an
\overline{H}
-coalgebra
((TX,\mu^{T}_{X}),\beta^{\star})
. The carrier of the final
H
-coalgebra can be canonically equipped with
T
-algebra structure, yielding the final
\overline{H}
-coalgebra. In such a case, the unique final homomorphism from any determinisation viewed as an
H
-coalgebra is also the final
\overline{H}
-coalgebra homomorphism.

\mathsf{RPTS}
fit into the framework of generalised determinisation, as there exists a natural transformation
\lambda:\mathcal{D}G\to G\mathcal{D}
that allows to lift
G
to an endofunctor on
{\mathsf{PCA}}

(
Silva and Sokolova 2011
)
. We will abuse notation and write
G:{\mathsf{PCA}}\to{\mathsf{PCA}}
for that lifting. Because of that, we make no notational distinction between the final coalgebras (and thus the final homomorphisms) of
G:{\mathsf{Set}}\to{\mathsf{Set}}
and
G:{\mathsf{PCA}}\to{\mathsf{PCA}}
.

Language semantics of
\mathsf{GPTS}
as a map into a final coalgebra.

Every
\mathsf{GPTS}
can be transformed into a
\mathsf{RPTS}
: in particular, there exists a natural transformation
\gamma:\mathcal{D}F\to G\mathcal{D}
with monomorphic components
(
Silva and Sokolova 2011
)
given by:

\gamma_{X}(\zeta)=\langle\zeta(\checkmark),\lambda a.\lambda x.\zeta(a,x)\rangle\qquad\qquad\qquad(\zeta\in\mathcal{D}FX)

Using that one can convert each
\mathsf{GPTS}

(X,\beta)
into the corresponding
\mathsf{RPTS}

(X,\gamma_{X}\circ\beta)
. It is worth pointing out, that not every reactive system can be obtained in that way. For example, any
\mathsf{RPTS}
having a state accepting with probability one and at least one letter for which the successor distribution has nonempty support would require the source
\mathsf{GPTS}
to have the total probability mass greater than one.

We are now ready to state the full construction of the probabilistic language semantics of
\mathsf{GPTS}
. Let
(X,\beta:X\to\mathcal{D}FX)
be a
\mathsf{GPTS}
. We can convert it into a reactive system
(X,\gamma_{X}\circ\beta)
and then determinise it into a
G
-coalgebra
(\mathcal{D}X,(\gamma_{X}\circ\beta)^{\star})
. We will abuse the terminology and sometimes call that coalgebra a determinisation of
\mathsf{GPTS}

(X,\beta)
. The language semantics given by generalised determinisation yields a map
\dagger(\gamma_{X}\circ\beta)^{\star}\circ\eta_{X}:X\to{A^{*}}\to[0,1]
. This map coincides with the explicit definition of
\llbracket-\rrbracket
we gave in
Equation

1
(this is a consequence of a result in
(
Silva and Sokolova 2011
)
).
Throughout this paper, we will be mainly interested in the probabilistic language semantics of one particular
\mathsf{GPTS}
: the Antimirov transition system, which is used to give operational semantics of
\mathsf{PRE}
.

Step 1: Algebra structure.

also carries the algebra structure. In such a setting, the transition function

Interlude: coalgebraic completeness theorems.

The completeness proof will follow a pattern of earlier work of Jacobs
(
Jacobs 2006
)
, Silva
(
Silva 2010
)
and Milius
(
Milius 2010
)
and show that the coalgebra structure on the
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
is isomorphic to the subcoalgebra of an appropriate final coalgebra, ie. the unique final coalgebra homomorphism from
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
is injective. The intuition comes from the coalgebraic modelling of deterministic automata studied in the work of Jacobs
(
Jacobs 2006
)
. In such a case, the final coalgebra is simply the automaton structure on the set of all formal languages, while the final homomorphism is given by the map taking a state of the automaton to a language it denotes. Restricting the attention to finite-state automata only yields
regular languages
. The set of regular languages can be equipped with an automaton structure, in a way that inclusion map into the final automaton on the set of all formal languages becomes a homomorphism. In such a case, Kozen’s completeness proof of Kleene Algebra
(
Kozen 1994
)
can be seen as showing isomorphism of the automaton of regular languages and the automaton structure on the regular expressions modulo
\mathsf{KA}
axioms.

Unfortunately, we cannot immediately rely on the identical pattern. Our semantics relies on determinising
\mathsf{GPTS}
, but unfortunately, determinising a finite-state
\mathsf{GPTS}
can yield
G
-coalgebras with infinite carriers. For example, determinising a single-state
\mathsf{GPTS}
would yield a
G
-coalgebra over the set of subdistributions over a singleton set, which is infinite. Luckily, all
G
-coalgebras we work with have additional algebraic structure. This algebraic structure will allow us to rely on the generalisations of the concept of finiteness beyond the category of sets, offered by the theory of locally finite presentable categories
(
Adamek and Rosicky 1994
)
. Being equipped with those abstract lenses, one can immediately see that the earlier mentioned infinite set of all subdistributions over a singleton set is also a free
{\mathsf{PCA}}
generated by a single element and thus finitely presentable
(
Sokolova and Woracek 2015
)
.

In particular, we will work with a
rational fixpoint
; a generalisation of the idea of subcoalgebra of regular languages to coalgebras for finitary functors
B:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}
over locally finitely presentable categories. The rational fixpoint provides a semantical domain for the behaviour of coalgebras whose carriers are finitely presentable in the same way as regular languages provide a semantic domain for all finite-state deterministic automata. The completeness proof will essentially rely on establishing that the coalgebra structure on
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
satisfies the universal property of the rational fixpoint.

We start by recalling basic notions of locally finite presentable categories
(
Adamek and Rosicky 1994
)
and properties of the rational fixpoint.

Lfp categories and the rational fixpoint—definitions and notation

\mathscr{D}
is a filtered category, if every finite subcategory
\mathscr{D}_{0}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{D}
has a cocone in
\mathscr{D}
. A filtered colimit is a colimit of the diagram
\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{C}
, where
\mathscr{D}
is a filtered category. A directed colimit is a colimit of the diagram
\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{C}
, where
\mathscr{D}
is a directed poset. We call a functor
finitary
if it preserves filtered colimits. An object
C
is
finitely presentable (fp)
if the representable functor
\mathscr{C}(C,-):\mathscr{C}\to{\mathsf{Set}}
preserves filtered colimits. Similarly, an object
C
is
finitely generated (fg)
if the representable functor
\mathscr{C}(C,-):\mathscr{C}\to{\mathsf{Set}}
preserves directed colimits of monos. Every fp object is fg, but the converse does not hold in general. A category
\mathscr{C}
is
locally finitely presentable (lfp)
if it is cocomplete and there exists a set of finitely presentable objects, such that every object of
\mathscr{C}
is a filtered colimit of objects from that set.
{\mathsf{Set}}
is the prototypical example of a locally finitely presentable category, where finite sets are the fp objects.

Let
B:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}
be a finitary functor. We write
\mathop{\mathsf{Coalg}_{\mathsf{fp}}}{B}
for the subcategory of
\mathop{\mathsf{Coalg}}{B}
consisting only of
B
-coalgebras with finitely presentable carrier. The
rational fixpoint
is defined as
(\varrho B,r)=\mathop{\mathrm{colim}}(\mathop{\mathsf{Coalg}_{\mathsf{fp}}}{B}\hookrightarrow\mathop{\mathsf{Coalg}}{B})
– a colimit of the inclusion functor from the subcategory of coalgebras with finitely presentable carriers. We call it a fixpoint, as the map
r:\varrho B\to B(\varrho B)
is an isomorphism
(
Adámek et al. 2006
)
.
Following
(
Milius et al. 2020
, Corollary 3.10, Theorem 3.12)
, if finitely presentable and finitely generated objects coincide in
\mathscr{A}
and
B:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}
is a finitary endofunctor preserving non-empty monomorphisms, then rational fixpoint is fully abstract - ie.
(\varrho B,r)
is a subcoalgebra of
(\nu B,t)
.

Proper functors.
It can be easily noticed that the generalised determinisation of coalgebras with finite carriers corresponds to algebraically structured coalgebras of a particular, well-behaved kind. Namely, their carriers are algebras which are free finitely generated. We write
\mathop{\mathsf{Coalg}_{\mathsf{free}}}{B}
for the subcategory of
\mathop{\mathsf{Coalg}}{B}
consisting only of
B
-coalgebras with free finitely generated carriers. The recent work of Milius
(
Milius 2018
)
characterised
proper
functors, for which in order to establish that some
B
-coalgebra is isomorphic to the rational fixpoint it will suffice to look at coalgebras with free finitely generated carriers. To put that formally:

Theorem 7.3 (
(
Milius 2018
, Corollary 5.9)
).

Let
B:{\mathsf{Set}}^{T}\to{\mathsf{Set}}^{T}
be a proper functor. Then a
B
-coalgebra
(R,r)
is isomorphic to the rational fixpoint if
(R,r)
is locally finitely presentable and for every
B
-coalgebra
(TX,c)
in
\mathop{\mathsf{Coalg}_{\mathsf{free}}}{B}
there exists a unique homomorphism from
TX
to
R
.

As much as
G:{\mathsf{PCA}}\to{\mathsf{PCA}}
is known to be proper
(
Sokolova and Woracek 2018
)
, not every
G
-coalgebra with a free finitely generated carrier corresponds to a determinisation of some
\mathsf{GPTS}
. This is simply too general, as some
G
-coalgebras with free finitely generated carriers might be determinisations of
\mathsf{RPTS}
not corresponding to any
\mathsf{GPTS}
. To circumvent that, instead of looking at all coalgebras for the functor
G:{\mathsf{PCA}}\to{\mathsf{PCA}}
, we can restrict our attention in a way that will exclude determinisations of
\mathsf{RPTS}
not corresponding to any
\mathsf{GPTS}
. To do so, define a functor
\hat{G}:{\mathsf{PCA}}\to{\mathsf{PCA}}
. Given a positive convex algebra
(X,\boxplus)
we set:

\displaystyle\hat{G}(X,\boxplus)=

\displaystyle\{(o,f)\in[0,1]\times X^{A}\mid\forall{a\in A}~\exists{p_{a}^{i}\in[0,1],x_{a}^{i}\in X}.

\displaystyle f(a)=\bigboxplus_{i\in I}p_{a}^{i}x_{a}^{i}\text{and}\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{i\in I}p_{a}^{i}\leq 1-o\}

The
{\mathsf{PCA}}
structure on
\hat{G}(X,\boxplus)
, as well as the action of
\hat{G}
on arrows is defined to be the same as in the case of
G
.
It can be immediately observed that
\hat{G}
is a subfunctor of
G
. Whenever the algebra structure is clear from the context, we write
\hat{G}X
for
\hat{G}(X,\boxplus)
. Most importantly for us, thanks to the result of Sokolova and Woracek, we know that
\hat{G}
is also proper
(
Sokolova and Woracek 2018
)
. We can now see the following correspondence.

Lemma 7.4.

\mathcal{D}F
-coalgebras with finite carrier are in one-to-one correspondence with
\hat{G}
-coalgebras with free finitely generated carrier.

\frac{\beta:X\to\mathcal{D}FX\text{ on }{\mathsf{Set}}}{\xi:(\mathcal{D}X,\mu_{X})\to\hat{G}(\mathcal{D}X,\mu_{X})\text{ on }{\mathsf{PCA}}}

In other words, every coalgebra structure map
\xi:(\mathcal{D}X,\mu_{X})\to\hat{G}(\mathcal{D}X,\mu_{X})
is given by
\xi=(\gamma_{X}\circ\beta)^{\star}
for some unique
\beta:X\to\mathcal{D}FX
.

Additionally, the coalgebra structure on
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
, which is at the centre of attention of the completeness proof, happens also to be a
\hat{G}
-coalgebra.

Lemma 7.5.

(({\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv},\alpha_{\equiv}),d)
is a
\hat{G}
-coalgebra.

Step 2: Systems of equations.

In order to establish that
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
is isomorphic to the rational fixpoint (which is the property that will eventually imply completeness), we establish the conditions of
Theorem

7.3
. One of the required things we need to show is that the determinisation of an arbitrary finite-state GPTS admits a unique homomorphism to the coalgebra carried by
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
. In other words, we need to convert states of an arbitrary finite-state GPTS to language equivalent expressions in a way which is unique up to the axioms of
\equiv
. This can be thought of as an abstract reformulation of one direction of the Kleene theorem to the case of PRE and GPTS. To make that possible, we give a construction inspired by Brzozowski’s equation solving method
(
Brzozowski 1964
)
of converting a DFA to the corresponding regular expression. We start by stating the necessary definitions:

Systems of equations—definitions and notation

A
left-affine system
on finite set
Q
of unknowns is a quadruple

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\langle

\displaystyle M:Q\times Q\to\mathsf{Exp},p:Q\times Q\to[0,1],

\displaystyle b:Q\to\mathsf{Exp},r:Q\to[0,1]\rangle

such that for all
q,q^{\prime}\in Q
,
\sum_{q^{\prime}\in Q}p_{q,q^{\prime}}+r_{q}=1
and
E(M_{q,q^{\prime}})=0
.

Let
{\sim}\subseteq{\mathsf{Exp}\times\mathsf{Exp}}
be a congruence relation. A map
h:Q\to\mathsf{Exp}
is
\sim
-solution if for all
q\in Q
we have that:

h(q)\sim\left(\bigoplus_{q^{\prime}\in Q}p_{q,q^{\prime}}\cdot M_{q,q^{\prime}}\mathbin{;}h(q^{\prime})\right)\oplus r_{q}\cdot b_{q}

A system representing the finite-state
\mathsf{GPTS}

(X,\beta)
is given by
\mathcal{S}(\beta)=\langle M^{\beta},p^{\beta},b^{\beta},r^{\beta}\rangle
where for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
we have that:

\displaystyle p^{\beta}_{x,x^{\prime}}=\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A}\beta(x)(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})

\displaystyle M^{\beta}_{x,x^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\bigoplus_{a\in A}\frac{\beta(x)(a,x^{\prime})}{p^{\beta}_{x,x^{\prime}}}\cdot a&\text{if }p^{\beta}_{x,x^{\prime}}\neq 0\\
{\mathsf{0}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle r^{\beta}_{x}=1-\sum_{(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})\in\operatorname{supp}(\beta(x))}\beta(x)(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})

\displaystyle b^{\beta}_{x}=\begin{cases}\frac{\beta(x)(\checkmark)}{r^{\beta}_{x}}\cdot{\mathsf{1}}&\text{if }r^{\beta}_{x}\neq 0\\
{\mathsf{0}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Note that for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
we have that
E\left(M^{\beta}_{x,x^{\prime}}\right)=0
. This property will be particularly useful when solving the systems up to
\equiv
using the
Unique
fixpoint axiom.

Unique solution of systems.
The original Brzozowski’s equation-solving method is purely semantic, as it crucially relies on Arden’s rule by providing solutions up to the language equivalence to the systems of equations. As much as it would be enough for an analogue of the one direction of Kleene’s theorem, for the purposes of our completeness argument, we need to argue something stronger. Namely, we show that we can uniquely solve each system purely through the means of syntactic manipulation using the axioms of
\equiv
. This is where the main complexity of the completeness proof is located. We show this property, by re-adapting the key result of Salomaa
(
Salomaa 1966
)
to the systems of equations of our interest.

Theorem 7.6.

Every left-affine system of equations admits a unique
\equiv
-solution.

The proof of the result above proceeds by induction on the size of the systems of equations. We first show that systems with only one unknown can be solved via the
Unique
fixpoint axiom. Systems of the size
n+1
can be reduced to systems of size
n
, by solving for one of the unknowns and substituting the obtained equation with
n
unknowns to the remaining
n
equations. We note that this reduction step is highly reliant on axioms
D2
and
S0
.

Example 7.7.

Consider the transition system from the left-hand side of
Example

2.2
. A map
h:\{q_{0},q_{1}\}\to\mathsf{Exp}
is an
\equiv
-solution to the system associated with that transition system, if and only if:

h(q_{0})\equiv a\mathbin{;}h(q_{1})\qquad h(q_{1})\equiv a\mathbin{;}h(q_{1})\oplus_{\frac{1}{4}}{\mathsf{1}}

Since
E(a)=0
, we can apply the unique fixpoint axiom and
e\mathbin{;}{\mathsf{1}}\equiv e
to the equation on the right to deduce that

h(q_{1})\equiv a^{\left[\frac{1}{4}\right]}
. Substituting it into the left equation yields
h(q_{0})\equiv a\mathbin{;}a^{\left[\frac{1}{4}\right]}
.

Step 3: Correspondence of solutions and homomorphisms.
We are not done yet, as in the last step we only proved properties of systems of equations and their solutions, while our main interest is in appropriate
\hat{G}
-coalgebras and their homomorphisms. As desired, it turns out that
\equiv
-solutions are in one-to-one correspondence with
\hat{G}
-coalgebra homomorphisms from determinisations of finite state
\mathsf{GPTS}
to the coalgebra structure on
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
.

Lemma 7.8.

For a finite set
X
, we have the following one-to-one correspondence:

\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 85.90334pt\hskip-85.90334pt\hbox{$\hbox{\hskip 85.90334pt\hskip-85.90334pt\hbox{$\hbox{\hskip 85.90334pt\hskip-85.90334pt\hbox{$\begin{gathered}\text{$\hat{G}$-coalgebra homomorphisms }\\
m:((\mathcal{D}X,\mu_{X}),(\gamma_{X}\circ\beta)^{\star})\to(({\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv},\alpha_{\equiv}),d)\end{gathered}$}\hskip-85.90334pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{15.0833pt}{\hbox{$\rule{171.80667pt}{0.2pt}$}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 85.90334pt}$}\hskip-85.90334pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{14.2222pt}{\hbox{$\rule{171.80667pt}{0.2pt}$}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 85.90334pt}$}\hskip-85.90334pt\hskip-83.80167pt\raisebox{23.5833pt}{\hbox{$\begin{gathered}\text{$\equiv$-solutions }h:X\to\mathsf{Exp}\text{ to a system }\mathcal{S}(\beta)\\
\text{ associated with $\mathcal{D}F$-coalgebra }(X,\beta)\end{gathered}$}}\hskip-83.80167pt\hskip 85.90334pt}}

Aside from the completeness argument, the above result also gives us an analogue of (one direction of) Kleene’s theorem for
\mathsf{GPTS}
as a corollary. The other direction, converting
\mathsf{PRE}
to finite-state
\mathsf{GPTS}
is given by the Antimirov construction, described in
Section

3
.

Corollary 7.9.

Let
(X,\beta)
be a finite-state
\mathcal{D}F
-coalgebra. For every state
x\in X
, there exists an expression
e_{x}\in\mathsf{Exp}
, such that the probabilistic language denoted by
x
is the same as
\llbracket e_{x}\rrbracket
.

In particular, if
\mathcal{S}(\beta)
is the system of equations associated with
(X,\beta)
and
h:X\to\mathsf{Exp}
is an
\equiv
-solution to it (which exists because of
Theorem

7.6
), then we set
e_{x}=h(x)
.

Step 4: Establish the universal property.

We are now ready to argue that the coalgebra structure on
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
is isomorphic to the rational fixpoint of the functor
\hat{G}
. Recall that by
Lemma

7.4
, every coalgebra in
\mathop{\mathsf{Coalg}_{\mathsf{free}}}{\hat{G}}
is a determinisation of some finite-state
\mathsf{GPTS}
. Combining it with the correspondence from
Lemma

7.8
and the fact that every left-affine system possesses a unique
\equiv
-solution (
Theorem

7.6
) we can conclude that any coalgebra in
\mathop{\mathsf{Coalg}_{\mathsf{free}}}{\hat{G}}
admits a unique
\hat{G}
-coalgebra homomorphism to
(({\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv},\alpha_{\equiv}),d)
. This establishes the first condition of
Theorem

7.3
. We are left with showing the remaining condition:

Lemma 7.10.

\hat{G}
-coalgebra
(({\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv},\alpha_{\equiv}),d)
is locally finitely presentable.

The proof of the above claim is available in the appendix of the full version of the paper. Essentially, we argue that starting from an arbitrary convex combination of finitely many expressions, there are finitely many expressions such that reachable states in the coalgebra structure on
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
can be seen as convex combinations of expressions from that set. Speaking more precisely, we show that every finitely generated subalgebra of
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
is contained in the subocalgebra of
{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}
with a finitely generated carrier. The proof makes use of the fact that every expression has finitely many Antimirov derivatives.
We can now combine the above results and conclude that:

Corollary 7.11.

(({\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv},\alpha_{\equiv}),d)
is isomorphic to the rational fixpoint for the functor
\hat{G}
.

Completeness.
At this point, all that remains is to make an additional observation that the universal property we have established implies completeness. Since
\hat{G}
preserves non-empty monomorphisms
(
Milius et al. 2020
)
and in
{\mathsf{PCA}}
finitely generated and finitely presentable objects coincide
(
Sokolova and Woracek 2015
)
the rational fixpoint is fully abstract, i.e. is a subcoalgebra of the final
\hat{G}
-coalgebra. Moreover, since
\hat{G}
is a subfunctor of
G
, the final
\hat{G}
-coalgebra is subcoalgebra of the final
G
-coalgebra. Combining those facts, allows to conclude that
\dagger d:{{\mathsf{Exp}}/{\equiv}}\to[0,1]^{A^{*}}
is injective. At this point, showing completeness becomes straightforward.

Theorem 7.12 (Completeness).

For all
e,f\in\mathsf{Exp}
, if
\llbracket e\rrbracket=\llbracket f\rrbracket
implies
e\equiv f

Proof.

Assume that
\llbracket e\rrbracket=\llbracket f\rrbracket
. Then, by
Lemma

6.5
we have that:

(\dagger d\circ[-])(e)=(\dagger d\circ[-])(f)

Since
\dagger d
is injective, we can cancel it on the left to obtain
[e]=[f]
, which is equivalent to
e\equiv f
.

∎

8.
Discussion

We introduced probabilistic regular expressions (
\mathsf{PRE}
), a probabilistic counterpart to Kleene’s regular expressions. As the main technical contribution, we presented a Salomaa-style inference system for reasoning about probabilistic language equivalence of expressions and proved it sound and complete. Additionally, we gave a two-way correspondence between languages denoted by
\mathsf{PRE}
and finite-state Generative Probabilistic Transition Systems. Our approach is coalgebraic and enabled us to reuse several recently proved results on fixpoints of functors and convex algebras. This abstract outlook guided the choice of the right formalisms and enabled us to isolate the key results we needed to prove to achieve completeness while at the same time reusing existing results and avoiding repeating complicated combinatorial proofs. The key technical lemma, on uniqueness of solutions to certain systems of equations, is a generalisation of automata-theoretic constructions from the 60s further exposing the bridge between our probabilistic generalisation and the classical deterministic counterpart.

Related work.
Probabilistic process algebras and their axiomatisations have been widely studied
(
Bandini and Segala 2001
;
Stark and Smolka 2000
;
Mislove et al. 2003
;
Bernardo 2022
)
with syntaxes featuring action prefixing and least fixed point operators instead of the regular operations of sequential composition and probabilistic loops. This line of research focussed on probabilistic bisimulation, while probabilistic language equivalence, which we focus on, stems from automata theory, e.g. the work of Rabin on probabilistic automata
(
Rabin 1963
)
. Language equivalence of Rabin automata has been studied from an algorithmic point of view
(
Kiefer et al. 2011
;
Kiefer et al. 2012
)
.

Stochastic Regular Expressions (
\mathsf{SRE}
)
(
Ross 2000
;
Beeh 2017
;
Getir et al. 2018
)
, which were one of the main inspirations for this paper, can also be used to specify probabilistic languages. The syntax of
\mathsf{SRE}
features probabilistic Kleene star and
n
-ary probabilistic choice, however, it does not include
{\mathsf{0}}
and
{\mathsf{1}}
. The primary context of that line of research was around genetic programming in probabilistic pattern matching, and the topic of axiomatisation was simply not tackled.

\mathsf{PRE}
can be thought of as a fragment of
\mathsf{ProbGKAT}

(
Różowski et al. 2023
)
, a probabilistic extension of a strictly deterministic fragment of Kleene Algebra with Tests, that was studied only under the finer notion of bisimulation equivalence. The completeness
\mathsf{ProbGKAT}
was obtained through a different approach to ours, as it relied on a powerful axiom scheme to solve systems of equations.

Our soundness result, as well as semantics via generalised determinisation, were inspired by the work of Silva and Sokolova
(
Silva and Sokolova 2011
)
, who introduced a two-sorted process calculus for reasoning about probabilistic language equivalence of
\mathsf{GPTS}
. Unlike
\mathsf{PRE}
, their language syntactically excludes the possibility of introducing recursion over terms which might immediately terminate. Moreover, contrary to our completeness argument, their result hinges on the subset of axioms being complete wrt bisimilarity, similarly to the complete axiomatisation of trace congruence of
\mathsf{LTS}
due to Rabinovich
(
Rabinovich 1993
)
. The use of coalgebra to model trace/language semantics is a well-studied topic
(
Jacobs et al. 2015
;
Rot et al. 2021
)
and other approaches besides generalised determinisation
(
Silva et al. 2010
;
Bonchi et al. 2017
)
included the use of Kleisli categories
(
Hasuo et al. 2007
)
and coalgebraic modal logic
(
Klin and Rot 2015
)
.
We build on the vast line of work on coalgebraic completeness theorems
(
Jacobs 2006
;
Silva 2010
;
Schmid et al. 2021b
;
Milius 2010
;
Bonsangue et al. 2013
)
, coalgebraic semantics of probabilistic systems
(
de Vink and Rutten 1999
;
Sokolova 2005
)
and fixpoints of the functors
(
Milius et al. 2020
;
Milius 2018
;
Sokolova and Woracek 2018
)
.

Future work.
A first natural direction is exploring whether one could obtain an
algebraic
axiomatisation of
\mathsf{PRE}
. Similarly to Salomaa’s system, our axiomatisation is unsound under substitution of letters by arbitrary expressions in the case of the termination operator used to give side condition to the unique fixpoint axiom. We are interested if one could give an alternative inference system in the style of Kozen’s axiomatisation
(
Kozen 1994
)
, in which the Kleene star is the least fixpoint wrt the natural order induced by the
+
operation and thus not requiring the side condition to introduce loops. In the case of
\mathsf{PRE}
, the challenge is that there is no obvious way of defining a natural order on
\mathsf{PRE}
in terms of
\oplus_{p}
operation.

Additionally, we would like to study if the finer relation
\equiv_{0}
is complete wrt probabilistic bisimilarity. One could view it as a probabilistic analogue of the problem of completeness of Kleene Algebra modulo bisimilarity posed by Milner
(
Milner 1984
)
, which was recently answered positively by Grabmayer
(
Grabmayer 2022
)
.

Finally, an interesting direction would be to study a more robust notion of
language distance

(
Baldan et al. 2018
)
of
\mathsf{GPTS}
by extending our axiomatisation to a system based on quantitative equational logic
(
Mardare et al. 2016
)
. Similar results were already obtained in the case of probabilistic process calculus of Stark and Smolka
(
Stark and Smolka 2000
)
for bisimulation distance
(
Bacci et al. 2018b
)
and distance between infinite traces
(
Bacci et al. 2018a
)
.

Appendix A
Antimirov derivatives are finite

Lemma A.1.

For all
e\in\mathsf{Exp}
, the set
\langle e\rangle
is finite. In fact, the number of of states is bounded above by
\#(-):\mathsf{Exp}\ \to\mathds{N}
, where
\#(-)
is defined recursively by:

\displaystyle\#({\mathsf{0}})=\#({\mathsf{1}})=1\quad\#(a)=2\quad\#(e\oplus_{p}f)=\#(e)+\#(f)

\displaystyle\#(e\mathbin{;}f)=\#(e)+\#(f)\quad\#(e^{[p]})=\#(e)+1

Proof.

We adapt the analogous proof for
\mathsf{GKAT}

(
Schmid et al. 2021a
)
.

For any
{e}\in\mathsf{Exp}
, let
|\langle e\rangle|
be the cardinality of the carrier set of the least subcoalgebra of
(\mathsf{Exp},\partial)
containing
e
. We show by induction that for all
e\in\mathsf{Exp}
it holds that
|\langle e\rangle|\leq\#({e})
.

For the base cases, observe that for
{\mathsf{0}}
and
{\mathsf{1}}
the subcoalgebra has exactly one state. Hence,
\#({\mathsf{0}})=1=|\langle{\mathsf{0}}\rangle|
. Similarly, we have
\#({\mathsf{1}})=1=|\langle{\mathsf{1}}\rangle|
.
For
a\in A
, we have two states; the initial state, which transitions with probability
1
on
a
to the state which outputs
\checkmark
with probability
1
.

For the inductive cases, assume that
|\langle e\rangle|\leq\#(e)
,
|\langle f\rangle|\leq\#(f)
and
p\in[0,1]
.

•

Every derivative of
{e}\oplus_{p}{f}
is either a derivative of
{e}
or
{f}
and hence
|\langle e\oplus_{p}f\rangle|\leq|\langle e\rangle|+|\langle f\rangle|=\#({e})+\#({f})=\#({e}\oplus_{p}{f})
.

•

In the case of
{e}\mathbin{;}{f}
, every derivative of this expression is either a derivative of
{f}
or some derivative of
{e}
followed by
{f}
. Hence,
|\langle e\mathbin{;}f\rangle|=|\langle e\rangle\times\{{f}\}|+|\langle f\rangle|\leq\#({e})+\#({f})=\#({e}\mathbin{;}{f})
.

•

For the probabilistic loop case, observe that every derivative of
{e}^{[{p}]}
is a derivative of
{e}
followed by
{e}^{[{p}]}
or it is the state that outputs
\checkmark
with probability
1
. It can be easily observed,that
|\langle e^{[p]}\rangle|\leq|\langle e\rangle|+1=\#({e})=\#({e}^{[p]})
.

∎

Appendix B
Positive convex algebras

Proposition B.1.

In any positive convex algebra, the following hold:

(1)

\bigboxplus_{i\in I}p_{i}\cdot x_{i}=\bigboxplus_{x\in\bigcup_{i\in I}\{x_{i}\}}\left(\sum_{x_{i}=x}p_{i}\right)\cdot x

(2)

be a congruence relation. Then,

{\bigboxplus_{i\in I}p_{i}\cdot x_{i}}\sim{\bigboxplus_{[x]_{\sim}\in\bigcup_{i\in I}\{[x_{i}]_{\sim}\}}\left(\sum_{x_{i}=x}p_{i}\right)\cdot x}

(3)

All terms

coincide and are all provably equivalent to the empty convex sum.

(4)

Let

be a finite index set, and let

and

be indexed collections. If

Proof.

In this lemma, we use

to denote Iverson bracket, which is

if

is true and
0
otherwise. For (1) we have that

(Projection axiom)

\displaystyle\bigboxplus_{i\in I}p_{i}\cdot x_{i}

\displaystyle=\bigboxplus_{x\in\cup_{i\in I}\{x_{i}\}}\left(\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}[x_{i}=x]\right)\cdot x

\displaystyle=\bigboxplus_{x\in\cup_{i\in I}\{x_{i}\}}\left(\sum_{x_{i}=x}p_{i}\right)\cdot x

(2) can be shown by picking a representative for each equivalence class and then using (1). For (3), by
