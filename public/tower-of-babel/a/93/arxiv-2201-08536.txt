Instance-Dependent Confidence and Early Stopping for Reinforcement Learning


Abstract
Various algorithms for reinforcement learning (RL) exhibit dramatic variation in their convergence rates as a function of problem structure. Such problem-dependent behavior is not captured by worst-case analyses and has accordingly inspired a growing effort in obtaining instance-dependent guarantees and deriving instance-optimal algorithms for RL problems. This research has been carried out, however, primarily within the confines of theory, providing guarantees that explain ex post the performance differences observed. A natural next step is to convert these theoretical guarantees into guidelines that are useful in practice. We address the problem of obtaining sharp instance-dependent confidence regions for the policy evaluation problem and the optimal value estimation problem of an MDP, given access to an instance-optimal algorithm. As a consequence, we propose a data-dependent stopping rule for instance-optimal algorithms. The proposed stopping rule adapts to the instance-specific difficulty of the problem and allows for early termination for problems with favorable structure.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) refers to a broad class of methods that
are focused on learning how to make (near)-optimal decisions in
dynamic environments. Although RL-based methods are now being
deployed in various application domains (e.g.,
[
26
,
11
,
21
]
), such deployments often lack a
secure theoretical foundation. Given that RL involves making
real-world decisions, often autonomously, the impact on humans can be
significant, and there is an urgent need to shore up the foundations,
providing practical and actionable guidelines for RL. A major part of
the challenge is that popular RL algorithms exhibit a variety of
behavior across domains and problem instances and existing methods and
associated guarantees, generally tailored to the worst-case setting,
fail to capture this variety. One way to move beyond worst-case
bounds is to develop guarantees that adapt to the problem difficulty,
helping to reveal what aspects of an RL problem make it an “easy” or
“hard” problem. Indeed, in recent years, such a research agenda has
begun to emerge and we have gained a refined understanding of the
instance-dependent nature of various reinforcement learning
problems
[
22
,
32
,
33
,
16
,
7
,
19
, e.g.,]
.

The broader challenge—and the focus of this paper—is to recognize
that RL involves decision-making under uncertainty, and to develop an
inferential theory for RL problems. Such theory must not only be

instance-dependent
, but also
data-dependent
, meaning
that quantities such as confidence intervals should be computable
using available data. The latter property is not shared by most past
instance-dependent guarantees in RL; with limited exceptions (e.g.,
Theorem 1(a) in the paper
[
19
]
), most results
from past work depend on population-level objects–such as probability
transition matrices, Bellman variances, or reward function
bounds—that are not known to the user. Due to this lack of
data-dependence, such results, while theoretically useful, cannot
actually be used by the practitioner in guiding the process of data
collection and inference.

Our work aims to close this gap between theory and practice by
providing theoretical guarantees that are both instance and
data-dependent. The resulting bounds are both sharp and computable
based on data, and we use our theory to design early stopping
procedures that output estimates with confidence intervals of
prescribed widths and coverage. These procedures lead to substantial
reductions in the amount of data required for a target accuracy. In
more detail, we make these contributions in the context of Markov
decision processes (MDPs) with a finite number of states and actions,
and problem-dependent confidence regions both for policy evaluation
and optimal value function estimation. Contrary to prior work on
instance-dependent analysis, our work allows a user to adapt their
data requirements for the specific MDP at hand by exploiting the local
difficulty of the MDP. As we show, doing so can lead to significant
reductions in the sample sizes required for effective learning.

1.2
Contributions

In brief, the main contributions of this paper are to provide
guarantees for policy evaluation and optimization that are both
instance-optimal and data-dependent. Our first main result, stated as
Theorem
1
, applies to a meta-procedure, which
takes as input any base procedure for policy evaluation that is
instance-optimal up to constant factors. It leverages this base
procedure using a portion of the dataset, and uses the resulting
output along with the remaining data to compute bounds on the
estimated value function. We prove that these bounds—which are
data-dependent bounds by construction—are also instance-optimal up
to constant factors. Thus, they can inverted so as to produce a
confidence region for the value function, and up to constant factors,
the width of this confidence region is as small as possible for a
pre-specified coverage. Next, based on the guarantees from
Theorem
1
, we introduce an early stopping
protocol for policy evaluation, known as the
EmpIRE
procedure,
that is guaranteed to output instance-optimal confidence regions up to
constant factors upon stopping. As we show both theoretically and in
simulation, use of this early stopping procedure can lead to
significant reductions in sample sizes relative the use of worst-case
criteria.

2
Some background

In this section, we provide some background on Markov decision
processes (MDPs), policy evaluation, and optimal value estimation
problems.

2.1
Markov decision processes

We start with a brief introduction to Markov decision processes (MDPs)
with finite state
\mathcal{X}
and action
\mathcal{U}
spaces; see the
books
[
18
,
2
,
20
]
for a more
in-depth background. In a Markov decision process, the state
x

evolves dynamically in time under the influence of the actions.
Concretely, there is a collection of probability transition kernels,

\{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}_{u}(\cdot\mid x)\mid(x,u)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}_{u}(x^{\prime}\mid x)
denotes the probability of a transition to the state

x^{\prime}
when the action
u
is taken at the current state

x
. In addition, an MDP is equipped with a reward function

\mathbf{r}
that maps every state-action pair
(x,u)
to a
real number
\mathbf{r}(x,u)
. The reward
\mathbf{r}(x,u)
is the reward received upon performing the action
u

in the state
x
. Overall, a given MDP is characterized by the
pair
(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}},\mathbf{r})
, along with a discount factor

\gamma\in(0,1)
.

A deterministic policy
\pi
is a mapping
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}
: the quantity
\pi(x)\in\mathcal{U}
indicates
the action to be taken in the state
x
. The value of a policy
is defined by the expected sum of discounted rewards in an infinite
sample path. More precisely, for a given policy
\pi
and discount
factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
, the value function for policy

\pi
is given by

\displaystyle V^{\pi}(x)\coloneq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}(x_{k},u_{k})\mid x_{0}=x\Big],\qquad\mbox{where $u_{k}=\pi(x_{k})$ for all $k\geq 0$}.

(1)

A closely related object is the action-value or
Q
-function
associated with the policy, which is given by

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(x,u)\coloneq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}(x_{k},u_{k})\mid(x_{0},u_{0})=(x,u)\Big],\qquad\text{where
$u_{k}=\pi(x_{k})$ for all $k\geq 1$}.

(2)

Two core problems in reinforcement learning—and those that we
analyze in this paper—are policy evaluation and policy optimization.
In the problem of
policy evaluation
, we are given a fixed
policy
\pi
, and our goal is to estimate its value function on
the basis of samples. In the
policy optimization
, our goal is
to estimate the optimal policy, along with the associated optimal

Q
-value function

\displaystyle Q^{\star}(x,u)

\displaystyle\coloneq\max_{\pi\in\mathbf{\Pi}}\;Q^{\pi}(x,u)\quad\mbox{for all $(x,u)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}$,}

(3)

again on the basis of samples. As we now describe, both of these
problems have equivalent formulations as computing the fixed points of
certain types of Bellman operators.

2.2
Policy evaluation and Markov reward processes

We begin by formalizing the problem of policy evaluation. For a given
MDP, if we fix some deterministic policy
\pi
, then the MDP
reduces to a Markov reward process (MRP) over the state space

\mathcal{X}
. More precisely, the state evolution over time is
determined by the set of transition functions

\{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}_{\pi(x)}(\cdot\mid x),x\in\mathcal{X}\}
, whereas the reward received when at state
x
is
given by
\mathbf{r}(x,\pi(x))
.

When the number of states is finite with
|\mathcal{X}|
, the transition
functions
\{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}_{\pi(x)}(\cdot\mid x),x\in\mathcal{X}\}
and the rewards
\{\mathbf{r}(x,\pi(x))\mid x\in\mathcal{X}\}
can be conveniently
represented as a
|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}|
matrix and a

|\mathcal{X}|
dimensional vector, respectively. For ease of notation,
we use
\mathbf{P}
to denote this
|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}|

matrix, and
r
to denote this
|\mathcal{X}|
dimensional
vector. Concretely, for any state
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we define

\displaystyle r(x)\coloneq\mathbf{r}(x,\pi(x))\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{P}(x^{\prime},x)\coloneq\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}_{\pi(x)}(x^{\prime}\mid x),

(4)

where
\mathbf{P}(x^{\prime},x)
denotes the row corresponding to

x
and the column corresponding to
x^{\prime}
. We will often use

\mathbf{P}(x^{\prime}\mid x)
to denote
\mathbf{P}(x^{\prime},x)
. With this formulation at hand, it is clear that evaluating
the value of the policy
\pi
for the MDP
\mathcal{P}
is the same as
finding the value of the MRP
\mathcal{M}=(r,\mathbf{P},\gamma)
with reward
r
and transition
\mathbf{P}
defined
in equation (
4
).

Bellman evaluation operator:

Given an MRP
\mathcal{M}=(r,\mathbf{P},\gamma)
, its value

V^{\star}
can be obtained as the unique fixed-point of the Bellman
evaluation operator
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}
. It acts on the set of value functions in
the following way:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}(V)(x)=r(x)+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbf{P}(x^{\prime}\mid x)V(x^{\prime})\quad\text{for all}\;\;x\in\mathcal{X}.

For finite-dimensional MDPs, the value and reward functions can be
viewed as
|\mathcal{X}|
-dimensional vectors, and the transition
function as a
|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}|
dimensional matrix. As a
result, the action of the Bellman operator
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}
can be written in the
following compact form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}(V)=r+\gamma\mathbf{P}V,

(5)

and the associated fixed point relation is given by
V^{\star}=\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}(V^{\star})
. See the standard
books
[
18
,
20
,
2
]
for more
details.

Generative observation model for MRPs:

In the learning setting, the pair
(\mathbf{P},r)
is unknown and
we assume that we have access to
i.i.d. samples

\{(R_{k},\textbf{Z}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{n}
from the reward
vector
r
and from the transition matrix

\mathbf{P}
. Concretely, given a sample index
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, we have for all state
x\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\textbf{Z}_{k}(\cdot,x)\sim\mathbf{P}(\cdot\mid x)\qquad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[R_{k}(x)]=r(x).

(6)

We also assume that the deviation of the reward sample

R_{k}(x)
from the true reward
r(x)
is
bounded:

\displaystyle|R(x)-r(x)|\leq R_{\mathrm{max}}\qquad\text{for all}\;\;x\in\mathcal{X}.

(7)

In other words, for every sample
k
, we observe for every state

x
a reward
R_{k}(x)
and the next state
x^{\prime}\sim\mathbf{P}(\cdot\mid x)
. Then we have
\textbf{Z}_{k}(x^{\prime},x)=1
, and the remaining entries in the row corresponding to

x
are
0
. Sometimes we will denote
\textbf{Z}_{k}(x^{\prime},x)
as
\textbf{Z}_{k}(x^{\prime}\mid x)
.

2.3
Policy optimization via optimal
Q
-function estimation

Recall that the goal of policy optimization is to find an optimal
policy along with the optimal
Q
-value function (cf.
equation (
3
)). For MDPs with finite state
space
\mathcal{X}
and action space
\mathcal{U}
, any
Q
-value
function can be represented as an element of
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{U}|}
.

Moreover, the optimal
Q
-function
Q^{\star}
is the
unique
fixed point
of the Bellman (optimality) operator
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
, an
operator on
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{U}|}
given
via

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}(Q)(x,u)\coloneq r(x,u)+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in x}\mathbf{P}_{u}(x^{\prime}\mid u)\max_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}}Q(x^{\prime},u^{\prime}).

(8)

Given
Q^{\star}
, an optimal policy
\pi^{\star}
is given by

\pi^{\star}(x)\in\arg\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}Q^{\star}(x,u)
. Again, we refer the reader to the
standard references
[
18
,
20
,
2
]
for more details.

Generative observation model for MRPs:

We operate in the
generative
observation model: we are given

n
i.i.d. samples of the form
\{(\mathcal{Z}_{k},\mathcal{R}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{n}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{k}
is a
matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{U}|}
and

\mathcal{Z}_{k}
is a collection of
|\mathcal{U}|
matrices in

\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}|}
indexed by

\mathcal{U}
. We denote by
\mathcal{Z}_{k}(x,u)
the
row-vector corresponding to state
x
and action
u
. The
row-vector is computed via sampling from the transition kernel

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}_{u}(\cdot\mid x)
, independently of all other

(x,u)
, and making the entry corresponding to the next
state
1
, and the remaining entries
0
. Concretely, for every state
action pair
(x,u)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}
, we
write

\displaystyle x^{\prime}\sim\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}_{u}(\cdot\mid x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{u}(\cdot\mid x)=\mathbf{1}_{x=x^{\prime}}.

(9)

We write
\mathcal{Z}_{1}+\mathcal{Z}_{2}
to indicate the collection of

|\mathcal{U}|
matrices that are the sum of the matrices from

\mathcal{Z}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
that match in the action. We also
assume that
\mathcal{R}_{k}(x,u)
is a bounded random
variable with mean
\mathbf{r}(x,u)
and that

|\mathcal{R}_{k}(x,u)-\mathbf{r}(x,u)|\leq\mathcal{R}_{\text{max}}
. Additionally,
\mathcal{R}_{k}(x,u)
is
taken to be independent of all other state-action pairs and of the
observations
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
.

3
Confidence intervals for policy evaluation

In this section, we propose instance-dependent confidence regions for
the policy evaluation problem. We start with a discussion on the
problem dependent functional that determines the difficulty for the
evaluation problem. As already detailed in
Section
2.2
, any MDP policy pair
(\mathcal{P},\pi)
naturally gives rise to an MRP, and the policy evaluation
problem is equivalent to finding the value of that MRP. Accordingly,
in the rest of the section, we discuss the problem of finding the
value of a general MRP
\mathcal{M}
.

3.1
Optimal instance dependence for policy evaluation

Given access to a generative sample
(R,\textbf{Z})
from an
MRP
\mathcal{M}=(r,\mathbf{P},\gamma)
, we can define the
single-sample empirical Bellman operator as

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}}(V)(x)

\displaystyle\coloneq R(x)+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\textbf{Z}(x^{\prime}\mid x)V(x^{\prime})\qquad\mbox{for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$.}

(10)

By construction, for any fixed value function
V
, the quantity

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}}(V)
is an unbiased estimate of
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}(V)
. In the
finite-dimensional setting,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}}(V)
can be considered a
random vector with entries given by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}}(V)(x)
, and
we can talk about its covariance matrix. In a recent
paper
[
7
]
, the authors show that the quantity that
determines the difficulty of estimating the value
V^{\star}
, given
access to i.i.d. generative samples following the sampling
mechanism (
6
), is the following
|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}|
covariance matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})\coloneq(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\texttt{val}}(\mathcal{M},V^{\star})(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-\top}.

(11)

Intuitively, the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\texttt{val}}(\mathcal{M},V^{\star})=\covar(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}}(V^{\star}))
captures the noise of the empirical
Bellman operator, evaluated at the true value
V^{\star}
. This term
is then compounded by powers of the discounted transition matrix

\gamma\mathbf{P}
, which captures how the perturbation in the
Bellman operator propagates over time, and thus gives rise to the
matrix
(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}
via the von Neumann
expansion.

In the paper
[
9
]
, it is shown the functional

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
arises in lower
bounds—in both asymptotic and non-asymptotic settings—on the error
of any procedure for estimating the value function. In particular,
any estimate
\tilde{V}_{n}
of the value function must necessarily
satisfy a lower bound of the form
\|\tilde{V}_{n}-V^{\star}\|_{\infty}\succsim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Moreover, the
authors
[
7
]
provide a practical scheme that achieves
this lower bound, modulo logarithmic factors. In particular, in
Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 of the paper
[
7
]
show
that a variance-reduced version of policy evaluation, using a total of

n
samples, returns an estimate
\widehat{V}_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{V}_{n}-V^{\star}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\precsim\frac{\sqrt{\log(|\mathcal{X}|/\delta)}}{\sqrt{n}}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{n})

(12)

Notably, this upper bound can be much smaller than a worst case upper
bound (see Section 3.2 in the paper
[
7
]
). In
particular, the variance-reduced PE scheme provides far more accurate
estimates for problems that are “easier”, as measured by the size of
the functional
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}
.

3.2
A procedure for computing data-dependent bounds

Guarantees of the type (
12
)—showing that a
certain procedure for policy evaluation is instance-optimal—are
theoretically interesting, but may fail to be practically useful. In
particular, the bound (
12
) depends on the
unknown population-level quantity

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}
, so that for any given
instance, we cannot actually evaluate the error bound achieved for a
given sample size. Conversely, when data is being collected in a
sequential fashion, as is often the case in practice, we cannot use
this theory to decide when to stop.

In this section, we rectify this gap between theory and practice by
introducing a data-dependent upper bound on the error in policy
evaluation (PE). In addition to the variance-reduced PE scheme
described in the paper
[
9
]
, there are other
procedures that could also be used to achieve an instance-optimal
bound, such as the ROOT-OP procedure applied to the Bellman
operator
[
12
,
15
]
. Rather than focus on
the details of a specific PE method, our theory applies to a generic
class of PE procedures, as we now define.

3.2.1
(\varphi_{f},\varphi_{s})
-instance-optimal algorithms

Suppose we have access to some procedure
\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{Eval}}
that estimates
the value function
V^{\star}
of an MRP
\mathcal{M}
. More precisely,
let
\widehat{V}_{n}
denote the estimate obtained from the
algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{Eval}}
using
n
generative samples

\{R_{k},\textbf{Z}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
. Our analysis applies
to procedures that are instance-optimal in a precise sense that we
specify here.

Given a failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
, let
\varphi_{f}
and

\varphi_{s}
be non-negative functions of
\delta
; depending on the
procedure under consideration, these functions may also involve other
known parameters (e.g. number of states, the discount factor

\gamma
, initialization of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{Eval}}
etc.), but
we omit such dependence to keep our notation streamlined. For any
such pair, we say that the procedure
\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{Eval}}
is
(\varphi_{f},\varphi_{s})
-instance optimal
if the estimate
\widehat{V}_{n}

satisfies the
\ell_{\infty}
-bound

\displaystyle\|\widehat{V}_{n}-V^{\star}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\varphi_{f}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}+\frac{\varphi_{s}(\delta)}{n}.

(13)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Thus, an
(\varphi_{f},\varphi_{s})
-instance-optimal procedure returns an estimate that,
modulo any inflation of the error due to
\varphi_{f}(\delta)
being
larger than one, achieves the optimal instance-dependent bound. Note
that the function
\varphi_{s}
is associated with the higher order term
(decaying as
n^{-1}
), so becomes negligible for larger sample
sizes. As a concrete example, as shown by the
bound (
12
), there is a variance-reduced version
of policy evaluation that is instance-optimal in this sense with

\varphi_{f}(\delta)\asymp\sqrt{\log(|\mathcal{X}|/\delta)}
. Our
results are based on the natural assumption that the functions

\varphi_{f}
and
\varphi_{s}
take values uniformly lower bounded by

1
, and are decreasing in
\delta
.

In practice, even if an estimator enjoys the attractive
guarantee (
13
), it cannot be exploited in
practice since the right-hand side depends on the
unknown

problem parameters
(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})
. The main result
of the following section is to introduce a data-dependent quantity
that (a) also provides a high probability upper bound on

\|\widehat{V}_{n}-V^{\star}\|_{\infty}
; and (b) matches the
guarantee (
13
) apart from changes to the
leading pre-factor and the higher order terms.

3.2.2
Constructing a data-dependent bound

In order to obtain a data-dependent bound on

\|\widehat{V}_{n}-V^{\star}\|_{\infty}
, it suffices to estimate
the quantity
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}
. Our main
result guarantees that there is a data-dependent estimate

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\widehat{V}_{n},\mathcal{D})
that leads to an upper bound
that holds with high probability, and is within constant factors of
the best possible bound. In terms of the shorthand
b(V)\coloneq R_{\mathrm{max}}+\gamma\|V\|_{\infty}
, our

empirical error estimate
takes the form

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{n}(\widehat{V}_{n},\mathcal{D},\delta)

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{2\sqrt{6}\cdot\varphi_{f}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\widehat{V}_{n},\mathcal{D})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}+\frac{2\varphi_{s}(\delta)}{n}+\frac{6b(\widehat{V}_{n})}{1-\gamma}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\log(8|\mathcal{X}|/\delta)}}{n-1}.

(14)

This error estimate is a function of a dataset
\mathcal{D}=(\mathcal{D}_{{n}},\mathcal{D}_{{n_{h}}})
where:

\bullet

the base estimator acts on
\mathcal{D}_{{n}}
with cardinality

n
to compute the value function estimate

\widehat{V}_{n}\coloneq\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{Eval}}(\mathcal{D}_{{n}})
,

\bullet

the smaller set
\mathcal{D}_{{2n_{h}}}
corresponds to samples that
are held-out and play a role only in the covariance estimate

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\widehat{V}_{n},\mathcal{D})
, as detailed in
Section
3.2.3
following our theorem statement. This data
set consists of
2n_{h}
samples with
n_{h}\coloneq 2\lceil\frac{32\cdot\log(8|\mathcal{X}|^{2}/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\rceil
.

With this set-up, we have the following guarantee:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{Eval}}
be any
(\varphi_{f},\varphi_{s}
)-instance-optimal
algorithm (cf. relation (
13
)). Then for any

\delta\in(0,1)
, given a dataset
\mathcal{D}=(\mathcal{D}_{{n}},\mathcal{D}_{{2n_{h}}})
with
n\geq\varphi_{f}^{2}(\delta)\frac{24\cdot 8\cdot\log(8|\mathcal{X}|^{2}/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}
samples, the empirical error estimate
\mathscr{E}_{n}

has the following guarantees with probability at least
1-3\delta
:

(a)

The
\ell_{\infty}
-error is upper bounded by the empirical
error estimate:

\displaystyle\|\widehat{V}_{n}-V^{\star}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\mathscr{E}_{n}(\widehat{V}_{n},\mathcal{D},\delta).

(15a)

(b)

Moreover, this guarantee is order-optimal in the sense that

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{n}(\widehat{V}_{n},\mathcal{D},\delta)\leq\frac{13\cdot\varphi_{f}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}+\frac{2\varphi_{s}(\delta)}{n}+\frac{32b(V^{\star})}{1-\gamma}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\log(\tfrac{8|\mathcal{X}|}{\delta})}}{n-1}.

(15b)

We prove this theorem in
Section
5.1
.

A few comments regarding Theorem
1
are in
order. Since the empirical error estimate
\mathscr{E}_{n}
can be computed
based on the data, we obtain a data-dependent confidence interval on

\|\widehat{V}_{n}-V^{\star}\|_{\infty}
. More precisely, we are
guaranteed the inclusion

\displaystyle\Big[\widehat{V}_{n}(x)-\mathscr{E}_{n},\widehat{V}_{n}(x)+\mathscr{E}_{n}\Big]\ni V^{\star}(x)\qquad\mbox{uniformly for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$}

(16)

with probability at least
1-3\delta
.

Note that the dominant term in the error estimate
\mathscr{E}_{n}
is
proportional to
n^{-1/2}\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\widehat{V}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n,n_{h}})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}
, and it corresponds to an
estimate of the dominant term
n^{-1/2}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}
from the
bound (
13
). The second
bound (
15b
) in
Theorem
1
ensures that
\mathscr{E}_{n}
provides an
optimal approximation, up to constant pre-factors, of the leading
order term on the right-hand side of the
bound (
13
).

3.2.3
Constructing the empirical covariance estimate

We now provide details on the construction of the empirical covariance
estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(\widehat{V}_{n};\mathcal{D})
. Recalling the
definition (
11
), our procedure is guided by the
decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})=\underbrace{(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{estimated using first}\\
\text{
small holdout dataset}\end{subarray}}\;\;\;\underbrace{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\texttt{val}}(\mathcal{M},V^{\star})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{estimated using}\\
\text{remaining data}\end{subarray}}\;\;\;\underbrace{(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-\top}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{estimated using
second}\\
\text{small holdout dataset}\end{subarray}}.

From the given data
\mathcal{D}=(\mathcal{D}_{{n}},\mathcal{D}_{{2n_{h}}})
, we partition
\mathcal{D}_{{2n_{h}}}
into two subsets

(\mathcal{D}_{{n_{h}}}^{1},\mathcal{D}_{{n_{h}}}^{2})
, as indexed by

\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
respectively. We use this data to compute
the empirical averages

\displaystyle\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}=\frac{1}{n_{h}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{1}}\textbf{Z}_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}=\frac{1}{n_{h}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{2}}\textbf{Z}_{i}.

(17)

Using the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{{n}}
, we use the procedure
\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{Eval}}

to compute the value function estimate
\widehat{V}_{n}
. We re-use the
samples
\mathcal{D}_{{n}}=\{(R_{j},\textbf{Z}_{j})\}_{j\in\mathcal{F}}
to compute the
V
-statistic

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(\widehat{V}_{n};\mathcal{D}_{{n}})

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\in\mathcal{F}\\
j<k\end{subarray}}(R_{j}-R_{k}+\gamma(\textbf{Z}_{j}-\textbf{Z}_{k})\widehat{V}_{n})(R_{j}-R_{k}+\gamma(\textbf{Z}_{j}-\textbf{Z}_{k})\widehat{V}_{n})^{\top}.

(18)

With these three ingredients, our empirical covariance estimate is
given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\widehat{V}_{n},\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}})^{-1}\;\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(\widehat{V}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{{n}})\;(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})^{-\top}.

(19)

Recalling that
n_{h}\coloneq 2\lceil\frac{32\cdot\log(8|\mathcal{X}|^{2}/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\rceil
, we
note that the hold-out sets will typically be very small relative to
the sample size
n
used to compute the estimate
\widehat{V}_{n}

itself.

3.3
Early stopping with optimality:
EmpIRE
procedure

In practice, it is often the case that policy evaluation is carried
out repeatedly, as an inner loop within some broader algorithm (e.g.,
policy iteration schemes or actor-critic methods). Due to repeated
calls to a policy evaluation routine, it is especially desirable in
these settings to minimize the amount of data used. In practice, it
is adequate to terminate a policy evaluation routine once some target
accuracy
\epsilon
is achieved; guarantees in the
\ell_{\infty}

such as those analyzed here, are particularly attractive in this
context.

In this section, we leverage the data-dependent bound from

) captures the noise present in the
empirical Bellman operator (
24
) as an
estimate of the population Bellman
operator (
8
), when evaluated at the
optimal value function
Q^{\star}
. As for the pre-factor
(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}^{\pi})^{-1}
, by a von Neumann expansion we can
write

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}^{\pi})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}^{\pi})^{k}.

The sum of the powers of
\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}^{\pi}
accounts for
the compounded effect of an initial perturbation when following the
Markov chain specified by an optimal policy
\pi
. Put simply, the
quantity (
25
) captures the noise accumulated when an
optimal policy
\pi
is followed.

The authors in the paper
[
9
]
also show that
under appropriate assumptions, the estimate
\widehat{Q}_{n}
,
obtained from a variance reduced
Q
-learning algorithm using

n
i.i.d. samples, satisfies the bound

\displaystyle\|\widehat{Q}_{n}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}\leq\varphi_{f}(\delta)\cdot\frac{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}(\mathbf{r},\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}},\gamma)}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{c_{2}}{n}

(26)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Here the functions

\varphi_{f}
and
\varphi_{s}
depend on the tolerance parameter

\delta\in(0,1)
, along with logarithmic factors in the
dimension. Furthermore, this algorithm achieves the non-asymptotic
local minimax lower bound for the optimal
Q
-function estimation
problem (see Theorem 1 in the
paper
[
9
]
).

4.2
A conservative yet useful upper bound

Motivated by the success in Section
3
, it is
interesting to ask if we can prove a data-dependent estimate for the
term
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}(\mathbf{r},\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}},\gamma)
. Observe that
the term
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}(\mathbf{r},\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}},\gamma)
from
equation (
25
) depends on the set of
all
optimal
policies
\Pi^{\star}
, and the authors are not aware of a
data-dependent efficient estimate without imposing restrictive
assumptions on the MDP
\mathcal{P}=(\mathbf{r},\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}},\gamma)
.

Interestingly, simple algebra reveals that

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}(\mathbf{r},\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}},\gamma)\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}}{1-\gamma}.

(27)

Observe that all entries of the PSD matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})

are upper bounded by the scalar
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}
,
the entries of the matrix
(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}^{\pi})^{-1}
are non-negative, and
\|(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}^{\pi})^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
. Combining these three observations yield the
claim (
27
).

It is interesting to compare the upper bound (
27
)
with a worst case upper bound on
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}(\mathbf{r},\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}},\gamma)
. In light of Lemma 7 from the
paper
[
1
]
, assuming
\mathcal{R}_{\text{max}}\leq 1
, and

\|\mathbf{r}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
for simplicity, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}(\mathbf{r},\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}},\gamma)\leq\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)^{1.5}}.

(28)

Combining the bounds (
27
)
and (
28
), we see that the upper
bound (
27
) is particularly useful when

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\gamma}}.

As an illustration, we now describe an interesting sequence of MDPs
for which the last condition is satisfied.

Example 1

(A continuum of illustrative examples)
.

Consider an MDP with two states
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
, two actions

\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
, and with transition functions and reward
functions given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}_{u_{1}}=\begin{bmatrix}p&1-p\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\quad\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}}_{u_{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbf{r}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
\tau&0\end{bmatrix}.

(29)

We assume that there is no randomness in the reward samples. Here the
pair
(p,\tau)
along with the discount factor
\gamma
are
parameters of the construction, and we consider a sub-family of these
parameters indexed by a scalar
\lambda\geq 0
. For any such

\lambda
and discount factor
\gamma\in(\tfrac{1}{4},1)
,
consider the setting

\displaystyle p=\frac{4\gamma-1}{3\gamma},\quad\text{and}\quad\tau=1-(1-\gamma)^{\lambda}.

With these choices of parameters, the optimal
Q
-function
Q^{\star}

takes the form

\displaystyle Q^{\star}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{(1-\gamma)+\gamma\tau(1-p)}{(1-\gamma)(1-\gamma p)}&\gamma\cdot\frac{(1-\gamma)+\gamma\tau(1-p)}{(1-\gamma)(1-\gamma p)}\\
\frac{\tau}{1-\gamma}&\frac{\gamma\tau}{1-\gamma}\end{bmatrix},

with an unique optimal policy
\pi^{\star}(x_{1})=\pi^{\star}(x_{2})=u_{1}
. We can then compute that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1-\tau}{1-\gamma p}\cdot\sqrt{p(1-p)}=c\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\lambda}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\gamma}}.

(30)

See Appendix
A.2
for the details of this
calculation.

Example
1
shows that for any
\lambda>0
, the bound

\frac{\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}}{1-\gamma}
is smaller than the worst case bound of
\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)^{1.5}}
by a factor of
\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)^{\lambda}}
. We point out that this gap is significant
when the discount factor
\gamma
is close to
1
. For instance,
for
\gamma=0.99
and
\lambda=1
, the upper
bound (
27
) is
10^{2}
times better than the worst
case bound. Alternatively, the bound (
26
) yields
an improvement of a factor of
10^{4}
, when compared to a worst case
value.

4.3
Data-dependent bounds for optimal
Q
-functions

In this section, we provide a data-dependent upper bound on

\|\widehat{Q}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}
, where the estimate

\widehat{Q}
is obtained from any algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}

satisfying certain convergence criterion.

4.3.1
Instance-valid algorithms

We assume that given access to
n
generative samples

\mathcal{D}_{n}\coloneq\{\mathcal{R}_{i},\mathcal{Z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
from an MDP
\mathcal{P}=(\mathbf{r},\boldsymbol{\mathscr{P}},\gamma)
, the output
\widehat{Q}_{n}\coloneq\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
, obtained using the data set

\mathcal{D}_{n}
, satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}\texttt{ condition:}\qquad\|\widehat{Q}_{n}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\varphi_{f}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(1-\gamma)}+\frac{\varphi_{s}(\delta)}{n}.

(31)

with probability
1-\delta
. Here
\varphi_{f}
and
\varphi_{s}

are functions of the tolerance level
\delta
; in practical
settings, they may in addition depend on the problem dimension in some
cases, but we suppress this dependence for simplicity.

We point out that any instance-optimal algorithm, i.e. algorithms
satisfying the condition (
26
), automatically
satisfies the condition (
31
). One added
benefit of the condition (
31
) is that this
condition is much easier to verify. To illustrate this point, in
Proposition
1
in
Appendix
C
, we show that the variance reduced

Q
-learning algorithm from the paper
[
9
]

satisfies the condition (
31
) under a

milder assumption
(cf. Proposition
1
in
Appendix
C
and Theorem 2 from the
paper
[
9
]
). In the rest of the section, we
focus on providing a data-dependent estimate for

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

4.3.2
Constructing a data-dependent bound

In order to obtain a data-dependent bound on

\|\widehat{Q}_{n}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}
, it suffices to
estimate the complexity term

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}
. Our next result
guarantees that there is a data-dependent estimate

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(\widehat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{D})
which provides an upper bound
that holds with high probability, and is within constant factors of

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}
. With a recycling of
notation, we define
b(Q)\coloneq R_{\mathrm{max}}+\gamma\|Q\|_{\infty}
,
and construct the

empirical error estimate

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{n}(\widehat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n},\delta)\coloneq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\cdot\varphi_{f}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(\widehat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}}{(1-\gamma)}+\frac{2\varphi_{s}(\delta)}{n}+\frac{8b(\widehat{Q}_{n})}{1-\gamma}\cdot\frac{\varphi_{f}(\delta)\sqrt{2\log(D/\delta)}}{n-1}.

(32)

In this error estimate, the base estimator uses the
n
-sample
dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
both to compute the
Q
-value function
estimate
\widehat{Q}_{n}\coloneq\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
. It
also uses the same data to estimate the covariance

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(\widehat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n})
, according to the
procedure given in Section
4.3.3
.

The following result summarizes the guarantees associated
with the empirical error estimate (
32
):

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}
be any
(\varphi_{f},\varphi_{s})
instance-dependent
algorithm (cf. relation (
31
)). Then for any

\delta\in(0,1)
, given a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
with

n\geq\varphi_{f}^{2}(\delta)\cdot\frac{32\cdot\log(4D/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}
samples, the following
holds with probability at least
1-2\delta
:

(a)

The
\ell_{\infty}
-error is upper bounded as

\displaystyle\|\widehat{Q}_{n}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}\leq\mathscr{E}_{n}(\widehat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n},\delta).

(33a)

(b)

Moreover, this guarantee is order-optimal in the sense that

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{n}(\widehat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n},\delta)\leq\frac{7\cdot\varphi_{f}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}}{1-\gamma}+\frac{6\varphi_{s}(\delta)}{n}+\frac{5b(Q^{\star})}{1-\gamma}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\log(D/\delta)}}{n-1}.

(33b)

See Section
5.2
for the
proof.

A few comments regarding Theorem
2
are
in order. Like in policy evaluation, the empirical error estimate

\mathscr{E}_{n}
can be computed based on the data and we obtain a
data-dependent confidence interval for
Q^{\star}
. In particular,
equation (
33a
) guarantees that

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{Q}_{n}(x,u)-\mathscr{E}_{n},\widehat{Q}_{n}(x,u)+\mathscr{E}_{n}\right]\ni Q^{\star}(x,u)\qquad\text{uniformly for all }x\in\mathcal{X},u\in\mathcal{U}

with probability at least
1-2\delta
.

We point out that the dominating term in the error estimate
\mathscr{E}_{n}

is proportional to
n^{-1/2}\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(\widehat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}
, and
this is an estimate of the dominating term
n^{-1/2}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}
from the
bound (
31
). Additionally, the
bound (
33b
) ensures that the proposed
data-dependent bound in equation (
33a
) is a
sharp approximation of
n^{-1/2}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}
from the
bound (
31
).

4.3.3
Estimation of
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}

In this section, we provide the details on the construction of the
estimate
\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(\widehat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}
of

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Given a
generative data set
\mathcal{D}_{n}\coloneq\{\mathcal{R}_{i},\mathcal{Z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we first use
\widehat{Q}_{n}=\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
to estimate
Q^{\star}
, and use the
same data set
\mathcal{D}_{n}
to provide an empirical estimate of the
quantity
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Observe
that it suffices to estimate the diagonal entries of the matrix

\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(Q^{\star})
. Accordingly, our empirical covariance
matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(\widehat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n})
, based on a
data set
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, is a
D\times D

diagonal matrix
. Concretely, let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}_{i}
denote the
empirical estimate of the Bellman optimality operator based on the

i^{th}
generative sample, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}_{i}(Q)(x,u)=\mathcal{R}_{i}(x,u)+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{Z}_{i}^{u}(x^{\prime}\mid x)\cdot\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}Q(x^{\prime},u),\quad\text{for all}\;\;(x,u)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}.

Recall that
\mathcal{Z}_{i}^{u}(x^{\prime}\mid x)
is
1
if and
only if the sample in
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
from
(x,u)
gives

x^{\prime}
as the next state, and zero otherwise. We define the

(x,u)^{th}
diagonal entry of the diagonal matrix

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(\widehat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n})
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(\widehat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n})((x,u),(x,u))=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\left\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}_{i}(\widehat{Q}_{n})(x,u)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}_{j}(Q)(x,u)\right\}^{2}.

(34)

4.3.4
Early stopping for optimal
Q
-functions

Inspired by Algorithm
EmpIRE
, we can take an instance-dependent
procedure
\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}
satisfying the
condition (
31
) and construct a similar
stopping procedure that terminates when the data-dependent upper bound
of
\|\widehat{Q}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}
estimate is below some
user-specified threshold
\epsilon
. Following Algorithm
EmpIRE
,
one would iteratively recompute the estimate
\widehat{Q}
using the
algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}
, and check the condition

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{2}\varphi_{f}(\delta_{n})}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{opt}}}(\widehat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{1/2}}{(1-\gamma)}+\frac{2\sqrt{2}\varphi_{f}(\delta_{n})\cdot\sqrt{16\log(D/\delta)}}{(1-\gamma)}\cdot\frac{b(\widehat{Q}_{n})}{n-1}+\frac{2\varphi_{s}{\delta_{n}}}{n}\leq\epsilon.

We terminate the algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{PolOpt}}
when this criterion is
satisfied. Again, the correctness of such stopping procedure follows
from Theorem
2
and a union bound.

5
Proofs

In this section, we provide proofs of our main results, with
Sections
5.1

and

devoted the proofs of

and

, respectively.

5.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Let us first introduce an auxiliary result that plays a key role in
the proof:

Lemma 1
.

For a given tolerance
\delta\in(0,1)
, consider a pair of
hold-out estimates
\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}
and
\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
, each based on
hold-out size
n_{h}\geq 32\cdot\frac{\log(4|\mathcal{X}|^{2}/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}
. For any positive semi-definite
matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, we have

\displaystyle\|(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}\leq 3\|(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}},

(35)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

See Section
5.1.3
for a
proof of this lemma.

Throughout the proof, we suppress the dependence of

\widehat{V}_{n}
on the sample size
n
. Additionally, we
adopt the shorthand notations

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\texttt{val}}(V^{\star})=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\texttt{val}}(\mathcal{M},V^{\star}),\quad\quad\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(V^{\star})=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(V^{\star};\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{F}}),\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\widehat{V})=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\widehat{V},\mathcal{D}_{n,n_{h}}).

5.1.1
Proof of Theorem
1
part (a)

We begin by proving the bound (
15a
). Using a
known empirical Bernstein bound (see Lemma
4
in
Appendix
A.1
), we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\|(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}_{\texttt{val}}(V^{\star})(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(V^{\star})(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{b(V^{\star})}{1-\gamma}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{8\log(|\mathcal{X}|/\delta)}{n-1}}

(36)

with probability at at least
1-\delta
.

Now suppose that we can prove that

\|(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(V^{\star})(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\sqrt{2}\|(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(\widehat{V})(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
+\tfrac{\sqrt{8}}{1-\gamma}\|\widehat{V}-V^{\star}\|_{\infty}

(37)

Then, by combining the last bound with
Lemma
1
, we find that

\|(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(V^{\star})(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\sqrt{6}\cdot\|(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}})^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(\widehat{V})(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
+\frac{\sqrt{8}}{1-\gamma}\|\widehat{V}-V^{\star}\|_{\infty}

with probability
1-\delta
. Substituting the last bound into
equation (
36
) and using the fact that the function

b
is
1
-Lipschitz in the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm, we deduce that

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\star}(r,\mathbf{P},V^{\star})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\sqrt{6}\cdot\|(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}})^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(\widehat{V})(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{b(\widehat{V})}{1-\gamma}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{8\log(|\mathcal{X}|/\delta)}{n-1}}\\
+\|\widehat{V}-V^{\star}\|_{\infty}\cdot\left(\frac{\sqrt{8}}{1-\gamma}+\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{8\log(|\mathcal{X}|/\delta)}{n-1}}\right),

with probability at least
1-2\delta
. Substituting the last
bound in the convergence rate condition (
13
),
invoking the sample size condition
n\geq\varphi_{f}^{2}(\delta)\cdot\frac{24\cdot 8\cdot\log(|\mathcal{X}|/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}
from
Theorem
1
yields the
claim (
15a
). It remains to prove the
bound (
37
).

Proof of the bound (
37
):

For any index
i\in[|\mathcal{X}|]
, we define the random variable of
interest
U_{i}\coloneq e_{i}^{T}(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(V^{\star})(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}e_{i}
.
With this definition, substituting the expression for the matrix

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(V^{\star})
we have

\displaystyle U_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\left(e_{i}^{T}(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}(R_{j}-R_{k}+\gamma(\textbf{Z}_{j}-\textbf{Z}_{k})V^{\star})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\left(e_{i}^{T}(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}(R_{j}-R_{k}+\gamma(\textbf{Z}_{j}-\textbf{Z}_{k})\widehat{V})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\left(e_{i}^{T}(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}(\textbf{Z}_{j}-\textbf{Z}_{k})(V^{\star}-\widehat{V})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 2e_{i}^{T}(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\texttt{val}}(\widehat{V})(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}e_{i}+\frac{8}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\cdot\|\widehat{V}-V^{\star}\|_{\infty}^{2}.

The second last inequality follows from triangle inequality, and
last inequality follows from the operator norm bound
\|(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
and the Holder’s inequality. This completes the
proof of the bound (
37
).

5.1.2
Proof of Theorem
1
part (b)

Here we prove the bound (
15b
). Overall, the
argument is similar to that of bound (
15a
). We
have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\widehat{V})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\frac{1}{2}}

5.1.3
Proof of Lemma
1

We begin by observing that the
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{diag}}
operator, despite
not being a norm, satisfies the triangle inequality. Indeed, for
square matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
with matching dimensions, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}

\displaystyle=\max_{i}|e_{i}^{T}(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})e_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i}\left(|e_{i}^{T}\mathbf{A}e_{i}|+|e_{i}^{T}\mathbf{B}e_{i}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i}|e_{i}^{T}\mathbf{A}e_{i}|+\max_{i}|e_{i}^{T}\mathbf{B}e_{i}|

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}+\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}.

(38)

We also require the following simple lemma:

Lemma 2
.

For holdout sample size satisfying
n_{h}\geq\frac{32\log(4|\mathcal{X}|/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}
and any vector

V\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|}
, we have

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}-\mathbf{P})V\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{(1-\gamma)}{\sqrt{2}}\|V\|_{\infty},

with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{4}
.

Proof.

From Hoeffding’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle[(\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}-\mathbf{P})\cdot V]_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{h}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{h}}\langle\textbf{Z}_{j}(i)-p_{i},V\rangle\leq 4\sqrt{\frac{\log(4|\mathcal{X}|/\delta)}{n_{h}}}\cdot\|V\|_{\infty},

with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{4|\mathcal{X}|}
. Here,

\textbf{Z}_{j}(i)
and
p_{i}
, respectively denote the
i^{th}
row vector
of the matrices
\textbf{Z}_{j}
and
\mathbf{P}
. Now, applying a union
bound over
|\mathcal{X}|
coordinates and using the lower bound on the
holdout sample size
n_{h}
yields the claim of
Lemma
2
.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Lemma
1
. For ease of notation, we use the
shorthands

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P},\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{I}-\gamma\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}},\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mathbf{A}}\coloneq\mathbf{I}-\gamma\widehat{\textbf{Z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}.

In light of the triangle inequality (
38
), it
suffices to prove that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-\top}-\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}\leq 2\|\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Simple algebra and another
application of the triangle inequality (
38
)
yields

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-\top}-\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{A}^{-\top}-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{-\top})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}+\|(\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|(\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{A}^{-\top}-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{-\top})\|_{\mathrm{diag}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{3}T_{j},

(39)

where we define

\displaystyle T_{1}\coloneq\|(\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}},\qquad T_{2}\coloneq\|(\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}},\qquad\text{and}

Bounding

We have

\displaystyle T_{1}=\|(\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-\top}\|_{\mathrm{diag}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}-\widetilde{\mathbf{A}})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-\top}e_{i}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\max_{i}\|\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-\top}e_{i}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\max_{i,j}|e_{j}^{T}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-\top}e_{i}|

1-\frac{\delta}{4}

6
Discussion

Our work addresses the problem of obtaining instance-dependent
confidence regions for the policy evaluation problem and the optimal
value estimation problem of an MDP, given access to an instance
optimal algorithm. The confidence regions are constructed by
estimating the instance-dependent functionals that control problem
difficulty in a local neighborhood of the given problem instance. For
both problems, the instance-dependent confidence regions are shown to
be significantly shorter for problems with favorable structure.

Our results also leave a few interesting questions. For instance, our
results and proof techniques heavily rely on the tabular nature of the
MDP. It will be interesting to see if such data-dependent guarantees
can be extended to policy evaluation and optimal value estimation
problems when a function approximation step is involved. Another
interesting direction could be improving the bound from
Theorem
2
for the optimal value
estimation problem. Additionally in the setting of policy evaluation,
we believe that the need for two independent holdout sets is
unnecessary and more likely a proof defect; we conjecture it suffices
to use one holdout set to estimate
(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{P})^{-1}
on both the left and right side.
