Convergence of Momentum-Based Heavy Ball Method with Batch Updating and/or Approximate Gradients
Tadipatri Uday Kiran Reddy, M. Vidyasagar

Abstract
In this paper, we study the well-known “Heavy Ball” method for convex and nonconvex optimization introduced by Polyak in 1964, and establish its convergence under a variety of situations. Traditionally, most algorithms use “full-coordinate update,” that is, at each step, every component of the argument is updated. However, when the dimension of the argument is very high, it is more efficient to update some but not all components of the argument at each iteration. We refer to this as “batch updating” in this paper. When gradient-based algorithms are used together with batch updating, in principle it is sufficient to compute only those components of the gradient for which the argument is to be updated. However, if a method such as backpropagation is used to compute these components, computing only some components of gradient does not offer much savings over computing the entire gradient. Therefore, to achieve a noticeable reduction in CPU usage at each step, one can use first-order differences to approximate the gradient. The resulting estimates are biased , and also have unbounded variance . Thus some delicate analysis is required to ensure that the HB algorithm converge when batch updating is used instead of full-coordinate updating, and/or approximate gradients are used instead of true gradients. In this paper, we establish the almost sure convergence of the iterations to the stationary point(s) of the objective function under suitable conditions; in addition, we also derive upper bounds on the rate of convergence. To the best of our knowledge, there is no other paper that combines all of these features. This paper is dedicated to the memory of Boris Teodorovich Polyak

Then we discuss various options for batch updating, and

[
2

Combined with the results in Section

In batch updating,

In particular, when

1

However, in deep neural network training, if a method such as back-propagation
to compute the gradient

In contrast, if first-order methods are used to

With this in mind, in Section

,
we introduce two different approaches to approximating

The first of these, which we describe as Approach A, is historic and
goes back to
[
24
,
25
]
.
While the analysis of Approach A is very easy, it is not very efficient.
Specifically, at each instant, Approach A requires
2n_{t}

is the number of components of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}
to be
updated at time

.
The second, which we call Approach B, is based on the
Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA) introduced in

[
26
]
.
In SPSA,

.
However, the analysis is considerably more complicated, and the

Having said that, the computational results presented in Section
5

In this paper, the convergence of the Heavy Ball algorithm with full
coordinate updating is established using the Robbins-Siegmund theorem

[
7
]
and a few generalizations thereof.
The contents of the present section can be used with other methods
besides the Heavy Ball method.
The authors believe that
any

proof that is based on the Robbins-Siegmund theorem

batch updating and/or approximate gradients.
Research along these lines is currently under way.

To introduce Options 1A through 4A, we proceed as follows:
Let

Lemma 2

\displaystyle y_{t,i}^{(A)}

4
4

4

Recall that Rademacher
random variables assume values in

and are independent of each other.

Moreover, it is assumed that
\Delta_{t+1,i},i\in[d]
are all independent
of the filtration
{\cal F}_{t}
.
The measurement errors
\xi_{t+1,1}^{+},\cdots,\xi_{t+1,d}^{+}
,

\xi_{t+1,1}^{-},\cdots,\xi_{t+1,d}^{-}
represent the measurement errors.
It is assumed that these
2d

{\cal F}_{t}

and are also independent of
\Delta_{t+1,j}
for each

If we define

y_{t+1,i}^{(B)}:=\frac{J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+c_{t}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1})-J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-c_{t}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1})}{2c_{t}\Delta_{t+1,i}},

(25)

\xi_{t+1,i}^{(B)}:=\frac{\xi_{t+1,i}^{+}-\xi_{t+1,i}^{-}}{2\Delta_{t+1,i}},

(26)

then one can express

{\bf g}_{t+1}^{(B)}={\bf y}_{t+1}^{(B)}+\frac{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{(B)}}{c_{t}}.

(27)

The assumptions about the measurement errors lead to

E_{t}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{(B)})={\bf 0}.

With these definitions, Option 1B consists of defining

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{(1B)}_{t+1}:=-{\bf g}_{t+1}^{(B)}=-\left({\bf y}_{t+1}^{(B)}+\frac{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{(B)}}{c_{t}}\right).

(28)

Once we define
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{(1B)}_{t+1}
, we can apply each of the three
batch updatings to it, namely single coordinate, multiple coorinate,
and Bernoulli.
These are referred to as Options 2B, 3B and 4B.

A major difference between the Class A options and the Class B options is this:
In the Class A options, we have that
{\bf y}_{t}^{(A)}\in{\cal M}({\cal F}_{t})
.
Hence the conditional variance
CV_{t}({\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}^{(1A)})
arises solely
from the term
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}/c_{t}
.
In contrast, in the Class B options,
it is
not true in general
that
{\bf y}_{t+1}^{(B)}\in{\cal M}({\cal F}_{t})
.
This is because the Rademacher variables
\Delta_{t+1,i}
are defined at time
t+1
.
Hence
{\bf y}_{t+1}^{(B)}
also contributes to
CV_{t}({\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}^{(1B)})
.
This makes the analysis more complicated compared to the Class A options.

It is possible to use other probability distributions for
\mathbf{\Delta}
.
For instance, in
[
32
]
, the perturbations

\Delta_{t+1,i}
are assumed to have Gaussian distributions.
Note that, if
\Delta_{t+1,i}
are binary, then
\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
,
and as a result,

\frac{\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}\|_{2}}{|\Delta_{t+1,i}|}=\sqrt{d},\;\forall i\in[d].

(29)

Therefore, while
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}
is random, the above ratio is not random.
With Gaussian variables, this ratio can be arbitrarily large, but with
ever lower probability.
This is an unnecessary complication that we wish to avoid.
A further generalization of SPSA is given in
[
33
]

which requires
2k
function evaluations instead of
2
,
where
k
is the order of G-SPSA. We do not study this extension in this paper.

Now we analyze Options 1B through 4B.
Because of the complexity of analyzing the B options, we divide the

Lemma 3

Define

{\bf w}_{t}^{B}:=E_{t}({\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}^{(1B)}).

(30)

E_{t}({\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}^{(kB)})={\bf w}_{t}^{B},\mbox{ for }k=2,3,4.

Proof.

Equation (
31

The proof of (

First,
1/\Delta_{t,i}=\Delta_{t,i}
because
\Delta_{t,i}
is bipolar.
Second, if

\Delta_{t,i}

Finally, since, each
\Delta_{t,i}
is independent of the
\sigma

{\cal F}_{t}

it follows that for any function

Next, if

Therefore the conditional variance
E_{t}((B_{t+1}-E_{t}(B_{t+1}))^{2})

(0,4)

(Better bounds are possible, but this one is good enough.)

It follows from (
28

{\bf w}_{t}^{(B)}+\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})=E_{t}({\bf y}_{t+1}^{(B)})+\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}).

Define

V_{t+1}:=\frac{J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+c_{t}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1})-J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-c_{t}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1})}{2c_{t}}.

Then

y_{t+1,i}^{(B)}=\frac{V_{t+1}}{\Delta_{t+1,i}}.

It is worth noting that the numerator is independent of the index
i
;
only the denominator depends on
i
.
So we analyze the numerator.
We can write

\displaystyle V_{t+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2c_{t}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d}{d\lambda}[J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\lambda c_{t}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1})]d\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2c_{t}}\int_{-1}^{1}c_{t}\langle\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1},\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\lambda c_{t}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1})\rangle d\lambda=A_{t+1}+B_{t+1},

where

A_{t+1}:=\frac{1}{2c_{t}}\int_{-1}^{1}c_{t}\langle\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1},\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rangle\;d\lambda=\langle\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1},\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rangle,

B_{t+1}:=\frac{1}{2c_{t}}\int_{-1}^{1}c_{t}\langle\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1},\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\lambda c_{t}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1})-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rangle d\lambda.

Fix an index
i\in[d]
, and define

u_{t+1,i}:=\frac{A_{t+1}}{\Delta_{t+1,i}}=A_{t+1}\Delta_{t+1,i},v_{t+1,i}:=\frac{B_{t+1}}{\Delta_{t+1,i}}=B_{t+1}\Delta_{t+1,i}.

Then

y_{t+1,i}^{(B)}=\frac{V_{t+1}}{\Delta_{t+1,i}}=u_{t+1,i}+v_{t+1,i}.

We will analyze each term separately.

We begin with
u_{t+1,i}
.
By the above,

A_{t+1}=\langle\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1},\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rangle=\Delta_{t+1,i}[\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})]_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\Delta_{t+1,j}[\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})]_{j}.

Therefore, noting that
\Delta_{t+1,i}^{2}=1
gives

u_{t+1,i}=\Delta_{t+1,i}A_{t+1}=[\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})]_{i}+\bar{A}_{t+1,i},

where

\bar{A}_{t+1,i}:=\sum_{j\neq i}\Delta_{t+1,i}\Delta_{t+1,j}[\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})]_{j}.

Next, observe that the product
\Delta_{t+1,i}\Delta_{t+1,j}
is independent
of the
\sigma
-algebra
{\cal F}_{t}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle E_{t}(\bar{A}_{t+1,i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}E_{t}(\Delta_{t+1,i}\Delta_{t+1,j}[\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})]_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E[\Delta_{t+1,i}\Delta_{t+1,j}]\sum_{j\neq i}[\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})]_{j}=0

whenever
j\neq i
.
Hence

E_{t}(u_{t+1,i})=[\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})]_{i},

As for
v_{t+1,i}

\nabla J(\cdot)

and write

\displaystyle|B_{t+1}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2c_{t}}\int_{-1}^{1}c_{t}\langle\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}\|_{2},\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\lambda c_{t}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1})-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}\rangle d\lambda

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}c_{t}L\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}|\lambda|d\lambda=\frac{c_{t}dL}{2}.

Thus

|v_{t+1,i}|\leq\frac{c_{t}dL}{2},\;\forall i\in[d].

As a result

|E_{t}(v_{t+1,i})|\leq\frac{c_{t}dL}{2},\;\forall i\in[d],

\|E_{t}({\bf v}_{t+1})\|_{2}=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}|E_{t}(v_{t+1,i})|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq\frac{c_{t}d^{3/2}L}{2}.

Now the above computations show that

E_{t}({\bf y}_{t+1}^{(B)})=E_{t}({\bf u}_{t+1})+E_{t}({\bf v}_{t+1})=\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})+E_{t}({\bf v}_{t+1}).

Therefore

E_{t}({\bf y}_{t+1}^{(B)}-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}))={\bf v}_{t+1}.

(33)

from which (
32
) follows.

3
Statements of Main Theorems

In this section we state the main theorems of this paper.

3.1
Assumptions on the Objective Function and Search Direction

In this paper, we study functions
J:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
that satisfy the
following
Standing Assumptions:

(A1)

J(\cdot)
is
{\cal C}^{1}
.
Moreover,
\nabla J(\cdot)
is globally Lipschitz-continuous with constant
L
.

(A2)

J(\cdot)
has a global infimum
J^{*}
, which is attained.

(A3)

J(\cdot)
has compact level sets.
Thus, for every fixed constant
r
, the set

L_{J}(r):=\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\leq r\}

is compact.

Note that an equivalent way of stating Assumption (A3) is that

\lim_{\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\rightarrow\infty}J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\infty.

Such a function is also called
radially unbounded
, or

coercive
.
We do not use either term in this paper.

As stated above, (A1)–(A3) are the standing assumptions.
Now we state other assumptions.
Note that different assumptions are used in different theorems.
For this purpose, we define

\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-J^{*}.

We also make use of the following concept
from
[
34
, Definition 1]
:
A function
\eta:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is
said to
belong to Class
{\cal B}
if
\eta(0)=0
, and in addition

\inf_{\epsilon\leq r\leq M}\eta(r)>0,\;\forall 0<\epsilon<M<\infty.

Note
\eta(\cdot)
is
not
assumed to be monotonic.
However, if
\eta:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is continuous, then

\eta(\cdot)
belongs to Class
{\cal B}
if and only if (i)
\eta(0)=0
,
and (ii)
\eta(r)>0
for all
r>0
.

(J1)

There exists a constant
H
such that

\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}^{2}\leq H\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\;\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(38)

(J2)

There is a function
\eta(\cdot)
belonging to Class
{\cal B}
such that

\eta(\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\leq\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2},\;\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(39)

(J3)

Define

S(J):=\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})={\bf 0}\}.

(40)

The assumption is:
There is a function

6
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we have proposed a general framework for analyzing the
convergence of batch updating methods in convex and nonconvex optimization.
In addition to full-coordinate updating which we call Option 1,
we have proposed three different batch updating methods
(called Options 2, 3, 4).
These include almost all the widely used batch updating, or
coordinate-updating methods that are currently used.
By relating the conditional expectation and the conditional variance
of Options 2, 3, and 4 to those of Option 1, we provide a general
methodology for proving the convergence of batch updating methods.
As a specific application, we have analyzed the well-known Heavy Ball method.
In addition to the use of batch updating, we also permit the use of
approximate gradients, based on just two function evaluations.
We are able to prove the convergence of the Stochastic Heavy Ball method
under each of these bat updating methods, with the use of either noisy
or approximate gradients.
Because our proof is based on some mild generalizations of the
well-known Robbins-Siegmund theorem
[
7
]
,
in principle our work will work with other optimization methods as well.
Our current plan is to extend the present results to methods such as
ADAM and RMSPROP, by adapting the methods of
[
40
]
.

We have also carried out a series of numerical computations to demonstrate the
following tentative conclusions:

•

When batch updating is applied to noisy gradients, methods such as
ADAM, NADAM, and RMSPROP outperform Stochastic versions of pure gradient
descent, Heavy Ball, and two variants of Nesterov’s method.

•

However, when batch updating is applied to approximate gradients,
Nesterov’s original method diverges, while ADAM, NADAM, and RMSPROP
barely show any reduction in the objective function.
In fact, they perform worse than the plain steepest descent.
On the other hand, Stochastic Heavy Ball performs the best.

Therefore further theoretical analysis is required to explore
why this is so.
