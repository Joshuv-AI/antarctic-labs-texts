High-Order Integration on regular triangulated manifolds reaches Super-Algebraic Approximation Rates through Cubical Re-parameterizations Thanks:
Gentian Zavalani, Oliver Sander, Michael Hecht

Abstract
We present a novel methodology for deriving high-order quadrature rules (HOSQ) designed for the integration of scalar functions over regular embedded manifolds. To construct the rules, we introduce square-squeezing —a homeomorphic multilinear hypercube-simplex transformation—reparametrizing an initial flat triangulation of the manifold to a hypercube mesh. By employing square-squeezing, we approximate the integrand and the volume element for each hypercube domain of the reparameterized mesh through interpolation in Chebyshev–Lobatto grids. This strategy circumvents the Runge phenomenon, replacing the initial integral with a closed-form expression that can be precisely computed by high-order quadratures. We prove novel bounds of the integration error in terms of the r th r^{\text{th}} -order total variation of the integrand and the surface parameterization, predicting high algebraic approximation rates that scale solely with the interpolation degree and not, as is common, with the average simplex size. For smooth integrals whose total variation is constantly bounded with increasing r r , the estimates prove the integration error to decrease even exponentially, while mesh refinements are limited to achieve algebraic rates. The resulting approximation power is demonstrated in several numerical experiments, particularly showcasing p-refinements to overcome the limitations of h-refinements for highly varying smooth integrals.

Given a compact, orientable,

C1)

1.2
Related work

The importance of computing integrals on manifolds
is reflected in the large number of articles addressing this subject. Approaches might be divided into mesh-free methods, requiring a partition of unity, and mesh-based methods. A comprehensive review of the entire literature is beyond the scope of this article. The following list highlights specific contributions that may directly relate to or complement our work.

R1)

The strength of mesh-free approaches, such as
moving least squares
, comes from their ability to approximate integrals with discontinuities for arbitrary function data. However, limitations are the stability of the involved regression methods
[
49
]
and the computational cost for computing a proper partition of unity. We recommend Belytschko et al.
[

]

Ray et al.
[

realise

High-Order Integration over Discrete (Triangulated) Surfaces

-order surface approximations. While the stabilized least-square regression avoids Runge’s phenomenon the computational costs rapidly increase with the order of the approximation.
Recent extensions
[
39
]

address the task of computing integrals over non-parametrized surfaces.

R3)

Piecewise polynomial approximations of regular hyper-surfaces
S=l^{-1}(0)

in

are studied by Dziuk and Elliot
[
19
]
. Realizations are given by Demlow
[

]
, Chien

, and Praetorius and Stenger

]
.
However, all approaches rest on interpolation in equidistant nodes

Consequently, they are sensitive to Runge’s phenomenon and become unstable for high orders. An extended investigation of the error analysis provided by

,

,

is given in
[

]
.

Reeger et al.
[

propose to use local radial basis function-generated finite differences (RBD-FD) for efficiently generating quadrature weights for arbitrary node sets. This enables to approximate surface integrals for any given function data.

While

In contrast, HOSQ’s rates are unspecified, but shown to reach super-algebraic up to exponential convergence.

3
Approximation theory on hypercubes

We now construct stable polynomial approximations of the
geometry functions
\varrho_{i}:\triangle_{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
For this, we re-parametrize them to functions
on the cube
\varphi_{i}=\varrho_{i}\circ\sigma:\square_{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
and approximate those using interpolation with
tensor-product polynomials. The resulting approximation
can be pulled back to the triangle domain
via
\sigma^{-1}:\triangle_{d}\to\square_{d}
.

3.1
Interpolation in the hypercube

Throughout this chapter,
f
is a generic
function on the standard square
\square_{d}
.
Afterwards,
f
may play the role of the coordinate functions of
the geometry parametrizations
\varphi_{i}

or pull-backs
f\circ\varphi_{i}
of the integrand
f:S\to\mathbb{R}
.

We consider interpolation in tensorial grids.

Definition 11
(Interpolation grid).

For numbers
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
let
P_{1},\dots,P_{d}\subseteq[-1,1]

be sets of size
|P_{i}|=n+1
each.
Then we call
G_{d,n}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}P_{i}
an interpolation grid.
For any multi-index
\alpha\in A_{d,n}

we denote with
p_{\alpha}=(p_{\alpha_{1},1},\dots,p_{\alpha_{d},d})\in G_{d,n}
,
p_{\alpha_{i},i}\in P_{i}
,
the corresponding grid node of
G_{d,n}
.

We use such a grid to define the corresponding
interpolation operator
Q_{G_{d,n}}:C^{0}(\square_{d})\to\Pi_{d,n}
,
f\mapsto Q_{G_{d,n}}f
, uniquely determined by
Q_{G_{d,n}}f(p_{\alpha})=f(p_{\alpha})

for all
p_{\alpha}\in G_{d,n}
. For an explicit representation, we generalize
one-dimensional Newton and Lagrange interpolation
to multivariate interpolation
on the grids
G_{d,n}

[
10
,
13
,
29
,
31
,
30
,
32
]
.

Definition 12
(Lagrange and Newton polynomials).

Let
G_{d,n}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}P_{i}
be an interpolation grid
indexed by a multi-index set
A_{d,n}
.
For each
\alpha\in A_{d,n}
the tensorial multivariate Lagrange polynomial is

L_{\alpha}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}l_{\alpha_{i},i}(x)\,,\quad l_{j,i}(x)=\prod_{k=0,k\not=j}^{n}\frac{x_{i}-p_{k,i}}{p_{j,i}-p_{k,i}}\,.

(17)

The
\alpha
-th tensorial multivariate Newton polynomial is

N_{\alpha}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\prod_{j=0}^{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i}-p_{j,i})\,,\quad p_{j,i}\in P_{i}\,.

(18)

Both the Lagrange and Newton polynomials form bases of the polynomial space
\Pi_{d,n}
induced by
A_{d,n}
.
As the
L_{\alpha}
satisfy

L_{\alpha}(p_{\beta})=\delta_{\alpha,\beta}

for all
\alpha\in A_{d,n}
,
p_{\beta}\in G_{d,n}

we deduce that
given a function
f:\square_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, the interpolant
Q_{G_{d,n}}f\in\Pi_{d,n}

can be computed as

Q_{G_{d,n}}f=\sum_{\alpha\in A_{d,n}}f(p_{\alpha})L_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha\in A_{d,n}}b_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}\,,

(19)

where the coefficients
b_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}

of the Newton interpolation can be computed
in closed form.
While Lagrange interpolation is primarily of theoretical
interest, the Newton form allows efficient and stable
evaluations of
Q_{G_{d,n}}f
at any point
\mathrm{x}\in\square_{d}
. In particular, recent results in

[

,

,

,

,
35
]
enable to
extend (
19
) for any choice of downward closed set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{d}
, including the case of any
l_{p}
-degree e.g. total
l_{1}
-degree.

The approximation power of polynomial interpolation is measured by
the
Lebesgue constant
—the operator norm
of the interpolation operator

Q_{G_{d,n}}:C^{0}(\square_{d})\to\Pi_{d,n}
given by

\Lambda(G_{d,n})=\|Q_{G_{d,n}}\|=\sup_{g\in C^{0}(\square_{d})}\frac{\|Q_{G_{d,n}}g\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}}{\|g\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}}=\Big\|\sum_{\alpha\in A_{d,n}}|L_{\alpha}|\Big\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}\,.

(20)

In the case of a one-dimensional interpolation domain
\square_{1}=[-1,1]

and the Chebyshev–Lobatto grid

\mathrm{Cheb}_{n}=\left\{\cos\Big(\frac{k\pi}{n}\Big)\;:\;0\leq k\leq n\right\}\,,

(21)

the Lebesgue constant
\Lambda(\mathrm{Cheb}_{n})
increases slowly as
n\to\infty

Indeed,

\Lambda(\mathrm{Cheb}_{n})=\frac{2}{\pi}\big(\log(n+1)+\gamma+\log(8/\pi)\big)+\mathcal{O}(1/n^{2})\,,

(22)

where

is the Euler–Mascheroni constant, see
[

,

,

,

,

,
6
]
, surveyed by
[

]
, see also
[

]
.
We extend this estimate to the

-dimensional case:

Lemma 13
.

The Lebesgue constant of the
d
-dimensional
Chebyshev–Lobatto grid

\mathrm{Cheb}_{d,n}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{Cheb}_{n}

is
\Lambda(\mathrm{Cheb}_{d,n})\leq\Lambda(\mathrm{Cheb}_{n})^{d}\in\mathcal{O}(\log(n+1)^{d})
.

Proof.

We consider the tensorial Lagrange polynomials
L_{\alpha}(\mathrm{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}l_{\alpha_{i},i}(x_{i})

in the Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes,
with
l_{j,i}
given in (
17
) and obtain

\displaystyle\Lambda(\mathrm{Cheb}_{d,n})

\displaystyle=\Big\|\sum_{\alpha\in A_{d,n}}|L_{\alpha}|\Big\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}\leq\Big\lVert\sum_{\alpha\in A_{d,n}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|l_{\alpha_{i},i}|\Big\rVert_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}

(23)

\displaystyle=\Big\lVert\big(\sum_{j=0}^{n}|l_{j,1}|\big)\cdots\big(\sum_{j=0}^{n}|l_{j,l}|\big)\ \cdots\big(\sum_{j=0}^{n}|l_{j,d}|\big)\Big\rVert_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}\,,\,\,\,1<l<d

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d}\Big\lVert\sum_{j=0}^{n}|l_{j,i}|\Big\rVert_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\Lambda(\mathrm{Cheb}_{n})\,.

With (
22
), this yields
\Lambda(\mathrm{Cheb}_{d,n})\leq\Lambda(\mathrm{Cheb}_{n})^{d}\in\mathcal{O}(\log(n+1)^{d})
.

3.2
Approximation errors in terms of the
r^{\text{th}}
total variation

We give a
d
-dimen- sional generalization of known error estimates with respect to the
r^{\text{th}}
total variation
.
We start with a multivariate version of a classic one-dimensional approximation result
[
66
]
.

Theorem 14
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
r\geq 0
, and
f
be of bounded
r^{\text{th}}
total variation, Definition
1
.
Then
f
can be expanded in a Chebyshev series

f(\mathrm{x})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}c_{\alpha}T_{\alpha_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots T_{\alpha_{d}}(x_{d})\,,\quad\text{with}\quad|c_{\alpha}|\leq\frac{4V_{f,r}}{\pi(q-r)^{r+1}}\,,

(24)

whenever
q=\|\alpha\|_{\infty}\geq r+1
.

Proof.

We recall that the Chebyshev polynomials
T_{\alpha}(\mathrm{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}T_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i})

are an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\square_{d})
with respect to the weighted

L^{2}
inner product with weight function
\omega_{d}(\mathrm{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}}
. Due to
[
41
, Theorem 4.1]
, any Lipschitz continuous function
f:\square_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
has a uniformly and absolutely convergent multivariate Chebyshev series
with coefficients

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{d}}{\pi^{d}}\int_{\square_{d}}\omega(\mathrm{x})f(\mathrm{x})T_{\alpha}(\mathrm{x})\,d\mathrm{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x_{d}^{2}}}T_{\alpha_{d}}(x_{d})\Big(\cdots\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}}f(\mathrm{x})T_{\alpha_{1}}(x_{1})dx_{1}\Big)\dots\Big)dx_{d}\,,

(25)

for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}\geq 1
,
and with
2/\pi
replaced by
1/\pi
in (
25
) if
\alpha_{i}=0
for some
1\leq i\leq d
.
By reordering we can assume w.l.o.g. that
\alpha_{d}=\|\alpha\|_{\infty}\geq r+1
. Following the argumentation of Theorems 7.1, 7.2 in
[
66
]
yields

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\Big|\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\omega_{1}(x_{d})g(x_{d})T_{\alpha_{d}}(x_{d})\,dx_{d}\Big|\leq\frac{2V_{g,r}}{\pi(q-r)^{r+1}}\,,

with

\displaystyle g(x_{d})

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{d-1}}{\pi^{d-1}}\int_{\square_{d-1}}\omega_{d-1}(\mathrm{y})f(\mathrm{y},x_{d})T_{\beta}(\mathrm{y})\,d\mathrm{y}\,,

where
\beta=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d-1})
,
\mathrm{y}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, and
V_{g,r}
denotes the
r^{\text{th}}
total variation of
g
.
From Definition
1
we observe that
V_{g,r}\leq V_{f,r}
, which yields the assertion.

We use this result in order to control the truncation error of the Chebyshev series.

Corollary 15
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
14
be fulfilled. We denote with

\mathcal{T}_{f,n}(\mathrm{x})=\sum_{\alpha\in A_{d,n}}c_{\alpha}T_{\alpha_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots T_{\alpha_{d}}(x_{d})

(26)

the truncated Chebyshev series of
f:\square_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to
A_{d,n}
, with
n>r
.

i)

The truncation error is bounded by

\|f-\mathcal{T}_{f,n}\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}\leq\frac{2d(n+1)V_{f,r}}{\pi r(n-r)^{r}}\in\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-(r-1)}\big)\,.

(27)

ii)

The truncation
error of the first-order partial derivatives is bounded by

\|\partial_{x_{i}}f-\partial_{x_{i}}\mathcal{T}_{f,n}\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}\leq\frac{2d(n+1)n^{2}V_{f,r}}{\pi(r-2)(n-r)^{r}}\in\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-(r-3)}\big)\,,\quad\forall i=1,\dots,d\,.

(28)

Proof.

i)
directly follows from Theorem
14
:
Since
T_{k}(\cos(x))=\cos(kx)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
we observe that
\|T_{k}\|_{C^{0}([-1,1])}\leq 1
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\|f-\mathcal{T}_{f,n}\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\setminus A_{d,n}}c_{\alpha}\|T_{\alpha_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots T_{\alpha_{d}}(x_{d})\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}\leq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\setminus A_{d,n}}|c_{\alpha}|

\displaystyle\leq d\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{2V_{f,r}}{\pi(k-r)^{r+1}}\leq d(n+1)\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{2V_{f,r}}{\pi(x-r)^{r+1}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{2d(n+1)V_{f,r}}{\pi r(n-r)^{r}}\,.

We show
ii)
for the partial derivative
\partial_{x_{1}}
by writing

\lVert\partial_{x_{1}}f-\partial_{x_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{f,n}\rVert_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}\leq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\setminus A_{d,n}}|c_{\alpha}|\lVert T^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}\rVert_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}\lVert T_{\alpha_{2}}\cdots T_{\alpha_{2}}\rVert_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}\,.

We recall that
T_{k}(x)=\cos{\left(k\arccos(x\right))}
for
-1\leq x\leq 1
and deduce
that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

T^{\prime}_{k}(x)=\frac{k\sin{\left(k\arccos\left(x\right)\right)}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}=\frac{k\sin{\left(kt\right)}}{\sin{\left(t\right)}}\,,\qquad t=\arccos(x)\,,

(29)

yielding
\|T^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}\|_{C^{0}(\square_{m})}=\alpha_{1}^{2}
.
Following
i)
, we compute

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{x_{1}}f-\partial_{x_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{f,n}\rVert_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\setminus A_{d,n}}|c_{\alpha}|\alpha_{1}^{2}\leq d\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{2V_{f,r}k^{2}}{\pi(k-r)^{r+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2d(n+1)V_{f,r}}{\pi}\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2}}{(k-r)^{r+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2d(n+1)V_{f,r}}{\pi}\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{2}}{(n-r)^{2}}\frac{1}{(k-r)^{r-1}}

(30)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2d(n+1)n^{2}V_{f,r}}{\pi(n-r)^{2}}\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(x-r)^{r-1}}d\mathrm{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{2d(n+1)n^{2}V_{f,r}}{\pi(n-r)^{2}}\frac{1}{(r-2)(n-r)^{r-2}}\,,

where we have used
\frac{k^{2}}{(k-r)^{2}}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(n-r)^{2}}
for
k\geq n\geq r+1
in (
30
).
The estimates for the derivatives
\partial_{x_{2}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}}

can be derived analogously.

With the previous results, we can bound the
approximation error of the Chebyshev–Lobatto interpolant of
f
.

Corollary 16
.

Let the assumption of Theorem
14
be satisfied and

Q_{G_{d,n}}f
be the interpolant of
f:\square_{d}\to\mathbb{R}

in the Chebyshev–Lobatto grid
\mathrm{Cheb}_{d,n}

Then the approximation errors of

and its first derivatives are bounded by

\displaystyle\|f-Q_{G_{d,n}}f\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f-\mathcal{T}_{f,n}\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}

(31)

and

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x_{i}}f-\partial_{x_{i}}Q_{G_{d,n}}f\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\partial_{x_{i}}f-\partial_{x_{i}}\mathcal{T}_{f,n}\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}\,,

(32)

for all
i=1,\dots,d
.

Proof.

The statement is a direct consequence of Theorem
14
, Corollary
15
, and the [Aliasing Theorems 4.1, 4.2]

f(\mathrm{x})-Q_{G_{d,n}}f(\mathrm{x})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\setminus A_{d,n}}c_{\alpha}\big(T_{\alpha_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots T_{\alpha_{d}}(x_{d})-T_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots T_{\beta_{d}}(x_{d})\big)\,,

is

\Big|\int_{S}f\,dS-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\int_{\square_{d}}Q_{G_{d,n}}(f\circ\varphi_{i})(\mathrm{x})\sqrt{\det\big((DQ_{G_{d,k}}\varphi_{i}(\mathrm{x}))^{T}DQ_{G_{d,k}}\varphi_{i}(\mathrm{x})\big)}\,d\mathrm{x}\Big|\\
\leq\varepsilon_{f}\mathrm{vol}(S)+\varepsilon_{f}\varepsilon_{\varphi}\mathrm{vol}(\square_{d})+\|f\|_{C^{0}(S)}\varepsilon_{\varphi}\,\mathrm{vol}(\square_{d})=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{n^{r-1}}\Big)+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{k^{r-3}}\Big)\,,

where
\mathrm{vol}(S)
and
\mathrm{vol}(\square_{d})
denote the volumes of
S
and
\square_{d}
, respectively, and

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{f}=\frac{4d(n+1)V_{f,r}}{\pi r(n-r)^{r}}\,,\qquad\varepsilon_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=d!\,\max\big\{\phi,\mu_{\varphi}\big\}^{d}\,,\qquad\mu_{\varphi}=\frac{4d(k+1)k^{2}V_{\varphi,r}}{\pi(r-2)(k-r)^{r}}\,,

with
\phi=\max_{i=1,\cdots,K}\|D\varphi_{i}\|_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}
and

V_{\varphi,r}=\max_{i=1,\cdots,K}V_{\varphi_{i},r}
.

Proof.

We set
\Phi_{i}=(D\varphi_{i}^{T}D\varphi_{i})^{1/2}
,
\Psi_{i}=(DQ_{G_{d,k}}\varphi_{i}^{T}DQ_{G_{d,k}}\varphi_{i})^{1/2}
, apply the Cauchy– Schwarz inequality, and estimate

\Big|\int_{S}f\,dS-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\int_{\square_{d}}Q_{G_{d,n}}(f\circ\varphi_{i})(\mathrm{x})\sqrt{\det(\Phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}))}\,d\mathrm{x}\Big|\\
\begin{aligned} &\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}\int_{\square_{d}}\big|f(\varphi_{i}(\mathrm{x}))-Q_{G_{d,n}}(f\circ\varphi_{i})(\mathrm{x})\big|\sqrt{\det(\Psi_{i}(\mathrm{x}))}\,d\mathrm{x}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\int_{\square_{d}}\big|f(\varphi_{i}(\mathrm{x}))-Q_{G_{d,n}}(f\circ\varphi_{i})(\mathrm{x})\big|\cdot\big\lVert\sqrt{\det(\Phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}))}-\sqrt{\det(\Psi_{i}(\mathrm{x}))}\big\rVert_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}\,d\mathrm{x}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\int_{\square_{d}}\big|f(\varphi_{i}(\mathrm{x}))\big|\cdot\big\lVert\sqrt{\det(\Phi_{i}(\mathrm{x}))}-\sqrt{\det(\Psi_{i}(\mathrm{x}))}\big\rVert_{C^{0}(\square_{d})}\,d\mathrm{x}\\
&\leq\varepsilon_{f}\mathrm{vol}(S)+\varepsilon_{f}\varepsilon_{\varphi}\mathrm{vol}(\square_{d})+\|f\|_{C^{0}(S)}\varepsilon_{\varphi}\mathrm{vol}(\square_{d})\,.\end{aligned}

The estimates for
\varepsilon_{f}
,
\varepsilon_{\varphi}
,
\mu_{\varphi}
follow from
Corollary
16
and
Lemma
18
, concluding the proof.

The approximated integral can now be computed using a
quadrature rule. There are two basic options: Either use
a quadrature rule for the cube domain
\square_{d}
directly,
or use a simplex rule and pull it back to
\square_{d}
by the
inverse of the square-squeezing map
\sigma_{*}

(effectively integrating over the original triangulation

\{\varrho_{i}\}
of
S
from Definition
2
).
While the former seems more natural, the latter is
more efficient, as simplex rules typically consist of
fewer points.

Corollary 20
(Quadrature rule error).

Under the assumptions of Theorem
19
denote with
\Phi_{i}=D\varphi_{i}^{T}D\varphi_{i}
,
\Psi_{i}=DQ_{G_{d,k}}\varphi_{i}^{T}DQ_{G_{d,k}}\varphi_{i}
, and

the polynomial approximations of

and
\varphi_{i}
of

-degree
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there is
0<v<1
, independent of
l
, such that for
1\leq l\in\mathbb{N}
large enough:

i)

Let

\mathrm{p}\in P,\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
be the points and weights of the tensorial Gauss–Legendre quadrature on

[

]
of order
N\in\mathbb{N}
, integrating any polynomial
Q\in\Pi_{d,M}
of

exactly. Then

=\sum_{\mathrm{p}\in P}\omega_{\mathrm{p}}Q_{G_{d,n}}f(\mathrm{p})\sqrt{\det\big((DQ_{k}\varphi_{i}(\mathrm{p}))^{T}DQ_{k}\varphi_{i}(\mathrm{p})\big)}+\mathcal{O}(v^{l+1})\,.

(38)

ii)

Let

be a cube–simplex transformation diffeomorphic in the interior

,

be the

(39)

with

Proof.

To prove

and rewrite:

where

. Hence,
\int_{\square_{d}}Q_{G_{d,n}}f(\mathrm{x})Q(\mathrm{x})d\mathrm{x}=\sum_{\mathrm{p}\in P}\omega_{\mathrm{p}}Q_{G_{d,n}}f(\mathrm{p})Q_{l}(\mathrm{p})
can be computed exactly due to the Gauss–Legendre quadrature of order

Consequently,
i)
is proven. Now

follows from
i)
by

\int_{\triangle_{d}}Q_{G_{d,n}}f(\sigma^{-1}(\mathrm{y}))\sqrt{\det\big((D\varrho_{i}(\mathrm{y}))^{T}D\varrho_{i}(\mathrm{y})\big)}\,d\mathrm{y}\\
\begin{aligned} &=\int_{\triangle_{d}}Q_{G_{d,n}}f(\sigma^{-1}(\mathrm{y}))\sqrt{\det\big((D\sigma^{-1}(\mathrm{y}))^{T}(D\varphi_{i}(\sigma^{-1}(\mathrm{y})))^{T}D\varphi_{i}(\sigma^{-1}(\mathrm{y}))D\sigma^{-1}(\mathrm{y})\big)}\,d\mathrm{y}\\

proving the statement.

Mesh quality

Since the integration error measured here involves in particular the error of approximating the geometry by polynomials it is reasonable to ask whether the integration error depends on the quality of the triangulation of
S
.
To investigate this, we repeat the Gauss-Bonnet validation one final time for the torus of Fig.
5(b)
.

We generate two meshes for the torus geometry,
both with
1232
triangles, shown in
Fig.
16
together with
a plot giving the distributions of the interior
angles. One of the grids is of high quality,
with all angles near
60^{\circ}
. The second mesh
was deliberately constructed to be of low quality,
featuring a wide range of angles, and even
triangles with inverted orientation.

We evaluate the performance of DCG and HOSQ based on square-squeezing and on Duffy’s transformation, when exploiting (pull-back) quadrature rules of order
14
.

The results are given in Fig.
16(d)
.
Neither HOSQ nor DCG seem to
seriously depend on the mesh quality.
As usual, HOSQ converges faster than DCG, and it
converges all the way to the machine precision
limit. DCG shows the same behavior as in all prior experiments,
becoming unstable for geometry approximation orders
beyond
8
.

6
Outlook

For extending the HOSQ to integration tasks on non-parameterized surfaces, we aim to use the
global polynomial level set
method (GPLS)
[
67
]
, developed by ourselves. GPLS delivers the required machine–precision–close implicit parameterization
S=l^{-1}(0)
for a broad class surfaces
S
, being only known in a set of sample points.
In combination with the regression techniques in
[
68
]
this will enable the computation of surface integrals if in addition, the integrand is only known at a priori given data points.

Our quadrilateral re-parameterizarion due to square-squeezing suggests that the proposed method has the potential to substantially contribute to triangular spectral element methods (TSEM)
[
37
,
34
]
, realizing fast spectral PDE solvers on surfaces
[
21
]
.
