Spectral properties of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator for spheroids
Denis S. Grebenkov

Abstract
We study the spectral properties of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator and the related Steklov problem in spheroidal domains ranging from a needle to a disk. An explicit matrix representation of this operator for both interior and exterior problems is derived. We show how the anisotropy of spheroids affects the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the operator. As examples of physical applications, we discuss diffusion-controlled reactions on spheroidal partially reactive targets and the statistics of encounters between the diffusing particle and the spheroidal boundary.

The spectral properties of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator and the
related Steklov problem were thoroughly investigated for Euclidean
domains and Riemannian manifolds (see the recent book
Levitin

and reviews

;

). We focus here on the most
basic setting of an Euclidean domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with a
smooth bounded boundary
\partial\Omega
. The Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
{\mathcal{M}}

is defined as a map of a function

on the
boundary
\partial\Omega
onto another function

on that
boundary such that

, where

\partial_{n}
is the normal derivative oriented outwards the domain

\Omega
, and

is the unique solution of the Dirichlet boundary
value problem:

\Delta u=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,\qquad u=f\quad\textrm{on}~\partial\Omega,

(1)

with

being the Laplace operator (here

are appropriate functional spaces, see Ref.

mathematical details and references). In other words, the operator

in the space

Steklov problem,

(3)

where the Steklov eigenvalues standing in the boundary condition are
identical to
\mu_{k}

eigenfunction
V_{k}
can be obtained as a harmonic extension of the
eigenfunction
v_{k}
of
{\mathcal{M}}

Matrix representation

A general solution of the Laplace equation reads

u(\alpha,\theta,\phi)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{m=-n}^{n}A_{mn}Q_{n}^{m}(\cosh\alpha)Y_{mn}(\theta,\phi),

(7)

where
A_{mn}
are arbitrary coefficients,
Q_{n}^{m}(z)
are the
associated Legendre functions of the second kind (see Appendix

B
), and

\displaystyle Y_{mn}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=a_{mn}P_{n}^{m}(\cos\theta)e^{im\phi},

(8)

\displaystyle a_{mn}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{4\pi}\,\frac{(n-m)!}{(n+m)!}}

are the normalized spherical harmonics, with
P_{n}^{m}(x)
being the
associated Legendre polynomials. The normal derivative reads

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=-\left.\frac{1}{h_{\alpha}}\partial_{\alpha}u\right|_{\alpha=\alpha_{0}}

(9)

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{m=-n}^{n}A_{mn}Y_{mn}(\theta,\phi)\frac{\sinh\alpha_{0}\,Q^{{}^{\prime}m}_{n}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}{a_{E}\sqrt{\cosh^{2}\alpha_{0}-\cos^{2}\theta}}\,,

where prime denotes the derivative with respect to the argument. In
other words, for a square-integrable function
f
on
\partial\Omega
, decomposed
on the complete basis of spherical harmonics,

f(\theta,\phi)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{m=-n}^{n}f_{mn}Y_{mn}(\theta,\phi)

(10)

(with coefficients
f_{mn}=A_{mn}Q_{n}^{m}(\cosh\alpha_{0})
), the action
of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
{\mathcal{M}}
is

[{\mathcal{M}}f](\theta,\phi)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{m=-n}^{n}f_{mn}\frac{c_{mn}\,Y_{mn}(\theta,\phi)}{\sqrt{\cosh^{2}\alpha_{0}-\cos^{2}\theta}}\,,

(11)

where

c_{mn}=-\frac{\sinh\alpha_{0}\,Q^{{}^{\prime}m}_{n}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}{a_{E}Q_{n}^{m}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}\,.

(12)

At the same time, the function
{\mathcal{M}}f
can also be decomposed on the
complete basis of
Y_{mn}
:

[{\mathcal{M}}f](\theta,\phi)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{m=-n}^{n}g_{mn}Y_{mn}(\theta,\phi).

(13)

Its coefficients

can be obtained by multiplying

11
) by
Y_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\sin\theta

integrating over
\theta
and
\phi

\displaystyle g_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sin\theta\,Y_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle\times\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{m=-n}^{n}f_{mn}\frac{c_{mn}\,Y_{mn}(\theta,\phi)}{\sqrt{\cosh^{2}\alpha_{0}-\cos^{2}\theta}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{m=-n}^{n}f_{mn}{\bf M}_{mn,m^{\prime}n^{\prime}},

where

{\bf M}_{mn,m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}=2\pi\,\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}a_{mn}a_{mn^{\prime}}\,c_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\,F_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha_{0}),

(14)

F_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(z)=\int\limits_{-1}^{1}dx\frac{P_{n}^{m}(x)P_{n^{\prime}}^{m}(x)}{\sqrt{z^{2}-x^{2}}}\,.

(15)

{\bf M}

are enumerated by double indices

employ the following order:

\begin{array}[]{c c c c c c c c c c c}mn:&00&(-1)1&01&11&(-2)2&(-1)2&02&12&22&\ldots\\

{\bf M}

Limit of a sphere

In the limit

, the prolate spheroid approaches the sphere of
radius
b
. In this limit, one has
a_{E}\to 0
and
\alpha_{0}\to\infty
such that

remains constant. As a
consequence, the coefficients in Eq. (
12

, whereas the
matrix elements in Eq. (
15
) behave in the leading order as

F_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha_{0})\approx\frac{\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}}{2\pi a_{mn}^{2}\cosh\alpha_{0}}\,,

(26)

implying that
{\bf M}_{mn,m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\to\tfrac{n+1}{b}\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}
. The diagonal structure of this matrix yields

\mu_{mn}=\frac{n+1}{b}\,,\qquad v_{mn}=\frac{1}{b}\,Y_{mn}(\theta,\phi).

(27)

We retrieve therefore the well-known eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator for the exterior of a sphere. Note
that the eigenvalues do not depend on

ranges
from
-n
to

, the degeneracy of the eigenvalue
\mu_{mn}
is

illustrated below, the anisotropy of spheroids breaks this symmetry
and reduces the degeneracy of eigenvalues.

Numerical implementation

For a practical implementation, the infinite-dimensional matrix
{\bf M}

has to be truncated to a finite size. In a basic setup, one can
choose the truncation order
n_{\rm max}
to keep
n=0,1,2,\ldots,n_{\rm max}
,
and then construct the truncated matrix of size
(n_{\rm max}+1)^{2}\times(n_{\rm max}+1)^{2}
, as detailed in Appendix
B
. A
numerical diagonalization of the truncated matrix provides an
approximation for a number of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
{\mathcal{M}}
.
As illustrated below, the accuracy of this approximation increases
rapidly with the truncation order
n_{\rm max}
.

A much faster procedure consists in dealing with the reduced matrices

\hat{{\bf M}}_{m}

{\bf M}_{m}
by removing zero
columns and rows. For instance, the matrix

from

\hat{{\bf M}}_{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c c}{\bf M}_{00,00}&0&{\bf M}_{00,02}&\ldots\\
0&{\bf M}_{01,01}&0&\ldots\\
{\bf M}_{02,00}&0&{\bf M}_{02,02}&\ldots\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
\end{array}\right).

(28)

In practice, we start from the truncated matrix
{\bf M}
of size

(n_{\rm max}+1)^{2}\times(n_{\rm max}+1)^{2}
and then dispatch its columns and
rows according to
m
ranging from
0
to
n_{\rm max}
, into matrices

\hat{{\bf M}}_{m}
. As the reduced matrix
\hat{{\bf M}}_{m}
is of much smaller
size
(n_{\rm max}+1-m)\times(n_{\rm max}+1-m)
, its numerical diagonalization
is significantly faster. Its eigenvalues approximate the eigenvalues

\mu_{mn}
of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
{\mathcal{M}}
; in turn, its
eigenvectors determine the eigenfunctions
v_{mn}
of
{\mathcal{M}}
via
Eq. (
24a
), and the Steklov eigenfunctions
V_{mn}

via Eq. (
24b
). The former eigenfunctions are
orthogonal to each other by construction. In turn, one needs to
impose their normalization according to Eq. (
18
).
We recall that this normalization is fixed up to an arbitrary phase
factor
e^{i\alpha}
. Further simplifications can be achieved for the
axisymmetric problem, see Appendix
B.5
.
It is worth noting that the reduced matrices
\hat{{\bf M}}_{m}
form a
block-diagonal matrix

\hat{{\bf M}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c c c}\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
\ldots&\hat{{\bf M}}_{-1}&0&0&\ldots\\
\ldots&0&\hat{{\bf M}}_{0}&0&\ldots\\
\ldots&0&0&\hat{{\bf M}}_{1}&\ldots\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
\end{array}\right),

(29)

which can be seen as a transformation of the original matrix
{\bf M}
by
re-ordering its columns and rows. Since these two matrices have the
same eigenvalues, it is much faster to search for the eigenvalues of
the matrix
\hat{{\bf M}}
, which are obtained by combining the
eigenvalues of its diagonal blocks
\hat{{\bf M}}_{m}
.

To illustrate the fast convergence of the numerical method, we choose
the prolate spheroid with semi-axes
a=0.5
and
b=1
and compute
three eigenvalues
\mu_{00}(n_{\rm max})
,
\mu_{01}(n_{\rm max})
, and

\mu_{02}(n_{\rm max})
of the truncated matrix
{\bf M}
of size
(n_{\rm max}+1)^{2}\times(n_{\rm max}+1)^{2}
as functions of the truncation order
n_{\rm max}
.
To estimate the truncation error, we subtract from
\mu_{0n}(n_{\rm max})

its value at
n_{\rm max}=20
, considered as a proxy of the limiting value

\mu_{0n}(\infty)
. Figure
2
shows how fast the
error decreases with
n_{\rm max}
. We checked the rapid convergence for
other values of
m
and
n
, as well as for various aspect ratios (not
shown). In particular, we found that larger truncation orders may be
needed to achieve high accuracy when
a/b
gets smaller. Moreover,
when
a/b
is close to
1
, the matrix elements

F_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha_{0})
vanish according to
Eq. (
26
) that requires a suitable rescaling and
further improvements of the numerical procedure described in Appendix

B
. Since the case of a sphere is fully explicit, we
do not study geometric settings of almost spherical domains.

A similar error analysis was performed for other problems (interior of
a prolate spheroid, oblate spheroids, see below). In the following,
we generally use
n_{\rm max}=10
that is sufficient to produce accurate
numerical results for all the considered settings.

(31)

Reflection symmetry

Since the domain
\Omega
is symmetric under reflection with respect
to the horizontal plane
z=0
, the Steklov eigenfunctions inherit
this symmetry. In fact, if
V(x,y,z)
is a Steklov eigenfunction,
corresponding to an eigenvalue
\mu
, then
V(x,y,-z)
is also an
eigenfunction corresponding to the same
\mu
. Moreover, their linear
combinations
V_{\pm}(x,y,z)=V(x,y,z)\pm V(x,y,-z)
are also
eigenfunctions, if they are not zero. There are thus three options:
(i)
V(x,y,z)
is symmetric:
V(x,y,z)=V(x,y,-z)
; (ii)
V(x,y,z)

is antisymmetric:
V(x,y,z)=-V(x,y,-z)
; or (iii)
V(x,y,z)
is
neither symmetric, nor antisymmetric, in which case
V_{+}(x,y,z)
and

V_{-}(x,y,z)
are symmetric and antisymmetric, respectively.

The structure of the matrices
{\bf M}_{m}
implies that the Steklov
eigenfunctions
V_{mn}(\alpha,\theta,\phi)
, determined by
Eq. (
24b
), are symmetric (resp., antisymmetric) under
reflection with respect to the horizontal plane
z=0
when
m+n
is
even (resp., odd). Indeed, the relation (
25
)
causes that the matrix elements
{\bf M}_{mn,mn^{\prime}}
are zero when
n+n^{\prime}
is
odd. As a consequence, the elements
[{\bf V}_{mn}]_{mn^{\prime}}
of its
eigenvector
{\bf V}_{mn}
are zero when
n+n^{\prime}
is odd, so that

v_{mn}(\pi-\theta,\phi)=\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}[{\bf V}_{mn}]_{mn^{\prime}}(-1)^{m+n^{\prime}}Y_{mn^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi),

where we used
Y_{mn^{\prime}}(\pi-\theta,\phi)=(-1)^{m+n^{\prime}}Y_{mn^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi)
according to Eq. (
25
). As
the terms with odd
n+n^{\prime}
vanish, one concludes that

v_{mn}(\alpha,\pi-\theta,\phi)=(-1)^{m+n}v_{mn}(\theta,\phi).

(32)

This reflection symmetry is clearly illustrated in
Fig.
3
. The same symmetry is preserved for the
Steklov eigenfunctions:

V_{mn}(\alpha,\pi-\theta,\phi)=(-1)^{m+n}V_{mn}(\alpha,\theta,\phi).

(33)

Half-spheroid in the half-space

The above symmetries provide a complementary insight onto the Steklov
problem in the upper half-space. Let

\displaystyle\Omega_{+}

\displaystyle=\biggl\{(x,y,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}~:~\frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{y^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{z^{2}}{b^{2}}>1,~z>0\biggr\}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{(\alpha,\theta,\phi)~:~\alpha>\alpha_{0},~0\leq\theta<\pi/2,~0\leq\phi<2\pi\bigr\}

be the exterior of a prolate spheroid in the upper half-space (the
second line highlights that the upper half-space corresponds to
0\leq\theta<\pi/2
in the prolate spheroidal coordinates). The
boundary of this domain is the union of the upper half-spheroidal
surface,

\partial\Omega_{+}=\bigl\{(\alpha,\theta,\phi)~:~\alpha=\alpha_{0},~0\leq\theta<\pi/2,~0\leq\phi<2\pi\bigr\},

and the remaining horizontal plane at
z=0
with a circular hole of
radius
a
:

\partial\Omega_{0}=\bigl\{(\alpha,\theta,\phi)~:~\alpha>\alpha_{0},~\theta=\pi/2,~0\leq\phi<2\pi\bigr\}.

According to Eq. (
33
), antisymmetric Steklov
eigenfunctions
V_{mn}
(with odd
m+n
) vanish at
\theta=\pi/2

that corresponds to the Dirichlet boundary condition on
\partial\Omega_{0}
. As a
consequence, they solve the mixed Steklov-Dirichlet exterior problem:

\displaystyle\Delta V_{mn}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega_{+},

(34a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}V_{mn}

\displaystyle=\mu_{mn}V_{mn}\quad\textrm{on}~\partial\Omega_{+},

(34b)

\displaystyle V_{mn}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\partial\Omega_{0}

(34c)

(here the notation
\partial_{n}
for the normal derivative should not
be confused with the index
n
). Equivalently, one can speak of
eigenvalues
\mu_{mn}
and eigenfunctions
v_{mn}
of the
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
{\mathcal{M}}^{D}
that maps a given function
f
on
the half-spheroidal boundary
\partial\Omega_{+}
onto another function
g={\mathcal{M}}^{D}f=(\partial_{n}u)_{|\partial\Omega_{+}}
, where
u
satisfies

\Delta u=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega_{+},\qquad u|_{\partial\Omega_{+}}=f,\qquad u|_{\partial\Omega_{0}}=0.

(35)

For instance,
v_{01}
,
v_{03}
,
v_{12}
and
v_{23}
shown in
Fig.
3
are examples of eigenfunctions of
{\mathcal{M}}^{D}
.

In turn, the symmetric Steklov eigenfunctions
V_{mn}
(with even

m+n
) satisfy

\left.\bigl(\partial_{n}V_{mn}\bigr)\right|_{\partial\Omega_{0}}=\left.\biggl(\frac{1}{h_{\theta}}\,\partial_{\theta}V_{mn}\biggr)\right|_{\theta=\pi/2}=0

that corresponds to the Neumann boundary condition on
\partial\Omega_{0}
. In
other words, they solve the mixed Steklov-Neumann exterior problem:

\displaystyle\Delta V_{mn}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega_{+},

(36a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}V_{mn}

\displaystyle=\mu_{mn}V_{mn}\quad\textrm{on}~\partial\Omega_{+},

(36b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}V_{mn}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{on}~\partial\Omega_{0}.

(36c)

Equivalently, one can speak of eigenvalues
\mu_{mn}
and
eigenfunctions
v_{mn}
of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
{\mathcal{M}}^{N}

that maps a given function
f
on the half-spheroidal boundary
\partial\Omega_{+}

onto another function
g={\mathcal{M}}^{N}f=(\partial_{n}u)_{|\partial\Omega_{+}}
, where

u
satisfies

\Delta u=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega_{+},\qquad u|_{\partial\Omega_{+}}=f,\qquad(\partial_{n}u)|_{\partial\Omega_{0}}=0.

(37)

For instance,
v_{00}
,
v_{02}
,
v_{11}
,
v_{22}
,
v_{13}
and

v_{33}
shown in Fig.
3
are examples of
eigenfunctions of
{\mathcal{M}}^{N}
.

III
Oblate spheroids

For the exterior of an oblate spheroid with semi-axes
a\leq b
,

\Omega=\biggl\{(x,y,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}~:~\frac{x^{2}}{b^{2}}+\frac{y^{2}}{b^{2}}+\frac{z^{2}}{a^{2}}>1\biggr\},

(38)

the computation is very similar so that we only sketch the main steps
and formulas. In the oblate spheroidal coordinates

(\alpha,\theta,\phi)
,

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\\
z\\
\end{array}\right)=a_{E}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cosh\alpha\cos\theta\cos\phi\\
\cosh\alpha\cos\theta\sin\phi\\
\sinh\alpha\sin\theta\\
\end{array}\right)\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}0\leq\alpha<\infty\\
-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\leq\theta\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}\\
0\leq\phi<2\pi\\
\end{array}\right\},

with
a_{E}=\sqrt{b^{2}-a^{2}}
(Fig.
1
b), the scale
factors determining the surface and volume elements, are

Korn

\displaystyle h_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=h_{\theta}=a_{E}\sqrt{\sinh^{2}\alpha+\sin^{2}\theta},

\displaystyle h_{\phi}

\displaystyle=a_{E}\cosh\alpha\cos\theta.

In these coordinates, the domain
\Omega
is still characterized by

\alpha>\alpha_{0}=\tanh^{-1}(a/b)
, and the action of the
Laplace operator reads

\displaystyle\Delta u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a_{E}^{2}(\sinh^{2}\alpha+\sin^{2}\theta)}\biggl[\frac{1}{\cosh\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\biggl(\cosh\alpha\frac{\partial u}{\partial\alpha}\biggr)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\cos\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\biggl(\cos\theta\frac{\partial u}{\partial\theta}\biggr)\biggr]+\frac{1}{a_{E}^{2}\cosh^{2}\alpha\,\cos^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial\phi^{2}}\,.

(39)

A general solution of the Laplace equation reads

u(\alpha,\theta,\phi)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{m=-n}^{n}A_{mn}Q_{n}^{m}(i\sinh\alpha)\bar{Y}_{mn}(\theta,\phi),

(40)

where
\bar{Y}_{mn}(\theta,\phi)=a_{mn}P_{n}^{m}(\sin\theta)e^{im\phi}
, and the action of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}u|_{\partial\Omega}=\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=-\left.\frac{1}{h_{\alpha}}\partial_{\alpha}u\right|_{\alpha=\alpha_{0}}

(41)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{m=-n}^{n}A_{mn}\bar{Y}_{mn}(\theta,\phi)\frac{\cosh\alpha_{0}\,Q^{{}^{\prime}m}_{n}(i\sinh\alpha_{0})}{ia_{E}\sqrt{\cosh^{2}\alpha_{0}-\cos^{2}\theta}}\,.

Multiplying this relation by
\bar{Y}_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\cos\theta
and integrating over
\theta
and
\phi
, one gets a matrix
representation of the operator
{\mathcal{M}}
on the orthonormal basis of

\bar{Y}_{mn}
:

{\bf M}_{mn,m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}=2\pi\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}a_{mn}a_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\,c_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\,\bar{F}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(\sinh\alpha_{0}),

(42)

where

c_{mn}=\frac{\cosh\alpha_{0}\,Q^{{}^{\prime}m}_{n}(i\sinh\alpha_{0})}{ia_{E}Q_{n}^{m}(i\sinh\alpha_{0})}

(43)

and

\bar{F}_{n,n^{\prime}}(z)=\int\limits_{-1}^{1}dx\frac{P_{n}^{m}(x)P_{n^{\prime}}^{m}(x)}{\sqrt{z^{2}+x^{2}}}.

(44)

According to Eq. (
15
), one also has

\bar{F}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(z)=iF_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(iz).

(45)

Since the structure of the matrix
{\bf M}
is the same as for prolate
spheroids, many properties of eigenfunctions of the
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator remain unchanged; in particular, they
can be classified according to their dependence on
\phi
via

e^{im\phi}
; we employ the double index
mn
in the following. Using
again the decomposition (
22
), one can diagonalize
separately the matrices
{\bf M}_{m}
to access the eigenvalues
\mu_{mn}

and to construct the eigenfunctions

v_{mn}(\theta,\phi)=\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}[{\bf V}_{mn}]_{mn^{\prime}}\bar{Y}_{mn^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi)

(46)

and the Steklov eigenfunctions

V_{mn}(\alpha,\theta,\phi)=\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{n^{\prime}}^{m}(i\sinh\alpha)}{Q_{n^{\prime}}^{m}(i\sinh\alpha_{0})}[{\bf V}_{mn}]_{mn^{\prime}}\bar{Y}_{mn^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi).

(47)

As previously, the Steklov eigenfunctions
V_{mn}
are symmetric for
even
m+n
and antisymmetric for odd
m+n
under reflection with
respect to the horizontal plane
z=0
. In particular, these
eigenfunctions solve the mixed Steklov-Neumann and Steklov-Dirichlet
exterior problems for the exterior of an oblate spheroid in the upper
half-space.

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{x^{2}+y^{2}<b^{2}}dx\,dy\,f(x,y)

\displaystyle\times\frac{z_{0}}{2\pi(z_{0}^{2}+(x-x_{0})^{2}+(y-y_{0})^{2})^{3/2}}\,,

\displaystyle A_{mn}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi\delta_{m,0}a_{0n}}{Q_{n}^{m}(0)}\,\frac{P_{n-1}(0)-P_{n+1}(0)}{n+1/2}

for odd

;
VanKampen
;

In the same vein, the representation (

IV
Two applications

As mentioned in Sec.
I
, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann
operator
{\mathcal{M}}
and its eigenfunctions have found numerous applications
in applied mathematics and engineering. Here we briefly discuss two
applications in physics. In Sec.
IV.1
, we start with
diffusion-controlled reactions in chemical physics and illustrate the
use of
{\mathcal{M}}
for representing the Robin boundary condition that is
often used to describe surface reactions on partially reactive
targets. In Sec.
IV.2
, we highlight their relation to
the statistics of encounters between a diffusing particle and the
boundary, which is relevant in statistical physics and in the theory
of reflected Brownian motion.

IV.1
Partially reactive boundaries

Diffusion-controlled reactions play an important role in physics,
chemistry and biology

Schuss
;
Metzler
;
Lindenberg
;
North66
;
Wilemski73
;
Calef83
;
Berg85
;
Rice85
;
Bressloff13
;
Benichou14
;
Grebenkov23n
.
In the idealized setting introduced by Smoluchowski

Smoluchowski17
, a reactant diffuses towards its partner or
towards a catalytic surface, and reacts upon their first encounter.
In many situations, however, the first encounter is not sufficient, as
the reactant and/or its partner may need to overcome an activation
energy barrier, to be in an appropriate “active” state, to arrive
onto a specific catalytic germ, to pass through a channel/pore,
etc.
Grebenkov23n
. If any of these conditions is failed, the
reactant resumes its bulk diffusion until the next encounter, and so
on. Starting from Collins and Kimball
Collins49
, such partial
reactions are described by imposing the Robin boundary condition, in
which the diffusive flux of particles towards the catalytic boundary
is
postulated
to be proportional to their concentration on that
boundary. In other words, the concentration
C
of diffusing
reactants in the steady-state regime obeys:

\displaystyle\Delta C

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{in}~\Omega,

(66a)

\displaystyle-D\partial_{n}C

\displaystyle=\kappa C\quad\textrm{on}~\partial\Omega,

(66b)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{|\bm{x}|\to\infty}C

\displaystyle=C_{0},

(66c)

where
D
is the diffusion coefficient,
\kappa\geq 0
is a constant
reactivity of the boundary, and
C_{0}
is the initial concentration
maintained at infinity. Setting
q=\kappa/D
and
C=C_{0}(1-u)
,
one can express the Robin boundary condition
\partial_{n}u+qu=q

on
\partial\Omega
with the help of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator as
{\mathcal{M}}u|_{\partial\Omega}+qu|_{\partial\Omega}=q
. Inverting this relation yields

\displaystyle u|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=({\mathcal{M}}+q)^{-1}q

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(v_{k},1)_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}}{\|v_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}}\,\frac{q}{\mu_{k}+q}\,v_{k},

(67)

where we used the orthogonality and completeness of the basis of the
eigenfunctions
\{v_{k}\}
of
{\mathcal{M}}
.

For prolate spheroids, a general solution (
7
) of the
Laplace equation can be written as

u(\alpha,\theta,\phi)=\sum\limits_{m,n}f_{mn}\frac{Q_{n}^{m}(\cosh\alpha)}{Q_{n}^{m}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}Y_{mn}(\theta,\phi),

(68)

where the coefficients
f_{mn}
are found from the restriction of
u

onto
\partial\Omega
as:

\displaystyle f_{mn}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,\sin\theta\,Y_{mn}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\,u|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(v_{k},1)_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}}{\|v_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}}\,\frac{q}{\mu_{k}+q}

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,\sin\theta\,Y_{mn}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\,v_{k}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(v_{k},1)_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}}{\|v_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}}\,\frac{q}{\mu_{k}+q}[{\bf V}_{k}]_{mn},

(69)

where we employed the representation (
17
). Using
Eqs. (
123
,
127
) from Appendix

B
, one can further simplify:

\displaystyle f_{mn}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{4\pi}}{a_{E}\sinh\alpha_{0}Q_{0}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{[{\bf V}_{k}^{*}]_{00}[{\bf V}_{k}]_{mn}}{({\bf V}_{k}^{\dagger}{\bf c}{\bf V}_{k})}\,\frac{q}{\mu_{k}+q}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{4\pi}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{[\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}^{*}]_{00}[\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}]_{mn}}{(\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}^{\dagger}\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k})}\,\frac{q}{\mu_{k}+q}\,,

(70)

where
\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}={\bf c}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf V}_{k}
, and the diagonal matrix

{\bf c}
is formed by
c_{mn}
. Since
\mu_{k}
and
\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}
are
eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Hermitian matrix
\tilde{{\bf M}}={\bf c}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf M}{\bf c}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, one can also rewrite the above
expression in a matrix form:

f_{mn}=\sqrt{4\pi}\,q\bigl[(\tilde{{\bf M}}+q{\bf I})^{-1}\bigr]_{mn,00},

(71)

where
{\bf I}
is the identity matrix. Similar representations were
discussed in
Piazza19
;
Grebenkov19
.

Knowing the concentration, one can easily deduce the total diffusive
flux onto the boundary:

\displaystyle J_{q}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}(-D\partial_{n}C)=DC_{0}\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}(\partial_{n}u)|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=DC_{0}q\bigl(1,{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{M}}+q)^{-1}1\bigr)_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle=DC_{0}q\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{|(1,v_{k})_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}|^{2}}{\|v_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}}\,\frac{\mu_{k}}{\mu_{k}+q}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi DC_{0}q}{\sinh\alpha_{0}Q_{0}^{2}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{[{\bf V}_{k}^{*}]_{00}\,[{\bf V}_{k}]_{00}}{({\bf V}_{k}^{\dagger}{\bf c}{\bf V}_{k})}\,\frac{1}{\mu_{k}+q}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi DC_{0}a_{E}q}{Q_{0}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{[\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}^{*}]_{00}\,[\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}]_{00}}{(\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}^{\dagger}\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k})}\,\frac{1}{\mu_{k}+q}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi DC_{0}a_{E}q}{Q_{0}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}\bigl[(\tilde{{\bf M}}+q{\bf I})^{-1}\bigr]_{00,00}.

(72)

Denoting by

\mathcal{C}=\frac{4\pi a_{E}}{Q_{0}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}=\frac{8\pi a_{E}}{\ln\bigl(\frac{b+a_{E}}{b-a_{E}}\bigr)}

(73)

the harmonic (Newtonian) capacity of the prolate spheroid, one gets

J_{q}=J_{\infty}\,q\bigl[(\tilde{{\bf M}}+q{\bf I})^{-1}\bigr]_{00,00},

(74)

where

J_{\infty}=DC_{0}\mathcal{C}\,

(75)

is the total diffusive flux onto a perfectly reactive prolate spheroid
(see also
Piazza19
).
As a consequence, the effect of partial reactivity is represented by

q\bigl[(\tilde{{\bf M}}+q{\bf I})^{-1}\bigr]_{00,00}
. In the limit of a
sphere (
a\to b
), this factor reduces to
q/(q+1/b)
so that one
retrieves the Collins-Kimball diffusive flux
Collins49

J_{q}=4\pi DC_{0}b\,\frac{qb}{1+qb}\qquad(a=b),

(76)

where
4\pi DC_{0}b
is the Smoluchowski diffusive flux
J_{\infty}
onto
a perfectly reactive sphere of radius
b

Smoluchowski17
.

For oblate spheroids, a similar computation involves

u(\alpha,\theta,\phi)=\sum\limits_{m,n}f_{mn}\frac{Q_{n}^{m}(i\sinh\alpha)}{Q_{n}^{m}(i\sinh\alpha_{0})}\bar{Y}_{mn}(\theta,\phi),

(77)

with the same expression (
71
) for the coefficients

. The total diffusive flux reads

J_{q}=J_{\infty}~q\bigl[(\tilde{{\bf M}}+q{\bf I})^{-1}\bigr]_{00,00}\,,

(78)

where

J_{\infty}=\frac{4\pi DC_{0}a_{E}}{iQ_{0}(i\sinh\alpha_{0})}=DC_{0}\mathcal{C},

(79)

and

\mathcal{C}=\frac{4\pi a_{E}}{iQ_{0}(i\sinh\alpha_{0})}=\frac{4\pi a_{E}}{\cos^{-1}(a/b)}

(80)

is the harmonic capacity of the oblate spheroid. In the case of a
disk, the total flux was thoroughly studied in
Grebenkov18b
.

IV.2
Statistics of boundary encounters

The Steklov eigenfunctions also play the central role in the
encounter-based approach to diffusion-controlled reactions

Grebenkov20
. In this context, one usually considers a more
general setting, in which the Laplace equation (
1
) is
replaced by the modified Helmholtz equation,
(p-D\Delta)u=0
,
with
p\geq 0
. The eigenfunctions of the related Steklov problem
determine the spectral expansion of the Laplace transform of the
encounter propagator
P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
, i.e., the joint probability
density of the position
\bm{X}_{t}
and the boundary local time
\ell_{t}
of
a particle that starts from a point
\bm{x}_{0}
and diffuses in a domain

\Omega
with the reflecting boundary
\partial\Omega

Grebenkov20
. Here
the boundary local time
\ell_{t}
can be understood as the (rescaled)
number of encounters between the diffusing particle and the boundary,
which is crucial for describing various phenomena occuring on the
boundary
Grebenkov19c
;
Grebenkov20c
;
Grebenkov21a
;
Grebenkov22d
.
The encounter propagator allows one to access most common
characteristics of diffusion-controlled reactions, including the
conventional propagator, the survival probability, the first-passage
time probability density, the harmonic measure density, the diffusive
flux, etc. For instance, one can investigate the first-crossing
time
\tau=\inf\{t>0~:~\ell_{t}>\ell\}
of a threshold
\ell
by
the boundary local time
\ell_{t}
. In other words, the random variable

\tau
is the first time instance when the number of encounters
exceeds a given threshold. The probability density of this random
variable,

U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})dt={\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\tau\in(t,t+dt)\},

(81)

P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
over the boundary

U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=D\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}d\bm{x}\,P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}).

For a bounded domain, any threshold is crossed with probability
1

U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
is normalized to
1
.
However, when a three-dimensional domain is unbounded, the particle
can escape to infinity and never return to the boundary
\partial\Omega

than

\ell

{\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\ell_{\infty}>\ell\}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(83)

which can be seen as the Laplace transform of
U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
,
evaluated at
p=0
. One can therefore employ the general spectral
expansion for the Laplace transform of the encounter propagator

P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})

Grebenkov20
, to determine this
probability in terms of the Steklov eigenfunctions for the Laplace
equation (i.e., for
p=0
) that we studied here:

{\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\ell_{\infty}>\ell\}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{[V_{k}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}}{\|v_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}}e^{-\mu_{k}\ell}\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}d\bm{x}\,v_{k}(\bm{x}).

(84)

V
Conclusions

In this paper, we investigated the spectral properties of the
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
{\mathcal{M}}
and the related Steklov problem for
anisotropic domains in three dimensions. Using prolate and oblate
spheroidal coordinates, we derived a matrix representation of
{\mathcal{M}}
on
the basis of spherical harmonics for both exterior and interior
problems. Its diagonalization allowed us to access the eigenvalues

\mu_{mn}
and eigenfunctions
v_{mn}
of
{\mathcal{M}}
, as well as the Steklov
eigenfunctions
V_{mn}
. These eigenfunctions inherited the
symmetries of the considered domains; in particular,
V_{mn}
depend
on the angle
\phi
via the factor
e^{im\phi}
, and are symmetric
(resp., antisymmetric) with respect to the horizontal plane
z=0

when
m+n
is even (resp., odd). As a consequence, they are also the
eigenfunctions of the mixed Steklov-Neumann (resp., Steklov-Dirichlet)
problems in the upper half-space. We also described recurrence
relations to speed up the numerical construction of truncated
matrices. While the interior spectral problem in a bounded domain
could alternatively be solved by other numerical methods

Andreev04
;
Yang09
;
Bi11
;
Li11
;
Li13
;
Xie14
;
Bi16
;
Bogosel16
;
Akhmetgaliyev17
;
Alhejaili19
;
Chen20
,
exterior spectral problems in unbounded domains are in general more
difficult to address by conventional tools.

We discussed the impact of domain anisotropy onto the behavior of
eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. In particular, we showed how the
eigenvalues of
{\mathcal{M}}
for the exterior of a prolate spheroid diverge in
the limit
a\to 0
(the exterior of a needle); in turn, in the
opposite limit of thin (disk-like) oblate spheroids, the eigenvalues
reach finite values. In this limit, we also got complementary
insights onto a classical mixed Dirichlet-Neumann problem in the
half-space.

Apart from their own fundamental interest in spectral geometry, the
Steklov eigenfunctions offer flexible meshless representations for
solutions of interior and exterior boundary value problems. In the
context of diffusion-controlled reactions, these eigenfunctions allow
one to incorporate the effect of partial reactivity, e.g., to compute
the steady-state concentration of particles that react on the
spheroidal boundary. In particular, we showed how the total diffusive
flux (i.e., the overall reaction rate) depends on the anisotropy of
the target. Another application from statistical physics concerns the
statistics of encounters between a diffusing particle and the
spheroidal target. The Steklov eigenfunctions determine the crossing
probability
{\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\ell_{\infty}>\ell\}
, i.e., the probability
that the (rescaled) number of encounters,
\ell_{\infty}
, exceeds a
given threshold
\ell
, before the particle escapes to infinity. The
crossing probability generalizes the hitting probability of the target
(the latter corresponding to
\ell=0
). In particular, we showed
that the smallest eigenvalue
\mu_{00}
of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann
operator determines the exponential decay of the crossing probability
at large thresholds
\ell
.

In summary, this study brings novel insights onto the spectral
properties of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator for exterior domains,
with the special emphasis on anisotropy. Even though the derived
eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are not as explicit as in the case of
the exterior of a ball, the matrix representation offers an efficient
numerical computation and allows for getting asymptotic results. One
straightforward extension of this study concerns diffusion across a
semi-permeable spheroidal boundary, which is a model of diffusive
exchange in anisotropic living cells or tissues

Richter74
;
Grimes18
;
Gadzinowski21
. The exchange between
interior and exterior compartments is usually described by a
transmission boundary condition that can be reformulated in terms of
two Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators for the interior and exterior
problems. Moreover, an extension of the encounter-based approach to
semi-permeable membranes allows one to treat much more sophisticated
exchange mechanisms

Bressloff22a
;
Bressloff22b
;
Bressloff22c
;
Bressloff22d
.
Another promising direction is related to the Dirichlet-to-Neumann
operator for the (modified) Helmholtz equation. As briefly discussed
in Sec.
IV.2
, the related Steklov eigenfunctions
determine the encounter propagator and thus most diffusion-reaction
characteristics in this system. While one could employ prolate/oblate
spheroidal wave functions, their analysis is more sophisticated and
less explicit. Alternatively, one can consider other domains, in
which the Laplace operator admits separation of variables in
appropriate curvilinear coordinates (e.g., a torus). A systematic
investigation of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator in such relatively
simple shapes can help to uncover the intricate relations between the
geometry and the spectral properties of this operator.

Appendix B
Numerical computation

In this section, we discuss a practical implementation of the proposed
method. The construction of the (truncated) matrix
{\bf M}
requires
computing the integral (
15
) and the coefficients
c_{mn}
,
both involving associated Legendre functions
P_{n}^{m}(x)
and

Q_{n}^{m}(x)
. For this reason, we briefly summarize the main steps in
the evaluation of these functions and related integrals.

B.1
Associated Legendre functions

The definition and basic properties of associated Legendre functions

P_{n}^{m}(x)
and
Q_{n}^{m}(x)
can be found in many textbooks, e.g., in

Abramowitz
(chapter 8). As we focus on integer
indices
n=0,1,2,\ldots
and
m=-n,-n+1,\ldots,n
, the numerical
computation of
P_{n}^{m}(x)
is fairly standard in the common range
-1\leq x\leq 1
(see, e.g., the built-in function
legendre
in
matlab). However, the computation of the coefficients
c_{mn}

requires evaluating both
P_{n}^{m}(z)
and
Q_{n}^{m}(z)
at
z=\cosh\alpha>1
or
z=i\sinh\alpha
. While
P_{n}^{m}(z)
with even
m
are just
polynomials and thus can be immediately evaluated at any complex
number, the computation is more subtle for other associated Legendre
functions that involve square roots and logarithms and thus require
cuts in the complex plane. Throughout this section, we follow
Ref.
Abramowitz
and distinguish two conventions by writing the
argument as
x
for
x\in(-1,1)
, or as
z
for
z\notin[-1,1]
.
For instance, two conventional definitions read for
m\geq 0
as:

\displaystyle P_{n}^{m}(x)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}(1-x^{2})^{m/2}\frac{d^{m}}{dx^{m}}P_{n}(x),

(94a)

\displaystyle P_{n}^{m}(z)

\displaystyle=(z^{2}-1)^{m/2}\frac{d^{m}}{dz^{m}}P_{n}(z),

(94b)

and

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{m}(x)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}(1-x^{2})^{m/2}\frac{d^{m}}{dx^{m}}Q_{n}(x),

(95a)

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{m}(z)

\displaystyle=(z^{2}-1)^{m/2}\frac{d^{m}}{dz^{m}}Q_{n}(z),

(95b)

where
P_{n}(z)
are the Legendre polynomials and
Q_{n}(z)
are the
Legendre functions of the second kind (see below). In the following,
we focus on
P_{n}^{m}(z)
and
Q_{n}^{m}(z)
and briefly describe their
numerical computation via the standard recurrence formulas for
completeness. As these formulas are identical for both
P_{n}^{m}(z)
and

Q_{n}^{m}(z)
, we consider the latter functions and then just mention
changes for
P_{n}^{m}(z)
.

In the first step, one can compute the Legendre functions
Q_{n}(z)
up
to the desired order
n
via the recurrence relation,

Q_{n}(z)=\frac{2n-1}{n}zQ_{n-1}(z)-\frac{n-1}{n}Q_{n-2}(z),

(96)

starting from

Q_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\ln\biggl(\frac{z+1}{z-1}\biggr),\quad Q_{1}(z)=zQ_{0}(z)-1.

(97)

From Eq. (
95b
), one can also evaluate

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{1}(z)=\frac{1}{n\sqrt{z^{2}-1}}\biggl(n(2n-1)zQ_{n}(z)-

(98)

\displaystyle[(2n-1)(n-1)z^{2}+n^{2}]Q_{n-1}(z)+(n-1)^{2}zQ_{n-2}(z)\biggr)

for
n\geq 2
, where we used another recurrence relation

\frac{d}{dz}Q_{n}(z)=\frac{n+1}{z^{2}-1}\bigl(Q_{n+1}(z)-zQ_{n}(z)\bigr).

(99)

One also has

Q_{1}^{1}(z)=\sqrt{z^{2}-1}\,Q_{0}(z)-\frac{z}{\sqrt{z^{2}-1}}\,.

(100)

Since we know
Q_{n}^{0}(z)=Q_{n}(z)
and
Q_{n}^{1}(z)
, the other functions

Q_{n}^{m}(z)
can be found via the recurrence relation:

Q_{n}^{m-1}(z)=\frac{2mz(z^{2}-1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}Q_{n}^{m}(z)+Q_{n}^{m+1}(z)}{(n+m)(n-m+1)}

(101)

for
m=-n+2,-n+3,\ldots,-1,0
, or

Q_{n}^{m+1}(z)=(n+m)(n-m+1)Q_{n}^{m-1}(z)-\frac{2mzQ_{n}^{m}(z)}{(z^{2}-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}

(102)

for
m=1,2,\ldots,n
. Note that the derivative of
Q_{n}^{m}(z)
can be
found via either of two equivalent relations

\displaystyle\frac{dQ_{n}^{m}(z)}{dz}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z^{2}-1}\biggl[(n+m)(n-m+1)\sqrt{z^{2}-1}\,Q_{n}^{m-1}(z)

\displaystyle-mzQ_{n}^{m}(z)\biggr],

(103)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z^{2}-1}\biggl[\sqrt{z^{2}-1}\,Q_{n}^{m+1}(z)+mzQ_{n}^{m}(z)\biggr].

(104)

The computation of
P_{n}^{m}(z)
relies on the same recurrence relations,
except for their initiation, namely, Eqs. (
97
,
100
)
are replaced by

P_{0}(z)=1,\quad P_{1}(z)=z,\quad P_{1}^{1}(z)=\sqrt{z^{2}-1}.

(105)

B.2
Alternative matrix representation

In the limit
\alpha_{0}\to 0
, the coefficients

\bar{F}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(\sinh\alpha_{0})
given by Eq. (
44
)
logarithmically diverge that makes their numerical computation more
subtle. It is therefore convenient to get an alternative matrix
representation that can be constructed even at
\alpha_{0}=0
. For
this purpose, we apply the operator
{\mathcal{M}}
to Eq. (
46
)
that yields

\displaystyle\mu_{mn}\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}[{\bf V}_{mn}]_{mn^{\prime}}\bar{Y}_{mn^{\prime}}=\mu_{mn}v_{mn}={\mathcal{M}}v_{mn}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}[{\bf V}_{mn}]_{mn^{\prime}}c_{mn^{\prime}}\frac{\bar{Y}_{mn^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{\cosh^{2}\alpha_{0}-\cos^{2}\theta}}\,,

where we wrote explicitly the action of
{\mathcal{M}}
onto a spherical harmonic

\bar{Y}_{mn^{\prime}}
via Eq. (
41
). Multiplying both sides
of this relation by
\sqrt{\cosh^{2}\alpha_{0}-\cos^{2}\theta}\,\bar{Y}^{*}_{mn_{0}}\cos\theta
and integrating over
\theta
and
\phi
,
we get

\mu_{mn}\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}[{\bf V}_{mn}]_{mn^{\prime}}\bar{G}_{mn^{\prime},mn_{0}}(\sinh\alpha_{0})=[{\bf V}_{mn}]_{mn_{0}}c_{mn_{0}},

(106)

where we used the orthogonality of spherical harmonics, and defined

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{mn,m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}(z)=\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int\limits_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}d\theta\,\cos\theta\,\bar{Y}_{mn}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle\times\sqrt{z^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta}\,\bar{Y}_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=2\pi\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}a_{mn}a_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}G_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(z),

(107)

with

G_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(z)=\int\limits_{-1}^{1}dx\,P_{n}^{m}(x)P_{n^{\prime}}^{m}(x)\sqrt{z^{2}+x^{2}}.

(108)

These coefficients resemble
\bar{F}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(z)
from
Eq. (
44
), except that the square root
\sqrt{z^{2}+x^{2}}
stands in the numerator and thus eliminates the divergence at
z=0
. Finally, Eq. (
106
) can be written as an eigenvalue
problem

\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}[{\bf V}_{mn}]_{mn^{\prime}}\frac{\bar{G}_{mn^{\prime},mn_{0}}(\sinh\alpha_{0})}{c_{mn_{0}}}=\frac{1}{\mu_{mn}}[{\bf V}_{mn}]_{mn_{0}}.

(109)

This relation implies that
{\bf V}_{mn}
is a
left
eigenvector of
the matrix with the elements

\bar{G}_{mn^{\prime},mn_{0}}(\sinh\alpha_{0})/c_{mn_{0}}
, whereas
1/\mu_{mn}
is
the associated eigenvalue. One sees that this is a matrix
representation of
{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}
, the inverse of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann
operator, which is called the Neumann-to-Dirichlet operator. The
numerical advantage of this matrix representation is that the elements

\bar{G}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(z)
do not diverge as
z\to 0
. In particular, one
can compute the matrix elements
\bar{G}_{mn^{\prime},mn_{0}}(0)/c_{mn_{0}}
and
then diagonalize the truncated matrix.

B.3
Recurrence formulas for the integral

Now we describe the recurrence formulas for computing the elements of
the matrix
{\bf M}
. These formulas are actually valid in a more general
case:

H_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}=\int\limits_{-1}^{1}dx\,f(x^{2})\,P_{n}^{m}(x)\,P_{n^{\prime}}^{m}(x),

(110)

where
f(x)
is a given integrable function. Due to the symmetry
relation (
25
), these integrals vanish when
n+n^{\prime}

is odd. They also are zero if
|m|>n
or
|m|>n^{\prime}
. The remaining
nonzero values can be found recursively by using the recurrence
relation for associated Legendre polynomials:

(n-m+1)P_{n+1}^{m}(x)=(2n+1)xP_{n}^{m}(x)-(n+m)P_{n-1}^{m}(x).

(111)

Applying this relation to both
P_{n}^{m}(x)
and
P_{n^{\prime}}^{m}(x)
in
Eq. (
110
), we get for even
n+n^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle H_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-1}^{1}dx\,f(x^{2})\,P_{n}^{m}(x)\frac{(2n^{\prime}-1)xP_{n^{\prime}-1}^{m}(x)-(n^{\prime}-1+m)P_{n^{\prime}-2}^{m}(x)}{n^{\prime}-m}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-1}^{1}dx\,f(x^{2})\biggl[\frac{(n-m+1)P_{n+1}^{m}(x)+(n+m)P_{n-1}^{m}(x)}{2n+1}(2n^{\prime}-1)P_{n^{\prime}-1}^{m}(x)-\frac{n^{\prime}-1+m}{n^{\prime}-m}P_{n}^{m}(x)P_{n^{\prime}-2}^{m}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{\prime}-1}{(2n+1)(n^{\prime}-m)}\biggl((n+1-m)H_{n+1,n^{\prime}-1}^{m}+(n+m)H_{n-1,n^{\prime}-1}^{m}\biggr)-\frac{n^{\prime}-1+m}{n^{\prime}-m}H_{n,n^{\prime}-2}^{m}.

(112)

As a consequence, if
H_{n,0}^{m}
and
H_{n,1}^{m}
are known, one
can evaluate the remaining elements.

For any
m>0
, one has to evaluate

\displaystyle H_{n,m}^{m}

\displaystyle=2\int\limits_{0}^{1}dx\,f(x^{2})\,P_{n}^{m}(x)\,\underbrace{(-1)^{m}(2m-1)!!\,(1-x^{2})^{m/2}}_{=P_{m}^{m}(x)}

\displaystyle=-(2m-1)2\int\limits_{0}^{1}dx\,f(x^{2})\,P_{m-1}^{m-1}(x)\,\sqrt{1-x^{2}}P_{n}^{m}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{2m-1}{2n+1}\biggl((n+m-1)(n+m)H_{n-1,m-1}^{m-1}

\displaystyle-(n-m+1)(n-m+2)H_{n+1,m-1}^{m-1}\biggr)

for even
m+n
, where we used another recurrence relation to express

\sqrt{1-x^{2}}P_{n}^{m}(x)
in terms of
P_{n\pm 1}^{m-1}(x)
. Similarly,
one needs

\displaystyle H_{n,m+1}^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{2m+1}{2n+1}\biggl((n+m-1)(n+m)H_{n-1,m}^{m-1}

\displaystyle-(n-m+1)(n-m+2)H_{n+1,m}^{m-1}\biggr)

for odd
m+n
. In other words, once the elements
H_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m-1}

are constructed, one can find the elements
H_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}
.
As a consequence, if one knows the elements
H_{n,0}^{0}
and

H_{n,1}^{0}
, one can first construct all the elements
H_{n,n^{\prime}}^{0}

by using recurrence relations (
112
), and then progressively
get the elements
H_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}
. We stress that this procedure does
not depend on the function
f
, which determines only the
initialization step, i.e., the elements
H_{n,0}^{0}
and

H_{n,1}^{0}
.

For the integrals
\bar{F}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(z)
from
Eq. (
44
), one has
f(y)=(z^{2}+y)^{-1/2}
, for which
we have

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{0,0}^{0}(z)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{z^{2}+1}+1}{\sqrt{z^{2}+1}-1}\right),

(113a)

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{1,1}^{0}(z)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{z^{2}+1}-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{z^{2}+1}+1}{\sqrt{z^{2}+1}-1}\right).

(113b)

Using the recurrence relations

P_{n}(x)=\frac{2n-1}{n}xP_{n-1}(x)-\frac{n-1}{n}P_{n-2}(x)

(114)

for Legendre polynomials
P_{n}(x)
and integrating by parts, one gets
two sets of relations

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{n+1,0}^{0}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{2n+1}{n+1}\bar{F}_{n,1}^{0}(z)-\frac{n}{n+1}\bar{F}_{n-1,0}^{0}(z),

(115a)

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{n+1,1}^{0}(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{n-1}{n+2}\bar{F}_{n-1,1}^{0}(z)-\frac{2n+1}{n+2}z^{2}\bar{F}_{n,0}^{0}(z).

(115b)

These relations determine all
\bar{F}_{n,0}^{0}(z)
and
\bar{F}_{n,1}^{0}(z)
,
from which one can construct recursively all
\bar{F}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(z)
, as
described above. According to Eq. (
45
), this
construction can also be applied to get the elements
F_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(z)
.

For the integrals
\tilde{G}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(z)
from Eq. (
108
), one
has
f(y)=(z^{2}+y)^{1/2}
, for which we have

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{0,0}^{0}(z)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{z^{2}+1}+\frac{z^{2}}{2}\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{z^{2}+1}+1}{\sqrt{z^{2}+1}-1}\right),

(116a)

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{1,1}^{0}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(z^{2}+2)\sqrt{z^{2}+1}}{4}-\frac{z^{4}}{8}\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{z^{2}+1}+1}{\sqrt{z^{2}+1}-1}\right).

(116b)

Using the recurrence relations and integrating by parts, one retrieves
Eq. (
115a
), which is completed by

\bar{G}_{n+1,1}^{0}(z)=-\frac{n-3}{n+4}\bar{G}_{n-1,1}^{0}(z)-\frac{2n+1}{n+4}z^{2}\bar{G}_{n,0}^{0}(z).

(117)

These relations determine all
\bar{G}_{n,0}^{0}(z)
and
\bar{G}_{n,1}^{0}(z)
,
from which one can construct recursively all
\bar{G}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(z)
, as
described above.

In the limit of a disk (
z=\sinh\alpha_{0}\to 0
), one gets

\bar{G}_{0,0}^{0}(0)=1
and
\bar{G}_{1,1}^{0}(0)=1/2
, from which all
other elements
\bar{G}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(0)
can be recursively constructed.

B.4
Orthogonality of eigenfunctions

Let us check that the eigenfunctions
v_{k}
obtained from the
eigenvectors
{\bf V}_{k}
of the matrix
{\bf M}
are orthogonal to each other.
For oblate spheroids, we have

\displaystyle(v_{k_{1}},v_{k_{2}})_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\int\limits_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}d\theta\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,h_{\theta}\,h_{\phi}\,v_{k_{1}}^{*}\,v_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle=a_{E}^{2}\cosh\alpha_{0}\sum\limits_{m_{1},n_{1},m_{2},n_{2}}[{\bf V}_{k_{1}}^{*}]_{m_{1}n_{1}}[{\bf V}_{k_{2}}]_{m_{2}n_{2}}

\displaystyle\times\bar{G}_{m_{1}n_{1},m_{2}n_{2}}(\sinh\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle=a_{E}^{2}\cosh\alpha_{0}\frac{1}{\mu_{k_{1}}}\sum\limits_{m_{2},n_{2}}[{\bf V}_{k_{1}}^{*}]_{m_{2}n_{2}}c_{m_{2}n_{2}}[{\bf V}_{k_{2}}]_{m_{2}n_{2}},

(118)

where we used Eqs. (
106
,
109
), and

\bar{G}_{m_{1}n_{1},m_{2}n_{2}}(\sinh\alpha_{0})
are given by
Eq. (
107
). It remains to show that the above sum vanishes
when
\mu_{k_{1}}\neq\mu_{k_{2}}
.

For this purpose, we rewrite Eq. (
42
) in a matrix form
as
{\bf M}={\bf F}{\bf c}
, where
{\bf F}
is the symmetric matrix with elements

\bar{F}_{mn,m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}(\sinh\alpha_{0})=2\pi\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}a_{mn}a_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\bar{F}_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m}(\sinh\alpha_{0}),

(119)

and
{\bf c}
is the diagonal matrix formed by
c_{mn}
. On one hand, one
can transpose the relation
{\bf M}{\bf V}_{k_{1}}=\mu_{k_{1}}{\bf V}_{k_{1}}
and
multiply it by
{\bf c}{\bf V}_{k_{2}}
on the right to get

{\bf V}_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}{\bf c}{\bf F}{\bf c}{\bf V}_{k_{2}}=\mu_{k_{1}}{\bf V}_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}{\bf c}{\bf V}_{k_{2}}.

On the other hand, multiplication of the relation
{\bf M}{\bf V}_{k_{2}}=\mu_{k_{2}}{\bf V}_{k_{2}}
by
{\bf V}_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}{\bf c}
on the left yields

{\bf V}_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}{\bf c}{\bf F}{\bf c}{\bf V}_{k_{2}}=\mu_{k_{2}}{\bf V}_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}{\bf c}{\bf V}_{k_{2}}.

Subtracting these equations, one gets

(\mu_{k_{1}}-\mu_{k_{2}}){\bf V}_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}{\bf c}{\bf V}_{k_{2}}=0,

that implies the orthogonality for any
\mu_{k_{1}}\neq\mu_{k_{2}}
:

{\bf V}_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}{\bf c}{\bf V}_{k_{2}}=\sum\limits_{m,n}[{\bf V}_{k_{1}}^{*}]_{mn}c_{mn}[{\bf V}_{k_{2}}]_{mn}=0,

(120)

and thus the orthogonality of the eigenfunctions
v_{k_{1}}
and

v_{k_{2}}
due to Eq. (
118
).

Since the matrix
{\bf M}
is not symmetric, its eigenvectors
{\bf V}_{k}
are
not orthogonal to each other; in fact, their orthogonality relation
(
120
) includes the weighting coefficient
c_{mn}
. For
this reason, it is more convenient to consider rescaled eigenvectors

\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}={\bf c}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf V}_{k}
, which are the eigenvectors of
the Hermitian matrix

\tilde{{\bf M}}={\bf c}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf M}{\bf c}^{-\frac{1}{2}}={\bf c}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf F}{\bf c}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(121)

In fact, one has
\tilde{{\bf M}}\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}=\mu_{k}\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}
,
with the same eigenvalue
\mu_{k}
. The rescaled eigenvectors

\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}
are orthogonal to each.

Note that Eq. (
118
) allows one to compute the

L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
-norm of each eigenfunction directly from the corresponding
eigenvector:

\displaystyle\|v_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}=\int\limits_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}d\theta\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,h_{\theta}\,h_{\phi}\,|v_{k}|^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{E}^{2}\cosh\alpha_{0}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\sum\limits_{m,n}[{\bf V}_{k}^{*}]_{mn}c_{mn}[{\bf V}_{k}]_{mn}

\displaystyle=a_{E}^{2}\cosh\alpha_{0}\frac{{\bf V}_{k}^{\dagger}{\bf c}{\bf V}_{k}}{\mu_{k}}=a_{E}^{2}\cosh\alpha_{0}\frac{\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}^{\dagger}\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}}{\mu_{k}}\,.

(122)

This straightforward computation helps to avoid numerical integration
over
\theta
. A similar computation for prolate spheroids yields:

\|v_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}=a_{E}^{2}\sinh\alpha_{0}\frac{{\bf V}_{k}^{\dagger}{\bf c}{\bf V}_{k}}{\mu_{k}}=a_{E}^{2}\sinh\alpha_{0}\frac{\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}^{\dagger}\tilde{{\bf V}}_{k}}{\mu_{k}}\,.

(123)

In the same vein, one can compute the projection of an eigenfunction

v_{k}
onto a constant. For oblate spheroids, one gets

\displaystyle(1,v_{k})_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}d\theta\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,h_{\theta}\,h_{\phi}\,v_{k}

\displaystyle=a_{E}^{2}\cosh\alpha_{0}\sum\limits_{m,n}[{\bf V}_{k}]_{mn}\bar{G}_{mn,00}(\sinh\alpha_{0})\sqrt{4\pi}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{4\pi}\,a_{E}^{2}\cosh\alpha_{0}\frac{c_{00}}{\mu_{k}}[{\bf V}_{k}]_{00},

(124)

where we used that
\bar{Y}_{00}=a_{00}=1/\sqrt{4\pi}
. For
instance, for the exterior problem, substitution of
c_{00}
from
Eq. (
43
) yields

(1,v_{k})_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\frac{\sqrt{4\pi}a_{E}}{iQ_{0}(i\sinh\alpha_{0})}\,\frac{[{\bf V}_{k}]_{00}}{\mu_{k}}\,.

(125)

For prolate spheroids, a similar computation gives

(1,v_{k})_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\sqrt{4\pi}\,a_{E}^{2}\sinh\alpha_{0}\frac{c_{00}}{\mu_{k}}[{\bf V}_{k}]_{00}.

(126)

For instance, for the exterior problem, substitution of
c_{00}
from
Eq. (
12
) yields

(1,v_{k})_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\frac{\sqrt{4\pi}\,a_{E}}{Q_{0}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}\,\frac{[{\bf V}_{k}]_{00}}{\mu_{k}}.

(127)

B.5
Axisymmetric setting

In many applications, it is sufficient to look at axisymmetric
eigenfunctions, which do not depend on the angle
\phi
and thus
correspond to
m=0
. These eigenfunctions can be constructed by
diagonalizing the matrix
{\bf M}_{0}
, for which the computation can be
further simplified. We briefly discuss this case below for prolate
and oblate spheroids.

Prolate spheroids

In this case, Eq. (
10
) is reduced to

f(\theta)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}\,\psi_{n}(\theta),

(128)

where

\psi_{n}(\theta)=\sqrt{n+1/2}\,P_{n}(\cos\theta)

(129)

are normalized Legendre polynomials. The action of
{\mathcal{M}}
reads then

[{\mathcal{M}}f](\theta)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}[{\bf M}_{0}f]_{n}\psi_{n}(\theta),

(130)

where the (infinite-dimensional) matrix
{\bf M}_{0}
represents the operator

{\mathcal{M}}
on the orthonormal basis of Legendre polynomials, with

\displaystyle[{\bf M}_{0}]_{n,n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle={\bf M}_{0n,0n^{\prime}}

(131)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(n+1/2)(n^{\prime}+1/2)}\,c_{0n^{\prime}}\,F_{n,n^{\prime}}^{0}(\cosh\alpha_{0}),

where

c_{0n}=-\frac{\sinh\alpha_{0}\,Q^{\prime}_{n}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}{a_{E}Q_{n}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}\,,

(132)

and

F_{n,n^{\prime}}^{0}(z)=\int\limits_{-1}^{1}dx\frac{P_{n}(x)P_{n^{\prime}}(x)}{\sqrt{z^{2}-x^{2}}}\,.

(133)

By symmetry, the elements
F_{n,n^{\prime}}^{0}(z)
are zero when
n+n^{\prime}
is odd.
Once the eigenvectors
{\bf V}_{0n}
of the matrix
{\bf M}_{0}
are found, the
eigenfunction
v_{0n}
and the Steklov eigenfunction
V_{0n}
are
given by

v_{0n}(\theta)=\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}[{\bf V}_{0n}]_{0n^{\prime}}\psi_{n^{\prime}}(\theta)

(134)

and

V_{0n}(\alpha,\theta)=\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{n^{\prime}}(\cosh\alpha)}{Q_{n^{\prime}}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}[{\bf V}_{0n}]_{0n^{\prime}}\psi_{n^{\prime}}(\theta).

(135)

Oblate spheroids

In the same vein, one employs the normalized Legendre polynomials

\psi_{n}(\theta)=\sqrt{n+1/2}\,P_{n}(\sin\theta)

(136)

to get the matrix representation of
{\mathcal{M}}
:

[{\bf M}_{0}]_{0n,0n^{\prime}}=\sqrt{(n+1/2)(n^{\prime}+1/2)}\,c_{0n^{\prime}}\,iF_{n,n^{\prime}}^{0}(i\sinh\alpha_{0}),

(137)

where

c_{0n}=\frac{\cosh\alpha_{0}Q^{\prime}_{n}(i\sinh\alpha_{0})}{ia_{E}Q_{n}(i\sinh\alpha_{0})}\,.

(138)

Disk

In the limit
a=0
, the coefficients
c_{0n}
from
Eq. (
138
) approach constants; in fact, using

\displaystyle Q_{2n}(0)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\frac{(2n)!}{4^{n}\,(n!)^{2}}\,\frac{\pi}{2i}\,,

(139)

\displaystyle Q_{2n-1}(0)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\frac{2^{2n-1}(n!)^{2}}{n(2n)!}\,,

(140)

and the recurrence relation (
99
) to evaluate
Q^{\prime}_{n}(0)=nQ_{n-1}(0)
, we deduce Eq. (
51
). Note that these
coefficients can be found iteratively as

c_{0n}=\frac{c_{0(n-2)}}{(1-1/n)^{2}}\,.

(141)

One has
c_{0n}\simeq n
as
n
increases.
