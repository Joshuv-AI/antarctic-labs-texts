Practical Phase Retrieval Using Double Deep Image Priors
Zhong Zhuang, David Yang, Felix Hofmann, David A. Barmherzig, Ju Sun

Abstract
Phase retrieval (PR) concerns the recovery of complex phases from complex magnitudes. We identify the connection between the difficulty level and the number and variety of symmetries in PR problems. We focus on the most difficult far-field PR (FFPR), and propose a novel method using double deep image priors. In realistic evaluation, our method outperforms all competing methods by large margins. As a single-instance method, our method requires no training data and minimal hyperparameter tuning, and hence enjoys good practicality.

Our focus on practical FFPR methods

FFPR: Formulation and Prior Arts

FFPR model
The object of interest is
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
, and
\mathbf{Y}=\left|\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{X})\right|^{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}}
. Here,
\mathcal{F}
is the oversampled 2D Fourier transform:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{F}_{m^{\prime}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{F}_{n^{\prime}}^{\intercal},

(2.1)

where
\mathbf{F}_{m^{\prime}}
and
\mathbf{F}_{n^{\prime}}
are
normalized discrete Fourier matrices. We always assume that
m^{\prime}\geq 2m-1
and
n^{\prime}\geq 2n-1
, which is necessary to ensure recoverability.

Prior arts on FFPR
Since we focus on practical FFPR, here we only discuss methods that have been tested on FFPR with at least partial success.

•

\displaystyle\text{find}\;\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{C}^{m^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}}\;\st\;\left|\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{Z})\right|^{2}=\mathbf{Y},\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Z})=\mathbf{0},

(2.2)

\mathbf{Z}

More refined support information can be naturally incorporated into the support constraint

Our method: FFPR using double DIPs

Deep image prior (DIP) for visual inverse problems
DIP and variants
[
43
]
parameterize visual objects as outputs of DNNs—typically structured convolutional networks to favor spatially smooth structures, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}=G_{\mathbf{\theta}}(\mathbf{z})
, where
\mathbf{z}
is normally a random but fixed seed, and
G_{\mathbf{\theta}}
is a trainable DNN paramaterized by
\mathbf{\theta}
. For a visual inverse problem of the form
\mathbf{y}\approx f(\mathbf{x})
where
\mathbf{y}
is the observation and
f
is the observation model, the classical regularized data-fitting formulation

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}}\;\ell\left(\mathbf{y},f(\mathbf{x})\right)+\lambda\Omega(\mathbf{x})

(3.1)

can now be empowered by DIP and turned into

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{\theta}}\;\ell\left(\mathbf{y},f\circ G_{\mathbf{\theta}}(\mathbf{z})\right)+\lambda\Omega\circ G_{\mathbf{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}).

(3.2)

This simple idea has recently claimed numerous successes in computer vision and computational imaging; see, e.g., the recent survey
[
44
]
, and our recent work addressing practicality issues around DIP
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
.
A salient feature of DIP is the strong structured prior it imposes through DNNs, with zero extra data
! Although the theoretical understanding of DIP is still far from complete, current theories attribute its success to two aspects: (1)
structured priors
imposed by convolutional and upsampling operations, and (2)
global optimization
due to significant overparameterization and first-order methods
[
48
,
49
]
.

Applying DIP to FFPR
As shown in

It is then tempting to try DIP, as (1) the objects we try to recover in scientific imaging are visual objects and probably can be blessed by the structured priors enforced by DIP, and (2) more importantly, the issue we encounter in solving the LS is exactly about global optimization, which could be eliminated by overparameterization in DIP

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{\theta}}\;\|\sqrt{\mathbf{Y}}-\left|\mathcal{F}\circ G_{\mathbf{\theta}}(\mathbf{z})\right|\|^{2}_{F},\quad G_{\mathbf{\theta}}(\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}.

Double DIPs boost the performance
For FFPR applications such as CDI,
\mathbf{X}
as a complex-valued object can often be naturally split into two parts with disparate complexity levels. For example, in Bragg CDI on crystals, the magnitude part on the support is known to have uniform values, but the phase part can have complex spatial patterns due to strains
[
51
,
52
,
53
]
; in CDI on live cells, the nonnegative real part contains useful information, and the imaginary part acts like small-magnitude noise
[
54
]
. In these cases, due to the apparent asymmetry in complexity, it makes sense to parameterize
\mathbf{X}
as two separate DIPs
[
55
,
47
]
instead of one:

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=G^{1}_{\mathbf{\theta}_{1}}\left(\mathbf{z}_{1}\right)e^{iG^{2}_{\mathbf{\theta}_{2}}\left(\mathbf{z}_{2}\right)},\;\text{or}\;\mathbf{X}=G^{1}_{\mathbf{\theta}_{1}}\left(\mathbf{z}_{1}\right)+iG^{2}_{\mathbf{\theta}_{2}}\left(\mathbf{z}_{2}\right).

(3.4)

This can be justified as balancing the learning paces: with a single DIP, “simple” part is learned much faster than the “complex” part; with double DIPs, we can balance the learning paces by making the learning rate for the “simple” part relatively small compared to that for the “complex” part. We observe a substantial performance boost in
Figs.

4
and
5
due to the double-DIP parametrization.

Fresnel and Fraunhofer approximations to the diffraction formula

Figure 7:
A schematic diffraction imaging system with parallel aperture and imaging planes (plot adapted from

In this section, we clarify the difference between the near-field and far-field models for phase retrieval, following
[
1
,
57
]
. Consider the propagation of a monochromatic wave (with wavelength
\lambda
) from an aperture plane
z=0
to a parallel imaging plane
z=z_{0}
with
z_{0}\gg\lambda
; see
Fig.

7
. Let
U(x,y,z)
denote the wave field. The celebrated Rayleigh-Sommerfeld diffraction formula dictates that

\displaystyle\boxed{U(x,y,z_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint U(\xi,\eta,0)\left(\frac{1}{r\left(\xi,\eta\right)}-ik\right)\frac{z_{0}}{r^{2}\left(\xi,\eta\right)}e^{ikr\left(\xi,\eta\right)}\;d\xi d\eta},

(A.1)

where
r\left(\xi,\eta\right)\doteq\sqrt{z_{0}^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}+(y-\eta)^{2}}
,
k\doteq\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}
is the wavenumber, and the effective domain of the double integral is the aperture
\Omega
. Moreover, write the domain of the image plane as
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Eq.

A.1
has an equivalent form:

\displaystyle\boxed{U(x,y,z_{0})=\iint\widehat{U}\left(f_{X},f_{Y},0\right)e^{ikz_{0}\sqrt{1-\left(\lambda f_{X}\right)^{2}-\left(\lambda f_{Y}\right)^{2}}}e^{i2\pi\left(f_{X}x+f_{Y}y\right)}\;df_{X}df_{Y}},

(A.2)

where
\widehat{U}\left(f_{X},f_{Y},0\right)=\iint U(x,y,0)e^{-i2\pi\left(f_{X}x+f_{Y}y\right)}\;df_{X}df_{Y}
is the 2D Fourier transform of the planar field
U(x,y,0)
. The equivalence is due to the convolution theorem: write
r^{\circ}\left(x,y\right)\doteq\sqrt{z_{0}^{2}+x^{2}+y^{2}}
and

h(x,y,z_{0})\doteq\frac{1}{2\pi}(\frac{1}{r^{\circ}(x,y)}-ik)\frac{z_{0}}{[r^{\circ}(x,y)]^{2}}e^{ikr^{\circ}(x,y)}
. Then
Eq.

A.1
can be written as
U(x,y,z_{0})=U(x,y,0)\ast h(x,y,z_{0})
. The equivalence is clear once we recognize that
\widehat{r^{\circ}}\left(f_{X},f_{Y},z_{0}\right)\doteq\iint h(x,y,z_{0})e^{-i2\pi\left(f_{X}x+f_{Y}y\right)}\;df_{X}df_{Y}=e^{ikz_{0}\sqrt{1-(\lambda f_{X})^{2}-\left(\lambda f_{Y}\right)^{2}}}
.

For
Eq.

A.1
,
\frac{1}{r\left(\xi,\eta\right)}-ik=\frac{1}{r\left(\xi,\eta\right)}-\frac{i2\pi}{\lambda}\approx\frac{2\pi}{i\lambda}
as
r\left(\xi,\eta\right)\gg\lambda
in practice. Hence we have the simplified form called the
Huygens-Fresnel principle
:

\displaystyle\boxed{U(x,y,z_{0})=\frac{z_{0}}{i\lambda}\iint U(\xi,\eta,0)\frac{1}{r^{2}\left(\xi,\eta\right)}e^{ikr\left(\xi,\eta\right)}\;d\xi d\eta}.

(A.3)

We can derive two approximations to
Eq.

A.3
, i.e., the Fresnel (i.e., near-field) and Fraunhofer (i.e., far-field) approximations. Noting that
\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}=1+\varepsilon/2-\varepsilon^{2}/8+O(\varepsilon^{3})
for
\left|\varepsilon\right|\ll 1
, we can approximate

\displaystyle r\left(\xi,\eta\right)\doteq\sqrt{z_{0}^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}+(y-\eta)^{2}}=z_{0}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{x-\xi}{z_{0}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{y-\eta}{z_{0}}\right)^{2}}

(A.4)

by its low-order Taylor expansions, provided that
\left(x-\xi\right)^{2}+\left(y-\eta\right)^{2}\ll z_{0}^{2}
for all
\left(\xi,\eta\right)\in\Omega
. First, we have
r^{2}\left(\xi,\eta\right)\approx z_{0}^{2}
using only the
0
-th order expansion. For the exponential term, since
k
is normally large, we use the
1
-st order expansion:

\displaystyle\exp\left(ikr\left(\xi,\eta\right)\right)

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left[ik\left(z_{0}+\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{2z_{0}}+\frac{(y-\eta)^{2}}{2z_{0}}\right)\right]=e^{ikz_{0}}\exp\left[\frac{ik}{2z_{0}}\left((x-\xi)^{2}+(y-\eta)^{2}\right)\right],

(A.5)

which is acceptable when

\displaystyle\frac{z_{0}k}{8}\left(\left(\frac{x-\xi}{z_{0}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{y-\eta}{z_{0}}\right)^{2}\right)^{2}\ll 1\Longleftrightarrow z_{0}^{3}\gg\frac{k}{8}\left[\left(x-\xi\right)^{2}+\left(y-\eta\right)^{2}\right]^{2}\quad\forall\,(\xi,\eta)\in\Omega,(x,y)\in\Sigma.

(A.6)

So we arrive at the famous Fresnel approximation

\displaystyle U(x,y,z_{0})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{e^{ikz_{0}}}{i\lambda z_{0}}\iint U(\xi,\eta,0)\exp\left[\frac{ik}{2z_{0}}\left((x-\xi)^{2}+(y-\eta)^{2}\right)\right]\;d\xi d\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{ikz_{0}}}{i\lambda z_{0}}e^{\frac{ik}{2z_{0}}\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)}\iint\left[U(\xi,\eta,0)e^{\frac{ik}{2z_{0}}\left(\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}\right)}\right]e^{-\frac{ik}{z_{0}}(x\xi+y\eta)}\;d\xi d\eta

If moreover
\frac{ik}{2z_{0}}\left(\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}\right)\ll 1\Longleftrightarrow z_{0}\gg\frac{k}{2}\left(\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}\right)\quad\forall\,(\xi,\eta)\in\Omega
,
then
e^{\frac{ik}{2z_{0}}\left(\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}\right)}\approx 1
and hence we obtain:

\displaystyle U(x,y,z_{0})\approx\frac{e^{ikz_{0}}}{i\lambda z_{0}}e^{\frac{ik}{2z_{0}}\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)}\iint U(\xi,\eta,0)e^{-\frac{ik}{z_{0}}(x\xi+y\eta)}\;d\xi d\eta

By assuming
\left(\lambda f_{X}\right)^{2}+\left(\lambda f_{Y}\right)^{2}\ll 1
and so
\sqrt{1-\left(\lambda f_{X}\right)^{2}-\left(\lambda f_{Y}\right)^{2}}\approx 1-\left(\lambda f_{X}\right)^{2}/2-\left(\lambda f_{Y}\right)^{2}/2
, we can approximate
Eq.

A.2
as

\displaystyle U(x,y,z_{0})

\displaystyle\approx e^{ikz_{0}}\iint\widehat{U}\left(f_{X},f_{Y},0\right)e^{-i\pi z_{0}\lambda(f_{X}^{2}+f_{Y}^{2})}e^{i2\pi\left(f_{X}x+f_{Y}y\right)}\;df_{X}df_{Y}

This is equivalent to the forward form of Fresnel approximation, due to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{1}{i\lambda z_{0}}e^{\frac{ik}{2z_{0}}\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)}\right)=e^{-i\pi\lambda z_{0}(f_{X}^{2}+f_{Y}^{2})},

(A.7)

and the convolution theorem again.

So when we measure the field intensity on the image plane,

\displaystyle\left|U(x,y,z_{0})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\propto\left|\iint\left[U(\xi,\eta,0)e^{\frac{ik}{2z_{0}}\left(\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}\right)}\right]e^{-\frac{ik}{z_{0}}(x\xi+y\eta)}\;d\xi d\eta\right|^{2}

(A.8)

\displaystyle\propto\left|\iint\widehat{U}\left(f_{X},f_{Y},0\right)e^{-i\pi z_{0}\lambda(f_{X}^{2}+f_{Y}^{2})}e^{i2\pi\left(f_{X}x+f_{Y}y\right)}\;df_{X}df_{Y}\right|^{2}

(A.9)

according to the Fresnel (near-field) approximation, and

\displaystyle\left|U(x,y,z_{0})\right|^{2}\propto\left|\iint U(\xi,\eta,0)e^{-\frac{ik}{z_{0}}(x\xi+y\eta)}\;d\xi d\eta\right|^{2}

(A.10)

by the Fraunhofer (far-field) approximation. Detailed discussion of discretization and computation can be found in
