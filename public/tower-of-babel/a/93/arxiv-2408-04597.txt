Components, large and small, are as they should be I: supercritical percolation on regular graphs of growing degree

Abstract

\frac{\delta^{2}s}{d}
with probability at least
1-\exp\left\{-\frac{\delta^{2}s}{d}\right\}
.

3
Proof of Theorem
1

Throughout this section, we assume that
G=(V,E)
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
1
. Given
H\subseteq G
, let
V_{L}(H)
be the set of vertices in large components in

, that is,

\displaystyle V_{L}(H)\coloneqq\left\{v\in V\colon|C_{H}(v)|\geq\frac{7\log n}{\epsilon^{2}}\right\}.

(2)

Let us first briefly discuss the proof’s strategy. We will utilise a double-exposure/sprinkling argument. We set
p_{2}=\frac{\epsilon^{3}}{d}
, and let
p_{1}
be such that
(1-p_{1})(1-p_{2})=1-p
. Note that
p_{1}\geq\frac{1+\epsilon-\epsilon^{3}}{d}
, and that
G_{p}
has the same distribution as
G_{p_{1}}\cup G_{p_{2}}
. (We remark here that the choice of value for

is tightly related to the Taylor series of

at

, as will become clear in the proof of Lemma

).
In Section

, we show that large components in
G_{p_{1}}
are typically ’everywhere dense’ in the sense that
whp
for every
v\in V
,

|B_{G}(v,\log_{d}\log n+1)\cap V_{L}(G_{p_{1}})|=\Omega(d\log n),

that is, we show that
whp
for every vertex
v\in V
, a constant proportion of the vertices in a ball of radius
\log_{d}\log n+1
centred in

are in large components in
G_{p_{1}}
. We note that already Ajtai, Komlós, and Szemeredi
[
1
]
used this broad strategy – showing that ‘large components’ are ‘everywhere dense’ – to establish the phase transition in the hypercube. Here, however, our graph does not necessarily have any product structure, and our assumptions are only on the expansion properties of the graph.

We continue by showing that typically there are no components in
G_{p_{1}}
whose order is between
\frac{7\log n}{\epsilon^{2}}
and
Cd\log n
. Then, we show in Lemma
3.5
that typically all components of order at least
\Omega(d\log n)
in
G_{p_{1}}
merge after sprinkling with probability
p_{2}
. This requires some delicate treatment, as we might need to find paths of length
\Omega(\log_{d}\log n)
in
G_{p_{2}}
, and the probability a path of length
\ell
is in
G_{p_{2}}
is
p_{2}^{\ell}
. We further show that
whp
all components in
G_{p_{1}}\cup G_{p_{2}}
, besides those intersecting with

, are of order at most
\frac{14\log n}{\epsilon^{2}}
(we note that the difference in the constant between here and the definition of large components above is intentional). This also requires some careful treatment — see Lemma
3.4
and the proof of Theorem
1
. Finally, we show that the total volume of vertices in components of order at least
\frac{14\log n}{\epsilon^{2}}
in

is of asymptotic order
y(\epsilon)n
.

Recall that
c_{3}\in(0,1]
, and note that we may assume throughout the proof, without loss of generality, that
c_{1},c_{2}\leq 1
.

3.1
Large components are ‘everywhere dense’

Throughout this section, we state the results for
G_{p}
, however they all follow through for
G_{p_{1}}
as well, with the natural substitution of
\epsilon\to\epsilon-\epsilon^{3}
.

Let
c^{\prime}\coloneqq c_{2}c_{3}^{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}
. This choice of a constant may seem peculiar at first, but we will shortly see its importance. We begin by analysing the typical behaviour of components incident to a fixed set of order
c^{\prime}d\log n
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S\subseteq V
with
|S|=c^{\prime}d\log n
. Then, the following holds.

1.

The probability there exists
U\subseteq S
such that

\displaystyle\left|\bigcup_{u\in U}C_{G_{p}}(u)\right|\in\left[\frac{c^{\prime}d\log n}{\epsilon^{3}},\frac{2c^{\prime}d\log n}{\epsilon^{3}}\right],

is at most
o\left(1/n\right)
.

2.

The probability there exists
U\subseteq S
with
|U|\geq(1-\epsilon^{2})c^{\prime}d\log n
such that

\displaystyle\left|\bigcup_{u\in U}C_{G_{p}}(u)\right|\leq Cd\log n,

is at most
o\left(1/n\right)
.

Proof.

Let
s\coloneqq|S|
.

1.

We restrict ourselves to
U^{\prime}\subseteq U
, such that
C_{G_{p}}(u)
are disjoint for each
u\in U^{\prime}
. Let
F
be a spanning forest of the components meeting
U^{\prime}
in
G_{p}
, such that
|V(F)|=k\in\left[\frac{s}{\epsilon^{3}},\frac{2s}{\epsilon^{3}}\right]
. The forest is composed of some
\ell\coloneqq|U^{\prime}|\leq s
tree components,
T_{1},\ldots,T_{\ell}
, where for every
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
,
V(T_{j_{1}})\cap V(T_{j_{2}})=\varnothing
.
We have that all the edges leaving
F
are not in
G_{p}
, and that each
T_{j}
contains a unique vertex
u_{j}\in U^{\prime}
for
1\leq j\leq\ell
. Note that if there is a subset
U\subseteq S
satisfying the conditions of the lemma, then such an
F
exists. Indeed, one can decompose
\bigcup_{u\in U}C_{G_{p}}(u)
into disjoint connected sets, and for each such set, choose one vertex in
U
, thus forming
U^{\prime}
and the forest
F
.

Let us now bound from above the probability such a forest
F
exists. We specify
\ell\in[s]
, the size
U^{\prime}
. Then, there are
\binom{s}{\ell}\leq 2^{s}
ways to choose
U^{\prime}\subseteq S
. We can then specify the forest
F
by choosing
|V(F)|=k\in\left[\frac{s}{\epsilon^{3}},\frac{2s}{\epsilon^{3}}\right]
, the sizes of the tree components
|T_{i}|=k_{i}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}k_{i}=k
, and finally the tree components
\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{\ell}\}
, for which by Lemma
2.3
there are at most
\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(ed)^{k_{i}-1}=(ed)^{k-\ell}
choices altogether. For a fixed forest
F
with
\ell
components there are
k-\ell
edges which must appear in
G_{p}
, which happens with probability
p^{k-\ell}
. Since
k\leq\frac{2s}{\epsilon^{3}}<Cd\log n
(for
C
large enough with respect to
c_{2},c_{3},\alpha,\epsilon
), by
3
there are at least
(1-\epsilon^{3})kd
edges in the boundary of
V(F)
which must not appear in
G_{p}
, which happens with probability at most
(1-p)^{(1-\epsilon^{3})kd}
. Thus, by the union bound, the probability such
F
exists is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}2^{s}\sum_{k=s/\epsilon^{3}}^{2s/\epsilon^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}>0\\
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{\ell}=k\end{subarray}}(ed)^{k-\ell}p^{k-\ell}(1-p)^{(1-\epsilon^{3})kd}.

We have that

\displaystyle(ed)^{k-\ell}p^{k-\ell}(1-p)^{(1-\epsilon^{3})kd}

\displaystyle\leq\left[e(1+\epsilon)\exp\left\{-(1+\epsilon)(1-\epsilon^{3})\right\}\right]^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}k}{4}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}s}{4\epsilon^{3}}\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{s}{4\epsilon}\right\},

where we used
1+x\leq\exp\left\{x-\frac{x^{2}}{3}\right\}
for small enough
x
, and that
k\geq\frac{s}{\epsilon^{3}}
. There are
\binom{k+\ell-1}{\ell}
ways to choose
k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}>0
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}k_{i}=k
. Recalling that
k\in\left[\frac{s}{\epsilon^{3}},\frac{2s}{\epsilon^{3}}\right]
and
\ell\in[s]
, we have that
\binom{k+\ell-1}{\ell}\leq\left(\frac{e\cdot 3s}{s\cdot\epsilon^{3}}\right)^{s}\leq\left(\frac{9}{\epsilon^{3}}\right)^{s}
. Altogether, the probability that such
F
exists is at most

\displaystyle s2^{s}\cdot\frac{2s}{\epsilon^{3}}\cdot\left(\frac{9}{\epsilon^{3}}\right)^{s}\exp\left\{-\frac{s}{4\epsilon}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{s\left(1+\log(9/\epsilon^{3})-\frac{1}{4\epsilon}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=o\left(1/n\right),

where in the last equality we used that
s=c^{\prime}d\log n
and that
\epsilon
is sufficiently small.

2.

Fix
U\subseteq S
, with
k=|U|\geq(1-\epsilon^{2})s
. We will utilise a variant of the Breadth First Search (BFS) exploration process. To that end, let
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{nd/2}
be a sequence of i.i.d. Bernoulli
(p)
random variables, and assume some order
\sigma
on the vertices of
G
. We maintain three sets of vertices:
W
, the set of vertices whose exploration has been completed;
A
, the set of active vertices, kept as a queue; and,
Y
, the set of vertices which we have yet to explore. We initialise
W=\varnothing
,
A=U
, and
Y=V\setminus U
, and let
v_{A}
be the first vertex in
A
. The algorithm stops once
A
is empty. If at moment
t
(that is, when we query the
t
-th edge) the set
A
is still non-empty, we consider the first (according to
\sigma
) neighbour (in
G
) of
v_{A}
in
Y
. If
X_{t}=1
, we move this vertex from
Y
to
A
, and continue. If
X_{t}=0
, we consider the next (according
\sigma
) neighbour (in
G
) of
v_{A}
in
Y
. If there are no remaining neighbours of
v_{A}
in
Y
, we move
v_{A}
from
A
to
W
, and continue. Note that, as in the BFS algorithm, we received a negative answer for all the queries about the edges between
W
and
Y
at all times.
Furthermore, once
A
is empty, we have that
G_{p}[W]
has the same distribution as
\cup_{u\in U}C_{G_{p}}(u)
.

Suppose that
A
is empty at some moment
t
and that at that moment
|W|=w\leq Cd\log n
. Then, by
3
, we have that
t\geq e_{G}(W,Y)=e_{G}(W,W^{C})\geq w(1-\epsilon^{3})d
, and we have received
w-k
positive answers. Thus, by Lemma
2.1
the probability of this event is at most

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Bin\left(w(1-\epsilon^{3})d,\frac{1+\epsilon}{d}\right)\leq w-k\right]\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{(\epsilon w/2+k)^{2}}{4w}\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-\epsilon k\right\}.

We have at most
n
ways to choose
w
, and at most

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=(1-\epsilon^{2})s}^{s}\binom{s}{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\epsilon^{2}s}\binom{s}{\ell}\leq\left(\frac{e}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)^{\epsilon^{2}s}

ways to choose
U\subseteq S
. Thus, by the union bound, the probability of this event is at most

\displaystyle n\cdot\left(\frac{e}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)^{\epsilon^{2}s}\exp\left\{-\epsilon(1-2\epsilon)s\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{2\log n+\epsilon^{2}\log(e/\epsilon^{2})s-\epsilon(1-2\epsilon)s/4\right\}=o\left(1/n\right).

∎

The following is an almost immediate conclusion of Lemma
3.1
(recall here the definition of
V_{L}(H)
, given in (
2
)).

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
S\subseteq V
with
|S|=c^{\prime}d\log n
. Then, with probability at least
1-o\left(1/n\right)
there exists a subset
X\subseteq S
, with
|X|\geq\epsilon^{2}c^{\prime}d\log n
, such that
X\subseteq V_{L}(G_{p})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
2
, with probability at least
1-o\left(1/n\right)
, every subset
U\subseteq S
with
|U|\geq(1-\epsilon^{2})c^{\prime}d\log n
satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\bigcup_{u\in U}C_{G_{p}}(u)\right|\geq Cd\log n.

Furthermore, we claim that by Lemma
3.1
1
, with probability at least
1-o\left(1/n\right)
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\left(V\setminus V_{L}(G_{p})\right)\cap\bigcup_{u\in U}C_{G_{p}}(u)\right|\leq\frac{c^{\prime}d\log n}{\epsilon^{3}}.

Indeed, suppose that
\left|\left(V\setminus V_{L}(G_{p})\right)\cap\bigcup_{u\in U}C_{G_{p}}(u)\right|\geq 1+c^{\prime}d\log n/\epsilon^{3}
. Then, we may associate these components with roots
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\in U
for some
m
. For every
i\in[m]
, by definition of
V_{L}(G_{p})
we have that
|C_{G_{p}}(u_{i})|\leq\frac{7\log n}{\epsilon^{2}}
. Thus, sequentially adding the volumes of these components, at the first moment we crossed the total volume of
\frac{c^{\prime}d\log n}{\epsilon^{3}}
, we have a total volume of at most
\frac{c^{\prime}d\log n}{\epsilon^{3}}+\frac{7\log n}{\epsilon^{2}}
, contradicting the assertion of Lemma
3.1
1
.

Hence, assuming that
C>\frac{c^{\prime}}{\epsilon^{3}}
, with probability at least
1-o\left(1/n\right)
, every subset
U\subseteq S
of size at least
(1-\epsilon^{2})c^{\prime}d\log n
has at least one vertex in
V_{L}(G_{p})
. Thus, with probability
1-o(1/n)
, there are less than
(1-\epsilon^{2})c^{\prime}d\log n
vertices in
S
which are in
V\setminus V_{L}(G_{p})
, and therefore there are at least
\epsilon^{2}c^{\prime}d\log n
vertices in
S
which are in
V_{L}(G_{p})
.
∎

We are now ready to show that large components are typically ‘everywhere dense’.

Lemma 3.3
.

Whp
every
v\in V
is at distance (in
G
) at most
1+\log_{d}\log n
from at least
\epsilon^{2}c^{\prime}d\log n
vertices in large components in
G_{p}
.

Proof.

Fix
v\in V
. By
2
,

\displaystyle|B_{G}\left(v,\log_{d}\log n\right)|\geq\min\left\{c_{2}\log n,(c_{3}d)^{\log_{d}\log n}\right\}\geq c_{2}c_{3}^{\log_{d}\log n}\log n\geq c_{2}c_{3}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\log n,

where we used that
c_{2},c_{3}\in(0,1]
and that
d\geq\log^{\alpha}n
. Furthermore, as we can always find a subset of size
c_{2}c_{3}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\log n\leq c_{2}\log n
in
B_{G}\left(v,\log_{d}\log n\right)
, by
2
,

\displaystyle|B_{G}\left(v,1+\log_{d}\log n\right)|\geq c_{3}d\cdot c_{2}c_{3}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\log n=c^{\prime}d\log n.

Hence, we can let
S_{v}\subseteq B_{G}\left(v,1+\log_{d}\log n\right)
be an arbitrary set of order
c^{\prime}d\log n
. By Corollary
3.2
, the probability that
|S_{v}\cap V_{L}(G_{p})|\leq\epsilon^{2}c^{\prime}d\log n
is at most
o(1/n)
. Union bound over the
n
choices of
v
completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Large components typically merge

We continue with double-exposure. Recall that
p_{2}=\frac{\epsilon^{3}}{d}
,
p_{1}\geq\frac{1+\epsilon-\epsilon^{3}}{d}
, and that
G_{p_{1}}\cup G_{p_{2}}
has the same distribution as
G_{p}
. Further, recall that
c^{\prime}=c_{2}c_{3}^{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}
, and let
r\coloneqq 1+\log_{d}\log n
, noting that
r\leq 1+\frac{1}{\alpha}
by our assumption on
d
.

Let us first show a ‘gap’ in the component sizes.

Lemma 3.4
.

Whp
, there is no connected set
K
in
G_{p}
with
|V(K)|\in\left[\frac{7\log n}{\epsilon^{2}},Cd\log n\right]
and
E_{G_{p_{1}}}(K,K^{C})=\varnothing
.

Note that the above implies that typically there are no components whose order lies in the interval
\left[\frac{7\log n}{\epsilon^{2}},Cd\log n\right]
, both in
G_{p_{1}}
and in
G_{p}
.

Proof.

Let
k\in\left[\frac{7\log n}{\epsilon^{2}},Cd\log n\right]
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{k}
be the event that there is a connected set
K
of order
k
in
G_{p}
, with
E_{G_{p_{1}}}(K,K^{C})=\varnothing
. Since every connected set has a spanning tree, if
\mathcal{A}_{k}
occurs, then there exists some tree
T
of order
k
in
G
, such that all its edges are in
G_{p}
, and none of the edges in
E_{G}\left(V(T),V\setminus V(T)\right)
are in
G_{p_{1}}
. By our assumption,
k\leq Cd\log n
and thus by
3
,
e_{G}\left(V(T),V\setminus V(T)\right)\geq(1-\epsilon^{3})kd
. By Lemma
2.3
, there are at most
n(ed)^{k-1}
trees of order
k
at
G
. Therefore, by the union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{A}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{T\text{ is a tree in }G,|V(T)|=k}p^{k-1}(1-p_{1})^{e\left(V(T),V\setminus V(T)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{T\text{ is a tree in }G,|V(T)|=k}p^{k-1}(1-p_{1})^{(1-\epsilon^{3})kd}

\displaystyle\leq n(edp)^{k-1}\exp\left\{-p_{1}(1-\epsilon^{3})dk\right\}.

Since

, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{A}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq n(e(1+\epsilon))^{k-1}\exp\left\{-(1+\epsilon-\epsilon^{3})(1-\epsilon^{3})k\right\}

\displaystyle\leq n\exp\left\{k\left(1+\log(1+\epsilon)-(1+\epsilon-3\epsilon^{3})\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq n\exp\left\{k\left(1+(\epsilon-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}+\epsilon^{3})-(1+\epsilon-3\epsilon^{3})\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq n\exp\left\{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}k}{3}\right\}=o(1/n),

where the last inequality holds for small enough
\epsilon>0
, and the equality follows since
k\geq\frac{7\log n}{\epsilon^{2}}
. Union bound over the less than
Cd\log n<n
relevant values of
k
completes the proof.
∎

6
Discussion

We showed that for a regular graph
G
of growing degree
d
, some very mild assumption on the edge expansion properties of
G
, and a fairly good control over the expansion of sets up to size
O(d\log n)
, suffices to ensure that
G
will exhibit the Erdős-Rényi component phenomenon (ERCP). We further showed that our edge expansion assumption on sets up to size
O(d\log n)
is fairly tight, in the sense that there are graphs with almost optimal edge expansion of sets up to size
\Omega(d\log n)
which do not exhibit the ERCP.

As mentioned in the introduction, it was shown by Frieze, Krivelevich and Martin
[
22
]
that pseudo-random
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs, where
\lambda=o(d)
, exhibit the ERCP. The classical results of Ajtai, Komlós, and Szemerédi
[
1
]
and of Bollobás, Kohayakawa, and Łuczak
[
9
]
show that the hypercube
Q^{d}
exhibits the ERCP as well. The proofs of these two results are quite different, with the first relying on quite a tight control on
edge-distribution
of the graph (through the expander mixing lemma), and the latter on the
product structure
of the hypercube (together with Harper’s edge isoperimetric inequality). Here, we demonstrated that the ERCP can be determined solely from the expansion properties of the graph, thereby providing a unified approach to this natural question.

As is evident from Theorems
1
,
3
and
4
, there is an intrinsic connection between the ‘global’ assumption on edge expansion (Property
1
), and the ‘local’ assumption on the edge expansion of small sets (Property
3
): the stronger the assumption on the global expansion is, the weaker the assumption on the expansion of small sets can be (and vice versa). As demonstrated by Theorem
2
