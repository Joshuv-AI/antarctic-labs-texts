Flasque Meadows
João Dias, Bruno Dinis

Abstract
. In analogy with flasque sheaves, we introduce the notion of flasque meadow as a common meadow where the transition maps are all surjective. We study some properties of flasque meadows and illustrate them with many examples and counterexamples.

1.
Introduction

The notion of common meadow has gained some attention lately due to recently discovered connections with ring theory and with nonstandard analysis
[
10
,
13
,
14
,
12
,
15
]
(see also
[
8
,
9
]
for other recent developments). Originally introduced by Bergstra and Tucker in
[
1
]
,
meadows
are algebraic structures with two operations, addition and multiplication, where both operations have inverses which are total. This implies in particular that one is allowed to divide by zero. The original idea was to view meadows as abstract data types given by equational axiomatizations
[
3
,
5
,
6
,
2
]
. The point is that one is then able to obtain simple term rewriting systems which are easier to automate in formal reasoning
[
2
,
7
]
. However, and as mentioned above, meadows are also algebraic structures whose study has interest on its own. For our purposes, two different kinds of meadows are particularly relevant:
common meadows
, introduced by Bergstra and Ponse in
[
4
]
and
pre-meadows with
\mathbf{a}
, introduced by the authors in
[
13
]
. In these classes of meadows the inverse of zero is an element, denoted
\mathbf{a}
, which is absorbent for both operations. The interesting feature of these two classes is that they can be decomposed as disjoint unions of rings
[
13
]
, which allows to adapt many of the results of ring theory onto them. Pre-meadows with
\mathbf{a}
differ from common meadows as the former do not require the existence of an inverse function. This turns out to be crucial in trying to enumerate finite common meadows
[
14
]
. The two structures are nevertheless related since
P
, a pre-meadow with
\mathbf{a}
such that
0\cdot P
is finite and the partial order in
0\cdot P
is a total order, is actually a common meadow.

In this paper we introduce a new class of meadows, called
flasque meadows
, inspired by a recently discovered connection between meadows and sheaves
[
15
]
. The term
flasque
originates from Sheaf Theory, where a sheaf is said to be flasque if the restriction maps are surjective
[
19
, p. 67]
. Flasque sheaves have the nice property of preserving some exact sequences. We study some basic properties of flasque meadows and illustrate them with many examples and counterexamples.

3.
Flasque Meadows

As shown in
[
12
, Proposition 3.4]
, there exists a class of common meadows
M
which is completely determined by
M_{0}
. We will see (in Proposition
3.7
) that in the class of pre-meadows with
\mathbf{a}
a similar phenomenon occurs. We recall that, given a pre-meadow
P
, the transition maps of
P
are ring homomorphisms
f_{0\cdot w,0\cdot z}:P_{0\cdot w}\to P_{0\cdot z}
. If the maps are surjective, then
P_{0\cdot w}/\ker(f_{0\cdot w,0\cdot z})
is isomorphic to
P_{0\cdot z}
. The case where the maps are surjective is of great interest since, in that case,
P_{0}
defines the entire meadow.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
P
be a pre-meadow with
\mathbf{a}
. We say that
P
is
flasque
if for all
0\cdot z,0\cdot w\in 0\cdot P
the transition maps
f_{0\cdot w,0\cdot z}:P_{0\cdot w}\to P_{0\cdot z}
are surjective.

The first examples of flasque pre-meadows are the pre-meadows with
\mathbf{a}
constructed from a ring, i.e. of the form

(1)

M(R):=\bigsqcup_{I\trianglelefteq R}R/I,

for some unital commutative ring
R
(see
[
12
]
for more on this class of meadows).

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring. Then
M(R)
is a flasque pre-meadow with
\mathbf{a}
.

Proof.

Since the transition maps are projections from
R/I
to
R/J
with
I\subseteq J
then they are surjective, and so
M(R)
is flasque.
∎

We now give an alternative condition for a pre-meadow with
\mathbf{a}
to be flasque.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
P
be a pre-meadow with
\mathbf{a}
then the following are equivalent:

(1)

for all
0\cdot z,0\cdot w\in 0\cdot P
the transition map
f_{0\cdot w,0\cdot z}:P_{0\cdot w}\to P_{0\cdot z}
is surjective.

(2)

for all
0\cdot z\in 0\cdot P
the transition map
f_{0,0\cdot z}:P_{0}\to P_{0\cdot z}
is surjective.

Proof.

The implication
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
is obvious.

Assume that for all
0\cdot z\in 0\cdot P
the transition map
f_{0,0\cdot z}:P_{0}\to P_{0\cdot z}
is surjective. Let
0\cdot z,0\cdot w\in 0\cdot P
. We have that
f_{0\cdot z}=f_{0\cdot w,0\cdot z}\circ f_{0\cdot w}
is surjective, so
f_{0\cdot w,0\cdot z}
is also surjective.
∎

If
P
is a flasque pre-meadow with
\mathbf{a}
, then
P
is determined by
P_{0}
and the lattice structure of
0\cdot P
. The following proposition reflects this fact.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
P
be a flasque pre-meadow with
\mathbf{a}
. Then
P=P_{0}+0\cdot P
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.6
, for all
x\in P
there exists some
y\in P_{0}
such that
x=y+0\cdot x
. Then
P\subseteq P_{0}+0\cdot P
, and so
P=P_{0}+0\cdot P
as we wanted.
∎

As a consequence of Proposition
3.7
, every
P
, a flasque pre-meadow with
\mathbf{a}
, decomposes into the sum of a ring
P_{0}
with a lattice
0\cdot P
. Moreover, if
P
is a flasque pre-meadow with
\mathbf{a}
and
P_{0}
is a field, then
P
must be a common meadow, by Proposition
3.6
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
M
be a common meadow. Then
M^{\prime}=M_{0}\times(0\cdot M\setminus\{\mathbf{a}\})\sqcup\{\mathbf{a}\}
, with the operations

\begin{cases}(x,0\cdot z)+(y,0\cdot w)=(x+y,0\cdot z\cdot w)\\
(x,0\cdot z)\cdot(y,0\cdot w)=(x\cdot y,0\cdot z\cdot w),\end{cases}

where
(x,0\cdot z),(y,0\cdot w)\in M^{\prime}
, is a common meadow. In particular, if
M
is flasque and such that
M_{0}
is a field, then
M
is isomorphic to
M^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
(M_{0},+,\cdot)
is a ring and
0\cdot M
is a semigroup, both operations are associative on
M^{\prime}
. Let
(x_{i},0\cdot z_{i})\in M^{\prime}
, with
i=1,2,3
. Then

\displaystyle(x_{1},z_{1})\cdot((x_{2},z_{2})+(x_{3},z_{3}))

\displaystyle=(x_{1}\cdot(x_{2}+x_{3}),z_{1}\cdot z_{2}\cdot z_{3})=

\displaystyle(x_{1}\cdot x_{2}+x_{1}\cdot x_{3},z_{1}\cdot z_{2}\cdot z_{1}\cdot z_{3})

\displaystyle=(x_{1},z_{1})\cdot(x_{2},z_{2})+(x_{1},z_{1})\cdot(x_{3},z_{3})

and so distributivity holds on
M^{\prime}
. The elements
(0,0)
and
(1,0)
are the identity of the sum and the product, respectively. For
(x,z)\in M^{\prime}
we have

(x,z)^{-1}=\begin{cases}(x^{-1},z),\text{ if }x\in M_{0}^{\times}\\
\mathbf{a},\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

The remaining common meadow axioms are easy to very.

If
M
is a flasque common meadow such that
M_{0}
is a field, then for all
0\cdot z\in 0\cdot M

M_{0\cdot z}\simeq M_{0}
. Since all surjective homomorphisms between fields are isomorphisms, the transition maps are all isomorphisms. Let
\Phi:M^{\prime}\to M
be the function defined by
\Phi(x,0\cdot z)=f_{0,0\cdot z}(x)=x+0\cdot z
, where
f_{0,0\cdot z}
are the transition maps of
M
. Since all transition maps are isomorphisms,
\Phi
must be bijective. Let us see that it is in fact an homomorphism of common meadows. Let
(x,0\cdot z),(y,0\cdot w)\in M^{\prime}
. Then

\displaystyle\Phi(x,0\cdot z)+\Phi(y,0\cdot w)

\displaystyle=x+0\cdot z+y+0\cdot w=

\displaystyle x+y+0\cdot z\cdot w

\displaystyle=\Phi(x+y,0\cdot z\cdot w).

Similarly, one shows that
\Phi(x,0\cdot z)\cdot\Phi(y,0\cdot w)=\Phi(x\cdot y,0\cdot z\cdot w)
. Also
\Phi(1,0)=1
. Hence
\Phi
is an isomorphism of common meadows, and so

is isomorphic to
M
.
∎

By Theorem
2.4
, a pre-meadow
P
is a common meadow if and only if for all
x\in P
the set
J_{x}=\{0\cdot z\in 0\cdot P\mid x+0\cdot z\in P_{0\cdot z}^{\times}\}
has a greatest element. That is, the study of the elements of the set
J_{x}
allows to check if a pre-meadow with
\mathbf{a}
is a common meadow. In general, this task is somewhat difficult. The next theorem gives a characterization, for flasque common meadows, which is easier to verify in practice. Indeed, it shows that, in that case, one only needs to verify the condition for the elements in
P_{0}
, which should be intuitively obvious since for flasque meadows
P_{0}
determines the whole meadow.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
P
be a flasque pre-meadow with
\mathbf{a}
. Then
J_{x}
has a greatest element, for all
x\in P
, if and only if it has a greatest element for all
x\in P_{0}
.

Proof.

The direct implication is obvious. Let
x\in P
. If
0\cdot x\in J_{x}
, then
J_{x}
has a unique maximal element. So, let us assume that
0\cdot x\notin J_{x}
. Since
P
is flasque, there exists some
y\in P_{0}
such that
x=y+0\cdot x
. Note that if
J_{x}\subseteq J_{y}
, then
0\cdot\overline{y}\cdot x\in J_{x}
, where
\overline{y}
is the greatest element of
J_{y}
. By the definition of
J_{x}
, for every element
0\cdot z\in J_{x}
we have
0\cdot z\leq 0\cdot x
. Since
J_{x}\subseteq J_{y}
we also have
0\cdot z\leq 0\cdot\overline{y}
. Hence
0\cdot z\leq 0\cdot\overline{y}\cdot x
, which implies that
0\cdot\overline{y}\cdot x
is the greatest element of
J_{x}
.
∎
