Introduction

Least-squares regression is a fundamental problem in statistics and machine learning, either from a practical or theoretical standpoint. However, classical methodologies for this problem assume the collected data is clean and light-tailed. Robust Statistics
[
23
,
19
,
32
,
24
]
aim in addressing robust estimation when either the sample is corrupted or the data generating distribution is too heavy-tailed.

In recent work, the minimax optimality of several robust estimation problems have been attained
[
3
,
4
]
and
[
18
]
. The construction of these estimators, however, is based on Tukey’s depth, a hard computational problem in higher dimensions. Fundamental recent works
[
13
,
27
]
have proposed alternative estimators that are both computationally tractable and statistically (near) optimal. For instance, near optimal robust estimators for the mean of a high-dimensional vector can be computed in nearly-linear time
[
5
,
17
,
12
,
20
,
9
]
. We refer to
[
14
,
30
]
for extensive surveys.

In this work, we revisit the problem of
heavy-tailed
least-squares regression assuming to have an
adversarially corrupted

n
-sized sample. Here, “adversarial” means that the sample, corrupted in both labels and features, has at most
\epsilon n
arbitrary outliers for some contamination fraction

\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
. In particular, the adversary mechanism can depend on the (unobserved) clean iid sample. The goal of this paper is to establish near-optimal statistical rates for this problem with a computationally tractable estimator and minimal assumptions. Precisely, our main result can be resumed as follows:

a)

Optimality in high-probability
. We assume that the feature vector
\boldsymbol{x}
has finite covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, with minimum eigenvalue
1/\mu^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2})>0
, maximum eigenvalue
\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|
and condition number
\kappa<\infty
, and satisfy the
L^{4}-L^{2}
hypercontractive property with constant
L>0
. Moreover, the noise-feature multiplier vector
\xi\boldsymbol{x}
is assumed to have finite covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
, with maximum eigenvalue
\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}\|<\infty
. Under these standard assumptions, with probability at least
1-\delta
, our proposed estimator achieves the
\ell_{2}
-norm estimation rate
\mu^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2})\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}\|^{1/2}(\sqrt{p/n}+\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n}+\sqrt{\epsilon})
with sample size of at least
n\geq CL^{4}\kappa^{2}(p\log p+\log(1/\delta))
and contamination fraction of at most
\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{CL^{4}\kappa^{2}}
. Here,
C>0
is a absolute constant. The mentioned rate and cut-offs in
(n,\epsilon)
are all optimal in
(n,p,\delta,\epsilon)
, including the constants
(\mu(\mathbb{B}_{2}),\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|,\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}\|)
, up to a log factor. Using a “least-squares methodology”, the statistical and optimization rates of our algorithm do not depend on the

\ell_{2}
-norm of the ground truth parameter, only on the condition numbers
(\mu^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2})\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}\|^{1/2},\kappa)
.

b)

Heterogeneous noise
. We are mainly concerned with the statistical learning framework over the linear class in mean least-square sense. In this set-up, the noise
\xi
can be arbitrarily dependent on
\boldsymbol{x}
and does not need to be symmetric.

c)

Tractability via spectral methods
. The seminal works
[
13
,
27
]
were the first to suggest that, to construct computationally tractable robust mean estimators, one must exploit the eigenstructure of the sample covariance matrix. Various approaches have been developed since then for robust linear regression. Some current approaches make use of significantly more time consuming approaches such as semi-definite programming (SDP) or sum-of-squares algorithms. Our estimator is computationally tractable by means of faster spectral methods
[
28
,
11
]
. The main computational bottleneck is to run a logarithmic number of iterations of a Multiplicative Weight Update algorithm (MWU)
[
26
,
1
]
in which every iteration requires to approximately solve a maximum eigenvalue problem. This can be done e.g. via a randomized power method.

d)

Unknown covariances and noise level
. With a light-tailed iid clean sample, the least squares estimator is known to be optimal without knowing the covariance matrices
(\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\Xi})
nor the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
. Likewise, our estimator satisfy (a)-(c) without the need to know
(\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\Xi})
nor
\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}\|
.

To the best of our knowledge, as discussed next, we believe this is the first analysis with a computationally tractable algorithm, based on a least-squares methodology instead of a gradient estimation methodology, satisfying simultaneously all the mentioned properties.

1.2
Framework

Let
(y,\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a label-feature pair with centered feature
\boldsymbol{x}
. Within a statistical learning framework, we wish to explain
y
trough
\boldsymbol{x}
via the linear class

F_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}):=\{\langle\cdot,\boldsymbol{b}\rangle:\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}.

Precisely, giving a sample of
(y,\boldsymbol{x})
, we wish to estimate

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}^{*}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathbb{E}\left(y-\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{b}\rangle\right)^{2}.

(4)

In particular, one has

y=\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{b}^{*}\rangle+\xi

with
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
having zero mean and satisfying

\mathbb{E}[\xi\boldsymbol{x}]=0
. This is our only assumption on
\xi
: we do not assume
\xi
and
\boldsymbol{x}
are independent nor that
\xi
is symmetric.

Assumption 1

(Heavy-tails)
.

Assume:

•

The feature vector
\boldsymbol{x}
has distribution
\Pi
and unknown finite non-singular covariance matrix

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}]

with known maximum eigenvalue
\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|<\infty
and minimum eigenvalue
\frac{1}{\mu^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2})}>0
. Moreover,
\boldsymbol{x}
satisfies the
L^{4}-L^{2}
norm equivalence condition with unknown constant
L>0
: for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{B}_{2}
,

\displaystyle\left\{\mathbb{E}|\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle|^{4}\right\}^{1/4}\leq L\left\{\mathbb{E}|\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle|^{2}\right\}^{1/2}.

(5)

•

The centered noise
\xi
satisfy

\mathbb{E}[\xi\boldsymbol{x}]=0
, has finite variance
and the multiplier vector
\xi\boldsymbol{x}
has unknown finite population covariance matrix

\boldsymbol{\Xi}:=\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}]

with unknown maximum eigenvalue
\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}\|
.

L^{4}-L^{2}
norm equivalence is also known as
bounded 4th moment
,
bounded kurtosis
or
hypercontractivity
conditions
[
33
]
.

We consider available a label-feature sample with adversarial contamination.

Assumption 2

(Label-feature adversarial contamination)
.

The contamination fraction will be denoted by

\epsilon:=\frac{o}{n}
. This means that it is available a label-feature sample
\{(\tilde{y}_{\ell},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{2n}
having an arbitrary subset of exactly
o
data points differing from the label-feature sample
\{(y_{\ell},\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{2n}
which is an independent iid copy of
(y,\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We use the notations

\xi_{\ell}:=y_{\ell}-\langle\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{b}^{*}\rangle
and

\tilde{\xi}_{\ell}:=\tilde{y}_{\ell}-\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{b}^{*}\rangle

for all
\ell\in[n]
.

We remark that assuming knowledge of

(\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|,\mu^{-2}(\mathbb{B}_{2}))
is not restrictive in the heavy-tailed corrupted model of Assumption
2
. Using a separate batch of the corrupted sample, there exist tractable robust estimators

(\hat{\lambda}_{\max},\hat{\lambda}_{\min})
satisfying, with high-probability,

\mathsf{a}_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|\leq\hat{\lambda}_{\max}\leq\mathsf{a}_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|

and

\mathsf{a}_{3}\mu^{-2}(\mathbb{B}_{2})\leq\hat{\lambda}_{\min}\leq\mathsf{a}_{4}\mu^{-2}(\mathbb{B}_{2})

for positive constants
(\mathsf{a}_{1},\mathsf{a}_{2},\mathsf{a}_{3},\mathsf{a}_{4})
. See for instance
[
33
]
. Using those estimates in our algorithms entail the same rate of Theorem
1.1
up to changes in absolute constants.

\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Typical classes we will use are the “linear classes”
F_{1}(\mathbb{B}):=\{\langle\cdot,\boldsymbol{v}\rangle:\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{B}\}
and
|F_{1}|(\mathbb{B}):=\{|\langle\cdot,\boldsymbol{v}\rangle|:\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{B}\}
, the “quadratic class”
F_{2}(\mathbb{B}):=\{\langle\cdot,\boldsymbol{v}\rangle^{2}:\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{B}\}
and the “product class”
F(\mathbb{B},\mathbb{B}^{\prime}):=\{\langle\cdot,\boldsymbol{u}\rangle\langle\cdot,\boldsymbol{v}\rangle:\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{B},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\}
.

Letting
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a centered random vector with distribution
\mathbf{P}
, we define the bilinear form

\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{\mathbf{P}}:=\mathbb{E}[\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle],

and the
L^{2}(\mathbf{P})
pseudo-norm

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{P}}:=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle^{2}]}.

We will also define the unit ellipsoid

\mathbb{B}_{\mathbf{P}}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{P}}\leq 1\}
with border

\mathbb{S}_{\mathbf{P}}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{P}}=1\}
.
Let
\mathbb{B}
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The
wimpy variance
of
\mathbb{B}
associated to the distribution of
\boldsymbol{z}
is the quantity

\sigma_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}(\mathbb{B}):=\sigma_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}(F_{1}(\mathbb{B})).

3
Concentration bounds

In all this section,
X
is a random variable taking values on some set
\mathbb{B}
with distribution
\mathbf{P}
and

\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
an iid copy of
X
. We also split the sample
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
into
K
blocks of equal size
B:=n/K
indexed by the partition

\bigcup_{k\in[K]}B_{k}=[n]
. When
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{R}
,
\mu
and

\sigma_{X}^{2}<\infty
will denote the mean and variance of
X
respectively and let
\overline{X}:=X-\mu
. When
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
X=\boldsymbol{z}
will be a centered
p
-dimensional random vector with covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
and
\{X_{i}=\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is an iid copy of
\boldsymbol{z}
.

3.1
Some general bounds

We define, for any
\eta\in[0,1]
, its
\eta
-quantile by
Q_{\overline{X},\eta}
, that is,

\displaystyle Q_{\overline{X},\eta}:=\sup\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}(\overline{X}\geq x)\geq 1-\eta\right\}.

(11)

We will assume without loss on generality that

X
is continuous. In particular,

\mathbb{P}(\overline{X}\geq Q_{\overline{X},\eta})=1-\eta.

The following result follows from the proofs in
[
31
]
. We give a proof for completeness in the Appendix.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\eta\in(0,1/2]
. Then, setting
Q:=Q_{\overline{X},1-\eta/2}
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-\eta n/1.8)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{\overline{X}_{i}\leq Q\}}\geq(1-0.75\eta)n.

(12)

Let
X
be real valued and

\hat{\mu}_{k}:=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\ell\in B_{k}}X_{\ell}.

We restate the following well-known bound for MOM of random variables
[
29
]
. We give a proof in the Appendix for completeness.

Lemma 3.2

(Random variable)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and any constant
C_{\alpha}>0
satisfying

\frac{1}{3C_{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{C_{\alpha}^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{C_{\alpha}^{2/3}}\leq\alpha.

Then, setting
r:=\sigma_{X}\sqrt{\frac{K}{n}}
, with probability at least
1-e^{-K/C_{\alpha}}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{1}_{\{|\hat{\mu}_{k}-\mathbb{E}|X||>C_{\alpha}\cdot r\}}\leq\alpha K.

(13)

By now it is well-known that the previous lemma can be generalized for the Empirical Process over a general class
F
of integrable functions
F:\mathbb{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with respect to
\mathbf{P}
. Define, for each
k\in[K]
and
f\in F
, the block empirical mean

\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{B_{k}}f:=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\ell\in B_{k}}f(X_{\ell}).

For ease, we use the following notation.

Definition 3.3
.

Let

\displaystyle r_{X,n,K}(F):=\frac{\mathscr{D}_{X,n}(F)}{n}\bigvee\sigma_{X}(F)\sqrt{\frac{K}{n}}.

(14)

The following result is Lemma 1 in
[
12
]
.

Lemma 3.4

(Empirical Process)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and any constant
C_{\alpha}>0
satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{28}{4C_{\alpha}}+\frac{4}{C_{\alpha}^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{C_{\alpha}^{2/3}}\leq\alpha.

(15)

Then, setting
r:=r_{X,n,K}(F)
, with probability at least
1-e^{-K/C_{\alpha}}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in F}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{1}_{\{|\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{B_{k}}f-\mathbf{P}f|>C_{\alpha}\cdot r\}}\leq\alpha K.

(16)

For instance, when
F=F_{1}(\mathbb{B}_{2})
one has
r_{\boldsymbol{z},n,k}=2\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})}{n}}\vee\sqrt{\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|\frac{K}{n}}
. When
F=F_{1}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbf{P}})
one has
r_{\boldsymbol{z},n,k}=2\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\vee\sqrt{\frac{K}{n}}
.

3.2
Concentration bounds for linear regression

For linear regression, we shall need the following bound for the Quadratic Process over the linear class.

Proposition 1

(Quadratic Process)
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfies the
L^{4}-L^{2}
norm equivalence property with constant
L>0
. Let

\alpha\in(0,1)
and constant
C_{\alpha}>0
satisfying (
15
).
Let
C>0
be an absolute constant in Lemma
7.4
in the Appendix. Set

\displaystyle r_{n,K}:=CL^{2}\sqrt{\frac{p\log p}{n}}\bigvee L^{2}\sqrt{\frac{K}{n}}.

(17)

Then,

(i)

Upper bound
: given

\rho\in(0,1/2]
and setting
C_{\rho}^{\prime}:=1+\sqrt{2/\rho}
, on an event of probability at least
1-e^{-\rho n/1.8}-e^{-K/C_{\alpha}}
, for all
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathbf{P}}
, for at least
(1-(\alpha+0.75\rho))K
of the blocks,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\ell\in B_{k}}\left(\langle\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle^{2}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\alpha}\left[r_{n,K}\bigvee CC^{\prime}_{\rho}\frac{p\log p}{n}\right].

(18)

(ii)

Lower bound
: for any
\theta>0
, on an event of probability at least
1-e^{-K/C_{\alpha}}
, for all
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathbf{P}}
, for at least
(1-\alpha)K
of the blocks,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\ell\in B_{k}}\left(1-\frac{L^{4}}{\theta}-\langle\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\alpha}\left[r_{n,K}\bigvee C\theta\frac{p\log p}{n}\right].

(19)

Corollary 1

(Product Process)
.

Given
\rho\in(0,1/2]
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, grant assumptions and definitions in Proposition
1
.
Set

\displaystyle r_{n,\rho,K}:=2r_{n,K}+CC_{\alpha}C_{\rho}^{\prime}\frac{p\log p}{2n}+2L^{2}\sqrt{\frac{CC_{\alpha}p\log p}{2n}}.

(20)

Then, on a event of probability at least
1-e^{-\rho n/1.8}-2e^{-K/C_{\alpha}}
, for all
[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}]\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathbf{P}}\times\mathbb{B}_{\mathbf{P}}
, for at least
(1-(2\alpha+0.75\rho))K
of the blocks,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\ell\in B_{k}}\left(\langle\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle-\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{\mathbf{P}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq r_{n,\rho,K}.

(21)

We next present some bounds that are suboptimal with respect to to the confidence level. Nevertheless, they are important to the pre-processing step of linear regression; we make a remark in this regard in the following.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{z}
satisfies the
L^{4}-L^{2}
norm equivalence condition with constant
L>0
.

Then, for all
k\in[K]
and all
t\geq 0
, setting

\displaystyle r_{t}:=L^{2}\left(7\sqrt{\frac{Kt}{n}}+4\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\right),

(22)

on a event of probability at least
1-2e^{-t}
, for all

\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{P}}^{2}-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\ell\in B_{k}}\langle\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leq r_{t}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{P}}^{2}.

(23)

Remark 1
.

The above lower bound can be seen as a MOM-type lower bound for quadratic processes
[
36
]
. It has two important features. First, a direct application of Theorem 3.1 in
[
36
]
leads to a rate of the form
r_{t}\asymp L^{2}\sqrt{\frac{K(p+t)}{n}}
; this is not useful in the optimal regime when
K\geq o\vee\log(1/\delta)
and
p\vee o\vee\log(1/\delta)\lesssim n
we are interested. Indeed, we will only use Proposition
2
with fixed confidence
t\asymp 1
. Second, the above bound holds
for every block

k\in[K]

uniformly over
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathbf{P}}
. While the rate in Proposition
2
is worse than the one in Proposition
1
with respect to the confidence parameter
t>0
, the uniformity on
(k,\boldsymbol{v})
does not hold in item (ii) of Proposition
1
. Indeed the blocks for which the lower bound in item (ii) of Proposition
1
holds
depend on

\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathbf{P}}
. The uniformity property will be fundamental in order to show that the initialization of our algorithm with the Median-of-Least-Squares is bounded in the mentioned regime for
(n,p,o,\delta)
.

The following lemma is immediate from Markov’s inequality and the parallelogram law satisfied by the
\ell_{2}
-norm.

Lemma 3.5
.

For all
k\in[K]
and all
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\ell\in B_{k}}\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{B}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}^{2}\right)(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\delta}}).

(24)

3.3
Random spherical rounding

Only within this section we assume that
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is a fixed (nonrandom) sequence of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The following proof is inspired by Proposition 1 in
[
12
]
. Still, we simplify the proof and enhance the probability level significantly by using a spherical distribution instead of the Gaussian distribution.

Proposition 3

(Spherical rounding of

\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}_{2})
to
\mathbb{S}_{2}
)
.

Suppose that there exist

\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{M}
and
D,\mathsf{b}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}_{\{\langle\mathchoice{{}\mkern-4.5mu}{{}\mkern-4.5mu}{{}\mkern-2.5mu}{}\langle\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\boldsymbol{z}_{i}^{\top},\mathbf{M}\rangle\mathchoice{{}\mkern-4.5mu}{{}\mkern-4.5mu}{{}\mkern-2.5mu}{}\rangle>D\}}>\mathsf{b}m.

(25)

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{S}_{2})
be the uniform distribution over
\mathbb{S}_{2}
. Define the random vector

\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\mathbf{M}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\theta}
where
\mathbf{M}^{1/2}
is the square root of
\mathbf{M}
.

Then for any
\varphi\in(0,\pi/2)
and
\mathsf{a}\in(0,1)
satisfying

\frac{2\varphi\mathsf{b}}{\pi\mathsf{a}}>1,

it holds with probability (on the randomness of

\boldsymbol{\theta}
) of at least

\left(\frac{2\varphi\mathsf{b}}{\pi}-\mathsf{a}\right)^{2}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{|\langle\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle|>(\cos\varphi)\sqrt{D}\right\}}>\mathsf{a}m.

(26)

Remark 2
.

We remark that the argument above is invariant to scaling. In particular, if one has the sane assumption of the proposition for some
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{M}(R\mathbb{S}_{2})
and
R>0
, then for
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{S}_{2})
, the statement of Proposition
3
still holds for
\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbf{M}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\theta}\in R\mathbb{B}_{2}
.

4
Pre-processing & probabilistic arguments

Set-up 1
.

We observe the corrupted data set

\{(\tilde{y}_{\ell},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{n}\cup\{(\tilde{y}_{\ell},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=n+1}^{2n}
and denote the unobserved clean data set by

\{(y_{\ell},\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{n}\cup\{(y_{\ell},\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=n+1}^{2n}
. Given
K\in[n]
, we further split the observed first bucket

\{(\tilde{y}_{\ell},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{n}
into
K
buckets
of same size
B:=n/K
indexed by the partition
\bigcup_{k\in[K]}\tilde{B}_{k}^{(1)}=[n]
. Similarly, the unobserved first bucket

\{(y_{\ell},\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{n}
is split into
K
buckets of same size
B:=n/K
indexed by the partition
\bigcup_{k\in[K]}B_{k}^{(1)}=[n]
. Here we assume
n
is divisible by
K
without loss on generality.

We will use the multivariate notion of median considered by Hsu-Sabato
[
22
]
. For that purpose, we introduce the following notation.

Definition 4.1
.

Given
\mathcal{W}:=\{\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_{K}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, define

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}(\boldsymbol{z}):=\min\left\{r\geq 0:|\mathbb{B}_{2}(\boldsymbol{z},r)\cap\mathcal{W}|>\frac{K}{2}\right\}.

(27)

Algorithm 1

\Pruning(\mathcal{D},K,\eta)

Input: sample
\{(\tilde{y}_{\ell},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{n}\cup\{(\tilde{y}_{\ell},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=n+1}^{2n}
, number of buckets
K
& quantile probability
\eta\in(0,1)
.

1:

Split the first batch
\{(\tilde{y}_{\ell},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{n}
into
K
buckets/batches of equal size
B:=n/K
indexed by the partition
\bigcup_{k\in[K]}\tilde{B}_{k}^{(1)}=[n]
.

2:

For each
k\in[K]
, compute the bucket least-squares estimator

\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}_{k}:=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\ell\in\tilde{B}_{k}^{(1)}}(\tilde{y}_{\ell}-\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{b}\rangle)^{2}.

3:

Compute the Hsu-Sabato’s multivariate median
\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{(0)}
of
\mathcal{W}:=\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}_{K}\}
, that is, set

\bar{k}:=\argmin_{k\in[K]}\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}_{k}),

and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{(0)}:=\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}_{\bar{k}}
.

4:

Using the second batch
\{(\tilde{y}_{\ell},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=n+1}^{2n}
, compute the order statistics

\tilde{R}_{1}^{*}\leq\ldots\leq\tilde{R}_{n}^{*}

of the sequence

\{\tilde{R}_{\ell}:=|\tilde{\xi}_{\ell}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{(0)})|\vee\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell}\|_{2}\}_{\ell=n+1}^{2n}
.

5:

Set
m:=(1-\eta)n
.

6:

Set
\{(\tilde{y}_{i},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
(with some abuse of notation) as the subsample obtained by removing from
\{(\tilde{y}_{\ell},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=n+1}^{2n}
the
n-m=\eta n
“top data points”, that is, points such that

\tilde{R}_{\ell}>\tilde{R}^{*}_{m}
.

7:

return

(\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{(0)},\tilde{R}^{*}_{m},\{(\tilde{y}_{i},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m})
.

Next we state a lemma ensuring that, with high probability, the initialization

\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{(0)}
lies at a constant distance to the ground truth
\boldsymbol{b}^{*}
and the pruned data set is bounded.

Lemma 4.2

(Boundedness of initialization & pruned sample)

\displaystyle o<K/4,\quad\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\eta/4,\quad L^{2}\left(7\sqrt{\frac{K\log(24)}{n}}+4\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(176)

and
\sigma
and gather the bounds in (

In that regard, by Chebyshev’s inequality and rotation invariance of variance

(177)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in F}\mathbb{P}(\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{B_{k}}f-\mathbf{P}f\geq C_{\alpha}r)\leq\frac{K}{n}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}_{X}(F)}{C_{\alpha}^{2}r^{2}}.

(178)

Again, by definition of

\displaystyle\frac{K}{n}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{X}^{2}(F)}{C_{\alpha}^{2}r^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{C_{\alpha}^{2}}.

The statement of the lemma then follows by setting
