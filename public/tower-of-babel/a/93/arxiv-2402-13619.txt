Covariant projective representations of Hilbert–Lie groups
Karl-Hermann Neeb, Francesco G. Russo

Abstract
Hilbert–Lie groups are Lie groups whose Lie algebra is a real Hilbert space whose scalar product is invariant under the adjoint action. These infinite-dimensional Lie groups are the closest relatives to compact Lie groups. In this paper we study unitary representations of these groups from various perspectives. First, we address norm-continuous, also called bounded, representations. These are well-known for simple groups, but the general picture is more complicated. Our first main result is a characterization of the discrete decomposability of all bounded representations in terms of boundedness of the set of coroots. We also show that bounded representations of type II and III exist if the set of coroots is unbounded. Second, we use covariance with respect to a one-parameter group of automorphisms to implement some regularity. Here we develop some perturbation theory based on half Lie groups that reduces matters to the case where a “maximal torus” is fixed, so that compatible weight decompositions can be studied. Third, we extend the context to projective representations which are covariant for a one-parameter group of automorphisms. Here important families of representations arise from “bounded extremal weights”, and for these the corresponding central extensions can be determined explicitly, together with all one-parameter groups for which a covariant extension exists. Keywords: Hilbert–Lie group, unitary representation, semibounded representation, infinite-dimensional Lie group, half Lie group MSC 2020: 22E65, 22E45, 22E10, 22E60.



1
Introduction

We call a real Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
endowed with an invariant
positive definite form turning it into a Hilbert space a

Hilbert–Lie algebra
. Accordingly, we call a
Lie group
G
whose Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
is a Hilbert–Lie algebra
a
Hilbert–Lie group
. Hilbert–Lie groups are the closest
infinite-dimensional relatives of compact Lie groups.
To understand their unitary representation theory thus
is a natural building block in the unitary representation
theory of infinite-dimensional Lie groups.
The present work addresses unitary representations of
Hilbert–Lie groups from a rather general perspective.

The simple infinite-dimensional Hilbert–Lie algebras
(all closed ideals are trivial)
are of the form
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})
(skew-adjoint Hilbert–Schmidt
operators), where
{\sf H}
is an infinite-dimensional real, complex
or quaternionic Hilbert space (
[
Sch60
]
).
This generalizes the well-known simple compact Lie algebras

{\mathfrak{o}}(n)
,
{\mathfrak{u}}(n)
and
\mathfrak{sp}(n)
.
If
B_{2}({\sf H})
denotes the ideal of Hilbert–Schmidt
operators on
{\sf H}
, then one obtains the associated Lie groups

\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})=\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits({\sf H})\cap(\mathbf{1}+B_{2}({\sf H}))
, consisting of unitary
operators that are Hilbert–Schmidt perturbations of the identity.
Hilbert–Lie algebras are orthogonal direct sums of their
center and their simple ideals (
[
Sch60
, §1.2, Thm. 1]
).
We call a Hilbert–Lie algebra
semisimple

if its center is trivial.

Section
2
recalls the cornerstones of the
structure of Hilbert–Lie algebras and the classification of the
simple ones, due to Schue
[
Sch60
,
Sch61
]
.
It thus provides a solid
foundation for the representation theory of Hilbert–Lie groups.
To understand one-parameter groups of automorphisms
of Hilbert–Lie groups,
it is instrumental to know that they preserve all simple ideals
(Lemma
210
). We also recall the root decomposition of
simple infinite-dimensional Hilbert–Lie algebras
because the boundedness of the set

\Delta^{\vee}
of coroots plays a key role for bounded representations
(Theorem
37
).

The main results of this paper are contained in Sections
3
,

4
and
5
. Each of these sections is devoted to a specific class
of representations: the bounded (=norm continuous) ones
(Section
3
),
those that are covariant for one-parameter groups
of automorphisms (Section
4
),
and projective representations with
suitable semiboundedness properties (Section
5
).

We start in Section
3
with bounded
representations.
For compact groups, it is well-known that
their unitary representations are direct sums of irreducible
ones and that the irreducible representations are finite-dimensional.
For Hilbert–Lie groups the situation is more complicated.
However, the
bounded
representations
are the most well-behaved. They are defined by

\pi
to be continuous with respect to the norm topology on
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{H})
.
By basic Lie theory, they correspond to
Lie algebra homomorphisms
{\mathfrak{g}}\to{\mathfrak{u}}(\mathcal{H})
for a complex
Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. A key result is
Theorem
37
, which leads to a complete classification,
provided the set
\Delta^{\vee}
of coroots is bounded,
which is the case for simple Hilbert–Lie algebras.
Then bounded representations are direct sums of irreducible ones, and these
can be classified in terms of extremal weights.
This should in particular be compared with the corresponding
result for unitary representations of compact groups,
where the boundedness condition is not needed to arrive at the
same conclusion (
[
HM06
,
HiNe12
]
).

For the group
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
, where
{\sf H}\cong\ell^{2}(J,{\mathbb{C}})
is complex,
the classification of bounded unitary representations
was obtained in
[
Ne98
]
,
and the irreducible ones where parametrized by
“highest weights”
\lambda=(\lambda_{j})_{j\in J}
. They all arise
as irreducible subrepresentations of finite tensor products
(
[
Ne14b
, Thm. 3.17]
,
[
Ki73
]
,
[
Ol78
]
,
[
Bo80
]
).
In
[
Ne12
]
these results have
been extended to the real and quaternionic case.
This shows that the bounded representations
of simple Hilbert–Lie groups resemble the representations
of compact groups.
This analogy can even be extended to a
Borel–Weil theory for coadjoint orbits of the group
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
,
which only produces the bounded irreducible representations
(
[
Bo80
]
; see also
[
Ne04
]
). In
[
Bo80
, Thms. 5.2, 5.5]
Boyer
also constructs factorial representation
of
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
of type II
1
and type III, respectively. Here we
refer to von Neumann algebras of types I, II and III as per
[
Sa71
]
).
This shows that, unlike the situation
for compact groups, the (unbounded) unitary representation
theory of
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
is not type I.

In the light of Boyer’s result, it is interesting that,
for semisimple Hilbert–Lie algebras with unbounded coroot systems,
bounded representations of type II and III also exist
(Theorem
311
).
As a consequence, a classification of the
irreducible ones is not a well-posed problem
(
[
Gli60
,
Sa67
]
). This provides a rather clear picture of the bounded
unitary representations of a general Hilbert–Lie group.

To go beyond bounded representations, we restrict our attention
to the simple Hilbert–Lie algebra

{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})
, where
{\sf H}
is a separable infinite-dimensional
complex Hilbert space, and the corresponding group
G=\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
. In Section
4
we ask
which restrictions it imposes
for a unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
G

to be extendable to a representation of

G\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
for a one-parameter group

(\alpha_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
of automorphisms of
G
.
For any such
\alpha
, there exists a selfadjoint operator

H
on
{\sf H}
with

\alpha_{t}(g)=e^{itH}ge^{-itH}\quad\mbox{ for }\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},g\in G

(Theorem
29
).
Therefore the problem is non-trivial if and only if

H
is not a Hilbert–Schmidt operator.

In Subsection
4.1
we prove a rather general
Perturbation Theorem (Theorem
43
)
that can in particular be used to reduce questions concerning
covariance of unitary representations with respect to some
\alpha

to the case where

is diagonalizable
(Corollary

). This observation is based on
a Weyl–von Neumann decomposition

, where

H_{d}
is diagonalizable and
H_{\rm HS}
is Hilbert–Schmidt.
We may assume henceforth that

is diagonalizable,
which implies that it fixes a maximal abelian subalgebra

{\mathfrak{t}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
pointwise. We write

T:=\exp{\mathfrak{t}}\subseteq G

for the corresponding “maximal torus”, an abelian connected
Banach–Lie group.
Then we show that any factorial unitary representation

(\pi,\mathcal{H})
that is a
T
-weight representation
,
i.e., has a basis of
T
-eigenvectors,
extends to
G\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
(Proposition
411
).
As these results apply equally well to projective representations,
we briefly discuss in Subsection
4.2
some techniques
for the identification of the corresponding cocycles. An important
feature of Hilbert–Lie algebras is that the invariant scalar product
provides a bijection between (bounded) derivations and
2
-cocycles.

For representations of
G\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
, we can ask for the existence
of ground states, as
recently studied in
[
MN16
,
NR24
]
,
but also investigate the semiboundedness of the extended representation,
or conditions
for
U_{t}:=\widehat{\pi}(e,t)
to have spectrum which is bounded from below.
To this end, we need to understand the structure of the subgroup

G^{0}=\mathop{{\rm Fix}}\nolimits(\alpha)
of
\alpha
-fixed points in
G
,
which is described in Subsection
4.3
.
Moreover, we need to understand which representations
of
G^{0}
can possibly arise on the minimal
U
-eigenspace.
As
G
contains a dense subgroup
G_{\rm alg}\cong\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{\infty}({\mathbb{C}})
,
which is a direct
limit of compact Lie groups, the techniques developed in

[
NR24
]
provide important necessary conditions for this to be
the case (Theorem
418
in Subsection
4.4
).

In Subsection
4.5
, we discuss a class of
representations which is a very special case of a
construction that has been used in
[
Bo80
]
to show that

\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
has factor representations of type II
1
.
In our context, this provides representations of
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})

that are covariant for the full automorphism group,
but contain no non-zero
T
-eigenvector.

The next larger class to bounded representations
are the semibounded ones,
whose theory has been developed
in
[
Ne08
,
Ne10b
,
Ne12
,
Ne14
,
NZ13
]
.
Since all finite-dimensional continuous unitary representations are bounded
and most of the unitary representations appearing in physics are semibounded
(see
[
DA68
,
PS86
,
Ne10b
,
KR87
]
),
semiboundedness is a natural regularity
condition for unitary representations of infinite-dimensional Lie groups.

Theorem
52

asserts that semibounded unitary representations of connected
Hilbert–Lie groups decompose discretely into bounded ones,
hence provide nothing new beyond what we have seen already in
Section
3
.
This is complemented by Theorem
54
, which,
for semibounded projective representations, shows
that they can all be lifted to representations of the simply connected
covering group. Its proof involves the remarkable Bruhat–Tits Fixed
Point Theorem (see Appendix
B
).

As a consequence, enlarging our context to projective semibounded
representations of
G
does not lead to new representations,
but projective representations of the groups

G\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
for which the spectrum of the one-parameter group

\pi(e,t)
is bounded below are far more interesting.
Here non-trivial central
{\mathbb{T}}
-extensions appear.
Luckily, the Hilbert space structure of
{\mathfrak{g}}
is quite helpful to
represent continuous Lie algebra cocycles by derivations.

As we go along, we learn that it is too restrictive
to consider
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
as a
Lie group, in particular, when we consider
one-parameter groups
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
of automorphisms
that act only continuously and not smoothly, i.e., their
infinitesimal generator is unbounded.
This is taken into account in
Subsection
5.2
, where we show that the existence of a
unitary representation of
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
,
whose restriction to the Lie group

\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})^{\infty}\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}

is semibounded, requires the infinitesimal generator
i\mathbf{d}
of
\alpha

to be semibounded as an operator on
{\sf H}

(Corollary
59
). This result builds on some fine analysis
of the coadjoint action of the semidirect product Lie group
(Theorem
58
).
If
\mathbf{d}
is unbounded, then
the topological group
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
is not a
Lie group, but it is a smooth Banach manifold and even a
half Lie group in the sense of
[
MN18
]
, a fact
that has already been exploited in the proof of
Theorem
43
. Then

\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})^{\infty}\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
is the Lie group
of all elements for which right multiplications are also smooth,
so that the passage between these two perspectives is well
captured by the half Lie group concept.

Subsections
5.3
and

5.4
are devoted to projective representations
of the restricted unitary group
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{\rm res}({\sf H},D)\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits({\sf H})

for an operator
D
on a Hilbert space
{\sf H}
,
where the “restriction” is expressed in terms of
gDg^{-1}-D
being
Hilbert–Schmidt. We recall in Theorem
517
(from
[
Ne04
]
)
that the extremal weight representation
of the trace class group
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{1}({\sf H})
, corresponding to a bounded weight,
all extend to projective representations of restricted groups,
which contain in particular the group
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
. So we obtain
a large family of projective representations of this Hilbert–Lie group.
In Theorem
517
we show that the restricted groups are maximal
“covariance groups” of these representations of
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{1}({\sf H})
.
These results provide a new perspective and
relevant information on significant constructions
which arise naturally in Quantum Field Theory
(see
[
PS86
,
KR87
,
Ne10b
]
).

Although we formulated several challenging problems in the
main text, we also add a brief section on perspectives, where we discuss
some directions of research that target important tools to
analyze unitary representations of infinite-dimensional Lie groups.

Hilbert–Lie groups and Lie algebras

This section presents the cornerstones of the

Sch61

We start in Subsection

Definition 21

(
[

A

An

If we want, in addition, that it is isometric, we shall always

Of course, we can also introduce complex Hilbert–Lie algebras, leaving unaltered Definition

Then the Closed Graph Theorem
[

Every Hilbert–Lie algebra

{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}})\oplus\widehat{\bigoplus}_{j\in J}{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}.

(9)

Each simple infinite-dimensional

for an infinite-dimensional real, complex or

It is easy to see that every ideal of

Remark 28

Note that every infinite-dimensional

2.2
Automorphism groups

In this short subsection we collect some results
on automorphism groups of Hilbert–Lie algebras.
We describe the automorphisms of the simple factors and show that
connected groups of automorphisms preserve all simple factors
(Lemma
210

in Proposition

of the Lie algebra of Hilbert–Schmidt operators.

For a complex Hilbert space
{\sf H}
, we write

\mathop{{\rm AU}}\nolimits({\sf H})
for the group of unitary (or antiunitary) isometries
of
{\sf H}
and

\mathop{{\rm PAU}}\nolimits({\sf H}):=\mathop{{\rm AU}}\nolimits({\sf H})/{\mathbb{T}}\mathbf{1}\cong\mathop{\rm PU{}}\nolimits({\sf H})\rtimes\{\mathop{{\rm id}}\nolimits,\sigma\},

denotes the projective unitary (or antiunitary) isometries of
{\sf H}
,
where
\sigma
is an anticonjugation of
{\sf H}
.

We recall the structure of the automorphism groups
of simple infinite-dimensional Hilbert–Lie algebras:

Theorem 29
.

(
[
Ne14
, Thm. 1.15]
)

The automorphism groups of the simple infinite-dimensional
Hilbert–Lie algebras are given by

\mathop{{\rm Aut}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H}))\cong\mathop{{\rm PAU}}\nolimits({\sf H})

for a complex Hilbert space
{\sf H}
,
and for the real and quaternionic case we have

\mathop{{\rm Aut}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{o}}_{2}({\sf H}))\cong\mathop{\rm O{}}\nolimits({\sf H})/\{\pm\mathbf{1}\}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathop{{\rm Aut}}\nolimits(\mathop{{\mathfrak{sp}}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H}))\cong\mathop{{\rm Sp}}\nolimits({\sf H})/\{\pm\mathbf{1}\}.

It follows in particular that the group
\mathop{{\rm Aut}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H}))

always has
2
connected components, that
\mathop{{\rm Aut}}\nolimits(\mathfrak{sp}_{2}({\sf H}))
is always
connected, and that
\mathop{{\rm Aut}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{o}}_{2}({\sf H}))
is connected if and only if

\mathop{{\rm dim}}\nolimits{\sf H}
is not even and finite (
[
Ne02
]
).
Automorphisms of finite order of simple
infinite-dimensional Hilbert–Lie algebras
are discussed in
[
Ne14
, App. D]
, with the purpose to study
related twisted loop groups with Hilbert target groups.
The group
\mathop{{\rm Aut}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{g}})
of the topological Lie algebra
automorphisms of the Hilbert–Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}

preserves the center
{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}})
and permutes the
simple ideals
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
in the decomposition (
9
).
The following lemma draws an important
conclusion for the action of connected groups on
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
It applies in particular to continuous
{\mathbb{R}}
-actions by automorphisms.

Lemma 210
.

If a connected topological group
acts on a Hilbert–Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}

by isometric automorphism and continuous orbit maps,
then it preserves all simple ideals of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

Proof.

Let
A
be a connected topological group acting on

{\mathfrak{g}}
by automorphisms and with continuous orbit maps.
Further, let
({\mathfrak{g}}_{j})_{j\in J}
denote the simple ideals of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
For any unit vector
x_{j}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
, the function

f\colon A\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad f(\gamma)=\langle x_{j},\gamma.x_{j}\rangle

is continuous with
f(e)=1
. Hence there exists an identity
neighborhood
U\subseteq A
with
f(\gamma)>0
for
\gamma\in U
.
Then
\gamma\in U
implies that
\gamma.x_{j}
is not orthogonal to
x_{j}
,
hence cannot be contained in any other simple ideal.
As
\gamma
permutes the simple ideals, it follows that

\gamma({\mathfrak{g}}_{j})={\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
. This shows that the stabilizer

A_{j}:=\{\gamma\in A\colon\gamma({\mathfrak{g}}_{j})={\mathfrak{g}}_{j}\}

is an open subgroup of
A
, thus equal to
A
by connectedness.
We conclude that
A
leaves
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
invariant.
∎

2.3
Root decomposition

In this section we review some basic facts
concerning real and complex root space decompositions of Hilbert–Lie algebras
(
[
Bo80
,
MN16
,
MN17
,
Ne12
,
Ne98
,
NeSt01
,
Sch60
,
Sch61
]
).
In the context of Hilbert–Lie algebras,
the role of Cartan subalgebras from the finite-dimensional
theory (
[
HiNe12
]
) is taken over by the
maximal abelian subalgebras; recall that for compact Lie algebras
these are precisely the Cartan subalgebras.
The existence of maximal abelian subalgebras follows immediately
from Zorn’s Lemma,
but, unlike the finite-dimensional context, they are not all conjugate
(
[
NeSt01
]
).
However, Schue’s work always implies the existence of a corresponding
root space decomposition in the topological sense
(
[
Sch61
]
).

Definition 211
.

(
[
Ne00
, §IX.1]
)
Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be a real Hilbert–Lie algebra and
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}
be its complexification.
For
x,y\in{\mathfrak{g}}
, we put
(x+iy)^{*}:=-x+iy
,
which defines an antilinear antiautomorphism of the complex
Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}
, so that

{\mathfrak{g}}=\{x\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}\mid x^{*}=-x\}.

(10)

Let
{\mathfrak{t}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
be a maximal abelian subalgebra
and
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}
be its complexification.
For a linear functional
\alpha\in{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
(the algebraic dual space),

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha}=\{x\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}\mid(\forall h\in{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}})\ [h,x]=\alpha(h)x\}

(11)

is called the corresponding
root space
, and

\Delta:=\Delta({\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}},{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}):=\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\setminus\{0\}\mid{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha}\not=\{0\}\}

(12)

is called the
root system
of the pair
({\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}},{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}})
.
We then have
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{0}={\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
because
{\mathfrak{t}}
is maximal abelian,
and

[{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha},{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\beta}]\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha+\beta}\quad\mbox{ for }\quad\alpha,\beta\in{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*}

follows from
the fact that
\mathop{{\rm ad}}\nolimits{\mathfrak{t}}
consists of derivations; in particular

[{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha},{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{-\alpha}]\subseteq{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be a Hilbert–Lie algebra and

{\mathfrak{t}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
be a maximal abelian subalgebra.
According to
[
Sch61
]
,
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}
defines an
orthogonal root space decomposition

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}={\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}\oplus\widehat{\bigoplus}_{\alpha\in\Delta}{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha}

which is a Hilbert space direct sum (indicated by the hat over
\oplus
).
As for compact Lie algebras, all root spaces

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha}={\mathbb{C}}x_{\alpha}
are one-dimensional
and
\alpha([x_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}^{*}])>0
for
0\not=x_{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha}
.
For every
\alpha\in\Delta
we have

\alpha({\mathfrak{t}})\subseteq i{\mathbb{R}}
and therefore

x\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha}
implies
x^{*}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{-\alpha}
.

Definition 212
.

(Coroots and Weyl group,
[
Sch61
, §2.2]
)
From
\alpha([x_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}^{*}])>0
it follows that
there exists a unique element

\alpha^{\vee}\in{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}\cap[{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha},{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{-\alpha}]\quad\mbox{ satisfying }\quad\alpha(\alpha^{\vee})=2.

It is called the
coroot of
\alpha

and
\Delta^{\vee}:=\{\alpha^{\vee}\mid\alpha\in\Delta\}

is called the
coroot system
.

The
Weyl group

\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}({\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}},{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}})\subseteq\mathop{{\rm GL}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}})
is the subgroup generated by
all reflections

r_{\alpha}(x):=x-\alpha(x)\alpha^{\vee}\quad\mbox{ for }\quad\alpha\in\Delta_{c}.

(13)

It acts on the dual space
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
by the adjoint maps

r_{\alpha}^{*}(\beta):=\beta-\beta(\alpha^{\vee})\alpha\quad\mbox{ for }\quad\beta\in{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*},2.10

(14)

and this action preserves the root system
\Delta
.

In the finite-dimensional case
\mathcal{W}
is finite (
[
Ne00
, Prop. VII.2.10]
),
but this is no longer true in the infinite-dimensional case
(
[
Ne98
, Rem. V.2]
).

If
{\mathfrak{g}}
is semisimple, then

{\mathfrak{t}}_{\rm alg}:=i\mathop{{\rm span}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}}(\Delta^{\vee})\subseteq{\mathfrak{t}}

is a dense subspace of
{\mathfrak{t}}
. We write

\langle x+iy,x^{\prime}+iy^{\prime}\rangle:=\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle+\langle y,y^{\prime}\rangle+i(\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle-\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle)

for the
unique hermitian extension

of the scalar product on
{\mathfrak{g}}
to
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}

(linear in the second argument). It
satisfies

\langle[x,y],z\rangle=\langle y,[x^{*},z]\rangle\quad\mbox{ for }\quad x,y,z\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}.

We denote by
{\mathfrak{t}}^{\prime}
, resp.,
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
,
the space of continuous linear functionals on
{\mathfrak{t}}
, resp.,
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
.
For
\alpha\in{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
, we write

\alpha^{\sharp}\in{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
for the uniquely determined element satisfying

\alpha(h)=\langle\alpha^{\sharp},h\rangle\quad\mbox{ for }\quad h\in{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}.

(15)

The bijection

\sharp\colon{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}\to{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
is antilinear, and

\alpha(\beta^{\sharp})=\langle\alpha^{\sharp},\beta^{\sharp}\rangle=\langle\beta^{\sharp},\alpha^{\sharp}\rangle=\beta(\alpha^{\sharp})\quad\mbox{ for }\quad\alpha,\beta\in i{\mathfrak{t}}^{\prime}.

For
h\in{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
and
x_{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha}
, the relation

\langle h,[x_{\alpha}^{*},x_{\alpha}]\rangle=\langle[x_{\alpha},h],x_{\alpha}\rangle=-\alpha(h)\langle x_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\rangle=-\langle h,\|x_{\alpha}\|^{2}\alpha^{\sharp}\rangle

then leads to

[x_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}^{*}]=\|x_{\alpha}\|^{2}\alpha^{\sharp}.

(16)

From
\alpha(\alpha^{\vee})=2
,
\alpha^{\sharp}\in{\mathbb{C}}\alpha^{\vee}

and
\alpha(\alpha^{\sharp})=\|\alpha^{\sharp}\|^{2}=\|\alpha\|^{2}

it follows that

\alpha^{\vee}=\frac{2}{\|\alpha\|^{2}}\alpha^{\sharp}\quad\mbox{ and therefore }\quad\|\alpha^{\vee}\|=\frac{2}{\|\alpha\|}.

(17)

The preceding discussion provides a rather direct way to determine
the number
c_{\mathfrak{g}}
in terms of the root decomposition:

Lemma 213
.

We have

c_{\mathfrak{g}}=\sup\{\|\alpha\|\colon\alpha\in\Delta\}=\frac{2}{\inf\{\|\alpha^{\vee}\|\colon\alpha\in\Delta\}}.

Proof.

Let
0\not=x_{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha}
.
Then
y_{\alpha}:=x_{\alpha}-x_{\alpha}^{*}\in{\mathfrak{g}}
with orthogonal summands, so
that

\|y_{\alpha}\|^{2}=2\|x_{\alpha}\|^{2}=\|i(x_{\alpha}+x_{\alpha}^{*})\|,

and

\|[i\alpha^{\vee},y_{\alpha}]\|=2\|i(x_{\alpha}+x_{\alpha}^{*})\|=2\|y_{\alpha}\|\leq c_{\mathfrak{g}}\|\alpha^{\vee}\|\|y_{\alpha}\|,

so that
c_{\mathfrak{g}}\geq\frac{2}{\|\alpha^{\vee}\|}=\|\alpha\|.

Conversely, for
h\in{\mathfrak{t}}
, the root decomposition shows that

\|\mathop{{\rm ad}}\nolimits h\|=\sup\{|\alpha(h)\|\colon\alpha\in\Delta\}\leq\|h\|\sup\{\|\alpha\|\colon\alpha\in\Delta\}.

Since every element in the dense subspace

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\rm alg}:={\mathfrak{t}}_{\rm alg}+\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta}\{x_{\alpha}-x_{\alpha}^{*}\colon x_{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\alpha}\}

is conjugate under an inner automorphism to an element of
{\mathfrak{t}}
,
and since conjugate elements have the same norm, it follows by continuity that

\|\mathop{{\rm ad}}\nolimits x\|\leq\|x\|\sup\{\|\alpha\|\colon\alpha\in\Delta\}

for
x\in{\mathfrak{g}},

and hence that
c_{\mathfrak{g}}\leq\sup\{\|\alpha\|\colon\alpha\in\Delta\}
.
∎

After these preparations, we can now introduce weights;
see also
[
MN17
,
HiNe12
,
Ne00
,
Ne98
]
for a similar formalization.

Definition 214
.

(Bounded and continuous weights,
[
Ne98
, §§I.11-14]
)
A linear functional
\lambda\colon({\mathfrak{t}}_{\rm alg})_{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}

is called a
weight
or an
integral weight
if
\lambda(\Delta^{\vee})\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}
.
A weight
\lambda
is said to be
bounded
if

\lambda(\Delta^{\vee})
is a bounded subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We write
\mathcal{P}\subseteq i{\mathfrak{t}}_{\rm alg}^{*}
for the group of weights and

\mathcal{P}_{b}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
for the subgroup of bounded weights.

Since not all bounded weights are continuous on
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\rm alg}

with respect to the subspace topology inherited from
{\mathfrak{t}}

(Example
215
),
we cannot identify
\mathcal{P}_{b}
with a subset of the topological dual
space
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
. Accordingly, we write

\mathcal{P}_{c}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{b}
for the subgroup of continuous bounded weights.

We now describe the relevant root data for the three concrete types of
Hilbert Lie algebras
{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})
, where
{\sf H}
is a
Hilbert space over
{\mathbb{K}}\in\{{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{H}}\}
.

Example 215
.

(Root data of unitary Lie algebras,
[
Ne12
, Ex. C.4]
)
Let
{\sf H}
be a complex Hilbert space with
orthonormal basis
(e_{j})_{j\in J}
.
Let
{\mathfrak{g}}:={\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})
and let
{\mathfrak{t}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
be the
subalgebra of all diagonal operators with respect to the
e_{j}
.
Then
{\mathfrak{t}}
is a maximal abelian subalgebra. The set of
roots of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathop{{\mathfrak{gl}}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
with respect to
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
is given by the root
system

\Delta=\{\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}\colon j\not=k\in J\}=:A_{J}.

Here the operator

E_{jk}e_{m}:=\delta_{km}e_{j}
is a
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
-eigenvector
in
\mathop{{\mathfrak{gl}}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
generating the corresponding eigenspace
and
\varepsilon_{j}(\mathop{{\rm diag}}\nolimits(h_{k})_{k\in J})=h_{j}
.
From
E_{jk}^{*}=E_{kj}
it follows that

(\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k})^{\vee}=E_{jj}-E_{kk}=[E_{jk},E_{kj}]=[E_{jk},E_{jk}^{*}],

which leads with (
17
) to

\|(\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k})^{\vee}\|=\sqrt{2}\quad\mbox{ and thus }\quad\|\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}\|=\sqrt{2}.

(18)

With Lemma
213
we now obtain
c_{\mathfrak{g}}=\sqrt{2}.

Weights:
We have

{\mathfrak{t}}_{\rm alg}=i\mathop{{\rm span}}\nolimits\{E_{jj}-E_{kk}\colon j\not=k\},

and each linear functional on
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\rm alg}
can be represented by a
function
\lambda\colon J\to{\mathbb{C}},j\mapsto\lambda_{j}
via

\lambda(E_{jj}-E_{kk})=\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}.

It is a weight if and only if all differences

\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}
are integral, and in this case
it can be represented by a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-valued function

\lambda\colon J\to{\mathbb{Z}}
. In this case its boundedness is equivalent to

\lambda(J)
being finite and it is continuous if and only if
its support
\mathop{{\rm supp}}\nolimits(\lambda)
is finite.

Weyl group
:
The Weyl group
\mathcal{W}
is isomorphic to the group
S_{(J)}
of finite
permutations of
J
, acting in the canonical way on
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
.
It is generated by the reflections
r_{jk}:=r_{\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}}

corresponding to the transpositions of
j
and
k
.
The Weyl group acts transitively on the set of roots and, in particular,
all roots have the same length with respect to the dual norm on
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}
.

This generalizes the root decomposition of
the reductive Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{u}}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathop{{\mathfrak{gl}}}\nolimits_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
with respect to the
Cartan subalgebra of diagonal matrices. It matches the general
context if
J=\{1,\ldots,n\}
is finite.

Remark 216
.

In many situations it is convenient to describe
real Hilbert spaces as pairs

({\sf H},\tau)
, where
{\sf H}
is a complex Hilbert space
and
\tau\colon{\sf H}\to{\sf H}
is a
conjugation
, i.e.,
an antilinear isometry with
\tau^{2}=\mathop{{\rm id}}\nolimits_{\sf H}
.

A quaternionic Hilbert space
{\sf H}
can be considered as a
complex Hilbert space
{\sf H}^{\mathbb{C}}
(the underlying complex Hilbert
space), endowed with an
anticonjugation

\tau
, i.e.,

\tau
is an antilinear isometry with
\tau^{2}=-\mathbf{1}
.

Example 217
.

(Root data of orthogonal Lie algebras,
[
Ne12
, Ex. C.6]
)
Let
{\sf H}
be a real Hilbert space
and
{\mathfrak{g}}:={\mathfrak{o}}_{2}({\sf H})
be the corresponding Hilbert–Lie
algebra. Let
{\mathfrak{t}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
be a maximal abelian subalgebra.
The fact that
{\mathfrak{t}}
is maximal
abelian implies that the common kernel

{\sf H}^{\mathfrak{t}}:=\bigcap_{x\in{\mathfrak{t}}}\ker x

(19)

is at most
one-dimensional. Since
{\mathfrak{t}}
consists of compact skew-symmetric operators,
the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
is simultaneously
diagonalizable on the complexification

{\sf H}_{\mathbb{C}}
, which implies that the space
({\sf H}^{\mathfrak{t}})^{\bot}

carries an isometric complex
structure
I
commuting with
{\mathfrak{t}}
and there exists an orthonormal
subset
(e_{j})_{j\in J}
of
({\sf H}^{\mathfrak{t}})^{\bot}
such that
the set
\{e_{j},Ie_{j}\colon j\in J\}
is a real
orthonormal basis of
({\sf H}^{\mathfrak{t}})^{\bot}

and the planes
{\mathbb{R}}e_{j}+{\mathbb{R}}Ie_{j}
are
{\mathfrak{t}}
-invariant.
If
{\sf H}^{{\mathfrak{t}}}
is non-zero, we write
e_{j_{0}}
for a unit vector
in this space and put
f_{j_{0}}:=e_{j_{0}}
.
For
j_{0}\not=j\in J
, we put

f_{j}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_{j}-iIe_{j})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad f_{-j}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_{j}+iIe_{j}).

Then
(f_{j})_{j\in J}
form a complex orthonormal basis of
{\sf H}_{\mathbb{C}}
consisting
of
{\mathfrak{t}}
-eigenvectors. For the complex bilinear extension

\beta\colon{\sf H}_{\mathbb{C}}\times{\sf H}_{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
of the scalar product on
{\sf H}
, we
have

\beta(f_{j_{0}},f_{j_{0}})=1,\quad\beta(f_{j},f_{j})=\beta(f_{-j},f_{-j})=0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\beta(f_{j},f_{-j})=1.

We conclude that
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
coincides with the space of those elements
in
{\mathfrak{o}}_{2}({\sf H}_{\mathbb{C}},\beta)={\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}
which are diagonal with the orthonormal basis consisting
of the
f_{\pm j}
. Hence
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}\cong\ell^{2}(J,{\mathbb{C}})

(as a Hilbert space), where

x\in{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
corresponds to the element
(x_{j})_{j\in J}\in\ell^{2}(J,{\mathbb{C}})

defined by
xf_{j}=x_{j}f_{j}
,
j\in J
.
For
x_{j}\in i{\mathbb{R}}
we then have

x\in{\mathfrak{t}}
with

xe_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}x(f_{j}+f_{-j})=\frac{x_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}(f_{j}-f_{-j})=-ix_{j}Ie_{j}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad xIe_{j}=ix_{j}e_{j}.

Therefore

\|x\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j\in J}\|xe_{j}\|^{2}+\|xIe_{j}\|^{2}=2\sum_{j\in J}|x_{j}|^{2}.

(20)

Writing
\varepsilon_{j}(x):=x_{j}
, we see that

\{\pm\varepsilon_{j}:j\in J\}
, together with
\varepsilon_{j_{0}}
if

{\sf H}^{\mathfrak{t}}\not=\{0\}
, is the set of
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
-weights of
{\sf H}_{\mathbb{C}}
.
Accordingly, the set of
roots of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}
with respect to
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
is given by

\Delta=\{\pm\varepsilon_{j}\pm\varepsilon_{k}\colon j\not=k,j,k\in J\}=:D_{J}\quad\mbox{ if }\quad{\sf H}^{\mathfrak{t}}=\{0\},

and

\Delta=\{\pm\varepsilon_{j}\pm\varepsilon_{k}\colon j\not=k,j,k\in J\}\cup\{\pm\varepsilon_{j}\colon j\in J\}=:B_{J}\quad\mbox{ if }\quad{\sf H}^{\mathfrak{t}}\not=\{0\}.

If
{\sf H}
is finite-dimensional and
\mathop{{\rm dim}}\nolimits{\sf H}=2n
is even,
then
{\sf H}^{\mathfrak{t}}=\{0\}
, and we obtain a root system of type
D_{n}
. If
\mathop{{\rm dim}}\nolimits{\sf H}=2n+1
is odd, then
\Delta
is of type
B_{n}

(
[
HiNe12
, Ex. 6.3.10]
).

We put
E_{j}:=E_{jj}-E_{-j,-j}
for
j\not=j_{0}
and
E_{j_{0}}:=E_{j_{0},j_{0}}
.
If
\Delta
is of type
D_{J}
, then the coroots are of the form

\pm E_{j}\pm E_{k},j\not=k
,
and
for
\Delta
of type
B_{J}
the coroots are of the form

(\pm\varepsilon_{j}\pm\varepsilon_{k})^{\vee}=\pm E_{j}\pm E_{k}\quad\mbox{ for }\quad j\not=k\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\pm\varepsilon_{j}^{\vee}=\pm 2E_{j},j\in J.

(21)

With (
20
) we now obtain

\|(\varepsilon_{j}\pm\varepsilon_{k})^{\vee}\|=2\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\|\varepsilon_{j}^{\vee}\|=2\sqrt{2},

which leads with Lemma
213
to
c_{\mathfrak{g}}=1.

Weights:
Here

{\mathfrak{t}}_{\rm alg}=i\mathop{{\rm span}}\nolimits\{E_{j}\colon j\in J\}

and each linear functional on
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\rm alg}
can be represented by a
function
\lambda\colon J\to{\mathbb{C}}
via

\lambda(E_{j})=\lambda_{j}.

For both types of root systems,
the weights are characterized by the condition

\lambda(J)\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}\quad\mbox{ or }\quad\lambda(J)\subseteq{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}+{\mathbb{Z}}

(see
[
Ne98
, Props. V.3, VII.2, VII.3]
for details).

Weyl group
:
For
B_{J}
we obtain the same Weyl group

\{\pm 1\}^{(J)}\rtimes S_{(J)}
as for
C_{J}
.
For
D_{J}
the reflection
r_{\varepsilon_{j}+\varepsilon_{k}}
changes the sign of the

j
- and the
k
-component, so that the
Weyl group
\mathcal{W}
is isomorphic to the group
\{\pm 1\}^{(J)}_{\rm even}\rtimes S_{(J)}
,
where
\{\pm 1\}^{(J)}_{\rm even}
is the subgroup of all those
elements with an even number of entries equal to
-1
.
For
D_{J}
the Weyl group acts transitively on the set of roots and,
in particular, all roots have the same length.
For
B_{J}
we have two
\mathcal{W}
-orbits, the roots
\pm\varepsilon_{j}
are short
and the roots
\pm\varepsilon_{j}\pm\varepsilon_{k}
,
j\not=k
, are long.

Example 218
.

(Root data of symplectic Lie algebras,
[
Ne12
, Ex. C.5]
)
For a complex Hilbert space
{\sf H}
with a conjugation
\tau
,
we consider the quaternionic Hilbert space
{\sf H}_{\mathbb{H}}:={\sf H}^{2}
, where the
quaternionic structure is defined by the anticonjugation

\widetilde{\tau}(v,w):=(\tau w,-\tau v)
(cf. Remark
216
).
Then

{\mathfrak{g}}:=\mathfrak{sp}_{2}({\sf H}_{\mathbb{H}})={\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H}_{\mathbb{H}})=\{x\in{\mathfrak{u}}({\sf H}^{2})\colon\widetilde{\tau}x=x\widetilde{\tau}\}

and

\mathfrak{sp}_{2}({\sf H}_{\mathbb{H}})_{\mathbb{C}}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&B\cr C&-A^{\top}\cr\end{pmatrix}\in B({\sf H}^{2})\colon B^{\top}=B,C^{\top}=C\right\}.

Let
(e_{j})_{j\in J}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\sf H}
and

{\mathfrak{t}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H}^{2})
be the
subalgebra of all diagonal operators with respect to the basis elements

(e_{j},0)
and
(0,e_{k})
of
{\sf H}^{2}
. Then
{\mathfrak{t}}

is maximal abelian in
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Moreover,
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}\cong\ell^{2}(J,{\mathbb{C}})
,
consists of diagonal operators of the form

h=\mathop{{\rm diag}}\nolimits((h_{j}),(-h_{j}))
with

\|h\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j\in J}\|he_{j}\|^{2}=\sum_{j\in J}|h_{j}|^{2}.

The set of
roots of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}
with respect to
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
is given by

\Delta=\{\pm 2\varepsilon_{j},\pm(\varepsilon_{j}\pm\varepsilon_{k})\colon j\not=k,j,k\in J\}=:C_{J},

where
\varepsilon_{j}(h)=h_{j}
. If we write

E_{j}\in{\mathfrak{t}}_{\mathbb{C}}
for the element defined by

\varepsilon_{k}(E_{j})=\delta_{jk}
, then the coroots are given by

(\varepsilon_{j}\pm\varepsilon_{k})^{\vee}=E_{j}\pm E_{k}\quad\mbox{ for }\quad j\not=k\quad\mbox{ and }\quad(2\varepsilon_{j})^{\vee}=E_{j}

(22)

and

\|(\varepsilon_{j}\pm\varepsilon_{k})^{\vee}\|=\sqrt{2},\qquad\|(2\varepsilon_{j})^{\vee}\|=1,

which leads with Lemma
213
to
c_{\mathfrak{g}}=2.

Here the roots
\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}
correspond to block diagonal operators,
the roots
\varepsilon_{j}+\varepsilon_{k}
to strictly upper triangular operators,
and the roots
-\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}
to strictly lower triangular operators.

Weights:
In particular, we have

{\mathfrak{t}}_{\rm alg}=i\mathop{{\rm span}}\nolimits\{E_{j}\colon j\in J\}

and we can represent functionals on
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\rm alg}
by
functions
\lambda\colon J\to{\mathbb{C}}
, so that
integrality is equivalent to
\lambda(J)\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}

(
[
Ne98
, Sect. VI]
).

Weyl group
:
The Weyl group
\mathcal{W}

is isomorphic to the group
\{\pm 1\}^{(J)}\rtimes S_{(J)}
,
where the factor on the left acts by finite
sign changes on
\ell^{2}(J,{\mathbb{R}})
. The
reflection
r_{\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}}
acts as a transposition and
the reflection
r_{2\varepsilon_{j}}
changes the sign of the
j
th component.
The Weyl group has two orbits in
C_{J}
, the short roots form a root system
of type
D_{J}
and the second orbit is the set

\{\pm 2\varepsilon_{j}\colon j\in J\}
of long roots.

From the preceding discussion of the Hilbert–Lie algebras

{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})
for Hilbert spaces over
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{H}}
, we
obtain the following nice relation:

Proposition 219
.

The norm
c_{\mathfrak{g}}
of the commutator bracket in
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})

over
{\mathbb{K}}\in{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{H}}
, satisfies

c_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}=\mathop{{\rm dim}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{K}}\in\{1,2,4\}.

(23)

3
Bounded representations of Hilbert–Lie groups

In this section we describe the bounded unitary representations
of connected Hilbert–Lie groups
G
,
which by basic Lie theory, correspond to
Lie algebra homomorphisms
\pi\colon{\mathfrak{g}}\to{\mathfrak{u}}(\mathcal{H})
for a complex
Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. The main result is
Theorem
37
, which leads to a complete classification,
provided the set
\Delta^{\vee}
of coroots is bounded,
which is the case for simple Hilbert–Lie algebras.
These representations are direct sums of irreducible ones, which
can be classified in terms of extremal weights.
For semisimple algebras with unbounded coroot systems,
representations of type II and III occur (Theorem
311
).

3.1
Direct sums of Hilbert–Lie algebras

According to Schue’s Theorem,
any Hilbert–Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
is an orthogonal direct sum

{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}})\oplus\widehat{\oplus}_{j\in J}{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
, where the
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
are simple
ideals. On each simple ideal
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
the norm is only determined up to a
positive factor. Changing these factors leads to Hilbert–Lie algebras
whose representation theory behaves very differently.

Definition 31
.

(
[
Ka90
]
,
[
PS86
]
)
The inner product of a simple Hilbert–Lie algebra

{\mathfrak{g}}
is said to be
normalized
if
the long roots
\alpha
satisfy
\|\alpha\|^{2}=2
.
The inner product of a general Hilbert–Lie algebra

{\mathfrak{g}}
is said to be
normalized
if it is normalized on each
simple ideal.

Lemma 32
.

Let
({\mathfrak{g}}_{j})_{j\in J}
be a family of
Hilbert–Lie algebras for which there exist
c,C>0

with

c\leq c_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}}\leq C\quad\mbox{ for all }\quad j\in J.

(24)

For each function
w\colon J\to(0,\infty)
, we define a
real inner product on the algebraic direct sum

\bigoplus_{j\in J}{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
via

\langle(x_{j}),(y_{j})\rangle:=\sum_{j\in J}w_{j}\langle x_{j},y_{j}\rangle.

(25)

Then the Hilbert completion
{\mathfrak{g}}
with respect to the corresponding norm
is a Lie algebra if and only if
\inf(w_{j})_{j\in J}>0
.

Proof.

The Hilbert completion
{\mathfrak{g}}
of
{\mathfrak{g}}^{0}:=\bigoplus_{j\in J}{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}

with respect to (
25
) is a Hilbert–Lie algebra
if and only if the bracket on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{0}
is continuous, we have
to show that

c_{\mathfrak{g}}:=\sup\{\|[x,y]\|\colon x,y\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{0},\|x\|,\|y\|\leq 1\}<\infty

is equivalent to
\inf(w_{j})_{j\in J}>0
.
In view of

c_{\mathfrak{g}}\geq\sup\{\|[x,y]\|\colon x,y\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{j},\sqrt{w_{j}}\|x_{j}\|,\sqrt{w_{j}}\|y_{j}\|\leq 1\}=\frac{c_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}}}{w_{j}},

the condition
\inf(w_{j})_{j\in J}>0
is necessary for
c_{\mathfrak{g}}<\infty
.

Suppose, conversely, that
\inf(w_{j})_{j\in J}>0
. Let

x,y\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{0}
with

\|x\|^{2},\|y\|^{2}\leq 1.

Then

\displaystyle\|[x,y]\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}w_{j}\|[x_{j},y_{j}]\|^{2}\leq\sum_{j}c_{j}^{2}w_{j}\|x_{j}\|^{2}\|y_{j}\|^{2}\leq\sup_{j\in J}\|y_{j}\|^{2}\cdot\sup\{c_{j}\colon j\in J\}^{2}\sum_{j}w_{j}\|x_{j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{j\in J}\|y_{j}\|^{2}\cdot\sup\{c_{j}\colon j\in J\}^{2}\leq\frac{\sup\{c_{j}\colon j\in J\}^{2}}{\inf\{w_{j}\colon j\in J\}}<\infty.

This proves the lemma with

c_{\mathfrak{g}}\leq\frac{\sup\{c_{j}\colon j\in J\}^{2}}{\inf\{w_{j}\colon j\in J\}}.\qed

Problem 33
.

Suppose that
J={\mathbb{N}}
and that the sequence

(w_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of weights satisfies
w_{n}\to 0
, so that the
above construction leads to a Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{g}}
to which
the Lie bracket does not extend continuously.
This is a typical situation one finds in the theory of half-Lie groups
(cf.
[
MN18
,
BHM23
]
). Is there a half-Lie group

G
with tangent space
T_{e}(G)={\mathfrak{g}}
? A natural candidate would be

G:=\Big\{(g_{n})\in\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}\colon\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w_{j}d_{G_{n}}(g_{n},e)^{2}<\infty\Big\},

where
d_{G_{n}}
is the natural biinvariant
metric on the simple Hilbert–Lie group
G_{n}
. Note however, that
exponential charts do not work because the exponential function is
singular on any
0
-neighborhood in
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

In view of Lemma
213
, the normalization condition is equivalent
to
c_{\mathfrak{g}}=\sqrt{2}
. This numerical restriction has some interesting consequences: for instance, if the assumption
(
24
) on the constants
c_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}}

in the preceding lemma is satisfied,
they can always be normalized to the value
\sqrt{2}

without changing the weighted direct sum Hilbert–Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Then the norms
of the coroots of the simple Lie algebras
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}

are bounded from below and above and we have

\|\alpha^{\vee}\|_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}=w_{j}\|\alpha^{\vee}\|^{2}_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}}\quad\mbox{ for }\quad\alpha\in\Delta({\mathfrak{g}}_{j},{\mathfrak{t}}_{j}).

(26)

Therefore
\inf(w_{j})_{j\in J}>0
if and only if
the norms of the coroots of
{\mathfrak{g}}
are bounded from below.

Remark 34
.

From (
26
) we further derive that, in
the context of Lemma
32
,
the set
\Delta_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\vee}
of coroots is bounded if and only if
the family
(w_{j})_{j\in J}
is bounded. As it is also assumed
to satisfy
\inf(w_{j})_{j\in J}>0
, this implies that the
Hilbert–Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
simply is the Hilbert direct sum

\widehat{\oplus}_{j\in J}{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
, provided the norms on the simple ideals

{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
are normalized. So we may as well put all weights

w_{j}
equal to
1
.

3.2
Decomposing bounded representations

We recall some standard notation, which is used in operator theory and in the theory of von Neumann algebras; in particular it is used in the next proposition.
For a subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
,
the commutant of
\mathcal{S}
is

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\{A\in B(\mathcal{H})\mid(\forall B\in\mathcal{S})\ AB=BA\}.

A
von Neumann algebra
can also be
defined via its commutant, in fact it is a
*
-invariant subalgebra
\mathcal{M}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
satisfying
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}
.
For a
*
-invariant set
\mathcal{S}
of operators,
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
is the von Neumann algebra generated by
\mathcal{S}
. A von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}

is called a
factor
if its center
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{M}):=\mathcal{M}\cap\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
coincides with
{\mathbb{C}}\mathbf{1}.

A unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of a group
G
is called
factorial
if

\pi(G)^{\prime\prime}
is a factor.
For the concept of factors of type I, II, III, we refer to
[
Sa71
, §2.2]
.

To deal with factor representations
of semisimple Hilbert–Lie groups, the following proposition
is an extremely useful tool. For finite products
and type I representations, it reduces the problem to the individual
factor groups.

Proposition 35
.

(Factoriality for product groups)
If

(\pi,\mathcal{H})
is a factor representation of the product
group
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
, then the following assertions
hold:

(a)

\pi|_{G_{1}}
and
\pi|_{G_{2}}
are factor representations.

(b)

If one of these is of type
I
, i.e.,
contains irreducible subrepresentations, then there exist
factor representations
(\pi_{j},\mathcal{H}_{j})
of
G_{j},j=1,2
,
with
\pi\cong\pi_{1}\otimes\pi_{2}.

If, in addition,
\pi
is irreducible, then both representations

\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are irreducible.

Proof.

(a) We identify
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
with the corresponding
subgroups of
G
.
Since
\pi(G_{1})\subseteq\pi(G_{2})^{\prime}
, we have

\mathcal{Z}(\pi(G_{1})^{\prime\prime})=\pi(G_{1})^{\prime}\cap\pi(G_{1})^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\pi(G_{1})^{\prime}\cap\pi(G_{2})^{\prime}\cap\pi(G)^{\prime\prime}=\pi(G)^{\prime}\cap\pi(G)^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{Z}(\pi(G)^{\prime\prime})={\mathbb{C}}\mathbf{1}.

So
\pi|_{G_{1}}
is a factor representation.
A similar argument shows that
\pi|_{G_{2}}
is a factor representation.

(b) If
\pi|_{G_{1}}
is of type
I
, then the Structure
Theorem for type I factors implies that

\mathcal{H}\cong\mathcal{H}_{1}\widehat{\otimes}\mathcal{H}_{2}

with
\pi(G_{1})^{\prime\prime}=B(\mathcal{H}_{1})\otimes\mathbf{1}

and
\pi(G_{2})\subseteq\pi(G_{1})^{\prime}=\mathbf{1}\otimes B(\mathcal{H}_{2})

(
[
Sa71
, Thm. 2.3.3]
).
Hence there exist representations

(\pi_{j},\mathcal{H}_{j})_{j=1,2}
of
G_{j}
, such that

\pi(g_{1})=\pi_{1}(g_{1})\otimes\mathbf{1}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\pi(g_{2})=\mathbf{1}\otimes\pi_{2}(g_{2})\quad\mbox{ for }\quad g_{j}\in G_{j},

so that
\pi=\pi_{1}\otimes\pi_{2}
.
If
\pi_{1}
or
\pi_{2}
is not irreducible, then
\pi

cannot be irreducible. Therefore the irreducibility of
\pi

implies that
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are both irreducible.
∎

The following theorem extends a well-known result
on finite-dimensional tori
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
to
a natural class of infinite-dimensional abelian
Banach–Lie groups that we encounter
in particular as identity components of centers of Hilbert–Lie groups.
Far a discussion of concrete examples of such groups,
we refer to Remark
315
later on.

Theorem 36
.

(Discrete decomposition of bounded representations—the abelian
case)

Let
Z
be a connected abelian Banach–Lie group
and
\widehat{Z}:=\mathop{{\rm Hom}}\nolimits(Z,{\mathbb{T}})
be its character group.
Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

If
\widehat{Z}
is countable, then all
norm-continuous unitary representations are
direct sums of eigenspaces, respectively,
of irreducible subrepresentations.

(b)

If
Z
is separable and all its norm-continuous
unitary representations are direct sums of eigenspaces, then

\widehat{Z}
is countable.

Proof.

Let
{\mathfrak{z}}:=\mathop{\bf L{}}\nolimits(Z)
be the Lie algebra of
Z
, so that

Z\cong{\mathfrak{z}}/\Gamma
, where
\Gamma\subseteq{\mathfrak{z}}
is a discrete
subgroup (cf.
[
GN
,
Ne06
]
). Then

\widehat{Z}\cong\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{z}}^{\prime}\colon\alpha(\Gamma)\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}\},

(27)

where we associate to
\alpha\in{\mathfrak{z}}^{\prime}
the character

\chi_{\alpha}(z+\Gamma):=e^{2\pi i\alpha(z)}
.

(a) Let
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
be a norm continuous
unitary representation of
Z
.
By
[
Ne08
, Thm. 4.1]
, this representation is obtained
from a spectral measure on the character group
\widehat{Z}
.
As
\widehat{Z}
is countable, this spectral measure is atomic
(see
[
Ru73
]
concerning the notion of atomic measures), and thus

\mathcal{H}=\widehat{\bigoplus}_{\chi\in\widehat{Z}}\mathcal{H}_{\chi}\quad\mbox{ with }\quad\mathcal{H}_{\chi}=\{v\in\mathcal{H}\colon(\forall z\in Z)\ \pi(z)v=\chi(z)v\}.

(b) We argue by contradiction. As in (
27
) above, we identify

\widehat{Z}
with a weak-
*
-closed subgroup of
{\mathfrak{z}}^{\prime}
.
If this group is uncountable, there exists an
R>0
, such that

\widehat{Z}_{R}:=\{\alpha\in\widehat{Z}\colon\|\alpha\|\leq R\}

is uncountable. The separability of
Z
now implies that
\widehat{Z}_{R}
,
endowed with the weak-
*
-topology, is a metrizable compact space,
hence also separable and therefore second countable. By compactness,
it is complete, hence a polish space. Let
\mathcal{B}
be the
\sigma
-algebra
of Borel subsets of
\widehat{Z}_{R}
. By
[
Fa00
, Thm. I.4]
,
the measurable space
(\widehat{Z}_{R},\mathcal{B})
is isomorphic to the unit interval

[0,1]
, endowed with the
\sigma
-algebra of Borel sets.
In particular, there exists a probability measure
\mu
on
\widehat{Z}_{R}
without
atoms. We consider the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(\widehat{Z}_{R},\mu)
, on
which

(\pi(z)f)(\alpha):=\chi_{\alpha}(z)f(\alpha)

defines a unitary representation of
Z
. This representation
is norm-continuous because
\widehat{Z}_{R}
is bounded in
{\mathfrak{z}}^{\prime}
.
If
f\in\mathcal{H}
is an eigenfunction for a character
\chi_{\beta}
,
then
f
vanishes
\mu
-almost everywhere on

\widehat{Z}_{R}\setminus\{\chi_{\beta}\}
, and since
\mu
has no atoms,
we must have
f=0
. Therefore
\mathcal{H}
contains no non-zero

Z
-eigenvectors.
∎

Definitively we have an interesting classification for connected
Hilbert–Lie groups
G
for which the identity component

Z(G)_{e}
of the center
Z(G)
is small enough.

Theorem 37
.

(Discrete decomposition of bounded representations—the general case)

For a connected Hilbert–Lie group
G
for which the character
group
\widehat{Z}
of
Z:=Z(G)_{e}
is countable and

\Delta^{\vee}\subseteq{\mathfrak{t}}
is bounded, the following assertions hold:

(i)

Every bounded
unitary representation of
G
is a direct sum of irreducible
ones.

(ii)

If
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}})\oplus\widehat{\bigoplus}_{j\in J}{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}

is the decomposition into center and simple ideals
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
,
then each irreducible
bounded unitary representation is a finite tensor product of
irreducible bounded representations of
{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}})
and of finitely many
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
.

(iii)

Irreducible bounded
unitary representations are extremal weight representations.

Proof.

Step 1:
From
[
Ne98
, Thm. 3.14]
and

[
Ne12
, Thms. D.5,6]
we know that (i) and (iii) hold
for infinite-dimensional simple Hilbert–Lie algebras. For finite-dimensional
ones, it follows from the representation theory of compact groups.
For the group
Z=Z(G)_{e}
, the corresponding assertion follows from
Theorem
36
.

Step 2:
For a finite subset
F\subseteq J
, we consider the subalgebra

{\mathfrak{g}}_{F}:={\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}})\oplus\sum_{j\in F}{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
with simple ideals
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
.
Since, for all simple factors, bounded representations decompose
into irreducible ones, hence are type I,
the irreducible bounded representations of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{F}

are tensor products of irreducible representations of the factors
(Proposition
35
). Hence (ii) and (iii) hold for
{\mathfrak{g}}_{F}
.

To verify (i) for
{\mathfrak{g}}_{F}
,
it suffices to show that each non-zero bounded representation
of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{F}
contains an irreducible one.
Since (i) holds for each simple ideal

{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
and also for
Z
(Theorem
36
),
any bounded unitary representation of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{F}

is an orthogonal sum of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
-isotypical
components and
{\mathfrak{z}}
-eigenspaces.
By induction this property is inherited by the direct sum
{\mathfrak{g}}_{F}

of
{\mathfrak{z}}
and finitely many simple ideals
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
.

We may therefore assume that the representation

(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
is isotypical for
{\mathfrak{z}}
and
each
({\mathfrak{g}}_{j})_{j\in F}
. This implies that its
restriction to
{\mathfrak{g}}_{F}
is a
tensor product of simple representations
\pi_{j}
of the ideals
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
,
a character
\chi
of
{\mathfrak{z}}
,
and a trivial representation. In particular, (i) follows for
{\mathfrak{g}}_{F}
.

Step 3:
Let
{\mathfrak{t}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
be maximal abelian and

\mathcal{P}\subseteq{\mathfrak{t}}^{\prime}
be the set of integral weights. For a finite subset

F\subseteq J
, we define
{\mathfrak{g}}_{F}
as above.
Our assumptions contain that

C:=\sup\{\|\alpha^{\vee}\|\colon\alpha\in\Delta\}<\infty.

If
\mu
is a weight not vanishing
on the simple ideals
({\mathfrak{g}}_{j})_{j\in F}
, then there are
mutually orthogonal roots

(\alpha_{j})_{j\in F}
with

\mu(\alpha^{\vee}_{j})\not=0
, hence

1\leq|\mu(\alpha^{\vee}_{j})|\leq C\|\mu|_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}}\|
and therefore

\|\mu\|^{2}\geq\sum_{j\in F}\|\mu|_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}}\|^{2}\geq\frac{|F|}{C^{2}}.

(28)

For a bounded representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
we thus obtain

|F|\leq C^{2}\|\pi\|^{2}
.

Step 4:
If
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
is a non-trivial bounded representation
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
and
j_{1}\in J
such that
\pi({\mathfrak{g}}_{j_{1}})\not=\{0\}
,
there exists a subrepresentation
(\pi_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1})
which
is non-trivial and isotypical for
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j_{1}}
.
If
j_{2}\not=j_{1}
is such that
\pi_{1}({\mathfrak{g}}_{j_{2}})\not=\{0\}
,
we find a subrepresentation
(\pi_{2},\mathcal{H}_{2})
of
(\pi_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1})
,
which is isotypical and non-trivial on
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j_{1}}
and
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j_{2}}
.
Proceeding like this, we find with Step 3 a maximal finite subset

F=\{j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{N}\}\subseteq J
and a subrepresentation

(\pi_{N},\mathcal{H}_{N})
of
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
which is isotypical and non-trivial
on
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j_{1}},\ldots,{\mathfrak{g}}_{j_{N}}
. By maximality, all other ideals act
trivially on
\mathcal{H}_{N}
.
For every bounded representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
,
we thus obtain with Zorn’s Lemma a direct sum decomposition
into subrepresentations which are isotypical on ideals of the form

{\mathfrak{g}}_{F}
,
F\subseteq J
finite, and trivial on the complementary ideal

{\mathfrak{g}}_{J\setminus F}
. This implies the assertion.
∎

From the classification of the bounded representations of
{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})

in
[
Ne98
,
Ne04
]
, we now obtain:

Corollary 38
.

If
{\mathfrak{g}}
is a Hilbert–Lie algebra and

{\mathfrak{t}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
a maximal abelian subalgebra for which
\Delta^{\vee}
is bounded, then
the bounded irreducible unitary representations
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
are parameterized
(via extremal weights)
by the set
\mathcal{P}_{c}/\mathcal{W}
of
\mathcal{W}
-orbits in the set of continuous weights

\mathcal{P}_{c}=\{\mu\in{\mathfrak{t}}^{\prime}\colon(\forall\alpha\in\Delta)\,\mu(\alpha^{\vee})\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}.

This parametrization is achieved by assigning to the
representation
\pi
the set
of extreme points in
\mathop{{\rm conv}}\nolimits(\mathcal{P}_{\pi})
, where

\mathcal{P}_{\pi}:=\{\mu\in{\mathfrak{t}}^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{H}^{\mu}\not=\{0\}\}
is the
set of
{\mathfrak{t}}
-weights of
\pi
.

Proof.

In view of Theorem
37
(iii), it suffices
to consider the case where
{\mathfrak{g}}
is simple.
As every bounded representation of the Hilbert–Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}

extends to a complex linear
*
-representation

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}\to B(\mathcal{H})
, we have to classify holomorphic
representations of the simply connected
complex Lie group
G_{\mathbb{C}}
with Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}
.
For
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})
,
\mathop{{\rm dim}}\nolimits{\sf H}=\infty
, it
follows from
[
Ne98
, Cor. III.11, Thm. III.14]

that these are highest weight representations.
If
{\mathfrak{g}}
is finite-dimensional, this is the classical Cartan–Weyl Theorem
of the highest weight.

For the classification in terms of Weyl group orbits of integral
weights, we refer to
[
Ne04
, Thm. III.16]
. Here the main point
is that the convex subset
\mathop{{\rm conv}}\nolimits(\mathcal{P}_{\pi})\subseteq i{\mathfrak{t}}^{\prime}
actually
has extreme points, i.e, extremal weights.
Then it turns out that the Weyl group

\mathcal{W}
acts transitively on the set of extremal weights,
and this establishes
the asserted correspondence.
∎

For finite-dimensional connected Hilbert–Lie groups
G
, i.e.,
Lie groups with compact Lie algebra,
we know that finite-dimensional (hence bounded)
unitary representations separate the points.
As
G
is a product of a vector group and a compact group,
this follows from
[
HM06
, Cor. 2.27]
.
The following remark shows that this result
does not extend to infinite-dimensional Hilbert–Lie groups.

Remark 39
.

If
K
is simply connected, then the corresponding
bounded unitary representations do in general not separate
the points. A typical example where this happens is the simply connected
covering group
\widetilde{\mathop{\rm U{}}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
, where
{\sf H}
is a complex infinite-dimensional
Hilbert space. For this group all bounded unitary representations
vanish on the kernel of the covering map

\widetilde{\mathop{\rm U{}}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})\to\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
, which is isomorphic to the fundamental
group
\pi_{1}(\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H}))\cong{\mathbb{Z}}
,
hence factors through
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
(
[
Ne98
]
).

Remark 310
.

(a) Suppose that the set
\Delta^{\vee}
of coroots is bounded for

\Delta=\Delta({\mathfrak{g}},{\mathfrak{t}})
and that
{\mathfrak{l}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
is a closed

{\mathfrak{t}}
-invariant subalgebra, so that
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\mathbb{C}}
is
adapted to the root decomposition of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{C}}
.
Then

{\mathfrak{l}}=({\mathfrak{l}}\cap{\mathfrak{t}})+({\mathfrak{l}}\cap\overline{[{\mathfrak{t}},{\mathfrak{g}}]})

and all coroots corresponding to root spaces in
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\mathbb{C}}
are
contained in
i{\mathfrak{t}}^{\prime}
for
{\mathfrak{t}}^{\prime}:={\mathfrak{l}}\cap{\mathfrak{t}}
.
Therefore
{\mathfrak{t}}^{\prime}
is maximal abelian in
{\mathfrak{l}}
and

\Delta^{\prime}:=\Delta^{\prime}({\mathfrak{l}},{\mathfrak{t}}^{\prime})
clearly satisfies

(\Delta^{\prime})^{\vee}\subseteq\Delta^{\vee}
. In particular,
the boundedness of
\Delta^{\vee}
implies the boundedness of

(\Delta^{\prime})^{\vee}
.

(b) From (a) it follows in particular that for all

-invariant

, the

37
applies.

3.3
Representations of type II and III

The following theorem complements Theorem
37
by asserting
that, if
\Delta^{\vee}
is unbounded, then the bounded representation
theory of
{\mathfrak{g}}
is not of type I.

Theorem 311
.

If
{\mathfrak{g}}
is a Hilbert–Lie algebra for which

\Delta^{\vee}
is unbounded, then it has bounded unitary
factor representations of type III and, if
{\mathfrak{g}}
is separable, also
of type II.

Proof.

According to Theorem
27
, we have
the orthogonal direct sum of ideals

{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}})\oplus\widehat{\bigoplus}_{j\in J}{\mathfrak{g}}_{j},

where the ideals
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
are simple. Since our assumption
only refers to the semisimple part of
{\mathfrak{g}}
, we may assume that

{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}})=\{0\}
.

We consider on
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
the unique norm with
c_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}}=2
, so that

\inf\{\|\alpha^{\vee}\|_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}}\colon\alpha\in\Delta_{j}\}=1

by Lemma
213
,
and since the possible ratios of the square lengths of roots
are
\{1,2,3,4\}
(
[
HiNe12
, Rem. 6.4.6]
), we then have

\sup\{\|\alpha^{\vee}\|_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}}\colon\alpha\in\Delta_{j}\}\leq 2.

We now write the norm on
{\mathfrak{g}}
as

\|x\|^{2}=\sum_{j\in J}w_{j}\|x_{j}\|_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}}^{2}

and observe that

w_{j}=\sup\{\|\alpha^{\vee}\|_{{\mathfrak{g}}}^{2}\colon\alpha\in\Delta_{j},\|\alpha^{\vee}\|_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}}=1\}.

Thus
(w_{j})_{j\in J}
is unbounded. Hence there exists a sequence

(j_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
w_{j_{n}}\geq n^{4}
. As we can always compose
representations of an ideal with the projection onto that ideal,
we may replace
J
by
{\mathbb{N}}
and assume that

w_{n}\geq n^{4}
.

For an irreducible unitary representations

(\rho_{n},\mathcal{H}_{n})
of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}
of highest weight
\lambda_{n}
, we have

\|\rho_{n}\|=\|\lambda_{n}\|
(this follows by reduction to

{\mathfrak{t}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\rho_{n}}\subseteq\mathop{{\rm conv}}\nolimits(\mathcal{W}\lambda_{n})
).
If
{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}
is finite-dimensional, we take
\rho_{n}
to be the
adjoint representations, and for infinite-dimensional ideals

{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}\cong{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}(\mathcal{H}_{n})
, we consider the representation
on
\mathcal{H}_{n}
for
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{H}}
, and on
{\sf H}_{n,{\mathbb{C}}}
for
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}}
.
We thus obtains a sequence of bounded unitary representations

\rho_{n}
by compact operators on
\mathcal{H}_{n}
, and such that

r:=\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|\rho_{n}\|<\infty.

Then the
C^{*}
-subalgebra
\mathcal{A}_{n}:=C^{*}(\rho_{n}({\mathfrak{g}}_{n}))\subseteq B(\mathcal{H}_{n})

coincides with the algebra
B_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{n})
of compact operators
because it acts irreducibly on
\mathcal{H}_{n}

(
[
Dix77
, Cor. 4.1.6]
).
As
\mathop{{\rm dim}}\nolimits\mathcal{H}_{n}\geq 2
for every
n
, we may choose rank-two
projections
P_{n}
on
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and consider the

C^{*}
-tensor product

\mathcal{A}:=\widehat{\bigotimes}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}(\mathcal{A}_{n},P_{n}),

specified by the isometric embeddings

\otimes_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{j}\to\otimes_{j=1}^{n+1}\mathcal{A}_{j},X\mapsto X\otimes P_{n+1}

(See
[
GrNe09
]
for more details on this construction).

Then we have continuous morphisms of Lie algebra

\eta_{n}\colon{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}\to\mathcal{A},\quad\eta_{n}(x)=P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{n-1}\otimes\rho_{n}(x)\otimes P_{n+1}\otimes\cdots

whose ranges commute pairwise and satisfy

\|\eta_{n}\|=\|\rho_{n}\|\leq r.

For any finite sum
x=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}+\cdots+{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}
,
we then obtain for

\eta(x):=\rho_{1}(x_{1})+\cdots+\rho_{n}(x_{n})

the estimate

\|\eta(x)\|\leq r\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|x_{j}\|\leq r\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}^{-1/2}\|x\|\leq r\|x\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}^{-1/2}\leq r\|x\|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{-2}.

Therefore the Lie algebra homomorphism

\eta\colon\sum_{n}{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}\to\mathcal{A}
extends to a continuous homomorphism

\eta\colon{\mathfrak{g}}\to~\mathcal{A}
.

That

\eta({\mathfrak{g}}_{1}+\cdots+{\mathfrak{g}}_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{A}_{n}\cong B_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{n})

is a
C^{*}
-generating subset
follows from the irreducibility of the representation

\rho_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\rho_{n}

of
{\mathfrak{g}}^{n}:={\mathfrak{g}}_{1}+\cdots+{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}

(
[
Dix77
, Cor. 4.1.6]
).
This implies that
\eta({\mathfrak{g}})
is
C^{*}
-generating in
\mathcal{A}
.
Therefore every
C^{*}
-representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
\mathcal{A}

yields a bounded unitary representation

(\pi\circ\rho,\mathcal{H})
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
with the same commutant.
In particular, every factor representation
of
\mathcal{A}
defines a bounded factor representation of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
We now argue that
\mathcal{A}
has non-type I factor representations.

In
\mathcal{A}
we consider the projection

P_{\infty}:=\otimes_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}P_{n}.

It defines the hereditary unital subalgebra

\mathcal{B}:=P_{\infty}\mathcal{A}P_{\infty}
onto which

\varepsilon\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B},\quad A\mapsto P_{\infty}AP_{\infty}

defines a conditional expectation, so in particular a completely positive map.
We thus obtain representations of
\mathcal{A}

by Stinespring dilation from the completely positive maps
of the form
\omega=\pi\circ\varepsilon
, where
(\pi,\mathcal{F})
is a
representation of
\mathcal{B}
(
[
BGN20
, Def. 3.17, Prop. 3.20]
).
Concretely, we have

\mathcal{B}\cong\bigotimes_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}P_{n}\mathcal{A}_{n}P_{n}\cong\bigotimes_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}M_{2}({\mathbb{C}}),

so that
\mathcal{B}
is a uniformly hyperfinite (UHF) algebra, hence not of type I
(
[
Po67
]
).
Let
(\rho,\mathcal{H})
be a factorial representation of
\mathcal{B}
which is not of type I
and consider the completely positive map
\omega:=\rho\circ\varepsilon
on
\mathcal{A}

and its minimal dilation
(\pi_{\omega},\mathcal{H}_{\omega})
. This is a
representation of
\mathcal{A}
containing
\mathcal{H}
as a closed
\mathcal{A}
-cyclic
\mathcal{B}
-invariant subspace on which the
\mathcal{B}
-representation is equivalent
to
\rho
.
Then
[
BGN20
, Lemmas 3.14 and 3.19]
imply that the commutant

\rho(\mathcal{B})^{\prime}
is isomorphic to the commutant

\pi_{\omega}(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}
. It follows that
\pi_{\omega}
is a factor
representation of
\mathcal{A}
of the same type.

Finally, we use
[
Sa71
, Thm. 4.6.4]
to see that a

C^{*}
-algebra which is not of type I has factorial representations
of type III. Further,
[
Sa71
, §4.6, Rem. 2]
implies that,
if it is separable, then it also has factor representations of type II.
∎

The technique used in the preceding proof is based on the general
observations that bounded representations of Lie groups
can be identified with representations of suitable
C^{*}
-algebras,
so that the well-developed representation theory of
C^{*}
-algebras
can be used to study bounded unitary Lie group representations.
We refer to
[
Ne17
]
for a more detailed outline of this technique.

Combing the preceding theorem
with Theorem
37
, we obtain:

Corollary 312
.

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be a semisimple Hilbert–Lie algebra.
Then all its bounded unitary representations are of type I if and only if

\Delta^{\vee}
is bounded.

Example 313
.

In
[
NeSe11
]
we have analyzed the bounded representations

\mathop{{\mathfrak{su}}}\nolimits_{2}(\mathcal{A})\cong\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathop{{\mathfrak{su}}}\nolimits_{2}({\mathbb{C}}),

where

\mathcal{A}
is a unital commutative Banach-
*
-algebra. For all these

\mathcal{A}

For
J={\mathbb{N}}
,
{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}=\mathop{{\mathfrak{su}}}\nolimits_{2}({\mathbb{C}})

Then

\|x_{n}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\|x\|}{n^{2}}
.
Note that

32
.

*

\ell^{1}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{C}})
.
The coroots of

, so that the sequence

).

{\sf H}

Remark 315
.

(Toroidal groups) Let
(w_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of positive numbers
and
V:=\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}},w,{\mathbb{R}})
the corresponding weighted
\ell^{2}
-space,
consisting of complex sequences
(x_{n})
for which

\|x\|_{2,w}^{2}=\sum_{n}w_{n}|x_{n}|^{2}<\infty.

Then the group
\Gamma:={\mathbb{Z}}^{({\mathbb{N}})}
is discrete in
V
if and only
if
\inf(w_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}>0
. Suppose that this is the case
and consider the abelian Lie group
T:=V/\Gamma
.
Then
\lambda=(\lambda_{n})\in V^{\prime}
is equivalent to

\|\lambda\|_{2,w^{-1}}^{2}=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{w_{n}}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}<\infty\quad\mbox{ because }\quad\sup\Big\{\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}x_{n}\colon\|x\|_{2,w}\leq 1\Big\}=\|\lambda\|_{2,w^{-1}}.

Such a sequence
(\lambda_{n})
defines a character

\chi_{\lambda}(x)=e^{2\pi i\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}x_{k}}
of
T
if
and only if
\lambda_{n}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, i.e.,

\widehat{T}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathbb{N}}\cap\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}},w^{-1},{\mathbb{R}}).

Each
\lambda\in\widehat{T}=\mathop{{\rm Hom}}\nolimits(T,{\mathbb{T}})
is finitely supported if and only if
the weight sequence
(w_{n})
is bounded from above, which is equivalent to the
boundedness of of the sequence of norms
\|e_{n}\|_{2,w}
of the
generators of
\Gamma
.
In this case it takes values in some interval
[a,b]\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
so that
V=\ell^{2}
and
\widehat{T}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{({\mathbb{N}})}
is countable.
Accordingly, every norm continuous representation of

T
(which is given by a spectral measure on
\widehat{T}
) is a direct
sum of irreducible (hence one-dimensional) representations
(cf. Theorem
36
).
So we have an analogy between the representation theory of
these groups for a bounded sequence of generators of
\Gamma

and the representation theory of Hilbert–Lie algebras
for which
\Delta^{\vee}
is bounded

37
and
311
).

4
Automorphism groups and derivations

Starting with this section, we shall restrict our attention
to the simple Hilbert–Lie algebra

{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})
, where
{\sf H}
is a separable infinite-dimensional
complex Hilbert space. We want to understand
which restrictions this imposes
for a unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
G=\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})

to be extendable to a representation of

G\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
for a one-parameter group

(\alpha_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
of automorphisms of
G
.
We know that, for any such
\alpha
, there exists a selfadjoint operator

H
on
{\sf H}
with

\alpha_{t}(g)=e^{itH}ge^{-itH}\quad\mbox{ for }\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},g\in G

(Theorem
29
).
Therefore the problem is non-trivial if and only if

H
is not a Hilbert–Schmidt operator.

In Subsection
4.1
we prove a rather general
Perturbation Theorem (Theorem
43
)
that can in particular be used to reduce questions concerning
covariance of unitary representations with respect to some
\alpha

to the case where
H
is diagonalizable
(Corollary
46
). This observation is based on
a Weyl–von Neumann decomposition
H=H_{d}+H_{\rm HS}
, where

H_{d}
is diagonalizable and
H_{\rm HS}
is Hilbert–Schmidt.
We may assume henceforth that
H
is diagonalizable,
which implies that it fixes a maximal abelian subalgebra

{\mathfrak{t}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
pointwise. We write

T:=\exp{\mathfrak{t}}\subseteq G

for the corresponding “maximal torus”, an abelian connected
Banach–Lie group.
Then we show that any factorial unitary representation

(\pi,\mathcal{H})
that is a
T
-weight representation
,
i.e., has a basis of
T
-eigenvectors,
extends to
G\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
(Proposition
411
).
As these results apply equally well to projective representations,
we briefly discuss in Subsection
4.2
some techniques
for the identification of the corresponding cocycles.

For representations of
G\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
, we can ask for the existence
of ground states, but
also investigate the semiboundedness of the extended representation,
or conditions
for
U_{t}:=\widehat{\pi}(e,t)
to have spectrum which is bounded from below.
To this end, we need to understand the structure of the subgroup

G^{0}=\mathop{{\rm Fix}}\nolimits(\alpha)
of
\alpha
-fixed points in
G
,
which is described in Subsection
4.3
.
Moreover, we need to understand which representations
of
G^{0}
can possibly arise on the minimal
U
-eigenspace.
As
G
contains a dense subgroup
G_{\rm alg}
which is a direct
limit of compact Lie groups, the techniques developed in

[
NR24
]
provide significant necessary conditions
(Theorem
418
in Subsection
4.4
).

In Subsection
4.5
, we discuss a class of
representations that is a very special case of a
construction that has been used in
[
Bo80
]
to show that

\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
has factor representations of type II
1
.
For our context, this provides representations
of
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
with no
T
-eigenvector which are covariant
for the full automorphism group.

4.1
The Weyl–von Neumann decomposition

The following result can be found in
[
DA68
, App. A]
.
It is originally due to John von Neumann and Hermann Weyl.
More recent generalizations
can be found in
[
Be71
]
and
[
Vo79
]
.

Theorem 41
.

(Weyl–Von Neumann Decomposition
Theorem,
[
JvN35
,
We09
]
)

Let
A
be a selfadjoint operator on a separable
complex Hilbert space. Then there exists a diagonalizable
selfadjoint operator
D
and a Hilbert–Schmidt operator

T
with
\|T\|_{2}<1
, such that

A=D+T.

Theorem 42
.

(Trotter Product Formula;
[
EN00
, Ex. III.5.11]
)

Let
(T_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a strongly continuous semigroup with
generator
A
on the Banach space
E
and

B\in B(E)
a bounded operator. Then
the semigroup
(U_{t})_{t\geq 0}
generated by
A+B
is given by
the Trotter–Product Formula

U_{t}x=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(T_{t/n}e^{tB/n}\Big)^{n}x\quad\mbox{ for }\quad x\in E.

Theorem 43
.

(Perturbation of one-parameter groups)

Let
G
be a connected Banach–Lie group and

(\alpha_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
be a one-parameter group in
\mathop{{\rm Aut}}\nolimits(G)
,
defining a continuous action of
{\mathbb{R}}
on
G
, so that
G\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}

is a topological group. Let
(\beta_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}

be a second such one-parameter group of automorphisms
of
G
such that the infinitesimal generators

D_{\alpha}
and
D_{\beta}
on
{\mathfrak{g}}
satisfy

D_{\beta}=D_{\alpha}+\mathop{{\rm ad}}\nolimits x\quad\mbox{ for some }\quad x\in{\mathfrak{g}}.

Then
G\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}\cong G\rtimes_{\beta}{\mathbb{R}}

as topological groups, and the isomorphism can be chosen to be

C^{1}
-for the product manifold structure.

Proof.

We consider the
half Lie group
\widehat{G}:=G\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
,
i.e.,
\widehat{G}
is a smooth manifold
and a topological group, for which left multiplication maps
are smooth (see
[
MN18
]
and
[
BHM23
]
for more
on half Lie groups).
Let
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}:={\mathfrak{g}}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}
be its tangent space
(which is not a Lie algebra if
D_{\alpha}
is unbounded, i.e.,

\alpha
is not norm-continuous).
According to
[
MN18
, Cor. 4.9]
,
the element
\mathbf{d}:=(x,1)\in\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
generates a

C^{1}
-one-parameter group
\delta\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to\widehat{G}
with

\delta^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{d}
. Now
[
MN18
, Cor. 4.15]
implies that
the one-parameter groups

\gamma_{1}(t):=(\exp(tx),0)
and
\gamma_{2}(t)=(0,t)

satisfy

\delta(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(\gamma_{1}(t/n)\gamma_{2}(t/n)\big)^{n}.

For the continuous action on
{\mathfrak{g}}
we thus obtain

\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(\delta(t))=\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(\gamma_{1}(t/n))\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(\gamma_{2}(t/n))\big)^{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(e^{\textstyle{\frac{t}{n}\mathop{{\rm ad}}\nolimits x}}\mathop{\bf L{}}\nolimits(\alpha_{t/n})\big)^{n}.

Theorem
42
now implies that

\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(\delta(t))=\mathop{\bf L{}}\nolimits(\beta_{t})
and hence that

\beta_{t}(g)=\delta(t)g\delta(t)^{-1}\quad\mbox{ for }\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},g\in G

because
G
is connected. We conclude that the map

\Phi\colon G\times_{\beta}{\mathbb{R}}\to G\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}},\quad(g,t)\mapsto(g,0)\delta(t)

is an isomorphism of topological groups.

Writing
\delta(t)=(\delta_{G}(t),t)
with a
C^{1}
-curve

\delta_{G}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to G
, we have

\Phi(g,t)=(g\delta_{G}(t),t)

and
\Phi^{-1}(g,t)=(g\delta_{G}(t)^{-1},t)
, showing
that
\Phi
actually is a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism.
∎

We record an important obvious consequence of the preceding theorem.

Corollary 44
.

In the context of
Theorem
43
, a
continuous homomorphism
\pi\colon G\to U

to some topological group
U
extends to a continuous
homomorphism
G\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}\to U
if and only if
this is the case for
G\rtimes_{\beta}{\mathbb{R}}
.

In the context of unitary and projective unitary representations,

can be formulated as follows. We refer to

JN19
]

Corollary 45
.

Proof.

We only have to apply the preceding corollary to

.
∎

Eventually, we apply these general results to the Hilbert–Lie group

.

(Perturbation of implementable one-parameter groups)

Remark 47
.

The Weyl–von Neumann decomposition
is far from unique in any sense.
For example we can move a summand

of

is not equivalent to one for which all eigenspaces are

4.2
Projective representations and central extensions

Many results of the preceding subsection apply equally well
to projective representations.
This motivates the brief discussion, in the present subsection,
of some techniques
for the identification of the corresponding cocycles. Here an important
feature of Hilbert–Lie algebras is that the invariant scalar product
provides a bijection between (bounded) derivations and
2
-cocycles.
For projective representations with a
T
-fixed ray, this permits us
to determine the corresponding Lie algebra cocycle rather directly.

If
\overline{\pi}\colon G\to\mathop{\rm PU{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{H})
is a
smooth projective
representation
of a Lie group
G
, i.e., the set of all rays in
the projective space
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{H})
with smooth orbit map is dense, then we obtain a
central Lie group extension

G^{\sharp}:=\{(U,g)\in\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{H})\times G\colon\overline{U}=\overline{\pi}(g)\}

(31)

of
G
by the circle group
{\mathbb{T}}
, see
[
JN19
]
. Then
\pi(U,g):=U
defines a
unitary representation of
G^{\sharp}
, lifting the
projective representation
\overline{\pi}
of
G
.

Corollary
46
has an interesting analog on the level of central
extensions. Any central Lie group extension
G^{\sharp}
of

G=\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
, leads to a central
Lie algebra extension

{\mathfrak{g}}^{\sharp}={\mathbb{R}}\oplus_{\omega}{\mathfrak{g}}\quad\mbox{ with }\quad[(t,x),(s,y)]=(\omega(x,y),[x,y]),

where
\omega\colon{\mathfrak{g}}\times{\mathfrak{g}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a continuous Lie algebra cocycle.
In view of
[
Ne04
, Lemma VII.1]
, for
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})
, the
continuous Lie algebra cocycles can all be written in the form

\omega_{\mathbf{d}}(x,y)=\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(\mathbf{d}[x,y]),\quad\mathbf{d}\in{\mathfrak{u}}({\sf H}),x,y\in{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H}).

As
\omega_{\mathbf{d}}=0
is equivalent to
\mathbf{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}i\mathbf{1}
,
the cocycle
\omega_{\mathbf{d}}
is a coboundary if and only if

\mathbf{d}\in{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})+{\mathbb{R}}i\mathbf{1}
. This leads to the isomorphism

H^{2}({\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H}),{\mathbb{R}})\cong{\mathfrak{u}}(\mathcal{H})/({\mathbb{R}}i\mathbf{1}+{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H}))

(cf. also
[
Ne02c
, Ex. 2.18]
and Remark
49
).
Theorem
41
now implies that each cohomology class
is represented by a cocycle

\omega_{\mathbf{d}}
for which
\mathbf{d}
is diagonalizable. Since
the second homotopy group
\pi_{2}(\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H}))

vanishes (combine
[
Ne02
, Thms. II.12, II.14]
on
the homotopy groups of
\mathop{{\rm GL}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
with the
polar decomposition
[
Ne04
, Prop. A.4]
),
all Lie algebra cocycles integrate to central Lie group
extensions of the associated simply connected Lie group
(
[
Ne02b
]
).

A
concrete model
for the central extension of
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})

corresponding to a diagonal operator
\mathbf{d}

described as follows.
The group
G=\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})

4.3
The fixed point group

Let
G=\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
,
(e_{j})_{j\in J}
be a Hilbert-Lie group on
{\sf H}
,
and
\mathbf{d}=\mathop{{\rm diag}}\nolimits(id_{j})_{j\in J}
a purely imaginary diagonal
operator, generating the one-parameter group

\alpha_{t}(g)=e^{t\mathbf{d}}ge^{-t\mathbf{d}}
of automorphisms of

.
In this section we briefly discuss the structure of the subgroup

G^{0}:=G^{0}(\alpha):=\mathop{{\rm Fix}}\nolimits(\alpha)=\{g\in G\colon(\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}})\,\alpha_{t}(g)=g\}

of
\alpha
-fixed points.

An operator is fixed by
\alpha
if and only if it commutes with

\mathbf{d}
, i.e., preserves all
H
-eigenspaces

{\sf H}_{\lambda}:=\ker(\mathbf{d}-\lambda\mathbf{1}).

We thus obtain

{\mathfrak{g}}^{0}:={\mathfrak{g}}^{0}(\mathbf{d})=\widehat{\bigoplus}_{\lambda\in i{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H}_{\lambda}).

Here infinite-dimensional eigenspaces correspond to infinite-dimensional
simple ideals of
{\mathfrak{g}}^{0}(\mathbf{d})
, and each finite-dimensional eigenspace
contributes a one-dimensional subspace to the center:

{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}^{0})=\widehat{\bigoplus}_{\mathop{{\rm dim}}\nolimits{\sf H}_{\lambda}<\infty}{\mathbb{R}}i\mathbf{1}_{{\sf H}_{\lambda}}.

Let
z_{\lambda}:=2\pi i\mathbf{1}_{{\sf H}_{\lambda}}
. Then

\exp(z_{\lambda})=\mathbf{1}
, so that

Z(G^{0})_{e}=\exp({\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}^{0}))\cong{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}^{0})/\Gamma\quad\mbox{ for }\quad\Gamma=\sum_{\mathop{{\rm dim}}\nolimits{\sf H}_{\lambda}<\infty}{\mathbb{Z}}z_{\lambda}.

Note that

\|z_{\lambda}\|_{2}=2\pi\sqrt{\mathop{{\rm dim}}\nolimits{\sf H}_{\lambda}}\geq 2\pi.

For
z=\sum_{\lambda}x_{\lambda}z_{\lambda}
with
x_{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we then have

\|z\|^{2}_{2}=\sum_{\lambda}|x_{\lambda}|^{2}\cdot\|z_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2},

so that we may consider
w_{\lambda}:=\|z_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}=4\pi^{2}\mathop{{\rm dim}}\nolimits{\sf H}_{\lambda}

as a weight (cf. Lemma
32
).

The subgroup
\Gamma\subseteq{\mathfrak{t}}
is discrete,
but Remark
315
applies only if
the dimensions of all finite-dimensional eigenspaces
{\sf H}_{\lambda}
are
bounded from above. In this case, it implies with
Theorem
36
that
all bounded representations of
Z(G^{0})_{e}

decompose discretely.

5
Semibounded representations
of Hilbert–Lie groups and central extensions

In Section
3
we discussed bounded
representations of Hilbert–Lie group. The next larger
class consists of semibounded representations,
whose theory has been developed
in
[
Ne08
,
Ne10b
,
Ne12
,
Ne14
,
NZ13
]
.

We start in Subsection
5.1
with
Theorem
52
, which
asserts that semibounded unitary representations of connected
Hilbert–Lie groups decompose discretely into bounded ones,
hence provide nothing new beyond what we have seen in
Section
3
.
This is complemented by Theorem
54
, which shows
that semibounded projective representations of Hilbert–Lie groups
can be lifted to representations of the simply connected
covering group. Its proof involves the remarkable Bruhat–Tits Fixed
Point Theorem (see Appendix
B
).

As a consequence, enlarging our context to projective semibounded
representations of
G
does not lead to new representations,
but projective representations of the groups

for which the spectrum of the one-parameter group

\pi(e,t)
is bounded below are far more interesting.
As we go along, we learn
that this is due to the fact that considering
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})
as a
Lie group is too restrictive, in particular, when we consider
one-parameter groups
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
of automorphisms
that act only continuously and not smoothly, i.e., their
infinitesimal generator is unbounded.
This is taken into account in
Subsection
5.2
, where we show that the existence of a
unitary representation of
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
,
whose restriction to the Lie group

is semibounded, requires the infinitesimal generator
i\mathbf{d}
of
\alpha

to be semibounded as an operator on
{\sf H}

(Corollary
59
). This result builds on some fine analysis
of the coadjoint action of the semidirect product Lie group
(Theorem
58
).
If
\mathbf{d}
is unbounded, then
the topological group
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
is not a
Lie group, but it is a smooth Banach manifold and even a
half Lie group in the sense of
[
MN18
]
,
which has already been used in the proof of
Theorem
43
. Then

\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})^{\infty}\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
is the Lie group
of all elements for which right multiplications are also smooth,
so that the passage between these two perspectives is well
captured by the half Lie group concept.

Subsections
5.3
and

5.4
are devoted to projective representations
of the restricted unitary group
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{\rm res}({\sf H},D)\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits({\sf H})

for an operator

on a Hilbert space
{\sf H}
,
where the “restriction” is expressed in terms of

being
Hilbert–Schmidt. We recall in Theorem

(from
[
Ne04
]
)
that the extremal weight representation
of the trace class group

, corresponding to a bounded weight,
all extend to projective representations of suitable restricted groups,
which contain in particular the group
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{2}({\sf H})

a large family of projective representations of

that define projective semibounded representations of the Lie group

.
In Theorem

we show that the restricted groups are maximal

These results provide a new perspective and

Definition 51

on

Remark 56
.

Consider the half Lie group

, the subgroup of elements in

Lemma 57
.

Let
A
and
B
be selfadjoint operators on the complex
Hilbert space
{\sf H}
and assume that
B
is bounded.
Then
A
is semibounded if and only if
A+B
is.

Proof.

For unit vectors
v\in\mathcal{D}(A)=\mathcal{D}(A+B)
, we have

\langle v,(A+B)v\rangle=\langle v,Av\rangle+\langle v,Bv\rangle\in\langle v,Av\rangle+[-\|B\|,\|B\|].

Thus
A
is semibounded if and only if
A+B
is.
∎

Theorem 58
.

If
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}={\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})^{\infty}

contains proper open
\beta
-invariant convex subsets,
then
i\mathbf{d}
is semibounded.

Proof.

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}
be a proper
\beta
-invariant open convex subset.
Replacing
\mathbf{d}
by
\mathbf{d}+x
for some
x\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}
, we may w.l.o.g. assume that

0\in\partial\Omega
. Note that
i\mathbf{d}
is semibounded if and only if

i(\mathbf{d}+x)
has this property (Lemma
57
).

The Hahn–Banach separation theorem implies the existence of a
non-zero continuous linear functional

\lambda\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty,^{\prime}}
with
\inf\lambda(\Omega)=\lambda(0)=0
.
This implies that

\lambda(g\mathbf{d}g^{-1}-\mathbf{d})\geq 0\quad\mbox{ for }\quad g\in G^{\infty}.

Derivatives along one-parameter subgroups of
G^{\infty}
lead to the conditions

\lambda([x,\mathbf{d}])=0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\lambda([x,[x,\mathbf{d}]])\geq 0\quad\mbox{ for }\quad x\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}.

(38)

The first condition implies that
\lambda\in({\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty})^{\prime}
is invariant under
the
{\mathbb{R}}
-action defined by conjugation with
e^{t\mathbf{d}}
.
For the Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{g}}
, we consider the decomposition

{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}={\mathfrak{g}}^{0}\oplus({\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}\cap({\mathfrak{g}}^{0})^{\bot}).

Then the definition of
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}
implies that
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty,0}={\mathfrak{g}}^{0}
, so that

{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}={\mathfrak{g}}^{0}\oplus\overline{[\mathbf{d},{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}]}
. Now
\lambda
vanishes on
[\mathbf{d},{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}]
, so that

\lambda\in({\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty,0})^{\prime}
. Therefore

\lambda(x)=\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}x)
for some
Hilbert–Schmidt operator
\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty,0}
.

Next we evaluate the second condition. First we observe that

\mathbf{d}
commutes with
\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}
, hence preserves all eigenspaces of

\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}
. For the spectral decomposition of
i\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}
,

{\sf H}=\widehat{\bigoplus}_{\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}}{\sf H}^{\mu},\quad\mbox{ we put }\quad\mathbf{d}_{\mu}:=\mathbf{d}|_{{\sf H}^{\mu}}.

Then

{\sf H}^{\mu}
is finite-dimensional for
\mu\not=0
.
We claim that

h(x,y):=\lambda([x^{*},[\mathbf{d},y]])=-\lambda([x^{*},[y,\mathbf{d}]])

defines a positive semidefinite hermitian form on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}_{\mathbb{C}}
. In fact, the
form is clearly sesquilinear, and from
\overline{\lambda(x)}=\lambda(\overline{x})
and
\lambda([\mathbf{d},{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}])=\{0\}
we derive

\overline{h(y,x)}=\lambda(\overline{[y^{*},[\mathbf{d},x]]})=\lambda([-y,[x^{*},\mathbf{d}]])=\lambda([[x^{*},\mathbf{d}],y])=\lambda([x^{*},[\mathbf{d},y]])=h(x,y),

so that
h
is hermitian. We thus have

h(x,x)=\lambda([x^{*},[\mathbf{d},x]])=\lambda([[-x^{*},[x,\mathbf{d}]])\geq 0\quad\mbox{ for all }\quad x\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}_{\mathbb{C}}

because this holds for
x\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}
by (
38
).
Let
\nu\not=\mu
and

x\in B({\sf H}^{\mu},{\sf H}^{\nu})=B_{2}({\sf H}^{\mu},{\sf H}^{\nu})\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}^{\infty}_{\mathbb{C}}

(recall that at least one of these spectral subspaces is finite-dimensional).
Then

[i\mathbf{b}_{\lambda},x]=(\nu-\mu)x\quad\mbox{ and }\quad[i\mathbf{b}_{\lambda},x^{*}]=(\mu-\nu)x^{*}

lead to

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\lambda([x^{*},[\mathbf{d},x]])=\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}[x^{*},[\mathbf{d},x]])

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(i\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}[x^{*},[-i\mathbf{d},x]])=-\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits([i\mathbf{b}_{\lambda},x^{*}][i\mathbf{d},x])

\displaystyle=-(\mu-\nu)\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(x^{*}[i\mathbf{d},x])=(\nu-\mu)\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(i\mathbf{d}xx^{*}-x^{*}xi\mathbf{d})

\displaystyle=(\nu-\mu)\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(i\mathbf{d}_{\nu}xx^{*}-x^{*}xi\mathbf{d}_{\mu})

\displaystyle=(\nu-\mu)\big(\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(i\mathbf{d}_{\nu}xx^{*})-\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(i\mathbf{d}_{\mu}x^{*}x)\big).

We assume w.l.o.g. that
\nu>\mu
. Consider an operators
x=P_{v,w}

of the form

P_{v,w}(u):=\langle w,u\rangle v,

where
w\in{\sf H}^{\mu}
and
v\in{\sf H}^{\nu}
are unit eigenvectors
for
\mathbf{d}
. Then

\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(i\mathbf{d}_{\mu}x^{*}x)=\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(i\mathbf{d}_{\mu}P_{w,w})=\langle w,i\mathbf{d}_{\mu}w\rangle\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(i\mathbf{d}_{\nu}xx^{*})=\langle v,i\mathbf{d}_{\nu}v\rangle.

We therefore obtain

\sup{\rm Spec}(i\mathbf{d}_{\mu})\leq\inf{\rm Spec}(i\mathbf{d}_{\nu})\quad\mbox{ for }\quad\mu<\nu.

Let us write this as
i\mathbf{d}_{\mu}\leq i\mathbf{d}_{\nu}
.

If the Hilbert–Schmidt operator

i\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}
has a positive eigenvalue, it has a maximal
eigenvalue
\mu_{\rm max}>0
. Then
i\mathbf{d}_{\mu_{\rm max}}\geq i\mathbf{d}_{\mu}
for all
other spectral values
\mu
of
i\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}

and the fact that
{\sf H}^{\mu_{\rm max}}
is
finite-dimensional implies that
i\mathbf{d}
is bounded from above.
Likewise, the existence of a negative eigenvalue of
i\mathbf{b}_{\lambda}

implies that
i\mathbf{d}
is bounded from below. As
\lambda
is non-zero,
one of these two cases occurs, so that
i\mathbf{d}
is semibounded.
∎

5.4

\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits({\sf H})_{\lambda}:=\{g\in\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits({\sf H})\colon\rho_{\lambda}\circ\alpha_{g}\sim\rho_{\lambda}\},

Example 514

(a) If

is constant

Remark 515
.

Let
(e_{j})_{j\in J}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\sf H}
and

be a permutation of the basis elements. We write

for the subgroup of all unitary operators

Since

is uniquely determined by the weight set

Remark 516
.

A necessary condition for

the momentum set

satisfies

Theorem 517

Suppose that

Recall from Theorem

(g.\omega_{\lambda})(x,y)=\omega_{\lambda}(\alpha_{g}^{-1}x,\alpha_{g}^{-1}y)=\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(\mathbf{d}\alpha_{g}^{-1}[x,y])=\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits(\alpha_{g}(\mathbf{d})[x,y])

we derive that the operator

g\mathbf{d}g^{-1}-\mathbf{d}
has to represent a trivial cocycle, which means that

g\mathbf{d}g^{-1}-\mathbf{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}i\mathbf{1}+{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})

(
[
Ne02c
, Ex. 2.18]
and Subsection
4.2
).
We consider the
1
-cocycle

\beta\colon\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits({\sf H})\to{\mathfrak{u}}({\sf H}),\quad\beta(g):=g\mathbf{d}g^{-1}-\mathbf{d}.

Suppose that

\beta(g)-\mu i\mathbf{1}\in{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H}).

Let
\overline{\beta}\colon\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits({\sf H})\to{\mathfrak{u}}({\sf H})/{\mathfrak{u}}_{\infty}({\sf H})
be the corresponding
cocycle modulo the subspace
{\mathfrak{u}}_{\infty}({\sf H})
of compact skew-hermitian
operators.
Then
\overline{\beta}(g)=\mu[i\mathbf{1}]
,
and since
[i\mathbf{1}]
is fixed, we obtain

\overline{\beta}(g^{n})=n\mu[i\mathbf{1}]\quad\mbox{ for }\quad n\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

As
\overline{\beta}
is bounded, it follows that
\mu=0
, hence that

\beta(g)
is Hilbert–Schmidt,
which is equivalent to

g\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{d}g=(g\mathbf{d}g^{-1}-\mathbf{d})g\in B_{2}({\sf H})
, i.e., to

g\in\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{\rm res}({\sf H},\mathbf{d})
.
∎

Note that the group
\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{\rm res}({\sf H},\mathbf{d})
does not change in the theorem above, if we replace
\mathbf{d}
by
\mathbf{d}+\mathop{{\rm ad}}\nolimits x
for some
x\in{\mathfrak{u}}_{2}({\sf H})

(see Corollary
45
).

Remark 518
.

Assume that
\mathbf{d}
is diagonalizable with finitely
many eigenvalues and eigenspaces

{\sf H}_{j}:=\ker(\mathbf{d}-id_{j}\mathbf{1})
,
j=1,\ldots,r
.
Accordingly, we write
g\in\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits({\sf H})
as a matrix

g=(g_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq r}
with

g_{ij}\in B({\sf H}_{j},{\sf H}_{i})
. The relation

g\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{d}g\in B_{2}({\sf H})
means that, for

i\not=j
, the corresponding matrix entry

(d_{j}-d_{i})g_{ij}
is Hilbert–Schmidt, and this is equivalent to

g_{ij}
being Hilbert–Schmidt. In this sense we have

\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits_{\rm res}({\sf H},\mathbf{d})=\{g=(g_{ij})\colon(\forall i\not=j)\,g_{ij}\in B_{2}({\sf H}_{j},{\sf H}_{i})\}.

6
Perspectives

Although we formulated several challenging problems in the
main text, we briefly discuss in this short final section
some directions of research that target important tools to
analyze unitary representations of infinite-dimensional Lie groups.

6.1
Representations of abelian Hilbert–Lie groups

Definition 61
.

A
toroidal Hilbert–Lie group
is a group of the form

T={\mathfrak{t}}/\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
is a discrete subgroup of
the real Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{t}}
, considered as an abelian Hilbert–Lie algebra
(
[
Ba91
]
), and
\Gamma
spans a dense subspace of
{\mathfrak{t}}
.
Assigning to
\alpha\in{\mathfrak{t}}^{\prime}
with
\alpha(\Gamma)\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}

the character
\chi_{\alpha}(x+\Gamma):=e^{2\pi i\alpha(x)}
, we then obtain
an isomorphism of groups

\widehat{T}\cong\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{t}}^{\prime}\colon\alpha(\Gamma)\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}\}\hookrightarrow\mathop{{\rm Hom}}\nolimits(\Gamma,{\mathbb{Z}}).

Any toroidal Lie group contains the torus groups

T_{F}:=(\mathop{{\rm span}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma_{F})/\Gamma_{F}
,
\Gamma_{F}\subseteq\Gamma

finitely generated, and the directed union
T_{0}
of these subgroups
is dense in

. Assume that
\Gamma
is countable.
Then

carries an intrinsic Lie group structure whose underlying
topology is the direct limit topology (
[
Gl05
]
). We
identify the character group
\widehat{T}
with a subgroup of

\widehat{T_{0}}\cong\mathop{{\rm Hom}}\nolimits(\Gamma,{\mathbb{Z}})
, consisting of
those characters of

which extend continuously to
T
.

An important example is the group

T:=\ell^{2}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{({\mathbb{N}})}
with
the dense subgroup

and
\widehat{T_{0}}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathbb{N}}\supseteq\widehat{T}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{({\mathbb{N}})}
.
That bounded representations of
T
are direct sums of eigenspaces follows
from Theorem
36
because
\widehat{T}
is countable.

It is an interesting problem to understand the
continuous unitary representations of
T
in terms of their
restrictions to
T_{0}
. This problem immediately reduces to cyclic
representations, which are given by positive definite functions.
As
T_{0}
is a nuclear group, positive definite functions are Fourier
transforms of measures
\mu
on
\widehat{T_{0}}
(
[
Ba91
]
).

Problem 62
.

Characterize those Borel measures
\mu
on
\widehat{T_{0}}\cong\mathop{{\rm Hom}}\nolimits(\Gamma,{\mathbb{Z}})
whose

\widehat{\mu}
extends to a continuous function on

.
This is equivalent to the function

\widehat{\mu}
to extend from
\mathop{{\rm span}}\nolimits\Gamma
to a continuous
function on
{\mathfrak{t}}
.
As
\widehat{T}
is a countable subset of
\widehat{T_{0}}
and

is atomic on

\widehat{T}
, it suffices to consider measures on the complement

.

We expect that this problem can be addressed by using the

[
Ya85
]
and
[
Xia72
]

for tools on measures on infinite-dimensional spaces.

6.2

The construction in Section

produces in particular projective semibounded

,

Appendix A
Invariant convex subsets of Hilbert–Lie algebras

Lemma A1
.

(
[
Ne20
, Lemma A.4]
)

Let
(V,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space
and
U\subseteq V
be a proper open convex subset with
complement
U^{c}=V\setminus U
.
Then the distance function

d_{U}(x):=\mathop{{\rm dist}}\nolimits(x,U^{c})=\inf\{\|x-y\|\colon y\in U^{c}\}

is continuous, concave and

d_{U}^{-1}\colon U\to{\mathbb{R}}

is a continuous convex function on

with

\lim_{x_{n}\to x\in\partial U}d_{U}^{-1}(x_{n})=\infty.

In particular, all the sets

U_{c}:=\{x\in U\colon d_{U}(x)\geq c\},\quad c>0,

are closed subsets of
V
.

Proposition A2
.

If
G
is a connected Hilbert–Lie group,
then each non-empty open
\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(G)
-invariant
convex subset
U\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
intersects the center.

Proof.

If
U={\mathfrak{g}}
, there is nothing to show because
0\in U
.
We may therefore assume that
U\not={\mathfrak{g}}
.
Then
U
is a proper open convex subset and the group

\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(G)
acts isometrically on

{\mathfrak{g}}
preserving
U
. Let
d_{U}\colon V\to{\mathbb{R}}
denote the distance from

\partial U
.
Then Lemma
A1
shows that, for every

c>0
,

U_{c}:=\{x\in U\colon d_{U}(x)\geq c\}

is a closed convex subset of
{\mathfrak{g}}
. Let

be such that
U_{c}\not=\emptyset
.
All these sets are
\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(G)
-invariant. Now

is a Bruhat–Tits space
with respect to the induced Hilbert metric from
{\mathfrak{g}}
and
since
\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(G)
acts on this space isometrically with bounded
orbits, the Bruhat–Tits Fixed Point Theorem
implies the existence of a fixed point

(
[
La99
]
).
Now it only remain to observe that

is the set of fixed points for the action of

\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(G)
on

.
∎

Definition B1

1

(a) In view of

\langle{\cal O}_{(1,0)},(t,x)\rangle=t+\Theta_{\omega}(G)(x),\quad\mbox{ we have }\quad B({\cal O}_{(1,0)})={\mathbb{R}}\times B(\Theta_{\omega}(G))\subseteq\widehat{\mathfrak{g}},

where, for a subset
C\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
, we put

B(C):=\{x\in{\mathfrak{g}}\colon\inf\langle C,x\rangle>-\infty\}.

Therefore
{\cal O}_{(1,0)}
is (semi-)equicontinuous in
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}

if and only if
\Theta_{\omega}(G)\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
has the corresponding
property.

(b) The subspace

{\cal O}_{(1,0)}^{\bot}=\{(t,x)\in\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}\colon\Theta_{\omega}(G)(x)=\{-t\}\}

is an invariant ideal of
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
which intersects the the central
line
{\mathbb{R}}\times\{0\}
trivially.
Its image in
{\mathfrak{g}}
consists of all those
elements
x\in{\mathfrak{g}}
for which the evaluation function

\mathop{{\rm ev}}\nolimits_{x}\colon{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad\mathop{{\rm ev}}\nolimits_{x}(\alpha)=\alpha(x)

is constant on
\Theta_{\omega}(G)
. Since
\Theta_{\omega}
is a

1

{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
, we have for any
g\in G

\displaystyle T_{g}(\Theta_{\omega})(g\cdot y)(x)

\displaystyle=\big(\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits^{*}(g)T_{e}(\Theta_{\omega})(y)\big)(x)=T_{e}(\Theta_{\omega})(y)(\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(g)^{-1}x)=\omega\big(\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(g)^{-1}x,y\big).

\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(G)x

\mathop{{\rm rad}}\nolimits(\omega)=\{y\in{\mathfrak{g}}\colon i_{y}\omega=0\}

{\cal O}_{(1,0)}^{\bot}

{\mathfrak{g}}

\mathop{{\rm Ad}}\nolimits(G)

{\mathfrak{g}}

\mathop{{\rm rad}}\nolimits(\omega)

The central extension splits on this ideal.
