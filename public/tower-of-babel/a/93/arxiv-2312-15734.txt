Superdiffusive limits beyond the Marcus regime for deterministic fast-slow systems
Ilya Chevyrev, Alexey Korepanov, Ian Melbourne

Abstract
We consider deterministic fast-slow dynamical systems of the form x k + 1 ( n ) = x k ( n ) + n − 1 A ( x k ( n ) ) + n − 1 / α B ( x k ( n ) ) v ( y k ) , y k + 1 = T y k , x_{k+1}^{(n)}=x_{k}^{(n)}+n^{-1}A(x_{k}^{(n)})+n^{-1/\alpha}B(x_{k}^{(n)})v(y_{k}),\quad y_{k+1}=Ty_{k}, where α ∈ ( 1 , 2 ) \alpha\in(1,2) and x k ( n ) ∈ ℝ m x_{k}^{(n)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m} . Here, T T is a slowly mixing nonuniformly hyperbolic dynamical system and the process W n ( t ) = n − 1 / α ∑ k = 1 [ n ​ t ] v ( y k ) W_{n}(t)=n^{-1/\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{[nt]}v(y_{k}) converges weakly to a d d -dimensional α \alpha -stable Lévy process L α L_{\alpha} . We are interested in convergence of the m m -dimensional process X n ​ ( t ) = x [ n ​ t ] ( n ) X_{n}(t)=x_{[nt]}^{(n)} to the solution of a stochastic differential equation (SDE) d ​ X = A ⁡ ( X ) ​ d ​ t + B ⁡ ( X ) ​ d ​ L α . dX=A(X)\,dt+B(X)\,dL_{\alpha}. In the simplest cases considered in previous work, the limiting SDE has the Marcus interpretation. In particular, the SDE is Marcus if the noise coefficient B B is exact or if the excursions for W n W_{n} converge to straight lines as n → ∞ n\to\infty . Outside these simplest situations, it turns out that typically the Marcus interpretation fails. We develop a general theory that does not rely on exactness or linearity of excursions. To achieve this, it is necessary to consider suitable spaces of “decorated” càdlàg paths and to interpret the limiting decorated SDE. In this way, we are able to cover more complicated examples such as billiards with flat cusps where the limiting SDE is typically non-Marcus for m ≥ 2 m\geq 2 .

Introduction

Homogenisation for systems with multiple timescales is
a longstanding and very active area of research
[
40
]
.
Recently there has been considerable interest in the case where the underlying multiscale system is deterministic, see
[
13
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
23
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
38
,
41
]
as well as our survey paper
[
14
]
.
Although the system is deterministic, chaotic dynamics often leads to a limiting stochastic differential equation (SDE) driven by Brownian motion.

We are interested in the case where the limiting SDE is driven by a superdiffusive
\alpha
-stable Lévy process. This situation was considered previously in
[
13
,
23
]
though, as we shall see, under restrictions that gave a misleadingly simple picture.

1.1
Background material on interpretation of stochastic integrals

When analysing SDEs, a key question is the appropriate interpretation of certain stochastic integrals. Consider the SDE

dX=A(X)\,dt+B(X)\,dW,\quad X(0)=\xi,

(1.1)

where
A:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
B:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times d}
are smooth,
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and
W
is a continuous
d
-dimensional stochastic process. If
W
is smooth, then (
1.1
) reduces to an ordinary differential equation (ODE). If
W
is
\eta
-Hölder with
\eta>\frac{1}{2}
, or alternatively of finite
p
-variation with
p<2
, then the integral
\int B(X)\,dW
is well-defined as a Riemann-Stieltjes integral and is called a Young integral
[
51
]
. Unfortunately, Brownian motion is
\eta
-Hölder only for
\eta<\frac{1}{2}
and is of finite
p
-variation only for
p>2
. Hence, when
W
is Brownian motion,
\int B(X)\,dW
is not generally well-defined as a Young integral, and the question arises as to whether the correct interpretation is Itô (which has the advantage of preserving the property of being a martingale), Stratonovich (which has the advantage of transforming under the standard laws of calculus)
or some other interpretation.
Wong and Zakai
[
50
]
attempted to resolve the issue of the interpretation of stochastic integrals by considering smooth approximations whereby smooth processes
W_{n}
converge weakly to
W
as
n\to\infty
. The SDE (
1.1
)
is then replaced by the ODE

dX_{n}=A(X_{n})\,dt+B(X_{n})\,dW_{n},\quad X_{n}(0)=\xi,

(1.2)

and it is hoped that computing the weak limit
X
of
X_{n}
will help to clarify the nature of the limiting stochastic integral. Under certain conditions, it was shown in
[
50
]

that
X_{n}\to_{w}X
where
X
satisfies (
1.1
) with the Stratonovich interpretation. The conditions in
[
50
]
hold for
d=m=1
and more generally under certain exactness conditions (for example, it suffices that
d=m
and
B^{-1}=dr
for some diffeomorphism
r:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
), but counterexamples for
d=m=2
were subsequently found by
[
36
,
46
]
.

When exactness fails, a fundamental issue is the lack of continuous dependence of the outputs
X_{n}
on the inputs
W_{n}
in equation (
1.2
). The theory of rough paths developed by Lyons
[
33
]
demonstrates that the answer is to view
X_{n}
as a function of processes
(W_{n},\int W_{n}\otimes\,dW_{n})
taking values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
. Working in the uniform topology on continuous paths in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}

is still insufficient, and it is necessary to consider
p
-variation
[
33
]
or Hölder topologies
[
20
,
21
]
.
This leads to nontrivial corrections to the Stratonovich integral via the “Lévy area” given by the weak limit of
\int W_{n}\otimes\,dW_{n}
.
In
[
28
]
, see also
[
29
,
14
,
15
]
,
it was shown using rough path theory that a large class of deterministic systems (maps or ODEs) give rise to smooth approximations of Brownian motion with well-defined and nontrivial Lévy area; the correct interpretation is generally neither Itô nor Stratonovich.

1.2
Stochastic integration with respect to Lévy processes

Now we turn to the case where continuous paths
W_{n}
converge weakly to
W
which is
an
\alpha
-stable Lévy process,
\alpha\in(0,2)
.
We write
L_{\alpha}
instead of
W
in this case, so the limiting SDE becomes

dX=A(X)\,dt+B(X)\,dL_{\alpha},\quad X(0)=\xi.

(1.3)

The Lévy process
L_{\alpha}
is tight in
p
-variation for some
p<2
.
Were
L_{\alpha}
continuous, the SDE (
1.3
) would have a comparatively simple
interpretation in the sense of Young as shown in
[
32
]
, but the discontinuities of
L_{\alpha}

leave (
1.3
) open to interpretations.

Again, the simplest situation is when
B
is exact; there it was shown in
[
23
, Section 5]
that the limiting SDE has the Marcus interpretation
[
34
]
(see
[
31
,
3
,
7
]
for the general theory of Marcus SDEs and their applications).
We note that the Marcus integral
plays the same role for Lévy processes that Stratonovich does for Brownian motion:
it is the integral that transforms under the standard laws of calculus.

It turns out that the Marcus integral may give the correct interpretation beyond the exact situation although the mechanism is somewhat different.
In
[
13
]
, we gave general conditions under which there is convergence to a Marcus SDE, but a crucial assumption was that
W_{n}
converges to
L_{\alpha}
in the
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
Skorokhod topology
on the Skorokhod space
D([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{m})
of càdlàg functions.

To circumvent nonexactness, we used topologies inspired by rough paths:
weak convergence of
W_{n}
no longer suffices and
we required in addition that
W_{n}
is tight in
p
-variation for some
p<2
.
Moreover, although convergence of
W_{n}
was assumed in the
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
topology,
X_{n}
need not converge in any of the
Skorokhod topologies
[
45
]
even
in the simplest situations
[
13
]
. Hence we
needed to consider convergence of
X_{n}
in generalised Skorokhod topologies as introduced recently in Chevyrev and Friz
[
12
]
.

In Theorem
3.7
, we extend the result in
[
13
]
to include general “linear excursions” for
W_{n}
(by which we mean that each excursion is asymptotically a line segment, possibly with overshoots).
This includes the situations where
W_{n}
converges weakly in the
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
or
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
topologies.

More importantly, in our main result Theorem
3.6
, we are able to show convergence of
X_{n}
when
W_{n}
does not have linear excursions and thus fails to converge in the
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
or
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
topologies.
When
d\geq 2
, linear excursions for
W_{n}
are highly unlikely
unless there is a very simple mechanism underlying the excursions, such as a neutral fixed point for an intermittent map.
Moreover, as shown in Example
1.1
below,
typically the limiting SDE is not Marcus when
B
is not exact and the excursions are not linear.

Example 1.1
.

We show that in the simplest nonexact examples, convergence to a Marcus integral typically fails unless the excursions of
W_{n}
are linear.
Take
d=m=2
,
A\equiv 0
,
\xi=0
and

B:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2},\qquad B(x_{1},x_{2})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&x_{1}\end{array}\right).

Then (
1.3
) becomes

dX^{1}=dL^{1},\qquad dX^{2}=X^{1}dL^{2},\qquad X(0)=0.

In particular,
X^{1}(t)=L^{1}(t)
and
X^{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}L^{1}(t)\,dL^{2}(t)
.

Choose
h=(h_{1},h_{2})\in C^{1}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
where
h(0)=(0,0)
,
h(1)=(1,1)
.
We define
W_{n}\in D([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
by

W_{n}(t)=\begin{cases}(0,0)&\text{if }0\leq t<\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{n}\\
h\big(n(t-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{n})\big)&\text{if }\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{n}\leq t<\frac{1}{2}\\
(1,1)&\text{if }\frac{1}{2}\leq t\leq 1\end{cases}.

Then
W_{n}
converges pointwise to
L\in D([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
given by

L(t)=\mathbf{1}_{t\geq\frac{1}{2}}(1,1)
.
Moreover, a direct calculation shows that
the solutions
X_{n}
to the ODE
dX_{n}=B(X_{n})\,dW_{n}

converge pointwise to
X\in D([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
where

X(t)=\mathbf{1}_{t\geq\frac{1}{2}}\big(1,\,\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(s)\,h_{2}^{\prime}(s)\,ds\big)
.

We distinguish the case where the excursion
of
W_{n}
is linear, so
\{h(s);\,0\leq s\leq 1\}
is contained in a line. In other words,
h_{1}\equiv h_{2}
. This includes
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
convergence as special cases. Indeed

W_{n}
converges to
L

in the
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
topology if and only if
\{h(s);\,0\leq s\leq 1\}
is the line segment joining
(0,0)
to
(1,1)
.
For convergence in the
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
topology, it is required moreover that
h_{1}
is non-decreasing.

In the linear case
h_{1}\equiv h_{2}
, we obtain by a change of variables that

X(t)=\mathbf{1}_{t\geq\frac{1}{2}}\big(1,1)

and the stochastic integral

dX=B(X)\,dL
has the Marcus interpretation.
Otherwise, even though the endpoints of the excursion for
W_{n}
on
[\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{n},\frac{1}{2}]

are fixed and the limiting path
L
is fixed, the values of the limiting integral
X
on
[\frac{1}{2},1]
depend sensitively on the excursion
h
.

Decorated paths

Example
1.1
shows that it is necessary to include excursions in the description of the limiting path
L
. Hence it is necessary to consider spaces of decorated càdlàg paths where jumps are accompanied by “decorations” according to the shape of the excursion. Such spaces were introduced by Whitt
[
49
]
and studied further by Freitas
et al.

[
19
,
18
]
. Here, we build on and generalise the spaces used by
Chevyrev
et al.

[
11
,
12
,
13
]
which sufficed for the Marcus limits in
[
13
]
.
As in
[
13
]
, weak convergence of
W_{n}
is insufficient to deduce convergence of
X_{n}
and we also require tightness of
W_{n}
in
p
-variation.

The decorated càdlàg space

{\mathscr{D}}
that we introduce in Section
2.1
has several advantages.
For our purposes, the main advantage is that it is well-suited for studying fast-slow systems
because there is a natural notion of a solution to a
“decorated differential equation” in
{\mathscr{D}}
, which moves beyond linear excursions of the driver and retains continuity of the solution map
{\mathscr{D}}\ni L_{\alpha}\mapsto X\in{\mathscr{D}}
to the SDE (
1.3
),
see Theorem
2.10
.
Our framework for interpreting (
1.3
) in the presence of jumps is essentially deterministic and has novelties even if
L_{\alpha}
has bounded variation.
It notably unifies the Marcus (geometric/canonical) and Itô (forward) interpretations of differential equations; see
[
12
,
22
]
for details on these interpretations.

Another advantage is that
{\mathscr{D}}
distinguishes more decorated paths than the spaces from
[
49
,
19
,
18
]
(see Example
2.3
).
In addition, our metric on
{\mathscr{D}}
is complete and separable whereas the metrics considered in
[
19
,
49
]
are not simultaneously complete and separable.
In this regard, it is worth recalling that the standard
{\mathcal{J}}_{1}
metric
\sigma_{\infty}
(
2.1
) on the càdlàg space
D
is separable but not complete.
Despite the fact that our construction of
{\mathscr{D}}
as an
extension of the spaces in
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
is rather technical, we have the
following notable characterisation.

Theorem 1.2
.

{\mathscr{D}}
is the completion of
D
in the standard
{\mathcal{J}}_{1}
metric
\sigma_{\infty}
.

This result is proved in Section
8.2
.
In addition,
{\mathscr{D}}
simultaneously generalises the classical Skorokhod
{\mathcal{J}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
topologies via different embeddings of
D
into
{\mathscr{D}}
(see Remark
2.8
).

Fast-slow systems

Let
\alpha\in(1,2)
.
In this paper, we consider multiscale equations of the form

\begin{cases}x_{k+1}^{(n)}=x_{k}^{(n)}+n^{-1}A(x_{k}^{(n)})+n^{-1/\alpha}B(x_{k}^{(n)})v(y_{k}),\\

1.4
Solving decorated SDEs

A key part of our paper is to show how to solve equations driven by decorated processes. In the framework of Example
1.1
, we argue that
W_{n}
converges to a decorated process
{\hat{L}}
consisting of
L
together with an excursion
\{h(s);\,0\leq s\leq 1\}
at the discontinuity point
t=\frac{1}{2}
.
Solving the equation
d{\widehat{X}}=B({\widehat{X}})\,d{\hat{L}}
yields a decorated process
{\widehat{X}}
, and we show that

X_{n}
converges weakly to
{\widehat{X}}
.

To solve

d{\widehat{X}}=B({\widehat{X}})\,d{\hat{L}}
we proceed as follows: first, we insert an interval
[\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}+\delta]
into the interval
[0,1]
and define a
\delta
-extension

L^{\delta}\in D([0,1+\delta],{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
coinciding with
L
on
[0,\frac{1}{2}]\cup[\frac{1}{2}+\delta,1+\delta]
(suitably displaced) and given by
h
(suitably scaled) on
[\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}+\delta]
.
Then we solve the Young equation
X^{\delta}=B(X^{\delta})\,dL^{\delta}
for

X^{\delta}\in D([0,1+\delta],{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
.
Finally, we collapse the inserted interval
[\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}+\delta]
back to a point
\frac{1}{2}
to obtain the desired decorated limit
{\widehat{X}}
consisting of a càdlàg path on
[0,1]
and an excursion at
t=\frac{1}{2}
.
Precise definitions are given in Section
2
.

2.2
p
-variation

Fix

.
The
p
-variation semi-norm of a function

is given by

|h|_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var};[a,b]}=\sup_{a=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{k}=b}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}|h(t_{j})-h(t_{j-1})|^{p}\Big)^{1/p},

understood as
\sup_{s,t\in[a,b]}|h_{s}-h_{t}|
if
p=\infty
.
Define also the norm

\|h\|_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}=|h(a)|+|h|_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}.

Now let
\phi\in\bar{\mathscr{D}}[a,b]
.
Recalling the definition of
\phi^{\delta}\in D[a,b+\delta]
,
we set
|\phi|_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}=|\phi^{\delta}|_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}
. This is well-defined, independent of
\delta
, and constant on equivalence classes by Lemma
8.11
.
We can therefore define

|\phi|_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}
for
\phi\in{\mathscr{D}}[a,b]
.
Let

{\mathscr{D}}^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}[a,b]=\{\phi\in{\mathscr{D}}[a,b]:|\phi|_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}<\infty\}.

(2.2)

Similarly, define
\bar{\mathscr{D}}^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}[a,b]
.

Remark 2.9
.

Let
h\in D[a,b]
with trivial lift

\iota h\in{\mathscr{D}}[a,b]
.
Then
|\iota h|_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}=|h|_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}
.

Next, we introduce a Skorokhod version of
p
-variation.
For
h_{1},h_{2}\in D[a,b]
, define the metric

\sigma_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}(h_{1},h_{2})=\inf_{\rho\in{\mathcal{R}}}\max\big\{|\rho-\mathrm{Id}|_{\infty},\|h_{1}\circ\rho-h_{2}\|_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}\big\}.

For
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\bar{\mathscr{D}}^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}[a,b]
,
we set

\alpha_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sigma_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}(\phi_{1}^{\delta},\phi_{2}^{\delta}).

Again, by Lemma
8.12
(ii)
this limit exists and is constant on equivalence classes.
In this way, we define the metric
\alpha_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}
on
{\mathscr{D}}^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}[a,b]
.

2.3
Differential equations driven by decorated paths

Fix
m,d\geq 1
and
p\in[1,2)
.
For
\gamma>0
, let
C^{\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
denote the space of
functions
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

\|f\|_{C^{\gamma}}=\max_{|\alpha|=0,\ldots,[\gamma]}\|D^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty}+\sup_{x\neq y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\max_{|\alpha|=[\gamma]}\frac{|D^{\alpha}f(x)-D^{\alpha}f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\gamma-[\gamma]}}<\infty.

(Note
f\in C^{N}
for
N\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
implies only that the
(N-1)
-th derivative of
f
is Lipschitz.)
Assume that
A\in C^{\beta}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},{\mathbb{R}}^{m})
for
\beta>1
and
B\in C^{\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times d})
for
\gamma>p
.
Let
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. We consider initial value problems of the form

dX=A(X)\,dt+B(X)\,d\phi,\quad X(0)=\xi,

(2.3)

where
\phi\in{\mathscr{D}}^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
X\in{\mathscr{D}}^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{m})
.

To interpret and solve (
2.3
), consider the decorated path
(\omega,\Pi)=((\iota\mathrm{Id},\phi),\Pi)\in\bar{\mathscr{D}}^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{1+d})
,
where
\mathrm{Id}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is the identity map.
Consider also a
\delta
-extension

\omega^{\delta}\in D^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}([0,1+\delta],{\mathbb{R}}^{1+d})
,
\delta>0
, as described in Section
2.1
.
There is a unique solution

X^{\delta}\in D^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}([0,1+\delta],{\mathbb{R}}^{m})
to the initial value problem

dX^{\delta}=(A,B)(X^{\delta})\,d\omega^{\delta},\quad X^{\delta}(0)=\xi

defined as a Young ODE, see Section
8.3
.
Define
(X,\Pi)\in\bar{\mathscr{D}}^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{m})
with

X(t_{j})\in D^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{m})
for
t_{j}\in\Pi
given by
the suitably rescaled version of
X^{\delta}
restricted to the interval
I_{j}=[c_{j},c_{j}+2^{-j}\delta/r]

(see Section
8.3
for details).
By construction,
X^{\delta}
is precisely the
\delta
-extension of
(X,\Pi)
.

As shown in Proposition
8.15
, the resulting map

{\mathcal{S}}:{\mathscr{D}}^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathscr{D}}^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{m}),\qquad\phi\mapsto X,

is well-defined and continuous (with respect to the
\alpha_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}
topology on domain and range).
We call
X
the
solution
of (
2.3
) and

{\mathcal{S}}
the
solution map
.

The following is our main convergence criterion for ODEs driven by random decorated paths, the proof of which we give at the end of Section

.

Theorem 2.10
.

Suppose
\phi_{n}
is a sequence of random variables in
{\mathscr{D}}^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
\phi
is a random variable in
{\mathscr{D}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, such that

\phi_{n}\to_{w}\phi
in the
\alpha_{\infty}
topology.
Suppose further that
|\phi_{n}|_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}
is tight.

Then
|\phi|_{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}<\infty
a.s. and
the solutions

X_{n}={\mathcal{S}}(\phi_{n}),\,X={\mathcal{S}}(\phi)\in{\mathscr{D}}^{p\text{-}\mathrm{var}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{m})

satisfy

X_{n}\to_{w}X

in the
\alpha_{q\text{-}\mathrm{var}}
topology for all
q>p
.

Dynamical setup and main results

In this section, we state the main result in this paper.

3.1
Assumptions

Let
\Lambda
be a compact metric space with Borel probability measure
\mu_{\Lambda}
,
and let
T:\Lambda\to\Lambda
be an ergodic and mixing measure-preserving dynamical system.
Fix
\Sigma\subset\Lambda
with
\mu_{\Lambda}(\Sigma)>0
. We define
the
first return time

R:\Sigma\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{+},\qquad R(x)=\inf\{n\geq 1:T^{n}x\in\Sigma\},

and the
first return map

f:\Sigma\to\Sigma,\qquad fx=T^{R(x)}x.

The map
f:\Sigma\to\Sigma
preserves the probability measure
\mu=\mu_{\Lambda}|_{\Sigma}/\mu_{\Lambda}(\Sigma)
.

As described below,
we assume that
T
falls within the so-called Chernov-Markarian-Zhang framework
[
35
,
10
]
.
Informally this means that
(i) we have good control on the first return time
R
, and
(ii)
f
is modelled by a Young tower with exponential tails.

This framework incorporates uniformly expanding/hyperbolic systems such as the intermittent maps
[
44
]
as analysed in
[
13
]
, but is flexible enough to include nonuniformly hyperbolic systems such as the billiards with flat cusps
[
27
]
from Example
1.3
.

Assumptions on the first return time.

Our first assumption is that
R
is regularly varying with exponent
\alpha\in(1,2)
.
That is

\mu(R>t)\sim\ell(t)t^{-\alpha}\quad\text{as $t\to\infty$}

(3.1)

where
\ell:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a slowly varying function.

Introduce
b_{n}>0
such that
b_{n}^{\alpha}\sim n\ell(b_{n})
as
n\to\infty
.
We require that for every
s>0
, there exist
\theta_{1}>1
and
C>0
such that

\mu(R>sb_{n}\text{ and }R\circ f^{j}>sb_{n})\leq Cn^{-\theta_{1}}\quad\text{for all $1\leq j\leq n$.}

(3.2)

This assumption means that large first returns are not too clustered.

Assumptions on the first return map.

We suppose that
(f,\Sigma,\mu)
is ergodic and mixing and modelled by a Young tower with exponential tails
[
52
]
as discussed further in Section
4
.
In particular,

•

There is an inducing set
Y\subset\Sigma
with

(not necessarily the first return time) such that
F=f^{\tau}
maps
Y
into
Y
;

•

The tail probabilities
\mu(\tau>n)
decay exponentially;

•

Associated to
F

\{Y_{j}:j\geq 1\}
of
Y
and
\tau
is constant on elements
Y_{j}
.

We require that
R
is constant on elements of

{\mathcal{C}}=\{f^{\ell}Y_{j}:0\leq\ell<\tau|_{Y_{j}},\,j\geq 1\}.

(3.3)

Let

\mu(R\mathbf{1}_{\Sigma_{i}}>t)\sim c_{i}\ell(t)t^{-\alpha}\quad\text{as $t\to\infty$}

Preliminaries about Gibbs-Markov maps and Young towers

In this section, we elaborate on the structure of the dynamical systems

(f,\Sigma,\mu)
and
(F,Y,\mu_{Y})
from Section

and we collect together some ergodic-theoretic properties of such dynamical systems for easy reference during

5
,
6
and
7
.

Let
({\bar{Y}},\mu_{\bar{Y}})
be a probability space with an at most countable measurable partition
\{{\bar{Y}}_{j}:\,j\geq 1\}
and let

{\bar{F}}:{\bar{Y}}\to{\bar{Y}}
be an ergodic measure-preserving map.
Define the separation time
s(y,y^{\prime})
to be the least integer
n\geq 0
such that
{\bar{F}}^{n}y
and
{\bar{F}}^{n}y^{\prime}
lie in distinct partition elements.
We assume that
s(y,y^{\prime})=\infty
if and only if
y=y^{\prime}
; then
d_{\theta}(y,y^{\prime})=\theta^{s(y,y^{\prime})}
is a metric for
\theta\in(0,1)
.

We say that

{\bar{F}}:{\bar{Y}}\to{\bar{Y}}
is a
(full-branch) Gibbs-Markov map
if
(i)
{\bar{F}}:{\bar{Y}}_{j}\to{\bar{Y}}
is a measure-theoretic bijection for each
j\geq 1
;
(ii) there exists
\theta\in(0,1)
such that

\log\xi
is
d_{\theta}
-Lipschitz, where

\xi=d\mu_{\bar{Y}}/d\mu_{\bar{Y}}\circ{\bar{F}}
.
For standard facts about Gibbs-Markov maps, we refer to
[
1
,
2
]
.

We say that a function
g
defined on
{\bar{Y}}
is
piecewise constant
if

g
is constant on partition elements.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
g\in L^{2}({\bar{Y}})
be piecewise constant with
\int_{\bar{Y}}g\,d\mu_{\bar{Y}}=0
.
Then

\biggr|\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\Big|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}g\circ{\bar{F}}^{j}\Big|\biggr|_{L^{2}({\bar{Y}})}\leq Cn^{1/2}|g|_{L^{2}({\bar{Y}})}

where
C>0
is a constant independent of
g
and
n
.

Proof.

We suppress the bars. Also,
|\cdot|_{p}
denotes
|\cdot|_{L^{p}(Y)}
.
Given
v:Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
continuous, we define
\|v\|_{\theta}=\|v\|_{\infty}+\operatorname{Lip}_{\theta}v
where

\operatorname{Lip}_{\theta}v=\sup_{y\neq y^{\prime}}|v(y)-v(y^{\prime})|/d_{\theta}(y,y^{\prime})
.
In particular, we can fix
\theta\in(0,1)
so that

\operatorname{Lip}_{\theta}\log\xi<\infty
.

Define the transfer operator
P:L^{1}(Y)\to L^{1}(Y)
(so

\int_{Y}Pv\,w\,d\mu_{Y}=\int_{Y}v\,w\circ F\,d\mu_{Y}
for
v\in L^{1}(Y)
,
w\in L^{\infty}(Y)
).
Then
(Pv)(y)=\sum_{j}{\xi(y_{j})}v(y_{j})
where
y_{j}
is the unique preimage of
y
under
F|_{Y_{j}}
.
There exists
C>0
such that

{\xi(y_{j})}\leq C\mu(Y_{j})
and
|{\xi(y_{j})}-{\xi(y^{\prime}_{j})}|\leq C\mu(Y_{j})d_{\theta}(y,y^{\prime})

for all
y,y^{\prime}\in Y_{j}
,
j\geq 1
.
It follows easily that

\|Pg\|_{\theta}\ll|g|_{1}\leq|g|_{2}
.

There exist constants
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
C>0
such that

\|P^{n}v\|_{\theta}\leq C\gamma^{n}\|v\|_{\theta}
for all continuous
v:Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
\int_{Y}v\,d\mu_{Y}=0
and all
n\geq 1
.
Define
\chi=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P^{n}g
.
Then
|\chi|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|P^{n-1}Pg\|_{\theta}\ll\|Pg\|_{\theta}\ll|g|_{2}
.

Now define the “martingale-coboundary decomposition”

g=m+\chi\circ F-\chi

where

|m|_{2}\ll|g|_{2}
. Since
m\in\ker P
,
it follows easily that

\big|\sum_{j<n}m\circ F^{j}\big|_{2}=n^{1/2}|m|_{2}
.
Moreover,

\{m\circ F^{j},\,j\geq 0\}
is a sequence
of reverse martingale differences so, by
Doob’s inequality,

\big|\max_{k\leq n}|\sum_{j<k}m\circ F^{j}|\big|_{2}\leq 2n^{1/2}|m|_{2}
. Hence

\Big|\max_{k\leq n}\big|\sum_{j<k}g\circ F^{j}\big|\Big|_{2}\leq 2n^{1/2}|m|_{2}+2|\chi|_{\infty}\ll n^{1/2}|g|_{2}

as required.
∎

We require that the map
(F,Y,\mu_{Y})
from Section
3.1

quotients to
a Gibbs-Markov map
({\bar{F}},{\bar{Y}},\mu_{\bar{Y}})
. Specifically,

a measure-preserving semiconjugacy

between

and

.

defined on

.

Suppose as in Section

Exponential Young towers

Let
(\Sigma,d)
be a metric space and
f:\Sigma\to\Sigma
a measurable map.
Let
Y\subset\Sigma
be a measurable subset,
Let
\tau:Y\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
be a return time (so
f^{\tau(y)}y\in Y
for

).
Define
F=f^{\tau}:Y\to Y

and let
\mu_{Y}
be an
F
-invariant ergodic Borel probability measure on

.

We suppose as above that

(F,Y,\mu_{Y})
quotients to a Gibbs-Markov map
({\bar{F}},{\bar{Y}},\mu_{\bar{Y}})
.
Moreover, we require that

(In particular,

Define the

f_{\Delta}:\Delta\to\Delta

\Delta=\{(y,\ell)\in Y\times{\mathbb{Z}}:0\leq\ell<\tau(y)\},\qquad f_{\Delta}(y,\ell)=\begin{cases}(y,\ell+1)&\text{if }\ell\leq\tau(y)-2\\
(Fy,0)&\text{if }\ell=\tau(y)-1\end{cases}

with ergodic

-invariant probability measure
\mu_{\Delta}=(\mu_{Y}\times{\rm counting})/\bar{\tau}
where

\bar{\tau}=\int_{Y}\tau\,d\mu_{Y}
.

Define the semiconjugacy

\pi_{\Delta}:\Delta\to\Sigma,\qquad\pi_{\Delta}(y,\ell)=f^{\ell}(y),

between
f_{\Delta}
and
f
. Then
\mu=(\pi_{\Delta})_{*}\mu_{\Delta}

is an
f
-invariant ergodic probability measure on
\Sigma
.
Under certain further technical assumptions as in Young
[
52
]
, we say that

is
modelled by a Young tower with exponential tails
.
(These technical assumptions ensure the validity of Theorem
4.4
and Lemma
4.5
below, and are not used further in this paper.)

For
n\geq 1
, define the
lap number

N_{n}:\Delta\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
to be the integer satisfying

\sum_{j=0}^{N_{n}(x)-1}\tau(F^{j}y)\leq n+\ell<\sum_{j=0}^{N_{n}(x)}\tau(F^{j}y)

(4.1)

for

.

Proposition 4.3
.

There exists
C>0
such that

\big|\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|N_{k}-k\bar{\tau}^{-1}|\big|_{L^{1}(\Delta)}\leq Cn^{1/2}

for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

Write
\tau_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\tau\circ F^{j}
.
Then, for
x=(y,\ell)\in\Delta
,

\tau_{N_{n}(x)}(y)-\tau(y)\leq\sum_{j=0}^{N_{n}(x)-1}\tau(F^{j}y)-\ell\leq n\leq\sum_{j=0}^{N_{n}(x)}\tau(F^{j}y)-\ell\leq\tau_{N_{n}(x)}(y)+\tau(F^{N_{n}(x)}y).

Hence,

|n-N_{n}(x)\bar{\tau}|\leq|\tau_{N_{n}(x)}(y)-N_{n}(x)\bar{\tau}|+\max\{\tau(y),\tau(F^{N_{n}(x)}y)\}.

Since
N_{n}\leq n
,

\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|k\bar{\tau}^{-1}-N_{k}(x)|\leq\bar{\tau}^{-1}H_{n}(y)

where

H_{n}(y)=\max_{0\leq j\leq n}|\tau_{j}(y)-j\bar{\tau}|+\max_{0\leq j\leq n}\tau(F^{j}y)
.

Since
\tau\in L^{2}(Y)

and
H_{n}
is independent of
\ell
,

\big|\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|k\bar{\tau}^{-1}-N_{k}|\big|_{L^{1}(\Delta)}\leq\bar{\tau}^{-1}\int_{Y}\tau\,\bar{\tau}^{-1}H_{n}\,d\mu_{Y}\ll|H_{n}|_{L^{2}(Y)}.

Applying Proposition
4.1
with
g=\tau-\bar{\tau}
,

\big|\max_{0\leq j\leq n}|\tau_{j}-j\bar{\tau}|\big|_{L^{2}(Y)}=\big|\max_{0\leq j\leq n}|\tau_{j}-j\bar{\tau}|\big|_{L^{2}({\bar{Y}})}\ll n^{1/2}.

Also,

|\max_{0\leq j\leq n}\tau\circ F^{j}|_{L^{2}(Y)}\leq n^{1/2}|\tau|_{L^{2}(Y)}.

Hence
|H_{n}|_{L^{2}(Y)}\ll n^{1/2}
as required.

is mixing and modelled by a Young tower with exponential tails.
Suppose that conditions (
3.2
) and (
3.4
) hold.
Then
W_{n}^{Z}\to_{\mu}{\tilde{L}}_{\alpha}
in the
{\mathcal{J}}_{1}
topology.

Our proof of Theorem
5.1

largely follows the approach in
[
26
, Section 4]

which was written specifically for billiards with flat cusps in the case
d=1
.

On the probability space
(\Sigma,\mu)
, we define the sequence of random point processes

{\mathcal{N}}_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{(\frac{j}{n}\,,\,b_{n}^{-1}{\widetilde{Z}}\circ f^{j-1})}

on
(0,\infty)\times({\mathbb{R}}^{e}\setminus\{0\})
.
The Lévy measure
\Pi
corresponding to the Lévy process
{\tilde{L}}_{\alpha}
is given by

\Pi(B)=\alpha\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{e-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{B}(rx)r^{-\alpha-1}\,dr\,d\nu(x)

where
\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{e}c_{i}\delta_{\omega_{i}}
is the spectral measure from Section
3.2
.
Let
{\mathcal{N}}
be the Poisson point process on
(0,\infty)\times({\mathbb{R}}^{e}\setminus\{0\})
with mean measure
\operatorname{Leb}\times\Pi
.

By
[
47
, Theorem 4.1]
(see also
[
48
, Theorem 1.2]
and
[
26
, Proposition 4.4]
), to prove Theorem
5.1
it is enough to verify two conditions:

Condition I (Point process convergence).

{\mathcal{N}}_{n}\to_{\mu}{\mathcal{N}}
as
n\to\infty

in the space of point measures defined on
(0,\infty)\times({\mathbb{R}}^{e}\setminus\{0\})
.

Condition II (Vanishing small values).

For every
\gamma>0
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu\biggl(\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\Bigl|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\bigl({\widetilde{Z}}\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<b_{n}\varepsilon\}}\bigr)\circ f^{j}-k\int_{\Sigma}{\widetilde{Z}}\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<b_{n}\varepsilon\}}\,d\mu\Bigr|>b_{n}\gamma\biggr)=0.

These conditions are verified in the next two subsections.
We denote by
B_{a}(c)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{e}
the open ball of radius
a
centred at
c
.

5.1
Point process convergence

In this subsection, we verify Condition I. We follow
[
26
, Section 4.5]

using
[
42
, Theorem 2.1]
.
It is enough to prove convergence of
{\mathcal{N}}_{n}
to
{\mathcal{N}}
on

(0,\infty)\times U,\qquad U={\mathbb{R}}^{e}\setminus\overline{B_{a_{0}}(0)}

for each fixed
a_{0}>0
.

Fix
a_{0}
and let

A_{n}=\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|>a_{0}b_{n}\}.

Let
{\mathcal{W}}
be the ring of subsets of
U
generated by sets of the type

\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{e}:a<|x|<a^{\prime},\ x/|x|\in E\}
, where
a_{0}<a<a^{\prime}
and

E\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{e-1}
is open with
\nu(\partial E)=0
.
Note that
{\mathcal{W}}
generates the Borel sigma-algebra on
U
and that
\Pi(\partial W)=0
for all
W\in{\mathcal{W}}
.

For a collection
{\mathcal{F}}
of measurable subsets of
\Sigma
, define

{\mathcal{Q}}_{p}({\mathcal{F}})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in{\mathcal{F}}\\
B\in\sigma(\bigcup_{j\geq p}f^{-j}{\mathcal{F}})\end{subarray}}\bigl|\mu(A\cap B)-\mu(A)\mu(B)\bigr|.

Let

J_{n}=b_{n}^{-1}{\widetilde{Z}}.

By
[
42
, Theorem 2.1]
, to prove Condition I it suffices to prove the following:

Lemma 5.2
.

(a)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu\bigl(J_{n}^{-1}W|A_{n}\bigr)=\Pi(W|U)
for all
W\in{\mathcal{W}}
.

(b)

{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}(J_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{W}}_{0})=o(\mu(A_{n}))

for every finite subset
{\mathcal{W}}_{0}
of
{\mathcal{W}}
.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
(a).

Without loss, we may suppose that
W=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{e}:a<|x|<a^{\prime},\,x/|x|\in E\}
where

a_{0}<a<a^{\prime}
and
E\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{e-1}
is open with
\nu(\partial E)=0
.
Since
Z-{\widetilde{Z}}
is constant, it follows from (
3.8
) that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}

\displaystyle\mu\bigl(J_{n}^{-1}W|A_{n}\bigr)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mu\big(|{\widetilde{Z}}|\in(ab_{n},a^{\prime}b_{n}),\,{\widetilde{Z}}/|{\widetilde{Z}}|\in E\big)}{\mu(|{\widetilde{Z}}|>a_{0}b_{n})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mu\big(|Z|\in(ab_{n},a^{\prime}b_{n}),\,Z/|Z|\in E\big)}{\mu(|Z|>a_{0}b_{n})}=a_{0}^{\alpha}(a^{-\alpha}-(a^{\prime})^{-\alpha})\nu(E).

On the other hand,
by the definition of
\Pi
,

\Pi(W|U)=\frac{\Pi(W)}{\Pi(U)}=\frac{(a^{-\alpha}-(a^{\prime})^{-\alpha})\nu(E)}{a_{0}^{-\alpha}},

so the result is proved.
∎

Next, we prove Lemma
5.2
(b).

Proposition 5.3
.

\mu(A_{n})\sim a_{0}^{-\alpha}n^{-1}
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Since
Z-{\widetilde{Z}}
is constant,
\mu(A_{n})\sim\mu(A_{n}^{\prime})

where
A_{n}^{\prime}=\{|Z|>a_{0}b_{n}\}
.

By (
3.4
),

\mu(|Z|>t)=\mu(R\mathbf{1}_{\bigcup_{i=1,\dots,e}\Sigma_{i}}>t)\sim\ell(t)t^{-\alpha}.

Using that
\ell
is slowly varying and that
b_{n}^{\alpha}\sim n\ell(b_{n})
,

\mu(A_{n})\sim\mu(A_{n}^{\prime})\sim\ell(a_{0}b_{n})(a_{0}b_{n})^{-\alpha}\sim a_{0}^{-\alpha}\ell(a_{0}b_{n})\ell(b_{n})^{-1}n^{-1}\sim a_{0}^{-\alpha}n^{-1},

as required.
∎

Let
\theta_{1}>1
be as in (
3.2
) for
s=a_{0}/2
. Without loss, we can suppose that
\theta_{1}\in(1,2)
.
Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
be as in Theorem
4.4
.

Proposition 5.4
.

There exists
C>0
such that

\mu(A_{n}\cap f^{-k}A_{n})\leq Cn^{-\theta_{1}}

for all
k,n\geq 1
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.4
,

\big|\mu(A_{n}\cap f^{-k}A_{n})-\mu(A_{n})^{2}\big|\ll\gamma^{k}

for all
k\geq 1
.
By Proposition
5.3
,

\mu(A_{n})^{2}\ll n^{-2}\leq n^{-\theta_{1}}
.
Hence

\mu(A_{n}\cap f^{-k}A_{n})\ll n^{-\theta_{1}}

uniformly in
k\geq n
.

Let
D=\{R>a_{0}b_{n}/2\}
. Then

A_{n}\textstyle\subset\{2|Z|>a_{0}b_{n}\}\subset\{2R>a_{0}b_{n}\}=D.

Hence
A_{n}\cap f^{-k}A_{n}\subset D\cap f^{-k}D
and,
by (
3.2
),
\mu(A_{n}\cap f^{-k}A_{n})\ll n^{-\theta_{1}}
uniformly in
1\leq k\leq n
.

Combining the estimates for
k\leq n
and
k\geq n
yields the desired result.
∎

Now fix
{\mathcal{W}}_{0}\subset{\mathcal{W}}
finite, and let
{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,p}={\mathcal{Q}}_{p}(J_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{W}}_{0})
.

Proposition 5.5
.

{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,p}\leq C\gamma^{p}
for all
n,p\geq 1
.

Proof.

Let
A\in J_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{W}}_{0}
,
B\in\sigma\big(\bigcup_{j\geq p}f^{-j}J_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{W}}_{0}\big)
. Write

B=f^{-p}B^{\prime}
where
B^{\prime}\in\sigma\big(\bigcup_{j\geq 0}f^{-j}J_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{W}}_{0}\big)
.
Then

|\mu(A\cap B)-\mu(A)\mu(B)|\ll\gamma^{p}

by Theorem
4.4
.
∎

Let
\tau_{A_{n}}(x)=\min\{n\geq 1:f^{n}x\in A_{n}\}
for
x\in\Sigma
.

Lemma 5.6
.

{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,1}\leq{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,p+1}+\mu(A_{n}\cap\{\tau_{A_{n}}\leq p\})+\mu(A_{n})\mu(\tau_{A_{n}}\leq p)

for all
n,p\geq 1
.

Proof.

This is identical to
[
26
, Lemma 4.9]
. We give the short argument for completeness.

Let
A\in J_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{W}}_{0}
and
B\in\sigma\bigl(\bigcup_{j\geq 1}f^{-j}J_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{W}}_{0}\bigr)
.
Note that
A\subset A_{n}
by definition of
{\mathcal{W}}
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{W}}_{0}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{K}\}
.
Observe that there exists a function

g:(\{0,1\}^{K})^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}}\to\{0,1\}
such that
\mathbf{1}_{B}=g(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots)
, where

Y_{i}=\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{J_{n}^{-1}W_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{1}_{J_{n}^{-1}W_{K}}\bigr)\circ f^{i}
.
Define
B^{\prime}\in\sigma\bigl(\bigcup_{j\geq p+1}f^{-j}(J_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{W}}_{0})\bigr)
by

\mathbf{1}_{B^{\prime}}=g(0,\ldots,0,Y_{p+1},Y_{p+2},\ldots).

Then

|\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{1}_{A},\mathbf{1}_{B^{\prime}})|\leq{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,p+1}
. Moreover,

|\mathbf{1}_{B}-\mathbf{1}_{B^{\prime}}|\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{A_{n}}\leq p\}}
, so

\displaystyle\bigl|\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{1}_{A},\mathbf{1}_{B})-\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{1}_{A},\mathbf{1}_{B^{\prime}})\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\mu\bigl(A\cap\{\tau_{A_{n}}\leq p\}\bigr)+\mu(A)\mu(\tau_{A_{n}}\leq p)

\displaystyle\leq\mu(A_{n}\cap\{\tau_{A_{n}}\leq p\})+\mu(A_{n})\mu(\tau_{A_{n}}\leq p).

The result follows.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.2
(b).

Let
p_{n}=[n^{\theta_{2}}]
where

0<\theta_{2}<\theta_{1}-1<1
.
Applying Proposition
5.4
,

\displaystyle\mu(A_{n}\cap\{\tau_{A_{n}}\leq p_{n}\})

\displaystyle=\mu\biggl(A_{n}\cap\bigcup_{k=1}^{p_{n}}f^{-k}A_{n}\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{p_{n}}\mu(A_{n}\cap f^{-k}A_{n})=O(p_{n}n^{-\theta_{1}})=o(n^{-1}).

Using Proposition
5.3
and invariance of
\mu
under
f
,

\mu(\tau_{A_{n}}\leq p_{n})=\mu\biggl(\bigcup_{k=1}^{p_{n}}f^{-k}A_{n}\biggr)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{p_{n}}\mu(f^{-k}A_{n})=p_{n}\mu(A_{n})=O(p_{n}n^{-1})=o(1).

Hence it follows from
Proposition
5.3
and Lemma
5.6
that

{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,1}\leq{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,p_{n}+1}+o(\mu(A_{n}))
.
By Propositions
5.3
and
5.5
,

{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,p_{n}+1}=o(n^{-1})=o(\mu(A_{n}))
, so

{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}(J_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{W}}_{0})={\mathcal{Q}}_{n,1}=o(\mu(A_{n}))
.
∎

This concludes the verification of Condition I.

5.2
Vanishing small values

In this subsection, we verify Condition II.
The regularly varying sequence
b_{n}
is asymptotically equivalent to a strictly increasing sequence, so for the purposes of proving Theorem
5.1
we may suppose without loss of generality that
b_{n}
strictly increases and
b_{1}=1
.

It is convenient to work at the level of the Young tower
f_{\Delta}:\Delta\to\Delta
.
Recall from Section
4
that
\pi_{\Delta}:\Delta\to\Sigma
,
\pi(y,\ell)=f^{\ell}y

is a measure-preserving semiconjugacy
from
(f_{\Delta},\Delta,\mu_{\Delta})
to
(f,\Sigma,\mu)
.
Let
\Delta_{i}=\pi_{\Delta}^{-1}\Sigma_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,e
.
Abusing notation, we denote lifted observables
R\circ\pi_{\Delta}:\Delta\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
,

{\widetilde{Z}}\circ\pi_{\Delta}:\Delta\to{\mathbb{R}}^{e}
simply by
R
,
{\widetilde{Z}}
and so on.

Fix
i=1,\dots,e
and set
R^{(i)}=R\mathbf{1}_{\Delta_{i}}
. Let
\bar{Z}=\int_{\Delta}Z\,d\mu_{\Delta}
and

{\widetilde{R}}_{\xi}^{(i)}=R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{i}R\in B_{\xi}(\bar{Z})\}}-\int_{\Delta}R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{i}R\in B_{\xi}(\bar{Z})\}}\,d\mu_{\Delta},\quad\xi>0.

The main step in the proof is:

Lemma 5.7
.

There is a constant
C>0
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}b_{n}^{-1}\biggl|\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\Big|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{\widetilde{R}}_{\varepsilon b_{n}}^{(i)}\circ f_{\Delta}^{j}\Big|\biggr|_{L^{1}(\Delta)}\leq C\varepsilon^{1-\alpha/2}\quad\text{for all $\varepsilon>0$.}

Most of the remainder of this subsection is concerned with proving Lemma
5.7
.
At the end of the subsection, we show that Condition II follows from the lemma.

We begin with the following consequence of condition (
3.2
).
Let
\theta_{1}>1
be as in (
3.2
) with
s=1
.

Proposition 5.8
.

There exists
a_{1}>0
such that

\mu_{\Delta}(R>\eta\text{ and }R\circ f^{j}>\eta)\ll\Big(\frac{\eta^{\alpha}}{\ell(\eta)}\Big)^{-\theta_{1}}\quad\text{for all $1\leq j\leq a_{1}\eta^{\alpha}/\ell(\eta)$, $\,\eta>1$.}

Proof.

Since
b_{n}
is strictly increasing, we can write

b_{n}=\tilde{b}(n)
where

\tilde{b}:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is strictly increasing
and
\tilde{b}(x)^{\alpha}\sim x\ell(\tilde{b}(x))
as
x\to\infty
.
Note that
\tilde{b}^{-1}(x)\sim x^{\alpha}/\ell(x)
as
x\to\infty
.
Choose
a_{1}>0
such that

a_{1}x^{\alpha}/\ell(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}\tilde{b}^{-1}(x)
for all
x\geq 1
.

Given
\eta>1=b_{1}
, there exists
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
such that

b_{n}<\eta\leq b_{n+1}
.
By (
3.2
),

\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta}(R>\eta\text{ and }R\circ f^{j}>\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{\Delta}(R>b_{n}\text{ and }R\circ f^{j}>b_{n})

\displaystyle\ll n^{-\theta_{1}}\ll(n+1)^{-\theta_{1}}\leq(\tilde{b}^{-1}(\eta))^{-\theta_{1}}\ll\Big(\frac{\eta^{\alpha}}{\ell(\eta)}\Big)^{-\theta_{1}}

for all
1\leq j\leq n
.
Finally, if
j\leq a_{1}\eta^{\alpha}/\ell(\eta)
,
then
j\leq\frac{1}{2}\tilde{b}^{-1}(\eta)\leq\frac{1}{2}(n+1)\leq n
so the
estimate holds for all such
j
.
∎

From now on, we fix
\theta_{1}
and
a_{1}
as in Proposition
5.8
.

Let
1\leq\eta\leq\xi
. Define

H=H_{\xi}:Y\to[0,\infty)
,

s=s_{\eta}:Y\to{\mathbb{Z}}
,

H(y)=\sum_{0\leq j\leq\tau(y)-1}(R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{i}R\in B_{\xi}(\bar{Z})\}})(y,j),\quad s(y)=\#\{0\leq j\leq\tau(y)-1:R(y,j)>\eta\}.

Note that
H=H^{\prime}+H^{\prime\prime}+H^{\prime\prime\prime}
, where

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\{s(y)=1\}}\sum_{j<\tau(y)}(R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{R>\eta\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{i}R\in B_{\xi}(\bar{Z})\}})(y,j),

\displaystyle H^{\prime\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j<\tau(y)}(R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{R\leq\eta\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{i}R\in B_{\xi}(\bar{Z})\}})(y,j),

\displaystyle H^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\{s(y)\geq 2\}}\sum_{j<\tau(y)}(R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{R>\eta\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{i}R\in B_{\xi}(\bar{Z})\}})(y,j).

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
\delta>0
. There exist
M,\,C>0
such that

(a)

|H^{\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}\leq C\ell(\xi)^{1/2}\xi^{1-\alpha/2}
,

(b)

|H^{\prime\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}\leq C\eta^{1-\alpha/2+\delta}
,

(c)

|H^{\prime\prime\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}\leq C\xi^{1+\delta}\Big(\frac{\eta^{\alpha}}{\ell(\eta)}\Big)^{-\theta_{1}/2}
,

for all
1<\eta\leq\xi
satisfying
M\log\xi\leq a_{1}\eta^{\alpha}/\ell(\eta)

and
\xi>|\bar{Z}|
.

Proof.

We use throughout that
R^{(i)}\leq R
for all
i
.

(a)

Observe that
H^{\prime}\leq\xi^{\prime}
where

\xi^{\prime}=\xi+|\bar{Z}|
.
Also for
0\leq n\leq\xi^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\mu_{Y}(H^{\prime}=n)

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{Y}(y\in Y:R(y,j)=n\text{ for some $0\leq j<\tau(y)$}\})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{Y}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{y\in Y:R(y,j)=n\}}\,d\mu_{Y}(y)=\bar{\tau}\mu_{\Delta}(R=n).

Hence

|H^{\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}^{2}=\sum_{n\leq\xi^{\prime}}n^{2}\mu_{Y}(H^{\prime}=n)\leq\bar{\tau}\sum_{n\leq\xi^{\prime}}n^{2}\mu_{\Delta}(R=n)\ll\sum_{n\leq\xi^{\prime}}n\mu_{\Delta}(R\geq n).

Using (
3.4
) and applying Karamata’s inequality, we conclude that

|H^{\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}^{2}\ll\ell(\xi^{\prime})\xi^{\prime 2-\alpha}\ll\ell(\xi)\xi^{2-\alpha}

as required.

(b)

Let
q>2
,
\varepsilon>0
and
observe that
|R\mathbf{1}_{\{R\leq\eta\}}|_{L^{q}(\Delta)}^{q}\ll\sum_{j\leq\eta}j^{q-1}\mu_{\Delta}(R\geq j)\ll\eta^{q+\varepsilon-\alpha}
.
Let
g_{j}(y)=(R\mathbf{1}_{\{R\leq\eta\}})\circ f_{\Delta}^{j}(y,0)
.
Since
f_{\Delta}
is measure-preserving,

\begin{split}\Big|\sum_{j<k}g_{j}\Big|_{L^{q}(Y)}^{q}&=\int_{Y}\Big|\sum_{j<k}g_{j}\Big|^{q}\,d\mu_{Y}\leq\int_{Y}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\tau(y)-1}\Big|\sum_{j<k}(R\mathbf{1}_{\{R\leq\eta\}})\circ f_{\Delta}^{j}(y,\ell)\Big|^{q}\,d\mu_{Y}(y)\\
&=\bar{\tau}\int_{\Delta}\Big|\sum_{j<k}(R\mathbf{1}_{\{R\leq\eta\}})\circ f_{\Delta}^{j}\Big|^{q}\,d\mu_{\Delta}\leq\bar{\tau}k^{q}|R\mathbf{1}_{\{R\leq\eta\}}|_{L^{q}(\Delta)}^{q}\ll k^{q}\eta^{q+\varepsilon-\alpha}.\end{split}

Note also that

H^{\prime\prime}\leq\sum_{j<\tau}g_{j}
.
Since
\tau
has exponential tails, there exists
c_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle|H^{\prime\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigl|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau=k\}}\sum_{j<k}g_{j}\Bigr|_{L^{2}(Y)}^{2}\ll\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-c_{0}k}\Bigl|\sum_{j<k}g_{j}\Bigr|_{L^{q}(Y)}^{2}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-c_{0}k}(k\eta^{(q+\varepsilon-\alpha)/q})^{2}\ll\eta^{2(q+\varepsilon-\alpha)/q}.

The desired estimate follows for
q
and
\varepsilon
sufficiently close to
2
and
0
.

(c)

Since
\tau
has exponential tails, there exists
c_{1}>0
such that

k^{2}\mu_{Y}(\tau=k)\ll e^{-c_{1}k}
.
Set
M=2\alpha\theta_{1}/c_{1}
.

Suppose that
1<\eta\leq\xi
satisfies

M\log\xi\leq a_{1}\eta^{\alpha}/\ell(\eta)
.
For any
y\in\{\tau=k,\,s\geq 2\}
, there exist

0\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<k
such that

R(y,j_{1})>\eta
and

R(y,j_{2})>\eta
.
By Proposition
5.8
,

\displaystyle\mu_{Y}(\tau=k,\;s\geq 2)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{\tau}\sum_{0\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<k}\mu_{\Delta}(R\circ f_{\Delta}^{j_{1}}>\eta\text{ and }R\circ f_{\Delta}^{j_{2}}>\eta)

\displaystyle\leq k\bar{\tau}\sum_{1\leq j<k}\mu_{\Delta}(R>\eta\text{ and }R\circ f_{\Delta}^{j}>\eta)\ll k^{2}\Big(\frac{\eta^{\alpha}}{\ell(\eta)}\Big)^{-\theta_{1}}

for all
k\leq a_{1}\eta^{\alpha}/\ell(\eta)
and hence for

k\leq M\log\xi
.

Noting that

H^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)\ll\mathbf{1}_{\{s(y)\geq 2\}}\,\xi\tau(y)
,

\displaystyle|H^{\prime\prime\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}^{2}

\displaystyle\ll\xi^{2}\int_{Y}\mathbf{1}_{\{s\geq 2\}}\,\tau^{2}\,d\mu_{Y}

\displaystyle\ll\xi^{2}\sum_{k\leq M\log\xi}k^{2}\mu_{Y}(\tau=k,\;s\geq 2)+\xi^{2}\sum_{k>M\log\xi}k^{2}\mu_{Y}(\tau=k).

Now,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq M\log\xi}k^{2}\mu_{Y}(\tau=k,\;s\geq 2)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{k\leq M\log\xi}k^{4}\Big(\frac{\eta^{\alpha}}{\ell(\eta)}\Big)^{-\theta_{1}}\ll(\log\xi)^{5}\Big(\frac{\eta^{\alpha}}{\ell(\eta)}\Big)^{-\theta_{1}},

\displaystyle\sum_{k>M\log\xi}k^{2}\mu_{Y}(\tau=k)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{k>M\log\xi}e^{-c_{1}k}\ll\xi^{-c_{1}M}\leq\eta^{-c_{1}M}=\eta^{-2\alpha\theta_{1}}.

Hence,

|H^{\prime\prime\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}^{2}\ll\xi^{2}(\log\xi+1)^{5}\Big(\frac{\eta^{\alpha}}{\ell(\eta)}\Big)^{-\theta_{1}}

and the result follows.
∎

Corollary 5.10
.

There is a constant
C>0
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}b_{n}^{-1}n^{1/2}|H_{\varepsilon b_{n}}|_{L^{2}(Y)}\leq C\varepsilon^{1-\alpha/2}\quad\text{for all $\varepsilon>0$.}

Proof.

Recalling that
\theta_{1}>1
, we
choose
\gamma\in(0,1/\alpha)
sufficiently close to
1/\alpha
that
\gamma\alpha\theta_{1}>1
.
We apply Proposition
5.9
with

\xi=\varepsilon b_{n}
,
\eta=n^{\gamma}
.
(Note that for all
\varepsilon>0
,
|\bar{Z}|\in{\mathbb{R}}^{e}
, the constraints
1<\eta\leq\xi
,
M\log\xi\leq a_{1}\eta^{\alpha}/\ell(\alpha)
,
\xi>|\bar{Z}|
are satisfied for
n
sufficiently large.)
Then

|H^{\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}\ll\varepsilon^{1-\alpha/2}b_{n}\Big(\frac{\ell(\varepsilon b_{n})}{\ell(b_{n})}\Big)^{1/2}\big(\ell(b_{n})b_{n}^{-\alpha}\big)^{1/2}\sim\varepsilon^{1-\alpha/2}b_{n}n^{-1/2}.

Also,
|H^{\prime\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}\ll n^{\gamma(1-\alpha/2+\delta)}=o(b_{n}n^{-1/2})
for
\delta>0
sufficiently small.
Finally,
|H^{\prime\prime\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}\ll b_{n}^{1+\delta}n^{-\gamma\alpha\theta_{1}/2}\tilde{\ell}(n)
where
\tilde{\ell}
is slowly varying.
Hence,

|H^{\prime\prime\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}\ll b_{n}^{1+\delta}n^{-\gamma\alpha\theta_{1}/2}n^{\delta}
.
Since
\gamma\alpha\theta_{1}>1
, we can shrink
\delta
if necessary so that

b_{n}^{\delta}n^{\delta}n^{-\gamma\alpha\theta_{1}/2}=o(n^{-1/2})
.
Hence,
|H^{\prime\prime\prime}|_{L^{2}(Y)}=o(b_{n}n^{-1/2})
.
∎

Define

\psi_{\xi}:\Delta\to[0,\infty)
by

\psi_{\xi}(y,\ell)=\sum_{0\leq j<\ell}(R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{i}R\in B_{\xi}(\bar{Z})\}})(y,j).

Corollary 5.11
.

There exists
C>0
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}b_{n}^{-1}\big|\max_{k\leq n}\psi_{\varepsilon b_{n}}\circ f_{\Delta}^{k}\big|_{L^{1}(\Delta)}\leq C\varepsilon^{1-\alpha/2}\quad\text{for all $\varepsilon>0$.}

Proof.

Define
h_{\xi}:\Delta\to[0,\infty)
by

h_{\xi}(y,\ell)=H_{\xi}(y)
.
Then

\max_{k\leq n}h_{\xi}\circ f_{\Delta}^{k}(y,\ell)\leq\max_{k\leq n}H_{\xi}(F^{k}y)
.
Since
\tau
has exponential tails,

\displaystyle\big|\max_{k\leq n}h_{\xi}\circ f_{\Delta}^{k}\big|_{L^{1}(\Delta)}

\displaystyle\leq\bar{\tau}^{-1}\int_{Y}\tau\max_{k\leq n}H_{\xi}\circ F^{k}\,d\mu_{Y}

\displaystyle\ll\big|\max_{k\leq n}H_{\xi}\circ F^{k}\big|_{L^{2}(Y)}\ll n^{1/2}|H_{\xi}|_{L^{2}(Y)}.

Hence it follows from Corollary
5.10
that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}b_{n}^{-1}|\max_{k\leq n}h_{\varepsilon b_{n}}\circ f_{\Delta}^{k}|_{L^{1}(\Delta)}\ll\varepsilon^{1-\alpha/2}
.
Since
0\leq\psi_{\varepsilon b_{n}}\leq h_{\varepsilon b_{n}}
, we obtain
the desired estimate for
\max_{k\leq n}\psi_{\varepsilon b_{n}}\circ f_{\Delta}^{k}
.
∎

Define
Q_{n,\xi}:\Delta\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
Q_{n,\xi}=N_{n}\int_{Y}H_{\xi}\,d\mu_{Y}-n\int_{\Delta}R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{i}R\in B_{\xi}(\bar{Z})\}}\,d\mu_{\Delta}
where
N_{n}
is the lap number defined in (
4.1
).

Proposition 5.12
.

There exists
C>0
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}b_{n}^{-1}\big|\max_{k\leq n}|Q_{k,\varepsilon b_{n}}|\big|_{L^{1}(\Delta)}\leq C\varepsilon^{1-\alpha/2}\quad\text{for all $\varepsilon>0$.}

Proof.

Observe that

Q_{n,\xi}=(N_{n}-n\bar{\tau}^{-1})\int_{Y}H_{\xi}\,d\mu_{Y}
. Hence

|Q_{n,\xi}|\leq|N_{n}-n\bar{\tau}^{-1}|\int_{Y}H_{\xi}\,d\mu_{Y}\leq|N_{n}-n\bar{\tau}^{-1}|\,|H_{\xi}|_{L^{2}(Y)}.

The result follows from Proposition
4.3
and Corollary
5.10
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.7
.

Let
{\widetilde{H}}_{\xi}=H_{\xi}-\int_{Y}H_{\xi}\,d\mu_{Y}
.
Define
G_{n,\xi}:Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
g_{n,\xi}:\Delta\to{\mathbb{R}}
,

G_{n,\xi}=\max_{k\leq n}\sum_{j<k}{\widetilde{H}}_{\xi}\circ F^{j},\quad g_{n,\xi}(y,\ell)=G_{n,\xi}(y).

By Proposition
4.1
and Corollary
5.10
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}b_{n}^{-1}|G_{n,\varepsilon b_{n}}|_{L^{2}(Y)}\ll\limsup_{n\to\infty}b_{n}^{-1}n^{1/2}|H_{\varepsilon b_{n}}|_{L^{2}(Y)}\ll\varepsilon^{1-\alpha/2}.

Since
\tau
has exponential tails,

\int_{\Delta}|g_{n,\varepsilon b_{n}}|\,d\mu_{\Delta}=\bar{\tau}^{-1}\int_{Y}\tau|G_{n,\varepsilon b_{n}}|\,d\mu_{Y}\ll|G_{n,\varepsilon b_{n}}|_{L^{2}(Y)},

so

\limsup_{n\to\infty}b_{n}^{-1}\int_{\Delta}|g_{n,\varepsilon b_{n}}|\,d\mu_{\Delta}\ll\varepsilon^{1-\alpha/2}.

(5.1)

Next, by the definition of the lap number
N_{n}
,

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{i}R\in B_{\xi}(\bar{Z})\}})(f_{\Delta}^{j}(y,\ell))=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{n}(y,\ell)-1}H_{\xi}(F^{j}y)+\psi_{\xi}(f_{\Delta}^{n}(y,\ell))-\psi_{\xi}(y,\ell),

and so

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}{\widetilde{R}}_{\xi}^{(i)}(f_{\Delta}^{j}(y,\ell))=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{n}(y,\ell)-1}{\widetilde{H}}_{\xi}(F^{j}y)+\psi_{\xi}(f_{\Delta}^{n}(y,\ell))-\psi_{\xi}(y,\ell)+Q_{n,\xi}(y,\ell).

Since
N_{n}\leq n
for all
n
,

\max_{k\leq n}\Big|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{\widetilde{R}}_{\xi}^{(i)}\circ f_{\Delta}^{j}(y,\ell)\Big|\ll\max_{k\leq n}\big|\sum_{j<k}{\widetilde{H}}_{\xi}(F^{j}y)\big|+\max_{k\leq n}\psi_{\xi}(f_{\Delta}^{k}(y,\ell))+\max_{k\leq n}|Q_{k,\xi}(y,\ell)|.

In other words,

\max_{k\leq n}\Big|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{\widetilde{R}}_{\xi}^{(i)}\circ f_{\Delta}^{j}\Big|\ll g_{n,\xi}+\max_{k\leq n}\psi_{\xi}\circ f_{\Delta}^{k}+\max_{k\leq n}|Q_{k,\xi}|.

Setting
\xi=\varepsilon b_{n}
, the result follows from (
5.1
), Corollary
5.11
and Proposition
5.12
.
∎

We can now complete the verification of Condition II.
First notice that

R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<\xi\}}=R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{|\omega_{i}R^{(i)}-\int_{\Delta}Z\,d\mu|<\xi\}}=R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{i}R\in B_{\xi}(\bar{Z})\}}.

Hence

Z\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<\xi\}}=\sum_{i}\omega_{i}R^{(i)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{i}R\in B_{\xi}(\bar{Z})\}}

and so

Z\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<\xi\}}-\int_{\Delta}Z\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<\xi\}}d\mu_{\Delta}=\sum_{i}\omega_{i}{\widetilde{R}}_{\xi}^{(i)}.

By Lemma
5.7
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}b_{n}^{-1}\biggl|\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\Big|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(Z\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<\varepsilon b_{n}\}}\big)\circ f_{\Delta}^{j}-k\int_{\Delta}Z\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<\varepsilon b_{n}\}}d\mu_{\Delta}\Big|\biggr|_{L^{1}(\Delta)}\ll\varepsilon^{1-\alpha/2}.

Also, by Proposition
4.1
with
g=\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<\varepsilon b_{n}\}}-\int_{\Delta}\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<\varepsilon b_{n}\}}d\mu_{\Delta}
,

\biggl|\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\Big|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<\varepsilon b_{n}\}}\circ f_{\Delta}^{j}-k\int_{\Delta}\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<\varepsilon b_{n}\}}d\mu_{\Delta}\Big|\biggr|_{L^{2}(\Delta)}\ll n^{1/2}.

Combining these two estimates yields

\limsup_{n\to\infty}b_{n}^{-1}\biggl|\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\Big|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big({\widetilde{Z}}\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<\varepsilon b_{n}\}}\big)\circ f_{\Delta}^{j}-k\int_{\Delta}{\widetilde{Z}}\mathbf{1}_{\{|{\widetilde{Z}}|<\varepsilon b_{n}\}}d\mu_{\Delta}\Big|\biggr|_{L^{1}(\Delta)}\ll\varepsilon^{1-\alpha/2}.

Hence Condition II follows from Markov’s inequality and the fact that
\pi_{\Delta}:\Delta\to\Sigma
is a measure-preserving semiconjugacy.
This completes the proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Turning to Step 2, recall

\Gamma h(t)&\text{if }s=1\text{ or }h(t^{-})=h(t)\end{cases}

Lemma 6.2
.

The map
G
is continuous from
(D([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{e}),\sigma_{\infty})
to
({\mathscr{D}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\alpha_{\infty})

Proof.

It is clear that

for all

since
G(h)(\cdot)(1)=\Gamma h
and
h
is càdlàg by assumption.

To prove continuity of
G
, let
\varepsilon>0
and
g,h\in D([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{e})
with
\sigma_{\infty}(g,h)<\varepsilon
.
Choose
\rho\in{\mathcal{R}}
such that
|g\circ\rho-h|_{\infty}<\varepsilon

and
|\rho-\mathrm{Id}|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
.
Let
\Pi=\{t_{1},t_{2}\ldots\}\subset[0,1]
be the set of
discontinuities of the triple
(g,g\circ\rho,h)

All
\delta
-extensions taken below are with respect to the set

.

Let
|\Gamma|
denote the operator norm of
\Gamma
and

|\Phi|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{S}}^{e-1},s\in[0,1]}|\Phi(x)(s)|
.
Choose

such that

|\Phi(x)(s)-\Phi(y)(s)|\leq L|x-y|
.
For

|G(g\circ\rho)^{\delta}-G(h)^{\delta}|_{\infty}\leq|\Gamma|\varepsilon+8\varepsilon|\Phi|_{\infty}+8\varepsilon L.

(6.1)

Note also that

\sigma_{\infty}(G(g)^{\delta},G(h)^{\delta})\leq|\Gamma|\varepsilon+8\varepsilon|\Phi|_{\infty}+8\varepsilon L+\varepsilon+\delta

Turning to the verification of (
6.1
),
let

and set

,

Let

(6.2)

Consider now

Let

We have the basic estimate

\sup_{t\in[c_{j},d_{j})}|a^{\delta}(t)-b^{\delta}(t)|\leq|\Gamma(\tilde{g}(t_{j})-h(t_{j}))|+2\Delta|\Phi|_{\infty}\leq|\Gamma|\varepsilon+2\Delta|\Phi|_{\infty}.

(6.3)

In addition, we note that

|\Delta_{g}-\Delta_{h}|<2\varepsilon
, so if

\Big|\frac{\Delta_{g}}{|\Delta_{g}|}-\frac{\Delta_{h}}{|\Delta_{h}|}\Big|\leq 2|\Delta_{g}-\Delta_{h}|/|\Delta_{g}|<4\varepsilon/(\Delta-2\varepsilon)<8\varepsilon/\Delta.

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[c_{j},d_{j})}|a^{\delta}(t)-b^{\delta}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq|\Gamma(\tilde{g}(t_{j}^{-})-h(t_{j}^{-}))|+|\Delta_{g}-\Delta_{h}||\Phi|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\qquad+|\Delta_{h}|{\textstyle\sup_{s\in[0,1]}}|\Phi(\Delta_{g}/|\Delta_{g}|)(s)-\Phi(\Delta_{h}/|\Delta_{h}|)(s)|

\displaystyle\leq|\Gamma|\varepsilon+2\varepsilon|\Phi|_{\infty}+8\varepsilon L.

(6.4)

Applying
(
6.3
) for

and (

) for

, we obtain

\sup_{t\in I_{j}}|a^{\delta}(t)-b^{\delta}(t)|\leq|\Gamma|\varepsilon+8\varepsilon|\Phi|_{\infty}+8\varepsilon L.

This combined with (

the required estimate (

)
∎

x\in D_{k}
by the assumption that
D_{k}
is closed.
Then by continuity of
H
on
D_{k}
and lower semicontinuity of
f
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}H_{k}(x_{n})=\lim_{m\to\infty}H_{k}(x_{n_{m}})=\lim_{m\to\infty}f\circ H(x_{n_{m}})\geq f\circ H(x)=H_{k}(x).

The claim is proved.

Next, by the Portmanteau lemma,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\E[H_{k}(X_{n})]\geq\E[H_{k}(X)].

Moreover,
H_{k}
is decreasing in
k
with
H_{k}\to f\circ H
pointwise, and therefore

\lim_{k\to\infty}\E[H_{k}(X)]=\E[f\circ H(X)].

On the other hand,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{n}\bigl(\E[H_{k}(X_{n})]-\E[f\circ H(X_{n})]\bigr)=0

by the assumption that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{n}\P(X_{n}\notin D_{k})=0
.
Combining these facts, we obtain

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\E[f\circ H(X_{n})]\geq\E[f\circ H(X)].

Since this holds for all bounded lower semicontinuous functions
f
,
it follows that
H(X_{n})\to_{w}H(X)
in
F
by the Portmanteau lemma.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2.10
.
