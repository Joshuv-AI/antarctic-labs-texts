Optimal form of time-local non-Lindblad master equations
Tobias Becker, André Eckardt

Abstract
Time-local quantum master equations that describe open quantum systems beyond the limit of ultraweak system-bath coupling are often not of Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) form. Prominent examples are the Redfield equation approximating general open quantum systems and the Hu-Paz-Zhang equation exactly describing a damped harmonic oscillator. Here, we show that not only the former, but also the latter can be brought to pseudo-Lindblad form, with a dissipator that resembles that of a GKSL equation, except for the fact that some of the terms have negative weights. Moreover, we systematically investigate transformations that leave the dissipator of pseudo-Lindblad equations unchanged, while changing the relative weight between its positive and negative terms. These can be used to minimize the weights of the negative terms, which is optimal both for the convergence of a recently developed quantum-trajectory unraveling of pseudo-Lindblad equations as well as for the truncation of the negative terms to obtain a GKSL equation.



I
Introduction

In many circumstances the coupling of a quantum system to its environment matters and has to be taken into account for predicting its transient or steady state.
At the same time, we are often not able to fully describe the state of the environment.
Unless the system is in thermal equilibrium, and thus captured by statistical mechanics
Campisi et al. 2009
;
Talkner and Hänggi 2020
;
Łobejko et al. 2022
;
Timofeev and Trushechkin 2022
;
Trushechkin et al. 2022
, we, therefore, need to resort to effective descriptions of the system that take the impact of the environment into account at least on an approximate level.
This can be done with the help of quantum master equations for the reduced density operator of the system
Bloch 1957
;
Redfield 1965
;
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Lindblad 1976
;
Cohen-Tannoudji et al. 1998
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
;
Walls and Milburn 2008
;
Hu et al. 1992
;
Karrlein and Grabert 1997
;
Paz 1994
;
Grabert et al. 1984
.
For ultraweak system-bath coupling (being small compared to the energy level splitting of the system), one obtains the quantum-optical master equation, which is of Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) form
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Lindblad 1976
;
Cohen-Tannoudji et al. 1998
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
;
Walls and Milburn 2008
.
This form brings many advantages: it is time local, induces a dynamics that is completely positive and trace preserving, and it can efficiently be unravelled using stochastic quantum trajectory simulations
Dalibard et al. 1992
;
Mølmer et al. 1993
;
Gardiner et al. 1992
;
Dum et al. 1992
;
Gisin and Percival 1992
;
Carmichael 1999
;
Daley 2014
.
However, the assumption of ultraweak system-bath coupling is often not fulfilled, especially for large quantum systems, where the level splitting tends to decrease with system size
Munro and Gardiner 1996
;
Wichterich et al. 2007
;
Vrajitoarea et al. 2022
.
Beyond ultraweak coupling one still finds time-local master equations, like the Redfield equation, but these are mostly not of GKSL form anymore.
However, often these equations can still be brought to pseudo-Lindblad form, with a dissipator that resembles that of a GKSL equation, except for the fact that some terms of the dissipator possess negative prefactors
Shaji and Sudarshan 2005
;
Piilo et al. 2009
;
de Vega and Alonso 2017
;
Gneiting 2020
;
Becker et al. 2021
;
Groszkowski et al. 2023
.
While pseudo-Lindblad equations can, in principle, violate the positivity of the density operator, when applied outside their regime of validity
Romero-Rochin and Oppenheim 1989
;
Suárez et al. 1992
;
Pechukas 1994
;
Geva et al. 2000
;
Montoya-Castillo et al. 2015
;
Hartmann and Strunz 2020
, it was recently shown that they still allow for an unravelling in terms of quantum-trajectories
Becker et al. 2023
and they can be approximated by a GKSL equation obtained from truncating the negative non-GKSL terms
Becker et al. 2021
.
Both approaches perform better, when the (relative) weights of the negative terms in the dissipator are smaller.

In this paper, we systematically investigate transformations that leave the generator of pseudo-Lindblad equations unchanged, while changing the absolute and relative weights of their positive and negative dissipation terms.
In particular, these transformations allow for bringing the equation into an optimal form with the negative dissipation terms having minimal weight.
We then apply them to the Redfield equation, which can be brought to pseudo-Lindblad form
Becker et al. 2021
, and show that the minimal weight of the negative dissipation term previously found in Ref.
Becker et al. 2021
, indeed, corresponds to the smallest possible value.
We also determine the pseudo-Lindblad form of the Redfield equation in energy representation.
We discuss the role of the negative dissipation terms in the limit of ultraweak coupling.
As another example, we also consider the Hu-Paz-Zhang master equation, which exactly solves a damped harmonic oscillator
Hu et al. 1992
;
Karrlein and Grabert 1997
;
Paz 1994
;
Grabert et al. 1984
.
We show that it can be brought to the same form as the Redfield equation and, therefore, also to pseudo-Lindblad form.

II
Symmetry transformations of pseudo-Lindblad dissipators

Our starting point is the pseudo-Lindblad equation for the density operator
\varrho
of the system (in units, where
\hbar=1
),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho(t)=-\mathrm{i}[H,\varrho(t)]+\mathcal{D}(A_{+})[\varrho(t)]-\mathcal{D}(A_{-})[\varrho(t)].

(1)

It is characterized by a Hamiltonian
H
, describing the coherent evolution, and two dissipation terms given by GKSL superoperators
\mathcal{D}(A)[\varrho(t)]=A\varrho(t)A^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{A^{\dagger}A,\varrho(t)\}
, with prefactors
\pm 1
and respective jump operators

A_{+}
and
A_{-}
. The weights of the dissipation terms are absorbed into the jump operators and all operators might be time dependent.
The motivation for considering the equation with one positive and one negative dissipation term is motivated by the fact that this is the form obtained both from the Redfield equation (see Ref.
Becker et al. 2021
and Sec.
III
below) and the Hu-Path-Zhang master equation (see Sec.
VII
).
Generally, pseudo-Lindblad equations are known to describe non-Markovian dynamics
Piilo et al. 2009
;
de Vega and Alonso 2017
.
In the respective parameter regime where a pseudo-Lindblad equation is valid, it does not lead to (relevant) violations of positivity
Shaji and Sudarshan 2005
.
This is seen both for phenomenological pseudo-Lindblad equations
Megier et al. 2017
as well as for microscopic ones as discussed further below.

It is known that (pseudo-)Lindblad equations are invariant under certain transformations that change the jump operators as well as the Hamiltonian
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
;
Colla and Breuer 2022
.
On the one hand, the separation between the coherent part, as it is described by the Hamiltonian
H
, and the dissipative part, characterized by the jump operators, is not unique and can be chosen to minimize the dissipation term
Colla and Breuer 2022
. In this paper, we focus on another possible transformation that modifies the dissipator alone. It is mentioned (but not exploited) in Ref.
Colla and Breuer 2022
.
Here, we construct it explicitly and use it to address a different problem, namely the possibility of reducing the weight of the negative relaxation term. As mentioned above, minimizing this weight is optimal for quantum-trajectory simulations
Becker et al. 2023
and GKSL approximations based on truncating the negative term
Becker et al. 2021
.

As a first step, we write the dissipation term as a bilinear form

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}[\varrho(t)]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}(A_{+})[\varrho(t)]-\mathcal{D}(A_{-})[\varrho(t)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=+,-}\sigma^{z}_{ij}\Bigg[A_{i}\varrho(t)A_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\Big\{A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{i},\varrho(t)\Big\}\Bigg],

(2)

with
\sigma^{z}_{ij}
being the elements of the Pauli-z-matrix.
In the form above, where two operators
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
act in this particular form on the density matrix, the coefficient matrix is referred to as Kossakowski matrix
Gorini et al. 1976
.
Here, the Kossakowski matrix is diagonal, but, in the next section, we generalize the concept to non-diagonal Kossakowski matrices.

We are interested in symmetry transformations
W\in\mathbb{C}^{2,2}
that leave the Kossakowski matrix invariant,

\displaystyle\sigma^{z}=W\sigma^{z}W^{\dagger}.

(3)

This defines a reference frame, with a different pair of positive and negative jump operators, i.e.,

\displaystyle(A_{+},A_{-})\rightarrow(A_{+},A_{-})W\equiv(A^{W}_{+},A^{W}_{-}),

(4)

which is a product of the row vector
(A_{+},A_{-})
and the matrix
W
.
Here, the Kossakowski matrix is diagonal with the entries
1
and
-1
, and, consequently,
Eq.

3
is the definition of a two dimensional pseudo-unitary matrix
W
.

For the components,

\displaystyle W=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}W_{11}&W_{12}\\
W_{21}&W_{22}\\
\end{array}\right),

the symmetry condition
\sigma^{z}=W\sigma^{z}W^{\dagger}
yields

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=|W_{11}|^{2}-|W_{12}|^{2},

(7)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=|W_{22}|^{2}-|W_{21}|^{2},

(8)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=W_{21}W_{11}^{*}-W_{22}W_{12}^{*},

(9)

which fixes four out of eight real entries in
W
.
Note that the latter identity gives two constraints, i.e., one condition from the real part and another condition from the imaginary part.
The first two conditions are satisfied by the parametrization

\displaystyle W=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{arg}(W_{11})}\cosh(w_1)&e^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{arg}(W_{12})}\sinh(w_1)\\
e^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{arg}(W_{21})}\sinh(w_2)&e^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{arg}(W_{22})}\cosh(w_2)\\
\end{array}\right),

where
\mathrm{arg}(W_{ij})
denotes the complex phase for the entry
W_{ij}=|W_{ij}|e^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{arg}(W_{ij})}
and where
w_{i}\geq 0
.
Further, from the third condition in
Eq.

9
, by taking the absolute value, it follows that
|W_{21}|/|W_{22}|=|W_{12}|/|W_{11}|
, which is satisfied for
w=w_{1}=w_{2}
.
As a final step, out of the four complex phases one complex phase is fixed, since
Eq.

9
gives

\mathrm{arg}(W_{11})+\mathrm{arg}(W_{22})-\mathrm{arg}(W_{12})-\mathrm{arg}(W_{21})=2\pi n
with integer
n
. All in all, we obtain the the most general parametrization of the symmetry transformation
W

\displaystyle W=e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi+\beta}{2}}\cosh(w)&e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi-\beta}{2}}\sinh(w)\\
e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi-\beta}{2}}\sinh(w)&e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi+\beta}{2}}\cosh(w)\\
\end{array}\right),

with four real parameters given by the argument
w\geq 0
of the hyperbolic functions and three complex phases
\varphi,\alpha
, and
\beta
.

By carrying out the matrix product (
4
)
we obtain a class of equivalent pseudo-Lindblad representations,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}[\varrho]=\mathcal{D}(A^{W}_{+}))[\varrho(t)]-\mathcal{D}(A^{W}_{-})[\varrho(t)],

(14)

with jump operators

\displaystyle A^{W}_{+}

\displaystyle=e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha+\mathrm{i}\frac{\beta}{2}}\left(e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\cosh(w)A_{+}+e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\sinh(w)A_{-}\right),

(15)

\displaystyle A^{W}_{-}

\displaystyle=e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha-\mathrm{i}\frac{\beta}{2}}\left(e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\sinh(w)A_{+}+e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\cosh(w)A_{-}\right),

(16)

for the positive and negative relaxation weight, respectively.
As a consequence of
Section

II
, and as can be verified easily, one has
A^{W}_{+}A^{W\dagger}_{+}-A^{W}_{-}A^{W\dagger}_{-}=A_{+}A^{\dagger}_{+}-A_{-}A^{\dagger}_{-}
, which is invariant under the transformation.
It is evident that the global phases of the jump operators drop out of the dissipators, so that we can choose the phases
\alpha
and
\beta
to be zero. Hence, they can be omitted, such that there are only two relevant symmetry parameters
w
and
\varphi
.

\displaystyle A_{+}^{(w,\varphi)}

\displaystyle=\left(e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\cosh(w)A_{+}+e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\sinh(w)A_{-}\right),

(17)

\displaystyle A_{-}^{(w,\varphi)}

\displaystyle=\left(e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\sinh(w)A_{+}+e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\cosh(w)A_{-}\right),

(18)

Using the freedom to choose the symmetry parameters, we can minimize the weight of the negative jump operator. In the following, we will do that explicitly both for the Redfield equation and the Hu-Paz-Zhang master equation.

III
Application to the Redfield equation

In this section we apply the symmetry transformation to the Redfield equation.
Based on the total system-bath Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{tot}}=H_{\mathrm{S}}+H_{\mathrm{SB}}+H_{\mathrm{B}}
, for weak system-bath coupling
H_{\mathrm{SB}}=S\otimes(B^{\dagger}+B)
, one obtains the Redfield equation for the reduced dynamics of the system.
It can be written in the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho(t)=-\mathrm{i}[H_{\mathrm{S}}+H_{\mathrm{LS}},\varrho(t)]+\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Red}}[\varrho(t)],

(19)

with Lamb-shift Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{LS}}
[
Appendix

A
] and Redfield dissipator

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Red}}[\varrho(t)]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\sigma^{x}_{ij}\Bigg[S_{i}\varrho(t)S_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\Big\{S_{j}^{\dagger}S_{i},\varrho(t)\Big\}\Bigg],

(20)

with non-diagonal Kossakowski matrix
\sigma^{x}
and operators
S_{1}=S
and
S_{2}=\mathbb{S}
. Here
\mathbb{S}
denotes the the convoluted coupling operator:

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}=\int_{0}^{\infty}C(t)S(-t)dt,

(21)

where the bath correlation function
C(t)=\expectationvalue{ B^\dagger(t) B(0) + B(t) B^\dagger(0)}
defined as the expectation values of the bath operators in the interaction picture see [
Appendix

A
].

By diagonalizing the Kossakowski matrix, one obtains a representation of the Redfield equation with new jump operators that act symmetrically on the state.
We consider the unitary transformation,

\displaystyle U=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&-1\\
1&1\\
\end{array}\right),

which defines a rotated frame in which the Kossakowski matrix becomes diagonal

\displaystyle\sigma^{x}\to U^{\dagger}\sigma^{x}U=\sigma^{z},

(24)

with the new set of jump operators

\displaystyle(S,\mathbb{S})\to(S,\mathbb{S})\,U\equiv(A_{+},A_{-}),

(25)

corresponding to the relaxation weights
1
and
-1
for
A_{+}
and
A_{-}
, respectively.

Here, it is immediately clear that the Kossakowski matrix has one negative eigenvalue and therefore the Redfield equation is not of GKSL form, but instead of pseudo-Lindblad form,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Red}}=\mathcal{D}(A_{+})-\mathcal{D}(A_{-}).

(26)

This equation is of the form of Eq. (
II
), which was the starting point of the previous section. It was obtained without invoking a basis representation. Below, we will explicitly consider the basis of energy eigenstates and analytically solve the eigenvalue problem for the resulting Kossakowski matrix.

The diagonal representation of the Redfield dissipator in pseudo-Lindblad form (
26
) allows to apply the symmetry transformation of the previous section.
Altogether, the transformation is given by a product of the diagonalization
U
and the symmetry transformation
W
,
(S,\mathbb{S})\to(A_{+}^{W},A_{-}^{W})=(S,\mathbb{S})\,UW
.
By carrying out the matrix product the new jump operators are

\displaystyle A_{+}^{(w,\varphi)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(

\displaystyle[e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\cosh(w)-e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\sinh(w)]S

(27)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle[e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\cosh(w)+e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\sinh(w)]\mathbb{S}\Big),

\displaystyle A_{-}^{(w,\varphi)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(-

\displaystyle[e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\cosh(w)-e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\sinh(w)]^{*}S

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle[e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\cosh(w)+e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi}{2}}\sinh(w)]^{*}\mathbb{S}\Big).

(28)

Using a different parametrisation, i.e.,
\cosh(w)-\sinh(w)=\lambda
and
\cosh(w)+\sinh(w)=1/\lambda
, one arrives at the equivalent representation

\displaystyle A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{\sigma}=A^{(w,\varphi)}_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\cos\varphi}}\Big[\sigma\lambda e^{\mathrm{i}\sigma\frac{\varphi}{2}}\ S+\frac{1}{\lambda}e^{-\mathrm{i}\sigma\frac{\varphi}{2}}\ \mathbb{S}\Big],

(29)

with
\sigma=+1,-1
.
This form was obtained previously in Ref.
Becker et al. 2021
and is particularly useful for minimizing the weight associated with the jump operator
A_{-}
.
Here, we reproduce this result, however, by considering the most general symmetry transformation and, thus, demonstrate that this is indeed the most general form.

The jump operators
A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{\sigma}
can, equivalently be obtained by first applying a symmetry transformation and then diagonalizing Kossakowski matrix.
This is shown explicitly in
Appendix

C
, where we start from transformations
\Lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{2,2}
that leave the Kossakowski matrix
\sigma^{x}
invariant,

\displaystyle\sigma^{x}=\Lambda\sigma^{x}\Lambda^{\dagger}.

(30)

The new jump operators in
Eq.

29
are then given by
(A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{+},A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{-})=(S,\mathbb{S})\,\Lambda U
.

IV
Minimize the negative weight

In this section we minimize the weight of the jump operator
A_{-}^{(\lambda,\varphi)}
for the Redfield equation by varying the parameters
\lambda
and
\varphi
. The results of this section review previous work described in Ref.
Becker et al. 2021
. Now, however, we know that we indeed find the minimal possible weight, since Eq. (
29
) describes the most general variation of the jump operators.

To begin with, we assign weights to the jump operators
A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{\sigma}
in
Eq.

29
, which we define as the Frobenius norm

\displaystyle\lVert A_{\sigma}^{(\lambda,\varphi)}\rVert^{2}=\tr\left\{A_{\sigma}^{(\lambda,\varphi)}(A_{\sigma}^{(\lambda,\varphi)})^{\dagger}\right\}.

(31)

The weights of the jump operators are then given by

\displaystyle\lVert A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{\sigma}\rVert^{2}=

\displaystyle\sigma\mathrm{Re}\tr(\mathbb SS)-\tan(\varphi)\mathrm{Im}\tr(\mathbb SS)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\cos(\varphi)}\left[\lambda^{2}\lVert S\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\lVert\mathbb{S}\rVert^{2}\right],

(32)

and change under the symmetry transformation
W
.
We define the optimal transformation by the symmetry parameters
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{min}}
that minimize the negative weight
\lVert A_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{min}}\rVert
.

It is interesting to observe that the difference of the positive and the negative weight,

\displaystyle\lVert A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{+}\rVert^{2}-\lVert A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{-}\rVert^{2}=2\mathrm{Re}\tr(\mathbb S S),

(33)

are independent of
\lambda
and
\varphi
and, thus, invariant under the symmetry transformation.
We will see below that it is this weight difference, which determines the rates in the quantum-optical master equation, which is derived from the Redfield equation. As a result, the quantum-optical master equation is also invariant under the transformation (
29
), as expected. Furthermore, the fact that the rate difference (
33
) is fixed, implies that by minimizing the absolute weight
\lVert A_{-}^{(\lambda,\varphi)}\rVert^{2}
with respect to the symmetry transformations
W
, at the same time we minimize also the relative weight of the negative dissipation,
\lVert A_{-}^{(\lambda,\varphi)}\rVert^{2}/\lVert A_{+}^{(\lambda,\varphi)}\rVert^{2}
, as well as the total dissipation weight
\lVert A_{+}^{(\lambda,\varphi)}\rVert^{2}+\lVert A_{-}^{(\lambda,\varphi)}\rVert^{2}
.

Interpreting
\lambda^{2}
and
\varphi
as variational parameters we find the minimal weights
Becker et al. 2021

\displaystyle\lVert A^{\mathrm{min}}_{\sigma}\rVert^{2}=\sigma\mathrm{Re}\tr(\mathbb SS)+\sqrt{\lVert S\rVert^{2}\lVert\mathbb{S}\rVert^{2}-[\mathrm{Im}\tr(\mathbb SS)]^{2}}

(34)

for the optimal symmetry parameters

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert\mathbb{S}\rVert}{\lVert S\rVert},

(35)

\displaystyle\sin\varphi_{\mathrm{min}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{Im}\tr(\mathbb S S)}{\lVert\mathbb{S}\rVert\lVert S\rVert}.

(36)

Let us now evaluate the jump operators and their weights in the energy-eigenbasis of the Hamiltonian,
H|k\rangle=E_{k}|k\rangle
.
For this purpose, we need to evaluate the traces
\lVert S\rVert
,
\tr(\mathbb S S)
, and
\lVert\mathbb{S}\rVert
. One finds matrix elements
\matrixelement{q}{S}{k}=S_{qk}
and
\matrixelement{q}{\mathbb S}{k}=G(\Delta_{qk})S_{qk}
, where
G(\Delta_{qk})
is the coupling density evaluated at the energy level splitting
\Delta_{qk}=E_{q}-E_{k}
see [
Appendix

D
].
For the traces, one obtains
\lVert S\rVert^{2}=\sum_{qk}|S_{qk}|^{2}
,
\tr(\mathbb SS)=\sum_{qk}G(\Delta_{qk})
and
\lVert\mathbb{S}\rVert^{2}=\sum_{qk}|S_{qk}|^{2}|G(\Delta_{qk})|^{2}
.
Here, it is handy to introduce the average

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{x}_{S}\equiv\frac{1}{\lVert S\rVert^{2}}\sum_{qk}|S_{nm}|^{2}x(\Delta_{nm}),

(37)

such that
\tr(\mathbb SS)=\lVert S\rVert^{2}\expectationvalue{G}_{S}
and
\lVert\mathbb{S}\rVert^{2}=\lVert S\rVert^{2}\expectationvalue{|G|^2}_{S}
.
The optimal parameters are
\lambda^{2}_{\mathrm{min}}=\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}_{S}}
and
\sin\varphi_{\mathrm{min}}=\expectationvalue{G''}_{S}/\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}_{S}}
and the minimal weights can be written as

\displaystyle\lVert A^{\mathrm{min}}_{\sigma}\rVert^{2}=\lVert S\rVert^{2}\expectationvalue{G'}_{S}\Big(\sigma+\sqrt{1+\frac{V[G^{\prime}]_{S}+V[G^{\prime\prime}]_{S}}{\expectationvalue{G'}_{S}^{2}}}\Big),

(38)

where we introduce the variance
V[x]_{S}\equiv\expectationvalue{x^2}_{S}-\expectationvalue{x}_{S}^{2}
.

In summary, we find model independent expressions for the optimal weights, Eq. (
34
), and the corresponding transformation parameters,
Eqs.

35
and
36
, and we can express them also in the energy basis. These are found to minimize the absolute and relative weight of
A_{-}
as well as of the total dissipation with respect to the symmetry transformations
W
.

V
Diagonalization of the Redfield dissipator in energy representation

In this section (as well as in Appendix
B
), we derive a pseudo-Lindblad form for the Redfield equation using a completely different approach. Namely, we analytically diagonalize the Kossakowski matrix of the Redfield equation in energy representation using a unitary transformation.

Usually, the Redfield equation is represented in the energy eigenbasis of the system, with eigenstate
\ket{q}
and corresponding energy
E_{q}
, with
q=1,\dots,D
.
The eigenbasis representation is relevant for the numerical implementation of the Redfield equation.
Furthermore, it is the basis in which the rotating-wave approximation is introduced to derive the quantum-optical master equation
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
;
Weiss 2012
.
In the energy basis, the Redfield dissipator takes the form [see
Appendix

B
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Red}}[\varrho(t)]=\sum\limits_{qk,q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle S_{qk}S_{k^{\prime}q^{\prime}}M_{(qk),(q^{\prime}k^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\Big[L_{qk}\varrho(t)L_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\Big\{L_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}L_{qk},\varrho(t)\Big\}\Big],

(39)

with the Hermitian Kossakowski matrix
M
,

\displaystyle M_{(qk),(q^{\prime}k^{\prime})}=G(\Delta_{qk})+G(\Delta_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}})^{*},

(40)

where
G(\Delta)
is again the complex coupling density.
As a novel result, we analytically solve the eigenvalue problem
Mv^{\sigma}=g_{\sigma}v^{\sigma}
of the Kossakowski matrix in Appendix
B
. In this way, we find a diagonal representation of the Redfield dissipator

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Red}}=\mathcal{D}(A_{+}^{0})-\mathcal{D}(A_{-}^{0}),

(41)

with jump operators

\displaystyle A_{\sigma}^{0}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{qk}\sqrt{|g_{\sigma}|}\frac{v^{\sigma}_{qk}}{\lvert v^{\sigma}\rvert}S_{qk}\outerproduct{q}{k},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\cos\varphi_{0}}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}e^{\mathrm{i}\sigma\frac{\varphi_{0}}{2}}\mathbb{S}+\sigma\lambda e^{-\mathrm{i}\sigma\frac{\varphi_{0}}{2}}S\right).

(42)

Qualitatively, the jump operators can be written in the general form of
Eq.

29
, however, with parameters

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}},

(43)

\displaystyle\sin\varphi_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\expectationvalue{G''}}{\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}}}.

(44)

In difference to the optimal parameters in
Eqs.

43
and
44
, here, the average is defined as
\expectationvalue{x}\equiv\frac{1}{D^{2}}\sum_{qk}x(\Delta_{qk})
, which is independent of the coupling operator
S
.
Therefore the parameters are not optimized for the coupling operator
S
.
The weights associated with these jump operators are of the same form as in
Eq.

38
, but with the optimal average
\expectationvalue{\cdot}_{S}
replaced by the bare average
\expectationvalue{\cdot}
[cf.
Appendix

B
].

VI
Ultraweak coupling limit and the relation to detailed balance

For ultraweak system-bath coupling, the Redfield equation can be reduced to the quantum-optical master equation
Carmichael 1999
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}R^{\mathrm{RWA}}[\varrho(t)]=&-\mathrm{i}[H_{\mathrm{LS}}^{\mathrm{RWA}},\varrho(t)]+\sum\limits_{qk}R_{kq}\mathcal{D}(L_{qk})[\varrho(t)],\end{split}

(45)

by performing the rotating-wave approximation.
Here,
L_{qk}=\outerproduct{q}{k}
and
R_{kq}=2\,G^{\prime}(\Delta_{qk})|S_{qk}|^{2}
are the jump operators and the associated weights, respectively.
Moreover, the Lamb-shift Hamiltonian reads
H_{\mathrm{LS}}^{\mathrm{RWA}}=\sum_{qk}G^{\prime\prime}(\Delta_{qk})|S_{qk}|^{2}\outerproduct{k}{k}
.
Since the weights are positive, the quantum-optical master equation is of GKSL form and the the quantity
R_{kq}=2\,G^{\prime}(\Delta_{qk})|S_{qk}|^{2}
can be understood as the rate for quantum jumps from state
\ket{q}
to
\ket{k}
.

For ultraweak coupling, the dynamics for the off-diagonal elements of the density matrix (coherences) decouples from the diagonal elements (populations).
The coherences decay exponentially and the populations
p_{q}(t)=\matrixelement{q}{\varrho(t)}{q}
obey a Pauli-type classical rate equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{q}(t)=\sum_{k}[R_{qk}p_{k}(t)-R_{qk}p_{q}(t)].

(46)

In equilibrium
\partial_{t}p_{q}(t)=0
,
Eq.

46
simplifies by noting
R_{kq}/R_{qk}=e^{-\beta\Delta_{qk}}
, which is known as the detailed balance condition, for which all terms vanish separately.
Then the steady state is the canonical Gibbs state,
p^{(0)}_{q}=e^{-\beta E_{q}}/\sum_{k}(e^{-\beta E_{k}})
, which is known to be the exact solution in the limit of ultraweak coupling
Campisi et al. 2009
;
Talkner and Hänggi 2020
.

Let us now show that both the negative and positive dissipation term in the pseudo-Lindblad representation of the Redfield dissipator are crucial to obtain the detailed balance condition and with that the correct thermal steady state.
Starting from the steady-state solution of the Redfield equation

\displaystyle 0=-\mathrm{i}[H_{\mathrm{S}}+H_{\mathrm{LS}},\varrho]+\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Red}}[\varrho],

(47)

we make a series expansion for the steady state up to second order in the system-bath coupling,
\varrho\simeq\varrho^{(0)}+\varrho^{(2)}
.
Evaluating
Eq.

47
for each order separately, one obtains the hierarchy of coupled equations,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}[H_{\mathrm{S}},\varrho^{(0)}],

(48)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}[H_{\mathrm{S}},\varrho^{(2)}]-\mathrm{i}[H_{\mathrm{LS}},\varrho^{(0)}]+\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Red}}[\varrho^{(0)}],

(49)

where we have used that both the Lamb-shift and the dissipator are of second order with respect to the coupling.
From
Eq.

48
, it follows that in zeroth order, the equilibrium state is diagonal in the energy basis, i.e.,
\matrixelement{q}{\varrho^{(0)}}{k}=\delta_{q,k}p_{q}^{(0)}
.
The populations follow from
Eq.

49
in second order.
We project
Eq.

49
to the populations and use that the commutator with a diagonal matrix is zero on the diagonal, such that
\matrixelement{q}{[H_\mathrm{LS},\varrho^{(2)}]}{q}=\matrixelement{q}{[H_\mathrm{S},\varrho^{(0)}]}{q}=0
and find

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\matrixelement{q}{\mathcal D^\mathrm{Red}[\dyad{k}{k}]}{q}\,p_{k}^{(0)}.

(50)

We now represent the Redfield dissipator in pseudo-Lindblad form,
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Red}}=\mathcal{D}(A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{+})-\mathcal{D}(A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{-})
.
Analogous to the quantum-optical master equation we find a rate equation

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{k}\Big(

\displaystyle(|A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{+,{qk}}|^{2}-|A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{-,qk}|^{2})p_{k}^{(0)}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle(|A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{+,kq}|^{2}-|A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{-,kq}|^{2})p_{q}^{(0)}\Big),

(51)

with rates given by the absolute squared matrix elements of the jump operators
|A^{W}_{\sigma,qk}|^{2}=|\matrixelement{q}{A^W_\sigma}{k}|^{2}
.
We see that the difference of the weights enters, which is independent of the symmetry transformation
W
. Thus, we can unambiguously identify the rates as

\displaystyle R_{qk}=|A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{+,{qk}}|^{2}-|A^{(\lambda,\varphi)}_{-,qk}|^{2},

(52)

which can be reduced to the well-known Golden-rule type expression, giving rise to the detailed-balance condition.

Another approximation of the Redfield equation giving rise to a GKSL master equation was discussed in Ref.
Becker et al. 2021
.
It is based on truncating the negative contribution of the Redfield equation in pseudo-Lindblad form.
We find that this is valid beyond the ultraweak coupling limit, where especially for large bath temperatures and systems driven out of equilibrium it performs better than the quantum-optical master equation obtained from the rotating-wave approximation.
However, as is also shown in Ref.
Lee and Yeo 2022
, the truncation of the negative rates eventually breaks the detailed balance condition and, consequently, the zeroth-order result differs from the canonical Gibbs state and, thus, does not provide the correct result in the limit of ultraweak coupling.
Furthermore, the equilibrium populations become a complicated function of the symmetry parameters
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{min}}
, the coupling operator
S
, as well as the complex coupling density
G(\Delta)
.
All this might be seen as a drawback of the truncation approximation and has to be considered for consistent results in the ultraweak coupling regime.
However, it also shows the relevance of the negative contribution.
In particular, here, for ultraweak coupling it is shown that negative relaxation weights do not violate the positivity of the population, but on the contrary are essential for a consistent weak coupling limit.
Thus, developing efficient methods to treat pseudo-Lindblad equations, including the negative terms are crucial.
One example is the the pseudo-Lindblad quantum-trajectory approach
Becker et al. 2023
, which is found to become more efficient for smaller weights of the negative dissipation term.

VII
Pseudo-Lindblad representation of the exact Hu-Paz-Zhang master equation

The damped harmonic oscillator with mass
M
and frequency
\Omega
is a paradigmatic example of an open quantum system, as it is one of the few exactly solvable open systems
Hu et al. 1992
;
Karrlein and Grabert 1997
;
Paz 1994
;
Grabert et al. 1984
.
The total system-bath Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{tot}}=

\displaystyle\frac{p^{2}}{2M}+\frac{M\Omega^{2}}{2}q^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k}^{\infty}\Bigg[\frac{p_{k}^{2}}{2m_{k}}+\frac{m_{k}\omega_{k}^{2}}{2}\Big(q_{k}-\frac{c_{k}}{m_{k}\omega_{k}^{2}}q\Big)^{2}\Bigg],

(53)

with displacement
q
and momentum
p
of the central oscillator.
The coupling between system and bath is of the form

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{SB}}=q\otimes B,

(54)

with bath operator
B=\sum_{k}^{\infty}-c_{k}q_{k}
, where the coefficients
c_{k}
are spring constants that determine the coupling strength between the individual bath modes and the system.
Yet, there is another term arising from the total system-bath Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{RN}}=\sum_{k}^{\infty}\frac{c_{k}^{2}}{2m_{k}\omega_{k}^{2}}\,q^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega}\frac{d\omega}{\pi}\,q^{2}

(55)

with spectral density,
J(\omega)=\pi\sum_{k}\frac{c_{k}^{2}}{2m_{k}\omega_{k}}\delta(\omega-\omega_{k})
.
The additional term in the Hamiltonian is a potential renormalization and cancels the frequency shift caused by the reorganization energy
G_{\mathrm{RN}}^{\prime\prime}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega}\frac{d\omega}{\pi}
[
Appendix

D
].

In the high-temperature regime, the reduced dynamics of the damped harmonic oscillator is described by the equation of Brownian motion, which is a GKSL master equation
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
.
In this section, we make the connection between the exact Hu-Paz-Zhang master equation and the pseudo-Lindblad equation, which depends on the parameters
\lambda
and
\varphi
as they were discussed in the previous sections.

The Hu-Paz-Zhang master equation
Hu et al. 1992
;
Karrlein and Grabert 1997
, also known as Caldeira-Leggett master equation
Caldeira and Leggett 1983
, reads

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\varrho(t)=&-\mathrm{i}\bigg[\frac{p^{2}}{2M}+\frac{M\gamma_{q}(t)}{2}q^{2},\varrho(t)\bigg]\\
&-M^{2}D_{p}(t)[q,[q,\varrho(t)]]-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\gamma_{p}(t)[q,\{p,\varrho(t)\}]\\
&+MD_{q}(t)[q,[p,\varrho(t)]].\end{aligned}

(56)

with time-dependent coefficients
\gamma_{q}(t)
,
\gamma_{p}(t)
,
D_{q}(t)
,
D_{p}(t)
, which are specified, e.g., in Ref.
Karrlein and Grabert 1997
. We do not explicitly reproduce the expressions for the coefficients here, since their precise form is lengthy and not relevant in the following.
The potential renormalization
\gamma_{q}(t)
and the dissipation rate
\gamma_{p}(t)
follow from the classical equation of motion for the damped harmonic oscillator
Grabert et al. 1984
.
The diffusion coefficients
D_{q}(t)
and
D_{p}(t)
follow from fluctuating forces in the bath and are temperature dependent
Grabert et al. 1984
.
The diffusion coefficients
D_{q}(t)
,
D_{p}(t)
are related to the
\gamma_{q}(t)
,
\gamma_{p}(t)
via the quantum fluctuation-dissipation theorem
Grabert et al. 1988
.

Algebraically, the Hu-Paz-Zhang master equation is quadratic in the operators
q
and
p
, which are the displacement operator and its canonically associated momentum
p
.
In addition, the master equation is hermiticity- and trace-preserving and, thus, it can be brought to Redfield form.
Let us now bring
Eq.

56
into the equivalent form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\bigg[\frac{p^{2}}{2M}+\frac{M\gamma_{q}(t)}{2}q^{2},\ \varrho(t)\bigg]

\displaystyle+\Bigg(\mathbb{S}^{\mathrm{HPZ}}\varrho(t)\,q-q\,\mathbb{S}^{\mathrm{HPZ}}\varrho(t)+\mathrm{H.c.}\Bigg),

(57)

with

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{\mathrm{HPZ}}=M^{2}D_{p}(t)\,q+\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{\gamma_{p}(t)}{2}-MD_{q}(t)\right)p.

(58)

Since the coupling operator is given by
S=q
, we can now see that the Hu-Paz-Zhang equation as written in
Eq.

57
has exactly the form of the Redfield equation, except for the fact that
\mathbb{S}^{\mathrm{HPZ}}
is not given by the convoluted coupling operator [
Eq.

21
], but rather by
Eq.

58
.

Since we have seen that the Hu-Paz-Zhang master equation is of Redfield form, we can now express it also in pseudo-Lindblad form by using the results of the previous sections.
The Lamb-shift Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H^{\mathrm{HPZ}}_{\mathrm{LS}}

\displaystyle=\frac{q\mathbb{S}^{\mathrm{HPZ}}-\mathbb{S}^{\mathrm{HPZ}\dagger}q}{2\mathrm{i}}=\frac{\gamma_{p}(t)}{4}\big\{q,p\big\}-\frac{MD_{q}(t)}{2\mathrm{i}}[q,p],

(59)

where due to
[q,p]=\mathrm{i}\mathds{1}
, the diffusion parameter
D_{q}(t)
gives a constant contribution and might be absorbed in an overall energy shift.
The jump operators are given by
Eq.

29
,

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{HPZ}}_{\sigma}=\frac{1/\lambda}{\sqrt{2\cos\varphi}}

\displaystyle\Big[\left(\sigma\lambda^{2}e^{-\mathrm{i}\sigma\varphi}+M^{2}D_{p}(t)\right)q

\displaystyle+\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{\gamma_{p}(t)}{2}-MD_{q}(t)\right)p\Big],

(60)

where the overall phase factor
e^{\mathrm{i}\sigma\frac{\varphi}{2}}
is omitted.

In Ref.
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
, a GKSL generator for the damped harmonic oscillator is found by adding a term to the Hu-Paz-Zhang master equation that is small in the high-temperature limit. Here, in the pseudo-Lindblad representation, we arrive at the same generator by choosing

\displaystyle\varphi=0,\qquad\lambda^{2}=M^{2}D_{p}(t),

(61)

and then truncating the negative contribution.
For this choice the first term in
A^{\mathrm{HPZ}}_{-}
cancels.
We now show that this choice actually corresponds to an optimal truncation and generalize it to arbitrary parameter regimes.

In the asymptotic limit with static coefficients, in the high-temperature regime, for weak coupling and large cutoff frequency, one obtains

\displaystyle\gamma_{q}

\displaystyle\simeq\Omega^{2},

(62)

\displaystyle\gamma_{p}

\displaystyle\simeq\gamma,

(63)

\displaystyle D_{q}

\displaystyle\simeq 0,

(64)

\displaystyle D_{p}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\gamma}{M\beta},

(65)

with coupling rate
\gamma
and inverse bath temperature
\beta
.
This is often referred to as quantum Brownian motion or the classical limit in Ref.
Karrlein and Grabert 1997
.
With these coefficients one arrives at the result of Ref.
Breuer and Petruccione 2007

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{HPZ}}_{+}\simeq\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\Bigg[\sqrt{\frac{4M}{\beta}}q+\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{4M}}p\Bigg].

(66)

At small, but finite inverse temperatures
\beta
, the truncated negative jump operator reads

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{HPZ}}_{-}\simeq\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{4M}}p.

(67)

Using the results obtained above, we can now show that the parameters (
61
), which describe the result of Ref.
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
, become optimal in the high-temperature limit of Brownian motion.
Moreover, we can generalize (or improve) the result of Ref.
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
by minimizing
\lVert A_{-}^{\mathrm{HPZ}}\rVert
also beyond this limit.
In principle, the Hilbert space of the harmonic oscillator is infinite dimensional, such that the norm of the displacement and momentum are not well-defined.
However, the state space can be truncated at an energy that is large compared to both the initial energy of the system and the temperature of the environment.
As a consequence of the truncation, the commutator of operators is always traceless (as follows immediately from cyclic permutations under the trace
\tr\{AB-BA\}=\tr\{AB-AB\}=0
), which breaks the commutation relation of bosonic operators for states at the truncation energy.
Namely, in a finite dimensional Hilbert space with cutoff
D
, the product of the bosonic operators in the energy basis is,

\displaystyle(aa^{\dagger})_{nm}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&2&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&D-1&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0&0\end{matrix}\right),

(68)

and

\displaystyle(a^{\dagger}a)_{nm}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&D-2&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0&D-1\end{matrix}\right),

(69)

so that
[a,a^{\dagger}]\neq 1
, i.e.,

\displaystyle([a,a^{\dagger}])_{nm}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0&1-D\end{matrix}\right)\neq\delta_{nm},

(70)

giving
\tr{[a,a^\dag]}=0
.

For the norm of the jump operators, one then obtains

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lVert{A^{\mathrm{HPZ}}}^{\sigma}\rVert^{2}&=\frac{1/\lambda^{2}}{2\cos\varphi}\Big[\\
&\big(\lambda^{4}+\sigma\lambda^{2}M^{2}D_{p}(t)2\cos\varphi+M^{4}D_{p}(t)^{2}\big)\lVert q\rVert^{2}\\
&+\left(\frac{\gamma_{p}(t)^{2}}{4}+M^{2}D_{q}(t)^{2}\right)\lVert p\rVert^{2}\Big],\end{aligned}

where the mixing term, involving
\tr(qp)=(\mathrm{i}/2)\tr([a,a^\dagger] )=0
, vanishes in a finite dimensional state space.
The latter can be seen from the relations
q=\sqrt{1/2M\Omega}(a^{\dagger}+a)
and
p=\mathrm{i}\sqrt{M\Omega/2}(a^{\dagger}-a)
, which also yield
\lVert p\|^{2}=M^{2}\Omega^{2}\lVert q\rVert^{2}
.

The weight difference of the Hu-Paz-Zhang master equation is given by [
Eq.

33
]

\displaystyle\lVert A^{\mathrm{HPZ}}_{+}\rVert^{2}-\lVert A^{\mathrm{HPZ}}_{-}\rVert^{2}=2M^{2}D_{p}(t)\lVert q\rVert^{2},

(71)

and is invariant under the symmetry parameters.

The optimal rescaling
\lambda^{\mathrm{HPZ}}
and complex phase
\varphi^{\mathrm{HPZ}}
, which minimize the negative contribution, are readily obtained from
Eqs.

35
and
36
,

\displaystyle{\lambda^{\mathrm{HPZ}}(t)}^{2}

\displaystyle=M^{2}D_{p}(t)\sqrt{1+\frac{\Omega^{2}\gamma_{p}(t)^{2}}{4M^{2}D_{p}(t)^{2}}+\Omega^{2}\frac{D_{q}(t)^{2}}{D_{p}(t)^{2}}},

(72)

\displaystyle\sin\varphi^{\mathrm{HPZ}}

\displaystyle\propto\mathrm{Im}\tr( \mathbb S^\mathrm{HPZ} S)=0,

(73)

for which we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert A^{\mathrm{HPZ}}_{\sigma}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=M^{2}D_{p}(t)

\displaystyle\left(\sigma+\sqrt{1+\frac{\Omega^{2}\gamma_{p}(t)^{2}}{4M^{2}D_{p}(t)^{2}}+\Omega^{2}\frac{D_{q}(t)^{2}}{D_{p}(t)^{2}}}\right)\lVert q\rVert^{2}.

(74)

This is a generalization of the result in Ref.
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
for arbitrary
\gamma_{p}(t)
,
D_{q}(t)
,
D_{p}(t)
.
In the limit of Brownian motion it reduces to the result of Ref.
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
, showing that this results corresponds to an optimal truncation.
Beyond that limit, it provides a better truncation.
Exploring the accuracy of this truncation is an interesting question for future research.

VIII
conclusion

Often quantum master equations are used to model the dynamics of open quantum systems.
Beyond ultraweak coupling these master equation are typically not of GKSL form, but instead of pseudo-Lindblad form, which allow for negative relaxation weights.
However, the representation of the pseudo-Lindblad equation is not unique.
Here, we have used symmetry transformations to systematically construct all possible representations of a generic pseudo-Lindblad equation with two jump operators.
Then, we showed that this scenario captures two important master equations, the Redfield equation and the Hu-Paz-Zhang master equation. By applying the symmetry transformations to these two examples, we can analytically find the respective optimal representation, which minimizes the negative dissipator. This minimization is optimal both for the approximation given by the truncation of the negative term (so that the resulting master equation is of GKSL form
Becker et al. 2021
) as well as for the efficiency of a recently proposed quantum trajectory unraveling of pseudo-Lindblad equations
Becker et al. 2023
.
Thus, the results provided here are of immediate use for the efficient simulation of open quantum systems beyond the limit of ultraweak coupling.

Appendix A
Microscopic description

For open quantum systems that are coupled to their environment, an effective description for the reduced dynamics of the system is of great interest. For ultraweak coupling between system and bath, GKSL master equations yield an adequate approximation. Often they are used to describe relaxation, dissipation and decoherence on a phenomenological level
Diehl et al. 2008
;
Lee et al. 2020
;
Reh et al. 2021
;
Denisov et al. 2019
. However away from equilibrium, the properties of open quantum systems depend on the details of their environment. A microscopic derivation of a master equation is therefore crucial.

The microscopic Hamiltonian of the total system-bath compound is denoted as
H_{\mathrm{tot}}=H_{\mathrm{S}}+H_{\mathrm{SB}}+H_{\mathrm{B}}

with
H_{\mathrm{S}}
and
H_{\mathrm{B}}
acting solely on the system and bath, respectively.
System and bath are coupled via the interaction Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{SB}}
acting on the joint Hilbert space of system and bath.
Without loss of generality, the interaction can be written in the canonical form, e.g., by doing a Schmidt decomposition,

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{SB}}=\sum_{\alpha}(S_{\alpha}\otimes B_{\alpha}^{\dagger}+S_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\otimes B_{\alpha}),

(75)

with
S_{\alpha}
referred to as coupling operator solely acting on the system and
B_{\alpha}
being some bath operator.
It is convenient to choose the dimensions of both S and B equal, i.e., the square root of a frequency.
Thereby, multiple coupling operators
S_{\alpha}
for
\alpha=1,2,\dots
are considered, only, for systems driven out of equilibrium by multiple reservoirs, for which we write
H_{\mathrm{B}}=\sum_{\alpha}{H_{\mathrm{B}}}_{\alpha}
.
To keep the notation simple, here we consider one coupling channel with Hermitian
S
in
H_{\mathrm{SB}}=S\otimes(B^{\dagger}+B)
.
Generalizations are easily found by reintroducing the summation over
\alpha
.

We are interested in the effective dynamics of the reduced state of the system by tracing over the bath degrees of freedom, i.e.,
\varrho(t)=\tr_{\mathrm{B}}\varrho_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)
. In a perturbative treatment up to second order in the system-bath coupling, this is widely known as Born-Markov theory, e.g., in Ref.
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
;
Weiss 2012
.
One obtains the time-local quantum master equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho(t)=-\mathrm{i}[H_{\mathrm{S}},\varrho(t)]+\mathcal{R}^{(2)}[\varrho(t)],

(76)

with Redfield superoperator

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{(2)}[\varrho(t)]=S\varrho\mathbb{S}^{\dagger}-S\mathbb{S}\varrho-\varrho\mathbb{S}^{\dagger}S,

(77)

for which we define the (time-dependent) convolution operator

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}=\int_{0}^{t}C(\tau)\,\mathbf{S}(-\tau)\ d\tau,

(78)

which possesses the same dimension as
S
, and bath correlation

\displaystyle C(\tau)=\expectationvalue{ B(\tau) B^\dagger}+\expectationvalue{ B^\dagger(\tau) B},

(79)

with
B(\tau)=e^{\mathrm{i}H_{\mathrm{B}}\tau}Be^{-\mathrm{i}H_{\mathrm{B}}\tau}
.

Further, one completes the latter terms in
Eq.

77
to a commutator and anti-commutator
-S\mathbb{S}\varrho(t)-\varrho(t)\mathbb{S}^{\dagger}S=-\mathrm{i}[(S\mathbb{S}-\mathbb{S}^{\dagger}S)/(2\mathrm{i}),\varrho(t)]-(1/2)\{S\mathbb{S}+\mathbb{S}^{\dagger}S,\varrho(t)\}
to arrive at

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{(2)}[\varrho(t)]

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}[H_{\mathrm{LS}},\varrho(t)]+\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Red}}[\varrho(t)],

(80)

with Lamb-shift Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{LS}}=\frac{S\mathbb{S}-\mathbb{S}^{\dagger}S}{2\mathrm{i}}.

(81)

The dissipative part has the familiar form

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Red}}[\varrho(t)]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\sigma^{x}_{ij}\Bigg[S_{i}\varrho(t)S_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\Big\{S_{j}^{\dagger}S_{i},\varrho(t)\Big\}\Bigg],

(82)

with Kossakowski matrix

\displaystyle\sigma^{x}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}0&\quad 1\\
1&\quad 0\\
\end{array}\right),

given by the Pauli-
x
-matrix and jump operators
S_{1}\equiv S
,
S_{2}\equiv\mathbb{S}
.

Whereas the Lamb-shift is well known for the quantum-optical master equation, i.e., within the rotating-wave approximation, the identification made here for the Redfield equation can be found, e.g., in Refs.
Farina and Giovannetti 2019
;
Timofeev and Trushechkin 2022
.
The Redfield Lamb-shift can be used as the basis for defining thermodynamic properties, such as heat and work beyond ultraweak coupling
Colla and Breuer 2022
. To this end, we remark that the operators in the dissipative part may be transformed by redefining the Lamb-shift.
In reference
Colla and Breuer 2022
a unique choice of the Lamb-shift is proposed by minimizing the dissipator.
In this work, we do not make use of this transformation but solely focus on the dissipative part defined above.

Appendix B
Unitary diagonalization of the Redfield equation in the system’s eigenbasis

We consider the Redfield equation represented in the eigenbasis of the system, i.e.,
H_{\mathrm{S}}\ket{q}=E_{q}\ket{q}

The convolution operator takes the form
\mathbb{S}_{t}=\sum_{qk}G_{t}(\Delta_{qk})S_{qk}L_{qk}
, with jump operators
L_{qk}=\outerproduct{q}{k}
and frequencies
\Delta_{qk}=E_{q}-E_{k}
.
Thereby,

\displaystyle G_{t}(\Delta)\equiv\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Delta\tau}C(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau,

(85)

defines the temperature-dependent coupling density [
Appendix

D
].
In the asymptotic limit
\lim_{t\to\infty}G_{t}(\Delta)=G(\Delta)
it is the half-sided Fourier transform of the bath correlation.

Because the Redfield equation is quadratic in the coupling operator the summation runs over four indices
q,k
and
q^{\prime},k^{\prime}
for the two frequencies
\Delta_{qk}
and
\Delta_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}
and can be brought to the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{(2)}[\varrho(t)]=&-\mathrm{i}\left[\sum_{qk,q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}S_{qk}S_{k^{\prime}q^{\prime}}\frac{G(\Delta_{qk})-G(\Delta_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}})^{*}}{2\mathrm{i}}L_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}L_{qk},\varrho(t)\right]\\
&+\sum\limits_{qk,q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}S_{qk}S_{k^{\prime}q^{\prime}}\left(G(\Delta_{qk})+G(\Delta_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}})^{*}\right)\Big[L_{qk}\varrho(t)L_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\Big\{L_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}L_{qk},\varrho(t)\Big\}\Big],\end{split}

(86)

which is the full Redfield superoperator and includes the Lamb-shift Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{LS}}
in the first line and the dissipator
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{Red}}
in the second line.

In the eigenbasis, the Redfield equation in (
86
) resembles the GKSL structure, except it is not diagonal in the jump operators
S_{qk}L_{qk}
, with
L_{qk}=\outerproduct{q}{k}
.
We obtain a diagonal representation by solving the eigenvalue problem of the
D^{2}
-dimensional, Hermitian coefficient matrix
M
with entries
M_{(qk),(q^{\prime}k^{\prime})}=G(\Delta_{qk})+G(\Delta_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}})^{*}
.
The eigenvalue problem reads

\displaystyle\sum_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}\big(G(\Delta_{qk})+G(\Delta_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}})^{*}\big)\,v^{\sigma}_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}=g_{\sigma}\,v^{\sigma}_{qk},

(87)

with real eigenvalues
g_{\sigma}
and eigenvectors
v^{\sigma}=(v^{\sigma}_{qk})
.

A numerical diagonalization for large systems is very demanding and lacks for a detailed understanding. Therefore, as an unprecedented result, we analytically solve the eigenvalue problem using simple algebraic methods. First, we find a closed quadratic equation for the non-zero eigenvalues
g_{\sigma}
, and secondly use this to construct the eigenvectors
v^{\sigma}
.

Thereby, it is useful to introduce averages of the form,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{x}\equiv\frac{1}{D^{2}}\sum_{qk}x_{qk},

(88)

such as
\expectationvalue{v^\sigma}\equiv\frac{1}{D^{2}}\sum_{qk}v^{\sigma}_{qk}
,
\expectationvalue{G^*v^\sigma}\equiv(1/D^{2})\sum_{qk}G(\Delta_{qk})^{*}v^{\sigma}_{qk}
, etc., and variance
V[x]\equiv\expectationvalue{x^2}-\expectationvalue{x}^{2}
.
As starting point, we formally solve the eigenvalue problem for
v^{\sigma}_{qk}
, assuming
g_{\sigma}\neq 0
,

\displaystyle v^{\sigma}_{qk}=\frac{D^{2}}{g_{\sigma}}\left(G(\Delta_{qk})\,\expectationvalue{v^\sigma}+\expectationvalue{G^*v^\sigma}\right),

(89)

and find it to be affine-linear in
G(\Delta_{qk})
. In the following, on the right-hand side, we get rid of the implicit dependence on the eigenvector
v^{\sigma}
by taking appropriate averages. Namely, by taking the average of
Eq.

89
we find the relation
\expectationvalue{v^\sigma}=\frac{D^{2}}{g_{\sigma}}\left(\expectationvalue{G}\,\expectationvalue{v^\sigma}+\expectationvalue{G^*v^\sigma}\right)
.

Firstly, for the eigenvalue
g_{\sigma}
, we eliminate
\expectationvalue{v^\sigma}=\expectationvalue{G^*v^\sigma}/(\frac{g_{\sigma}}{D^{2}}-\expectationvalue{G})
and arrive at,

\displaystyle v^{\sigma}_{qk}=\frac{D^{2}}{g_{\sigma}}\expectationvalue{G^*v^\sigma}\left(\frac{G(\Delta_{qk})}{\frac{g_{\sigma}}{D^{2}}-\expectationvalue{G}}+1\right).

(90)

Here, in order to cancel
\expectationvalue{G^*v^\sigma}
we multiply by
G(\Delta_{qk})^{*}
and, yet again, take the average. It follows,
1=\frac{D^{2}}{g_{\sigma}}\big(\frac{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}}{\frac{g_{\sigma}}{D^{2}}-\expectationvalue{G}}+\expectationvalue{G^*}\big)
.
This is a quadratic equation for
g_{\sigma}
,
0=(\frac{g_{\sigma}}{D^{2}}-\expectationvalue{G^*})(\frac{g_{\sigma}}{D^{2}}-\expectationvalue{G})-\expectationvalue{|G|^2}
, which has the solution
\frac{g_{\sigma}}{D^{2}}=\expectationvalue{G}+\expectationvalue{G^*}\pm\sqrt{(\expectationvalue{G}+\expectationvalue{G^*})^{2}+\expectationvalue{|G|^2}-|\expectationvalue{G}|^{2}}
. With real and imaginary part, i.e.,
G=G^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}G^{\prime\prime}
, it follows
\frac{g_{\sigma}}{D^{2}}=\expectationvalue{G'}\pm\sqrt{\expectationvalue{G'}^{2}+\expectationvalue{G'^2}-\expectationvalue{G'}^{2}+\expectationvalue{G''^2}-\expectationvalue{G''}^{2}}
, which can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{g_{\sigma}}{D^{2}}=\expectationvalue{G'}\left(1+\sigma\sqrt{1+\frac{V[G^{\prime}]+V[G^{\prime\prime}]}{\expectationvalue{G'}^{2}}}\right),

(91)

with
\sigma=\pm 1
.

Secondly, for the eigenvectors
v^{\sigma}
, we start again from
Eq.

89
and eliminate
\expectationvalue{G^*v^\sigma}=\expectationvalue{v^\sigma}(\frac{g_{\sigma}}{D^{2}}-\expectationvalue{G})
,

\displaystyle v^{\sigma}_{qk}=\frac{D^{2}}{g_{\sigma}}\expectationvalue{v^\sigma}\left(G(\Delta_{qk})+\sigma\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\sigma\varphi_{0}}\right),

(92)

where the second term is given by the complex quantity

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{g_{\sigma}}{D^{2}}-\expectationvalue{G}&=\sigma\sqrt{\expectationvalue{G'}^{2}+V[G^{\prime}]+V[G^{\prime\prime}]}-\mathrm{i}\expectationvalue{G''},\\
&=\sigma\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}-\expectationvalue{G''}^{2}}-\mathrm{i}\expectationvalue{G''},\\
&\equiv\sigma\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\sigma\varphi_{0}},\end{split}

(93)

for which, in the last line,
\varphi_{0}
defines the complex phase, which can be interpreted geometrically in the figure below.
For the complex phase, it is
\tan\varphi_{0}=\frac{\overline{G^{\prime\prime}}}{\sqrt{\overline{|G|^{2}}-\overline{G^{\prime\prime}}^{2}}}
,
\sin\varphi_{0}=\frac{\overline{G^{\prime\prime}}}{\sqrt{\overline{|G|^{2}}}}
,
\cos\varphi_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{\overline{|G|^{2}}-\overline{G^{\prime\prime}}^{2}}}{\sqrt{\overline{|G|^{2}}}}
with
\varphi_{0}\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2})
.

\varphi_{0}
\mathrm{Re}z=\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}-\expectationvalue{G''}^{2}}
\mathrm{Im}z=-\expectationvalue{G''}
|z|=\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}}

Complex quantity

visualized in the complex plane.

As a final step, the vector
v^{\sigma}
is normalized.
The squared norm of the eigenvector is given by

\displaystyle|v^{\sigma}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}|v^{\sigma}_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}}|^{2},

\displaystyle=\frac{D^{4}}{g_{\sigma}^{2}}\lvert{\expectationvalue{v}^{\sigma}}\rvert^{2}\sqrt{\expectationvalue{ |G|^2}}2(\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}}+\sigma\mathrm{Re}[e^{\mathrm{i}\sigma\varphi_{0}}\expectationvalue{G}]),

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\frac{D^{4}}{g_{\sigma}^{2}}\lvert{\expectationvalue{v}^{\sigma}}\rvert^{2}\sqrt{\expectationvalue{ |G|^2}}2\cos\varphi_{0}\\
&\qquad\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}}}{\cos\varphi_{0}}+\sigma\expectationvalue{G'}-\tan\varphi_{0}\,\expectationvalue{G''}\Big).\end{split}

(94)

Using the expressions for the complex phase [see
Fig.

1
] the latter term reduces to
\frac{\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}}}{\cos\varphi_{0}}+\sigma\expectationvalue{G'}-\tan\varphi_{0}\,\expectationvalue{G''}=\sqrt{\expectationvalue{G'^2}+V[G^{\prime}]+V[G^{\prime\prime}]}+\sigma\expectationvalue{G'}=\frac{\sigma g_{\sigma}}{D^{2}}=\frac{|g_{\sigma}|}{D^{2}}
.
We arrive at the normalized eigenvector

\displaystyle\frac{v^{\sigma}_{qk}}{|v^{\sigma}|}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2|g_{\sigma}|\cos\varphi_{0}}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}e^{\mathrm{i}\sigma\frac{\varphi_{0}}{2}}G(\Delta_{qk})+\sigma\lambda_{0}e^{-\mathrm{i}\sigma\frac{\varphi_{0}}{2}}\right),

(95)

for which we neglect the irrelevant phase factor
\frac{\expectationvalue{v^\sigma}}{|\expectationvalue{v^\sigma}|}e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\varphi_{0}}{2}}
and introduce the rescaling parameter
\lambda_{0}^{2}\equiv\sqrt{\expectationvalue{|G|^2}}
to match with the notation of
Eq.

29
.

In summary, by diagonalization of the coefficient matrix,

\displaystyle G(\Delta_{qk})+G(\Delta_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}})^{*}=\sum_{\sigma=+1,-1}g_{\sigma}\frac{v^{\sigma}_{qk}}{\lvert v^{\sigma}\rvert}\frac{{(v^{\sigma}_{q^{\prime}k^{\prime}})}^{*}}{\lvert v^{\sigma}\rvert},

(96)

the Redfield equation (

(97)

and jump operators,

In the following, the symmetry transformation
\Lambda
for the Kossakowski matrix
\sigma^{x}
of the Redfield equation is constructed,
\sigma^{x}=\Lambda\sigma^{x}\Lambda^{\dagger}

Starting with four arbitrary complex entries in
\Lambda

\displaystyle\Lambda=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}\Lambda_{11}&\Lambda_{12}\\
\Lambda_{21}&\Lambda_{22}\\
\end{array}\right),

the symmetry condition yields the three coupled equations

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Re}[\Lambda_{11}\Lambda_{12}^{*}]=2|\Lambda_{11}||\Lambda_{12}|\cos( \mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{11} ) - \mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{12})),

(101)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Re}[\Lambda_{22}\Lambda_{21}^{*}]=2|\Lambda_{22}||\Lambda_{21}|\cos( \mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{22} ) - \mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{21})),

(102)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{11}\Lambda_{22}^{*}+\Lambda_{12}\Lambda_{21}^{*}

\displaystyle=\Big(|\Lambda_{11}|\Lambda_{22}|e^{\mathrm{i}(\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{11})-\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{12})+\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{21})-\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{22}))}+|\Lambda_{12}||\Lambda_{21}|\Big)e^{\mathrm{i}(\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{12})-\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{21}))},

(103)

where
\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{ij})
denotes the complex phase for the matrix element
\Lambda_{ij}=|\Lambda_{ij}|e^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{ij})}
.
The conditions above correspond to four real-valued equations that fix four out of eight parameters in
\Lambda
.

In more detail, from the first and second line one obtains the phase differences
\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{11})-\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{12})=\pi/2+k\pi
and
\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{21})-\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{22})=\pi/2+l\pi
with

With this, it follows in the third line that the factor

Consequently, the second factor
e^{\mathrm{i}(\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{12})-\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{21}))}
must also be real, so that one obtains
\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{12})-\mathrm{arg}(\Lambda_{21})=l^{\prime}\pi
with
l^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}

\end{array}\right),

Appendix D
Temperature-dependent coupling density
G(\Delta)

Typically, for open quantum systems the bath is assumed to remain in a thermal state. The bath excitations, the bath correlations respectively, decay on a time scale that is fast as compared to the dynamics of the system. In the Markovian limit all excitations leaving the system decay fast enough, such that they do not act back on the system at a later time.
In this regime, the dynamics of the system is described by a time local generator.
In a perturbative treatment up to second order in the interaction Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{SB}}
, the Redfield master equation (
77
) is obtained.
Therein, the bath correlation
C(\tau)
appears in a convolution integral with the Heisenberg operator
{S}(-\tau)=e^{-\mathrm{i}H_{\mathrm{S}}\tau}Se^{\mathrm{i}H_{\mathrm{S}}\tau}
.

As a first step, the Heisenberg operator is formally represented in the eigenbasis of system, such that the time-dependent convolution can be written as
\mathbb{S}_{t}=\sum_{qk}G_{t}(\omega_{q}-\omega_{k})|q\rangle\langle q|S|k\rangle\langle k|
, where
\omega_{q}
and
\ket{q}
are the eigenfrequencies and eigenstates of
H_{\mathrm{S}}
.
We define the temperature dependent coupling density
Valleau et al. 2012

\displaystyle G_{t}(\Delta)\equiv\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Delta\tau}C(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau,

(111)

which is also referred to as
spectral noise power
, e.g., in Ref.
Xu and Ankerhold 2023
.
However, in this work the integration always starts at time
t=0
. Therefore the coupling density is the half-sided Fourier transform and is a complex quantity.
In the asymptotic limit
\lim_{t\to\infty}G_{t}(\Delta)=G(\Delta)
this expression becomes the half-sided Fourier transform of the bath correlation.
By inserting the integral expression
C(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}J(\omega)n_{\beta}(\omega)\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\pi}
, with
n_{\beta}(\omega)=[\exp(\beta\omega)-1]^{-1}
one arrives at

\displaystyle G(\Delta)=J(\Delta)n_{\beta}(\Delta)+\mathrm{i}\ \principalvalue{\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{J(\omega) n_\beta(\omega) }{\omega- \Delta} \frac{\mathrm{d} \omega}{\pi}},

(112)

where the time integration is already performed by making use of the Sokhotskyi-Plemelj formular,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\mathrm{i}E\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau=\pi\delta(E)+\mathrm{i}\principalvalue{\frac{1}{E} },

(113)

which often referred to as Kramers-Kronig relation.

For second-order master equations the coupling density is to be evaluated at the frequencies
\Delta_{qk}=E_{q}-E_{k}
, associated with the energy level splittings, and the quantities
G(\Delta_{qk})
appear in the tensor elements of the Redfield equation. Even though some literature refers to them as transition rates in general this term might be misleading:
On the one hand, there exist different notations, i.e. for golden rule type expressions
G(\Delta)=2\pi J(\Delta)n_{\beta}(\Delta)
or different definitions of the coupling strength
G(\Delta)\propto\gamma^{2}
, e.g. in Ref.
Hone et al. 2009
.
On the other hand, a simple physical interpretation for the coupling density might be possible only for the quantum-optical master equation, for which the diagonal elements of the density matrix obey a Pauli rate equation. Thereby the rate for the transition
\ket{q}\rightarrow\ket{k}
between energy eigenstates is given by
R_{kq}=2G^{\prime}(\Delta_{qk})\lvert\matrixelement{q}{\hat{S}}{k}\rvert^{2}
and obeys the detailed-balance condition.
Also, for the quantum-optical master equation the imaginary part of the bath-correlation function only modifies the coherent dynamics by shifting the eigenenergies.
Since in this particular case, it only affects the transient dynamics, but not the steady state, it is often neglected.
However, beyond the secular approximation, the imaginary part of the bath correlation function is of crucial importance.
In fact, it mainly determines the difference between the quantum optical and Redfield master equation
Thingna et al. 2012
.
In the following, some basic properties of
G^{\prime\prime}(\Delta)
are discussed that stem directly from the integral representation.
By using the antisymmetry of the spectral density
J(\Delta)
, the region of integration can be limited to positive frequencies only,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} G^{\prime\prime}(\Delta)&=\principalvalue{\int_0^\infty\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega^2-\Delta^2} \big[\omega(n_\beta(\omega) + n_\beta(-\omega)) + \Delta(n_\beta(\omega) - n_\beta(-\omega))\big] \frac{\mathrm d \omega}{\pi} }\\
&=\underbrace{-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\pi}}_{G^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{RN}}}\underbrace{+\principalvalue{\int_0^\infty\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega^2 - \Delta^2} \left[ \Delta- \frac{\Delta^2}{\omega} \right] \frac{\mathrm d \omega}{\pi}}}_{G^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\Delta)}+\underbrace{2\Delta\principalvalue{\int_0^\infty\frac{J(\omega) n_\beta(\omega)}{\omega^2-\Delta^2} \frac{\mathrm{d} \omega}{\pi}}}_{G^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{th}}(\Delta)},\end{aligned}

(114)

where we used the symmetries of the Bose function
n_{\beta}(\omega)+n_{\beta}(-\omega)=-1
and
n_{\beta}(\omega)-n_{\beta}(-\omega)=2n_{\beta}(\omega)+1
.
Above,
G^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{RN}}
is the reorganization energy, which gives a contribution to the Lamb-shift Hamiltonian
Weiss 2012
.
One also identifies zero-point fluctuation
G^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\Delta)
and thermal noise
G^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{th}}(\Delta)
.
The wording
zero-point fluctuation
and
thermal noise
are vaguely used in the literature and might not agree with the definition given in this paper.
Though a very similar discussion is given in Refs.
Albash et al. 2012
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
.
Note, the identification of the latter as thermal noise is based on the fact that it vanishes for zero temperature.
In this limit, the rates to higher excited states are exponentially suppressed, which in the quantum-optical master equation leads to relaxation towards the ground state.
Formally, for zero temperature in
Eq.

112
one replaces
n_{\beta}(\omega)=-\Theta(-\omega)
, for which only
G^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{RN}}+G^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\Delta)
contributes to the imaginary part.
