A Simple Construction of Tournaments with Finite and Uncountable Dichromatic Number
Arpan Sadhukhan

Abstract
The dichromatic number χ ⁡ ( G → ) \chi(\vec{G}) of a digraph G → \vec{G} is the minimum number of colors needed to color the vertices V ⁡ ( G → ) V(\vec{G}) in such a way that no monochromatic directed cycle is obtained. In this note, for any k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} , we give a simple construction of tournaments with dichromatic number exactly equal to k k . The proofs are based on a combinatorial lemma on partitioning a checkerboard which may be of independent interest. We also generalize our finite construction to give an elementary construction of a complete digraph of cardinality equal to the cardinality of ℝ \mathbb{R} and having an uncountable dichromatic number. Furthermore, we also construct an oriented balanced complete n n -partite graph K → n ( m ) \vec{K}^{(m)}_{n} , such that the minimum number of colors needed to color its vertices such that there is no monochromatic directed triangle is greater than or equal to n ​ m / ( n + 2 ​ m − 2 ) nm/(n+2m-2) .

Introduction

The dichromatic number
\chi(\vec{G})
of a digraph
\vec{G}
is the minimum number of colors needed to color the vertices
V(\vec{G})
in such a way that no monochromatic directed cycle is obtained. This concept extends the notion of the chromatic number of a graph and was introduced by Neumann-Lara
[
11
]
. Erdős and Neumann-Lara
[
4
]
proved that there exist tournaments with dichromatic number
O(n/\log n)
, where
n
is the number of vertices of the tournament. Unlike the chromatic number, determining a graph with a specific dichromatic number, even for tournaments, poses challenges in construction. In this paper, we provide a straightforward construction method for tournaments with a dichromatic number precisely equal to any given natural number
k
. Although such construction is known in literature
[
12
]
, our construction is simpler and very different. The dichromatic number of digraphs, particularly tournaments, has been extensively studied, a few of those can be found in
[
7
,
13
,
14
]
. It is to be noted that the dichromatic number of tournaments is also related to the Erdős-Hajnal conjecture
[
2
]
. Let
K^{(m)}_{n}
denote a complete balanced
n
-partite graph where each partition (or maximum independent set) has exactly
m
vertices. Mohar
et al.

[
10
]
proved the existence of an orientation of
K^{(m)}_{n}
, whose dichromatic number is strictly greater than
\min\{nm/4\log(nm),n/2\}
, using a probabilistic approach. In this paper, we explicitly construct an oriented balanced complete
n
-partite graph
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
, such that the minimum number of colors needed to color its vertices such that there is no monochromatic triangle is greater than or equal to
nm/(n+2m-2)
. Thus the constructed digraph
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
has dichromatic number greater than or equal to
nm/(n+2m-2)
. Additional insights and some recent developments on the dichromatic number are explored in
[
3
,
6
,
16
]
.

The dichromatic number for an infinite digraph
\vec{G}
is the smallest cardinal
\kappa
for which
V(\vec{G})
can be colored with
\kappa
many colors avoiding monochromatic
directed cycles. Within the framework of Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with the Axiom of Choice (
ZFC
), Galvin and Shelah
[
5
]
constructed a tournament with cardinality
\aleph_{1}
with uncountable dichromatic number, where
\aleph_{1}
is the first uncountable cardinal. Soukup
[
15
]
also proved similar and more generalized results in
ZFC
. All these results relied on deep set theoretic theorems and knowledge. In fact Soukup
[
15
]
highlighted the perceived non-triviality, within
ZFC
, of discovering a single digraph with an uncountable dichromatic number. Even for finding tournaments of cardinality and dichromatic number
\aleph_{1}
, Soukup used a deep result of J.Moore. He posed the challenge of establishing an elementary proof for the existence of a tournament with cardinality
\aleph_{1}
and an uncountable dichromatic number. In this note by assuming the continuum hypothesis along with
ZFC
, we construct a tournament with cardinality
\aleph_{1}
and uncountable dichromatic number. Although this does not answer Soukup’s question in
ZFC
, our construction distinguishes itself by its elementary nature, offering a clear and easily understandable approach with minimal reliance on set-theoretic prerequisites. Notably, our construction serves as a natural and intuitive extension of the finite tournament discussed in section
2.2
. Related results on dichromatic number of infinite graphs can be found in
[
8
,
9
]
.

V(G)

.

2.1
Special sparse partition of a checkerboard

In this section, we prove a few combinatorial results on partitioning of a
n\times m
checkerboard into some desired subsets. These results will be used to prove bounds on the dichromatic number of the constructed tournaments and balanced complete n-partite graphs in Sections
2.2
and
2.3
. Suppose we have an
n\times m
checkerboard
G_{n\times m}
with
n\times m
cells, indexed by
(i,j)
with
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
and
j\leqslant m
. We give a total ordering of the cells of
G_{n\times m}
, we say that
(i_{1},j_{1})<(i_{2},j_{2})
iff either
i_{1}<i_{2}
or
i_{1}=i_{2}
and
j_{1}<j_{2}
. A subset
S
of the cells of
G_{n\times m}
is said to be
c-sparse
if for any two cells of
S
with the same
y
-coordinate say
(a_{1},b)
and
(a_{2},b)
, then
y\neq b
and
(a_{1},b)<(x,y)<(a_{2},b)
implies
(x,y)\notin S
. A partition
P
of
G_{n\times m}
is called
c-sparse
if all the sets of the partition are
c-sparse
. Let
\sigma(G_{n\times m})=\min\{|P|:P\;\text{is a \emph{c-sparse} partition of}\;G_{n\times m}\}
denote the minimum number of disjoint
c-sparse
sets needed to cover
G_{n\times m}
. Also define a subset
S
of the cells of
G_{n\times m}
to be
weak-c-sparse
if for any two cells of
S
with the same
y
-coordinate say
(a_{1},b)
and
(a_{2},b)
, then
y\neq b
,
a_{1}<x<a_{2}
and
(a_{1},b)<(x,y)<(a_{2},b)
implies
(x,y)\notin S
. Let
R_{i}=\{(i,j):1\leqslant j\leqslant m\},C_{j}=\{(i,j):1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}
denote the
i^{th}
row and the
j^{th}
column of
G_{n\times m}
respectively for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
and
j\leqslant m
.

We prove the following theorem:

Theorem 2.1
.

\sigma(G_{n\times n})
equals the smallest natural number strictly greater than
\frac{n}{2}
.

Before we prove the theorem we proceed to prove a few necessary lemmas. Below is a trivial observation.

Observation 2.2
.

Let
S
is a c-sparse set of
G_{n\times m}
. If we delete some rows and some columns of
G_{n\times m}
to obtain a
n^{\prime}\times m^{\prime}
checkerboard
G_{n^{\prime}\times m^{\prime}}
with the ordering of the cells induced from
G_{n\times m}
. Then
S\cap G_{n^{\prime}\times m^{\prime}}
is a c-sparse set of
G_{n^{\prime}\times m^{\prime}}
.

Now we prove that the maximum size of a
c-sparse
set of
G_{n\times n}
is upper bounded by
2n-1
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
S
be a c-sparse set of
G_{n\times n}
, then
|S|\leqslant 2n-1
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
. Clearly, for
n=1
the theorem holds trivially. Now say the theorem holds for
n=k-1
. Let
S
be a
c-sparse
subset of
G_{k\times k}
with cardinality greater than or equal to
2k
. Let
S^{\prime}
be a subset of
S
with exactly
2k
cells. Observe that
S^{\prime}
is trivially
c-sparse
. Let
(i^{*},j^{*})
be the smallest cell that belongs to
S^{\prime}
. Then either
|S^{\prime}\cap R_{i^{*}}|\leqslant 1
or
|S^{\prime}\cap C_{j^{*}}|\leqslant 1
, otherwise let
(i^{*},j^{\prime})
and
(i^{\prime},j^{*})
be two cells of
S^{\prime}
with
i^{\prime}>i^{*}
and
j^{\prime}>j^{*}
. Then
(i^{*},j^{*})<(i^{*},j^{\prime})<(i^{\prime},j^{*})
and
j^{\prime}\neq j^{*}
which is a contradiction to the fact that
S^{\prime}
is
c-sparse
. Now we divide the proof into two cases.

Case 1
:
R_{i^{*}}\cap S^{\prime}
only consists of the element
(i^{*},j^{*})
.

Then
C_{j^{*}}\cap S^{\prime}
must have at least
2
cells other than the cell
(i^{*},j^{*})
, otherwise
|(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{*}})\cap S^{\prime}|\leqslant 2
. Now
G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{*}})
is a
k-1\times k-1
checkerboard and let the ordering of the cells be induced from
G_{k\times k}
. So by Observation
2.2
we know that
S^{\prime}\cap G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{*}})
is a
c-sparse
set of
G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{*}})
. Also
|S^{\prime}\cap G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{*}})|=2k-2
which is a contradiction to the induction hypothesis. So we have
|C_{j^{*}}\cap S^{\prime}|\geqslant 3
, hence by pigeon hole principle there exists
j^{\prime\prime}\leqslant k
such that
|C_{j^{\prime\prime}}\cap S^{\prime}|\leqslant 1
. So we have
|(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{\prime\prime}})\cap S^{\prime}|\leqslant 2
, hence
G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{\prime\prime}})
is a
k-1\times k-1
checkerboard and let the ordering of the cells be induced from
G_{k\times k}
. We have
|S^{\prime}\cap G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{\prime\prime}})|\geqslant 2k-2
, and since by Observation
2.2
we know that
S^{\prime}
is
c-sparse
in
G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{\prime\prime}})
, we again have a contradiction to the induction hypothesis.

Case 2
:
C_{j^{*}}\cap S^{\prime}
only consists of the element
(i^{*},j^{*})
.

Then
R_{i^{*}}\cap S^{\prime}
must have at least
2
cells other than the cell
(i^{*},j^{*})
, otherwise
|(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{*}})\cap S^{\prime}|\leqslant 2
. Now
G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{*}})
is a
k-1\times k-1
checkerboard and let the ordering of the cells be induced from
G_{k\times k}
. So by Observation
2.2
we know that
S^{\prime}\cap G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{*}})
is a
c-sparse
set of
G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{*}})
. Also
|S^{\prime}\cap G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{*}}\cup C_{j^{*}})|=2k-2
which is a contradiction to the induction hypothesis. So we have
|R_{i^{*}}\cap S^{\prime}|\geqslant 3
, hence by pigeon hole principle there exists
i^{\prime\prime}\leqslant k
such that
|R_{i^{\prime\prime}}\cap S^{\prime}|\leqslant 1
. So we have
|(R_{i^{\prime\prime}}\cup C_{j^{*}})\cap S^{\prime}|\leqslant 2
, hence
G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{\prime\prime}}\cup C_{j^{*}})
is a
k-1\times k-1
checkerboard and let the ordering of the cells be induced from
G_{k\times k}
. We have
|S^{\prime}\cap G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{\prime\prime}}\cup C_{j^{*}})|\geqslant 2k-2
, and since by Observation
2.2
we know that
S^{\prime}
is
c-sparse
in
G_{k\times k}\setminus(R_{i^{\prime\prime}}\cup C_{j^{*}})
, we again have a contradiction to the induction hypothesis.

Hence both the cases above cannot happen, thus finishing the proof.

∎

Now we prove an upper bound on the size of
c-sparse
set for the general
n\times m
checkerboard.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
S
be a c-sparse set of
G_{n\times m}
, then
|S|\leqslant n+m-1
.

Proof.

Let
S
be a
c-sparse
subset of
G_{n\times m}
with cardinality greater than or equal to
n+m
. Let
S^{\prime}
be a subset of
S
with exactly
n+m
cells. Observe that
S^{\prime}
is trivially
c-sparse
. We divide the proof into three cases.

•

Case 1:
n>m

Let
R^{\prime}_{j}=R_{j}\cap S^{\prime}
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
. Assume
|R^{\prime}_{j_{i}}|\leqslant|R^{\prime}_{j_{i+1}}|
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
. Now observe that if

\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}|R^{\prime}_{j_{i}}|>n-m.

then clearly
|R^{\prime}_{j_{n-m+1}}|\geqslant 2
and hence

\sum_{i=1}^{n}|R^{\prime}_{j_{i}}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}|R^{\prime}_{j_{i}}|+\sum_{i=n-m+1}^{n}|R^{\prime}_{j_{i}}|\geqslant(n-m+1)+2m>n+m.

This is a contradiction as
|S^{\prime}|=n+m
.
So we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}|R^{\prime}_{j_{i}}|\leqslant n-m.

Hence deleting the rows
R_{j_{i}}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n-m
we obtain a
m\times m
checkerboard
G_{m\times m}
such that
S^{\prime}\cap G_{m\times m}\geqslant n+m-(n-m)=2m
which contradicts Lemma
2.3
. Thus finishing the proof for
Case 1
.

•

Case 2:
m>n

The proof of this case is exactly similar to the proof for
Case 1
, the only difference is instead of rows
R_{j}
, we do the same argument with the columns
C_{j}
.

•

Case 3:
m=n

This case directly follows from Lemma
2.3
.

Thus finishing the proof.
∎

Now consider the checkerboard
G_{n\times n}
, where
n
is an odd natural number. Now let
D_{i}\subseteq G_{n\times n}=\{(x,y):(x-y)=i\}
be the diagonals of
G_{n\times n}
. So clearly for
i>n-1
and
i<-n+1
,
D_{i}
is an empty set. So clearly
\bigcup\limits_{i=-n+1}^{n-1}D_{i}=G_{n\times n}
. Let
S_{2k}=D_{2k}\cup D_{2k+1}\cup D_{2k-n}\cup D_{2k-n-1}
for
0\leqslant k\leqslant(n-1)/2
. In the following lemma, we show that the subset
S_{2k}
of
G_{n\times n}
, where
n
is an odd natural number, is
c-sparse
. For an illustration, see figure
1
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
0\leqslant k\leqslant(n-1)/2
, the subset
S_{2k}
of
G_{n\times n}
, where
n
is an odd natural number, is c-sparse.

Proof.

Suppose
S_{2k}
is not
c-sparse
. Then there exists
(x_{1},y),(x_{2},y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S_{2k}
such that
(x_{1},y)<(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})<(x_{2},y)
with
y^{\prime}\neq y
. Observe that no two points in
D_{i}
have the same y-coordinate. Now we divide the proof into two exhaustive sets of cases.

Case 1:
One of the points between
(x_{1},y)
and
(x_{2},y)
lies in
D_{2k}\cup D_{2k+1}
and the other lies in
D_{2k-n}\cup D_{2k-n-1}
.

Without loss of generality, assume
(x_{1},y)\in D_{2k}\cup D_{2k+1}
and
(x_{2},y)\in D_{2k-n}\cup D_{2k-n-1}
. So
(x_{1}-y)\geqslant 2k
and
(x_{2}-y)\leqslant 2k-n
, hence
x_{1}-x_{2}\geqslant n
, which is a contradiction as they are points of
G_{n\times n}
.

Case 2:
Both the points
(x_{1},y)
and
(x_{2},y)
lies either in
D_{2k}\cup D_{2k+1}
or in
D_{2k-n}\cup D_{2k-n-1}
.

Assume
(x_{1},y)\in D_{2k}
and
(x_{2},y)\in D_{2k+1}
. So,
x_{2}=x_{1}+1
. Now since
(x_{1},y)<(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})<(x_{2},y)
, either
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(x_{1},y^{\prime})
with
y^{\prime}>y
or
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(x_{1}+1,y^{\prime})
with
y^{\prime}<y
. In the former case
(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime})<(x_{1}-y)=2k
, also since
y^{\prime}-y\leqslant n-1
,
(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime})=(x_{1}-y^{\prime})=(x_{1}-y-(y^{\prime}-y))\geqslant 2k-n+1
. So in this case
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
cannot lie in
S_{2}k
. Now in the latter case when
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(x_{1}+1,y^{\prime})
with
y^{\prime}<y
, we have
(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime})=(x_{1}+1-y^{\prime})>(x_{1}+1-y)\geqslant 2k+1
, so again in this case
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
cannot belong to
S_{2k}
. Now the only other possibility that remains is that
(x_{1},y)\in D_{2k-n-1}
and
(x_{2},y)\in D_{2k-n}
, then again using similar arguments it is easy to show that
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
cannot belong to
S_{2k}
.

Thus, we have proved that the set
S_{2k}
is
c-sparse
.
∎

Now we proceed to prove Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
n^{*}
be the smallest integer strictly greater than
n/2
.
From Lemma
2.3
we know that the cardinality of any
c-sparse
set is strictly less than
2n
. Hence if
P
is a partition
G_{n\times n}
into
c-sparse
sets, then clearly
|P|>n^{2}/2n=n/2\geqslant n^{*}-1
.

On the other hand if
n
is odd, we know from Lemma
2.5
that for
0\leqslant k\leqslant(n-1)/2
, the sets
S_{2k}
are
c-sparse
. Also trivially
\bigcup\limits_{i=0}^{(n-1)/2}S_{2k}=\bigcup\limits_{i=-n+1}^{n-1}D_{i}=G_{n\times n}
. So
P^{\prime}=\{S_{2k}:0\leqslant k\leqslant(n-1)/2\}
, form a
c-sparse
partition of
G_{n\times n}
. Also
|P^{\prime}|=(n+1)/2=n^{*}
. Now it is easy to see that if there is a
c-sparse
partition of
G_{n\times n}
into
k
sets, then trivially there exists a
c-sparse
partition of
G_{n-1\times n-1}
into
k
sets. So if
n
is even, we already know that there is a
c-sparse
partition of
G_{n+1\times n+1}
into
(n+2)/2
sets, so there is a
c-sparse
partition of
G_{n\times n}
into
(n+2)/2=n^{*}
sets. Thus finishing the proof.

Now we prove an upper bound on the size of a

G_{n\times n}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
S
be a weak-c-sparse set of
G_{n\times m}
, then
|S|\leqslant n+2m-2
.

Proof.

Let
(i_{j},j)
denote the smallest element of
S\cap C_{j}
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant m
. Note that if
S\cap C_{j}=\emptyset
, then the smallest element does not exist. Let
S^{\prime}=S\setminus\{(i_{j},j):1\leqslant j\leqslant n\}
, denote the subset obtained by deleting the smallest elements of every column that is present in
S
. Observe that
S^{\prime}
is trivially a
weak-c-sparse
set of
G_{n\times m}
. Hence
S^{\prime}
is not
c-sparse
only if there exist three cells
(a_{1},b),(x,y),(a_{2},b)
such that
y\neq b
,
x=a_{1}
or
x=a_{2}
,
(a_{1},b)<(x,y)<(a_{2},b)
and
(x,y)\in S^{\prime}
. Now we divide the proof into two cases.

Case 1:
y\neq b
,
x=a_{1}
,
(a_{1},b)<(x,y)<(a_{2},b)
and
(x,y)\in S^{\prime}

By construction of
S^{\prime}
, we know that
(a_{1},b)
is not the smallest element of
S\cap C_{b}
. Let
(i_{b},b)
denote the smallest element of
S\cap C_{b}
. So we have
y\neq b
,
(i_{b},b),(x,y),(a_{2},b)\in S
and
(i_{b},b)<(x,y)<(a_{2},b)
which is a contradiction as
S
is a
weak-c-sparse
set of
G_{n\times m}
.

Case 2:
y\neq b
,
x=a_{2}
,
(a_{1},b)<(x,y)<(a_{2},b)
and
(x,y)\in S^{\prime}

The proof is similar to the proof for
Case 1
. Let
(i_{b},b)
and
(i_{y},y)
denote the smallest element of
S\cap C_{b}
and
S\cap C_{y}
respectively. If
i_{y}<a_{1}
then we have
b\neq y
,
(i_{y},y)<(a_{1},b)<(x,y)
. If
a_{1}<i_{y}<a_{2}
, then we have
y\neq b
,
(a_{1},b)<(i_{y},y)<(a_{2},b)
. Hence
i_{y}
must be equal to
a_{1}
, otherwise, we get a contradiction as
S
is a
weak-c-sparse
set. Now again if
i_{y}=a_{1}
, then we have
b\neq y
and
(i_{b},b)<(i_{y},y)<(a_{2},b)
, which is again a contradiction as
S
is a
weak-c-sparse
set.

Now observe that by construction it is clear that
S^{\prime}\cap R_{1}=\emptyset
. Also we have
S^{\prime}
is
c-sparse
subset of
G_{n\times m}
, hence trivially it is also a
c-sparse
subset of
G_{n-1\times m}
which is obtained from
G_{n\times m}
by deleting the first row
R_{1}
.
So by Lemma
2.4
, we have
|S^{\prime}|\leqslant n+m-2
. Also by construction is easy to see that
|S|-|S^{\prime}|\leqslant m
. Hence
|S|\leqslant n+2m-2
. Thus finishing the proof.

2.2
Tournaments with any given fixed dichromatic number

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be any fixed natural number. In this section, we give an easy construction of a tournament with dichromatic number exactly equal to
k
. Let
G_{2k-1\times 2k-1}
be a checkerboard of size
(2k-1)^{2}
. Let the cells
(i,j)
be totally ordered with
(i_{1},j_{1})<(i_{2},j_{2})
iff either
i_{1}<i_{2}
or
i_{1}=i_{2}
and
j_{1}<j_{2}
. Now we construct a tournament
\vec{T}_{k}
with the number of vertices equal to
(2k-1)^{2}
(Note that the notation
\vec{T}_{k}
is slightly different from the standard notation of a tournament, where the subscript denotes the number of vertices but here it actually denotes the dichromatic number).

Construction of
\vec{T}_{k}
:

•

The
(2k-1)^{2}
cells of
G_{2k-1\times 2k-1}
denote all the vertices of
\vec{T}_{k}
. The vertices are also totally ordered with the order induced from the total ordering of the cells of
G_{2k-1\times 2k-1}
.

•

If cells
(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2})
lie in the same column of
G_{2k-1\times 2k-1}
(that is
j_{1}=j_{2}
), then the edge between
(i_{1},j_{1})
and
(i_{2},j_{2})
is directed from
(i_{1},j_{1})
to
(i_{2},j_{2})
iff
(i_{1},j_{1})<(i_{2},j_{2})
.

•

If cells
(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2})
does not lie in the same column of
G_{2k-1\times 2k-1}
(that is
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
), then the edge between
(i_{1},j_{1})
and
(i_{2},j_{2})
is directed from
(i_{1},j_{1})
to
(i_{2},j_{2})
iff
(i_{1},j_{1})>(i_{2},j_{2})
.

Now we will prove that the dichromatic number of
\vec{T}_{k}
is exactly equal to
k
. We define a subset of vertices of
\vec{T}_{k}
to be
c-sparse
iff the corresponding subset of cells of
G_{2k-1\times 2k-1}
is
c-sparse
.

We state the following lemma which is very well known
[
1
]
and easy to show.

Lemma 2.7
.

A tournament is acyclic iff it contains no directed triangles.

Now we observe that the
c-sparse
sets of
\vec{T}_{k}
are in one-to-one correspondence with the acyclic subtournaments of
\vec{T}_{k}
.

Lemma 2.8
.

A subset of vertices
V
of
\vec{T}_{k}
is c-sparse iff the induced subtournament
\vec{T}_{k}[V]
is acyclic.

Proof.

First, suppose the subset of vertices
V
of
\vec{T}_{k}
is not
c-sparse
. Then there exists
(x_{1},y),(x_{2},y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in V
such that
(x_{1},y)<(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})<(x_{2},y)
with
y^{\prime}\neq y
. So clearly the points
(x_{1},y),(x_{2},y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in V
form a directed triangle in
\vec{T}_{k}[V]
and hence
\vec{T}_{k}[V]
is not acyclic.

Now suppose
\vec{T}_{k}[V]
is not acyclic then by Lemma
2.7
we know that
\vec{T}_{k}[V]
contains a directed triangle. Let
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2}),(x_{3},y_{3})
form a directed triangle. Without loss of generality assume
(x_{3},y_{3})>(x_{2},y_{2})
and
(x_{3},y_{3})>(x_{1},y_{1})
. Also without loss of generality assume the vertex
(x_{3},y_{3})
has an incoming edge from
(x_{1},y_{1})
and an outgoing edge to
(x_{2},y_{2})
. Now by construction, we know that
y_{1}=y_{3}
and
y_{2}\neq y_{3}
. Since
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2}),(x_{3},y_{3})
form a directed triangle, we also know that
(x_{2},y_{2})
has an outgoing edge to
(x_{1},y_{1})
. Now since
y_{2}\neq y_{1}
, by construction we have
(x_{2},y_{2})>(x_{1},y_{1})
. So we have
(x_{1},y_{1})<(x_{2},y_{2})<(x_{3},y_{3})
with
y_{2}\neq y_{1}=y_{3}
, which implies that
V
is not
c-sparse
. Thus finishing the proof.
∎

Now we have everything needed to show that the dichromatic number of
\vec{T}_{k}
is exactly equal to
k
.

Theorem 2.9
.

The dichromatic number of
\vec{T}_{k}
is exactly equal to
k
.

Proof.

Suppose we color the vertices of
\vec{T}_{k}
such that the vertices of each color class induce an acyclic subtournament. Then by Lemma
2.8
, we know that the set of vertices of each color class is a
c-sparse
set and also any
c-sparse
set corresponds to an acyclic induced subtournament. So by Theorem
2.1
we know that
k
colors are necessary and also sufficient to color the vertices of
\vec{T}_{k}
, such that the vertices of each color class form an induced acyclic subtournament. Hence the dichromatic number of
\vec{T}_{k}
is exactly equal to
k
.
∎

2.3
A lower bound for the dichromatic number of an oriented complete balanced
n
-partite graphs

Let
K^{(m)}_{n}
denote a complete balanced
n
-partite graph where each partition (or maximum independent set) has exactly
m
. vertices.

Let
G_{n\times m}
be a checkerboard of size
nm
. Let the cells
(i,j)
be totally ordered with
(i_{1},j_{1})<(i_{2},j_{2})
iff either
i_{1}<i_{2}
or
i_{1}=i_{2}
and
j_{1}<j_{2}
. Now we construct an oriented complete balanced
n
-partite graph
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
.

Construction:

•

The
nm
cells of
G_{n\times m}
, denote all the vertices of
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
. Also, each row indicates one partition(or maximum independent set) of
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
. The vertices are also totally ordered with the order induced from the total ordering of the cells of
G_{n\times m}
.

•

If cells
(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2})
lie in the same column of
G_{n\times m}
(that is
j_{1}=j_{2}
), then the edge between
(i_{1},j_{1})
and
(i_{2},j_{2})
is directed from
(i_{1},j_{1})
to
(i_{2},j_{2})
iff
(i_{1},j_{1})<(i_{2},j_{2})
.

•

If cells
(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2})
does not lie in the same column of
G_{n\times m}
(that is
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
), then the edge between
(i_{1},j_{1})
and
(i_{2},j_{2})
is directed from
(i_{1},j_{1})
to
(i_{2},j_{2})
iff
(i_{1},j_{1})>(i_{2},j_{2})
.

We define a subset of vertices of
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
to be
weak-c-sparse
iff the corresponding subset of cells of
G_{n\times m}
is
weak-c-sparse
. We begin with the simple observation below.

Observation 2.10
.

For subset of vertices
V
of
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
if the induced subdigraph
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}[V]
has no directed triangle then
V
is weak-c-sparse.

Proof.

Suppose the subset of vertices
V
of
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
is not
weak-c-sparse
. Then there exists
(x_{1},y),(x_{2},y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in V
such that
(x_{1},y)<(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})<(x_{2},y)
with
x_{1}<x^{\prime}<x_{2}
and
y^{\prime}\neq y
. So clearly by construction the points
(x_{1},y),(x_{2},y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in V
form a directed triangle in the induced subdigraph
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}[V]
.
∎

Theorem 2.11
.

The minimum number of colors needed to color the vertices of
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
such that there is no monochromatic directed triangle is greater than or equal to
nm/(n+2m-2)
.

Proof.

Suppose we color the vertices
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
such that the vertices colored with the same color do not induce a directed triangle. Now by Observation
2.10
, we know that any subset of vertices of
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
which do not induce a directed triangle must be a
weak-c-sparse
set of
G_{n\times m}
. Hence by Lemma
2.6
, each color class must have cardinality less than or equal to
n+2m-2
. Hence the dichromatic number of
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
is greater than or equal to
nm/(n+2m-2)
.
∎

Remark:
It is trivial to observe that Theorem
2.11
implies that the dichromatic number of the constructed oriented graph
\vec{K}^{(m)}_{n}
is greater than or equal to
nm/(n+2m-2)
.

2.4
Uncountable tournaments with uncountable dichromatic number

In this section, we assume the continuum hypothesis and
ZFC
. Hence we know that
\aleph_{1}
is the cardinality of
\mathbb{R}
. We will now construct a tournament on
\aleph_{1}
vertices with dichromatic number
\aleph_{1}
, which is also a very natural generalization of the finite tournament
\vec{T}_{k}
constructed in Section
2.2
.

For a point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we denote by
x(p)
and
y(p)
to be the
x
-coordinate and
y
-coordinate of the point
p
respectively.
Let
\vec{T}_{\aleph_{1}}
be a tournament with vertex set being all the points on the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(Note that cardinality of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is same as the cardinality of
\mathbb{R}
). Hence there is an edge between any two vertices of
T_{\aleph_{1}}
. Let the vertices of
T_{\aleph_{1}}
denoted by
(i,j)
be totally ordered with
(i_{1},j_{1})<(i_{2},j_{2})
iff either
i_{1}<i_{2}
or
i_{1}=i_{2}
and
j_{1}<j_{2}
. Now we detail the construction of the uncountable tournament
\vec{T}_{\aleph_{1}}
below.

Construction of
\vec{T}_{\aleph_{1}}
:

•

Let
V(\vec{T}_{\aleph_{1}})=\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

•

For any two vertices
(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2})
with
j_{1}=j_{2}
, the edge between
(i_{1},j_{1})
and
(i_{2},j_{2})
is directed from
(i_{1},j_{1})
to
(i_{2},j_{2})
iff
(i_{1},j_{1})<(i_{2},j_{2})
.

•

For any two vertices
(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2})
with
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
, then the edge between
(i_{1},j_{1})
and
(i_{2},j_{2})
is directed from
(i_{1},j_{1})
to
(i_{2},j_{2})
iff
(i_{1},j_{1})>(i_{2},j_{2})
.

We now state a standard well-known lemma in real analysis which easily follows from the separability property of
\mathbb{R}
. We will use the lemma in the proof of the main theorem.

Lemma 2.12
.

There do not exist uncountably many pairwise disjoint, non-empty open intervals of
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 2.13
.

The tournament
\vec{T}_{\aleph_{1}}
has uncountable dichromatic number.

Proof.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction let
C=\{C_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
denote a countable coloring of
\vec{T}_{\aleph_{1}}
such that there is no monochromatic directed cycle in
\vec{T}_{\aleph_{1}}
. Note that since all the points of the plane are vertices of
\vec{T}_{\aleph_{1}}
,
C
can be also viewed as a coloring of all the points in the plane. Let
V(C_{i})
denote the vertices of
\vec{T}_{\aleph_{1}}
which are colored with the color
C_{i}
. Let
L_{r}
denote the line
y=r
. Let
X=\{L_{r}:r\in\mathbb{R}\}
be the set of all lines parallel to the x-axis. Define a function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, such that
f(L_{r})=i
iff
i=\min\{j\in\mathbb{N}:|V(C_{j})\cap L_{r}|\geqslant 3\}
. Since
C
is a coloring of the whole plane all the points of the line
L_{r}
must be colored using some color in
C
. Also, observe that
C
is countable a set and
L_{r}
is an uncountable set for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
, there must exist at least three points in
L_{r}
which get the same color. So the function
f
is well-defined. Since
X
is uncountable there exists
i^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the set
S=\{r\in\mathbb{R}:f(L_{r})=i^{*}\}
is uncountable. For any
r\in S
, let,

m_{r}=\inf_{p\in L_{r}\cap V(C_{i^{*}})}x(p)\;\text{and}\;M_{r}=\sup_{p\in L_{r}\cap V(C_{i}^{*})}x(p)

. For
r\in S
, let
I_{r}
denote the open interval
(m_{r},M_{r})
. Note that
m_{r}\neq M_{r}
, for all
r\in S
as
|V(C_{i^{*}})\cap L_{r}|\geqslant 3
, hence
I_{r}
is non-empty for all
r\in S
.

Claim 2.14
.

For
r\neq r^{\prime}\in S
, the open interval
(m_{r},M_{r})
is disjoint from
(m_{r^{\prime}},M_{r^{\prime}})
.

{claimproof}

If for some point
p_{r^{\prime}}\in L_{r^{\prime}}\cap V(C_{i^{*}})
,
x(p_{r^{\prime}})\in(m_{r},M_{r})
. Then by definition from
m_{r}
and
M_{r}
, there exists
q_{r},q^{\prime}_{r}\in L_{r}\cap V(C_{i^{*}})
, such that
x(p_{r^{\prime}})\in(x(q_{r}),x(q^{\prime}_{r}))
. Now since
x(q_{r})<x(p_{r^{\prime}})<x(q^{\prime}_{r})
and
y(q_{r})=y(q^{\prime}_{r})
, by construction we see that
p_{r^{\prime}},q_{r},q^{\prime}_{r}
form a directed monochromatic triangle in
\vec{T}_{\aleph_{1}}
, which is a contradiction. Hence for any point
p_{r^{\prime}}\in L_{r^{\prime}}\cap V(C_{i^{*}})
,
x(p_{r^{\prime}})\notin(m_{r},M_{r})
. So if
(m_{r},M_{r})\cap(m_{r^{\prime}},M_{r^{\prime}})
is non-empty then
(m_{r},M_{r})\subseteq(m_{r^{\prime}},M_{r^{\prime}})
. Now since
|V(C_{i^{*}})\cap L_{r}|\geqslant 3
, there exists
p^{*}_{r}\in L_{r}\cap V(C_{i}^{*})
,
x(p^{*}_{r})\in(m_{r},M_{r})
. So we have
x(p^{*}_{r})\in(m_{r^{\prime}},M_{r^{\prime}})
. But by symmetry we also have for any point
p_{r}\in L_{r}\cap V(C_{i}^{*})
,
x(p_{r})\notin(m_{r^{\prime}},M_{r^{\prime}})
, which gives a contradiction. Hence the open interval
(m_{r},M_{r})
is disjoint from
(m_{r^{\prime}},M_{r^{\prime}})
.

Let
I=\{I_{r}:r\in S\}
be a collection of non-empty open intervals of
\mathbb{R}
. Now since
S
is uncountable,
I
is uncountable. Also by Claim
2.14
, we know that the intervals in
I
are pairwise disjoint. Hence by Lemma
2.12
we have a contradiction. Thus finishing our proof.

∎
