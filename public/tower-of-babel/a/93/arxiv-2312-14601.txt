Generalized Moment Estimators based on Stein Identities
Simon Nik, Christian H. Weiß

Abstract
For parameter estimation of continuous and discrete distributions, we propose a generalization of the method of moments (MM), where Stein identities are utilized for improved estimation performance. The construction of these Stein-type MM-estimators makes use of a weight function as implied by an appropriate form of the Stein identity. Our general approach as well as potential benefits thereof are first illustrated by the simple example of the exponential distribution. Afterward, we investigate the more sophisticated two-parameter inverse Gaussian distribution and the two-parameter negative-binomial distribution in great detail, together with illustrative real-world data examples. Given an appropriate choice of the respective weight functions, their Stein-MM estimators, which are defined by simple closed-form formulas and allow for closed-form asymptotic computations, exhibit a better performance regarding bias and mean squared error than competing estimators. Key words: asymptotic distribution; method of moments; parametric distributions; Stein identity.



1
Introduction

For many parametric distributions, so-called Stein identities are available, which rely on moments of functional expressions of a corresponding random variable. These identities are named after Charles Stein, who developed the idea of uniquely characterizing a certain distribution family by such a moment identity
(
Stein 1972
;
Stein 1986
, see)
. Many examples of both continuous and discrete distributions together with their Stein characterizations can be found in
Stein et al. 2004
;
Sudheesh 2009
;
Sudheesh & Tibiletti 2012
;
Landsman & Valdez 2016
;
Weiß & Aleksandrov 2022
;
Anastasiou et al. 2023
and the references therein. Stein identities are not a mere tool of probability theory. During the last years, there was also a lot of research activity on statistical applications of Stein identities, for example to goodness-of-fit (GoF) tests
(
Betsch et al. 2022
;
Weiß et al. 2023
)
and control charts
(
Weiß 2023
)
, among others.
In the present article, however, another application of Stein identities is investigated and exemplified, namely to the parameter estimation of continuous or discrete distributions. The idea to construct generalized types of method-of-moments (MM) estimators based on an appropriate type of Stein identity plus weighting function, referred to as Stein-MM estimators, was first explored in some applications by
Arnold et al. 2001
and
Wang & Weiß 2023
. Recently,
Ebner et al. 2023
discussed the Stein-MM approach in a much broader way, and also the present article provides a comprehensive treatment of Stein-MM estimators for various distributions.
The main motivation for considering Stein-MM estimation is that the weighting function might be chosen in such a way that the resulting estimator shows better properties (e. g., a reduced bias or mean squared error (MSE)) than the default MM estimator or other existing estimators. Despite the additional flexibility offered by the weighting function, the Stein-MM estimators are computed from simple closed-form expressions, and consistency and asymptotic normality are easily established, also see
Ebner et al. 2023
.

In what follows, we apply the proposed Stein-MM estimation to three different distribution families. We start with the illustrative example of the exponential (Exp) distribution in Section
2
. This simple one-parameter distribution mainly serves to demonstrate the general approach for deriving the Stein-MM estimator and its asymptotics, and it also indicates the potential benefits of using the Stein-MM approach for parameter estimation.
Afterward in Section
3
, we examine a more sophisticated type of continuous distribution, namely the two-parameter inverse Gaussian (IG) distribution. In Section
4
, we then turn to a discrete distribution family, namely the two-parameter negative-binomial (NB) distribution. Illustrative real-world data examples are also presented in Sections
3
–
4
.
Note that neither the exponential distribution nor any discrete distribution have been considered by
Ebner et al. 2023
, and their approach to the Stein-MM estimation of the IG-distribution differs from the one proposed here, see the details below. Also
Arnold et al. 2001
;
Wang & Weiß 2023
did not discuss any of the aforementioned distributions.
Finally, we conclude in Section
5
and outline topics for future research.

2
Stein Estimation of Exponential Distribution

The exponential distribution is the most well-known lifetime distribution, which is characterized by the property of being memory-less. It has positive support and depends on the parameter
\lambda>0
, where its probability density function (pdf) is given by
\phi(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}
for
x>0
and zero otherwise.
A detailed survey about the properties of and estimators for the
\textup{Exp}(\lambda)
-distribution can be found in
Johnson et al. 1995
.
Given the independent and identically distributed (i. i. d.) sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
with
X_{i}\sim\textup{Exp}(\lambda)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, the default estimator of
\lambda>0
, which is an MM estimator and the maximum likelihood (ML) estimator at the same time, is given by
\hat{\lambda}=1/\overline{X}
, where
\overline{X}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
denotes the sample mean. This estimator is known to neither be unbiased, nor to be optimal in terms of the MSE, see
Elfessi & Reineke 2001
.
To derive a generalized MM estimator with perhaps improved bias or MSE properties, we consider the exponential Stein identity according to
Stein et al. 2004
, which states that

\displaystyle X\sim\textup{Exp}(\lambda)\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(X)]=\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[f(X)]

(2.1)

for any piecewise differentiable function
f
with
f(0)=0
such that
\mathbb{E}\big[|f^{\prime}(X)|\big]
,
\mathbb{E}\big[|f(X)|\big]
exist.
Solving (
2.1
) in
\lambda
and using the sample moments
\overline{f^{\prime}(X)}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{\prime}(X_{i})
and
\overline{f(X)}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})
instead of the population moments, the class of Stein-MM estimators for
\lambda
is obtained as

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{Exp}}=\frac{\,\overline{f^{\prime}(X)}\,}{\overline{f(X)}}.

(2.2)

Note that the choice
f(x)=x
leads to the default estimator
\hat{\lambda}=1/\overline{X}
. Generally,
f(x)\not=x
might be interpreted as a kind of weighting function, which assigns different weights to large or low values of
X
than the identity function does.
For deriving the asymptotic distribution of the general Stein-MM estimator
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{Exp}}
, we first define the vectors
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}
with
i=1,\ldots,n
as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}\coloneqq\big(f^{\prime}(X_{i}),f(X_{i})\big)^{\top}.

(2.3)

Their mean equals

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{Z}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}]=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(X_{i})]\\
\mathbb{E}[f(X_{i})]\end{pmatrix}\overset{\eqref{Lionel}}{=}\mu_{f}(0,1,0)\begin{pmatrix}\lambda\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(2.4)

where we define
\mu_{f}(k,l,m)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[X^{k}\cdot f(X)^{l}\cdot f^{\prime}(X)^{m}]
for any
k,l,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
. Then, the following central limit theorem (CLT) holds.

Theorem 2.1

If
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i. i. d. according to
\textup{Exp}(\lambda)
, then the sample mean
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}}
of
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{1},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{n}
according to (
2.3
) is asymptotically normally distributed as

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\big(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{Z}\big)\ \xrightarrow{\text{d}}\ \textup{N}\big(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\big)\quad\text{with}\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=(\sigma_{ij})_{i,j=1,2},

where
\textup{N}(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})
denotes the multivariate normal distribution, and where the covariances are given as

\displaystyle\sigma_{11}

\displaystyle=\mu_{f}(0,0,2)-\lambda^{2}\cdot\mu_{f}^{2}(0,1,0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{22}

\displaystyle=\mu_{f}(0,2,0)-\mu_{f}^{2}(0,1,0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{12}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\big(\sigma_{22}-\mu_{f}^{2}(0,1,0)\big)=\lambda\big(\sigma_{22}-\tfrac{1}{2}\mu_{f}(0,2,0)\big).

The proof of Theorem
2.1
is provided by Appendix
A.1
.
In the second step of deriving the asymptotics of
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{Exp}}
, we define the function
g(u,v)\coloneqq\frac{u}{v}
. Then,
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{Exp}}=g(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}})
and
\lambda=g(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{Z})
. Applying the Delta method
(
Serfling 1980
)
to Theorem
2.1
, the following result follows.

Theorem 2.2

If
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i. i. d. according to
\textup{Exp}(\lambda)
, then
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{Exp}}
is asymptotically normally distributed, where the asymptotic variance and bias are given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{f,\textup{Exp}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{\mu_{f}(0,0,2)}{\mu_{f}^{2}(0,1,0)},\qquad\mathbb{B}_{f,\textup{Exp}}=\frac{\lambda}{2n}\cdot\frac{\mu_{f}(0,2,0)}{\mu_{f}^{2}(0,1,0)}.

The proof of Theorem
2.2
is provided by Appendix
A.2
. Note that the moments
\mu_{f}(k,l,m)
involved in Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
can sometimes be derived explicitly, see the subsequent examples, while they can be computed by using numerical integration otherwise.

After having derived the asymptotic variance and bias without explicitly specifying the function
f
, let us now consider the special case
f_{a}:[0;\infty)\to[0;\infty)
,
f_{a}(x)=x^{a}
, as our first illustrative example (where
a=1
leads to the default estimator
\hat{\lambda}=1/\overline{X}
). Here, we have to restrict to
a>0
to ensure that the condition
f(0)=0
in (
2.1
) holds. Using that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{a}]=\frac{\Gamma(a+1)}{\lambda^{a}}\quad\text{for }X\sim\textup{Exp}(\lambda)\text{ if }a>-1,

(2.5)

the following corollary to Theorem
2.2
is derived.

Corollary 2.3

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i. i. d. according to
\textup{Exp}(\lambda)
, and let
f_{a}(x)=x^{a}
with
a>\tfrac{1}{2}
. Then,
\hat{\lambda}_{f_{a},\textup{Exp}}
is asymptotically normally distributed, where the asymptotic variance and bias are given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{f_{a},\textup{Exp}}^{2}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{n}\binom{2(a-1)}{a-1},\qquad\mathbb{B}_{f_{a},\textup{Exp}}=\frac{\lambda}{2n}\binom{2a}{a}.

Furthermore, the MSE equals

\displaystyle\text{MSE}_{f_{a},\textup{Exp}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{f_{a},\textup{Exp}}^{2}+\mathbb{B}_{f_{a},\textup{Exp}}^{2}=\lambda^{2}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\binom{2(a-1)}{a-1}+\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\binom{2a}{a}^{2}\,\bigg].

The proof of (
2.5
) and Corollary
2.3
is provided by Appendix
A.3
. Note that in Corollary
2.3
,
\binom{r}{s}
denotes the generalized binomial coefficient given by
\Gamma(r+1)/\Gamma(s+1)/\Gamma(r-s+1)
.

As a second illustrative example, let us consider the functions
f_{u}(x)=1-u^{x}
with
u\in(0;1)
, which are again sublinear, but this time also bounded from above by one. Again, we can derive a corollary to Theorem
2.2
, this time by using the moment formula

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[u^{X}\big]=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-\ln(u)}\quad\text{for }X\sim\textup{Exp}(\lambda)\text{ if }u\in(0;1).

(2.6)

Corollary 2.4

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i. i. d. according to
\textup{Exp}(\lambda)
, and let
f_{u}(x)=1-u^{x}

u\in(0;1)
. Then,
\hat{\lambda}_{f_{u},\textup{Exp}}
is asymptotically normally distributed, where the asymptotic variance and bias are given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{f_{u},\textup{Exp}}^{2}=\frac{\lambda}{n}\frac{(\lambda-\ln(u))^{2}}{\lambda-2\ln(u)},\qquad\mathbb{B}_{f_{u},\textup{Exp}}=\frac{\sigma_{f_{u},\textup{Exp}}^{2}}{\lambda-\ln(u)}.

Furthermore, the MSE equals

\displaystyle\text{MSE}_{f_{u},\textup{Exp}}

\displaystyle=\Big(1+\tfrac{\lambda}{n(\lambda-2\ln(u))}\Big)\cdot\sigma_{f_{u},\textup{Exp}}^{2}.

The proof of (
2.6
) and Corollary
2.4
is provided by Appendix

.

We conclude this section by pointing out two further application scenarios for the use of Stein-MM estimators
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{Exp}}
. First, in analogy to recent Stein-based GoF-tests such as in
Weiß et al. 2023
,
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{Exp}}
might be used for GoF-applications. More precisely, the idea could be to select a set
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{K}\}
of weighting functions, and to compute
\hat{\lambda}_{f_{k}}
for all
k=1,\ldots,K
. As any
\hat{\lambda}_{f_{k}}
is a consistent estimator of
\lambda
according to Theorem
2.2
, the obtained values
\{\hat{\lambda}_{f_{1}},\ldots,\hat{\lambda}_{f_{K}}\}
should vary closely around
\lambda
. For other continuous distributions with positive support, such as the IG-distribution considered in the next Section
3
, we cannot expect that
\overline{f^{\prime}(X)}/\overline{f(X)}
has an (asymptotically) unique mean for different
f
, see Remark
3.1
, so a larger variation among the values in
\{\hat{\lambda}_{f_{1}},\ldots,\hat{\lambda}_{f_{K}}\}
is expected. Such a discrepancy in variation might give rise for a formal exponential GoF-test. But as the focus of this article is on parameter estimation, we postpone a detailed investigation of this GoF-application to future research.

A second type of application is illustrated by Table
1
, which refers to a simulation experiment with
10^{5}
replications per scenario. For simulated i. i. d.
\textup{Exp}(1)
-samples of sizes
n\in\{10,25,50,100\}
, about 10 % of the observations were randomly selected and contaminated by an additive outlier, namely by adding 5 to the selected observations. Note that the topic of outliers in exponential data received considerable interest in the literature
(
Johnson et al. 1995
, pp. 528–530)
. Then, different estimators
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{Exp}}
are computed from the contaminated data, where the first three choices of the weighting function
f
are characterized by a sublinear increase, whereas the fourth function,
f(x)=x
, corresponds to the default estimator
\hat{\lambda}=1/\overline{X}
. Table

shows that all MM estimators are affected by the outliers, e. g., in terms of the strong negative bias. But comparing the four columns of bias and MSE values, respectively, it gets clear that the novel Stein-MM estimators are more robust against the outliers, having both lower bias and MSE than
\hat{\lambda}
. Especially the choice
f(x)=\ln(1+x)
, a logarithmic weighting scheme, leads to a rather robust estimator. The relatively good performance of the Stein-MM estimators can be explained by the fact that the weighting functions increase sublinearly (which is also beneficial for bias reduction in non-contaminated data, recall the above discussion), so the effect of large observations is damped. To sum up, by choosing an appropriate weighting function
f
within the Stein-MM estimator
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{Exp}}
, one cannot only achieve a reduced bias and MSE, but also a reduced sensitivity towards outlying observations.

3
Stein Estimation of Inverse Gaussian Distribution

Like the exponential distribution considered in the previous Section
2
, the IG-distribution with parameters
\mu,\lambda>0
, abbreviated as
\textup{IG}(\mu,\lambda)
, has positive support, where the pdf is given by

\displaystyle\phi(x)\ =\ \sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}}\,e^{\lambda/\mu}\,x^{-3/2}\,\exp\!\bigg(-\frac{\lambda}{2\mu}\Big(\frac{x}{\mu}+\frac{\mu}{x}\Big)\bigg)\quad\text{for }x>0,\text{ and 0 otherwise}.

The IG-distribution is commonly used as a lifetime model (as it can be related to the first-passage time in random walks), but it may also simply serve as a distribution with positive skewness and, thus, as an alternative to, e. g., the lognormal distribution
(
Folks & Chhikara 1978
, see)
. Detailed surveys about the properties and applications of
\textup{IG}(\mu,\lambda)
, and on many further references, can be found in
Folks & Chhikara 1978
;
Seshadri 1999
as well as in
Johnson et al. 1995
. In what follows, the moment properties of
X\sim\textup{IG}(\mu,\lambda)
are particularly relevant. We have
\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu
,
\mathbb{E}[1/X]=1/\mu+1/\lambda
, and
\mathbb{V}[X]=\mu^{3}/\lambda
. In particular, positive and negative moments are related to each other by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[X^{-k}\Big]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X^{k+1}]}{\mu^{2k+1}}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathbb{E}\Big[(\mu/X)^{k}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[(X/\mu)^{k+1}\Big]\quad\text{for }k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(3.1)

see
Tweedie 1957a
as well as the aforementioned surveys.

Remark 3.1

At this point, let us briefly recall the discussion in Section
2
(p.
2
), where we highlighted the property that for i. i. d. exponential samples, the quotient
\overline{f^{\prime}(X)}/\overline{f(X)}
has an (asymptotically) unique mean for different
f
. From counterexamples, it is easily seen that this property is not true for
\textup{IG}(\mu,\lambda)
-data. The Delta method implies that the mean of
\overline{f^{\prime}(X)}/\overline{f(X)}
is asymptotically equal to
\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(X)]\big/\mathbb{E}[f(X)]
, which equals

•

1/\mathbb{E}[X]=1/\mu
for
f(x)=x
, but

•

2\mathbb{E}[X]/\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=2\mu\big/\big(\mu^{2}(1+\tfrac{\mu}{\lambda})\big)=2\lambda\big/\big(\mu(\lambda+\mu)\big)
for
f(x)=x^{2}
.

From now on, let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be an i. i. d. sample from
\textup{IG}(\mu,\lambda)
, which shall be used for parameter estimation. Here, one obviously estimates
\mu
by the sample mean
\overline{X}
, but the estimation of
\lambda
is more demanding. In the literature, the MM and ML estimation of
\lambda
have been discussed (see the details below), while our aim is to derive a generalized MM estimator with improved bias and MSE properties based on a Stein identity. In fact, as we shall see, our proposed approach can be understood as a unifying framework that covers the ordinary MM and ML estimator as special cases. A Stein identity for
\textup{IG}(\mu,\lambda)
has been derived by
Koudou & Ley 2014
, which states that

\displaystyle X\sim\textup{IG}(\mu,\lambda)\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathbb{E}\big[f(X)\,(\lambda X^{2}-\mu^{2}X-\lambda\mu^{2})\big]\ =\ 2\mu^{2}\ \mathbb{E}\big[X^{2}f^{\prime}(X)\big]

(3.2)

holds for all differentiable functions
f:(0;\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}f(x)\,\phi(x)=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}f(x)\,\phi(x)=0
. Solving (
3.2
) in
\lambda
and using the sample moments
\overline{h(X)}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(X_{i})
instead of
\mathbb{E}\big[h(X)\big]
(where
h
might be any of the functions involved in (
3.2
)), the class of Stein-MM estimators for
\lambda
is obtained as

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{IG}}=\frac{\,\overline{X}^{2}\big(2\,\overline{X^{2}f^{\prime}(X)}+\overline{Xf(X)}\,\big)\,}{\overline{X^{2}f(X)}-\overline{X}^{2}\,\overline{f(X)}}.

(3.3)

Here, the ordinary MM estimator of
\lambda>0
, i. e.,
\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{MM}}=\overline{X}^{3}/S^{2}
with
S^{2}
denoting the empirical variance
(
Tweedie 1957b
)
, is included as the special case
f\equiv 1
, whereas the ML estimator
\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{ML}}=\overline{X}\big/\big(\overline{X}\cdot\overline{1/X}-1\big)

(
Tweedie 1957a
)
follows for
f(x)=1/x
. Hence, (
3.3
) provides a unifying estimation approach that covers the established estimators as special cases.

Remark 3.2

At this point, a reference to Example 2.9 in
Ebner et al. 2023
is necessary. As already mentioned in Section
1
, also
Ebner et al. 2023
proposed a Stein-MM estimator for the IG-distribution, which, however, differs from the one developed here. The crucial difference is given by the fact that
Ebner et al. 2023
tried a joint estimation of
(\mu,\lambda)
based on (
3.2
), namely by jointly solving two equations that are implied by (
3.2
) if using two different weight functions
f_{1}\not=f_{2}
. The resulting class of estimators, however, does not cover the existing MM and ML estimators, so
Ebner et al. 2023
did not pursue the Stein-MM estimation of the IG-distribution further. By contrast, as we did not see notable potential for improving the estimation of
\mu
by
\overline{X}
(recall the diverse optimality properties of the sample mean as an estimator of the population mean
(
Shuster 1982
, e. g.,)
), we used (
3.2
) to only derive an estimator for
\lambda
. In this way, we were able to recover both the MM and ML estimator of
\lambda
within (
3.3
).

For deriving the asymptotic distribution of our general Stein-MM estimator
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{IG}}
from (
3.3
), we first define the vectors
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}
with
i=1,\ldots,n
as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}\coloneqq\Big(X_{i},\,f(X_{i}),\,X_{i}f(X_{i}),\,X_{i}^{2}f(X_{i}),\,X_{i}^{2}f^{\prime}(X_{i})\Big)^{\top}.

(3.4)

Their mean equals

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{Z}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}]=\Big(\mu,\,\mu_{f}(0,1,0),\,\mu_{f}(1,1,0),\,\mu_{f}(2,1,0),\,\mu_{f}(2,0,1)\Big)^{\top}.

(3.5)

Then, the following CLT holds.

Theorem 3.3

If
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i. i. d. according to
\textup{IG}(\mu,\lambda)
, then the sample mean
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}}
of
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{1},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{n}
according to (
3.4
) is asymptotically normally distributed as

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\big(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{Z}\big)\ \xrightarrow{\text{d}}\ \textup{N}\big(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\big)\quad\text{with}\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=(\sigma_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots,5},

where
\textup{N}(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})
denotes the multivariate normal distribution, and where the covariances are given as

\displaystyle\sigma_{11}=\mu^{3}/\lambda,

\displaystyle\sigma_{23}=\mu_{f}(1,2,0)-\mu_{f}(0,1,0)\cdot\mu_{f}(1,1,0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{12}=\mu_{f}(1,1,0)-\mu\cdot\mu_{f}(0,1,0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{24}=\mu_{f}(2,2,0)-\mu_{f}(0,1,0)\cdot\mu_{f}(2,1,0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{13}=\mu_{f}(2,1,0)-\mu\cdot\mu_{f}(1,1,0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{25}=\mu_{f}(2,1,1)-\mu_{f}(0,1,0)\cdot\mu_{f}(2,0,1),

\displaystyle\sigma_{14}=\mu_{f}(3,1,0)-\mu\cdot\mu_{f}(2,1,0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{34}=\mu_{f}(3,2,0)-\mu_{f}(1,1,0)\cdot\mu_{f}(2,1,0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{15}=\mu_{f}(3,0,1)-\mu\cdot\mu_{f}(2,0,1),

\displaystyle\sigma_{35}=\mu_{f}(3,1,1)-\mu_{f}(1,1,0)\cdot\mu_{f}(2,0,1),

\displaystyle\sigma_{22}=\mu_{f}(0,2,0)-\mu_{f}^{2}(0,1,0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{45}=\mu_{f}(4,1,1)-\mu_{f}(2,1,0)\cdot\mu_{f}(2,0,1),

\displaystyle\sigma_{33}=\mu_{f}(2,2,0)-\mu_{f}^{2}(1,1,0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{55}=\mu_{f}(4,0,2)-\mu_{f}^{2}(2,0,1),

\displaystyle\sigma_{44}=\mu_{f}(4,2,0)-\mu_{f}^{2}(2,1,0).

The proof of Theorem
3.3
is provided by Appendix
A.5
.

In the second step of deriving the asymptotics of
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{IG}}
, we define the function
g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5})\coloneqq x_{1}^{2}(2x_{5}+x_{3})/(x_{4}-x_{1}^{2}x_{2})
. Then,
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{IG}}=g(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}})
and
\lambda=g(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{Z})
. Applying the Delta method
(
Serfling 1980
)
to Theorem
3.3
, the following result follows.

Theorem 3.4

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i. i. d. according to
\textup{IG}(\mu,\lambda)
, and define
\vartheta_{f,\textup{IG}}\coloneqq\mu_{f}(2,1,0)-\mu^{2}\mu_{f}(0,1,0)
. Then,
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{IG}}
is asymptotically normally distributed, where the asymptotic variance and bias, respectively, are given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{f,\textup{IG}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Bigg[\,\frac{\mu^{4}}{\vartheta_{f}^{2}}\Bigg[\mu_{f}(2,2,0)-\mu_{f}(1,1,0)\Big(\frac{\lambda\vartheta_{f}}{\mu^{2}}+2\mu_{f}(2,0,1)\Big)+4\mu_{f}(3,1,1)+4\mu_{f}(4,0,2)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-4\mu_{f}^{2}(2,0,1)\Bigg]+\frac{2\lambda\mu}{\vartheta_{f}^{2}}\Bigg[\mu^{3}\Bigg(\mu_{f}(1,2,0)+2\mu_{f}(2,1,1)-\frac{\lambda\vartheta_{f}}{\mu^{2}}\cdot\mu_{f}(0,1,0)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\mu\Big(\mu_{f}(3,2,0)+2\mu_{f}(4,1,1)\Big)+\mu_{f}(2,1,0)\Big(4\mu_{f}(2,1,0)+4\mu_{f}(3,0,1)-\frac{\lambda\vartheta_{f}}{\mu}\Big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mu\vartheta_{f}^{2}}\Bigg[\mu^{5}\cdot\mu_{f}(0,2,0)+\mu^{3}\cdot\mu_{f}(0,1,0)\Big(\vartheta_{f}+\mu_{f}(2,1,0)\Big)-2\mu^{3}\cdot\mu_{f}(2,2,0)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+4\mu_{f}(2,1,0)\Big(\mu^{2}\cdot\mu_{f}(1,1,0)-\mu^{3}\cdot\mu_{f}(0,1,0)-\mu_{f}(3,1,0)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\mu\Big(3\mu_{f}^{2}(2,1,0)+\mu_{f}(4,2,0)\Big)\Bigg]\,\Bigg],

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{f,\textup{IG}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Bigg[\frac{1}{\mu\vartheta_{f}^{2}}\Bigg[\mu^{2}\cdot\mu_{f}(2,1,0)\Big(\mu_{f}(2,1,0)+3\mu^{2}\cdot\mu_{f}(0,1,0)\Big)+\lambda\bigg(\mu^{5}\cdot\mu_{f}(0,2,0)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\mu\cdot\mu_{f}(4,2,0)-2\mu_{f}(3,1,0)\cdot\mu_{f}(2,1,0)+4\mu^{2}\cdot\mu_{f}(1,1,0)\cdot\mu_{f}(2,1,0)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-2\mu^{3}\cdot\mu_{f}(2,2,0)\bigg)\Bigg]+\frac{\mu}{\vartheta_{f}^{3}}\Bigg[\mu_{f}(2,1,0)\Bigg(2\mu_{f}^{2}(2,1,0)-\mu\cdot\mu_{f}(3,2,0)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+4\mu_{f}(2,1,0)\cdot\mu_{f}(3,0,1)-2\mu\cdot\mu_{f}(4,1,1)+\mu^{3}\Big(\mu_{f}(1,2,0)+2\mu_{f}(2,1,1)\Big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\mu^{2}\cdot\mu_{f}(0,1,0)\Bigg(2\mu_{f}(3,1,0)\Big(2\mu_{f}(2,0,1)+\mu_{f}(1,1,0)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+2\mu_{f}^{2}(2,1,0)+4\mu_{f}(2,1,0)\cdot\mu_{f}(3,0,1)+\mu^{3}\Big(\mu_{f}(1,2,0)+2\mu_{f}(2,1,1)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\mu\Big(\mu_{f}(3,2,0)+2\mu_{f}(4,1,1)\Big)\Bigg)\Bigg]\Bigg].

The proof of Theorem
3.4
is provided by Appendix
A.6
.

Before we discuss the effect of
f
on bias and MSE of
\hat{\lambda}_{f,\textup{IG}}
, let us first consider the special cases of the ordinary MM and ML estimator. Their asymptotics are immediate consequences of Theorem
3.4
. For the MM estimator
\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{MM}}
, we have to choose
f\equiv 1
such that
f^{\prime}\equiv 0
. As a consequence,

\mu_{1}(k,l,m)=\mathbb{E}[X^{k}]\quad\text{if }\quad m=0,\quad\text{and 0 otherwise}.

(3.6)

This leads to a considerable simplification of Theorem
3.4
, see Appendix
A.7
, which is summarized in the following corollary.

Corollary 3.5

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i. i. d. according to
\textup{IG}(\mu,\lambda)
, then
\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{MM}}=\overline{X}^{3}/S^{2}
is asymptotically normally distributed with asymptotic variance
\sigma_{\textup{MM}}^{2}=\frac{2}{n}\,\lambda(\lambda+3\mu)
and bias
\mathbb{B}_{\textup{MM}}=\frac{3}{n}\,(\lambda+3\mu)
.

While we are not aware of a reference providing these asymptotics, they can be verified by using
Tweedie 1957b
. There, normal asymptotics for the reciprocal
1/\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{MM}}
are provided:
\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{MM}}^{-1}-\lambda^{-1}\big)\ \sim\textup{N}\big(0,\ 2(1+3\mu/\lambda)/\lambda^{2}\big)
. Applying the Delta method with
g(x)=1/x
and
g^{\prime}(x)=-1/x^{2}
to it, we conclude that
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{MM}}-\lambda)
has the asymptotic variance
\lambda^{4}\cdot 2(1+3\mu/\lambda)/\lambda^{2}=2\lambda\,(\lambda+3\mu)
like in Corollary
3.5
.

Next, we consider the special case of the ML estimator
\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{ML}}
, which follows by choosing
f(x)=1/x
such that
f^{\prime}(x)=-1/x^{2}
. Again, the joint moments
\mu_{f}(k,l,m)
simplify a lot:

\mu_{1/x}(k,l,m)=(-1)^{m}\,\mathbb{E}[X^{k-l-2m}]\quad\text{for all }k,l,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(3.7)

Together with Theorem
3.4
, see Appendix
A.8
, we get the following corollary.

Corollary 3.6

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i. i. d. according to
\textup{IG}(\mu,\lambda)
, then
\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{ML}}=\overline{X}\big/\big(\overline{X}\cdot\overline{1/X}-1\big)
is asymptotically normally distributed with asymptotic variance
\sigma_{\textup{ML}}^{2}=\frac{2}{n}\,\lambda^{2}
and bias
\mathbb{B}_{\textup{ML}}=\frac{3}{n}\,\lambda
.

Comparing Corollaries
3.5
and
3.6
, it is interesting to note that the MM estimator has larger asymptotic bias and variance than the ML estimator:
\sigma_{\textup{MM}}^{2}=\sigma_{\textup{ML}}^{2}+\tfrac{6\lambda\mu}{n}
and
\mathbb{B}_{\textup{MM}}=\mathbb{B}_{\textup{ML}}+\tfrac{9\mu}{n}
.
To verify the asymptotics of Corollary
3.6
, note that the ML estimator
\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{ML}}
has been shown to follow an inverted-
\chi^{2}
distribution:
\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{ML}}\ \sim\ n\,\lambda\cdot\text{Inv-}\chi^{2}_{n-1}

(see
Tweedie 1957a
, p. 368)
. Using the formulae for mean and variance of
\text{Inv-}\chi^{2}_{n-1}

(see
Bernardo & Smith 1994
, p. 431)
, we get

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{ML}}]=\tfrac{n}{n-3}\,\lambda=(1+\tfrac{3}{n-3})\,\lambda\approx(1+\tfrac{3}{n})\,\lambda,\quad\mathbb{V}[\hat{\lambda}_{\textup{ML}}]=\tfrac{2\,n^{2}}{(n-3)^{2}(n-5)}\,\lambda^{2}\approx\tfrac{2}{n}\,\lambda^{2}

for large
n
, which agrees with Corollary
3.6
.

4
Stein Estimation of Negative-binomial Distribution

While the previous sections (and also the research by
Ebner et al. 2023
) solely focussed on continuous distributions, let us now turn to the case of discrete-valued random variables. Here, the most relevant type are count random variables
X
, having a quantitative range contained in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The probably most well-known distributions for counts are Poisson and binomial distributions, both depending on the (normalized) mean as their only model parameter. But as already discussed in Section
3
, there is hardly any potential for finding a better estimator of the mean than the sample mean, so we do not further discuss these distributions. Instead, we focus on another popular count distribution, namely the NB-distribution with parameters
\nu>0
and
\pi\in(0;1)
, abbreviated as
\textup{NB}(\nu,\pi)
. Such
X\sim\textup{NB}(\nu,\pi)
has the range
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, probability mass function (pmf)
\mathbb{P}(X=x)=\binom{\nu+x-1}{x}\,(1-\pi)^{x}\,\pi^{\nu}
, and mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{\nu(1-\pi)}{\pi}
. By contrast to the equidispersed Poisson distribution, its variance
\sigma^{2}:=\mathbb{V}[X]=\frac{\nu(1-\pi)}{\pi^{2}}
is always larger than the mean (overdispersion), which is an important property for applications in practice. A detailed survey about the properties of and estimators for the
\textup{NB}(\nu,\pi)
-distribution can be found in
Johnson et al. 2005
. Instead of the original parametrization by
(\nu,\pi)
, it is often advantageous to consider either
(\mu,\nu)
or
(\mu,\pi)
, where
\nu
or
\pi
, respectively, serve as an additional dispersion parameter once the mean
\mu
has been fixed. In case of the
(\mu,\nu)
-parametrization, it holds that
\pi=\tfrac{\nu}{\nu+\mu}
and
\mathbb{V}[X]=\tfrac{\nu+\mu}{\nu}\,\mu
, whereas we get
\nu=\tfrac{\pi\,\mu}{1-\pi}
and
\mathbb{V}[X]=\tfrac{1}{\pi}\,\mu
for the
(\mu,\pi)
-parametrization. Besides the ease of interpretation, these parametrizations are advantageous in terms of parameter estimation. While MM estimation is rather obvious, namely
\mu
by
\overline{X}
and
\nu_{\textup{MM}}=\overline{X}^{2}/(S^{2}-\overline{X})
,
\pi_{\textup{MM}}=\overline{X}/S^{2}
, ML estimation is generally demanding as there does not exist a closed-form solution, see the discussion by
Kemp & Kemp 1987
, i. e., numerical optimization is necessary. However, there is an important exception:
the NB’s ML estimator of the mean
\mu
is given by
\overline{X}

(
Kemp & Kemp 1987
, p. 867)
,
i. e.,
\overline{X}
is both the MM and ML estimator with its known appealing performance. So it suffices to find an adequate estimator for
\nu
or
\pi
, respectively, the ML estimators of which do not have a closed-form expression.

These difficulties in estimating
\nu
or
\pi
, respectively, serve as our motivation for deriving a generalized MM estimator. For this purpose, we consider the NB’s Stein identity according to
Brown & Phillips 1999
, which can be expressed as either

\displaystyle X\sim\textup{NB}(\nu,\,\tfrac{\nu}{\nu+\mu})\quad\text{iff}

\displaystyle\quad\nu\,\mathbb{E}\big[X\,f(X)-\mu f(X+1)\big]=\mu\,\mathbb{E}\big[X\,\big(f(X+1)-f(X)\big)\big],

(4.1)

or

\displaystyle X\sim\textup{NB}(\tfrac{\pi\,\mu}{1-\pi},\,\pi)\quad\text{iff}

\displaystyle\quad\pi\,\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mu)\,f(X+1)\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[X\big(f(X+1)-f(X)\big)\big],

(4.2)

for any function
f
such that
\mathbb{E}\big[|f(X)|\big]
,
\mathbb{E}\big[|f(X+1)|\big]
exist. Note that the discrete difference
\Delta f(x):=f(x+1)-f(x)
in (
4
) and (
4
) plays a similar role as the continuous derivative
f^{\prime}(x)
in the previous Stein identities (
2.1
) and (
3.2
).

Stein-MM estimators are now derived by solving (
4
) in
\nu
or (
4
) in
\pi
, respectively, and by using again sample moments
\overline{h(X)}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(X_{i})
instead of the involved population moments
\mathbb{E}\big[h(X)\big]
(with
\mu
being estimated by
\overline{X}
).
As a result, the (closed-form) classes of Stein-MM estimators for
\nu
and
\pi
are obtained as

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{f,\textup{NB}}=\frac{\,\overline{X}\,\overline{X\,\Delta f(X)}\,}{\,\overline{X\,f(X)}-\overline{X}\,\overline{f(X+1)}\,},\qquad\hat{\pi}_{f,\textup{NB}}=\frac{\,\overline{X\,\Delta f(X)}\,}{\,\overline{X\,f(X+1)}-\overline{X}\,\overline{f(X+1)}\,}.

(4.3)

Note that the choice
f(x)=x
(hence
\Delta f(x)=1
) leads to the default MM estimators given above. The ML estimators are not covered by (
4.3
) this time, because they do not have a closed-form expression at all. Note, however, that the so-called “weighted-mean estimator” for
\nu
in (2.6) of
Kemp & Kemp 1987
, which was motivated as a kind of approximate ML estimator, is covered by (
4.3
), namely by choosing
f_{\alpha}(x)=\alpha^{x}
with
\alpha\in(0;1)
. It is also worth pointing to
Savani & Zhigljavsky 2006
, who define an estimator of
\nu
based on the moment
\overline{f(X)}
for some specified
f
; their approach, however, usually does not lead to a closed-form estimator.

For deriving the asymptotic distribution of the general Stein-MM estimator
\hat{\nu}_{f,\textup{NB}}
or
\hat{\pi}_{f,\textup{NB}}
, respectively, we first define the vectors
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}
with
i=1,\ldots,n
as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}\coloneqq\Big(X_{i},\ f(X_{i}+1),\ X_{i}\,f(X_{i}),\ X_{i}\,f(X_{i}+1)\Big)^{\top}.

(4.4)

Their mean equals

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{Z}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}]=\Big(\mu,\ \tilde{\mu}_{f}(0,0,1),\ \tilde{\mu}_{f}(1,1,0),\ \tilde{\mu}_{f}(1,0,1)\Big)^{\top},

(4.5)

where we define
\tilde{\mu}_{f}(k,l,m)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[X^{k}\cdot f(X)^{l}\cdot f(X+1)^{m}]
for any
k,l,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then, the following CLT holds.

Theorem 4.1

If
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i. i. d. according to a negative binomial distribution, then the sample mean

) is asymptotically normally distributed as

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\big(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{Z}\big)\ \xrightarrow{\text{d}}\ \textup{N}\big(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\big)\quad\text{with}\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=(\sigma_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots,4},

Our first special case shall be the function
f_{\alpha}(x)=\alpha^{x}
with
\alpha\in(0;1)
, which is inspired by
Kemp & Kemp 1987
. For evaluating the asymptotics in Theorems
4.1
–
4.3
, we need to compute the moments

\tilde{\mu}_{f_{\alpha}}(k,l,m)=\mathbb{E}[X^{k}\cdot f_{\alpha}(X)^{l}\cdot f_{\alpha}(X+1)^{m}]=\alpha^{m}\,\mathbb{E}[X^{k}\cdot\alpha^{(l+m)X}].

As shown in the following, this can be done by explicit closed-form expressions. The idea is to utilize the probability generating function (pgf) of the NB-distribution,

\text{pgf}\,(z)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[z^{X}]\ =\ \bigg(\frac{\pi}{1-(1-\pi)z}\bigg)^{\nu},

together with the following property:

\mathbb{E}\big[X_{(k)}\,z^{X}\big]\ =\ z^{k}\cdot\frac{d^{k}}{dz^{k}}\,\text{pgf}\,(z),

where
x_{(r)}\coloneqq x\cdots(x-r+1)
for
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
denote the falling factorials. The main result is summarized by the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4

Let
X\sim\textup{NB}(\nu,\,\pi)
. For the mixed factorial moments, we have

\mathbb{E}[X_{(k)}z^{X}]\ =\ \frac{(1-\pi)^{k}\,(\nu+k-1)_{(k)}\,z^{k}}{\big(1-(1-\pi)z\big)^{k}}\,\text{pgf}\,(z).

The proof of Lemma
4.4
is provided by Appendix
A.12

, where
S_{k,j}
are the Sterling numbers of the second kind
(see
Johnson et al. 2005
, p. 12)
. Then,
\mathbb{E}[X^{k}\cdot\alpha^{(l+m)X}]
follows by plugging-in
z=\alpha^{l+m}
into Lemma
4.4
.

5
Conclusions

In this article, we demonstrated how Stein characterizations of (continuous or discrete) distributions can be utilized to derive improved moment estimators of model parameters. The main idea is to choose an appropriate type of weighting function such that the resulting Stein-MM estimator has lower variance and bias than existing estimators. Here, the choice of the weighting function can be done based on asymptotic distributions: as the Stein-MM estimators are given by closed-form expressions with asymptotic normality, one can easily derive an optimal choice from a given family of weighting functions. This procedure was exemplified for three types of distribution: the exponential distribution, the inverse Gaussian distribution, and the negative-binomial distribution. For all these distribution families, we observed an appealing performance in various aspects, and we also demonstrated the application of our findings to real-world data examples.

Our research also gives rise to several directions for future research. While our main focus was on selecting the weight function with respect to minimal bias or variance, we also briefly pointed out in Section
2
that such a choice could also be motivated by robustness to outliers. In fact, there are some analogies to “M-estimation” as introduced by
Huber 1964
. It appears to be promising to analyze if robustified MM estimators can be achieved by suitable classes of weighting function. As another direction for future research (briefly sketched in Section
2
), the performance of GoF-tests based on Stein-MM estimators should be investigated. Finally, one should analyze Stein-MM estimators in a regression or time-series context.

The asymptotic normality immediately follows from the Lindeberg–Lévy CLT

A.3
Proof of Corollary
2.3

We start by proving (
2.5
). As the pdf of
X
is given by
\phi(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}
for
x>0
and zero otherwise, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{a}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{a}\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{a}\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(a+1)}{\lambda^{a}}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{a}\frac{\lambda^{a+1}}{\Gamma(a+1)}e^{-\lambda x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}_{=1}=\frac{\Gamma(a+1)}{\lambda^{a}},

where the integrand of the last integral is the pdf of a two-parameter gamma distribution, namely
\textup{Gamma}(a+1,\tfrac{1}{\lambda})
with
a>-1
and
\lambda>0
according to
Johnson et al. 1995
. Note that
\mathbb{E}[X^{a}]
corresponds to
\mu_{f_{a}}(0,1,0)
in our notation. In view of Theorem
2.2
, we also need the following moments, which are implied by (
2.5
):

\displaystyle\mu_{f_{a}}(0,2,0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[X^{2a}\big]=\frac{\Gamma(2a+1)}{\lambda^{2a}}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{if }a>-\tfrac{1}{2},

\displaystyle\mu_{f_{a}}(0,0,2)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[(aX^{a-1})^{2}\big]=a^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\big[X^{2(a-1)}\big]=a^{2}\,\frac{\Gamma(2a-1)}{\lambda^{2(a-1)}}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{if }a>\tfrac{1}{2}.

Inserting these expressions into Theorem
2.2
, we get

\displaystyle\sigma_{f_{a},\textup{Exp}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{\mu_{f_{a}}(0,0,2)}{\mu_{f_{a}}^{2}(0,1,0)}=\frac{a^{2}}{n}\frac{\lambda^{2a}}{\lambda^{2(a-1)}}\,\frac{\Gamma(2a-1)}{\Gamma(a+1)^{2}}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{n}\,\frac{\Gamma(2a-1)}{\Gamma(a)^{2}}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{n}\binom{2(a-1)}{a-1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{f_{a},\textup{Exp}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2n}\cdot\frac{\mu_{f_{a}}(0,2,0)}{\mu_{f_{a}}^{2}(0,1,0)}=\frac{\lambda}{2n}\frac{\Gamma(2a+1)}{\Gamma(a+1)^{2}}=\frac{\lambda}{2n}\binom{2a}{a}.

This completes the proof of Corollary
2.3
.

A.4
Proof of Corollary
2.4

Let us start by deriving (
2.6
). We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[u^{X}\big]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{x}\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{x\ln(u)}e^{-\lambda x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x(\lambda-\ln(u))}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-\ln(u)}.

This immediately leads to

\displaystyle\mu_{f_{u}}(0,1,0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[1-u^{X}\big]=1-\mathbb{E}\big[u^{X}\big]=\frac{-\ln(u)}{\lambda-\ln(u)},

\displaystyle\mu_{f_{u}}(0,2,0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[(1-u^{X})^{2}\big]=1-2\mathbb{E}\big[u^{X}\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[(u^{2})^{X}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{-\ln(u)-\lambda}{\lambda-\ln(u)}+\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-2\ln(u)}=\frac{2\ln(u)^{2}}{(\lambda-\ln(u))(\lambda-2\ln(u))},

\displaystyle\mu_{f_{u}}(0,0,2)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[(\ln(u)\,u^{X})^{2}\big]=\ln(u)^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\big[(u^{2})^{X}\big]=\frac{\lambda\ln(u)^{2}}{\lambda-2\ln(u)}.

Insertion into Theorem
2.2
leads to

\displaystyle\sigma_{f_{u},\textup{Exp}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{\mu_{f_{u}}(0,0,2)}{\mu_{f_{u}}^{2}(0,1,0)}=\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{\lambda\ln(u)^{2}}{\lambda-2\ln(u)}\,\frac{(\lambda-\ln(u))^{2}}{\ln(u)^{2}}=\frac{\lambda}{n}\frac{(\lambda-\ln(u))^{2}}{\lambda-2\ln(u)},

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{f_{u},\textup{Exp}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2n}\cdot\frac{\mu_{f_{u}}(0,2,0)}{\mu_{f_{u}}^{2}(0,1,0)}=\frac{\lambda}{2n}\,\frac{2\ln(u)^{2}}{(\lambda-\ln(u))(\lambda-2\ln(u))}\,\frac{(\lambda-\ln(u))^{2}}{\ln(u)^{2}}=\frac{\lambda}{n}\frac{\lambda-\ln(u)}{\lambda-2\ln(u)}.

Finally, the MSE equals

\displaystyle\text{MSE}_{f_{u},\textup{Exp}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{f_{u},\textup{Exp}}^{2}+\mathbb{B}_{f_{u},\textup{Exp}}^{2}=\frac{\lambda}{n}\frac{(\lambda-\ln(u))^{2}}{\lambda-2\ln(u)}+\bigg[\frac{\lambda}{n}\frac{\lambda-\ln(u)}{\lambda-2\ln(u)}\bigg]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{n}\frac{(\lambda-\ln(u))^{2}}{(\lambda-2\ln(u))^{2}}\,\bigg[\lambda-2\ln(u)+\frac{\lambda}{n}\bigg]=\frac{\lambda}{n}\frac{(\lambda-\ln(u))^{2}}{(\lambda-2\ln(u))^{2}}\,\bigg[\lambda(1+\tfrac{1}{n})-2\ln(u)\bigg].

This completes the proof of Corollary
2.4
.

A.12
Proof of Lemma
4.4

We proof Lemma
4.4
by induction.

Base case:
For
k=0
with
x_{(0)}=1
, we get
\mathbb{E}[z^{X}]=\text{pgf}\,(z)
.

Inductive step:
Let us assume that the statement holds for a given
k
. Then,

\mathbb{E}[X_{(k+1)}z^{X}]\ =\ z^{k+1}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{(k+1)}z^{X-k-1}]=z^{k+1}\,\frac{d}{dz}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{(k)}z^{X-k}].

By the induction hypothesis, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{(k+1)}z^{X}]

\displaystyle=z^{k+1}\,\frac{d}{dz}\,\frac{(1-\pi)^{k}(\nu+k-1)_{(k)}}{(1-(1-\pi)z)^{k}}\,\bigg(\frac{\pi}{1-(1-\pi)z}\bigg)^{\nu}

\displaystyle=z^{k+1}\,(1-\pi)^{k}(\nu+k-1)_{(k)}\pi^{\nu}\cdot\frac{d}{dz}\,(1-(1-\pi)z)^{-(\nu+k)}

\displaystyle=z^{k+1}\,(1-\pi)^{k}(\nu+k-1)_{(k)}\pi^{\nu}\cdot(\nu+k)(1-\pi)\,(1-(1-\pi)z)^{-(\nu+k+1)}

\displaystyle=z^{k+1}\frac{(1-\pi)^{k+1}(\nu+k)_{(k+1)}}{(1-(1-\pi)z)^{k+1}}\,\text{pgf}\,(z).

This completes the proof of Lemma
4.4
.
