From DK-STP to Non-square General Linear Algebra and General Linear Group
Daizhan Cheng

Abstract
A new matrix product, called dimension keeping semi-tensor product (DK-STP), is proposed. Under DK-STP, the set of m × n m\times n matrices becomes a semi-group ( G ⁡ ( m × n , 𝔽 ) G(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}}) ), and a ring, denoted by R ⁡ ( m × n , 𝔽 ) R(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}}) . Then the action of semi-group G ⁡ ( m × n , 𝔽 ) G(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}}) on dimension-free Euclidian space, denoted by ℝ ∞ {\mathbb{R}}^{\infty} , is discussed. This action leads to discrete-time and continuous time S-systems. Their trajectories are calculated, and their invariant subspaces are revealed. Through this action, some important concepts for square matrices, such as eigenvalue, eigenvector, determinant, invertibility, etc., have been extended to non-square matrices. Particularly, it is surprising that the famous Cayley-Hamilton theory can also been extended to non-square matrices. Finally, the Lie bracket can also be defined, which turns the set of m × n m\times n matrices into a Lie algebra, called non-square (or STP) general linear algebra, denoted by g ​ l ​ ( m × n , 𝔽 ) gl(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}}) . Moreover, a Lie group, called the non-square (or STP) general Lie group and denoted by G ​ L ​ ( m × n , 𝔽 ) GL(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}}) , is constructed, which has g ​ l ​ ( m × n , 𝔽 ) gl(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}}) as its Lie algebra. Their relationship with classical Lie group G ​ L ​ ( m , 𝔽 ) GL(m,{\mathbb{F}}) and Lie algebra g ​ l ​ ( m , 𝔽 ) gl(m,{\mathbb{F}}) has also been revealed.

Second, instead of

For two matrices
A,B
, if the column number of
A
equals the row number of
B
, then the classical matrix product is defined. In this case we say that
A
and
B
satisfy dimension matching condition. All the STPs, including MM-STPs, MV-STPs, and VV-STPs, are generalizations of the corresponding classical products in linear algebra. That is, when the required dimension matching condition is satisfied, they coincide with the classical matrix (vector) products.

Moreover, a significant advantage of STPs is: they keep the fundamental properties of the classical MM, NV, or VV products available.
This advantage makes the usage of STPs very convenient. Hence they received wide applications in many fields.

The basic idea for all STPs is the same, which can be described as follows: When the dimension matching condition for factor elements (matrices or vectors) does not satisfied, we use certain matrices, such as
I_{n}
, to enlarge the matrices or certain vector, such as
{\bf 1}_{n}
, to enlarge the vectors through Kronicker product. Eventually, the enlarged matrices or vectors satisfy dimension matching condition, and then the conventional products of the enlarged matrices or vectors are considered as the STP of the original matrices or vectors. Roughly speaking, the enlargements change the sizes of the matrices or vectors, but they do not change the “information” contained in the original matrices or vectors. This fact makes STPs meaningful. That is, the STP represents the “product” of original matrices or vectors in certain sense.

In addition to many engineering or dynamic system related applications of STP, a challenging theoretical problem is how to describe the action of matrices of various dimensions on vector spaces of various dimensions. Because STPs have removed the dimension restriction of matrix-matrix or matrix-vector products, this action becomes dimension-varying (or overall, dimension-free). To explore such dimension-free actions, we first introduce some new concepts, which provide a framework for such dimension-free actions.

Consider the set of matrices with arbitrary dimensions as
[
10
]

{\mathcal{M}}=\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n},

and the dimension-free Euclidian space is defined as

{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}:=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

Then

becomes a monoid (i.e., semi-group with identity); the action of

on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}

\vec{\ltimes}:{\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
(or

[

]

is an inner product over

.

Recently, this kind of systems have been developed into dynamic systems over dimension-free manifold

The purpose of this paper is to propose a new STP, called the dimension keeping STP (DK-STP) and denoted by

\ltimes

. Dimension keeping means if both two factor matrices are of the same dimension, say, they are in
{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}

a ring, denoted by
R(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})

The group action of
G(m\times n,{\mathbb{R}})
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
is then explored. Based on this action, the corresponding dynamic systems are also proposed and investigated in detail. As byproducts, some basic concepts of square matrices have been extended to non-square matrices. They are:
eigenvalues, eigenvectors, determinant, invertibility, etc. The Cayley-Hamilton theorem has also be extended to non-square matrices.

Finally, a Lie bracket is defined to produce a Lie algebra, called non-square (or STP) general linear algebra, denoted by
gl(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
. Certain properties are obtained. Starting from this Lie algebra, its corresponding non-square (or STP) general linear group, denoted by
GL(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
can be deduced. The outline of this paper is depicted in Figure

II
DK-STP

Definition II.1

Let
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
and
B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p\times q}
,
t=\lcm(n,p)
. The DK-STP of
A
and
B
, denoted by
A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times q}
, is defined as follows.

\displaystyle A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B:=\left(A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n}\right)\left(B\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p}\right).

(11)

Remark II.2

(i)

It is easy to verify that when the dimension matching condition is satisfied, i.e.,
n=p
, the DK-STP coincides with classical matrix product. Hence, similarly to two kinds of MM-STPs, the DK-STP is also a generalization of classical matrix product.

(ii)

The two kinds of MM-STPs are not suitable for matrix-vector product, because in general the results are not vectors. Hence they can not realize linear mappings over vector spaces, and the two corresponding MV-STPs have been established to perform linear mappings. Unlike them, DK-STP can realize MM-product and MV-product simultaneously.

(iii)

Comparing with the MM-STP defined in Definition
I.1

[
6
,
7
]
, this DK-STP has minimum size
m\times q
, no matter whether the dimension matching condition is satisfied. That is why the product is named as dimension keeping STP.

(iv)

If two matrices
A
and
B
have the same dimension, the dimension of their DK-STP remains the same. This is a nice property.

Remark II.3

(i)

Definition
II.5
implies that the block-multiplication rule for DK-STP is available.

Lemma II.8

Let
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
r=\gcd(m,n)
. Divide both
x
and
y
into
r
equal parts as

x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{r})
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{r})
. Then

\displaystyle x~\vec{\cdot}~y=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}~\vec{\cdot}~y_{i}.

(28)

Proof.
Note that
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{m/r}
and
y_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n/r}
. Let
t=\lcm(m,n)
and
t^{\prime}=\lcm(m/r,n/r)
. Then
t^{\prime}=t/r
. Hence

\frac{t}{m}=\frac{t^{\prime}}{m/r}~;\quad\frac{t}{n}=\frac{t^{\prime}}{n/r}.

Using it, a straightforward computation verifies (
28
).

\Box

Using Lemma
II.8
, the following result is easily verifiable.

Proposition II.9

Let
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
,
B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p\times q}
, and
r=\gcd(p,q)
. Split
A
into
r
equal size rows and

into

equal size columns as

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1,1}&A_{1,2}&\cdots&A_{1,r}\\
A_{2,1}&A_{2,2}&\cdots&A_{2,r}\\
\vdots&~&~&~\\
A_{\ell,1}&A_{\ell,2}&\cdots&A_{\ell,r}\\
\end{bmatrix}

and

B=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1,1}&B_{1,2}&\cdots&B_{1,\mu}\\
B_{2,1}&B_{2,2}&\cdots&B_{2,\mu}\\
\vdots&~&~&~\\
B_{r,1}&B_{r,2}&\cdots&A_{r,\mu}\\
\end{bmatrix},

where

and

could be arbitrary. The row size of

(as well as the column size of

Then the block multiplication rule is correct. That is, Let

Lemma II.11

Let
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
. Then

(i)

\displaystyle A\otimes{\bf 1}_{\alpha}^{T}=A\left(I_{n}\otimes{\bf 1}_{\alpha}^{T}\right).

(29)

(ii)

\displaystyle A\otimes{\bf 1}_{\beta}=\left(I_{m}\otimes{\bf 1}_{\beta}\right)A.

(30)

Proof.

(i)

\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{RHS of (\ref{2.3})}=A\left(I_{n}\otimes{\bf 1}_{\alpha}^{T}\right)\\
=\left(A\otimes 1\right)\left(I_{n}\otimes{\bf 1}_{\alpha}^{T}\right)\\
=A\otimes{\bf 1}_{\alpha}^{T}=\mbox{LHS of (\ref{2.3})}.\end{array}

(ii)

\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{RHS of (\ref{2.4})}=\left(I_{m}\otimes{\bf 1}_{\beta}\right)A\\
=\left(I_{m}\otimes{\bf 1}_{\beta}\right)\left(A\otimes 1\right)\\

\Box

Proposition III.1

(i)

(Distributivity)

(Associativity)

\displaystyle(A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,C=A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,(B\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,C).

(57)

The proof of (i) is straightforward.

To prove (ii), let

(ii)

(i)

\begin{array}[]{l}A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,(BC)=A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,\left[B\Col_{1}(C),\cdots,B\Col_{t}(C)\right]\\

Taking transpose yields (

IV
DK-STP Ring

Recall that if
A,B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
then

A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
.
This fact makes

\ltimes

a dimension invariant operator over
{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
. Taking Proposition
III.1
into consideration, the following claim is obvious.

Proposition IV.1

\left({\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n},+,\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,\right)
is a ring, denoted by
R(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
.

Remark IV.2

According to
[
25
]
,
(R,\times,+)
is a ring, if

(i)

(R,+)
is an abelian group;

(ii)

(R,\times)
is a semi-group;

(iii)

(Distributivity)

\begin{array}[]{l}(a+b)\times c=a\times c+b\times c,\\
a\times(b+c)=a\times b+a\times c.\end{array}

Following this definition, it is straightforward to verify Proposition
IV.1
. (Some other references, say,
[
29
]
, require
(R,\times)
to be a monoid (semi-group with identity).)

Remark IV.3

It is ready to verify that
({\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n},+,\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,)
,
({\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n},+,\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{w})
, and
({\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n},+,\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{w})
are also rings, denoted by
R_{\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
,
R^{w}_{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
, and
R^{w}_{\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
respectively. Hence, we also understand that
R_{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})=R(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
. All these rings are called DK-STP rings. But for statement ease, hereafter the default one is
R(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})=R_{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
.

Consider the sub-rings of DK-STP ring.

Definition IV.4

[
25
]
Let
(R,+,*)
be a ring and
H\subset R
. If
(H,+,*)
is also a ring, it is a sub-ring of
R
.

In the following some examples for sub-rings of DK-STP ring are presented.

Example IV.5

Consider DK-STP ring
R(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
.

(i)

Let
{\bf r}=(r_{1},\cdots,r_{s})\subset[1,m]
.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{(m\backslash{\bf r})\times n}:=\left\{A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}\;|\;\Row_{r}(A)=0,\quad r\in{\bf r}\right\}.

(61)

Then it is ready to verify that

{\mathcal{M}}_{(m\backslash{\bf r})\times n}\subset{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}

is a sub-ring.

(ii)

Let
{\bf r}=(r_{1},\cdots,r_{s})\subset[1,n]
.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times(n\backslash{\bf r})}:=\left\{A\in{\bf M}_{m\times n}\;|\;\Col_{r}(A)=0,\quad r\in{\bf r}\right\}.

(62)

Then

{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times(n\backslash{\bf r})}\subset{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}

is a sub-ring.

(iii)

Let
{\bf r}=(r_{1},\cdots,r_{\alpha})\subset[1,m]
and
{\bf s}=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{\beta})\subset[1,n]
.

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathcal{M}}_{(m\backslash{\bf r})\times(n\backslash{\bf s})}:=\left\{A\in{\bf M}_{m\times n}\;|\;\right.\\
~~\left.\Col_{r}(A)=0,\Row_{s}=0\quad r\in{\bf r},s\in{\bf s}\right\}.\end{array}

Then

{\mathcal{M}}_{(m\backslash{\bf r})\times(n\backslash{\bf s})}\subset{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}

is a sub-ring.

(iv)

The above arguments are also true for
R_{\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
,
R^{w}_{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
,
R^{w}_{\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
.

Next, we consider the ring homomorphism.

Definition IV.6

[
25
]
Let
(R_{i},+_{i},*_{i})
,
i=1,2
be two rings.

(i)

\phi:R_{1}\rightarrow R_{2}
is a ring homomorphism, if

\displaystyle\phi(r+_{1}s)=\phi(r)+_{2}\phi(s),\quad r,s\in R_{1}.

(65)

and

\displaystyle\phi(r*_{1}s)=\phi(r)*_{2}\phi(s),\quad r,s\in R_{1}.

(66)

(ii)

\phi:R_{1}\rightarrow R_{2}
is a ring isomorphism, if it is a one-to-one and onto homomorphism. Moreover, its inverse
\phi^{-1}:R_{2}\rightarrow R_{1}
is also a ring homomorphism.

(iii)

If
\phi:R\rightarrow R
is a ring isomorphism, it is called an automorphism.

Lemma IV.7

(i)

\displaystyle{\bf 1}_{p\times q}={\bf 1}_{p}\otimes{\bf 1}_{q}^{T}={\bf 1}_{q}^{T}\times{\bf 1}_{p}.

(67)

(ii)

\displaystyle{\bf 1}_{p\times q}\otimes{\bf 1}_{s}={\bf 1}_{s}\otimes{\bf 1}_{p\times q}.

(68)

(iii)

\displaystyle{\bf 1}_{p\times q}\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{s}={\bf 1}^{T}_{s}\otimes{\bf 1}_{p\times q}.

(69)

(iv)

\displaystyle J^{2}_{s}=J_{s}.

(70)

Proof.

(i)

It can be proved by a straightforward verification.

(ii)

Using (i), we have

\begin{array}[]{l}{\bf 1}_{p\times q}\otimes{\bf 1}_{s}=\left({\bf 1}_{p}\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{q}\right)\otimes{\bf 1}_{s}\\
={\bf 1}_{p}\otimes{\bf 1}_{s}\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{q}\\
={\bf 1}_{s}\otimes{\bf 1}_{p}\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{q}\\
={\bf 1}_{s}\otimes{\bf 1}_{p\times q}\\
\end{array}

(iii)

The proof is similar to the one for (ii).

(iv)

It can be verified by a straightforward calculation.

\Box

As special cases of (
67
)-(
69
) we have

\displaystyle J_{p}=\frac{1}{p}{\bf 1}_{p}\otimes{\bf 1}_{p}^{T}=\frac{1}{p}{\bf 1}^{T}_{p}\otimes{\bf 1}_{p}.

(71)

\displaystyle J_{p}\otimes{\bf 1}_{s}={\bf 1}_{s}\otimes J_{p}.

(72)

\displaystyle J_{p}\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{s}={\bf 1}^{T}_{s}\otimes J_{p}.

(73)

The following Theorem is fundamental for
R(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
homomorphism.

Theorem IV.8

(i)

Let
\pi_{1}:{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{sm\times sn}
be defined by

\pi_{1}:A\mapsto J_{s}\otimes A.

Then
\pi_{1}
is a ring homomorphism.

(ii)

Let
\pi_{2}:{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{sm\times sn}
be defined by

\pi_{2}:A\mapsto A\otimes J_{s}.

Then
\pi_{2}
is a ring homomorphism.

(iii)

\displaystyle\pi_{1}(R(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}}))\cong\pi_{2}(R(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})).

(74)

Proof.
Let
A,B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
.

(i)

Since
\pi_{i}
,
i=1,2
are linear mappings, it is obvious that

\pi_{i}(A+B)=\pi_{i}(A)+\pi_{i}(B),\quad i=1,2.

Set
t=\lcm(m,n)
, then

\begin{array}[]{l}\pi_{1}(A)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,\pi_{1}(B)=\\
(J_{s}\otimes A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n})(J_{s}\otimes B\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p})\\
=J_{s}^{2}\otimes(A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B)=J_{s}\otimes(A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B)=\pi_{1}(A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B).\end{array}

(ii)

\begin{array}[]{l}\pi_{2}(A)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,\pi_{2}(B)\\
=\left(A\otimes J_{s}\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n}\right)\left(B\otimes J_{s}\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p}\right)\\
=\left(A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n}\otimes J_{s}\right)\left(A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n}\otimes J_{s}\right)\\
=\left(A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n}\right)\left(B\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p}\right)\otimes J^{2}_{s}\\
=\left(A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n}\right)\left(B\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p}\right)\otimes J_{s}\\
=\pi_{2}(A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B).\end{array}

We conclude that

R(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})\simeq R(sm\times sn,{\mathbb{F}}).

(3)

Define

\varphi(A\otimes J_{s})=J_{s}\otimes A,\quad A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}.

We show that
\varphi
is an isomorphism.

\begin{array}[]{l}\varphi[(A\otimes J_{s})+(B\otimes J_{s})]\\
=\varphi[(A+B)\otimes J_{s}]\\
=J_{s}\otimes(A+B)\\
=\varphi[A\otimes J_{s}]+\varphi[B\otimes J_{s}],\\
\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{l}\varphi[(A\otimes J_{s})\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,(B\otimes J_{s})]\\
=\varphi[(A\otimes J_{s}\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n})(B\otimes J_{s}\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p})]\\
=\varphi[(A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n}\otimes J_{s})(B\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p}\otimes J_{s})]\\
=\varphi[((A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n})(B\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p}))\otimes J_{s})]\\
=J_{s}\otimes((A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n})(B\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p}))\\
=(J_{s}\otimes A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n})(J_{s}\otimes B\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p}))\\
=\varphi(A)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,\varphi(B).\end{array}

Hence
\varphi
is a ring homomorphism.

To see
\varphi
is an isomorphism, one sees easily that
\varphi
is one-to-one and onto. Hence, we have only to show that
\varphi^{-1}
is also an homomorphism. We have

\begin{array}[]{l}\varphi^{-1}[(J_{s}\otimes A)+(J_{s}\otimes B)]\\
=\varphi^{-1}[J_{s}\otimes(A+B)]\\
=(A+B)\otimes J_{s}\\
=\varphi^{-1}[J_{s}\otimes A]+\varphi^{-1}[J_{s}\otimes B],\\
\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{l}\varphi^{-1}[(J_{s}\otimes A)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,(J_{s}\otimes B)]\\
=\varphi^{-1}[(J_{s}\otimes A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n})(J_{s}\otimes B\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p})]\\
=\varphi^{-1}[J^{2}_{s}\otimes(A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n})(B\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p})]\\
=((A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n})(B\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p}))\otimes J_{s}\\
=(A\otimes{\bf 1}^{T}_{t/n}\otimes J_{s})(B\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/p}\otimes J_{s})\\
=\varphi^{-1}(A)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,\varphi^{-1}(B).\end{array}

Hence
\varphi^{-1}
is also a ring homomorphism. We conclude that
\varphi
is a ring isomorphism.

\Box

Theorem IV.10

Consider the ring
R(m\times n,{\mathbb{R}})
. Let
t=\lcm(m,n)
,
r=\gcd(m,n)
,
a=m/r
,
b=n/r
, and

be an orthogonal matrix, i.e.,
M_{r}^{T}=M_{r}^{-1}
.
Then
\psi:{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
, defined by

\displaystyle A\mapsto\left(M_{r}\otimes I_{a}\right)A\left(M^{T}_{r}\otimes I_{b}\right),\quad A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n},

(75)

is a ring automorphism.

Proof.

First, we show
\psi
is a ring homomorphism.
It is obvious that

\psi(A+B)=\psi(A)+\psi(B),\quad A,B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}.

We prove

\displaystyle\psi(A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B)=\psi(A)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,\psi(B),\quad A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}.

(76)

Note that

\begin{array}[]{l}\psi(A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B)=[(M_{r}\otimes I_{a})A]\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,[B(M^{T}_{r}\otimes I_{b})]\\
=(M_{r}\otimes I_{a})[A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B](M^{T}_{r}\otimes I_{b})\\
=[(M_{r}\otimes I_{a})A\Psi_{n\times m}B(M^{T}_{r}\otimes I_{b})],\\
\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{l}\psi(A)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,\psi(B)=[(M_{r}\otimes I_{a})A(M_{r}^{T}\otimes I_{b}]\Psi_{n\times m}\\
~~[(M_{r}\otimes I_{a})B(M^{T}_{r}\otimes I_{b})]\\
=(M_{r}\otimes I_{a})A[(M_{r}^{T}\otimes I_{b}]\Psi_{n\times m}(M_{r}\otimes I_{a})B(M^{T}_{r}\otimes I_{b}).\end{array}

Hence, to prove (
76
), it is enough to show

\displaystyle(M_{r}^{T}\otimes I_{b}]\Psi_{n\times m}(M_{r}\otimes I_{a})=\Psi_{n\times m}.

(77)

\begin{array}[]{l}(M_{r}^{T}\otimes I_{b}]\Psi_{n\times m}(M_{r}\otimes I_{a})\\
=(M_{r}^{T}\otimes I_{b}\otimes{\bf 1}_{a}^{T})(M_{r}\otimes I_{a}\otimes{\bf 1}_{b})\\
=I_{r}\otimes(I_{b}\otimes{\bf 1}_{a}^{T})(I_{a}\otimes{\bf 1}_{b})\\
=(I_{r}\otimes I_{b}\otimes{\bf 1}_{a}^{T})(I_{r}\otimes I_{a}\otimes{\bf 1}_{b})\\
=(I_{n}\otimes{\bf 1}_{a}^{T})(I_{m}\otimes{\bf 1}_{b})\\
=\Psi_{n\times m}.\end{array}

Next, we show that
\psi
is a ring isomorphism. It is enough to show that
\psi^{-1}
exists and is also a ring homomorphism. Define
\phi:{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
by

A\mapsto\left(M^{T}_{r}\otimes I_{a}\right)A\left(M_{r}\otimes I_{b}\right),\quad A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}.

Then it is obvious that
\phi
is also a ring homomorphism. Moreover,
\phi=\psi^{-1}
.

\Box

\displaystyle R(m\times n,{\mathbb{C}})\cong R(n\times m,{\mathbb{C}}).

(79)

Remark IV.14

Proposition
IV.13
is obviously true for
R_{\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
,
R^{w}_{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
, and
R^{w}_{\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
.

V-A

Dimension-Free Euclidian Space

This subsection presents a brief review for dimension-free Euclidian space, which provides a state space for dimension-varying dynamic systems. The dimension-varying linear (control) systems have been investigated in
[
9
,
10
,
12
]
, the dimension-varying non-linear (control) systems have been investigated in
[
18
]
.

Recall that the dimension-free Euclidian space is constructed by
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Definition V.1

[
18
]
Assume
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
, which are specified as
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
\lcm(m,n)=t
. Then the addition (subtraction) of
x
and
y
is defined by

\displaystyle x\vec{\pm}y:=\left(x\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/m}\right)\pm\left(y\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/n}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{t}.

(80)

With the addition (subtraction), defined by (
80
) and conventional scalar product,
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
becomes a pseudo-vector space, which satisfies all the requirements of a vector space except that
x\vec{-}y=0
does not imply
x=y

[
1
]
.

Definition V.2

[
18
]
Assume
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
, which are specified as
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
\lcm(m,n)=t
.

(i)

The inner product of
x,~y
is defined by

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\left<x,y\right>_{{\mathcal{V}}}:=\frac{1}{t}x\vec{\cdot}y\\
=\frac{1}{t}\left(x\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/m}\right)^{T}\left(y\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/n}\right).\end{array}

(ii)

The norm of
x
is defined by

\displaystyle\|x\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}:=\sqrt{\|\left<x,x\right>_{{\mathcal{V}}}\|}.

(83)

(iii)

The distance of
x
and
y
is defined by

\displaystyle d_{{\mathcal{V}}}(x,y):=\|x\vec{-}y\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}.

(84)

With the distance, defined by (
84
),
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
becomes a topological space with the distance deduced topology. (But it is not Hausdorff.)

x
and
y
are said to be equivalent, if
x\vec{-}y=0
(or equivalently,
d_{{\mathcal{V}}}(x,y)=0
), denoted by
x\leftrightarrow y
. Define

\displaystyle\Omega:={\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}/\leftrightarrow.

(85)

Denote by

\bar{x}:=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}\;|\;y\leftrightarrow x\}.

\displaystyle\bar{x}\vec{\pm}\bar{y}:=\overline{x\vec{\pm}y},\quad\bar{x},\bar{y}\in\Omega.

(86)

Then (
86
) is properly defined. Moreover, with this addition (subtraction) and conventional scalar product,
\Omega
becomes a vector space.
Moreover, define

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left<\bar{x},\bar{y}\right>_{{\mathcal{V}}}:=\left<x,y\right>_{{\mathcal{V}}},\quad x\in\bar{x},\;y\in\bar{y},\\
\|\bar{x}\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}:=\|x\|_{{\mathcal{V}}},\quad x\in\bar{x},\\
d_{{\mathcal{V}}}(\bar{x},\bar{y}):=d_{{\mathcal{V}}}(x,y),\quad x\in\bar{x},\;y\in\bar{y}.\\
\end{cases}

(87)

The inner product, norm, and distance on
\Omega
are all properly defined, which turn
\Omega
a (Hausdorff) topological vector space
[
28
]
.

Recall that
{\mathcal{M}}:=\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
is the set of matrices, which acts on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
by

x\mapsto A\vec{\ltimes}x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
as defined by (
3
), (or
x\mapsto A\vec{\circ}x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
as defined by (
5
)). Hereafter, for statement ease, only the first type of action is considered. In fact, almost all the arguments are also applicable to the second type action.

The action (
3
) (as well as (
5
)) is called a group action, which means
({\mathcal{M}},\ltimes)
is a monoid, and the action satisfies the following properties:

\displaystyle(A\ltimes B)\vec{\ltimes}x=A\vec{\ltimes}(B\vec{\ltimes}x);

(88)

and

\displaystyle E\vec{\ltimes}x=x,

(89)

where
E=1\in{\mathcal{M}}
is the identity.

Using action (
3
), a dynamic system can be defined as

\displaystyle x(t+1)=A(t)\vec{\ltimes}x(t),\quad x(0)=x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty},

(90)

which is called a semi-group system (or S-system)
[
32
]
.

Moreover, it is also a dynamic system, that is,
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
is a topological space, and for a fixed
A
,
x\mapsto A\vec{\ltimes}x
(as a mapping:
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
) is continuous
[
37
]
.
The only inferior is: the state space
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
is not Hausdorff. To overcome this, we turn to quotient space as follows.

Let
A,B\in{\mathcal{M}}
.
A,B
are said to be equivalent, denoted by
A\sim B
, if there exist identity matrices
I_{m}
and
I_{n}
, such that

A\otimes I_{m}=B\otimes I_{n}.

Denote by

\left<A\right>:=\{B\in{\mathcal{M}}\;|\;B\sim A\},

and the set of equivalence classes is denoted by

\displaystyle\Sigma:={\mathcal{M}}/\sim.

(91)

Then the action of
{\mathcal{M}}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
can be transferred to the action of
\Sigma
on
\Omega
by

\displaystyle<A>\vec{\ltimes}\bar{x}:=\left<A\vec{\ltimes}x\right>,\quad A\in\left<A\right>,\;x\in\bar{x}.

(92)

The dynamic system (
87
) can also be transferred to
\Omega
as

\displaystyle\bar{x}(t+1)=\left<A(t)\right>\vec{\ltimes}\bar{x}(t),\quad\bar{x}(0)\in\Omega,\;\left<A(t)\right>\in\Sigma,

(93)

which surely needs to be well posed, that can be proved.

Now (
93
) is a dynamic system over a vector space
\Sigma
, which is also a Hausdorff space
[
18
]
. A detailed argument can be found in
[
11
,
18
]
.

V-B

DK-STP based Group Action of Matrices on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}

In previous subsection one sees that unlike classical linear system over
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, to construct dynamic systems over
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
, we need both MM-STP and MV-STP. Fortunately, when DK-STP is used, we go back to the classical situation, where one operator

\ltimes

is enough for both matrix-matrix product and matrix-vector product. This is an advantage of DK-STP.

Consider the following dynamic system

\displaystyle x(t+1)=A(t)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,x(t),\quad x(0)=x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty},\;A(t)\in{\mathcal{M}}.

(94)

According to Proposition
III.1
, it is clear that (
94
) is an S-system. To see it is also a dynamic system, we have to estimate the norm of
A\in{\mathcal{M}}
with respect to DK-STP.

Definition V.3

Let
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}\subset{\mathcal{M}}
. The DK-norm of
A
, denoted by
\|A\|_{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}
, is defined by

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}=\sup_{0\neq x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}}\frac{\|A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,x\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}}{\|x\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}}.

(95)

This norm can be calculated as follows.

Proposition V.4

The DK-norm of
A
, defined by (
95
) is

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{m}\|\Row_{j}(A)\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{V}}}}.

(96)

Proof.
Denote by

y=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{m})^{T}=A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,x.

Using Schwarz inequality, we have

y_{j}=\Row^{T}_{j}(A)~\vec{\cdot}~x\leq\|\Row_{j}(A)\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}\|x\|_{{\mathcal{V}}},\quad j\in[1,m].

Hence

\begin{array}[]{l}\|y\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sqrt{\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{m}[\|\Row_{j}(A)\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}]^{2}\|x\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{V}}}}\\
=\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{m}[\|\Row_{j}(A)\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}]^{2}}\|x\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}.\end{array}

That is,

\|A\|_{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{m}\|\Row_{j}(A)\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{V}}}}.

Note that when
x={\bf 1}_{n}
the equality reaches, which implies that

\|A\|_{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}\geq\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{m}\|\Row_{j}(A)\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{V}}}}.

We conclude that (
95
) satisfies (
96
).

\Box

Proposition
V.4
ensures that (
94
) is a dynamic system.

V-C

Generalized Cayley-Hamilton Theorem

The following result is called the generalized Cayley-Hamilton theorem, which extends Cayley-Hamilton theorem to arbitrary matrices.

Theorem V.13

(Generalized Cayley-Hamilton Theorem) Let
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
and
r=\min(m,n)
. Set

\Pi(A):=\begin{cases}\Pi_{A},\quad r=m,\\
\Pi_{A^{T}},\quad r=n,\end{cases}

and denote by
p(x)=x^{r}+p_{r-1}x^{r-1}+\cdots+p_{0}
the characteristic polynomial of
\Pi(A)
. Then

\displaystyle A^{<r+1>}+p_{r-1}A^{<r>}+\cdots+p_{0}A=0.

(108)

Proof.
First, assume
m\leq n
. By definition and using (
101
), we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\Pi_{A}^{m}+p_{m-1}\Pi_{A}^{m-1}+\cdots+p_{0}I_{m}\\
=(A\Psi_{(n,m)})^{m}+p_{m-1}(A\Psi_{(n,m)})^{m-1}+\cdots+p_{0}I_{m}\\
=A^{<m>}\Psi_{(n,m)}+p_{m-1}A^{<m-1>}\Psi_{(n,m)}+\cdots+p_{0}I_{m}=0.\\
\end{array}

Multiplying
A
on right side yields

A^{<m+1>}+p_{m-1}A^{<m>}+\cdots+p_{0}A=0,

which verifies (
108
).

Next, assume
n<m
. Similar argument leads to

(A^{T})^{<n+1>}+p_{n-1}A^{<n>}+\cdots+p_{0}A^{T}=0.

Taking transpose on both sides yields (
108
).

\Box

Remark V.14

(i)

From the proof one sees easily that: using the characteristic function of
\Pi_{A}
we can have a (
108
) with
r=m
and using
the characteristic function of
\Pi_{A^{T}}
we can have another (
108
) with
r=n
. Both of them are correct, but we prefer to choose the lower order one.

(ii)

To see this is a generalization of classical Cayley-Hamilton Theorem, it is clear that when
m=n
(
108
) degenerates to

\displaystyle A^{n+1}+p_{n-1}A^{n}+\cdots+p_{0}A=0.

(109)

Deleting
A
yields the classical Cayley-Hamilton Theorem. (Even if
A
is singular, it still can be deleted from (
108
). Because selecting
a nonsingular sequence
\{A_{n}\}
and let
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}=A
proves the required equality.)

(iii)

We may define a formal identity
I_{m\times n}\in R(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
, which satisfies

\displaystyle I_{m\times n}\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,A=A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,I_{m\times n}=A,\quad\forall A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}.

(110)

Then (
108
) can be written as

\displaystyle A^{<r>}+p_{r-1}A^{<r-1>}+\cdots+p_{0}I_{m\times n}=0.

(111)

But remember that here
I_{m\times n}
is not a matrix, so (
111
) is only a convenient formal expression.

(iv)

Let
p(x)=x^{r}+p_{r-1}x^{r-1}+\cdots+p_{0}
be any annihilating polynomial of
\Pi_{A}
or
\Pi_{A^{T}}
. Then
(
108
) remain available. Note that now in general
r\neq\min(m,n)
. Particularly, we are interested in the minimum annihilating polynomial.

The
\Pi
-determinant
Det
has following properties.

(i)

Det
is a generalization of
\det
. That is,

\displaystyle Det(A)=\det(A),\quad A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\times n}.

(114)

(ii)

\displaystyle Det(A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B)=Det(A)Det(B),\quad A,B\in{\mathcal{M}}.

(115)

(iii)

If
Det(A)\neq 0
, then
A
is of full rank. That is, if
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
, then

\displaystyle Det(A)\neq 0\Rightarrow\rank(A)=\min(m,n).

(116)

The converse is not true.

Proof.

(i)

Since for a square matrix
A
we have
\Pi_{A}=A
, the conclusion follows.

(ii)

Let
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
. Since
\Pi_{A}=A\Psi_{n\times m}
. If
\rank(A)<\min(m,n)
, then
\Pi_{A}
is singular. Hence,

Det(A)=0
.
116
is verified.

The following counterexample shows that the converse is not true.
Consider

A=\begin{bmatrix}1&-2&1\\
1&0&0\end{bmatrix},

which is of full rank. But

\Pi_{A}=A\Psi_{3,2}=A\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\
1&1\\
0&2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
2&0\end{bmatrix},

which is singular.

\Box

Theorem V.19

Assume
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
is
\Pi
-invertible, then there exists a unique
B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
, (specified by
B_{1}
for
m\leq n
and
B_{2}
for
m>n
) called the
\Pi
-inverse of
A
, such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}B_{1}\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,I_{m}=I_{m},\quad m\leq n,\\
I_{n}\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B_{2}=I_{n},\quad m>n.\\
\end{cases}

(117)

Proof.

First, assume
m\leq n
:

Let
x^{m}+p_{m-1}x^{m-1}+\cdots+p_{0}
be the characteristic function of
\Pi_{A}
. Since
A
is
\Pi
-invertible,
p_{0}\neq 0
. Then

\begin{array}[]{l}(A\Psi_{n\times m})^{m}+p_{m-1}(A\Psi_{n\times m})^{m-1}+\cdots+p_{0}I_{m}\\
=A^{<m>}\Psi_{n\times m}+p_{m-1}A^{<m-1>}\Psi_{n\times m}+\cdots\\
~~+p_{1}A\Psi_{n\times m}+p_{0}I_{m}\\
=\left[(A^{<m>}+p_{m-1}A^{<m-1>}+\cdots+p_{1}A)\right]\Psi_{n\times m}~~+p_{0}I_{m}\\
=\left\{\left[A^{<m-1>}+p_{m-1}A^{<m-2>}+\cdots+p_{2}A\right]\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,A\right.\\
~~+\left.p_{1}(\Psi_{m\times n}\Psi_{n\times m})^{-1}\Psi_{m\times n}\Psi_{n\times m}A\right\}\Psi_{n\times m}+p_{0}I_{m}\\
=\left\{\left[A^{<m-1>}+p_{m-1}A^{<m-2>}+\cdots+p_{2}A\right.\right.\\
~~+\left.\left.p_{1}(\Psi_{m\times n}\Psi_{n\times m})^{-1}\Psi_{m\times n}\right]\Psi_{n\times m}A\right\}\Psi_{n\times m}+p_{0}I_{m}\\
\end{array}

Set

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}B_{1}:=A^{(-1)}=-\frac{1}{p_{0}}\left[A^{<m-1>}+p_{m-1}A^{<m-2>}+\cdots+p_{2}A\right.\\
~~+\left.p_{1}(\Psi_{m\times n}\Psi_{n\times m})^{-1}\Psi_{m\times n}\right],\\
\end{array}

which is called the
\Pi
-inverse of
A
.
Then we have

(B_{1}\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,A)\Psi_{n\times m}=B\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,I_{m}=I_{m}.

Next, assume
m>n
:

Let the characteristic function of
\Pi_{A^{T}}
be
x^{n}+q_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+q_{0}
.
Using the previous proof, we have that

B_{2}^{T}\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,A^{T}\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,I_{n}=I_{n}.

Than is

I_{n}\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B=I_{n},

where

\begin{array}[]{l}B_{2}^{T}:=(A^{T})^{(-1)}=-\frac{1}{q_{0}}\left[(A^{T})^{<n-1>}+q_{n-1}(A^{T})^{<n-2>}\right.\\
~~\left.+\cdots+q_{2}(A^{T})+q_{1}(\Psi_{n\times m}\Psi_{m\times n})^{-1}\Psi_{n\times m}\right].\\
\end{array}

That is,

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}B_{2}:=(A)^{(-1)}=-\frac{1}{q_{0}}\left[A^{<n-1>}+q_{n-1}A^{<n-2>}+\cdots\right.\\
~~\left.+q_{2}A+q_{1}\Psi_{m\times n}(\Psi_{n\times m}\Psi_{m\times n})^{-1}\right].\\
\end{array}

\Box

It is natural to define an “exponential” function as

Using this notation, the solution of (

Assume

\displaystyle x(t)=x_{0}\vec{+}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}A^{<i>}\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,x_{0}~~=x_{0}\vec{+}x_{1}\vec{+}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=2}^{\infty}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\Pi^{i-1}_{A}x_{1},

We refer to

Definition VI.1

[
3
]
A vector space
V
with a binary operator, called Lie bracket, is a Lie algebra, if

(i)

(Bi-linearity)

\displaystyle[ax+by,z]=a[x,z]+b[y,z],\quad x,y,z\in V,\;a,b\in{\mathbb{F}}.

(141)

(ii)

(Skew-symmetry)

\displaystyle[x,y]=-[y,x],\quad x,y\in V.

(142)

(iii)

(Jacobi Identity)

\displaystyle[[x,y],z]+[[y,z],x]+[[z,x],y]=0,\quad x,y,z\in V.

(143)

Remark VI.2

[
3
]
Consider

\displaystyle[A,B]=AB-BA,\quad A,B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\times n}.

VIII
Concluding Remarks

In this paper a new STP, called (left) DK-STP and denoted by

\ltimes

, has been proposed. Using it, the corresponding ring, Lie algebra, and Lie group are presented. The algebraic objects concerned in this paper can be described as:

R(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})\xrightarrow{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}gl(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})\xrightarrow{Exp}GL(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}}).

The action of
G(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
on dimension-free vector space
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
is also considered, which proposed discrete-time/continuous-time dynamic systems as

G(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})\xrightarrow{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,}{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}\rightarrow~\mbox{S-system}\rightarrow\mbox{dynamic system}.

Meanwhile, by introducing the square restriction
\Pi_{A}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{m\times n}
of
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
, some interesting things have been obtained, including eigenvalue, eigenvector, determinant, invertibility, etc., for non square matrices. Particularly, the Cayley-Hamilton theory can also be extended to non-square matrices.

In addition, the right DK-STP, weighted (left) DK-STP, and weighted right DK-STP are also briefly introduced. They have similar properties as (left) DK-STP.

This paper may pave a road for further development of STP of matrices.

Though these new STPs shown many interesting properties, it can not be used to replace existing STPs, because they have quite different properties, which makes their functions different. Unlike existing STPs, application of these new STPs is still waiting for exploring.

There are many related topics remain for further study. The following are some of them.

(i)

Understanding
gl(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})
and
GL(m\times n,{\mathbb{F}})

The investigation of non-square general linear group and general linear algebra is only a beginning. To reveal their more properties is theoretically important and interesting. Particularly, general “non-square” Lie group and Lie algebra may be developed. Say, group representation of nonlinear mapping

(ii)

If we use dimension keeping STP to the nominal model (
