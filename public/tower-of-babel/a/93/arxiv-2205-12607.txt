Discontinuities cause essential spectrum
Oliver Butterley, Giovanni Canestrari, Sakshi Jain

Abstract
. We study transfer operators associated to piecewise monotone interval transformations and show that the essential spectrum is large whenever the Banach space bounds L ∞ L^{\infty} and the transformation fails to be Markov. Constructing a family of Banach spaces we show that the lower bound on the essential spectral radius is optimal. Indeed, these Banach spaces realise an essential spectral radius as close as desired to the theoretical best possible case.



1.
Introduction

Describing the statistical properties of a hyperbolic dynamical system is an important question and one powerful approach to this problem is to study the associated transfer operator acting on a “good” choice of Banach space (see
[
30
]
and references within).
In recent decades these techniques have seen massive development
and many systems were studied in this way with valuable results being obtained
[
26
]
.
Results include statistical properties (invariant measures, CLT, LLT, decay of correlations, large deviations, zeta functions, etc.), stability of these properties under perturbations
[
27
]
and rigorous numerics
[
29
]
.
Different Banach spaces were used in various applications and often it was beneficial to engineer appropriate Banach spaces.
At present we have, a “bestiary”
[
37
]
of available spaces, each with benefits for different settings and purposes.

Arguably, particularly with physical applications in mind, discontinuities are natural.
For example, in billiard systems discontinuities arise from tangential collisions
[
13
]
and in Lorenz-like flows discontinuities arise from shearing close to the singularity
[
41
]
.
A weight of evidence suggests that discontinuities don’t worsen the statistical properties of the system even if they cause technical difficulties (e.g.,
[
2
,
9
]
).

Let
\mathcal{L}:\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{B}
be a transfer operator associated to some dynamical system.
Since the Banach space must contain the observables of interest, we take the point of view that, at the very least, it includes
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
.
Consequently, it also contains
\mathcal{L}^{n}(\mathcal{C}^{\infty})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

and so discontinuities of the underlying system force the Banach space to contain observables with discontinuities.
On the other hand, if the space is big, the spectrum can be huge and consequently useless (further details in the following section).
The aim is to choose a Banach space for which the essential spectrum is as small as possible.
Once this is done, one may proceed to determine the point spectrum or “resonances” of the system (see, e.g.,
[
12
,
18
]
and references within).
Moreover the point spectrum is essentially independent of the choice of Banach space, it is inherent in the system studied
[
7
]
.

Our present focus is piecewise monotone interval transformations.
Classically these are studied using
\operatorname{BV}
and the essential spectral radius is equal to a value depending on the expansion
[
24
]
.
However, for
C^{\infty}
systems without discontinuities, the essential spectral radius can be made as small as desired by choosing a Banach space of smoother observables
[
15
]
and so we ask ourselves:

Question
.

Does there exist a Banach space for studying piecewise monotone interval transformations on which the essential spectrum is as small as desired?

Somewhat unfortunately, one success of this present work is to answer this question in the negative when the transformation fails to be Markov.

A piecewise monotone transformation

In Theorem

The key idea is to construct eigenvalues of the dual operator in a way that relies on the discontinuities.
Consequently this component of the spectrum has a different origin compared to the essential spectrum studied previously

and which is due to limited differentiability.
This part of the work gives a lower estimate on the essential spectral radius which holds for any Banach space.
In Theorem

Consequently the natural next question is:

Given a piecewise monotone interval transformation which fails to be Markov, does there exist a Banach space on which the essential spectral radius is smaller than for

In Theorem

Intimately connected to the present work and of physical relevance is the task of understanding when the correlations of
C^{\infty}
observables admit a description in terms of resonances (see definition and discussion in
[
18
, §1]
).
It is known (see
[

,

,

,
18
]
and citations within) that all uniformly expanding and uniformly hyperbolic
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
maps (in any dimension) admit Ruelle resonances to arbitrary precision.
This includes Markov expanding systems and pseudo Anosov diffeomorphisms, which are
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
in the sense that, apart from a set of discontinuities/singularities, they are
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
and this set is both invariant under the dynamics and has zero measure.
Essentially all proofs of resonances rely on spectral considerations for the transfer operator.
As such, the present work demonstrates that discontinuities are an obstacle to the precision with which the correlations of observables can be described by resonances.

4.
Background on essential spectrum

In this section we isolate some relatively standard functional analytic details which are useful for the present work.
We say that
z
lies in the essential spectrum
\operatorname{EssSpec}(\mathcal{L})
of a bounded operator
\mathcal{L}
if
(z\operatorname{id}-\mathcal{L})
is not a Fredholm operator.
There are five different definitions of essential spectrum in common usage (see, e.g.,
[
17
, I.4]
where the one we use corresponds to the third).
The different definitions in general aren’t equivalent however the essential spectral radius is equal for all five definitions
[
17
, Corollary 4.11]
.
The notion of essential spectrum we use here is invariant under compact perturbations (this is true for four out or the five definitions)
[
17
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Let
\operatorname{\underline{EssSpec}}(\mathcal{L})
denote the complement of the unbounded component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{EssSpec}(\mathcal{L})
.
I.e., this is the union of
\operatorname{EssSpec}(\mathcal{L})
and all the bounded components of the complement.
Similarly
\operatorname{\underline{Spec}}(\mathcal{L})
denotes the complement of the unbounded component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Spec}(\mathcal{L})
.
These are the “filled” versions of the usual notions.

In our work we arrive at the point where we know that the spectrum contains a complete disc.
According to one of the definitions of the essential spectrum this would imply that the entire disc is essential spectrum.
However this version of the essential spectrum might not be invariant under compact perturbation and this is a property which we require in the present argument.
We know that
[
16
, Theorem 4.3.18]
:

If
\mathcal{L}
is a bounded operator then
\operatorname{Spec}(\mathcal{L})\setminus\operatorname{\underline{EssSpec}}(\mathcal{L})
consists of isolated eigenvalues with finite algebraic and geometric multiplicities.

This allows us to conclude the following observation.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a bounded operator
and let

.
If

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{\underline{Spec}}(\mathcal{L})\supseteq\{\left|\lambda\right|\leq C\}
there must exist a connected set

13

13

.

where the entire unit disc is in the spectrum but that only the unit circle is in the part of the spectrum which is invariant under compact perturbation.

We also need to be able to connect the the essential spectrum of the restriction of an operator to the spectrum of the operator itself.

, we have that
1
is a non-trivial discontinuity for the map
T_{m,\rho}
.
Furthermore, since such an orbit will visit only the sets
\omega_{2}
and
\omega_{3}
, we have that

\Lambda(T_{m,\rho},\tfrac{1}{T_{m,\rho}^{\prime}},1)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\biggl(\frac{1}{\rho}\cdot(1/m)^{n-1}\biggr)^{1/n}=\frac{1}{m}.

To finish, we observe that
1
is the only non-trivial discontinuity so that

\Lambda(T_{m,\rho},\tfrac{1}{T_{m,\rho}^{\prime}})=\Lambda(T_{m,\rho},\tfrac{1}{T_{m,\rho}^{\prime}},1)
.
∎

On the other hand, the next lemma gives an estimate of the quantity which corresponds to the essential spectral radius on BV.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all
m\geq 4
and
\rho\in(0,m)
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|{\tfrac{1}{(T_{m,\rho}^{n})^{\prime}}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1/n}\geq\frac{m-3}{m}.

Proof.

We take advantage of the fact that
0
is a fixed point. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
I_{n}=[0,\frac{1}{10}(\frac{m-3}{m})^{n}]
.
We observe that
\left\|{1}/{(T^{n})^{\prime}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\geq\left\|{1}/{(T^{n})^{\prime}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{n})}
.
On the other hand, for every
m\geq 4
,
\left\|{1}/{(T^{n})^{\prime}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{n})}=(\frac{m-3}{m})^{n}
, since
T^{k}(x)\in\omega_{1}
for every
k\leq n
.
∎

Let
c\in(0,1)
and choose
m
sufficiently large such that

\frac{m-3}{m}-\frac{1}{m}>c.

Then, by Lemma
5.1
, we can fix
\rho
such that
1
is a non-trivial discontinuity for the transformation
T_{m,\rho}
and
\Lambda(T_{m,\rho},\tfrac{1}{T_{m,\rho}^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{m}
.
On the other hand, thanks to Lemma
5.2
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|{{1}/{(T_{m,\rho}^{n})^{\prime}}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1/n}\geq\frac{m-3}{m}
, independent of
\rho
.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
2
.

6.
Custom Banach space

This section is devoted to the construction of custom Banach spaces,
and hence the proof of Theorem
3
.

The argument of Keller
[
24
]
for the lower bound to the essential spectral radius on
\operatorname{BV}
suggests that we need to be more restrictive on the possibility of jumps in the observables.
A first choice could be
\operatorname{SBV}
, the space of “special” bounded variation functions.
However this space is still too large in the sense that it still permits the construction that Keller uses.
Learning from this we permit discontinuities only where we absolutely need to because
of the discontinuities of the interval transformation. This permits us to encode the asymptotic behaviour of the
discontinuities introducing norms adapted to their orbits.

Throughout this section we work under the assumptions of Theorem
3
, in particular we suppose that
T:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
piecewise monotone transformation (with associated partition
\Omega
) and that
\varphi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}
has a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
extension to
\overline{\omega}
for each
\omega\in\Omega
and is uniformly bounded away from zero.
Moreover
T
is uniformly expanding.
Let
\eta=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\smash{\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\varphi(T^{k})}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1/n}

and
\lambda=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|1/(T^{n})^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1/n}<1
.
Fix, for the remainder of this section,
\widetilde{\Lambda}>\Lambda^{\sup}(T,\varphi)
.
Since
\lambda<1
we may choose
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
r\geq 1
sufficiently large such that
\eta\lambda^{r-1}<\widetilde{\Lambda}
.

Remark
.

The assumptions of Theorem
3
can be relaxed to
T
and
\varphi
being merely
\mathcal{C}^{r}
as long as
r\in\mathbb{N}
is sufficiently large such that the above inequality is satisfied.

Recall that, as defined in Section
3
,
\Gamma\subset\mathcal{I}
denotes the boundary of the partition elements
\omega\in\Omega
and
\Delta
denotes
\cup_{k=0}^{\infty}T^{k}\Gamma
, the set of all images of all discontinuities.
The following is an extension and rephrasing of Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
.

Lemma 6.1
.

There exists a finite set of points
{\{b_{k}\}}_{k=1}^{N_{2}}
and finite number (indexed by
1\leq j\leq N_{1}
) of countable sets of points
{\{a_{j,k}\}}_{k=0}^{\infty}
such that,

a_{j,k}\notin\Gamma
,

\Delta={\{b_{k}\}}_{k=1}^{N_{2}}\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_{1}}{\{a_{j,k}\}}_{k=0}^{\infty},

and,

(12)

T^{-n}a_{j,k}\cap\Delta=\{a_{j,k-n}\},\quad\quad\text{ for all $j$, $k\geq n$}.

Proof.

As mentioned at the beginning of Section
3
, there are at most a finite number of non-trivial discontinuity orbits and this is the origin of
N_{1}
.
Lemma
3.2
implies the result stated here, with the minor difference that now we keep track of all discontinuity orbits.
For each of these infinite orbits there exists some iterate after which the required property (
12
) is satisfied and the orbit will not return to
\Gamma
(Lemma
3.1
& Lemma
3.2
).
In this way we define the
a_{j,k}
.
The
b_{k}
are defined to be the finite number of points of
\Delta
remaining.
∎

For the remainder of this section we suppose that the
b_{k}
and
a_{j,k}
are fixed as determined in Lemma
6.1
.
We write
\partial\Omega_{n}=\cup_{\omega\in\Omega_{n}}\partial\omega
.

Lemma 6.2
.

T^{-n}(a_{j,k+n})\cap\partial\Omega_{n}=\emptyset

for all
j,k,n
.
In particular
a_{j,k}\notin\partial\Omega_{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose, for sake of contradiction, there exists
y
such that
T^{n}y=a_{j,k+n}
and
T^{p}y\in\Gamma
for
p\leq n-1
.
Then,
T^{n-p}T^{p}y=a_{j,k+n}
and so there exists
a\in\Gamma
such that
T^{n-p}a=a_{j,k+n}
.
This is a contradiction by Lemma
6.1
.
The final claim follows from the fact that
a_{j,k}\in T^{-n}(a_{j,k+n})
.
∎

We say that
h:[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}
is piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
with respect to a finite partition
0=p_{0}<p_{1}<...<p_{N}=1
,
if
{\left.h\right|}_{(p_{l},p_{l+1})}
admits a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
extension
up to
[p_{l},p_{l+1}]
, for all
0\leq l\leq N-1
.
We define
\mathfrak{B}_{0}
to be the set of all such
h
for which there exists a finite partition of
[0,1]
with
p_{l}\in\Delta
,
which makes
h
piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
with respect to it.

Recall that
J\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
was defined (
3
) to be the jump in a piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
function at a given point.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}
,

k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
0\leq j\leq N_{1}
.
Then

\left|J\left(\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h,a_{j,k+1}\right)\right|=\left|\varphi(a_{j,k})\right|\ \left|J\left(h,a_{j,k}\right)\right|.

Proof.

This holds, as proved in Lemma
3.4
, since the crucial property is that
h
only has discontinuities at points in
\Delta
.
∎

6.1.
The Banach space

We use the notation
\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}{}
and
\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{j}}
to denote the absolutely continuous part and jump part of the derivative respectively.
In other words, for any
h\in\operatorname{SBV}
(see, e.g.,
[
1
]
), the weak derivative has the form
\operatorname{D}h=\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}{h}+\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{j}}{h}
where
\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}{h}\in L^{1}
and
\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{j}}{h}
can be written as a sum of delta functions.
Note that when
h
is piecewise-
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}h=h^{\prime}
, wherever the latter is well-defined.

We observe that

(13)

\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}\mathfrak{B}_{0}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}_{0},\quad\quad\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}\mathfrak{B}_{0}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}_{0}.

Recall that
r\in\mathbb{N}
was fixed at the beginning of this section.
Let
\zeta={\{\zeta_{a}\}}_{a\in\Delta}
be a set of positive numbers (to be defined shortly).
For any
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}
we define

(14)

\left\|h\right\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,r}}=\sum_{t=0}^{r}\left\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{t}h\right\|_{L^{1}}+\sum_{t=0}^{r-1}\left\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{t}h\right\|_{\mathfrak{J}_{\zeta}}

where

\left\|h\right\|_{\mathfrak{J}_{\zeta}}=\sum_{a\in\Delta}\zeta_{a}\ \left|J\left(h,a\right)\right|.

We refer to the first sum in this definition as the
continuous
part of the norm and the second sum as the
jump
part of the norm.

This norm has some resemblance to
\operatorname{BV}
or
\operatorname{SBV}
.
In the case when
r=0
, the norm corresponds to the
L^{1}
norm.
In the case when
r=1
and
\zeta_{a}
is equal to unity for every
a\in\Delta
, then the norm corresponds to the usual
\operatorname{BV}
norm and the Banach space is the subset of
\operatorname{SBV}
(in turn a subset of
\operatorname{BV}
) where discontinuities are only permitted at points in
\Delta
.
As anticipated, we now fix
\zeta
.
For all
1\leq j\leq N_{1}
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let

(15)

\zeta(j,k)=\frac{\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k}}{|\varphi_{k}(a_{j,0})|},

where, as before,
\varphi_{k}=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\varphi\circ T^{j}
.
We hence define,
for any
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}
,

(16)

\left\|h\right\|_{\mathfrak{J}_{\zeta}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\zeta(j,k)\left|J\left(h,a_{j,k}\right)\right|+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{2}}\left|J\left(h,b_{k}\right)\right|.

This corresponds to setting
\zeta_{a_{j,k}}=\zeta(j,k)
, for all
j
,
k
and setting
\zeta_{a}=1
otherwise.

The Banach space
\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,r}
is defined as the closure of
\mathfrak{B}_{0}
with respect to the
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{r,\zeta}}
norm.

Lemma 6.4
.

For all
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}
,
\left\|h\right\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,r}}<\infty
.

Proof.

We know that (
13
),
\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}
, whenever
0\leq l\leq r
, and the restriction of
\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h
to any interval of continuity
admits a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
extension up to the boundary of such interval.
Notice that each
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}
has only a finite number of discontinuities and so the jump part of the norm is finite.
Moreover,
\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h
has finite
L^{1}
-norm.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

If
s+l\leq r
, then

\left\|\smash{\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h}\right\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,s}}\leq\left\|h\right\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,r}}
for all
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,r}
.

Proof.

By density it suffices to consider
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}
.
According to the definition of the norm (
14
),

\displaystyle\left\|\smash{\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h}\right\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{s}\left\|\smash{\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l+t}h}\right\|_{L^{1}}+\sum_{t=0}^{s-1}\left\|\smash{\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l+t}h}\right\|_{\mathfrak{J}_{\zeta}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{s+l}\left\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{t}h\right\|_{L^{1}}+\sum_{t=0}^{s+l-1}\left\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{t}h\right\|_{\mathfrak{J}_{\zeta}}=\left\|\smash{h}\right\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,s+l}}.

Since it was assumed that
s+l\leq r
the conclusion follows.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

There exists
C>0
such that, for all
l+1\leq r
and all
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,r}
,

\left\|\smash{\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h}\right\|_{\operatorname{BV}}\leq C\left\|h\right\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,r}}.

Proof.

There exists
C>1
such that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\left|\varphi_{k}(a_{j,0})\right|\leq C\widetilde{\Lambda}^{k}
(recall that
\widetilde{\Lambda}>\Lambda^{\sup}(T,\varphi)
, which is the maximum of
\Lambda^{\sup}(T,\varphi,a)
where
a
ranges over the set of non-trivial discontinuities) and so, using the definition of
\zeta
,

\frac{1}{\zeta(j,k)}=\left|\varphi_{k}(a_{j,0})\right|\widetilde{\Lambda}^{-k}\leq C.

In particular,
1\leq C\zeta(j,k)
.
Consequently, for all
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}
,

\begin{split}\sum_{a\in\Delta}|J(h,a)|&\leq{C}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\zeta(j,k)\left|J\left(h,a_{j,k}\right)\right|+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{2}}\left|J\left(h,b_{k}\right)\right|\\
&\leq C{\|h\|}_{\mathfrak{J}_{\zeta}}.\end{split}

Observe that (since any discontinuities of
h
lie in
\Delta
),

\displaystyle{\|h\|}_{\operatorname{BV}}

\displaystyle={\|h\|}_{L^{1}}+{\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}{h}\|}_{L^{1}}+\sum_{a\in\Delta}\left|J(h,a)\right|,

\displaystyle{\|h\|}_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,1}}

\displaystyle={\|h\|}_{L^{1}}+{\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}{h}\|}_{L^{1}}+{\|h\|}_{\mathfrak{J}_{\zeta}}.

Hence, we have shown that, for all
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}
,
{\|h\|}_{\operatorname{BV}}\leq C{\|h\|}_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,1}}
.

Using this together with Lemma
6.5
and the density of
\mathfrak{B}_{0}
implies the statement of the lemma.
∎

6.2.
Lasota-Yorke inequality

In the next lemmas we estimate the
\mathfrak{B}_{r,\zeta}
norm of
\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}^{n}
and hence obtain a type of Lasota-Yorke inequality.
The following is the relevant distortion result in the present setting.

Lemma 6.7
.

There exists
C>0
and a set
{\{A_{l,p,n}\}}_{l,p,n}
of functions
A_{l,p,n}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}
such that,
for all
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}
,
0\leq p\leq r
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

(17)

D_{a}^{p}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}^{n}h\right)=\sum_{l=0}^{p}\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}^{n}(A_{l,p,n}\cdot\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h),

the
A_{l,p,n}
are
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
on each
\omega\in\Omega_{n}
,

\left\|A_{l,p,n}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C

and
A_{p,p,n}={((T^{n})^{\prime})}^{-p}
.

The above lemma is proved in Section
7
.

In two common cases the derivative of
\varphi
is zero:
(1) In the case of the transfer operator associated to the measure of maximal entropy;
(2) In the case of the transfer operator associated to the SRB measure for a piecewise affine transformation, here
\varphi=\frac{1}{\left|T^{\prime}\right|}
and is piecewise constant.
In both these cases the terms that appear in Lemma
6.7
are:

A_{j,k,n}=\begin{cases}((T^{n})^{\prime})^{-k}&\text{if $j=k$}\\
0&\text{if $j<k$}.\end{cases}

We don’t use the above in the present work but, if required, it can be verified from the formulae given in Lemma
7.2
.

Lemma 6.8
.

There exists
C>0
such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

k\geq n
,
0\leq t\leq r
and
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}
,

\left|J\left(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{t}(\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\varphi}h)),a_{j,k}\right)\right|\leq C\sum_{l=0}^{t}\left|J\left(\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\varphi}(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h),a_{j,k}\right)\right|.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.7
there exist functions
A_{l,t,n}
(with key properties) such that

J\left(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{t}(\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\varphi}h),a_{j,k}\right)=\sum_{l=0}^{t}J\left(\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}^{n}(A_{l,t,n}\cdot\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h),a_{j,k}\right),

and, by definition of
\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}
,

J\left(\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}^{n}(A_{l,t,n}\cdot\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h),a_{j,k}\right)=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{n}}J\left((A_{l,t,n}\cdot\varphi_{n}\cdot\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h)\circ T^{n}|_{\omega}^{-1},a_{j,k}\right).

Lemma
6.1
implies that for all
a_{j,k}
,
k\geq n
, there exists a unique
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega_{n}
such that
{T^{n}|_{\omega^{\prime}}}^{-1}(a_{j,k})=a_{j,k-n}\in\Delta

and for
\omega\neq\omega^{\prime}
,
{T^{n}|_{\omega}}^{-1}(a_{j,k})\notin\Delta
.
Furthermore,

\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{n}}J\left({(A_{l,t,n}\cdot\varphi_{n}\cdot\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h)\circ{T^{n}|_{\omega}}^{-1}},a_{j,k}\right)\\
=A_{l,t,n}(a_{j,k-n})J\left((\varphi_{n}\cdot\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h)\circ{T^{n}|_{\omega^{\prime}}}^{-1},a_{j,k}\right).

This is because
A_{l,t,n}\cdot\varphi_{n}
is continuous at any point in

T^{-n}(a_{j,k})
,
k\geq n
(Lemma
6.2
& Lemma
6.7
), and,
\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h
is discontinuous only in
\Delta
(
13
).
Similarly,

\displaystyle J\left((\varphi_{n}\cdot\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h)\circ{T^{n}|_{\omega^{\prime}}}^{-1},a_{j,k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{n}}J\left({(\varphi_{n}\cdot\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h)\circ{T^{n}|_{\omega}}^{-1}},a_{j,k}\right)

\displaystyle=J\left(\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\varphi}(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h),a_{j,k}\right).

Collecting together the above equalities we have shown that

\left|J\left(\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}^{n}(A_{l,t,n}\cdot\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h),a_{j,k}\right)\right|\leq\left\|A_{l,t,n}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left|J\left(\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\varphi}(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}h),a_{j,k}\right)\right|.

The statement of the lemma follows since Lemma
6.7
tells that
\left\|A_{l,t,n}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
is bounded uniformly in
n
.
∎

For convenience we use the following notation for the continuous part and jump part of the norm, respectively,

\left\|h\right\|_{\mathfrak{C}^{r}}=\sum_{j=0}^{r}{\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{j}h\|}_{L^{1}},\quad\quad\left\|h\right\|_{\mathfrak{J}_{\zeta}^{r}}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}{\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{j}h\|}_{\mathfrak{J}_{\zeta}}.

By change of variables,
since
\left\|\varphi\cdot T^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
is finite,
for all
h\in L^{1}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

(18)

\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h\right\|_{L^{1}}\leq\left\|\varphi_{n}\cdot\smash{\left(T^{n}\right)^{\prime}}\cdot h\right\|_{L^{1}}.

Lemma 6.9
.

There exists some
C>0
such that, for all
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{0}
,

{\|\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h\|}_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,r}}\leq C{\|h\|}_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,r}}.

In particular,
\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}
extends to a continuous operator on
\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,r}
.

Proof.

We estimate

{\|\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h\|}_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,r}}=\sum_{l=0}^{r}{\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h\|}_{L^{1}}+\sum_{l=0}^{r-1}{\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h\|}_{\mathfrak{J}_{\zeta}}
.
For any
0\leq l\leq r
, using Lemma
6.7
(for
n=1
) and (
18
),

\displaystyle{\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h\|}_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=0}^{l}{\|\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}(A_{s,l,1}\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{s}h)\|}_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=0}^{l}{\|\varphi\cdot T^{\prime}\cdot A_{s,l,1}\cdot\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{s}h\|}_{L^{1}}\leq C\sum_{s=0}^{l}{\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{s}h\|}_{L^{1}},

for some
C>0
.
This takes care of the continuous part of the norm.
As for the jump part, we have, for any
0\leq l\leq r-1

\displaystyle{\|\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h\|}_{\mathfrak{J}_{\zeta}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\zeta(j,k)\left|J\left(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}(\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h),a_{j,k}\right)\right|

\displaystyle+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{2}}\left|J\left(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h,b_{k}\right)\right|+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}\left|J\left(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}(\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h),a_{j,0}\right)\right|\right).

Let us start with the final pair of sums.
Crucially this is a finite number of terms.
By Lemma
6.6
and Lemma
6.7
, we have that, for some
C>0
,

(19)

\displaystyle\left|J\left(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h,b_{k}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=0}^{l}\left|J\left(\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}(A_{s,l,1}\cdot\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{s}h),b_{k}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\ \#\Omega\sum_{s=0}^{l}{\|\varphi\cdot A_{s,l,1}\cdot\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{s}h\|}_{L^{\infty}}\leq C{\|h\|}_{\mathfrak{B}_{\zeta,r}}.

An identical estimate holds for the terms involving
\left|J\left(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h,a_{j,0}\right)\right|
.
This takes care of the finite sum.

For the first sum, the remaining term to be estimated, Lemma
6.8
implies,

\left|J\left(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}(\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h),a_{j,k}\right)\right|\leq C\sum_{s=0}^{l}\left|J\left(\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{s}h),a_{j,k}\right)\right|.

Lemma
6.3
implies that this is bounded by

C\sum_{s=0}^{l}\left|J\left(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{s}h,a_{j,k-1}\right)\right|
.
Summing over
k
, this means that

\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\zeta(j,k)\left|J\left(\operatorname{D}_{\operatorname{a}}^{l}(\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}h),a_{j,k}\right)\right|\leq C\left\|h\right\|_{\mathfrak{J}_{\zeta}^{r}},

as required.
∎

Subsequently we will prove inequalities concerning
\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}
and Lemma
6.9
means that it is sufficient to prove them on
\mathfrak{B}_{0}
.

Lemma 6.10
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
C_{n}>0
such that,
if
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{r,\zeta}
,
