A Spatial Interference Approach to Account for Mobility in Air Pollution Studies with Multivariate Continuous Treatments
Heejun Shin, Danielle Braun, Kezia Irene, Michelle Audirac, Joseph Antonelli

Abstract
We develop new methodology to improve our understanding of the causal effects of multivariate air pollution exposures on public health. Typically, exposure to air pollution for an individual is measured at their home geographic region, though people travel to different regions with potentially different levels of air pollution. To account for this, we incorporate estimates of the mobility of individuals from cell phone mobility data to get an improved estimate of their exposure to air pollution. We treat this as an interference problem, where individuals in one geographic region can be affected by exposures in other regions due to mobility into those areas. We propose policy-relevant estimands and derive expressions showing the extent of bias one would obtain by ignoring this mobility. We additionally highlight the benefits of the proposed interference framework relative to a measurement error framework for accounting for mobility. We develop novel estimation strategies to estimate causal effects that account for this spatial spillover utilizing flexible Bayesian methodology. Lastly, we use the proposed methodology to study the health effects of ambient air pollution on mortality among Medicare enrollees in the United States.



Keywords:
Causal inference, Interference, Air pollution epidemiology, Mobility, Spatial statistics

1
Introduction

Estimating the health effects of air pollution is a crucially important scientific question as there exists a large public health burden due to exposure to ambient air pollution
(
Cohen et al., 2017
;
Burnett et al., 2018
)
. For a review of the vast epidemiological literature showing the adverse effects of air pollution, we point readers to
Kampa and Castanas, 2008
. In nearly all related studies, exposure to air pollution is measured at an individual’s residence or home geographic area. This ignores the fact that people travel to different geographic regions with potentially different levels of air pollution, and this can lead to biased estimates of the effects of air pollution. Additionally, most work has focused on examining the impact of a single pollutant at a time. Humans are exposed to an array of ambient air pollutants, which has led to a shift towards estimating the health effects of multiple pollutants simultaneously
(
Dominici et al., 2010
)
. In this work, we address both of these issues by investigating the impact of mobility on estimation of the effects of air pollution in a way that can be incorporated with both single or multi-pollutant analyses.

Estimating the causal effects of environmental pollutants comes with a number of challenges, some of which are ubiquitous when utilizing observational data, while others are unique to environmental or spatial settings
(
Reich et al., 2021
)
. Unmeasured confounding is always a threat to observational studies, though some work has been done to alleviate this if the unmeasured variables are spatially correlated, which is common in environmental settings
(
Papadogeorgou et al., 2019a
)
. When dealing with multiple pollutants, estimation of the health effects of air pollution also becomes difficult, which has led to the development of flexible approaches that all allow for nonlinear associations and interactions between pollutants
(
Herring, 2010
;
Bobb et al., 2015
;
Antonelli et al., 2020
)
. Another complication when estimating the causal effects of multivariate exposures is that it can be difficult to define policy-relevant estimands when pollutants are highly correlated and potentially emitted from the same source. Despite these difficulties, a causal inference approach to environmental epidemiology has been advocated for
(
Carone et al., 2020
;
Sommer et al., 2021
)
. Reasons for this push include the clear definition of target estimands, removing reliance on a particular statistical model, the ability to use flexible approaches, clarity on the assumptions needed to identify causal effects, and sensitivity analysis to violations of these assumptions
(
Dominici and Zigler, 2017
)
.

Another issue of particular relevance to this work is interference, which occurs when units are affected by the exposure status of other units in the study
(
Hong and Raudenbush, 2006
;
Sobel, 2006
;
Hudgens and Halloran, 2008
)
. A critical issue when dealing with interference is simplifying the interference structure, or the manner in which the exposure status of certain units can affect the outcomes of other units. Partial interference is commonly assumed, where interference is restricted to clusters or a subset of the data points
(
VanderWeele and Tchetgen, 2011
;
Tchetgen and VanderWeele, 2012
;
Papadogeorgou et al., 2019b
)
. Other approaches incorporate spatial proximity, where the treatment status of nearby observations are more likely to affect a unit’s outcome
(
Wang et al., 2020
;
Giffin et al., 2022
)
. More general interference structures, such as those seen in social networks can also be accommodated
(
Aronow and Samii, 2017
;
Ogburn et al., 2022
)
. This has also seen attention in the air pollution literature to better understand the impact of interventions on power plants
(
Zigler et al., 2020
;
Zigler and Papadogeorgou, 2021
)
, or the impacts of the EPA’s non-attainment designations
(
Zirkle et al., 2021
)
. Recently, there have been efforts to investigate unknown or misspecified interference structures
(
Sävje et al., 2021
;
Weinstein and Nevo, 2023
)
. Despite this surge in interest, nearly all work has focused on univariate, binary treatments.

In this paper, we address a number of important issues that aim to understand the impact of air pollution on health outcomes holistically. Our approach estimates causal effects for both single and multiple pollutant studies, while simultaneously accounting for mobility of individuals across geographic regions. We address mobility by incorporating cell phone mobility data and a novel interference framework. Specifically, we let outcomes depend on exposures at both 1) the home geographic region of individuals, and 2) a weighted average of exposure at neighboring areas based on weights determined by cell-phone mobility data. This extends the literature on causal inference under interference to settings with multivariate, continuous treatments, while leading to improved estimates of the overall impact of air pollution. We develop policy-relevant estimands for causal effects of the air pollution mixture, and derive bias formulas for when mobility is ignored, as is typically the case in environmental epidemiology. Further, we develop flexible Bayesian estimators of causal effects in this setting, and use our framework to gain epidemiological insights into the effects of air pollution on the elderly population in the United States.

2
Estimands and identifying assumptions

Throughout we observe
\bm{D}_{i}=(Y_{i},\bm{W}_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,n
from a population of interest. In our motivating study, the unit
i
will correspond to United States zip codes.
Y_{i}
is an outcome of interest for zip code
i
, and we are interested in estimating the effect of air pollution exposure on this outcome. We let
\bm{W}_{i}
denote the
q
-dimensional value of exposures, while
\bm{X}_{i}
is a
p
-dimensional vector of pre-treatment confounders. Throughout we let lowercase letters such as
\bm{w}
represent realizations of random variables, and let the subscript
i
denote values for unit
i
in the data, but we will frequently omit it for simplicity and ease of exposition.

2.1
Potential outcomes under interference

Commonly it is assumed that potential outcomes of a unit are not affected by exposure levels of other units, typically referred to as
no interference
between units
(
Cox, 1958
)
or, when combined with the
no-multiple-versions-of-treatment
assumption, the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA,
Rubin, 1980
). To see how this is insufficient for our setting, consider two scenarios in which a unit is exposed to the same level of exposure in its own geographic area, but is exposed to either low or high levels of exposure during day to day mobility. If these exposures have an effect on the outcome, we would expect the potential outcomes in these two scenarios to differ, leading to violations of SUTVA due to multiple versions of treatment that depend on the level of neighboring exposure values. For this reason, we first define potential outcomes as
Y_{i}(\bm{w}^{all})
, which is the outcome we would observe for unit
i
if the entire sample were exposed to pollution levels
\bm{w}^{all}=(\bm{w}_{1},\bm{w}_{2},...,\bm{w}_{n})^{\prime}
, and assume that
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(\bm{w}^{all})
. This leads to a prohibitively large set of potential outcomes, which we can reduce the complexity of using similar ideas to
Aronow and Samii, 2017
and
Forastiere et al., 2021
by making the following assumption:

Assumption 1

(Interference SUTVA)
.

For any
\bm{w}^{all}
and
{\bm{w}^{all}}^{\prime}
such that
\bm{w}_{i}^{all}={\bm{w}_{i}^{all}}^{\prime}
and
g(\bm{w}_{-i}^{all})=g({\bm{w}_{-i}^{all}}^{\prime})
, we have that

Y_{i}(\bm{w}^{all})=Y_{i}({\bm{w}^{all}}^{\prime}).

The potential outcome is now a function of a unit’s own exposure and all remaining exposures, but only through
g(\cdot)
. We can therefore write our potential outcomes as
Y_{i}(\bm{w},\bm{g})
, which is the outcome we would observe if unit
i
was exposed to home exposure levels
\bm{w}
and neighboring exposure levels
\bm{g}
. We denote observed values of neighboring exposures as
\bm{G}_{i}=g(\bm{W}_{-i})
, and discuss the choice of
g(\cdot)
in Section
4
.

2.2
Policy-relevant estimands

Now that we have defined potential outcomes, we can discuss the estimands of interest. Suppose there exist baseline home and neighboring exposure levels of interest given by
\bm{w}
and
\bm{g}
, and a policy is expected to change exposure levels by
\bm{\delta}_{w}
and
\bm{\delta}_{g}
, respectively. Then we can define the effect of this change as

\displaystyle\lambda(\bm{w},\bm{g},\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{\delta}_{g})

\displaystyle=\E[Y(\bm{w}+\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{g}+\bm{\delta}_{g})]-\E[Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})]

\displaystyle=\E[Y(\bm{w}+\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{g}+\bm{\delta}_{g})]-\E[Y(\bm{w}+\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{g})]

\displaystyle\hskip 14.45377pt+\E[Y(\bm{w}+\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{g})]-\E[Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})]

\displaystyle\equiv\lambda_{sp}(\bm{w},\bm{g},\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{\delta}_{g})+\lambda_{dir}(\bm{w},\bm{g},\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{\delta}_{g}),

where we have decomposed the effect into direct effects of home exposure and spillover effects of neighboring exposure. While these are meaningful estimands, it is not always possible for all units in a study to have exposure set to either
\bm{w}
or
\bm{g}
, or reasonable to assume a constant shift in exposure across all units. Certain units have different exposure values with differential impacts of a policy aimed at reducing air pollution. For instance, implementing a policy at power plants would not reduce exposure in areas of the country far from a power plant. An alternative is to investigate a sample level estimand that accounts for different air pollution mixtures across geographic areas. Suppose that a policy changes exposure in all
n
areas by
\bm{\Delta}=[\bm{\Delta}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\bm{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime}]^{\prime}
, where
\bm{\Delta}_{i}
is the impact of the policy on air pollution in region
i
. Depending on the mobility of individuals in each region, this alters home and neighborhood exposures by
\bm{\Delta}_{wi}
and
\bm{\Delta}_{gi}
, respectively. This estimand is defined as

\displaystyle\omega(\bm{\Delta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg\{Y_{i}(\bm{W}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{wi},\bm{G}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{gi})-Y_{i}(\bm{W}_{i},\bm{G}_{i})\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\{Y_{i}(\bm{W}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{wi},\bm{G}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{gi})-Y_{i}(\bm{W}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{wi},\bm{G}_{i})\big\}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.45377pt+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\{Y_{i}(\bm{W}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{wi},\bm{G}_{i})-Y_{i}(\bm{W}_{i},\bm{G}_{i})\big\}

\displaystyle\equiv\omega_{sp}(\bm{\Delta})+\omega_{dir}(\bm{\Delta})

This estimand allows for policy-relevant and region-specific estimands, while additionally accounting for the fact that these changes in exposure affect other units due to travel among geographic areas. Lastly, as we are working with observational data, we must make additional assumptions to estimate these quantities from observed data. In particular, we make a conditional exchangeability assumption:

Assumption 2

(Joint unconfoundedness)
.

Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}\bm{W},\bm{G}\mid\bm{X}

This assumption states that we have measured all common causes of the exposures and the outcome
Y
. We also make a positivity assumption, which informally states that the joint density of
(\bm{W,G})
conditional on
\bm{X}
is positive. We leave technical details of this assumption to Appendix
A
as this assumption differs across estimands.

3
Bias when mobility is ignored

Here we derive expressions for the bias in estimating the impact of a policy to reduce air pollution that one would obtain if they ignored mobility into nearby regions. We first focus on a simplified setting with univariate exposures and linear models to provide intuition for this bias, and relate it to exposure measurement error, which has a rich literature in environmental epidemiology
(
Szpiro and Paciorek, 2013
)
. We then derive expressions in more general settings with multivariate exposures.

3.1
Illustration with linear models

We first begin with a simple example with univariate exposures, and to simplify the calculations, we scale both
W
and
G
to have marginal variance 1 and correlation
\rho
. We assume that the potential outcomes are generated from the model given by

Y(w,g)=\tau w\beta_{w}+(1-\tau)g\beta_{g}+\epsilon

where
\tau
is the proportion of time spent in the home zip code. We explore two approaches first: 1) a linear model regressing the outcome against just
W
, and 2) a linear model regressing the outcome against
W^{*}=\tau W+(1-\tau)G
, which can be seen as the true pollution exposure for an individual based on their time spent in different locations. We define the parameters from these two models as

\displaystyle(\tilde{\beta}_{0},\tilde{\beta}_{w})

\displaystyle=\underset{\beta_{0},\beta}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \E[(Y-\beta_{0}-W\beta)^{2}]

\displaystyle(\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{*},\tilde{\beta}_{w}^{*})

\displaystyle=\underset{\beta_{0},\beta}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \E[(Y-\beta_{0}-W^{*}\beta)^{2}].

Our focus will be on the parameters
\tilde{\beta}_{w}
and
\tilde{\beta}_{w}^{*}
. The first of these parameters corresponds to a naïve analysis that ignores mobility and relates pollution to health only through the exposure at the home region, which is common practice in environmental epidemiology. The second parameter of interest,
\tilde{\beta}_{w}^{*}
looks at the impact of using the true weighted average exposure based on movement throughout the day. In Appendix
B
, we show that these parameters are given by the following expressions:

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{w}

\displaystyle=\tau\beta_{w}+\rho(1-\tau)\beta_{g}

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{w}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau^{2}\beta_{w}+\rho\tau(1-\tau)\beta_{w}+\rho\tau(1-\tau)\beta_{g}+(1-\tau)^{2}\beta_{g}}{\tau^{2}+2\tau(1-\tau)\rho+(1-\tau)^{2}}.

Now suppose that we are interested in estimating the effect of increasing pollution everywhere by one unit, which amounts to increasing both
W
and
G
by 1. The true causal effect in this case would be
\tau\beta_{w}+(1-\tau)\beta_{g}
. Unsurprisingly the naïve approach,
\tilde{\beta}_{w}
, is biased except in the extreme case where
\rho=1
. Importantly, If
\beta_{w}
and
\beta_{g}
have the same sign, which is reasonable in environmental applications, the naïve approach will underestimate the effect of pollution.

Moving to the second parameter, we show in Appendix
B
that
\tilde{\beta}_{w}^{*}
is equal to the true effect if any one of three conditions hold: 1)
\rho=1
, 2)
\tau\in\{0,0.5,1\}
, or 3)
\beta_{w}=\beta_{g}
. The first two conditions are unlikely to hold in practice as we don’t expect
W
and
G
to be perfectly correlated, or for people to spend exactly 0%, 50%, or 100% of the time in their home area. The final condition implies that pollution is equally harmful in home and neighboring areas, which is reasonable in some settings, but can fail to hold in others. One way in which these effects could differ is if people spend more time outside while in neighboring areas, whereas they spend more time inside when in their home area. Indoor and outdoor pollution can vary substantially, which would lead to differences in the impact of these two pollution sources. This shows the importance of separating the effects of pollution of
W
and
G
as it will provide unbiased estimates of health effects regardless of the value of
(\tau,\rho,\beta_{w},\beta_{g})
.

3.2
General bias formulas

Now we investigate the bias incurred by ignoring mobility when estimating
\lambda(\bm{w},\bm{g},\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{\delta}_{g}).
without assuming univariate exposures or a linear model. We can define the value obtained when ignoring
\bm{G}
by
\lambda_{naive}(\bm{w},\bm{g},\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{\delta}_{g})
, which is given below:

\displaystyle\lambda_{naive}(\bm{w},\bm{g},\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{\delta}_{g})

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{x}}\Big\{\E(Y\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w}+\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{X}=\bm{x})-\E(Y\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Big\}dF(\bm{x}).

This quantity targets an analogous quantity to
\lambda(\bm{w},\bm{g},\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{\delta}_{g})
that adjusts for confounders
\bm{X}
and attempts to isolate the effect of
\bm{W}
, but ignores the potential impact of
\bm{G}
. We have shown in Appendix
B
that the difference between
\lambda_{naive}(\bm{w},\bm{g},\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{\delta}_{g})
and
\lambda(\bm{w},\bm{g},\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{\delta}_{g})
is

\displaystyle\E_{X}\bigg[

\displaystyle\E(Y\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{G}=\bm{g},\bm{X}=\bm{x})-\E_{G\mid W,X}[\E(Y\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{G},\bm{X}=\bm{x})]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\E_{G\mid W,X}[\E(Y\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w}+\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{G},\bm{X}=\bm{x})]-\E(Y\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w}+\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{G}=\bm{g}+\bm{\delta}_{g},\bm{X}=\bm{x})\bigg]

This result shows that we are only guaranteed to obtain unbiased estimates of
\lambda(\bm{w},\bm{g},\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{\delta}_{g})
when
\E(Y|\bm{W},\bm{G},\bm{X})=\E(Y|\bm{W},\bm{X})
. This would only occur if
\bm{G}
has no effect on
Y
, or if
\bm{W}
and
\bm{G}
are perfectly correlated. The reason for this bias is that the naïve approach, which does not account for
\bm{G}
, effectively averages over the range of possible values for
\bm{G}
, instead of fixing it at the desired level of
\bm{g}
or
\bm{g}+\bm{\delta}_{g}
.

4
Incorporating cell phone mobility data

All ideas presented thus far hold for any choice of
g(\cdot)
, but now we discuss this choice further in the context of air pollution studies incorporating cell phone mobility data. We can first define an
n\times n
matrix
\bm{T}
, such that
T_{ij}
represents the total time that cell phone users from zip code
i
spent in zip code
j
. Aggregated mobility data is provided by Cuebiq, a location intelligence platform. Data is collected from anonymized users who have opted-in to provide access to their location data anonymously, through a CCPA and GDPR-compliant framework. Through its Social Impact program, Cuebiq provides mobility insights for academic research and humanitarian initiatives. The Cuebiq responsible data sharing framework enables research partners to query anonymized and privacy-enhanced data, by providing access to an auditable, on-premise Data Cleanroom environment. All final outputs provided to partners are aggregated in order to preserve privacy.

Given these estimates of mobility, we define
\tau_{i}=T_{ii}/\sum_{k}T_{ik},
which is the proportion of time that individuals in zip code
i
spent in their home region. Next, define
\bm{\alpha}_{i}
to be an
n-
dimensional vector of weights such that
\alpha_{ii}=0
and

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}=\frac{T_{ij}}{\sum_{k\neq i}T_{ik}}\text{ for }i\neq j.

These weights reflect the proportion of the time spent outside of their home region that was spent in zip code
j
. As before, we define
\bm{W}_{i}
be the exposure to air pollution in zip code
i
. Our mobility-based exposure is given by
\bm{G}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{ij}\bm{W}_{j},

which is a weighted average of exposure in other zip code weighted by the amount of time spent in each zip code. Figure
1
shows these home and mobility based exposures for yearly average
PM
2.5

levels in Connecticut in 2016. The left panel shows that exposure is elevated along the main interstate in Connecticut as well as near the largest city, Hartford. The right panel shows that individuals in other regions are also exposed to these elevated
PM
2.5

levels due to their daily travel patterns.

5
Misspecification of weights

In this section, we assess the robustness of our findings to misspecification of
\tau_{i}
, which is important because 1) the population from the cell phone mobility data may not be representative of the population that we are estimating the causal effect for, and 2) the values of
\tau_{i}
are estimates from cell phone mobility data and there is inherent variability in these estimates. For simplicity of calculations, we use the scenario of Section
3.1
and let both
W
and
G
be univariate random variables with correlation
\rho
. Throughout we denote the true values as
\tau_{i}^{*}
, but we incorrectly assume that it is
\tau_{i}
. The true regression function in this setting is given by

\displaystyle\E(Y_{i}\mid W,G)=\beta_{w}^{*}\tau_{i}^{*}W_{i}+\beta_{g}^{*}(1-\tau_{i}^{*})G_{i},

(1)

and our goal is to estimate
\omega(\bm{\Delta})
with
\bm{\Delta}=\vec{1}_{n}
. In this setting, the expectation of the estimand is
\beta_{w}^{*}\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 1.63547pt\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 3.4162pt\makebox[0.0pt][c]{\rule[8.17754pt]{8.12407pt}{0.43057pt}}}{\tau^{*}}+\beta_{g}^{*}(1-\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 1.63547pt\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 3.4162pt\makebox[0.0pt][c]{\rule[8.17754pt]{8.12407pt}{0.43057pt}}}{\tau^{*}})
where
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 1.63547pt\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 3.4162pt\makebox[0.0pt][c]{\rule[8.17754pt]{8.12407pt}{0.43057pt}}}{\tau^{*}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}^{*}/n
. For brevity, we denote the estimand by
\omega
and its estimator using misspecified weights by
\widehat{\omega}=\widehat{\beta}_{w}\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 1.11942pt\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 1.57968pt\makebox[0.0pt][c]{\rule[5.59721pt]{4.45105pt}{0.43057pt}}}{\tau}+\widehat{\beta}_{g}(1-\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 1.11942pt\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 1.57968pt\makebox[0.0pt][c]{\rule[5.59721pt]{4.45105pt}{0.43057pt}}}{\tau})
, where
\widehat{\beta}_{w}
and
\widehat{\beta}_{g}
are obtained from fitting model
1
with
\tau_{i}
instead of
\tau_{i}^{*}
.

5.1
Misspecification by scalar multiplication

We first study misspecification of the form
\tau_{i}=\tau_{i}^{*}/c
for all
i
and
c>0
. This scenario can occur when the target population differs from the one used to measure mobility. We focus on this situation specifically, as in Section
7
we estimate causal effects in the Medicare population in the United States, which contains individuals over 65 years of age, who are likely to travel less than the overall population observed in the cell phone mobility data. Assuming further that
\tau
is independent of exposure levels, we show in Appendix
C
that the asymptotic bias is given by

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\E(\widehat{\omega}-\omega)=\frac{\beta_{g}^{*}(1-c)(1-\rho)\text{Var}(\tau)\Big[\rho\E(\tau)-(1+\rho)\E(\tau^{2})\Big]}{\Big(\E(\tau^{2})\E[(1-\tau)^{2}]-\rho^{2}\E^{2}[\tau(1-\tau)]\Big)}

The bias depends on 1) the degree of misspecification, given by
1-c
, 2) the correlation between
W
and
G
, and 3) the distribution of
\tau
across the units. Clearly, the bias will be close to zero if
c
is near 1 and we have very little misspecification. We also get unbiased results when
\rho=1
or
\rho=\E(\tau^{2})/\{\E(\tau)+\E(\tau^{2})\}
. Lastly, the magnitude of bias is a function of the variability in
\tau
(and therefore
\tau^{*}
). In the extreme case where individuals spend equal amounts of time in their home zip code and
\var(\tau^{*})=0
, our estimate is unbiased. It is important to note that both
\rho
and the moments of
\tau
can be estimated from the observed data, and therefore we can estimate the degree of bias that would result from such misspecification. We have observed that when travel weights are misspecified by 50%
(0.5<c<1.5)
and
\rho
is greater than 0.4, which is realistic for environmental applications, the resulting bias is typically bounded above by
0.05\beta_{g}^{*}
. This is a very small degree of bias despite a large degree of misspecification, which shows that effect estimates are moderately robust to this form of misspecification.

5.2
Misspecification due to measurement error

Now we consider a scenario where
\tau^{*}_{i}=\tau_{i}+\eta_{i}\in[0,1]
and
\eta_{i}
are independent and identically distributed random variables having a mean of 0 and a finite second moment. In Appendix
C
, we show that this leads to asymptotically unbiased estimation. Arguably a more realistic scenario, however, is one where
\tau_{i}=\tau_{i}^{*}+\eta_{i}
. This is plausible in our scenario, as the mobility estimates are taken from a finite sample size of cell phone users, and therefore there will be variability in these estimates around their true values. For ease of calculation, we consider the situation where
W
and
G
are independent, i.e.
\rho=0
. This scenario produces the largest bias in Section
3.1
unless the home and the neighborhood exposures are negatively correlated, which is unlikely in practice. Given that this is essentially a worst case scenario, the bias expression presented below can be used as a bound of the bias we would get when the mobility weights are measured with noise. As shown in Appendix
C
,
\E(\widehat{\omega}-\omega)
converges to

\displaystyle\beta_{w}^{*}\left\{\frac{\E[(1-\tau^{*}-\eta)^{2}]\E[{\tau^{*}}^{2}]}{\E[(\tau^{*}+\eta)^{2}]\E[(1-\tau^{*}-\eta)^{2}]}\E(\tau^{*})-\E(\tau^{*})\right\}+

\displaystyle\qquad\beta_{g}^{*}\left\{\frac{\E[(\tau^{*}+\eta)^{2}]\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}]}{\E[(\tau^{*}+\eta)^{2}]\E[(1-\tau^{*}-\eta)^{2}]}\E(1-\tau^{*})-\E(1-\tau^{*})\right\}

\displaystyle\equiv\beta_{w}^{*}\left\{\xi_{w}\E(\tau^{*})-\E(\tau^{*})\right\}+\beta_{g}^{*}\left\{\xi_{g}\E(1-\tau^{*})-\E(1-\tau^{*})\right\}.

We show in Appendix
C
that both
\xi_{w}
and
\xi_{g}
are continuous, decreasing functions of
\E(\eta^{2})\geq 0
with
\lim_{\E(\eta^{2})\rightarrow\infty}\xi_{w}=\lim_{\E(\eta^{2})\rightarrow\infty}\xi_{g}=0
and positive second derivatives. If there is no measurement error, i.e.,
\E(\eta^{2})=0
, then
\xi_{w}=\xi_{g}=1
, and we have unbiased estimates. To highlight a situation with significant amounts of measurement error, assume that
\tau^{*}
follows a uniform distribution between 0.25 and 0.75, and the measurement error
\eta
is uniformly distributed between -0.25 and 0.25. In this scenario,
\xi_{w}=\xi_{g}=0.93
, which leads to very small amounts of bias. A crucial finding from this result is that measurement error leads to conservative estimates of causal effects as long as the signs of
\beta_{w}^{*}
and
\beta_{g}^{*}
are the same, which is realistic in practice.

6
Estimation

As described in Appendix
A
, all estimands described in Section
2.2
can be estimated using the conditional mean of the outcome,
\E(Y|\bm{W},\bm{G},\bm{X})
, which we model as

\displaystyle\E(Y|\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{G}=\bm{g},\bm{X}=\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\tau f(\bm{w})+(1-\tau)h(\bm{g})+l(\bm{x})

where
f(\cdot)
,
h(\cdot)
, and
l(\cdot)
are arbitrary functions. We work within the Bayesian paradigm and obtain posterior samples of the mean outcome regression, which we denote as
\E^{(b)}(Y|\bm{W},\bm{G},\bm{X})
for
b=1,\dots,B
posterior samples. If interest is in estimating a mean potential outcome, such as
\E[Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})]
, this can be done by obtaining posterior samples of

\E^{(b)}[Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}^{(b)}\E^{(b)}(Y|\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{G}=\bm{g},\bm{X}=\bm{X}_{i})\quad\text{for }b=1,\dots,B,

where
\bm{\gamma}\sim\text{Dirichlet}(1,\dots,1)
are weights for each individual with expectation
\E(\gamma_{i})=1/n
as in the Bayesian bootstrap
(
Rubin, 1981
)
. The weights are utilized to account for uncertainty in using the observed covariate distribution to approximate the true covariate distribution in the population. Point estimation and inference can be obtained directly from this posterior distribution through posterior summaries. We can also use this model to target sample level estimands such as
\omega(\bm{\Delta})
. These estimands include
Y_{i}(\bm{W}_{i},\bm{G}_{i})
, which is equal to
Y_{i}
under Assumption
1
. For this reason, posterior samples of this estimand can be calculated as

\displaystyle\omega^{(b)}(\bm{\Delta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg\{\E^{(b)}(Y|\bm{W}=\bm{W}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{wi},\bm{G}=\bm{G}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{gi},\bm{X}=\bm{X}_{i})-Y_{i}\bigg\}.

Obtaining posterior samples of direct or spillover effects, such as
\omega_{dir}(\bm{\Delta})
or
\omega_{sp}(\bm{\Delta})
, can be done analogously.

We first explore a Bayesian semiparametric model that allows for nonlinear associations between exposures and the outcome, that additionally incorporates shrinkage priors that assume the effects of
\bm{W}
and
\bm{G}
are not drastically different. Incorporating this information a priori can lead to more efficient estimates of the
f(\cdot)
and
h(\cdot)
functions as information can be borrowed across the two functions, while still allowing them to differ. We accomplish this through the following regression model:

\displaystyle\E(Y|\bm{W},\bm{G},\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{i}\bm{\theta}+\tau_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\beta_{jm}\phi_{m}(w_{ij})+(1-\tau_{i})\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\beta_{jm}+\zeta_{jm})\phi_{m}(g_{ij})

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{i}\bm{\theta}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\Big\{\tau_{i}\bm{\phi}_{wj}\bm{\beta}_{j}+(1-\tau_{i})\bm{\phi}_{gj}(\bm{\beta}_{j}+\bm{\zeta}_{j})\Big\},

where
\{\phi_{m}(\cdot)\}_{m=1}^{M}
is a set of orthogonal basis functions,
\bm{\phi}_{wj}=(\phi_{1}(w_{ij}),\dots,\phi_{M}(w_{ij}))
, and
\bm{\beta}_{j}=(\beta_{j1},\dots,\beta_{jM})
. All other vectors are constructed analogously. We have a common parameter vector
\bm{\beta}_{j}
used for estimating both the
f(\cdot)
and
h(\cdot)
functions, and we let
\bm{\zeta}_{j}
capture deviations between these two functions. To encourage shrinkage across these two functions we adopt the following horseshoe prior distribution
(
Carvalho et al., 2010
)
:

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{j}|\sigma^{2},\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\sim MVN(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\textbf{I}),\qquad\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\sim IG(1,1)\qquad\sigma^{2}\sim IG(1,1)

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}_{j}|\sigma^{2},\lambda_{j}^{2},\nu^{2}\sim MVN(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}\nu^{2}\textbf{I}),\qquad\lambda_{j}\sim C^{+}(1),\qquad\nu\sim C^{+}(1)

where
\sigma^{2}
is the residual variance of
Y
,
MVN(\cdot,\cdot)
is the multivariate normal distribution,
IG(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Inverse-Gamma distribution, and
C^{+}(k)
denotes the Half-Cauchy distribution with the density function
f(z)=1/\{\pi k(1+z^{2}/k^{2})\}
. This prior distribution shrinks
\bm{\zeta}_{j}
towards zero if the effects of the home and neighborhood exposures are similar, which can lead to more efficient estimation of causal effects. Finishing the prior specification, we use a non-informative normal prior distribution for
\bm{\theta}
. Sampling from this model is relatively straightforward by introducing additional latent variables that allow for conditionally conjugate updates of each parameter within a Gibbs sampling algorithm. Details can be found in Appendix
D
. A simulation study highlighting the performance of this shrinkage prior distribution can be found in Appendix
E
. These simulations show that shrinkage can drastically improve estimation of causal effects when the home and mobility exposures have similar effects, and does not negatively impact inference when these effects are very different. It also highlights that even in the presence of misspecified mobility parameters, accounting for mobility tends to perform better than ignoring mobility completely.

This model can be extended in a number of different directions by including nonlinear effects of covariates
\bm{X}
or including interactions between exposures. Fully nonparametric Bayesian approaches could also be used and have proven effective for causal inference problems
(
Linero and Antonelli, 2022
;
Shin et al., 2024
)
. We restrict attention to the basis function model here for computational speed as our subsequent health effects analysis entails a very large sample size, and to facilitate the aforementioned shrinkage of the two functions. In principle, however, this shrinkage could be extended to nonparametric prior distributions as well, and the effects of air pollution would be estimated with additional flexibility.

8
Discussion

In this paper, we introduced a novel approach to estimating the causal effects of multivariate exposures in the presence of spatial interference due to mobility of individuals across different regions. By incorporating cell phone mobility data, we are able to obtain improved estimates of the overall impact of air pollution. We proposed policy-relevant causal estimands, and derived bias formulas for them when the no-interference assumption is wrongly assumed. We developed a new estimation procedure that allows us to incorporate the prior knowledge that the effects of the home and mobility-based exposures are likely similar, while still allowing them to differ if needed. Theoretical results and simulation studies suggest that the proposed model gives us a better assessment of the causal effects of air pollution, and that ignoring mobility will likely lead to under-estimation of the effects of air pollution. Lastly, we applied our framework to a large-scale national study of the health effects of air pollution mixtures on mortality in the Medicare population of the United States, and found a significant impact of air pollution on mortality. In situations where the correlation between home and mobility exposures was lower, incorporating mobility tended to increase the magnitude of these effect estimates, suggesting this could be useful for studies moving forward.

While this paper is the first to incorporate people’s mobility into its analysis using an interference approach, there are limitations to our approach and associated future research directions. One drawback of our study is that the cell phone mobility data comes from the general United States population in 2019, whereas our inferences are on the Medicare population in the years 2000-2016. While national mobility patterns have likely not shifted too substantially during this time period, the Medicare cohort represents individuals over 65 years of age, and therefore their mobility likely differs from the general population. We derived some robustness results protecting against misspecification of mobility, and our simulations suggest that accounting for mobility with misspecified mobility patterns is still better than ignoring mobility altogether (which is itself a form of misspecifying mobility). Despite this, future research could aim to find mobility data that more closely aligns with the population being studied. An additional issue that is true for all causal analyses of observational data, is that our results rely on certain untestable assumptions, and therefore sensitivity analysis to these assumptions could be incorporated to strengthen our findings. It is important to note, however, that while we focused on causal effects, our findings about the impact of mobility would also apply to general (non-causal) conditional associations of air pollutant health effects, and are therefore applicable to a wide range of environmental studies.

Appendix A
Positivity assumption and identification

In the manuscript, we noted both the interference version of SUTVA and unconfoundedness as key assumptions necessary to infer causal effects from observational data in our setting. In addition to those assumptions, we must also make a positivity assumption on the joint distribution of the exposures and covariates. While we could posit a general positivity assumption that would apply to both estimands, we instead present two separate assumptions, each tailored to a corresponding estimand. This allows the respective assumptions to be weaker, as positivity only needs to hold for certain exposure and covariate values, and these differ across estimands. Recall that the population level estimand
\lambda(\cdot)
is defined as
\lambda(\bm{w},\bm{g},\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{\delta}_{g})=\E[Y(\bm{w}+\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{g}+\bm{\delta}_{g})]-\E[Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})]
. This estimand imposes a fixed set of home and mobility-based exposures for all values of the covariates. Therefore, we need the joint density of
\bm{W}
and
\bm{G}
to be positive at both
(\bm{w},\bm{g})
and
(\bm{w}+\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{g}+\bm{\delta}_{g})
for all covariate values. Formally, we require

\displaystyle f_{\bm{W},\bm{G}|\bm{X}}((\bm{w},\bm{g})|\bm{X}=\bm{x})>0\hskip 14.45377pt\text{and}\hskip 14.45377ptf_{\bm{W},\bm{G}|\bm{X}}((\bm{w}+\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{g}+\bm{\delta}_{g})|\bm{X}=\bm{x})>0,

to hold with probability 1, where the probability is taken over the distribution of covariates
\bm{X}
. Note that here, we have defined
f_{\bm{W},\bm{G}|\bm{X}}
to be the conditional joint density of
\bm{W}
and
\bm{G}
given
\bm{X}
. Given this assumption, along with the assumptions outlined in Section
2.1
, this estimand is identified according to the following:

\displaystyle\E[Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})]

\displaystyle=\E\Big\{\E[Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}]\Big\}

\displaystyle=\E\Big\{\E[Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{G}=\bm{g},\bm{X}=\bm{x}]\Big\}

\displaystyle=\E\Big\{\E[Y\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{G}=\bm{g},\bm{X}=\bm{x}]\Big\}

The third equality holds from the interference SUTVA assumption, while the second equality relies on both unconfoundedness and positivity. Unconfoundedness allows us to condition on
\bm{G}
and
\bm{W}
due to conditional independence of
Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})
and
(\bm{W},\bm{G})
. Positivity ensures that the set we are conditioning on is well defined.

The sample level estimand
\omega(\bm{\Delta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\{Y_{i}(\bm{W}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{wi},\bm{G}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{gi})-Y_{i}(\bm{W}_{i},\bm{G}_{i})\big\}
differs in multiple ways. For one, it employs different exposure values and interventions for each observation, separately. Additionally, it is a sample-level estimand and therefore is not strictly identifiable in the sense that it can be written solely as a function of the observed data distribution. Nonetheless, we are using
E(Y\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{G}=\bm{g},\bm{X}=\bm{X}_{i})
as an estimator for the unknown
Y_{i}(\bm{w},\bm{g})
, and therefore a corresponding positivity assumption helps ensure that there is empirical support in the data for estimating
E(Y\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{G}=\bm{g},\bm{X}=\bm{X}_{i})
and that we are not overly reliant on model extrapolation. Since our goal is estimating
Y_{i}(\bm{W}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{wi},\bm{G}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{gi})
for each
i
, our corresponding positivity assumption is

\displaystyle f_{\bm{W},\bm{G}|\bm{X}}((\bm{W}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{wi},\bm{G}_{i}+\bm{\Delta}_{gi})|\bm{X}=\bm{X}_{i})>0\hskip 14.45377pt\text{for}\hskip 14.45377pti=1,...,n.

This is arguably a much weaker assumption, particularly for small values of the intervention, because we are guaranteed that
f_{\bm{W},\bm{G}|\bm{X}}((\bm{W}_{i},\bm{G}_{i})|\bm{X}=\bm{X}_{i})>0
by the presence of data point
i
. This also shows why we do not need to make a similar positivity assumption at the current set of exposures
(\bm{W}_{i},\bm{G}_{i})
because it is already guaranteed from our observed data.

Appendix B
Derivation of bias results

Here we provide justification for the bias calculations seen in Section
3
. We begin with the simple setting of linear models from Section
3.1
, and then move to the more general results of Section
3.2
.

B.1
Linear model setting

Recall that we investigate the following two traditional approaches:

\displaystyle(\tilde{\beta}_{0},\tilde{\beta}_{w})

\displaystyle=\underset{\beta_{0},\beta}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \E[(Y-\beta_{0}-W\beta)^{2}]

\displaystyle(\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{*},\tilde{\beta}_{w}^{*})

\displaystyle=\underset{\beta_{0},\beta}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \E[(Y-\beta_{0}-W^{*}\beta)^{2}].

We write

\displaystyle\E[(Y-\beta_{0}-W\beta)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E[(\tau W\beta_{w}+(1-\tau)G\beta_{g}+\epsilon-\beta_{0}-W\beta)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E[W^{2}\beta^{2}-2W\beta\{\tau W\beta_{x}+(1-\tau)G\beta_{g}-\beta_{0}+\epsilon\}

\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt+\{\tau W\beta_{x}+(1-\tau)G\beta_{g}-\beta_{0}+\epsilon\}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}-2\beta\E[\tau W^{2}\beta_{x}+(1-\tau)WG\beta_{g}-W\beta_{0}+W\epsilon]+C

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}-2\beta\E[\tau W^{2}\beta_{x}+(1-\tau)WG\beta_{g}]+C

for some constant
C
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{w}=\E[\tau W^{2}\beta_{x}+(1-\tau)WG\beta_{g}]=\tau\beta_{x}+(1-\tau)\rho\beta_{g}.

Similarly, since

\displaystyle\E[(Y-\beta_{0}-W^{*}\beta)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E[[\tau W\beta_{w}+(1-\tau)G\beta_{g}+\epsilon-\beta_{0}-\{\tau W+(1-\tau)G\}\beta]^{2}]

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}^{2}-2\beta_{0}\E[\tau W\beta_{w}+(1-\tau)G\beta_{g}+\epsilon-\{\tau W+(1-\tau)G\}\beta]+C

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}^{2}+C,

\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{*}=0
, and given
\tilde{\beta}_{0}=\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{*}=0
, we can write

\displaystyle\E[(Y-\beta_{0}-W^{*}\beta)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E[[\tau W\beta_{w}+(1-\tau)G\beta_{g}+\epsilon-\{\tau W+(1-\tau)G\}\beta]^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E[\{\tau W+(1-\tau)G\}^{2}]\beta^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt-2\E[\{\tau W+(1-\tau)G\}\{\tau W\beta_{w}+(1-\tau)G\beta_{g}+\epsilon\}]\beta+C.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{w}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{\E[\{\tau W+(1-\tau)G\}\{\tau W\beta_{w}+(1-\tau)G\beta_{g}+\epsilon\}]}{\E[\{\tau W+(1-\tau)G\}^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau^{2}\beta_{w}+\tau(1-\tau)\rho\beta_{g}+\tau(1-\tau)\rho\beta_{w}+(1-\tau)^{2}\beta_{g}}{\tau^{2}+2\tau(1-\tau)\rho+(1-\tau)^{2}}.

Our next goal is to show under what conditions
\tilde{\beta}_{w}^{*}
equals the true causal effect of
\tau\beta_{w}+(1-\tau)\beta_{g}
. To do so, we can take the difference and set it equal to zero:

\displaystyle\frac{\tau^{2}\beta_{w}+\rho\tau(1-\tau)\beta_{w}+\rho\tau(1-\tau)\beta_{g}+(1-\tau)^{2}\beta_{g}}{\tau^{2}+2\tau(1-\tau)+\rho(1-\tau)^{2}}-(\tau\beta_{w}+(1-\tau)\beta_{g})=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\beta_{w}+\rho\tau(1-\tau)\beta_{w}+\rho\tau(1-\tau)\beta_{g}+(1-\tau)^{2}\beta_{g}

\displaystyle-(\tau\beta_{w}+(1-\tau)\beta_{g})(\tau^{2}+2\tau(1-\tau)+\rho(1-\tau)^{2})=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\

\displaystyle\beta_{w}[2(\rho-1)\tau^{3}-3(\rho-1)\tau^{2}+(\rho-1)\tau]+\beta_{g}[-2(\rho-1)\tau^{3}+3(\rho-1)\tau^{2}-(\rho-1)\tau]=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\

\displaystyle\tau(\tau-1)(2\tau-1)(\beta_{w}-\beta_{g})(\rho-1)=0.

This final expression shows that the parameter takes the desired value when any of the following three conditions hold: 1)
\rho=1
, 2)
\tau\in\{0,0.5,1\}
, or 3)
\beta_{w}=\beta_{g}
.

B.2
Bias formula for
\lambda(w,g,\delta_{w},\delta_{g})

First we can write down how to calculate
\E[Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})]
, which is given by

\displaystyle\E[Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})]

\displaystyle=\E_{X}\bigg[\E[Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\E_{X}\bigg[\E[Y(\bm{w},\bm{g})\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{G}=\bm{g},\bm{X}=\bm{x}]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\E_{X}\bigg[\E[Y\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{G}=\bm{g},\bm{X}=\bm{x}]\bigg]

The naive approach that ignores
\bm{G}
takes the following form:

\displaystyle\E_{X}\bigg[\E[Y\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{X}=\bm{x}]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\E_{X}\bigg[\E_{G\mid W,X}\Big(\E[Y\mid\bm{W}=\bm{w},\bm{G}=\bm{g}^{\prime},\bm{X}=\bm{x}]\Big)\bigg].

Now we can see where the difference in these two expressions lies. In the correct expression, the neighboring exposure value is set to the desired level
\bm{g}
, while the naive approach averages over possible
\bm{G}
values instead of fixing at the desired level. This is even more problematic when we try to estimate
\E[Y(\bm{w}+\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{g}+\bm{\delta}_{g})]
as the desired neighborhood exposure level
\bm{g}+\bm{\delta}_{g}
might be very different from the average value of
\bm{G}
under the conditional distribution
\bm{G}\mid\bm{W},\bm{X}
. We can plug in these two expressions into the definition of
\lambda(\bm{w},\bm{g},\bm{\delta}_{w},\bm{\delta}_{g})
to get the overall result.

Misspecification of weights proof

In this section, we prove the bias results presented in Section
5
where we misspecify the travel weights
\tau

\tau^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=\bigg(-2\Big\{\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E(\eta^{2})+\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}]\Big\}\Big\{\E^{2}(\eta^{2})+\{\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})+\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}])\}\E(\eta^{2})+\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}]\Big\}^{2}+

\displaystyle 2\Big\{\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E^{2}(\eta^{2})+2\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}]\E(\eta^{2})+\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E^{2}[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}]\Big\}\Big\{\E^{2}(\eta^{2})+\{\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})+\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}])\}\E(\eta^{2})+\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}]\Big\}

\displaystyle\Big\{\{\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})+\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}])\}+2\E(\eta^{2})\Big\}\bigg)/Den

\displaystyle=\bigg(-\Big\{\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E(\eta^{2})+\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}]\Big\}\Big\{\E^{2}(\eta^{2})+\{\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})+\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}])\}\E(\eta^{2})+\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}]\Big\}

\displaystyle+\Big\{\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E(\eta^{2})+2\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}]\E(\eta^{2})+\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})\E^{2}[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}]\Big\}\Big\{\{\E({\tau^{*}}^{2})+\E[(1-\tau^{*})^{2}])\}+2\E(\eta^{2})\Big\}\bigg)/Den^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(-AB+A^{\prime}B^{\prime})/Den^{\prime}\geq 0

One can see that
0\leq A\leq A^{\prime}
,
0\leq B\leq B^{\prime}
, and
Den^{\prime}>0
, which suggests that
\xi_{w}
is concave which concludes the proof. An analogous proof for
\xi_{g}
is straightforward.

Appendix D
Derivation of full conditional densities

In this section, we derive the full conditional distributions under the prior described in Section
6
. Recall that we consider the following horseshoe regression hierarchy for the regression coefficients:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\bm{\beta}_{j}|\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\sim MVN(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\textbf{I}),\qquad\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\sim IG(1,1)\\
&\bm{\zeta}_{j}|\lambda_{j}^{2},\nu^{2}\sim MVN(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}\nu^{2}\textbf{I}),\qquad\lambda_{j}\sim C^{+}(1),\qquad\nu\sim C^{+}(1)\\
&\sigma^{2}\sim IG(1,1)\end{split}

(3)

where
\sigma^{2}
is the residual variance of
Y
,
\bm{\zeta}_{j}=(\zeta_{j1},\cdots,\zeta_{jM})^{\prime}
,
MVN(\cdot,\cdot)
is the multivariate normal distribution,
IG(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Inverse-Gamma distribution, and
C^{+}(k)
denotes the Half-Cauchy distribution with the density function
f(z)=1/\{\pi k(1+z^{2}/k^{2})\}
. Although the Half-Cauchy distribution is not conditionally conjugate for the normal distribution,
Makalic and Schmidt, 2015
proposed an alternative way to construct the equivalent model by introducing an auxiliary variable with a double Inverse-Gamma structure. It uses the fact that if there are random variables such that
V^{2}|W\sim IG(1/2,1/W)
and
W\sim IG(1/2,1/k^{2})
, then
V\sim C^{+}(k)
. Thus, we define the following hierarchy which is equivalent to the one described in Equation
3
:

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{j}|\sigma^{2},\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\sim MVN(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\textbf{I}),\qquad\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\sim IG(1,1)

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}_{j}|\sigma^{2},\lambda_{j}^{2},\nu^{2}\sim MVN(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}\nu^{2}\textbf{I})

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}^{2}|r_{j}\sim IG(1/2,1/r_{j}),\qquad r_{j}\sim IG(1/2,1)

\displaystyle\nu^{2}|s\sim IG(1/2,1/s),\qquad s\sim IG(1/2,1)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\sim IG(1,1)

Now all priors above lead to conditionally conjugate posterior updates, and therefore Gibbs sampling from their full conditional posterior distributions is straightforward. We can write
\bm{Y}\sim MVN((\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})\bm{\beta}+\bm{\phi}_{g}\bm{\zeta},\sigma^{2}I)
where
\bm{\phi}_{w}
and
\bm{\phi}_{g}
are
n\times qM
matrices with the
i^{th}
row of
\tau_{i}\bm{\phi}_{wi}
and
(1-\tau_{i})\bm{\phi}_{gi}
, respectively. Then, the full conditional posterior distributions are given by:

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}|\cdot\sim MVN(\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\beta}(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})^{\prime}(\bm{y}-\bm{\phi}_{g}\bm{\zeta})/\sigma^{2},\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\beta}),\quad\sigma_{\beta}^{2}|\cdot\sim IG(1+qM/2,1+\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}|\cdot\sim MVN(\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\zeta}\{\bm{\phi}_{g}^{\prime}(\bm{y}-(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})\bm{\beta})/\sigma^{2}\},\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\zeta})

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}^{2}|\cdot\sim IG((1+M)/2,1/r_{j}+\|\bm{\zeta}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}\nu^{2})),\quad r_{j}|\cdot\sim IG(1,1+1/\lambda_{j}^{2})

\displaystyle\nu^{2}|\cdot\sim IG((1+qM)/2,1/s+\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}\bm{\varLambda}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}/(2\sigma^{2})),\quad s|\cdot\sim IG(1,1+1/\nu^{2})

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}|\cdot\sim IG(1+qM+n/2,1+\{\|\bm{y}-(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})\bm{\beta}-\bm{\phi}_{g}\bm{\zeta}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}/\sigma_{\beta}^{2}+\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}\bm{\varLambda}_{*}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}\}/2)

where
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\beta}=\{(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})^{\prime}(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})/\sigma^{2}+1/(\sigma^{2}\sigma_{\beta}^{2})\}^{-1}
.
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\zeta}=\sigma^{2}\{\bm{\phi}_{g}^{\prime}\bm{\phi}_{g}+\bm{\varLambda}_{*}^{-1}\}^{-1}
where
\bm{\varLambda}_{*}=\nu^{2}\bm{\varLambda}
and
\bm{\varLambda}=\diag(\lambda_{1}^{2},\cdots,\lambda_{1}^{2},\lambda_{2}^{2},\cdots,\lambda_{q}^{2})
, a
qM
-dimensional diagonal matrix with the elements being
M
replicates of each
\lambda_{j}
. Now we go into more detail of how to derive each full conditional distribution. The full conditional density of
\bm{\beta}
given all the other parameters and data is

\displaystyle\pi(\bm{\beta}|\cdot)

\displaystyle\propto p(\bm{y}|\cdot)\times\pi(\bm{\beta}|\sigma^{2},\sigma_{\beta}^{2})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\bm{y}-(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})\bm{\beta}-\bm{\phi}_{g}\bm{\zeta}\|_{2}^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2\sigma^{2}\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1}{2}\Big[\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\{(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})^{\prime}(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})/\sigma^{2}+1/(\sigma^{2}\sigma_{\beta}^{2})\}\bm{\beta}-2\bm{\beta}^{\prime}(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})^{\prime}(\bm{y}-\bm{\phi}_{g}\bm{\zeta})/\sigma^{2}\Big]\bigg)

which is a kernel of the multivariate normal distribution with the covariance matrix
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\beta}=\{(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})^{\prime}(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})/\sigma^{2}+1/(\sigma^{2}\sigma_{\beta}^{2})\}^{-1}
and the mean vector
\widetilde{\mu}_{\beta}=\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\beta}(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})^{\prime}(\bm{y}-\bm{\phi}_{g}\bm{\zeta})/\sigma^{2}
. Since
\sigma_{\beta}^{2}
affects the likelihood only through
\bm{\beta}
, the full conditional density of
\sigma_{\beta}^{2}
is

\displaystyle\pi(\sigma_{\beta}^{2}|\cdot)

\displaystyle\propto\pi(\bm{\beta}|\sigma^{2},\sigma_{\beta}^{2})\times\pi(\sigma_{\beta}^{2})

\displaystyle\propto\dfrac{1}{(\sigma_{\beta}^{2})^{qM/2}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}\bigg)\times\frac{1}{(\sigma_{\beta}^{2})^{1+1}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{1}{(\sigma_{\beta}^{2})^{1+1+qM/2}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{1+\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}{\sigma_{\beta}^{2}}\bigg).

Thus, it follows
IG(1+qM/2,1+\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))
. For the full conditional distribution of
\bm{\zeta}=(\bm{\zeta}_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{q}^{\prime})^{\prime}
, we define
\bm{\varLambda}=\diag(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{q})
, a
qM
-dimensional diagonal matrix with the elements of
M
replicates of each
\lambda_{j}
, and
\bm{\varLambda}_{*}=\nu^{2}\bm{\varLambda}
. Given the independence priors for
\bm{\zeta}_{j}
’s, we observe

\displaystyle\pi(\bm{\zeta}|\cdot)

\displaystyle\propto p(\bm{y}|\cdot)\times\prod_{j=1}^{q}\pi(\bm{\zeta}_{j}|\sigma^{2},\lambda_{j}^{2},\nu^{2})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\bm{y}-(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})\bm{\beta}-\bm{\phi}_{g}\bm{\zeta}\|_{2}^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}\bm{\varLambda}_{*}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}\bigg)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1}{2}\Big[\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}\Big\{\dfrac{\bm{\phi}_{g}^{\prime}\bm{\phi}_{g}+\bm{\varLambda}_{*}^{-1}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big\}\bm{\zeta}-2\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}\{\bm{\phi}_{g}^{\prime}(\bm{y}-(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})\bm{\beta})/\sigma^{2}\}\Big]\bigg),

which is again a kernel of the multivariate normal distribution with the covariance matrix
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\zeta}=\sigma^{2}\{\bm{\phi}_{g}^{\prime}\bm{\phi}_{g}+\bm{\varLambda}_{*}^{-1}\}^{-1}
and the mean vector
\widetilde{\mu}_{\zeta}=\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\zeta}\{\bm{\phi}_{g}^{\prime}(\bm{y}-(\bm{\phi}_{w}+\bm{\phi}_{g})\bm{\beta})/\sigma^{2}\}
. The conditional posterior of the local and global variance parameters are conjugate as

\displaystyle\pi(\lambda_{j}^{2}|\cdot)

\displaystyle\propto\pi(\bm{\zeta}_{j}|\sigma^{2},\lambda_{j}^{2},\nu^{2})\times\pi(\lambda_{j}^{2}|r_{j})

\displaystyle\propto\dfrac{1}{(\lambda_{j}^{2})^{M/2}}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{\|\bm{\zeta}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}\nu^{2}}\bigg)\dfrac{1}{(\lambda_{j}^{2})^{1/2+1}}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1/r_{j}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\propto\dfrac{1}{(\lambda_{j}^{2})^{(1+M)/2+1}}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1/r_{j}+\|\bm{\zeta}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}\nu^{2})}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\pi(\nu^{2}|\cdot)

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{j=1}^{q}\pi(\bm{\zeta}_{j}|\sigma^{2},\lambda_{j}^{2},\nu^{2})\times\pi(\nu^{2}|s)

\displaystyle\propto\dfrac{1}{(\nu^{2})^{qM/2}}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}\bm{\varLambda}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}}{2\sigma^{2}\nu^{2}}\bigg)\dfrac{1}{(\nu^{2})^{1/2+1}}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1/s}{\nu^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\propto\dfrac{1}{(\nu^{2})^{(1+qM)/2+1}}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1/s+\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}\bm{\varLambda}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}/(2\sigma^{2})}{\nu^{2}}\bigg)

and therefore,
\lambda_{j}^{2}|\cdot\sim IG((1+M)/2,1/r_{j}+\|\bm{\zeta}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}\nu^{2}))
for all
j=1,...,q
and
\nu^{2}|\cdot\sim IG((1+qM)/2,1/s+\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}\bm{\varLambda}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}/(2\sigma^{2}))
. Further, the updates for the auxiliary variables are

\displaystyle\pi(r_{j}|\cdot)

\displaystyle\propto\pi(\lambda_{j}^{2}|r_{j})\times\pi(r_{j})

\displaystyle\propto\dfrac{1}{r_{j}^{1/2}}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1/r_{j}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\bigg)\dfrac{1}{(r_{j})^{1/2+1}}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1}{r_{j}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\propto\dfrac{1}{(r_{j})^{1+1}}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1+1/\lambda_{j}^{2}}{r_{j}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\pi(s|\cdot)

\displaystyle\propto\pi(\nu^{2}|s)\times\pi(s)

\displaystyle\propto\dfrac{1}{s^{1/2}}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1/s}{\nu^{2}}\bigg)\dfrac{1}{(s)^{1/2+1}}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1}{s}\bigg)

\displaystyle\propto\dfrac{1}{(s)^{1+1}}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1+1/\nu^{2}}{s}\bigg)

r_{j}|\cdot\sim IG(1,1+1/\lambda_{j}^{2})

\displaystyle\pi(\sigma^{2}|\cdot)

Appendix E
Simulation Studies

In our simulation studies, we generate data in the following manner:

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{i}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}MVN(\mathbf{0}_{q},\Sigma_{q\times q})\quad\text{ for $i=1,...,n$ where }\Sigma_{jk}=\begin{cases}1&j=k\\
0.7^{|j-k|}&j\neq k\end{cases}.

Throughout we set
q=5
, and
n=1000
. Given that correlation between pollutants is frequently observed in environmental studies, we focus on the case where pollutants are highly correlated. Next, we generate mobility weights
\tau_{i}
from a
\nbeta(30,10)
distribution, so that people travel outside of their home area for 75% of the day on average. Further, we assume that people in each region travel to 10 different areas, i.e.
\sum_{j}1(\alpha_{ij}\neq 0)=10
, each having similar pollution levels to the home region in order to make home and neighborhood exposures positively correlated. This was done to match what we found empirically in Section
4
. Mathematically, for the
i^{th}
region, we sample 10 indices from
(1,...,i-1,i+1,...n)
with probabilities of
(p_{i,1},...,p_{i,i-1},p_{i,i+1},...,p_{i,n})
where
p_{i,j}\propto\exp\big(-0.4\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}_{i}-\widetilde{\bm{W}}_{j}\|_{2}\big)
. This leads to an average correlation between home and neighborhood exposures of 0.7. After selecting where to travel, for each
j\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
where
\mathcal{I}_{i}
is an index set of travel regions of the
i^{th}
region, a travel proportion
\alpha_{ij}
is determined by its selection probability scaled by the sum of selection probabilities of the 10 regions,
p_{i,j}/\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{i}}p_{i,k}
. Given
\tau
,
\bm{\alpha}
,
\bm{W}
, and
\bm{G}
, we let the outcome be generated by

\displaystyle Y_{i}

\displaystyle=\tau_{i}\bm{\phi}_{wi}\bm{\beta}+(1-\tau_{i})\bm{\phi}_{gi}\bm{\gamma}+\epsilon_{i}

\displaystyle=\tau_{i}\bm{\phi}_{wi}\bm{\beta}+(1-\tau_{i})\bm{\phi}_{gi}(\bm{\beta}+\bm{\zeta})+\epsilon_{i}

where
\epsilon_{i}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^{2})
, and
\bm{\phi}_{wi}
and
\bm{\phi}_{gi}
are
3^{rd}
-degree orthogonalized polynomial basis expansions of
\bm{W}_{i}
and
\bm{G}_{i}
, respectively. We set

\bm{\beta}=(.5,.5,0,0,.3,.3,0,0,.7,.7,0,...,0)^{\prime}

so the first 4 exposures have up to cubic effects on the outcome. We assume that the difference between the effects of home and neighborhood exposures follows
\bm{\zeta}\sim MVN(0,\sigma^{2}_{\zeta}\vec{I})
. We expect the home and neighborhood exposures to have similar, but not necessarily the same, effects on the outcome, and therefore, we simulate three different scenarios: 1)
\sigma_{\zeta}=0
(
No difference
), 2)
\sigma_{\zeta}=0.15
(
Small difference
), and 3)
\sigma_{\zeta}=0.3
(
Moderate difference
).

Our goal is to understand the sample average causal effect of a 0.5 increment of each exposure in all areas, which corresponds to estimating
\omega(\bm{\Delta})
with
\bm{\Delta}_{1}=\cdots=\bm{\Delta}_{n}=(.5,...,.5)^{\prime}
. For all approaches except for the naive approach, we also estimate
\omega_{dir}(\bm{\Delta})
and
\omega_{sp}(\bm{\Delta})
as defined in Section
2.2
.
The results of the simulation are presented in Figure
8
where all metrics are averaged over 500 data sets. We examine the mean squared error (MSE) as well as the coverage obtained from our 95% credible intervals. To facilitate comparisons across scenarios, the MSE for each scenario is divided by the minimum MSE across all estimators for that scenario, which implies that an MSE of one is the lowest MSE possible. We consider 4 different methods: 1) a model that employs the shrinkage prior to the difference between home and neighborhood coefficients described in Section
6
(Shrinkage), 2) the same model without the shrinkage prior (Non-shrinkage), 3) a misspecified model that utilizes shrinkage, but also uses an incorrectly measured
\tau
that is
0.75
times the true mobility weights (Misspecified), and 4) a standard model that does not use mobility information at all, which is standard in air pollution studies (No mobility). We find that all models that incorporate mobility weights outperform the
no mobility
approach both in terms of the MSE and coverage, which is expected given our bias results presented in Section
3
. We also see reasonably good results in terms of coverage for the models that use a misspecified
\tau
, which is expected given our bias results in Section
5
, but it negatively affects the MSE. Lastly, we see significant improvements in MSE by incorporating shrinkage of the two functions into the model, and the shrinkage model outperforms the model without shrinkage, even when there are moderate differences between the true home and mobility-based effects.
