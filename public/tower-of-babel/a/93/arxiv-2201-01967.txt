Fibered multicategory theory
Claudio Pisani

Abstract
Given a fibration in groupoids d : 𝒟 → ℐ d:{\cal D}\to{\cal I} , we define a fibered multicategory as a particular functor p : ℳ → ℐ p:{\cal M}\to{\cal I} , where ℳ {\cal M} has the same objects as 𝒟 {\cal D} , and its arrows a : X → Y a:X\to Y should be thought of as families of arrows in the multicategory, indexed by p ​ Y pY . The key axiom extends the reindexing of objects, given by d d , to a reindexing of arrows in ℳ {\cal M} along pullback squares in ℐ {\cal I} . When 𝒟 {\cal D} is included in ℳ {\cal M} , in an appropriate sense, one gets again fibered categories. In this context, cartesian fibered multicategories are defined and studied in a natural way.

Introduction

In the last decades, generalized multicategories have been the object of renewed interest
(see for instance
[
Leinster, 2003
]
,
[
Hermida, 2004
]
and
[
Cruttwell & Shulman, 2010
]
).
The focus is usually set on the monad that gives the generalized domains of arrows, and on
the appropriate environment for the monad itself.
Our emphasis here lies rather on the fibered aspects of a symmetric multicategory
{\cal M}
, which derive from
considering
families
of arrows in
{\cal M}
as the arrows of a
category
over finite sets.
The resulting abstract notion of “fibered multicategory”
p:{\cal M}\to{\cal I}\,
generalizes that of fibered category
in that cartesian arrows (giving “reindexing of objects”) are not in principle included in
{\cal M}
.

Remark 1
.

Although there is a potential conflict of terminology with the concept of
“fibration of multicategories” studied in
[
Hermida, 2004
]
and in related works,
note that notion refers instead to a particular kind of morphism of multicategories.
So, the context should mitigate the risk of confusion.

The present theory has developed from the attempt to come to terms with two aspects
of symmetric multicategories.
On the one hand, in the classical definition one considers finite
sequences
of objects
as the domain of arrows. However, this seems a somewhat unnatural restriction
(analogous, and in fact related, to the choice of working in skeletal categories)
and one may want to allow arbitrary finite families of objects as domain.
This step was in fact already considered by
[
Beilinson & Drinfeld, 2004
]
(“pseudo-tensor categories”)
and by
[
Leinster, 2003
]
(“fat multicategories”), though those definitions seem somewhat
ad hoc
,
especially in the way they treat identities.

On the other hand, there is the natural idea of associating, to a symmetric multicategory
{\cal M}
,
the ordinary category of finite families (or sequences) of arrows in
{\cal M}
.
In the case of operads, this construction dates back at least to
[
May & Thomason, 1978
]
where,
for some reasons, these “categories of operators” are then considered as categories
over pointed finite sets (rather than on finite sets), somehow rendering less sharp the concept.
The construction is also related to the free strict monoidal category on
{\cal M}

(see for instance
[
Leinster, 2003
]
).

Embracing wholeheartedly both these perspectives, we are led to consider the category
{\cal M}^{\prime}
of finite families
of objects and of arrows in
{\cal M}
, with its projection functor
p:{\cal M}^{\prime}\to\Set_{f}
.
Thus, an arrow

in
{\cal M}^{\prime}
(of “shape”
pa:I\to J
in
\Set_{f}
)
is a family of arrows in
{\cal M}
, indexed by
J=pY
,
and the “multicategorical” composition in
{\cal M}
is encoded in the “categorical” composition in
{\cal M}^{\prime}
.
The other piece of structure on
{\cal M}^{\prime}
to take in account is the usual reindexing for its objects
X=x_{i}\,\,\,(i\in I)
,
which can be encoded by a discrete fibration
d:{\cal D}\to\Set_{f}
, where
{\cal D}
has the same objects as
{\cal M}^{\prime}
.

Now, in order to develop a sensible theory of multicategories relative to a general base category
{\cal I}
,
it is natural to look for the relevant relations between the arrows of
{\cal M}^{\prime}
and those of
{\cal D}
.
Among them, it seems central the possibility of reindexing arrows in

1.1
Outline of the paper

In Section
2
, fibered multicategories are defined and confronted with
classical symmetric multicategories.
We also present the related concepts of fibered monoid and of monoid in a fibered multicategory.
In sections
2.7
and
2.8
, we define representability and universal products.
In section
2.10
, we show how the concept of commuting endomorphisms
also finds a natural place in this abstract framework.

In Section
3
we consider the connections of fibered multicategories with ordinary fibered categories.
Of course, the latter are included in the former: given a fibered category
p:{\cal C}\to{\cal I}
, we get a
fibered multicategory by considering it paired with the restriction
d:{\cal D}\to{\cal I}
of
p
to the
p
-cartesian arrows;
the special squares are then simply the commutative squares in
{\cal C}
(with cartesian horizontal arrows)
over pullbacks in
{\cal I}
.
If
{\cal C}
is an ordinary category, and
p:{\cal C}\to\Set_{f}
is the usual family fibration,
the correponding fibered multicategory is associated to the “sequential” multicategory
{\cal C}_{\blacktriangleright}
of families
of concurrent arrows (see
[
Pisani, 2014
]
).

Conversely, we will see that when
{\cal D}
can be included in
{\cal M}
in a precise sense,
then
p:{\cal M}\to{\cal I}\,\,
is in fact a fibered category.
In this context, the Grothendieck construction can be seen as the free way to transform
a fibered unary multicategory
p:{\cal M}\to{\cal I}

(that is, one in which all the arrows of
{\cal M}
are projected to isomorphisms) into a fibered category.
The fact that the construction yields an equivalence reflects (in the more general context)
the elementary fact that unary multicategories are equivalent to categories (or to sequential multicategories).

In Section
4
, we show how in this abstract framework there is an effective notion of cartesian structure.
A fibered cartesian multicategory has, in addition to the special squares, also “special triangles”
giving “covariant reindexing” of arrows.
These special triangles form a discrete opfibration over commutative triangles in
{\cal I}

(with a chosen
d
-lifting of its top side)
and are related to special squares by some sort of Beck-Chevalley and Frobenius laws.
As expected, in a cartesian multicategory representability (by a tensor product) becomes an
“algebraic” or “absolute” property and coincides with the existence of “universal” or “cartesian” products.

3.2
Connections with sequential multicategories

If we apply the Grothendieck construction to the usual reindexing functor on
\Set_{f}

of finite families of objects and arrows of a category
{\cal C}
,
we obtain a fibered category which, as a fibered multicategory,
is the standard multicategory
{\cal C}_{\blacktriangleright}
of discrete cocones in
{\cal C}

(that is, arrows in
{\cal C}_{\blacktriangleright}
are families of concurrent arrows in
{\cal C}
).

In
[
Pisani, 2014
]
, discrete cocone multicategories are characterized abstractly as those
multicategories with a “central monoid”, that is those in which each object carries a monoid
such that any arrow is a monoid morphism.
There, they are named “sequential multicategories”, a somewhat unhappy choice,
especially in the light of the present emphasis on families in place of sequences.

Remark 13
.

Note that among the consequences of the central monoid characterization
of sequential multicategories there is the well known characterization of cartesian
monoidal categories (that is, those in which
\otimes=\times
) as those with a central comonoid.
Indeed, a sequential multicategory is representable if and only if it is cocartesian monoidal,
which gives the dual result.

As we have just mentioned, in the present framework sequential multicategories
are just the standard fibered multicategories associated to the usual family
fibration on
\Set_{f}
of a category
{\cal C}
.
Thus, we are led to consider, more generally, any fibration as a sort of fibered
sequential multicategory.
This view is also substantiated by the observation that
if
{\cal M}=(d,p,{\rm sp}({\cal M},{\cal D}))
is the fibered multicategory associated to a fibration,
then any object carries a monoid, since any section of
d:{\cal D}\to{\cal I}
gives, via
F:{\cal D}\to{\cal M}
,
a section of
p:{\cal M}\to{\cal I}
(recall the definition of object and of monoid in section
2.11
).
Furthermore, diagram (
9
) says in a sense that any map in
{\cal M}

is “locally” a monoid morphism. Thus, fibered categories have indeed also a sort
of “central monoid characterization” among fibered multicategoris.

In
[
Pisani, 2014
]
it is also proved that the sequential reflection of a symmetric multicategory
{\cal M}

is given by
{\cal M}\otimes_{BV}1_{\blacktriangleright}
, where
\otimes_{BV}
is the Boardman-Vogt tensor product
and
1_{\blacktriangleright}
is the terminal multicategory. By the definition of the Boardman-Vogt tensor product,

{\cal M}\otimes_{BV}1_{\blacktriangleright}
is essentially the result of adding a monoid structure to each object of
{\cal M}
,
in such a way that any arrow of
{\cal M}
commutes with these monoids.
This generalizes to the “free fibration” construction on a fibered multicategory
(as sketched in section
3.1
above), where one adds the arrows of
{\cal D}
to those of
{\cal M}

in such a way that the special squares (giving reindexing) become commutative squares.

4
Cartesian fibered multicategories

Cartesian multicategories are usually considered as a variant of Lawvere theories.
Indeed, a (multisorted) algebraic theory can be encoded in a
cartesian multicategory
{\cal M}
, and its models (say in sets) are the cartesian functors
{\cal M}\to\Set
.
One advantage with respect to finite product categories is that in a cartesian multicategory
the products are in principle only “virtual” , so that they have usually a simpler description.
For example, if
R
is a ring, the Lawvere theory for
R
-modules is given by
matrices
with entries in
R
,
while the associated cartesian multicategory is given by
sequences
with entries in
R

(see example
17
).
Indeed, the idea of a cartesian multicategory is that if it is representable
(that is, corresponds to a monoidal category)
then it is represented by universal products (that is, the tensor product is cartesian).
This is made precise in theorem
18
, where it is shown that
a fibered cartesian multicategory is stably representable if and only if it has universal products
(as defined in Section
2
).
Indeed, both conditions are equivalent to the existence of “algebraic products”, that are defined
equationally in cartesian multicategories and then are “absolute” with respect to cartesian functors.

There are various equivalent definitions of cartesian multicategories in the literature
(see for instance
[
Pisani, 2014
]
or
[
Shulman, 2016
]
).
Usually, they are considered as a sort of variation of symmetric multicategories, where
the action of bijective mappings on arrows is extended to an action of all mappings.
From our point of view, we have seen that the action of bijective mappings on the arrows
of a symmetric multicategory is related to the
contravariant
reindexing of arrows
along pullbacks (see section
2.4
).
We will show now that the action on arrows in a cartesian multicategory is instead
given by a sort of
covariant
reindexing of arrows, related to the contravariant
one by some kind of Beck-Chevalley and Frobenius laws.

4.6
Cartesian fibered categories

If
{\cal M}
is the fibered multicategory associated to a fibered category
p:{\cal M}\to{\cal I}

(see proposition
11
) then it is stably representable if and only if the fibration
p
has sums
(in the sense of fibered categories, see for instance
[
Streicher, 2018
]
).
Indeed, since in this case the special squares are commutative squares in
{\cal M}

with cartesian horizontal arrows over pullbacks in
{\cal I}
,
the stability condition of definition
2.7
becomes the Beck-Chevalley condition for bifibrations.
Similarly,
{\cal M}
has universal products if and only if the fibration
p
has products,
that is the opposite fibration has sums.

Thus, if we say that a fibered category is cartesian if the associated fibered multicategory is so,
as a corollary of theorem
18
we get:

Proposition 20
.

A cartesian fibered category
{\cal M}
has sums if and only if it has products and,
if this is the case, they coincide and are absolute (with respect to the cartesian morphisms
of definition
14
).

Recall that the usual family fibration of a category
{\cal C}
on
\Set_{f}
corresponds,
as a fibered multicategory, to the standard sequential multicategory
{\cal C}_{\blacktriangleright}

(see section
3.2
).
In this case, as shown in
[
Pisani, 2014
]
, a cartesian structure on
{\cal C}_{\blacktriangleright}
amounts to an
enrichment of
{\cal C}
in commutative monoids (see also example
17
).
Thus we find again the following fact, which is well-known in the case of abelian categories

Proposition 21
.

A category
{\cal C}
, enriched in commutative monoids, has finite sums if and only if
it has finite products and, if this is the case, they coincide and are absolute.

To conclude, we present a partial generalization of the above mentioned correspondence
between cartesian structures on
{\cal C}_{\blacktriangleright}
and enrichments of
{\cal C}
in commutative monoids.
First, recall from section

that an object in a fibered multicategory is a section of

;
in the case of fibered categories it is then a section

of
