Abstract
A uniform approach to computing with infinite objects like real numbers, tuples of these, compacts sets, and uniformly continuous maps is presented. In work of Berger it was shown how to extract certified algorithms working with the signed digit representation from constructive proofs. Berger and the present author generalised this approach to complete metric spaces and showed how to deal with compact sets. Here, we unify this work and lay the foundations for doing a similar thing for the much more comprehensive class of compact Hausdorff spaces occurring in applications. The approach is of the same computational power as Weihrauch’s Type-Two Theory of Effectivity.



1
Introduction

In investigations on exact computations with continuous objects such as the real numbers, objects are usually represented by streams of finite data. This is true for theoretical studies in the Type-Two Theory of Effectivity approach (cf. e.g.
[
29
]
) as well as for practical research, where prevalently the signed digit representation is used (cf.
[
14
,
21
,
8
]
), but also others
[
17
,
16
,
28
]
. Berger
[
6
]
showed how to use the method of program extraction from proofs to extract certified algorithms working with the signed digit representation in a semi-constructive logic allowing inductive and co-inductive definitions.

In order to generalise from the different finite objects used in the various stream representations, Berger and the present author
[
11
]
used the abstract framework of what was coined
digit space
, i.e. a bounded complete non-empty metric space
X
enriched with a finite set
D
of contractions on
X
, called
digits
, that cover the space, that is

X=\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,d[X]\mid d\in D\,\}$},

where
d[X]=\mbox{$\{\,d(x)\mid x\in X\,\}$}
. Spaces of this kind were studied by Hutchinson in his basic theoretical work on self-similar sets
[
19
]
and used later also by Scriven
[
27
]
in the context of exact real number computation.

Digit spaces are compact and weakly hyperbolic, where the latter property means that for every infinite sequence
d_{0},d_{1},\ldots
of digits the intersection
\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}d_{0}\circ\cdots\circ d_{n}[X]
contains
at most
one point
[
15
]
. Compactness, on the other hand, implies that each such intersection contains
at least
a point. By this way every stream of digits denotes a uniquely determined point in
X
. Because of the covering property it follows conversely that each point in
X
has such a code.

With respect to the operations of adding a digit
d
to the left side of a stream and applying a map
d
in
D
to an element of
X
, respectively, the space of digit streams as well as
X
are algebras of the same signature and the coding map is a morphism respecting these operations.

A central aim of the joint research was to lay the foundation for computing with non-empty compact sets and for extracting algorithms for such computations from mathematical proofs. It is a familiar fact that the set of all non-empty compact subsets of a bounded complete metric space is a bounded and complete space again with respect to the Hausdorff metric
[
18
,
24
]
. However, as was shown in
[
11
]
, in general there is no finite set of contractions that covers the hyperspace. On the other hand, non-empty compact subsets can be represented in a natural way by finitely branching infinite trees of digits. Moreover, all characterisations in
[
11
]
derived for the stream representation of the elements of the digit space hold true for the tree representation of the non-empty compact subsets of the space.

The goal of the investigation presented in this paper is to show that a uniform approach to computing with continuous objects comprising the non-empty compact sets case can be obtained by allowing the contractions of a digit space to be multi-ary. Points are then no longer represented by digit streams but by finitely branching infinite trees, called
D
-trees
.
As we will see, not only a uniform version of the results in
[
11
]
can be derived, but also an analogue of Berger’s inductive co-inductive characterisation of the (constructively) uniformly continuous endofunctions on the unit interval
[
6
]
, which allows representing also such functions as finitely branching infinite trees.

There is also a second objective which results from the observation that the essential properties needed in the approach pursued in
[
11
]
are covering, compactness and weak hyperbolicity. So, it seems that more generality is gained by starting from spaces with these properties.

Besides the general framework and the hyperspace of non-empty compact subsets, the construction of product spaces is presented. In both cases it is investigated whether important properties are inherited under the constructions. Moreover, to demonstrate the power of the framework, several results from topology are derived that are relevant for applications.

It is well known that the product and the hyperspace construction are both functorial. Here, we give proofs of the functoriality on the basis of the co-inductive characterisations of the spaces involved, that means, we use co-induction and/or a combination of induction and co-induction.

In his seminal 1951 paper on spaces of subsets
[
23
]
, Michael showed that compact unions of compact sets are compact again. We give a non-topological proof of this result, based on co-induction. Other results we derive in a similar way include the fact that singleton sets are compact, as are direct images of compact sets under uniformly continuous functions. From the proofs algorithms transforming tree representations of points
x
into tree representations of the compact sets
\{x\}
, and tree representation of uniformly continuous functions
f
as well as tree representations of compact sets
K
into tree representations of the compact sets
f[K]
, respectively, can be extracted.

The paper is organised as follows: Section
2
contains a short introduction to inductive and co-inductive definitions and the proof methods they come equipped with. As a first application, finite and finitely-branching infinite trees are defined in Section
3
. Digit spaces are iterated function systems. In Section
4
function systems with multi-ary functions are considered and essential results derived.

As said, the central aim of this paper is to present a uniform approach to computing with infinite objects like real numbers, and tuples or compact sets of such. In Section
5
we restrict our study to the case of extended iterated function systems where the underlying space is a compact metric space and the maps making up the function system are contractions. There is a vast number of effectivity studies for metric spaces. Here, the aim is to show that what was obtained in
[
11
]
remains true in the more general case of multi-ary digit maps. A particular consequence is the equivalence of the present approach with Weihrauch’s Type-Two Theory of Effectivity
[
29
]
.

In Weihrauch’s approach one has to deal with representations explicitly. Often this requires involved codings which makes it hard for the usual mathematician to follow the proof argument. Proof extraction is an approach that avoids dealing with representations: the representations of objects as well as the algorithms computing with them are automatically extracted from formal proofs. Section
6
contains a short introduction. As is shown, the tree representation considered in Section
4
results from the co-inductive characterisation of the space.

In Section
7
the equivalence between the property that every point of a digit space is the limit of a regular Cauchy sequence of elements of a dense base, and the co-inductive characterisation of the space is derived in a constructive fashion. Via proof extraction computable translations between the Cauchy representation used in Type-Two Theory of Effectivity and the tree representation can be obtained.

In the following two sections the construction of new spaces from given ones is considered.
In Section
8
products are examined. All properties of extended iterated function systems and digit spaces, respectively, considered in this paper are inherited from the factor spaces to their product.

The hyperspace of non-empty compact subsets is studied in Section
9
. All but one of the properties investigated are inherited to the hyperspace. Only for weak hyperbolicity this is still open in the general case. If the underlying space is a metric one and all digits are contracting, also this property holds.

As a consequence, in both cases the equivalence result derived in Section
7
carries over to the derived spaces. In
[
11
]
separate proofs had to be given for digit spaces and their hyperspaces. Particularly in the latter case, the proof was quite involved.

Section
10
contains a generalisation of Berger’s inductive co-inductive characterisation of the uniformly continuous functions on the unit interval to the digit space case. On the basis of the characterisation it is shown that the function class is closed under composition

The last two sections address applications of the framework to topology. In Section
11
the functoriality of the hyperspace construction as well as properties of continuous functions that map into the hyperspace are derived. Section
12
, finally, contains a co-inductive treatment of Michael’s result.

The paper finishes with a Conclusion.

2
Inductive and co-inductive definitions

Let
X
be a set and
\mathcal{P}(X)
its powerset. An operator
\Phi\colon\mathcal{P}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(X)
is
monotone
if for all
Y,Z\subseteq X
,

if
Y\subseteq Z
, then
\Phi(Y)\subseteq\Phi(Z)
;

and a set
Y\subseteq X
is
\Phi
-closed
(or a pre-fixed point of
\Phi
) if
\Phi(Y)\subseteq Y
. Since
\mathcal{P}(X)
is a complete lattice,
\Phi
has a least fixed point
\mu\Phi
by the Knaster-Tarski Theorem. If
P\subseteq X
, we mostly write

P(x)\overset{\mu}{=}\Phi(P)(x),

instead of
P=\mu\Phi
.
\mu\Phi
can be defined to be the least
\Phi
-closed subset of
X
. Thus, we have the
induction principle
stating that for every
Y\subseteq X
.

If
\Phi(Y)\subseteq Y
then
\mu\Phi\subseteq Y

For monotone operators
\Phi,\Psi\colon\mathcal{P}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(X)
define

\Phi\subseteq\Psi:\Leftrightarrow(\forall Y\subseteq X)\,\Phi(Y)\subseteq\Psi(Y).

It is easy to see that the operation
\mu
is monotone, i.e., if
\Phi\subseteq\Psi
, then
\mu\Phi\subseteq\mu\Psi
. This allows us to derive the following strengthening of the induction principle.

Lemma 2.1

(
Strong Induction Principle
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\Phi\colon\mathcal{P}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(X)
be a monotone operator. Then,

If
\Phi(Y\cap\mu\Phi)\subseteq Y
, then
\mu\Phi\subseteq Y
.

Proof.

Let
\Psi(Y):=\Phi(Y\cap\mu\Phi)
and assume that
\Psi(Y)\subseteq Y
. Then
\mu\Psi\subseteq Y
, by the induction principle. Since
Y\cap\mu\Phi\subseteq Y
, we moreover have that
\Phi(Y)\supseteq\Phi(Y\cap\mu\Phi)=\Psi(Y)
. Hence,
\mu\Psi\subseteq\mu\Phi
. It follows that

\mu\Psi=\Psi(\mu\Psi)=\Phi(\mu\Psi\cap\mu\Phi)=\Phi(\mu\Psi).

Therefore,
\mu\Phi\subseteq\mu\Psi
, again by induction, and whence
\mu\Phi\subseteq Y
.
∎

Dual to inductive definitions are
co-inductive definitions
. A subseteq
Y
of
X
is called
\Phi
-co-closed
(or a post-fixed point of
\Phi
) if
Y\subseteq\Phi(Y)
. By duality,
\Phi
has a largest fixed point
\nu\Phi
which can be defined as the largest
\Phi
-co-closed subset of
\Phi
. So, we have the
co-induction
and the
strong co-induction principle
, respectively, stating that for all
Y\subseteq X
.

If
Y\subseteq\Phi(Y)
, then
Y\subseteq\nu\Phi
.

and

If
Y\subseteq\Phi(Y\cup\nu\Phi)
, then
Y\subseteq\nu\Phi
.

Note that for
P\subseteq X
we also write

P(x)\overset{\nu}{=}\Phi(P)(x)

instead of
P=\nu\Phi
.

Lemma 2.2

(
Half-strong Co-induction Principle
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\Phi\colon\mathcal{P}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(X)
be a monotone operator. Then,

if
Y\subseteq\Phi(Y)\cup\nu\Phi
then
Y\subseteq\nu\Phi
.

Proof.

Let
\Psi(Y):=\Phi(Y)\cup\nu\Phi
. Then
\Psi
is monotone and pointwise larger than
\Phi
. Hence,

\nu\Phi\subseteq\nu\Psi.

(1)

On the other hand

\nu\Psi=\Psi(\nu\Psi)=\Phi(\nu\Psi)\cup\nu\Phi=\Phi(\nu\Psi)

since
\nu\Phi=\Phi(\nu\Phi)\subseteq\Phi(\nu\Psi)
, by (
1
). Hence,

\nu\Psi\subseteq\nu\Phi,

(2)

by co-induction.

The premise of half-strong co-induction means
Y\subseteq\Psi(Y)
. Therefore,

Y\subseteq\nu\Psi
, by co-induction, from which we obtain with (
2
) that
Y\subseteq\nu\Phi
.
∎

The following examples are taken from
[
6
]
.

Example 2.3
.

(
natural numbers
)
Define
\Phi\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
by

\Phi(Y):=\{0\}\cup\mbox{$\{\,y+1\mid y\in Y\,\}$}.

Then
\mu\Phi=\mathbb{N}=\{\,0,1,\ldots\,\}
. The induction principle is logically equivalent to the usual zero-successor-induction on
\mathbb{N}
; if
0\in Y
and
(\forall y\in Y)(y\in Y\to y+1\in Y)
, then
(\forall y\in\mathbb{N})\,y\in Y
.

Example 2.4
.

(
signed digits and the interval
[-1,1]
)
Set
\mathbb{I}:=[-1,1]
and for every signed digit
d\in\mathrm{SD}:=\{-1,0,1\}
define
\mathrm{av}_{d}\colon\mathbb{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{I}
by

\mathrm{av}_{d}(x):=(x+d)/2.

Let
\mathbb{I}_{d}:=\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(\mathrm{av}_{d})
. Then
\mathbb{I}_{d}=[d/2-1/2,d/2+1/2]
and
\mathbb{I}
is the union of the
\mathbb{I}_{d}
.

Define
\Phi\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})
by

\Phi(Z):=\mbox{$\{\,x\in\mathbb{I}\mid(\exists d\in\mathrm{SD})(\exists y\in Z)\,x=\mathrm{av}_{d}(y)\,\}$}

and let
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{I}}:=\nu\Phi
. Then
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{I}}\subseteq\mathbb{I}
. Since moreover,
\mathbb{I}\subseteq\Phi(\mathbb{I})
, it follows with co-induction that also
\mathbb{I}\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{I}}
. Hence,
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{I}}=\mathbb{I}
. The point of this definition is that the proof of
\mathbb{I}\subseteq\Phi(\mathbb{I})
has an interesting computational content:
x\in\mathbb{I}
must be given in such a way that it is possible to find
d\in\mathrm{SD}
so that
x\in\mathbb{I}_{d}
. This means that
d/2
is a
first approximation
of
x
. The computational content of the proof of
\mathbb{I}\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{I}}
, roughly speaking, iterates the process of finding approximations to
x
ad infinitum, i.e. it computes a
signed digit representation
of
x
, that is, a stream
a_{0}:a_{1}:\cdots
of signed digits with

x=\mathrm{av}_{a_{0}}(\mathrm{av}_{a_{1}}(\cdots))=\sum_{i\geq 0}a_{i}\cdot 2^{-(i+1)}.

D
-Trees

Let
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}

over
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Define
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{*}:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}

A

that is closed under initial segments. We will consider trees the nodes of which are labelled with elements of a fixed non-empty set D that comes equipped with an outdegree
\mathrm{ar}\colon D\rightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

The subsequent definition says when
T=(S,L)

S\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}^{*}
and

-tree of height
n
.

Definition 3.1
.

•

T
is a
D
-tree of height
0
, if
S=\{\langle\,\,\rangle\}
and for some
d\in D
,
L(\langle\,\,\rangle)=d
;

•

T
is a
D
-tree of height

S\subseteq D^{n+1}
and there are
d\in D
and
D
-trees
T_{1},\ldots,T_{\mathrm{ar}(d)}

such that

\displaystyle S=\{\langle\,\,\rangle\}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{\mathrm{ar}(d)}i\star S_{i},

\displaystyle L(\langle\,\,\rangle)=d\quad\text{and}\quad L(i\star s)=L_{i}(s),

for
1\leq i\leq\mathrm{ar}(d)
and
s\in S_{i}
. We write
T=[d;T_{1},\ldots,T_{\mathrm{ar}(d)}]
in this case.

Let
\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{D}
be the set of all
D
-trees of height
n
and
\mathcal{T}^{\ast}_{D}:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{D}
.

Definition 3.2
.

For
D
-trees
T
and
T^{\prime}
of height
n
and
n+1
, respectively, we say that
T
is an immediate prefix of
T^{\prime}
and write
T\prec T^{\prime}
, if

•

either
n=0
,
T=(\{\langle\,\,\rangle\},\langle\,\,\rangle\mapsto d)
, and
T^{\prime}=[d;T_{1},\ldots,T_{\mathrm{ar}(d)}]
,

•

or

and

so that

and
T_{\nu}\prec T^{\prime}_{\nu}
, for all
1\leq\nu\leq\mathrm{ar}(e)
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}^{*}
be a tree. A labelling
L\colon S\rightarrow D
is
compatible
if for all
s\in S
,

\mbox{$\{\,i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\mid(\exists s\in S)\,s\star i\in S\,\}$}=\{1,\ldots,\mathrm{ar}(L(s))\}.

Define the set
\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D}
of

-trees
by

\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D}:=\mbox{$\{\,(S,L)\mid\text{$S$ is a tree with compatible labelling $L$}\,\}$}.

Since every node

, every infinite

For

\displaystyle\mathrm{root}(T):=L(\langle\,\,\rangle),

\displaystyle\mathrm{subtree}(T):=((S_{1},L_{1}),\ldots,(S_{\mathrm{ar}(L(\langle\,\,\rangle))},L_{\mathrm{ar}(L(\langle\,\,\rangle))})),

where
S_{i}:=\mbox{$\{\,s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{*}\mid i\star s\in S\,\}$}
and

, for

Theorem 3.3
.

Define the functor
\Lambda_{D}\colon\mathbf{Set}\rightarrow\mathbf{Set}
by

\Lambda_{D}(X):=\bigcup_{d\in D}\{d\}\times X^{\mathrm{ar}(d)}.

Then
(\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D},\mathrm{root}\times\mathrm{subtree})
is a terminal co-algebra of
\Lambda_{D}
.

The theorem is a consequence of general results on the existence of terminal co-algebras in
[
1
,
25
]
.

Definition 3.4
.

A relation
R\subseteq\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D}\times\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D}
is a

if for

,

.

Proposition 3.7

The aim of the research in

Definition 5.1

[Brattka and Presser

\lambda(u,v)\in Q^{2}.\ \rho(u,v)

In what follows we will work with finite objects such as basic elements, tuples as well as finite sets of basic elements, digits or

Extracting digital trees from co-inductive proofs

In this section we recast the theory of topological digit spaces in a constructive setting with the aim to extract programs that provide effective representations of certain objects or transformations between different representations. As one of the main results on this basis we will obtain effective transformations between the Cauchy representation of digit spaces and the digital tree representation showing that the two representations are effectively equivalent. The method of program extraction is based on a version of realisability, and the main constructive definition and proof principles will be induction and co-induction. The advantage of the constructive approach lies in the fact that proofs can be carried out in a representation-free way. Constructive logic and the Soundness Theorem guarantee automatically that proofs are witnessed by effective and provably correct transformations on the level of representations.

Regarding the theory of realisability and its applications to constructive analysis we refer the reader to Schwichtenberg and Wainer
[
26
]
, Berger and Seisenberger
[
9
]
and Berger
[
6
]
. Here, we only recall main facts. We largely follow the exposition in
[
11
]
. The logic used is many-sorted first-order logic extended by the formation of inductive and co-inductive predicates. Note that although the logic is based on intuitionistic logic a fair amount of classical logic is available. For example, any disjunction-free formula that is classically true may be admitted as an axiom
[
12
]
.

Realisability assigns to each formula
A
an unary predicate
\mathbf{R}(A)
to be thought of as the set of realisers of
A
. Instead of
\mathbf{R}(A)(a)
one often writes
a\,\mathbf{r}\,A
(“
a
realises
A
”). The realiser
a
can be typed or untyped, but for the understanding of what follows, details about the nature of realisers are irrelevant. It suffices to think of them as being (idealised, but executable) functional programs or (Oracle-)Turing machines. The crucial clauses of realisability for the propositional connectives are:

\displaystyle c\,\mathbf{r}\,(A\vee B):=(\exists a)(c=(0,a)\wedge a\,\mathbf{r}\,A)\vee(\exists b)(c=(1,b)\wedge b\,\mathbf{r}\,B)

\displaystyle f\,\mathbf{r}\,(A\to B):=(\forall a)(a\,\mathbf{r}\,A\to f(a)\,\mathbf{r}\,B)

\displaystyle c\,\mathbf{r}\,(A\wedge B):=\mathbf{p}_{0}(c)\,\mathbf{r}\,A\wedge\mathbf{p}_{1}(c)\,\mathbf{r}\,B

\displaystyle c\,\mathbf{r}\,\bot:=\bot.

Hence, an implication is realised by a function and a conjunction by a pair (accessed by left and right projections,
\mathbf{p}_{0}(\cdot)
,
\mathbf{p}_{1}(\cdot)
).

Quantifiers are treated uniformly in this version of realisability:

\displaystyle a\,\mathbf{r}\,(\forall x)\,A(x):=(\forall x)\,a\,\mathbf{r}\,A(x)

\displaystyle a\,\mathbf{r}\,(\exists x)\,A(x):=(\exists x)\,a\,\mathbf{r}\,A(x).

By this way, variables
x
are allowed to range over abstract mathematical objects without prescribed computational meaning. Therefore, the usual interpretation of
a\,\mathbf{r}\,(\forall x)\,A(x)
to mean
(\forall x)\,a(x)\,\mathbf{r}\,A(x)
makes no sense as one would use the abstract object
x
as input to the program
a
.

For atomic formulas
P(\vec{t})
, where
P
is a predicate and
\vec{t}
are terms, realisability is defined in terms of a chosen predicate
\tilde{P}
with one extra argument place, that is,

a\,\mathbf{r}\,P(\vec{t}):=\tilde{P}(a,\vec{t}).

(5)

The choice of the predicate
\tilde{P}
allows to fine tune the computational content of proofs.

So far, only first-order logic has been covered. Next, we explain how inductive and co-inductive definitions are realised. An inductively defined predicate
P
is defined as the least fixed point of a monotone predicate transformer
\Phi(X,\vec{x})
, that is the formula
(\forall\vec{x})(X(\vec{x})\to Y(\vec{x}))\to(\forall\vec{x})(\Phi(X,\vec{x})\to\Phi(Y,\vec{x}))
, with free predicate variables
X
and
Y
, must be provable. Then one has the closure axiom

(\forall\vec{x})(\Phi(P,\vec{x})\to P(\vec{x}))

as well as the induction schema

(\forall\vec{x})(\Phi(\mathcal{A},\vec{x})\to\mathcal{A}(\vec{x}))\to(\forall\vec{x})(P(\vec{x})\to\mathcal{A}(\vec{x}))

for every predicate
\mathcal{A}
defined by some formula
A(\vec{x})
as
\mathcal{A}(\vec{x})\leftrightarrow A(\vec{x})
.
Realisability for
P
is defined as in (
5
) by defining
\tilde{P}
inductively via the operator
\tilde{\Phi}(\tilde{X},a,\vec{x})
, where
\tilde{\Phi}
is obtained from
\Phi
by replacing every occurrence of the form
a\,\mathbf{r}\,X(\vec{x})
in the expression obtained by unravelling the formula
a\,\mathbf{r}\,\Phi(X,\vec{x})
according to the definition of
\Phi
, by
\tilde{X}(a,\vec{x})
, for a fresh predicate variable
\tilde{X}
. Then one has the closure axiom

(\forall a,\vec{x})(a\,\mathbf{r}\,\Phi(P,\vec{x})\to\tilde{P}(a,\vec{x}))

as well as the induction schema:

(\forall a,\vec{x})(a\,\mathbf{r}\,\Phi(\mathcal{A},\vec{x})\to a\,\mathbf{r}\,\mathcal{A}(\vec{x}))\to(\forall a,\vec{x})(\tilde{P}(a,\vec{x})\to a\,\mathbf{r}\,\mathcal{A}(\vec{x})).

Dually,
\Phi
also gives rise to a co-inductively defined predicate
Q
defined as the greatest fixed point of
\Phi
. Hence, one has the co-closure axiom

(\forall\vec{x})(Q(\vec{x})\to\Phi(Q,\vec{x}))

and the co-induction schema:

(\forall\vec{x})(\mathcal{A}(\vec{x})\to\Phi(\mathcal{A},\vec{x}))\to(\forall\vec{x})(\mathcal{A}(\vec{x})\to Q(\vec{x})).

Realisability for
Q
is defined by defining
\tilde{Q}
co-inductively by the same operator
\tilde{\Phi}
as above, hence, the co-closure axiom

(\forall a,\vec{x})(\tilde{Q}(a,\vec{x})\to a\,\mathbf{r}\,\Phi(Q,\vec{x}))

and the co-induction schema:

(\forall a,\vec{x})(a\,\mathbf{r}\,\mathcal{A}(\vec{x})\to a\,\mathbf{r}\,\Phi(\mathcal{A},\vec{x}))\to(\forall a,\vec{x})(a\,\mathbf{r}\,\mathcal{A}(\vec{x})\to\tilde{Q}(a,\vec{x})).

The basis of program extraction from proofs is the Soundness Theorem.

Theorem 6.1

(
Soundness Theorem
[
5
,
10
]
).

From a constructive proof of a formula
A
from assumptions
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}
one can extract a program
M(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
such that
M(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\,\mathbf{r}\,A
is provable from the assumptions
a_{1}\,\mathbf{r}\,B_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\,\mathbf{r}\,B_{n}
.

If one wants to apply this theorem to obtain a program realising formula
A
one must provide terms
K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}
realising the assumptions
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}
. Then it follows that the term
M(K_{1},\ldots,K_{n})
realises
A
.

That realisers do actually
compute
witnesses is shown in Berger
[
5
]
and Berger and Seisenberger
[
9
]
by a
Computational Witness Theorem
that relates the denotational definition of realisability with a lazy operational semantics.

There is an important class of formulas where realisers do not matter: A formula
B
is
non-computational
if

(\forall a)(a\,\mathbf{r}\,B\leftrightarrow B).

Non-computational formulas can simplify program extraction of realisers dramatically. We will, however, not go into further details here and refer the reader to
[
11
]
.

In formalising the theory of topological digit spaces, the real number set as well as the underlying space
X
are regarded as a sort. All arithmetic constants and functions we wish to talk about as well as the metric
\rho
, in case
X
is a metric space, are admitted as constant or function symbols. The predicates
=
,
<
and
\leq
are considered as non-computational. Furthermore, all true non-computational statements about real number as well as the axioms of a metric space are admitted as axioms.

In order to be able to deal with the hyperspace of non-empty compact sets, which will be studied in Section
9
, a powersort
\mathcal{P}(x)
is added for every sort
x
, equipped with a non-computational element-hood relation
\epsilon
, as well as a function sort
s\to t
for any two sorts
s
and
t
, equipped with an application operation and operations such as composition. In addition, for every non-computational formula
A(x)
the comprehension axiom

(\exists u)(\forall x)(x\,\epsilon\,u\leftrightarrow A(x))

is added. (
A(x)
may contain other free variables than
x
.) This is an example of a non-computational formula we wish to accept as true. Again, we refer to
[
11
]
for further details and examples.

In Lemma
4.4
a co-inductive characterisation for covering extended IFS was derived. Classically, this is rather uninteresting, but, constructively, it is significant, since, as we will see next, from a constructive proof of
\mathbb{C}_{X}(x)
one can extract a
D
-tree
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D}
so that
[\![T]\!]=x
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
(X,D)
be a topological digit space. Then the realisers of a statement
\mathbb{C}_{X}(x)
are exactly the
D
-trees
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D}
representing
x
, that is

T\,\mathbf{r}\,(\mathbb{C}_{X}(x))\Longleftrightarrow[\![T]\!]=x.

In particular, from a constructive proof of
\mathbb{C}_{X}(x)
one can extract an infinite
D
-tree representation of
x
.

Proof.

By the realisability definition above the predicate
T\,\mathbf{r}\,\mathbb{C}_{X}(x)
is defined co-inductively as

\begin{split}[d;T_{1},\ldots,T_{\mathrm{ar}(d)}]\,\mathbf{r}\,\mathbb{C}_{X}(x)\overset{\nu}{=}\hskip 256.0748pt\\
(\exists y_{1},\ldots,y_{\mathrm{ar}(d)}\in X)\,x=d(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\mathrm{ar}(d)})\wedge(\forall 1\leq\kappa\leq\mathrm{ar}(d))\,T_{\kappa}\,\mathbf{r}\,\mathbb{C}_{X}(y_{\kappa}).\end{split}

(6)

This allows us to show the ‘if’ part by co-induction. That means we have to show that the implication from left to right in (
6
) holds if the relation
\cdot\,\mathbf{r}\,\cdot
is replaced by the relation
[\![\cdot]\!]=\cdot
. This, however, is consequence of Corollary
4.9
.

For the converse implication it is sufficient to show that

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})(\forall T\in\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D})(\forall x\in X)\,(T\,\mathbf{r}\,\mathbb{C}_{X}(x)\Rightarrow x\in\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(f_{T^{(n)}})),

which we do by induction on
n
. Assume that
T\,\mathbf{r}\,\mathbb{C}_{X}(x)
. Then
T=[d;T_{1},\ldots,T_{\mathrm{ar}(d)}]
and there are
y_{1},\ldots,y_{\mathrm{ar}(d)}\in X
so that
x=d(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\mathrm{ar}(d)})
and
T_{\kappa}\,\mathbf{r}\,\mathbb{C}_{X}(y_{\kappa})
, for
1\leq\kappa\leq\mathrm{ar}(d)
. It follows that
x\in\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d)
. Thus the case
n=0
is true. If
n>0
, it follows by the induction hypothesis that
y_{\kappa}\in\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(f_{T_{\kappa}^{(n-1)}})
. Hence,
x\in\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(f_{T^{(n)}})
.
∎

7
Equivalence with the Cauchy representation

As pointed out above, we will now derive the equivalence between the Cauchy representation used in Type-Two Theory of Effectivity and the tree representation introduced here in a constructive fashion. Formalised in many-sorted first-order logic extended by the formation of inductive and co-inductive predicates, the proofs allow the extraction of programs computing translations between the two representations. The method of proof extraction is based on a version of realisability. As said, the advantage of the constructive approach lies in the fact that proofs can be carried out in a representation-free way.

Let
(X,D,Q)
be a computable digit space and let the predicate
\mathbb{A}_{X}\subseteq X
be defined by:

\mathbb{A}_{X}(x):\Leftrightarrow(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})(\exists u\in Q)\,\rho(x,u)<2^{-n}.

A realiser of
\mathbb{A}_{X}(x)
is a regular Cauchy sequence in
X
converging to
x
and a realiser of
\mathbb{C}_{X}(x)
is a
D
-tree
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D}
such that
x=[\![T]\!]
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
(X,D,Q)
be a computable digit space. Then
\mathbb{C}_{X}\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{X}
.

Proof.

Fix
z\in\mathbb{A}_{X}^{\text{eff}}
. Because of Lemma
5.8
it suffices to show that

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})(\forall x\in X)(\mathbb{C}_{X}(x)\rightarrow(\exists u\in Q^{(z)}_{D})\,\rho(x,u)\leq M\cdot q^{n}),

which will be done by induction on
n
. If
n=0
, let
u
be any element in
Q^{(z)}_{D}
. For
n+1
, assume
\mathbb{C}_{X}(x)
. Then there are
d\in D
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{\mathrm{ar}(d)}\in X
with
\mathbb{C}_{X}(y_{\kappa})
, for
1\leq\kappa\leq r
, so that
x=d(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\mathrm{ar}(d)})
. By induction hypothesis, there exist
v_{1},\ldots,v_{\mathrm{ar}(d)}\in Q^{(z)}_{D}
such that for all
1\leq\kappa\leq\mathrm{ar}(d)
,
\rho(y_{\kappa},v_{\kappa})<M\cdot q^{n}
. Set
u:=d(v_{1},\ldots,v_{\mathrm{ar}(d)})
. Then
u\in Q^{(z)}_{D}
and
\rho(x,u)\leq q\cdot\max_{\kappa}\rho(y_{\kappa},v_{\kappa})\leq M\cdot q^{n+1}
.
∎

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
(X,D,Q)
be a well-covering and decidable computable digit space with approximable choice. Then
\mathbb{A}_{X}\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{X}
.

Proof.

The theorem is derived by co-induction. Hence assume
\mathbb{A}_{X}(x)
. We have to find
d\in D
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{\mathrm{ar}(d)}\in X
so that
x=d(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\mathrm{ar}(d)})
and
\mathbb{A}_{X}(y_{\kappa})
, for
1\leq\kappa\leq\mathrm{ar}(d)
. Let
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
be a well-covering number. Using
\mathbb{A}_{X}(x)
, pick
\hat{u}\in Q
such that
\rho(x,\hat{u})<\varepsilon/2
. Pick
d\in D
such that
\mathrm{B}_{\rho}(\hat{u},\varepsilon)\subseteq\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d)
. Then
x\in\mathrm{B}_{\rho}(\hat{u},\varepsilon)
.

By Proposition
5.15
,
d
has a computable right inverse
d^{\prime}
. Set
\vec{y}:=d^{\prime}(x)
. Since
d^{\prime}
has a computable modulus of continuity, we can, given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, compute a number
k(n)
so that for
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d)
, if
\rho(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})<2^{-k(n)}
then
\rho(d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}),d^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime}))<2^{-n-1}
. Using assumption
\mathbb{A}_{X}(x)
again, we find
u\in Q
such that
\rho(x,u)<2^{-k(n)}
. It follows that
\rho(d^{\prime}(x),d^{\prime}(u))<2^{-n-1}
. By the computability of
d^{\prime}
we can moreover compute a basic element
\vec{v}\in Q^{\mathrm{ar}(d)}
with

\rho(d^{\prime}(u),\vec{v})<2^{-n-1}
. Hence,
\rho(\vec{y},\vec{v})<2^{-n}
. Let
\vec{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\mathrm{ar}(d)})
and
\vec{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{\mathrm{ar}(d)})
, then we have that
\rho(y_{\kappa},v_{\kappa})<2^{-n}
, for
1\leq\kappa\leq\mathrm{ar}(d)
, which shows that
\mathbb{A}_{X}(y_{\kappa})
.
∎

8
Products

In this and the following section we study how canonical IFS structures can be introduced on spaces obtained by the usual constructions of new spaces from given ones, and whether the properties examined so far are inherited in these cases. We start with the product construction.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be non-empty topological spaces and
X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
endowed with the product topology. As is well known,
X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
is Hausdorff, exactly if
X_{i}
is Hausdorff, for each
1\leq i\leq n
; and analogously for compactness by Tychonov’s Theorem
[
30
]
.

Now, assume that
(X_{1},D_{1})
, …,
(X_{n},D_{n})
are extended IFS. Without restriction suppose that all
d\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}
have the same arity, say
s_{D}
. Otherwise, let
s_{D}:=\max\mbox{$\{\,\mathrm{ar}(d)\mid d\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\,\}$}
and replace
d\in D_{i}
by
\hat{d}
defined by

\hat{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s_{D}}):=d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\mathrm{ar}(d)}),

for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{s_{D}}\in X_{i}
.

We will introduce an IFS structure on
\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. For
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})\in\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}
define

\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle\colon(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i})^{s_{D}}\rightarrow\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}

by

\begin{split}\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle((x^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,x^{(1)}_{n}),\ldots,(x^{(s_{D})}_{1},\ldots,x^{(s_{D})}_{n})):=\hskip 113.81102pt\mbox{}\\
(d_{1}(x^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,x^{(s_{D})}_{1}),\ldots,d_{n}(x^{(1)}_{n},\ldots,x^{(s_{D})}_{n})),\end{split}

for
(x^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,x^{(1)}_{n}),\ldots,(x^{(s_{D})}_{1},\ldots,x^{(s_{D})}_{n})\in\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
,
and let

D^{\times}:=\mbox{$\{\,\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle\mid(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})\in\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\,\}$}.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
(X_{1},D_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},D_{n})
be extended IFS. Then also
(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},D^{\times})
is an extended IFS. Moreover, the following statements hold:

1.

If
(X_{i},D_{i})
is compact, for all
1\leq i\leq n
, so is
(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},D^{\times})
.

2.

If
(X_{i},D_{i})
is covering, for all
1\leq i\leq n
, so is
(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},D^{\times})
.

3.

If
(X_{i},D_{i})
is well-covering, for all
1\leq i\leq n
, so is
(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},D^{\times})
.

Proof.

(
1
) follows with Tychonov’s Theorem.

(
2
) As is ensued by the definition of
\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle
,

\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle)=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d_{i}).

(7)

Hence,

\displaystyle\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle)\mid(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})\in\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\,\}$}

\displaystyle\quad=\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d_{i})\mid(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})\in\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\,\}$}

\displaystyle\quad=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d_{i})\mid d_{i}\in D_{i}\,\}$}

\displaystyle\quad=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}.

(
3
) follows similarly as
\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d_{i}))=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d_{i}))
.
∎

When dealing with a tuple
\vec{x}
via the extended IFS
(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},D^{\times})

\vec{x}
is considered as a structureless object, not as a composed one. We will now investigate how from a tree representing
\vec{x}
we can access the components of
\vec{x}
.

For
1\leq i\leq n
define the map
\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{i}\colon\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D^{\times}}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D_{i}}
co-recursively by

\displaystyle\mathrm{root}(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{i}([\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle;T_{1},\ldots,T_{s_{D}}])):=d_{i}

\displaystyle\mathrm{subtree}(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{i}([\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle;T_{1},\ldots,T_{s_{D}}])):=(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{i}(T_{1}),\ldots,\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{i}(T_{s_{D}})).

Similarly, define
\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Cons}\nolimits^{(n)}\colon\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D_{i}}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D^{\times}}
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{root}(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Cons}\nolimits^{(n)}([d_{1};T^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,T^{(1)}_{s_{D}}],\ldots,[d_{n};T^{(n)}_{1},\ldots,T^{(n)}_{s_{D}}])):=\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374pt(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Cons}\nolimits^{(n)}(T^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,T^{(n)}_{1}),\ldots,\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Cons}\nolimits^{(n)}(T^{(1)}_{s_{D}},\ldots,T^{(n)}_{s_{D}})).

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
(X_{1},D_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},D_{n})
be extended IFS. Then for every infinite

-tree

,

∎

This result allows us to study the inheritability of weak hyperbolicity of extended IFS to their product.

Lemma 8.3
.

For all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and all
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D^{\times}}

\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(f_{T^{(m)}})=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(f_{\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{i}(T)^{(m)}}).

We proof the statement by induction on

. Let

In case
m=0
, we have that
T^{(0)}=\{\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle\}
and
\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{i}(T)^{(0)}=\{d_{i}\}

f_{T^{(0)}}=\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle
and
f_{\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{i}(T)^{(0)}}=d_{i}
. As seen in (
7

Now, suppose that
m>0
. Then

f_{T^{(m)}}=\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle\circ(f_{T^{(m-1)}_{1}}\times\cdots\times f_{T^{(m-1)}_{s_{D}}})

f_{\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{i}(T)^{(m)}}=d_{i}\circ(f_{\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{i}(T_{1})^{(m-1)}}\times\cdots\times f_{\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{i}(T_{s_{D}})^{(m-1)}}).

Therefore, it follows with the induction hypothesis that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(f_{T^{(m)}})

\displaystyle=\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle[\bigtimes_{i=1}^{s_{D}}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(f_{T^{(m-1)}_{i}})]

\displaystyle=\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle[\bigtimes_{i=1}^{s_{D}}\bigtimes_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(f_{\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{j}(T_{i})^{(m-1)}})]

\displaystyle=\bigtimes_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(f_{\mathop{\mathstrut\rm Pr}\nolimits^{(n)}_{j}(T)^{m-1}}).\qed

form which the subsequent results immediately follow.

Theorem 8.5
.

Let
(X_{1},D_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},D_{n})

For the remainder of this section we will restrict our investigations to the special case of digit spaces.

Proposition 8.6
.

For
1\leq i\leq n
, let
(X_{i},D_{i})
be a digit space with metric

. Then the following statements hold:

1.

(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},D^{\times})
is a digit space with respect to the maximum metric

.

2.

If for each
1\leq i\leq n
,

is a countable dense subset of

so that

is computable, then
\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}
is a countable dense subset of
\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
with
(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\rho,\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i})
being computable as well.

3.

If for every
1\leq i\leq n
,
(X_{i},D_{i},Q_{i})
is a computable digit space, then so is
(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},D^{\times},\linebreak\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i})
.

Proof.

(
1
)
As we have already seen,
(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},D^{\times})
is a compact covering extended IFS. It remains to show that all maps in

are contractive.

For
1\leq i\leq n
, let

be the contraction factor of

, and be
q
their maximum.
For
1\leq j\leq s_{D}
, let
\vec{x}^{(j)},\vec{y}^{(j)}\in\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
with
\vec{x}^{(j)}=(x^{(j)}_{1},\ldots,x^{(j)}_{n})
and similarly for
\vec{y}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\rho(\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle(\vec{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\vec{x}^{(s_{D})}),\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle(\vec{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\vec{y}^{(s_{D})}))

\displaystyle\quad=\rho((d_{1}(x^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,x^{(s_{D})}_{1}),\ldots,d_{n}(x^{(1)}_{n},\ldots,x^{(s_{D})}_{n})),

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt(d_{1}(y^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,y^{(s_{D})}_{1}),\ldots,d_{n}(y^{(1)}_{n},\ldots,y^{(s_{D})}_{n})))

\displaystyle\quad=\max_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(d_{i}(x^{(1)}_{i},\ldots,x^{(s_{D})}_{i}),d_{i}(y^{(1)}_{i},\ldots,y^{(s_{D})}_{i}))

\displaystyle\quad\leq q_{i}\cdot\max_{i=1}^{n}\rho((x^{(1)}_{i},\ldots,x^{(s_{D})}_{i}),(y^{(1)}_{i},\ldots,y^{(s_{D})}_{i}))

\displaystyle\quad\leq q\cdot\max_{i=1}^{n}\max_{j=1}^{s_{D}}\rho_{j}(x^{(j)}_{i},y^{(j)}_{i})

\displaystyle\quad=q\cdot\max_{j=1}^{s_{D}}\max_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{j}(x^{(j)}_{i},y^{(j)}_{i})

\displaystyle\quad=q\cdot\max_{j=1}^{s_{D}}\rho(\vec{x}^{(j)},\vec{y}^{(j)})

\displaystyle\quad=q\cdot\rho((\vec{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\vec{x}^{(s_{D})}),(\vec{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\vec{y}^{(s_{D})})).

(
2
)
As is well known,

is a countable dense subset of

, and obviously

is a computable metric space.

(
3
)
It remains to verify that all digits in

are computable. For

and set

, where

. Moreover, for

\vec{v}^{(j)}

Hence,

for all

\rho(\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle(\vec{u}^{(1)},\ldots,\vec{u}^{(s_{D})}),\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\rangle(\vec{v}^{(1)},\ldots,\vec{v}^{(s_{D})}))\leq\theta.

By our assumption there is a procedure

and

9
The hyperspace of non-empty compact subsets

In many applications, e.g. in mathematical economics, one needs to deal with multi-valued functions that when considered as set-valued maps have compact sets as values. In this section we will study how spaces of compact sets fit into the framework developed so far.

For a topological space
X
with topology
\tau
, let
\mathcal{K}(X)
be the set of all non-empty compact subsets of
X
, endowed with the Vietoris
\tau_{V}
topology (also called the finite topology), that is the topology generated by the sets

\lgroup U;V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}\rgroup:=\mbox{$\{\,K\in\mathcal{K}(X)\mid K\subseteq U\wedge(\forall 1\leq\kappa\leq n)K\cap V_{\kappa}\not=\emptyset\,\}$}

with
U,V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}\in\tau
. The following facts are well known
[
20
,
23
]
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
(X,\tau)
and
(Y,\eta)
be topological spaces, and
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
. Then the following statements hold:

1.

If
Q
is dense in
(X,\tau)
, then the set
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
of all non-empty finite subsets of
Q
is dense in
(\mathcal{K}(X),\tau_{V})
. Moreover, if
Q
is countable, so is
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
.

2.

(\mathcal{K}(X),\tau_{V})
is Hausdorff if, and only if,
(X,\tau)
is Hausdorff.

3.

(\mathcal{K}(X),\tau_{V})
is a compact Hausdorff space if, and only if,
(X,\tau)
is a compact Hausdorff space.

4.

If
f
is continuous, so is the map
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)\mapsto f[K]
with respect to the Vietoris topologies on
\mathcal{K}(X)
and
\mathcal{K}(Y)
.

5.

The operation of taking the union of two compact sets is continuous with respect to the Vietoris topology.

In case of the first statement, the elements of
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
will be called
basic subsets
. For compact Hausdorff spaces
X
and
Y
, and continuous
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
define
\mathcal{K}(f)\colon\mathcal{K}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(Y)
by
\mathcal{K}(f)(K):=f[K]
, for
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
. Then
\mathcal{K}
is an endo-functor on the category of compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps.

Next, assume that
(X,D)
is a compact extended IFS. For
r>0
and pairwise distinct
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\in D
define
[d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}]\colon\bigtimes_{\kappa=1}^{r}\mathcal{K}(X^{\mathrm{ar}(d_{\kappa})})\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(X)
by

[d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}](K_{1},\ldots,K_{r})=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}d_{\kappa}[K_{\kappa}].

Since the
d_{\kappa}
are continuous, the sets
d_{\kappa}[K_{\kappa}]
are compact and so is their finite union. As follows from the definition,

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}([d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}])=\{\,K\in\mathcal{K}(X)\mid(\exists K_{1},\ldots,K_{r}\in\mathcal{K}(X))\,K=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}K_{\kappa}\wedge\hskip 56.9055pt\mbox{}\\
(\forall 1\leq\kappa\leq r)\,K_{\kappa}\in\mathcal{K}(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d_{\kappa}))\,\}\end{split}

Let
m
be the cardinality of
D
. If
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})\in D^{r}
such that the
d_{\kappa}
are pairwise distinct, then
r\leq m
. Hence, there are at most
\sum_{\kappa=1}^{m}m^{\kappa}
such maps. Set

\mathcal{K}(D):=\mbox{$\{\,[d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}]\mid\text{$d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\in D$ pairwise distinct with $r>0$}\,\}$}.

Note that according to Definition
4.2
, in an extended IFS
(X,D)
the maps
d\in D
have to be of type
X^{\mathrm{ar}(d)}\to X
. In case of the maps
[d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}]
this is only true if
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
are unary.

Proposition 9.2
.

Let
(X,D)
be a compact IFS. Then
(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(D))
is a compact extended IFS. Moreover, the following statements hold:

1.

If
(X,D)
is covering, so is
(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(D))
.

2.

If
(X,D)
is well-covering, so is
(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(D))
.

Proof.

(
1
) It suffices to show that

\mathcal{K}(X)\subseteq\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(\bar{d})\mid\bar{d}\in\mathcal{K}(D)\,\}$}.

(8)

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
,
E:=\mbox{$\{\,d\in D\mid K\cap\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d)\not=\emptyset\,\}$}
, say
E=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\}
, and for
d\in E
,
K_{d}=d^{-1}[K\cap\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d)]
. As compact sets,
K
and
d[X]
are closed, whence
K_{d}
is closed as well and thus compact.

Since
(X,D)
is covering, it follows for
x\in K
that there is some
d\in E
with
x\in\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d)\cap K
. Hence,
x\in\bigcup_{d\in E}d[K_{d}]
. This shows that
K=[e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}](K_{e_{1}},\ldots,K_{e_{r}})
, which means that
K
is contained in the right hand side of (
8
).

(
2
) We need to show that for every
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
there is some
\bar{d}\in\mathcal{K}(D)
and some
O\in\tau_{V}
so that
K\in O\subseteq\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(\bar{d})
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
E:=\mbox{$\{\,d\in D\mid\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d))\cap K\not=\emptyset\,\}$}
, say
E=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\}
. Then, we have that

K\in[\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(e_{\kappa}));\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(e_{1})),\ldots,\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(e_{r}))].

It remains to show that

[\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(e_{\kappa}));\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(e_{1})),\ldots,\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(e_{r}))]\subseteq\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}([e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}]).

Let to this end
K^{\prime}\in[\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(e_{\kappa}));\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(e_{1})),\ldots,\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(e_{r}))]
and for

, set
K^{\prime}_{\kappa}:=e^{-1}_{\kappa}[K^{\prime}\cap\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(e_{\kappa})]
. Then
K^{\prime}_{\kappa}\not=\emptyset
. Moreover, since
K^{\prime}\subseteq\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}\mathop{\mathstrut\rm int}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(e_{\kappa}))
, we have that

. Thus,

.
∎

Similar to the product case, when dealing with a compact set
K
via the extended IFS
(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(D))
,
K
is dealt with as an abstract object, not as a set of other objects. We will now study how from a tree representing
K
we can access elements of
K
. Note that we are assuming that all digits
d\in D
have arity 1.

Definition 9.3
.

The relation
S\in\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D}
is a derived tree of
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{\mathcal{K}(D)}
(short:
S\in\partial T
) is co-inductively defined by

[d;S_{1}]\in\partial[[d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}];T_{1},\ldots,T_{r}]\overset{\nu}{=}(\exists 1\leq\kappa\leq r)\,d=d_{\kappa}\wedge S_{1}\in\partial T_{\kappa}.

Lemma 9.4
.

Let
(X,D)
be a compact IFS. Then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{\mathcal{K}(D)}
,

f_{T^{(n)}}(X^{(\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n)}))})=\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,f_{S^{(n)}}[X^{\mathrm{ar}(S^{(n)})}]\mid S\in\partial T\,\}$}

Proof.

We use induction on
n
. Assume that
T=[[d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}];T_{1},\ldots,T_{r}]
. For
n=0
we have that
T^{(0)}=[d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}]
and
\mbox{$\{\,S^{(0)}\mid S\in\partial T\,\}$}=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\}
. So the statement holds in this case. For
n>0
we obtain with the induction hypothesis that

\displaystyle f_{T^{(n)}}(X^{(\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n)}))})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}d_{\kappa}[f_{T^{(n-1)}_{\kappa}}(X^{(\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n-1)}_{\kappa}))})]

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}d_{\kappa}[\bigcup_{S_{\kappa}\in\partial T_{\kappa}}f_{S^{(n-1)}_{\kappa}}[X^{\mathrm{ar}(S^{(n-1)}_{\kappa})}]]

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}\bigcup_{S_{\kappa}\in\partial T_{\kappa}}d_{\kappa}[f_{S^{(n-1)}_{\kappa}}[X^{\mathrm{ar}(S^{(n-1)}_{\kappa})}]]

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{S\in\partial T}f_{S^{(n)}}[X^{\mathrm{ar}(S^{(n)})}]\qed

Theorem 9.5
.

Let
(X,D)
be a topological digit space with unary digits only, and
(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(D))
be weakly hyperbolic. Then, for every
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{\mathcal{K}}(D)
,

[\![T]\!]=\mbox{$\{\,[\![S]\!]\mid S\in\partial T\,\}$}

Proof.

Let
S\in\partial T
. Then it follows with the preceding lemma that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

[\![S]\!]\in f_{S^{(n)}}[X^{\mathrm{ar}(S^{(n)})}]\subseteq f_{T^{(n)}}(X^{(\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n)}))}).

Moreover, by Corollary
4.14
we have that
[\![T]\!]=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{T^{(n)}}(X^{(\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n)}))})
. Now, let
U
be an open neighbourhood of
[\![T]\!]
in
X
. Then
\lgroup U;X\rgroup
is an open neighbourhood of
[\![T]\!]
in
\mathcal{K}(X)
. Hence, there is some
n\in\mathbb{N}
so that
f_{T^{(m)}}(X^{(\mathrm{ar}(T^{(m)}))})\subseteq U
, for all
m\geq n
, which implies that
[\![S]\!]\in U
. Thus,

[\![S]\!]\in\bigcap\mbox{$\{\,U\in\tau\mid[\![T]\!]\subseteq U\,\}$}.

Obviously,
[\![T]\!]\subseteq\bigcap\mbox{$\{\,U\in\tau\mid[\![T]\!]\subseteq U\,\}$}
. To show the converse inclusion, assume that there is some
x\in\bigcap\mbox{$\{\,U\in\tau\mid[\![T]\!]\subseteq U\,\}$}\setminus[\![T]\!]
. Since compact Hausdorff spaces are regular
[
30
]
, there exist disjoint open sets
V,W\in\tau
with
x\in V
and
[\![T]\!]\subseteq W
. So,
x\notin W
and thus
x\notin\bigcap\mbox{$\{\,U\in\tau\mid[\![T]\!]\subseteq U\,\}$}
, a contradiction. It follows that
[\![S]\!]\in[\![T]\!]
.

Note that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{T^{(n)}}(X^{(\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n)}))})\in f_{T^{(n)}}[\mathcal{K}(X)^{\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n)})}]
and therefore

\displaystyle f_{T^{(n)}}(X^{(\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n)}))})

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup f_{T^{(n)}}[\mathcal{K}(X)^{\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n)})}]

\displaystyle=\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,f_{T^{(n)}}(\vec{K})\mid\vec{K}\in\mathcal{K}(X)^{\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n)})}\,\}$}\subseteq f_{T^{(n)}}(X^{(\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n)}))}).

Now, conversely, let
x\in[\![T]\!]
. According to the definition,
\{[\![T]\!]\}\in f_{T^{(n)}}[\mathcal{K}(X)^{\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n)})}]
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that is,
[\![T]\!]\in f_{T^{(n)}}(X^{(\mathrm{ar}(T^{(n)}))})
, by what we have just seen. Thus,
x\in f_{S^{(n)}}[X^{\mathrm{ar}(S^{(n)})}]
, for some
S\in\partial T
. Observe that for each such tree
S
the sequence
(f_{S^{(\kappa)}}[X^{\mathrm{ar}(S^{(\kappa)})}])_{\kappa\in\mathbb{N}}
is decreasing.

It follows that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there is a finite
D
-tree
N
of height
n
with
x\in f_{N}[X^{\mathrm{ar}(N)}]
and
N=S^{(n)}
, for some
S\in\partial T
. With respect to the prefix relation the set of these finite
D
-trees
N
is a finitely branching infinite tree. By König’s Lemma it must have an infinite path, which in our case is a sequence
(N_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of initial subtrees of derived trees of
T
such that
N_{n}\prec N_{n+1}
. In other words, there is some
\hat{S}\in\mathcal{T}^{\omega}_{D}
with

, for all

. Hence,

Let
(N,M)\in R

and

\mathrm{root}(L_{n})=\mathrm{root}(N)=d
. Since

and
\mathrm{subtree}(L_{n})\in\partial M_{\kappa}
, for all

Besides covering and well-covering, there are further central properties of which it will be important to know if they are also inherited from
(X,D)
to
(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(D))
. Unfortunately, in case of weak hyperbolicity, this has to remain an open problem in the general case.

If, however,

is a metric space and all digit maps are contracting, then
\mathcal{K}(X)
is also a metric space with respect to the
Hausdorff metric
,
\rho_{\mathrm{H}}
, given by

\rho_{\mathrm{H}}(K,K^{\prime}):=\inf\mbox{$\{\,\varepsilon\geq 0\mid K\subseteq\mathrm{B}_{\rho}(K^{\prime},\varepsilon)\wedge K^{\prime}\subseteq\mathrm{B}_{\rho}(K,\varepsilon)\,\}$}

with
\mathrm{B}_{\rho}(K,\varepsilon):=\mbox{$\{\,x\in X\mid(\exists y\in K)\,\rho(x,y)<\varepsilon\,\}$}
, and the maps in
\mathcal{K}(D)
are contracting. The contraction factor even remains the same. The topology induced by the Hausdorff metric is equivalent to the Vietoris topology. So, in this case, which is also the prevalent case in applications, both IFS are weakly hyperbolic. As we know from Theorem
4.13
, the assumption that
X
comes equipped with a metric from which the topology on

is induced, is not a restriction.

Finally in this section, let us study how computability properties are inherited to the hyperspace.

Lemma 9.6
.

Let

be a computable metric space. Then

Proof.

Since

terminates its computation after finitely many steps and outputs ACCEPT, if

be the following program:

(0)

Set

(1)

, …,

Proposition 9.7
.

Let

be a computable digit space with only unary digits. Then

Proof.

Let
[d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}]\in\mathcal{K}(D)
. As we have seen, the domain of this map is the space

Every digit

(

Next, we have to show that there is a procedure

of the domain of

Inheritability of decidability is an immediate consequence of the subsequent technical lemma.

Lemma 9.8
.

Let
(X,D)
be a digit space. Then for any finite subset

,
[d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}]\in\mathcal{K}(D)
, and

\mathrm{B}_{\rho_{\mathrm{H}}}(U,\theta)\subseteq\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}([d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}])
if, and only if,

1.

(\forall u\in U)(\exists 1\leq\kappa\leq r)\,\mathrm{B}_{\rho}(u,\theta)\subseteq\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d_{\kappa})
and

2.

(\forall 1\leq\kappa\leq r)(\exists u\in U)\,\mathrm{B}_{\rho}(u,\theta)\subseteq\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d_{\kappa})
.

Proof.

Let
U=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}
.
For the ‘if’ part assume that
K\in\mathrm{B}_{\rho_{\mathrm{H}}}(U,\theta)
. Then there is some rational number
\theta^{\prime}<\theta
with
\rho_{\mathrm{H}}(U,K)\leq\theta^{\prime}
. For
1\leq\sigma\leq n
let

. Then

is non-empty and compact. Moreover,

. By our assumption there is some

so that
\mathrm{B}_{\rho}(u_{\sigma},\theta)\subseteq\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(d_{\kappa_{\sigma}})
. Hence, there is some non-empty compact

with

. Because of our second assumption, every
1\leq\kappa\leq r
is among the

with this property. Thus,

.

In case of the ‘only-if’ part, we show the contrapositive. First, assume that there is some
1\leq\kappa_{0}\leq r
so that for every

,

, but

.

Next, assume that there is some
1\leq\sigma_{0}\leq n
such that for all

Then

, but
K^{\prime}\not\in\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}([d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}])
.
∎

In

Assume that

is a right inverse of

.

Now, assume
(Y,E)
to be a further extended IFS, define

\begin{split}\Phi^{X,Y}(F)(G):=\{\,f\mid(\exists e\in E)(\exists f_{1},\ldots,f_{\mathrm{ar}(e)}\in F)\,f=e\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{\mathrm{ar}(e)})\vee\hskip 42.67912pt\mbox{}\\

(9)

Obviously,

(W)

11
Compact-valued functions

After having discussed how to obtain co-inductive characterisations of product and hyperspaces, as well as of uniformly continuous functions, which are the usual morphisms in the category of compact Hausdorff spaces, we will now investigate compact-valued maps in this framework.

A central goal is to derive the functoriality of the compact hyperspace construction within the scope of co-inductive characterisations of spaces and morphisms. That is, we show that for maps
f\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}
,
\mathcal{K}(f)\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y))}
, where for compact sets
K
,
\mathcal{K}(f)(K):=f[K]
. Classically, this is a well known fact. However, the proofs given here are new, and, as has already been pointed out, algorithms computing the action of the functor on morphisms can be extracted in case that the underlying IFS are covering, compact and weakly hyperbolic; similarly in the other cases considered in this section.

In a first step we will show that the continuous image of a compact set is compact again. As follows from the definition, the algorithm evaluating a function in
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}
on a given tuple of inputs uses the information coming with the components of the input tuple only in as much as it is needed, and also by dealing with each input component separately, not in parallel. This property seems to be too weak to be able to lift the function to the hyperspace of non-empty compact sets. The problem is that in the hyperspace, the compact sets are approximated by iterating digit maps that operate on the hyperspace. By doing so, the elements of the hyperspace are considered as abstract objects, not as sets of points or tuples of such. The internal structure is invisible to the IFS on the hyperspace.

In the subsequent Proposition we get around the problem by dealing with compact sets that are cubes, but without such information being available it is not clear how to proceed. In the general case we therefore restrict ourselves to unary maps. Multi-ary maps
f\colon X^{n}\rightarrow Y
are then subsumed by using the IFS
(X^{n},D^{\times})
instead of
(X,D)
.

Proposition 11.1
.

Let
(X,D),(Y,E)
be compact IFS,
f\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}
and
K_{1},\ldots,K_{\mathrm{ar}(f)}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
. Then

.

Proof.

For the co-inductive proof we need to derive a stronger statement.
Let to this end

and define

(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}f_{i})(\vec{x}):=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\{f_{i}(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm pr}\nolimits^{(k)}_{i}(\vec{x}))\},

for
\vec{x}\in\bigtimes_{i=1}^{k}X^{\mathrm{ar}(f_{i})}
. Set

\mathbb{M}(X,Y):=\mbox{$\{\,\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}\mid k\geq 1\wedge(\forall 1\leq j\leq k)\,f_{j}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}\,\}$}.

In general, the maps in

will be multi-valued. For such maps
h\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
and
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
,
h[K]:=\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,h(x)\mid x\in K\,\}$}
.

Let

H:=\mbox{$\{\,g[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(g)}]\mid g\in\mathbb{M}(X,Y)\wedge K_{1},\ldots,K_{\mathrm{ar}(g)}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}\,\}$}.

We will show that
H\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}
. For
\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathcal{K}(Y)
define

\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(\mathbb{Z}):=\mbox{$\{\,M\subseteq Y\mid(\exists\bar{d}\in\mathcal{K}(D))(\exists M_{1},\ldots,M_{\mathrm{ar}(\bar{d})}\in\mathbb{Z})\,M=\bar{d}(M_{1},\ldots,M_{\mathrm{ar}(\bar{d})})\,\}$}.

Then
\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}=\nu\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}
. Hence, we must prove that
H\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)
. For
g\in\mathbb{M}(X,Y)
let

H(g):=\mbox{$\{\,g[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(g)}]\mid K_{1},\ldots,K_{\mathrm{ar}(g)}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}\,\}$}.

We first show that for all
f\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}
,
H(f)\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)
.

Since
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}=\mathcal{J}^{X,Y}(\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)})=\mu\Phi^{X,Y}(\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)})
, we can use induction to this aim. Set

G:=\mbox{$\{\,f\in\mathbb{F}(X,Y)\mid H(f)\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)\,\}$},

then we need derive that

\Phi_{X,Y}(\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)})(G)\subseteq G.

We have to consider the two cases:

(W)

If
e\in E
and
h\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}
, then
H(e\circ h)\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)
.

(R)

If
f\in\mathbb{F}(X,Y)
and
1\leq i\leq\mathrm{ar}(f)
so that for all
d\in D
,
H(f\circ d^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))})\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)
, then
H(f)\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)
.

(W) Assume that
e\in E
and
h\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}
, and let
K_{1},\ldots,K_{\mathrm{ar}(h)}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
. Then we need to prove that
(e\circ h)[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(h)}]\in\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)
.

We have that

(e\circ h)[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(h)}]=e[h[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(h)}]]=[e](h[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(h)}]).

Since,
h\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}
and
K_{1},\ldots,K_{\mathrm{ar}(h)}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
, it follows that
h[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(h)}]\in H
. Therefore,
(e\circ h)[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(h)}]\in\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)
.

(R) Let
f\in\mathbb{F}(X,Y)
and
1\leq i\leq\mathrm{ar}(f)
so that for all
d\in D
,
H(f\circ d^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))})\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)
. Moreover, for
1\leq\kappa\leq\mathrm{ar}(f)
, let
K_{\kappa}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
. Then there exist
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\in D
and
N_{1},\ldots,N_{r}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
so that
K_{i}=[d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}](N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})
. It follows that

\displaystyle f[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(f)}]

\displaystyle\quad=f[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{i-1}\times[d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}](N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})\times K_{i+1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(f)}]

\displaystyle\quad=f[K_{1}\times\ldots\times K_{i-1}\times(\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}d_{\kappa}[N_{\kappa}])\times K_{i+1}\times\cdots K_{\mathrm{ar}(f)}]

\displaystyle\quad=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}f[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{i-1}\times d_{\kappa}[N_{\kappa}]\times K_{i+1}\times\cdots K_{\mathrm{ar}(f)}]

\displaystyle\quad=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}(f\circ d_{\kappa}^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))})[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{i-1}\times N_{\kappa}\times K_{i+1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(f)}].

Since
d_{\kappa}\in D
, for
1\leq\kappa\leq r
, it follows with our assumption that there are
e^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,e^{(\kappa)}_{s_{\kappa}}\in E
and
M^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,M^{(\kappa)}_{s_{\kappa}}\in H
such that

\begin{split}(f\circ d_{\kappa}^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))})[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{i-1}\times N_{\kappa}\times K_{i+1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(f)}]\hskip 56.9055pt\mbox{}\\
=[e^{(\kappa)},\ldots,e^{(\kappa)}_{s_{\kappa}}](M^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,M^{(\kappa)}_{s_{\kappa}}).\end{split}

Thus,

\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}(f\circ d_{\kappa}^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))})[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{i-1}\times N_{\kappa}\times K_{i+1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(f)}]=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}\bigcup_{\sigma_{\kappa}=1}^{n_{\kappa}}e^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}[M^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}].

Note that the maps
e^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}
with
1\leq\kappa\leq r
and
1\leq\sigma_{\kappa}\leq n_{\kappa}
need not be pairwise distinct. Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}\in E
be pairwise distinct so that
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}\}=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}\{e^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,e^{(\kappa)}_{n_{\kappa}}\}
. Then

\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{r}\bigcup_{\sigma_{\kappa}=1}^{n_{\kappa}}e^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}[M^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}]=[e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}](L_{1},\ldots,L_{r}),

where for
1\leq\iota\leq\ell
,
L_{\iota}:=\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,M^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}\mid 1\leq\kappa\leq r\wedge 1\leq\sigma_{\kappa}\leq n_{\kappa}\wedge e^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}=e_{\iota}\,\}$}
. Since
M^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}=h^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}[\bigtimes_{\iota_{\kappa,\sigma_{\kappa}}=1}^{\mathrm{ar}(h^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}})}\widetilde{M}^{(\kappa,\sigma_{\kappa})}_{\iota_{\kappa,\sigma_{\kappa}}}]
, for some
h^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}\in\mathbb{M}(X,Y)
and
\widetilde{M}^{(\kappa,\sigma_{\kappa})}_{\iota_{\kappa,\sigma_{\kappa}}}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
, we have

\begin{split}L_{\iota}=\hskip 363.62617pt\\
(\bigcup\nolimits_{1\leq\kappa\leq r,1\leq\sigma_{\kappa}\leq n_{\kappa}:\mathcal{K}(e^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}})=\mathcal{K}(e_{\iota})}h^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}})[\bigtimes\nolimits_{1\leq\kappa\leq r,1\leq\sigma_{\kappa}\leq n_{\kappa}:\mathcal{K}(e^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}})=\mathcal{K}(e_{\iota})}\bigtimes\nolimits_{\iota_{\kappa,\sigma_{\kappa}}=1}^{\mathrm{ar}(h^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}})}\widetilde{M}^{(\kappa,\sigma_{\kappa})}_{\iota_{\kappa,\sigma_{\kappa}}}].\end{split}

(12)

It follows that
L_{\iota}\in H
and thus
f[K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{\mathrm{ar}(f)}]\in\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)
.

Observe that because of the possibility that the
e^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}
are not pairwise distinct and in particular Line (
12
) we have to consider multi-valued maps in this proof.

Finally, we let
g\in\mathbb{M}(X,Y)
. It remains to show that
H(g)\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)
. Let to this end
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}
with
g=f_{1}\cup\cdots\cup f_{k}
, and
K^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
, for
1\leq\kappa\leq k
and
1\leq\sigma_{\kappa}\leq\mathrm{ar}(f_{\kappa})
.

As we have just seen,
f_{\kappa}[K^{(\kappa)}_{1}\times\cdots\times K^{(\kappa)}_{\mathrm{ar}(f_{\kappa})}]\in\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)
. Hence, there are
e^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,e^{(\kappa)}_{r_{\kappa}}\in E
and
M^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,M^{(\kappa)}_{r_{\kappa}}\in H
, for
1\leq\kappa\leq k
, with

f_{\kappa}[K^{(\kappa)}_{1}\times\cdots\times K^{(\kappa)}_{\mathrm{ar}(f_{\kappa})}]=[e^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,e^{(\kappa)}_{r_{\kappa}}](M^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,M^{(\kappa)}_{r_{\kappa}}).

Again the
e^{(\kappa)}_{\iota_{\kappa}}
with
1\leq\kappa\leq k
and
1\leq\iota_{\kappa}\leq r_{\kappa}
need not be pairwise distinct. By proceeding as in the previous proof step we can find pairwise distinct
e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{\ell}\in E
and
L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell}\in H
so that
\{e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{\ell}\}=\{\,e^{(\kappa)}_{\iota_{\kappa}}\mid 1\leq\kappa\leq k\wedge 1\leq\iota_{\kappa}\leq r_{\kappa}\,\}
and

\displaystyle g[K^{(1)}_{1}\times\cdots\times K^{(k)}_{\mathrm{ar}(f_{k})}]

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{k}f_{\kappa}[K^{(\kappa)}_{1}\times\cdots\times K^{(\kappa)}_{\mathrm{ar}(f_{\kappa})}]

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{k}[e^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,e^{(\kappa)}_{r_{\kappa}}](M^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,M^{(\kappa)}_{r_{\kappa}})

\displaystyle=[e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{\ell}](L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell}),

showing that
g[K^{(1)}_{1}\times\cdots\times K^{(k)}_{\mathrm{ar}(f_{k})}]\in\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(Y)}(H)
.

For the subsequent technical lemma we extend the union taking operation to compact-valued maps. For Hausdorff spaces
X,Y,Z
such that
Z
is compact, and maps
f\colon X\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(Z)
and
g\colon Y\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(Z)
define
f\cup g\colon X\times Y\to\mathcal{K}(Z)
by

(f\cup g)(x,y):=f(x)\cup g(y).

Lemma 11.2
.

Let
(X,D)
,
(Y,E)
be compact IFS and

F:=\mbox{$\{\,\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{K}(f_{i})\mid k\geq 1\wedge(\forall 1\leq i\leq k)\,f_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{(1)}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}\,\}$}.

Then
\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
is closed under union.

Proof.

Let

A:=\mbox{$\{\,f\in\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y))\mid(\forall g\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F))\,f\cup g\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)\,\}$}.

We will prove by induction that
\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)\subseteq A
, that is we have to show that

\Phi^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)(A)\subseteq A,

which in turn means that we have to verify Rules (W) and (R).

(
\text{W}_{\text{main}}
) Let
\bar{e}\in\mathcal{K}(E)
with
\bar{e}=[e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}]
, for
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\in E
. Moreover, let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in F
. We must demonstrate that
\bar{e}\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{r})\in A
. To this end we have to show that for all
g\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
,
\bar{e}\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{r})\cup g\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
. For
h\in\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y))
set

B(h):=\mbox{$\{\,g\in\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y))\mid h\cup g\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)\,\}$}.

Let
h:=\bar{e}\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{r})
. We prove that
\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)\subseteq B(h)
, that is we have to demonstrate that

\Phi^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)(B(h))\subseteq B(h),

(13)

which is done by side induction.

(
\text{W}_{\text{side}}
) Let
\hat{e}\in\mathcal{K}(E)
, say
\hat{e}=[e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{s}]
with
e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{s}\in E
. Furthermore, let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\in F
. We need to show that

(\bar{e}\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{r}))\cup(\hat{e}\circ(g_{1}\times\cdots\times g_{s}))\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F).

Note that
\bar{e}=\mathcal{K}(e_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{K}(e_{r})
and analogously for
\hat{e}
. Then

\begin{split}(\bar{e}\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{r}))\cup(\hat{e}\circ(g_{1}\times\cdots\times g_{s}))=\hskip 128.0374pt\\
\mathcal{K}(e_{1})\circ f_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{K}(e_{r})\circ f_{r}\cup\mathcal{K}(e^{\prime}_{1})\circ g_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{K}(e^{\prime}_{s})\circ g_{s}.\end{split}

Let
R:=\mbox{$\{\,(j_{1},j_{2})\mid 1\leq j_{1}\leq r\wedge 1\leq j_{2}\leq s\wedge e_{j_{1}}=e^{\prime}_{j_{2}}\,\}$}
. Then we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(e_{1})\circ f_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{K}(e_{r})\circ f_{r}\cup\mathcal{K}(e^{\prime}_{1})\circ g_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{K}(e^{\prime}_{s})\circ g_{s}

\displaystyle\quad=[e_{i_{1}},\ldots,e_{i_{k}},e_{i_{k+1}},\ldots,e_{i_{k^{\prime}}},e^{\prime}_{i_{k^{\prime}+1}},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{i_{\ell}}]\circ((f_{i_{1}}\cup g_{i_{1}})\times\cdots\times(f_{i_{k}}\cup g_{i_{k}})\times

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204ptf_{i_{k+1}}\times\cdots\times f_{i_{k^{\prime}}}\times g_{i_{k^{\prime}+1}}\times\cdots\times g_{i_{\ell}}),

where
1\leq k\leq k^{\prime}\leq\ell
and
i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell},i^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,i^{\prime}_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
so that

\displaystyle R=\{(i_{1},i^{\prime}_{1}),\ldots,(i_{k},i^{\prime}_{k})\},

\displaystyle\{1,\ldots,r\}\setminus\mathop{\mathstrut\rm pr}\nolimits^{(2)}_{1}[R]=\{i_{k+1},\ldots,i_{k^{\prime}}\},

\displaystyle\{1,\ldots,s\}\setminus\mathop{\mathstrut\rm pr}\nolimits^{(2)}_{2}[R]=\{i_{k^{\prime}+1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\}.

It follows that
e_{i_{1}},\ldots,e_{i_{k}},e_{i_{k+1}},\ldots,e_{i_{k^{\prime}}},e^{\prime}_{i_{k^{\prime}+1}},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{i_{\ell}}
are pairwise distinct and
f_{i_{1}}\cup g_{i_{1}},\ldots,f_{i_{k}}\cup g_{i_{k}},f_{i_{k+1}},\ldots,f_{i_{k^{\prime}}},g_{i_{k^{\prime}+1}},\ldots,g_{i_{\ell}}\in F
. Thus,
(\bar{e}\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{r}))\cup(\hat{e}\circ(g_{1}\times\cdots\times g_{s}))\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
, by Rule (W).

(
\text{R}_{\text{side}}
) Let
g\in\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y))
and
1\leq i\leq\mathrm{ar}(g)
so that
g\circ\bar{e}^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(g))}\in B(h)
, for all
\bar{e}\in\mathcal{K}(E)
. Then
h\cup(g\circ\bar{e}^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(g))})\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
. Set
\hat{h}:=h\cup g
. Obviously,
\mathrm{ar}(\hat{h})=\mathrm{ar}(h)+\mathrm{ar}(g)
. For
j:=\mathrm{ar}(h)+i
we therefore have that
\hat{h}\circ\bar{e}^{(j,\mathrm{ar}(\hat{h}))}=h\cup(g\circ\bar{e}^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(g))})
. It follows that
\hat{h}\circ\bar{e}^{(j,\mathrm{ar}(\hat{h}))}\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
, for all
\bar{e}\in\mathcal{K}(E)
, from which we obtain with Rule (R) that
\hat{h}\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
. Hence,
g\in B(h)
. This proves (
13
).

(
\text{R}_{\text{main}}
) It remains to verify Rule (R) in the main induction. Let
f\in\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y))
and
1\leq i\leq\mathrm{ar}(f)
such that for all
\bar{d}\in\mathcal{K}(D)
,
f\circ\bar{d}^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))}\in A
. Thus,
(f\circ\bar{d}^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))})\cup g\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
, for all
g\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
. Set
\tilde{h}_{g}:=f\cup g
. Then
\mathrm{ar}(\tilde{h}_{g})=\mathrm{ar}(f)+\mathrm{ar}(g)
and for all
\bar{d}\in\mathcal{K}(D)
,

\tilde{h}_{g}\circ\bar{d}^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(\tilde{h}_{g}))}=(f\circ\bar{d}^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))})\cup g.

Since
(f\circ\bar{d}^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))})\cup g\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
, by our assumption, we obtain with Rule (R) that
\tilde{h}_{g}\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
. Thus
f\in A
.
∎

Theorem 11.3
.

Let
(X,D),(Y,E)
be compact IFS. Then for all
f\in\mathbb{C}^{(1)}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}
,

\mathcal{K}(f)\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y))}.

Proof.

Again we need to derive a stronger statement. Let

F:=\mbox{$\{\,\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{K}(f_{i})\mid k\geq 1\wedge(\forall 1\leq i\leq k)\,f_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{(1)}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}\,\}$}.

We will show that
F\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y))}
. By using co-induction it is sufficient to prove that
F\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
.

Set

B:=\mbox{$\{\,f\in\mathbb{F}(X,Y)\mid\mathrm{ar}(f)=1\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(f)\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)\,\}$}.

Then we show first that
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}\subseteq B
. Because
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}=\mathcal{J}^{X,Y}(\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)})
, we only need to derive that
\mathcal{J}^{X,Y}(\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)})\subseteq B
. To achieve this we use induction, that is we show
\Phi^{X,Y}(\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)})(B)\subseteq B
. Hereto we have to verify the following two rules:

(W)

If
e\in E
and
h\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}
, then
e\circ h\in B
.

(R)

If
f\in\mathbb{F}(X,Y)
and
1\leq i\leq\mathrm{ar}(f)
such that for all
d\in D
,
f\circ d^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))}\in B
, then
f\in B
.

(W) We have that
\mathcal{K}(e\circ h)=\mathcal{K}(e)\circ\mathcal{K}(h)=[e]\circ\mathcal{K}(h)
. Now, assume that
\mathrm{ar}(e\circ h)=1
, then also
\mathrm{ar}(h)=1
.
Since
\mathcal{K}(h)\in F
and
[e]\in\mathcal{K}(E)
, it follows with Rule (W) for
\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
that
[e]\circ\mathcal{K}(h)\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
. Thus,
e\circ h\in B

(R) Let
f\in\mathbb{F}(X,Y)
and
1\leq i\leq\mathrm{ar}(f)
so that for all
d\in D
,
f\circ d^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))}\in B
. Suppose that
\mathrm{ar}(f\circ d^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))})=1
. Then
\mathrm{ar}(f)=1
and hence
i=1
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{K}(f\circ d)\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
. Now, let
\bar{d}\in\mathcal{K}(D)
with
\bar{d}=[d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}]
. Note that
[d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}]=\mathcal{K}(d_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{K}(d_{s})
. It follows that

\mathcal{K}(f)\circ[d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}]=\mathcal{K}(f)\circ(\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{s}\mathcal{K}(d_{\kappa}))=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{s}\mathcal{K}(f)\circ\mathcal{K}(d_{\kappa})=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{s}\mathcal{K}(f\circ d_{\kappa}).

By our assumption,
f\circ{d_{\kappa}}\in B
. Hence,
\mathcal{K}(f\circ d_{\kappa})\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
, for all
1\leq\kappa\leq s
, as a consequence of which we obtain with Lemma
11.2
that
\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{s}\mathcal{K}(f\circ d_{\kappa})\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
. This shows that
\mathcal{K}(f)\circ\bar{d}\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
, for all
\bar{d}\in\mathcal{K}(D)
. By Rule (R) for
\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
we thus have that
\mathcal{K}(f)\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
, which means that
f\in B
.

Finally, let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{(1)}_{\mathbb{F}(X,Y)}
. Then
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in B
and hence
\mathcal{K}(f_{j})\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
, for
1\leq j\leq k
. With Lemma
11.2
we therefore have that also
\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\mathcal{K}(f_{j})\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
, which shows that
F\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(Y)}(F)
.
∎

The simplest compact sets are the singleton sets of points of the underlying space.

Lemma 11.4
.

Let
(X,D)
be a compact IFS. Then
\mbox{$\{\,\{x\}\mid x\in\mathbb{C}_{X}\,\}$}\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{Z}:=\mbox{$\{\,\{x\}\mid x\in\mathbb{C}_{X}\,\}$}
. The statement will be derived by co-induction. To this end we have to show that
\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(X)}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Let
x\in X
. Since
x\in\mathbb{C}_{X}
, there are
d\in D
and
y\in\mathbb{C}_{X}
with
x=d(y)
. Then
\{y\}\in\mathbb{Z}
and

[d](\{y\})=d[\{y\}]=\{d(y)\}=\{x\}.\qed

\eta\colon\mathbb{C}_{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
by letting

\eta(x):=\{x\}.

Proposition 11.5
.

Let
(X,D)
be a compact IFS. Then
\eta\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,\mathcal{K}(X))}
.

Proof.

. Note that for any
d\in D
,
[d]\in\mathcal{K}(D)

[d]\circ\eta=\eta\circ d
. By Rule (W) we have that
[d]\circ\eta\in\mathcal{J}^{X,\mathcal{K}(X)}(\{\eta\})
, i.e.,
\eta\circ d\in\mathcal{J}^{X,\mathcal{K}(X)}(\{\eta\})
. Therefore
\eta\in\mathcal{J}^{X,\mathcal{K}(X)}(\{\eta\})
, by Rule (R).

Theorem 11.6
.

Let
(X,D)
,
(Y,E)
be extended IFS and
(Z,C)
be a compact IFS. Then, for
f\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,\mathcal{K}(Z))}
and
g\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))}
,
f\cup g\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))}
.

Proof.

The proof of this result essentially follows the lines of the proof of Lemma
11.2
. However, now we use strong co-induction. Let

A:=\mbox{$\{\,f\cup g\mid f\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,\mathcal{K}(Z))}\wedge g\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))}\,\}$}.

Then we need to show that
A\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(A\cup\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})
. Set

B:=\mbox{$\{\,f\in\mathbb{F}(X,\mathcal{K}(Z))\mid(\forall g\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})\,f\cup g\in\mathcal{J}^{X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(A\cup\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})\,\}$}.

We show that
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,\mathcal{K}(Z))}\subseteq B
. Since
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,\mathcal{K}(Z))}=\mathcal{J}^{X,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,\mathcal{K}(Z))})
, it suffices to prove that
\mathcal{J}^{X,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,\mathcal{K}(Z))})\subseteq B
. By induction we show that
\Phi^{X,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,\mathcal{K}(Z))})(B)\subseteq B
, which means that we have to verify Rules (W) and (R).

(
\text{W}_{\text{main}}
) Let
\bar{c}\in\mathcal{K}(C)
with
\bar{c}=[c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}]
, for
c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}\in C
. Moreover, let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,\mathcal{K}(Z))}
. We must demonstrate that
\bar{c}\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{r})\in B
. To this end we have to show that for all
g\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))}
,
(\bar{c}\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots f_{r}))\cup g\in\mathcal{J}^{X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(A\cup\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})
. For
h\in\mathbb{F}(X,\mathcal{K}(Z))
set

H(h):=\mbox{$\{\,g\in\mathbb{F}(Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))\mid h\cup g\in\mathcal{J}^{X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(A\cup\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})\,\}$}.

Let
h:=\bar{c}\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{r})
. We prove that
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))}\subseteq H(h)
, for which it suffices to demonstrate that

\Phi^{Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})(H(h))\subseteq H(h),

(14)

which is done by side induction.

(
\text{W}_{\text{side}}
) Let
\hat{c}\in\mathcal{K}(C)
, say
\hat{c}=[c^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,c^{\prime}_{s}]
with
c^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,c^{\prime}_{s}\in C
. Furthermore, let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))}
. We need to show that

(\bar{c}\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{r}))\cup(\hat{c}\circ(g_{1}\times\cdots\times g_{s}))\in\mathcal{J}^{X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(A\cup\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))}).

Note again that
\bar{c}=\mathcal{K}(c_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{K}(c_{r})
, and analogously for
\hat{c}
. Then

\begin{split}(\bar{c}\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{r}))\cup(\hat{c}\circ(g_{1}\times\cdots\times g_{s}))=\hskip 128.0374pt\\
\mathcal{K}(c_{1})\circ f_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{K}(c_{r})\circ f_{r}\cup\mathcal{K}(c^{\prime}_{1})\circ g_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{K}(c^{\prime}_{s})\circ g_{s}.\end{split}

As in the proof of Lemma
11.2
it follows that there are pairwise distinct
c^{\prime\prime}_{i_{1}}
, …,
c^{\prime\prime}_{i_{k}}
,
c^{\prime\prime}_{i_{k+1}}
, …,
c^{\prime\prime}_{i_{k^{\prime}}}
, …,
c^{\prime\prime}_{i_{k^{\prime}+1}}
,
c^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}\in\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{r},c^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,c^{\prime}_{s}\}
so that (up to some reshuffling of arguments)

\begin{split}\mathcal{K}(c_{1})\circ f_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{K}(c_{r})\circ f_{r}\cup\mathcal{K}(c^{\prime}_{1})\circ g_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{K}(c^{\prime}_{s})\circ g_{s}\hskip 130.88284pt\mbox{}\\
=[c^{\prime\prime}_{i_{1}},\ldots,c^{\prime\prime}{i_{\ell}}]\circ((f_{i_{1}}\cup g_{i_{1}})\times\cdots\times(f_{i_{k}}\cup g^{\prime}_{i_{k}})\times f_{i_{k+1}}\times\cdots\times f_{i_{k^{\prime}}}\times g_{i_{k^{\prime}+1}}\times\cdots\times g_{i_{\ell}}).\end{split}

Then
[c^{\prime\prime}_{i_{1}},\ldots,c^{\prime\prime}{i_{\ell}}]\in\mathcal{K}(C)
,
f_{i_{1}}\cup g_{i_{1}},\ldots,f_{i_{k}}\cup g_{i_{k}}\in A
, and
f_{i_{k+1}},\ldots,f_{i_{k^{\prime}}},g_{i_{k^{\prime}+1}},\ldots,g_{i_{\ell}}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))}
, where we identify maps
f\colon X^{n}\rightarrow Z
with
f\circ(\mathop{\mathstrut\rm pr}\nolimits^{(n+1)}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathop{\mathstrut\rm pr}\nolimits^{(n+1)}_{n})\colon X^{n}\times Y\to Z
, and similarly for maps
g\colon Y^{m}\rightarrow Z
. Thus,
(\bar{c}\circ(f_{1}\times\cdots\times f_{r}))\cup(\hat{c}\circ(g_{1}\times\cdots\times g_{s}))\in\mathcal{J}^{X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(A\cup\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})
by Rule (W).

Note that the remaining proof steps are essentially the same as in the proof of Lemma
11.2
. We include them only for completeness reasons.

(
\text{R}_{\text{side}}
) Let
g\in\mathbb{F}(Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))
and
1\leq i\leq\mathrm{ar}(g)
so that
g\circ e^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(g))}\in B(h)
, for all
e\in E
. Then
h\cup(g\circ e^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(g))})\in\mathcal{J}^{X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(F\cup\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})
. Let
\hat{h}:=h\cup g
. Then
\mathrm{ar}(\hat{h})=\mathrm{ar}(h)+\mathrm{ar}(g)
. For
j:=\mathrm{ar}(h)+i
we therefore have that
\hat{h}\circ e^{(j,\mathrm{ar}(\hat{h}))}=h\cup(g\circ e^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(g))})
. It follows that
\hat{h}\circ e^{(j,\mathrm{ar}(\hat{h}))}\in\mathcal{J}^{X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(F\cup\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})
, for all
e\in E
, from which we obtain by Rule (R) that
\hat{h}\in\mathcal{J}^{X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(F\cup\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})
. Hence,
g\in B(h)
. This proves (
14
).

(
\text{R}_{\text{main}}
) It remains to verify Rule (R) in the main induction. Let
f\in A
and
1\leq i\leq\mathrm{ar}(f)
such that for all
d\in D
,
f\circ d^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))}\in A
. Thus,
(f\circ d^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))})\cup g\in\mathcal{J}^{X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(F\cup\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})
, for all
g\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))}
. Set
\bar{h}_{g}:=f\cup g
. Then
\mathrm{ar}(\bar{h}_{g})=\mathrm{ar}(f)+\mathrm{ar}(g)
and for all
d\in D
,

\bar{h}_{g}\circ d^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(\bar{h}_{g}))}=(f\circ d^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))})\cup g.

Since
(f\circ d^{(i,\mathrm{ar}(f))})\cup g\in\mathcal{J}^{X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(F\cup\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})
by our assumption, we obtain with Rule (R) that
\bar{h}_{g}\in\mathcal{J}^{X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z)}(F\cup\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X\times Y,\mathcal{K}(Z))})
. Thus
f\in A
.
∎

Since the identity on any extended IFS is in
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(X,X)}
, by Lemma
10.3
(
1
), it follows in particular that the binary operation of taking unions is in
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{K}(X)\times\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(X))}
.

Corollary 11.7
.

Let
(X,D)
be a compact IFS. Then the following two statements hold:

1.

\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
is closed under taking finite unions, that is, for all
K,M\subseteq X
,

K,M\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}\Rightarrow K\cup M\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}.

2.

\cup\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{K}(X)\times\mathcal{K}(X),\mathcal{K}(X))}
.

The first statement follows from the second one by Proposition
10.5
.

12
Michael’s Theorem

In his seminal 1951 paper on spaces of subsets
[
23
]
, E. Michael showed that the union of all sets in a compact set of compact sets is compact again. We will reprove this result in a non-topological way by using only the co-inductive characterisations of the spaces involved.

The difficulty we have to overcome herewith is that, as we have seen in Section
9
, in general the canonical maps in
\mathcal{K}^{2}(D)
are no longer of type
\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)^{r}\to\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
, even if all maps in
D
are unary. Thus,
(\mathcal{K}^{2}(X),\mathcal{K}^{2}(D))
will not be an extended IFS any more.

Let
[[d^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(1)}_{r_{1}}],\ldots,[d^{(n)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(n)}_{r_{n}}]]\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(D)
and
\mathbb{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{K}_{n}\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\bigcup[[d^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(1)}_{r_{1}}],\ldots,[d^{(n)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(n)}_{r_{n}}]](\mathbb{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{K}_{n})

\displaystyle\quad=\bigcup\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{n}[d^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(\kappa)}_{r_{\kappa}}][\mathbb{K}_{\kappa}]

\displaystyle\quad=\bigcup\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{n}\mbox{$\{\,\bigcup_{{\sigma_{\kappa}}=1}^{r_{\kappa}}d^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}[K^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}]\mid(K^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,K^{(\kappa)}_{r_{\kappa}})\in\mathbb{K}_{\kappa}\,\}$}

\displaystyle\quad=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{n}\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,\bigcup_{{\sigma_{\kappa}}=1}^{r_{\kappa}}d^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}[K^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}]\mid(K^{(\kappa)}_{1},\ldots,K^{(\kappa)}_{r_{\kappa}})\in\mathbb{K}_{\kappa}\,\}$}

\displaystyle\quad=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{n}\bigcup_{{\sigma_{\kappa}}=1}^{r_{\kappa}}\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,d^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}[K^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}]\mid K^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}\in\mathop{\mathstrut\rm pr}\nolimits^{({r_{\kappa}})}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}[\mathbb{K}_{\kappa}]\,\}$}

\displaystyle\quad=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{n}\bigcup_{{\sigma_{\kappa}}=1}^{r_{\kappa}}\bigcup\mathcal{K}(d^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}})[\mathop{\mathstrut\rm pr}\nolimits^{({r_{\kappa}})}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}[\mathbb{K}_{\kappa}]].

Now, let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\in D
be pairwise distinct so that

\{\mathcal{K}(e_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(e_{m})\}=\mbox{$\{\,\mathcal{K}(d^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}})\mid 1\leq\sigma_{\kappa}\leq r_{\kappa}\wedge 1\leq\kappa\leq n\,\}$}.

Moreover, set

\mathbb{M}_{\iota}:=\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,\mathop{\mathstrut\rm pr}\nolimits^{(r_{\kappa})}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}[\mathbb{K}_{\kappa}]\mid\mathcal{K}(d^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}})=\mathcal{K}(e_{\iota})\wedge 1\leq\sigma_{\kappa}\leq r_{\kappa}\wedge 1\leq\kappa\leq n\,\}$},

for
1\leq\iota\leq m
. Then

\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{n}\bigcup_{{\sigma_{\kappa}}=1}^{r_{\kappa}}\mathcal{K}(d^{(\kappa)}_{\sigma_{\kappa}})[\mathop{\mathstrut\rm pr}\nolimits^{({r_{\kappa}})}_{\sigma_{\kappa}}[\mathbb{K}_{\kappa}]]=\bigcup_{\iota=1}^{m}\mathcal{K}(e_{\iota})[\mathbb{M}_{\iota}]=[\mathcal{K}(e_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(e_{m})](\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{m}).

Let us summarise what we have just seen.

Lemma 12.1
.

Let
(X,D)
be an IFS. Then, given
[[d^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(1)}_{r_{1}}],\ldots,[d^{(n)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(n)}_{r_{n}}]]\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(D)
, we can compute
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\in D
so that when given in addition
\mathbb{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{K}_{n}\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
, we can furthermore define
\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{m}\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
such that

\bigcup[[d^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(1)}_{r_{1}}],\ldots,[d^{(n)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(n)}_{r_{n}}]](\mathbb{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{K}_{n})=\bigcup[\mathcal{K}(e_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(e_{m})](\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{m}).

This gives us a hint on how to deal with unions over compact collections of compact sets.

For
\mathbb{K},\mathbb{M}\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
, we write
\mathbb{K}\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}\mathbb{M}
, , if

\bigcup\mathbb{K}=\bigcup\mathbb{M}.

Moreover, we set
\mathcal{K}_{D}:=\mbox{$\{\,\mathcal{K}(d)\mid d\in D\,\}$}
. Note that
(\mathcal{K}^{2}(X),\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{K}_{D}))
is a compact IFS, and let

\begin{split}\Omega^{\langle 2\rangle}_{X}(\mathcal{Z}):=\{\,\mathbb{K}\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)\mid(\exists d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}\in D)\hskip 142.26378pt\\
(\exists\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,M_{m}\in\mathcal{Z})\,\mathbb{K}\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}[\mathcal{K}(d_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(d_{s})](\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{s})\,\},\end{split}

for
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
. We define
\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)}:=\nu\Omega^{\langle 2\rangle}_{X}
.

Lemma 12.2
.

Let
(X,D)
be a compact covering IFS. Then
\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)=\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)}
.

Proof.

We have that
\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
by definition. For the converse inclusion note that it follows as in the proof of Proposition
9.2
(
1
) that

\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)=\bigcup\mbox{$\{\,\mathop{\mathstrut\rm range}(\bar{d})\mid\bar{d}\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(D)\,\}$}.

Therefore, if
\mathbb{K}\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
, then there is some
[[d^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(1)}_{r_{1}}],\ldots,[d^{(n)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(n)}_{r_{n}}]]\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(D)
and there are
\mathbb{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{K}_{n}\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
so that
\mathbb{K}=[[d^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(1)}_{r_{1}}],\ldots,[d^{(n)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(n)}_{r_{n}}]](\mathbb{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{K}_{n})
. Because of Lemma
12.1
we can now compute
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\in D
and define
\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{m}\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
so that
\mathbb{K}\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}[\mathcal{K}(e_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(e_{m})](\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{m})
. Thus,
\mathbb{K}\in\Omega_{X}^{\langle 2\rangle}(\mathcal{K}^{2}(X))
. With co-induction it follows that
\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)}
.
∎

Let
d_{1},\ldots,s_{s}\in D
and note that

\begin{split}\bigcup[\mathcal{K}(d_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(d_{s})](\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{s})&=\bigcup\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{s}\mathcal{K}(d_{\kappa})[\mathbb{M}_{\kappa}]\\
&=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{s}\bigcup_{M\in\mathbb{M}_{\kappa}}\mathcal{K}(d_{\kappa})(M)\\
&=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{s}\bigcup_{M\in\mathbb{M}_{\kappa}}d_{\kappa}[M]\\
&=\bigcup_{\kappa=1}^{s}d_{\kappa}[\bigcup\mathbb{M}_{\kappa}]\\
&=[d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}](\bigcup\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\bigcup\mathbb{M}_{s}).\end{split}

(15)

It follows that
[\mathcal{K}(d_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(d_{s})]
respects
\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}
and can hence be lifted to the quotient
\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}
. We denote the lifted map by
[\mathcal{K}(d_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(d_{s})]^{*}
and set

\mathcal{K}^{*}(\mathcal{K}_{D}):=\{\,[\mathcal{K}(d_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(d_{s})]^{*}\mid s>0\wedge d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}\in D\,\}.

Lemma 12.3
.

Let
(X,D)
be a compact covering IFS. Then also
(\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}},\mathcal{K}^{*}(\mathcal{K}_{D}))
is a compact covering IFS. Moreover,
\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}}=\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)}/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}
.

Proof.

\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}
is a topological space with the quotient topology. Since
\bigcup\{K\}=K
, for every
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
,
\bigcup\colon\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(X)
is an onto map, which is well known, to be continuous
[
20
, Corollary 7.2.4]
. Since
X
is compact, also
\mathcal{K}(X)
,
\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
and
\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}
are compact. Moreover,
\mathcal{K}(X)
and
\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
are Hausdorff spaces. Thus,
\bigcup
is a closed mapping and, as a consequence, the Vietoris topology on
\mathcal{K}(X)
is equivalent to the quotient topology with respect to
\bigcup

[
30
, Theorem 9.2]
. It follows that the quotient topology on
\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}
is Hausdorff.

By Lemma
12.1
,
(\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}},\mathcal{K}^{*}(\mathcal{K}_{D}))
is covering. The remaining statement follows with co-induction.

Let
\mathbb{K}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}}
. Then there are
d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}\in D
and
\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{s}\in\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
, so that

[\mathbb{K}]_{\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}}=[\mathcal{K}(d_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(d_{s})]^{*}([\mathbb{M}_{1}]_{\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}},\ldots,[\mathbb{M}_{s}]_{\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}})=[[\mathcal{K}(d_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(d_{s})](\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{s})]_{\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}}.

Hence,
\mathbb{K}\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}[\mathcal{K}(d_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(d_{s})](\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{s})
,
which shows that
\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)}/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}
. The converse inclusion follows similarly.
∎

Assume that
(\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}},\mathcal{K}^{*}(\mathcal{K}_{D}))
is weakly hyperbolic. Then it follows that from each
\mathcal{K}^{*}(\mathcal{K}_{D})
-tree representing an equivalence class in
\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)/\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}
one obtains a
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{K}_{D})
-tree representing the same class by stripping off the ‘
*
’ decoration. This also shows that
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{K}_{D})
-trees witnessing that
\mathbb{K}\in\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)}
do not only represent
\mathbb{K}
but all sets in the
\mathbin{\overset{\cup}{=}}
-equivalence class of
\mathbb{K}
. Such multi-representations are also common in Weihrauch’s Type-Two Theory of Effectivity.

Proposition 12.4
.

Let
(X,D)
be a compact covering IFS. Then
\mbox{$\{\,\bigcup\mathbb{K}\mid\mathbb{K}\in\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)}\,\}$}\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{K}\in\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)}
. Then there are (constructively)
d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}\in D
and
\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{K}^{2}(X)}
so that

\bigcup\mathbb{K}=\bigcup[\mathcal{K}(d_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(d_{s})](\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{s})=[d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}](\bigcup\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\bigcup\mathbb{M}_{s}),

where the last equality holds by (
15
).
It follows that

\mbox{$\{\,\bigcup\mathbb{K}\mid\mathbb{K}\in\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)}\,\}$}\subseteq\Omega_{\mathcal{K}(X)}(\mbox{$\{\,\bigcup\mathbb{K}\mid\mathbb{K}\in\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)}\,\}$}).

With co-induction we therefore obtain that
\mbox{$\{\,\bigcup\mathbb{K}\mid\mathbb{K}\in\mathbb{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)}\,\}$}\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{K}(X)}
.
∎

This is a constructive version of Michael’s Theorem: If all IFS involved are weakly hyperbolic, given a
\mathcal{K}_{D}
-tree representing
\mathbb{K}
we can compute a
\mathcal{K}(D)
-tree representing
\bigcup\mathbb{K}
.

The next results even allows the extraction of an algorithm for the computation of union as an operation from
\mathcal{K}^{2}(X)
to
\mathcal{K}(X)
.

Theorem 12.5
.

Let
(X,D)
be a compact covering IFS. Then
\bigcup\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{K}^{2}(X),\mathcal{K}(X))}
.

Proof.

We again use co-induction and show that
\{\bigcup\}\subseteq\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X),\mathcal{K}(X)}(\{\bigcup\})
. Let to this end
d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}\in D
. As we have seen in the preceding proof,

[d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}]\circ(\bigcup\times\cdots\times\bigcup)=\bigcup\circ[\mathcal{K}(d_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(d_{s})].

By Rule (W) for
\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X),\mathcal{K}(X)}
we have for all
d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}\in D
that
[d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}]\circ(\bigcup\times\cdots\times\bigcup)\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X),\mathcal{K}(X)}(\{\bigcup\})
. Thus,
\bigcup\circ[\mathcal{K}(d_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}(d_{s})]\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X),\mathcal{K}(X)}(\{\bigcup\})
, for all
d_{1},\ldots,d_{s}\in D
, from which it follows with Rule (R) that
\bigcup\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{K}^{2}(X),\mathcal{K}(X)}(\{\bigcup\})
.
∎

13
Conclusion

In this paper, a uniform framework for computing with infinite objects like real numbers, compact sets, tuples of such, and uniformly continuous maps is presented. It combines and extends the approaches developed in a series of papers by Berger and co-authors
[
5
,
6
,
8
,
9
,
11
]
.
In particular, it allows to deal with compact-valued maps and their selection functions. Maps of this kind abundantly occur in applied mathematics. They are studied in set-valued analysis
[
2
,
3
]
, and have applications in areas such as optimal control and mathematical economics, to mention a few. In addition, they are used to model non-determinism.

The framework is based on covering extended iterated function systems, where the underlying spaces are compact metric spaces and the contraction maps in the function systems are allowed to be multi-ary, or, more generally, weakly hyperbolic compact covering extended iterated function systems operating on Hausdorff spaces with, not necessarily unary, maps in the function system. Because of the covering condition co-inductive characterisations of the functions systems can be given. Results with computational content are then derived by constructively reasoning on the basis of these characterisations. Realisability facilitates the extraction of algorithms from the corresponding proofs. In so doing, points of the spaces are represented by finitely branching infinite trees. The computational power of the approach is that of Type-Two Theory of Effectivity.
