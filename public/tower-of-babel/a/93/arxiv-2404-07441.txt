Near Optimal Alphabet-Soundness Tradeoff PCPs
Dor Minzer, Kai Zhe Zheng

Abstract
We show that for all ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , for sufficiently large q ∈ ℕ q\in\mathbb{N} that is a power of 2 2 , for all δ > 0 \delta>0 , it is NP-hard to distinguish whether a given 2 2 -Prover- 1 1 -Round projection game with alphabet size q q has value at least 1 − δ 1-\delta , or value at most 1 / q 1 − ε 1/q^{1-\varepsilon} . This establishes a nearly optimal alphabet-to-soundness tradeoff for 2 2 -query PCPs with alphabet size q q , improving upon a result of [Chan, J. ACM 2016]. Our result has the following implications: 1. Near optimal hardness for Quadratic Programming: it is NP-hard to approximate the value of a given Boolean Quadratic Program within factor ( log ⁡ n ) 1 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) (\log n)^{1-o(1)} under quasi-polynomial time reductions. This improves upon a result of [Khot, Safra, ToC 2013] and nearly matches the performance of the best known algorithms due to [Megretski, IWOTA 2000], [Nemirovski, Roos, Terlaky, Mathematical Programming 1999] and [Charikar, Wirth, FOCS 2004] that achieve O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) approximation ratio. 2. Bounded degree 2 2 -CSPs: under randomized reductions, for sufficiently large d > 0 d>0 , it is NP-hard to approximate the value of 2 2 -CSPs in which each variable appears in at most d d constraints to within a factor of ( 1 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ​ d 2 (1-o(1))\frac{d}{2} , improving upon a result of [Lee, Manurangsi, ITCS 2024]. 3. Improved hardness results for connectivity problems: using results of [Laekhanukit, SODA 2014] and [Manurangsi, Inf. Process. Lett., 2019], we deduce improved hardness results for the Rooted k k -Connectivity Problem, the Vertex-Connectivity Survivable Network Design Problem and the Vertex-Connectivity k k -Route Cut Problem.

Introduction

The PCP theorem is a fundamental result in theoretical computer science with many equivalent formulations
[
20
,
4
,
3
]
. One of the formulations asserts
that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that given a satisfiable
3
-SAT formula
\phi
, it is NP-hard to find an assignment that satisfies at least
(1-\varepsilon)
fraction of the constraints. The PCP theorem has a myriad
of applications within theoretical computer science,
and of particular interest to this paper are its applications to hardness of approximation.

The vast majority of hardness of approximation results
are proved via reductions from the PCP theorem above.
Oftentimes, to get a strong hardness of approximation
result, one must first amplify the basic PCP theorem into a result with stronger parameters
[
25
,
24
,
21
,
29
]

(see
[
47
]
for a survey). To discuss these parameters, it is
often convenient to view the PCP through the problem of
2
-Prover-
1
-Round Games, which we define next.
1
1

1

Strictly speaking,
the notion below is often referred to as projection
2
-Prover-
1
-Round games. As we only deal with projection games in this paper we omit the more general definition.

Definition 1.1
.

An instance
\Psi
of
2
-Prover-
1
-Round Games consists of a bipartite
graph
G=(L\cup R,E)
, alphabets
\Sigma_{L}
,
\Sigma_{R}
, and a collection of constraints

\Phi=\{\phi_{e}\}_{e\in E}
specified by maps
\phi_{e}\colon\Sigma_{L}\to\Sigma_{R}
.

1.

The alphabet size of
\Psi
is defined to
be
|\Sigma_{L}|+|\Sigma_{R}|
.

2.

The value of
\Psi
is defined to be
the maximum fraction of edges
e\in E

that can be satisfied by any assignment.
That is,

{\sf val}(\Psi)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}A_{L}\colon L\to\Sigma_{L}\\
A_{R}\colon R\to\Sigma_{R}\end{subarray}}\frac{|\{e=(u,v)\in E~|~\phi_{e}(A_{L}(u))=A_{R}(v)\}|}{|E|}.

The combinatorial view of
2
-Prover-
1
-Round
Games has its origins in an equivalent,
active view of PCP in terms
of a game between a verifier and two all-powerful
provers. The verifier and the two provers have access
to an instance
\Psi
of
2
-Prover-
1
-Round Games,
and the provers may agree beforehand on a strategy;
after this period they are not allowed to communicate.
The verifier then
picks a random edge,
e=(u,v)
, from the
2
-Prover-
1
-Round game, sends
u
to the first prover and
v
to the second prover, and then receives a label in response from each one of them. Finally, the verifier checks that these labels satisfy the constraint
\phi_{e}
, and if so accepts (and otherwise rejects).
It is easy to see that the value of the
2
-Prover-
1
-Round game is equal to the acceptance probability of
the verifier under the best strategy of the provers.
This view will be useful for us later.

The majority of hardness of
approximation results are proved by combining
the basic PCP theorem of
[

,
4
,
3

[

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists
\gamma>0
such that for sufficiently
large
R
, given a
2
-Prover-
1
-Round game

\Psi

1.

YES case:
{\sf val}(\Psi)=1
.

2.

NO case:
{\sf val}(\Psi)\leqslant\frac{1}{R^{\gamma}}
.

While the soundness guarantee in

1.2
is often sufficient, some applications require strengthenings of it. In particular, one could require the soundness to be as small as possible with respect to the alphabet size (as opposed to just being small in absolute terms). The tradeoff between the soundness error of the PCP
and the alphabet size of the PCP is the main focus
of this paper.

Since a random
assignment to
\Psi
satisfies in expectation at least

\frac{1}{R}
fraction of the constraints, the best alphabet to soundness tradeoff one could hope for in
Theorem

1.2
is with
\gamma=1-o(1)
. Khot and Safra
[
31

were the first to achieve a reasonable bound for
\gamma

\gamma=1/6
with imperfect completeness (i.e.,
{\sf val}(\Psi)\geqslant 1-o(1)
instead of
{\sf val}(\Psi)=1
in the YES case). Subsequently, Chan
[
11
]
obtained an improvement (using different techniques), showing that one can get
\gamma=1/2-o(1)
(again with imperfect
completeness).

1.1
Main Results

1.1.1
Near Optimal Alphabet vs Soundness Tradeoff

The main result of this work improves upon all prior results, and shows that one may take
\gamma=1-o(1)

in
Theorem

1.2
, again with imperfect completeness. Formally, we show:

Theorem 1.3
.

For all
\varepsilon,\tau>0
, for sufficiently large
R
,
given a
2
-Prover-
1
-Round game
\Psi
, it is NP-hard
to distinguish between the following two cases:

1.

YES case:
{\sf val}(\Psi)\geqslant 1-\tau
.

2.

NO case:
{\sf val}(\Psi)\leqslant\frac{1}{R^{1-\varepsilon}}
.

As discussed below,
Theorem

1.3
has several applications to combinatorial
optimization problems. These
applications require additional features from the
instances produced in
Theorem

1.3
which
are omitted from the formulation for the sake of clarity.
For instance, one application requires a good tradeoff between the size of the instance and the size of the alphabet,
which our construction achieves (see the discussion following
Theorem

1.4
). Other applications require
the underlying constraint graph to be bounded-degree and biregular, which our construction also achieves (after mild modifications; see
Theorem

7.2
).

1.1.2
Application: NP-Hardness of Approximating Quadratic Programs

An instance of the Quadratic Programming problem consists of a
quadratic form
Q(x)=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}x_{i}x_{j}
where
a_{i,i}=0
for all
i
, and the goal is to maximize
Q(x)
over
x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
. It was previously known that this problem admits an
O(\log n)
approximation
algorithm
[
35
,
40
,
12
]
and is NP-hard to approximate
within factor
(\log n)^{1/6-o(1)}

[
2
,
31
]
under quasi-polynomial reductions.
As a first application of
Theorem

1.3
, we improve the hardness of approximation result for the Quadratic Programming problem:

Theorem 1.4
.

It is NP-hard to approximate
Quadratic Programming to within a factor of
(\log n)^{1-o(1)}
under quasi-polynomial reductions.

Theorem

1.4
is proved via a connection between

2
-Prover-
1
-Round Games and Quadratic Programming
due to Arora, Berger, Hazan, Kindler, and Safra
[
2
]
. This connection requires a
good tradeoff between the alphabet size, the soundness error
of the PCP, and the size of the PCP, and the construction in
Theorem

1.4
has
a sufficiently good tradeoff: letting

N
be the size of the instance,
the alphabet size is

(\log N)^{1-o(1)}
and
the soundness error is

(\log N)^{-1+o(1)}
.
2
2

2

We remark that, due to the use of the long-code, Chan
[
11
]
does not achieve a good enough trade-off between the alphabet size and the instance size. This is the reason his result does not yield an improvement over the inapproximability result for Quadratic Programming of Khot and Safra
[
31
]
.

Relevance to the sliding scale conjecture?

We do not know
how to use our techniques to achieve soundness error that is
smaller than inversely poly-logarithmic in the instance size.
In particular, our techniques have no bearing
on the sliding scale conjecture,
which asserts the existence of a PCP with
O(1)
queries, polynomial size, polynomial alphabet size, and inverse polynomial soundness
[
39
]
.
A key feature of our PCP reduction is the so-called “covering property”, which seems to inherently limit the soundness of our construction to be at most inversely logarithmic in the instance size.

2
-CSPs

A
2
-Constraint Satisfaction Problem (
2
-CSP) instance

X
, a set of constraints
C
,

The goal is to find an assignment

There is a simple

-CSP problem for instances

. Lee and Manurangsi proved a

hardness of approximation result assuming the

[
33
]
. Unconditionally, they show the problem to be

\left(\frac{1}{3}-o(1)\right)d

Using the ideas of Lee and Manurangsi, our main
result implies a nearly matching NP-hardness
result for bounded degree
2
-CSPs:

Theorem 1.5
.

For all
\eta>0
, for sufficiently large
d
,
approximating the value of
2
-CSPs with
degree at most
d
within factor

\left(\frac{1}{2}-\eta\right)d
is NP-hard under
randomized reductions.

As in
[
33
]
,

1.5
has a further
application to finding independent sets in

k
-claw is the

vertex graph which consists of one center vertex connected to

does not contain

k
-claw as an induced subgraph. There is a polynomial time algorithm that approximates the size of the largest

within

\frac{k}{2}

6

46

[

\left(\frac{1}{3}+o(1)\right)k

As in

Our Techniques

Theorem

Both of these components incorporate ideas from the proof of the
2
-to-
1
Games Theorem. The outer PCP is constructed using smooth parallel repetition

while the inner PCP

,
15
,
16
,
28
]
.

The novelty in this paper, in terms of techniques, is twofold. First, in order to get such a strong alphabet to soundness tradeoff, we must consider a Grassmann test in a different and extreme regime of parameters. Additionally, we have to work with much lower soundness error than prior works. These differences complicate matters considerably. Second, our soundness analysis is more involved than that of the
2
-to-
1
-Games Theorem. As is the case in
[
27
,
15
,
16
,
28
]
, we too use global hypercontractivity, but we do so more extensively.
We also require quantitatively stronger versions of
global hypercontractivity over the Grassmann graph
which are due to
[
19
]
.
Our analysis also incorporates ideas from the plane versus
plane test and direct product testing
[
43
,
26
,
36
]
, from classical PCP
theory
[
31
]
, as well as from error correcting codes
[
22
]
. All of these tools are necessary to prove our main technical statement —
Lemma

1.7
below — which
is a combinatorial statement perhaps of independent interest.

We now elaborate on each one of the components separately.

1.2.1
The Inner PCP

Our inner PCP is based on the subspace vs subspace
low-degree test. Below, we first give a general
overview of the objective in low-degree testing.
We then discuss
the traditional notion of soundness as well as a
non-traditional notion of soundness for low-degree tests. Finally, we explain the low-degree test
used in this paper, the notion of soundness that we
need from it, and the way that this notion of soundness is used.

Low-degree tests in PCPs.

Low-degree tests have been a vital component in PCPs since their inception, and
much attention has been devoted to improving their
various parameters. The goal in low-degree testing is
to encode a low-degree function
f\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
via a table (or a few tables) of values in a locally testable way. Traditionally, one picks a parameter
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
(which is thought of as a constant and is most often just
2
) and encodes the function
f
by the table
T
of restrictions of
f
to
\ell
-dimensional affine subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. For the case
\ell=2
, the test associated with this encoding is known as the Plane vs Plane test
[
43
]
. The Plane vs Plane test proceeds by picking two planes
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
intersecting on a line, and then checking that

T[P_{1}]
and
T[P_{2}]
agree on
P_{1}\cap P_{2}
. It is easy to see that the test has perfect completeness, namely that a valid table of restrictions
T
passes
the test with probability
1
. In the other direction, the soundness error of the
test — which is a converse
type statement — is much less clear (and is crucial towards applications in PCP). In the context of the Plane vs Plane
test, it is known that if a table
T
, that assigns to
each plane a degree
d
function, passes the Plane vs
Plane test with probability
\varepsilon\geqslant q^{-c}
(where

c>0
is a small absolute constant), then there is a degree

d
function
f
such that
T[P]\equiv f|_{P}
on at
least
\Omega(\varepsilon)
fraction of the planes.

Nailing down the value of the constant
c
for which
soundness holds is an interesting open problem which
is related to soundness vs alphabet size vs instance
size tradeoff in PCPs
[
38
,
7
,
36
]
. Currently,
the best known analysis for the Plane vs Plane test
[
38
]
shows that one may take
c=1/8
. Better analysis is known for higher dimensional encoding
[
7
,
36
]
, and for the
3
-dimensional
version of it a near optimal soundness result is known
[
36
]
.

Low-degree tests in this paper.

In the context of the current paper, we wish to encode
linear functions
f\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and we do so by
the subspaces encoding. Specifically, we set
integer parameters
\ell_{1}\geqslant\ell_{2}
, and encode
the function
f
using the table
T_{1}
of the restrictions of
f
to all
\ell_{1}
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and the table
T_{2}

of the restrictions of
f
to all
\ell_{2}
-dimensional
linear subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. The test we consider
is the natural inclusion test:

1.

Sample a random
\ell_{1}
-dimensional subspace

L_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and a random
\ell_{2}
-dimensional
subspace
L_{2}\subseteq L_{1}
.

2.

Read
T_{1}[L_{1}]
,
T_{2}[L_{2}]
and accept if they agree on
L_{2}
.

As is often the case, the completeness of the test — namely the fact that valid tables
T_{1},T_{2}
pass the
test with probability
1
— is clear. The question
of most interest then is with regards to the soundness
of the test. Namely, what is the smallest
\varepsilon
such that any two tables
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
that assign linear
functions to subspaces and pass the test with
probability
\varepsilon
, must necessarily “come from” a legitimate linear function
f
?

Traditional notion of soundness.

As the alphabet vs soundness tradeoff is key to the discussion herein, we begin by remarking that the alphabet size of the above encoding is
q^{\ell_{1}}+q^{\ell_{2}}=\Theta(q^{\ell_{1}})
(since there
are
q^{\ell}
distinct linear functions on a linear
space of dimension
\ell
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
). Thus, ideally
we would like to show that the soundness error
of the above test is
q^{-(1-o(1))\ell_{1}}
. Alas,
this is false. Indeed, it turns out that one may
construct assignments that pass the test with
probability at least
\Omega(\max(q^{-\ell_{2}},q^{\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}}))
that do not have significant
correlation with any linear function
f
:

1.

Taking
T_{1},T_{2}
randomly by assigning to each subspace a random linear function, one can easily see that the test passes with probability
\Theta(q^{-\ell_{2}})
.

2.

Taking linear subspaces
W_{1},\ldots,W_{100q^{\ell_{1}}}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of codimension
1
randomly, and a random linear
function
f_{i}\colon W_{i}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
for each

i
, one may choose
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
as follows.
For each
L_{1}
, pick a random
i
such that
L_{1}\subseteq W_{i}
(if such
i
exists) and assign
T_{1}[L_{1}]=f_{i}|_{L_{1}}
. For each
L_{2}
, pick a random
i
such that
L_{2}\subseteq W_{i}
(if such
i
exists) and assign
T_{2}[L_{2}]=f_{i}|_{L_{2}}
. Taking

L_{2}\subseteq L_{1}
randomly, one sees that with
constant probability
L_{2}
has
\Theta(q^{\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}})
many possible
i
’s,
L_{1}
has
\Theta(1)
many possible
i
’s and furthermore
there is at least one
i
that is valid for both
of them. With probability
\Omega(q^{\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}})
this common
i
is chosen for both
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, and in this case, the test on
(L_{1},L_{2})
passes. It follows that, in expectation,
T_{1},T_{2}
pass the test with probability
\Omega(q^{\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}})
.

In light of the above, it makes sense that the best possible alphabet vs soundness tradeoff we may achieve
with the subspace encoding is by taking
\ell_{2}=\ell_{1}/2
. Such a setting of the parameters would give alphabet size
R=q^{\ell_{1}}
and (possibly) soundness error
\Theta(1/\sqrt{R})
.
This tradeoff is not good enough for our purposes, and so we must venture beyond the traditional
notion of soundness.

Non-traditional notion of soundness.

The above test was first considered in the context
of the
2
-to-
1
Games Theorem, wherein one takes
q=2
and
\ell_{2}=\ell_{1}-1
. In this setting, the
test is not sound in the traditional sense; instead, the test is shown to satisfy a non-standard notion of soundness, which
nevertheless is sufficient for the purposes of constructing a PCP. More specifically, in
[
28
]

it is proved that for all
\varepsilon>0
there is
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that for sufficiently large
\ell
and for tables
T_{1},T_{2}
as above, there are subspaces
Q\subseteq W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with
{\sf dim}(Q)+{\sf codim}(W)\leqslant r
and a linear function
f\colon W\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that

\Pr_{Q\subseteq L_{1}\subseteq W}[T_{1}[L_{1}]\equiv f|_{L_{1}}]\geqslant\varepsilon^{\prime}(\varepsilon)>0.

We refer to the set

\{L\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}~|~{\sf dim}(L)=\ell_{1},Q\subseteq L\subseteq W\}

as the zoom-in of
Q
and zoom-out of
W
. While this result is sufficient for the
purposes of
2
-to-
1
Games, the dependency between

\ell
and
\varepsilon
(and thus, between the soundness
and the alphabet size) is not good enough for us.

Our low-degree test.

It turns out that
the proper setting of parameters for us is
\ell_{2}=(1-\delta)\ell_{1}
where
\delta>0

is a small constant. With these parameters, we are
able to show that for
\varepsilon\geqslant q^{-(1-\delta^{\prime})\ell_{1}}
(where
\delta^{\prime}=\delta^{\prime}(\delta)>0

is a vanishing function of
\delta
), if
T_{1}
,
T_{2}

pass the test with probability at least
\varepsilon
, then
there are subspaces
Q\subseteq W
with
{\sf dim}(Q)+{\sf codim}(W)\leqslant r=r(\delta)\in\mathbb{N}
, and
a linear function
f\colon W\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
such
that

\Pr_{Q\subseteq L_{1}\subseteq W}[T_{1}[L_{1}]\equiv f|_{L_{1}}]\geqslant\varepsilon^{\prime}(\varepsilon)=\Omega(\varepsilon).

Working in the very small soundness regime of

\varepsilon\geqslant q^{-(1-\delta^{\prime})\ell_{1}}
entails many challenges, however. First,
dealing with such small soundness requires us to use a strengthening of the
global hypercontractivity result of
[
28
]
in
the form of an optimal level
d
inequality due to
Evra, Kindler and Lifshitz
[
19
]
. Second, in the context of
[
28
]
,
\varepsilon^{\prime}
could be
any function of
\varepsilon
(and indeed it ends up
being a polynomial function of
\varepsilon
). In the
context of the current paper, it is crucial that

\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon^{1+o(1)}
, as opposed to, say,
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon^{1.1}
. The reason is that, as we
are dealing with very small
\varepsilon
, the result would be trivial for
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon^{1.1}
and not
useful towards the analysis of the PCP (as then

\varepsilon^{\prime}
would be below the threshold
q^{-\ell_{1}}

which represents the agreement a random linear
function
f
has with
T_{1}
).

1.2.2
Getting List Decoding Bounds

As is usually the case in PCP reductions, we require a list
decoding version for our low-degree test. Indeed,
using a standard argument we are able to show
that in the setting that
\ell_{2}=(1-\delta)\ell_{1}

and
\varepsilon\geqslant q^{-(1-\delta^{\prime})\ell_{1}}
, there is

r=r(\delta,\delta^{\prime})\in\mathbb{N}
such that
for at least
q^{-\Theta(\ell_{1})}
fraction of
subspaces
Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of dimension
r
, there
exists a subspace
W
with codimension at most
r
and
Q\subseteq W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, as well as
a linear function
f\colon W\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
, such
that

\Pr_{Q\subseteq L_{1}\subseteq W}[T_{1}[L_{1}]\equiv f|_{L_{1}}]\geqslant\varepsilon^{\prime}(\varepsilon)=\Omega(\varepsilon).

(1)

This list decoding version theorem alone is not enough.
In our PCP construction, we compose the inner
PCP with an outer PCP (that we describe below), and
analyzing the composition requires decoding
global linear functions (from a list decoding version
theorem as above) in a coordinated manner between two non communicating parties. Oftentimes, the
number of possible global functions that may be
decoded is constant, in which case randomly sampling
one among them works. This is not the case
for us, though: if
(Q,W)
and
(Q^{\prime},W^{\prime})

are distinct zoom-in and zoom-out pairs for which
there are linear functions
f_{Q,W}
and
f_{Q^{\prime},W^{\prime}}

satisfying (
1
), then the functions
f_{Q,W}

and
f_{Q^{\prime},W^{\prime}}
could be completely different. Thus,
to achieve a coordinated decoding procedure, we must:

1.

Facilitate a way for the two parties to agree on a zoom-in and zoom-out pair
(Q,W)
with noticeable probability.

2.

Show that for each
(Q,W)
there are at most

{\sf poly}(1/\varepsilon)
linear functions
f_{Q,W}
for which

\Pr_{Q\subseteq L_{1}\subseteq W}[T_{1}[L_{1}]\equiv f_{Q,W}|_{L_{1}}]\geqslant\varepsilon^{\prime}.

The second item is precisely the reason
we need
\varepsilon^{\prime}
to be
\varepsilon^{1+o(1)}
; any worse dependency, such as
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon^{1.1}
would lead
to the second item being false. We also remark that
the number of functions being
{\sf poly}(1/\varepsilon)

is important to us as well. There is some slack in this bound,
but a weak quantitative bound such as
{\sf exp}({\sf exp}(1/\varepsilon))
would have been insufficient for some
of our applications. Luckily, such bounds can be deduced from
[
22
]
for the case of linear functions.
3
3

3

In the case of higher degree functions (even quadratic functions) some bounds are known
[
23
,
8
]
but they would not have been good enough for us.

We now discuss the first item.
It turns out that agreeing on the zoom-in
Q

can be delegated to the outer PCP, in the sense that our outer PCP game will provide the two parties with a coordinated zoom-in
Q
(while still being sound).
Morally speaking, this works because in our list decoding theorem,
the fraction of zoom-ins

that work is significant.
Coordinating zoom-outs is more difficult, and this
is where much of the novelty in our analysis lies.

1.2.3
Coordinating Zoom-outs

For the sake of simplicity and to focus on the main ideas, we ignore zoom-ins for now and assume that the
list decoding statement holds with no

the list decoding theorem asserts that there exists
a zoom-out
W
of constant codimension on which
there is a global linear function agreeing with the table

. However, there
could be many such zoom-outs

, say
W_{1},\ldots,W_{m}

and say all of them were of codimension

The main issue is that the number

is intermediate, say

.

This issue had already appeared in

Therein, this issue is resolved by showing that

This result
is not good enough for us, due to the polynomial

and

the agreement of

and

We resolve this issue by showing a stronger, essentially optimal version of the above assertion

1.2.4
The Outer PCP

Our outer PCP game is the same as that of
[
27
,
15
]
, which is a smooth
parallel repetition of the equation versus
variables game of Hastad
[
25
]
(or of
[
29
]
for the application to Quadratic Programming). As in there, we equip this game
with the “advice” feature to facilitate zoom-in
coordination (as discussed above). For the sake of completeness we elaborate
on the construction of the outer PCP below.

We start with an instance of
{\sf Gap3Lin}
that has a large
gap between the soundness and completeness. Namely,
we start with an instance
(X,{\sf Eq})
of linear equations over

\mathbb{F}_{q}
in which each equation has the form

ax_{i_{1}}+bx_{i_{2}}+cx_{i_{3}}=d
. It is known
[
25
]

that for all
\eta>0
, it is NP-hard to distinguish between the following two cases:

1.

YES case:
{\sf val}(X,{\sf Eq})\geqslant 1-\eta
.

2.

NO case:
{\sf val}(X,{\sf Eq})\leqslant\frac{1.1}{q}
.

The standard
Variable versus Equation game
proceeds as follows:

1.

The verifier picks an equation
e
uniformly at random from
{\sf Eq}
and lets
U=\{x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},x_{i_{3}}\}
be the variables in it.

2.

The verifier samples
V\subseteq U
of size
1
uniformly.

The verifier then sends
U
to the first prover, and
V
to the second prover. The verifier expects to receive from each prover an assignment to the variables they received, and accepts if their assignments are consistent and satisfy the equation
e
. It is easy to see that if

{\sf val}(X,{\sf Eq})\geqslant 1-\eta
, then the provers have a strategy that makes the verifier accept with probability at least
1-\eta
, and if
{\sf val}(X,{\sf Eq})\leqslant\frac{1.1}{q}
, then for any provers’ strategy, the verifier accepts with probability at most
1-\Omega(1)
.

Now, we modify the standard Variable versus Equation game into the
smooth Variable versus Equation game with
r
-advice
, which proceeds as follows.
The verifier has a smoothness parameter
\beta>0
and picks a random equation
e
, say
ax_{i_{1}}+bx_{i_{2}}+cx_{i_{3}}=d
, from
(X,{\sf Eq})
. Then:

1.

With probability
1-\beta

the verifier takes
U=V=\{x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},x_{i_{3}}\}
and vectors

u_{1}=v_{1},\ldots,u_{r}=v_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
sampled uniformly and independently.

2.

With probability
\beta
, the verifier
sets
U=\{x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},x_{i_{3}}\}
,
chooses a set consisting of a single variable
V\subseteq U
uniformly at random. The verifier picks
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
uniformly and independently
and appends to each
v_{i}
the value
0
in
the coordinates of
U\setminus V
to get
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
.

After that, the verifier sends
U
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}

to the first prover and
V
and
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
to the
second prover. The verifier expects to get from them
\mathbb{F}_{q}
assignments to the variables in
U

and in
V
, and accepts if and only if these assignments
are consistent, and furthermore the assignment to
U
satisfies the equation
e
.

Denoting the smooth Variable versus Equation game above by
{\sf\Psi}
, it is easy to see that if
{\sf val}(X,{\sf Eq})\geqslant 1-\eta
, then
{\sf val}(\Psi)\geqslant 1-\eta
, and
if
{\sf val}(X,{\sf Eq})\leqslant 1.1/q
, then
{\sf val}(\Psi)\leqslant 1-\Omega(q^{-r}\beta)
.
Indeed, to see the last assertion, note that the probability that
V
is a proper subset of
U
and that
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
are all
0
, is
q^{-r}\beta
. In that case, the game being played is the standard Variable versus Equation game, which has value at most
1-\Omega(1)
as discussed earlier.
The gap between
1-\eta

and
1-\Omega(q^{-r}\beta)
is too weak for us, and thus we
apply parallel repetition.

In the parallel repetition of the smooth Variable versus Equation game with advice, denoted by
\Psi^{\otimes k}
, the verifier picks
k
equations
uniformly and independently
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}
, and picks
U_{i}
,
u^{i}_{1},\ldots,u^{i}_{r}
and
V_{i}
,
v^{i}_{1},\ldots,v^{i}_{r}
for each
i=1,\ldots,k
from
e_{i}
independently. Thus, the questions
of the provers may be seen as
U=U_{1}\cup\ldots\cup U_{k}
and
V=V_{1}\cup\ldots\cup V_{k}
and their advice
is
\vec{u}_{j}=(u^{1}_{j},\ldots,u^{k}_{j})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
for
j=1,\ldots,r
and

\vec{v}_{j}=(v^{1}_{j},\ldots,v^{k}_{j})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
for
j=1,\ldots,r
respectively. The verifier expects to get from the first prover a vector in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
which specifies an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
assignment to
U
, and from the second prover a vector
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
which specifies an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
assignment to
V
. The verifier accepts if and only if
these assignments are consistent and the assignment
of the first prover satisfies all of
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}
.
It is clear that if
{\sf val}(X,{\sf Eq})\geqslant 1-\eta
,
then
{\sf val}(\Psi^{\otimes m})\geqslant 1-k\eta
.
Using the parallel repetition theorem of Rao
[
42
]
we argue that if
{\sf val}(X,{\sf Eq})\leqslant\frac{1.1}{q}
, then
{\sf val}(\Psi^{\otimes k})\leqslant 2^{-\Omega(\beta q^{-r}k)}
. The game
\Psi^{\otimes k}
is
our outer PCP game.

.

In the case
of the Quadratic Programming application we require

1.2.5
Composing the Outer PCP and the Inner PCP Game

To compose the outer and inner PCPs, we take the outer PCP game, only keep the questions
U
to the first
prover and consider an induced
2
-Prover-
1
-Round
game on it. The alphabet is
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3k}
, and we think of a response to a question
U
as an
\mathbb{F}_{q}

assignment to the variables of
U
. There is
a constraint between
U
and
U^{\prime}
if there is a question
V
to the second prover such that
V\subseteq U\cap U^{\prime}
. Denoting the assignments to

U
and
U^{\prime}
by
s_{U}
and
s_{U^{\prime}}
, the constraint
between
U
and
U^{\prime}
is that
s_{U}
satisfies all
of the equations that form
U
,
s_{U^{\prime}}
satisfies
all of the equations that form
U^{\prime}
, and

s_{U}
,
s_{U^{\prime}}
agree on
U\cap U^{\prime}
.

The composition amounts to replacing each question
U
with a copy of our inner PCP. Namely, we identify between the question
U
and the space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
, and then replace
U
by a copy of the
\ell_{2},\ell_{1}
subspaces graph of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
. The answer
s_{U}
is naturally identified with the linear function
f_{U}(x)=\langle s_{U},x\rangle
, which is then encoded by the subspaces encoding via tables of assignments
T_{1,U}
and
T_{2,U}
.

The constraints of the composed PCP must check that:
(1) side conditions: the encoded vector
s_{U}
satisfies the equations
of
U
, and (2) consistency:
s_{U}
and
s_{U^{\prime}}
agree
on
U\cap U^{\prime}
.

The first set of constraints is addressed by the folding technique, which we omit from this discussion.
The second set of constraints is addressed by the

\ell_{1}
vs
\ell_{2}
subspace test, except that we
have to modify it so that it works across blocks
U

and
U^{\prime}
. This completes the description of the
composition step of the other PCP and the inner PCP,
and thereby the description of our reduction.

Analyzing the Composed PCP.

Traditionally, the analysis of the composed PCP relates its soundness with the soundness of the outer PCP and the soundness of the inner PCP. Such analysis, however, fails in our case. Instead, and similarly to prior works
[
27
,
16
]
, our analysis relates the soundness of the composed PCP with the non-standard notion of soundness of the inner PCP (as discussed above) and the soundness of a modification of the (standard) outer PCP game, as presented above. The goal of this modification is to allow the provers to correlate their choice for the zoom-in set
Q
, and it is achieved by the additional vectors
\{\vec{u}_{j}\}_{j=1,\ldots,r}
and
\{\vec{v}_{j}\}_{j=1,\ldots,r}
that the verifier sends to the provers in the outer PCP game.

1.2.6
The Covering Property

We end this introductory section by discussing the
covering property, which is an important feature of
our outer PCP construction enabling the composition step to go through. The covering property
first appeared in
[
31
]
and later more extensively
in the context of the
2
-to-
1
Games
[
27
,
15
]
.
To discuss the covering property, let

k\in\mathbb{N}
be thought of as large, let

\beta\in(0,1)
be thought of as
k^{-0.99}

and consider pairwise disjoint sets
U_{1},\ldots,U_{k}
where each
U_{i}
has size
3
(in our context,
U_{i}

will be the set of variables in the
i
th equation the
verifier chose). Let
U=U_{1}\cup\ldots\cup U_{k}
, and consider
the following two distributions over tuples in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
:

1.

Sample

x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
uniformly.

2.

For each
i
independently, take
V_{i}=U_{i}
with
probability
1-\beta
and otherwise take
V_{i}\subseteq U_{i}

randomly of size
1
, then set
V=V_{1}\cup\ldots\cup V_{k}
.
Sample
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
uniformly and lift them to points
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
by appending
0
’s in
U\setminus V
.
Output the lifted points.

In
[
27
]
it is shown that the two distributions above are

q^{3\ell}\beta\sqrt{k}
close in statistical distance, which
is good enough for
Theorem

1.3
. This is insufficient for
Theorem

1.4
, though.
4
4

4

The reason is that, letting
N
be the size of the instance
we produce, it holds that
k
is roughly
\log N

and
q^{\ell}
is the alphabet size. To have small statistical
distance, we must have
k\leqslant q^{6\ell}
, hence the soundness error
could not go lower than
(\log N)^{-1/6}
.
Carrying out a
different analysis, one can show that the two
distributions are close with better parameters and in
a stronger sense: there exists a set
E
of
\ell
tuples
which has negligible measure in both distributions, such
that each tuple not in
E
is assigned the same probability
under the two distributions up to factor
(1+o(1))
.
We are able to prove this statement provided that
k
is
only slightly larger than
q^{2\ell}
, which is still insufficient.

The issue with the above two distributions is that they are
actually far from each other if, say,
k=q^{1.9\ell}
.
To see that, one can notice that the expected number of
i
’s
such that each one of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}
has the form

(a,0,0)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
on coordinates corresponding to
U_{i}

is very different. In the first distribution, this expectation
is
\Theta(q^{-2\ell}k)
which is less than 1, whereas in the second distribution it is at least
\beta k/3\geqslant k^{0.01}
.

To resolve this issue and to go all the way through in the Quadratic Programming application, we have to modify the
distributions in the covering property so that (a) they will be
close even if
k=q^{1.01\ell}
, and (b) we can still use
these distributions in the composition step in our analysis
of the PCP construction. Indeed, this is the route we take,
and the two distributions we use are defined as follows:

1.

Sample

x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
uniformly.

2.

For each
i
independently, take
V_{i}=U_{i}
with
probability
1-\beta
and otherwise take
V_{i}\subseteq U_{i}

randomly of size
1
, then set
V=V_{1}\cup\ldots\cup V_{k}
.
Sample
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
uniformly,
and let
w_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
be the vector of the coefficients of the equation
e_{i}
forming
U_{i}
.
Lift the points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}

to
x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{\ell}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
by appending
0
’s in
U\setminus V
and take
y_{j}=x_{j}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i,j}w_{i}
where
\alpha_{i,j}

are independent random elements from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Output
y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell}
.

We show that for a suitable choice of
k
and
\beta
, these distributions are close even in the case that

k=q^{1.01\ell}
.
5
5

5

More specifically, one takes a small
c>0
and chooses
\beta=k^{2c/3-1}
,
k=q^{(1+c)\ell}
.

Indeed, as a sanity check one could count the expected number
of appearances of blocks of the form
(0,a,0)\in\mathbb{F}^{3}

and see they are very close (
q^{-2\ell}k
versus
(1-\beta)q^{-2\ell}k+\beta kq^{-\ell}
).
In this setting of parameters,
k
is roughly equal to the
alphabet size — which can be made to be equal
(\log N)^{1-o(1)}
under
quasi-polynomial time reductions.

Remark 1.9
.

A tight covering property is crucial for obtaining the tight hardness of approximation factor in
Theorem

1.4
. In the reduction of
[
2
]
from
2
-Prover-

-Round
games to Quadratic Programming, the size of the resulting instance is exponential in the alphabet size and the soundness error remains roughly the same. In our case the alphabet size is roughly

hardness for Quadratic Programming.

1.3
Open Problems

We end this introductory section by mentioning a few open problems for future research.

1.

The Low-degree CSP Conjecture.
It would be interesting to see if our techniques have any bearing on the conjecture of
[
13
]
. This conjecture
is a sort of a mixture between the
d
-to-
1

games and sliding scale conjectures, and it focuses
on the relation between the
instance size and the soundness error, while allowing the alphabet to be quite
large. The conjecture is motivated by improved hardness
results for densest
k
-subgraph
style problems, and below we give a rough description of it.

The
2
-to-
1
(or
2
-to-
2
) Theorem asserts that the soundness of a PCP can be arbitrarily small constant in a
d
-to-
1
(or
d
-to-
d
) game even if
d
is fixed; it does not apply well in the sub-constant soundness regime, though. The parallel repetition theorem asserts that for every
d\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, one can get a
d(n)
-to-
d(n)
PCP with soundness which is polynomial in
1/d(n)
. The Low-degree CSP conjecture asks for a PCP that has
d(n)
growing with the instance size, whose soundness decays more rapidly than polynomial in
d
. Specifically, it asks for
d(n)=2^{\log^{\delta}n}
and soundness
2^{-\log^{1/2+\delta}n}
.

2.

Perfect completeness.
Our reduction starts with the
{\sf Gap3Lin}
problem and thus inherently has imperfect completeness. It would be very interesting if one could prove a version of
Theorem

1.3
with perfect completeness. As far as we know no result better than
Theorem

1.2
is known to date. While we are not aware of any direct applications of it, we believe such a result may lead to techniques which are able to give strong PCPs with perfect completeness (which are quite rare).

3.

Other applications.
As shown above, it is sometimes the case that results proved assuming the Unique-Games Conjecture can be translated into NP-hardness results using
Theorem

1.3
. We suspect there should be other such problems.

2.1
The Grassmann Domain

In this section we present the Grassmann domain and associated Fourier analytic tools that are required for our analysis of the inner PCP. Throughout this section we fix parameters
n,\ell
with
1\ll\ell\ll n
, and a prime power
q
(which we will eventually pick to be
2
).

2.1.1
Basic Definitions

The Grassmann domain
{\sf Grass}_{q}(n,\ell)
consists of the set of all
\ell
-dimensional subspaces
L\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. At times we will have a vector space
W
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and we may write
{\sf Grass}_{q}(W,\ell)
to mean
{\sf Grass}_{q}(n,\ell)
, where
n
is the dimension of
W
(under some identification of
W
with
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
). When the field size
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is clear from context we drop the subscript and simply write
{\sf Grass}(n,\ell)
or
{\sf Grass}(W,\ell)
.

The number of
\ell
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is counted by the Gaussian binomial coefficient,
\begin{bmatrix}{n}\\
{\ell}\end{bmatrix}_{q}
. The following
standard fact gives a formula for the Gaussian
binomial coefficients, and we omit the proof.

Fact 2.1
.

Suppose
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant\frac{n}{2}
, then the number of vertices in
{\sf Grass}_{q}(n,\ell)
is given by

\begin{bmatrix}{n}\\
{\ell}\end{bmatrix}_{q}=\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{q^{n}-q^{i}}{q^{\ell}-q^{i}}.

Bipartite Inclusion Graphs

We will often consider bipartite inclusion graphs between
{\sf Grass}_{q}(n,\ell)
and
{\sf Grass}_{q}(n,\ell^{\prime})
for
\ell^{\prime}<\ell
. This graph has an edge between every
L\in{\sf Grass}_{q}(n,\ell)
and
L^{\prime}\in{\sf Grass}_{q}(n,\ell^{\prime})
such that
L\supseteq L^{\prime}
. We refer to the family of such graphs as
Grassmann graphs
. The edges of these graphs are the motivation for the test used in our inner PCP. While we never refer to this graph explicitly, it will be helpful to have it in mind.

Zoom-ins and Zoom-outs.

For subspaces
Q\subseteq W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and a dimension
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, let

{\sf Zoom}_{\ell}[Q,W]=\{L\in{\sf Grass}_{q}(n,\ell)\;|\;Q\subseteq L\subseteq W\}.

We refer to
Q
as a zoom-in and
W
as a zoom-out. When
W=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
,
{\sf Zoom}_{\ell}[Q,W]
is the zoom-in on
Q
, and when
Q=\{0\}
,
{\sf Zoom}_{\ell}[Q,W]
is the zoom-out on
W
.

One notion that will be important to us is
(r,\varepsilon)
-pseudo-randomness, which measures how large the fractional size of a set of subspaces
\mathcal{L}
can be in a zoom-in/zoom-out restrictions of “size

\mu(\mathcal{L})=\Pr_{L\in{\sf Grass}_{q}(n,\ell)}[L\in\mathcal{L}].

We remark that the dimension
\ell

For subspaces

\mu_{Q,W}(\mathcal{L})=\Pr_{L\in{\sf Grass}_{q}(n,\ell)}[L\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;Q\subseteq L\subseteq W].

For a zoom-in

\mu_{\circ,W}(\mathcal{L})=\Pr_{L\in{\sf Grass}_{q}(n,\ell)}[L\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;L\subseteq W].

2.2
Hardness of 3LIN

In this section we cite several hardness of approximation results for the problem of solving linear equations over
finite fields, which are the starting point of our reduction.
We begin by defining the
{\sf 3Lin}
and the
{\sf Gap3Lin}
problem.

Definition 2.5
.

For a prime power
q
, an instance of
{\sf 3Lin}

(X,{\sf Eq})
consists of a set of variables
X
and
a set of linear equations
{\sf Eq}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Each equation in
{\sf Eq}
depends on exactly three variables in
X
, each variable appears in at most
10
equations, and any two distinct equations in
{\sf Eq}
share at most a single variable.

The goal in the
{\sf 3Lin}
problem is to find an assignment

A\colon X\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
satisfying as many of the equations
in
E
as possible. The maximum fraction of equations that
can be satisfied is called the value of the instance.
We remark that usually in the literature,
the condition that two equations in

E
share at most a single variable is not included
in the definition of
{\sf 3Lin}
, as well as the bound on the
number of occurrences of each variable.

For
0<s<c\leqslant 1
, the problem
{\sf Gap3Lin}[c,s]
is the promise
problem wherein the input is an instance
(X,{\sf Eq})
of
{\sf 3Lin}

promised to either have value at least
c
or at most
s
,
and the goal is to distinguish between these two cases.
The problem
{\sf Gap3Lin}[c,s]
with various settings of
c
and
s

will be the starting point for
our reductions.

In the proof of
Theorem

1.3
, we will use a classical result of Håstad
[
25
]
,
stating that for
general
{\sf 3Lin}
instances (i.e., without the additional conditions
that two equations share at most a single variable and each variable occurs in at most
10
equations),
the problem
{\sf Gap3Lin}[1-\varepsilon,1/q+\varepsilon]
is NP-hard for all
constant
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
. This result implies
the following theorem by elementary reductions (similarly to
Theorem

2.7
; see also
[
37
, Footnote 14]
):

Theorem 2.6
.

There exists
s<1
such that for every
\eta>0
and prime
q
,
{\sf Gap3Lin}\left[1-\eta,s\right]
is
{\sf NP}
-hard.

In the proof of
Theorem

1.4
we will need a hardness result for
{\sf 3Lin}
with completeness close to
1
, and we will use a hardness result of Khot and Ponnuswami
[
29
]
. Once
again, their result does not immediately guarantee the fact
that any two equations share at most a single variable and that each variable occurs in at most
10
equations,
however it can be achieved by
an elementary reduction.

Theorem 2.7
.

For any
q
power of
2
, there is a reduction from
{\sf SAT}
with size
n
to a
{\sf Gap3Lin}[1-\eta,1-\varepsilon]
instance with size
N
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
\{0,1\}
-coefficients, where

•

Both
N
and the running time of the reduction are bounded by
2^{O(\log^{2}n)}
;

•

\eta\leqslant 2^{-\Omega(\sqrt{\log N})}
;

•

\varepsilon\geqslant\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\log^{3}N}\right)
.

Proof.

Using
[
29
, Theorem 4]
we get the statement above without the additional property that any two distinct equations share at most a single variable.
To get this property we reduce an instance
(X,{\sf Eq})
to an instance
(X^{\prime},{\sf Eq}^{\prime})
as
follows: for each equation
e\in{\sf Eq}
given as
x+y+z=b
for
x,y,z\in X
, we produce the new variables
x_{e},y_{e},z_{e}
and add the equations

x+y_{e}+z_{e}=b
,
x_{e}+y+z_{e}=b
and
x_{e}+y_{e}+z=b
. We note that each variable in
(X^{\prime},{\sf Eq}^{\prime})
appears in at most
10
equations, and that if

{\sf val}(X,{\sf Eq})\geqslant 1-\eta
, then
{\sf val}(X^{\prime},{\sf Eq}^{\prime})\geqslant 1-\eta
, so the completeness is clear. For the soundness analysis suppose that
A
is an assignment to
(X^{\prime},{\sf Eq}^{\prime})
satisfying at least
1-\varepsilon
fraction of the equations. By an averaging argument for at least
1-3\varepsilon
fraction of
e\in{\sf Eq}
, we have that
A
satisfies all of the equations
x+y_{e}+z_{e}=b
,
x_{e}+y+z_{e}=b
and
x_{e}+y_{e}+z=b
. By adding them up and using the fact that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has characteristic
2
we get that
A
also satisfies the equation
x+y+z=b
, giving that
{\sf val}(X,{\sf Eq})\geqslant 1-3\varepsilon
and completing the soundness analysis.
∎

3
The Outer PCP

In this section, we describe our outer PCP game.
In short, our outer PCP is a smooth parallel repetition of a “variable versus equation” game with advice. This outer PCP was
first considered in
[
31
]
without the advice feature, and
then in
[
27
]
with the advice feature.

3.1
The Outer PCP construction

Let
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}
be parameters. Our
reduction starts with an instance
(X,{\sf Eq})
of the
{\sf Gap3Lin}[1-\varepsilon_{1},1-\varepsilon_{2}]

problem, and we present it gradually. We begin by presenting the
basic Variable versus Equation Game, then equip
it with the additional features of smoothness and advice, and finally perform parallel repetition.

3.1.1
The Variable versus Equation Game

We first convert the instance
(X,{\sf Eq})
into an instance
of
2
-Prover-
1
-Round Games, and it will be convenient
for us to describe it in the active view with a verifier
and
2
provers.

In the Variable versus Equation game, the verifier picks an equation
e\in{\sf Eq}
uniformly at random, and then chooses a random variable
x\in e
. The verifier sends the question
e
, i.e. the three variables appearing in
e
, to the first prover, and sends the variable
x
to the second prover. The provers are expected to answer with assignments to their received variables, and the verifier accepts if and only if the two assignments agree on
x
and the first prover’s assignment satisfies the equation
e
. If the verifier accepts then we also say that the provers pass. This game has the following completeness and soundness, which are both easy to see (we omit the formal proof):

1.

Completeness:
If
(X,{\sf Eq})
has an assignment satisfying
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of the equations, then the provers have a strategy that passes with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.

2.

Soundness:
If
(X,{\sf Eq})
has no assignment satisfying more than
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of the equations, then the provers can pass with probability at most
1-\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
.

3.1.2
The Smooth Variable versus Equation Game

We next describe a smooth version of the Variable versus Equation game. In this game, the verifier has a parameter

\beta\in(0,1]
, and it proceeds as follows:

1.

The verifier chooses an equation
e\in{\sf Eq}
uniformly,
and lets
U
be the set of variables in
e
.

2.

With probability
1-\beta
, the verifier chooses

V=U
. With probability
\beta
, the verifier chooses

V\subseteq U
randomly of size
1
.

3.

The verifier sends
U
to the first prover, and
V
to the second prover.

4.

The provers respond with assignments to the variables they receive, and the verifier accepts if and only if their
assignments agree on
V
and the assignment to
U
satisfies
the equation
e
.

The smooth Variable versus Equation game has the following
completeness and soundness property, which are again easily
seen to hold (we omit the formal proof).

1.

Completeness:
If
(X,{\sf Eq})
has an assignment satisfying
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of the equations, then the provers have a strategy that passes with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.

2.

Soundness:
If
(X,{\sf Eq})
has no assignment satisfying more than
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of the equations, then the provers can pass with probability at most
1-\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{3}
.

3.1.3
The Smooth Variable versus Equation Game with Advice

Next, we introduce the feature of advice into the smooth
Variable versus Equation Game. This “advice” acts as shared randomness which may help the provers in their strategy; we show though that it does not considerably change the soundness. The game is denoted by
G_{\beta,r}
for
\beta\in(0,1]
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and proceeds as follows:

1.

The verifier chooses an equation
e\in{\sf Eq}
uniformly,
and lets
U
be the set of variables in
e
.

2.

With probability
1-\beta
, the verifier chooses

V=U
. With probability
\beta
, the verifier chooses

V\subseteq U
randomly of size
1
.

3.

The verifier picks vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}

uniformly and independently. If
U=V
the verifier takes
u_{i}=v_{i}
for all
i
, and
otherwise the verifier takes the vectors
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}

where for all
i=1,\ldots,r
, the vector
u_{i}
agrees with
v_{i}
on the coordinate of
V
, and is

0
in the coordinates of
U\setminus V
.

4.

The verifier sends
U
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
to the first prover, and
V
and
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
to the second prover.

5.

The provers respond with assignments to the variables they receive, and the verifier accepts if and only if their
assignments agree on
V
and the assignment to
U
satisfies
the equation
e
.

Below we state the completeness and soundness of this game:

1.

Completeness:
If
(X,{\sf Eq})
has an assignment satisfying
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of the equations, then the provers have a strategy that passes with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
. This is easy to see.

2.

Soundness:
If
(X,{\sf Eq})
has no assignment satisfying more than
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of the equations, then the provers can pass with probability at most
1-\frac{q^{-r}\beta\varepsilon}{3}
. Indeed, suppose that the
provers can win the game with probability at least

1-\eta
. Note that with probability at least
\beta q^{-r}
it holds
that
U\neq V
and all the vectors
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}

and
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
are all
0
, in which case the provers
play the standard Variable versus Equation game. Thus,
the provers’ strategy wins in the latter game with
probability at least
1-\frac{\eta}{q^{-r}\beta}
, so we must have that
\frac{\eta}{q^{-r}\beta}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
.

3.1.4
Parallel Repetition of the Smooth Variable versus Equation Game with Advice

Finally, our outer PCP is the
k
-fold parallel repetition of
G_{\beta,r}
, which we denote by
G^{\otimes k}_{\beta,r}
.
Below is a full description of it:

1.

The verifier chooses equations
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\in{\sf Eq}
uniformly and independently, and lets
U_{i}
be the set of variables in
e_{i}
.

2.

For each
i
independently, with probability
1-\beta
, the verifier chooses

V_{i}=U_{i}
. With probability
\beta
, the verifier chooses

V_{i}\subseteq U_{i}
randomly of size
1
.

3.

For each
i
independently, the verifier picks vectors
v_{1}^{i},\ldots,v_{r}^{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i}}

uniformly and independently. If
U_{i}=V_{i}
the verifier takes
u_{j}^{i}=v_{j}^{i}
for
j=1,\ldots,r
, and
otherwise the verifier takes the vectors
u_{1}^{i},\ldots,u_{r}^{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U_{i}}

where for all
j=1,\ldots,r
, the vector
u_{j}^{i}
agrees with
v_{j}^{i}
on the coordinate of
V_{i}
, and is

0
in the coordinates of
U_{i}\setminus V_{i}
.

4.

The verifier sets
U=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}U_{i}
and
u_{j}=(u_{j}^{1},\ldots,u_{j}^{k})
for each
j=1,\ldots r
, and

V=\cup_{i=1}^{k}V_{i}
and
v_{j}=(v_{j}^{1},\ldots,v_{j}^{k})

for each
j=1,\ldots,r
. The verifier sends
U
and

u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
to the first prover, and
V
and

v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
to the second prover.

5.

The provers respond with assignments to the variables they receive, and the verifier accepts if and only if their
assignments agree on
V
and the assignment to
U
satisfies
the equations
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}
.

Next, we state the completeness and the soundness of the game

G_{\beta,r}^{\otimes k}
, and we begin with its completeness.

Claim 3.1
.

If
(X,{\sf Eq})
has an assignment satisfying at least
1-\varepsilon

of the equations, then the provers can win

G_{\beta,r}^{\otimes k}
with probability at least
1-k\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Let
A
be an assignment that satisfies at least
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of the equations in
E
, and consider the strategy
of the provers that assigns their variables according to
A
.
Note that whenever each one of the equations
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}

the verifier chose is satisfied by
A
, the verifier accepts.
By the union bound, the probability this happens is at least

1-k\varepsilon
.
∎

Next, we establish the soundness of the game
G_{\beta,r}^{\otimes k}
.

Claim 3.2
.

If there is no assignment to
(X,{\sf Eq})
satisfying at least
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction
of the equations, then the provers can win

G_{\beta,r}^{\otimes k}
with probability at most

2^{-\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}q^{-r}\beta k)}
.

Proof.

We appeal to the parallel repetition theorem for projection games
of Rao
[
42
]
, but we have to do so carefully. That theorem
states that if
\Psi
is a
2
-Prover-
1
-Round game with

{\sf val}(\Psi)\leqslant 1-\eta
, then
{\sf val}(\Psi^{\otimes k})\leqslant 2^{-\Omega(\eta^{2}k)}
. We cannot apply the theorem
directly on
G_{\beta,r}
(as the square is too costly for us).
Instead, we consider the game
\Psi=G_{\beta,r}^{\lceil q^{r}/\beta\rceil}
and note that it has value bounded away from
1
.

Write
{\sf val}(\Psi)=1-\eta
.
For each coordinate
i
, the probability that
U_{i}=V_{i}
or
U_{i}\neq V_{i}
but
v_{1}^{i},\ldots,v_{r}^{i}
are all
0
, is equal to
1-\beta q^{-r}
. Thus, by independence the probability that this happens for
\lceil q^{r}/\beta\rceil
coordinates is at most

(1-\beta q^{-r})^{\lceil q^{r}/\beta\rceil}\leqslant e^{-1+o(1)}\leqslant 0.9
.
In particular, with probability
at least
0.1
there exists at least one coordinate
i

in which
U_{i}\neq V_{i}
and all of the advice vectors
v_{1}^{i},\ldots,v_{r}^{i}
and
u_{1}^{i},\ldots,u_{r}^{i}
are all
0
.
Thus, there exists a coordinate
i
and a fixing for the questions of the provers outside
i
so that the answers of
the players to the
i
th coordinate win the standard Variable
versus Equation game with probability at least
1-10\eta
.
It follows that
1-10\eta\leqslant 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
, and so

\eta\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{30}
.

We conclude from Rao’s parallel repetition theorem that

{\sf val}(G_{\beta,r}^{\otimes k})={\sf val}(\Psi^{\otimes\frac{k}{\lceil q^{r}/\beta\rceil}})\leqslant 2^{-\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}q^{-r}\beta k)}.\qed

Viewing the advice as subspaces.

Due to the fact that each variable appears in at most
O(1)

equations, it can easily be seen that with probability
1-O(k^{2}/n)
, all variables in
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}
are distinct.
In that case, note that the
r
vectors of advice
to the second prover,
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
,
are uniform, and the second prover may consider their span
Q_{V}
. Note that, conditioned on (the likely event of) the advice vectors being linearly independent,
Q_{V}
is a uniformly random
r

dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
.
As for the first prover, the vectors
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
are not uniformly distributed. Nevertheless,
as shown by the covering property from
[
31
,
27
]
(and presented below), the distribution of
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
is
close to uniform over
r
-tuple of vectors from
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
.
Thus, the first prover can also take their span, call it

Q_{U}
, and think of it as a random
r
-dimensional subspace
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
(which is highly correlated to
Q_{V}
).

4.1
The Underlying Graph

Our reduction starts from an instance
(X,{\sf Eq})

{\sf Gap3Lin}

from

, which is a slightly different distribution than what we have. Indeed, when sampling
A
as above and then
Q\subseteq A
of dimension
r_{1}
, the subspace
Q
is uniformly random
r_{1}
-dimensional subspace in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
conditioned on
Q\cap H_{U}=\{0\}
. However,
\left(1-q^{r_{1}-2k}\right)
-fraction of
r_{1}
-dimensional subspaces
Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
satisfy
Q\cap H_{U}=\{0\}
, so overall we get that at least
\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}q^{-5\ell^{2}}-q^{r_{1}-2k}\right)\geqslant q^{-6\ell^{2}}
-fraction of all
r_{1}
-dimensional
Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
satisfy the condition of the theorem.
∎

5.2
The Covering Property

In this section, we present the so-called “covering property”, which is a feature of our PCP construction that allows us to move between the first prover’s distribution over
2\ell
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
and the second prover’s distribution over
2\ell
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
. Similar covering properties are shown in
[
31
,
27
]
; however, obtaining the optimal quadratic-programming hardness result in
Theorem

1.4
requires a stronger analysis that goes beyond the covering properties of
[
31
,
27
]
. We are able to obtain a covering property that holds with the following parameters set in the outer PCP:

k=q^{2(1+c)\ell}\quad,\quad\beta=q^{-2(1+2c/3)\ell},

(7)

where
c>0
is a constant arbitrarily small relative to
\delta
.

Remark 5.4
.

We remark that the content
of this section is only necessary if we take
k=q^{(2+c)\ell}
as in (
7
). This setting is used to get a good tradeoff between the size, alphabet size and soundness of the PCP, which is crucial in the proof of
Theorem

1.4
. If one is only interested in the tradeoff between the alphabet size and the soundness, i.e. only wants to prove
Theorem

1.3
, then one can afford to take
k
as a much bigger function of
\ell
. For example, taking
k=q^{100\ell^{2}}
, one could use the covering property from
[
31
,
27
]
instead of the more complicated covering property below, and replace the auxiliary lemma in
Section

5.2.4
by a simple averaging argument.

5.2.1
The Basic Covering Property

We start by stating a basic form of the improved covering property, and defer its proof to
Appendix

C
. Fix a question
U=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{k})
to the first prover and recall that
H_{U}=\spa(v_{e_{1}},\ldots,v_{e_{k}})
where
v_{e_{i}}
is the vector of coefficients of
e_{i}
. For
V\subset U
we view
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
as the subspace consisting of vectors that are zero on all indices in
U\setminus V
. The covering property we show will relate the following two distributions:

\mathcal{D}:

•

Choose
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2\ell}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
uniformly.

•

Output the list
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2\ell})
.

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}:

•

Choose
V\subseteq U
according to the Outer PCP.

•

Choose
x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{2\ell}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
uniformly.

•

Choose
w_{1},\ldots,w_{2\ell}\in H_{U}
uniformly, and set
x_{i}=x^{\prime}_{i}+w_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant 2\ell
.

•

Output the list
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2\ell})
.

The covering property used in
prior works asserts that the distribution
\mathcal{D}

is statistically close to a variant of the distribution

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. This closeness is not good enough for us, however,
as we will want to consider events of rather small probability
under
\mathcal{D}
and still assert that their probability
is roughly the same under
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

Our version of the covering property deviates from those of prior works in two ways. First, in these earlier works
the distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
was generated without the addition of the random vectors
w_{1},\ldots,w_{2\ell}
from
H_{U}
. As explained in the
introduction, this distribution is not good enough
for the purpose of
Theorem

1.4
, and we must consider
the distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
above instead. Second, the notion
of statistical closeness is too strict for us, and is in fact not achievable. To get around this hurdle, we instead show a “contiguity-type” statement, asserting that almost all inputs
x
are assigned the same
probability under these two distributions up to factor
1+o(1)
.

More precisely, setting
\eta=q^{-100\ell^{100}}
throughout this subsection, we show:

Lemma 5.5
.

There exists
E\subseteq\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}\right)^{2\ell}
such that both
\mathcal{D}(E)
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(E)
are at most
\eta^{40}
, and for all
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2\ell})\notin E
we have

0.9\leqslant\frac{\mathcal{D}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2\ell})}{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2\ell})}\leqslant 1.1.

Proof.

The proof is deferred to
Appendix

C
.
∎

5.2.2
The Covering Property with Zoom-ins

Lemma

5.5
represents the basic form
of the covering property, but for our application we require
a version which incorporates zoom-ins and advice.
Namely, we will actually be interested in the case where
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
are conditioned on some
r_{1}
-dimensional zoom-in
Q
, for an arbitrary dimension
r_{1}\leqslant\frac{10}{\delta}
. To make notation simpler, let us write
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2\ell})
and use
\spa_{r_{1}}(x)
to denote
\spa(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r_{1}})
. Additionally, for any set
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}\right)^{2\ell}
, define

\mathcal{D}_{Q}(\mathcal{L}):=\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q]=\frac{\mathcal{D}(\{x\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q\})}{\mathcal{D}(\{x\;|\;\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q\})}.

Also define
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}
similarly as

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(\mathcal{L}):=\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}[x\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q]=\frac{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\{x\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q\})}{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\{x\;|\;\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q\})}.

From
Lemma

5.5
we can conclude that for
any
\mathcal{L}\subseteq(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U})^{2\ell}
that is not too small, the measure
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})
is within at least a constant factor of
\mathcal{D}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})
for nearly all
Q
:

Lemma 5.6
.

For any
\mathcal{L}\subseteq(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U})^{2\ell}
, we have

\Pr_{Q}\left[\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})\geqslant 0.8\cdot\mathcal{D}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})-\eta^{20}\right]\geqslant 1-3\eta^{20},

where
Q
is the span of
r_{1}
uniformly random vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof all of the expectations and probabilities over
Q
choose
Q
as in the lemma statement. Let
E
be the small set of tuples from
Lemma

5.5
, so that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},Q=\spa_{r_{1}}(x)}[\mathcal{D}_{Q}(E)]\leqslant\eta^{40}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime},Q=\spa_{r_{1}}(x)}[\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(E)]\leqslant\eta^{40}.

(8)

Let
{\sf Bad}_{1}=\{x\in\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}\right)^{2\ell}\;|\;\mathcal{D}_{Q}(E)\geqslant\eta^{20},Q:=\spa_{r_{1}}(x)\}
and
{\sf Bad}_{2}=\{x\in\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}\right)^{2\ell}\;|\;\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(E)\geqslant\eta^{20},Q:=\spa_{r_{1}}(x)\}
. By the union bound we have

\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x\in{\sf Bad}_{1}\cup{\sf Bad}_{2}]\leqslant\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x\in{\sf Bad}_{1}]+\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x\in{\sf Bad}_{2}].

By the first expectation (
8
) and Markov’s inequality, we have
\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x\in{\sf Bad}_{1}]\leqslant\eta^{20}
. To bound the second term, we have

\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[x\in{\sf Bad}_{2}]=\sum_{x\in{\sf Bad}_{2}}\mathcal{D}(x)\leqslant\sum_{x\in{\sf Bad}_{2}\setminus E}\mathcal{D}(x)+\sum_{x\in E}\mathcal{D}(x)\leqslant\sum_{x\in{\sf Bad}_{2}\setminus E}1.1\cdot\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)+\eta^{40}\leqslant 1.1\cdot\eta^{20}+\eta^{40}.

In the penultimate transition we are using
Lemma

5.5
to bound
\mathcal{D}(x)\leqslant 1.1\cdot\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)
if
x\notin E
as well as to bound
\mathcal{D}(E)\leqslant\eta^{40}
. In the final transition we are using the fact
\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}[x\in{\sf Bad}_{2}]\leqslant\eta^{20}
, which follows by an application of Markov’s inequality on the second expectation in (
8
).

Altogether, we get that with probability at least
1-3\eta^{20}
, we have
x\notin{\sf Bad}_{1}\cup{\sf Bad}_{2}
, and
Q:=\spa_{r_{1}}(x)
satisfies
\mathcal{D}_{Q}(E),\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(E)\leqslant\eta^{20}
. In this case we have,

\displaystyle\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q}\mathcal{D}(x)\geqslant\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q,x\notin E}\mathcal{D}(x)

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q,x\notin E}0.9\cdot\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=0.9\cdot\left(\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)-\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q,x\in E}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)\right),

where we used
Lemma

5.5
in the
second transition. Dividing both sides by
\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)
gives that

\frac{\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q}\mathcal{D}(x)}{\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)}\geqslant 0.9(1-\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(E))\geqslant 0.9(1-\eta^{20})\geqslant 0.89.

(9)

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L},\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)}{\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{0.89\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}\cap\overline{E},\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)}{\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q}\mathcal{D}(x)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{0.9\cdot 0.89\cdot\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}\cap\overline{E},\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q}\mathcal{D}(x)}{\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q}\mathcal{D}(x)}

\displaystyle\geqslant 0.8\cdot\mathcal{D}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})-\frac{\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q,x\in E}\mathcal{D}(x)}{\sum_{\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q}\mathcal{D}(x)}

\displaystyle=0.8\cdot\mathcal{D}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})-\mathcal{D}_{Q}(E)

\displaystyle\geqslant 0.8\cdot\mathcal{D}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})-\eta^{20},

where we used (
9
) in the second transition and
\mathcal{D}_{Q}(E)\leqslant\eta^{20}
in the last transition.
∎

5.2.3
The Covering Property for the Advice

We will also need a lemma that applies to
r_{1}
-dimensional subspaces for some constant
r_{1}=O(\delta^{-1})
. This is to handle the fact that the zoom-in
Q
is sampled uniformly from
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
after
V
is chosen according to the outer PCP, and then lifted to a subspace over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
, instead of uniformly from
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
. For a fixed question
U
to the first prover, let
\mathcal{D}_{r_{1}}
denote the uniform measure over
\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}\right)^{r_{1}}
and let
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{r_{1}}
denote the following distribution over
\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}\right)^{r_{1}}
:

•

Choose
V\subseteq U
according to the Outer PCP.

•

Choose
x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{r_{1}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
uniformly.

•

Choose
w_{1},\ldots,w_{r_{1}}\in H_{U}
uniformly, and set
x_{i}=x^{\prime}_{i}+w_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r_{1}
.

•

Output the list
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r_{1}})
.

The distributions
\mathcal{D}_{r_{1}}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{r_{1}}
have the following property.

Lemma 5.7
.

For
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}\right)^{r_{1}}
such that
\mathcal{D}_{r_{1}}(\mathcal{Q})\geqslant q^{-10\ell^{10}}
, we have

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{r_{1}}(\mathcal{Q})\geqslant 0.8\cdot\mathcal{D}_{r_{1}}(\mathcal{Q}).

Proof.

Take
E
from
Lemma

5.5
and define

\mathcal{L}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2\ell})\in\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}\right)^{2\ell}\;|\;(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r_{1}})\in\mathcal{Q}\}.

Then
\mathcal{D}_{r_{1}}(\mathcal{Q})=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L})
,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{r_{1}}(\mathcal{Q})=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathcal{L})
, and

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{r_{1}}(\mathcal{Q})=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathcal{L})\geqslant\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}\setminus E}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)\geqslant 0.9\cdot\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}\setminus E}\mathcal{D}(x)\geqslant 0.9\cdot\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L})-\mathcal{D}(E)\geqslant 0.8\cdot\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L})=0.8\cdot\mathcal{D}_{r_{1}}(\mathcal{L}),

where we used
Lemma

5.5
in the second inequality, and the fact that
\mathcal{D}(E)\leqslant\eta^{40}
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{D}_{r_{1}}(\mathcal{Q})\geqslant q^{-10\ell^{10}}
in the penultimate transition.
∎

5.2.4
An Auxiliary Lemma

We conclude this section with an auxiliary lemma that will be used in the analysis.
Throughout this subsection, we fix a question
U=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{k})
to the first prover, where
U_{i}
denotes the variables from the
i
th equation, say
e_{i}
, and a subspace
Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
of dimension
r
, and we consider the distribution over the second prover’s question,
V

r
advice vectors having span

\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
over
(V,Q^{\prime})
defined as follows:

•

For each
i\in[k]

.

•

Choose
r

•

Output
(V,\spa(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}))

Then the distribution we are interested in is the marginal distribution
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,Q)
. After
V
is chosen, we let
V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}
be the variables in
V
from equations
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}
respectively. We start with a helpful intermediate result, showing that in the sampling procedure, for any
Q
and

, the probability
V_{i}\neq U_{i}
is roughly at most
\beta
even when conditioned on
V_{j}
already being chosen for

(where
\mathcal{I}
can be an arbitrary subset of indices). For a vector
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
, we use

to denote its restriction to the variables in
U_{i}
, and think of
v^{i}
as being in the
3

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U_{i}}
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Fix a question
U=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{k})
, a dimension
r
subspace
Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
, a parameter
0<\beta<q^{-2\ell}
, and let
\mathcal{A}
be defined accordingly as above using
U
and
\beta
. Let
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[k]
be an arbitrary set of indices and suppose for
j\in\mathcal{I}
,
V_{j}\subseteq U_{j}
has already been chosen. Then, for any
i\in[k]\setminus\mathcal{I}

\Pr_{V\sim\mathcal{A}(\cdot,Q)}[V_{i}\neq U_{i}\;|\;\{V_{j}\}_{\forall j\in\mathcal{I}}]:=\Pr_{V_{i}\subseteq U_{i}\;Q^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}}[V_{i}\neq U_{i}\;|\;Q^{\prime}=Q,\{V_{j}\}_{\forall j\in\mathcal{I}}]\leqslant\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)q^{-3r}}.

In words, the above probability is the probability that variables are dropped in equation
i
, when sampling
V\subseteq U
, conditioned on the span of the first
r
advice vectors equaling
Q
, and conditioned on
V_{j}
being fixed for
j\in\mathcal{I}
.
8
8

8

We implicitly assume that the probability space we consider is nonempty, so that there exist
V_{s}\subseteq U_{s}
for
s\notin\mathcal{I}
such that
Q^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
after setting
V=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}V_{i}
, with
V_{j}
fixed as above for
j\in\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

View each vector in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
as indexed by the variables appearing in
U
. It will be helpful to briefly recall how
V\subseteq U
and the advice subspace
Q
are chosen according to the conditional distribution we consider. First, for
j\in\mathcal{I}
,
V_{j}\subseteq U_{j}
is already fixed. For each
j\in[k]\setminus\mathcal{I}
independently we set
U_{j}=V_{j}
with probability
1-\beta
, or with probability
\beta
, we set
V_{j}
to be a uniformly random subset of
U_{j}
of size
1
. After choosing
V_{j}
, we choose
r
advice vectors
v^{j}_{1},\ldots,v^{j}_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i}}
uniformly at random. We then set
V=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}V_{j}
, for each
t\in[r]
set the advice vector
v_{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
to be the concatenation of
v^{1}_{t},\ldots,v^{k}_{t}
, and set
Q^{\prime}=\spa(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})
.

It is clear that there is some distribution over bases
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
of
Q
such that

\Pr_{V_{j}\subseteq U_{j},\forall j\notin\mathcal{I},\;Q^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}}\left[V_{i}\neq U_{i}\;|\;Q^{\prime}=Q\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}}\left[\Pr_{V_{j}\subseteq U_{j},\forall j\notin\mathcal{I},\;Q^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}}\left[V_{i}\neq U_{i}\;|\;v_{t}=a_{t}~\forall t\in[r]\right]\right],

where the expectation on the right is over this distribution of bases
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
of
Q
. We do not need to know what this distribution is. We only use the fact that, by its existence, there is some basis
a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}
which maximizes the inner probability on the right, and fixing this basis
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
of

\displaystyle\Pr_{V_{j}\subseteq U_{j},\forall j\notin\mathcal{I},\;Q^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}}\left[V_{i}\neq U_{i}\;|\;Q^{\prime}=Q\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Pr_{V_{j}\subseteq U_{j},\forall j\notin\mathcal{I},\;Q^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}}\left[V_{i}\neq U_{i}\;|\;v_{t}=a_{t}~\forall t\in[r]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr\limits_{V_{j}\subseteq U_{j},\forall j\notin\mathcal{I},\;v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}}\left[V_{i}\neq U_{i}\land(v_{t}=a_{t}~\forall t\in[r])\right]}{\Pr\limits_{V_{j}\subseteq U_{j},\forall j\notin\mathcal{I},\;v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}}[v_{t}=a_{t}~\forall t\in[r]]}.

is chosen independently, the above probability becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\Pr\limits_{V_{i}\subseteq U_{i},v^{i}_{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i}}}\left[V_{i}\neq U_{i}\land(v^{i}_{t}=a^{i}_{t}~\forall t\in[r])\right]\prod\limits_{j\in[k]\setminus\{i\}}\Pr\limits_{V_{j}\subseteq U_{j},v^{j}_{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{j}}}\left[v^{j}_{t}=a^{j}_{t}~\forall t\in[r]\right]}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}\Pr\limits_{V_{j}\subseteq U_{j},v^{j}_{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{j}}}\left[v^{j}_{t}=a^{j}_{t}~\forall t\in[r]\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr\limits_{V_{i}\subseteq U_{i},v^{i}_{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i}}}\left[V_{i}\neq U_{i}\land(v^{i}_{t}=a^{i}_{t}~\forall t\in[r])\right]}{\Pr\limits_{V_{i}\subseteq U_{i},v^{i}_{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i}}}\left[v^{i}_{t}=a^{i}_{t}\;\forall t\in[r]\right]}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\beta}{\Pr\limits_{V_{i}\subseteq U_{i},v^{i}_{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i}}}\left[v^{i}_{t}=a^{i}_{t}\;\forall t\in[r]\right]}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)q^{-3r}}.

In the third transition, we upper bound the numerator by

, and in the last transition we lower bound the denominator by

, which is the probability that

and

for all

.
∎

In the soundness analysis we will find some zoom-in and zoom-out pair
(Q,W)
for the larger prover, and we will be interested in projecting it into a question
V
of the smaller prover. In particular, we will be interested in the distribution of the codimension
W\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
. The following consequence of
Lemma

5.8
upper bounds the probability its codimension is smaller by
j
than the codimension of
W
.

Lemma 5.9
.

For integer
r,s\geqslant 0
, the following holds for sufficiently large
k
. Let
U
be a fixed question to the first prover in the outer PCP consisting of
3k
distinct variables in some set of
k
equations, let
Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
be a subspace of dimension
r
, and let
W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
be a codimension
s
subspace that contains both
H_{U}
and
Q
. Let
V\subseteq U
be a random question to the second prover chosen according to the outer PCP, conditioned on
Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
, and let
W[V]=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}\cap W
. Then, for each
0\leqslant j\leqslant s
, we have

\Pr_{V\sim\mathcal{A}(\cdot,Q)}[\codim(W[V])=s-j]\leqslant(2s\beta q^{3r})^{j},

where the codimension is with respect to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
.

Proof.

Let us label the variables in
U
as
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{3k})
so that the entries of vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
are indexed by variables in
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{3k})

U
are labeled as
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}

V

\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
and then choose which variables to drop from it according to the sampling procedure of the outer PCP. Specifically, at step
i
, let
e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{i-1}\in\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
be the equations that have been processed already. We choose
e^{\prime}_{i}\in\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}\setminus\{e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{i-1}\}
. Then, with probability
1-\beta
we do not drop any variables from
e^{\prime}_{i}
, and with probability
\beta
we drop a uniformly random two out of the three variables from
e^{\prime}_{i}
. Recall that when a variable is dropped when obtaining
V
, every vector in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
has coordinate zero at the index of the dropped variable.

Let
V_{0}=U
and let
V_{i}
be the state of
V
after step
i
in this process, i.e. after we have processed equations
e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{i}\in\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
and chosen which variables to drop from these equations. Also define
W[V_{i}]:=W\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i}}
and say that
\codim(W[V_{i}])
is the codimension of
W[V_{i}]
with respect to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i}}
. We will first show that it is possible to adaptively choose the first
k-s
equations so that the codimension does not drop, i.e.
\codim(W[V_{0}])=\cdots=\codim(W[V_{k-s}])
. For each one of the remaining
s
steps we will (roughly) show that the codimension drops by
1
with probability at most
\beta
, and does not change with probability
1-\beta
.

At step
i
, call an equation
e
saturated if the restriction of
W[V_{i-1}]
to the variables in
e
has dimension
3
. If we choose
e^{\prime}_{i}=e
, it is straightforward to check that
\codim(W[V_{i}])=\codim(W[V_{i-1}])
with probability
1
. We argue inductively that for the first
k-s
steps, we can always adaptively choose a saturated equation, and thus have
\codim(W[V_{0}])=\cdots=\codim(W[V_{k-s}])
.

Fix an arbitrary
i
such that
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-s
and suppose
\codim(W[V_{i-1}])=s
. We claim that there can be at most
s
unsaturated equations in
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}\setminus\{e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{i-1}\}
. To see this, note that for each equation
e\in\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}\setminus\{e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{i-1}\}
that is unsaturated there is a corresponding vector in the dual space of
W[V_{i-1}]\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i-1}}
supported only on the indices corresponding to variables in
e
. Thus, for any set of distinct unsaturated equations, these vectors in the dual space are linearly independent. Since the dual space of
W[V_{i-1}]
has dimension at most
s
, there are thus at most
s
unsaturated equations. Therefore, if
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-s
, then there is always at least one saturated equation in
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}\setminus\{e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{i-1}\}
, and we can adaptively choose
e^{\prime}_{i}
to be this equation.

We now show that for the unsaturated equations, the codimension can drop by
1
with probability at most
\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)q^{-3r}}
and otherwise the codimension does not change. Let
e_{i}
be an equation that is unsaturated. By
Lemma

5.8
, with probability at least
1-\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)q^{-3r}}
no variables in
e_{i}
are dropped and in this case we have
V_{i}=V_{i+1}
,
W[V_{i}]=W[V_{i+1}]
, and hence
\codim(W[V_{i}])=\codim(W[V_{i+1}])
.

With the remaining at most
\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)q^{-3r}}
probability, two variables are dropped, and in this case we show that the codimension decreases by at most
1
.
Suppose
a_{1},a_{2}
are the characteristic vectors for the variables that are dropped. Recall that
H_{U}\subseteq W
at the start, and let
w_{i}
be the coefficient vector of the
i
th equation so that
w_{i}\in H_{U}
. Then,
w_{i}\in W[V_{i}]
, so it must be the case that
a_{1}
is not yet in the dual space of
W[V_{i}]
, and so, when viewing both
W[V_{i+1}]
and
W[V_{i}]
as subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i}}
, we get that
\dim(W[V_{i}])-\dim(W[V_{i+1}])\geqslant 1
. Combining this with the fact that
\dim\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i}}\right)-\dim\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i+1}}\right)=2
, where again both spaces are viewed as subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i}}
it follows that the codimension of
W[V_{i+1}]
relative to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i+1}}
is at most one more than that of
W[V_{i}]
relative to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V_{i}}
.

Altogether, this shows that for
k-s
of the steps
i
, the codimension does not change, while for the remaining
s
steps, the codimension drops by
1
with probability at most
\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)q^{-3r}}
and remains the same otherwise. As a result,

\Pr_{V\sim\mathcal{A}(\cdot,Q)}[\codim(W[V])=s-j]\leqslant\binom{s}{j}\left(\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)q^{-3r}}\right)^{j}\leqslant(2s\beta q^{3r})^{j},

where the second inequality is by union bounding over all possible combinations of
j
out of
s
steps where the codimension may drop by
1
.
∎

We finish this section by stating a corollary of
Lemma

5.9
which will be used in the soundness analysis. The setting one should have in mind is that we
have a zoom-in and zoom-out pair
(Q,W)
and an event

\mathcal{L}\subseteq(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U})^{2\ell}
inside it with noticeable probability, where
U
is a question to the first prover. The lemma asserts that
over the choice of the question
V
to the smaller prover, the projection of the event
\mathcal{L}
to
the zoom-in and zoom-out pair
(Q,W[V])
still has
noticeable probability:

Lemma 5.10
.

The following holds for any integers
r,s>0
,
\ell
sufficiently large relative to
r
and
s
,
k>\ell
,
0<\beta<q^{-2\ell}
, and
C>2sq^{-2\ell}
. Let
U
be a fixed question to the first prover in the outer PCP consisting of
3k
variables in some set of
k

Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
be a zoom-in of dimension
r
, let
W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
be a zoom-out of codimension
s
containing
H_{U}
and
Q
.

Also let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}\right)^{2\ell}
be such that
\spa(x)\subseteq W
for every
x\in\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})\geqslant q^{-s(2\ell-r)}\cdot C
. With probability at least
\beta^{s+2}
over
V
(chosen according to the marginal of
\mathcal{A}
defined above, with parameter
\beta
), we have

\Pr_{x^{\prime}_{i}\in W[V],w_{i}\in H_{U}}\left[x^{\prime}+w\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;\spa_{r}(x^{\prime}+w)=Q\right]\geqslant\frac{C}{4(s+1)2^{s}q^{3rs}}.

In the probability above, the sampling procedure is that of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}
with
V
fixed and we use
x^{\prime}
and
w
to denote
x^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{2\ell}),w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{2\ell})
respectively.

Proof.

For each
0\leqslant j\leqslant s
, let
E_{j}
be the set of
V\subseteq U
such that
\codim(W[V])=s-j
, and let
p_{j}:=\Pr_{V\sim\mathcal{A}(\cdot,Q)}[V\in E_{j}]
, where
V
is chosen according to the outer PCP.
Then
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})\geqslant q^{-s(2\ell-r)}\cdot C
implies that

\sum_{j=0}^{s}p_{j}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{V\sim E_{j}}\left[\Pr_{x^{\prime}_{i}\in W[V],w_{i}\in H_{U}}\left[x^{\prime}+w\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;\spa_{r}(x^{\prime}+w)=Q\right]\right]\geqslant q^{-s(2\ell-r)}\cdot C.

(10)

In the above expectation
V\sim E_{j}
means choosing
V
according to the distribution
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,Q)
conditioned on
V\in E_{j}
. For each
j
and
V\in E_{j}
, we can bound the inner term using the fact that if
x\in\mathcal{L}
, then
\spa(x^{\prime}+w)\subseteq W
, so as a result of
W\supseteq H_{U}
, we must have
\spa(x^{\prime})\subseteq W[V]
. Thus,

\displaystyle\Pr_{x^{\prime}_{i}\in W[V],w_{i}\in H_{U}}\left[x^{\prime}+w\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;\spa_{r}(x^{\prime}+w)=Q\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Pr_{x^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V},w_{i}\in H_{U}}[x^{\prime}_{r+1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{2\ell}\in W[V]]

\displaystyle\leqslant q^{-(s-j)(2\ell-r)},

where in the second inequality we use the fact that
\codim(W[V])=s-j
. Call
j
rare if
p_{j}\leqslant\beta^{j+1}
. The contribution to the sum above from rare

is at most

\sum_{j\text{ rare}}\beta^{j+1}\cdot q^{-(s-j)(2\ell-r)}\leqslant s\cdot\beta\cdot q^{-s(2\ell-r)}\leqslant q^{-s(2\ell-r)}\frac{C}{2},

where we used
\beta\leqslant q^{-2\ell}\leqslant\frac{C}{2s}
.
Removing this contribution from (
10
) it follows that there exists

that is not rare satisfying

p_{j}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{V\in E_{j}}\left[\Pr_{x^{\prime}_{i}\in W[V],w_{i}\in H_{U}}\left[x^{\prime}+w\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;\spa_{r}(x^{\prime}+w)=Q\right]\right]\geqslant q^{-s(2\ell-r)}\cdot\frac{C}{2(s+1)}.

(11)

Fix this

henceforth. Using again the fact that if
x^{\prime}+w\in\mathcal{L}
, then
\spa(x^{\prime}+w)\subseteq W
and
\spa(x^{\prime})\subseteq W[V]
, we get

\displaystyle p_{j}\cdot q^{-(s-j)(2\ell-r)}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{V\in E_{j}}\left[\Pr_{x^{\prime}_{i}\in W[V],w_{i}\in H_{U}}\left[x^{\prime}+w\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;\spa_{r}(x^{\prime}+w)=Q\right]\right]

\displaystyle=p_{j}\cdot q^{-(s-j)(2\ell-r)}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{V\in E_{j}}\left[\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}{x^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V},w_{i}\in H_{U},}\\
{x_{i}=x^{\prime}_{i}+w_{i},i\in[2\ell]}\end{subarray}}[x^{\prime}+w\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;\spa_{r}(x)=Q,\;\spa(x^{\prime})\subseteq W[V]]\right]

\displaystyle=p_{j}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{V\in E_{j}}\left[\Pr_{x^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V},w_{i}\in H_{U}}[x^{\prime}+w\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;\spa_{r}(x^{\prime}+w)=Q]\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant q^{-s(2\ell-r)}\cdot\frac{C}{2(s+1)},

where the last transition uses (
11
). Dividing the last inequality by

we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{V\in E_{j}}\left[\Pr_{x^{\prime}_{i}\in W[V],w_{i}\in H_{U}}[x^{\prime}+w\in\mathcal{L}\;|\;\spa_{r}(x^{\prime}+w)=Q]\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{q^{-j(2\ell-r)}}{p_{j}}\cdot\frac{C}{2(s+1)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{q^{-j(2\ell-r)}}{(2s\beta q^{3r})^{j}}\cdot\frac{C}{2(s+1)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{C}{2(s+1)2^{s}q^{3rs}}.

where in the second inequality we apply the upper bound on

from
Lemma

5.9
, and in the last transition we use
\beta\leqslant q^{-2\ell}
. Now let

C^{\prime}:=\frac{C}{2(s+1)2^{s}q^{3rs}}.

By an averaging argument, conditioned on
V\in E_{j}
with probability at least
\frac{C^{\prime}}{2}
the probability in the expectation above is at least
\frac{C^{\prime}}{2}
.
Overall, as
j
is not rare, we get that the probability that the expectation above is at least
\frac{C^{\prime}}{2}
over the choice of
V
is at least
p_{j}\frac{C^{\prime}}{2}\geqslant\beta^{s+2}
.
∎

5.3
The Number of Maximal Zoom-Outs is Bounded

Theorem

5.2
suggests that the two provers can agree on a zoom-in with reasonable probability using their advice. The same cannot be said for zoom-outs however, and to circumvent this issue we must
develop further tools. In this section, we define the notion of
maximal
zoom-outs and show that for a fixed zoom-in
Q
, the number of maximal zoom-outs is bounded.

5.3.1
Generic Sets of Subspaces

To deal with large collections of zoom-outs
we define a special property of zoom-outs that is called “genericness”. To motivate it, let
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
be an ambient space and suppose
W_{1},W_{2}\subseteq W_{\mathsf{amb}}

are distinct subspaces of codimension
r
. Then
W_{1}\cap W_{2}
is a subspace
whose codimension is between
2r
and
r+1
. For a typical pair
of subspaces, the intersection
W_{1}\cap W_{2}
has codimension
2r
though,
and in this case we say the two subspaces are generic. Genericness is useful probabilistically because if
W_{1},W_{2}
are generic, then the event that a randomly chosen
2\ell
-dimensional subspace is contained in
W_{1}
, and the event the subspace is contained in
W_{2}
, are almost independent. Below is a formal definition which also applies to a set of subspaces rather than just two:

Definition 5.11
.

We say that a set
\mathcal{S}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{N}\}
of codimension
r
subspaces of
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
is
t
-generic with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
if for any
t
distinct subspaces, say
W_{i_{1}},\ldots,W_{i_{t}}\in\mathcal{S}
, we have
\codim(\bigcap_{1\leqslant j\leqslant t}W_{i_{j}})=t\cdot r
. When the ambient space
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
is clear from context we simply say that
\mathcal{S}
is
t
-generic.

Throughout this section, it will be convenient to denote by
\codim_{V}(W)
the codimension of
W\cap W_{\mathsf{amb}}
with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
. When no subscript is used, the identity of the subspace
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
should be clear from context.

We remark that any set of subspaces that is
t
-generic with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
is also
t^{\prime}
-generic with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
for any
t^{\prime}<t
. In this section we show a sunflower-type lemma, stating that any large set of codimension
r
subspaces inside
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
contains a large set of subspaces that are
t
-generic with respect to
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
for some
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}\subseteq W_{\mathsf{amb}}
.
Below is a formal statement.

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
t,r\in\mathbb{N}
be integers and let
\mathcal{S}
be a set of
N
subspaces each with codimension
r
inside of
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
. Then there exists a subspace
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}\subseteq W_{\mathsf{amb}}
and a set of subspaces
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
such that:

•

\left|\mathcal{S^{\prime}}\right|\geqslant\frac{N^{\frac{1}{(r+1)\cdot(t-1)!}}}{q^{3r}}
.

•

Each
W_{i}\in\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
is contained in
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
and has codimension
s
with respect to
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
, where
s\leqslant r
.

•

\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
is
t
-generic with respect to
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
.

To show
Lemma

5.12
we need two auxiliary lemmas. The first,
Lemma

5.13
, states that for
j\geqslant 2
, any
j
-generic set of subspaces contains a large
(j+1)
-generic set of subspaces.
The second,
Lemma

5.14
, states that a given collection of subspaces is either already
2
-generic, or else many subspaces in the collection are contained in a common hyperplane.

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{N}\}
be a set of
N
-subspaces of codimension
r
inside of
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
that is
j
-generic with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
for
j\geqslant 2
, then there is a subset
\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{N^{\prime}}\}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
of size

N^{\prime}\geqslant\frac{N^{1/j}}{q^{r}}

that is
(j+1)
-generic with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
.

Proof.

Fix any
j
distinct subspaces in
\mathcal{S}
, say
W_{1},\ldots,W_{j}
and let
W=W_{1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{j}
. Since
\mathcal{S}
is
j
-generic,
\codim(W)=j\cdot r
. We claim that there are at most
q^{j\cdot r}
subspaces
W_{i^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{j}\}
such that
\codim(W\cap W_{i^{\prime}})\leqslant(j+1)r-1
. Call such subspaces bad, and suppose for the sake of contradiction that there are more than
q^{jr}
bad subspaces
W_{i^{\prime}}
. Then for each bad
W_{i^{\prime}}
we have,

\displaystyle\dim(W_{i^{\prime}}+W)

\displaystyle=\dim(W_{i^{\prime}})+\dim(W)-\dim(W_{i^{\prime}}\cap W)

\displaystyle\leqslant(\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})-r)+(\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})-jr)-(\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})-(j+1)r+1)

\displaystyle=\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})-1.

Therefore, for each
W_{i^{\prime}}
, the space
W+W_{i^{\prime}}
is contained in a hyperplane
H
such that
H\supseteq W
. There are at most
q^{\codim(W)}-1=q^{j\cdot r}-1
hyperplanes
H
containing
W
, and by the pigeonhole principle it follows that there are two bad subspaces say
W_{i^{\prime}_{1}},W_{i^{\prime}_{2}}
that are both contained in the same hyperplane
H
. This is a contradiction however, as by the
j
-genericness of
\mathcal{S}
, we must have

\displaystyle\dim(W_{i^{\prime}_{1}}+W_{i^{\prime}_{2}})

\displaystyle=\dim(W_{i^{\prime}_{1}})+\dim(W_{i^{\prime}_{2}})-\dim(W_{i^{\prime}_{1}}\cap W_{i^{\prime}_{2}})

\displaystyle=2(\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})-r)-(\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})-2r)

\displaystyle=\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}}),

and hence
W_{i^{\prime}_{1}}
and
W_{i^{\prime}_{2}}
cannot both be contained in the hyperplane
H
.

The lemma now follows from the claim we have just shown. Construct a subset
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
greedily as follows:

1.

Initialize
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
by picking
j
arbitrary
subspaces from
\mathcal{S}
and inserting them to
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
.

2.

For any
j
subspaces in
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
, say

W_{1},\ldots,W_{j}
, remove any
W^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
which
is bad for them.

3.

If
\mathcal{S}
is not empty, pick some
W\in\mathcal{S}
, insert it to
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
and iterate.

Note that trivially, the collection
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
will be

(j+1)
-generic in the end of the process. To lower bound the
size of
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
, note that when
|\mathcal{S^{\prime}}|=s
, the number
of elements from
\mathcal{S}
that have been deleted is
at most
s^{j}q^{jr}
, and hence so long as this value is less than

N
, we may do another iteration. Thus, we must have that

s\geqslant\left(\frac{N}{q^{jr}}\right)^{1/j}=\frac{N^{1/j}}{q^{r}}

when the process terminates.
∎

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{N}\}
be a set of subspaces of
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
of codimension
r
. Then for any integer
m\geqslant 1
, at least one of the following holds.

•

There are
m
subspaces, say
W_{1},\ldots,W_{m}
that are
2
-generic with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
.

•

There is a subspace
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}\subseteq W_{\mathsf{amb}}
of codimension
1
that contains
N^{\prime}\geqslant\frac{N}{mq^{r}}
of these subspaces, say
W_{1},\ldots,W_{N^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Note that for any
1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant N
, we have
\codim(W_{i}\cap W_{j})\leqslant 2r
. Consider the graph with vertices
W_{1},\ldots,W_{N}
and
W_{i}
,
W_{j}
are adjacent if and only if
i\neq j
and
\codim(W_{i}\cap W_{j})\leqslant 2r-1
. If every vertex in this graph has degree at most
\lfloor\frac{N}{m}\rfloor
, then we are done as there is an independent set of size
m
and these subspaces satisfy the first condition. Suppose this is not the case. Then there is a vertex, say
W_{N}
, that has at least
\lceil\frac{N}{m}\rceil
neighbors, say
W_{1},\ldots,W_{\lceil\frac{N}{m}\rceil}
. For
1\leqslant i\leqslant\lceil\frac{N}{m}\rceil
, we have
\codim(W_{N}\cap W_{i})\leqslant 2r-1
, so

\dim(W_{i}+W_{N})=\dim(W_{i})+\dim(W_{N})-\dim(W_{i}\cap W_{N})\leqslant\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})-1.

Thus
W_{i}+W_{N}
is always contained in a codimension
1
subspace of
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
that contains
W_{N}
. Since the number of such subspaces is
q^{r}-1
, one of these codimension
1
subspaces of
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
, say
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
, contains at least

\frac{\lceil N/m\rceil}{q^{r}-1}\geqslant\frac{N}{mq^{r}}

of the subspaces in the list
W_{1},\ldots,W_{\lceil\frac{N}{m}\rceil}
.
∎

Repeatedly applying
Lemma

5.14
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 5.15
.

Let
\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{N}\}
be a set of subspaces of
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
of codimension
r
with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
. Then, there exists a subspace
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}\subseteq W_{\mathsf{amb}}
, an integer
1\leqslant s\leqslant r
, and a subset of
m\geqslant\frac{N^{\frac{1}{r+1}}}{q^{r}}
subspaces from this set, say
\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{m}\}
, which are all contained in
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
and satisfy the following two conditions.

•

Each
W_{i}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
, has codimension
s
with respect to
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
.

•

\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{m}\}
is
2
-generic with respect to
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
.

Proof.

To start set
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}=W_{\mathsf{amb}}
. If the
W_{i}
’s have codimension
1
in
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
then the result holds.

Otherwise, if the conclusion does not hold, then apply
Lemma

5.14
with
m=\frac{N^{\frac{1}{r+1}}}{q^{r}}
. Either the first condition of
Lemma

5.14
holds and we are done, or we can find a new subspace,
W^{\prime\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
, of codimension
1
inside the current
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
containing at least
\frac{N}{mq^{r}}
of the
W_{i}
’s. Set
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}=W^{\prime\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
and repeat. Note that the codimension of the
W_{i}
’s with respect to
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
drops by
1
after every iteration, so we will repeat at most
r
times before reaching the desired conclusion. This yields a list of
W_{i}
’s that satisfy the conditions of size at least

\frac{N}{(mq^{r})^{r}}=N^{\frac{1}{r+1}}\geqslant m.\qed

Combining
Corollary

5.15
and
Lemma

5.13
, we get
Lemma

5.12
as follows:

Proof of
Lemma

5.12
.

By
Corollary

5.15
, there is a collection
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of size
|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|\geqslant\frac{N^{\frac{1}{r+1}}}{q^{r}}
and

such that
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
is
2
-generic with respect to
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
, and each
W_{i}
has (the same) codimension

with respect to
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
. Applying
Lemma

5.13

t-2
times, there is a set of
t
-generic subspaces relative to
W^{\prime}_{\mathsf{amb}}
,
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
, of size

\left|\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}\right|\geqslant\left(\left(\left(|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{r}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\cdots\right)\cdot\frac{1}{q^{r}}\right)^{\frac{1}{t-1}}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{r}}\geqslant\frac{\left|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\right|^{\frac{1}{(t-1)!}}}{q^{2r}}\ \geqslant\frac{N^{\frac{1}{(r+1)\cdot(t-1)!}}}{q^{3r}}.\qed

In addition to
Lemma

5.12
, we state another useful feature of generic sets of subspaces, formalized in
Lemma

5.16
below. The lemma asserts that if a collection
\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{N}\}
is generic, and one zooms-outs from the ambient space
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
into a hyperplane
H
, then
one gets an induced collection
\{W_{1}\cap H,\ldots,W_{N}\cap H\}

which is almost as generic.

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{N}\}
be a collection of subspaces of codimension
r
that is
t
-generic with respect to some space
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
for an even integer
t
, and let
H
be a hyperplane in
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
. Then the collection of subspaces

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\{W_{1}\cap H,\ldots,W_{N}\cap H\}
can be made a
\frac{t}{2}
-generic set of subspaces with respect to
H
with codimension
r
inside of
H
by removing at most
\frac{t}{2}
subspaces
W_{i}\cap H
from it.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is not
\frac{t}{2}
-generic with respect to
H
with codimension
r
inside of
H
, as otherwise we are done. In this case, there must exist
\frac{t}{2}
distinct subspaces, say
W_{1}\cap H,\ldots,W_{\frac{t}{2}}\cap H\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that the codimension of
W_{1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{\frac{t}{2}}\cap H
with respect to
H
, is less than
tr/2
. As
H
has codimension
1
with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
it follows that

\codim\left(W_{1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{\frac{t}{2}}\cap H\right)\leqslant\frac{t}{2}\cdot r,

(12)

where now the codimension is with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
.
Since
\mathcal{S}
is
t
-generic (and thus
\frac{t}{2}
-generic as well) with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
, this implies that
W_{1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{\frac{t}{2}}\subseteq H
.
Indeed, this is because
W_{1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{\frac{t}{2}}
already has codimension
\frac{tr}{2}
, so by (
12
), further intersecting with
H
does not result in a smaller subspace, and hence this subspace must be inside
H
.

Now delete
W_{1}\cap H,\ldots,W_{\frac{t}{2}}\cap H
from
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. We claim that the resulting set is
\frac{t}{2}
-generic with respect to
H
. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that it is not. Then there must be another
\frac{t}{2}
distinct subspaces, say
W_{\frac{t}{2}+1}\cap H,\ldots,W_{t}\cap H\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that
W_{\frac{t}{2}+1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{t}\subseteq H
, implying

\left(W_{1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{\frac{t}{2}}\right)+\left(W_{\frac{t}{2}+1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{t}\right)\subseteq H.

This is a contradiction however, as
\mathcal{S}
is
t
-generic with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
, so
\codim(W_{1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{t})=tr
, and

\displaystyle\dim\left((W_{1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{\frac{t}{2}})+(W_{\frac{t}{2}+1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\dim\left(W_{1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{\frac{t}{2}}\right)+\dim\left(W_{\frac{t}{2}+1}\cap\cdots\cap W_{t}\right)-\dim\left(W_{1}\cap W_{2}\cap\cdots\cap W_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=2\left(\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})-\frac{t}{2}r\right)-\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})+tr=\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})>\dim(H),

and contradiction.
∎

The following result is a version of
Lemma

5.16
for codimensions larger than
1
:

Lemma 5.17
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a collection of subspaces of codimension
r
that is
2^{K}
-generic with respect to
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
and let
B
be a subspace of codimension
j
. Then, there is a set of subspaces
\mathcal{W}_{B}
such that the following holds

•

Each
W_{B}\in\mathcal{W}_{B}
is equal to
W\cap B
for some
W\in\mathcal{W}
.

•

The set of subspaces
\mathcal{W}_{B}
is
2^{K-j}
-generic with respect to
B
and each subspace in
\mathcal{W}_{B}
has codimension
r
.

•

|\mathcal{W}|-2^{K}\leqslant|\mathcal{W}_{B}|\leqslant|\mathcal{W}|

Proof.

We will obtain
\mathcal{W}_{B}
by removing a small number of subspaces from
\{W\cap B\;|\;W\in\mathcal{W}\}
. There is a sequence of subspaces
W_{\mathsf{amb}}=B_{0}\supseteq B_{1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq B_{j}=B
, such that
B_{i+1}
is a hyperplane inside of
B_{i}
. Do the following,

1.

Initialize
\mathcal{W}_{0}=\mathcal{W}
and set
i=1
.

2.

Set
\mathcal{W}_{i}=\{W\cap B_{i}\;|\;W\in\mathcal{W}_{i-1}\}
. Then, using
Lemma

5.16
, remove at most
2^{K-i-1}
-many subspaces to turn
\mathcal{W}_{i}
into a
2^{K-i}
-generic collection with respect to
B_{i}
.

3.

Stop if
i=j
, otherwise, increase
i
by
1
and return to step
2
.

We verify the three properties. The first property holds by construction. For the second property, it is clear that the output is a set of subspaces
\mathcal{W}_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{B}
that is
2^{K-j}
-generic with respect to
B
, and by
Lemma

5.16
, the codimension of each
W\cap B\in\mathcal{W}_{B}
with respect to
B
is still
r
. Finally, for the third property, note that during each iteration, at most
2^{K-i-1}
subspaces are removed by
Lemma

5.16
, so altogether we remove at most
\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}2^{i}\leqslant 2^{K}
subspaces.
∎

5.3.2
The Sampling Lemma

As explained earlier, the notion of genericness is useful probabilistically,
and in this section we state and prove a sampling lemma about generic collections which is necessary for
our analysis. We work inside a subspace
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
. Fix an arbitrary zoom-in
Q\subseteq W_{\mathsf{amb}}
of dimension
a
, and let
\mathcal{S}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{m}\}
be a
2
-generic collection of subspaces of
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
of codimension
r
all containing
Q
. Also let
\mathcal{A}
be a set of
j
-dimensional subspaces containing
Q
. Consider the following two probability measures over
{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]
:

1.

The distribution
\mu
which is uniform over
{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]
.

2.

The distribution
\nu_{\mathcal{S}}
, wherein a subspace is sampled
by first picking
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
uniformly and then
sampling a subspace from
{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{i}]
uniformly.

Throughout this section one should think of
j
as much larger than
r
and
a
, and
\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})
as much larger than
j
. Concretely, we require
j\geqslant 2^{100}r^{2}a^{2}
and
\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})\geqslant 2^{100}j
. The main content of this section is the following lemma,
asserting that the measures

\mu
and
\nu_{\mathcal{S}}
are close in
statistical distance provided that
m
is large. More precisely:

Lemma 5.18
.

Let
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
be the ambient space and let
a,r,j
be dimensions such that
j\geqslant 2^{100r^{2}a^{2}}
,
\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})\geqslant 2^{100j}
. Let
Q\subseteq W_{\mathsf{amb}}
be a subspace of dimension
\dim(Q)=a
, and let
\mathcal{S}
be a
2
-generic collection of subspaces of
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
such that
|\mathcal{S}|=m
, each subspace in
\mathcal{S}
has codimension
r
with respect
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
, and each subspace in
\mathcal{S}
contains
Q
. Then, for any set of
j
-dimensional subspaces
\mathcal{L}\subset{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]
, we have

\left|\nu_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{L})-\mu(\mathcal{L})\right|\leqslant\frac{3q^{\frac{r}{2}(j-a)}}{\sqrt{m}}.

We now set up some notations for the proof of
Lemma

5.18
. For
L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]
let

N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)=|\{W_{i}\in\mathcal{S}\;|\;L\subseteq W_{i}\}|,

(13)

and for an arbitrary pair of distinct
W_{i},W_{i^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}
define the following quantities:

\begin{split}D&=|\{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]\;|\;L\subseteq W_{i}\}|\\
p_{1}&=\Pr_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[L\subseteq W_{i}]\\
p_{2}&=\Pr_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[L\subseteq W_{i}\cap W_{i^{\prime}}]\\
\end{split}

(14)

We note that all of these quantities are well defined as they do not
depend on the identity of
W_{i}
and

W_{i^{\prime}}
.
The following bound on
p_{1}
will be useful in this section.

Lemma 5.19
.

Let
p_{1}
be as in (
14
) and suppose
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
and the dimensions
a,r,j
satisfy
j\geqslant 2^{100r^{2}a^{2}}
,
\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})\geqslant 2^{100j}
. Then
|p_{1}-q^{-r(j-a)}|\leqslant\frac{q^{j}}{|W_{\mathsf{amb}}|}
.

Proof.

We have,

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=\Pr_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[L\subseteq W_{i}]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-a}\in V}[v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-a}\in W_{i}\;|\;\dim(Q+\spa(v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-a}))=j]

Additionally,

\Pr_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-a}\in W_{\mathsf{amb}}}[v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-a}\in W_{i}]=q^{-r(j-a)},

so

\left|p_{1}-q^{-r(j-a)}\right|\leqslant\Pr_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-a}\in V}[\dim(Q+\spa(v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-a}))<j]\leqslant\frac{q^{j}}{|W_{\mathsf{amb}}|}.\qed

Using our notations and
|{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]|\cdot p_{1}=D
we have that

\mu(L)=\frac{1}{|{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]|}=\frac{p_{1}}{D},\qquad\qquad\qquad\nu_{\mathcal{S}}(L)=\frac{N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)}{m}\cdot\frac{1}{D}=\frac{N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)}{mD},

(15)

so comparing
\mu
and
\nu_{\mathcal{S}}
amounts to comparing
p_{1}
and
N_{{\mathcal{S}}}/m
.
In the following claim we analyze the expectation and variance
of
N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)
when
L
is chosen uniformly from
{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]
:

Claim 5.20
.

Let
r,a,j,Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}},N_{S}
be as defined above.
We have,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)]=p_{1}m\quad\text{and}\quad\var(N_{\mathcal{S}}(L))\leqslant p_{1}m,

where the variance is over uniform
L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{amb}]
.

Proof.

By linearity of expectation

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Pr_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[L\subseteq W_{i}]=p_{1}m,

and we move on to the variance analysis.
To bound
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)^{2}]
, write

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}_{L\subseteq W_{i}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant m\cdot\Pr_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[L\subseteq W_{i}]+m^{2}\cdot\Pr_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[L\subseteq W_{i}\cap W_{i^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=p_{1}m+p_{2}m^{2}.

It follows that,

\var(N_{\mathcal{S}}(L))\leqslant p_{1}m+p_{2}m^{2}-p_{1}^{2}m^{2}.

Finally note that by the
2
-genericness
p_{2}
and
p_{1}^{2}
are nearly the same value, and it can be checked using (
14
) that
p_{2}\leqslant p_{1}^{2}
and so
\var(N_{\mathcal{S}}(L))\leqslant p_{1}m
.
∎

Combining Chebyshev’s inequality with
Claim

5.20
, we conclude the following lemma:

Lemma 5.21
.

For any
c>0
it holds that

\Pr_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}\Biggl[\left|N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)-p_{1}m\right|\geqslant c\cdot p_{1}m\Biggr]\leqslant\frac{1}{c^{2}p_{1}m}\leqslant\frac{1.01\cdot q^{r(j-a)}}{c^{2}m},

where recall the chosen
L
has dimension
j
,
\dim(W_{\mathsf{amb}})\geqslant 2^{100}q^{j}
,
\mathcal{S}
is a
2
-generic set of
m
codimension
r
subspaces all containing a fixed subspace of dimension
a
, and

p_{1}=\Pr_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[L\subseteq W_{i}]\in\left[q^{-r(j-a)}-\frac{q^{j}}{|W_{\mathsf{amb}}|},q^{-r(j-a)}+\frac{q^{j}}{|W_{\mathsf{amb}}|}\right]

and

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)]=p_{1}m.

In words,
p_{1}
the probability that a random
L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]
is contained in some fixed codimension
r
,
W_{i}
subspace of
W_{\mathsf{amb}}
.

Proof.

The first inequality is an immediate result of Chebyshev’s inequality with the bounds from
Claim

5.20
. To get the second inequality, we apply the lower bound on
p_{1}
from
Lemma

5.19
to get

p_{1}\geqslant q^{-r(j-a)}-\frac{q^{j}}{|W_{\mathsf{amb}}|}\geqslant\frac{1}{1.01q^{r(j-a)}}.

∎

Lastly, we use
Claim

5.20
to prove
Lemma

5.18
.

Proof of
Lemma

5.18
.

We have,

\displaystyle|\mu(\mathcal{L})-\nu_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{L})|=\frac{1}{mD}\left|\sum\limits_{L\in\mathcal{L}}N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)-p_{1}m\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{mD}\sum\limits_{L\in\mathcal{L}}\left|N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)-p_{1}m\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{|{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]|}{mD}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{L\in{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]}[|N_{\mathcal{S}}(L)-p_{1}m|],

and by Cauchy-Schwartz we get that

|\mu(\mathcal{L})-\nu_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{L})|\leqslant\frac{|{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]|}{mD}\sqrt{\var(N_{\mathcal{S}}(L))}.

Plugging in
Claim

5.20
and using
|{\sf Zoom}_{j}[Q,W_{\mathsf{amb}}]|=D/p_{1}
we get that

|\mu(\mathcal{L})-\nu_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{L})|\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{1}m}}\leqslant\frac{3q^{\frac{r}{2}(j-a)}}{\sqrt{m}}.

In the second inequality, we apply the lower bound on
p_{1}
from
Lemma

5.19
to get

p_{1}\geqslant q^{-r(j-a)}-\frac{q^{j}}{|W_{\mathsf{amb}}|}\geqslant\frac{1}{9q^{r(j-a)}}.

∎

5.3.3
An Upper Bound on the Number of Maximal Zoom-outs

For this subsection, we work in the second prover’s space,
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
, and make the assumption that
|V|\gg\ell
, say
|V|\geqslant 2^{100}q^{\ell}
to be concrete. We first establish several results in the simplified setting where there is no zoom-in. After that we show how to deduce an analogous result with a zoom-in. Throughout this section, we fix

T
to be a table that assigns, to each
L\in{\sf Grass}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V},2\ell)
, a linear function on
L
.

Definition 5.22
.

Given a table
T
on
{\sf Grass}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V},2\ell)
and a subspace
Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
, we call a zoom-out, function pair,
(W,g_{W})
, where
Q\subseteq W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
and
g_{W}:W\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
(C,\zeta)
-maximal with respect to
T
on
Q
if

\Pr_{L\in{\sf Grass}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V},2\ell)}[g_{W}|_{L}\equiv T[L]\;|\;Q\subseteq L\subseteq W]\geqslant C,

and there does not exist another zoom-out function pair,
(W^{\prime},g_{W^{\prime}})
such that
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}\supseteq W^{\prime}\supsetneq W
,
g_{W^{\prime}}:W^{\prime}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
g_{W^{\prime}}|_{W}\equiv g|_{W}
and

\Pr_{L\in{\sf Grass}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V},2\ell)}[g_{W^{\prime}}|_{L}\equiv T[L]\;|\;Q\subseteq L\subseteq W^{\prime}]\geqslant\zeta C.

In the case that
Q=\{0\}
, we say that
(W,g_{W})
is
(C,\zeta)
-maximal with respect to
T
.

In the above statement,
C
should be thought of as small and

\zeta\in(0,1]
should be thought of as an absolute constant. With this in mind, one should think of a zoom-out function pair
(W,g_{W})
as being
(C,\zeta)
maximal if the agreement between
g_{W}
and the table
T
on entries in
{\sf Zoom}_{2\ell}[Q,W]
is not “explained” by the agreement of a larger zoom-out function pair,
(W^{\prime},g_{W^{\prime}})
, where
W^{\prime}\supseteq W
and
g_{W^{\prime}}|_{W}=g_{W}
. By definition every zoom-out function pair
(W,f)
, where
f
has non-trivial agreement with
T
inside
W
, is in some sense contained in a maximal zoom-out function pair
(W^{\prime},f^{\prime})
(possibly with slightly lower agreement). This point is encapsulated by the following lemma.

Lemma 5.23
.

Let
T
be a table on
{\sf Grass}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V},2\ell)
,
Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
, and
W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V}
be a subspace of codimension
r
containing
Q
. Suppose that there exists a linear function
g_{W}:W\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that

\Pr_{L\in{\sf Grass}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V},2\ell)}[g_{W}|_{L}\equiv T[L]\;|\;Q\subseteq L\subseteq W]\geqslant C.

Then there exists a subspace
W^{\prime}\supseteq W
and a linear function
g_{W^{\prime}}:W^{\prime}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that
g_{W^{\prime}}|_{W}\equiv g_{W}
and
(g_{W^{\prime}},W^{\prime})
is
(C\zeta^{-r},\zeta)
-maximal.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of
Definition

5.22
. If
(W,g_{W})
is
(C,\zeta)
-maximal then we are done. Otherwise, there must exist
W_{1},g_{W_{1}}
such that
W_{1}\supsetneq W
,
g_{W_{1}}|_{W}\equiv g_{W}
and

\Pr_{L\in{\sf Grass}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V},2\ell)}[g_{W_{1}}|_{L}\equiv T[L]\;|\;Q\subseteq L\subseteq W_{1}]\geqslant\zeta C.

We can repeat this argument at most
r
times before obtaining some
(g_{W^{\prime}},W^{\prime})
that is
(C\zeta^{-r},\zeta)
maximal and satisfies
W^{\prime}\supseteq W
and
g_{W^{\prime}}|_{W}\equiv g_{W}
.
∎

Using clique consistency:

After constructing

•

Choose

Thus, the subspace

, while the second marginal of

. It is helpful, because the covering property from

Claim 6.4
.

Let
(Q,Q^{\prime})
be output by
\mathcal{D}_{{\sf adv}}
. Then the following two statements are true.

•

If

,
Q^{\prime}\cap H_{U}=\{0\}
, then
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{{\sf lucky}}
,
Q\cap H_{U}=\{0\}
.

•

\mathcal{D}_{Q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{D}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})
, and as a consequence if
Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}_{{\sf smooth}}
then
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{{\sf smooth}}
.

Proof.

Let
(Q,Q^{\prime})
be as described so that
Q=\spa(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r_{1}})
and
Q^{\prime}=\spa(x_{1}+w_{1},\ldots,x_{r_{1}}+w_{r_{1}})
for some
x_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
and
w_{i}\in H_{U}
.

We start with the first item. Suppose
Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}_{{\sf lucky}}
,
Q^{\prime}\cap H_{U}=\{0\}
. To see that
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{{\sf lucky}}
, note that
Q\oplus H_{U}=Q^{\prime}\oplus H_{U}
, so as explained earlier we have
W_{Q^{\prime}}=W_{Q}
, and
g_{Q,W_{Q}}\equiv g_{Q^{\prime},W_{Q^{\prime}}}
, so the result follows from (
18
). It is also easy to see that
Q\cap H_{U}=\{0\}
.

We move onto the second item of showing
\mathcal{D}_{Q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{D}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})
. We will first show
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})
and then describe how the other equality can be shown similarly. Let
A:Q^{\prime}\to Q
be the linear transformation that maps
x_{i}+w_{i}
to
x_{i}
. Abusing notation, for
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2\ell})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
satisfying
\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q
, let us write
A(x)=(A(x_{1}),\ldots,A(x_{r_{1}}),x_{r_{1}+1},\ldots,x_{2\ell})
. Note that
A
is a bijection between the following two sets

\mathcal{A}_{1}=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}\;|\;\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q^{\prime}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{A}_{2}=\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}\;|\;\spa_{r_{1}}(y)=Q\}.

Then,

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})=\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1}}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)}{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})=\frac{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(y)}{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}_{2}}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(y)}

(21)

Now we claim that the map
A
is a measure preserving (under measure
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
) bijection between
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
and
\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}
.

First note that for any
x\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
, we have
A(x)-x\in\left(H_{U}\right)^{2\ell}
, so it is straightforward to verify that
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(A(x))
. It follows that
A
is a bijection between
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
which preserves measure under
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, and consequently the denominators in (
21
) are the same.

For the numerators we have noted that if
x\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1}
then
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(x)=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(A(x))
, and it remains to show that
x\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1}
if and only if
A(x)\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}
. To this end observe that if
x\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1}
then
\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q^{\prime}
and

T_{1}[\spa(x)\oplus H_{U}]|_{\spa(x)}\equiv g_{Q^{\prime},W_{Q^{\prime}}}|_{\spa(x)}.

Then, note that
\spa(A(x))\oplus H_{U}=\spa(x)\oplus H_{U}
,
W_{Q}=W_{Q^{\prime}}
, and
g_{Q,W_{Q}}\equiv g_{Q^{\prime},W_{Q^{\prime}}}
. Moreover,
g_{Q,W_{Q}}
agrees with the side conditions on
H_{U}
, so the above implies that
g_{Q,W_{Q}}|_{\spa(x)\oplus H_{U}}\equiv T_{1}[\spa(x)\oplus H_{U}]
, and consequently,

T_{1}[\spa(x)\oplus H_{U}]|_{\spa(A(x))}\equiv g_{Q^{\prime},W_{Q^{\prime}}}|_{\spa(A(x))}.

It follows that
A(x)\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}
as desired. The other direction follows similarly. Since
x\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1}
if and only if
A(x)\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}
and the map
A
is also a bijection from
\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1}
to
\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}
which preserves measure under
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. Thus, the numerators in (
21
) are the same and we can conclude that
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})
.

The proof that
\mathcal{D}_{Q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{D}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})
proceeds similarly, except that
x
and
A(x)
trivially have the same measure under
\mathcal{D}
, because
\mathcal{D}
is the uniform distribution over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{U}
.

We conclude that
\mathcal{D}_{Q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{D}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{Q}(\mathcal{L})
, so by the definition of

we get that if

, then

.
∎

With

6.4

under

Applying

, both provers receive an advice subspace

,

, and

(22)

Note that by definition of

(24)

Now, for a randomly chosen question to the second prover,
V\subseteq U
, conditioned on the advice being

in

over the second prover’s question

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}{x^{\prime}_{i}\in W_{Q}[V],w_{i}\in H_{U},}\\
{x_{i}=x^{\prime}_{i}+w_{i},\forall i\in[2\ell]}\end{subarray}}\left[x\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{Q}\;|\;\spa_{r_{1}}(x)=Q\right]\geqslant\frac{C}{\frac{80}{\delta}\cdot 2^{10/\delta}q^{30/\delta}}:=C^{\prime}.

We call such
V
consistent, so that
V
is consistent with probability at least
\beta^{10/\delta+2}
. Now we will show that if

Fix a consistent

, so that (
25
) holds. Let us now bound the fraction of agreement that

inside of

\begin{split}&\Pr_{L^{\prime}\in{\sf Grass}_{q}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V},2\ell)}[T^{\prime}_{1}[L^{\prime}]\equiv g_{Q,W_{Q}}|_{L^{\prime}}\;|\;Q\subseteq L^{\prime}\subseteq W_{Q}[V]]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\geqslant\Pr_{x^{\prime}_{i}\in W_{Q}[V],L^{\prime}=\spa(x^{\prime})}[T^{\prime}_{1}[L^{\prime}]\equiv g_{Q,W_{Q}}|_{L^{\prime}}\;|\;Q\subseteq L^{\prime}]-q^{2\ell-k}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\geqslant\Pr_{x^{\prime}_{i}\in W_{Q}[V],L^{\prime}=\spa(x^{\prime})}[T_{1}[L^{\prime}\oplus H_{U}]|_{L^{\prime}}\equiv g_{Q,W_{Q}}|_{L^{\prime}}\;|\;Q\subseteq L^{\prime}]-q^{2\ell-2k}-q^{2\ell-k}\\

&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-q^{2\ell-2k}-q^{2\ell-k}\\

We note that in the events of interest for the probabilities above, it is implied that the event of interest only occurs if

and

are not defined. In the first transition, we are using the fact that the

Continuing, the probability from the last line is equal to

\begin{split}&\Pr_{x^{\prime}_{i}\in W_{Q}[V],L^{\prime}=\spa(x^{\prime})}[T_{1}[L^{\prime}\oplus H_{U}]|_{L^{\prime}}\equiv g_{Q,W_{Q}}|_{L^{\prime}}\;|\;\spa_{r_{1}}(x^{\prime})=Q]\\

(27)

The first transition is because the distribution over

containing

\begin{split}&\Pr_{L^{\prime}\in{\sf Grass}_{q}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{V},2\ell)}[T^{\prime}_{1}[L^{\prime}]\equiv g_{Q,W_{Q}}|_{L^{\prime}}\;|\;Q\subseteq L^{\prime}\subseteq W_{Q}[V]]\geqslant C^{\prime}-q^{2\ell-2k}-q^{2\ell-k}\geqslant\frac{C^{\prime}}{8}.\end{split}

, the number of
