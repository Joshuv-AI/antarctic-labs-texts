Meta-Posterior Consistency for the Bayesian Inference of Metastable Systems
Zachary P. Adams, Sayan Mukherjee

Abstract
The vast majority of the literature on learning dynamical systems or stochastic processes from time series has focused on stable or ergodic systems, for both Bayesian and frequentist inference procedures. However, most real-world systems are only metastable, that is, the dynamics appear to be stable on some time scale, but are in fact unstable over longer time scales. Consistency of inference for metastable systems may not be possible, but one can ask about metaconsistency: Do inference procedures converge when observations are taken over a large but finite time interval, but diverge on longer time scales? In this paper we introduce, discuss, and quantify metaconsistency in a Bayesian framework. We discuss how metaconsistency can be exploited to efficiently infer a model for a sub-system of a larger system, where inference on the global behavior may require much more data, or there is no theoretical guarantee as to the asymptotic success of inference procedures. We also discuss the relation between metaconsistency and the spectral properties of the model dynamical system in the case of uniformly ergodic and non-ergodic diffusions.

Introduction

The inference of models from data is a central step in many scientific and engineering applications.
One hopes that, as more data are obtained, a model inferred from these data converges to a model which is the “best” representation of the data.
When this convergence occurs, the inference procedure used is said to be
consistent
.
Previous studies have shown that, if the system from which data are obtained is
stable
(for instance, in the sense that it is ergodic), then most reasonable inference procedures will be consistent
[
45
,
49
]
.

As we usually obtain data from systems which are influenced by external factors, or of which our understanding is incomplete, few real-world systems are truly stable.
Often, they are only
metastable
, exhibiting behaviour that appears to be stable on some time scale, but unstable on longer time scales.
For instance, metastable behaviour is recognized as playing a significant role in physiological systems
[
24
,
31
]
, population dynamics
[
21
,
28
]
, human and animal microbiomes
[
12
,
64
,
73
]
, weather systems
[
43
,
57
]
, and multiagent systems
[
9
]
.
While metastability has been considered by scientists from various disciplines over the years, there is little to no rigorous mathematical work on the effects of metastability on inference procedures.
In such metastable systems, we conjecture that inference schemes are
metaconsistent
, in that they appear to converge for a large but finite amount of data, but that this convergence is transient.

In this paper we introduce, discuss, and precisely quantify metaconsistency in a Bayesian framework.
We present results on when metaconsistency occurs, and discuss how metaconsistency can be exploited to more efficiently infer a model for a sub-system of a larger system when the amount of data needed to make inferences about the larger system is impractical.
Finally, in the case of data modelled by continuous time dynamical systems, such as stochastic differential equations (SDEs), we demonstrate the connection between metaconsistency and the spectral theory of dynamical systems.
More specifically, the main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

•

A formal introduction of the concept of metaconsistency (Definition
1.2
);

•

A precise characterization of consistency for ergodic diffusions, with all rates appearing therein related to the spectral gaps and Fisher information of the models, presenting qualitatively known results in as quantitative a fashion as possible (Theorem
4.1
);

•

A precise characterization of metaconsistency for ergodic and non-ergodic diffusions in a Bayesian framework, all rates appearing therein again being related to the spectral gaps and Fisher information of the models (Theorem
4.2
);

•

A result describing how, in some scenarios, one can speed up learning rates by restricting to each metastable region, learning the dynamics there, and then “annealing” what is learned back together (Theorem
5.1
).

Before proceeding, we fix the setup and notation of our Bayesian framework in Section
1.1
.
Following this,
we close the introduction with a short review of the relevant literature in Section
1.2
.
In Section
2
we restate qualitatively known results on the rates of posterior consistency under conditions of
local asymptotic normality
in as quantitative a fashion as possible that applies to finite time regimes.
In Section
3
we introduce the concept of metaconsistency and present results allowing for its quantification.
In Section
4
the timescales of metaconsistency are precisely quantified for metastable diffusion processes.
Sections
4.3
&
4.4
apply our results to study metaconsistency for diffusions in a double well potential, in both cases where the double well is globally or only locally attracting.
In Section
5
we discuss how, in certain scenarios, one may translate a metastable decomposition of a diffusion process into a linear decomposition of parameter space, and how this decomposition may be exploited to improve posterior contraction rates.

1.1
Setup and notation

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a set of parameters.
To each
\theta\in\Theta
we associate a unique probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
on an ambient measurable space with filtration
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\left(\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}\right)
.
Let
E
be a Banach space.
For
t>0
, the measure

\mathbb{P}_{\theta}^{t}(\,\cdot\,)\,\coloneqq\,\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left[X_{[0,t]}\in\,\cdot\,\right]

corresponds to the distribution of an
E
-valued stochastic process
X=(X_{s})_{t\geq 0}
over the time interval
[0,t]
, where we write
X_{[0,t]}\coloneqq(X_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}
.
We presume the existence of a
ground truth

\theta_{0}\in\Theta
.

In the terminology of statistics,
\mathbb{P}^{t}\coloneqq\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}^{t};\,\theta\in\Theta\}
is a
statistical model
.
For any
\sigma
-finite measure
\nu
such that

\mathbb{P}_{\theta}^{t}\ll\nu
for each
\theta\in\Theta
and
t>0
, the
likelihood function
is given by the Radon-Nikodym derivative

L_{t}(X\mid\theta)\,\coloneqq\,\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{\theta}^{t}}{d\nu}(X),\qquad\theta\in\Theta,

where
X=(X_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is an observed path.
In the following, we find it convenient to take
\nu=\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
, though, as a consequence of the Likelihood Principle, one could take any other
\sigma
-finite dominating measure proportional to
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}

[
38
]
.

Given a (possibly random)
prior distribution

\pi_{0}
on
\Theta
, we define the
normalization constant
as

\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\pi_{0}}(X)\,\coloneqq\,\int_{\Theta}L_{t}(X\mid\theta)\,\pi_{0}(d\theta).

The
posterior distribution
is then defined as

\pi_{t}(d\theta\mid X)\,\coloneqq\,\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\pi_{0}}(X)}L_{t}(X\mid\theta)\,\pi_{0}(d\theta).

(1)

For
t>0
we denote by
\pi_{t}
the posterior distribution as a map
(F,X)\mapsto\pi_{t}(F\mid X)
.

Definition 1.1
.

The statistical model
\mathbb{P}^{t}
is
consistent
, or exhibits
posterior consistency
(at
\theta_{0}
), when there exists a family of measurable subsets
(F_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of
\Theta
such that
F_{t}\rightarrow\{\theta_{0}\}
as
t\rightarrow\infty
and, for any
\eta>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{t}(F_{t})<1-\eta\right]\,\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{}\,0.

(2)

If
\mathbb{P}^{t}
is consistent at
\theta_{0}
, we say that it has a
posterior contraction rate
(PCR) of
(\epsilon_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and a
posterior contraction certainty rate
(PCCR) of
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
if
\epsilon_{t}\rightarrow 0
and
\eta_{t}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, and

\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{t}\left(B_{\epsilon_{t}}(\theta_{0})\right)<1-\eta_{t}\right]\,\leq\,\eta_{t},

(3)

where
B_{\epsilon}(\theta_{0})\subset\Theta
denotes the ball of radius
\epsilon
centered at
\theta_{0}
.

We emphasize that most studies only focus on the PCR of Bayesian schemes, ignoring the PCCR.
Note that it is entirely possible that the “Markov type” bound in (
3
) may not be optimal.
When
\eta_{t}
is obtained such that
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{t}(B_{\epsilon_{t}}(\theta_{0}))<1-\eta_{t}\right]\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow 0
, one can likely get a better bound than just
\eta_{t}
on the right hand side of (
3
), perhaps a “DKW type” bound.
For simplicity, we define the PCCR as the quantity such that (
3
) as written holds, rather than looking for optimal right hand side bounds.

We now formalize the concept of
metaconsistency
.
While the definition remains somewhat imprecise due to lack of specification of the time interval appearing in the definition, we obtain quantitative characterizations of metaconsistent timescales in the following sections.

Definition 1.2
.

A statistical model
\mathbb{P}^{t}
is
metaconsistent
, or
meta-posterior consistent
(at
\theta_{0}
), if there exist
(\hat{\epsilon}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\,(\hat{\eta}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in[0,\infty)
such that
\hat{\epsilon}_{t}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
and
\hat{\eta}_{t}\ll 1
for all
t
in some finite time interval
[\underline{t},\overline{t}]
, and

\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{t}(B_{\hat{\epsilon}_{t}}(\theta_{0}))<1-\hat{\eta}_{t}\right]\,\leq\,\hat{\eta}_{t}.

We refer to
(\hat{\epsilon}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\hat{\eta}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as the
meta-posterior contraction rate
(m-PCR) and
meta-posterior contraction certainty rate
(m-PCCR), respectively.

The concept of “local asymptotic normality” is often used in the analysis of inference procedures, and will be used throughout the paper.
For a linear space
U
and
x\in U
, let
U-x=\{u-x:u\in U\}
.

.

A statistical model
\mathbb{P}^{t}
is
Locally Asymptotically Normal
(LAN) at

if there exist

a family of linear maps
\phi=(\phi_{t})
,
\phi_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\Theta-\theta_{0}
, such that
\phi_{t}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
,

2.

a sequence of random variables
\Delta=(\Delta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that
\Delta_{t}=\Delta_{t}(X)
converges in
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
-mean square to a standard

As discussed further in Section
4
, many models satisfy a similar condition, but where
\Delta_{\infty}
has a nontrivial covariance matrix.
See for instance
[

]
.
This condition is often referred to as a
locally asymptotic mixed normality (LAMN)
.
The interested reader may trace our proofs, and note that allowing for the LAMN condition does not significantly change their logic.

Gaussian
\Delta_{\infty}
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, and

3.

a family of random maps
r=(r_{t})
,
r_{t}:\Theta\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\Theta-\theta_{0}
, such that

as

in

such that the equality

Remark 1.1
.

As
\Theta
is assumed to be finite dimensional, we restrict ourselves to the parametric setting.
However, we expect many of the results below to translate to the nonparametric setting with suitable modifications to the decay rates appearing in our main results.

2
Posterior consistency from LAN conditions

2.1
Statement of main result

We here discuss how one can obtain precise estimates on the PCR and PCCR from conditions of local asymptotic normality (LAN).
LAN conditions are well known to be sufficient for posterior consistency.
The contribution of this section is the detail with which we present estimates on PCRs and PCCRs from LAN conditions, facilitating the comparison of the true values with the metastable values in the sections to follow.
Furthermore, the theory outlined here allows us to explicitly compute all bounds on the PCR and PCCR for a class of metastable diffusions in Section
4.3
below.

Assumption 2.1
.

Fix a (random) prior distribution
\pi_{0}
on
\Theta
with
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
-almost surely finite moments.
The family
(\mathbb{P}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is
(\phi,\Delta,r)
-LAN at some fixed
\theta_{0}
which is almost surely contained in the support of
\pi_{0}
.
Denoting by
\left|\phi_{t}\right|
the smallest singular value of
\phi_{t}
,
\left|\phi_{t}\right|\rightarrow 0
and
\left|\phi_{t}\right|^{-\alpha}\phi_{t}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Defining

m_{t}(du)\coloneqq\exp\left(-\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{t}-u\right\|^{2}+r_{t}\right)\,\pi_{0}(du),

(4)

there exists a deterministic
\underline{m}>0
such that
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
-almost surely,
m_{t}(\Theta)\geq\underline{m}
for all
t>0
.

Under the LAN condition, the PCR is determined by the rate at which
\phi_{t}
goes to zero.
Moreover, we can characterize the PCCR in terms of the rates at which
\Delta_{t}
converges to
\Delta_{\infty}
(in mean square), and at which
r_{t}
converges to zero (in mean).
To state this more precisely,
define

\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)\,\coloneqq\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty}\right)\right\|^{2}\right],\qquad\varepsilon_{r}(t)\,\coloneqq\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\int_{\Theta}\left|r_{t}\right|\,\pi_{0}(d\theta)\right],\qquad t\geq 0.

(5)

For fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\delta>0
, define

F_{t}\,\coloneqq\,\left|\phi_{t}\right|^{\alpha}B_{\delta}(\theta_{0}),\qquad U_{t}\,\coloneqq\,\left|\phi_{t}\right|^{\alpha}\phi_{t}^{-1}(B_{\delta}(0)),\qquad t\geq 0.

(6)

By Assumption
2.1
,
F_{t}\rightarrow\{\theta_{0}\}
and
U_{t}\rightarrow\Theta
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, while both
\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t),\varepsilon_{r}(t)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
It will be seen that the posterior distribution concentrates on
F_{t}
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, so that the PCR can be characterized by
\left|\phi_{t}\right|
, while the PCCR can be characterized by the rates at which
\Delta_{t}\rightarrow\Delta_{\infty}
,
r_{t}\rightarrow 0
, and
\pi_{0}(U_{t}^{c})\rightarrow 0
, in various senses.

Proposition 2.1
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the model
\mathbb{P}^{t}
is consistent at
\theta_{0}
with a PCR determined by
\left|\phi_{t}\right|
, and PCCR determined by
\varepsilon_{\Delta},\varepsilon_{r}
.
In particular, let
(F_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(U_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the sequences of subsets of
\Theta
defined in (
6
), and let
\varepsilon_{\Delta}
and
\varepsilon_{r}
be as in (
5
).
Then, there exists a constant
c>0
depending solely on
\pi_{0}
such that, defining

H(t)\,\coloneqq\,\underline{m}^{-1}\left(\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)+c\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)^{1/2}+\varepsilon_{r}(t)+\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{0}(U_{t}^{c})\right]\right),

(7)

F_{t}\rightarrow\{\theta_{0}\}
and
H(t)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, and for
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{t}(F_{t}\mid X)<1-H(t)^{1/2}\right]\,\leq\,H(t)^{1/2}.

(8)

Remark 2.1
.

Requiring
m_{t}(\Theta)
to be bounded away from zero uniformly in
t>0
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
-almost surely, as in Assumption
2.1
, is essentially requiring that each realization of
\pi_{0}
and the density
\exp\left(-\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{t}-u\right\|^{2}+r_{t}\right)
are concentrated on regions of parameter space that have sufficient overlap.
Previous studies have obtained posterior consistency with a PCR of order
t^{-1/2}
without making such an assumption, see for instance
[
45
,
49
]
.
However, we suggest that this is due to the fact that these studies are only concerned with
asymptotic
consistency, whereas the purpose of this study is to shorten the bridge between theory and practice, obtaining bounds that hold in the finite time regime as well.
We also note that a random prior is in agreement with the practice of
hierarchical Bayes
, which is a popular approach to Bayesian inference in the applied statistics community.
One motivation for hierarchical Bayesian models or multilevel models is to allow for the dependence of the joint probability model over parameters.
In our case for example the means of the random priors.
The idea of different levels of probability goes back at least to the 1950’s, see for instance
[
39
]
.

Remark 2.2
.

In applications, such as the case of ergodic diffusions considered in Section
4
, below, one expects
\left|\phi_{t}\right|
,
\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)
, and
\varepsilon_{r}(t)
to decay at algebraic rates
t^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1/2]
, as discussed in Section
1.2
, where
\alpha=1/2
in the parametric setting.
However, in many cases more precise information than this can be obtained.
As seen in Section
4
,

\left|\phi_{t}\right|\,\simeq\,s_{1}^{-1}t^{-1/2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\varepsilon_{\delta}(t),\varepsilon_{r}(t)\,\simeq\,(1-e^{-\gamma t})\gamma^{-1}t^{-1}+s_{1}^{-1}t^{-1},

where
s_{1}
is the Fisher information (to be defined) and
\gamma
is the spectral gap of the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
under
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
.
Consequently, when restricting attention to a metastable state increases the Fisher information, the PCR is improved (as in the example of Section
4.3
, while if doing so increases the spectral gap of the true process, the PCCR is improved (as in the example of Section
4.4
).
As we discuss in Appendix
A
, restricting a metastable system to a single metastable state greatly increases the spectral gap of the system.

2.2
Proof of Proposition
2.1

Before proving Proposition
2.1

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(m_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a sequence of (possibly random) measures on a measurable space
(E,\mathscr{F})
converging in total variation to a nontrivial regular measure
m
.
Letting
(U_{t})_{t\geq 0}\subset\mathscr{F}
be a nested sequence of measurable sets such that
U=\bigcup_{t\geq 0}U_{t}
and assuming that
\inf_{t\geq 0}m_{t}(U)\eqqcolon\underline{m}>0
,

\left|\frac{m_{t}(U_{t})}{m_{t}(U)}-1\right|\,\leq\,\underline{m}^{-1}\left(2\left\|m_{t}-m\right\|_{TV}+\left|m(U_{t})-m(U)\right|\right)\,\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{}\,0.

Proof.

An application of the triangle inequality yields the result,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{m_{t}(U_{t})}{m_{t}(U)}-1\right|

\displaystyle\leq\,\left|\frac{m_{t}(U_{t})-m(U_{t})}{m_{t}(U)}\right|+\left|\frac{m(U_{t})-m(U)}{m_{t}(U)}\right|+\left|\frac{m(U)-m_{t}(U)}{m_{t}(U)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\,\underline{m}^{-1}\left(2\left\|m_{t}-m\right\|_{TV}+\left|m(U_{t})-m(U)\right|\right).

∎

In the setting of a
(\phi,\Delta,r)
-LAN statistical model, we take
m_{t}
as in (
4
),

m_{t}(du)\coloneqq\exp\left(-\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{t}-u\right\|^{2}+r_{t}\right)\,\pi_{0}(du),

and similarly define

m(du)\coloneqq\exp\left(-\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\infty}-u\right\|^{2}\right)\,\pi_{0}(du).

(9)

We now obtain a result on the rate of convergence of
\left\|m_{t}-m\right\|_{TV}
to zero as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let Assumption
2.1
hold, so that
(\mathbb{P}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is
(\phi,\Delta,r)
-LAN at
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
.
Define
m_{t}
as in (
4
),
m
as in (
9
), and
\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)
and
\varepsilon_{r}(t)
as in (
5
).
Then,

\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|m_{t}-m\right\|_{TV}\right]\,\leq\,\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)+c\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)^{1/2}+\varepsilon_{r}(t),

where
c>0
is a constant depending solely on the prior
\pi_{0}
, and
\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t),\,\varepsilon_{r}(t)
, tend to zero as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

Remark that, from the reverse triangle inequality,

\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{t}-u\right\|^{2}\,\geq\,\left\|\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty}\right)\right\|^{2}+2\left\|\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty}\right)\right\|\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\infty}-u\right\|+\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\infty}-u\right\|^{2}.

It then follows that, as the exponential has Lipschitz constant one on the negative half line,

\displaystyle 2\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|m_{t}-m\right\|_{TV}\right]

\displaystyle=\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\int_{\Theta}\left|e^{-\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{t}-u\right\|^{2}+r_{t}}-e^{-\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\infty}-u\right\|^{2}}\right|\,\pi_{0}(du)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\int_{\Theta}e^{-\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{t}-u\right\|^{2}}\left|e^{-\left\|\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty}\right)\right\|^{2}-\left\|\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty}\right)\right\|\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\infty}-u\right\|+r_{t}}-1\right|\,\pi_{0}(du)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\int_{\Theta}\left\|\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty}\right)\right\|^{2}+\left\|\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty}\right)\right\|\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\infty}-u\right\|+\left|r_{t}\right|\,\pi_{0}(du)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty}\right)\right\|^{2}\right]+2\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty}\right)\right\|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\int_{\Theta}\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\infty}-u\right\|\,\pi_{0}(du)^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\int_{\Theta}\left|r_{t}\right|\,\pi_{0}(du)\right]

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\,\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)+c\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)^{1/2}+\varepsilon_{r}(t),

where we remark that the constant
c>0
in the last line above only depends on the prior
\pi_{0}
.
∎

We are now prepared to prove posterior consistency of a statistical model from LAN conditions, paying attention to rates of contraction and contraction certainty.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Let
m_{t}
and
m
be as defined in (
4
), and for measurable
F\subset\Theta
let
U_{t}(F)\coloneqq\phi_{t}^{-1}(F-\theta_{0})
.
Note that in our setup,
U_{t}(\Theta)=\Theta
.
Due to the LAN property of
(\mathbb{P}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
,

\pi_{t}(F\mid X)\,=\,\frac{\int_{U_{t}(F)}e^{-\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{t}-u\right\|^{2}+r_{t}}\,\pi_{0}(du)}{\int_{\Theta}e^{\left\|\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{t}-u\right\|^{2}+r_{t}}\,\pi_{0}(du)}\,=\,\frac{m_{t}(U_{t}(F))}{m_{t}(\Theta)}.

So long as
\theta_{0}\in F
, it follows that
U_{t}(F)\rightarrow\Theta
as
t\rightarrow\infty
(otherwise
U_{t}(F)\rightarrow\varnothing
).
Hence,
noting that
m(U)\leq\pi_{0}(U)
for all measurable
U\subset\Theta
almost surely, and applying Lemmas
2.1
&
2.2
yields

\displaystyle\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left|\pi_{t}(F\mid X)-1\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\underline{m}^{-1}\left(2\left\|m_{t}-m\right\|_{TV}+m(U_{t}(F)^{c})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\,\underline{m}^{-1}\left(\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)+c\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)^{1/2}+c\varepsilon_{r}(t)+\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{0}(U_{t}(F)^{c})\right]\right).

Consequently, by Markov’s inequality we have that, for any
\eta>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{t}(F\mid X)<1-\eta\right]\,\leq\,\eta^{-1}\underline{m}^{-1}\left(\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)+c\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)^{1/2}+c\varepsilon_{r}(t)+\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{0}(U_{t}(F)^{c})\right]\right).

Taking at each point in time
t>0
the set
F_{t}=\left|\phi_{t}\right|^{\alpha}B_{\delta}(\theta_{0})
,

U_{t}(F_{t})\,=\,\phi_{t}^{-1}(\left|\phi_{t}\right|^{\alpha}B_{\delta}(\theta_{0})-\theta_{0})\,=\,\left|\phi_{t}\right|^{\alpha}\phi_{t}^{-1}B_{\delta}(0),

which tends to the whole parameter space
\Theta
as

by Assumption
2.1
.
By the regularity of
\pi_{0}
, this completes the proof.
∎

3
Metaconsistency

3.1
Statement of main result

In this section, we do not require
(\mathbb{P}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
to be LAN.
Instead, we make the following assumption, presupposing the existence of
another
family
(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
, such that the distributions of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
under
(\mathbb{P}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
and
(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
are equal up to a stopping time
\tau
, and such that
(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}

is
LAN.
One could think of the following discussion as a backwards coupling argument, similar to the decouplings studied in
[
22
,
46
,
88
]
– though unlike in those works, here we take the decoupling time
\tau
as known, and construct a process that is perfectly coupled up to
\tau
.
In the metastable setting,
\tau
is the transition time from one of the metastable subsystems of the true system, and
(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is a modified model such that the metastable subsystem is globally stable.
See Section
4
below for concrete examples.

Assumption 3.1
.

There exists a stopping time
\tau
and a family of probability measures
(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
such that, for a fixed (random) prior distribution
\pi_{0}
on
\Theta
, for
\pi_{0}
-almost all
\theta\in\Theta
and
t>0
,

1.

\mathbb{P}_{\theta}[t<\tau]>0
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}[\tau<\infty]=1
.

2.

The distributions of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
under
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
and
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}
are equal on the event
\{t<\tau\}
,
i.e.

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}\left[X_{[0,t]}\in\,\cdot\,,t<\tau\right]\,=\,\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left[X_{[0,t]}\in\,\cdot\,,t<\tau\right]\quad\text{ for each $\theta\in\Theta$ and $t>0$}.

(10)

3.

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}\left[t<\tau\right]\,=\,\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left[t<\tau\right]
for each
\theta\in\Theta
and
t>0
.

4.

The family
(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is
(\hat{\phi},\hat{\Delta},\hat{r})
-LAN at some specific
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
, and satisfies Assumption
2.1
.

As in Section
2
, we require some auxiliary notation to state the main result of this section.
For fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\delta>0
, and variable
t>0
, define the sets

\hat{F}_{t}\,\coloneqq\,|\hat{\phi}_{t}|^{\alpha}B_{\delta}(\theta_{0}),\qquad\hat{U}_{t}\,\coloneqq\,|\hat{\phi}_{t}|^{\alpha}\hat{\phi}_{t}^{-1}(B_{\delta}(0)).

(11)

It is shown below that the m-PCR can be characterized by the decay rate of
\hat{\phi}_{t}
.
This is due to the fact that, in the metastable setting, the posterior
appears
to concentrate on the set
\hat{F}_{t}
defined in (
11
) up to the stopping time
\tau
.
Moreover, define
\varepsilon_{\hat{\Delta}}
and
\varepsilon_{\hat{r}}
as in (
5
), and
\hat{\underline{m}}\coloneqq\,\inf_{t\geq 0}\hat{m}_{t}(\Theta)
, where
\hat{m}_{t}
is the measure defined in (
4
), but with
\Delta_{t}
and
r_{t}
replaced by
\hat{\Delta}_{t}
and
\hat{r}_{t}
, and expectations being taken with respect to
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}
.

In analogy with Section
2
, define

\hat{H}(t)\,\coloneqq\,\hat{\underline{m}}^{-1}\left(\varepsilon_{\hat{\Delta}}(t)+c\varepsilon_{\hat{\Delta}}(t)^{1/2}+\varepsilon_{\hat{r}}(t)+\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{0}(\hat{U}_{t}^{c})\right]\right),

(12)

which tends to zero under Assumption
3.1
by Proposition
2.1
.
The main result of this section describes how the posterior consistency of
\hat{\pi}_{t}
relates to the behaviour of
\pi_{t}
.
We can now state the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold, and let
\hat{H}(t)
be as defined in (
12
), and
\hat{F}_{t},\,\hat{U}_{t},
as in (
11
) for some fixed
\delta>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Then, for
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{t}(\hat{F}_{t}\mid X)<1-\hat{H}(t)^{1/2}\right]\,\leq\,\hat{H}(t)^{1/2}+\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[t>\tau\right].

(13)

Before proceeding with the proof of Theorem
3.1
, let us make a few remarks.
Suppose that
both
Assumptions
2.1
&
3.1
hold, so that the estimates in (
8
) & (
13
) both hold.
If, additionally,

1.

\hat{H}(t)
decays to zero much faster than
H(t)
,

2.

\hat{F}_{t}
contracts at a rate at least as fast as
F_{t}
, and

3.

\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}[t>\tau]
remains small on a time scale where
\hat{H}(t)
is small,

then we observe meta-posterior consistency, in the sense of Definition
1.2
.
In Section
4
below, we consider the case where
X
is an ergodic diffusion under
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
.
We see that the PCCRs
H(t)
and
\hat{H}(t)
are determined by both the spectral gap and Fisher information of
X
under
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
and
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}
, respectively, while the contraction rates of
F_{t}
and
\hat{F}_{t}
are determined purely by the Fisher information of
X
under
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
and
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}
.
When the ergodic diffusion in consideration is metastable, it is known that restricting attention (in a sense to be made precise) to a single metastable state increases the spectral gap
[
65
]
.
On the other hand, restricting attention to a single metastable state may, as shown in the examples of Sections
4.3
&
4.4
, either increase or decrease the Fisher information of the system.
When the Fisher information decreases upon restricting attention to a metastable state, how much it does so depends on how much less the metastable state “sees” the parameter to be estimated.
In the example of Section
4.3
, the restriction does not cost us as much information, but does greatly increase the spectral gap of the system we consider, so that the approach seems worth it.

Counter-intuitively, in the example of Section
4.4
, we see that the Fisher information of a system can be
increased
by restricting attention to a subsystem.
This is in particular the case if there is some degenerate symmetry in the system, so that the Fisher information is zero for the whole system, but may be positive for a subsystem.
On the other hand, when the Fisher information of a system is decreased by restricting attention to a single metastable subsystem, we explain in Section
5
how the theory outlined in this paper can still be used to improve the PCR of the inference of a metastable diffusion.

3.2
Proof of Theorem
3.1

From Proposition
2.1
, it immediately follows that a posterior distribution constructed with respect to
(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
must exhibit posterior consistency.
To be precise, letting

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}^{t}\,\coloneqq\,\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}\left[X_{[0,t]}\in\,\cdot\,\right],\qquad\hat{L}_{t}(X\mid\theta)\,\coloneqq\,\frac{d\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}^{t}}{d\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}^{t}}(X),\qquad\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t}(X\mid\pi_{0})\,\coloneqq\,\int_{\Theta}\hat{L}_{t}(X\mid\theta)\,\pi_{0}(d\theta),

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{t}(d\theta\mid X)\,\coloneqq\,\frac{1}{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t}(X\mid\pi_{0})}\hat{L}_{t}(X\mid\theta)\,\pi_{0}(d\theta),

(14)

we have the following corollary to Proposition
2.1
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold, and let
\hat{F}_{t},\,\hat{U}_{t}
be as defined in (
11
) for fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\delta>0
.
Let
\varepsilon_{\hat{\Delta}},\,\varepsilon_{\hat{r}},
be as above.
Then,
\hat{F}_{t}\rightarrow\{\theta_{0}\}
and
\hat{U}_{t}\rightarrow\Theta
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, and

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\hat{\pi}_{t}(\hat{F}_{t}\mid X)<1-\hat{H}(t)^{1/2}\right]\,\leq\,\hat{H}(t)^{1/2},

(15)

where
\hat{H}(t)
, defined in (
12
), tends to zero as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Since
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}[\,\cdot\,,t<\tau]=\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}[\,\cdot\,,t<\tau]
for
\theta\in\Theta
and
t>0
,

L_{t}(X|\theta)\,=\,\hat{L}_{t}(X|\theta)\qquad\text{ for $X\in\{t<\tau\}$}.

(16)

Indeed, this is a consequence of the following fact:
For measures
\mu,\,\nu,
on a measurable space
(E,\mathcal{B}(E))
such that
\mu\ll\nu
, we have for
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(E)
that

\mu(A\cap B)\,=\,\int_{A\cap B}\frac{d\mu(\cdot)}{d\nu(\cdot)}(x)\,d\nu(x),

and

\mu(A\cap B)\,=\,\int_{A}\frac{d\mu(\cdot\cap B)}{d\nu(\cdot\cap B)}\,d\nu(x\cap B)\,=\,\int_{A\cap B}\frac{d\mu(\cdot\cap B)}{d\nu(\cdot\cap B)}\,d\nu(x).

It follows that, for
\nu
-almost all
x\in E
, we have

\frac{d\mu(\cdot\cap B)}{d\nu(\cdot\cap B)}(x)\,=\,\frac{d\mu(\cdot)}{d\nu(\cdot)}(x)\,1_{B}(x).

Consequently, by (
16
), for
X\in\{t<\tau\}
we have that
\pi_{t}(d\theta\mid X)=\hat{\pi}_{t}(d\theta\mid X)
.
For
t>0
,
a\in(0,1)
, and measurable
F\subset\Theta
, we then have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{t}(F)<a\right]

\displaystyle\leq\,\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{t}(F)<a,\,t<\tau\right]+\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[t>\tau\right]

(17)

\displaystyle=\,\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\hat{\pi}_{t}(F)<a,\,t<\tau\right]+\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[t>\tau\right]

\displaystyle\leq\,\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\hat{\pi}_{t}(F)<a\right]+\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[t>\tau\right].

Taking (
15
) & (
17
) together with
a=1-\hat{H}^{1/2}(t)
completes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

4
Application to ergodic diffusions

We here apply the theory developed above to diffusions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, parameterized by
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and governed by SDEs of the form

dX_{t}\,=\,V_{\theta}(X_{t})\,dt+\sigma\,dW_{t}

(18)

over an ambient probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\theta})
.

(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Wiener process,
\sigma>0
is fixed, and for each
\theta\in\Theta
the drift coefficient
V_{\theta}
is sufficiently regular.
When (
18
) is ergodic, we find precise bounds on the rates of decay of the quantities
\phi_{t}
,
\varepsilon_{r}
and
\varepsilon_{\Delta}
, appearing in
(\phi,\Delta,r)
-LAN setup of Theorem
4.1
:
\phi_{t}
decays at a rate proportional to the Fisher information;
\varepsilon_{\Delta}
decays at a rate proportional the spectral gap of the observed process;
and
\varepsilon_{r}
decays at a rate proportional to a combination of the Fisher information and the spectral gap.
By Proposition
2.1
, this allows us to characterize the PCR and PCCR of (
18
).
We also consider cases where the models in (
18
) are

1.

not ergodic, but, prior to an almost surely finite stopping time, are equivalent in law to models which are ergodic;

2.

ergodic, and, up to an almost surely finite stopping time, are equivalent to models which are also ergodic, but converge to their ergodic distributions at much faster rates;

3.

ergodic, and together have zero Fisher information, but prior to a stopping time are equivalent in law to a family of models with positive Fisher information.

In these cases, we show that the posterior distribution exhibits
metaconsistency
, as defined in Section
1
, and are able to precisely characterize the m-PCR and m-PCCR.

In Section
4.3
we consider a concrete example of an ergodic diffusion in a double-well potential.
Using our results, we obtain precise estimates on how much faster the posterior contracts when the diffusion remains in a neighbourhood of only one of its wells.
The theory outlined in Section
3
then allows us to conclude a much faster m-PCCR compared to the PCCR of this system.
We also consider an example of an ergodic diffusion in a multi-well potential which is not identifiable, in that the Fisher information
s_{1}
is equal to zero for the whole system, while the Fisher information of the restricted system is positive.

4.1
Statement of main results

We assume that for probability measures
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, (
18
) with initial distribution
\nu
has a unique solution, denoted
X_{t}(\nu)
, up to a possibly finite explosion time
\tau_{exp}
.
See
[
47
, Chapter 5]
for further details on the solution theory of (
18
).
When convenient, we write
X_{t}=X_{t}(\nu)
for the solution process of (
18
).

Assumption 4.1
.

For each
\theta\in\Theta
,
\tau_{exp}=\infty
almost surely, and (
18
) is uniformly ergodic, so there exists a unique probability measure

and constants

\left\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left[X_{t}\in\,\cdot\,\right]-\mu_{\theta}(\,\cdot\,)\right\|_{TV}\,\leq\,k(\theta)e^{-\gamma(\theta)t}\qquad\text{ for $t>0$}.

(19)

For
\theta\in\Theta
let
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}^{t}=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left[X_{[0,t]}(\nu)\in\,\cdot\,\right]
denote the distribution of the solution over the time interval
[0,t]
with arbitrary initial distribution
\nu
(which may depend on
\theta\in\Theta
).

appearing in (

of (

).
See Appendix

for further discussion of the constants

In addition to Assumption
4.1
, we require a condition guaranteeing the identifiability of the parameters
\Theta
in a statistical model.
Below, we assume that
d\geq p
.
If we were to relax this assumption, our statistical model would be
locally asymptotically mixed normal
, instead of simply LAN
[
66
]
.
That is, the limiting random variable
\Delta_{\infty}
in the definition of LAN models would have a nontrivial covariance matrix.
As the LAMN setting complicates the exposition of this section, we assume

to ease the presentation.

Assumption 4.2
.

Fix a (random) prior
\pi_{0}
with
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
-almost surely finite moments.
Let
d\geq p
and Assumption
4.1
hold, along with the following.

1.

For
t>0
, we have
\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|X_{t}\right\|\right]\leq Kt

.

2.

For each

\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}

Letting

(20)

4.

\pi_{0}(\theta)=\rho_{0}(u)\overline{\pi}(d\theta)
, where
\overline{\pi}
is deterministic.

Slightly abusing terminology, we refer to
I_{\theta_{0}}
in Assumption
4.2
(ii) as the
Fisher information matrix
, and

as the

of the statistical model consisting of the SDEs (
18
).
For further justification, compare the matrix
\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}(\xi)\right]
with the “Fisher information matrix” of
[
49
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let the family of SDEs (
18
) parameterized by
\theta\in\Theta
satisfy Assumption

.
Then, the family of posterior distributions
(\pi_{t}(\,\cdot\mid X))_{t\geq 0}

\theta_{0}\in\Theta

\delta>0
,
\alpha\in(0,1)

t\geq 0
, letting

\epsilon_{t}\,\coloneqq\,\delta/s_{1}^{\alpha}t^{\alpha/2},\qquad F_{t}\,\coloneqq\,B_{\epsilon_{t}}(\theta_{0}),\quad\text{ and }\quad U_{t}\,\coloneqq\,t^{1/2}I_{\theta_{0}}^{1/2}B_{\epsilon_{t}}(0),

(21)

it holds that
F_{t}\rightarrow\{\theta_{0}\}
and
U_{t}\rightarrow\Theta
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, and

\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{t}(F_{t}\mid X)<1-H(t)^{1/2}\right]\,\leq\,H(t)^{1/2}.

where

H(t)\,\coloneqq\,C\left(\left(\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(\theta_{0})t}}{\gamma(\theta_{0})t}\right)^{1/2}+\left(\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(\theta_{0})t}}{\gamma(\theta_{0})t}\right)+\frac{1}{s_{1}^{2}t}+\frac{1}{s_{1}^{3}t^{3/2}}+\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{0}(U_{t}^{c})\right]\right),

for a constant
C>0
.

The constant
C
appearing in Theorem
4.1
will be discussed further, in the context of a concrete example, in Section
4.3
below.

Remark 4.1
.

Note that for many choices of
\pi_{0}
,
\ExpOp\left[\pi_{0}(U_{t}^{c})\right]
and
\ExpOp\left[\pi_{0}(\hat{U}_{t}^{c})\right]
decay to zero rapidly; for instance when
\ExpOp\left[\pi_{0}\right]
is Gaussian, or when
\ExpOp\left[\pi_{0}\right]
is the uniform distribution on a bounded neighbourhood of
\theta_{0}
, in which latter case
\ExpOp\left[\pi_{0}(\hat{U}_{t}^{c})\right]
is zero after finite time.
Of the other four terms in the definition of
H(t)
, two depend on
\gamma(\theta_{0})
, and two depend on
s_{1}
.
In metastable systems, it is well known that
\gamma(\theta_{0})
is exponentially small in
\sigma>0
, slowing down the PCCR.
The following assumption describes a setup that may be used to get around this problem, at least on finite time scales.

Assumption 4.3
.

There exists a domain
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a family of vector fields
\hat{V}_{\theta}
,
\theta\in\Theta
, such that
\hat{V}_{\theta}=V_{\theta}
on
O
and the family of SDEs

d\hat{X}\,=\,\hat{V}_{\theta}(\hat{X})\,dt+\sigma\,dW

(22)

satisfy Assumption
4.2
, with constant
\hat{\gamma}(\theta)
and Fisher information
\hat{s}_{1}
.
We define the exit time of (
18
) from
O
as
\displaystyle{\tau\,\coloneqq\,\inf\left\{t>0\,:\,X_{t}\in\partial O\right\}}
.

This assumption allows us to state our main result on metastable diffusions as follows.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let Assumption
4.3
hold.
Then, for fixed
\delta>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, letting

\hat{\epsilon}_{t}\,\coloneqq\,\delta/\hat{s}_{1}^{\alpha}t^{\alpha/2},\qquad\hat{F}_{t}\,\coloneqq\,B_{\hat{\epsilon}_{t}}(\theta_{0}),\quad\text{ and }\quad\hat{U}_{t}\,\coloneqq\,t^{1/2}\hat{I}_{\theta_{0}}^{1/2}B_{\hat{\epsilon}_{t}}(0),

it holds that
\hat{F}_{t}\rightarrow\{\theta_{0}\}
and
\hat{U}_{t}\rightarrow\Theta
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, and

\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{t}(\hat{F}_{t})\,<\,1-\hat{H}(t)^{1/2}\right]\,\leq\,\hat{H}(t)^{1/2}+\mathbb{P}[t>\tau],

(23)

where here, for a constant
\hat{C}>0
,

\hat{H}(t)\,\coloneqq\,\hat{C}\left(\left(\frac{1-e^{-\hat{\gamma}(\theta_{0})t}}{\hat{\gamma}(\theta_{0})t}\right)^{1/2}+\left(\frac{1-e^{-\hat{\gamma}(\theta_{0})t}}{\hat{\gamma}(\theta_{0})t}\right)+\frac{1}{\hat{s}_{1}^{2}t}+\frac{1}{\hat{s}_{1}^{3}t^{3/2}}+\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\pi_{0}(\hat{U}_{t}^{c})\right]\right)

The constant
\hat{C}
appearing in Theorem
4.2
will be compared with the constant
C
from Theorem
4.1
, in the context of a concrete example, in Section
4.3
below.

Through Theorem
4.1
, we see that in the case of diffusions, the learning rate is determined by 1. the spectral gap of our observed process and 2. the smallest singular value of the Fisher information matrix.
In the case of diffusions with no true stable state, the spectral gap may not exist, while in the case of a stable diffusions with several metastable states contained within a globally stable domain, the spectral gap may be quite small.
Additionally, as we see in the following example, metastability may lead to problems of parameter identifiability when each of the metastable regions of a systems have qualitatively similar behaviour.
However, if one restricts attention to a single metastable region of the system, both of these issues may be overcome, as the restricted system will be truly stable with a relatively large spectral gap.
As we illustrate in the examples of Section
4.3
, this may substantially improve the learning rate in certain scenarios.
The case where restricting attention to a single metastable state increases the spectral gap of the system, but decreases the Fisher information, is discussed in Section
5
.

&
4.2

To prove Theorem
4.1
, we begin by demonstrating that the family of SDEs given by (
18
) is LAN under Assumption
4.2
.
By Girsanov’s Theorem, the likelihood ratio of a given model probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}^{t}
given
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}^{t}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative

\frac{d\mathbb{P}^{t}_{\theta}}{d\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}^{t}}\,=\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}V_{\theta}^{2}-V_{\theta_{0}}^{2}\,ds+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}V_{\theta}-V_{\theta_{0}}\,dX_{s}\right),

where we use the shorthand
V_{\theta}=V_{\theta}(X_{s})
,
V_{\theta}^{2}=\left\|V_{\theta}\right\|^{2}
, and
V_{\theta}\,dX_{s}=\left\langle V_{\theta},dX_{s}\right\rangle
.
See
[
49
, Theorem 1.12]
for a proof in the setting of one-dimensional diffusions.
Under Assumption
4.2
, an asymptotic version of the following result on the LAN property of
(\mathbb{P}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is known.
However, in this section we sharpen the result, and obtain precise bounds that hold in the finite time regime.

Proposition 4.1

(Local asymptotic normality)
.

Under Assumption
4.2
,
(\mathbb{P}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is LAN at each
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
.
In particular, define
\phi_{t}=\phi_{t}(\theta_{0})\,\coloneqq\,t^{-1/2}I_{\theta_{0}}^{-1/2}
,
let

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}(X)\cdot u\,\coloneqq\,\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})[\phi_{t}u]}{\sigma^{2}}\left[dX_{s}-V_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})\,ds\right],\quad\text{ and }

(24)

\displaystyle r_{t}(X,u)\,\coloneqq\,\left(\left\|u\right\|^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\left\|\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})[\phi_{t}u]\right\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,ds\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial_{\theta}^{2}V_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})[\phi_{t}u]^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,[dX_{s}-V_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})ds]

\displaystyle+O\left(\sigma^{3}\left\|\phi_{t}u\right\|^{3}(t+X_{t})\right)\,.

Writing
\Delta_{t}=\Delta_{t}(X)
and
r_{t}=r_{t}(X,u)
, for all
t>0
,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
X\in C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{d})
it holds that

\frac{d\mathbb{P}^{t}_{\theta_{0}+\phi_{t}u}}{d\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}^{t}}\,=\,\exp\left(\Delta_{t}\cdot u-\left\|u\right\|^{2}+r_{t}\right),

(25)

where
r_{t}\rightarrow 0
in
\pi_{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
-mean and
\Delta_{t}
tends to a
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
random variable in
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
-mean square as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

We again use the shorthand
V_{\theta}=V_{\theta}(X_{t}),\,\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta}=\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta}(X_{t})
.
Taking second order Taylor approximations of
V_{\theta}
and
V_{\theta}^{2}
in the parameter
\theta
centered at
\theta_{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{\theta}^{t}}{d\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}^{t}}

\displaystyle=\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}V_{\theta}^{2}-V_{\theta_{0}}^{2}\,ds+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}V_{\theta}-V_{\theta_{0}}\,dX_{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\,\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}V_{\theta_{0}}\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}[\theta-\theta_{0}]+\left[\left\|\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}\right\|^{2}+V_{\theta_{0}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}V_{\theta_{0}}\right]\left[\theta-\theta_{0}\right]^{2}+O(\left\|\theta-\theta_{0}\right\|^{3})\,ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}[\theta-\theta_{0}]+\partial_{\theta}^{2}V_{\theta_{0}}[\theta-\theta_{0}]^{2}+O(\left\|\theta-\theta_{0}\right\|^{3})\,dX_{s}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\,\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}V_{\theta_{0}}\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}[\theta-\theta_{0}]+\left[\left\|\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}\right\|^{2}+V_{\theta_{0}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}V_{\theta_{0}}\right]\left[\theta-\theta_{0}\right]^{2}\,ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}[\theta-\theta_{0}]+\partial_{\theta}^{2}V_{\theta_{0}}[\theta-\theta_{0}]^{2}\,dX_{s}+O\left(\sigma^{-2}\left\|\theta-\theta_{0}\right\|^{3}(t+X_{t})\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\,\exp\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}}{\sigma^{2}}[\theta-\theta_{0}]\,[dX_{s}-V_{\theta_{0}}ds]-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\left\|\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}[\theta-\theta_{0}]\right\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial_{\theta}^{2}V_{\theta_{0}}[\theta-\theta_{0}]^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,[dX_{s}-V_{\theta_{0}}ds]+O\left(\sigma^{-2}\left\|\theta-\theta_{0}\right\|^{3}(t+X_{t})\right)\bigg).

Now, making the transformation
u\coloneqq\phi_{t}^{-1}[\theta-\theta_{0}]
(noting that
I_{\theta_{0}}^{-1/2}
is a
p\times p
matrix with non-zero singular values, and hence invertible), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbb{P}^{t}_{\theta_{0}+\phi_{t}u}}{d\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}^{t}}

\displaystyle=\,\exp\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}}{\sigma^{2}}[\phi_{t}u]\,[dX_{s}-V_{\theta_{0}}ds]-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\left\|\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}[\phi_{t}u]\right\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial_{\theta}^{2}V_{\theta_{0}}[\phi_{t}u]^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,[dX_{s}-V_{\theta_{0}}ds]+O\left(\sigma^{-2}\left\|\phi_{t}u\right\|^{3}(t+X_{t})\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\,\exp\left(\Delta_{t}\cdot u-\left\|u\right\|^{2}+r_{t}\right),

by the definitions of
\Delta_{t}
and
r_{t}
in (
24
).
It remains to be shown that
\Delta_{t}
converges to a standard normal random variable in
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
-mean square, and that
r_{t}
converges to zero in
\pi_{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
-mean.
Let
\Delta_{\infty}
and
\Delta_{\infty}^{\prime}
be standard
p
- and
d
-dimensional standard normal random variables, respectively.
Note that for a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the equalities

dX_{s}-V_{\theta_{0}}\,ds\,=\,\sigma\,dW_{s}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Delta_{\infty}\cdot u\,=\,u\cdot I_{p,d}\cdot\Delta_{\infty}^{\prime}\,=\,t^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{t}u\cdot I_{p,d}\cdot dW_{s}

hold in
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
-law.
Consequently, from the definition of
\phi_{t}
we have by Itô’s isometry that

\displaystyle\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty})\cdot u\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|t^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{-1}\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}\cdot I_{\theta_{0}}^{-1/2}\cdot u\cdot\,dW_{s}-t^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{t}u\cdot I_{p,d}\cdot\,dW_{s}\right\|^{2}\right]

(26)

\displaystyle=\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\sigma^{-1}\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}\cdot I_{\theta_{0}}^{-1/2}\cdot u-u\cdot I_{p,d}\right\|^{2}\,ds\right]

for
u\in\Theta
.
Taking
t\rightarrow\infty
and applying Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem, together with Fatou’s lemma,

\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty})\cdot u\right\|^{2}\right]\,\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow\infty]{}\,\left\|\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\sigma^{-1}\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}(\xi_{1})\right]\cdot I_{\theta_{0}}^{-1/2}\cdot u-u\cdot I_{p,d}\right\|^{2},

(27)

where
\xi_{1}
is another random variable distributed as
\mu_{\theta_{0}}
under
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
.
The last quantity on the right hand side of (
27
) is zero, by the definition of
I_{\theta_{0}}^{-1/2}
and
I_{p,d}
.
We defer the proof of the decay of
r_{t}
to Proposition
4.2
below.
∎

We now study the rates of decay of
\varepsilon_{\Delta}
and
\varepsilon_{r}
under Assumption
4.2
.
This result demonstrates how the certainty of the contraction of the posterior distribution about the true parameter value is controlled by the spectral gap
\gamma(\theta_{0})
of (
18

\theta=\theta_{0}
and the smallest singular value of
\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}(\xi)\right]

Proposition 4.2
.

Let Assumption
4.2
hold.
Then, with
\gamma(\theta)
as the constant appearing in (
19
), there exist
c_{\Delta},c_{r}>0
depending solely on
V_{\theta_{0}}
and
\pi_{0}
, such that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|\left(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty}\right)\right\|^{2}\right]\,\leq\,c_{\Delta}\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(\theta_{0})t}}{\gamma(\theta_{0})t}\qquad\text{and}

(28)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{r}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\int_{\Theta}\left|r_{t}\right|\,\pi_{0}(du)\right]\,\leq\,c_{r}\left(\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(\theta_{0})t}}{\gamma(\theta_{0})t}+\frac{1}{s_{1}^{2}t}+\frac{1}{s_{1}^{3}t^{3/2}}\right).

Proof.

As in (
26
), using
u\cdot I_{p,d}=I_{d,p}\cdot u
, we have that

\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|(\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty})\cdot u\right\|^{2}\right]\,=\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\left(\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}\cdot\sigma^{-1}I_{\theta_{0}}^{-1/2}-I_{d,p}\right)\cdot u\right\|^{2}\,ds\right].

Consequently,

\displaystyle\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{\infty}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}\cdot\sigma^{-1}I_{\theta_{0}}^{-1/2}-I_{d,p}\right\|^{2}\,ds\right]

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\,\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}g_{1}(X_{s})\,ds\right].

Now, recall the dual representation of the total variation distance: for any two probability measures
P,\,Q,
on a metric space
M
,

\left\|P-Q\right\|_{TV}\,=\,\frac{1}{2F}\sup_{\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\leq F}\left|P(f)-Q(f)\right|.

We assume that
\left\|g_{1}\right\|_{\infty}>0
, otherwise the result is trivial.
Then, due to (
4.1
) and the fact that
\mu_{\theta_{0}}(g_{1})=0
, which follows from (

), we see that

\displaystyle\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}g_{1}(X_{s})\,ds\right]

\displaystyle=\,t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[g_{1}(X_{s})\right]\,ds

(29)

\displaystyle\leq\,2^{-1}\left\|g_{1}\right\|_{\infty}^{-1}\int_{0}^{1}k(\theta)e^{-\gamma(\theta_{0})ts}\,ds

\displaystyle=\,2^{-1}\left\|g_{1}\right\|_{\infty}^{-1}k(\theta)\frac{1}{\gamma(\theta_{0})t}\left(1-e^{-\gamma(\theta_{0})t}\right),

completing the proof for
\varepsilon_{\Delta}(t)
.
Indeed, we may take
c_{\Delta}\,=\,2^{-1}\left\|g_{1}\right\|^{-1}_{\infty}k(\theta)
, which is well defined when
\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}
is not a constant.

The proof for the first term in
\varepsilon_{r}(t)
follows the same lines.
Indeed, using Assumption
4.2
, observe that the first term is

\displaystyle\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\int_{\Theta}\left|\left\|u\right\|^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{-2}\left\|\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})[\phi_{t}u]\right\|^{2}\,ds\right|\,\pi_{0}(du)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\,\int_{\Theta}\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left|\left\|u\right\|^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{-2}\left\|\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})[\phi_{t}u]\right\|^{2}\,ds\right|\rho_{0}(u)\right]\,\overline{\pi}(du)

\displaystyle\leq\,\int_{\Theta}\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left\|u\right\|^{2}-\left\|\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})[\sigma^{-1}I_{\theta_{0}}^{-1/2}u]\right\|^{2}\right|\rho_{0}(u)\,ds\right]\,\overline{\pi}(du)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\,\int_{\Theta}\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}g_{2}(u,X_{s})\,ds\right]\,\overline{\pi}(du)

\displaystyle\leq\,\left(2^{-1}\int_{\Theta}\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|g_{2}(u,\,\cdot\,)\right\|_{\infty}^{-1}\right]\,\overline{\pi}(du)\right)k(\theta)\frac{1}{\gamma(\theta_{0})t}\left(1-e^{-\gamma(\theta_{0})t}\right)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\,C^{\prime}\frac{1}{\gamma(\theta_{0})t}\left(1-e^{-\gamma(\theta_{0})t}\right),

where, similar to the constant
c_{\Delta}
, note that
C^{\prime}
is well defined when
\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta_{0}}
is not a constant.

To treat the
\pi_{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}

dX_{s}-V_{\theta_{0}}\,ds=\sigma\,dW_{s}
in
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
-law, and use Hölder’s inequality and Itô’s isometry to compute

\displaystyle\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\int_{\Theta}\,\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{-2}\partial_{\theta}^{2}V_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})[\phi_{t}u]^{2}\,dW_{s}\right\|\pi_{0}(d\theta)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\,\int_{\Theta}\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{\theta}^{2}V_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})[\sigma^{-1}\phi_{t}u]^{2}\rho_{0}(u)^{1/2}\,dW_{s}\right\|^{2}\right]\,\overline{\pi}(du)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Theta}\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[t^{-2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\partial_{\theta}^{2}V_{\theta_{0}}(X_{s})[\sigma^{-1}I_{\theta_{0}}^{-1/2}u]^{2}\rho_{0}(u)^{1/2}\right\|^{2}\,ds\right]\,\overline{\pi}(du)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|V_{\theta_{0}}\right\|_{C^{2}}\sigma^{-2}\left\|I_{\theta_{0}}^{-1/2}\right\|^{2}\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\int_{\Theta}\left\|u\right\|^{4}\,\pi_{0}(du)\right]^{1/2}t^{-1}

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\,C^{\prime\prime}s_{1}^{-2}t^{-1}.

Finally, Assumption
4.2

Taking

4.3
Learning diffusions in a non-degenerate double well

Let us now consider a concrete example to which the theory developed above applies.
Theorems
4.1
&
4.2
, together with the small noise asymptotics of the spectra of diffusions generators summarized in Appendix
A
, allow us to precisely estimate PCRs, PCCRs, m-PCRs, and m-PCCRs.
In this example we see that the PCR and m-PCR are similar, while the m-PCCR decays at a rate several orders of magnitude faster than that of the PCCR.

Consider a diffusion on
\mathbb{R}
, governed by the SDE

dX_{t}\,=\,\nabla U_{\theta}(X_{t})\,dt+\sigma\,dW_{t},

(30)

where
\sigma>0
is a small parameter controlling noise amplitude, which we treat as fixed,
\theta\in\Theta=\mathbb{R}
, and
U_{\theta}
is the double well potential

U_{\theta}(x)\,=\,\left(x^{2}+2x+\theta\right)\left(x^{2}-2x\right).

With parameter
\theta_{0}=0
, the system (
30
) exhibits two metastable states at
\pm\sqrt{2}
, at the bottoms of two wells with depths equal to
4
.

We assume that we have obtained a time series generated by (
30
) with
\sigma=0.1
and
\theta_{0}=0
, and wish to learn the parameter
\theta\in\Theta
from an initial guess given in the form of a Gaussian prior
\pi_{0}
.
For any
\theta\in\Theta
, the system (
30
) is ergodic, with a unique ergodic measure given by

\mu_{\theta}(dx)\,=\,e^{-\sigma^{-2}U_{\theta}(x)}\,dx.

It is straightforward to check that (
30
) satisfies the conditions of Theorem
A.1
in Appendix
A
, so that (
30
) satisfies Assumption
4.1
for some
c(\theta),\,\gamma(\theta)>0
.
Indeed, up to a small multiplicative constant which is less than
2
, quantified in Appendix
A
, we have

\gamma(\theta_{0})\,\simeq\,\left(8^{3/2}\pi^{-1}\right)e^{-8\sigma^{-2}}\qquad\text{ for the parameter value $\theta_{0}=0$}.

(31)

In particular,
\gamma(\theta_{0})
becomes arbitrarily small for sufficiently small
\sigma>0
, due to the bistable nature of our system.
See (
43
) in Appendix
A
.
For
\sigma=0.1
, this is

\gamma(\theta_{0})\,\simeq\,(1.389\ldots)\times 10^{-11}.

The Fisher information may be computed, in this case, as

s\,\coloneqq\,\left|\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\partial_{\theta}\nabla U_{\theta}(\xi)\right]\right|\,=\,\left|2\left(1-\ExpOp_{\theta_{0}}\left[\xi\right]\right)\right|\,=\,2,

5
Metastable decomopositions of parameter space

In the sections above, we have seen that we may overcome the slow-down of a Bayesian posterior caused by the small spectral gap of a metastable diffusion by restricting attention to exactly one of its metastable states.
However, there is the possibility that the Fisher information of the system restricted to a single metastable state is much less than the Fisher information of the whole system.
But, if the Fisher information in each metastable state is somehow independent of that in the other states, in a sense to be made precise, then it seems natural that we might be able to speed up learning by focusing attention on each metastable state, marginalizing the “slow to learn” directions in parameter space, and then tensoring the “sub-posteriors” constructed from each metastable subsystem together.
In this way, as we demonstrate, one overcomes the slow down in Bayesian inference caused by the small spectral gap of the whole system and the potentially small Fisher information of the metastable subsystems.
To make this all precise, we introduce the following assumption.
For the sake of not overburdening our notation, we focus on the setting of systems with precisely two metastable states, though the same arguments generalize to the case of
n\in\mathbb{N}
metastable states, suitable modifications being made.

6
Discussion

The Bayesian PCR of a statistical model consisting of ergodic diffusions can be precisely characterized in terms of the Fisher information of the model, while the PCCR is characterized as a combination of the Fisher information and spectral gaps of the model.
In the case of metastable diffusions, the spectral gap is very small, leading to a slow PCCR, which may limit the ability to make inferences from observations of metastable time series due to the amount of data needed.
This slow down in the PCCR for metastable diffusions may be overcome by limiting attention to a single metastable state, as described in Section
4
and demonstrated for a diffusion in a double well potential in Section
4.3
.
Restricting attention to a single metastable subsystem may have the added benefit of increasing the Fisher information, in particular in the non-identifiable setting, where the Fisher information may be zero due to a degenerate symmetry in the modeled system.
For instance, in Section
4.4
, we see how restricting attention to one metastable state of a metastable system can make a non-identifiable model identifiable by removing such symmetry.
On the other hand, in the case where each metastable state of a system is only affected by changes in a subspace of parameter space, restricting to a single metastable state may slow down the PCR by leading to a small Fisher information.
To circumvent this, in Section
5
we have seen how one may further restrict the posterior distribution constructed from each metastable subsystem to the directions in parameter space which the metastable subsystem can see, and then take the product of the marginalized “sub” posteriors.
Doing so allows us to overcome possible slowdowns in both the PCR and PCCR of metastable systems caused by a small spectral gap of the whole system, and a small Fisher information of the sub-systems.
Future work may focus on algorithmic implementations of this theory, as well as applications to the foundations of time series inference and machine learning.
It would also be of interest to understand how metastability of the statistical models would influence decompositions of the parameter space
\Theta
when
\Theta
is fundamentally nonlinear, as in the nonparametric setting where
\Theta
is infinite dimensional.

.

Before proceeding, we fix some notation.
For a negative semi-definite essentially self adjoint operator
\mathcal{A}:D(\mathcal{A})\subset H\rightarrow H
on a separable Hilbert space
H
, let
\spec(-\mathcal{A})
denote the spectrum of
-\mathcal{A}
.
Let
\lambda(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\inf\spec(-\mathcal{A})
denote the bottom eigenvalue of the spectrum of
-\mathcal{A}
, and let
\gamma(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\inf\spec(-\mathcal{A})\backslash\{\lambda(\mathcal{A})\}
be the next closest point in the spectrum of
-\mathcal{A}
.
Let
\kappa(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\gamma(\mathcal{A})-\lambda(\mathcal{A})
denote the spectral gap of
\mathcal{A}
.

Assumption A.1
.

The system (
41
) has global in time solutions, and is uniformly ergodic with unique ergodic measure
\mu
.
Moreover, (
41
) has initial distribution
\nu=h\mu
with
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mu)
, and

1.

U
is a non-negative
C^{3}
Morse function satisfying the growth condition
[
65
, Assumption 1.5]
.

2.

The potential
U
has exactly
n\in\mathbb{N}
local minima, labeled
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},
and for
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
the Hessian
H_{k}\coloneqq\nabla^{2}U(x_{k})
is strictly positive definite.

3.

Between any two minima
x_{i},x_{j}
, the saddle height between
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
is attained at a unique critical point of
U
, denoted
s_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

4.

For each pair
i,j
,
\tilde{H}_{i,j}\coloneqq\nabla^{2}U(s_{i,j})
has precisely one negative eigenvalue, denoted
\lambda_{i,j}
.

5.

The minima
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
are ordered such that
x_{1}
is a global minimum of
U
, and for some
\eta>0
,

U(s_{1,2})-U(x_{2})\,\geq\,U(s_{1,i})-U(x_{i})+\eta,\qquad i\in\{3,\ldots,n\}.

Under Assumption
A.1
, the Markov generator
\mathcal{L}
of (
41
) exists in the
L^{2}_{\mu}
-topology, and its action on
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mu)
can be expressed as

\mathcal{L}f\,=\,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta f-\nabla U\cdot\nabla f,

(42)

In this appendix, we collect rigorous results on the spectral properties of
\mathcal{L}
, and its restriction to neighbourhoods of the
x_{k}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions.
These results justify our estimates on the constant
\gamma(\theta_{0})
appearing in Assumption
4.1
in the case of metastable diffusions, and on the rate of escape via estimates on the distribution of
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\left[t<\tau\right]
, as appears in Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem A.1
.

Let Assumption
A.1
hold.
Then,
\lambda(\mathcal{L})=0
is a simple eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}
, and

\gamma(\mathcal{L})\,\simeq\,\left(\frac{2\left|\lambda_{1,2}\right|\sqrt{\det\nabla^{2}H_{2}}}{\pi\sqrt{\left|\det\nabla^{2}\tilde{H}_{1,2}\right|}}\right)\exp\left(2\frac{U(x_{2})-U(s_{1,2})}{\sigma^{2}}\right),

(43)

equality holding up to a multiplicative constant of order
1+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}}\left|\ln\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right|^{3/2}
.
Moreover,

\left\|\mathbb{P}\left[X_{t}\in\,\cdot\,\right]-\mu(\,\cdot\,)\right\|_{TV}\,\leq\,e^{-\gamma(\mathcal{L})t/2}\left\|h-1\right\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}}.

(44)

Proof.

By
[
65
]
, (
41
) has a spectral gap
\gamma(\mathcal{L})
such that (
43
) holds up to a multiplicative constant of order
1+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}}\left|\ln\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right|^{3/2}
(we note that
[
65
]
requires

, but this translates to our setting by simply adding a constant to

Equation (
44
) then follows from
[
10
, Theorem 2.1]
.
∎

As suggested by the discussion of Section
4
on ergodic diffusions, to study the metastable behaviour of (
41
) near each
x_{k}
we consider a modified SDE with a single well at
x_{k}
.
Specifically, fix
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\delta>0
such that
B_{\delta}(x_{k})
is strictly contained in the basin of attraction of
x_{k}
, and fix a smooth potential
\hat{U}_{\delta}
satisfying

1.

\hat{U}_{\delta}=U
in the ball
B_{\delta}(x_{k})
, and

2.

\hat{U}_{\delta}
is uniformly convex, so there exists
u_{2}>0
such that
\nabla^{2}\hat{U}_{\delta}(x)\geq u_{2}1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Then, consider

dX_{t}\,=\,\nabla\hat{U}_{\delta}(X_{t})\,dt+\sigma\,dW_{t},

(45)

and its associated Markov generator
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}
defined in the topology of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\hat{\mu}_{\delta})
, where
\hat{\mu}_{\delta}
is the unique ergodic measure of (
45
) (guaranteed to exist by Assumption
A.1
and the construction of
\hat{U}_{\delta}
).
The generator
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}
acts on
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\hat{\mu}_{\delta})
as

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}f\,=\,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta f+\nabla\hat{U}_{\delta}\cdot\nabla f.

(46)

Theorem A.2
.

Let Assumption
A.1
hold.
Then, (
45
) has a global in time solution
(\hat{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and a unique ergodic measure
\hat{\mu}
.
Moreover,

\left\|\mathbb{P}[\hat{X}_{t}\in\,\cdot\,]-\hat{\mu}(\,\cdot\,)\right\|_{TV}\,\leq\,e^{-\hat{\gamma}t},

(47)

where for all
\sigma>0
,
\hat{\gamma}=u_{2}
.

Proof.

By the Bakry-Emery criterion, (
45
) satisfies a Poincaré inequality with constant
1/u_{2}
, see
[
4
, Proposition 4.8.1]
.
The contraction (
47
) then follows from
[
10
, Theorem 2.1]
.
∎

Now, with
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\delta>0
as above, define

\tau_{\delta}\,\coloneqq\,\inf\left\{t>0\,:\,X_{t}\in\partial B_{\delta}(x_{k})\right\},

and for
f\in L^{2}(B_{\delta}(x_{k}),\mu)
set

P_{t}^{\delta}f(x)\,\coloneqq\,\ExpOp\left[f(X_{t})1_{\{t<\tau_{\delta}\}}\right].

Just as for the Markov semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the sub-Markov semigroup
(P_{t}^{\delta})_{t\geq 0}
is strongly continuous in the topology of
L^{2}(B_{\delta}(x_{k}),\mu)
.
It therefore has a strong generator, which we denote by
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
, and which for
f\in C^{2}_{0}(B_{\delta}(x_{k}))\cap L^{2}(B_{\delta}(x_{k}),\mu)
can be expressed as

\mathcal{L}_{\delta}f(x)\,=\,\beta\Delta f(x)-\nabla U(x)\cdot\nabla f(x).

That is,
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
has the same form as
\mathcal{L}
, but with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
B_{\delta}(x_{k})
.
Now, we have the following result on the escape time of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
from the well
B_{\delta}(x_{k})
.

Theorem A.3
.

\lambda(\mathcal{L}_{\delta})
is a strictly negative simple eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
, and the spectral gap
\kappa(\mathcal{L}_{\delta})
is bounded away from zero uniformly in
\sigma>0
.
Finally, for a constant
c>0
independent of
\sigma>0
, up to a multiplicative error term of order

it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left[t<\tau_{\delta}\right]\,=\,ce^{-\lambda(\mathcal{L}_{\delta})t}+O\left(e^{-\kappa(\mathcal{L}_{\delta})t}\right).

(48)

Moreover, up to a multiplicative error term of order
1+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}}\left|\ln\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right|^{3/2}
, we have

\lambda(\mathcal{L}_{\delta})\,\simeq\,\left(\frac{2\left|\lambda_{1,2}\right|\sqrt{\det\nabla^{2}H_{2}}}{\pi\sqrt{\left|\det\nabla^{2}\tilde{H}_{1,2}\right|}}\right)\exp\left(2\frac{U(x_{2})-U(s_{1,2})}{\sigma^{2}}\right).

(49)

Proof.

The expression for

can be found, for instance, in
[

]

The asymptotic behaviour of

follows from

∎
