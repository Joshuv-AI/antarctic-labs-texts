Impact of Dataset Properties on Membership Inference Vulnerability of Deep Transfer Learning
Marlon Tobaben, Hibiki Ito, Joonas Jälkö, Yuan He, Antti Honkela

Abstract
Membership inference attacks (MIAs) are used to test practical privacy of machine learning models. MIAs complement formal guarantees from differential privacy (DP) under a more realistic adversary model. We analyse MIA vulnerability of fine-tuned neural networks both empirically and theoretically, the latter using a simplified model of fine-tuning. We show that the vulnerability of non-DP models when measured as the attacker advantage at fixed false positive rate reduces according to a simple power law as the number of examples per class increases, even for the most vulnerable points, but the dataset size needed for adequate protection of the most vulnerable points is very large.

Introduction

Membership inference attacks
(
Shokri et al. 2017
;
Carlini et al. 2022
, MIAs;)
and differential privacy
(
Dwork et al. 2006
, DP;)
provide complementary means of deriving lower and upper bounds for the privacy loss of a machine learning algorithm.
Yet, the two operate under slightly different threat models.
DP implicitly assumes a very powerful adversary with access to all training data except the target point and provides guarantees against every target point.
MIAs assume an often more realistic adversary model with access to just the data distribution, and the unknown training data becoming latent variables introducing stochasticity into the attack, but the practical evaluation is statistical and cannot provide universal guarantees.

In this paper, we seek to explore the area between DP and typical MIA evaluations.
Inspired by an empirical finding that average MIA vulnerability of neural network fine-tuning strongly reduces as the number of samples in the target class increases (see
Figure

1
), we develop theory of optimal MIA against a simple model of neural network fine-tuning and reproduce the decrease in vulnerability.
Furthermore, the theoretical model predicts that the vulnerability of all individual samples should reduce as the number of samples increases, which we are able to verify empirically.

Figure 1
:
We observe a power-law relation between MIA vulnerability and
S
(shots, i.e., examples per class) when attacking a ViT-B Head fine-tuned using LiRA. The solid line is median and the error bars the min/max bounds for the Clopper-Pearson CIs over six seeds.

To achieve our goal, we theoretically analyze and systematically apply two state-of-the-art black-box MIAs, LiRA
(
Carlini et al. 2022
)
and RMIA
(
Zarifzadeh et al. 2024
)
, to help understand practical privacy risks when fine-tuning deep-learning-based classifiers without DP protections. For the theoretical model that we analyse, LiRA is the optimal attack by the Neyman–Pearson lemma
(
Neyman and Pearson 1933
)
. Under the black-box threat model, in which the adversary does not have access to the model parameters, LiRA and RMIA have been shown to empirically outperform other attacks, especially when the number of shadow models is sufficiently large.

We focus on transfer learning using fine-tuning because this is increasingly used for all practical applications of deep learning and especially important when labeled examples are limited, which is often the case in privacy-sensitive applications.
Our case study focuses on understanding and quantifying factors that influence the vulnerability of non-DP deep transfer learning models to MIA.
In particular, we theoretically study the relationship between the number of examples per class, which we denote as shots (
S
), and MIA vulnerability (true positive rate
tpr
at fixed false positive rate
fpr
) for a simplified model of fine-tuning, and derive a power-law relationship (
Figure

1
) in the form

\displaystyle\log(\textsc{tpr}-\textsc{fpr})=-\beta_{S}\log(S)-\beta_{0}.

(1)

We complement the theoretical analysis with extensive experiments over many datasets with varying sizes, in the transfer learning setting for image classification tasks, and observe the same power-law.
Based on extrapolation from our results, the number of examples per each class that are needed for adequate protection of the most vulnerable samples appears very high.

Related work

There has been evidence that classification models with more classes are more vulnerable to MIA
(
Shokri et al. 2017
)
, models trained on fewer samples can be more vulnerable
(
Chen et al. 2020
;
Németh et al. 2023
)
, and classes with less examples tend to be more vulnerable
(
Chang and Shokri 2021
;
Kulynych et al. 2022
;
Tonni et al. 2020
)
.
A larger generalisation error, which is related to dataset size, has also been shown to be sufficient for MIA success
(
Song and Mittal 2021
)
, though not necessary
(
Yeom et al. 2018
)
.
Similarly, minority subgroups tend to be more affected by DP
(
Suriyakumar et al.
;
Bagdasaryan et al. 2019
)
.

Feldman and Zhang 2020
showed that neural networks trained from scratch can memorise a large fraction of their training data, while the memorisation is greatly reduced for fine-tuning.
Additionally,
Tobaben et al. 2023
reported how the MIA vulnerability of few-shot
image classification is affected by the number of shots.

Yu et al. 2023
studied the relationship between the MIA vulnerability and individual privacy parameters for different classes.
Recently, worst-case MIA vulnerability has gained more attention
(
Guépin et al. 2024
;
Meeus et al. 2024
;
Azize and Basu 2024
)
.
Nonetheless, the prior works do not consider the rate of change in the vulnerability evaluated at a low
fpr
, as dataset properties change.
Our work significantly expands on these works by explicitly identifying a quantitative relationship between dataset properties and MIA vulnerability, i.e., the power-law in
Equation

1
.

Background

Notation
for the properties of the training dataset
\mathcal{\mathcal{D}}
:

1

C
for the number of classes

2

S
for shots (examples per class)

3

\mathcal{|\mathcal{D}|}
for training dataset size (
\mathcal{|\mathcal{D}|}=CS
).

We denote the number of MIA shadow models with
M
.

Membership inference attacks (MIAs)
aim to infer whether a particular sample was part of the training set of the targeted model
(
Shokri et al. 2017
)
. Thus, they can be used to determine lower bounds on the privacy leakage of models to complement the theoretical upper bounds obtained through DP.

Likelihood Ratio attack

(
Carlini et al. 2022
,
LiRA
;)

While many different MIAs have been proposed
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
, in this work we consider the Likelihood Ratio Attack (LiRA). LiRA is a strong attack that assumes an attacker that has black-box access to the attacked model, knows the training data distribution, the training set size, the model architecture, hyperparameters and training algorithm.
Based on this information, the attacker can train so-called shadow models
(
Shokri et al. 2017
)
which imitate the model under attack but for which the attacker knows the training dataset.

LiRA exploits the observation that the loss function value used to train a model is often lower for the examples that were part of the training set compared to those that were not.
For a target tuple
(\bm{x},y)
, where
y
is a label, LiRA trains the shadow models:

1

with
(\bm{x},y)
as a part of the training set (
(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}
) and

2

without
\bm{x}
in the training set (
(\bm{x},y)\notin\mathcal{D}
)

.
After training the shadow models,
(\bm{x},y)
is passed through the shadow models, and
based on the losses (or predictions) two Gaussian distributions are formed:
one for the losses of
(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}
shadow models, and one for the

(\bm{x},y)\notin\mathcal{D}
.
Finally, the attacker computes the loss for the point
\bm{x}
using the model under
attack and determines using a likelihood ratio test on the distributions built
from the shadow models whether it is more likely that
(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}
or
(\bm{x},y)\notin\mathcal{D}
.
When the true distributions of the shadow models are Gaussians, LiRA is the optimal attack
provided by the Neyman–Pearson lemma
(
Neyman and Pearson 1933
)
.
We use an optimization by
Carlini et al. 2022
for performing LiRA for
multiple models and points without training a computationally infeasible number
of shadow models. It relies on sampling the shadow datasets in a way that each sample is in expectation half of the time included in the training dataset of a shadow model and half of the time not. At attack time each model will be attacked once using all other models as shadow models.

Robust Membership Inference Attack

(
Zarifzadeh et al. 2024
,
RMIA
;)
RMIA is a new MIA algorithm, which aims to improve performance when the number of shadow models is limited. We show both theoretically and empirically that the power-law also holds for RMIA.

Measuring MIA vulnerability
Using the chosen MIA score of our attack,
we can build a binary classifier to predict whether a sample belongs to the
training data or not. The accuracy profile of such classifier
can be used to measure the success of the MIA. More specifically, throughout
the rest of the paper, we will use the true positive rate (
tpr
) at a
specific false positive rate (
fpr
) as a measure for the vulnerability.
Identifying even a small number of examples with high confidence is considered
harmful
(
Carlini et al. 2022
)
and thus we focus on the regions of small
fpr
.

Measuring the uncertainty for
tpr

The
tpr
values from the LiRA-based classifier can be seen as maximum
likelihood-estimators for the probability of producing true positives among the
positive samples. Since we have a finite number of samples for our estimation,
it is important to estimate the uncertainty in these estimators. Therefore, when
we report the
tpr
values for a single repeat of the learning algorithm, we
estimate the stochasticity of the
tpr
estimate by using Clopper-Pearson
intervals
(
Clopper and Pearson 1934
)
. Given
tp
true positives
among
p
positives, the
1-\alpha
confidence Clopper-Pearson interval for the

tpr
is given as

\begin{split}&B(\alpha/2;\textsc{tp},\textsc{p}-\textsc{tp}+1)<\textsc{tpr}<B(1-\alpha/2;\textsc{tp}+1,\textsc{p}-\textsc{tp}),\end{split}

(2)

where
B(q;a,b)
is the
q
th-quantile of
\text{Beta}(a,b)
distribution.

3
Theoretical analysis

In this section, we seek to theoretically understand the impact of the dataset properties on the MIA vulnerability.
It is known that different data points exhibit different levels of MIA vulnerability depending on the underlying distribution
(
Aerni et al. 2024
;
Leemann et al. 2024
, e.g. )
.
Therefore, we start with analysing
per-example
MIA vulnerabilities.
In order to quantitatively relate dataset properties to these vulnerabilities, a simplified model is formulated.
Within this model, we prove a power-law between the per-example vulnerability and the number
S
of examples per class.
Finally, the per-example power-law is analytically extended to
average-case
MIA vulnerability, for which we provide empirical evidence in
Section

4
.
We primarily focus on the analysis of LiRA, since it is the optimal attack in our simplified model. In
Appendix

B
we show that similar theoretical results also hold for RMIA.

3.1
Preliminaries

First, let us restate the MIA score from LiRA as defined by
Carlini et al. 2022
. Denoting the logit of a target model
\mathcal{M}
applied on a target data point
(\bm{x},y)
as
\ell(\mathcal{M}(\bm{x}),y)
, LiRA computes the MIA score as the likelihood ratio

\displaystyle\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x})=\frac{p(\ell(\mathcal{M}(\bm{x}),y)\mid\mathbb{Q}_{\text{in}}(\bm{x},y))}{p(\ell(\mathcal{M}(\bm{x}),y)\mid\mathbb{Q}_{\text{out}}(\bm{x},y))},

(3)

where the
\mathbb{Q}_{\text{in}/\text{out}}
denote the hypotheses that
(\bm{x},y)
was or was not in the training set of
\mathcal{M}
.
Carlini et al. 2022
approximate the IN/OUT hypotheses as normal distributions. Denoting
t_{\bm{x}}=\ell(\mathcal{M}(\bm{x}),y)
, the score becomes

\displaystyle\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x})=\frac{\mathcal{N}(t_{\bm{x}};\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x}),\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x})^{2})}{\mathcal{N}(t_{\bm{x}};\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x}),\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x})^{2})},

(4)

where the
\hat{\mu}_{\text{in/out}}(\bm{x})
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\text{in/out}}(\bm{x})
are the means and standard deviations for the IN/OUT shadow model losses for
(\bm{x},y)
. Larger values of
\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x})
suggest that
(\bm{x},y)
is more likely in the training set and vice versa. Now, to build a classifier from this score, the LiRA tests if
\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x})>\tau
for some threshold
\tau
. Note that the attacker only has a finite set of shadow models to estimate the IN/OUT parameters. Therefore, the MIA scores become random variables over the true population level IN/OUT distributions.

3.2
Computing the
tpr
for LiRA

Using the LiRA formulation of
Equation

4
, the
tpr
for the target point
(\bm{x},y)
for LiRA is defined as

\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x})=\Pr_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}\sim{\mathbb{D}}^{|\mathcal{D}|},\phi^{M}}\left(\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x})\geq\tau\mid(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}\right),

(5)

where
\tau
is a threshold that defines a rejection region of the likelihood ratio test, and
\phi^{M}
denotes the randomness in shadow set sampling and shadow model training (see
Section

A.1
for derivation).

We define the average-case
tpr
for LiRA by taking the expectation over the data distribution:

\overline{\textsc{tpr}}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}=\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{x},y)\sim{\mathbb{D}}}[\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x})]

(6)

3.3
Per-example MIA vulnerability

Although LiRA models
t_{\bm{x}}
by a normal distribution, we consider a more general case where the true distribution of
t_{\bm{x}}
is of the location-scale family.
That is,

t_{\bm{x}}=\begin{cases}\mu_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x})+\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x})Z&\text{ if }(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}\\
\mu_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x})+\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x})Z&\text{ if }(\bm{x},y)\notin\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}},\end{cases}

(7)

where
Z
has the standard location and unit scale, and
\mu_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x}),\mu_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x})
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x}),\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x})
are the locations and scales of IN/OUT distributions of
t_{\bm{x}}
.
We assume that the target and shadow datasets have a sufficient number of examples.
This allows us to also assume that
\hat{\sigma}(\bm{x})=\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x})=\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x})
and
\sigma(\bm{x})=\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x})=\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x})
, where
\hat{\sigma}(\bm{x})
is the standard deviation of
t_{\bm{x}}
estimated from shadow models and
\sigma(\bm{x})
is the true scale parameter of
t_{\bm{x}}
. (See
Section

A.2
for the validity of these assumptions).
The following result reduces the LiRA vulnerability to the location and scale parameters of
t_{\bm{x}}
.

Lemma 1

(Per-example LiRA vulnerability)
.

Suppose that the true distribution of
t_{\bm{x}}
is of location-scale family with locations
\mu_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x}),\mu_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x})
and scale
\sigma(\bm{x})
, and that LiRA models
t_{\bm{x}}
by
\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x}),\hat{\sigma}(\bm{x})^{2})
and
\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x}),\hat{\sigma}(\bm{x})^{2})
.
Assume that an attacker has access to the underlying distribution
{\mathbb{D}}
.
Then for a large enough number of examples per class and infinitely many shadow models, the LiRA vulnerability of a fixed target example is

\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}1-F_{Z}\left(F_{Z}^{-1}(1-\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x}))-\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x})-\mu_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x})}{\sigma(\bm{x})}\right)&\text{ if }\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x})>\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x})\\
F_{Z}\left(F_{Z}^{-1}(\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x}))-\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x})-\mu_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x})}{\sigma(\bm{x})}\right)&\text{ if }\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{x})<\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{x}),\end{cases}

(8)

where
F_{Z}
is the cdf of
Z
with the standard location and unit scale, assuming that the inverse of
F_{Z}
exists.

Proof.

See
Section

A.3
.
∎

Here we assume that an attacker trains shadow models with the true underlying distribution.
However, in real-world settings the precise underlying distribution may not be available for an attacker.
We relax this assumption in
Section

A.4
so that the attacker only needs an approximated underlying distribution for the optimal LiRA as in
Lemma

1
.

3.4
A simplified model of the optimal membership inference

Now we construct a simplified model of membership inference that streamlines the data generation and shadow model training.

We sample vectors on a high-dimensional unit sphere and classify them based on inner product with estimated class mean. This model is easier to analyse theoretically than real-world deep learning examples.
We generate the data and form the classifiers (which are our target models) as follows:

1.

For each class, we first sample a true class mean
m_{c}
on a high dimensional unit sphere that is orthogonal to all other true class means (
\forall i,j\in\{1,\dots,C\},i\neq j:m_{i}\perp m_{j}
).

2.

We sample
2S
vectors
\bm{x}_{c}
for each class. We assume that they are Gaussian distributed around the the true class mean
\bm{x}_{c}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{c},\Sigma)
where the
\Sigma
is the in-class covariance.

3.

For each ”target model” we randomly choose a subset of size
CS
from all generated vectors and compute per-class means
\hat{m}_{c}
.

4.

The computed mean is used to classify sample
\bm{x}
by computing
the inner product
\langle\bm{x},\hat{m}_{c}\rangle
as a metric of similarity.

The attacker has to infer which vectors have been used for training the classifier. Instead of utilising the logits (like in many image classification tasks), the attacker can use the inner products of a point with the cluster means. Since the inner product score follows a normal distribution, LiRA with infinitely many shadow models is the optimal attack by the Neyman–Pearson lemma
(
Neyman and Pearson 1933
)
, which states that the likelihood ratio test is the most powerful test for a given FPR.

This simplified model resembles a linear (Head) classifier often used in transfer learning when adapting to a new dataset. We also focus on the linear (Head) classifier in our empirical evaluation in
Section

4
. In the
linear classifier, we find a matrix
W
and biases
b
, to optimize
the cross-entropy between the labels and logits
Wv+b
, where
v

denotes the feature space representation of the data. In the
simplified model, the rows of
W
are replaced by the cluster means
and we do not include the bias term in the classification.

Now, applying Lemma
1
to the simplified model yields the following result.

Theorem 2

(Per-example LiRA power-law)
.

Fix a target example
(\bm{x},y)
. For the simplified model with arbitrary
C
and infinitely many shadow models, the per-example LiRA vulnerability is given as

\log(\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x})-\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x}))\\
=-\frac{1}{2}\log S-\frac{1}{2}\Phi^{-1}(\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x}))^{2}+\log\frac{|\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}\rangle|}{\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}\sqrt{2\pi}}+\log(1+\xi(S))

(9)

where
m_{\bm{x}}
is the true mean of class
y
and
\xi(S)=O(1/\sqrt{S})
.
For large
S
we have

\log(\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x})-\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x}))\approx-\frac{1}{2}\log S-\frac{1}{2}\Phi^{-1}(\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x}))^{2}+\log\frac{|\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}\rangle|}{\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}\sqrt{2\pi}}.

(10)

Proof.

See
Section

A.5
.
∎

An immediate upper bound is obtained from Theorem
2
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\log(\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x})-\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x}))\leq-\frac{1}{2}\log S-\frac{1}{2}\Phi^{-1}(\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x}))^{2}+\log\frac{||\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}||}{\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}\sqrt{2\pi}}\\
+\log(1+\xi(S)).

(11)

This implies that if
||\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}||
is bounded, then the worst-case vulnerability is also bounded.
Hence we can significantly reduce the MIA vulnerability of all examples in this non-DP setting by simply increasing the number of examples per class.

Now the following corollary extends the power-law to the average-case MIA vulnerability.

Section

.

Corollary 3

(Average-case LiRA power-law)
.

For the simplified model with arbitrary
C
, sufficiently large
S
and infinitely many shadow models, we have

\log(\overline{\textsc{tpr}}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}-\overline{\textsc{fpr}}_{\mathrm{LiRA}})\approx-\frac{1}{2}\log S-\frac{1}{2}\Phi^{-1}(\overline{\textsc{fpr}}_{\mathrm{LiRA}})^{2}+\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{x},y)\sim{\mathbb{D}}}\left[\frac{|\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}\rangle|}{\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}\sqrt{2\pi}}\right]\right).

(12)

A.6
.

5
Discussion

Trying to bridge empirical MIA vulnerability and formal DP guarantees is not an easy task because of different threat models and different nature of bounds (statistical vs. universal).
While we were able to show both theoretically (
Section

3
) and empirically (
Section

4
) that having more examples per class provides protection against MIA in fine-tuned neural networks, the numbers required for significant protection (
Section

4.5
) limit the practical utility of this observation.

Using a level of formal DP bounds that provide meaningful protection as a yardstick, at least tens of thousands of examples per class are needed for every class even at
\textsc{fpr}=0.001
.
The
\epsilon
values used here are formal upper bounds and must not be compared to actual DP deep learning with comparable privacy budgets, as the latter would be far less vulnerable.
At this number of samples per class and a good pre-trained model, the impact of DP training is often negligible.
This stresses the importance of formal guarantees like DP for privacy protection.

Broader Impact

Our work systematically studies factors influencing privacy risk of trained ML models. This has significant positive impact on users training ML models on personal data by allowing them to understand and limit the risks.

Limitations

We mostly consider LiRA in our paper which is optimal for our simiplified model of membership inference (
Section

3.4
) but for the transfer-learning experiments (
Section

4
) there might be stronger attacks in the future. Furthermore, our results assume well-behaved underlying distributions. Formal bounds on MIA vulnerability would require something like DP. In addition, both our theoretical and empirical analysis focus on deep transfer learning using fine-tuning. Models trained from scratch are likely to be more vulnerable.

We will publish the code in an open repository after acceptance of the paper.

A.1
Formulating LiRA

Let
\mathcal{M}
be our target model and
\ell(\mathcal{M}(\bm{x}),y)
be the loss of the model on a target example
(\bm{x},y)
.
The goal of MIA is to determine whether
(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}
.
This can be formulated as a hypothesis test:

\displaystyle H_{0}:(\bm{x},y)\notin\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}

(A1)

\displaystyle H_{1}:(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}.

(A2)

Following
(
Carlini et al. 2022
)
, we formulate the Likelihood Ratio Attack (LiRA).
LiRA exploits the difference of losses on the target model under
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
.
To model the IN/OUT loss distributions with few shadow models, LiRA employs a parametric modelling.
Particularly, LiRA models
t_{\bm{x}}
by a normal distribution.
That is, the hypothesis test formulated above can be rewritten as

\displaystyle H_{0}^{\prime}:t_{\bm{x}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{out}},\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{out}}^{2})

(A3)

\displaystyle H_{1}^{\prime}:t_{\bm{x}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{in}},\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}).

(A4)

The likelihood ratio is now

\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x})=\frac{\mathcal{N}(t_{\bm{x}};\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{in}},\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{in}}^{2})}{\mathcal{N}(t_{\bm{x}};\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{out}},\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{out}}^{2})}.

(A5)

LiRA rejects
H_{0}^{\prime}
if and only if

\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x})\geq\tau,

(A6)

concluding that
H_{1}^{\prime}
is true, i.e., identifying the membership of
(\bm{x},y)
.
Thus, the true positive rate of this hypothesis test given as

\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x})=\Pr_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}\sim{\mathbb{D}}^{|\mathcal{D}|},\phi^{M}}\left(\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x})\geq\tau\mid(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}\right),

(A7)

where
\phi^{M}
denotes the randomness in the shadow set sampling and shadow model training.

A.2
On the assumption of shared scale

In
Section

3
we assumed that for LiRA
\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{in}}=\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{out}}
.
Using the simplified model formulated in
Section

3.4
, we show that for large enough number
S
of examples per class these assumptions are reasonable.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}=\{(\bm{x}_{j,1},j),...,(\bm{x}_{j,S},j)\}_{j=1}^{C}
. Then the IN/OUT LiRA scores are given as

\displaystyle s_{y}^{\mathrm{(in)}}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x},\frac{1}{S}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{S-1}\bm{x}_{y,i}+\bm{x}\right)\rangle=\langle\bm{x},\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\bm{x}_{y,i}\rangle+\langle\bm{x},\frac{1}{S}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{y,S})\rangle

(A8)

\displaystyle s_{y}^{\mathrm{(out)}}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x},\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\bm{x}_{y,i}\rangle.

(A9)

Since for the simplified model scores follow Gaussian distributions,
\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}=\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}=\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{out}}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}=\Var(s_{y}^{\mathrm{(in)}})=\frac{1}{S}\Var(\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}_{y,i}\rangle)-\frac{1}{S^{2}}\Var(\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}_{y,i}\rangle)=\frac{1}{S}\left(1-\frac{1}{S}\right)\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}

(A10)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{out}}^{2}=\Var(s_{y}^{\mathrm{(out)}})=\frac{1}{S}\Var(\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}_{y,i}\rangle)=\frac{1}{S}\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}.

(A11)

Thus, the differences
\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{in}}-\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{out}}
are negligible for large
S
.

In

1

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}\log\tau}{\sigma(\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{in}}-\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{out}})}+\frac{\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{in}}+\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{out}}}{2\sigma}-\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{out}}}{\sigma}=\begin{cases}F_{Z}^{-1}(1-\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x}))&\text{ if }\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{in}}>\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{out}}\\

Appendix B
Theoretical analysis of RMIA

Similar to LiRA, RMIA is based on shadow model training and computing the attack statistics based on a likelihood ratio. The main difference to LiRA is that RMIA does not compute the likelihood ratio based on aggregated IN/OUT statistics, but instead compares the target data point against random samples
(\bm{z},y_{\bm{z}})
from the target data distribution. After computing the likelihood ratios over multiple
(\bm{z},y_{\bm{z}})
values, the MIA score is estimated as a proportion of the ratios exceeding a preset bound. This approach makes RMIA a more effective attack when the number of shadow models is low.

B.1
Formulating RMIA

In the following, let us denote a target point by
(\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})
with
y_{\bm{x}}
being the label of
\bm{x}
.
Let us restate how RMIA
(
Zarifzadeh et al. 2024
)
builds the MIA score. RMIA augments the likelihood-ratio with a sample from the target data distribution to calibrate how likely you would obtain the target model if
(\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})
is replaced with another sample
(\bm{z},y_{\bm{z}})
. Denoting the target model parameters with
\theta
, RMIA computes the
pairwise
likelihood ratio

\displaystyle\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x},\bm{z})=\frac{p(\theta\mid\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})}{p(\theta\mid\bm{z},y_{\bm{z}})},

(A60)

and the corresponding MIA score is given as

\displaystyle\text{Score}_{\text{RMIA}}(\bm{x})=\Pr_{(\bm{z},y_{\bm{z}})\sim\mathbb{D}}(\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x},\bm{z})>\gamma),

(A61)

where
\mathbb{D}
denotes the training data distribution.

Zarifzadeh et al. 2024
show two approaches to compute
\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x},\bm{z})
: the direct approach and the effecient Bayesian approach.
In the following theoretical analysis, we focus on the direct approach that is an approximation of the efficient Bayesian approach, as
Zarifzadeh et al. 2024
empirically demonstrates that these approaches exhibit similar performances.

Let
\hat{\mu}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}
denote, respectively, the mean and standard deviation of
t_{b}=\ell(\mathcal{M}(b),y_{b})
estimated from shadow models, where
a
denotes which of
(\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})
and
(\bm{z},y_{\bm{z}})
is in the training set.
By Equation 11 in
(
Zarifzadeh et al. 2024
)
, the pairwise likelihood ratio is given as

\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x},\bm{z})=\frac{p(\theta\mid\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})}{p(\theta\mid\bm{z},y_{\bm{z}})}\approx\frac{\mathcal{N}(t_{\bm{x}};\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}},\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}^{2})\mathcal{N}(t_{\bm{z}};\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}},\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}^{2})}{\mathcal{N}(t_{\bm{x}};\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{x}},\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{z},\bm{x}}^{2})\mathcal{N}(t_{\bm{z}};\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{z}},\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{z},\bm{z}}^{2})},

(A62)

where
\hat{\mu}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}
are, respectively, the mean and standard deviation of
t_{b}
estimated from shadow models when the training set contains
\bm{a}
but not
\bm{b}
.
Then RMIA exploits the probability of rejecting the pairwise likelihood ratio test over
(\bm{z},y_{\bm{z}})\sim{\mathbb{D}}
:

\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})=\Pr_{(\bm{z},y_{\bm{z}})\sim{\mathbb{D}}}\left(\mathrm{LR}(x,\bm{z})\geq\gamma\right),

(A63)

where
{\mathbb{D}}
is the underlying data distribution.
Similar to LiRA, the classifier is built by checking if
\text{Score}_{\text{RMIA}}(\bm{x})>\tau
. In the following, we will use the direct computation of likelihood-ratio as described in Equation 11 of
Zarifzadeh et al. 2024
which approximates
\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x},\bm{z})
using normal distributions.
Thus, RMIA rejects
H_{0}
if and only if

\Pr_{(\bm{z},y_{\bm{z}})\sim{\mathbb{D}}}\left(\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x},\bm{z})\geq\gamma\right)\geq\tau,

(A64)

identifying the membership of
\bm{x}
.
Hence the true positive rate of per-example RMIA is given as

\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})\\
=\Pr_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}\sim{\mathbb{D}}^{|\mathcal{D}|},\phi^{M}}\left(\Pr_{(\bm{z},y_{\bm{z}})\sim{\mathbb{D}}}\left(\mathrm{LR}(\bm{x},\bm{z})\geq\gamma\right)\geq\tau\mid(\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}\wedge(\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})\notin\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}\right),

(A65)

where
\phi^{M}
denotes the randomness in the shadow set sampling and shadow model training.

We define the average-case
tpr
for RMIA by taking the expectation over the data distribution:

\overline{\textsc{tpr}}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}=\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})\sim{\mathbb{D}}}[\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})].

(A66)

Per-example RMIA

\displaystyle\left(\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_{\bm{x}}}{\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{x}}^{2}}(\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{x}})+\frac{\sigma_{\bm{z}}}{\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{z}}^{2}}(\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{z}})}_{A}\right)Z

(A88)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{x}}}{2\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{x}}^{2}}(2\mu_{\bm{z},\bm{x}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{x}})+\frac{\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{z}}}{2\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{z}}^{2}}(2\mu_{\bm{z},\bm{z}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{z}})}_{B}

(A89)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle AZ+B.

(A90)

Notice that
A
and
B
are functions of
\bm{z}
and independent of
Z
. Also note that taking probability over
Z
corresponds to calculating probability over
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}\sim{\mathbb{D}}^{n}
. Thus, since we can set
\gamma>1
, using Markov’s inequality and the triangle inequality, we have in the limit of infinitely many shadow models

\displaystyle\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(x)=

\displaystyle\Pr_{Z}\left(\Pr_{\bm{z}}(\lambda_{\bm{z}}\geq\log\gamma)\geq\tau\right)

(A91)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{Z}\left(\Pr_{\bm{z}}(|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|\geq\log\gamma)\geq\tau\right)

(A92)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{Z}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|]}{\log\gamma}\geq\tau\right)

(A93)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{Z}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|]\geq\tau\log\gamma\right)

(A94)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{Z}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]|Z|+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|B|]\geq\tau\log\gamma\right)

(A95)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{Z}\left(|Z|\geq\frac{\tau\log\gamma-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|B|]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]}\right)

(A96)

Therefore, we can upper-bound
\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(x)\leq\alpha
by setting

\alpha=1-F_{|Z|}\left(\frac{\tau\log\gamma-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|B|]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]}\right).

(A97)

That is,

\frac{\tau\log\gamma-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|B|]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]}=F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha).

(A98)

Now let

q=\lambda_{\bm{x}}-\lambda_{\bm{z}}=\frac{(\mu_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}-\mu_{\bm{z},\bm{x}})(\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{x}})}{\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{x}}^{2}}+\frac{(\mu_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}-\mu_{\bm{z},\bm{z}})(\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{z}})}{\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{z}}^{2}}.

(A99)

Note that
q
is also independent of
t
, thereby
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]
being a constant. By the similar argument using Markov’s inequality and the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(x)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{Z}\left(\Pr_{\bm{z}}(\lambda_{\bm{x}}\geq\log\gamma)\geq\tau\right)

(A100)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{Z}\left(\Pr_{\bm{z}}(|\lambda_{\bm{x}}|\geq\log\gamma)\geq\tau\right)

(A101)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{Z}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{x}}|]}{\log\gamma}\geq\tau\right)

(A102)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{Z}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{x}}|]\geq\tau\log\gamma\right)

(A103)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{Z}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]|Z|+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|B|]+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]\geq\tau\log\gamma\right)

(A104)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{Z}\left(|Z|\geq\frac{\tau\log\gamma-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|B|]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]}\right).

(A105)

Hence we obtain

\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(x)\leq 1-F_{|Z|}^{-1}\left(F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]}\right).

(A106)

∎

Note that, unlike per-example LiRA, we must assume that the attacker has access to the underlying distribution for the optimal RMIA as the
Equations

A74
and
A75
depend on the parameters computed from shadow models.

B.3
RMIA power-law

Employing Lemma
5
and the simplified model, we obtain the following upper bound for RMIA performance.

Theorem 6

(Per-example RMIA power-law)
.

Fix a target example
(\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})
. For the simplified model with large
S
and infinitely many shadow models, the per-example RMIA vulnerability is given as

\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})\leq 1-F_{|Z|}\left(F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)-\frac{\Psi(\bm{x},C)}{\sqrt{S}}\right),

(A107)

where
\alpha\geq\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})
,
F_{|Z|}
is the cdf of the standard folded normal distribution, and

\Psi(\bm{x},C)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle^{2}}{||\bm{x}||^{2}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle^{2}}{||\bm{z}||^{2}}\mid y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}}\right]+(C-1)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}\rangle^{2}}{||\bm{x}||^{2}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{z}-m_{\bm{z}}\rangle^{2}}{||\bm{z}||^{2}}\mid y_{\bm{z}}\not=y_{\bm{x}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left|\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle}{||\bm{x}||}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle}{||\bm{z}||}\right|\mid y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}}\right]+(C-1)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left|\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}\rangle}{||\bm{x}||}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{z}-m_{\bm{z}}\rangle}{||\bm{z}||}\right|\mid y_{\bm{z}}\not=y_{\bm{x}}\right]}.

(A108)

In addition, we have

\log(\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})-\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x}))\approx-\frac{1}{2}\log S-\frac{1}{2}F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)^{2}+\log\frac{\Psi(\bm{x},C)}{\sqrt{\pi/2}}.

(A109)

Proof.

To apply
Lemma

5
, we will calculate
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}=\{(\bm{x}_{j,1},j),\dots,(\bm{x}_{j,S},j)\}_{j=1}^{C}
. Let
s_{y_{a}}^{(\bm{b})}(\bm{a})
denote the score of
(\bm{a},y_{\bm{a}})
when
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}
contains
(\bm{b},y_{\bm{b}})
but not the other example. Using similar argument as in the proof of
Theorem

2
, we have

\displaystyle s_{y_{\bm{x}}}^{(\bm{x})}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S}\langle\bm{x},\sum_{i=1}^{S}\bm{x}_{y_{\bm{x}},i}+\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{y_{\bm{x}},S}\rangle

(A110)

\displaystyle s_{y_{\bm{x}}}^{(\bm{z})}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{S}\langle\bm{x},\sum_{i=1}^{S}\bm{x}_{y_{\bm{x}},i}+\bm{z}-\bm{x}_{y_{\bm{x}},S}\rangle\quad&\text{ if }y_{\bm{x}}=y_{\bm{z}}\\
\frac{1}{S}\langle\bm{x},\sum_{i=1}^{S}\bm{x}_{y_{\bm{x}},i}\rangle\quad&\text{ if }y_{\bm{x}}\not=y_{\bm{z}}\end{cases}

(A111)

\displaystyle s_{y_{\bm{z}}}^{(\bm{x})}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{S}\langle\bm{z},\sum_{i=1}^{S}\bm{x}_{y_{\bm{x}},i}+\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{y_{\bm{x}},S}\rangle\quad&\text{ if }y_{\bm{x}}=y_{\bm{z}}\\
\frac{1}{S}\langle\bm{z},\sum_{i=1}^{S}\bm{x}_{y_{\bm{z}},i}\rangle\quad&\text{ if }y_{\bm{x}}\not=y_{\bm{z}}\end{cases}

(A112)

\displaystyle s_{y_{\bm{z}}}^{(\bm{z})}(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S}\langle\bm{z},\sum_{i=1}^{S}\bm{x}_{y_{\bm{z}},i}+\bm{z}-\bm{x}_{y_{\bm{z}},S}\rangle.

(A113)

Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\mu_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x},m_{y_{\bm{x}}}\rangle+\frac{1}{S}\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{y_{\bm{x}}}\rangle

(A114)

\displaystyle\mu_{\bm{z},\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\langle\bm{x},m_{y_{\bm{x}}}\rangle+\frac{1}{S}\langle\bm{x},\bm{z}-m_{y_{\bm{x}}}\rangle\quad&\text{ if }y_{\bm{x}}=y_{\bm{z}}\\
\langle\bm{x},m_{y_{\bm{x}}}\rangle\quad&\text{ if }y_{\bm{x}}\not=y_{\bm{z}}\end{cases}

(A115)

\displaystyle\mu_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\langle\bm{z},m_{y_{\bm{x}}}\rangle+\frac{1}{S}\langle\bm{z},\bm{x}-m_{y_{\bm{x}}}\rangle\quad&\text{ if }y_{\bm{x}}=y_{\bm{z}}\\
\langle\bm{z},m_{y_{\bm{z}}}\rangle\quad&\text{ if }y_{\bm{x}}\not=y_{\bm{z}}\end{cases}

(A116)

\displaystyle\mu_{\bm{z},\bm{z}}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{z},m_{y_{\bm{x}}}\rangle+\frac{1}{S}\langle\bm{z},\bm{z}-m_{y_{\bm{x}}}\rangle\quad

(A117)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}

(A118)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\sqrt{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}},

(A119)

where
m_{y_{\bm{z}}}
is the true class mean of class
y_{\bm{z}}
.

Now recall that

\displaystyle q=

\displaystyle\frac{(\mu_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}-\mu_{\bm{z},\bm{x}})(\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{x}})}{\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{x}}^{2}}+\frac{(\mu_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}-\mu_{\bm{z},\bm{z}})(\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{z}})}{\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{z}}^{2}}

(A120)

\displaystyle A=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{\bm{x}}}{\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{x}}^{2}}(\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{x}})+\frac{\sigma_{\bm{z}}}{\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{z}}^{2}}(\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}-\hat{\mu}_{\bm{z},\bm{z}}).

(A121)

Since
t_{\bm{x}}
and
t_{\bm{z}}
follow normal distributions, the location and scale parameters of the true distributions correspond to the mean and standard deviations estimated from infinitely many shadow models, respectively. Thus, we have

\displaystyle q=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mu_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}-\mu_{\bm{z},\bm{x}}}{\sigma_{\bm{x}}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\mu_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}-\mu_{\bm{z},\bm{z}}}{\sigma_{\bm{z}}}\right)^{2}

(A122)

\displaystyle A=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}-\mu_{\bm{z},\bm{x}}}{\sigma_{\bm{x}}}+\frac{\mu_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}-\mu_{\bm{z},\bm{z}}}{\sigma_{\bm{z}}}.

(A123)

Using the law of total expectation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]=

\displaystyle\Pr_{\bm{z}}(y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}})\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|\mid y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}}]+\sum_{j=1,j\not=y_{\bm{x}}}^{C}\Pr_{\bm{z}}(y_{\bm{z}}=j)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|\mid y_{\bm{z}}=j]

(A124)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left.\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-z\rangle}{\sqrt{S}\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{S}\sqrt{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}}\right)^{2}\ \right|\ y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}}\right]

(A125)

\displaystyle+\frac{C-1}{C}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left.\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{y_{\bm{x}}}\rangle}{\sqrt{S}\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{z}-m_{y_{\bm{z}}}\rangle}{\sqrt{S}\sqrt{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}}\right)^{2}\ \right|\ y_{\bm{z}}\not=y_{\bm{x}}\right]

(A126)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{CS}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left.\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle^{2}}{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle^{2}}{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}\ \right|\ y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}}\right]

(A127)

\displaystyle+\frac{C-1}{CS}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left.\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{y_{\bm{x}}}\rangle^{2}}{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{z}-m_{y_{\bm{z}}}\rangle^{2}}{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}\ \right|\ y_{\bm{z}}\not=y_{\bm{x}}\right],

(A128)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]=

\displaystyle\Pr_{\bm{z}}(y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}})\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|\mid y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}}]+\sum_{j=1,j\not=y_{\bm{x}}}^{C}\Pr_{\bm{z}}(y_{\bm{z}}=j)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|\mid y_{\bm{z}}=j]

(A129)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left.\left|\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{S}\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{S}\sqrt{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}}\right|\ \right|\ y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}}\right]

(A130)

\displaystyle+\frac{C-1}{C}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left.\left|\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}\rangle}{\sqrt{S}\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{z}-m_{\bm{z}}\rangle}{\sqrt{S}\sqrt{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}}\right|\ \right|\ y_{\bm{z}}\not=y_{\bm{x}}\right]

(A131)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C\sqrt{S}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left.\left|\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}}\right|\ \right|\ y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}}\right]

(A132)

\displaystyle+\frac{C-1}{C\sqrt{S}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left.\left|\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}\rangle}{\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{z}-m_{\bm{z}}\rangle}{\sqrt{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}}\right|\ \right|\ y_{\bm{z}}\not=y_{\bm{x}}\right].

(A133)

Hence we obtain

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]}\\
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle^{2}}{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle^{2}}{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}\mid y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}}\right]+(C-1)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}\rangle^{2}}{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{z}-m_{\bm{z}}\rangle^{2}}{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}\mid y_{\bm{z}}\not=y_{\bm{x}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left|\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}}\right|\mid y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}}\right]+(C-1)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left|\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}\rangle}{\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{z}-m_{\bm{z}}\rangle}{\sqrt{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}}\right|\mid y_{\bm{z}}\not=y_{\bm{x}}\right]}.

(A134)

Now
Lemma

5
yields

\displaystyle\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\leq 1-F_{|Z|}\left(F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]}\right)

(A135)

\displaystyle=1-F_{|Z|}\left(F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)-\frac{\Psi(\bm{x},C)}{\sqrt{S}}\right),

(A136)

where
F_{|Z|}
is the cdf of the folded normal distribution and

\Psi(\bm{x},C)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle^{2}}{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle^{2}}{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}\mid y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}}\right]+(C-1)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}\rangle^{2}}{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{z}-m_{\bm{z}}\rangle^{2}}{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}\mid y_{\bm{z}}\not=y_{\bm{x}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left|\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{x}-\bm{z}\rangle}{\sqrt{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}}\right|\mid y_{\bm{z}}=y_{\bm{x}}\right]+(C-1)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}\left[\left|\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}\rangle}{\sqrt{\bm{x}^{T}\Sigma\bm{x}}}+\frac{\langle\bm{z},\bm{z}-m_{\bm{z}}\rangle}{\sqrt{\bm{z}^{T}\Sigma\bm{z}}}\right|\mid y_{\bm{z}}\not=y_{\bm{x}}\right]}.

(A137)

This completes the first half of the theorem.

Now that from
Lemma

5
we have

\displaystyle\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\Pr_{Z}\left(\Pr_{\bm{z}}(\lambda_{\bm{z}}+q\geq\log\gamma)\geq\tau\right)

(A138)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{Z}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|]+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]}{\log\gamma}\geq\tau\right)

(A139)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{Z}\left(|Z|\geq F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]}\right)

(A140)

\displaystyle\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\Pr_{Z}\left(\Pr_{\bm{z}}(\lambda_{\bm{z}}\geq\log\gamma)\geq\tau\right)

(A141)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{Z}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|]}{\log\gamma}\geq\tau\right)

(A142)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{Z}\left(|Z|\geq F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)\right).

(A143)

We claim that the bound for
\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})
(
Equation

A142
) is as tight as that for
\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})
(
Equation

A139
) for sufficiently large
S
.
Let us denote

\displaystyle\kappa_{\textsc{tpr}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\bm{z}}(\lambda_{\bm{z}}+q\geq\log\gamma)

(A144)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\textsc{fpr}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\bm{z}}(\lambda_{\bm{z}}\geq\log\gamma).

(A145)

Since
q=O(1/S)
, for large
S
we approximate

\kappa_{\textsc{tpr}}(\gamma)-\kappa_{\textsc{fpr}}(\gamma)\approx p_{\lambda_{\bm{z}}}(\log\gamma)\frac{c_{0}}{S},

(A146)

for some constant
c_{0}
. Since
\lambda_{\bm{z}}=O(1/\sqrt{S})
, the scaled random variable
\hat{\lambda}_{\bm{z}}=\sqrt{S}\lambda_{\bm{z}}
is almost independent of
S
. Then by the change of variables formula, we have

p_{\lambda_{\bm{z}}}(\log\gamma)\frac{c_{0}}{S}=p_{\hat{\lambda}_{\bm{z}}}(\sqrt{S}\log\gamma)\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{S}}.

(A147)

Without loss of generality we may set
\log\gamma=1/\sqrt{S}
. Thus, we have

\kappa_{\textsc{tpr}}(1/\sqrt{S})-\kappa_{\textsc{fpr}}(1/\sqrt{S})\approx p_{\hat{\lambda}_{\bm{z}}}\left(\sqrt{S}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\right)\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{S}}=p_{\hat{\lambda}_{\bm{z}}}(1)\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{S}}.

(A148)

This quantity scales as
O(1/\sqrt{S})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})-\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\Pr_{t}(\kappa_{\textsc{tpr}}(1/\sqrt{S})\geq\tau)-\Pr_{t}(\kappa_{\textsc{fpr}}(1/\sqrt{S})\geq\tau)

(A149)

\displaystyle\approx p_{\kappa_{\textsc{fpr}}(1/\sqrt{S})}(\tau)(\kappa_{\textsc{fpr}}(1/\sqrt{S})-\kappa_{\textsc{tpr}}(1/\sqrt{S}))

(A150)

\displaystyle\approx p_{\kappa_{\textsc{fpr}}(1/\sqrt{S})}(\tau)p_{\hat{\lambda}_{\bm{z}}}(1)\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{S}}.

(A151)

Note that
\kappa_{\textsc{fpr}}(1/\sqrt{S})
and, consequently,
\tau
are independent of
S
for large enough
S
since

\kappa_{\textsc{fpr}}(1/\sqrt{S})=\Pr_{\bm{z}}\left(\lambda_{\bm{z}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\right)=\Pr_{\bm{z}}\left(\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{\bm{z}}}{\sqrt{S}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\right)=\Pr_{\bm{z}}(\hat{\lambda}_{\bm{z}}\geq 1).

(A152)

Hence
\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})-\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})=O(1/\sqrt{S})
. On the other hand, from
Equations

A139
and
A142
we have for sufficiently large
S

\displaystyle\Pr_{Z}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|]}{\log\gamma}\geq\tau\right)-\Pr_{Z}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|]}{\log\gamma}+\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]}{\log\gamma}\geq\tau\right)

(A153)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr_{Z}\left(\sqrt{S}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|]\geq\tau\right)-\Pr_{Z}\left(\sqrt{S}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|]+\frac{c_{1}}{\sqrt{S}}\geq\tau\right)

(A154)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr_{Z}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\hat{\lambda}_{\bm{z}}|]\geq\tau\right)-\Pr_{Z}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\hat{\lambda}_{\bm{z}}|]+\frac{c_{1}}{\sqrt{S}}\geq\tau\right)

(A155)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle p_{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\hat{\lambda}_{\bm{z}}|]}(\tau)\frac{c_{1}}{\sqrt{S}},

(A156)

where
c_{1}
is some constant. Since for large
S
,
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\hat{\lambda}_{\bm{z}}|]
and
\tau
are independent of
S
,
Equation

A156
scales as
O(1/\sqrt{S})
.

Now let

\displaystyle\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\Pr_{Z}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|]+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]}{\log\gamma}\geq\tau\right)-v_{\textsc{tpr}}

(A157)

\displaystyle\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\Pr_{Z}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|]}{\log\gamma}\geq\tau\right)-v_{\textsc{fpr}}

(A158)

for some
v_{\textsc{tpr}},v_{\textsc{fpr}}\geq 0
which evaluate the tightness of the bounds. Then we have

\displaystyle v_{\textsc{tpr}}-v_{\textsc{fpr}}

(A159)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{Z}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|]+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]}{\log\gamma}\geq\tau\right)-\Pr_{Z}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|\lambda_{\bm{z}}|]}{\log\gamma}\geq\tau\right)-(\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x})-\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x}))

(A160)

\displaystyle=O(1/\sqrt{S}).

(A161)

Hence we conclude that for sufficiently large
S
, the bound for
\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x})
is as tight as that for
\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{LiRA}}(\bm{x})
.

Therefore, noting that
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]/\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]=O(1/\sqrt{S})
, for large
S
we obtain

\displaystyle\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})-\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})

(A162)

\displaystyle\approx\Pr_{Z}\left(|Z|\geq F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]}\right)-\Pr_{Z}\left(|Z|\geq F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)\right)

(A163)

\displaystyle\approx p_{|Z|}\left(F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)\right)\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|q|]}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[|A|]}

(A164)

\displaystyle=p_{|Z|}\left(F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)\right)\frac{\Psi(\bm{x},C)}{\sqrt{S}}.

(A165)

Since
|Z|
follows the standard folded normal distribution,

p_{|Z|}\left(F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)\right)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)^{2}\right).

(A166)

It follows that

\log(\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})-\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x}))\approx-\frac{1}{2}\log S-\frac{1}{2}F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)^{2}+\log\frac{\Psi(\bm{x},C)}{\sqrt{\pi/2}}.

(A167)

∎

As for the LiRA power-law, bounding
||\bm{x}-m_{\bm{x}}||
and
||\bm{z}-m_{\bm{z}}||
will provide a worst-case upper bound for which the power-law holds.

Finally, the per-example RMIA power-law is also extended to the average-case:

Corollary 7

(Average-case RMIA power-law)
.

For the simplified model with sufficiently large
S
and infinitely many shadow models, we have

\log(\overline{\textsc{tpr}}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}-\overline{\textsc{fpr}}_{\mathrm{RMIA}})\approx-\frac{1}{2}\log S-\frac{1}{2}F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)^{2}+\log\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})\sim{\mathbb{D}}}\left[\frac{\Psi(\bm{x},C)}{\sqrt{\pi/2}}\right]\right),

(A168)

where
\alpha\geq\overline{\textsc{fpr}}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}
and
F_{|Z|}
is the cdf of the standard folded normal distribution.

Proof.

By
Theorem

6
and the law of unconscious statistician, we have for large
S

\displaystyle\overline{\textsc{tpr}}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}-\overline{\textsc{fpr}}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathscr{D}}p(\bm{x})(\textsc{tpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x})-\textsc{fpr}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}(\bm{x}))\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(A169)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{\mathscr{D}}p(\bm{x})\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi/2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)^{2}}\frac{\Psi(\bm{x},C)}{\sqrt{S}}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(A170)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi/2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)^{2}}\int_{\mathscr{D}}p(\bm{x})\frac{\Psi(\bm{x},C)}{\sqrt{S}}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(A171)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)^{2}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})\sim{\mathbb{D}}}\left[\frac{\Psi(\bm{x},C)}{\sqrt{\pi/2}}\right],

(A172)

where
p(\bm{x})
is the density of
{\mathbb{D}}
at
(\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})
, and
\mathscr{D}
is the data domain. Hence we obtain

\log(\overline{\textsc{tpr}}_{\mathrm{RMIA}}-\overline{\textsc{fpr}}_{\mathrm{RMIA}})\approx-\frac{1}{2}\log S-\frac{1}{2}F_{|Z|}^{-1}(1-\alpha)^{2}+\log\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(\bm{x},y_{\bm{x}})\sim{\mathbb{D}}}\left[\frac{\Psi(\bm{x},C)}{\sqrt{\pi/2}}\right]\right).

(A173)

∎

Appendix C
Training details

C.1
Parameterization

We utilise pre-trained feature extractors BiT-M-R50x1 (R-50)
(
Kolesnikov et al. 2020
)
with 23.5M parameters and Vision Transformer ViT-Base-16 (ViT-B)
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2021
)
with 85.8M parameters, both pretrained on the ImageNet-21K dataset
(
Russakovsky et al. 2015
)
. We download the feature extractor checkpoints from the respective repositories.

Following
Tobaben et al. 2023
that show the favorable trade-off of parameter-efficient fine-tuning between computational cost, utility and privacy even for small datasets, we only consider fine-tuning subsets of all feature extractor parameters. We consider the following configurations:

•

Head:
We train a linear layer on top of the pre-trained feature extractor.

•

FiLM:
In addition to the linear layer from Head, we fine-tune parameter-efficient FiLM
(
Perez et al. 2018
)
adapters scattered throughout the network. While a diverse set of adapters has been proposed, we utilise FiLM as it has been shown to be competitive in prior work
(
Shysheya et al. 2023
;
Tobaben et al. 2023
)
.

C.2
Hyperparameter tuning

Our hyperparameter tuning is heavily inspired by the comprehensive few-shot experiments by
Tobaben et al. 2023
. We utilise their hyperparameter tuning protocol as it has been proven to yield SOTA results for (DP) few-shot models.
Given the input
\mathcal{\mathcal{D}}
dataset we perform hyperparameter tuning by splitting the
\mathcal{\mathcal{D}}
into 70
\%
train and 30
\%
validation.
We then perform the specified iterations of hyperparameter tuning using the tree-structured Parzen estimator
(
Bergstra et al. 2011
)
strategy as implemented in Optuna
(
Akiba et al. 2019
)
to derive a set of hyperparameters that yield the highest accuracy on the validation split.
This set of hyperparameters is subsequently used to train all shadow models with the Adam optimizer
(
Kingma and Ba 2015
)
.

C.4
Compute resources

All experiments but the R-50 (FiLM) experiments are run on CPU with 8 cores and 16 GB of host memory. The training time depends on the model (ViT is cheaper than R-50), number of shots
S
and the number of classes
C
but ranges for the training of one model from some minutes to an hour. This assumes that the images are passed once through the pre-trained backbone and then cached as feature vectors. The provided code implements this optimization.

The R-50 (FiLM) experiments are significantly more expensive and utilise a NVIDIA V100 with 40 GB VRAM, 10 CPU cores and 64 GB of host memory. The training of 257 shadow models then does not exceed 24h for the settings that we consider.

We estimate that in total we spend around 7 days of V100 and some dozens of weeks of CPU core time but more exact measurements are hard to make.

Appendix E
Details on Section
4.5

In
Section

4.5
, we compare the results of our empirical models of vulnerability (
Sections

4.3
and
4.4
) to DP bounds. Below we explain how we make the connection between both.

First, we compute the upper bound on the
tpr
for a given
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP privacy budget at a given
fpr
using
Equation

A175
reformulated from
Kairouz et al. 2015
below:

Theorem 8

(
Kairouz et al. 2015
)
.

A mechanism
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP if and only if for all adjacent
\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}

\textsc{tpr}\leq\min\{e^{\epsilon}\textsc{fpr}+\delta,1-e^{-\epsilon}(1-\delta-\textsc{fpr})\}\ .

(A175)

For a given
(\epsilon,\delta)
and
fpr
we then obtain a value for the
tpr
.

Next, we use the linear model from
Section

4.3
to solve for the minimum
S
predicted to be required given
C=2
classes in our example. The coefficients can be found in
Table

A8
for the average case and
Section

4.4
for the worst-case. We solve the
tpr
from the linear model as

\displaystyle\log_{10}(\textsc{tpr}-\textsc{fpr})=\beta_{S}\log_{10}(S)+\beta_{C}\log_{10}(C)+\beta_{0}

(A176)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\textsc{tpr}=S^{\beta_{S}}C^{\beta_{C}}10^{\beta_{0}}+\textsc{fpr}.

(A177)

Now, we find the minimum
S
that the
tpr
from
Equation

A177
upper bounds the
tpr
of
Equation

A175
as

\displaystyle S^{\beta_{S}}C^{\beta_{C}}10^{\beta_{0}}+\textsc{fpr}=\min\{e^{\epsilon}\textsc{fpr}+\delta,1-e^{-\epsilon}(1-\delta-\textsc{fpr})\}

(A178)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle S=\left(\frac{\min\{e^{\epsilon}\textsc{fpr}+\delta,1-e^{-\epsilon}(1-\delta-\textsc{fpr})\}-\textsc{fpr}}{C^{\beta_{C}}10^{\beta_{0}}}\right)^{1/{\beta_{S}}}

(A179)
