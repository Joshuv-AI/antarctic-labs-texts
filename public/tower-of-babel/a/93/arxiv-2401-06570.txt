Discrete constant mean curvature cylinders and isothermic tori
Joseph Cho, Katrin Leschke, Yuta Ogata

Abstract
. We consider the monodromy problem of Darboux transforms of discrete isothermic surfaces using the integrable theory of discrete polarised curves. Then we provide, for the first time, closed-form discrete parametrisations of discrete isothermic cylinders, discrete constant mean curvature cylinders, and discrete isothermic tori.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 53A70; Secondary 53A10, 53A31.

1.
Introduction

Discrete differential geometry aims to examine discrete surfaces through integrable systems technique (see, for example,
[
8
]
), and has gained interest recently from both mathematical and applicational viewpoints.
However, a large number of results concerning structure-preserving discretisation of surfaces concern their local behaviour.
Here, we aim to consider a global aspect of discrete surfaces, focusing on the case of
discrete isothermic surfaces
.

Isothermic surfaces
[
9
]
constitute an integrable class of surfaces
[
22
]
(see also
[
12
]
), and admit spectral deformations
[
3
,
4
,
15
,
16
]
and
Darboux transformations

[
23
]
.
In particular, Darboux transformations have been used to construct various isothermic surfaces with topological constraints by considering the
monodromy problem
: notably, constant mean curvature (cmc) cylinders called bubbletons can be obtained as Darboux transformations of circular cylinders
[
2
]
(see also
[
32
]
), while non-trivial isothermic tori were found via Darboux transforms of the homogeneous tori in
3
-sphere
[
1
]
.

On the other hand,
discrete isothermic surfaces
were defined in
[
5
]
via the permutability of Darboux transformations
[
3
]
, and
Darboux transformations
for discrete isothermic surfaces were defined in
[
24
]
using a discrete Riccati-type equation that is equivalent to a cross-ratios conditon.
A gauge theoretic approach to discrete isothermicity and its Darboux transforms was explored in both the quaternionic setup and the lightcone model
[
25
,
13
]
, where isothermicity is characterised by a existence of a certain
1
-parameter family of discrete flat connections on the discrete trivial bundle, with Darboux transforms given by their parallel sections.
One can also consider the integrable reduction to the class of discrete cmc surfaces, so that certain Darboux transformations of a given discrete cmc surface result in another discrete cmc surfaces
[
24
,
13
]
.

In this paper, we consider the monodromy problem for Darboux transformations of discrete isothermic surfaces to construct discrete isothermic cylinders, including discrete cmc cylinders, and discrete isothermic tori.
In particular, we are interested in obtaining
closed-form discrete parametrisations
for explicit examples of such surfaces; thus, we will use the quaternionic setup for isothermicity, as this approach has proven to be a useful tool in obtaining explicit solutions to Riccati-type equations in both the smooth and discrete settings (see, for example,
[
17
,
19
]
).

In Section
2
, we first review the gauge theoretic description of discrete isothermic surfaces and discrete polarised curves in the quaternionic setup; we will also briefly review the theory of discrete isothermic cmc surfaces.
Then in Section
3
, we view discrete isothermic surfaces as successive Darboux transformations of discrete polarised curves (a discrete analogue of the result obtained for semi-discrete isothermic surfaces in
[
11
]
).
This change in viewpoint is crucial for solving the monodromy problem, as we show that the monodromy problem of Darboux transformations of discrete isothermic surfaces can be reduced to that of discrete polarised curves (see Theorem
3.5
).

In Section
4
, we consider Darboux transformations of explicit discrete isothermic surfaces.
After obtaining discrete isothermic cylinders as Darboux transformations of the discrete circular cylinder in Section
4.3
, we use the fact that discrete circular cylinders are discrete cmc surfaces to find the Darboux transforms that are discrete cmc cylinders in Section
4.4
; these are the discrete analogues of the bubbletons.
Then we switch our attention to the Darboux transformations of homogeneous tori in
3
-sphere, and obtain explicit examples of discrete isothermic tori in Section
4.5
. (See also Figure
1
.)

Importantly, we provide, for the first time,
closed-form discrete parametrisations
of all the explicit examples we consider by solving the discrete Riccati-type equation (see (
4.6
), (
4.10
), or (
4.16
)); the explicitness of our formulation allows us to observe in Section
4.6
that under the appropriate continuum limit, the closure of Darboux transforms is preserved.
Thus, our work suggests that structure preserving discretisation can provide valuable insights in search of new examples of compact isothermic surfaces.

2.2.1.
Discrete isothermic surfaces

For a simply connected
\Sigma^{2}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}

Definition 2.1

A discrete surface

\cratio(f_{i},f_{j},f_{k},f_{\ell})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(f_{i}-f_{j})(f_{j}-f_{k})^{-1}(f_{k}-f_{\ell})(f_{\ell}-f_{i})^{-1}=\frac{\mu_{i\ell}}{\mu_{ij}}

Remark 2.2

The definition of isothermicity implies that the image of every elementary quadrilateral

For the discrete

we have from

In this case, the dual surface

\dif{f}_{ij}^{*}=\omega_{ij},

(2.2)

\cratio(f^{*}_{i},f^{*}_{j},f^{*}_{k},f^{*}_{\ell})=\frac{\mu_{i\ell}}{\mu_{ij}}.

(2.3)

We note here that the Christoffel transform of a discrete isothermic surface is well-defined up to translations and homotheties.
This freedom can be observed in the choice of the cross-ratios factorising function

Now to introduce the vector bundle approach to discrete isothermic surfaces in the realm of Möbius geometry, take the trivial bundles

On the other hand, the definition of Darboux transforms

2.2.3.
Constant mean curvature surfaces

An important subclass of isothermic surfaces is the class of constant mean curvature (cmc) surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

Discrete isothermic cmc surfaces have been defined using various characterisations in the smooth case.
Among many, we highlight a few that are particularly relevant to us:

•

surfaces with constant mean curvature defined via Steiner’s formula

31

29

7

•

surfaces with Christoffel transform as parallel surfaces
[

24

•

[

,

•

surfaces admitting linear conserved quantities in the Minkowski

[

These various definitions have been shown to be equivalent (see, for example,

13
]

For the purposes of the examination of Darboux transforms of discrete cmc surfaces, we will use the following definition:

Definition 2.8

(
[

)

Let

be a discrete isothermic surface.
Then

is a discrete cmc

surface if it admits a Christoffel transform

so that

|f^{*}_{i}-f_{i}|^{2}=\frac{1}{H^{2}}

at every vertex

.
In this case, the Christoffel transform

is referred to as the parallel cmc surface of

This definition of discrete cmc surfaces via parallel cmc surfaces allows us to recover another characterisation as follows:

Fact 2.9

(
[
24
, § 5]
)
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\cong\Im\mathbb{H}
be a discrete cmc surface with parallel cmc surface

.
Then
f^{*}
is a Darboux transform of

with spectral parameter

.

We have seen that discrete isothermic cmc surfaces admit a
4

\mathbb{R}^{3}

Subject to a condition on the initial point, the Darboux transforms are again cmc surfaces:

Fact 2.10

(
[
24
, § 5]
)
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\cong\Im\mathbb{H}
be a discrete cmc-

Remark 2.11

The condition for the Darboux transform of a cmc surfaces to be again a cmc surface can also be given in terms of linear conserved quantities

2.3.
Discrete polarised curves and the monodromy problem

We now briefly review the integrable theory of discrete polarised curves (see
[
19
]
for a detailed explanation).
For some simply-connected discrete domain
\Sigma^{1}\subset\mathbb{Z}
, let
\mu
be a nowhere vanishing function on the unoriented edges of
\Sigma^{1}
so that
(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})
is a polarised domain.
Discrete maps
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{H}
, or their lifts
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{HP}^{1}
, are called discrete polarised curves.
Then the
dual curve

f^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{H}
of
f
can be defined via

\dif{f}^{*}_{ij}=\frac{1}{\mu_{ij}}\dif{f}_{ij}^{-1}.

The integrable structure of discrete polarised curves can again be described in terms of families of (flat) connections: putting
D(\lambda)_{ji}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{i\}\times\mathbb{H}^{2}\to\{j\}\times\mathbb{H}^{2}
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
defined on every edge
(ij)
via

D(\lambda)_{ji}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Id_{ji}+\begin{pmatrix}0&\dif{f}_{ij}\\
\lambda\dif{f}^{*}_{ij}&0\end{pmatrix},

(2.8)

let
r(\lambda)_{ji}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{i\}\times\mathbb{H}^{2}\to\{j\}\times\mathbb{H}^{2}
be defined as

r(\lambda)_{ji}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(G\bullet D(\lambda))_{ji}=G_{j}D(\lambda)_{ji}G_{i}

(2.9)

where
G=(e\,\,\psi)
so that

\displaystyle r(\lambda)_{ji}

\displaystyle=\Id_{ji}+\lambda\eta_{ji}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Id_{ji}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}f_{j}\dif{f}^{*}_{ij}&-f_{j}\dif{f}^{*}_{ij}f_{i}\\
\dif{f}^{*}_{ij}&-\dif{f}^{*}_{ij}f_{i}\end{pmatrix}

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\pi_{i}+\frac{\mu_{ij}-\lambda}{\mu_{ij}}\pi_{j}.

The projective transformation
r(\lambda)^{P}_{ji}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{i\}\times\mathbb{HP}^{1}\to\{j\}\times\mathbb{HP}^{1}
induced by
r(\lambda)_{ji}
defines a connection on the trivial bundle
\underline{\mathbb{HP}}^{1}
, called the
(flat) connection associated to
f
.

Darboux transformations of discrete polarised curves are given in terms of a cross-ratios condition
[
19
, Definition 3.3]
(see also
[
20
, Definition 3.1]
):

Definition 2.12
.

Two discrete polarised curves
f,\hat{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{H}
are called a
Darboux pair
with spectral parameter
\nu
if

\cratio(f,f_{j},\hat{f}_{j},\hat{f}_{i})=\frac{\nu}{\mu_{ij}}

on every edge
(ij)
.
In such case, one curve is called a
Darboux transform
of the other curve with spectral parameter
\mu
.

The Darboux transformations of discrete polarised curves can be given a characterisation in terms of the associated (flat) connections:

Fact 2.13
.

Let
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{HP}^{1}
be a discrete polarised curve with associated connection
r(\lambda)^{P}
.
Then
\hat{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{HP}^{1}
is a Darboux transform of
L
with spectral parameter
\nu
if and only if
\hat{F}
is
r(\nu)^{P}
–parallel.

For explicit calculations, the
r(\nu)^{P}
–parallel condition (
2.7
) for Darboux transformations can be reformulated as follows.
Suppose that
\hat{F}
is
r(\nu)^{P}
–parallel so that for some
\hat{\psi}\in\Gamma\hat{F}
, we have

r(\nu)_{ji}\hat{\psi}_{i}=\hat{\psi}_{j},

on any edge
(ij)
.
Now, if we define
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=G^{-1}\hat{\psi}
, then we have

D(\nu)_{ji}\phi_{i}=G^{-1}_{j}r(\nu)_{ji}\hat{\psi}_{i}=\phi_{j}.

Therefore, if
\phi=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix}
for some
a,b\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma^{1}\to\mathbb{H}
, then
a
and
b
must satisfy

\begin{pmatrix}\dif{a}_{ij}\\
\dif{b}_{ij}\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}\dif{f}_{ij}b_{i}\\
\nu\dif{f}^{*}_{ij}a_{i}\end{pmatrix},

(2.11)

and the new Darboux transform
\hat{f}
is then given by
\hat{f}=f+ab^{-1}
.

To review the monodromy problem for Darboux transformations of discrete polarised curves
[
19
, § 3.3]
, suppose that
F,\hat{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{HP}^{1}
are a Darboux pair of curves, and further assume that
F
is an
M
-periodic curve, that is,

F_{m}=F_{m+M}

for all
m\in\Sigma^{1}
.
If the
monodromy matrix

\mathcal{M}_{r,\nu}
is defined by

\mathcal{M}_{r,\nu}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\prod_{\iota=m}^{m+M-1}r(\nu)_{(\iota,\iota+1)},

then the eigenvectors
\phi
of
\mathcal{M}_{r,\nu}
correspond to
parallel sections with multipliers
, that is, for
\hat{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Span_{\mathbb{H}}\{\phi\}
, we have

\hat{F}_{m}=\hat{F}_{m+M}

where
\hat{F}
is
r(\nu)^{P}
–parallel.
A spectral parameter
\nu
is called a
resonance point
if every parallel section is a parallel section with multiplier.
In other words, using a resonance point as a spectral parameter always gives closed Darboux transforms, regardless of the choice of the initial condition.

3.
Monodromy of discrete isothermic surfaces

The integrable structure of discrete isothermic surfaces and that of discrete polarised curves closely resemble each other.
In this section, we formalise the resemblance and interpret the integrability of discrete isothermic surfaces in terms of transformations of discrete polarised curves.
The reduction to curves will be crucial to examining the monodromy of Darboux transformations of discrete isothermic surfaces.
We note here that such reduction for the semi-discrete case has been considered in
[
11
]
, where semi-discrete isothermic surfaces
[
27
]
are characterised as successive Darboux transforms of smooth polarised curves.

3.1.
Discrete isothermic surfaces in terms of discrete polarised curves

Since we are interested in discrete curvature lines, let
\Sigma^{2}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be a discrete rectangular domain, that is,
\Sigma^{2}=\Sigma^{1}\times\tilde{\Sigma}^{1}
for simply-connected domains
\Sigma^{1},\tilde{\Sigma}^{1}\subset\mathbb{Z}
.

Suppose that
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma^{2}\to\mathbb{H}
is a discrete isothermic surface with cross-ratios factorising function
\mu
.
Viewing
\mu
as a function defined on the unoriented edges of
\Sigma^{2}
, we can consider the pair
(\Sigma^{2},\frac{1}{\mu})
as a
discrete polarised domain
, and view discrete isothermic surfaces as defined on such polarised domains.
Therefore, given a discrete isothermic surface on a polarised domain, all of its Christoffel transforms and Darboux transforms are defined on the same polarised domain.

Denote by
f^{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma^{1}\to\mathbb{H}
any discrete curvature line of
f
defined on the corresponding simply-connected domain
\Sigma^{1}
.
Since the cross-ratios factorising function
\mu
serves as the polarisation of the domain
\Sigma^{2}
, we can consider the restriction of
\mu
to the unoriented edges of
\Sigma^{1}
; therefore, we see that
f^{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{H}
is a discrete polarised curve.
In particular, we have that
\mu
is an edge-labeling; thus every discrete curvature line in a family is defined on the same polarised domain
(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})
.

Take any two neighbouring discrete curvature lines
f^{0},f^{1}
of
f
: without loss of generality, let
f^{0}=f_{m,n_{0}}
and
f^{1}=f_{m,n_{0}+1}
for some fixed
n_{0}
.
Then since
\mu
is an edge-labeling, we can denote the common polarisation on the edges
((m,n_{0}),(m,n_{0}+1))
for any
m
as
\mu_{(n_{0},n_{0}+1)}
.
The definition of discrete isothermic surfaces in Definition
2.1
tells us that on any edge
(m,m+1)
, we have

\cratio(f_{m,n_{0}},f_{m+1,n_{0}},f_{m+1,n_{0}+1},f_{m,n_{0}+1})=\frac{\mu_{(n_{0},n_{0}+1)}}{\mu_{(m,m+1)}}.

Thus, the definition of Darboux transformations of discrete polarised curves in Definition
2.12
allows us to deduce the following:

Proposition 3.1
.

Any two neighbouring discrete curvature lines of a discrete isothermic surface form a Darboux pair of discrete polarised curves.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that the cross-ratios factorising functions are well-defined only up to constant multiplication.
Thus, when restricting the polarised domain of a discrete isothermic surface to the polarised domain of a curvature line, there is a freedom of choice in the scaling of the polarisation.

In a similar manner, for a Darboux pair of discrete isothermic surfaces
f,\hat{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{2},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{H}
, consider a corresponding pair of discrete curvature lines
f^{0},\hat{f}^{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{H}
defined on a restricted discrete polarised domain.
Then we have for any edge
(ij)\subset\Sigma^{1}
,

\cratio(f^{0}_{i},f^{0}_{j},\hat{f}^{0}_{j},\hat{f}^{0}_{i})=\frac{\nu}{\mu_{ij}}.

Thus the definition of Darboux transformations of discrete isothermic surfaces in Definition
2.5
directly yield the following observation:

Proposition 3.3
.

Any two corresponding discrete curvature lines of a discrete Darboux pair of isothermic surfaces form a Darboux pair of discrete polarised curves.

Furthermore, the well-definedness of Darboux transformations of discrete isothermic surfaces implies the permutability of Darboux transformations of discrete polarised curves:

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{H}
be a polarised curve, with Darboux transforms
f_{1},f_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{H}
of
f
with respective spectral parameter
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
.
Then there exists a fourth discrete polarised curve
f_{12}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{H}
that is a simultaneous Darboux transform of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
with respective spectral parameter
\nu_{2}
and
\nu_{1}
.

3.2.
Reduction of the monodromy problem

So far, we have obtained the reduction of the integrable structure of discrete isothermic surfaces and its Darboux transformations into a lattice of Darboux transformations of discrete polarised curves.
Such change in perspective is crucial for our consideration of the monodromy problem of Darboux transforms of discrete isothermic surfaces.

Thus, let
f
be a discrete isothermic surface with a period
M
; without loss of generality, we will assume that
f
is periodic in the
m
-curvature line direction so that
f_{m,n}=f_{m+M,n}
for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
.

Now suppose that
\hat{f}
is a Darboux transformation of
f
with spectral parameter
\nu
.
Let us further assume that one of the
m
-curvature lines of
\hat{f}
is periodic with the same period as
f
, i.e.

\hat{f}_{m,n_{0}}=\hat{f}_{m+M,n_{0}}

for some fixed
n_{0}
.
Then from the definition of Darboux transformations, the cross-ratios condition

\cratio(f_{m,n_{0}},f_{m,n_{0}+1},\hat{f}_{m,n_{0}+1},\hat{f}_{m,n_{0}})=\frac{\nu}{\mu_{(n_{0},n_{0}+1)}}

for any
m
implies that
\hat{f}_{m,n_{0}+1}
must be determined uniquely from the values of

f_{m,n_{0}},\quad f_{m,n_{0}+1},\quad\hat{f}_{m,n_{0}},\quad\nu,\quad\mu_{(n_{0},n_{0}+1)}.

However, all of these values are
M
-periodic in the
m
-direction; thus, we have that
\hat{f}_{m,n_{0}+1}
must also be periodic, that is,

\hat{f}_{m,n_{0}+1}=\hat{f}_{m+M,n_{0}+1}.

Propogating to the entire domain, we conclude that the entire surface is periodic, i.e., for any
n
,

\hat{f}_{m,n}=\hat{f}_{m+M,n}.

We have thus proved that the monodromy problem of Darboux transformations of discrete isothermic surfaces can be reduced to a monodromy problem of Darboux transformations of a single discrete curvature line:

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{2},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{H}
be a discrete isothermic surface with one family of periodic curvature lines.
If
\hat{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{2},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{H}
is a Darboux transform of
f
, then the corresponding family of curvature lines of
\hat{f}
are also periodic with the same period if and only if one of the corresponding family of curvature lines is periodic.

Therefore, if
f
is a discrete isothermic surfaces with periodic
m
-curvature lines, we can obtain new periodic isothermic surfaces as follows:

(1)

Choose any base point
f_{m_{0},n_{0}}
of
f
and the
m
-curvature line
f_{m,n_{0}}
through that point.

(2)

Viewing
f_{m,n_{0}}
as a closed discrete polarised curve, find the spectral parameter
\nu
and the initial condition
\hat{f}_{m_{0},n_{0}}
to obtain closed Darboux transform
\hat{f}_{m,n_{0}}
(as a polarised curve).

(3)

Use the spectral parameter
\nu
and the initial condition
\hat{f}_{m_{0},n_{0}}
to obtain a new periodic discrete isothermic surface
\hat{f}
.

In particular, if we have that all curvature lines of a discrete isothermic surface are periodic so that
f
is a discrete torus, then we can also obtain new discrete isothermic tori as follows:

(1)

Choose any base point
f_{m_{0},n_{0}}
of
f
, and take the
m
-curvature line
f_{m,n_{0}}
and
n
-curvature line
f_{m_{0},n}
through that point.

(2)

Viewing both curves as closed discrete polarised curves, find a spectral parameter
\nu
and an initial condition
\hat{f}_{m_{0},n_{0}}
to obtain simultaneously closed Darboux transforms
\hat{f}_{m,n_{0}}
and
\hat{f}_{m_{0},n}
of
f_{m,n_{0}}
and
f_{m_{0},n}
, respectively.

(3)

Use the spectral parameter
\nu
and the initial condition
\hat{f}_{m_{0},n_{0}}
to obtain a new periodic discrete isothermic torus
\hat{f}
.

The main obstacle for the construction of new discrete isothermic tori via Darboux transformations is the second step.

4.
Periodic discrete isothermic surfaces

In this section, we put our theory to test to obtain new examples of periodic discrete isothermic surfaces.
In particular, we will consider the Darboux transforms of surfaces of revolution in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and obtain discrete analogues of isothermic bubbletons (see, for example,
[
18
, § 2.2]
) via Darboux transformations.
By restricting to the case of discrete circular cylinders, we will also obtain the discrete analogues of cmc cylinders called bubbletons
[
32
]
.
Finally, considering the Darboux transforms of homogeneous tori in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, we will construct the discrete analogues of isothermic tori found in
[
1
]
(see also
[
26
, Example 5.4.25]
).

4.1.
Closed Darboux transforms of discrete circles – revisited

The commonground for all the cases we consider in the section is the fact that the closed curvature lines will be multiply-covered discrete circles with constant polarisation.
Thus we first review the closure conditions for the Darboux transforms of multiply-covered discrete circles, where we generalise the calculations from
[
19
, Example 3.8]
to allow for any radius and any constant polarisation.
We note here that we will only consider the discrete circles constructed via uniform sampling of the smooth circle, to obtain explicit results.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(\Sigma^{1},\frac{1}{\mu})\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}\cong\Im\mathbb{H}
be a
\rho
–fold cover of the discrete circle given by

f_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathbbm{j}re^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}m}

for some
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
.
Throughout the paper, we will assume that
M>2
.
Give
f
a constant polarisation which we write as

\frac{1}{\mu_{ij}}=\alpha|1-e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}|^{2}

for some non-zero real constant
\alpha
.
Note that for
\alpha=r^{2}
, then we have

\frac{1}{\mu_{ij}}=r^{2}|1-e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}|^{2}=|f_{i}-f_{j}|^{2}=|{\dif{f}_{ij}}|^{2}

so that
f
is polarised by arc-length.
Then we obtain that
f
is an
\rho M
–periodic discrete polarised curve for any
\rho\in\mathbb{N}
.

To consider the Darboux transforms, let
\hat{F}=\hat{\psi}\mathbb{H}
be
r(\nu)^{P}
–parallel, and
\phi=\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix}
where
\phi=G^{-1}\hat{\psi}

Rewriting (
2.11
), we have that on any three consecutive vertices
(ijp)

\nu\dif{f}_{ij}^{*}a_{i}-(\dif{f}_{ij})^{-1}\dif{a}_{ij}+(\dif{f}_{jp})^{-1}\dif{a}_{jp}=0.

In the case of discrete circles under consideration, the recurrence relation reads

e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}a_{p}-(1+e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}})a_{j}+(1-\nu\alpha|1-e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}|^{2})a_{i}=0.

If we set
a=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}a_{0}+\mathbbm{j}a_{1}
, for some functions
a_{0},a_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma^{2}\to\mathbb{C}

a=a_{0}^{+}c_{0}+a_{0}^{-}c_{1}+\mathbbm{j}(a_{1}^{+}c_{2}+a_{1}^{-}c_{3})

for some constants
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle a_{0,m}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(e^{-\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}(1\pm s)+(1\mp s))\right)^{m}

\displaystyle a_{1,m}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}(1\mp s)+(1\pm s))\right)^{m}

s\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sqrt{1-4\nu\alpha}

To consider the monodromy, we notice that
\phi=\begin{pmatrix}a\\

is an eigenvector of the monodromy matrix if and only if

\phi_{m}h=\phi_{m+\rho M}

(4.3)

h\in\mathbb{H}
.
However, due to the recurrence relation for
a,b

To investigate when the curve admits resonance points, we define

a^{\pm}_{\iota,m}h_{\iota}^{\pm}=a^{\pm}_{\iota,m+\rho M}

h_{0}^{+}=h_{0}^{-}=h_{1}^{+}=h_{1}^{-}.

Since

However, note that

\displaystyle h_{0}^{\pm}=a^{\pm}_{0,\rho M}

\nu

\rho,k\in\mathbb{N}

4.2.
Discrete isothermic cylinders

Suppose we have a discrete surface of revolution in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\cong\Im\mathbb{H}
given by

f_{m,n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathbbm{i}q_{n}+\mathbbm{j}p_{n}e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}m}

for some functions
p,q\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
.
As calculated in
[
13
, § 2]
, we then have that for
i=(m,n)
,

\cratio(f_{i},f_{j},f_{k},f_{\ell})=-\frac{p_{n}p_{n+1}}{(p_{n}-p_{n+1})^{2}+(q_{n}-q_{n+1})^{2}}|1-e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}|^{2}=\frac{\mu_{i\ell}}{\mu_{ij}}.

(4.7)

Clearly,
f
has
M
-periodic
m
-curvature lines.

To consider the monodromy of its Darboux transforms over
\rho
–fold cover in the
m
-direction, we use Theorem
3.5
and consider a single
m
-curvature line, say
f_{m,n_{0}}
for some fixed

.
By Remark

, we have some freedom to choose the correct polarisation
\mu

from (

).
Thus we choose

\frac{1}{\mu_{ij}}=|1-e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}|^{2}

so that any two neighbouring

-curvature lines will be a Darboux pair with spectral parameter

\frac{1}{\mu_{i\ell}}=-\frac{(p_{n}-p_{n+1})^{2}+(q_{n}-q_{n+1})^{2}}{p_{n}p_{n+1}}.

If we assume without loss of generality that
q_{n_{0}}=0

f_{m,n_{0}}=\mathbbm{j}p_{n_{0}}e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}m}.

Thus we have that

is a discrete polarised circle with constant polarisation.

Using the discussions in Section
4.1

\nu
as the resonance point (
4.5

Therefore, Theorem

\hat{f}
of

\nu=\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{M}\tan^{2}\frac{k\pi}{\rho M}\right)

\rho M

4.3.
Discrete isothermic bubbletons via discrete circular cylinder

Now suppose that
f
is a discrete circular cylinder, given by

f_{m,n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathbbm{i}q_{n}+\mathbbm{j}e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}m}.

Then this is a special case of the discrete surface of revolution, so that

\cratio(f_{i},f_{j},f_{k},f_{\ell})=-\frac{1}{(q_{n}-q_{n+1})^{2}}|1-e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}|^{2}=\frac{\mu_{i\ell}}{\mu_{ij}}.

Then any Darboux transform
\hat{f}
of

We now aim to obtain explicit parametrisations for such discrete cylinders: for this, we assume that

(1)

the spectral parameter

\displaystyle a_{m,0}

\displaystyle=\left(e^{-\frac{\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}\cos\tfrac{\pi}{M}\sec\tfrac{k\pi}{\rho M}\right)^{m}\alpha_{m},

\displaystyle\alpha_{m}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{k\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho M}m}c^{+}+e^{\frac{k\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho M}m}c^{-},

\displaystyle\beta_{m}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{j}\left(e^{-\frac{k\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho M}m}\sin{\tfrac{(\rho+k)\pi}{\rho M}}c^{+}+e^{\frac{k\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho M}m}\sin{\tfrac{(\rho-k)\pi}{\rho M}}c^{-}\right)

To find

a_{m,0}=\gamma_{m}^{+}+\gamma_{m}^{-}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{m,0}=\mathbbm{i}(\gamma_{m}^{+}-\gamma_{m}^{-})\sqrt{-\nu}

Using (
4.9

\gamma_{m}^{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{-\frac{\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}\cos\tfrac{\pi}{M}\sec\tfrac{k\pi}{\rho M}\right)^{m}\left(\alpha_{m}\mp\frac{\mathbbm{i}}{2\sqrt{-\nu}}\csc{\tfrac{\pi}{M}}\sec{\tfrac{k\pi}{\rho M}}\beta_{m}\right).

Thus, we have found closed-form solutions for the functions

and

\hat{f}_{m,n}=f_{m,n}+a_{m,n}b_{m,n}^{-1}.

Example 4.1
.

For example, if we additionally assume that

4.4.
Discrete cmc bubbletons

Discrete circular cylinders are discrete cmc surfaces, which can be verified as follows: noting that

\displaystyle f^{*}_{m,n}-f^{*}_{m+1,n}

\displaystyle=\dif{f}^{*}_{ij}=\frac{1}{\mu_{ij}}\dif{f}_{ij}^{-1}=-\mathbbm{j}e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}m}+\mathbbm{j}e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}(m+1)}

\displaystyle f^{*}_{m,n}-f^{*}_{m,n+1}

\displaystyle=\dif{f}^{*}_{i\ell}=\frac{1}{\mu_{i\ell}}\dif{f}_{i\ell}^{-1}=\mathbbm{i}q_{n}-\mathbbm{i}q_{n+1},

we take

f^{*}_{0,0}=\mathbbm{i}q_{0}-\mathbbm{j}

as the initial condition to obtain

f^{*}_{m,n}=\mathbbm{i}q_{n}-\mathbbm{j}e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}m}

as the dual surface.
Therefore, we have

f^{*}_{m,n}-f_{m,n}=-2\mathbbm{j}e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}m},

so that

|f^{*}-f|^{2}=4,

allowing us to conclude that
f
is a discrete cmc surface with
H\equiv\frac{1}{2}
by Definition
2.8
.

Thus, if we can choose the correct initial condition using Fact
2.10
, then we will obtain new discrete cmc cylinders via Darboux transformations.
To find such initial condition, we first note that

\cratio(f_{i},f_{j},f^{*}_{j},f^{*}_{i})=\frac{1}{4}|1-e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}|^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\cratio(f_{i},f_{\ell},f^{*}_{\ell},f^{*}_{i})=-\frac{1}{4}(q_{n}-q_{n+1})^{2}

so that
f^{*}
is a Darboux transform of
f
with spectral parameter
H^{2}=\frac{1}{4}
(confirming Fact
2.9
).
Thus for some point
i\in\Sigma^{2}
, we must find
\hat{f}_{i}
so that

|\hat{f}_{i}-f^{*}_{i}|^{2}=4\left(1-\frac{1}{4\nu}\right),

which exists if and only if
\nu>\frac{1}{4}
or
\nu<0
.

To investigate the conditions on

for the spectral parameter at a resonance point

\nu=\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{M}\tan^{2}\frac{k\pi}{\rho M}\right)

\nu>\frac{1}{4}
or
\nu<0

k=\rho

\nu=0

k\neq\rho
.
Suppose that
0<k<\rho
.
On the inverval
u\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
,
\sin u
is a strictly increasing positive function while
\cos u
is a strictly decreasing positive function so that

0<\sin\frac{k\pi}{\rho M}<\sin\frac{\pi}{M}\quad\text{and}\quad 0<\cos\frac{\pi}{M}<\cos\frac{k\pi}{\rho M}.

Thus, we deduce

0<\tan\frac{k\pi}{\rho M}<\tan\frac{\pi}{M},

that is,

0<\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{M}\tan^{2}\frac{k\pi}{\rho M}<1.

This implies that

0<\nu<\frac{1}{4}

so that we do not have discrete cmc cylinders appearing as Darboux transforms of the discrete circular cylinders when
k<\rho
.
This is the discrete analogue of the smooth case investigated in
[
32

(see also
[
17

On the other hand, if
k>\rho

M
so that
\frac{k}{\rho M}<\frac{1}{2}
, we have

0<\sin\frac{\pi}{M}<\sin\frac{k\pi}{\rho M}<1\quad\text{and}\quad 0<\cos\frac{k\pi}{\rho M}<\cos\frac{\pi}{M}.

Thus, in this case

0<\tan\frac{\pi}{M}<\tan\frac{k\pi}{\rho M}

so that

1<\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{M}\tan^{2}\frac{k\pi}{\rho M}.

In other words, we have
\nu<0
.
Thus, when we choose the spectral parameter
\nu
at the resonance point with
k>\rho

M
, we can find a correct initial condition to obtain another discrete cmc cylinder via Darboux transformation.
These are the discrete analogues of the bubbletons constructed in
[
32
]
.

4.5.
Discrete isothermic tori

To construct non-trivial examples of discrete isothermic tori, let us consider the discrete homogeneous tori in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
as our given surface
f
so that

f_{m,n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=qe^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{N}n}+\mathbbm{j}pe^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}m}

for non-zero
M,N\in\mathbb{Z}
and
p,q\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
p^{2}+q^{2}=1
.
We take the
\rho_{1}
–fold cover in the
m
-direction and
\rho_{2}
–fold cover in the
n
-direction.
Then we calculate that

\cratio(f_{i},f_{j},f_{k},f_{\ell})=-\frac{p^{2}|1-e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}|^{2}}{q^{2}|1-e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{N}}|^{2}}=\frac{\mu_{i\ell}}{\mu_{ij}}.

Thus we assume without loss of generality that

\frac{1}{\mu_{ij}}=p^{2}|1-e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}|^{2},\quad\frac{1}{\mu_{i\ell}}=-q^{2}|1-e^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{N}}|^{2},

so that every
m
-curvature line is arc-length polarised while every
n
-curvature line is negative arc-length polarised.

Choosing any base point
(m_{0},n_{0})
, let us first take the
m
-curvature line
f_{m,n_{0}}
through the base point.
Then since we have a discrete circle with constant polarisation, we deduce that any of its Darboux transforms
\hat{f}_{m,n_{0}}
with spectral parameter

\nu_{1}=\frac{1}{4p^{2}}\left(1-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{M}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{1}\pi}{\rho_{1}M}\right)

for any
k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
results in a closed discrete polarised curve.
Similarly, the
n
-curvature line
f_{m_{0},n}
is also a discrete circle with constant polarisation, so that any of its Darboux transforms
\hat{f}_{m_{0},n}
with spectral parameter

\nu_{2}=-\frac{1}{4q^{2}}\left(1-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{N}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}N}\right)

for
k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
results in a closed discrete polarised curve.

Thus, if we choose
p
and
q
so that

\displaystyle p^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{M}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{1}\pi}{\rho_{1}M}}{\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{N}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}N}-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{M}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{1}\pi}{\rho_{1}M}},

(4.12)

\displaystyle q^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{N}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}N}}{\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{M}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{1}\pi}{\rho_{1}M}-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{N}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}N}},

then we have
p^{2}+q^{2}=1
, and

\nu\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{N}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}M}-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{M}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{1}\pi}{\rho_{1}M}\right).

(4.13)

This implies that for such
p
and
q
, we have that every Darboux transform with such spectral parameter
\nu=\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}
gives a discrete isothermic torus.
These are the discrete analogues of the isothermic tori found in
[
1
]
(see also
[
26
]
).

To obtain closed-form parametrisations of the discrete isothermic tori, let us take
p
and
q
as in (
4.12
) so that the spectral parameter satisfies

\nu=\frac{1}{4p^{2}}\left(1-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{M}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{1}\pi}{\rho_{1}M}\right)=-\frac{1}{4q^{2}}\left(1-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{N}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}N}\right).

Since
f_{m,0}=q+\mathbbm{j}pe^{\frac{2\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}m
, we use the discussions from Section
4.1
to obtain that

\displaystyle a_{m,0}

\displaystyle=\left(e^{-\frac{\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}\cos\tfrac{\pi}{M}\sec\tfrac{k_{1}\pi}{\rho_{1}M}\right)^{m}\alpha_{m},

(4.14)

\displaystyle b_{m,0}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2p}\csc{\tfrac{\pi}{M}}\sec{\tfrac{k_{1}\pi}{\rho_{1}M}}\left(e^{-\frac{\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}\cos\tfrac{\pi}{M}\sec\tfrac{k_{1}\pi}{\rho_{1}M}\right)^{m}\beta_{m}

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{m}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{k_{1}\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho_{1}M}m}c^{+}+e^{\frac{k_{1}\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho_{1}M}m}c^{-},

\displaystyle\beta_{m}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{j}\left(e^{-\frac{k_{1}\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho_{1}M}m}\sin{\tfrac{(\rho_{1}+k_{1})\pi}{\rho_{1}M}}c^{+}+e^{\frac{k_{1}\pi_{1}\mathbbm{i}}{\rho_{1}M}m}\sin{\tfrac{(\rho_{1}-k_{1})\pi}{\rho_{1}M}}c^{-}\right)

for some

Now to solve along an
n
-curvature line direction, we fix some

and note that

-curvature lines are also circles; thus, we can use a completely analogous argument in Section

to find that

\displaystyle a_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\big(e^{\frac{\pi\mathbbm{i}}{N}}\cos\tfrac{\pi}{N}\sec\tfrac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}N}\big)^{n}\Big(e^{\frac{k_{2}\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho_{2}N}n}\gamma^{+}_{m}+e^{-\frac{k_{2}\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho_{2}N}n}\gamma^{-}_{m}\Big),

\displaystyle b_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}-\tfrac{1}{2q}\csc{\tfrac{\pi}{N}}\sec{\tfrac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}N}}\big(e^{-\frac{\pi\mathbbm{i}}{N}}\cos\tfrac{\pi}{N}\sec\tfrac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}N}\big)^{n}\\
\qquad\Big(e^{\frac{k_{2}\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho_{2}N}n}\sin\tfrac{(\rho_{2}+k_{2})\pi}{\rho_{2}N}\gamma^{+}_{m}+e^{-\frac{k_{2}\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho_{2}N}n}\sin\tfrac{(\rho_{2}-k_{2})\pi}{\rho_{2}N}\gamma^{-}_{m}\Big)\end{multlined}

for some quaternion valued functions
\gamma^{\pm}_{m}
of

.
To find
\gamma^{\pm}_{m}

in (

\displaystyle a_{m,0}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{+}_{m}+\gamma^{-}_{m},

\displaystyle b_{m,0}

\gamma^{\pm}_{m}

\displaystyle=\!\begin{multlined}\mp\tfrac{1}{2}\sec{\tfrac{\pi}{N}}\csc{\tfrac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}N}}\big(e^{-\frac{\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}\cos\tfrac{\pi}{M}\sec\tfrac{k_{1}\pi}{\rho_{1}M}\big)^{m}\\

\displaystyle=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mp\tfrac{1}{2}\sec{\tfrac{\pi}{N}}\csc{\tfrac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}N}}\big(e^{-\frac{\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}}\cos\tfrac{\pi}{M}\sec\tfrac{k_{1}\pi}{\rho_{1}M}\big)^{m}\tilde{\gamma}^{\pm}_{m}.

Summarzing, we have

a_{m,n}b_{m,n}^{-1}=\!\begin{multlined}-2q\sin\tfrac{\pi}{N}\cos\tfrac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}N}e^{-\frac{\pi\mathbbm{i}}{M}m}e^{\frac{\pi\mathbbm{i}}{N}n}\Big(e^{\frac{k_{2}\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho_{2}N}n}\tilde{\gamma}^{+}_{m}-e^{-\frac{k_{2}\pi\mathbbm{i}}{\rho_{2}N}n}\tilde{\gamma}^{-}_{m}\Big)\\

where

2.7

4.6.
Continuum limit of the resonance points

Note that the calculation of resonance points in each section is dependent on the number of subdivisions of the smooth circle.
Therefore, for the resonance points, taking the continuum limit corresponds to taking the limit as the number of subdivisions tend to infinity.
As we have investigated the monodromy problem of the discrete isothermic surfaces under structure-preserving discretisation, it is fair to expect that the resonance points of the discrete objects also converge to those of the smooth counterpart under the continuum limit.
We can check the convergence explicitly as follows.

For the surface of revolution case, we see that

\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{M}\tan^{2}\frac{k\pi}{\rho M}\right)=\frac{\rho^{2}-k^{2}}{4\rho^{2}}

for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, which is identical to the resonance point of surface of revolutions in the smooth case (see, for example,
[
18
, Section 2]
).

Also for the homogeneous tori case, we can similarly check that resonance points of the discrete case (
4.13
) converge to that of the smooth case:

\displaystyle\lim_{M,N\to\infty}\nu

\displaystyle=\lim_{M,N\to\infty}\frac{1}{4}\left(\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{N}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{2}\pi}{\rho_{2}N}-\cot^{2}\frac{\pi}{M}\tan^{2}\frac{k_{1}\pi}{\rho_{1}M}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{k_{2}^{2}}{\rho_{2}^{2}}-\frac{k_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{1}^{2}}\right).

In fact, it follows that even the radii of the discrete homogeneous tori admitting isothermic tori as Darboux transforms converge to the radii of the smooth case.
We can check this explicitly by noting that

\lim_{M,N\to\infty}(1-4\nu p^{2})=\frac{k_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{1}^{2}},\quad\lim_{M,N\to\infty}(1+4\nu q^{2})=\frac{k_{2}^{2}}{\rho_{2}^{2}},

which is consistent with the smooth case in
[
26
, Equation (5.24)]
.

These observations suggest that the monodromy problem of Darboux transforms of smooth isothermic surfaces can be investigated via structure preserving discretisation without any numerical integration.
