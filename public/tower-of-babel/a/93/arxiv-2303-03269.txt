Aristotle’s Second-Order Logic
Clarence Protin

Abstract
This paper has the following aims. We study the metalogic of the Organon and other works of Aristotle with special attention to the evidence provided by the later work of Galen. We attempt to formalize the metalogic implicit in the Analytics and the Physics which is at the same time, so we argue, the logic implicit in the Topics, and our proposal is that this consists in an extension of second-order logic presented in a variant of natural deduction which includes non-monadic predicates which we call Aristotelian second-order logic (ASOL) Furthermore we argue that Aristotle actually made conscious use of the key quantifier rules as well as propositional rules thus lending further support to the thesis argued by Bobzien [ 6 ] that ancient logic had sufficient means to deal effectively with multiple generality. The second part of the paper is dedicated to deploying ASOL to formalize the Topics and showing the logical, linguistic and philosophical interest of this often neglected work.

Why do we consider ESOL as being an extension of second-order logic rather than a fragment of third-order logic ? We are of the view that what defines the ’order’ of a logic is what is quantified over and what kind of corresponding variable there is. First-order logic quantifies over individuals and has individual variables. Second-order logic quantifies over entities corresponding to
n
-ary predicates with individual arguments and has the corresponding
n
-ary predicate term variables. Third-order logic thus would have to quantify over entities corresponding to predicates which can involve both individual arguments and
n
-ary predicates of individuals arguments and furthermore it would require variables to represent these last. Such a quantification and such variables are lacking in ESOL although the predicate term symbols
\alpha^{(\tau)}
can be considered in some sense as being ’third-order’ when involving
n
-predicate term arguments.

There is an alternative version of the existential elimination rules (both first- and second-order) inspired by Hilbert’s

The operator

If we have that there exists an A, then we can form the term ’an A’ so as to be able to postulate in the context of a proof: let a be an A. An example of a natural language expression of the rule above: every man has a father, hence, every man’s father is the father of that man (or: if every man has a father then a father of a man is that man’s father). Metalogic of the Analytics

There have been many attempts to formalize Aristotle’s syllogistic expounded in the
Prior Analytics
in both its strictly assertoric and modal variants (see
[
17
]
for references). Some approaches involve monadic (modal or otherwise) first-order logic and other approaches use variants (modal of otherwise) of propositional logic. Additionally many authors assume that Aristotle reasons much like a modern logician by constructing models to refute the validity of certain syllogistic figures.
The above approaches usually set up a minimal set of axioms together with a deductive system and attempts to show the agreement (at in least for most cases) between the derivable syllogisms and those considered valid by Aristotle (how Aristotle himself derived or justifies the validity or non-validity playing a secondary role). Our concern is different. We are interested in the structure of the proofs in the
Analytics
themselves, how Aristotle argues for the validity or non-validity of a certain figure or propositional conversion which is reminiscent of the modern division between metalogic and object logic.
A noteworthy characteristic of the
Analytics
is the presence of variable letters ranging over Aristotelian
terms
which (so we argue) usually correspond to monadic predicate terms in second-order logic. There is a distinguished class of entities for which it is meaningful to ask if such entities fall or do not fall under such monadic predicate term - these we call
individuals
. Thus we can ask of a monadic predicate term is predicated or not of a given individual.

It is patent that a formalization of Aristotle’s reasoning must be a term-logic, that is, terms must be the basic building blocks - the most basic division being between individual terms (corresponding to ’first substances’) and non-individual terms. Sentences (note that the
heis logos
,
De Int.
17a16-17 cannot be identified with ’atomic’ sentences in the modern sense as they may contain connectives) are then built up from combinations of terms in a specified manner. Predication - and in Aristotle there are many distinct notions of predication hiding under the verb
\emph{esti}
- corresponds to combinations of terms tagged according to the kind of predication being considered. Thus if ① corresponded to predicating a non-individual term
A
of an individual term
a
(let us say according to ’essence’) and ② corresponded to predicating a non-individual term
B
of another
A
according to ’essence’ then grammatically (for SOV languages like Ancient Greek) and notationally the most natural representation would be aA① and AB②. Such a representation is necessarily if we wish to formalize correctly Aristotle’s essentially intensional logic: the predication of definition and the predication of property (
idion
) both imply that the terms have equal ’extension’ (in the sense of individuals falling under them) but the types of predication are different as are the terms: ’being able to laugh’ is not the same term as ’rational animal’ but both are co-extensional with ’man’. Thus we have grounds for holding that an extension of second-order logic like ASOL (in which the circled number tags correspond to our second-order predicates and ① to simple concatenation in reverse order
Aa
) is an adequate minimal formalization for the logical-argumentative apparatus in the
Organon
and even, as we shall discuss later, in the
Physics
.
What we wrote above applies to ’atomic’ sentences. But do not modern formal systems with their full inductive definitions of formulas have greater complexity and precision than can be reconstructed from Aristotle’s system embodying an only seemingly rudimentary account of complex sentences ? Against this worry we reply that the fact of the matter is that rigorous structural inductive definitions of sentences are not an exclusive possession of modern logic. A perfectly ’modern’ definition of the formulas of what we would now consider an extension of modal propositional logic (a multi-modal propositional logic) is found in Ockham’s
Summa Logicae
[
14
]
[79-80]. Going back to antiquity there is strong evidence for such inductive definitions in Stoic Logic
[
6
]

The Logic of the Topics

Aristotle’s
Topics
is generally considered to be an early and indeed composite work, reflecting perhaps rather than specifically Aristotelian ideas, common dialectical procedures employed at the Platonic Academy. Indeed the intended purpose of the work is precisely to serve as a debating manual. It is also the part of the
Organon
that has received less attention from scholars. We hold that such a neglect is undeserved and that the
Topics
is quite a rich work and of considerable interest in its own right. Indeed it is impossible to do justice to all its aspects in the scope of the present paper. The
Topics
transcends the strictly ’logical’ concerns of the
Analytics
as grammatical, semantic, ontological, pragmatic and epistemic aspects equally predominate. From the semantic angle the concern with ambiguity and synonymity is almost an obsession, a fact that is likely explained by preoccupation with facing the rhetorical devices of the sophists. In this section we only consider some aspects of the purely logical content of the
Topics
.
The
Topics
is a debate manual, but for a very restricted and formalized kind of debate, concerned with set of basic relations among terms

Unfortunately Aristotle’s view of grammar and his theory of terms and concepts is only documented in a few scattered passages (but see chapter 4 of book IV which deals with relative terms and their governed cases.). We know more about Stoic grammar and its grammar-based logic. A basic linguistic entity is the sentence or protasis. A sentence is made up of two linguistic expressions connected by a certain propositional relation. The two expressions are called subject and predicate depending on their position in the propositional relation. Only noun phrases can be a subject but adjectival and verbal constructions can be predicates. These can becomes subjects after a certain syntactic transformation. The original concept of ’verb’ appears to have been that of predicate. A ’term’ oros is what can be a subject. However there is also a sense in which a verb is term to which is added a temporal aspect.
The Stoic concept of kategorema seems to fit more exactly the concept of predicate. A predicate can be considered in itself, as unsaturated by any concrete subject. In Plato’s
Sophist

Formalizing book V

In this section we focus on book V and discuss some of the challenges facing the ASOL approach and its versatility in solving them.

It is probable that the elaborate theory of definition in book VI grew initially from the theory of property (idion) . Some textual evidence can be adduced in favor of this (cf. 154b). There are passages in which Aristotle relaxes all the complex structural requirements on the definiens term, even simple terms are allowed to be definitions; on the other hand a passage in 132a indicates that property must have a genus + specifications structure just like definition. How are we to understand that ’terrestrial bipedial animal’ indicates the essence of man but that (132a) ’tame (hêmeron) animal’ or ’animal gifted with knowledge’ somehow does not ? It might be tempting to view book V of the Topics, dealing with property, to be both among the oldest portions of the said work and perhaps even as having constituted an independent treatise. It is true that definition, even difference as well as predication ’according to essence’, are mentioned (and contrasted with property) in book V, but not in a detailed way, rather the passages in question could easily be viewed as later interpolations or revisions. We propose that book V be studied on its own in a more-or-less self-contained way.

In the most basic sense, property is related to co-extensionality as in

Conclusion

We have shown how ASOL is the most natural framework to interpret the ’metalogic’ of the
Analytics
and the logic implicit in the
Topics
. The structure of the sentence and inferences involved confirms the competency of ancient logicians with regards to quantifier logic and multiple generality.
If second-order logic is the most natural framework to read the
Organon
as a whole then this sheds new light of the standard narrative of the development of Western logic suggesting a much greater formal and conceptual continuity between ancient logic and Frege’s system of the
Begriffsschrift
which is usually equated to second-order logic with a term-forming extension operator. If Frege’s formal system has structural similarities with Aristotelian logic as argued for in this paper, his philosophy of logic and language has been argued (by Bobzien and others ) to have been influenced by the Stoics.

We now discuss in light of the present work the system of argumentation for which the
Topics
was written. In the Platonic Dialogues (particularly the early

and middle ones) as well as in Aristotle there is the fundamental supposition that philosophical knowledge can be gained by finding the right definition of terms (under the proviso that the term in question is acknowledged to exist, that is, have non-empty extension.). This concern is not abandoned in the
Analytics
. The Socratic attitude is that the first step to wisdom consists in testing one’s beliefs which can lead to the valuable cathartic knowledge of the error of one’s beliefs. Thus a major part of philosophical activity involved the testing (i.e. attempt at refutation) of proposed definitions of terms - something which Aristotle acknowledges to be easier than finding correct definitions

A massive amount of arguments were compiled during the eras of the late Academia and first Stoic schools, as testified by accounts of the lives of Chrysippus and Carneades.
. In a modern framework how are we to understand (broadly speaking) the dialectical game of the
Topics
? In ASOL in which we assume we have a finite collection of constants, functions and predicate symbols (first and second order) which represent the disambiguated vocabulary of a common language. The non-logical axioms we assume are

. Then Bob basically has to try to derive a contradiction

. The way the game was played apparently involved Alice confirming every move made by Bob, as attested in the Socratic dialogues (where it is rare for the ’opponent’ not to agree with Socrates’ moves). The ’moves’ can be

Lysis
although the types of definition considered do not fall into Aristotle’s scheme for monadic predicates terms outlined in book VI of the
Topics
. As discussed at the end of the previous section, it would be interesting to study the early and middle Platonic dialogues from a second-order perspective as in the present work so as to provide additional evidence for logical competency in dealing with multiple generality among the ancients.

We can of course question if this dialectical game could lead to actual progress in philosophy or to certain or plausible knowledge about reality. In particular the exact scope of

seems to be vague and uncertain; the assumption of the possession of a perfectly disambiguated language is itself implausible (although it should be said that the
Topics

Rather what was required is a preliminary radical epistemic, semantic and logical analysis in order to set up a relevant and meaningful system - and such concerns are in no way foreign to the mature Aristotle: things better known to us are not necessarily better known in themselves
