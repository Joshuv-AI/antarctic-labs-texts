A non-autonomous p p -Adic diffusion equation on time changing graphs
Patrick Erik Bradley, Ángel Morán Ledezma

Abstract
Motivated by the recently proven presence of ultrametricity in physical models (certain spin glasses) and the very recent study of Turing patterns on locally ultrametric state spaces, first non-autonomous diffusion operators on such spaces, where finitely many compact p p -adic spaces are joined by a graph structure, are studied, including their Dirichlet and van Neumann eigenvalues. Secondly, the Cauchy problem for the heat equation associated with these operators is solved, its solution approximated by Trotter products, and thirdly, the corresponding Feller property as well as the Markov property (a Hunt process) is established.



1
Introduction

The Gibbs measure associated with certain mean-field spin glass models, including the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model, was conjectured to have ultrametric support in the thermodynamic limit

[
11
]
. This conjecture, known as the
Parisi ultrametricity conjecture
, was proved by Panchenko under the condition that the so-called
Ghirlanda-Guerra identities
are satisfied
[
13
]
.
This conjecture (and now result) has motivated up to this day the study of random processes on ultrametric state spaces, the most studied type being diffusion on the
p
-adic numbers, cf. e.g.
[
12
,
8
]
and many other research articles in the physical and mathematical literature.

Ultrametricity of a space means that it has a hierarchical structure, i.e. its points can be viewed as the boundary of a tree, and therefore its topology, as well as any physical process on it, are determined by this tree structure. The
p
-adic numbers form a convenient mathematical framework for studying ultrametric spaces by viewing those trees as part of the regular tree structure of the
p
-adic numbers—as long as the vertex degrees do not exceed the bound given by the
p
-adic regularity.
However, if the state space is only locally ultrametric, then it seemed unclear how to mathematically model diffusion on such structures until Zúñiga-Galindo’s idea of modelling interactions between those local ultrametric pieces appeared via an externally imposed graph structure on this ensemble of pieces which then can be viewed as disjoint compact open subsets of the
p
-adic number field
\mathbb{Q}_{p}

[
19
]
. He even argues that
p
-adic analysis is a natural tool for studying Turing patterns on mean-field models.
In his case, these local pieces were modelled as
p
-adic balls, and thus he was lead to define a
p
-adic Laplacian integral operator resembling the integral operator representation of the well-studied Vladimirov (or Vladimirov-Taibleson) operator, cf.
[
16
,
18
]
.

The study of non-autonomous diffusion on
p
-adic domains seems to have been given significantly less consideration in the literature.
This motivates the authors of this article to investigate diffusion processes on locally hierarchical structures whose interactions (given as weighted edges) are time-dependent. The vertices of the underlying time-dependent graph are fixed in this setting. After clarifying the construction of
p
-adic operators associated with finite graphs—the “dictionary” set up in
[
2
]
needs a correction—properties of a time-dependent version of the Zúñiga operators from
[
19
]
are studied, proving that the uniform continuity of the kernel function implies the strong continuity of the new time-dependent operator (Theorem
2.11
) which is necessary for proving the Feller property later.
Dirichlet and van Neumann eigenvalues are calculated (Theorem
2.13
), whereby the corresponding boundary conditions are generalised from graph theory. Thereafter,
the non-autonomous Cauchy problem for the heat equation associated with these operators is solved (Theorem
3.9
), thereby approximating the solution using Trotter products, and then finding that the temporal discretisation error is in first order
O\left(\frac{1}{n}e^{\frac{1}{n}}\right)
(Corollary
3.11
). In the end the Feller property (Theorem
4.6
) and corresponding Markov property—a Hunt process—is established (Theorem
4.8
).

Remark 2.5

This is important in order to define an analogous operator to the Laplacian operator in the

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}u(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{K_{N}}A(x,y)(u(y)-u(x))dy

\lambda_{S,\mathbb{L}}^{(N)}(t)=\inf\limits_{f\neq 0\atop\int_{S}f(x)\gamma(x)\,dx=0}\frac{p^{N}\int_{\Omega_{S}^{*}(t)}A(x,y,t)\left\lvert f(x)-f(y)\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dy}{\int_{S}\left\lvert f(x)\right\rvert^{2}\gamma(x)(t)\,dx}

where it is assumed that
\delta_{N}S(t)\neq\emptyset
for this instance of
t\geq 0
.

The Dirichlet and von Neumann eigenvalues of
\mathcal{G}(t)
w.r.t.
S
will be denoted as

\lambda_{1,\mathcal{G}}^{(D)}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{S,\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}(t),

respectively.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\psi\colon S\to\mathbb{C}
be a Kozyrev wavelet. Then
\psi\in N_{S}^{*}(t)
, i.e.
\psi
satisfies the von Neumann boundary condition.

Proof.

Assume that the Kozyrev wavelet
\psi
is supported in a ball
B\subset B_{p^{-N}(I)}
for some vertex
I\in V(S)
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{(x,y)\in\partial_{N}S(t)}

\displaystyle A(x,y,t)(\psi(x)-\psi(y))\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{(x,y)\in\partial_{N}S(t)}A(x,y,t)\,dy\,\psi(x)

\displaystyle-\int_{(x,y)\in\partial_{N}S(t)}A(x,y,t)\psi(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=0-0=0

where the left term vanishes, because
x\in\delta_{N}S(t)
, and
the right integral vanishes, because
\psi
is a Kozyrev wavelet, and from Kozyrev’s theorem that

\int_{K_{N}}\psi(x)\,dx=0,

cf. e.g.
[
10
, Thm. 3.29]
or
[
1
, Thm. 9.4.2]
.
∎

Theorem 2.13
.

Let
S\subseteq K_{N}
be compact open, and assume that

\delta S(t)
is non-empty for
t\in[0,a)
with
a>0
. Then the following statements hold true:

1.

\lambda_{1,\mathbb{L}}^{(D)}(t)\leq\min{\left\{\lambda_{1,\mathcal{G}}^{(D)}(t),\hat{\gamma}_{S}(t)\right\}}\leq 1,

2.

\lambda_{S,{\mathbb{L}}}^{(N)}(t)\leq\min{\left\{\lambda_{S,\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}(t),\hat{\gamma}_{S}(t)\right\}}
,

where

\hat{\gamma}_{S}(t)=\min\limits_{I\in V(S)}\frac{\deg_{\partial V(S)(t)}(I)}{\int_{S}\gamma(t)(x)\,dx}

and
\deg_{\partial V(S)(t)}(I)

is the degree of vertex
I
in the edge boundary
\partial V(S)(t)
, viewed as a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(t)
, for
t\in[0,a)
.

Proof.

The inequalities
\lambda_{1,\mathbb{L}}^{(D)}(t)\leq\lambda_{1,\mathcal{G}}^{(D)}(t)
and
\lambda_{S,\mathbb{L}}^{(N)}(t)\leq\lambda_{S,\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}(t)
are obvious. Now, let
\psi
be a Kozyrev wavelet supported in
B_{p^{-r}}(I)\subset B_{p^{-N}}(I)\cap S
for some vertex
I\in V(S)
.
It clearly satisfies the Dirichlet boundary condition, and, by Lemma
2.12
, also the von Neumann boundary condition.
Hence, the Dirichlet and the von Neumann eigenvalues are both less than or equal to:

\frac{\sum\limits_{J\in\delta V(S)(t)}A_{IJ}}{\int_{S}\gamma(x)(t)\,dx}=\frac{\deg_{\partial V(S)(t)}(I)}{\int_{S}\gamma(x)(t)\,dx}

where
\delta V(S)(t)
is the vertex boundary of the set
V(S)\subset V
in
\mathcal{G}(t)
. This now shows the first inequality in
1.
, and that of
2.

The remaining inequality in
1
. is given in
[
5
, §8.4]
. This proves the theorem.
∎

The following examples show that the inequalities can be strict:

Example 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{G}(t)
be the complete graph on three vertices
a,b,c
, and let
S=B_{p^{-N}}(a)\sqcup B_{p^{-N}}(b)
.
Then, according to
[
5
, §8.4]
,
\lambda_{1,\mathcal{G}}^{(D)}(t)
is an eigenvalue of the submatrix of the graph-Laplacian of
\mathcal{G}(t)
spanned by the vertices
a,b
.
This is the matrix

L_{S}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-\frac{1}{2}\\
-\frac{1}{2}&1\end{pmatrix}

with eigenvalues
\frac{3}{2}
and
\frac{1}{2}
.
Hence,
\lambda_{1,\mathcal{G}}^{(D)}(t)=\frac{1}{2}
. But since the degree of each vertex of
\mathcal{G}(t)
equals
2
, it follows that

\lambda_{1,\mathbb{L}}^{(D)}(t)\leq\hat{\gamma}_{S}(t)=\frac{1}{4}<\frac{1}{2}=\lambda_{1,\mathcal{G}}^{(D)}(t)

for given

.

Example 2.15
.

Let
\mathcal{G}(t)
be the complete graph on two vertices
a,b
, and let

. Then any non-trivial function

This means that

and consequently

.

3.1
Review of the abstract Cauchy problem

Let
A(t)
be a family of operators with
t\in\mathbb{R}
on a Banach space
X
. The following problem is addressed:

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t)=A(t)u(t)\\
u(s)=x\in X\end{cases}

(6)

with
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\geq s
. The system
(\ref{nACP_abstract})
is called (nACP).

Definition 3.1
.

A continuous function
u:[s,\infty)\rightarrow X
is called a (strict)
solution
of (
6
), if
u\in C^{1}([s,\infty),X)
,
u(t)\in D(A(t))
for all
t\geq s
,
u(s)=x
, and
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=A(t)u
for
t\geq 0
.

The solution of non-autonomous Cauchy problem on some Banach space
X
can be given by a so called
evolution family
which can be defined as follows, cf.
[
15
]
:

Definition 3.2
.

A family
(U(t,s))_{t\geq s}
of linear operators on a Banach space
X
is called an
evolution family
for (
6
), if

1.

U(t,r)U(r,s)=U(t,s)
,
U(t,t)=id
,
t\geq s\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.

The mapping
(s,t)\mapsto U(t,s)
is strongly continuous.

3.

||U(t,s)||\leq Me^{\omega(t-s)}
for some
M\geq 1
,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
,
t\geq s\in\mathbb{R}
.

4.

For
s\in\mathbb{R}
the regularity space

Y_{s}:=\{y\in X:[s,\infty)\ni t\rightarrow U(t,s)y\ solves\ (nACP)\}

is dense in X, where
U(t,s)y
is the unique solution of (nACP).

If there exists a solving evolution family, then (
6
) is called
well-posed
. We have the following Theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
X
a Banach space and for every
t>0
let
A(t)
be a bounded linear operator on
X
. If the function
t\mapsto A(t)
is strongly continuous for
0\leq t<T

(that is,
t\mapsto A(t)u
is continuous for each
u\in X
), then for every
x\in X
the problem (
6
) is well-posed.

Proof.

[
7
, Thm. 7.1.1]
.
∎

We can apply the above Theorem to the problem (
5
) for time-changing graphs.

Proposition 3.4
.

The system (
5
) is well-posed.

Proof.
This is a direct consequence of Theorem

2.11
. ∎

Remark 3.5
.

If we let
U(t,s)x=u(t)
, where
u(t)
is the solution of (
5
), then the following holds true:

1.

||U(t,s)||\leq e^{\int_{s}^{t}||\mathbb{L}(\tau)||d\tau}
.

2.

(t,s)\mapsto U(t,s)
is continuous in the uniform operator topology for
s\leq t
.

3.

\partial U(t,s)/\partial t=\mathcal{L}(t)U(t,s)
.

4.

\partial U(t,s)/\partial s=-U(t,s)\mathcal{L}(s)
.

This is due to the fact that
t\mapsto\mathbb{L}(t)
is continuous in the uniform norm topology, cf.
[
14
, Theorem 5.2]
.

In general, the solution does not have a closed form, but in special cases it does, as the following example shows.

Example 3.6
.

Under the hypothesis of Theorem
3.3
, assume that
A(t)
and
\int_{s}^{t}A(\tau)d\tau
commute for each
s,t
. Then

U(t,s)=e^{\int_{s}^{t}A(\tau)d\tau}

with the integral
\int A(\tau)d\tau
being interpreted ”elementwise”.

Proof.

[
7
, Example 7.1.9]
.
∎

3.2
Reviewing the autonomous case for graphs

First, we review how to solve the heat equation in the autonomous case:

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x,t)=\mathbb{L}u(x,t)\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\in L^{2}(K_{n},\mathbb{C})\end{cases}\,,

(7)

where
\mathbb{L}
is interpreted as
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}(t_{0})
for some
t_{0}\geq 0
. The following Theorem allows us to apply the Fourier Method (or the separation of variables method) to solve the system (
7
).

Theorem 3.7

(Zúñiga-Galindo, 2020)
.

The elements of the set:

\Spec(L)\bigsqcup\{-\gamma_{I}:I\in V(\mathcal{G})\}

are the eigenvalues of of
\mathbb{L}
. The corresponding eigenfunctions are given by the following infinite set

\left\{\frac{\varphi_{\mu}}{||\varphi_{\mu}||_{2}}:\mu\in\Spec(L)\right\}\bigsqcup\left\{\Psi_{j,I,r}:I\in V(\mathcal{G}),j\in\{1,...,p-1\},r\in\mathbb{Z},r\geq N\right\},

where the functions
\varphi_{\mu}(x)
are defined by:

\varphi_{\mu}(x)=\sum_{J\in V(\mathcal{G})}\varphi_{\mu}(J)\Omega(p^{-N}|x-J|_{p})

(8)

and the vector
(\varphi_{\mu}(J))_{J\in V(\mathcal{G})}
is an eigenvector of the (negative semi-definite) Laplacian matrix
L
associated with adjacency matrix
A
, corresponding to
\mu\in\Spec(L)
, and
\Psi_{r,I,j}
are the Kozyrev functions of the form (
1
). Furthermore we have the following orthogonal decomposition

L^{2}(K_{N},\mathbb{C})=X_{N}\oplus\mathcal{L}_{0}^{2}(K_{N}).

(9)

Proof.

[
19
, Thm. 10.1]
.
∎

According to
[
19
, Thm. 4.2]
, the Cauchy problem of the heat equation (
7
), with
t\geq 0
has a solution which is unique. The solution is given by the semigroup
\{e^{t\mathbb{L}}\}
generated by
\mathbb{L}
. By the decomposition (
9
), we propose a solution of the form

u(x,t)=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L)}c_{\mu}(t)\varphi_{\mu}(x)+\sum_{\supp(\Psi_{j,I,r})\subset K_{N}}c_{r,I,j}(t)\psi_{j,I,r}(x),

(10)

where the infinite set
\{\Psi_{j,I,r}|\supp(\Psi_{j,I,r})\subset K_{N}\}
is described in Theorem
3.7
. Hence, the solution is an infinite sum, and the convergence takes place in
L^{2}(K_{N})
. After inserting this into (
7
) we obtain

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial c_{\mu}}{\partial t}=\mu c_{\mu}\\
\frac{\partial c_{r,I,j}}{\partial t}=-\gamma_{I}c_{j,I,r}\end{cases}\,.

where
\mu
and
\gamma_{I}
are the eigenvalues associated to
\varphi_{\mu}
and
\psi_{j,I,r}
, respectively.

This proves that

e^{t\mathbb{L}}u_{0}(x)=u(x,t)=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L)}C_{\mu}e^{\mu t}\varphi_{\mu}(x)+\sum_{\supp(\Psi_{j,I,r})\subset K_{N}}C_{j,I,r}e^{-\gamma_{I}t}\psi_{j,I,r}(x).

(11)

with
C_{I}
and
C_{j,I,r}
is uniquely determined by the initial conditions

u_{0}(x)=u(x,0)=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L)}C_{\mu}\varphi_{\mu}(x)+\sum_{\supp(\Psi_{j,I,r})\subset K_{N}}C_{j,I,r}\psi_{j,I,r}(x),

(12)

and solution (
11
) is an infinite convergent sum.

3.3
Approximating the non-autonomous case for graphs

We now want to use the Trotter product formula to approximate the operator
U(t,s)
, i.e., the solutions of (
5
). As approximating operators we choose the product

\prod_{k=1}^{n}e^{t/n\mathcal{L}(s+kt/n)}=e^{t/n\mathcal{L}(s+t)}\cdot e^{t/n\mathcal{L}(s+t-t/n)}\dots e^{t/n\mathcal{L}(s+t/n)}.

We have the following proposition:

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\{A(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
be a family of bounded and strongly continuous operators on a Banach space
X
, and let
U(t,s)
be its respective evolution family. Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{n}e^{t/nA(s+kt/n)}x=U(s+t,s)x

(13)

for all
x\in X
and uniformly for
s
and
t
in compact intervals of
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, respectively.

Proof.

[
6
, Ch. III.5.9, Prop.]
.
∎

Now we outline how to apply Proposition
3.8
to approximate the solution of (
5
). For
0<b
, and
\varepsilon>0
, we have

||e^{t/n\mathbb{L}(t)}\cdot e^{t/n\mathbb{L}(t-t/n)}\dots e^{t/n\mathbb{L}(t/n)}u_{0}(x)-U(t,0)u_{0}(x)||_{L^{2}}<\varepsilon

with
0\leq t<b
, and some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Take
0\leq t_{0}<b
fixed, hence, in order to compute the approximate (respect to the
L^{2}
norm) solution we have to solve (
7
) by iterating the initial conditions, or in other words we have to compute
e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0})}\cdot e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}-t_{0}/n)}\dots e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}/n)}u_{0}(x)
. We can view this approximation like snapshots of the system at
n\geq 0
discrete time points.
We call this the
temporal discretisation
, and we fix some notation:

1.

Let
\mu(t_{0})
be an eigenvalue of the matrix
L(t_{0})
and
\varphi_{\mu}(x,t_{0})
the associated eigenfunction.

2.

Let
\{e^{t\mathbb{L}(t_{0})}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the semigroup generated by the operator
\mathbb{L}(t_{0})
for some
t_{0}\geq 0
.

3.

Let
\gamma_{I}(t_{0})=\sum_{J}A_{IJ}(t_{0})

Therefore, for a fixed
t_{0}
, the solution of the Cauchy problem in the autonomous case (
7
) associated to the operator
\mathbb{L}(t_{0})
, with initial condition
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)
as in (
12
), is given by

\displaystyle e^{t\mathbb{L}(t_{0})}

\displaystyle u_{0}(x)=u(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu=\mu(t_{0})\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}C_{\mu}(t_{0})e^{\mu(t_{0})t}\varphi_{\mu}(x,t_{0})

\displaystyle+\sum_{r,I,j}C_{j,I,r}(t_{0})e^{-\gamma_{I}(t_{0})t}\psi_{j,I,r}(x).

Now it is clear that

\displaystyle e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}/n)}u_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu=\mu(t_{0})\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}C_{\mu}(t_{0}/n)e^{\mu(t_{0}/n)t_{0}/n}\varphi_{\mu}(x,t_{0}/n)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j,I,r}C_{r,I,j}(t_{0}/n)e^{-\gamma_{I}(t_{0}/n)t_{0}/n}\psi_{j,I,r}(x).

(14)

The next step is to compute
e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(2t_{0}/n)}e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}/n)}u_{0}(x)
. For this we need to find the solution to the Cauchy problem (
7
) associated to the operator
\mathbb{L}(2t_{0}/n)
with initial condition
u_{1}(x)=e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}/n)}u_{0}(x)
, and evaluate the solution
t\mapsto e^{t\mathbb{L}(2t_{0}/n)}e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}/n)}u_{0}(x)
in
t=t_{0}/n
.

Define for a given adjacency matrix
A(t)
the corresponding weighted graph
G_{A(t)}
whose adjacency matrix is
A(t)
. The corresponding
L^{2}
-space will be denoted as
L^{2}(G_{A(t)},\mathbb{C})
. It is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{\left\lvert V(\mathcal{G})\right\rvert}
.

To find the solution of the form (
10
) we need to express the initial condition
u_{1}(x)=e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}/n)}u_{0}(x)
as an element of
L^{2}(G_{A(2t_{0}/n)},\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathcal{L}_{0}^{2}(K_{N})
,
that is

e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}/n)}u_{0}(x)=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}B_{\mu}\varphi_{\mu}(x,2t_{0}/n)+\sum_{j,I,r}B_{j,I,r}\psi_{r,I,j}(x).

Then, after computing the coefficients
B_{\mu}
and
B_{j,I,r}
the desired solution will be given by

\begin{split}e^{t\mathbb{L}(2t_{0}/n)}&e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}/n)}u_{0}(x)=u_{1}(x,t)\\
&\hskip-14.22636pt=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}B_{\mu}e^{\mu(2t_{0}/n)t}\varphi_{\mu}(x,2t_{0}/n)+\sum_{j,I,r}B_{j,I,r}e^{-\gamma_{I}(2t_{0}/n)t}\psi_{j,I,r}(x).\end{split}

(15)

Comparing (
14
) and (
15
), it is clear that
B_{j,I,r}=C_{j,I,r}e^{-\gamma_{I}(t_{0}/n)t_{0}/n}
. This is due to the fact that in decomposition (
9

\mathcal{L}_{0}^{2}(K_{N})
remains constant in time. Now we need to compute the constants
B_{\mu}
. For this, note that

\omega:=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}B_{\mu}\varphi_{\mu}(x,2t_{0}/n)=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}C_{\mu}e^{\mu(t_{0}/n)t_{0}/n}\varphi_{\mu}(x,t_{0}/n)

Note that for each
s\geq 0
, the set

{\left\{\varphi_{\mu}(x,s)=\varphi_{\mu(s)}(x)\mid\mu(s)\in\Spec(L(s))\right\}}

is a basis for the finite dimensional space
X_{N}
. Hence, the coefficients
B_{\mu}
are given by the basis change matrix from the basis
\{\varphi_{\mu}(x,t_{0}/n)\}
to the basis
\{\varphi_{\mu}(x,2t_{0}/n)\}
. The basis change matrix
M=[M_{\lambda,\mu}]
is given by the following equalities:

\varphi_{\mu}(x,2t_{0}/n)=\sum_{\lambda\in V(\mathcal{G})}M_{\lambda,\mu}\varphi_{\lambda}(x,t_{0}/n).

In general, the basis change matrix from the basis
\{\varphi_{\mu}(x,s)\}
to the basis
\{\varphi_{\mu}(x,t)\}

M
will be given by

M=M(s)^{-1}M(t),

where the matrices
M(\tau)
are the
modal
matrices of the Laplacian matrix, that is, the
\mu
-column of
M(\tau)
is given by the eigenvector
[\varphi_{\mu}(K,\tau)]_{K\in V(\mathcal{G})}
. Then, it is clear that the coefficients
B_{\mu}
are given by

[B_{\mu}]_{\mu}=M^{-1}[C_{\mu}e^{\mu(t_{0}/n)t_{0}/n}]_{\mu}.

Thus,

[B_{\mu}]_{\mu}=M(2t_{0}/n)^{-1}M(t_{0}/n)\diag(e^{\mu(t_{0}/n)t_{0}/n})[C_{\mu}]_{\mu}

and

B_{j,I,r}=C_{j,I,r}e^{-\gamma_{I}(t_{0}/n)t_{0}/n}.

The process continues by solving the Cauchy problem for the operator
\mathbb{L}(3t_{0}/n)
with initial condition
e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(2t_{0}/n)}e^{t/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}/n)}u_{0}(x)
. In order to do so, we repeat the process described above. That is we take as initial condition

\displaystyle u_{1}(x,t_{0}/n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}B_{\mu}e^{\mu(2t_{0}/n)t_{0}/n}\varphi_{\mu}(x,2t_{0}/n)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j,I,r}C_{j,I,r}e^{-\gamma_{I}(t_{0}/n)t_{0}/n-\gamma_{I}(2t_{0}/n)t}\psi_{j,I,r}(x)

and express it as an element of
L^{2}(G_{A(3t_{0}/n)},\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathcal{L}_{0}^{2}(K_{N})
.
Now,

u_{1}(x,t_{0}/n)=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}D_{\mu}\varphi_{\mu}(x,3t_{0}/n)+\sum_{j,I,r}D_{j,I,r}\psi_{r,I,j}(x).

Again, we obtain the coefficients
D_{\mu}
and
D_{j,I,r}
by:

[D_{\mu}]_{\mu}=M(3t_{0}/n)^{-1}M(2t_{0}/n)\diag(e^{\mu(2t_{0}/n)t_{0}/n})[B_{\mu}]_{\mu}

and

D_{j,I,r}=B_{j,I,r}e^{-\gamma_{I}(2t_{0}/n)t_{0}/n}.

We can simplify these expressions by putting the value of
B_{\mu}
and
B_{j,I,r}
in the above equalities, and we get

[D_{\mu}]_{\mu}=M(3t_{0}/n)^{-1}M(t_{0}/n)\diag(e^{\mu(t_{0}/n)+\mu_{I}(2t_{0}/n)})[C_{\mu}]_{\mu}

and

D_{j,I,r}=C_{j,I,r}e^{-\gamma_{I}(t_{0}/n)t_{0}/n-\gamma_{I}(2t_{0}/n)t}.

In the end, we obtain the function
e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0})}\dots e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}/n)}u_{0}(x)\in L^{2}(K_{N})
, of the form

\begin{split}e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0})}&\cdot e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}-t_{0}/n)}\dots e^{t_{0}/n\mathbb{L}(t_{0}/n)}u_{0}(x)=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}Q_{\mu}(n,t_{0})\varphi_{\mu}(x,t_{0})\\
&+\sum_{\supp(\Psi_{j,I,r})\subset K_{N}}C_{r,I,j}(t_{0}/n)e^{-\frac{t_{0}}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{I}(k\frac{t_{0}}{n})}\Psi_{j,I,r}(x),\end{split}

where the coefficients
Q_{\mu}(n,t_{0})
are given by the equation

[Q_{\mu}(n,t_{0})]_{\mu}=M(t_{0})^{-1}M(t_{0}/n)\diag(e^{\frac{t_{0}}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu(k\frac{t_{0}}{n})})[C_{\mu}(t_{0}/n)]_{\mu}

For simplicity, we rename the approximation above:

\displaystyle U_{n}(t,0)u_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}Q_{\mu}(n,t)\varphi_{\mu}(x,t)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\supp(\Psi_{j,I,r})\subset K_{N}}C_{r,I,j}(t/n)e^{-\frac{t}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{I}(k\frac{t}{n})}\Psi_{j,I,r}(x)

Then, for a general initial condition
s
, we obtain:

\displaystyle U_{n}(t,s)u_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}Q_{\mu}(n,t,s)\varphi_{\mu}(x,t_{0})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\supp(\Psi_{j,I,r})\subset K_{N}}C_{r,I,j}(s+\frac{t-s}{n})e^{-\frac{t-s}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{I}(s+k\frac{t-s}{n})}\Psi_{j,I,r}(x),

where the coefficients
Q_{\mu}(n,t,s)
are computed by the following equation:

[Q_{\mu}(n,t,s)]=M(t)^{-1}M\left(s+\frac{t-s}{n}\right)\diag\left(e^{\frac{t-s}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu(s+k\frac{t-s}{n})}\right)[C_{\mu}(s+\frac{t-s}{n})]_{\mu}

where the numbers
C_{\mu}(s+\frac{t-s}{n})
are the Fourier coefficients of the initial condition at time
s+\frac{t-s}{n}
. Note that in
L^{2}(K_{N})
we have the following convergence:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}U_{n}(t,s)u_{0}(x)=U(t,s)u_{0}(x),

and we can expand the function
U(t,s)u_{0}(x)\in L^{2}(K_{N})
by its Fourier series using the decomposition
L^{2}(G_{A(t_{0})},\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathcal{L}_{0}^{2}(K_{N})
:

U(t,s)u_{0}(x)=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}Q_{\mu}(t,s)\varphi_{\mu}(x,t_{0})+\sum_{\supp(\Psi_{j,I,r})\subset K_{N}}Q_{r,I,j}\Psi_{j,I,r}(x).

Hence, by Parseval’s equality we obtain:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}Q_{\mu}(n,t,s)=Q_{\mu}(t,s)

and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}C_{r,I,j}\left(s+\frac{t-s}{n}\right)e^{-\frac{t-s}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{I}(s+k\frac{t-s}{n})}=C_{r,I,j}(s)e^{-\int_{s}^{t}\gamma_{I}(\tau)d\tau},

where the last equality occurs by the continuity of
t\mapsto\gamma_{I}(t)
. On the other hand, the first limit is more complicated, because it depends on the continuity of the matrix function
t\mapsto M(t)
, more specifically, on the continuity of the eigenvectors:
t\mapsto\varphi_{\mu}(t)
, where the vector
\varphi_{\mu}=[\varphi_{\mu}(K,t)]_{K}
is the eigenvector of the Laplacian matrix
L(t)
associated to
\mu(t)\in\Spec(L(t))
.

Let
t\mapsto B(t)
be a matrix function. If
B(t)
is real symmetric, then we can find an orthonormal basis
\{\varphi_{\mu}(t)\}_{\mu}
of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
formed by eigenvectors of
B(t)
. It can be shown that if the matrix
B(t)
depends analytically on
t
inside a real interval, then we can chose an orthonormal basis
\{\varphi_{\mu}(t)\}_{\mu}
that depends analytically on
t

[
9
, Ch. II.4]
,
and in particular, continuously on
t
. If the analyticity assumption is deleted, the eigenvectors may not depend continuously on
t
. Therefore, if we suppose that the adjacency matrix
A(t)
depends analytically on
t
, then we have the following:

\begin{split}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}[Q_{\mu}(n,t,s)]_{\mu}&=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}M(t)^{-1}M\left(s+\frac{t-s}{n}\right)\\
&\cdot\diag\left(e^{\frac{t-s}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu(s+k\frac{t-s}{n})}\right)\left[C_{I}\left(s+\frac{t-s}{n}\right)\right]_{I}\\
&=M(t)^{-1}M(s)\diag\left(e^{\int_{s}^{t}\mu(\tau)d\tau}\right)[C_{\mu}(s)]_{\mu}=[Q_{\mu}(t,s)]_{\mu}.\end{split}

But, in order to have the above limit, it is sufficient to suppose that the eigenvectors
\varphi_{\mu}(t)
are right continuous at the initial condition
s\geq t_{0}
.

This proves the following:

Theorem 3.9
.

If the eigenvectors of the Laplacian matrices
L(t)
are right continuous at some
s\geq 0
, then the evolution family of the Cauchy problem (
5
) (with initial condition at
s
) is given by

\displaystyle U(t,s)u_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}Q_{\mu}(t,s)\varphi_{\mu}(x,t)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\supp(\Psi_{j,I,r})\subset K_{N}}C_{r,I,j}(s)e^{-\int_{s}^{t}\gamma_{I}(\tau)d\tau}\Psi_{j,I,r}(x),

where
t\geq s
,

[Q_{\mu}(t,s)]=M(t)^{-1}M(s)\diag\left(e^{\int_{s}^{t}\mu(\tau)d\tau}\right)[C_{\mu}(s)]_{\mu}\,,

and the coefficients
C_{\mu}(s)
and
C_{r,I,j}(s)
are uniquely determined by the initial condition

u_{0}(x)=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L(t_{0}))}C_{\mu}(s)\varphi_{\mu}(x,s)+\sum_{\supp(\Psi_{j,I,r})\subset K_{N}}C_{r,I,j}(s)\Psi_{j,I,r}(x).

In particular, this result holds for all
s\geq 0
if the adjacency matrix
A(t)
depends analytically on
t
.

Remark 3.10
.

Note that this is consistent with the autonomous problem, that is, when
L(t)=L
is constant. In this case, the matrices
M(t)
and the functions
t\mapsto\mu(t)
,
t\mapsto\varphi_{\mu}(t)
are constant, thus the solution is given by

\displaystyle U(t,s)u_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(L)}C_{\mu}(s)e^{(t-s)\mu}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\supp(\Psi_{j,I,r})\subset K_{N}}C_{r,I,j}(s)e^{-(t-s)\gamma_{I}}\Psi_{j,I,r}(x)

for
t\geq s\geq 0
.

Corollary 3.11
.

Assuming analyticity of
L(t)
, the first-order approximation error in the temporal discretisation is
O\left(\frac{1}{n}e^{\frac{1}{n}}\right)
.

Proof.

For the temporal discretisation, observe that by replacing each analytic function with its first order approximation leads to
an error of
O(1/n)
in each entry of the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
L(t)
, and an error of
O(e^{1/n})

in each exponential integral, because the temporal discretisation is in this case a Riemann sum. Hence, the
error in each coefficient of the solution is given by the
product error, and this is in first order
O\left(\frac{1}{n}e^{\frac{1}{n}}\right)
, which proves the assertion.
∎

4
Inhomogeneous Markov processes and Feller evolution.

4.1
Reviewing inhomogeneous Markov processes

In this section we review some definitions and results necessary to prove that the non-autonomous abstract Cauchy problem (
5
) has attached to it an inhomogeneous Markov process.

In the sequel
E
denotes a separable complete metrizable topological Hausdorff space. In other words
E
is a Polish space. The space
C_{b}(E)
is the space of all complex valued bounded continuous functions. The space
C_{b}(E)
is equipped with the uniform norm:
||\cdot||_{\infty}
, and the strict topology
\mathcal{T}_{\beta}
.
It is considered as a subspace of the bounded Borel measurable functions
L^{\infty}(E)
, also endowed with the supremum norm.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be a measurable space. A filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
,
t\in[0,T]
, is a family of sub-
\sigma
-algebras of
\mathcal{F}
such that if
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq T
, then
\mathcal{F}_{t_{1}}\subset\mathcal{F}_{t_{2}}
. A two-parameter filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\tau}
,
0\leq\tau\leq t\leq T
, is a family of sub-
\sigma
-algebras of
\mathcal{F}
that is increasing in
t
and decreasing in
\tau
. Let
X_{s}
,
s\in[0,T]
be a stochastic process, with state space
(E,\mathcal{E})
. The
\sigma
-algebra
\sigma(X_{s}:0\leq s\leq t)
is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra such that all the state variables
X_{s}
with
0\leq s\leq t
are measurable.
The process
X_{s}
,
s\in[0,T]
, generates the following two-parameter filtration:

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\tau}=\sigma(X_{s}:\tau\leq s\leq t)

with
0\leq\tau\leq t\leq T
.

We recall some definitions:

Definition 4.1
.

A transition probability funcition
P(r,x;s,A)
, where
0\leq r<s\leq T
,
x\in E
, and
A\in\mathcal{E}
, is a nonnegative function for which the following condition hold:

•

For fixed
r,s
and
A
,
P
is a nonnegative Borel measurable function on
E
.

•

For fixed
r,s
and
x
,
P
is a Borel measure on
\mathcal{E}
.

•

P(r,x;s,E)=1
for all
r,s
and
x
.

•

P(r,x;s,A)=\int_{E}P(r,x;y,dy)P(u,y;s,A)
for all
r<u<s
, and
A
.

The number
P(r,x;s,A)
can be interpreted as the probability of the following event: The random system located at
x\in E
at time
r
hits the target
A\subset E
at time
s
.

Definition 4.2
.

The process

\{(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T}^{\tau},\mathbb{P}_{\tau,x}),(X(t):T\geq t\geq 0),(E,\mathcal{E})\}

is called a time-inhomogeneous Markov process if

\mathbb{E}_{\tau,x}\left[f(X(t))|\mathcal{F}_{s}^{\tau}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{s,X(s)}[f(X(t))]

\mathbb{P}_{\tau,x}
-almost surely. Here
f
is a bounded Borel measurable function defined on the state space
E
and
\tau\leq s\leq t\leq T
.

Let
P
be a transition probability function. It is possible to construct a filtered measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\tau})
, a family of probability measures
\mathbb{P}_{\tau,x}
,
x\in E
,
\tau\in[0,T]
, on the space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T}^{\tau})
, and a Markov process
X_{t}
,
t\in[0,T]
such that

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\tau}=\sigma(X_{s}:\tau\leq s\leq t)

with
0\leq\tau\leq t\leq T
and

\mathbb{P}_{\tau,x}(X_{t}\in A)=P(\tau,x;t,A)

with
0\leq\tau\leq t\leq T,

A\in\mathcal{E}
.

Definition 4.3
.

A family
\{P(s,t):0\leq s\leq t\leq T\}
of operators defined on
L^{\infty}(E)
is called a
Feller Evolution
or
Feller propagator
on
C_{b}(E)
if it possesses the following properties:

1.

It leaves
C_{b}(E)
invariant:
P(s,t)C_{b}(E)\subset C_{b}(E)
for
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
;

2.

It is an evolution:
P(\tau,t)=P(\tau,s)P(s,\tau)
for all
\tau,s,t
for which
0\leq\tau\leq s\leq t
and
P(t,t)=I
,
t\in[0,T]
;

3.

If
0\leq f\leq 1
,
f\in C_{b}(E)
, then
0\leq P(s,t)f\leq 1
, for
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
.

4.

If the function
(s,t,x)\mapsto P(s,t)f(x)
is continuous on the space
\Lambda:=\{(s,t,x)\in[0,T]\times[0,T]\times E:s\leq t\}
.

In the following theorem we see that with a Feller evolution a strong Markov process can be associated in such a way that the one-dimensional distributions or marginals are determined by the operators
f\mapsto P(\tau,t)f
,
f\in C_{b}(E)
. In fact every operator
P(\tau,t)
can be written as

P(\tau,t)f(x)=\int P(\tau,x;t,dy)f(y)

for
f\in C_{b}(E)
. Where the mapping

(\tau,x,t,B)\mapsto P(\tau,x;t,B)

with
(\tau,x,t,B)\in[0,T]\times E\times[0,T]\times\mathcal{E}
,
\tau\leq t
, is a sub-probability transition function.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\{P(\tau,t):\tau\leq t\leq T\}
be a Feller evolution in
C_{b}(E)
. Then there exists a strong Markov process (in fact a Hunt Process) such that
[P(\tau,t)f](X)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau,x}[f(X(t))]
,
f\in C_{b}(E)
,
t\geq 0
. Moreover, this Markov process possesses the properties in
[
17
, Thm. 2.9.]
.

Proof.

[
17
, Thm. 2.9]
.
∎

4.2
Stochastic process attached to a non-autonomous equation

In this section we prove that the system

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x,t)=\mathbb{L}(t)u(x,t)\\
u(x,s)=u_{s}(x)\in C(K_{n},\mathbb{C})\end{cases}

(16)

generates a time-inhomogeneous Markov process, where the operators
\mathbb{L}(t)
satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem
2.11
. In more generality, we study the following Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x,t)=A(t)u(x,t)\\
u(x,s)=u_{s}(x)\in C(E)\,,\end{cases}

(17)

where
E
is a compact Polish space. Thus,
C_{b}(E)=C(E)
. Also, we suppose that
t\mapsto A(t)
is continuous in the uniform norm topology (In particular
A(t)
is bounded for all
t\geq 0
). Moreover, we suppose that for every
\tau\geq 0
,
A(\tau)
generates a strongly continuous, positive, contraction semigroup
\{e^{tA(\tau)}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
C(E)
. By Theorem
3.3
,
the system (
17
)
is well-posed for every initial condition
u_{s}(x)\in C(E)
. Therefore, there exists an evolution family
U(t,s)
, such that
U(t,s)u_{s}(x)
is the unique solution of the Cauchy problem (
17
).
Moreover,
U(t,s)
satisfies the properties in Remark
3.5
.

Lemma 4.5
.

The evolution
U(t,s)
described above is a Feller evolution.

Proof.

We verify the properties of Definition
4.3
.
The verification of
1
. and
2
. is straightforward.
We now prove condition
3
. Let
f\in C(E)
such that
0\leq f\leq 1
. By Proposition
3.8
,
we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{n}e^{\frac{(t-s)}{n}A(s+k\frac{(t-s)}{n})}f(x)=U(t,s)f(x)

in the supremum norm, and with
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
. Therefore, we also have point-wise convergence. Since every semigroup
\{e^{tA(\tau)}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a strongly continuous, positive, contraction semigroup we have

0\leq\prod_{k=1}^{n}e^{\frac{(t-s)}{n}A(s+k\frac{(t-s)}{n})}f(x)\leq 1.

We obtain the desire result by taking
n\rightarrow\infty
. For condition
4
., note that, since the space
E
is Polish, we can prove the continuity in terms of sequences. Let
(t,s,x)\in\Lambda
, with
s\leq t
and
x\in E
. And let
(t_{n},s_{n},x_{n})
a convergent sequence, with
s_{n}\leq t_{n}
and
x_{n}\in E
, such that
(t_{n},s_{n},x_{n})\rightarrow(t,s,x)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. We have

\begin{split}|U(t_{n},s_{n})f(x_{n})-U(t,s)f(x)|&\leq|U(t_{n},s_{n})f(x_{n})-U(t,s)f(x_{n})|\\
&+|U(t,s)f(x_{n})-U(t,s)f(x)|\\
\leq&||U(t_{n},s_{n})-U(t,s)||_{\infty}||f||_{\infty}\\
&+|U(t,s)f(x_{n})-U(t,s)f(x)|\end{split}

Since
(t,s)\mapsto U(t,s)
is continuous in the uniform norm topology (Remark

3.5
.
2
.),
and the function
x\mapsto U(t,s)f(x)
is continuous in
E
, by taking
n\rightarrow\infty
the right hand side of the above inequality tends to zero. This now proves that
U(t,s)
is a Feller evolution. ∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\{A(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be a set of bounded operators on
C(E)
. Suppose that
t\mapsto A(t)
is continuous in the uniform norm topology and that each
A(t)
generates a strongly continuous, positive, contraction semi-group. Then the Cauchy problem (
17
)
is well-posed and its evolution family
U(t,s)
generates a strong Markov process with the properties listed in Theorem
4.4
.

Proof.
This is a consequence of Lemma
4.5

and Theorem
4.4
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

The literature does not seem to contain a result proving that a non-autonomous Cauchy problem has attached to it a Markov process. That is why the above result was obtained using the techniques learned above in order to find an aproximate solution (Proposition
3.8
).
In particular, it is important to note that evolution families are the two-parameter generalizations of semigroups, and analogously, Feller evolution families are the two parameter generalizations of Feller semigroups.

Theorem 4.8
.

There exists a probabilty transition function
P(t,x;s,\cdot)
, where
(t,x,s)\in[0,T]\times K_{N}\times[0,T]
, and
s\leq t
, on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
K_{N}
, such that the Cauchy problem (
16
)
has a unique solution of the form

u(x,t)=\int_{K_{N}}u_{s}(x)P(t,x;s,dy).

In addition,
P(t,x;s,\cdot)
is the transition function of a Markov process with the properties listed in Theorem
4.4
.

Proof.
We have to proove that
t\mapsto\mathbb{L}(t)
satisfies the hypothesis in Theorem
4.6
.
By
[
19
, Lem. 4.1]
, we know that
\mathbb{L}(t)
generates a strongly continuous, positive, contraction semigroup on
C(K_{N})
for each
t\geq 0
. hence, we only have to show that
t\mapsto\mathbb{L}(t)
is continuous in the uniform norm topology. Recall from the proof of Theorem

2.11
that

||\mathbb{L}(t)u-\mathbb{L}(s)u||_{\infty}\leq||\mathcal{A}(t)u-\mathcal{A}(s)u||_{\infty}+||\Gamma(t)u-\Gamma(s)u||_{\infty}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, and
\delta>0
such that

|t-s|<\delta\implies|A(x,y,t)-A(x,y,s)|<\varepsilon\ and\ |\gamma(t)(x)-\gamma(s)(x)|<\varepsilon

We have that

\begin{split}||\mathcal{A}(t)u-\mathcal{A}(s)u||_{\infty}&=\sup_{x\in K_{N}}\left|\int_{K_{N}}(A(x,y,t)-A(x,y,s))u(y)dy\right|\\
&\leq\varepsilon||u||_{\infty}\Vol(K_{N})\end{split}

(18)

and

||\Gamma(t)u-\Gamma(s)u||_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in K_{N}}|(\gamma(t)(x)-\gamma(s)(x))||u(x)|\leq\varepsilon||u||_{\infty}\,.

If
||u||_{\infty}\leq 1
, then

||\mathbb{L}(t)u-\mathbb{L}(s)u||_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon(\Vol(K_{N})+1).

This inequality implies the continuity in the uniform norm topology. We now apply Theorem
4.4
and Theorem
4.6
to obtain the result. ∎
