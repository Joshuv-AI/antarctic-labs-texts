When does the mean network capture the topology of a sample of networks?
François G. Meyer

Abstract
The notion of Fréchet mean (also known as “barycenter”) network is the workhorse of most machine learning algorithms that require the estimation of a “location” parameter to analyse network-valued data. In this context, it is critical that the network barycenter inherits the topological structure of the networks in the training dataset. The metric – which measures the proximity between networks – controls the structural properties of the barycenter. This work is significant because it provides for the first time analytical estimates of the sample Fréchet mean for the stochastic blockmodel, which is at the cutting edge of rigorous probabilistic analysis of random networks. We show that the mean network computed with the Hamming distance is unable to capture the topology of the networks in the training sample, whereas the mean network computed using the effective resistance distance recovers the correct partitions and associated edge density. From a practical standpoint, our work informs the choice of metrics in the context where the sample Fréchet mean network is used to characterize the topology of networks for network-valued machine learning. Keywords: Fréchet mean; statistical network analysis



1
Introduction

There has been recently a flurry of activity around the design of machine learning
algorithms that can analyze “network-valued random variables”
[e.g.,
Dubey and Müller, 2020
;
Ghoshdastidar et al., 2020
;
Ginestet et al., 2017
;
Kolaczyk et al., 2020
;
Lunagómez et al., 2021
;
Petersen and Müller, 2019
;
Xu, 2020
;
Zambon et al., 2019
, and
references
therein]
. A
prominent question that is central to many such algorithms is the estimation of the mean
of a set of networks. To characterize the mean network we borrow the notion of barycenter
from physics, and define the Fréchet mean as the network that minimizes the sum of the squared
distances to all the networks in the ensemble. This notion of centrality is well adapted
to metric spaces
[
Chowdhury and Mémoli, 2018
;
Jain, 2016
;
Kolaczyk et al., 2020
, e.g.,]
, and the Fréchet mean network has
become a standard tool for the statistical analysis of network-valued data.

In practice, given a training set of networks, it is important that the topology of the
sample Fréchet mean captures the mean topology of the training set. To provide a theoretical
answer to this question, we estimate the mean network when the networks are sampled from a
stochastic block model. The stochastic block models
[
Snijders, 2011
;
Abbe, 2018
]
have great
practical importance since they provide tractable models that capture the topology of real
networks that exhibit community structure. In addition, the theoretical properties (e.g.,
degree distribution, eigenvalues distributions, etc.) of this ensemble are well
understood. Finally, stochastic block models provide universal approximants to networks
and can be used as building blocks to analyse more complex networks

[
Airoldi et al., 2013
;
Ferguson and Meyer, 2023
;
Olhede and Wolfe, 2014
]
.

In this work, we derive the expression of the sample Fréchet mean of a stochastic block model
for two very different distances: the Hamming distance
[
Mitzenmacher and Upfal, 2017
]
and the
effective resistance perturbation distance
[
Monnig and Meyer, 2018
]
. The Hamming distance, which
counts the number of edges that need to be added or subtracted to align two networks
defined on the same vertex set, is very sensitive to fine scale fluctuations of the
network connectivity. To detect larger scale changes in connectivity, we use the
resistance perturbation distance
[
Monnig and Meyer, 2018
]
. This network distance can be tuned to
quantify configurational changes that occur on a network at different scales: from the
local scale formed by the neighbors of each vertex, to the largest scale that quantifies
the connections between clusters, or communities
[
Monnig and Meyer, 2018
]
. See
[
Akoglu et al., 2015
;
Donnat and Holmes, 2018
;
Wills and Meyer, 2020
, and
references therein]
for recent surveys on network
distances.

Our analysis shows that the sample Fréchet mean network computed with the Hamming distance is
unable to capture the topology of networks in the sample. In the case of a sparse
stochastic block model, the Fréchet mean network is always the empty network. Conversely, the
Fréchet mean computed using the effective resistance distance recovers the underlying network
topology associated with the generating process: the Fréchet mean discovers the correct
partitions and associated edge densities.

1.1
Relation to existing work

To the best of our knowledge, we are not aware of any theoretical derivation of the sample
Fréchet mean for any of the classic ensemble of random networks. Nevertheless, our work share
some strong connections with related research questions.

The Fréchet mean network as a location parameter.
Several authors have proposed
simple models of probability measures defined on spaces of networks, which are
parameterized by a location and a scale parameter
[
Banks and Carley, 1994
;
Lunagómez et al., 2021
]
. These
probability measures can be used to assign a likelihood to an observed network by
measuring the distance of that network to a central network, which characterizes the
location of the distribution. The authors in
[
Lunagómez et al., 2021
]
explore two choices for
the distance: the Hamming distance, and a diffusion distance. Our choice of distances is
similar to that of
[
Lunagómez et al., 2021
]
.

Existing metrics for the Fréchet mean network.
The concept of Fréchet mean
necessitates a choice of metric (or distance) on the probability space of networks. The
metric will influence the characteristics that the mean will inherit from the network
ensemble. For instance, if the distance is primarily sensitive to large scale features
(e.g., community structure or the existence of highly connected “hubs”), then the mean
will capture these large scale features, but may not faithfully reproduce the fine scale
connectivity (e.g., the degree of a vertex, or the presence of triangles).

One sometimes needs to compare networks of different sizes; the edit distance, which
allows for creation and removal of vertices, provides an elegant solution to this
problem. When the networks are defined on the same vertex set, the edit distance becomes
the Hamming distance
[
Han et al., 2016
]
, which can also be interpreted as the entrywise

\ell_{1}
norm between the two adjacency matrices. Replacing the
\ell_{1}
norm with the

\ell_{2}
norm yields the Frobenius norm, which has also been used to compare networks
(modulo an unknown permutation of the vertices – or equivalently by comparing the
respective classes in the quotient set induced by the action of the group of permutations

[
Jain, 2016
;
Kolaczyk et al., 2020
]
). We note that the computation of the sample Fréchet mean network
using the Hamming distance is NP-hard
[
Chen et al., 2019
, e.g.,]
. For this reason, several
alternatives have been proposed
[
Ginestet et al., 2017
, e.g.,]
.

Both the Hamming distance and Frobenius norm are very sensitive to the fine scale edge
connectivity. To probe a larger range of scales, one can compute the mean network using
the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the respective network adjacency matrices

[
Ferguson and Meyer, 2023
;
Ferrer et al., 2005
;
White and Wilson, 2007
]
.

1.2
Content of the paper: our main contributions

Our contributions consists of two results.

The network distance is the Hamming distance.
We prove that when the
probability space is equipped with the Hamming distance, then the sample Fréchet mean network
converges in probability to the sample median network (computed using the majority rule), in
the limit of large sample size. This result has significant practical
consequences. Consider the case where one needs to estimate a “central network” that
captures the connectivity structure of a training set of sparse networks. Our work implies
that if one uses the Hamming distance, then the sample Fréchet mean will be the empty network.

The network distance is the resistance perturbation distance.
We
prove that when the probability space is equipped with the resistance perturbation
distance, then the adjacency matrix of the sample Fréchet mean converges to the sample mean
adjacency matrix with high probability, in the limit of large network size. Our
theoretical analysis is based on the stochastic block model
[
Abbe, 2018
]
, a model of
random networks that exhibit community structure. In practical applications, our work
suggests that one should use the effective resistance distance to learn the mean topology
of a sample of networks.

2
Network ensemble and distances

2.1
The network ensemble

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the set of all simple labeled networks with vertex set
[n]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\big\{1,\ldots,n\big\}
, and let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of
n\times n
adjacency matrices of networks in

\mathcal{G}
,

\mathcal{S}=\left\{\bm{A}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n};\text{where}\;a_{ij}=a_{ji},\text{and}\;a_{i,i}=0;\;1\leq i<j\leq n\right\}.

(1)

Because there is a unique correspondence between a network
G=(V,E)
and its adjacency
matrix
\bm{A}
, we sometimes (by an abuse of the language) refer to an adjacency matrix

\bm{A}
as a network. Also, without loss of generality we assume throughout the paper that
the network size
n
is even.

We introduce the matrix
\bm{P}
that encodes the edges density within each community and
across communities.
\bm{P}
can be written as the Kronecker product of the following two matrices,

\bm{P}=\begin{bmatrix}p&q\\
q&p\end{bmatrix}\otimes\bm{J}_{n/2}

(2)

where
\bm{J}_{n/2}
is the
n/2\times n/2
matrix with all entries equal to
1
. We denote
by
\mathcal{G}\mspace{-1mu}\big(\mspace{-2mu}n,p,q\mspace{-1mu}\big)
, the probability space
\mathcal{S}
equipped with the probability measure,

\forall\bm{A}\in\mathcal{S},\mspace{32mu}\mspace{2mu}\mathbb{P}\mspace{-2mu}\left(\mspace{-2mu}\bm{A}\mspace{-2mu}\right)=\prod_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle 1\leq i\leq n/2}}{{\scriptstyle 1\leq j\leq n/2}}}\mspace{-16mu}p^{a_{ij}}[1-p]^{1-a_{ij}}\mspace{-16mu}\prod_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle\mspace{24mu}1\leq i\leq n/2}}{{\scriptstyle\mspace{-48mu}n/2+1\leq j\leq n}}}\mspace{-16mu}q^{a_{ij}}[1-q]^{1-a_{ij}}.

(3)

\mathcal{G}\mspace{-1mu}\big(\mspace{-2mu}n,p,q\mspace{-1mu}\big)
is referred to as a two-community stochastic blockmodel
[
Abbe, 2018
]
. One can
interpret the stochastic blockmodel as follows: the nodes of a network
G\in\mathcal{G}
are partitioned into two communities. The first
n/2
nodes constitute community

C_{1}
; the second community,
C_{2}
, comprises the remaining
n/2
nodes. Edges in the
graph are drawn from independent Bernoulli random variables with the following probability
of success:
p
for edges within each community, and
q
for the across-community edges.

2.2
The Hamming distance between networks

Let
\bm{A}
and
\bm{A}^{\prime}
be the adjacency matrices of two unweighted networks defined on the
same vertex set. The Hamming distance
[
Mitzenmacher and Upfal, 2017
]
is defined as follows.

Definition 1
.

The Hamming distance between
\bm{A}
and
\bm{A}^{\prime}
is defined as

d_{H}(\bm{A},\bm{A}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\big\|\bm{A}-\bm{A}^{\prime}\big\|_{1},

(4)

where the elementwise
\ell_{1}
norm of a matrix
\bm{A}
is given by

\big\|\bm{A}\big\|_{1}=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}|a_{ij}|
.

Because the distance
d_{H}
is not concerned about the locations of the edges that are
different between the two graphs,
d_{H}(\bm{A},\bm{A}^{\prime})
is oblivious to topological differences
between
\bm{A}
and
\bm{A}^{\prime}
. For instance, if
\bm{A}
and
\bm{A}^{\prime}
are sampled from
\mathcal{G}\mspace{-1mu}\big(\mspace{-2mu}n,p,q\mspace{-1mu}\big)
, then
the complete removal of the across-community edges induces the same distance as the
removal, or addition, of that same number of edges in either community. In other words, a
catastrophic change in the network topology cannot be distinguished from benign
fluctuations in the local connectivity within either community. To address the limitation
of the Hamming distance we introduce the resistance distance
[
Monnig and Meyer, 2018
]
.

2.3
The resistance (perturbation) distance between networks

For the sake of completeness, we review the concept of effective resistance

[
Doyle and Snell, 1984
;
Klein and Randić, 1993
, e.g.,]
. Given a weighted network
G=(V,E)
, we transform
G

into a resistor network by replacing each edge
e
, with weight
w_{e}
, by a resistor with
resistance
1/w_{e}
. The
effective resistance

R_{ij}
between two vertices
i
and

j
is defined as the voltage applied between
i
and
j
that is required to maintain a
unit current through the terminals formed by
i
and
j
. The effective resistance

R_{ij}
provides a measure of vertex affinity in terms of connectivity.

The resistance-perturbation distance (or resistance distance for short) is based on
comparing the effective resistances matrices
\bm{R}
and
\bm{R}^{\prime}
of
G
and
G^{\prime}

respectively. To simplify the discussion, we only consider networks that are connected with
high probability. All the results can be extended to disconnected networks as explained in

[
Monnig and Meyer, 2018
]
.

Definition 2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
be two networks defined on the same vertex set

[n]
. Let
R
and
R^{\prime}
denote the effective resistances of
G
and
G^{\prime}

respectively. We define the resistance-perturbation distance
[
Monnig and Meyer, 2018
]
to be

\drpt\left(G,G^{\prime}\right)=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left\lvert{R_{ij}}-{R^{\prime}_{ij}}\right\rvert^{2}.

(5)

3
Main Results

We first review the concept of sample Fréchet mean, and then present the main results.
We consider the probability space
(\mathcal{S},\pr)
formed by the adjacency matrices of networks
sampled from
\mathcal{G}\mspace{-1mu}\big(\mspace{-2mu}n,p,q\mspace{-1mu}\big)
. We equip
\mathcal{S}
with a distance
d
, which is either the Hamming
distance or the resistance distance. Let
\bm{A}^{(k)},1\leq k\leq N
be adjacency matrices
sampled independently from
\mathcal{G}\mspace{-1mu}\big(\mspace{-2mu}n,p,q\mspace{-1mu}\big)

3.1
The sample Fréchet mean

The sample Fréchet function evaluated at
\bm{B}\in\mathcal{S}
is defined by

\widehat{F}_{2}\mspace{-2mu}\left(\bm{B}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}d^{2}(\bm{B},\bm{A}^{(k)}).

(6)

The minimization of the Fréchet function
\widehat{F}_{2}\mspace{-2mu}\left(\bm{B}\right)
gives rise to the concept of sample Fréchet mean
[
Fréchet, 1947
]
, or network barycenter
[
Sturm, 2003
]
.

Definition 3
.

The sample Fréchet mean network is the set of adjacency matrices
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]
solutions to

\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]=\underset{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{S}}{\theargmin}\mspace{4mu}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}d^{2}(\bm{B},\bm{A}^{(k)}).

(7)

Solutions to the minimization problem (
7
) always exist, but need
not be unique. In theorem
1
and theorem
2
, we prove that the
sample Fréchet mean network of
\mathcal{G}\mspace{-1mu}\big(\mspace{-2mu}n,p,q\mspace{-1mu}\big)
is unique, when
d
is either the Hamming distance or
the resistance distance.

A word on notations is in order here. It is customary to denote by
\bm{\mu}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]
the
population
Fréchet mean network of the probability distribution
\pr
,
[
Sturm, 2003
, see
e.g.,]
, since the adjacency matrix
\bm{\mu}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]
characterizes the location of
the probability distribution
\pr
. Because we use hats to denote sample (empirical)
estimates, we denote by
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]
the adjacency matrix of the
sample
Fréchet mean
network.

3.2
The sample Fréchet mean of
\mathcal{G}\mspace{-1mu}\big(\mspace{-2mu}n,p,q\mspace{-1mu}\big)
computed with the Hamming distance

The following theorem shows that the sample Fréchet mean network converges in probability to the
sample Fréchet median network, computed using the majority rule, in the limit of large sample
size,
N
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]
be the sample Fréchet mean network computed using the Hamming distance. Then,

\forall\varepsilon>0,\mspace{16mu}\exists N_{0},\forall N\geq N_{0},\mspace{16mu}\mspace{2mu}\mathbb{P}\mspace{-2mu}\left(\mspace{-2mu}d_{H}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big],\widehat{\bm{m}}\left[\pr\right])<\varepsilon\mspace{-2mu}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

(8)

where
\widehat{\bm{m}}\left[\pr\right]
is the adjacency matrix computed using the majority rule,

\forall i,j\in[n],\quad\widehat{\bm{m}}\left[\pr\right]_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\;\sum_{k=1}^{N}a^{(k)}_{ij}\geq N/2,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(9)

Remark 1
.

The matrix
\widehat{\bm{m}}\left[\pr\right]
is the sample Fréchet median network (e.g.,
[
Jiang et al., 2001
]
, solution
to the following minimization problem
[
Banks and Carley, 1994
]

\widehat{\bm{m}}\left[\pr\right]=\underset{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{S}}{\theargmin}\mspace{4mu}\widehat{F}_{1}\mspace{-2mu}\left(\bm{B}\right),

(10)

where
\widehat{F}_{1}
is the Fréchet function associated to the sample Fréchet median, defined by

\widehat{F}_{1}\mspace{-2mu}\left(\bm{B}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_{H}(\bm{A}^{(k)},\bm{B}).

(11)

The function

3.3
The sample Fréchet mean of
\mathcal{G}\mspace{-1mu}\big(\mspace{-2mu}n,p,q\mspace{-1mu}\big)
computed with the resistance distance

Here we equip the probability space
\big(\mathcal{S},\pr\big)
with the resistance metric
defined by (
5
). Let
\bm{A}^{(k)},1\leq k\leq N
be adjacency matrices
sampled independently from
\mathcal{G}\mspace{-1mu}\big(\mspace{-2mu}n,p,q\mspace{-1mu}\big)
, and let
\bm{R}^{(k)}
be their effective
resistances. Because the resistance metric relies on the comparison of connectivity at
multiple scales, we expect that the sample Fréchet mean network recovers the topology induced
by the communities.

The next theorem proves that the sample Fréchet mean converges toward the expected adjacency
matrix
\bm{P}
(see
2
) in the limit of large networks.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]
be the sample Fréchet mean computed using the effective resistance
distance. Then

\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{A}\right]=\bm{P},

(31)

in the limit of large network size, with high probability.

Proof of theorem
2
.

The proof combines three elements. We first observe that the effective resistance of the
sample Fréchet mean is the sample mean effective resistance.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]
be the sample Fréchet mean computed using the resistance distance. Then

\widehat{R}_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}R_{ij}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big])=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}R^{(k)}_{ij}

(32)

Proof of lemma
5
.

The proof relies on the observation that the Fréchet function in (
7
),
is a quadratic function of
\widehat{R}_{ij}=R_{ij}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big])
. Indeed, we have

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left\lvert\widehat{R}_{ij}-R^{(k)}_{ij}\right\rvert^{2}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\lvert\widehat{R}_{ij}-R^{(k)}_{ij}\right\rvert^{2}

(33)

where we have used the definition of the effective resistance distance given by
(
5
). The minimum of (
33
) is given by (
32
).
∎∎

The previous lemma allows us to recover
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]
from its effective resistance, given
by (
32
). Instead of pursuing this strategy, we prove in the next lemma
that
1/N\sum_{k=1}^{N}R^{(k)}_{ij}
is concentrated around
\mathbb{E}\left[R\right]_{ij}
in the limit of
large network size,
n
. In addition, we derive the expression of
\mathbb{E}\left[R\right]_{ij}
in the case
of a two community stochastic block model.

Lemma 6
.

The effective resistance of the sample Fréchet mean network
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]
is given by

R_{ij}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big])=\mathbb{E}\left[R\right]_{ij}+{\scriptstyle\mathscr{O}}_{P}\textstyle\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\right),

(34)

where
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{R}\right]
is the population mean effective resistance of the probability
space
\big(\mathcal{S},\pr\big)
, and is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{R}\right]=\frac{4}{n(p+q)}\bm{J}+\frac{(p-q)}{(p+q)}\frac{4}{n^{2}q}\bm{K},

(35)

where
\bm{J}=\bm{J}_{n}
, and
\bm{K}
is the
n\times n
matrix associated with the cross-community edges,

\bm{K}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\otimes\bm{J}_{n/2}.

(36)

Remark 2
.

We can explain expression (
35
) with a simple circuit argument. We
first analyse the case where
i
and
j
belong to the same community, say
C_{1}
. In this
case, we can neglect the other community
C_{2}
because of the bottleneck created by the
across-community edges. Consequently,
C_{1}
is approximately an Erdős-Rényi network wherein the
effective resistance
{R_{ij}}
concentrates around
4/(n(p+q))

[
Von Luxburg et al., 2014
]
, and we
obtain the first term in (
35
).

On the other hand, when the vertices
i
and
j
are in distinct communities, then a
simple circuit argument shows that

{R_{ij}}\approx\frac{2}{n(p+q)}+\frac{1}{k}+\frac{2}{n(p+q)},

(37)

where
k
is the number of across-community edges, creating a bottleneck with effective
resistance
1/k
between the two communities
[
Levin et al., 2009
]
; each term

2/(n(p+q))
accounts for the effective resistance from node
i
(respectively
j
) to a
node incident to an across-community edge. Because the number of across-community edges,

k
, is a binomial random variable, it concentrates around its mean,
qn^{2}/4
. Finally,

1/k
is a binomial reciprocal whose mean is given by
4/(qn^{2})+{\scriptstyle\mathscr{O}}\textstyle\left(\displaystyle 1/n^{3}\right)

[
Rempała, 2004
]
, and we recover the second term of (
35
).

Remark 3
.

The result in lemma
6
can be extended to a stochastic
block model of any geometry for which we can derive the analytic expression of the
dominant eigenvalues; see
[
Löwe and Terveer, 2024
;
Avrachenkov et al., 2015
, see e.g.,]
for equal size
communities, and
[
Chakrabarty et al., 2020
, see e.g.,]
for the more general case of
inhomogeneous random networks.

Before presenting the proof of lemma
6
, we introduce
the normalized Laplacian matrix
[
Bapat, 2010
, e.g.]
. Let
\bm{A}
be the adjacency matrix
of a network
(V,E)
, and let
\bm{D}
be the diagonal matrix of degrees,
d_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}
. We normalize
\bm{A}
in a symmetric manner, and we define

{\widehat{\bm{A}}}=\bm{D}^{-1/2}\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1/2},

(38)

where
\bm{D}^{-1/2}
is the diagonal matrix with entries
1/\sqrt{d_{i}}
. The normalized
Laplacian matrix is defined by

\mathcal{L}=\bm{I}-{\widehat{\bm{A}}},

(39)

where
\bm{I}
is the identity matrix.
\mathcal{L}
is positive semi-definite
[
Bapat, 2010
]
, and we
will consider its Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse,
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}
.

Proof of lemma
6
.

The lemma relies on the characterization of
\bm{R}
in terms of
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}

[
Bapat, 2010
]
,

{R_{ij}}=\langle\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j},\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j})\rangle,

(40)

where
\bm{u}_{i}=(1/\sqrt{d_{i}})\mspace{8mu}\bm{e}_{i}
, and
\bm{e}_{i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
vector of
the canonical basis.

Let
1=\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\lambda_{n}\geq-1
be the eigenvalues of
{\widehat{\bm{A}}}
,
and let
\bm{\Pi}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\Pi}_{n}
be the corresponding orthogonal projectors,

{\widehat{\bm{A}}}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{m}\bm{\Pi}_{m},

(41)

where
\bm{\Pi}_{1}=\tau^{-1}{\bm{d}^{1/2}}{\bm{d}^{1/2}}^{T}
, with
{\bm{d}^{1/2}}=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{d_{1}}&\cdots&\sqrt{d_{n}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
, and
\tau=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}
. Because
\bm{\Pi}_{1}
is also the
orthogonal projection on the null space of
\mathcal{L}
, we have

\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}=(\mathcal{L}+\bm{\Pi}_{1})^{-1}\mspace{-2mu}-\bm{\Pi}_{1}=\left(\bm{I}-({\widehat{\bm{A}}}-\bm{\Pi}_{1})\right)^{-1}\mspace{-8mu}-\bm{\Pi}_{1}=\bm{I}-\bm{\Pi}_{1}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{1-\lambda_{2}}\bm{\Pi}_{2}+\bm{Q},

(42)

where

\bm{Q}=\sum_{m=3}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{m}}{1-\lambda_{m}}\bm{\Pi}_{m}.

(43)

Substituting (
42
) into (
40
), we get

R_{ij}=\langle\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j},(\bm{I}-\bm{\Pi}_{1})(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j})\rangle+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{1-\lambda_{2}}\langle\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j},\bm{\Pi}_{2}(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j})\rangle+\langle\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j},\bm{Q}(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j})\rangle

(44)

The first (and dominant) term of (
44
) is

\langle\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j},\left(\bm{I}-\bm{\Pi}_{1}\right)(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j})\rangle=\langle\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j},\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{d_{i}}+\frac{1}{d_{j}}.

(45)

Let us examine the second term of (
44
).
Löwe and Terveer [2024]
provide the
following estimate for
\lambda_{2}
,

\lambda_{2}=\frac{p-q}{p+q}+\omega(n),\mspace{24mu}\text{where}\mspace{24mu}\omega(n)={\displaystyle\mathcal{O}}\mspace{-4mu}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\log{n}}{n(p+q)}}\right).

(46)

The corresponding eigenvector
\bm{z}
is given, with probability
(1-{\scriptstyle\mathscr{O}}\textstyle\left(\displaystyle 1\right))
, by
[
Deng et al., 2021
]
,

z_{i}=\sigma_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+{\scriptstyle\mathscr{O}}\textstyle\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(47)

where the “sign” vector
\bm{\sigma}
, which encodes the community, is given by

\sigma_{i}=\begin{cases}\mspace{12mu}1&\text{if}\mspace{56mu}1\leq i\leq n/2,\\
-1&\text{if}\mspace{8mu}n/2+1\leq i\leq n.\\
\end{cases}

(48)

We derive from (
47
) the following
approximation to
\langle\bm{u}_{i},\bm{\Pi}_{2}\bm{u}_{j}\rangle
,

\langle\bm{u}_{i},\bm{\Pi}_{2}\bm{u}_{j}\rangle=\bm{u}_{i}^{T}\bm{z}\bm{z}^{T}\bm{u}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}z_{i}z_{j}=\frac{1}{n\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+{\scriptstyle\mathscr{O}}_{P}\textstyle\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\right).

(49)

We therefore have

\langle\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j},\bm{\Pi}_{2}(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j})\rangle=\frac{1}{nd_{i}}+\frac{1}{nd_{j}}-\frac{2}{n\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+{\scriptstyle\mathscr{O}}_{P}\textstyle\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\right).

(50)

The degree
d_{i}
of node
i
is a binomial random variable, which concentrates around its
mean,
p(n/2-1)+qn/2\approx n(p+q)/2
for large network size. Also,
1/d_{i}
is a
binomial reciprocal that also concentrates around its mean, which is given by
2/((p+q)n)+{\scriptstyle\mathscr{O}}\textstyle\left(\displaystyle 1/n^{2}\right)

[
Rempała, 2004
]
. We conclude that in the limit of large network size,

\langle\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j},\bm{\Pi}_{2}(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j})\rangle=\frac{4}{n^{2}(p+q)}\bigg(1-\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\bigg)+{\scriptstyle\mathscr{O}}_{P}\textstyle\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\right).

(51)

Combining (
46
) and (
51
) yields

\langle\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j},\frac{\lambda_{2}}{1-\lambda_{2}}\bm{\Pi}_{2}(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j})\rangle=\frac{(p-q)}{(p+q)}\frac{4}{n^{2}q}\frac{\big(1-\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\big)}{2}+{\scriptstyle\mathscr{O}}_{P}\textstyle\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\right).

(52)

We note that

\frac{(p-q)}{(p+q)}\frac{4}{n^{2}q}\frac{\big(1-\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\big)}{2}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{4}{n(p+q)}&\text{if $i$ and $j$ are in the same community}\\
\displaystyle\frac{4}{n(p+q)}+\frac{(p-q)}{(p+q)}\frac{4}{n^{2}q}&\text{if $i$ and $j$ are in
different communities,}\end{cases}

(53)

which confirms that
\langle\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j},\frac{\lambda_{2}}{1-\lambda_{2}}\bm{\Pi}_{2}(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j})\rangle
provides the correction in (
35
) created by the
bottleneck between the communities. Finally, we show in
section
5.4
that the last term in the expansion (
44
)
of
R_{ij}
can be neglected,

\big\lvert\langle\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j},\bm{Q}(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j})\rangle\big\rvert\leq\left(\frac{1}{d_{i}}+\frac{1}{d_{j}}\right)\frac{8\sqrt{2}}{(np)^{3/2}}\quad\text{almost surely.}

(54)

This concludes the proof the lemma.∎∎

Let
\bm{R}
be the

\bm{A}

\bm{R}=\displaystyle\frac{4}{n(p+q)}\bm{J}+\frac{p-q}{p+q}\frac{4}{n^{2}q}\bm{K},\mspace{24mu}\text{where}\mspace{8mu}\bm{K}\mspace{8mu}\text{is given by (\ref{lamatriceK})}.

The proof is elementary and relies on the following three identities. First, we recover

4
Discussion of our results

This paper provides analytical estimates of the sample Fréchet mean network when the sample is
generated from a stochastic block model. We derived the expression of the sample Fréchet mean
when the probability space
\mathcal{G}\mspace{-1mu}\big(\mspace{-2mu}n,p,q\mspace{-1mu}\big)
is equipped with two very different distances: the
Hamming distance and the resistance distance. This work answers the question raised by

Lunagómez et al. [2021]
“what is the “mean” network (rather than how do we estimate the
success-probabilities of an inhomogeneous random network), and do we want the “mean”
itself to be a network?”.

We show that the sample mean network is an unweighted network whose topology is usually
very different from the average topology of the sample. Specifically, in the regime of
networks where
\min p_{ij}<1/2
(e.g., networks with
{\scriptstyle\mathscr{O}}\textstyle\left(\displaystyle n^{2}\right)
but
\omega(n)

edges), then the sample Fréchet mean is the empty network, and is pointless. In contrast, the
resistance distance leads to a sample Fréchet mean that recovers the correct topology induced
by the community structure; the edge density of the sample Fréchet mean network is the
expected edge density of the random network ensemble. The effective resistance distance is
thus able to capture the large scale (community structure) and the mesoscale, which spans
scales from the global to the local scales (the degree of a vertex).

This work is significant because it provides for the first time analytical estimates of
the sample Fréchet mean for the stochastic blockmodel, which is at the cutting edge of
rigorous probabilistic analysis of random networks
[
Abbe, 2018
]
. The technique of proof
that is used to compute the sample Fréchet mean for the Hamming distance can be extended to
the large class of inhomogeneous random networks
[
Kovalenko, 1971
]
. It should also be
possible to extend our computation of the Fréchet mean with the resistance distance to
stochastic block models with
K
communities of arbitrary size, and varying edge density.

From a practical standpoint, our work informs the choice of distance in the context where
the sample Fréchet mean network has been used to characterize the topology of networks for
network-valued machine learning (e.g., detecting change points in sequences of networks

[
Ghoshdastidar et al., 2020
;
Zambon et al., 2019
]
, computing Fréchet regression
[
Petersen and Müller, 2019
]
, or cluster
network datasets
[
Xu, 2020
]
). Future work includes the analysis of the sample Fréchet mean
when the distance is based on the eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian
Wills and Meyer [2020]
.

\bigg\|\sum_{m=3}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{m}}{1-\lambda_{m}}\bm{\Pi}_{m}\bigg\|^{2}=\sum_{m=3}^{n}\bigg\lvert\frac{\lambda_{m}}{1-\lambda_{m}}\bigg\rvert^{2}\big\|\bm{\Pi}_{m}\big\|^{2}\leq\sum_{m=3}^{n}2\big\lvert\lambda_{m}\big\rvert^{2}\big\|\bm{\Pi}_{m}\big\|^{2}\leq 2\bigg[\max_{m=2}^{n}\big\lvert\lambda_{m}\big\rvert\bigg]^{2},

(87)

because the
\bm{\Pi}_{m}
are orthonormal projectors such that
\sum_{m=1}^{n}\bm{\Pi}_{m}=\bm{I}
.
Using the following concentration result (e.g., Theorem 3.6 in
[
Chung et al., 2003
]
),

\max_{m=3}^{n}\big\lvert\lambda_{m}\big\rvert\leq\frac{8}{\sqrt{np}}\quad\text{almost surely.}

(88)

we conclude that

\big\lvert\langle\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j},\bm{Q}(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j})\rangle\big\rvert\leq\left(\frac{1}{d_{i}}+\frac{1}{d_{j}}\right)\frac{8\sqrt{2}}{\left(np\right)^{3/2}}\quad\text{almost surely.}

(89)

∎∎
