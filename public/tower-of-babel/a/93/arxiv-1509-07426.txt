MM Algorithms for Variance Components Models
Hua Zhou, Liuyi Hu, Jin Zhou, Kenneth Lange

Abstract
Variance components estimation and mixed model analysis are central themes in statistics with applications in numerous scientific disciplines. Despite the best efforts of generations of statisticians and numerical analysts, maximum likelihood estimation and restricted maximum likelihood estimation of variance component models remain numerically challenging. Building on the minorization-maximization (MM) principle, this paper presents a novel iterative algorithm for variance components estimation. MM algorithm is trivial to implement and competitive on large data problems. The algorithm readily extends to more complicated problems such as linear mixed models, multivariate response models possibly with missing data, maximum a posteriori estimation, penalized estimation, and generalized estimating equations (GEE). We establish the global convergence of the MM algorithm to a KKT point and demonstrate, both numerically and theoretically, that it converges faster than the classical EM algorithm when the number of variance components is greater than two and all covariance matrices are positive definite. Keywords: generalized estimating equations (GEE), global convergence, matrix convexity, linear mixed model (LMM), maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation, maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), minorization-maximization (MM), missing data, multivariate response, penalized estimation, restricted maximum likelihood (REML), variance components model

Introduction

Variance components and linear mixed models are among the most potent tools in a statistician’s toolbox. They are essential topics in graduate-level linear model courses and the subject of many
current papers and research monographs
(
Rao and Kleffe, 1988
;
Searle et al., 1992
;
Rao, 1997
;
Khuri et al., 1998
;
Demidenko, 2013
)
. Their applications in agriculture, biology, economics, genetics, epidemiology, and medicine are too numerous to cover here in detail. The recommended books
(
Verbeke and Molenberghs, 2000
;
Weiss, 2005
;
Fitzmaurice et al., 2011
)

stress longitudinal data analysis.

Given an observed
n\times 1
response vector
{\bm{y}}
and
n\times p
predictor matrix
{\bm{X}}
, the simplest variance components model postulates that
{\bm{Y}}\sim N({\bm{X}}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$})
, where

\Omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{i},

and the
{\bm{V}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{V}}_{m}
are
m
fixed positive semidefinite matrices. The parameters of the
model can be divided into mean effects
(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})
and variance components

(\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{m}^{2})
, summarized by vectors
\beta
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2}
. Throughout
we assume
\Omega
is positive definite. The extension to singular
\Omega

will not be pursued here. Estimation revolves around the log-likelihood function

\displaystyle L(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}-\frac{1}{2}({\bm{y}}-{\bm{X}}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{-1}({\bm{y}}-{\bm{X}}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}).

(1)

Among the commonly used methods for estimating variance components, maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)
(
Hartley and Rao, 1967
)
and restricted (or residual) MLE (REML)
(
Harville, 1977
)
are the most popular. REML first projects
{\bm{y}}
to the null space of
{\bm{X}}
and then estimates variance components based on the projected responses. If the columns of the matrix
{\bm{B}}
span the null space of

{\bm{X}}^{T}
, then REML estimates the
\sigma_{i}^{2}
by maximizing the log-likelihood of the redefined response
vector
{\bm{B}}^{T}{\bm{Y}}
, which is normally distributed with mean
{\mathbf{0}}
and covariance
{\bm{B}}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}{\bm{B}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}^{2}{\bm{B}}^{T}{\bm{V}}_{i}{\bm{B}}
.

There exists a large literature on iterative algorithms for finding MLE and REML
(
Laird and Ware, 1982
;
Lindstrom and Bates, 1988
;
Lindstrom and Bates, 1990
;
Harville and Callanan, 1990
;
Callanan and Harville, 1991
;
Bates and Pinheiro, 1998
;
Schafer and Yucel, 2002
)
. Fitting variance component models remains a challenge in models with a large sample size
n
or a large number of variance components
m
. Newton’s method
(
Lindstrom and Bates, 1988
)
converges quickly but is numerically unstable owing to the non-concavity of the log-likelihood. Fisher’s scoring algorithm replaces the observed information matrix in Newton’s method by the
expected information matrix and yields an ascent algorithm when safeguarded by step halving. However the calculation and inversion of expected information matrices cost
O(mn^{3})+O(m^{3})
flops for unstructured
{\bm{V}}_{i}
and quickly become impractical when either
n
or
m
is large. The expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm initiated by Dempster et al. is a third alternative

(
Dempster et al., 1977
;
Laird and Ware, 1982
;
Laird et al., 1987
;
Lindstrom and Bates, 1988
;
Bates and Pinheiro, 1998
)
. Compared to Newton’s method, the EM algorithm is easy to implement and numerically stable, but painfully slow to converge. In practice, a strategy of priming Newton’s method by a few EM steps leverages the stability of EM and the faster convergence of second-order methods. Quasi-Newton methods dispense with explicit calculation of the observed information while achieving a superlinear rate of convergence.

In this paper we derive a minorization-maximization (MM) algorithm for finding the MLE and REML estimates of variance components. We prove global convergence of the MM algorithm to a Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) point and explain why MM generally converges faster than EM for models with more than two variance components. We also sketch extensions of the MM algorithm to the multivariate response model with possibly missing responses, the linear mixed model (LMM), maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation, penalized estimation, and generalized estimating equations (GEE). The numerical efficiency of the MM algorithm is illustrated through simulated data sets and a genomic example with more than 200 variance components.

Preliminaries

Background on MM algorithms

Throughout we reserve Greek letters for parameters and indicate the current iteration number by a superscript
t
. The MM principle for maximizing an objective function
f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
involves minorizing the objective function
f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
by a surrogate function
g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
around the current iterate
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}
of a search
(
Lange et al., 2000
)
. Minorization is defined by the
two conditions

\displaystyle f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})

(2)

\displaystyle f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})\>,\quad\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}.

In other words, the surface
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mapsto g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
lies below the
surface
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mapsto f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
and is tangent to it at the point
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}
. Construction of the minorizing function
g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
constitutes the first
M of the MM algorithm. The second M of the algorithm maximizes the surrogate
g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})

rather than
f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
. The point
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)}
maximizing

g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
satisfies the ascent property

f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})\geq f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
. This fact follows from the inequalities

\displaystyle f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\geq\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}),

(3)

reflecting the definition of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)}
and the tangency and domination conditions (
2
). The ascent property makes the MM algorithm remarkably stable. The validity of the descent property depends only on increasing
g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
, not on maximizing
g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
. With obvious changes, the MM algorithm also applies to minimization rather than to maximization. To minimize a function
f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
, we majorize it by a surrogate function
g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
and minimize
g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
to produce the next iterate
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)}
. The acronym should not be confused with the maximization-maximization algorithm in the variational Bayes context
(
Jeon, 2012
)
.

The MM principle
(
De Leeuw, 1994
;
Heiser, 1995
;
Kiers, 2002
;
Lange et al., 2000
;

finds applications in multidimensional scaling

,
ranking of sports teams

, variable selection
(

(

, and many other areas
(

. The celebrated EM principle
(

)
is a special case of the MM principle. The
Q

Thus, both EM and MM share the same advantages: simplicity, stability, graceful adaptation to constraints, and the tendency to avoid large matrix inversion. The more general MM perspective frees algorithm derivation from the missing data straitjacket and invites wider applications
(
Wu and Lange, 2010
)
. Figure

EM and MM algorithms often exhibit slow convergence. Fortunately, this defect can be remedied by off-the-shelf acceleration techniques for fixed point iterations. The recently developed squared iterative method (SQUAREM)
(
Varadhan and Roland, 2008
)
and the quasi-Newton acceleration method
(
Zhou et al., 2011

are particularly attractive, given their simplicity and minimal memory and computational costs. Our numerical experiments feature the unadorned MM algorithm and the quasi-Newton accelerated MM (aMM) algorithm based on one secant pair. Using more secant pairs is likely to further improve performance.

Convex matrix functions

For symmetric matrices we write
{\bm{A}}\preceq{\bm{B}}
when
{\bm{B}}-{\bm{A}}
is positive semidefinite and
{\bm{A}}\prec{\bm{B}}
if
{\bm{B}}-{\bm{A}}
is positive definite. A matrix-valued function
f
is said to be (matrix) convex if

\displaystyle f[\lambda{\bm{A}}+(1-\lambda){\bm{B}}]

\displaystyle\preceq

\displaystyle\lambda f({\bm{A}})+(1-\lambda)f({\bm{B}})

for all
{\bm{A}}
,
{\bm{B}}
, and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Our derivation of the MM variance components algorithm hinges on the convexity of the two functions mentioned in the next lemma.

Lemma 1
.

(a) The matrix fractional function
f({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})={\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{B}}^{-1}{\bm{A}}
is jointly convex in the

m\times n
matrix
{\bm{A}}
and the
m\times m
positive definite matrix
{\bm{B}}
.
(b) The log determinant function
f({\bm{B}})=\ln\det{\bm{B}}
is concave on the set of positive definite matrices.

Proof.

The matrix fractional function is matrix convex because its epigraph

\displaystyle\{({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}},{\bm{C}}):{\bm{B}}\succ{\mathbf{0}},{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{B}}^{-1}{\bm{A}}\preceq{\bm{C}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}},{\bm{C}}):{\bm{B}}\succ{\mathbf{0}},\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{B}}&{\bm{A}}\\
{\bm{A}}^{T}&{\bm{C}}\end{pmatrix}\succeq{\mathbf{0}}\right\}

is a convex set. Here
{\bm{C}}
varies over the set of
n\times n
positive semidefinite matrices.
The equivalence of these two epigraph representations is proved in
Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004
. For the concavity of the log determinant, see
Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004
.

Lemma 2

The Hadamard product of a positive definite matrix with a positive semidefinite matrix with positive diagonal entries is positive definite.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\!\!=\!\!

The major computational cost of Algorithm 1 is inversion of the covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{(t)}
at each iteration. Problem specific structures such as block diagonal matrices or a diagonal matrix plus a low-rank matrix are often exploited to speed up matrix inversion. The special case of
m=2
variance components deserves attention as repeated matrix inversion can be avoided by invoking the
simultaneous congruence decomposition for two symmetric matrices, one of which is positive definite

(
Rao, 1973
;
Horn and Johnson, 1985
)
. This decomposition is also called the generalized eigenvalue decomposition
(
Golub and Van Loan, 1996
;
Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004
)
. If one assumes
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}=\sigma_{1}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{1}+\sigma_{2}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{2}
and lets
({\bm{V}}_{1},{\bm{V}}_{2})\mapsto({\bm{D}},{\bm{U}})
be the decomposition with
{\bm{U}}
nonsingular,
{\bm{U}}^{T}{\bm{V}}_{1}{\bm{U}}={\bm{D}}
diagonal, and
{\bm{U}}^{T}{\bm{V}}_{2}{\bm{U}}={\bm{I}}
, then

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}^{-T}(\sigma_{1}^{2(t)}{\bm{D}}+\sigma_{2}^{2(t)}{\bm{I}}_{n}){\bm{U}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{-(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}(\sigma_{1}^{2(t)}{\bm{D}}+\sigma_{2}^{2(t)}{\bm{I}}_{n})^{-1}{\bm{U}}^{T}

\displaystyle\det(\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{(t)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det(\sigma_{1}^{2(t)}{\bm{D}}+\sigma_{2}^{2(t)}{\bm{I}}_{n})\det({\bm{U}}^{-T}{\bm{U}}^{-1})

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det(\sigma_{1}^{2(t)}{\bm{D}}+\sigma_{2}^{2(t)}{\bm{I}}_{n})\det({\bm{V}}_{2}).

With the revised responses
\tilde{\bm{y}}={\bm{U}}^{T}{\bm{y}}
and the revised predictor matrix

\tilde{\bm{X}}={\bm{U}}^{T}{\bm{X}}

only vector operations and costs
O(n)

(\tilde{\bm{y}},\tilde{\bm{X}})
and observation weights
w_{i}^{(t)}=(\sigma_{1}^{2(t)}d_{i}+\sigma_{2}^{2(t)})^{-1}

Numerical experiments

This section compares the numerical performance of MM, quasi-Newton accelerated MM, EM, and Fisher scoring on simulated data from a two-way ANOVA random effects model and a genetic model. For ease of comparison, all algorithm runs start from
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2(0)}={\mathbf{1}}
and terminate when the relative change
(L^{(t+1)}-L^{(t)})/(|L^{(t)}|+1)
in the log-likelihood is less than
10^{-6}
.

Two-way ANOVA:
We simulated data from a two-way ANOVA random effects model

\displaystyle y_{ijk}=\mu+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}+(\alpha\beta)_{ij}+\epsilon_{ijk},

where
\alpha_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{1}^{2})
,
\beta_{j}\sim N(0,\sigma_{2}^{2})
,
(\alpha\beta)_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma_{3}^{2})
, and
\epsilon_{ijk}\sim N(0,\sigma_{e}^{2})
are jointly independent. This corresponds to
m=4
variance components. In the simulation, we set
\sigma_{2}^{2}=\sigma_{3}^{2}=\sigma_{e}^{2}
and varied the ratio
\sigma_{1}^{2}/\sigma_{e}^{2}
; the numbers of levels
a
and
b
in factor 1 and factor 2, respectively; and the number of observations
c
in each combination of factor levels. For each simulation scenario, we simulated 50 replicates. The sample size was
n=abc
for each replicate.

Tables
1
and
2
show the average number of iterations and the average runtimes when there are
a=b=5
levels of each factor. Based on these results and further results not shown for other combinations of
a
and
b
, we draw the following conclusions. Fisher scoring takes the fewest iterations. The MM algorithm always takes fewer iterations than the EM algorithm. Accelerated MM further improves the convergence rate of MM. The faster rate of convergence of Fisher scoring is outweighed by the extra cost of evaluating and inverting the covariance matrix. When the sample size
n=abc
is large, Fisher scoring takes much longer than either EM or MM.

Genetic model:
We simulated a quantitative trait
{\bm{y}}
from a genetic model with two variance components and covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}=\sigma_{a}^{2}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}+\sigma_{e}^{2}{\bm{I}}
, where
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}
is a full-rank empirical kinship matrix estimated from the genome-wide measurements of 212 individuals using Option 29 of the Mendel software
(
Lange et al., 2013
;
Lange et al., 2005
)
. In this example, Fisher scoring excels at smaller
\sigma_{a}^{2}/\sigma_{e}^{2}
ratios, while accelerated MM is fastest at larger
\sigma_{a}^{2}/\sigma_{e}^{2}
ratios.

In summary, the MM algorithm appears competitive even in small-scale examples. Modern applications often involve a large number of variance components. In this setting, the EM algorithm suffers from slow convergence and Fisher scoring from an extremely high cost per iteration. Our genomic example in Section

4
Global convergence of the MM algorithm

The KKT necessary conditions for a local maximum

\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2}=(\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{m}^{2})
of the log-likelihood
(
1
) require each component of the score vector to satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma_{i}^{2}}L(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\{0\}&\sigma_{i}^{2}>0\\
(-\infty,0]&\sigma_{i}^{2}=0\,.\end{cases}

In this section we establish the global convergence of Algorithm
1
to a KKT point. To reduce the notational burden, we assume that
{\bm{X}}
is null and omit estimation of fixed effects
\beta
. The analysis easily extends to the MLE case. Our convergence analysis relies on characterizing the properties of the objective function
L(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})
and the MM algorithmic mapping
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2}\mapsto M(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})
defined by equation (
8
). Special attention must be paid to the boundary values
\sigma_{i}^{2}=0
. We prove convergences for two cases, which cover most applications.

Assumption 1
.

All
{\bm{V}}_{i}
are positive definite.

Assumption 2
.

{\bm{V}}_{1}
is positive definite, each
{\bm{V}}_{i}
is nontrivial,
{\cal H}=\text{span}\{{\bm{V}}_{2},\ldots,{\bm{V}}_{m}\}
has dimension
q<n
, and
{\bm{y}}\notin{\cal H}
.

The genetic model in Section
3
satisfies Assumption 1, while the two-way ANOVA model satisfies Assumption 2. The key condition
{\bm{y}}\notin\text{span}\{{\bm{V}}_{2},\ldots,{\bm{V}}_{m}\}
in the second case is critical for the existence of an MLE or an REML
(
Demidenko and Massam, 1999
;
Grządziel and Michalski, 2014
)
. We will derive a sequence of lemmas en route to the global convergence result declared in Theorem
1
.

Lemma 3
.

Under Assumption 1 or 2, the log-likelihood function (
1
) is coercive in the sense that the
super-level set
S_{c}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2}\geq{\mathbf{0}}:L(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})\geq c\}
is compact for every
c
.

Proof.

Let us first prove the assertion when all of the covariance matrices
{\bm{V}}_{i}

are positive definite. If we set
r=\|\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2}\|_{1}
and
\alpha_{i}=r^{-1}\sigma_{i}^{2}

for each
i
, then the log-likelihood satisfies

\displaystyle L(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{n}{2}\ln r-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big)-\frac{1}{2r}{\bm{y}}^{T}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big)^{-1}{\bm{y}}.

The functions
\ln\det\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big)
and

{\bm{y}}^{T}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big)^{-1}{\bm{y}}
of
\alpha
are
defined and continuous on the unit simplex and hence bounded there.
The dominant term
-\frac{n}{2}\ln r
of the loglikelihood
tends to
-\infty
as
r
tends to
\infty
.

To prove the assertion under Assumption 2, consider first the case
{\bm{V}}_{1}={\bm{I}}_{n}
. Setting
\alpha_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{1}^{2}
for
i=2,\ldots,m
reduces the loglikelihood to

\displaystyle L(\sigma_{1}^{2},\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{n}{2}\ln\sigma_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\Big({\bm{I}}_{n}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big)-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}{\bm{y}}^{T}\Big({\bm{I}}_{n}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big)^{-1}\!\!{\bm{y}}.

(11)

The middle term on the right satisfies

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\Big({\bm{I}}_{n}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0

because
\det\,({\bm{I}}_{n}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i})\,\geq\,\det{\bm{I}}_{n}=1.

Now let
{\bm{U}}=({\bm{U}}_{q},{\bm{U}}_{n-q})
be an
n\times n
orthogonal matrix whose left columns

{\bm{U}}_{q}
span
{\cal H}
and whose right columns
{\bm{U}}_{n-q}
span
{\cal H}^{\perp}
. The identity

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}^{T}\Big({\bm{I}}_{n}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big){\bm{U}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{I}}_{q}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{U}}_{q}^{T}{\bm{V}}_{i}{\bm{U}}_{q}&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&{\bm{I}}_{n-q}\end{pmatrix}

follows from the orthogonality relations
{\bm{U}}_{n-q}^{T}{\bm{V}}_{i}\,=\,{\bm{U}}_{n-q}^{T}{\bm{U}}_{q}\,=\,{\mathbf{0}}_{(n-q)\times n}
. This in turn implies

\displaystyle\Big({\bm{I}}_{n}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}\begin{pmatrix}({\bm{I}}_{q}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{U}}_{q}^{T}{\bm{V}}_{i}{\bm{U}}_{q})^{-1}&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&{\bm{I}}_{n-q}\end{pmatrix}{\bm{U}}^{T}

\displaystyle\succeq

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&{\bm{I}}_{n-q}\end{pmatrix}{\bm{U}}^{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}_{n-q}{\bm{U}}_{n-q}^{T}.

Therefore the quadratic term in equation (
11
) is bounded below by the positive constant

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}^{T}\Big({\bm{I}}_{n}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big)^{-1}\!\!{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}^{T}{\bm{U}}_{n-q}{\bm{U}}_{n-q}^{T}{\bm{y}}\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\|\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}^{\perp}}{\bm{y}}\|^{2}\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits>\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits 0.

Here the assumption
{\bm{y}}\notin{\cal H}
guarantees the projection property

\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}^{\perp}}{\bm{y}}\neq{\mathbf{0}}
.

Next we show that the loglikelihood tends to
-\infty
when
\sigma_{1}^{2}
tends to
0
or
\infty
or
when
\|\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}\|_{2}
tends to
\infty
. The second of the two inequalities

\displaystyle L(\sigma_{0}^{2},\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{n}{2}\ln\sigma_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\Big({\bm{I}}_{n}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big)-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}\|\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}^{\perp}}{\bm{y}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{n}{2}\ln\sigma_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}\|\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}^{\perp}}{\bm{y}}\|^{2}

renders the claim about
\sigma_{1}^{2}
obvious. To prove the claim about
\alpha
, we
make the worst case choice
\sigma_{i}^{2}=\|\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}^{\perp}}{\bm{y}}\|^{2}

in the first inequality.
It follows that

\displaystyle L(\sigma_{0}^{2},\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\Big({\bm{I}}_{n}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big)-\frac{n}{2}\ln\|\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}^{\perp}}{\bm{y}}\|^{2}-\frac{n}{2}.

If
\alpha_{j}
tends to
\infty
, then the inequality

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\Big({\bm{I}}_{n}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\alpha_{i}{\bm{V}}_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\Big({\bm{I}}_{n}+\alpha_{j}{\bm{V}}_{j}\Big)\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ln(1+\alpha_{j}\lambda_{jk})

holds, where the
\lambda_{jk}
are the eigenvalues of
{\bm{V}}_{j}
.
At least one of these eigenvalues is positive because
{\bm{V}}_{j}
is nontrivial.
It follows that
L(\sigma_{0}^{2},\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$})
tends to
-\infty

in this case as well.

For the general case where
{\bm{V}}_{1}
is non-singular but not necessarily
{\bm{I}}_{n}
, let

{\bm{V}}^{1/2}_{1}
be the symmetric square root of

5
MM versus EM

Examination of Tables

{\bm{X}}
is null.
Since the EM principle is just a special instance of the MM principle, we can compare their convergence properties in a unified framework. Consider an MM map

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle dM(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\infty})(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\infty}),

where

at the optimal point
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\infty}
of

(

demonstrate that

\displaystyle dM(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\infty})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{I}}-[d^{2}g(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\infty}\mid\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\infty})]^{-1}d^{2}f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\infty}).

In other words, the local convergence rate is determined by how well the surrogate surface

approximates the objective surface
f(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})

near the optimal point
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\infty}

is called the

(
Meng and Rubin, 1991

. Fast convergence occurs when the surrogate

and scale matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}_{i}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
. The log density of the posterior distribution is

\displaystyle L({\bm{B}},\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}|{\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{vec}({\bm{Y}}-{\bm{X}}{\bm{B}})^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{-1}\mathrm{vec}({\bm{Y}}-{\bm{X}}{\bm{B}})

(18)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\nu_{i}+d+1)\ln\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{tr}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{-1})+c,

where
c
is an irrelevant constant. Invoking the minorizations (
6
) and (
14
) for the first two terms and the supporting hyperplane minorization

\displaystyle-\ln\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\ln\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{(t)}-\mathrm{tr}\{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{-(t)}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{(t)})\}

for
-\ln\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}
gives the surrogate function

\displaystyle g(\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}|\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}^{(t)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{tr}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{-(t)}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}\otimes{\bm{V}}_{i}))-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{tr}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{(t)}{\bm{R}}^{(t)T}{\bm{V}}_{i}{\bm{R}}^{(t)}\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{(t)}\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\nu_{i}+d+1)\mathrm{tr}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{-(t)}\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{tr}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{-1})+c^{(t)}.

The optimal
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}
satisfies the stationarity condition

\displaystyle({\bm{I}}_{d}\otimes{\mathbf{1}}_{n})^{T}[({\mathbf{1}}_{d}{\mathbf{1}}_{d}^{T}\otimes{\bm{V}}_{i})\odot\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{-(t)}]({\bm{I}}_{d}\otimes{\mathbf{1}}_{n})+(\nu_{i}+d+1)\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{-(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{(t)}{\bm{R}}^{(t)T}{\bm{V}}_{i}{\bm{R}}^{(t)}\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{(t)}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}_{i})\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{i}^{-1}

and can be found using Lemma
6
.

6.5
Variable selection

In the statistical analysis of high-dimensional data, the imposition of sparsity leads to better interpretation and more stable parameter estimation. MM algorithms mesh well with penalized estimation. The simple variance components model (
1
) illustrates this fact.
For the selection of fixed effects, minimizing the lasso-penalized log-likelihood

\displaystyle-L(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})+\lambda\sum_{j}|\beta_{j}|

is often recommended
(
Schelldorfer et al., 2011
)
. The only change to the MM Algorithm
1
is that in estimating
\beta
, one solves a lasso penalized general least squares problem rather than an ordinary general least squares problem.
The updates of the variance components
\sigma_{i}^{2}
remain the same. For selection among a large number of variance components, one can minimize the ridge-penalized log-likelihood

\displaystyle-L(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}^{2}

subject to the nonnegativity constraints
\sigma_{i}^{2}\geq 0
. Here the standard deviations

\sigma_{i}
are the underlying parameters. The variance update (
8
) becomes

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{2(t+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{2(t)}\sqrt{\frac{({\bm{y}}-{\bm{X}}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(t)})^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{-(t)}{\bm{V}}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{-(t)}({\bm{y}}-{\bm{X}}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(t)})}{\mathrm{tr}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{-(t)}{\bm{V}}_{i})+2\lambda}},\quad i=1,\ldots,m.

The updates for the fixed effects
\beta
are unaffected. Equation (
6.5
)
clearly exhibits shrinkage but no thresholding. The lasso penalized log-likelihood

\displaystyle-L(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}

(19)

subject to nonnegativity constraint
\sigma_{i}\geq 0
achieves both ends. The update of
\sigma_{i}
is chosen among the positive roots of a quartic equation and the boundary 0, whichever yields a lower objective value.

6.6
Beyond the linear model

One can extend the MM algorithms to binary and discrete response data with the framework of generalized estimating equations (GEE)
(
Liang and Zeger, 1986
)
. Again consider
n
independent data clusters
({\bm{y}}_{i},{\bm{X}}_{i})
. In longitudinal studies,
({\bm{y}}_{i},{\bm{X}}_{i})
would be the responses and clinical covariates of subject
i
at different time points. In genetic studies,
({\bm{y}}_{i},{\bm{X}}_{i})
would be the trait values and covariates of individuals within family
i
. GEE captures the within-cluster correlation by specifying the first two moments of the conditional distribution of
{\bm{y}}_{i}
given
{\bm{X}}_{i}
, namely
\mu_{ij}=E(y_{ij}|{\bm{x}}_{ij})
and
\sigma^{2}_{ij}=\text{Var}(y_{ij}|{\bm{x}}_{ij})
. If one assumes that
y_{ij}
follows an exponential family with canonical link, then

\displaystyle\mu_{ij}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(\theta_{ij})\;\;\textrm{ and }\;\;\sigma^{2}_{ij}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits\phi\mu^{\prime}(\theta_{ij}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,\;j=1,\ldots,n_{i},

where
\mu(t)
is a differentiable canonical link function,
\mu^{\prime}(t)
is its first derivative,
\theta_{ij}={\bm{x}}_{ij}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}
is the linear systematic part of
y_{ij}
associated with the covariates,
\phi
is an over-dispersion parameter, and
\beta
is the vector of fixed effects.

The GEE estimator of
\beta
solves the equation

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}d\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})^{T}{\bm{V}}_{i}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})[{\bm{y}}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{0}}_{p},

where
{\bm{y}}_{i}=(y_{i1},\dots,y_{in_{i}})^{T}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})=[\mu_{i1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}),\ldots,\mu_{in_{i}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})]^{T}
,
{\bm{V}}_{i}=\text{cov}({\bm{y}}_{i})
is the working covariance matrix of the
i
-th subject, and
d\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})
is the differential of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})
. In longitudinal studies,
{\bm{V}}_{i}
is often parameterized as
{\bm{V}}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})={\bm{A}}_{i}^{1/2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}){\bm{R}}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}){\bm{A}}_{i}^{1/2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})
, where
{\bm{A}}^{1/2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})
is a diagonal matrix with standard deviations
\sigma_{ij}
along its diagonal, and
{\bm{R}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$})
is a correlation matrix with parameters
\alpha
. This parameterization is too restrictive in many other applications. For instance, in genetic studies, it is critical to dissect the variance into different sources such as additive, dominance, and household environment
(
Lange, 2002
)
. This suggests the variance component parameterization

\displaystyle{\bm{R}}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{a}^{2}2\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{i}+\sigma_{d}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}_{7,i}+\sigma_{h}^{2}{\bm{H}}_{i}+\sigma_{e}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{n_{i}},\quad\sigma_{a}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}+\sigma_{h}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits=\mathop{\;\>}\nolimits 1,

where in the
i
-th family
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{i}
is the theoretical kinship matrix,
\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}_{7,i}
is the dominance variance matrix, and
{\bm{H}}_{i}
is the household indicator matrix. The matrices

\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{i}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}_{7,i}
, and
{\bm{H}}_{i}
are correlation matrices, and the simplex constraint ensures
{\bm{R}}_{i}
is as well. In general, the variance component parameterization
{\bm{R}}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma_{j}^{2}{\bm{R}}_{ij}
with the simplex constraint in force is reasonable. In this setting the GEE update of
\beta
given
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2}
solves the equation

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{A}}_{i}^{-1/2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}){\bm{R}}_{i}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2}){\bm{A}}^{-1/2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})[{\bm{y}}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{0}}_{p}.

This is just the classical GEE update. The difficulty lies in updating
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2}
given
\beta
. We propose minimizing the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi({\bm{R}}_{i})
, where
\psi(t)
is a scalar convex loss function. Example loss functions include the Mahalanobis criterion

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{1}({\bm{R}}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\bm{y}}_{i}-\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{i})^{T}\widehat{\bm{A}}_{i}^{-1/2}{\bm{R}}_{i}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})\widehat{\bm{A}}_{i}^{-1/2}({\bm{y}}_{i}-\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{i})

and the sum of squared Frobenius distances

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{2}({\bm{R}}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{tr}\left[({\bm{y}}_{i}-\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{i})({\bm{y}}_{i}-\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{i})^{T}-\widehat{\bm{A}}_{i}^{1/2}{\bm{R}}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2})\widehat{\bm{A}}_{i}^{1/2}\right]^{2}.

The convexity of
\psi_{1}(t)
entails a minorization similar to the minorization
(
5
). Minimizing the surrogate then yields the MM update

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}^{2(t+1)}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\bm{y}}_{i}-\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{i})^{T}\widehat{\bm{A}}_{i}^{-1/2}{\bm{R}}_{i}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2(t)}){\bm{R}}_{ij}{\bm{R}}_{i}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2(t)})\widehat{\bm{A}}_{i}^{-1/2}({\bm{y}}_{i}-\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\bm{y}}_{i}-\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{i})^{T}\widehat{\bm{A}}_{i}^{-1/2}{\bm{R}}_{i}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2(t)}){\bm{R}}_{ij}{\bm{R}}_{i}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{2(t)})\widehat{\bm{A}}_{i}^{-1/2}({\bm{y}}_{i}-\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{i})}.

Under
\psi_{2}(t)
the MM update boils down to projection onto the simplex. Further exploration of these ideas probably deserves another paper and will be omitted here for the sake of brevity.

7
A numerical example

Quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping aims to identify genes associated with a quantitative trait. Current sequencing technology measures millions of genetic markers in study subjects. Traditional single-marker tests suffer from low power due to the low frequency of many markers and the
corrections needed for multiple hypothesis testing. Region-based association tests are a powerful alternative for analyzing next generation sequencing data with abundant rare variants.

Suppose
{\bm{y}}
is a
n\times 1
vector of quantitative trait measurements on
n
people,
{\bm{X}}
is an
n\times p
predictor matrix (incorporating predictors such as sex, smoking history, and principal components for ethnic admixture), and
{\bm{G}}
is an
n\times m
genotype matrix of
m
genetic variants in a pre-defined region. The linear mixed model assumes

\displaystyle{\bm{Y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}+{\bm{G}}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$},\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}\sim N({\mathbf{0}},\sigma_{g}^{2}{\bm{I}}),\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\sim N({\mathbf{0}},\sigma_{e}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{n}),

where
\beta
are fixed effects,
\gamma
are random genetic effects, and
\sigma_{g}^{2}
and
\sigma_{e}^{2}
are variance components for the genetic and environmental effects, respectively. Thus, the phenotype vector
{\bm{Y}}
has covariance
\sigma_{g}^{2}{\bm{G}}{\bm{G}}^{T}+\sigma_{e}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{n}
, where
{\bm{G}}{\bm{G}}^{T}
is the kernel matrix capturing the overall effect of the
m
variants. Current approaches test the null hypothesis
\sigma_{g}^{2}=0
for each region separately and then adjust for multiple testing
(
Lee et al., 2014
)
. Hence, we consider the joint model

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}+s_{1}^{-1/2}{\bm{G}}_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{1}+\cdots+s_{m}^{-1/2}{\bm{G}}_{m}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{m}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$},

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{i}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle N({\mathbf{0}},\sigma_{i}^{2}{\bm{I}}),\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\sim N({\mathbf{0}},\sigma_{e}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{n})

and select the variance components
\sigma_{i}^{2}
via the penalization (
19
). Here
s_{i}
is the number of variants in region
i
, and the weights
s_{i}^{-1/2}
put all variance components on the same scale.

We illustrate this approach using the COPDGene exome sequencing study (
http://www.copdgene.org/
)
(
Regan et al., 2010
)
. After quality control,
399
individuals and 646,125 genetic variants remain for analysis. Genetic variants are grouped into 16,619 genes to expose those genes associated with the complex trait
height
. We include
age
,
sex
, and the top 3 principal components in the mean effects. Because the number of genes vastly exceeds the sample size
n=399
, we first pare the 16,619 genes down to 200 genes according to their marginal likelihood ratio test p-values and then carry out penalized estimation of the 200 variance components in the joint model (
19
). This is similar to the sure independence screening strategy for selecting mean effects
(
Fan and Lv, 2008
)
. Genes are ranked according to the order they appear in the lasso solution path. Table
4
lists the top 10 genes together with their marginal LRT p-values. Figure
2
displays the corresponding segment of the lasso solution path. It is noteworthy that the ranking of genes by penalized estimation differs from the ranking according to marginal p-values. The same phenomenon occurs in selection of highly correlated mean predictors. This penalization approach for selecting variance components warrants further theoretical study. It is reassuring that the simple MM algorithm scales to high-dimensional problems.

8
Discussion

The current paper leverages the MM principle to design powerful and versatile algorithms
for variance components estimation. The MM algorithms derived are notable for their simplicity, generality, numerical efficiency, and theoretical guarantees. Both ordinary MLE and REML are apt to benefit. Other extensions are possible. In nonlinear models
(
Bates and Watts, 1988
;
Lindstrom and Bates, 1990
)
, the mean response is a nonlinear function in the fixed effects
\beta
. One can easily modify the MM algorithms to update
\beta
by a few rounds of Gauss-Newton iteration. The variance components updates remain unchanged.

One can also extend our MM algorithms to elliptically symmetric densities

\displaystyle f({\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa(\delta^{2})}}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}(\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$})^{\frac{1}{2}}}

defined for
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where

\delta^{2}=({\bm{y}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$})^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{-1}({\bm{y}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$})

denotes the Mahalanobis distance between
{\bm{y}}
and
\mu
.
Here we assume that the function
\kappa(s)
is strictly increasing and strictly
concave. Examples of elliptically symmetric densities include the multivariate
t
, slash,
contaminated normal, power exponential, and stable families.
Previous work
(
Huber and Ronchetti, 2009
;
Lange and Sinsheimer, 1993
)
has focused on using the MM principle
to convert parameter estimation for these robust families into parameter estimation
under the multivariate normal. One can chain the relevant majorization

\kappa(s)\leq\kappa(s^{(t)})+\kappa^{\prime}(s^{(t)})(s-s^{(t)})
with our
previous minorizations and simultaneously split variance components and
pass to the more benign setting of the multivariate normal.
