Thermalization of Gauge Theories from their Entanglement Spectrum Preprint: UMD-PP-021-04
Niklas Mueller, Torsten V. Zache, Robert Ott

Abstract
Using dual theories embedded into a larger unphysical Hilbert space along entanglement cuts, we study the Entanglement Structure of 𝐙 2 \mathbf{Z}_{2} lattice gauge theory in ( 2 + 1 ) (2+1) spacetime dimensions. We demonstrate Li and Haldane’s conjecture, and show consistency of the Entanglement Hamiltonian with the Bisognano-Wichmann theorem. Studying non-equilibrium dynamics after a quench, we provide an extensive description of thermalization in 𝐙 2 \mathbf{Z}_{2} gauge theory which proceeds in a characteristic sequence: Maximization of the Schmidt rank and spreading of level repulsion at early times, self-similar evolution with scaling coefficients α = 0.8 ± 0.2 \alpha=0.8\pm 0.2 and β = 0.0 ± 0.1 \beta=0.0\pm 0.1 at intermediate times, and finally thermal saturation of the von Neumann entropy.

\alpha=0.8\pm 0.2\,\quad\beta=0.0\pm 0.1\,.

Our observation implies that thermalization occurs through turbulent transport of probability from the
‘high energy’ (small probability) towards the low lying part of the ES (large probability). The errors quoted for
\alpha,\beta
include finite-volume and errors from the statistical procedure of extracting them; a detailed analysis, including results on larger lattices, can be found in Supplemental Material.

Summary and Conclusions.
In this letter, we explored the entanglement structure of LGTs to characterize ground states, quantum phase transitions and thermalization, using dual theories of
\mathbf{Z}^{2+1}_{2}
embedded into a larger gauge-variant HS only along entanglement boundaries
Buividovich and Polikarpov 2008
;
Casini et al. 2014
;
Aoki et al. 2015
;
Ghosh et al. 2015
;
Lin and Radicevic 2020
;
Chen et al. 2020
. Our fairly simple approach, see Supplemental Material for details, can be generalized to
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
and
U(1)
LGTs; non-Abelian theories
Douçot et al. 2004
;
Raychowdhury and Stryker 2020a
;
Davoudi et al. 2020b
;
Ciavarella et al. 2021
;
Wiese 2021
are more challenging. Ising-like dualities
Drouffe et al. 1979
;
Douçot et al. 2004
;
Mathur and Sreeraj 2015
, prepotential-
Mathur 2005
;
Mathur 2007
;
Anishetty and Raychowdhury 2014
;
Mathur et al. 2010
and ‘Loop-String-Hadron’
Raychowdhury and Stryker 2020b
formulations are promising approaches, and will be explored in future work.

We demonstrated Li and Haldane’s entanglement-boundary conjecture
Li and Haldane 2008
for
\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{2+1}
gauge theory, both analytically (in perturbation theory) and numerically using exact diagonalization. Moreover, we reconstructed the Entanglement Hamiltonians of ground states, finding consistency with expectations from the Bisognano-Wichmann theorem
Kokail et al. 2020
;
Bisognano and Wichmann 1975
;
Bisognano and Wichmann 1976
at arbitrary coupling.
Using the closing of the Entanglement Gap of the ES, we determine the confinement/deconfinement phase transition at
\epsilon_{c}=0.38\pm 0.09
. We find agreement within error bars with the infinite volume results, demonstrating the potential usefulness of Entanglement Structure, compared to computing volume versus boundary law scaling of Wilson loop operators.

Our most important result is that
\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{2+1}
thermalization occurs in
clearly separated stages: Starting from an initial (unentangled) product state, the system maximizes its Schmidt rank quickly, followed by rapid spreading of level repulsion
throughout the ES at early times. An intermediate regime is characterized by self-similar scaling of the Schmidt spectrum, reminiscent of wave turbulence and universality in (semi-)classical systems, with scaling coefficients
\alpha=0.8\pm 0.2
,
\beta=0.0\pm 0.1
.

This observation strongly hints at a reconciliation of the (naively different) quantum versus classical thermalization paradigms, i.e. in terms of matrix elements of observables
Deutsch 1991
;
Srednicki 1994
versus
ergodicity, chaos and universality
Nazarenko 2011
. Because time evolution in quantum mechanics is linear, quantum chaos is hidden in the complexities of energy eigenfunctions
Srednicki 1994
, however, (and perhaps not so surprisingly
Garrison and Grover 2018
) it becomes evident in the Entanglement Spectrum.
Our analysis provides a systematic path for the quantification and classification of this behavior, which is likely generic for gauge and non-gauge systems and in line with the ETH. Our numerical investigations are not exhaustive, and could be extended to, e.g., studying the build-up of volume law entanglement, spectral form factors
Chang et al. 2019
;
Guhr et al. 1998
, or higher order level spacing ratios
Tekur and Santhanam 2020
of the ES. It would also be interesting to apply our techniques to systems with many-body localization
Brenes et al. 2018
.

Apart from the importance of (2+1)d LGTs for, e.g., topological quantum computation
Kitaev 2003
;
Satzinger et al. 2021
, and condensed matter physics
Kasper et al. 2020
;
Wen 2004
, the Entanglement structure of Abelian and non-Abelian gauge theories, such as QCD, may be crucial for thermalization in high energy and nuclear physics, where it is largely unexplored. Examples are the apparent quick thermalization and hydrodynamization of the Quark-Gluon-Plasma in ultra-relativistic heavy ion collisions
Berges et al. 2020
or the structure of QCD bound states in deeply inelastic scattering experiments
Kharzeev and Levin 2017
;
Mueller et al. 2020
;
Tu et al. 2020
.

Understanding thermalization of quantum many-body systems, in particular of gauge theories, is a unique opportunity for quantum computers and analog
simulators

;
Monroe 2002
;
Blais et al. 2004
;
Cirac and Zoller 2012
;
Hauke et al. 2012
;
Preskill 2018
;
Martinez et al. 2016
;
Klco et al. 2018
;
Zache et al. 2018
;
Davoudi et al. 2020a
;
Mil et al. 2020
;
de Jong et al. 2021
;
Schweizer et al. 2019
;

Entanglement structure of quantum many-body states can be extracted in state-of-the-art quantum simulation experiments, see e.g.

I.3
Optimal local approximation

of the Entanglement Hamiltonian

Given the exact reduced state
\rho_{A}
, we wish to obtain an optimal local approximation

\rho_{A}\approx\sigma_{A}
where

\displaystyle\sigma_{A}\equiv\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in A}\beta_{\mathbf{n}}h_{\mathbf{n}}}\;,

\displaystyle Z=\text{Tr}\left[e^{-\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in A}\beta_{\mathbf{n}}h_{\mathbf{n}}}\right]

(25)

where
Z=Z(\{\beta_{\mathbf{n}}\})
,
with variational parameters
\beta_{\mathbf{n}}
and local operators
h_{\mathbf{n}}
. We obtain
\sigma_{A}
by minimizing the relative entropy (Kullback-Leibler divergence)
Kullback and Leibler 1951

\displaystyle S(\rho_{A}||\sigma_{A})

\displaystyle\equiv\text{Tr}\left[\rho_{A}\left(\log\rho_{A}-\log\sigma_{A}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-S(\rho_{A})+\log Z+\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\beta_{\mathbf{n}}\langle h_{\mathbf{n}}\rangle\;,

(26)

where

\langle h_{\mathbf{n}}\rangle=\text{Tr}\left[\rho_{A}h_{\mathbf{n}}\right]
are the expectation values in the state
\rho_{A}
and
S(\rho_{A})
is the corresponding von Neumann entropy. Motivated by the BW theorem, in practice we choose all contributions of the subsystem to the Hamiltonian, i.e. all electric and magnetic energy terms fully supported in
A
,
as the operators
h_{\mathbf{n}}
.
It is well known
Kullback and Leibler 1951
that the relative entropy is a convex function with
S(\rho_{A}||\sigma_{A})\geq 0
where equality is obtained if
\rho_{A}=\sigma_{A}
. While this enables an efficient minimization, we nevertheless need to evaluate
Z
several hundreds to thousands of times in order to obtain convergence. Since our numerical simulations are based on exact diagonalization, we are limited to relatively small system sizes which we have slightly increased by incorporating translational invariance of the variational parameters parallel to the entanglement cut.

I.5
Scaling Analysis

Outlined below are the details of the statistical analysis to determine the scaling coefficients
\alpha,\beta
, closely following
Mace et al. 2020
. Assuming a scaling form of the Schmidt spectrum of states
n
in an intermediate time window,

\displaystyle P(n,t)=\tau^{-\alpha}P(\tau^{\beta}n)\,,

(32)

where
\tau=\epsilon(t-t_{0})
and
t_{0}
is the onset time of self-similarity, we determine
\alpha,\,\beta
and
t_{0}
by the following procedure: First, we define a reference function

\displaystyle f_{\rm ref}(t,\hat{n}=\tau^{\beta}n)=\log\{\tau^{-\alpha}_{\rm ref}P(\tau_{\rm ref}^{\beta}n)\}\,.

(33)

where
\epsilon\cdot t_{\rm ref}=6
. We compare this reference function to a number of
N_{t}
test times,

\displaystyle f_{\rm test}(t,\hat{n})=\log\{\tau^{-\alpha}_{\rm test}P(\tau_{\rm test}^{\beta}n)\}\,.

(34)

where
\epsilon\cdot t_{\rm test}\in\{8,12,16,24,30,40,50\}
. We quantify deviation from ideal scaling in terms of

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\alpha,\beta,t_{0})=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{t\in t_{\rm test}}\frac{\int\frac{d\hat{n}}{\hat{n}}(f_{\rm ref}(\hat{n})-f_{\rm test}(\hat{n}))^{2}}{\int\frac{d\hat{n}}{\hat{n}}(f_{\rm ref}(\hat{n}))^{2}}\,.

(35)

and restrict
n
to the scaling window
n\in[130,1300]
which we vary to determine the error of our fit. We checked that a different choice of test times within the scaling regime
does not change the outcome of our fit.
Note that the spectrum
\hat{n}
is discrete, and thus the integral should be replaced by a sum, but the same equation holds in the limit of a continuous spectrum.

The likelihood of a given set of parameter to reproduce the ideal rescaling function is given by

\displaystyle W(\alpha,\beta,t_{0})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\exp\Big(-\chi^{2}(\alpha,\beta,t_{0})/\chi^{2}_{\rm min}\Big)

(36)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a normalization, and
\chi^{2}_{\rm min}
is the minimum of Eq. (
35
),
\chi_{\rm min}=4\cdot 10^{-5}
, which is obtained for

\alpha\approx 0.75
,
\beta\approx 0.04
and
\epsilon\cdot t_{0}\approx 2.0
, shown in the main text.

Fig.
9
(top) shows the reduced likelihood distributions
W(\alpha,\beta)
,
W(\alpha,t_{0})
and
W(\beta,t_{0})
obtained by integrating one of three variables, i.e.
W(\alpha,\beta,)=\int dt_{0}W(\alpha,\beta,t_{0})
. Fig.
9
(bottom) shows the
integrated distributions
W(\alpha)
,
W(\beta)
and
W(t_{0})
, and the values we quote in the main text are determined from these distributions,

\alpha=0.8\pm 0.2\,\quad\beta=0.0\pm 0.1\,,

and
\epsilon\cdot t_{0}=1.8\pm 0.5
. These were determined by fitting Fig.
9
(bottom) locally with Gaussians.
