The three-dimensional Seiberg-Witten equations for 3 / 2 − 3/2- spinors: a compactness theorem
Ahmad Reza Haj Saeedi Sadegh, Minh Lam Nguyen

Abstract
. The Rarita-Schwinger-Seiberg-Witten (RS-SW) equations are defined similarly to the classical Seiberg-Witten equations, where a geometric non-Dirac-type operator replaces the Dirac operator called the Rarita-Schwinger operator. In dimension four, the RS-SW equation was first considered by the second named author [ 15 ] . The variational approach will also give us a three-dimensional version of the equations. The RS-SW equations share some features with the multiple-spinor Seiberg-Witten equations, where the moduli space of solutions could be non-compact. In this note, we prove a compactness theorem regarding the moduli space of solutions of the RS-SW equations defined on 3-manifolds. Keywords: Rarita-Schwinger operator, Seiberg-Witten equations, gauge theory

1.
Introduction

Let
Y
be a closed Riemannian 3-manifold with fixed spin structure
\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}
with
W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}
denoting the spinor bundle. We denote by
D
the Dirac operator on
W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}
and denote by
D^{T}
the twisted Dirac operator on the twisted vector bundle
W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\otimes TY
, where
TY
is equipped with the Levi-Civita connection. The bundle of 3/2-spinors,
W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}
, is given as the kernel of the Clifford multiplication
morphism
\gamma:W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\otimes TY\to W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}
. We have an orthogonal decomposition
of vector bundles
W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\otimes TY=W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\oplus W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}
, with respect to which the twisted Dirac operator has the following matrix form:

D^{T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}D&P^{*}\\
\hline\cr P&Q\end{array}\right)

where
P:\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}})\to\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\otimes TY)
is the
Penrose operator
and
Q:\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}})\to\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}})

is the
Rarita-Schwinger operator
. Similar to Dirac operators, the operator
Q
is a first-order elliptic differential operator that is essentially self-adjoint. The bundle of
3/2
-spinors is not a Clifford module so the Rarita-Schwinger operator is not a Dirac-type operator.

For the gauge-theoretic purposes, fix a
U(1)
-line bundle
\mathscr{L}
over
Y
and denote by
\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})
its affine space of all
U(1)
-connections.
For any
A\in\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})
, one then obtains the twisted Rarita-Scwhinger operator

Q_{A}:\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})\to\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L}).

The Rarita-Schwinger-Seiberg-Witten equation (RS-SW) is the following system
for the pairs
(A,\phi)\in\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})
:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}Q_{A}\phi=0\\
F_{A}=\mu(\phi)\end{cases}

where
F_{A}
is curvature of the connection
A
, and
\mu
is the quadratic function that gives the traceless part of
\phi\phi^{*}
. Fix
A_{0}
to be the referenced
U(1)
-connection in
\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})
. The solutions of this equation correspond to critical points of the modified Chern-Simons-Dirac functional

(1.2)

\mathcal{L}^{RS}(A,\phi)=-\int_{Y}(A-A_{0})\wedge(F_{A}+F_{A_{0}})+\frac{1}{2}\int_{Y}\langle Q_{A}\phi,\phi\rangle

where the Dirac operator is replaced by the Rarita-Schwinger operator.

A solution
(A,\phi)
of (
1.1
) is called
reducible
if
\phi=0
. By blowing up along the locus of the reducible solutions
[
13
]
, we then obtain the blown-up equations

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{4}}=1\\
Q_{A}\psi=0\\
\epsilon^{2}F_{A}=\mu(\psi)\end{cases}

for triples
(A,\psi,\epsilon)\in\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})\times(0,\infty)
. In this paper, we will study the moduli space of solutions of (
1.3
) up to gauge transformations.

There have been several generalizations to the classical Seiberg-Witten equations, one of which is the
multiple-spinor Seiberg-Witten equations
. The four-dimensional version of the equations was first considered in
[
6
]
and later on was systematically studied by Taubes in
[
20
]
. Haydys and Walpuski
[
10
]
and several other people also study the three-dimensional variant of the multiple-spinor Seiberg-Witten equations. Broadly speaking, each variant of the multiple-spinor Seiberg-Witten equations associates with a choice of a compact Lie group
G
equipped with a quaternionic representation
G\to Sp(S)
, where
Sp(S)
is the group of quaternion-linear isometries on a quaternionic vector space
S
. For example, when
G=U(1)
, then one obtains the classical Seiberg-Witten equations. Note that Dirac (or Dirac-type) operators are among the key ingredients in defining these equations.

One could consider (
1.1
) as a kind of generalization to the classical Seiberg-Witten equations where the Dirac operator is replaced with the Rarita-Schwinger operator. The Rarita-Schwinger operator was first defined by Rarita and Schwinger in 1941 to study wave functions in supergravity and superstring theory
[
16
]
. It is studied extensively in theoretic Physics. In contrast to its Dirac cousin, the Rarita-Schwinger operator has not been studied as much in Mathematics. However, there are some recent interests in the applications of the Rarita-Schwinger operator in geometry and topology by several people, see
[
22
]
,
[
12
]
and
[
4
]
.

1.1.
Main results

Similar to some other variants of the generalized Seiberg-Witten equations, the RS-SW equations could have non-compact moduli space of solutions. In dimension four, the non-compactness of the moduli space of the RS-SW equations is exhibited by some topological condition on the manifold via
Pin(2)-
equivariant degree theory
[
15
]
. In dimension three, to the best of our knowledge, there is no such analogous statement. As a result, one must take on a more analytical approach to understand the behavior of solutions of the three-dimensional RS-SW equations.

Since the quadratic map
\mu
in the curvature equation of the system fails to be proper, we do not have an a priori universal bound on the
3/2-
spinors. However, if a hypothetical universal bound does exist for the
3/2-
spinor solutions, then roughly speaking, via integration-by-parts argument, we should expect that the moduli space should be (sequentially) compact in some appropriate sense. Note that the first equation in the system is homogeneous. This suggests that we should re-scale the equations so that, at the very least, we have a uniform finite norm of the
3/2-
spinor solutions at the expense of introducing an extra unknown to the system. Hence, the hope is that some sort of integration-by-parts argument will also apply to us.

With that being said, regarding the moduli space of the RS-SW equations, the main featured theorem of the paper is the following.

Theorem 1.1

(cf. Theorem
3.1
, Theorem
6.1
)
.

Fix a Riemannian metric
g
on a smooth, oriented, closed three-dimensional manifold
Y
.

(1)

Let
\{(A_{n},\psi_{n},\epsilon_{n})\}\subset\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})\times(0,\infty)
be a sequence of solutions of the blown-up RS-SW equations (cf. (
3.2
)). There exists a constant
c
depending only on the Riemannian metric
g
on
Y
,
A_{0}
such that if
\limsup\epsilon_{n}>c
, then after passing through a subsequence and up to gauge transformations
(A_{n},\psi_{n},\epsilon_{n})
converges to a solution
(A,\psi,\epsilon)
in the
C^{\infty}
topology.

(2)

Let
\{(A_{n},\psi_{n},\epsilon_{n})\}\subset\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})\times(0,\infty)
be a sequence of solutions of (
3.2
) where
\{F_{A_{n}}\}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{6}-
norm. If
\limsup\epsilon_{n}=0
, then

(a)

There is a closed nowhere-dense subset
Z\subset Y
, a connection
A\in\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L}|_{Y\setminus Z})
and a
3/2-
spinor
\psi\in\Gamma(Y\setminus Z,W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})
such that
(A,\psi,0)
solves (
3.2
). Furthermore, after to passing through a subsequence,
|\psi_{n}|
converges to
|\psi|
in

.

(b)

On

converges weakly to

converges weakly to
\psi

L^{2}_{2,loc}
.

As we vary the Riemannian metric on the three-manifold, a version of Theorem
1.1
is still true. Specifically, we have

Theorem 1.2

(cf. Theorem
6.2
)
.

Denote
\mathfrak{M}

. Let
\{g_{n}\}

Remark 1.3
.

One should compare our main theorem (cf. Theorem
1.1
) with the compactness results of Haydys-Walpuski in
[
10
]
, and of Taubes in
[
20
]
. However, there are two notable differences in our statement. Firstly, we have to blow up our equations by rescaling the
3/2-
spinor unknown by the reciprocal of its global
L^{4}-
norm. Secondly, we have to impose a universal bound on the curvatures of the connections. These two differences will addressed in a later subsection. It would be interesting to know if one has an analogous result without assuming the universal bound on the curvatures.

Follow
[
10
]
, we call the potential limiting solutions that appear in the moduli space of the RS-SW equations
3/2-
Fueter sections
(cf. Definition
6.4
). It turns out that the existence of
3/2-
Fueter sections is an obstruction for the moduli space to be compact.

Corollary 1.4

(cf. Corollary
6.5
)
.

Let
g\in\mathfrak{M}
. If there is no
g-3/2-
Fueter section, then the charged-
k
moduli space (cf. Definition
6.3
) is always compact on some neighborhood of
g
in
\mathfrak{M}
.

Remark 1.5
.

In the multiple-monopole Seiberg-Witten equations setting, the limiting objects of the associated moduli space correspond to solutions of a certain non-linear Dirac operator defined on a hyper-Kähler fiber bundle over the three-manifold
[
9
]
. We suspect that an analogous correspondence also holds for our
3/2-
Fueter sections. In particular, we believe that the
3/2-
Fueter sections should appear as solutions to a certain non-linear Rarita-Schwinger operator defined on some hyper-Kähler fiber bundle over
Y
. This circle of ideas will be addressed elsewhere in our future work.

Remark 1.6
.

The existence of Fueter sections (or
\mathbb{Z}/2-
harmonic spinors) have been shown by Doan-Walpuski in
[
7
]
. To the best of our knowledge, there is no such analogous statement for the
3/2-
Fueter sections.

Lastly, just as the study of Rarita-Schwinger fields is intimately tied with the deformation problem of Einstein metrics, the non-existence of
g-3/2-
Fueter sections gives us some interesting information about the Einstein geometry of the underlying three-manifold. Recall that to define the RS-SW equations, besides picking a Riemannian metric
g
, we also have to pick a
\text{spin}^{c}
structure.

Corollary 1.7

(cf. Corollary
2.22
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}
be a
\text{spin}^{c}
structure on
Y
such that
c_{1}(\mathfrak{s}_{1/2})
is torsion in
H^{2}(Y;\mathbb{Z})
. Let
g
be a Riemannian metric on
Y
. If there is no
g-3/2-
Fueter section associated to the RS-SW equations corresponding to
g
and
\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}
, then

(1)

The charged moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{k}(g)
is compact for some charge
k
.

(2)

Either

is not an Einstein metric with non-negative scalar curvature or
(A,\psi)
is never a solution where
A
is a flat
U(1)-
connection on
det\,(\mathfrak{s}_{1/2})
.

1.2.
Outline of the argument

As a convention, all geodesic balls we consider throughout this paper have radius
r\in(0,\ r_{0}]
, where
r_{0}
is the injective radius of
Y
that is taken to be much lesser than 1.
Sobolev completions of the space of connections and spinors play an essential role in the analysis of various mathematical gauge-theoretic elliptic PDEs, so they will be of importance in our setup. Firstly, we describe how these spaces are defined in the setting of the RS-SW equations. We use the notation
L^{p}_{k}
to describe the space of
L^{p}-
integrable objects with their derivatives up to order
k
.

Fix a referenced
U(1)-
connection
A_{0}
on a complex line bundle
\mathscr{L}
over
Y
. Every other connection
A
can be written as
A=A_{0}+a
, where
a\in\Gamma(Y,T^{*}Y\otimes i\mathbb{R}):=i\Omega^{1}(Y)
. Denote
\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})
by the space of all
U(1)-
connections on
\mathscr{L}
. Then
\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})
can be viewed as an affine space over
i\Omega^{1}(Y)
. The
L^{p}_{k}-
Sobolev completion of
\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})
is taken to be the Sobolev completion of
i\Omega^{1}(Y)
with respect to the fixed referenced connection
A_{0}
. In particular,
\left\lVert A\right\rVert^{p}_{L^{p}_{k}}
is taken to by
\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left\lVert\nabla^{\otimes j}_{A_{0}}a\right\rVert^{p}_{L^{p}}
. The topology on
\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})
induced by the
L^{p}_{k}-
norm does not change as one changes the referenced connection.

The
3/2-
spinors are sections of the bundle
T^{*}Y\otimes W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L}
. A covariant derivative for the
3/2-
spinors is constructed from the Levi-Civita connection and a covariant derivative
\nabla_{A}
, where
A\in\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})
. We consider the
L^{p}_{k}-
Sobolev completion of the space of
3/2-
spinors by using
\nabla_{A_{0}}
. That is,
\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{p}_{L^{p}_{k}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\lVert\nabla^{\otimes j}_{A_{0}}\psi\right\rVert^{p}_{L^{p}}
. Once again, changing the fixed referenced connection does change the topology induced by the Sobolev norm on
\Gamma(T^{*}Y\otimes W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})
.

With the notations explained, we give an outline for the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Following
[
13
, Chapter 6]
, and similar to
[
10
]
, we study a blow-up version of the RS-SW equations. The first part of the theorem (cf. Theorem
3.1
) follows from a combination of the Weitzenböck formula of the Rarita-Schwinger operator (cf. Lemma
2.14
or Lemma
3.2
) and Green’s integration by parts formula (cf. Lemma
3.6
). This is a standard technique that is also used to prove similar compactness results for the multiple-spinor Seiberg-Witten equations
[
10
]
and the equations of flat
PSL(2,\mathbb{C})-
connections
[
18
]
. However, there is one notable technical difference that we now describe. Unlike the Dirac operator, the square of the Rarita-Schwinger operator contains an additional second-order term contributed by the
Penrose operator

P_{A}
(a more detailed definition is given in Subsection 2.1). As a result, in our version of Green’s integration by parts formula, there is an appearance of
\left\lVert P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
which we have to control. Essentially, we can control the divergence of
\psi
by the curvature
F_{A}
(cf. Lemma
3.5
) via Uhlenbeck’s gauge fixing lemma (cf. Lemma
3.4
) at the expense of
\psi
having unit
L^{4}-
norm. In short, the main reason to use the
L^{4}-
norm has to do with the requirement for higher regularity conditions due to the appearance of the Penrose operator in the Weitzenböck formula. Thus in our set-up, we blow up the equations differently compared to other variants of generalized Seiberg-Witten equations
[
10
]
by re-scaling the
3/2-
spinor by the reciprocal of its global
L^{4}-
norm. Since the
L^{4}-
norm is continuous with respect to the topology of the configuration space, this is enough for the blow-up construction.

The second part of the theorem (cf. Theorem
6.1
) is more involved. We prove that, for a solution
(A,\psi,\epsilon)
of the blown-up RS-SW equations (
3.2
), we can uniformly control the
L^{2}_{2,A}-
norm of
\psi
on a geodesic ball
B(x,r)
on
Y
as long as
r
is less than the
critical radius
(cf. Definition
4.7
)
\rho(x)
at
x
. To control
\rho
, we use a
frequency function

N(r)
, which measures how much
\psi
vanishes near
x
. Roughly speaking, if the vanishing order of
\psi
near
x
is not too large, then we always have a lower bound for the critical radius. Hence by Uhlenbeck’s local slice theorem (cf. Lemma
4.1
), connections can be put in appropriate gauge-slice so that we can establish convergence away from the vanishing set of the
3/2-
spinors.

The frequency function is an important tool for studying the nodal set of eigenfunctions and the growth rate of solutions of elliptic PDEs. It was first developed by Almgren
[
2
]
and Agmon
[
1
]
. The gauge-theoretic version of the frequency function was first considered by Taubes
[
18
]
and then Haydys-Walpuski
[
10
]
. In spirit, we follow closely the approach of
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
10
]
in our setup with a technical difference. For us to obtain a uniform control of the
L^{2}_{2,A}-
norm of the rescaled
3/2-
spinor solution
\psi
on
B(x,r)
and a control the critical radius
\rho(x)
, we must establish some local estimates in terms of
\epsilon
for
\psi
. Just as in the case of the first part of the main theorem, to achieve this, we have to rely on the control of divergence of
\psi
by the curvature
F_{A}
. Therefore, it seems natural for us to impose a universal for bound for the curvatures
F_{A}
and study the behavior of such a sequence of solutions.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

In this section, we briefly survey the current literature about the Rarita-Schwinger operator. A motivation to why we study the RS-SW equations as a generalization of the classical Seiberg-Witten equations is also given. Although the main analytical problem of the RS-SW equations is similar to other variants of generalized Seiberg-Witten equations, some of the stark differences are addressed. The main theorem (cf. Theorem
1.1
) regarding the compactness phenomenon of the moduli space of the RS-SW equations is stated. Lastly, we give a brief outline of the strategy employed in proving the main theorem and discuss the motivation of the approach we take.

In Section 2, we present some preliminary material about the construction of Rarita-Schwinger operators. A general definition of the Rarita-Schwinger operator on a Clifford module is given in Subsection 2.1 (cf. Definition
2.1
). In Subsection 2.2, we construct the Rarita-Schwinger operator associated with a
\text{spin}^{c}
structure on a three or four-dimensional manifold. Various Weitzenböck-type formulas associated with the Rarita-Schwinger operators will be derived in Section 2 (cf. Lemma
2.9
, Lemma
2.14
). These formulas play an important role in many computations and estimates which are employed throughout the paper. Although the results of the paper mainly concern the three-dimensional setting, we think that it is also appropriate to include the four-dimensional construction so that we have the terminologies to discuss our future works in the discussion section. Having established the background, in Subsection 2.3, we define the three-dimensional RS-SW equations (cf. (
2.2
), (
3.1
)).

The proof of the main theorem (cf. Theorem
1.1
) is divided into two parts. In Section 3, we give proof of the first part of our main theorem (cf. Theorem
3.1
). The rest of the paper, namely Section 4–6, will be devoted to the proof of the second part of the main theorem (cf. Theorem
6.1
). The technical tool in the analysis of PDEs we use to prove the second part of the main theorem is the
frequency function
method. To apply this method effectively to our gauge-theoretic equations, we must exhibit some sort of control on the
3/2-
spinors by the curvatures. The main content of Section 4 is to establish this control. In section 5, we define our gauge-theoretic frequency function (cf. Definition
5.1
) associated with the RS-SW equations (cf. (
2.2
), (
3.1
)). Various necessary analyses of our frequency function will be established to set up for Section 6 (cf. Proposition
5.14
, Corollary
5.11
, Corollary
5.13
, Proposition
5.16
). In Section 6, we give a proof of the second part of our main theorem (cf. Theorem
6.1
).

Section 7 is reserved for the discussion of some of the conjectural pictures regarding the RS-SW equations. Several of the future works and problems will also be stated in this section.

2.
The equations

2.1.
Rarita-Schwinger operator on a Clifford module

Suppose
M
is a smooth, oriented, closed Riemannian manifold of dimension
n
and
S
is a Clifford module over
M
. Let
\nabla
be a compatible connection on
S\to M
, i.e., it is compatible with a fixed hermitian metric on
S
and the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla^{LC}
of
M
associated with some fixed Riemannian metric
g
on
M
(e.g, see
[
17
]
or
[
5
]
). Together with the Clifford multiplication
\gamma
, we have
D=\gamma\circ\nabla:\Gamma(S)\to\Gamma(S)
, which is called the
Dirac operator
of
S
. When
n
is even, there is a chirality of
S
induced by
\gamma
extended to top complexified volume form
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}
of
M
. Then one has the following orthogonal decomposition
S=S^{+}\oplus S^{-}
, where
S^{\pm}
corresponds to the
\pm 1-
eigenspace of
\gamma(\omega_{\mathbb{C}})
. With respect to the above decomposition, we have

D=\begin{pmatrix}0&D^{-}\\
D^{+}&0\end{pmatrix},\text{i.e,}\quad\quad\quad D^{\pm}:\Gamma(S^{\pm})\to\Gamma(S^{\mp}).

Consider
S\otimes TM
. One can show that
S\otimes TM
is also a Clifford module over
M
. The Clifford multiplication on such bundle is defined by tensoring
\gamma
with the identity on
TM
. We are also going to use
\gamma
to refer to this new Clifford multiplication. A compatible connection on
S\otimes TM
is given by
\nabla:=\nabla\otimes 1+1\otimes\nabla^{LC}
. Then one obtains another Dirac operator
D^{T}:\Gamma(S\otimes TM)\to\Gamma(S\otimes TM)
. Again, when
n
is even,
D^{T}
exchanges the chirality of
S\otimes TM
,

D=\begin{pmatrix}0&D^{T-}\\
D^{T+}&0\end{pmatrix},\text{i.e,}\quad\quad\quad D^{T\pm}:\Gamma(S^{\pm}\otimes TM)\to\Gamma(S^{\mp}\otimes TM).

We want to think of
\gamma
as follows. It is a linear map
\gamma:TM\otimes S\to S
. Denote
ker\,\gamma:=S_{3/2}
, this is a sub-bundle of
TM\otimes S
. One can show that we have the following orthogonal decomposition
TM\otimes S\cong S_{3/2}\oplus\iota(S)
, where
\iota:S\to S\otimes T^{*}M\cong S\otimes TM
is an embedding given by

\iota(\phi)(v)=-\frac{1}{n}\gamma(v\otimes\phi)=-\frac{1}{n}\gamma(v)\phi,\quad\quad\phi\in\Gamma(S),v\in\Gamma(TM).

Let
\pi
be the orthogonal projection from
TM\otimes S
onto
S_{3/2}
. With this orthogonal projection, we have the
Penrose operator

P=\pi\circ\nabla
, and another first order operator
Q=\pi\circ D^{T}|_{S_{3/2}}
. In fact, with respect to the orthogonal decomposition
TM\otimes S\cong S_{3/2}\oplus\iota(S)
, one can write
D^{T}
in matrix from as following

\displaystyle D^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{2-n}{n}\iota D\iota^{-1}&2\iota P^{*}\\
\frac{2}{n}P\iota^{-1}&Q\end{pmatrix}.

Definition 2.1
.

Q:\Gamma(S_{3/2})\to\Gamma(S_{3/2})
is called the
Rarita-Schwinger operator
of a Clifford module
S
over

.

Definition 2.2
.

\psi\in\Gamma(S_{3/2})
is called a

if and only if
Q\psi=0
and

. Since

,
\psi
is a Rarita-Schwinger field if and only if
\psi\in ker\,Q
and divergence-free. Equivalently, one can view Rarita-Schwinger field

as a harmonic

\gamma(\psi)=0
.

Remark 2.3
.

One can check that an explicit formula of the projection

is given by
\pi=1-\iota\circ\gamma
.

Remark 2.4
.

Just like

,

is a first-order elliptic operator that is formally self-adjoint, see

3

.

Remark 2.5
.

When

also exchanges the chirality of

. We put in the superscript decorations

Remark 2.6

Since

Remark 2.7
.

Via the canonical isomorphism induced by

, we have
\psi\in\Gamma(TM\otimes S)\cong\Gamma(T^{*}M\otimes S)
. Thus, we can view

.

Next, we establish some Weitzenböck-type formulas related to the Rarita-Schwinger operator
Q
. Consider the covariant exterior derivative
d^{\nabla}_{k}:\Lambda^{k}T^{*}M\otimes S\to\Lambda^{k+1}T^{*}M\otimes S
. Let
\pi_{k}:\Lambda^{k}T^{*}M\otimes S\to\Lambda^{k-1}T^{*}Y\otimes S
be a map defined (locally) by

\pi_{k}(\psi)(v_{1},\cdots,v_{k-1})=\sum_{j}\gamma(e_{j})\psi(e_{j},v_{1},\cdots,v_{k-1}).

As a convention, we set
\pi_{0}=0
. Note that
\pi_{1}(\psi)=\gamma(\psi)
. With the maps
\pi_{k}
and
d^{\nabla}_{k}
, we define
D^{k,T}=\pi_{k+1}d^{\nabla}_{k}+d^{\nabla}_{k-1}\pi_{k}:\Lambda^{k}T^{*}M\otimes S\to\Lambda^{k}T^{*}M\otimes S
, which is called the
higher-spin Dirac operator
. For example, consider
D^{0,T}\equiv D
and
D^{1,T}\equiv D^{T}
. These are the Dirac operators on
S
and
S\otimes TM
that we defined above, respectively.

Lemma 2.8
.

We have the following formulas

(1)

D^{T}\iota+\iota D=\displaystyle\frac{2}{n}d^{\nabla}
.

(2)

D^{T}d^{\nabla}_{0}-d^{\nabla}_{0}D=\displaystyle\pi_{2}(F^{\nabla})
, where
F^{\nabla}
is the curvature of the connection
\nabla
on a Clifford module
S
.

Proof.

Let
\{e_{j}\}
be a local orthonormal frame on
M
and
\phi\in\Gamma(S)
. We have

D^{T}\iota(\phi)(e_{j})=\sum_{i}\gamma(e_{i})\nabla_{e_{i}}\left(-\frac{1}{n}\gamma(e_{j})\phi\right)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\gamma(e_{i})\gamma(e_{j})\nabla_{e_{i}}\phi.

On the other hand,

\iota(D\phi)(e_{j})=-\frac{1}{n}\gamma(e_{j})D\phi=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\gamma(e_{j})\gamma(e_{i})\nabla_{e_{i}}\phi.

Thus
D^{T}\iota(\phi)(e_{j})+\iota(D\phi)(e_{j})=\displaystyle-\frac{2}{n}\nabla_{e_{j}}\phi
. This proves the first part. To show the second part, we appeal to the definition of the higher-spin Dirac operator described above. We have

\displaystyle D^{T}d^{\nabla}_{0}-d^{\nabla}_{0}D

\displaystyle=(\pi_{2}d^{\nabla}_{1}+d^{\nabla}_{0}\pi_{1})d^{\nabla}_{0}-d^{\nabla}_{0}D

\displaystyle=\pi_{2}d^{\nabla}_{1}d^{\nabla}_{0}+d^{\nabla}_{0}D-d^{\nabla}_{0}D=\pi_{2}F^{\nabla}

This is exactly the formula in the second part.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

We have the following formulas

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{2(2-n)}{n^{2}}PD+\frac{2}{n}QP=\frac{2}{n}\pi\pi_{2}(F^{\nabla})
.

(2)

\displaystyle Q^{2}+\frac{4}{n}PP^{*}=\nabla^{*}\nabla+\frac{s}{4}+\pi(\gamma\otimes F^{\nabla})
, where
s
is the scalar curvature of
g
and
\gamma\otimes F^{\nabla}
is understood as the Clifford contraction of the twisting curvature
F^{\nabla}
on the Clifford module
S
.

Proof.

We apply
D^{T}
to both sides of Lemma
2.8
part one and part two to obtain

\displaystyle(D^{T})^{2}\iota

\displaystyle=-D^{T}\iota D+\frac{2}{n}D^{T}d^{\nabla}=\left(\iota D-\frac{2}{n}d^{\nabla}\right)D+\frac{2}{n}D^{T}d^{\nabla}

(2.1)

\displaystyle=\iota D^{2}+\frac{2}{n}(D^{T}d^{\nabla}-d^{\nabla}D)=\iota D^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\pi_{2}(F^{\nabla})

Note that in the matrix form of
D^{T}
, we have

(D^{T})^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\frac{2-n}{n}\right)^{2}\iota D^{2}\iota^{-1}+\frac{4}{n}\iota P^{*}P\iota^{-1}&\frac{2(2-n)}{n}\iota DP^{*}+2\iota P^{*}Q\\
\frac{2(2-n)}{n^{2}}PD\iota^{-1}+\frac{2}{n}QP\iota^{-1}&\frac{4}{n}PP^{*}+Q^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

So the lower left block of
(D^{T})^{2}
is determined exactly by
\pi(D^{T})^{2}\iota
. Thus, when combined with (
2.1
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{2(2-n)}{n^{2}}PD+\frac{2}{n}QP=\frac{2}{n}\pi\pi_{2}(F^{\nabla}).

This proves the first part. To see the second part, recall the Weitzenböck formula for
(D^{T})^{2}
,

(D^{T})^{2}=\nabla^{*}\nabla+\frac{s}{4}+\gamma\otimes F^{\nabla}.

One simply applies
\pi
to both sides of the above formula and arrives at the Weitzenböck-type formula for the Rarita-Schwinger operator
Q
.
∎

Remark 2.10

2.8

Lemma 2.11
.

If
\nabla
is a flat connection of

, then

preserves the orthogonal decomposition

.

Proof.

If

\frac{2(2-n)}{n^{2}}PD+\frac{2}{n}QP=0\Rightarrow\frac{2(2-n)}{n^{2}}DP^{*}+\frac{2}{n}P^{*}Q=0.

2.3.
The Seiberg-Witten equations for
3/2-
spinors

Let
\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}
be a
\text{spin}^{c}
structure on a
3
-manifold
Y
. We consider the following system

(2.2)

\begin{cases}Q_{A}\psi=0,\\
F_{A}=\gamma^{-1}(\mu(\psi)).\end{cases}

The unknowns of the system (
2.2
) are pairs
(A,\psi)
, where
2A\in\mathcal{A}(det\,\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}),
which is the space of all unitary connections on the determinant line bundle, and
\psi\in\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}})
. We have that the Clifford multiplication
\gamma:i\Lambda^{2}T^{*}Y\to i\mathfrak{su}(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}})
is an isometry and
\mu
is a quadratic map defined on
\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}})
as following

\psi\in\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}})\subset\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\otimes TY)\cong\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\otimes T^{*}Y).

This means that we can view
\psi\in\Gamma(End\,(TY,W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}))
. Then
\psi\psi^{*}
as composition of operators is a section of the bundle
End\,(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}})
that is self-adjoint. And
\mu(\psi)
is taken to be the traceless part of
\psi\psi^{*}
so that
\mu(\psi)\in i\mathfrak{su}(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}})
. Unlike the quadratic map defined in the classical Seiberg-Witten equations, this map
\mu
is not proper, i.e., there might be a non-zero
\psi
such that
\mu(\psi)=0
. This feature is one of the many difficulties in analyzing solutions of (
2.2
).

The equations (
2.2
) fall under the umbrella of abelian gauge theory. The gauge group is given by
\mathcal{G}=Maps(Y\to U(1))
and it acts on the configuration space
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}(det\,\mathfrak{s}_{1/2})\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}})
of (
2.2
) by the following: For
u\in\mathcal{G},2A\in\mathcal{A},\psi\in\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}})
,

u\cdot A=A-u^{-1}du,\quad u\cdot\psi=u\psi.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the defining map of (
2.2
),

\mathcal{F}(A,\psi)=(Q_{A}\psi,F_{A}-\gamma^{-1}(\mu(\psi))).

\mathcal{F}
can be shown to be
\mathcal{G}-
equivariant. As a result, solutions of (
2.2
) are preserved under
\mathcal{G}-
symmetry.

It turns out that (
2.2
) can be viewed as the equations of motion of the following modified Chern-Simon-Dirac functional that was introduced in the Introduction section. Let
A_{0}
be a fixed referenced connection. We recall the definition of the functional (cf. (
1.2
)).

\mathcal{L}^{RS}(A,\psi)=-\int_{Y}(A-A_{0})\wedge(F_{A}+F_{A_{0}})+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{Y}\langle Q_{A}\psi,\psi\rangle.

Consider a small change in
(A,\psi)
given by
(A+tb,\psi+t\phi)
, where
t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
. Note that

\displaystyle(A+tb-A_{0})\wedge(F_{A}+tdb+F_{A_{0}})

\displaystyle=(A-A_{0})\wedge(F_{A}+F_{A_{0}})+

\displaystyle+t(A-A_{0})\wedge db+tb\wedge(F_{A}+F_{A_{0}})+O(t^{2})

\displaystyle\langle Q_{A+tb}(\psi+t\phi),\psi+t\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Q_{A}\psi,\psi\rangle+

\displaystyle+2t\,Re\langle\phi,Q_{A}\psi\rangle+t\langle b\cdot\psi,\psi\rangle+O(t^{2}).

As a result, by Stoke’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle d_{(A,\psi)}\mathcal{L}^{RS}(b,\phi)

\displaystyle=\int_{Y}b\wedge F_{A}+\int_{Y}\langle b\cdot\psi,\psi\rangle+\int_{Y}\,Re\langle\phi,Q_{A}\psi\rangle

Recall that
b
can be viewed as a traceless skew-adjoint endomorphism of the spinor bundle, so it can be easily seen that
\langle b\cdot\psi,\psi\rangle=\langle b,\gamma^{-1}(\mu(\psi))\rangle
. Since
\star_{3}^{2}=1
, we can rewrite the above formula as

\displaystyle d_{(A,\psi)}\mathcal{L}^{RS}(b,\phi)=\langle b,\star_{3}F_{A}+\gamma^{-1}(\mu(\psi))\rangle_{L^{2}}+Re\langle\phi,Q_{A}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Thus, the gradient of
\mathcal{L}^{RS}
at
(A,\psi)
is given as

grad\,\mathcal{L}^{RS}(A,\psi)=(\star_{3}F_{A}+\gamma^{-1}(\mu(\psi)),Q_{A}\psi).

Since
\gamma(\star_{3}\alpha)=-\gamma(\alpha)
for a
1-
form
\alpha
, the critical points of
\mathcal{L}^{RS}
correspond to the solutions of (
2.2
). We summarize the above discussion in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.18
.

The RS-SW equations (
2.2
) give the minimizing condition of the modified Chern-Simon-Dirac functional associated with the Rarita-Schwinger operator

\mathcal{L}^{RS}(A,\psi)=-\int_{Y}(A-A_{0})\wedge(F_{A}+F_{A_{0}})+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{Y}\langle Q_{A}\psi,\psi\rangle.

Proposition 2.19
.

Suppose
g
is an Einstein metric on
Y
such that it has non-negative scalar curvature. Let
(A,\psi)
be a solution to (
2.2
). If
A
is flat, correspondingly,
c_{1}(\mathfrak{s}_{1/2})
is torsion in
H^{2}(Y;\mathbb{Z})
, then
\psi
must be a Rarita-Schwinger field.

Proof.

By Corollary
2.15
part one, since
A
is flat, we have
\displaystyle Q_{A}P_{A}=\frac{1}{3}P_{A}D_{A}
. We then apply
Q_{A}
to both sides of this identity and apply Corollary
2.15
part one again to obtain
Q_{A}^{2}P_{A}=\frac{1}{3}Q_{A}P_{A}D_{A}=\frac{1}{9}P_{A}D_{A}^{2}=\frac{1}{9}P_{A}\left(\nabla^{*}_{A}\nabla_{A}+\frac{s}{4}\right)
.
One can easily check that the Penrose operator commutes with the Böchner Laplacian
\nabla^{*}_{A}\nabla_{A}
. As a result, we see that
Q_{A}^{2}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{9}\left(\nabla^{*}_{A}\nabla_{A}+\frac{s}{4}\right)
on
im\,P_{A}
. Furthermore, by taking the adjoint of both sides of
\displaystyle Q_{A}P_{A}=\frac{1}{3}P_{A}D_{A}
, one also has
\displaystyle P_{A}^{*}Q_{A}=\frac{1}{3}D_{A}P_{A}^{*}
. Hence, a similar argument as in the proof of Lemma
2.11
will give us

Q_{A}:ker\,P_{A}^{*}\to ker\,P_{A}^{*},\quad Q_{A}:im\,P_{A}\to im\,P_{A}.

Thus if we decompose
\psi=\psi_{1}\oplus\psi_{2}
, where
\psi_{1}\in ker\,P^{*}_{A}
and
\psi_{2}\in im\,P_{A}
, then
Q_{A}\psi=0\Rightarrow Q_{A}\psi_{1}=0,\quad Q_{A}\psi_{2}=0
. We focus on the
\psi_{2}-
part. Because we also have
Q_{A}^{2}\psi_{2}=0
, we are going to consider an
L^{2}-
paring with
\psi_{2}
and apply the Weitzenböck formula of
Q_{A}^{2}
restricted to
im\,P_{A}
to expand

0=\langle Q_{A}^{2}\psi_{2},\psi_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\frac{1}{9}|\nabla_{A}\psi_{2}|^{2}+\frac{s}{36}|\psi_{2}|^{2}\geq\frac{s}{36}|\psi_{2}|^{2}.

As a result, if
s\geq 0
, then
\psi_{2}\equiv 0
. Therefore,
\psi=\psi_{1}
must be divergence-free. In other words,
\psi
is a Rarita-Schwinger field as claimed.
∎

3.
The first compactness theorem

Recall that the RS-SW equations in dimension 3 read as follows

(3.1)

\begin{cases}Q_{A}\phi=0,\\
F_{A}=\mu(\phi).\end{cases}

Here
\phi\in\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})
,
A
is a
U(1)-
connection on the line bundle
\mathscr{L}\to Y
and
\mu
is the quadratic map defined on
\Gamma(T^{*}Y\otimes W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})=\Gamma(Hom\,(TY,W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L}))
given by
\mu(\phi)=\phi\phi^{*}-\dfrac{1}{2}|\phi|^{2}\cdot 1
. Modding out by the gauge group
\mathcal{G}=Maps(Y\to U(1))
, the moduli space of solutions of (
3.1
) could be non-compact. That is, there could be a sequence of solutions
\{(A_{n},\phi_{n})\}
to (
3.1
) such that
\left\lVert\phi_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{4}}\to\infty
. For this reason, we blow up our equation by introducing an extra unknown to the equations. Suppose
(A,\phi)
is a solution to (
3.1
), denote

\psi=\epsilon\phi,\,\text{where }\,\epsilon=\frac{1}{\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{4}}}.

Note that then
\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{4}}=1
, and
Q_{A}\psi=\epsilon Q_{A}\phi=0
. Furthermore,

F_{A}=\mu(\phi)=\mu(\psi/\epsilon)\Rightarrow\epsilon^{2}F_{A}=\mu(\psi).

As a result, with the new unknown
\epsilon\in(0,\infty)
, the equations (
3.1
) now read as following

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{4}}=1,\\
Q_{A}\psi=0,\\
\epsilon^{2}F_{A}=\mu(\psi).\end{cases}

Solutions of (

) are triples of the form
(A,\psi,\epsilon)\in\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})\times(0,\infty)
. Similar to (

), the equations (
3.2
) is
\mathcal{G}-
invariant. Of course, when we fix a referenced connection
A_{0}
on
\mathscr{L}
, any other

, where

. Then (

) can also be equivalently re-written as

(3.3)

\begin{cases}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{4}}=1\\
Q_{A_{0}}\psi+\pi(a\cdot\psi)=0\\
\epsilon^{2}(F_{A_{0}}+da)=\mu(\psi).\end{cases}

The first compactness theorem we will prove regarding the moduli space of solutions of (

) is the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\{(A_{n},\psi_{n},\epsilon_{n})\}\subset\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})\times(0,\infty)
be a sequence of solutions of (

on

A_{0}
such that if

converges to a solution

in the

topology.

We begin the proof of Theorem
3.1
. Firstly, recall that we have the Weitzenböck formula for the twisted Rarita-Schwinger operator.

Lemma 3.2

(
Weitzenböck formula of

)
.

For all
(A,\psi)\in\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})

Q^{2}_{A}\psi+\dfrac{4}{3}P_{A}P_{A}^{*}\psi=\nabla^{*}_{A}\nabla_{A}\psi+\dfrac{s}{4}\psi+\pi(F_{A}\psi)-\pi(1\otimes Ric)\psi,

where

is the twisted Penrose operator,
s
is the scalar curvature of the Riemannian metric
g
on
Y
, and
Ric
is the associated Ricci curvature. ∎

Since
P_{A}^{*}=(\pi\circ\nabla_{A})^{*}=\nabla_{A}^{*}\circ\pi^{*}
, for any
\psi\in\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})
, we have
\pi^{*}\psi=\pi(\psi)=\psi
. As a result,
P_{A}^{*}\psi=\nabla^{*}_{A}\psi
. With respect to the referenced connection
A_{0}
, we re-write the divergence of
\psi
as
\nabla^{*}_{A}\psi=\nabla^{*}_{A_{0}}\psi+a^{*}\psi
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
(a,\psi)\in i\Omega^{1}(Y)\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})
, we have
\left\lVert a^{*}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\sqrt{3}\left\lVert a\cdot\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
.

Proof.

Note that
a^{*}\psi
is defined as following: Since
\psi
can be viewed as a spinor-valued
1-
form, in local frame
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
, it can be written as
\psi=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\psi_{i}\otimes e^{i}
, where
\psi_{i}\in\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})
. Then simply,
a^{*}\psi=\sum_{i=1}^{3}a(e_{i})\psi_{i}
. By triangle inequality and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

|a^{*}\psi|^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}|a(e_{i})\psi_{i}|\right)^{2}\leq 3\sum_{i=1}^{3}|a(e_{i})|^{2}|\psi_{i}|^{2}\leq 3|a|^{2}|\psi|^{2}.

At the same time, recall that the Clifford multiplication by
a\in i\Omega^{1}(Y)
on
\Gamma(T^{*}Y\otimes W_{\mathfrak{s}_{1/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})
gives us a skew-adjoint linear map. Thus,

|a\cdot\psi|^{2}=\langle a\cdot\psi,a\cdot\psi\rangle=-\langle a^{2}\cdot\psi,\psi\rangle=|a|^{2}|\psi|^{2}.

As a result, we have

\left\lVert a^{*}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}=\int_{Y}|a^{*}\psi|^{2}\leq 3\int_{Y}|a\cdot\psi|^{2}=3\left\lVert a\cdot\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}.

After taking the square root, we have the desired estimate.
∎

The following
U(1)-

Lemma 3.4

(

)

Suppose

Lemma 3.5

For any

Firstly, based on the above observation and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{Y}|P^{*}_{A}\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{Y}|\nabla^{*}_{A}\psi|^{2}\leq\int_{Y}(|\nabla^{*}_{A_{0}}\psi|+|a^{*}\psi|)^{2}\leq 2\int_{Y}(|\nabla^{*}_{A_{0}}\psi|^{2}+|a^{*}\psi|^{2}).

Since

\displaystyle\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\Delta|\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle Q_{A}^{2}\psi+\dfrac{4}{3}P_{A}P^{*}_{A}\psi-\dfrac{s}{4}\psi-\pi(F_{A}\psi)+\pi(1\otimes Ric)\psi,\psi\right\rangle-|\nabla_{A}\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{Y}|\nabla_{A}\psi|^{2}

4.

(A,\psi)\in\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})
, we have

-\dfrac{1}{3}P_{A}D_{A}\psi+Q_{A}P_{A}\psi=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(Ric-\frac{s}{3}\right)\psi+\pi\pi_{2}F_{A}\psi.

Here
\pi_{2}
is the map that takes spinor-valued
2-
forms to spinor-valued
1-
forms. ∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
(A,\psi,\epsilon)\in V_{k_{0}}(B(x,r))\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L}|_{B(x,r)})\times(0,\infty)
that solves (
3.2
), we have

\left\lVert P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}(B(x,r))}\leq c\left(\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}+\int_{B(x,r)}|F_{A}|^{2}\cdot|\psi|^{2}+k_{0}\sqrt{k_{0}^{2}+1}+k_{0}+1\right),

where
B(x,r)
is a small geodesic ball on
Y
and
k_{0}
is as in Theorem
4.1
.

Proof.

We take the adjoint of the Weitzenböck-type formula in Lemma
4.3
to obtain

-\dfrac{1}{3}D_{A}P_{A}^{*}\psi=\frac{1}{2}\left(Ric-\frac{s}{3}\right)^{*}\psi+F_{A}\pi_{2}^{*}\pi(\psi)=\frac{1}{2}\left(Ric-\frac{s}{3}\right)^{*}\psi+F_{A}\pi_{2}^{*}\psi.

If we write
A=A_{0}+a
, then we can re-arrange the above identity as

(4.4)

D_{A_{0}}P^{*}_{A}\psi=-a\cdot P^{*}_{A}\psi-\frac{3}{2}\left(Ric-\frac{s}{3}\right)^{*}\psi-3F_{A}\pi^{*}\psi.

Combine
4.4
with the elliptic estimate and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}(B(x,r))}\leq c\left(\left\lVert D_{A_{0}}P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}+\left\lVert P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}\right)

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq c\left(\left\lVert a\cdot P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int_{B(x,r)}\left|\left(Ric-\frac{s}{3}\right)^{*}\psi\right|^{2}+\int_{B(x,r)}|F_{A}\pi_{2}^{*}\psi|^{2}+k_{0}+1\right).

It is not hard to see that

\displaystyle\int_{B(x,r)}\left|\left(Ric-\frac{s}{3}\right)^{*}\psi\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\int_{B(x,r)}|\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{B(x,r)}|F_{A}\pi^{*}_{2}\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\int_{B(x,r)}|F_{A}|^{2}\cdot|\psi|^{2}.

Thus, (
4
) can be further estimated by

(4.6)

\left\lVert P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}}\leq c\left(\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}+\int_{B(x,r)}|F_{A}|^{2}|\psi|^{2}+\left\lVert a\cdot P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+k_{0}+1\right).

It remains for us to control
\left\lVert a\cdot P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}
. The Sobolev multiplication theorem gives us

\left\lVert a\cdot P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq c_{3}\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}}\left\lVert P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{4}}.

And the local slice theorem (Theorem
4.1
) already tells us that

\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}}\leq c_{4}\int_{B(x,r)}|F_{A}|^{2}\leq c_{4}k_{0}.

Thus, we further control
\left\lVert a\cdot P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}
by controlling
\left\lVert P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{4}}
. To achieve this, firstly, by applying the Minkowski inequality, we have

(4.7)

\int_{B(x,r)}|P^{*}_{A}\psi|^{4}\leq\int_{B(x,r)}|\nabla^{*}_{A_{0}}\psi+a\cdot\psi|^{4}\leq 2^{3}\int_{B(x,r)}(|\nabla^{*}_{A_{0}}\psi|^{4}+|a\cdot\psi|^{4}).

Since
\nabla^{*}_{A_{0}}
is a first-order differential operator, it extends to a bounded operator from
L^{4}_{1}\to L^{4}
. Hence, the elliptic estimate obtains for us

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla^{*}_{A_{0}}\psi\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{5}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{4}_{1}(B(x,r))}

\displaystyle\leq c_{6}(\left\lVert Q_{A_{0}}\psi\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{4}}+\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{4}})\leq c_{7}(\left\lVert Q_{A_{0}}\psi\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{4}}+1).

Since
(A,\psi,\epsilon)
is a solution of (
3.2
), the above estimate implies
\left\lVert\nabla^{*}_{A_{0}}\psi\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{4}}\leq c_{8}(\left\lVert a\cdot\psi\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{4}}+1)
. Hence, combine with (
4.7
), we get that

(4.8)

\int_{B(x,r)}|P^{*}_{A}\psi|^{4}\leq c_{9}\left(\int_{B(x,r)}|a\cdot\psi|^{4}+1\right).

By the Sobolev multiplication theorem, for any
p>12
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert a\cdot\psi\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{10}\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{2}_{1}}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{p}}

(4.9)

\displaystyle\leq c_{11}\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{2}_{1}}\cdot\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{\infty}(B(x,r))}\leq c_{12}\left\lVert F_{A}\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}.

The last estimate of (
4
) is given by Uhlenbeck’s local slice theorem (Theorem
4.1
) and Lemma
4.2
(cf. (
4.1
)). We use (
4
) to further estimate the RHS of (
4.8
) as following

(4.10)

\int_{B(x,r)}|P^{*}_{A}\psi|^{4}\leq c_{13}\left(\left\lVert F_{A}\right\rVert^{4}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}+1\right)=c_{14}(k_{0}^{2}+1).

Combine (
4.10
) and the Sobolev multiplication estimate for
\left\lVert a\cdot P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}
earlier, we have
\left\lVert a\cdot P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}

\leq c_{3}c_{4}c_{15}k_{0}\sqrt{k_{0}^{2}+1}
. Apply this estimate to the RHS of (
4.6
), we immediately obtain our desired estimate.
∎

We need one more useful identity before getting to the main point of this section, which is the curvature-control of
\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{A}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{loc}}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\psi\in\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})
such that
Q_{A}\psi=0
and and arbitrary
f\in C^{\infty}(Y)
. Then one has the following

\nabla^{*}_{A}\nabla_{A}(f\psi)=-\frac{s}{4}f\psi-\pi(F_{A}(f\psi))+\pi(1\otimes Ric)f\psi+\frac{4}{3}fP_{A}P^{*}_{A}\psi-2\pi\nabla^{A}_{\nabla f}\psi+(\Delta f)\psi.

Proof.

Firstly, it is not difficult to see that

\displaystyle D^{T\,2}_{A}(f\psi)

\displaystyle=D^{T}_{A}(D^{T}_{A}(f\psi))=D^{T}_{A}(fD^{T}_{A}\psi+df\cdot\psi)

\displaystyle=fD^{T\,2}_{A}\psi+df\cdot D^{T}_{A}\psi-df\cdot D^{T}_{A}\psi-2\nabla^{A}_{\nabla f}\psi+(\Delta f)\psi

\displaystyle=fD^{T\,2}_{A}\psi-2\nabla^{A}_{\nabla f}\psi+(\Delta f)\psi.

Thus, when applying the orthogonal projection
\pi
onto the
3/2-
spinor bundle to both sides of the above identity, we have

Q^{2}_{A}(f\psi)+\frac{4}{3}P_{A}P^{*}_{A}(f\psi)=f\left(Q^{2}_{A}\psi+\frac{4}{3}P_{A}P^{*}_{A}\psi\right)-2\pi\nabla^{A}_{\nabla f}\psi+(\Delta f)\psi.

Remember that
Q_{A}\psi=0
so that we can re-arrange the above formula as

(4.11)

Q^{2}_{A}(f\psi)=-\frac{4}{3}P_{A}P^{*}_{A}(f\psi)+\frac{4}{3}fP_{A}P^{*}_{A}\psi-2\pi\nabla^{A}_{\nabla f}\psi+(\Delta f)\psi.

On the other hand, the Weitzenböck formula for
Q^{2}_{A}
(Lemma
3.2
) gives us

(4.12)

Q^{2}_{A}(f\psi)=-\frac{4}{3}P_{A}P^{*}_{A}(f\psi)+\nabla^{*}_{A}\nabla_{A}(f\psi)+\frac{s}{4}f\psi+\pi(F_{A}(f\psi))-\pi(1\otimes Ric)f\psi.

Compare (
4.11
) and (
4.12
), we have our formula.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove the main result of this section.

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose
(A,\psi,\epsilon)\in V_{k_{0}}(B(x,r))\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L}|_{B(x,r)})\times(0,\infty)
. For any
\delta\in(0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle r^{1/2}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{A}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B(x,r(1-\delta)))}\lesssim_{\delta}\,\,\,

\displaystyle r^{-3/2}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}+r^{-1/2}\left\lVert\nabla_{A}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}

\displaystyle+r^{1/2}\left\lVert F_{A}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B(x,r))}+O(1).

Here,
B(x,r)
is a small geodesic ball around
x
on
Y
, and
k_{0}
is a constant as in Theorem
4.1
.

Proof.

Since the statement is scale-invariant, without loss of generality, we shall work on the geodesic ball
B(x,1)
of radius
1
. Let
\chi
be a cut-off function such that
supp\,\chi\subseteq B(x,1-\delta/2)
and
\chi
equals to
1
on
B(x,1-\delta)
. By integration by parts, we have

(4.13)

\int|\nabla^{2}_{A}(\chi\psi)|^{2}\lesssim\int\left(|\nabla^{*}_{A}\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)|^{2}+|F_{A}|\cdot|\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)|^{2}+|F_{A}|\cdot|\psi|\cdot|\nabla^{2}_{A}(\chi\psi)|\right).

Using the identity in Lemma
4.5
, we can estimate

(4.14)

\int|\nabla^{*}_{A}\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)|^{2}\lesssim_{\delta}\int\left(|\psi|^{2}+|F_{A}|^{2}|\chi\psi|^{2}+|\nabla_{A}\psi|^{2}+|P_{A}P^{*}_{A}\psi|^{2}\right).

Keeping the first three terms of the RHS of (
4.14
) unchanged, we estimate the RHS of (
4.14
) further as follows. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

(4.15)

\int|P_{A}P_{A}^{*}\psi|^{2}\leq\int|\nabla_{A_{0}}P^{*}_{A}\psi+a\cdot P^{*}_{A}\psi|^{2}\lesssim\int(|\nabla_{A_{0}}P^{*}_{A}\psi|^{2}+|a\cdot P^{*}_{A}\psi|^{2}).

Since
\nabla_{A_{0}}:L^{2}_{1}\to L^{2}
is bounded,
\left\lVert\nabla_{A_{0}}P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\lesssim\left\lVert P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}}
. From this, by Lemma
4.4
, we then have

\left\lVert P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}(B(x,1))}\leq c\left(\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,1))}+\int_{B(x,1)}|F_{A}|^{2}\cdot|\psi|^{2}+k_{0}\sqrt{k_{0}^{2}+1}+k_{0}+1\right).

Combine with the fact that somewhere in the proof of Lemma
4.4
, we also established that
\left\lVert a\cdot P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}

\leq c_{3}c_{4}c_{15}k_{0}\sqrt{k_{0}^{2}+1}
to obtain a further estimate of (
4.15
)

\int|P_{A}P^{*}_{A}\psi|^{2}\lesssim c\left(\int_{B(x,1)}|\psi|^{2}+\int_{B(x,1)}|F_{A}|^{2}|\psi|^{2}+2k_{0}\sqrt{k_{0}^{2}+1}+k_{0}+1\right).

This means that we have the following estimate for (
4.14
)

\int|\nabla^{*}_{A}\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)|^{2}\lesssim_{\delta}\int\left(|\psi|^{2}+|F_{A}|^{2}|\psi|^{2}+|\nabla_{A}\psi|^{2}\right)+O(1).

In turn, we obtain an estimate for the LHS of (
4.13
)

\displaystyle\int|\nabla^{2}_{A}(\chi\psi)|^{2}\lesssim_{\delta}\,\,\,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\nabla_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int|F_{A}|^{2}|\psi|^{2}

(4.16)

\displaystyle+\int\left(|F_{A}|\cdot|\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)|^{2}+|F_{A}|\cdot|\chi\psi|\cdot|\nabla^{2}_{A}(\chi\psi)|\right)+O(1).

Keeping the first three terms of the RHS of (
4
) the same, we give estimates for the last two terms in the integrand of the RHS of (
4
). By the Peter-Paul version of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for all
\tau>0
, we have

\displaystyle\int|F_{A}|\cdot|\chi\psi|\cdot|\nabla^{2}_{A}(\chi\psi)|

\displaystyle\leq\int\left(\tau^{-1}|F_{A}|^{2}\cdot|\chi\psi|^{2}+\tau|\nabla^{2}_{A}(\chi\psi)|^{2}\right)

(4.17)

\displaystyle\leq\tau^{-1}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert F_{A}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\tau\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{A}(\chi\psi)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}.

If
\tau
is small enough, the second term of the RHS of (
4
) can be moved to the LHS of (
4
). Now for the fourth term in the integrand of the RHS of (
4
), we use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\int|F_{A}|\cdot|\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int|F_{A}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int|\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)|^{4}\right)^{1/2}

(4.18)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert F_{A}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\cdot\left\lVert\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{4}}\leq\sqrt{k_{0}}\cdot\left\lVert\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{4}}.

By the Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation inequality

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{4}}\lesssim\left\lVert\nabla f\right\rVert^{3/4}_{L^{2}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{1/4}_{L^{2}}

the second factor on the RHS of (
4
) is estimated by

(4.19)

\left\lVert\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{4}}\lesssim\left\lVert\nabla_{A}^{2}(\chi\psi)\right\rVert^{3/2}_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)\right\rVert^{1/2}_{L^{2}}.

Finally, by the Peter-Paul version of Young’s inequality, for all
u,v\geq 0
,
\tau>0
, and
1/p+1/q=1
with
p,q>1
,

uv\leq\frac{1}{p}\tau^{p}u^{p}+\frac{1}{q}\tau^{-q}v^{q}

applied in the situation when
u=\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{A}(\chi\psi)\right\rVert^{3/2}_{L^{2}}
,
v=\left\lVert\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)\right\rVert^{1/2}_{L^{2}}
, and
(p,q)=(4/3,4)
, we have

(4.20)

\left\lVert\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{4}}\lesssim\frac{3}{4}\tau^{4/3}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{A}(\chi\psi)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\tau^{-4}\left\lVert\nabla_{A}(\chi\psi)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}.

When
\tau
is sufficiently small, the first term on the RHS of (
4.20
) can be moved to the LHS of (
4
). Therefore, with all of the above estimates combined, we obtained the desired estimate of the proposition.
∎

Definition 4.7
.

The
critical radius
\rho(x)
of a connection

is

\rho(x)=\sup\left\{r\in(0,r_{0}]:r^{1/2}\left\lVert F_{A}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}\leq 1\right\}.

We combine Lemma
4.2
(cf. (
4.1
), (
4.2
), (
4.3
)) with Proposition
4.6
to immediate obtain the following corollary.

.

For all
(A,\psi,\epsilon)\in V_{k_{0}}(B(x,\rho(x)/2))\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L}|_{B(x,\rho(x)/2)})\times(0,\infty)
that are solutions of (
3.2
), we have

\rho(x)^{1/2}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{A}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B(x,\rho(x)/2))}=O(1).

Here, as before,
B(x,\rho(x)/2)
is a geodesic ball around

on

, and

4.1
. ∎

5.
A frequency function for the RS-SW equations

We continue with our analysis of the sequence of solutions
(A_{n},\psi_{n},\epsilon_{n})
of (
3.2
) when
\limsup\epsilon_{n}=0
. Roughly speaking, we expect that up to a subsequence and gauge transformations, such a sequence of solutions of (
3.2
) will converge to a solution away from a certain singular set on
Y
. To proceed, we first define a
frequency function
for the RS-SW equations. The notion of frequency function was first introduced by Almgren in the study of critical sets of elliptic partial differential equations
[
2
]
. It was also used to analyze the moduli space of solutions of the multiple-spinor Seiberg-Witten equations. In fact, the frequency function for the RS-SW equations is an adaptation of the ones used in
[
10
]
and
[
18
]
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
x
be any point on
Y
and
0<r\leq r_{0}<<1
where
r_{0}
is the injective radius of
Y
. For any
(A,\psi,\epsilon)\in\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L})\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})\times(0,\infty)
, we define

H_{x}(r)=\int_{B(x,r)}\left(|\nabla_{A}\psi|^{2}+\epsilon^{-2}|\mu(\psi)|^{2}-\dfrac{4}{3}\langle P_{A}P^{*}_{A}\psi,\psi\rangle\right),\quad h_{x}(r)=\int_{\partial B(x,r)}|\psi|^{2}.

The
frequency function

N_{x}:(0,r_{0}]\to(0,r_{0}]
of (
3.2
) is given by

N_{x}(r)=\dfrac{rH_{x}(r)}{h_{x}(r)}.

When the context is clear, sometimes we will ignore the subscript of the base point
x
and write
N,H,h
instead.

For the rest of this section, we will assume that
x
is a fixed base-point on
Y
, and
(A,\psi,\epsilon)\in V_{k_{0}}(B(x,r))\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L}|_{B(x,r)})\times(0,\infty)
is a solution of (
3.2
), where
k_{0}
is the constant that appears in Theorem
4.1
. We will study the dependence of
N
on the base point (cf. Proposition
5.14
). Furthermore, we will establish the following important properties of
N
:

(1)

N
is almost monotone in
r
. (cf. Corollary
5.11
)

(2)

N
controls the growth of
h
. (cf. Corollary
5.13
)

(3)

|\psi|(x)
controls
N
. (cf. Proposition
5.16
)

Versions of Proposition
5.14
, Corollary
5.11
, Corollary
5.13
, and Proposition
5.16
appear in the original context of
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
. They also appear in the gauge-theoretic setting of
[
10
]
,
[
18
]
and
[
20
]
. The novelty of these statements in the current context is the appearance of the Penrose operator in our definition of the frequency function. Once a control of
\langle P_{A}P^{*}_{A}\psi,\psi\rangle
is well understood, we demonstrate that standard technique can also be applied to deduce the expected properties of
N
listed above. Closely following
[
10
]
, we include the proofs of these statements in the next few subsections.

Before we get into the analysis of the frequency function
N
, we need to establish some preliminary observations about
3/2-
spinors on the boundary of a three-ball. These facts might be well-known, but we will nevertheless record them in the following subsection for the sake of self-containment and clarity.

5.1.
3/2-
spinors on the boundary

Consider the punctured ball
\dot{B}(x,r)
by removing the base-point
x
. It is foliated by the surfaces
\partial B(x,r)
with the normal vector field
\partial_{r}
in the radial direction. The restriction of the spinor bundle on
\dot{B}(x,r)
to the boundary is exactly the spinor bundle on
\partial B(x,r)
, which we will denote by
S
and
\tilde{S}
, respectively. Let
\tilde{\gamma},\tilde{\nabla},
and
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}
be the Clifford multiplication, compatible connection, and the Dirac operator associated to the bundle of spinor bundle on
\partial B(x,r)
. For any vector
v\in T\partial B(x,r)
, we have

(5.1)

\gamma(v)=-\gamma(\partial_{r})\tilde{\gamma}(v),\quad\nabla_{v}=\tilde{\nabla}_{v}+\dfrac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{2r}\tilde{\gamma}(v),\quad\mathbf{D}=\gamma(\partial_{r})\left(\nabla_{r}+\dfrac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}-\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\right).

Denote
\mathbf{D}^{TB}
by the Dirac operator associated with the bundle of spinor-valued
1-
forms. Let
\mathbf{P}
be the Penrose operator and
\mathbf{Q}
be the Rarita-Schwinger operator on
B(x,r)
. We also consider
\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B}
,
\mathbf{\tilde{P}}
,
\mathbf{\tilde{Q}}
to be the Dirac operators on
T\partial B(x,r)\otimes\tilde{S}
, the Penrose operator and the Rarita-Schwinger operator on
\partial B(x,r)
, respectively. Finally, we write
W
,
\tilde{W}
as the
3/2-
spinor bundles on
B(x,r)
and
\partial B(x,r)
, respectively. The objective of this subsection is to derive an analog of the last formula of (
5.1
) for the Rarita-Schwinger operators.

Suppose
\Phi\in\Gamma(\tilde{W})
is a
3/2-
spinor on
\partial B(x,r)
. Naturally, we can view it as a
3/2-
spinor on
B(x,r)
but restricted to the boundary. Note that then we have the following orthogonal decomposition
\Gamma(W|_{\partial B(x,r)})=\Gamma(\tilde{W})\oplus\Gamma(\tilde{W}^{\perp})
. First, we would like to describe
\Gamma(\tilde{W}^{\perp})
explicitly.

Let
\{\partial_{r},e_{1},e_{2}\}
be orthonormal vector fields on
B(x,r)
where
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
are orthonormal vector fields on
\partial B(x,r)
. Then in local normal coordinate, we can write
\phi
as
\Phi=\phi_{1}\otimes e_{1}+\phi_{2}\otimes e_{2}
, where
\phi_{i}\in\Gamma(\tilde{S})
. By virtue of
\Phi
being a
3/2-
spinor, it is not hard to see that
\phi_{1}=-\partial_{r}\cdot\phi_{2}
. As a result,

\Gamma(\tilde{W})=\{-\partial_{r}\cdot\phi\otimes e_{1}+\phi\otimes e_{2}:\phi\in\Gamma(\tilde{S})\}.

Suppose
\Psi
is any
3/2-
spinor of
B(x,r)
restricted to the boundary such that
\Psi\in\Gamma(\tilde{W}^{\perp})
. In local normal coordinate, we write
\Psi=\psi\otimes\partial_{r}+\psi_{1}\otimes e_{1}+\psi_{2}\otimes e_{2}
, where
\psi,\psi_{i}\in\Gamma(\tilde{S})
. Then for any
\phi\in\Gamma(\tilde{S})
, we must have

\langle-\partial_{r}\cdot\phi\otimes e_{1}+\psi\otimes e_{2},\psi\otimes\partial_{r}+\psi_{1}\otimes e_{1}+\psi_{2}\otimes e_{2}\rangle=0.

Since the Clifford multiplication is skew-adjoint, the above is equivalent to

\langle\phi,\partial_{r}\cdot\psi_{1}+\psi_{2}\rangle=0,\quad\quad\forall\phi\in\Gamma(\tilde{S}).

As a result, we must have
\psi_{1}=\partial_{r}\cdot\psi_{2}
. Furthermore, since
\Psi
is a
3/2-
spinor, we have

\partial_{r}\cdot\psi+e_{1}\cdot\psi_{1}+e_{2}\cdot\psi_{2}=0.

Combine with the fact that
\psi_{1}=\partial_{r}\cdot\psi_{2}\equiv\partial_{r}\cdot\psi
, we obtain
\psi=2\tilde{\gamma}(e_{2})\psi
. Therefore,

\Gamma(\tilde{W}^{\perp})=\{2\tilde{\gamma}(e_{2})\psi\otimes\partial_{r}+\partial_{r}\cdot\psi\otimes e_{1}+\psi\otimes e_{2}:\psi\in\Gamma(\tilde{S})\}.

By a change of variable, setting
\psi\to e_{2}\cdot\psi
, then in local normal coordinate any
3/2-
spinor
\Psi\in\Gamma(\tilde{W}^{\perp})
can be written as

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=-2\partial_{r}\cdot\psi\otimes\partial_{r}+e_{1}\cdot\psi\otimes e_{1}+e_{2}\cdot\psi\otimes e_{2}

\displaystyle=-3\iota(\psi)-3\partial_{r}\cdot\psi\otimes\partial_{r}.

Therefore, if we define the embedding
\hat{\iota}:\tilde{S}\to TB(x,r)\otimes S|_{\partial B(x,r)}=T\partial B(x,r)\otimes\tilde{S}\oplus(\mathbb{C}\partial_{r}\otimes\tilde{S})
to be
\hat{\iota}(\psi)=-3\iota(\psi)-3\partial_{r}\cdot\psi\otimes\partial_{r}
, then
\Gamma(\tilde{W}^{\perp})=\hat{\iota}(\tilde{S})
. We summarize the above discussion in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
W
,
\tilde{W}
be the
3/2-
spinor bundle on
B(x,r)
and
\partial B(x,r)
, respectively. Let
\tilde{S}
be the spinor bundle on
\partial B(x,r)
induced by restricting
S
to the boundary. We have the following orthogonal decomposition

W|_{\partial B(x,r)}=\tilde{W}\oplus\hat{\iota}(\tilde{S})
. ∎

Define
L:\tilde{S}\to\tilde{S}\otimes\mathbb{C}\partial_{r}
to be a vector bundle isomorphism (in fact, it is also an isometry) given by
L(\phi)=\phi\otimes\partial_{r}
. Note that then
\hat{\iota}(\tilde{S})\oplus\tilde{W}\cong(\tilde{S}\otimes\mathbb{C}\partial_{r})\oplus\tilde{W}
via

T=\begin{pmatrix}L\hat{\iota}^{-1}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

This fact will be useful later when we perform a coordinate change on the matrix representation of
\mathbf{D}^{TB}
.

Recall that the Levi-Civita connection on the boundary is related to the Levi-Civita connection on the whole
B(x,r)
as follows

\nabla^{LC}_{v}\bullet=\tilde{\nabla}^{LC}_{v}\bullet+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle v,\bullet\rangle\partial_{r},\quad\quad\forall v\in\Gamma(T\partial B(x,r)).

With this fact, we compute
\mathbf{D}^{TB}|_{\partial B(x,r)}
in matrix form with respect to the orthogonal decomposition
TB(x,r)\otimes S|_{\partial B(x,r)}=(\tilde{S}\otimes\mathbb{C}\partial_{r})\oplus T\partial B(x,r)\otimes\tilde{S}
. By definition of the Dirac operator, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{TB}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{2}e_{i}\cdot(\nabla_{e_{i}}\otimes 1+1\otimes\nabla^{LC}_{e_{i}})=\mathbf{D}\otimes 1+\sum_{i=1}^{2}e_{i}\cdot\otimes\nabla^{LC}_{e_{i}},\quad\quad\text{ where }e_{0}\equiv\partial_{r}

\displaystyle=\partial_{r}\cdot\left(\nabla_{r}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2}}}{r}-\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\right)\otimes 1+\partial_{r}\cdot\otimes\nabla^{LC}_{r}-\sum_{i=1}^{2}\partial_{r}\cdot e_{i}\tilde{\cdot}\otimes\left(\tilde{\nabla}^{LC}_{e_{i}}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle e_{i},\bullet\rangle\partial_{r}\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{r}\cdot\left\{\nabla_{r}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}-\left(\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\otimes 1+\sum_{i=1}^{2}e_{i}\tilde{\cdot}\otimes\tilde{\nabla}^{LC}_{e_{i}}\right)-\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\sum_{i=1}^{2}e_{i}\tilde{\cdot}\otimes\langle e_{i},\cdot\rangle\partial_{r}\right\}.

Hence, when we apply
\mathbf{D}^{TB}
to
\psi\otimes\partial_{r}
, we obtain

\mathbf{D}^{TB}(\psi\otimes\partial_{r})=\partial_{r}\cdot\left(\nabla_{r}(\psi\otimes\partial_{r})+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\psi\otimes\partial_{r}-\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\psi\otimes\partial_{r}\right).

On the other hand, if we apply
\mathbf{D}^{TB}
to
\psi_{1}\otimes e_{i}
, we get

\mathbf{D}^{TB}(\psi_{i}\otimes e_{i})=\partial_{r}\cdot\left(\nabla_{r}(\psi_{i}\otimes e_{i})+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\psi_{i}\otimes e_{i}-\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B}(\psi_{i}\otimes e_{i})-\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}e_{i}\tilde{\cdot}\psi_{i}\otimes\partial_{r}\right).

As a result, we obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 5.3
.

With respect to the orthogonal decomposition
TB(x,r)\otimes S=(S\otimes\mathbb{C}\partial_{r})\oplus T\partial B(x,r)\otimes S
,
\mathbf{D}^{TB}
has the following matrix form

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{TB}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{r}\cdot(\nabla_{r}+e^{O(r^{2})}/r-\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\otimes 1)&e^{O(r^{2})}/r\,\tilde{\gamma}\otimes\partial_{r}\\
0&\partial_{r}\cdot(\nabla_{r}+e^{O(r^{2})}/r-\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B})\end{pmatrix}.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
\Phi
be a
3/2-
spinor on
B(x,r)
. The restriction of
\Phi
to
\partial B(x,r)
can be written as
\Phi=\hat{\iota}(\phi)+\Psi
, where
\Psi\in\Gamma(\tilde{W})
and
\phi\in\Gamma(\tilde{S})
. Then
\mathbf{Q}\Phi|_{\partial B(x,r)}
equals to

\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\iota}L^{-1}\partial_{r}\cdot\left(\nabla_{r}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}-\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\otimes 1\right)L\hat{\iota}^{-1}&0\\
0&\tilde{\pi}\partial_{r}\cdot\left(\nabla_{r}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2}}}{r}-\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B}\right)\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\iota}(\phi)\\
\Psi\end{pmatrix},

where
\tilde{\pi}
is the orthogonal projection from
T\partial B(x,r)\otimes\tilde{S}
onto the
3/2-
spinor bundle
\tilde{W}
.

Proof.

Note that
\tilde{\gamma}\equiv 0
when restricted to
\Gamma(\tilde{W})
. And a change of basis via
T
immediately tells us that the matrix representing
\mathbf{Q}|_{\Gamma(\tilde{W})}
is given by

T^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\tilde{\pi}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{r}\cdot(\nabla_{r}+e^{O(r^{2})}/r-\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\otimes 1)&e^{O(r^{2})}/r\,\tilde{\gamma}\otimes\partial_{r}\\
0&\partial_{r}\cdot(\nabla_{r}+e^{O(r^{2})}/r-\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B})\end{pmatrix}T.

Then, a straightforward calculation yields the result.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

\nabla_{r}
maps
3/2-
spinors to
3/2-
spinors on
\partial B(x,r)
.

Proof.

Let
\Psi
be a
3/2-
spinor on
\partial B(x,r)
. In local normal coordinate, we have

\nabla_{r}\Psi=\nabla_{r}\psi_{1}\otimes e_{1}+\nabla_{r}\psi_{2}\otimes e_{2}.

Applying
\gamma
to both sides of the above identity, we get

\gamma(\nabla_{r}\psi)=\gamma(e_{1})\nabla_{r}\psi_{1}+\gamma(e_{2})\nabla_{r}\psi_{2}.

By the compatibility of the connection with the Clifford multiplication and the fact that we are working with normal coordinates, we can re-write the above identity as
\gamma(\nabla_{r}\psi)=\nabla_{r}(\gamma(e_{1})\psi_{1})+\nabla_{r}(\gamma(e_{2})\psi_{2})=\nabla_{r}(\gamma(e_{1})\psi_{1}+\gamma(e_{2})\psi_{2})=0
. Therefore,
\nabla_{r}\Psi
is also another
3/2-
spinor as claimed.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

\gamma(\partial_{r})
maps
3/2-
spinors to
3/2-
spinors on
\partial B(x,r)
.

Proof.

Let
\Psi
be
3/2-
spinor on
\partial B(x,r)
. In the local normal coordinate, we have
\psi=\psi_{1}\otimes e_{1}+\psi_{2}\otimes e_{2}
. Thus,
\gamma(\partial_{r})\psi=\gamma(\partial_{r})\psi_{1}\otimes e_{1}+\gamma(\partial_{r})\psi_{2}\otimes e_{2}
. We compute

\displaystyle\gamma(\gamma(\partial_{r})\psi)

\displaystyle=\gamma(e_{1})\gamma(\partial_{r})\psi_{1}+\gamma(e_{2})\gamma(\partial_{r})\psi_{2}

\displaystyle=-\gamma(\partial_{r})\gamma(e_{1})\psi_{1}-\gamma(\partial_{r})\gamma(e_{2})\psi_{2}=-\gamma(\partial_{r})(\gamma(e_{1})\psi_{1}+\gamma(e_{2})\psi_{2}).

Clearly, this implies that
\gamma(\partial_{r})\Psi
must also be a
3/2-
spinor.
∎

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
\Phi
be a harmonic
3/2-
spinor on
B(x,r)
, i.e,
\mathbf{Q}\Phi=0
. Consider the restriction of
\Phi
to
\partial B(x,r)
that is written as
\Phi=\hat{\iota}(\phi)+\Psi
, where
\phi\in\Gamma(\tilde{S})
and
\Psi\in\Gamma(\tilde{W})
. Then we must have

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\phi\otimes\partial_{r}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{r}\phi\otimes\partial_{r}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\phi\otimes\partial_{r}

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{Q}}\Psi

\displaystyle=\nabla_{r}\Psi+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\Psi.

Proof.

(
5.2
) follows directly from Corollary
5.4
. It remains for us to prove (
5.3
). From Corollary
5.4
, Lemma
5.5
and Lemma
5.6
, we have that

\partial_{r}\cdot\nabla_{r}\Psi+\partial_{r}\cdot\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\Psi-\tilde{\pi}(\partial_{r}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B}\Psi)=0.

It is not hard to see that

\tilde{\pi}(\partial_{r}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B}\Psi)=\partial_{r}\cdot\mathbf{\tilde{Q}}\Psi-\tilde{\iota}\tilde{\gamma}(\partial_{r}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B}\Psi)+\partial_{r}\cdot\tilde{\iota}\tilde{\gamma}(\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B}\psi).

To complete the proof, we must calculate the second and third terms on the RHS of the above identity. Note that in local normal coordinate, we have
\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B}\Psi=\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\psi_{1}\otimes e_{1}+\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\psi_{2}\otimes e_{2}
. As a result,

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B}\Psi)

\displaystyle=e_{1}\tilde{\cdot}\,\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\psi_{1}+e_{2}\tilde{\cdot}\,\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\psi_{2}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{\tilde{D}}(e_{1}\tilde{\cdot}\psi_{1})-2\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}\psi_{1}-\mathbf{\tilde{D}}(e_{2}\tilde{\cdot}\psi_{2})-2\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}\psi_{2}=2\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\Psi.

Applying
\partial_{r}\cdot\tilde{\iota}
to both sides of the above equation, we get

\partial_{r}\cdot\tilde{\iota}(\tilde{\gamma}(\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B}\psi))=-\partial_{r}\cdot e_{1}\tilde{\cdot}\,\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\Psi\otimes e_{1}-\partial_{r}\cdot e_{2}\tilde{\cdot}\,\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\Psi\otimes e_{2}.

On the other hand, it is not difficult to see that
\tilde{\gamma}(\partial_{r}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B}\Psi)=-2\partial_{r}\cdot\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\Psi
. Note that
e_{i}\tilde{\cdot}\partial_{r}=-\partial_{r}\cdot e_{i}\tilde{\cdot}
. Then we have

\displaystyle-\tilde{\iota}\tilde{\gamma}(\partial_{r}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{T\partial B}\Psi)

\displaystyle=2\tilde{\iota}(\partial_{r}\cdot\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\Psi)

\displaystyle=-e_{1}\tilde{\cdot}\partial\cdot\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\Psi\otimes e_{1}-e_{2}\tilde{\cdot}\partial\cdot\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\Psi\otimes e_{2}

\displaystyle=\partial_{r}\cdot e_{1}\tilde{\cdot}\,\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\Psi\otimes e_{1}+\partial_{r}\cdot e_{2}\tilde{\cdot}\,\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\Psi\otimes e_{2}.

Thus,
\partial_{r}\cdot\nabla_{r}\Psi+\partial_{r}\cdot\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\Psi-\partial_{r}\cdot\mathbf{\tilde{Q}}\Psi=0
. By applying the Clifford multiplication by
\partial_{r}
to both sides, we obtain (
5.3
) as claimed.
∎

We are now ready to prove the properties of
N
stated at the beginning of the section.

5.2.
Analysis of
N

Note that the first derivative of the frequency function
N(r)
is given by

(5.4)

N^{\prime}(r)=\frac{H(r)}{h(r)}+\frac{rH^{\prime}(r)}{h(r)}-\frac{rh^{\prime}(r)H(r)}{h^{2}(r)}.

To understand the monotonicity of
N
, we need to analyze
H^{\prime},h^{\prime}
further.

Lemma 5.8
.

h(r)
satisfies the following properties:

(5.5)

h^{\prime}(r)=\frac{2h(r)}{r}+\int_{\partial B(x,r)}\partial_{r}|\psi|^{2}+O(r)h(r).

(5.6)

h^{\prime}(r)=\frac{(2+2N(r)+O(r^{2}))h(r)}{r}.

(5.7)

\int_{B(x,r)}|\psi|^{2}\lesssim rh(r).

Proof.

The proof of (
5.5
) is a standard computation in PDE. When the metric is flat, note that a change of variable that turns the circle centered at
x
of radius
r
to a unit concentric circle and the product rule allows us to write

h^{\prime}(r)=\frac{2}{r}\int_{\partial B(x,r)}|\psi|^{2}+\int_{\partial B(x,r)}\partial_{r}|\psi|^{2}.

If the metric is not flat, there is an additional error term given by
O(r)h(r)
. We move on to the proof of (
5.6
) and (
5.7
). Firstly, we make the following claim.

Claim 1:

\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}\lesssim(1+N(r))h(r)
.

Indeed, recall the following fact

(5.8)

\int_{B(x,r)}d(x,\bullet)^{-2}f^{2}\lesssim r^{-1}\int_{\partial B(x,r)}f^{2}+\int_{B(x,r)}|df|^{2}.

We apply (
5.8
) to
f:=|\psi|
to obtain

\displaystyle r^{-2}\int_{B(x,r)}|\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim r^{-1}\int_{\partial B(x,r)}|\psi|^{2}+\int_{B(x,r)}|d|\psi||^{2}

(5.9)

\displaystyle=r^{-1}h(r)+\int_{B(x,r)}|d|\psi||^{2}.

The second term on the RHS of (
5.2
) can be estimated further by Kato’s inequality to arrive at
r^{-2}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}\lesssim rh(r)+\left\lVert\nabla_{A}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}
. As a result, we do have

(5.10)

\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}\lesssim(1+N(r))h(r).

Claim 2:

h^{\prime}(r)>0
.

We apply the integration by parts formula from Lemma
3.6
to
U=B(x,r)
and
f\equiv 1
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B(x,r)}

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{8}{3}\langle P_{A}P^{*}_{A}\psi,\psi\rangle+\frac{s}{2}|\psi|^{2}-2\langle(1\otimes Ric)\psi,\psi\rangle+2\epsilon^{-2}|\mu(\psi)|^{2}+2|\nabla_{A}\psi|^{2}\right)

(5.11)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B(x,r)}\partial_{r}|\psi|^{2}.

The LHS of (
5.2
) can be re-arranged to become
2H(r)+O(1)\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}
. So, we do have

(5.12)

2H(r)+O(1)\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}=\int_{\partial B(x,r)}\partial_{r}|\psi|^{2}.

Now, we combine (
5.12
) with (
5.10
) and (
5.5
) to immediately obtain

h^{\prime}(r)=\frac{(1+O(r^{2}))(2+2N(r))h(r)}{r}.

Since
r\leq r_{0}
, the above implies that
h^{\prime}(r)>0
as claimed. Lastly, (
5.6
) follows directly from the fact that
h^{\prime}(r)>0
. And (
5.6
) can be verified by combining (
5.12
) with (
5.6
) and (
5.5
).
∎

Lemma 5.9
.

H
satisfies the following

(5.13)

H^{\prime}(r)=\frac{H(r)}{r}+\int_{\partial B(x,r)}\left(3|\nabla^{A}_{r}\psi|^{2}+\epsilon^{-2}|i(\partial_{r})\mu(\psi)|^{2}\right)+O((1+N(r))h(r))+O(1).

Proof.

Using Stoke’s theorem and a change of variable that turns the ball centered at
x
of radius
r
to a unit concentric ball, we already have

(5.14)

H^{\prime}(r)=\frac{H(r)}{r}+\int_{\partial B(x,r)}\left(|\nabla_{A}\psi|^{2}+\epsilon^{-2}|\mu(\psi)|^{2}-\dfrac{4}{3}\langle P_{A}P^{*}_{A}\psi,\psi\rangle\right).

It remains for us to match up the second terms of the RHS’s of (
5.14
) and (
5.13
). When restrict
\psi
to the boundary, we write it as
\psi=\hat{\iota}(\phi)+\xi
. In local normal coordinate,
\xi=\xi_{1}\otimes e_{1}+\xi_{2}\otimes e_{2}
, where
\xi_{i}\in\Gamma(\tilde{S})
. Note that for each
e_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle|\nabla_{e_{i}}\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{i}}\phi\otimes\partial_{r}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}e_{i}\tilde{\cdot}\phi\otimes\partial_{r}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\xi_{i}\otimes\partial_{r}+\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{i}}\xi+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{2r}e_{i}\tilde{\cdot}\xi\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{i}}T\psi|^{2}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{i}}\phi\otimes\partial_{r},e_{i}\tilde{\cdot}\phi\otimes\partial_{r}\rangle+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{i}}\xi,e_{i}\tilde{\cdot}\xi\rangle+

\displaystyle+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r^{2}}\langle e_{i}\tilde{\cdot}\phi,\xi_{i}\rangle+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r^{2}}|\xi_{i}|^{2}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{4r^{2}}|\phi|^{2}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{4r^{2}}|\xi|^{2}+\frac{2e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{i}}\phi,\xi_{i}\rangle.

Thus,
|\nabla_{e_{1}}\psi|^{2}+|\nabla_{e_{2}}\psi|^{2}
can be written as

\displaystyle|\tilde{\nabla}T\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\phi,\phi\rangle-\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\mathbf{D}^{TB}\xi,\xi\rangle+\frac{3e^{O(r^{2})}}{2r^{2}}|\xi|^{2}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{2r^{2}}|\phi|^{2}+

\displaystyle+\frac{2e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}\phi,\xi_{1}\rangle+\frac{2e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}\phi,\xi_{2}\rangle.

When integrating the above expression over
\partial B(x,r)
, we can replace the last two terms with

\int_{\partial B(x,r)}\frac{2e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\phi,\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\xi\rangle.

Therefore, combining with Corollary
5.7
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(x,r)}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-|\nabla_{r}\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B(x,r)}|\tilde{\nabla}T\psi|^{2}-\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\nabla_{r}\phi,\phi\rangle-\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\nabla_{r}\xi,\xi\rangle+

(5.15)

\displaystyle+\frac{2e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\phi,\mathbf{\tilde{P}}\xi\rangle-\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{2r^{2}}|\phi|^{2}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{2r^{2}}|\xi|^{2}.

On the other hand, by the Weitzenböck formulae of the Rarita-Schwinger operator and Dirac operator on
\partial B(x,r)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(x,r)}|\tilde{\nabla}T\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B(x,r)}\langle\tilde{\nabla}^{*}\tilde{\nabla}T\psi,T\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B(x,r)}\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\nabla}^{*}\tilde{\nabla}&0\\
0&\tilde{\nabla}^{*}\tilde{\nabla}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\phi\otimes\partial_{r}\\
\xi\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\phi\otimes\partial_{r}\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle.

(5.16)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B(x,r)}|\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\phi|^{2}-\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{2r^{2}}|\phi|^{2}+|\mathbf{\tilde{Q}}\xi|^{2}+2|\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\xi|^{2}-\frac{3e^{O(r^{2})}}{2r^{2}}|\xi|^{2}

Using Corollary
5.7
again, we can expend the first and the third term on the RHS of (
5.16
) and re-arrange to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(x,r)}|\tilde{\nabla}T\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B(x,r)}|\nabla_{r}\phi|^{2}+\frac{2e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\nabla_{r}\phi,\phi\rangle+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{2r^{2}}|\phi|^{2}+

(5.17)

\displaystyle+|\nabla_{r}\xi|^{2}+\frac{2e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\nabla_{r}\xi,\xi\rangle-\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{2r^{2}}|\xi|^{2}+2|\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\xi|^{2}.

Plug in (
5.17
) into the first term on the RHS of (
5.15
), we get

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(x,r)}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-|\nabla_{r}\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B(x,r)}|\nabla_{r}T\psi|^{2}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\nabla_{r}T\psi,T\psi\rangle+

(5.18)

\displaystyle+\frac{2e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\phi,\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\xi\rangle+2|\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\xi|^{2}.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
4.2
, it is not hard to see that

\left\lVert\nabla_{r}T\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial B(x,r))}\lesssim 2\left\lVert T\nabla_{r}\psi\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert^{2}=2\left\lVert\nabla_{r}\psi\right\rVert^{2}+O(1).

Hence, we can re-write (
5.18
) as

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}=\int_{\partial B(x,r)}3|\nabla_{r}\psi|^{2}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\nabla_{r}T\psi,T\psi\rangle+\frac{2e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\phi,\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\xi\rangle+2|\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\xi|^{2}+O(1).

The above immediately implies that
\left\lVert\nabla\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial B(x,r))}-\dfrac{4}{3}\left\lVert\mathbf{P}\psi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial B(x,r))}
is exactly equals to

(5.19)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(x,r)}3|\nabla_{r}\psi|^{2}+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\nabla_{r}T\psi,T\psi\rangle+\frac{2e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\phi,\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\xi\rangle+2|\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\xi|^{2}-\frac{4}{3}|\mathbf{P}^{*}\psi|^{2}+O(1).

Via the change of basis given by
T
, we can think of the norm of the divergence of
\psi
as follows

\displaystyle|\mathbf{P}^{*}\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\langle\tilde{\mathbf{P}}^{*}\xi,\nabla_{r}\phi\rangle+|\tilde{\mathbf{P}}^{*}\xi|^{2}+|\nabla_{r}\phi|^{2}

\displaystyle=-2d\,\langle\tilde{\mathbf{P}}^{*}\xi,\phi\rangle+\langle\nabla_{r}\tilde{\mathbf{P}}^{*}\xi,\phi\rangle+|\tilde{\mathbf{P}}^{*}\xi|^{2}+|\nabla_{r}\phi|^{2}.

As a result, (
5.19
) can be re-written as

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(x,r)}3|\nabla_{r}\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\langle\nabla_{r}T\psi,T\psi\rangle+\left\langle\phi,\frac{2e^{O(r^{2})}}{r}\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\xi+\frac{8}{3}\nabla_{r}\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\xi\right\rangle

(5.20)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{3}|\mathbf{\tilde{P}}^{*}\xi|^{2}-\frac{4}{3}|\nabla_{r}\phi|^{2}+O(1).

But by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}|\tilde{\mathbf{P}}^{*}\xi|^{2}

\displaystyle\left\langle\phi,\frac{8}{3}\nabla_{r}\tilde{\mathbf{P}}^{*}\xi\right\rangle

Part 3.
In this part, we give a proof of Proposition
5.17
.

Proof of Proposition
5.17
We argue by contradiction. Suppose that for any arbitrarily small
\kappa>0
, there is a solution
(A,\psi,\epsilon)\in V_{k_{0},6}\times\Gamma(W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L}|_{B(x,\rho(x))})\times(0,\infty)
such that
N_{x}(50\rho(x))\leq\kappa
but
\rho(x)\leq\kappa
.

Claim 1:
There is an
x^{\prime}\in B(x,2\rho(x))
such that

\rho(x^{\prime})\leq\rho(x),\quad\quad\rho(x^{\prime})\leq 2\min_{y\in B(x^{\prime},\rho(x^{\prime}))}\{\rho(y)\}.

We show the existence of
x^{\prime}
by an inductive argument. The construction is a toss-pick procedure. To get a feel of the construction, we start with some base cases. Let
x_{0}:=x
. Obviously,
\rho(x_{0})=\rho(x)
. If
\rho(x_{0})\leq 2\min\{\rho(y):y\in B(x_{0},\rho(x_{0}))\}
, then we take
x^{\prime}:=x_{0}
. Otherwise, we pick another point
x_{1}\in B(x_{0},\rho(x_{0}))
such that
\rho(x_{1})<\rho(x)/2
. This implies that
\rho(x_{1})<\rho(x)
. Now if
\rho(x_{1})\leq 2\min\{\rho(y):y\in B(x_{1},\rho(x_{1}))\}
, then we take
x^{\prime}:=x_{1}
. Otherwise, we pick an
x_{2}\in B(x_{1},\rho(x_{1}))
such that
\rho(x_{2})<\rho(x_{1})/2<\rho(x)/2^{2}
. This implies that
\rho(x_{2})<\rho(x)
. By repeating this construction, we obtain a sequence
\{x_{n}\}
where
\rho(x_{n})<\rho(x)/2^{n}
. If this sequence is infinite, then
\rho(x_{n})\to 0
as
n
approaches infinity. But this is simply impossible because the function
\rho(\bullet)
is bounded below away from zero for a particular solution
(A,\psi,\epsilon)
. As a result, the construction must stop at some
x_{n}
where we have

\rho(x_{n})\leq\rho(x),\quad\quad\rho(x_{n})\leq 2\min_{y\in B(x_{n},\rho(x_{n}))}\{\rho(y)\}.

Furthermore, we have
x_{n}\in B(x,2\rho(x))
because

d(x_{n},x)\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}d(x_{j+1},x_{j})\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\rho(x_{j})<\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\dfrac{\rho(x)}{2^{j}}<2\rho(x).

Claim 2:
Let
x^{\prime}
be as in Claim 1. For every
y\in B(x^{\prime},\rho(x^{\prime}))
, we have

\rho(y)\lesssim\kappa,\quad\quad N_{y}(\rho(y))\lesssim\kappa.

Note that the function
r\mapsto r^{1/2}\left\lVert F_{A}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B(x,r))}
is monotone. From Claim 1, we also have
\rho(x^{\prime})
small. If
\rho(x^{\prime})\left\lVert F_{A}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x^{\prime},\rho(x^{\prime})))}<1
, then we can add a small emough amount to
\rho(x^{\prime})
so that the newly obtained quantity
r^{\prime}
is still within
(0,r_{0}]
and
r^{\prime}\left\lVert F_{A}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x^{\prime},r^{\prime}))}=1
. This would lead to a contradiction with the definition of the critical radius
\rho(x^{\prime})
. Thus,
\rho(x^{\prime})\left\lVert F_{A}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(B(x^{\prime},\rho(x^{\prime}))}=1
. With this being understood, for any
y\in B(x^{\prime},\rho(x^{\prime}))
, we clearly have
B(x^{\prime},\rho(x^{\prime}))\subset B(y,2\rho(x^{\prime}))
. As a result,

\int_{B(y,2\rho(x^{\prime}))}|F_{A}|^{2}\geq\int_{B(x^{\prime},\rho(x^{\prime}))}|F_{A}|^{2}=\dfrac{1}{\rho(x^{\prime})}>\dfrac{1}{2\rho(x^{\prime})}.

By the monotonicity, the above implies that
\rho(y)<2\rho(x^{\prime})\leq 2\rho(x)\lesssim\kappa
. At the same time, since

, by Corollary
5.11
and Proposition
5.14

, we have

N_{y}(\rho(y))\leq e^{O(\kappa^{2})}N_{y}(25\rho(y))+O(\kappa^{2})\lesssim N_{x}(50\rho(x))+O(\kappa^{2})\lesssim\kappa.

Claim 3:
There exists a finite number of points
\{y_{1},\cdots,y_{n}\}\subset B(x^{\prime},\rho(x^{\prime}))
, where

is large enough independent of
x^{\prime}
such that

From Claim 1, we know that for all
y\in B(x^{\prime},\rho(x^{\prime}))
,
\rho(y)\geq\rho(x^{\prime})/2
. Consider a cover of
B(x^{\prime},\rho(x^{\prime}))

\bigcup_{y\in B(x^{\prime},\rho(x^{\prime}))}B(y,\rho(x^{\prime})/4).

Since
B(x^{\prime},\rho(x^{\prime}))

\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B(y_{i},\rho(x^{\prime})/4),

. Obviously for each

Now, we combine the three claims to get to a contradiction. By Claim 2, Corollary

Thus, from Claim 3, we deduce that

This implies that

6.
The second compactness theorem

In this section, we prove the second compactness theorem regarding the moduli space of solutions of (

3.2

converges to
|\psi|
in

(2)

On

Note that if

\rho(x)\gtrsim\min\{1,|\psi|^{1/\omega}(x)\}.

There are two cases to consider.

Case 1:

In this scenario, there are two sub-cases. If
|\psi|(x)\geq 1

\rho(x)\gtrsim 1

\dfrac{||\psi|(x)-|\psi|(y)|}{d(x,y)^{1/2}}\lesssim|\psi|(x)\leq\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=O(1).

If
|\psi|(x)<1
, then we have
|\psi|(y)\leq|\psi|(x)\lesssim\rho(x)^{\omega}\lesssim d(x,y)^{\omega/2}
. As a result, a similar estimate above yields for us

\dfrac{||\psi|(x)-|\psi|(y)|}{d(x,y)^{\omega/2}}=O(1).

Case 2:

Since we assume

4.6

\displaystyle\dfrac{||\psi|(x)-|\psi|(y)|}{d(x,y)^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert\nabla_{A}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{6}(B_{\rho(x)/2})}\lesssim\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{A}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B_{\rho(x)/2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\rho(x)^{-1/2}\lesssim d(x,y)^{-1/4}.

Hence after re-arranging, we obtain

. This means that we do have a uniform control for

in
C^{0,\alpha}-
topology. By continuity, the nodal set
Z:=|\psi|^{-1}(0)
is closed.

Now we prove the second assertion in the theorem. We prove the weak convergence locally first. Let
x\in Y\setminus Z
. By Proposition
5.15
, after passing through a subsequence, the critical radius
\rho(x_{n})
of
(A_{n},\psi_{n},\epsilon_{n})
is bounded below by a constant
2R
that depends only on
|\psi|(x)
. From the observation above, if necessary, we can make
R
smaller so that
|\psi_{n}|
is bounded away from zero on

. There exists a cover of
B(x,R)
such that on each member of the covering set, we have
L^{2}-
bounds for
F_{A_{n}}
by Proposition
5.22
and Proposition
5.16
. Note that we can take this
L^{2}-
bounds to be the minimum between the constants provided by the aforementioned propositions and the universal bound of the curvatures in the hypothesis. As a result, we can use Proposition
4.6
(or Corollary
4.8
) to get
L^{2}_{2,A_{n}}-
bounds
\psi_{n}
. Putting
A_{n}
inside Uhlenbeck gauge on

, by the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, after passing through a subsequence and re-arranging the index,
(A_{n},\psi_{n})
converges weakly to
(A,\psi)
in
L^{2}_{1}
and
L^{2}_{2}
topology, respectively. A standard argument tells us that we can patch these local gauge transformations together to obtain a global one on
Y\setminus Z
. Note that the limiting solution
(A,\psi)
must satisfy the following degenerate equations,

Q_{A}\psi=0,\quad\quad\quad\mu(\psi)=0.

Finally, one can follow verbatim the argument in
[

]
to achieve the result that
Z
is nowhere dense in

.
∎

Recall that to write down the three-dimensional RS-SW equations, besides fixing a
\text{spin}^{c}
structure on

g
on
Y
. Thus, theorem
3.1
and Theorem

are true for a specific a priori fixed

. The proof above can be adapted to this more general setting. In particular, we have the following.

Theorem 6.2
.

Denote
\mathfrak{M}
by the space of all Riemannian metrics on
Y
. Let
\{g_{n}\}
be a sequence of metrics on
Y
converging to
g\in\mathfrak{M}
. Let
\{(A_{n},\psi_{n},\epsilon_{n})\}
be a sequence of solutions of the
g_{n}-
(
3.2
) equations (i.e, the blown-up
g_{n}-
RS-SW equations) such that the
L^{6}-
norms of
F_{A_{n}}
are all uniformly bounded.

(1)

If
\limsup\epsilon_{n}>c>0
, then after passing through a subsequence and up to gauge transformations
\{(g_{n},(A_{n},\psi_{n},\epsilon_{n}))\}
converges to
(g,(A,\psi,\epsilon))
in the
C^{\infty}
topology.

(2)

If
\limsup\epsilon_{n}=0
, then there exists a closed nowhere-dense subset
Z\subset Y
, a connection
A
on

, a

\psi
on

such that

(a)

Q_{A}\psi=0
and
\mu(\psi)=0

(b)

\displaystyle\int_{Y\setminus Z}|\psi|^{4}=1
and
\displaystyle\int_{Y\setminus Z}|\nabla_{A}\psi|^{2}<\infty

(c)

|\psi|
extends to a

Hölder continuous function on

where
Z=|\psi|^{-1}(0)

furthermore,
A_{n}
converges weakly to

and

Motivated by the above theorem, we make the following definition regarding the limiting objects that appear in the moduli space of charge

of the three-dimensional RS-SW equations.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
g
be a Riemannian metric on
Y
. We say a moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{k}(g)
of
charge
k
of the three-dimensional RS-SW equations (

) to be the space of gauge equivalence classes of solutions
(A,\psi)
where

is bounded by

. Specifically,

\mathcal{M}_{k}(g)=\left\{(A,\psi):\begin{array}[]{l}(A,\psi)\text{ solves \eqref{eq:first compactness.1}}\\
\left\lVert F_{A}\right\rVert_{L^{6}(Y)}\leq k\end{array}\right\}\bigg/\mathcal{G}.

Definition 6.4
.

Suppose
g
is a Riemannian metric on
Y
. Let
Z\subset Y
be a closed proper subset of
Y
and
(A,\psi)\in\mathcal{A}(\mathscr{L}|_{Y\setminus Z})\times\Gamma(Y\setminus Z,W_{\mathfrak{s}_{3/2}}\otimes\mathscr{L})
. We call a triple

a

if it satisfies the conditions

stated in Theorem

.

It turns out that the existence of

Fueter sections on

provides an obstruction for the compactness of
\mathcal{M}_{k}(g)
.

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
g\in\mathfrak{M}
. If there is no
g-3/2-
Fueter section, then
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is always compact on some neighborhood of
g
in
\mathfrak{M}
.

Proof.

Since there is no
g-3/2-
Fueter section, by Theorem
6.2
(or Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
6.1
), the set
\{\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{4}(Y)}:[A,\psi]\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(g)\}
must have finite supremum, say,
\nu
. Suppose that there is a sequence of metric
\{g_{n}\}
converging to
g
such that for each
n
we have

\sup\{\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{4}(Y)}:[A,\psi]\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(g_{n})\}\geq\nu+1.

This means that for each
n
(also
g_{n}
), there is a corresponding
(A_{n},\psi_{n})
such that
\left\lVert\psi_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{4}}\geq\nu+1
. By Theorem
6.2
, if the first scenario holds for this sequence
\{(g_{n},(A_{n},\psi_{n}))\}
, then the sequence converges to
(g,(A,\psi))
, where
(A,\psi)\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(g)
. This means that
\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{4}}\geq\nu+1>\nu
, which contradicts with the observation in the beginning. If the second scenario holds instead, this is also not possible because otherwise, we would obtain the existence of a
g-3/2-
Fueter section. Therefore, there must be a a neighborhood around
g
in
\mathfrak{M}
such that for all
g^{\prime}
in such neighborhood,

\sup\{\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{4}(Y)}:[A,\psi]\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(g^{\prime})\}<\nu+1.

By Theorem
3.1
, we must have
\mathcal{M}_{k}(g^{\prime})
is compact in the
C^{\infty}
topology.
∎

7.
Discussion

The non-compactness of the moduli space is a hurdle to derive the monopole invariants. The results about the compactness of the moduli space of the RS-SW equations in this paper serve as the first step in the program of defining a Seiberg-Witten type invariant using the Rarita-Schwinger operator. Corollary
6.5
provides an obstruction for compactness, i.e., the non-existence of these so-called
3/2
-Fueter sections will ensure the compactness of the moduli space. Conjecturally, we expect that there should never be a
3/2
-Fueter section. A few indicators are pointing to this.

Firstly, note that if
(A,\psi)
is a solution where
\psi
is a Rarita-Schwinger field, then
(A,\psi)
is also a solution to a certain
3-
spinor Seiberg-Witten equations. Although the existence of limiting object in the moduli space of
2-
spinor Seiberg-Witten equations has been established by Doan and Walpuski
[
7
]
, such an analogous statement does not exist for
n-
spinor where
n>2
. Furthermore, the recent work of Bär and Mazzeo
[
4
]
suggests that there is a sequence of spin manifolds that have an arbitrarily large number of Rarita-Schwinger fields; however, these manifolds have dimensions at least 4. In the case of multiple-spinor Seiberg-Witten equations, Haydys showed that there is a correspondence between the limiting objects in the moduli space with (non-trivial) solutions to a certain non-linear Dirac-type operator defined on a hyper-Kähler fiber bundle over
Y

[
9
]
. Via Hitchin’s
hyper-Kähler quotient construction

[
11
]
, the hyper-Kähler fiber bundle over
Y
has fibers given by
\mu^{-1}(0)/U(1)
. Roughly, the non-linear Dirac operator is a Dirac-type operator
\mathfrak{F}
defined on the vertical component of the hyper-Kähler fiber bundle. Such an operator is called the
Fueter operator
, and its solutions are called
Fueter sections
. The study of the existence of limiting objects in the moduli space turns into the study of the existence of non-trivial (singular) Fueter sections.

Morally, whenever there is a Dirac operator, there is a corresponding Rarita-Schwinger operator. Thus, it seems natural to ask whether there is an analogous correspondence in the setting of the Rarita-Schwinger operator and
3/2-
spinors. Inspired by the work of Haydys in
[
9
]
, we propose that
g-
3/2-Fueter sections defined above correspond to non-trivial solutions of a certain non-linear Raritia-Schwinger operator associated with the Fueter operator. Note that, in the setting of
3/2-
spinors, the hyper-Kähler fiber bundle is cut down by a linear space
\gamma^{-1}(0)
. This more restrictive condition may lend insights into why one should not expect any 3/2-Fueter sections at all.

We expect the dimension of the moduli space to be zero (at least when
b_{1}(Y)>1
). Similar to the classical Seiberg-Witten equations, one hopes the invariants are obtained as a “signed” count of
the solutions
[
14
]
. Conjecturally, this requires dealing with the “transversality” of the monopole equation and showing that generic perturbations of the RS-SW equation have only irreducible solutions. This is the next goal of the authors.

Since solutions of the RS-SW equations correspond to the critical points of the modified Chern-Simon-Dirac functional
\mathcal{L}^{RS}
(cf. Proposition
2.18
), a compactness and transversality result of the moduli space naturally leads to the following questions

Question 7.1
.

Is there a homological invariant associated with
\mathcal{L}^{RS}
via the Floer theory program? If so, does such a Floer homology associated with the RS-SW equations categorify the conjectural numerical invariants described above?

Various gauge-theoretic Floer homology theories turn out to have a formal
(3+1)-
TQFT picture, see
[
8
]
,
[
13
]
. If the answer to the above question is positive, it is possible to develop a similar TQFT picture for a Floer homology associated with the RS-SW equations. Thus, one should expect that the homological invariant via functoriality should let us define (indirectly) numerical invariants associated with the RS-SW equations for closed
4-
manifolds. Such a supposed numerical invariant is difficult to achieve directly due to the non-compactness of the moduli space of the
4-
D RS-SW equations
[
15
]
.

To implement the Floer theory program in this setting, one has to understand the gradient flow line equations of
\mathcal{L}^{RS}
. It can be shown that the negative gradient flow lines of
\mathcal{L}^{RS}
satisfy the four-dimensional version of the RS-SW equations defined on
Y\times\mathbb{R}

(7.1)

\begin{cases}Q^{+}_{B}\phi=0,\\
F^{+}_{B}=\gamma^{-1}(\mu(\phi)).\end{cases}

A differential of a Floer complex should be defined by the count of solutions of (
7.1
) interpolating between critical points of
\mathcal{L}^{RS}
. Thus, it is natural for us to investigate the compactification problem of the moduli space of (
7.1
).

Problem 7.2
.

Develop a gauge-theoretic frequency function that can be applied to the analysis of four-dimensional RS-SW equations defined on four-manifolds with cylindrical ends.

We hope to explore these directions in our future works.
