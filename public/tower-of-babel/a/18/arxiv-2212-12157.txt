Contact Angle Hysteresis on Rough Surfaces Part I: Mechanical Energy Balance Framework
Dalton J.E. Harvie 1

Abstract
Using as a starting point conservation of momentum, a multiphase mechanical energy balance equation is derived that accounts for multiple material phases and interfaces present within a moving control volume. This balance is applied to a control volume that is anchored to a three phase contact line as it advances over the surface of a rough and chemically homogeneous solid. Using semi-quantitative models for the material behaviour occurring within the control volume, an order-of-magnitude analysis is performed to find what terms within the balance are significant, producing an equation that can be used to predict contact angle hysteresis from a knowledge of interface dynamics occurring around the three phase contact line. In addition to this equation, the theory also answers several questions that have been discussed within the wetting literature: Namely that (static) contact angle hysteresis is a function of conditions around the three phase contact line, as opposed to the surrounding flow system; That contact angle hysteresis results from interface ‘jumps’ that dissipate energy, rather than directly from contact line deformation; That interfacial dynamics is required to predict contact angle hysteresis, but that these dynamics should be interpreted via energy conservation, and; That dynamic contact angles depend on kinetic energy transport around the three phase contact line, as well as local energy dissipation. The framework has been derived using assumptions of incompressible Newtonian fluids, reversible interface formation and zero-strain solids — future work could relax these assumptions to make the theory more generally applicable.

Introduction

The ability of a liquid to ‘wet’ a solid is described by the angle that the liquid makes with the solid when the interface is stationary — the static contact angle. For a liquid in contact with an ideal, smooth and chemically homogeneous surface, this angle
\theta_{\mathrm{e}}
is unique and given by Young’s Equation. Real surfaces, which are often rough on a variety of length scales, are chemically heterogeneous and/or involve some type of irreversible work of adhesion, display a range of equilibrium contact angles: The maximum is the static advancing angle
\theta_{\mathrm{a}}
, above will the interface will advance, and the minimum the static receding angle,
\theta_{\mathrm{r}}
, below which it will recede. The difference between these two angles is defined as the range of contact angle hysteresis (CAH). CAH,
\theta_{\mathrm{a}}
and
\theta_{\mathrm{r}}
are critical wetting parameters that determine (for example) how easily drops can move over solid surfaces, under what conditions liquid films will smoothly coat surfaces or whether gas injection will aid particle floatation. Technologies that depend on CAH angles include established processes such as industrial separation devices or the wetting behaviour of fabrics, through to more novel processes such as transparent self-cleaning surfaces for solar power generation or low-energy liquid fuel separation membranes
(
Cassie & Baxter 1944
;
Wu et al. 2002
;
Feng et al. 2004
;
Sun et al. 2005
;
Callies & Quéré 2005
;
Li et al. 2013
)
. For the design and optimisation of these processes general and validated wetting theories are needed that can predict the CAH range. However, as highlighted via several recent works such theories are not yet available, with fundamental questions remaining about the nature of the wetting process
(
Eral et al. 2013
;
Jiang et al. 2019
;
Butt et al. 2022
)
. The purpose of this study is to derive an energy conservation framework that can be applied to predict CAH angles.

The early energy-based wetting theories of Wenzel and Cassie remain influential in interpreting wetting phenomena. Considering the energy change that occurs as a liquid/gas interface advances a small distance over the surface of a rough solid,
Wenzel 1936
proposed that the apparent contact angle is related to the roughness
r
of the surface, defined as the total to projected surface area ratio (see section
4.1
). In deriving this theory,
Wenzel 1936
assumed that the liquid completely wets each surface undulation.
Cassie & Baxter 1944
recognised that such ‘complete’ wetting did not necessarily occur, and derived an expression for the apparent contact angle on a partially wet surface in terms of the wetted and non-wetted liquid areas per projected solid area, being
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, respectively (see section
4.1
). The Wenzel and
Cassie & Baxter 1944
equations are useful for understanding and interpreting experimental data, however, theories based solely on these concepts
(
Bico et al. 2002
;
Patankar 2003
, e.g.)
are not predictive as the proportion of solid surface wetted by a liquid is not known
a priori
. Also, these theories give only one static contact angle for a rough structured surface, rather than the CAH range that is observed experimentally.

Another series of works is based on the concept of energy minimisation of an entire drop sitting on a rough surface.
Johnson Jr & Dettre 1964
computed the free energy of drops residing at the centre of concentric sinusoidal roughness rings, showing that the energy of the system oscillated as the drop volume increased and the interface advanced over each ring. They interpreted the amplitude of these energy oscillations as energy barriers that must be overcome by macroscopic vibrational energy to allow interface movement, implying that as the height of surface roughness decreases, the range of CAH should also decrease. This conclusion is contrary to experimental evidence however which shows that roughness-induced CAH strongly depends on surface topology (relative shape), rather than absolute roughness size, provided that gravitational and Laplace pressure effects can be neglected on the lengthscale of the roughness
(
Öner & McCarthy 2000
;
Dorrer & Rühe 2008
;
Li et al. 2016
;
Jiang et al. 2019
)
. Other studies have used similar static free-energy minimisation concepts to explain CAH for a variety of periodically shaped surfaces
(
Extrand 2002
;
Brandon et al. 2003
;
Marmur 2006
;
Marmur 2022
)
, however in general the results do not qualitatively agree with observation. For example,
Brandon et al. 2003
used minimal surface energy modelling to show that the apparent contact angle range for a drop on a doubly periodic undulating surface approached a single value (the Cassie angle) as the drop size to roughness ratio increased, again contradicting the above referenced experimental observations that show that the CAH range becomes quite constant at large droplet to roughness size ratios. A related question also remains about these analyses: Is it really necessary to consider the energy of the entire flow system (most commonly a droplet) to calculate the CAH range, or is it instead a property associated with the three phase contact line (TPCL) that can be applied to a wide variety of surrounding flow systems? This is an unresolved question that has garnered conflicting opinions
(
Gao & McCarthy 2007
;
McHale 2007
;
Gao & McCarthy 2007
;
Nosonovsky 2007
;
Panchagnula & Vedantam 2007
;
Marmur 2022
)
.

Other studies conclude that CAH is substantially a property of the TPCL region
(
Nosonovsky 2007
;
Panchagnula & Vedantam 2007
)
and that CAH has its origin in the energy dissipation that occurs around the TPCL as it advances over the rough solid. Central to this concept is that the advancing and receding angles are defined as those measured while the interface is moving (albeit at a vanishingly slow velocity) rather than being determined solely by static thermodynamic states. An influential study in this vein is
Joanny & de Gennes 1984
who proposed a model for CAH on a surface that contains a dilute number of ‘strong defects’ as a model for surface contamination or dilutely distributed surface roughness. The theory considered the ‘pinning’ and subsequent ‘depinning’ or ‘jumping’ of the contact line as it advanced over a surface, assuming that during each interface jump surface potential energy is dissipated to heat.
Joanny & de Gennes 1984
calculated this energy dissipation amount under ideal conditions and incorporated it into an equation for CAH relevant to dilute defect surfaces. More recent works have experimentally observed the pinning/depinning behaviour of the fluid interface near the TPCL
(
Jiang et al. 2019
;
Priest et al. 2009
;
Forsberg et al. 2010
)
. Other studies have used a variety of energy conservation principles to extend
Joanny & de Gennes 1984
’s work to periodic surfaces
(
Raj et al. 2012
;
Butt et al. 2017
;
Jiang et al. 2019
)
or interpreted measurements of CAH in terms contact line energy dissipation and interfacial ‘jumping’ dynamics
(
Priest et al. 2007
;
Priest et al. 2013
;
Dorrer & Rühe 2008
;
Song et al. 2022
)
. Despite these successes however questions remain about this conceptual model of CAH; around what specific TPCL region should energy be conserved, how should the energies of real rough surfaces that may contain randomly shaped structures or micro bubbles/droplets be incorporated in the energy analysis, and why must the energy dissipated due to interface jumping dynamics be balanced by only interfacial (rather than material) stress movements?

On a slightly different track
Joanny & de Gennes 1984
’s work also explored the influence of ’weak’ surface defects on CAH, referring to smooth defects as those that cause the fluid interface near the solid to become distorted, but that do not result in the aforementioned ‘pinning’ and ‘depinning’ behaviour of the TPCL.
Joanny & de Gennes 1984
’s conclusion was that isolated weak defects do not generally result in hysteresis, however other works have extended this analysis to conclude that distributions of weak surface heterogeneities can cause CAH
(
Pomeau & Vannimenus 1985
;
Robbins & Joanny 1987
;
Öpik 2000
)
. In related work
Cox 1983
examined how an interface changes as it moves over a gently undulating sinusoidal periodic rough surface, showing that when the interface moved in the direction of roughness periodicity TPCL ‘jumps’ occurred (i.e., ‘strong’ defects leading to CAH), but when advancing in other directions relative to the periodicity direction the interface moved continuously (i.e., ‘weak’ defects producing no CAH). It should be noted that these theoretical studies predict the possible shapes that a fluid interface can take when passing over arrangements of surface heterogeneities, and from these the range of CAH angles are inferred: In general the link between CAH angles calculated via these interface topology methods and those calculated by energy conservation has not been established.

Reviewing this body of literature key questions about wetting behaviour remain. Specifically there is conjecture regarding how energy conservation, fluid interface distortion and the dynamics of interface ‘jumping’ can be combined to predict CAH. Critically, there is no experimental consensus or fundamental analysis that shows how energy conservation should be applied to predict CAH — works that are based on energy conservation around entire droplets in general do not predict experimentally observed CAH trends, while studies that are based on energy changes around a moving TPCL lack a rigorous energy conservation basis. Further, while many studies do view contact line jumping as a source of CAH (‘strong’ defects), there is confusion over whether fluid interface distortion (‘weak’ defects) in isolation can produce CAH, and how studies that predict CAH angles via interface topology and dynamics can be mathematically related to CAH angles resulting from energy conservation.

This study addresses these questions. Specifically we derive a rigorous energy conservation framework which when applied to the moving TPCL can be used to predict contact angle hysteresis (CAH). We do this by first deriving a general multiphase mechanical energy balance (MMEB) equation that (section
2
) is equivalent to the conventional single phase mechanical energy balance equation but includes terms associated with interfacial stresses acting between each of the material phases. We then apply this MMEB equation to a cylindrical control volume that is anchored to a TPCL as it moves across a rough solid surface at a vanishingly slow speed (section
3
). By adopting order-or-magnitude models that describe how each of the materials behave within the control volume during the advance, we neglect terms that are insignificant to the calculation and derive a resulting energy conservation equation that predicts CAH given knowledge of the interfacial dynamics occurring within the TPCL. A strength of the analysis is that mathematical constraints are derived that determine under what physical conditions the theory will be valid (section
4
), giving insight into past and future modelling theories and experimental wetting studies.

\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv}}}\delta_{\mathrm{S},ij}dS=\frac{l_{ij}}{\sqrt{1-(\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{S},ij})^{2}}},

By using equation (

Firstly, as the surface stress is a sum of contributions from each interface type, we have neglected any ‘line tension’ stresses that may occur at the intersection between interfaces. While no concensus regarding the existence these stresses has been reached in the literature, most studies suggest that even if line tension does exist, it has a negligible effect on macroscopically measureable contact angles

3
Calculating the advancing contact angle on a rough solid surface

In this section we analyse the macroscopic contact angle of an interface between two immiscible fluids that slowly advances over a rough solid. The analysis uses the multiphase mechanical energy balance derived in section
2
, applied to a small control volume (CV) which moves with the advancing three phase contact line across the surface of the solid. Using semi-quantitative models for material and interface behaviour, an order of magnitude analysis is performed to determine which terms within the energy balance are significant, and from this an expression for the advancing contact angle is found that is valid in the limit of an infinitely slowly moving interface.

h_{\mathrm{mol}}\ll h_{\mathrm{rough}}\ll r_{\mathrm{cv}},l_{\mathrm{cv}},X_{\mathrm{cv}}\ll h_{\mathrm{surround}}.

(11)

Defining

The precise centreline of the moving control volume is defined to lie at the intersection between two averaged planes: the ‘average solid surface plane’ and ‘projected fluid interface plane’. The locations of these planes are defined as those of the solid surface and fluid interfaces, respectively, averaged over distances of
\mathcal{O}\left(r_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
(where

means ‘order

\left[\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{S},12}\right]_{\text{TPCL}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{w}}=\cos\theta_{\mathrm{e}},

(12)

where
\left[\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{S},12}\right]_{\text{TPCL}}

\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{w}}
is the outwardly directed wall normal at the same contact point and

\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{S},12}
) and solid (
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{w}}
) normals along the TPCL, it follows that close to the rough solid surface the fluid interface will have local curvatures that are characteristic of the solid roughness — that is, of
\mathcal{O}\left(1/h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
— and that these curvatures will exist within distances of
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
from the TPCL. Conversely, further from the solid surface the topology of the interface varies over the larger lengthscales of the surrounding flow (indeed, this can be used to define
h_{\mathrm{surround}}
), so the curvature of the interface there approaches
\mathcal{O}\left(1/h_{\mathrm{surround}}\right)
. Hence, as
h_{\mathrm{rough}}\ll r_{\mathrm{cv}}\ll h_{\mathrm{surround}}
(equation (
11

\mathcal{O}\left(r_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
produces a projected fluid interface plane that is perfectly flat on the lengthscales of the CV, and as the centreline of the control volume is defined as the intersection between the projected fluid interface plane and average solid surface plane, on the scale of the control volume its geometry is that of a perfect cylinder, with a perfectly straight centreline.

This description of the fluid interface topology and geometry of the CV has further implications for the mechanical energy balance application. At distances of
\mathcal{O}\left(r_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
from the TPCL, the actual fluid interface and projected fluid interface will at all times be coincident and perfectly flat on these lengthscales. Hence, given that the projected fluid interface is used to define the centreline of the CV, where the actual fluid interface intersects the circumference of the CV (labeled as
S_{\mathrm{cv,cir}}
in Figure

in Figure

\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
from the TPCL, the actual fluid interface will be flat, coincident with the projected fluid interface and perfectly normal to the CV boundary. Closer to the TPCL however the interface will undulate with curvatures of
\mathcal{O}\left(1/h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
, crossing the CV boundary at angles with are not necessarily normal to
S_{\mathrm{cv,end}}
.

3.2
Describing material dynamics within the moving control volume

As well as defining the physical system and CV geometry used in the mechanical energy balance, to be able to perform an order of magnitude analysis on its various terms we need to quantitatively describe how the materials within the volume behave as a function of both space and time. Specifically we need conceptual models for how the fluid velocities, pressures, interface topology and solid stresses vary as the CV advances.

Within the fluid phase we assume that for the majority of the advancing time
\tau
the TPCL and surrounding fluid both move continuously at the slow speeds of
\mathcal{O}\left(v_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
. We refer to the system as being in ‘equilibrium’ when this is the case and define the velocity field existing during these times as
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}=\mathcal{O}\left(v_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
. However, at certain times during
\tau
, local areas of the TPCL will become pinned by particular surface defects, creating local interface deflections that become larger as the remainder of the TPCL continues to advance. Eventually, once the surrounding TPCL has advanced some distance of
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
from the pinning location, these contact line sections will either detach from the surface defect and return to the main TPCL, or detach from the TPCL and form isolated bubbles/droplets of entrained fluid within the surface roughness. Either way, these detachment processes cause the local TPCL and surrounding fluid to move at much faster capillary driven speeds than the continuous CV advance speed (
v_{\mathrm{cv}}
), causing a viscous dissipation of energy. This slip-stick dissipative motion has been described previously as the cause of contact angle hysteresis
(
Joanny & de Gennes 1984
;
Raj et al. 2012
;
Butt et al. 2017
;
Jiang et al. 2019
)
and as discussed in the Introduction has also been experimentally observed
(
Jiang et al. 2019
;
Priest et al. 2009
;
Forsberg et al. 2010
)
. In this study we define the local interface and fluid speeds associated with these capillary driven events as
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}=\mathcal{O}\left(v_{\mathrm{cap}}\right)
, and the total time during which there is a dissipation event occurring within the control volume as
\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}
. We further assume that the number of defects within the control volume (
N
) is small enough and the capillary velocity (
v_{\mathrm{cap}}
) large enough that only one dissipation event occurs within the CV at any one time. With these assumptions, and for convenience assuming that the analysis duration
\tau
commences and finishes while the system is in equilibrium, we can split the total time over which the analysis is being conducted
\tau
into a number of ‘dissipation events’ (
N
) and ‘equilibrium stages’ (
N+1
), with the
k
th dissipation event starting at
\widehat{t}_{k}
and lasting for
{\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k}
, and the
k
th equilibrium stage starting at
\widetilde{t}_{k}
and lasting for
\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}
. The schematic timeline of Figure
4
illustrates this decomposition. The following relationships result:

\begin{gathered}\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k}\quad\quad\tau=\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}+\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}\\
{\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k}=\widetilde{t}_{k+1}-\widehat{t}_{k}\quad\quad\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}=\widehat{t}_{k}-\widetilde{t}_{k}\\
\widetilde{t}_{1}=0\quad\quad\widetilde{t}_{N+1}+\widetilde{\Delta t}_{N+1}=\tau\end{gathered}

(13)

In terms of notation used in the remainder of the analysis, variables annotated with a ‘hat’ correspond to properties associated with individual dissipation events (where parts of the TPCL are moving at
\mathcal{O}\left(v_{\mathrm{cap}}\right)
), variables annotated with a ‘tilde’ correspond to properties associated with the system while in equilibrium (where the entire TPCL is moving at at most
\mathcal{O}\left(v_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
), and variables annotated with a ‘bar’ correspond to the entire advancing period
\tau
. The decomposition of
\tau
into separate dissipation and equilibrium stages is a key concept used in the subsequent energy analysis.

Figure 4:
The entire analysis time
\tau
is split into a number

of dissipation periods (indicated in blue) where a portion of the TPCL moves at velocities of
\mathcal{O}\left(v_{\mathrm{cap}}\right)
, interspersed between
N+1

\mathcal{O}\left(v_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
.

A number of mathematical constraints follow from the dynamic model of fluid movement described above. Firstly, the analysis assumes that
v_{\mathrm{cv}}\ll v_{\mathrm{cap}}
. By assuming that the fluids are Newtonian with a stress defined by

\mathsf{T}_{\mathrm{M}}=-p\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{}}+\mu\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}+(\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}})^{T}\right]

(14)

an order of magnitude analysis on the augmented Navier-Stokes equations (as defined by equations (
1
) and (
2
)) shows that capillary driven fluid velocities can be limited by either viscous dissipation or momentum acceleration/advection terms during any dissipation event. Hence, a conservative estimate for the capillary driven velocity scale is given by equating the interfacial stress term to either the viscous dissipation or momentum acceleration/advection terms, giving

v_{\mathrm{cap}}=\min\left(\frac{\sigma}{\mu},\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\rho h_{\mathrm{rough}}}}\right)

(15)

In this expression and subsequent order of magnitude analyses, properties such as
\sigma
,
\mu
and
\rho
are order of magnitude estimates only, which for most expressions can be taken as the maximum of the different phase properties existing within the CV. Equation (
15
) places a constraint on the maximum
v_{\mathrm{cv}}
that can be used given that
v_{\mathrm{cv}}\ll v_{\mathrm{cap}}
.

Interestingly, equation (
15
) predicts that capillary velocities are limited by momentum acceleration/advection terms rather than viscous dissipation on most practical surfaces. To illustrate, for a water droplet within air advancing over a rough solid surface, using
\mu
and
\rho
from the water phase and
\sigma
as the surface tension coefficient between air and water gives
\sigma/\mu\approx 72\,\mathrm{m/s}
. However, for all surface roughness values
h_{\mathrm{rough}}\gtrapprox h_{\mathrm{rough,crit}}=\mu^{2}/(\rho\sigma)=14\,\mathrm{nm}
the capillary velocity will be limited by the momentum acceleration/advection term (
\sqrt{\sigma/(\rho h_{\mathrm{rough}})}
) and hence will determine
v_{\mathrm{cap}}
for this system. Indeed, for a more typical surface roughness of
h_{\mathrm{rough}}=10\,\mathrm{\mu m}
equation (
15
) gives
v_{\mathrm{cap}}\approx 3\,\mathrm{m/s}
meaning that for practical surfaces as long as
v_{\mathrm{cv}}
is of the order cm/s or less
v_{\mathrm{cv}}\ll v_{\mathrm{cap}}
will be satisfied. We consider the variation of
v_{\mathrm{cap}}
in more detail in section
4.2
. Note that even though the local velocities existing during a dissipation event may be determined by a balance between capillary and momentum acceleration/advection terms, the Reynolds number for the motion near the surface roughness is not large (
\sim 20
for the above
h_{\mathrm{rough}}=10\,\mathrm{\mu m}
system). This means that the size of the region where velocities are
\mathcal{O}\left(v_{\mathrm{cap}}\right)
during dissipation events is only of
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
, and importantly these high velocities will not exist at the circumference of the CV, located at roughly
r_{\mathrm{cv}}
from the TPCL.

A second constraint required by the dynamic model of fluid movement outlined above is that
\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}\ll\tau
. To understand what conditions this places on the physical system we first recognise that the time taken for each dissipation event can be estimated from the interface velocity and distance travelled during each event —
\widehat{\Delta t}_{k}=\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}/v_{\mathrm{cap}}\right)
— and that as there are
N
dissipation events occurring during
\tau
,
\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}=\mathcal{O}\left(Nh_{\mathrm{rough}}/v_{\mathrm{cap}}\right)
. Recognising that the number of dissipation events occurring during the advance duration is
\mathcal{O}\left(A_{\mathrm{cv}}/h_{\mathrm{rough}}^{2}\right)
where
A_{\mathrm{cv}}=X_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}
, and that
\tau=X_{\mathrm{cv}}/v_{\mathrm{cv}}
leads to

\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}}{\tau}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{l_{\mathrm{cv}}}{h_{\mathrm{rough}}}\frac{v_{\mathrm{cv}}}{v_{\mathrm{cap}}}\right)\ll 1

(16)

Equation (
16
) can always be satisfied provided that
v_{\mathrm{cv}}
is small enough, which is obviously the case when determining
\theta_{\mathrm{a}}
as this angle is derived in the limit of
v_{\mathrm{cv}}\rightarrow 0
. If the energy balance is being applied to moving interfaces however (i.e.,
v_{\mathrm{cv}}\neq 0
) equation (
16
) places a constraint on the maximum applicable
v_{\mathrm{cv}}
. This is discussed further in section
4.2
.

Two final conceptual models concerning the continuous fluid movement occurring during the equilibrium stages of the flow have to be developed in order to apply the contact angle mechanical energy balance: These models are for velocity gradient and pressure, both of which relate to the fluid stress.

For the velocity gradient, we note that during equilibrium stages the TPCL advances at a speed of
\mathcal{O}\left(v_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
over the solid, resulting in a velocity discontinuity at the solid surface if the conventional continuum non-slip fluid boundary condition is applied. Indeed, velocity profiles which satisfy the Navier-Stokes equations and that are consistent with both a moving TPCL and the non-slip boundary condition are available
(
Moffatt 1964
;
Huh & Scriven 1971
)
, however these result in velocity gradients near that TPCL that increase as
1/r
(where
r
is the distance to the TPCL). We find that integrating these gradients over the region surrounding the TPCL in our energy balance results in a energy dissipation term for non-zero
v_{\mathrm{cv}}
that diverges logarithmically in an unphysical fashion, as others have found
(
Huh & Scriven 1971
)
. Solutions proposed to this problem, which we invoke here, all involve removing or limiting the stress (equivalently velocity gradients) within the fluid at small distances (
h_{\mathrm{mol}}
) from the TPCL. Various justifications for this limiting have been proposed
(
Huh & Scriven 1971
;
Joanny & de Gennes 1984
;
Petrov & Petrov 1992
)
, but most revolve around a breakdown of the Newtonian or continuum model of a fluid at the TPCL where individual molecules or particles within the fluid must ‘jump’ along the solid. The implications of this limit are discussed further in section
4.2
. Putting these concepts together we hence estimate the velocity gradients existing within the fluid during the equilibrium stages as

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{v_{\mathrm{cv}}}{\max(r,h_{\mathrm{mol}})}\right)

(17)

where as discussed
h_{\mathrm{mol}}
is a small lengthscale related to the molecular (or non-continuum) nature of the fluid.

For the continuous flow pressure variation, we perform an order of magnitude on the single-phase Navier-Stokes equations, recognising that pressure gradients may develop within either fluid in response to both viscous stress and momentum acceleration/advection terms
2
2

2

Gravitational effects are not included when estimating the pressure variation as in general the fluid may not be at rest (and hence may not experience a hydrostatic pressure variation), however we note that inclusion of a gravitational contribution to the pressure variation would not alter the final results of the energy balance presented in section
3.3.10
as equivalent gravitational terms contribute to this balance via the gravitational potential energy transport term
\widetilde{T}_{3,k}
.
. Given these assumptions we describe the pressure variation within either fluid phase as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}p}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\rho\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\mu\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}^{2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}}{\max(r,h_{\mathrm{mol}})}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\mu v_{\mathrm{cv}}}{\left[\max(r,h_{\mathrm{mol}})\right]^{2}}\right)

(18)

where again the fluid stress has been limited within a distance of
h_{\mathrm{mol}}
from the TPCL. Noting that the equilibrium stage fluid pressure
\widetilde{p}
is relative to some point in the surrounding fluid far from the TPCL, and that under these slow flow conditions there is a potential pressure jump over the fluid interface due to the surrounding interface curvature of
\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma/h_{\mathrm{surround}}\right)

\widetilde{p}=\mathcal{O}\left(\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\mu v_{\mathrm{cv}}}{\max(r,h_{\mathrm{mol}})}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma}{h_{\mathrm{surround}}}\right)+p_{0}

(19)

where

is some reference pressure located at a point away from the TPCL, but within the vicinity of the CV. All models describing the conceptual behaviour of the fluid phases during the equilibrium stages have now been defined.

For the solid phase, to apply the contact angle mechanical energy balance we need models that describe how the solid velocities and stresses vary within this phase as the TPCL advances over the rough surface. In this study we invoke the simplest possible model by assuming that velocities within the solid are everywhere zero. With this assumption the energy balance becomes independent of solid phase stresses. On physical grounds zero velocities can be justified within the solid by assuming it is a yield-stress (or plastic) material that does not experience a stress exceeding its yield-stress during the analysis time. In reality this is probably justifiable for most solids used in engineering applications, but for soft solids used in (eg) biomedical applications energy dissipation within the solid phase may be significant. This is certainly an area for future work that could be incorporated into the presented mechanical energy balance framework but is not advanced here.

3.3
Applying the mechanical energy balance to the moving control volume

With the physical system defined and semi-quantitative models for how the materials within the CV behave as it advances over the rough solid, we can now apply the mechanical energy balance to find the advancing contact angle.

With these assumptions the change in mechanical energy over the duration of the advance is evaluated as

\displaystyle\overline{T}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}r_{\mathrm{cv}}h_{\mathrm{rough}}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\rho gr_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}h_{\mathrm{rough}}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma h_{\mathrm{rough}}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\rho gl_{\mathrm{cv}}h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma h_{\mathrm{rough}}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)

(37)

where in the last line we have used
\mathcal{O}\left(r_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(X_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
. The above three terms are later compared to other terms present in equation (
35
) to gauge their significance in determining the advancing contact angle
\theta_{\mathrm{a}}
.

3.3.3
Examining term
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\widehat{T}_{0,k}

In a similar fashion to
\overline{T}_{0}
, but here applied over each dissipation period,
\widehat{T}_{0,k}
represents the change in mechanical energy within the CV occurring over the period of the
k
th dissipation event. Defining
\widehat{\Delta a}_{k}=a(t=\widehat{t}_{k}+{\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k})-a(t=\widehat{t}_{k})
this change in energy can be expressed as

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{0,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\widehat{\Delta E}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\int_{V_{\mathrm{cv}}}\frac{1}{2}\widehat{\Delta(\rho v^{2})}_{k}dV+\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\int_{V_{\mathrm{cv}}}\widehat{\Delta\rho}_{k}\hat{\Phi}dV+\sum_{i<j}\frac{\sigma_{ij}\widehat{\Delta A_{ij}}_{k}}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}

(38)

where
\widehat{\Delta A_{ij}}_{k}
is the change in area of interface
ij
that is contained within the CV and that occurs over the
k
th dissipation event.

In order to evaluate the magnitude of the terms appearing in equation (
38
), we return to our conceptual model for how the fluid and interfaces behave during dissipation events. During a dissipation event, an area of the TPCL ‘depins’ from a particular surface defect and moves at a capillary-driven velocity to a new ‘equilibrium’ interface position. These dissipation events cause interfacial areas to change by
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}^{2}\right)
, and as interfacial curvatures resulting from the roughness extend by
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
into the fluid, cause fluid properties to change within a volume of
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}^{3}\right)
near the TPCL. Hence changes to the kinetic energy and density within a volume of
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}^{3}\right)
caused by each dissipation event will contribute to the first two terms on the RHS of equation (
38
), while changes of
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}^{2}\right)
to the interfacial areas of each phase combination due to each dissipation event will contribute to the third term on the RHS of this equation.

Concurrently, over the duration of each dissipation event (
{\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k}
) continuous movement of the TPCL still occurs across the rough surface, and this movement also contributes to the terms on the RHS of equation (
38
). Specifically, within
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
of the TPCL, or a volume of
\mathcal{O}\left({\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k}v_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
, there will be a change in kinetic energy and density of the fluid occurring due to the continuous TPCL movement which will add contributions to the first two terms on the RHS of equation (
38
). Similarly, for the third term on the RHS of equation (
38
), there will also be a change in interfacial areas of
\mathcal{O}\left({\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k}v_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
due to the continuous TPCL movement that also needs to be included.

Hence, summing changes due to both the specific dissipation event and continuous TPCL movement occurring during each dissipation period, the magnitude of
\widehat{T}_{0,k}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{0,k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}h_{\mathrm{rough}}^{3}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{3}h_{\mathrm{rough}}{\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\rho gh_{\mathrm{rough}}^{4}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\rho gl_{\mathrm{cv}}v_{\mathrm{cv}}h_{\mathrm{rough}}{\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma h_{\mathrm{rough}}^{2}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma v_{\mathrm{cv}}{\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)

(39)

where each pair of the six terms corresponds to the first, second and third terms appearing on the RHS of equation (
38
), respectively, with the first of each pair corresponding to changes caused by the specific capillary-driven dissipation event, and the second of each pair corresponding to the changes due to continuous TPCL movement that occurs during each dissipation event period.

Returning to equation (
35
), it is actually the sum of
\widehat{T}_{0,k}
from all
N
dissipation events that is required in the contact angle mechanical energy balance. Performing this sum on equation (
39
), while noting
\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k}=\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}
,
N=\mathcal{O}\left(X_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}/h_{\mathrm{rough}}^{2}\right)
and
v_{\mathrm{cv}}\tau=X_{\mathrm{cv}}
leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\widehat{T}_{0,k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}h_{\mathrm{rough}}\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}}{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\rho gh_{\mathrm{rough}}^{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\rho gl_{\mathrm{cv}}h_{\mathrm{rough}}\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}}{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}}{\tau}\right)

(40)

Noting from equation (
16
) that
\mathcal{O}\left(\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}/\tau\right)\ll 1
, terms involving this ratio can be neglected in comparison to other terms, and recognising that the
\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\right)
term in the above originated from the final term of equation (
38
), we arrive at

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\widehat{T}_{0,k}=\mathcal{O}\left(\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\rho gh_{\mathrm{rough}}^{2}\right)+\widehat{\Delta\sigma}

(41)

Here the specific dissipation event surface energy change per area traversed has been defined as

\widehat{\Delta\sigma}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\frac{\widehat{\Delta A_{ij}}_{k}}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}

(42)

This variable represents the sum of changes to potential surface energies occurring within the CV due to all capillary-driven dissipation events, per projected area of solid traversed. This variable has a magnitude of
\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\right)
. It is similar to the
W_{d}
term used in
Joanny & de Gennes 1984
and will become key in determining
\theta_{\mathrm{a}}
from the contact angle energy analysis.

The next six terms all correspond to energy transfers that occur during the equilibrium stages of the advance.

3.3.4
Examining term
\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{1,k}

This term represents transport of surface potential energy through the boundary of the CV during the equilibrium stages due only to movement of the CV. Introducing the shorthand notation
\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}=\int_{\widetilde{t}_{k}}^{\widetilde{t}_{k}+\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}
, applying equation (
23
) over
\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}
gives

\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{1,k}=\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\delta_{\text{S},ij}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}dSdt

(43)

Further, noting that
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}
on the ends of the CV (
S_{\mathrm{cv,end}}
), and that
\sigma_{ij}
is non-zero only within three thin regions on the circumference of the CV (
S_{\mathrm{cv,cir}}
) where the interfaces cross its boundary, equation (
43
) can be written as the sum of three terms

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{1,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,top}}}\sigma_{12}\delta_{\text{S},12}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}dSdt

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}}\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\delta_{\text{S},ij}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}dSdt

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,br}}}\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\delta_{\text{S},ij}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}dSdt

(44)

Here, as indicated in Figure
5
,
S_{\mathrm{cv,top}}
,
S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}
and
S_{\mathrm{cv,br}}
are three thin regions located on
S_{\mathrm{cv,cir}}
that just contain the fluid interface (top), interfaces associated with the solid surface under phase
\bigcirc
1
(bl = bottom left) and interfaces associated with the solid surface under phase
\bigcirc
2
(br = bottom right), respectively.

Figure 5:
Three thin regions on the circumference of the CV (
S_{\mathrm{cv,cir}}
) are defined that contain all of the phase interfaces that intersect with this boundary.

For the first term on the RHS of equation (
44
), as
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}=-\cos\theta_{\mathrm{a}}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\sin\theta_{\mathrm{a}}\boldsymbol{e}_{3}
where the fluid interface crosses the CV boundary at
S_{\mathrm{cv,top}}
, and as
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}=v_{\mathrm{cv}}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}=-v_{\mathrm{cv}}\cos\theta_{\mathrm{a}}
at this location. Further, following the interface description outlined in Section
3.1
,
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{S},ij}=0
at this location at all times, and as
S_{\mathrm{cv,top}}
is infinitely thin in the circumferential direction at this location equation (
4
) shows that
\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,top}}}\delta_{\text{S,}ij}dS=l_{\mathrm{cv}}
. Hence the first term on the RHS of equation (
44
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,top}}}\sigma_{12}\delta_{\text{S,}ij}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}dSdt

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sigma_{12}v_{\mathrm{cv}}\cos\theta_{\mathrm{a}}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}

\displaystyle=-\left(1-\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}}{\tau}\right)\sigma_{12}\cos\theta_{\mathrm{a}}

(45)

The second and third terms on the RHS of equation (
44
) represent the transport of surface potential energy associated with the rough solid surface out of, and into, respectively, the control volume as it advances across the solid. Note that these surface energies may include contributions from micro-droplets or bubbles confined within the surface roughness, as well as from the surface energy between the solid and adjacent bulk fluid phase.

Focusing our explanation on the second term on the RHS of (
44
) that involves an integral over
S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}
, we first note that as
S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}
only has to include phase interfaces that are associated with the surface roughness, the circumferential height of
S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}
is only of
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
. Further as
h_{\mathrm{rough}}\ll r_{\mathrm{cv}}
(via equation (
11
)) we have
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}=-\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
at this location and consequently
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}=-v_{\mathrm{cv}}
. Splitting the temporal integration using equation (
13
) leads to

\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}}\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\delta_{\text{S,}ij}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}dSdt=\\
-\frac{v_{\mathrm{cv}}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}}\delta_{\text{S,}ij}dSdt\\
-\frac{v_{\mathrm{cv}}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{{\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}}\delta_{\text{S},ij}dSdt

(46)

For the first term on the RHS of this equation, we define a new volume
\overleftarrow{V}
that is created by sweeping
S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}
over the solid surface for the duration of the advance. Formally we define a coordinate
x_{\mathrm{cv}}=tv_{\mathrm{cv}}
that increases in the direction of
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
and measures the progress of the CV as it moves over the solid surface, giving

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{cv}}\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}}\delta_{\text{S,}ij}dSdt

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\int_{0}^{X_{\mathrm{cv}}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}}\delta_{\text{S,}ij}dSdx_{\mathrm{cv}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\int_{\overleftarrow{V}}\delta_{\text{S,}ij}dV

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\overleftarrow{A}_{ij}

(47)

where
\overleftarrow{A}_{ij}
is the area of each interface type
ij
associated with the rough solid surface that leaves the CV during its advance over the solid.

For the second term on the RHS of equation (
46
) we calculate its order of magnitude rather than derive an expression, noting that
\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\widehat{\Delta t}}_{k}=\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}
and
\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}}\delta_{\text{S},ij}dS=\mathcal{O}\left(l_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
. Incorporating these expressions and equation (
47
) back into equation (
46
) gives

\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}}\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\delta_{\text{S,}ij}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}dSdt=-\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\frac{\overleftarrow{A}_{ij}}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}}{\tau}\sigma\right)

(48)

completing the evaluation of the second term on the RHS of equation (
44
).

Finally, performing an analogous calculation for the third term on the RHS of equation (
44
) for the solid surface interfaces
\overrightarrow{A}_{ij}
that lie under phase
\bigcirc
2
and that enter the CV through
S_{\mathrm{cv,br}}
during the advance, and noting again as per equation (
16
) that terms involving the factor
\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}/\tau
can be neglected relative to those that do not include this term, the surface potential energy transport term from equation (
44
) can be written as

\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{1,k}=-\sigma_{12}\cos\theta_{\mathrm{a}}+\overrightarrow{\sigma}-\overleftarrow{\sigma}

(49)

where the compound surface energies associated with the solid interface that is leaving (behind and to the left of the TPCL) and entering (in front and to the right of the TPCL) the advancing CV are defined by

\overleftarrow{\sigma}=\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\frac{\overleftarrow{A}_{ij}}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\overrightarrow{\sigma}=\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\frac{\overrightarrow{A}_{ij}}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}},

(50)

respectively. We note that these compound surface energies incorporate any micro-bubbles or droplets that could possibly be contained within the solid surface roughness. For the leaving energy (
\overleftarrow{\sigma}
) these could be formed during the discussed dissipation events that occur as the TPCL sweeps over rough solid. For the entering energy (
\overrightarrow{\sigma}
) these could be pre-existing within the surface roughness, possibly a result of previous wetting processes (such as receding back over a previously wetted surface).

3.3.5
Examining term
\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{2,k}

This term represents the transport of kinetic energy into the CV during the equilibrium stages, and is given by

\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{2,k}=\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv}}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\rho v^{2}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}})dSdt

(51)

Noting from section
3.2
that velocities are zero within the solid phase and within the equilibrium stages within the fluid are
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}=\mathcal{O}\left(v_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
, and that the fluid areas of
S_{\mathrm{cv,cir}}
and
S_{\mathrm{cv,end}}
have areas of
\mathcal{O}\left(r_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
and
\mathcal{O}\left(r_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}\right)
, respectively, the magnitude of this term is given by

\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{2,k}=\mathcal{O}\left((\tau-\tau_{\mathrm{cap}})\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{3}\frac{(r_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}+l_{\mathrm{cv}}r_{\mathrm{cv}})}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}r_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)

(52)

In deriving the final term in this expression we have used
\mathcal{O}\left(l_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(r_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
and neglected a term containing
\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}/\tau
relative to one that does not, consistent with equation (
16
).

3.3.6
Examining term
\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{3,k}

This term represents the transport of gravitational potential energy into the CV during the equilibrium stages, and is given by

\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{3,k}=\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv}}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\rho\hat{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{cv}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}})dSdt

(53)

In a very similar fashion to the last term this term has a magnitude given by

\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{3,k}=\mathcal{O}\left((\tau-\tau_{\mathrm{cap}})\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}g\frac{r_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\rho gr_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}\right)

(54)

where we have additionally assumed that
\hat{\Phi}=\mathcal{O}\left(gr_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
over
S_{\mathrm{cv,cir}}
and
\hat{\Phi}=\mathcal{O}\left(gl_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
over
S_{\mathrm{cv,end}}
.

3.3.7
Examining term
\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{4,k}

The next term is a contributor to the work that the interfaces outside the CV do on the material inside the CV. Using equations (
26
) and (
30
) this term is given by

\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{4,k}=-\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\delta_{\text{S,}ij}\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{S,}ij}\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{S,}ij}:\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}dSdt

(55)

As the integral contains the surface delta function
\delta_{\text{S,}ij}
, like
\widetilde{T}_{1,k}
, only four component surfaces of
S_{\mathrm{cv}}
give non-zero contributions to the integral: Namely
S_{\mathrm{cv,top}}
,
S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}
,
S_{\mathrm{cv,br}}
and
S_{\mathrm{cv,end}}
. We evaluate
\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{S,}ij}\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{S,}ij}:\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}
on each of these surfaces.

On
S_{\mathrm{cv,top}}
,
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{S,}ij}=0
as here the fluid interface is perfectly flat and normal to the CV boundary (as discussed in section
3.1
) so this surface makes no contribution to equation (
55
). For
S_{\mathrm{cv,bl}}
and
S_{\mathrm{cv,br}}
the interfaces associated with these surface areas have
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}=0
(the solid velocity) at the CV circumference, so these surfaces also make no contribution to
\widetilde{T}_{4,k}
. Finally, over
S_{\mathrm{cv,end}}
, at distances from the TPCL that are much larger than
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
,
\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{S,}ij}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}=0
as like over
S_{\mathrm{cv,top}}
here the fluid interface is flat and normal to the CV boundary. However at distances from the TPCL on
S_{\mathrm{cv,end}}
that are of
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
,
\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{S,}ij}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}
is not zero as in this region the interface has curvature of
\mathcal{O}\left(1/h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
, as described in section
3.1
. Further, within this region the fluid velocity
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}=\mathcal{O}\left(v_{\mathrm{cv}}\right)
as the TPCL may be advancing over the solid here during these equilibrium stages. Hence there is a contribution to the integral in equation (
55
) from the inner part of
S_{\mathrm{cv,end}}
only, giving overall

\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{4,k}=\mathcal{O}\left((\tau-\tau_{\mathrm{cap}})\sigma v_{\mathrm{cv}}\frac{h_{\mathrm{rough}}}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma\frac{h_{\mathrm{rough}}}{l_{\mathrm{cv}}}\right)

(56)

where as per equation (
16
) a term involving the factor
\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}/\tau
has been neglected.

3.3.8
Examining term
\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{5,k}

This term represents the work that the material stresses
\mathsf{T}_{\mathrm{M}}
are doing on the material inside the CV. Using equations (
27
) and (
30
) this term is defined as

\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{5,k}=\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv}}}\mathsf{T}_{\mathrm{M}}:\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}dSdt

(57)

Noting that within the solid
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}=\boldsymbol{0}_{\mathrm{}}
(as discussed section
3.2
), and applying the fluid Newtonian stress equation (
14
) this term can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{5,k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,fluid}}}\widetilde{p}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}dSdt

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{cv}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\int_{\widetilde{\Delta t}_{k}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,fluid}}}\mu\left[\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}+(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}})^{T}\right]:\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}dSdt

(58)

where we have used the identity
\mathsf{I}_{\mathrm{}}:\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}
and
S_{\mathrm{cv,fluid}}
represents the surface of the CV within the fluid phase. Equilibrium stage fluid properties
\widetilde{p}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}
are relevant during these time intervals and are substituted from the steady-state order of magnitude expressions of equations (
19
) and (
17
), respectively, as discussed in section
3.2
, giving

\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{5,k}=-\frac{(\tau-\tau_{\mathrm{cap}})}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}}\times\\
\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,fluid}}}\left[\mathcal{O}\left(\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}+\frac{\mu v_{\mathrm{cv}}}{\max(r,h_{\mathrm{mol}})}+\frac{\sigma}{h_{\mathrm{surround}}}\right)+p_{0}\right]\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}dS\\
+\frac{(\tau-\tau_{\mathrm{cap}})}{X_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}}\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,fluid}}}\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\mu v_{\mathrm{cv}}}{\max(r,h_{\mathrm{mol}})}\right):\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}dS

(59)

For the term involving the reference pressure
p_{0}
, as this is constant at any given time it can come out of the integral, leaving
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{cv}}
which is zero when integrated over
S_{\mathrm{cv,fluid}}
as the fluid is incompressible (using Gauss’ theorem). Hence this reference pressure term does not contribute to
\widetilde{T}_{5,k}
. For the remainder we gather like terms and evaluate in an order of magnitude sense over the circumference and ends of the CV that are within the fluid region as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widetilde{T}_{5,k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left\{(1-\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}}{\tau})\frac{1}{l_{\mathrm{cv}}}\left[\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,cir}}}\left(\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}+\frac{\mu v_{\mathrm{cv}}}{\max(r,h_{\mathrm{mol}})}+\frac{\sigma}{h_{\mathrm{surround}}}\right)dS\right.\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left.\left.+\int_{S_{\mathrm{cv,end}}}\left(\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}+\frac{\mu v_{\mathrm{cv}}}{\max(r,h_{\mathrm{mol}})}+\frac{\sigma}{h_{\mathrm{surround}}}\right)dS\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left\{(1-\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{cap}}}{\tau})\frac{1}{l_{\mathrm{cv}}}\left[\left(\rho v_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}+\frac{\mu v_{\mathrm{cv}}}{r_{\mathrm{cv}}}+\frac{\sigma}{h_{\mathrm{surround}}}\right)r_{\mathrm{cv}}l_{\mathrm{cv}}\right.\right.

3.3.11
Interpretation of specific dissipation event surface energy change
\widehat{\Delta\sigma}

The contact angle energy balance given in equation (
66
) involves the specific dissipation event surface energy change
\widehat{\Delta\sigma}
, which as previously defined is the sum of all surface energy changes occurring within the CV as a result of individual dissipation events (equation (
42
)). Consistent with the physical model of how the contact line advances over the rough solid as outlined in section
3.2
, surface energy is dissipated to heat (via viscous stresses) during each dissipation event resulting in a negative
\widehat{\Delta\sigma}
. Hence an alternative nomenclature for this term that is consistent with this contact line movement model (as discussed in the introduction,
(
Joanny & de Gennes 1984
, e.g.)
) is to define the specific dissipation per projected area of solid traversed due to surface roughness as
D\approx-\widehat{\Delta\sigma}
. In this section we formalise this relationship by performing an order of magnitude analysis on an energy balance conducted over all dissipation events occurring during the advance period to show that the surface energy liberated via
\widehat{\Delta\sigma}
is in fact dissipated to heat during these periods.

In order to perform this dissipation event analysis, we need models for how the materials within the CV behave during the dissipation periods. As for the equilibrium periods, we assume that the solid does not deform (following the justifications from section
3.2
) giving
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}=0
within the solid during these times. For the fluids we require a description of the velocity
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}
, velocity gradient
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}
and pressure
\widehat{p}
existing during these times. For the velocity, noting that the continuous equilibrium velocities still exist within the dissipation periods, and assuming that significant capillary driven velocities only exist within an region of size
\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
local to each dissipation event (as the Reynolds number of these motions is not large), we define

\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}})=\begin{cases}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}+\mathcal{O}\left(v_{\mathrm{cap}}\right)&\text{if }r_{\mathrm{cap}}<\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)\\
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(68)

where
r_{\mathrm{cap}}=|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{cap}}|
is the distance to the centre of the relevant
k
th dissipation event, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{cap}}
is the location of the particular
k
th dissipation event that is centred on the centreline of the CV
3

3

For notational simplicity here and in subsequent dissipation event model definitions we do not indicate what specific dissipation event variables such as

,
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{cap}}

refer to
. For the velocity gradient we similarly define

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}})=\begin{cases}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{v_{\mathrm{cap}}}{\max(r_{\mathrm{cap}},h_{\mathrm{mol}})}\right)&\text{if }r_{\mathrm{cap}}<\mathcal{O}\left(h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)\\

Tracing back through the dissipation energy analysis presented in Appendix

The ratio of these two terms depends on the roughness scale capillary velocity Reynolds number as well as various lengthscale ratios, so that in general neither can assumed to be dominant. The second term directly represents energy conversion to heat during the capillary driven dissipation events, as envisaged. While the first term does not directly relate to energy dissipation, it does represent the transport of energy through the ends of the CV and hence parallel to the TPCL. As the analysis shows that no significant energy transport occurs over the circumference of the CV during the dissipation events, the energy represented by the first term in equation (

to represent the specific energy dissipation per area travelled due to surface roughness.

It should be reiterated however that the discussion in this section does not change the way in which
D

-\widehat{\Delta\sigma}
) is evaluated, being defined solely in terms of surface energy changes that occur over all dissipation events. Indeed a strength of this framework is that

can be calculated based solely on these surface energy changes, rather than needing to know the dynamic and transient details of each individual dissipation event.

4.2
Range of validity

One strength of this energy balance approach for predicting contact angles is that it not only provides a framework for predicting the angle, but also specifies under what conditions the predicted angle will be valid. Specifically, for equation (
73
) to be representative of a real system all conditions specified by equations (
74
) and (
75
) must be satisfied. Continuing the discussion from section
3.2
, we examine what physical limitations these conditions place on applying the theory to the common water/air system, with results shown in Figure
6
.

In terms of lengthscales, evaluating
r_{\mathrm{cv,grav}}
from equation (
80
) requires that
r_{\mathrm{cv}}\ll r_{\mathrm{cv,grav}}=2.7\,\mathrm{mm}
, which is approximately satisfied if we adopt
r_{\mathrm{cv}}=r_{\mathrm{cv,max}}=r_{\mathrm{cv,grav}}/10=0.27\,\mathrm{mm}
for the water system (that is, one order of magnitude less). From the separation of lengthscales equation (
74
) this places an upper constraint on the roughness applicable under the theory of
h_{\mathrm{rough}}\lessapprox 27\,\mathrm{\mu m}
, which is indicated by the right bound of the shaded area in Figure
6

h_{\mathrm{mol}}\ll h_{\mathrm{rough}}
, and given that the molecular size of water is around
0.27\,\mathrm{nm}
this places a lower limit on
h_{\mathrm{rough}}
of around
2.7\,\mathrm{nm}
as indicated by the left bound of the shaded area in Figure
6

100\,\mathrm{nm}
in their analysis citing the influence of van der Waals and double-layer forces at these lengthscales
(
de Gennes 1985
)
: While still continuum forces, these are not accounted for in our hydrodynamically-based framework. Non-continuum effects such as thermal fluctuations that could influence interface topologies at very small lengthscales are also not considered. Hence, while the lower roughness size limit of this theory requires further validation, overall the conclusion is that CAH can be significant even for surfaces that have very small sized roughness (at least in an engineering sense), a conclusion that is supported by experiments
(
Delmas et al. 2011
;

It is interesting that aside from these lengthscale and velocity constraints (discussed below), there is no reference to the absolute size of the surface roughness contained within the energy analysis. While models of

As discussed in the introduction, this is consistent with a growing number of observations
(
Öner & McCarthy 2000

Dorrer & Rühe 2008

). This constraint is represented by equation (
16
). However, it is possible that this constraint could be relaxed if a different approach to the energy balance was adopted: Rather than requiring that the total time for dissipation events be relatively small, an alternative approach that was advanced in earlier versions of this theory
(
Harvie et al. 2008
)
is to locate dissipation events not only in time but also in space within the CV, allowing more than one dissipation event to be occurring within the analysis concurrently. This approach is mathematically more onerous to produce rigorous conditions on theory validity, but is worthy of future research as it could relax the
v_{\mathrm{cv,cap}}
constraint on
v_{\mathrm{cv}}
under conditions where the CV size is of a macroscopic (i.e. mm) size but the solid roughness much smaller. Note that if the
v_{\mathrm{cv,cap}}
restraint were relaxed, the next constraint on the dynamic contact angle would come from
v_{\mathrm{cv,ke}}
which is related to kinetic energy transport through the CV volume, rather than
v_{\mathrm{cv,vis}}
which is concerned with viscous dissipation occurring within the CV volume (and more specifically around the TPCL). Interestingly previous theories have focused on predicting dynamic contact angles from knowledge of viscous or molecular energy dissipation occurring around the TPCL
(
Moffatt 1964
;
Huh & Scriven 1971
;
Voinov 1976
;
de Gennes 1985
;
Petrov & Petrov 1992
)
, rather than considering kinetic energy transport within the TPCL region — in contrast to this framework that suggests that kinetic energy transport is more important than viscous dissipation in predicting dynamic contact angles. Validating this result and producing new models of dynamic contact angle is another area for future research suggested by this energy conservation framework.

4.3
Other model limitations

In addition to the advance velocity, roughness size and CV size constraints discussed in the previous section, other assumptions used in the derivation of the energy balance theory will not be valid for some systems, but could form avenues for future extensions of the theory.

Inherent in the contact angle framework, and enabling multiphase mechanical energy balance presented in section
2
, is the assumption that there is no irreversible work involved in interface creation (or destruction). Physically irreversibilities could be present in a system via chemical or molecular effects associated with interface formation, including effects due to surfactants or other types of surface active molecules. In the context of dynamic contact angle modelling, some work has been done in incorporating molecular dissipation and adsorption when predicting advancing contact angles
(
Blake & Haynes 1969
;
de Gennes 1985
;
Brochard-Wyart & Gennes 1992
;
Mohammad Karim 2022
)
. Following this work and recognising the parallels between these interfacial formation irreversibilities and dissipation due to TPCL movement, it is likely that this irreversible interfacial work will appear in the contact angle energy balance as a dissipation term that is in addition to the hydodynamically based
D
, but this does require reformulation of the general mechanical energy balance and application to the moving contact line CV to show this rigorously. A further assumption used by the model is that the solid is chemically homogeneous. This assumption could be relaxed in a straight-forward manner by including more than one solid interface type in the contact angle energy analysis.

Another limitation of the presented work is that fluids are assumed to be incompressible, and no dissolution or evaporation of the fluids is permitted. Both of these assumptions are physically limiting when one of the phases is a gas. Physically a gas will behave differently to a liquid in cases where a micro-bubble is formed within the solid roughness, caused by the specific dynamics of the fluid interface as it advances over solid defects. Within a formed micro-bubble the laplace pressure will be high (
\mathcal{O}\left(\sigma/h_{\mathrm{rough}}\right)
) which will cause the density of gas to increase and hence the volume of the micro-bubble to decrease. How significant these affects are depends on the bubble size, interfacial tension and gas equation of state. Further, high pressures within the bubble will drive gas dissolution into the surrounding fluid phase, further reducing the volume of the formed micro-bubbles. These changes to the micro-bubble size will in turn affect the leaving compound solid surface energy
\overleftarrow{\sigma}
, affecting the predicted advancing contact angle. In terms of the energy framework, fluid compressibility could be implemented quite easily by relaxing the
\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{}}=0
constraint used when evaluating the boundary pressure work terms. Gas dissolution is more complex however, as this process is transient, and so the framework would need to recognise over what timescale the dissolution process is taking place, and hence where in the energy balances the changes in (particularly) interfacial energy should occur. Micro-droplet evaporation is a related phenomena not accounted for by the theory that could also change
\overleftarrow{\sigma}
and
\overrightarrow{\sigma}
depending on the wetting history of the solid surface and volatility of the fluids. It would be interesting to account for dissolution and evaporation effects within this framework as both introduce a transient or history effect to the wetting behaviour of the system. Again, this suggests avenues for future theoretical and experimental research.

A further limitation of the theory relates to the size of roughness considered. As presented the roughness is characterised by a single lengthscale
h_{\mathrm{rough}}
which represents both the height of surface defects and the spacing between them. An extension to this theory could distinguish between these two lengthscales, allowing better analysis of surfaces composed of a dilute number of strong defects, or surfaces that are gently undulating. A second assumption relating to the size of the defects is that only one lengthscale is considered in the analysis, whereas real surfaces (particularly biological inspired super-hydrophobic surfaces) can possess a hierarchical range of roughnesses
(
Feng et al. 2002
)
. A potential extension of the theory would be to use a cascade of CV sizes to predict the contact angles at each lengthscale, with the equilibrium contact angles used at each lengthscale determined from contact angle predictions performed using smaller lengthscale CVs. In this way the macroscopic contact angle could be determined from knowledge of roughness topologies at each lengthscale.

Finally the stress model for the solid material used in this study effectively implies that there are no energy changes occurring within the CV that are related to the solid phase. More complex solid stress models could certainly be incorporated into the framework, representing (for example) the advance of fluids over soft or semi-liquid materials. Introducing deformable solids would not only introduce additional dissipation, but would also require additional dynamical modelling or measuring of the fluid, solid and interfacial behaviours occurring around the TPCL. This is also the topic of on-going work.

5
Conclusion

Starting from a statement of momentum conservation, a mechanical energy conservation framework has been derived that allows the contact angle hysteresis (CAH) range to be predicted from knowledge of the interfacial dynamics that occur around an advancing three phase contact line (TPCL). Unlike most previous works the analysis is not specific to a particular wetting regime (e.g., Cassie or Wenzel) or particular surface structure (e.g., holes, poles, periodic, dilute etc). The analysis also resolves a number of questions about wetting on rough surfaces that have been the source of confusion in the literature:

•

A suitable control volume (CV) can be defined that is anchored to the TPCL around which mechanical energy conservation can be rigorously performed. This shows that at least for the static CAH range, and conditional upon the CV lengthscale and speed satisfying four conditions (i.e., equations (
77
-
80
)), the advancing and receding angles are independent of the surrounding flow. This is significant as it means that the energy of the entire flow system (e.g., the entire drop) need not be considered when predicting CAH, resolving extensive discussion within the field
(
Gao & McCarthy 2007
;
McHale 2007
;
Gao & McCarthy 2007
;
Nosonovsky 2007
;
Panchagnula & Vedantam 2007
;
Marmur 2022
)
.

•

By adopting semi-quantitative models for how the fluids and phase interfaces behave within the advancing CV that are based on experimental observation, we have shown from energy conservation that CAH directly results from the ‘pinning’ and ‘depinning’ or ‘jumping’ of the fluid interface, deriving a general expression for this dissipation in terms of surface areas pre and post each jumping event. No dissipation or CAH results from contact line distortion (in isolation), showing (in the language of
Joanny & de Gennes 1984
) that only ‘strong’ defects (as opposed to ‘weak’ defects) cause CAH. This is consistent with the initial observations of
Joanny & de Gennes 1984
but conflicts with some subsequent studies
(
Pomeau & Vannimenus 1985
;
Robbins & Joanny 1987
;
Öpik 2000
)
.

•

Studies that consider how a fluid interface advances over a rough surface have previously inferred what the macroscopic CAH range is from the results, but without clear justification
(
Cox 1983
)
. The present work shows how interfacial and energy analyses can be related: Namely, interfacial dynamic models can be used to predict dissipation and generated (leaving) compound solid surface energies which can then be fed back into the derived energy conservation framework to predict the CAH range. Only for specific simple systems will the CAH range inferred from interfacial analysis match that predicted via energy conservation and observed at the macroscopic scale.

•

While not the central focus of this study, the energy conservation analysis suggests that when calculating dynamic contact angles kinetic energy transport around the TPCL is at least as significant as hydrodynamic energy dissipation occurring around the TPCL. This requires more theoretical and experimental confirmation, but could explain some of the discrepancies between existing dynamic contact angle models and experimental observation
(
Mohammad Karim 2022
)
.

Many limitations of the framework have also been discussed and could form the basis of future extensions, including considering compressible, evaporating or dissolving fluids, irreversible work associated with interface creation or destruction, solid chemical heterogeneity, soft solids, and non-negligible CV advance speeds. These are topics of on-going work in our group.
