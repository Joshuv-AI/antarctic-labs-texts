Computationally efficient orthogonalization for pairwise comparisons method
Julio Benítez, Waldemar W. Koczkodaj, Adam Kowalczyk

Abstract
Orthogonalization is one of few mathematical methods conforming to mathematical standards for approximation. Finding a consistent PC matrix of a given an inconsistent PC matrix is the main goal of a pairwise comparisons method. We introduce an orthogonalization for pairwise comparisons matrix based on a generalized Frobenius inner matrix product. The proposed theory is supported by numerous examples and visualizations. Keywords: pairwise comparison, orthogonalization, orthogonal basis, inner matrix product, approximation, group theory.



1
Introduction

Assessing the best alternative is vital for practically every decision-making process. For it, a pairwise comparisons method is often used. Its introduction is attributed to R. Llull in 13th century (see,
[
6
]
).
By comparing the relative importance of criteria in pairs,
a pairwise comparisons (PC or PCs depending on the context) matrix
M
is created. Its entries,
m_{ij}
, are ratios (computed or assessed) of the compared entities
E_{i}
and
E_{j}
. Entities could be physical (e.g., areas of shapes) or abstract (e.g., software quality) concepts. The accuracy of PC method was analyzed in a Monte Carlo study (see
[
8
]
).

In the final step of the pairwise comparisons method, a vector of priorities is computed. Coordinates of this vector are the weights of alternatives. The largest coordinate corresponds to the best alternative. For practical use, it should be normalized to a total of one. It reflects the percentage contribution of individual coordinates.

Each entry
m_{ij}>0
of a PC matrix measures the preference of alternative
i
over alternative
j
, and if
we use multiplicative comparisons, one has
m_{ji}=1/m_{ij}
(called
reciprocity
). Evidently,
m_{ii}=1
since an alternative
i
is equal to itself.

More often than not, the inconsistency of human experts occurs in a PC matrix for the size
n\geq 3
. This means that for a PC matrix, it is very probable that there exists a cycle of alternatives indexed by
i,j,k
such that
m_{ij}m_{jk}\neq m_{ik}
. Therefore, inconsistency analysis is needed.

By using a logarithm mapping applied to each PC matrix
M
, we create PC matrix
A
. The conditions
m_{ji}=1/m_{ij}
and
m_{ij}m_{jk}=m_{ik}
are transformed into linear conditions
a_{ji}=-a_{ij}
and
a_{ij}+a_{jk}=a_{ik}
. It enables us to use linear algebra methods.
One of the tools for dealing with the inconsistency of the pairwise comparison matrices is to find the closest consistent matrix under a suitable distance in the set of
n\times n
matrices. If we define an inner product in the set of
n\times n
PC matrices, this closest consistent matrix can be found by an orthogonal projection onto a consistent subspace of PC matrices.
To find this projection, we find a basis of the aforementioned consistent subspace. If such a basis
{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{k}

is orthogonal, the projection of any vector
{\bf v}
is:

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\langle{\bf v},{\bf v}_{i}\rangle}{\|{\bf v}_{i}\|^{2}}{\bf v}_{i}.

The first orthogonal basis for computing a consistent approximation to a PC matrix was proposed in
[
10
]
. Subsequently, it was improved in
[
11
]
.

This presentation is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we analyze additively consistent PC matrices
of order
n
, denoted by
l_{n}
. Moreover,
we generalize the standard Frobenius inner product (i.e,
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle=\tr(AB^{T})
) obtaining a modified
inner product depending on an arbitrary positive definite matrix
W
(i.e.,
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle_{W}=\tr(AWB^{T})
),
where
\tr(\cdot)
stands for the trace of a matrix.
It generates the decomposition

g_{n}=l_{n}\perp h_{n,W}
, where
g_{n}
is the subset composed of
n\times n
skew-symmetric matrices.

In Section
3
, we obtain a
W
-orthogonal basis of
l_{n}
.
In Section
4
, we get a characterization of
h_{n,W}
which determines a PC matrix belongs to
h_{n,W}
.

In Section
5
, we use graph theory to propose a (non-orthogonal) basis of
h_{n}
. No computation is needed to find this kind of basis.

2
Theoretical foundations for decomposition of PC matrices

Let us denote by
M_{n,n}
the set of
n\times m
matrices and by
M_{n,n}^{+}
the subset of
M_{n,n}
of positive matrices.
We will consider any vector of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a column (i.e., a matrix in
M_{n,1}
). A matrix
A=[a_{ij}]\in M_{n,n}^{+}
is called

consistent
if
a_{ij}a_{jk}=a_{ik}
for all
1\leq i,j,k\leq n
and is called
reciprocal
if
a_{ji}=1/a_{ij}
for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Any consistent PC matrix is reciprocal but not all reciprocal PC matrices are consistent.
If
U
and
W
are subspaces of the vector space
V
, we denote
by
V=U\perp W
the situation
V=U\oplus W
together
U=W^{\perp}
.

For
A=[a_{ij}]\in M_{n,n}^{+}
, we can define PC matrix
\mu(A)=[\ln(a_{ij})]
. Also, given
B\in M_{n,n}
, we can define the matrix

\varphi(B)=[\exp(b_{ij})]
. Evidently, if
A\in M_{n,n}^{+}
, then
A
is reciprocal if and only if
\mu(A)
is skew-symmetric.

If
M
is a reciprocal PC matrix (i.e.,
m_{ji}=1/m_{ij}
for all

i,j
), then
A=\mu(M)
satisfies
a_{ji}=-a_{ij}
for all
i,j
.
And, If
M
is a consistent PC matrix (i.e.,
m_{ij}m_{jk}=m_{ik}

for all
i,j,k
), then
A=\mu(M)
satisfies
a_{ij}+a_{jk}=a_{ik}
for all
i,j,k
.

By using a logarithm mapping applied to each mulitiplicative PC matrix
M
, we can create an additive PC matrix
A
.
If a matrix
M
is consistent, then the elements of
A=\mu(M)
satisfy

a_{ik}+a_{kj}=a_{ij}

for every
i,j,k

\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Such matrix
A
is named as
additively consistent
.

Let us denote by
U_{n}
the
n\times n
matrix having all its components equal to 1.
For every dimension
n>0
, the following group

G_{n}:=\{A\in M_{n,n}^{+}:A\cdot A^{T}=U_{n}\}

is an abelian group of
n\times n
matrices with the operation

\begin{array}[]{c}\cdot:G_{n}\times G_{n}\rightarrow G_{n}\\
(A,B)\rightarrow A\cdot B=[a_{ij}\cdot b_{ij}]\end{array}

where “
\cdot
” is the Hadamard product. Furthermore,
G_{n}
is a Lie group; its tangent space
T_{U_{n}}(G_{n})
of

G_{n}
at unity
U_{n}
consists of all
n\times n
skew-symmetric matrices (admitting a natural structure of a linear space and also of a Lie algebra):

g_{n}:=\{{\mu(A):A\in G_{n}}\}=\{{B\in M_{n,n}:\ b_{ij}+b_{ji}=0}\}.

The reader is encouraged to consult
[
12
]
for a more comprehensive treatment of Lie groups and Lie algebras of PC matrices.
The dimension of
g_{n}
is
\frac{1}{2}(n^{2}-n)
.

For the subspace
l_{n}
composed of additively consistent PC matrices:

l_{n}:=\{B\in g_{n}:\ b_{ij}+b_{jk}+b_{ki}=0\}

The following fundamental equivalences hold:

\begin{split}B\in l_{n}&\iff\varphi(B)\text{ is consistent}\\
&\iff\text{ there exist }w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}>0\text{ such that }[\varphi(B)]_{ij}=w_{i}/w_{j}\\
&\iff\text{ there exist }v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\text{ such that }[B]_{ij}=v_{i}-v_{j}.\end{split}

(2.1)

Obviously, every additively consistent matrix
A=[a_{ij}]
is skew symmetric, i.e.,
a_{ij}=-a_{ji}
for all
i,j
.

Let us use the linear mapping
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to M_{n,n}
defined by:

f_{n}(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}-\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{v}^{T},

(2.2)

where
\mathbf{1}_{n}=[1,\ldots,1]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. From definition we have
[f(\mathbf{v})]_{ij}=v_{i}-v_{j}
.

The last of the fundamental equivalences implies that
l_{n}
is the image subspace of the mapping
f_{n}
. Evidently, the kernel of
f_{n}
is the subspace spanned by
\mathbf{1}_{n}
. Consequently,
\dim l_{n}=\dim\im f_{n}=\dim\mathbb{R}^{n}-\dim\ker f_{n}=n-1
.

The basic properties of the trace operator are as follows:

\tr:M_{n,n}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

a)

The trace is a linear operator, i.e.,
\tr(A+B)=\tr(A)+\tr(B)
and
\tr(\alpha A)=\alpha\tr(A)

for any
A,B\in M_{n,n}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

b)

\tr(A)=\tr(A^{T})
for any
A\in M_{n,n}
.

c)

If
A\in M_{n,m}
and
B\in B_{m,n}
, then
\tr(AB)=\tr(BA)
.

The third property will be frequently used for
m=1
since
BA
is a scalar.

The characterization of
l_{n}
given by the existence of a (ranking) vector as in (
2.1
) is the most important fact for many applications. The existence of such an (exact) vector is rather exceptional however, and the best one may hope for is an approximal existence of it.

In order to formalize this, let us consider a linear subspace of matrices
\mathcal{L}
such that

l_{n}\subset\mathcal{L}\subset M_{n,n}

and a quadratic norm

\|\cdot\|^{2}:\mathcal{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad A\mapsto\|A\|^{2}:=\langle A|A\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
is is an inner product on
\mathcal{L}
. Given a matrix
M\in\mathcal{L}
,
we wish to find its closest consistent approximation as follows:

M^{*}=\{A\in l_{n}:\|A-M\|^{2}\text{ is minimal}\}.

(2.3)

It is well known (see any standard book on linear algebra) that
M^{*}
is equal to the unique matrix in
l_{n}
,
being the
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
-orthogonal projection of
M
onto
l_{n}
.

Let us consider the special case where
\|\cdot\|
is the Frobenius norm, i.e.,

\langle A|B\rangle=\tr(AB^{T})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}b_{ij},

for each
A=(a_{ij}),B=(b_{ij})\in\mathcal{L}
. In such a case the solution of the optimization (
2.3
)
can be given in a closed form by the row averages of
M
(see
[
3
]
):

M^{*}=\frac{1}{n}f_{n}(M\mathbf{1}_{n}).

Equivalently, using the coordinate representation for
M=[m_{ij}]
, we have

m_{ij}^{*}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(m_{jk}-m_{ik}),\quad\forall 1\leq i,j\leq n.

However, for a generic inner product on
\mathcal{L}
, the direct computation of
M^{*}
could be very challenging,
especially, if dimensionality
n
becomes high. This is the case of some applications to genomics where
n
is measured
in multiple thousands. The following fundamental result shows a path for constructive solution to such a challenge.

Theorem 2.2
.

Given a linear
\mathcal{L}
subspace of matrices containing consistent matrices, an inner product

\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
on
\mathcal{L}
and a matrix
M\in\mathcal{L}
.

(i)

There exists an inner product
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that for any set of

\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle_{n}
-orthonormal vectors
\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
which are

\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle_{n}
-orthogonal to vector
\mathbf{1}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the matrices

B_{1}=f_{n}(\mathbf{e}_{1}),\ldots,B_{n-1}=f_{n}(\mathbf{e}_{n-1})

(2.4)

form an
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
-orthonormal basis for the linear subspace
l_{n}
of
\mathcal{L}
.

(ii)

The solution of the optimization (
2.3
) for the closest consistent approximation has the form

M^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\langle M|B_{i}\rangle B_{i}.

Proof.

The proof of (i) is given by construction. As we mentioned before, the kernel of
f_{n}
is the subspace
spanned by
\mathbf{1}_{n}
. We define the mapping
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\langle\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y}\rangle_{n}=(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n})(\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n})+\langle f_{n}(\mathbf{x})|f_{n}(\mathbf{y})\rangle.

(2.5)

It is obvious that this mapping is symmetric and bilinear:

\langle\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y}\rangle_{n}=\langle\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}\rangle_{n},\qquad\langle c\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y}\rangle_{n}=c\langle\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y}\rangle_{n},\qquad\langle\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{y}\rangle_{n}=\langle\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y}\rangle_{n}\langle\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{y}\rangle_{n}.

for any vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. It is also positive definite, since for any
vector
{\bf 0}\neq\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\langle\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}\rangle_{n}=(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n})^{2}+\langle f_{n}(\mathbf{x})|f_{n}(\mathbf{x})\rangle>0,

since both terms on the right-hand-side of the equation above are always nonnegative and either:

(a)

f_{n}(\mathbf{x})\neq 0
in which case
\langle f(\mathbf{x})|f(\mathbf{x})\rangle>0
since
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
is
an inner product on
\mathcal{L}
, or

(b)

\mathbf{x}=c\mathbf{1}_{n}\in\ker(f_{n})
,
c\neq 0
and
(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n})^{2}=n^{2}c^{2}>0
.

We conclude that
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle_{n}
is an inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In such a case orthonormal vectors

\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,n-1
, as in (
2.4
) always exist, and for any such selection, by the
definition of the mapping
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
given in (
2.5
), we have

\langle f_{n}(\mathbf{e}_{i})|f_{n}(\mathbf{e}_{j})\rangle=\langle\mathbf{e}_{i}|\mathbf{e}_{j}\rangle_{n}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i=j,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

for any
1\leq i,j\leq j\leq n-1
.

This completes the proof of part (i) of the theorem. The part (ii) follows from (i) using a well-known argument
used in linear algebra or optimization theory (Fourier expansion in a finite dimensional vector space).
∎

If
W
is a symmetric positive definite
n\times n
matrix,
then
{\bf u}^{T}W{\bf v}
defines an inner product for
{\bf u},{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(for example, see
[
15
, Example 7.3]
). The aforementioned observations extend to modification of the standard Frobenius inner product in
the space of
n\times n
matrices as follows:
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle=\tr(AB^{T})
.

We will use the following inner product in
M_{n,n}
:

\left\langle A,B\right\rangle_{W}=\tr(AWB^{T}),

where
W
is a positive definite matrix. For
W=I_{n}
(
I_{n}
is the identity matrix of order
n
),
this inner product reduces to the standard Frobenius inner product, and for the sake of the brevity, we will
denote
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle=\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{I_{n}}
.
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{W}
is indeed an inner product.

The use of this matrix
W
is justified because some comparisons may be important than others.
This kind of weighting can be done by a variance-covariance matrix
W
. This matrix is definite positive and the
meaning of the entry
w_{ij}
is the covariance between entities
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
. Observe that
the covariance between
X_{i}
and
X_{i}
is the variance of
X_{i}
. The largest
w_{ii}
is, the more important
is the assessment
i
is. If
w_{ij}>0
(
w_{ij}<0
), then the assessment
i
and
j
are positive (negative)
correlated:
the more important the assessment
i
is, the more (less) important the assessment
j
is.

We will consider several inner products in
M_{n,n}
calling matrices
A
and
B

W
-orthogonal for
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle_{W}=0
, and simply call
A
and
B
orthogonal for
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle=0
.

We will denote
h_{n,W}
the orthogonal complement in
g_{n}
of
l_{n}
by using the aforementioned inner product

\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{W}
. When
W=I_{n}
, we will simply denote
h_{n}
. Observe that
g_{n}=l_{n}\oplus h_{n,W}
.
A study of some algebraic properties of the subspaces
g_{n}
,
l_{n}
, and
h_{n}
was done in
[
7
]
.

Let us define:

L_{n}:=\varphi(l_{n})\qquad\text{and}\qquad H_{n,W}:=\varphi(h_{n,W})

getting
G_{n}\simeq L_{n}\times H_{n,W}
.

In
[
12
, Theorem 6.4]
, this decomposition is done for
W=I
and
n=3
.
Consequently, we have:

g_{n}=l_{n}\perp h_{n,W}.

For
W=I_{n}
, the subspace
h_{n}
was analyzed by Barzilai in
[
2
]
, where he called this kind of matrices
as totally inconsistent.

Recalling that the orthogonal complement is taken in
g_{n}
, the dimension of
h_{n,W}=l_{n}^{\bot}
is:

\dim g_{n}-\dim l_{n}=\frac{n^{2}-n}{2}-(n-1)=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}.

We summarize the fundamental dimensions:

\dim g_{n}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2},\qquad\dim l_{n}=n-1,\qquad\dim h_{n,W}=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2},

W
-orthogonal basis of
l_{n}

In this section, we will find a
W
-orthogonal basis of
l_{n}
. It will allow us projecting (orthogonally) any matrix of

g_{n}
onto
h_{n,W}
and
l_{n}
. By using the the elementwise exponential mapping
\varphi
, we can decompose an arbitrary reciprocal PC matrix.

We can establish a bijection from the set of
n\times n
skew-symmetric matrices to
\mathbb{R}^{n(n-1)/2}
in the following way:

B=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&b_{12}&b_{13}&\cdots&b_{1n}\\
-b_{12}&0&b_{23}&\cdots&b_{2n}\\
-b_{13}&-b_{23}&0&\cdots&b_{3n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-b_{1n}&-b_{2n}&-b_{3n}&\cdots&0\end{array}\right]\cong[b_{12},b_{13},\ldots,b_{n-1,n}]^{T}.

(3.1)

The standard Frobenius inner product of skew-symmetric
n\times n
matrices corresponds to the standard inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{(n^{2}-n)/2}
as we show by the next theorem:

Let
B=[b_{ij}]
and
C=[c_{ij}]
be matrices. It is well known that the Frobenius inner product satisfies

\left\langle B,C\right\rangle=\tr(BC^{T})=\sum_{ij}b_{ij}c_{ij}
. So, if in addition
B
and
C
are skew-symmetric, then

\left\langle B,C\right\rangle=2\sum_{i<j}b_{ij}c_{ij}
.

Therefore, the concepts of orthogonality and the orthogonal projection are the same if we
consider the inner product
\left\langle B,C\right\rangle=\tr(BC^{T})
or via the isomorphism given in (
3.1
).

Subsequently, we will find a
W
-orthogonal basis of
l_{n}
. To this end, we need the following lemma
(the special case
W=I_{n}
was given in
[
4
]
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let the mapping
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to M_{n,n}
be defined as in (
2.2
). Let

\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
W
be a positive definite matrix in
M_{n,n}
. Then

\left\langle f_{n}(\mathbf{v}),f_{n}(\mathbf{w})\right\rangle_{W}=(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}W\mathbf{1}_{n})\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{v}-(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}W\mathbf{w})(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}\mathbf{v})-(\mathbf{v}^{T}W\mathbf{1}_{n})(\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n})+n(\mathbf{v}^{T}W\mathbf{w}).

For
W=I_{n}
, the above expression is reduced to:

\left\langle f_{n}(\mathbf{v}),f_{n}(\mathbf{w})\right\rangle_{W}=2n\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{w}-2(\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n})(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n}).

Proof.

Recall that
\mathbf{x}^{T}W\mathbf{y}
is a scalar for arbitrary
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We have

\begin{split}\langle f_{n}(\mathbf{v})&,f_{n}(\mathbf{w})\rangle_{W}=\tr(f_{n}(\mathbf{v})Wf_{n}(\mathbf{w})^{T})\\
&=\tr\left[(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}-\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{v}^{T})W(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}-\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{w}^{T})^{T}\right]\\
&=\tr\left[\mathbf{v}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}W\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{w}^{T}-\mathbf{v}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}W\mathbf{w}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}-\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{v}^{T}W\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{w}^{T}+\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{v}^{T}W\mathbf{w}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}\right]\\
&=(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}W\mathbf{1}_{n})\tr(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{w}^{T})-(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}W\mathbf{w})\tr(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T})-\\
&\phantom{=(}-(\mathbf{v}^{T}W\mathbf{1}_{n})\tr(\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{w}^{T})+(\mathbf{v}^{T}W\mathbf{w})\tr(\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T})\\
&=(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}W\mathbf{1}_{n})\tr(\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{v})-(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}W\mathbf{w})\tr(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}\mathbf{v})\\
&\phantom{=(}-(\mathbf{v}^{T}W\mathbf{1}_{n})\tr(\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n})+(\mathbf{v}^{T}W\mathbf{w})\tr(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n})\\
&=(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}W\mathbf{1}_{n})\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{v}-(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}W\mathbf{w})(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}\mathbf{v})-(\mathbf{v}^{T}W\mathbf{1}_{n})(\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n})+n(\mathbf{v}^{T}W\mathbf{w}).\end{split}

The proof is finished.
∎

\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(3.2)

Formula
3.2

Theorem 3.2
.

Let us define the mapping
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to M_{n,n}
as in (
2.2
).
Let
W
be a positive definite matrix in
M_{n,n}
.
Let
\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be non-zero vectors orthogonal to
\mathbf{1}_{n}
.
Define
M=(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}W\mathbf{1}_{n})I_{n}+nW
.

a)

If
\mathbf{y}_{i}M\mathbf{y}_{j}=0
for any
i\neq j
, then

\{f_{n}(\mathbf{y}_{1}),\ldots,f_{n}(\mathbf{y}_{n-1})\}
is a
W
-orthogonal basis of
l_{n}
.

b)

If
\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n-1}\}
is an orthogonal basis of the orthogonal complement of
\mathbf{1}_{n}
in

4

The following theorem characterizes the subset

(in
g_{n}

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
W
be a positive definite matrix in
M_{n,n}
and let
B\in M_{n,n}
be a skew-symmetric matrix. Then

a)

B\in h_{n,W}
if and only if
BW\mathbf{1}_{n}={\bf 0}
.

b)

B\in h_{n}
if and only if
B\mathbf{1}_{n}={\bf 0}
.

Proof.

First, let us prove item b).
Let us define
\mathbf{b}=B\mathbf{1}_{n}
. We have for any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\begin{split}\left\langle B,f_{n}(\mathbf{v})\right\rangle&=\tr(Bf_{n}(\mathbf{v})^{T})=\tr\left(B(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}-\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{v}^{T})^{T}\right)=\tr\left(B(\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{v}^{T}-\mathbf{v}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T})\right)\\
&=\tr(B\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{v}^{T})-\tr(B\mathbf{v}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T})=\tr(\mathbf{b}\mathbf{v}^{T})-\tr(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}B)\\
&=\tr(\mathbf{b}\mathbf{v}^{T})+\tr(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}B^{T})=\tr(\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{b})+\tr(\mathbf{v}(B\mathbf{1}_{n})^{T})\\
&=\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{b}+\tr(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{b}^{T})=\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{b}+\tr(\mathbf{b}^{T}\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{b}^{T}\mathbf{v}=2\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{b}.\end{split}

Therefore, we have, in view of
l_{n}=\im f_{n}
and
h_{n}=l_{n}^{\perp}

\begin{split}B\in h_{n}&\iff 0=\left\langle B,f_{n}(\mathbf{v})\right\rangle\text{ for all }\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
&\iff 0=\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{b}\text{ for all }\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\iff\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}\iff B\mathbf{1}_{n}=\mathbf{0}.\end{split}

To prove a), let us note that
\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle_{W}=\tr(XWY^{T})=\left\langle XW,Y\right\rangle
.

\begin{split}B\in h_{n,W}&\iff 0=\left\langle B,f_{n}(\mathbf{v})\right\rangle_{W}\text{ for all }\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
&\iff 0=\left\langle BW,f_{n}(\mathbf{v})\right\rangle\text{ for all }\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\iff BW\in h_{n}\iff BW\mathbf{1}_{n}={\mathbf{0}}.\end{split}

The theorem is proved. ∎

6
Conclusions

A generalized Frobenius inner product was proposed for orthogonalization of a PC matrix. A
W
-orthogonal basis of
l_{n}
can be obtained in a simpler way (see Section
3
).
The proposed orthogonalization creates new opportunities for applications.

A (non-orthogonal) basis of
h_{n}
can be obtained (without computational effort) by using the proposed procedure specified in Subsection
5.1
. Subsequently, the classic Gram-Schmidt process can be used. Orthogonalizalion is one of few mathematical methods conforming to mathematical standards for approximation of an inconsistent PC matrix by a consistent PC matrix. However, simple heuristics may be used for complex systems requiring immediate solutions as demonstrated in
[
9
]
.
