Abstract
Reed–Muller (RM) codes are known for their good ML ( ML ) performance in the short block-length regime. Despite being one of the oldest classes of channel codes, finding a low complexity soft-input decoding scheme is still an open problem. In this work, we present a versatile decoding architecture for RM codes based on their rich automorphism group. The decoding algorithm can be seen as a generalization of multiple-bases belief propagation (MBBP) and may use any polar or RM decoder as constituent decoders. We provide extensive error-rate performance simulations for successive cancellation (SC)-, SC-list (SCL)- and belief propagation (BP)-based constituent decoders. We furthermore compare our results to existing decoding schemes and report a near-ML performance for the RM(3,7)-code (e.g., 0.04 0.04 dB away from the ML bound at BLER of 10 − 3 10^{-3} ) at a competitive computational cost. Moreover, we provide some insights into the automorphism subgroups of RM codes and SC decoding and, thereby, prove the theoretical limitations of this method with respect to polar codes.

Preliminaries

II-A

Reed–Muller Codes

RM
codes were first introduced in 1954 by David E. Muller as an algebraic coding scheme
[
1
]
with their first efficient decoding algorithm introduced in the same year by Irving Reed
[
2
]
. The basic idea is to treat codewords as the evaluation of polynomials. For an
RM
code, each message is interpreted as a multilinear polynomial
u(\mathbf{z})
in
m
binary variables
z_{i}
, where
i\in\{0,\cdots,m-1\}
, and maximum degree
r
(usually called the order of the
RM
code), over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. This can be written as

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=u(z_{0},\cdots,z_{m-1})=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}u_{i}\cdot g_{i}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{g}^{T}(\mathbf{z}),

(1)

where
\mathbf{g}^{T}(\mathbf{z})
denotes the vector of length
k
containing all monomials of maximum degree
r
in descending order. The number of such monomials is given as

k=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{m}{i}=\binom{m}{0}+\binom{m}{1}+\cdots+\binom{m}{r}.

(2)

To obtain a codeword, the message polynomial is evaluated at all points in the space
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
, resulting in
N=2^{m}
codeword bits. Alternatively, the
\mathbf{g}^{T}(\mathbf{z})
may be evaluated to form the generator matrix
\mathbf{G}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{eval}\left(u(\mathbf{z})\right)=\mathbf{u}\cdot\underbrace{\operatorname{eval}\left(\mathbf{g}^{T}(\mathbf{z})\right)}_{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{G}.

(3)

To easily relate
RM
and polar codes, we introduce this evaluation in reverse binary order
2
2

2

This does not change the code, as reversing the bit order is contained in the automorphism group of RM codes.
, i.e., the vector
\mathbf{z}
is associated with the codeword bit
x_{i}
with
i=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(1-z_{j})\cdot 2^{j}
. That way, the generator matrix
\mathbf{G}
can be obtained by selecting those rows of the
N\times N
Hadamard matrix
\mathbf{G}_{N}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}1&0\\
1&1\end{array}\right]^{\otimes m}
that have an index with a Hamming weight of at least
m-r
, where
(\cdot)^{\otimes m}
denotes the
m
-th Kronecker power of a matrix
[
16
]
. In the polar coding context, the position of these rows are referred to as the
information bit positions
, while the position of the removed rows are the
frozen bit positions
.

II-B

Polar Codes

The cornerstone upon which polar codes are constructed is the channel polarization concept, where, initially,
N
copies of a
DMC
(
DMC
) are converted by recursive application of a
channel transform
into
N
synthetic channels. Those synthetic channels show a polarization behavior in being either sufficiently good (i.e., noiseless) to hold the information bits for transmission, or sufficiently poor (i.e., noisy) to not carrying any information at all and, thus, set to a fixed value known for both transmitter and receiver, hence
frozen
. The above statement only holds strictly as the code length
N=2^{m}
gets larger assuming
SC
decoding.
Throughout this work, we set the value of the frozen-bit positions to “0”.

Without loss of generality, a
polar code
refers to the set of synthetic bit-channels used for information transmission or, equivalently, their complementary set of bit-channels set to be frozen, denoted by the information set
\mathbb{A}
and the frozen set
\bar{\mathbb{A}}
, respectively. Eventually, the indices of the information set refers to the rows selected from
\mathbf{G}_{N}
(which is also called the
polarization matrix
in the context of polar codes), that constitute the
k\times N
polar code generator matrix.

According to this view of polar codes,
RM
and polar codes differ only in the row-selection criterion from
\mathbf{G}_{N}
. Therefore one can infer that the row-selection criterion of the
RM
codes optimizes the code performance under
MAP
(
MAP
) decoding, whereas that of polar codes optimizes the code performance under
SC
decoding. For that,
RM
codes are characterized by a better
MAP
threshold when compared to polar codes, while, however, decoders that manage to approach the RM code’s MAP threshold are of impractical complexity, which leaves the door open for further research in finding a practical
RM
decoding strategy that achieves the RM code’s MAP performance
[
24
]
.

In the same way as
RM
codes, polar codes can be viewed in terms of evaluation of polynomials
[
25
]
. There is a one-to-one mapping from rows
i
of
\mathbf{G}_{N}
to monomials in
m
variables given by

i\mapsto\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(z_{j})^{1-i_{j}},

(4)

where
i_{j}
denotes the
j
-th bit in the binary representation of
i
, i.e.,
i=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}i_{j}\cdot 2^{j}
. A polar code is then defined according to Eq. (
3
) by a monomial vector
\mathbf{g}^{T}(\mathbf{z})
that contains the monomials corresponding to the information set
\mathbb{A}
. We further define
\mathcal{M}_{m}
as the set of all monomials in
m
variables and denote by
I~=~\left\{{g_{i}(\mathbf{z})}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{m}
the (unordered) set of monomials of a polar code. Hence,
\mathbb{A}
,
I
and
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{z})
are all equivalent representations of the same polar code.

Moreover, most practical polar code designs fulfill the so-called partial ordering of the monomials (and therefore synthetic channels) making them
decreasing monomial codes
. These codes are the only group of polar codes where algebraic properties are known and therefore of particular value for algebraic decoding.
We quickly recapitulate the definition of decreasing monomial codes as given in
[
25
]
:

Definition (Decreasing Monomial Code):
A polar code with monomial set
I
is said to be a
decreasing monomial code
, if

\forall g\in I,\forall f\in\mathcal{M}_{m}\text{ with }f\preccurlyeq g\Rightarrow f\in I

(5)

holds, i.e., if a synthetic channel carries information, then all
better
channels (according to the partial order) must also be nonfrozen. This partial order ‘
\preccurlyeq
’ of monomials is defined as

z_{i_{1}}\cdots z_{i_{s}}\preccurlyeq z_{j_{1}}\cdots z_{j_{s}}\Leftrightarrow i_{k}\leq j_{k}\forall k

(6)

for monomials of equal degree and

\displaystyle f\preccurlyeq g\Leftrightarrow\exists g^{*}|g

\displaystyle\text{ with }\operatorname{deg}(g^{*})=\operatorname{deg}(f)\text{ and }f\preccurlyeq g^{*}

(7)

if
\operatorname{deg}(f)<\operatorname{deg}(g)
. For more details on decreasing monomial codes, we refer the interested reader to
[
25
]
.

Polar Code Automorphisms

To this point, the automorphism group of general polar codes is unknown. However, for decreasing monomial codes (i.e., practically relevant polar codes), a subset of the automorphism group is known to be the lower-triangular affine group LTA(
m
). It is characterized by pairs
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})
, where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m\times m}

m

m

m

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}^{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{U}

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{U}

(10)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{U})^{T}=\mathbf{U}^{T}\mathbf{L}^{T}(\mathbf{P}^{-1})^{T}=\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\tilde{\mathbf{U}}\tilde{\mathbf{P}},

\displaystyle L_{i,s}

\displaystyle=L_{i,s+1}\boxplus L_{i+2^{s},s+1},

(14)

\displaystyle L_{i+2^{s},s}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{u_{i,s}}\cdot L_{i,s+1}+L_{i+2^{s},s+1},

(15)

where
L_{i,s}
is the soft
LLR
value of the
i^{th}
node at stage
s
, and the so-called
box-plus
function is defined as

a\boxplus b\triangleq\log\left(\dfrac{e^{a+b}+1}{e^{a}+e^{b}}\right).

(16)

The recursion terminates at the left-most stage (i.e., in the leaf nodes) where either a hard decision on the
LLR
is made (for information bits) or the frozen bit value 0 is returned (for frozen bits), which can be expressed as

\displaystyle u_{i,0}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&i\in\bar{\mathbb{A}}\\
\operatorname{HD}(L_{i,0})&i\in\mathbb{A}\\
\end{cases},

(17)

with

\displaystyle\operatorname{HD}(L)\triangleq\begin{cases}0&L\geq 0\\
1&L<0\\
\end{cases}.

(18)

The hard decisions are then propagated right according to

\displaystyle u_{i,s+1}

\displaystyle=u_{i,s}\oplus u_{i+2^{s},s},

(19)

\displaystyle u_{i+2^{s},s+1}

\displaystyle=u_{i+2^{s},s}.

(20)

Note that the equations impose a fixed order of computation, i.e., to compute Eq. (
15
) one has to first recurse Eq. (
14
) all the way to the very left, perform the hard decision Eq. (
17
) and propagate
u_{i,s}
according to Eq. (
19
) back to the right. Finally, the message estimate and the codeword estimate of
SC
decoding is given by the left and right-most hard-decisions, respectively, i.e.,
\hat{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{u}_{m}
. While being an asymptotically optimal decoding scheme for long polar codes,
SC
decoding of
RM
codes suffers from a poor error-rate performance which limits its practical use as a standalone decoder for
RM
codes.

II-D
2
SCL Decoding

Instead of the one-branch
SC
decoding where bits are hard-decided in a sequential manner affecting all decoding decisions yet-to-come, the breadth-first search
SCL
decoding strategy branches out while decoding, continuing in both possible values of each bit
u_{i}
in a soft manner. The exponential growth of this search process is restricted by the predefined list size
L
, which defines the maximum number of parallel branches considered. Path metrics are then assigned to each single decoding path and used to truncate the list of branches after reaching that limit, only keeping the
L
most promising candidates.
It was shown in
[
18
]
that a list size of practical complexity was sufficient for polar codes to work close to its
ML
performance under
SCL
decoding. However, for
RM
codes, impractically large list sizes are required to perform close-to-optimum under
SCL
decoding
[
28
]
.
A variant to
SCL
decoding is proposed in
[
7
]
, where the decoding is started with a list of permuted received LLRs using a subset of stage shuffle permutations. We refer to this variant as Dumer-Shabunov (DS) decoding.

II-D
4
Naïve Belief Propagation Decoding

BP
is a well-known decoding method for
LDPC
(
LDPC
) codes. It is based on message passing on the Tanner graph of the parity-check matrix. Using its duality property, the parity-check matrix of
RM
codes is found as the generator matrix of the dual
RM
code.
BP
decoding can be performed over the Tanner graph of this naïve parity-check matrix. However, the performance of this decoder is poor due to the numerous cycles in the graph induced by the high density of the parity-check matrix.

II-D
5
Minimum Weight Parity-Check Belief Propagation Decoding

Minimum weight parity-check belief propagation (
MWPC-BP
) decoding introduced in
[
12
]
is based on the concept of iterative decoding over an overcomplete parity-check matrix. An online algorithm tailored to the noisy received sequence
\mathbf{y}
is used to construct the overcomplete parity-check matrix only based on minimum weight parity-checks. These are found as the minimum weight codewords of the dual
RM
code. Additionally, an attenuation factor for all check-to-variable node messages is applied to mitigate short cycle effects.

II-D
6
Neural Belief Propagation Decoding

Neural belief propagation (
NBP
) decoding as introduced in
[
13
]
treats the unrolled Tanner graph of the code as a neural network (NN), while assigning
trainable
weights to all of its edges leading to a
soft
Tanner graph. These trainable weights are optimized based on
SGD
(
SGD
) technique. The intuition behind why this algorithm enhances the error-rate performance even for dense factor graphs, is that the effect of graph cycles can be mitigated by the learned weights per edges over the whole graph.

II-D
7
Pruned Neural Belief Propagation Decoding

Pruned neural belief propagation (pruned-
NBP
) decoding
[
14
]
combines the idea of
MWPC-BP
together with
NBP
. To get started, a redundant parity-check matrix containing (all or some of) the minimum weight parity-checks is constructed. During the offline training phase, all edges connected to a check node are assigned a single trainable weight and the least effective (i.e., least contributing) check node is
pruned
(i.e., removed) from the graph. The authors of
[
14
]
refer to this decoder as
D_{1}
. The error-rate performance of this algorithm can be further enhanced by assigning trainable weights per edge at the expense of larger memory requirements to save all weights per edges, resulting in decoder
D_{3}
. Furthermore, a pruned
NBP
decoder without any weights is introduced as
D_{2}
, however with the expense of a significant degradation in error-rate performance.

III
Automorphism Ensemble Decoding

Ensemble decoding uses multiple constituent decoders (i.e., a decoder
ensemble
of size
M
) to generate a set of codeword estimates; and selects one of these codewords, using a predefined metric, as the decoder output. Typically, a least-squares metric is used, as this corresponds to the
ML
decision for the
AWGN
channel. Hence, this method is also called
ML-in-the-list
, as it selects the optimal candidate from the list of codeword estimates. This can be formulated as

\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},j\in\left\{1,\dots,M\right\}}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\mathbf{y}\right\|^{2}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},j\in\left\{1,\dots,M\right\}}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\hat{x}_{j,i}\cdot y_{i},

(21)

where
\hat{x}_{j,i}\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}
,
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
is the estimated codeword from decoder
j
for the received vector
\mathbf{y}
and
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
is the final codeword estimate of the ensemble.

MBBP
is a well-known example for ensemble decoding that uses
M

BP
decoders, each based on a different random parity-check matrix
[
11
]
. Another instance of ensemble decoding is
BPL
decoding of polar codes, where the stages of the
FFG
are randomly permuted for each constituent decoder
[
21
]
.

In this work, we propose
automorphism ensemble decoding
for RM codes with the main idea being to make use of the already existent polar decoders, namely, SC, BP and SCL decoders.
Furthermore, we use the RM code symmetry in the decoding algorithm itself, as permuting a valid RM codeword with a permutation from the code’s automorphism group results in another valid RM codeword.

An abstract view of our proposed decoding algorithm is shown in Fig.
1
.
The input to the decoder is the received noisy codeword
\mathbf{y}
.
We randomly sample
M
different permutations from the RM automorphism group, where each permutation is denoted by
\pi_{j}
, with
j
being the decoder index and
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,M\}
.
The
\mathbf{y}
-vector is interleaved (i.e., permuted) with the
M
different permutations
\pi_{j}
leading to
M
permuted noisy codewords
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{j}
, where
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,M\}
.
Now we decode every
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{j}
-vector using one polar/RM decoder (e.g., BP, SC or SCL) independently and the output from the decoder is the interleaved estimated codeword
\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}_{j}
.
A de-interleaving phase is applied to all
M
interleaved estimated codewords
\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}_{j}
and, thus, we have the
M
estimated codewords
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
. Let
\operatorname{Dec}(\cdot)
denote the decoding function that maps
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{j}
to
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j}^{\prime}
, then we can write the interleaved decoding as

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=\pi^{-1}_{j}\left(\operatorname{Dec}\left(\pi_{j}(\mathbf{y})\right)\right).

(22)

Similar to
MBBP
and
BPL
decoding, our proposed decoding algorithm uses the
ML-in-the-list
picking rule according to Eq. (
21
) to choose the most likely codeword from the list to get the final decoder output
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
.

As most decoders are linear, their decoding behavior is only dependent on the noise induced by the channel, and not the choice of the transmitted codeword (see Lemma 3 in Appendix
-A
for a proof of this fact for
SC
decoders). Therefore, decoding using automorphisms according to Eq. (
22
) corresponds to permuting the
noise
. It is reasonable to conclude that suboptimal (i.e., not ML) decoders may react differently to noise realizations in different permutations, which is exactly the property that automorphism ensemble decoding seeks to exploit.

Our proposed algorithm can be therefore seen as a natural generalization of the
BPL
decoder
[
21
]
: We still use
M
parallel independent decoders; however, we are no longer constrained to BP decoding for the constituent decoder (i.e., we can use
SC
,
BP
or
SCL
decoders as independent constituent decoders).

Furthermore, we use a more general set of permutations. It was shown in
[
29
]
that the stage-shuffling of the
FFG
is equivalent to a bit-interleaving operation while keeping the factor graph unchanged; with the permutations corresponding to the automorphism subgroup
\Pi(m)
. In contrast, we use permutations from the whole RM code automorphism group
\operatorname{GA}(m)
(rather than only
\Pi(m)
, which is used in
BPL
decoding as proposed in
[
21
]
).

In the following,
Aut-
M
-Dec
will denote the proposed decoding scheme, with
Dec
being the constituent decoder and
M
the ensemble size. If applicable,
L
denotes the list size of
SCL
constituent decoders.

It is worth mentioning that the usage of a
BP
decoder as a constituent decoder has some similarities when compared to
automorphism group decoding
of
BCH
(
BCH
) and Golay codes for the BEC
[
30
]
and for the AWGN channel
[
31
]
.
Automorphism group decoding is based on permuting the received sequence exploiting automorphisms of the code while applying an iterative message passing algorithm.

A similar usage of an SC decoder as constituent decoder was reported in
[
23
]
and
[
32
]
.
However, the used permutations were only constrained to the ones corresponding to stage-shuffling the code’s factor graph.
As will be shown later, this constraint degrades the error-rate performance when compared to choosing the permutations from the full automorphism group of the code (i.e., our proposed approach).

IV
Automorphisms of the SC Decoder

We will now present an interesting fact about
SC
decoding of polar and RM codes using permutations.

Theorem 2
:
Permutations
\pi\in\operatorname{LTA}(m)
commute with the SC decoding operation of a decreasing monomial code
\mathcal{C}
, i.e.,

\operatorname{SC}(\pi(\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{ch}}))=\pi\left(\operatorname{SC}(\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{ch}})\right).

(23)

In other words, it does not matter whether we first permute the received
LLR
vector
\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{ch}}
and then decode or decode first and then permute.

Proof:
The proof is given in Appendix
-A
.

Corollary 2.1 (SC decoding with LTA automorphisms)
:
All output candidate codewords from an automorphism-SC decoder are identical. Consequently, there is no gain in using LTA permutations for automorphism-SC decoding (i.e., ensemble decoding with SC as a constituent decoder) compared to a single SC decoder.

Proof:
The
j
-th candidate codeword of automorphism-SC decoding with permutation
\pi_{j}\in\operatorname{LTA}(m)
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=\pi^{-1}_{j}\left(\operatorname{SC}\left(\pi_{j}(\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{ch}})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Th.2}}}{{=}}\pi^{-1}_{j}\left(\pi_{j}\left(\operatorname{SC}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{ch}}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{SC}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{ch}}\right),

(24)

where Theorem 2 has been applied in the second step.
∎

This has far-reaching consequences for polar codes, whose only known automorphisms lie in the
\operatorname{LTA}(m)
subgroup. Hence, Automorphism-SC decoding is bound to fail for general polar codes (i.e., leads to the same error-rate performance as a plain SC decoder).

Furthermore, we can combine the results of Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 as follows:

Corollary 2.2 (LTA absorption)
:
Automorphism-SC decoding
absorbs
every permutation from
\operatorname{LTA}(m)
, i.e., for
RM
codes, it is sufficient to use permutations that are a product of a permutation from
\operatorname{UTA}(m)
and a permutation from
\Pi(m)
, rather than the full automorphism group
\operatorname{GA}(m)
, without any loss in performance.

Proof:
By Theorem 1, we can factor every permutation
\pi_{j}
from
\operatorname{GA}(m)
as
\pi_{j}=\pi_{j,L}\circ\pi_{j,U}\circ\pi_{j,P}
. If we apply Automorphism-SC, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j}

\displaystyle=(\pi^{-1}_{j,P}\circ\pi^{-1}_{j,U}\circ\pi^{-1}_{j,L})\left(\operatorname{SC}\left((\pi_{j,L}\circ\pi_{j,U}\circ\pi_{j,P})(\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{ch}})\right)\right)

(25)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Th.2}}}{{=}}(\pi^{-1}_{j,P}\circ\pi^{-1}_{j,U}\circ\pi^{-1}_{j,L}\circ\pi_{j,L})\left(\operatorname{SC}\left((\pi_{j,U}\circ\pi_{j,P})(\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{ch}})\right)\right)

(26)

\displaystyle=(\pi^{-1}_{j,P}\circ\pi^{-1}_{j,U})\left(\operatorname{SC}\left((\pi_{j,U}\circ\pi_{j,P})(\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{ch}})\right)\right),

(27)

where again Theorem 2 has been applied in the second step.
∎

At this point, it should be noted that all this argumentation holds for constituent
SCL
decoders (namely, Automorphism-SCL decoding), as the decoding sequence is identical to the
SC
decoder.
In addition, as this is both an effect of the factor graph and the decoding procedure, we expect similar (but far less pronounced) behavior also for constituent
BP
decoders, which use the same factor graph. This is confirmed by
BLER
simulations as shown in Section
V
.

For

VI
Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, we propose an automorphism-based decoding algorithm for RM codes.
Since polar codes can be seen as a generalization of RM codes, we utilize well-known decoding algorithms of polar codes in the context of RM decoding, namely
SC
,
SCL
and
BP
.
We present near-ML error-rate performance for the RM(3,7)-code (e.g.,
0.04
dB away from the ML bound at BLER of
10^{-3}
).
Furthermore, we report a decoder complexity comparison for the RM(3,7)-code from an operation level perspective.

To the best of our knowledge, our proposed iterative Aut-BP decoders using the RM code automorphism group as permutations are the best iterative decoders reported in literature thus far in terms of error-rate performance when compared to the best previously known iterative decoding schemes for RM codes. Furthermore, the proposed Aut-SC decoders outperform
SCL
decoding both in terms of error-rate and complexity, as no sorting operations are required.

We show that the RM code automorphism group can be divided into three different sub-groups of different performance.
Based on this classification, we show some theoretical limitations of our proposed decoding algorithm with respect to polar codes.

An interesting open problem is the on-the-fly selection of the best set of
M
different permutations from the general affine group (i.e., automorphism selection) which are tailored to the specific received noisy codeword
\mathbf{y}
. As previously observed in
[
37
]
, this might lead to a significant reduction in the number of automorphisms needed to reach a certain fixed error-rate performance and, thus, reduces the overall complexity of our proposed decoding scheme.

Proof of Theorem 2

Before we can prove Theorem 2, we have to establish a few properties of decreasing monomial codes, SC decoding and LTA permutations.

Definition 1 (Upper and Lower Subcode):
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a polar code of length
N=2^{m}
which is, with a slight abuse of notation, defined by the logical vector
\mathbb{A}=\left[a_{i}\right]
, where
a_{i}
denotes whether bit-channel
i
is a frozen or a non-frozen bit-channel. The
upper and lower subcodes

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{u}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{l}}
of length
N/2=2^{m-1}
are then given by the first half
\mathbb{A}_{0}^{2^{m-1}-1}
and the second half
\mathbb{A}_{2^{m-1}}^{2^{m}-1}
, respectively. The original polar code
\mathcal{C}
is formed via the Plotkin construction
[
3
,
38
]
from
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{u}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{l}}
as

\mathcal{C}=\left\{\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{u}}\oplus\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{l}}|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{l}}):\quad\mathbf{x}_{u}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{u}},\mathbf{x}_{l}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{l}}\right\}.

(30)

Lemma 1 (Splitting of Dec. Monomial Codes):
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a decreasing monomial code with monomial set
I
. Then
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{u}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{l}}
are also decreasing monomial codes. Moreover,
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{u}}
is completely contained in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{l}}
.

Proof:

The set of monomials
I_{\mathrm{u}}
of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{u}}
is given by

I_{\mathrm{u}}=\left\{g\div z_{m-1}:\quad g\in I,\,z_{m-1}|g\right\}.

(31)

Let
f,g\in I
be both divisible by
z_{m-1}
and
f\preccurlyeq g
. Then, by the definition of the partial order,
f\div z_{m-1}\preccurlyeq g\div z_{m-1}
, as the degrees decrement by one and all other variable indices remain the same. Therefore,
I_{\mathrm{u}}
belongs to a decreasing monomial code.

Similarly, the set of monomials
I_{l}
of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{l}}
is given by

I_{\mathrm{l}}=\left\{g:\quad g\in I,\,z_{m-1}\nmid g\right\}.

(32)

That this set belongs to a decreasing monomial code follows directly from the definition of the partial order, as the most significant variable index
m-1
is not involved in any monomial.
Lastly, to show that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{u}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{l}}
, we only need to show that
I_{\mathrm{u}}\subseteq I_{\mathrm{l}}
. Let
f\in I_{\mathrm{u}}
, i.e.,
\exists g\in I
such that
g=f\cdot z_{m-1}
. Therefore,
f\preccurlyeq g
and
z_{m-1}\nmid f
and, thus,
f\in I_{\mathrm{l}}
.
∎

Lemma 2 (Pointwise Products):
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a decreasing monomial code of length
N=2^{m}
and let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{u}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{l}}
denote its upper and lower subcodes, respectively. Furthermore, let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{RM}}
denote the
\operatorname{RM}(1,m-1)
code (i.e., an augmented Hadamard code of length
2^{m-1}
). Then the following statement holds:

\mathbf{x}_{u}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{u}},\;\;\;\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{RM}}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{RM}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{u}}\odot\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{RM}}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{l}},

(33)

where ‘
\odot
’ denotes a pointwise (i.e., component-wise) multiplication of two vectors.

Proof:

By definition, the monomial set
I_{\mathrm{RM}}
of the
\operatorname{RM}(1,m-1)
code is

I_{\mathrm{RM}}=\left\{1\right\}\cup\left\{z_{j}:\quad 0\leq j<m-1\right\}.

(34)

The pointwise product of interest is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{u}}\odot\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{RM}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{eval}\left(u_{\mathrm{u}}(\mathbf{z})\cdot u_{\mathrm{RM}}(\mathbf{z})\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{eval}\left((\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{u}}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{u}^{T}(\mathbf{z}))\cdot(\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{RM}}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{RM}}^{T}(\mathbf{z}))\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{|I_{\mathrm{u}}|-1}\sum_{j=0}^{|I_{\mathrm{RM}}|-1}\operatorname{eval}\left(u_{\mathrm{u},i}\cdot u_{\mathrm{RM},j}\cdot g_{\mathrm{u},i}(\mathbf{z})\cdot g_{\mathrm{RM},j}(\mathbf{z})\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{eval}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tilde{u}_{k}\cdot\tilde{g}_{k}(\mathbf{z})\right),

(35)

with some
\tilde{u}_{k}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
\tilde{g}_{k}(\mathbf{z})
monomials from all possible products

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{prod}}

\displaystyle=I_{\mathrm{u}}\odot I_{\mathrm{RM}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\{f\cdot h:\quad f\in I_{\mathrm{u}},h\in I_{\mathrm{RM}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{f\cdot z_{j}:\quad f\in I_{\mathrm{u}},0\leq j<m-1\right\}\cup\left\{f\cdot 1:\quad f\in I_{\mathrm{u}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{g^{\prime}=\underbrace{g\div z_{m-1}\cdot z_{j}}_{\preccurlyeq g\Rightarrow g^{\prime}\in I}:\,g\in I,z_{m-1}|g,0\leq j<m-1\right\}\cup I_{\mathrm{u}}

\displaystyle\subseteq I_{\mathrm{l}}\cup I_{\mathrm{u}}\subseteq I_{\mathrm{l}},

(36)

where we used that
z_{m-1}\nmid g^{\prime}
and, thus,
g^{\prime}\in I_{\mathrm{l}}
and the fact that
I_{\mathrm{u}}\subseteq I_{\mathrm{l}}
, as established in Lemma 1. Note that any square terms
z_{j}^{2}
in the product are implicitly replaced by
z_{j}
, since they evaluate to the same expression over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. As any
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{u}}\odot\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{RM}}
is generated by the encoding rule of the polar code with monomial set
I_{\mathrm{prod}}
, it is also a codeword of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{l}}
.
∎

Note that the inverse is not necessarily true, i.e., there might be a codeword in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{l}}
that cannot be written as a pointwise product
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{u}}\odot\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{RM}}
.

Lemma 3 (Decoder Linearity):
For any received LLR vector
\mathbf{L}
and any codeword
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}
, we have the property

\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\operatorname{SC}\left(\mathbf{L}\odot(-1)^{\mathbf{x}}\right)=\operatorname{SC}\left(\mathbf{L}\right)\oplus\mathbf{x},

(37)

where
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
denotes the codeword estimate of
SC
decoding. In other words, if we flip the signs of the components of
\mathbf{L}
according to some codeword
\mathbf{x}
, the
SC
codeword estimate will shift exactly by
\mathbf{x}
, i.e., the
SC
decoder does not favor any codeword over another.

Proof:
We will prove this Lemma via induction over the the dimension
m
of the code. The base case for
m=0
is straightforward:

u^{\prime}=\operatorname{SC}\left((-1)^{x}L\right)=\begin{cases}0=u\oplus x&\text{frozen}\\
\operatorname{HD}\left((-1)^{x}L\right)=u\oplus x&\text{non-frozen}\end{cases}

(38)

In the frozen case we used the fact that the only valid codeword is
x=0
.

For the induction step
m\mapsto m+1
we use
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{u}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{l}
according to Def. 1. By applying Eq. (
14
), we find that

\displaystyle L_{i,m}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left((-1)^{x_{i}}\cdot L_{i,m+1}\right)\boxplus\left((-1)^{x_{j}}\cdot L_{j,m+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(L_{i,m+1}\boxplus L_{j,m+1}\right)\cdot(-1)^{x_{i}\oplus x_{j}}

\displaystyle=L_{i,m}\cdot(-1)^{x_{i}\oplus x_{j}},

(39)

where we define
j=i+2^{m}
for easier readability. As
[x_{i}]~=~\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{u}}~\oplus~\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{l}}
and
[x_{j}]=\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{l}}
, we know that
[x_{i}~\oplus~x_{j}]~=~\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{u}}~\oplus~\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{l}}~\oplus~\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{l}}~=~\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{u}}~\in~\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{u}}
is in fact a codeword and we can use the induction assumption for the upper branch to claim that

u_{i,m}^{\prime}=u_{i,m}\oplus x_{i}\oplus x_{j}

(40)

Similarly, the lower branch equates to

\displaystyle L_{j,m}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{u_{i,m}\oplus x_{i}\oplus x_{j}}\cdot(-1)^{x_{i}}\cdot L_{i,m+1}+(-1)^{x_{j}}\cdot L_{j,m+1}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{u_{i,m}\oplus x_{i}\oplus x_{j}\oplus x_{i}}\cdot L_{i,m+1}+(-1)^{x_{j}}\cdot L_{j,m+1}

\displaystyle=L_{j,m}\cdot(-1)^{x_{j}}.

(41)

Obviously,
[x_{j}]\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{l}}
, so we can use the induction assumption for the lower branch to claim that

u_{j,m}^{\prime}=u_{j,m}\oplus x_{j}.

(42)

The hard decision propagates right as

\displaystyle u_{i,m+1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=u_{i,m}\oplus x_{i}\oplus x_{j}\oplus u_{j,m}\oplus x_{j}=u_{i,m+1}\oplus x_{i},

(43)

\displaystyle u_{j,m+1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=u_{j,m}\oplus x_{j}=u_{j,m+1}\oplus x_{j},

(44)

which completes the proof.
∎
