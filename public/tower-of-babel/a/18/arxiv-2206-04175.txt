Boundary h ∗ h^{\ast} -polynomials of rational polytopes Thanks: We are indebted to three anonymous referees for numerous helpful comments.
Esme Bajo, Matthias Beck

Abstract
. If P P is a lattice polytope (i.e., P P is the convex hull of finitely many integer points in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} ) of dimension d d , Ehrhart’s famous theorem (1962) asserts that the integer-point counting function | n ​ P ∩ ℤ d | |nP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}| is a degree- d d polynomial in the integer variable n n . Equivalently, the generating function 1 + ∑ n ≥ 1 | n ​ P ∩ ℤ d | ​ z n 1+\sum_{n\geq 1}|nP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\,z^{n} is a rational function of the form h ∗ ​ ( z ) ( 1 − z ) d + 1 \frac{h^{\ast}(z)}{(1-z)^{d+1}} ; we call h ∗ ​ ( z ) h^{\ast}(z) the h ∗ h^{\ast} -polynomial of P P . There are several known necessary conditions for h ∗ h^{\ast} -polynomials, including results by Hibi (1990), Stanley (1991), and Stapledon (2009), who used an interplay of arithmetic (integer-point structure) and topological (local h h -vectors of triangulations) data of a given polytope. We introduce an alternative ansatz to understand Ehrhart theory through the h ∗ h^{\ast} -polynomial of the boundary of a polytope, recovering all of the above results and their extensions for rational polytopes in a unifying manner. We include applications for (rational) Gorenstein polytopes and rational Ehrhart dilations.



1.
Introduction

For a
d
-dimensional
rational polytope

P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(i.e., the convex hull of finitely
many points in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
) and a positive integer
n
, let
\operatorname{ehr}_{P}(n)
denote the number of lattice points in
nP
.
Ehrhart’s famous theorem
[
9
]
says that

(1.1)

\operatorname{ehr}_{P}(n)=\mathrm{vol}(P)\,n^{d}+k_{d-1}(n)\,n^{d-1}+\cdots+k_{1}(n)\,n+k_{0}(n)\,,

where
k_{0}(n),k_{1}(n),\dots,k_{d-1}(n)
are periodic functions in
n
.
We call
\operatorname{ehr}_{P}(n)
the
Ehrhart quasipolynomial
of
P
. Ehrhart proved that each
period of
k_{0}(n),k_{1}(n),\dots,k_{d-1}(n)
divides the
denominator
of
P
, which is the smallest
integer
q
such that
qP
is a
lattice polytope
(i.e., the convex hull of
points in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
).
The
Ehrhart series
is the rational generating function

\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z):=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\operatorname{ehr}_{P}(n)z^{n}=\frac{h^{\ast}_{P}(z)}{(1-z^{q})^{d+1}}\,,

where
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
is a polynomial of degree less than
q(d+1)
, the

h^{\ast}
-polynomial
of
P
(see, e.g.,
[
5
, Section 3.8]
).
The best known instance is when
q=1
, i.e.,
P
is a lattice polytope, in which case
\operatorname{ehr}_{P}(n)
is a polynomial.

Note that the
h^{\ast}
-polynomial depends not only on
q
(though that is implicitly determined by
P
), but
also on our choice of representing the rational function
\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)
, which in our form
will not be in lowest terms. For example, the Ehrhart series of the line segment

P=[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
is

\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)=\frac{1+z+z^{2}+z^{3}}{(1-z^{2})^{2}}=\frac{1+z^{2}}{(1-z)(1-z^{2})},

but we typically insist that
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)=1+z+z^{2}+z^{3}
. Occasionally (e.g., in
Corollary
4.4
below) we use different
representations.

Stanley proved that
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
has nonnegative integer coefficients. While
his original proof used commutative algebra
[
19
]
based on work
of Hochster
[
16
]
, and a second proof of Stanley proceeded via shelling
arguments
[
20
]
, there is an “easy” conceptual proof using half-open triangulations (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Chapter 5]
).
Stanley’s theorem was refined by Stapledon
[
26
]
(for lattice polytopes, i.e.,
q=1
) and
Beck–Braun–Vindas-Meléndez
[
3
]
(for rational polytopes), who showed
that the
h^{\ast}
-polynomial can be decomposed using palindromic polynomials with
nonnegative coefficients. A
palindromic polynomial

f(z)=\sum f_{i}z^{i}
has symmetric
coefficients, that is,
f_{i}=f_{\text{deg}(f)-i}
for
i=0,\ldots,\text{deg}(f)
;
equivalently,
z^{\text{deg}(f)}f(\frac{1}{z})=f(z)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
P
be a full-dimensional rational polytope with denominator
q
and let
\ell
be the smallest positive integer such that
\ell P
contains an interior lattice point. Then

\frac{1+z+\dots+z^{\ell-1}}{1+z+\dots+z^{q-1}}\,h_{P}^{\ast}(z)=a(z)+z^{\ell}\,b(z)\,,

where
a(z)
and
b(z)
are palindromic polynomials with nonnegative integer coefficients.

We remark that our assumption that
P
is full dimensional and our
convention for
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
imply that
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
is divisible by
1+z+\dots+z^{q-1}
, because the
leading coefficient of
\operatorname{ehr}_{P}(n)
is constant (namely, the volume of
P
) and thus
z=1

is the unique pole with maximal order of the rational function
\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)
; see, e.g.,

[
24
, Theorem 4.1.1]
.
Thus the decomposition of the polynomial

\frac{1+z+\dots+z^{\ell-1}}{1+z+\dots+z^{q-1}}\,h_{P}^{\ast}(z)
into two palindromic
polynomials as in Theorem
1.2
is unique and an easy exercise. The point of
Theorem
1.2
is that
a(z)
and
b(z)
have nonnegative coefficients.

As was pointed out in
[
3
]
,
Theorem
1.2
immediately implies the inequalities

\displaystyle h^{\ast}_{0}+\cdots+h^{\ast}_{j+1}\geq h^{\ast}_{q(d+1)-1}+\cdots+h^{\ast}_{q(d+1)-1-j}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle j=0,\dots,\left\lfloor\tfrac{q(d+1)-1}{2}\right\rfloor-1\,,

\displaystyle h^{\ast}_{s}+\cdots+h^{\ast}_{s-j}\geq h^{\ast}_{0}+\cdots+h^{\ast}_{j}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle j=0,\dots,s\,,

where
s:=\deg{h^{\ast}_{P}(z)}
. For the case
q=1
(i.e., lattice polytopes), they go back to Hibi
[
11
,
14
]
and Stanley
[
21
]
.

The case
q=\ell=1
of Theorem
1.2
was proved much earlier by Betke–McMullen
[
7
]
.
Writing a (combinatorially defined) polynomial as a sum of two palindromic polynomials as in
Theorem
1.2
is often referred to as a
symmetric decomposition

and has applications beyond a refinement of nonnegativity; we mention one representative
(much more can be found, e.g., in
[
8
]
):
p(z)=a(z)+z\,b(z)
, with

a(z)
and
b(z)
palindromic, is alternatingly increasing (i.e., the coefficients of
p(z)

satisfy
0\leq p_{0}\leq p_{d}\leq p_{1}\leq p_{d-1}\leq\cdots
) if and only if the
coefficients of both
a(z)
and

b(z)
are nonnegative and unimodal (i.e., the coefficients increase up to some index and
then decrease).

Betke–McMullen’s, Stapledon’s, as well as Beck–Braun–Vindas-Meléndez’s proofs use local

h
-vectors of a triangulation, their nonnegativity, and the Dehn–Sommerville relations.
Our main goal is to give an “easy” conceptual proof of Theorem
1.2
—in
particular, one that is independent of local

-vectors (though they are
hiding under the surface).
Our

is to study the
h^{\ast}
-polynomial of the

of a rational polytope,
and our second goal is to exhibit that such a study is worthwhile, with the hope for further
applications.
The connection to Theorem

equals
a(z)
, for any

2.
The
h^{\ast}
-polynomial of the Boundary of a Polytope

We start by addressing some of the subtleties in defining the Ehrhart series (and thus the
h^{\ast}
-polynomial) of
\partial P
, especially when we ultimately compute the Ehrhart series using a half-open triangulation of the boundary.
The reason for the convention (
1.3
)
that
\operatorname{Ehr}_{\partial P}(z)
(and therefore also
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
) has constant term 1 is
Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity
(see, e.g.,
[
6
, Corollary 5.4.5]
): it says that the rational generating functions
\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)
and

\operatorname{Ehr}_{P^{\circ}}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\operatorname{ehr}_{P^{\circ}}(n)z^{n}=\frac{h^{\ast}_{P^{\circ}}(z)}{(1-z^{q})^{d+1}}

are related via

\operatorname{Ehr}_{P^{\circ}}(\tfrac{1}{z})=(-1)^{d+1}\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)

or, equivalently,

z^{q(d+1)}\,h^{\ast}_{P^{\circ}}(\tfrac{1}{z})=h^{\ast}_{P}(z)\,.

As

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\partial P}(z)=\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)-\operatorname{Ehr}_{P^{\circ}}(z),

we obtain

h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)=\frac{h^{\ast}_{P}(z)-h^{\ast}_{P^{\circ}}(z)}{1-z^{q}}

and thus
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
is palindromic, i.e.,

(2.1)

z^{qd}\,h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(\tfrac{1}{z})=h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)\,.

(See
[
6
, Proposition 5.6.3]
for connections to more general self-reciprocal complexes and the
Dehn–Sommerville relations.)

So
h^{\ast}
-polynomials of boundaries of polytopes are in a sense more restricted than

h^{\ast}
-polynomials of polytopes. Even further, as we will show in Theorem
2.5
below,

h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
has no internal zero coefficients, which is far
from true for
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
(see, e.g.,
[
15
]
).

On the other hand, there is the following alternative extension of nonnegativity of
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
due to Stanley
[
22
]
(we state only the version for lattice polytopes):

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
P
and
Q
be lattice polytopes with
P\subseteq Q
. Then
h_{P}^{\ast}(z)\leq h_{Q}^{\ast}(z)

coefficient-wise.

Monotonicity does not hold for
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
, as the following example exhibits.

Example 2.3
.

Let
P=\mathrm{conv}\{(0,0),(0,2),(2,0),(2,2)\}

and
Q=\mathrm{conv}\{(0,0),(0,2),(2,0),(3,3)\}
.
Then

h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)=1+6z+z^{2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad h^{\ast}_{\partial Q}(z)=1+4z+z^{2}.

Thus
P\subseteq Q
but
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)\geq h^{\ast}_{\partial Q}(z)
coefficient-wise.

Moreover, even though
\partial P\subseteq P
, it is not always true that
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)\leq h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
: while
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
always has degree
qd
, there are many polytopes for
which
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
has lower degree. On the other hand, the
\ell=q=1
case of Theorem
1.2
implies

h^{\ast}_{P}(z)=h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)+z\,b(z)\,,

thus when
P
is a lattice polytope containing an interior lattice point, it is true that

h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)\leq h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
. The rational version gives us a more general
necessary condition in terms of
\ell
and
q
for
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)\leq h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
to hold (again, assuming Theorem
1.2
):

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a rational polytope with denominator
q
, and let
\ell\geq 1
be
the smallest dilate of
P
that contains an interior lattice point. If
\ell\leq q
, then

h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)\leq h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
. (In particular, when
P
is a lattice polytope,
\ell=1
suffices.)

Proof.

By Theorem
1.2
(with the interpretation
a(z)=h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
)
and the assumption
\ell\leq q
,

h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)\leq(1+z+\dots+z^{\ell-1})\,\frac{h^{\ast}_{P}(z)}{1+z+\dots+z^{q-1}}\leq(1+z+\dots+z^{q-1})\,\frac{h^{\ast}_{P}(z)}{1+z+\dots+z^{q-1}}

coefficient-wise.
∎

The following lower-bound result is a
restatement of a theorem of Stapledon
[
26
, Theorem 2.14]
(and a simplified proof) in our language:

Theorem 2.5
.

If
P
is a lattice
d
-polytope with boundary
h^{\ast}
-polynomial
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)=h^{\ast}_{\partial P,d}z^{d}+\dots+h^{\ast}_{\partial P,1}z+h^{\ast}_{\partial P,0}
then

1=h^{\ast}_{\partial P,0}\leq h^{\ast}_{\partial P,1}\leq h^{\ast}_{\partial P,j}\qquad\text{ for }j=2,\dots,d-1.

In particular,
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
has positive coefficients.

This result parallels a lower-bound theorem of
Hibi
[
14
]
, who proved that if
P
is a
d
-dimensional lattice polytope with

h^{\ast}
-polynomial
h_{P}^{\ast}(z)=h^{\ast}_{P,d}z^{d}+\dots+h^{\ast}_{P,1}z+h^{\ast}_{P,0}
of degree
d

(i.e.,
P
contains an interior lattice point), then
1=h_{P,0}^{\ast}\leq h_{P,1}^{\ast}\leq h^{\ast}_{P,j}
for
j=2,\dots,d-1
.
Our proof mirrors that of Hibi; it turns out that adapting it for
h_{\partial P}^{\ast}(z)
simplifies the proof.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

Let
T
be a triangulation of
\partial P
that uses every lattice point in
\partial P

(i.e., every lattice point in
\partial P
is a vertex of a simplex in
T
),
with
h
-vector
(h_{0},\dots,h_{d})
defined, as usual, via

h_{d}\,x^{d}+h_{d-1}\,x^{d-1}+\dots+h_{0}=\sum_{k=-1}^{d-1}f_{k}\,x^{k+1}(1-x)^{d-1-k}

where
f_{k}
denotes the number of
k
-simplices in
T
and
f_{-1}=1
.
The definitions of
h
and
h^{\ast}
imply

(2.6)

h_{1}=f_{0}-d=\left|\partial P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}\right|-d=h^{\ast}_{\partial P,1}\,.

(Stanley
[
23
]
proved much more about relations between
h
and

h^{\ast}
.)
Barnette’s famous lower bound theorem
[
2
]
(see also
[
12
, Theorem 13.1]
) says

(2.7)

h_{1}\leq h_{j}\qquad\text{ for }j=2,\dots,d-1.

Finally, we apply a theorem of Betke and McMullen
[
7
, Theorem 2]
to our
situation: it yields

(2.8)

h_{j}\leq h^{\ast}_{\partial P,j}\qquad\text{ for }j=0,\dots,d.

(Betke–McMullen
[
7
]
prove much more, giving a formula for
h^{\ast}
in terms
of local
h
-vectors.)
The inequalities (
2.6
), (
2.7
), and (
2.8
) line up to
complete our proof.
∎

We finish this section by recalling, for the record, another set of inequalities for

h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
in the special (and important) case that

1=h^{\ast}_{\partial P,0}\leq h^{\ast}_{\partial P,1}\leq\dots\leq h^{\ast}_{\partial P,\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad h^{\ast}_{\partial P,j}\leq\binom{h^{\ast}_{\partial P,1}+j-1}{j}\,.

3.
Half-open Triangulations

Our approach is to triangulate
\partial P
into disjoint half-open simplices of dimension
d-1
, in order to
avoid inclusion–exclusion arguments. There is a subtlety stemming from our convention that the
h^{\ast}
-polynomial of
\partial P
has constant term 1, which we need to address here.

Before introducing the half-open boundary triangulations we will use in the later proofs, we recall the Ehrhart series of a
half-open
simplex. Let
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the simplex with vertices
\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{d+1}\in\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where the facets opposite
\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{r}
are missing. That is, let

\Delta=\left\{\lambda_{1}\bm{v}_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{d+1}\bm{v}_{d+1}:\begin{array}[]{l}\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}>0\\
\lambda_{r+1},\dots,\lambda_{d+1}\geq 0\\
\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{d+1}=1\end{array}\right\}\,.

Its Ehrhart series is

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\Delta}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\operatorname{ehr}_{\Delta}(n)z^{n},

with constant term 1 if and only if
\Delta
is closed, therefore it is possible for its expression as a rational function to be improper. That is, as seen in the construction below, the Ehrhart series can still be expressed in the form

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\Delta}(z)=\frac{h^{\ast}_{\Delta}(z)}{(1-z^{q})^{d+1}},

but it is possible for the
h^{\ast}
-polynomial to have degree equal to
q(d+1)
. The
following tiling argument of the homogenization of a rational simplex will also be crucial to the proof of Theorem
1.2
in the next section.

Construction 3.1
.

We follow
[
6
, Section 4.6]
and define the
homogenization
of
\Delta
to be the half-open cone

\operatorname{hom}(\Delta):=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathbb{R}_{>0}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}_{j}\\
1\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{j=r+1}^{d+1}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}_{j}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}.

Observe that the intersection of
\operatorname{hom}(\Delta)
with the hyperplane
x_{d+1}=n
gives
precisely a copy of the
n
th dilate of
\Delta
, whence

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\Delta}(z)=\sum_{\bm{x}\in\operatorname{hom}(\Delta)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}z^{x_{d+1}}.

Define the
fundamental parallelepiped
of
\Delta
to be

\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}:=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(0,1]\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{j}\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{j=r+1}^{d+1}[0,1)\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{j}\\
q\end{pmatrix}.

Because the generators of
\operatorname{hom}(\Delta)
are linearly independent,
\operatorname{hom}(\Delta)
can
be tiled with translates of
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}
. More precisely, every
point in
\operatorname{hom}(\Delta)
can be uniquely expressed as the sum of a nonnegative integral
combination of the
\binom{q\bm{v}_{j}}{q}
and an integral point in
\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}
. This yields

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\Delta}(z)=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d+1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}z^{qk_{1}+\dots+qk_{d+1}+m_{d+1}}=\frac{\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}z^{m_{d+1}}}{(1-z^{q})^{d+1}}\,.

Observe that we chose the fundamental parallelepiped to have generators each with last coordinate
q
, however it is not necessary for this to be the case. For example, suppose
q_{1},\dots,q_{d+1}
are such that
\bm{v}_{j}\in\frac{1}{q_{j}}\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
. Then we can tile
\operatorname{hom}(\Delta)
with translates of the fundamental parallelepiped

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\Delta}:=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(0,1]\begin{pmatrix}q_{j}\bm{v}_{j}\\
q_{j}\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{j=r+1}^{d+1}[0,1)\begin{pmatrix}q_{j}\bm{v}_{j}\\
q_{j}\end{pmatrix}

to obtain the expression for the Ehrhart series

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\Delta}(z)=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d+1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\Delta}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}z^{q_{1}k_{1}+\dots+q_{d+1}k_{d+1}+m_{d+1}}=\frac{\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\Delta}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}z^{m_{d+1}}}{(1-z^{q_{1}})\cdots(1-z^{q_{d+1}})}\,.

Therefore, the denominator in our resulting expression of the Ehrhart series depends on our choice of fundamental parallelepiped, a subtlety that will be important in the following section.

Naturally, we would like to construct a triangulation of
\partial P
such that

(3.2)

h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)=\sum_{\Delta\in T}h^{\ast}_{\Delta}(z)

when
T
is a disjoint half-open triangulation of
\partial P
. We achieve this as follows:

•

For a
d
-dimensional rational polytope
P
with denominator
q
, choose a rational triangulation
T
of
\partial P
into
(d-1)
-dimensional
simplices with denominators dividing
q
. This can be accomplished, for example, by picking a triangulation that uses only the vertices of
P
.

•

Use the convention to express the Ehrhart series as if each simplex
\Delta\in T

has denominator
q
, i.e., define

h^{\ast}_{\Delta}(z)=(1-z^{q})^{d}\operatorname{Ehr}_{\Delta}(z)\,.

This guarantees that we will already have a common denominator when adding up the Ehrhart series of the simplices in the boundary triangulation.

•

Apply Construction
3.3
below to turn
T
into a disjoint triangulation with exactly one closed simplex. This guarantees that the constant term of
\operatorname{Ehr}_{\partial P}(z)
will match the constant term of the sum of the Ehrhart series of the simplices in
T
.

Once we are able to construct such a disjoint boundary triangulation
T
with exactly one closed simplex, we will obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\Delta\in T}\frac{h^{\ast}_{\Delta}(z)}{(1-z^{q})^{d}}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\Delta\in T}\operatorname{ehr}_{\Delta}(n)z^{n}=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\operatorname{ehr}_{\partial P}(n)=\frac{h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)}{(1-z^{q})^{d}}\,,

as desired.

Fortunately, the following construction gives a disjoint boundary triangulation
T
into half-open

(d-1)
-simplices, exactly one of which is closed; for such a
T
, we will have (
3.2
).

Construction 3.3
.

Fix a rational polytope
P
and a rational triangulation
T
of
\partial P
into

(d-1)
-dimensional simplices. We will construct, from this given
T
, a
disjoint

boundary triangulation into half-open simplices.
Choose any point
\bm{x}\in P^{\circ}
and let
T^{\prime}
be the triangulation of
P
given by coning over
T
, i.e.,

T^{\prime}:=\left\{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta):\Delta\in T\right\}

where for

\Delta=\left\{\lambda_{1}\bm{v}_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{d}\bm{v}_{d}:\begin{array}[]{l}\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d}\geq 0\\
\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{d}=1\end{array}\right\}

we define

\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta):=\left\{\lambda_{1}\bm{v}_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{d}\bm{v}_{d}+\lambda_{d+1}\bm{x}:\begin{array}[]{l}\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d+1}\geq 0\\
\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{d+1}=1\end{array}\right\}.

Choose a point
\bm{y}\in P
that is
generic
with respect to

, i.e.,
\bm{y}
is not contained in
any facet-defining hyperplane of any simplex in
T^{\prime}
. For each simplex
\Delta^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}
, remove all
facets of
\Delta^{\prime}
that are
visible
from
\bm{y}
, i.e., remove all facets
F
of

\Delta^{\prime}
such that
\bm{y}
is not in the halfspace corresponding to
F
that defines

\Delta^{\prime}
(see, e.g.,
[

, Chapter 5]
for more details about half-open triangulations using a

into a disjoint triangulation of
P
, which restricts to a disjoint
triangulation of
\partial P
. Moreover, the only closed simplex in the disjoint triangulation of

\partial P
is the one corresponding to
\Delta^{\prime}
that contains
\bm{y}
.

4.
A Proof of Theorem
1.2
via Half-open Pyramids

We will now prove Theorem
1.2
using a half-open triangulation of the
boundary of the polytope. The geometric interpretation of the coefficients of the

h^{\ast}
-polynomial of a half-open simplex in Construction
3.1
allows us to
compare
h^{\ast}
-polynomials of simplices via their fundamental parallelepipeds, which
will ultimately allow us to compare
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
and
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
. In the
lattice case, we will see the finite geometric series
1+z+\dots+z^{\ell-1}
arise
naturally as the correction between the denominator of the Ehrhart series of a lattice
polytope and the denominator of the Ehrhart series of a rational polytope with all
integral vertices except for one vertex with denominator
\ell
. In the rational case, we will see the term
\frac{1+z+\dots+z^{\ell-1}}{1+z+\dots+z^{q-1}}
arise as the correction between the denominator of the Ehrhart series of a rational polytope with denominator
q
and the denominator of the Ehrhart series of a rational polytope with all denominator-
q
vertices except for one vertex with denominator
\ell
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
T
be a disjoint rational triangulation of
\partial P
into
(d-1)
-dimensional rational half-open
simplices with denominator
q
. By Construction
3.3
, we may assume that exactly one simplex in
T
is
closed. Let
\bm{x}\in P^{\circ}
be such that
\ell\bm{x}\in(\ell P)^{\circ}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
; by the minimality of

\ell
, we know that
\bm{x}
has denominator
\ell
.
Given
\Delta\in T
, let
\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)
be the corresponding half-open
d
-simplex.
These simplices give rise to the disjoint rational triangulation
T^{\prime}:=\{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta):\Delta\in T\}
of
P
.

We first claim that for any
\Delta\in T
,

(4.1)

h^{\ast}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)}(z)=h^{\ast}_{\Delta}(z)+z^{\ell}\,b_{\Delta}(z)

for some polynomial
b_{\Delta}(z)
with nonnegative coefficients.
To see this, let
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
be the vertices of
\Delta
(so

q\bm{v}_{1},\dots,q\bm{v}_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
). Relabel the vertices such that the facets of
\Delta
opposite
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{r}
are missing and the facets of
\Delta
opposite
\bm{v}_{r+1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d}
are present in
\Delta
.

Now we apply Construction
3.1
with the half-open parallelepiped

\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}:=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(0,1]\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{j}\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}[0,1)\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{j}\\
q\end{pmatrix}

yielding

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\Delta}(z)=\frac{\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}z^{m_{d+1}}}{(1-z^{q})^{d}}\,.

Meanwhile,
\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)
is a
d
-dimensional simplex with vertices
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d},\bm{x}
, with
missing facets opposite
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{r}
and included facets opposite
\bm{v}_{r+1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d},\bm{x}
. Then we again apply Construction
3.1
(noting the choice of fundamental parallelepiped) to obtain a tiling of

\operatorname{hom}(\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta))=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathbb{R}_{>0}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}_{j}\\
1\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}_{j}\\
1\end{pmatrix}+\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}\\
1\end{pmatrix}

with translates of the half-open parallelepiped

\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(0,1]\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{j}\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}[0,1)\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{j}\\
q\end{pmatrix}+[0,1)\begin{pmatrix}\ell\bm{x}\\
\ell\end{pmatrix}

giving

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)}(z)=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d+1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}z^{qk_{1}+\dots+qk_{d}+\ell k_{d+1}+m_{d+1}}=\frac{\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}z^{m_{d+1}}}{(1-z^{q})^{d}(1-z^{\ell})}\,.

Now let
\bm{m}
be a lattice point in
\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)}
, say

(4.2)

\bm{m}=\alpha_{1}\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{1}\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\dots+\alpha_{d}\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{d}\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\beta\begin{pmatrix}\ell\bm{x}\\
\ell\end{pmatrix}

with
0<\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}\leq 1
and
0\leq\alpha_{r+1},\dots,\alpha_{d},\beta<1
.
If
\beta=0
, then
\bm{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}
.
If
\beta>0
, then the first
d
coordinates of
\bm{m}
are given by

\alpha_{1}q\bm{v}_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}q\bm{v}_{d}+\beta\ell\bm{x}\,,

and since
\beta>0
, this is a lattice point in
m_{d+1}(\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)\setminus\Delta)
, where

m_{d+1}=\alpha_{1}q+\dots+\alpha_{d}q+\beta\ell\,.

But by the minimality of
\ell
, we know that
j(\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)\setminus\Delta)
contains no lattice points for
j=1,\dots,\ell-1
; thus,
m_{d+1}\geq\ell
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle h^{\ast}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}z^{m_{d+1}}=\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}z^{m_{d+1}}+z^{\ell}\,b_{\Delta}(z)

\displaystyle=h^{\ast}_{\Delta}(z)+z^{\ell}\,b_{\Delta}(z)

for some polynomial
b_{\Delta}(z)
with nonnegative integral coefficients,
and this proves our claim (
4.1
).

Summing over all
\Delta\in T
now yields

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Delta\in T}\operatorname{Ehr}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)}(z)=\frac{\sum_{\Delta\in T}h^{\ast}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},\Delta)}(z)}{(1-z^{q})^{d}(1-z^{\ell})}=\frac{\sum_{\Delta\in T}\left(h^{\ast}_{\Delta}(z)+z^{\ell}\,b_{\Delta}(z)\right)}{(1-z^{q})^{d}(1-z^{\ell})}\,.

Define
b_{P}(z):=\sum_{\Delta\in T}b_{\Delta}(z)
, which we know to be a polynomial with nonnegative
integral coefficients. Thus

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{\ast}_{P}(z)}{(1-z^{q})^{d+1}}=\frac{h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)+z^{\ell}\,b_{P}(z)}{(1-z^{q})^{d}(1-z^{\ell})}

and so

\displaystyle\frac{1+z+\dots+z^{\ell-1}}{1+z+\dots+z^{q-1}}\,h^{\ast}_{P}(z)

\displaystyle=h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)+z^{\ell}\,b_{P}(z)\,.

By (
2.1
),
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
is palindromic with
z^{qd}\,h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(\frac{1}{z})=h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
, and the palindromicity of

b_{P}(z)=\frac{\frac{1-z^{\ell}}{1-z^{q}}\,h^{\ast}_{P}(z)-h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)}{z^{\ell}}

follows from this,

h_{P}^{\ast}(z)=h_{P^{\circ}}^{\ast}(z)+(1-z^{q})h_{\partial P}^{\ast}(z)
,
and Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity:

\displaystyle z^{qd-\ell}b_{P}(\tfrac{1}{z})

\displaystyle=z^{qd-\ell}\ \frac{\frac{1-\frac{1}{z^{\ell}}}{1-\frac{1}{z^{q}}}\,h_{P}^{\ast}(\frac{1}{z})-h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(\frac{1}{z})}{\frac{1}{z^{\ell}}}

\displaystyle=z^{qd}\left(\frac{1-\frac{1}{z^{\ell}}}{1-\frac{1}{z^{q}}}\Big(h_{P^{\circ}}^{\ast}(\tfrac{1}{z})+(1-\tfrac{1}{z^{q}})h_{\partial P}^{\ast}(\tfrac{1}{z})\Big)-h_{\partial P}^{\ast}(\tfrac{1}{z})\right)

\displaystyle=z^{qd}\left(\frac{1-\frac{1}{z^{\ell}}}{1-\frac{1}{z^{q}}}\,h_{P^{\circ}}^{\ast}(\tfrac{1}{z})+(1-\tfrac{1}{z^{\ell}})h_{\partial P}^{\ast}(\tfrac{1}{z})-h_{\partial P}^{\ast}(\tfrac{1}{z})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z^{\ell}}\left(\frac{z^{\ell}-1}{z^{q}-1}\,z^{q(d+1)}\,h_{P^{\circ}}^{\ast}(\tfrac{1}{z})-z^{qd}\,h_{\partial P}^{\ast}(\tfrac{1}{z})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z^{\ell}}\left(\frac{1-z^{\ell}}{1-z^{q}}\,h_{P}^{\ast}(z)-h_{\partial P}^{\ast}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=b_{P}(z)\,.\qed

Remark 4.3
.

If
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
is a lattice polytope, it is well known that
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
equals the

h^{\ast}
-polynomial of
\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{e_{d}},P)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Our above proof implies for more
general pyramids

h^{\ast}_{P}(z)\leq h^{\ast}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},P)}(z)

coefficient-wise, for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
not in the affine span of
P
. Moreover, the proof reveals a rational analogue:

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
be a rational polytope with denominator
q
. Then

h^{\ast}_{P}(z)=h^{\ast}_{{\rm Pyr}(\bm{e_{d}},P)}(z)\,,

where
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)=(1-z^{q})^{d}\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)
and

h^{\ast}_{{\rm Pyr}(\bm{e_{d}},P)}(z)=(1-z^{q})^{d}(1-z)\operatorname{Ehr}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{e_{d}},P)}(z)
. If
\bm{x}\in\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, then

h^{\ast}_{P}(z)\leq h^{\ast}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},P)}(z)\,,

where
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)=(1-z^{q})^{d}\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)
and

h^{\ast}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},P)}(z)=(1-z^{q})^{d}(1-z^{r})\operatorname{Ehr}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\bm{x},P)}(z)
.

We finish this section with proving Corollary
1.4
.

Proof of Corollary
1.4
.

It is well known that
\frac{h^{\ast}_{P}(1)}{d!}
equals the volume of
P
, which in turn equals
k_{d}
;
this follows, e.g., by writing
\operatorname{ehr}_{P}(n)
in terms of the binomial-coefficient basis

\binom{n}{d}
,
\binom{n+1}{d}
, …,
\binom{n+d}{d}
(and then the
coefficients are precisely the coefficients of
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
; see, e.g.,

[
5
, Section 3.5]
).
By the same reasoning,
\frac{h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(1)}{(d-1)!}
equals the sum of the volumes of the
facets of
P
, each measures with respect to the sublattice in the affine span of the facet. This sum, in
turn, is well known to equal
2\,k_{d-1}
. Putting it all together,

\ell\,d!\,k_{d}=\ell\,h^{\ast}_{P}(1)\geq h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(1)=2(d-1)!\,k_{d-1}\,,\

where the inequality follows from specializing Theorem
1.2
(with the interpretation
a(z)=h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
) at
z=1
.
∎

5.
Rational Reflexive and Gorenstein Polytopes

A
d
-dimensional polytope
P
is
reflexive
if it is a lattice polytope that contains the origin in its interior and one of the following (equivalent) statements holds:

(1)

the dual polytope of
P
, i.e.,

P^{\ast}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\bm{x}\cdot\bm{y}\leq 1\text{ for all }\bm{y}\in P\}\,,

is a lattice polytope;

(2)

P=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathbf{A}\bm{x}\leq\mathbf{1}\}
, where
\mathbf{A}
is an integral matrix and
\mathbf{1}
is a vector of all 1s.

Since
h^{\ast}
-polynomials are invariant under integral translation, we allow translates of reflexive polytopes. Now we need not require that
\mathbf{0}\in P^{\circ}
, and the following statements are all equivalent to being an integral translate of a reflexive polytope:

(1)

|P^{\circ}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|=1
and
(t+1)P^{\circ}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=tP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
;

(2)

P
contains a unique interior lattice point that is lattice distance 1 away from each facet of
P
;

(3)

z^{d}\,h^{\ast}_{P}(\frac{1}{z})=h^{\ast}_{P}(z)\,.

If
P
is a lattice polytope and there exists an integer
g\geq 1
such that
gP
is reflexive, we say that
P
is
g
-
Gorenstein
.

By retracing the steps in the proof of Theorem
1.2
but observing that
there is no
\beta>0
case if
P
is reflexive, we can obtain a relationship between

h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
and
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
. Similarly, we can do this for Gorenstein
polytopes. Both relationships quickly imply that
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
is palindromic since

h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
is. In this section we will see these results as
corollaries of more general theorems for
rational
polytopes.

Fiset and Kasprzyk generalized the notion of being reflexive to rational polytopes in
[
10
]
. They define a polytope
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be
rational reflexive
if it is the convex hull of
finitely many rational points in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
, contains the origin in its interior, and has a lattice dual polytope. Equivalently, a rational polytope
P
is
rational reflexive
if it has a hyperplane description
P=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathbf{A}\bm{x}\leq\mathbf{1}\}
, where
\mathbf{A}
is an integral matrix.

Fiset and Kasprzyk prove the following:

Theorem 5.1
.

If
P
is a rational reflexive polytope, then
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
is palindromic.

In their proof, they define the “
h^{\ast}
-polynomials” corresponding to each of the
components of the Ehrhart quasipolynomial of the rational polytope, show (using
reciprocity) that the
i
th such polynomial is equal to the
(q-1-i)
th polynomial with
coefficients read backwards, and finally show that the
h^{\ast}
-polynomial of the rational
polytope can be written in terms of these smaller polynomials. This is a generalized
version of Hibi’s proof
[
13
]
that lattice polytopes with lattice duals have
symmetric
h^{\ast}
-polynomials. Our proof in the previous section shows the lattice version
in an alternative way, and in this section, we will show the rational version. By
(
2.1
), Fiset–Kasprzyk’s theorem is an immediate corollary of the following:

Theorem 5.2
.

If
P
is a rational reflexive polytope with denominator
q
, then

h^{\ast}_{P}(z)=\left(1+z+\dots+z^{q-1}\right)h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)\,.

Proof.

Fix a disjoint half-open triangulation
T
of
\partial P
into
(d-1)
-dimensional simplices
using the vertices of
P
. Fix
\Delta\in T
, say
\Delta=\mathrm{conv}(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d})
with

\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d}\in\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where the facets of
\Delta
opposite
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{r}

are missing and the facets opposite
\bm{v}_{r+1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d}
are included.
We now follow the steps in our proof of Theorem
1.2
using
the fundamental parallelepiped

\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}:=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(0,1]\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{j}\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}[0,1)\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{j}\\
q\end{pmatrix}

yielding

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\Delta}(z)=\frac{\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}z^{m_{d+1}}}{(1-z^{q})^{d}}

and

\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\mathbf{0},\Delta)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(0,1]\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{j}\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}[0,1)\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{j}\\
q\end{pmatrix}+[0,1)\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
1\end{pmatrix}

giving

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\mathbf{0},\Delta)}(z)=\frac{\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\mathbf{0},\Delta)}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}z^{m_{d+1}}}{(1-z^{q})^{d}(1-z)}\,.

Fix a point
\bm{p}\in\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\mathbf{0},\Delta)}
, say

\bm{p}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\begin{pmatrix}q\bm{v}_{j}\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\beta\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
1\end{pmatrix},

with
0<\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}\leq 1
and
0\leq\alpha_{r+1},\dots,\alpha_{d},\beta<1
. The simplex
\Delta
is contained in some facet-defining hyperplane of
P
, say

H=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{d}x_{d}=1\right\},

where
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consider the value of
a_{1}p_{1}+\dots+a_{d}p_{d}
(where
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d+1}
are the coordinates of
\bm{p}
). If
v_{i,j}
denotes the
j
th coordinate of
\bm{v}_{i}
, then

\displaystyle a_{1}p_{1}+\dots+a_{d}p_{d}

\displaystyle=a_{1}(\alpha_{1}qv_{1,1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}qv_{d,1})+\dots+a_{d}(\alpha_{1}qv_{d,1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}qv_{d,d})

\displaystyle=q\alpha_{1}(a_{1}v_{1,1}+\dots+a_{d}v_{1,d})+\dots+q\alpha_{d}(a_{1}v_{d,1}+\dots+v_{d,d})\,.

Since each of
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d}\in H
, this is equal to

q\alpha_{1}+\dots+q\alpha_{d}=p_{d+1}-\beta\,.

If
\bm{p}
is a
lattice
point in

\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\mathbf{0},\Delta)}
, then both
a_{1}p_{1}+\dots+a_{d}p_{d}
and

p_{d+1}
are integers, so
\beta
must also be an integer. This forces
\beta=0
and thus

(5.3)

\mathcal{F}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\mathbf{0},\Delta)}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}=\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}.

Therefore,

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\operatorname{Pyr}(\mathbf{0},\Delta)}(z)=\frac{h^{\ast}_{\Delta}(z)}{(1-z^{q})^{d}(1-z)}\,.

Summing over all
\Delta
, we obtain

\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)=\frac{h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)}{(1-z^{q})^{d}(1-z)}

and so
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)=(1+z+\dots+z^{q-1})h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
.
∎

Observe that a (lattice) reflexive polytope is a rational reflexive polytope that happens
to be a lattice polytope, so letting
q=1
yields the following result:

Corollary 5.4
.

If
P
is a (lattice) reflexive polytope, then
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)=h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
.

Remark 5.5
.

We note that for rational reflexive polytopes (as opposed to lattice reflexive polytopes)
it is not necessarily the case that

P=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathbf{A}\bm{x}\leq\mathbf{1}\}
implies that the origin is
lattice distance 1 away from each facet-defining hyperplane of
P
. It is possible for a given
facet-defining hyperplane to not contain any lattice points and thus be lattice distance
less than 1 from the origin. However, the equality (
5.3
) between the sets of lattice points in the two fundamental parallelepipeds still holds.

We extend the definition of a rational reflexive to a rational Gorenstein polytope in a natural way. Let
P
be a rational polytope and let
g\geq 1
be an integer. We say that
P
is
rational
g
-Gorenstein
if
gP
is an integral translate of a (lattice) reflexive polytope. We allow
gP
to be a translate of a reflexive polytope so that we do not force
P
to have an interior point. Also note that, if we have a rational
g
-Gorenstein polytope with denominator
q
, we must have
q|g
.

Theorem 5.6
.

If
P
is a rational
g
-Gorenstein polytope with denominator
q
, then

h^{\ast}_{P}(z)=\frac{1+z+\dots+z^{q-1}}{1+z+\dots+z^{g-1}}\,h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)\,.

Sketch of Proof.

The unique interior lattice point of
gP
is lattice distance
1
away from the facet-defining hyperplanes of
gP
, so there is some polynomial
f(z)
such that

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\partial P}(z)=\frac{f(z)}{(1-z^{g})^{d}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)=\frac{f(z)}{(1-z^{g})^{d+1}}\,.

On the other hand,
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
and
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
are such that

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\partial P}(z)=\frac{h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)}{(1-z^{q})^{d}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\operatorname{Ehr}_{P}(z)=\frac{h^{\ast}_{P}(z)}{(1-z^{q})^{d+1}}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-z^{q})^{d}}{(1-z^{g})^{d}}\,f(z)=\frac{(1-z^{q})^{d}}{(1-z^{g})^{d}}\,\frac{(1-z^{g})^{d+1}}{(1-z^{q})^{d+1}}\,h^{\ast}_{P}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-z^{g}}{1-z^{q}}\,h^{\ast}_{P}(z)=\frac{1+z+\dots+z^{g-1}}{1+z+\dots+z^{q-1}}\,h^{\ast}_{P}(z)\,.\qed

Observe that a (lattice)
g
-Gorenstein polytope is a rational
g
-Gorenstein polytope that
happens to be lattice, so letting
q=1
yields the following generalization of
Corollary
5.4
.

Corollary 5.7
.

If
P
is a (lattice)
g
-Gorenstein polytope, then

h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)=(1+z+\ldots+z^{g-1})h^{\ast}_{P}(z)\,.

Due to the palindromicity of
h^{\ast}_{\partial P}(z)
, these relations immediately imply that reflexive, Gorenstein, rational reflexive, and rational Gorenstein polytopes all have palindromic
h^{\ast}
-polynomials.

6.
Rational Ehrhart Theory

In the last decade, several works have been devoted to an Ehrhart theory of rational polytopes where we
now allow
rational
(or, equivalently, real) dilation factors. The fundamental structure of the Ehrhart
counting function in the rational parameter
\lambda>0
is that

\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;\lambda):=\left|\lambda P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}\right|

is a quasipolynomial in
\lambda
(i.e., has the same structure as (
1.1
)
except that now
\lambda
is a rational variable); this was first shown by Linke
[
17
]
, who proved several other
results about
\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;\lambda)
, including an analogue of Ehrhart–Macdonald
reciprocity; see also

[
1
,
25
,
27
,
18
]
.
Our goal in this section is to prove an analogue of Theorem
1.2
in this setting.

To this end, we first recall
rational Ehrhart series
, which were introduced only
recently
[
4
]
. Suppose the full-dimensional rational polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
given by the irredundant halfspace description

P\ =\ \left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\,\mathbf{A}\,\bm{x}\leq\bm{b}\right\},

where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times d}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that the greatest common divisor of

b_{j}
and the entries in the
j
th row of
\mathbf{A}
equals
1
, for every
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
We define the
codenominator

r
of
P
to be the least common multiple of the nonzero entries
of
\bm{b}
.
It turns out that
\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;\lambda)
is fully determined by
evaluations at rational numbers
\lambda
with denominator
2r
; if
\mathbf{\mathbf{0}}\in P
then we actually need to know only evaluations at rational numbers
\lambda
with denominator
r

[
4
, Corollary 5]
.
This motivates a study of the two generating functions

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(P;z\right):=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;\tfrac{n}{r})\,z^{\frac{n}{r}}

and

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\sf{ref}}\left(P;z\right):=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;\tfrac{n}{2r})\,z^{\frac{n}{2r}}\,,

which have the following rational form, as shown in
[
4
, Theorem 12]
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
P
be a rational
d
-polytope with codenominator
r
.

(a)

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that
\frac{m}{r}P
is a lattice polytope. Then

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(P;z\right)=\frac{{h^{*}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;m)}}{(1-z^{\frac{m}{r}})^{d+1}}

where
{h^{*}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;m)}
is a polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[z^{\frac{1}{r}}]
of degree
<m(d+1)
with nonnegative integral coefficients.

(b)

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that
\frac{m}{2r}P
is a lattice polytope. Then

\operatorname{Ehr}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\sf{ref}}\left(P;z\right)=\frac{{h^{*\sf{ref}}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;m)}}{(1-z^{\frac{m}{2r}})^{d+1}}

where
{h^{*\sf{ref}}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;m)}
is a polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[z^{\frac{1}{2r}}]
of degree
<m(d+1)
with nonnegative integral coefficients.

Possibly more important than this theorem are the consequences one can derive from it,
and
[
4
]
(re-)proved several previously-known and novel results in
rational Ehrhart theory. The latter include the facts that
{h^{*}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;m)}
is palindromic if
\mathbf{0}\in P^{\circ}
and that, if
r|m
, extracting the terms with integer exponents from
{h^{*}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;m)}
returns
h^{\ast}_{P}(z)
, which results in yet another proof of the Betke–McMullen version of Theorem
1.2
(the
\ell=1
case).

Theorem
6.1
is based on the observation that

{h^{*}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;m)}=h^{\ast}_{\frac{1}{r}P}(z^{\frac{1}{r}})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad{h^{*\sf{ref}}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;m)}=h^{\ast}_{\frac{1}{2r}P}(z^{\frac{1}{2r}});

note that
m
is implicitly included in the
h^{\ast}
-polynomial, as we really mean the numerator of the Ehrhart series of
\frac{1}{r}P
(respectively,
\frac{1}{2r}P
) when the denominator is
(1-z^{m})^{d+1}
.
The same observation yields the following variant of Theorem
1.2
for
rational Ehrhart theory.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
P
be a rational polytope with codenominator
r
.

(1)

If
\mathbf{0}\in P^{\circ}
, then
{h^{*}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;m)}
is palindromic.

(2)

If
\mathbf{0}\in\partial P
, let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that
\frac{m}{r}P
is a lattice polytope and let
\ell\geq 1
be the smallest dilate of
\frac{1}{r}P
that contains an interior point. Then

\frac{1-z^{\frac{\ell}{r}}}{1-z^{\frac{m}{r}}}\,{h^{*}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;m)}=a(z)+z^{\frac{\ell}{r}}\,b(z)

where
a(z)={h^{*}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\partial P;z;m)}
and
b(z)
are palindromic polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[z^{\frac{1}{r}}]
with nonnegative integer coefficients.

(3)

If
\mathbf{0}\notin P
, let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that
\frac{m}{2r}P
is a lattice polytope and let
\ell\geq 1
be the smallest dilate of
\frac{1}{2r}P
that contains an interior point. Then

\frac{1-z^{\frac{\ell}{2r}}}{1-z^{\frac{m}{2r}}}\,{h^{*\sf{ref}}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;m)}=a(z)+z^{\frac{\ell}{2r}}\,b(z)

where
a(z)={h^{*\sf{ref}}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\partial P;z;m)}
and
b(z)
are palindromic polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[z^{\frac{1}{2r}}]
with nonnegative integer coefficients.

Example 6.3
.

Let
P=\mathrm{conv}\{(0,0),(0,2),(5,2)\}
, alternatively the intersection of the halfspaces

\{x_{1}\geq 0\}\cap\{x_{2}\leq 2\}\cap\{5x_{2}-2x_{1}\geq 0\}.

From this, we can see that
P
has codenominator
r=2
. Since
\mathbf{0}\in\partial P
, we look at the rational dilate

\tfrac{1}{2}P=\mathrm{conv}\{(0,0),(0,1),(\tfrac{5}{2},1)\}.

The first dilate of
\frac{1}{2}P
containing an interior lattice point is the
\ell=2
nd dilate, which contains the points
(1,1)
and
(2,1)
. We choose
m=r=2
minimal, and we compute the associated
h^{\ast}
-polynomial of
\frac{1}{2}P
:

h^{\ast}_{\frac{1}{2}P}(z)=(1-z^{2})^{3}\,\operatorname{Ehr}_{\frac{1}{2}P}(z)=1+4z+7z^{2}+6z^{3}+2z^{4}

and so

\displaystyle{h^{*}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;2)}

\displaystyle=1+4z^{\frac{1}{2}}+7z+6z^{\frac{3}{2}}+2z^{2}

\displaystyle=(1+4z^{\frac{1}{2}}+6z+4z^{\frac{3}{2}}+z^{2})+z(1+2z^{\frac{1}{2}}+z)\,.

We can check that the first polynomial in the decomposition is equal to

\displaystyle{h^{*}_{\mathbb{Q}}(P;z;2)}

\displaystyle=h^{\ast}(\partial\tfrac{1}{2}P;z^{\frac{1}{2}})=\frac{h^{\ast}(\frac{1}{2}P;z^{\frac{1}{2}})-h^{\ast}(\frac{1}{2}P^{\circ};z^{\frac{1}{2}})}{1-(z^{\frac{1}{2}})^{2}}

\displaystyle=1+4z^{\frac{1}{2}}+6z+4z^{\frac{3}{2}}+z^{2}.

Moreover, the power in front of the second polynomial is
z=z^{\frac{2}{2}}=z^{\frac{\ell}{r}}
and both polynomials are palindromic.
