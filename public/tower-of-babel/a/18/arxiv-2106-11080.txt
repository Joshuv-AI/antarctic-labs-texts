Linear Codes Associated to Symmetric Determinantal Varieties: Even Rank Case
Peter Beelen, Trygve Johnsen, Prasant Singh

Abstract
. We consider linear codes over a finite field 𝔽 q {\mathbb{F}}_{q} , for odd q q , derived from determinantal varieties, obtained from symmetric matrices of bounded ranks. A formula for the weight of a codeword is derived. Using this formula, we have computed the minimum distance for the codes corresponding to matrices upperbounded by any fixed, even rank. A conjecture is proposed for the cases where the upper bound is odd. At the end of the article, tables for the weights of these codes, for spaces of symmetric matrices up to order 5 5 , are given. We also correct typographical errors in Proposition 1.1/3.1 of [ 3 ] , and in the last table, and we have rewritten Corollary 4.9 of that paper, and the usage of that Corollary in the proof of Proposition 4.10.



1.
Introduction

One of the most natural and interesting ways to construct classes of linear error-correcting codes is by using the language of projective systems and considering the
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-rational points of a projective algebraic variety defined over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
as a projective system. For a more detailed study of projective systems and linear codes associated to them, we refer to
[
13
, Chapter 1]
. Determinantal varieties in different projective spaces are classical examples of projective algebraic varieties defined over finite fields, and therefore it is natural to study codes associated to these varieties. The study of codes associated to generic determinantal varieties was initiated by Beelen-Ghorpade-Hasan
[
2
]
, and the minimum distance of the code associated to the variety defined by the vanishing of
2\times 2
minors of a generic matrix was determined. Later Beelen-Ghorpade
[
1
]
studied other codes associated to generic determinantal varieties and together with several interesting properties, the minimum distances also of these codes were computed.

Determinantal varieties in the space of symmetric (skew-symmetric) matrices, i.e. the variety defined by the vanishing of all
(t+1)\times(t+1)
minors of a generic symmetric (skew-symmetric) matrix of order
m
, where
t\leq m
, is another example of classical varieties defined over a finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. For a detailed exposition of these varieties, we refer to
[
6
,
8
]
. Like generic determinantal varieties, these varieties are also an interesting class of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-varieties with many
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-rational points. Therefore, it is natural to study the codes corresponding to these varieties. The study of codes associated to determinantal varieties in the space of skew-symmetric matrices was initiated by the first and the last named authors
[
4
]
. They found an iterative formula for the weight of a general codeword and using this formula they computed the minimum distance of these codes. In the present article, we consider the codes associated to determinantal varieties in the space of
\it{symmetric}
matrices, and in the even rank cases, we compute the minimum distance of these codes.

Linear codes corresponding to symmetric determinantal varieties of rank at most
t
can be obtained by evaluating homogeneous, linear functions in
m^{2}
variables
X_{1,1},\ldots,X_{1,m},\ldots,X_{m,1},\ldots,X_{m,m}
at points corresponding to symmetric matrices
[X_{i,j}]
of rank at most
t
.
All important information about these codes
C_{symm}(t,m)
can be read off from their shorter “sister codes”
\widehat{C}_{symm}(t,m)
, each of which is obtained from
C_{symm}(t,m)
, by skipping the zero matrices, and otherwise evaluating the linear functions in only one matrix in each multiplicative equivalence class of matrices of rank at most
t
.
In other words, we then consider the projective system of such matrices. We will consider these codes in parallel. Finding the length and dimensions of these codes is easy, and the following result will be proved in Section
3
:

Proposition 1.1
.

The linear code
\widehat{C}_{symm}(t,m)
is an
[\hat{\nu}_{m}(t),\hat{k}]
linear code where
\hat{\nu}_{m}(t)
is given by equation (
5
) below with

(1)

{\nu}_{m}(t)=\sum\limits_{r=0}^{t}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor}\frac{q^{2i}}{q^{2i}-1}\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(q^{m-i}-1)\right)\text{ and }\hat{k}={m+1\choose 2}.

To find the minimum distance and possible weights of codewords, however, a lot of calculations are needed. A first step in order to obtain control over the situation is taken in Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
, where one shows that there are at most
2m+1
weights that are possible.
In one of our main results, Theorem
3.7
, we determine these weights. Our results are analogues of

[
4
, Corollary 3.3, Theorem 3.4]
, and also of

[
11
, Theorem 3]
for Grassmann codes
C(2,m)
. Theorem
3.7
, however, is not sufficiently transparent to read off the minimum distance of our codes immediately. Let the symmetric affine determinantal variety
\mathbf{{\mathbb{S}}}(2t,m)
be defined by the vanishing of all
(2t+1)\times(2t+1)
minors of a generic symmetric matrix of size
m
.

The main result of this article is the following theorem:

Theorem 1.2
.

The minimum distance of the code
\widehat{C}_{symm}(2t,m)
is

q^{m-1}\nu_{m-1}(2t-2)+q^{2t-1}\mu_{m-1}(2t-1)+(q^{2t-1}-q^{-1})\mu_{m-1}(2t),

where
\nu_{m-1}(2t-2)=\lvert\mathbf{{\mathbb{S}}}(2t-2,m-1)\rvert
, and
\mu_{m-1}(2t-1)
denotes the number of symmetric matrices of size
m-1
and rank
2t-1
.

Theorem
1.2
, in combination with Proposition
1.1
, give the 3 basic parameters (length, dimension, minimum distance) of our codes.

Theorem
1.2
is parallel to
[
4
, Theorem 3.11]
for codes from skew-symmetric matrices. The computational complexity, however, building on Theorem
3.7
, is much larger than in the analogous computations in the skew-symmetric case, and to a great part, it involves a detailed treatment of quadratic hypersurfaces in projective and affine spaces and the three classes (hyperbolic, elliptic, and parabolic) of such hypersurfaces. In contrast to skew-symmetric matrices, the symmetric ones also may have odd rank, but for reasons of space, we leave the determination of the minimum distances of
C_{symm}(t,m)
and
\widehat{C}_{symm}(t,m)
for odd
t
to future research. We end this paper, however, by giving a conjecture, and also a list of all possible concrete weights appearing, for
m=3,4,5
and all
t
up to
5
.
These results clearly indicate that our conjecture holds.

2.
Symmetric Determinantal Varieties over the field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}

Let
m
be a positive integer,
q
be a prime power, and let
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
be the finite field with
q
elements. An
m\times m
matrix
A=(a_{ij})
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
is called
symmetric
if
a_{ij}=a_{ji}
for every
1\leq i,\,j\leq m
. We use the notations
S_{m}
to denote the space of
m\times m
symmetric matrices over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and
A^{T}
to denote the transpose of a matrix
A
. It is well known that
S_{m}
can be identified with the affine space
\mathbb{A}^{m+1\choose 2}
. Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{ij})
be an
m\times m
matrix of independent indeterminates
X_{ij}
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. For
0\leq t\leq m
, we denote by
\mathbb{S}(t,m)
, the affine algebraic variety in
S_{m}
given by the vanishing of
(t+1)\times(t+1)
minors of
\mathbf{X}
; in other words

(2)

\mathbb{S}(t,m)=\{A\in S_{m}:\operatorname{rank}(A)\leq t\}

For any
0\leq r\leq m
we define

S(r,m)=\{A\in S_{m}:\operatorname{rank}(A)=r\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mu_{m}(t)=|S(r,m)|.

The following formula from
[
10
, Theorem 2]
gives the number of symmetric matrices of size
m
and rank
r

(3)

\mu_{m}(r)=\prod_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor}\frac{q^{2i}}{q^{2i}-1}\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(q^{m-i}-1).

From the definition it is clear that

\mathbb{S}(t,m)=\bigcup\limits_{r=0}^{t}S(r,m)

and the union is disjoint. Let
\nu_{m}(t)

be the cardinality of the set
\mathbb{S}(t,m)
. We have

(4)

\nu_{m}(t)=\sum\limits_{r=0}^{t}\mu_{m}(r).

Note that the defining equations of
\mathbb{S}(t,m)
are square-free homogeneous equations of degree
t+1
. Consider the projective variety in
\mathbb{P}^{{m+1\choose 2}-1}
defined by these equations. This variety in the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{{m+1\choose 2}-1}
is known as the
symmetric determinantal variety
and we denote it by
\mathbf{\hat{\mathbb{S}}}(t,m)
. Note that
\mathbb{S}(t,m)
is the affine cone over
\mathbf{\hat{\mathbb{S}}}(t,m)
. Therefore, if
\hat{\nu}_{m}(t)
denote the cardinality of the set
\mathbf{\hat{\mathbb{S}}}(t,m)
, then

(5)

\hat{\nu}_{m}(t)=\frac{\nu_{m}(t)-1}{q-1}.

3.
The Linear Code Associated to the Symmetric Determinantal Variety
\mathbf{\hat{\mathbb{S}}}(t,m)

We open this section by recalling the notion of a projective system. For details, we refer to
[
13
, Ch. 1]
. An
[n,k,d]
projective system is a (multi)set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{k-1}
that is not contained in a hyperplane of
\mathbb{P}^{k-1}
, and the parameters
n
and
d
are defined by

|\Omega|=n,\qquad d=n-\max\{|\Omega\cap H|:\;H\text{ a hyperplane of }\mathbb{P}^{k-1}\}.

Every
[n,k,d]
projective system corresponds to an equivalence class of
[n,k,d]
linear code and vice-versa. Linear codes corresponding to several classical varieties have been studied by several authors. In this section, we are going to study the projective systems, and hence linear codes, associated to the determinantal varieties
\mathbf{\hat{\mathbb{S}}}(t,m)
. First, we briefly give the construction of this linear code corresponding to
\mathbf{\hat{\mathbb{S}}}(t,m)
.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[\mathbf{X}]_{1}
denote the vector space over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
of linear homogeneous polynomials in
m^{2}
variables
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
, for
1\leq i,j\leq m
. Let
\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{\hat{\nu}_{m}(t)}\}\subseteq\mathbb{S}(t,m)
be a subset of representatives of distinct points of
\mathbf{\hat{\mathbb{S}}}(t,m)
in some fixed order. Consider the evaluation map

(6)

\operatorname{Ev}:{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[\mathbf{X}]_{1}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\hat{\nu}_{m}(t)}\;\text{defined by }f(\mathbf{X})\mapsto c_{f}=(f(P_{1}),\ldots,f(P_{\hat{\nu}_{m}(t)})).

The image of the evaluation map is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\hat{\nu}_{m}(t)}
. This subspace is called the
symmetric determinantal code
and is denoted by
\widehat{C}_{symm}(t,m)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The linear code
\widehat{C}_{symm}(t,m)
is a
[\hat{\nu}_{m}(t),\hat{k}]
linear code where
\hat{\nu}_{m}(t)
is given by equation (
5
) with

(7)

{\nu}_{m}(t)=\sum\limits_{r=0}^{t}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor}\frac{q^{2i}}{q^{2i}-1}\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(q^{m-i}-1)\right)\text{ and }\hat{k}={m+1\choose 2}.

Proof.

The proof of the proposition is simple. The statement about the length of the code
\widehat{C}_{symm}(t,m)
follows from equations (
3
), (
4
) and (
5
).
This gives the length of the code. For the dimension of the code, it is enough to prove that
\mathbf{\hat{\mathbb{S}}}(t,m)
does not lie on a hyperplane of
\mathbb{P}^{{m+1\choose 2}-1}
. Equivalently, it is enough to show that for
1\leq t\leq m
, the span of the affine algebraic variety
\mathbb{S}(t,m)
is the vector space
S_{m}
. Let
E_{ij}
be the
m\times m
matrix with
(i,j)^{\it th}
entry
1
and all other entries zero. It is well known that the set
\{E_{ij}+E_{ji}:1\leq i,j\leq m\}\cup\{E_{ii}:1\leq i\leq m\}
is a basis of
S_{m}
, therefore it is enough to show that the span of
\mathbb{S}(t,m)
contains the set
\{E_{ij}+E_{ji}:1\leq i,j\leq m\}\cup\{E_{ii}:1\leq i\leq m\}
. Note that
S(1,m)\subseteq\mathbb{S}(t,m)
for every
1\leq t\leq m
. Therefore, we will show that for every
i,j
the matrix
E_{ij}+E_{ji}
can be written as a sum of rank one symmetric matrices. If
i=j
then there is nothing to do as
E_{ii}
itself is a rank one symmetric matrix. So assume
i<j
. Let
[X_{ij}]
be the
m\times 1
column vector with the
i^{\it th}
and
j^{\it th}
entries being one, and the rest of the coordinates being zero. Then
X_{ij}X_{ij}^{T}
is a rank one symmetric matrix and

E_{ij}+E_{ji}=X_{ij}X_{ij}^{T}-E_{ii}-E_{jj}.

This completes the proof.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

Consider the linear map
\operatorname{Ev}_{1}:{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[\mathbf{X}]_{1}\to{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\nu_{m}(t)}
defined by
f(\mathbf{X})\mapsto(f(A_{1}),\ldots,f(A_{\nu_{m}(t)})
where
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\nu_{m}(t)}\}=\mathbb{S}(t,m)
in some fixed order. The image of this linear map is a subspace of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\nu_{m}(t)}
and we denote this subspace by
C_{symm}(t,m)
. The codes
\widehat{C}_{symm}(t,m)
and
C_{symm}(t,m)
are quite similar in the sense that the length of these codes is related by equation (
5
) and both are of the same dimension. Further, let
f(\mathbf{X})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[\mathbf{X}]_{1}
be a linear polynomial and
\widehat{c}_{f}\in\widehat{C}_{symm}(t,m)
and
c_{f}\in C_{symm}(t,m)
are the corresponding codewords, then the Hamming weights of these codewords are related by

(8)

\operatorname{W_{H}}(c_{f})=(q-1)\operatorname{W_{H}}(\widehat{c}_{f}).

Therefore to understand the minimum distance of the code
\widehat{C}_{symm}(t,m)
it is enough to understand the minimum distance of the code
C_{symm}(t,m)
. Therefore, for the rest of the section, we will concentrate on the code
C_{symm}(t,m)
.

For the rest of the article, we assume that
q
is odd
(in the case of even
q
one encounters difficulties with some of the calculations that we apply to obtain our results, but it is conceivable that the cases of even and odd
q
are very similar. We make no claims about this case). Let
f=\sum f_{ij}X_{ij}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[\mathbf{X}]_{1}
be a function and
c_{f}\in C_{symm}(t,m)
be the corresponding codeword. Let
F=(f_{ij})
be the coefficient matrix of
f
and
A
be a symmetric matrix. Then

f(A)=\operatorname{tr}(FA).

Since the characteristic of the field is not
2
and
A
is a symmetric matrix we can find a symmetric matrix
G
such that
\operatorname{tr}(FA)=\operatorname{tr}(GA)
for every symmetric matrix
A
. Indeed, we choose
G
to be the matrix whose
(i,j)^{\it th}
entry is
\frac{f_{ij}+f_{ji}}{2}
for
1\leq i,j\leq m
. Therefore, for every codeword
c\in C_{symm}(t,m)
there is a symmetric matrix
F=(f_{ij})
such that the codeword
c
is given by

c=(\operatorname{tr}(FA_{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{tr}(FA_{\nu_{m}(t)}))

where
A_{1},\ldots,A_{\nu_{m}(t)}
are matrices in
\mathbb{S}(t,m)
. If
F
is a matrix and
f(\mathbf{X})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[\mathbf{X}]_{1}
is a function whose coefficient matrix is
F
, then we use the notation
c_{F}
to denote the codeword
c_{f}\in C_{symm}(t,m)
. In the next proposition, we see that corresponding to every codeword
c\in C_{symm}(t,m)
, there is a diagonal matrix such that the weights of
c
and the codeword corresponding to the diagonal matrix are the same.

Proposition 3.3
.

For every codeword
c\in C_{symm}(t,m)
there exists a diagonal matrix
F=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,\ldots,1,\delta,0,\ldots 0)
with
\delta
a non-zero element of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, and

\operatorname{W_{H}}(c)=\operatorname{W_{H}}(c_{F}).

Proof.

For any non-singular
m\times m
matrix
L
and any
0\leq r\leq t
, the map
P\mapsto L^{T}PL
gives a permutation of the set
S(r,m)
and hence of the set
\mathbb{S}(t,m)
. For any codeword
c
we can find a symmetric matrix
F
such that
c=c_{F}
. Since
F
is symmetric, we can find
[
10
, Lemma 6]
a nonsingular matrix
L
such that
LFL^{T}=G=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,\ldots,1,\delta,0,\ldots 0)
as in the theorem. Now we claim that
G
is the required matrix. Note that

\operatorname{tr}(GP)=\operatorname{tr}(LFL^{T}P)=\operatorname{tr}(F(L^{T}PL)).

This completes the proof of the theorem. ∎

In the next proposition, we see that the weight of a codeword
c_{F}\in C_{symm}(t,m)
corresponding to a diagonal form
F
as in the above proposition depends only on whether
\delta
is a square or a non-square.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
F_{i}=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,\ldots,1,\delta_{i},0,\ldots,0)
for
i=1,2
be two diagonal matrices of the same rank
k
and let
c_{F_{i}}\in C_{symm}(t,m)
be the corresponding codewords. If
\delta_{2}/\delta_{1}
is a square, then
W_{H}(c_{F_{1}})=W_{H}(c_{F_{2}})
.

Proof.

By assumption, we can find a nonzero
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
satisfying
b^{2}\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}
. Now consider the matrix
P=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,\ldots,1,b,1,\ldots,1)
with
b
at the
k^{\it th}
position. Then
PF_{1}R^{T}=F_{2}
and the map
M\mapsto P^{T}MP
is a bijection between the support of
c_{F_{2}}
and
c_{F_{1}}
. Therefore they have the same weight.
∎

From the above proposition, it is clear that for every
1\leq k\leq m
there can be at most two different weights for codewords in
C_{symm}(t,m)
. In other words, the weight of codewords corresponding to functions
f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X})=X_{11}+\cdots+X_{k-1k-1}+\delta X_{kk}
for
1\leq k\leq m
will be all possible weights of the code
C_{symm}(t,m)
and hence of the code
\widehat{C}_{symm}(t,m)
. Moreover, the minimum of the weights
\mathrm{w_{H}}(f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X}))
gives the minimum distance of the code
C_{symm}(t,m)
. Next, we will give an iterative formula for the weight of codewords corresponding to functions
f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X})
. But before that, we fix some notations for the rest of the article. First, from now on, whenever we refer to the weight of the function
f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X})
, we always mean the weight of the codeword
c_{f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X})}\in C_{symm}(t,m)
corresponding to the function
f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X})
. For any
0\leq k\leq m
and
0\leq t\leq m
, we define

\mathrm{Supp}_{t}(f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X})):=\{A\in\mathbb{S}(t,m):f^{\delta}_{k}(A)\neq 0\}\text{ and }\mathrm{W}_{k}^{\delta}(t,m)=\left|\mathrm{Supp}(f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X}))\right|.

Clearly,
\mathrm{W}_{k}^{\delta}(t,m)
denotes the weight of the codeword
c_{f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X})}\in C_{symm}(t,m)
.
Also, for any fixed
0\leq t\leq m
and
0\leq r\leq t
, we define

T_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)=\{A\in S(r,m):f^{\delta}_{k}(A)\neq 0\},\qquad\mathrm{w}_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)=|T_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)|.

Notice that

(9)

\mathrm{Supp}_{t}(f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X}))=\cup_{r=1}^{t}T_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)\text{ and }\mathrm{W}_{k}^{\delta}(t,m)=\sum\limits_{r=1}^{t}\mathrm{w}_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)

We will call
\mathrm{w}_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)
the
r^{\it th}
restricted weight of the function
f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X})
in
C_{symm}(t,m)
.

Note that for
k=0
, the function
f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X})
is the zero function, and hence
w_{0}^{\delta}(r,m)=0
for any
r
, and as a consequence
W_{0}^{\delta}(t,m)=0
for any
t
. Next, for any
B\in S(r,m)
and
{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
, we define

\lambda_{B}(r,m)=\left|\{vB:v\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{m}\text{ and }vBv^{T}=0\}\right|

and

\gamma_{{\alpha}}(B,r,m)=\left|\{vB:v\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{m}\text{ and }vBv^{T}+{\alpha}=0\}\right|.

(These entities are well defined since
vB=uB
implies that
vBv^{T}=uBu^{T}
.)
Now let
1\leq k\leq m
and
1\leq t\leq m
be fixed. In view of equation (
9
), to determine the weight
\mathrm{W}_{k}^{\delta}(t,m)
, it is enough to determine
\mathrm{w}_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)
for each
1\leq r\leq t
. Therefore, in the next lemma, we give a formula for
\mathrm{w}_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)
in terms of
\lambda_{B}(r,m)
and
\gamma_{{\alpha}}(B,r,m)
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
0\leq r\leq m
and
k\geq 1
, the
r^{\it th}
restricted weight of the codeword
f^{\delta}_{k}(\mathbf{X})
in
C_{symm}(t,m)
is given by

(10)

\displaystyle\mathrm{w}_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)

\displaystyle=(q-1)(q^{m-1}-q^{r-2})\mu_{m-1}(r-2)+(q-2)q^{r-1}\mu_{m-1}(r-1)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(r-1,m-1)\\
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)=0\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{B}(r-1,m-1)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathclap{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(r-1,m-1)\\
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)={\alpha}\end{subarray}}\gamma_{{\alpha}}(B,r-1,m-1)-\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(r,m-1)\\
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)=0\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{B}(r,m-1)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathclap{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(r,m-1)\\
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)={\alpha}\end{subarray}}\gamma_{{\alpha}}(B,r,m-1)

Remark 3.6
.

In the case
k=1
this simplifies a lot, since

f_{1-1}^{\delta}=f_{0}^{\delta}=0
, and moreover the weight of
f_{1}^{\delta}
is equal to the weight of
f_{1}^{1}
for all
\delta
.

Proof.

Consider the following map

(11)

\displaystyle T_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\psi}S(r-2,m-1)\cup S(r-1,m-1)\cup S(r,m-1)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi(A)=B

where
B
is obtained from
A
by deleting the first row and first column. Clearly, if
A\in S(r,m)
then
\psi(A)=B
is a symmetric matrix of size
m-1
and of rank either
r-2,\;r-1
or
r
. Therefore, the map
\psi
is well-defined. Further, it is clear from the definition that
\mathrm{w}_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)=\left|T_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)\right|
, we use the map
\psi
and fibers of this map to compute
\mathrm{w}_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)
. The main idea is to compute the cardinality
\left|\psi^{-1}(B)\right|
of the fibers of
\psi
for every
B\in S(r-2,m-1)\cup S(r-1,m-1)\cup S(r,m-1)
. We divide the counting of
\left|\psi^{-1}(B)\right|
into three different cases.

Case (I):
Suppose
B\in S(r-2,m-1)
. In this case,

\psi^{-1}(B)=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}z&u\\
u^{T}&B\end{bmatrix}:u\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{m-1}\setminus\mathrm{Rowspan}(B),\text{ and }z\neq-f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)\right\}.

Therefore, in this case, we get

(12)

\left|\psi^{-1}(B)\right|=(q-1)(q^{m-1}-q^{r-2}).

Case (II):
Suppose
B\in S(r-1,m-1)
. In this case, we have

Theorem 3.7
.

For any

and

f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)={\alpha}\end{subarray}}\gamma_{{\alpha}}(B,t,m-1)

where

(17)

\theta_{q}(t,m)=(q-1)q^{m-1}\nu_{m-1}(t-2)+(q-1)q^{t-1}\mu_{m-1}(t-1)+q^{t}\mu_{m-1}(t)

Proof.

The proof is a simple consequence of equation (
9
) and Lemma
3.5
. We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}_{k}^{\delta}(t,m)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{r=1}^{t}\mathrm{w}_{k}^{\delta}(r,m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{t}(q-1)q^{m-1}\mu_{m-1}(r-2)-\sum_{r=1}^{t}(q-1)q^{r-2}\mu_{m-1}(r-2)

\displaystyle+\sum_{r=1}^{t}(q-2)q^{r-1}\mu_{m-1}(r-1)+\sum_{r=1}^{t}q^{r}\mu_{m-1}(r)

\displaystyle+\sum_{r=1}^{t}\qquad\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(r-1,m-1)\\

\displaystyle-\sum_{r=1}^{t}\qquad\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(r,m-1)\\

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}_{k}^{\delta}(t,m)

Remark 3.8

Every

(2)

If

(i)\;\;c(X_{1}X_{2}+\cdots+X_{2t-1}X_{2t})\text{ or }\newline

Definition 3.9

The expressions in (1) and(2)(i),(ii) will be denoted by

of parabolic, hyperbolic, and elliptic quadratic forms, respectively. We will in general denote a symmetric matrix

can be transformed to a parabolic, hyperbolic, or elliptic quadratic form of standard type, as described above, respectively.

From the discussion above, it is clear that if

From

(18)

v_{\pm 1}(2r,m)=\frac{q^{r}\pm 1}{2}\dfrac{\prod\limits_{i=0}^{2r-1}(q^{m}-q^{i})}{\prod\limits_{i=0}^{r-1}(q^{2r}-q^{2i})}

\displaystyle\lambda_{B}(r,m)=\begin{cases}q^{2t},\text{ if }r=2t+1,\\

Theorem 3.10
.

The Hamming weight of the codeword
f_{1}^{\delta}(X)\in C_{symm}(t,m)
is given by

\displaystyle W_{1}^{\delta}(t,m)=\begin{cases}(q-1)q^{m-1}\nu_{m-1}(t-2)+(q-1)q^{t-1}\mu_{m-1}(t-1)\\
+(q-1)q^{t-1}\mu_{m-1}(t),\text{ if }t\text{ is odd },\\
\\
(q-1)q^{m-1}\nu_{m-1}(t-2)+(q-1)q^{t-1}\mu_{m-1}(t-1)\\
+(q-1)(q^{t-1}-q^{-1})\mu_{m-1}(t),\text{ if }t\text{ is even }.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

From Theorem
3.7
, we have

\displaystyle W_{1}^{\delta}(t,m)

\displaystyle=(q-1)q^{m-1}\nu_{m-1}(t-2)+(q-1)q^{t-1}\mu_{m-1}(t-1)+q^{t}\mu_{m-1}(t-1)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(t,m-1)\\

f_{0}^{\delta}(B)={\alpha}\end{subarray}}\gamma_{{\alpha}}(B,t,m-1)

Note that,
f_{0}^{\delta}(X)
is the zero function and therefore for every
1\leq t\leq m
and
B\in S(t,m-1)
, we have
f_{0}^{\delta}(B)=0
. Also, in view of the above expression, it is clear that to determine
W_{1}^{\delta}(t,m)
, it is enough to determine

\Lambda(t,m-1)=\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(t,m-1)\\
f_{0}^{\delta}(B)=0\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{B}(t,m-1).

Note that the sum in the expression for
\Lambda(t,m-1)
is over the set
S(t,m-1)
, since for any
B\in S(t,m-1)
, we have
f_{0}^{\delta}(B)=0
. Now we divide the proof into two cases.

Case 1.

If
t
is odd: In this case, every matrix
B\in S(2t+1,m-1)
is parabolic and therefore, from equation (
19
), we get

\lambda_{B}(t,m-1)=q^{t-1}
for every
B\in S(t,m-1)
. Therefore, we get

\Lambda(t,m-1)=\mu_{m-1}(t)q^{t-1}.

Putting this value in the expression for
W_{1}^{\delta}(t,m)
, we have

\displaystyle W_{1}^{\delta}(t,m)

\displaystyle=(q-1)q^{m-1}\nu_{m-1}(t-2)+(q-1)q^{t-1}\mu_{m-1}(t-1)

\displaystyle+q^{t}\mu_{m-1}(t)-\mu_{m-1}(t)q^{t-1}

\displaystyle=(q-1)q^{m-1}\nu_{m-1}(t-2)+(q-1)q^{t-1}\mu_{m-1}(t-1)

\displaystyle+(q-1)q^{t-1}\mu_{m-1}(t).

If
t
is even: In this case, there are
v_{+1}(t,m-1)
hyperbolic matrices and
v_{-1}(t,m-1)
elliptic matrices
B\in S(t,m-1)
. From (
19
), we know that if
B\in S(t,m-1)
is hyperbolic, then
\lambda_{B}(t,m-1)=q^{t-1}+q^{t/2}-q^{t/2-1}
and if if

\displaystyle W_{1}^{\delta}(t,m)

\displaystyle=(q-1)q^{m-1}\nu_{m-1}(t-2)+(q-1)q^{t-1}\mu_{m-1}(t-1)+q^{t}\mu_{m-1}(t)

\displaystyle-v_{+1}(t,m-1)(q^{t-1}+q^{t/2}-q^{t/2-1})-v_{-1}(t,m-1)(q^{t-1}-q^{t/2}+q^{t/2-1})

\displaystyle=(q-1)q^{m-1}\nu_{m-1}(t-2)+(q-1)q^{t-1}\mu_{m-1}(t-1)+q^{t}\mu_{m-1}(t)

\displaystyle-q^{t-1}\mu_{m-1}(t)-(q^{t/2}-q^{t/2-1})(v_{+1}(t,m-1)-v_{-1}(t,m-1))

where we are using the the facts that

. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

4.
The minimum weight of the codes codes
C_{symm}(t,m)
and
\widehat{C}_{symm}(t,m)

In this section, we determine the minimum distance of codes
C_{symm}(t,m)
and hence of codes
\widehat{C}_{symm}(t,m)
in the even rank cases. For convenience, we choose to denote the rank by
2t
, and not
t
, then. In order to prove the main theorem of the section, we need to introduce the following notations that are going to be useful. For any
0\leq 2t\leq m

\displaystyle P_{k}^{\delta}(2t-1,m)

\displaystyle=\{B\in S(2t-1,m):f_{k}^{\delta}(B)=0\},

\displaystyle H_{k}^{\delta}(2t,m)

\displaystyle=\{B\in S(2t,m):B\text{ is hyperbolic and }f_{k}^{\delta}(B)=0\},\text{ and }

\displaystyle E_{k}^{\delta}(2t,m)

\displaystyle=\{B\in S(2t,m):B\text{ is elliptic and }f_{k}^{\delta}(B)=0\}.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any
1\leq k\leq m
, the difference

of the weights
W_{k}^{\delta}(2t,m)
and

is given by

q^{t}\left((v_{+1}(2t,m-1)-v_{-1}(2t,m-1))-(h_{k-1}^{\delta}(2t,m-1)-e_{k-1}^{\delta}(2t,m-1))\right).

Proof.

Recall that, from Theorem
3.7
, we have

\displaystyle W_{k}^{\delta}(2t,m)

\displaystyle=\theta_{q}(t,m)-\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(t,m-1)\\
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)=0\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{B}(t,m-1)-\sum_{\mathclap{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}}\;\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(t,m-1)\\
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)={\alpha}\end{subarray}}\gamma_{{\alpha}}(B,t,m-1)

where
\theta_{q}(t,m)
is given by equation (
17
). Thus,
W_{k}^{\delta}(2t,m)-W_{1}^{\delta}(2t,m)
is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(2t,m-1)\\
f_{0}^{\delta}(B)=0\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{B}(2t,m-1)\quad+\quad\sum_{\mathclap{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(2t,m-1)\\
f_{0}^{\delta}(B)={\alpha}\end{subarray}}\gamma_{{\alpha}}(B,2t,m-1)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(2t,m-1)\\
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)=0\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{B}(2t,m-1)\quad-\quad\sum_{\mathclap{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}}\;\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(2t,m-1)\\
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)={\alpha}\end{subarray}}\gamma_{{\alpha}}(B,2t,m-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(2t,m-1)\\
B\text{ is hyperbolic}\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{B}(2t,m-1)\quad+\quad\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(2t,m-1)\\
B\text{ is elliptic}\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{B}(2t,m-1)\quad-\quad\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(2t,m-1)\\
B\text{ hyp.; }f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)=0\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{B}(2t,m-1)

\displaystyle-\quad\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(2t,m-1)\\
B\text{ ellip.; }f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)=0\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{B}(2t,m-1)\quad-\quad\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(2t,m-1)\\
B\text{ hyp.; }f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)\neq 0\end{subarray}}}\gamma_{f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)}(B,2t,m-1)

\displaystyle-\quad\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in S(2t,m-1)\\
B\text{ ellip.; }f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)\neq 0\end{subarray}}}\gamma_{f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)}(B,2t,m-1)

\displaystyle=v_{+1}(2t,m-1)(q^{2t-1}+q^{t}-q^{t-1})+v_{-1}(2t,m-1)(q^{2t-1}-q^{t}+q^{t-1})

\displaystyle-h_{k-1}^{\delta}(2t,m-1)(q^{2t-1}+q^{t}-q^{t-1})-e_{k-1}^{\delta}(2t,m-1)(q^{2t-1}-q^{t}+q^{t-1})

\displaystyle-(v_{+1}(2t,m-1)-h_{k-1}^{\delta}(2t,m-1))q^{t-1}(q^{t}-1)

\displaystyle-(v_{-1}(2t,m-1)-e_{k-1}^{\delta}(2t,m-1))q^{t-1}(q^{t}+1).

Simplifying the above expression, we get the desired result.

∎

The next corollary then gives a necessary and sufficient condition for
W_{1}^{\delta}(2t,m)
to be the minimum weight of the code
C_{symm}(2t,m)

Corollary 4.2

Proof.

The corollary follows from the last theorem as

\displaystyle W_{k}^{\delta}(2t,m)-W_{1}^{\delta}(2t,m)\geq 0

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
B\in S(2t-2,m-1)
and
A\in S(2t,m)
be such that by deleting the first row and first column of
A
we get
B
. Then
A
is hyperbolic iff
B
is hyperbolic. Further, If
B\in S(2t-2,m-1)
, then

|\phi_{1}^{-1}(B)|=\begin{cases}q^{m-1}-q^{2t-2},\text{ if B is hyperbolic,}\\
0,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
B\in S(2t-2,m-1)
and
L\in GL_{m-1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
be a matrix such that
LBL^{T}=D
is on the standard hyperbolic or elliptic form described in Definition
3.9
. Let

A=\begin{bmatrix}{\alpha}&u\\
u^{T}&B\end{bmatrix}.

If we take

M_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0^{T}&L\end{bmatrix},

then

M_{1}AM_{1}^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}{\alpha}&z\\
z^{T}&D\end{bmatrix},

where
z=uL^{T}
. Note that
\operatorname{rank}A=\operatorname{rank}B+2
if and only if
z\notin\mathrm{Rowspan}(D)
. If
A\in S(2t,m)
, we may write
z=yD+b
, where
b=(0,\ldots,0,b_{2t-1},\ldots,b_{m-1})
and
b\neq 0
, i.e.,
b_{i}\neq 0
for some
i
. Now take

M_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-y-b\\
0^{T}&I\end{bmatrix}.

Then we have

\displaystyle M_{2}(M_{1}AM_{1}^{T})M_{2}^{T}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}{\alpha}&b\\
b^{T}&D\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c|c@{}}{\alpha}&0&c\\
\hline\cr 0^{T}&D_{1}&0\\
\hline\cr c&0&0\end{array}\right]

for some nonzero
c
. Here
D_{1}
is obtained by writing
D
as a block diagonal matrix with a bottom diagonal zero. Note that
A
and
M_{2}(M_{1}AM_{1}^{T})M_{2}^{T}
correspond to the same standard form of quadratics, up to constant
c
(Definition
3.9
). In fact, the quadratic form corresponding to
M_{2}(M_{1}AM_{1}^{T})M_{2}^{T}
can be obtained from the quadratic form corresponding to
A
by a linear change of variables. The quadratic form corresponding to
A
and
B
have the same standard form also (up to
c
). That is:
A
is hyperbolic if and only
B
is hyperbolic. This is true since the difference between the two quadratic forms corresponding to
D_{1}
and the full matrix is of the form

(\alpha+2c)XY
for two independent linear forms
X
and
Y
that are linearly independent of those linear forms appearing in
D_{1}
. Now let
B\in S(2t-2,m-1)
be hyperbolic with
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)={\alpha}
, then

\phi_{1}^{-1}(B)=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}-{\alpha}&u\\
u^{T}&B\end{bmatrix}:u\notin\mathrm{Rowspan}(B)\right\}.

Consequently,

|\phi_{1}^{-1}(B)|=q^{m-1}-q^{2t-2}.

∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
B\in S(2t,m-1)
and
A\in S(2t,m)
be such that by deleting the first row and first column of
A
we get
B
. Then
A
is hyperbolic iff
B
is hyperbolic. Further, If
B\in S(2t-2,m-1)
, then

|\phi_{2}^{-1}(B)|=\begin{cases}q^{2t-1}+q^{t}-q^{t-1},\text{ if B is hyperbolic and }f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)=0,\\

Proof.

Let
B\in S(2t,m-1)
and let
L\in GL_{m-1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})

This proves that

have the same standard form, i.e.

\phi_{1}^{-1}(B)=\emptyset

\phi_{1}^{-1}(B)=\{A|A=\begin{bmatrix}uBu^{T}&uB\\
Bu^{T}&B\end{bmatrix}:uBu^{T}=0\}

Since

The second case is when

Using hyperbolic nature of

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
B\in S(2t-1,m-1)
be a matrix satisfying
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)=0
.Then

|\phi_{1}^{-1}(B)|=\frac{(q-1)}{2}q^{t-1}(q^{t-1}+1),

and

|\phi_{2}^{-1}(B)|=\frac{(q-1)}{2}q^{t-1}(q^{t-1}-1)

Proof.

Let
B\in S(2t-1,m-1)
with
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)=0
. There exists a nonsingular matrix
L\in GL_{m-1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
such that
LBL^{T}
gives the quadratic form
cX_{1}^{2}+X_{2}X_{3}+\cdots+X_{2t-2}X_{2t-1}
for some
c\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
. If
A\in S(2t,m)
is the matrix such that by deleting the first row and columns of
A
we obtain
B
and
f_{k}^{\delta}(A)=0
, then

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&uB\\
Bu^{T}&B\end{bmatrix}\text{ such that }uBu^{T}\neq 0.

Clearly,

A\in\phi_{1}^{-1}(B)(\phi_{2}^{-1}(B))\iff A\text{ is hyperbolic }(elliptic).

Now, if we take

M=\begin{bmatrix}1&-u\\
u^{T}&L\end{bmatrix},

then we have

MAM^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}-uBu^{T}&0\\
0^{T}&LBL^{T}\end{bmatrix}.

Note that,
\operatorname{rank}(MAM^{T})=\operatorname{rank}(A)=2t
, therefore,
uBu^{T}\neq 0
. If
uBu^{T}=\beta
, then the quadratic form corresponding to
MAM^{T}
is

-\beta X_{0}^{2}+cX_{1}^{2}+X_{2}X_{3}+\cdots+X_{2t-2}X_{2t-1}.

Therefore, the matrix
MAM^{T}
and hence
A
is hyperbolic (elliptic) if and only
-\frac{\beta}{c}
is square(non-square). Now, let
\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
be fixed such that
-\frac{\beta}{c}
is a square. Then every

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&uB\\
Bu^{T}&B\end{bmatrix}\text{ such that }uBu^{T}=-\beta

is hyperbolic and hence lies in
\phi_{1}^{-1}(B)
. In this case the matrix
B_{\beta}
is hyperbolic, therefore from equation (
20
), we get the number of such
A
is
q^{t-1}(q^{t-1}+1)
. Further, for a fixed
B\in S(2t-1,m-1)
as in the beginning of the proof, there are
(q-1)/2

\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
such that
-\frac{-\beta}{c}
is square. This gives

|\phi_{1}^{-1}(B)|=\frac{(q-1)}{2}q^{t-1}(q^{t-1}+1).

Repeating a similar argument in the case when
-\frac{\beta}{c}
is non-square and keeping in mind that in this case, the matrix
B_{\beta}
is elliptic, we get

|\phi_{2}^{-1}(B)|=\frac{(q-1)}{2}q^{t-1}(q^{t-1}-1).

∎

In the previous cases, we have found the cardinalities of the fibers
\phi_{i}^{-1}(B)
for
i=1,2
in all cases except in the case
B\in S(2t-1,m-1)
satisfying
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)\neq 0
. In all the cases we have treated, the cardinalities of fibers
\phi_{i}^{-1}(B)
for
i=1,2
are uniform. On the contrary, the fibers
\phi_{i}^{-1}(B)
for
i=1,2
for
B\in S(2t-1,m-1)
satisfying
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)\neq 0
are irregular, i.e., they may have different cardinalities for different
B\in S(2t-1,m-1)
. It is true, however, that in the case
B\in S(2t-1,m-1)
satisfying
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)\neq 0
, the
difference
of the cardinalities of fibers
\phi_{1}^{-1}(B)
and
\phi_{2}^{-1}(B)
is constant. To be more precise; we have the following lemma:

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
B\in S(2t-1,m-1)
be a matrix satisfying
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)\neq 0
. Then

|\phi_{1}^{-1}(B)|-|\phi_{2}^{-1}(B)|=-q^{t-1}.

Proof.

Let
B\in S(2t-1,m-1)
be a matrix satisfying
f_{k-1}^{\delta}(B)={\alpha}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
. Let
L\in GL_{m-1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
be a non-singular matrix such that the quadratic form corresponding to
LBL^{T}
is
cX_{1}^{2}+X_{2}X_{3}+\cdots+X_{2t-2}X_{2t-1}
for linearly independent linear forms
X_{i},
, where
i=1,2,\cdots,2t-1
, and
c\neq 0
. Let
A\in S(2t,m)
be such that
A\in\phi_{i}^{-1}(B)
, then

A=\begin{bmatrix}-{\alpha}&uB\\
Bu^{T}&B\end{bmatrix}\text{ such that }uBu^{T}\neq-{\alpha}.

Clearly,

A\in\phi_{1}^{-1}(B)(\phi_{2}^{-1}(B))\iff A\text{ is hyperbolic (elliptic). }

Now, if we take

M=\begin{bmatrix}1&-u\\
u^{T}&L\end{bmatrix},

then we have

A^{\prime}=MAM^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}-{\alpha}-uBu^{T}&0\\
0^{T}&LBL^{T}\end{bmatrix}.

Note that both
A
and
A^{\prime}
are of the same type. We have the following:

1.

\frac{\alpha+uBu^{T}}{c}\neq 0
.

2.

The quadratic form of
A
is hyperbolic iff

\frac{\alpha+uBu^{T}}{c}
is a non-zero square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

3.

The quadratic form of
A
is elliptic iff

\frac{-\alpha-uBu^{T}}{c}
is a non-square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

The first statement holds since the rank of
A
is
2t
, not
2t-1
, which it would have been if the
\frac{\alpha+uBu^{T}}{c}
were zero. The second and third statements follow since the quadratic form of
A^{\prime}
is of type:

c(X_{1}^{2}-\frac{\alpha+uBu^{T}}{c}X_{0}^{2})+X_{2}X_{3}+\cdots+X_{2t-2}X_{2t-1},
and
(X_{1}^{2}-\frac{\alpha+uBu^{T}}{c}X_{0}^{2})
is factorizable into a product of two linear terms if and only
\frac{\alpha+uBu^{T}}{c}
is a non-zero square.

So we set out to find the number of
uB
such that

\frac{\alpha+uBu^{T}}{c}
is a non-zero square (thus giving hyperbolic
A
), and then the number of
uB
such that

\frac{\alpha+uBu^{T}}{c}
is a non-square (thus giving elliptic
A
).

Given a fixed non-zero square
\gamma^{2}
we look at the equation:

\frac{\alpha+uBu^{T}}{c}=\gamma^{2}.

We the substitute:

v=uL^{-1}
, and the equation transforms to:

\frac{\alpha+v(LBL^{T})v^{T}}{c}=\gamma^{2}.

The number of
v(LBL^{T})=uBL^{T}
satisfying this equation is then equal to the number of
uB
satisfying this equation
(since
L
is invertible), which again is equal to the number of
uB
satisfying the original and identical equation

\frac{\alpha+uBu^{T}}{c}=\gamma^{2}.

We then prefer to look at the number of
vB^{\prime}
satisfying

\frac{\alpha+vB^{\prime}v^{T}}{c}=\gamma^{2},

for
B^{\prime}=LBL^{T}

since we already have introduced the quadratic form associated to
B^{\prime}
(and thereby the coefficient
c
).
We now insert
vB^{\prime}v^{T}=c(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}X_{3}+\cdots+X_{2t-2}X_{2t-1})
again and obtain that the previous equation can be expressed as

X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{\prime}X_{3}+\cdots+X_{2t-2}^{\prime}X_{2t-1}-(\gamma^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{c})=0,

for linearly independent
X_{i}
and
X_{j}^{\prime}
(here we have set
X_{j}^{\prime}=X_{j}/c
for even
j
).
For such a fixed
\gamma
the number of solutions is (what we will call)
v_{H},v_{P},v_{E}
, according to whether

\gamma^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{c}
is a non-zero square, zero, or a non-square respectively. Here:

(i)

v_{H}
is the number of points on a projective quadric defined by a hyperbolic quadric of rank
2t
minus the number of points on a projective quadric defined by a parabolic quadric of rank
2t-1.

(ii)

v_{P}
is the number of points on an affine quadric defined by a parabolic quadric of rank
2t-1.

(iii)

v_{E}
is the number of points on a projective quadric defined by an elliptic quadric of rank
2t
minus the number of points on a projective quadric defined by a parabolic quadric of rank
2t-1.

Formulas for
v_{H},v_{P}
, and
v_{E}
can be found from equation (
19
). It is not hard to see that

(24)

v_{H}-v_{P}=v_{P}-v_{E}=q^{t-1}.

Let
e
be a fixed non-square in
F_{q}
. We will investigate the number of “hyperbolic”
A^{\prime}
; these are the ones corresponding to solutions to

(25)

X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{\prime}X_{3}+\cdots+X_{2t-2}^{\prime}X_{2t-1}-(\gamma^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{c})=0,

for the
(q-1)/2
non-zero squares
\gamma^{2}
, and thereafter the number of “elliptic”
A^{\prime}
; these are the ones corresponding to solutions to

(26)

X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{\prime}X_{3}+\cdots+X_{2t-2}^{\prime}X_{2t-1}-(e\gamma^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{c})=0,

for the
(q-1)/2
non-squares
e\gamma^{2}.
We split into 4 cases:

(1): Both
\frac{\alpha}{c}
and
-\frac{\alpha}{c}
are non-squares.
We investigate the condition (
26
)

\gamma^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{c}=T^{2},

for
T\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This single equation is equivalent to:

•

\gamma-T=\frac{\alpha}{c}\beta

•

\gamma+T=\frac{\alpha}{c}\beta^{-1},

simultaneously, for
some

\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
.

The two last equations, taken alone, give
q-1
solution pairs
(\gamma_{0},T_{0})
, one for each
\beta
in question.
Since neither
\alpha/c
nor
-\alpha/c
are squares,
both
\gamma_{0}
and
T_{0}
are non-zero in all these solutions, so none of them correspond to the ”forbidden”
\gamma=0
that would have made
A^{\prime}
parabolic of rank
2t-1
, and none of them gives a zero for
(\gamma^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{c})
. The last observation gives that the number of solutions to (
25
) never is
v_{P}
, so for the
\gamma^{2}
that matches some
T^{2}
