1
The Runge-Kutta method and Gaussian quadrature nodes

The Runge-Kutta methods are a family of iterative methods for approximating the solutions of ordinary differential equations.
A method can be represented in a form of a famous Butcher tableau:

\begin{array}[]{c|c}\boldsymbol{c}&\mathbf{A}\\
\hline\cr&\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\end{array},

where
\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
represent the nodes and the weights and the matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is called the Runge-Kutta matrix of order
m
.
More about the origin of the methods can be found in
[
1
]
.

1.1
Gaussian quadrature nodes

On function spaces, there is a natural scalar product.
For integrable functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a scalar product can be defined as

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}f\,g.

(1)

This allows us to define orthogonality on function spaces.
The family of orthogonal polynomials can be used to construct a high order Gaussian-quadrature rules.
Let

\int_{0}^{1}p(s)ds=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}p(t_{i})

(2)

define a quadrature rule, where some points
t_{i}
may be fixed and while other are roots of an orthogonal polynomial. Then, the right sum computes the integral correctly
for polynomials of degree
2m-2
or less.
Depending on the choice of these points, we get different quadrature rules such as Legendre, Radau, Lobatto, etc.
The integration weights
w_{i}
are positive and the integration points
t_{i}
are inside the integration interval
[
2
]
.
In our case, without loss of generality, we will assume that
t_{i}\in[0,1]
.

Let
l_{j}
be a Lagrange interpolation polynomial defined in points
t_{i}
:

l_{j}(t)=\prod_{i\neq j}\frac{t-t_{i}}{t_{j}-t_{i}},\quad j=1,\dots,m.

If we define the Runge-Kutta matrix as

\mathbf{A}_{ij}=\int_{0}^{t_{i}}l_{j}(s)ds,

(3)

we end up with a high order Runge-Kutta method where the vector
\boldsymbol{c}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})
and
\boldsymbol{b}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})
.

2
Bounds for
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}

Let The Runge-Kutta matrix be defined as in (
3
). In order to compute lower and upper bounds for
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}
, we firstly have to understand what does a matrix-vector product
\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}
mean. The product behaves as

\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}l_{j}(s)ds\\
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}l_{j}(s)ds\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\int_{0}^{t_{m}}l_{j}(s)ds\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}l_{j}(s)ds\\
\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}l_{j}(s)ds\\
\vdots\\
\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}l_{j}(s)ds\\
\end{bmatrix}.

This motivates us to construct a one-to-one map from a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
to a polynomial
p\in P_{m-1}
, where
p:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}l_{j}
.
This is uniquely defined because the polynomial
p
passes through
m
points
(t_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
and is of degree
m-1
.
Then, the sum
\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}l_{j}(s)ds
is nothing more than
\int_{0}^{t_{i}}p(s)ds
and we can conclude that multiplying a vector with a matrix
\mathbf{A}
is as computing integrals of a corresponding polynomial.

Now, we are interested in computing the norm
\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\infty}
. By definition, the norm is

\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\infty}=1}\lVert\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\infty},

(4)

but the infinity norm of a matrix can also be computed as

\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\mathbf{A}_{ij}|=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigg|\int_{0}^{t_{i}}l_{j}(s)ds\bigg|,

which is the maximum row-sum of absolute values of its entries. From this, we can see that

\displaystyle t_{i}=\bigg|\int_{0}^{t_{i}}1ds\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}l_{j}(s)ds\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{t_{i}}l_{j}(s)ds\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigg|\int_{0}^{t_{i}}l_{j}(s)ds\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\mathbf{A}_{ij}|\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\infty}.

This yields a lower bound:
t_{m}\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\infty}
.

Now we will redefine the definition of the matrix norm
(
4
)
in a way that we do not compute the maximum over all vectors

\boldsymbol{x}
for which
\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}=1
, but over uniquely defined polynomials
p
since
\mathbb{R}^{m}\ni\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto p\in P_{m-1}
is a one-to-one map, as discussed above.
Proving
\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
is then equivalent to proving

\max_{1\leq i\leq M}\bigg|\int_{0}^{t_{i}}p(s)ds\bigg|\leq 1

(5)

holds, for
p\in P_{m-1}
and
\max_{1\leq i\leq M}|p(t_{i})|=1
.

2.1
Exact quadrature for degree
2m-2
or higher

First, using the integral Cauchy–Bunyakovsky-Schwarz inequality on the scalar product defined as in (
1
), we can get an upper bound on

\big|\int_{0}^{t_{i}}p(s)ds\big|
as

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{t_{i}}p(s)ds\bigg|^{2}=\bigg|\int_{0}^{t_{i}}p(s)\cdot 1ds\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\int_{0}^{t_{i}}p^{2}(s)ds\bigg|\bigg|\int_{0}^{t_{i}}1^{2}ds\bigg|

(6)

\displaystyle\leq t_{i}\int_{0}^{t_{i}}p^{2}(s)ds

(7)

\displaystyle\leq t_{i}\int_{0}^{1}p^{2}(s)ds.

(8)

Since
p^{2}\in P_{2m-2}
, using the quadrature rule (
2
) which correctly
computes integrals of polynomials of degree
2m-2
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}p^{2}(s)ds=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}p^{2}(t_{i})\leq\underbrace{\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\bigg)}_{=1}\max_{1\leq i\leq M}p^{2}(t_{i})=\max_{1\leq i\leq M}p^{2}(t_{i}).

Because we are computing (
5
) over polynomials which satisfy

\max_{1\leq i\leq M}|p(t_{i})|=1
, we know that
\max_{1\leq i\leq M}p^{2}(t_{i})=1
, thus

\int_{0}^{1}p^{2}(s)ds\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq M}p^{2}(t_{i})=1.

(9)

Now (
8
) + (
9
) yields

\bigg|\int_{0}^{t_{i}}p(s)ds\bigg|^{2}\leq t_{i}.

Thus,

t_{i}\leq\bigg|\int_{0}^{t_{i}}p(s)ds\bigg|\leq\sqrt{t_{i}}.

This proves that
t_{m}\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{t_{m}}\leq 1
holds for nodes originating from the Gaussian quadrature that integrates polynomials of degree
2m-2
exactly.

2.2
Gauss-Lobatto nodes

The Gauss-Lobatto quadrature integrates polynomials of accuracy
2m-3
, therefore the same
argument why is
\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
does not hold. However, we will show that this is
still true for
m\geq 2
. The Gauss-Lobatto quadrature on
[0,1]
is
defined as

\int_{0}^{1}p(s)ds=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}p(t_{i})+R_{m},

(10)

where
R_{m}=-cp^{(2m-2)}(\xi),\,c\geq 0,\,\xi\in[0,1]
, see
[
2
]
for details.
If we manage to bind
\int_{0}^{1}p^{2}(s)ds\leq 1
, then using the same train of thought and arguments as in
(
8
)
, we are done.

Let
p^{2}(s)=as^{2m-2}+\dots
, where
a>0
. We can write

\int_{0}^{1}p^{2}(s)ds=\int_{0}^{1}\big(p^{2}(s)-as^{2m-2}\big)ds+\int_{0}^{1}as^{2m-2}ds.

The polynomial
p^{2}(s)-as^{2m-2}
is of degree
2m-3
and can be computed with
quadrature (
10
) as

\int_{0}^{1}\big(p^{2}(s)-as^{2m-2}\big)ds=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\big(p^{2}(t_{i})-at_{i}^{2m-2}\big).

This yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}p^{2}(s)ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\big(p^{2}(t_{i})-at_{i}^{2m-2}\big)+\int_{0}^{1}as^{2m-2}ds

(11)

\displaystyle\leq 1-a\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}t_{i}^{2m-2}+\int_{0}^{1}as^{2m-2}ds,

(12)

where the inequality holds because
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}p^{2}(t_{i})\leq 1
for

\max_{1\leq i\leq M}|p(t_{i})|\leq 1
.
Now using the quadrature (
10
) on a function
t^{2m-2}
yields

\int_{0}^{1}s^{2m-2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}t_{i}^{2m-2}-c(2m-2)!

and combining this with (
12
) gives

\int_{0}^{1}p^{2}(s)ds\leq 1-ac(2m-2)!\leq 1,

because
ac\geq 0
.
This proves that
t_{m}\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{t_{m}}\leq 1
is also true for the Gauss-Lobatto quadrature.

3
The characteristic polynomial of
\mathbf{A}

Let

\omega(t)=(t-t_{1})\dots(t-t_{m})=t^{m}+a_{m-1}t^{m-1}+\dots+a_{0}

be the polynomial of nodes.
If
t_{i}
are the roots of an orthogonal polynomial defining the Gaussian quadrature, then
\omega
is exactly that scaled polynomial. We will show that the characteristic polynomial of
\mathbf{A}
is

p_{A}(\lambda)=m!t^{m}+(m-1)!a_{m-1}t^{m-1}+\dots+a_{0}.

(13)

The eigenvalue of matrix
\mathbf{A}
is a scalar
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
that satisfies

\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\lambda\boldsymbol{x},

for some
\boldsymbol{x}\neq 0
.
In other words, using a one-on-one map to the polynomial space
P_{m-1}
, we can reformulate the eigenproblem as finding a polynomial
p\neq 0
such that

\int_{0}^{t_{i}}p(s)ds=\lambda p(t_{i}),\quad i=1,\dots,m.

Substituting
p
with
g^{\prime}
, where
g\in P_{m}
yields

g(t_{i})-g(0)=\lambda g^{\prime}(t_{i}),\quad i=1,\dots,m.

(14)

From here we see that the eigenvector represented as a polynomial
g
is not uniquely
defined, because if
g
is a solution of (
14
), then
d_{m}g(t)+d_{0}
is also a solution. Therefore, without loss of generality, we can assume that
g
has the form

g(t)=t^{m}+d_{m-1}t^{m-1}+\dots+d_{1}t.

Setting
d_{m}=1
is equivalent to imposing that the eigenvector is normalized which yields a uniqueness of the eigenvector.
However, here we impose
\boldsymbol{x}
has a fixed infinity norm, one that may deffer from 1.
The part where we demand that
d_{0}=0
comes from substituting
p
with
g^{\prime}
.
As a consequence we have
g(0)=0
.
With these assumptions,
g
is uniquely defined since we have
m
nonlinear equations and
m
unknowns:
(d_{m-1},\dots,d_{1},\lambda)
that are described with

g(t_{i})=\lambda g^{\prime}(t_{i}),\quad i=1,\dots,m.

(15)

Let
G(t):=g(t)-\lambda g^{\prime}(t)
, where g is the solution of (
15
).
The difference
G-\omega
is a polynomial of degree
m-1
and both
\omega
and
G
are monic polynomials of degree
m
.
Because
G-\omega
is zero in
m
different points, we conclude that
G=\omega
.
Equations (
15
) can now be rewritten in a continuous form as

g(t)-\lambda g^{\prime}(t)=\omega(t).

Since the polynomials coefficients on the left side should be equal to the coefficients on the right side of the upper equation, we get

\displaystyle-\lambda d_{1}

\displaystyle=a_{0}

\displaystyle d_{1}-2\lambda d_{2}

\displaystyle=a_{1}

\displaystyle d_{2}-3\lambda d_{3}

\displaystyle=a_{2}

\displaystyle d_{m-2}-(m-1)\lambda d_{m-1}

\displaystyle=a_{m-2}

\displaystyle d_{m-1}-m\lambda

\displaystyle=a_{m-1}.

Substituting the equations from the bottom to the second from the top, we get

d_{1}=m!\lambda^{m-1}+(m-1)!a_{m-1}\lambda^{m-2}+\dots+2\lambda a_{2},

(16)

whereas from the first one we get
d_{1}=-a_{0}/\lambda
.
Substituting this in (
16
) we end up with a polynomial
p_{A}
as in (
13
) which zeros are the eigenvalues of our matrix
\mathbf{A}
. In other words,
p_{A}
is exactly the characteristic polynomial of
\mathbf{A}
.

It remains to comment why
\lambda=0
is not a viable option for an eigenvalue. Matrix
\mathbf{A}
is a mapping in a fashion

(t_{1}^{k},\dots,t_{m}^{k})\rightarrow\frac{1}{k+1}(t_{1}^{k+1},\dots,t_{m}^{k+1}),\quad k=0,\dots,m-1,

if the quadrature rule defining
\mathbf{A}
integrates polynomials of degree
m-1
correctly.
The vectors
(t_{1}^{k},\dots,t_{m}^{k})
form columns of the Vandermonde matrix
\boldsymbol{V}
which is known to be nonsingular if the points
t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
are distinct. Because of this, we have
\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{V}=\boldsymbol{D}_{m}\boldsymbol{V}
, where
\boldsymbol{D}_{m}=\operatorname{diag}(1,1/2,\dots,1/m)
. From here we see that
\mathbf{A}
is nonsingular as a product of nonsingular matrices.

4
Conclusion

We proved that
t_{m}\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{t_{m}}\leq 1
holds for nodes originating from the Gaussian quadrature that integrates polynomials of degree
2m-2
exactly.
Is is also true for the Gauss-Lobatto quadrature.
Additionally, the characteristic polynomial of
\mathbf{A}
can be found, when the matrix is nonsingular.
The matrix is nonsingular if it originates from a quadrature that integrates polynomials in an exact way up to degree
m-1
.
