Complete Norm Preserving Extensions of Holomorphic Functions
Jim Agler, Łukasz Kosiński, John E. M c Carthy

Abstract
We show that for every connected analytic subvariety V V there is a pseudoconvex set Ω \Omega such that every bounded matrix-valued holomorphic function on V V extends isometrically to Ω \Omega . We prove that if V V is two analytic disks intersecting at one point, if every bounded scalar valued holomorphic function extends isometrically to Ω \Omega , then so does every matrix-valued function. In the special case that Ω \Omega is the symmetrized bidisk, we show that this cannot be done by finding a linear isometric extension from the functions that vanish at one point.

Introduction

By a
Cartan pair
we mean a pair
(\Omega,V)
where

\Omega
is a connected pseudo-convex set in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and
V
is an analytic subvariety of
\Omega
.
The name is homage to H. Cartan, who proved that every holomorphic function on
V
(i.e. a function that locally agrees with the restriction of a holomorphic function defined on an
open set in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
) extends to a holomorphic function on all of
\Omega

[
6
]
. We say that a pair

(\Omega,V)
is a
norm preserving pair
(np pair for short) if it is a Cartan pair with the additional property that every bounded
holomorphic function on
V
extends isometrically to a bounded holomorphic function on
\Omega
.

For a fixed domain
\Omega
, several papers have studied what analytic subvarieties gave rise to np pairs
[
3
,
8
,
14
,
11
,
12
]
. If
\Omega
is suitably nice, the conclusion of these papers was that
V
had to be a holomorphic retract of
\Omega
for
(\Omega,V)
to be an np pair.
However, this is not true in general. The simplest example is the np pair
(\Delta,T)
,
where
\Delta
is the diamond

\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:\ |z_{1}|+|z_{2}|<1\}
, and
T=(\mathbb{D}\times\{0\})\cup(\{0\}\times\mathbb{D})
.

In
[
1
]
, the perspective was shifted, to start with
V
and try to find a pseudoconvex set
G
so that

(G,V)
forms an np pair.
We showed this can always be done:

Theorem 1.1
.

[
1
]

If
(\Omega,V)
is a Cartan pair, then their exists
G
such that
(G,V)
is an np pair.

The first goal of this note is to extend Theorem
1.1
to the matrix and operator-valued case.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
G
be a domain of holomorphy, and
V
an analytic subvariety of
G
. We say
(G,V)

if for every separable Hilbert space

.

Theorem 1.3
.

If
(\Omega,V)
is a Cartan pair, and
V
is connected, then their exists
G
such that
(G,V)
is a complete np-pair.

We prove Theorem
1.3
in Section
3

Since any Stein manifold embeds properly as a submanifold into

Notice that if

We do not know the answer to the following question.

3
Complete np pairs

Throughout this section we shall assume that
(\Omega,V)
is a Cartan pair and that
V
is connected.

It was proved by Bishop
[
5
]
and Fujimoto
[
7
]
that if
(\Omega,V)
is a Cartan pair, then every
B(\mathcal{H})
-valued holomorphic function on
V
extends to a
B(\mathcal{H})
-valued holomorphic function on
\Omega
. With this tool in hand one could try to prove Theorem
1.3
by repeating the proof from one dimensional case. The main problem that appears here is that Montel’s theorem fails for holomorphic functions with values in infinite dimensional vector spaces. There are topologies on
B(\mathcal{H})
for which a Montel-type theorem does hold and even such that
{\rm Hol}(V,B(\mathcal{H}))
is paracompact, but then the projection

{\rm Hol}(\Omega,B(\mathcal{H}))\to{\rm Hol}(V,B(\mathcal{H}))

is not not open, so Michael’s selection theorem cannot be used.
So we shall adopt a new strategy, which is to establish a link between complete and linear norm preserving extensions.

Recall that
f:\Omega\to B(\mathcal{H})
is holomorphic if and only if it is weakly holomorphic, i.e.
\Lambda(f)
is a holomorphic function for any
\Lambda\in B(\mathcal{H})^{\prime}
. If
f
is locally bounded, a weaker condition needs to be verified for a function to be holomorphic:

Lemma 3.1
.

If
G
is open, and
f:G\to B(\mathcal{H})
is locally bounded, then
f
is holomorphic if and only if
z\mapsto\langle f(z)h,k\rangle
is a holomorphic function.

Proof.

This can be proved in a similar way to proving that weakly holomorphic functions are holomorphic. See
[
13
, Thm. 6.1]
for a formal proof.
∎

The Montel theorem fails in
{\rm Hol}(\Omega,B(\mathcal{H}))
. However, it is true if we equip
B(\mathcal{H})
with the WOT topology:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(f_{n})\subset\mathscr{S}(\Omega,B(\mathcal{H}))
. Then there is a subsequence
(f_{n_{k}})
and
f\in\mathscr{S}(\Omega,B(\mathcal{H}))
such that
\langle f_{n_{k}}(z)h,k\rangle
converges to
\langle f(z)h,k\rangle
locally uniformly on
\Omega
for each
h,k\in\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

For an orthonormal basis
e_{i}
we apply the regular Montel theorem
to
\langle f_{n}(z)e_{i},e_{j}\rangle
, and then using a Cantor diagonal argument we end up with
f
.

It is elementary to see that
f
satisfies desired properties.
∎

For a final proof we need a few more preparatory results.

Lemma 3.3
.

Fix a point
a\in V
.
Then
(\Omega,V)
is completely norm preserving if and only if each
f\in\mathscr{S}_{a}(V,B(\mathcal{H}))
has an extension to an element
F\in\mathscr{S}(\Omega,B(\mathcal{H}))
.

Proof.

If
||f(a)||<1
, there exists an automorphism
m
of the unit ball of
B(\mathcal{H})
such that
(m\circ f)(a)=0

[
9
]
. As
h=m\circ f\in\mathscr{S}(V,B(\mathcal{H}))
, the assumption of the lemma implies that there exists
H\in\mathscr{S}(\Omega,B(\mathcal{H}))
such that
H|V=h
. But then if we define
F=m^{-1}\circ H
,
F\in\mathscr{S}(\Omega,B(\mathcal{H}))
and
F|V=f
.

If
||f(a)||=1
, we approximate
f
uniformly with
f_{n}\in\mathscr{S}(\Omega,B(\mathcal{H}))
such that
||f_{n}(a)||<1

(eg.
f_{n}=\frac{n-1}{n}f
). It follows from the previous case that there are
F_{n}\in\mathscr{S}(\Omega,B(\mathcal{H}))
that extend
f_{n}
. Applying Lemma
3.2
to
F_{n}
we find
F\in\mathscr{S}(\Omega,B(\mathcal{H}))
that clearly extends
f
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

[
1
, Lem. 3.3]

If
(\Omega,V)
is a Cartan pair and
a\in V
, then
\mathscr{S}_{a}(V)
is a compact subset of

\mathcal{O}(V)
.

The following result was proved in
[
1
, Thm. 3.5]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
a\in V
, there is a continuous function
S:{\rm Hol}(V)\to{\rm Hol}(\Omega)
such that
S(f)|V=f
for
f\in\mathcal{O}(V)
. Moreover, for each
a\in V
there is an open
G\subset\Omega
such that
(G,V)
is a Cartan pair and
S(\mathscr{S}_{a}(V))\subset\mathscr{S}_{a}(G)
.

With this tool in hand we can prove the following linear extension result. Let
L^{2}_{h}(G)
denote
the weighted Bergman space obtained from using the Gaussian measure. (If
G
has finite volume, we could
just use the standard Bergman space).

Lemma 3.6
.

Fix
a\in V
. Then there is a pseudoconvex domain
D
,
V\subset D\subset\Omega
, and a linear isomorphic extension map

\mathscr{S}_{a}(V)\to\mathscr{S}_{a}(D).

Proof.

Let
G
and
S:{\rm Hol}(V)\to{\rm Hol}(G)
be as in Lemma
3.5
.

Then the inclusion
\iota:\mathscr{S}_{a}(G)\subset L_{h}^{2}(G)
is continuous;
composing with
S
we get a continuous extension operator

\iota\circ S:\mathscr{S}_{a}(V)\to L_{h}^{2}(G).

Let
P
be the orthogonal projection from
L^{2}_{h}(G)
onto
\{g\in L_{h}^{2}(\Omega):\ g|_{V}=0\}^{\perp}
.
Then

E(f)\ =\ P[\iota\circ S(f)]

is the
element in
L_{h}^{2}(G)
that extends
f
and has minimal norm. It is straightforward to see that it is linear. By the Cauchy formulas the inclusion
L_{h}^{2}(G)\subset\mathcal{O}(G)
is continuous. Thus, we can construct a continuous and linear extension operator (which we will also call
E
)

E:\mathscr{S}_{a}(V)\to\mathcal{O}(G).

Define

D_{1}:=\left(\bigcap\{z\in G:|E(f)(z)|<1\ \forall f\in\mathscr{S}_{a}(V)\}\right)^{\circ}.

To see
V\subset D_{1}
, suppose
b\in V\setminus D_{1}
. Then there exist sequences
b_{n}\in G
converging to
b
,
and
f_{n}
in
\mathscr{S}_{a}(V)
, such that
|E(f_{n})(b_{n})|\geq 1
.
By Lemma
3.4
, some subsequence of
(f_{n})
converges to a function
f\in\mathscr{S}_{a}(V)
.
Since
E
is continuous,
|E(f)(b)|\geq 1
. This would violate the maximum principle.

Define
D
to be the connected component of
D_{1}
that contains
V
.
By
[
10
, Prop. 4.1.7]
,

D
is pseudoconvex.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
(\Omega,V)
be a Cartan pair,
a\in V
, and assume that there is an isometric linear operator

E:\mathscr{S}_{a}(V)\to\mathscr{S}_{a}(\Omega).

Then
(\Omega,V)
is a complete np-pair.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
3.3
that it is enough to show that any mapping in
\mathscr{S}_{a}(V,B(\mathcal{H}))
has an extension to
\mathscr{S}(\Omega,B(\mathcal{H}))
. So fix
f\in\mathscr{S}_{a}(V,B(\mathcal{H}))
and
z\in\Omega
. Applying the Riesz reprezentation theorem to
the maps

\mathcal{H}\ni k\mapsto E(\langle f(\cdot)h,k\rangle)(z),

where
h\in\mathcal{H}
, we get for each
z\in\Omega
and
h\in\mathcal{H}
,
a vector

\Psi(z,h)\in\mathcal{H}
such that

E(\langle f(\cdot)h,k\rangle)(z)\ =\ \langle\Psi(z,h),k\rangle.

Note that
h\mapsto\Psi(z,h)
is linear since
E
is,
so we can define
F(z):\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
by
F(z)h=\Psi(z,h)
. It is straightforward to check that
F(z)\in B(\mathcal{H}))
and
||F(z)||\leq 1
. Since
z\mapsto F(z)
is holomorphic by Lemma
3.1
, we are done.
∎

Combining Proposition
3.7
and Lemma
3.6
, we have proved Theorem
1.3
.

To go to infinite dimensions, we shall use realization formulas.

Proof of theorem

.

The implications (iii)
\Rightarrow

\Rightarrow

Let us show (i)

Let
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}({\mathcal{T}},B(\mathcal{H}))

By using the newtwork realization formula (

and unitary operators

\varphi(\psi_{1}(\lambda))=A_{1}\lambda+B_{1}\lambda(I-D_{1}\lambda)^{-1}C_{1}\lambda

\varphi(\psi_{2}(\lambda))=A_{2}\lambda+B_{2}\lambda(I-D_{2}\lambda)^{-1}C_{2}\lambda.

Replacing
U_{1}
with
\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}A_{1}&B_{1}&0\\
C_{1}&D_{1}&0\\

with
\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}A_{2}&0&B_{2}\\

where

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{K}

Consider the following holomorphic map

Claim.
There is a sequence
\Phi_{n}\in\mathscr{S}(G,B(\mathcal{M}))
that approximates

in the following sense:
\Phi_{n}(\psi_{1}(\lambda))=\lambda U_{1}\,
and

If

\tau\lambda

that are diagonalizable and converge

Each

Linear vs. complete

Consider two particular examples:

1.

The diamond
\Delta=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:\ |z_{1}|+|z_{2}|<1\}
and the two crossed discs
T:=(\mathbb{D}\times\{0\})\cup(\{0\}\times\mathbb{D}).

2.

The symmetrized bidisk
\mathbb{G}_{2}
and the set
{\mathcal{T}}=\{(2\lambda,\lambda^{2}):\lambda\in\mathbb{D}\}\cup\{(\beta+\bar{\beta}\lambda,\lambda):\lambda\in\mathbb{D}\}
from (
1.8
).

It follows from Theorem
1.5
that both
(\Delta,T)
and
(\mathbb{G}_{2},{\mathcal{T}})
are complete np-pairs.

Another way to prove this for
(\Delta,T)
is to observe that the map that sends
f
in

\mathscr{S}_{0}(T,B(\mathcal{H}))

to the function
\{z\mapsto f(z_{1},0)+f(0,z_{2})\}
in
\mathscr{S}(\Delta,B(\mathcal{H}))
is linear, and then apply Proposition

3.7
. We shall show that this argument cannot be used for
(\mathbb{G}_{2},{\mathcal{T}})
.

Let us introduce some additional notation before proving this.
Let
\Sigma=\{(2\lambda,\lambda^{2}):\ \lambda\in\mathbb{D}\}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}=\{0\}\times\mathbb{D}
.
Let

[f(\lambda),g(\lambda)]
denote the function on
\Sigma\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
that is equal to
f(\lambda)
on
(2\lambda,\lambda^{2})
and
g(\lambda)
on
(0,\lambda)
.
For
b\in\mathbb{D}
let
m_{b}
be a Möbius map
m_{b}(\lambda)=\frac{b-\lambda}{1-\bar{b}\lambda}.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be given by (
1.8
), and
let
a\in{\mathcal{T}}
. There is no
linear isometric extension
operator from
\mathscr{S}_{a}({\mathcal{T}})
to
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{G}_{2})
.

Proof.

Since all sets of the form (
1.8
) are holomorphically equivalent, it suffices to prove the assertion for
{\mathcal{T}}=\Sigma\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
.

For unimodular
\alpha
and
\beta
consider the function
f_{\alpha,\beta}:{\mathcal{T}}\to\mathbb{D}
given by the formula:

f_{\alpha,\beta}(s,p)=\begin{cases}\alpha s/2,\quad\text{on $\Sigma$},\\
\beta p,\quad\text{on ${\mathcal{D}}_{0}$}.\end{cases}

So
f_{\alpha,\beta}=[\alpha\lambda,\beta\lambda]
.
Let
\omega=\beta\alpha^{-1}
. It was shown in
[
2
]
that
\alpha\Phi_{\omega}
extends
f_{\alpha,\beta}
where

\Phi_{\omega}(s,p):=\frac{s/2+\omega p}{1+\omega s/2}.

Claim.
We shall show that
\alpha\Phi_{\beta\alpha^{-1}}
is the unique np extension of
f_{\alpha,\beta}
to
\mathbb{G}_{2}\to\mathbb{D}
:

To prove the claim let
F
be some extension of
f_{\alpha,\beta}
that has norm 1.
Let
\omega\in\mathbb{T}
. Then

F(s,p)=\alpha s/2+\beta(p-(s/2)^{2})+O(s(s^{2}-4p)),

since
F
minus the first two terms vanishes on
{\mathcal{T}}
.
With
\pi
as in (
2.1
), we get

F(\pi(\lambda,\omega\lambda))=\alpha\frac{1+\omega}{2}\lambda-\beta\left(\frac{1-\omega}{2}\right)^{2}\lambda^{2}+O(\lambda^{3}).

Then the Schwarz lemma implies that the map

\lambda\mapsto\frac{1}{\lambda}F(\pi(\lambda,\omega\lambda))

is a Möbius map from
\mathbb{D}
to
\mathbb{D}
. Thus if
G
is another extension,
F\circ\pi
and
G\circ\pi
coincide on
\{|\lambda|=|\mu|:\ (\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{D}^{2}\}
, and the claim follows.

\lhd

Suppose that there is
a\in{\mathcal{T}}
and a linear isometric operator
L:\mathscr{S}_{a}({\mathcal{T}})\to\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{G}_{2})
. Let us consider two cases.

i)
a=(0,\lambda_{0})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
. Note that
\lambda\mapsto[m_{\beta a}(\alpha\lambda),m_{\beta a}(\beta\lambda)]
belongs to the Schur class
\mathscr{S}_{a}({\mathcal{T}})
. The crucial fact following from the Claim is that the equality

L[m_{\beta\lambda_{0}}(\alpha\lambda),m_{\beta\lambda_{0}}(\beta\lambda)]=m_{\beta\lambda_{0}}(\alpha\Phi_{\beta/\alpha})

(5.2)

holds for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{T}
.
Writing out (
5.2
), and using
\omega=\bar{\alpha}\beta
, we get

L\left[\frac{\beta\lambda_{0}-\alpha\lambda}{1-\bar{\beta}\bar{\lambda}_{0}\alpha\lambda},\frac{\beta\lambda_{0}-\beta\lambda}{1-\lambda\bar{\lambda}_{0}}\right]\ =\ \frac{\beta\lambda_{0}-\alpha\Phi_{\omega}(s,p)}{1-\bar{\beta}\bar{\lambda}_{0}\alpha\Phi_{\omega}(s,p)}.

Dividing by
\beta
we get

L\left[\frac{\lambda_{0}-\lambda\bar{\omega}}{1-\lambda\bar{\lambda}_{0}\bar{\omega}},\frac{\lambda_{0}-\lambda}{1-\lambda\bar{\lambda}_{0}}\right]=\frac{\lambda_{0}-\bar{\omega}\Phi_{\omega}(s,p)}{1-\bar{\lambda}_{0}\bar{\omega}\Phi_{\omega}(s,p)}.

(5.3)

Write

\Phi_{\omega}(s,p)\ =\ \frac{s/2+\omega p}{1+\omega s/2}\ =\ \frac{\bar{\omega}s/2+p}{\bar{\omega}+s/2},

and expand both sides of (
5.3
) in powers of
\bar{\omega}
.
Expanding the left hand side we get

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\lambda_{0}-\lambda\bar{\omega}}{1-\lambda\bar{\lambda}_{0}\bar{\omega}},\frac{\lambda_{0}-\lambda}{1-\lambda\bar{\lambda}_{0}}\right]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\bar{\omega}^{n}f_{n}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\lambda_{0},\frac{\lambda_{0}-\lambda}{1-\lambda\bar{\lambda}_{0}}\right]+\bar{\omega}\left[(|\lambda_{0}|^{2}-1)\lambda,0\right]+\sum_{n\geq 2}\bar{\omega}^{n}f_{n},

where
f_{n}\in H^{\infty}({\mathcal{T}})
,
f_{n}(a)=0
, and the series converges uniformly, so
L
can be applied term by term.
The right hand side gives

\frac{\lambda_{0}-\bar{\omega}\Phi_{\omega}(s,p)}{1-\bar{\lambda}_{0}\bar{\omega}\Phi_{\omega}(s,p)}\ =\ \lambda_{0}-\bar{\omega}\frac{2p}{s}(1-|\lambda_{0}|^{2})+O(\bar{\omega}^{2}).

Comparing the constant terms, we would have

L\left[\lambda_{0},\frac{\lambda_{0}-\lambda}{1-\lambda\bar{\lambda}_{0}}\right]=\lambda_{0},

a contradiction.

ii) We are left with the case
a=(2\lambda_{0},\lambda_{0}^{2})\in\Sigma
,
\lambda_{0}\neq 0
. We shall proceed as before starting with a function
[m_{\alpha\lambda_{0}}(\alpha\lambda),m_{\alpha\lambda_{0}}(\beta\lambda)]
that clearly lies in
\mathscr{S}_{a}({\mathcal{T}})
.
As before, we get that

L\left[\frac{\lambda_{0}-\lambda}{1-\bar{\lambda}_{0}\lambda},\frac{\lambda_{0}-\omega\lambda}{1-\bar{\lambda}_{0}\omega\lambda}\right]=\frac{\lambda_{0}-\Phi_{\omega}(s,p)}{1-\bar{\lambda}_{0}\Phi_{\omega}(s,p)},\quad\omega\in\mathbb{T}.

(5.4)

Expanding in powers of
\omega
and looking at the coefficient of
\omega
, we get

L[0,\lambda]=\frac{p-(s/2)^{2}}{(1-\bar{\lambda}_{0}s/2)^{2}}.

(5.5)

As
[0,\lambda]
lies in
\mathscr{S}_{a}({\mathcal{T}})
, we must have that the function
(s,p)\mapsto\frac{p-(s/2)^{2}}{(1-\bar{\lambda}_{0}s/2)^{2}}
sends the symmetrized bidisc to the unit disc. In particular, putting
(s,p)=(\lambda+\mu,\lambda\mu)
for
\lambda,\mu
in the unit disc we
would get
the inequality

|(\lambda-\mu)/2|\ \leq\ |1-\bar{\lambda}_{0}(\lambda+\mu)/2|

(5.6)

holds for
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{D}^{2}
.
This however is not possible whenever
\lambda_{0}\neq 0
.
Indeed, let
t=|\lambda_{0}|
. Then (
5.6
) is equivalent to the claim that

|\lambda-\mu|^{2}\ \leq\ |2-t(\lambda+\mu)|^{2}\qquad\forall(\lambda,\mu)\in\overline{\mathbb{D}^{2}},

since by continuity the inequality would extend to the boundary. Assume both
\lambda
and
\mu
are unimodular, then this becomes

-2(1+t^{2})\Re(\bar{\lambda}\mu)+4t\Re(\lambda+\mu)\ \leq\ 2+2t^{2}.

Let
\lambda=e^{i\theta}
and
\mu=e^{-i\theta}
.
We get the inequality

-2(1+t^{2})\cos(2\theta)+4t\cos(\theta)\ \leq\ 2+2t^{2}.

(5.7)

By calculus, the maximum of the left hand side comes when we
choose
\theta
so that

\cos(\theta)=\frac{t}{1+t^{2}},\quad\sin(\theta)=\sqrt{1-\frac{t^{2}}{(1+t^{2})^{2}}}.

Then (
5.7
) becomes

2\frac{1+4t^{2}+t^{4}}{1+t^{2}}\ \leq\ 2(1+t^{2}).

This clearly fails unless
t=0
.
∎
