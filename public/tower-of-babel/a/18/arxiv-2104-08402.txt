Regularized Maximum Likelihood Estimation for the Random Coefficients Model
Fabian Dunker Emil Mendoza Marco Reale University of Canterbury University of Canterbury University of Canterbury

Abstract
The random coefficients model Y i = β 0 i + β 1 i ​ X 1 i + β 2 i ​ X 2 i + … + β d i ​ X d i Y_{i}={\beta_{0}}_{i}+{\beta_{1}}_{i}{X_{1}}_{i}+{\beta_{2}}_{i}{X_{2}}_{i}+\ldots+{\beta_{d}}_{i}{X_{d}}_{i} , with 𝐗 i \mathbf{X}_{i} , Y i Y_{i} , 𝜷 i {\bm{\beta}}_{i} i.i.d, and 𝜷 i {\bm{\beta}}_{i} independent of 𝐗 i \mathbf{X}_{i} is often used to capture unobserved heterogeneity in a population. We propose a quasi-maximum likelihood method to estimate the joint density distribution of the random coefficient model. This method implicitly involves the inversion of the Radon transformation in order to reconstruct the joint distribution, and hence is an inverse problem. Nonparametric estimation for the joint density of 𝜷 i = ( β 0 i , … , β d i ) {\bm{\beta}}_{i}=({\beta_{0}}_{i},\ldots,{\beta_{d}}_{i}) based on kernel methods or Fourier inversion have been proposed in recent years. Most of these methods assume a heavy tailed design density f 𝐗 f_{\mathbf{X}} . To add stability to the solution, we apply regularization methods. We analyze the convergence of the method without assuming heavy tails for f 𝐗 f_{\mathbf{X}} and illustrate performance by applying the method on simulated and real data. To add stability to the solution, we apply a Tikhonov-type regularization method.

Introduction

Unobserved heterogeneity in a population is an important problem in statistics and econometrics. A common way to account for unobserved heterogeneity is the use of random effects or mixed models which usually impose parametric assumptions on the distribution of the random effects. This is a significant restriction on the structure of the heterogeneity. In recent years the linear random coefficient model, which is a nonparametric alternative, attracted significant attention in the literature. This model is given by

Y_{i}=\beta_{0i}+\beta_{1i}X_{1i}+\beta_{2i}X_{2i}+\ldots+\beta_{di}X_{di}.

(1)

With
{\bm{\beta}}_{i}=(\beta_{0i},\beta_{1i},\beta_{2i},\ldots,\beta_{di})^{\top}
and
\mathbf{X}_{i}=(1,X_{1i},X_{2i},\ldots X_{di})^{\top}
we can write (
1
) as
Y_{i}={\bm{\beta}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}
. We assume that
\mathbf{X}_{i}
,
Y_{i}
,
{\bm{\beta}}_{i}
are i.i.d random variables with
{\bm{\beta}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{X}_{i}
being independent, and that
Y_{i}
and
\mathbf{X}_{i}
are observed while
{\bm{\beta}}_{i}
is unknown. The objective is to estimate the joint density
f_{\bm{\beta}}
of
{\bm{\beta}}_{i}
nonparametrically.

In this paper we propose and analyze a regularized maximum likelihood estimator for the density
f_{\bm{\beta}}

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\bm{\beta}}=\argmin_{f\geq 0,\,\|f\|_{L^{1}}=1}-\ell(f|Y_{1},\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots Y_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})+\alpha\mathcal{R}(f)

(2)

Here
\ell
is the log-likelihood functional,
\alpha\geq 0
is a smoothing parameter, and
\mathcal{R}
is a convex lower semi-continuous functional which regularizes the estimator. Both
\alpha
and
\mathcal{R}
are chosen by the statistician. The choice of
\mathcal{R}
can consider a priori information about
f_{\bm{\beta}}
like smoothness or sparsity. Details of the method and examples for
\mathcal{R}
will be presented in Section
2
. Estimating
f_{\bm{\beta}}
from observations of
Y_{i}
and
\mathbf{X}_{i}
is an ill-posed inverse problem. The degree of ill-posedness depends on the tail behavior of the design density
f_{\mathbf{X}}
.

In this paper we give conditions for method (
2
) to achieve the optimal rates for design densities with different tail behavior or with bounded support. We evaluate the the small sample performance in simulations and have a data application in consumer demand.

Nonparametric estimation in model (
1
) with one regressor, i.e.
d=1

Beran and Hall, 1992

. Testing in model (
1

Breunig and Hoderlein, 2018
;
Dunker et al., 2019
.

Regularized maximum likelihood methods have been considered for other problems than model (
1

Werner and Hohage, 2012

2
Method

Throughout this paper we assume that
{\bm{\beta}}
has a Lebesgue density
f_{\bm{\beta}}
. If in addition the conditional density
f_{Y|\mathbf{X}}
exists, the two densities are connected by the integral equation

\displaystyle f_{Y|\mathbf{X}}(y|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})=\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}\big\{\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=y\big\}f_{\bm{\beta}}(\mathbf{b})d\mu_{d}(\mathbf{b})=\int_{\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=y}f_{\bm{\beta}}(\mathbf{b})d\mu_{d}(\mathbf{b})

(3)

where
\mathbbm{1}
denotes the index function and
\mu_{d}
the induced Lebesgue measure on the
d
-dimensional hyper-plane given by
\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{X}=Y
. It was pointed out in
Beran et al., 1996
that (
3
) becomes the well known Radon transformation
f_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}}=\mathcal{R}(f_{\bm{\beta}})
when
\mathbf{X}
and
Y
are normalized to
{\bm{\Theta}}=\mathbf{X}/\|\mathbf{X}\|
and
S=Y/\|\mathbf{X}\|
. The injectivity of the Randon transformation implies that
f_{\bm{\beta}}
is identified when the support of
{\bm{\Theta}}
contains an open neighborhood, see
Masten, 2018
for minimal conditions.

It is well known that an integral equation of the first kind like (
3
) and the Radon transformation are ill-posed inverse problems, i.e.
f_{\bm{\beta}}
does not depend continuously on
f_{Y|\mathbf{X}}
or
f_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}}
. Nonparametric estimation of
f_{\bm{\beta}}
requires regularization. It is possible to derive a nonparametric estimate for
f_{\bm{\beta}}
by inverting the Radon transformation on a nonparametric estimator
\hat{f}_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}}
. We want to avoid this two step approach and suggest an estimator that allows the statistician to use a priori knowledge of
f_{\bm{\beta}}
if available.

Our method is based on maximum likelihood estimation. The likelihood functional for
f_{\bm{\beta}}
given the observations is

\mathcal{L}(f_{\bm{\beta}}|Y,\mathbf{X})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbbm{R}}\mathbbm{1}\big\{{\bm{\beta}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}=Y_{i}\big\}f_{\bm{\beta}}(\mathbf{b})d\mu(\mathbf{b}),

(4)

which leads to the expression for the average log-likelihood

\bar{\ell}(f_{\bm{\beta}}|Y,\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[\int_{\mathbbm{R}}\mathbbm{1}\big\{{\bm{\beta}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}=Y_{i}\big\}f_{\bm{\beta}}(b)d\mu(\mathbf{b})\right].

(5)

Direct maximization of
\bar{\ell}(f_{\bm{\beta}}|Y,\mathbf{X})
over all densities it not feasible due to ill-posedness and will lead to overfitting. We stabilize the problem by adding a penalty term
\alpha\mathcal{R}(f_{\bm{\beta}})
and state the estimator as a minimization problem with negative log-likelihood

\displaystyle\hat{f_{\bm{\beta}}}_{\alpha}=\argmin_{f\geq 0,\,\|f\|_{L^{1}}=1}-\bar{\ell}(f|Y,\mathbf{X})+\alpha\mathcal{R}(f).

(6)

Here
\alpha\geq 0
is a regularization parameter that controls a bias variance trade-off. Furthermore,
\mathcal{R}
is a convex lower semi-continuous functional. Typical choices for
\mathcal{R}
are

•

Squared
L^{2}
norm:
\mathcal{R}(f)=\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|f\|_{2}^{2}
.

•

Sobolev Norm for
H^{r}
:
\mathcal{R}(f)=\|f_{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\|f^{\prime}_{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\ldots+\|f^{(r)}_{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}
.

•

L^{p}
norm:
\mathcal{R}(f)=\|f\|_{L^{p}}^{p}=\|f\|_{p}^{p}
with
0\leq p\leq\infty
.

•

Sobolev Norm for
W^{r}_{p}
:
\mathcal{R}(f)=\|f_{\beta}\|_{p}^{p}+\|f^{\prime}_{\beta}\|_{p}^{p}+\ldots+\|f^{(r)}_{\beta}\|_{p}^{p}
.

•

Entropy:
\mathcal{R}(f)=\int{f(\mathbf{b})\ln{f(\mathbf{b})}d\mathbf{b}}
.

While an
L^{p}
penalty implies that the estimator
\hat{f}_{\bm{\beta}}
is in
L^{p}
, a Sobolev norm enforces more smoothness on the estimator. Note that an entropy penalty is natural for estimating a density. It does not imply that
f_{\bm{\beta}}
belongs to a particular
L^{p}
space other than
L^{1}
and only enforces a finite entropy. If a priori knowledge about
f_{\bm{\beta}}
like smoothness is available,
\mathcal{R}
can be chosen accordingly.

In addition to the regularization functional the regularization parameter
\alpha
needs to be chosen. As usual in nonparametric estimation the optimal
\alpha
depends on the unknown
f_{\bm{\beta}}
. We propose adaptive estimation by Lepskii’s principle which is popular for inverse problems, e.g.
Tsybakov, 2000
,
Bauer and Hohage, 2005
,
Mathé, 2006
,
Hohage and Werner, 2016
,
Werner, 2018
, and
Holzmann and Meister, 2020
. It is computationally cheaper than many other parameter selection rules like cross-validation, which is a big advantage since our problem is already computationally demanding.

The principle works as follows: we compute the estimator
\hat{f}_{{\bm{\beta}}_{\alpha_{1}}},\ldots,\hat{f}_{{\bm{\beta}}_{\alpha_{m}}}
for several parameters
\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{1}<\ldots<\alpha_{m}
together with a bound on the standard deviation of each estimator which is a non-increasing function
\Psi:\{i,\ldots,m\}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
which is specified in Theorem
5
. The Lepskii regularization parameter
\alpha_{\bar{j}}
is selected by

\displaystyle\bar{j}:=\max\{j\leq m,\|{f_{\bm{\beta}}}_{\alpha_{i}},{f_{\bm{\beta}}}_{\alpha_{j}}\|\leq 2\Psi(i),\text{ for all }i\leq j\}.

For further details on this parameter choice we refer to
Lepskiĭ, 1991
,
Mathé, 2006
, and
Hohage and Werner, 2016
.

Our method can be interpreted as an elastic net with
L^{1}
penalty combined with the penalty term given by
\mathcal{R}
. It was poined out in
Heiss et al., 2019
that the constraint
\|f_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{L^{1}}=1
together with a finite difference discretization of
f_{\bm{\beta}}
is equivalent to an
\ell^{1}
penalty on the discretized values of
f_{\bm{\beta}}
. Without the additional penalty term
\alpha\mathcal{R}(f_{\bm{\beta}})
this would lead to an unwanted shrinkage in the estimate which would set
\hat{f}_{\bm{\beta}}
to
0
at most grid points and produce relatively large values for
\hat{f}_{\bm{\beta}}
at only a few grid points. To reduce this LASSO-type effect
Heiss et al., 2019
introduce an additional quadratic constraint which transforms the method into an elastic net. This is analogous to our regularization term
\alpha\mathcal{R}(f_{\bm{\beta}})
although our setup is more flexible since it allows for a wide range of regularization functionals and not only for quadratic
\mathcal{R}
.

3
Convergence rates

In this section we establish some theoretical properties of the estimator, such as the existence, and uniqueness of the solution to (
6
), as well as its convergence properties. For technical reasons, we will state the theory for the normalized variables
{\bm{\Theta}}=\mathbf{X}/\|\mathbf{X}\|
and
S=Y/\|\mathbf{X}\|
. Note that the estimator
\hat{f}_{\bm{\beta}}
in (
6
) does not change when we replace
\mathbf{X}
by
{\bm{\Theta}}
and
Y
by
S
.

3.1
Assumptions

We define the linear compact integral operator

(Tf)({\bm{\theta}},s)=\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}\big\{\mathbf{b}^{\top}{\bm{\theta}}=s\big\}f(\mathbf{b})d\mu_{d}(\mathbf{b}).

Hence, we are solving the inverse ill-posed problem

f_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}}=Tf_{\beta}.

(7)

We start by discussing the assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

We have i.i.d. samples
S_{i},{\bm{\Theta}}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
generated by model (
1
) with independent
{\bm{\beta}}_{i}
and
{\bm{\Theta}}_{i}
. Let
B\subset L^{1}(\mathbbm{R}^{d+1})
be convex with
f_{\bm{\beta}}\in B
and
T:B\rightarrow H^{s}(D)
where
H^{s}
is an
L^{2}
based Sobolev space with
s>\frac{d+1}{2}
, and
D\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d+1}
is some bounded Lipschitz domain.

Assumption 2
.

\mathcal{R}:B\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
is convex, and lower semi-continuous.

Assumption
1
is a technical assumption that we need to derive convergence rates. It is a mild smoothness assumption on the
Tf_{\bm{\beta}}
and needs
Tf_{\bm{\beta}}
to have bounded support. This implies that also
f_{\bm{\beta}}
has bounded support. Assumption
2
holds for many regularization terms, e.g. for the examples listed in Section
2
. For more examples see
Hohage and Werner, 2016
.

Theorem 1
.

If the assumptions
1
and
2
hold, then (
6
) has a unique minimizer
\hat{f_{\beta}}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha>0
.

As a smoothness assumption for the convergence rate results we use a variational source condition which has been used extensively in the inverse problems literature, e.g.
Flemming and Hofmann, 2010
,
Werner and Hohage, 2012
,
Dunker and Hohage, 2014
,
Hohage and Werner, 2016
. It uses Bregman distance between some density
f
and
f_{\bm{\beta}}
with respect to
\mathcal{R}
and some
f^{*}_{\bm{\beta}}\in\partial\mathcal{R}(f_{\bm{\beta}})
where
\partial\mathcal{R}(f_{\bm{\beta}})
is the subdifferential of the convex functional
\mathcal{R}
at
f_{\bm{\beta}}
. The Bregman distance with respect to
\mathcal{R}

and
f^{*}_{\bm{\beta}}
is defined as

D_{\mathcal{R}}^{f^{*}_{\bm{\beta}}}(f,\,f_{\bm{\beta}}):=\mathcal{R}(f)-\mathcal{R}(f_{\bm{\beta}})-\langle f^{*}_{\bm{\beta}},f-f_{\bm{\beta}}\rangle.

For quadratic penalty in Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{R}=\|\cdot\|^{2}
we have

D_{\mathcal{R}}^{f^{*}_{\bm{\beta}}}(f,\,f_{\bm{\beta}})=\|f-f_{\bm{\beta}}\|^{2}
.
In general,
D_{\mathcal{R}}^{f^{*}_{\bm{\beta}}}
is nonnegative with

D_{\mathcal{R}}^{f^{*}_{\bm{\beta}}}(f_{\bm{\beta}},f_{\bm{\beta}})=0
, but it is not necessarily
symmetric or satisfies a triangle inequality. We will also use the Kullback-Leibler divergence
\mathbbm{KL}(f,g)=\int f\ln\left(f/g\right)\,dx
.

Assumption 3

(Variational Source Condition)
.

There exists
f_{\beta}^{*}\in\partial{R(f_{\beta})}
,
c>0
and a concave, monotonically increasing function
\lambda:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
with
\lambda(0)=0
such that

cD_{\mathcal{R}}^{f_{\beta}^{*}}(f,f_{\bm{\beta}})\leq\mathcal{R}(f)-\mathcal{R}(f_{\bm{\beta}})+\lambda(\mathbbm{KL}(f_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}};Tf))

(8)

for all densities
f
.

The function
\lambda
is called index function.
The rate with which
\lambda
increases compares the smoothing properties of
T
with the smoothness of
f_{\bm{\beta}}
. The slower
\lambda
increases the faster the convergence of the method as we will see in Theorem
3
.

Assumption
3
is an abstract assumption which does not refer to a classical smoothness class like Sobolev or Hölder spaces. The popularity of this assumption in the literature comes from the fact that it is not only sufficient but also necessary for a certain convergence rate,
Flemming, 2010
;
Flemming, 2012b
;
Flemming, 2012a
. In other words, the class of functions which fulfil Assumption
3
with a specific
\lambda
is the largest class of functions for which the convergence rate in Theorem
3
is achieved. For several operators and regularisation functionals variatioinal source conditions are implied by classical smoothness assumptions, see
Hohage and Weidling, 2017
;
Hohage and Miller, 2019
;
Weidling et al., 2020
. Assumption
3
hold for
f_{\bm{\beta}}
in an
L^{2}
based Sobolev
H^{r}
space in the following special case.

Theorem 2
.

Let
f_{\bm{\beta}}\in H^{r}(\mathbbm{R}^{n})
with
0<r\leq d-1
. Assume that
f_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}}
is bounded and that
f_{\bm{\Theta}}
is bounded and bounded away from zero. On the function class
\mathcal{T}=\{f\in L^{1}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})|Tf\in L^{2},\|Tf\|_{\infty}\leq c_{f}\}
with some
c_{f}>0
Assumption
2
holds true for
\mathcal{R}=\|\cdot\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
with
\lambda(x)=x^{\frac{2r}{2r+d-1}}
. More generally, for
\mathcal{R}=\|\cdot\|^{2}_{H^{a}}
with
0<r-a\leq d-1
Assumption
2
holds true on
\mathcal{T}
with
\lambda(x)=x^{\frac{2(r-a)}{2(r-a)+d-1}}
.

3.2
Convergence Rate Results

The following theorem establishes the convergences rates with a priori parameter choice.

Theorem 3
.

If assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and if
\alpha
is chosen as

\alpha^{-1}\in-\partial(-\lambda)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(9)

we obtain the following convergence rate

\mathbbm{E}\big[D_{R}^{f_{\bm{\beta}}^{*}}(\hat{f_{\bm{\beta}}}_{\alpha},f_{\bm{\beta}})\big]=\mathcal{O}\left(\lambda\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right),\;\;n\mapsto\infty

The last theorem gives convergence rates in terms of the Bregman distance with respect to
\mathcal{R}
. This is an unusual convergence measure but it comes with easy interpretations in many cases. If for example
\mathcal{R}
is maximum entropy regularization, the Bregman distance dominates the
L^{1}
norm
D_{R}^{f_{\bm{\beta}}^{*}}(f,f_{\bm{\beta}})>\|f-f_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{L^{1}}
. Hence, Theorem
3
implies convergence rates for the
L^{1}
norm. If
\mathcal{R}
is a quadratic penalty like
\mathcal{R}=\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
or
\mathcal{R}=\|\cdot\|_{H^{a}}^{2}
, the Bregman distance coincides with the squared norm
D_{R}^{f_{\bm{\beta}}^{*}}(f,f_{\bm{\beta}})>\|f-f_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{H^{a}}^{2}
. This leads to the following lemma.

Theorem 4
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
2
hold and use the a priori parameter choice
\alpha=\frac{2(r-a)+d-1}{2(r-a)}n^{\frac{1-d}{4(r-a)+2(d-1)}}
. Then

\mathbbm{E}\big[\|\hat{f_{\bm{\beta}}}_{\alpha}-f_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{H^{a}}^{2}\big]=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\frac{r-a}{2(r-a)+d-1}}\right).

Note that the theorem proves convergence of the mean integrated squared error for
a=0
. Hence,
MISE(\hat{f_{\beta}}_{\alpha})=\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-\frac{r}{2r+d-1}}\big)
with parameter choice
\alpha=cn^{\frac{1-d}{4(r-a)+2(d-1)}}
with some constant
c>0
.

The choice of
\alpha
in Theorems
3
and
4
requires a priori knowledge about the smoothness of the unknown
f_{\bm{\beta}}
. This is usually not available in practice. A popular and computationally inexpensve data-driven parameter choice for
\alpha
is Lepskii’s principle as in
Lepskiĭ, 1992
,
Mathé, 2006
, and
Hohage and Werner, 2016
. For sake of simplicity we state the result only for quadratic penalty in Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{R}=\|\cdot\|
.

The principle works as follows: we compute
\hat{f_{\bm{\beta}}}_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,\hat{f_{\bm{\beta}}}_{\alpha_{m}}
for
\alpha_{1}=c_{L}\frac{\ln(n)}{\sqrt{n}}
and
\alpha_{i+1}=r\alpha_{i}
with some constants
c_{L}>0,r>1
.

\bar{j}:=\min\{j\leq m,\|\hat{f_{\bm{\beta}}}_{\alpha_{i}}-\hat{f_{\bm{\beta}}}_{\alpha_{j}}\|\leq 8r^{\frac{1-i}{2}}),\text{ for all }i<j\}.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{R}=\|\cdot\|_{H^{a}}^{2}
be a Sobolev regularization functional. If Assumption
3
holds with
c\geq\frac{1}{2}
, then with the Lepskii parameter choice
\alpha_{\bar{j}}

\mathbbm{E}[\|\hat{f_{\bm{\beta}}}_{\alpha_{\bar{j}}}-f_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{H^{a}}^{2}]=\mathcal{O}\left(\lambda\left(\frac{\ln(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right).

(10)

If in addition the assumptions of Theorem
2
hold, then

\mathbbm{E}\big[\|\hat{f_{\bm{\beta}}}_{\alpha}-f_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{H^{a}}^{2}\big]=\mathcal{O}\left(\ln(n)^{\frac{2(r-a)}{2(r-a)+d-1}}~n^{-\frac{r-a}{2(r-a)+d-1}}\right).

As usual we lose a log-factor for adaptive estimation. Other
a posteriori
methods might perform better than Lepskii’s principle e.g. cross-validation. However, these methods are computationally expensive and not feasible in our setup. Hence, we favor Lepskii’s principle for its simplicity and computational efficiency.

4
Implementation

4.1
Discretization

We discretized
f_{\beta}
over a fixed regular grid with
m
grid points. Applying this discretization allowed for the evaluation of the integral expression
\int_{\mathbbm{R}}\mathbbm{1}\big\{{\bm{\beta}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}=Y_{i}\big\}f_{\bm{\beta}}(\mathbf{b})d\mathbf{b}
using a finite volume approach. This integral expression is simply the integral of the joint density
f_{\beta}
over the
d
-dimensional hyperplanes parametrized by the sample points
Y_{i}=\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}
. In this implementation, the model was specified to have one regressor in which case the hyperplanes are simply lines.

The amount of information that can be known about the shape of
f_{\bm{\beta}}
relies on the amount of coverage that the lines parametrized by the sample observations have over the estimation grid. Figure 1 illustrates this concept for the case of the random coefficients model with one regressor and random slope, with random design.

y_{i}={\beta_{0}}_{i}+{\beta_{1}}_{i}{x_{1}}_{i}

(11)

It is clear from equation (
11
) that the coverage of the lines over the estimation grid depends highly on the design density as it specifies the slopes of the different lines parametrized by the sample. In section
5.2
we will discuss how our method is more robust with respect to poor angle coverage which can be brought about by either undesired tail behavior in the distribution of the regressor, or the said regressor having too narrow of a support.

Example line coverage of the estimation grid. Lines are parametrized as
Y_{i}=\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}
, or by
\beta_{0}=y_{i}-\beta_{1}x_{1_{i}}
.

4.2
Optimization of the Likelihood Functional

The discretized likelihood functional is a
m
-dimensional function,
{\bar{\ell}}^{*}:\mathbbm{R^{m}}\mapsto\mathbbm{R}
. We denote the discretized form of the regularization functionals as,
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}({f^{*}}):\mathbbm{R^{m}}\mapsto\mathbbm{R}
, where
f^{*}=\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}

The nonlinear program for the minimization of the conditional average log-likelihood functional was setup as follows:

\hat{f_{\bm{\beta}}}=\argmin_{f^{*}}-{\bar{\ell}^{*}}(f^{*}|X,Y)+\alpha\mathcal{R}^{*}(f^{*})

(12)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\text{subject to}:\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{j}\Delta b=1,\\
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{m}\geq 0.\\
\\
\end{array}

The convexity of the problem allows us to use a trust-region constrained algorithm based on an interior point algorithm for constrained large-scale nonlinear programs developed in
Byrd et al., 1999
. This particular algorithm excels in solving high-dimensional minimization problems, which is the case for the implementation as
f_{\beta}
can scale up to the order of thousands in terms of dimensionality. We used the Python implementation of the algorithm in
Byrd et al., 1999
.

5
Monte Carlo Simulations

We checked the small sample performance of the method in Monte Carlo simulations with two different test examples, one with a normally distributed regressor, and one with a regressor which has bounded support. We compared the results to a kernel-based estimator akin to the one proposed in
Hoderlein et al., 2010
. We found that the kernel based estimator in our implementation required us to
cut-off negative point-estimates and to normalize the final reconstruction,
\hat{f_{\beta}}
, otherwise it produced very poor results.

To test the performance of both methods we conducted a simulation study by generating data for the Random Coefficients model with random slope, and one regressor as specified by equation
11
. We then evaluated its ability to reconstruct the underlying distribution of the random coefficients by comparing it to the known true distribution through measuring the empirical mean integrated squared error (MISE) across one-hundred Monte Carlo simulation runs which we repeat for the different sample sizes. We also considered in our study the variance of the integrated squared error (ISE), as we believe stability of the reconstructions is as important to consider as their accuracy.

7
Conclusion

We provide an alternative method of estimating the joint distribution of
{\bm{\beta}}
through Regularized Maximum Likelihood Estimations (RMLE). To combat the instability of the estimate caused by the implicit inversion of the Radon Transform, we apply Tikhonov-type regularization methods.

Compared to its well-known counter part, the kernel method, the RMLE method is more robust with respect to tail behavior of the design density. Although more robust, the method is not completely unaffected by the behavior of the design density, as the rate of decay of the singular values of the operator, and consequently the degree of ill-posedness of the problem,
T
is determined by the distribution of the regressors.

Monte Carlo simulations illustrate that both methods work well in a scenario where the regressor has unbounded support, but in the case where the regressor has bounded support the RMLE method produces more accurate reconstructions.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Note that the operator
T
is equivalent to the
d
-dim Radon transformation if
f_{\bm{\Theta}}
is bounded away from
0
on a hemisphere of
\mathbbm{S}^{d-1}
. Hence,
T^{*}T
has eigen functions
\varphi(x)=\exp(ix\cdot k)
with
k\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
. The ill-posedness follows from by Theorem 3.1 in
Hertle, 1983
. For all
f\in H^{q}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
, we have
T^{*}Tf\in H^{q+d-1}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
. Using the spectral calculus yields
(T^{*}T)^{\mu}f\in H^{q+\mu(d-1)}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
. Hence, for
a<r
,
f_{\beta}
fulfills the following spectral source condition: There exists
\rho\in H^{a}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
such that
(T^{*}T)^{\mu}\rho=f_{\bm{\beta}}
with
\mu=\frac{r-a}{d-1}
.

As shown in
Hofmann and Yamamoto, 2010
;
Flemming, 2012b
;
Flemming, 2012a
;
Hohage and Werner, 2016
, this spectral source condition implies the variational source condition

cD_{\mathcal{R}}^{f_{\beta}^{*}}(f,f_{\bm{\beta}})\leq\mathcal{R}(f)-\mathcal{R}(f_{\bm{\beta}})+\lambda(\|f_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}}-Tf\|^{2}_{L^{2}})

with some
c>0
,
\mathcal{R}=\|\cdot\|_{H^{a}}^{2}
, and
\lambda(x)=x^{\frac{2\mu}{2\mu+1}}=x^{\frac{2(r-a)}{2(r-a)+d-1}}
if
\mu\leq 1
.

We apply the inequality
\|\varphi-\psi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{2}{3}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+\frac{4}{3}\|\psi\|_{\infty}\right)\mathbbm{KL}(\varphi;\psi)
which holds for all nonnegative functions
\varphi,\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
with
\varphi-\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
, see
Borwein and Lewis, 1991
;
Dunker and Hohage, 2014
. Since
\lambda
is a power function, we get

\displaystyle\lambda(\|f_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}}-Tf\|^{2}_{L^{2}})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\left(\left(\frac{2}{3}\|f_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}}\|_{\infty}+\frac{4}{3}\|Tf\|_{\infty}\right)\mathbbm{KL}(f_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}};Tf)\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(\frac{2}{3}\|f_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}}\|_{\infty}+\frac{4}{3}\|Tf\|_{\infty}\right)\lambda\left(\mathbbm{KL}(f_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}};Tf)\right)

Hence, there is a constant
c^{\prime}>0
such that

c^{\prime}D_{\mathcal{R}}^{f_{\beta}^{*}}(f,f_{\bm{\beta}})\leq\mathcal{R}(f)-\mathcal{R}(f_{\bm{\beta}})+\lambda\left(\mathbbm{KL}(f_{S|{\bm{\Theta}}};Tf)\right)

This proves the theorem.

∎
