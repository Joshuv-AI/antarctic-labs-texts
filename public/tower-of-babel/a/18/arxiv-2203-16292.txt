Abstract
If prices of assets traded in a financial market are determined by non-linear pricing rules, different versions of the Call-Put Parity have been considered. We show that, under monotonicity, parities between call and put options and discount certificates characterize ambiguity-sensitive (Choquet and/or Šipoš) pricing rules, i.e., pricing rules that can be represented via discounted expectations with respect to non-additive probability measures. We analyze how non-additivity relates to arbitrage opportunities and we give necessary and sufficient conditions for Choquet and Šipoš pricing rules to be arbitrage free. Finally, we identify violations of the Call-Put Parity with the presence of bid-ask spreads. Keywords: Asset Pricing, Choquet and/or Šipoš pricing, No Arbitrage, Put-Call Parity, Call-Put Parity, Discount Certificate–Call Parity, Market Frictions. JEL Classification Number: G12, D81, C71.

Introduction

The aim of asset pricing models is to associate to marketed securities prices that are consistent with the absence of arbitrage opportunities. Typically, the models assume a reference probability over a state space and frictionless pricing rules, as it is done, for instance, in the seminal paper of Harrison and Kreps
[
24
]
. In this case, assets are valued by a linear pricing rule, or, equivalently, as the discounted expectation with respect to the so-called risk-neutral probability. However, both assumptions are questionable. Since at least Ellsberg
[
19
]
, the decision theory literature suggests a switch to (non-probabilistic) uncertainty. A general framework without an objective probability was already studied in Kreps
[
27
]
and was studied more recently by Riedel
[
31
]
, Cassese
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
and Burzoni, Riedel and Soner
[
5
]
. Moreover, important evidence of the presence of frictions in financial markets (transaction costs, taxes, bid-ask spreads) has led to the study of non-linear pricing rules that can account for frictions, as in Garman and Ohlson
[
21
]
, Prisman
[
30
]
, Ross
[
32
]
, Bensaid, Lesne, Pages and Scheinkman
[
3
]
and Jouini and Kallal
[
25
]
.

This article drops both assumptions of the existence of an objective probability and of the linearity of pricing rules. More precisely, following the ideas first developed in Chateauneuf, Kast and Lapied
[
16
]
, we consider pricing rules that are Choquet integrals with respect to a
non-additive
risk-neutral probability, i.e., Choquet pricing rules.
In a recent paper, Cerreia-Vioglio, Maccheroni and Marinacci
[
10
]
studied financial markets (without a reference probability and with frictions) in which a version of the parity between call and put options is maintained. They show that, under some basic assumptions, pricing rules satisfying that parity are precisely Choquet pricing rules. This result is surprising as it connects these two apparently unrelated notions.

However, when prices are non-linear, as for Choquet pricing rules, there are several ways to define the price parity between call and put options. Moreover, without further assumptions on the non-additive risk-neutral probability, arbitrage opportunities may arise. The parity between put and call options is a fundamental non-arbitrage relation in finance, originally derived in Stoll
[
38
]
. Call options (denoted here
c^{+}
) and put options (
c^{-}
) are related by the simple mathematical fact that a real-valued function can be written as the difference between its positive and negative parts:
c=c^{+}-c^{-}
. Loosely speaking, this equality means that an agent who wants to obtain
c
can buy
c^{+}
and sell
c^{-}
. Obviously, we could write as well
c^{-}=c^{+}-c
, and one can get
c^{-}
buying
c^{+}
and selling
c
. These two different replication strategies were already described in Stoll
[
38
]
, see also Klemkosky and Resnick
[
26
]
.

When prices are obtained through non-linear pricing rules, the two non-arbitrage strategies yield two different price parities. The former parity is considered by Cerreia-Vioglio
et al.

[
10
]
and it is called Put-Call Parity (PCP). The latter was analyzed by Chateauneuf
et al.

[
16
]
, and led to the Call-Put Parity (CPP).
This paper contributes to the literature of (non-linear) pricing by arbitrage in several ways. The first part of our paper, Section
3
, focuses on put-call parities and how they characterize non-linear pricing rules. The second part of the paper, Section
4
, imposes a non-arbitrage condition and analyzes its implications on non-linear pricing rules.

Our first contribution in Section
3
provides the formal relationship between the two parities. We show that CPP of Chateauneuf
et al.

[
16
]
is a stronger assumption: it corresponds to PCP of Cerreia-Vioglio
et al.

[
10
]
plus the absence of bid-ask spreads. Second, we improve the main result of Cerreia-Vioglio
et al.

[
10
]
. They proved that a pricing rule satisfies PCP, monotonicity and translation invariance if and only if it is a Choquet pricing rule. Theorem
1
shows that PCP and monotonicity are enough for the characterization. Thus we demonstrate that the connection between PCP and the Choquet pricing rule runs even deeper than suggested by the result of Cerreia-Vioglio
et al.

[
10
]
. Third, we study what happens when CPP replaces PCP. Theorem
2
characterizes CPP by a stronger version of Choquet pricing rules, called Choquet-Šipoš pricing rules. These are pricing rules that are at the same time Choquet and Šipoš integrals. The Šipoš integral is an integral with respect to a non-additive measure that, in general, can differ from the Choquet integral, see Šipoš
[
36
]
. However when CPP holds, the two coincide.

The last result of Section
3
answers the following natural question. Is there a financial relation that characterizes Šipoš pricing rules that are not at the same time Choquet pricing rules? Discount certificates are well known financial options that pays the minimum between the value of an underlying asset and a fixed cap
k
. We show in Theorem
3
that the two key ingredients to obtain Šipoš pricing rules are
(i)
the parity between discount certificates and call options, and
(ii)
the absence of bid-ask spreads. To the best of our knowledge, our is the first paper to provide a natural justification for the use of Šipoš pricing rules for asset pricing.

In Section
4
, we assume that pricing rules are either Choquet or Šipoš pricing rules, and we analyze what happens when one imposes the absence of arbitrage opportunities. Intuitively, an arbitrage opportunity is an asset that yields positive payoff and that can be bought at zero or negative cost.
The parity between put and call options is one of the simplest and best understood no-arbitrage relations. However, in general, neither Choquet nor Šipoš pricing rules guarantee that markets are arbitrage-free. We study which additional conditions one has to impose in order to eliminate arbitrage opportunities.

Let
f
be a Choquet pricing rule used by a dealer to set asset prices in the market. The main result of this section, Theorem
5
shows that there are no arbitrage opportunities if and only if there exists an additive risk-neutral measure
\mu
which is smaller than the risk-neutral capacity
v
associated to
f
. The existence of an additive measure
\mu
below
v
generalizes the fundamental theorem of asset pricing obtained in the absence of frictions. On the other hand, Theorem
5
shows that a Šipoš pricing rule is arbitrage free if and only if it is linear. This implies that whenever markets do not allow arbitrage opportunities but bid-ask spread are observed, the strong parity CPP of Chateauneuf
et al.

[
16
]
must be violated.

This violation of CPP, in which the marketed price of put options is cheaper than the theoretical one (i.e., the price of building a portfolio that replicates a put), was empirically observed when puts were introduced in financial markets, see Klemkosky and Resnick
[
26
]
and Cremers and Weinbaum
[
18
]
. However, it is important to note that this violation of CPP is consistent with the absence of arbitrage opportunities (and with PCP of Cerreia-Vioglio
et al.

[
10
]
).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the framework and our notation. Section
3
studies call-put parities and the discount certificate-call parity. This section characterizes Choquet, Choquet-Šipoš and Šipoš pricing rules. Section
4
investigates arbitrage-free Choquet and Šipoš pricing rules. Section
5
concludes. All proofs are gathered in the Appendix.

The Model

This paper considers the simple framework of a stochastic two-date model:
t=0
(today) is known,
t=1
(tomorrow) is uncertain. Uncertainty is represented by a set
\Omega
(finite or infinite) of
states of nature
, endowed with a
\sigma
-algebra
{\cal A}
. One, and only one, state of nature will be realized tomorrow and will be known. Note that we do not assume that there is a reference probability defined on
(\Omega,{\cal A})
.

A
payoff
, or
contingent claim
, is a bounded, real-valued,
{\cal A}
-measurable random variable
x:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(or, if
\Omega
is finite, a vector
x:=(x(\omega))_{\omega\in\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
) with
x(\omega)
representing the payoff (money) at
t=1
if state
\omega
prevails. We denote
B(\Omega,{\cal A})
the set of all contingent claims. We adopt the convention that if
x(\omega)<0
then
|x(\omega)|
is paid by the agent and if
x(\omega)>0
then
|x(\omega)|
is received.
For every
A\in{\cal A}
we denote by
\boldsymbol{1}_{A}
the payoff in
B(\Omega,{\cal A})
defined by
\boldsymbol{1}_{A}(\omega)=1
if
\omega\in A
and
\boldsymbol{1}_{A}(\omega)=0
otherwise. We set
\boldsymbol{1}_{\emptyset}=0
. Note that
B(\Omega,{\cal A})
comes equipped with the usual partial order,
\geq
, where
x\geq y
if and only if
x(\omega)\geq y(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.
Let
x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
, we denote
x^{+}:=x\vee 0
its
positive part
and
x^{-}:=(-x)\vee 0
its
negative part
. Finally we say that two contingent claims
x,y\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
are
comonotonic
if
(x(\omega)-x(\omega^{\prime}))(y(\omega)-y(\omega^{\prime}))\geq 0
for all
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega
.

A
pricing rule

is a non-zero function
f:B(\Omega,{\cal A})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that associates to every payoff
x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
the price/cost
f(x)
to be paid today for the
delivery of the random payoff
x
at
t=1
. Given
x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
,
|f(x)|
is paid if
f(x)>0
and received if
f(x)<0
hence
f(x)
is the buying (ask) price, the price one pays to buy
x
, and the (bid) selling price is then
-f(-x)
which is the amount received if one sells
x
.
This paper will take the pricing rule
f:B(\Omega,{\cal A})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as a primitive concept. A pricing rule
f
is

•

Monotonic:
:
f(x)\geq f(x^{\prime})
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
such that
x\geq x^{\prime}
.

•

Positively Homogeneous:

f(tx)=tf(x)
for all
x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
and all
t\geq 0
.

•

Translation Invariant:
:
f(x+t\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega})=f(x)+f(t\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega})
for all
x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

•

Subadditive:

f(x+x^{\prime})\leq f(x)+f(x^{\prime})
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
.

•

Linear:

f(\alpha x+\beta x^{\prime})=\alpha f(x)+\beta f(x^{\prime})
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

Comonotonicly Additive:

f(x+x^{\prime})=f(x)+f(x^{\prime})
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
comonotonic.

•

No Bid-Ask Spread:

f(x)=-f(-x)
for all
x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
.

•

Non-negative Bid-Ask Spread:

f(x)\geq-f(-x)
for all
x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
.

•

Non-negative:

x\geq 0
implies
f(x)\geq 0
.

If
f(x)>-f(-x)
then the price for buying
x
(bid price) in the market is higher than the price for selling
x
(ask price). In this case there is a
bid-ask spread
. One can also have a bid-ask spread when

f(x)<-f(-x)
but this situation will not prevail since it generates an arbitrage opportunity (see Section
4
).

A
capacity

v
on the measurable space
(\Omega,{\cal A})
is a set function
v:{\cal A}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
such that
v(\emptyset)=0
and which is monotone, that is, for all
A,B\in{\cal A},\,A\subseteq B
implies
v(A)\leq v(B)
. The capacity is said to be normalized, also called
non-additive probability
, if
v(\Omega)=1
. A capacity
v:{\cal A}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is
concave,
also called submodular, if, for all
A,B\in{\cal A}
,
v(A\cup B)+v(A\cap B)\leq v(A)+v(B)
.
A
probability

\mu:{\cal A}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a normalized capacity which is (finitely) additive, that is,
A\cap B=\emptyset
implies
\mu(A\cup B)=\mu(A)+\mu(B)
. The conjugate of the capacity
v:{\cal A}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is the capacity
v^{*}:{\cal A}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
defined by

v^{*}(A):=v(\Omega)-v(A^{c})
for all
A\in{\cal A}
and we say that the capacity
v
is
auto-conjugate
if
v=v^{*}
. Note that a capacity is auto-conjugate if and only if
v(A)+v(A^{c})=v(\Omega)
for all
A\in{\cal A}
.

Consider a capacity
v
on
(\Omega,{\cal A})
. The function
f
is said to be a
Choquet pricing rule
with respect to
v
if

f(x)=\int_{\Omega}^{C}{x\,dv}:=\int_{-\infty}^{0}{(v(\{x\geq t\})-v(\Omega))\,dt}+\int_{0}^{+\infty}{v(\{x\geq t\})\,dt}\text{ for all }x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A}),

where
\{x\geq t\}=\{\omega\in\Omega|x(\omega)\geq t\}
. The notation
\int^{C}
indicates that the integral is a Choquet integral (we drop subscript
\Omega
from the integral when no confusion arises). We will use the notation
\int
for the standard integral with respect to an additive capacity.
We note that the capacity
v
associated with the Choquet pricing rule
f
is uniquely defined, it satisfies
v(A):=f(\boldsymbol{1}_{A})
for all
A\in{\cal A}
, and
v
is called the risk-neutral capacity (associated with the Choquet pricing rule
f
).

We say that
f
is a
Šipoš pricing rule
with respect to
v
if

f(x)=\int_{\Omega}^{S}{x\,dv}:=\int^{C}{x^{+}\,dv}-\int^{C}{x^{-}\,dv}\;\text{ for all }x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A}).

Note that Choquet and Šipoš integrals coincide if
x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
is non-negative. One fundamental difference between the two integrals is that the former satisfies translation invariance, while the latter has no bid-ask spread.

Finally, we say that
f
is a
Choquet-Šipoš pricing rule
when the Choquet and Šipoš integrals coincide with
f
for the same capacity
v
, that is

f(x)=\int_{\Omega}^{S}{x\,dv}=\int_{\Omega}^{C}{x\,dv}\;\text{ for all }x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A}).

We recall (Proposition
2
) that
f
is a
Choquet-Šipoš pricing rule
if and only if
f
is a
Choquet pricing rule
with respect to an auto-conjugate capacity
v
.

We end this section with a remark. Suppose that
f
is a Choquet pricing rule with respect to
v
. We can normalize the capacity
v
, by defining the non-additive probability
\bar{v}:{\cal A}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
\bar{v}(A):={v(A)\over v(\Omega)}
for all
A\in{\cal A}
, and obtain a riskless interest rate
r>-1
uniquely defined by
f(\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega})={1\over 1+r}
.
1
1

1

Note that
v(\Omega)=f(\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega})>0
otherwise one would obtain the contradiction
f(x)=0
for all
x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
.
Then a Choquet pricing rule can be written as

f(x)={1\over 1+r}\int^{C}x\,d\bar{v},\,\forall x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A}).

This (non-additive) probabilistic formulation, taken from Cerreia-Vioglio
et al.

[
10
]
, allows to interpret the cost
f(x)
of every payoff
x
as the present value of its non-additive expectation, where the present value is calculated with the riskless interest rate. The same can be done for Šipoš and Choquet-Šipoš pricing rules.

Characterization of pricing rules through financial parities

3.1
Call-Put Parity(ies)

This section analyzes the implications on pricing rules of the two different parities between prices of call and put options introduced by Chateauneuf
et al.

[
16
]
and Cerreia-Vioglio
et al.

[
10
]
.

A call option with strike
k
and expiration date
T
is a financial contract that gives the option buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy a stock, bond, or commodity at price
k
at time
T
. The stock, bond, or commodity is called the underlying asset. A call buyer profits when the underlying asset increases in price.
A put option gives the right, but not the obligation, to sell the underlying asset at price
k
at time
T
.

In our two-period financial economy, given an underlying asset
x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
, a call option with strike
k\geq 0
is defined by
c_{x,k}=(x-k\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega})^{+}
and the related put option is defined by
p_{x,k}=(k\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega}-x)^{+}
. The following equality holds:

x=c_{x,k}-p_{x,k}+k\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega},

(3.1)

and says that one can replicate the underlying
x
by buying a call, selling a put with strikes
k
, and buying
k
units of the bond. Given a pricing rule
f
, Cerreia-Vioglio
et al.

[
10
]
used equality in (
3.1
) in order to derive the Put-Call Parity (PCP)

f(x)=f(c_{x,k})+f(-p_{x,k})+f(k\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega}).

(PCP)

Obviously, the mathematical equality in (
3.1
) can be re-written as

p_{x,k}=c_{x,k}-x+k\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega}.

(3.2)

Hence a put with strike
k
can be replicated buying a call with the same strike, selling the underlying asset, and buying
k
units of the bond. Chateauneuf
et al.

[
16
]
used equation (
3.2
) to derive a different parity that they named Call-Put Parity (CPP)

f(p_{x,k})=f(c_{x,k})+f(-x)+f(k\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega}).

(CPP)

If
f
is linear,
PCP
and
CPP
are equivalent. The following result provides the formal relation between the two parities. It shows that
CPP
of Chateauneuf
et al.

[
16
]
is stronger than
PCP
of Cerreia-Vioglio
et al.

[
10
]
as
CPP
is equivalent to
PCP
and the absence of bid-ask spreads.

.

Let
f:B(\Omega,{\cal A})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a pricing rule. Then
(i)\Leftrightarrow(ii)
.

(i)

f
satisfies

;

(ii)

f
satisfies
PCP
and no bid-ask spreads.

3.2
Discount Certificate-Call Parity

In order to pin down Šipoš pricing rules we have to introduce discount certificates. A
discount certificate
on an asset
x
with cap
k\geq 0
, denoted
d_{x,k}
, is a contingent claim that in state
\omega
pays
x
if
x(\omega)\leq k
and
k
if
x(\omega)>k
, or equivalently,

d_{x,k}=x\wedge k\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega}.

Noting that
d_{x,k}=(x-k\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega})\wedge 0+k\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega}
and recalling that
c_{x,k}:=[x-k\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega}]^{+}=(x-k\boldsymbol{1}_{\Omega})\vee 0
one can conclude

x=c_{x,k}+d_{x,k}.

(3.3)

Therefore asset
x
can be replicated buying a call and a discount certificate. Given a pricing rule
f
, Cerreia-Vioglio
et al.

[
10
]
used equation (
3.3
) to derive the Discount Certificate-Call Parity (DCP)

f(x)=f(c_{x,k})+f(d_{x,k}).

(DCP)

In their paper they used
DCP
, monotonicity and translation invariance in order to derive another characterization of Choquet pricing rules. In Theorem
3
we show that
DCP
and no bid-ask spreads pin down Šipoš pricing rules.

Theorem 3
.

Let
f:B(\Omega,{\cal A})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a monotone pricing rule. Then

4
Absence of Arbitrage Opportunities

In the characterizations given in Section

, no property is stated on the capacity

associated to the pricing rule

Intuitively, a payoff

(with a gain today). Without the subadditivity assumption, there is a need to eliminate other arbitrage opportunities. Consider for the moment

\&

, that is payoffs

for which

) and a gain today. The following result characterizes Choquet pricing rules for which there is no buy
\&
sell arbitrage opportunity. Finally note that the absence of buy
\&
sell arbitrage opportunities is equivalent to the property Non-negative Bid-Ask Spreads, i.e.,
f(x)+f(-x)\geq 0
for all
x\in B(\Omega,{\cal A})
.

Definition 1

5
Conclusion

The first part of our paper studies different formulations of the famous put-call parity in a framework where pricing rules are non-linear. Cerreia-Vioglio
et al.

[
10
]
study a parity called Put-Call Parity (PCP). They prove that PCP, together with translation invariance and monotonicity, characterizes Choquet pricing rules. Chateauneuf
et al.

[
16
]
study a different Call-Put Parity (CPP). Our first result studies the relation between the two parities and shows that CPP is equivalent to PCP and no bid-ask spreads. Our second result improves the characterization of Cerreia-Vioglio
et al.

[
10
]
as it shows that translation invariance is redundant. Third, replacing PCP by CPP, we obtain Choquet-Šipoš pricing rules, which are pricing rules that are at the same time Choquet and Šipoš pricing rules. Fourth, we characterize Šipoš pricing rules using the Discount Certificate-Call parity and no bid-ask spreads.

The second part of our paper studies the implication of an arbitrage free (AF) condition on pricing rules. We show that, in general, Choquet and Šipoš pricing rules are not AF. If
f
is a Choquet pricing rule with respect to a capacity
v
, then the market is AF if and only if the anticore of
v
is non-empty. If
f
is a Šipoš pricing rule then
f
is AF if and only if it is linear.
Finally, we characterize bid-ask spreads as violations of CPP. This shows that, for pricing rules
à la
Choquet, one can observe at the same time PCP, absence of arbitrage opportunities and a violation of CPP (i.e., non-negative bid-ask spreads).
