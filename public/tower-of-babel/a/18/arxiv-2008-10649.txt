Extension Quiver for Lie Superalgebra 𝖖 ⁡ ( 𝟑 ) \boldsymbol{\mathfrak{q}(3)} This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Representation Theory and Integrable Systems in honor of Vitaly Tarasov on the 60th birthday and Alexander Varchenko on the 70th birthday. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Tarasov-Varchenko.html
Nikolay GRANTCHAROV † and Vera SERGANOVA ‡

Abstract
We describe all blocks of the category of finite-dimensional 𝔮 ⁡ ( 3 ) \mathfrak{q}(3) -supermodules by providing their extension quivers. We also obtain two general results about the representation of 𝔮 ⁡ ( n ) \mathfrak{q}(n) : we show that the Ext quiver of the standard block of 𝔮 ⁡ ( n ) \mathfrak{q}(n) is obtained from the principal block of 𝔮 ⁡ ( n − 1 ) \mathfrak{q}(n-1) by identifying certain vertices of the quiver and prove a “virtual” BGG-reciprocity for 𝔮 ⁡ ( n ) \mathfrak{q}(n) . The latter result is used to compute the radical filtrations of 𝔮 ⁡ ( 3 ) \mathfrak{q}(3) projective covers.

Introduction

The “queer” Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{q}(n)
is an interesting super analogue of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
. Other related queer-type Lie superalgebras include the subsuperalgebra
\mathfrak{sq}(n)
obtained by taking odd trace 0, and for
n\geq 3
, the simple Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{psq}(n)
obtained by taking the quotient of the commutator
[\mathfrak{q}(n),\mathfrak{q}(n)]
by the center. These queer superalgebras have a rich representation theory, partly due to the Cartan subsuperalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
not being abelian and hence having nontrivial representations, called Clifford modules.

Finite-dimensional representation theory of
\mathfrak{q}(n)
was initiated in
[
16
]
and developed in
[
20
]
. Algorithms for computing characters of irreducible finite-dimensional representations were obtained in
[
21
,
22
]
using methods of supergeometry and in
[
3
,
4
]
using a categorification approach. Finite-dimensional representations of half-integer weights were studied in detail in
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. In
[
18
]
, the blocks in the category of finite-dimensional

\mathfrak{q}(2)
-modules semisimple over the even part were classified and described using quivers and relations. A general classification of blocks was obtained in
[
24
]
using translation functors and supergeometry.

In this paper, we describe the blocks in the category of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{q}(3)
and
\mathfrak{sq}(3)
modules semisimple over the even part in terms of quiver and relations. We found that to describe blocks of
\mathfrak{q}(n)
in general, it remains to consider the principal block. For
n=3
, this is the first example of a wild block in
\mathfrak{q}
.
Our main tools are relative Lie superalgebra cohomology and geometric induction.

In Section
2
, we describe some background information for
\mathfrak{q}(n)
and quivers, and we formulate our main theorems, Theorems
2.7
and
2.8
. In Section
3
, we introduce geometric induction and prove a “virtual” BGG reciprocity law, Theorem
3.9
, that generalizes
[
13
]
to the queer Lie superalgebras. This result allows us to describe radical filtrations of all finite-dimensional indecomposable projective modules for
\mathfrak{sq}(3)
and
\mathfrak{q}(3)
. Diagrams of these are provided in Appendix
A
. In Section
4
, we prove a result on self extensions of simples for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
, Theorem
4.1
, and for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sq}(n)
, Theorem
4.5
. In Section
5
, we show the standard block for
\mathfrak{q}(n)
is closely related to the principal block of
\mathfrak{q}(n-1)
, Proposition
5.1
, and in particular deduce the quiver for
\mathfrak{sq}(3)
and
\mathfrak{q}(3)
standard block. Finally in Section
6
, we compute the quiver for principal block of
\mathfrak{sq}(3)
and
\mathfrak{q}(3)
.

2
Preliminaries and main theorem

2.1
General definitions

Throughout we work with
{\mathbf{C}}
as the ground field. We set
{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}={\mathbf{Z}}/2{\mathbf{Z}}
. Recall that a
vector superspace

V=V_{\bar{0}}\oplus V_{\bar{1}}
is a
{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}
-graded vector space. Elements of
V_{\overline{0}}
and
V_{\overline{1}}
are called even and odd, respectively. If
V
,
V^{\prime}
are superspaces, then the space
\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathbf{C}}}(V,V^{\prime})
is naturally
{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}
-graded with grading
f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathbf{C}}}(V,V^{\prime})_{s}
if
f(V_{{r}})\subset V_{{r+s}}^{\prime}
for all
{r}\in{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}
.

A
superalgebra
is a
{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}
-graded, unital, associative algebra
A=A_{\overline{0}}\oplus A_{\overline{1}}
which satisfies
A_{{r}}A_{s}\subset A_{{r+s}}
. A
Lie superalgebra
is a superspace
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{1}}
with bracket operation
[\,,\,]\colon\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathfrak{g}\allowbreak\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
which preserves the graded version of the usual Lie bracket axioms. The
universal enveloping algebra

U(\mathfrak{g})
is
{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}
-graded and satisfies a PBW type theorem
[
16
]
.
A
\mathfrak{g}
-module
is a left
{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}
-graded
U(\mathfrak{g})
-module. A
morphism
of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules
M\rightarrow M^{\prime}
is an element of
\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathbf{C}}}(M,M^{\prime})_{\bar{0}}
satisfying
f(xm)=xf(m)
for all
m\in M,x\in U(\mathfrak{g})
. We denote by
\mathfrak{g}
-mod the category of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules. This is a symmetric monoidal category. The primary category of interest
\mathcal{F}
consists of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-modules which are semisimple over
\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}
. We stress that we only allow for
parity preserving
morphisms in
\mathcal{F}
. In this way,
\mathcal{F}
is an abelian rigid symmetric monoidal category: for
V,W\in\mathcal{F}
, define
V\otimes W
and
V^{*}
using the coproduct and antipode of
U(\mathfrak{g})
, respectively:

\delta(x)=x\otimes 1+1\otimes x,\qquad S(x)=-x\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathfrak{g}.

For
V\in\mathfrak{g}
-mod, we denote by
S(V)
the symmetric superalgebra. As a
\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}
-module,
S(V)
is isomorphic to
S(V)=S(V_{\bar{0}})\otimes\Lambda(V_{\bar{1}})
, where
\Lambda(V_{\bar{1}})
is the exterior algebra of
V_{\bar{1}}
in the category of vector spaces. For
V
a
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
-module and
W
a
\mathfrak{g}_{2}
-module, we define the outer tensor product
V\boxtimes W
to be the
\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2}
-module with the action for
(q_{1},q_{2})\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2}
given by

(q_{1},q_{2})(v\boxtimes w):=(-1)^{\overline{q_{2}}\overline{v}}(q_{1}v\boxtimes q_{2}w).

We define the (super)dimension of
V\in\mathfrak{g}
-mod as follows. Let
{\mathbf{C}}[\varepsilon]
be polynomial algebra with variable
\varepsilon
and denote two-dimensional
{\mathbf{C}}
-algebra
{\mathbf{C}}[\varepsilon]/\big(\varepsilon^{2}-1\big)
as
\widetilde{{\mathbf{C}}}
. Then

\dim(V):=\dim_{{\mathbf{C}}}(V_{\overline{0}})+\dim_{{\mathbf{C}}}(V_{\bar{1}})\varepsilon\in\widetilde{{\mathbf{C}}}.

The
parity change functor

\Pi\colon A\text{\rm-smod}\rightarrow A{\rm-smod}
is defined as follows: For
M\in A\text{\rm-smod}
,
\Pi(M)_{\bar{0}}:=M_{\bar{1}}
and
\Pi(M)_{\bar{1}}:=M_{\bar{0}}
and the action on
m\in\Pi(M)
is
a\cdot m=(-1)^{\bar{a}}am
. Lastly, if
f\colon M\rightarrow N
is a morphism of supermodules, then
\Pi f\colon\Pi M\rightarrow\Pi N
is
\Pi f=f
.

2.2
The queer Lie superalgebra
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{q}(n)}

By definition, the
queer Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{q}(n)
is the Lie subsuperalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(n|n)
leaving invariant an odd automorphism of the standard representation
p
with the property
p^{2}=-1
. In matrix form,

\mathfrak{q}(n)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B&A\end{pmatrix}\colon A,B\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbf{C}})\right\},\qquad\text{if}\quad p=\begin{pmatrix}0&1_{n}\\
-1_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
. The even (resp. odd) subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
consists of block matrices with
B=0
(resp.
A=0
). For
1\leq i,j\leq n
, we define the standard basis elements as

e_{i,j}^{\bar{0}}=\begin{pmatrix}E_{i,j}&0\\
0&E_{i,j}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad e_{i,j}^{\bar{1}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&E_{i,j}\\
E_{i,j}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{1}},

where
E_{i,j}
denote the elementary matrix.
Observe the odd trace
\operatorname{otr}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
B&A\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right):=\operatorname{tr}(B)
annihilates the commutator
[\mathfrak{q}(n),\mathfrak{q}(n)]
. Let

\mathfrak{sq}(n)=\{X\in\mathfrak{q}(n)\colon\operatorname{otr}(X)=0\}.

Furthermore,
\operatorname{otr}(XY)
defines a nondegenerate
\mathfrak{g}
-invariant odd bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}
. In particular, we have an isomorphism
\mathfrak{q}(n)^{*}\cong\Pi\mathfrak{q}(n)
of
\mathfrak{q}(n)
-modules.

All Borel Lie superalgebras
\mathfrak{b}\subset\mathfrak{g}
are conjugate to the “standard” Borel, i.e., block matrices where
A,B\in\mathfrak{gl}(n)
are upper triangular. The
nilpotent subsuperalgebra

\mathfrak{n}
consists of block matrices where
A
,
B
are strictly upper triangular.

In the standard basis, the supercommutator has the form

[e_{ij}^{\sigma},e_{kl}^{\tau}]=\delta_{jk}e_{il}^{\sigma+\tau}-(-1)^{\sigma\tau}\delta_{il}e_{kj}^{\sigma+\tau},

where
\sigma,\tau\in{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}
. The
Cartan superalgebra

\mathfrak{h}
has basis
e_{ii}^{\sigma}
for
1\leq i\leq n
,
\sigma\in{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}
. The elements
H_{i}:=e_{ii}^{\bar{0}}
,
\overline{H}_{i}:=e_{ii}^{\bar{1}}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, form a basis for
\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}
,
\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{1}}
, respectively. Let
\{\varepsilon_{i}\,|\,i=1,\dots,n\}\subset\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}^{*}
denote the dual basis of
\{H_{i}\}
. There is a root decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}
with respect to the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
given by

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},

where
\Phi=\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}\,|\,1\leq i\neq j\leq n\}
is the same as the set of roots of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbf{C}})
. For a root
\alpha=\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}
we have
\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}=1+\varepsilon
because
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{i,j}^{\sigma}\colon\sigma\in{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}\}
. The positive roots are
\Phi^{+}:=\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}\colon 1\leq i<j\leq n\}
. The simple roots are
\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i+1}\colon 1\leq i\leq n-1\}
. The Weyl group for
\mathfrak{q}(n)
is
W=S_{n}
, the symmetric group on
n
letters.

By
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
we denote the Cartan subsuperalgebra of
\mathfrak{sq}(n)
.
A
weight
is by definition an element
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}^{*}
and we write it in the form
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
with respect to the standard basis
(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n})
. We say
\lambda
is integral if
\lambda_{i}\in{\mathbf{Z}}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. We say
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}^{*}
is
typical
if
\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}\neq 0
for all
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
. We introduce partial ordering on
\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}^{*}
via
\lambda\leq\mu
if and only if
\mu-\lambda\in\mathbf{N}\Phi^{+}
. Finally, we define
\rho_{0}:=1/2\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\alpha
.

2.3
Irreducible
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{h}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}
-representations

Following
[
20
, Proposition 1]
, we now define for each
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}^{*}
a simple
\mathfrak{h}
-supermodule. Define an even superantisymmetric bilinear form
F_{\lambda}\colon\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{1}}\times\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{1}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{C}}
as
F_{\lambda}(u,v):=\lambda([u,v])
. Let
K_{\lambda}=\operatorname{Ker}F_{\lambda}
and
E_{\lambda}=\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{1}}/K_{\lambda}
. The restriction of
F_{\lambda}
to
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
will be denoted by
F^{\prime}_{\lambda}
and we set

K^{\prime}_{\lambda}=\operatorname{Ker}F^{\prime}_{\lambda}
and
E^{\prime}_{\lambda}=\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}_{\bar{1}}/K^{\prime}_{\lambda}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}^{*}
.

(a)

If there exists
i
such that
\lambda_{i}=0
, then

\dim E_{\lambda}=\dim E^{\prime}_{\lambda}=|\{i\colon\lambda_{i}\neq 0\}|.

(b)

If all
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
and
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\neq 0
, then

\dim E^{\prime}_{\lambda}=n-1,\qquad\dim E_{\lambda}=n.

(c)

If all
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
and
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}=0
, then

\dim E^{\prime}_{\lambda}=n-2,\qquad\dim E_{\lambda}=n.

Proof.

It is straightforward that
K_{\lambda}
is the span of
\bar{H}_{i}
for all
i
such that
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
. Hence

\dim E_{\lambda}=|\{i\colon\lambda_{i}\neq 0\}|.

To compute
K^{\prime}_{\lambda}
, consider the basis
\{\bar{H}_{i}-\bar{H}_{i+1}\,|\,i=1,\dots,n-1\}
of
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}_{\bar{1}}
. Then

\displaystyle K^{\prime}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\{u\in\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}_{\bar{1}}\,|\,\lambda([u,\bar{H}_{i}-\bar{H}_{i+1}])=0\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n-1\}

\displaystyle=\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{1}}\,|\,u_{1}+\dots+u_{n}=0,u_{i}\lambda_{i}=u_{i+1}\lambda_{i+1}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n-1\}.

Suppose first without loss of generality
\lambda_{1}=\dots=\lambda_{k}=0
, where
k\geq 1
. This forces
u_{k+1}=\dots=u_{n}=0
and
u_{1}+\dots+u_{k}=0
, so
K^{\prime}_{\lambda}
has a basis

\big\{\bar{H}_{1}-\bar{H}_{2},\dots,\bar{H}_{k-1}-\bar{H}_{k}\big\}

and
\dim K^{\prime}_{\lambda}=k-1
.
Thus,
\dim E^{\prime}_{\lambda}=\dim\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}_{\bar{1}}-\dim{K^{\prime}_{\lambda}}=n-k
.

Next, suppose all
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
. Then similarly we compute

K^{\prime}_{\lambda}=\begin{cases}{\mathbf{C}}\big(\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}},\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}},\frac{1}{\lambda_{3}},\dots,\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\big)&\text{if }\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}=0,\\
0&\text{if }\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\neq 0.\end{cases}

∎

Let
\dim E_{\lambda}=m>0
. On the vector superspace
E_{\lambda}
,
F_{\lambda}
induces a nondegenerate bilinear form, also denoted
F_{\lambda}
. Let
\operatorname{Cliff}(\lambda)
be the
Clifford superalgebra
defined by
E_{\lambda}
and
F_{\lambda}
. Then (1)
\operatorname{Cliff}(\lambda)
is isomorphic to Cliff
(m)
, the Clifford superalgebra with generators
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}
and relations
e_{i}^{2}=1
, (2)
\dim\operatorname{Cliff}(\lambda)=2^{m-1}(1+\varepsilon)
, and (3) the category
\operatorname{Cliff}(\lambda)
-mod is semisimple (e.g.,
[
19
]
).

If
m
is odd, then there exists a unique simple
\operatorname{Cliff}(m)
-module, denoted by
v(m)
, which is invariant under parity change (this follows from existence of an odd automorphism). If
m
is even, then there exists
2
nonisomorphic simple
\operatorname{Cliff}(m)
-modules
v(m)
and
\Pi v(m)
which are swapped by the parity change functor. Using the surjective homomorphism
U(\mathfrak{h})\rightarrow\operatorname{Cliff}(\lambda)
with kernel
(H_{i}-\lambda_{i},K_{\lambda})
, we lift
v(m)
to an
\mathfrak{h}
-module which we denote by
v(\lambda)
. Lemma
2.1
implies

\dim v(\lambda)=\dim(v(m))=2^{\left\lfloor{(m-1)/2}\right\rfloor}(1+\varepsilon),

where
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the integer part of
x\in\mathbf{R}
. Furthermore, this construction provides a complete irredundant collection of all finite-dimensional simple
\mathfrak{h}
-supermodules.

Next define the
Verma module

M_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda):=U(\mathfrak{g})\otimes_{U(\mathfrak{b})}v(\lambda),

where the action of
\mathfrak{n}^{+}
on
v(\lambda)
is trivial.

Let

\Lambda=\{\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}^{*}\colon\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i+1}\in{\mathbf{Z}}\}.

The set of
\mathfrak{g}
-dominant integral weights is

\Lambda^{+}=\{\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}^{*}\colon\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i+1}\in{\mathbf{Z}}_{\geq 0}\ \text{and}\ \lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}\Rightarrow\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}=0\}.

Below is the main theorem about irreducible
\mathfrak{g}
-modules, first proven by V. Kac.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
16
]
)
.

1.

For any weight
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}^{*}
,
M_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)
has a unique maximal submodule
N(\lambda)
, hence a unique simple quotient,
L_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)
.

2.

For each finite-dimensional irreducible
\mathfrak{g}
-module
V
, there exists a unique weight
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
such that V is a homomorphic image of
M_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)
.

3.

L_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda):=M_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)/N_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)
is finite dimensional if and only if
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
.

We will often omit the subscript
\mathfrak{g}
in the notation for Verma, simple, and projective modules.

2.4
The category
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
. Denote by
\mathcal{F}^{n}
, or simply
\mathcal{F}
the category consisting of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-supermodules semisimple over
\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}
(so the center of
\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}
acts semisimply), with morphisms being parity preserving. The full subcategory of
\mathcal{F}
consisting of modules with integral weights is equivalent to the category of finite-dimensional
G
-modules, where
G
is the algebraic supergroup with
\text{Lie}(G)=\mathfrak{g}
and
G_{\bar{0}}={\rm GL}(n)
.

Let
Z(\mathfrak{g})
be the center of the universal enveloping algebra
U(\mathfrak{g})
. A central character is a homomorphism
\chi\colon Z(U(\mathfrak{g}))\rightarrow{\mathbf{C}}
. We say that a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
has central character
\chi
if for any
z\in Z(\mathfrak{g})
,
m\in M
, there exists a positive integer
n
such that
(z-\chi(z)\text{id})^{n}.m=0
. It is well known from linear algebra that any finite-dimensional indecomposable
\mathfrak{g}
-module has a central character, hence
\mathcal{F}^{n}=\oplus\mathcal{F}^{n}_{\chi}
, where
\mathcal{F}^{n}_{\chi}
is the subcategory of modules admitting central character
\chi
. In the most cases
\mathcal{F}^{n}_{\chi}
is indecomposable, i.e., a block in the category
\mathcal{F}^{n}
. The only exception is
\mathcal{F}^{n}_{\chi}
for even
n
and typical central character
\chi
. In this case

\mathcal{F}^{n}_{\chi}
is semisimple and has two non-isomorphic simple objects
L(\lambda)
and
\Pi L(\lambda)
.

Similarly to the Lie algebra case, there is a canonical injective algebra homomorphism, the
Harish-Chandra homomorphism

[
8
,
25
]
,

\text{HC}\colon\ Z(\mathfrak{g})\hookrightarrow S(\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}})^{W}.

Given any
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}^{*}
, we define
\chi_{\lambda}\colon Z(\mathfrak{g})\rightarrow{\mathbf{C}}
to be the unique homomorphism making

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z(\mathfrak{g})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S(\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}})^{W}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbf{C}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm HC}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi_{\lambda}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda^{W}}

commute, where
\lambda^{W}
is the natural homomorphism induced by
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}^{*}
. If
\chi=\chi_{\lambda}
for some
\lambda
, we denote
\mathcal{F}_{\chi_{\lambda}}
by
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
.
Given a central character
\chi_{\lambda}
with
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
, we define its
weight
to be the formal sum

\text{\rm wt}(\lambda):=\delta_{\lambda_{1}}+\dots+\delta_{\lambda_{n}},

where
\delta_{i}=-\delta_{-i}
and
\delta_{0}=0
. A fundamental result by Sergeev
[
25
]
implies:

Theorem 2.3
.

For
\lambda,\mu\in\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}^{*}
,
\chi_{\lambda}=\chi_{\mu}
if and only if
\text{\rm wt}(\lambda)=\text{\rm wt}(\mu)
.

The following classification theorem about blocks in
\mathcal{F}^{3}
is important for us. It is an immediate consequence of
[
24
, Theorem 5.8]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})\in\Lambda^{+}\cap{\mathbf{Z}}^{3}
be a dominant integral weight and
|\lambda|
be the number of non-zero coordinates in
\text{\rm wt}(\lambda)
.

•

(
the strongly typical block
)
If
|\lambda|=3
, then
\mathcal{F}^{3}_{\lambda}
is semisimple and contains one up to isomorphism simple module;

•

(
the typical block
)
If
|\lambda|=2
, then
\mathcal{F}^{3}_{\lambda}
is equivalent to the block
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{(0)}
for
\mathfrak{q}(1)
;

•

(
the standard block
)
If
|\lambda|=1
, then
\mathcal{F}^{3}_{\lambda}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{F}^{3}_{(1,0,0)}
;

•

(
the principal block
)
If
|\lambda|=0
, then
\mathcal{F}^{3}_{\lambda}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{F}^{3}_{(0,0,0)}
.

Furthermore, if
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
but
\lambda\notin{\mathbf{Z}}^{3}
, then either
\lambda
is typical, so
\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}\neq 0

\forall\,i,j

Let

An

\lambda

A
system of relations
on
Q
is a map

\displaystyle R(\nu,\mu)\circ\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathbf{C}}Q}(\lambda,\nu)\subset R(\lambda,\mu)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathbf{C}}Q}(\nu,\mu)\circ R(\lambda,\nu)\subset R(\lambda,\mu).

together with linear maps

3.1
Relative cohomology of Lie superalgebras

Let
\mathfrak{t}\subset\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie subsuperalgebra and
M
a
\mathfrak{g}
-module. For
p\geq 0
, define

C^{p}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{t};M)=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{t}}(\wedge^{p}(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{t}),M),

where
\wedge^{p}(\mathfrak{g})
is the
super
wedge product. The differential maps
d^{p}\colon C^{p}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{t};M)\to C^{p+1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{t};M)
are defined in the same way as for Lie algebras,
see for example
[
2
, Section 2.2]
. The
relative cohomology
are defined by

\operatorname{H}^{p}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{t};M)=\operatorname{Ker}d^{p}/\operatorname{Im}d^{p-1}.

We will be interested in the case when
\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}
. Then the relative cohomology describe the extension groups in the category
\mathcal{F}
of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-modules semisimple over
\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}
.
More precisely, we have the following relation:

\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathcal{F}}^{p}(M,N)\cong\operatorname{H}^{p}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}};M^{*}\otimes N).

From here on out, we will use
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{g}}^{i}(-,-)
to denote
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathcal{F}}^{i}(-,-)
. For conciseness, we often write
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{q}}
or
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{sq}}
to denote
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}
or
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ext}^{i}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}({\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{C}})\cong\begin{cases}S^{i}(\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}^{*})^{\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}}&\text{if $i$ even},\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}\!\qquad\text{and}\!\!\qquad\operatorname{Ext}^{i}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}({\mathbf{C}},\Pi{\mathbf{C}})\cong\begin{cases}S^{i}(\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}^{*})^{\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}}&\text{if $i$ odd},\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Note that
\mathfrak{g}_{1}\cong\Pi\mathfrak{g}_{1}
as a
\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}
-module and therefore
\Lambda^{i}(\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{1}}^{*})\cong\Pi^{i}S^{i}(\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}^{*})
. Therefore

\displaystyle C^{i}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}};\mathbf{C})\cong\begin{cases}S^{i}(\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}^{*})^{\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}}&\text{if $i$ even},\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad C^{i}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}};\Pi{\mathbf{C}})\cong\begin{cases}S^{i}(\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}^{*})^{\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}}&\text{if $i$ odd},\\

3.2
Geometric induction

We next provide a few facts about geometric induction following the exposition in
[
12
,
22
]
. Let
\mathfrak{p}
be any parabolic subsuperalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
containing
\mathfrak{b}
. Let
G=Q(n)
, and
P
,
B
be the corresponding Lie supergroups of
\mathfrak{p}
,
\mathfrak{b}
. For a
P
-module
V
, we denote by the calligraphic letter
\mathcal{V}
the vector bundle
G\times_{P}V
over the generalized grassmannian
G/P
. See
[
17
]
for the construction. Note that the space of sections of
\mathcal{V}
on any open set has a natural structure of a
\mathfrak{g}
-module; in other words the sheaf of sections of
\mathcal{V}
is a
\mathfrak{g}
-sheaf. Therefore the cohomology groups
H^{i}(G/P,\mathcal{V})
are
\mathfrak{g}
-modules. Define the geometric induction functor
\Gamma_{i}
from category of
\mathfrak{p}
-modules to category of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules as

\Gamma_{i}(G/P,V):=H^{i}(G/P,\mathcal{V^{*}})^{*}.

It is also possible to define
\Gamma_{i}(G/P,V)
without the need of proving the rather technical question of existence of
G/P
. Namely, consider the Zuckerman functor from the category of
P
-modules to
G
-modules
defined by

H^{0}(G/P,V):=\Gamma_{\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}}(\operatorname{Hom}_{U(\mathfrak{p})}(U(\mathfrak{g}),V)),

where
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}}(M)
denotes the set of
\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}
-finite vectors of
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
. One can show easily that
H^{0}(G/P,V)
has a unique
G
-module structure compatible with the
\mathfrak{g}
-action. It is also straightforward that

H^{0}(G/P,V)
is left exact and the right
adjoint to the restriction functor
G\text{\rm-mod}\to P\text{\rm-mod}
. We define
H^{i}(G/P,\cdot)
to be its right derived functors. Using this definition we can define
\Gamma_{i}(G/P,V)
for any
V

whose weights are in
\Lambda
.

We state some well known results.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
12
,
15
]
)
.

The functor
\Gamma_{i}
satisfies the following properties.

1.

For any short exact sequence of
P
-modules

0\rightarrow U\rightarrow V\rightarrow W\rightarrow 0,

there is a long exact sequence of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules

\cdots\rightarrow\Gamma_{1}(G/P,W)\rightarrow\Gamma_{0}(G/P,U)\rightarrow\Gamma_{0}(G/P,V)\rightarrow\Gamma_{0}(G/P,W)\rightarrow 0.

2.

For a
P
-module
V
and a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
,

\Gamma_{i}(G/P,V\otimes M)=\Gamma_{i}(G/P,V)\otimes M.

3.

\Gamma_{0}(G/P,V)
is the maximal finite-dimensional quotient of
M_{\mathfrak{p}}(V):=U(\mathfrak{g})\otimes_{U(\mathfrak{p})}V
in the sense that any finite-dimensional quotient of
M_{\mathfrak{p}}(V)
is a quotient of
\Gamma_{0}(G/P,V)
.

If
G=Q(n)
, then all parabolic subgroups containing the standard Borel subgroup
B
are in bijection with those of
{\rm GL}(n)
. Hence they are enumerated by partitions. The Levi subgroup
L
of parabolic
P
is isomorphic to
Q(m_{1})\times\dots\times Q(m_{k})
with
m_{1}+\dots+m_{k}=n
. A weight
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
is called
\mathfrak{p}
-typical if

\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}=0\qquad\text{implies}\qquad m_{1}+\dots+m_{s}<i,j\leq m_{1}+\dots+m_{s+1}.

Proposition 3.4

(typical lemma,
[
22
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Let
P
be any parabolic supergroup containing
B
and suppose
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
is
\mathfrak{p}
-typical, where
\mathfrak{p}:=Lie(P)
. Then

\Gamma_{i}(G/P,L_{\mathfrak{p}}(\lambda))=\begin{cases}L(\lambda)&\text{if }i=0,\\
0&\text{if }i>0.\end{cases}

Now, for any parabolic supergroup
P
containing
B
, define the multiplicity

m_{P}^{i}(\lambda,\mu):=[\Gamma_{i}(G/P,L_{\mathfrak{p}}(\lambda)):L_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mu)].

Proposition 3.5
.

If
\lambda>\mu
, then

m^{0}_{B}(\lambda,\mu)\geq\dim\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{g}}^{1}(L(\lambda),L(\mu)).

Proof.

Suppose
0\rightarrow L(\mu)\rightarrow V\rightarrow L(\lambda)\rightarrow 0
is an extension. Then
V
contains a highest weight vector
v_{\lambda}
of weight
\lambda
coming from the inverse image of that of
L(\lambda)
. Since
V
is indecomposable,
V
is generated by
v_{\lambda}
and since
\mu<\lambda
,
V=U(\mathfrak{g}).v_{\lambda}
is annihilated by
\mathfrak{n}^{+}
. Thus
V
is a highest weight module of weight
\lambda
, so it is a finite-dimensional quotient of
M(\lambda)
and consequently by Proposition
3.3
(3), it is a quotient of
\Gamma_{0}(G/B,L_{\mathfrak{b}}(\lambda))
. Each such isomorphism class of extension
V
thus gives rise to a distinct subquotient
L(\mu)
in
\Gamma_{0}(G/B,L_{\mathfrak{b}}(\lambda)).
Consequently,
\dim\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{g}}^{1}(L(\lambda),L(\mu))\leq[\Gamma_{0}(G/B,L_{\mathfrak{b}}(\lambda)):L(\mu)]=m^{0}_{B}(\lambda,\mu)
.
∎

3.3
Virtual BGG reciprocity

We now formulate a “virtual” BGG reciprocity theorem for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sq}(n)
or
\mathfrak{q}(n)
which will be used to compute composition factors of indecomposable projective covers,
P_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)
of
L_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)
.
This result is a generalization of Theorem 1 in
[
13
]
in the case when Cartan subalgebra is not purely even. In this section we consider the quotient
\mathcal{K}^{\Pi}(\mathcal{F})
of the Grothendieck ring

\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{F})
by the relation
[X]=[\Pi X]
. Then
\mathcal{K}^{\Pi}(\mathcal{F})
has a basis
\{[L(\lambda)]\,|\,\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}\}
and
[X:L(\lambda)]_{\Pi}
is the coefficient

a_{\lambda}
in the decomposition
[X]=\sum a_{\lambda}[L(\lambda)]
.

Denote by
\Lambda_{0}^{+}:=\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}_{0}^{*}\,|\,\langle\lambda,\check{\beta}\rangle\in\mathbf{Z}_{>0},\;\forall\,\beta\in\Delta_{\overline{0}}^{+}\}
. For
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
,
\Lambda_{0}^{+}
consists of dominant integral weights for which at most one
\lambda_{i}
is zero. For
M\in\mathcal{F}^{\Pi}
, define
\mathcal{R}:={\mathbf{Z}}[e^{\mu}]_{\mu\in\Lambda}
and the
character
of
M

\text{\rm Ch}(M):=\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda}\dim(M_{\mu})e^{\mu}\in\mathcal{R},

where we put
\dim X:=\dim X_{\bar{0}}+\dim X_{\bar{1}}
.
Then
Ch
defines an injective homomorphism
\mathcal{K}^{\Pi}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathcal{R}
.

For any
\lambda\in\Lambda
we define an
Euler characteristic
as

\mathcal{E}(\lambda):=\sum_{\mu}\sum_{i=0}^{\dim(G/B)_{\bar{0}}}(-1)^{i}[\Gamma_{i}(G/B,v({\lambda})):L(\mu)]_{\Pi}[L(\mu)],

where
\Gamma_{i}
is the dual to geometric induction functor as defined in Section
2.2
.
It is straightforward to check (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Theorem 4.25]
) that for
\lambda\in\Lambda
such that
\text{\rm wt}(\lambda)=\gamma
, then
[\mathcal{E}(\lambda)]\in\mathcal{K}^{\Pi}(\mathcal{F}_{\gamma})
.

Let us comment on the relation between this Euler characteristic the one defined in
[
4
]
. There, the author considered an induction from the maximal parabolic
P_{\lambda}
to which
v({\lambda})
extends, i.e.,

\mathcal{E}_{P}(\lambda):=\sum_{\mu}\sum_{i=0}^{\dim(G/P_{\lambda})_{\bar{0}}}(-1)^{i}[\Gamma_{i}(G/P_{\lambda},v({\lambda})):L(\mu)][L(\mu)].

If
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
is regular then
P=B
and
\mathcal{E}_{P}(\lambda)=\mathcal{E}(\lambda)
and if
\lambda
is not regular
\mathcal{E}(\lambda)=0
while
\mathcal{E}_{P}(\lambda)\neq 0
. It was shown in
[
4
]

that
\mathcal{E}_{P}(\lambda)
form a basis of the Grothendieck group of
\mathcal{F}
.

The following result is a straightforward generalization of
[
12
, Lemma 1.2]
.

Lemma 3.8
.

The Euler characteristic
\mathcal{E}(\lambda)
satisfies

1.

\text{\rm Ch}(\mathcal{E}(\lambda))=\dim v({\lambda})D\sum_{w\in S_{n}}\varepsilon(w)e^{w.\lambda},

where

D=\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\frac{e^{\alpha/2}+e^{-\alpha/2}}{e^{\alpha/2}-e^{-\alpha/2}}.

2.

For all
w\in W
,

\mathcal{E}(\lambda)=\varepsilon(w)\mathcal{E}(w.\lambda).

3.

Let
\Lambda^{+}_{0}
denote the set of regular dominant weights with respect to
\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}
. The set

\{\text{\rm Ch}(\mathcal{E}(\lambda)),\lambda\in\Lambda_{0}^{+}\}

is linearly independent in the ring
\mathcal{R}
.

We call a simple
\mathfrak{g}
-module
L(\lambda)
of
type M
if
\Pi L(\lambda)
is not isomorphic to
L(\lambda)
and of
type Q
if
\Pi L(\lambda)\cong L(\lambda)
.
Note that the type of
L(\lambda)
is the same as the type of
v(\lambda)
. Furthermore, for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
the type depends on the number of non-zero entries in
\lambda
: the type is
M
, if this number is even, and
Q
if it is odd.
For example,
L(1,0,0)
is of type Q and
L(0)
is of type M. We set

t(\nu)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }L(\nu)\ \text{type }M,\\
0&\text{if }L(\nu)\ \text{type }Q.\end{cases}

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
or
\mathfrak{sq}(n)
. Let
\mu\in\Lambda^{+}
and
b_{\mu,\lambda}
be the coefficients occurring in the expansion

\mathcal{E}(\mu)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}}b_{\mu,\lambda}[L(\lambda)].

Then there exists coefficients
a_{\lambda,\mu}
such that for
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
,

[P(\lambda)]=\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda_{0}^{+}}a_{\lambda,\mu}\mathcal{E}(\mu)

and

a_{\lambda,\mu}=2^{t(\mu)-t(\lambda)}\gamma_{\mu}b_{\mu,\lambda},

where

\gamma_{\mu}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ \mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)\ \text{and }\prod\mu_{i}\neq 0,\ \text{or}\ \mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sq}(n)\ \text{and}\ \sum\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}\neq 0,\\
2&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We follow the proof of
[
13
, Theorem 1]
. First, we have the Bott reciprocity formula

\dim\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{g}}(P(\lambda),\Gamma_{i}(V))=\dim\operatorname{Ext}^{i}_{B}(V,P(\lambda))=\dim H^{i}(\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}};V^{*}\otimes P(\lambda)).

(3.1)

Let
C^{i}(\mathfrak{n},-)
stand for the
i
-th term of the cochain complex computing
H^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{n},-)
. Note that
P(\lambda)
and hence
C^{i}(\mathfrak{n};V^{*}\otimes P(\lambda))
is projective and injective in the category of

\mathfrak{h}
-modules semisimple over
\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}}
.
Hence
H^{j}(\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}_{\bar{0}};C^{i}(\mathfrak{n},V^{*}\otimes P(\lambda)))=0
for any
i
and
j\geq 1
. Therefore the first term of the spectral sequence for the pair

(\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{h})

implies that

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}\dim\operatorname{Ext}^{i}_{B}(v(\mu),P(\lambda))=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}\dim\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{h}}(v(\mu),C^{i}(\mathfrak{n},P(\lambda))).

(3.2)

Furthermore, we have

[M:L(\lambda)]_{\Pi}=\begin{cases}\dim\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{g}}(P(\lambda)\oplus\Pi P(\lambda),M)&\text{if }L(\lambda)\text{ type }M,\\
\dim\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{g}}(P(\lambda),M)&\text{if }L(\lambda)\text{ type }Q.\end{cases}

(3.3)

Define
b^{i}_{\mu,\lambda}
by

b^{i}_{\mu,\lambda}:=\begin{cases}\dim\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{h}}(v(\mu)\oplus\Pi v(\mu),C^{i}(\mathfrak{n},P(\lambda)))&\text{if }L(\lambda)\text{ type }M,\\
\dim\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{h}}(v(\mu),C^{i}(\mathfrak{n},P(\lambda)))&\text{if }L(\lambda)\text{ type }Q.\end{cases}

By application of (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) we obtain

b_{\mu,\lambda}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}b^{i}_{\mu,\lambda}.

For any module
M\in\mathcal{F}
projective over
\mathfrak{h}
we have the equality

\frac{\dim M_{\mu}}{\dim\hat{v}(\mu)}=\begin{cases}\dim\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{h}}(v(\mu)\oplus\Pi v(\mu),M)&\text{if }v(\mu)\text{ type }M,\\
\dim\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{h}}(v(\mu),M)&\text{if }v(\mu)\text{ type }Q,\end{cases}

(3.4)

where
\hat{v}(\mu)
is the corresponding indecomposable injective
\mathfrak{h}
-module.
In other words we get

\text{\rm Ch}(M)=\sum_{\mu\ \text{type M}}\dim\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{h}}(v(\mu)\oplus\Pi v(\mu),M)e^{\mu}+\sum_{\mu\ \text{type Q}}\dim\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{h}}(v(\mu),M)e^{\mu}.

If
\lambda
is of type Q we obtain

\displaystyle\text{\rm Ch}(C^{i}(\mathfrak{n},P(\lambda)))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\ \text{type M}}2b_{\mu,\lambda}^{i}\dim\hat{v}(\mu)e^{\mu}+\sum_{\mu\ \text{type Q}}b_{\mu,\lambda}^{i}\dim\hat{v}(\mu)e^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}2^{t(\mu)-t(\lambda)}b_{\mu,\lambda}^{i}\dim\hat{v}(\mu)e^{\mu}.

If
\lambda
is of type M we obtain

\displaystyle\text{\rm Ch}(C^{i}(\mathfrak{n},P(\lambda)))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\ \text{type M}}b_{\mu,\lambda}^{i}\dim\hat{v}(\mu)e^{\mu}+\sum_{\mu\ \text{type Q}}\frac{1}{2}b_{\mu,\lambda}^{i}\dim\hat{v}(\mu)e^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}2^{t(\mu)-t(\lambda)}b_{\mu,\lambda}^{i}\dim\hat{v}(\mu)e^{\mu}.

Taking alternating sum over
i
we get

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}\text{\rm Ch}(C^{i}(\mathfrak{n},P(\lambda)))=\sum_{\mu}2^{t(\mu)-t(\lambda)}b_{\mu,\lambda}\dim\hat{v}(\mu)e^{\mu}.

On the other hand, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}\text{\rm Ch}(C^{i}(\mathfrak{n},P(\lambda)))=\text{\rm Ch}(P(\lambda))\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha}}{1+e^{-\alpha}}=D^{-1}\text{\rm Ch}(P(\lambda)).

This implies

\text{\rm Ch}(P(\lambda))=D\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda}b_{\mu,\lambda}\dim\hat{v}(\mu)2^{t(\mu)-t(\lambda)}e^{\mu}.

By
S_{n}
-invariance of
\text{\rm Ch}(P(\lambda))
, we get

b_{\mu,\lambda}=\varepsilon(w)b_{w.\mu,\lambda}\qquad\forall\,w\in S_{n}.

This together with
\dim\hat{v}(\mu)=\dim\hat{v}(w.\mu)
implies

\displaystyle\text{\rm Ch}(P(\lambda))

\displaystyle=D\sum_{w\in W}\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}_{0}}b_{\mu,\lambda}\varepsilon(w)\dim\hat{v}(\mu)2^{t(\mu)-t(\lambda)}e^{w.\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}_{0}}\frac{\dim\hat{v}(\mu)}{\dim v(\mu)}2^{t(\mu)-t(\lambda)}b_{\mu,\lambda}\text{\rm Ch}(\mathcal{E}(\mu)).

Therefore we obtain the relation

a_{\lambda,\mu}=\frac{\dim\hat{v}(\mu)}{\dim v(\mu)}2^{t(\mu)-t(\lambda)}b_{\mu,\lambda}.

(3.5)

Since
\mu\in\Lambda_{0}^{+}
at most one
\mu_{i}=0
. Therefore, we get: for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
,
v(\mu)=\hat{v}(\mu)
if all
\mu_{i}\neq 0
; for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sq}(n)
,
v(\mu)=\hat{v}(\mu)
if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}\neq 0
.
In remaining cases
\frac{\dim\hat{v}(\mu)}{\dim v(\mu)}=2
.
∎

3.4
General lemma

To study relations between block for
\mathfrak{sq}(n)
and
\mathfrak{q}(n)
we consider the induction and restriction functors

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ind}\colon\ \mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}\to\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)},\qquad M\mapsto\operatorname{Ind}^{\mathfrak{q}(n)}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}M,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Res}\colon\ \mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}\to\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)},\qquad M\mapsto\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}M.

The Frobenius reciprocity implies that
\operatorname{Ind}
is left adjoint of
\operatorname{Res}
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
M
be a projective
\mathfrak{sq}(n)
-module with
\Pi M\cong M
and let
\mathcal{A}=\operatorname{End}_{\mathfrak{sq}}(M)
,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\operatorname{End}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\operatorname{Ind}M)
. Assume that there exists
\theta\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

such that
\operatorname{Ker}\theta=\operatorname{Im}\theta
and
\operatorname{Ker}\theta\cap(1\otimes M)=\{0\}
. Then
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cong\mathcal{A}\otimes{\mathbf{C}}[\theta]/\big(\theta^{2}\big)
.

Proof.

Note that our assumptions imply
\operatorname{Res}\operatorname{Ind}M=M\oplus M
.
Consider injective homomorphism
\operatorname{Ind}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and
\operatorname{Res}\colon\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to\text{Mat}_{2}\otimes A
.
Furthermore, for
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\operatorname{Res}\operatorname{Ind}\gamma=\left(\begin{matrix}\gamma&\gamma^{\prime}\\
0&\gamma\end{matrix}\right)

for some
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
.
The condition
\theta^{2}=0
implies

\operatorname{Res}\theta=\left(\begin{matrix}0&0\\
\text{\rm Id}&0\end{matrix}\right).

The Frobenius reciprocity implies for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
,

\text{if}\quad\operatorname{Res}\varphi=\left(\begin{matrix}0&\sigma\\
0&\tau\end{matrix}\right)\qquad\text{then}\qquad\operatorname{Res}\varphi=0.

We have

\displaystyle[\operatorname{Res}\operatorname{Ind}\gamma,\operatorname{Res}\theta]=\left(\begin{matrix}\gamma^{\prime}&0\\
0&-\gamma^{\prime}\end{matrix}\right),\qquad[\operatorname{Res}\operatorname{Ind}\gamma,\operatorname{Res}\theta]-\operatorname{Res}\operatorname{Ind}\gamma^{\prime}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&-\gamma^{\prime\prime}\\
0&-2\gamma^{\prime}\end{matrix}\right),

hence
\gamma^{\prime}=0
.

Thus, we have proved that
\operatorname{Ind}(\mathcal{A})
commutes with
\theta
. Thus there is an injective homomorphism
\mathcal{A}\otimes{\mathbf{C}}[\theta]/\big(\theta^{2}\big)\to\mathcal{A}
. The dimension argument implies that it is an isomorphism.
∎

4
Self extensions

4.1
Self extensions for
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{q}(n)}

The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k},0,\dots,0,-\lambda_{k+m+1},\dots,-\lambda_{n})\in\Lambda^{+}
such that all
\lambda_{i}>0
be a dominant integral weight in
\mathfrak{q}(n)
. Then

\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}^{1}(L(\lambda),\Pi L(\lambda))=\begin{cases}{\mathbf{C}}&\text{if }m>0,\\
0&\text{if }m=0.\end{cases}

If
L(\lambda)\neq\Pi L(\lambda)
, then

\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}^{1}(L(\lambda),L(\lambda))=0.

Theorem
4.1
implies parts (1) and (2) of our main theorem
2.8
. Namely,
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(L(\lambda),L(\mu))\neq 0\Rightarrow\text{\rm wt}(\lambda)=\text{\rm wt}(\mu)
. Thus, by Theorem
4.1
, there are no extensions in the strongly typical block, and there is a unique extension
\frac{L(\lambda)}{\Pi L(\lambda)}
in the typical block. Thus in the typical block, the projective cover of
L(\lambda)
is
P(\lambda)=\frac{L(\lambda)}{\Pi L(\lambda)}
(Theorem
3.9
). Then
a\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{q}}(P(\lambda),\Pi P(\lambda))
implies
a^{2}=0
.

Proof.

The key idea is to take parabolic invariants to reduce the problem to finding extensions between trivial modules.
Let
\lambda
be as in the theorem. Define the parabolic subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
by

\mathfrak{p}:=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{1<i<j\leq n}\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}}\oplus\bigoplus_{k<i<j\leq k+m}\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{i}}.

Its Levi subalgebra
\mathfrak{l}\subset\mathfrak{p}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{q}(m)\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
where
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{h}
is the centralizer of
\mathfrak{q}(m)
in
\mathfrak{h}
. Let

n_{\mathfrak{p}}:=\bigoplus_{i\leq k<j\leq n}\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}}\oplus\bigoplus_{k<i\leq k+m<j\leq n}\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}}

be the nilpotent radical of
\mathfrak{p}
.

We first observe that taking
n_{\mathfrak{p}}
invariants is a functor from
\mathfrak{q}(n)
-mod to
\mathfrak{l}
-mod. Next, suppose
L(\lambda)^{n_{\mathfrak{p}}}
had a nontrivial
\mathfrak{l}
-invariant subspace
N
. Because
\mathfrak{l}
preserves the
\lambda
-weight space, and the lower parabolic nilpotent part only lowers the
\lambda
-weight space, we must have
U(\mathfrak{q}(n))N_{\lambda}\subsetneq L(\lambda)_{\lambda}\Rightarrow U(\mathfrak{q}(n))N\subsetneq L(\lambda)\Rightarrow N=0
, contradiction. Thus
L(\lambda)^{n_{\mathfrak{p}}}
is simple
\mathfrak{l}
-module.
On the other hand,
L(\lambda)_{\lambda}
is also an irreducible
\mathfrak{l}
-module of highest weight
\lambda
. So by the characterization of the simple highest weight
\mathfrak{l}
-modules,
L(\lambda)^{n_{\mathfrak{p}}}=L(\lambda)_{\lambda}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Using the above notation, the following linear maps are injective

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}^{1}(L(\lambda),L(\lambda))\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{l}}^{1}(L(\lambda)^{n_{\mathfrak{p}}},L(\lambda)^{n_{\mathfrak{p}}}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}^{1}(L(\lambda),\Pi L(\lambda))\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{l}}^{1}(L(\lambda)^{n_{\mathfrak{p}}},\Pi L(\lambda)^{n_{\mathfrak{p}}}).

Proof.

Suppose we had a sequence of
\mathfrak{q}(n)
-modules
0\rightarrow L(\lambda)\rightarrow M\rightarrow L(\lambda)\rightarrow 0
such that taking
n_{\mathfrak{p}}
invariants results in a split short exact sequence of
\mathfrak{l}
-modules

0\rightarrow L(\lambda)^{n_{\mathfrak{p}}}\xrightarrow{\phi}M^{n_{\mathfrak{p}}}\xrightarrow{\psi}L(\lambda)^{n_{\mathfrak{p}}}\rightarrow 0.

From before, we know this sequence is the same as

0\rightarrow L(\lambda)_{\lambda}\xrightarrow{\phi}M_{\lambda}\xrightarrow{\psi}L(\lambda)_{\lambda}\rightarrow 0.

Existence of a splitting maps means there exists an
\mathfrak{l}
-module homomorphism
\delta\colon L(\lambda)_{\lambda}\rightarrow M_{\lambda}
such that
\psi\circ\delta=\text{id}_{L(\lambda)_{\lambda}}
. Thus, we know

M_{\lambda}=\phi(L(\lambda)_{\lambda})\oplus\delta(L(\lambda)_{\lambda}).

Let
L^{\prime}=U(\mathfrak{q}(n)).\delta(L(\lambda)_{\lambda})
. Then

\displaystyle M=U(\mathfrak{q}(n)).M_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=U(\mathfrak{q}(n)).(\phi(L(\lambda)_{\lambda})\oplus\delta(L(\lambda)_{\lambda}))

\displaystyle\subset U(\mathfrak{q}(n))\phi(L(\lambda)_{\lambda})+U(\mathfrak{q}(n))\delta(L(\lambda)_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\phi(L(\lambda))+L^{\prime}

where in the last line we use that
\phi
is a
\mathfrak{q}(n)
-module homomorphism and
U(\mathfrak{q}(n))L(\lambda)_{\lambda}=L(\lambda)
. Thus
M=\phi(L(\lambda))+L^{\prime}
.

To show the sum is direct, observe
\phi
injective and
L(\lambda)
simple implies
\phi(L(\lambda))
is simple, so
\phi(L(\lambda))\cap L^{\prime}=0
or
\phi(L(\lambda))
. The case

\phi(L(\lambda))\cap L^{\prime}=\phi(L(\lambda))
is impossible, as

(L^{\prime}\cap\Phi(L(\lambda)))_{\lambda}=L^{\prime}_{\lambda}\cap(\Phi(L(\lambda)))_{\lambda}=0.

This shows that the sequence of
\mathfrak{q}(n)
modules splits also.
∎

If
m=0
, then
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}(L(\lambda),L(\lambda))=0
. This follows from

\dim\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}^{1}(L(\lambda),\Pi L(\lambda))\leq\dim\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{h}(n)}^{1}(L(\lambda)^{n_{\mathfrak{p}}},\Pi L(\lambda)^{n_{\mathfrak{p}}})=0,

where we used
m=0\Rightarrow\mathfrak{l}=\mathfrak{h}
and Lemma
4.2
for the first step, and the well known fact that Clifford supermodules are semisimple when
\lambda
is nondegenerate, for the second step
[
19
]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
\lambda
is as in Theorem
4.1
and
m>0
and
v(\lambda)
is considered as a simple
\mathfrak{l}
-module, then
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}(v(\lambda),\Pi v(\lambda))={\mathbf{C}}

and
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}(v(\lambda),v(\lambda))=0
if
v(\lambda)
is of Type M.

Proof.

We start with general observation.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{g}=A\oplus B
, where
A,B
are Lie superalgebras and
M=M_{A}\boxtimes M_{B}
is a
\mathfrak{g}
-supermodule. Then

\displaystyle H^{1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{0};M)=H^{1}(A,A_{0};M_{A})\boxtimes H^{0}(B,B_{0};M_{B})\oplus H^{1}(A,A_{0};\Pi M_{A})\boxtimes H^{0}(B,B_{0};\Pi M_{B})

\displaystyle\hphantom{H^1($\mathfrak{g}$,$\mathfrak{g}$_0; M) =}{}\oplus H^{0}(A,A_{0};M_{A})\boxtimes H^{1}(B,B_{0};M_{B})

\displaystyle\hphantom{H^1($\mathfrak{g}$,$\mathfrak{g}$_0; M) =}{}\oplus H^{1}(A,A_{0};\Pi M_{A})\boxtimes H^{1}(B,B_{0};\Pi M_{B}).

Now write
v(\lambda)=\mathbf{C}\boxtimes v(\lambda^{\prime})
for
\mathfrak{l}=\mathfrak{q}(m)\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
.
Then since
v(\lambda^{\prime})
is a projective
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
-module we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}(v(\lambda),\Pi v(\lambda))=\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}(m)}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{C})\otimes\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}}(v(\lambda^{\prime}),\Pi v(\lambda^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\hphantom{\operatorname{Ext}^1_{\mathfrak{l}}(v(\lambda),\Pi v(\lambda))=}{}\oplus\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}(m)}(\mathbf{C},\Pi\mathbf{C})\otimes\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}}(v(\lambda^{\prime}),v(\lambda^{\prime}))

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}(v(\lambda),v(\lambda))=\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}(m)}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{C})\otimes\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}}(v(\lambda^{\prime}),v(\lambda^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\hphantom{\operatorname{Ext}^1_{\mathfrak{l}}(v(\lambda),v(\lambda))=}{}\oplus\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}(m)}(\mathbf{C},\Pi\mathbf{C})\otimes\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}}(v(\lambda^{\prime}),\Pi v(\lambda^{\prime})).

By Theorem
3.1
we have
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}(m)}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{C})=0
and
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}(m)}(\mathbf{C},\Pi\mathbf{C})=\mathbf{C}
. The lemma follows.
∎

By Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
we have that

\dim\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}(L(\lambda),\Pi L(\lambda))\leq 1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}(L(\lambda),L(\lambda))=0

if
L(\lambda)
is not isomorphic to
\Pi L(\lambda)
.

It remains to show that there exists a non-trivial extension between
L(\lambda)
and
\Pi L(\lambda)
. For this we consider an indecomposable
(1|1)
-dimensional
q(n)
-module
U
with a basis

u\in U_{\bar{0}}
,
\bar{u}\in U_{\bar{1}}
and with action given by

X\bar{u}=0,\qquad Xu=\operatorname{otr}X\bar{u},\qquad\forall\,\ X\in\mathfrak{q}(n).

Then we have an exact sequence of
\mathfrak{q}(n)
-modules

0\to\Pi L(\lambda)\to L(\lambda)\otimes U\to L(\lambda)\to 0.

To see that it does not split take
p
such that
\lambda_{p}=0
. On the weight space
(L(\lambda)\otimes U)_{\lambda}
the odd basis element
\overline{H}_{p}
acts non-trivially while
its action on
(L(\lambda)\oplus\Pi L(\lambda))_{\lambda}
is obviously trivial.
The proof of Theorem
4.1
is complete.
∎

4.2
Self extensions for
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k},0,\dots,0,-\lambda_{k+m+1},\dots,-\lambda_{n})
such that all
\lambda_{i}>0
be a dominant integral weight in
\mathfrak{q}(n)
.

1.

\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}^{1}(L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda),L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda))=0
if
L(\lambda)\neq\Pi L(\lambda)
;

2.

\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}^{1}(L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda),\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda))=\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}^{1}(L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda),L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda))=0

if
m>0
or
m=0
and
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\neq 0
;

3.

\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}^{1}(L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda),\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda))={\mathbf{C}}
if
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}=0
.

Proof.

Note that Lemma
4.2
can be generalized to the case of
\mathfrak{sq}(n)
, namely if
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{p}\cap\mathfrak{sq}(n)
,
\mathfrak{l}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{l}\cap\mathfrak{sq}(n)
and
v^{\prime}(\lambda)
is the irreducible
\mathfrak{l}^{\prime}
-module, the map

\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}^{1}(L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda),(\Pi)L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda))\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{l}^{\prime}}^{1}(v^{\prime}(\lambda),(\Pi)v^{\prime}(\lambda))

is injective.
We claim that
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{l}^{\prime}}^{1}(v^{\prime}(\lambda),(\Pi)v^{\prime}(\lambda))=0
for all
\lambda
which do not satisfy (3). Indeed, if
m=0
and
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\neq 0
,
K^{\prime}_{\lambda}=0
(see Section
2.3
) and hence

v^{\prime}(\lambda)
is projective. If
m>0
, then
\mathfrak{sq}(m)
is an ideal in
\mathfrak{l}^{\prime}
which acts trivially on
v^{\prime}(\lambda)
and
\Pi v^{\prime}(\lambda)
and the quotients
\mathfrak{l}^{\prime}/\mathfrak{sq}(m)\cong\mathfrak{l}/\mathfrak{q}(m)\cong\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
. Since

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{sq}(m)}({\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{C}})=\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{sq}(m)}({\mathbf{C}},\Pi{\mathbf{C}})=0,

using spectral sequence we get

H^{1}(\mathfrak{l}^{\prime},\mathfrak{l}^{\prime}_{\bar{0}},v^{\prime}(\lambda)^{*}\otimes(\Pi)v^{\prime}(\lambda))\cong H^{1}(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime},\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}_{\bar{0}},v^{\prime}(\lambda)^{*}\otimes(\Pi)v^{\prime}(\lambda))=0.

It remains to consider the case
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}=0
. In this case one-dimensional
K^{\prime}_{\lambda}
lies in the radical of the corresponding Clifford algebra, therefore we have

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{l}^{\prime}}(v^{\prime}(\lambda),\Pi v^{\prime}(\lambda))={\mathbf{C}}.

To construct a non-trivial extension over
\mathfrak{sq}(n)
consider the induced module
\mathrm{Ind}^{\mathfrak{q}(n)}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda)
isomorphic to
L(\lambda)=L_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}(\lambda)
. It is the middle term of an exact sequence of
\mathfrak{sq}(n)
-modules

0\to L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda)\xrightarrow{\varphi}L(\lambda)\to\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda)\to 0.

Let us check that the sequence does not split. Using Frobenius reciprocity, we compute

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda),L(\lambda))=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}(\Pi L(\lambda),L(\lambda)).

If
n
is odd, then
L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda)
is isomorphic to
\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda)
and
L(\lambda)
is isomorphic to
\Pi L(\lambda)
as a
\mathfrak{q}(n)
-module. So

{\mathbf{C}}\varphi=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda),L(\lambda))=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}(\Pi L(\lambda),L(\lambda)).

If
n
is even then
L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda)
is not isomorphic to
\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda)
and
L(\lambda)
is not isomorphic to
\Pi L(\lambda)
as an
\mathfrak{q}(n)
-module.
Therefore

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}(n)}(\lambda),L(\lambda))=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}(\Pi L(\lambda),L(\lambda))=0.

In both cases, the sequence does not split.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
and let
\operatorname{Res}
,
\operatorname{Ind}
denote
\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{sq}}^{\mathfrak{q}}
,
\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{sq}}^{\mathfrak{q}}
respectively.

(a)

If there exists
i
such that
\lambda_{i}=0
, then

\operatorname{Res}L(\lambda)=L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda),\qquad\operatorname{Ind}L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)=\frac{L(\lambda)}{\Pi L(\lambda)}.

(b)

If all
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
and
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\neq 0
, then

\operatorname{Res}L(\lambda)=L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)\oplus\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda),\qquad\operatorname{Ind}L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)=L(\lambda).

(c)

If all
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
and
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}=0
, then

\operatorname{Res}L(\lambda)=\frac{\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)}{L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)},\qquad\operatorname{Ind}L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)=L(\lambda).

Proof.

By PBW theorem for
\mathfrak{q}(n)
,
\mathfrak{sq}(n)
, given a finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{sq}(n)
module
M
,
\dim\operatorname{Res}\operatorname{Ind}M=2\dim M
. Suppose we are in case (a). By Lemma
2.1
(a),
\operatorname{Res}L(\lambda)=L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)
and
\operatorname{Ind}L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)
is the middle term of the exact sequence of
\mathfrak{q}(n)
modules

0\rightarrow L(\lambda)\xrightarrow{\phi}\operatorname{Ind}L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)\rightarrow\Pi L(\lambda)\rightarrow 0.

Now, since there some
\lambda_{i}=0
,
L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)\cong\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)
if and only if
L(\lambda)\cong\Pi L(\lambda)
. Then repeating the argument from Theorem
4.5
, we conclude the sequence is nonsplit. For case (b), note that Lemma
2.1
(b) implies
L(\lambda)\cong\Pi L(\lambda)
if and only if
L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)\not\cong\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)
, and Theorem
4.5
implies there is no self extension
\frac{L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)}{\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)}
. Finally, case (c) was done in Theorem
4.5
.
∎

5
Standard block

In this section, we compute the Ext-quiver for the standard block of
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(3)
and
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sq}(3)
.

5.1
Induction and restriction functors

Our goal is to establish a connection between the standard block
\mathcal{F}^{n}_{(1,0,\dots,0)}
and the principal block
\mathcal{F}^{n-1}_{(0)}
. As a first step we use the geometric induction in the case when it is an exact functor.

Consider the parabolic subalgebra

\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{2\leq i\leq n}\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{i}}\oplus\bigoplus_{2\leq i\neq j\leq n}\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}}.

Its Levi subalgebra
\mathfrak{l}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{q}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{q}(n-1)
. Let

n_{\mathfrak{p}}=\bigoplus_{2\leq i\leq n}\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{i}}

denote the nilpotent radical of
\mathfrak{p}
.

Let
t
be a positive integer. A dominant integral weight
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
of
\mathfrak{q}(n)
is called
t
-admissible
if
\lambda=(t,\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n})
such that
t+\lambda_{i}\neq 0
, in other words the first mark
of
\lambda
is
t
and
\lambda
is
\mathfrak{p}
-typical.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{l}}(t)
denote the category of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{l}
-modules on which
H_{1}
acts by
t
and all weights of
\mathfrak{q}(n-1)
have integral marks strictly less than
t
. Let
\mathcal{F}^{n}(t)
denote the Serre
subcategory of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
generated
by
L(\lambda)
for all
t
-admissible
\lambda
. Define the functors

\Gamma^{t}\colon\ \mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{l}}(t)\to\mathcal{F}^{n}(t),\qquad R^{t}\colon\ \mathcal{F}^{n}(t)\to\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{l}}(t)

by

\Gamma^{t}(M):=\Gamma_{0}(G/P,M),\qquad R^{t}:=\operatorname{Ker}(H_{1}-t).

Proposition 5.1
.

The functors
\Gamma^{t}
and
R^{t}
define an equivalence between
\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{l}}(t)
and
\mathcal{F}^{n}(t)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.4
,
\Gamma_{i}(G/P,M)=0
for
i>0
and every
M\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{l}}(t)
. Furthermore,
\Gamma_{0}(G/P,M)
is simple if
M
is simple. On the other hand,
R^{t}(N)=H^{0}(n_{\mathfrak{p}},N)
for any
N\in\mathcal{F}^{n}(t)
.
That implies
\Gamma^{t}
is left adjoint to
R^{t}
, both functors are exact and establish bijection on the sets of isomorphism classes of simple objects in both categories. Hence these functors provide an equivalence between the
two categories.
∎

5.2
Reduction to
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{q}(n-1)}

Note that every module in
\mathcal{F}_{l}(t)
is of the form
L_{\mathfrak{q}(1)}\boxtimes M
for some
M\in\mathcal{F}^{n-1}
.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k},1,0,\dots,0,-\lambda_{k},\dots,-\lambda_{1})
,
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k^{\prime}},1,0,\dots,0,\linebreak-\mu_{k^{\prime}},\dots,-\mu_{1})
be
\mathfrak{q}(n)
dominant weights in the standard block. For
t>>\max\{\lambda_{1},\mu_{1}\}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}^{1}(L(\lambda),L(\mu))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{q}(n)}^{1}\big(L\big(t,\tilde{\lambda}\big),L(t,\tilde{\mu})\big)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{q}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{q}(n-1)}^{1}\big(L(t)\boxtimes L\big(\tilde{\lambda}\big),L(t)\boxtimes L(\tilde{\mu})\big)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{q}(n-1)}^{1}\big(L\big(\tilde{\lambda}\big),L(\tilde{\mu})\big)\oplus\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathfrak{q}(n-1)}^{1}\big(L\big(\tilde{\lambda}\big),\Pi L(\tilde{\mu})\big),

where
\tilde{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1}-1,\dots,\lambda_{k}-1,0,\dots,0,1-\lambda_{k},\dots,1-\lambda_{1})
and
\tilde{\mu}=(\mu_{1}-1,\dots,\mu_{k^{\prime}}-1,0,\dots,0,\allowbreak 1-\mu_{k^{\prime}},\dots,1-\mu_{1})
. If
k=0
, then
\lambda=(1,0,\dots,0)
and
\tilde{\lambda}=0
, and similarly for
k^{\prime}=0
.

Proof.

The first equality follows from
[
24
, Lemma 5.12]
, which shows there is an equivalence of categories between
\mathcal{F}^{n}_{(1,0,\dots,0)}
(“standard block”) and
\mathcal{F}^{n}_{(t,0,\dots,0)}
given by a
composition of translation functors. Under this equivalence,
L(\lambda)
maps to
L\big(t,\tilde{\lambda}\big)
. The second equality follows from Proposition
5.1
. The last equality follows from Lemma
4.4
.
∎

Now, using the
\mathfrak{q}(2)
Ext quiver in
[
18
, Theorem 27]
, this corollary computes all extensions occurring in Theorem
2.8
(4).

Remark 5.3
.

In the standard block, the
\mathfrak{sq}(3)
extensions are the same as the
\mathfrak{q}(3)
extensions. Indeed, lemma
4.6
shows if
\lambda=(a,1,-a)
,
\mu=(b,1,-b)
, then
\operatorname{Res}L(\lambda)=L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)\oplus\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)
and
\operatorname{Ind}L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)=L(\lambda)
. Now Shapiro’s lemma implies

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\operatorname{Ind}L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda),L(\mu))=\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{sq}}(L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda),L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\mu)\oplus\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\mu)),\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\operatorname{Ind}\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda),L(\mu))=\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda),L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\mu)\oplus\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\mu)).

Since
L(\lambda)=\Pi L(\lambda)
, we have
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}}(L(\lambda),L(\mu))=\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{sq}}(L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda),L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\mu))
. Also
\operatorname{Res}L(1,0,0)=L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(1,0,0)
, hence
\operatorname{Ind}L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)=\operatorname{Ind}\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)
implies by Shapiro’s lemma

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{q}}(L(\lambda),L(1,0,0))=\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{sq}}(L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda),L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(1,0,0))=\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda),L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(1,0,0)).

5.3
Relations

5.3.1
Relations for
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sq}(3)}

All irreducible modules and projective covers considered here will be for
\mathfrak{sq}(3)
, and we will omit the subscripts from
L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)
and
P_{\mathfrak{sq}}(\lambda)
. If
\lambda\neq(1,0,0)
,
\mu=(a,1,-a),a>1
, then
[P(\lambda):\mathcal{E}(\mu)]=[\mathcal{E}(\mu):L(\lambda)]
by Theorem
3.9
. We note
L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(a,1,-a)\neq\Pi L_{\mathfrak{sq}}(a,1,-a)
. If
\lambda=(1,0,0)
, then
[P(\lambda):\mathcal{E}(\mu)]=2[\mathcal{E}(\mu):L(\lambda)]
.
Now, the character formula for
L(\lambda):\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})\in\Lambda^{+}
is shown in
[
22
]
to equal the generic character formula for all
\lambda\neq(1,0,0)
. This combined with character formula for
\mathcal{E}(\lambda)
(
