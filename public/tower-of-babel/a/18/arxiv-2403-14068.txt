Characterizing the fourth-moment phenomenon of monochromatic subgraph counts via influences
Nitya Mani, Dan Mikulincer

Abstract
. We investigate the distribution of monochromatic subgraph counts in random vertex 2 2 -colorings of large graphs. We give sufficient conditions for the asymptotic normality of these counts and demonstrate their essential necessity (particularly for monochromatic triangles). Our approach refines the fourth-moment theorem to establish new, local influence-based conditions for asymptotic normality; these findings more generally provide insight into fourth-moment phenomena for a broader class of Rademacher and Gaussian polynomials.



1.
Introduction

The study of local properties of large-scale graphs plays a pivotal role in computer science, statistics, and probability. In some cases, such local properties allow us to infer information about the global structure of the associated graph
[
21
]
. In other cases, restricting to neighborhoods can reduce the complexity of statistical or computational tasks over the entire graphs
[
4
]
. In this work, we focus on the task of subgraph counting and study the distribution of monochromatic subgraphs for random vertex colorings.

Consider a sequence of simple undirected graphs
G_{n}=(V(G_{n}),E(G_{n}))
, with vertex sets
V(G_{n})=\{1,2,\ldots,v(G_{n})\}
and edge sets
E(G_{n})
. Further, let
H=(V(H),E(H))
be some connected reference graph. Our object of study is the
monochromatic subgraph count
statistic
T(H,G_{n})
; the number of monochromatic copies of
H
in
G_{n}
with a random vertex coloring. That is, for some
c\in\mathbb{N}
, independently color each vertex
v\in V(G_{n})
uniformly at random, using
c
colors. We say that a copy of
H
in
G_{n}
is
monochromatic
if all of its vertices have the same color. The statistic
T(H,G_{n})
is a sum of simple indicator random variables, and, depending on the graph structure, these indicators may appear independent, leading to a central limit theorem. We aim to make this intuition precise and establish simple (largely necessary and sufficient) conditions on the underlying graph sequence
G_{n}
that imply the asymptotic normality of the statistic
T(H,G_{n})
. (Other settings, where the limiting distribution is typically not Gaussian, e.g. the Poisson regime or dense
G_{n}
regime, have also been studied
[
8
,
7
,
2
,
5
,
10
]
).

As a motivating example, consider the case where
H=K_{2}
, i.e.
H
is a single edge. For a random coloring of
V(G_{n})
,
T(K_{2},G_{n})
counts the number of monochromatic edges in
G_{n}
. If we envision
G_{n}
as describing say, a social network, then
T(K_{2},G_{n})
counts the number of pairs of friends in
G_{n}
that have the same color, thus generalizing the classical birthday paradox. Such generalizations were previously studied in a variety of works
[
2
,
1
,
12
,
15
,
16
,
20
,
19
,
18
]
.
The asymptotic distribution of
T(K_{2},G_{n})
exhibits various universality phenomena. In particular, using the method of moments, Bhattacharya, Diaconis, and Mukherjee
[
5
]
concluded that
T(K_{2},G_{n})
is asymptotically normal whenever
e(G_{n})\to\infty
and the fourth moment of a suitably normalized version of
T(K_{2},G_{n})
converges to
3
(the fourth moment of the normal distribution).

The principle that a central limit theorem for non-linear functions of random fields can arise as a consequence of convergence of the corresponding sequence of fourth moments arises in a variety of problems. The celebrated
fourth-moment phenomenon
was first observed for Wiener-Itô stochastic integrals in
[
29
]
, and
[
25
]
later proved a quantitative version. In subsequent years, the fourth-moment approach has appeared as a governing principle in the field of normal approximations. In particular, it has proven widely influential in proving central limit theorems in the Wiener space

[

.

Returning to the problem of monochromatic subgraph counts, beyond edges, the fourth-moment phenomenon was observed for monochromatic triangles
[
6
]
when

c\geq 5
, and for general monochromatic subgraphs
H
when
c\geq 30

[

]
. Thus, when
c\geq 30
, in order for
T(H,G_{n})
to be asymptotically normal, it is necessary and sufficient that
\mathbb{E}\left[T(H,G_{n})^{4}\right]
converges to an appropriate quantity. This idea has continued to be generalized in
[
9
]
.

It is tempting to expect that sufficiency of the fourth-moment condition continues to hold with a small number of colors. However, in the aforementioned results
[
6
,
14
]
, some lower bound on the number of colors turns out to be
necessary
for the validity of the fourth-moment phenomenon. The necessity can be seen in
[
6
, Section 4.2]
which provides examples of families of graphs
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where the fourth moment of a suitably normalized version of
T(H,G_{n})
(using
2
colors) converges to
3
, but
T(H,G_{n})
can be readily seen to not be asymptotically normal. These examples raise the following natural question:

Suppose that the vertices of
G_{n}
are colored with a small number of colors. Can one find a necessary and sufficient condition on
G_{n}
to ensure the asymptotic normality of
T(H,G_{n})
?

It is precisely this question that we tackle, focusing on
c=2
, the smallest non-trivial number of colors.
At the technical level, we refine and leverage the machinery of the fourth-moment theorem and adapt it to polynomial functionals of Bernoulli variables. This refinement reveals a new type of easy-to-verify
local
properties of
G_{n}
. Combining these properties with established
global
moment considerations allows us to distill the above-mentioned
failures
of the fourth-moment phenomenon for
T(H,G_{n})
. Our main contributions are as follows.

(1)

For triangles (
H=\triangle
), we completely characterize the asymptotic normality of
T(\triangle,G_{n})
. We establish a new
influence
condition that together with the fourth-moment condition determines whether
T(\triangle,G_{n})
is approximately normal. Our new condition is easy to check, is combinatorial in nature, and is inspired by the notion of influences from Boolean analysis. Informally, it requires that a single edge cannot appear in too many different triangles.

(2)

In the general setting, when
H
is not a triangle, we show that the influence condition remains sufficient and conjecture that it is also necessary. We provide some evidence for this conjecture by relaxing the influence condition.

(3)

We also prove new quantitative convergence rates for the normalized version of
T(H,G_{n})
. When the influences of the graph are small, our rates turn out to be faster than the known results in the literature. Our result is rather general and applies to other related problems, such as monochromatic hypergraph counts and edge colorings.

(4)

Finally, as a technical tool that may be of independent interest, we extend the fourth-moment phenomenon to a broader class of Rademacher and Gaussian polynomials, which do not necessarily belong to a single chaos.

1.1.
Setup and Main Results

Given a subgraph
H
and a sequence of graphs
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we shall henceforth use
T(H,G_{n})
to denote the number of monochromatic copies of
H
in
G_{n}
when we
2
-color the vertices
v\in V(G_{n})
, uniformly at random, with independent colors
X_{v}\sim\Rad(\frac{1}{2})
2
2

2

Here
\Rad(\frac{1}{2})
stands for the Rademacher distribution, i.e. the uniform distribution on
\{-1,1\}
.
. More formally, we have:

(1.1)

\displaystyle T(H,G_{n}):=\frac{1}{|\Aut(H)|}\sum_{\mathbf{s}\in V(G_{n})_{V(H)}}\mathbf{1}\{X_{=\mathbf{s}}\}\underbrace{\prod_{(i,j)\in E(H)}a_{s_{i}s_{j}}(G_{n})}_{=:a_{H,\mathbf{s}}(G_{n})},

where

–

V(G_{n})_{r}:=\{\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\in V(G_{n})^{r}:s_{i}\text{ distinct}\}
.

–

\Aut(H)

H

\sigma

(i,j)\in E(H)
if and only if
(\sigma(i),\sigma(j))\in E(H)
.

–

We let
A(G_{n})=(a_{ij}(G_{n}))_{i,j\in V(G_{n})}
be the adjacency matrix of
G_{n}
, so that
a_{ij}=\mathbf{1}\{(i,j)\in E(G_{n})\}

–

\mathbf{1}\{X_{=\mathbf{s}}\}:=\mathbf{1}\{X_{s_{1}}=\cdots=X_{s_{r}}\}
is the indicator variable that all of the vertices of
\mathbf{s}\in V(G_{n})_{r}
have the same color.

Throughout, we assume
H
is simple and connected and that
v(H)=r\geq 2
. We denote the centered and rescaled version of
T(H,G_{n})
by

(1.2)

\displaystyle Z(H,G_{n}):=\frac{T(H,G_{n})-\mathbb{E}[T(H,G_{n})]}{\sigma_{H}(G_{n})},\quad\sigma_{H}(G_{n}):=\sqrt{\Var[T(H,G_{n})]}.

We also let

(1.3)

\displaystyle N(H,G_{n}):=\frac{1}{|\Aut(H)|}\sum_{s\in V(G_{n})_{r}}\prod_{(i,j)\in E(H)}a_{s_{i}s_{j}}(G_{n}),

be the number of copies of
H
in
G_{n}.

The following definition will be instrumental in characterizing the asymptotic distribution of
Z(H,G_{n})
.

Definition 1.1

(Influential vertices and edges)
.

Given an integer
k\in[r]
and
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{k})\in V(G_{n})_{k}
(ordered) let

D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G_{n})
be the number of copies of
H
in
G_{n}
that include vertices
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}
.
More precisely,

(1.4)

\displaystyle D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G_{n}):=\frac{1}{\Aut(H)}\sum_{\mathbf{s}\in V(G_{n})_{r}:\mathbf{s}\supset\mathbf{w}}a_{H,\mathbf{s}}(G_{n}).

We say that vertex
w\in V(G_{n})
is an
\varepsilon
-influential vertex
if

D_{w}(H,G_{n})\geq\varepsilon\sigma_{H}(G_{n})
. We say that a pair of vertices
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},w_{2})\in V(G_{n})_{2}
is
\varepsilon
-influential pair
if
D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G_{n})\geq\varepsilon\sigma_{H}(G_{n})
. If
e=\{w_{1},w_{2}\}\in E(G_{n})
, we will sometimes also call
\mathbf{w}
an
\varepsilon
-influential edge
and denote its influence as
D_{e}(H,G_{n})
.

When
\varepsilon
can be taken to a be constant in
n
, we typically omit the “
\varepsilon
” and simply refer to collections of vertices as
influential
. As we shall show in
Lemma

2.3
,
1.1
is intimately related to the notion of influence from Boolean analysis
[
30
, Chapter 2.2]
. This notion also bears similarity to the
local counts
used in
[
4
]
and a similar notion studied in
[
27
]
(discussed further in
Section

1.2
).

Quantitative central limit theorems under a low-influence condition.

Our main idea is to compare
Z(H,G_{n})
(which turns out to be a polynomial in Rademacher variables) to a polynomial in Gaussian variables. In some sense, we replace the
2
-coloring of
G_{n}
with a continuous Gaussian “coloring”. This comparison, facilitated by the
Invariance Principle
of
[
23
]
, allows us to invoke known results about central limit theorems for polynomials in Gaussian spaces. In particular, we shall rely on the fourth-moment theorem of Nualart and Peccati
[
29
]
.

The first hurdle we encounter is that known results in Gaussian spaces, such as the ones in
[
29
]
, mostly revolve around polynomials belonging to a Wiener chaos, i.e. Hermite polynomials. Since
Z(H,G_{n})
is not a Hermite polynomial in general, we begin by extending the fourth-moment theorem to certain families of multi-linear polynomials that do not necessarily belong to a single Wiener chaos. For polynomials belonging to this family, we generalize the quantitative version of the fourth moment theorem given in
[
24
]
to prove
3.2
, giving a necessary condition on such polynomials to ensure that the fourth-moment theorem holds.

Among other examples,
3.2
holds for multi-linear polynomials with positive coefficients. Since
Z(H,G_{n})
is such a polynomial, this allows us to obtain our main quantitative convergence theorem,
3.1
, which bounds the
W_{1}
and
d_{2}
distances (defined in
Eqs.

2.1
and
2.3
) between
Z(H,G)
and a standard Gaussian in terms of the fourth-moment discrepancy
\mathbb{E}[Z(H,G_{n})^{4}]-3
and the maximum influence of a vertex
\max_{w\in V(G_{n})}D_{w}(H,G_{n}).

Previous work establishing a fourth-moment theorem
Z(H,G_{n})
(for
H=\triangle
in
[
6
]
and for general
H
in
[
14
]
) used martingale central limit theorems to derive convergence rates in the
Kolmogorov distance
:

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(H,G),\mathcal{Z}):=\sup\limits_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(Z(H,G)\leq t\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{Z}\leq t\right)\right|,

where
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
is a standard Gaussian.
Since
d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(H,G),\mathcal{Z})
can be bounded in terms of
W_{1}
or
d_{2}
, informally, when there are no influential vertices, we have the following consequence of
3.1
.

Theorem 1.2

(Informal)
.

Let
\{G_{n}\}
be a sequence of graphs with no influential vertices (asymptotically) and let
H
be a fixed connected simple graph. Then,

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(H,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})\lesssim\min\left((\mathbb{E}\left[Z(H,G_{n})^{4}\right]-3)^{\frac{1}{12}},\max\limits_{w\in V(G_{n})}D_{w}(H,G_{n})(\mathbb{E}\left[Z(H,G_{n})^{4}\right]-3)^{\frac{1}{8}}\right).

Moreover, if
H=\triangle
is a triangle, we obtain

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(\triangle,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})\lesssim(\mathbb{E}\left[Z(\triangle,G_{n})^{4}\right]-3)^{\frac{1}{8}}.

Remark
.

1.2
is based on a rather general convergence result for polynomials in Rademacher random variables. Thus the obtained bounds also apply, mutatis mutandis, to similar problems. For example, a similar bound also holds if the colors are not chosen uniformly, or for variants in which we consider an edge coloring of
G_{n}
, rather than a vertex coloring, or when allowing
G_{n}
to be a hypergraph. In another neighboring problem, related to motif estimation
[
4
]
, one asks to count the number of monochromatic subgraphs of a
given
color. Up to the change in the definition of
Z(H,G_{n})
, the bound in
1.2
applies to this version verbatim. We further elaborate on this problem in
Section

1.2
.

In the setting of
2
-coloring graphs with no influential vertices, we are able to bypass the difficulties encountered by the previous martingale arguments. Our result improves on the rates obtained in
[
14
]
, where the authors achieved a convergence rate of
\approx(\mathbb{E}\left[Z(H,G)^{4}\right]-3)^{\frac{1}{20}}
. To our knowledge, these are the best-known rates in this setting. Notably,
1.2
offers a substantial improvement when
H=\triangle
, arguably one of the more fundamental cases. Furthermore, as will be evident from
3.1
, our rates further improve when considering the
W_{1}
and
d_{2}
metrics.

Characterizing the asymptotic normality of monochromatic triangles counts with pairwise influences.

In the above result, we impose the sufficient condition that
\{G_{n}\}
lacks influential vertices. There are numerous examples of interest satisfying this condition, such as vertex-transitive graphs, some sparse graphs, and certain types of random graphs. However, a moment of thought will reveal that this condition is not necessary. For example, take
G_{n}
to be a star on
n
vertices, and
H=K_{2}
an edge. Then, as a sum of independent variables
Z(K_{2},G_{n})\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, yet the middle vertex is clearly influential. More generally, if each
G_{n}
has a single influential vertex, by the symmetry of the colors (and conditioning on the color of
v
,
X_{v}
), one may see that
1.2
continues to hold for
Z(H,G_{n})
. Keeping this in mind, one might expect that if all influential vertices are “sufficiently far away from each other” (and so do not interact in fourth-moment calculations), then the fourth-moment theorem should continue to hold. This suggests that the true obstruction of a fourth moment theorem might be having subsets of nearby vertices
\mathbf{w}
that are as a collection, influential.

In our next result, we apply this argument to triangles, when
H=\triangle
. In this case, we strengthen
3.1
by showing that a
necessary and sufficient
condition for the fourth moment theorem to hold is that
G_{n}
lacks
influential edges
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider a sequence of graphs
\{G_{n}\}
with
\mathcal{I}_{n}
the corresponding sets of influential vertices, and let
\sigma_{\triangle}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(T(\triangle,G_{n}))}.
Suppose that
\sup_{n}|\mathcal{I}_{n}|<\infty
and
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
is a standard Gaussian.

(1)

If for every
n
, there exists
e\in E(G_{n})
such that

\lim\sup_{n\to\infty}\frac{D_{e}(\triangle,G_{n})}{\sigma_{\triangle}}>0

Then,

\lim\sup_{n\to\infty}d(Z(\triangle,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})>0.

(2)

Conversely, let
M_{n}:=\max\limits_{e\in E(G_{n})}\frac{D_{e}(\triangle,G_{n})}{\sigma_{\triangle}}.
Then, for
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
,

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(\triangle,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})\lesssim\left(M_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}+(\mathbb{E}\left[Z(\triangle,G_{n})^{4}\right]-3)\right)^{\frac{1}{5}}.

Putting
1.3
in words, as long as the number of influential vertices is bounded uniformly over
n
, for
Z(\triangle,G_{n})
to be asymptotically normal, it is
necessary and sufficient
that
G_{n}
contains no influential edges and that
\mathbb{E}\left[Z(\triangle,G_{n})^{4}\right]\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}3.
Thus,
1.3
gives a complete characterization of sequences
\{G_{n}\}
for which the monochromatic triangle count is approximately normal. We remark that
1.3
includes the additional condition of having a bounded number of influential vertices. We regard this as a technical condition that seems to be an artifact of the proof of necessity.

Towards a characterization of general monochromatic subgraph counts.

Regarding more general subgraph counts, we establish sufficiency of having no influential pairs. Since every vertex participating in an influential pair is necessarily an influential vertex, this condition is weaker than the condition imposed by
1.2
, thus yielding a stronger result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Consider a sequence of graphs
\{G_{n}\}
, a graph
H
, and let
\sigma_{H}=\sigma_{H}(G_{n}).
If
M_{n}:=\max\limits_{\mathbf{w}\in V(G_{n})_{2}}\frac{D_{\mathbf{w}}(\triangle,G_{n})}{\sigma_{H}},
then, for
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
,

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(H,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})\lesssim\left(M_{n}^{2}+(\mathbb{E}\left[Z(H,G_{n})^{4}\right]-3)\right)^{\frac{1}{20}}.

We now discuss possible generalizations of the first part of
1.3
. Our proof of the necessity condition for triangles proceeds by representing
Z(\triangle,G_{n})
as a quadratic polynomial in Rademacher variables. Such a quadratic representation is absent for general
H
and so there is no way to generalize the proof verbatim. However, we conjecture that some adaptation to polynomials of higher degree should exist and that the above phenomenon holds more generally:

Conjecture 1.5
.

Given a fixed graph
H
and graph sequence
\{G_{n}\}
, if for all
N
, there exists some
n>N
such that
G_{n}
has an influential pair of vertices
\mathbf{w}=(v,w)\in V(G_{n})_{2}
, then
Z(H,G_{n})\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 14.2914pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 14.2914pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 9.9135pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 9.9135pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}}}}\mathscr{N}(0,1)
.

We believe that a careful case analysis of possible tail behaviors of polynomials in Rademacher variables is the key to proving
1.5
. While such an analysis seems to be beyond the scope of this work, we provide some evidence for its validity. In
5.8
we show that the conjecture holds with respect to a stronger notion of influence. In this stronger notion, we ask that a single pair of vertices be incident to a constant fraction of the copies of
H
in
G_{n}
.

One possible approach to prove
1.5
is to establish some discrepancy between lower-order moments of
Z(H,G_{n})
and the standard Gaussian. However, the counterexamples to the fourth-moment phenomenon in
[
6
]
are all graphs with a single influential pair, where
\mathbb{E}\left[Z(\triangle,G_{n})^{4}\right]=3
, in line with
1.3
. In
Section

5.3
we show that trying to look at higher fixed moments is likely also doomed to fail. We provide an explicit construction of a graph containing an influential edge, such that
Z(\triangle,G_{n})
matches the first
6
moments of the standard Gaussian but where
Z(\triangle,G_{n})
is not asymptotically Gaussian. The implication here is that a solution to
1.5
should probably look at more refined statistics of
Z(H,G_{n})
which capture the actual behavior of the tails.

There is another natural approach to try which leads to a very natural question. When
c\geq 30
, using the results from
[
4
]
, it is not hard to show that the existence of an influential pair implies
\mathbb{E}\left[Z(H,G_{n})^{4}\right]-3>\varepsilon>0
. Hence with many colors, an influential pair precludes a CLT. This begs the following question about the monotonicity of the CLT with respect to the number of colors.

Question 1.6
.

Let
c^{\prime},c\in\mathbb{N}
with
c^{\prime}>c
, and suppose that when using
c
colors,
Z(H,G_{n})\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}\mathscr{N}(0,1).
Is it still true that
Z(H,G_{n})\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}\mathscr{N}(0,1),
when the vertices of
G_{n}
are colored using
c^{\prime}
colors instead?

A positive answer to
1.6
coupled with the previous observation would provide a positive answer to
1.5
. It seems plausible that
1.6
is true; the more colors used the lower in magnitude the correlations between the different copies of
H
in
G_{n}
. Common wisdom about the CLT suggests that it should be easier to approximate
Z(H,G_{n})
by a Gaussian in this case.

1.2.
Related Works

The idea of representing
Z(H,G_{n})
as a polynomial in Rademacher variables appeared before
in
[
5
]
. In the paper, the authors observed that for monochromatic edges, the fourth-moment phenomenon for
Z(K_{2},G_{n})
could be reduced to a fourth-moment phenomenon for a homogeneous quadratic polynomial in Rademacher variables. Note, however, that in light of
1.4
, there is a qualitative difference between counting monochromatic edges and any other shape. When
H=K_{2}
, and
e(G_{n})\to\infty
, no edge is influential.

More generally, fourth-moment theorems for polynomials in Rademacher random variables were studied before in
[
17
]
(see also
[
11
]
for an earlier result). In the paper, the authors focus on
homogeneous
polynomials and employ a discrete variant of the Malliavin calculus to derive comparable bounds to our
3.1
. Notably, the maximal influence also plays an important role in their results. Using similar techniques, the paper
[
33
]
later extended the convergence result to a multivariate setting in the asymptotic regime. As our approach is different and relies on the Invariance Principle, we are able to address a somewhat more general setting and consider non-homogeneous polynomials. Not only that, by restricting our attention to polynomials induced by monochromatic subgraph counts we identify the pairwise influence condition from
1.3
and
1.4
, rather than the single variable influence, as the main quantity governing the validity of the central limit theorem.

We finish by discussing the related problem of subgraph sampling and motif estimation. In this problem, given a large graph
G
and a reference graph
H
, one wishes to estimate the
N(H,G)
. To reduce the complexity, a natural approach is to subsample the vertices of
G
with some probability
p
and obtain a new graph
\tilde{G}
. It is then a standard procedure to obtain an unbiased estimator for
N(H,G)
from
N(H,\tilde{G})
. To make the connection with the present setting, when coloring
G
with
c
colors, we take
p=\frac{1}{c}
, and
N(H,\tilde{G})
becomes the number of monochromatic copies of
H
of a
given
fixed color. Thus, the subgraph sampling statistic is an asymmetric version of the monochromatic subgraph count statistic. From a statistical point of view, for a sequence of graphs
\{G_{n}\}
, establishing the asymptotic normality of
N(H,\tilde{G_{n}})
is important for applications such as hypothesis testing.

The asymptotic normality of
N(H,\tilde{G_{n}})
was extensively studied in
[
4
]
, where the authors proved a fourth-moment theorem when
c\geq 20
(equivalently when
p\leq\frac{1}{20}
). Similar to the case of monochromatic subgraphs, this result leaves open the question of asymptotic normality for small
c
. When
c=2
, as mentioned above,
N(H,\tilde{G_{n}})
can also be represented as a polynomial in Rademacher variables, and so Theorem
3.1
applies almost verbatim. We thus get a fourth-moment theorem when
G
does not have influential vertices. In contrast to our problem, due to the asymmetric nature of
N(H,\tilde{G_{n}})
we do not expect that the existence, or lack, of an influential pair, will play an important role. As an example, consider
G_{n}
a star on
n
vertices, which has an influential vertex and no influential pairs, and
H=K_{2}
an edge. It is readily seen that
N(H,\tilde{G_{n}})
is not asymptotically normal. For
N(H,\tilde{G_{n}})
, it seems reasonable to expect that the fourth-moment phenomenon will be completely determined by the existence of influential vertices.

Preliminaries

Notation

\{G_{n}\}

G_{n}=(V(G_{n}),E(G_{n}))
having
v(G_{n})
vertices and

e(G_{n})\to\infty
and take
H
to be a simple connected graph on
r

as fixed when

.

V(G_{n})_{r}
the set of ordered
r
-tuples of distinct vertices from
G_{n}
. We take
T(H,G_{n}),Z(H,G_{n}),
and
N(H,G_{n})
as defined in
Eq.

1.1
,
Eq.

1.2
, and
Eq.

1.3
respectively.

We use
a(n)\lesssim b(n)
to denote that there is some constant
C>0
such that
a(n)\leq Cb(n)
for all
n
and define
a(n)\gtrsim b(n)
analogously.
We allow the implicit constant
C
to depend on the graph
H
and its size
r
, but not on other parameters such as
n
, or the graph
G_{n}
.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}
we set
[k]=\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
, and for a set
S
we denote by
\binom{S}{m}

m
-subsets of
S
,

\binom{S}{\leq m}

2.1.
Normal Approximations for Polynomials

Our aim in this section is to introduce some background and useful results concerning the central limit theorem for polynomial functionals. While some of the mentioned results hold in greater generality, we shall only focus on multi-linear polynomials with either Gaussian or Rademacher variables, as required in our setting. For this, we shall use the standard index notation for multi-linear polynomials.

For
I\subset[n]
an index set, and
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we set
x^{I}=\prod\limits_{i\in I}x_{i}
. Thus, every multi-linear polynomial
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of degree
d
can be written as,
F(x)=\sum\limits_{|I|\leq d}\alpha_{I}x^{I}
, where
\{\alpha_{I}\}
are some set of coefficients. If
F
is
homogeneous
of degree
d
, then
\alpha_{I}=0
whenever
|I|<d
.

The first result we present is the
fourth-moment theorem
of Nualart and Peccati
[
29
]
. In our setting, the theorem deals with a sequence
\{F_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
of homogeneous multi-linear polynomials of degree
d
and an
n
-dimensional standard Gaussian vector
\mathcal{N}
. According to the theorem, as
k\to\infty
,
F_{k}(\mathcal{N})
converges in law to a Gaussian if and only if
\mathbb{E}\left[F_{k}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\sigma^{2}
, for some
\sigma>0
, and
\mathbb{E}\left[F_{k}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}3\sigma^{4}
. The quantity
3\sigma^{4}
is precisely the
4^{\mathrm{th}}
moment of a Gaussian with variance
\sigma^{2}
. In this regard, the upshot of the theorem is the following: a priori, a central limit theorem for
F_{k}(N)
necessitates the convergence of all moments; however, as long as the variance stabilizes, it suffices for the
4^{\mathrm{th}}
moment to converge.

We shall require a quantitative and multivariate form of the fourth-moment theorem, as it appears in
[
24
]
. To state this quantitative version we first need to introduce the
Wasserstein distance
(the reader is referred to
[
32
, Chapter 6]
for an in-depth exposition of Wasserstein distances). If
X
and
Y
are two random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we define their Wasserstein distance as,

(2.1)

W_{1}(X,Y):=\inf\limits_{(X,Y)}\mathbb{E}\left[\|X-Y\|\right],

where the infimum runs over all couplings of
X
and
Y
, that is, random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2m}
whose marginals on the first (resp. last)
m
coordinates have the same law as
X
(resp.
Y
). According to the Kantrovich-Rubinstein duality, the Wasserstein distance can also be formulated as the divergence over all
1
-Lipschitz functions,

(2.2)

W_{1}(X,Y)=\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}\\
f\text{ is }1\text{-Lipschitz}\end{subarray}}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[f(Y)\right]\right|.

In light of this characterization, we shall also consider the weaker and smoother
d_{2}
distance:

d_{2}(X,Y)=\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}\\
\|f\|_{C_{2}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[f(Y)\right]\right|.

Above
\|f\|_{C_{2}}:=\max\{\|\nabla f\|_{\infty},\|\nabla^{2}f\|_{\infty}\}.
In other words,
\|f\|_{C_{2}}\leq 1
implies that
f
and
\nabla f
are
1
-Lipschitz. By (
2.2
) it is now clear that,

(2.3)

d_{2}(X,Y)\leq W_{1}(X,Y).

As will be soon evident, when dealing with random variables having small variances, it is sometimes beneficial to use the
d_{2}
distance over the Wasserstein distance. For monochromatic subgraph counts, the
d_{2}
distance will help us deal with influential shapes. A key property of both distances is that convergence in
W_{1}
, and hence also in
d_{2}
, implies convergence in law.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
24
, Theorem 1.5]
. Let
m\geq 1
, and for
i\in[m]
, let
F_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a homogeneous multi-linear polynomial of degree
d_{i}>0
, with
d_{i}\neq d_{j}
for
i\neq j
. Further let
\mathcal{N}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{m})
, and define the
m\times m
diagonal matrix

\Sigma:=\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F_{1}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right],\dots,\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]\right)
.
Taking
\widetilde{F}:=(F_{1}(\mathcal{N}),\dots,F_{m}(\mathcal{N}))\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\Sigma)
, if we let

\displaystyle\Delta(F):=

\displaystyle\Bigg[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{2d_{i}4^{d_{i}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F_{i}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\mathbb{E}\left[F_{i}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\bigg(\sqrt{2\mathbb{E}\left[F_{j}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F_{i}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\mathbb{E}\left[F_{i}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ +d_{j}2^{d_{j}}\sqrt{2(d_{i}+d_{j})!\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F_{i}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\mathbb{E}\left[F_{i}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{2}\right)}\bigg)\Bigg],

then

\displaystyle W_{1}(\widetilde{F},\widetilde{N})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|_{\op}}\cdot\|\Sigma^{-1}\|_{\op}\cdot\Delta(F),

\displaystyle d_{2}(\widetilde{F},\widetilde{N})

\displaystyle\leq\Delta(F).

Remark
.

We remark that
2.1
is more general and applies to Hermite polynomials, or iterated stochastic integrals, on the Wiener space. Since multi-linear and homogeneous polynomials are also Hermite polynomials we chose to state the theorem this way, with slightly worse but simpler constants, as best befits our needs.

The fourth moment theorem (
2.1
) deals with a Gaussian approximation of polynomials with Gaussian variables. However, in our problem,
T(H,G)
is a polynomial in Rademacher random variables. We are thus interested in studying the law of
F(X)
when
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is some multi-linear polynomial and
X\sim\mathrm{Rad}(\frac{1}{2})^{\otimes n}
is an
n
-dimensional random vector with independent Rademacher entries. As it turns out, the Gaussian is universal for this problem, in the sense that if
F(\mathcal{N})
is approximately Gaussian, then the same is also true for
F(X)
, c.f.
[
27
]
. The converse is not true in general though, and one can find a polynomial
F
, such that
F(X)
can be well approximated by a Gaussian, while
F(\mathcal{N})
cannot. It is precisely these kinds of polynomials which we shall need to handle. To distinguish between the different cases, our main tool will be the
Invariance Principle
of Mossel, O’Donnell, and Oleszkiewicz
[
23
]
.

Before stating the theorem, for a multi-linear polynomial
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
F(x)=\sum\limits_{|I|\leq d}\alpha_{I}x^{I}
, we define the
influence
of a subset
J\subseteq[n]
of variables, as

(2.4)

\mathrm{inf}_{J}(F)=\sum\limits_{J\subseteq I}\alpha_{I}^{2}.

If
J=\{i\}
is a singleton, we will also write
\mathrm{inf}_{J}(F)=\mathrm{inf}_{i}(F).
The Invariance Principle then says that as long as there are no influential variables,
F(\mathcal{N})
and
F(X)
behave roughly the same.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
30
, Corollary 11.68]

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a multi-linear polynomial of degree
d
and let
\mathcal{N}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{n})
and
X\sim\mathrm{Rad}(\frac{1}{2})^{\otimes n}
. Then, if
\mathrm{Var}(F(\mathcal{N}))=1
,

W_{1}(F(X),F(\mathcal{N}))\lesssim 2^{d}\left(\max\limits_{i\in[n]}\mathrm{inf}_{i}(F)\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

2.2.
Monochromatic Counts as Multi-linear Polynomials

We shall now show how the monochromatic count
T(H,G)
can be represented as a multi-linear polynomial.
To see this, for a fixed
connected
graph
H
on vertex set
V(H)
, and
r:=v(H)
we define the
H
-indicator polynomial,
\mathbb{I}_{H}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}
. For the definition of
\mathbb{I}_{H}
, we index the coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{r}
by
V(H)
, and write

\mathbb{I}_{H}(x):=\prod\limits_{v\in V(H)}(x_{v}+1)+(-1)^{r}\prod\limits_{v\in V(H)}(x_{v}-1)=2\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\subset V(H)\\
|U|=2i\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{u\in U}x_{u}.

The equality follows by noticing that all odd-degree monomials must vanish.

We now record several facts concerning the polynomial
\mathbb{I}_{H}
. First, it is a multi-linear polynomial of degree at most
r
, and its
m
-homogeneous component is given by

(2.5)

\mathbb{I}_{H,m}(x)=\begin{cases}2\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\subset V(H)\\
|U|=m\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{u\in U}x_{u}&m\equiv 0\pmod{2}\\
0&m\equiv 1\pmod{2}.\end{cases},

Second, it is indeed an indicator in the following sense: when restricted to
2
-colorings of
H
, encoded by
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{r}
, we have
\mathbb{I}_{H}(x)=2^{r}
if
x=\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)
or
x=-\mathbf{1}
, and
\mathbb{I}_{H}(x)=0
otherwise. Finally,
\mathbb{I}_{H}
does not depend on the actual graph structure of
H
, only on the number of vertices of
H
, and is invariant to permutations of its coordinates.
We note here that if
X\sim\mathrm{Rad}(\frac{1}{2})^{\otimes r}
, then

(2.6)

\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}_{H}(X)]=\mathbb{I}_{H,0}=2.

Example
.

Suppose that
H=\triangle
is a triangle on the vertex set
\{u,v,w\}
. In that case

(2.7)

\mathbb{I}_{\triangle}(x_{u},x_{v},x_{w})=2(x_{u}x_{v}+x_{u}x_{w}+x_{v}x_{w}+1).

It is easy to see that
\mathbb{I}_{\triangle}(\mathbf{1})=\mathbb{I}_{\triangle}(-\mathbf{1})=8
, and that
\mathbb{I}_{\triangle}(-1,1,1)=0
, with the same being true for all other
2
-colorings.

With this definition of
\mathbb{I}_{H}
, we can now rewrite (
1.1
) as,

(2.8)

T(H,G):=\frac{1}{2^{r}|\Aut(H)|}\sum_{\mathbf{s}\in V(G)_{r}}\mathbb{I}_{H}(x|_{\mathbf{s}})a_{H,\mathbf{s}}(G),

where
x|_{\mathbf{s}}=(x_{v})_{v\in\mathbf{s}}
. Equivalently, if
\mathcal{H}(G)
stands for the set of all (unlabeled) copies of
H
inside
G
and
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{v(G)}
is a
2
-coloring of
V(G)
we can also write
T(H,G)
in a more standard polynomial form as

F_{H,G}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{r}}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{H}(G)}\mathbb{I}_{H}(x|_{\mathbf{s}}).

Note that the choice of ordering in
\mathbf{s}
does not matter, since
\mathbb{I}_{H}
is invariant to permutations of its coordinates.
In light of
2.2
, our next goal is to understand the influences of
F_{H,G}(x)
. Since the entries of
x
are indexed by
V(G)
, we can identify index sets of
F_{H,G}
and subsets
\mathbf{w}\subset V(G)
and write in multi-linear form,

F_{H,G}(x)=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{w}\in{\binom{V(G)}{\leq r}}}\alpha_{\mathbf{w}}x^{\mathbf{w}}.

Let us now express
\alpha_{\mathbf{w}}
in terms of the graph structure of
G
.
Looking at (
2.5
), it is clear that when
|\mathbf{w}|
is even,
x^{\mathbf{w}}
appears in the sum precisely once for every copy of
H
in
G
that includes
\mathbf{w}
. In other words,
\alpha_{\mathbf{w}}=\frac{1}{2^{r-1}}D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G)
, as in (
1.4
). When
|\mathbf{w}|
is odd then
\alpha_{\mathbf{w}}=0
, and we obtain the expression,

(2.9)

F_{H,G}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{r-1}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{w}\in\binom{V(G)}{2i}}D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G)x^{\mathbf{w}}.

We can now make the connection between the influence of a single variable, as defined by (
2.4
), and the influence of a vertex, as in (
1.4
).

Lemma 2.3
.

With the above notation, for any
v\in V(G)
,

\mathrm{inf}_{v}(F_{H,G})\leq\frac{1}{2^{r-1}}D^{2}_{v}(H,G).

Proof.

From the representation in (
2.9
) we get,

\mathrm{inf}_{v}(F_{H,G})=\frac{1}{4^{r-1}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbf{w}\\
|\mathbf{w}|\equiv 0\text{ mod }2\end{subarray}}D^{2}_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G).

By inclusion, it is also clear that for any
\mathbf{w}\in V(G)
that contains
v
,
D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G)\leq D_{v}(H,G)
. Moreover, for any
m\leq r
,

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbf{w}\\
|\mathbf{w}|=m\end{subarray}}D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G)=\binom{r-1}{m-1}D_{v}(H,G).

The coefficient
\binom{r-1}{m-1}
counts the number of ways
v
can be extended to a subset of size
m
inside a copy of
H
. Combining the above observations,

\mathrm{inf}_{v}(F_{H,G})\leq\frac{D_{v}(H,G)}{4^{r-1}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r/2}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbf{w}\\
|\mathbf{w}|=2i\end{subarray}}D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G)=\frac{D^{2}_{v}(H,G)}{4^{r-1}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r/2}\binom{r-1}{2i-1}\leq\frac{D^{2}_{v}(H,G)}{2^{r-1}}.

∎

3.
A Fourth Moment Theorem for Monochromatic Counts

In this section, we prove our main quantitative convergence results, which show that for graphs with no influential vertices, the fourth-moment condition suffices for a central limit theorem to hold for
Z(H,G_{n})
. Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{N}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{n})
, and write
\widetilde{F}=\frac{F-\mathbb{E}\left[F[\mathcal{N}]\right]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(F(\mathcal{N}))}}
, for the normalized version of the function
F
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
H
be a fixed connected
r
-vertex graph and
G
another graph. For
Z(H,G)
defined in (
1.2
),
\mathcal{N}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{v(G)})
,
X\sim\mathrm{Rad}(\frac{1}{2})^{\otimes r}
, and
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
,
set

m_{4}(H,G):=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\right)^{\frac{1}{4}},

where
F_{H,G}
is as in (
2.9
).
Then,

\displaystyle W_{1}(Z(H,G),\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\limits_{v\in V(G)}\left[\left(\frac{D_{v}^{2}(H,G)}{{\mathrm{Var}(F_{H,G}(X))}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}+D_{v}(H,G)m_{4}(H,G)\right],

\displaystyle d_{2}(Z(H,G),\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\limits_{v\in V(G)}\left[\left(\frac{D_{v}^{2}(H,G)}{{\mathrm{Var}(F_{H,G}(X))}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}+m_{4}(H,G)\right].

Let us make some remarks concerning
3.1
. First, if a sequence of graphs
\{G_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
has no influential vertices, then, by definition,

\max\limits_{v\in V(G_{n})}\left(\frac{D_{v}^{2}(H,G_{n})}{{\mathrm{Var}(F_{H,G}(X))}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

Recall now that convergence in
d_{2}
implies convergence in distribution. Hence for
Z(H,G_{n})\xrightarrow[\text{in law}]{n\to\infty}\mathcal{Z}
is it sufficient (and also necessary) that
m_{4}(G_{n},H)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

The fourth-moment discrepancy
m_{4}
is defined in terms of the Gaussian measure. However, when there are no influential vertices, by
2.2
, one could replace the Gaussian variables with Rademacher ones, expressing the fourth moment of
Z(H,G)
and arrive asymptotically at the same expression.

The proof of
3.1
will be conducted in several steps. We shall first prove a central limit theorem for the polynomial
F_{H,G}
with Gaussian variables and then use the Invariance Principle from
2.2
to replace the Gaussian variables with Rademacher ones.

3.1.
Normal Approximation for non-homogeneous polynomials

We begin by extending the fourth-moment theorem to non-homogeneous polynomials. For a multi-linear polynomial of degree
d
,
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, given by
F(x)=\sum_{I\in{\binom{[n]}{\leq d}}}\alpha_{I}x^{I}
, denote by
F_{m}
its degree
m
homogeneous component, whenever
m\leq d
. In other words,
F_{m}(x)=\sum_{I\in\binom{[n]}{m}}\alpha_{I}x^{I}
.
We now derive a necessary condition on multi-linear polynomials to ensure that the fourth-moment theorem holds.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a degree
d
multi-linear polynomial, and for
m\leq d
, let
F_{m}
be its degree
m
homogeneous component. Further, let
\mathcal{N}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{n})
, and suppose that the following conditions are met:

•

\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{N})\right]=0
and
\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]=1
.

•

For every distinct
m,m^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime},m^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq d
, we have the following inequalities

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})F^{3}_{m^{\prime}}(\mathcal{N})\right]\geq 0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})F_{m^{\prime}}(\mathcal{N})F^{2}_{m^{\prime\prime}}(\mathcal{N})\right]\geq 0,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})F_{m^{\prime}}(\mathcal{N})F_{m^{\prime\prime}}(\mathcal{N})F_{m^{\prime\prime\prime}}(\mathcal{N})\right]\geq 0.

Then, if
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1),

\displaystyle W_{1}(F(\mathcal{N}),\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}\cdot\max\limits_{m\leq d}\mathbb{E}\left[F^{2}_{m}(\mathcal{N})\right]^{-1}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\right),

\displaystyle d_{2}(F(\mathcal{N}),\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\right),

where
C_{d}>0
depends only on
d
.

Proof.

The main idea of the proof is to use
2.1
. To utilize the theorem, we shall need to bound the fourth moment of
F(\mathcal{N})
in terms of the fourth moment of its homogeneous components. Towards this, we first note that the assumption
\mathbb{E}[F(\mathcal{N})]=0
implies
F_{0}=0
, and so,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{d}F_{m}(\mathcal{N})\right)^{4}\right]\geq\sum\limits_{m=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]+6\sum\limits_{m\neq m^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}F_{m^{\prime}}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right],

where the inequality follows since all missing terms were assumed to be positive. Now, observe that as multi-linear and homogeneous polynomials, for every
m\neq m^{\prime}
,
F_{m}
and
F_{m^{\prime}}
are Hermite polynomials and belong to some Wiener chaos. By
[
22
, Theorem 1]
, their squares are positively correlated,
\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}F_{m^{\prime}}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m^{\prime}}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right].
Combining this inequality with the previous bound yields,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]\geq\sum\limits_{m=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]+6\sum\limits_{m\neq m^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m^{\prime}}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right].

On the other hand, it is readily seen that
\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})F_{m^{\prime}}(\mathcal{N})\right]=0
, whenever
m\neq m^{\prime}
, and so
1=\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[F^{2}_{m}(\mathcal{N})\right].
So,

3=3\left(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]\right)^{2}=3\sum\limits_{m=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{2}+6\sum\limits_{m\neq m^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]\left[F_{m^{\prime}}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right],

and

(3.2)

\sum\limits_{m=1}^{d}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{2}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3.

Now, in order to invoke
2.1
, let
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
be a
d
-dimensional Gaussian with independent coordinates such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\right]=0
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{m}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]
. Set,
\widetilde{F}=\{F_{m}(\mathcal{N})\}_{m=1}^{d}
,
m(F):=\max\limits_{m}\mathbb{E}\left[F^{2}_{m}(\mathcal{N})\right]^{-1}
and observe
\max\limits_{m\leq d}\mathbb{E}\left[F^{2}_{m}(\mathcal{N})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[F^{2}(\mathcal{N})\right]=1
. Thus, according to
2.1
, if
\Delta(F)
is as in the theorem,

\displaystyle\Delta(F)\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m=1}^{d}\sqrt{2d4^{d}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m\neq m^{\prime}}\sqrt{2\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m^{\prime}}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}+d2^{d}\sqrt{2(2d)!\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{2d^{2}4^{d}\sum\limits_{m=1}^{d}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{2}\right)}+2d\left(\sum\limits_{i=m}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{8^{d}(2d)!\sum\limits_{m=1}^{d}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\mathbb{E}\left[F_{m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{4\cdot 8^{d}(2d)!\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\right)}+2d\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

The second inequality uses the elementary inequality
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{x_{i}}\leq\sqrt{d\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}}
, valid whenever
x_{i}\geq 0
. The third inequality is (
3.2
).

To finish the proof, suppose that
(\widetilde{F},\widetilde{\mathcal{N}})
are coupled with the optimal
W_{1}
coupling, and let
\bf{1}
be the all ones vector. So, by
2.1
, since

,

\displaystyle W_{1}(F(\mathcal{N}),\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\langle\widetilde{F},{\bf{1}}\rangle-\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{N}},{\bf 1}\rangle\right|\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\langle\widetilde{F}-\widetilde{\mathcal{N}},{\bf{1}}\rangle\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\|{\bf 1}\|\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widetilde{F}-\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\|\right]=\sqrt{d}\cdot W_{1}(\widetilde{F},\widetilde{\mathcal{N}})\leq C_{d}\cdot m(F)\cdot\Delta(F).

A similar argument works for

, by noting that if
\|h\|_{C_{2}}\leq 1
for some

, then
\widetilde{h}(x):=h(\langle{\bf{1}},x))
, satisfies
\|\widetilde{h}\|_{C_{2}}\leq d.

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

be a multi-linear polynomial of degree

The result follows from the following simple observation:

In other words, any monomial with Gaussian variables has a non-negative expectation. It is now enough to note that each of the expectations in (

.

3.2.
Graph Polynomials

Let us now show that the fourth-moment theorem holds for monochromatic counts in graphs with no influential vertices.
For graphs
G
and
H
, recall the polynomials
\mathbb{I}_{H}
and
F_{H,G}
defined in
Section

2.2
.
To apply
3.2
, we first need to estimate the Gaussian variance of the
m

F_{H,G,m}
, for even
m
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
G
and
H
be graphs with
v(H)=r
. Then, if
\mathcal{N}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{v(G)})
, for any
0<m\leq r
even,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{r-1}}\binom{r}{m}N(H,G)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[F_{H,G,m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{4^{r-1}}\binom{r}{m}N(H,G)\max\limits_{v\in V(G)}D_{v}(H,G),

where
N(H,G)
is the number of copies of
H
in
G
, as in (
1.3
).
Moreover, for triangles, when
H=\triangle
,

\mathbb{E}\left[F_{\triangle,G,2}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]=\mathrm{Var}(F_{\triangle,G}(\mathcal{N}))
.

Proof.

The representation in (
2.9
) along with a simple calculation shows that for even
m

\mathbb{E}\left[F_{H,G,m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{4^{r-1}}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{w}\in{\binom{V(G)}{m}}}D^{2}_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G).

Since every copy of
H
contains
\binom{r}{m}
subsets of size
m
, we also have,

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{w}\in\binom{V(G)}{m}\end{subarray}}D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G)=\binom{r}{m}N(H,G).

For any
\mathbf{w}
and
v\in\mathbf{w}
,
D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G)\leq D_{v}(H,G)
, which establishes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F_{H,G,m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{4^{r-1}}\max\limits_{v\in V(G)}D_{v}(H,G)\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{w}\in\binom{V(G)}{m}}}D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G)=\frac{1}{4^{r-1}}\binom{r}{m}N(H,G)\max\limits_{v\in V(G)}D_{v}(H,G).

Similarly, since
D_{v}(H,G)
is a non-negative integer,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{r-1}}\binom{r}{m}N(H,G)=\frac{1}{4^{r-1}}\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{w}\in\binom{V(G)}{m}}}D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[F_{H,G,m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right],

which concludes the proof in the general case.

The result for triangles follows by observing that
F_{\triangle,G}
is quadratic, as in (
2.7
). Thus,

F_{\triangle,G}=F_{\triangle,G,0}+F_{\triangle,G,2},

with
F_{\triangle,G,0}=\mathbb{E}\left[F_{\triangle,G}(\mathcal{N})\right].

∎

We can now prove a central limit theorem-type result for
F_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
G
and
H
be graphs with
v(H)=r
. Then, for
\mathscr{N}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{|V(G)|})
,
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
, and the normalized polynomial
\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N}):=\frac{F_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})-\mathbb{E}\left[F_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})\right]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(F_{H,G}(\mathcal{N}))}}
,

\displaystyle W_{1}(\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N}),\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\limits_{v\in V(G)}D_{v}(H,G)\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\right),

\displaystyle d_{2}(\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N}),\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\right).

Moreover, for triangles, when
H=\triangle
,

W_{1}(\widetilde{F_{\triangle,G}}(\mathcal{N}),\mathcal{Z})\lesssim\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F_{\triangle,G}}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F_{\triangle,G}}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Proof.

As observed from (
2.9
),
F_{H,G}
is a multi-linear polynomial of degree at most
r
, and all of its coefficients are positive. Thus,
Lemma

3.3
and
3.2
imply,

\displaystyle W_{1}(\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N}),\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\limits_{m\leq r}\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G,m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\right),

\displaystyle d_{2}(\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N}),\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G}(\mathcal{N})^{4}\right]-3\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)

For triangles, by
Lemma

3.5
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F_{\triangle,G,2}}(\mathcal{N})\right]=1
, which resolves this case. For general
H
, it will suffice to show that

(3.3)

\max\limits_{m\leq r}\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G,m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{-1}\leq 2^{r}\max\limits_{v\in V(G)}D_{v}(H,G).

By
Lemma

3.5
, for any
0<m\leq r
even,

\mathbb{E}\left[F_{H,G,m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]\geq\frac{1}{4^{r-1}}\binom{r}{m}N(H,G)
.
Similarly, since
\mathrm{Var}(F_{H,G}(\mathcal{N}))=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}\left[F_{H,G,m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right],

\mathrm{Var}(F_{H,G}(\mathcal{N}))\leq\frac{1}{4^{r-1}}\max\limits_{v\in V(G)}D_{v}(H,G)\sum\limits_{m^{\prime}=1}^{r}\binom{r}{m^{\prime}}N(H,G)\leq\frac{1}{2^{r-2}}\max\limits_{v\in V(G)}D_{v}(H,G)N(H,G).

So,

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{F}_{H,G,m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]^{-1}=\frac{\mathrm{Var}(F_{H,G}(\mathcal{N}))}{\mathbb{E}\left[F_{H,G,m}(\mathcal{N})^{2}\right]}\leq\max\limits_{v\in V(G)}D_{v}(H,G)\frac{4^{r-1}N(H,G)}{2^{r-2}\binom{r}{m}N(H,G)},

which is (
3.3
).
∎

4.
A Closer Look at Monochromatic Triangles

In this section, we focus on monochromatic triangle counts, taking
H=\triangle
, and prove
1.3
. We recall the polynomial expression in (
2.7
):

\mathbb{I}_{\triangle}(x_{u},x_{v},x_{w})=2(x_{v}x_{u}+x_{v}x_{w}+x_{u}x_{w}+1).

For graph
G
, let
\mathcal{T}(G)
be the set of unlabeled triangles in
G
. Given a two-coloring
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{v(G)}
define the centered, normalized count

Z_{\triangle}(x):=\widetilde{F}_{\triangle,G}(x)=\frac{1}{4\sigma_{\triangle}}\sum\limits_{T\in\mathcal{T}(G)}(\mathbb{I}_{\triangle}(x|_{T})-2)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\triangle}}\sum\limits_{uv\in E(G)}D_{uv}(\triangle,G)x_{u}x_{v},

where for
T=\{u,v,w\}
,
x|_{T}=\{x_{v},x_{u},x_{w}\}
, and

(4.1)

\sigma^{2}_{\triangle}:=\mathrm{Var}(T(\triangle,G))=\frac{1}{16}\sum\limits_{e\in E(G)}D^{2}_{e}(\triangle,G).

The expression on the right-hand side follows from (
2.9
), noting that the only pairs of vertices
u,v\in V(G)
where
D_{uv}(\triangle,G)>0
are those that form an edge
uv\in E(G).

We study the distribution of
Z_{\triangle}(X)
when
X\sim\mathrm{Rad}(\frac{1}{2})^{\otimes v(G)}
, so that
Z_{\triangle}(X)=Z(\triangle,G)
. It will be convenient to start by replacing
X
with a Gaussian vector, and in light of
2.2
, we begin by handling influential variables. If
\{G_{n}\}
is a sequence of graphs let us denote by
\mathcal{I}_{n}
the set of influential variables. Formally,
\mathcal{I}_{n}=(v_{i}^{(n)})_{i=1}^{k}\subset V(G_{n})
is an ordered tuple of vertices, such for any
i=[k]

\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{D_{v_{i}^{(n)}}(\triangle,G_{n})}{\sigma_{\triangle}}>0,

and for any other vertices
w^{(n)}\in V(G_{n})
, where there exists
N
such that for all
n>N,

w^{(n)}\not\in\mathcal{I}_{n},
we have

\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{D_{w^{(n)}}(\triangle,G_{n})}{\sigma_{\triangle}}=0.

Note that for simplicity, we are implicitly assuming that the number of influential vertices is uniformly bounded in
n
.

4.1.
Influential Edges Forbid CLTs

Our first goal will be to show that when
G_{n}
contains an influential edge, then the CLT cannot hold, and thus having no influential edge is a necessary condition for the asymptotic normality of
Z(\triangle,G_{n})=Z_{\triangle}(X)
when
X\sim\Rad(\frac{1}{2})^{\otimes v(G_{n})}
. This is precisely the first item of
1.3
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of graphs with
\mathcal{I}_{n}
the sets of their influential vertices. Suppose that
\sup\limits_{n}|\mathcal{I}_{n}|=m<\infty
and that there exists a sequence of influential edge
e_{n}\in E(G_{n})
, such that

\lim\sup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{D_{e_{n}}(\triangle,G_{n})}{\sigma_{\triangle}}>0.

Then, if
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
,

\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(\triangle,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})>0.

Our strategy for dealing with influential variables in
Z_{\triangle}(x)
will be to fix their values. For a partial coloring
\rho:{\mathcal{I}_{n}}\to\{\pm 1\}
we write
Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}:V(G_{n})\setminus\mathcal{I}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
for the function
Z_{\triangle}
where every
v\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
is assigned the color
\rho(v)
.
We can express
Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}
in the following way

\displaystyle Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\sigma_{\triangle}}\left[\sum\limits_{u,v\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}D_{uv}(\triangle,G_{n})\rho(u)\rho(v)+\sum\limits_{u\in\mathcal{I}_{n},v\notin\mathcal{I}_{n}}D_{uv}(\triangle,G_{n})\rho(u)x_{v}+\sum\limits_{u,v\notin\mathcal{I}_{n}}D_{uv}(\triangle,G)x_{u}x_{v}\right]

\displaystyle=Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)}+Z_{\triangle}^{(1,\rho)}(x)+Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(x).

For
i=0,1,2
,
Z_{\triangle}^{(i,\rho)}
stands for the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
homogeneous part of
Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}
. It is also clear from the expression that
Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}
does not depend on
\rho
. Let us collect some facts about this decomposition when the variables
x_{v}
is taken to be Gaussian.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{N}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{|V(G_{n})\setminus\mathcal{I}_{n}|})
, then the following conditions hold:

(1)

There exists a sequence
\{\lambda_{i,n}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}
with
|\lambda_{1,n}|\geq|\lambda_{2,n}|\geq\cdots
and independent standard Gaussians
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
such that,

Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{law}}}{{=}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i,n}(M_{i}^{2}-1).

(2)

For every partial coloring
\rho
, there exists
\sigma_{\rho}^{2}\in[0,1]
, such that
Z_{\triangle}^{(1,\rho)}(\mathcal{N})\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\rho}).

(3)

Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
for every
\rho
if and only if there does not exist an influential edge.

Proof.

For the first assertion, we note that
Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})
is a quadratic form in Gaussian variables. The result is obtained by diagonalizing the form and using the fact that the Gaussian is rotationally invariant, see
[
26
, Proposition 2.7.13]
for more details.

The second assertion follows since
Z_{\triangle}^{(1,\rho)}(\mathcal{N})
is a centered linear functional of Gaussian variables and hence Gaussian. The fact that
\sigma_{\rho}^{2}\leq 1
follows by calculating the variance and comparing to (
4.1
).

For the last item, let
R\sim\Rad(\frac{1}{2})^{\otimes|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}
be a random coloring of the influential vertices. Since
R
is centered, the condition
Z_{\triangle}^{(0,R)}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
is equivalent to
\mathrm{Var}_{R}(Z_{\triangle}^{(0,R)})\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.
Since
R(u)R(u^{\prime})
is uncorrelated with
R(v)R(v^{\prime})
whenever
\{u,u^{\prime}\}\neq\{v,v^{\prime}\}
we get that

\mathrm{Var}_{R}(Z_{\triangle}^{(0,R)})=\frac{1}{16\sigma^{2}_{\triangle}}\sum\limits_{uv\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}D^{2}_{uv}(\triangle,G_{n}).

Suppose that we have an influential edge i.e.
u_{n},v_{n}\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
such that

\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{D_{u_{n}v_{n}}(\triangle,G_{n})}{\sigma_{\triangle}}>0.

In this case it is clear that
\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}_{R}(Z_{\triangle}^{(0,R)})>0
as well. On the other hand, if there are no influential edges then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{R}(Z_{\triangle}^{(0,R)})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\max\limits_{u,v\in V(G)}\frac{D_{uv}(\triangle,G_{n})}{\sigma_{\triangle}}\right)\frac{1}{16\sigma_{\triangle}}\sum\limits_{u,v\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}D_{uv}(\triangle,G_{n})=o(1),

where we have used the variance representation (
4.1
), and that
\sup\limits_{n}|\mathcal{I}_{n}|=m<\infty
.
∎

We can now establish one particular case where
Z_{\triangle}(\mathcal{N})
does not converge to a Gaussian. If there is at least one large coefficient in the quadratic decomposition of
Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}
, then
Z_{\triangle}(X)
will have a sub-exponential behavior and so cannot converge to a Gaussian.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of graphs with
\{\mathcal{I}_{n}\}
the sets of their influential vertices. Suppose that
\sup\limits_{n}|\mathcal{I}_{n}|=m<\infty
. Further let
\lambda_{1,n}
be as in
Lemma

4.2
, and assume

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}{|\lambda_{1,n}|}=c>0.

Then, there exists some constant
c^{\prime}>0
, such that for every partial coloring
\rho:\mathcal{I}_{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
, and all
t>0
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(\mathcal{N})|>t\right)\geq e^{-c^{\prime}t},

where
\mathcal{N}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{|V(G_{n})\setminus\mathcal{I}_{n}|})
.

Proof.

We fix some large
t>10
, and bound
\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})|\geq t\right)
from below. Towards that, recall the expression
Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})
from
Lemma

4.2
, since we may assume
\lambda_{1,n}\geq\frac{c}{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda_{1,n}(M_{1}^{2}-1)\geq t+4\right)\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})-\lambda_{1,n}(M_{1}^{2}-1)|\leq 4\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left(M_{1}^{2}\geq\frac{t+4+\lambda_{1,n}}{\lambda_{1,n}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left(M_{1}^{2}\geq\frac{t+4+\frac{c}{2}}{\frac{c}{2}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{-\beta t},

where
\beta>0
is some constant, which depends on
c
.
The first inequality uses the independence between
\{M_{i}\}
, the second inequality uses Chebyschev’s inequality for
Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})-\lambda_{1,n}(M_{1}^{2}-1)
, which has
\mathrm{Var}(|Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})-M_{1}^{2}|)=\sum\limits_{i=2}^{\infty}\lambda_{i,n}^{2}\leq 1
, and the fourth inequality is a tail bound for the chi-squared random variable
M_{1}^{2}
.
But now, by
Lemma

4.2
, for any
\rho
,
Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)}+Z_{\triangle}^{(1,\rho)}(\mathcal{N})\sim\mathscr{N}(Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)},\sigma^{2}_{\rho})
with
\sigma^{2}_{\rho}\leq 1
, and
Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)}\leq m^{2}
. Thus, we have for some constant
\alpha>0
, independent of
\rho
, and for all large
t>10
,

\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)}+Z_{\triangle}^{(1,\rho)}(\mathcal{N})|\geq t\right)\leq e^{-\alpha t^{2}}.

So,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(\mathcal{N})|>t\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})|\geq 2t,|Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)}+Z_{\triangle}^{(1,\rho)}(\mathcal{N})|\leq t\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})|\geq 2t\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)}+Z_{\triangle}^{(1,\rho)}(\mathcal{N})|\leq t\right)-1

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{-\beta t}+1-e^{-\alpha t^{2}}-1=\frac{1}{2}e^{-\beta t}-e^{-\alpha t^{2}}\geq e^{-c^{\prime}t},

for some
c^{\prime}>0
.
∎

We also need to handle the case where
Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})
is not sub-exponential. In that case, as we shall show that
Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})
, and hence
Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(\mathcal{N})
, is approximately Gaussian, which makes
Z_{\triangle}(X)
approximately a Gaussian mixture. When there is an influential edge this mixture will be non-trivial, thus not a Gaussian.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of graphs with
\mathcal{I}_{n}
the set of their influential vertices. Further let
\lambda_{1,n}
be as in
Lemma

4.2
, and assume

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}|{\lambda_{1,n}}|=0.

Then, there exists a subsequence
n_{k}
, such that for every partial coloring
\rho:\mathcal{I}_{n}\to\{-1,1\}
,

Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(\mathcal{N})\xrightarrow[\mathrm{in\ law}]{n_{k}\to\infty}\mathscr{N}(Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)},\sigma^{\prime 2}_{\rho}),

for some
\sigma^{\prime 2}_{\rho},
and
\mathcal{N}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{|V(G_{n})\setminus\mathcal{I}_{n}|})
.

Proof.

If
\mathrm{Var}(Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N}))\xrightarrow{n\to 0}0
, then, by
Lemma

4.2

Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(\mathcal{N})\xrightarrow[\mathrm{in\ law}]{n\to\infty}Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)}+Z_{\triangle}^{(1,\rho)}(\mathcal{N})\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\sigma_{\rho}^{2}).

Thus, we suppose that
\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N}))=\eta^{2}>0
.
By
Lemma

4.2
, we can write
Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})
as
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i,n}(M_{i}^{2}-1),
and observe that by the Berry-Eseen inequality
[
3
]

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N}),\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}})\leq\max\limits_{i}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(\lambda_{i,n}(M_{i}^{2}-1))^{3}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[(\lambda_{i,n}(M_{i}^{2}-1))^{2}\right]}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N}))}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{1,n}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N}))}},

where we have passed to a subsequence and where
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\eta^{2})
.
This implies that

Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})\xrightarrow[\mathrm{in\ law}]{n_{k}\to\infty}\mathscr{N}(0,\eta^{2}).

Moreover, since
Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}
is a homogeneous quadratic polynomial,
Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})
belongs to the second Wiener chaos and its limit is independent of
\mathcal{N}
, see
[
28
, Theorem 3.1]
. Thus, by
Lemma

4.2

Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(\mathcal{N})\xrightarrow[\mathrm{in\ law}]{n_{k}\to\infty}\mathscr{N}(0,\sigma_{\rho}^{2}+\eta^{2}).

∎

Having covered the two cases above we can now prove
4.1
.

Proof of
4.1
.

We separate the proof into two cases, depending on the behavior of
Z_{\triangle}^{(2)}(\mathcal{N})
, as in
Lemma

4.2
. Recall that
|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\leq m
for some choice of
m
uniformly bounded in
n
.

Case I:
Suppose that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\lambda_{i,n}|>0
. By passing to a subsequence, and applying
Lemma

4.3
, we obtain a constant
c^{\prime}>0
, such that for large enough
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(\mathcal{N})|>t\right)\geq e^{-c^{\prime}t},

uniformly in
\rho.
Thus, let
X\sim\mathrm{Rad}(\frac{1}{2})^{\otimes v(G_{n})}
, so that
Z(\triangle,G_{n})=Z_{\triangle}(X)
.
By the law of total probability,

\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}(X)|>t\right)\geq\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum\limits_{\rho:\mathcal{I}_{n}\to\{\pm 1\}}\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(X)|>t\right)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum\limits_{\rho:\mathcal{I}_{n}\to\{\pm 1\}}\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(\mathcal{N})|>t\right)\gtrsim e^{-c^{\prime}t}.

The final limit follows by observing that for a given partial assignment
\rho
of all influential vertices, per
Lemma

2.3
,
Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}
has no influential variables and thus
2.2
implies convergence.
Since
\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{Z}|\geq t\right)\leq 2e^{-t^{2}/2}
for
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
, we conclude the proof by choosing an appropriately large
t
.

Case II:

Suppose that
\lim_{n\to\infty}|\lambda_{i,n}|=0
, and let
X\sim\mathrm{Rad}(\frac{1}{2})^{\otimes V(G_{n})}
be as above.
Let
Y
be a Gaussian mixture with law

Y\overset{\text{law}}{=}\frac{1}{2^{|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}}\sum\limits_{\rho:\mathcal{I}_{n}\to\{\pm 1\}}\mathscr{N}(Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)},\sigma^{\prime 2}_{\rho}),

where the variances

\sigma^{\prime 2}_{\rho}
are as in
Lemma

4.4
. We claim that
Z_{\triangle}(X)
and
Y
are asymptotically the same.
Indeed, by the law of total probability,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z_{\triangle}(X),Y)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z_{\triangle}(X),Z_{\triangle}(\mathcal{N}))+d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z_{\triangle}(\mathcal{N}),Y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}}\sum\limits_{\rho}d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(X),Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(\mathcal{N}))+d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z_{\triangle}(\mathcal{N}),Y).

Since
Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}
has no influential variables,
2.2
implies

\frac{1}{2^{|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}}\sum\limits_{\rho}d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(X),Z_{\triangle}^{(\rho)}(\mathcal{N}))\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0,

while the condition on
\lambda_{1,n}
and
Lemma

4.4
ensure that

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z_{\triangle}(\mathcal{N}),Y)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

Thus,
d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z_{\triangle}(X),Y)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.
and it will be enough to show, that when
G_{n}
contains an influential edge, then

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
is bounded away from
0
(where
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
). Since
Y
is a finite Gaussian mixture, and since the family of finite Gaussian mixtures is identifiable,
[
31
]
, for
Y\xrightarrow[\text{in law}]{n\to\infty}\mathcal{Z}
it is necessary and sufficient that, for every
\rho
,

Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\text{ and }\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}1.

The third item in
Lemma

4.2
along with the existence of an influential edge rules out the possibility of
Z_{\triangle}^{(0,\rho)}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
for every
\rho
. Hence
d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Y,\mathcal{Z})
must remain bounded away from
0
.
∎

4.2.
Revisiting the Fourth-Moment Phenomenon for
Z(\triangle,G_{n})

We now prove the second item of
1.3
and show that when
G_{n}
does not contain an influential edge, then the fourth-moment phenomenon persists.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
{G_{n}}
be a sequence of graphs and set
M_{n}:=\max\limits_{e\in E(G)}\frac{D_{e}(\triangle,H)}{\sigma_{\triangle}}
. Then, for
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
,

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(\triangle,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})\lesssim\left(M_{n}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[Z(\triangle,G_{n})^{4}\right]-3\right)^{1/5}.

Proof.

We shall require several results from
[
6
]
concerning the fourth moment of
Z(\triangle,G_{n})
, and its distance to a Gaussian.
First,
[
6
, Theorem 1.1, Lemma 4.1]
established that

(4.2)

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(\triangle,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})\lesssim\left(\left(\frac{1+N(\triangle_{4},G_{n})}{\sigma_{\triangle}^{4}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}+\frac{\sum\limits_{s=1}^{4}N(\triangle_{s},G_{n})+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{28}N(H_{s},G_{n})}{\sigma^{4}_{\triangle}}\right)^{1/5}.

Here
\triangle_{s}
stands for an
s
-pyramid (
s
triangles glued at a common edge) and
\{H_{s}\}_{s=1}^{28}
is a list of
28
graphs given in
[
6
, Lemma 4.1]
. In this lemma, the authors also show that there is a list of positive constants
\{\delta_{s}\}_{s=1}^{4}
and
\{c_{s}\}_{s=1}^{28}
, such that

(4.3)

\mathbb{E}\left[Z(\triangle,G_{n})^{4}\right]-3=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\triangle}^{4}}\left[-\sum\limits_{s=1}^{4}\delta_{s}N(\triangle_{s},G_{n})+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{28}c_{s}N(H_{s},G_{n})\right].

Our key observation is that we can enumerate the
s
-pyramids by enumerating over edges
e\in E(G_{n})
, and choosing
s
different triangles which contain
e
. Recall that for
e\in E(G_{n})
, there are precisely
D_{e}(\triangle,G_{n})
triangles which include
e
, and so

N(\triangle_{s},G_{n})\leq\sum\limits_{e\in E(G_{n})}D_{e}(\triangle,G_{n})^{s}.

We thus have, for every
1\leq s\leq 4
,

\displaystyle\frac{N(\triangle_{s},G_{n})}{\sigma^{4}_{\triangle}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sum\limits_{e\in E(G_{n})}D_{e}(\triangle,G_{n})^{4}}{\sigma_{\triangle}^{4}}\leq M_{n}^{2}\cdot\frac{\sum\limits_{e\in E(G_{n})}D_{e}(\triangle,G_{n})^{2}}{\sigma_{\triangle}^{2}}\leq 10M_{n}^{2},

where in the final inequality, we used the expression for
\sigma_{\triangle}^{2}
given in
Eq.

4.1
.
Plugging this into
Eq.

4.3
we get, for some constant
C>0
,

-CM_{n}^{2}+\frac{\sum\limits_{s=1}^{28}N(H_{s},G_{n})}{C\sigma_{\triangle}^{4}}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Z(\triangle,G_{n})^{4}\right]-3.

Applying the same bound to
Eq.

4.2
yields that,

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(\triangle,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})\lesssim\left(M_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\sum\limits_{s=1}^{28}N(H_{s},G_{n})}{\sigma^{4}_{\triangle}}\right)^{1/5}.

With the above two inequalities, we can now conclude with

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(\triangle,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})\lesssim\left(M_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[Z(\triangle,G_{n})^{4}\right]-3\right)\right)^{1/5}.

∎

5.
Towards Characterizing Asymptotic Normality of General Monochromatic Subgraph Counts

In this section, we generalize some of the ideas presented in
Section

4
to more general monochromatic subgraph counts and give evidence towards
1.5
.

5.1.
Graph Sequences Without Influential Pairs Exhibit Fourth-Moment Phenomena

We begin by generalizing
4.5
to the general case. We shall prove
1.4
and show that the fourth-moment phenomenon holds whenever the sequence
G_{n}
does not contain an influential pair. For convenience, we recall the statement of the theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider a sequence of graphs
\{G_{n}\}
, a graph
H
, and let
\sigma_{H}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(T(H,G_{n}))}.
If
M_{n}:=\max\limits_{e\in E(G_{n})}\frac{D_{e}(\triangle,G_{n})}{\sigma_{H}},
then, for
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
,

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(H,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})\lesssim\left(M_{n}^{2}+(\mathbb{E}\left[Z(H,G_{n})^{4}\right]-3)\right)^{\frac{1}{20}}.

In
4.5
, we showed that the distance of
Z(\triangle,G_{n})
to a standard Gaussian
\mathcal{Z}
was controlled by the maximum influence of a pair of vertices and the divergence of the fourth moment from
3=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}^{4}]
. The key idea in the argument was observing that the upper bound of
[
6
, Theorem 1.1, Lemma 4.1]
on
d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(\triangle,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})
was given by a sum over various connected
4
-joins
of triangles (ways to glue
4
triangles together into a connected graph). These
4
-joins were observed to be a subset of those which appeared with some coefficients in the fourth-moment difference
\mathbb{E}[Z(\triangle,G_{n})^{4}]-3
, and the only
4
-joins with negative coefficients in this fourth-moment difference had counts controlled by the influence of edges.

The work of
[
6
]
was extended to general
H
in
[
14
]
, enabling us to analogously extend
4.5
to general
H
. Before stating the needed result from
[
14
]
, we begin first define the set of
4
-joins appearing in the error term.

Definition 5.2

(Good join)
.

Given a simple connected graph
H
, a
good join
of
H
is a graph formed by joining
4
copies of
H
, say
H_{1},H_{2},H_{3},H_{4}
, such that for all
i=1,2,3,4

(5.1)

\displaystyle\bigg|V(H_{1}\cup H_{2}\cup H_{3}\cup H_{4})\bigg|-\bigg|V\big(\bigcup_{j\neq i}H_{j}\big)\bigg|\leq|V(H)|-2.

and

(5.2)

\displaystyle|V(H_{1}\cup H_{2}\cup H_{3}\cup H_{4})|\leq\min_{\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{4}}\bigg\{|V(H_{\pi_{1}}\cup H_{\pi_{2}})|+|V(H_{\pi_{3}}\cup H_{\pi_{4}})|-2\bigg\}.

In the expression above, we take the minimum over
\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{4}
, the set of permutations of
(1,2,3,4)
. We denote the collection of all good joins of
H
by
\mathcal{G}_{H}
, and by
N(\mathcal{G}_{H},G_{n})
the number of different good joins in
G_{n}
.

Theorem 5.3
.

(
[
14
, Theorem 1.4]
)

Fix a connected graph
H
with
v(H)=r\geq 3
and consider
Z(H,G_{n})
defined in (
1.2
). Then, we have that

d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(H,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})\lesssim\left(\frac{N(\mathcal{G}_{H},G_{n})}{\sigma_{H}(G_{n})^{4}}\right)^{1/20}

We will also use the following result (contained in the
c=2
case of
[
14
, Proposition 4.3]
).

Proposition 5.4

(
[
14
]
)
.

Fix connected graph
H
with
v(H)=r
and consider a graph sequence
\{G_{n}\}
. For
\mathbf{s}\in V(G_{n})_{r}
,
let

W_{\mathbf{s}}:=\frac{1}{|\Aut(H)|}a_{H,\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{1}\{1_{X_{=\mathbf{s}}}\}-{2}^{1-r})=\frac{1}{|\Aut(H)|}a_{H,\mathbf{s}}Z_{\mathbf{s}},

where
Z_{\mathbf{s}}:=\mathbf{1}_{X_{=\mathbf{s}}}-2^{1-r},
and observe that
Z(H,G_{n})=\frac{1}{\sigma_{H}(G_{n})}\sum_{\mathbf{s}}W_{s}.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z(H,G_{n})^{4}]-3

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{H}(G_{n})^{4}}\sum_{\{\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2},\mathbf{s}_{3},\mathbf{s}_{4}\}\in\mathcal{G}_{H}}\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathbf{s}_{1}}W_{\mathbf{s}_{2}}W_{\mathbf{s}_{3}}W_{\mathbf{s}_{4}}]

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{\sigma_{H}(G_{n})^{4}}\underbrace{\sum_{|\mathbf{s}_{1}\cap\mathbf{s}_{2}|\geq 2}\sum_{|\mathbf{s}_{3}\cap\mathbf{s}_{4}|\geq 2}}_{\{\mathbf{s}_{1}\bigcup\mathbf{s}_{2}\}\bigcap\{|\mathbf{s}_{3}\bigcup\mathbf{s}_{4}\}|\geq 2}\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathbf{s}_{1}}W_{\mathbf{s}_{2}}]\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathbf{s}_{3}}W_{\mathbf{s}_{4}}].

In order to prove
5.1
, we will need to understand more delicately which joins make positive and negative contributions to the fourth moment error above.

Lemma 5.5
.

For
\{\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2},\mathbf{s}_{3},\mathbf{s}_{4}\}\in\mathcal{G}_{H}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mathbf{s}_{1}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{2}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{3}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{4}}]=-2^{5-4r}+2^{3-2r}\sum_{i<j}2^{-|\mathbf{s}_{i}\cup\mathbf{s}_{j}|}-2^{2-r}\sum_{i=1}^{4}2^{-|\cup_{j\neq i}\mathbf{s}_{j}|}+2^{1-|\cup_{i}\mathbf{s}_{i}|}

Proof.

This follows by the principle of inclusion-exclusion:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mathbf{s}_{1}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{2}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{3}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{4}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\subset[4]}(-1)^{4-|A|}\mathbb{E}\left[2^{(1-r)(4-|A|)}\prod_{j\in A}\mathbf{1}\{X_{=\mathbf{s}_{j}}\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\subset[4]}(-1)^{4-|A|}2^{(1-r)(4-|A|)+1-|\bigcup_{j\in A}{\mathbf{s}}_{j}|}.

\displaystyle=2^{4(1-r)+1}-4\cdot 2^{3(1-r)+1-r}+\sum_{i<j}2^{2(1-r)+1-|\mathbf{s}_{i}\cup\mathbf{s}_{j}|}-\sum_{i=1}^{4}2^{(1-r)+1-|\cup_{j\neq i}\mathbf{s}_{j}|}+2^{1-|\cup_{i}\mathbf{s}_{i}|}

\displaystyle=-2^{5-4r}+2^{3-2r}\sum_{i<j}2^{-|\mathbf{s}_{i}\cup\mathbf{s}_{j}|}-2^{2-r}\sum_{i=1}^{4}2^{-|\cup_{j\neq i}\mathbf{s}_{j}|}+2^{1-|\cup_{i}\mathbf{s}_{i}|}.

∎

Similarly to
Eq.

4.1
, we can more generally express
\Var(T(H,G_{n}))
in terms of total influences of subsets of vertices, as computed explicitly in
[
14
]
, and is the analog of (
4.1
) for more general shapes.

Observation 5.6
.

For connected graph
H
with
v(H)=r,
we have that

(5.3)

\sigma_{H}(G_{n})^{2}=\Var(T(H,G_{n}))=\Theta\left(\sum_{k=2}^{r}\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in V(G_{n})_{k}}D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G_{n})^{2}\right)=\Theta\left(\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in V(G_{n})_{2}}D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G_{n})^{2}\right)

With these pieces, we can show that if
G_{n}
lacks influential pairs and the fourth moment of
Z(H,G_{n})
is asymptotically
3
, then
T(H,G_{n})
is asymptotically Gaussian.

Proof of
5.1
.

We consider two cases on
\{\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2},\mathbf{s}_{3},\mathbf{s}_{4}\}\in\mathcal{G}_{H}
, letting the corresponding
4
-join by this
4
-tuple be the graph
H^{\prime}
.

(1)

Suppose that for some
i\neq j

|\mathbf{s}_{i}\cap\mathbf{s}_{j}|\geq 2
(without loss of generality take
i=1,j=2
). Then, by summing over pairs of vertices
\mathbf{w}_{12}\subset\mathbf{s}_{1}\cap\mathbf{s}_{2}
, we get

\frac{N(H^{\prime},G_{n})}{\sigma_{H}(G_{n})^{4}}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{H}(G_{n})^{4}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{w}_{12},\mathbf{w}_{3},\mathbf{w}_{4}\in V(G_{n})_{2}\end{subarray}}D_{\mathbf{w}_{12}}(H,G_{n})^{2}D_{\mathbf{w}_{3}}(H,G_{n})D_{\mathbf{w}_{4}}(H,G_{n})\lesssim M_{n}^{2}\frac{\sum\limits_{\mathbf{w}}D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G_{n})^{2}}{\sigma_{H}(G_{n})^{2}}\lesssim M_{n}^{2},

where the final inequality follows by
5.6
.

(2)

Else
\{\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2},\mathbf{s}_{3},\mathbf{s}_{4}\}\in\mathcal{G}_{H}
forms a good join
H^{\prime}
, where for every
i\neq j\in[4]
,
|\mathbf{s}_{i}\cap\mathbf{s}_{j}|\leq 1
. The only contribution of such joins to
\mathbb{E}[Z(H,G_{n})^{4}]-3
is given by
\frac{1}{\sigma_{H}^{4}}\mathbb{E}[W_{\mathbf{s}_{1}}W_{\mathbf{s}_{2}}W_{\mathbf{s}_{3}}W_{\mathbf{s}_{4}}]
. Since every good join is connected, we must have
v(H^{\prime})\leq r+3(r-1)=4r-3
. Further since this good join has no pair of copies of
H
that share more than
1
vertex, we additionally have
v(H^{\prime})\geq 4r-6
. In order for
\{\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2},\mathbf{s}_{3},\mathbf{s}_{4}\}
to be a good join, for at least
4
of the
6
pairs
\{\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{j}\}_{i\neq j}
, we must have
|\mathbf{s}_{i}\cap\mathbf{s}_{j}|=1
. By
Lemma

5.5
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mathbf{s}_{1}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{2}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{3}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{4}}]

\displaystyle=-2^{5-4r}+2^{3-2r}\sum_{i<j}2^{-|\mathbf{s}_{i}\cup\mathbf{s}_{j}|}-2^{2-r}\sum_{i=1}^{4}2^{-|\cup_{j\neq i}\mathbf{s}_{j}|}+2^{1-|\cup_{i}\mathbf{s}_{i}|}

\displaystyle\overset{(*)}{\geq}2^{5-4r}+2^{3-2r}\cdot(4\cdot 2^{-(2r-1)}+2\cdot 2^{-2r})-2^{2-r}\cdot(4\cdot 2^{-(v(H^{\prime})-(r-2))})+2^{1-v(H^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=7\cdot 2^{4-4r}-2^{1-v(H^{\prime})},

where in (*), we used that
|\mathbf{s}_{i}\cap\mathbf{s}_{j}|=1
for at least
4
pairs
\{\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{j}\}_{i\neq j}
, and that since
\{\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2},\mathbf{s}_{3},\mathbf{s}_{4}\}
forms a good join, for any
i\in[4],

|\cup_{j\neq i}\mathbf{s}_{j}|\geq v(H^{\prime})-(r-2)
. Since
7\cdot 2^{4-4r}-2^{1-v(H^{\prime})}>0
for
v(H^{\prime})\geq 4r-5
, we turn our attention to the case where
v(H^{\prime})=4r-6
. In this case, we necessarily have
|\mathbf{s}_{i}\cap\mathbf{s}_{j}|=1
for all
6
pairs, and the above bound tightens to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mathbf{s}_{1}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{2}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{3}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{4}}]

\displaystyle=-2^{5-4r}+2^{3-2r}\sum_{i<j}2^{-|\mathbf{s}_{i}\cup\mathbf{s}_{j}|}-2^{2-r}\sum_{i=1}^{4}2^{-|\cup_{j\neq i}\mathbf{s}_{j}|}+2^{1-|\cup_{i}\mathbf{s}_{i}|}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{5-4r}+2^{3-2r}\cdot(6\cdot 2^{-(2r-1)})-2^{2-r}\cdot(4\cdot 2^{-(v(H^{\prime})-(r-2))})+2^{1-v(H^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=0.

The above estimates imply that for whenever
\{\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2},\mathbf{s}_{3},\mathbf{s}_{4}\}\in\mathcal{G}_{H}
forms a good join
H^{\prime}
, where for every
i\neq j\in[4]
,
|\mathbf{s}_{i}\cap\mathbf{s}_{j}|\leq 1
, we have
\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mathbf{s}_{1}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{2}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{3}}Z_{\mathbf{s}_{4}}]\geq 0
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{H}\subset\mathcal{G}_{H}
be the tuples
\{\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2},\mathbf{s}_{3},\mathbf{s}_{4}\}\in\mathcal{G}_{H}
that form a good join
H^{\prime}
, where for every
i\neq j\in[4]
,
|\mathbf{s}_{i}\cap\mathbf{s}_{j}|\leq 1
. Putting the pieces together from the above two cases, we can conclude that

\mathbb{E}[Z(H,G_{n})^{4}]-3\gtrsim-M_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{H}(G_{n})^{4}}\sum_{H^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{H}}N(H^{\prime},G_{n}).

Combining the above inequality with
5.3
gives the desired result:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{kol}}(Z(H,G_{n}),\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{N(\mathcal{G}_{H},G_{n})}{\sigma_{H}(G_{n})^{4}}\right)^{1/20}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(M_{n}^{2}+\left(\mathbb{E}[Z(H,G_{n})^{4}]-3\right)\right)^{1/20}.

∎

5.2.
Strongly Influential Pairs Forbid CLTs

We now give some evidence towards
1.5
, by showing the conjecture holds for a stronger notion of influence. Namely, we show that if
\{G_{n}\}
has
strongly influential pairs
, those forbid a CLT.

Definition 5.7

(Strongly influential vertices)
.

A
k
-tuple of vertices
\mathbf{w}\in V(G_{n})_{k}
is
\varepsilon
-
strongly influential
, if
D_{\mathbf{w}}(H,G_{n})\geq\varepsilon N(H,G_{n}).

It is readily seen that
N(H,G_{n})\gtrsim\sigma_{H}(G_{n})
, which justifies calling the tuple in the above definition
strongly
influential. Below we observe that when
G_{n}
contains an influential pair, then
Z(H,G_{n})
will be bounded almost surely,
uniformly in
n
. Clearly,
Z(H,G_{n})
is not a Gaussian in this case.

Proposition 5.8
.

Consider a fixed
H
and graph sequence
\{G_{n}\}
. If for all
N
, there exists some
n>N
such that
G_{n}
has a strongly influential pair of vertices
\mathbf{w}=(v,w)\in V(G_{n})_{2}
, then
Z(H,G_{n})\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 14.2914pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 14.2914pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 9.9135pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 9.9135pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}}}}\mathscr{N}(0,1)
.

Proof.

Fix connected simple graph
H
on
r
vertices. Consider graph
G_{n}
with strongly influential vertex pair
\mathbf{z}\in V(G_{n})_{2},
so that
D_{\mathbf{z}}(H,G_{n})\gtrsim T(H,G_{n}).

Per
5.6
, we can lower bound the variance as

\sigma_{H}(G_{n})^{2}\gtrsim D_{\mathbf{z}}(H,G_{n})^{2}\gtrsim N(H,G_{n})^{2}

However, since
N(H,G_{n})\geq T(H,G_{n})
, this implies that

Z(H,G_{n})=\frac{T(H,G_{n})-\mathbb{E}T(H,G_{n})}{\sigma_{H}(G_{n})}\leq\frac{T(H,G_{n})}{\sigma_{H}(G_{n})}=O(1)

is bounded almost surely. Therefore,
Z(H,G_{n})\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 13.82817pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 13.82817pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 12.34909pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 12.34909pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\xrightarrow{\text{in law}}}}}\mathscr{N}(0,1)
.
∎

Remark
.

There are some other more specific cases where influential pairs can forbid a CLT. For example, suppose that
H=K_{4}
, so that as in
Eq.

2.9
,
F_{H,G}=F_{H,G,0}+F_{H,G,2}+F_{H,G,4}
.
The calculations in
Lemma

3.5
show that

\mathbb{E}\left[F_{H,G,4}^{2}\right]\leq N(H,G)\text{ and }\mathbb{E}\left[F^{2}_{H,G,2}\right]\gtrsim\max\limits_{v\in V(G)}D_{v}^{2}(H,G).

Thus, let
\{G_{n}\}
be a sequence of graphs satisfying
D_{v}(H,G_{n})\gg\sqrt{N(H,G_{n})}
, and set
\widetilde{F_{H,G}}

. Then,
\widetilde{F_{H,G_{n}}}=\widetilde{F_{H,G_{n},2}}+o(1)

is much weaker than having a strongly influential pair, yet still stronger than
D_{v}(H,G_{n})=\Omega\left(\sigma_{H}(G_{n})\right)
.

5.3.

Appendix A
Bounding the Kolmogorov Distance by the
d_{2}
istance

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
and
Y
be any random variable, then,

\sup\limits_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(Y\leq t\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{Z}\leq t\right)\right|\lesssim d_{2}(Y,\mathcal{Z})^{\frac{1}{3}}.

Proof.

The proof is rather standard and follows, as in
[
13
, Theorem 3.3]
, by regularizing the step function. Thus, for a positive small
\delta>0
, and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, set

h_{t,\delta}(x)=\begin{cases}1&x\leq t\\
\frac{((t+\delta-x)^{3}(6t^{2}+\delta^{2}+3\delta x+6x^{2}-3t(\delta+4x)))}{\delta^{5}}&t<x<t+\delta\\
0&x\geq t+\delta\end{cases}.

The values of
h_{t,\delta}
in
[t,t+\delta]
were chosen as the unique minimal degree interpolating polynomial to ensure that
h_{t,\delta}
is twice continuously differentiable in
\mathbb{R}
. In particular, a straightforward, but tedious, calculation shows

\|h_{t,\delta}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{15}{8\delta}\text{ and }\|h_{t,\delta}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{10}{\sqrt{3}\delta^{2}}.

Thus, for small
\delta
,
\|h_{t,\delta}\|_{C_{2}}\leq\frac{10}{\sqrt{3}\delta^{2}}
, and

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[h_{t,\delta}(Y)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[h_{t,\delta}(\mathcal{Z})\right]\right|\leq\frac{10}{\sqrt{3}\delta^{2}}d_{2}(Y,\mathcal{Z}).

Since
0<h_{t,\delta}(x)<1
for
x\in(t,t+\delta)
we may conclude,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y\leq t\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{Z}\leq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[h_{t,\delta}(Y)\right]-\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{Z}\leq t\right)

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{E}\left[h_{t,\delta}(Y)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[h_{t,\delta}(\mathcal{Z})\right]|+|\mathbb{E}\left[h_{t,\delta}(\mathcal{Z})\right]-\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{Z}\leq t\right)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{10}{\sqrt{3}\delta^{2}}d_{2}(Y,\mathcal{Z})+\mathbb{P}\left(t<\mathcal{Z}<t+\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{10}{\sqrt{3}\delta^{2}}d_{2}(Y,\mathcal{Z})+\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.

Optimizing over
\delta
, we get
\mathbb{P}\left(Y\leq t\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{Z}\leq t\right)\leq 2d_{2}(X,\mathcal{Z})^{\frac{1}{3}},
and a similar argument bounds
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{Z}\leq t\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Y\leq t\right)
.
∎
