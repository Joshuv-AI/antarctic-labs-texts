Finite Element Approximation and Analysis of a Viscoelastic Scalar Wave Equation with Internal Variable Formulations Journal: Journal of Computational and Applied Mathematics
Yongseok Jang, Simon Shaw

Abstract
We consider linear scalar wave equations with a hereditary integral term of the kind used to model viscoelastic solids. The kernel in this Volterra integral is a sum of decaying exponentials (The so-called Maxwell, or Zener model) and this allows the introduction of one of two types of families of internal variables, each of which evolve according to an ordinary differential equation (ODE). There is one such ODE for each decaying exponential, and the introduction of these ODEs means that the Volterra integral can be removed from the governing equation. The two types of internal variable are distinguished by whether the unknown appears in the Volterra integral, or whether its time derivative appears; we call the resulting problems the displacement and velocity forms. We define fully discrete formulations for each of these forms by using continuous Galerkin finite element approximations in space and an implicit ‘Crank-Nicolson’ type of finite difference method in time. We prove stability and a priori bounds, and using the FEniCS environment, https://fenicsproject.org/ (The FEniCS project version 1.5, Archive of Numerical Software, 3 (100), 9–23, 2015.) give some numerical results. These bounds do not require Grönwall’s inequality and so can be regarded to be of high quality, allowing confidence in long time integration without an a priori exponential build up of error. As far as we are aware this is the first time that these two formulations have been described together with accompanying proofs of such high quality stability and error bounds. The extension of the results to vector-valued viscoelasticity problems is straightforward and summarised at the end. The numerical results are reproducible by acquiring the python sources from https://github.com/Yongseok7717 , or by running a custom built docker container (instructions are given).

Materials that exhibit both elastic and viscous response to imposed load and/or

[
1
]

The viscoelasticity literature contains a large number of

2

Weak formulations

Our first step is to define the test space
V
,

\displaystyle V=\left\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\ |\ v=0\ \textrm{ on }{\Gamma_{D}}\right\},

and then, multiplying (
1.1
) by
v\in V
, integrating
by parts and using the boundary data gives, in a standard way, that

\displaystyle\left(\rho\ddot{u}(t),v\right)+(\sigma(t),\nabla v)=F_{d}(t;v)

(2.1)

for all
v\in V
where the time dependent linear form
F_{d}
is defined by

F_{d}(t;v)=\left(f(t),v\right)+(g_{N}(t),v)_{L_{2}(\Gamma_{N})}.

(2.2)

We now need to substitute for the stress using either the displacement or velocity forms.

2.1

Displacement form

Recalling (
1.6
) and (
1.8
) we write

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to4.5713pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to4.5713pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.70715pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.26068pt{}}}$\hss}}}\sigma(t)=D\nabla\left(u(t)-\sum_{{q}=1}^{N_{\varphi}}\psi_{q}(t)\right),

(2.3)

where, for
1\leq{q}\leq N_{\varphi}
, the internal variables are defined by

\displaystyle\psi_{q}(t):=\frac{\varphi_{q}}{\tau_{q}}\int^{t}_{0}e^{-(t-s)/\tau_{q}}u(s)\ ds,

(2.4)

and satisfy the following ODEs,

\displaystyle\dot{\psi}_{q}(t)=\frac{\varphi_{q}}{\tau_{q}}u(t)-\frac{1}{\tau_{q}}\psi_{q}(t)\qquad\textrm{for }q=1,2,\ldots,N_{\varphi},

(2.5)

with
\psi_{q}(0)=0

\displaystyle\left(\rho\ddot{u}(t),v\right)+a(u(t),v)-\sum_{q=1}^{N_{\varphi}}a(\psi_{q}(t),v)=F_{d}(t;v)\qquad\forall v\in V.

(2.6)

In this the symmetric bilinear form
a\colon V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

a(w,v)=\left(D\nabla w,\nabla v\right)
and is easily shown to be continuous on
V
.
Moreover, it follows from our assumption on
\Gamma_{D}
that it is also coercive on
V
,

[
25
]
, and so the
energy norm
defined by
\|v\|_{V}:=\sqrt{a(v,v)}
,
for
v\in V
, satisfies
\kappa\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{V}^{2}\leq D\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}
for a positive constant

\kappa

(V,a(\cdot,\cdot))
is a Hilbert space equivalent
to
(H^{1}(\Omega),(\cdot,\cdot)_{H^{1}(\Omega)})
.

We use this bilinear form, or energy inner product, to enforce each internal variable ODE,
and then arrive at the following weak problem.

(P1)

Find
(0,T]\to V
maps
u
,
\psi_{1}
,
\psi_{2},\ldots,\psi_{N_{\varphi}}
such that

\displaystyle\left(\rho\ddot{u}(t),v\right)+a(u(t),v)-\sum_{q=1}^{N_{\varphi}}a(\psi_{q}(t),v)

\displaystyle=F_{d}(t;v)\qquad

\displaystyle\forall v\in V,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\tau_{q}a(\dot{\psi}_{q}(t),v)+a(\psi_{q}(t),v)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{q}a(u(t),v)\qquad

\displaystyle\forall v\in V,\ q=1,\ldots,N_{\varphi}

(2.8)

with
u(0)=u_{0},

\dot{u}(0)=w_{0}
and
\psi_{q}(0)=0,\ \forall{q}\in\{1,\ldots,N_{\varphi}\}
.

2.2

Velocity form

On the other hand, using (
1.7
) and (
1.8
)

for each

\dot{\zeta}_{q}(t)+\frac{1}{\tau_{q}}\zeta_{q}(t)=\varphi_{q}\dot{u}(t)\qquad\textrm{for }q=1,2,\ldots,N_{\varphi},

Using this in (

)
and incorporating (

(P2)
Find
(0,T]\to V

,
\zeta_{1}
,
\zeta_{2},\ldots,\zeta_{N_{\varphi}}

\displaystyle\left(\rho\ddot{u}(t),v\right)+\varphi_{0}a(u(t),v)+\sum_{q=1}^{N_{\varphi}}a(\zeta_{q}(t),v)=F_{v}(t;v)\qquad

\displaystyle\forall v\in V,

(2.11)

\displaystyle\tau_{q}a(\dot{\zeta}_{q}(t),v)+a(\zeta_{q}(t),v)=\tau_{q}\varphi_{q}a(\dot{u}(t),v)\qquad

\displaystyle\forall v\in V,\ q=1,\ldots,N_{\varphi},

(2.12)

with

\dot{u}(0)=w_{0}
,
\zeta_{q}(0)=0,\ \forall{q}

and
F_{v}(t;v)=F_{d}(t;v)-\sum\limits_{{q}=1}^{N_{\varphi}}\varphi_{q}e^{-t/\tau_{q}}a(u_{0},v)

Fully discrete formulation: Displacement form

Let
V^{h}\subset V
be a conforming finite element space built with
continuous piecewise Lagrange basis functions with respect to an underlying
quasi-uniform mesh with mesh-size characterised by
h
.
We write
t_{n}=n\Delta t
with time step
\Delta t=T/N
for

N\in\mathbb{N}
, and denote the fully discrete approximations to

and
\dot{u}
by

and

\dot{u}(t_{n})=\dot{u}^{n}\approx W_{h}^{n}\in V^{h}

Furthermore, we will use the following approximations,

\frac{\ddot{u}(t_{n+1})+\ddot{u}(t_{n})}{2}\approx\frac{W^{n+1}_{h}-W^{n}_{h}}{\Delta t}\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad\frac{u(t_{n+1})+u(t_{n})}{2}\approx\frac{Z^{n+1}_{h}+Z^{n}_{h}}{2},

and will impose the relation

\frac{W^{n+1}_{h}+W^{n}_{h}}{2}=\frac{Z^{n+1}_{h}-Z^{n}_{h}}{\Delta t}

Our fully discrete formulation for
(P1)

\mathrm{(\mathbf{P1})}^{h}

Find

,

,
{\Psi}_{h1}^{n}
,
{\Psi}_{h2}^{n},\ldots,
{\Psi}_{hN_{\varphi}}^{n}\in V^{h}

\displaystyle\left(\rho\frac{W^{n+1}_{h}-W^{n}_{h}}{\Delta t},v\right)+a\left(\frac{Z_{h}^{n+1}+Z_{h}^{n}}{2},v\right)-

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{q=1}^{N_{\varphi}}a\left(\frac{{\Psi}_{hq}^{n+1}+{\Psi}_{hq}^{n}}{2},v\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{F_{d}(t_{n+1};v)+F_{d}(t_{n};v)}{2},

\displaystyle\tau_{q}a\left(\frac{{\Psi}_{hq}^{n+1}-{\Psi}_{hq}^{n}}{\Delta t},v\right)+a\left(\frac{{\Psi}_{hq}^{n+1}+{\Psi}_{hq}^{n}}{2},v\right)

\displaystyle a(Z_{h}^{0},{v})

\displaystyle=a({u_{0}},{v}),

\displaystyle\left(W^{0}_{h},v\right)

We now turn to error bounds and begin by defining the elliptic projection

Lemma 3.1
.

[

[

\left\lVert w-Rw\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{r}|w|_{H^{r}(\Omega)}

Lemma 3.2

Suppose that

From Lemma

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq k\leq N}\left\lVert u(t_{k})-Z^{k}_{h}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq CT\lVert u\rVert_{H^{4}(0,T;H^{s_{2}}(\Omega))}(h^{r}+\Delta t^{2}),

for a positive constant
C
with the same qualities as above.

Proof.

The proof is parallel to that of Corollary
3.1
but instead of using the
result from Theorem
3.2
, we use Theorem
4.2
instead.

6

Conclusions

Our two fully discrete formulations demonstrate optimal energy and
L_{2}
spatial error
estimates, and second order temporal error estimates, both in theory, and
in numerical tests. We took the usual step in assuming ideal conditions for the
proofs although in practical problems one cannot always expect such optimality.
For example, if we had lower spatial regularity due, say, to corner singularities we
would expect the energy estimates to be of the order
O(h^{\min(s_{1}+1,s_{2})-1}+\Delta t^{2})

where the specific values of the exponents would depend on the geometry and strength of
the singularity. Also, with such reduced regularity we would be unlikely to have the
necessary elliptic regularity for a higher order
L_{2}
estimate.

If, on the other hand, the regularity in
time was reduced then we would expect to see
\Delta t^{2}
replaced by
\Delta t^{\beta}
,
for
\beta<2
, in the above. This may stem from the loads
f
and
g
being non-smooth
in time, possibly even discontinuous in some applications (we can think of intermittent hammer blows on a structure for example). Our assumptions on the temporal behaviour
of
f
and
g
are quite strong, and these allowed us to circumvent the use of
Grönwall’s lemma. Although it would be an interesting to see how these
assumptions could be relaxed while retaining the sharper bounds, we have no choice here
but to defer this to a later study.

This scalar, or antiplane shear, problem studied above can can be straighforwardly
elevated to a vector-valued problem representing dynamic linear viscoelasticity by making
some notational changes and using product Hilbert spaces. For details see, for example,

[
3
,
20
]
but, in brief, for this we would define the
Cauchy infinitesimal strain tensor

{{\varepsilon}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)
for
i,j=1,\ldots,d
,
d=2
or
3
and where
\boldsymbol{u}
is the displacement vector.
The tensor
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.73055pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.73055pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0225pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.69862pt{}}}$\hss}}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})
then plays the
role of
\nabla u
. We then replace
D
with
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to7.76053pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to7.76053pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to5.43236pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.88025pt{}}}$\hss}}}\boldsymbol{D}
,
a symmetric positive definite fourth order tensor, and the stress tensor

\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to4.5713pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to4.5713pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.70715pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.26068pt{}}}$\hss}}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is given in direct analogy to the scalar analogues in
(
1.6
) and (

).
The resulting variational formulation uses the symmetric bilinear form

a(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v})=\left(\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to7.76053pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to7.76053pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to5.43236pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.88025pt{}}}$\hss}}}\boldsymbol{D}\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.73055pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.73055pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0225pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.69862pt{}}}$\hss}}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}),\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.73055pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.73055pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to3.0225pt{}}}$\hss}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{{$\underline{{\hbox to2.69862pt{}}}$\hss}}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)
for
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}
where
\boldsymbol{V}=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{d}\ |\ \boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})=0\textrm{ on }\Gamma_{D}\}
. The coercivity of this form follows from Korn’s inequality (e.g.
[

,
32
,

,
27
]
), and continuity follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Internal variables can be defined by exact analogy with those
above, and continous and discrete variational problems can similarly posed. The stability
and error analyses then go through in the same way as above with the help of
the elasticity theory estimates in
[
34
,
27
,
35
]
,
and we will obtain similarly optimal bounds without the use of Grönwall’s inequality.
