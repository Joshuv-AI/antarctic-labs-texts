ON A FAMILY OF 2 2 -AUTOMATIC SEQUENCES GENERATING ALGEBRAIC CONTINUED FRACTIONS IN characteristic 2 2 Thanks: This work is supported by the National Science Foundation of China grant 12001216.
Yining Hu

Abstract
. We present family of automatic sequences that define algebraic continued fractions in characteristic 2 2 . This family is constructed from ultimately period words and contains the period-doubling sequence.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
11B85, 11J70, 11B50, 11Y65, 05A15, 11T55

1.
Introduction

Ultimately periodic sequences are the simplest of all sequences. In this article
we study a family
\mathcal{S}
of automatic sequences generated by ultimately periodic
sequences and the continued fractions defined by them.

Each sequence
\bf{s}
in
\mathcal{S}
is built from
an ultimately periodic sequence
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n}=p_{0}\ldots p_{l-1}(e_{0}\ldots e_{d-1})^{\infty}
in the following way.
Starting from the empty word
W_{0}
, we consider the sequence of words

(W_{n})_{n\geq 0}
such that
W_{n+1}=W_{n},\varepsilon_{n},W_{n}

(note the comma is for concatenation and it will be omitted when it is
suitable).
Hence, we have
W_{1}=\varepsilon_{0}
,
W_{2}=\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{0}
,
etc.
Note that, for all
n\geq 1
,
W_{n}
is a palindrome, centred in
\varepsilon_{n}
,
of length
2^{n}-1
.

By construction, the word
W_{n+1}
starts by
W_{n}
and
and we define
\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})
to be the limit of
(W_{n})
.
Hence, we have

\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{3},\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{2},....

We illustrate the construction of
\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})
with two basic cases.

1)
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=a,b,(c)^{\infty}
then

\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})=a,b,a,c,a,b,a,c,....=(a,b,a,c)^{\infty}.

Here
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
is ultimately constant and

\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})
is periodic. Consequently a basic property on continued
fractions shows that
\CF(\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}))
is quadratic over
\mathbb{F}_{2}(t)
.

2)
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=(a,b)^{\infty}
then

\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})=a,b,a,a,a,b,a,b,a,b,...

Here
\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})
is the celebrated period-doubling sequence.
It has been proved that
\CF(s(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}))
satisfies an algebraic
equation of degree
4
(see
[
7
,
8
]
).

Each sequence in
\mathcal{S}
is
2
-automatic. The definition and relevant
results about automatic sequences are briefly recalled in Section
1.3
.

Continued fractions defined by two classical example of automatic sequences
in characteristic
2
were studied in
[
7
,
5
,
8
]
,
where it was established that the Thue-Morse and period-doubling sequences
both define algebraic continued fractions of degree
4
.
On the other hand, both sequences define quadratic formal power series.
The situation is different from the real case:
Bugeaud
[
4
]
proved in 2013 that the continued
fraction expansion of an algebraic real number of degree at least
3
is
not automatic.

Our family
\mathcal{S}
is a natural generalization of the period-doubling
sequence. We prove in this article that every sequence from this family
defines an algebraic continued fraction in characteristic
2
.

In Section
1.1
we give the notation necessary for stating our
main result in
1.2
.
In Section
2
we prove the main theorem, and in Section

3
we give three examples. Finally, in Section
4
we
make a comment on the link between continued fractions and Riccati differential
equations.

1.1.
Continued Fractions in Power Series Rings

Let
A=\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{k}\}
be a finite alphabet. We treat
a_{j}
as formal
variables and define

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2}[A]:=\mathbb{F}_{2}[a_{0},\ldots,a_{k}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2}(A):=\mathbb{F}_{2}(a_{0},\ldots,a_{k})

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2}((A)):=\mathbb{F}_{2}((\frac{1}{a_{0}},\ldots,\frac{1}{a_{k}})).

Here
\mathbb{F}_{2}((\frac{1}{a_{0}},\ldots,\frac{1}{a_{k}}))
denotes the ring of
power series of the form

(1.1)

\sum_{n_{0},\ldots,n_{k}\geq N}c_{n_{0},n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}a_{0}^{-n_{0}}\cdots{a_{k}}^{-n_{k}},

where
N
is an integer and
c_{n_{0},n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

Let
(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence taking values in
A
. It defines a
formal power series
\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}z^{n}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[A][[z]]
.

We define a norm on
\mathbb{F}_{2}((A))
by assigning a series of the form
(
1.1
)
the number
2^{-m}
, where
m=\min\{n_{0}+n_{1}\cdots+n_{k}\mid\ c_{n_{0},n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}\neq 0\}
with the convention that
\min\emptyset=\infty
. This norm makes
\mathbb{F}_{2}((A))
an ultrametric space.

The continued fraction
\CF(\mathbf{u})=[u_{0},u_{1},\ldots]
is defined as
the limit of the sequence
([u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}])_{n}
:

\displaystyle[u_{0}]

\displaystyle=u_{0},

\displaystyle[u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}]

\displaystyle=u_{0}+\frac{1}{[u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}]}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}((A)),

for
n\geq 1
.
For example,

\displaystyle[u_{0},u_{1},u_{2}]

\displaystyle=u_{0}+\cfrac{1}{u_{1}+\frac{1}{u_{2}}}

\displaystyle=u_{0}+\frac{u_{1}^{-1}}{1+(u_{1}u_{2})^{-1}}

\displaystyle=u_{0}+u_{1}^{-1}+u_{1}^{-2}u_{2}^{-1}+u_{1}^{-3}u_{2}^{-2}+\cdots

The convergence of this sequence is proved in the same way
as in the case of classical contineud fraction for real numbers.

We may also choose to specialize the letters in
A
to non-constant polynomials
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]
(this gives a continuous map from
\mathbb{F}_{2}((A))
to
\mathbb{F}_{2}((1/z))
),
and then
\CF({\mathbf{u}})
can be seen as formal power series in
\mathbb{F}_{2}((1/z))
.
For basic information on continued fractions, particularly in power series rings, the reader is refered to
[
9
]
.

1.2.
Main result

We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=p_{0}\ldots p_{l-1}(e_{0}\ldots e_{d-1})^{\infty}
be an
ultimately periodic sequence over the alphabet
A
.

(i) The continued fraction
\CF(\se)
is algebraic of degree at
most
2^{d}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}(A)
. It is of degree
2^{d}
if
e_{0}
is different
from the other letters.

(ii) The formal power series

F:=\sum_{j\geq 0}s_{j}z^{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}(A)[[z]].

is algebraic of degree at most
2^{d-1}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}(A)(z)
.
It is of degree
2^{d-1}
if
e_{0}
is different from the other letters.

Remark 1
.

For the formal power series in
in Theorem
1.1
and in the examples, we chose to keep the letters of
an automatic sequence as formal variables instead of mapping them to elements
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, because in the latter case, the formal power series and
consequently its minimal polynomial depend on the mapping.
For an automatic sequence
\bf{u}
taking values
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
,
it is always possible treat the letters as formal variables and get
a minimal polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},x][y]
. This is because
F

can be decomposed into a finite sum
\sum a_{n}F_{n}
, where each
F_{n}
is algebraic
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
. Therefore each
a_{n}F_{n}
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})(x)
and so is
F
.

1.3.
Automatic sequences and algebraicity

Here we recall briefly the definition and properties of automatic sequences.
We refer the readers to
[
2
]
for a comprehensive exposition
on the subject.
A sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
is said to be
k
-automatic if it can be generated by a
finite
k
-automaton. In this article we will use an equivalent definition.
The
k
-kernel of a sequence
(u_{n})_{n}
is defined as the smallest set
containing
(u_{n})_{n}
and stable under the operations
(v_{n})_{n}\mapsto(v_{kn+r})_{n}
for
r=0,1,\ldots,k-1
.
The sequence
\bf{u}
is said to be
k
-automatic if its
k
-kernel is finite.

The well-known Theorem of Christol, Kamae, Mendès France and Rauzy

[
6
]

states that a formal power series in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[[x]]
is algebraic over

\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
if and only if the sequence of its coefficients is
p
-automatic.
This theorem is generalized by Salon
[
10
]
to multivariate cases:
We define the
k
-kernel of a multi-dimensional sequence

\mathbf{u}=(u_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}})_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}}

to be the smallest set containing
\bf{u}
and stable under the operations

(v_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}})_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}}\mapsto(v_{k\cdot n_{1}+r_{1},\ldots,k\cdot n_{d}+r_{d}})_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}}

for
0\leq r_{1},\ldots,r_{d}<k
. The sequence
\bf{u}
is said to be

k
-automatic if its
k
-kernel is finite. When
\bf{u}
takes values in

\mathbb{F}_{p}
, then the formal power series
\sum u_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}}x_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdot x_{d}^{n_{d}}

is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
if and only if
\bf{u}
is

k
-automatic.

For
r=0,1,\ldots,p-1
, the Cartier operators
\Lambda_{r}
are defined as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{r}:\mathbb{F}_{p}[[x]]

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p}[[x]]

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}u_{n}x^{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}u_{pn+r}x^{n}.

Thus for
f\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[[x]]
, we have

f=\sum_{r=0}^{p-1}x^{r}(\Lambda_{r}f)^{p}.

In the multivariate case, the Cartier operators are

\displaystyle\Lambda_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{d}}:\mathbb{F}_{p}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]]

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]]

\displaystyle\sum u_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}}x_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{n_{d}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum u_{pn_{1}+r_{1},\ldots,pn_{d}+r_{d}}x_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{n_{d}}.

For
f\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]]
, we have

f=\sum_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{d}=0}^{p-1}x_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{r_{d}}(\Lambda_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{d}}f)^{p}.

The following lemma will be used in the proof of theorem
1.1
:

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
\mathbf{u}=(u_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}})_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}}
be a
p
-automatic sequence taking value in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Let

\bf{v}
be an element from the
p
-kernel of
\bf{u}
, then the degree of
algebraicity of

\sum v_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}}x_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdot x_{d}^{n_{d}}
is less than or equal to that of

\sum u_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}}x_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdot x_{d}^{n_{d}}
.

Proof.

Let
f=\sum u_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}}x_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdot x_{d}^{n_{d}}
.
We only need to prove that for all
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}^{d}
,
for
\Lambda:=\Lambda_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{d}}
, the degree of algebraicity of

\Lambda f
is less than or equal to
that of
f
.
From the proof of Christol’s theorem we know that there exists
n\geq 0

and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots a_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
such that

f=a_{1}f^{p^{1}}+a_{2}f^{p^{2}}+\cdots+a_{n}f^{p^{n}}.

We make use of the following property of
\Lambda
:
for two power series
g
and
h
, we have

\Lambda(g\cdot h^{p})=\Lambda(g)\cdot h,

and

\Lambda\frac{g}{h}=\Lambda\frac{g\cdot h^{p-1}}{h^{p}}=\frac{\Lambda{(g\cdot h^{p-1}})}{h}.

Therefore

\Lambda f=\Lambda(a_{1})f+\Lambda(a_{2})f^{p}+\cdots+\Lambda(a_{n})f^{p^{n-1}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})[f].

We conclude that the degree of
\Lambda f
is less than or equal to
that of
f
.
∎

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

To prove of the first part of theorem
1.1
, we consider
the truncation
[s_{0},...,s_{2^{n}-2}]
of the continued fraction

\CF(\se)
for
n\geq 1
. This corresponds to the palindrome
W_{n}
.
For
n\geq 1
, we set
u_{n}/v_{n}=[s_{0},...,s_{2^{n}-2}]
. We have

(u_{1},v_{1})=(s_{0},1)
and
(u_{2},v_{2})=(s_{0}s_{1}s_{2}+s_{0}+s_{2},s_{1}s_{2}+1)
, etc.
The first step of the proof was introduced in the author’s previous work
with Lasjaunias concerning
the particular case of the period-doubling sequence. In Lemma 3.2 from

[
8
]
, using
basic properties of continuants, we proved that the pair
(u_{n},v_{n})

satisfies a simple recurrence relation. Indeed, for
n\geq 1
, we have

(R)

u_{n+1}=\varepsilon_{n}u_{n}^{2}\mbox{ and }v_{n+1}=\varepsilon_{n}u_{n}v_{n}+1.

From (
R
) we get immediately

\frac{v_{n+1}}{u_{n+1}}=\frac{v_{n}}{u_{n}}+\frac{1}{u_{n}}

and therefore we obtain

\frac{v_{n}}{u_{n}}=\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}\frac{1}{u_{k}}

for
n\geq 1
. Consequently

(
\star
)

\frac{1}{\CF({\mathbf{s(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})}})}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{v_{n}}{u_{n}}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{u_{n}}.

It is easier to work with the following series which differs by a rational
fractional from
1/\CF(\se)
:

G:=\sum_{n>l}\frac{1}{u_{n}}.

By regrouping every
d
terms and using (
R
), we decompose
G
as follows

(2.1)

G=\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}\frac{G_{n}^{2}}{e_{n}},

where

G_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u_{l+n+kd}}

for
n=0,1,\ldots,d-1
.
It follows from their definition and (
R
) that

\{G_{n}\}
satisfy the following relations

(2.2)

G_{0}=\frac{1}{u_{l}}+\frac{G_{d-1}^{2}}{e_{d-1}}

and

(2.3)

G_{n}=\frac{G_{n-1}^{2}}{e_{n-1}}

for
n=1,2,\ldots,d-1
, with the convention
u_{0}=1
,
from which we deduce

(2.4)

G_{0}=\frac{1}{u_{l}}+\frac{G_{0}^{2^{d}}}{e_{d-1}e_{d-2}^{2}\cdots e_{0}^{2^{d-1}}}.

Using (
2.1
), (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), we can express
G
in terms of
G_{0}

G=\frac{1}{u_{l}}+G_{0}+\frac{G_{0}^{2}}{e_{0}}+\frac{G_{0}^{4}}{e_{0}^{2}e_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{G_{0}^{2^{d-1}}}{e_{0}^{2^{d-2}}e_{1}^{2^{d-3}}\cdots e_{d-2}}.

This means that the degree algebraicity of
G
(and that of
\CF(\se)
) is at
most that of
G_{0}
. This proves that the degree of algebraicity of

\CF(\se)
is at most
2^{d}
.

When
e_{0}
is different from all other letters,
G_{0}
is in the
2
-kernel of

G
, so that by lemma
1.2
, the degree of
G
is at least that
of
G_{0}
.
To show that degree of algebraicity of
G_{0}
is at least
2^{d}
, consider the
series

g=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{e_{0}^{2^{k\cdot d}}}

that satisfies

e_{0}\cdot g^{2^{d}}+e_{0}\cdot g+1=0.

This is an irreducible polynomial by Eisenstein’s criterion.
If we project all the letters in
G_{0}
to
e_{0}
, then we get the series

e_{0}\cdot\sum_{k=l}^{\infty}\frac{1}{e_{0}^{2^{k\cdot d}}}
, which has the
same degree of algebraicity as
g
. Since projection cannot increase degree,
we conclude that in this case, the degree of algebraicity of
G_{0}
, and
therefore that of
\CF(\se)
is
2^{d}
.

To prove the second part of the theorem, we need the following lemma

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that the letters
e_{j}\;(0\leq j<d)
are all distinct and that they are
different from the letters
p_{j}\;(0\leq j<l)
. For
j=0,1,\ldots,d-1
, define

P_{j}:=\{k\mid s_{k}=e_{j}\}.

Then

(2.5)

P_{j+1}=\{2k+1\mid k\in P_{j}\}\quad\mbox{ for }j=0,1,\ldots,d-2

and

(2.6)

P_{0}=\{2k+1\mid k\in P_{d-1}\}\cup\{(2k+1)\cdot 2^{l}-1\mid k=1,2,\ldots\}.

Proof.

To simplify notation, we write
e_{n}
instead of
e_{n\pmod{d}}
when
n\geq d
.
By definition,
\se
can be written in two ways:

(2.7)

W_{l}e_{0}W_{l}e_{1}W_{l}e_{0}W_{l}e_{2}W_{l}e_{0}W_{l}e_{1}W_{l}e_{0}W_{l}\ldots

and

(2.8)

W_{l+1}e_{1}W_{l+1}e_{2}W_{l+1}e_{1}W_{l+1}e_{3}W_{l+1}e_{1}W_{l+1}e_{2}W_{l+1}e_{1}W_{l+1}\ldots

Letter
e_{0}
appears
at the same places in (
2.7
) as
e_{1}
in (
2.8
), except
that the length of the block
W_{l}
is
2^{l}-1
, while that of
W_{l+1}
is

2^{l+1}-1
.
By the assumption on
(e_{j})
and
(p_{j})
, the letter
e_{j}
does not
appear in
W_{l+j}

(0\leq j<d)
.
The above observation allows us to conclude that

P_{1}=\{2k+1\mid k\in P_{0}\}.

Similar argument shows that

P_{j+1}=\{2k+1\mid k\in P_{j}\}\quad\mbox{ for }j=1,\ldots,d-2.

To prove (
2.6
),
we also write
\se
in two ways:

(2.9)

W_{l+d-1}e_{d-1}W_{l+d-1}e_{0}W_{l+d-1}e_{d-1}W_{l+d-1}e_{1}W_{l+d-1}e_{d-1}W_{l+d-1}e_{0}\ldots

and

(2.10)

W_{l+d}e_{0}W_{l+d}e_{1}W_{l+d}e_{0}W_{l+d}e_{2}W_{l+d}e_{0}W_{l+d}e_{1}W_{l+d}e_{0}W_{l+d}\ldots

As with previous cases, the letter
e_{0}
appears
at the same places in (
2.10
) as
e_{d-1}
in (
2.9
),
except that the length of the block
W_{l+d}
is
2^{l+d}-1
,
while that of
W_{l+d-1}
is
2^{l+d-1}-1
. Unlike previous cases, the letter
e_{0}
also occurs in
W_{l+d}
at indices
(2k+1)\cdot 2^{l}-1
for
k=1,2,\ldots
That’s how we get the additional term in (
2.6
).
∎

We consider another sequence

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}=p_{0}^{\prime}\ldots p_{l-1}^{\prime}(e_{0}^{\prime}\ldots e_{d-1}^{\prime})^{\infty}
.
where the letters
p_{j}^{\prime}

(0\leq j<l)
and
e_{j}^{\prime}

(0\leq j<d)

are all distinct, so that we can
apply lemma
2.1
to obtain the sets
P_{j}
for
j=0,1,\ldots,d-1

corresponding to the sequence
\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime})
.

We decompose the series
F
as follows

(2.11)

F=R+e_{0}F_{0}+\cdots+e_{d-1}F_{d-1},

where

R=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-2}s_{k}z^{k}}{1+z^{2^{l}}}

is a rational function which accounts for the preperiodic part of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}

and

F_{n}=\sum_{j\in P_{n}}z^{j}

for
n=0,1,\ldots,d-1
.

From lemma
2.1
we deduce the relations

(2.12)

F_{n}=zF_{n-1}^{2}

for
n=1,\ldots,d-1
.
On the other hand, since the supports of
R
and
F_{0},\ldots,F_{d-1}
form a
partition of
\mathbb{N}
, we have

(2.13)

F_{0}=\frac{1}{1+z}-\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-2}z^{k}}{1+z^{2^{l}}}-\sum_{n=1}^{d-1}F_{n}=\frac{z^{2^{l}-1}}{1+z^{2^{l}}}-\sum_{n=1}^{d-1}F_{n}.

From (
2.12
) and (
2.13
) we deduce

(2.14)

F_{0}=\frac{z^{2^{l}-1}}{1+z^{2^{l}}}-\sum_{n=1}^{d-1}z^{2^{n}-1}F_{0}^{2^{n}}.

Using (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) we can express
F
using
F_{1}

(2.15)

F=R+\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}e_{n}z^{2^{n}-1}F_{0}^{2^{n}}.

This shows that the degree of
F
is at most that of
F_{0}
.

The degree of
F_{0}
is
2^{d-1}
: if
l
were
0
, in place of
F_{0}
, by

2.11
, we would have a series
f
that satisfies

f=\frac{1}{1+z}-\sum_{n=1}^{d-1}z^{2^{n}-1}f^{2^{n}}.

Therefore the series
h:=zf
satisfy

(1+z)\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}h^{2^{n}}+z=0,

which is an irreducible polynomial in
h
by Eisenstein’s criterion.

The support of
f
is the positions of
e_{0}
in

\mathbf{s}((e_{0}e_{1}\ldots e_{d-1})^{\infty})=W_{0}e_{0}W_{0}e_{1}W_{0}e_{0}W_{0}e_{2}W_{0}e_{0}W_{0}e_{1}W_{0}e_{0}W_{0}\ldots

Comparing the above expression with (
2.7
) we obtain

F_{0}=z^{2^{l}-1}f^{2^{l}}.

Therefore
f
is in the
2
-kernel of
F_{0}
. By lemma
1.2

the degree of
F_{0}
is at least that of
f
.

When
e_{0}
is different from the other letters, we can obtain
F_{0}
from
F

mapping
e_{0}
to
1
and the other letters to
0
. This show that in this case
the degree of
F
is at least that of
F_{0}
.

3.
Examples

Example 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=(ab)^{\infty}
. Then
\bf{s(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})}
is the period-doubling
sequence.

4.
Continued Fractions and Riccati Differential Equations

In 1977, Baum and Sweet
[
3
]

considered considered the subset
D
of irrational continued fractions

\alpha
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}((1/t))
such that

\alpha=[0,a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n},\ldots]

where
\deg(a_{i})=1
(i.e.,
a_{i}=t
or
t+1
) for
i\geq 1
.
Consider the subset
P
of
\mathbb{F}_{2}((1/t))

P:=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{2}((1/t))\mid\alpha=\sum_{i<0}a_{i}t^{i}\}.

Hence
P
contains
D
. They proved the following theorem:

Theorem 4.1
.

[Baum-Sweet, 1977]
An element
\alpha\in P
is in
D
if and only if
\alpha
satisfies

\alpha^{2}+t\alpha+1=(1+t)\beta^{2}

for some
\beta\in P
.

This theorem has the following equivalent formulation (see
[
8
]

for a proof):

Theorem
4.1
′
.

An element
\alpha\in P
is in
D
if and only if
\alpha
satisfies

(\alpha\cdot t(t+1))^{\prime}=\alpha^{2}+1.

In
[
5
]
and
[
8
]
, it was proved that the Thue-Morse
and period-doubling continued fraction both satisfy the Riccati differential
equation

(ab(a+b)f)^{\prime}=(ab)^{\prime}(1+f^{2}).

We prove that these are in fact particular cases of a more general result:

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\bf{u}
be a sequence taking value in
\{a,b,a+b\}
, where
a
,

b
and
a+b
(if it is in the image of
\bf{u}
) are non-constant polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[t]
, then the continued
fraction

f=[u_{0},u_{1},\ldots]\in\mathbb{F}_{2}((1/t))

satisfies the Riccati differential equation

(ab(a+b)f)^{\prime}=(ab)^{\prime}(1+f^{2}).

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Let
P_{n}/Q_{n}

(n\geq-1)
be the convergents of
f
. Recall that

(P_{0},Q_{0})=(u_{0},1),\quad(P_{-1},Q_{-1})=(1,0)

and

(4.1)

P_{n}=u_{n}P_{n-1}+P_{n-2},\quad Q_{n}=u_{n}Q_{n-1}+Q_{n-2}\quad\mbox{ for }n\geq 1.

Then we only have to prove that

\lim_{n}(ab(a+b)f_{n})^{\prime}-(ab)^{\prime}(1+f_{n}^{2})=0,

where
f_{n}=\frac{P_{n}}{Q_{n}}.

Define

F_{n}:=ab(a+b)P_{n}Q_{n}+ab(P_{n}^{2}+Q_{n}^{2}),

then

(ab(a+b)f_{n})^{\prime}-(ab)^{\prime}(1+f_{n}^{2})=\frac{F_{n}^{\prime}}{Q_{n}^{2}}.

We prove by induction that for all
n\geq 0
,

(4.2)

F_{n}=ab+g_{n}^{2}

for some

g_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[a,b]
.

It is easy to check that the claim is true for
n=-1,0
. Suppose now

n>1
and that the claim holds for
n-1
and
n-2
.
From the recurrence relations (
4.1
) we deduce

\displaystyle F_{n}=u_{n}^{2}F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}+u_{n}ab(a+b)(P_{n-1}Q_{n-2}+P_{n-2}Q_{n-1}).

Notice that
P_{n-1}Q_{n-2}+P_{n-2}Q_{n-1}=1
by a basic property of
convergents, and
if
u_{n}=a
or
u_{n}=b
,
u_{n}ab(a+b)=a^{2}b^{2}+u_{n}^{2}ab
, if
u_{n}=a+b
,

u_{n}ab(a+b)=u_{n}^{2}ab
.
We have

F_{n}=ab+u_{n}^{2}g_{n-1}^{2}+g_{n-2}^{2}+a^{2}b^{2}

if
u_{n}=a
or
u_{n}=b
, and

F_{n}=ab+u_{n}^{2}g_{n-1}^{2}+g_{n-2}^{2}

if
u_{n}=a+b
.
Thus we have proven (
4.2
).

Therefore

\frac{F_{n}^{\prime}}{Q_{n}^{2}}=\frac{a^{\prime}b+b^{\prime}a}{Q_{n}^{2}}.

Since the valuation of
1/Q_{n}
in
1/t
is at least
n
, the above
term converges to
0
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}((1/t))
as
n
goes to infinity.
∎
