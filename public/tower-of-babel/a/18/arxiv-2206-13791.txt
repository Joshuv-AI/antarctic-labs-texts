A sharp estimate for the genus of embedded surfaces in the 3 3 -sphere

Abstract
. 1.
Introduction

Suppose
\Sigma
is an umbilical surface in the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, i.e. the difference of the principal curvatures
k_{2}-k_{1}\equiv 0
, then
\Sigma
is a geodesic sphere. Our result shows that in a
3
-manifold with sectional curvature bounded below by
1
, if the two principal curvatures
k_{1}
,
k_{2}
are close enough in an integral sense, then it is homeomorphic to a sphere. More generally, we can show that if
M
is a closed Riemannian
3
-manifold whose sectional curvature is bounded below by
1
, then a certain integral involving the gap between the two principal curvatures of a closed surface controls the genus of
\Sigma
. See Theorem
2
.

For example, as a corollary of our result, we can give a sharp bound of the genus of a surface in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
:

Theorem 1

(Corollary
4
)
.

For any closed embedded surface
\Sigma
of genus
g
in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
,

4\pi^{2}g(\Sigma)\leq\int_{\Sigma}f\left(|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|\right).

Here
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}
is the traceless second fundamental form of
\Sigma
and
f(t)=\sqrt{2}t+\left(t^{2}-2\right)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
.

The equality holds if and only if
\Sigma
is a geodesic sphere or a Clifford torus.

As a result of this pinching estimate, by using a compactness result of Choi-Schoen
[
6
]
, we can prove the following compactness result:

Corollary 1

(Corollary
11
)
.

Let
M
be a closed orientable three-dimensional Riemannian manifold whose sectional curvature of
M
is bounded from below by
1
and
C\geq 0
.
Then the space of closed orientable embedded minimal surfaces
\Sigma
with
\|A\|_{L^{3}(\Sigma)}\leq C
is compact in the
C^{k}
topology for any
k\geq 2
.

As another corollary, we can prove the following
L^{3}
gap theorem for minimal surfaces in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

Corollary 2

(Corollary
6
)
.

A closed embedded minimal surface
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
with
\int_{\Sigma}|A|^{3}<3\sqrt{2}\pi^{2}
is an equator.

There is a number of existing results of pinching estimates and rigidity theorems for surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the literature. For example, Pogorelov
[
16
, p. 493]
proved that if
\Sigma
is a closed convex surface and the ratio of its principal curvatures are uniformly close to
1
, then
\Sigma
is close to a round sphere. In
[
20
]
, a similar result is proved by replacing the pointwise condition by some integral condition. In
[
7
,
8
]
, the authors proved that if the
L^{2}
norm of the traceless second fundamental form is small enough, then the surface is
W^{2,2}
or
C^{0}
close to a sphere. See also
[
17
, Ch. 6]
for a survey on this matter.

There are works on the rigidity theorems in other space forms as well. Most noticably, there are pioneering rigidity theorems for minimal submanifolds in a sphere, due to Simons
[
18
]
, Lawson
[
13
]
and Chern-do Carmo-Kobayashi
[
4
]
, in which rigidity for oriented closed minimal submanifolds in
\mathbb{S}^{n+p}
with
|A|^{2}\leq\frac{n}{2-\frac{1}{p}}
is obtained. For pinching estimates, in
[
3
, Theorem 1.3]
(cf. also
[
15
]
), for a closed hypersurface in the standard space forms, a pinching result of the variance of the mean curvature
H
is proved, which involves a bound on the
L^{2}
norm of the traceless second fundamental form. On the other hand, this estimate is modelled on the umbilical hypersurfaces (i.e. geodesic spheres) and no topological or geometric information is known if the
L^{2}
norm of the traceless second fundamental form is large, whereas our result can be used to give some topological information about the surface given a bound on a certain integral of the norm of the traceless second fundamental form.

The organization of the remaining sections in this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we present a proof of an estimate for the genus of a surface embedded in a three-manifold with curvature bounded below by
1
.
Section
3
establishes the connection between this estimate and the norm of the traceless second fundamental form, establishing the main result. Furthermore, we explore some applications, including a compactness result regarding the space of minimal surfaces in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.
In the Appendix, Section
4

L^{2}
norm of the traceless second fundamental form. By providing numerical examples, we demonstrate that our estimate cannot be derived from the classical estimate and, in fact, performs consistently better in all the computed cases.

2.
An estimate of the genus

To prove the main result, we will need the following proposition, which results from a refinement of a volume estimate by Heintze and Karcher.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a closed orientable embedded surface in a closed orientable three-dimensional Riemannian manifold
M
. Assume the sectional curvature of
M
is bounded from below by
1
.
Let
k_{1}\leq k_{2}
be the principal curvatures of
\Sigma
(w.r.t. some unit normal), then

\displaystyle 4\pi^{2}g(\Sigma)\leq 2\left(2\pi^{2}-|M|\right)+\int_{\Sigma}\left(k_{2}-k_{1}-(1+k_{1}k_{2})\left(\tan^{-1}k_{2}-\tan^{-1}k_{1}\right)\right).

Here
g(\Sigma)
is the genus of
\Sigma
.

The following lemma is sketched in
[
5
]
and is well-known to experts. For reader’s convenience, we provide slightly more details here.

Lemma 1
.

With the assumption in Proposition
1
,
\Sigma
separates
M
into two components.

Proof.

This is equivalent to showing that
H_{0}(M\backslash\Sigma)\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}
.

As the Ricci curvature of
M
is positive, its first Betti number
b_{1}(M)
is zero. By deRham’s theorem,
H^{1}(M,\mathbb{R})=0
, and hence by universal coefficient theorem,
\mathrm{rank}(H_{1}(M))=0
as a finitely generated Abelian group.
By Poincare duality, the cohomology group
H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})
has rank zero. Since
M
is compact, the cohomology groups are all finitely generated Abelian groups and consequently we have that every element of
H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})
is torsion.
Consider the exact sequence (
[
9
, p. 200]
)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{Z}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}(\Sigma;\mathbb{Z})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{3}(M,\Sigma;\mathbb{Z})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{3}(M;\mathbb{Z})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{3}(\Sigma;\mathbb{Z})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{Z}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}

Since
\mathbb{Z}
has no non-zero torsion element, we have
\mathrm{im}(\phi)=0
. Using the first isomorphism theorem and exactness we deduce that

\mathrm{ker}(g)=\mathrm{im}(f)\cong\mathbb{Z}/\mathrm{ker}(f)=\mathbb{Z}/\mathrm{im}(\phi)\cong\mathbb{Z}
.
Applying the first isomorphism theorem to
g
, we get the isomorphism

H^{3}(M,\Sigma;\mathbb{Z})/\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathrm{im}(g)\cong\mathbb{Z}
,
from which it follows that
H^{3}(M,\Sigma;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}
. By Poincare-Alexander-Lefschetz duality (
[
1
, Theorem 8.3]
), we then get the isomorphism

H_{0}(M\backslash\Sigma)\cong H^{3}(M,\Sigma;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}.

∎

Proof of Proposition
1
.

By Lemma
1
,
\Sigma
separates
M
into two components,
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
.

It is clear that our inequality does not depend on the choice of the normal, so we can without loss of generality assume
\{k_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2}
(with
k_{1}\leq k_{2}
) are the principal curvatures of
\Sigma
w.r.t. the unit outward normal of
\Omega_{1}
. By
[
10
, Corollary 3.3.1]
(cf. also
[
14
, (10”), (11”)]
), the volume of
\Omega_{1}
is bounded from above:

\begin{split}|\Omega_{1}|\leq&\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{c(p)}(\cos t-k_{1}\sin t)(\cos t-k_{2}\sin t)dtdS(p),\end{split}

where
c(p)
is the cut distance of
\Sigma
w.r.t.
\Omega_{1}
. In
[
10
]
, the integrand is estimated by using the AM-GM inequality. In order to obtain a more precise estimate, we keep the integrand unchanged.
The cut distance at each point is bounded by above by the focal length, which is not longer than
\cot^{-1}(k_{2})
by Jacobi field comparison, as we have a lower bound on the sectional curvature. So direct integration gives

\begin{split}|\Omega_{1}|\leq&\int_{\Sigma}\int_{0}^{\cot^{-1}(k_{2})}(\cos t-k_{1}\sin t)(\cos t-k_{2}\sin t)dtdS\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}(-k_{1}+(1+k_{1}k_{2})\cot^{-1}(k_{2})).\end{split}

Similarly, with respect to the outward unit normal of
\Omega_{2}
, the principal curvatures are
-k_{2}\leq-k_{1}
, so we have

\displaystyle|\Omega_{2}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}(k_{2}+(1+k_{1}k_{2})\cot^{-1}(-k_{1})).

By adding the two inequalities and using the identity
\tan^{-1}x=\frac{\pi}{2}-\cot^{-1}x
,

\begin{split}2|M|\leq&\int_{\Sigma}(k_{2}-k_{1}+(1+k_{1}k_{2})(\cot^{-1}(k_{2})+\cot^{-1}(-k_{1})))\\
=&\int_{\Sigma}\left(k_{2}-k_{1}+(1+k_{1}k_{2})\left(\pi-\left(\tan^{-1}(k_{2})-\tan^{-1}(k_{1})\right)\right)\right)\\
\leq&\int_{\Sigma}\left(k_{2}-k_{1}+\pi K-(1+k_{1}k_{2})\left(\tan^{-1}(k_{2})-\tan^{-1}(k_{1})\right)\right).\end{split}

(2.1)

By the Gauss-Bonnet theorem, the result follows. Note also that
|M|\leq|\mathbb{S}^{3}|=2\pi^{2}
by the volume comparison theorem.
∎

3.
Applications

Our aim is to draw some conclusions from Proposition
1
.

Lemma 2
.

For any
k_{2}\geq k_{1}
,

-\left(1+k_{1}k_{2}\right)\left(\tan^{-1}k_{2}-\tan^{-1}k_{1}\right)\leq 2\left(-1+\left(\frac{k_{2}-k_{1}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{k_{2}-k_{1}}{2}\right).

The equality holds if and only if
k_{1}=\pm k_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
k_{2}-k_{1}=2t
and
k_{2}+k_{1}=2s
. It is equivalent to show that

f(t,s)=2\left(t^{2}-1\right)\tan^{-1}t+\left(1+s^{2}-t^{2}\right)\left[\tan^{-1}(s+t)-\tan^{-1}(s-t)\right]\geq 0
for
t\geq 0
. By symmetry we can also assume
s\geq 0
.

This is an equality when
s=0
, so it suffices to show that
\frac{\partial f}{\partial s}\geq 0
.
We compute

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial s}=

\displaystyle 2s\left(\tan^{-1}(s+t)-\tan^{-1}(s-t)\right)+\left(1+s^{2}-t^{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1+(t+s)^{2}}-\frac{1}{1+(t-s)^{2}}\right).

As
\frac{\partial f}{\partial s}(0,s)=0
, it in turn suffices to show that
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial f}{\partial s}\geq 0
. By direct computation,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial f}{\partial s}=\frac{32t^{2}s\left(1+t^{2}+s^{2}\right)}{\left(1+(t-s)^{2}\right)^{2}\left(1+(t+s)^{2}\right)^{2}}\geq 0.

Therefore the inequality holds.
From the above, the equality holds if and only if
s=0
or
t=0
, which is equivalent to
k_{1}=\pm k_{2}
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

With the same assumption and notation in Proposition
1
, we have

4\pi^{2}g(\Sigma)\leq 2\left(2\pi^{2}-|M|\right)+\int_{\Sigma}\left[\sqrt{2}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|+\left(|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{2}-2\right)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right].

(3.1)

If the equality holds, then
\Sigma
is either umbilical or is a minimal surface. Moreover, the cut distance (from both sides of
\Sigma
) at each point of
\Sigma
must equal to the focal length.

Proof.

Let
k_{1}\leq k_{2}
be the principal curvatures, then we have
|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|=\frac{k_{2}-k_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}
.
By Proposition
1

\displaystyle 4\pi^{2}g(\Sigma)\leq\int_{\Sigma}\left(k_{2}-k_{1}-(1+k_{1}k_{2})(\tan^{-1}k_{2}-\tan^{-1}k_{1})\right).

So by Lemma
2
,

\displaystyle 4\pi^{2}g(\Sigma)\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}(k_{2}-k_{1})+2\int_{\Sigma}\left(-1+\left(\frac{k_{2}-k_{1}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{k_{2}-k_{1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\left[\sqrt{2}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|+\left(|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{2}-2\right)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right].

We remark that
\sqrt{2}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|+\left(|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{2}-2\right)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
is strictly increasing in
|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|
. Indeed, for
f(t)=\sqrt{2}t+\left(t^{2}-2\right)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
,
f^{\prime}(t)=\frac{2\sqrt{2}t^{2}}{2+t^{2}}+2t\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)>0
for
t>0
. In particular, the integrand on the RHS is always non-negative.

We now assume the equality holds. By
[
10
, 3.4.8]
, all planes of
M
containing a tangent vector to a geodesic segment emanating orthogonally from
\Sigma
have sectional curvature
1
, up to its focal length. (
2.1
) also shows that
R^{M}_{1221}=1
and so the sectional curvature along
\Sigma
must be constantly equal to
1
.
∎

In view of Theorem
2
, in the following, we define the function

f(t)=\sqrt{2}t+\left(t^{2}-2\right)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

(3.2)

Remark 1
.

The inequality in Theorem
2
can also be written in a form such that the integrand is expressed as a power series of
|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|
:

\displaystyle 4\pi^{2}g(\Sigma)\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(2\pi^{2}-|M|\right)+\int_{\Sigma}f(|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(2\pi^{2}-|M|\right)+\int_{\Sigma}\left[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{l+1}\frac{8l}{4l^{2}-1}\left(\frac{|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2l+1}\right].

It is not hard to check that
f
is a strictly increasing convex function with
f(0)=0
.

Observe that the Taylor series above is alternating and starts from the third order term. Indeed we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

For
t\geq 0
,
f(t)\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}t^{3}}{3}
and the inequality is strict unless
t=0
.

Proof.

For
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{2\sqrt{2}t^{3}}{3}-f(t)\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{2t}{2+t^{2}}\left(\sqrt{2}t\left(1+t^{2}\right)-\left(2+t^{2}\right)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{2t}{2+t^{2}}\left(\sqrt{2}t\left(1+t^{2}\right)-\left(2+t^{2}\right)\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)>0.

∎

By Lemma
3
, instead of the bound in Theorem
2
, we can bound the genus by a weaker but simpler expression:

Corollary 3
.

With the same assumption and notation in Proposition
1
, we have

2\pi^{2}g(\Sigma)\leq 2\pi^{2}-|M|+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\int_{\Sigma}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{3}.

If the equality holds, then
\Sigma
is umbilical.

Corollary 4
.

For any closed surface
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, we have

(1)

4\pi^{2}g(\Sigma)\leq\int_{\Sigma}\left[\sqrt{2}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|+\left(|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{2}-2\right)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right].

The equality holds if and only if
\Sigma
is a geodesic sphere or is a Clifford torus.

(2)

2\pi^{2}g(\Sigma)\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\int_{\Sigma}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{3}.

The equality holds if and only if
\Sigma
is a geodesic sphere.

Proof.

By Theorem
2
and Corollary
3
, we only have to consider the equality case. By Lemma
3
, it suffices to prove (
1
).

By Lemma
2
, at each point in
\Sigma
, we have either

or
k_{1}+k_{2}=0
. Suppose there is an umbilical point

such that the principal curvatures

. Then by continuity,

in a neighborhood of

. By the usual proof of the Schur’s lemma by using the Codazzi equation, we conclude that on this neighborhood,

. By a standard connectedness argument we then conclude that
\Sigma
has constant principal curvatures and is a geodesic sphere. The remaining possibility is of course

, i.e.
\Sigma
is a minimal surface.

Suppose now
\Sigma
is a minimal surface. From the proof of Proposition
1
, the cut distance (from both sides of
\Sigma
) at each point must also equal to the focal length
\cot^{-1}k_{2}
. Assume there exists a point such that

\Sigma
inside
\Omega_{1}
is also a cut point, and so its distance from
\Sigma
is
\cot^{-1}(0)=\frac{\pi}{2}
. By

is the hemisphere and so

is not the equator, then

must be a torus. By Brendle’s proof of the Lawson’s conjecture
[

]
,

is a Clifford torus.
∎

For any surface, we have
|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{2}=|A|^{2}-2H^{2}
, where

is the normalized mean curvature.
In particular,
|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{2}=|A|^{2}
on a minimal surface.

Corollary 5
.

For any closed embedded minimal surface
\Sigma
in

4\pi^{2}g(\Sigma)\leq\int_{\Sigma}\left[\sqrt{2}|A|+\left(|A|^{2}-2\right)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{|A|}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right].

The equality holds if and only if
\Sigma

in

\mathrm{Area}(\Sigma)\leq 8\pi\left\lfloor\frac{g+3}{2}\right\rfloor
. Therefore

\displaystyle\max_{\Sigma}|A|\geq f^{-1}\left(\frac{4\pi^{2}g-2(2\pi^{2}-|M|)}{8\pi\left\lfloor\frac{g+3}{2}\right\rfloor}\right)=f^{-1}\left(\frac{2\pi^{2}(g-1)+|M|}{4\pi\left\lfloor\frac{g+3}{2}\right\rfloor}\right).

In particular, if
M=\mathbb{S}^{3}
, then

\max_{\Sigma}|A|\geq f^{-1}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{g}{\left\lfloor\frac{g+3}{2}\right\rfloor}\right).

∎

Corollary 11
.

Let
M
be a closed orientable three-dimensional Riemannian manifold whose sectional curvature of

is bounded from below by

and

.
Then

(1)

The space of closed orientable embedded minimal surfaces

Appendix: Comparison with the classical estimate

In this appendix, we present a comparison between our estimate and a more well-known estimate involving the
L^{2}
norm of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}
.

It is well-known that the genus of
\Sigma
can also be bounded in terms of
\int_{\Sigma}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{2}
when
M
has sectional curvature at least
1
.
To see this, note that the Gaussian curvature
K_{\Sigma}
of
\Sigma
satisfies
K_{\Sigma}\geq 1+k_{1}k_{2}
by the Gauss equation and the assumption that the sectional curvature of
M
is at least
1
. As
-k_{1}k_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{2}
, by integrating this inequality and using the Gauss-Bonnet formula, we have

\begin{split}4\pi g&=4\pi-\int_{\Sigma}K_{\Sigma}\\
&\leq 4\pi-|\Sigma|+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{2}.\end{split}

(4.1)

It appears that this estimate is simpler compared to (
3.1
), and the
L^{2}
norm of
|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|
in the estimate seems to be preferable to the
L^{3}
norm of
|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|
as seen in Corollary
3
.

However, it is important to note that this inequality does not imply our estimate (
3.1
). In fact, we have verified this through numerous numerical examples, which indicate that (
3.1
) is better than (
4.1
). This observation demonstrates that (i) our estimate is novel and (ii) it is not possible to derive (
3.1
) from (
4.1
). Note also that we do not impose any assumption on the area in Theorem
2
.

To compare the two estimates, we have computed examples using “ellipsoids” in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Upon examining the proof of (
4.1
), it is evident that it cannot be sharp for “small” surfaces that are nearly umbilical, which have genus
0
. (However, it can still be sharp after taking the floor on the right-hand side.)

To aid our computation, we observe that the surface integral
\int_{\Sigma}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{2}dS
is conformally invariant. Specifically, the spherical metric can be expressed as
g_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}=\frac{4}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}}\overline{g}
under the stereographical projection, where
\overline{g}
represents the flat metric on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Therefore, we can calculate

{A}_{i}^{j}=\frac{1+r^{2}}{2}\overline{A}_{i}^{j}-r\overline{g}\left(\partial_{r},\nu\right)\delta_{i}^{j},

where
r=|x|
and
\overline{A}
denotes the second fundamental form with respect to
\overline{g}
. From this, we see that
|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{{A}}}|=\frac{1+r^{2}}{2}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{\overline{A}}}|
and
\int_{\Sigma}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|^{2}dS_{g_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}}=\int_{\Sigma}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{\overline{A}}}|^{2}dS_{\overline{g}}
. Additionally, note that
|\Sigma|_{g_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}}=\frac{4}{(1+r^{2})^{2}}|\Sigma|_{\overline{g}}
, and so we can freely rescale
\Sigma
such that
|\Sigma|_{g_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}}\to 0
without affecting other terms in (
4.1
). However, we do not employ this observation in our numerical verification.

We now consider the family of ellipsoids
\{\frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{y^{2}}{b^{2}}+\frac{z^{2}}{c^{2}}=1\}
, where the genus
g=0
.
We compare the differences between the two estimates

E_{2}=\lfloor 1-\frac{1}{4\pi}|\Sigma|_{g_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}}+\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{\overline{A}}}|^{2}dS_{\overline{g}}\rfloor
and

E_{f}=\lfloor\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}f\left(|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{A}}|\right)dS_{g_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}}\rfloor
, where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is the floor function.

Using Mathematica

, we have computed the differences
E_{2}-E_{f}
for all
a,b,c
ranging between
{1,\cdots,10}
. Our experiment demonstrates that for all

E_{2}-E_{f}\geq 0
