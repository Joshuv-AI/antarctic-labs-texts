On the vorticity threshold for steady water waves
Evgeniy Lokharu, Miles H. Wheeler

Abstract
One of the most striking features of two-dimensional steady water waves with adverse vorticity is the emergence of stagnation points in the flow, even along branches of solutions that are initially unidirectional. In this paper we show that, for constant adverse vorticity above a certain explicit threshold, the first stagnation point must appear on the bed directly below the crest. This is in contrast to waves with favourable or even small adverse vorticity, where it is known that bottom stagnation cannot occur, leading to extreme waves exhibiting surface singularities. We also establish several related inequalities for unidirectional waves with strong adverse vorticity, including a new upper bound on the amplitude.



1
Introduction

The present paper concerns steady water waves with large vorticity. We assume an ideal fluid of constant unit density occupies a two-dimensional region of finite depth, bounded below by a flat bottom and above by a free surface. Neglecting the effects of surface tension and air motion, the hydrodynamic pressure is continuous across this interface and coincides with the constant air pressure there. We work with the full steady Euler equations, formulated in non-dimensional variables where the relative mass flux and the gravity are scaled to unity; see Section
2
for a detailed description. The (non-dimensional) relative velocity field is denoted by
(u,v)
, and the surface is always given by the graph
y=\eta(x)>0
. The flow is assumed to be rotational, where the vorticity function
\omega=v_{x}-u_{y}
is constant and
adverse
, that is
\omega=-\gamma<0
. While even small waves with constant adverse vorticity may have counter currents
Wahlén (2009)
;
Kozlov et al. (2020)
, we are focused here on waves that are
unidirectional
in the sense that
u>0
everywhere in the fluid.

We consider only smooth classical solutions, either Stokes waves or solitary waves. Here a Stokes wave is one with a periodic profile having exactly one crest and trough in each minimal period, while a solitary wave is one that converges uniformly to a flat background state at infinity. In the unidirectional setting, it is known that both Stokes
Constantin and
Escher (2004)
;
Constantin
et al. (2007)
;
Hur (2007)
and solitary
Hur (2008)
;
Kozlov et al. (2021)
waves are necessarily symmetric, and that solitary waves are ‘waves of elevation’ with exactly one crest. Moreover, solitary waves have a supercritical wave speed
Keady and Norbury (1978)
;
Wheeler (2015)
;
Kozlov et al. (2021)
. By a laminar flow we mean a trivial solution where the velocity is purely horizontal and the free surface is completely flat.

Our first main result can now be stated as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Along any continuous family of unidirectional Stokes or solitary waves, connected to a laminar flow and with constant adverse (dimensionless) vorticity

\displaystyle-\omega=\gamma>\gamma_{\star}\approx 1.37184,

the horizontal velocity satisfies the lower bound

u\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\eta\quad\textup{on the surface $y=\eta(x)$}.

(1.1)

In particular, extreme or overhanging waves cannot form before a stagnation point has emerged on the bottom.

This result is consistent with numerical observations of a certain threshold value of adverse vorticity. Below the threshold value waves approach surface stagnation as in the irrotational setting, while for adverse constant vorticity above the threshold one first observes bottom stagnation and the formation of eddies after that. Such a transition was reported by
Teles da Silva and
Peregrine (1988)
for solitary waves, and by
Ko and Strauss (2008a)
;
Ko and Strauss (2008b)
for periodic waves. More recently,
Ribeiro et al. (2017)
numerically calculated the regions in parameter space where the number of stagnation points changes for periodic waves. We refer the reader to the introduction of the latter reference for a more thorough discussion.

By combining Theorem
1
with existing global bifurcation results
Haziot and Wheeler (2023)
, we are able to obtain the existence of solitary waves with a single stagnation point. Similar statements can be shown for Stokes waves.

Theorem 2
.

For any
\gamma>\gamma_{\star}
, there exists a smooth solitary wave for which
u>0
everywhere in the fluid except one point at the bottom beneath the crest, where
u=0
.

Asymptotically sharper estimates than (
1.1
) can be obtained for any large adverse vorticity. To simplify the formulas we introduce the shorthand

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\eta(x),\qquad\check{\eta}=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\eta(x)

for the depth of the fluid at a crest or trough, with the convention that the ‘trough’ of a solitary wave of elevation is located at infinity.

Theorem 3
.

Along any curve of unidirectional solitary waves, with constant adverse vorticity
\gamma>\gamma_{\star}
and bifurcating from a shear flow, the following is true.

(i)

Everywhere along the surface we have

u\geq\gamma\check{\eta}-O(\gamma^{-5/2})=\sqrt{2\gamma}-O(\gamma^{-1}).

(ii)

At every crest we have

u\geq\sqrt{2\gamma}-O\big(\gamma^{-\tfrac{5}{2}}\big)\quad\textup{and}\quad\hat{\eta}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}}+O\big(\gamma^{-\tfrac{7}{2}}\big).

(iii)

The amplitude satisfies

\hat{\eta}-\check{\eta}=O(\gamma^{-2}).

The same is true for unidirectional Stokes waves provided the Bernoulli constant
r
satisfies the bound

r\leq\gamma+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}}.

(1.2)

Here the
O(\,\cdot\,)
terms refer to the limit as
\gamma\to\infty
, and the implied constants are absolute. In particular, they are independent of the solution and of the vorticity.

The bound (
1.2
) automatically holds, for instance, for curves of solutions with fixed
r,\gamma
and variable wavelength. See Section
3
for more details.

At first glance it may seem surprising that strong adverse vorticity has the same sort of damping effect on amplitude as favorable vorticity
Constantin et al. (2021)
;
Lokharu et al. (2023)
;
Haziot and Strauss (2026)
, especially when numerical observations predict that a strong adverse current should
increase
wave height and steepness
Teles da Silva and
Peregrine (1988)
;
Swan et al. (2001)
;
Vanden-Broeck (1994)
. The explanation for this discrepancy is that such waves are only possible after the unidirectionality assumption has been violated, highlighting the importance of critical layers for the existence of large-amplitude solutions.

2
Problem formulation

Working in a reference frame traveling with the wave, the flow inside the fluid is
modeled by the steady Euler equations

\displaystyle uu_{x}+vu_{y}

\displaystyle=-P_{x},

(2.1a)

\displaystyle uv_{x}+vv_{y}

\displaystyle=-P_{y}-g,

(2.1b)

\displaystyle u_{x}+v_{y}

\displaystyle=0.

(2.1c)

Here
(u,v)
is the two-dimensional relative velocity field,
P
is the pressure,
g>0
is the gravitational constant, and the equations hold true within the two-dimensional fluid domain
D=\{(x,y):x\in\mathbb{R},\,0<y<\eta(x)\}
. The kinematic and dynamic boundary conditions are

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }y=0,

(2.1d)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=u\eta_{x}

\displaystyle\text{on }y=\eta,

(2.1e)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=P_{\mathrm{atm}}

\displaystyle\text{on }y=\eta,

(2.1f)

where
P_{\mathrm{atm}}
denotes the constant atmospheric pressure.

We restrict our attention to unidirectional waves satisfying

u>0

(2.2)

in
D
, and work in dimensionless units where the relative mass flux

\displaystyle m=\int_{0}^{\eta}u\,\mathrm{d}y

and gravitational constant
g
are both normalized to
1
. Lastly, we assume that the vorticity

\omega=v_{x}-u_{y}=-\gamma<0

is constant and adverse.

Introducing a stream function
\psi
satisfying
\psi_{x}=-v
and
\psi_{y}=u
, with the normalization that
\psi=0
at the bottom, we can reformulate (
2.1
) as

\displaystyle\Delta\psi-\gamma

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }D,

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2}+y

\displaystyle=r

\displaystyle\text{on }y=\eta,

(2.3b)

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\text{on }y=\eta,

(2.3c)

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }y=0,

(2.3d)

where here
r
is a (non-dimensional) Bernoulli constant. The assumption (
2.2
) translates to

\psi_{y}>0

(2.3e)

everywhere in
D
.

3
Preliminaries

An important part of the analysis is related to laminar flows subject to (
2.2
), whose stream functions can be obtained as
\psi=\Psi(y;s)
where

\Psi(y;s)=\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma y^{2}+sy

and the parameter
s>0
is the horizontal velocity at the bottom. Note that we can restrict to positive values of
s
because of our assumption (
2.3e
). The corresponding depth and the Bernoulli constant are given explicitly by

d(s)=\frac{-s+\sqrt{s^{2}+2\gamma}}{\gamma},\qquad R(s)=\frac{1}{2}s^{2}+\gamma+d(s).

Thus, for each
s>0
the pair
\psi=\Psi(y;s)
and
\eta=d(s)
satisfies (
2.2
) and solves
(
2.3
)
with Bernoulli constant
r=R(s)
.

From the above formulas we see that the depth
d(s)
is a monotonically decreasing function of
s>0
, while
R(s)
decreases for
s\in[0,s_{c}]
and increases for
s\geq s_{c}
for some critical value
s_{c}>0
satisfying
R^{\prime}(s_{c})=0
. More explicitly,
s_{c}>0
is the unique solution to

s_{c}-\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{s_{c}}{\gamma\sqrt{s_{c}^{2}+2\gamma}}=0.

Assuming
\gamma
is large, we have

s_{c}=\frac{1}{\gamma}+O(\gamma^{-3/2}).

For the corresponding depth, we find

d(s_{c})=\frac{-s_{c}+\sqrt{s_{c}^{2}+2\gamma}}{\gamma}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}}-\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}+O(\gamma^{-5/2}).

Note also that for any
r\in(R(s_{c}),R(0)]
there are exactly two roots
s_{-}(r)<s_{+}(r)
to the equation
R(s)=r
.

The largest possible value for
d=d(s)
is attained at
s=0
and is given by

d(0)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}},

while the corresponding Bernoulli constant is the quantity

R(0)=\gamma+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}}

appearing on the right hand side of (
1.2
).
It is known from
Kozlov et al. (2015)
that an arbitrary non-laminar and unidirectional solution must have a Bernoulli constant
r
that is strictly greater than
R(s_{c})
. Furthermore, unidirectional laminar flows have Bernoulli constants strictly less than
R(0)
.

4
Proof of the main results

Lemma 4
.

For any
\lambda\geq\tfrac{\gamma}{2}
, the minimum of
\psi_{y}-\lambda y
restricted to the surface is achieved at the crest.

Proof.

This is essentially proved in
(
Kozlov and Lokharu, 2023
, Proposition 3.3)
, but we briefly outline the argument here for completeness. Consider the auxiliary function

\displaystyle f=\tfrac{1}{2}\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2}+y+(\lambda-\gamma)\psi.

Straightforward calculations show that

\displaystyle\Delta f+af_{x}+bf_{y}=\frac{2+(2\lambda-\gamma)(2\psi_{y}+\lambda\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2})}{\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2}}\geq 0,

where the coefficients
a,b
are given explicitly by

\displaystyle a=-2\big(f_{x}-(2\lambda-\gamma)\psi_{y})\big)\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{-2},\qquad b=-2\bigl(-2+f_{y}-(2\lambda-\gamma)\psi_{y}\big)\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{-2}.

Moreover,
f
is constant on the surface, and satisfies
f_{y}=1+\lambda\psi_{y}>0
on the bottom. By the maximum principle,
f
therefore achieves its maximum at every point along the surface. The Hopf lemma then gives

\displaystyle 0<f_{y}=\frac{\eta_{xx}\psi_{y}^{2}+1}{1+\eta_{x}^{2}}+\lambda\psi_{y}=-\frac{\psi_{y}}{\eta_{x}}\frac{d}{dx}(\psi_{y}(x,\eta(x))-\lambda\eta(x))

along the surface. Here the second equality holds wherever
\eta_{x}\neq 0
, and both equalities are established by repeatedly differentiating the boundary conditions and using the resulting equations to eliminate partials of
\psi
in favor of derivatives of
\eta
. Thus the total derivatives of
\eta(x)
and
\psi_{y}(x,\eta(x))-\lambda\eta(x)
have opposite signs, and the result follows.
∎

In what follows the quantity

\hat{u}=\psi_{y}(0,\hat{\eta}),

representing the horizontal relative velocity at the crest, plays a crucial role.

Lemma 5
.

If
\hat{\eta}\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}\hat{u}
, then
\hat{\eta}\leq 2/\hat{u}
.

Proof.

Consider the harmonic function

\displaystyle h=\psi_{y}-\lambda y,\qquad\lambda=\frac{\hat{u}}{\hat{\eta}}=\frac{\psi_{y}(0,\hat{\eta})}{\hat{\eta}}.

The choice of
\lambda
guarantees that
h
vanishes at the crest. By assumption we also have
\lambda\geq\gamma/2
, so that Lemma
4
implies that the minimum of
h
along the surface is achieved at the crest. Thus
h\geq 0
on the surface. On the bed
h=\psi_{y}>0
, and so the maximum principle implies that
h\geq 0
throughout the fluid. Integrating this inequality along the crest line, we discover

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{0}^{\hat{\eta}}\big(\psi_{y}(0,y)-\lambda y\big)\,\mathrm{d}y=1-\frac{\lambda}{2}{\hat{\eta}}^{2}.

Rearranging, substituting the definition of
\lambda
, and canceling a common factor of
\hat{\eta}>0
yields the desired upper bound.
∎

The inequality in (
4.1
) is shown in the shaded region bounded by the two thicker curves. The thin curve shows
(\hat{u},r)
values for laminar flows, with a minimum

-value of

. Simple numerical calculations reveal that

Corollary 6
.

There are no solutions whose Bernoulli constant
r
lies in the interval

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\hat{u}^{2}+\frac{2}{\hat{u}}<r\leq\frac{1}{2}\hat{u}^{2}+\frac{2}{\gamma}\hat{u}.

(4.1)

Proof.

Evaluating the dynamic boundary condition (
2.3b
) at the crest gives
r=\frac{1}{2}\hat{u}^{2}+\hat{\eta}
. Thus (
4.1
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\hat{u}}<\hat{\eta}\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}\hat{u},

or in other words that the hypothesis of Lemma
5
holds but the conclusion does not hold.
∎

4.1
) is sketched as the shaded region in Figure

Proposition 7
.

Let
\gamma>0
be such that
R(s_{c})>\gamma/2+2/\sqrt{\gamma}
. Then along any global bifurcation curve of Stokes or solitary wave solutions, connected to a laminar flow, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{y}(0,\hat{\eta})=\hat{u}\geq u_{*}(r)>\sqrt{\gamma},

(4.2)

where
u_{*}(r)
is the largest root of
u_{*}^{2}/2+2/u_{*}=r
.

Proof.

Any bifurcation curve of Stokes or solitary waves starts with a laminar flow
located to the right of the shaded region in Figure
1
. Recall
from Section
3
that any non-laminar solution must have
r>R(s_{c})
. Thus if
\gamma>0
satisfies
R(s_{c})>\gamma/2+2/\sqrt{\gamma}
,
all solutions along the curve will satisfy
r>\gamma/2+2/\sqrt{\gamma}
,
and hence correspond to points in Figure
1
lying above the corner
of the shaded region. By Corollary
6
they must also stay to
the right of the shaded region, and the desired inequality follows.
∎

For large
r
, the function
u_{*}(r)
appearing in (
4.2
) has the expansion

\displaystyle u_{*}(r)=\sqrt{2r}-\frac{1}{r}-\frac{3}{2^{3/2}}\frac{1}{r^{5/2}}+O(r^{-4}).

(4.3)

Moreover, as shown in Figure
1
, we have

\displaystyle u_{*}(R(0))=\sqrt{2\gamma}.

Also, as all solutions automatically have

\displaystyle r>R(s_{c})=\gamma+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}+O(\gamma^{-5/2}),

(4.4)

the hypothesis
R(s_{c})>\gamma/2+2/\sqrt{\gamma}
holds provided
\gamma
is sufficiently large. Numerically calculating
R(s_{c})
and plotting it as a function of
\gamma
, it appears that
\gamma\geq 1.372
is sufficient.

4.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

First we establish that there exists exactly one
\gamma=\gamma_{\star}\approx 1.372
such that

R(s_{c}(\gamma);\gamma)=\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\gamma}}.

For this purpose we consider the function

\rho(\gamma)=R(s_{c}(\gamma),\gamma)-\frac{\gamma}{2}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\gamma}}.

Computing the derivative and taking into account that

\frac{\partial R}{\partial s}(s_{c}(\gamma),\gamma)=0,

we find

\begin{split}\rho^{\prime}(\gamma)&=\frac{\partial R}{\partial\gamma}(s_{c},\gamma)-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma^{3}}}=-\frac{d(s_{c})}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma\sqrt{s_{c}^{2}+2\gamma}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma^{3}}}\\
&=-\frac{2}{\gamma(s_{c}+\sqrt{s_{c}^{2}+2\gamma})}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma\sqrt{s_{c}^{2}+2\gamma}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma^{3}}}>\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\gamma\sqrt{s_{c}^{2}+2\gamma}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma^{3}}}>0.\end{split}

Thus, since
\rho(\gamma)
is strictly increasing, negative for small
\gamma
, and is positive for large
\gamma
, there exists a unique zero. A simple numerical computation reveals that
\gamma_{\star}\approx 1.37184
.

Now consider a bifurcation curve of solutions with
\gamma>\gamma_{\star}
starting at a laminar flow. By Proposition
7
and the above argument, any solution along this curve satisfies (
4.2
). From Lemma
4
we know that
\psi_{y}-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma y
has minimum along the surface at the crest, and hence

\psi_{y}-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\eta\geq\psi_{y}(0,\hat{\eta})-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\hat{\eta}

everywhere along the surface. From (
4.2
) and the definition of
u_{*}(r)
we have
\hat{\eta}<2/u_{*}(r)
, and so the above inequality implies

\psi_{y}\geq\frac{1}{2}\gamma\eta+u_{*}(r)-\frac{\gamma}{u_{*}(r)}>\frac{1}{2}\gamma\eta,

where in the last step we have used
u_{*}(r)>\sqrt{\gamma}
. The proof is complete.
∎

4.2
Proof of Theorem
3

Let us prove the first claim. Note that by Lemma
4
the function
\psi_{y}-\gamma y
attains its minimum along the surface at the crest, which gives

\psi_{y}\geq\gamma\eta+\hat{u}-\gamma\hat{\eta}\geq\gamma\check{\eta}+\hat{u}-\gamma\hat{\eta}.

Now we need to compute an asymptotic expansion in
\gamma
for
\hat{u}-\gamma\hat{\eta}
. We rewrite

\displaystyle\hat{u}-\gamma\hat{\eta}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}-\gamma\big(r-\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{u}^{2}\big)\geq u_{*}(r)-\gamma\big(r-\tfrac{1}{2}u_{*}^{2}(r)\big)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2r}-\frac{1}{r}-\frac{3}{2^{3/2}}\frac{1}{r^{5/2}}+O(r^{-4})

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma\left(r-\frac{1}{2}\left[\sqrt{2r}-\frac{1}{r}-\frac{3}{2^{3/2}}\frac{1}{r^{5/2}}+O(r^{-4})\right]^{2}\right).

Here we have used Proposition
7
and (
4.3
).
Next we apply (
4.4
) and, after an elementary but long computation, obtain
\hat{u}-\gamma\hat{\eta}\geq O(\gamma^{-5/2})
.
Then it is left to estimate
\check{\eta}
. Using the assumption
r\leq R(0)
we get the upper bound
\check{\eta}<r\leq R(0)=\sqrt{2/\gamma}
. For the lower bound we use the inequality
\check{\eta}>d(s_{1})
, which follows from the assumption on
r
, and where
s_{1}>0
is the unique solution to
R(s_{1})=R(0)
; see
Kozlov et al. (2015)
. A simple Taylor expansion shows that
d(s_{1})=\sqrt{2/\gamma}+O(\gamma^{-2})
.

The two remaining statements can be obtained in a similar fashion.

4.3
Proof of Theorem
2

We shall consider for a fixed
\gamma>\gamma_{\star}
the bifurcation curve of solitary waves
\mathcal{C}
constructed by
Haziot and Wheeler (2023)
, containing small-amplitude unidirectional solutions. Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{uni}}
be a connected component of unidirectional waves. First, we note that the Bernoulli constant remains bounded along
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{uni}}
, which follows from
Lokharu (2021)
. Thus, it follows from Theorem
1
that the surface is free from stagnation points. We claim that the norms of solutions are therefore uniformly bounded along
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{uni}}
. To see this, first note that the fluid depth is uniformly bounded from above and is separated from zero from below along
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{uni}}
, by using an upper bound for
r
and bounds from
Kozlov et al. (2015)
. Then one needs to know that
\psi_{y}>\delta>0
in a neighbourhood of the surface. But this is true by the following reasoning. The function
\psi_{y}-\varepsilon y
is harmonic and we can choose
\varepsilon
(depending only on
\gamma
) so that it is positive at the surface (note that
\psi_{y}
is uniformly separated from zero for all unidirectional solutions connected to the laminar flow). Then
\psi_{y}-\varepsilon y>0
everywhere by the maximum principle.
Now a local norm estimate as in Proposition 3.2 of
Kozlov and Lokharu (2023)
is valid along a uniform neighbourhood around the surface. Near the bottom we always have a uniform estimate in terms of
\gamma
by the classical elliptic theory. Now it follows directly by Theorem 1.1 in
Haziot and Wheeler (2023)
that the bifurcation curve
\mathcal{C}
must leave the unidirectional regime. By the maximum principle and a compactness argument, this transition must occur at a smooth solution where
\psi_{y}
vanishes somewhere along the bottom. Finally, by applying the Hopf lemma to
\psi_{x}
we know that
\psi_{y}
attains its minimum along the bottom at the point directly below the crest. The proof is complete.
