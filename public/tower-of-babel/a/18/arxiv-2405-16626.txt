Magnetized compressible turbulence with a fluctuation dynamo and Reynolds numbers over a million
James R. Beattie, Christoph Federrath, Ralf Klessen, Salvatore Cielo, Amitava Bhattacharjee

Abstract
Supersonic magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) turbulence is a ubiquitous state for many astrophysical plasmas. However, even the basic statistics for this type of turbulence remains uncertain. We present results from supersonic MHD turbulence simulations at unparalleled resolutions, with plasma Reynolds numbers of over a million. In the kinetic energy spectrum we find a break between the scales that are dominated by kinetic energy, with spectral index − - 2, and those that become strongly magnetized, with spectral index − - 3/2. By analyzing the Helmholtz decomposed kinetic energy spectrum, we find that the compressible modes are not passively mixed through the cascade of the incompressible modes. At high magnetic Reynolds number, above 10 5 10^{5} , we find a power law in the magnetic energy spectrum with spectral index − - 9/5. On the strongly magnetized, subsonic scales the plasma tends to self-organize into locally relaxed regions, where there is strong alignment between the current density, magnetic field, velocity field and vorticity field, depleting both the nonlinearities and magnetic terms in the MHD equations, which we attribute to plasma relaxation on scales where the magnetic fluctuations evolve on shorter timescales than the velocity fluctuations. This process constrains the cascade to inhomogenous, volume-poor, fractal surfaces between relaxed regions, which has significant repercussions for understanding the nature of magnetized turbulence in astrophysical plasmas and the saturation of the fluctuation dynamo.



1
Introduction

Magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) turbulence is ubiquitous across many scales in the Universe, whether it be the solar wind, accretions disks, each of the phases of the interstellar medium (ISM) of galaxies, or even the intracluster medium (ICM) between galaxies
(
Mac Low &
Klessen, 2004
;
Bruno &
Carbone, 2013
;
Brüggen &
Vazza, 2015
;
Ferrière, 2020
;
Fielding et al., 2023
;
Rosotti, 2023
;
Hopkins
et al., 2024
)
. In these plasmas, turbulence plays a diverse set of roles, from the transport of magnetic flux, momentum, and thermal energy, to regulating the rate in which cold gas is turned into stars
(
Mac Low &
Klessen, 2004
;
Federrath &
Klessen, 2012
;
Mohapatra &
Sharma, 2019
;
Kempski &
Quataert, 2022
)
. In the cold phases of the ISM, and in thin accretions disks, the rest frame root-mean-squared (rms) turbulent velocity fluctuations
\delta u
on the outer scale of the turbulence
\ell_{0}
are trans-to-supersonic,
\delta u/c_{s}\gtrsim 1
, where
c_{s}
is the local sound speed
(
Beattie et al., 2019
;
Federrath
et al., 2016
;
Ferrière, 2020
;
Rosotti, 2023
;
Hopkins
et al., 2024
)
. Even in solar wind plasmas, characterized by strong magnetic fields (thermal-to-magnetic pressure ratios –
plasma beta
– of the order of unity or less) and
\delta u/c_{s}\ll 1
, the turbulence is weakly compressible and shows significant deviations from incompressible MHD turbulence phenomenology
(
Zank &
Matthaeus, 1992
;
Bhattacharjee
et al., 1998
)
. All of these plasmas harbor extremely large plasma Reynolds numbers that facilitate the nonlinear transfer of energy across vast ranges of scales through the turbulence cascade, and exhibit high levels of magnetization, maintained by a variety of magnetic dynamos
(
Rincon, 2019
;
Brandenburg &
Ntormousi, 2023
)
. Therefore, to understand the turbulence in these plasmas, one has to understand supersonic, compressible MHD turbulence at extremely high Reynolds numbers. However, most of the theories and large Reynolds number calculations are for incompressible, subsonic turbulence
(
Iroshnikov, 1964
;
Kraichnan, 1965
;
Goldreich &
Sridhar, 1995
;
Boldyrev, 2006
;
Beresnyak, 2014
;
Galishnikova
et al., 2022
;
Dong
et al., 2022
)
, limiting their applicability for understanding the basic statistical properties of these supersonic turbulent systems.

Hydrodynamical, supersonic turbulence has been simulated at hydrodynamical Reynolds numbers
\mathrm{Re}=\delta u\ell_{0}/\nu\gtrsim 10^{6}
where
\nu
is the coefficient of kinematic viscosity, demonstrating the existence of a scale-separated double turbulent cascade in the kinetic energy spectra
(
Federrath et al., 2021
)
. Based on the second-order structure functions, scales
\ell_{s}<\ell<\ell_{0}
exhibit a
Burgers (1948)
cascade, with
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim k^{-2}
, while scales
\ell_{\nu}<\ell<\ell_{s}
follow a
Kolmogorov (1941)
cascade, with
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim k^{-5/3}
(with
She &
Leveque (1994)
intermittency corrections), where
k=2\pi/\ell
is the wavenumber,
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
is the kinetic energy spectrum,
\ell_{s}
represents the sonic scale where
\delta u(\ell_{s})=c_{s}
and
\ell_{\nu}
is the viscous dissipation scale. No such calculation exists for supersonic MHD turbulence at
\mathrm{Re}\gtrsim 10^{6}
, until the simulation presented in this study. Analogous to the hydrodynamical Reynolds number, the magnetic Reynolds number is defined as
\mathrm{Rm}=\delta u\ell_{0}/\eta
, where
\eta
is the plasma resistivity.
Similar to supersonic turbulence, the numerical evidence for a break scale in three-dimensional, incompressible MHD turbulence has been accumulating over only the last few years
(
Dong
et al., 2022
;
Galishnikova
et al., 2022
;
Fielding et al., 2023
;
Grete
et al., 2023
)
, precluded by the theory of extremely high
\mathrm{Rm}
reconnection-driven plasmoid instabilities that disrupt and modify the cascade
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k_{\perp})\sim\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k_{\perp})\sim k_{\perp}^{-11/5}
, where
k_{\perp}
is the wavevector perpendicular to the large-scale magnetic field
(
Comisso
et al., 2018
;
Boldyrev &
Loureiro, 2020
;
Dong
et al., 2022
)
. To capture this phenomenon, simulations require
\mathrm{Rm}\gtrsim 10^{5}
so that thin current sheets become unstable to the tearing instability
(
Bhattacharjee et al., 2009
;
Uzdensky
et al., 2010
;
Loureiro &
Uzdensky, 2015
;
Boldyrev &
Loureiro, 2017a
;
Mallet et al., 2017
)
. This happens on scales where the instability growth timescale is shorter than the dynamical timescale of the turbulence, leading to the break scale
k_{*}\sim\mathrm{Rm}^{4/7}

(
Loureiro &
Boldyrev, 2017
;
Dong
et al., 2022
)
. This has been measured in a recent strong-field, decaying MHD turbulence simulation at
\mathrm{Rm}\sim 10^{5}

(
Dong
et al., 2022
)
, but it is still an important and open question if it will be realized in other turbulence regimes.

Further pertinent to the study of driven MHD turbulence is the concept of alignment. In strong Alfvénic turbulence, the nonlinear timescale
t_{\rm nl}
associated with the MHD cascade rate is set by interactions between counter-propagating Alfvén wavepackets
\mathbf{z}_{\perp}^{\mp}=\mathbf{u}_{\perp}\mp\mathbf{b}_{\perp}
that cascade perpendicular to the magnetic field, where
\mathbf{b}_{\perp}
is in velocity units. It is
t_{\rm nl}\sim 1/(k_{\perp}\mathbf{z}^{\mp}_{\perp}\sin\theta)
, where
\theta
is the angle between
\mathbf{z}^{+}_{\perp}
and
\mathbf{z}^{-}_{\perp}
. To change
t_{\rm nl}
one can change the alignment between
\mathbf{z}^{+}
and
\mathbf{z}^{-}

(
Boldyrev, 2006
;
Perez &
Boldyrev, 2009
;
Mallet et al., 2015
)
, or put the turbulence in a state where
\mathbf{u}\propto\pm\mathbf{b}

(
Dobrowolny et al., 1980
;
Chernoglazov et al., 2021
)
, the latter completely halting the cascade. One can attain such a highly-aligned
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{b}
configuration by realizing a minimum energy state
(
Matthaeus et al., 2008
)
, or a maximum entropy state
(
Banerjee
et al., 2023
)
, through the process of plasma relaxation. For a relaxation process that approximately preserves the rugged, inviscid, MHD invariants, magnetic helicity and cross helicity, this gives
\mathbf{u}\propto\mathbf{b}\propto\mathbf{j}\propto\bm{\omega}
, (see
5.1
) where
\mathbf{j}=(1/\mu_{0})\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbf{b}
is the current density,
\mu_{0}
is the magnetic permittivity, and
\bm{\omega}=\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbf{u}
is the fluid vorticity. Hence, such a process does not only tend to align
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{b}
, as in dynamical alignment
(
Boldyrev, 2006
;
Perez &
Boldyrev, 2009
)
, but requires alignment to be spread also across
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{b}
and the respective curls, in turn depleting all of the nonlinearities of the MHD equations (see
5.1
). Such states may be realized through a number of different processes, are sensitive to the energy flux from the turbulent driving source, and may only be observed if there is significant scale-separation between
\ell_{0}
and scales within the cascade
(
Banerjee
et al., 2023
)
, as is the case for the simulations presented in our study.

We aim to address the nature of the spectral and alignment statistics of turbulence in a regime that combines both supersonic rms velocities and
\mathrm{Re}\sim\mathrm{Rm}\gtrsim 10^{6}
– an unexplored parameterisation of turbulence – also requiring that the magnetic field we use is self-consistently generated from a magnetic dynamo (as opposed to an imposed field). This requires an extremely large dynamical range that resolves many orders of magnitude of spatial scales in the turbulence, from the large-scale shocks to the thin current sheets, hence, this can only be done by pushing numerical codes and computing infrastructure to the very limits of modern capacities.

2
Results

2.1
The world’s largest supersonic MHD turbulence simulation:

We present the first results from an ensemble of driven, supersonic,
\delta u/c_{s}=4.3\pm 0.2
, magnetized turbulence simulations that have a magnetic field being self-consistently maintained by the turbulent dynamo in saturation, providing a volume integral energy ratio of
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}/\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}=0.242\pm 0.022
. The grids vary from
2520^{3}
(
\mathrm{Rm}\sim\mathrm{Re}\sim 10^{5})
up to
10080^{3}
(
\mathrm{Rm}\sim\mathrm{Re}\sim 3\times 10^{6}
), discretised on a triply-periodic domain with length
L
. Presently, these are the largest supersonic, magnetized simulations in the world, almost an order of magnitude larger in grid resolution (and Reynolds numbers) compared to previous simulations in this regime
(
Fielding et al., 2023
;
Grete
et al., 2023
)
and are the first simulations to resolve both a supersonic and subsonic cascade with a self-consistently maintained magnetic field. The simulations utilised over 80 million CPU hours distributed across nearly 140,000 compute cores on the high-performance supercomputer, SuperMUC-NG, at the Leibniz Supercomputing Centre. We integrate the
10080^{3}
simulation for
t\approx 2t_{0}
, where
t_{0}=\ell_{0}/\delta u
is the turnover time on the driving scale of the turbulence
\ell_{0}=L/2
(or equivalently
kL/2\pi=2
), allowing for time-averaging of all key statistics across
\approx 2t_{0}
, making for robust, statistically significant results. We provide details on the simulation methods in
4
.

2.2
Structure of the gas and current density:

In Figure
1
we show a two-dimensional slice of the

simulation, with the logarithmic, mean-normalized magnitude of the current density

8

et al., 2022
)
. The

field shows shocked, gaseous filaments in red, and deep voids in green, with fluctuations

)
.

2.4
Energy spectra and fundamental turbulence scales:

This unique numerical experiment resolves a broad range of scales and challenges profoundly the tenets of MHD turbulence theories. We show this directly by measuring the time-averaged, isotropic
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
(a) and
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)
(c) in Figure
3
(see
4.2
for spectra definitions).
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
and
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)
are significantly different from one another, both structurally and in amplitude, as shown with the
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)/\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
ratio, Figure
3
(d). This deviates significantly from current incompressible MHD turbulence theories, which predict the same power laws for
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)
and
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)

(
Schekochihin, 2022
)
. We find a
Burgers (1948)
spectrum
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim k^{-2}
on large scales, where
\delta u(k)>c_{s}
,
k_{\rm cor}^{u}\sim k_{0}>k>k_{\rm eq}=(10.6\pm 0.7)2\pi/L
, with
k_{\rm cor}^{u}
the correlation scale of the turbulence and
k_{\rm eq}
the
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k_{\rm eq})=\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k_{\rm eq})
energy equipartition (or MHD) scale. On the
\delta u(k)<c_{s}
range of scales
k_{\rm s}=(32\pm 2)2\pi/L<k<k_{\rm u}^{u}
where
k_{\rm s}
is the sonic scale and
k_{\rm u}^{u}
is the inner scale, we find an
Iroshnikov (1964)
-
Kraichnan (1965)
(IK) spectrum
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim k^{-3/2}
. The
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)/\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
spectrum is maximized at
k_{\rm s}
, hence the sonic transition
\delta u(k_{\rm s})=c_{s}
is the most magnetized scale in the turbulence. This is a similar dichotomy as found in supersonic, hydrodynamical turbulence
(
Federrath et al., 2021
)
, but now with the additional effect that the magnetic field is dominant at
k>k_{\rm eq}
. No such dichotomy exists in
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)
, which shows a self-similar range of scales on
k_{\rm s}<k<k_{\rm u}^{b}
with
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)\sim k^{-9/5}
, which is inconsistent with theories for Alfvénic turbulence
(
Goldreich &
Sridhar, 1995
)
, dynamic alignment
(
Boldyrev, 2006
)
, and high-
\mathrm{Rm}
tearing instabilities
(
Galishnikova
et al., 2022
;
Dong
et al., 2022
)
. We report all empirical measurements of slopes in
5
. Similar spectra have previously been measured in subsonic turbulence at high
\mathrm{Re}
in
(
Fielding et al., 2023
)
. Based on the physical size-scale of the plasmoids in the voids, we show the upper bound of
k_{*}
on each of the panels. We see no significant spectral steepening on these scales. We further decompose the velocity into incompressible and compressible modes and show the spectra for those modes in Figure
3
(b). We find the compressible mode spectrum follows roughly
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim k^{-2}
for all
k
, and the incompressible mode spectrum closely matches the total kinetic spectrum, with a slightly shallower spectrum
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim k^{-7/4}
on the
k>k_{\rm eq}
scales. This shows that the compressible modes are not passively tracing the incompressible modes, as is the standard result from previous compressible theories
(
Lithwick &
Goldreich, 2001
)
, but are potentially either transported non-locally via shocks across all scales, or undergo their own cascade. This is the first MHD simulation to resolve two kinetic energy cascades within a single simulation, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim k^{-2}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim k^{-3/2}
, as well as a magnetic cascade,
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)\sim k^{-9/5}
, unexplained by any current MHD turbulence theory. The spectra we have shown are globally isotropic, which are measured in the absence of an imposed magnetic field. However, this does not mean that there is no local anisotropy, as expected for MHD turbulence. We provide an analysis of the local anisotropy in

2.5
The alignment statistics of supersonic turbulence:

An IK-type spectrum
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim k^{-3/2}
has been associated with a depletion of the nonlinearities in the turbulence through the process of alignment
(
Boldyrev, 2006
;
Mallet et al., 2015
;
Chernoglazov et al., 2021
;
Banerjee
et al., 2023
)
, compared to the strongly nonlinear spectrum,
\sim k^{-5/3}

(
Goldreich &
Sridhar, 1995
)
. Alignment could be dynamical, from deformations of counter-propagating shear Alfvén wave packets
(
Boldyrev, 2006
;
Perez &
Boldyrev, 2009
)
, or come from plasma relaxation, if the relaxation timescale is shorter than the nonlinear one. Solar wind and terrestrial magnetosheath studies suggest that this is indeed possible, but the relaxation process happens in localized regions rather than globally in the whole plasma
(
Matthaeus et al., 2008
;
Pecora
et al., 2023
)

To understand what scales are able to relax in the plasma we now perform a scale-dependent timescale analysis. Because we know that we must treat the magnetic and velocity spectrum separately, as per the previous section, we must also treat the magnetic and velocity timescales separately, as opposed to combining them together into a single nonlinear timescale, as is the standard practice in driven turbulence studies
(
Schekochihin, 2022
)
. We approximate the scale-dependent relaxation timescale as
t_{\rm relax}(k)\sim t_{\rm A}(k)=1/[k\delta v_{\rm A}(k)]
, assuming that the process is related to the dynamical timescale of the magnetic field fluctuations, as has been found previously in
Dobrowolny et al. (1980)
. Because the turbulence is sourced through the velocity fluctuations, we compare this with the scale-dependent velocity fluctuation timescale
t_{\rm turb}(k)=1/[k\delta u(k)]
, assuming that it is the source of the turbulent motions in the velocity that perturb each scale away from relaxation. This process can be qualitatively understood through the principle of vanishing nonlinear transfers
(
Banerjee
et al., 2023
)
, where on each scale there is a competition between relaxation and the source of the turbulence, which, in a steady-state provides a deviation away from a relaxed state. We plot
t_{\rm relax}/t_{\rm turb}
as a function of
k
in Figure

. For
k>k_{\rm eq}
, where
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\propto k^{-3/2}
and
\delta u(k)/c_{s}<1
, we find that
t_{\rm relax}/t_{\rm turb}<1
, so the plasma is able to relax and deplete the nonlinearities faster than the turbulence is able to perturb the magnetic field out of a relaxed state. Conversely, for
k<k_{\rm eq}
and
\delta u(k)/c_{s}>1
,
t_{\rm relax}/t_{\rm turb}>1
, and the turbulence is able to maintain strong nonlinear transfers, without significant weakening from relaxation. We therefore associate the shallow
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim k^{-3/2}
spectrum with the depletion of nonlinearities via the local competition between plasma relaxation and the turbulence.

2.6
The local anisotropy of MHD turbulence with no net magnetic flux

Our simulations have no net magnetic flux, commonly referred to in the turbulent dynamo community as isotropic MHD. However, even though the magnetic and velocity fields are globally isotropic, with no preferential direction or orientation, the statistics in the coordinate frame of the local magnetic field need not be so, and a number of authors have shown that this is indeed the case, even for isotropic MHD
(
Beresnyak &
Lazarian, 2009
;
St-Onge
et al., 2020
;
Fielding et al., 2023
)
. Following these studies, we compute two-dimensional
(\ell_{\parallel},\ell_{\perp})
anisotropic structure functions for the velocity in the frame of the local mean magnetic field. This allows us to directly compare against the anisotropy predictions for incompressible MHD turbulence
(
Goldreich &
Sridhar, 1995
;
Boldyrev, 2006
;
Mallet &
Schekochihin, 2017
)
. As in
4.3
, we define
\widehat{\mathbf{b}_{\bm{\ell}}}
, and then the conditional, anisotropic structure functions,

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{u}^{2}(\ell_{\parallel}|\theta_{\bm{\ell},\widehat{\mathbf{b}_{\bm{\ell}}}})

\displaystyle=\left\langle{[\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})-\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}+\bm{\ell})]^{2}\Big|\;\;0\leq\theta_{\bm{\ell},\widehat{\mathbf{b}_{\bm{\ell}}}}<\pi/18}\right\rangle_{\bm{\ell}},

(1)

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{u}^{2}(\ell_{\perp}|\theta_{\bm{\ell},\widehat{\mathbf{b}_{\bm{\ell}}}})

\displaystyle=\left\langle{[\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})-\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}+\bm{\ell})]^{2}\Big|\;\;8\pi/18<\theta_{\bm{\ell},\widehat{\mathbf{b}_{\bm{\ell}}}}\leq\pi/2}\right\rangle_{\bm{\ell}},

(2)

where

\displaystyle 0\leq\theta_{\bm{\ell},\widehat{\mathbf{b}_{\bm{\ell}}}}=\arccos\frac{|\bm{\ell}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{b}_{\bm{\ell}}}|}{|\bm{\ell}|}\leq\pi/2.

(3)

Next, we find
\ell_{\parallel}
as a function of
\ell_{\perp}
by matching the amplitudes between the two structure functions, e.g.

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{u}^{2}(\ell_{\parallel}|\theta_{\bm{\ell},\widehat{\mathbf{b}_{\bm{\ell}}}})

\displaystyle=\delta\mathbf{u}^{2}(\ell_{\perp}|\theta_{\bm{\ell},\widehat{\mathbf{b}_{\bm{\ell}}}}),

(4)

and then matching the
\ell_{\parallel}
for the corresponding
\ell_{\perp}
that satisfies this relation
(
St-Onge
et al., 2020
;
Fielding et al., 2023
)
. We plot the
\ell_{\parallel}
as a function of
\ell_{\perp}
for the
10080^{3}
simulation in Figure

, compensating
\ell_{\parallel}
by
\ell_{\perp}^{2/3}
to directly test if the anisotropy is consistent with the two-dimensional anisotropic predictions
\ell_{\parallel}\sim\ell_{\perp}^{2/3}

(
Goldreich &
Sridhar, 1995
)
. We find that indeed there is significant scale-dependent anisotropy, with
\ell_{\parallel}\sim\ell_{\perp}^{5/6}
, stronger scale-dependent anisotropy than the prediction for both two-dimensional anisotropy
(
Goldreich &
Sridhar, 1995
)
and three-dimensional anisotropy
\ell_{\parallel}\sim\ell_{\perp}^{1/2}
(
Boldyrev, 2006
;
Mallet &
Schekochihin, 2017
)
. This relation persists over a large range of scales, including both
\ell_{\rm eq}
and
\ell_{\rm s}
. Deviations away from these relations have been found before in similar, (but incompressible), isotropic MHD dynamo experiments, at much lower plasma Reynolds numbers
(
St-Onge
et al., 2020
)
.

2.7
Tearing instabilities and plasmoid chains in current sheets

Tearing instabilities are ubiquitous in reconnecting current sheets when the magnetic resistivity is small compared to the out-flow velocity from the reconnecting sheet
(
Bhattacharjee et al., 2009
;
Uzdensky
et al., 2010
;
Loureiro &
Uzdensky, 2015
;
Boldyrev &
Loureiro, 2017a
;
Mallet et al., 2017
;
Comisso
et al., 2018
;
Boldyrev &
Loureiro, 2020
)
. One can parameterize this ratio using the Lundquist number
S=\ell_{\rm sheet}v_{\rm A}/\eta
, where
\ell_{\rm sheet}
is the length of the sheet,
v_{\rm A}
is the characteristic plasma out-flow velocity of the sheet, and
\eta
is the magnetic resistivity. For low
S
, the reconnection rate is given by
\epsilon_{\rm rec}\sim S^{-1/2}
, the slow reconnection regime, critically relying on the resistivity through
S
. However, for
S\gtrsim 10^{5}
, the current sheets may become unstable to tearing modes instabilities if the nonlinear timescale of the turbulence is longer than the growth timescale of the tearing modes
(
Loureiro &
Boldyrev, 2017
;
Mallet et al., 2017
;
Boldyrev &
Loureiro, 2017b
;
Comisso
et al., 2018
;
Loureiro &
Boldyrev, 2020
;
Dong
et al., 2022
)
. This gives rise to regions of intense filamentary currents that spontaneously disrupt the sheet. This instability allows
\epsilon_{\rm rec}
to become independent of
S

(
Bhattacharjee et al., 2009
;
Uzdensky
et al., 2010
)
, entering a fast reconnection regime, and exciting turbulent modes with a
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)\sim k^{-11/5}
spectrum
(
Dong
et al., 2022
)
. Based on Figure
1
,
\ell_{\rm sheet}\approx\ell_{0}
, and using the
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)/\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
spectra in Figure
3
,
v_{\rm A}\approx 0.1\delta u
on
\ell_{0}
. Hence, in our
10080^{3}
simulations, where
\mathrm{Rm}\sim 3\times 10^{6}
,
S\gtrsim 10^{5}
on the current sheet length scale, as required for the fast reconnection regime.

We search for tearing-type instabilities in our
10080^{3}
simulations to understand what scales they may influence, in an effort to understand if they change the nature of the energy cascade in supersonic magnetohydrodynamic turbulence. We show a zoom-in of an isolated set of intense current sheets in Figure
8
, going from the full
kL/2\pi=1
mode domain down to the
kL/2\pi=100
mode in top-most panels, revealing chains of plasmoid-like structures, which are the generic outcome of the current sheet becoming tearing unstable
(
Bhattacharjee et al., 2009
;
Uzdensky
et al., 2010
;
Loureiro &
Uzdensky, 2015
;
Boldyrev &
Loureiro, 2017a
;
Mallet et al., 2017
;
Comisso
et al., 2018
;
Boldyrev &
Loureiro, 2020
)
, developing in the sheets. Both sheets are found within the gas density voids in the turbulence, but several other disrupted, fractal sheets can be found in the shocked gas (see Figure
1
to find that shocked gas corresponds to the large current clumps in Figure
8
). The plasmoids are small, no larger than
kL/2\pi\sim 100
, placing them on the
k_{*}L/2\pi\gtrsim 100
modes in the energy spectra shown in Figure
3
, which are resolved in our simulations. Even though these plasmoids are present, it is not clear that the reconnection-mediated energy cascade
(
Dong
et al., 2022
)
translates perfectly to the supersonic regime, where the current sheet structure itself may be disrupted by large-scale shocks in both the velocity and gas density. In force balance, which the shocked gas tends toward,
c_{s}^{2}\bm{\nabla}\rho=\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{b}/\mu_{0}
for our isothermal plasma
(
Robertson &
Goldreich, 2018
;
Mocz &
Burkhart, 2018
)
, which means that the current sheets form on orthogonal flux surfaces to
\bm{\nabla}\rho
,
\mathbf{j}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\rho=0
. Hence, the structure of the current sheets are sensitive to the volume-filling properties of
\bm{\nabla}\rho
, which makes the role of plasmoids in the supersonic regime significantly different from the subsonic, incompressible regime
(
Dong
et al., 2022
)
.

3
Summary and conclusions

By running the world’s largest driven supersonic MHD turbulence simulations, we have revealed the existence of two scale-separated
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
cascades – a Burgers-type cascade (
\sim k^{-2}
), which hosts supersonic, kinetic-energy-dominated motions, and an IK-type cascade (
\sim k^{-3/2}
), which hosts subsonic, magnetically-dominated motions. Many authors have measured a
k^{-3/2}
spectrum before
(
Maron &
Goldreich, 2001
;
Müller &
Grappin, 2005
;
Perez &
Boldyrev, 2009
;
Bowen et al., 2018
;
Dong
et al., 2022
)
, which is attributed to strong, three-dimensional-anisotropic MHD turbulence undergoing dynamical alignment. However, we show explicitly in Figure
5
that our scale-dependent alignment
|\theta_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{b}}|\sim\ell^{1/8}
is inconsistent with the dynamical alignment prediction
|\theta_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{b}}|\sim\ell^{1/4}

(
Boldyrev, 2006
;
Perez &
Boldyrev, 2009
)
. Moreover, based on the preference for
\mathbf{u}\propto\mathbf{b}\propto\mathbf{j}\propto\bm{\omega}
, we argue that on the modes where
\delta u/c_{s}<1
and
k>k_{\rm eq}
, it is plasma relaxation in local patches of the turbulence that gives rise to the weaker
\sim k^{-3/2}
spectrum, as has been conjectured previously, motivated by measurements of
\sim 1\;\text{AU}
regions of the solar wind and the terrestrial magnetosheath
(
Matthaeus et al., 2008
;
Osman et al., 2011
;
Pecora
et al., 2023
)
. This means that both the magnetic and kinetic energy cascades happen inhomogeneously through the plasma, and fastest along the boundary surfaces between aligned and anti-aligned
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{b}
regions. Understanding the size scale and stability of these relaxed regions
(
Hosking et al., 2020
, discussed previously in the appendix of )
will be important for determining the role of turbulence in all astrophysical systems, since a growing stable, relaxed region can halt the cascade completely.

As for
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)
, we find a single
\sim k^{-9/5}
cascade that is significantly different from the
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
cascades, and thus necessitates separate theoretical treatment
(
Grete
et al., 2021
)
, even on the scales where
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)
, as shown in Figure
3
(d). At a fundamental level, this means we cannot combine the velocity and magnetic fluctuations into a single nonlinear timescale. Indeed, different cascade exponents between the energy spectra have been found in the solar wind before, with the magnetic spectrum becoming as steep as
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)\sim k^{-2}
, which has been associated with intermittent structures in the plasma
(
Bowen et al., 2018
;
Dunn et al., 2023
)
. We want to further highlight that this spectrum is emergent on the scales that are relaxing faster than being perturbed by the turbulence (see Figure
6
), and hence we cannot ignore that
\sim k^{-9/5}
is likely a feature of the
\mathbf{b}\propto\pm\mathbf{j}
and
\mathbf{u}\propto\pm\mathbf{b}
relaxation states. With the two cascades in the kinetic energy, the single cascade in magnetic energy, the new scale-dependent alignment relations, and relaxation state results, this study describes a new paradigm for understanding supersonic MHD turbulence, which we hope stimulates many further fundamental, theoretical investigations.

Finally, the results in this study should be brought into the context of the saturation of the
\mathrm{Pm}\sim 1
fluctuation dynamo, which is, after all, what we are simulating in this high
\mathrm{Re}
and
\mathrm{Rm}
turbulent regime. Moreover, because our simulation resolves both super and subsonic
k
modes, and the dynamics in both of the regimes are scale separated, this simulation is very useful for understanding the saturation of the subsonic turbulent dynamo, where we can confidently say that any effect from the turbulent driving will be negligible due to the large scale separation between
k_{0}
and
k_{\rm s}
. Panel (d) in Figure
3
, shows that all of the subsonic
k
have a stationary state where
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)\gtrsim\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
, indicating that if there is enough scale separation between the driving and subsonic cascade, the subsonic modes should all reach a strongly magnetized state. This state is important for the dynamo saturation mechanism, because as the
k
modes become magnetized,
t_{\rm A}<t_{\rm turb}
, which means that
\mathbf{b}
can evolve faster than
\mathbf{u}
, and dynamically configure into a relaxed, aligned state, as we show in Figure
6
. This paints a picture for the saturation of the dynamo based upon local relaxation, that we will now describe, but with the intention to only describe the broad picture and leave the details for future works. Consider the kinematic dynamo, where
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}\ll\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}
. From
Kazantsev (1968)
theory, or even simple scaling arguments, the growth rate is associated with dynamical timescale on the viscous scale,
t_{\nu}\sim\ell_{\nu}^{2}/\nu\sim\ell_{\nu}/u_{\nu}

(
Kazantsev, 1968
;
Schekochihin et al., 2004
;
Galishnikova
et al., 2022
;
Kriel et al., 2022
;
Beattie et al., 2023a
)
. Once
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(\ell_{\nu})\sim\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(\ell_{\nu})
,
t_{\rm A}\sim t_{\nu}
on
\ell_{\nu}
, and there is a transition from the kinematic to the nonlinear dynamo regime. This implies
\delta u_{\nu}\sim\delta b_{\nu}
(where
\delta b_{\nu}
is the magnetic field fluctuation on
\ell_{\nu}
), and means that
\delta b_{\nu}
is able to relax to a state
\delta u_{\nu}\parallel\pm\delta b_{\nu}
. This turns off the magnetic flux generation on this scale, since
\nabla\times(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{b})
can be turned off geometrically if
\delta u_{\nu}\parallel\pm\delta b_{\nu}
. Now the next eddy on
\ell>\ell_{\nu}
is responsible for the magnetic flux generation, but as each successively larger scale grows and
\delta u_{\ell}\sim\delta b_{\ell}
, then that scale is able to relax into a
\delta u\parallel\pm\delta b
state, moving the magnetic flux generation to larger and large scales, until it is being sourced on the largest scales
\sim\ell_{\rm eq}
, where
\delta u
and
\delta b
are aligned the least. This is a dynamical process, happening on
t_{\rm A}
timescales, making the dynamo saturation process dynamical and not resistive. It requires no additional effective turbulent resistivity suppressing the growth
(
Schober
et al., 2015
;
Xu &
Lazarian, 2016
)
, which is inconsistent with direct measurements of the magnetic energy equation showing that resistivity terms decrease during saturation when compared to the kinematic stage
(
Seta &
Federrath, 2021
)
. The saturated state is therefore one that is aligned on small scales, with magnetic flux being generated on large scales, resulting in a classically structured turbulent magnetic spectrum, peaked at low
k
, as shown in Figure
3
, rather than a Kazantsev one.

4
Methods

4.1
Numerical simulations

Basic numerical code:

We use a heavily modified version of the magnetohydrodynamical (MHD) code
flash

(
Fryxell
et al., 2000
;
Dubey
et al., 2008
)
. Our code uses a highly-optimized, hybrid-precision
(
Federrath et al., 2021
)
, positivity-preserving, second-order MUSCL-Hancock HLL5R Riemann scheme
(
Bouchut
et al., 2010
;
Waagan et al., 2011
)
to solve the compressible, ideal, MHD fluid equations in three dimensions,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\mathbf{u}\right)=0,

(5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\!\left(\rho\mathbf{u}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\mathbf{u}\!\otimes\!\mathbf{u}+p\mathbb{I}-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\mathbf{b}\!\otimes\!\mathbf{b}\right)=\rho\bm{f},

(6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{b}+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{b}\otimes\mathbf{u})=0,

(7)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}=0,

(8)

\displaystyle p=c_{s}^{2}\rho+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{b},

(9)

where
\rho
,
\mathbf{u}
,
\mathbf{b}
and
\mu_{0}
are the gas density, the velocity and magnetic fields, and the magnetic permittivity, respectively. Equation
9
relates the scalar pressure
p
to
\rho
via the isothermal equation of state with constant sound speed
c_{s}
, as well as the pressure contribution from the magnetic field. We work in units
c_{s}=\rho_{0}=\mu_{0}=L=1
, where
\rho_{0}
is the mean gas density and
L
is the characteristic length scale of the system, such that
L^{3}=\mathcal{V}=1
is the volume. We discretize the equations over a triply periodic domain of
[-L/2,L/2]
in each dimension, with grid resolutions
2520^{3}
,
5040^{3}
and
10080^{3}
– the largest grids in the world for simulations of this fluid turbulence regime. In order to drive turbulence, a turbulent forcing term
\bm{f}
is applied in the momentum equation (details below). This set of equations including the forcing term is the standard approach in modeling driven, magnetized turbulence. These calculations were only possible as part of a large-scale high performance computing project, large-scale project 10391, at Leibniz Supercomputing Centre in Garching, Germany. They were run on the supercomputer SuperMUC-NG. For the
10080^{3}
simulation, and the power-spectrum calculations, we utilized close to 140,000 compute cores, and close to 80M-core hours.

Turbulent driving:

We choose to drive the turbulence with a turbulent Mach number of
\delta u/c_{s}\approx 4
to ensure that we resolve a sufficient range of both supersonic
\delta u>c_{s}
and subsonic
\delta u<c_{s}
scales
(
Federrath et al., 2021
)
. We apply a non-helical stochastic forcing term
\bm{f}
in Equation
6
, following an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck stochastic process
(
Eswaran &
Pope, 1988
;
Schmidt et al., 2009
;
Federrath
et al., 2010
)
, using the
TurbGen
turbulent forcing module
(
Federrath
et al., 2010
;
Federrath
et al., 2022
)
. The forcing is constructed in Fourier space such that kinetic energy is injected at the smallest wavenumbers, peaking at
\ell_{0}^{-1}=k_{0}L/2\pi=2
and tending to zero parabolically in the interval
1\leq kL/2\pi\leq 3
, allowing for self-consistent development of turbulence on smaller scales,
kL/2\pi>3
, as routinely performed in turbulence box studies. To replenish the large-scale compressible modes and shocks, we decompose
\bm{f}
into its incompressible (
\nabla\cdot\bm{f}=0
) and compressible (
\nabla\times\bm{f}=0
) modes
(
Federrath
et al., 2010
)
, and drive the turbulence with equal amounts of energy in each of the modes.

Initial conditions and hierarchical interpolation:

We initialize
\rho(x,y,z)=\rho_{0}
and
\mathbf{u}=\bm{0}
. The total magnetic field
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}_{0}+\mathbf{b}_{\rm turb}
is composed of both a mean (external or guide) field
\mathbf{b}_{0}
and turbulent
\mathbf{b}_{\rm turb}
component. The
\mathbf{b}_{\rm turb}
evolves self-consistently with the MHD turbulence via Equation
7
, whereas
\mathbf{b}_{0}
remains constant throughout the simulation due to magnetic flux conservation. For our simulations,
\mathbf{b}_{0}=0
, and only the isotropic, turbulent magnetic field remains,
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}_{\rm turb}
. Regardless of the initial field amplitude, the same small-scale dynamo saturation is reached
(
Beattie et al., 2023b
)
. The same also holds for different seed magnetic fields
(
Seta &
Federrath, 2020
)
. Hence, given enough integration time, we can initialize a magnetic field with any initial structure and amplitude and be confident that it will result in the same saturation. Such that we do not use computational resources on the fast or nonlinear dynamo stages
(
Federrath, 2016
;
Rincon, 2019
)
, we initialize the magnetic field amplitude and structure close to the saturated state. From our previous experiments at lower resolutions, this is
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}/\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}\approx 1/4
(or
\mathcal{M}_{\rm A}\approx 2
, where
\mathcal{M}_{\rm A}=\delta u/\delta v_{\rm A}
is the Alfvén Mach number), and with a significant amount of power at all
k
. We find that a uniform initial
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)
, with sufficiently high
k
modes included relaxes very quickly to the saturated dynamo state. Hence we use a simple, isotropic uniform top-hat spectrum within
1\leq kL/2\pi\leq 50
, which generates as a divergence-free, static, random field using
TurbGen

(
Federrath
et al., 2010
;
Federrath
et al., 2022
)
, where the total integral of the spectrum is set such that the magnetic energy is
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}\approx\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}/4
, and
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}=\rho_{0}\delta u^{2}/2
is estimated directly from the driving.

Driven MHD turbulence in this regime takes
\approx(1-2)t_{0}
, where
t_{0}=\ell/\delta u
is the turbulent turnover time on the outer scale, to shed the influence of its initial conditions and establish a stationary state
(
Federrath
et al., 2010
;
Price &
Federrath, 2010
;
Beattie et al., 2022b
)
. To avoid expending compute resources on simulating this transient state, we only apply the previously discussed initial conditions to the
2520^{3}
simulation. For the remaining
5040^{3}
and
10080^{3}
simulations, we interpolate the initial conditions hierarchically from the simulations with lower resolutions, i.e., we initialize the
5040^{3}
simulation with linearly interpolated initial conditions from the
t\approx 2t_{0}
state of the
2520^{3}
simulation and the
t\approx 3t_{0}
state of the
5040^{3}
simulation for the
10080^{3}
simulation. We use linear interpolation to preserve
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}=0
between grid interpolations. It takes a tiny fraction of
t_{0}
,
t\sim\mathrm{Re}^{-1/2}t_{0}
, to populate the new modes after the interpolation onto the higher-resolution grid. Hence this provides an adequate method for minimizing the amount of compute time spent making the
5040^{3}
and
10080^{3}
simulations stationary.

Estimating the Reynolds numbers:

Our numerical model is an implicit large eddy simulation (ILES), which relies upon the spatial discretisation to supply the numerical viscosity and resistivity as a fluid closure model. Recently, a detailed characterization of our code’s numerical viscous and resistive properties, specifically for turbulent boxes, has been performed by comparing the ILES model with direct numerical simulations (DNS), which have explicit viscous and resistive operators in Equation
6
and Equation

, where

and
N_{\mathrm{Re}}\in[0.8,4.4]
and

,
5040
and

,
\mathrm{Re}_{5\rm k}\in[5\times 10^{5},1.3\times 10^{6}]
,
\mathrm{Re}_{10k}\in[1.4\times 10^{6},5.3\times 10^{6}]
and
\mathrm{Rm}_{2\rm k}\in[1.9\times 10^{5},4.9\times 10^{5}]

5040

10080

File I/O:

flash
is parallelised with
mpi
. File I/O is based on the
hdf

138,\!240
cores (3024 compute nodes on SuperMUC-NG) and produce approximately
100

each (approximately

in total), efficient file I/O is extremely important. In order to achieve the highest efficiency when reading and writing these huge files, we use parallel-
hdf

This proved to be an extremely efficient method, providing an I/O throughput that is close to the physical maximum of approximately

reachable on the SuperMUC-NG

Major code optimizations:

Our version of
flash
is highly optimized for solving large-scale hydrodynamical and MHD problems
(
Federrath et al., 2021
)
. Specifically, the number of stored 3D fields are reduced to the bare minimum required for these simulations (only the gas density and three velocity and magnetic field components are stored). All calls to the equation of state routines are performed inline, directly in the Riemann solver. The code is precision hybridized such that all fluid variables are stored in single precision (4 bytes per floating-point number), but critical operations are performed in double-precision arithmetic (8 bytes per floating-point number), which retains the accuracy of the full double-precision computations. These efforts significantly reduce the computational time and the required amount of
mpi
communication, as well as the overall memory consumption. In addition, the single-precision operations benefit from a higher SIMD count and lower cache occupancy, for a further parallel speedup. As a result the code is almost
4\times
faster and requires
4.1\times
less memory than the
flash
public release, while retaining the full accuracy. Previous studies have further characterized the performance and comparison of our code with the public
flash
version
(
Cielo et al., 2020
)
.

4.2
Definition of energy spectra and turbulent scales

Energy spectra:

The magnetic energy spectrum is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)=\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\int\operatorname{d}\!{\Omega_{k}}\;\tilde{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{k})\tilde{\mathbf{b}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{k})4\pi k^{2},

(10)

and kinetic energy spectrum is defined as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)=\frac{\rho_{0}}{2}\int\operatorname{d}\!{\Omega_{k}}\;\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{k})\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{k})4\pi k^{2},

(11)

where the tilde indicates the Fourier transform of the underlying field variable, dagger the complex conjugate and the
\int\operatorname{d}\!{\Omega_{k}}4\pi k^{2}
is the shell integral over fixed
k
shells, producing isotropic, one-dimensional energy spectra. Note that other definitions of the kinetic energy spectrum have been used in the literature, which define
\mathbf{w}=\sqrt{\rho}\mathbf{u}
and then take the square Fourier transform of
\mathbf{w}
to construct the kinetic energy spectrum
(
Federrath
et al., 2010
;
Kritsuk
et al., 2007
;
Federrath, 2013
;
Grete et al., 2017
;
Grete
et al., 2020
;
Grete
et al., 2023
)
. However, we pick the simplest definition of
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
to allow us to more easily compare with theories of incompressible turbulence
(
Iroshnikov, 1964
;
Kraichnan, 1965
;
Goldreich &
Sridhar, 1995
;
Boldyrev, 2006
)
, and even compressible theories
(
Bhattacharjee
et al., 1998
;
Lithwick &
Goldreich, 2001
;
Federrath et al., 2021
)
, which adopt the same
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
definition as we do in this study.

Inner and outer scales:

We use the following definitions for the inner and outer scales of the turbulent cascades, using
u
and
b
superscripts to differentiate between the kinetic energy and magnetic energy scales, respectively. For both the
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)
and
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
, the outer scale is directly related to the integral or correlation scale of the energy spectrum,

\displaystyle\frac{k_{\rm cor}L}{2\pi}=\frac{\displaystyle\int\operatorname{d}\!{k}\,\mathcal{E}(k)}{\displaystyle\int\operatorname{d}\!{k}\,(kL/2\pi)^{-1}\mathcal{E}(k)},

(12)

which for
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
closely tracks the driving scale
k_{\rm cor}^{u}\sim k_{0}
, but for
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)
depends on a range of parameters, like the growth stage of the dynamo and the strength of the large-scale magnetic field
(
Beattie
et al., 2022a
;
Beattie et al., 2023b
)
. For our
10080^{3}
simulation,
k_{\rm cor}
of the magnetic field is
k_{\rm cor}^{b}L/2\pi\sim 10
, highlighting how the magnetic field is intrinsically a small-scale field. For the inner scale we take the maximum of the
(kL/2\pi)^{2}\mathcal{E}(k)
spectrum,

\displaystyle\frac{k_{\rm u}L}{2\pi}=\left(\frac{\max\left\{(kL/2\pi)^{2}\mathcal{E}(k)\operatorname{d}\!{k}\right\}}{\displaystyle\int\operatorname{d}\!{k}\,\mathcal{E}(k)}\right)^{1/2},

(13)

which probes the smallest scale of the magnetic and velocity gradients, since, e.g. for the velocity,
k^{2}\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\operatorname{d}\!{k}\sim k^{2}u^{2}\sim(\nabla\otimes\mathbf{u})^{2}
, defining the end of the turbulent cascade and the start of diffusion-dominated scales. We show both of these scales in panel (a) and (c) in Figure
3
.

The sonic scale:

The first transition that we investigate is the
k
-space sonic scale, denoted as
k_{\rm s}L/2\pi
. Using Parseval’s theorem, we calculate the rms velocity as a function of scale through the relation

\displaystyle\frac{\delta u(k)}{c_{s}}=\left(\frac{2}{\rho_{0}}\int^{\infty}_{k}\operatorname{d}\!{k^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k^{\prime})\right)^{1/2},

(14)

where
k_{\rm s}L/2\pi
is the
k
mode where
\delta u(k_{\rm s})=c_{s}

(
Federrath
et al., 2010
;
Federrath &
Klessen, 2012
;
Federrath et al., 2021
)
. We present this calculation in Figure
9
and determine the
k_{\rm s}L/2\pi
root for the sonic transition

\displaystyle\delta u(k)/c_{s}-1=0,

(15)

which is indicated by the dashed line. For the
10080^{3}
simulation, we find
k_{\rm s}L/2\pi=32\pm 2
, for
5040^{3}
,
k_{\rm s}L/2\pi=29\pm 2
and
2520^{3}
,
k_{\rm s}L/2\pi=24\pm 2
. For
k>k_{\rm s}
, the plasma becomes subsonic with
\delta u(k)/c_{s}<1
, and for
k<k_{\rm s}
, the plasma is supersonic with
\delta u(k)/c_{s}>1
. This is marked by the horizontal black dashed line. As previously found, there is a smooth transition between these two flow regimes rather than a sharp discontinuity
(
Federrath et al., 2021
)
.

Energy equipartition scale:

The second transition that we study is the transition from kinetic energy dominated turbulence,
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)>\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)
to magnetic energy dominated turbulence,
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)>\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
, which is equivalent to comparing the turbulent Alfvén timescale,
t_{\rm A}=\ell/\delta v_{\rm A}(\ell)
, where
\delta v_{\rm A}
is the rms Alfvén velocity of the plasma, with the turbulent velocity timescale
t_{\rm turb}=\ell/\delta u(\ell)
. If
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)>\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)
, then
t_{\rm turb}<t_{\rm A}
and vice versa for
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)<\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)
. For strong guide field turbulence, this has been previously called the MHD scale
(
Goldreich &
Sridhar, 1995
;
Lithwick &
Goldreich, 2001
)
, but we use the more general energy equipartition scale nomenclature,
k_{\rm eq}L/2\pi
, as in the main text. Even though comparing these timescales looks like a calculation about critical balance, since both timescales are describing intrinsically nonlinear fluctuations, this is not a probe for weak versus strong turbulence
(
Perez &
Boldyrev, 2008
;
Meyrand
et al., 2016
)
. To determine
k_{\rm eq}L/2\pi
we find the root of
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)/\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)-1=0
. We plot the full
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)/\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)
spectrum and
k_{\rm eq}L/2\pi
mode in panel (d) of Figure
3
. For the
10080^{3}
simulation, we find
k_{\rm eq}L/2\pi=10.6\pm 0.7
, for
5040^{3}
,
k_{\rm eq}L/2\pi=11.9\pm 0.7
and
2520^{3}
,
k_{\rm eq}L/2\pi=13.1\pm 0.6
.

Definitions of scale-dependent alignment structure functions

To compute the scale-dependent alignment structure function shown in Figure

(d), we first define our increments,

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})-\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}+\bm{\ell}),

(16)

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{r})-\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{r}+\bm{\ell}),

(17)

for separation vector
\bm{\ell}
. Next, we define a local mean magnetic field direction,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{\bm{\ell}}=\frac{\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{r})+\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{r}+\bm{\ell})}{\|\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{r})+\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{r}+\bm{\ell})\|},

(18)

and then find the perpendicular component to the local field for each of the fluid variables, e.g. for
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{b}
,

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}\mathbf{u}_{\perp}

\displaystyle=\bm{\delta}\mathbf{u}-(\bm{\delta}\mathbf{u}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{\bm{\ell}})\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{\bm{\ell}},

(19)

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}\mathbf{b}_{\perp}

\displaystyle=\bm{\delta}\mathbf{b}-(\bm{\delta}\mathbf{b}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{\bm{\ell}})\widehat{\mathbf{b}}_{\bm{\ell}},

(20)

which is the standard definition for these quantities
(
Chernoglazov et al., 2021
;
Dong
et al., 2022
)
. Next we construct the ratio between first-order structure functions,

\displaystyle|\theta_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{b}}(\ell)|\sim|\sin\theta_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{b}}(\ell)|=\frac{\left\langle{|\bm{\delta}\mathbf{u}_{\perp}\times\bm{\delta}\mathbf{b}_{\perp}|}\right\rangle_{\ell}}{\left\langle{|\bm{\delta}\mathbf{u}_{\perp}||\bm{\delta}\mathbf{b}_{\perp}|}\right\rangle_{\ell}}.

(21)

We do this for each relaxation variable pair,
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{b}
,
\mathbf{j}
and
\mathbf{b}
, and lastly
\bm{\omega}
and
\mathbf{u}
. We use
2\times 10^{12}
sampling pairs to ensure that the structure functions are converged at all scales
(
Federrath et al., 2021
)
. Furthermore, we construct the structure functions across a number of realizations in the stationary state, and then time-average the structure function to produce Figure

5.1
Basics principles of classical, global plasma relaxation

A relaxed plasma is one that is in a minimum energy state for a given set of constraints. For an ideal MHD plasma, the constraints, which are the ideal quadratic invariants of the plasma, are the cross helicity,
H_{c}=\left\langle{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{b}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}
, magnetic helicity
H_{m}=\left\langle{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}
and the total energy
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}+\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}
, where
\mathbf{b}=\nabla\times\mathbf{a}
is the magnetic vector potential and
\mathcal{V}
the volume. Finding the minimum energy state is then akin to using the variational principle to find the minima of the energy functional,

\displaystyle\delta\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\iiint\operatorname{d}\!{\mathcal{V}}\;\;\left[\mathcal{E}-2\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{b})-2\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b})\right]\right)=0,

(22)

where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are the Lagrangian multipliers and
\delta
is the variational operator. The Lagrange multipliers are completely determined by the initial values of the constraints. Constructing
\mathcal{E}
directly from Equation
6
-
7
, one can solve the above equation to show that the minimum energy state is,

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\lambda_{2}\mathbf{b},\quad\quad\mathbf{j}=\frac{(1-\lambda_{2})^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}\mathbf{b},\quad\quad\mathbf{u}=\frac{(1-\lambda_{2})^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}\bm{\omega},

(23)

(
Pecora
et al., 2023
)
. Depending on the sign of
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, the minimum energy state for fixed
H_{c}
and
H_{m}
is the alignment or anti-alignment of
\mathbf{u}
,
\mathbf{b}
,
\mathbf{j}
and
\bm{\omega}
. Note that this is a global minimization, over the whole volume
\mathcal{V}
, which we show here as the
modus operandi
for problems of this nature. Alignment of these vectors matter
(
Banerjee
et al., 2023
)
. Alignment between
\mathbf{u}
and
\bm{\omega}
reduces the nonlinearity that gives rise to the hydrodynamical turbulence, because
\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\otimes\mathbf{u}=\nabla u^{2}/2+\mathbf{u}\times\bm{\omega}
. For incompressible, strong Alfvénic turbulence models
(
Boldyrev, 2006
;
Perez &
Boldyrev, 2009
)
, the nonlinear timescale of the turbulence becomes slower in the presence of
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{b}
alignment, and the nonlinearity parameter, weaker
(
Mallet et al., 2015
;
Chernoglazov et al., 2021
)
. The alignment between
\mathbf{j}
and
\mathbf{b}
suppresses the Lorentz force
\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{b}
, creating force-free, Taylor relaxed states. For the turbulent dynamo, the magnetic energy flux is sourced from the induction term in Equation
7
,
\nabla\times(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{b})=\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{b}\otimes\mathbf{u})
, and hence both the generation and maintenance of a magnetic field is sensitive to the scale-dependent alignment of
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{b}
.

As we show in Figure
5
, the one-point probability density functions of the angles between
\mathbf{u}
,
\mathbf{b}
,
\mathbf{j}
and
\bm{\omega}
variables show a strong preference for aligned, relaxed states. In Figure S5 we show a two-dimensional slice of the underlying angle fields, revealing the structure that gives rise to one-point probability density functions. Similar to the magnetosheath discussed in
(
Pecora
et al., 2023
)
, we find that the turbulence “cellularises” into locally relaxed patches, unlike what we have described above, which is a global relaxed state. The outer scale of the patches changing for the different variables, and hence the relations derived above will not strictly hold for our plasma. The largest scale patches are in
\theta_{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{b}}
, which have fluctuations on scales of order
\ell_{\rm eq}/L
, which we directly annotate on the plot. This is completely consistent with what we have presented in the main text, where scales below
\ell_{\rm eq}/L
are able to relax faster than be perturbed out of relaxation from the turbulence, shown in Figure
6
, and that it is scales below
\ell_{\rm eq}/L
where the
\mathcal{E}_{\rm kin}(k)\sim k^{-3/2}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\rm mag}(k)\sim k^{-9/5}
spectra emerge, which we argue is due to relaxation. Both the other angle fields exhibit much smaller-scale features.
