Measurement incompatibility signature cannot be stochastically distilled
Huan-Yu Ku, Chung-Yun Hsieh, Costantino Budroni

Abstract
We show that the incompatibility of a set of measurements cannot be increased by subjecting them to a filter, namely, by combining them with a device that post-selects the incoming states on a fixed outcome of a stochastic transformation. This result holds for several measures of incompatibility, such as those based on robustness and convex weight. Expanding these ideas to Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen steering experiments, we are able to solve the problem of the maximum steerability obtained with respect to the most general local filters in a way that allows for an explicit calculation of the filter operation. Moreover, our results generalize to nonphysical maps, i.e., positive but not completely positive linear maps.

Introduction

A clear answer can identify the cost of preparing incompatibility as well as uncover the fundamental limit on manipulating incompatibility.

Preliminary notions

In quantum mechanics, a measurement is a procedure that generates a classical outcome with a certain probability for any input state. In abstract terms, we can denote a measurement procedure as a mapping from quantum states to probability distributions, namely,

\mathcal{M}:\rho\mapsto\{p(a)\}_{a}.

(1)

Clearly,
\{p(a)\}_{a}
satisfies
p(a)\geq 0
for all
a
and
\sum_{a}p(a)=1
. Moreover, this mapping must be linear

, since if I consider a probabilistic mixtures of states
\rho=\lambda\rho_{1}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{2}

0<\lambda<1

By Frechét-Riesz theorem, any continuous linear functional
\varphi

\phi

\varphi(\psi)=\langle\phi|\psi\rangle

\langle A,B\rangle=\tr[A^{\dagger}B]

, namely,

p(a)=\tr[M_{a}\rho],

(2)

To satisfy the positivity and normalization constraints for

M_{a}\geq 0
for all

\sum_{a}M_{a}=\openone

Heinosaari and Ziman 2011

Note that, in this construction and in particular in the derivation of the Born rule in Eq. (
2

\rho\mapsto p(a)
. When the mapping is not linear, as it is the case for experiment with postselection, e.g., filtering, the Born rule and, consequently, the representation of a measurement as a POVM is no longer guarantee. We will come back to this point.

In the general case of multiple measurements, a fundamental notion is that of
measurement incompatibility
. The incompatibility of quantum measurements refers to the impossibility of accessing experimental outcomes simultaneously e.g., the position and momentum of a quantum system. Considering multiple inputs
x
, so we have a collection of POVMs
\vec{M}:=\{M_{a|x}\}_{a,x}
, which we call a
measurement assemblage
(MA). If there exists a single POVM
\{G_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
that can fully characterize the set of POVMs by classical post-processing, the MA is said to be jointly measurable (JM) i.e.,
M_{a|x}=\sum_{\lambda}p(a|x,\lambda)G_{\lambda}
, where
p(a|x,\lambda)
is a conditional probability. If there is no such description, the measurement assemblage is said to be
incompatible
. Incompatibility can be quantified in different ways, for instance via the

incompatibility robustness

Uola et al. 2015
;
Haapasalo 2015
defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{IR}(\vec{M})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\{t\geq 0\;|\;\exists\vec{N}~\text{MA and}~\vec{D}\in\mathbb{JM}\ \text{s.t.}

(3)

\displaystyle(M_{a|x}+tN_{a|x})/(1+t)=D_{a|x}\ \forall~a,x\},

where
\mathbb{JM}
denotes the set of JM measurement assemblages. Several other measures can be introduced by putting restrictions on the MA
\vec{N}
; see, e.g., Ref.
Designolle et al. 2019
, or using different convex decompositions
Pusey 2015
.

Steering is a protocol in which one party, Alice, performs measurements
\vec{A}=\{A_{a|x}\}_{a,x}
on her half
of a bipartite system in the initial state
\rho_{\rm AB}
and sends the measurement information, i.e., input
x

and output
a
, to Bob. In this way, Bob obtains a collection of local states labeled as

\vec{\sigma}:=\{\sigma_{a|x}\}_{a,x}
, where
\sigma_{a|x}=\tr_{\rm A}[\rho_{\rm AB}(A_{a|x}\otimes\openone)]
is in an unnormalized form. We call
\vec{\sigma}
a state assemblage (SA). It satisfies

\sigma_{a|x}\geq 0,\forall a,x
and
\sum_{a}\sigma_{a|x}=\rho_{\rm B}=\tr_{A}[\rho_{\rm AB}],\forall x
. A SA
is
unsteerable
if there is a
local-hidden-state
(LHS) model, namely, if we can write

\sigma_{a|x}=\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)p(a|x,\lambda)\rho_{\lambda},\forall a,x
, and
steerable
otherwise.
Steering can be quantified, for instance, by the
steering robustness

Piani and Watrous 2015
defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{SR}(\vec{\sigma})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\{t\geq 0\;|\;\exists\;\vec{\xi}\;\text{SA and},\;\vec{\tau}\in\mathbb{LHS}~\text{s.t.}

(4)

\displaystyle(\sigma_{a|x}+t\xi_{a|x})/(1+t)=\tau_{a|x}\ \forall~a,x\}

where
\mathbb{LHS}
is the set of SA admitting a LHS model.

The SA
\vec{\sigma}
is unsteerable, whenever
\rho_{\rm AB}
is separable or
\vec{A}
is JM, i.e., both entanglement and measurement incompatibility are necessary for steering. This connection is indeed much deeper
Uola et al. 2014
;
Quintino et al. 2014
and steering and incompatibility can be seen as equivalent problems
Uola et al. 2015
. A central notion in this correspondence is that of
steering-equivalent-observables
(SEOs)
Uola et al. 2015
which map a SA to a MA as

B_{a|x}:=\rho_{\rm B}^{{-1/2}}\sigma_{a|x}\rho_{\rm B}^{{-1/2}},\text{ for }\rho_{\rm B}\text{ full-rank,}

(5)

or an analogous expression with an additional isometry in case
\rho_{\rm B}
is not full rank; see Ref.
Uola et al. 2015
for more details. It is straightforward to see that SEOs define equivalence classes among SAs, i.e., via the relation of having the same SEO. These equivalence classes were recently studied from the perspective of local filters
Ku et al. 2022a
.
We recall that a filter corresponds, operationally, to performing a measurement and post-selecting on a specific outcome.
Mathematically, this operation is represented by a completely positive (CP) and trace-nonincreasing (TNI) map,
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
, followed by a post-selection of the state:

\rho\mapsto\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\rho)}{\tr[\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\rho)]}.

(6)

Crucially, the filter’s action on quantum states must include a post-selection, which leads to a nonlinear transformation. This is unavoidable and it plays a central role at the moment of defining a notion of
filtered measuremet
, as we discuss below.
Physically,
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
can be viewed as part of a bigger measurement
\{\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\}_{\omega}
, which is a
quantum instrument
(each
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
is CP and
\sum_{\omega}\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
is CPTP), and we use
\omega
to specify the measurement outcome.
Importantly, it was shown in Ref.
Ku et al. 2022a
that such classes also define which SAs can be transformed into each other via
{\rm LF_{1}}
operations
, which are filters of the form
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\cdot)=K(\cdot)K^{\dagger}
with
K^{\dagger}K\leq\openone
, i.e., local filters consisting of only one Kraus operator.
Moreover, the maximal steerability within one class corresponds to the incompatibility of the SEO, namely,

\max_{\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\in{\rm LF_{1}}}\mathbb{SR}\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\vec{\sigma})}{p(\omega)}\right)=\max_{\rho_{\rm B}}\mathbb{SR}\left(\rho_{\rm B}^{{1/2}}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm B}^{{1/2}}\right)=\mathbb{IR}(\vec{B}),

(7)

where
\vec{B}
is the SEO of
\vec{\sigma}
,
\omega
represents the outcome on which one post-selects with probability
p(\omega)=\tr[\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\sum_{a}\sigma_{a|x})]
;
see Appendix A.
In the following, when we talk about steerability (incompatibility) we implicitly refer to the steerability (incompatibility) robustness, unless explicitly stated otherwise.
This is indeed the most widely used steering measure, from which experimentally testable steering inequalities can be derived
Cavalcanti and Skrzypczyk 2016a
;
Hsieh et al. 2016
;
Ku et al. 2018
;
Tendick et al. 2023
. We thus focus on it first, but our results generalize to other measures, as we discuss later on.

III
Filtering measurements and operationally quantifying their incompatibility

The first step to derive our result is to reason what it means to
filter
a measurement.
In the Schrödinger picture, filtering a state
\rho
corresponds to the application of a CPTNI map
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
, as
\rho\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\rho)/\text{tr}[\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\rho)]
. We emphasize, again, that the non-linear transformation is necessary. In contrast in the Heisenberg picture, the direct application of the adjoint map on a POVM
\vec{M}
, i.e.,
\{\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}_{\omega}(M_{a})\}_{a}
, is not possible, as this provides an object that is not properly normalized
\sum_{a}\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}_{\omega}(M_{a})\neq\openone
.
In order to derive the correct mathematical representation for the filtered measurement, one has to start from the physical procedure of a measurement in quantum mechanics.
In the lab, we perform the filter between the state preparation
\rho
and the measurement
\{M_{a}\}_{a}
. When the correct outcome
\omega
is not observed, we discard all experimental rounds.
The analog of Eq. (
1
) for the case of both filter and measurement, then, can be written as

\rho\mapsto\{p(a)\}_{a}=\left\{\frac{\text{tr}[M_{a}\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\rho)]}{\text{tr}[\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\rho)]}\right\}_{a}=\left\{\frac{\tr[\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}_{\omega}(M_{a})\rho]}{\tr[\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}_{\omega}(\openone)\rho]}\right\}_{a},

(8)

associating to arbitrary state
\rho
a probability
p(a)
.
This expression can be seen as the generation of the Born’s rule when post-selection is involved, since when
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
is CPTP, it returns to the standard Born’s rule. Note that Eq. (
8
) gives a unique and state-independent definition of filtered measurement.
The nonlinearity of the map is unavoidable, as the measurement procedure involves a post-selection on the filter being passed. In particular, this implies that the mapping between states and probabilities induced by a filtered measurement can no longer be mathematically described by linear operators (POVMs) in the trace expression, as in Eq. (
2
) (see also the explicit discussion of non-POVM property of a filtered measurement by Arai and Hayashi
Arai and Hayashi 2024
).

A natural question is, then, how to define incompatibility for filtered measurements, if they can no longer be described by POVMs. For this, reasoning in terms of operations in the lab turns out to be crucial. More precisely, we can reason in terms of quantum information tasks for which incompatibility is a resource and ask the question:
can we improve the performances of our measurement devices by applying a filter?
An affirmative answer denotes an enhancement of the resource, i.e., measurement incompatibility. One of such tasks is quantum steering
Uola et al. 2014
;
Quintino et al. 2014
;
Uola et al. 2015
. In particular, it has been shown
Ku et al. 2022a
that, for a given MA
\vec{A}
,
\mathbb{IR}
can be operationally characterized in a steering scenario as:

\mathbb{IR}(\vec{A})=\max_{\rho_{\rm AB}}\ \mathbb{SR}(\vec{\sigma}(\rho_{\rm AB})),

(9)

with
\{\sigma_{a|x}(\rho_{\rm AB})\}_{a,x}
the SA obtained by measuring
\vec{A}
on the bipartite state
\rho_{\rm AB}
; see Appendix A for details. In simple terms, the incompatibility of measurements is understood as the maximal steerability that can be obtained when applying such measurement to a bipartite quantum state. With such an operational characterization, not directly involving the notion of POVM for the filtered measurement, we can proceed to prove our main result.

IV
Measurement incompatibility cannot be stochastically distilled

According to Eq. (
9
), incompatibility is the maximum steerability over all bipartite states. Then, applying the definition
of filtered measurement, i.e., Eq. (
8
), to the steering scenario, we transform the SA

\sigma_{a|x}(\rho_{\rm AB})\mapsto\sigma_{a|x}(\rho^{\prime}_{\rm AB})=\frac{\tr_{\rm A}[(A_{a|x}\otimes\openone)(\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\otimes\openone)\rho_{\rm AB}]}{{\rm tr}[(\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\otimes\openone)\rho_{\rm AB}]}

(10)

The effect of the filter is then to modify the initial state
\rho_{\rm AB}\mapsto\rho^{\prime}_{\rm AB}
. Since we are considering
\max_{\rho_{\rm AB}}
, this can never increase the value of the right-hand side of Eq (
9
).
We thus obtained

Result 1
.

Measurement incompatibility cannot be increased by filters;
i.e., it cannot be stochastically distilled.

This result requires a recently proposed operational approach to quantify measurement incompatibility
Ku et al. 2022a
: measurement incompatibility is equivalent to optimal steering distillation under local filters.
Notice that, even if we use a steering scenario to describe and quantify measurement incompatibility operationally, the no-go Result
1
is independent of the specific task chosen. For instance, the same argument applies when defining
incompatibility robustness in terms of state discrimination tasks. This evolves a more complicated structure due to the nonlinearity in the definition
Skrzypczyk et al. 2019
;
Buscemi et al. 2020
; see Appendix C.

Moreover, analogous arguments apply to different scenarios. First, one can show that the incompatible weight
Pusey 2015
, which has an operational meaning in both quantum steering and state-exclusion tasks
Uola et al. 2020a
;
Ducuara and Skrzypczyk 2020
, cannot be distilled by filtering.
Second, a similar no-go result hold when there is a single measurement involved, i.e., independently of incompatibility. we show that our argument is also valid for
an informativeness measurement
(a property of a
single
measurement). This is the case of the
robustness of measurement

Skrzypczyk and Linden 2019
, i.e., the minimal amount of noise that needs to be added to the given single measurement before it becomes trivial and can be operationally defined via state-discrimination tasks. Also in this case, we show that it cannot be increased by filtering (see Appendix C).
We thus uncover a series of general no-go results for measurement resources under filters.

Our results imply that once
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
is a positive linear map, it cannot distill incompatibility.
Hence, at a mathematical level, our no-go result also rules out various non-physical linear maps, such as positive linear maps that are not CP or trace-increasing
(in the latter case, the physical interpretation is more problematic since one cannot make sense of the notion of post-selection and the associated renormalization of probability).
Finally, our result provides a clear physical insight: multi-copy measurement settings are essential for distilling measurement resources. In fact, the use of multiple copies of a measurement device has also been recently discussed in relation to simulating a target measurement using multiple imperfect copies
Linden and Skrzypczyk 2022
;
Kim et al. 2024
;
Corlett et al. 2024
.

V
Filtering operations on quantum steering

Here, we discuss the effect of positive linear maps, which we later on interpret as filters, on the different elements of a SA. The goal is to show that local filters described by a single Kraus operator, denoted by
{\rm LF_{1}}
, are always enough to obtain the maximum steerability over all filter operations, denoted by
{\rm LF}
.
We start with the following simple observation:

Observation 1
.

For a reduced state
\rho_{\rm B}
such that
\mathbb{SR}(\rho_{\rm B}^{{1/2}}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm B}^{{1/2}})=\mathbb{IR}(\vec{B})
, as in Eq. (
7
), we can choose
\vec{\xi}
and
\vec{\tau}
appearing in the definition of
\mathbb{SR}(\rho_{\rm B}^{{1/2}}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm B}^{{1/2}})
such that their reduced states coincide with
\rho_{\rm B}
, i.e.,
\sum_{a}\xi_{a|x}=\sum_{a}\tau_{a|x}=\rho_{\rm B}
.

We detailed the proof in Appendix D.
This observation suggests that the definitions of
\mathbb{IR}
and
\mathbb{SR}
are completely equivalent when we consider the maximal element in an SEO class. Moreover, this observation implies that the
consistent steering robustness

\mathbb{SR}^{\rm c}(\vec{\sigma})

Cavalcanti and Skrzypczyk 2016b
(a robustness with the requirement that the noise term
\vec{\xi}
has the same reduced state as the original SA) quantifies the maximal steerability over the SEO class of
\vec{\sigma}
, or equivalently, over all possible SA that can be reached via local filters. Indeed, it has been shown that
\mathbb{SR}^{\rm c}(\vec{\sigma})=\mathbb{IR}(\vec{B})
, see
Chen et al. 2016
;
Chen et al. 2018
.

From Obs.
1
, another useful result follows:

Result 2
.

Given an MA
\vec{B}
and a positive map
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SR}\left(\frac{\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\mathcal{E}_{\omega}^{\dagger}(\vec{B})\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}}{p(\omega)}\right)\leq\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SR}\left(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right),\end{split}

(11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SR}\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2})}{p(\omega)}\right)\leq\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SR}\left(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right),\end{split}

(12)

where
p(\omega)=\tr[\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\rho_{\rm{B}})]
.

See Appendix E for the proof.
So far, this result remains at some abstract level. For instance, notice that
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
is not necessarily a physical map, i.e., CP and trace-non-increasing, but just a positive linear map.
To see its physical significance, (in Appendix F) we show that
{\rm LF_{1}}
are enough to obtain maximum steerability:

Result 3
.

The maximal steerability obtainable by
{\rm LF_{1}}
filtering, is the same as the one obtainable with general filters in
{\rm LF}
, namely,

\max_{\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\in{\rm LF_{1}}}\mathbb{SR}\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\vec{\sigma})}{p(\omega)}\right)=\max_{\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\in{\rm LF}}\mathbb{SR}\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\vec{\sigma})}{p(\omega)}\right).

(13)

This result completes the classification of steerability with respect to local operations presented in Ref.
Ku et al. 2022a
: the maximal steerability obtainable with
{\rm LF_{1}}
filters
is the same as the one obtainable with general
{\rm LF}
filters.
This completely solves the problem of finding optimal local filters for stochastic steering distillation, since the optimal LF1 can be efficiently computed by a semi-definite program (SDP)
Ku et al. 2022a
;
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
;
Skrzypczyk and Cavalcanti 2023
.
Notice that
{\rm LF}
filters are able to change the SEO class of the SA, contrary to what happens with two-way
{\rm LF_{1}}

Ku et al. 2022a
, and that Result
3
holds for any positive linear map
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
.
Finally, it seems that Res.
3
can be obtained by decomposing
{\rm LF}
filters individually and use the optimal one to achieve left-hand side of Eq. (
13
). We emphasize it is not the case due to the non-convexity and non-linearity of the transformation. Hence, the decomposition of
{\rm LF}
cannot be directly applied to the
\mathbb{SR}
.

The second physical implication of Result
2
reads

Result 4
.

Consider an experiment where Alice steers Bob’s state
via a pure bipartite state
\rho_{\rm AB}=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|_{\rm AB}
.
If Bob’s assemblage
\vec{\sigma}
is already optimal w.r.t. Bob’s local operations, i.e.,
\mathbb{SR}(\vec{\sigma})=\max_{\mathcal{E}_{\omega}\in{\rm LF}}\mathbb{SR}\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\vec{\sigma})}{p(\omega)}\right)
, then its steerability cannot be increased by performing any filter on Alice’s side.

See Appendix G for the proof.
This result states that for a bipartite pure state and whenever steerability is already maximized by performing some local filter on Bob’s side, Alice cannot further increase it by performing local filters.
This result can also be seen as the impossibility for Alice to distill the incompatibility of the MA using local filters, aligning with the findings in Res.
1
.
Notice that this is no longer true if Alice and Bob share a mixed state and
indeed the steerability can be increased via local filters on Alice’s side, as it is the case for the
phenomenon of
hidden steerability

Quintino et al. 2015
;
Pramanik et al. 2019
. Indeed, trying to argue
in terms of a purification of the initial state does not work. This phenomenon is not restricted to
quantum steering, but it was also observed in Bell
nonlocality
Popescu 1995
;
Gisin 1996
;
Liang et al. 2012
;
Hirsch et al. 2013
and quantum
teleportation
Li et al. 2021
.

Here, we overcome the difficulties associated with the definition of filtered measurements and prove that measurement incompatibility can never be stochastically distilled. Our results hold for task-based incompatibility measures such as the incompatibility robustness and incompatible weight, as well as a wide range of robustness-based measures including a single measurement scenario.

Our results complete the previous investigations on the resource theory of measurement incompatibility

Appendix B: Operational interpretation of a filtering measurement

Before introducing the concept of a filtered measurement, let us first discuss the effect of measurement in quantum physics. From a physical perspective, a measurement is a procedure that, given a (quantum) system, produces an outcome with a certain probability. Hence, mathematically, a measurement maps a quantum state to a post-measurement quantum state and a distribution over measurement outcomes. Importantly, when one ignores the post-measurement state, the only relevant object is the distribution over classical outcomes.
This fact can be described by the mapping

\mathcal{M}:\rho\mapsto\{p(a)\}_{a}.

(18)

The physics of the problem imposes several constraints on such a mapping. First, it must be that
p(a)\geq 0
for all
a
and
\sum_{a}p(a)=1
, namely, it should map states to probability distributions. Second, such a map must be linear. This comes from the fact that, if one considers a probabilistic mixture of two states, i.e.,
\rho=\lambda\rho_{1}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{2}
, with
0<\lambda<1
, then the corresponding probability distribution must satisfy
p(a)=\lambda p_{1}(a)+(1-\lambda)p_{2}(a)
, where
p_{i}
is the outcome distribution associated with the state
\rho_{i}
. This implies convex-linearity of the map, however, it can be extended by linearity with an argument analogous to that of
Hardy 2001
;
Barrett 2007
, since the objects outside of the convex set of states do not have a physical meaning.

The linearity of the map implies, by Riesz-Frechét theorem, that it can be as a scalar product on the space of linear operators (see, e.g.,
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
), namely, with the usual Born rule

p(a)=\tr[M_{a}\rho].

(19)

Moreover, the linearity and normalization of probability, for any input state
\rho
, imply that the operators
\{M_{a}\}_{a}
must satisfy
M_{a}\geq 0
, for all
a
, and
\sum_{a}M_{a}=\openone
. In other words, this is a concise derivation of the usual notion of POVM. This idea can, then, be connected with the standard measurement in textbook quantum mechanics, namely, the projective measurement via Naimark dilation theorem (see, e.g.,
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
), i.e., every POVM is implemented as a projective measurement on a larger system.

The above standard argument for the derivation of POVMs representation for quantum measurement is crucial to understand why a filtered measurement can no longer be described by a POVMs. The failure of the linearity of the mapping, which is inevitable in the case of filtering due to the presence of the post-selection on a desired outcome, implies that one must go beyond both the standard Born rule as well as the notion of POVM to describe a filtered measurement.

Before discussing the case of filters, let us first analyze the case of a deterministic transformation, i.e., a quantum channel, applied to a measurement. A quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
, in the Schrödinger picture, is a mapping from quantum states to quantum states that is completely positive (CP) and trace-preserving (TP). The same channel can be viewed in the Heisenberg picture and it is represented by the adjoint map
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}
, which now maps POVMs into POVMs. In particular,
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}
is CP and unital, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\openone)=\openone
. These properties guarantee that, when applied to an MA
\vec{A}
, gives a new MA
\vec{A}^{\prime}
.

In operational terms, the channel is applied between the state preparation and the measurement, and it can be equivalently seen as a transformation of the state (Schrödinger picture) or a transformation of the measurement (Heisenberg picture).
From this operational perspective, one can generalize this notion to the idea of filtering a quantum measurement, in a practical and experimentally feasible way.
We recall that filtering a state corresponds to the application of a CP trace-non-increasing (TNI) map to a quantum state, followed by a renormalization, such that the resulting state has again trace one, namely

\rho\mapsto\frac{\mathcal{I}(\rho)}{\tr[\mathcal{I}(\rho)]}.

(20)

Operationally, this corresponds to post-selecting only those experimental rounds in which the filter is passed. The CPTNI map
\mathcal{I}
can be thought as a part of a quantum instrument
\{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\}
, i.e., a quantum measurement, with possible outcomes “pass”, associated with
\mathcal{I}
, and “fail”, associated with
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, such that
\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
is a CPTP map.
Without post-selection, the process remains to be deterministic due to the fact that
\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
is a CPTP linear map. Therefore, to implement a single copy state distillation, a nonlinear transformation due to post-selection is necessary.
There is no way around it.

One can, then, apply the same reasoning for quantum channels to the case of filters from both
physical
and
mathematical
perspectives (see also the first paragraph in this section).

Physically
, applying a filter between the state preparation and the measurement procedure can be equivalently seen as a part of the former or of the latter (by using Schrödinger and Heisenberg pictures).
In both cases, one should take into account the filter’s failure and the corresponding post-selection probability.
Again, due to such a post-selection, the transformation of the quantum state must be nonlinear, as in Eq. (
20
).
Consequently, a measurement described by a POVM cannot be mapped to another POVM in the case of postselection.
It must be mapped to a more complicated operation, which includes the filter measurement, the associated post-selection procedure, and the original measurement.
Mathematically, applying the filter
\mathcal{I}_{\omega}
to the measurement procedure
\mathcal{M}
in Eq. (
18
), we obtain a new mapping, represented by the pair
(\mathcal{I}_{\omega}^{\dagger},\{M_{a}\}_{a})
, such that the probability for the outcome
a
with an arbitrary input state
\rho
reads, in analogy with Eq. (
18
), as

\mathcal{I}_{\omega}(\mathcal{M}):\rho\mapsto\left\{p_{\omega}(a)=\frac{\tr[\mathcal{I}_{\omega}^{\dagger}(M_{a})\rho]}{\tr[\mathcal{I}_{\omega}^{\dagger}(\openone)\rho]}\right\}_{a}.

(21)

In other words, the action of the new measurement (in terms of the probability distribution) is completely described by the following unique mapping:

(\cdot)\mapsto\tr[\mathcal{I}_{\omega}^{\dagger}(M_{a})(\cdot)]/\tr[\mathcal{I}_{\omega}^{\dagger}(\openone)(\cdot)].

(22)

Notice that, even if the mapping is nonlinear, as one should expect given the presence of a post-selection procedure, the definition of the new measurement mapping is state-independent.
Hence, it is a physically well-defined (unique and state-independent) measurement procedure, which cannot be described by POVMs. Finally, note that this definition can be easily adapted to a set of POVMs by conditioning on a classical index set
x
i.e.,
\{M_{a|x}\}_{a,x}
.
To make a concrete example, one can consider the measurement of photon polarization after inserting a beamsplitter and performing post-selection, i.e., discarding one of the paths coming out of the beamsplitter.
This type of physical measurement, which is ubiquitous in lab practices, combines POVMs with filters.
Hence, any discussion of filtered measurement must acknowledge this fact and generalize the notion of measurement beyond that of a POVM.

Since a filtered measurement
is no longer a POVM, we have to generalize the standard mathematical definition of incompatibility quantification for a measurement. Here, we consider operational definitions of incompatibility quantification, which allows us to study filtering measurement analytically.
First, let us recall the standard steering experiment and show that the filtered measurement can be used in a steering experiment to generate a valid SA. Alice and Bob share a bipartite system in the state
\rho_{AB}
. Alice performs a measurement on her half of the system, by selecting a measurement setting
x
and obtaining a measurement outcome
a
. The classical information
(x,a)
is then sent to Bob, which uses it to label the system corresponding to that round, i.e.,
\sigma_{a|x}
. After repeating this procedure several times, Bob has collected an SA
\{\sigma_{a|x}\}_{a,x}
on which he can perform state tomography (or measure a steering inequality) to decide whether the SA is steerable or not.

The case of the filtered measurement
(\mathcal{I}^{\dagger},\{A_{a|x}\}_{a,x})
works analogously.
The only difference is that now Alice must inform Bob, via classical communication, either of the input-output pair
(x,a)
or of her filter’s failure.
In the latter case, Bob can discard his system in that round.
Now, Bob’s resulting SA is simply the SA obtained if Alice and Bob were to share the bipartite state

\rho^{\prime}_{AB}\coloneqq\frac{(\mathcal{I}\otimes{\rm id})(\rho_{AB})}{\tr[(\mathcal{I}\otimes{\rm id})(\rho_{AB})]},

(23)

where we denoted with
{\rm id}
the identity channel, and Alice was to perform the original set of measurements. It is then clear that the steering experiment is well-defined also with a filtered measurement.
Importantly, as we reported in Eq. (4) of the main text, we can further use steering experiments to evaluate and estimate incompatibility robustness.
These allow us to use steering experiments (which are experimentally feasible and operationally clear) to quantify the effect of filters on measurements.
Finally, we remark that the operational definition of the filtered measurement also allows us to apply it to the state-discrimination task
Uola et al. 2019
;
Carmeli et al. 2019
;
Skrzypczyk et al. 2019
;
Buscemi et al. 2020
as well as for the state-exclusion task
Uola et al. 2020a
;
Ducuara and Skrzypczyk 2020
; see below for a detailed discussion.

Note that adding an additional element
\openone-\mathcal{I}_{\omega}^{\dagger}(\openone)
can make
\mathcal{I}_{\omega}^{\dagger}(M_{a})
a complete POVM. Still, in this work, we adopt Eq.
6
so that post-selection’s effect is automatically included.
In fact, the additional POVM element
\openone-\mathcal{I}_{\omega}^{\dagger}(\openone)
corresponds to the failure of the filter. This can be observed by
\mathcal{I}_{\omega}+\mathcal{I^{\prime}}
is CPTP (or, equivalently,
(\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}_{\omega}+\mathcal{I^{\prime}}^{\dagger})(\openone)=\openone
which is CP-unital map,
i.e., preserving POVMs
). In the other direction, it has been shown that measurement incompatibility cannot be distilled by considering quantum pre-processing
Buscemi et al. 2020
that takes both success and failure of filters into account in the measurement transformation. Thus, merely adding an additional POVM cannot distill measurement incompatibility.

Appendix C: No stochastic distillation of measurement incompatibility from state discrimination and exclusion tasks

We explore the connection between Result 1 and state discrimination tasks. Indeed, measurement incompatibility can be operationally defined also in terms of state-discrimination tasks, i.e., for a MA
\vec{A}
we have
Uola et al. 2019
;
Carmeli et al. 2019
;
Skrzypczyk et al. 2019
;
Buscemi et al. 2020

1+\mathbb{IR}(\vec{A})=\sup_{\mathcal{S}}\frac{p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(\vec{A},\mathcal{S})}{\max_{\vec{O}\in\mathbb{JM}}p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(\vec{O},\mathcal{S})},

(24)

where
\mathcal{S}:=\{p(a,x),\rho_{a|x}\}
is the state ensemble involved in the state discrimination task
with prior information
Carmeli et al. 2018
, and
p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(\vec{A},\mathcal{S})
is the success
probability associated with the MA
\vec{A}
and the task
\mathcal{S}
. We refer the reader to

Uola et al. 2019
;
Carmeli et al. 2019
;
Skrzypczyk et al. 2019
;
Buscemi et al. 2020
for formal details.
The question is how to define a generalization of this procedure when the filtering of the measurement takes place. First, we notice that the filtering operation is placed between the state preparation and the measurement. Hence, following the same steps as in the steering scenario, one can operationally interpret the filtering of the measurement as the filtering of the incoming states.
The filtered-measurement incompatibility, then, involves the ratio between the success probability in the discrimination task with the original MA and a the optimal jointly measurable one, namely,

\sup_{\mathcal{S}}\frac{p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(\vec{A},\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\mathcal{S}))}{\max_{\vec{O}\in\mathbb{JM}}p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(\vec{O},\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\mathcal{S}))},

(25)

where, with a slight abuse of notation, we defined
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\mathcal{S}):=\{p(a,x|\omega),\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\rho_{a|x})/p(\omega|a,x)\}
, and the probabilities
p(\omega|a,x)=\tr[\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\rho_{a|x})]
and

p(a,x|\omega)=\frac{p(a,x,\omega)}{p(\omega)}=\frac{p(a,x)p(\omega|a,x)}{\sum_{a,x}p(\omega|a,x)p(a,x)}.

(26)

Indeed, the effect of the postselection associated with the filter operation is to change the probability of appearances of the states.
From Eq. (
25
), it is then clear that the ratio between the two guessing probabilities cannot be increased as we are performing the same optimization, but now on a restricted set of state, i.e., the image of the filter
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
.

The same reasoning can be applied to the incompatible weight
\mathbb{IW}(\vec{A})

in state exclusion tasks
Uola et al. 2020a
;
Ducuara and Skrzypczyk 2020

1-\mathbb{IW}(\vec{A})=\inf_{\mathcal{S}}\frac{p_{\rm succ}^{\rm excl}(\vec{A},\mathcal{S})}{\max_{\vec{O}\in\mathbb{JM}}p_{\rm succ}^{\rm excl}(\vec{O},\mathcal{S})},

(27)

where
p_{\rm succ}^{\rm excl}(\vec{A},\mathcal{S})
refers to the success probability of such a task. One immediately sees that filter operations can only transform the state ensemble
\mathcal{S}
into a new one, hence, they cannot decrease the infimum on the r.h.s. of Eq. (

Moreover, as similar argument applies also to the robustness of measurement (ROM), recently introduced in

\mathbb{ROM}(M):=\min\{t|(M_{a}+tN_{a})/(1+t)=q(a)\openone\}

can be defined in terms of a discrimination task

(28)

where
\mathcal{S}:=\{p_{x},\rho_{x}\}
is a state ensemble, and

and

are the probability of guessing the correct label

M

\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
on the ensemble
\mathcal{S}
only provides a valid ensemble, so it is not able to increase
\mathbb{ROM}(M)

\mathcal{S}

Finally, we comment on the fact that this seems to be the only meaningful generalization of discrimination-based operational definitions of incompatibility, since the only the possible alternative definition has several problematic properties, as we discuss below. As such, the definition provided in Eq. (
25
), and the analogous definitions for the
\mathbb{IW}
and
\mathbb{ROM}
, seem the only plausible ones. Let us first have a look at the
\mathbb{ROM}
case, which is the simplest one. Now, consider as an alternative definition the case in which the ratio is computed between the guessing probability in the filtered and unfiltered game

\sup_{\mathcal{S}}\frac{p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(M,\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\mathcal{S}))}{p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(\mathcal{S})}.

(29)

It is easy to verify that there exists very simple measurements and filters in which this value becomes arbitrarily high. Consider the case of a qubit system with the following game:
\mathcal{S}=\{p_{x},\rho_{x}\}_{x=0}^{N}
with states
\rho_{0}=|0\rangle\langle 0|
, and
\rho_{i}=|1\rangle\langle 1|
for
i=1,\ldots,N
, appearing with probability
p_{0}=\varepsilon
and
p_{i}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{N}
, for
i>0
and some arbitrary small
\varepsilon
. The optimal guessing probability for the case without measurement
p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(\mathcal{S})
is rather straightforward: the player guesses the state with the highest probability , i.e.,
p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(\mathcal{S})=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{N}
. Alternatively, one can guess at random any of the state, except the first one. It is clear that we can make such a probability arbitrary small, since
p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}\rightarrow 0
for

. The strategy with the measurement does not perform much better, as one can only distinguish between
\rho_{0}
and all the others. The guessing probability is then
p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(M,\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\mathcal{S}))=\varepsilon+\frac{1-\varepsilon}{N}
. This can be obtained with the POVM
\{|0\rangle\langle 0|,|1\rangle\langle 1|,0,\ldots,0\}
or, equivalently, with
\{|0\rangle\langle 0|,\frac{1}{N}|1\rangle\langle 1|,\ldots,\frac{1}{N}|1\rangle\langle 1|\}
. Now, consider the game in which we implement a filter
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\cdot)=|0\rangle\langle 0|\cdot|0\rangle\langle 0|
. The result is that the filtered game has
p_{0}=1
and
p_{x}=0
for
x>0
. Equivalently, one can have a slightly rotated filter, such that
p_{0}=1-\varepsilon^{\prime}
and
p_{x}=\varepsilon^{\prime}/N
for
x>0
, such that the game keeps the same number of inputs. It is then clear that, while
p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(\mathcal{S})\approx 0
,
p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(M,\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\mathcal{S}))=1
, or
\approx 1
if we do not filter exactly with
|0\rangle\langle 0|
. Such an operational definition of the filtered
\mathbb{ROM}
is problematic for two reasons. First, one can obtain an arbitrary high value of
\mathbb{ROM}
, whereas, from the SDP definition of
\mathbb{ROM}
one can see that it is trivially bounded by the number of outcomes of
M
minus 1; see
Skrzypczyk and Linden 2019
. Secondly, this increased robustness is not associated with an improved ability to discriminating the states, but rather to some “cheating strategy” based on manipulating the probability that they appear.

The same argument can be applied, almost identical, to the case of
\mathbb{IR}
. Consider a qubit system with the MA
\vec{A}=\{A_{a|0}\}_{a=0,1}
defined as
A_{a|0}=|a\rangle\langle a|
for
a=0,1
and the discrimination game defined by the same state ensemble
\mathcal{S}
as before. This is a special case of a MA, in which only one input appears, so it should not provide any advantage over jointly measurable MA. Notice that one can construct more complicated games by taking copies of it, but this fact is irrelevant for our argument. The guessing probability for the unfiltered case, which includes the
\mathbb{JM}
case, is given by

\max_{\vec{O}\in\mathbb{JM}}p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(\vec{O},\mathcal{S})=\sum_{a=0,1}p(a)\tr[A_{a}\rho_{a}]=\varepsilon+\frac{1-\varepsilon}{N}.

(30)

Conversely, by applying the same filter as before, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\cdot)=|0\rangle\langle 0|\cdot|0\rangle\langle 0|
, one obtains
p_{\rm succ}^{\rm discr}(M,\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\mathcal{S}))=1
. Again, the ratio between the two guessing probability can grow arbitrary high, since one is constant and the other tends to
0
, in contradiction with the definition of
\mathbb{IR}
, for which a simple bound in terms of the number of inputs and outputs can be obtained; see
Uola et al. 2015
. Moreover, this increase in the ratio does not corresponds to an increasing in the ability of distinguishing state, but it comes only from “cheating strategy” based on the manipulation of the probability of appearance of the states. Similar considerations can be made for
\mathbb{IW}
, for which any value between
0
and
1
can be obtained, without necessarily improving the state-discrimination capabilities of the measurement.

Appendix H: Robustness-based measures and proof of Result 5

Here, we extend the result from
Ku et al. 2022a
, namely,
\mathbb{IR}(\vec{B})=\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SR}(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2})
, to other measures of robustness, such as the random and free-state consistent robustness, both in the incompatibility and steering scenarios.

Let us recall the definition of random incompatibility robustness
Cavalcanti and Skrzypczyk 2016b
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{IR}^{\rm R}(\vec{B})=\min\left\{t\geq 0\;\middle|\;\frac{B_{a|x}+t\openone/|\mathcal{A}|}{1+t}=\sum_{\lambda}p(a|x,\lambda)G_{\lambda},G_{\lambda}\geq 0,\sum_{\lambda}G_{\lambda}=\openone\right\},

(43)

where
|\mathcal{A}|=n_{a}
is the number of the outcomes
a
.
On the other hand, the random steering robustness (see the definition in Ref.
Cavalcanti and Skrzypczyk 2016b
) of a SA
\vec{\sigma}
with reduced state
\sum_{a}\sigma_{a|x}=\rho_{\rm B}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{SR}^{\rm R}(\vec{\sigma})=\min\left\{t\geq 0\;\middle|\;\frac{\sigma_{a|x}+t\rho_{\rm B}/|\mathcal{A}|}{1+t}=\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)p(a|x,\lambda)\rho_{\lambda},\rho_{\lambda}\geq 0,\tr[\rho_{\lambda}]=1\right\}.

(44)

Let us also recall the definition of free-state robustness measures. The JM incompatibility robustness is given by (see, e.g.,
Cavalcanti and Skrzypczyk 2016b
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{IR}^{\rm{JM}}(\vec{B})=\min\left\{t\geq 0\;\middle|\;\frac{B_{a|x}+t\sum_{\lambda}p^{\prime}(a|x,\lambda)G^{\prime}_{\lambda}}{1+t}=\sum_{\lambda}p(a|x,\lambda)G_{\lambda};\text{ with }G_{\lambda},G^{\prime}_{\lambda}\geq 0\ \forall\lambda,\sum_{\lambda}G_{\lambda}=\sum_{\lambda}G^{\prime}_{\lambda}=\openone\right\}.

(45)

Similarly, the consistent LHS steering robustness (see the definition in
Cavalcanti and Skrzypczyk 2016b
) of a SA
\vec{\sigma}
with reduced state
\sum_{a}\sigma_{a|x}=\rho_{\rm B}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{SR}^{\rm{C-LHS}}(\vec{\sigma})=\min\left\{t\geq 0\;\middle|\;\frac{\sigma_{a|x}+t\sum_{\lambda}p^{\prime}(\lambda)p^{\prime}(a|x,\lambda)\rho^{\prime}_{\lambda}}{1+t}=\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)p(a|x,\lambda)\rho_{\lambda};\text{ $\rho_{\lambda},\rho^{\prime}_{\lambda}$: states s.t. $\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)\rho_{\lambda}=\rho_{\rm B}$}\right\}.

(46)

XV.1

Lemmas relevant to Result 5

Before proceeding to the proof of Result 5, we need some intermediate results. More precisely, we need to prove that

Lemma 1
.

The maximal consistent LHS steering robustness within an SEO class is the same as the JM incompatibility robustness of the SEO; namely

\mathbb{IR}^{\rm{JM}}(\vec{B})=\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SR}^{\rm{C-LHS}}\left(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right).

(47)

Moreover, every full-rank state is an optimal
\rho_{\rm{B}}
.

Proof.—

Let
t^{*}=\mathbb{IR}^{\rm{JM}}(\vec{B})
with the decomposition
\vec{B}=(1+t^{*})\vec{D}-t^{*}\vec{D}^{\prime}
appearing in the definition of
\mathbb{IR}^{\rm JM}(\vec{B})

\vec{D},\vec{D}^{\prime}
are in
\mathbb{JM}

Then, for every
\rho_{\rm B}
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}=(1+t^{*})\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}\vec{D}\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}-t^{*}\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}\vec{D}^{\prime}\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2},

(48)

which is a valid decomposition for
\mathbb{SR}^{\rm{C-LHS}}\left(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right)
.
This shows that
\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SR}^{\rm{C-LHS}}\left(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right)\leq\mathbb{IR}^{\rm{JM}}(\vec{B})
.

To prove the tightness of this inequality, let us consider an arbitrary full-rank state
\hat{\rho}_{\rm B}
.
Let us write
\hat{t}=\mathbb{SR}^{\rm{C-LHS}}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\rm B}^{1/2}\vec{B}\hat{\rho}_{\rm B}^{1/2}\right)
with the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\rm B}^{1/2}\vec{B}\hat{\rho}_{\rm B}^{1/2}=\left(1+\hat{t}\right)\vec{\xi}-\hat{t}\vec{\tau},

(49)

where, by the definition of
\mathbb{SR}^{\rm C-LHS}
, both
\vec{\xi},\vec{\tau}
are in
\mathbb{LHS}
, and both of them have the same reduced state
\hat{\rho}_{\rm B}
that is full-rank.
By applying the map
\hat{\rho}_{\rm B}^{-1/2}(\cdot)\hat{\rho}_{\rm B}^{-1/2}
, we can write

\displaystyle\vec{B}=\left(1+\hat{t}\right)\vec{D}-\hat{t}\vec{N},

(50)

where
\vec{D},\vec{N}
are the SEOs of
\vec{\xi},\vec{\tau}
, meaning that both of them are in
\mathbb{JM}
.
This is therefore a valid decomposition for
\mathbb{IR}^{\rm{JM}}(\vec{B})
.
Consequently, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{IR}^{\rm{JM}}(\vec{B})\leq\hat{t}=\mathbb{SR}^{\rm{C-LHS}}\left(\hat{\rho}_{\rm B}^{1/2}\vec{B}\hat{\rho}_{\rm B}^{1/2}\right)\leq\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SR}^{\rm{C-LHS}}\left(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right),

(51)

which completes the proof.

\square

It is worth mentioning that our proof also shows that every full-rank state can be used to construct the optimal solution.
This makes our proof also constructive, since we know how to explicitly choose an optimal
\rho_{\rm B}
.
By using the same proof, a similar result also holds for the random robustness:

.

The maximal random steering robustness within an SEO class is the same as the random incompatibility robustness of the SEO; namely

Moreover, every full-rank state is an optimal
\rho_{\rm{B}}

Proof.—
One can use exactly the same proof of Lemma
1
to show this result.

Appendix I: Weight-based measure and proof of Result 6

In this section, we extend our Obs. 1 and Res. 2, 3, 4 from robustness-based measures to the weight-based measures.
We now recall their definition. The incompatible weight
Pusey 2015
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{IW}(\vec{B})=\min\Big\{

\displaystyle t\geq 0\;\Big|\;B_{a|x}=tN_{a|x}+(1-t)O_{a|x}~\forall a,x,\ \vec{N}\text{~~is any MA, }\vec{O}\in\mathbb{JM}\Big\}.

(54)

Similarly, the steerable weight
Skrzypczyk et al. 2014
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{SW}(\vec{\sigma})=\min\Big\{

\displaystyle t\geq 0\;\Big|\;\sigma_{a|x}=t\xi_{a|x}+(1-t)\tau_{a|x}~\forall a,x,\ \vec{\xi}\text{~~is any SA},\vec{\tau}\in\mathbb{LHS}\Big\}.

(55)

Before we show our statements in this section, we first derive the primal SDP of the
\mathbb{IW}
by reformulating the optimization problem as

N_{a|x}=\frac{B_{a|x}-(1-t)O_{a|x}}{t}\geq 0
.
If we define
(1-t)=s
, we have

B_{a|x}-s\sum_{\lambda}D(a|x,\lambda)G_{\lambda}\geq 0
.
Here, we use the definition of the joint measureability
O_{a|x}=\sum_{\lambda}D(a|x,\lambda)G_{\lambda}
with
G_{\lambda}
being a POVM satisfying
\sum_{\lambda}G_{\lambda}=\openone
.
Finally, by defining
\tilde{G}_{\lambda}=sG_{\lambda}
, we arrive at the primal form of the SDP

\displaystyle\text{Given}

\displaystyle\vec{B}

(56)

\displaystyle\text{Find}

\displaystyle\max s=1-\mathbb{IW}(\vec{B})

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle B_{a|x}\geq\sum_{\lambda}D(a|x,\lambda)\tilde{G}_{\lambda},

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{\lambda}\geq 0~~\text{and}~~\sum_{\lambda}\tilde{G}_{\lambda}=s\openone.

Here, the last constraint is from
\sum_{\lambda}\tilde{G}_{\lambda}=s\sum_{\lambda}G_{\lambda}=s\openone
.
The Lagrangian associated with the primal SDP is

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=s+\sum_{a,x}\tr\left[\omega_{a|x}\left(B_{a|x}-\sum_{\lambda}D(a|x,\lambda)\tilde{G}_{\lambda}\right)\right]+\tr\left[Z\left(s\openone-\sum_{\lambda}\tilde{G}_{\lambda}\right)\right]+\sum_{\lambda}\tr\left[Y_{\lambda}\tilde{G}_{\lambda}\right]

One can directly check that

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{IW}(\vec{B})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\geq 0,\\

(58)

where the upper bound is the dual SDP.
To write it down, note that
the Lagrangian is independent of the primal variable if

and

XVI.1

Lemmas relevant to Result 6

To show the main result of this subsection, we first show the following lemma:

Lemma 3
.

Given a MA
\vec{B}
, the weight-based measures satisfy

\mathbb{SW}\left(\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}\right)\leq\mathbb{IW}(\vec{B})\;\text{ for all }\rho_{\rm B}.

(61)

Proof.—

We apply the positive map
\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}(\cdot)\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}
, where
\rho_{\rm B}
is an arbitrary quantum state, to the elements of the decomposition appearing in Eq. (
54
). Then we define

\vec{\xi}=\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}\vec{N}\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}
and
\vec{\tau}=\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}\vec{O}\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}
. The decomposition in
\mathbb{IW}
, then, becomes a valid decomposition for
\mathbb{SW}(\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2})
, since
\vec{O}\in\mathbb{JM}
implies that
\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}\vec{O}\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}\in\mathbb{LHS}
, which proves the claim.

\square

We now show our main result in this subsection:

Lemma 4
.

The maximal steerable weight in the SEO class is equivalent to the incompatible weight of the SEO; i.e.,

\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SW}\left(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right)=\mathbb{IW}(\vec{B}),

(62)

where the optimal
\rho_{\rm{B}}
can be obtained by solving the dual SDP of
\mathbb{IW}(\vec{B})
.

Proof.—
By Lemma
3
, it is enough to prove that any feasible solution of the problem on the r.h.s. of Eq. (
62
) allows us to construct a feasible solution of the problem on the l.h.s. with a lower objective function. In other words, we want to prove the opposite inequality with respect to Lemma
3
. Let us start with the feasible solutions,
X
and
\vec{\omega}
, of
\mathbb{IW}(\vec{B})
defined in Eq. (
59
). Since the operator
X
is hermitian, it can always be decomposed as
X=X^{+}-X^{-}
satisfying
X^{\pm}\geq 0
and
X^{+}X^{-}=X^{-}X^{+}=0
. Because of
\tr(X)=1
, we have
\tr(X^{+})\geq 1
. We then define the following variables:

\displaystyle\eta_{\rm{B}}=\frac{X^{+}}{\tr(X^{+})},\quad\vec{\sigma}=\eta_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\eta_{\rm{B}}^{1/2},\quad\vec{F}=\eta_{\rm{B}}^{-1/2}\vec{\omega}\eta_{\rm{B}}^{-1/2},

(63)

where the inverse is defined after a projection on the range of
\eta_{\rm{B}}
, which is also the range of
X^{+}
.
Let
\Pi_{X^{+}}
be the projector onto
X^{+}
’s range. Then we have
\eta_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\eta_{\rm{B}}^{-1/2}=\eta_{\rm{B}}^{-1/2}\eta_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}=\Pi_{X^{+}}
.
The objective function of Eq. (
59
) can be re-written by

\displaystyle\sum_{a,x}\tr\left(B_{a|x}\omega_{a|x}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,x}\tr\left[B_{a|x}\omega_{a|x}\left(\Pi_{X^{+}}+(\openone-\Pi_{X^{+}})\right)\right]\geq\sum_{a,x}\tr\left(B_{a|x}\omega_{a|x}\Pi_{X^{+}}\right)=\sum_{a,x}\tr\left[B_{a|x}\left(\Pi_{X^{+}}+(\openone-\Pi_{X^{+}})\right)\omega_{a|x}\Pi_{X^{+}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{a,x}\tr\left(B_{a|x}\Pi_{X^{+}}\omega_{a|x}\Pi_{X^{+}}\right)=\sum_{a,x}\tr\left(\eta_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}{B}_{a|x}\eta_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\eta_{\rm{B}}^{-1/2}\omega_{a|x}\eta_{\rm{B}}^{-1/2}\right)=\sum_{a,x}\tr\left(\sigma_{a|x}F_{a|x}\right),

(64)

which is the objective function of
1-\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SW}\left(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right)
. See Eq. (
60
) for comparison.

We now show that the constraints in Eq. (
60
) are satisfied.
Applying the CP map
\eta_{\rm{B}}^{-1/2}(\cdot)\eta_{\rm{B}}^{-1/2}
on
\sum_{a,x}D(a|x,\lambda)\omega_{a|x}\geq X
, which is the first constraint in Eq. (
59
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{a,x}D(a|x,\lambda)F_{a|x}\geq\eta_{\rm{B}}^{-1/2}X\eta_{\rm{B}}^{-1/2}=\eta_{\rm{B}}^{-1/2}X_{+}\eta_{\rm{B}}^{-1/2}=\tr[X^{+}]\openone\geq\openone.

(65)

Notice that, without loss of generality, we can restrict the space in Eq. (
60
) to be the range of
X^{+}
, since by construction it includes the ranges of both
\sigma_{a|x}
and
F_{a|x}
for all
a,x
.
Consequently,
\vec{F}
is a feasible solution to Eq. (
60
), meaning that

\displaystyle\sum_{a,x}\tr\left(\sigma_{a|x}F_{a|x}\right)\geq 1-\mathbb{SW}\left(\eta_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\eta_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right)\geq 1-\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SW}\left(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right).

(66)

Together with Eq. (
XVI.1
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{a,x}\tr\left(B_{a|x}\omega_{a|x}\right)\geq 1-\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SW}\left(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right)

(67)

for every feasible solutions
X
and
\vec{\omega}
of
\mathbb{IW}(\vec{B})
’s dual SDP in Eq. (
59
).
In other words, by minimizing over all feasible solutions, we obtain

1-\mathbb{IW}(\vec{B})\geq 1-\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SW}\left(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right)
; namely,

\displaystyle\mathbb{IW}(\vec{B})\leq\max_{\rho_{\rm{B}}}\mathbb{SW}\left(\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\right),

(68)

which,using Lemma
3
, concludes the proof.
This also means that optimal solutions to
\mathbb{IW}(\vec{B})
’s dual SDP in Eq. (
59
) can help us to construct optimal state
\rho_{\rm B}
.

\square

XVI.2

Proof of Result 6

The first part of the proof, i.e., Eq. (
14
) in the main text, comes from the above lemmas. The impossibility of distillability of
\mathbb{IW}
has been proven in Supp. C
X
. To conclude, we need to show that Obs. 1 and Res. 2, 3, and 4 hold for
(\mathbb{IR},\mathbb{SR})
substituted with
(\mathbb{IW},\mathbb{SW})
.
For Obs. 1, it is enough to show that from any solution of
\mathbb{IW}(\vec{B})
we can construct an assemblage
\vec{\sigma}=\eta_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}\vec{B}\eta_{\rm{B}}^{1/2}
as in Lemma
4
above. The corresponding solution for
\mathbb{SW}(\vec{\sigma})
has the SA
\vec{\xi}
and
\vec{\tau}
appearing in Eq. (
55
) with the same reduced state as
\vec{\sigma}
.
For Res. 2, one can proceed similarly to the proof in Supp. E
XII
and construct a convex decomposition for the SA:
\rho_{\rm B}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{\omega}^{\dagger}(\vec{B})\rho_{\rm B}^{\frac{1}{2}}/p(\omega)
, necessary to compute its
\mathbb{SW}
, from the convex decomposition of
\vec{B}
. One proceeds similarly as in Supp. E
XII
also for the SA:
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2}\vec{B}\rho_{\rm B}^{1/2})/p(\omega)
.
For Res. 3, it is enough to use Res. 2 together with Lemma.
4
and the fact that
