An alternative proof of the vulnerability of retrieval in high intrinsic dimensionality neighborhood
Teddy Furon

Abstract
This paper investigates the vulnerability of the nearest neighbors search, which is a pivotal tool in data analysis and machine learning. The vulnerability is gauged as the relative amount of perturbation that an attacker needs to add onto a dataset point in order to modify its neighbor rank w.r.t. a query. The statistical distribution of this quantity is derived from simple assumptions. Experiments on six large scale datasets validate this model up to some outliers which are explained in term of violations of the assumptions.



1
Introduction

The search of Nearest Neighbors (NN) not only appears in the top-10 algorithms in data mining according to
[
Wu et al. 2008
]
, but it is also at the root of many machine learning methods ranging from detection
[
Gu et al. 2019
]
, classification
[
Yang et al. 2020
]
, and regression
[
Devroye et al. 1994
]
.

Chen and Shah 2018
explain its popularity because it is a flexible, non parametric, interpretable, and computationally efficient method.

As such, many security and safety-oriented applications rely on information retrieval operated by the NN search.
The existence of an attacker intentionally circumventing the NN search becomes a plausible threat.
For instance, social networks often compare uploaded content to a collection of materials to detect ‘forbidden’ content (terrorism, pedopornography, fake news,
etc
) or to identify copyright holders (content monetization). The detection of outliers to spot fraud in financial transactions or intrusion in security systems is another example of critical applications of the NN search
[
Gu et al. 2019
]
.

The NN search recently went to the rescue of other machine learning techniques.
At training time,
Bahri et al. 2020
use the NN search to sanitize the tranining set detecting mislabeled data, while
Weerasinghe et al. 2021
prevent poisoning attacks.
At test time,
Nguyen and Raff 2018
use it as a defense against adversarial examples to robustify regression.

Cohen et al. 2020
,
Papernot and McDaniel 2018
, and
Ma et al. 2018
share the same idea to detect adversarial images by considering them as outliers.
They gauge the confidence of the classifier according to the neighbors of the input in the training set in some activation layer of the neural network.
Note that this defense has been under scrutiny in the works of
Sitawarin and Wagner 2019
and
Athalye et al. 2018
.

Wang et al. 2018
analyze the theoretical robustness of classification based on NN search against adversarial attacks and propose in
[
Yang et al. 2020
]
to sieve the training set to prune out isolated points that cause vulnerability.

The versatility and ubiquity of the NN search motivate research on its intrinsic vulnerability, independently of any machine learning procedure.
It is of utmost importance to analyze the security of this key primitive before its deployment in applications where security matters.
Our article follows the approach funded by
Amsaleg et al. 2020
.
It especially deals with vectors (features extracted from objects) living in high dimensional space and compared with the Euclidean distance.
It quantifies the amount of perturbation in this feature space needed to delude the retrieval mechanism in terms of modification of the nearest neighbors.
This is exemplified by the scenario where the attacker modifies the
k_{x}
nearest neighbor in the dataset to reduce its neighboring rank to a target
k_{t}<k_{x}
.

Under some broad assumptions, our contribution models the necessary amount of perturbation as a random variable and reveals its precise distribution depending on ranks
k_{x}
and
k_{t}
, the size
n
of the dataset, and on a scalar index
\ell
characterising the distances of the dataset points in the neighborhood.
Section
4
shows that this r.v. converges to a simple distribution when the dataset size
n
grows to infinity.
Experiments in Sect.
5
over six large scale datasets (four of them gather one billion dataset points each) of very different nature shows a very close fit with the asymptotical model.
Section
6
extends results to a plurality scenarios where the query (or a dataset point) is modified so that the
k_{x}
-NN becomes the
k_{t}
-NN: if
k_{t}<k_{x}
, the perturbated point gets closer to the anchor, if
k_{t}>k_{x}
it is moved farther away.

2
Related Work

2.1
Experimental Evidence of the Vulnerability

The forgery of query image deflecting nearest neighbor search has a long history in Content-Based Image Retrieval (CBIR) and image classification.

Do et al. 2012
motivate their work by pinpointing that CBIR became a technology brick used in applications where security is at stake, like content monetization on social networks.
In the pre-era of deep learning, these authors delude such a mechanism (
i.e.
a given dataset image is no longer returned by the
k
-NN search)
by exploiting the Achilles’ heel of the SIFT local description (orientation).

Tolias et al. 2019
play the opposite game with nowadays deep learning representation: the query image (
e.g.
depicting a flower) is manipulated to retrieve images matching another visual content (
e.g.
the Eiffel tower) to protect the privacy of the querying user.
Xiao and Wang 2021
investigate the same scenario but with black-box transferability.
The general poor level of security is due to two weak points:
i
) deep neural networks provide discriminative but sensitive feature vectors easily corrupted by image manipulation, a well-known fact in the literature of adversarial examples
[
Sabour et al. 2016
]
, and
ii
) a small move in this high dimensional feature space may be sufficient to dramatically change the nearest neighbors as demonstrated by
Amsaleg et al. 2020
.

Our paper is dedicated to this later vulnerability.

2.2
Theoretical Evidence of the Vulnerability

Wang et al. 2018
extend the theoretical analysis of classification based on nearest neighbors.
The classic theory shows how the probability of correct classification behaves for fixed
k
and
n
increasing
[
Devroye et al. 1996
, Chap. 5]
or the universal consistency when
k
is allowed to grow with
n
s.t.
\nicefrac{{k}}{{n}}\to 0

[
Devroye et al. 1996
, Chap. 6]
.

Wang et al. 2018
generalize this to the adversarial risk by introducing the concept of astuteness: not only a query
\mathbf{q}
should be correctly classified, but so are all the points surrounding it at a distance lower than a robustness radius
r
. They outline the vulnerability of NN classifier in the following way: zero robustness is expected for a fixed
k
and one has to set
k
to a very large amount
\Omega(\sqrt{dn\log n})
to asymptotically reach the robustness of the optimal Bayesian classifier.

Amsaleg et al. 2020
are the only authors we are aware of studying the core robustness of the NN search
out of any supervision task
.
There is no label and thus no law
p(Y|X)
, but just a collection of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
They do not make assumption about the distribution of the points around the query but only about their distances.
They show that, asymptotically as the size of the dataset increases,
the necessary relative amount of perturbation has a simple expression.
Their Theorem 2 states that if the perturbation amount is bigger than this lower bound, then the attacker is sure to reach his goal.

Our work offers a much more accurate model of the amount of perturbation revealing its asymptotical and non-asymptotical distributions.
Indeed, our results contradict the Theorem 2 of
[
Amsaleg et al. 2020
]
.

3.2
Statistical Model

The relative amount of perturbation depends on the configuration of the dataset points locally around
\mathbf{q}
.

Amsaleg et al. 2020
consider that this dataset of points is indeed random.
This means that distances
t
and
x
are occurrences of random variables
T_{n}
and
X_{n}
(note that
0<T_{n}<X_{n}
),
and so is the relative amount of perturbation
\delta
w.r.t. random variable
\Delta_{n}:=1-T_{n}/X_{n}
.
The subscript
n
stresses that the size of the dataset is a major factor.

Amsaleg et al. 2020
do not impose a specific distribution of the dataset points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but only assume a statistical distribution of their distances from
\mathbf{q}
.

Assumption 3.1
.

The distances of the dataset points w.r.t. the query
\mathbf{q}
are random variables independent and identically distributed,
whose c.d.f. is denoted by
F:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to[0,1]
and p.d.f.
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

We should have written
F_{\mathbf{q}}
and
f_{\mathbf{q}}
to outline that this statistical model only holds for the distances from
\mathbf{q}
; we omit this subscript for the sake of simplicity.

A second assumption deals with the behaviour of the c.d.f.
F
around 0.

Assumption 3.2
.

The distances of the dataset points w.r.t. the query
\mathbf{q}
are
absolutely continuous
r. v., whose c.d.f.
F
is a regularly varying function
Bingham et al. 1987
around
0^{+}
with index
\ell>0
in the sense that,
\forall\lambda>0

\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\frac{F(\lambda x)}{F(x)}=\lambda^{\ell}.

(2)

Again, we use a simplified notation although the value of the index
\ell
of the regular variation is specific to the given
\mathbf{q}
because it models the distribution of the distance from
\mathbf{q}
.
Indeed,
Amsaleg et al. 2020
call it the Local Intrinsic Dimension (LID) to insist that it only characterises the local neighborhood of the query point.

This assumption is motivated by the theory of extreme values
[
Feller 1991
, Chap.VIII]
.
As
n
increases while the ranks
k_{x}
and
k_{t}
are fixed, the distances of the
k_{x}
and
k_{t}
nearest neighbors tap into the lower tail of the distance distribution, which ought to be regularly varying because it is lower bounded by 0.
This implies that the
absolute
amount of perturbation
X_{n}-T_{n}
asymptotically goes to zero.
Yet, the asymptotical behavior of the
relative
amount
\Delta_{n}=(X_{n}-T_{n})/X_{n}
is not trivial.
In the end, the neighborhood of
\mathbf{q}
is parametrized with the single index
\ell
.

As an example, if the neighbors are Gaussian distributed spanning an affine hyperplane of dimension
d^{\prime}\leq d
and passing through query
\mathbf{q}
, then
\ell=d^{\prime}
.
The converse is of course not true. Indeed, index
\ell
can be much smaller than the representational dimension
d
.

For two natural integers

The kernel density estimator of

In the same way, we learn an empirical marginal p.d.f.

(3rd cardinal spline KDE with Silverman rule
[

Our goal is to compare the empirical joint p.d.f.

37

Figure

For further investigation, we would like to compare the empirical marginal and the asymptotical p.d.f. of
\Delta

To gather a maximum of samples, we normalize the LID index
\ell
with the following transformation:

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that
\Delta
follows the asymptotical distribution given in Prop.
4.2
with index
\ell
and the ranks
(k_{t},k_{x})

then
\tilde{\Delta}=1-(1-\Delta)^{\nicefrac{{\ell}}{{\ell_{0}}}}

Proof.

Since

\tilde{\Delta}=1-(1-\Delta)^{\nicefrac{{\ell}}{{\ell_{0}}}}=1-B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell_{0}}}}

We apply this trick to create the samples

with one common unique index value
\ell_{0}=10
.
It is as if all the measurement were coming from independent neighborhoods realizing the statistical model with index
\ell_{0}

This section presents the limits of the proposed model.
It first explains the very light discrepancies visible in Fig.

Convergence Rate

and

5.3.2
Inaccurate Estimation of the Index
\ell

All the empirical p.d.f.’s have a slightly different skewness than the theoretical plot.
We do not have access to the index
\ell
, we can only estimate its value from the distances of the
k_{x}
nearest neighbors.
The measurement
\delta
is normalized into
\tilde{\delta}
by the estimated index
\hat{\ell}
.
Even if
\delta
were occurences of r.v.
\Delta
,
\tilde{\delta}
does not follow the distribution of Prop.
5.1
due to the estimation noise present in
\ell
.

This discrepancy is almost not visible for
CIFAR
,
ImageNet
, and
BigANN
.
For these datasets, the estimated index is rarely below 5 as shown in Fig.
5
.
We evaluate the inaccuracy of the Hill estimator by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov goodness of fit test value for typical estimations
\hat{\ell}
.
Figure
9
shows that indeed estimating indices
\ell<10
is difficult. This is especially true for
DEEP
and may explain its heavier tail in Fig.
7
.
The joint p.d.f. displayed in Appendix
D
confirm this phenomenon: for all datasets, the differences between empirical and theoretical are more visible at lower index value (
i.e.
on the left side of the heat map representation).

5.3.3
Mixture of distributions

Figure
10
shows typical failures of the model.
We use some of the earlier datasets,
MSSPACEV

MSTuring
, and
DEEP
but on a specific setup
(k_{t},k_{x})=(1,100)
.
Note that this setup was not an issue for
BigANN
in Fig.
7
.

Assumption
3.1
supposes that the distances from the query follow a unique distribution.
This might not be the case for some queries. We envisage the following cases:
The
k_{x}=100
smallest distances may correspond to:

1.

a subset of documents matching the query,

2.

a subset of documents not matching the query,

3.

the union of two matching / non matching subsets.

Cases 1 and 2 comply with assumption
3.1
, but not case 3.

MSSPACEV
and
MSTuring
are collections of data related to web search with vector encodings of queries and documents.
It is expected that a web search ends up with returned documents ranked first because they have a semantic connection to the query (
i.e.
a match) or because it statically happens that their distances are small.
Case 3 might also happen with
DEEP
which is a collection of features extracted from “
images of the web
”, dixit
Babenko and Lempitsky 2016
.
Over such a huge collection of pictures scrapped from the internet, it is likely that there exist duplicates or quasi-copy of some query images in the dataset, yielding case 3.

This is a priori not the case with
CIFAR
and
ImageNet
.
For these last two datasets, the feature vectors are extracted with a classifier deep neural network and the images are coming from a collection meant for classification.
For instance, if the query is an image of a dog, it will match the hundreds of dog images in the collection.
Therefore, case 1 is likely and this does not violate our assumption.
These are our suppositions, there is no way to certify occurrences of case 3.

We now explain with a hand-waving rationale that the mixture of distributions explains the heavy tails in Fig.
10
.
Suppose for instance that the
k_{m}
nearest neighbors are matches following a different distribution than the
k_{x}-k_{m}
followers, with
k_{t}\leq k_{m}<k_{x}
.
Measurement
\hat{\ell}
may be corrupted by this mixture of distributions, but since it is essentially the inverse of an average over
k_{x}
distances (
13
),
the estimate is robust provided that
k_{m}\ll k_{x}
.
On the contrary, measurement
\delta
is very sensitive to distance
t
(
1
).
Therefore, if
k_{t}<k_{m}
, distance
t=d_{k_{t}}
is expected to be significantly smaller than the others, and
\delta
gets close to 1.
Moreover, a value
\delta\lesssim 1
is indeed spread over a wide upper interval due to the normalization mapping tuned with a very low ratio
\nicefrac{{\hat{\ell}}}{{\ell_{0}}}
.
Fig.
9
shows that
DEEP
,
MSSPACEV
, and
MSTuring
have queries with low estimates
\hat{ell}
.
This amplifies the discrepancy and explains the heavy upper tail.
This means that, under case 3, the perturbation is bigger than statistically predicted by the model.

The fact that these failures do not happen in Fig.
7
where
k_{t}=10
, means that
1<k_{m}<10
in
DEEP
,
MSSPACEV
, and
MSTuring
.
Indeed, the smaller
k_{t}
the more likely a case 3 occurs.

6
Discussion

As in
Amsaleg et al. 2020
, the analysis can be extended to two cases:

•

The modified point goes away from a reference point in the sense that the target rank
k_{t}
is bigger than the actual rank
k_{x}
.

•

It is the query
\mathbf{q}
that is perturbed and the dataset points are untouched. The query is pushed closer to (or away from) a target dataset point so that its rank decreases (resp. increases).

The second point is easy to handle by re-defining
\Delta_{n}=\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{X_{n}}}-1
with
T_{n}\geq X_{n}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

For two natural integers
k_{x}<k_{t}
, the relative amount of perturbation
\Delta_{n}
converges in distribution to
\Delta=B^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}-1
with
B\sim Beta(k_{x},k_{t}-k_{x})
, as the size
n
of the dataset increases.

Proof.

From Prop.
4.2
, we have that the ratio of two neighbor distances converges to
B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}
with
B
following a Beta distribution.
Here, we have
X_{n}<T_{n}
, so that
(X_{n}/T_{n})^{\ell}\leq 1
converges to
Beta(k_{x},k_{t}-k_{x})
and
\Delta_{n}
converges to
\Delta=B^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}-1

∎

The second point applies as well provided that the index
\ell
now characterizes the distances from the target dataset point.
As
n
increases, the query and this dataset point get closer (for a fixed rank
k_{x}
) and
Amsaleg et al. 2020
argue that they asymptotically share the same index value.
In other words, the index
\ell
can be considered as a very smooth continuous function over the location in the space.

As said in the introduction, the vulnerability of retrieval also depends on the ability to forge a content, say an image, whose extracted feature vector exactly corresponds to
\mathbf{y}
.
This point is out of our scope and
Sabour et al. 2016
and
Tolias et al. 2019
give experimental evidence as far as description with deep neural networks is concerned. If one knows the Lipschitz constant
C
of the neural network, then
C^{-1}\Delta
gives a statistical lower bound of the Euclidean distortion in the input space.
Yet, such an image may not exist because the description may not be a surjection.
Targeting point
\mathbf{y}
reduces the distortion in the feature space but not necessarily in the input space.

7
Conclusion

The article
Amsaleg et al. 2020
gives asymptotical statistics about the relative amount of perturbation outlining its dependence on the Local Intrinsic Dimensionality.
This new proof refines this result by providing the exact expression of its cumulative distribution function for fixed
n
(
6
) and its convergence to the limit distribution (
7
).
Expectation, median, and mode show similar dependence on the Local Intrinsic Dimensionality index
\ell
.
The relative amount of necessary perturbation not only decreases in expectation but also concentrates around this expectation when

\ell
increases for a fixed rank ratio
\nicefrac{{k_{t}}}{{k_{x}}}
, or for an increasing rank ratio and a fixed index
\ell
.

F_{\Delta_{n}}(\delta)=\begin{cases}&0,\quad\forall\delta\leq 0\\

and
\forall\delta\in(0,1)

\displaystyle=

Appendix B
Proof of Proposition
4.2

We use the following version of Laplace’s method stated in
Bonnaillie-Noël 2004
:

Lemma B.1
.

Let
g,h
:
(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
two continuous functions s.t.

•

\int_{0}^{1}|g(\xi)|e^{h(\xi)}d\xi<+\infty

•

\exists\delta_{0}>0,\forall\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}),\forall\xi\in[\delta,1),h(x)<h(\delta)

•

Around
0^{+}
,

\displaystyle g(\xi)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle A\xi^{\alpha},\quad\text{with }\alpha>-1

(22)

\displaystyle h(\xi)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle a-c\xi^{\beta}+o(\xi^{\beta}),\quad\text{with }c>0,\beta>0

(23)

Then

\int_{0}^{1}g(\xi)e^{nh(\xi)}d\xi\approx\frac{A}{\beta}\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+1}{\beta}\right)e^{an}(cn)^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{\beta}}\quad\mbox{as }n\to+\infty.

(24)

We then rewrite (
20
) in the form
\int_{0}^{1}g(\xi)e^{nh(\xi)}dx/B(k_{x},n-k_{x}+1)
with

\displaystyle h(\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log(1-\xi)

(25)

\displaystyle g(\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-I_{\nicefrac{{F((1-\delta)F^{-1}(\xi))}}{{\xi}}}(k_{t},k_{x}-k_{t})\right)\xi^{k_{x}-1}(1-\xi)^{-k_{x}},

(26)

Thanks to the assumption (
2
), we know that

\lim_{\xi\to 0^{+}}\frac{F((1-\delta)F^{-1}(\xi))}{\xi}=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\frac{F((1-\delta)x)}{F(x)}=(1-\delta)^{\ell}.

(27)

This allows to instantiate the constants defined in Lemma
B.1
as

\displaystyle A=I_{1-(1-\delta)^{\ell}}(k_{x}-k_{t},k_{t}),\quad\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{x}-1,

\displaystyle a=0,\quad c=1,\quad\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1.

(28)

This leads to the following asymptotical expression:

F_{\Delta_{n}}(\delta)\approx I_{1-(1-\delta)^{\ell}}(k_{x}-k_{t},k_{t})\frac{n!}{n^{k_{x}}(n-k_{x})!}\quad\mbox{as }n\to+\infty,

(29)

and to the limit function
F_{\Delta}
:

\forall\delta\in(0,1)

F_{\Delta}(\delta):=\lim_{n\to+\infty}F_{\Delta_{n}}(\delta)=I_{1-(1-\delta)^{\ell}}(k_{x}-k_{t},k_{t}),

(30)

thanks to the well-known asymptotic convergence
\Gamma(n+\alpha)\approx\Gamma(n)n^{\alpha}
,

Appendix C
Proof of proposition
4.3

This appendix uses the fact that
\Delta=1-B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}
with
B\sim Beta(k_{t},k_{x}-k_{x})
.

C.1
Expectation

We did not find any close-form expression for
\mathbb{E}[\Delta]
.
Yet, the expectation and the variance of
B\sim Beta(k_{t},k_{x}-k_{x})
equal

\mathbb{E}[B]=\frac{k_{x}-k_{t}}{k_{x}},\quad\mathbb{V}[B]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[B](1-\mathbb{E}[B])}{(k_{x}+1)}.

(31)

We see that
B
concentrates around its expectation as
k_{x}
becomes large.
The second order Taylor series around
\mathbb{E}[B]
gives

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{0}+A_{1}(B-\mathbb{E}[B])+A_{2}(B-\mathbb{E}[B])^{2}+o((B-\mathbb{E}[B])^{2})\quad\text{with}

(33)

\displaystyle A_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{E}[B]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}},\quad A_{1}=-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}\mathbb{E}[B]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}-1},\quad A_{2}=-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2\ell}}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}-1)\mathbb{E}[B]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}-2}.

(34)

Applying the expectation operator on both sides of the equation we obtain: for
k_{x}\gg 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{E}[B]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}-\frac{1}{2\ell}\left(\frac{1}{\ell}-1\right)\mathbb{E}[B]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}-2}\mathbb{V}[B]=1-\left(\frac{k_{t}}{k_{x}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}\left(1-\frac{\ell-1}{2\ell^{2}}\frac{k_{x}-k_{t}}{k_{t}(k_{x}+1)}\right).

(35)

C.2
Median

Translating quantiles of
B
to quantiles of
\Delta
is easy as
x\to 1-x^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}
is a strictly decreasing function over
(0,1)
:
The
p
-quantile of
B
equals
I^{-1}_{p}(k_{t},k_{x}-k_{t})
so that the
p
-quantile of
\Delta
equals
1-(I^{-1}_{1-p}(k_{t},k_{x}-k_{t}))^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}
.
For instance, the median
\bar{\Delta}
of
\Delta
is simply

\bar{\Delta}=1-\bar{B}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}
.
The median
\bar{B}
of
Beta(\alpha,\beta)
is usually approximated by
(\alpha-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}})/(\alpha+\beta-2/3)
for
\alpha\geq 2
and
\beta\geq 2
.
This translates to the following approximation: for
k_{t}\geq 2
and
k_{x}\geq k_{t}+2
:

\displaystyle\bar{\Delta}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 1-\left(\frac{k_{t}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}{k_{x}-\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}=1-(\nicefrac{{k_{t}}}{{k_{x}}})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}\left(1+\frac{2k_{t}-k_{x}}{k_{t}(3k_{x}-2)}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}.

(36)

C.3
Mode

We first compute the p.d.f. by derivating the c.d.f
F_{\Delta}
(
7
):

f_{\Delta}(\delta|\ell)=\frac{\ell}{B(k_{x}-k_{t},k_{t})}\left(1-(1-\delta)^{\ell}\right)^{k_{x}-k_{t}-1}(1-\delta)^{k_{t}\ell-1}\mathds{1}_{[0,1]}(\delta).

(37)

If
k_{t}\ell>1
and
k_{x}-k_{t}>1
, the derivative of the p.d.f. cancels in three points:
\delta\in\{0,\delta_{m},1\}
.
Since
f_{\Delta}(0)=f_{\Delta}(1)=0
, and
f_{\Delta}(\delta)\geq 0,\,\forall\delta\in(0,1)
, this shows that

\delta_{m}:=1-\left(\frac{k_{t}\ell-1}{(k_{x}-1)\ell-1}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}=1-\left(\frac{k_{t}}{k_{x}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}\left(1+\frac{(\ell+1)k_{t}-k_{x}}{\ell k_{x}k_{t}-(\ell+1)k_{t}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}

(38)

is the unique maximum, hence the mode of this distribution.

C.4
Special Case:
k_{t}=1

If
k_{t}=1
, then

and
K:=B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}=1-\Delta
follows by definition a Kumaraswamy distribution
\mathcal{K}(\ell,k_{x}-1)
.
This gives simpler formulas:

•

\mathbb{E}[\Delta]=1-\mathbb{E}[B]=1-(k_{x}-1)B(1+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}},k_{x}-1).

(39)

•

Median:

\bar{\Delta}=1-\bar{K}=1-\left(1-2^{-\frac{1}{k_{x}-1}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}.

(40)

•

\mathsf{mode}=1-\left(\frac{\ell-1}{(k_{x}-1)\ell-1}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\ell}}}.

(41)
