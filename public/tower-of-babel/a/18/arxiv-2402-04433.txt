Abstract
. We study online changepoint detection in the context of a linear regression model. We propose a class of heavily weighted statistics based on the CUSUM process of the regression residuals, which are specifically designed to ensure timely detection of breaks occurring early on during the monitoring horizon. We subsequently propose a class of composite statistics, constructed using different weighing schemes; the decision rule to mark a changepoint is based on the largest statistic across the various weights, thus effectively working like a veto-based voting mechanism, which ensures fast detection irrespective of the location of the changepoint. Our theory is derived under a very general form of weak dependence, thus being able to apply our tests to virtually all time series encountered in economics, medicine, and other applied sciences. Monte Carlo simulations show that our methodologies are able to control the procedure-wise Type I Error, and have short detection delays in the presence of breaks.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the linear regression model

(1)

y_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\beta_{t}+\epsilon_{t},

and test whether the slopes
\beta_{t}
are constant over time - i.e.
\beta_{t}=\beta_{0}
for all
t\geq 1
- or not. In particular, we study

sequential/online
changepoint detection: after observing (
1
) over a training period
1\leq t\leq m
with constant slopes
\beta_{0}
, we check whether, as new data come in,
\beta_{t}
differs
from the previous
\beta_{0}
for some
t>m
. Tests are carried out in real
time, at each
t>m
, as soon as the new datapoint
\left(y_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}
has been recorded.

Since the seminal contribution by
Page 1954
, online
changepoint detection has proven highly relevant to all applied sciences,
and we refer to a recent review by
Aue and
Kirch 2023
for the current
state of the art. In manufacturing, timely detection of malfunctioning in a
production line is important for quality control (see e.g.
Page 1955a
, and
Page 1955b
); in medicine,
changepoint analysis is relevant in the context of sequential medical trials
(
Armitage 2004
), or to detect the onset of an epileptic
seizure using EEG trajectories (see e.g.
Ombao
et al. 2001
); and, in
epidemiology, detecting the onset of a pandemic by monitoring whether the
number of (daily) cases starts exhibiting an explosive dynamics (see e.g.
Horváth and
Trapani 2023
) is crucial to public health decisions. In economics and
finance, the instability of model parameters is an issue of pivotal
importance due to its consequences on forecasting and policy decision
making; see,
inter alia
, the contributions by
Pástor and
Stambaugh 2001
,
Pettenuzzo and
Timmermann 2011
, and
Smith and
Timmermann 2021
. We also refer to
Romano
et al. 2023
, and the
references therein, for further examples illustrating the importance of
quick online changepoint detection. Finding instability in
\beta_{t}
has
obvious implications on the reliability of a model like (
1
),
and on decisions based on it: as
Hansen 2001
puts it, in the
presence of a changepoint “inferences about economic
relationships can go astray, forecasts can be inaccurate, and policy
recommendations can be misleading or worse” (p. 127).

The literature has developed numerous tests for the ex-post detection of
changepoints, and we refer to the reviews by
Horváth and
Rice 2014

and
Casini and
Perron 2019
, for an insightful analysis of the state of
the art and of future directions. Despite its practical importance, the
issue of real-time detection is somewhat underdeveloped compared to ex-post
testing. In a seminal contribution,
Chu
et al. 1996
develop a
methodology to monitor (
1
), based on the CUSUM process of the
residuals. The intuition is that, given an estimate of
\beta_{0}
(say
\widetilde{\beta}
) computed during a training sample in which no breaks
were detected, the “prediction errors”
y_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\widetilde{\beta}
calculated for
t>m
will
fluctuate around zero under the null of no changepoint, whereas they will
have a bias in the presence of a changepoint. Thus, the corresponding CUSUM
process will either fluctuate around zero with increasing variance as
t

increases, or it will have a drift after a changepoint. Hence, if, at each
t
, the CUSUM process stays within a boundary, the null of no break will
not be rejected; conversely, a break will be flagged at the first crossing
of such a boundary.
Chu
et al. 1996
derive the weak limit of the
CUSUM process. This allows to compute critical values, and ultimately to
control the procedure-wise Type I Error. In addition to ensuring size
control, the timely detection of a changepoint is also very important;
Horváth et al. 2004
and
Horváth et al. 2007
propose a
family of boundary functions which afford a faster break detection when a
changepoint is present. These boundary functions, which represent a major
simplification of the ones studied in
Chu
et al. 1996
, are based
on a parameterisation which depends on a user-chosen quantity (say,
0\leq\eta\leq 1/2
) which determines the weights assigned to the CUSUM
fluctuations: as
\eta
increases, the weight also increases, and therefore
higher power/faster detection may be expected. Several recent contributions
also propose statistics based on the CUSUM process - as a leading example,
which is drawing more and more attention in the literature,
Kirch and
Weber 2018
,
Yu
et al. 2020
,
Romano
et al. 2023
, and
Horváth and
Trapani 2023
, study a standardised version of the CUSUM process (called
the “Page-CUSUM”) based on comparing the
average during the training period with the average computed during the
monitoring period using a moving window, and choosing the length of such a
window in order to maximise the discrepancy between the two averages in
order to ensure an “optimal” detection
delay when a changepoint is present. Whilst different from our approach,
even in this case the CUSUM process is suitably weighted in order to enance
fast changepoint detection.

Hypotheses of interest and the main contributions of this paper

As mentioned above, we study sequential detection of changepoints in (
1
), allowing for the presence of exogenous and dynamic
regressors, and for a very general form of serial dependence in both
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and
\epsilon_{t}
. After observing the model over a
training sample of size
m
where no changes in the regression coefficients
have occurred, we test for the null hypothesis of no change occurring at
each point in time, i.e.

(2)

H_{0}:\beta_{t}=\beta_{0}\text{ for all }t\geq m+1.

We make two main contributions. Firstly, we design a class of heavily
weighted CUSUM-type statistics which ensure fast detection for early
occurring changepoints. We call these
Rényi statistics
,
inspired by
Horváth
et al. 2020
who, building on an idea by
Rényi 1953
, propose a similar idea for the purpose of offline
changepoint detection. We develop the full-blown asymptotics under the null,
and study the consistency, and the limiting behaviour of the detection
delay, under the alternative. We show that Rényi statistics deliver an
excellent performance in the presence of early occurring breaks. On the
other hand, when breaks occur at a later stage, heavily weighted statistics
offer a worse performance compared to CUSUM-type statistics using lighter
weights. Indeed, no weighing scheme can ensure uniformly fast changepoint
detection, and different values of
\eta
yield faster detection for
different break locations (see also
Kirch and
Stoehr 2022a
, and
Kirch and
Stoehr 2022b
). Thus, as a second contribution, we build a
composite online detection scheme. Our method is inspired by the popular
“majority vote” classifiers (see the
review by
Dietterich 2000
), where different classification
rules are combined into one rule based e.g. on the mode (or
“majority vote”) thereof. We propose a
“veto-based” changepoint learning
procedure, where, in essence, at each point in time
t\geq m+1
the CUSUM process is simultaneously weighted using several weighing schemes, with a
changepoint being flagged up as long as the null is rejected for at least
one set of weights. Heuristically, this procedure should yield the fastest
changepoint detection, irrespective of the location of the changepoint,
whilst still offering size control. Referring to the papers by
Kirch and
Weber 2018
,
Yu
et al. 2020
, and
Romano
et al. 2023

mentioned above, in essence the sequential detection methodology they
investigate is based on using a combination of CUSUM functionals with
different windows, which is highly effective but comes at a relatively high
computational cost. In our case, we similarly propose the combination of
several statistics constructed with different specifications, but at a much
lower computational cost.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. We present our model and
the main assumptions, in Section
2
. Rényi statistics are
studied in Section
3
; veto-based statistics are in Section
4
. We report a comprehensive Monte Carlo exercise in Section
5
.
Section
6
concludes.

2.
Model and assumptions

In this section, we introduce our main model and assumptions. Our workhorse
model is the linear regression model of equation (
1
), viz.

y_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\beta_{t}+\epsilon_{t},

where
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is a
d
-dimensional vector of covariates with
coordinates denoted as
x_{j,t}
,
1\leq j\leq d
, including a constant -
viz.
x_{1,t}=1
,
t\geq 1
. In (
1
), the regression
coefficients
\beta_{t}
may be time-varying. We begin with considering a
static model, where no lagged values of
y_{t}
are used; in Section
3.3
, we extend our results to the dynamic model. As a final remark, (
1
) naturally nests the standard location model, where
d=1
,
and sequential monitoring boils down to monitoring for shifts in the mean of

y_{t}
.

We now list the main assumptions. We begin by providing a definition of
L_{\nu}
-decomposable Bernoulli shifts
.

Definition 1
.

The sequence
\left\{m_{t},-\infty<t<\infty\right\}

forms an
L_{\nu}
-decomposable Bernoulli shift if and only if
m_{t}=h\left(\eta_{t},\eta_{t-1},...\right)
, where:
\left\{\eta_{t},-\infty<t<\infty\right\}
is an i.i.d. sequence with values
in a measurable space
S
;
h\left(\cdot\right):S^{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a non random measurable function;
\left|m_{t}\right|_{\nu}<\infty
; and
\left|m_{t}-\widetilde{m}_{t,\ell}\right|_{\nu}\leq c_{0}\ell^{-a}
, for some
c_{0}>0
and
a>0
, where
\widetilde{m}_{t,\ell}=h\left(\eta_{t},...,\eta_{t-\ell+1},\widetilde{\eta}_{t-\ell,t,\ell},\right.

\left.\widetilde{\eta}_{t-\ell-1,t,\ell}...\right)
, with
\left\{\widetilde{\eta}_{s,t,\ell},-\infty<s,\ell,t<\infty\right\}
i.i.d. copies of
\eta_{0}
, independent of
\left\{\eta_{t},-\infty<t<\infty\right\}
.

Since the seminal works by
Wu 2005
and
Berkes
et al. 2011
, decomposable Bernoulli shifts have proven a
convenient way to model dependent time series, mainly due to their
generality and to the fact that it is much easier to verify whether a
sequence forms a decomposable Bernoulli shift than e.g. verifying mixing
conditions. Virtually all the most common DGPs in econometrics and
statistics can be shown to satisfy Definition
1
:
Liu and
Lin 2009
,
inter alia
, provide various theoretical
results, and numerous examples including ARMA models, ARCH/GARCH sequences,
and other nonlinear time series models (such as e.g. random coefficient
autoregressive models and threshold models). Moreover, it is easy to verify
that if
\left\{m_{t},-\infty<t<\infty\right\}
is an
L_{\nu}
-decomposable Bernoulli shift, then
\left\{m_{t}^{\kappa},-\infty<t<\infty\right\}
is an
L_{\nu/\kappa}
-decomposable Bernoulli shift.
Hence, the weak dependence of Definition
1
is sufficiently
flexible to allow to study changes in the mean, and also in higher order
moments, of
m_{t}
.

We are now ready to present our assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

(i)
\left\{\epsilon_{t},-\infty<t<\infty\right\}

forms an
L_{4}
-decomposable Bernoulli shift with
E\left(\epsilon_{t}\right)=0
,
\left|\epsilon_{t}\right|_{2}>0
, and
a>2
;
(ii) for
2\leq j\leq d
,
\left\{x_{j,t},-\infty<t<\infty\right\}

forms an
L_{4}
-decomposable Bernoulli shift with
a>2
.

Assumption 2
.

(i)
E\left(\epsilon_{t}\right)=0
; (ii)
E\left(x_{j,t}\epsilon_{t}\right)=0
, for all
2\leq j\leq d
; (iii)
E\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\right)=\mathbf{C}
, where
\mathbf{C}
is such that
0<s_{\min}\left(\mathbf{C}\right)\leq s_{\max}\left(\mathbf{C}\right)<\infty
.

Assumption
1
requires that both the error term
\epsilon_{t}

and the regressors
\mathbf{x}_{t}
admit at least four moments. In essence,
this assumption entails the validity of various limit theorems, and in
particular of a uniform estimate of the convergence rate (also known as
“strong approximation”) of the invariance
principle (see Lemma
B.1
). In principle, it would be possible to
reduce the moment existence requirement, upon assuming independence between
\epsilon_{t}
and
\mathbf{x}_{t}
. As far as serial dependence is
concerned, the requirement that
a>2
is quite mild; indeed the DGPs
typically used in statistics and econometrics have an
exponential

rate of decay for
\left|m_{t}-\widetilde{m}_{t,\ell}\right|_{\nu}
. As far as Assumption
2
is concerned, parts
(i)

and
(ii)
are standard; part
(iii)
rules out collinearity,
by stating that the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{C}
is bounded away from
zero.

3.
Sequential detection of changepoints

Our statistics are based on comparing the regression coefficients estimated
at each point in time over the monitoring horizon against a benchmark
estimate obtained during a training period
1\leq t\leq m
in which no break
was observed.

Assumption 3
.

\beta_{t}=\beta_{0}
, for all
1\leq t\leq m
.

After
m
, the monitoring procedure starts, for the null hypothesis of (
2
). Let
\widehat{\beta}_{m}
be the LS estimator using data in the
training sample, viz.

(3)

\widehat{\beta}_{m}=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}y_{t}\right),

and define the LS residuals

(4)

\widehat{\epsilon}_{t}=y_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\widehat{\beta}_{m},\text{ for }t\geq m+1.

Heuristically, if no changepoint is present,
\widehat{\beta}_{m}
is a
valid estimator of
\beta_{0}
throughout the monitoring period; hence, it
can be expected that
\widehat{\epsilon}_{t}
will fluctuate across zero
for all
t\geq m+1
. Conversely, in the presence of a break, this creates a
bias term in
\widehat{\epsilon}_{t}
. Hence, a natural way of testing for
changepoints is to use, as detector, a CUSUM-type statistic defined as the
partial sums process of the residuals over the monitoring horizon, i.e.

(5)

Q\left(m,k\right)=\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\widehat{\epsilon}_{t},\text{ \ \ for
}k\geq 1.

We assume that the monitoring does not go on forever, but it is carried out
over a horizon of length, say,
T_{m}
, after which it is terminated.

Assumption 4
.

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}T_{m}=\infty
.

Note that, in Assumption
4
, the monitoring horizon can be
“long”, i.e. we allow for
T_{m}=\Omega\left(m^{\lambda}\right)
with
\lambda\geq 1
, but it can also be
“short”, i.e.
T_{m}=o\left(m\right)
;
the latter case is seldom considered in the literature. Under such a
closed-ended monitoring scheme, our null hypothesis of interest in (
2
) becomes

(6)

H_{0}:\beta_{t}=\beta_{0}\text{ for all }m+1\leq t\leq m+T_{m}.

In practice,
Q\left(m;k\right)
is compared against a function of
k
,
and, as soon as it exceeds it, a changepoint is detected. Such a function is
called the
boundary function
; its specification is important in
order to ensure the procedure-wise control of Type I Errors under
H_{0}

and, on the other hand, to ensure power and a timely detection in the
presence of a break.
Horváth et al. 2004
and
Horváth et al. 2007
suggest using, in (
9
), a family
of boundary function indexed by a user-chosen parameter
0\leq\eta\leq 1/2
, defined as

(7)

g_{\eta}(m,k)=\sigma m^{1/2}\left(1+\frac{k}{m}\right)\left(\frac{k}{m+k}\right)^{\eta},

where

(8)

\sigma^{2}=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}E\left(m^{-1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\epsilon_{t}\right)^{2},

is the long-run variance of
\left\{\epsilon_{t},-\infty<t<\infty\right\}
. Hence, a changepoint is marked according to the decision rule

(9)

\tau_{m}=\begin{cases}\inf\{k\geq 1:|Q\left(m;k\right)|\geq c_{\alpha,\eta}g_{\eta}\left(m;k\right)\},\\
T_{m},\ \text{if}\ |Q\left(m;k\right)|\leq c_{\alpha,\eta}g_{\eta}\left(m;k\right)\ \text{for\ all}\ 1\leq k\leq T_{m},\end{cases}

where
c_{\alpha,\eta}
is a critical value for a pre-specified,
user-defined nominal size
\alpha
. In (
7
), intuitively, the
term
m^{1/2}\left(1+k/m\right)
is a norming sequence which ensures that
Q\left(m;k\right)
is properly normed; conversely,
\left(k/\left(m+k\right)\right)^{\eta}
is a weight function. Heuristically, the larger

\eta
, the smaller the boundary function
g_{\eta}(m,k)
, and therefore
the higher the detection ability of our monitoring scheme.
Aue and
Horváth 2004
and
Aue
et al. 2009
prove that this intuition is
correct, by showing that the detection delay in the presence of a break is
inversely related to
\eta
. Building on this intuition, here we study (
7
) with
\eta\in(1/2,\infty)
.

3.1.
Asymptotics under the null for Rényi statistics

In order to study the limiting distribution under the null, note that (
9
) states that the event
\left\{\tau_{m}<T_{m}\right\}
is
tantamount to having

\max_{1\leq k\leq T_{m}}\left|Q\left(m;k\right)\right|\geq c_{\alpha,\eta}g_{\eta}(m;k).

Using the Law of the Iterated Logarithm, it can be shown that
\max_{1\leq k\leq T_{m}}\left|Q\left(m;k\right)\right|\geq c_{\alpha,\eta}g_{\eta}(m;k)\overset{a.s.}{\rightarrow}\infty
; however, we can derive
a well-defined limit for

\max_{a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{\left|Q\left(m;k\right)\right|}{g_{\eta}(m;k)}\geq c_{\alpha,\eta},

for a user-specified trimming sequence
a_{m}
such that

(10)

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}a_{m}=\infty\text{, \ \ and \ \ }\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{a_{m}}{\min\left\{m,T_{m}\right\}}=0.

In (
10
),
a_{m}
needs to diverge as
m\rightarrow\infty
, but this
can happen at an arbitrarily slow rate. From an operational viewpoint, this
entails that our monitoring scheme cannot start straight after
m
, but only
after
m+a_{m}
periods, so that (
9
) modifies into

(11)

\tau_{m}=\begin{cases}\inf\{k\geq a_{m}:|Q\left(m;k\right)|\geq c_{\alpha,\eta}g_{\eta}(m;k)\},\\
T_{m},\ \text{if}\ |Q\left(m;k\right)|\leq c_{\alpha,\eta}g_{\eta}(m;k)\ \text{for\ all}\ a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m};\end{cases}

of course,
a_{m}
cannot be longer than
T_{m}
.

Define the norming sequence
r_{m}=a_{m}/\left(a_{m}+m\right)
. The
following theorem provides the limiting distribution of our statistics under
the null hypothesis.

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
4
and (
10
), as
m\rightarrow\infty
with
d=O\left(m^{1/4}\right)
, it holds
that, for all
\eta>1/2

r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\max_{a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{\left|Q(m;k)\right|}{g_{\eta}(m;k)}\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\rightarrow}\sup_{1\leq u<\infty}\frac{\left|W(u)\right|}{u^{\eta}}.

Theorem
1
provides the weak limit of the ratio between the
detector
Q(m;k)
and the boundary function
g_{\eta}\left(m;k\right)
,
thus offering a way of computing asymptotic critical values. We are not
aware of any closed form expression for the quantiles of
\sup_{1\leq u<\infty}u^{-\eta}|W(u)|
, which therefore must be computed by simulation.
However, when
\eta\leq 1
, exploiting the scale transformation of the
Wiener process we obtain

\sup_{1\leq u<\infty}\frac{|W(u)|}{u^{\eta}}\overset{\mathcal{D}}{=}\sup_{1\leq u<\infty}\frac{|W(u^{-1})|}{\left(u\right)^{\eta-1}}\overset{\mathcal{D}}{=}\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}\frac{|W(s)|}{s^{1-\eta}};

in such a case, the quantiles of
\sup_{1\leq u<\infty}u^{-\eta}|W(u)|

are the same as those in Table 1 in
Horváth et al. 2004
, computed
for the case
1-\eta
.

As far as
a_{m}
is concerned, it would be desirable to have
a_{m}
as
small as possible, so as to ensure a fast detection of breaks occurring
close to the beginning of the monitoring horizon. Also, Theorem
1
requires the restriction
d=O\left(m^{1/4}\right)
. This can
be read in two ways: on the one hand, given
m
, it poses a limit on the
dimension of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
the size of (
1
), indicating
that some dimension reduction must be carried out prior to monitoring (
1
). On the other hand, the size of the training sample
m
can be
viewed as a tuning parameter whose choice depends, inter alia, on
d
.

We conclude by noting that, in order to make our statistics feasible, an
estimator of the long-run variance
\sigma^{2}
is needed. Based on
Assumption
1
, we propose a standard,
weighted-sum-of-covariances estimator, computed using the full training
sample. Seeing as no breaks occur between
1\leq t\leq m
, such an estimator
can be expected to be consistent under both the null and the alternative.
Let
\widehat{\gamma}_{j}=m^{-1}\sum_{t=j+1}^{m}\widehat{\epsilon}_{t}\widehat{\epsilon}_{t-j}
; we propose the following Bartlett-type estimator

(12)

\widehat{\sigma}_{m}^{2}=\widehat{\gamma}_{0}+2\sum_{j=1}^{H}\left(1-\frac{j}{H+1}\right)\widehat{\gamma}_{j},

where
H
is a bandwidth parameter. Other estimators are possible, and we
refer to
Andrews 1991
and
Casini 2023
for useful references.

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
3
, it holds
that

\widehat{\sigma}_{m}^{2}-\sigma^{2}=O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{H}\right)+O_{P}\left(\frac{dH}{m^{1/2}}\right).

Lemma
1
indicates that, up to an appropriate choice of the bandwidth

H
,
\widehat{\sigma}_{m}^{2}
is a consistent estimator of
\sigma^{2}
.
The lemma suggests that a possible choice, designed to balance the
traditional “bias” term of order
O_{P}\left(1/H\right)
and the “variance” term of order
O_{P}\left(dH/m^{1/2}\right)
,
is
H=O\left(d^{-1/2}m^{1/4}\right)
.

3.2.
Monitoring schemes based on Rényi statistics under
alternatives

Under the alternative, we assume that there is a changepoint at
k^{\ast}
:

(13)

H_{A}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\beta_{t}=\beta_{0}&1\leq t<m+k^{\ast},\\
\beta_{t}=\beta_{A}&m+k^{\ast}\leq t\leq m+T_{m}.\end{array}\right.

We allow the break size to depend on the sample size
m
, thus defining
\Delta_{m}=\beta_{A}-\beta_{0}
.

Theorem 2
.

We assume that Assumptions
1
-
4
, (
10
) and
\mathbf{c}_{1}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\neq 0
, are satisfied.
Assume further that either: (i)
k^{\ast}=o\left(a_{m}\right)
and

(14)

\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}a_{m}^{1/2}\mathbf{c}_{1}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}=\infty;

or (ii)
\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}a_{m}^{-1}k^{\ast}>0
,
\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}T_{m}/k^{\ast}>1
and

(15)

\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}m^{1/2}\left(\frac{k^{\ast}}{m+k^{\ast}}\right)^{1-\eta}\mathbf{c}_{1}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}=\infty.

Then it holds that, for all
\eta>1/2
,
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\tau_{m}<T_{m}|H_{A}\right)=1
.

Part
(i)
of Theorem
2
states that our statistics have
power versus a changepoint occurring close to the beginning of the
monitoring horizon. Interestingly, under (
14
), breaks can be
“small”, and the case
\left\|\Delta_{m}\right\|\rightarrow 0
as
m\rightarrow\infty
can be considered;
however, breaks cannot be “too small”.
Conceptually similar results are derived in
Horváth and
Trapani 2022

for the case of offline changepoint detection. According to part
(ii)
of the theorem, tests also have power versus later occurring breaks, i.e.
under
\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}a_{m}^{-1}k^{\ast}>0
and (
15
). For example, considering the case of
k^{\ast}=O\left(m\right)
, condition (
15
) boils down to

(16)

\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}a_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\left(k^{\ast}\right)^{1-\eta}\left|\mathbf{c}_{1}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\right|=\infty,

which indicates that, whenever
\eta\leq 1
, detection will take place.
However, upon carefully studying the proof of Theorem 2.2 in
Horváth et al. 2004
, it follows that whenever
\eta<1/2

changepoints are detected as long as
m^{1/2}\left|\mathbf{c}_{1}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\right|\rightarrow\infty
, which is more
easily satisfied than (
16
), and allows for smaller
changepoint magnitudes. Hence, whilst ensuring power versus early
changepoints, the use of heavy weights in the boundary function
g_{\eta}\left(m;k\right)
reduces the ability to detect changepoints occurring
later on during the monitoring horizon. This can be seen by considering the
case of
\eta>1
in (
16
), or the case where
m^{-1}k^{\ast}\rightarrow\infty
. In the latter case, condition
(ii)
is tantamount to

(17)

\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}a_{m}^{\eta-1/2}m^{1-\eta}\left|\mathbf{c}_{1}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\right|=\infty,

where again values of
\eta>1
are detrimental to the power of the
monitoring scheme. Finally, although we have focussed on the case of an
abrupt break, following our proofs it is possible to verify that our
monitoring schemes have power also in the presence of smooth breaks.

Theorem
2
indicates that our statistics seem particularly suited
to the detection of early changepoints. We now study this case more
in-depth, considering the asymptotics for the detection delay in the
presence of early occurring changes.

Assumption 5
.

(i)
k^{\ast}=o\left(a_{m}\right)
; (ii)
\left\|\mathbf{c}_{1}\right\|=\Omega\left(d^{1/2}\right)
; (iii)
as
m\rightarrow\infty
,
a_{m}^{1/2}d^{1/2}\left\|\Delta_{m}\right\|\rightarrow\infty
.

Assumption
5
deals with the location and the size of
the changepoint. According to part
(i)
, the break is assumed to
occur early, before the start of the monitoring procedure. This can be read
in conjunction with similar results derived in
Aue and
Horváth 2004
and
Aue
et al. 2009
, where the asymptotic distribution of
\tau_{m}
is
derived under the assumption of an early break.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
5
and (
10
), it holds that
\tau_{m}\overset{\mathcal{P}}{\rightarrow}a_{m}
.

Theorem
3
states that, if a break occurs prior to the trimming
point
a_{m}
, then it is detected immediately at the start of the
monitoring procedure. This result offers an important insight on the
relevance and practical use of our statistics. The theory derived in
Aue and
Horváth 2004
and
Aue et al. 2008
indicates that using
\eta\leq 1/2
ensures detection of changepoints occurring not too soon
after
m
; in particular, using
\eta=1/2
ensures a short delay - of order

O\left(\sqrt{\ln\ln m}\right)
- in detecting breaks occurring
o\left(\sqrt{\ln\ln m}\right)
periods from
m
. Based on this and on Theorem
3
, in the case of our statistics, the delay is of order
a_{m}
for
breaks occurring
o\left(a_{m}\right)
periods from
m
: upon choosing
a_{m}=o\left(\sqrt{\ln\ln m}\right)
, this entails that early occurring
breaks are found even more quickly than when using
\eta=1/2
, with the
added advantage that one does not need to use the asymptotic critical values
computed for the case
\eta=1/2
, where convergence is notoriously slow.

4.
Veto-based changepoint learning

Our theory, and the theory developed in other contributions (e.g.
Aue and
Horváth 2004
and
Aue et al. 2008
), suggests that
different values of
\eta
work better for different changepoint locations.
Hence, any sequential monitoring methodology based on a specific
\eta
can
be expected to offer superior detection timeliness in some cases, and to be
dominated by different choices of
\eta
in other cases; this issue is also
echoed in a recent contribution by
Kirch and
Stoehr 2022b
.

In this section, we consider a combination of several detection rules. Upon
inspecting our proofs and the proofs in
Horváth et al. 2004
, it
can be shown that test statistics using
\eta<1/2
and
\eta>1/2
are
asymptotically independent of each other,
1
1

1

Intuitively, this is due to the facts that: (1) the limiting distribution
when
\eta<1/2
is determined by the “central” part of
\left\{W\left(u\right),u\geq 0\right\}
, whereas, when using
\eta>1/2
, the limit is completely
determined by the “early” part of
\left\{W\left(u\right),u\geq 0\right\}
(see also the comments at the
end of the proof of Theorem
1
); and (2) that the Wiener
process has independent increments.
although of course two or more
statistics with different
\eta>1/2
are not independent of each other (the
same holds for statistics with different
\eta<1/2
). Hence, it is in
principle possible to combine different test statistics, although using a
combination of tests, e.g. with the Bonferroni correction, is bound to be
subject to the typical criticisms of being overconservative and, therefore,
liable to not detect (timely, or at all) a changepoint. Hence, in the light
of the considerations above, and in order to ensure the fast changepoint
detection whilst controlling for false positives, we propose a different,
composite sequential detection scheme. We base our methodology on a
“veto-based” approach, where several
statistics (corresponding to different choices of
\eta
) are employed
simultaneously, and the null is rejected as long as at least one statistic
exceeds its critical value. Heuristically, the veto-based decision rule
offers a compromise between the various choices of
\eta
, ensuring that
detection delay is good irrespective of the changepoint location.

Recall the detector
Q\left(m;k\right)
defined in (
5
). For each
choice of
\eta_{j}
,
j=1,2,...,J
, we have a corresponding boundary
function
g_{\eta_{j}}(m;k)
, and a critical value at nominal level
\alpha
,
c_{\alpha,\eta_{j}}
, with rejection occurring at the first value of
k\geq 1
such that
\left|Q\left(m;k\right)\right|>c_{\alpha,\eta_{j}}g_{\eta_{j}}(m;k)
. We combine several statistics using
0\leq\eta_{j}\leq 1
; recall also that, when
\eta_{j}>1/2
, the monitoring
starts after
a_{m}
periods from the beginning of the
1\leq k\leq T_{m}

horizon. Hence, the veto-based procedure stops at
\widetilde{\tau}_{m}

defined as

(19)

\widetilde{\tau}_{m}=\begin{cases}\inf\{k\geq 1:|Q\left(m;k\right)|\geq C_{\alpha}\min_{1\leq j\leq J}c_{\alpha,\eta_{j}}d_{a_{m},\eta}g_{\eta_{j}}\left(m;k\right)\},\\
T_{m},\ \text{if}\ |Q\left(m;k\right)|\leq C_{\alpha}\min_{1\leq j\leq J}c_{\alpha,\eta_{j}}d_{a_{m},\eta}g_{\eta_{j}}\left(m;k\right)\ \text{for\ all}\ 1\leq k\leq T_{m},\end{cases}

where

\widetilde{r}_{m}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if }\eta_{j}<1/2,\\
r_{m}&\text{if }\eta_{j}\geq 1/2,\end{array}\right.

and

d_{a_{m},\eta_{j}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if }k\geq a_{m}\text{ \ or \ }\eta_{j}<1/2,\\
\infty&\text{if }k<a_{m}\text{ \ and \ }\eta_{j}>1/2.\end{array}\right.

In (
19
),
C_{\alpha}
is the procedure-wise critical value,
such that
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\widetilde{\tau}_{m}<T_{m}|H_{0}\right)=\alpha
.

In order to implement (
19
), we require an asymptotic
approximation of the procedure-wise critical value
C_{\alpha}
. Define
\widetilde{\eta}_{j}=\eta_{j}I\left(\eta_{j}\leq 1/2\right)+\left(1-\eta_{j}\right)I\left(\eta_{j}>1/2\right)
, and modify Assumption
4
to consider a long monitoring horizon.

Assumption 7
.

(i)
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}T_{m}=\infty
; (ii)
\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}T_{m}/m>0
; (iii)
T_{m}=\Omega\left(m^{\lambda}\right)
, with
\lambda\geq 1
.

Assumption
7
is required to derive the asymptotics of
monitoring statistics with
\eta<1/2
(see
Horváth et al. 2004
).

The following results characterise the null distribution, and the power
under alternatives, of veto-based statistics.

Theorem 5
.

We assume that Assumptions
1
-
3
and
7
, (
10
) and
d=o\left(m^{1/4}\right)
, are satisfied. Then, under the null, as
m\rightarrow\infty
it holds that

(20)

\max_{1\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{\left|Q\left(m;k\right)\right|}{\min_{1\leq j\leq J}c_{\alpha,\eta_{j}}\widetilde{r}_{m}^{1/2-\eta_{j}}d_{a_{m},\eta}g_{\eta_{j}}(m;k)}\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\rightarrow}\sup_{0<u<1}\frac{\left|W\left(u\right)\right|}{\min_{1\leq j\leq J}c_{\alpha,\eta_{j}}u^{\widetilde{\eta}_{j}}}.

Theorem 6
.

We assume that the assumptions of Theorem
5

hold, and further that either: (i)
k^{\ast}=o\left(a_{m}\right)
and (
14
); or (ii)
\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}a_{m}^{-1}k^{\ast}>0
and (
15
) hold; or (iii) that
k^{\ast}=O\left(m\right)

and

(21)

\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\max_{1\leq j\leq J}\frac{\left(k^{\ast}\right)^{1-\eta_{j}}}{\widetilde{r}_{m}^{1/2-\eta_{j}}m^{1/2-\eta_{j}}}\left|\mathbf{c}_{1}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\right|=\infty.

Then it holds that

(22)

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\widetilde{\tau}_{m}<T_{m}|H_{A}\right)=1.

Some comments are in order. Theorem
5
stipulates that,
asymptotically

(23)

P\left(\sup_{0<u<1}\frac{\left|W\left(u\right)\right|}{\min_{1\leq j\leq J}c_{\alpha,\eta_{j}}u^{\widetilde{\eta}_{j}}}\leq C_{\alpha}\right)=\alpha;

hence,
C_{\alpha}
can be obtained by standard Monte Carlo techniques.

According to Theorem
6
, the veto-based decision rule does
have power versus changepoints; note that the proposition studies only the
case
k^{\ast}=O\left(m\right)
- i.e. breaks occurring not too late
during the monitoring period - but it could be extended to the case where
k^{\ast}
is “bigger” than
m
after
elementary, if tedious, algebra. Condition (
21
) is a
high-level requirement which can be more easily interpreted by considering
some leading examples. To begin with, we investigate the case
k^{\ast}=c_{0}m
, corresponding to a break occurring later on during the monitoring
horizon. In such a case, a possible question is how small can the size of
the break,
\left|\mathbf{c}_{1}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\right|
, be.
When
\eta_{j}<1/2
, the non-centrality parameter in (
21
)
is proportional to
m^{1/2}\left|\mathbf{c}_{1}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\right|
, which entails that nontrivial power is found versus
breaks of magnitude at least
O\left(m^{-1/2}\right)
; note that this
holds irrespective of the actual value of
\eta_{j}
. On the other hand,
when
\eta_{j}>1/2
, (
21
) is proportional to
a_{m}^{\eta_{j}-1/2}m^{1-\eta_{j}}\left|\mathbf{c}_{1}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\right|
, suggesting that the larger the choice of
\eta_{j}
, the
larger
\left|\mathbf{c}_{1}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\right|
needs to
be to be detected. In this case, the veto-based rule will be triggered by
the statistics with
\eta_{j}<1/2
, thus ensuring power versus small breaks.

In conclusion, we would like to point out that an alternative approach to
the “classical” CUSUM detectors employed
here, is the so-called Page-CUSUM detectors (see e.g.
Fremdt 2015
,
Kirch and
Weber 2018
, and
Romano
et al. 2023
), defined as

(24)

Q^{\dagger}(m;k)=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\left|\sum_{i=m+\ell}^{m+k}\frac{\left(y_{i}-\widehat{\beta}_{m}y_{i-1}\right)y_{i-1}}{1+y_{i-1}^{2}}\right|.

Horváth and
Trapani 2023
consider the weighted version of (
24
). Intuitively,
at each point in time
1\leq k\leq T_{m}
, the detector is set equal to the
“worst case” observed up to
k
. This
should make the detector
Q^{\dagger}(m;k)
more sensitive, and more prone
to using only the observations which, up to time
k
, are affected by a
possible changepoint. Indeed, the simulations in
Horváth and
Trapani 2023
show that
the use of the Page-CUSUM detector does offer some improvement on the
standard CUSUM, but the main gains can be ascribed to the use of heavier and
heavier weighing schemes. Furthermore, the use of Page-CUSUM methodologies
is marred by their computational complexity (see however the solution
proposed in a recent contribution by
Romano
et al. 2023
). In this
respect, the veto-based approach suggested in this section is similar, in
spirit, to (
24
); instead of using the “worst case
scenario” across
k
, we use the (computationally simpler)
“worst case scenario” across several
values of
\eta
.

6.
Conclusions and discussion

In this paper, we investigate the well-known, and highly important, issue of
(fast) online changepoint detection. We focus on a linear regression model,
in the possible presence of weak dependence, which also nests the cases of
changepoint detection in the location of a time series
\left\{y_{t},-\infty<t<\infty\right\}
; moreover, we use a flexible definition
of dependence which allows to readily extend our methods to test for changes
in the higher order moments of
\left\{y_{t},-\infty<t<\infty\right\}
.

We make two contributions. First, we introduce a class of statistics, called

Rényi statistics
and based on the heavily weighted CUSUM
process of the regression residuals, which are specifically designed to
detect early occurring changepoints. Second, we propose a composite,
veto-based, test statistic which combines different weighted versions of the
CUSUM process of the residuals, designed to ensure timely detection of
changepoints occurring at any point in time over the monitoring horizon. Our
Monte Carlo evidence shows that our statistics offer good control of the
probability of the procedure-wise Type I Error under the null, also
affording timely detection under the alternative. Whilst we focus, as far as
the empirical illustration is concerned, on a macroeconomic example, we
would like to point out that our methodology can be applied to a wide
variety of contexts in virtually all applied sciences.

Several interesting questions remain open and are worth pursuing. In the
construction of heavily weighted CUSUM statistics, the only tuning parameter
is the sequence
a_{m}
defined in (
10
). Our simulations suggest that
a good choice to ensure both size control and timely detction is
a_{m}=\ln\ln m
in all cases considered; however, having an automated, data-driven
selection rule would be desirable. Heuristically, our proofs (and our
simulations) show that the quality of the asymptotic distribution under the
null is determined by two factors: the speed of convergence in the Gaussian
approximation (see Lemma
B.1
), and the impact of
a_{m}
in the
limiting behaviour of
\max_{a_{m}/m\leq u\leq T_{m}/m}\left|W\left(u/\left(1+u\right)\right)\right|/\left(u/\left(1+u\right)\right)^{\eta}
(see the proof of Theorem
1
). Seeing as
a_{m}

plays a role only in determining the limit of a heavily weighted Wiener
process, a possible suggestion to the applied user would be to choose the
optimal
a_{m}
by running a simulation with
i.i.d.
N\left(0,1\right)
data, generated with training and monitoring samples of size
m

and
T_{m}
respectively, determining which choice of
a_{m}
yields the
best size control without having an overly conservative procedure. As a
second important extension, recently the literature has considered
extensions of online change-point detection methods to different data
structures such as e.g. network data.
Yu
et al. 2020
and
Dubey
et al. 2021
study online changepoint detection in networks, and
Wang
et al. 2021
extend the CUSUM to the network case. Our
methodologies could lend themselves to being extended to other types of data
structures. These issues have a high priority on the authors’ research
agenda.

B.
Preliminary lemmas

Henceforth, we use the following notation:
\left\lceil\varrho\right\rceil
is the ceiling of a real number
\varrho
. We also use the convention
that, if a summation involves a non-integer index, this is rounded up - e.g.

\sum_{i=1}^{\varrho}=\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lceil\varrho\right\rceil}
for
any
\varrho>1
.

Lemma B.1
.

We assume that Assumption
1
(i) holds. Then,
for every
m
, two independent standard Wiener processes
\left\{W_{1,m}\left(k\right),k\geq 1\right\}
and
\left\{W_{2,m}\left(k\right),k\geq 1\right\}
whose distribution does not depend on
m
can
be defined on a suitably larger probability space such that, for some
0<\zeta_{1}<1/2
, it holds that

(B.1)

\max_{1\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{1}{k^{\zeta_{1}}}\left|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\epsilon_{t}-\sigma W_{1,m}(k)\right|=O_{P}(1),

and

(B.2)

\frac{1}{m^{\zeta_{2}}}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{m}\epsilon_{t}-\sigma W_{2,m}(m)\right|=O_{P}(1),

where
\sigma^{2}
is defined in (
8
).

Proof.

The desired result follows immediately from applying Theorem B.1 in
Aue et al. 2014
; note that the proof of the theorem is based on the
blocking argument, whence the independence of
\left\{W_{1,m}\left(k\right),k\geq 1\right\}
and
\left\{W_{2,m}\left(k\right),k\geq 1\right\}
.

Lemma B.2
.

We assume that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Then it holds that

\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\epsilon_{t}\right\|=O_{P}\left(\left(\frac{d}{m}\right)^{1/2}\right).

Proof.

We begin by estimating
E\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\epsilon_{t}\right\|^{2}
, noting that

(B.3)

E\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\epsilon_{t}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{m}x_{j,t}\epsilon_{t}\right|_{2}^{2}.

We now show that Assumptions
1
and
2
entail that
the sequence
z_{j,t}=x_{j,t}\epsilon_{t}
is a zero mean,
L_{2}
-decomposable Bernoulli shift. Indeed, Assumption
2

immediately yields
E\left(z_{j,t}\right)=0
; further, consider the
coupling constructions

\displaystyle\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}=h_{j}^{x}\left(\eta_{j,t}^{x},...,\eta_{j,1}^{x},\widetilde{\eta}_{j,0,t,t}^{x},\widetilde{\eta}_{j,-1,t,t}^{x},...\right),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\epsilon}_{t,t}=h^{\epsilon}\left(\eta_{t}^{\epsilon},...,\eta_{1}^{\epsilon},\widetilde{\eta}_{0,t,t}^{\epsilon},\widetilde{\eta}_{-1,t,t}^{\epsilon},...\right),

where
\left\{\widetilde{\eta}_{j,0,t,t}^{x},\widetilde{\eta}_{j,-1,t,t}^{x},...\right\}
are independent copies of
\left\{\eta_{j,0}^{x},\eta_{j,-1}^{x},...\right\}
, and similarly
\left\{\widetilde{\eta}_{j,0,t,t}^{\epsilon},\widetilde{\eta}_{j,-1,t,t}^{\epsilon},...\right\}
. It holds that

\displaystyle x_{j,t}\epsilon_{t}=

\displaystyle\left(x_{j,t}\pm\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right)\left(\epsilon_{t}\pm\widetilde{\epsilon}_{t,t}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{z}_{j,t,t}+\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\left(\epsilon_{t}-\widetilde{\epsilon}_{t,t}\right)+\widetilde{\epsilon}_{t,t}\left(x_{j,t}-\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right)+\left(x_{j,t}-\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right)\left(\epsilon_{t}-\widetilde{\epsilon}_{t,t}\right),

where

\widetilde{z}_{j,t,t}=\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\widetilde{\epsilon}_{t,t},

is the coupled version of
z_{j,t}
. Hence it follows that

\displaystyle\left|z_{j,t}-\widetilde{z}_{j,t,t}\right|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\left(\epsilon_{t}-\widetilde{\epsilon}_{t,t}\right)\right|_{2}+\left|\widetilde{\epsilon}_{t,t}\left(x_{j,t}-\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right)\right|_{2}+\left|\left(x_{j,t}-\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right)\left(\epsilon_{t}-\widetilde{\epsilon}_{t,t}\right)\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right|_{4}\left|\epsilon_{t}-\widetilde{\epsilon}_{t,t}\right|_{4}+\left|x_{j,t}-\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right|_{4}\left|\widetilde{\epsilon}_{t,t}\right|_{4}+\left|\epsilon_{t}-\widetilde{\epsilon}_{t,t}\right|_{4}\left|x_{j,t}-\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right|_{4}

having used Minkowski’s inequality in the first line, and the
Cauchy-Schwartz inequality in the second one. Assumption
1
now
immediately yields that there exists a constant
0<c_{0}<\infty
such that

\left|z_{j,t}-\widetilde{z}_{j,t,t}\right|_{2}\leq c_{0}t^{-a},

for
a>2
. Hence, we can directly apply Proposition 4 in
Berkes
et al. 2011
, obtaining that, for all
1\leq j\leq d
, there
exist constants
c_{j}<\infty
such that

(B.4)

\left|\sum_{t=1}^{m}x_{j,t}\epsilon_{t}\right|_{2}^{2}\leq c_{j}m.

Putting (
B.4
) in (
B.3
), we finally obtain that
E\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\epsilon_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq c_{0}dm
, whence the desired result follows from Markov inequality.

Lemma B.3
.

We assume that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Then it holds that

\max_{1\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{1}{k^{\zeta_{3}}}\left\|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{c}_{1})\right\|=O_{P}(d^{1/2}),

for all
\zeta_{3}>1/2,
where
\mathbf{c}_{1}=(1,E(x_{2,0}),...,E(x_{d,0}))^{\prime}
.

Proof.

It holds that

\displaystyle P\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{1}{k^{\zeta_{3}}}\left\|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{c}_{1})\right\|\geq xd^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle P\left(\max_{0\leq\ell\leq\left\lceil\ln T_{m}\right\rceil}\max_{\exp\left(\ell\right)\leq k\leq\exp\left(\ell+1\right)}\frac{1}{k^{\zeta_{3}}}\left\|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{c}_{1})\right\|\geq xd^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lceil\ln T_{m}\right\rceil}P\left(\max_{\exp\left(\ell\right)\leq k\leq\exp\left(\ell+1\right)}\frac{1}{k^{\zeta_{3}}}\left\|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{c}_{1})\right\|\geq xd^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lceil\ln T_{m}\right\rceil}P\left(\max_{\exp\left(\ell\right)\leq k\leq\exp\left(\ell+1\right)}\left\|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{c}_{1})\right\|\geq xd^{1/2}\exp\left(\zeta_{3}\ell\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{0}x^{-p}d^{-p/2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lceil\ln T_{m}\right\rceil}\exp\left(-p\zeta_{3}\ell\right)E\left(\max_{\exp\left(\ell\right)\leq k\leq\exp\left(\ell+1\right)}\left\|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{c}_{1})\right\|^{p}\right)

for any
2\leq p\leq 4
. Further, we have

\displaystyle E\left(\max_{\exp\left(\ell\right)\leq k\leq\exp\left(\ell+1\right)}\left\|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{c}_{1})\right\|^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d^{p/2-1}\sum_{j=2}^{d}E\left(\max_{\exp\left(\ell\right)\leq k\leq\exp\left(\ell+1\right)}\left|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}(x_{j,t}-Ex_{j,0})\right|^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d^{p/2-1}\sum_{j=2}^{d}E\left(\max_{\exp\left(\ell\right)\leq k\leq\exp\left(\ell+1\right)}\left|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}(x_{j,t}-Ex_{j,0})\right|^{p}\right).

Recall that
x_{j,t}-Ex_{j,0}
is a zero mean,
L_{4}
-decomposable
Bernoulli shift which satisfies the assumptions of Proposition 4 in
Berkes
et al. 2011
; using Theorem 1 in
Móricz 1976
, it
follows that

E\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq\exp\left(\ell\right)}\left|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}(x_{j,t}-Ex_{j,0})\right|^{p}\right)\leq c_{j}\exp\left(\frac{p}{2}\ell\right).

Hence, putting all together it follows that

P\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{1}{k^{\zeta_{3}}}\left\|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{c}_{1})\right\|\geq xd^{1/2}\right)\leq c_{0}x^{-p}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\ln T_{m}}\exp\left(p\ell\left(-\zeta_{3}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)\leq c_{1},

whenever
\zeta_{3}>1/2
, which proves the desired result.

Lemma B.4
.

We assume that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Then it holds that

\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right)\right\|_{F}=O_{P}\left(\frac{d}{\sqrt{m}}\right)

Proof.

We begin by showing that, for all
2\leq h,j\leq d
,
x_{h,t}x_{j,t}
is an
L_{2}
-decomposable Bernoulli shift with
a>2
. Indeed, consider the coupling

\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}=g_{j}^{x}\left(\eta_{j,t}^{x},...,\eta_{j,1}^{x},\widetilde{\eta}_{j,0,t,t}^{x},\widetilde{\eta}_{j,-1,t,t}^{x},...\right),

where
\left\{\widetilde{\eta}_{j,0,t,t}^{x},\widetilde{\eta}_{j,-1,t,t}^{x},...\right\}
are independent copies of
\left\{\eta_{j,0}^{x},\eta_{j,-1}^{x},...\right\}
, and similarly
\left\{\widetilde{\eta}_{j,0,t,t}^{\epsilon},\widetilde{\eta}_{j,-1,t,t}^{\epsilon},...\right\}
,
2\leq j\leq d
. It holds that

\displaystyle x_{h,t}x_{j,t}=

\displaystyle\left(x_{h,t}\pm\widetilde{x}_{h,t,t}\right)\left(x_{j,t}\pm\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{z}_{j,h,t,t}+\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\left(x_{h,t}-\widetilde{x}_{h,t,t}\right)+\widetilde{x}_{h,t,t}\left(x_{j,t}-\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right)+\left(x_{j,t}-\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right)\left(x_{h,t}-\widetilde{x}_{h,t,t}\right),

where
\widetilde{z}_{j,h,t,t}=\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\widetilde{x}_{h,t,t}

is the coupled version of
z_{j,h,t}=x_{h,t}x_{j,t}
. Hence it follows that

\displaystyle\left|z_{j,t}-\widetilde{z}_{j,h,t,t}\right|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\left(x_{h,t}-\widetilde{x}_{h,t,t}\right)\right|_{2}+\left|\widetilde{x}_{h,t,t}\left(x_{j,t}-\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right)\right|_{2}+\left|\left(x_{j,t}-\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right)\left(x_{h,t}-\widetilde{x}_{h,t,t}\right)\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right|_{4}\left|x_{h,t}-\widetilde{x}_{h,t,t}\right|_{4}+\left|x_{j,t}-\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right|_{4}\left|\widetilde{x}_{h,t,t}\right|_{4}+\left|x_{j,t}-\widetilde{x}_{j,t,t}\right|_{4}\left|x_{h,t}-\widetilde{x}_{h,t,t}\right|_{4}

having used Minkowski’s inequality in the first line, and the
Cauchy-Schwartz inequality in the second one. Assumption
1
(ii)
now immediately yields that there exists a constant
0<c_{0}<\infty
such that

\left|z_{j,h,t}-\widetilde{z}_{j,h,t,t}\right|_{2}\leq c_{0}t^{-a}.

We now note that

\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j,h=1}^{d}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{m}\left(x_{h,t}x_{j,t}-\mathbf{C}_{h,j}\right)\right|^{2}.

Using Proposition 4 in
Berkes
et al. 2011
, it follows that

\left|\sum_{t=1}^{m}\left(x_{h,t}x_{j,t}-\mathbf{C}_{h,j}\right)\right|_{2}^{2}\leq c_{hj}m,

where the constants
c_{hj}
are finite. Hence the desired result
immediately follows from Markov inequality.

Lemma B.5
.

We assume that Assumptions
1
-
3
, and
d=O\left(m^{1/4}\right)
, hold. Then it holds that

\displaystyle r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\max_{a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{\left|\displaystyle\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\widehat{\epsilon}_{t}\right|}{m^{1/2}\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{k}{m}\right)\displaystyle\left(\frac{k}{k+m}\right)^{\eta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\max_{a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{\left|\displaystyle\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\epsilon_{t}-\displaystyle\frac{k}{m}\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{m}\epsilon_{t}\right|}{m^{1/2}\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{k}{m}\right)\displaystyle\left(\frac{k}{k+m}\right)^{\eta}}+o_{P}(1).

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\widehat{\epsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\left(y_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\widehat{\beta}_{m}\right)=\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\left(\epsilon_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\left(\widehat{\beta}_{m}-\beta_{0}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\epsilon_{t}-\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\epsilon_{t}\right).

We begin by showing that

r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\max_{a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{\left|\displaystyle\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\left(\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}-\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right)\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\epsilon_{t}\right)\right|}{m^{1/2}\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{k}{m}\right)\displaystyle\left(\frac{k}{k+m}\right)^{\eta}}=o_{P}(1).

Indeed

\displaystyle r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\max_{a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{\left|\displaystyle\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\left(\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}-\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right)\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\epsilon_{t}\right)\right|}{m^{1/2}\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{k}{m}\right)\displaystyle\left(\frac{k}{k+m}\right)^{\eta}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\max_{a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{\left\|\displaystyle\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\|\left\|\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}-\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right\|_{F}\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\epsilon_{t}\right\|}{m^{1/2}\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{k}{m}\right)\displaystyle\left(\frac{k}{k+m}\right)^{\eta}}.

By stationarity implied by Assumption
1
, and standard
arguments, it follows immediately that
\left\|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\|=O_{P}\left(d^{1/2}k\right)
. Further, note that, using
Taylor’s expansion

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}-\mathbf{C}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\pm\mathbf{C}\right)^{-1}=\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\left(\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\pm\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)\mathbf{C}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{C}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{C}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right)\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right)+o\left(\left\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right)\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right\|_{F}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right)\mathbf{C}^{-1}+o\left(\left\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right)\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right\|_{F}\right).

Hence it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}-\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right\|_{F}-\left\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right)\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o\left(\left\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right)\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right\|_{F}\right),

and

\left\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right)\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right\|_{F}\leq s_{\max}^{2}\left(\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right)\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right\|_{F}=O_{P}\left(\frac{d}{m^{1/2}}\right),

using Lemma
B.4
and seeing as
s_{\max}\left(\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right)=s_{\min}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{C}\right)
and, by Assumption
2
(iii)
,
s_{\min}\left(\mathbf{C}\right)>0
. By a
similar logic

\left\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right)\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right\|_{F}\leq s_{\max}^{2}\left(\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right)\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right\|_{F}=O_{P}\left(dm^{-1/2}\right).

Thus it finally follows that

(B.5)

\left\|\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}-\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right\|_{F}=O_{P}\left(\frac{d}{m^{1/2}}\right).

Finally, using Lemma
B.2
and (
B.5
)

\displaystyle r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\max_{a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\|\left\|\left(\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}-\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right)\right\|_{F}\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\epsilon_{t}\right\|}{m^{1/2}\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{k}{m}\right)\displaystyle\left(\frac{k}{k+m}\right)^{\eta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{P}\left(1\right)r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\max_{a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{\left(d^{1/2}k\right)\left(dm^{-1/2}\right)\left(d^{1/2}m^{-1/2}\right)}{m^{1/2}\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{k}{m}\right)\displaystyle\left(\frac{k}{k+m}\right)^{\eta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{P}\left(1\right)r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}d^{2}m^{-1/2}\max_{a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m}}\left(\frac{k}{k+m}\right)^{1-\eta}.

We note that, depending on the values of
\eta
, there are two bounds. If
\eta\leq 1
, then
\max_{a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m}}\left(k/\left(m+k\right)\right)^{1-\eta}
is bounded; conversely, if
\eta>1
, the maximum is
attained at
\left(a_{m}/\left(m+a_{m}\right)\right)^{1-\eta}
. Hence,
putting all together, it finally holds that

\displaystyle r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\max_{a_{m}\leq k\leq T_{m}}\frac{\left|\sum_{t=m+1}^{m+k}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}\right)^{-1}\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}\epsilon_{t}\right)\right|}{m^{1/2}\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{k}{m}\right)\displaystyle\left(\frac{k}{k+m}\right)^{\eta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{P}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{m^{\min\left\{1,\eta\right\}}}a_{m}^{\max\left\{1/2,\eta-1/2\right\}}\right)=o_{P}\left(1\right),

on account of the fact that
d=O\left(m^{1/4}\right)

where the second term follows from the CLT, viz.

Equation (

), and the fact that we are assuming

, imply that

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\sigma Z+a_{m}^{-1/2}\sum_{t=m+k^{\ast}+1}^{m+a_{m}}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}>0\right)=1,

and

(B.8)

a_{m}^{-1/2}\sum_{t=m+k^{\ast}+1}^{m+a_{m}}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\overset{\mathcal{P}}{\rightarrow}\infty,

as
m\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, as
m\rightarrow\infty

P\left(r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\frac{\left|Q\left(m;a_{m}\right)\right|}{g_{\eta}\left(m;a_{m}\right)}\leq x\right)=P\left(\sigma Z+a_{m}^{-1/2}\sum_{t=m+k^{\ast}+1}^{m+a_{m}}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\leq x\right)+o_{P}\left(1\right).

Given that, by (
B.8
)

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\sigma Z+a_{m}^{-1/2}\sum_{t=m+k^{\ast}+1}^{m+a_{m}}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\leq c_{\alpha,\eta}\right)=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}P\left(Z\leq\frac{c_{\alpha,\eta}}{\sigma}-\frac{1}{\sigma}a_{m}^{-1/2}\sum_{t=m+k^{\ast}+1}^{m+a_{m}}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime}\Delta_{m}\right)=0,

putting all the above together, we finally have

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}P\left(r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\frac{\left|Q\left(m;a_{m}\right)\right|}{g_{\eta}\left(m;a_{m}\right)}\leq c_{\alpha,\eta}\right)=0.

We now conclude, by noting that the event
\left\{\tau_{m}>a_{m}\right\}

is equivalent to having

\left\{r_{m}^{\eta-1/2}\left|Q\left(m;a_{m}\right)\right|\leq c_{\alpha,\eta}g_{\eta}\left(m;a_{m}\right)\right\}.

We have shown that
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\tau_{m}>a_{m}\right)=0
, whence
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\tau_{m}=a_{m}\right)=1
.
