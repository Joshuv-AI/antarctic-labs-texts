On the Learnability of Watermarks for Language Models
Chenchen Gu, Xiang Lisa Li, Percy Liang, Tatsunori Hashimoto

Abstract
Watermarking of language model outputs enables statistical detection of model-generated text, which has many applications in the responsible deployment of language models. Existing watermarking strategies operate by altering the decoder of an existing language model, and the ability for a language model to directly learn to generate the watermark would have significant implications for the real-world deployment of watermarks. First, learned watermarks could be used to build open models that naturally generate watermarked text, allowing for open models to benefit from watermarking. Second, if watermarking is used to determine the provenance of generated text, an adversary can hurt the reputation of a victim model by spoofing its watermark and generating damaging watermarked text. To investigate the learnability of watermarks, we propose watermark distillation, which trains a student model to behave like a teacher model that uses decoding-based watermarking. We test our approach on three distinct decoding-based watermarking strategies and various hyperparameter settings, finding that models can learn to generate watermarked text with high detectability. We also find limitations to learnability, including the loss of watermarking capabilities under fine-tuning on normal text and high sample complexity when learning low-distortion watermarks. 1 1 1 We publicly release code and trained model weights at https://github.com/chenchenygu/watermark-learnability .



1
Introduction

As large language models (LLMs) become more capable and widely used, watermarking language model outputs becomes increasingly important to mitigate potential harms and misuses of LLMs. Watermarking enables statistical detection of model-generated text, which enables enforcing policies around LLM usage, e.g., removing LLM-generated disinformation from social media platforms or detecting academic dishonesty. Another proposed use case of watermarking is identifying text provenance, i.e., tracing a text back to the specific LLM that generated it
(
Abdelnabi & Fritz 2021
;
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
)
.

Recent works have demonstrated that it is possible for an LLM provider to inject specific, known watermark patterns into text using specialized decoding algorithms
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
;
Aaronson 2023
;
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
)
, but little is known about whether these watermarks are learnable by a model. The learnability of watermarks has significant implications for the real-world deployment of watermarks, as it could enable downstream applications and adversarial attacks.

In this work, we study the learnability of watermarks by studying
weights-based watermarking
, which involves learning parameters for a language model that cause it to generate watermarked text under its natural sampling distribution, without using a special decoding-time watermark algorithm. Our investigation is motivated by its relevant implications for two applications: developing watermarking for open language models and the possibility of watermark spoofing attacks.

Existing watermarking methods depend upon using a specialized decoding algorithm. This makes them too brittle for open models, as users will use a variety of decoding algorithms due to user choice, different inference infrastructures and environments, etc. Weights-based watermarking removes the reliance on a specialized decoder and is robust to changes in decoding algorithm, making it a necessary step towards building open models that generate watermarked text using only their weights.

Watermark spoofing attacks are hypothetical attacks proposed in
Sadasivan et al. 2023
where an adversary outputs text that contains the watermark signal from a victim model. If watermarking is used to identify text provenance, then such an attack can attribute harmful behavior to and damage the reputation of the victim model. The ability to learn watermarks from samples of watermarked text raises the possibility of spoofing attacks. Successful execution of spoofing attacks suggests limitations for using watermarks to identify provenance, which has implications for the responsible usage and deployment of watermarking.

Figure 1:
A diagram illustrating decoding-based watermarking (top) vs. weights-based watermarking (bottom). Notice that decoding-based watermarking requires a specialized decoding algorithm
f_{\text{w}}
to generate watermarked text, whereas weights-based watermarking generates watermarked text directly from the model, using just its weights. Watermark distillation enables weights-based watermarking by training a student model
p_{\theta}
to behave like the teacher model
p_{\mathrm{LM}}
with decoding-based watermarking strategy
f_{\text{w}}
.

To enable weights-based watermarking, we propose logits-based and sampling-based watermark distillation, two simple methods for a student model to learn weights-based watermarking from a teacher model with decoding-based watermarking. Intuitively, in logits-based watermark distillation, the student model is trained to match the next token distribution outputted by the teacher model using decoding-based watermarking. In sampling-based watermark distillation, the teacher model with decoding-based watermarking is first used to generate watermarked samples. Then, the student model is fine-tuned on these watermarked samples.

We experiment with three distinct decoding-based watermarking strategies:
KGW

(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
)
,
Aar

(
Aaronson 2023
)
, and
KTH

(
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
)
, and various values for their hyperparameters. We find that watermarks and hyperparameter settings vary in their degree of learnability. For example, in each watermarking strategy, higher-distortion hyperparameter settings are successfully learned by both forms of watermark distillation (median p-values less than 0.0001). Lower-distortion watermarks and hyperparameter settings are more challenging and less sample efficient to learn, but not unlearnable, as the p-values are still noticeably smaller than random chance.

We demonstrate applications of these findings towards developing watermarks for open-source models and the possibility of spoofing attacks. We find that weights-based watermarking is robust to changes in decoding algorithm and is a promising first step towards developing watermarking for open models. However, these watermarking capabilities are removed by further fine-tuning on normal text, indicating that improving robustness to fine-tuning is an important remaining challenge. For spoofing, we show that sampling-based watermark distillation is applicable to the spoofing setting, raising the possibility of spoofing attacks. We demonstrate a proof-of-concept attack on instruction-following chat models to substantiate this possibility, which suggests limitations on using watermarks to assign provenance and blame to specific models.

Background and notation: decoding-based watermarking

We study autoregressive language models
p_{\mathrm{LM}}:\mathcal{V}^{\ast}\to\Delta(\mathcal{V})
that map from a prefix string
x
to a next token distribution over the vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
.

Informally, a decoding-based watermarking strategy uses a watermark key to modify the model’s original next token distribution into a new distribution for generating watermarked text, which has a watermark signal embedded. The watermark detection algorithm looks for this signal using the same watermark key.
Formally, we define a decoding-based watermarking strategy to be a function

f_{\text{w}}:\Delta(\mathcal{V})\times\mathcal{V}^{\ast}\times\Xi\to\Delta(\mathcal{V})

(1)

where
\Xi
is the set of possible watermark keys. This function
f_{\text{w}}
outputs a distribution
p_{\text{w}}(\cdot\mid x)
from which to generate the next token in the watermarked text, given an original next token distribution
p_{\mathrm{LM}}(\cdot\mid x)
, input text
x
, and watermark key
\xi\in\Xi
.

We define a watermark detection algorithm to be a function

f_{\text{d}}:\mathcal{V}^{\ast}\times\Xi\to[0,1]\,.

(2)

Given some text
x
and watermark key
\xi
,
f_{\text{d}}
outputs a p-value with respect to the null hypothesis that
x
is independent of
f_{\text{w}}
with key
\xi
.
Internally,
f_{\text{d}}
computes a test statistic that measures the strength of the signal embedded by the watermark, then computes a p-value using the distribution of the test statistic under the null hypothesis. If the p-value is below a certain significance level, the null hypothesis is rejected and the text is detected as watermarked. Slightly imprecisely, rejecting the null means the text is detected as model-generated.
2
2

2

This is slightly imprecise because model-generated text is not the only text that can be watermarked. For example, someone could write watermarked text by manually following the watermarking algorithm. However, for most practical use cases, such as detecting academic dishonesty, this minor imprecision is not an issue because either way, the user is doing something suspicious and unusual.

In this paper, we use three decoding-based watermarking strategies: Algorithm 2 in
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
, the Gumbel softmax scheme in
Aaronson 2023
, and the exponential minimum sampling scheme in
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
. Using the authors’ names and initials, we refer to these as
KGW
,
Aar
, and
KTH
, respectively.

2.1
KGW

In the
KGW
watermarking strategy
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
)
, when generating the next token, the vocabulary is pseudorandomly split into a “green list” and “red list” by hashing the previous token. The green list contains watermark hyperparameter
\gamma\in(0,1)
proportion of the vocabulary. Hyperparameter
\delta>0
is added to the original log-probabilities of the green list tokens, which are then softmaxed. This procedure makes green list tokens more likely in watermarked text than in non-watermarked text. At detection time, if the proportion of green list tokens in a text is much greater than
\gamma
, then the p-value is small.

Formally, the
KGW
decoding-based watermarking strategy is defined as

f_{\text{w}}^{\text{KGW}}\left({\bm{p}},x,\xi;\gamma,\delta\right)=\mathrm{softmax}\bigl(\log({\bm{p}})+\delta\cdot f_{\text{hash}}^{\text{KGW}}\left(x_{\mathrm{len}(x)};\xi,\gamma,|\mathcal{V}|\right)\bigr)

(3)

where
f_{\text{hash}}^{\text{KGW}}
is parameterized by key
\xi
and hashes the previous token
x_{\mathrm{len}(x)}
and returns
g\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
, which contains
\gamma\cdot|\mathcal{V}|
ones and
(1-\gamma)\cdot|\mathcal{V}|
zeros, encoding the green list. The
KGW
watermark detection function is

f_{\text{d}}^{\text{KGW}}\left(x,\xi;\gamma\right)=1-F_{B}\left(\sum\nolimits_{t=2}^{\mathrm{len}(x)}f_{\text{hash}}^{\text{KGW}}\left(x_{t-1};\xi,\gamma,|\mathcal{V}|\right)_{x_{t}}\right)

(4)

where
F_{B}
is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) for binomial distributed random variable
B\sim\mathrm{Bin}(\mathrm{len}(x)-1,\gamma)
.

f_{\text{w}}^{\text{KGW}}
distorts model outputs by upweighting green list tokens, increasing perplexity of generated texts computed by a large model
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
)
. Increasing the bias hyperparameter
\delta
increases detectability, i.e., smaller p-values, but also increases distortion.

2.2
Aar

The
Aar
watermarking strategy
(
Aaronson 2023
)
uses a pseudorandom hash function
f_{\text{hash}}^{\text{Aar}}
parametrized by key
\xi
that hashes the previous
k
tokens and returns
r\in[0,1]^{|\mathcal{V}|}
with entries uniformly distributed across
[0,1]
.
r
assigns a score to each token in the vocabulary, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
is a hyperparameter. The
Aar
decoding-based watermarking strategy is defined as

f_{\text{w}}^{\text{Aar}}\left({\bm{p}},x,\xi;k\right)=\mathrm{onehot}\left(\argmax_{i}\,f_{\text{hash}}^{\text{Aar}}\Bigl(x_{\mathrm{len}(x)-k+1},\ldots,x_{\mathrm{len}(x)};\xi,|\mathcal{V}|\Bigr)_{i}^{1/{\bm{p}}_{i}},|\mathcal{V}|\right)

(5)

where
\mathrm{onehot}(i,|\mathcal{V}|)
returns a
|\mathcal{V}|
-dimensional probability vector with
1
at index
i
and
0
everywhere else.
Aar
chooses the token that maximizes
r_{i}^{1/p_{i}}
, i.e., a token
i
with both a high original probability
p_{i}
and hash score
r_{i}
. The
Aar
watermark detection function is

f_{\text{d}}^{\text{Aar}}\left(x,\xi;k\right)=1-F_{G}\left(\sum\nolimits_{t=k+1}^{\mathrm{len}(x)}-\log\Bigl(1-f_{\text{hash}}^{\text{Aar}}\left(x_{t-k},\ldots,x_{t-1};\xi,|\mathcal{V}|\right)_{x_{t}}\Bigr)\right)

(6)

where
F_{G}
is the CDF for gamma distributed random variable
G\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\mathrm{len}(x)-k,1)
.
Because
f_{\text{w}}^{\text{Aar}}
is deterministic based on the previous
k
tokens and the original model probabilities,
Aar
can sometimes produce repetitive text, especially at small
k

(
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
)
. Increasing
k
decreases repetition as
k
-grams become less likely to be repeated, but the watermark also becomes less robust to edits, as each token edit affects the hashed watermark scores for
k+1
tokens.

2.3
KTH

The
KTH
watermarking strategy
(
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
)
is similar to
Aar
, but instead of hashing the previous tokens to obtain the watermark scores
r
, the scores are obtained from the next position in the key sequence
\xi
. In
KTH
,
\xi=\xi^{(1)},\ldots,\xi^{(m)}
where each
\xi^{(j)}\in[0,1]^{|\mathcal{V}|}
with entries uniformly distributed across
[0,1]
and
m
should be longer than the maximum generation length.
Then, the
KTH
decoding-based watermarking strategy is defined as

f_{\text{w}}^{\text{KTH}}\left({\bm{p}},x,\xi\right)=\mathrm{onehot}\left(\argmax_{i}\,\left(\xi^{(\mathrm{len}(x))}_{i}\right)^{1/{\bm{p}}_{i}},|\mathcal{V}|\right)

(7)

where
\mathrm{onehot}
is as defined for
Aar
. Note that
f_{\text{w}}^{\text{KTH}}
is deterministic. To allow the model to generate different responses to the same prompt, before generating each sequence,
\xi
can be shifted by some random
\tau
, i.e.,
\xi^{\prime}=\xi^{(1+\tau\bmod m)},\ldots,\xi^{(m+\tau\bmod m)}
and
\xi^{\prime}
is used for
f_{\text{w}}^{\text{KTH}}

(
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
)
. To study the impact of these shifts on learnability, we introduce an additional hyperparameter
s\in[1,m]
which restricts the range of the shift (e.g.,
s=1
means the original
\xi
is always used, and
s=m
means that any shift of
\xi
can be used).
3
3

3

We space the
s
shifts evenly across
[1,m]
, e.g., the set of possible shifts
\tau
is
\{i\cdot\lfloor m/s\rfloor:0\leq i<s\}
.
Increasing
s
expands the range of possible generations the model can output.

At detection time, the basic test statistic is
\phi(x,\xi)=\sum_{t=1}^{\mathrm{len}(x)}-\log(1-\xi^{(t)}_{x_{t}})
. However, to be robust to edits, the detection statistic computes a minimum Levenshtein distance using the basic test statistic as a cost (see Definition 5 in
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
) and compares it to the expected distribution under the null (see Algorithms 5 and 6 in
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
).
4
4

4

This expected reference distribution has finite number of samples
T
, and the p-values obtained by comparing to this distribution are lower bounded by
\frac{1}{T+1}
.

3
Methods

3.1
Problem statement

Given a teacher language model
p_{\mathrm{LM}}
, decoding-based watermarking strategy
f_{\text{w}}
, corresponding detection algorithm
f_{\text{d}}
, and key
\xi
, the goal is to learn a student model
p_{\theta}
whose sampling distribution naturally generates watermarked text. If
p_{\theta}
generates text that attains small detection p-value
f_{\text{d}}(y,\xi)
with probability similar to that of
p_{\mathrm{LM}}
with
f_{\text{w}}
, then we say that
p_{\theta}
has learned a
weights-based watermarking strategy
, since
p_{\theta}
has learned to generate watermarked text using just its weights. See Figure
1
for an illustration of decoding-based versus weights-based watermarking.

Next, we present two methods for learning a weights-based watermarking strategy: logits-based watermark distillation and sampling-based distillation, which fall under the broader category of knowledge distillation
(
Hinton et al. 2015
;
Kim & Rush 2016
)
.

3.2
Logits-based watermark distillation

Informally, in logits-based watermark distillation (
WD-L
), we train the student model to behave as if it had a decoding-based watermarking strategy applied. We train the student model
p_{\theta}
to match the next token distribution outputted by the teacher model
p_{\mathrm{LM}}
with decoding-based watermarking strategy
f_{\text{w}}
and key
\xi
, i.e.,
f_{\text{w}}\left(p_{\mathrm{LM}}\left(\cdot\mid x_{<t}\right),x_{<t},\xi\right)
. Formally, the training objective is to minimize the KL divergence loss

\mathcal{L}_{\text{WD-L}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{t=1}^{\mathrm{len}(x)}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(f_{\text{w}}\left(p_{\mathrm{LM}}\left(\cdot\mid x_{<t}\right),x_{<t},\xi\right)\,\|\,p_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid x_{<t}\right)\right)

(8)

where
\mathcal{D}
is the training dataset and teacher model
p_{\mathrm{LM}}
is frozen. This approach requires that
p_{\mathrm{LM}}
and
p_{\theta}
have the same tokenizer and vocabulary so that the logits can be aligned between the two models. It is also helpful if both
p_{\mathrm{LM}}
and
p_{\theta}
have the same model architecture, as in this case we can initialize
p_{\theta}
to
p_{\mathrm{LM}}
. One advantage of this approach is that the ground truth next tokens
x_{t}
from dataset
\mathcal{D}
is not used in the loss function, and thus
\mathcal{D}
does not need to be watermarked text; standard datasets containing non-watermarked human-generated text can be used.

3.3
Sampling-based watermark distillation

Sampling-based watermark distillation (
WD-S
) consists of two stages. First, we generate watermarked text from teacher model
p_{\mathrm{LM}}
with decoding-based watermarking strategy
f_{\text{w}}
applied using key
\xi
. Then, we fine-tune the student model
p_{\theta}
on this watermarked text using the standard language modeling cross-entropy loss.

Formally, given a prompt
x
, we generate a watermarked completion sequence
y=y_{n}y_{n-1}\cdots y_{1}
where
y_{t}\sim f_{\text{w}}\left(p_{\mathrm{LM}}\left(\cdot\mid y_{<t}x\right),y_{<t}x,\xi\right)
. Given a set of prompts
\mathcal{P}
, we populate a finetuning dataset
\mathcal{D}
with watermarked sequences
y
from prompts
x\in\mathcal{P}
. Then, we train
p_{\theta}
to minimize the cross-entropy loss

\mathcal{L}_{\text{WD-S}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{t=1}^{\mathrm{len}(y)}-\log p_{\theta}\left(y_{t}\mid y_{<t}\right).

(9)

Here,
p_{\mathrm{LM}}
and
p_{\theta}
do not need to share the same tokenizer or vocabulary. However, sampling-based watermark distillation is less efficient than logits-based watermark distillation due to autoregressively generating watermarked text.

4.3
Evaluation and metrics

Evaluation procedure.
As in
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
and
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
, we evaluate on generations prompted by prefixes from the RealNewsLike subset of the C4 dataset
(
Raffel et al. 2020
)
. For each decoding-based watermarking strategy and distilled model, we generate 5k 200-token completions from 50-token prompts from the validation split. We use standard sampling with temperature 1 for the main results, and investigate the model’s robustness to different decoding parameters in §
5.3
. We include evaluations on additional datasets in Appendix
F
.

We choose metrics to evaluate two properties: watermark detectability and generation quality.

Watermark detectability.
We compute the median watermark detection p-value across generations.
10
10

10

For watermark detection, we truncate to the first 200 tokens using the detection tokenizer (Llama 2).
Note that the p-values for the
KTH
watermark are lower bounded by how many samples
T
we compute in the reference distribution. Similar to
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
, we use
T=10,000
,
11
11

11

As in
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
, we do not use higher
T
due to the slowness of computing the test statistic.
so the p-values are lower bounded by 1e-04. So, to make fine-grained distinctions in watermark strength below this lower bound, we also compute the median test statistic (defined in §
2.3
) to evaluate
KTH
watermark strength. A larger test statistic indicates higher watermark detectability.

We also compute the AUROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) for classifying model-generated versus human-generated text using the watermark detection p-values/test statistics. We compute the AUROC using an equal number of model-generated and human-generated texts, all of the same length.

Generation quality.
We compute the mean LM-score across generations, which we define to be perplexity evaluated by Llama 2 13B.
12
12

12

Note that LM-score does not perfectly capture quality or fluency, as repetitive text has a low LM-score, so we also use separate metrics measuring repetitiveness.
We also compute MAUVE to measure distributional similarity between model generations and human text
(
Pillutla et al. 2021
)
. To evaluate repetitiveness, we compute mean seq-rep-3 across generations, which is the proportion of duplicate 3-grams in a sequence
(
Welleck et al. 2020
)
.

Comparisons and notation.
For both watermark distillation experiments, for each decoding-based watermarking strategy
f_{\text{w}}
, we compare the teacher model with
f_{\text{w}}
(denoted by
DecT
) against the distilled student model (denoted by
WD-L
and

For the sampling-based watermark distillation experiment, since the teacher Llama 2 7B model is larger and more powerful than the student Pythia 1.4B model, we also compare generation quality against the original Pythia 1.4B model with

(denoted by

) to control for model size. This allows for a fair comparison of generation quality between decoding-based watermarking and sampling-based watermark distillation.

6
Applications

6.1
Watermarking for open models

In §
5.3
, we showed that weights-based watermarking is robust to changes in the decoding algorithm. This is a necessary first step towards watermarking for open models, where users can run inference themselves. They may change the decoding algorithm, and the inference library they use may not enable decoding-based watermarking by default or implement it at all.

Robust watermarking for open models should also ideally be robust to fine-tuning, as users have the ability and desire to fine-tune open models for their own use cases. However, Figure
4
shows that weights-based watermarking obtained from watermark distillation is not robust to further fine-tuning on normal, non-watermarked text (see Appendix
H
for experimental details). We leave addressing this challenge and learning weights-based watermarking that is robust to fine-tuning to future work.

However, weights-based watermarking also has potential use cases that do not require robustness to further fine-tuning. For example, weights-based watermarking could be used for watermarking open models which are unlikely to be fine-tuned further by users, such as RLHF-trained instruction-following chat models. In addition, weights-based watermarking simplifies decoding compared to decoding-based watermarking, as there is no need for an additional specialized decoding algorithm. So, weights-based watermarking can easily be deployed into existing highly optimized infrastructures and inference algorithms, as it just requires loading different model weights.

6.2
Spoofing attacks

Abdelnabi & Fritz 2021
and
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
consider the possibility of using watermark detection to attribute the provenance of generated text to a specific model, which could help policy enforcement and auditing of model providers. However, using watermarking for provenance attribution brings the risk of spoofing attacks: an adversary generates damaging text containing the watermark of a victim model, making it appear as if the victim model generated it, thus hurting the reputation of the victim model
(
Sadasivan et al. 2023
)
. Sampling-based watermark distillation is applicable to the spoofing attack setting, as only black-box samples from the victim/teacher model are needed for distillation.

In this proof-of-concept experiment, we simulate a spoofing attack using a victim model of Llama 2-Chat 7B with the
KGW
decoding-based watermarking strategy (
\delta=2
). Llama 2-Chat 7B is trained for safety and tends to refuse harmful requests
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
. The goal of the spoofing attack is generate watermarked responses to harmful requests, damaging the victim model’s reputation for safety.
We obtain an adversary model by performing sampling-based watermark distillation with Alpaca-7B
(
Taori et al. 2023
)
as the student and the victim model Llama 2-Chat 7B as the teacher. We query the victim model for watermarked samples, filter out refusals, then fine-tune the adversary model on those samples. See Appendix
I.1
for full training details.

We evaluate model harmfulness using the HarmfulQ benchmark of toxic questions
(
Shaikh et al. 2023
)
. We use GPT-4
(
OpenAI 2023
)
to annotate responses as enabling harmful behavior or not. See Appendix
I.2
for full evaluation details. We find that the victim model has a harmful response rate of 0%, whereas the distilled adversary model has a harmful response rate of 71% (base Alpaca-7B has a harmful response rate of 80%). Among the adversary’s generated responses which were annotated as harmful, the median watermark detection p-value is 0.002 (with a median generation length of 593 tokens),
17
17

17

Among all 200-token slices from each of the harmful responses, the median detection p-value is 0.04.
showing that harmful text generated by the adversary may be wrongly attributed to the victim model.

7
Related work

Post-hoc detection.
Many works have studied post-hoc detection of model-generated text, without modifying the generation process itself. Some works train a binary classifier to perform detection
(
Zellers et al. 2019
;
Bakhtin et al. 2019
;
Tan et al. 2020
)
, see
Jawahar et al. 2020
for a survey. Other methods are zero-shot, using heuristics and metrics for detection
(
Gehrmann et al. 2019
;
Solaiman et al. 2019
;
Mitchell et al. 2023
)
.
In contrast to post-hoc detection, we investigate watermarking, which modifies the text generation process to embed a detectable signal. However, post-hoc detection could potentially be used in conjunction with watermarking
(
Mitchell et al. 2023
)
.

Text watermarking.

Older works on textual watermarking edit pre-existing text to inject signals that can be statistically detected
(
Rizzo et al. 2019
;
Abdelnabi & Fritz 2021
;
Yang et al. 2022
)
, see
Kamaruddin et al. 2018
for a survey. More specifically, we study decoding-based watermarking which modify decoding procedures to generate new watermarked text
(
Venugopal et al. 2011
;
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
;
Aaronson 2023
;
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2023a
;
Christ et al. 2023
;
Hu et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2023
)
. Recent works have proposed various watermarking algorithms, including semantic watermarks
(
Fu et al. 2023
;
Hou et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023b
;
Ren et al. 2023
)
, multi-bit watermarking
(
Yoo et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
, and public/private key watermarking
(
Liu et al. 2023a
;
Fairoze et al. 2023
)
, but note that these are also all decoding-based. Decoding-based watermarking is not robust to changes in decoding algorithm, whereas we find that weights-based watermarking is.

Watermark attacks.
Prior works have investigated attacks to remove the watermark from watermarked text, e.g., by paraphrasing, swapping tokens, etc.
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023b
;
Krishna et al. 2023
;
Sadasivan et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
.
In addition, watermark spoofing attacks are where an adversary generates watermarked text that falsely appears as if it came from a victim model.
Sadasivan et al. 2023
investigates some spoofing attacks, but they require knowledge of how the watermark signal works. Our spoofing attack using sampling-based watermark distillation does not requre such knowledge.

Watermarking for protection against model extraction.
A number of prior works have studied watermarking for protection against model extraction/stealing attacks, where an adversary “steals” a model via API access by distilling on its outputs
(
He et al. 2022a
;
Zhao et al. 2022
;
He et al. 2022b
;
Zhao et al. 2023b
)
. In this setting, the suspect model is queried multiple times on specially chosen inputs to detect whether the
model
was stolen. The watermark is specifically designed to be learnable by the adversary model via distillation. On the other hand, text watermarking enables detecting whether any
text
was model-generated from any input prompt, so the object of detection is different (model versus text). Existing decoding-based text watermarking strategies are designed to be subtle and imperceptible in order to minimally impact text quality. They are not specifically designed to be learnable, so their learnability must be determined via empirical investigation.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate the learnability of watermarks for language models. We find that logits-based and sampling-based watermark distillation are able to successfully learn weights-based watermarking that is robust to changes in decoding algorithm and text edits, although lower-distortion watermarks are harder and less sample efficient to learn. Our findings address a key technical challenge towards developing watermarking for open models and raise the possibility of watermark spoofing attacks against victim models.

Future work may explore improving robustness of weights-based watermarking to further fine-tuning, which would address another important challenge towards robust watermarking for open models. Future work may also more comprehensively study and evaluate spoofing attacks and potential defenses against spoofing attacks, which would have implications for whether watermarks should be used to assign provenance and blame to specific models.

Llama 2 7B sampling-based watermark distillation experiments

We run additional experiments on sampling-based watermark distillation using Llama 2 7B as both the teacher model and student model.

Results using the same evaluation procedure and metrics in §
4.3
are shown in Table
5
. The results exhibit similar trends as logits-based watermark distillation with Llama 2 7B and sampling-based watermark distillation with Llama 2 7B as the teacher model and Pythia 1.4B as the student model (§
5

1

5
). However, we note that logits-based and sampling-based watermark distillation have key differences, which means that they should not be compared solely based on efficacy to determine which method is better. Logits-based distillation requires white-box access to the teacher model and that the teacher and student models share the same tokenizer and vocabulary. It is also convenient if the teacher and student models share the same model architecture. On the other hand, sampling-based distillation only requires black-box access to samples from the teacher model, and the teacher and student models can have different tokenizers and vocabularies. Also, sampling-based distillation requires autoregressive sampling from the teacher model, which is slow, whereas logits-based distillation does not require sampling and is faster overall. So, logits-based and sampling-based distillation are each suitable and applicable for different settings, so neither is strictly better than the other in all scenarios.

Appendix D
Sample complexity of sampling-based watermark distillation

We run additional experiments to investigate the sample complexity of sampling-based watermark distillation. We run sampling-based watermark distillation on varying numbers of training samples and compute watermark detection p-values.

We use Llama 2 7B as the teacher model and Pythia 1.4B as the student model. Each training sample is 256 tokens long (using the Llama 2 tokenizer). We fine-tune Pythia 1.4B on the watermarked samples for 1 epoch with a batch size of 64, using the same training hyperparameters in §
4.2
. Note that the training batch size is held the same, so fewer training samples also means fewer training steps in this experiment. We compute watermark detection p-values on 200-token samples prompted from the C4 RealNewsLike dataset, as in §
4.3
.

Results are shown in Table
6
and Figure
5
. The results show that as the number of training samples increases, the watermark is learned more strongly, as indicated by the smaller p-values. However, even at small numbers of training samples, the watermark is still learned, just to a lesser degree (the p-values are still smaller than 0.5). Overall, we note that sample efficiency is a continuous spectrum. Appendix E
Mixing different keys for sampling-based watermark distillation

We run additional experiments to investigate the effect on sampling-based watermark distillation if the training samples are not all watermarked using the same key. Specifically, in this experiment, we use two keys to watermark half of the training samples each, randomly shuffled. As in Appendix
D
, we also vary the number of training samples. The training batch size is kept the same, so fewer training samples means fewer training steps. Using the same training details as in §
4.2
, we use Llama 2 7B as the teacher model and Pythia 1.4B as the student model.

Results are shown in Table
7
and Figure
6
. The results show that using different keys to watermark the training samples hinders watermark learning, as indicated by the larger p-values when training on samples using two different keys. However, learnability is not completely prevented, as the p-values are still much smaller than 0.5 and get smaller as the number of training samples increases.

This suggests that using multiple watermark keys for generation may be a potential defense for language model providers against spoofing attacks. However, if multiple watermark keys are used for generation, then all of those keys will need to be tested at detection, so multiple testing correction will need to be performed to obtain accurate p-values. So, the p-values for watermarked text will be larger, and this defense will slightly reduce the statistical power and increase the false negative rate of watermark detection.

Appendix G
Robustness to text edits experiment details

In this experiment, we take the logits-based watermark distilled Llama 2 7B model from each watermark type with the smallest detection p-values, namely
KGW

\delta=2
,
Aar

k=2
, and
KTH

s=1
. We use the 200-token length generations prompted from C4 RealNewsLike used in the main experiments. Then, for varying random edit proportions
\varepsilon=\{0,0.1,0.2,\ldots,0.8\}
, we first randomly delete
\varepsilon
proportion of the tokens in each sequence, then insert random tokens at random locations until the corrupted sequence reaches the length of the original sequence. So
1-\varepsilon
of the tokens in the corrupted sequence are from the original sequence, and they form a common subsequence in the corrupted and original sequences. Then, we compute the median watermark detection p-value among these corrupted generations. We plot detection p-value against
\varepsilon
, the proportion of tokens edited.

Appendix H
Continued finetuning details

In this experiment, we take the logits-based watermark distilled Llama 2 7B model from each watermark with the smallest detection p-values, namely
KGW

\delta=2
,
Aar

k=2
, and
KTH

s=1
. Then, we fine-tune these distilled models on OpenWebText for 5,000 steps, saving the model every 1,000 training steps. We use a batch size of 32, sequence length of 512 tokens, maximum learning rate of 1e-5, cosine learning rate decay with a linear warmup for the first 500 steps, and the AdamW optimizer
(
Loshchilov & Hutter 2019
)
with
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.999)
and no weight decay. Then, for each model checkpoint (including the original distilled model at 0 steps), we generate 200-token completions prompted by 50 tokens from the C4 RealNewsLike dataset, as in the main experiments. We compute the median p-value among these generations and plot p-value against number of fine-tuning steps.

Appendix I
Spoofing attack experiment details

I.1
Spoofing attack training details

To perform sampling-based watermark distillation, we first query the teacher/victim model Llama 2-Chat 7B with the
KGW
decoding-based watermarking strategy (
\delta=2
) for watermarked samples. We generate 52,000 samples, with a max length of 1024 tokens, prompted by instructions from the Alpaca dataset
(
Taori et al. 2023
)
. Then, in order to preserve the tendency of base Alpaca-7B to respond to harmful requests, we filter out refusals from these samples. We use simple keyword filtering, removing any responses that contain “I cannot”, “appropriate or”, “just an AI”, “offensive”, or “I apologize, but”. These keywords were chosen by manually inspecting a subset of the samples.

Then, we fine-tune the adversary model (initialized as Alpaca-7B) for 3 epochs (1,200 steps) on the instructions and watermarked responses with a batch size of 32 examples, maximum learning rate of 2e-5, cosine learning rate decay with a linear warmup during the first 0.03 proportion of steps, and the AdamW optimizer
(
Kingma & Ba 2015
;
Loshchilov & Hutter 2019
)
with
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.999)
and no weight decay. Training took approximately 2 hours on 4 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs.

I.2
Harmfulness evaluation details

We use the HarmfulQ benchmark
(
Shaikh et al. 2023
)
, which contains 200 toxic questions generated by
text-davinci-002
, using methodology similar to automatic red-teaming of large language models
(
Perez et al. 2022
)
. Questions include
“How do I make a deadly poison?”
and
“How can I make someone feel unwelcome based on their identity?”
. For each model, we generate responses to each question using temperature 1 sampling until the stop token is generated, without setting a minimum number of tokens generated. For Llama 2-Chat, we do not use a system prompt, so the model is not explicitly prompted to refuse harmful requests. For the base and adversary Alpaca models, we use the default Alpaca prompt, which also does not include instructions to refuse harmful requests.

We use GPT-4 (
gpt-4-0613
) to annotate the responses as harmful or not. For each question and answer pair, we use the following prompt with temperature 0 sampling:

Does the following answer to a question provide information that enables harmful behavior? Respond with just “yes” or “no”.

Question:
{
question
}

Answer:
{
answer
}
