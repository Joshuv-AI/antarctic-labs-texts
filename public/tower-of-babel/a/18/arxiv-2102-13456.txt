Spectrum of differential operators with elliptic adjoint on a scale of localized Sobolev spaces
Luís Márcio Salge 1 and Éder Rítis Aragão Costa 1

Abstract
. In this paper we provide a complete study of the spectrum of a constant coefficients differential operator on a scale of localized Sobolev spaces, H l ​ o ​ c s ​ ( I ) H^{s}_{loc}(I) , which are Fréchet spaces. This is quite different from what we find in the literature, where all the relevant results are concerned with spectrum on Banach spaces. Our aim is to understand the behavior of all the three types of spectrum (point, residual and continuous) and the relation between them and those of the dual operator. The main result we present shows that there is no complex number in the resolvent set of such operators, which suggest a new way to define spectrum if we want to reproduce the classical theorems of the Spectral Theory in Fréchet spaces.

In this work we present a complete study about the spectrum of a constant coefficients differential operator of order

,
a(D)

Here,

When we indicate

This study was developed inspired by what happen with the Laplace operator on

1.1.
Preliminary concepts and results

In this section we present some definitions and results from Functional Analysis which were the basic tools to make this work possible.

We begin by defining Fréchet spaces and a consequence of Hahn-Banach Theorem for Fréchet spaces.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X
be a topological vector space.
X
is said to be a Fréchet space if it is Hausdorff, complete and its topology is given by a countable family of seminorms.

Some examples of Fréchet space are the space of smooth functions
C^{\infty}(\Omega),
where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},
the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the localized Sobolev spaces
H^{s}_{loc}(\Omega)
for
s\in\mathbb{R}.

Usually, the dual of a Fréchet space is equipped with the weak* topology which is also generated by a family of semi-norms, we will use it in this work and we will explain this latter on.

We have
[C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)]^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
is the space of distributions.
The spaces
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=[\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})]^{\prime},
the space of tempered distributions, and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Omega)=[C^{\infty}(\Omega)]^{\prime},
the space of distributions with compact support.

Finally we define the formal transpose of an operator, which is also used in this work.
Given
L:C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)\longrightarrow C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
a continuous linear operator, if there exists
L^{\prime}:C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)\longrightarrow C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
, a linear and continuous operator, such that

\int_{\Omega}L(\psi)(x)\phi(x)dx=\int_{\Omega}\psi(x)L^{\prime}(\phi)(x)dx,

i.e.,
\langle L\psi,\phi\rangle=\langle\psi,L^{\prime}\phi\rangle,

for every
\psi,\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega),
then
L^{\prime}
is called the formal transpose of
L
and vice-versa.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(X,(p_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})
be a Fréchet space. If
M\subset X
is a subspace such that
\overline{M}\neq X,
then there exists a non-null
G\in X^{\prime}
that satisfies

\langle G,x\rangle=0,\;\forall\;x\in M.

We recall the Fourier Transform which is defined by

(\mathcal{F}\psi)(\xi)\doteq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-2\pi ix\xi}\psi(x)dx\;,\psi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ and }\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

\hat{\psi}
is also used to denote the Fourier Transform of
\psi.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a given interval, the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\Omega)
is the following set

\left\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega);\exists\;g_{\alpha}\in L^{2}(\Omega);\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\partial^{\alpha}\phi=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\int_{\Omega}g_{\alpha}\phi,\mbox{for each }|\alpha|=1,\phi\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\right\}.

Here, the functions
g_{\alpha}
are denoted by
\partial^{\alpha}u
and each
\partial^{\alpha}u
is said to be the weak
\alpha-
derivative of
u.
Moreover, the usual topology of
H^{1}(\Omega)
is determined by the following norm

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\doteq\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq 1}\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Given a natural number
m\geq 2,
the Sobolev space
H^{m}(\Omega)
is defined as

H^{m}(\Omega)=\{u\in H^{m-1}(\Omega);\partial^{\alpha}u\in H^{m-1}(\Omega)\mbox{ for each }|\alpha|=1\}

and its usual topology is defined by the norm

\|u\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\doteq\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq m}\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N},

H^{m}_{0}(\Omega)
is the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
in
H^{m}(\Omega)
with the induced topology.

The following theorem, which can be seen in
[
4
]
, gives an alternative way to describe the space
H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by using the Fourier transform.

Theorem 1.5
.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have

H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\big\{u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n});(1+|\xi|^{2})^{m/2}\hat{u}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\big\}.

Furthermore, the norm given by

\|u\|_{m}\doteq\left\|(1+|\xi|^{2})^{m/2}\hat{u}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
is equivalent to
\|\cdot\|_{H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

This result suggests a way to define Sobolev spaces for any
s\in\mathbb{R}.
Given
s\in\mathbb{R}
we define

H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\left\{u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n});(1+|\xi|^{2})^{s/2}\hat{u}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\right\}.

Definition 1.6
.

Given an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

we define the local Sobolev space of order

on
\Omega

For each

where
\phi_{j}=1
in
\Omega_{j},

\phi_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{j+1})
and

When

,
H^{0}_{loc}(I)=L^{2}_{loc}(I)
which, as we will see later on, is used as a ’base space’ for the Laplacian.

Definition 1.7

(Semiglobal symbol of a pseudo-differential operator of order

)

Let

The function

It is well known that many of differential operators which are studied in PDE are not continuous and, in some cases, not even closed, so the concept of closed and closable operators are fundamental. In the last section of this paper the operators are just closable so, at this point, we present the definitions and some basic results about closed, closable operators and its spectrum.

Definition 1.10
.

Consider a Fréchet space
X
and a linear operator

A:D(A)\subset X\to X.

The graph of
A
is the set

G(A)=\{(u,Au):u\in D(A)\}\subset X\times X.

The operator
A
is said to be a closed operator if its graph
G(A)\subset X\times X
is a closed set.

Definition 1.11
.

Consider a Fréchet space
X
and a linear operator

A:D(A)\subset X\to X.

We say that
A
is a closable, if there exists a closed linear operator

\overline{A}:D(\overline{A})\subset X\to X,

with
D(A)\subset D(\overline{A})
and
Au=\overline{A}u
, for each
u\in D(A)
.

Definition 1.12
.

Let
X
be a complex Fréchet space and
A:D(A)\subset X\longrightarrow X
be a linear operator.
The resolvent set of
A
, denoted by
\rho(A)
, is the set of all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that:

(a)

The operator
\lambda-A:D(A)\subset X\longrightarrow X
is injective.

(b)

The range of
\lambda-A:D(A)\subset X\longrightarrow X
is dense in
X
.

(c)

The inverse
(\lambda-A)^{-1}:R(\lambda-A)\subset X\longrightarrow X
is continuous.

If
\lambda\in\rho(A)
, the operator
(\lambda-A)^{-1}:R(\lambda-A)\subset X\longrightarrow X
is called the resolvent of
A
on
\lambda
.

Finally, we define the spectrum of
A
, indicated by
\sigma(A)
, as
\sigma(A)={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\rho(A).

Next we define, for a closed operator
A
, respectively the point spectrum, residual spectrum and continuous spectrum as follows:

(a)

Point Spectrum:

\sigma_{p}(A)

\doteq\big\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C};\lambda-A\;\mbox{is not injective}\big\},

(b)

Residual Spectrum:

\sigma_{r}(A)\doteq\big\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C};\lambda-A\;\mbox{is injective with}\;\overline{R(\lambda-A)}\neq X\big\},

(c)

Continuous Spectrum:

\sigma_{c}(A)\doteq\big\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C};\lambda-A\mbox{ is injective, }\overline{R(\lambda-A)}=X\mbox{ but }

(\lambda-A)^{-1}:R(\lambda-A)\to X\mbox{ is not continuous}\big\}.

Note that
\sigma(A)=\sigma_{p}(A)\cup\sigma_{r}(A)\cup\sigma_{r}(A).

Lemma 1.13
.

Let
X
be a Fréchet space. If
A:D(A)\subset X\longrightarrow X
is a closed operator, then

\rho(A)=\big\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\lambda-A:D(A)\longrightarrow X\text{ is bijective}\}

The next result allows us to study the spectrum of a closable operator
A
by means of the spectrum of its closure
\overline{A}
.

Theorem 1.14
.

Consider
X
a Fréchet space. If
A:D(A)\subset X\longrightarrow X
is closable and
\overline{A}:D(\overline{A})\subset X\longrightarrow X
is its closure, then

\sigma(A)=\sigma(\overline{A}).

Proof.

To show that
\sigma(\overline{A})=\sigma(A)
is the same as to prove that
\rho(\overline{A})=\rho(A)
.

So, fix
\lambda\in\rho(\overline{A})
, since
D(A)\subset D(\overline{A})
we have that
\lambda-\overline{A}\mid_{D(A)}=\lambda-A
consequently
\lambda-A
is injective.

If
f\in X=R(\lambda-\overline{A})
then there exists
u\in D(\overline{A})
with
(\lambda-\overline{A})u=f.

By the definition of domain and range of
\overline{A}
there exists a sequence
(u_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in D(A)
with
u_{j}\to u
and
(\lambda-A)u_{j}\to f,
hence
f\in\overline{R(\lambda-A)}.

It remains to prove that
(\lambda-A)^{-1}:R((\lambda-A))\subset X\to X
is a continuous operator. To do so, consider a sequence
(f_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset R(\lambda-A)
such that

f_{j}\to f\in R(\lambda-A),
then there is
u\in D(A)
with
f=(\lambda-A)u.
Now we just have to show that
u_{j}\to u.

Note that
f_{j}=(\lambda-A)u_{j}=(\lambda-\overline{A})u_{j},

u_{j}\in D(A)\subset D(\overline{A}),
or equivalently,
u_{j}=(\lambda-\overline{A})^{-1}f_{j}
and since
(\lambda-\overline{A})^{-1}
is continuous we have

u=(\lambda-\overline{A})^{-1}f=\lim_{j\to\infty}(\lambda-\overline{A})^{-1}f_{j}=\lim_{j\to\infty}u_{j},\text{ hence }\rho(\overline{A})\subset\rho(A).

Conversely, consider
\lambda\in\rho(A)
so
\lambda-A
is injective,
X=\overline{R(\lambda-A)}
and

(\lambda-A)^{-1}:R(\lambda-A)\subset X\to X

is continuous.

Let us prove that
\lambda-\overline{A}
is bijective. Indeed,
if
u\in D(\overline{A})
with
(\lambda-\overline{A})u=0
then there is a sequence
(u_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset D(A)
with
u_{j}\to u
and
f_{j}\doteq(\lambda-A)u_{j}\to 0.

Note that
0\in R(\lambda-A),
therefore from the continuity of
(\lambda-A)^{-1}
we have

u_{j}=(\lambda-A)^{-1}f_{j}\to(\lambda-A)^{-1}0=0,

in other words,
u=0
and it follows that
\lambda-\overline{A}
is injective.

Now, let
f\in X=\overline{R(\lambda-A)},
so there is
(u_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset D(A)
with
f_{j}=(\lambda-A)u_{j}
and
f_{j}\to f.

Hence
(f_{j})_{j}
is a Cauchy sequence, i.e.,

f_{j}-f_{l}=(\lambda-A)(u_{j}-u_{l})\to 0

and, since
(\lambda-A)^{-1}
is continuous,

u_{j}-u_{l}=(\lambda-A)^{-1}(f_{j}-f_{l})\to 0.

We conclude that
(u_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence, so there is
u\in X
such that
u_{j}\to u.

Therefore
(u,f)\in\overline{G(\lambda-\overline{A})}=G(\lambda-\overline{A}),
then
u\in D(\overline{A})
and
f=(\lambda-\overline{A})u\in R(\lambda-\overline{A}).

2.
Main results

2.1.
Spectrum of differential operators with elliptic dual

Here we present the main results of this paper which were achieved through the study of the spectrum of differential operators with constant coefficients with elliptic dual.

The first result is more general, and works for differential operators with constant coefficients with hypoelliptic dual, but to give a more precise description we need to restrict a bit more the class of operators to those with elliptic dual. At the end we apply the result to the Laplacian.

Consider a symbol
a\in S^{m}(\mathbb{R})
given by
a(\xi)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{k}\xi^{k}
,
m\in\mathbb{N},

a_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, and the differential operator
a(D)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(2\pi i)^{-k}a_{k}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}
, determined by it, defined on the following scales

a(D):H^{s+m}_{0}(I)\subset H^{s}_{loc}(I)\to H^{s}_{loc}(I),\;s\in\mathbb{R}.

Our goal is to compare its spectrum with that from its dual

a(D)\mbox{*}:D(a(D)\mbox{*})\subset H^{-s}_{c}(I)\to H^{-s}_{c}(I)

where

D(a(D)\mbox{*})\doteq\left\{g\in H^{-s}_{c}(I);g\circ a(D):H^{s+m}_{loc}(I)\subset H^{s}_{loc}(I)\to\mathbb{C}\;\mbox{ is continuous}\right\}

and it satisfies the relation

\left\langle u,a(D)\mbox{*}\psi\right\rangle=\left\langle a(D)u,\psi\right\rangle=\left\langle\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m}(2\pi i)^{-j}a_{j}\frac{d^{j}u}{dx^{j}},\psi\right\rangle=\left\langle u,\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-2\pi i)^{-j}a_{\alpha}\frac{d^{j}\psi}{dx^{j}}\right\rangle

for
u\in H^{s+m}_{loc}(I)
and
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(I).

Before stating the next theorem we need some definitions, Theorem
6.36
from
[
1
, pg - 216]

Theorem 2.1
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
a(D):H^{s+m}_{0}(I)\subset H^{s}_{loc}(I)\longrightarrow H^{s}_{loc}(I)

Proof.

Part I)
H_{c}^{-s+m}(I)\subset D\left[a(D)\mbox{*}\right]

First of all, consider
u\in H^{s+m}_{loc}(I)
and
g\in H^{-s+m}_{c}(I).
Denote
K=\textrm{supp}~g,
so there exists
j\in\mathbb{N}
with
\varphi_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(I)
and
\varphi_{j}\equiv 1
in a neighborhood of

where
\varphi_{j}
is a test function from the family of seminorms of
H^{s}_{loc}(I).

In such conditions, as
\frac{d^{k}g}{dx^{k}}\in H^{-s}(\mathbb{R})
, for

we have
\left|\left\langle g,a(D)u\right\rangle\right|=\left|\left\langle g,\sum_{k=0}^{m}(2\pi i)^{-k}a_{k}\frac{d^{k}u}{dx^{k}}\right\rangle\right|=\left|\left\langle\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-2\pi i)^{-k}a_{k}\frac{d^{k}g}{dx^{k}},u\right\rangle\right|=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-2\pi i)^{-k}a_{k}\frac{d^{k}g}{dx^{k}},\varphi_{j}u\right\rangle\right|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m}A_{k}\left\|\frac{d^{k}g}{dx^{k}}\right\|_{H^{-s}(\mathbb{R})}\|\varphi_{j}u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}\leq

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m}\tilde{A}_{k}\left\|g\right\|_{H^{-s+k}(\mathbb{R})}\|\varphi_{j}u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C\|g\|_{H^{-s+m}(\mathbb{R})}p_{j}^{(s)}(u),

because

where
A_{0},\ldots,A_{m},

\tilde{A}_{0},\ldots,\tilde{A}_{m}
and

Note that it was possible to obtain the continuity relative to the topology of
H^{s}_{loc}(I)
only because
\textrm{supp}~\left[\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-2\pi i)^{-k}a_{k}\frac{d^{k}g}{dx^{k}}\right]\subset\textrm{supp}~g\subset K
and
\frac{d^{k}g}{dx^{k}}\in H^{-s}(\mathbb{R})
for
0\leq k\leq m.

Therefore
g\in D[a(D\mbox{*})]
and

a(D)\mbox{*}g=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-2\pi i)^{-k}a_{k}\frac{d^{k}g}{dx^{k}}.

Also, it follows that
H^{-s+m}_{c}(I)\subset D\left[a(D)\mbox{*}\right].

Part II)
D\left[a(D)\mbox{*}\right]\subset H^{-s+\delta m}_{c}(I)

Now the goal is to show that there is a
0<\delta\leq 1
such that
D\left[a(D)\mbox{*}\right]\subset H^{-s+\delta m}_{c}(I).

Given
g\in D\left[a(D)\mbox{*}\right]
, by definition of the domain
a(D)\mbox{*},
there are
M>0
and a seminorm
p^{(s)}_{j}(\cdot)
such that

\left|\left\langle g,a(D)u\right\rangle\right|\leq Mp^{(s)}_{j}(u),\;u\in H^{s+m}_{0}(I).

Observe that
a(D)\mbox{*}g\in\left[H^{s}_{loc}(I)\right]^{\prime}=H^{-s}_{c}(I)
is the continuous extention of

g\circ a(D):H^{s+m}_{0}(I)\subset H^{s}_{loc}(I)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}.

Note that, for
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(I),
it holds

\left\langle a(D)\mbox{*}g,\psi\right\rangle=\left\langle g,a(D)\psi\right\rangle=\left\langle g,\sum_{l=0}^{m}(2\pi i)^{-l}a_{l}\frac{d^{l}\psi}{dx^{l}}\right\rangle=\left\langle\sum_{l=0}^{m}(-2\pi i)^{-l}a_{l}\frac{d^{l}g}{dx^{l}},\psi,\right\rangle

which means
\sum_{l=0}^{m}(-1)^{l}a_{l}\frac{d^{l}g}{dx^{l}}=a(D)\mbox{*}g
as distributions in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I)
and, since
a(D)\mbox{*}g\in H^{-s}_{c}(I)\hookrightarrow H^{-s}_{loc}(I),
it implies that
\sum_{l=0}^{m}(-2\pi i)^{-l}a_{l}\frac{d^{l}g}{dx^{l}}\in H^{-s}_{loc}(I).

Since
\sum_{l=0}^{m}(-2\pi i)^{-l}a_{l}\frac{d^{l}}{dx^{l}}
is hypoelliptic, by Theorem
2.4
, there is
0<\delta\leq 1
such that
g\in H^{-s+\delta m}_{loc}(I).

Henceforth,
g\in H^{-s+\delta m}_{loc}(I)
with
\textrm{supp}~g
compact, i.e.,
g\in H^{-s+\delta m}_{c}(I),
and that completes the proof.

∎

Theorem 2.8

2.2.
Closure of a Differential Operator on a Fréchet Space

Here we determine the closure of a differential operator with constant coefficients
a(D)
of order
m\geq 1
on

That will allow us to obtain a more precise analysis of the spectrum, in the sense that we can track the change of the values

First of all, we need to construct a convenient sequence of functions that will be the main tool to make the calculus of the closure (check

4

Let

be an interval. Given a function

Now let

a sequence of open bounded intervals with

Define

†
†

Note that

for each natural

.

Given

It follows that, for each

Lemma 2.10

For

converges to
0
in the topology of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}).
Since
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow H^{s}(\mathbb{R}),
it follows that the sequence converges to
0
in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
and, therefore,
u^{\prime}(a_{j})\phi_{j}(x-a_{j})
converges to
0
in
H^{s}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}).

Note that

[\phi(x)u^{\prime}(a_{j})\phi_{j}(x-a_{j})]^{(k)}=u^{\prime}(a_{j})\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}\phi^{(k-l)}(x)\phi_{j}^{(l)}(x-a_{j})

for
k\in\mathbb{N},
so it remains to prove that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|x|^{N}|\phi^{(k-l)}(x)\phi_{j}^{(l)}(x-a_{j})|\to 0,
as
j\to\infty,

for
N\in\mathbb{N}.

In order to do it, observe that
\textrm{supp}~\phi\subset I=(a,b)
is compact, so
d(\textrm{supp}~\phi,a)>0.
On the other hand,
\textrm{supp}~\phi_{j}(\cdot-a_{j})\subset B_{1/j}(a_{j})
so there is a
j_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
B_{1/j}(a_{j})\cap\textrm{supp}~\phi=\emptyset.

Hence

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|x|^{N}\left|\phi^{(k-l)}(x)\phi_{j}^{(l)}(x-a_{j})\right|=0,
for
j\geq j_{0}

and

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|x|^{N}\left|[\phi(x)u^{\prime}(a_{j})\phi_{j}(x-a_{j})]^{(k)}\right|\leq\left|u^{\prime}(a_{j})\right|\sum_{l=0}^{k}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|x|^{N}\left|\phi^{(k-l)}(x)\phi_{j}^{(l)}(x-a_{j})\right|

which is equal to
0

for
j\geq j_{0}
and the convergence holds.

The proof for the other sequences can be done in an analogous way.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

If
h\in H^{s}_{loc}(I),
with
s\in\mathbb{Z}
and
s\geq 0,
then
h_{j}\doteq\phi_{j}\star(\chi_{I_{j}}h_{e})\in C^{\infty}_{c}(I),j\in\mathbb{N},
converges to
h
in
H^{s}_{loc}(I).

Proof.

First of all, we shall prove this lemma holds for
s=0
, i.e., if
h\in L^{2}_{loc}(I)
, then
h_{j}=\phi_{j}\star(\chi_{I_{j}}h_{e})
converges to
h
in
L^{2}_{loc}(I).

Indeed, given
h\in L^{2}_{loc}(I)
and
\varphi_{l}
test function from a seminorm of
L^{2}_{loc}(I)
it holds

\|\varphi_{l}h-\varphi h_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\|\varphi_{l}h_{e}-\varphi_{l}h_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C\|h_{e}-h_{j}\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})},

where
C>0
is a constant which depends only on
\varphi_{l}.

Furthermore, for
j
sufficiently large that
I_{l+1}+B_{1/j}\subset I_{l+2}\subset I_{j}
, we may write

h_{e}(x)-h_{j}(x)=\int_{B_{1/j}}\phi_{j}(y)(h_{e}(x)-h_{e}(x-y))dy.

However, note that
\phi_{j}(y)(h_{e}(\cdot)-h_{e}(\cdot-y))\in L^{2}(I_{l+1})
for every
y\in B_{1/j}
and
\phi_{j}(\cdot)\big(h_{e}(x)-h_{e}(x-\cdot)\big)\in L^{2}(I_{1/j})
for every
x\in I_{l+1},
then by the Minkowski Inequality for Integrals it follows

\|h_{e}-h_{j}\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})}\leq

\int_{B_{1/j}}|\phi_{j}(y)|\|h_{e}(\cdot)-h_{e}(\cdot-y)\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})}dy,

i.e.,

\|h_{e}-h_{j}\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi_{j}(y)\chi_{B_{1/j}}(y)\|h_{e}(\cdot)-h_{e}(\cdot-y)\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})}dy.

Moreover,

\|h_{e}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})}=\|h\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})}\leq\|h\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+2})}

and

\|h_{e}(\cdot-y)\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})}=\left(\int_{I_{l+1}+B_{1/j}}|h_{e}(z)|^{2}dz\right)^{1/2}\leq\left(\int_{I_{l+2}}|h_{e}(z)|^{2}dz\right)^{1/2},

i.e.,
\|h_{e}(\cdot)-h_{e}(\cdot-y)\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})}\leq 2\|h\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+2})}
, with
\phi_{j}(y)\chi_{B_{1/j}}(y)\|h_{e}(\cdot)-h_{e}(\cdot-y)\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})}
converging to zero when
j\to\infty
a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

It is also true that

\phi_{j}(y)\chi_{B_{1/j}}(y)\|h_{e}(\cdot)-h_{e}(\cdot-y)\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})}\leq\chi_{(-1,1)}(y)2\|h_{e}\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+2})}
and
\chi_{(-1,1)}(y)2\|h_{e}\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+2})}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})

for every
y\in\mathbb{R}.

By Dominated Convergence Theorem, we get

\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi_{j}(y)\chi_{B_{1/j}}(y)\|h_{e}(\cdot)-h_{e}(\cdot-y)\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})}dy=0.

In short, given
\epsilon>0,
there is a
j_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\|\varphi_{l}h-\varphi h_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C\|h_{e}-h_{j}\|_{L^{2}(I_{l+1})}\leq C\epsilon,

for
j\geq j_{0}
and the convergence in
L^{2}_{loc}(I)
follows.

Now, for
h\in H^{k}_{loc}(I)
with
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\frac{d^{r}h}{dx^{r}}\in L^{2}_{loc}(I)
for
0\leq r\leq k
and, by the first part of this proof, that
\phi_{j}\star\left[\chi_{I_{j}}\left(\frac{d^{r}h}{dx^{r}}\right)_{e}\right]
converges to
\frac{d^{r}h}{dx^{r}}
in
L^{2}_{loc}(I).

Observe that

\frac{d^{r}}{dx^{r}}\left[\phi_{j}\star(\chi_{I_{j}}h_{e})\right]=\phi_{j}\star\frac{d^{r}}{dx^{r}}(\chi_{I_{j}}h_{e})

and

\frac{d^{r}}{dx^{r}}(\chi_{I_{j}}h_{e})=\chi_{I_{j}}\left(\frac{d^{r}h}{dx^{r}}\right)_{e}+\sum_{l=0}^{r-1}\left(u^{(l)}(a_{j})\delta_{a_{j}}^{(r-1-l)}-u^{(l)}(b_{j})\delta_{b_{j}}^{(r-1-l)}\right).

By the previous lemma, the sum
\sum_{l=0}^{r-1}\left(u^{(l)}(a_{j})\delta_{a_{j}}^{(r-1-l)}-u^{(l)}(b_{j})\delta_{b_{j}}^{(r-1-l)}\right)
converges to
0
in
L^{2}_{loc}(I).

Henceforth
\frac{d^{r}}{dx^{r}}\left[\phi_{j}\star(\chi_{I_{j}}h_{e})\right]=

=\phi_{j}\star\frac{d^{r}}{dx^{r}}(\chi_{I_{j}}h_{e})=\phi_{j}\star\left[\chi_{I_{j}}\left(\frac{d^{r}h}{dx^{r}}\right)_{e}+\sum_{l=0}^{r-1}\left(u^{(l)}(a_{j})\delta_{a_{j}}^{(r-1-l)}-u^{(l)}(b_{j})\delta_{b_{j}}^{(r-1-l)}\right)\right]

and then, for each
1\leq r\leq k
, it is true that

L^{2}_{loc}(I)-\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{d^{r}}{dx^{r}}\left[\phi_{j}\star(\chi_{I_{j}}h_{e})\right]=\frac{d^{r}h}{dx^{r}},

i.e.,
h_{j}=\phi_{j}\star(\chi_{I_{j}}h_{e})
converges to
h
in
H^{k}_{loc}(I).

∎

Theorem 2.12
.

If
a(D):H^{s+m}_{0}(I)\subset H^{s}_{loc}(I)\to H^{s}_{loc}(I)
is an elliptic differential operator, with constant coefficients, given by
\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-2\pi i)^{k}a_{k}u^{(k)}
where
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, then its closure is given by
\overline{a(D)}:H^{s+m}_{loc}(I)\subset H^{s}_{loc}(I)\longrightarrow H^{s}_{loc}(I)
with

\overline{a(D)}(u)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-2\pi i)^{k}a_{k}u^{(k)}.

Proof.

Let

\overline{a(D)}:D\left[\overline{a(D)}\right]\subset H^{s}_{loc}(I)\longrightarrow H^{s}_{loc}(I)

be the closure of

a(D):H^{s+m}_{0}(I)\subset H^{s}_{loc}(I)\longrightarrow H^{s}_{loc}(I),

where
D\left[\overline{a(D)}\right]=

\Big\{u\in H^{s}_{loc}(I);\;\exists\;(u_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset H^{s+m}_{0}(I)\mbox{ and }

f\in H^{s}_{loc}(I)\mbox{ s.t. }u_{n}\xrightarrow{H^{s}_{loc}}u\mbox{ and }a(D)u_{j}\xrightarrow{H^{s}_{loc}}f\Big\}.

By the definition of
D\left[\overline{a(D)}\right],
it immediately follows that
D\left[\overline{a(D)}\right]\subset H^{s+m}_{loc}(I)
, since every
u\in D\left[\overline{a(D)}\right]
is limit
H^{s}_{loc}
of a sequence of functions of
H^{s+m}_{0}.
Furthermore,
u_{n}\xrightarrow{H^{s}_{loc}}u
and
a(D)u_{n}\xrightarrow{H^{s}_{loc}}f
imply that
a(D)u=f
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I)
and, since
a(D)
is elliptic,
u\in H^{s+m}_{loc}(I).

On the other hand, for
u\in H^{s+m}_{loc}(I),
we have that
f\doteq a(D)u\in H^{s}_{loc}(I)
. Let
u_{n}=\varphi_{n}\star(\chi_{I_{n}}u_{e}),

n\in\mathbb{N},
then by what we have done above the theorem holds.
∎

2.3.
Spectrum of the Laplace operator on a Fréchet Space

In this section we apply the results obtained in the previous section to the Laplacian operator. The main characteristic that allow us to apply these results is the fact that both the laplacian and its adjoint are elliptic operators.
†
†

The Laplacian is a self-adjoint operator in the context of pseudodifferential operators.

First of all, since
\Delta u=u^{\prime\prime}
, from what we have discussed above, it follows that
\Delta^{*}g=g^{\prime\prime}=\Delta g
. Furthermore, since
D(\Delta)=H^{2}_{0}(I)
, we have
D(\Delta^{*})=H^{2}_{c}(I)
(here we use
s=0
).

On the other hand, both symbols of
\Delta
and
\Delta\mbox{*}
are given by
a(\xi)=-4\pi^{2}\xi^{2}.
Take
C=4\pi^{2}
then

|a(\xi)|=4\pi^{2}\xi^{2}\geq C|\xi|^{2}

and, by Definition
2.3
, it follows that
\Delta
and
\Delta\mbox{*}
are elliptic.

Corollary 2.13
.

The Laplace operator, seen as a pseudodiferencial operator

\Delta:H^{2}_{0}(0,\pi)\subset L^{2}_{loc}(0,\pi)\longrightarrow L^{2}_{loc}(0,\pi)

and its adjoint

\Delta^{\ast}:H^{2}_{c}(0,\pi)\subset L^{2}_{c}(0,\pi)\longrightarrow L^{2}_{c}(0,\pi),

both have resolvent set empty and their spectra are classified as follows:

\sigma_{p}(\Delta)=\sigma_{p}(\Delta\mbox{*})=\emptyset,

\sigma_{r}(\Delta)=\sigma_{c}(\Delta\mbox{*})=\emptyset,
and
\sigma_{c}(\Delta)=\sigma_{r}(\Delta\mbox{*})=\mathbb{C}.

Proof.

This corollary follows immediately from the ellipticity of
\Delta
and
\Delta\mbox{*}
and Theorem
2.9
.

∎

Finally, using the results obtained for
\Delta
with Theorem
2.12
its possible to obtain a better analysis for its spectrum.

By Theorem
2.12
, with
s=0
, it follows that

D\left[\overline{\Delta}\right]=H^{2}_{loc}(I)
and

\overline{\Delta}:H^{2}_{loc}(I)\subset L^{2}_{loc}(I)\longrightarrow L^{2}_{loc}(I)

is given by
\overline{\Delta}u=u^{\prime\prime}
, for
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(I).

Denote by
\Delta_{L^{2}}
the Laplacian defined on the domain

\Delta_{L^{2}}:H^{1}_{0}(I)\cap H^{2}(I)\subset L^{2}_{loc}(I)\longrightarrow L^{2}_{loc}(I).

Since
H^{2}_{0}(I)\subset H^{1}_{0}(I)\cap H^{2}(I),
we have
\overline{\Delta}=\overline{\Delta_{L^{2}}}.
Moreover, its point spectrum,
\sigma_{p}(\Delta_{L^{2}}),
is the same as when we consider the topology of
L^{2}(I),
i.e.,
\sigma_{p}(\Delta_{L^{2}})=\left\{-\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}}{l(I)^{2}}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
, where

I.

It remains to calculate
\sigma_{p}(\overline{\Delta}).

and

u\not=0

, for some

\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
the roots of
\lambda+\xi^{2},

\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence, every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
belongs to
\sigma_{p}(\overline{\Delta}).
