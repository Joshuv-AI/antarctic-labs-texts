Big Ramsey Degrees of Countable Ordinals
Joanna Boyland, William Gasarch, Nathan Hurtig, Robert Rust

Abstract
Ramsey’s theorem on countable infinite sets states that for all natural numbers n , n, for all finite colorings of the n n -element subsets of some infinite countable set, there exists an infinite countable homogeneous subset. What if we seek a homogeneous subset that is also order-equivalent to the original set? Let S S be a linearly ordered set and n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} . The big Ramsey degree of n n in S S , denoted T ⁡ ( n , S ) T(n,S) , is the least integer t t such that, for any finite coloring of the size n n subsets of S S , there exists S ′ ⊆ S S^{\prime}\subseteq S such that (i) S ′ S^{\prime} is order-equivalent to S S , and (ii) if the coloring is restricted to the size n n subsets of S ′ S^{\prime} then at most t t colors are used. Mašulović & Šobot (2021) showed that T ⁡ ( n , ω + ω ) = 2 n T(n,\omega+\omega)=2^{n} . From this one can obtain T ⁡ ( n , ζ ) = 2 n T(n,\zeta)=2^{n} . We give a direct proof that T ⁡ ( n , ζ ) = 2 n T(n,\zeta)=2^{n} . Mašulović and Šobot (2021) also showed that for all countable ordinals α < ω ω \alpha<\omega^{\omega} and n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} , T ⁡ ( n , α ) T(n,\alpha) is finite. We find exact values of T ⁡ ( n , α ) T(n,\alpha) for all ordinals α \alpha less than ω ω \omega^{\omega} and all n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} .

3.2
Summary of Results

Our work is most similar to that of Mašulović & Šobot
[
19
]
,
who study the conditions under which
T(n,\beta)
is finite. Their methods
rely on what they call a “top-down” approach, where they prove that the
property of finite big Ramsey degrees is passed from ordinals of the form
\omega^{n}

to their subsums of lesser ordinals. Our methods are “bottom-up”: we provide explicit formulas
for calculating the degree of an ordinal number from its subsums. This enables
us to compute exact results, unlike the work of Mašulović & Šobot.

In other words, ordinal
numbers less than
\omega^{\omega}
can be viewed as summations of smaller
and simpler ordinals of the form
\omega^{n}
. Our main result relies on the
big Ramsey degrees of these simpler ordinals to determine the big Ramsey
degrees of all ordinals less than
\omega^{\omega}.

In this paper, we do the following.

1.

In Section
4
we show that
T(n,\zeta)=2^{n}
. This can be obtained
by the result due to Mašulović & Šobot
[
19
]
that

T(n,\omega+\omega)=2^{n}
, but we give a direct proof using different methods.

2.

In Section
5
we find the big Ramsey degrees of
\omega\cdot k
for finite
k
.

3.

In Sections
6
and
7
we find a big Ramsey degree of
\omega^{2}

and introduce our main method for finding big Ramsey degrees of ordinals.

4.

In Sections
8
and
9
we find the big Ramsey degrees of
\omega^{d}
and

\omega^{d}\cdot k
for finite
d,k.

5.

In Section
10
we find the big Ramsey degrees of all ordinals
\alpha<\omega^{\omega}
.

\operatorname{COL}^{\prime}(x)=(\operatorname{COL}(-x),\operatorname{COL}(x)).

2.7

that is
\omega
-1-homogeneous with respect to
\operatorname{COL}^{\prime}

Theorem 5.2

Proof.

We give a

-coloring of

that has no (

)-homogeneous

.
We represent

where each

.
If an element

is the

, we represent it as the ordered pair

Before defining the coloring in general we give an example with

and

We define the color of the element

e=\{(3,12),(50,2),(110,12),(110,7777),(117,3)\}

•

Order the ordered pairs by their second coordinates.

•

If some elements have the same second coordinate, break the tie with their first coordinates. At this point in our example we have

((50,2),(117,3)(3,12),(110,12),(110,7777)).

•

Remove the second coordinates to get

This sequence is the element’s color.

Since the set of possible colors is the set of all 5-tuples of numbers

possible colors.

In general, for any
e=\{(i_{1},x_{1}),\ldots,(i_{n},x_{n})\}
, order the ordered pairs by their second coordinates, and in case of ties, use their first coordinates to determine the ordering. Then

\operatorname{COL}(e)
is the sequence of first coordinates after ordering.

Notice that the number of colors is the number of size
n
tuples where each
number is in
\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Hence there are
k^{n}
colors.
We leave it to the reader to show that there can be no
(\omega\cdot k)
-
(k^{n}-1)
-homogeneous
H
. The key idea of the proof, much like the previous lower bounds in this paper, is to use the property of order-equivalence to find elements of
H
that produce each of the
k^{n}
colors.

By the proof,
T(n,\omega\cdot k)\geq k^{n}
, and so by Theorem
5.1
,
T(n,\omega\cdot k)=k^{n}
.

∎

The Big Ramsey Degree of 2 in

This section provides a concrete example involving ordinals greater than

\omega^{2}

\operatorname{COL}\colon\binom{\omega^{2}}{2}\to[4]

\displaystyle\operatorname{COL}(\omega\cdot a_{1}+a_{2},\omega\cdot a_{3}+a_{4})=\begin{cases}1&a_{1}<a_{2}<a_{3}<a_{4}\\

7
Coloring Rules

We introduce a concept called
coloring rules
(hereafter CRs) to prove
general results about
T(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)
. The concept behind CRs is built on the
ideas of Blass et al.
[
3
]
.
We motivate the concept by examining the proof of Theorem
6.2
.

The proof of Theorem
6.2
used four functions
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
, and
f_{4}
.
These functions were chosen to cover
H
in a way where the color of every edge in
H

was output by one of
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},
or
f_{4}
.
We note a function that we did
not
use:

f\colon\binom{\omega}{4}\to\binom{\omega^{2}}{2}
defined by

f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=\operatorname{COL}(\{\omega\cdot x_{1}+x_{3},\omega\cdot x_{2}+x_{3}\}),

with
x_{1}<x_{2}<x_{3}<x_{4}.

We didn’t use
f
in the lower bound proof because
f
didn’t cover
any
edges in
H
: we constructed
H
in a
way where distinct copies of
\omega
had distinct finite coefficients. When
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
, no matter the values of
x_{1},x_{2},
and

x_{3}
, the elements

\omega\cdot x_{1}+x_{3}
and
\omega\cdot x_{2}+x_{3}
can’t both be from
H
.

We define a notion of colorings that each of
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},
and
f_{4}
satisfy for
\binom{\omega^{2}}{2}.

We also show how to count these colorings, and how these colorings are linked to big Ramsey degrees.

Definition 7.1
.

We now define CRs (coloring rules) rigorously. We impose a structure on edges and list criteria that CRs must satisfy.
For integers
n,d,k\geq 0
, an edge
e=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
in
\binom{\omega^{d}\cdot k}{n}
consists of
n
points which are elements of
\omega^{d}\cdot k
. Each element
p_{i}
is equal to

\omega^{d}\cdot b_{i}+\omega^{d-1}\cdot a_{i,d-1}+\omega^{d-2}\cdot a_{i,d-2}+\cdots+\omega^{1}\cdot a_{i,1}+a_{i,0},

where
a_{i,j}\geq 0
and
0\leq b_{i}<k
.

Thus, any edge
e
is defined by the
n
values of the
b_{i}
’s and the
n\cdot d
values of the
a_{i,j}
’s.

A
CR (coloring rule)
on
\binom{\omega^{d}\cdot k}{n}
is a pair
(\mathcal{Y},\preceq_{\mathcal{X}})
of constraints on these values
b_{i}
and
a_{i,j}
satisfying certain properties we enumerate below. Firstly,
\mathcal{Y}
is an assignment of the values for the
b_{i}
; formally, it is a map
\mathcal{Y}:[n]\to\{0,\dots,k-1\}
from indices of the
b_{i}
to the possible values for the
b_{i}
. Having
\mathcal{Y}(i)=v
means we constrain
b_{i}=v
.
Meanwhile,
\preceq_{\mathcal{X}}
is a total preorder (i.e. every two of elements are comparable, and
\preceq_{\mathcal{X}}
is reflexive and transitive) on the indices of the
a_{i,j}
. Having
(i,j)\preceq_{\mathcal{X}}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
means we constrain
a_{i,j}\leq a_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
.

We often denote
\mathcal{Y}
using an ordered list of clauses:

b_{1}=\mathcal{Y}(1),b_{2}=\mathcal{Y}(2),\dots,b_{n}=\mathcal{Y}(n).

We often denote
\preceq_{\mathcal{X}}
by a permutation of the expressions
a_{1,0},\dots,a_{n,d-1}
interspersed by either
<
or
=
. We write
a_{i,j}<a_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
to mean that both
(i,j)\preceq_{\mathcal{X}}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
and
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\not\preceq_{\mathcal{X}}(i,j)
and we write
a_{i,j}=a_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
to mean both
(i,j)\preceq_{\mathcal{X}}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
and
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\preceq_{\mathcal{X}}(i,j)
. Note that
x=y<z
and
y=x<z
are two representations of the same preorder, so this notation is not unique. One example of such a representation is

a_{1,1}=a_{2,1}<a_{1,0}<a_{2,0}.

We refer to the expressions
a_{i,j}
and
b_{i}
when used in our notation for
\mathcal{Y}
and
\preceq_{X}
as ‘variables’, and will describe different preorders
\preceq_{X}
by discussing permutations of some sequence of
a_{i,j}
.

To be a CR the following must hold:

1.

If
d\geq 1
, then for all
i<i^{\prime},

a_{i,0}<a_{i^{\prime},0}.
Otherwise when
d=0
,
b_{i}<b_{i^{\prime}}
for all
i<i^{\prime}
. (The element indices are ordered by their lowest-exponent variable.)

2.

For all
i,i^{\prime}
,
(\exists j\;a_{i,j}=a_{i^{\prime},j})\implies b_{i}=b_{i^{\prime}}
. (Two elements with any
a
values that are the same must have the same
b
value.)

3.

For all
j>j^{\prime},

a_{i,j}<a_{i,j^{\prime}}
. (The high-exponent variables of each element are strictly less than the low-exponent variables.)

4.

a_{i,j}=a_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\implies j=j^{\prime}
. (Only variables with the same exponent can be equal.)

5.

a_{i,j}\neq a_{i^{\prime},j}\implies a_{i,j-1}\neq a_{i^{\prime},j-1}
for all
j>0
. (Two elements that differ in a high-exponent variable differ in all lower-exponent variables.)

An example of a CR for
\binom{\omega^{2}}{2}
is

b_{1}=0,~b_{2}=0,~a_{1,1}=a_{2,1}<a_{1,0}<a_{2,0}.

Note that because
k=1
in the example, we must have
b_{i}=0
for every
i
.

Definition 7.2
.

The
size
of a CR is how many equivalence classes its
a_{i,j}
form under
\preceq_{X}
:
for example,
a_{1,1}=a_{2,1}<a_{1,0}<a_{2,0}
would have size
p=3
regardless of
b_{i}
. Therefore a CR’s size
p
can be no larger than
n\cdot d
.

Definition 7.3
.

1.

P_{p}\left(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k\right)
is the number of CRs of size
p
there are for
\binom{\omega^{d}\cdot k}{n}
.

2.

P\left(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k\right)
is the total number of CRs there are for
\binom{\omega^{d}\cdot k}{n}
of any size. It can be calculated as

\sum_{p=0}^{n\cdot d}P_{p}(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k).

We will show
T(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)=P(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)
.

Definition 7.4
.

An edge

\{\omega^{d}\cdot b_{i}+\omega^{d-1}\cdot a_{i,d-1}+\omega^{d-2}\cdot a_{i,d-2}+\cdots+\omega^{1}\cdot a_{i,1}+a_{i,0}\colon 1\leq i\leq n\}

satisfies
some CR if
\mathcal{Y}(i)=b_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq n
and if

(i,j)\preceq_{\mathcal{X}}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\iff a_{i,j}\leq a_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
for all
1\leq i,i^{\prime}\leq n
and
0\leq j,j^{\prime}<d.

Note that some edges might not satisfy any CRs.

8
Big Ramsey Degrees of
\omega^{d}

This section is devoted to the case where
k=1
in
\binom{\omega^{d}\cdot k}{n}
. When
k=1
, each
b_{i}
in a CR is forced to be 0. Then all
b_{i}
values are the same, so criterion
2
of Definition
7.1
is always satisfied. In this section, our proofs focus only on how
\preceq_{X}
permutes the
a_{i,j}
variables. In this section, when values for
b_{i}
are not specified, they are assumed to be all 0 by default.

This section aims to show equality between big Ramsey degrees and numbers of CRs. We start with a recurrence that counts CRs.

Lemma 8.1
.

For integers
n,d\geq 0
,

\displaystyle P_{p}\left(n,\omega^{d}\right)=\begin{cases}0&d=0\land n\geq 2\\
1&n=0\land p=0\\
0&n=0\land p\geq 1\\
1&d=0\land n=1\land p=0\\
0&d=0\land n=1\land p\geq 1\\
1&d=1\land n\geq 1\land n=p\\
0&d=1\land n\geq 1\land n\neq p\\
0&d\geq 2\land n\geq 1\land p=0\\
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{p-1}\binom{p-1}{i}P_{i}\left(j,\omega^{d-1}\right)P_{p-1-i}\left(n-j,\omega^{d}\right)&d\geq 2\land n\geq 1\land p\geq 1\end{cases}

Proof.

Case 1.
First, suppose
n\geq 2
and
d=0
. As argued at the beginning of this section,
b_{i}=0
for all
i
. But by criterion
1
of Definition
7.1
, since
d=0
we need
b_{1}<b_{2}
, so no CRs are possible regardless of size
p
. This proves the first case of the result.

Case 2.
Suppose
n=0
.
Since there are no
b_{i}
or
a_{i,j}
, the criteria are vacuously satisfied. There is only one CR and it has size
p=0
. This proves the second and third cases of the result.

Case 3.
When both
d=0
and
n\leq 1
,
criterion
1
of Definition
7.1
is
vacuously satisfied with either no
b_{i}
or
b_{1}=0
. Again, because
n\cdot d=0
, there are no
a_{i,j}
. Thus there is only one CR of size
p=0
, which proves the fourth and fifth cases of the result.

Case 4.
Now suppose
n\geq 1
and
d=1
. To ensure criterion
1
of Definition
7.1
, each of the
n
variables
a_{i,0}
can only form one CR
a_{1,0}<a_{2,0}<\cdots<a_{n,0}
of size

P_{n}\left(n,\omega^{d}\right)=1
and
P_{p}\left(n,\omega^{d}\right)=0
for

. This proves the sixth and seventh cases of the result.

Case 5.
Finally, consider
n\geq 1,d\geq 2
. By the definition of a CR, because
n\cdot d>0,
there are no CRs of size
p=0.
We prove this final case by showing that the process described below creates all possible CRs of an expression.

Let
n\geq 1
,
d\geq 2
, and
p\geq 1
be naturals. Let
1\leq i\leq n
and
0\leq s\leq p-1
be naturals. As we proceed through the process, we work with an example of
n=4
,
d=5
,
p=13
,
m=2
, and
s=5
.

We create

\binom{p-1}{s}P_{s}\left(m,\omega^{d-1}\right)P_{p-1-s}\left(n-m,\omega^{d}\right)

CRs of size
p
by combining
P_{s}\left(m,\omega^{d-1}\right)
CRs of size
s
and
P_{p-1-s}\left(n-m,\omega^{d}\right)
CRs of size
p-1-s
, with
\binom{p-1}{s}
CRs derived from each pair.

Let
\tau_{1}
represent one of the
P_{s}\left(m,\omega^{d-1}\right)
CRs of
\binom{\omega^{d-1}}{m}
of size
s
, and
\tau_{2}
represent one of the
P_{p-1-s}\left(n-m,\omega^{d}\right)
CRs of
\binom{\omega^{d}}{n-m}
of size

. In our example, let

\displaystyle\tau_{1}

\displaystyle\colon a_{1,3}=a_{2,3}<a_{1,2}=a_{2,2}<a_{1,1}=a_{2,1}<a_{1,0}<a_{2,0}

\displaystyle\tau_{2}

\displaystyle\colon a_{1,4}=a_{2,4}<a_{1,3}=a_{2,3}<a_{1,2}=a_{2,2}<a_{2,1}<a_{1,1}<a_{1,0}<a_{2,0}.

Then we can combine each
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
to form
\binom{p-1}{s}

in
\tau_{2}
to
a_{i+m,j}
, and combine the equivalence classes of the CRs together, preserving each CR’s original ordering of its own equivalence classes: there are
\binom{p-1}{i}
ways to do this. In our example, after changing the indices of
\tau_{2}
we have

\displaystyle\tau_{1}

\displaystyle\colon a_{1,3}=a_{2,3}<a_{1,2}=a_{2,2}<a_{1,1}=a_{2,1}<a_{1,0}<a_{2,0}

\displaystyle\tau_{2}

\displaystyle\colon a_{3,4}=a_{4,4}<a_{3,3}=a_{4,3}<a_{3,2}=a_{4,2}<a_{4,1}<a_{3,1}<a_{3,0}<a_{4,0}

and one of the
\binom{12}{5}
permutations is

\displaystyle a_{3,4}=a_{4,4}<a_{1,3}=a_{2,3}<a_{1,2}=a_{2,2}<a_{3,3}=a_{4,3}<a_{3,2}=a_{4,2}

\displaystyle<a_{1,1}=a_{2,1}<a_{4,1}<a_{1,0}<a_{3,1}<a_{3,0}<a_{2,0}<a_{4,0}.

This new CR likely breaks criterion

according to where
a_{i,0}
is in the ordering of all

\displaystyle a_{2,4}=a_{4,4}<a_{1,3}=a_{3,3}<a_{1,2}=a_{3,2}<a_{2,3}=a_{4,3}<a_{2,2}=a_{4,2}

\displaystyle<a_{1,1}=a_{3,1}<a_{4,1}<a_{1,0}<a_{2,1}<a_{2,0}<a_{3,0}<a_{4,0}.

There are now
d\cdot(n-m)+(d-1)\cdot m=n\cdot d-m

for

at the front of the new CR, bringing its size to

. We insert

9.2

Theorem 9.4
.

For
n,d,k\in\mathbb{N}
,
T\left(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k\right)\geq P\left(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k\right)
.
Therefore, by Theorem
9.3
,
T(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)=P(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)
.

Proof.

If
P(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)=0
, then this is satisfied vacuously because
T(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)\geq 0
. Suppose
P(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)\geq 1
. Note that all CRs of
\binom{\omega^{d}\cdot k}{n}
are disjoint from each other. That is, for any edge
e\in\binom{\omega^{d}\cdot k}{n}
, if
e
satisfies
\tau^{\prime}
, then it does not satisfy any nonequivalent CR of
\binom{\omega^{d}\cdot k}{n}
. This is because if
e
were to satisfy two CRs
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
, then
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
must share the same
b_{i}
values, equivalence classes, and order, so the CRs must be equivalent. Therefore, we can index them
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{P(n,\omega^{d})}
and construct a coloring
\operatorname{COL}\colon\binom{\omega^{d}\cdot k}{n}\to[P(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)]
with

\displaystyle\operatorname{COL}(e)=\begin{cases}i&e\textnormal{ satisfies }\tau_{i}\\
1&\textnormal{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Similar to Theorem
6.2
, our coloring has two ways to output color 1, both
through the satisfaction of
\tau_{1}
and through the catch-all case. The part that forces
color 1 to be present in all order-equivalent subsets is the satisfaction of
\tau_{1}
.
For arbitrary
H\approx\omega^{d}\cdot k
and
\tau
, we find variables
b_{i}
and
e_{i,j}
where

\displaystyle\{\omega^{d}b_{1}+\omega^{d-1}e_{1,d-1}+\cdots+\omega^{1}e_{1,1}+e_{1,0},\ldots,\omega^{d}b_{n}+\omega^{d-1}e_{n,d-1}+\cdots+\omega e_{n,1}+e_{n,0}\}

satisfies
\tau
.

For any
H\approx\omega^{d}\cdot k
and
\tau
, we first separate
H

into
k
ordered sets by the leading coefficient, each order-equivalent to
\omega^{d}
.

Then, if there are equivalence classes in
\tau
, using the process formally described in the proof of Theorem
8.4
,
we consider the leading equivalence class of
\tau
. By criterion
2

of Definition
7.1
, all variables in that equivalence class
must come from the same set order-equivalent to
\omega^{d}
. We assign a finite value to
that equivalence class, and move to the next class
with a potentially different
b
value
using the assigned finite value as a lower bound for the next one. We can repeat this process to find
e_{i,j}
that satisfy every CR of
\binom{\omega^{d}\cdot k}{n}
for arbitrary
H\approx\omega^{d}
. Then, we can assign the
b_{i}
directly according to
\tau
.

If there are no equivalence classes in
\tau
(it has size

), we can simply assign the variables
b_{i}
directly according to
\tau
.

Therefore for all
H\approx\omega^{d}
,

|\operatorname{COL}\big(\binom{H}{n}\big)|\geq P(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)
. Hence
T(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)\geq P\left(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k\right)
. Therefore, by Theorem
9.3
,
T(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)=P(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)
.
∎

10
Big Ramsey Degrees of Ordinals less than
\omega^{\omega}

10.1
General Coloring Rules

We defined CRs (coloring rules) to compute big Ramsey degrees of ordinals of the form
\omega^{d}\cdot k
. We’ll
now extend the definition to
GCRs
(general coloring rules), which allows us to compute big Ramsey degrees for all ordinals less than
\omega^{\omega}
.

Definition 10.1
.

We now define GCRs rigorously. Much like the definition of CRs, we impose a structure on edges, and then list criteria that GCRs must satisfy.
Consider some ordinal
\alpha<\omega^{\omega}
:

\alpha\approx\omega^{d}\cdot k_{d}+\omega^{d-1}\cdot k_{d-1}+\cdots+\omega\cdot k_{1}+k_{0}.

Then
\alpha
is the addition of
d+1
ordinals each of the form
\omega^{i}\cdot k_{i}
.
For any element
\beta
of
\alpha
, there is some
i
such that

\beta\in\omega^{d}\cdot k_{d}+\cdots+\omega^{i+1}\cdot k_{i+1}+\omega^{i}\cdot k_{i}\text{ and }\beta\not\in\omega^{d}\cdot k_{d}+\cdots+\omega^{i+1}\cdot k_{i+1}.

In this case, we write that

\beta

originated
from the
\omega^{i}\cdot k_{i}
part of
\alpha
. Note that the set of
\beta\in\alpha
that originated from the
\omega^{i}\cdot k_{i}
part of
\alpha
is order-equivalent to
\omega^{i}\cdot k_{i}.

For an integer
n\geq 0
, there are
n
elements
p_{i}
in any

e\in\binom{\alpha}{n}
. Unlike the definition of CRs, in this definition, we will need to allow each element of
e
to
have anywhere from 0 to
d
variables
a_{i,j}
, depending on which of the
d+1
ordered sets the element originated from.
For
1\leq i\leq n
, we use
c_{i}
for the
number of such variables that the element
p_{i}
has (therefore the element originated from the
\omega^{c_{i}}\cdot k_{c_{i}}
part of
\alpha
). We denote each element of
e
as

\omega^{d}\cdot k_{d}+\cdots+\omega^{c_{i}+1}\cdot k_{c_{i}+1}+\omega^{c_{i}}\cdot b_{i}+\omega^{c_{i}-1}\cdot a_{i,c_{i}-1}+\omega^{c_{i}-2}\cdot a_{i,c_{i}-2}+\cdots+\omega^{1}\cdot a_{i,1}+a_{i,0}.

where
0\leq b_{i}<k_{c_{i}}
and
0\leq a_{i,j}
. Note that for all elements originating from the
\omega^{c_{i}}\cdot k_{c_{i}}
part of
\alpha,
the leading terms

\omega^{d}\cdot k_{d}+\cdots+\omega^{c_{i}+1}\cdot k_{c_{i}+1}

are always the same; only the variables
c_{i},b_{i},
and
a_{i,j}
are needed to uniquely identify an element.

A
general coloring rule
, hereafter referred to as a GCR, is a triple
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{Y},\preceq_{\mathcal{X}})
of constraints on the
c_{i},b_{i},
and
a_{i,j}
.

\mathcal{C}
is a map
\mathcal{C}:[n]\to\{0,\dots,d\}
from indices
i
to the corresponding values
c_{i}
.
\mathcal{Y}
is a map
\mathcal{Y}:[n]\to\omega
such that
\mathcal{Y}(i)\leq k_{\mathcal{C}(i)},
mapping from indices
i
to the values
b_{i}
.
\preceq_{\mathcal{X}}
is a total preorder on the indices of the
a_{i,j}
. We continue to use the same notation to represent the preorder.

GCRs must fulfill the following criteria (only criterion
6
below is different from its corresponding criterion in Definition
7.1
, the definition for CRs):

1.

If
d\geq 1
, then for all
i<i^{\prime},

a_{i,0}<a_{i^{\prime},0}.
Otherwise when
d=0
,
b_{i}<b_{i^{\prime}}
for all
i<i^{\prime}
. (The element indices are ordered by their lowest-exponent variable.)

2.

a_{i,j}=a_{i^{\prime},j}\implies b_{i}=b_{i^{\prime}}
for all
j
. (Elements with any
a
values that are the same must have the same
b
value.)

3.

For all
j>j^{\prime},

a_{i,j}<a_{i,j^{\prime}}.
(The high-exponent variables of each element are strictly less than the low-exponent variables.)

4.

a_{i,j}=a_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\implies j=j^{\prime}
. (Only variables with the same exponent can be equal.)

5.

a_{i,j}\neq a_{i^{\prime},j}\implies a_{i,j-1}\neq a_{i^{\prime},j-1}
for all
j>0
. (Elements that differ in a high-exponent variable differ in all lower-exponent variables.)

6.

c_{i}\neq c_{i^{\prime}}\implies(b_{i}\neq b_{i^{\prime}}
and
a_{i,j}\neq a_{i^{\prime},j})
for all
0\leq j<d
. (Different
c
variables mean different
b
and
a
variables.)

For an edge

e=\{\omega^{c_{i}}\cdot b_{i}+\omega^{c_{i}-1}\cdot a_{i,c_{i}-1}+\omega^{c_{i}-2}\cdot a_{i,c_{i}-2}+\cdots+\omega^{1}\cdot a_{i,1}+a_{i,0}\colon 1\leq i\leq n\},

e

satisfies
the GCR
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{Y},\preceq_{\mathcal{X}})
if
c_{i}=\mathcal{C}(i)
and
b_{i}=\mathcal{Y}(i)
for every
1\leq i\leq n
, and if
(i,j)\preceq_{\mathcal{X}}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\iff a_{i,j}\leq a_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
for all
1\leq i,i^{\prime}\leq n
,
0\leq j<c_{i},
and
0\leq j^{\prime}<c_{i^{\prime}}.

Definition 10.2
.

We again define the
size
of a GCR to be how many equivalence classes its
a
variables form. A GCR’s size
p
is still bounded above by
d\cdot n
.

Definition 10.3
.

1.

S_{p}(n,\alpha)
is the number of GCRs of size
p
there are for
\binom{\alpha}{n}
.

2.

S(n,\alpha)
is the total number of GCRs there are for
\binom{\alpha}{n}
regardless of size. It can be calculated as

\sum_{p=0}^{n\cdot d}S_{p}(n,\alpha).

We will show
T(n,\alpha)=S\left(n,\alpha\right)
.

Lemma 10.4
.

For
n,d,k,p\in\mathbb{N}
,
S_{p}(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)=P_{p}(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha=\omega^{d}\cdot k
. Suppose some
c_{i}\neq d
. Then
b_{i}<k_{c_{i}}
by Definition
10.1
, so
b_{i}<0
, which is impossible. Thus
c_{i}=d
. Then there are the same count of
n\cdot d
variables
a_{i,j}
being permuted, the new criterion
6
has no effect because all
c_{i}
are equal. Hence both are under the same restrictions so
S_{p}(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)=P_{p}(n,\omega^{d}\cdot k)
.
∎

Lemma 10.5
.

For all
\alpha<\omega^{\omega}
with

\displaystyle\alpha\approx\omega^{d}\cdot k_{d}+\omega^{d-1}\cdot k_{d-1}+\cdots+\omega\cdot k_{1}+k_{0}\text{ with }k_{d}\neq 0\text{ and }d>0,~~~~~~~~~

\displaystyle S_{p}\left(n,\alpha\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{p}{i}P_{i}\left(j,\omega^{d}\cdot k_{d}\right)S_{p-i}\left(n-j,\omega^{d-1}\cdot k_{d-1}+\cdots+\omega\cdot k_{1}+k_{0}\right).

When the conditions
k_{d}\neq 0
and
d>0
cannot be satisfied, then
\alpha<\omega
and
S_{p}(n,k_{0})=P_{p}(n,k_{0})
.

Proof.

When
k_{0}
is the only nonzero
k
term, Lemma
10.4
shows
S_{p}(n,\alpha)=P_{p}(n,\alpha)
. When
d\geq 1
, we describe a process of combining CRs with GCRs to create GCRs for
\binom{\alpha}{n}
.

Let
n\geq 0,p\geq 0
be naturals. For some
\alpha<\omega^{\omega}
, let
0\leq m\leq n
and
0\leq s\leq p
be integers. We create

\binom{p}{s}P_{s}\left(m,\omega^{d}\cdot k_{d}\right)S_{p-s}\left(n-m,\omega^{d-1}\cdot k_{d-1}+\cdots+k_{0}\right)

GCRs, with each GCR having
m
elements from the
\omega^{d}\cdot k_{d}
part of
\alpha
and
n-m
elements from parts with lower exponents.

Let
\tau_{1}
represent one of the
P_{s}\left(m,\omega^{d}\cdot k_{d}\right)
CRs of
\binom{\omega^{d}\cdot k_{d}}{m}
of size
s
, and
\tau_{2}
represent one of the
S_{p-s}\left(n-m,\omega^{d-1}\cdot k_{d-1}+\cdots+k_{0}\right)
GCRs of
\binom{\omega^{d-1}\cdot k_{d-1}+\cdots+k_{0}}{n-m}
of size
p-s
. We change
\tau_{1}
into a GCR by assigning it
c_{i}=d
for all
c_{i}
.

Then we can combine each
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
to form
\binom{p}{s}
unique new GCRs of size
p
: Change the indices
\tau_{2}
and permute the equivalence classes as in the proof of Lemma
8.1
. Note that we do not insert a leading equivalence class – this is because we do not need to increase the exponent or size of
\tau_{1}
.

We can keep each
c_{i}
and
b_{i}
value the same, and change their indices alongside the
a_{i,j}
variables to ensure criterion
1
.

Each GCR is unique by the
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
used to create it because the process is invertible: we can identify the elements originally from
\tau_{1}
because they uniquely have
c_{i}=d
.

We claim each GCR created by this process has the properties described by Definition
10.1
: Because all
c_{i}
are equal for
\tau_{1}
, criterion
6
is satisfied for the elements from
\tau_{1}
.
Criterion
1
is satisfied by changing the indices of the variables.
The remaining criteria are satisfied because
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
satisfied them and their internal orders and equivalence classes were preserved in permuting the equivalence classes. Therefore this process does not overcount GCRs.

We also claim that every GCR of
\binom{\alpha}{n}
is counted by this process: each can be mapped to some
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
that create it by a similar argument to proving that the process creates unique GCRs 2 paragraphs above.
∎

. Note that all GCRs of
\binom{\alpha}{n}
are disjoint from each other. That is, for any edge
e\in\binom{\alpha}{n}
, if
e
satisfies
\tau^{\prime}
, then it does not satisfy any nonequivalent GCR of
\binom{\alpha}{n}
. This is because if
e
were to satisfy two GCR
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
, then
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
must share the same
c_{i}
,
b_{i}
, equivalence classes, and order, so the GCRs must be equivalent. Therefore, we can index them
\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{S(n,\alpha)}
and construct a coloring
\operatorname{COL}\colon\binom{\alpha}{n}\to[S(n,\alpha)]
with

\displaystyle\operatorname{COL}(e)=\begin{cases}i&e\textnormal{ satisfies }\tau_{i}\\
1&\textnormal{otherwise}\end{cases}

For arbitrary
H\approx\alpha
and a GCR
\tau
for
\alpha
, we can assign
c_{i}
and
b_{i}
based on
\tau
. Then we can apply a similar process to the one used in Theorem
9.4
to find
e_{i,j}
variables that match the permutation of
a_{i,j}
variables.

Let
\alpha\approx\omega^{d}\cdot k_{d}+\omega^{d-1}\cdot k_{d-1}+\cdots+\omega\cdot k_{1}+k_{0}
.
We can separate
H
into
d+1
sets each order-equivalent to
\omega^{j}\cdot k_{j}
for
0\leq j\leq d
, and separate each of those into
k_{j}
sets order-equivalent to
\omega^{j}
.

Then, for each equivalence class in
\tau
, using the process formally described in the proof of Theorem
8.4
,
we consider the leading equivalence class of
\tau
. By criteria
2
and
6

of Definition
10.1
, all variables in that equivalence class
must come from the same set order-equivalent to
\omega^{j}
. We assign a finite value to
that equivalence class and move to the next class with potentially different

c
and
b
values, using the assigned finite value as a lower bound for the next one. We can repeat this process to find
e_{i,j}
that satisfy each GCR of
\binom{\alpha}{n}
for arbitrary
H\approx\alpha
.

Therefore for all

H\approx\alpha
,

|\operatorname{COL}\big(\binom{H}{n}\big)|\geq S(n,\alpha)
so
T(n,\alpha)\geq S\left(n,\alpha\right)
.
∎
