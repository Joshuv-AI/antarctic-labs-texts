Quotient-convergence of Submodular Setfunctions
Kristóf Bérczi, Márton Borbényi, László Lovász, László Márton Tóth

Abstract
We introduce the concept of quotient-convergence for sequences of submodular set functions, providing, among others, a new framework for the study of convergence of matroids through their rank functions. Extending the limit theory of bounded degree graphs, which analyzes graph sequences via neighborhood sampling, we address the challenge posed by the absence of a neighborhood concept in matroids. We show that any bounded set function can be approximated by a sequence of finite set functions that quotient-converges to it. In addition, we explicitly construct such sequences for increasing, submodular, and upper continuous set functions, and prove the completeness of the space under quotient-convergence. Keywords: Matroid limits, Quotients, Quotient-convergence, Submodular setfunctions

Introduction

The theory of graph limits provides tools to analyze sequences of graphs, offering insights into the structural similarities of large graphs through analytic methods. This analytic viewpoint helps to understand complex networks and their applications in various fields of mathematics and computer science, such as statistical physics, network theory and probability theory. For graph sequences, convergence of a growing sequence of graphs
can be defined in two complementary was: in terms of the distributions of small subgraphs sampled randomly from the members of the sequence (left-convergence), and in terms of homomorphisms into small graphs (right-convergence). Under mild conditions, these two notions turn out equivalent (see
[
1
,
4
]
for bounded-degree graphs and
[
5
,
6
]
for dense graphs).

Once convergence is defined, there is a natural question to assign a limit object for convergent graph sequences. Depending on the specifics of the sequence, such limit objects can be described as unimodular random locally finite graphs, graphons, graphings, scaling limits, and so on; see
[
11
]
for details. Once a limit object is defined in a given class of structures, the “soficity” question arises: is every member of this class a limit object of finite structures? This question can be quite easy (e.g. for dense graphs) or a long-standing open problem (e.g. the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture for bounded-degree graphs).

Matroids capture fundamental graph features such as spanning trees, matchings, connectivity and planarity, and thus provide a unified framework for studying graph-related problems; see
[
17
,
14
,
15
]
. The question of a limit theory for matroids has been raised by several authors. Left-convergence, based on local properties, has been studied in
[
9
]
; this concept focuses on small (finite) subsets. We take the other route and introduce right-convergence, better reflecting macroscopic properties of matroids. One possible axiomatization of matroids is through the rank function, which extends the standard notion of rank of a set of vectors. The most important property of the rank function
rk
is that it is submodular:

\text{\rm rk}(X)+\text{\rm rk}(Y)\geq\text{\rm rk}(X\cap Y)+\text{\rm rk}(X\cup Y)

for all subsets
X,Y
of the ground set.

Limits of matroid rank functions and other important submodular setfunctions from combinatorics are submodular setfunctions on infinite sets, which leads to an analytic study of submodularity. Independently of the combinatorial applications, this research direction was initiated as early as in the 1950s by Choquet
[
7
]
, starting a line of research of “nonlinear integral” and related topics; see
[
8
,
10
]
. The study of connections between the analytic and combinatorial theories of submodularity has recently been initiated
[
13
]
and a connection with local-global convergence of bounded-degree graphs has been established in
[
12
]
and our parallel paper
[
2
]
.

In this paper, we introduce a form of right-convergence, called
quotient-convergence
, of a sequence of setfunctions; this provides, in particular, a notion of convergence of matroids through their rank functions (Section
2
). We show that every quotient-convergent sequence of finite increasing submodular setfunctions converges to an increasing submodular setfunction on a Borel space (Section
3
), where we call a setfunction
finite
if its domain is finite. This implies by standard arguments the completeness of the space of increasing submodular setfunctions under quotient-convergence. We settle the soficity problem by proving that for any bounded setfunction, there exists a sequence of finite setfunctions that quotient-converges to it, and we also give an explicit construction of such a sequence when the setfunction is increasing, submodular and continuous from above (Section
4
).

The limit object is not unique; our construction provides a limiting submodular setfunction that is continuous from below. In fact, the most natural limit object is a submodular setfunction on the clopen subsets of the Cantor set (if we allow setfunctions defined on set-algebras rather than sigma-algebras). The relationship between these representations of the limit is not fully understood.

2
Quotient-convergence

We need some definitions. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of subsets of the set
J
. A
setfunction
on
(J,\mathcal{F})
is a function
\varphi\colon\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
. A
lattice family
is a family
\mathcal{F}
of subsets of a set
J
closed under union and intersection. A setfunction
\varphi
on a lattice family
(J,\mathcal{F})
is
submodular
, if

\varphi(X)+\varphi(Y)\geq\varphi(X\cap Y)+\varphi(X\cup Y)

for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{F}
. Submodularity is known to be equivalent to
\varphi(A\cup X)-\varphi(A\cup Y)\geq\varphi(X)-\varphi(Y)
for all
A,X,Y\in\mathcal{B}
with
X\subseteq Y
, also referred to as the
diminishing returns
property. In this paper, we will assume that
\varphi(\emptyset)=0
.

We need several different smoothness conditions on a setfunction
\varphi
. If
\mathcal{F}
is closed under countable union, then we say that
\varphi
is lower continuous (or continuous from below), if for every sequence
X_{1}\subseteq X_{2}\subseteq\ldots
of sets in
\mathcal{F}
, if
\varphi(\cup_{n}X_{n})=\lim_{n}\varphi(X_{n})
.
Upper continuous
(or continuous from above) is defined analogously. We say that an increasing setfunction
\varphi
defined on the compact subsets of a topological space is
continuous from the right
if for all
\varepsilon>0
and
K\in\mathcal{K}
, there exists an open set
U
such that
\varphi(K^{\prime})<\varphi(K)+\varepsilon
for all
K^{\prime}\subseteq U
,
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
.

2.1
The quotient profile

We denote by
\mathbb{N}
the
set of positive integers
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
k
-quotient set

Q_{k}(\varphi)
as the set of all quotients of
\varphi
on
[k]
. In other words,
Q_{k}(\varphi)
is the set of all setfunctions
\varphi\circ F^{-1}
, where
F\colon J\to[k]
is a measurable map. The set
Q_{k}(\varphi)
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2^{k}}
, with the two coordinates
\varphi\circ F^{-1}(\emptyset)=0
and
\varphi\circ F^{-1}(J)=\varphi(J)
being fixed. One can think of
F
as a measurable partition of
J
into
k
(possibly empty) parts, and
\varphi\circ F^{-1}
is a quotient of
\varphi
by substituting each partition class by a single element. Therefore, we also use the notation
\varphi/\mathcal{P}
for denoting the quotient of
\varphi
corresponding to a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
J
. Note that
Q_{k}(\varphi)
is invariant under the permutations of
[k]
. We call the sequence
(Q_{k}(\varphi)\colon k\in\mathbb{N})
the
quotient profile
of
\varphi
. The sequence of closures
(\overline{Q}_{k}(\varphi)\colon k\in\mathbb{N})
is the
closed quotient profile
of
\varphi
. Often we only need to speak about the
quotient set

Q(\varphi)=\cup_{k}Q_{k}(\varphi)
of all finite quotients of
\varphi
.

The quotient profile contains a lot of information about the setfunction. Let us give some examples.

Example 2.1

(Finitary properties)
.

Many properties can be expressed in terms of the function values on finite set-algebras contained in
(J,\mathcal{B})
: for example, increasing, submodular, infinite-alternating, finitely additive,
0
-
1
valued. Equivalently, such a property holds for a setfunction
\varphi
if and only if it holds for every quotient
\varphi/\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathcal{P}
is a partition into a finite number of measurable sets. Such properties are called
finitary
in
[
3
]
.

Example 2.2

(Ideals)
.

Every
0
-
1
valued increasing submodular setfunction on
(J,\mathcal{B})
is of the form
\varphi(X)={\mathbbm{1}}(X\notin\mathcal{I})
, where
\mathcal{I}
is a set ideal. Every quotient set
Q_{k}(\varphi)
consists of all setfunctions
\psi_{k,A}\colon 2^{[k]}\to\{0,1\}
where
\psi_{k,A}(X)={\mathbbm{1}}(A\cap X\not=\emptyset)
for some nonempty subset
A\subseteq[k]
. Furthermore, among these setfunctions, those defined by maximal ideals are exactly the ones that are finitely additive. This is a finitary property, and so this means that all finite quotients are defined by maximal ideals, i.e., they are of the form
\delta_{x}
for a single element
x
. However, the profile does not indicate whether the ideal is principal or not.

Example 2.3

(Binary matroids)
.

A
matroid

M=(J,\text{\rm rk})
is defined by its finite
ground set

J
and its
rank function

\text{\rm rk}\colon 2^{J}\to\mathbb{Z}
that satisfies the
rank axioms
: (R1)
\text{\rm rk}(\emptyset)=0
, (R2)
X\subseteq Y\Rightarrow\text{\rm rk}(X)\leq\text{\rm rk}(Y)
, (R3)
\text{\rm rk}(X)\leq|X|
, and (R4)
\text{\rm rk}(X)+\text{\rm rk}(Y)\geq\text{\rm rk}(X\cap Y)+\text{\rm rk}(X\cup Y)
. Observe that (R2) asserts that the rank functions is increasing, while (R4) asserts the rank function being submodular. A subset
X\subseteq J
is
independent
if
|X|=\text{\rm rk}(X)
. The
rank
of the matroid is
\text{\rm rk}(J)
.

Let
M=(J,\text{\rm rk})
be a matroid and
A\subseteq J
. The
deletion of
A
, also called the
restriction to
J\setminus A
results in the matroid
M|A=(J\setminus A,\text{\rm rk}^{\prime})
with rank function
\text{\rm rk}^{\prime}(X)=\text{\rm rk}(X)
for
X\subseteq J\setminus A
. The
contraction of
A
results in a matroid
M/A=(J\setminus A,\text{\rm rk}^{\prime\prime})
with rank function
\text{\rm rk}^{\prime\prime}(X)=\text{\rm rk}(X\cup A)-\text{\rm rk}(A)
for
X\subseteq J\setminus A
. A matroid that can be obtained from
M
by a sequence of restrictions and contractions is called a
minor
of
M
.

The quotient profile of the rank function of a matroid carries a lot of structural information about the matroid itself, one particularly interesting example being the representability over the two-element field. A matroid is called
binary
if its elements can be identified with the columns of a
(0,1)
-matrix in such a way that a subset of elements is independent if and only if the corresponding columns are linearly independent in GF(2). Tutte
[
16
]
showed that binary matroids admit a forbidden minor characterization: a matroid is binary if and only if it has no minor isomorphic to
U_{4,2}
.

Let
M=(J,\text{\rm rk})
be a matroid. Then, by looking at
Q_{6}(\text{\rm rk})
, one can decide if the matroid is binary or not. Indeed, by Tutte’s characterization,
M
is binary if and only if its ground set does not admit a partition into six parts
J=J_{1}\cup\dots\cup J_{6}
such that
J_{i}
contains a single element for
i=1,2,3,4
, and
\text{\rm rk}\big((\cup_{i\in I}J_{i})\cup J_{5}\big)-\text{\rm rk}(J_{5})=\min\{|I|,2\}
for each
I\subseteq[4]
. In other words,
M
is binary if and only if
Q_{6}(\text{\rm rk})
does not contain a setfunction
f\colon[6]\to\mathbb{Z}
with
f(I\cup\{5\})-f(\{5\})=\min\{|I|,2\}
for each
I\subseteq[4]
.

A property of matroids is
minor-closed
, if it is inherited by minors. Every minor-closed property can be defined by excluding a list of minors (quite often, but not always, a finite list). Many fundamental properties of matroids are minor-closed (e.g., linearly representable, graphic, cographic, algebraic). The argument above shows that every minor-closed property described by a finite list of excluded minors can be read off from an appropriate quotient set
Q_{k}(\text{\rm rk})
.

For two setfunctions
\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}
, we say that
\varphi
is a
lift
of
\varphi^{\prime}
if
\varphi^{\prime}
is a quotient of
\varphi
. Let
A
be a family of finite setfunctions. We say that
A
is
quotient-closed
, if every quotient of a function in
A
is also contained in
A
. We say that
A
has the
common lift property
, if any finite set of functions in
A
has a common lift in
A
.

We close this section with a simple lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

The quotient profile of any setfunction
\varphi
is quotient-closed and has the common lift property.

Proof.

Trivially, every quotient of a quotient of
\varphi
is a quotient of
\varphi
. Given a finite set of quotients, every quotient is defined by a corresponding partition, and the quotient defined by the common refinement of these partitions is a common lift.
∎

The proof shows that if
\varphi_{i}\in Q_{k_{i}}(\varphi)
, then
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{m}
have a common lift in
Q_{k_{1}\cdots k_{m}}(\varphi)
.

2.2
Convergence

Let
\varphi_{n}
be a setfunction on a set-algebra
(J_{n},\mathcal{B}_{n})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that the sequence
(\varphi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
is
quotient-convergent
if for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the quotient sets
(Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
form a Cauchy sequence in the Hausdorff distance
d_{{\text{\rm Haus}}_{2^{k}}}
on the
2^{k}
-dimensional Euclidean space. Let
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\text{\rm Haus}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}
denote convergence in this distance. We say that
(\varphi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})

quotient-converges to

\varphi
for some setfunction
\varphi
if the sequence
(Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
converges to
Q_{k}(\varphi)
in the Hausdorff distance, and denote this by
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\varphi
. We can combine these Hausdorff distances
d_{{\text{\rm Haus}}_{2^{k}}}
into a single pseudometric as follows. For two setfunctions
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
, define

\displaystyle d(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}d_{{\text{\rm Haus}}_{2^{k}}}(Q_{k}(\varphi_{1}),Q_{k}(\varphi_{2})).

(1)

Then quotient-convergence of a sequence
\varphi_{n}
means that it is a Cauchy sequence, and
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\varphi
means convergence in this pseudometric. It is easy to see that a quotient-convergent sequence of setfunctions is uniformly bounded.

An important example of quotient-convergent sequences of setfunctions is the following. A
tower
is a sequence
(\varphi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
of setfunctions on finite set-algebras
(J_{n},2^{J_{n}})
, such that
\varphi_{n}
is a quotient of
\varphi_{n+1}
for every
n
. We assume for convenience that
J_{n}=[v_{n}]
for some
v_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
, and that
\varphi_{n}(\emptyset)=0
1
1

1

We could also allow
J_{n}
to be a Polish space, and the setfunction
\varphi_{n}
to be defined on the Borel subsets. Then, with a few additional assumptions, many results in the following section could be extended, but the treatment would become too technical and not to the point of this paper.
. Trivially,
\varphi_{n}(J_{n})=\varphi_{1}(J_{1})
for every
n\geq 1
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Every tower is quotient-convergent.

Proof.

Let
(\varphi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
be a tower. We may assume that
0\leq\varphi_{n}\leq 1
. By Lemma
2.4
, we have

\overline{Q}_{k}(\varphi_{n})\subseteq\overline{Q}_{k}(\overline{Q}_{v_{n}}(\varphi_{n+1}))=\overline{Q}_{k}(\varphi_{n+1}).

(2)

So for every
k
, the sequence
(\overline{Q}_{k}(\varphi_{n})\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
is increasing, which implies trivially that it is convergent in the Hausdorff distance.
∎

The following lemma is a certain converse to Lemma
2.4
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
S_{k}
be a nonempty set of setfunctions on
[k]
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and assume that
S=\cup_{k}S_{k}
is quotient-closed and has the common lift property. Then there are setfunctions
\varphi_{k}\in S_{k}
such that
(\varphi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
is a tower, and
\cup_{n}Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})
is a dense subset of
S_{k}
.

Proof.

For every
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
A_{k}^{n}
be a finite
(1/k)
-net in
S_{k}
, and set
B_{n}=\cup_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}^{n}
. Thus,
B_{n}
is a finite, fine net in the first
n
sets
S_{k}
. Let
\psi_{0}\in S_{0}
, and let
\psi_{n}\in S
be a common lift of
\psi_{n-1}
and all setfunctions in
B_{n}
. Then
(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\dots)
is a tower, and since
S
is quotient-closed,
\cup_{n}Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})\subseteq S_{k}
. Also,
A_{k,n}\in\cup_{n}Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})
, which implies that
\cup_{n}Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})
is dense in
S_{k}
.

The sequence
(\psi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
does not contain an element from every
S_{k}
, but it is easy to “pad” it to a sequence
(\varphi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
as in the lemma. In fact, if
\psi_{n}\in S_{k_{n}}
, then we can define
\varphi_{k_{n}}=\psi_{n}
, and define
\varphi_{k}
for
k=k_{n+1}-1,k_{n+1}-2,\dots,k_{n}+1
recursively by merging two elements of
[k+1]
that are mapped onto one in the quotient map
\psi_{n+1}\to\psi_{n}
.
∎

We continue with more examples.

Example 2.7

(Uniform and sparse paving matroids)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq r\leq n
, we define a
uniform matroid

U_{n,r}
on
[n]
by its rank function
\text{\rm rk}(X)=\min\{|X|,r\}
. Let us normalize to obtain the submodular function
\rho_{n,r}(X)=\text{\rm rk}(X)/n=\min\{|X|/n,r/n\}
. Then
\rho_{n,r}
is the truncation of the uniform probability measure
\pi_{n}
on
[n]
by
r/n
. Hence for every partition
\mathcal{P}
of
[n]
, the submodular setfunction
\rho_{n,r}/\mathcal{P}
is the truncation of
\pi_{n}/\mathcal{P}
by
r/n
. In particular, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
Q_{k}(\rho_{n,r})
can be obtained from
Q_{k}(\pi_{n})
by truncating all its elements by
r/n
. Let
1\leq r_{n}<n
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
where
r_{n}\sim c\cdot n
for some fixed constant
0<c<1
. Let
\lambda
be the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
, and let
\lambda_{c}=\min\{\lambda,c\}
. Then it is not difficult to check that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
Q_{k}(\rho_{n,r_{n}})\to Q_{k}(\lambda_{c})
in Hausdorff distance, and hence
\rho_{n,r_{n}}\rightarrowtail\lambda_{c}
.

A much larger class of matroids can be obtained by the following slight modification of uniform matroids. Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{r}
where
n>r
, and assume that
|A\cap B|\leq r-2
for any two sets
A,B\in\mathcal{H}
. Define

\text{\rm rk}_{\mathcal{H}}(X)=\begin{cases}|X|&\text{if $|X|\leq r-1$},\\
r-1&\text{if $X\in\mathcal{H}$},\\
r&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then
M_{\mathcal{H}}=([n],\text{\rm rk})
is a matroid of rank
r
, called a
sparse paving matroid
defined by
\mathcal{H}
. Specifically, choosing
\mathcal{H}=\emptyset
gives the uniform matroid
U_{n,r}
. Let us normalize to obtain the submodular function

\rho_{\mathcal{H}}(X)=\text{\rm rk}_{\mathcal{H}}(X)/n
. Then

\rho_{n,r}(X)-\frac{1}{n}\leq\rho_{\mathcal{H}}(X)\leq\rho_{n,r}(X).

This implies that if
r_{n}\sim c\cdot n
and
\mathcal{H}_{n}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{r_{n}}
as above, then
d_{{\text{\rm Haus}}_{2^{k}}}\big(Q_{k}(\rho_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}),Q_{k}(\rho_{n,r_{n}})\big)\to 0
,
and hence
\rho_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}\rightarrowtail\lambda_{c}
.

(Dense convergence and graphons)
.

Let
G_{n}=(V_{n},E_{n})

3
Construction of the limit object

This section is dedicated to the proof that the space of submodular setfunctions (endowed with the pseudometric
1
) is complete. Formally, we prove the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\varphi_{n}\colon n\in N)
be a quotient-convergent sequence of increasing submodular setfunctions over any set-algebras. Then there exists an increasing submodular setfunction
\varphi\colon\mathcal{B}(C)\to\mathbb{R}
defined on the Borel sets of the Cantor set such that
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\varphi
.

The proof will consist of two parts. In Section
3.1
, we describe how to construct a submodular setfunction on the algebra of clopen subsets of the Cantor set. Then the function is extended to all Borel subsets using a classical result of Choquet (Section
3.2
). Theorem
3.1
will follow easily by combining these results.

3.1
Limit object on the algebra of clopen subsets

In this section, we construct a submodular setfunction on the clopen subsets of the Cantor set, which is a limit object for a quotient-convergent sequence of submodular setfunctions.

Lemma 3.2
.

For every tower
(\varphi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
of finite setfunctions, there is a setfunction
\varphi
on the algebra
\mathcal{H}
of clopen sets of the Cantor set and a refining sequence of partitions
(\mathcal{P}_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
of
(C,\mathcal{H})
, such that
\varphi_{n}=\varphi/\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Furthermore,
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\varphi
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi_{n}
be defined on the finite Boolean algebra
(J_{n},2^{J_{n}})
. By the definition of quotients and of towers, there are maps
f_{n}\colon J_{n+1}\to J_{n}
such that
\varphi_{n}=\varphi_{n+1}\circ f_{n}^{-1}
.

Consider the inverse limit of the system of setfunctions
\varphi_{n}
and maps
f_{n}
. Explicitly, we consider the set
X
of all sequences
(x_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
such that
x_{n}\in J_{n}
and
x_{n}=f_{n}(x_{n+1})
. We define the map
\Theta_{n}\colon J\to J_{n}
as the projection onto the
n^{\text{th}}
coordinate:

\Theta_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)=x_{n}
. Then
\Theta_{n}=f_{n}\circ\Theta_{n+1}
. This set can be endowed with a metric, in which the distance of
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
and
(y_{1},y_{2},\dots)
is
2^{-r}
, where
r
is the length of their longest common prefix. This topology of
X
is totally disconnected, metrizable and compact.

For every
n
, the sets
\{\Theta_{n}^{-1}(u)\mid u\in J_{n}\}
form a finite partition
\mathcal{P}_{n}
of
J
, and they generate a (finite) set-algebra
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. Clearly,
(\mathcal{P}_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
is a refining sequence of partitions into sets generating
\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{A}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{n+1}
, and
\mathcal{H}=\cup_{n}\mathcal{H}_{n}
is the set-algebra
\mathcal{H}
of clopen subsets of
X
. We define
\varphi(X)=\varphi_{n}(\Theta_{n}(X))
for
X\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
. It is easy to see that this value is independent of
n
, as soon as
X\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
. Trivially,
\varphi
is a setfunction on
\mathcal{H}
, and
\varphi_{n}=\varphi\circ\Theta_{n}^{-1}
. So
\varphi_{n}
is a quotient of
\varphi
. In terms of the partitions, this means that
\varphi/\mathcal{P}_{n}
is isomorphic to
\varphi_{n}
.

To prove the last assertion, notice that
Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})\subseteq Q_{k}(\varphi)
. It suffices to show that
\cup_{k}Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})=Q_{k}(\varphi)
. Indeed, if
\alpha\in Q_{k}(\varphi)
, then
\alpha=\varphi/\mathcal{P}
for a finite
k
-partition into sets in
\mathcal{H}
. These partition classes belong to
\mathcal{A}_{n}
for a sufficiently large
n
. This implies that the sets
\{\Theta_{n}(U)\mid U\in\mathcal{P}\}
are disjoint, and form a partition
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
of
[n]
. Then
\varphi/\mathcal{P}=\varphi_{n}/\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, showing that
\alpha\in Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})
.

Right now, we have constructed a limit object on some totally disconnected compact metric space
X
. To obtain a representation on the Cantor set, we use the well-known fact that
X
can be embedded homomorphically as a closed subset
Y
of the Cantor set
C
. Defining
\varphi^{\prime}(U)=\varphi(Y\cap U)
, we get a representation on the clopen subsets of the Cantor set.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

For every quotient-convergent sequence
(\varphi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
of finite setfunctions there is a setfunction
\varphi
on the algebra
\mathcal{H}
of clopen sets of the Cantor set such that
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\varphi
.

Proof.

As remarked, a quotient-convergent sequence is uniformly bounded, and so we may assume that
-1\leq\varphi_{n}\leq 1
. By definition, the sets
Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})

(n\in\mathbb{N})
form a Cauchy sequence in the Hausdorff distance for every
k
. Since the space of compact subsets of compact set is compact in this pseudometric, there are compact sets
S_{k}
such that
\overline{Q}_{k}(\varphi_{n})\to S_{k}
. From the fact that every
Q=\cup_{k}Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})
is quotient-closed, it follows that
S=\cup_{k}S_{k}
is quotient closed. From the fact that
Q
has the common lift property, along with the remark after Lemma
2.4
, it follows that
S
has the common lift property. Hence, by Lemma
2.6
, there are setfunctions
\psi_{k}\in S_{k}
such that
(\psi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
is a tower, and
\cup_{n}\overline{Q}_{k}(\psi_{n})=S_{k}
, which is equivalent to saying that
\overline{Q}_{k}(\psi_{n})\to S_{k}
. By Lemma
3.2
, there is a setfunction
\varphi
on the algebra
\mathcal{H}
such that
\psi_{n}\rightarrowtail\varphi
, or
\overline{Q}_{k}(\psi_{n})\to\overline{Q}_{k}(\varphi)
, which means that
\overline{Q}_{k}(\varphi)=S_{k}
. Then
\overline{Q}_{k}(\varphi_{n})\to\overline{Q}_{k}(\varphi)
, which means that
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\varphi
.
∎

Let us point out that a very similar argument gives that if the setfunctions
\varphi_{n}
are increasing and/or submodular, then so is their limit function on the clopen subsets of the Cantor set.

3.2
Extension to all Borel sets

In this section we extend the limit setfunction constructed in the previous section from the clopen subsets to all Borel subsets of the Canter set, so that the closed quotient profile is preserved. From now on, we need the condition that the setfunction is increasing and submodular, but these properties will also be preserved. We use two results of Choquet
[
7
]
; see also
[
10
, Chapter 30]
for a more recent introduction. A setfunction on the compact subsets of a topological space is called an
alternating capacity of order
2
if it is increasing, submodular, and continuous from the right. One of the main results in
[
7
]
is the following theorem.

Theorem 3.4

(Choquet)
.

Let
\varphi_{\mathcal{K}}
be an alternating capacity of order
2
on the compact subsets of a Polish space. For any subset
S
, let

\varphi_{*}(S)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\subseteq S\\
K\text{ compact}\end{subarray}}\varphi_{\mathcal{K}}(K)

be the inner capacity, and let

\varphi^{*}(S)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq U\\
U\text{ open}\end{subarray}}\varphi_{*}(U)

be the outer capacity.
Then the setfunction
\varphi^{*}
is increasing and submodular. Furthermore, if
B
is Borel (or analytic) set, then
\varphi_{*}(B)=\varphi^{*}(B)
.

We also need a simple but very useful technical lemma from
[
7
]
.

Lemma 3.5

(Choquet)
.

Let
\phi
be an increasing submodular set function on a lattice family

(J,\mathcal{F})
. For
i\in[n]
, let
A_{i}\subseteq B_{i}
be pairs of sets in
\mathcal{F}
. Then

\phi(\cup_{i=1}^{n}B_{i})-\phi(\cup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\phi(A_{i})-\phi(B_{i})).

Turning to the extension from the clopen sets to Borel sets, this will be carried out in two steps: first, we extend the setfunction from the family
\mathcal{H}
of clopen subsets to the family
\mathcal{K}
of compact subsets, then to the family
\mathcal{B}
of Borel sets. We will denote these setfunctions by
\psi_{\mathcal{H}}
,
\psi_{\mathcal{K}}
and
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}
. We start with
\psi_{\mathcal{H}}=\varphi
, where
\varphi
is the function provided by Corollary
3.3
.

First, we extend
\varphi
to all compact sets by setting

\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(K)\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\subseteq H\\
H\in\mathcal{H}\end{subarray}}\psi_{\mathcal{H}}(H).

(3)

Since
\psi_{\mathcal{H}}
is increasing,
\psi_{\mathcal{K}}
is indeed an extension of
\psi_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

The function
\psi_{\mathcal{K}}
is increasing, submodular and continuous from the right.

Proof.

If
K\subseteq K^{\prime}
, then we have
\{H\in\mathcal{H}|\ K^{\prime}\subseteq H\}\subseteq\{H\in\mathcal{H}|\ K\subseteq H\}
, so the infimum in (
3
) cannot be larger for
K
than for
K^{\prime}
. This proves that
\psi
is increasing.

Let
K_{1},K_{2}
be compacts sets,
\varepsilon>0
, and
H_{1},H_{2}
be clopen sets such that
K_{1}\subseteq H_{1}
,
K_{2}\subseteq H_{2}
and
\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(H_{1})-\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(K_{1})<\varepsilon
,
\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(H_{2})-\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(K_{2})<\varepsilon
. Then

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(K_{1})+\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(K_{2}){}

\displaystyle{}>\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(H_{1})+\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(H_{2})-2\varepsilon

\displaystyle{}\geq\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(H_{1}\cap H_{2})+\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(H_{1}\cup H_{2})-2\varepsilon

\displaystyle{}\geq\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(K_{1}\cap K_{2})+\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(K_{1}\cup K_{2})-2\varepsilon,

where the second inequality holds by submodulartiy of
\psi_{\mathcal{H}}
on the clopen sets, while the third inequality holds by
K_{1}\cap K_{2}\subseteq H_{1}\cap H_{2}
and
K_{1}\cup K_{2}\subseteq H_{1}\cup H_{2}
. Since the inequality is true for any
\varepsilon>0
, taking
\varepsilon\to 0
proves the submodularity of
\psi
.

Finally, let
\varepsilon>0
and
K\in\mathcal{K}
. Then, by the definition of the extension, there exists not only an open but even a clopen set
U
with
\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(K^{\prime})<\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(K)+\varepsilon
for all
K^{\prime}\subseteq U
,
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
. This proves continuity from the right.
∎

We use Theorem
3.4
to extend our function from
\mathcal{K}
to
\mathcal{B}
. By Lemma
3.6
,
\psi_{\mathcal{K}}
is an alternating capacity of order
2
, so we can apply Choquet’s theorem to extend the function to
\mathcal{B}
by defining
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(B)\coloneqq\psi_{*}(B)=\psi^{*}(B)
.

Remark 3.7
.

There is another way to extend the function to all Borel sets. First, let us define
\overline{\psi}(S)\coloneqq\inf_{S\subseteq H\in\mathcal{H}}\psi_{\mathcal{H}}(H)
. Note that the value only depends on the closure of the set, so we can also express it as
\overline{\psi}(S)=\psi_{\mathcal{K}}(\overline{S})
. Second, we make this function continuous from below by setting
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(S)\coloneqq\overline{\psi}_{\bullet}(S)=\inf_{\mathcal{S}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\overline{\psi}(S_{n})
, where
\mathcal{S}=\{(S_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})\mid|\ S_{n}\text{ is ascending},\ \cup_{n}S_{n}=S\}
. Note that this approach is similar to the definition of the Lebesgue measure, where first one defines the Jordan measure for clopen subsets, then extend it to all subsets as the Jordan outer measure, and then makes it continuous from below to obtain the Lebesgue outer measure. One can prove that the function thus obtained is the same as the construction by Choquet, see
[
13
, Chapter 6]
for further details.

Since
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}
extends
\psi_{\mathcal{H}}
, is clear that
Q_{k}(\psi_{\mathcal{H}})\subseteq Q_{k}(\psi_{\mathcal{B}})
. To prove that their closures are equal, we show that each Borel partition can be approximated by a clopen partition.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
K_{1},\dots,K_{q}
be pairwise disjoint compact subsets and
U_{1},\dots,U_{q}
, open subsets of the Cantor set
C
such that
K_{i}\subseteq U_{i}
and
\cup_{i=1}^{q}U_{i}=C
. Then for every
i\in[q]
there exist clopen sets
H_{i}
such that
K_{i}\subseteq H_{i}\subseteq U_{i}
and
\{H_{1},\dots,H_{q}\}
forms a partition of
C
.

Proof.

For all
x\in C
, let us choose a clopen subset
U_{x}
containing
x
as follows. If
x\in K_{i}
, then
U_{x}\subseteq U_{i}
and
U_{x}\cap K_{j}=\emptyset
for all
j\not=i
. If
x\in(\cup_{i=1}^{q}K_{i})^{c}
, then
U_{x}\cap(\cup_{i=1}^{q}K_{i})=\emptyset
and there exists an index
i\in[q]
with
U_{x}\subseteq U_{i}
. As the clopen subsets form a basis for the topology, we can find a
U_{x}
with the given property for all
x\in C
. Since
C\subseteq\cup_{x\in C}U_{x}
and
C
is compact, there exist a finite list of elements
x_{1},...,x_{n}
such that
C\subseteq\cup_{i=1}^{n}U_{x_{i}}.

Let
V_{1},V_{2},...,V_{\ell}
be the atoms in the set-algebra generated by the clopen sets
U_{x_{1}},U_{x_{2}},\dots,U_{x_{n}}
. Consider an arbitrary index
j\in[\ell]
. If
V_{j}
intersects
K_{i}
, then it cannot intersect
K_{k}
for
k\neq i
. Indeed,
V_{j}\subseteq U_{x_{t}}
for some
t\in[n]
, and
U_{x_{t}}
intersects at most one of the compact sets
K_{1},\dots,K_{q}
. Furthermore, we have
V_{j}\subseteq U_{x_{t}}\subseteq U_{i}
. In this case, we add
V_{j}
to the set
H_{i}
. If
V_{j}
does not intersect any of the compact sets
K_{i}
, then it is still covered by
U_{x_{\ell}}
for some
\ell\in[n]
, and so it is covered by
U_{i}
for some
i\in[q]
. In this case, we choose such an index
i
and add
V_{j}
to the set
H_{i}
.

By construction, we clearly have
H_{i}\subseteq U_{i}
and also
K_{i}\subseteq L^{n}_{i}\subseteq H_{i}
, where
L^{n}_{i}
consists of the
n^{\text{th}}
level clopen sets which intersects
K_{i}
.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
F\colon C\to[q]
be a Borel measurable function. Then for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a continuous map
G\colon C\to[q]
such that
d(\psi_{\mathcal{B}}\circ F^{-1},\psi_{\mathcal{B}}\circ G^{-1})<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Let
B_{i}=F^{-1}(\{i\})
and let
\delta>0
. Then, by Theorem
3.4
, there exists
K_{i}\subseteq B_{i}\subseteq U_{i}
such that
K_{i}
is compact,
U_{i}
is open,
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(B_{i})-\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(K_{i})<\delta
and
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(U_{i})-\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(B_{i})<\delta
. Clearly, the sets
K_{1},\dots,K_{q}
are pairwise disjoint while
\cup_{i=1}^{q}U_{i}
covers the whole space. By Lemma
3.8
, there exists a partition
C=H_{1}\cup\dots\cup H_{q}
into clopen sets such that
K_{i}\subseteq H_{i}\subseteq U_{i}
. Let
G
be defined as
G(i)\coloneqq\{j\mid i\in H_{j}\}
.

For any set
A\subseteq[q]
, let
B_{A}=\cup_{i\in A}B_{i}
,
K_{A}=\cup_{i\in A}K_{i}
,
H_{A}=\cup_{i\in A}H_{i}
and
U_{A}=\cup_{i\in A}U_{i}
. Using Lemma
3.5
, we have
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(B_{A})-\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(K_{A})<\delta|A|
and
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(U_{A})-\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(B_{A})<\delta|A|
, implying
|\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(B_{A})-\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(H_{A})|<\delta|A|.
Therefore,
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}\circ F^{-1}
and
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}\circ G^{-1}
can differ by at most
\delta q
in each coordinate. By choosing
\delta
small enough, the statements follows.
∎

We get the following corollary.

Corollary 3.10
.

The setfunctions
\psi_{\mathcal{H}}
and
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}
have the same quotient profile. Therefore, if
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\psi_{H}
then
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\psi_{\mathcal{B}}
.

Proof.

Clearly,
Q_{k}(\psi_{\mathcal{H}})\subseteq Q_{k}(\psi_{\mathcal{B}})
. By Lemma
3.9
, it follows that
Q_{k}(\psi_{\mathcal{B}})\subseteq\overline{Q_{k}(\psi_{\mathcal{H}})}
, thus
\overline{Q_{k}(\psi_{\mathcal{H}})}=\overline{Q_{k}(\psi_{\mathcal{B}})}
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
3.1
follows by Corollaries
3.3
and
3.10
.

Corollary 3.11
.

The space of increasing normalized submodular setfunctions on Borel spaces, endowed with the pseudometric (
1
), is compact.

Proof.

As the metric comes from the embedding into the compact metric space
\prod_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{K}([0,1]^{2^{k}})
, where
\mathcal{K}(A)
is the compact subsets of
A
endowed with the Hausdorff metric, the space of increasing normalized submodular setfunctions on Borel spaces is totally bounded. Theorem
3.1
states that this space is complete. From these observations, it follows that the space in question is compact.
∎

4
Finite approximations

In this section we answer the
soficity question
, which is the natural question to characterize the limit objects of convergent sequences. In our case the answer is easy: Every bounded setfunction defined on a set-algebra is a limit.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\varphi
be a bounded setfunction defined on a set-algebra. Then there exists a sequence of finite quotients
\varphi_{n}
of
\varphi
such that
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\varphi
.

It is easy to see that we may assume that
\varphi_{n}
is defined on the subsets of
[n]
.

Proof.

The construction will be similar to part of the proof of Theorem
3.1
. For every
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
A_{k}^{n}
be a finite
(1/n)
-net in
Q_{k}(\varphi)
, and set
B_{n}=\cup_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}^{n}
. So
B_{n}
intersects each of the first
n
quotient sets in a finite
(1/n)
-net. Let
\varphi_{n}
be a common lift of the setfunctions in
B_{n}
. We claim that
\varphi_{n}
quotient-converges to
\varphi
as
n
tends to infinity. The functions
\varphi_{n}
are finite and are quotients of
\varphi
, so
Q_{\ell}(\varphi_{n})\subseteq Q_{\ell}(\varphi)
. For all
\ell\leq n
,
Q_{\ell}(\varphi_{n})
contains a
(1/n)
-net of
Q_{\ell}(\varphi)
, thus
d_{{\text{\rm Haus}}_{2^{\ell}}}(Q_{\ell}(\varphi_{n}),Q_{\ell}(\varphi))\leq 1/n
. Therefore,
Q_{\ell}(\varphi_{n})\to Q_{\ell}(\varphi)
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, implying
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\varphi
.
∎

Under further restrictions on
\varphi
, we can give an explicit construction for a sequence of finite setfunctions quotient-converging to
\varphi
. We need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\varphi
be an increasing submodular setfunction on a sigma-algebra
(J,\mathcal{B})
, continuous from above, with
\varphi(\emptyset)=0
. Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
be a set-algebra generating
\mathcal{B}
. Then, for every
X\in\mathcal{B}
and every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
Y\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\varphi(X\triangle Y)<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{X\in\mathcal{B}\mid\text{for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $Y\in\mathcal{A}$ with $\varphi(X\triangle Y)<\varepsilon$}\}
. We claim that
\mathcal{S}
is a sigma-algebra. We clearly have
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, hence
\emptyset\in\mathcal{S}
. Since for the symmetric difference we have
(J\setminus X)\triangle(J\setminus Y)=X\triangle Y
,
\mathcal{S}
is closed under complementation. For
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exist sets
Y_{1},Y_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\varphi(X_{i}\triangle Y_{i})<\varepsilon/2
. As
(X_{1}\cup X_{2})\triangle(Y_{1}\cup Y_{2})\subseteq(X_{1}\triangle Y_{1})\cup(X_{2}\triangle Y_{2})
, the monotonicity and submodularity of
\varphi
gives

\varphi\bigl((X_{1}\cup X_{2})\triangle(Y_{1}\cup Y_{2})\bigr)\leq\varphi(X_{1}\triangle Y_{1})+\varphi(X_{2}\triangle Y_{2})\leq\varepsilon.

Since
Y_{1}\cup Y_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
, this implies that
X_{1}\cup X_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
. Thus, by
\mathcal{S}
being closed under complementation, we get that
X_{1}\cap X_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
.

To show that
\mathcal{S}
is also closed under countable union, let
\varepsilon>0
,
X_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
, and
X=\cup_{i}X_{i}
. For
i\in\mathbb{N}
, set
Z_{i}=X_{1}\cup\ldots\cup X_{i}
. Then
Z_{i}\subseteq Z_{i+1}
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
\cup_{i}Z_{i}=X
, hence the continuity of
\varphi
from above implies
\varphi(X\setminus Z_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Thus we have
\varphi(X\setminus Z_{n})<\varepsilon/2
if
n
is large enough. Note that
Z_{n}\in\mathcal{S}
since
\mathcal{S}
is closed under finite unions, so there is a set
Y\in\mathcal{A}
with
\varphi(Y\triangle Z_{n})<\varepsilon/2
. By the submodularity of
\varphi
, these together lead to
\varphi(X\triangle Y)<\varepsilon
, implying
X\in\mathcal{S}
. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Now we are ready to state the theorem.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\varphi
be a bounded, increasing, submodular setfunction on a sigma-algebra
(J,\mathcal{B})
, continuous from above, with
\varphi(\emptyset)=0
. Let
(\mathcal{P}_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N})
be a refining sequence of measurable partitions of
J
into a finite number of sets in
\mathcal{B}
such that
\cup_{n}\mathcal{P}_{n}
generates
\mathcal{B}
. Then
\varphi/{\mathcal{P}_{n}}\rightarrowtail\varphi
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{n}
denote the finite set-algebra generated by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Since the partitions form a refining sequence,
\mathcal{A}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{n+1}
clearly holds. If we set
\mathcal{A}=\cup_{n}\mathcal{A}_{n}
, then
\mathcal{A}
is a countable set-algebra that generates
\mathcal{B}
. Let
\varphi_{n}=\varphi/\mathcal{P}_{n}
. We claim that
\varphi_{n}\rightarrowtail\varphi
.

We start with the observation that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})\subseteq Q_{k}(\varphi).

(4)

This follows immediately from the fact that
\varphi_{n}
is a quotient of
\varphi
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{k})
. By Lemma
4.2
, there are sets
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\varphi(X_{i}\triangle Z_{i})<\varepsilon/(2k^{3})
. Our goal is to modify the sets
X_{i}
“a little” to get a partition. Recall that
\varphi
is assumed to be increasing, submodular with
\varphi(\emptyset)=0
; we will use these properties without explicitly referring to them. Note that

J\setminus(\cup_{i=1}^{k}X_{i})\subseteq\cup_{i=1}^{k}(Z_{i}\setminus X_{i})
, and hence

\varphi\left(J\setminus(\cup_{i=1}^{k}X_{i})\right)\leq\varphi\left(\cup_{i=1}^{k}(Z_{i}\setminus X_{i})\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi(Z_{i}\setminus X_{i})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2k^{2}}.

So, by replacing
X_{k}
with
X_{k}\cup\big(J\setminus(\cup_{i=1}^{k}X_{i})\big)
, we may assume that
\cup_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}=J
, at the cost of increasing the error bound from
\varepsilon/(2k^{3})
to
\varepsilon/(2k^{2})
. Define
Y_{1}=X_{1}
, and for
1<i\leq k
, let
Y_{i}=X_{i}\setminus(Y_{1}\cup\dots\cup Y_{i-1})
. The sets
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}
are clearly disjoint and form a partition
\mathcal{Y}
of
J
into sets in
\mathcal{A}
. By the definition of
\mathcal{A}
, this means that
\mathcal{Y}
is a partition of
J
into sets in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
for every sufficiently large
n
, and so
\varphi/\mathcal{Y}\in Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})
for sufficiently large
k
and
n\geq k
. By the definition of
Y_{i}
, we have

\varphi(Y_{i}\triangle X_{i})=\varphi(X_{i}\setminus Y_{i})=\varphi\bigl(X_{i}\cap(X_{1}\cup\dots\cup X_{i-1})\bigr)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\varphi(X_{i}\cap X_{j}).

Since
Z_{i}\cap Z_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
, we have
X_{i}\cap X_{j}\subseteq(X_{i}\setminus Z_{i})\cup(X_{j}\setminus Z_{j})
, this can be further bounded as

\varphi(Y_{i}\triangle X_{i})\leq i\cdot\varphi(X_{i}\setminus Z_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\varphi(X_{j}\setminus Z_{j})\leq(2k-1)\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2k^{2}}.

This implies

\varphi(Y_{i}\triangle Z_{i})\leq\varphi(Y_{i}\triangle X_{i})+\varphi(X_{i}\triangle Z_{i})\leq(2k-1)\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2k^{2}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2k^{2}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{k}.

Therefore, for every
I\subseteq[k]
, we get

|\varphi(\cup_{i\in I}Z_{i})-\varphi(\cup_{i\in I}Y_{i})|\leq\sum_{i\in I}\varphi(Y_{i}\triangle Z_{i})\leq\varepsilon.

In other words, the quotients
\varphi/\mathcal{Z}
and
\varphi/\mathcal{Y}
satisfy
\|\varphi/\mathcal{Z}-\varphi/\mathcal{Y}\|\leq\varepsilon
. Since
\varphi/\mathcal{Z}
is an arbitrary quotient in
Q_{k}(\varphi)
while
\varphi/\mathcal{Y}
is in
Q_{k}(\varphi_{n})
, together with (
4
) we obtain that

d_{{\text{\rm Haus}}_{2^{k}}}(Q_{k}(\varphi),Q_{k}(\varphi_{n}))\leq\varepsilon.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
