Hodge and Frobenius colevels of algebraic varieties
Daqing Wan, Dingxin Zhang

Abstract
We provide new, improved lower bounds for the Hodge and Frobenius colevels of algebraic varieties (over 𝐂 \mathbf{C} or over a finite field) in all cohomological degrees. These bounds are expressed in terms of the dimension of the variety and multi-degrees of its defining equations. Our results lead to an enhanced positive answer to a question raised by Esnault and the first author. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. Primary: 14F20 ; Secondary: 11M38 , 14G15 , 32S35 .

Background and motivation

These notes present our investigation into the cohomological colevels
of algebraic varieties in both the Betti and
\ell
-adic contexts,
motivated by our previous
p
-adic findings
[
WZ25
]
. We provide
new, improved lower bounds for the Hodge and Frobenius colevels of
algebraic varieties (over
\mathbf{C}
or over the algebraic closure
of a finite field) across all cohomological degrees.

These bounds are formulated in terms of the dimension and the
multi-degrees of the defining equations of the variety. For a
detailed explanation, refer to Theorems
2.2
,

2.3
, and
2.5
. These
theorems offer an enhanced affirmative response to a question
(Question
1.11
) posed by Esnault and the first author in

[
EW22
]
.

Before examining the specific results presented in Section

2
, this introductory section will review some
background material, and illustrate the various aspects of the
problems we are about to address.

1.1
Hodge colevel and Frobenius colevel

Let us recall the definitions of
cohomological colevel
in two
different contexts.

Definition 1.1

(Frobenius colevel)
.

Let
k
be an algebraic closure of the prime field

\mathbf{F}_{p}
, and let
\ell\neq p
be another prime number.
Let
X
be a finite-type, separated scheme over
k
. Assume

\mathbf{F}_{q}
is a finite subfield of
k
such that
X
is
defined over
\mathbf{F}_{q}
, i.e.,

X=X_{0}\otimes_{\mathbf{F}_{q}}k
for some

\mathbf{F}_{q}
-scheme
X_{0}
. Let
F_{q}\colon X\to X

denote the geometric Frobenius (over
\mathbf{F}_{q}
). The
operator
F_{q}
acts on the
\ell
-adic cohomology

\mathrm{H}_{c}^{i}(X;\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})
by transporting the
structures. We say that
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X;\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})
has

(Frobenius) colevel

\geqslant m
if all Frobenius eigenvalues are
divisible by
q^{m}
in the ring of algebraic integers.

Note that the condition of having a Frobenius colevel
\geqslant m
is
geometric in nature: if
X
is defined over

\mathbf{F}_{q^{\prime}}
, another finite field of characteristic

p
, then the eigenvalues of
F_{q}
acting on

\mathrm{H}^{i}(X;\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})
are divisible by
q^{m}
if
and only if the eigenvalues of
F_{q^{\prime}}
acting on

\mathrm{H}^{i}(X;\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})
are divisible by

q^{\prime m}
.

Definition 1.2

(Hodge colevel)
.

Assume
k=\mathbf{C}
. Let
X
be a finite-type, separated scheme
over
\mathbf{C}
, with associated analytic space
X^{\text{an}}
.
The compactly supported cohomology

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{Q})
is endowed with a
canonical mixed Hodge structure. We say that

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{Q})
has
(Hodge)
colevel

\geqslant m
if the Hodge filtration

F^{\bullet}\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{C})
on

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{C})
satisfies the
condition

F^{m}\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{C})=\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{C})
.

These colevel conditions can also be defined for
“abstract” mixed Hodge structures or Frobenius modules. Let

r\in\mathbf{Z}
and
K
be a field. We say that a filtered

K
-vector space
(V,\mathrm{Fil}^{\bullet}V)
has (Hodge)
colevel
\geqslant r
if
V=\mathrm{Fil}^{r}V
. If
V
is a
finite-dimensional
K[F]
-module, we say that
V
has
(Frobenius) colevel
\geqslant r
if all the eigenvalues of
F
can
be expressed as
q^{r}\cdot\beta
, where
\beta
is some
algebraic integer.
Dually, we say that a filtered vector space

(V,\mathrm{Fil}^{\bullet}V)
has (Hodge) level
\leqslant r
if

\mathrm{Fil}^{r+1}V=0
; we say that a finite-dimensional

K[F]
-module
V
has (Frobenius) level
\leqslant r
if, for any
eigenvalue
\alpha
of
F
,
q^{r}\cdot\alpha^{-1}
is an
algebraic integer.

In the sequel, we will use the notation
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X)
to
represent
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{Q})
or

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X;\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})
when the base field
k
is
either irrelevant to the discussion or clearly understood from the
context.

If
X
is proper over
k
, then the compactly supported cohomology

\mathrm{H}^{\ast}_{c}(X)
is the same as the usual cohomology. In
such cases, we will omit the subscript
c
.

Example 1.3
.

The colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{2i}(\mathbf{P}^{n})
is
\geqslant i
. If

X
is a variety over
k
of dimension
\leqslant n
, then the
colevel of
\mathrm{H}_{c}^{2n}(X)
is
\geqslant n
.

When
k=\mathbf{C}
and
X
is both smooth and projective, the
statement that

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{Q})=\mathrm{H}^{i}(X^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{Q})

has Hodge colevel
\geqslant m
is equivalent to the condition that

\mathrm{H}^{i-a}(X;\Omega^{a}_{X})=0
for
m>a
. Here,

\Omega^{a}_{X}=0
if
a<0
.

1.2
Geometric colevel and cohomological colevel

The cohomological colevel conditions discussed above are connected to
the well-known conjectures about the existence of algebraic cycles in
the following manner. Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over

k
. If there exists a subvariety
Z
of codimension
\geqslant m

such that the map
\mathrm{H}_{Z}^{i}(X)\to\mathrm{H}^{i}(X)
is
surjective—where the left-hand side denotes the cohomology of
X

supported on
Z
—then we say that the
geometric colevel
of

\mathrm{H}^{i}(X)
is
\geqslant m
. For the general definition of the
colevel filtration (
la filtration par le coniveau
), the reader
is referred to
[
Gro68
, §10.1]
or

[
DK73
, Exposé XX, §2]
. It can be shown that if the geometric
colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{i}(X)
is
\geqslant m
, then the cohomological
(i.e., Frobenius or Hodge) colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{i}(X)
is also

\geqslant m
. See
[
Gro68
, p. 167]
.

Example 1.4
.

Let
X
be a proper nonsingular variety over
k
. Let
k(X)

denote the rational function field of
X
, and let

\overline{k(X)}
be an algebraic closure of
k(X)
. Suppose that

\mathrm{CH}_{0}\left(X\times_{k}\operatorname{Spec}\overline{k(X)}\right)\otimes_{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{Q}\simeq\mathbf{Q}

(for example, proper nonsingular rationally connected varieties,
including all Fano manifolds, satisfy this condition). Then

\mathrm{H}^{i}(X)
has geometric colevel
\geqslant 1
for any

i>0
. Consequently, depending on the context, the Frobenius or
Hodge colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{i}(X)
is also at least
1
. For
further information, see
[
Esn03b
]
.

Conversely, if the cohomological colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{i}(X)
is

\geqslant m
, the generalized Hodge conjecture in the Betti case, and
the generalized Tate conjecture in the
\ell
-adic case, state that
the geometric colevel is also at least
m
, see

[
Gro68
, §10]
and
[
Gro69
]
.

Remark 1.5
.

There is a more sophisticated version of the generalized Hodge
conjecture, also proposed by Grothendieck. This version is applicable
to both singular and non-proper varieties and is formulated in terms
of motives. It states that if
M
is an “effective mixed motive”
whose de Rham realization
M_{\mathrm{DR}}
has colevel at least

\mu
(with respect to the Hodge filtration), then
M
can be
expressed as
N\otimes\mathbf{Q}(-\mu)
, where
N
is some
effective mixed motive and
\mathbf{Q}(-\mu)
denotes the Tate
twist.

It is well known that the existence of a subvariety
Z
that
supports the cohomology (or the existence of
N
in the more
sophisticated conjecture) has significant arithmetic consequences.
For instance, when
k=\mathbf{C}
and
X
is smooth and
projective, the generalized Hodge conjecture suggests that a Hodge
colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{i}(X)
being
\geqslant m
implies that a
sufficiently general mod
p
reduction of
X
should also have
Frobenius colevel
\geqslant m
. Let us elaborate on this for readers who
may not be familiar with this circle of ideas.

If
\mathrm{H}^{i}(X)
has Hodge colevel
\geqslant m
, the generalized
Hodge conjecture predicts the existence of a subvariety
Z
of
X

with codimension
\geqslant m
such that the following map is surjective:

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{Z^{\mathrm{an}}}(X^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{Q})\to\mathrm{H}^{i}(X^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{Q}).

By the comparison theorem between étale and singular cohomology, the map of
\ell
-adic cohomology groups

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{Z}(X;\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})\to\mathrm{H}^{i}(X;\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})

(1.6)

is also surjective. Now, let us consider a spread-out of
X
, i.e.,
a smooth projective morphism
\mathcal{X}\to\operatorname{Spec}R
—where

R
is a finitely generated subring of
\mathbf{C}
which is
smooth over
\mathbf{Z}
—such that

\mathcal{X}\otimes_{R}\mathbf{C}=X
. Note that the residue
fields of the maximal ideals of
R
are finite fields.

Upon replacing
\operatorname{Spec}R
by a smaller nonempty open
affine, we may assume the Zariski closure
\mathcal{Z}
of
Z
is
flat over
R
. For any closed point
b
of

\operatorname{Spec}R
, we form the following cartesian diagram

Remark 1.7

One cannot expect the Hodge colevel of

.

1.3
Common lower bounds for cohomological colevels

More generally, for
any
(without smooth proper condition) flat
morphism
\mathcal{X}\to\operatorname{Spec}R
, with

R\subset\mathbf{C}
finitely generated over
\mathbf{Z}
, and
for
b
being “sufficiently general”, the Hodge colevels of
X

are not larger than the Frobenius colevels of
X_{b}
2
2

2

This
can be seen, for instance, by spreading out a proper hypercovering
of the generic fiber of
\mathcal{X}\to\operatorname{Spec}R
,
whose entries are normal crossing complements of smooth projective
varieties.
However, one cannot expect this to hold for every
b
, even when
the generic fiber of
\mathcal{X}\to\operatorname{Spec}R
is
smooth and projective. For a counterexample, see

[
EX09
, Theorem 1.2]
.
.

This conceptual explanation, appealing as it is, is not very
practical. For instance, it cannot even be used to deduce the
classical theorem of Ax and
Katz
[
Ax64
,
Kat71
]
(on Frobenius
colevels of primitive cohomology of a
possibly singular

projective variety) from a Hodge-theoretic result
(cf.
[
ENS92
]
): we could indeed lift the defining
equations, but we have no idea whether the prime we start with is
“sufficiently general”.

Therefore, it is desirable to establish
common
lower bounds for
the Hodge colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X)
and the
Frobenius colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X_{b})
,
regardless of whether
b
is general or not, and irrespective of how
singular the specialization
X_{b}
may be. Ideally, this should be
done in a purely formal manner, so that the argument is applicable in
both the Frobenius and Hodge contexts. Such lower bounds have been
extensively studied in the literature, with a notable example being
the following integrality theorem of Deligne.

Theorem 1.8

(Deligne)
.

Let
k
be either a finite field, or
\mathbf{C}
.
Let
X
be a finite type, separated scheme over
k
of dimension

n
. Then for any integer
j\in\mathbf{Z}
,
\mathrm{H}^{n+j}_{c}(X)

has colevel
\geqslant\max\{0,j\}
.
3
3

3

The finite field case was proved by Deligne in

[
DK73
, Exposé XXI, Corollarie 5.5.3]
.
The theorem for Hodge colevels (which is not needed in this paper) can be shown as follows.
When
X
is nonsingular, the theorem follows from Poincaré duality and
Deligne’s
definition
of Hodge filtration in terms of logarithmic forms;
if
X
is possibly singular, one proceeds by induction on dimension,
and use the long exact sequence

\cdots\to\mathrm{H}^{n+j}_{c}(X\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}X_{\mathrm{sing}})\to\mathrm{H}^{n+j}_{c}(X)\to\mathrm{H}^{n+j}_{c}(X_{\mathrm{sing}})\to\cdots,

where
X_{\mathrm{sing}}
is the singular locus of the reduced structure of

X
, which has lower dimension. Applying the theorem for nonsingular variety
to
X\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}X_{\mathrm{sing}}
, and inductive hypothesis to

X_{\mathrm{sing}}
finishes the proof.

The bounds provided by Deligne’s theorem are quite crude, as they
apply to
any
variety over
k
. To improve upon this, one
could consider the type of polynomial equations that define
X
and
investigate whether the colevels can be bounded from below by
quantities that depend solely on these equations. One set of such
quantities consists of the following numbers. These numbers appear,
for instance, in the classical Ax–Katz theorem

[
Ax64
,
Kat71
]
and in the first author’s
work
[
Wan00
]
on the “polar part” of zeta functions of
algebraic varieties.

Definition 1.9
.

For a sequence of integers
d_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant d_{r}\geqslant 1
and
integers
N,j\geqslant 0
, define

\mu_{j}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})=j+\max\left\{0,\left\lceil\frac{N-j-\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}}{d_{1}}\right\rceil\right\}.

Note that we have

\mu_{j}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})=j+\mu_{0}(N-j;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})\geqslant j
,
and the sequence
\mu_{j}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
is increasing in
j
:

\mu_{0}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})\leqslant\mu_{1}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})\leqslant\mu_{2}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})\leqslant\cdots.

The following theorem summarizes the best known lower bounds in this
regard, which are the result of the contribution from several
mathematicians, including Ax, Katz, Deligne, Dimca, Esnault, Nori,
Srinivas, del Angel, the first author, among others.

(For a closed subvariety
Y
of
\mathbf{P}^{N}
, set

\mathrm{H}^{\ast}(Y)_{\mathrm{prim}}=\operatorname{Coker}(\mathrm{H}^{\ast}(\mathbf{P}^{N})\to\mathrm{H}^{\ast}(Y))
.)

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
k
be either an algebraic closure of a finite field, or the field
\mathbf{C}
of complex numbers.

1.

(Affine Case).
Let
X
be the affine variety in
\mathbf{A}^{N}
of dimension
n

defined by
f_{1}=\cdots=f_{r}=0
, where
f_{i}\in k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
,
has degree
\leqslant d_{i}
.

(a)

The colevels of
\mathrm{H}^{\ast}_{c}(X)
and

\mathrm{H}^{\ast}_{c}(\mathbf{A}^{N}\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}X)
are
at least
\mu_{0}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
, and

(b)

for any

,
the colevels of

\mathrm{H}^{N-1+j}_{c}(X)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{H}^{N+j}_{c}(\mathbf{A}^{N}\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}X)

are at least
\mu_{j}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
.

2.

(Projective Case).

Let
Y
be the projective variety in
\mathbf{P}^{N}
of dimension
n

g_{1}=\cdots=g_{r}=0
, where

g_{i}\in k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{N}]
is homogeneous of degree
\leqslant d_{i}
.

(a)

The colevels of
\mathrm{H}^{\ast}(Y)_{\mathrm{prim}}
and

\mathrm{H}_{c}^{\ast}(\mathbf{P}^{N}\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}Y)
are
at least
\mu_{0}(N+1;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
, and

(b)

for
0\leqslant j\leqslant n

and

\mathrm{H}^{n+1+j}_{c}(\mathbf{P}^{N}\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}{Y})

\mu_{j}(N+1;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
.

1.4
A brief history of Theorem
1.10

Item (
1a
) for Frobenius colevel was inspired by the
classical theorem of Ax and
Katz
[
Ax64
,
Kat71
]
, which asserts that
the reciprocal zeros and poles of the zeta function

of

are divisible by
\mu_{0}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
in the ring
of algebraic integers. But the Ax–Katz theorem does not directly
imply (
1a
), as there may be cancellation of Frobenius
eigenvalues in the zeta function. The form as stated is due to
Esnault and Katz
[
EK05
, Theorems 2.1–2.3 and
§5.3]
, based on a method
developed by
del Angel
[
dAR94
]
. This
lower bound is essentially optimal in general, as the Ax–Katz bound is
sharp.

Item (
2a
) for Hodge colevels was proved by Deligne
in
[
DK73
, Exposé XI]
when
Y
is a smooth complete
intersection, and historically this result motivated Katz’s
improvement of Ax’s theorem. For an arbitrary hypersurface
Y
,
(
2a
) for Hodge colevel was proved by Deligne and
Dimca
[
DD90
]
.
For an arbitrary complete intersection
Y
, (
2a
) for Hodge

[
Esn90
]
.
For an arbitrary projective

, (
2a
) for Hodge colevel was
proved by Esnault–Nori–Srinivas
[
ENS92
]
.

For Frobenius colevels,
Esnault
[

]

proved (
2a
) for complete intersections. The general result
(
2a
) is proved by Esnault and
Katz
[
EK05
, Theorem 4.1(1)]
.
This part of the estimate is essentially sharp in general, again by
Ax–Katz and by Deligne’s result for smooth complete intersections
above.

As hinted by Deligne’s Theorem

, compactly
supported cohomology groups beyond the middle dimension tend to have

) and (

) both

]

Motivated by this, (

[

]
for projective

Y
.
Esnault–Katz
[

]
proved,

\geqslant\mu_{j}(N+1;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
. This is more general but weaker
than (

Improved colevel bounds

To state our refined colevel bounds, let us introduce
some sequences of numbers.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{r}
and
N
be positive integers. Assume
that
d_{1}\geqslant d_{2}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant d_{r}
. For each integer

e\in\mathbf{Z}
, and
i=1,2,\ldots,r

d_{i}^{\ast}(e)=\begin{cases}d_{i},&\text{if }i\leqslant e,\\
1,&\text{if }i>e,\text{ and }d_{i}=d_{1},\\
0,&\text{if }i>e,\text{ and }d_{i}<d_{1}.\end{cases}

So if
e\leqslant 0
,
d_{i}^{\ast}(e)=1
or
0

d_{i}=d_{1}
or
d_{i}<d_{1}
. If

d_{i}^{\ast}(e)=d_{i}
.

Define

\nu_{j}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})=j+\max\left\{0,\left\lceil\frac{N-j-\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}^{\ast}(e)}{d_{1}}\right\rceil\right\}.

Since
d_{i}^{*}(e)
is increasing in
e
, it is clear that
\nu_{j}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
is decreasing in
e
.
This gives the decreasing relation

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle=\nu^{(-1)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})=\nu^{(0)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})

\displaystyle\geqslant\nu_{j}^{(1)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\nu_{j}^{(r)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})=\nu_{j}^{(r+1)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})=\cdots

\displaystyle=\mu_{j}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}).

Our improved colevel bounds beyond middle dimension is the following.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X
be an
n
-dimensional Zariski closed subset of
\mathbf{A}^{N}

defined by polynomials
f_{1}=\cdots=f_{r}=0
.
Let
d_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant d_{r}

be a sequence of positive integers such that
d_{i}\geqslant\deg f_{i}
.
Then the colevels of
\mathrm{H}^{n+j}_{c}(X)
and
\mathrm{H}_{c}^{n+j+1}(\mathbf{A}^{N}\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}X)

are at least

\nu_{j}^{(N-n)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
for all
0\leqslant j\leqslant n
.

Our improved colevel bounds before middle dimension is the following.

Theorem 2.3
.

In the situation of Theorem
2.2
,
for
0\leqslant m\leqslant r-(N-n)
, the colevels of

\mathrm{H}^{N-r+m}_{c}(X)
and
\mathrm{H}_{c}^{N-r+m+1}(\mathbf{A}^{N}\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}X)
are at least

\nu_{0}^{(r-m)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
.

Under the hypothesis of Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
,
we have

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X)=0
for
i<N-r

(this is well-known, an elementary argument is given in
[
WZ25
, Lemma 6.2]
); also by general facts about cohomology,
we have

\mathrm{H}^{n+j}_{c}(X)=0
for
j>n
.
Thus the two theorems cover all possibly nonzero compactly supported cohomology groups of

X
. The integer
r-(N-n)
is always non-negative. It is zero if and only if

X
is a set-theoretic complete
intersection by
r
equations.

The assertions for the complement
\mathbf{A}^{N}\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}X
follow from
the results about
X
by using the long exact sequence

\cdots\to\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(\mathbf{A}^{N}\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}X)\to\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(\mathbf{A}^{N})\to\mathrm{H}_{c}^{i}(X)\to\cdots

and the well-known fact that

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(\mathbf{A}^{N})=\begin{cases}0,&i\neq 2N,\\
\Lambda(-N),&i=2N.\end{cases}

Thus, Theorems
2.2
and
2.3

cover all possibly nonzero compactly supported cohomology of
\mathbf{A}^{N}\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}X

except the top-dimensional (
i=2N
) one, which is trivially of colevel
N
.

Since
\nu_{j}^{(N-n)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})\geqslant\mu_{j}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
,
we see Theorem
2.2
implies, under its hypothesis, that

Corollary 2.4
.

The colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{n+j}_{c}(X)
is at least

\mu_{j}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
.

Thus, Theorem
2.2
gives an affirmative answer to Question
1.11
and hence also
improves Theorem
1.12
. For if
X
is a complete intersection by given
r
equations, namely,
r=N-n
, one has

\nu_{j}^{(N-n)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})=\mu_{j}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
. In this case, Theorem
2.2

is the same as Theorem
1.12
. Thus, strict improvements of Theorem
2.2

over Theorem
1.12
can only occur for non-complete intersections.
See Examples
6.1
and
6.2
.

Similarly, since
\nu_{0}^{(r-m)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})\geqslant\mu_{0}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
, the lower bounds given by Theorem
2.3

are improvements of the Ax–Katz type bound
\mu_{0}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
.

From Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
, we can formally
deduce the following projective version which is an improvement of Theorem
1.10
(
2a
,
2b
).

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
Y
be a Zariski closed subset of
\mathbf{P}^{N}
of dimension
n
,
defined by homogeneous polynomial equations
g_{1}=\cdots=g_{r}=0
.
Assume that
\deg g_{i}\leqslant d_{i}
,
d_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant d_{r}
.
Then

•

for all integers
0\leqslant j\leqslant n
, the colevels of
\mathrm{H}^{n+j}(Y)_{\mathrm{prim}}
and

\mathrm{H}^{n+j+1}_{c}(\mathbf{P}^{N}\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}Y)
are at least
\nu^{(N-n)}_{j}(N+1;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
;

•

for all integers
0\leqslant m\leqslant r-(N-n)
, the colevels of
\mathrm{H}^{N-r+m}(Y)_{\mathrm{prim}}
and

\mathrm{H}^{N-r+m+1}_{c}(\mathbf{P}^{N}\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}Y)
are at least
\nu^{(r-m)}_{0}(N+1;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
.

It is worth remarking that we
do not
give new proofs of
Theorems
1.10
(
1a
,
2a
) (which are
ultimately based on the Ax–Katz theorem, a theorem whose known proofs are all

p
-adic in nature). Rather, these results are the starting point for our proofs of
Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
.

As an arithmetic application, we give the following corollary which improves the “polar” part of
the Ax–Katz theorem for the zeta function of an arbitrary affine variety, extending the complete intersection case treated in
[
Wan00
]
.
This result can also be derived from the corresponding Frobenius colevel bounds in rigid cohomology
[
WZ25
]
.

Corollary 2.6
.

In the situation of Theorem
2.2
, assume now that
k
is a finite field of
q
elements.

1.

If
r=N-n
(complete intersection), then all reciprocal poles of the zeta function
Z_{X}(t)^{(-1)^{N-r+1}}

are divisible, as algebraic integers, by
q^{\mu_{1}(N;d_{1},\cdots,d_{r})}
.

2.

If
r>N-n
(non–complete intersection), then all reciprocal poles of the zeta function
Z_{X}(t)^{(-1)^{N-r+1}}

are divisible, as algebraic integers, by
q^{\nu_{0}^{(r-1)}(N;d_{1},\cdots,d_{r})}
.

The following sequence of colevel bounds is increasing:

\nu_{0}^{(r)}(N;d_{1},\cdots,d_{r})\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\nu_{0}^{(N-n)}(N;d_{1},\cdots,d_{r})\leqslant\nu_{1}^{(N-n)}(N;d_{1},\cdots,d_{r})\leqslant\cdots.

By the trace formula, the zeta function
Z_{X}(t)^{(-1)^{N-r+1}}
is given by the product

\prod_{m=0}^{r-(N-n)}\det(I-t\cdot F\mid\mathrm{H}_{c}^{N-r+m}(X))^{(-1)^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\det(I-t\cdot F\mid\mathrm{H}_{c}^{n+j}(X))^{(-1)^{N-r+n+j}},

where
F
is the geometric Frobenius map. The factor for
m=0
is clearly in
the numerator. By Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
,
the reciprocal roots of all other factors
(including all polar factors) are divisible, as algebraic integers,
by
q^{\nu_{0}^{(r-1)}(N;d_{1},\cdots,d_{r})}
(
m=1
) if
r>N-n
, and
by
q^{\nu_{1}^{(N-n)}(N;d_{1},\cdots,d_{r})}
,
which is
q^{\mu_{1}(N;d_{1},\cdots,d_{r})}
, if
r=N-n
.
∎

We close this section by proving the following very elementary lemma,
which summarizes some basic properties of
the numbers
\nu_{j}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
.
These properties will be used in our proof.

Lemma 2.7
.

The numbers
\nu_{j}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
satisfy the following
properties.

1.

\nu^{(r)}_{j}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})=\mu_{j}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
.

2.

\nu_{j}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
is decreasing in
e
and
d_{i}
,
increasing in
j
.

3.

For any
e\in\mathbf{Z}
,
i\geqslant 1
,

j\geqslant 0
, we have

\nu^{(e)}_{j+i}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})\geqslant\nu_{j}^{(e-i)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}).

4.

Suppose
1\leqslant e\leqslant r_{1}\leqslant r-1
, then

\nu_{j+(r-r_{1}-1)}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r_{1}})\geqslant\nu_{j}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}).

The proofs of (
1
) and (
2
) are
straightforward and omitted. Clearly, it suffices to prove
(
3
) for
i=1
. We first assume

1\leqslant e\leqslant r
. In this case, we have following estimate:

\displaystyle\nu_{j+1}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})

\displaystyle=j+1+\max\left\{0,\left\lceil\frac{N-j-1-\sum_{i}d_{i}^{\ast}(e)}{d_{1}}\right\rceil\right\}

\displaystyle=j+\max\left\{1,\left\lceil\frac{N-j+(d_{1}-d_{e})-\sum_{i\neq e}d_{i}^{\ast}(e)-1}{d_{1}}\right\rceil\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant j+\max\left\{1,\left\lceil\frac{N-j-\sum_{i}d_{i}^{\ast}(e-1)}{d_{1}}\right\rceil\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\nu_{j}^{(e-1)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}).

If
e\geqslant r+1
, then
d_{i}(e)=d_{i}=d_{i}(e-1)
, the
property reduces to the inequality

\max\left\{0,\frac{N-j-1-\sum_{1}^{r}d_{\nu}}{d_{1}}\right\}+j+1\geqslant j+\max\left\{0,\frac{N-j-\sum_{1}^{r}d_{\nu}}{d_{1}}\right\},

which is clearly valid since
d_{1}\geqslant 1
.
If
e\leqslant 0
, then the property reduces to

\max\left\{0,\frac{N-j-1-M\cdot d_{1}}{d_{1}}\right\}+j+1\geqslant\max\left\{0,\frac{N-j-M\cdot d_{1}}{d_{1}}\right\}+j

where
M=\operatorname{Card}\{\nu:d_{\nu}=d_{1}\}
.
This is also valid since
d_{1}\geqslant 1
.

To prove (
4
), we simply compute:

\displaystyle\nu_{j+(r-r_{1}-1)}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r_{1}})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle j+(r-r_{1}-1)+\max\left\{0,\left\lceil\frac{N-j-(r-r_{1}-1)-\sum_{i=1}^{r_{1}}d_{i}^{\ast}(e)}{d_{1}}\right\rceil\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant{}

\displaystyle j+\max\left\{0,\left\lceil\frac{N-j+(r-r_{1}-1)(d_{1}-1)+\sum_{i=r_{1}+1}^{r}d^{\ast}_{i}(e)-\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}^{\ast}(e)}{d_{1}}\right\rceil\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant{}

\displaystyle\nu_{j}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})

The same proof shows that for integer
i\geqslant 0
and
1\leqslant e\leqslant r_{1}\leqslant r-i
, then

\nu_{j+(r-r_{1}-i)}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r_{1}})\geqslant\nu_{j}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}).

But we will only need the case
i=1
as stated in the lemma.
This completes the proof.
∎

Conventions

In the following,
k
will either be an algebraic closure finite
field, or the field
\mathbf{C}
of complex numbers. We fix a prime
\ell
different from the characteristic of
k
.

If
k
is an algebraic closure of a finite field, and if
K
is a
constructible
\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}
-complex on a variety
X

(“arithmetic situation”), we will assume that
K
is equipped with
a Frobenius action (a complex of “Weil sheaves”). This is the case
when
X=X_{0}\otimes_{\mathbf{F}_{q}}k
is defined over some
finite subfield
\mathbf{F}_{q}
, and
K
is the inverse image of
some
\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}
-complex on
X_{0}
.

If
k=\mathbf{C}
(“Betti situation”), and if
K
is a
constructible complex with
\mathbf{Q}
-coefficient on

X^{\mathrm{an}}
, then we use the symbol

\mathrm{H}^{\ast}_{c}(X;K)
and
\mathrm{H}^{\ast}(X;K)

to denote the cohomology of the complex
K
on the analytic space

X^{\mathrm{an}}
, with or without compact supports. We will assume
that
K
underlies a mixed Hodge module, so that

\mathrm{H}_{c}^{\ast}(X;K)
and
\mathrm{H}^{\ast}(X;K)
are a
mixed Hodge structures.

In either case, the constant sheaf
\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}
or

\mathbf{Q}
will be denote by
\Lambda
, and
\Lambda(m)

denotes the
m
th
Tate twist. When we write

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{(c)}(X)
, we mean

\mathrm{H}^{i}_{(c)}(X;\Lambda)
.

We shall construct and use several maps between cohomology groups in
the main text. They all come from the standard 6-operations.
Therefore, in the arithmetic situation, these maps are easily shown to
be compatible with the Frobenius actions, once we choose a field of
definition of the variety and the complexes; in the Betti situation,
these maps are maps of Hodge structures.

R\iota_{B}^{!}Rf_{!}\mathbf{D}K\simeq Rf^{\prime}_{!}R(\iota^{\prime}_{B})^{!}\mathbf{D}K
.
Here
\mathbf{D}K
is the Verdier dual of
K
.
Replacing
K
by
\mathbf{D}K
in this latter isomorphism,
using
Rf_{!}=Rf_{\ast}
(as
f
is proper)
and
Rf^{\prime}_{!}=Rf^{\prime}_{\ast}
, we conclude that

Rf^{\prime}_{\ast}(\iota^{\prime}_{B})^{-1}K[-2](-1)\simeq Rf^{\prime}_{\ast}R(\iota^{\prime}_{B})^{!}K.

(3.5)

Since
f
is a finite morphism, so is
f^{\prime}
. In this situation, we
have
Rf^{\prime}_{\ast}=f^{\prime}_{\ast}
. For any closed point
b
of
B
,
the stalks of both sides of (
3.5
) at
b
are
the direct sums of the stalks of
(\iota^{\prime}_{B})^{-1}K[-2](-1)
and

R(\iota^{\prime}_{B})^{!}K
at the points in
f^{-1}(b)
. Since the
purity map induces an isomorphism on the direct sum of these stalks,
the purity map itself is necessarily an isomorphism.
Therefore, (
3.5
) implies that

K|_{B\times_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}X}[-2](-1)\simeq R(\iota^{\prime}_{B})^{!}K
.
This finishes the proof when
f
is a finite morphism.

Theorem 3.6

(Deligne)
.

Let
f\colon X\to\mathbf{P}^{N}
be a quasi-finite morphism to a projective space.
Let
K
be a perverse sheaf on
X
. Then for a sufficiently general
hyperplane
B
, the restriction morphism

\mathrm{H}^{i}(X;K)\to\mathrm{H}^{i}(f^{-1}B;K|_{f^{-1}B})

is injective if
i=-1
, and bijective if
i<-1
.

Remark 3.7
.

Going through the proof of Theorem
3.6
, one
finds that in the statement one may replace the perversity of
K

by
K
satisfying the
cosupport condition
. Recall that a
constructible complex
K
is said to be
semiperverse
, or to
satisfy the
support condition
, if

\dim\operatorname{Supp}\mathcal{H}^{i}(K)\leqslant-i
. In terms of
the perverse
t
-structure, this means that
K
has vanishing
positive perverse cohomology sheaves. If
\mathbf{D}K
satisfies
the support condition then we say
K
satisfies the

cosupport condition
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
f\colon X\to\mathbf{P}^{N}
be a quasi-finite morphism. Let

be a perverse sheaf on

(or more generally,

satisfies

). Then for a sufficiently general

, there exists a Gysin map

\mathrm{H}^{i-2}_{c}(X\times_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}B;K|_{X\times_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}B}(-1))\to\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(X;K),

which is surjective if

, and is bijective if

.

We choose a sufficiently generic hyperplane

such that the

3.6
holds for

, and such that the conclusion of

, and is bijective for

.
Taking Verdier duality yields that the natural map

, and is bijective for

.

4
The method of del Angel and Esnault–Katz

Situation 4.1
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]
be a collection
of polynomials. Let
X
be the common zero locus of

f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
in
\mathbf{A}^{N}
.
Assume that
d_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant d_{r}
is a sequence of numbers
such that
d_{i}\geqslant\deg f_{i}
.
Let
n=\dim X
. In general,
n\geqslant N-r
; the equality holds
if and only if
X
is a (set-theoretic) complete intersection by the
r
defining equations.

The following lemma proves the main theorems when
X
is a
set-theoretic complete intersection, and it also proves
Theorem
1.12
.

Lemma 4.2
.

In Situation
4.1
, the colevel of

\mathrm{H}^{n+j}_{c}(X)
is

\geqslant\mu_{j}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
for
j\geqslant 0
.

Choose
n
sufficiently general hyperplanes
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}
.
Since
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}
are general, for each
j
,

X\cap B_{1}\cap\cdots\cap B_{j}
is a Zariski closed subset cut
out in
\mathbf{A}^{N-j}
by equations of degrees at most

d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
, and has dimension equal to
n-j
.

Applying the Gysin lemma, Lemma
3.9
, to
X_{i}

gives the following chain of maps:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{n}_{c}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{n+1}_{c}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{n+2}_{c}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{n-1}_{c}(X\cap B_{1};\Lambda(-1))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{n}_{c}(X\cap B_{1};\Lambda(-1))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{n-2}_{c}(X\cap B_{1}\cap B_{2};\Lambda(-2))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ddots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sim}

It follows that the colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{n+j}_{c}(X)
is at least
the colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{n-j}_{c}(X\cap B_{1}\cap\cdots\cap B_{j})
plus

.
By the theorem of Esnault and

1.10
(

),
the colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{n-j}_{c}(X\cap B_{1}\cap\cdots\cap B_{j})
is a least
\mu_{0}(N-j;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
.
It follows that the colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{n+j}_{c}(X)
is at least

\mu_{0}(N-j;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})+j=\mu_{j}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
.
∎

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{i-2}(Y;\Lambda(-1))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{i-2}(Y\cap A;\Lambda(-1))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{i}(Y)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\smile c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{Y}(1))}

(5.7)

where the left arrow is the restriction map. For the moment, let us
grant the existence of the factorization
(
5.7
), and see how it implies the theorem.
Since
A
can be arbitrarily chosen, we shall choose it so that

\dim Y\cap A=\dim Y-1=n-1
. Viewing
A
as an
N-1

dimensional projective space, we can apply the inductive hypotheses to
the
(n-1)
-dimensional scheme
Y\cap A
. Therefore, the colevel
of
\mathrm{H}^{i-2}(Y\cap A;\Lambda(-1))_{\mathrm{prim}}
is at
least

(
\alpha
)

1+\nu_{0}^{(r-(m-1))}(N+1;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
if

i=N-r+m
(that is,
i-2=(N-1)-r+(m-1)
), for

1\leqslant m\leqslant r-(N-n)
;

(
\beta
)

1+\nu_{j-1}^{((N-1)-(n-1))}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
, if

i=n+j
(that is,
i-2=(n-1)+(j-1)
),
j\geqslant 1
.

The induction proof is concluded by observing the following elementary
facts:

(0)

(
5.7
) gives a factorization

\mathrm{H}^{i-2}(Y;\Lambda(-1))_{\mathrm{prim}}\to\mathrm{H}^{i-2}(Y\cap A;\Lambda(-1))_{\mathrm{prim}}\to\mathrm{H}^{i}(Y)_{\mathrm{prim}};

of (
5.6
)

(
1_{e}
)

1+\nu_{0}^{(e+1)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})\geqslant\nu_{0}^{(e)}(N+1;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
,
for any
e\in\mathbf{Z}
, and

(
2_{e}
)

1+\nu^{(e)}_{j-1}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})=\nu_{j}^{(e)}(N+1;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})

for any
j\geqslant 1
.

Indeed, given Item (0), (
\alpha
) and (
1_{r-m}
) imply (a), and
(
\beta
) and (
2_{N-n}
) imply (b). Item (0) is straightforward,
and Item (2) can be seen simply by expanding the definition. To prove
(1), we note that by (2), we have

1+\nu_{0}^{(e+1)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})=\nu_{1}^{(e)}(N;d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
. Then (1) follows from
Lemma
2.7
(
3
).
∎

It remains to explain the existence of the factorization
(
5.7
).

The Chern class

c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}(1))\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(\mathbf{P}^{N};\Lambda(1))

can be regarded as a mapping, in the derived category of constructible
complexes of
\Lambda
-sheaves

\Lambda_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}(-1)[-2]\xrightarrow{\smile c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}(1))}\Lambda_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}.

(5.8)

Let
A
be a hyperplane in
\mathbf{P}^{N}
, and let
\iota\colon A\to\mathbf{P}^{N}
be
the inclusion morphism. The relative purity theorem implies that

\iota^{!}\Lambda_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}=\Lambda_{A}(-1)[-2]
. From this, we obtain a
“Gysin morphism”
\iota_{\ast}\Lambda_{A}(-1)[-2]\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Gys}}\Lambda_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}
arising from the counit morphism
\iota_{\ast}\iota^{!}\Lambda_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}\to\Lambda_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}
. The mapping
(
5.8
) can be identified with

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Lambda_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}(-1)[-2]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota_{\ast}\Lambda_{A}(-1)[-2]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Lambda_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\smile c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}(1))}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Gys}}

(5.9)

This identification holds because the cycle class of
A
coincides with

c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}(1))
.

Since taking the Chern class is compatible with the restriction of
invertible sheaves, applying the
\ast
-inverse image to
(
5.8
) with respect to

Y\hookrightarrow\mathbf{P}^{N}
gives rise to the Chern class of

\mathcal{O}_{Y}(1)
. By restricting
(
5.9
) to
Y
and applying the
proper base change theorem, we obtain the following factorization:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Lambda_{Y}(-1)[-2]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota^{\prime}_{\ast}\Lambda_{Y\cap A}(-1)[-2]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Lambda_{Y},}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\smile c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{Y}(1))}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Gys}}

(5.10)

where
\iota^{\prime}\colon Y\cap A\to Y
denotes the inclusion morphism.

Applying
R\Gamma(Y;-)
to the sheaf-theoretic factorization
(
5.10
) gives the cohomological
factorization (
5.7
), as claimed.
∎

Remark 5.11
.

A version of this proof appeared in
[
WZ25
]
. However, the argument
there contained a gap: it incorrectly claimed that the image of
u
in
(
5.4
) was always “non-primitive”. The proof presented
above corrects this oversight: although the image of
u
may indeed be
primitive, it arises as the Gysin image of a hyperplane section, whose
colevel can be controlled through induction. We thank an anonymous
referee for bringing this issue to our attention.

6
Two examples

We end with two “determinantal” examples illustrating how the new bounds
provided by Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
are sharper
than the previously known (or anticipated) bounds.

Example 6.1
.

Let
e
be a positive integer
\geqslant 4
.
Let
N=e^{2}
. View
\mathbf{A}^{N}
as the space of
e\times e
-matrices.
Consider the following variety

X=\{A\in\mathbf{A}^{N}:\text{ all principal }(e-1)\text{-minors are zero}\}.

Then
X
is defined by the vanishing of
r=e
equations of degree
e-1
.
The Ax–Katz bound implies that the colevels of
\mathrm{H}^{\ast}_{c}(X)
are
\geqslant 2
.

According to Wheeler
[
Whe15
, Theorem 4]
,
X
has two irreducible
components:
X=X_{1}\cup X_{2}
, where
X_{1}=\{A\in\mathbf{A}^{N}:\operatorname{rank}A\leqslant e-2\}
,

X_{2}
is the Zariski closure of
\{A\in X:\det A\neq 0\}
.
Moreover,
\dim X_{2}=N-e
. On the other hand, it is
well-known that
\dim X_{1}=e^{2}-4
(
[
ACGH85
, p. 67, Proposition]
). Thus the dimension of
X
is
n=e^{2}-4
.
The affine variety
X
in
\mathbf{A}^{N}
is a complete intersection if
e=4
, not a complete intersection if
e\geqslant 5
.

The improved bounds, Theorem
2.2
and

2.3
, give the following:

•

(before middle dimension) for
0\leqslant m\leqslant e-4
, the colevel
of
\mathrm{H}^{e^{2}-e+m}_{c}(X)
is at least

\left\lceil m+1-\frac{m-1}{e-1}\right\rceil
which is
m+1
if

1\leqslant m\leqslant e-4
, and
2
if
m=0
(Theorem
1.10

only shows that these colevels are
\geqslant 2
);

•

(beyond middle dimension) for
0\leqslant j\leqslant e^{2}-4
, the
colevel of
\mathrm{H}^{e^{2}-4+j}_{c}(X)
is at least

j+\max\left\{0,e-4+\left\lceil\frac{4-j}{e-1}\right\rceil\right\}
.
When
e\geqslant 5
, this latter value is
j+e-3
if

0\leqslant j\leqslant 3
,
j+e-4
if
4\leqslant j\leqslant e+2
,
j+e-5
if

e+3\leqslant j\leqslant 2e+1
, etc. By contrast, the colevel lower bound
anticipated by Esnault and the first author
(Theorem
1.12
) is

j+\max\left\{0,\left\lceil\frac{e-j}{e-1}\right\rceil\right\}
, which is
j+2
if
j=0
,

j+1
if
1\leqslant j\leqslant e-1
and
j
if
j\geqslant e
.

Example 6.2
.

Notation as Example
6.1
, consider

X^{\prime}=\{A\in\mathbf{A}^{N}:\operatorname{rank}A\leqslant e-1,\text{ all principal }(e-1)\text{-minors are zero}\}.

Then
X^{\prime}
is cut out by
r=e+1
equations: the vanishing
of the
e
principal minors (degree
e-1
polynomials),
and
\det A=0
(a degree
e
polynomial).
Thus
d_{1}=e
,
d_{2}=\ldots=d_{r}=e-1
.

From Wheeler’s theorem cited above it is easy to see that
we can write
X^{\prime}=X_{1}\cup X^{\prime}_{2}
, where
X^{\prime}_{2}=X_{2}\cap\{\det=0\}
.
Thus
X^{\prime}_{2}
has codimension
e+1\geqslant 5
in
\mathbf{A}^{N}
.
It follows that
X^{\prime}
has codimension
4
in
\mathbf{A}^{N}
.

Since
\frac{N-\sum d_{i}}{d_{1}}=0
,
the bounds given by Theorem
1.12
do not
improve (and is the same as) Deligne’s bounds, Theorem
1.8
.
By contrast, Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
give the following better lower bounds.

•

Before middle dimension, for
0\leqslant m\leqslant e-3
, we have:

\text{colevel of }\mathrm{H}^{e^{2}-(e+1)+m}_{c}(X^{\prime})\geqslant\left\lceil e-1-\frac{e-1}{e}(e-m)\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{(e-1)m}{e}\right\rceil.

The Esnault–Katz colevel lower bound in this case is
0
.

•

Beyond middle dimension, for
0\leqslant j\leqslant e^{2}-4
, we have

\displaystyle\text{colevel of }\mathrm{H}_{c}^{e^{2}-4+j}(X^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geqslant j+\max\left\{0,\left\lceil\frac{e^{2}-4e+3-j}{e}\right\rceil\right\}

\displaystyle=j+\max\left\{0,e-4+\left\lceil\frac{3-j}{e}\right\rceil\right\}.

When
e\geqslant 5
, this latter value is
j+e-3
if
0\leqslant j\leqslant 2
,
j+e-4
if
3\leqslant j\leqslant e+2
,
j+e-5
if
e+3\leqslant j\leqslant 2e+2
etc.
By contrast, the colevel lower bound given by Theorem
1.12
is
j
.
