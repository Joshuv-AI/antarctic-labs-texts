Particle-based, rapid incremental smoother meets particle Gibbs
Gabriel Cardoso, Eric Moulines, Jimmy Olsson

Abstract
The particle-based, rapid incremental smoother ( PARIS ) is a sequential Monte Carlo technique allowing for efficient online approximation of expectations of additive functionals under Feynman–Kac path distributions. Under weak assumptions, the algorithm has linear computational complexity and limited memory requirements. It also comes with a number of non-asymptotic bounds and convergence results. However, being based on self-normalised importance sampling, the PARIS estimator is biased; its bias is inversely proportional to the number of particles but has been found to grow linearly with the time horizon under appropriate mixing conditions. In this work, we propose the Parisian particle Gibbs ( PPG ) sampler, whose complexity is essentially the same as that of the PARIS and which significantly reduces the bias for a given computational complexity at the price of a modest increase in the variance. This method is a wrapper in the sense that it uses the PARIS algorithm in the inner loop of particle Gibbs to form a bias-reduced version of the targeted quantities. We substantiate the PPG algorithm with theoretical results, including new bounds on bias and variance as well as deviation inequalities. We illustrate our theoretical results with numerical experiments supporting our claims.

In this paper, we focus on the problem of recursively computing smoothed expectations

\eta_{0:n}h_{n}=\int h_{n}(x_{{0:n}})\,\eta_{0:n}(\mathrm{d}x_{{0:n}}),\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

2.1
Many-body Feynman–Kac models

In the following we assume that all random variables are defined on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})

2.2
Backward interpretation of Feynman–Kac path flows

Suppose that each kernel
Q_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, defined in (
1.3
), has a transition density
q_{n}
with respect to some dominating measure
\lambda_{n+1}\in\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{X}_{n+1})
. Then for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\eta\in\mathsf{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X}_{n})
we may define the
backward kernel

\overleftarrow{Q}_{n,\eta}:\mathsf{X}_{n+1}\times\mathcal{X}_{n}\ni(x_{n+1},A)\mapsto\frac{\int\mathbbm{1}_{A}(x_{n})q_{n}(x_{n},x_{n+1})\,\eta(\mathrm{d}x_{n})}{\int q_{n}(x_{n}^{\prime},x_{n+1})\,\eta(\mathrm{d}x_{n}^{\prime})}.

(2.2)

Now, denoting, for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
,

B_{n}:\mathsf{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{X}_{0:n-1}\ni(x_{n},A)\mapsto\idotsint\mathbbm{1}_{A}(x_{0:n-1})\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\overleftarrow{Q}_{m,\eta_{m}}(x_{m+1},\mathrm{d}x_{m}),

(2.3)

we may state the following—now classical—
backward decomposition
of the Feynman–Kac path measures, a result that will play a pivotal role in the following.

Proposition 1
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
it holds that
\gamma_{{0:n}}=\gamma_{{n}}\varotimes B_{n}
and
\eta_{0:n}=\eta_{n}\varotimes B_{n}
.

Although the decomposition in
Proposition

1
is well known (see,
e.g.
,
[
14
,
16
]
), we provide a proof in
Section

5.1
for completeness. Using the backward decomposition, one can obtain a particle approximation of a given Feynman–Kac path measure
\eta_{0:n}
by first sampling, in an initial forward pass, particle clouds
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m}\}_{m=0}^{n}
from
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}\varotimes{\boldsymbol{M}}_{0}\varotimes\cdots\varotimes{\boldsymbol{M}}_{n-1}
and then sampling, in a subsequent backward pass, say
N
conditionally independent paths
\{\tilde{\xi}_{0:n}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
from
\mathbb{B}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n},\cdot)
, where

\mathbb{B}_{n}:\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{0:n}\times\mathcal{X}_{0:n}\ni(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:n},A)\mapsto\idotsint\mathbbm{1}_{A}(x_{0:n})\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\overleftarrow{Q}_{m,\mu(\boldsymbol{x}_{m})}(x_{m+1},\mathrm{d}x_{m})\right)\mu(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})(\mathrm{d}x_{n})

(2.4)

is a Markov kernel describing the time-reversed dynamics induced by the particle approximations generated in the forward pass. (Here and in the following we use blackboard notation to denote kernels related to many-body path spaces.) Finally,
\mu(\{\tilde{\xi}_{0:n}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})h
is returned as an estimator of
\eta_{0:n}h
for any
\eta_{0:n}
-integrable test function
h
. This algorithm is in the literature referred to as the
forward–filtering backward–simulation
(FFBSi)
algorithm
and was introduced in
[
21
]
; see also
[
7
,
17
]
.
More precisely, given the forward particles
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m}\}_{m=0}^{n}
, each path
\tilde{\xi}_{0:n}^{i}
is generated by first drawing
\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{i}
uniformly among the particles
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}
in the last generation and then drawing, recursively,

\tilde{\xi}_{m}^{i}\sim\overleftarrow{Q}_{m,\mu(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m})}(\tilde{\xi}_{m+1}^{i},\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{q_{m}(\xi_{m}^{j},\tilde{\xi}_{m+1}^{i})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}q_{m}(\xi_{m}^{\ell},\tilde{\xi}_{m+1}^{i})}\delta_{\xi_{m}^{j}},

(2.5)

i.e.
, given
\tilde{\xi}_{m+1}^{i}
,
\tilde{\xi}_{m}^{i}
is picked at random among the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m}
according to weights proportional to
\{q_{m}(\xi_{m}^{j},\tilde{\xi}_{m+1}^{i})\}_{j=1}^{N}
.
Note that in this basic formulation of the FFBSi algorithm, each backward-sampling operation (
2.5
) requires the computation of the normalising constant
\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}q_{m}(\xi_{m}^{\ell},\tilde{\xi}_{m+1}^{i})
, which implies an overall quadratic complexity of the algorithm. Still, this heavy computational burden can eased by means of an effective accept–reject technique discussed in
Section

2.4
.

2.3
Conditional dual processes and particle Gibbs

The
dual process
associated with a given Feynman–Kac model (
1.1
–
1.2
) and a given trajectory
\{z_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
z_{n}\in\mathsf{X}_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is defined as the canonical Markov chain with kernels

{\boldsymbol{M}}_{n}\langle z_{n+1}\rangle:\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{n}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{n+1}\ni\\
(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},A)\mapsto\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\left(\Phi_{n}(\mu(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}))^{\varotimes i}\varotimes\delta_{z_{n+1}}\varotimes\Phi_{n}(\mu(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}))^{\varotimes(N-i-1)}\right)(A),

(2.6)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}\langle z_{0}\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\left(\eta_{0}^{\varotimes i}\varotimes\delta_{z_{0}}\varotimes\eta_{0}^{\varotimes(N-i-1)}\right).

As clear from (

while drawing independently the remaining elements in the same vector from

at a randomly selected position in
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n+1}
while drawing independently the remaining elements from
\Phi_{n}(\mu(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}))

In order to describe compactly the law of the conditional dual process we define the Markov kernel

\mathbb{C}_{n}:\mathsf{X}_{0:n}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{0:n}\ni(z_{{0:n}},A)\mapsto\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}\langle z_{0}\rangle\varotimes{\boldsymbol{M}}_{0}\langle z_{1}\rangle\varotimes\cdots\varotimes{\boldsymbol{M}}_{n-1}\langle z_{n}\rangle(A).

\mathbb{B}_{n}\varotimes\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0:n}=\gamma_{{0:n}}\varotimes\mathbb{C}_{n}.

In
[
16
]
, each state

]
. More specifically, assume that we have generated a state

(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:n}[\ell],\zeta_{0:n}[\ell])
comprising a dual process with associated path on the basis of
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
iterations of the sampler; then the next state
(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:n}[\ell+1],\zeta_{0:n}[\ell+1])
is generated in a Markovian fashion by, first, sampling
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:n}[\ell+1]\sim\mathbb{C}_{n}(\zeta_{0:n}[\ell],\cdot)
and, second, sampling
\zeta_{0:n}[\ell+1]\sim\mathbb{B}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:n}[\ell+1],\cdot)
. After arbitrary initialisation (and the discard of possible burn-in), this procedure produces a Markov trajectory
\{(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:n}[\ell],\zeta_{{0:n}}[\ell])\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
, and under weak additional technical conditions this Markov chain admits (
2.9
) as its unique
invariant distribution. In such case, the Markov chain is ergodic
[
19
, Chapter 5]
, and the marginal distribution of the conditioning path
\zeta_{{0:n}}[\ell]
converges to the target distribution
\eta_{0:n}
. Therefore, for every
h\in\mathsf{F}(\mathcal{X}_{0:n})
, it holds that

\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}h(\zeta_{{0:n}}[\ell])=\eta_{0:n}h,\quad\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.s.}

2.4
The
PARIS
algorithm

In the following we assume that we are given a sequence
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
additive state functionals
of type (
1.4
). This problem is particularly relevant in the context of maximum-likelihood-based parameter estimation in general state-space models,
e.g.
, when computing the
score-function
(the gradient of the log-likelihood function) via the Fisher identity or when computing the intermediate quantity
of the
expectation–maximization
(EM)
algorithm
, in which case
\eta_{0:n}
and
h_{n}
correspond to the joint state posterior and an element of some sufficient statistic, respectively; see
[
8
,
17
,
14
,
26
,
24
]

and the references therein.

Interestingly, as noted in
[
6
,
14
]
, the backward decomposition allows, when applied to additive state functionals, a forward recursion for the expectations
\{\eta_{0:n}h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. More specifically, using the forward decomposition
h_{n+1}(x_{{0:n+1}})=h_{n}(x_{{0:n}})+\tilde{h}_{n}(x_{n},x_{n+1})
and the backward kernel
B_{n+1}
defined in (
2.3
), we may write, for
x_{n+1}\in\mathsf{X}_{n+1}
,

\displaystyle B_{n+1}h_{n+1}(x_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\int\overleftarrow{Q}_{n,\eta_{n}}(x_{n+1},\mathrm{d}x_{n})\int\left(h_{n}(x_{0:n})+\tilde{h}_{n}(x_{n},x_{n+1})\right)B_{n}(x_{n},\mathrm{d}x_{0:n-1})

\displaystyle=\overleftarrow{Q}_{n,\eta_{n}}(B_{n}h_{n}+\tilde{h}_{n})(x_{n+1}),

(2.10)

which by
Proposition

1
implies that

\eta_{0:n+1}h_{n+1}=\eta_{n+1}\overleftarrow{Q}_{n,\eta_{n}}(B_{n}h_{n}+\tilde{h}_{n}).

(2.11)

Since, as we have seen, the marginal flow
\{\eta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can be expressed recursively via the mappings
\{\Phi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, (
2.11
) provides, in principle, a basis for online computation of
\{\eta_{0:n}h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. To handle the fact that the marginals are generally intractable we may, following
[
14
]
, plug particle approximations
\mu(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n+1})
and
\overleftarrow{Q}_{n,\mu(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n})}
(see (
2.5
)) of
\eta_{n+1}
and
\overleftarrow{Q}_{n,\mu(\eta_{n})}
, respectively, into the recursion (
2.11
). More precisely, we proceed recursively and assume that we at time
n
have at hand a sample
\{(\xi_{n}^{i},\beta_{n}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
of particles with associated statistics, where each statistic
\beta_{n}^{i}
serves as an approximation of
B_{n}h_{n}(\xi_{n}^{i})
; then evolving the particle cloud according to
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n+1}\sim{\boldsymbol{M}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n},\cdot)
and updating the statistics using (
2.10
), with
\overleftarrow{Q}_{n,\eta_{n}}
replaced by
\overleftarrow{Q}_{n,\mu(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n})}
, yields the particle-wise recursion

\beta_{n+1}^{i}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{q_{n}(\xi_{n}^{\ell},\xi_{n+1}^{i})}{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{n}(\xi_{n}^{\ell^{\prime}},\xi_{n+1}^{i})}\left(\beta_{n}^{\ell}+\tilde{h}_{n}(\xi_{n}^{\ell},\xi_{n+1}^{i})\right),\quad i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket,

(2.12)

and, finally, the estimator

\mu(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n})(\operatorname{id})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{i}

(2.13)

of
\eta_{0:n}h_{n}
, where we have set
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}\coloneqq(\beta_{n}^{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}^{N})
,
i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
. The procedure is initialised by simply letting
\beta_{0}^{i}=0
for all
i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
.
Note that (
2.13
) provides a particle interpretation of the backward decomposition in
Proposition

1
. This algorithm is a special case of the
forward–filtering backward–smoothing
(FFBSm)
algorithm
(see
[
3
,
21
,
17
,
27
]
) for additive functionals satisfying (
1.4
). It allows for online processing of the sequence
\{\eta_{0:n}h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, but has also the appealing property that only the current particles
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}
and statistics
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}
need to be stored in the memory. However, since each update (
2.12
) requires the summation of
N
terms, the scheme has an overall
quadratic
complexity in the number of particles, leading to a computational bottleneck in applications to complex models that require large particle sample sizes
N
.

In order to detour the computational burden of this forward-only implementation of FFBSm, the
PARIS
algorithm
[
24
]
updates the statistics
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}
by replacing each sum (
2.12
) by a Monte Carlo estimate

\beta_{n+1}^{i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left(\tilde{\beta}_{n}^{i,j}+\tilde{h}_{n}(\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{i,j},\xi_{n+1}^{i})\right),\quad i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket,

(2.14)

where
\{(\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{i,j},\tilde{\beta}_{n}^{i,j})\}_{j=1}^{M}
are drawn randomly among
\{(\xi_{n}^{i},\beta_{n}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
with replacement, by assigning
(\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{i,j},\tilde{\beta}_{n}^{i,j})
the value of
(\xi_{n}^{\ell},\beta_{n}^{\ell})
with probability
q_{n}(\xi_{n}^{\ell},\xi_{n+1}^{i})/\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{n}(\xi_{n}^{\ell^{\prime}},\xi_{n+1}^{i})
, and the Monte Carlo sample size
M\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
is supposed to be much smaller than
N
(say, less than
5
). Formally,

\{(\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{i,j},\tilde{\beta}_{n}^{i,j})\}_{j=1}^{M}\sim\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{q_{n}(\xi_{n}^{\ell},\xi_{n+1}^{i})}{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{n}(\xi_{n}^{\ell^{\prime}},\xi_{n+1}^{i})}\delta_{(\xi_{n}^{\ell},\beta_{n}^{\ell})}\right)^{\varotimes M},\quad i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket.

The resulting procedure, summarised in
Algorithm

1
, allows for online processing with constant memory requirements, since it only needs to store the current particle cloud and the estimated auxiliary statistics at each iteration. Moreover, in the case where the Markov transition densities of the model can be uniformly bounded,
i.e.
, there exists, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, an upper bound
\bar{\sigma}_{n}>0
such that for all
(x_{n},x_{n+1})\in\mathsf{X}_{n}\times\mathsf{X}_{n+1}
,
m_{n}(x_{n},x_{n+1})\leq\bar{\sigma}_{n}
(a weak assumption satisfied for most models of interest), a sample
(\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{i,j},\beta_{n}^{i,j})
can be generated by drawing, with replacement and until acceptance, candidates
(\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{i,\ast},\tilde{\beta}_{n}^{i,\ast})
from
\{(\xi_{n}^{i},\beta_{n}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
according to the normalised particle weights
\{g_{n}(\xi_{n}^{\ell})/\sum_{\ell^{\prime}}g_{n}(\xi_{n}^{\ell^{\prime}})\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
(obtained as a by-product in the generation of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n+1}
) and accepting the same with probability
m_{n}(\tilde{\xi}_{n}^{i,\ast},\xi_{n+1}^{i})/\bar{\sigma}_{n}
. As this sampling procedure bypasses completely the calculation of the normalising constant
\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{n}(\xi_{n}^{\ell^{\prime}},\xi_{n+1}^{i})
of the targeted categorical distribution, it yields an overall
\mathcal{O}(MN)
complexity of the algorithm as a whole; see
[
17
]
for details.

Increasing
M
improves the accuracy of the algorithm at the cost of additional computational complexity. As shown in
[
24
]
, there is a qualitative difference between the cases
M=1
and
M\geq 2
, and it turns out that the latter is required to keep
PARIS
numerically stable. More precisely, in the latter case, it can be shown that the
PARIS
estimator
\mu(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n})
satisfies, as
N
tends to infinity while
M
is held fixed, a central limit theorem (CLT) at the rate
\sqrt{N}
and with an
n
-normalised asymptotic variance of order
\mathcal{O}(1-1/(M-1))
. As clear from this bound, using a large
M
is only to waste the computational work, and setting
M
to
2
or
3
typically works well in practice.

3
The Parisian particle Gibbs (
PPG
) sampler

We now introduce the
Parisian particle Gibbs
(
PPG
)
algorithm
.
For all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
, let
\mathsf{Y}_{n}\coloneqq\mathsf{X}_{0:n}\times\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{n}\coloneqq\mathcal{X}_{0:n}\varotimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, let
\mathsf{Y}_{0}\coloneqq\mathsf{X}_{0}\times\{0\}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{0}\coloneqq\mathcal{X}_{0}\varotimes\{\{0\},\emptyset\}
. An element of
\mathsf{Y}_{n}
will always be denoted by
y_{n}=(x_{0:n|n},b_{n})
. The Parisian particle Gibbs sampler comprises, as a key ingredient, a
conditional
PARIS
step
, which updates recursively a set of
\mathsf{Y}_{n}
-valued random variables

\upsilon_{n}^{i}\coloneqq(\xi_{0:n|n}^{i},\beta_{n}^{i})
,
i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
.
Let
(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote the corresponding many-body process, each
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n}\coloneqq\{(\xi_{0:n|n}^{i},\beta_{n}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
taking on values in the space
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}_{n}\coloneqq\mathsf{Y}_{n}^{N}
, which we furnish with a
\sigma
-field
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{n}\coloneqq\mathcal{Y}_{n}^{\varotimes N}
. The space
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}
and the corresponding
\sigma
-field
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{0}
are defined accordingly.
For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:n|n}
for the collection
\{\xi_{0:n|n}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of paths in
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n}
, and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n|n}
for the collection
\{\xi_{n|n}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of end points of the same.

In the following we let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a fixed time horizon, and describe in detail how the
PPG
approximates
\eta_{0:n}h_{n}
iteratively. In short, at each iteration
\ell
, the
PPG
produces, given an input conditional path
\zeta_{{0:n}}[\ell]
, a many-body system
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n}[\ell+1]
by means of a series of conditional
PARIS
operations; after this, an updated path
\zeta_{{0:n}}[\ell+1]
, serving as input at the next iteration, is generated by picking one of the paths
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:n|n}[\ell+1]
in
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n}[\ell+1]
at random. At each iteration, the produced statistics
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}
(in
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n}
) provides an approximation of
\eta_{0:n}h_{n}
according to (
2.13
).

More precisely, given the path
\zeta_{{0:n}}[\ell]
, the conditional
PARIS
operations are executed as follows. In the initial step,
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0|0}[\ell+1]
are drawn from
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}\langle\zeta_{0}[\ell]\rangle
defined in (
2.7
) and
\upsilon_{0}^{i}[\ell+1]\leftarrow(\xi_{0|0}^{i}[\ell+1],0)
for all
i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
; then, recursively for
m\in\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket
, assuming access to
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{m}[\ell+1]
, we

(1)

generate an updated particle cloud
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m+1}[\ell+1]\sim{\boldsymbol{M}}_{m}\langle\zeta_{m+1}[\ell]\rangle(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m}[\ell+1],\cdot)
,

(2)

pick at random, for each
i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
, an ancestor path with associated statistics
(\tilde{\xi}_{0:m}^{i,1}[\ell+1],\tilde{\beta}_{m}^{i,1}[\ell+1])
among
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{m}[\ell+1]
by drawing

(\tilde{\xi}_{0:m}^{i,1}[\ell+1],\tilde{\beta}_{m}^{i,1}[\ell+1])\sim\sum_{s=1}^{N}\frac{q_{m}(\xi_{m|m}^{s}[\ell+1],\xi_{m+1}^{i}[\ell+1])}{\sum_{s^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{m}(\xi_{m|m}^{s^{\prime}}[\ell+1],\xi_{m+1}^{i}[\ell+1])}\delta_{\upsilon_{m}^{s}[\ell+1]},\quad i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket,

(3)

draw, with replacement,
M-1
ancestor particles and associated statistics
\{(\tilde{\xi}_{m}^{i,j}[\ell+1],\tilde{\beta}_{m}^{i,j}[\ell+1])\}_{j=2}^{M}
at random from
\{(\xi_{m|m}^{s}[\ell+1],\beta_{m}^{s})[\ell+1]\}_{s=1}^{N}
according to

\{(\tilde{\xi}_{m}^{i,j}[\ell+1],\tilde{\beta}_{m}^{i,j}[\ell+1])\}_{j=2}^{M}\\
\sim\left(\sum_{s=1}^{N}\frac{q_{m}(\xi_{m|m}^{s}[\ell+1],\xi_{m+1}^{i}[\ell+1])}{\sum_{s^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{m}(\xi_{m|m}^{s^{\prime}}[\ell+1],\xi_{m+1}^{i}[\ell+1])}\delta_{(\xi_{m|m}^{s}[\ell+1],\beta_{m}^{s}[\ell+1])}\right)^{\varotimes(M-1)},

(4)

set, for all
i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
,
\xi_{0:m+1|m+1}^{i}[\ell+1]\leftarrow(\tilde{\xi}_{0:m}^{i,1}[\ell+1],\xi_{m+1}^{i}[\ell+1])
and
\upsilon_{m+1}^{i}[\ell+1]\leftarrow(\xi_{0:m+1|m+1}^{i}[\ell+1],\beta_{m+1}^{i}[\ell+1])
, where

\beta_{m+1}^{i}[\ell+1]\leftarrow M^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left(\tilde{\beta}_{m}^{i,j}[\ell+1]+\tilde{h}_{m}(\tilde{\xi}_{m}^{i,j}[\ell+1],\xi_{m+1}^{i}[\ell+1])\right).

This conditional
PARIS
procedure is summarised in
Algorithm

2
.

Once the set of trajectories and associated statistics
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n}[\ell+1]
is formed by means of
n
recursive conditional
PARIS
updates, an updated path
\zeta_{{0:n}}[\ell+1]
is drawn from
\mu(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:n|n}[\ell+1])
. A full sweep of the
PPG
is summarised in
Algorithm

3
.

The following Markov kernels will play an instrumental role in the following. For a given path
\{z_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
, the conditional
PARIS
update in
Algorithm

2
defines an inhomogeneous Markov chain on the spaces
\{(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}_{m},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{m})\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
with kernels

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{m+1}\ni(\boldsymbol{y}_{m},A)\mapsto\int\,{\boldsymbol{M}}_{m}\langle z_{m+1}\rangle(\boldsymbol{x}_{m|m},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{m+1})\,\boldsymbol{S}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y}_{m},\boldsymbol{x}_{m+1},A),\quad m\in\mathbb{N},

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}_{m}:\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}_{m}\times\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{m+1}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{m+1}\ni(\boldsymbol{y}_{m},\boldsymbol{x}_{m+1},A)

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mapsto\idotsint\mathbbm{1}_{{A}}\left(\Big\{\Big((\tilde{x}_{0:m}^{i,1},x_{m+1}^{i}),\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left(\tilde{b}_{m}^{i,j}+\tilde{h}_{m}(\tilde{x}_{m}^{i,j},x_{m+1}^{i})\right)\Big)\Big\}_{i=1}^{N}\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{q_{m}(x_{m|m}^{\ell},x_{m+1}^{i})}{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{m}(x_{m|m}^{\ell^{\prime}},x_{m+1}^{i})}\delta_{y_{m}^{\ell}}(\mathrm{d}(\tilde{x}_{0:m}^{i,1},\tilde{b}_{m}^{i,1}))\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{q_{m}(x_{m|m}^{\ell},x_{m+1}^{i})}{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{m}(x_{m|m}^{\ell^{\prime}},x_{m+1}^{i})}\delta_{(x_{m|m}^{\ell},b_{m}^{\ell})}\right)^{\varotimes(M-1)}(\mathrm{d}(\tilde{x}_{m}^{i,2},\tilde{b}_{m}^{i,2},\ldots,\tilde{x}_{m}^{i,M},\tilde{b}_{m}^{i,M}))\right)\,.

In addition, we introduce the joint law

\mathbb{S}_{n}:\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{0:n}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{n}\ni(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:n},A)\\
\mapsto\idotsint\mathbbm{1}_{{A}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{n})\,\boldsymbol{S}_{0}(\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{1})\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{S}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y}_{m},\boldsymbol{x}_{m+1},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{m+1}),

(3.2)

where we have defined
\mathbf{J}\coloneqq\operatorname{Id}_{N}\varotimes(0,1)^{\intercal}
.

The kernel
\mathbb{S}_{n}
can be viewed as a
superincumbent sampling kernel
describing the distribution of the output
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n}
generated by a sequence of
PARIS
iterates when the many-body process
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m}\}_{m=0}^{n}
associated with the underlying SMC algorithm is given. This allows us to describe alternatively the
PPG
as follows: given
\zeta_{0:n}[\ell]
, draw
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:n}[\ell+1]\sim\mathbb{C}_{n}(\zeta_{0:n}[\ell],\cdot)
; then, draw
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n}[\ell+1]\sim\mathbb{S}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:n}[\ell+1],\cdot)
and pick a trajectory
\zeta_{0:n}[\ell+1]
from
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:n|n}[\ell+1]
at random. The following proposition, which will be instrumental in the coming developments, establishes that the conditional distribution of
\zeta_{0:n}[\ell+1]
given
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0:n}[\ell+1]
coincides, as expected, with the particle-induced backward dynamics
\mathbb{B}_{n}
.

Proposition 2
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{0:n}\in\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{0:n}
, and
h\in\mathsf{F}(\mathcal{X}_{0:n})
,

\int\mathbb{S}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:n},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{n})\,\mu(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:n|n})h=\mathbb{B}_{n}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:n}).

Finally, we define the Markov kernel induced by the
PPG
as well as the extended probability distribution targeted by the same. For this purpose, we introduce the extended measurable space
(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{n},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{n})
with

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{n}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}_{n}\times\mathsf{X}_{0:n},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{n}\varotimes\mathcal{X}_{0:n}.

The
PPG
described in
Algorithm

3
defines a Markov chain on
(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{n},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{n})
with Markov transition kernel

\mathbb{K}_{n}:\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{n}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\ni(\boldsymbol{y}_{n},z_{0:n},A)\\
\mapsto\iiint\mathbbm{1}_{A}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{n},\tilde{z}_{0:n})\,\mathbb{C}_{n}(z_{0:n},\mathrm{d}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0:n})\,\mathbb{S}_{n}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0:n},\mathrm{d}\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{n})\,\mu(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0:n|n})(\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}_{0:n}).

Note that the values of
\mathbb{K}_{n}
defined above do not depend on
\boldsymbol{y}_{n}
, but only on
(z_{0:n},A)
. For any given initial distribution
\upxi\in\mathsf{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X}_{0:n})
, let
\mathbb{P}_{\upxi}
be the distribution of the canonical Markov chain induced by the kernel
\mathbb{K}_{n}
and the initial distribution
\upxi
. In the special case where
\upxi=\delta_{z_{0:n}}
for some given path
z_{0:n}\in\mathsf{X}_{0:n}
, we use the short-hand notation
\mathbb{P}_{\delta_{z_{0:n}}}=\mathbb{P}_{z_{0:n}}
.
In addition, denote by

K_{n}:\mathsf{X}_{0:n}\times\mathcal{X}_{0:n}\ni(z_{0:n},A)\mapsto\iiint\mathbbm{1}_{A}(\tilde{z}_{0:n})\,\mathbb{C}_{n}(z_{0:n},\mathrm{d}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0:n})\,\mathbb{S}_{n}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0:n},\mathrm{d}\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{n})\,\mu(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0:n|n})(\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}_{0:n})

the path-marginalised version of
\mathbb{K}_{n}
. By
Proposition

2
it holds that
K_{n}=\mathbb{C}_{n}\mathbb{B}_{n}
, which shows that
K_{n}
coincides with the Markov transition kernel of the backward-sampling-based particle Gibbs sampler discussed in
Section

2.3
.

Finally, in order prepare for the statement of our theoretical results on the
PPG
we need to introduce the following Feynman–Kac path model
with a frozen path
. More precisely, for a given path
z_{0:n}\in\mathsf{X}_{0:n}
, define, for every
m\in\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket
, the unnormalised kernel

Q_{m}\langle z_{m+1}\rangle:\mathsf{X}_{m}\times\mathcal{X}_{m+1}\ni(x_{m},A)\mapsto\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)Q_{m}(x_{m},A)+\frac{1}{N}g_{m}(x_{m})\,\delta_{z_{m+1}}(A)

and the initial distribution
\eta_{0}\langle z_{0}\rangle:\mathcal{X}_{0}\ni A\mapsto(1-1/N)\eta_{0}(A)+\delta_{z_{0}}(A)/N
.
Given these quantities, define, for
m\in\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket
,

\gamma_{{m}}\langle z_{0:m}\rangle\coloneqq\eta_{0}\langle z_{0}\rangle Q_{0}\langle z_{1}\rangle\cdots Q_{m-1}\langle z_{m}\rangle

along with the normalised counterpart

\eta_{m}\langle z_{0:m}\rangle\coloneqq\gamma_{{m}}\langle z_{0:m}\rangle/\gamma_{{m}}\langle z_{0:m}\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathsf{X}_{0:m}}}
.
Finally, we introduce, for
m\in\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket
, the kernels

B_{m}\langle z_{0:m-1}\rangle:\mathsf{X}_{m}\times\mathcal{X}_{0:m-1}\ni(x_{m},A)\mapsto\idotsint\mathbbm{1}_{A}(x_{0:n-1})\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\overleftarrow{Q}_{m,\eta_{m}\langle z_{0:m}\rangle}(x_{m+1},\mathrm{d}x_{m}),

as well as the path model
\eta_{0:m}\langle z_{0:m}\rangle\coloneqq B_{m}\langle z_{0:m-1}\rangle\varotimes\eta_{m}\langle z_{0:m}\rangle
.

4
Main results

4.1
Theoretical results

This section will be devoted to establishing our main result, namely the exponentially contracting bias bound stated in
Theorem

2
below. This result will be proved under the following strong mixing assumptions, which are standard in the literature (see
[
12
,
18
,
13
,
16
]
).

A 4.1

(strong mixing)
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
\underaccent{\bar}{\tau}_{n}
,
\bar{\tau}_{n}
,
\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}_{n}
, and
\bar{\sigma}_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\ast}
such that

(i)

\underaccent{\bar}{\tau}_{n}\leq g_{n}(x_{n})\leq\bar{\tau}_{n}

Proof.

Pick arbitrarily
\varphi\in\mathsf{F}(\mathcal{X}_{n:n+1})
and write, using definition (
2.3
) and the fact that
Q_{n}
has a transition density,

\displaystyle\iint\varphi(x_{n:n+1})\,\gamma_{{n}}(\mathrm{d}x_{n})\,Q_{n}(x_{n},\mathrm{d}x_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\iint\varphi(x_{n:n+1})\gamma_{{n}}[q_{n}(\cdot,x_{n+1})]\,\lambda_{n+1}(\mathrm{d}x_{n+1})\,\frac{\gamma_{{n}}(\mathrm{d}x_{n})q_{n}(x_{n},x_{n+1})}{\gamma_{{n}}[q_{n}(\cdot,x_{n+1})]}

\displaystyle=\iint\varphi(x_{n:n+1})\,\gamma_{{n+1}}(\mathrm{d}x_{n+1})\,\overleftarrow{Q}_{n,\eta_{n}}(x_{n+1},\mathrm{d}x_{n}).

(5.8)

Now, by (
2.10
) it holds that

B_{n+1}h_{n+1}(x_{n+1})=\int\overleftarrow{Q}_{n,\eta_{n}}(x_{n+1},\mathrm{d}x_{n})\left(\tilde{h}_{n}(x_{n:n+1})+\int h_{n}(x_{0:n})\,B_{n}(x_{n},\mathrm{d}x_{0:n-1})\right);

therefore, by applying (
5.8
) with

\varphi(x_{n:n+1})\coloneqq f_{n+1}(x_{n+1})\left(\tilde{h}_{n}(x_{n:n+1})+\int h_{n}(x_{0:n})\,B_{n}(x_{n},\mathrm{d}x_{0:n-1})\right)

we obtain that

\displaystyle\gamma_{{n+1}}(f_{n+1}B_{n+1}h_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\iint\varphi(x_{n:n+1})\,\gamma_{{n+1}}(\mathrm{d}x_{n+1})\,\overleftarrow{Q}_{n,\eta_{n}}(x_{n+1},\mathrm{d}x_{n})

\displaystyle=\iint\varphi(x_{n:n+1})\,\gamma_{{n}}(\mathrm{d}x_{n})\,Q_{n}(x_{n},\mathrm{d}x_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{{n}}(Q_{n}f_{n+1}B_{n}h_{n}+Q_{n}\tilde{h}_{n}f_{n+1}).

Now the proof is concluded by noting that since
\gamma_{{n+1}}=\gamma_{{n}}Q_{n}
,
\gamma_{{n+1}}\tilde{f}_{n+1}=\gamma_{{n}}Q_{n}\tilde{f}_{n+1}
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
,
h_{n}\in\mathsf{F}(\mathcal{Y}_{n})
, and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}\in\mathsf{M}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{0})
it holds that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}[h_{n}(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n})\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0|0},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n|n}]=\mathbb{S}_{n}h_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0|0},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n|n}),\quad\mathbb{P}^{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}\mbox{-a.s.}

Proof.

Pick arbitrarily
v_{n}\in\mathsf{F}(\mathcal{X}_{0:n})
. We show that

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}[v_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0|0},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n|n})h_{n}(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n})]=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}[v_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0|0},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n|n})\mathbb{S}_{n}h_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0|0},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n|n})],

(5.9)

from which the claim follows. Using the definition (
5.7
), the left-hand side
of the previous identity may be rewritten as

\displaystyle\idotsint\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0}[\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}](\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{0})\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{P}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y}_{m},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{m+1})\,h_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{n})v_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0|0},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n|n})

\displaystyle=\idotsint\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{0|0})\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}{\boldsymbol{M}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}_{m|m},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{m+1})\,\boldsymbol{S}_{0}(\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{x}_{0|0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{1})

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{S}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y}_{m},\boldsymbol{x}_{m+1},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{m+1})\,h_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{n})v_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0|0},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n|n})

\displaystyle=\idotsint\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}{\boldsymbol{M}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}_{m},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{m+1})\,\boldsymbol{S}_{0}(\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{1})

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{S}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y}_{m},\boldsymbol{x}_{m+1},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{m+1})\,h_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{n})v_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}).

Thus, we may conclude the proof by using the definition (
3.2
) of
\mathbb{S}_{n}
together with Fubini’s theorem.
∎

Lemma 4
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
and
h_{n}\in\mathsf{F}(\mathcal{Y}_{n})
it holds that

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}\left[\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right)h_{n}(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n})\right]=\int\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0:n}\mathbb{S}_{n}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{n})\,h_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{n}).

Proof.

The claim of the lemma is a direct implication of
Lemma

3
; indeed, by applying the tower property and the latter we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}\left[\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right)h_{n}(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}\left[\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right)\mathbb{S}_{n}h_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0|0},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n|n})\right]

\displaystyle=\idotsint\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}_{m})\,{\boldsymbol{M}}_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}_{m},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{m+1})\,\mathbb{S}_{n}h_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:n})

\displaystyle=\int\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0:n}\mathbb{S}_{n}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{n})\,h_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{n}).

∎

Proposition 6
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
,
(N,M)\in(\mathbb{N}^{\ast})^{2}
, and
(f_{n},\tilde{f}_{n})\in\mathsf{F}(\mathcal{X}_{n})^{2}
,

\int\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0:n}\mathbb{S}_{n}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{n})\,\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{b_{n}^{i}f_{n}(x_{n|n}^{i})+\tilde{f}_{n}(x_{n|n}^{i})\}\right)=\gamma_{{n}}(f_{n}B_{n}h_{n}+\tilde{f}_{n}).

Proof.

Applying
Lemma

4
yields

\int\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0:n}\mathbb{S}_{n}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{n})\,\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{b_{n}^{i}f_{n}(x_{n|n}^{i})+\tilde{f}_{n}(x_{n|n}^{i})\}\right)\\
=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}\left[\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right)\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{\beta_{n}^{i}f_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{i})+\tilde{f}_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{i})\}\right]\,.

(5.10)

In the following we will use repeatedly the following filtrations. Let

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\coloneqq\sigma(\{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{m}\}_{m=0}^{n})

be the
\sigma
-field generated by the output of the
PARIS
(
Algorithm

1
) during the first
n
iterations. In addition, let
\mathcal{F}_{n}\coloneqq\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}\vee\sigma(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n|n})
.

We proceed by induction. Thus, assume that the statement of the proposition holds true for a given
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
and consider, for arbitrarily chosen
(f_{n+1},\tilde{f}_{n+1})\in\mathsf{F}(\mathcal{X}_{n+1})^{2}
,

{\mathbb{E}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}\left[\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right)\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{\beta_{n+1}^{i}f_{n+1}(\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{i})+\tilde{f}_{n+1}(\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{i})\}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\right]\\
=\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right){\mathbb{E}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}[\beta_{n+1}^{1}f_{n+1}(\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{1})+\tilde{f}_{n+1}(\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{1})\mid\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}]\,,

where we used that the variables
\{\beta_{n+1}^{i}f_{n+1}(\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{i})+\tilde{f}_{n+1}(\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
. Note that, by symmetry,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}\left[\beta_{n+1}^{1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n+1}\right]

\displaystyle=\int\boldsymbol{S}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{n},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n+1|n+1},\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{n+1})\,b_{n+1}^{1}

\displaystyle=\idotsint\left(\prod_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{q_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell},\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{1})}{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell^{\prime}},\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{1})}\delta_{(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell},\beta_{n}^{\ell})}(\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}_{n}^{1,j},\mathrm{d}\tilde{b}_{n}^{1,j})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\times\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left(\tilde{b}_{n}^{1,j}+\tilde{h}_{n}(\tilde{x}_{n}^{1,j},\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{q_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell},\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{1})}{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell^{\prime}},\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{1})}\left(\beta_{n}^{\ell}+\tilde{h}_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell},\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{1})\right).

(5.11)

Thus, using the tower property,

{\mathbb{E}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}\left[\beta_{n+1}^{1}f_{n+1}(\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{1})\mid\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\right]\\
=\int\Phi_{n}(\mu(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n|n}))(\mathrm{d}x_{n+1})\,f_{n+1}(x_{n+1})\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{q_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell},x_{n+1})}{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell^{\prime}},x_{n+1})}\left(\beta_{n}^{\ell}+\tilde{h}_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell},x_{n+1})\right),

and consequently, using definition (
2.1
),

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right){\mathbb{E}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}\left[\beta_{n+1}^{1}f_{n+1}(\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{1})\mid\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right)\int\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{i},x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\hskip 17.07164pt\times f_{n+1}(x_{n+1})\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{q_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell},x_{n+1})}{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell^{\prime}},x_{n+1})}\left(\beta_{n}^{\ell}+\tilde{h}_{n}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell},x_{n+1})\right)\,\lambda_{n+1}(\mathrm{d}x_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right)\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\left(\beta_{n}^{\ell}Q_{n}f_{n+1}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell})+Q_{n}(\tilde{h}_{n}f_{n+1})(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell})\right).

Thus, applying the induction hypothesis,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}\left[\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right)\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{n+1}^{i}f_{n+1}(\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}\left[\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right)\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\left(\beta_{n}^{\ell}Q_{n}f_{n+1}(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell})+Q_{n}(\tilde{h}_{n}f_{n+1})(\xi_{n|n}^{\ell})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma_{{n}}\left(Q_{n}f_{n+1}B_{n}h_{n}+Q_{n}(\tilde{h}_{n}f_{n+1})\right).

(5.12)

In the same manner, it can be shown that

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}\left[\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right)\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{f}_{n+1}(\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{i})\right]=\gamma_{{n}}Q_{n}\tilde{f}_{n+1}.

(5.13)

Now, by (
5.12
–
5.13
) and
Lemma

2
,

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}\left[\left(\prod_{m=0}^{n}\boldsymbol{g}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m|m})\right)\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{\beta_{n+1}^{i}f_{n+1}(\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{i})+\tilde{f}_{n+1}(\xi_{n+1|n+1}^{i})\}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt=\gamma_{{n}}\left(Q_{n}f_{n+1}B_{n}h_{n}+Q_{n}(\tilde{h}_{n}f_{n+1}+Q_{n}\tilde{f}_{n+1})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt=\gamma_{{n+1}}(f_{n+1}B_{n+1}h_{n+1}+\tilde{f}_{n+1}),

which shows that the claim of the proposition holds at time
n+1
.

It remains to check the base case
n=0
, which holds trivially true as
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
,
B_{0}h_{0}=0
by convention, and the initial particles
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0|0}
are drawn from
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}
. This completes the proof.
∎

A
Numerical results

Given measurable spaces
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{X})
and
(\mathsf{Z},\mathcal{Z})
, a
hidden Markov model
(HMM) is a bivariate stochastic process
\{(X_{m},Z_{m})\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
where
(X_{m},Z_{m})
takes on values in
(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Z},\mathcal{X}\varotimes\mathcal{Z})
and where
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
(the
state sequence
) is a (possibly unnormalised) Markov chain which is only partially observed through the observation process
\{Z_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
. We denote by
\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\chi
the Markov transition kernels and initial distribution of
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, respectively. Conditionally on the unobserved state sequence, the observations are assumed to be independent and such that the conditional distribution of each
Z_{m}
only depends on the corresponding state
X_{m}
and has a density
g_{m}(X_{m},\cdot)
with respect to some dominating measure. HMMs are used today in a variety of scientific and engineering disciplines; see
[
2
,
9
,
10
]
. Inference in HMMs typically involves the computation of conditional distributions of unobserved states given observations. Of particular interest are the sequences of
filter distributions
, where the filter at time
m\in\mathbb{N}
, denoted
\eta_{m}
, is defined as the conditional distribution of
X_{m}
given
Z_{{0:m}}\coloneqq(Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{m})
, and the
joint-smoothing distributions
, where the joint-smoothing distribution at time
m
, denoted
\eta_{0:m}
, is defined as the joint conditional distribution of the states
X_{{0:m}}=(X_{0},\ldots,X_{m})
given
Z_{{0:m}}
. Consequently,
\eta_{m}
is the marginal of
\eta_{0:m}
with respect to the last state
X_{m}
. Given a sequence
\{z_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
of fixed observations, it can be shown (see
[
9
, Section 3]
for details) that
\{\eta_{0:m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a Feynman–Kac model as defined in
Section

1
with Markov kernels
\{M_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
potential functions
g_{m}\coloneqq g(\cdot,z_{m})
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, on
\mathsf{X}
.

We now numerically assess the proposed algorithm on two different models, namely (i) a linear Gaussian state-space model (for which the filter and joint-smoothing distribution flows are available in a closed form) and (ii) a stochastic volatility model proposed in
[

\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\{\beta_{n-1}^{\ell}Q_{n-1}f_{n}(\xi_{n-1}^{\ell})+Q_{n-1}(\tilde{h}_{n-1}f_{n}+\tilde{f}_{n})(\xi_{n-1}^{\ell})\}}{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N}g_{n-1}(\xi_{n-1}^{\ell^{\prime}})}\\
\leq\upkappa_{n}

and
\eta_{n-1}\langle z_{0:n-1}\rangle g_{n-1}\geq\underaccent{\bar}{\tau}_{n-1}
,
the inequality

\mathbb{P}^{\boldsymbol{P},z}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}\left(|\operatorname{I}^{(2)}_{N}|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\mathsf{c}_{n-1}\exp\left(-\mathsf{d}_{n-1}\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{\tau}_{n-1}^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{\bar{\tau}_{n-1}^{2}\upkappa^{2}_{n}}N\right).

(C.8)

The last term
\operatorname{I}^{(3)}_{N}
is treated along similar lines; indeed, by the induction hypothesis, since
\|q_{n-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{\tau}_{n-1}\bar{\sigma}_{n-1}
,

\mathbb{P}^{\boldsymbol{P},z}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}\left(\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}q_{n-1}(\xi_{n-1}^{\ell},z_{n})\left(\beta_{n-1}^{\ell}+\tilde{h}_{n-1}(\xi_{n-1}^{\ell},z_{n})\right)\right.\right.\\
\left.\left.\vphantom{\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}}-\eta_{n-1}\langle z_{0:n-1}\rangle[q_{n-1}(\cdot,z_{n})\{B_{n-1}\langle z_{0:n-1}\rangle h_{n-1}(\cdot)+\tilde{h}_{n-1}(\cdot,z_{n})\}]\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\\
\leq\mathsf{c}_{n-1}\exp\left(-\mathsf{d}_{n-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\bar{\tau}_{n-1}\bar{\sigma}_{n-1}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\|\tilde{h}_{m}\|_{\infty}}\right)^{2}N\right)

and

\mathbb{P}^{\boldsymbol{P},z}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}\left(\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}q_{n-1}(\xi_{n-1}^{\ell},z_{n})-\eta_{n-1}\langle z_{0:n-1}\rangle[q_{n-1}(\cdot,z_{n})]\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\\
\leq\mathsf{c}_{n-1}\exp\left(-\mathsf{d}_{n-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\bar{\tau}_{n-1}\bar{\sigma}_{n-1}}\right)^{2}N\right).

Thus, since

\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{q_{n-1}(\xi_{n-1}^{\ell},z_{n})}{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{N}q_{n-1}(\xi_{n-1}^{\ell^{\prime}},z_{n})}\left(\beta_{n-1}^{\ell}+\tilde{h}_{n-1}(\xi_{n-1}^{\ell},z_{n})\right)\leq\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\|\tilde{h}_{m}\|_{\infty}

and
\eta_{n-1}\langle z_{0:n-1}\rangle[q_{n-1}(\cdot,z_{n})]\geq\underaccent{\bar}{\tau}_{n-1}
, the generalised Hoeffding inequality provides

\mathbb{P}^{\boldsymbol{P},z}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}\left(|\operatorname{I}^{(3)}_{N}|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\mathsf{c}_{n-1}\exp\left(-\mathsf{d}_{n-1}\left(\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{\tau}_{n-1}\varepsilon}{2\bar{\tau}_{n-1}\bar{\sigma}_{n-1}\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\|\tilde{h}_{m}\|_{\infty}}\right)^{2}N\right).

(C.9)

Finally, combining the bounds (
C.7
–
C.9
) completes the proof. ∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
X
be an
\mathbb{R}^{d}

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, satisfying
\mathbb{P}(|X|\geq t)\leq c\exp(-t^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))
for every
t\geq 0
and some
c>0
and
\sigma>0
. Then for every

it holds that
\mathbb{E}[|X|^{p}]\leq cp^{p/2}\sigma^{p}

Proof.

Using Fubini’s theorem and the change of variable formula,

\mathbb{E}\left[|X|^{p}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}pt^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(|X|\geq t)\,\mathrm{d}t=cp2^{p/2-1}\sigma^{p}\Gamma(p/2),

where
\Gamma
is the Gamma function. It remains to apply the bound
\Gamma(p/2)\leq(p/2)^{p/2-1}
(see
[
1
]
), which holds for

∎

7

Proof of

Like previously, we establish

Proposition 9

For every

,

where
\upkappa_{n}
is defined in (

We preface the proof of
Proposition

9
by a technical lemma providing a bound on the bias of ratios of random variables.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\upalpha
and
\upbeta
be (possibly dependent) random variables defined on some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and such that
\mathbb{E}[\upalpha^{2}]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}[\upbeta^{2}]<\infty
. Moreover, assume that there exist
c>0
and
d>0
such that
|\upalpha/\upbeta|\leq c
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
|a/b|\leq c

\mathbb{E}[(\upalpha-a)^{2}]\leq c^{2}d^{2}
, and
\mathbb{E}[(\upbeta-b)^{2}] \leq d^{2}

\left|\mathbb{E}[\upalpha/\upbeta]-a/b\right|\leq 2c(d/b)^{2}+c|\mathbb{E}[\upbeta-b]|/|b|+|\mathbb{E}[\upalpha-a]|/|b|.

(C.10)

Proof.

Using the identity

\mathbb{E}[\upalpha/\upbeta]-a/b=\mathbb{E}[(\upalpha/\upbeta)(b-\upbeta)^{2}]/b^{2}+\mathbb{E}[(\upalpha-a)(b-\upbeta)]/b^{2}+a\mathbb{E}[b-\upbeta]/b^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\upalpha-a]/b,

the claim is established by applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the assumptions of the lemma according to

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}[\upalpha/\upbeta]-a/b\right|

\displaystyle\leq c\mathbb{E}[(\upbeta-b)^{2}]/b^{2}+\{\mathbb{E}[(\upalpha-a)^{2}]\mathbb{E}[(\upbeta-b)^{2}]\}^{1/2}/b^{2}+|a||\mathbb{E}[\upbeta-b]|/b^{2}+|\mathbb{E}[\upalpha-a]|/b^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2c(d/b)^{2}+c|\mathbb{E}[\upbeta-b]|/|b|+|\mathbb{E}[\upalpha-a]|/|b|.

∎
