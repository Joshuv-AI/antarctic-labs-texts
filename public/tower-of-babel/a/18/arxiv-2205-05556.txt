Abstract
In theoretical ecology, models describing the spatial dispersal and the temporal evolution of species having non-overlapping generations are often based on integrodifference equations. For various such applications the environment has an aperiodic influence on the models leading to nonautonomous integrodifference equations. In order to capture their long-term behaviour comprehensively, both pullback and forward attractors, as well as forward limit sets are constructed for general infinite-dimensional nonautonomous dynamical systems in discrete time. While the theory of pullback attractors, but not their application to integrodifference equations, is meanwhile well-established, the present novel approach is needed in order to understand their future behaviour.

Introduction

Integrodifference equations not only occur as temporal discretisations of integrodifferential equations or as time-
1
-maps of evolutionary differential equations, but are of interest in themselves. First and foremost, they are a popular tool in theoretical ecology to describe the dispersal of species having non-overlapping generations (see, for instance,
lutscher:19
or
kot:schaeffer:86
;
volkov:lui:07
;
jacobsen:jin:lewis:15
). While the theory of Urysohn or Hammerstein integral equations is now rather classical
martin:76
, both numerically and analytically, our goal is here to study their iterates from a dynamical systems perspective. This means one is interested in the long term behaviour of recursions based on a fixed nonlinear integral operator. In applications, the iterates for instance represent the spatial distribution of interacting species over a habitat. One of the central questions in this context is the existence and structure of an attractor. These invariant and compact sets attract bounded subsets of an ambient state space
X
and fully capture the asymptotics of an autonomous dynamical system
hale:88
;
sell:you:02
. The dynamics inside the attractor can be very complicated and even chaotic
day:junge:mischaikow:04
.

Extending this situation, the main part of this paper is devoted to general nonautonomous difference equations in complete metric spaces. Their right-hand side can depend on time allowing to model the dispersal of species in temporally fluctuating environments
bouhours:lewis:16
;
jacobsen:jin:lewis:15
being not necessarily periodic. Thus, the behaviour depends on both the initial and the actual time. This is why many dynamically relevant objects are contained in the extended state space
{\mathbb{Z}}\times X
(one speaks of nonautonomous sets)
johnson:munoz:09
, rather than being merely subsets of the state space
X
as in the autonomous case. Furthermore, a complete description of the dynamics in a time-variant setting necessitates a strict distinction between forward and pullback convergence
johnson:munoz:09
;
kloeden:rasmussen:10
. For this reason only a combination of several attractor notions yields the full picture:

•

The
pullback attractor

carvalho:langa:robinson:10
;
kloeden:00
;
kloeden:rasmussen:10
;
poetzsche:10b
is a compact, invariant nonautonomous set which attracts all bounded sets from the past. As fixed target problem, it is based on previous information, at a fixed time from increasingly earlier initial times. Since it consists of bounded entire solutions to a nonautonomous system (see
(
poetzsche:10b
, p. 17, Cor. 1.3.4)
), a pullback attractor can be seen as an extension of the global attractor to nonautonomous problems and apparently captures the essential dynamics to a certain point. Meanwhile the corresponding theory is widely developed in discrete and continuous time. However, pullback attractors reflect the past rather than the future of systems (see
kloeden:poetzsche:rasmussen:11
) and easy examples demonstrate that differential or difference equations with identical pullback attractors might have rather different asymptotics as
t\to\infty
and possibly feature limit sets, which are not captured by the pullback dynamics.

•

This led to the development of
forward attractors
, which are also compact and invariant nonautonomous sets
kloeden:rasmussen:10
. This dual concept depends on information from the future and given a fixed initial time, the actual time increases beyond all bounds — they are a moving target problem. Forward attractors are not unique, independent of pullback attractors, but often do not exist. Nevertheless, we will describe forward attractors using a pullback construction, even though this has the disadvantage that information on the system over the entire time axis
{\mathbb{Z}}
is required.

•

Therefore, it was suggested in
kloeden:lorenz:16
to work with
forward limit sets
, a concept related to the uniform attractor due to
vishik:92
. They correctly describe the asymptotic behaviour of all forward solutions to a nonautonomous difference equation. These limit sets have forward attraction properties, but different from pullback and forward attractors, they are not (even positively) invariant and constitute a single compact set, rather than a nonautonomous set. Nonetheless, asymptotic forms of positive (and negative) invariance do hold.

The situation for forward attractors and limit sets is not as well-established as their pullback counterparts and deserves to be developed for the above reasons. Their initial construction in
kloeden:lorenz:16
;
kloeden:yang:16
requires a locally compact state space, but recent continuous-time results in
cui:kloeden:yang:19
, which extend these to infinite-dimensional dynamical systems, will be transferred here. We indeed address nonautonomous difference equations in (not necessarily locally compact) metric and Banach spaces, introduce the mentioned attractor types and study their properties.

This brings us to our second purpose. The above abstract setting allows concrete applications to a particularly interesting class of infinite-dimensional dynamical systems in discrete time, namely integrodifference equations (IDEs for short). We provide sufficient criteria for the existence of pullback attractors tailor-made for a quite general class of IDEs. Their right-hand sides go beyond pure integral operators and might also include superposition operators, which are used to describe populations having a sedentary fraction. Such results follow from a corresponding theory of set contractions contained in
(
poetzsche:10b
, pp. 15ff)
,
(
martin:76
, pp. 79ff)
. For completely continuous right-hand sides (i.e., Urysohn operators) we construct forward limit sets and provide an application to asymptotically autonomous IDEs. We restrict to rather simple IDEs in the space of continuous functions over a compact domain as state space. More complicated equations and the behaviour of attractors under spatial discretisation will be tackled in future papers.

The contents of this paper are as follows: In Sect.
2
we establish the necessary terminology and provide a useful dissipativity condition for nonautonomous difference equations. The key notions related to pullback convergence, i.e., limit sets and attractors are reviewed and established in Sect.
3
. The subsequent Sect.
4
addresses the corresponding notions in forward time. In detail, it establishes forward limit sets and their (weakened) invariance properties. For a class of asymptotically autonomous equations it is shown that their forward limit sets coincide with the global attractor of the limit equation. Moreover, a construction of forward attractors is suggested. Finally, in Sect.
5
we provide some applications to various IDEs. In particular, we illustrate the above theoretical results by studying pullback attractors and forward limit sets.

2.1
Semilinear difference equations

Let

Then (

Lemma 1

(linear growth)

In case

Additive difference equations

3.1
Pullback limit sets

The

3.2
Pullback attractors

4
Forward convergence

In the previous section, we constructed pullback attractors of pullback asymptotically compact nonautonomous difference equations (

Δ

) as pullback limit sets of such absorbing sets. Our next aim is to provide related notions in forward time. Due to the conceptional difference between pullback and forward convergence some modifications are necessary, yet.

Above all, this requires a discrete interval
{\mathbb{I}}
to be unbounded above. Now the right-hand sides
{\mathscr{F}}_{t}:U_{t}\to U_{t+1}
,
t\in{\mathbb{I}}
, are defined on a common closed subset
U_{t}=U\subseteq X
, i.e., the extended state space
{\mathcal{U}}={\mathbb{I}}\times U
has constant fibres. Therefore, the general solution
\varphi:\left\{(t,\tau,u)\in{\mathbb{I}}\times{\mathcal{U}}:\,\tau\leq t\right\}\to U
is well-defined.

Given a nonautonomous set
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}
, a difference equation (

Δ

) is said to be

•

{\mathcal{A}}
-asymptotically compact
, if there exists a compact set
K\subseteq U
such that
K
forward attracts
{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\varphi(\tau+s;\tau,{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)),K\bigr)=0\quad\text{for all }\tau\in{\mathbb{I}},

•

strongly
{\mathcal{A}}
-asymptotically compact
, if there exists a compact set
K\subseteq U
so that every sequence
\bigl((s_{n},\tau_{n})\bigr)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{I}}
with
s_{n}\to\infty
,
\tau_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\varphi(\tau_{n}+s_{n};\tau_{n},{\mathcal{A}}(\tau_{n})),K\bigr)=0.

Remark 3

If
{\mathcal{A}}
is positively invariant, then strong
{\mathcal{A}}
-asymptotic compactness (needed in Thm.
4.3
below) is a tightening of
{\mathcal{A}}
-asymptotic compactness (required in Thm.
4.2
). Indeed, suppose that the sequence
(\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\varphi(t_{n};\tau,{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)),K\bigr))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
does not converge to
0
. Hence, the strong
{\mathcal{A}}
-asymptotic compactness of (

Δ

) and positive invariance of
{\mathcal{A}}
yields the contradiction

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\varphi(t_{n};\tau,{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)),K\bigr)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}\bigl({\mathscr{F}}_{t_{n}}(\varphi(t_{n}-1;\tau,{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)),K\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{dist}\bigl({\mathscr{F}}_{t_{n}}({\mathcal{A}}(t_{n}-1)),K\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

4.1
Forward limit sets

Let us investigate the forward dynamics of (

Δ

) inside a nonautonomous set
{\mathcal{A}}
. We first capture the forward limit points from a single fibre
{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
:

Lemma 3

Suppose that
{\mathcal{A}}\neq\emptyset
is a bounded nonautonomous set. If (

Δ

) is
{\mathcal{A}}
-asymptotically compact with a compact subset
K\subseteq U
, then the fibres

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tau):=\bigcap_{0\leq s}\overline{\bigcup_{s\leq t}\varphi(\tau+t;\tau,{\mathcal{A}}(\tau))}\subseteq K\quad\text{for all }\tau\in{\mathbb{I}}

(16)

are nonempty, compact, and forward attract
{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
, i.e.,

\lim_{s\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\varphi(\tau+s;\tau,{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)),\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)\bigr)=0.

(17)

An analogous result for pullback limit sets is given in
(
poetzsche:10b
, p. 9, Lemma 1.2.12)
.

Remark 4
(characterisation of
\Omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\tau)
)

The fibres
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
,
\tau\in{\mathbb{I}}
, consist of points
v
such that there is a sequence
\bigl((s_{n},a_{n})\bigr)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}=\infty
,
a_{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi(\tau+s_{n};\tau,a_{n})=v.

(18)

This readily yields the monotonicity
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}_{2}

\Rightarrow

\Omega_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}(\tau)\subseteq\Omega_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}(\tau)
for all
\tau\in{\mathbb{I}}
.

Proof

Let
\tau\in{\mathbb{I}}
. Given a sequence
y_{n}:=\varphi(\tau+s_{n};\tau,a_{n})\in\varphi(\tau+s_{n};\tau,{\mathcal{A}}(\tau))
with
s_{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty
and
a_{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
, by the
{\mathcal{A}}
-asymptotic compactness of (

Δ

), we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq\operatorname{dist}\bigl(y_{n},K\bigr)\leq\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\varphi(\tau+s_{n},\tau,{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)),K\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Since
K\subseteq U
is compact,
\operatorname{dist}\bigl(y_{n},K\bigr)=\min_{k\in K}d(y_{n},k)
. This implies that there exist a sequence
(k_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
K
satisfying

\displaystyle d(y_{n},k_{n})=\operatorname{dist}\bigl(y_{n},K\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

and a subsequence
(k_{n_{j}})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converging to
\bar{k}\in K
. Thus,

\displaystyle 0\leq d\bigl(y_{n_{j}},\bar{k}\bigr)\leq d\bigl(y_{n_{j}},k_{n_{j}}\bigr)+d\bigl(k_{n_{j}},\bar{k}\bigr)=\operatorname{dist}\bigl(y_{n_{j}},K\bigr)+d\bigl(k_{n_{j}},\bar{k}\bigr)\xrightarrow[j\to\infty]{}0,

which implies that the subsequence
(y_{n_{j}})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges to
\bar{k}
. Hence, by the characterisation (
18
),
\bar{k}\in\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
, i.e.,
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
is nonempty.

Now choose a sequence
(v_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
. By Rem.
4
, for each fixed
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there is a sequence
\bigl((s_{m}^{n},a_{m}^{n})\bigr)_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
satisfying
\lim_{m\to\infty}s_{m}^{n}=\infty
and
a_{m}^{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\varphi(\tau+s_{m}^{n};\tau,a_{m}^{n})=v_{n},

i.e., for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
M=M(n,\varepsilon)\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\displaystyle d\bigl(\varphi(\tau+s_{m}^{n};\tau,a_{m}^{n}),v_{n}\bigr)<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }m\geq M.

Since
\lim_{m\to\infty}s_{m}^{n}=\infty
, there is a
M^{\prime}=M^{\prime}(n)\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
s_{m}^{n}>n
for all
m\geq M^{\prime}
. Pick
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{n}
,
(\bar{s}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
as a subsequence
(s_{m_{n}}^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
(s_{m}^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
(\bar{a}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
as a subsequence
(a_{m_{n}}^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
(a_{m}^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in such a way that

\displaystyle m_{1}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{1,M,M^{\prime}\right\}+1,

\displaystyle m_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{1,M,M^{\prime},m_{n}\right\}+1\quad\text{for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Clearly,
m_{n+1}>\max\left\{1,M,M^{\prime},m_{n}\right\}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Hence, we constructed a sequence
\bigl((\bar{s}_{n},\bar{a}_{n})\bigr)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bar{s}_{n}=\infty
and
\bar{a}_{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
such that

\displaystyle d\bigl(\varphi(\tau+\bar{s}_{n};\tau,\bar{a}_{n}),v_{n}\bigr)<\tfrac{1}{n}\quad\text{for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle 0\leq\operatorname{dist}\bigl(v_{n},K\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq d\bigl(\varphi(\tau+\bar{s}_{n};\tau,\bar{a}_{n}),v_{n}\bigr)+\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\varphi(\tau+\bar{s}_{n};\tau,{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)),K\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{n}+\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\varphi(\tau+\bar{s}_{n};\tau,{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)),K\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Similarly as above, because
K
is compact, there is a subsequence
(v_{n_{j}})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converging to
\bar{v}\in K
. Moreover, since
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
is closed by definition,
\bar{v}\in\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
, which implies that
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
is compact. Also note that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}\bigl(v_{n},K\bigr)=0
, so
v_{n}\in K
, i.e.,
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)\subseteq K
.

Suppose that
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})
does not forward attract
{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})
for some
\tilde{\tau}\in{\mathbb{I}}
, i.e., there exist a real
\tilde{\varepsilon}>0
and a sequence
(\tilde{s}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{s}_{n}=\infty
and

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\varphi(\tilde{\tau}+\tilde{s}_{n};\tilde{\tau},{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})),\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})\bigr)\geq\tilde{\varepsilon}\quad\text{for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(19)

Although the supremum in the Hausdorff semidistance in the left-hand side of (
19
) may not be attained due to no condition ensuring that the image
\varphi(\tilde{\tau}+s_{n};\tilde{\tau},{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau}))
is compact, there still exists a point
\tilde{y}_{n}:=\varphi(\tilde{\tau}+\tilde{s}_{n};\tilde{\tau},\tilde{a}_{n})\in\varphi(\tilde{\tau}+\tilde{s}_{n};\tilde{\tau},{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau}))
for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
\tilde{a}_{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})
such that

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\varphi(\tilde{\tau}+\tilde{s}_{n};\tilde{\tau},{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})),\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})\bigr)-\tfrac{\tilde{\varepsilon}}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\tilde{y}_{n},\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\varphi(\tilde{\tau}+\tilde{s}_{n};\tilde{\tau},{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})\bigr),\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})).

The above inequalities in fact give
\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\tilde{y}_{n},\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})\bigr)\geq\tfrac{\tilde{\varepsilon}}{2}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. On the other hand, since
\tilde{y}_{n}\in\varphi(\tilde{\tau}+s_{n};\tilde{\tau},{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau}))
, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\tilde{y}_{n},K\bigr)\leq\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\varphi(\tilde{\tau}+\tilde{s}_{n};\tilde{\tau},{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})),K\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0

and thus
\tilde{y}_{n}\in K
. Moreover, since
K
is compact, there is a convergent subsequence
(\tilde{y}_{n_{j}})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with limit
\tilde{y}\in K
. This shows

by definition, and thus

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\tilde{y},\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{\tau})\bigr)<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0,

a contradiction to (
19
). Hence, every
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
must forward attract
{\mathcal{A}}(\tau)
.
∎

Comparing the respective relations (

4.3
Forward attractors

In the previous Sect.

Mimicking the approach for pullback attractors, we define a

one says that

is as a
