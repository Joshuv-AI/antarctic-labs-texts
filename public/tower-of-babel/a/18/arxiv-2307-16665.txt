The time-fractional diffusion-wave equations have remarkably
different properties from the cases
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=2
, and
one distinguished character is less smoothing or averaging effect
for solutions,
which implies that original profiles of initial values can be
preserved more compared with the case
\alpha=1
, but
less compared with
\alpha=2
. Such a difference brings
drastic differences especially for inverse problems for
time-fractional diffusion-wave equations, and such an
example is well-posedness of the backward problems in time, which
is a strong contrast to
\alpha=1
, and we can refer only to
Floridia, Li, Yamamoto
[
4
]
, Floridia and Yamamoto
[
5
]

as a few recent works.

Moreover by such less smoothing effect, we can expect the uniqueness in
simultaneously detemining initial values and source terms, because
the effect from initial values and the one from the source
can be separately reflected in the observation data.
This can not be correct for the cases
\alpha=1
and even
\alpha=2
, as the
following examples show for cases of ordinary differential equations.

Example.

Let
\lambda\neq 0
be an arbitrarily chosen real constant.
Let
a,f\in\mathbb{R}
and

\mu(t)=\chi_{(0,T)}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&1,\quad 0<t<T,\\
&0,\quad t\geq T.\end{array}\right.

(i) Case
\alpha=1
:

We consider

\frac{du}{dt}(t)=-\lambda u(t)+\chi_{(0,T)}(t)f,\quad u(0)=a.

Then

u(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&e^{-\lambda t}a+e^{-\lambda t}\frac{e^{\lambda T}-1}{\lambda}f,\quad t>T,\\
&e^{-\lambda t}a+\frac{1-e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda}f,\quad 0<t<T.\end{array}\right.

Therefore
u(t)=0
for
T_{1}<t<T_{2}
if and only if

a\lambda+(e^{\lambda T}-1)f=0
, which does not imply the
uniqueness for two constants
a,f
.

(ii) Case
\alpha=2
:

We consider

\frac{d^{2}u}{dt^{2}}(t)=-\lambda u(t)+\chi_{(0,T)}(t)f,\quad u(0)=a,\quad\frac{du}{dt}(0)=b

with
\lambda=r^{2}>0
.
Then

\displaystyle u(t)=a\cos rt+b\frac{\sin rt}{r}+f\int^{T}_{0}\frac{\sin r(t-s)}{r}ds=a\cos rt+b\frac{\sin rt}{r}+\frac{\cos r(T-t)-\cos rt}{r^{2}}f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(a+\frac{\cos rT-1}{r^{2}}f\right)\cos rt+\left(\frac{b}{r}+\frac{\sin rT}{r^{2}}f\right)\sin rt,\quad t>T.

Therefore,
u(t)=0
for
T_{1}<t<T_{2}
if and only if

a+\frac{\cos rT-1}{r^{2}}f=\frac{b}{r}+\frac{\sin rT}{r^{2}}f=0.

In general we cannot conclude that
a=b=f=0
.

Preliminaries

It is known that the elliptic operator
A
with the domain

\mathcal{D}(A)=H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
has a countably infinite number of
eigenvalues, and by
c\leq 0
in
\Omega
in (1.3), we can number as

0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots\longrightarrow\infty.

Let
P_{n}
be the eigenprojection for the eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
of

A
:
AP_{n}v=\lambda_{n}P_{n}v
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We write

\|f\|:=\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad(f,g):=\int_{\Omega}f(x)g(x)dx.

Henceforth by
E_{\beta,\gamma}(z)
with
\beta>0
and
\gamma>0
,
we denote the Mittag-Leffler function (e.g., Podlubny
[
24
]
):

E_{\beta,\gamma}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(\beta k+\gamma)},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

The power series can readily verify that
E_{\beta,\gamma}(z)
is an
entire function in
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

Lemma 3 (eigenfunction expansions)
.

Let

[formula]

The series are convergent in the corresponding spaces indicated in
Lemma 1.

The proof can be found for example, in
[
25
]
.

We further show

Lemma 5.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed.
We assume that
p_{1},...,p_{N}
are mutually distinct positive constants
and
a_{1},...,a_{N}\in\mathbb{R}

If

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{a_{k}}{t^{p_{k}}}+O\left(\frac{1}{t^{r}}\right)=0\quad\mbox{as $t\to\infty$},

then
a_{k}=0
as long as
p_{k}<r
with

.

Proof of Lemma 5.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
that
p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{N}
.
We have

a_{1}+\sum_{k=2}^{N}a_{k}\frac{1}{t^{p_{k}-p_{1}}}=O\left(\frac{1}{t^{r-p_{1}}}\right)

as
t\to\infty
. Therefore,
a_{1}=0
.
Next

\frac{a_{2}}{t^{p_{2}}}+\sum_{k=3}^{N}a_{k}\frac{1}{t^{p_{k}}}=O\left(\frac{1}{t^{r}}\right),

that is,

a_{2}+\sum_{k=3}^{N}a_{k}\frac{1}{t^{p_{k}-p_{2}}}=O\left(\frac{1}{t^{r-p_{2}}}\right)

as
t\to\infty
. Therefore,
a_{2}=0
. We can continue this argument
as long as
p_{k}<r
for
k=1,2,...,N
. Thus we
completes the proof of Lemma 5.

\blacksquare

\lambda_{n}
,

Then it is known (e.g., Courant and Hilbert

1.

We have proved the uniqueness in simultaneously determining
factors
f(x)
and
\mu(t)
of a source term and initial values
a

and/or
b
, except for some values of orders
\alpha
.
The proof is by equating the terms
t^{-\alpha m(k)}
, etc.
with the same orders of the asymptotic expansions of the solutions.
Those terms are generated by initial data and a source term:

\int^{T}_{0}(t-s)^{-\alpha m(k)-1}\mu(s)ds
with

(see
(3.5) and (3.6)).
Without conditions (1.6), (1.7), (1.11) and (1.12) on

,
infinitely many terms are overlapped and our argument cannot conclude the
uniqueness. So far, in general, we do not know the uniqueness without such
conditions on
\alpha
.
However in a case of time-fractional ordinary differential equations,
although the separation of the terms is not perfect, we can prove the
uniqueness corresponding to Theorem 2,
thanks to that an unknown initial value is scalar.
More precisely,

Proposition 1.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
be arbitrarily given.
For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we consider

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&d_{t}^{\alpha}u(t)=-\lambda u(t)+\mu(t),\\
&u(0)=a,\quad\mbox{if $0<\alpha<1$},\\
&u(0)=a,\quad\frac{du}{dt}(0)=b,\quad\mbox{if $1<\alpha<2$}.\end{array}\right.

We assume that
\mu(t)
satisfies (1.4), and
T<T_{1}<T_{2}
. Then

(i) Case
0<\alpha<1
: if
u(t)=0
for
T_{1}<t<T_{2}
, then

a=0
and
\mu(t)=0
for
0<t<T
.

(ii) Case
1<\alpha<2
: if
u(t)=0
for
T_{1}<t<T_{2}

and
\mu(t)=0
for
0<t<T
.

Proof of Proposition 1.

It is known (e.g., Kilbas, Srivastava and Trujillo
[
15
]
, p.141) that

[formula]

u(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&aE_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})+\int^{t}_{0}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda(t-s)^{\alpha})\mu(s)ds,\quad 0<\alpha<1,\\
&aE_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})+btE_{\alpha,2}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})\\
+&\int^{t}_{0}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda(t-s)^{\alpha})\mu(s)ds,\quad 1<\alpha<2,\quad t>0.\end{array}\right.

In view of (3.1) with

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})=\frac{(-1)^{m(1)+1}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha m(1))}\frac{1}{\lambda^{m(1)}}\frac{1}{t^{\alpha m(1)}}+O(t^{-\alpha m(2)}),\\
&tE_{\alpha,2}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})=\frac{(-1)^{m(1)+1}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha m(1))}\frac{1}{\lambda^{m(1)}}\frac{1}{t^{\alpha m(1)-1}}+O(t^{-\alpha m(2)+1}),\\
&t^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})=\frac{(-1)^{m(1)}}{\Gamma(-\alpha m(1))}\frac{1}{\lambda^{m(1)+1}}\frac{1}{t^{\alpha m(1)+1}}+O(t^{-\alpha m(2)-1})\end{array}\right.

as

By (5.2) and

[formula]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{m(1)}}{\Gamma(-\alpha m(1))}\frac{1}{\lambda^{m(1)+1}}\int^{T}_{0}(t-s)^{-\alpha m(1)-1}\mu(s)ds+\int^{T}_{0}O((t-s)^{-\alpha m(2)-1})\mu(s)ds,

.
In terms of
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
, we see that

f(x_{0})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(P_{n}f)(x_{0})=0
, which contradicts the
assumption
f(x_{0})\neq 0
. Therefore,
\mu\equiv 0
in
(0,T)
.
Thus the proof of Proposition 2 is complete.

\blacksquare
