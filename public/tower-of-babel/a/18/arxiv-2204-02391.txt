Hamiltonicity after reversing the directed edges at a vertex of a Cartesian product
Dave Witte Morris

Abstract
Let C → m \vec{C}_{m} and C → n \vec{C}_{n} be directed cycles of length m m and n n , with m , n ≥ 3 m,n\geq 3 , and let P ⁡ ( C → m □ C → n ) P(\vec{C}_{m}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n}) be the digraph that is obtained from the Cartesian product C → m □ C → n \vec{C}_{m}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n} by choosing a vertex v v , and reversing the orientation of all four directed edges that are incident with v v . (This operation is called “pushing” at the vertex v v .) By applying a special case of unpublished work of S. X. Wu, we find elementary number-theoretic necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a hamiltonian cycle in P ⁡ ( C → m □ C → n ) P(\vec{C}_{m}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n}) . A consequence is that if P ⁡ ( C → m □ C → n ) P(\vec{C}_{m}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n}) is hamiltonian, then gcd ⁡ ( m , n ) = 1 \gcd(m,n)=1 , which implies that C → m □ C → n \vec{C}_{m}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n} is not hamiltonian. This final conclusion verifies a conjecture of J. B. Klerlein and E. C. Carr. Keywords. hamiltonian cycle, Cartesian product, directed cycle, pushing at a vertex, reverse edges. 2020 AMS Classification. 05C45, 05C20, 05C76.

Statement of the main result

This note explains that a special case of unpublished work of S. X. Wu
[
11
]
(or slightly later published work of S. J. Curran et al.
[
1
]
) provides the following elementary number-theoretic necessary and sufficient conditions for
P(\vec{C}_{m}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n})
to be hamiltonian.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
\vec{C}_{m}
and
\vec{C}_{n}
be directed cycles of length
\geq 3
. The digraph
P(\vec{C}_{m}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n})
has a hamiltonian cycle if and only if

(1)

\gcd(m,n)=1
,

(2)

\min\{\,a_{m,n}(0,-2),\ a_{m,n}(-2,0)\,\}<\min\bigl\{\,a_{m,n}(0,-1),\,a_{m,n}(-1,0)\,\bigr\}
,
and

(3)

\displaystyle\gcd\left(\frac{a_{m,n}(0,-4)}{m},\ \frac{a_{m,n}(-4,0)}{n}\right)=1
.

If
\gcd(m,n)=1
, then it is well known (and easy to see) that
\vec{C}_{m}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n}
is not hamiltonian
[
3
, Thm. 28.1, p. 510]
. Therefore, the

has the following consequence, which was conjectured by J. B. Klerlein and E. C. Carr
[
6
, p. 94]
:

Corollary 1.2
.

If
\vec{C}_{m}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n}
is hamiltonian
(
and
m,n\geq 3
)
, then
P(\vec{C}_{m}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n})
is not hamiltonian.

Remarks 1.3
.

(1)

1.1
requires
m
and
n
to be at least

. The remaining case was settled by J. B. Klerlein and E. C. Carr
[

P(\vec{C}_{2}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n})
is hamiltonian if and only if

.
(Since

and

are hamiltonian, it is clear that

would be false if it allowed the case where

(2)

J. B. Klerlein and E. C. Carr also determined whether

is hamiltonian in certain other special cases. In particular, they

We now explain how to derive

[

The conclusions of the

are symmetric under interchanging

and

, so we may assume that the conditions in the statement of

hold. For
a=b=2
, this means:

\displaystyle a_{m,n}(-2,0)=\min\left\{\begin{matrix}a_{m,n}(-2,-2),\ a_{m,n}(-2,-1),\ a_{m,n}(-2,0),\\[3.0pt]
a_{m,n}(-2,-2),\ a_{m,n}(-1,-2),\ a_{m,n}(0,-2)\end{matrix}\right\}

(3.1)

and

\displaystyle\gcd\left(n-2-2\left\lfloor\frac{a_{m,n}(-2,0)}{m}\right\rfloor,\ 2\,\frac{a_{m,n}(-2,0)}{n}\right)=1.

(3.2)

(
1
)
Note that

must be odd. (Otherwise, both terms in the
\gcd
of (

) are even, which contradicts the fact that the
\gcd
is

exists, we know that
\gcd(m,n)\in\{1,2\}
. From the fact that

is odd, we conclude that
\gcd(m,n)=1
.

(
2
)
Since

a_{m,n}(i-1,j-1)=a_{m,n}(i,j)-1

(unless
i\equiv j\equiv 0\ (\mathop{\mathrm{mod}}\lcm(m,n))
),

(3.3)

we have

\displaystyle\text{$a_{m,n}(-2,-1)=a_{m,n}(-1,0)-1$
\quad and \quad$a_{m,n}(-1,-2)=a_{m,n}(0,-1)-1$}.

(3.4)

Therefore, we see from (
3.1
) that (
2
) holds.

(For reversing the argument, note that
a_{m,n}(-2,-2)=mn-2
, so it is always true that
a_{m,n}(-2,0)<a_{m,n}(-2,-2)
, and also note that the inequality
a_{m,n}(-2,0)<a_{m,n}(0,-2)
can be achieved by interchanging

and

if it does not already hold.)

(
3
)
Note that

a_{m,n}(-2,0)+a_{m,n}(0,-2)=mn-2,

because the left-hand side is congruent to
-2
modulo both
m
and
n
(and we know from (
1
) that
m
and
n
are relatively prime).
Since (by (
3.1
)) we have
a_{m,n}(-2,0)<a_{m,n}(0,-2)
,
this implies that

a_{m,n}(-2,0)<\frac{mn}{2}-1,

so
a_{m,n}(-4,0)=2\,a_{m,n}(-2,0)
. Therefore, we have

2\,\frac{a_{m,n}(-2,0)}{n}=\frac{a_{m,n}(-4,0)}{n}.

Hence, in order to establish that (

) is the same as conclusion (

) of the

, all that remains is to show

Since

is a multiple of

, we see that the left-hand side is

\displaystyle n-2-2\left(\frac{a_{m,n}(-2,0)+2}{m}-1\right)

\displaystyle=n-2\,\frac{a_{m,n}(-2,0)+2}{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{mn-2\,a_{m,n}(-2,0)-4}{m}.

Also note that

mn-2\,a_{m,n}(-2,0)-4>mn-2\left(\frac{mn}{2}-1\right)-4=-2.

It is therefore easy to see that

(because the two sides are congruent modulo both

Lemma 4.4

(Wu

Proof.

Let

We claim that every coset of the subgroup

. By repeating this argument, we conclude, for every

The claim implies that every vertex of

1\leq j<k
, neither
v+j(1,-1)+(1,0)
nor
v+j(1,-1)+(1,0)
is in
R_{2,2}
.

From (
c
) and (
d
) (combined with
4.3
) and induction, we see that either every element of
I_{v,k}
travels by
(1,0)
, or every element of
I_{v,k}
travels by
(0,1)
.

To complete the proof, we will show that there is no choice about whether these vertices travel by
(1,0)
or by
(1,0)
: it is uniquely determined for each
v
. First of all, if
v+(0,1)\in R_{2,2}
, then
v
cannot travel by
(0,1)
, so it must travel by
(1,0)
; hence, every vertex in
I_{v,k}
must travel by
(0,1)
. On the other hand, if
v+(0,1)\notin R_{2,2}
, then (by (
a
)) we must have
v-(1,-1)\in R_{2,2}
, so the vertex
v-(1,-1)
is not in
H
, and therefore cannot travel by
(1,0)
. Since
v+(0,1)
must have an in-edge, we conclude that
v
travels by
(0,1)
; hence, every vertex in
I_{v,k}
must travel by
(0,1)
.
∎

Lemma 4.5

(cf.
[
11
, Thm. 10]
)
.

For
m,n\geq 3
, the digraph
(\vec{C}_{m}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n})\smallsetminus R_{2,2}
has a vertex-disjoint cycle cover if and only if
a_{m,n}(-2,0)
and
a_{m,n}(0,-2)
exist, and

\min\bigl\{\,a_{m,n}(-2,0),\ a_{m,n}(0,-2)\,\bigr\}<\min\bigl\{\,a_{m,n}(0,-1),\ a_{m,n}(-1,0)\,\bigr\}.

Furthermore, if the digraph does have a vertex-disjoint cycle cover, then the number of vertices that travel by
(1,0)
in this subdigraph is exactly twice the left-hand side of the above inequality.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
)
If a vertex
u
travels by
(1,0)
, and we let

\displaystyle r(u)=\min\{\,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\mid u+k(1,-1)\in R_{2,2}\,\},

(4.6)

then it follows from
4.3
(
1
) (and induction on
k
) that
u+k(1,-1)
travels by
(1,0)
for
0\leq k<r(u)
. This implies

\text{$u+k(1,-1)+(1,0)\notin R_{2,2}$ for $0\leq k<r(u)$}.

In particular, we can apply this with
u=(1,-1)
, since this vertex travels by
(1,0)
because
u+(0,1)=(1,0)\in R_{2,2}
. We have

\displaystyle R_{2,2}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}(0,1),&(1,1),\\
(0,0),&(1,0)\phantom{,}\end{matrix}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}(1,-1)+(-1,2),&(1,-1)+(0,2),\\
(1,-1)+(-1,1),&(1,-1)+(0,1)\phantom{,}\end{matrix}\right\}

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}u+a_{m,n}(-1,-2)\cdot(1,-1),&u+a_{m,n}(0,-2)\cdot(1,-1),\\
u+a_{m,n}(-1,-1)\cdot(1,-1),&u+a_{m,n}(0,-1)\cdot(1,-1)\phantom{,}\end{matrix}\right\},

so

\displaystyle r(u)=\min\{\,a_{m,n}(-1,-2),\ a_{m,n}(0,-2),\ a_{m,n}(-1,-1),\ a_{m,n}(0,-1)\}.

(4.8)

Also, since

\displaystyle u+a_{m,n}(-2,-1)\cdot(1,-1)+(1,0)=(1,-1)+(-2,1)+(1,0)=(0,0)\in R_{2,2}

and

\displaystyle u+a_{m,n}(-2,-2)\cdot(1,-1)+(1,0)=(1,-1)+(-2,2)+(1,0)=(0,1)\in R_{2,2},

we know that
u+a_{m,n}(-2,-1)\cdot(1,-1)
and
u+a_{m,n}(-2,-2)\cdot(1,-1)
do not travel by
(1,0)
, so

\displaystyle r(u)\leq\min\bigl\{\,a_{m,n}(-2,-1),\ a_{m,n}(-2,-2)\,\}.

(4.9)

Since
a_{m,n}(-2,-2)=\lcm(m,n)-2
is very large, it is almost entirely irrelevant in (
4.9
), but it does imply that
a_{m,n}(-1,-1)=\lcm(m,n)-1
is not the only term that exists in the right-hand side of (
4.8
). This implies that

\gcd(m,n)\in\{1,2\}
, so

a_{m,n}(-2,0)
and
a_{m,n}(0,-2)
exist.

We may now assume that
a_{m,n}(0,-1)
and (equivalently)
a_{m,n}(-1,0)
exist, for otherwise the inequality in the statement of the
Section
is vacuously true.
We may also assume (by interchanging
m
and
n
if necessary) that

\min\bigl\{\,a_{m,n}(0,-1),\ a_{m,n}(-1,0)\,\bigr\}=a_{m,n}(-1,0).

Thus, we see from (
3.4
) that (
4.9
) is equivalent to the condition that
a_{m,n}(0,-2)<a_{m,n}(-1,0)
. This establishes the inequality in the statement of the
Section
.

(
\Leftarrow
)
Let
u=(1,-1)
and assume, without loss of generality, that

a_{m,n}(-1,0)<a_{m,n}(0,-1).

Since

a_{m,n}(-1,0)+a_{m,n}(0,-1)=a_{m,n}(-1,-1)=\lcm(m,n)-1,

this implies that
a_{m,n}(-1,0)<\lcm(m,n)/2
, so
a_{m,n}(-2,0)=2\,a_{m,n}(-1,0)>a_{m,n}(-1,0)
. So we see from the assumption of this direction of the proof that

\displaystyle a_{m,n}(0,-2)<a_{m,n}(-1,0)=\min\bigl\{\,a_{m,n}(-1,0),\ a_{m,n}(0,-1),\ a_{m,n}(-2,0)\,\bigr\}.

(4.10)

We then conclude from (
4.7
) (and the definition of
r(u)
in (
4.6
)) that

\displaystyle r(u)=a_{m,n}(0,-2)

(4.11)

and (using (
3.3
)) that

u+k(1,-1)+(1,0)\notin R_{2,2}
for
0\leq k<r(u)
.

Let
u^{\prime}=u-(1,0)=(0,-1)
. We claim that

\displaystyle r(u^{\prime})=r(u).

(4.12)

To see this, first note that

u^{\prime}+r(u)\cdot(1,-1)=(0,-1)+a_{m,n}(0,-2)\cdot(1,-1)=(0,-1)+(0,2)=(0,1)\in R_{2,2},

so
r(u^{\prime})\leq r(u)
. On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle R_{2,2}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}(0,1),&(1,1),\\
(0,0),&(1,0)\phantom{,}\end{matrix}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}(0,-1)+(0,2),&(0,-1)+(1,2),\\
(0,-1)+(0,1),&(0,-1)+(1,1)\phantom{,}\end{matrix}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}u^{\prime}+a_{m,n}(0,-2)\cdot(1,-1),&u^{\prime}+a_{m,n}(1,-2)\cdot(1,-1),\\
u^{\prime}+a_{m,n}(0,-1)\cdot(1,-1),&u^{\prime}+a_{m,n}(1,-1)\cdot(1,-1)\phantom{,}\end{matrix}\right\},

so

\displaystyle r(u^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\min\bigl\{\,a_{m,n}(0,-2),\ a_{m,n}(1,-2),\phantom{{}+1}\ a_{m,n}(0,-1),\ a_{m,n}(1,-1)\phantom{{}+1}\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\min\bigl\{\,a_{m,n}(0,-2),\ a_{m,n}(0,-3)+1,\ a_{m,n}(0,-1),\ a_{m,n}(0,-2)+1\,\bigr\}

From (

), we know that the only value in this minimum that could possibly be smaller than

. However, we have

a_{m,n}(0,-2)+a_{m,n}(0,-1)<a_{m,n}(-1,0)+a_{m,n}(0,-1)=\lcm(m,n)-1<\lcm(m,n),

Note that, for

, we have

u^{\prime}+k(1,-1)+(1,0)=u+k(1,-1)\notin R_{2,2}.

Also note that
u
and

are the only vertices in
(\vec{C}_{m}\mathbin{\Box}\vec{C}_{n})\smallsetminus R_{2,2}

. Therefore, we can construct a spanning subdigraph

in which a vertex travels by
(1,0)
if it is in the set

\left\{v+k(1,-1)\;\middle|\;\begin{matrix}v\in\{u,u^{\prime}\},\\

otherwise. By construction (and (

, and

, then

also travels by
(1,0)
. Hence, no vertex has invalence

, so the in-degree (and out-degree) of every vertex of

is

, which means that

is the desired vertex-disjoint cycle cover.

Furthermore, we know from the construction of

(together with (
4.10
) and (
4.11
)) that the number of vertices that travel by
(1,0)
is as specified in the final sentence of the statement of the
Section
. Since

is the only vertex-disjoint cycle cover (see
4.4
), this completes the proof.
∎

Direct proof of
2.5
.

4.5
provides necessary conditions for the existence of a hamiltonian cycle (in particular,
1.1
(
2
) must hold), but they is not sufficient, because we need an additional condition that determines whether the cycle cover is a single cycle, rather than a union of several cycles. This condition is provided by the “knot class
,
” which is a topological concept that was introduced into the study of Cartesian products of directed cycles by S. J. Curran
[
2
, §4]
.

Namely, let
H
be the vertex-disjoint cycle cover, and suppose the number of vertices of
H
that travel by
(1,0)
is
x
, and the number that travel by
(0,1)
is
y
. Then
x/m
and
y/n
are integers, and the knot class of
H
is defined to be the ordered pair
(x/m,y/n)

[
2
, Rem. 4.5]
. The theory
[
2
, Prop. 4.12(a)]
tells us that

H
consists of a single cycle if and only if
\gcd(x/m,y/n)=1
.

(4.13)

We now use (
4.13
) to show that
1.1
(
1
) is a necessary condition for
H
to be a hamiltonian cycle. The key is to notice that if
\gcd(m,n)\neq 1
, then since
4.5
tells us that
a_{m,n}(-2,0)
exists, we must have
\gcd(m,n)=2
, so

m
and
n
are even.

However, if we assume, without loss of generality, that
a_{m,n}(0,-2)<a_{m,n}(-2,0)
, then the last sentence of
4.5
tells us that

x=2\,a_{m,n}(0,-2).

Since the number of vertices of
H
is
mn-4
, this implies

y=mn-4-x=mn-4-2\,a_{m,n}(0,-2)=mn-2\,a_{m,n}(2,0).

Since
m
is even, it is now obvious that

\frac{x}{m}=2\,\frac{a_{m,n}(0,-2)}{m}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{y}{n}=m-2\,\frac{a_{m,n}(2,0)}{n}

are even. Hence,
\gcd(x/m,y/n)\neq 1
, so we see from (
4.13
)
H
is not a hamiltonian cycle.

To complete the proof, we now consider the situation where
1.1
(
1
) holds, which means that
\gcd(m,n)=1
.
Since
x/m
and
y/n
are integers, we know that

x\equiv 0\ (\mathop{\mathrm{mod}}m)
and
y\equiv 0\ (\mathop{\mathrm{mod}}n)
.

Also, since the number of vertices of
H
is
mn-4
, we know that

x+y=mn-4\equiv-4\ (\mathop{\mathrm{mod}}mn).

Combining these congruences tells us that

x\equiv-4\ (\mathop{\mathrm{mod}}n)
and
y\equiv-4\ (\mathop{\mathrm{mod}}m)
.

So
x=a_{m,n}(0,-4)
and
y=a_{m,n}(-4,0)
. We conclude from (
4.13
) that
H
consists of a single cycle (and is therefore a hamiltonian cycle) if and only if the condition in
1.1
(
3
) holds.
∎
