Integrated density of states for the Poisson point interactions on 𝐑 3 \mathbf{R}^{3}


Abstract
We determine the principal term of the asymptotics of the integrated density of states (IDS) N ⁡ ( λ ) N(\lambda) for the Schrödinger operator with point interactions on 𝐑 3 \mathbf{R}^{3} as λ → − ∞ \lambda\to-\infty , provided that the set of positions of the point obstacles is the Poisson configuration, and the interaction parameters are bounded i.i.d. random variables. In particular, we prove N ( λ ) = O ( | λ | − 3 / 2 ) N(\lambda)=O(|\lambda|^{-3/2}) as λ → − ∞ \lambda\to-\infty . In the case that all interaction parameters are equal to a constant, we give a more detailed asymptotics of N ⁡ ( λ ) N(\lambda) , and verify the result by a numerical method using the R programming language. As a byproduct, we give a rigorous definition of the Schrödinger operators with point interactions on a bounded open set with the Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions by the method of quadratic form, and study fundamental properties about the counting functions of eigenvalues; e.g., Dirichlet–Neumann bracketing, etc.

Introduction

Let
Y
be a set of points in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
(
d=1,2,3
)
which is locally finite,
that is,

\#\{y\in Y;|y|<R\}

is finite for every
R>0
,
where
\#S
is the cardinality of a set
S
.
Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{y})_{y\in Y}
be a sequence of real numbers.
We define
a linear operator

-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}

on
L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
by

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}u:=

\displaystyle\ -\Delta|_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y}u,

\displaystyle D(-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}):=

\displaystyle\ \{u\in L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\cap H^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y)\,;\,-\Delta|_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y}u\in L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle\qquad u\mbox{ satisfies }(BC)_{y}\mbox{ for every }y\in Y\},

where

\Delta|_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y}u=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial^{2}u/\partial x_{j}^{2}

is defined as an element of the Schwartz distribution
{\cal D}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y)
,
and
D(H)
is the domain of a linear operator
H

(the notation
A:=B
means that
A
is defined by
B
).
The boundary condition
(BC)_{y}
at the point
y\in Y

is defined as follows.

•

For
d=1
,

\displaystyle u(y-0)=u(y+0)=u(y),\quad u^{\prime}(y+0)-u^{\prime}(y-0)=\alpha_{y}u(y).

•

For
d=2
,

\displaystyle u(x)=u_{y,0}\log|x-y|+u_{y,1}+o(1)\quad(x\to y),\quad 2\pi\alpha_{y}u_{y,0}+u_{y,1}=0.

•

For
d=3
,

\displaystyle u(x)=u_{y,0}|x-y|^{-1}+u_{y,1}+o(1)\quad(x\to y),\quad-4\pi\alpha_{y}u_{y,0}+u_{y,1}=0.

Here,
\displaystyle u(y\pm 0)=\lim_{\epsilon\to\pm 0}u(y\pm\epsilon)
,
and
u_{y,0}
and
u_{y,1}
are constants.

It is known that the operator
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
is self-adjoint
on
L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

under suitable conditions on
\alpha
and
Y
,
e.g.,
the uniform discreteness condition

\displaystyle d_{*}:=\inf_{y,y^{\prime}\in Y,\ y\not=y^{\prime}}|y-y^{\prime}|>0

(1.1)

(see e.g.,
[
3
]
).
Since the support of the interaction is concentrated on the set
Y
,
the quantum system governed by the Hamiltonian
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
is called ‘point interaction model’,
‘zero-range interaction model’, ‘delta interaction model’,
or ‘Fermi pseudo potential model’, etc.
The monograph
by Albeverio et al.
[
3
]

contains
most of the basic facts about the operator
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
,
and references up to 2004.

Let us review known results
about the
random point interactions
,
that is, the case that
\alpha
or
Y
depends
on some random parameter
\omega
.
There are numerous results in the case
d=1

(references are found in
[
31
]
).
When
d=2
or
3
,
there are several results
in the case
Y=\mathbf{Z}^{d}
or its random subset,
and

\alpha_{\omega}=(\alpha_{\omega,y})_{y\in\mathbf{Z}^{d}}

are i.i.d. (independently, identically distributed) random variables.
Albeverio, Høegh-Krohn, Kirsch, and Martinelli
[
4
]

studied the spectrum of
-\Delta_{\alpha_{\omega},\mathbf{Z}^{d}}
.
Boutet de Monvel, and Grinshpun
[
7
]

and
Hislop, Kirsch, and Krishna
[
18
]
proved
the Anderson localization near the bottom of the spectrum.
Recently
Hislop, Kirsch, and Krishna
[
19
]
studied
the eigenvalue statistics.
Dorlas, Macris, and Pulé
[
16
]

studied the random point interactions on

\mathbf{R}^{2}

in a constant magnetic field.

In the present paper,
we consider the following case.

Assumption 1.1
.

(i)

The space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
is a probability space.
The random set
Y_{\omega}
(
\omega\in\Omega
) is the Poisson configuration
(the support of the Poisson point process) on
\mathbf{R}^{d}

with intensity measure
\rho dx
for some constant
\rho>0
.

(ii)

The parameters
\alpha_{\omega}=(\alpha_{\omega,y})_{y\in Y_{\omega}}

are real-valued i.i.d. random variables
with common distribution
\nu
on
\mathbf{R}
.
Moreover,
(\alpha_{\omega,y})_{y\in Y_{\omega}}

are independent of
Y_{\omega}
.

We denote
H_{\omega}=-\Delta_{\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega}}
in the sequel.
For the definition of the Poisson point process,
see Appendix (section
7.1
).
For the rigorous meaning of
‘
(\alpha_{\omega,y})_{y\in Y_{\omega}}

are independent of

’,
see the assumption (I7) in section

.
Under Assumption
1.1
,
the Schrödinger operator with random scalar potential

\displaystyle-\Delta+\sum_{y\in Y_{\omega}}\alpha_{\omega,y}V_{0}(x-y)\quad(\mbox{$V_{0}$: scalar function})

(1.2)

is a mathematical model which governs the motion of a quantum particle in an amorphous material,
and is extensively studied in the theory of random Schrödinger
operators (see e.g.,
[
8
,
31
]
).
Formally,
the operator
-\Delta_{\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega}}

is a special case
of (
1.2
), in the sense that
V_{0}
is a zero-range potential.
Notice that the condition (
1.1
) is almost surely not satisfied under
Assumption
1.1
.
When
d=1
,
the operator
H_{\omega}

under Assumption
1.1

is studied by several authors
(
[
17
,
29
,
26
,
30
]
).
Especially, the self-adjointness is proved in
[
30
]

(or it can be proved by an application of
the deterministic result
[
25
]
).
When
d=2
or
3
,
the operator
H_{\omega}
under Assumption

is first studied recently by the authors
[
21
]
.
The results of
[
21
]
are summarized as follows.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
21
]
)
.

Let
d=1,2
or
3
, and
suppose that Assumption
1.1
holds.
Then, the following holds.

(1)

The operator
H_{\omega}
is self-adjoint almost surely.

(2)

The spectrum
\sigma(H_{\omega})
is given as follows.
When
d=1
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(H_{\omega})=\begin{cases}[0,\infty)&(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits\nu\subset[0,\infty)),\\
\mathbf{R}&(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits\nu\cap(-\infty,0)\not=\emptyset),\end{cases}

almost surely.
When
d=2,3
, we have
\sigma(H_{\omega})=\mathbf{R}
almost surely.

Next we introduce the integrated density of states.
For a bounded open set
U
in
\mathbf{R}^{d}

with
Y\cap\partial U=\emptyset
,
let
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y,U}^{D}
(resp.
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y,U}^{N}
)
be the restriction of the operator
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}

on
U
with Dirichlet boundary conditions
(resp. Neumann boundary conditions) on the boundary
\partial U
,
defined by the method of quadratic form
(for the rigorous definition, see section
6.2
).
For
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}
and
\sharp=D
or
N
,
let
N_{\alpha,Y,U}^{\sharp}(\lambda)

be the number of the eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y,U}^{\sharp}

less than or equal to
\lambda
, counted with multiplicity.
For
(\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega})
satisfying Assumption
1.1
,
we define for
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle N_{\omega}^{\sharp}(\lambda):=

\displaystyle\ \lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{N_{\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega},Q_{L}}^{\sharp}(\lambda)}{|Q_{L}|}\quad(\sharp=D\mbox{ or }N),

(1.3)

where
Q_{L}=(0,L)^{d}
,
L\in\mathbf{N}

(
\mathbf{N}
is the set of positive integers),
and

|E|
is the Lebesgue measure of a Lebesgue measurable set
E
.
Under Assumption
1.1
,
we see that
Y_{\omega}\cap\partial Q_{L}=\emptyset
almost surely,
so
-\Delta^{\sharp}_{\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega},Q_{L}}
is well-defined almost surely.
We can also prove that for every
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}
,
the right hand side
of (
1.3
) converges almost surely under Assumption
1.1
,
and

\displaystyle N_{\omega}^{\sharp}(\lambda)=

\displaystyle\ \lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}[N_{\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega},Q_{L}}^{\sharp}(\lambda)]}{|Q_{L}|}\quad(\sharp=D\mbox{ or }N)

(1.4)

almost surely,
where
\mathbf{E}[X]
is the expectation of a random variable
X

(see Theorem
6.8
below).
The equation (
1.4
) implies that

N^{\sharp}_{\omega}(\lambda)
is
deterministic,
that is,

N^{\sharp}_{\omega}(\lambda)
is a random variable
that is constant w.r.t.
\omega
,
after a possible correction on an event of probability
0
.
From this reason,
we denote the right hand side of (
1.4
) by
N^{\sharp}(\lambda)
,
in the sequel.
Furthermore, we can prove that

N^{N}(\lambda+0)=N^{D}(\lambda+0)

for every
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}
,
where
f(\lambda+0):=\lim_{\epsilon\to+0}f(\lambda+\epsilon)

(see Theorem
6.9
below).
We define
N(\lambda):=N^{\sharp}(\lambda+0)
(
\sharp=D
or
N
)
for
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}
,
then
N(\lambda)
is independent of the boundary condition

\sharp=D
or
\sharp=N
).
Moreover,
if
N^{\sharp}(\lambda)
(
\sharp=D
or
N
)
is continuous at
\lambda

(this condition holds for all
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}

except at most a countable subset of
\mathbf{R}
),
then
N^{D}(\lambda)=N^{N}(\lambda)=N(\lambda)

(Theorem
6.9
).
We call the function
N(\lambda)
the

integrated density of states (IDS)
.

For
\lambda<0
,
it is convenient to introduce other counting function

\displaystyle N_{\alpha,Y}(\lambda):=

\displaystyle\ \#\{\mu\leq\lambda\,;\,\mu\mbox{ is an eigenvalue of }-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}\},

\displaystyle N_{\alpha,Y,U}(\lambda):=

\displaystyle\ N_{\alpha_{U},Y_{U}}(\lambda),\quad Y_{U}=Y\cap U,\ \alpha_{U}=\alpha|_{Y_{U}},

where eigenvalues are counted with multiplicity,

\alpha|_{Y_{U}}
is the restriction of
the sequence
\alpha=(\alpha_{y})_{y\in Y}
on the subset

Y_{U}
.
Notice that the operator
-\Delta_{\alpha_{U},Y_{U}}

is defined on
L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
(not
L^{2}(U)
).
We can prove that
if

(\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega})
satisfies Assumption
1.1
,

\lambda<0
and

N(\lambda)=N^{D}(\lambda)=N^{N}(\lambda)
, then

\displaystyle N(\lambda)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{N_{\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega},Q_{L}}(\lambda)}{|Q_{L}|}=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}[N_{\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega},Q_{L}}(\lambda)]}{|Q_{L}|}

(1.5)

almost surely
(see Proposition
6.7
below).
The advantage of the new expression (
1.5
) is that

N_{\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega},Q_{L}}(\lambda)
can be calculated explicitly
(Proposition
7.4
).

The asymptotics of IDS near the bottom of the spectrum
is intensively studied in the theory of random Schrödinger operators
(see e.g.,
[
8
,
31
,
23
,
36
]
).
In the present paper,
we give an asymptotic formula of IDS
N(\lambda)
for our operator

H_{\omega}
as
\lambda\to-\infty
in the case
d=3
,
under Assumption
1.1
.

First we consider the case where
\alpha_{\omega}=(\alpha_{\omega,y})_{y\in Y_{\omega}}
is a
constant sequence
,
that is,
\alpha_{\omega,y}
is equal to a deterministic value
independent of
\omega
and
y
.
We denote its common value by
\alpha\in\mathbf{R}
,
and write

-\Delta_{\alpha,Y_{\omega}}:=-\Delta_{\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega}}

for simplicity.
In order to state our main result, we introduce some auxiliary functions.
If
s>\mathop{\rm max}\limits(0,-4\pi\alpha)
,
it is easy to see that
the following equation with respect to
R

\displaystyle s-\frac{e^{-sR}}{R}=-4\pi\alpha

(1.6)

has a unique positive solution
R
,
which we denote by
R_{\alpha}(s)
.
We denote the inverse function of
R=R_{\alpha}(s)

by
s=s_{\alpha}(R)
,
then
s_{\alpha}
is a real-valued monotone decreasing function defined on

(0,1/(4\pi\alpha))
for
\alpha>0
,
or
on
(0,\infty)
for
\alpha\leq 0
.
Particularly when
\alpha=0
,
the equation (
1.6
) becomes
a simple equation
sR-e^{-sR}=0
.
We denote the unique positive solution of
t-e^{-t}=0
by

t_{0}(\doteqdot 0.567...
).
Then we have explicitly

\displaystyle R_{0}(s)=\frac{t_{0}}{s},\quad s_{0}(R)=\frac{t_{0}}{R}.

For
\alpha\not=0
, we still have

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(s)\sim\frac{t_{0}}{s}\quad(s\to\infty),\quad s_{\alpha}(R)\sim\frac{t_{0}}{R}\quad(R\to+0),

(1.7)

where
f\sim g
means
f/g\to 1

(Proposition
2.1
).
The function
R_{\alpha}(s)
can be
computed with high accuracy
with the aid of some numerical tool.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
d=3
.
Suppose that
\alpha_{\omega}
and
Y_{\omega}
satisfy
Assumption
1.1
.
Suppose further that

\alpha_{\omega}
is a constant sequence
with its common value
\alpha
.
Let
N(\lambda)
be the corresponding IDS
for the operator
H_{\omega}=-\Delta_{\alpha,Y_{\omega}}
.
Then, for any
\epsilon
with
0<\epsilon<3
,
we have

\displaystyle N(-s^{2})=\frac{2\pi}{3}\rho^{2}R_{\alpha}(s)^{3}+O(s^{-6+\epsilon})\quad(s\to\infty).

(1.8)

In particular, the principal term of the asymptotics is given by

\displaystyle N(-s^{2})\sim\frac{2\pi}{3}\rho^{2}t_{0}^{3}s^{-3}\quad(s\to\infty).

(1.9)

In section
5
, we give a numerical verification
of Theorem
1.3
,
using the R programming language.

Next we consider the case that
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits\nu
is bounded,
i.e.,
there exist constants
\alpha_{-}
and
\alpha_{+}
such that

\alpha_{-}\leq\alpha_{\omega,y}\leq\alpha_{+}

for every
y\in Y_{\omega}
, almost surely.
By the monotonicity of the negative eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}

with respect to
\alpha
(statement (1) of Proposition
7.5
),
we have

\displaystyle N_{\alpha_{-},Y_{\omega},Q_{L}}(\lambda)\geq N_{\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega},Q_{L}}(\lambda)\geq N_{\alpha_{+},Y_{\omega},Q_{L}}(\lambda)

for every
\lambda<0
and every
L\in\mathbf{N}

with
Y_{\omega}\cap\partial Q_{L}=\emptyset
, almost surely.
Since the principal term of the asymptotics (
1.9
) is independent of
\alpha
,
we have the following corollary.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
d=3
.
Suppose that
\alpha_{\omega}
and
Y_{\omega}
satisfy
Assumption
1.1
,
and
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits\nu
is bounded.
Then, we have the asymptotics

\displaystyle N(-s^{2})\sim\frac{2\pi}{3}\rho^{2}t_{0}^{3}s^{-3}\quad(s\to\infty).

(1.10)

Let us compare the asymptotics (
1.10
) with
the corresponding result for the scalar type Poisson random potential

\displaystyle-\Delta+\sum_{y\in Y_{\omega}}V_{0}(x-y),

(1.11)

where

V_{0}
is a real-valued function and

Y_{\omega}
is the Poisson configuration.
If
V_{0}\leq 0
,
V_{0}
has global minimum at
x=0
,
and
V_{0}
satisfies some regularity and decaying conditions,
it is shown in the monograph of Pastur and Figotin

[
31
, Theorem 9.4]
that

\displaystyle\log N(\lambda)=-\frac{|\lambda|}{|V_{0}(0)|}\log|\lambda|\cdot(1+o(1))\quad(\lambda\to-\infty).

(1.12)

The asymptotics (
1.12
) implies that
N(\lambda)
decays super-exponentially,
much faster than the polynomial decay (
1.10
).

Heuristically, the asymptotics (
1.12
) is explained as follows.
If the operator (
1.11
)
has a bound state localized near a point
a

with negative energy
\lambda
,
there must be a potential well
of depth
|\lambda|
near the point
a
.
Thus it is necessary that at least

\displaystyle n=\left[\frac{|\lambda|}{|V_{0}(0)|}\right]

(1.13)

points of
Y_{\omega}
are in the ball of small radius
\epsilon

centered at
a
,
where
[x]
is the greatest integer less than or equal to
x
.
The probability of this event is

\displaystyle\frac{(\rho|B_{\epsilon}(0)|)^{n}}{n!}e^{-\rho|B_{\epsilon}(0)|},

(1.14)

where
B_{\epsilon}(0)
is a ball of radius
\epsilon

centered at the origin.
Then, roughly speaking,
the asymptotics (
1.12
) is deduced from
(
1.13
), (
1.14
), and
the Stirling formula

\displaystyle n!\sim(2\pi n)^{1/2}\left({n}/{e}\right)^{n}\quad(n\to\infty).

In the case of point interactions,
the situation is completely different.
Consider the case where

is a two-point set,
and

is a constant sequence.
Then,

we can prove that the operator

has a negative eigenvalue

\lambda=-(s_{\alpha}(R))^{2}
,
where

(see section 2),
and

as

).
The leading asymptotics of

is given by the expectation number
of pairs within the distance

of

(Lemma

).
The expectation of the number of such pairs

),

2
Spectrum of the Hamiltonian with one or two point interactions

Let
d=3
,

Y
be a finite set in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
, and

\alpha=(\alpha_{y})_{y\in Y}
be a sequence of real numbers.
Put
N:=\#Y
.
It is known that

\displaystyle\ \sigma(-\Delta_{\alpha,Y})=\sigma_{p}(-\Delta_{\alpha,Y})\cup[0,\infty),

\displaystyle\ \sigma_{p}(-\Delta_{\alpha,Y})\subset(-\infty,0],

where
\sigma_{p}(-\Delta_{\alpha,Y})
is the point spectrum of

-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}

(see
[
3
, Theorem II.1.1.4, and Errata in page 485]
,
see also Proposition
7.4
and Proposition
7.6
in Appendix).
More explicitly,

\sigma_{p}(-\Delta_{\alpha,Y})\cap(-\infty,0)
for
N=1,2
is given as follows.

When
N=1
,
Proposition
7.4
implies that

-s^{2}
(
s>0
) is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}

if and only if

\displaystyle\alpha+\frac{s}{4\pi}=0.

Then we see that

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}(-\Delta_{\alpha,Y})\cap(-\infty,0)=\begin{cases}\emptyset&(\alpha\geq 0),\\
\{-(4\pi\alpha)^{2}\}&(\alpha<0).\end{cases}

When
N=2
,
Y=\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
,

\alpha_{y_{1}}=\alpha_{y_{2}}=\alpha
,
and
R:=|y_{1}-y_{2}|>0
,
Proposition
7.4
implies that

-s^{2}
(
s>0
) is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}

if and only if

\displaystyle\left|\begin{matrix}\displaystyle\alpha+\frac{s}{4\pi}&\displaystyle-\frac{e^{-sR}}{4\pi R}\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{e^{-sR}}{4\pi R}&\displaystyle\alpha+\frac{s}{4\pi}\end{matrix}\right|=0\Leftrightarrow\left(\alpha+\frac{s}{4\pi}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{e^{-sR}}{4\pi R}\right)^{2}=0.

This equation is satisfied if and only if

\displaystyle s+\frac{e^{-sR}}{R}=-4\pi\alpha

(2.1)

or

\displaystyle s-\frac{e^{-sR}}{R}=-4\pi\alpha.

(2.2)

The solution
s
to the equation (
2.1
)
does not exist if
\alpha\geq 0
,
and is bounded by
-4\pi\alpha
if
\alpha<0
.
The equation (
2.2
) is the same as
(
1.6
), and
the solution is equal to
s_{\alpha}(R)
by definition.
An asymptotics of
s_{\alpha}(R)

and that of its inverse
R_{\alpha}(s)
are given as follows.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let

t_{0}
be the unique positive solution of

t-e^{-t}=0
.

(1)

Suppose
\alpha=0
.
Then we have

s_{0}(R)=t_{0}/R

and

R_{0}(s)=t_{0}/s
.

(2)

Suppose
\alpha\not=0
.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0
,
there exists a positive constant
R_{0}
dependent on
\alpha
and

\epsilon

such that

\displaystyle\frac{t_{0}-\epsilon}{R}<s_{\alpha}(R)<\frac{{t_{0}+\epsilon}}{R}

(2.3)

for any
R
with
0<R<R_{0}
,

Let us calculate the expectation of the number of

For

.
For the Poisson configuration

\displaystyle n_{y}(R):=\#(Y_{\omega}\cap B_{y}(R)).

(3.1)

Proposition 3.1

Let
Y_{\omega}
be the Poisson configuration
with intensity
\rho dx
, where

Let

and put

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{E}[\#\{y\in Y_{\omega}\cap Q_{L}\,;\,n_{y}(R)=n\}]}{|Q_{L}|}=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}|B_{0}(R)|^{n-1}\rho^{n}e^{-\rho|B_{0}(R)|}.

(3.2)

Proof.

Let

If

.
Moreover,

and

in the definition of

by

,
when we calculate the left hand side of (

Then,

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{E}[\#\{y\in Y_{\omega}\cap Q_{L}\,;\,n_{y}(R)=n\}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}[\#\{y\in Y_{\omega}\cap Q_{L}\,;\,n_{y}(R)=n\}\mid\#Y_{\omega}^{\prime}=k]\cdot\mathbf{P}(\#Y_{\omega}^{\prime}=k),

where

is the conditional expectation of
A

under the condition
B

Under the condition

\displaystyle\ \frac{|Q_{L}|}{|Q_{L}^{\prime}|}\cdot\frac{(k-1)!}{(n-1)!(k-n)!}\cdot\left(\frac{|B_{y_{j}}(R)|}{|Q_{L}^{\prime}|}\right)^{n-1}\cdot\left(\frac{|Q_{L}^{\prime}|-|B_{y_{j}}(R)|}{|Q_{L}^{\prime}|}\right)^{k-n}

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbf{P}(y_{j}\in Q_{L},\ n_{y_{j}}(R)=n\mid\#Y_{\omega}^{\prime}=k)\cdot\mathbf{P}(\#Y_{\omega}^{\prime}=k)

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=n}^{\infty}k\cdot\frac{|Q_{L}|}{|Q_{L}^{\prime}|}\cdot\frac{(k-1)!}{(n-1)!(k-n)!}\cdot\left(\frac{|B_{0}(R)|}{|Q_{L}^{\prime}|}\right)^{n-1}\cdot\left(\frac{|Q_{L}^{\prime}|-|B_{0}(R)|}{|Q_{L}^{\prime}|}\right)^{k-n}

\displaystyle\ \frac{|Q_{L}||B_{0}(R)|^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-\rho|Q_{L}^{\prime}|}\rho^{n}e^{\rho(|Q_{L}^{\prime}|-|B_{0}(R)|)}

\displaystyle\ \frac{|Q_{L}||B_{0}(R)|^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\rho^{n}e^{-\rho|B_{0}(R)|}.

6
Basics of IDS

In this section,
we shall prove some fundamental results
about the definition of IDS (the integrated density of states)
given by (
1.3
),
namely,
the existence of IDS,
and its independence from the boundary conditions,
in the sense that
N^{D}(\lambda+0)=N^{N}(\lambda+0)
.
The results are well-known in the case of
the Schrödinger operators with random
scalar
potential

H_{\omega}=-\Delta+V_{\omega}
(cf.
[
8
,
31
]
),
however, we check the results here again since
we cannot directly apply the known proofs to
our operators
-\Delta_{\alpha_{\omega},Y_{\omega}}
,
under Assumption
1.1
.
The results in this section are applicable in all dimensions
d=1,2,3
.
Though the results are well-known in one-dimensional case

[
17
,
26
,
29
]
,
we give results here for readers’ use.

We use the following notations and facts in the sequel.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space.
We denote the inner product on
\mathcal{H}
by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{H}}
,
and the norm by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
.
The inner product is assumed to be sesqui-linear,
that is,
conjugate linear with respect to the first component,
and linear with respect to the second component.
Let
A
be a self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
.
We denote the spectral projection operator for
A
on a interval
I

by
P_{I}(A)
.
We say
A
is
lower semi-bounded
if there exists

C\in\mathbf{R}
such that

\displaystyle(u,Au)_{\mathcal{H}}\geq C\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

for every
u\in D(A)
.
Define a norm
\|\cdot\|_{Q(q_{A})}
on
D(A)
by

\displaystyle\|u\|_{Q(q_{A})}^{2}:=(u,Au)_{\mathcal{H}}+(-C+1)\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

for
u\in D(A)
.
We denote the completion of
D(A)
with respect to the norm

\|\cdot\|_{Q(q_{A})}
by
Q(q_{A})
,
which is a subspace of
\mathcal{H}
since the norm
\|\cdot\|_{Q(q_{A})}

is stronger than
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
.
Then the norm
\|\cdot\|_{Q(q_{A})}
and the sesqui-linear form

\displaystyle q_{A}(u,v):=(u,Av)_{\mathcal{H}}

defined for
u,v\in D(A)
is uniquely extended to
Q(q_{A})

by continuity.
Then
q_{A}
is a quadratic form, that is,

q_{A}
is densely defined sesqui-linear form on
Q(q_{A})
.
We denote
q_{A}[u]:=q_{A}(u,u)
.
The quadratic form
q_{A}
is symmetric (
q_{A}(v,u)=\overline{q_{A}(u,v)}
),
closed (
(Q(q_{A}),\|\cdot\|_{Q(q_{A})})
is complete),
and lower semi-bounded (
q_{A}[u]\geq C\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

for some
C\in\mathbf{R}
and every
u\in Q(q_{A})
).
We call
q_{A}
the quadratic form associated with the operator
A
.
It is known that there exists one-to-one correspondence
between a lower semi-bounded self-adjoint operator
A

and a symmetric, lower semi-bounded, closed quadratic form
q_{A}

(see
[
32
, section VIII.6]
), as follows.
If
q_{A}
is given, the operator domain
D(A)
is given by

\displaystyle D(A)=\{u\in Q(q_{A});\mbox{there exists $C>0$ such that}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt|q_{A}(v,u)|\leq C\|v\|_{\mathcal{H}}\mbox{ for every }v\in Q(q_{A})\}.

Then, for
u\in D(A)
, the Riesz lemma implies that there exists
unique
Au\in\mathcal{H}
such that
q_{A}(v,u)=(v,Au)

for every
v\in Q(q_{A})
,
and this is the definition of
Au
.

Let
U\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
be an open set.
Let
L^{2}(U)
be the space of square-integrable functions,
and the inner product and the norm on
L^{2}(U)

are given by

\displaystyle(u,v)_{L^{2}(U)}=\int_{U}\overline{u}v\,dx,\quad\|u\|_{L^{2}(U)}=(u,u)^{1/2}

for
u,v\in L^{2}(U)
.
When
U=\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
we sometimes use the abbreviation

\|u\|=\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}
, etc.
For vector-valued functions

u=(u_{1},...,u_{d}),v=(v_{1},...,v_{d})\in L^{2}(U)^{d}
,
we denote

\displaystyle(u,v)_{L^{2}(U)^{d}}=\int_{U}\overline{u}\cdot v\,dx,\quad\|u\|_{L^{2}(U)^{d}}=(u,u)_{L^{2}(U)^{d}}^{1/2},

where
\overline{u}\cdot v=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\overline{u}_{j}v_{j}
.
The space
H^{j}(U)
(
j=1,2
) is the Sobolev space
of
j
-th order, defined by

\displaystyle H^{1}(U)=\{u\in L^{2}(U);\nabla u\in L^{2}(U)^{d}\},

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{1}(U)}^{2}=\|u\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(U)^{d}}^{2},

\displaystyle H^{2}(U)=\{u\in H^{1}(U);\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{k}}\in L^{2}(U)\ (j,k=1,...,d)\},

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{2}(U)}^{2}=\|u\|_{H^{1}(U)}^{2}+\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\left\|\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2},

where

\nabla u=(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{1}},...,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{d}})
,
and the derivatives are defined
as an element of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)

(the Schwartz distributions on
U
).
The space
C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
consists of
u\in C^{\infty}(U)
such that

\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits u
is compact and
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits u\subset U
.
We also denote
the completion of
C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
with respect to

H^{j}
-norm by
H^{j}_{0}(U)
(
j=1,2
).
The space
L^{2}_{\rm loc}(U)
consists of all
measurable functions
u

such that
\chi u\in L^{2}(U)
for every
\chi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
.
The spaces
H^{j}_{\rm loc}(U)
(
j=1,2
) are defined similarly.

6.1
Min-max principle

In this subsection,
we recall the min-max principle and its several consequences.
In the sequel, we use the following notation.
Let
A
be a lower semi-bounded self-adjoint operator
on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
If
N:=\dim\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits P_{(-\infty,\inf\sigma_{\rm ess}(A))}(A)
,
then we denote

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(A):=\begin{cases}\mbox{$j$-th smallest eigenvalue of $A$}&(1\leq j\leq N),\\
\inf\sigma_{\rm ess}(A)&(j\geq N+1),\end{cases}

for
j\in\mathbf{N}
,
where the eigenvalues are counted with multiplicity.
We define the eigenvalue counting function
N(\lambda;A)
of
A
by

\displaystyle N(\lambda;A):=\dim\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits P_{(-\infty,\lambda]}(A)

for
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}
.
According to the
min-max principle

[
33
, Theorem XIII.1,2]
,
we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(A)=\sup_{v_{1},...,v_{j-1}\in\mathcal{H}}\left(\inf_{u\in Q(q_{A})\cap\langle v_{1},...,v_{j-1}\rangle^{\bot},\,\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1}q_{A}[u]\right)

(6.1)

for every
j\in\mathbf{N}
, where

\langle v_{1},...,v_{j-1}\rangle

is the linear hull of

v_{1},...,v_{j-1}

and
W^{\bot}
is the orthogonal complement
of a subspace
W
.
Here we prepare two variants of the formula (
6.1
).

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space,
and
A
be a lower semi-bounded self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(A)=\sup_{\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits V\leq j-1}\left(\inf_{u\in V\cap Q(q_{A}),\,\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1}q_{A}[u]\right)

(6.2)

for every
j\in\mathbf{N}
,
where the supremum ranges over all the linear subspaces
V
of
\mathcal{H}

with
\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits V:=\dim(\mathcal{H}/V)\leq j-1
.
Moreover, if
S
is a countable dense subset of
\mathcal{H}
,
then we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(A)=\sup_{v_{1},...,v_{j-1}\in S}\left(\inf_{u\in Q(q_{A})\cap\langle v_{1},...,v_{j-1}\rangle^{\bot},\,\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1}q_{A}[u]\right)

(6.3)

for every
j\in\mathbf{N}
.

Remark.

Compared with
(
6.1
),

V
is not necessarily a closed subspace in (
6.2
).
This generalization is useful in the proof of the next proposition.
When the quadratic form

q_{A}[u]
depends on some additional parameter

\omega
in a measure space
\Omega
,
the formula
(
6.3
) is useful when we prove the measurability
of
\lambda_{j}(A)
with respect to
\omega
.

Proof.

We denote the right hand side of
(
6.2
) and (
6.3
)
by
\mu_{1,j}(A)
and
\mu_{2,j}(A)
,
respectively.
Clearly we have

\displaystyle\mu_{2,j}(A)\leq\lambda_{j}(A)\leq\mu_{1,j}(A).

We shall prove that the converse inequalities hold.

First we assume that

\mu_{1,j}(A)>\lambda_{j}(A)
,
and take
a\in\mathbf{R}
such that
\mu_{1,j}(A)>a>\lambda_{j}(A)
.
Then there exists a subspace
V
of
\mathcal{H}

with
\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits V\leq j-1

such that

\displaystyle q_{A}[u]>a

for every
u\in V\cap Q(q_{A})
with
\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1
.
Since
a>\lambda_{j}(A)
,
we have
\dim\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits P_{(-\infty,a)}(A)\geq j
,
and there exist independent vectors

\varphi_{k}\in Q(q_{A})
(
k=1,...,j
) such that
\|\varphi_{k}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1

and

\displaystyle q_{A}[\varphi_{k}]<a\quad(k=1,\ldots,j).

Thus we see that
\varphi_{k}\not\in V
,
which contradicts with the fact that
\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits V\leq j-1
.
Therefore we conclude that
\mu_{1,j}(A)=\lambda_{j}(A)
.

Next, we will show that
\mu_{2,j}(A)\geq\lambda_{j}(A)
.
Take a subspace
\Phi_{j}
of

\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits P_{[\lambda_{1}(A),\lambda_{j-1}(A)]}(A)

such that

\displaystyle\dim\Phi_{j}=j-1,\quad\Phi_{j}^{\bot}\subset\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits P_{[\lambda_{j}(A),\infty)}(A).

Let
P_{j}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\Phi_{j}
.
Then we have for
u\in Q(q_{A})

\displaystyle q_{A}[u]=

\displaystyle\ q_{A}[P_{j}u]+q_{A}[(1-P_{j})u]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \lambda_{1}(A)\|P_{j}u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\lambda_{j}(A)\|(1-P_{j})u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \lambda_{j}(A)\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-(\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{1}(A))\|P_{j}u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

(6.4)

For
v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-1}\in\mathcal{H}
,
put
V:=\langle v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-1}\rangle^{\bot}
,
and let
P_{V}
be the orthogonal projection onto
V
.
Then we have by (

)

\displaystyle\ \inf_{u\in Q(q_{A})\cap V,\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1}q_{A}[u]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \lambda_{j}(A)-(\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{1}(A))\sup_{u\in Q(q_{A})\cap V,\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1}\|P_{j}u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \lambda_{j}(A)-(\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{1}(A))\|P_{j}P_{V}\|,

(6.5)

where
\|P_{j}P_{V}\|

is the operator norm of
P_{j}P_{V}
on
\mathcal{H}
.

Let us take an orthonormal basis

\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{j-1}
of
\Phi_{j}
,
and take
v_{k,\ell}\in S
(
k=1,\ldots,j-1
,
\ell\in\mathbf{N}
)
such that

v_{k,\ell}\to\varphi_{k}
as
\ell\to\infty
.
Put
V_{\ell}=\langle v_{1,\ell},\ldots,v_{j-1,\ell}\rangle^{\bot}
.
Then we can prove

P_{V_{\ell}}\to 1-P_{j}
as (
\ell\to\infty
)
in the operator norm,
so
\|P_{j}P_{V_{\ell}}\|\to 0
.
Then (
6.5
) implies

\displaystyle\mu_{2,j}(A)\geq\lambda_{j}(A)-(\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{1}(A))\|P_{j}P_{V_{\ell}}\|\to\lambda_{j}(A)\quad(\ell\to\infty).

Thus we have
\mu_{2,j}(A)\geq\lambda_{j}(A)
,
and we conclude
\mu_{2,j}(A)=\lambda_{j}(A)
.
∎

Next we recall some consequences about the eigenvalue counting function,
derived from the min-max principle.

Proposition 6.2
.

For
j=1,2
, let
\mathcal{H}_{j}
be a separable Hilbert space,
and let
A_{j}
be a lower semi-bounded self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}_{j}
.
Assume that there exists a linear isometry
T

from
\mathcal{H}_{1}
to
\mathcal{H}_{2}
such that

TQ(q_{A_{1}})\subset Q(q_{A_{2}})
.

(1)

Assume that

\displaystyle q_{A_{1}}[u]\geq q_{A_{2}}[Tu]

for every
u\in Q(q_{A_{1}})
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle N(\lambda;A_{1})\leq N(\lambda;A_{2})

(6.6)

for every
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}
.

(2)

Assume that

\displaystyle q_{A_{1}}[u]\leq q_{A_{2}}[Tu]

for every
u\in Q(q_{A_{1}})
, and

\displaystyle\dim(Q(q_{A_{2}})/TQ(q_{A_{1}}))=k.

(6.7)

Then, we have

\displaystyle N(\lambda;A_{2})\leq N(\lambda;A_{1})+k

(6.8)

for every
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}
.

Remark.

The statement (1) is well-known (e.g.,
[
10
, Lemme 5.1]
).
The statement (2) is considered to be well-known,
but we give a proof here for readers’ use.

Proof.

Since
T
is an isometry,
we can assume that
\mathcal{H}_{1}

is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{2}
,
and
T
is the inclusion operator
Tv=v
,
without loss of generality.
Then by assumption,

Q(q_{A_{1}})\subset Q(q_{A_{2}})
.

(1) Since
Q(q_{A_{1}})\subset\mathcal{H}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\{V\cap Q(q_{A_{1}});V\subset\mathcal{H}_{1},\dim(\mathcal{H}_{1}/V)\leq j-1\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \{V\cap Q(q_{A_{1}});V\subset\mathcal{H}_{2},\dim(\mathcal{H}_{2}/V)\leq j-1\}.

Thus we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(A_{1})=

\displaystyle\ \sup_{V\subset\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits V\leq j-1}\inf_{u\in V\cap Q(q_{A_{1}}),\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}=1}q_{A_{1}}[u]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sup_{V\subset\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits V\leq j-1}\inf_{u\in V\cap Q(q_{A_{1}}),\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}=1}q_{A_{1}}[u]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \sup_{V\subset\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits V\leq j-1}\inf_{u\in V\cap Q(q_{A_{2}}),\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}=1}q_{A_{2}}[u]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \lambda_{j}(A_{2}).

Thus

\lambda_{j}(A_{1})\leq\lambda

implies

\lambda_{j}(A_{2})\leq\lambda
,
and (
6.6
) holds.

(2) Let
W_{1}
be a subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{2}
such that

\displaystyle W_{1}\cap Q(q_{A_{2}})=\{0\},\ W_{1}\oplus Q(q_{A_{2}})=\mathcal{H}_{2}.

Put
W:=Q(q_{A_{1}})\oplus W_{1}
, then
\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits W=k
by (
6.7
).

If a subspace
V\subset\mathcal{H}_{2}

satisfies
\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits V\leq j-1
,
then
\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits(V\cap W)\leq j+k-1

and
V\cap Q(q_{A_{1}})=(V\cap W)\cap Q(q_{A_{2}})
.
Thus we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{j+k}(A_{2})=

\displaystyle\ \sup_{\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits V\leq j+k-1}\inf_{u\in Q(q_{A_{2}})\cap V}q_{A_{2}}[u]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \sup_{\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits V\leq j-1}\inf_{u\in Q(q_{A_{2}})\cap(V\cap W)}q_{A_{2}}[u]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \sup_{\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits V\leq j-1}\inf_{u\in Q(q_{A_{1}})\cap V}q_{A_{1}}[u]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \lambda_{j}(A_{1}).

Therefore,

\lambda_{j+k}(A_{2})\leq\lambda
implies

\lambda_{j}(A_{1})\leq\lambda
,
and (
6.8
) holds.
∎

6.2
Boundary conditions

In this subsection,
we give a rigorous definition of the operators
-\Delta^{\sharp}_{\alpha,Y,U}

(
\sharp=D
or
N
), given in the introduction.
The operators with point interactions on a bounded domain
are studied by several authors (e.g.,
[
9
,
6
,
28
]
).
In particular,
the papers
[
6
,
28
]
define

-\Delta^{D}_{\alpha,Y,U}
by giving the operator domain,
using the basis of
the deficiency subspaces of the minimal operator

\displaystyle-\Delta^{D}_{\mathop{\rm min}\limits,Y,U}u:=-\Delta|_{Y\cap U}u,

\displaystyle u\in D(-\Delta^{D}_{\mathop{\rm min}\limits,Y,U}):=\{u\in H^{2}(U)\cap H^{1}_{0}(U);

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102ptu(y)=0\mbox{ for every }y\in Y\cap U\},

provided that the boundary
\partial U

is sufficiently smooth.
The paper
[
28
]
also gives the form domain of the quadratic form
associated with
-\Delta^{D}_{\alpha,Y,U}
.
In Lemma
6.5
below,
we define the operators

-\Delta^{\sharp}_{\alpha,Y,U}
(
\sharp=D,N
)
by the method of quadratic form.
The form domain is defined by using compactly supported basis functions

\phi_{y}
(
y\in Y\cap U
), which is useful for our analysis.
The advantages of this method are as follows.

(i)

We can define
the operators

-\Delta^{\sharp}_{\alpha,Y,U}
(
\sharp=D,N
)
on any bounded open set
U
with
\partial U\cap Y=\emptyset
.

(ii)

The properties of eigenvalue counting functions,
e.g., Dirichlet–Neumann bracketing,
are easily proved by the definitions of quadratic forms.

If the boundary
\partial U
of
U
is sufficiently smooth,
then we prove that the corresponding self-adjoint operators
coincide with the ones defined in former papers.

For this purpose,
first we study the quadratic form
q_{\alpha,Y}

associated with the operator
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
.
The quadratic form
q_{\alpha,Y}
is constructed in

[
3
, Appendix F]
and references therein,
by an indirect method using a quadratic form defined on a weighted
L^{2}
-space.
We give here a direct expression of
q_{\alpha,Y}
,
which is suitable for our purpose.

When
d=1
, a simple expression of
q_{\alpha,Y}
is
well-known
(see e.g.,
[
3
, page 79]
).
Let
Y
be a finite set in
\mathbf{R}
and

\alpha=(\alpha_{y})_{y\in Y}
a real-valued sequence.
Then the quadratic form
q_{\alpha,Y}
associated with
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}

and the form domain
Q(q_{\alpha,Y})
are given as

\displaystyle q_{\alpha,Y}[u]:=\|u^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R})}+\sum_{y\in Y}\alpha_{y}|u(y)|^{2},

\displaystyle u\in Q(q_{\alpha,Y}):=H^{1}(\mathbf{R}).

Notice that the value
u(y)
makes sense,
since
u\in H^{1}(\mathbf{R})
is continuous on
\mathbf{R}

by the Sobolev embedding theorem.
Then, for a bounded open interval
U
such that

\partial U\cap Y=\emptyset
,
we define the operator
-\Delta^{\sharp}_{\alpha,Y,U}
(
\sharp=D,N
) as
the self-adjoint operator corresponding to the quadratic form

q^{\sharp}_{\alpha,Y,U}
defined by

\displaystyle q^{D}_{\alpha,Y,U}[u]:=\|u^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}+\sum_{y\in Y\cap U}\alpha_{y}|u(y)|^{2},\quad u\in Q(q^{D}_{\alpha,Y,U}):=H^{1}_{0}(U),

\displaystyle q^{N}_{\alpha,Y,U}[u]:=\|u^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}+\sum_{y\in Y\cap U}\alpha_{y}|u(y)|^{2},\quad u\in Q(q^{N}_{\alpha,Y,U}):=H^{1}(U).

Then it is easy to see that

u\in D(-\Delta^{D}_{\alpha,Y,U})
satisfies
the Dirichlet boundary condition
u(x)=0
on
\partial U
,
and

u\in D(-\Delta^{N}_{\alpha,Y,U})
satisfies
the Neumann boundary condition
u^{\prime}(x)=0
on
\partial U
.

When
d=2
or
3
,
we cannot define
q^{\sharp}_{\alpha,Y,U}
in the same way,
because

(i)

u\in D(-\Delta_{\alpha,Y})

does not
imply

\nabla u\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d})^{d}
,
so
\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})^{d}}^{2}

does not make sense, and

(ii)

u(y)

does not
make sense
for
u\in H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

So we use a different method using an operator core of
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
.

First we begin with an elementary lemma
about the Sobolev spaces.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
d\geq 2
.
Let
Y
be a finite set in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
Then,
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y)

is dense in
H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

Remark.

The statement of Lemma
6.3
is a well-known consequence of
the theory of polar sets (cf.
[
1
]
);
here we give a proof for readers’ use.
The conclusion is true also for
d\geq 4
,
but it is false when
d=1
,
since the map
H^{1}(\mathbf{R})\ni u\mapsto u(y)
is continuous.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
Y=\{0\}
.
By
[
1
, Theorem 3.23]
,

C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})
is dense in

H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
if and only if
\{0\}
is
(1,2)
-polar,
that is,
if
u\in H^{-1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
satisfies
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits u\subset\{0\}
,
then
u=0
.
Here

\displaystyle H^{-1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}):=\{u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d});\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|\hat{u}(\xi)|^{2}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-1}d\xi<\infty\},

where
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is the space of the tempered distributions,
and
\hat{u}
is the Fourier transform of
u
.

Assume that
u\in H^{-1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits u\subset\{0\}
.
Then, by
[
35
, Theorem 6.25]
,
there exist
N\in\mathbf{N}
and constants
c_{j_{1},...,j_{d}}
such that

\displaystyle u=\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{d}\leq N}c_{j_{1},...,j_{d}}\frac{\partial^{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{d}}}{\partial x_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{d}^{j_{d}}}\delta_{0},

where
the sum ranges over all non-negative integers

j_{1},...,j_{d}
with
j_{1}+\ldots+j_{d}\leq N
,
and
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac delta distribution at
x=0
.
Taking the Fourier transform, we have

\displaystyle\hat{u}(\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{d}\leq N}c_{j_{1},...,j_{d}}(i\xi_{1})^{j_{1}}\cdots(i\xi_{d})^{j_{d}}.

However, since
d\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|p(\xi)|^{2}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-1}d\xi=\infty

for every non-zero polynomial
p(\xi)
.
Thus
u\in H^{-1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
implies that
c_{j_{1},...,j_{d}}=0
for all

j_{1},...,j_{d}
, so
u=0
.
Thus the conclusion holds.
∎

Next we specify the quadratic form
associated with
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
,
when
d=2
or
3
and
Y
is a finite set.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
d=2
or
3
.
Let
Y
be a finite set in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
and
\alpha=(\alpha_{y})_{y\in Y}
be a sequence of real numbers.
Let
q_{\alpha,Y}:=q_{-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}}
be the quadratic form
associated with
the semi-bounded self-adjoint operator
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
,
and
Q(q_{\alpha,Y})
be the form domain of
q_{\alpha,Y}
.
Let

\displaystyle d_{*}:=\mathop{\rm min}\limits_{y,y^{\prime}\in Y,y\not=y^{\prime}}|y-y^{\prime}|,

and
d_{0}
be any number such that
0<d_{0}\leq d_{*}
.
Let
\chi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R})
such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\chi(r)=1&(r\leq d_{0}/4),\\
0\leq\chi(r)\leq 1&(d_{0}/4<r<d_{0}/3),\\
\chi(r)=0&(r\geq d_{0}/3).\end{cases}

For each
y\in Y
, let

\displaystyle\phi_{y}(x):=\begin{cases}\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|+\alpha\right)\chi(|x-y|)&(d=2),\\
\left(\frac{1}{4\pi}|x-y|^{-1}+\alpha\right)\chi(|x-y|)&(d=3),\end{cases}

\displaystyle A_{y}:=\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}\,;\,d_{0}/4\leq|x-y|\leq d_{0}/3\}.

Then, the following holds.

(1)

The form domain
Q(q_{\alpha,Y})

is given as

\displaystyle Q(q_{\alpha,Y})=H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\oplus\bigoplus_{y\in Y}\mathbf{C}\phi_{y},

(6.9)

where
\oplus
denotes the algebraic direct sum of subspaces.

(2)

The quadratic form
q_{\alpha,Y}

is given as

\displaystyle\ q_{\alpha,Y}(u,v)=(\nabla u_{0},\nabla v_{0})_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})^{d}}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{y\in Y}\Bigl((c_{y}\nabla\phi_{y},\nabla v_{0})_{L^{2}(A_{y})^{d}}+(\nabla u_{0},d_{y}\nabla\phi_{y})_{L^{2}(A_{y})^{d}}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-\overline{c_{y}}\int_{\partial A_{y}}\overline{\frac{\partial\phi_{y}}{\partial n}}v_{0}\,dS-d_{y}\int_{\partial A_{y}}\overline{u_{0}}\frac{\partial\phi_{y}}{\partial n}dS

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\overline{c_{y}}d_{y}(\phi_{y},-\Delta\phi_{y})_{L^{2}(A_{y})}\Bigr),

(6.10)

\displaystyle\ u=u_{0}+\sum_{y\in Y}c_{y}\phi_{y},\ v=v_{0}+\sum_{y\in Y}d_{y}\phi_{y}\in Q(q_{\alpha,Y}),

\displaystyle\ u_{0},v_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),\ c_{y},d_{y}\in\mathbf{C}.

Here,
\partial\phi_{y}/\partial n=\nabla\phi_{y}\cdot n
,

n
is the unit outer normal vector on
\partial A_{y}
,
and
dS
is the surface measure on
\partial A_{y}
.

Remark.

1. The surface integrals in (
6.10
) make sense,
since
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
implies that

u_{0}|_{\partial A_{y}}\in H^{1/2}(\partial A_{y};dS)\subset L^{2}(\partial A_{y};dS)
,
by the trace theorem
[
37
, section 8]
.

2.
The equality (
6.9
) implies that
the form domain
Q(q_{\alpha,Y})
is independent of
\alpha
,
since the regular part of
\phi_{y}
, i.e.
\alpha_{y}\chi(|x-y|)
,
belongs to
H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,
and the singular part is independent of
\alpha
.
Similarly, the right hand side of (
6.9
)
is independent of the choice of
d_{0}
.

Proof.

(1) By definition,
\phi_{y}\in L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for
y\in Y
.
Moreover, it is easy to check that

\displaystyle\Delta|_{\mathbf{R}^{2}\setminus Y}\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|+\alpha\right)=0,

\displaystyle\Delta|_{\mathbf{R}^{3}\setminus Y}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi}|x-y|^{-1}+\alpha\right)=0.

Then the Leibniz rule implies

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(-\Delta|_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y}\phi_{y})\subset\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits\nabla\chi(|\cdot-y|)\subset A_{y}.

(6.11)

By (
6.11
), we have
-\Delta|_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y}\phi_{y}\in L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
Moreover, since
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits\phi_{y}\cap Y=\{y\}
and

\phi_{y}
satisfies the boundary condition
(BC)_{y}
,
we see that
\phi_{y}\in D(-\Delta_{\alpha,Y})
.

Let
D_{0}=H^{2}_{0}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y)
,
the closure of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y)

with respect to
H^{2}
-norm.
Since the symmetric operator
-\Delta|_{C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y)}

has deficiency indices
(N,N)
(
N=\#Y
)(see
[
3
]
),
we see that
the dimension of the quotient space

D(-\Delta_{\alpha,Y})/D_{0}

is equal to
N
.
Moreover,
\phi_{y}\not\in D_{0}
, since
D_{0}\subset H^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

and any element of
H^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is continuous for
d=2
or
3
,
by the Sobolev embedding theorem.
Thus the space

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}=C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus{Y})\oplus\bigoplus_{y\in Y}\mathbf{C}\phi_{y}

is an operator core of
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
.
So
\widetilde{D}
is also a form core of
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
.

Since
Y
is a finite set,
the operator
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
is lower semi-bounded.
Let
M=-\inf\sigma(-\Delta_{\alpha,Y})+1
,
and define the norm
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
on
\widetilde{D}
by

\displaystyle\|u\|_{Q}^{2}=(u,-\Delta|_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y}u)+M\|u\|^{2}

for
u\in\widetilde{D}
.
By the choice of
M
, we have

\displaystyle\|u\|_{Q}^{2}\geq\|u\|^{2}

for
u\in\widetilde{D}
,
so
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
is actually a norm on
\widetilde{D}
.
Since
\widetilde{D}
is a form core,

Q(q_{\alpha,Y})
is
the closure of
\widetilde{D}
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
.
Thus, in order to prove (
6.9
),
it is sufficient to prove

(i)

\widetilde{D}
is dense in

\widetilde{Q}:=H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\oplus\bigoplus_{y\in Y}\mathbf{C}\phi_{y}
,
and

(ii)

\widetilde{Q}
is a closed subspace of
Q(q_{\alpha,Y})
.

To see (i), notice that
we have for
u_{0}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y)

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{Q}^{2}=\|\nabla u_{0}\|^{2}+M\|u_{0}\|^{2},

which is equivalent to
H^{1}
-norm.
Thus
the closure of

C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y)

with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{Q}

coincides with
the closure of

C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y)

with respect to
H^{1}
-norm,
which is equal to
H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

by Lemma
6.3
.
Thus (i) holds.

To see (ii),
notice that
Q(q_{\alpha,Y})
is a Hilbert space
equipped with the inner product

\displaystyle(u,v)_{Q}=q_{\alpha,Y}(u,v)+M(u,v),

H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is a closed subspace of
Q(q_{\alpha,Y})
,
and
\dim(\widetilde{Q}/H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))=\#Y<\infty.

Therefore
(ii) is a consequence of the following general statement;
‘if
X
is a Hilbert space,

V_{1}
is a closed subspace of
X
,

V_{2}
is a subspace of
X
with
V_{2}\supset V_{1}

and
\dim(V_{2}/V_{1})<\infty
,
then
V_{2}
is a closed subspace of
X
’.

(2)
Let
u,v\in\widetilde{D}
. Then
u
is written as

\displaystyle u=u_{0}+\sum_{y\in Y}c_{y}\phi_{y},\ v=v_{0}+\sum_{y\in Y}d_{y}\phi_{y},

\displaystyle u_{0},v_{0}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y),\ c_{y},d_{y}\in\mathbf{C}.

Then the quadratic form
q_{\alpha,Y}(u,v)=(u,-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}v)

is calculated as follows.

\displaystyle(u,-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}v)=

\displaystyle\ (u_{0},-\Delta v_{0})+\sum_{y\in Y}\Bigl\{(u_{0},d_{y}(-\Delta|_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y}\phi_{y}))

\displaystyle+(c_{y}\phi_{y},-\Delta v_{0})+(c_{y}\phi_{y},d_{y}(-\Delta|_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y}\phi_{y}))\Bigr\},

(6.12)

since
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits\phi_{y}

(y\in Y)
are disjoint.
By (
6.11
), we have by integration by parts

\displaystyle(u_{0},-\Delta v_{0})=

\displaystyle(\nabla u_{0},\nabla v_{0}),

\displaystyle(u_{0},d_{y}(-\Delta|_{\mathbf{R^{d}}\setminus Y}\phi_{y}))=

\displaystyle\ (u_{0},d_{y}(-\Delta\phi_{y}))_{L^{2}(A_{y})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ (\nabla u_{0},d_{y}\nabla\phi_{y})_{L^{2}(A_{y})^{d}}-d_{y}\int_{\partial A_{y}}\overline{u_{0}}\frac{\partial\phi_{y}}{\partial n}dS,

\displaystyle(c_{y}\phi_{y},-\Delta v_{0})=

\displaystyle\ (c_{y}(-\Delta|_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y}\phi_{y}),v_{0})=(c_{y}(-\Delta\phi_{y}),v_{0})_{L^{2}(A_{y})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ (c_{y}\nabla\phi_{y},\nabla v_{0})_{L^{2}(A_{y})^{d}}-\overline{c_{y}}\int_{\partial A_{y}}\overline{\frac{\partial\phi_{y}}{\partial n}}v_{0}dS,

\displaystyle(c_{y}\phi_{y},d_{y}(-\Delta|_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus Y}\phi_{y}))=

\displaystyle\ \overline{c_{y}}d_{y}(\phi_{y},-\Delta\phi_{y})_{L^{2}(A_{y})}.

Combining these equalities with
(
6.12
), we have (
6.10
)
for
u\in\widetilde{D}
.
Moreover,
if we fix
c_{y}
and
d_{y}
,
the right hand side of (
6.10
) is continuous
with respect to
u_{0},v_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,
by the trace theorem
[
37

(i.e.

6.3
Existence of IDS

In this subsection,
we shall prove the almost sure existence of IDS,
defined in (

),
by the functional analytic method
using the Dirichlet–Neumann bracketing (cf.
[
8
]
or

[
23
, section 2.3]
).
In the present paper
Y_{\omega}
is assumed to be the
Poisson configuration,
but here we prove our results under more general assumptions
described below,
for the future development.

Let
d=1,2
or
3
.
Let
(\Omega_{1},P_{\Omega_{1}})
be a probability space,
and
\xi_{\omega_{1}}
(
\omega_{1}\in\Omega_{1}
) be a
non-negative integer-valued random measure on

(\mathbf{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))

(
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
)
satisfying the following conditions.

(I1)

For any Borel measurable set
S
in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
the function
\omega_{1}\mapsto\xi_{\omega_{1}}(S)
is a measurable
map from
\Omega_{1}
to
[0,\infty]
.
Moreover, if
S
is bounded, then

\xi_{\omega_{1}}(S)
is finite almost surely.

(I2)

The measure
\xi_{\omega_{1}}
is simple, almost surely.

Here, we say a non-negative integer-valued measure
\xi
on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
is

simple
if
\xi(\{x\})\leq 1
for every
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}

(cf.
[
27
]
).
If
\xi_{\omega_{1}}
satisfies (I1),
then we call
\xi_{\omega_{1}}
a
boundedly finite point process
,
and if
\xi_{\omega_{1}}
additionally satisfies (I2),
then we say that the process
\xi_{\omega_{1}}
is
simple

(cf.
[
27
,
13
,
20
]
).
A point process
\xi_{\omega_{1}}
satisfying (I1) and (I2)
is identified with the support

Y_{\omega_{1}}
of the measure
\xi_{\omega_{1}}
.
The set
Y_{\omega_{1}}
is a locally finite subset of
\mathbf{R}^{d}

and
\xi_{\omega_{1}}(S)=\#(Y_{\omega_{1}}\cap S)
,
almost surely.
In the sequel,
we also call
Y_{\omega_{1}}
a point process
by abuse of terminology,
and describe the assumptions in terms of
Y_{\omega_{1}}
.

Additionally, we assume the following.
Let
X
be the measure space of all locally finite subsets
Y
of

\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
equipped with the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the maps

\{Y\mapsto\#(Y\cap S)\}_{S}
, where
S
ranges over
all bounded Borel subsets of
\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
We use the notation

\displaystyle A+x:=\{Y+x\in X;Y\in A\},\quad Y+x:=\{y+x\in\mathbf{R}^{d};y\in Y\},

for
A\in X
and
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
The terminologies used below are taken from the standard textbooks
(cf.
[
27
,
13
,
20
]
).

(I3)

The intensity measure

\mu(S):=\mathbf{E}_{\Omega_{1}}[\#(Y_{\omega_{1}}\cap S)]

is
boundedly finite
,
that is,
\mu(S)<\infty
for any bounded measurable set
S
.

(I4)

The process
Y_{\omega_{1}}
is
\mathbf{Z}^{d}
-

\displaystyle Y_{\omega_{1}}+n=Y_{T_{n}\omega_{1}}

almost surely, for every
n\in\mathbf{Z}^{d}
.

(I5)

The process

\mathbf{Z}^{d}

of

\displaystyle A+n=A

or

(I6)

\#(Y_{\omega_{1}}\cap(\partial Q_{1}+n))=0

almost surely,

7.1
Poisson point process

The definition of the Poisson point process is as follows (see e.g.,
[
34
]
).

Definition 7.1
.

Let
S
be a Borel measurable set in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
(
d\geq 1
).
Let
\mu_{\omega}
be a random measure on
S

dependent on
\omega\in\Omega
for some probability space
\Omega
.
For a positive constant
\rho
,
we say
\mu_{\omega}
is the Poisson point process on
S

with intensity measure
\rho dx

if the following conditions hold.

(i)

For every Borel measurable set
E
in
S

with the Lebesgue measure
|E|<\infty
,

\mu_{\omega}(E)
is an integer-valued random variable on
\Omega
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\mu_{\omega}(E)=k)=\frac{(\rho|E|)^{k}}{k!}e^{-\rho|E|}

for every non-negative integer
k
.

(ii)

For any disjoint Borel measurable sets
E_{1},\ldots,E_{n}
in
S

with finite Lebesgue measure,
the random variables
\{\mu_{\omega}(E_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}

are independent.

We call the support
Y_{\omega}

of the Poisson point process measure
\mu_{\omega}

the Poisson configuration on
S
.

We review a construction of the Poisson configuration on
S

when
|S|
is finite
(for the proof, see
[
34
, Theorem 1.2.1]
).

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
S
be a Borel measurable set in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
(
d\geq 1
)
with Lebesgue measure
|S|<\infty
.
Let
\rho>0
be a constant.
Let
n_{\omega}
be a random variable
obeying the Poisson distribution with parameter
\rho|S|
.
Let
\{y_{j,\omega}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be independent
\mathbf{R}^{d}
-valued
random variables obeying the uniform distribution on
S
,
which are independent of
n_{\omega}
.
Define the random set
Y_{\omega}
by

\displaystyle Y_{\omega}=\begin{cases}\{y_{j,\omega}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\omega}}&(n_{\omega}\geq 1),\\
\emptyset&(n_{\omega}=0).\end{cases}

Then,
Y_{\omega}
is a Poisson configuration on
S
with intensity

\rho dx
.

The Poisson configuration on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
can be constructed
by dividing
\mathbf{R}^{d}
into disjoint cubes
Q_{j}
,
constructing independent Poisson configurations
Y_{j}
on
Q_{j}
,
and taking the union of
Y_{j}
.

7.2
Palm distribution

We quote some basic definitions
about the Palm distribution of a random measure,
from
Daley–Vere-Jones
[
14
]
.
We say a Borel measure

on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
is

in
\mathbf{R}^{d}

Let

be the space of boundedly finite measures

on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
equipped with the topology of weak convergence
and the corresponding Borel

-algebra.
Let
\xi
be a boundedly finite

on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
that is,

is a measurable map
from some probability space
\Omega

to
M

We assume that the
first moment measure

(or the
intensity measure
)

\displaystyle\mu(U):=\mathbf{E}[\xi_{\omega}(U)]

is also boundedly finite,
where
\mathbf{E}[f(\omega)]=\int_{\Omega}f(\omega)dP(\omega)

is the expectation of a measurable function

with respect to the probability measure

on
\Omega
.
Then, it is known that

a family of probability measures

on

such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}g(x,\xi_{\omega})d\xi_{\omega}(x)\right]=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[g(x,\xi_{\omega})\right]d\mu(x)

for any non-negative measurable

on
\mathbf{R}^{d}\times M

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(\omega)]=\int_{\Omega}f(\omega)dP_{x}(\omega)

[

The measure

\xi

The local Palm distribution

is,
roughly speaking,
the conditional distribution of
the random measure
\xi

under the condition

.

In the case that
\xi
is
the homogeneous Poisson point process on
\mathbf{R}^{d}

with intensity measure

are equal (cf.
[
14

),
where

is the Dirac measure supported on

.
Thus we have the following corollary.

\xi

\mathbf{R}^{d}

, where
\rho

By using (

we give another proof of Proposition
3.1

For

), we have

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(s,\alpha_{1}^{\prime})\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{m}(s,\alpha_{1}^{\prime})\leq 0.

By (3) of Proposition
7.4
, this inequality implies

\displaystyle N_{\alpha^{\prime},Y}(-s^{2})\geq m=N_{\alpha,Y}(-s^{2})

(7.5)

for
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
satisfying (
7.4
).
Then we can prove (
7.5
) in general case
by using (
7.5
) in the case (
7.4
) repeatedly.

(2) This statement is a consequence of
Lemma
6.4

and Proposition
6.2
with
T=Id
.
∎

In
[
3
, Theorem II.1.1.4]
,
it is stated that
0
is not an eigenvalue of
the operator
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
,
if
Y
is a finite set.
But this statement is incorrect.
According to ‘Errata and Addenda’ in
[
3
, page 485]
,
the unpublished manuscript
‘A remark on the existence of zero-energy bound states for
point interaction Hamiltonians’ by G. F. Dell’Antonio and G. Panati
gives an example of
(\alpha,Y)
with
\#Y=3
such that

0
is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
.
In general, there exist pairs
(\alpha,Y)
with

2\leq\#Y<\infty
, such that

0
is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
.
This fact is proved in
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
when
the space dimension
d=2
,
and also pointed out in the remark of
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
when
d=3
.
Here we give a simple proof of this fact when
d=3
,
for readers’ use.

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
d=3
.
Let
Y=(y_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
be a finite subset of
\mathbf{R}^{3}

with
\#Y=n<\infty
.
Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
be a sequence of real numbers.
Let
A(0)
be the
n\times n
matrix
A(s)
given in (
7.2
) with
s=0
,
that is,

\displaystyle A(0)=(a_{jk}(0)),\quad a_{jk}(0)=\begin{cases}\alpha_{j}&(j=k),\\
-\frac{1}{4\pi|y_{j}-y_{k}|}&(j\not=k).\end{cases}

If
\det A(0)=0
and
\mathop{\rm Ker}\nolimits A(0)
has a
non-zero vector
c=(c_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
with

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}=0,

(7.6)

then

0
is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\det A(0)=0
and
\mathop{\rm Ker}\nolimits A(0)
has a non-zero vector

c=(c_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
with
\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}=0
.
Let
u
be the function given by

\displaystyle u(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{c_{j}}{4\pi|x-y_{j}|}.

Since

\displaystyle u(x)=\frac{u_{y_{j},0}}{|x-y_{j}|}+u_{y_{j},1}+O(|x-y_{j}|)\quad(x\to y_{j}),

\displaystyle u_{y_{j},0}=\frac{c_{j}}{4\pi},\quad u_{y_{j},1}=\sum_{k\not=j}\frac{c_{k}}{4\pi|y_{k}-y_{j}|},

the function
u
satisfies

(BC)_{y_{j}}:-4\pi\alpha_{j}u_{y_{j},0}+u_{y_{j},1}=0

for every
j=1,\ldots,n
,
if and only if
c\in\mathop{\rm Ker}\nolimits A(0)
.
The function
u
also satisfies
the Laplace equation
-\Delta u=0
on
\mathbf{R}^{3}\setminus Y
.
Moreover, since

\displaystyle\frac{c_{j}}{4\pi|x-y_{j}|}=\frac{c_{j}}{4\pi|x|}+O(|x|^{-2})\quad(|x|\rightarrow\infty),

the condition (
7.6
) implies that
the function
u
satisfies
u(x)=O(|x|^{-2})
as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
,
and
u\in L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{3})
.
Thus
u
is an eigenfunction of
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}

with eigenvalue
0
.
∎

For example, let
n=2
,

Y=\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
with
|y_{1}-y_{2}|=R>0
,
and
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=-1/(4\pi R)
.
Then the matrix

\displaystyle A(0)=-\frac{1}{4\pi R}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}

satisfies
\det A(0)=0
,
and
\mathop{\rm Ker}\nolimits A(0)
has a non-zero vector
c=(1,-1)^{t}
.
Thus
0
is an eigenvalue of the operator
-\Delta_{\alpha,Y}
.

7.4
Estimate for the operator norm

We review an elementary estimate
for the operator norm of a matrix operator.

Proposition 7.7
.

For a countable set
X
,
let
M=(m_{xy})_{x,y\in X}
be a

\mathbf{C}
-valued matrix satisfying

\displaystyle C_{1}:=\sup_{x\in X}\sum_{y\in Y}|m_{xy}|<\infty,\quad C_{2}:=\sup_{y\in Y}\sum_{x\in X}|m_{xy}|<\infty.

Then, the operator on
\ell^{2}(X)

defined by

\displaystyle Mf(x)=\sum_{y\in Y}m_{xy}f(y)\quad(f\in\ell^{2}(X))

is a bounded linear operator on
\ell^{2}(X)

and the operator norm
\|M\|
satisfies

\displaystyle\|M\|\leq\sqrt{C_{1}C_{2}}.

Especially when
M
is a Hermitian operator (
\overline{m_{xy}}=m_{yx}
),
we have
C_{1}=C_{2}
and
\|M\|\leq C_{1}
.

Proof.

For every
x\in X
, we have by the Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle|Mf(x)|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\ \left(\sum_{y\in Y}|m_{xy}||f(y)|\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{y\in Y}|m_{xy}|\cdot\sum_{y\in Y}|m_{xy}||f(y)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ C_{1}\sum_{y\in Y}|m_{xy}||f(y)|^{2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\|Mf\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}|Mf(x)|^{2}\leq C_{1}\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{y\in Y}|m_{xy}||f(y)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ C_{1}\sum_{y\in Y}\left(\sum_{x\in X}|m_{xy}|\right)|f(y)|^{2}\leq C_{1}C_{2}\|f\|^{2}.

∎
