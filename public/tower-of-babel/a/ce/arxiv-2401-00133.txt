Lossless digraph signal processing via polar decomposition
Feng Ji

Abstract
In this paper, we present a signal processing framework for directed graphs. Unlike undirected graphs, a graph shift operator such as the adjacency matrix associated with a directed graph usually does not admit an orthogonal eigenbasis. This makes it challenging to define the Fourier transform. Our methodology leverages the polar decomposition to define two distinct eigendecompositions, each associated with different matrices derived from this decomposition. We propose to extend the frequency domain and introduce a Fourier transform that jointly encodes the spectral response of a signal for the two eigenbases from the polar decomposition. This allows us to define convolution following a standard routine. Our approach has two features: it is lossless as the shift operator can be fully recovered from factors of the polar decomposition. Moreover, it subsumes the traditional graph signal processing if the graph is directed. We present numerical results to show how the framework can be applied.

Introduction

The field of graph signal processing (GSP) is experiencing significant growth and focuses on the analysis of signals defined on graphs
[

In GSP, the key idea revolves around the vector space of graph signals, where each node in a given graph is assigned a numerical value by a graph signal. As signals on a graph form a vector space, GSP employs linear transformations, such as the graph Fourier transform (GFT) and graph filters, to examine and explore relationships among graph signals.

On the other hand, despite a growing need for applications that involve directed graphs
(digraphs)
[
9
,
10
]
,
the intrinsic asymmetry of their edge connections presents challenges in defining GFTs. First, the basic graph operators become non-diagonalizable for a large number of digraphs. This makes it challenging to define key concepts such as the frequency domain and convolution. Second, “ordering" the graph Fourier basis, so that we can interpret them as low or high frequencies is difficult. This is because the eigenvalues, which can be used for the ordering, can be complex numbers.

As a consequence, a variety of approaches have been proposed to address these challenges. For example, studies directly using the adjacency matrix as the graph operator have suggested utilizing the Jordan decomposition
[
11
,
12
]
or the singular value decomposition
[
13
]
of the adjacency matrix for constructing GFT. Symmetrization of the adjacency matrix has also been studied
[
14
]
.
[
15
]
proposes solving an optimization based on graph directed variation to obtain an orthonormal basis for Fourier transform.
There are studies that define the graph operator as decomposed matrices of the adjacency matrix, producing diagonalizable matrices
[
16
,
17
]
. In
[
18
]
, the authors propose to directly modify the graph Laplacian so that the resulting one is Hermitian and hence diagonalizable.
The recent work
[
19
]
proposes to modify the graph structure so that the corresponding graph operator on the resulting graph becomes diagonalizable. However, these approaches have their respective shortcomings. For example, the Jordan decomposition is not orthogonal. Hence, signal energy is not preserved after the Fourier transformation, and the basis vectors have non-trivial interactions among themselves. The approaches of optimization and topology perturbation are usually not lossless. The original directed graph topology cannot be faithfully recovered from the basis of the Fourier transform.

In this paper, we propose a lossless digraph signal process scheme. There are two main ingredients of our approach. For a graph shift operator (GSO)
\mathbf{S}
, which may not be symmetric, we consider the polar decomposition of
\mathbf{S}
. This means that
\mathbf{S}
is the product of a unitary matrix
\mathbf{U}
and symmetric matrix
\mathbf{P}
, both are orthogonally diagonalizable. Then, we introduce the Fourier transform that utilizes the orthonormal basis of
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{P}
jointly. Unlike the traditional GSP, the frequency domain is
n^{2}
dimensional instead of
n
dimensional, which allows us to package more information and thus the framework is lossless. Moreover, we also demonstrate that our framework subsumes the traditional GSP if
\mathbf{S}
is symmetric, and hence we have a strict generalization of GSP.

II
Preliminaries

In this section, we review the basics of graph signal processing (GSP)
[
1
]
and polar decomposition
[
20
]
. In the paper, we use the term “GSP” exclusively for the traditional graph signal processing on undirected graphs.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph of size
n
. If
G
is undirected, GSP requires a symmetric graph shift operator (GSO)
\mathbf{S}
. Common choices of
\mathbf{S}
include its adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
and the Laplacian matrix
\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{D}
, where
\mathbf{D}
is the degree matrix. By the spectral theorem, the symmetric matrix
\mathbf{S}
admits an eigendecomposition
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{V}\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{{}^{\intercal}}
, where
\bm{\Lambda}
is the diagonal matrix consisting of eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}
and the columns of
\mathbf{V}
are the corresponding eigenvectors. Thus
\mathbf{V}
is an orthogonal matrix. Suppose
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
has a fixed ordering. A graph signal
\mathbf{f}=(x_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
x_{i}
is the signal assigned to
v_{i}
. The graph Fourier transform (GFT) of
\mathbf{x}
w.r.t.
\mathbf{S}
is

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{f}}=\mathbf{V}^{{}^{\intercal}}\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(1)

The frequency domain is indexed by the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}
.

However, if
G
is directed, then we have the challenge that
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{A}
or
\mathbf{L}
may not be symmetric. We usually do not have an eigendecomposition and thus important signal processing notions such as GFT (
1
) cannot be properly defined. We propose to resolve this issue using matrix polar decomposition
[
20
,
21
]
, which is reviewed next.

Recall that for any
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{S}
, its polar decomposition is
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}
, where
\mathbf{U}
is an orthogonal matrix and
\mathbf{P}
is symmetric and positive semi-definite. Moreover, if
\mathbf{S}
is invertible, then the decomposition is unique, and
\mathbf{P}
is positive definite. Explicitly, let
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{V}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}^{{}^{\intercal}}
be the singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{S}

[
22
]
, then
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{W}^{{}^{\intercal}}
and
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{W}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}^{{}^{\intercal}}
.

Both
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{P}
are normal and thus they admit (complex) eigendecompositions, i.e.,
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{V}_{1}\bm{\Lambda}_{1}\mathbf{V}_{1}^{H}
and
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{V}_{2}\bm{\Lambda}_{2}\mathbf{V}_{2}^{H}
, where
\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{2}
are unitary,
\bm{\Lambda}_{1},\bm{\Lambda}_{2}
are diagonal and
H
is the adjoint operator (Hermitian transpose). Geometrically,
\mathbf{P}
performs scaling, while
\mathbf{U}
performs a rotation/reflection. By convention, we increasingly order the eigenvalues of
\bm{\Lambda}_{2}
according to the absolute value and those of
\bm{\Lambda}_{1}
according to the complex phase. We shall use
\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{2}
to define the Fourier transform in the next section.

III
The Fourier transform

In this section, we define the Fourier transform. We use the same notations as in
Section

II
for the polar decomposition. To motivate, consider a (complex) graph signal
\mathbf{f}
. We want to study its shifted signal
\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{f})=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{f})
. Its magnitude
|\mathbf{g}|
is the same as
|\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{f})|
. Therefore, taking ordinary GFT (
1
) w.r.t.
\mathbf{P}
gives us “smoothness” information of
\mathbf{f}
. However, rotational information is missing if we ignore
\mathbf{U}
. To incorporate contributions from both
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{U}
, we follow the idea of
[
8
]
and propose to expand the spectral domain from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
. Following the ordering of the matrix multiplication, the proposed Fourier transform should encode how
\mathbf{f}
respond w.r.t.
\mathbf{P}
and how eigenvectors of
\mathbf{P}
should respond w.r.t.
\mathbf{U}
(cf.
Section

VI-C
below).

Specifically, we denote the columns of
\mathbf{V}_{1}
by
\{\mathbf{v}_{1,1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{1,n}\}
and of
\mathbf{V}_{2}
by
\{\mathbf{v}_{2,1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{2,n}\}
. They are the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{V}_{1}
and
\mathbf{V}_{2}
respectively. For the signal
\mathbf{f}
, we may express it as

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{2,i},

(2)

where
\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{i}=\langle\mathbf{v}_{2,i},\mathbf{f}\rangle=\mathbf{v}_{2,i}^{H}\mathbf{f}
is the GFT of
\mathbf{f}
w.r.t.
\mathbf{P}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the complex inner product.

On the other hand, for each
\mathbf{v}_{2,i}
, we may inspect its “angle” w.r.t. to each direction of rotation
\mathbf{v}_{1,j}
as
p_{i,j}=\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,j},\mathbf{v}_{2,i}\rangle
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{2,i}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}p_{i,j}\mathbf{v}_{1,j}.

(3)

Combining (
2
) and (
3
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\Big(\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{i}p_{i,j}\Big)\mathbf{v}_{1,j}.

Therefore, we define the Fourier transform by considering contributions from
\mathbf{V}_{1}
and
\mathbf{V}_{2}
jointly.

Definition 1
.

The
Fourier transform
w.r.t.
\mathbf{S}
is
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{S}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
is defined as follows: for a signal
\mathbf{f}
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{f})_{i,j}=\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{i}p_{i,j}=\langle\mathbf{v}_{2,i},\mathbf{f}\rangle\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,j},\mathbf{v}_{2,i}\rangle.

(4)

For simplicity, we usually denote
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{f})
by
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{f})
or
\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}
if
\mathbf{S}
is clear from the context.

The
inverse transform

\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{S}}:\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
(or simply
\mathcal{I}
) is

\displaystyle(a_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\mapsto\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i,j}\mathbf{v}_{1,j}.

To give an explicit matrix formula for the Fourier transform, let
\mathbf{g}
be any (column) vector, and use
D(\mathbf{g})
to denote the diagonal matrix with diagonal
\mathbf{g}
. Moreover, use
\mathbf{c}
to denote the constant (column) vector with each entry
1
. Then we have the following observations.

Lemma 1
.

1.

\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{f})=\mathbf{V}_{1}^{H}\mathbf{V}_{2}D(\mathbf{V}_{2}^{H}\mathbf{f})
and
\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{M})=\mathbf{V}_{1}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{c}
for
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
.

2.

Parseval’s identity:
{\left\lVert{\mathbf{f}}\right\rVert}={\left\lVert{\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{f})}\right\rVert}
, where
{\left\lVert{\cdot}\right\rVert}
is the complex Euclidean norm.

Proof.

(a) follows directly from the definition. For (b), to show
{\left\lVert{\mathbf{f}}\right\rVert}^{2}={\left\lVert{\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{f})}\right\rVert}^{2}
, we compute

\displaystyle{\left\lVert{\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{f})}\right\rVert}^{2}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}|\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{i}p_{i,j}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}|\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{i}|^{2}\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}|p_{i,j}|^{2}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}|\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{i}|^{2}{\left\lVert{\mathbf{v}_{2,i}}\right\rVert}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}|\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{i}|^{2}={\left\lVert{\mathbf{f}}\right\rVert}^{2}.

∎

The proposed framework recovers that of traditional GSP. To see this, suppose the GSO
\mathbf{S}
is normal, e.g., symmetric or orthogonal. In this case, both
\mathbf{V}_{1}
and
\mathbf{V}_{2}
can be chosen to be equal. This implies that
p_{i,j}=\delta_{i,j}=1
if
i=j
and
0
otherwise. Therefore,
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{f})_{i,j}=\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{i}
if
i=j
and
0
otherwise. This is the reason why for undirected graphs or directed cycle graphs, we only need
n
(diagonal) components in GSP.

Example 1
.

In this example, we show sample spectral plots (in Fig.
1
) of the same signal on different directed graphs. Let
G_{0},\ldots,G_{5}
be directed graphs on
50
nodes. For the extreme cases,
G_{0}
is the undirected cycle graph and
G_{5}
is the directed cycle graph. Intermediately, for
0<k<5
,
G_{k}
is obtained from
G_{0}
by making randomly chosen
10k
edges directed according to the ordering of the nodes. Shift operators are the (directed) Laplacians
\mathbf{L}_{k},0\leq k\leq 5
. We choose a bandlimited signal
\mathbf{f}
w.r.t.
\mathbf{L}_{0}
. The spectral plots of the componentwise absolute values of its Fourier transforms w.r.t.
\mathbf{L}_{k},0\leq i\leq 5
are shown in Fig.
1
. For
\mathbf{L}_{0}
, the result is the same as that traditional GSP offers (placed along the diagonal). If we let
k
increase, we see that the patterns change gradually. We shall see that this supports the result on graph perturbation in
Section

V
below. If the graph differs more from

, then the spectrum of

is more spread out, suggesting the usefulness of having a lossless signal processing framework.

IV
Convolution

Important tools in GSP are the convolutions
[
1
,
23
,
24
]
. One way to interpret the convolution is to consider it as a polynomial in the GSO
\mathbf{S}
. We can do so for GSP for the particular reason that
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{W}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}^{{}^{\intercal}}
is diagonalizable and a monomial takes the form
\mathbf{S}^{m}=\mathbf{W}\bm{\Sigma}^{m}\mathbf{W}^{{}^{\intercal}}
. Therefore, for the expression
\bm{\Sigma}^{m}
, we essentially take entrywise multiplication in the frequency domain. However, this does not hold for an
\mathbf{S}
from a directed graph, if it is not diagonalizable. Therefore, we may consider the interpretation that convolution is a multiplication in the frequency domain
[
24
]
. For this, we use the identification of the frequency domain with
\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
. More precisely, we define:

Definition 2
.

For
\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
, let
\mathbf{g}\odot\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
be the entrywise multiplication, i.e., the Hadamard product. Moreover, defined
\mathbf{h}^{k}=\mathbf{h}\odot\cdots\odot\mathbf{h}
to be the
k
-fold self multiplication. For any
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
, it defines a
convolution

\mathbf{h}*:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}*\mathbf{f}=\mathcal{I}\Big(\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}\odot\mathbf{h}\Big).

For the polar decomposition of
\mathbf{S}
, let the diagonal entries of
\bm{\Lambda}_{1}
(resp.
\bm{\Lambda}_{2}
) be
(\lambda_{1,i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
(resp.
(\lambda_{2,i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
).

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
be defined by
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{S},i,j}=\lambda_{2,i}\lambda_{1,j}
. Then
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{S}}*\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{f})
.

Proof.

From the definition, we directly evaluate
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{S}}*\mathbf{f}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{S}}*\mathbf{f}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\Big(\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{i}p_{i,j}\lambda_{2,i}\lambda_{1,j}\Big)\mathbf{v}_{1,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\lambda_{1,j}\Big(\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{i}p_{i,j}\lambda_{2,i}\Big)\mathbf{v}_{1,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\lambda_{1,j}\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,j},\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{i}\lambda_{2,i}\mathbf{v}_{2,i}\rangle\mathbf{v}_{1,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\lambda_{1,j}\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,j},\mathbf{V}_{2}\bm{\Lambda}_{2}\mathbf{V}_{2}^{H}\mathbf{f}\rangle\mathbf{v}_{1,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\lambda_{1,j}\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,j},\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{f})\rangle\mathbf{v}_{1,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}\big(\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{f})\big)=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{f}).

∎

As
\mathbf{U}
is unitary and
\mathbf{P}
is positive semi-definite,
\lambda_{2,i}
is always non-negative and it can be interpreted as the radius, while
\lambda_{1,j}
is of the form
e^{\mathbf{i}\theta}
. Hence,
\lambda_{2,i}\lambda_{1,j}
is the polar form of the complex number
\mathbf{h}_{i,j}
. As a consequence of
2
, if
\mathbf{S}
is normal, then
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{S}}^{k}*
is just the monomial convolution filter
\mathbf{S}^{k}
.

We have the freedom to choose
\mathbf{h}
to construct different convolution filters. For example, if
\mathbf{h}
is a characteristic signal valued in
\{0,1\}
, then we have a bandpass filter. Sampling theory can be developed analogously to
[
8
]
. For another construction, consider any function
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
. We use
f(\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{S}})
to denote the signal in
\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
whose
(i,j)
-th entry is
f(\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{S},i,j})=h(\lambda_{2,i}\lambda_{1,j})
. Examples of
f
include polynomials or functions of the form
1/(1+cx)
(cf.
Section

VII
).

V
Graph perturbation

In this section, we discuss graph perturbation, which is used in
[
19
]
to modify a directed graph to obtain a diagonalizable shift operator. Many procedures are considered as a perturbation. For example, adding or removing a small percentage of directed edges is considered as a perturbation. Another example is a small change in the edge weights if the graph is weighted. Though our framework is lossless without modifying the graph structure, we still want to understand how such perturbations affect the Fourier transform and filtering.

In this section, we use
{\left\lVert{\cdot}\right\rVert}
to denote the matrix operator norm, and
{\left\lVert{\cdot}\right\rVert}_{F}
to denote the matrix Frobenius norm, the same as the
2
-norm. All norms are equivalent for finite dimensional spaces. For example, for any matrix
\mathbf{M}
of rank
r
, we have
{\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}}\right\rVert}\leq{\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}}\right\rVert}_{F}\leq\sqrt{r}{\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}}\right\rVert}
.

For another shift operator
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
, denote
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}-\mathbf{S}
by
\delta\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
, as the perturbation matrix. Usually, we are interested in small perturbation, which means
{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}
is small. In this subsection, we assume that
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
are invertible and
non-derogatory
, i.e., both factors in the polar decomposition do not have repeated eigenvalues. If
{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}
is sufficiently small, then
\mathbf{S}
being invertible and non-derogatory implies the same holds for
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
.

To state the following observation, let
P_{m}(x,y)
be the polynomial
(x+y)^{m}-y^{m}
. We notice that each of its monomial terms has at least one
x
factor.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose sequences of matrices
\mathbf{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{M}_{m}
and
\mathbf{M}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\mathbf{M}_{m}^{\prime}
satisfy: for
1\leq i\leq m
, matrices
\mathbf{M}_{i},\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\prime}
are of the same size and
\mathbf{M}=\prod_{1\leq i\leq m}\mathbf{M}_{i}
,
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}=\prod_{1\leq i\leq m}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\prime}
are well-defined. Let
\delta\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\prime}-\mathbf{M}_{i}
. If there are
x,y>0
such that
{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}\leq x,{\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}_{i}}\right\rVert}\leq y,1\leq i\leq m
, then
{\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}^{\prime}-\mathbf{M}}\right\rVert}\leq P_{m}(x,y)
.

Proof.

Notice that
{\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}^{\prime}-\mathbf{M}}\right\rVert}={\left\lVert{\prod_{1\leq i\leq m}(\mathbf{M}_{i}+\delta\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\prime})-\prod_{1\leq i\leq m}\mathbf{M}_{i}}\right\rVert}
. We expand the product
\prod_{1\leq i\leq m}(\mathbf{M}_{i}+\delta\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\prime})
and apply the triangle inequality. The norm
{\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}^{\prime}-\mathbf{M}}\right\rVert}
is upper bounded by a sum of the form
{\left\lVert{\prod_{1\leq i\leq m}\mathbf{N}_{i}}\right\rVert}
with
\mathbf{N}_{i}
either
\mathbf{M}_{i}
or
\delta\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\prime}
, and at least one of them is
\delta\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\prime}
. Then as
{\left\lVert{\cdot}\right\rVert}
is submultiplicative, we obtain the desired an upper bound
P_{m}(x)
by replacing
\mathbf{M}_{i}
with
y
and
\delta\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\prime}
by
x
.
∎

The Fourier transform
1
and convolutions are linear transformations, and hence it makes sense to talk about their operator norms and Frobenius norms.

Theorem 1
.

There are constants
\epsilon
and
C
independent of
\delta\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
such that if
{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}_{F}\leq\epsilon
, then the following holds

1.

{\left\lVert{\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{S}}-\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}}\right\rVert}\leq P_{3}(C{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}_{F},1)
.

2.

{\left\lVert{\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{S}}^{k}*-\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}^{k}*}\right\rVert}\leq P_{2k+4}(C{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}_{F},\max(1,\lambda_{2,n}))
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{V}_{i}^{\prime},\bm{\Lambda}_{i}^{\prime},i=1,2
be the unitary and diagonal factors resulting from the polar decomposition of
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
. Denote
\delta\mathbf{V}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathbf{V}_{i}^{\prime}-\mathbf{V}_{i},\delta\bm{\Lambda}_{i}^{\prime}=\bm{\Lambda}_{i}^{\prime}-\bm{\Lambda}_{i},i=1,2
. We first claim that there is a constant
C
independent of
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
and
n
such that for
i=1,2
, then the following holds

1.

{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{V}_{i}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}_{F}\leq C{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}_{F}
, and

2.

{\left\lVert{\delta\bm{\Lambda}_{i}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}_{F}\leq C{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}_{F}
.

The inequalities follow from the matrix perturbation theory as follows. Let
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
be the unique polar decomposition of the invertible matrix
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
and write
\delta\mathbf{U}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}-\mathbf{U},\delta\mathbf{P}^{\prime}=\mathbf{P}^{\prime}-\mathbf{P}
. Then by
[
25
, Theorem 1]
and
[
26
]
, we have
{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}_{F}\leq c_{0}{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}_{F}
and
{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{P}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}_{F}\leq\sqrt{2}{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}_{F}
, where
c_{0}
depends only on the singular values of
\mathbf{S}
.

VI-A

Different choices of eigenbasis

The polar decomposition
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}
may not be unique. Moreover, even for fixed
\mathbf{U}
or
\mathbf{P}
, they may have repeated eigenvalues, thus violating the non-derogatory condition of
1
. As a consequence, there is an ambiguity in the matrices
\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{2}
needed to define the Fourier transform
1
. To (partially) resolve this issue, we propose an optimization framework.

Let
d(\cdot,\cdot)
be a metric on the space of
n\times n
unitary matrices
\mathcal{U}_{n}
such as the metric induced by the operator norm. For a polar decomposition
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}
, we propose to construct the unitary matrices of eigenvectors
\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{2}
by solving the following optimization

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{2}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}}d(\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{2}),\text{ provided }\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{2}\text{ form eigenbasis of }\mathbf{U},\mathbf{P}.

(5)

An important example is when
\mathbf{S}
is the Laplacian of a connected undirected graph
G
. In this case, by convention, we apply the canonical polar decomposition
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{I}\mathbf{S}
, where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix. Solving the above optimization results in
\mathbf{V}_{1}=\mathbf{V}_{2}
and
d(\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{2})=0
. This agrees with what is being considered in GSP. Suppose
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
is the Laplacian of connected undirected
G^{\prime}
that is a small perturbation of
G
. Then we still have a constant
C
independent of
G^{\prime}
such that
{\left\lVert{\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{S}}-\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}}\right\rVert}\leq C{\left\lVert{\delta\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\right\rVert}_{F}
as long as
\mathbf{S}
does not have repeated eigenvalues by
1
. Similarly, if
\mathbf{S}
is the adjacency matrix of the directed cycle graph, then for the canonical polar decomposition
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{I}
, we again have
\mathbf{V}_{1}=\mathbf{V}_{2}
by solving (
5
) with
d(\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{2})=0
.

More generally, the same discussion works if
\mathbf{S}
is
normal
, in which case
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{P}
are simultaneously diagonalizable, and solving (
5
) also yields
\mathbf{V}_{1}=\mathbf{V}_{2}
with
d(\mathbf{V}_{1},\mathbf{V}_{2})=0
.

VI-C

General matrix decomposition

The Fourier transform (
4
) has the element of the chain rule in calculus. More precisely, for a signal
\mathbf{f}
, the polar decomposition trivially implies that
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{f})
. In the expression
\langle\mathbf{v}_{2,i},\mathbf{f}\rangle\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,j},\mathbf{v}_{2,i}\rangle
, we view
\langle\mathbf{v}_{2,i},\mathbf{f}\rangle
as the “partial derivative of
\mathbf{f}
w.r.t.
\mathbf{v}_{2,i}
, and the factor
\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,j},\mathbf{v}_{2,i}\rangle
as the “partial derivative” of
\mathbf{v}_{2,i}
w.r.t.
\mathbf{v}_{1,j}
. This gives us hints on how to generalize the framework of the paper.

Instead of considering the polar decomposition
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}
, let

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{M}_{1}\cdots\mathbf{M}_{k},k>1

(6)

be any decomposition of
\mathbf{S}
into a product of normal matrices
\mathbf{M}_{i},1\leq i\leq k
. Assume that for each
\mathbf{M}_{i}
, there is an eigendecomposition
\mathbf{M}_{i}=\mathbf{V}_{i}\bm{\Lambda}_{i}\mathbf{V}_{1}^{H}
such that
\mathbf{V}_{i}
is unitary and
\bm{\Lambda}_{i}
is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. Let the columns of
\mathbf{V}_{j}
be
\{\mathbf{v}_{i,j},1\leq j\leq n\}
. Then the Fourier transform of a signal
\mathbf{f}
is a map
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n^{k}}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}\mapsto\Big(\prod_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i,j_{i}},\mathbf{v}_{i+1,j_{i+1}}\rangle\Big)\langle\mathbf{v}_{k,j_{k}},\mathbf{f}\rangle,1\leq j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\leq n,

as a generalization of (
4
) in
1
.

If the eigendecompositions of
\mathbf{M}_{i}
’s are non-unique, as in the case of the polar decomposition, we may jointly estimate
\mathbf{V}_{i},1\leq i\leq k
by solving the following optimization taking the form analogous to (
5
):

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{V}_{k}}\sum_{1\leq i\leq k-1}d(\mathbf{V}_{i},\mathbf{V}_{i+1}),\text{ provided }\mathbf{V}_{i}\text{ forms an eigenbasis of }\mathbf{M}_{i}.

We notice that the codomain of the Fourier transform is
n^{k}
dimensional. Therefore, it will be intractable if
k
is large. This means that it is only reasonable to consider only the matrix decomposition of a small number of factors. Examples of such a matrix decomposition include the reverse polar decomposition, the singular value decomposition (SVD)
[
22
]
, and the Sinkhorn decomposition involving doubly stochastic matrix
[
30
]
.

VIII
Conclusions

This paper introduces a novel signal processing framework for directed graphs. Our approach utilizes the polar decomposition to introduce a novel Fourier transform with a larger frequency domain than that of traditional GSP. This enables us to define convolution using a standard procedure. Our method possesses two notable features: it ensures losslessness, as the shift operator can be entirely reconstructed from the polar decomposition factors, and it encompasses traditional graph signal processing when applied to directed graphs. For future work, we shall study how the framework might be used for learning directed graph topology.
