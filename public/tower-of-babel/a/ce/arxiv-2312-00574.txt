Symmetric functions in noncommuting variables in superspace
Diego Arcis Camilo González Sebastián Márquez

Abstract
In 2004, Rosas and Sagan developed the theory of symmetric functions in noncommuting variables, achieving results analogous to classical symmetric functions. On the other hand, the same year, Desrosiers, Lapointe and Mathieu introduced the theory of symmetric functions in superspace, involving both commuting and anticommuting variables, extending the classic theory. Here, we introduce symmetric functions in noncommuting variables in superspace. We extend the classical symmetric functions in noncommuting variables to superspace: monomial, power sum, elementary and complete homogeneous functions. These functions generalize both those studied by Rosas and Sagan and those studied by Desrosiers, Lapointe, and Mathieu. Additionally, we define Schur–type functions in noncommuting variables in superspace.

Introduction

Symmetric functions play a significant role in mathematics, particularly in areas such as algebraic combinatorics, representation theory, and Hopf algebras, among others
[
23
,
31
]
. They have been widely studied and are important not only in mathematics but also in connection with integrable models in physics
[
24
]
. Specifically, symmetric functions turn out to be eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonian of the Calogero–Moser–Sutherland model
[
29
,
15
]
. Roughly speaking, symmetric functions are formal power series in infinitely many commuting variables, remaining invariant under any permutation of their variables. Symmetric functions form an algebra with bases indexed by partitions of integers. Some of these bases include the monomial symmetric functions, power sum symmetric functions, elementary symmetric functions, and complete homogeneous symmetric functions.

An important family of symmetric functions that form a basis are the so-called Schur functions, appearing as limits of characters of the general linear group and associated with the irreducible characters of the symmetric group via the Schur–Weyl duality. Schur functions can be defined through various methods: by using semistandard Young tableaux, via the Jacobi–Trudy identity, or directly through a quotient involving Vandermonde-type determinants
[
31
, Chapter 7]
.

Several generalizations of the algebra of symmetric functions
\mathrm{\bf Sym}
have been studied, such as the algebra of quasisymmetric functions
\mathrm{\bf QSym}

[
19
]
and its dual Hopf algebra of noncommuting symmetric functions
\mathrm{\bf NSym}

[
18
]
, among others
[
25
,
16
,
20
]
. Quasisymmetric functions can be regarded as a refinement of symmetric functions in the sense that they remain invariant under a weaker form of symmetry.

Another generalization of symmetric functions involves noncommuting variables, introduced by Wolf in 1936
[
32
]
. The algebra of symmetric functions in noncommuting variables
\mathrm{\bf NCSym}
was studied by Rosas and Sagan in
[
27
]
, establishing results similar to the commuting case. In particular, they defined bases analogous to the monomial, power, elementary, and complete homogeneous functions, indexed by set partitions. They showed relations between these bases and studied their projection to the algebra of symmetric functions in commuting variables. Also, by using combinatorics of tableaux, they introduced a family of Schur–type functions in noncommuting variables, satisfying properties similar to the classical Schur functions; however, this family does not constitute a basis of
\mathrm{\bf NCSym}
. Using a noncommuting version of the Jacobi–Trudi determinant, a new family of Schur–type functions was introduced in
[
2
]
, refining the one in
[
27

and constituting a basis of
\mathrm{\bf NCSym}

[

and the connection with supercharacter theory
[

,
32
]
. Some results related to the Hopf structure of
\mathrm{\bf NCSym}
were obtained in
[
5
,
4
,
6
]
.

Given the connection of symmetric functions with integrable models, and motivated by a supersymmetric generalization of the Calogero–Moser–Sutherland model
[
10
,
13
]
, it was developed in
[
11
,
12
]
a new family of symmetric functions called

, involving commuting variables

together with anticommuting variables

A natural problem is to consider symmetric functions involving noncommuting and anticommuting variables, which generalize both symmetric functions in noncommuting variables and symmetric functions in superspace. Here, we address this problem. Specifically, we extend the classical bases and the Schur–type functions in noncommuting variables to superspace, which project naturally onto their commuting counterparts in superspace. The paper is organized as follows.

In Section 2, we introduce two new classes of combinatorial objects called
partial set supercompositions
and
set superpartitions

In Section 3, we introduce the algebra of symmetric functions in noncommuting variables in superspace

\mathrm{\bf sNCSym}
. Also, we provide combinatorial formulas for the product of these functions. Other bases of
\mathrm{\bf sNCSym}
are studied in Section 4. In particular, we define power sum, elementary, and complete homogeneous symmetric functions in superspace. We also show the relations between these bases.

Section 5 is devoted to studying how symmetric functions in noncommuting variables in superspace project to the algebra of functions in commuting variables in superspace

\mathrm{\bf sSym}

In Section 6, we define Schur–type functions in noncommuting variables in superspace in the sense of
[
27

, using super semistandard Young tableaux introduced in
[
22
]
. We express these functions in terms of the monomial basis and show their projection onto Schur–type functions in commuting variables in superspace, also given in
[

]

3
Functions in superspace

In this section, we define the algebra of symmetric functions in noncommuting variables in superspace. We introduce monomial symmetric functions in superspace and show that these ones form a basis of it. Also, we give combinatorial formulas for the product of these functions.

The

\sum_{K\,\sqsubseteq\,N\,\sqsubseteq\,L}\mu(K,N)=\delta_{K,L}

A well known formula in terms of

(4.5)

\mu(K,L)=\sum_{[F_{1},\ldots,\,F_{q}]}(-1)^{q-1},

where the sum is taken over all chains joining

Proposition 4.15

For set superpartitions

Proof.

First, we show the multiplicativity of power sum functions. Let
A=\{H\mid H\in K\shuffle L,\,K\succeq I,\,L\succeq J\}
. By definition,
H\succeq I/J
for all

. Conversely, let
H=(H_{1},\ldots,H_{k})\succeq I/J
. Note that two different fermionic blocks of
I/J
cannot be contained in the same fermionic block of
H
. Then, for each

\displaystyle K_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H_{i}\cap[n]_{0}&\text{if $H_{i}$ contains a fermionic block of $I$,}\\
H_{i}\cap[n]&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle L_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H_{i}\cap[n+1,n+p]_{0}&\text{if $H_{i}$ contains a fermionic block of $J[n]$,}\\
H_{i}\cap[n+1,n+p]&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Observe that
H_{i}=K_{i}\sqcup L_{i}
for all

. This implies that
H\in K\shuffle L
, where
K
and
L
are the set superpartitions obtained by removing the empty components from
(K_{1},\ldots,K_{k})

and

p_{I}p_{J}=\sum_{K\succeq I}\sum_{L\succeq J}m_{K}m_{L}=\sum_{K\succeq I}\sum_{L\succeq J}\left(\sum_{H\in K\shuffle L}m_{H}\right)=\sum_{H\in A}m_{H}=\sum_{H\succeq I/J}m_{H}=p_{I/J}.

To show the multiplicativity of elementary functions, we first observe that if
K\sqsubseteq I
and

K/L\sqsubseteq I/J
. Conversely, if
H\sqsubseteq I/J

obtained by removing the blocks of

–shifting the collection produced by removing the blocks of

\omega

\omega(p_{K}p_{L})=\omega(p_{K/L})=(-1)^{K/L}p_{K/L}=(-1)^{K}p_{K}(-1)^{L}p_{L}=\omega(p_{K})\omega(p_{L}).

5
Commuting variables and inner product

In this section, we show formulas that project our bases in their commuting counterpart, and we prove that there is an isometry from
\mathrm{\bf sNCSym}
to
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
.

Let
\Q^{\theta}[x]=\Q_{\!\!}^{\theta}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle/I
, where
I
is the ideal of
\Q_{\!\!}^{\theta}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle
generated by the elements
x_{i}x_{j}-x_{j}x_{i}
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote by
\rho
the associated natural projection
\Q_{\!\!}^{\theta}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle\to\kern-8.00003pt\to\Q^{\theta}[x]
. The image of
\mathrm{\bf sNCSym}
under this projection is the algebra
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
of symmetric functions in commuting variables in superspace, introduced in
[
10
]
. The bases of this algebra are indexed by the so–called superpartitions.

5.1
Symmetric functions in commuting variables in superspace

Recall that bases of the classic symmetric functions in commuting variables are indexed by partitions of numbers
[
23
,
31
]
. A
partition

\lambda
of
degree

n=:\deg(\lambda)
is a decreasing sequence
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
of nonnegative integers, called
components
, such that
\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}=n
. In general, every finite sequence of nonnegative integers
u
can be converted into a partition
\operatorname{sort}(u)
by sorting its components in decreasing order. The partition
\lambda
can also be denoted by
(1^{r_{1}},\ldots,n^{r_{n}})
, where
r_{i}
is the
multiplicity
of
i
, that is, the number of times that
i
occurs in
\lambda
. We set
\lambda!=\lambda_{1}!\cdots\lambda_{k}!
and
\lambda^{!}=r_{1}!\cdots r_{n}!
. For instance, if
\lambda=(5,5,5,3,3,1,1,1,1)
, then

\lambda=(1^{4},3^{3},5^{2}),\qquad\lambda!=5!\,5!\,5!\,3!\,3!,\qquad\lambda^{!}=4!\,3!\,2!.

As we mentioned above, the bases of
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
are indexed by superpartitions
[
14
]
. A
superpartition

\Lambda
of degree
n

[
10
, Subsection 4.2.2]
is an array
(\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{m}\,;\Lambda_{m+1},\ldots,\Lambda_{k})
of nonnegative integers, called
components
, satisfying
\Lambda_{1}+\cdots+\Lambda_{k}=n
, where
\Lambda^{a}:=(\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{m})
is a partition of different components and
\Lambda^{s}:=(\Lambda_{m+1},\ldots,\Lambda_{k})
is a usual partition. Components of
\Lambda^{a}
are called
fermionic components
of
\Lambda
, and the numbers
m,k
are called the
fermionic degree
and the
length
of
\Lambda
, respectively. Set
\Lambda!=\Lambda^{a}!\Lambda^{s}!
. The degree, fermionic degree and length of
\Lambda
will be denoted as with set superpartitions, that is,
\deg(\Lambda)
,
\operatorname{df}(\Lambda)
and
\ell(\Lambda)
respectively. The collection of superpartitions of degree
n
will be denoted by
\mathrm{sPar}_{n}
, and the subcollection of elements with fermionic degree
m
will be denoted by
\mathrm{sPar}_{n,m}
. As with usual partitions, every array of nonnegative integers
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m};u_{m+1},\ldots,u_{k})
, with
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
all different, can be converted into a superpartition
\operatorname{sort}(u)
, where
\operatorname{sort}(u)^{a}=\operatorname{sort}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})
and
\operatorname{sort}(u)^{s}=\operatorname{sort}(u_{m+1},\ldots,u_{k})
.

Some of the classical symmetric functions in superspace in commutating variables were defined in
[
14
]
. Indeed, given a superpartition
\Lambda=(\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{m};\Lambda_{m+1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n})
, we have the following

•

Monomial symmetric functions in superspace
:

m_{\Lambda}(x,\theta)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\theta_{\sigma(1)}\cdots\theta_{\sigma(m)}x_{\theta(1)}^{\Lambda_{1}}\cdots x_{\sigma(n)}^{\Lambda_{n}},

where the sum considers no repeated terms.

•

Power–sum symmetric functions in superspace
:
p_{\Lambda}=\tilde{p}_{\Lambda_{1}}\cdots\tilde{p}_{\Lambda_{m}}p_{\Lambda_{m+1}}\cdots p_{\Lambda_{n}}
, where

\tilde{p}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}x_{i}^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad p_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{r},\quad\text{for}\quad k\geq 0,r\geq 1.

•

Elementary symmetric functions in superspace
:
e_{\Lambda}=\tilde{e}_{\Lambda_{1}}\cdots\tilde{e}_{\Lambda_{m}}e_{\Lambda_{m+1}}\cdots e_{\Lambda_{n}}
, where

\tilde{e}_{k}=m_{(0;1^{k})}\quad\text{and}\quad e_{r}=m_{(\emptyset;1^{r})},\quad\text{for}\quad k\geq 0,r\geq 1.

•

Complete homogeneous symmetric functions in superspace
:
h_{\Lambda}=\tilde{h}_{\Lambda_{1}}\ldots\tilde{h}_{\Lambda_{m}}h_{\Lambda_{m+1}}\cdots h_{\Lambda_{n}}
, where

\tilde{h}_{k}=\sum_{|\Lambda|=k,\,\operatorname{df}(\Lambda)=1}(\Lambda_{1}+1)m_{\Lambda}\quad\text{ and}\quad h_{r}=\sum_{|\Lambda|=r,\,\operatorname{df}(\Lambda)=0}m_{\Lambda},\quad\text{for}\quad k\geq 0,r\geq 1.

Observe that the bases of
\mathrm{\bf Sym}
are recovered whenever
\Lambda^{a}=\emptyset
.

For every set superpartition

Given a superpartition
\Lambda

\Lambda
is

\binom{n}{\Lambda}:=k_{1}\cdots k_{m}\binom{n}{\,\,\Lambda^{\!+}}=\frac{n!}{\Lambda!(\Lambda^{s})^{!}}.

Proof.

Observe that every set superpartition of type
\Lambda
can be obtained from a usual set partition of type
\Lambda^{\!+}
by appending zeros to some of its blocks. Indeed, if
I
is a set superpartition of type
\Lambda
, then
J=I^{+}
is a set partition of type
\Lambda^{\!+}
. Conversely, if
J
is a set partition of type
\Lambda^{+}
, then there are
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
in
J\cup\{\emptyset\}
such that
|B_{i}|=\Lambda_{i}
for all
i\in[m]
. We get a set superpartition
I=K\backslash\{\emptyset\}
of type
\Lambda
, where
K
is obtained from
J\cup\{\emptyset\}
by replacing
B_{i}
by
B_{i}\cup\{0\}
. The proof follows from the fact that for each
\Lambda_{i}
with
i\in[m]
, there are
k_{i}
blocks
B_{i}\in J\cup\{\emptyset\}
such that
|B_{i}|=\Lambda_{i}
together with Lemma
5.1
.
∎

Now, we will study the image of the bases of
\mathrm{\bf sNCSym}
under the projection
\rho
onto the algebra of commuting variables
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
. First, we prove that set superpartitions needed to describe
\rho
on the basis of monomial functions are the ones whose fermionic blocks have all different sizes.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
I
be a set superpartition having at least two fermionic blocks
B,B^{\prime}
such that
|B|=|B^{\prime}|
. Then
\rho(m_{I})=0
.

Proof.

Let
k=\ell(I)
, and let
i,j\in[k]
such that
B,B^{\prime}
are the
i
th and
j
th blocks of
I
respectively. Consider
g\in\{m_{I},p_{I}\}
, and let
r=\theta_{a_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}\cdots\theta_{a_{k}}^{\epsilon_{k}}x_{b_{1}}\cdots x_{b_{n}}
be a monomial occurring in
g
. Then
r=\epsilon\theta_{a_{i}}\theta_{a_{j}}\theta_{d_{1}}\cdots\theta_{d_{r}}x_{b_{1}}\cdots x_{b_{n}}
for some
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
, where
r=\operatorname{df}(I)-2
. Since
g
is symmetric, the monomial
\sigma(r)=-\epsilon\theta_{a_{i}}\theta_{a_{j}}\theta_{d_{1}}\cdots\theta_{d_{r}}x_{\sigma(b_{1})}\cdots x_{\sigma(b_{n})}
also occurs in
g
, where
\sigma
is the transposition exchanging
a_{i}
with
a_{j}
. Since
|B|=|B^{\prime}|
, then
x_{\sigma(b_{1})}\cdots x_{\sigma(b_{n})}=x_{b_{1}}\cdots x_{b_{n}}
in
\Q^{\theta}[x]
. Thus
\rho(\sigma(r))=-\rho(r)
. As it holds for every monomial occurring in
g
, then
\rho(g)=0
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

For every
K\in s\mathcal{C}_{n,m}
and
\delta\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
, we have
\rho(b_{\delta K})=\rho(b_{K})
, where
b
denotes any of the basis elements studied above.

Proof.

Since
\delta\circ b_{K}=b_{\delta\circ K}
, then, every monomial occurring in
b_{\delta\circ K}
can be written as

\delta\circ(\theta_{a_{1}}\cdots\theta_{a_{m}}x_{b_{1}}\cdots x_{b_{n}})=\theta_{a_{1}}\cdots\theta_{a_{m}}x_{b_{\delta^{-1}(1)}}\cdots x_{b_{\delta^{-1}(n)}},

where
\theta_{a_{1}}\cdots\theta_{a_{m}}x_{b_{1}}\cdots x_{b_{n}}
is a monomial occurring in
b_{K}
. Now, since
x
–variables become commuting under the action of
\rho
, then
\rho(\theta_{a_{1}}\cdots\theta_{a_{m}}x_{b_{\delta^{-1}(1)}}\cdots x_{b_{\delta^{-1}(n)}})=\rho(\theta_{a_{1}}\cdots\theta_{a_{m}}x_{b_{1}}\cdots x_{b_{n}})
. Thus,
\rho(b_{\delta\circ K})=\rho(b_{K})
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

For every set superpartition
I
with fermionic blocks of different sizes, we have

1.

\rho(m_{I})=(-1)^{\varepsilon(I)}(\Lambda(I)^{s})^{!}m_{\Lambda(I)}
.

2.

\rho(p_{I})=(-1)^{\varepsilon(I)}p_{\Lambda(I)}
.

3.

\rho(e_{I})=(-1)^{\varepsilon(I)}\Lambda(I)!e_{\Lambda(I)}
.

4.

\rho(h_{I})=(-1)^{\varepsilon(I)}\Lambda(I)!h_{\Lambda(I)}
.

Furthermore, if
J
is a set superpartition with blocks of the same size, we have
\rho(b_{J})=0
, where
b
is any of the basis elements above.

Proof.

Due to Lemma
5.4
, it is enough to take
I=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k})\in s\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
as a convex set superpartition.

To prove (1), consider
\Lambda(I)^{s}=(1^{k_{1}},\ldots,n^{k_{n}})
. Since
I
is convex, every monomial
u\in M(I)
can be written as

u=\theta_{a_{1}}\cdots\theta_{a_{m}}x_{a_{1}}^{|I_{1}|-1}\cdots x_{a_{m}}^{|I_{m}|-1}x_{b_{m+1}}^{|I_{m+1}|}\cdots x_{b_{k}}^{|I_{k}|}.

Observe that for every permutation
\sigma
satisfying
\sigma(\{b_{m+1},\ldots,b_{k}\})=\{b_{m+1},\ldots,b_{k}\}
with
|I_{i}|=|I_{j}|
whenever
\sigma(b_{i})=b_{j}
, and
\sigma(b)=b
for all
b\not\in\{b_{m+1},\ldots,b_{k}\}
, we have

\rho(\theta_{a_{1}}\cdots\theta_{a_{m}}x_{a_{1}}^{|I_{1}|-1}\cdots x_{a_{m}}^{|I_{m}|-1}x_{\sigma(b_{m+1})}^{|I_{m+1}|}\cdots x_{\sigma(b_{k})}^{|I_{k}|})=\rho(u).

Since, for each
i\in[n]
, there are
k_{i}
blocks of size
i
in
(I_{m+1},\ldots,I_{k})
, so there are
k_{1}!\cdots k_{n}!=(\Lambda(I)^{s})^{!}
such permutations. Finally, by sorting the
\theta
–variables, we obtain
\rho(m_{I})=(-1)^{\varepsilon(I)}(\Lambda(I)^{s})^{!}m_{\Lambda(I)}
.

To prove (2) to (4) we will use the fact that the bases are multiplicative (Proposition
4.15
).

Observe that
\rho(p_{[n]})=p_{n}
and
\rho(p_{[n]_{0}})=\tilde{p}_{n}
, where
p_{[n]}=p_{\{[n]\}}
and
p_{[n]_{0}}=p_{\{[n]_{0}\}}
. For
i\in[k]
, let
n_{i}=|I_{i}|-1
if
i\leq m
, and
n_{i}=|I_{i}|
otherwise. Since
I
is convex and the basis is multiplicative, we have

\rho(p_{I})=\rho(p_{[n_{1}]_{0}}\cdots p_{[n_{m}]_{0}}p_{[n_{m+1}]}\cdots p_{[n_{k}]})=\tilde{p}_{n_{1}}\cdots\tilde{p}_{n_{m}}p_{n_{m+1}}\cdots p_{n_{k}}=(-1)^{\varepsilon(I)}p_{\Lambda(I)}.

The argument is similar for the remaining bases by observing that

\rho(e_{[n]})=n!e_{n},\qquad\rho(e_{[n]_{0}})=n!\tilde{e}_{n},\qquad\rho(h_{[n]})=n!h_{n},\qquad\rho(h_{[n]_{0}})=n!\tilde{h}_{n}.

∎

We may define a
lifting map

\tilde{\rho}:\mathrm{\bf sSym}\to\mathrm{\bf sNCSym}
, which result to be a right inverse of
\rho
, as the linear map satisfying

(5.1)

\tilde{\rho}(m_{\Lambda})=\frac{\Lambda!}{n!}\sum_{\Lambda(I)=\Lambda}(-1)^{\varepsilon(I)}m_{I}.

Proposition 5.6
.

The composition
\rho\circ\tilde{\rho}
is the identity map on
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
.

Proof.

By (
1
) of Proposition
5.5
together with Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
5.2
, we have

\rho(\tilde{\rho}(m_{\Lambda}))=\frac{\Lambda!}{n!}\sum_{\Lambda(I)=\Lambda}(-1)^{\varepsilon(I)}\rho(m_{I})=\frac{\Lambda!}{n!}\sum_{\Lambda(I)=\Lambda}(\Lambda(I)^{s})^{!}m_{\Lambda(I)}=\frac{\Lambda!}{n!}\binom{n}{\Lambda}(\Lambda(I)^{s})^{!}m_{\Lambda}=m_{\Lambda}.

Therefore
\rho\circ\tilde{\rho}
is the identity map on
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
.
∎

5.2
Inner product

It was shown in

that

can be endowed with an inner product

satisfying

are superpartitions,

and

Here we extend the inner product of

[

]
to superspace and show that, with respect to this inner product,

is an isometry from
\mathrm{\bf sNCSym}

Let
\left\langle\,,\right\rangle
be the bilinear form on

satisfying
\left\langle m_{I},h_{J}\right\rangle=(-1)^{\binom{m}{2}}n!\delta_{I,J}

Lemma 5.7
.

For every
I,J\in s\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
, we have
(I\wedge_{\sigma}J)!=(J\wedge_{\sigma^{-1}}I)!
.

Proof.

Let
(K_{1},\ldots,K_{p})=I\wedge_{\sigma}J
, and let
(K_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,K_{q}^{\prime})=J\wedge_{\sigma^{-1}}I
. By definition, we have
K^{\prime}_{\sigma(i)}=J_{\sigma(i)}\cap I_{\sigma^{-1}(\sigma(i))}=J_{\sigma(i)}\cap I_{i}=I_{i}\cap J_{\sigma(i)}=K_{i}
. This implies that
K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{m}=K_{1}^{\prime}\cup\cdots\cup K_{m}^{\prime}=:A
. Hence,
(K_{m+1},\ldots,K_{p})=(K_{m+1}^{\prime},\ldots,K_{q}^{\prime})=(I\backslash A)\wedge(J\backslash A)
with

. Therefore
(I\wedge_{\sigma}J)!=(J\wedge_{\sigma^{-1}}I)!
.
∎

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
I,J
be two set superpartitions of bidegree
(n,m)
. Then

\left\langle h_{I},h_{J}\right\rangle=n!\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}(I\wedge_{\sigma}J)!(-1)^{\operatorname{inv}(\sigma)}

Furthermore,
\left\langle h_{I},h_{J}\right\rangle=\left\langle h_{J},h_{I}\right\rangle
.

Proof.

Due to Corollary
4.10
, we have

\left\langle h_{I},h_{J}\right\rangle=\left\langle\sum_{(\sigma,\,K)}(I\wedge_{\sigma}K)!(-1)^{\operatorname{inv}(\sigma)}m_{K},h_{J}\right\rangle=\sum_{(\sigma,K)}(I\wedge_{\sigma}K)!(-1)^{\operatorname{inv}(\sigma)}\left\langle m_{K},h_{J}\right\rangle,

where
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
and
J\in s\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Then, because of
\left\langle m_{K},h_{J}\right\rangle=(-1)^{\binom{m}{2}}n!\delta_{K,\,J}
, we obtain the result

\left\langle h_{I},h_{J}\right\rangle=\sum_{(\sigma,K)}(I\wedge_{\sigma}K)!(-1)^{\operatorname{inv}(\sigma)}(-1)^{\binom{m}{2}}n!\delta_{K,\,J}=(-1)^{\binom{m}{2}}n!\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}(I\wedge_{\sigma}K)!(-1)^{\operatorname{inv}(\sigma)}.

Lemma
5.7
and the fact that
\operatorname{inv}(\sigma)
and
\operatorname{inv}(\sigma^{-1})
have the same parity imply that
\left\langle h_{I},h_{J}\right\rangle=\left\langle h_{J},h_{I}\right\rangle
.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
I,J
be two set superpartitions of bidegree
(n,m)
. Then

\left\langle\,p_{I},p_{J}\right\rangle=\frac{(-1)^{\binom{m}{2}}n!}{|\mu(\hat{0}_{n,m},I)|}\delta_{I,J}.

Proof.

Due to Corollary
4.4
and Proposition
4.14
, we have

p_{I}=\sum_{K\succeq I}m_{K},\qquad p_{J}=\frac{1}{|\mu(\hat{0}_{n,m},J)|}\sum_{L\preceq J}\mu(L,J)h_{L},

where
K,L
are nontrivial partial set supercompositions of bidegree
(n,m)
. Then,

\left\langle\,p_{I},p_{J}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{|\mu(\hat{0}_{n,m},J)|}\sum_{K\succeq I}\sum_{L\preceq J}\mu(L,J)\left\langle m_{K},h_{L}\right\rangle.

Since
K,L
are nontrivial, then
K=(\sigma,\bar{K})
and
L=(\tau,\bar{L})
for some
\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
. Thus

\left\langle m_{K},h_{L}\right\rangle=(-1)^{\operatorname{inv}(\sigma)+\operatorname{inv}(\tau)}\left\langle m_{\bar{K}},h_{\bar{L}}\right\rangle.

We will distinguish four cases. If
I\succ J
as set superpartitions, then
\bar{L}\preceq J\prec I\preceq\bar{K}
. Thus
\left\langle m_{K},h_{L}\right\rangle=0
. Therefore
\left\langle\,p_{I},p_{J}\right\rangle=0
.

If
I,J
are not comparable as set superpartitions, either
\bar{K}
is not comparable with
J
, or
\bar{K}\succ J
, otherwise
I\preceq\bar{K}\preceq J
, which is a contradiction. Thus,
\mu(K,J)h_{K}
is not an addend of
p_{J}
, which implies
\left\langle\,p_{I},p_{J}\right\rangle=0
.

Now, if
I\prec J
as set superpartitions, Remark
4.3
implies that
I\prec(\alpha^{-1},J)
, as partial set supercomposition, for some
\alpha\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left\langle\,p_{I},p_{J}\right\rangle&=&{\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mu(\hat{0}_{n,m},J)|}\sum_{(\alpha,\,I)\preceq L\preceq J}\mu(L,J)\left\langle m_{(\alpha^{-1},L)},h_{L}\right\rangle},\\[14.22636pt]
&=&{\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mu(\hat{0}_{n,m},J)|}\sum_{(\alpha,\,I)\preceq L\preceq J}(-1)^{\operatorname{inv}(\alpha)}\mu(L,J)\left\langle m_{L},h_{L}\right\rangle},\\[14.22636pt]
&=&{\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{\operatorname{inv}(\alpha)}(-1)^{\binom{m}{2}}n!}{|\mu(\hat{0}_{n,m},J)|}\sum_{(\alpha,I)\preceq L\preceq J}\mu(L,J)=0.}\end{array}

Therefore
{\displaystyle\left\langle\,p_{I},p_{J}\right\rangle=\frac{(-1)^{\binom{m}{2}}n!}{|\mu(\hat{0}_{n,m},I)|}}\delta_{I,J}
.
∎

Lemma 5.10
.

For every
K,L\in s\mathcal{C}_{n,m}
and
(\sigma,\tau)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{m}
, we have
(K\sqcap L)!=\left(\tau\triangleright\sigma(K)\,\sqcap\,\tau\triangleright\sigma(L)\right)!

Write

and

(\sigma(K)^{+}\backslash\sigma(A))\wedge(\sigma(L)^{+}\backslash\sigma(A)))=(\sigma(N_{m+1}),\ldots,\sigma(N_{q}))

implies that

.
∎

Proposition 5.11
.

The bilinear form
\left\langle\,,\right\rangle
is an inner product on

Schur symmetric functions in superspace

In this section, we define Schur–type functions in noncommuting variables in superspace by means super semistandard Young tableaux. Also, we express these functions in terms of the monomial basis and show their projection on Schur–type functions in commuting variables in superspace.

Recall that partitions of integers can be represented by

. Indeed, the
Young diagram
or simply the

of a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})

k

i
th row contains exactly
\lambda_{i}
boxes. See Figure
4
.

:
Diagram of the partition
\lambda=(7,4,4,2,1)
.

\mu
is said to be

in a partition
\lambda

\mu\subseteq\lambda
, if
\ell(\mu)\leq\ell(\lambda)
and
\mu_{i}\leq\lambda_{i}
for all
i\in[\ell(\mu)]
. If
\mu\subseteq\lambda
, the
skew diagram

\lambda/\mu
is the diagram obtained by removing the boxes from the diagram of
\lambda
that belong to the diagram of
\mu
. The skew diagram
\lambda/\mu
is called an
horizontal
r
–strip
(resp.
vertical
r
–strip
) if
\deg(\lambda)-\deg(\mu)=r
and each column (resp. row) has at most one box. See Figure
5
.

Figure 5
:
A horizontal
7

\lambda/\mu
, where
\lambda=(8,7,5,4,2,1)
and

.

\Lambda=(\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{m};\Lambda_{m+1},\ldots,\Lambda_{k})\in\mathrm{sPar}_{n,m}
with the pair
(\Lambda^{\oplus},\Lambda^{+})
, where

\Lambda^{\oplus}=\operatorname{sort}(\Lambda_{1}+1,\ldots,\Lambda_{m}+1,\Lambda_{m+1},\ldots,\Lambda_{k}).

Observe that the skew diagram
\Lambda^{\oplus}/\Lambda^{+}
is simultaneously an horizontal
m
–strip and an vertical

–strip. The superpartition
\Lambda
can be represented either by the pair of diagrams of
(\Lambda^{\oplus},\Lambda^{+})
, or by the diagram of
\Lambda^{\oplus}
in which the boxes of
\Lambda^{\oplus}/\Lambda^{+}
has been replaced by circles. These diagrams, accepting at most one round box at the end of each row, will be called
super Young diagram
. See Figure

. The

of a superpartition

obtained by conjugating both
\Lambda^{\oplus}
and

.

of

\Lambda
and

and

, satisfying:

and

(

enumii)

(

\Lambda_{(i)}

Remark 6.10

Some particular elementary and homogeneous symmetric functions can be recovered from Schur functions by taking

Remark 6.11
.

Observe that
\{S_{\Lambda}\}_{\Lambda}
and
\{\bar{S}_{\Lambda}\}_{\Lambda}
are not bases for
\mathrm{\bf sNCSym}
. Indeed, bases of
\mathrm{\bf sNCSym}
are indexed by set superpartitions, however there is no bijection between superpartitions and set superpartitions. In the non-superspace case, a first definition of Schur functions was given in
[
27
]
by using classic semistandard Young tableaux, nevertheless these ones do not constitute a basis of
\mathrm{\bf NCSym}
either. A basis of Schur–type functions for
\mathrm{\bf NCSym}
was given in
[
2
]
by using a noncommuting version of the Jacobi–Trudi determinant. They also proved that Schur functions in
[
27
]
are sums of them. In superspace, even in the commuting case, there is no a version of the Jacobi–Trudi determinant. Thus, the Jacobi–Trudi identity for Schur functions is still an open problem, as does the case for our Schur–type functions.

Remark 6.12
.

Recall that the product of classical Schur functions can be expressed as a linear combination of Schur functions by using the so–called Littlewood-Richardson coefficients. See for example
[
31
, Equation (7.64)]
. In superspace, as
\{s_{\Lambda}\}_{\Lambda}
is a basis of
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
, every product

can also be written as a linear combination of them, however the existence of a general combinatorial rule to get the coefficients involved here is still an open problem
[
22
, Section 9]
. As with classical Schur functions, in some particular cases, the product can be described by means of Pieri rules in superspace
[
22
, Section 4]
. In our case, as mentioned in Remark

, the collection
\{S_{\Lambda}\}_{\Lambda}
is not a basis for
\mathrm{\bf sNCSym}
, and therefore the product
S_{\Lambda}S_{\Omega}
is not necessarily a linear combination of these functions. For instance, by applying Proposition

, we have

S_{(0;1)}S_{(0;1)}=m_{(\{0\},\{1\})}m_{(\{0\},\{1\})}=-m_{(\{0,1\},\{0,2\})}.

As mentioned in Subsection

, the type of the set superpartition
(\{0,1\},\{0,2\})
is not well defined, therefore this product is not a linear combination of our Schur functions. However, it could be an interesting problem to get Pieri–like rules for more specific cases, such as when the first superpartition is simply a partition.
