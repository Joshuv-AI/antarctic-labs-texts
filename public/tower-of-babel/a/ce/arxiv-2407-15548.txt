Correspondences on Riemann surfaces: (non-uniform) hyperbolicity and graph attractors
Laurent Bartholdi, Dzmitry Dudko, Kevin M. Pilgrim

Abstract
. We consider certain correspondences on a Riemann surface, and show that they admit a weak form of hyperbolicity: sufficiently long loops get shorter under lifting at a fixed point and closing. In terms of their algebraic encoding by bisets, this translates to contraction of fundamental group elements along sequences arising from iterated lifting. As an application, we show that apart from the usual Lattès counterexamples, for any rational map on ℂ ^ {\widehat{\mathbb{C}}} with 4 4 post-critical points, there is a finite invariant collection of isotopy classes of curves into which every curve is attracted under iterated lifting. More generally, among graphs of given complexity, there exists a finite invariant collection of isotopy classes of graphs into which every graph is attracted. Applied to sufficiently rich graphs, the graph attractor provides a finite set of topological normal forms for the rational map. We also present a strategy towards proving the same statements for maps with more than 4 4 post-critical points.

Prologue

Thanks to recent efforts, many key aspects of the theory of surface homeomorphisms have been extended to the non-invertible setting of post-critically finite (PCF) branched self-coverings of
S^{2}
, now known as
Thurston maps
. Common themes include obstructions to geometrization in the form of multicurves with certain invariance properties
[
18
,
19
,
40
]
, actions on Teichmüller space
[
6
,
39
]
, on curve and tree complexes
[
5
]
, and on sets of graphs and related objects such as train tracks
[
42
,
41
]
. An analogous decomposition theory has been developed
[
33
]
with application to algorithmic aspects
[
1
,
37
,
38
,
21
]
.

The case of PCF hyperbolic polynomials is well understood, but stands apart from both homeomorphisms and general Thurston maps. The forward-invariant
Hubbard tree
of such a polynomial serves as a complete combinatorial invariant
[
36
]
. Under lifting by such a map, all curves (and all nearby structures like generic trees) converge exponentially fast to a finite invariant set of curves (and to a small combinatorial neighborhood of the Hubbard tree)
[
5
]
; see also Remark (
2
) in §
1.1
. This is in contrast with both the case of typical homeomorphisms, for which curves become exponentially more complicated under iteration (though they do converge to an invariant train-track), and with many obstructed Thurston maps, which may have wandering or infinitely many cycles of curves.

In the setting of a PCF rational map
f
, a good substitute for train tracks of homeomorphisms, as well as Hubbard trees of polynomials, seems to be an invariant planar graph

which is rich enough to allow reconstruction of

from its restriction to
\Gamma

[

,
22
,
15
]

The main objective of our paper is to develop a satisfactory similar theory beyond the polynomial case. In this realm, a central difficulty arises from the existence of obstructed twists

of a general PCF rational map

, which implies that the mapping class biset of
f
is not contracting in the sense of Nekrashevych, and which greatly complicates the analysis; see again Remark (
2
). Our work analyzes the tension between two opposing forces: the “non-uniform contraction” induced by lifting, and the “additive correction” required to ensure that the process we analyze is iterative, see (

One of our main results, Theorem

, states that non-uniform contraction eventually dominates when

4

3

4

1.
Introduction

An
analytic covering self-correspondence
is a pair of maps

F=(\phi,\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{S}),\qquad\text{ simply written }\qquad F=\phi\circ\rho^{-1}\colon\mathcal{S}\multimap\mathcal{S}

between finite area hyperbolic Riemann surfaces, where
\phi\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{S}
is a covering map,
\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{S}
is any analytic map, and throughout this text ‘
\multimap
’ denotes a multivalued map; see §
2
. The dynamics of such correspondences is the subject of much recent attention; see e.g.
[
11
,
10
,
12
,
9
,
27
,
16
]
. Of particular interest for us are examples arising as correspondences on moduli space induced by so-called Thurston maps with four postcritical points; see §
3
and §
3.1
.

If we endow
\mathcal{S}
with its hyperbolic metric, and pull it back via
\phi
to
\mathcal{T}
, then the map
\rho
becomes distance non-increasing, by the Schwarz-Pick Lemma. In many cases,
\rho
strictly decreases distances — that is, it is a contraction; this occurs if and only if
\rho
is not a covering. In this case, we call the correspondence
admissible
; see §
2.2
.

It is natural to analyze a dynamical system through its effect on the fundamental group. To this end, we suppose
F
has a
fixed point
,
\tilde{\star}\in\mathcal{T}
. This means

\star\coloneqq\phi(\tilde{\star})=\rho(\tilde{\star}).

Equivalently, and conveniently, a fixed point is an element
\star\in\mathcal{S}
together with a choice of preimage
\tilde{\star}\in\phi^{-1}(\star)
for which
\rho(\tilde{\star})=\star
. We also fix a finite set

(1)

{\sf X}\subseteq\{\text{paths in $\mathcal{S}$ that start at $\star$ and end at some element of $\rho(\phi^{-1}(\star))$}\}.

We consider infinite sequences
g^{(0)},g^{(1)},g^{(2)},\ldots
that are orbits under iteration of the following one-to-finite multivalued map on the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S},\star)
,

(2)

g^{(n)}\multimap\rho\circ\phi^{*}(g^{(n)}).\overline{{\sf x}_{n}}\eqqcolon g^{(n+1)}.

We call such an infinite sequence an
{\sf X}
-ray in the direction of
\star
above
g^{(0)}
; see Definition
5.1
. Here and below,

•

\phi^{*}(g^{(n)})\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{T}
is the unique lift of
g^{(n)}
under
\phi
starting at the given lift
\tilde{\star}
of the basepoint
\star
as in the definition of fixed point;

•

\rho\circ\phi^{*}(g^{(n)})\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{S}
is its image under
\rho
;

•

{\sf x}_{n}\in{\sf X}
is an arbitrary element of
{\sf X}
which joins the basepoint
\star
to the endpoint of
\rho\circ\phi^{*}(g^{(n)})
;

•

\overline{{\sf x}_{n}}
is the path
{\sf x}_{n}
traversed in the opposite direction;

•

‘
.
’ denotes concatenation of paths.

If
M\geq\max\{d_{\mathcal{S}}(\star,s):s\in\rho(\phi^{-1}(\star))\}
and
{\sf X}
consists of all paths in
\mathcal{S}
that start at
\star
, end at some element of
\rho(\phi^{-1}(\star))
, and have length at most
M
, then
{\sf X}
is necessarily finite, by properness of the hyperbolic metric, and any finite orbit
g^{(0)},\dots,g^{(n)}
can be extended to an
{\sf X}
-ray.

For a path
\gamma
, we denote by
|\gamma|
its hyperbolic length. This induces a proper norm
|g|
on the group
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S},\star)
. Since
\rho
is not a covering, then it is a contraction, so we have

|\rho\circ\phi^{*}(g^{(n)})|<|g^{(n)}|

and therefore

(3)

|g^{(n+1)}|\leq|\rho\circ\phi^{*}(g^{(n)}).\overline{{\sf x}_{n}}|<|g^{(n)}|+\max_{{\sf x}\in{\sf X}}|{\sf x}|.

Thus, the contraction induced by pulling back via
F
is opposed by an additive correction of magnitude at most
\max_{{\sf x}\in{\sf X}}|{\sf x}|
coming from concatenation with
{\sf x}\in{\sf X}
. Our main result is that the contraction eventually dominates:

Theorem A

(Finite Attractor for
{\sf X}
-rays)
.

Assume that
F=\phi,\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{S}
is an admissible correspondence, and
\star\in\mathcal{S}
is a fixed point of
F
. Then for every finite
{\sf X}
as in (
1
) there is a finite attractor
A({\sf X})\subset\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S},\star)
such that for every
{\sf X}
-ray
g^{(0)},g^{(1)},g^{(2)},\ldots
in the direction of
\star
, the elements
g^{(n)}
belong to
A({\sf X})
for all
n
sufficiently large.

1.1.
Application to Thurston theory

See Theorem
7.5
for a detailed formulation. Let
f\colon(\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}},P)\righttoleftarrow
be a rational map, with
P
a finite subset of
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
that contains the critical values of
f
and satisfies
f(P)\subseteq P
, a
post-critically finite
map. By taking preimages, the map
f
induces a pullback operation on the set of
multicurves
: isotopy classes of collections of pairwise disjoint and non-isotopic essential curves in
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}-P
. Somewhat similarly,
f
induces a multivalued map on certain sets of trees whose vertices contain
P
, and on the set of graphs arising as
spines
(graphs that are deformation retracts) for
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}-P
. Under iteration:

•

if
T_{n}
is a tree on
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
with
T_{n}\supset P
, then
T_{n+1}
can be chosen to be any subtree of
f^{-1}(T_{n})
, pruned so that the
complexity
—defined as the sum of the number of edges and vertices—remains bounded under iteration;

•

if
\Gamma_{n}\subset\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}-P
is a spine, then
\Gamma_{n+1}
can be chosen to be any subspine of
f^{-1}(\Gamma_{n})
.

If
f
is a homeomorphism of infinite order, then it is well known that
f^{-n}(\gamma)
, for an essential closed curve, will typically get more and more complicated as
|n|
increases — it converges to a
lamination
on
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus P
. If
f
has degree
\geq 2
and is expanding (for example, rational), however, there is a tension between
f^{-1}
being contracting on the one hand, and
f^{-1}(\gamma)
consisting of, in the worst case,
\deg(f)
branches of
\gamma
. Our main corollary, below, shows that the contraction of
f^{-1}
wins, at least in some settings.

If
\#P=4
, and
f
is rational and not a so-called Lattès example, then the correspondence
F=\phi,\rho
on moduli space
\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\mathscr{M}_{0,4}=\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}-\{0,1,\infty\}
induced by
f
is admissible. This allows us to deduce:

Corollary B
.

Assume
\#P=4
and that
f
is a non-Lattès rational map. Then there is a finite attractor for the pullback iteration on multicurves, on trees, and on spines: there is a finite collection of such objects such that every multicurve/tree/spine, upon pulling back sufficiently many times, reaches the attractor.

We sketch in §
1.3
how our approach could help generalizing Corollary
B
to arbitrarily large
P
.

We note that we have only mentioned curves in this brief subsection, but that our arguments apply equally well to graphs of given complexity (say, given number of vertices and edges). This should have useful applications to the study of graphs of minimal entropy capturing the dynamics of
f
.

Remarks

(1)

The existence of a finite attractor for curves, as in Corollary
B
, was conjectured for general
P
by the third author in the 2000s, see
[
34
]
. Its extension to graphs is natural; unlike the pullback map on curves, the pullback map on e.g. spine graphs is always non-trivial.

(2)

The conjecture is known in the case of post-critically finite polynomials
[
32
,
5
]
. In fact, for hyperbolic polynomials Equation (
3
) takes the stronger form

(4)

|g^{(n+1)}|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}|g^{(n)}|+\max_{x\in{\sf X}}|{\sf x}|\quad\text{for some }\lambda>1

with respect to the word metric, and all our results easily follow from (
4
). On the other hand, (

) cannot hold if a map has an obstructed twist.

(3)

The failure of (
4
) in the general setting motivated the first two authors to put forth
[
3

, which can be thought of as a version of Corollary
B
with quantified number of steps to reach the attractor.

(4)

In the case of curves, Corollary

is known in several cases; see
[
35
]
for a survey.

(5)

For the case of trees, the pullback relation we consider is quite similar in spirit to the “ivy iteration” method of
[
41
]
; their implementation finds trees invariant-up-to-isotopy under a quadratic rational map

.

(6)

A result of Hlushchanka shows that a critically fixed rational map is determined by the planar isomorphism class of its so-called charge graph; see
[
24
]
. Hlushchanka and Prochorov show, using intersection theory applied to arcs on the sphere, that an induced pullback iteration on graphs analogous to the ones considered here is shown to converge to the charge graph; see
[
23
]
.

(7)

The exclusion of Lattès examples is necessary — for example, the flexible Lattès examples fix every curve.

(8)

See
[
14
]
for a survey of recent results on finding invariant graphs; its main result asserts that given any critically finite rational map

, there is some iterate

for which

preserves exactly some finite graph containing the postcritical set of

. The authors note there that one cannot always specify the precise form of this graph; e.g.

]

, there is no Jordan curve

.

(9)

We note the following subtlety in Corollary

, namely, that for trees, the size of the attractor necessarily grows upon passing to iterates. For example, suppose

is a rational map with finite post-critical set

and Julia set the whole sphere, and

is any tree with vertex set containing

. Taking preimages, one obtains for each

a cell structure on
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
. Since

. It follows that for some sufficiently large iterate

(10)

We conjecture that Theorem

, we present a strategy towards its proof.

For

is an arbitrary Thurston map with postcritical set

Outline of proof of Corollary
B
.

The first step is to note that the map

induces an admissible correspondence on the moduli space
\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\mathscr{M}_{0,4}=\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}-\{0,1,\infty\}

The elements

comprising the terms of an

The property of being an
{\sf X}
-ray can be abstracted in the setting of a

This biset appears in two guises, one in which elements are paths (with concatenation of paths as operation), and one in which they are Thurston maps (with composition of maps as operation); these two points of view correspond to the avatars of the fundamental group as a set of paths or a set of deck transformations.

Working in the realm of paths, we apply Theorem

1.2.
Outline of the proof of Theorem
A

Our main result is proven as a consequence of the following:

Theorem C
.

Suppose
F=\phi,\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{S}
is an admissible correspondence, and
\star\in\mathcal{S}
is a fixed point of
F
. Let
\star
also denote the constant path at
\star
. Fix a finite collection
{\sf X}
of curves as in (
1
). Then there are constants
\kappa>0,N\in{\mathbb{N}},\epsilon>0,\xi>0
such that for every
g\in\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S},\star)
and every
{\sf X}
-ray of loops
(g^{(n)})
in the direction of
\star
above
g
, we have

(1)

|g^{(n+1)}|\leq|g^{(n)}|+\xi
for all
n
;

(2)

if
|g|>\kappa
then
|g^{(n)}|\leq|g|-\epsilon
for some

.

Note that Conclusion (1) follows easily from the definition of

Let us now outline the proof of Theorem

as a finite, very large number (so as to dwarf any universal constant

), and consider

.

The proof proceeds by contradiction. Let us assume that Theorem

does not hold. We begin by a “soft” preparation of the sequence of curves violating Theorem
C
: there is a
tight sequence

(g_{m})_{m}
in
G\coloneqq\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S},\star)
with

(5)

|g_{m}|>m\text{ and }|g_{m}^{(n)}|>|g_{m}|-1/m\qquad\text{for all }n\in\{0,\dots,m\}.

In other words, for a sufficiently large
m>\!\!>1
, the lift
g_{m}\leadsto g^{(n)}_{m}
of

does not lose any length. Consider then the

of

(6)

g_{m}=\ell_{0,m}.r_{1,m}\ldots r_{k_{m},m}.\ell_{k_{m},m},

in which the

, are in the thin part of

while the

Since

is uniformly contracting on the thick pieces

We let

be the maximal number of roundabouts in

whose lengths increase to infinity (with respect to all subsequences

), see (

). We then define

as the maximum over all

.

Let us next impose an additional combinatorial assumption that

is

, see Definition
6.6
:
the number of roundabouts in
(g_{m})_{m}
whose lengths increase to infinity equals

. For very tight sequences, (
7
) takes the following form: by ignoring “short” roundabouts and for all sufficiently large

, we have

(8)

|g_{m}|=\sum_{i=1}^{K}|r_{i,m}|+\mathcal{O}(1),\qquad|r_{i,m}|\to\infty\quad\text{for all $i$},

see Lemma
6.8
.

Since
\mathcal{S}
has dimension

, every roundabout

lies in a small neighborhood of a puncture

. Denoting by
\sphericalangle r_{i,m}
the

of

around

:

(9)

|r_{i,m}|=2\log_{+}|\sphericalangle r_{i,m}|+\mathcal{O}(1).

Let us analyze what happens under lifting by

: above each puncture

, the map

is given locally by a Puiseux series

.
We consider the thick-thin decompositions of the lifts

. Since

is very tight, the thin-thick decomposition persists under lifting, see §

, and we may write each

with every roundabout

. Equation (

(11)

\displaystyle|g_{m}^{(n)}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{K}|\ell_{i,m}^{(n)}|+\sum_{i=1}^{K}2\log_{+}|t_{i,m}^{(n)}\sphericalangle r_{i,m}|+\mathcal{O}(1)

\displaystyle=|g_{m}|+\sum_{i=0}^{K}|\ell_{i,m}^{(n)}|+\sum_{i=1}^{K}2\log t_{i,m}^{(n)}+\mathcal{O}(1)

In Lemma

\displaystyle|g_{m(s)}^{(n(s))}|

\displaystyle=|g_{m(s)}|+\sum_{i=1}^{K}2\log t_{i,{m(s)}}^{(n(s))}+\mathcal{O}(1)

\displaystyle=|g_{m(s)}|+2\log\Big(t_{1,{m(s)}}^{(n(s))}\cdots t_{K,{m(s)}}^{(n(s))}\Big)+\mathcal{O}(1).

Past this preparation step, we arrive at the heart of the argument, requiring precise analytic estimates. We claim (see Lemma

) that the local degrees

satisfy

(13)

\displaystyle t^{(n)}_{1,m}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\exp(-\eta n)),

\displaystyle t^{(n)}_{i,m}t^{(n)}_{i+1,m}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\exp(-\eta n))\text{ for all }i\in\{1,\dots,K-1\},

for some

. Consider indeed two consecutive roundabouts, and the part of

between them. We compare that curve to the (infinite) geodesic connecting the punctures

and

, see the curve

) of length, so its lift under

Multiplying an appropriate subset (depending on the parity of

so (

The key point of the argument is that the linear loss of length

in (

in (

1.3.
Strategy towards an extension to higher dimension

We believe that Theorem

holds with respect to any fixed point

of
F
.

This conjecture implies Corollary
B
for the rational map associated with
\star
.

We elaborate below on the strategy towards proving Conjecture
D
, based on the arguments outlined in §
1.2
. We expect that the setting of tight and very tight sequences together with the Extraction Argument is applicable, see (
14
); a key challenge is to establish a variant of (
13
). We anticipate that an appropriate decomposition theory for generalized roundabouts would resolve the involved difficulties.

Let us consider a tight sequence
(g_{m})_{m}
in
G\coloneqq\pi_{1}(\mathscr{M}_{0,d},\star)
as in (
5
); we write
g_{m}\colon[0,1]\to\mathscr{M}_{0,d}
. For every
s\in[0,1]
, the point

represents a marked Riemann sphere
\tau_{m,s}=(S^{2},P_{g_{m}(s)})
and we denote by
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{m,s}
the multicurve (possibly empty) representing the Thin-Thick decomposition of
\tau_{m,s}
.

Since
g_{m}(s)
is a tight sequence, lifting via
F^{-n}\colon g_{m}(s)\leadsto g_{m}^{(n)}(s)
almost preserves the infinitesimal length of
g_{m}
for most

. This infinitesimal length is described by the quadratic differential
q_{m,s}\equiv q_{m,s}(z)dz^{2}
on
\tau_{m,s}
dual to
\dot{g}_{m}
; the induced map

should almost preserve
q_{m,s}
. It is shown in
[

that

can be decomposed relatively to the multicurve
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{m,s}
on

. It is a

that, in this decomposition, most of the mass of

should be either within fat annuli (thin parts of the decomposition) or within the small spheres with a non-hyperbolic orbifold (namely, on which the induced map has degree

or is doubly covered by a torus endomorphism). We expect that there is a well-defined minimal submulticurve
\mathscr{C}_{m,s}\subset\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{m,s}
such that the decomposition of
(\tau_{m,s},{\mathscr{C}}_{m,s})
combines all small spheres of
(\tau_{m,s},\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{m,s})
with a non-parabolic orbifold that “can be combined”; compare with the Levy multicurve from
[
2
]
. Finally, we
conjecture
that there is a decomposition of
g_{m}
into
generalized roundabouts

r_{i,m}

(14)

g_{m}=\ell_{0,m}.r_{1,m}\dots r_{K,m}.\ell_{K,m}\hskip 56.9055pt\text{similar to~\eqref{eq:g_m:decomp},}

where
r_{i,m}
are maximal subcurves of the

so that

is non-empty and depends continuously (i.e., is stable) on

within

may or may not be within a compact subpart of

Assuming (

as follows. Let us suppose that the decomposition of

Conventions and notation

Composition of functions

•

Concatenation of paths is denoted

A smooth map

2.
Correspondences

2.1.
Terminology

(1)

A
topological self-correspondence
is a pair of continuous maps
\phi,\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{S}
of topological spaces. We think of it as a multivalued map
F\colon\mathcal{S}\multimap\mathcal{S}
given by
F=\phi\circ\rho^{-1}
. The
n
th iterate of
F
in this “forward” direction will be denoted by
F^{(n)}
.

(2)

A
covering correspondence
is a topological self-correspondence for which
\phi
is a finite covering map.

A remark about directionality is in order. For a covering correspondence
F
, local branches of
F^{-1}
exist, so it might seem more natural to define
F
going in the other direction. Indeed, there are interesting examples where
\rho
is constant, making
\rho^{-1}
rather pathological
[
8
]
. However, the simplest examples are when
\rho
is injective, such as in the case of the Rabbit Example in §
3.1
below. Also, with this convention of direction,
F
has the path-lifting property.

(3)

If
\phi
and
\rho
are both continuous, both analytic, etc. we use the adjective
topological
,
analytic
, etc. to describe the correspondence.

(4)

A
backward orbit
of
s\in\mathcal{S}
is a sequence
t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in\mathcal{T}
with
\phi(t_{0})=s
and
\rho(t_{i})=\phi(t_{i+1})
for all
i=0,\dots,n-1
. Abusing notation, suppressing the fact that the
t_{n}
’s are part of the definition, it is a sequence
s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in\mathcal{S}
with
F(s_{i+1})=s_{i}
for all
i=0,\dots,n-1
.

(5)

The
backward orbit

F^{-{\mathbb{N}}}(s_{0})
of
s_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
is the set of all backward orbits of
s_{0}
.

(6)

A
fixed point
of a self-correspondence is a point
\tilde{\star}\in\mathcal{T}
such that
\phi(\tilde{\star})=\rho(\tilde{\star})
. Equivalently, a fixed point is a point
\star\in\mathcal{S}
together with a choice of
\tilde{\star}\in\phi^{-1}(\star)
satisfying
\rho(\tilde{\star})=\star
. We again abuse notation and refer to
\star
as a fixed point of the self-correspondence if such a
\tilde{\star}
exists, with the understanding that a chosen lift
\tilde{\star}
is part of the data.

If
\phi,\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{S}
is a covering correspondence, then any path
\gamma
in
\mathcal{S}
can be lifted under
\phi
, uniquely once a preimage of
\gamma(0)
under
\phi
has been chosen; and then mapped by
\rho
to obtain another path in
\mathcal{S}
. If a choice of preimage of
\gamma(0)
is available from the context, we refer to this new path as
F^{*}(\gamma)
.

2.2.
Admissible complex correspondences

Our main results concern analytic correspondences on Riemann surfaces satisfying a type of “critical finiteness” property that is strictly weaker than that defined in
[
12
]
. In our setting, cusps also play a distinguished role. Thus in order to distinguish our setting from that of others, we encapsulate these properties into the notion of an “admissible complex correspondence”, which we now define precisely. Suppose that

(1)

\widehat{\mathcal{T}},\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
are compact Riemann surfaces (and in particular are connected);

(2)

\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{T}),\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{S})
are finite subsets of
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
, respectively;

(3)

\mathcal{T}\coloneqq\widehat{\mathcal{T}}\setminus\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{T})
,
\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\widehat{\mathcal{S}}\setminus\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{S})
, so that now the sets
\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{T}),\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{S})
become the ends of
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{S}
, respectively;

(4)

\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{S}
are hyperbolic Riemann surfaces;

(5)

\phi\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{S}
is a analytic covering map of finite degree
D
larger than one;

(6)

\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{S}
is analytic and is not a covering map.

We refer to a correspondence
\phi,\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{S}
satisfying (1)-(6) above as an
admissible complex correspondence
.

2.3.
Properties

An admissible complex correspondence satisfies several properties.

(1)

The maps
\phi,\rho
extend over their sets of ends to analytic maps (which we denote by the same symbols)
\phi,\rho\colon\widehat{\mathcal{T}}\to\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
, so
\phi(\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{T}))\subseteq\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{S})
and
\rho^{-1}(\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{S}))\subseteq\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{T})
.

(2)

The multivalued map
F\coloneqq\phi\circ\rho^{-1}
has the property that its
forward post-critical set

\bigcup_{n\geq 0}F^{(n)}(\{\text{branch values of }\phi\})

is contained in the (sometimes larger) finite set
\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{S})
.

(3)

the map
\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{S}
is a contraction: if we give
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S}
the hyperbolic metric with constant curvature
-1
, then
\|d\rho(t)\|_{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S}}<1
at all points
t\in\mathcal{T}
; in particular, every periodic point of the multivalued map
F\colon\mathcal{S}\multimap\mathcal{S}
given by
F=\phi\circ\rho^{-1}
is repelling. Thus, each admissible complex correspondence is
weakly hyperbolic
, in the sense that
\|d\rho(t)\|_{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S}}<1
at all points
t\in\mathcal{T}
.

(4)

We have
\deg(\rho)<\deg(\phi)
, by the following argument due to Walter Parry. By removing a set of area zero from
\mathcal{S}
, we may find a simply-connected subset
U\subseteq\mathcal{S}
above which
\rho
is a trivial covering with preimages
U_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,\deg(\rho)
. Then

\displaystyle\deg(\phi)\text{area}(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=\text{area}(\mathcal{T})=\text{area}(U_{1})+\cdots+\text{area}(U_{\deg(\rho)})

\displaystyle>\deg(\rho)\text{area}(U)=\deg(\rho)\text{area}(\mathcal{S}),

since
\rho
is a strict contraction.

(5)

Combined with
[
17
]
*Theorem 5.3, the previous Property (4) implies that the number of points of period
n
grows exponentially with
n
.

(6)

Given
s_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
, the accumulation set
\text{acc}(F^{-{\mathbb{N}}}(s_{0}))\subset\overline{\mathcal{S}}
of its backward orbit (defined as the set of all points
s\in\overline{\mathcal{S}}
such that there exists a backward orbit of
s_{0}
accumulating at
s
) is nonempty, by compactness of
\overline{\mathcal{S}}
. It is also independent of
s_{0}
.

To see this, let
s_{0},s_{1},\dots
be a backward orbit of
s
. Suppose first
s\in\text{acc}(F^{-{\mathbb{N}}}(s_{0}))\cap\mathcal{S}
. Pick some other
s_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
. Join
s_{0}
to
s_{0}^{\prime}
by a path of
\mathcal{S}
-hyperbolic length say
L
. By weak hyperbolicity and path-lifting, there is a backward orbit
s_{0}^{\prime},s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime},\ldots
such that the hyperbolic distances between
s_{n},s_{n}^{\prime}
satisfy
|s_{n}-s_{n}^{\prime}|<L
for each
n
. If some subsequence
s_{n_{k}}\to s\in\mathcal{S}
then since
\|dF^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}}<\lambda<1
in e.g. a
2L
-neighborhood of
s
we have
|s_{n_{k}}-s_{n_{k}}^{\prime}|\to 0
. If some subsequence
s_{n_{k}}\to s\in\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{S})
then we also have
s_{n_{k}^{\prime}}\to s
since hyperbolic balls about
s_{n_{k}}
of fixed radius
2L
become smaller and smaller in any compatible metric on
\overline{\mathcal{S}}
as
s_{n_{k}}\to s
. A similar argument treats the case when
s\in\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{S})
.

Thus the
limit set

\Lambda(\phi,\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{S})\subset\overline{\mathcal{S}}
, defined as
\text{acc}(F^{-{\mathbb{N}}}(s_{0}))
for some (equivalently, any)
s_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
is well-defined, and backward-invariant in the sense that
F^{-1}(\Lambda)=\Lambda
.

2.4.
Dynamical regularity

Suppose
\phi,\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{S}
is an admissible complex correspondence. By definition, it satisfies the weak hyperbolicity property. If the limit set
\Lambda
is a nonempty compact subset of
\mathcal{S}
, we say that
\phi,\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{S}
is
uniformly hyperbolic
. Equivalently: there is a nonempty compact subset
K\subset\mathcal{S}
for which
F^{-1}(K)\subset K
. Between the weak and uniform hyperbolic correspondences, we single out the
strongly subhyperbolic

such that

uniformly contracts lengths of curves.

2.5.
Bisets

In this section, we recast the notion of
{\sf X}
-ray using the natural, but perhaps less familiar to dynamicists, algebraic language of bisets associated to correspondences.

We draw heavily from
[
1
]
. For groups
G,H
, an
H
-
G
-biset
is a set equipped with a left
H
-action and a right
G
-action that commute. Bisets generalize the homomorphism on fundamental group induced by a continuous map: given pointed path-connected topological spaces
(\mathcal{T},\star^{\prime})
and
(\mathcal{S},\star)
, any map
\psi\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{S}
,
that need not preserve basepoints
, gives rise to a
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{T},\star^{\prime})
-
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S},\star)
-biset

B(\psi)=\{\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{S}:\gamma(0)=\psi(\star^{\prime}),\gamma(1)=\star\}/{\simeq},

where ‘
\simeq
’ denotes homotopy with respect to the endpoints. The actions are
[\beta]\cdot[\gamma]\cdot[\alpha]=[(\psi\circ\beta).\gamma.\alpha]
, for based loops
\alpha,\beta
in
(\mathcal{S},\star),(\mathcal{T},\star^{\prime})
respectively.

We shall not recall the general definition of the biset of a correspondence
F=\phi,\rho:\mathcal{T}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{S}
, but restrict to the case in which
\phi
is a covering, when (see
[
1
]
*Lemma 4.4) it may be defined by

B(F)=\{(\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{S},p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}):\gamma(0)=\phi(p^{\prime})=\star,\gamma(1)=\rho(p^{\prime})\}/{\simeq},

again with ‘
\simeq
’ denoting homotopy that preserves the relations indicated between
\gamma
and
p^{\prime}
. It is a
G
-
G
-biset for the group
G=\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S},\star)
. The left action is by pre-catenation, and the right action of
[\alpha]
on
[\gamma,p^{\prime}]
is computing by lifting
\alpha
to a path
\widetilde{\alpha}\coloneqq\phi^{*}(\alpha)
starting at
p^{\prime}
and setting
[\gamma,p^{\prime}]\cdot[\alpha]=[\gamma.(\rho\circ\widetilde{\alpha}),\widetilde{\alpha}(1)]
.

In particular,
B(F)
is
left-free
: there is a subset
{\sf X}
of
B(F)
such that, when only considering the left action,
B(F)\cong G\times{\sf X}
. We call such
{\sf X}
a
basis
for the biset; it is of course not unique. We have
|{\sf X}|=\deg(\phi)
, and
{\sf X}
will be of the form
{\sf X}=\{({\sf x}_{p^{\prime}},p^{\prime}):p^{\prime}\in\phi^{-1}(\star)\}
for some choice of paths
{\sf x}_{p^{\prime}}
from
\star
to
\rho(p^{\prime})
for every possible
p^{\prime}\in\phi^{-1}(\star)
.

Given an
H
-
G
-biset
B
and a
K
-
H
-biset
C
, their
composition
is the biset

C\otimes_{H}B=\frac{C\times B}{(ch,b)=(c,hb)\;\forall c\in C,h\in H,b\in B},

The assignment of bisets to topological correspondences is functorial under composition, hence iteration. If
B
is a
G-G
biset, so are the products
B^{\otimes n}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The element
{\sf b}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\sf b}_{n}\in B^{\otimes n}
is represented by a concatenation of paths
\undertilde{b}_{1}.\undertilde{b}_{2}.\ldots.\undertilde{b}_{n}
of paths, with
\undertilde{b}_{1}
based at
\star
, where
F^{(i-1)}(\undertilde{b}_{i})={\sf b}_{i}\in B
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, and where each
\undertilde{b}_{i}
starts at the endpoint of
\undertilde{b}_{i-1}
. The actions of
G
are again by pre-concatenation (which is free), and by lifting under
F^{(n)}
and post-concatenation. If
{\sf X}
is a basis of
B
then
{\sf X}^{n}
, the set of words of length
n
in the alphabet
{\sf X}
, is a basis for
B^{\otimes n}
.

Examples: post-critically finite rational maps

3.1.
Examples of maps with four postcritical points

Let us detail some maps with
\#P=4
. In this setting, we may identify
\text{Teich}(S^{2},P)
with the upper-half plane
{\mathbb{H}}
, the moduli space with a triply-punctured sphere, e.g.
\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}

with the well-known modular function so that its deck group becomes the principal congruence subgroup
\Gamma(2)\coloneqq\{A\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}):A\equiv 1\pmod{2}\}
acting on
{\mathbb{H}}
.

Rabbit polynomial

When

is the so-called
rabbit
complex polynomial
f(z)=z^{2}+c
, whose critical point

is periodic of period
3
and
\Im(z)>0
, after suitable normalizations the correspondence becomes
F(x)=1-\frac{1}{x^{2}}
, a single-valued post-critically finite hyperbolic rational function with three postcritical points lying in a common superattracting three-cycle
0\mapsto\infty\mapsto 1\mapsto 0
. The correspondence on moduli space is therefore uniformly hyperbolic. This example is studied in detail in
[
4
]
.

Dendrite polynomial

When
f
is the so-called
dendrite
complex polynomial
f(z)=z^{2}+i
, whose critical point
z=0
is preperiodic with preperiod
1
and period

, after suitable normalizations the correspondence becomes
F(x)=(-1+2/x)^{2}
, a post-critically finite rational function with Julia set equal to the whole sphere. The orbifold of this map in the sense of Douady-Hubbard
[
18

turns out to be Euclidean with signature
(2,4,4)
. The corresponding Euclidean length orbifold metric on

shows that this correspondence on moduli space is strongly subhyperbolic, and thus uniformly expanding on the entire completion of moduli space with respect to this metric. This is also studied in detail in
[
4
]
.

Lodge’s example

When

is the example featured in the thesis of R. Lodge
[
28
,
8
]
, the correspondence on moduli space may be described as follows. Let

4.
Hyperbolic geometry

In this section, we assume that
\mathcal{S}
is a hyperbolic Riemann surface which is conformally isomorphic to the complement of a finite nonempty subset
\text{\sc cusps}(\mathcal{S})
contained in a compact surface
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
, and
\mathcal{S}
is equipped with its hyperbolic metric of curvature
-1
. Choose a basepoint
\star\in\mathcal{S}
, and write
G\coloneqq\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S},\star)
. Fix a universal cover
\pi\colon({\mathbb{H}},\tilde{\star})\to(\mathcal{S},\star)
. For a rectifiable path
\gamma
in
\mathcal{S}

|\gamma|
denotes its hyperbolic length.

4.1.
Hyperbolic metric on
G

Consider
g\in G
. The geodesic joining
\tilde{\star}
to
g\cdot\tilde{\star}
projects to a closed curve on
\mathcal{S}
which, upon removing
\star
, is an open geodesic segment. While a closed curve, we emphasize that this is typically not a closed geodesic; there is typically an angle formed at
\star
. Abusing notation, we will often denote this geodesic representative by the same symbol,
g
. We denote by
|g|
the hyperbolic length of this segment. This defines the
hyperbolic norm
on
G
. Since the covering group acts properly discontinuously, the hyperbolic norm on
G
is proper: for any constant
L>0
, the set of
g\in G
with
|g|<L
is finite.

Caution is in order: though we have used the term “norm”, the hyperbolic norm on
G
is not bi-Lipschitz equivalent to a word metric norm induced by a finite generating set. For example, suppose
g
is a primitive peripheral loop about some cusp of
\mathcal{S}
, and consider the sequence of powers,
g^{n}
. The word norms of
g^{n}
grow linearly, while the hyperbolic norms of
g^{n}
grow logarithmically.

4.2.
Cusp neighborhoods

Consider a cusp
x

\mathcal{S}
. For a cusp circumference parameter
\delta>0
sufficiently small, there is a neighborhood

of

isometric to
\{z\in{\mathbb{H}}:\Im(z)>1/\delta\}/\langle z\mapsto z+1\rangle

\partial B(x,\delta)
is the parameter
\delta

Proposition 4.2

4.4.

We continue, assuming that we are in the setup of §

(Winding numbers)
.

Assume the setup of Propositions

(1)

(2)

Suppose that

•

Here is an example. More general notions could be formulated; the one below is simple enough to illustrate our themes.

that spend larger and larger amounts of time (length) in some fixed cusp neighborhood. An index
i\in\{1,\ldots,K^{\prime}\}
for which
\limsup_{m\to\infty}|r_{i,m}|=\infty
will be called an
unbounded roundabout
. Thus every tight sequence has a nonempty set of unbounded roundabouts.

The property of being a tight sequence is not
a priori
preserved under lifting under
F
. However, given a sequence
(g_{m}^{(n)})_{m}
of
{\sf X}
-rays in the direction of
f
, from the two-dimensionally-indexed array
(g^{(n)}_{m})_{n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, it is always possible to extract a tight sequence — and one may in fact prescribe a lower bound on the levels of lifts involved. In the statement below, the sequences referred to are sequences of natural numbers. The precise form of the lower bound on
m(t)
in the statement is not important here; we include it merely for concreteness.

Lemma 6.4

(Robust extraction)
.

Suppose
(g_{m})
is a tight sequence. Given

(1)

an increasing sequence
N(t),t=1,2,\ldots
with
N(t)>t
, and

(2)

an increasing sequence
m(t),t=1,2,\ldots
with
m(t)>\!\!>N(t)
sufficiently large (specifically,
m(t)>M(t)\coloneqq N(t)+t^{2}(N(t)\xi+1)+t
),

there exists a sequence
n(t),t=1,2,\ldots
with
n(t)\in[N(t),M(t)]
such that the sequence

h_{t}\coloneqq g_{m(t)}^{(n(t))}\in G

is tight.

Proof.

We first verify that the lengths of the
h_{t}
’s grow as required. Since the sequence
(g_{m})
is tight, applying both conditions of Definition
6.2
we find

|h_{t}|=|g_{m(t)}^{(n(t))}|>|g_{m(t)}|-\frac{1}{m(t)}>m(t)-\frac{1}{m(t)}>N(t)>t.

We next verify that the lengths of the lifts of the
h_{t}
satisfy the required lower bound. Fix
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
. To ease notation, put
h\coloneqq h_{m(t)}^{(N(t))}
. Consider the orbit segment of length
t
under lifting given by

h,h^{(1)},h^{(2)},\ldots,h^{(t)}.

If for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
we have

|h^{(j)}|>|h|-\frac{1}{t},

then we may set
n(t)\coloneqq N(t)
. Otherwise, there exists
j_{1}\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
with

|h^{(j_{1})}|\leq|h|-\frac{1}{t}.

We now repeat this argument, starting with
h^{(j_{1})}
. That is, we consider the orbit segment of length
t
under lifting given by

h^{(j_{1})},h^{(j_{1}+1)},h^{(j_{1}+2)},\ldots,h^{(j_{1}+t)}.

If for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
we have

|h^{(j_{1}+j)}|>|h|-\frac{1}{t},

then we may set
n(t)\coloneqq N(t)+j_{1}
. Otherwise, we find an integer
j_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
for which

|h^{(j_{1}+j_{2})}|\leq|h^{(j_{1})}|-\frac{1}{t}\leq|h|-\frac{2}{t}.

Continuing in this manner, if we fail to find the desired bounds some number of times, say
s
, then we have the upper bound on lengths

|h^{(j_{1}+j_{2}+\ldots+j_{s})}\leq|h|-\frac{s}{t}.

We now proceed to bound
s
. Unwinding the definition of
h
, the previous inequality yields

\left|g_{m(t)}^{(N(t)+j_{1}+\ldots+j_{s})}\right|\leq\left|g_{m(t)}^{(N(t))}\right|-\frac{s}{t}.

The basic length upper bound (Proposition
6.1
) and the fact that
(g_{m})_{m}
is a tight sequence then imply

|g_{m(t)}|-\frac{1}{m(t)}<\left|g_{m(t)}^{(N(t)+j_{1}+\ldots+j_{s})}\right|\leq|g_{m(t)}|+N(t)\xi-\frac{s}{t}.

This implies

s\leq t(N(t)\xi+1).

This bound on
s
yields that among the sequence of lifts

h^{(1)},h^{(2)},\ldots,h^{(t^{2}(N(t)\xi+1)+t)}

there must be an index
i=i(t)\in\{1,2,\ldots,t^{2}(N(t)\xi+1)+t\}
such that the subsequence of consecutive lifts

h^{(i)},h^{(i+1)},h^{(i+2)},\ldots,h^{(i+t)}

has the property that the desired lower bounds

|h^{(i+j)}|>|h^{(i)}|-\frac{1}{t},j=1\ldots t

hold. Putting
n(t)\coloneqq N(t)+i(t)
and
h_{t}\coloneqq g_{m(t)}^{(n(t))}
, the Lemma is proven.
∎

6.3.
m
-roundabouts

For a tight sequence
(g_{m})
, Proposition
6.3
implies that as
m\to\infty
, the number of roundabouts is universally bounded independently of the chosen sequence by the constant
K^{\prime}
, and that the lengths of some—but perhaps not all—of these roundabouts must become unbounded, and so (by Lemma
4.3
) spiral more and more around some cusps. We wish our decompositions to focus only on these cusps, and not on cusps for which the spiraling is bounded.

Choose and fix a large
roundabout parameter

\mu>\!\!>\zeta
.

Definition 6.5

(
\mu
-roundabout)
.

Consider
g\in G
. The
(\delta,\zeta)
-thick-thin decomposition with roundabout parameter
\mu
is given by

g=\ell_{0}.r_{1}\ldots r_{k}.\ell_{k}

where

(1)

each
r_{i}
is a roundabout in the
(\delta,\zeta)
-thick-thin decomposition;

(2)

|r_{i}|\geq\mu
for each
1\leq i\leq k
;

(3)

the
r_{i}
are maximal with respect to (
1
) and (
2
).

We call the
r_{i}
the
\mu
-
roundabouts
or
\mu
-
thin
parts, and the
\ell_{i}

\mu
-thick parts. In this notation, we say that
g
has
\mu
-roundabouts

r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
. The estimate used in the proof of Lemma
4.3
then implies that if
|r_{i}|\geq\mu
, then
r_{i}
winds at least
\sphericalangle(r_{i})\approx\exp(\mu)
times around the corresponding puncture.

Now suppose
(g_{m})
is a tight sequence and
\mu>\!\!>1
is a roundabout parameter. Applying Proposition
6.3
, we find the following. Since every
\mu
-roundabout is an ordinary roundabout, and the number of ordinary roundabouts is uniformly bounded by the constant
K^{\prime}
of that Proposition, we have

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\#\{\text{$\mu$-roundabouts in }g_{m}\}<\infty.

Obviously this limit decreases as
\mu
increases, and it cannot decrease to zero, since every tight sequence has at least one unbounded roundabout. Thus

(23)

k((g_{m}))\coloneqq\lim_{\mu\to\infty}\limsup_{m\to\infty}\#\{\text{$\mu$-roundabouts in }g_{m}\}\in[1,K^{\prime}].

In other words,
k=k((g_{m}))
is the smallest integer such that the
g_{m}
’s eventually have at most
k
unbounded roundabouts. From Proposition
6.3
, we know
k\leq K^{\prime}
.

6.4.
Very tight sequences

Proposition
6.3
provides a universal upper bound on the number of unbounded roundabouts in a tight sequence. To capture the extremal case, we set

K\coloneqq\sup\{k((g_{m})):(g_{m})\text{ is tight}\}\leq K^{\prime}<\infty.

The definition below captures the extremal tight sequences with respect to this bound.

Definition 6.6

(Very tight sequences)
.

A
very tight sequence
is a tight sequence
(g_{m})
in
G
such that, additionally, the number of
m
-roundabouts in
g_{m}
is precisely the stable extremal number,
K
, of unbounded roundabouts whenever
m
is sufficiently large.

Note that if
(g_{m})_{m}
is any tight sequence for which
k((g_{m}))=K
realizes the extremal number of unbounded roundabouts, then by passing to subsequences we may assume that in fact the sequence
(g_{m})_{m}
is very tight. Summarizing: if Theorem
C
fails, then there is a very tight sequence.

We will often make claims on terms
g_{m}
that hold whenever
m
is sufficiently large; we abbreviate this as “something holds eventually for
g_{m}
”. Thus in a very tight sequence eventually the number of
m
-roundabouts of
g_{m}
is precisely
K
.

Lemma 6.7
.

There is a constant
\lambda>0
such that, for every very tight sequence
(g_{m})_{m}
, eventually all the thick segments
\ell_{m,i}
, for

, of the

-thick-thin decomposition of

with roundabout parameter

have length at most
\lambda
.

Proof.

Assume the contrary. Let

be the stable bound on the number of roundabouts as in Definition
6.6
. Then for every lower length bound

, there is a very tight sequence
(g_{m,n})_{m}
satisfying the condition:
for all sufficiently large
m
, in the thick-thin decomposition of
g_{m,n}
with roundabout parameter
m
,

g_{m,n}=\ell_{m,n,0}.r_{m,n,1}\ldots r_{m,n,i}.\ell_{m,n,i}\ldots\ell_{m,n,K},

some
m
-thick segment
\ell_{m,n,i}
has length at least
n
.
A word about notation here:

does not denote a level of iteration.

Fix now
n
large, and consider the ordinary
0
-thick decomposition of Proposition
4.2
, applied to

must be further decomposed, say as

\ell_{m,n,i}=L_{0}.R_{0}\ldots L_{q}.R_{q}.

By Proposition
6.3
, we have

, a universal constant, and
|L_{j}|\leq L^{\prime}
for each

|\ell_{m,n,i}|>n
we have then that
\sum_{j=0}^{q}|R_{j}|>n-K^{\prime}L^{\prime}
and so some

Now we extract a diagonal subsequence

from the

, choose first

so large that

, and then from the very tight sequence

, choose

so large that in the

-thick-thin decomposition of the

(as in the previous paragraph) with length at least

is a tight sequence with at least

was assumed to be very tight, thus realizing the maximal number of unbounded roundabouts.
∎

Summarizing:

Lemma 6.8

(Lengths in decomposition of very tight sequences)
.

There are constants
\lambda>0
and
K\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
such that the following hold. Suppose
(g_{m})
is very tight, and

g_{m}=\ell_{0,m}.r_{1,m}.\ell_{1,m}\ldots r_{K,m}.\ell_{K,m}

is the
(\delta,\zeta)
-thick-thin decomposition with roundabout parameter
m
of its

,

|\ell_{i,m}|<\lambda\text{ for all }i=0,\ldots,K,

and since
(g_{m})
is very tight,

|r_{i,m}|>m\text{ for all }i=1,\ldots,K.

That is, in a very tight sequence, eventually the terms consist of
K+1
“short” segments in the thick part (each of length at most
\lambda
), separated by precisely
K
roundabouts that are becoming unbounded at rate at least
m
. We next show that, to some extent, this structure persists along the terms

of the
{\sf X}
-ray above each

, at least when

.

6.5.
Roundabouts lift to roundabouts

Let
(g_{m})
be a very tight sequence; it has precisely
K
unbounded roundabouts, by definition. In this subsection, we show that for each iterate
n=1,2,3,\ldots
, the sequence
(g_{m}^{(n)})_{m}
also has precisely
K
unbounded roundabouts.

Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Let
\phi^{(n)},\rho^{(n)}\colon\mathcal{T}^{(n)}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{S}
be the
n
th iterate of the correspondence. Consider
m>\!\!>n
. In the remainder of this paragraph, to ease notation, when denoting paths, we temporarily drop sub- and super-scripts indicating the dependence on
m
and

(\delta,\zeta)

When we lift

\tilde{g}=\tilde{\ell}_{0}.\tilde{r}_{1}\ldots\tilde{r}_{K}.\tilde{\ell}_{K}.

Since
\phi^{(n)}
is a local isometry, and

\lambda\geq|\ell_{i}|=|\tilde{\ell}_{i}|\text{ for all }i=0\ldots K

where

-thick-thin decomposition in

For this paragraph, we re-introduce the notation giving the dependence on

\tilde{g}_{m}\subset\mathcal{T}^{(n)}

\rho^{(n)}(\tilde{g}_{m})\eqqcolon\undertilde{g}_{m}=\undertilde{\ell}_{m,0}.\undertilde{r}_{m,1}.\cdots.\undertilde{r}_{m,K}.\undertilde{\ell}_{m,K}

In the next subsection, we will explore the difference. By definition, the element

m-\frac{1}{m}\leq|g_{m}|-\frac{1}{m}\leq|g_{m}^{(n)}|\leq|\undertilde{g}_{m}.\overline{\undertilde{{\sf x}}_{m}}|\leq|g_{m}|+n\xi.

Summarizing: for fixed

\undertilde{g}_{m}.\undertilde{{\sf x}}_{m}\subset\mathcal{S}

In this paragraph, we set up some notation that we will need later. Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{K}
be the sequence of cusps surrounded by the

Our next goal is to show that each point

\mathcal{S}

Write

The second map is a contraction; let us show that near

6.6.
How thick and thin lengths change under lifting

We continue the discussion and notation of the previous subsection.

Lemma 6.9

(Lifting very tight sequences)
.

Fix an index

7.1.
Planar graphs

Definition 7.1
.

A
planar graph
is a finite graph
\Gamma=(V,E)
embedded in
S^{2}
, with

nonempty, such that
\Gamma\cap P\subset V
. Its underlying subset of
S^{2}
is denoted

\#\Gamma\coloneqq\#V+\#E
. Two planar graphs are

if there is an isotopy of
S^{2}
fixing
P
sending one to the other and inducing a bijection between their vertices and between their edges.

The definition of equivalence does not allow for any of the following: a collapse of inessential loops, coalescence of two isotopic components into a single component, or addition or deletion of vertices of valence two. We put no other restrictions on planar graphs.

Here is an example. Suppose
C
is a simple closed curve in
S^{2}\setminus P
. Choose any point of
C
, and decree it to be a vertex. This gives a planar graph
\Gamma
with one vertex and one edge, which is a loop.

We denote by

Proposition 7.2
.

For each
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the group
G
acts on the set
\text{\sc{graphs}}(P,M)
with finitely many orbits.

Proof.

We use induction on the complexity. The conclusion is clear for the base case of a graph consisting of a single vertex. The inductive step breaks down into cases according to whether we add a vertex or an edge. The only case of substance is that of adding an edge between two existing vertices. A standard application of the Alexander method
[
19
]
*§2.3 yields the result.
∎

g^{(0)}\in G
with
\Gamma^{(0)}\coloneqq g^{(0)}\Gamma_{0}\in\text{\sc{graphs}}(P,M)/G
. Once this choice is made, we will show that the rest of the sequence

For the base case, we factor

7.3.
Finite graph attractor

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 7.5

(Graph attractors)
.

Suppose
\#P=4
and
f\colon({\widehat{\mathbb{C}}},P)\to({\widehat{\mathbb{C}}},P)
is a nonexceptional post-critically finite rational map whose postcritical set lies in
P
. Then for every
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists a finite collection
\text{\sc{graphs}}(P,M,f)\subset\text{\sc{graphs}}(P,M)
of planar graphs, each of complexity at most
M
, closed under sublifting by
f
, such that, for every
M
-bounded orbit
(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\ldots)
of graphs, we have
\Gamma_{n}\in\text{\sc{graphs}}(P,M,f)
for all
n
large enough.

In particular,
\text{\sc{graphs}}(P,M,f)
naturally forms the stateset of a finite automaton, with states given by
G\coloneqq{\operatorname{PMod}}({\widehat{\mathbb{C}}},P)
-orbits, and transitions given by taking all possible sublifts.

Proof.

We denote by
F=\phi,\rho\colon\mathcal{T}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{S}
the correspondence on moduli space associated to
f
and by
\star
the fixed point corresponding to
f
.
By Proposition
7.4
, there is a finite set
\text{\sc{graphs}}(P,M)/G
and a collection of curves (Thurston maps)
{\sf X}
such that for any
M
-bounded
f
-orbit of graphs
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1},\ldots
there is an
{\sf X}
-ray
(g^{(n)})
with
g^{(n)}(\Gamma_{n})\in\text{\sc{graphs}}(P,M)/G
for each
n
.

By Corollary
5.5
, there is a finite set
A\subset G
independent of
g
and of
\Gamma_{0}
such that
g^{(n)}\in A
for all
n
sufficiently large. We put
\text{\sc{graphs}}(P,M,f)\coloneqq A^{-1}\text{\sc{graphs}}(P,M)/G
. The definitions give
\Gamma_{n}\in\text{\sc{graphs}}(P,M,f)
for all
n
sufficiently large.
∎

7.4.
Proof of Corollary
B

We recall the statement:

Corollary B
.

Assume that
\#P=4
and
f
is a non-Lattès rational map. Then there is a finite attractor for the pullback iteration on multicurves, on trees, and on spines.

For general
\#P
, we will show that in each of the settings of multicurves, trees, and spines, we can associate a
G
-invariant collection of graphs and a notion of pullback such that the graph complexity remains bounded under iteration. Upon restricting to the case
\#P=4
, the Corollary then follows from Theorem
7.5
.

Proof for multicurves.

By adding a vertex to each component, the set of isotopy classes of multicurves in
S^{2}-P
embeds into the family
\text{\sc{graphs}}(P,M)
where
M=2(\#P-3)
. The set of multicurves is
G
-invariant; with finitely many orbits. We define pullback of such graphs as follows. Given a multicurve
\Gamma
, we consider
f^{-1}(\Gamma)
, and modify it as follows: (i) delete all inessential and peripheral elements; (ii) consolidate essential components of the preimage which are isotopic in
S^{2}-P
into a single component; (iii) lift the graph structure from
\Gamma
; and finally (iv) delete all but one vertex. Defined in this way, the terms of pullback orbits have complexity at most
M
.
∎

Proof for spines.

A
spine
for
S^{2}-P
is a finite 3-valent graph in
S^{2}-P
which, as a subset of the sphere, arises as the image of a deformation retract of
S^{2}-P
; thus each complementary region in
S^{2}
is topologically a disk containing exactly one element of a
P
. From Euler’s Formula and being 3-valent, it is easy to see that the complexity of a spine is
M\coloneqq 5\#P-10
. The set of spines is
G
-invariant; thus by Proposition
7.2
, there are finitely many
G
-orbits of spines.

Lemma 7.6
.

The following properties of spines hold:

(1)

Suppose
Q\subset S^{2}
is finite, with
\#Q\geq 3
, and
q\in Q
. Then any spine
\Gamma
for
S^{2}-Q
contains a spine for
S^{2}-(Q-\{q\})
.

(2)

Suppose
P\subset Q
are finite, with
\#P\geq 2
. Then any spine
\Gamma
for
S^{2}-Q
contains a spine for
S^{2}-P
.

(3)

If
f\colon(S^{2},P)\to(S^{2},P)
is a Thurston map and
\Gamma
is a spine for
S^{2}-P
, then
f^{-1}(\Gamma)
contains a spine for
S^{2}-P
.

Proof.

For (1), let
U_{q}
be the unique face of
\Gamma
containing
q
. Since
\Gamma
is a spine, there exists
p\in Q-\{q\}
such that the face
U_{p}
containing
p
has the property that
\overline{U}_{p}\cap\overline{U}_{q}
contains an edge
e\subset\Gamma
. Since
p\neq q
, the set
\Gamma^{\prime}\coloneqq\Gamma-\{\text{interior}(e)\}
is connected. If
e
is not a loop, we declare the common endpoint of its ends to be an ordinary point, and not a vertex; we obtain a new spine
\Gamma^{\prime}
. If
e
is a loop at
v
, we further prune
\Gamma^{\prime}
by also deleting the dangling edge
e^{\prime}
joining
v
(which is now valence
1
in
\Gamma^{\prime}
) to say
w
, and finally declaring
w
to be an ordinary point, and not a vertex; we obtain a new spine
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
. Statement (2) then follows by (1) and induction. Statement (3) follows from (2) upon setting
Q\coloneqq f^{-1}(P)
.
∎

Thus for a spine
\Gamma
, we define pullback by applying Lemma
7.6
, choosing any subgraph of
f^{-1}(\Gamma)
which is again a spine.
∎

Proof for trees.

Given
P\subset S^{2}
finite with
\#P\geq 2
, an
admissible tree containing
P
is a tree containing
P
such that any vertex of valence
1
or
2
lies in
P
. The complexity of such a tree is at most
M\coloneqq 4\#P-1
. To see this: for a tree
T
, we denote by
C(T)
its complexity, and by
V_{k}(T)
the set of vertices of valence
k
. Let
T^{\prime}
be the tree obtained from
T
by deleting vertices of valence
2
. Thus
C(T)\leq C(T^{\prime})+2\#V_{2}(T)\leq C(T^{\prime})+2\#P
. An easy induction argument shows that the number of interior vertices of
T^{\prime}
is at most the number of leaves of
T^{\prime}
, yielding
\sum_{k\geq 3}V_{k}(T^{\prime})\leq\#P
. Thus
\#V(T^{\prime})=\sum_{k\geq 1}V_{k}(T^{\prime})\leq 2\#P
. Euler’s Formula gives
\#E(T^{\prime})=\#V(T^{\prime})-1
and so
C(T^{\prime})\leq 2\#P-1
. Combined with the first estimate, this yields the claimed complexity bound
M
. In particular the set of such trees is
G
-invariant, with finitely many orbits.

.

(1)

is finite, with

, and

(1) If

we set

as graph, and we are done. If

we set

as graph but decree

Thus for an admissible tree
