Robust Fine-Tuning of Deep Neural Networks with Hessian-based Generalization Guarantees
Haotian Ju, Dongyue Li, Hongyang R. ZhangNortheastern University, Boston

Abstract
We consider fine-tuning a pretrained deep neural network on a target task. We study the generalization properties of fine-tuning to understand the problem of overfitting, which has often been observed (e.g., when the target dataset is small or when the training labels are noisy). Existing generalization measures for deep networks depend on notions such as distance from the initialization (i.e., the pretrained network) of the fine-tuned model and noise stability properties of deep networks. This paper identifies a Hessian-based distance measure through PAC-Bayesian analysis, which is shown to correlate well with observed generalization gaps of fine-tuned models. Theoretically, we prove Hessian distance-based generalization bounds for fine-tuned models. We also describe an extended study of fine-tuning against label noise, where overfitting remains a critical problem. We present an algorithm and a generalization error guarantee for this algorithm under a class conditional independent noise model. Empirically, we observe that the Hessian-based distance measure can match the scale of the observed generalization gap of fine-tuned models in practice. We also test our algorithm on several image classification tasks with noisy training labels, showing gains over prior methods and decreases in the Hessian distance measure of the fine-tuned model.

Introduction

Fine-tuning a large pretrained model is a common approach to performing deep learning
as foundation models become readily available. While this approach improves upon supervised learning in various cases, overfitting has also been observed during fine-tuning.
Understanding the cause of overfitting is challenging since dissecting the issue in practice requires a precise measurement of generalization errors for deep neural networks.
In this work, we analyze the generalization error of fine-tuned deep models using PAC-Bayes analysis and data-dependent measurements based on deep net Hessian.
With this analysis, we also study the robustness of fine-tuning against label noise.

Generalization bounds using PAC-Bayesian analysis

and perturbed with an appropriately reshaped

at every layer.
Then, there exists a fixed value

that does not grow with

and

\displaystyle\ell_{\mathcal{Q}}(f_{W}(x),y)-\ell(f_{W}(x),y)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left(\big\langle\Sigma_{i},\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\big\rangle+C_{1}\left\|\Sigma_{i}\right\|_{F}^{3/2}\right).

3
Experiments

This section conducts experiments to evaluate our proposed algorithm.
We evaluate the robustness of Algorithm
1
under various noisy environments and architectures.
First, we validate that when the training labels are corrupted with class conditional label noise, our approach is robust even under up to 60% of label noise.
Second, on six weakly-supervised image classification tasks, our algorithm improves the prediction accuracy over prior approaches by 3.26% on average.
Lastly, we present a detailed analysis that validates the theory, including comparing our generalization bound with previous results and comparing the Hessian distance measure for various fine-tuning methods.
The code repository for reproducing the experiments is at
https://github.com/VirtuosoResearch/Robust-fine-tuning
.

Datasets.

We evaluate the robustness of our approach in image and text classification tasks.
For image classification, we use six domains of object classification tasks from the DomainNet dataset.

4
Conclusion

This work approached generalization for fine-tuning deep networks using a PAC-Bayesian analysis.
The analysis shows that besides distance from initialization, Hessian plays a crucial role in measuring generalization.
Our theoretical contribution involves Hessian distance-based generalization bounds for various fine-tuning methods on deep networks.
We empirically show that the Hessian distance measure accurately correlates with the generalization error of fine-tuning in practice.
Although we state our results for fine-tuned models, it is worth mentioning that the same statement holds in the supervised learning setting.
In this case, the distance vector
v_{i}
in statement (
3
) corresponds to the difference between the random initialization of the network and the final trained weight matrix.
Besides, our theoretical result suggests a principled algorithm for fine-tuning with noisy labels.
Experiments demonstrate its robustness under synthetic and real-world label noise.

We describe two questions for future work.
First, our result requires the activation mappings of the neural network to be twice-differentiable. It would be interesting to extend our result to non-smooth activation functions such as ReLU networks (with a loss function such as the cross-entropy loss). For example, in the practical usage of ReLU activation, it is conceivable that non-differentiable states rarely occur. One way is to use smoothed analysis and argue that such non-differentiable states occur with negligible probability.
Second, it would be interesting to apply Hessian to understand generalization in other black-box models.
In a follow-up paper, we apply our techniques to improve the state-of-the-art generalization bounds for graph neural networks
[
116
]
.
More broadly, we hope our work inspires further study on the interplay between second-order gradients and generalization.

Proofs

We will analyze the generalization performance of fine-tuning using PAC-Bayesian analysis.
One chooses a
prior
distribution, denoted as
\mathcal{P}
, and a
posterior
distribution, denoted as
\mathcal{Q}
.
The
perturbed
empirical loss of
f_{W}
, denoted by
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{Q}}(f_{W})
, is the average of
\ell_{\mathcal{Q}}(f_{U}(x),y)
over the training dataset.
The
perturbed
expected loss of
f_{W}
, denoted by
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{Q}}(f_{W})
, is the expectation of
{\ell_{\mathcal{Q}}(f_{W}(x),y)}
, for a sample
x
drawn from
\mathcal{D}
with class label
y
.
We will use the following PAC-Bayes bound from Theorem 2,
[
66
]
(see also Theorem 2.1,
[
106
]
).

Theorem A.1
.

Suppose the loss function
\ell(x,y)
lies in a bounded range
[0,C]
given any
x\in\mathcal{X}
with label
y
.
For any
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\delta\in(0,1]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the following holds

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{Q}}(f_{W})\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{Q}}(f_{W})+\frac{C\Big(KL(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Big)}{2\beta(1-\beta)n}.

(10)

This result provides flexibility in setting
\beta
.
Our results will set
\beta
to balance the perturbation error of
\mathcal{Q}
and the KL divergence between
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
.
We will need the KL divergence between the prior
\mathcal{P}
and the posterior
\mathcal{Q}
in the PAC-Bayesian analysis. This is stated in the following result.

Proposition A.2
.

Suppose the noise perturbation at layer
i
is drawn from a Gaussian distribution with mean zero and covariance
\Sigma_{i}
, for every
i=1,\dots,L
.
Then, the KL divergence between
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
is equal to

\displaystyle KL(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(W_{i}-\hat{W}_{i}^{(s)})^{\top}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(W_{i}-\hat{W}_{i}^{(s)}).

As a corollary, if the population covariance of the noise distribution at every layer is isotropic, i.e.,
\Sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\id
, for any
i=1,\dots,L
, then the KL divergence between
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
is equal to

\displaystyle KL(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{\|W_{i}-\hat{W}_{i}^{(s)}\|_{F}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{i}}.

Proof.

The proof is based on the definition of multivariate normal distributions.
Let
Z_{i}
be the weight matrix of every layer
i
in the posterior distribution, for
i
from
1
to
L
.
By the definition of the KL divergence between two distributions, we have

\displaystyle KL(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim\mathcal{Q}}\left[\log\Big(\frac{\mathcal{Q}(Z)}{\mathcal{P}(Z)}\Big)\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim\mathcal{Q}}\left[\log\mathcal{Q}(Z)-\log\mathcal{P}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim\mathcal{Q}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(Z_{i}-W_{i})^{\top}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(Z_{i}-W_{i})+\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(Z_{i}-\hat{W}_{i}^{(s)})^{\top}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(Z_{i}-\hat{W}_{i}^{(s)})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim\mathcal{Q}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\tr\left[{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(Z_{i}-W_{i}){\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(Z_{i}-W_{i})^{\top}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\right]-\tr\left[{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(Z_{i}-\hat{W}_{i}^{(s)}){\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(Z_{i}-\hat{W}_{i}^{(s)})^{\top}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\right]\right].

In the above equation, we recall that the expectation of
Z_{i}
is
W_{i}
.
Additionally, the population covariance of
Z_{i}
is
\Sigma_{i}
.
Therefore, after canceling out common terms, we get

\displaystyle KL(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(W_{i}-\hat{W}_{i}^{(s)})^{\top}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(W_{i}-\hat{W}_{i}^{(s)}).

The proof is complete.
∎

\hat{W_{i}}-\hat{W_{i}}^{(s)}
. By the PAC-Bayes bound of Theorem
A.1
and Proposition
A.2
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{Q}}\big(f_{\hat{W}}\big)-\frac{1}{\beta}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{Q}}\big(f_{\hat{W}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\Big(KL(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Big)}{2\beta(1-\beta)n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Big)}{2\beta(1-\beta)n}.

(24)

Combining equations (
19
), (
23
), (
24
), with probability at least
1-2\delta
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\big(f_{\hat{W}}\big)-\frac{1}{\beta}\bar{\mathcal{L}}\big(f_{\hat{W}}\big)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C_{2}\rho\sqrt{\log(C_{3}n/\delta)}}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sigma_{i}^{2}+\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}-1\Big)\rho\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sigma_{i}^{2}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\{1,\dots,k\}}\left\lvert\tr[\mathbf{H}_{i}(\ell(f_{\hat{W}}(x),y))]\right\rvert

\displaystyle+\Big(\frac{\rho}{\beta}+1\Big)\sum_{j=1}^{L}C_{1}\sigma_{i}^{3}+\frac{C(\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\log\frac{1}{\delta})}{2\beta(1-\beta)n}.

(25)

In equation (
25
), the upper bound of
\mathcal{L}\big(f_{\hat{W}}\big)-\frac{1}{\beta}\bar{\mathcal{L}}\big(f_{\hat{W}}\big)
relies on
\sigma_{i}
and
\beta
. Our goal is to select
\sigma_{i}>0
and
\beta\in(0,1)
to minimize equation (
25
).
This is achieved when:

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{2}=\sqrt{\frac{C\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}}{4\rho(1-\beta)^{2}n\cdot\max_{x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\{1,\dots,k\}}\left\lvert\tr[\mathbf{H}_{i}(\ell(f_{\hat{W}}(x),y))]\right\rvert}},

(26)

for any
1\leq i\leq L
, following the condition:

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}-1\Big)\rho\sigma_{i}^{2}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\{1,\dots,k\}}\left\lvert\tr[\mathbf{H}_{i}(\ell(f_{\hat{W}}(x),y))]\right\rvert=\frac{C}{2\beta(1-\beta)n}\frac{\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}.

Then, equation (
25
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\big(f_{\hat{W}}\big)\leq

\displaystyle\Bigg(\frac{1}{\beta}\bar{\mathcal{L}}\big(f_{\hat{W}}\big)+\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sqrt{\frac{C\rho\max_{x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\{1,\dots,k\}}\left\lvert\tr[\mathbf{H}_{i}(\ell(f_{\hat{W}}(x),y))]\right\rvert\cdot\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}}{\beta^{2}n}}\Bigg)

(27)

\displaystyle+\Bigg(\frac{C_{2}\rho\sqrt{\log(C_{3}n/\delta)}}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sigma_{i}^{2}+\frac{C}{2\beta(1-\beta)n}\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\Big(\frac{\rho}{\beta}+1\Big)C_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sigma_{i}^{3}\Bigg).

(28)

Set
\beta
as a fixed value close to 1 that does not grow with
n
and
1/\delta
.
Let
\epsilon=(1-\beta)/\beta
. We get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\big(f_{\hat{W}}\big)\leq

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)\Bigg(\bar{\mathcal{L}}\big(f_{\hat{W}}\big)+\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sqrt{\frac{C\rho\max_{x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\{1,\dots,k\}}\left\lvert\tr[\mathbf{H}_{i}(\ell(f_{\hat{W}}(x),y))]\right\rvert\cdot\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}}{n}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle+\Bigg(\frac{C_{2}\rho\sqrt{\log(C_{3}n/\delta)}}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sigma_{i}^{2}+\Big(\frac{\rho}{\beta}+1\Big)C_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sigma_{i}^{3}+\frac{C}{2\beta(1-\beta)n}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigg).

Let
\xi
be defined as follows. We have

\displaystyle\xi=\frac{C_{2}\rho\sqrt{\log(C_{3}n/\delta)}}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sigma_{i}^{2}+\Big(\frac{\rho}{\beta}+1\Big)C_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sigma_{i}^{3}+\frac{C}{2\beta(1-\beta)n}\log\frac{1}{\delta}=O\Big(n^{-3/4}\Big),

where we notice that
\sigma_{i}^{2}=O\left(n^{-1/2}\right)
because of equation (
26
).
Thus, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\big(f_{\hat{W}}\big)\leq(1+\epsilon)\bar{\mathcal{L}}\big(f_{\hat{W}}\big)+(1+\epsilon)\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sqrt{\frac{C\rho\max_{x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\{1,\dots,k\}}\left\lvert\tr[\mathbf{H}_{i}(\ell(f_{\hat{W}}(x),y))]\right\rvert\cdot\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}}{n}}+\xi.

Since
\left\|v_{i}\right\|\leq\alpha_{i}
, for any
i=1,\dots,L
, we have finished the proof.
∎

A.3
Proof of Lemma
2.4

In this section, we provide the proof of Lemma
2.4
.
We need a perturbation analysis of the Hessian as follows.
Given an
L
-layer neural network
f_{W}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}
and any input
z^{0}\in\mathcal{X}
, let
z_{W}^{i}=\phi_{i}(W_{i}z_{W}^{i-1})
be the activated output vector from layer
i
, for any
i=1,2,\dots,L
.

We show that the Hessian matrix is Lipschitz-continuous. In particular, there exists a fixed value
G
that does not grow with
n
and
\delta^{-1}
, such that the changes in the Hessian can be quantified below.

Proposition A.5
.

Suppose that Assumption
A.3
holds.
The change in the Hessian output of the loss function
\ell(f_{W}(x),y)
for
W_{i}
under perturbation
U
can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\mid_{W_{i}+U_{i},\forall 1\leq i\leq L}-\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\right\|_{F}\leq G\|U\|_{{}_{F}},

(29)

where
G
has the following equation:

\displaystyle G=\frac{3}{2}(L+1)^{2}e^{6}\kappa_{2}\kappa_{1}^{2}\kappa_{0}^{3(L+1)}\Big(\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|x\right\|\prod_{l=0}^{L}d_{l}\prod_{h=1}^{L}\left\|W_{h}\right\|_{2}\Big)^{3}\Big(\max_{1\leq i\leq L}\frac{1}{\left\|W_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{L}\frac{1}{\left\|W_{h}\right\|_{2}}\Big).

(30)

where
\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\geq 1
is the maximum over the Lipschitz constants, first-order derivative Lipschitz constants, and second-order derivative Lipschitz constants of all the activation functions and the loss function.

Provided with the Lipschitz-continuity conditions, now we are ready to state the proof of Lemma
2.4
, which shows the uniform convergence of the loss Hessian.

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

Let
B
,
\epsilon>0
,
p=\sum_{i=1}^{L}d_{i}d_{i-1}
, and let
S=\{\text{vec}(W)\in\real^{p}:\left\|\text{vec}(W)\right\|_{2}\leq B\}
. Then there exists an
\epsilon
-cover of
S
with respect to the
\ell_{2}
-norm at most
\max\Big(\big(\frac{3B}{\epsilon}\big)^{p},1\Big)
elements from Example 5.8,
[
86
]
. From Proposition
A.5
, the Hessian
\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]
is
G
-Lipschitz for all
(W+U),W\in S
and any
i=1,2,\cdots,L
. Then we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W+U}(x),y)]-\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\right\|_{F}\leq G\left\|U\right\|_{F},

where
G
is defined in equation (
30
). For parameters
\delta,\epsilon>0
, let
\mathcal{N}
be the
\epsilon
-cover of
S
with respect to the
\ell_{2}
-norm. Define the event

\displaystyle E=\Big\{\forall W\in\mathcal{N},\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x_{j}),y_{j})]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\right]\right\|_{F}\leq\delta\Big\}.

By the matrix Bernstein inequality, we have

\text{Pr}(E)\geq 1-4|\mathcal{N}|d_{i}d_{i-1}\exp(-n\delta^{2}/(2\mathcal{H}_{i}^{2})).

Next, for any
W\in S
, we can pick some
W+U\in\mathcal{N}
such that
\left\|U\right\|_{F}\leq\epsilon
. We have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W+U}(x),y)]\right]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\right]\right\|_{F}\leq G\left\|U\right\|_{F}\leq G\epsilon,\text{ and}

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W+U}(x_{j}),y_{j})]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x_{j}),y_{j})]\right\|_{F}\leq G\left\|U\right\|_{F}\leq G\epsilon.

Therefore, for any
W\in S
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x_{j}),y_{j})]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\right]\right\|_{F}\leq 2G\epsilon+\delta.

We will also set the value of
\delta
and
\epsilon
. First, set
\epsilon=\delta/(2G)
so that conditional on
E
,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x_{j}),y_{j})]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\right]\right\|_{F}\leq 2\delta.

The event
E
happens with a probability of at least:

\displaystyle 1-4|\mathcal{N}|d_{i}d_{i-1}\exp(-n\delta^{2}/(2\mathcal{H}_{i}^{2}))=1-4d_{i}d_{i-1}\exp(\log|\mathcal{N}|-n\delta^{2}/(2\mathcal{H}_{i}^{2})).

We have
\log|\mathcal{N}|\leq p\log(3B/\epsilon)=p\log(6BG/\delta)
. If we set

\delta=\sqrt{\frac{4p\mathcal{H}_{i}^{2}\log(3\tau BGn/\mathcal{H}_{i})}{n}}

so that
\log(3\tau BGn/\mathcal{H}_{i})\geq 1
(because
n\geq\frac{e\mathcal{H}_{i}}{3BG}
and
\tau\geq 1
), then we get

\displaystyle p\log(6BG/\delta)-n\delta^{2}/(2\mathcal{H}_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p\log\left({\frac{6BG\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{4p\mathcal{H}_{i}^{2}\log(3\tau BGn/\mathcal{H}_{i})}}}\right)-2p\log(3\tau BGn/\mathcal{H}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p\log\left({\frac{3BG\sqrt{n}}{\mathcal{H}_{i}\sqrt{p\log(3\tau BGn/\mathcal{H}_{i})}}}\right)-2p\log(3\tau BGn/\mathcal{H}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle p\log(3\tau BGn/\mathcal{H}_{i})-2p\log(3\tau BGn/\mathcal{H}_{i})

(
\tau\geq 1,\log(3\tau BGn/\mathcal{H}_{i})\geq 1
)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-p\log(3\tau BGn/\mathcal{H}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-p\log(e\tau).

(
3BGn/\mathcal{H}_{i}\geq e
)

Therefore, with probability greater than

1-4|\mathcal{N}|d_{i}d_{i-1}\exp(-n\delta^{2}/(2\mathcal{H}_{i}^{2}))\geq 1-4d_{i}d_{i-1}(e\tau)^{-p}\geq 1-4d_{i}d_{i-1}/(e\tau),

the following estimate holds:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x_{j}),y_{j})]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\right]\right\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{\frac{16p\mathcal{H}_{i}^{2}\log(3\tau BGn/\mathcal{H}_{i})}{n}}.

Denote
\delta^{\prime}=4d_{i}d_{i-1}/(e\tau)
,
C_{2}=4\mathcal{H}_{i}\sqrt{p}
, and
C_{3}=12d_{i}d_{i-1}BG/(e\mathcal{H}_{i})
. With probability greater than
1-\delta^{\prime}
, the final result is:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x_{j}),y_{j})]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\right]\right\|_{F}\leq C_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(C_{3}n/\delta^{\prime})}{n}}.

This completes the proof of Lemma
2.4
.
∎

\displaystyle(j-i+2)\Big(1+\frac{1}{L}\Big)^{6(j+1)}\kappa_{2}\kappa_{1}^{2}\kappa_{0}^{3j}\prod_{l=i-1}^{j+1}d_{l}^{3}\Bigg(\frac{\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|x\right\|_{2}}{\left\|W_{i}\right\|_{2}}\prod_{h=1}^{j+1}\left\|W_{h}\right\|_{2}\Bigg)^{2}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|x\right\|\prod_{h=1}^{j+1}\left\|W_{h}\right\|_{2}\sum_{h=1}^{j+1}\frac{\left\|U_{h}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|W_{h}\right\|_{2}}.

Equation (
60
) times
(d_{i}d_{i-1})^{2}
is at most

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}(j-i+2)^{2}\Big(1+\frac{1}{L}\Big)^{6(j+1)}\kappa_{2}\kappa_{1}^{2}\kappa_{0}^{3j}\prod_{l=i-1}^{j+1}d_{l}^{3}\Bigg(\frac{\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|x\right\|}{\left\|W_{i}\right\|_{2}}\prod_{h=1}^{j+1}\left\|W_{h}\right\|_{2}\Bigg)^{2}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|x\right\|\prod_{h=1}^{j+1}\left\|W_{h}\right\|_{2}\sum_{h=1}^{j+1}\frac{\left\|U_{h}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|W_{h}\right\|_{2}}.

By combining the above five cases, we have finished the proof of the induction step.
∎

Remark.

We remark that the bulk of the work in this proof is to get an explicit formula of
G
— the Lipschitz bound of the neural network Hessian. If one wants to get the order rather than its exact value, the calculation can be significantly simplified.

A.5
Proof of Lemma
2.3

Claim A.8
.

Let
f_{W}
be a feed-forward neural network with the activation function
\phi_{i}
for any
i=1,2,\cdots,L
, parameterized by the layer weight matrices
W
.
Let
U
be any matrices with the same dimension as
W
.
For any
W
and
U
, the following identity holds

\displaystyle\ell(f_{W+U}(x),y)=

\displaystyle\ell(f_{W}(x),y)+\langle U,\nabla_{W}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y))\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U),\mathbf{H}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U)\rangle+R_{2}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y)).

where
R_{2}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y)))
is a second-order error term in the Taylor’s expansion.

Proof.

The proof of Claim
A.8
follows by the fact that the activation
\phi_{i}(x)
is twice-differentiable.
From the mean value theorem, let
\eta
be a matrix that has the same dimension as
W
and
U
. There must exist an
\eta
between
W
and
U+W
so that the following equality holds:

\displaystyle R_{2}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y))=\langle{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U),\big(\mathbf{H}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\mid_{\eta}-\mathbf{H}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\big){\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U)\rangle.

This completes the proof of the claim.
∎

Based on the above claim, we prove Taylor’s expansion of noise stability.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

We take the expectation over
U
for both sides of the equation in Lemma
A.8
. The result becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}[\ell(f_{W+U}(x),y)]=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{U}\big[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)+\langle U,\nabla_{W}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y))\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U),\mathbf{H}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U)\rangle+R_{2}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y))\big].

Then, we use the notation of the distribution
\mathcal{Q}
on
\mathbb{E}_{U}[\ell(f_{W+U}(x),y)]
. We have

\displaystyle\ell_{\mathcal{Q}}(f_{W}(x),y)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]+\mathbb{E}_{U}[\langle U,\nabla_{W}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y))\rangle]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{U}[\langle{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U),\mathbf{H}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U)\rangle]+\mathbb{E}_{U}[R_{2}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y))].

Since
\mathbb{E}[U_{i}]=0
for every
i=1,2,\cdots,L
, the first-order term will be

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}[\langle U,\nabla_{W}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y))\rangle]=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}[\langle U_{i},\nabla_{W}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y))\rangle]=0.

(65)

Since we assume that
U_{i}
and
U_{j}
are independent variables for any
i\neq j
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}[\langle{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U),\mathbf{H}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U)\rangle]=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}_{U}[\langle{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U_{i}),\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(W_{i})\partial{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(W_{j})}\ell(f_{W}(x),y){\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U_{j})\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}_{U}[\langle{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U_{i}),\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U_{i})\rangle].

For each
U_{i}
, let
\Sigma_{i}
be the variance of
U_{i}
. The second-order term will be

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}[\langle{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U_{i}),\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U_{i})\rangle]=\big\langle\Sigma_{i},\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\big\rangle.

(66)

The expectation of the error term
R_{2}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y))
be the following

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}[R_{2}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y))]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}[\langle{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U){\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U)^{\top},\big(\mathbf{H}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\mid_{\eta}-\mathbf{H}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\big)\rangle]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}[\langle{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U_{i}){\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U_{i})^{\top},\big(\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\mid_{\eta_{i}}-\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\big)\rangle].

From Proposition
A.5
, let
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{9}C_{1}
equal to the following number

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}(L+1)^{2}e^{6}\kappa_{2}\kappa_{1}^{2}\kappa_{0}^{3L}\Big(\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|x\right\|_{2}\max_{1\leq i\leq L}\frac{1}{\left\|W_{i}\right\|_{2}}\prod_{h=0}^{L}d_{h}\prod_{h=1}^{L}\left\|W_{h}\right\|_{2}\Big)^{3}.

Hence, we have the following inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}\Big[\langle{\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U_{i}){\bm{\mathrm{vec}}}(U_{i})^{\top},\big(\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\mid_{\eta_{i}}-\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\big)\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}\Big[\left\|U_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}\left\|\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\mid_{\eta_{i}}-\mathbf{H}_{i}[\ell(f_{W}(x),y)]\right\|_{F}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}\Big[\left\|U_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{9}C_{1}\left\|U_{i}\right\|_{F}\Big]

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{3}}{9}C_{1}\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}\Big[\left\|U_{i}\right\|_{F}^{3}\Big].

By standard facts, we have that
(\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}\left\|U_{i}\right\|_{F}^{3})^{1/3}\leq\sqrt{3}\left\|\Sigma_{i}\right\|_{F}
.
As a result, the error term
R_{2}(\ell(f_{W}(x),y))
is smaller than

\frac{\sqrt{3}}{9}C_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{L}(\sqrt{3}\left\|\Sigma_{i}\right\|_{F})^{3}=C_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|\Sigma_{i}\right\|_{F}^{3/2}.

Thus, the proof is complete.
∎
