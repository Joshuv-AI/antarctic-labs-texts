OLKAVS: An Open Large-Scale Korean Audio-Visual Speech Dataset
Jeongkyun Park, Jung-Wook Hwang, Kwanghee Choi, Seung-Hyun Lee, Jun Hwan Ahn, Rae-Hong Park, Hyung-Min Park

Abstract
Inspired by humans comprehending speech in a multi-modal manner, various audio-visual datasets have been constructed. However, most existing datasets focus on English, induce dependencies with various prediction models during dataset preparation, and have only a small number of multi-view videos. To mitigate the limitations, we recently developed the Open Large-scale Korean Audio-Visual Speech (OLKAVS) dataset, which is the largest among publicly available audio-visual speech datasets. The dataset contains 1,150 hours of transcribed audio from 1,107 Korean speakers in a studio setup with nine different viewpoints and various noise situations. We also provide the pre-trained baseline models for two tasks, audio-visual speech recognition and lip reading. We conducted experiments based on the models to verify the effectiveness of multi-modal and multi-view training over uni-modal and frontal-view-only training. We expect the OLKAVS dataset to facilitate multi-modal research in broader areas such as Korean speech recognition, speaker recognition, pronunciation level classification, and mouth motion analysis.

To mitigate the limitations, we developed a new audio-visual speech dataset,
Open Large-scale Korean Audio-Visual Speech (OLKAVS)
dataset.
We gathered a total of 1,150 hours of audio from 1,107 Korean speakers in a studio setup with corresponding Korean transcriptions.
We recorded a speaker from 9 viewpoints: frontal, horizontal (up and down), vertical (left and right), and diagonal (upper left, upper right, lower left, and lower right).
Both scripted and spontaneous speech data are available in the dataset.
We emphasize that, to the best of our knowledge, OLKAVS is the largest dataset regarding the number of hours compared to existing audio-visual datasets, especially for the multi-view datasets.
OLKAVS can facilitate the research on a multi-modal approach to various tasks such as audio-visual speech recognition and lip reading.
To further assist future research, we make the pre-trained baseline models for both tasks publicly available.

II
Related Works

In this section, we briefly review the publicly available audio-visual speech datasets, summarized in
Table

I
.
Decades of research on lip reading led to the construction of multiple datasets, albeit the limited sample size of classical datasets, e.g., AVLetters
[
17
]
, CUAVE
[
18
]
, OuluVS
[
20
]
and GRID
[
19
]
.
All four datasets contain less than 50 speakers with limited vocabulary.
AVLetters and CUAVE datasets contain utterances of the English alphabet and 10 digits, respectively, while GRID and OuluVS datasets include short sentences but suffer from vocabulary limitations.

AVICAR
[
15
]
and OuluVS2
[
6
]
consider full sentences with more various vocabularies.
The vocabularies of both datasets are more than a thousand words.
Interestingly, AVICAR and OuluVS2 also provide multi-view videos from four and five different angles, respectively.

However, the datasets above have a fundamental limitation of containing only a predefined or limited set of words.
To develop larger datasets for word utterances and reflect real-world speech with unrestricted vocabulary, LRW proposed a data collection pipeline to label TV news footage automatically.
The pipeline-based approach has influenced numerous follow-up sentence-based audio-visual speech datasets: LRS2-BBC
[
5
,
14
]
, LRS3-TED
[
9
]
, and MV-LRS
[
11
]
.
Whereas LRS2-BBC and LRS3-TED differ only by their data source and dataset size, MV-LRS primarily focused on providing multi-view video, dividing the data into five categories based on the face angle to facilitate the multi-view evaluation.

Moreover, most of the datasets mentioned above contain English speech; only a few attempts have been made to collect non-English speech.
CI-AVSR dataset
[
13
]
covers the Cantonese spoken commands in the car environments, similar to AVICAR.
Other non-English datasets, such as LRW-1000
[
8
]
, GLips
[
10
]
, and MISP2021
[
12
]
, leverage the automated pipeline similar to LRW
[
4
]
.
LRW-1000 and MISP2021 contain Mandarin word and sentence utterances, respectively, whereas GLips used the German parliament footage as the source video.
VoxCeleb2
[
7
]
contains multilingual speech in more than 100 languages, but there are no transcriptions available in the dataset.

Finally, we discuss several audio-visual datasets containing Korean speech.

Jo et al. 2000

[
16
]
collected predefined syllables of a single speaker with 7 views.
Also,
Lee and Park 2008

[
22
]
and
Lee et al. 2019

[
23
]
collected predefined word utterances, such as digits and city names, of 56 and 9 speakers, respectively.
Unfortunately, the size of all the datasets is too minuscule to support deep learning-driven models; moreover, some are not publicly available.
Hence, we are highly motivated to build the open large-scale Korean audio-visual speech dataset, OLKAVS.

III
OLKAVS Dataset

In this section, we describe our dataset preparation process in detail.
OLKAVS contains 1,150 and 5,750 hours of audio and video in total, respectively, where we recorded five videos per single audio.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest audio-visual speech dataset, especially for multi-view datasets.

Then, randomly chosen sentences from each topic are combined to build a script with an average of 2,750 characters (5 minutes long on average).
Generated scripts are provided to the speakers when recording.

III-B
2
Audio with various noise scenarios

To simulate outdoor recording conditions inside the studio environment, we played the pre-recorded noise inside the studio.
We constructed six different noise scenarios: no noise, indoor ambiance, indoor noise (laughing and clapping), traffic noise, construction-site noise, and natural outdoor noise (flowing water and animal sounds).
All the noise audios were collected from the corresponding real-world situations.

III-C

Data Cleansing and Post-processing

III-C
1
Transcription

After recording, we transcribed all the scripted and spontaneous speech.
Even though the scripts were given to the speakers, some of the speakers mispronounced a few words or spoke pause fillers such as ‘um,’ ‘ah,’ or ‘err,’ where everything was transcribed afterward.

III-C
2
Data cleansing

We manually filtered out the problematic recordings by the following failure criteria: the entire lip or facial region is not recorded, the filming environment is too dark, the noise level is too high, or videos and the audio are not in sync.
We also trimmed every audio-visual pair to have the same length.

III-C
3
Data splits

The OLKAVS dataset provides predefined splits of train, validation, and evaluation with a ratio of 8:1:1.
The dataset is split based on speaker ID so that no speaker co-occurs on different splits.
Only the train and validation sets are made publicly available.

III-C
4
Facial landmarks

For ease of use, we also provide the bounding box coordinates of the lip and the face.
We used the face alignment estimation module from
Bulat and Tzimiropoulos 2017

[
24
]
.
Facial landmarks were extracted every 3 seconds and linearly interpolated for the frames inbetween.

III-C
5
File formats

Videos are stored in MP4 format with the FHD (1,920
\times
1,080) resolution and 10-Mbps bitrates.
Audios are encoded in WAV format with stereo channels and 48-kHz sampling rates.
Transcriptions are in JSON format, including all the details of file formats, speaker information on ID, age, and gender, whether the speaker is an expert, and the utterances’ start and end times.

We expect the OLKVAS dataset to further facilitate various research outside audio-visual speech recognition and lip reading, especially leveraging its large-scale data.

,

, but also comparable to the audio-only Korean speech dataset
[

.
Also, our pre-trained audio-visual speech recognition model can be useful when fine-tuned to other languages
[

.

As OLKAVS contains speaker identity information of 1,107 speakers, it can be used for speaker recognition tasks

.
Also, the metadata on whether the speaker is an expert can be used as a binary label for language fluency for automatic pronunciation level classification

OLKAVS is multi-view, so it contains more challenging real-world lip-reading scenarios than the front-only case.

IV
Experiments

In this section, we provide the baselines of the OLKAVS dataset for the two common tasks: audio-visual speech recognition and multi-view lip reading.
The former was conducted to analyze the effectiveness of multi-modal training over uni-modal training on changing noise levels, whereas the latter focused on utilizing multi-view data for increasing lip reading performance over frontal-view-only data.

IV-A

Data Pre-processing

Videos were cropped with the bounding boxes of the lips that are provided in the dataset.
Then, to train and evaluate sentence-wise, we split the audio-visual data based on the timestamps in the transcripts.
Also, we modified the Korean transcripts to be more prediction-friendly: removing punctuations and converting numbers into corresponding words.
We tokenized the transcripts into grapheme units of 21 vowels, 19 initial consonants, and 27 final consonants.
Refer to
[
28
]
for more details on pre-processing the Korean transcripts for speech recognition.

IV-B

Speech Recognition

IV-B
1
Architecture

As shown in
Figure

2
, we used the hybrid conformer architecture of
[
3
]
for the audio-visual model (AV-model), which is the state-of-the-art model on LRS3-TED
[
9
]
.
The architecture is the cascade of a ResNet-like convolutional network and the conformer as the encoder for both image sequences and audio waveforms with the hybrid decoder.
For the audio-only (A-model) and visual-only (V-model) variants, following
[
3
]
, we simply dropped the corresponding encoder and the fusion module.

Fig. 2
:

Hybrid conformer architecture for audio-visual speech recognition.

However, to reduce the required GPU memory size, we modified the original architecture by adopting the lip reading visual front-end module from
[
14
]
, pre-trained with several English lip reading datasets
[
4
,
11
,
14
]
, which is frozen during training.
To make the feature dimension equal, we attached a learnable linear layer between the visual front-end and encoder, converting 512-dimensional features to 256 dimensions.

Both audio and visual front-ends used the modified ResNet-18 architecture
[
29
]
.
For the first layer, 1D- and 3D-convolution layer is used for raw waveform inputs and video inputs, respectively, which is followed by the residual network.
A simple multi-layer perceptron (MLP) was used to fuse the audio and visual features.
The encoder and the decoder consisted of 12 conformer layers and 6 transformer layers, respectively, of which multi-head attentions used 8 heads and 256 feature dimensions.

IV-B
2
Training details

We used the same settings for all the models, AV-model, A-model, and V-model, following the original setting of
Ma et al. 2021

[
3
]
where they used the weighted sum of two losses, the connectionist temporal classification (CTC) loss and cross-entropy (CE) loss.
However, for simplicity, we used the simple average of two losses.
We used the Adam optimizer with the learning rate of 0.0001 with the Noam learning rate scheduler, similar to the original setting.
Due to the memory constraints, we used different batch sizes of 8, 12, and 16 for AV-model, A-model, and V-model, respectively.

To verify the effectiveness of utilizing all the views to improve the lip-reading performance

demonstrates that the frontal lip reading performance of the All-model (44.86%) was superior to that of the F-model (55.00%) with an 18% relative reduction in CERs, which reaffirmed the results of
[

that multi-view training is superior to that of the frontal-view-only.

We also compared the performance of the All-model on various views to analyze the difficulty of lip reading from each view.
Frontal lip reading in the medium-high level performed better than the other medium-high views, which differs from the results in
[
27
]
.
We suspect that frontal data being twice as large compared to the other views caused the model to be biased toward the frontal data.
Indeed, lip reading of the centered views performed worse than that of the right and left views in each of the upper and lower views, confirming the results in
[
27
]
.
Also, as the lower angle showed lip movement better, its performance was better than that of the upper angle.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a novel audio-visual speech dataset, the Open Large-scale Korean Audio-Visual Speech (OLKAVS) dataset, which is the largest publicly available audio-visual speech dataset, to the best of our knowledge.
It contains 1,150 hours of transcribed audio from 1,107 Korean speakers with nine different viewpoints and various noise situations.
We also provided pre-trained models for audio-visual speech recognition and multi-view lip reading, which serves as a future research baseline and a good starting point for fine-tuning.
Our experiments demonstrated that using both multi-modal and multi-view data during training improved upon the uni-modal and frontal-view-only data.
Finally, we emphasize that the OLKAVS dataset can be utilized for broader research areas outside of audio-visual speech recognition and lip reading.
