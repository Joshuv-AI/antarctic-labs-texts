David Cruz-Uribe, OFS, Jacob Glidewell

Abstract
. Motivated by the limitations of the traditional definitions of the Riemann-Stieltjes and Darboux-Stieltjes integrals, we introduce a generalized Darboux-Stieltjes integral that is equivalent to an earlier generalization by Ross [ 19 ] . Our definition builds upon an approach to the Darboux-Stieltjes integral recently introduced by the first author and Convertito [ 5 ] . We show that our definition agrees with all previous definitions, but that the class of integrable functions is much larger. We develop all the analogs of the classic results for the Riemann integral, and rectify the problems inherent in the definition of the Darboux-Stieltjes integral in [ 5 ] . In particular, we show the Bounded Convergence Theorem holds for our definition and that it agrees with the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral.

1.
Introduction

The Stieltjes integral plays an important role in analysis and in applications. However, since its introduction it has been recognized that its original definition had serious drawbacks, and there have been several attempts to improve it, none completely successful. The goal of this paper is to introduce a new definition which we believe overcomes the drawbacks of previous definitions. To support this claim and to provide context for our results, we will first review the history of the Stieltjes integal. This review is not intended to be comprehensive, but should suffice to illustrate our point. Hereafter,
B[a,b]
will be the set of bounded functions on the interval
[a,b]
,
I[a,b]
, will be increasing functions, and
BV[a,b]
will be functions of bounded variation.

Stieltjes
[
22
]
first introduced the Riemann-Stieltjes integral by generalizing the definition of the Riemann integral.

Definition 1.1
.

Given
f\in B[a,b]
,
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
, a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
of
[a,b]
and sample points
x_{i}^{*}\in[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
, we form a Riemann-Stieltjes sum

\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i}^{*})[\alpha(x_{i})-\alpha(x_{i-1})].

If there exists
A
such that for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for every Riemann-Stieltjes sum with
|\mathcal{P}|<\delta
,

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i}^{*})[\alpha(x_{i})-\alpha(x_{i-1})]-A\right|<\epsilon,

then we define the Riemann-Stieltjes integral of
f
with respect to
\alpha
by

(RS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=A.

Stieltjes introduced this integral, hereafter the RS-integral, to study continued fractions. Later, M. Riesz
[
17
]
showed the importance of this definition by using the RS-integral to characterize the space of linear functionals on
C[a,b]
, the space of continuous functions on
[a,b]
. (See also F. Riesz and Sz.-Nagy
[
16
]
.) Both Stieltjes and Riesz were primarily interested in this integral when the integrand
f
was a continuous function.

Straightforward examples (see
[
5
, Example 5.30]
) show that the RS-integral only exists if
f
and
\alpha
have no common points of discontinuity. To overcome this weakness and widen the class of functions for which a Stieltjes-type integral is defined, Pollard
[
14
]
gave two equivalent definitions: the Pollard-Stieltjes integral (PS-integral) and what is now referred to as the Darboux-Stieltjes integral (DS-integral). The PS-integral changes Definition
1.1
by replacing the convergence of the Riemann-Stieltjes sums (referred to as "norm convergence" in
[
8
]
) with a weaker form of convergence (referred to as "
\sigma
-convergence" in
[
8
]
). More precisely, for each
\epsilon>0
, it only considers the Riemann-Stieltjes sums on the refinements of a specific partition that depends on
\epsilon
,
\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}
, instead of all partitions with
|\mathcal{P}|<\delta
. (See Definition
9.10
for more details.)

The DS-integral generalizes the definition of the classical Darboux integral (often mistakenly referred to as the Riemann integral). Pollard only stated it for
\alpha
increasing; however, it can be extended to all
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
using linearity and the Jordan decomposition theorem.

Definition 1.2
.

Given
f\in B[a,b]
,
\alpha\in I[a,b]
, and a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, define

\overline{M}_{i}=\sup\{f(x)\;:x\in[x_{i-1},x_{i}]\},\quad\overline{m}_{i}=\inf\{f(x)\;:x\in[x_{i-1},x_{i}]\},

and form the upper and lower sums

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}^{e}(f,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{M}_{i}[\alpha(x_{i})-\alpha(x_{i-1})],

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}^{e}(f,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{m}_{i}[\alpha(x_{i})-\alpha(x_{i-1})].

If
\inf U_{\alpha}^{e}(f,\mathcal{P})=\sup L_{\alpha}^{e}(f,\mathcal{P})=A
, where the infimum and supremum are taken over all partitions of
[a,b]
, then we define the Darboux-Stieltjes integral of
f
with respect to
\alpha
by

(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=A.

For
\alpha\in I[a,b]
, Pollard showed the PS-integral and the DS-integral are equivalent and agree with the RS-integral whenever the latter exists. However, both of these integrals are more general than the RS-integral: there exist functions which are DS-integrable but whose RS-integrals do not exist since
f
and
\alpha
have common discontinuities. The correct necessary condition in this case (see
[
13
, Theorem 4.13]
) is that the DS-integral exists if at each common discontinuity
x\in[a,b]
, at least one of
f
or
\alpha
is right continuous and at least one is left continuous at
x
.

Several other versions of the Stieltjes integral have appeared in the literature. Hildebrandt
[
8
]
and Dushnik
[
6
]
studied generalizations of the PS-integral. Pollard gave an “interior" version of the DS-integral where
\overline{M}_{i}
and
\overline{m}_{i}
are replaced with
M_{i}
and
m_{i}
, the supremum and infimum of
f(x)
when
x\in(x_{i-1},x_{i})
. To distinguish this from Definition
1.2
we will sometimes refer to that one as the "exterior" DS-integral. (This explains the superscript "
e
" that appears above.) An equivalent version of the interior DS-integral was independently discovered by Convertito and the first author and developed in
[
5
]
. It is equivalent to the exterior DS-integral wherever the the latter is defined, but is defined on a larger collection of functions: in
[
5
, Theorem 5.19]
they showed that a necessary condition for the integral to exist is that at each common discontinuity
x\in[a,b]
,
\alpha
is right continuous or
f(x+)
exists, and
\alpha
is left continuous or
f(x-)
exists.

The RS, DS, and PS-integrals were the definitions used for the Stieltjes integral in many of the standard analysis books in the second half of the twentieth century. The RS-integral was used by Taylor and Mann
[
23
]
. Apostol
[
1
]
used the PS-integral and showed the equivalence of the DS-integral as theorem. He noted in an exercise that the RS-integral is not equivalent. Bartle
[
2
]
also used the PS-integral and gave the RS and DS-integrals as exercises. From a pedagogic perspective, we note that neither author explains the rationale for using
\sigma
-convergence in their definition. Rudin
[
21
]
used the DS-integral, as did Burkhill and Burkhill
[
3
]
, and Krantz
[
11
]
. Protter and Morrey
[
15
]
and Wheeden and Zygmund
[
24
]
defined both the RS and DS-integrals and highlighted the fact that they are not equivalent.

Ross
[
19
,
18
]
, motivated by the restrictive necessary conditions for the RS and DS-integrals to exist, proposed a modified version of the DS-integral.

Definition 1.3
.

Given
f\in B[a,b]
,
\alpha\in I[a,b]
, and a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, define

M_{i}=\sup\{f(x)\;:x\in(x_{i-1},x_{i})\},\quad m_{i}=\inf\{f(x)\;:x\in(x_{i-1},x_{i})\},

and

J_{\alpha}(f,\mathcal{P})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}f(x_{i})[\alpha(x_{i}+)-\alpha(x_{i}-)].

Form the upper and lower sums

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}^{r}(f,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=J_{\alpha}(f,\mathcal{P})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}[\alpha(x_{i}-)-\alpha(x_{i-1}+)],

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}^{r}(f,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=J_{\alpha}(f,\mathcal{P})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}[\alpha(x_{i}-)-\alpha(x_{i-1}+)].

If
\inf U_{\alpha}^{r}(f,\mathcal{P})=\sup L_{\alpha}^{r}(f,\mathcal{P})=A
, where the infimum and supremum are taken over all partitions of
[a,b]
, then we define the Ross-Darboux-Stieljtes (RDS) integral of
f
with respect to
\alpha
by

(RDS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=A.

Ross
[
19
]
also gave an equivalent definition in terms of a modified RS-integral. He loosely motivated the additional term
J_{\alpha}
used to define the upper and lower sums by suggesting that it allows individual points to be assigned a positive "weight." It is clear from other comments in
[
18
]
that he was also motivated by the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral.

Remark 1.4
.

Another variant of the RS-integral was proposed by Ter Horst
[
9
]
which is a generalization of the definition given by Ross. His definition is a hybrid between the classical Stieltjes integrals and the more complex definition of the Lebesgue integral. We will not discuss it in detail in this paper.

As noted above, in
[
5
]
the authors developed the interior DS-integral using an equivalent definition in terms of approximation by step functions. (See Definition
2.8
below.) Like Ross, they wanted to weaken the restrictive necessary conditions associated with the standard definitions. They were also motivated by pedagogical concerns: they felt that their definition was intuitive and also created a bridge between classical undergraduate analysis and graduate analysis and measure theory. However, as they pointed out in their introduction, there are two significant problems with the interior DS-integral. First, the Bounded Convergence Theorem (the analog of the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem for the DS-integral) does not hold for this definition of the Stieltjes integral unless
\alpha
is continuous. See
[
5
, Example 4.49 and Theorem 4.50]
. Second, and perhaps more problematic from an advanced perspective, this definition does not always agree with the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral when
f
and
\alpha
have a common discontinuity.

The goal of this paper is to address the problems with the interior DS-integral as defined in
[
5
]
. We give a definition which is equivalent to Definition
1.3
but which adopts the perspective taken in
[
5
]
. In particular, we draw a more explicit connection to the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral. In the definitions of the RS, PS and DS-integrals, as well as in Definition
2.6
below, the "
\alpha
-length" of a partition interval
[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
is given by
\alpha(x_{i})-\alpha(x_{i-1})
. The problem with this, as Ross correctly noted, is that the Stieltjes integral should give weight to individual points in the partition if they correspond to jump discontinuities of
\alpha
. To accomplish this, we will replace this definition of
\alpha
-length with the following: given an open interval
I=(c,d)
, we define
\alpha(I)=\alpha(d-)-\alpha(c+)
, where
x\pm
denotes the right and left-sided limits at
x
. We also define the
\alpha
-length of a single point
x
by
\alpha(\{x\})=\alpha(x+)-\alpha(x-)
. This modest change lets us correctly capture all the behavior of the Stieltjes integral when
f
and
\alpha
have common discontinuities. This notion of
\alpha
-length is implicit in the definition of
J_{\alpha}
in Definition
1.3
; it also corresponds to the premeasure used to define the Lebesgue-Stieltjes measure. (See
[
4
, Section 4.1]
.)

Given our definition, we show that all of the standard properties of the Stieltjes integral can be easily derived. These results can be thought of as extending and complementing the development of the interior DS-integral in
[
5
]
and the development of the RDS-integral in
[
19
]
. We then prove some more advanced theorems which show that in some sense the RDS-integral is the right definition to use. We completely characterize the functions which are RDS-integrable by proving the precise analogue of the Lebesgue criterion for Riemann-Darboux integrability.

Theorem 1.5

(RDS-Lebesgue Criterion)
.

Given
f\in B[a,b]
and
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if the subset of
[a,b]
where
f
is discontinuous has
\alpha
-measure
0
.

As an immediate consequence of this theorem we have that if
f
and
\alpha
are functions of bounded variation, then the RDS-integral of
f
with respect to
\alpha
exists.
We also prove that the Bounded Convergence theorem holds for the RDS-integral.

Theorem 1.6

(RDS Bounded Convergence Theorem)
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and a uniformly bounded sequence of functions
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
on
[a,b]
, if

•

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{n}\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
,

•

f_{n}\to f
pointwise as
n\to\infty
,

•

f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
,

then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Finally, we prove that the RDS-integral agrees with the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral whenever the former is defined.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we give some preliminary definitions and sketch the approach in
[
5
]
to the Darboux-Stieltjes integral. This will serve as a foundation for our definition of the RDS-integral, which we give in Sections
3
and
4
. In Section
4
we also prove some basic properties of the RDS-integral, including the RDS criterion, an analogue of the Darboux criterion for integrability. Finally, we give sufficient conditions, in terms of continuity, for the RDS and DS-integrals to agree. In Section
5
, we use the RDS criterion to prove linearity, additivity, monotonicity in the integrand and linearity of the integrator. In Section
6
, we prove two convergence theorems: uniform convergence in the integrand and BV-norm convergence in the integrator. In Section
7
we consider necessary and sufficient conditions for RDS-integrability and prove the RDS-Lebesgue criterion. As an application we prove a generalization of integration by parts for the RDS-integral. In Section
8
we prove the Bounded Convergence Theorem. Finally, in Section
9
, we compare various definitions of the Stieltjes integral. We first give a characterization the functions whose RDS and DS-integrals agree. We then give three alternative definitions of a generalized Riemann-Stieltjes integral and discuss their relationship with the RDS-integral. Finally, we prove that the RDS-integral and the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral agree.

Throughout, our notation will follow that used in
[
5
]
; similarly, in our proofs we will draw upon a number of results proved in this work. In Sections
3
–
6
many of our proofs are very similar to proofs of the corresponding result for the Darboux or DS-integrals, and we have tried to strike a balance between being self-contained and repeating arguments that can be found elsewhere. The results in Sections
7
–
9
are (with one exception) all new, and we give complete proofs.

2.
The Darboux-Stieltjes Integral

In this section we briefly state the definition of the interior Darboux-Stieltjes integral as defined in
[
5
, Chapter 4]
. This will help to motivate the definition of the RDS-integral that we will give in Section
3
below. As part of of this we define functions of bounded variation; additional properties of functions of bounded variation will be called upon throughout the text, and we refer to
[
5
, Chapter 3]
for complete information.

Fundamental to all of our definitions is the notion of a partition of an interval.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a closed interval
[a,b]
, a partition of
[a,b]
is a finite set of points
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
such that

a=x_{0}<x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots<x_{n-1}<x_{n}=b.

Given
\mathcal{P}
, define the disjoint partition intervals
I_{i}=(x_{i-1},x_{i})
for each
1\leq i\leq n
. The mesh size
|\mathcal{P}|
of a partition is the length of the longest partition interval:

|\mathcal{P}|=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\{x_{i}-x_{i-1}\}.

Definition 2.2
.

Given partitions
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
and
\mathcal{Q}=\{y_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m}
of the interval
[a,b]
, we say
\mathcal{Q}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{P}
if
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{Q}
. We say
\mathcal{R}
is a common refinement of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
if
\mathcal{R}
is a refinement of both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
.

We will use the above notation throughout. Moreover, given a second partition
\mathcal{Q}=\{y_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m}
, we will use
J_{j}=(y_{j-1},y_{j})
for each
1\leq j\leq m
for the partition intervals of
\mathcal{Q}
.

We can now define the bounded variation of a function.

Definition 2.3
.

Given a function
f\in B[a,b]
and a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
[a,b]
, define the variation of
f
on
\mathcal{P}
by

V(f,\mathcal{P})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}|f(x_{i})-f(x_{i-1})|.

We say that
f
is of bounded variation, and write
f\in BV[a,b]
, if

V(f,[a,b])=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}V(f,\mathcal{P})<\infty,

where the supremum is taken over all partitions
\mathcal{P}
of
[a,b]
.

For the definition of the DS-integral, we recall the Jordan decomposition theorem: if
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
, then there exist functions
\alpha^{+},\,\alpha^{-}\in I[a,b]
such that
\alpha=\alpha^{+}-\alpha^{-}
.

The definition of the DS-integral occurs in two stages. First, we define it for step functions. The integrals of step functions will take the place of the upper and lower Darboux sums in the traditional definition, e.g., Definition
1.2
.

Definition 2.4
.

A function
f\in B[a,b]
is a step function if there exists a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
[a,b]
and real numbers
\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that
f(x)=c_{i}
if
x\in I_{i},1\leq i\leq n
. The function f is said to be a step function with respect to the partition
\mathcal{P}
.

Definition 2.5
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and an open interval
I=(c,d)\subset[a,b]
, we define the (classical)
\alpha
-length of
I
to be
\alpha(I)=\alpha(d)-\alpha(c)
.

We refer to this as the classical
\alpha
-length because of the role it played historically in the definition of the Stieltjes integral. Below in Definition
3.1
we will redefine it to correspond to the premeasure used to define the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral.

Definition 2.6
.

Given
f\in S[a,b]
defined with respect to a partition
\mathcal{P}
, suppose
f(x)=c_{i}
for
x\in I_{i}
. Define the Stieltjes integral with respect to
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
by

(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\alpha(I_{i}).

As the second step, we define the DS-integral of any bounded function. To do so, we define the collections of step functions that bracket
f
.

Definition 2.7
.

Given
f\in B[a,b]
, define the sets

\displaystyle S_{U}(f,[a,b])

\displaystyle=\{u\in S[a,b]\;:\;f(x)\leq u(x),x\in[a,b]\},

\displaystyle S_{L}(f,[a,b])

\displaystyle=\{v\in S[a,b]\;:\;f(x)\geq v(x),x\in[a,b]\}.

We say
u,\,v\in S[a,b]
bracket
f
if
u\in S_{U}(f,[a,b])
and
v\in S_{L}(f,[a,b]).

Definition 2.8
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
, define

\displaystyle L^{i}_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=\sup\left\{\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha\;:\;v\in S_{L}(f,[a,b])\right\},

\displaystyle U^{i}_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=\inf\left\{\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha\;:\;u\in S_{U}(f,[a,b])\right\}.

If
L^{i}_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=U^{i}_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])
, then we say
f
is (interior) Darboux-Stieltjes integrable with respect to
\alpha
and write

(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha

as their common value.
Moreover, for
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
, if there exists a decomposition
\alpha=\alpha^{+}-\alpha^{-}
where
f\in DS_{\alpha^{+}}[a,b]
and
f\in DS_{\alpha^{-}}[a,b]
, then we define
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{+}-(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{-}.

We denote the set of functions which are Darboux-Stieltjes integrable with respect to
\alpha
as
DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

The Darboux-Stieltjes integral is well-defined; in particular, Definition
2.8
applied to a step function agrees with Definition
2.6
. Moreover, this definition is equivalent to the definition of the interior DS-integral described in Section
1
: details are left to the interested reader. See the proof of the corresponding result for the RDS-integral, Corollary
4.8
.

3.
The Ross-Darboux-Stieltjes Integral for Step functions

We now turn to the definition of the RDS-integral. Following the pattern of the definition of the DS-integral described in Section
2
, in this section we give a new definition of
\alpha
-length and define the RDS-integral of step functions. We will define the integral in general, and prove some of its basic properties, in Section
4
.

We first give our revised definition of
\alpha
-length, which takes more careful account of the jump discontinuities of the integrator
\alpha
.

Definition 3.1
.

Define the
\alpha
-length of an open interval
I=(c,d)
as

\mu_{\alpha}(I)=\alpha(d-)-\alpha(c+),

of a closed interval
\overline{I}=[c,d]
as

\mu_{\alpha}(\;\overline{I}\;)=\alpha(d+)-\alpha(c-),

and of a singleton set
\{c\}
as

\mu_{\alpha}(\{c\})=\alpha(c+)-\alpha(c-).

We also extend this to half-open intervals

\mu_{\alpha}((c,d])=\alpha(d+)-\alpha(c+)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{\alpha}([c,d))=\alpha(d-)-\alpha(c-).

Clearly,
\mu_{\alpha}
is finitely additive. Moreover, if
\alpha
is increasing,
\mu_{\alpha}
is nonnegative.

Remark 3.2
.

In dealing with a function
\alpha
defined on a closed interval
[a,b]
, we adopt the following conventions:
\alpha(a-)=\alpha(a)
and
\alpha(b+)=\alpha(b)
. This convention will be important in the proving the additivity of the RDS-integral. See the proofs of Theorems
3.9
and
5.4
.

We now define the integral of a step function. Notice the definition has two terms: the first is analogous to Ross’s jump term
J_{\alpha}(f,\mathcal{P})
in Definition
1.3
, and the second is the classical step function integral as in Definition
2.6
.

Definition 3.3
.

Given
f\in S[a,b]
defined with respect to a partition
\mathcal{P}
, suppose
f(x)=c_{i}
for
x\in I_{i}
. Define the Ross-Stieltjes integral with respect to
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
by

(\mathcal{P})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\sum_{i=0}^{n}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i}).

Clearly, a step function can be defined with respect to multiple paritions: for instance, a constant function is a step function with respect to any partition. Therefore, we have to show our definition is well-defined, i.e., that it is independent of the chosen partition.

Proposition 3.4
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
, if
f\in S[a,b]
is defined with respect to both partitions
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, then

(\mathcal{P})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(\mathcal{Q})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

First, suppose
\mathcal{Q}=\{y_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
with only one additional point. That is,
m=n+1
.
Note there exists indices
1\leq\ell\leq n
,
1\leq k\leq n
such that

y_{k-1}=x_{\ell-1}<y_{k}<y_{k+1}=x_{\ell}.

Hence,
f(y_{k})=c_{\ell}=d_{k}=d_{k+1}
. Then,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{Q})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}f(y_{j})\mu_{\alpha}(\{y_{j}\})+\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}d_{j}\mu_{\alpha}(J_{j})

\displaystyle\qquad=f(y_{k})\mu_{\alpha}(\{y_{k}\})+\sum_{i=0}^{n}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+d_{k}\mu_{\alpha}(J_{k})+d_{k+1}\mu_{\alpha}(J_{k+1})-c_{\ell}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{\ell})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad=f(y_{k})\mu_{\alpha}(\{y_{k}\})+\sum_{i=0}^{n}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+c_{\ell}[\alpha(y_{k}-)-\alpha(y_{k-1}+)+\alpha(y_{k+1}-)-\alpha(y_{k}+)-\alpha(x_{\ell}-)+\alpha(x_{\ell-1}+)]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad=f(y_{k})\mu_{\alpha}(\{y_{k}\})+c_{\ell}[\alpha(y_{k}-)-\alpha(y_{k}+)]+\sum_{i=0}^{n}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad=f(y_{k})\mu_{\alpha}(\{y_{k}\})-f(y_{k})\mu_{\alpha}(\{y_{k}\})+\sum_{i=0}^{n}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=0}^{n}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad=(\mathcal{P})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Next, assume
\mathcal{Q}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{P}
. Thus, we can write
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P}\cup\{t_{1},\dots,t_{r}\}
. Define
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\mathcal{P}_{0}\cup\{t_{1}\},\mathcal{P}_{2}=\mathcal{P}_{1}\cup\{t_{2}\},\dots,\mathcal{P}_{r}=\mathcal{P}_{r-1}\cup\{t_{r}\}=\mathcal{Q}
. From the previous case, for each
0\leq i\leq r-1
,

(\mathcal{P}_{i})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(\mathcal{P}_{i+1})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

By combining these equalities, we have

(\mathcal{P})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(\mathcal{P}_{0})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(\mathcal{P}_{r})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(\mathcal{Q})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Finally, we prove the general case. Let
\mathcal{R}
be a common refinement of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
. Thus, by the previous case,

(\mathcal{P})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(\mathcal{R})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(\mathcal{Q})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

∎

Given Proposition
3.4
, we may drop the indication of the partition and simply write

(S)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha

for the RDS integral of a step function. In fact, for the rest of this section, for brevity we will drop the "
(S)
" as well. We will use this notation again in Section
4
.

The canonical example of a step function is the Heaviside function; we will use this function repeatedly throughout this paper.

Example 3.5
.

Given
c\in\mathbb{R}
, let

H_{c}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x<0,\\
c,&x=0,\\
1,&x>0.\end{cases}

Then, for every
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle(S)\int_{-1}^{1}H_{a}(x)\,dH_{b}=a.

However,

(DS)\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}H_{a}(x)\,dH_{b}=1-b.

Remark 3.6
.

In
[
5
]
, the right continuous function
H_{1}
is referred to as "the" Heaviside function, and the left continuous function
H_{0}
is referred to as the "Jeaviside" function.

Proof.

We use the partition
\mathcal{P}=\{-1,0,1\}
. Note
H_{c}
is a step function with respect to this partition. Now,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}H_{a}(x)\,dH_{b}

\displaystyle=H_{a}(-1)\mu_{H_{b}}(\{-1\})+H_{a}(0)\mu_{H_{b}}(\{0\})+H_{a}(1)\mu_{H_{b}}(\{1\})

\displaystyle\quad+(0)\mu_{H_{b}}((-1,0))+(1)\mu_{H_{b}}((0,1))

\displaystyle=(0)(0)+(a)(1)+(1)(0)+(0)(0)+(1)(0)

\displaystyle=a.

However,

\displaystyle(DS)\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}H_{a}(x)\,dH_{b}

\displaystyle=(0)H_{b}((-1,0))+(1)H_{b}((0,1))

\displaystyle=(0)(b-0)+(1)(1-b)

\displaystyle=1-b.

∎

In Example
3.5
,
a
is the desired value: since
H_{b}
is flat except at zero where it jumps, the integral should only "see"
H_{a}
at zero and be equal to its value there. Moreover, this agrees with the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral, since this is what is gotten by integrating the function
H_{a}
against a point mass at the origin (modeled by integrating with respect to
H_{b})
. The DS-integral, however, only correctly models integration against a point mass when the integrand is continuous: see
[
5
, Lemma 5.6]
.

Another application of Definition
3.3
we will continually use is the integral of a constant.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be the constant function
f(x)=c
for every
x\in[a,b]
. Then

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=c\mu_{\alpha}([a,b]).

Proof.

Note that
f
is a step function with respect to the trivial partition
\{a,b\}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=f(a)\mu_{\alpha}(\{a\})+f(b)\mu_{\alpha}(b)+c\mu_{\alpha}((a,b))

\displaystyle=c(\alpha(a+)-\alpha(a-)+\alpha(b+)-\alpha(b-)+\alpha(b-)-\alpha(a+))

\displaystyle=c(\alpha(b+)-\alpha(a-))

\displaystyle=c\mu_{\alpha}([a,b]).

∎

We now show that the usual properties of the Stieltjes integral hold: linearity, additivity, monotonicity, and linearity of integrator. The proofs of these results are very similar to the proofs for the DS-integral, but with some differences. To illustrate this we give the proofs of the first two results, and refer to
[
5
]
for the others.

Theorem 3.8
.

Given
f,g\in S[a,b]
, for any
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{a}^{b}[r_{1}f(x)+r_{2}g(x)]\,d\alpha=r_{1}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha+r_{2}\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

We argue as in
[
5
, Theorem 1.18, Theorem 4.7]
.
Given step functions
f,g
, by passing to a common refinement, we may assume both are defined with respect to the partition
\mathcal{R}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
where
f(x)=c_{i},\;g(x)=d_{i}
for
x\in I_{i}
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}[r_{1}f(x)+r_{2}g(x)]\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}[r_{1}f(x_{i})+r_{2}g(x_{i})]\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}[r_{1}c_{i}+r_{2}d_{i}]\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle=r_{1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+r_{2}\sum_{i=0}^{n}g(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})

\displaystyle\quad+r_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})+r_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle=r_{1}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha+r_{2}\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\,d\alpha.

∎

Theorem 3.9
.

Given
f\in S[a,b]
, for any
m\in(a,b)
,

\int_{a}^{m}f(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{m}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

The corresponding result for the DS-integral is proved in
[
5
, Theorem 1.18, Theorem 4.7]
but there are some differences in the proof for the RDS-integral.

Proof.

By passing to a refinement, suppose
f
is a step function on
[a,b]
with respect to a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
which contains
m
, so there exists
i_{0}
where
x_{i_{0}}=m
. Then,
f
is a step function on
[a,m]
with respect to the partition
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{i_{0}}
and on
[m,b]
with respect to the partition
\{x_{i}\}_{i=i_{0}}^{n}
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{i_{0}-1}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+f(m)[\alpha(m+)-\alpha(m-)]+\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{n}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{i_{0}}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})+\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{n}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{i_{0}-1}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+f(m)[\alpha(m)-\alpha(m-)]+\sum_{i=1}^{i_{0}}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle\quad+f(m)[\alpha(m+)-\alpha(m)]+\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{n}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{n}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i}).

Note that on
[a,m]
,
\alpha(m+)=\alpha(m)
and similarly on
[m,b]
,
\alpha(m-)=\alpha(m)
. Hence,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{m}f(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{m}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

∎

Theorem 3.10
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
and
f,g\in S[a,b]
, if for every
x\in[a,b]
,
f(x)\geq g(x)
, then

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha\geq\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

The proof is the same as for the DS-integral: see
[
5
, Theorem 1.18, Theorem 4.7]
.
∎

Theorem 3.11
.

Given
\alpha,\beta\in BV[a,b]
, let
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and define
\gamma=r_{1}\alpha+r_{2}\beta\in BV[a,b]
. Then, for every
f\in S[a,b]
,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\gamma=r_{1}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha+r_{2}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta.

Proof.

The proof is the same as for the DS-integral: see
[
5
, Theorem 4.10]
.
∎

4.
The Ross-Darboux-Stieltjes Integral

In this section we define the RDS-integral for an arbitrary bounded function
f
and prove some basic properties of the integral. Additional properties, proved using the so-called RDS criterion, Theorem
4.5
, will be given in Section
5
. We follow the approach in
[
5
]
as outlined in Section
2
and approximate the integral of
f
by the integral of step functions that bracket it. For the next definition recall
S_{U}
and
S_{L}
, given in Definition
2.7
.

Definition 4.1
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
, define

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=\sup\left\{(S)\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha\;:\;v\in S_{L}(f,[a,b])\right\},

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=\inf\left\{(S)\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha\;:\;u\in S_{U}(f,[a,b])\right\}.

If
L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])
, then we say
f
is Ross-Darboux-Stieltjes integrable with respect to
\alpha
and we denote their common value by

(RDS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Moreover, for
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
, if there exists a decomposition
\alpha=\alpha^{+}-\alpha^{-}
with
\alpha^{\pm}\in I[a,b]
where
f\in RDS_{\alpha^{+}}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{\alpha^{-}}[a,b]
, then we define
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

(RDS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(RDS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{+}-(RDS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{-}.

We denote the set of functions which are Ross-Darboux-Stieltjes integrable with respect to
\alpha
as
RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

For brevity, unless we need to work with different definitions of the integral, hereafter we will omit the notation "
(RDS)
" and simply write
\int_{a}^{b}f\,d\alpha
.

To work with this definition, we need to establish three things. First, we have to show that it is well-defined in the sense that all of the objects in the definition exist.

Proposition 4.2
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
,
L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])
and
U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])
always exist and
L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])\leq U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])
.

The same questions needed to be resolved for the DS-integral as defined above. This is done in the discussion after
[
5
, Definition 4.12]
. We include the short proof for completeness.

Proof.

Fix
f\in B[a,b]
. Then, there exists
m,M
such that
m\leq f(x)\leq M
for all
x\in[a,b].
Hence,
u_{M}(x)=M
is in
S_{U}(f,[a,b])
and
v_{m}(x)=m
is in
S_{L}(f,[a,b])
, so these sets are non-empty. Moreover, fix
u\in S_{U}(f,[a,b])
and
v\in S_{L}(f,[a,b])
. Since
v(x)\leq f(x)\leq u(x)
for all
x\in[a,b]
, we have by Theorem
3.10
,

(S)\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha\leq(S)\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha.

Thus, the set of integrals of step functions in
S_{U}(f,[a,b])
is bounded below by
(S)\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha
so the infimum
U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])
exists and

(S)\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha\leq U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b]).

From here, we see the set of
\alpha
-integrals of step functions in
S_{L}(f,[a,b])
is bounded above by
U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])
so the supremum
L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])
exists as well and

L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])\leq U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b]).

∎

Second, we must show that given a step function, the integrals defined by Definitions
3.3
and
4.1
agree. Again a similar question is addressed after
[
5
, Definition 4.12]
for the DS-integral.

Proposition 4.3
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f\in S[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

(S)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

First, suppose
\alpha\in I[a,b]
. Fix
f\in S[a,b]
. Then we have that
f\in S_{L}(f,[a,b])
and
f\in S_{U}(f,[a,b])
. Hence, by Proposition
4.2
,

(S)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha\leq L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])\leq U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])\leq(S)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Therefore,

L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=(S)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha

as desired.

Now, suppose
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
. Then there exists a decomposition
\alpha=\alpha^{+}-\alpha^{-}
. By the previous case,
f\in RDS_{\alpha^{+}}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{\alpha^{-}}[a,b]
. Hence,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and by Theorem
3.11
,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{+}-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{-}\\
=(S)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{+}-(S)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{-}=(S)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

∎

Remark 4.4
.

Given Proposition
4.3
, hereafter we will use either definition when computing the integral of a step function.

Third, we need to show that given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
the definition of the RDS-integral is independent of the Jordan decomposition of
\alpha
used. This requires more work; the final proof is in Theorem
4.12
below. To prove this result we need some preliminary results. The first is the following characterization of RDS-integrability. This corresponds to the Darboux criterion for the Darboux integral and the corresponding result for the DS-integral (see
[
5
, Theorems 1.27, 4.17]
) and we will refer to it as the Ross-Darboux-Stieltjes criterion or RDS criterion. The short proof is nearly identical to the proofs of these results; we include the details for completeness.

Theorem 4.5
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
u,\,v\in S[a,b]
which bracket
f
, that is,
v(x)\leq f(x)\leq u(x)
for each
x\in[a,b]
, and

\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha<\epsilon.

Proof.

Suppose
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. By Definition
4.1
and the properties of infimum and supremum, there exists
u,\,v\in S[a,b]
such that
v(x)\leq f(x)\leq u(x)
and

\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha-\frac{\epsilon}{2}<\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha<\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha+\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

We rearrange and use Theorem
3.8
to get the desired inequality.

Now for the converse, suppose for each
\epsilon>0
, there exists
u,\,v\in S[a,b]
such that
v(x)\leq f(x)\leq u(x)
for each
x\in[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha<\epsilon.

Fix such
\epsilon>0
. Then,

\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha\leq L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])\leq U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])\leq\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha.

Hence,

U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])-L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])\leq\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha<\epsilon.

Since
\epsilon
is arbitrary, this is true for all
\epsilon>0
and thus,
U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])
. By Definition
4.1
, we have the result.
∎

Before continuing our proof of Theorem
4.12
, we pause to give two corollaries of the RDS criterion. The first will be used below in later sections.
The proof is identical to that for the DS-integral (see
[
5
, Corollaries 1.28, 4.18]
) and so we omit the details.

Corollary 4.6
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
, if
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and
|f(x)|\leq M
for every
x\in[a,b]
, then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists step functions
u,\,v
such that

-M\leq v(x)\leq f(x)\leq u(x)\leq M

and

\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha<\epsilon.

As our second corollary we prove that our definition of the RDS-integral in Definition
4.1
in terms of step functions is equivalent to the definition by Ross in Definition
1.3
in terms of upper and lower sums. To do so, we introduce the notion of best-fit step functions (see
[
5
, Definition 1.30]
).

Definition 4.7
.

Given
f\in B[a,b]
and a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
[a,b]
, define the best-fit step functions of
f
with respect to
\mathcal{P}
, denoted
\hat{u},\hat{v}\in S[a,b]
, as follows: on each partition interval
I_{i}
, let

M_{i}=\sup\{f(x)\;:\;x\in I_{i}\},\quad m_{i}=\inf\{f(x)\;:\;x\in I_{i}\},

and define
\hat{u}(x)=M_{i}
and
\hat{v}(x)=m_{i}
for
x\in I_{i}
. At each partition point
x_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
, let
\hat{u}(x_{i})=\hat{v}(x_{i})=f(x_{i})
.

Observe that for any
\alpha\in I[a,b]
,

\int_{a}^{b}\hat{u}(x)\,d\alpha=U_{\alpha}^{r}(f,\mathcal{P})\>\text{ and }\;\int_{a}^{b}\hat{v}(x)\,d\alpha=L_{\alpha}^{r}(f,\mathcal{P}).

Moreover, if
u,\,v
are step functions with respect to the partition
\mathcal{P}
that bracket
f
, then we immediately have that for all
x\in[a,b]
,

(4.1)

v(x)\leq\hat{v}(x)\leq f(x)\leq\hat{u}(x)\leq u(x).

Using this observation, we can pass between the RDS criterion in Theorem
7.13
and the corresponding result proved by Ross
[
19
, Theorem 35.6]
using his definition. As an immediate consequence we have that the two definitions are equivalent. To distinguish them, temporarily denote the set of functions integrable by Definition
1.3
by
RDS^{*}_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

Corollary 4.8
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a partition
\mathcal{P}
and best-fit step function
\hat{u},\hat{v}
of
f
with respect to
\mathcal{P}
such that

(4.2)

U_{\alpha}^{r}(f,\mathcal{P})-L_{\alpha}^{r}(f,\mathcal{P})=\int_{a}^{b}\hat{u}(x)-\hat{v}(x)\,d\alpha<\epsilon.

As a consequence,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
f\in RDS_{\alpha}^{*}[a,b]
and the two integrals agree.

Proof.

We argue as for the Darboux integral in
[
5
, Corollary 1.32]
. Suppose first that
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. By the RDS criterion, Theorem
4.5
, there exist
u,\,v\in S[a,b]
which bracket
f
such that

\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\;d\alpha<\epsilon.

We may assume that
u
and
v
are step functions with respect to a common partition
\mathcal{P}
. Let
\hat{u}
and
\hat{v}
be the best-fit step functions of
f
with respect to
\mathcal{P}
. Then by (
4.1
), we have that
\hat{u}(x)-\hat{v}(x)\leq u(x)-v(x)
, and so
by Theorem
3.10
,

\int_{a}^{b}\hat{u}(x)-\hat{v}(x)\,d\alpha\leq\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha<\epsilon.

Therefore, by
[
19
, Theorem 35.6]
,
f\in RDS^{*}_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

Conversely, if (
4.2
) holds for every
\epsilon>0
, then, since
\hat{u}
and
\hat{v}
are step functions that bracket
f
, by Theorem
4.5
we have
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

Finally, to see that the integrals agree, note that given
\epsilon>0
and the best fit step functions such that (
4.2
) holds, then by their respective definitions we have that

\bigg|(RDS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha-(RDS^{*})\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha\bigg|\leq\int_{a}^{b}\hat{u}(x)-\hat{v}(x)\,d\alpha<\epsilon.

Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, the two integrals are equal.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
such that
\alpha(x)=c
is constant. Then, for every
f\in B[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

(4.3)

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=0.

Proof.

We argue as for the DS-integral in
[
5
, Theorem 4.20]
. Note
\alpha\in I[a,b]
. Fix
f\in B[a,b]
. Fix
u\in S_{U}(f,[a,b])
defined with respect to a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
of
[a,b]
For each
i
,

\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})=\alpha(x_{i}+)-\alpha(x_{i}-)=c-c=0;

similarly,
\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})=0
. Hence, for every
u\in S_{U}(f,[a,b])
,

\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha=0

and so
U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=0
. By the same argument,
L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=0
. Thus,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and (
4.3
) holds.
∎

The proof of the following lemma is identical to the proof for the DS-integral
[
5
, Prop 4.21]
, and we omit the details.

Lemma 4.10
.

Given
\alpha,\beta\in I[a,b]
and
r_{1},r_{2}\geq 0
, define
\gamma=r_{1}\alpha+r_{2}\beta\in I[a,b]
. If
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{\beta}[a,b]
, then
f\in RDS_{\gamma}[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\gamma=r_{1}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha+r_{2}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta.

Corollary 4.11
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
, if
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, then
f\in RDS_{\alpha+c}[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d[\alpha+c].

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemma
4.9
and Lemma
4.10
.
∎

We can now prove the independence of the RDS-integral from the decomposition of the integrator
\alpha
as the difference of increasing functions. The proof is essentially the same as for the DS-integral
[
5
, Lemma 4.27]
, but we include the short proof for completeness.

Theorem 4.12
.

For
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
, given two different decompositions
\alpha=\alpha^{+}-\alpha^{-}=\beta^{+}-\beta^{-},
if
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
with respect to both decompositions, then

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{+}-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{-}=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta^{+}-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta^{-}.

Proof.

Let
\gamma=\alpha^{+}+\beta^{-}=\alpha^{-}+\beta^{+}
. Then
\gamma\in I[a,b]
and by Lemma
4.10
,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{+}+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta^{-}=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\gamma=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{-}+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta^{+}.

If we rearrange terms, we get the desired equality.
∎

By Theorem
4.12
, when computing the RDS-integral we can choose any decomposition of
\alpha
for which it is defined. Determining such a decomposition may seem like a problem, but we will show that if a function is RDS-integrable with respect to any decomposition of
\alpha
, then it is always integrable with respect to a canonical decomposition of
\alpha
in terms of its positive and negative variation. The proof requires a definition and two lemmas. For the first definition, recall the definition of the variation
V(f,[a,b])
given in Definition
2.3
.

Definition 4.13
.

Fix
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
, and define the variation function of
\alpha
,
V\alpha
, by

V\alpha(x)=V(\alpha,[a,x]).

Further, define the positive and negative variations of
\alpha
by

P\alpha=\frac{1}{2}(V\alpha+\alpha-\alpha(a))\qquad\text{and}\qquad N\alpha=\frac{1}{2}(V\alpha-\alpha+\alpha(a)).

The functions
V\alpha,\,P\alpha,\,N\alpha
are all increasing and yield a Jordan decomposition of
f
. For a proof, see
[
5
, Section 3.3]
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
,
V\alpha,\,P\alpha,\,N\alpha\in I[a,b]
and

\alpha(x)=P\alpha(x)-N\alpha(x)+\alpha(a).

The second lemma is a kind of converse to Lemma
4.10
.

Lemma 4.15
.

Given
\alpha,\beta\in I[a,b]
and
r_{1},r_{2}>0
, define
\gamma=r_{1}\alpha+r_{2}\beta\in I[a,b]
. If
f\in RDS_{\gamma}[a,b]
, then
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{\beta}[a,b].

Proof.

It suffices to show
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
since by a symmetric argument,
f\in RDS_{\beta}[a,b]
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. By Theorem
4.5
, there exist step functions
u,\,v
which bracket
f
such that

\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\gamma<r_{1}\epsilon.

Then, since
u-v
is nonnegative and
r_{2}\beta
is increasing, by Theorems
3.10
and
3.11
,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{1}}\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d(r_{1}\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{r_{1}}\left(\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d(r_{1}\alpha)+\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d(r_{2}\beta)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{1}}\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\gamma

\displaystyle<\epsilon.

Hence, by Theorem
4.5
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b].

∎

Theorem 4.16
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. Moreover,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha.

Proof.

We argue as in
[
5
, Theorem 4.28]
for the DS-integral. Suppose first that
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. By Lemma
4.14
,
P\alpha,N\alpha\in I[a,b]
. Since
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, there exists a decomposition
\alpha=\alpha^{+}-\alpha^{-}
such that
f\in RDS_{\alpha^{+}}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{\alpha^{-}}[a,b]
. Thus, for all
x\in[a,b]

\alpha(x)=[\alpha^{+}(x)-\alpha^{+}(a)]-[\alpha^{-}(x)-\alpha^{-}(a)]+\alpha(a).

By
[
5
, Theorem 3.21]
, there exists
\beta\in I[a,b]
such that
\alpha^{+}(x)-\alpha^{+}(a)=P\alpha(x)+\beta(x)
and
\alpha^{-}(x)-\alpha^{-}(a)=N\alpha(x)+\beta(x)
. By Lemma
4.10
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha^{+}-\alpha^{+}(a)}[a,b]=RDS_{P\alpha+\beta}[a,b]
. Thus, by Lemma
4.15
,
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
, and
f\in RDS_{\beta}[a,b]
.

Now suppose
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. Since
P\alpha+\alpha(a)\in I[a,b]
. By Lemmas
4.9
and
4.10
,

f\in RDS_{P\alpha+\alpha(a)}[a,b]
. Then, by Definition
4.1
, since
\alpha=P\alpha-N\alpha+\alpha(a)
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b].

Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{+}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d[\alpha^{+}(a)]+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta,

and

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{-}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d[\alpha^{-}(a)]+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta.

Hence,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{+}-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha^{-}=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha.

∎

We conclude this section by showing that the DS-integral given in Definition
2.8
and the RDS-integral agree if the integrator or integrand are continuous. We will give a complete characterization of the relationship between these two definitions of the integral in Section
9
.
To state our first result, let
CBV[a,b]
be the set of functions which are both continuous and of bounded variation on
[a,b]
.

Theorem 4.17
.

Given
\alpha\in CBV[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and in this case,

(4.4)

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

First, consider
\alpha\in I[a,b]
. Suppose
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Fix
u\in S[a,b]
. Since
\alpha\in C[a,b]
,

\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha.

By Definition
2.8
and Definition
4.1
, we have

L^{i}_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])=U^{i}_{\alpha}(f,[a,b]).

Thus,
f\in DS[a,b]
. The reverse implication is proved in the same way, and it is immediate that (
4.4
) holds.

Now, consider
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
. Suppose
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Then, by Theorem
4.16
,
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. By
[
5
, Definition 3.16]
, it is easy to see that
P\alpha,N\alpha\in CBV[a,b]
. By the previous case,
f\in DS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in DS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. By
[
5
, Theorem 4.28]
,
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. The converse follows similarly since all these implications are reversible. By the previous case,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha.

Therefore, by Theorem
4.16

and
[
5
, Theorem 4.28]
, (
4.4
) holds.
∎

Theorem 4.18
.

Suppose
f\in C[a,b]
and
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
. Then,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and in this case, (
4.4
) holds.

Proof.

By Theorem
7.1
below and
[
5
, Theorem 4.35]
, if
f\in C[a,b]
, then
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. It suffices to show the integrals are equal.

First, consider
\alpha\in I[a,b]
. If
\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])=\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)=0
, then
\alpha
is constant. Hence, by Lemma
4.9
and
[
5
, Lemma 4.20]
, we have both integrals are
0
. Otherwise, suppose
\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])=\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)>0
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. Since
f
is continuous on
[a,b]
, it is uniformly continuous. Thus, there exists
\delta>0
such that for every
x,y\in[a,b]
with
|x-y|<\delta
,

|f(x)-f(y)|<\frac{\epsilon}{\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])}.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
N>\frac{b-a}{\delta}.
Moreover, let
\mathcal{P}_{N}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}=\{a+\frac{b-a}{N}i\}_{i=0}^{N}
be a regular partition of
[a,b]
. Hence,
\overline{I_{i}}=[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
is compact so
f
attains its extrema on each interval. For each
1\leq i\leq N
, let

M_{i}=\sup\{f(x)\;:\;x\in\overline{I_{i}}\}=f(x_{i}^{M})

and

m_{i}=\inf\{f(x)\;:\;x\in\overline{I_{i}}\}=f(x_{i}^{m}).

Define step functions
u,\,v
by

(4.5)

u(x)=\begin{cases}M_{i},&x\in I_{i},\\
f(x_{i}),&x=x_{i},\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad v(x)=\begin{cases}m_{i},&x\in I_{i},\\
f(x_{i}),&x=x_{i}.\end{cases}

Observe that

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha-(DS)\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\sum_{i=0}^{N}f(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}M_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\alpha(I_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}f(x_{i})[\alpha(x_{i}+)-\alpha(x_{i})]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(x_{i})[\alpha(x_{i})-\alpha(x_{i}-)]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{N}M_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\alpha(I_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}M_{i+1}[\alpha(x_{i}+)-\alpha(x_{i})]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}M_{i}[\alpha(x_{i})-\alpha(x_{i}-)]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{N}M_{i}[\alpha(I_{i})+\alpha(x_{i}-)-\alpha(x_{i})-\alpha(x_{i-1}+)+\alpha(x_{i-1})]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\alpha(I_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{N}M_{i}\alpha(I_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\alpha(I_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad<\frac{\epsilon}{\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha(I_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{\epsilon}{\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])}(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\epsilon.

A similar argument yields

(DS)\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha<\epsilon.

Hence,

\left|(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha\right|<\epsilon.

Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, (
4.4
) holds.

Now, we consider the case
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
. By the previous case,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha.

Therefore, by Theorem
4.16

and
[
5
, Theorem 4.28]
, (
4.4
) holds.
∎

5.
Consequences of the RDS Criterion

In this section we develop the basic properties of the RDS-integral as a consequence of the RDS Criterion, Theorem
4.5
. In particular, we show that the RDS-integral is linear, additive, monotonic, and linear in the integrator. In several cases the proofs are essentially identical to the proofs of the corresponding results for the Darboux integral and DS-integral given in
[
5
]
, and so we omit the details. We will instead concentrate on those results where some additional care is needed because of the additional term in the definition of the RDS-integral of step functions.

Theorem 5.1
.

(Linearity) Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f,g\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, for any
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
r_{1}f+r_{2}g\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}[r_{1}f(x)+r_{2}g(x)]\,d\alpha=r_{1}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha+r_{2}\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\,d\alpha.

The proof of Theorem
5.1
is an immediate consequence of Lemmas
5.2
and
5.3
. The proof of the first is identical to the proof for the DS-integral in
[
5
, Theorem 4.19, Theorem 4.30]
; the proof of the second is identical to the proofs in
[
5
, Theorem 1.39, Theorem 4.28, Theorem 4.30]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f,g\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
,
f+g\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)+g(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\,d\alpha.

Lemma 5.3
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
cf\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}cf(x)\,d\alpha=c\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Theorem 5.4
.

(Additivity) Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
, for any
m\in(a,b)
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,m]
and
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[m,b]
. Furthermore,

(5.1)

\int_{a}^{m}f(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{m}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

The proof of the first part is similar to that for the DS-integral in
[
5
, Theorem 1.39, Theorem 4.19, Theorem 4.30]
, but the second part requires more care. First, consider
\alpha\in I[a,b]
. Fix
m\in(a,b)
and suppose
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. Then by Theorem
4.5
, there exists
u,\,v\in S[a,b]
which bracket
f
such that

\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha<\epsilon.

Thus,
u|_{[a,m]},v|_{[a,m]}\in S[a,b]
and they bracket
f
on
[a,m]
. Moreover, by Theorem
3.10
, we have

\int_{m}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha\geq 0,

and by Theorem
3.9
,

\int_{a}^{m}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha\\
\leq\int_{a}^{m}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{m}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha<\epsilon.

Hence, by Theorem
4.5
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,m]
. By a similar arguement,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[m,b]
.

Now, fix
f
such that
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,m]
and
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[m,b]
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. By Theorem
4.5
, there exists
u_{1},v_{1}\in S[a,m]
and
u_{2},v_{2}\in[m,b]
such that both pairs bracket
f
and

\int_{a}^{m}u_{1}(x)-v_{1}(x)\,d\alpha<\frac{\epsilon}{2},\quad\int_{m}^{b}u_{2}(x)-v_{2}(x)\,d\alpha<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

We combine
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
as follows: let

u(x)=\begin{cases}u_{1}(x),&x\in[a,m),\\
f(m),&x=m,\\
u_{2}(x),&x\in(m,b].\end{cases}

Similarly, we combine
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
as follows: let

v(x)=\begin{cases}v_{1}(x),&x\in[a,m),\\
f(m),&x=m,\\
v_{2}(x),&x\in(m,b].\end{cases}

It is easy to see
v(x)\leq f(x)\leq u(x)
for all
x\in[a,b]
. Also, observe that on
[a,m]
,
u(x)-v(x)\leq u_{1}(x)-v_{1}(x)
and on
[m,b]
,
u(x)-v(x)\leq u_{2}(x)-v_{2}(x).
Thus, by Theorems
3.9
and
3.10
,

\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{m}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{m}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha\\
\leq\int_{a}^{m}u_{1}(x)-v_{1}(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{m}^{b}u_{2}(x)-v_{2}(x)\,d\alpha<\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon.

Hence, by Theorem
4.5
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.
Further, by Definition
4.1
,

\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha\leq\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha\leq\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha

and

\int_{a}^{m}v(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{m}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha\leq\int_{a}^{m}f(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{m}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha\leq\int_{a}^{m}u(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{m}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha.

Therefore,

\left|\int_{a}^{m}f(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{m}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha\right|\leq\epsilon

for all
\epsilon>0
, and so (
5.1
) holds.

Now, consider
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
. Fix
m\in(a,b)
. Suppose
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Then by Theorem
4.16
,
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. By the previous case,
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,m],f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[m,b],f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,m]
, and
f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[m,b].
Then by Theorem
4.16
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,m]
and
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[m,b]
. The reverse implications hold as well. Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{m}f(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{m}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{m}f(x)\,dP\alpha+\int_{m}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\int_{a}^{m}f(x)\,dN\alpha-\int_{m}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

∎

The proof of the following result is identical to the proof for the DS-integral in
[
5
, Theorem 1.39, Theorem 4.19, Theorem 4.30]
and so is omitted.

Theorem 5.5
.

(Monotonicity) Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
and
f,g\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, if for every
x\in[a,b]
,
f(x)\geq g(x)
, then

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha\geq\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\,d\alpha.

Theorem 5.6
.

(Linearity of the Integrator) Given
\alpha,\beta\in BV[a,b]
and
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, define
\gamma=r_{1}\alpha+r_{2}\beta\in BV[a,b]
. If
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{\beta}[a,b]
, then
f\in RDS_{\gamma}[a,b],
and

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\gamma=r_{1}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha+r_{2}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta.

The proof of Theorem
5.6
is an immediate consequence of Lemmas
5.7
and
5.8
. The proofs of both lemmas are the same as that for the DS-integral
[
5
, Theorem 4.30]
, and so omitted.

Lemma 5.7
.

Given
\alpha,\beta\in BV[a,b]
, define
\gamma=\alpha+\beta\in BV[a,b]
. If
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{\beta}[a,b]
, then
f\in RDS_{\gamma}[a,b],
and

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\gamma=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta.

Lemma 5.8
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
, define
\gamma=c\alpha\in BV[a,b]
. If
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, then
f\in RDS_{\gamma}[a,b],
and

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\gamma=c\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

We conclude this section with two lemmas, the first of which uses the RDS Criterion in its proof, and which we will use below.

Lemma 5.9
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, if we define
f^{+}(x)=\max(f(x),0)
, then
f^{+}\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b].
Similarly, if we set
f^{-}(x)=\max(-f(x),0)
, then
f^{-}\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

Proof.

We will prove this for
f^{+}
; the proof for
f^{-}
is essentially the same. First, consider
\alpha\in I[a,b]
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. By Theorem
4.5
, there exists step functions
u_{f},v_{f}
on some common partition
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
of
[a,b]
which bracket
f
such that

\int_{a}^{b}u_{f}(x)-v_{f}(x)\,d\alpha<\epsilon.

Let
u_{f}(x)=c_{i}
and
v_{f}(x)=d_{i}
for
x\in I_{i}
. Define
u,\,v
by

u(x)=\begin{cases}\max(c_{i},0),&x\in I_{i},\\
f^{+}(x),&x=x_{i},\end{cases}

and

v(x)=\begin{cases}\max(d_{i},0),&x\in I_{i},\\
f^{+}(x),&x=x_{i}.\end{cases}

Then, for
x\in I_{i}
,

v(x)=\max(d_{i},0)\leq\max(f(x),0)\leq\max(c_{i},0)=u(x)

and for
x=x_{i}
,
v(x)=f^{+}(x)=u(x)
. Moreover, if
\max(c_{i},0)=c_{i}
, then

\max(c_{i},0)-\max(d_{i},0)=c_{i}-\max(d_{i},0)\leq c_{i}-d_{i}

and if
\max(c_{i},0)=0
, then

0\leq\max(c_{i},0)-\max(d_{i},0)=-\max(d_{i},0)\leq 0.

Hence, since
c_{i}-d_{i}\geq 0
, we have

\max(c_{i},0)-\max(d_{i},0)\leq\max(c_{i}-d_{i},0)=c_{i}-d_{i}.

Now by Definition
3.3
,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}[u(x_{i})-v(x_{i})]\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\max(c_{i},0)-\max(d_{i},0))\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\max(c_{i},0)-\max(d_{i},0))\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}-d_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n}[u_{f}(x_{i})-v_{f}(x_{i})]\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}-d_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}u_{f}(x)-v_{f}(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle<\epsilon.

So by Theorem
4.5
,
g\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

Now, consider
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
. Then by Theorem
4.16
,
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. By the previous case,
f^{+}\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f^{+}\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. Hence, by Theorem
4.16
,
f^{+}\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
as desired.
∎

.

Given
f\in B[a,b]
,
f(x)=f^{+}(x)-f^{-}(x)
and
|f(x)|=f^{+}(x)+f^{-}(x)
.

.

and

,
|f|\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

6.
Convergence Theorems

In this section, we begin to consider limits and the RDS-integral. Our ultimate goal is to prove the bounded convergence theorem, but we defer that proof to Section
8
as we will need results from Sections
6
and
7
to prove it. Here we prove that the limit and the integral can be exchanged in two cases: first when the integrands converge uniformly, and second when the integrators converge in bounded variation norm.

Theorem 6.1
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and a sequence
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of bounded functions on
[a,b]
, suppose
f_{k}\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and there exists a function
f
on
[a,b]
such that
f_{k}\to f
uniformly. Then,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

(6.1)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{k}(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

First, we consider the case when
\alpha\in I[a,b]
. Suppose
\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])=\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)=0
. Then,
\alpha
is constant. By Lemma
4.9
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and for each
k\in\mathbb{N},

\int_{a}^{b}f_{k}(x)\,d\alpha=0=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Hence, (
6.1
) holds.

Now suppose
\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])=\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)>0.
Fix
\epsilon>0
. By uniform convergence, there exists
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
x\in[a,b]
,

|f_{K}(x)-f(x)|<\frac{\epsilon}{3(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}.

Since
f_{K}\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, by Theorem
4.5
, there exists
u_{K},v_{K}
step functions with respect to a common partition
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
which bracket
f_{K}
such that

\int_{a}^{b}u_{K}(x)-v_{K}(x)\,d\alpha<\frac{\epsilon}{3}.

Let
u_{K}(x)=c_{i}
and
v_{K}(x)=d_{i}
for
x\in I_{i}
.

Define step functions
u,\,v
as

u(x)=u_{K}(x)+\frac{\epsilon}{3(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}

and

v(x)=v_{K}(x)-\frac{\epsilon}{3(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}

Hence, for
x\in[a,b]
,

f(x)<f_{K}(x)+\frac{\epsilon}{3(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}\leq u_{K}(x)+\frac{\epsilon}{3(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}=u(x)

and

f(x)>f_{K}(x)-\frac{\epsilon}{3(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}\geq v_{K}(x)-\frac{\epsilon}{3(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}=v(x).

Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}[u(x_{i})-v(x_{i})]\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}+\frac{\epsilon}{3(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}-d_{i}+\frac{\epsilon}{3(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))})\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}[u_{K}(x_{i})-v_{K}(x_{i})]\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}-d_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{2\epsilon}{3(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}u_{K}(x)-v_{K}(x)\,d\alpha+\frac{2\epsilon}{3(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}\int_{a}^{b}1\,d\alpha

\displaystyle<\frac{\epsilon}{3}+\frac{2\epsilon}{3(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))

\displaystyle=\epsilon.

So by Theorem
4.5
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b].

To show that (
6.1
) holds, fix
\epsilon>0
. Then, there exists
K
such that for all
k\geq K^{\prime}
,

|f_{k}(x)-f(x)|<\frac{\epsilon}{2(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}.

Hence, for
k\geq K^{\prime}
,

\left|\int_{a}^{b}f_{k}(x)\,d\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha\right|=\left|\int_{a}^{b}f_{k}(x)-f(x)\,d\alpha\right|\leq\int_{a}^{b}|f_{k}(x)-f(x)|\,d\alpha\\
\leq\int_{a}^{b}\frac{\epsilon}{2(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}\,d\alpha=\frac{\epsilon}{2(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))}(\alpha(b)-\alpha(a))=\frac{\epsilon}{2}<\epsilon.

Now consider
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
. Then by Theorem
4.16
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{k}\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f_{k}\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. By the previous case,
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. Thus,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Moreover,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{k}(x)\,d\alpha=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left[\int_{a}^{b}f_{k}(x)\,dP\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f_{k}(x)\,dN\alpha\right]\\
=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

∎

To state our result on convergence of the integrators, we first give the definition of the bounded variation norm, following
[
5
, Proposition 3.43]
; recall the definition of the variation of a BV function in Definition
2.3
.

Definition 6.2
.

The function
\|\cdot\|_{BV}:BV[a,b]\to[0,\infty)
defined for
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
by

\|\alpha\|_{BV}=\sup_{x\in[a,b]}|\alpha(x)|+V\alpha(b).

Remark 6.3
.

For a proof that
BV[a,b]
is a normed space with respect to this norm, and for properties of this space, see
[
5
, Section 3.6]
.

Theorem 6.4
.

Given a sequence of functions
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
, suppose for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha_{k}\in BV[a,b]
and
\alpha_{k}\to\alpha
in BV norm. Moreover, suppose
f\in RDS_{\alpha_{k}}[a,b]
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

(6.2)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha_{k}=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

First, we consider the case where all
\alpha_{k}\in I[a,b]
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. Note
f\in B[a,b]
so there exists
M>0
such that
|f(x)|\leq M
for all
x\in[a,b]
. Further, there exists
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that

||\alpha-\alpha_{k}||_{BV}<\frac{\epsilon}{3M}.

Since
f\in RDS_{\alpha_{k}}[a,b]
, by Corollary
4.6
, there exist step functions
u_{K},v_{K}
such that

-M\leq v_{K}(x)\leq f(x)\leq u_{K}(x)\leq M

and

\int_{a}^{b}u_{K}(x)-v_{K}(x)\,d\alpha_{k}<\frac{\epsilon}{3}.

Suppose
u_{K},v_{K}
are defined with respect to the common partition
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
where
u_{K}(x)=c_{i},v_{K}(x)=d_{i}
for
x\in I_{i}
. Define step functions
u,\,v
by

u(x)=\begin{cases}u_{K}(x),&x\in I_{i},\\
f(x_{i}),&x=x_{i},\\
\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad v(x)=\begin{cases}v_{K}(x),&x\in I_{i},\\
f(x_{i}),&x=x_{i}.\\
\end{cases}

So,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(u(x_{i})-v(x_{i}))\mu_{\alpha_{k}}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}-d_{i})\mu_{\alpha_{k}}(I_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}-d_{i})\mu_{\alpha_{k}}(I_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n}(u_{K}(x_{i})-v_{K}(x_{i}))\mu_{\alpha_{k}}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}-d_{i})\mu_{\alpha_{k}}(I_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}u_{K}(x)-v_{K}(x)\,d\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle<\frac{\epsilon}{3}.

Then by Lemma
5.11
,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha_{k}+\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d(\alpha-\alpha_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha_{k}+\left|\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d(\alpha-\alpha_{k})\right|

\displaystyle<\frac{\epsilon}{3}+\int_{a}^{b}|u(x)-v(x)|\,dV(\alpha-\alpha_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{3}+2M\int_{a}^{b}\,dV(\alpha-\alpha_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{3}+2MV(\alpha-\alpha_{k})(b)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{3}+2M\|\alpha-\alpha_{k}\|_{BV}

\displaystyle<\frac{\epsilon}{3}+2M\frac{\epsilon}{3M}

\displaystyle=\epsilon.

Therefore, by Theorem
4.5
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

To show that (
6.2
) holds, fix
\epsilon>0
. Since
\alpha_{k}\to\alpha
in BV norm, there exists
K^{\prime}
such that for all
k\geq K^{\prime}
,

\|\alpha_{k}-\alpha\|_{BV}<\frac{\epsilon}{M}.

Then, for all
k\geq K^{\prime}
,

\left|\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha_{k}-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha\right|=\left|\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d(\alpha_{k}-\alpha)\right|\leq\int_{a}^{b}|f(x)|\,dV(\alpha_{k}-\alpha)\\
\leq M\int_{a}^{b}dV(\alpha_{k}-\alpha)=MV(\alpha_{k}-\alpha)(b)\leq M\|\alpha_{k}-\alpha\|_{BV}<\epsilon.

Now, consider
\alpha_{k}\in BV[a,b]
. Then by Theorem
4.16
,
f\in RDS_{P\alpha_{k}}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{N\alpha_{k}}[a,b]
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. By
[
5
, Proposition 3.47]
,
P\alpha_{k}\to P\alpha,N\alpha_{k}\to N\alpha
in
BV
norm. Thus,
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
by the previous case. Therefore,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Moreover,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha_{k}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left[\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha_{k}-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha_{k}\right]\\
=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

∎

Finally, we note that in Theorem
6.4
, convergence in BV norm is necessary, in the sense that it cannot be replaced with the weaker condition of uniform convergence (which is implied by convergence in norm). This was shown for the interior DS-integral in
[
5
, Example 4.52]
. In this example all the integrators are continuous, so by Theorem
4.17
we conclude that the same example holds for the RDS-integral as well.

7.
RDS Integrability Theorems

In this section we turn to the following problem: given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
, characterize the functions
f\in B[a,b]
such that
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Our goal is to prove a version of the Lebesgue criterion for the RDS-integral, Theorem
7.13
; this will let us completely characterize the
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
in terms of the discontinuities of
f
and
\alpha
. To accomplish this, we need to work with the so-called saltus decomposition of functions in
BV[a,b]
: this will allow us to study the behavior of the RDS-integral at a discontinuity of
\alpha
by considering a Heaviside function that corresponds to this discontinuity.

Finally, as an application of the results in this section, we prove a generalized version of integration by parts for the RDS-integral. For the classical definitions of the RS or DS-integrals, integration by parts can be stated as an integrability theorem: see, for example,
[
2
, Theorem 29.7]
. While we do not prove such a version here, it still seemed natural to include integration by parts in this section.

We begin with a classical result that also holds for all definitions of the Stieltjes integral.

Theorem 7.1
.

Suppose
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f\in C[a,b]
. Then,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

Proof.

We will first consider the case when
\alpha\in I[a,b]
. The proof is very similar to the proof of Theorem
4.18
. Suppose
\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])=\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)=0
. Then,
\alpha
is constant, and so by Lemma
4.9
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

Now suppose
\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])=\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)>0
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. Since
f
is continuous on the closed interval
[a,b]
, it must also be uniformly continuous on
[a,b]
. Fix
\delta
,
N
and the regular partition
\mathcal{P}_{N}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}=\{a+\frac{b-a}{N}i\}_{i=0}^{N}
. Define step functions
u,\,v
as in (
4.5
). Then
u,\,v
bracket
f
on
[a,b]
, and, since the partition intervals have lengths less than
\delta
, if
x\in I_{i}
, then

u(x)-v(x)=M_{i}-m_{i}=f(x_{i}^{M})-f(x_{i}^{m})=|f(x_{i}^{M})-f(x_{i}^{m})|<\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)},

and if
x=x_{i}
,
u(x)-v(x)=0
. Hence, by Definition
3.3
,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N}[u(x_{i})-v(x_{i})]\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}[M_{i}-m_{i}]\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle<\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{N}\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)}\int_{a}^{b}1\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\epsilon.

Therefore, by Theorem
4.5
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

Finally, if
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
, then by the previous case,
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. Thus, by Theorem
4.16
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

∎

In our next result we show that if the integrand is a function of bounded variation, then it is integrable with respect to any integrator in
BV[a,b]
. Already with this result, we see that the RDS-integral is much more general than the classical definitions of the Stieltjes integral: the RS-integral does not exist if
f
and
\alpha
have a common discontinuity (see
[
5
, Theorem 5.31]
), and the classical exterior DS-integral only exists in certain cases (see
[
13
, Theorem 4.13]
). This result also holds for the interior DS-integral (see
[
5
, Theorem 4.38]
) and was one of the motivations for using that definition.

To prove this result we introduce the saltus decomposition which decomposes a function of bounded variation into a continuous piece and an infinite sum of Heaviside functions. This decomposition is developed in detail in
[
5
, Section 3.5]
; here we refine that definition slightly to meet our needs.

Definition 7.2
.

A saltus set in
[a,b]
is a sequence of ordered pairs
\{(x_{i},a_{i})\}
such that the points
x_{i}
are a collection of disjoint points in
[a,b]
, and the
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
a_{i}\neq 0
, are such that

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|a_{i}|<\infty.

Associated with any saltus set is a saltus function,

f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}H_{c}(x-x_{i}),

where
c\in\mathbb{R}
. If
c=0
,
f
is a left continuous saltus function, and if
c=1
, it is a right continuous saltus function. A saltus function is reduced if when
c=1
,
x_{i}\neq a
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, or if when
c=0
,
x_{i}\neq b
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 7.3
.

Given
f\in BV[a,b]
, there exists a saltus decomposition into functions of bounded variation

(7.1)

f=Gf+S_{L}f+S_{R}f,

where
Gf
is continuous,
S_{L}f
is a reduced, left continuous saltus function, and
S_{R}f
is a reduced, right continuous saltus function.

Proof.

Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be the set of discontinuities of
f
. By
[
5
, Theorem 3.38]
, we have a saltus decomposition
(
7.1
),
where
Gf
is a continuous function of bounded variation, and

S_{L}f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(f(x_{i}+)-f(x_{i})\big)H_{0}(x-x_{i})\;\text{and}\;S_{R}f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(f(x_{i})-f(x_{i}-)\big)H_{1}(x-x_{i})

are left and right continuous saltus functions (which are functions of bounded variation: see
[
5
, Proposition 3.37]
).
It follows from this definition (also see
[
5
, Remark 3.42]
) that we may assume that
b
is not in the saltus set of
S_{L}f
since
f(b+)-f(b)=0
so even if
f
is discontinuous at
b
, the corresponding term in the definition of
S_{L}f
is
0
. Similarly, we may assume that
a
is not in the saltus set of
S_{R}f
. Thus, both
S_{L}f
and
S_{R}f
are reduced saltus functions.
∎

Remark 7.4
.

The reduced saltus functions
S_{L}f
and
S_{R}f
satisfy
S_{L}f(a)=S_{R}f(a)=0
.

Theorem 7.5
.

Suppose
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f\in BV[a,b]
. Then,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

Proof.

By Proposition
7.3
, form the saltus decomposition of
f
. By Theorem
7.1
,
Gf\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. By
[
5
, Lemma 3.36]
, there exists a sequence of step functions
\{S_{L}^{n}f\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
S_{L}^{n}f\to S_{L}f
uniformly. Since these are step functions,
S_{L}^{n}f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
by Proposition
4.3
. Thus, by Theorem
6.1
,
S_{L}f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Similarly,
S_{R}f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Therefore, by Theorem
5.1
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b].

∎

We now consider the RDS-integral when the integrand
\alpha
is a saltus function. We will show that every bounded function is RDS-integrable when the integrator is a saltus function that arises in the saltus decomposition of a function of bounded variation. As a first step we give two key results for integrators that are Heaviside functions. These results capture the fact that such integrators act like Lebesgue-Stieltjes measures that are point masses. This behavior highlights where our definition improves on the historic ones. (Compare this result to the behavior of the interior DS-integral in
[
5
, Lemma 5.6]
.)

Proposition 7.6
.

Suppose
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
is the Heaviside function
\alpha(x)=H_{c}(x-x_{0})
for some
x_{0}\in(a,b),c\in\mathbb{R}
and
f\in B[a,b]
. Then,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

(7.2)

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=f(x_{0}).

Proof.

Since
f\in B[a,b]
, there exists
M>0
such that
|f(x)|\leq M
for every
x\in[a,b]
. Define step functions
u,\,v
by

u(x)=\begin{cases}M,&x\in[a,b]\backslash\{x_{0}\},\\
f(x_{0}),&x=x_{0},\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad v(x)=\begin{cases}-M,&x\in[a,b]\backslash\{x_{0}\},\\
f(x_{0}),&x=x_{0}.\end{cases}

Then
v(x)\leq f(x)\leq u(x)
and, by Definition
3.3
,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=u(a)\mu_{\alpha}(\{a\})+u(x_{0})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{0}\})+u(b)\mu_{\alpha}(\{b\})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+M\cdot\mu_{\alpha}((a,x_{0}))+M\cdot\mu_{\alpha}((x_{0},b))

\displaystyle=M\cdot 0+f(x_{0})\cdot 1+M\cdot 0+M\cdot 0+M\cdot 0

\displaystyle=f(x_{0}).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=v(a)\mu_{\alpha}(\{a\})+v(x_{0})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{0}\})+v(b)\mu_{\alpha}(\{b\})

\displaystyle\quad\quad-M\cdot\mu_{\alpha}((a,x_{0}))-M\cdot\mu_{\alpha}((x_{0},1))

\displaystyle=-M\cdot 0+f(x_{0})\cdot 1-M\cdot 0-M\cdot 0-M\cdot 0

\displaystyle=f(x_{0}).

Thus by Definition
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
,

f(x_{0})\leq L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])\leq U_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])\leq f(x_{0}),

which proves that
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and (
7.2
) holds.
∎

In Proposition
7.6
we assumed that the discontinuity of the Heaviside function was in the interior of the interval
(a,b)
. We now consider when the discontinuity lies at
x_{0}=a
or
x_{0}=b
. If
c=1
and
x_{0}=a
then
H_{c}(x-x_{0})=1
, a constant function on
[a,b]
and so, the result would not be true by Lemma
4.9
. A similar situation happens if
c=0
and
x_{0}=b
. Thus, we consider only the combinations
c=0,x_{0}=a
or
c=1,x_{0}=b
.

Proposition 7.7
.

Suppose
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
is the Heaviside function
\alpha(x)=H_{c}(x-x_{0})
with either
x_{0}=a,c=0
or
x_{0}=b,c=1
. If
f\in B[a,b]
, then
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=f(x_{0}).

Proof.

Suppose
x_{0}=a
and
c=0
. Define
u,\,v
as in the proof of Proposition
7.6
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=u(a)\mu_{\alpha}(\{a\})+u(b)\mu_{\alpha}(\{b\})+M\cdot\mu_{\alpha}((a,b))

\displaystyle=f(a)\cdot(1-c)+M\cdot 0+M\cdot 0

\displaystyle=f(a),

and

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=v(a)\mu_{\alpha}(\{a\})+v(b)\mu_{\alpha}(\{b\})-M\cdot\mu_{\alpha}((a,b))

\displaystyle=f(a)\cdot(1-c)-M\cdot 0-M\cdot 0

\displaystyle=f(a).

Thus, as in the proof of Proposition
7.6
, we are done. The other case
x_{0}=b,c=1
, follows similarly.
∎

Theorem 7.8
.

Suppose
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
is a reduced saltus function of the form

\alpha(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}H_{c}(x-x_{i}).

If
f\in B[a,b]
, then
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}f(x_{i}).

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{n}
denote the partial sums of
\alpha
: that is,

\alpha_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}H_{c}(x-x_{i}).

By
[
5
, Proposition 3.48]
,
\alpha_{n}\to\alpha
in BV norm. Moreover, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha_{n}
is a linear combination of Heaviside functions. Therefore, by Propositions
7.6
and
7.7
, and Theorem
5.1
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha_{n}}[a,b]
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, by Theorem
6.4
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha_{n}\\
=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dH_{c}(x-x_{i})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}f(x_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}f(x_{i}).

∎

We have two immediate corollaries of Theorem
7.8
. First, we have that the Dirichlet function,

D(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x\in\mathbb{Q},\\
0,&x\not\in\mathbb{Q},\end{cases}

is always integrable against a saltus function. This stands in contrast to both the interior and exterior DS-integrals.

Corollary 7.9
.

Let
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
be a reduced saltus function. Then
D\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

Second, we can show that the values of
\alpha
at its discontinuities in the interior of
[a,b]
do not affect the integral, so we can normalize the integrand by assuming it is left or right continuous.

Corollary 7.10
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
, let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be its set of discontinuities and suppose
\alpha
is not discontinuous at
b
. Suppose
\beta\in BV[a,b]
is such that
\alpha(x)=\beta(x)
for all
x\neq x_{i}
and
\beta(x_{i})=\beta(x_{i}-)
for every
i
. (That is,
\beta
is left continuous.) Then,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
f\in RDS_{\beta}[a,b]
and in this case,

(7.3)

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta.

The same is true if we assume
\alpha
is not discontinuous at
a
and we take
\beta
to be right continuous.

Proof.

We will prove this for
\beta
left continuous; the proof if it is right continuous is essentially the same.
Let
c_{i}=\alpha(x_{i})-\alpha(x_{i}-)
. Then by Proposition
7.3
, we have that
\{(x_{i},c_{i}):c_{i}\neq 0\}
forms the saltus set for
S_{R}\alpha
. Since
S_{R}\alpha
is reduced, we have that
a\not\in\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
. by assumption,
b\not\in\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
.

Since
\alpha(x_{i}-)=\beta(x_{i}-)=\beta(x_{i})
,

\alpha(x)-\beta(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\neq x_{i},\\
c_{i},&x=x_{i}.\end{cases}

By Definition
7.2
,

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|c_{i}|<\infty,

so we can rewrite this as

\alpha(x)-\beta(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}H_{1}(x-x_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}H_{0}(x-x_{i}).

The righthand side is the difference of two reduced saltus functions. Therefore, by Theorems
7.8
and
5.6
, for any
f\in B[a,b]
, we have
f\in RDS_{\alpha-\beta}[a,b]
.

To complete the proof, first suppose
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Then, by Theorem
5.6
, since
\beta=-(\alpha-\beta)+\alpha
, we have
f\in RDS_{\beta}[a,b]
. Alternatively, suppose
f\in RDS_{\beta}[a,b]
. Again, by Theorem
5.6
, we have
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. To see that (
7.3
) holds, note that by Theorems
7.8
and
5.6
,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\beta=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d(\alpha-\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}f(x_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}f(x_{i})=0.

∎

We now give a complete characterization of those functions
f
which are integrable with respect to a given function of bounded variation
\alpha
. To put our result into context, recall that the Lebesgue criterion (see
[
5
, Theorem 2.2]
) states that a bounded function
f
is Darboux integrable if and only if the set of discontinuities of
f
has Lebesgue measure
0
. Lebesgue measure corresponds to the Lebesgue-Stieltjes measure induced by the continuous integrator
\alpha(x)=x
. Our result is the exact analogue of this, and does not require the additional hypotheses needed for either the interior or exterior DS-integral. To state it we need a definition.

Definition 7.11
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
, a set
E\subset[a,b]
has
\alpha
-measure
0
if for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a collection
\{I_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of open intervals such that

E\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}I_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})<\epsilon.

Remark 7.12
.

When
\alpha
is continuous, this definition of
\alpha
-measure
0
agrees with
[
5
, Definition 5.17]
. If
\alpha
has a non-trivial saltus component, Definition
7.11
detects point masses, while the definition in
[
5
]
does not.

Theorem 7.13
.

(RDS-Lebesgue Criterion) Given
f\in B[a,b]
and
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if the subset of
[a,b]
where
f
is discontinuous has
G(P\alpha)
-measure
0
and
G(N\alpha)
-measure
0
where
G(P\alpha),G(N\alpha)
are the continuous pieces of the saltus decomposition of
P\alpha,N\alpha
respectively.

Remark 7.14
.

An equivalent version of Theorem
7.13
, with a very different proof couched in terms of his definition of a generalized RS-integral, was given by Ter Horst
[
9
, Theorem D]
.

The following lemma let us takes advantage of the saltus decomposition to reduce to only considering the
G\alpha
-measure of the discontinuities of
f
.

Lemma 7.15
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
f\in RDS_{G\alpha}[a,b]
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. By Theorem
7.8
,
f\in RDS_{S_{L}\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{S_{R}\alpha}[a,b]
. Since
G\alpha=\alpha-S_{L}\alpha-S_{R}\alpha
, by Theorem
5.6
,
f\in RDS_{G\alpha}[a,b]
. Conversely, suppose
f\in RDS_{G\alpha}[a,b]
. Since
f\in RDS_{S_{L}\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{S_{R}\alpha}[a,b]
, again by Theorem
5.6
we have that
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.13
.

We first suppose that
\alpha\in I[a,b]
.
Let
D\subset[a,b]
be the of discontinuities of
f
. We will show
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
D
has
G\alpha
-measure
0
. If
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, then by Lemma
7.15
,
f\in RDS_{G\alpha}[a,b]
.
Since
G\alpha
is continuous, by Theorem
4.18
,
f\in DS_{G\alpha}[a,b]
. We can now use the characterization of the DS-integral in
[
5
, Proposition 5.25]
, which gives that this is the case if and only if
D
has
G\alpha
-measure
0
. (Note by Remark
7.12
, the definitions of
G\alpha
agree.)

Conversely, suppose
D
has
G\alpha
-measure
0
. By
[
5
, Proposition 5.25]
,
f\in RDS_{G\alpha}[a,b]
. By Theorem
7.8
,
f\in RDS_{S_{L}\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{S_{R}\alpha}[a,b]
. Hence, by Theorem
5.6
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

Now suppose that
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
. If
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, then by Theorem
4.16
,
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. From the previous case,
D
has
G(P\alpha)
-measure
0
and
G(N\alpha)
-measure
0
. The converse follows since this argument is reversible.
∎

We conclude this section by proving a generalization of integration by parts. As with the DS-integral (see
[
5
, Theorem 5.3]
), the classical formula for integration by parts need not hold. Fix
c,\,d\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in(a,b)
; then we have that (see Lemma
3.5
)

\int_{a}^{b}H_{c}(x-t)\,dH_{d}(x-t)+\int_{a}^{b}H_{d}(x-t)\,dH_{c}(x-t)=c+d;

On the other hand,
H_{d}(b)H_{c}(b)-H_{d}(a)H_{c}(a)=1
. Consequently, we need to introduce a term,
A(t)
, into the formula for integration by parts that compensates for a common discontinuity at a point
t
. This term is the same as the the error term introduced in
[
4
, Theorem 6.2.2]
for the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral.

Theorem 7.16
.

Fix
\alpha,\beta\in BV[a,b]
. If
S
is the set of common discontinuities of
\alpha
and
\beta
, then

\int_{a}^{b}\alpha(x)\,d\beta+\int_{a}^{b}\beta(x)\,d\alpha=\alpha(b)\beta(b)-\alpha(a)\beta(a)+\sum_{t\in S}A_{\alpha,\beta}(t),

where

A_{\alpha,\beta}(t)=[\alpha(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha(t+)+\alpha(t-))]\mu_{\beta}(\{t\})+[\beta(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}(\beta(t+)+\beta(t-))]\mu_{\alpha}(\{t\}).

Remark 7.17
.

Arguing as in
[
5
, Lemma 5.2]
, we see that
A(t)=0
if the discontinuities of
\alpha
and
\beta
at
t
are balanced; that is,

\alpha(t)=\frac{\alpha(t-)+\alpha(t+)}{2},\qquad\beta(t)=\frac{\beta(t-)+\beta(t+)}{2}.

The proof of Theorem
7.16
requires three lemmas whose proofs we defer until after the main proof.

Lemma 7.18
.

Given
\alpha,\beta\in BV[a,b]
, with reduced saltus decompositions

\alpha=G\alpha+S_{L}\alpha+S_{R}\alpha,\quad\beta=G\beta+S_{L}\beta+S_{R}\beta,

we have that

A_{\alpha,\beta}(t)=A_{S_{L}\alpha,S_{L}\beta}(t)+A_{S_{R}\alpha,S_{R}\beta}(t).

Lemma 7.19
.

Given
\alpha,\beta\in BV[a,b]
, if
\alpha
is continuous and
\beta
is a reduced, left or right continuous saltus function, then

\int_{a}^{b}\alpha(x)\,d\beta+\int_{a}^{b}\beta(x)\,d\alpha=\alpha(b)\beta(b)-\alpha(a)\beta(a).

Lemma 7.20
.

Given
\alpha,\beta\in BV[a,b]
, suppose they are both reduced, left or right continuous saltus functions. If
S
is the set of common discontinuities of
\alpha
and
\beta
, then

\int_{a}^{b}\alpha(x)\,d\beta+\int_{a}^{b}\beta(x)\,d\alpha=\alpha(b)\beta(b)-\alpha(a)\beta(a)+\sum_{t\in S}A_{\alpha,\beta}(t).

Proof of Theorem
7.16
.

We first consider three special cases: (i) both
\alpha,\beta
are continuous; (ii)
\alpha
is continuous,
\beta
is a reduced, left or right continuous saltus function; (iii) both
\alpha,\beta
are reduced, left or right continuous saltus functions. In Case (i), since both
\alpha
and
\beta
are continuous, by Theorems
4.17
and
4.18
the RDS and DS-integrals agree, and the desired identity follows from
[
5
, Proposition 5.5]
. Case (ii) follows from Lemma
7.19
. Case (iii) follows from Lemma
7.20
.

We now argue in general. Fix
\alpha,\,\beta\in BV[a,b]
.
By Proposition
7.3
, we have the saltus decompositions

\alpha=G\alpha+S_{L}\alpha+S_{R}\alpha,\quad\beta=G\beta+S_{L}\beta+S_{R}\beta,

where the saltus functions are reduced. Then by Theorems
5.1
and
5.6
, and by Lemma
7.18
we can decompose the integrals and argue as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\alpha(x)\,d\beta+\int_{a}^{b}\beta(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}G\alpha(x)+S_{L}\alpha(x)+S_{R}\alpha(x)\,d[G\beta+S_{L}\beta+S_{R}\beta]

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{a}^{b}G\beta(x)+S_{L}\beta(x)+S_{R}\beta(x)\,d[G\alpha+S_{L}\alpha+S_{R}\alpha]

\displaystyle=\alpha(b)\beta(b)-\alpha(a)\beta(a)+\sum_{t\in S}A_{S_{L}\alpha,S_{L}\beta}(t)+\sum_{t\in S}A_{S_{R}\alpha,S_{R}\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=\alpha(b)\beta(b)-\alpha(a)\beta(a)+\sum_{t\in S}A_{\alpha,\beta}(t),

where to get the term
\alpha(b)\beta(b)-\alpha(a)\beta(a)
we have used the saltus decomposition to recombine terms gotten from Cases (i) and (ii).
∎

Proof of Lemma
7.18
.

Observe that

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}(\{t\})=\alpha(t+)-\alpha(t-)

\displaystyle=G\alpha(t)+S_{L}\alpha(t+)+S_{R}\alpha(t)

\displaystyle\quad-G\alpha(t)-S_{L}\alpha(t)-S_{R}\alpha(t-)

\displaystyle=\mu_{S_{L}\alpha}(\{t\})+\mu_{S_{R}\alpha}(\{t\}).

Hence,

\displaystyle A_{\alpha,\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=[\alpha(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha(t+)+\alpha(t-))]\mu_{\beta}(\{t\})+[\beta(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}(\beta(t+)+\beta(t-))]\mu_{\alpha}(\{t\})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}[S_{L}\alpha(t)+S_{R}\alpha(t)-S_{L}\alpha(t+)-S_{R}\alpha(t-)][\mu_{S_{L}\beta}(\{t\})+\mu_{S_{R}\beta}(\{t\})]

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}[S_{L}\beta(t)+S_{R}\beta(t)-S_{L}\beta(t+)-S_{R}\beta(t-)][\mu_{S_{L}\alpha}(\{t\})+\mu_{S_{R}\alpha}(\{t\})]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}[\mu_{S_{R}\alpha}(\{t\})-\mu_{S_{L}\alpha}(\{t\})][\mu_{S_{L}\beta}(\{t\})+\mu_{S_{R}\beta}(\{t\})]

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}[\mu_{S_{R}\beta}(\{t\})-\mu_{S_{L}\beta}(\{t\})][\mu_{S_{L}\alpha}(\{t\})+\mu_{S_{R}\alpha}(\{t\})]

\displaystyle=\mu_{S_{R}\alpha}(\{t\})\mu_{S_{R}\beta}(\{t\})-\mu_{S_{L}\alpha}(\{t\})\mu_{S_{L}\beta}(\{t\})

\displaystyle=A_{S_{R}\alpha,S_{R}\beta}(t)+A_{S_{L}\alpha,S_{L}\beta}(t).

∎

Proof of Lemma
7.19
.

Let
\{(x_{i},b_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be the saltus set associated to
\beta
;
then

\beta(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}H_{c}(x-x_{i}),

where
c\in\{0,1\}
. By Theorem
7.8
,

\int_{a}^{b}\alpha(x)\,d\beta=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}\alpha(x_{i}).

Let
\beta_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}H_{c}(x-x_{i})
. By
[
5
, Lemma 3.36]
,
\beta_{n}\to\beta
uniformly. By Theorem
6.1
,

\int_{a}^{b}\beta(x)\,d\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}\beta_{n}(x)\,d\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\int_{a}^{b}H_{c}(x-x_{i})\,d\alpha\\
=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(\alpha(b)-\alpha(x_{i}))=\alpha(b)\beta(b)-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}\alpha(x_{i}).

By Proposition
7.3
,
\beta(a)=0
. Hence,

\int_{a}^{b}\alpha(x)\,d\beta+\int_{a}^{b}\beta(x)\,d\alpha=\alpha(b)\beta(b)=\alpha(b)\beta(b)-\alpha(a)\beta(a).

∎

Proof of Lemma
7.20
.

Let

\alpha(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}H_{c}(x-x_{i}),\quad\beta(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}H_{d}(x-t_{j}).

First, suppose
c=d=0
. Then by left continuity,

A(t)=-(\alpha(t+)-\alpha(t-))(\beta(t+)-\beta(t-)).

By Theorem
7.8
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\alpha(x)\,d\beta+\int_{a}^{b}\beta(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}\alpha(t_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\beta(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}\sum_{i\;:\;x_{i}<t_{j}}a_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\sum_{j\;:\;t_{j}<x_{i}}b_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j\;:\;t_{j}>x_{i}}a_{i}b_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j\;:\;t_{j}<x_{i}}a_{i}b_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j\;:\;t_{j}\neq x_{i}}a_{i}b_{j}.

Now fix
i\in\mathbb{N}
. If
x_{i}\not\in\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{j\;:\;t_{j}\neq x_{i}}a_{i}b_{j}

\displaystyle=a_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}=a_{i}\beta(b).

Next, suppose
x_{i}\in\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
. That is, there exists
j_{0}
such that
x_{i}=t_{j_{0}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\;:\;t_{j}\neq x_{i}}a_{i}b_{j}

\displaystyle=a_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}-a_{i}b_{j_{0}}=a_{i}\beta(b)-a_{i}b_{j_{0}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\alpha(x)\,d\beta+\int_{a}^{b}\beta(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\alpha(b)\beta(b)-\sum_{t\in S}(\alpha(t+)-\alpha(t-))(\beta(t+)-\beta(t-))

\displaystyle=\alpha(b)\beta(b)-\alpha(a)\beta(a)+\sum_{t\in S}A_{\alpha,\beta}(t)

The case
c=d=1
follows similarly as the previous case.

Finally, suppose
c=0,d=1
. Observe that
A_{\alpha,\beta}(t)=0
. Then, by Theorem
7.8
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\alpha(x)\,d\beta+\int_{a}^{b}\beta(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}\alpha(t_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\beta(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}\sum_{i\;:\;x_{i}<t_{j}}a_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\sum_{j\;:\;t_{j}\leq x_{i}}b_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j\;:\;t_{j}>x_{i}}a_{i}b_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j\;:\;t_{j}\leq x_{i}}a_{i}b_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{i}b_{j}

\displaystyle=\alpha(b)\beta(b)-\alpha(a)\beta(b).

∎

8.
The Bounded Convergence Theorem

In this section, we prove the Bounded Convergence Theorem for the RDS integral. As we noted before, this theorem is not true for the interior DS-integral defined in
[
5
]
unless
\alpha
is continuous; our new definition therefore overcomes this technical problem. For the convenience of the reader we restate it from Section
1
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and a uniformly bounded sequence of functions
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
on
[a,b]
, if

•

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{n}\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
,

•

f_{n}\to f
pointwise as
n\to\infty
,

•

f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
,

then

(8.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Our proof of Theorem
8.1
is modeled on the proof of the bounded convergence theorem for the Riemann integral due to Luxemburg
[
12
]
. We have organized our proof as follows. In the main part of the proof, we show that we can reduce to the case when our sequence of functions
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is nonnegative and decreasing, and the integrator
\alpha
is continuous; this special case is given in Lemma
8.2
. To prove this lemma we need to approximate this decreasing sequence by a sequence of continuous functions; this is done in Lemma
8.3
. Given this lemma, to prove Lemma
8.2
we apply Dini’s Theorem to get uniform convergence, and finally use Theorem
6.1
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
continuous and a sequence of functions
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
on
[a,b]
, if

•

\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is pointwise decreasing,

•

f_{n}(x)\geq 0
for every
x\in[a,b]
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

f_{n}\to 0
pointwise as
n\to\infty
,

then

\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{\alpha}(f_{n},[a,b])=0.

We defer the proof of Lemma
8.2
to below.

Proof of Theorem
8.1
.

Suppose first that
\alpha\in I[a,b]
. We will first prove the theorem for the special case when
f_{n}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in[a,b],

n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
f_{n}\to 0
pointwise. By Proposition
7.3
, we can decompose

\alpha=G\alpha+S_{L}\alpha+S_{R}\alpha

where
G\alpha
is continuous and
S_{L}\alpha
and
S_{R}\alpha
are reduced saltus functions.
By Theorem
7.8
and Lemma
7.15
, we have
f_{n}\in RDS_{G\alpha}[a,b]
,
f_{n}\in RDS_{S_{L}\alpha}[a,b]
and
f_{n}\in RDS_{S_{R}\alpha}[a,b]
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By Theorem
5.6
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left[\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,dG\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,dS_{L}\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,dS_{R}\alpha\right],

and so to prove (
8.1
) it will suffice to show that each of the integrals on the righthand side converges to
0
and
n\rightarrow\infty
.

We first consider
G\alpha
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
p_{n}=\sup_{k\geq n}f_{k}.
Then,
0\leq f_{n}(x)\leq p_{n}(x)
for
x\in[a,b]
and
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
decreases pointwise. In particular, for
x\in[a,b]
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{n}(x)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x)=0.

Hence, by Lemma
8.2
,

0\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,dG\alpha\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,dG\alpha\\
=\limsup_{n\to\infty}L_{G\alpha}(f_{n},[a,b])\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}L_{G\alpha}(p_{n},[a,b])=\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{G\alpha}(p_{n},[a,b])=0.

Therefore,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,dG\alpha=0.

We next consider
S_{L}\alpha
. Let
\{(x_{i},a_{i})\}
be the saltus set for
S_{L}\alpha
. Then we can write

S_{L}\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}H_{c}(x-x_{i})
where
c=0
.
By Lemma
7.8
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,dS_{L}\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}f_{n}(x_{i}).

Let
M
be the uniform bound for
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. By Definition
7.2
,

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|a_{i}||f_{n}(x_{i})|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}M|a_{i}|<\infty.

Thus, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}f_{n}(x_{i})

converges absolutely.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
be such that

\sum_{i=K+1}^{\infty}M|a_{i}|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

For each
1\leq i\leq K
, let
N_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that for
n\geq N_{i}
,
f_{n}(x_{i})<\frac{\epsilon}{2|a_{i}|K}.
If we set
N=\max_{1\leq i\leq K}\{N_{i}\}
, then for
n\geq N
,

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}f_{n}(x_{i})\right|\leq\left|\sum_{i=1}^{K}a_{i}f_{n}(x_{i})\right|+\left|\sum_{i=K+1}^{\infty}a_{i}f_{n}(x_{i})\right|\\
\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}|a_{i}|\frac{\epsilon}{2|a_{i}|K}+\sum_{i=K+1}^{\infty}M|a_{i}|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon.

Hence,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,dS_{L}\alpha=0.

Finally, for
S_{R}\alpha
, we can apply the same argument with
c=1
to get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,dS_{R}\alpha=0.

This completes the proof of this special case.

Now we will prove the theorem for the general case of
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. Define for each
n\in\mathbb{N},

g_{n}(x)=|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|.

Note that
g_{n}\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and
\{g_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a uniformly bounded sequence of functions. By the previous case,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|\,d\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}g_{n}(x)\,d\alpha=0.

By Lemma
5.11
,

0\leq\left|\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)-f(x)\,d\alpha\right|\leq\int_{a}^{b}|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|\,d\alpha,

and so, by the squeeze theorem,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)-f(x)\,d\alpha=0,

which yields (
8.1
).

Finally, we prove the theorem for
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
. By Theorem
4.16
,
f_{n}\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
. Therefore, by the previous case applied to
P\alpha
and
N\alpha
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,d\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigg(\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,dP\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,dN\alpha\bigg)\\
=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dP\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dN\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

∎

To prove Lemma
8.2
we need another lemma, whose proof we give below.

Lemma 8.3
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
continuous and
f\in B[a,b]
nonnegative, for each
\epsilon>0
, there exists
g\in C[a,b]
such that
0\leq g(x)\leq f(x)
for every
x\in[a,b]
and

L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])<\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\,d\alpha+\epsilon.

Proof of Lemma
8.2
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. By Lemma
8.3
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a continuous function
g_{n}
such that
0\leq g_{n}(x)\leq f_{n}(x)
for
x\in[a,b]
and

(8.2)

L_{\alpha}(f_{n},[a,b])<\int_{a}^{b}g_{n}(x)\,d\alpha+\frac{\epsilon}{2^{n}}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
h_{n}=\min\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}
. Then,
0\leq h_{n}\leq g_{n}\leq f_{n}
and
h_{n}\in C[a,b]
. Thus,
\{h_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
decreases to
0
pointwise. Therefore, by Dini’s Theorem
[
2
, Exercise 24.H]
, the sequence
\{h_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges uniformly to
0
on
[a,b]
. Hence, by Theorem
6.1
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}h_{n}(x)\,d\alpha=0.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in[a,b]
. Then,
h_{n}(x)=g_{j}(x)
for some
1\leq j\leq n
. Thus,

(8.3)

\displaystyle h_{n}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\max\{g_{i}(x),\dots,g_{n}(x)\}-g_{i}(x)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=g_{j}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\max\{g_{i}(x),\dots,g_{n}(x)\}-g_{i}(x))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\geq g_{j}(x)+\max\{g_{j}(x),\dots,g_{n}(x)\}-g_{j}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\max\{g_{j}(x),\dots,g_{n}(x)\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\geq g_{n}(x).\

Since
x
is arbitrary, this is true for all
x\in[a,b]
.

For
1\leq i\leq n
,
\max\{g_{i},\dots,g_{n}\}
is continuous, so by Theorem
7.1
,

\max\{g_{i},\dots,g_{n}\}\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Moreover, since
\max\{g_{i},\dots,g_{n}\}\leq\max\{f_{i},\dots,f_{n}\}=f_{i}
as
\{f_{i}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is pointwise decreasing, for
1\leq i\leq n
we have that

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}(f_{i},[a,b])

\displaystyle\geq L_{\alpha}(\max\{g_{i},\dots,g_{n}\},[a,b])

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\max\{g_{i}(x),\dots,g_{n}(x)\}\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\max\{g_{i}(x),\dots,g_{n}(x)\}-g_{i}(x)\,d\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}g_{i}(x)\,d\alpha.

If we combine this estimate with (
8.2
), we get

(8.4)

\int_{a}^{b}\max\{g_{i}(x),\dots,g_{n}(x)\}-g_{i}(x)\,d\alpha\leq L_{\alpha}(f_{i},[a,b])-\int_{a}^{b}g_{i}(x)\,d\alpha<\frac{\epsilon}{2^{i}}.

Therefore, by Theorem
5.5
and by (
8.3
) and (
8.4
),

\displaystyle 0\leq L_{\alpha}(f_{n},[a,b])

\displaystyle<\frac{\epsilon}{2^{n}}+\int_{a}^{b}g_{n}(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2^{n}}+\int_{a}^{b}h_{n}(x)\,d\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{a}^{b}\max\{g_{i}(x),\dots,g_{n}(x)\}-g_{i}(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2^{n}}+\int_{a}^{b}h_{n}(x)\,d\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\epsilon}{2^{i}}

\displaystyle=\epsilon+\int_{a}^{b}h_{n}(x)\,d\alpha.

Thus, for every
\epsilon>0
,

0\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}L_{\alpha}(f_{n},[a,b])\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}L_{\alpha}(f_{n},[a,b])\leq\epsilon+\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}h_{n}(x)\,d\alpha=\epsilon,

which gives

\limsup_{n\to\infty}L_{\alpha}(f_{n},[a,b])=\limsup_{n\to\infty}L_{\alpha}(f_{n},[a,b])=0.

Hence,

\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{\alpha}(f_{n},[a,b])=0.

∎

Proof of Lemma
8.3
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. By Definition
4.1
, there exists a step function
v(x)\leq f(x)
defined with respect to the partition
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
such that
v(x)=c_{i}
for
x\in I_{i}
and

L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])\leq\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha+\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Without loss of generality, by Corollary
4.6
we may assume that
v(x)\geq 0
for
x\in[a,b]
. Let
M=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\{c_{i}\}+1
. Since
\alpha
is continuous on
[a,b]
, it is uniformly continuous on
[a,b]
. Thus, there exists a
\delta_{1}>0
such that for every
x,y\in[a,b]
with
|x-y|<\delta_{1}
,

|\alpha(x)-\alpha(y)|<\frac{\epsilon}{4MN}.

Let
\delta=\min(\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1},\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\{\frac{x_{i}-x_{i-1}}{4}\}).

We now define the function
g
on
[a,b]
by redefining
f
in each neighborhood
(x_{i}-\delta,x_{i}+\delta)
,
1\leq i\leq n-1
, if
x_{i}
is a discontinuity of
f
. (If
x_{0}
or
x_{n}
is a discontinuity, this argument immediately adapts to the intervals
[x_{0},x_{0}+\delta)
,
(x_{n}-\delta,x_{n}]
.) For each
i
, if
v(x_{i})\leq\min\{c_{i},c_{i+1}\}
, let
g
be the linear function such that
g(x_{i}-\delta)=c_{i},g(x_{i})=v(x_{i}),g(x_{i}+\delta)=c_{i+1}
. On the other hand, if
v(x_{i})>\min\{c_{i},c_{i+1}\}
, let
g
be the piecewise linear function with
g(x_{i}-\delta)=c_{i},g(x_{i})=\min\{c_{i},c_{i+1}\},g(x_{i}+\delta)=c_{i+1}
. Since the pieces of
g
are continuous and agree at the endpoints,
g
is continuous. Moreover,

0\leq g(x)\leq v(x)\leq f(x).

We now estimate as follows: since
\alpha
is continuous and increasing, by the definition of the RDS-integral of a step function,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_{i}}g(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{x_{i-1}+\delta}^{x_{i}-\delta}g(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}((x_{i-1}+\delta,x_{i}-\delta))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}((x_{i-1},x_{i}))-\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}[\mu_{\alpha}((x_{i-1},x_{i-1}+\delta])+\mu_{\alpha}([x_{i}-\delta,x_{i}))]

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha-\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}[|\alpha(x_{i-1}+\delta)-\alpha(x_{i-1})|+|\alpha(x_{i})-\alpha(x_{i}-\delta)|]

\displaystyle>\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha-\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\frac{\epsilon}{2NM}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha-\sum_{i=1}^{N}M\frac{\epsilon}{2NM}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha-\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Therefore, we have

L_{\alpha}(f,[a,b])\leq\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha+\frac{\epsilon}{2}<\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\,d\alpha+\epsilon.

∎

9.
RDS Integral and Related Integrals

In this section we continue our discussion of alternative definitions of the Stieltjes integral, begun in Section
1
, and compare them to the RDS-integral. As we showed above in Corollary
4.8
, our definition of the RDS-integral is equivalent to the original definition by Ross, Definition
1.3
. Further, as shown in
[
5
]
, the interior DS-integral is more general than the Riemann-Stieltjes and exterior Darboux-Stieltjes integrals, and agrees with them when they exist. Therefore, our first step is to characterize when the RDS-integral and the interior DS-integral agree. We began this in Theorems
4.17
and
4.18
, but here we will use Theorem
7.13
to give a complete characterization. Then we will define Riemann and Pollard-type integrals corresponding to the RDS-integral. The Pollard-type integral is equivalent to the RDS-integral while the Riemann-type integral is still weaker. We will also briefly consider a variant of the RS-integral introduced by Ross that is equivalent to the RDS-integral. Finally, we show the RDS-integral agrees with the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral whenever the former is defined.

To compare the RDS-integral and the interior DS-integral, we will use the following characterization of interior DS-integrability proved in
[
5
, Theorem 5.19]
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
,
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if:

(1)

the subset of
[a,b]
where
f
is discontinuous has
G\alpha
-measure
0
, where
G\alpha
is the continous part of the saltus decomposition of
\alpha
;

(2)

If
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is the set of discontinuities of
\alpha
, then for each
i
,

(a)

\alpha
is right continuous at
x_{i}
or
f(x_{i}+)
exists, and

(b)

\alpha
is left continuous at
x_{i}
or
f(x_{i}-)
exists.

As an immediate consequence of Theorems
7.13
and
9.1
, we have the following generalization of Theorems
4.17
and
4.18
. Note that the assumption that
f
and
\alpha
have no common discontinuities is necessary for the RS-integral to exist and be equal to the DS-integral, and so this is a natural sufficient condition to consider.

Theorem 9.2
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
, suppose
f
and
\alpha
have no common discontinuities. Then
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Moreover,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

Since
f
and
\alpha
have no common discontinuities,
f(x\pm)=f(x)
at each discontinuity
x
of
\alpha
, so the second condition in Theorem
9.1
is automatically fulfilled. By Remark
7.12
, since
G\alpha
is continuous, the definition of
G\alpha
-measure
0
used in Theorem
7.13
agrees with that used in Theorem
7.13
. Hence,
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

We now prove that the two integrals agree. By Proposition
7.3
we have the reduced saltus decomposition

\alpha=G\alpha+S_{L}\alpha+S_{R}\alpha
. Let
\{(x_{i},a_{i})\}
be the reduced saltus set for
S_{L}\alpha
and
\{(y_{i},b_{i})\}
be the reduced saltus set for
S_{R}\alpha
. By Theorems
5.6
,
4.17
, and
7.8
we have

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dG\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dS_{R}\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dS_{L}\alpha

\displaystyle=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dG\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}f(x_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}f(y_{i})

\displaystyle=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dG\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}f(x_{i}+)+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}f(y_{i}-);

by the corresponding results for the DS-integral,
[
5
, Proposition 5.9]
and
[
5
, Theorem 4.30c]
,

\displaystyle=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dG\alpha+(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dS_{R}\alpha+(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dS_{L}\alpha

\displaystyle=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

∎

Arguing as we did in the proof of Theorem
9.2
, it is clear from Theorems
7.13
and
9.1
that if a function
f
is interior DS-integrable, it is RDS-integrable. However, as we showed in Lemma
3.5
, the two integrals may not agree if they share a common discontinuity. By carefully considering the additional continuity conditions in the second part of Theorem
9.1
, we can give a complete characterization of when the two integrals agree in terms of a condition reminiscent of the error term introduced for integration by parts, Theorem
7.16
.

Theorem 9.3
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
, let
G\alpha+S_{L}\alpha+S_{R}\alpha
be its reduced saltus decomposition, with
\{(x_{i},a_{i})\}
and
\{(y_{i},b_{i})\}
the reduced saltus sets for
S_{L}\alpha
and
S_{R}\alpha
.
Then for any
f\in B[a,b]
,
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and

(9.1)

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha

if and only if
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
,
f(x_{i}+)
and
f(y_{i}-)
exist for all
i
and

(9.2)

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}[f(x_{i})-f(x_{i}+)]+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}[f(y_{i})-f(y_{i}-)]=0.

Proof.

First suppose
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
(and so
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
) and

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

We will prove that (
9.2
) holds. By
[
5
, Proposition 4.22]
,
f\in DS_{S_{L}\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in DS_{S_{R}\alpha}[a,b]
. Then, by Theorem
9.1
,
S_{L}\alpha
is not right continuous for any
x_{i}
and so
f(x_{i}+)
exists. Similarly,
S_{R}\alpha
is not left continuous for any
y_{i}
and so
f(y_{i}-)
exists. Further, if we apply Theorem
5.6
and
[
5
, Theorem 4.30c]
to each side of (
9.1
), we get that

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dG\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dS_{L}\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dS_{R}\alpha\\
=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dG\alpha+(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dS_{L}\alpha+(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dS_{R}\alpha.

Moreover, by Theorem
4.17
, we have

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dG\alpha=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dG\alpha.

Therefore, by Theorem
7.8
and
[
5
, Proposition 5.9]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}f(x_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}f(y_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dS_{L}\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dS_{R}\alpha

\displaystyle=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dS_{L}\alpha+(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dS_{R}\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}f(x_{i}+)+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}f(y_{i}-).

If we rearrange terms, we get (
9.2
).

Now suppose that
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
,
f(x_{i}+)
and
f(y_{i}-)
exist, and (
9.2
) holds. Then, by Theorem
9.1
,
f\in DS_{S_{L}\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in DS_{S_{R}\alpha}[a,b]
. Moreover, since
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, by Lemma
7.15
,
f\in RDS_{G\alpha}[a,b]
and so by Theorem
4.17
,
f\in DS_{G\alpha}[a,b]
. Therefore, by
[
5
, Theorem 4.30c]
,
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Finally, if we argue exactly as we did at the end of the proof of Theorem
9.2
, we have that (
9.1
) holds.
∎

The hypothesis (
9.2
) in Theorem
9.3
is often not easy to check. However, as a corollary we give a practical sufficient condition that is weaker than that in Theorem
9.2
. In particular, if
\alpha
is not discontinuous at the endpoints of
[a,b]
, then by Corollary
7.10
we may assume that
\alpha
is either left or right continuous, depending on
f
.

Corollary 9.4
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
, suppose for every common point of discontinuity, either:

(1)

\alpha
is right continuous and
f
is left continuous, or

(2)

\alpha
is left continuous and
f
is right continuous.

Then
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Moreover,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(DS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

First given our hypothesis on
f
and
\alpha
, by Theorems
7.13
and
9.1
, and arguing as we did in the proof of Theorem
9.2
we have that
f\in DS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

It remains to show that integrals are equal.
Let
\{(x_{i},a_{i})\}
be the reduced saltus set for
S_{L}\alpha
and
\{(y_{i},b_{i})\}
be the reduced saltus set for
S_{R}\alpha
. We will show that (
9.2
) holds by showing
f(x_{i}+)=f(x_{i})
and
f(y_{i}-)=f(y_{i})
for all

. If
x_{i}
is a common point of discontinuity between
f
and
\alpha
, then
\alpha
is not right continuous at
x_{i}
as it is in the saltus set for
S_{L}\alpha
. Thus, by assumption,
\alpha
is left continuous and
f
is right continuous; that is,
f(x_{i}+)=f(x_{i})
. Otherwise, if
x_{i}
is not a common point of discontinuity, then
f
must be continuous at
x_{i}
and again
f(x_{i}+)=f(x_{i})
. A similar argument shows that
f(y_{i}-)=f(y_{i})
for all

.
∎

We now consider Riemann-type integral corresponding to the RDS-integral. In our definition we will follow the approach used in
[
5
, Sections 2.2 and 5.3]
. We will reinterpret the Riemann sums used to define the RS-integral (see Definition
1.1
) as the integral of a step function from a restricted class we call Riemann step functions However, for our definition we will use the RDS-integral of the step function, resulting in a more generalized Riemann sum.

Definition 9.5
.

A tagged partition
\mathcal{P}^{*}
of
[a,b]
is a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
of
[a,b]
together with a collection of sample points
\{x_{i}^{*}\}_{i=0}^{n}
where
x_{i}^{*}\in\overline{I}_{i}.
Given
f\in B[a,b]
and a tagged partition
\mathcal{P}^{*}
of
[a,b]
, we say
r\in S[a,b]
is a Riemann step function of
f
with respect to
\mathcal{P}^{*}
if
r(x)=f(x_{i}^{*})
for
x\in I_{i}
and
f(x_{i})=f(x_{i})
for each
1\leq i\leq n
. The collection of all Riemann step functions of
f
defined with respect to all tagged partitions
\mathcal{P}^{*}
is denoted by
R^{*}(f,\mathcal{P})
.

Definition 9.6
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
, we say
f
is modified Riemann-Stieltjes integrable on
[a,b]
with respect to
\alpha
if there exists
A\in\mathbb{R}
such that for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
so that for any partition
\mathcal{P}
with
|\mathcal{P}|<\delta
and any
r\in R^{*}(f,\mathcal{P})
,

\left|\int_{a}^{b}r(x)\,d\alpha-A\right|<\epsilon.

In this case we define the value of the modified Riemann-Stieltjes integral of
f
with respect to
\alpha
by

(mRS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=A

and the collection of all such
f
by
mRS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

Remark 9.7
.

It might seem more natural to refer to this integral as the Ross-Riemann-Stieltjes integral or the RRS-integral. However, we have reserved that notation for Riemann-type integral introduced by Ross: see Definition
9.13
below.

However, as is the case with the RS and DS-integrals, this definition does not agree with the definition of the RDS-integral, as the set of integrable functions is smaller. In fact, we can use the same example as for those integrals: see
[
5
, Example 5.30]
.

Example 9.8
.

Given
\alpha=H_{1}\in BV[-1,1]
, there exists a function
f
, namely
H_{1}
, where
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[-1,1]
while
f\not\in mRS_{\alpha}[-1,1]
.

Proof.

Since
H_{1}\in S[-1,1]
, we have by Proposition
4.3
that
H_{1}\in RDS_{\alpha}[-1,1].
To show that
H_{1}
is not mRS-integrable, fix
A\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\epsilon=\frac{1}{2}
. Fix
\delta>0
and let
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
be a partition such that
|\mathcal{P}|<\delta
and
0
is not a partition point. Then, there exists
1\leq i_{0}\leq n
where
0\in I_{i_{0}}
. Thus,
\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i_{0}})=1
while
\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})=0
for all
i\neq i_{0}
. Let
r,s\in R^{*}(f,\mathcal{P})
such that
x_{i_{0}}^{*}
lies in
(0,x_{i_{0}})
for
r
and
x_{i_{0}}^{*}
lies in
(x_{i_{0}-1},0)
for
s
. Hence,
r(x)=1
and
s(x)=0
for
x\in I_{i_{0}}.
Therefore,

\int_{-1}^{1}r(x)\,d\alpha=\sum_{i=0}^{n}r(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}r(x_{i}^{*})\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})=r(x_{i_{0}}^{*})=1,

and the same argument shows that

\int_{-1}^{1}s(x)\,d\alpha=0.

Thus,

1=\left|\int_{-1}^{1}r(x)\,d\alpha-\int_{-1}^{1}s(x)\,d\alpha\right|\leq\left|\int_{-1}^{1}r(x)\,d\alpha-A\right|+\left|\int_{-1}^{1}s(x)\,d\alpha-A\right|.

Hence, one of the terms on the right is at least
\epsilon=\frac{1}{2}
and so we have the desired step function in
R^{*}(f,\mathcal{P}).
Therefore,
H_{1}\not\in RS_{\alpha}[-1,1]
.
∎

Remark 9.9
.

We leave it as an open problem to determine if the mRS-integral and RDS-integrals agree whenever the former is defined. Moreover, there is also a modified definition for which the same questions can be asked. Since in Definition
9.5
the sample point can be taken as one of the endpoints of the partition interval, we might think of this as an "exterior" modified RS-integral. It is possible to define an "interior" modified RS-integral, by restricting the sample points to be in the interior of the partition interval. We leave it as on open problem to define and characterize the interior modified RS-integral and determine its relationship to the exterior version we define and to the RDS-integral. Similar questions exist for the RS and DS integrals: see
[
5
, Theorem 5.31, Exercises 5.20, 5.29]
.

As Example
9.8
makes clear, the problem arises because a common point of discontinuity can be an interior point of a partition interval, no matter how small the mesh size. As we noted in the introduction, to avoid this problem for the RS-integral, Pollard introduced the notion of "
\sigma
-convergence" which leads to a modified definition of the limit in the RS-integral. Here we show that the same technique can be used to define a Ross-Pollard-Stieltjes integral that is equivalent to the RDS-integral.

Definition 9.10
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
, we say
f
is Ross-Pollard-Stieltjes integrable on
[a,b]
with respect to
\alpha
if there exists
A\in\mathbb{R}
such that for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a partition
\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}
so that for any refinement
\mathcal{Q}
and any
r\in R^{*}(f,\mathcal{Q})
,

\left|\int_{a}^{b}r(x)\,d\alpha-A\right|<\epsilon.

In this case we define the value of the Ross-Pollard-Stieltjes integral of
f
with respect to
\alpha
by

(RPS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=A

and the collection of all such
f
by
RPS_{\alpha}[a,b]
.

For brevity, we will only prove that the RPS-integral and the RDS-integral are equivalent for
\alpha\in I[a,b]
. To prove the general case when
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
we would need to prove that the RPS-integral satisfied a canonical decomposition of the integrator analogous to Theorem
4.16
for the RDS integral. Such a result is true for the RS-integral: see
[
5
, Lemma 5.38]
.

Theorem 9.11
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if
f\in RPS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. In this case,

(9.3)

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(RPS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Proof.

If
\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])=0
, then
\alpha
is constant and so
f
is integrable in both definitions with common value is
0
. Indeed, by Lemma
4.9
, we have this for the RDS-integral. To see this for the RPS-integral, fix
\epsilon>0
and let
\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}
be the trivial partition. Then, for any refinement
\mathcal{Q}
and
r\in R^{*}(f,\mathcal{Q})
,

\left|\int_{a}^{b}r(x)\,d\alpha\right|=0<\epsilon.

Hence, without loss of generality,
\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])>0
.

Suppose first that
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. Let

A=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. By Definition
4.1
, there exists
u,\,v\in S[a,b]
which bracket
f
and

\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha<A+\epsilon,\quad\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha>A-\epsilon.

Without loss of generality, suppose
u,\,v
are step functions with respect to a common partition
\mathcal{P}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{P}
. Let
\mathcal{Q}
be any refinement of
\mathcal{P}
and fix any
r\in R^{*}(f,\mathcal{Q})
. Since
u,\,v
are also step functions with respect to
\mathcal{Q}
and bracket
f
we have that
v(x)\leq r(x)\leq u(x)
for all
x\in[a,b]
. Thus, by Theorem
3.10
,

A-\epsilon<\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha\leq\int_{a}^{b}r(x)\,d\alpha\leq\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha<A+\epsilon.

Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, by Definition
9.10
,
f\in RPS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and (
9.3
) holds.

Now suppose
f\in RPS_{\alpha}[a,b]
and let

A=(RPS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. By Definition
9.10
, there exists a partition
\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
such that for any refinement
\mathcal{Q}
of
\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}
and
r\in R^{*}(f,\mathcal{Q})
,

\left|\int_{a}^{b}r(x)\,d\alpha-A\right|<\frac{\epsilon}{4}.

It will actually be enough to consider
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}
. Denote the partition intervals of
\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}
by
I_{i}
. Let
c_{i}=\sup_{x\in\overline{I_{i}}}f(x)
and
d_{i}=\inf_{x\in\overline{I_{i}}}f(x)
. Since
f
is bounded, these are well-defined. Note that there exist
y_{i},z_{i}\in\overline{I_{i}}
such that

f(y_{i})+\frac{\epsilon}{4\cdot\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])2^{i}}>c_{i},\quad f(z_{i})-\frac{\epsilon}{4\cdot\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])2^{i}}<d_{i}.

Define

u(x)=\begin{cases}c_{i},&x\in I_{i},\\
f(x),&x=x_{i},\end{cases}\quad v(x)=\begin{cases}d_{i},&x\in I_{i},\\
f(x),&x=x_{i}.\end{cases}

Observe that
u,\,v
bracket
f
. Now define

r_{u}(x)=\begin{cases}f(y_{i}),&x\in I_{i},\\
f(x),&x=x_{i},\end{cases}\quad r_{v}(x)=\begin{cases}f(z_{i}),&x\in I_{i},\\
f(x),&x=x_{i}.\end{cases}

Notice that
r_{u},r_{v}\in R^{*}(f,\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon})
and

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}u(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle<\sum_{i=0}^{n}r_{u}(x_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(\{x_{i}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(y_{i})\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\epsilon}{4\cdot\mu_{\alpha}([a,b])2^{i}}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{a}^{b}r_{u}(x)\,d\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\epsilon}{4\cdot 2^{i}}

\displaystyle<\int_{a}^{b}r_{u}(x)\,d\alpha+\frac{\epsilon}{4}.

Similarly,

\int_{a}^{b}v(x)\,d\alpha>\int_{a}^{b}r_{v}(x)\,d\alpha-\frac{\epsilon}{4}.

Therefore,

\int_{a}^{b}u(x)-v(x)\,d\alpha<\int_{a}^{b}r_{u}(x)\,d\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}r_{v}(x)\,d\alpha+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\\
=\left[\int_{a}^{b}r_{u}(x)\,d\alpha-A\right]+\left[A-\int_{a}^{b}r_{v}(x)\,d\alpha\right]+\frac{\epsilon}{2}<\epsilon.

Hence, by Theorem
4.5
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
. By the previous argument, (
9.3
) holds.
∎

gave an alternative definition of a Riemann-type integral associated to the RDS-integral. His idea was to replace the mesh size of a partition with a mesh size defined using the

-lengths of the interval.

Definition 9.12

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
, the

-mesh size of a partition

|\mathcal{P}|_{\alpha}=\max_{i}\mu_{\alpha}(I_{i}).

Using this he defined what we refer to as the Ross-Riemann-Stieltjes integral, and showed that it was equivalent to the RDS-integral. We refer the reader to
[
19
]
for the proof.

Definition 9.13
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
, we say
f
is Ross-Riemann-Stieltjes integrable on
[a,b]
with respect to
\alpha
if there exists
A\in\mathbb{R}
such that for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
so that for any partition
\mathcal{P}
with
|\mathcal{P}|_{\alpha}<\delta
and any
r\in R^{*}(f,\mathcal{P})
,

\left|\int_{a}^{b}r(x)\,d\alpha-A\right|<\epsilon.

We define the value of the Ross-Riemann-Stieltjes integral of
f
with respect to
\alpha
by

(RRS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=A

and denote the collection of all such
f
by

.

Theorem 9.14
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
,
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
if and only if

. In this case,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=(RRS)\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Remark 9.15
.

In
[
19
]
, Ross only defined the
\alpha
-mesh size of a partition, and the RRS-integral for increasing
\alpha
. It is an open problem to extend his definition to
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and determine whether this more general definition is equivalent to the RDS-integral.

Finally, we will show that the RDS-integral agrees with the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral whenever the former is defined. We use the standard definition for the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral which can be found, for example, in Kamke
[
10
]
, Royden
[
20
]
, or Hewitt and Stromberg
[
7
]
. (See also Carter and van Brunt
[
4
]
,which uses an equivalent but nonstandard definition.) Recall that
\mu_{\alpha}
, as given in Definition
3.1
, is a premeasure on
[a,b]
. Therefore, by the Caratheodory Extension Theorem
[
20
, Section 12.2]
, there exists a regular Borel measure
\mu^{\alpha}
that agrees with
\mu_{\alpha}
on intervals. We will denote the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral associated to
\alpha
by

\int_{[a,b]}f(x)\,d\mu^{\alpha}.

Theorem 9.16
.

Given
\alpha\in BV[a,b]
and
f\in B[a,b]
, if
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, then
f
is Lebesgue-Stieltjes integrable on
[a,b]
and

(9.4)

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha=\int_{[a,b]}f(x)\,d\mu^{\alpha}.

To prove Theorem
9.16
we need one lemma whose proof is given below.

Lemma 9.17
.

Given
\alpha\in I[a,b]
, if
f\in RDS_{\alpha}[a,b]
, then there exist two sequences of step functions
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
\{v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
v_{n}(x)\leq v_{n+1}(x)\leq f(x)\leq u_{n+1}(x)\leq u_{n}(x)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in[a,b]
and

(9.5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}u_{n}\,d\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}v_{n}\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha.

Proof of Theorem
9.16
.

Since the premeasure of
\mu^{\alpha}
is
\mu_{\alpha}
, they agree on intervals, and so we immediately have that step functions are Lebesgue-Stieltjes integrable and their RDS and LS-integrals agree.

First, we consider the case
\alpha\in I[a,b]
and
f
is nonnegative. By Lemma
9.17
, there exists a decreasing sequence of step functions
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and an increasing sequence
\{v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
u_{n}
and
v_{n}
bracket
f
for all
n
and (
9.5
) holds. Since these sequences are monotone and bounded, we can define their pointwise limits

u(x)=\inf_{n}u_{n}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad v(x)=\sup_{n}v_{n}(x).

Note that
v(x)\leq f(x)\leq u(x)
and
u
and
v
are bounded on
[a,b]
. Thus, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem and the equality of the step function integrals,

\int_{[a,b]}v\,d\mu^{\alpha}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{[a,b]}v_{n}\,d\mu^{\alpha}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}v_{n}\,d\alpha=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha\\
=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{a}^{b}u_{n}\,d\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{[a,b]}u_{n}\,d\mu^{\alpha}=\int_{[a,b]}u\,d\mu^{\alpha}.

Since
u(x)-v(x)\geq 0
and their integrals agree, we have
u=v

\mu^{\alpha}
-a.e. Hence,
f=u

\mu^{\alpha}
-a.e., and so it is Lebesgue-Stieltjes integrable and (
9.4
) holds.

We next consider the general case. By Theorem
4.16
,
f\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. By Lemma
5.9
,
f^{\pm}\in RDS_{P\alpha}[a,b]
and
f^{\pm}\in RDS_{N\alpha}[a,b]
. But by the previous case,
f^{\pm}
are LS-integrable with respect to
\mu^{P\alpha}
and
\mu^{N\alpha}
and their LS and RDS-integrals agree. Further, since
\alpha=P\alpha-N\alpha+\alpha(a)
, by
[
10
, Theorem 9.1.0]
,
\mu^{\alpha}=\mu^{P\alpha}-\mu^{N\alpha}
. Therefore, we can estimate as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{a}^{b}f^{+}(x)\,dP\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f^{+}(x)\,dN\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f^{-}(x)\,dP\alpha+\int_{a}^{b}f^{-}(x)\,dN\alpha

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{[a,b]}f^{+}(x)\,d\mu^{P\alpha}-\int_{[a,b]}f^{+}(x)\,d\mu^{N\alpha}-\int_{[a,b]}f^{-}(x)\,d\mu^{P\alpha}+\int_{[a,b]}f^{-}(x)\,d\mu^{N\alpha}

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{[a,b]}f(x)\,d\mu^{P\alpha}-\int_{[a,b]}f(x)\,d\mu^{N\alpha}

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{[a,b]}f(x)\,d\mu^{\alpha}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
9.17
.

We will construct the decreasing sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
; the construction of the increasing sequence
\{v_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is nearly identical. By Definition
4.1
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a step function
u^{\prime}_{n}
, defined with respect to a partition
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, such that
u^{\prime}_{n}(x)\geq f(x)
for all
x\in[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}u^{\prime}_{n}(x)\,d\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d\alpha<\frac{1}{n}.

Further, we may assume without loss of generality that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Let
u_{n}
be the best fit step function of
f
with respect to
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Since
\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
,
f(x)\leq u_{n+1}(x)\leq u_{n}(x)
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in[a,b]
. Finally,

0\leq\int_{a}^{b}u_{n}(x)\ d\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\ d\alpha\leq\int_{a}^{b}u^{\prime}_{n}(x)\ d\alpha-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\ d\alpha<\frac{1}{n}.

Therefore, the limit for
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in (
9.5
) holds.
∎
