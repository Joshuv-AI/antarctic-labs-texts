Three applications of coverings to difference patterns
Thomas Karam

Abstract
We show that a conceptually simple covering technique has surprisingly rich applications to density theorems and conjectures on patterns in sets involving set differences. These applications fall into three categories: (i) analogues of these statements to distance 2 2 versions of the pattern, (ii) reduction of these statements to relative versions, and (iii) reductions of these statements to a quasirandom case with respect to some quantities that affect the number of realisations of the pattern.

1.1
Background on patterns in set systems

Ramsey theory largely consists in statements broadly asserting that if a subcollection of a collection of objects is large enough, then we can find some configuration of elements (usually some pair of elements, or some
k
-tuple of elements for some fixed
k
) of the subcollection that satisfies some desired property. A famous example is Ramsey’s theorem (
[
14
]
, discovered in 1930) which says that if
k
is an integer and a large enough complete graph
K_{n}
has its edges each coloured in red or blue then it must eventually contain a monochromatic complete graph
K_{k}
(see
[
4
]
for a recent breakthrough of Campos, Griffiths, Morris and Sahasrabudhe on the upper bound on the smallest such
n
, and
[
10
]
for a further quantitative improvement by Gupta, Ndiaye, Norin and Wei). Within these statements, a large class of results is known as that of density theorems. There, the assumptions are that the subcollection contains at least a fixed positive proportion of the objects of the ambient collection, and then that the size of the whole collection is large enough depending on this proportion. One of the celebrated such statements is Szemerédi’s theorem
[
17
]
first proved in 1975.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k
be a positive integer and let
\delta>0
. If
n
is large enough depending on
k,\delta
only, then every subset
A\subset[n]
with size at least
\delta n
contains an arithmetic progression of length
k
.

The present paper will focus on density theorems as opposed to colouring theorems. For instance, Szemerédi’s theorem is viewed as the density version of van der Waerden’s theorem
[
18
]
, which states that if
r,k
are positive integers then any colouring of the integers with
r
colours contains an arithmetic progression with length
k
. Conversely, van der Waerden’s theorem is viewed as the colouring version of Szemerédi’s theorem. For any positive integer
n
, colouring the integers of
[n]
with
r
colours ensures in particular that one colour is used for at least a proportion
1/r
of these integers, and Szemerédi’s theorem hence implies van der Waerden’s theorem. More generally, the density version of a statement usually implies its colouring version for the same reason. In the converse direction however, the density version of a colouring result is not always true, as can be seen from several examples starting with Ramsey’s theorem itself. Indeed if
n
is an integer which for simplicity we require to be even, then the complete bipartite graph
K_{n/2,n/2}
contains
n^{2}/4
edges, so in particular at least a quarter of the total number of edges of
K_{n}
, but does not contain any triangle, and that remains true however large
n
is taken to be. As for the results for which the density version is true, the proof of the density version is usually much more involved than that of the colouring version. Again, this may already be seen to be the case with the proofs of Szemerédi’s theorem, compared to those of van der Waerden’s theorem.

Besides Szemerédi’s theorem, there are several density results which have received attention and for which the matter of the optimal bounds is an active research topic. For instance, obtaining reasonable bounds for a multidimensional version of Szemerédi’s theorem is still a wide open problem, and recent work of Peluse
[
12
]
establishes such bounds in a first special case of “
L
-shaped” configurations.

But the purely qualitative side of density theorems is also still far from completely understood. For instance, Gowers
[
7
]
describes the following conjecture (in its version with

Let

1.2
Main results

This paper will primarily be focused around a covering argument which allows us to obtain surprisingly diverse conclusions related to the last few conjectures, and more broadly to set difference patterns.

With Conjecture
1.6
in sight, we may define an

than in dense subsets of

To prove that the graph-theoretic statement (v) is equivalent to Conjecture

Nonetheless, we note that the equivalence between (ii) and (iv) shows in particular that the difference between the square difference and clique difference conjectures, Conjecture

1.7
and Conjecture
1.8

Finally, our third main result will involve reducing Conjecture

Linear forms

Suppose that we want to show that for some sequence

of distinct elements of

Then one line of argument runs as follows. Suppose that we can, for large enough

(i)

After that a double-counting argument allows us to conclude as desired: the average density

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}}\frac{|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{C}|}{|\mathcal{C}|}

K^{-1}|\Omega_{n}|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{A\in\Omega_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{A\in\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{C}}

and then, by changing the order in which we take expectations, as

\delta K^{-1}|\Omega_{n}|\mathbb{E}_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{A\in\mathcal{C}}.

Assumption (iii) then shows that for every

the inner expectation is equal to

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}}\frac{|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{C}|}{|\mathcal{C}|}=\delta(|\Omega_{n}|L/|\mathcal{W}_{n}|K)=\delta:

(2)

indeed both products

Y\setminus(X_{1}\cup\dots\cup X_{j})
drops below
p^{2}+mp
(as until then the procedure that we have described is guaranteed to work). We then take
R
to be the union of
[n]\setminus Y
and of the elements of
Y\setminus(X_{1}\cup\dots\cup X_{j})
. In both cases we have
\phi(X_{i,j})=0
for each
(i,j)\in[t]\times[m]
, which establishes the first condition.

We next establish (
9
). In both cases, because every collection
\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)
contains the same number of sets, the distribution of
\Phi(X_{i},U)
with
(X_{i},U)
chosen at random is equal to the distribution
D
of
\Phi(U)
where we first choose
i\in[t]
uniformly at random, and then choose the subset
U
of
[n]^{d}\setminus X_{i}^{d}
uniformly at random. In the first case, all elements of the support

Z(\Phi)=\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{d})\in[n]^{d}:a_{z_{1}}\dots a_{z_{d}}\neq 0\}

of
\Phi
belong to
[n]^{d}\setminus X_{i}^{d}
so the distribution
D
is exactly the distribution of
\Phi(A)
when
A
is chosen uniformly at random in
\mathcal{P}([n]^{d})
. In the second case, the intersection
Z(\Phi)\cap([n]^{d}\setminus X_{i}^{d})
contains
(Z(\phi)\cap R)^{d}
which has size at least
(n/8)^{d}\geq n/8
. Proposition
3.3
hence shows that assuming that
n
is large enough, for every choice of
i\in[t]
the distribution
D
conditioned on
i
charges every element of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with probability mass at most
\epsilon/2
away from
1/p
. By the law of total probability this is the case for the distribution
D
itself, so for that of
\Phi(X_{i},U)
. Meanwhile, since
|Z(\Phi)|\geq|Z(\phi)|\geq n/2
, Proposition
3.3
shows that this is the case as well for the distribution of
\Phi(A)
as well. The bound (
9
) then follows from the triangle inequality.

Let
\sigma\in\{1,p\}
be the common size of the sets
X_{i,j}
. The last item follows from applying Proposition
3.1
: there, we take the property
P:\mathcal{P}([\sigma m]^{d})\to\{0,1\}
to be that the subset of
[\sigma m]^{d}
has a constant indicator function on each product

[\sigma(j_{1}-1)+1,\sigma j_{1}]\times\dots\times[\sigma(j_{d}-1)+1,\sigma j_{d}]

with
j_{1},\dots,j_{d}\in[m]
(not necessarily distinct), and we take the pairs
(X_{i},<_{i})
to be such that for every
i\in[t]
the total ordering
<_{i}
has the elements of
X_{i,1}
as its
\sigma
lowest elements, then the elements of
X_{i,2}
as its
\sigma
lowest elements after that, and so forth. ∎

The next statement provides us with a density increment for dense collections
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{P}([n]^{d})
that are distinguishable from
\mathcal{P}([n]^{d})
using some linear form
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
. In Proposition
3.5
we assume that the integer
m
is fixed and that the sets
X_{1},\dots,X_{t}
are as in the conclusion of Proposition
3.4
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
d
be a positive integer, let
\delta>0
,
\eta\in(0,1]
, and let
p
be a prime. Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is a collection of subsets of
[n]^{d}
that has density
\delta
, and that there exists a linear form
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
such that such that the corresponding linear form
\Phi:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{[n]^{d}}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
satisfies

|\mathbb{P}_{A\in\mathcal{A}}(\Phi(A)=y)-\mathbb{P}_{A\in\mathcal{P}([n]^{d})}(\Phi(A)=y)|\geq\eta

for some
y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. If
n
is large enough depending on
d,m,\delta,\eta,p
only then there exists
i\in[t]
and a subset
U\subset[n]^{d}\setminus X_{i}^{d}
such that

|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|\geq\delta(1+\eta/3)|\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|.

Proof.

We write
P
for
\mathbb{P}_{A\in\mathcal{P}([n]^{d})}(\Phi(A)=y)
. The set
\mathcal{A}_{y}
defined by

\mathcal{A}_{y}=\{A\in\mathcal{A}:\Phi(A)=y\}

has density at least
\delta(P+\eta)
inside
\mathcal{P}([n]^{d})
, so by the inequality (
6
) from the deduction of Theorem
3.2
from Proposition
3.1
, applied to
\mathcal{A}_{y}
instead of
\mathcal{A}
and to
\epsilon=\delta\eta/3
, we have for
n
large enough depending on
d,m,\delta,\eta
only the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A}\mathbbm{1}_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{y}}N(A)

\displaystyle\geq(\delta(P+\eta)-\delta\eta/6)(1-\delta\eta/6)\mathbb{E}_{A}N(A)

\displaystyle\geq\delta(P+2\eta/3)\mathbb{E}_{A}N(A).

Using the double-counting identities

\displaystyle\sum_{A}N(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i,U)}|\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|

\displaystyle\sum_{A}\mathbbm{1}_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{y}}N(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i,U)}\mathbbm{1}_{\Phi(X_{i},U)=y}|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|

shows that

\mathbb{E}_{(i,U)}\mathbbm{1}_{\Phi(X_{i},U)=y}|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|\geq\delta(P+2\eta/3)\mathbb{E}_{(i,U)}|\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|.

By the second property guaranteed by Proposition
3.4
, the proportion of
(i,U)
for which
\Phi(X_{i},U)=y
is at most
P+\eta/3
, so for
n
large enough depending on
d,m,\delta,\eta,p
only we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(i,U):\mathbbm{1}_{\Phi(X_{i},U)=y}}|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|

\displaystyle\geq\delta\frac{P+2\eta/3}{P+\eta/3}\mathbb{E}_{(i,U)}|\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|

\displaystyle\geq\delta(1+\eta/3)\mathbb{E}_{(i,U)}|\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|

since
P+\eta\leq 1
. As all collections
\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)
have the same size, the expectation in the last quantity may be restricted to the
(i,U)
satisfying
\Phi(X_{i},U)=y
. It follows that

|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|\geq\delta(1+\eta/3)|\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|

for some
(i,U)
. ∎

Iterating this density increment finally allows us to deduce Theorem
1.12
.

Proof of Theorem
1.12
.

For every
\eta>0
, the statement
(S_{\eta})
follows from Conjecture
1.6
as a special case, so it suffices to prove the converse direction. Let
d\geq 1,\delta>0
be fixed, and let
\eta>0
be a value that verifies
(S_{\eta})
for these parameters. If we have

|\mathbb{P}_{A\in\mathcal{A}}(\Phi(A)=y)-\mathbb{P}_{A\in\mathcal{P}([n]^{d})}(\Phi(A)=y)|\leq\eta

for every linear form
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
and every
y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
then we are done by
(S_{\eta})
. Otherwise, we may take
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
such that

|\mathbb{P}_{A\in\mathcal{A}}(\Phi(A)=y)-\mathbb{P}_{A\in\mathcal{P}([n]^{d})}(\Phi(A)=y)|\geq\eta/p,

and Proposition
3.5
then provides a collection
\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)
such that

|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|\geq\delta(1+\eta/3p)|\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)|.

The collection
\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)
can be seen to be isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}([n^{(1)}]^{d})
with
n^{(1)}=m
, using the map
h:\mathcal{P}([n^{(1)}]^{d})\to\mathcal{C}(X_{i},U)
defined by

h(B)=U\cup\bigcup_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{d})\in B}(X_{i,j_{1}}\times\dots\times X_{i,j_{d}}),

so we have obtained that
\mathcal{A}
contains a subset which is isomorphic to a subset
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
of
\mathcal{P}([n^{(1)}]^{d})
with density at least
\delta(1+\eta/3p)
in
\mathcal{P}([n^{(1)}]^{d})
. All that we have described so far holds for
n
large enough depending (for fixed
\delta,\eta,p
) only on
m
. As
n
tends to infinity, we may hence also make
m
tend to infinity, and therefore
n^{(1)}
as well. We then iterate: if

|\mathbb{P}_{A\in\mathcal{A}^{(1)}}(\Phi(A)=y)-\mathbb{P}_{A\in\mathcal{P}([n^{(1)}]^{d})}(\Phi(A)=y)|\leq\eta

for every linear form
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n^{(1)}}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
and every
y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
then we are done by
(S_{\eta})
, and otherwise we obtain that
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
(and hence
\mathcal{A}
) contains a subset isomorphic to some subset
\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
of
\mathcal{P}([n^{(2)}]^{d})
with density at least
\delta(1+\eta/3p)^{2}
, where
n^{(2)}
tends to infinity with
n
. After any
q
iterations the density of
\mathcal{A}^{(q)}
is at least
\delta(1+\eta/3p)^{q}
; as it must be at most
1
we have
q\leq\log(\delta^{-1})/\log(1+\eta/3p)
. Therefore, we can find
n’
tending to infinity with
n
in a way that depends only on
\delta
,
\eta
,
p
such that
\mathcal{A}
contains a subset isomorphic to some subset
\mathcal{A}’
of
\mathcal{P}([n’]^{d})
with density at least
\delta
and which satisfies

|\mathbb{P}_{A\in\mathcal{A}’}(\Phi(A)=y)-\mathbb{P}_{A\in\mathcal{P}([n^{\prime}]^{d})}(\Phi(A)=y)|\leq\eta

for every linear form
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n’}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
and every
y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. We conclude by applying
(S_{\eta})
. ∎
