Adaptive Momentum Benefits Score-based Generative Models
Ziqing Wen, Ping Luo, Jiahuan Wang, Xiaoge Deng, Tao Sun *, Dongsheng Li

Abstract
Score-based Generative Models (SGMs) have achieved remarkable success in generation tasks by establishing a diffusion process that gradually perturbs real data into Gaussian noise and then learning a reverse process to reconstruct the data from noise. However, conventional denoising methods, such as Langevin dynamics and numerical stochastic differential equation solvers, rely on randomness but suffer from slow generation speeds due to the high number of score function evaluations. To address this, we propose a plug-and-play Markov adaptive momentum sampling method, inspired by the strong connection between the SGMs’ sampling process and stochastic gradient descent optimization. Our approach accelerates the transformation process without introducing additional hyperparameters. Theoretically, we establish a rigorous foundation by analyzing the relationship between the distribution of samples generated by our method and the original data distribution. This provides a novel perspective on the analysis of sampling distributions. Empirically, our method demonstrates superior efficiency, generating high-quality images/graphs in significantly fewer sampling steps—achieving a speedup of 2× to 5×—while maintaining or surpassing baseline performance on various image and graph generation benchmarks. These results underscore the effectiveness of adaptive momentum in enhancing the efficiency of SGMs.



I
Introduction

Diffusion models
[
1
,
2
]
, owing to their strong capacity for learning complex data distributions, have achieved remarkable progress and found applications across diverse domains
[
3

.

Two prominent classes of diffusion models share the principle of progressively corrupting training data with noise and then learning to reverse this corruption in order to synthesize new samples. The first approach, Score Matching with Langevin Dynamics (SMLD)
[
5
,
6
]
, trains a network to estimate the score function (i.e., the gradient of the log-density of the data distribution). Using these estimated scores together with Langevin Dynamics (LD)
[

A second influential approach is Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM)
[
1
]
, which formulates the reverse process probabilistically, enabling high-quality image generation. Building on these foundations, Song et al.
[
2
]
proposed a unified score-based stochastic differential equation (SDE) framework that generalizes both diffusion and score-based models. Throughout this paper, we collectively refer to these methods as Score-based Generative Models (SGMs).

SGMs have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in a wide range of generative tasks, including image synthesis
[
1
,
2
]
, video generation
[
9
]
, and graph generation
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. Nevertheless, their success comes at a high computational cost: generating high-fidelity images often requires more than 1,000 sampling steps, leading to significantly slower inference compared to alternatives such as GANs
[
13
]
. This motivates the need for more efficient samplers.

Recently, much of the progress in accelerating samplers has focused on Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) solvers
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
or on approaches that introduce additional training objectives
[
17
]
. A key limitation of ODE-based samplers is their
deterministic
nature: once the initial point is fixed, the generation trajectory is uniquely determined. This restricts their flexibility, whereas stochastic sampling remains crucial in SGMs. Introducing randomness not only enhances sample diversity and fidelity but also enables the computation of likelihoods
[
17
,
18
]
.

Stochastic generation via Langevin dynamics is particularly relevant because of its close connection to stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
[
19
,
2
]
. LD iteratively moves in the direction of the log-density gradient while adding noise, which can be viewed as a noisy gradient ascent procedure that approximates maximum likelihood estimation. This connection suggests that optimization techniques developed for SGD may be fruitfully adapted to improve stochastic sampling in SGMs.

SGD itself has become a cornerstone for large-scale optimization due to its efficiency and strong empirical success
[
20
]
. To accelerate convergence, Polyak’s Heavy Ball (HB) method
[
21
]
augments SGD with a momentum term that captures trends in parameter updates, thereby guiding subsequent iterations more effectively. Its stochastic counterpart, the Stochastic Heavy Ball (SHB) method, introduces a momentum parameter

[
22
,
23
]
. These methods not only reduce hyperparameter sensitivity but also improve stability and performance, offering a promising direction for momentum-based optimization
[
24
]
and, by extension, for advancing stochastic sampling in SGMs.

I-A

Contributions

In this paper, motivated by the limitations of existing approaches and the proven effectiveness of adaptive momentum in SGD, we propose
Adaptive Momentum Sampling (AMS)
(Algorithm
1
). After introducing the necessary background, we provide a theoretical analysis of the properties of the sampling distribution induced by AMS and its time evolution. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

A novel plug-and-play sampler.
We introduce a hyperparameter-free sampling method that integrates adaptive momentum from SGD into SGMs. Our sampler operates directly on the reverse SDE process, requires no additional training, and provides a theoretically grounded framework for advancing gradient-based sampling optimization.

•

Theoretical characterization.
Using the Wasserstein distance, we analyze the sampling process of SGMs within a Markovian framework. We prove that the distribution induced by AMS is stationary and converges to a unique distribution as the number of sampling steps increases. Moreover, we establish explicit relationships between the mean and variance of this distribution and the maximum-likelihood point of
p(\mathbf{x})
, offering new insights into the theoretical underpinnings of SGM sampling.

•

Empirical validation.
We instantiate AMS as a corrector within Predictor–Corrector (PC) samplers
[
2
]
, referred to as the
Momentum Corrector (MC)
in Algorithm

Background

II-A

Related Works

Numerous accelerated samplers have been proposed for SGMs. For example,
[
17
]
introduced Critically-damped Langevin Dynamics (CLD) by incorporating velocity into the conventional Langevin Dynamics framework. However, this approach requires a modified training objective and model re-training. In contrast,
[
16
,
25
,
26
]
proposed training-free momentum-based Langevin samplers, which introduce additional hyperparameters that require careful tuning. Our method, by comparison, operates in a plug-and-play manner and does not require any task-specific hyperparameter tuning.

Most existing accelerated samplers primarily focus on ODE-based optimization, where deterministic sampling eliminates noise terms, allowing for larger step sizes
[
18
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
27
]
. For example,
[
15
]
introduced Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM), a non-Markovian sampler derived from the Euler method. Due to the deterministic nature of non-Markovian sampling, these methods lack diversity in generated images, as no noise is introduced during the sampling process. In contrast, Langevin dynamics inherently incorporates randomness and has a well-established physical interpretation. To the best of our knowledge, there are no existing training-free and hyperparameter-free accelerated Langevin samplers. In this work, we focus on developing stochastic samplers that retain the advantages of Langevin dynamics while achieving efficient sampling acceleration.

II-B

Score Matching with Langevin Dynamics

For any continuously differentiable probability density function
p(\mathbf{x})
, we define its score function as
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x})
. Once this score function is available, new samples can be generated from an initial sample using the Langevin dynamics (LD) scheme outlined below

\mathbf{x}^{t+1}=\mathbf{x}^{t}+\alpha\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x}^{t})+\sqrt{2\alpha}\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}^{t}),\ \ \ 1\leq t\leq T,

(1)

where
\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
is a noise term dependent on or independent of
\mathbf{x}^{t}
,
\alpha
is a step size parameter chosen to be sufficiently small,
T
is chosen to be sufficiently large. In the annealed Langevin dynamics
[
28
]
, the noise term is a zero-centered Gaussian noise with covariance
\mathbf{I}
, which can be represented by
\bm{\varepsilon}
. The convergence of
\mathbf{x}^{t}
to
p(\mathbf{x})
is guaranteed under certain regularity conditions
[
7
,
8
]
.
[
29
]
suggests integrating all the noise information to establish a step size sequence
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with a schedule
\alpha_{i}=\alpha\sigma_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{n}^{2}
, named Annealed Langevin sampler (ALS).

The key to LD’s (
1
) application lies in how to estimate the unknown gradient
\nabla\log p(\mathbf{x}^{t})
. Denoising Score Matching (DSM)
[
30
]
with Langevin dynamics involves estimating the score function for the objective data distribution
p(\mathbf{x})
. This can be achieved by training a Noise Conditional Score Network (NCSN)
[
29
]
, denoted by
s_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\sigma)
, by minimizing the following loss
[
6
]

\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\rm data}(\mathbf{x})}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\sigma_{i}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}|\mathbf{x})}\Big[\big\|\sigma_{i}s_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\sigma_{i})+\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}}{\sigma_{i}}\big\|^{2}\Big].

(2)

Here,
p_{\rm data}(\mathbf{x})
represents the underlying true data distribution of our target dataset,
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are pre-set noise scales satisfying
\sigma_{1}>\sigma_{2}>\cdots>\sigma_{n}
, and
p_{\sigma_{i}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}|\mathbf{x})
represents the corrupted data distribution. In practice, we use the perturbation kernel
p_{\sigma}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}|\mathbf{x}):=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}};\mathbf{x},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
.

A notable contribution in this area is the establishment of a connection between the forward process and the SDE, along with a solution to the reverse SDE using a reverse diffusion predictor
[
2
]
. For the forward process in SMLD, we have

d\mathbf{x}=f(\mathbf{x},t)dt+g(t)d\mathbf{w},

(3)

where
\mathbf{w}
is the standard Wiener process,
f(\cdot,t):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a function depicting the diffusion process of
\mathbf{x}^{t}
, and
g(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a scalar function that depends on the diffusion time
t
.
We present the corresponding SDE of Variance Exploding (VE) and Variance Preserving (VP) in
[
2
]
as follows

\displaystyle d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{d[\sigma^{2}(t)]}{dt}}d\mathbf{w},\quad(\text{VE}),

\displaystyle d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)\mathbf{x}dt+\sqrt{\beta(t)}d\mathbf{w},\quad(\text{VP}),

where
\beta(t)
corresponds to the variation of noise with respect to the time step. Thus, we can deduce the reverse form of the SDE, and the sampling process is conducted along the reverse SDE

d\mathbf{x}=\big(f(\mathbf{x},t)-g^{2}(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\big)dt+g(t)d\bar{\mathbf{w}}.

(4)

II-C

Integrate Momentum in SMLD

In SMLD, samples are generated by reversing the process using LD in Eq. (
1
). Notably, this update procedure can be interpreted as performing gradient ascent on the log-probability function—using the score
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x})
as the ascent direction—which establishes a clear connection between Langevin dynamics and SGD. The key distinction is that LD incorporates stochasticity into the sampling process, whereas traditional SGD operates deterministically.

Consider the following SGD with recursion

\mathbf{x}^{t+1}=\mathbf{x}^{t}-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{t}),

(5)

where
\alpha
denotes the step size hyperparameter, and
f
is a manually defined loss function. We can regard the Langevin dynamics as a special form of SGD with additional noise
\bm{\varepsilon}
. Thus, by integrating advanced techniques from SGD into Langevin dynamics, we can design an improved sampling schedule for SGMs.

The Normalized Stochastic Heavy Ball (NSHB) method introduces a momentum term for SGD Eq. (
5
) and updates
\mathbf{x}
with momentum and gradient

\mathbf{x}^{t+1}=\mathbf{x}^{t}-\alpha\mathbf{m}^{t},\mathbf{m}^{t}=\beta_{t}\mathbf{m}^{t-1}+(1-\beta_{t})\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{t}),

(6)

where
\beta\in[0,1)
is the momentum hyperparameter and requires additional adjustment.

We treat the gradient
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{t})
as the score function
-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x})
. By leveraging the trained score network from Eq. (
2
) and introducing a noise term
\bm{\varepsilon}
, we can reformulate NSHB. Specifically, we replace the gradient with the trained score network
s_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{t},\sigma)
to obtain a novel sampling method, as detailed below

\mathbf{x}^{t+1}={\mathbf{x}}^{t}+\tilde{\alpha}_{t}\mathbf{m}^{t}+\sqrt{2\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}^{t}),

(7)

where
\mathbf{m}^{t}=\beta_{t}\mathbf{m}^{t-1}+(1-\beta_{t})s_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{t},\sigma_{t})
.

Our Algorithm

\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{T}\otimes\mathbf{T}
is invertible. Moreover, if
\pi_{\alpha}^{*}
is the stationary distribution of Eq. (
9
), then

\displaystyle\operatorname{vec}\left(\underset{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}{\int}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}^{*})^{\otimes 2}\pi_{\alpha}^{*}(d\mathbf{z})\right)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{T}\otimes\mathbf{T})^{-1}\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}).

where
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\varepsilon}^{t}(\bm{\varepsilon}^{t})^{T}]
is the covariance matrix of the additive noise, and
\mathbf{z}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{x}^{*}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
. These two propositions highlight the fact that, for a given quadratic function, the sample distribution is stationary and unique.

Strong Convexity Case

In this section, we dive into analyzing the convergence of Eq. (
6
) in the strongly convex case. In the local convergence analysis, we consider the terminal annealed regime where

\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}=\alpha\kappa(\mathbf{x}^{*})+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{3/2}).

Thus, the local Langevin perturbation has covariance

2\tilde{\alpha}\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}\mathbf{I}=\alpha^{2}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^{*})+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{5/2}),

where
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^{*})=2(1+\beta)^{2}\kappa(\mathbf{x}^{*})\mathbf{I}
.

Proposition 3
.

Assume
\alpha\in(0,1/L)
and
f
is strongly convex. Let

\mathbf{P}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}=\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}^{*}),\mathbf{H}:=\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}^{*}),

and define the local transition matrix

\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{H}}:=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\beta)\mathbf{I}-\alpha(1-\beta)\mathbf{H}&-\beta\mathbf{I}\\

Suppose the local additive noise in the augmented recursion satisfies

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\varepsilon}_{z,t}\bm{\varepsilon}_{z,t}^{T}\right]=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{2}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^{*})+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{\frac{5}{2}})&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{\varepsilon}_{z,t}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathbf{x},t}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}
is the augmented additive noise. If
\rho(\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{H}})<1
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{vec}\left(\underset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\int}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\otimes 2}\pi_{\alpha}(d\mathbf{x})\right)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{P}\otimes\mathbf{P})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{H}}\otimes\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{H}})^{-1}\operatorname{vec}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{2}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^{*})&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{\frac{3}{2}}).

Our theoretical analyses demonstrate that our method ensures a stationary and unique sampling distribution and tends to sample high probability density points with high frequency. Furthermore, the converged distribution remains closely aligned with the distribution learned by the SGM, reinforcing the theoretical soundness of our approach.

On the Validity of Assumptions.
Our convergence analysis is built under classical assumptions such as smoothness and strong convexity, which provide theoretical tractability and help interpret the behavior of the proposed sampler. Although such assumptions may not strictly hold in general SGMs, they are approximately satisfied in local regions of the loss landscape, especially when the model is close to convergence. Moreover, in practice, SGMs often include regularization (e.g., weight decay, score matching penalties) that induces a locally convex structure. To verify this local strong convexity, we perform 1000-step SDE sampling to generate CIFAR-10 and FFHQ images using the pre-trained VE/VP model
[
18
]
. We estimate the local convexity coefficient
\mu
, and the condition number
L/\mu
of the Hessian. Specifically, at each trajectory point
\mathbf{x}^{t}
with noise level
\sigma_{t}
, we define the residual function for a pre-trained SGM as follows

f(\mathbf{x};\sigma_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\|s_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\sigma_{t})-s_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{t},\sigma_{t})\|^{2},

where
s_{\theta}
denotes the trained score-based network and
s_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{t},\sigma_{t})
serves as the local reference output for evaluating quadratic growth. This local quadratic loss measures the deviation of the score network output around
\mathbf{x}
, enabling us to probe the local convexity of the score landscape via its second-order geometry.

The strong convexity coefficient
\mu
is computed via the quadratic growth condition: for a random perturbation
\mathbf{d}
with radius
r
, we have

\mu=\frac{2\left[f(\mathbf{x}^{t}+\mathbf{d})-f(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{t}),\mathbf{d}\rangle\right]}{\|\mathbf{d}\|^{2}}.

The Lipschitz constant
L
is estimated via the following perturbation

L=\frac{\|s_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}+\bm{\varepsilon},\sigma_{t})-s_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\sigma_{t})\|}{\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|}.

We sample
n=50
random directions at each trajectory point with radius
r\in\{0.01,0.02,0.05,0.1\}
and aggregate the resulting statistics. The local condition number is computed from
L/\mu
. While these estimates are approximate due to finite sampling and local perturbations, they provide valuable insights into the local geometry of the score-based generative model. The results are shown in Figure
1
. The results demonstrate that throughout the sampling process, the local convexity coefficient
\mu
remains positive, and the condition number remains well-behaved. This provides experimental evidence supporting the validity of our strong convexity assumption.

Computational Complexity.

We further analyze the per-step computational complexity of the proposed adaptive momentum sampling method. Similar to standard Langevin sampling, the dominant computational cost arises from the forward evaluation of the score network

, which requires
\mathcal{O}(C_{\text{net}})
floating-point operations (FLOPs), where
C_{\text{net}}
denotes the complexity of the neural network.

Compared with the standard Langevin update, our method introduces additional operations for computing the adaptive momentum parameter
\beta
. Specifically, computing
\beta
requires evaluating two vector differences
(\mathbf{g}^{t}-\mathbf{g}^{t-1})
and
(\mathbf{x}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t-1})
, which costs
2d
FLOPs, and two
\ell_{2}
norms, each requiring approximately
2d
FLOPs (including elementwise squares and summation). Therefore, the computation of
\beta
requires approximately
6d
FLOPs. The remaining operations, including scalar arithmetic and projection, contribute only a negligible constant cost.

As a result, the per-step computational complexity of our method can be written as

C_{\text{AMS}}=C_{\text{net}}+6d+\mathcal{O}(1),

where
d
denotes the dimensionality of the data. In comparison, the standard Langevin update requires approximately

C_{\text{Langevin}}=C_{\text{net}}+\mathcal{O}(d).

In practical diffusion and score-based generative models, the neural network evaluation dominates the total cost (i.e.,
C_{\text{net}}\gg d
). Consequently, the additional
6d
operations introduced by the adaptive momentum computation represent a negligible overhead. This theoretical analysis is consistent with the empirical runtime results reported in Tables
I
and
VIII
.

VI
Conclusion

Motivated by traditional SGD optimization, we propose a novel gradient-based sampling method leveraging the Markov technique for existing SGMs. This sampler requires no additional information beyond the score function, making it broadly applicable. Theoretically, we analyze the convergence properties and efficiency within the convex optimization framework using Markov Chains, providing an alternative perspective to the conventional SDE/ODE-based analysis in SGM sampling. Our method enables the examination of data distribution properties during sampling, such as computing the mean and variance at each step, revealing the sampling distribution’s evolution with the sampling time step
t
. By bridging SGD and gradient-based sampling, our approach opens new avenues for accelerating SGM applications. Experimentally, we validate our method across various image and graph generation tasks using different datasets and SGMs. Our results demonstrate that it significantly enhances SGM performance in reverse SDE sampling without incurring substantial additional overhead. This underscores its broad applicability across diverse tasks and models. Overall, our Markov Chain-based sampler presents distinct advantages for SGM sampling, offering both efficiency and scalability.

VII
Discussion

We outline several potential directions for our work here.

Potential Extension to ODE-based Samplers

Although our method is developed for stochastic samplers, the adaptive momentum mechanism is broadly applicable and may also be extended to deterministic ODE-based samplers, such as flow-matching generative models. Existing momentum-accelerated ODE methods (e.g.,
[
16
]
) improve convergence but need additional hyperparameters, making practical tuning more difficult. In contrast, our adaptive momentum approach introduces no additional hyperparameters while still providing acceleration. This suggests that similar adaptive strategies could be incorporated into ODE-based samplers to improve efficiency. Investigating this extension could help improve the efficiency and stability of ODE samplers in future studies.

Broader Evaluation

In this work, we primarily focus on image and graph generation, demonstrating the effectiveness of our adaptive momentum method across a range of models and datasets. SGMs have also been successfully applied to text generation
[
57
]
and video generation
[
9
]
. Extending our adaptive momentum sampling approach to these tasks presents both challenges and opportunities. On the one hand, conditioning on high-dimensional text embeddings may introduce additional complexity, affecting stability and convergence. On the other hand, our momentum-based approach is plug-and-play and requires no additional hyperparameters, which could help accelerate conditional sampling while maintaining high generation quality
[
58
,
59
]
.

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\theta,\hat{\theta})=\mathbf{E}\left[\|\mathbf{z}^{0}-\hat{\mathbf{z}}^{0}\|^{2}\right]

\mathbf{z}^{0}
and
\hat{\mathbf{z}}^{0}

\{\bm{\varepsilon}^{t}\}_{t\geq 1}

(\mathbf{z}^{t},\hat{\mathbf{z}}^{t})
belongs to
\Pi(\theta R_{\alpha}^{t},\hat{\theta}R_{\alpha}^{t})

\mathbf{c}:=\begin{bmatrix}-\alpha(1-\beta)\mathbf{b}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}.

Under the synchronous coupling, we derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\theta R_{\alpha}^{t},\hat{\theta}R_{\alpha}^{t})\leq\mathbf{E}\|\mathbf{z}^{t}-\hat{\mathbf{z}}^{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}^{t-1}+\mathbf{c}+\bm{\varepsilon}^{t}-\mathbf{T}\hat{\mathbf{z}}^{t-1}-\mathbf{c}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{z}^{t-1}-\hat{\mathbf{z}}^{t-1})\|^{2}=\dots=\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{T}^{t}(\mathbf{z}^{0}-\hat{\mathbf{z}}^{0})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{T}^{t}\|^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{z}^{0}-\hat{\mathbf{z}}^{0}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{T}^{t}\|^{2}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\theta,\hat{\theta}).

Using Gelfand’s Theorem
[
63
]
, for any
0<\epsilon<1-\rho(\mathbf{T})
, there exists
C_{\epsilon}>0
such that

\|\mathbf{T}^{t}\|\leq C_{\epsilon}(\rho(\mathbf{T})+\epsilon)^{t}.

Hence,

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\theta R_{\alpha}^{t},\hat{\theta}R_{\alpha}^{t})\leq C_{\epsilon}(\rho(\mathbf{T})+\epsilon)^{t}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\theta,\hat{\theta}).

This indicates that, for any
\theta,\hat{\theta}
,

\underset{t\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\theta R_{\alpha}^{t},\hat{\theta}R_{\alpha}^{t})=0.

(10)

Let
r=\rho(\mathbf{T})+\epsilon<1
. For any
m>n
, by the triangle inequality and the contraction estimate above, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(\theta R_{\alpha}^{m},\theta R_{\alpha}^{n})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=n}^{m-1}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\theta R_{\alpha}^{k+1},\theta R_{\alpha}^{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=n}^{m-1}\mathcal{W}_{2}((\theta R_{\alpha})R_{\alpha}^{k},\theta R_{\alpha}^{k})

\displaystyle\leq C_{\epsilon}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\theta R_{\alpha},\theta)\sum_{k=n}^{m-1}r^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\epsilon}r^{n}}{1-r}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\theta R_{\alpha},\theta).

Thus
(\theta R_{\alpha}^{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Cauchy sequence in

(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}),\mathcal{W}_{2})
. Since this space is complete, there exists

\pi_{\alpha}^{\theta}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
such that

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\theta R_{\alpha}^{t},\pi_{\alpha}^{\theta})=0.

In the following, we prove
\pi_{\alpha}^{\theta}
does not depend on
\theta
and is unique. Assume that there exists a distribution
\pi_{\alpha}^{\hat{\theta}}
that satisfies
\underset{t\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\theta}R_{\alpha}^{t},\pi_{\alpha}^{\hat{\theta}})=0
.
By the triangle inequality, we have

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\pi_{\alpha}^{\theta},\pi_{\alpha}^{\hat{\theta}})\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}(\pi_{\alpha}^{\theta},\theta R_{\alpha}^{t})+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\theta R_{\alpha}^{t},\hat{\theta}R_{\alpha}^{t})+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\theta}R_{\alpha}^{t},\pi_{\alpha}^{\hat{\theta}}).

Taking the limits as

and using Eq. (
10

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\pi_{\alpha}^{\theta},\pi_{\alpha}^{\hat{\theta}})=0
. We see that
\pi_{\alpha}^{\theta}=\pi_{\alpha}^{\hat{\theta}}
. Therefore, we can denote
\pi_{\alpha}^{\theta}
as
\pi_{\alpha}^{*}
. We can see that
\pi_{\alpha}^{*}
is unique for given
\alpha

Moreover, since
R_{\alpha}
is induced by the affine map with additive noise in Eq. (
9
), it is continuous with respect to
\mathcal{W}_{2}
. Hence,

\theta R_{\alpha}^{t+1}=(\theta R_{\alpha}^{t})R_{\alpha}\to\pi_{\alpha}^{*}R_{\alpha}.

On the other hand,
\theta R_{\alpha}^{t+1}\to\pi_{\alpha}^{*}
. By uniqueness of the limit in
\mathcal{W}_{2}
, we obtain

\pi_{\alpha}^{*}R_{\alpha}=\pi_{\alpha}^{*}.

To this end, we begin to investigate the spectral radius of matrix
\mathbf{T}
, denote the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{T}
by
\lambda
,

\displaystyle\text{det}\begin{bmatrix}(1+\beta)\mathbf{I}-\alpha(1-\beta)\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}&-\beta\mathbf{I}\\
\mathbf{I}&-\lambda\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\text{det}\begin{bmatrix}(1+\beta-\lambda-\frac{\beta}{\lambda})\mathbf{I}-\alpha(1-\beta)\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{I}&-\lambda\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}=0

By

(11)
