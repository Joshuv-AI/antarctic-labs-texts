High- and low-energy many-body effects of graphene in a unified approach
Alberto Guandalini, Giovanni Caldarelli, Francesco Macheda, Francesco Mauri

Abstract
We show that the many-body features of graphene band structure and electronic response can be accurately evaluated by applying many-body perturbation theory to a tight-binding (TB) model. In particular, we compare TB results for the optical conductivity with previous ab-initio calculations, showing a nearly perfect agreement both in the low energy region near the Dirac cone ( ∼ 100 \sim 100 meV), and at the higher energies of the π \pi plasmon ( ∼ 5 \sim 5 eV). A reasonable agreement is reached also for the density-density response at the Brillouin zone corner. With the help of the reduced computational cost of the TB model, we study the effect of self-consistency on the screened interaction ( W W ) and on the quasi-particle corrections, a task that is not yet achievable in ab-initio frameworks. We find that self-consistency is important to reproduce the experimental results on the divergence of the Fermi velocity, as confirmed by previous studies, while it marginally affects the optical conductivity. Finally, we study the robustness of our results against doping or the introduction of a uniform dielectric environment.

II.1
Quasi-particle excitations

We consider a periodic crystal with
N
electrons per unit cell interacting via the Coulomb interaction. In the following, we discretize reciprocal space momenta by considering a Born-Von Karman supercell made of
N_{k}
unit cells. The Hamiltonian of this system may be written as

\hat{H}=\hat{H}^{0}+\hat{H}^{1}\ ,

(1)

where
\hat{H}^{0}=\hat{T}+\hat{V}
is a single-particle Hamiltonian,
\hat{T}
being the kinetic energy and
\hat{V}
the potential one, and
\hat{H}^{1}
is the Coulomb repulsion between electrons.

\hat{V}
includes the interaction between electrons and nuclei, and usually includes a mean field self-consistent potential, e.g. the Hartree or Kohn-Sham one, so to improve the starting point before applying perturbation theory.
If this is the case, the mean field potential must be subtracted from
\hat{H}^{1}
so to avoid double counting.
In this work,
\hat{H^{0}}
is described by a TB Hamiltonian which parameters are determined via ab-initio calculations, and therefore implicitly contain the effect of a self-consistent mean field potential.
The double counting is avoided by considering only the irreducible vertex in the expression for the electronic self-energy, as described later.

The single particle problem for the periodic system is solved by determining the eigenvalues
\epsilon^{0}_{m\bm{k}}
(band energies) and eigenvectors
\varphi^{0}_{m\bm{k}}
(Bloch functions) of
\hat{H}^{0}
as

\hat{H}^{0}\varphi^{0}_{m\bm{k}}=\epsilon^{0}_{m\bm{k}}\varphi^{0}_{m\bm{k}}\ .

(2)

From Eq. (
2
), we define the non-interacting Green’s function as

G^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=\lim\limits_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\sum\limits_{m\bm{k}}\varphi^{0}_{m\bm{k}}(\bm{r})\varphi^{0*}_{m\bm{k}}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\\
\times\left[\frac{f^{0}_{m\bm{k}}}{\omega-\epsilon^{0}_{m\bm{k}}-i\eta}+\frac{(1-f^{0}_{m\bm{k}})}{\omega-\epsilon^{0}_{m\bm{k}}+i\eta}\right]\ ,

(3)

where
f^{0}_{m\bm{k}}
are the Fermi-Dirac occupation numbers corresponding to
\epsilon^{0}_{m\bm{k}}
.

In the framework of many body perturbation theory, it can be shown that interaction effects are effectively included in a similar fashion as Eq. (
2
) by including a dynamical self-energy operator
\Sigma

\left[\hat{H}^{0}+\Sigma(\epsilon_{m\bm{k}})\right]\varphi_{m\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{m\bm{k}}\varphi_{m\bm{k}}\ .

(4)

\epsilon_{m\bm{k}}
and
\varphi_{m\bm{k}}
are the quasi-particle energies and orbitals that enter in the determination of the interacting single-particle Green’s function

G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=\lim\limits_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\sum\limits_{m\bm{k}}\varphi_{m\bm{k}}(\bm{r})\varphi^{*}_{m\bm{k}}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\\
\times\left[\frac{f_{m\bm{k}}}{\omega-\epsilon_{m\bm{k}}-i\eta}+\frac{(1-f_{m\bm{k}})}{\omega-\epsilon_{m\bm{k}}+i\eta}\right]\ ,

(5)

where
f_{m\bm{k}}
are the quasi-particle occupation numbers corresponding to
\epsilon_{m\bm{k}}
.

Eq. (
4
) may be reformulated in terms of a Dyson equation that relate
G
and
G^{0}
via
\Sigma
as

G(\omega)=G^{0}(\omega)+G^{0}(\omega)\Sigma(\omega)G(\omega)\ ,

(6)

where space coordinates and integrals have been omitted to ease of notation.
In practice, Eq. (
6
) is often simplified via the the quasi-particle orbital approximation, which consists in taking
\varphi\approx\varphi^{0}
.
With this approximation and assuming non-degeneracy for simplicity, quasi-particle energies may be written as

\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}=\epsilon^{0}_{n\bm{k}}+\matrixelement{n\k}{\Sigma(\eig_{n\k})}{n\k},

(7)

where we used the Dirac notation to identify the Bloch functions. By Taylor-expanding the self-energy
\Sigma
around the bare energy
\epsilon^{0}
, the above equation may also be rewritten as

\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}=\epsilon^{0}_{n\bm{k}}+Z_{n\bm{k}}\matrixelement{n\k}{\Sigma(\eig^0_{n\k})}{n\k},

(8)

where

Z_{n\mathbf{k}}=\left[\left.1-\langle n{\bf k}|\partial\Sigma(\omega)/\partial\omega|n{\bf k}\rangle\right|_{\,\omega=\epsilon^{0}_{n{\bf k}}}\right]^{-1}

is the renormalization factor.

II.1.1
Approximations of the self-energy

The exact expression of the self-energy as a function of the screened interaction
W
is given by the Hedin’s equations.
A detailed explanation can be found in Ref.
38

In this paragraph we remind the expressions for the self-energy that are relevant for this work.

Within the GW approximation
[

,
40
,
41
,
42
]
, the self-energy is given by first-order perturbation theory with respect to the screened interaction
W
as

\Sigma^{\mathrm{GW}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})\\
=iG(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})W(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime}+0^{+})\ .

(9)

In the screened-exchange (SX) approximation, the dynamical screened interaction
W
in Eq. (
9

\Sigma^{\mathrm{SX}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})\\
=iG(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})W(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\ ,

(10)

where
W(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})=W(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega=0)
is the static screened interaction.
The Fourier transform of
\Sigma^{\mathrm{SX}}
over time
\Sigma^{\mathrm{SX}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=\Sigma^{\mathrm{SX}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})
is frequency independent and reads

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\mathrm{SX}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})=-n(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})W(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\ ,

(11)

\displaystyle n(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})=\sum_{m\bm{k}}f_{m\bm{k}}\varphi_{m\bm{k}}(\bm{r})\varphi^{*}_{m\bm{k}}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\ ,

(12)

where
n(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})
is the quasi-particle density matrix.
The quasi-particle Dyson equation in Eq. (
6
) with a SX self-energy is sketched in terms of Feynman diagrams in Fig.
1
(a).

Finally, in the G
0
W
0
approximation, both the one-particle Green function
G
and screened interaction
W
are approximated by their bare counterparts

\Sigma^{\mathrm{G^{0}W^{0}}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})\\
=iG^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})W^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime}+0^{+})\ ,

(13)

where
W^{0}
is the screened interaction of the bare system.

The static approximation of the screened interaction may be applied also to the G
0
W
0
self-energy, obtaining the non self-consistent screened-exchange (SX
0
) approximation

\Sigma^{\mathrm{SX^{0}}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})=-n^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})W^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\ .

(14)

II.2
Density response functions and dielectric properties

The electron-hole (e-h) propagator
L
is the main quantity of interest in the calculation of density-density response and dielectric functions with many-body perturbation theory
[
38
]
.
L
is determined via the BSE, which expresses the linear response of the single-particle Green’s function
G
to an external non-local source
[
39
,
43
,
44
]
and reads

L(\bm{Q},\omega)=L^{0}(\bm{Q},\omega)+L^{0}(\bm{Q},\omega)K(\bm{Q})L(\bm{Q},\omega)\ .

(15)

\bm{Q}
is the center-of-mass momentum of the e-h pair,

K
is a kernel including the effect of interaction and
L^{0}
is the bare e-h propagator, describing the non-interacting evolution of two quasi-particles, with the following real space representation:

L^{0}(\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\bm{r}_{3},\bm{r}_{4},\omega)=2\sum\limits_{nm}\sum\limits_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}(f_{m\bm{k}^{\prime}}-f_{n\bm{k}})\\
\times\lim\limits_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\varphi_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r}_{1})\varphi^{*}_{m\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{r}_{2})\varphi^{*}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r}_{3})\varphi_{m\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{r}_{4})}{\omega-(\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{m\bm{k}^{\prime}})+i\eta}\ .

(16)

If the single-particle Green’s function
G
is obtained from a SX self-energy, the kernel is composed by two terms

\displaystyle K=K^{\mathrm{H}}+K^{\mathrm{SX}},

(17)

where
K^{\mathrm{H}}
is the response due to the Hartree potential and
K^{\mathrm{SX}}
contains the attractive e-h interaction.
Their matrix elements are conveniently expressed in the Hilbert space of e-h single particle transitions
\{{\ket{t}\}}=\{|n\bm{k}\to m\bm{k}+\bm{Q}\rangle\}
, where
n
,
m
are band indexes and
\bm{k}
is a wave-vector of the Brillouin zone (BZ).
In particular we consider
\bra{t_1}=\langle n_{1}\bm{k}_{1}\to m_{1}\bm{k}_{1}+\bm{Q}|
and
\ket{t_2}=|n_{2}\bm{k}_{2}\to m_{2}\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{Q}\rangle
, where
\bm{Q}
can assume values outside the first Brillouin zone, and obtain the matrix elements

\matrixelement{t_1}{K^{\H}(\qph)}{t_2}=2\int d\bm{r}\int d\bm{r}^{\prime}\varphi^{*}_{m_{1}\bm{k}_{1}+\bm{Q}}(\bm{r})\varphi^{*}_{n_{2}\bm{k}_{2}}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\\
\times v(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\varphi_{n_{1}\bm{k}_{1}}(\bm{r})\varphi_{m_{2}\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{Q}}(\bm{r}^{\prime}),

(18)

\matrixelement{t_1}{K^{\SX}(\qph)}{t_2}=-\int d\bm{r}\int d\bm{r}^{\prime}\varphi^{*}_{m_{1}\bm{k}_{1}+\bm{Q}}(\bm{r})\varphi^{*}_{n_{2}\bm{k}_{2}}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\\
\times W(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\varphi_{n_{1}\bm{k}_{1}}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\varphi_{m_{2}\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{Q}}(\bm{r})\ .

(19)

v
is the Coulomb interaction

\displaystyle v(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{r}}\frac{1}{|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}|},

(20)

where
\varepsilon_{r}
is an effective dielectric constant taking into account the possible dielectric screening of an embedding isotropic medium. Notice that
\bra{t_1}
and
\ket{t_2}
contain electron-hole transitions mediated by the same wave-vector
\bm{Q}
, due to momentum conservation as made explicit in the following by Eqs. (
30
) and (
31
).
The BSE given by Eq. (
15
) along with the kernel in Eq. (
17
) are represented in terms of Feynman diagrams in Fig.
1
(c) and (d).

In the SX approximation, the BSE may be recast as an eigenvalue problem of the two-body Hamiltonian

\matrixelement{t_1}{\hat{H}^{\eh}}{t_2}=E_{t_{1}}(\bm{Q})\delta_{t_{1}t_{2}}+f_{t_{1}}\matrixelement{t_1}{K(\qph)}{t_2}\ ,

(21)

where
E_{t_{1}}(\bm{Q})=(\epsilon_{m\bm{k}+\bm{Q}}-\epsilon_{n\bm{k}})
are the e-h excitation energies and
f_{t_{1}}=f_{n\bm{k}}-f_{m\bm{k}+\bm{Q}}
the occupation factors.
We note in Eq. (
21
) we are including both resonant and anti-resonant transitions, thus we are not employing the Tamm–Dancoff approximation
[
38
]
.
The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the e-h Hamiltonian in Eq. (
21
) are excitonic energies and wavefunctions
[
45
]
.
The diagonal component of the density-density response function may be related to the inverse of the e-h Hamiltonian as

\chi(\bm{Q},\omega)\\
=\sum\limits_{t_{1}t_{2}}\rho_{t_{1}}^{*}(\bm{Q})\left[\matrixelement{t_1}{\w\mathbb{1}-\hat{H}^{\eh}(\qph)}{t_2}\right]^{-1}\rho_{t_{2}}(\bm{Q}),

(22)

\rho_{t}(\bm{Q})=\matrixelement{n\k}{e^{-i\qph\cdot r}}{m\k+\qph}.

(23)

\rho_{t}(\bm{Q})
are the oscillator strengths; if one is interested in the non-diagonal components of Eq. (
22
), then only the oscillator strengths have to be changed in order to contain the dependence on local fields.
The inverse dielectric function is expressed, in terms of
\chi
, as

\varepsilon^{-1}(\bm{Q},\omega)=1+v(\bm{Q})\chi(\bm{Q},\omega)\ ,

(24)

where
v(\bm{Q})
is the Fourier transform of the Coulomb interaction in Eq. (
20
).

For the remainder of this section, we suppose
\bm{Q}
is a small momentum transfer near
\Gamma
.
As mentioned in the introduction, in this work we are interested in the optical absorption, i.e. the current response computed under the condition of null macroscopic electric field. The Coulomb kernel without the macroscopic component,
\overline{v}
is obtained by setting
\overline{v}_{\mathbf{G}=0}(\bm{Q})=0
, where

is the Fourier transform of the Coulomb potential. The corresponding kernel, given by Eq. (

\overline{K}^{\mathrm{H}}
.

The correspondent density-density response function is

\overline{\chi}(\bm{Q},\omega)\\
=\sum\limits_{t_{1}t_{2}}\rho_{t_{1}}^{*}(\bm{Q})\left[\matrixelement{t_1}{\w\mathbb{1}-\hat{\bar{H}}^{\eh}(\qph)}{t_2}\right]^{-1}\rho_{t_{2}}(\bm{Q})\ .

(25)

In the above equation,
\hat{\bar{H}}^{\mathrm{eh}}
is the same of Eq. (
21
) but with
K^{\mathrm{H}}
replaced by
\overline{K}^{\mathrm{H}}
.
The response function
\overline{\chi}
is related to the macroscopic dielectric matrix via

\varepsilon(\bm{Q},\omega)=1-v(\bm{Q})\overline{\chi}(\bm{Q},\omega)\ .

(26)

The formulation presented in this section is valid for three-dimensional crystals. For systems that are periodic in two directions but finite sized the third, e.g. graphene, more care as to be taken, as shown in the next section.

III
Dielectric properties of a 2D crystal with finite thickness

Graphene is a low-dimensional system.
In the last years, several studies have shown the importance to take into account the 2D nature of monolayer materials in the description of electronic screening
[
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
.
In ab-initio calculations based on a plane-wave representation of the orbitals, one possibility is to cutoff the Coulomb interaction so to remove spurious interaction between replicas
[
50
,
51
]
.
In our model, we instead take into account the nanosized nature of graphene by hypothesizing an analytic form of the orbitals in the non-periodic directions, as discussed in Ref.
52
and summarized in the following.

We consider a 2D crystal, thus a system periodic in two dimensions
(x,y)
and nanosized in the
z
direction with a typical thickness
d
.
In the long wavelength limit (
|\bm{Q}|d\ll 1
) and within the RPA, the screening properties of the layer are the same of a dielectric sheet located at
z=0

[
53
]
.
Outside this limit, finite thickness effects may become relevant, and they may depend on the nature of the electronic orbitals that determine the out-of-plane thickness of the material.
In this work, we are mainly interested in long-rage effects due to the Coulomb interaction.
For this reason, the accurate description of the
z
dependence of the electronic orbitals is not critical, and we therefore approximate it by a rectangular profile
[
52
]
.
Thus, we write the electronic orbitals as

\varphi_{i\bm{k}}(\bm{r})\approx\varphi_{i\bm{k}}(\bm{r}_{\parallel})\frac{\tilde{\Theta}(z)}{\sqrt{d}}\ ,

(27)

where
\bm{r}_{\parallel}=(x,y)
and
\tilde{\Theta}(z)\equiv\Theta(|z|-d/2)
is the Heaviside theta.
We emphasize that, since the Bloch theorem is valid only in the in-plane coordinates, the crystallographic momentum
\bm{k}
is oriented along the 2D periodic directions.

Thanks to Eq. (
27
),
z
integrals in the electronic self energy
\Sigma
and kernels
K
may be performed analytically to find effective equations of 2D sheets with a modified Coulomb interaction.

In fact, by substituting Eq. (
27
) into Eq. (
11
), the matrix elements of
\Sigma^{\mathrm{SX}}
between two generic states
\ket{a}
and
\ket{b}
are

\matrixelement{a}{\Sigma^{\SX}}{b}=\sum\limits_{m\bm{k}}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}f_{m\bm{k}^{\prime}}\matrixelement{a}{e^{i(\qscr+\G)\cdot\r_{\parallel}}}{m\k}\\
\times W^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\matrixelement{m\k}{e^{-i(\qscr+\G')\cdot\r_{\parallel}}}{b}

(28)

where

W^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\int\limits_{-d/2}^{d/2}dz\int\limits_{-d/2}^{d/2}dz^{\prime}W_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\bm{q},z,z^{\prime}).

(29)

Eq.
29
is the Fourier transform of the screened interaction along the periodic directions and averaged along the non-periodic direction, where
\mathbf{q}
is reduced to the first Brillouin zone and
\{\mathbf{G}\}
are 2D reciprocal lattice vectors.

In the same way, by substituting Eq. (
27
) into Eqs. (
18
)-(
19
), and Fourier transforming
v
and
W
along the periodic directions, we find

\matrixelement{t_1}{K^{\H}(\qph)}{t_2}=\frac{2}{A}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{G}}\matrixelement{m_1\k_1+\qph}{e^{i(\qph+\G)\cdot\r_{\parallel}}}{n_1\k_1}v^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}}(\bm{Q})\matrixelement{n_2\k_2}{e^{-i(\qph+\G)\cdot\r'_{\parallel}}}{m_2\k_2+\qph},

(30)

\matrixelement{t_1}{K^{\SX}(\qph)}{t_2}=\frac{1}{A}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\matrixelement{m_1\k_1+\qph}{e^{i(\k_1-\k_2+\G)\cdot\r_{\parallel}}}{m_2\k_2+\qph}W_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{2D}}(\bm{k}_{1}-\bm{k}_{2})\matrixelement{n_2\k_2}{e^{-i(\k_1-\k_2+\G')\cdot\r'_{\parallel}}}{n_1\k_1},

(31)

where
A
is the graphene unit-cell area and

v^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}}(\bm{q})=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\int\limits_{-d/2}^{d/2}dz\int\limits_{-d/2}^{d/2}dz^{\prime}v_{\mathbf{G}}(\bm{q},z,z^{\prime})=\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon_{r}|\bm{q}+\mathbf{G}|}F(|\bm{q}+\mathbf{G}|d)

(32)

is an effective 2D Coulomb interaction that includes finite (orbital-independent) thickness effects through the form factor

F(x)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\int\limits_{-d/2}^{d/2}dz\int\limits_{-d/2}^{d/2}dz^{\prime}e^{-|x/d||z-z^{\prime}|}\\
=\frac{2}{x}\left(1+\frac{e^{-x}-1}{x}\right)\ .

(33)

The Fourier transform of the density-density response function, assuming the
z
profile of Eq. (
27
), shows the same rectangular
z
profile as the electronic orbitals

\chi(\bm{Q},z,z^{\prime},\omega)=\chi^{\mathrm{2D}}(\bm{Q},\omega)\frac{\tilde{\Theta}(z)\tilde{\Theta}(z^{\prime})}{d^{2}}\ .

(34)

This is inherited by the out-of-plane form of
\chi^{0}
, which is directly expressed as a function of orbitals, via the Dyson equation. The 2D effective density-density response functions
\chi^{\mathrm{2D}}
and
\overline{\chi}^{\mathrm{2D}}
are evaluated from Eqs. (
22
) and (
25
) with the kernels defined in Eqs. (
30
)-(
31
).

The 3D Fourier transforms of the inverse and macroscopic dielectric functions are not defined for a 2D crystal, as described in more detail in Ref.
54
.
Still, we can define effective 2D dielectric functions averaged along the
z
direction as

\varepsilon^{-1,\mathrm{2D}}(\bm{Q},\omega)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\int\limits_{-d/2}^{d/2}dz\int\limits_{-d/2}^{d/2}dz^{\prime}\varepsilon^{-1}(\bm{Q},z,z^{\prime},\omega)\\
=1+v^{\mathrm{2D}}(\bm{Q})\chi^{\mathrm{2D}}(\bm{Q},\omega),

(35)

\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}(\bm{Q},\omega)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\int\limits_{d/2}^{d/2}dz\int\limits_{d/2}^{d/2}dz^{\prime}\varepsilon_{M}(\bm{Q},z,z^{\prime},\omega)\\
=1-v^{\mathrm{2D}}(\bm{Q})\overline{\chi}^{\mathrm{2D}}(\bm{Q},\omega).

(36)

Finally, the long wavelength limit of the 2D effective conductivity is related to the 2D dielectric function via the following relation
[
55
]
:

\varepsilon^{\mathrm{2D}}(\bm{Q}\to 0,\omega)-1=\lim\limits_{|\bm{Q}|\to 0}2\pi i\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{2D}}(\omega)Q}{\omega+i\eta}\ .

(37)

By combining Eq. (
37
) with Eq. (
35
), we find the 2D optical conductivity as

\sigma^{\mathrm{2D}}(\omega)=\lim\limits_{|\bm{Q}|\to 0}\frac{i(\omega+i\eta)\overline{\chi}^{\mathrm{2D}}(\bm{Q},\omega)}{|\bm{Q}|^{2}}\ .

(38)

We note that
\overline{\chi}^{\mathrm{2D}}\to 0
as
|\bm{Q}|\to 0
with a quadratic power, thus the optical conductivity is finite.

IV
Many-body perturbation theory applied to graphene TB model

In this section, we describe how we evaluate the quasi-particle corrections and response functions of graphene starting from the five nearest neighbour TB model for graphene described in Appx.
A
.
In addition we show how we model the screened interaction
W
for graphene.

IV.1
Screened-exchange self-energy

Our aim is to evaluate the SX self-energy corrections within the TB model for graphene.
In this case, the quasi-particle Hamiltonian, given by Eq. (
4
), may be written in the basis of the Bloch functions as

\displaystyle H^{\mathrm{SX}}_{\bm{k}}=H^{0}_{\bm{k}}+\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix
0 & \Sigma_{\k}^{\SX} \\
\Sigma_{\k}^{\SX*} & 0
\endlx@physics@matrix)\ ,H^{0}_{\bm{k}}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix
\mathcal{g}_{\k} & \mathcal{f}_{\k} \\
\mathcal{f}^*_{\k} & \mathcal{g}_{\k}
\endlx@physics@matrix)\ .

(39)

H^{0}_{\bm{k}}
is the TB Hamiltonian, obtained as described in App.
A
; its matrix elements are defined in Eq. (
53
) and (
52
).
For freestanding graphene (i.e. at zero doping), the diagonal terms of the self-energy are
\bm{k}
- and atom-independent, thus they can be neglected.
For simplicity, we neglect the diagonal terms of the self-energy even in the doped case, as for low doping these terms are not expected to change the physical conclusions.

The eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
39
) give the quasi-particle energies

\epsilon_{\pi^{*}/\pi\bm{k}}=\mathcal{g}_{\bm{k}}\pm|\mathcal{f}_{\bm{k}}+\Sigma_{\bm{k}}^{\mathrm{SX}}|\ .

(40)

Self-energy corrections do not change the phase of the spin components
\phi
in a first nearest neighbour tight-binding model, as demonstrated in the supporting information of Ref.
37
.
We numerically verify that also in our five-nearest neighour tight-binding model the phase
\phi
[defined in Eq. (
56
)] do not appreciably change.
Thus, we can safely use the quasi-particle orbital approximation
\varphi_{n\bm{k}}\approx\varphi^{0}_{n\bm{k}}
already described in Sec.
II.1
.

We now consider Eq. (
28
), and disregard non-diagonal local field effects. As we derive in Appx.
B
,
\Sigma_{\bm{k}}^{\mathrm{SX}}
assumes the form

\Sigma^{\mathrm{SX}}_{\bm{k}}=\frac{-1}{N_{k}A}\sum\limits_{\bm{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\Delta f_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}|\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}W^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\phi^{*}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\ ,

(41)

where
\Delta f_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}=f_{\pi^{*}\bm{k}^{\prime}}-f_{\pi\bm{k}^{\prime}}
,
\phi
is the spinorial phase defined in Eq. (
56
) and
\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}}
is the atomic form factor defined in Eq. (
60
).
We note
W
depends only on one
\mathbf{G}
vector as we neglect some local field effects in screening.
This approximation is described in Sec.
IV.2
.

The screened Coulomb of Eq. (
41
) implicitly depends on the set of energies
\{\epsilon_{\pi^{*}/\pi\bm{k}}\}
determined via Eq.
41
. This means that a self-consistent procedure is needed in order to evaluate
W
, in the SX approximation, which we implement in our framework. Beside this, we also study the case where the screened interaction is evaluated once and for all, in a non self-consistent way, using the TB energies; we refer to this screened interaction as
W^{0}
, and to the approximation as SX
0
. In this approximation, Eqs. (
39
) and (
40
) are still valid, but with a self-energy containing
W^{0}
as

\Sigma^{\mathrm{SX^{0}}}_{\bm{k}}=\frac{-1}{N_{k}A}\sum\limits_{\bm{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\Delta f^{0}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}|\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}W^{0,\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\phi^{*}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\ ,

(42)

where
\Delta f^{0}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}=f^{0}_{\pi^{*}\bm{k}^{\prime}}-f^{0}_{\pi\bm{k}^{\prime}}
.
As it regards the electronic populations, we find that for the electronic temperature studied in this work (
T=0
or
T=4
K), we can safely set
\Delta f_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\approx\Delta f^{0}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}
.
In the following subsection, we model the screened interaction in graphene for both SX and SX
0
approximations.

IV.2
Screened interaction

In this section we describe our model for the screened interaction, within the RPA, that is used both in the evaluation of the quasi-particle self-energy Eq. (
28
) and correlation kernel of the BSE equation Eq. (
31
).
The effective screened interaction of a 2D crystal within the orbital approximation given by Eq. (
27
) may be written as

W^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\bm{Q},\omega)=\tilde{\varepsilon}^{-1,\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\bm{Q},\omega)v^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\bm{Q})\ ,

(43)

where
\tilde{\varepsilon}^{-1,\mathrm{2D}}
is the inverse dielectric function, obtained from Eq. (
35
) with a density-density response
\tilde{\chi}
obtained within the RPA

\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\bm{Q},\omega)\\
=\left[\mathbb{1}-\chi^{0,\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}}(\bm{Q},\omega)v^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}}(\bm{Q})\right]^{-1}\chi^{0,\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\bm{Q},\omega)\ .

(44)

A sketch of the Feyman diagrams included in the screened interaction
W
can be found in Fig.
1
(b).

\chi^{0,\mathrm{2D}}
is the Fourier transform of the irreducible polarizability

\chi^{0,\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\bm{q},\omega)=2\lim\limits_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\sum\limits_{m,n=\pi}^{\pi^{*}}\sum\limits_{\bm{k}}(f_{m\bm{k}}-f_{n\bm{k}+\bm{q}})\frac{\matrixelement{n\k+\qscr}{e^{i(\qscr+\G)\cdot\r}}{m\k}\matrixelement{m\k}{e^{-i(\qscr+\G')\cdot\r}}{n\k+\qscr}}{\omega-(\epsilon_{n\bm{k}+\bm{q}}-\epsilon_{m\bm{k}})+i\eta}\ .

(45)

For simplicity, we neglect off-diagonal matrix elements of
\chi^{0,\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}
, which has been shown to be a good approximation for graphene
[
53
]
.
Thus, we write
\chi^{0,\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}=\chi^{0,\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}}\delta_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}
,
W^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}=W^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}}\delta_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}
and we define the short-hand notations
\chi^{0,\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}}\equiv\chi^{0,\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}}
and
W_{\mathbf{G}}=W_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}}
.
Within the proposed approximations, the screened interaction can be written as

W^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}}(\bm{q})=\frac{v^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}}(\bm{q})}{1-v^{\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}}(\bm{q})\chi^{0,\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}}(\bm{q})}\ .

(46)

The electronic screening is encoded in the irreducible polarizability.

In the self-consistent SX, Eqs. (
40
), (
41
), (
45
) and (
46
) must be solved self-consistently, as the irreducible polarizability depend on the quasi-particle energies.
In Fig.
1
(a) and (b) we sketch the Feynman diagrams included in the self-consistent SX.
This is analogous to self-consistent quasi-particle GW theory
[
56
]
with the static approroximation of the screened interaction.
In the non-self consistent case (SX
0
) we simply use
W^{0}
.

Practically, we approximate the static screened interaction
W
or
W^{0}
of graphene with screening obtained from low energy models.
In fact, it is known from the literature that low-energy excitations are the most important ones in the description of long-wavelength screening
[
31
]
, that is the responsible of graphene many-body features.
This procedure speeds up the calculations, as the evaluation of the screened interaction is usually the most time-consuming part of a many-body perturbation theory simulation.
We now list the explicit forms of
W
and
W^{0}
used in this work for different doping levels. The effect of the embedding in the screened interaction is treated in Sec.
IV.4
.

IV.2.1
W^{0}
of freestanding graphene

For this zero doping case, we approximate the static irreducible polarizability, given by Eq. (
45
), with the one of the Dirac cone model at
T=0
K

\chi^{0,\mathrm{2D}}_{\mathbf{G}}(\bm{q})=-\frac{|\bm{q}+\mathbf{G}|}{4v_{\mathrm{F}}}\ ,

(47)

where
v_{\mathrm{F}}
is the Fermi velocity of the TB model.

By inserting Eqs. (
47
) and (
32
) into Eq. (
46
), the screened interaction can be evaluated analytically.

IV.2.2
W^{0}
of doped graphene

Also in this case we approximate the static irreducible polarizability of the TB model with the one of the Dirac cone model at
T=0
K.
In case of finite doping, the irreducible polarizability has been already derived in Ref.
57
, and it reads

\chi_{\mathbf{G}}^{0,\mathrm{2D}}(\bm{q})=-\frac{2k_{\mathrm{F}}}{\pi v_{\mathrm{F}}}\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} 1&,\ \ \ |\bm{q}+\mathbf{G}|\leq 2k_{\mathrm{F}}\\
1+\frac{\pi|\bm{q}+\mathbf{G}|}{8k_{\mathrm{F}}}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-\frac{4k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{|\bm{q}+\mathbf{G}|^{2}}}-\frac{|\bm{q}+\mathbf{G}|}{4k_{\mathrm{F}}}\sin^{-1}\frac{2k_{\mathrm{F}}}{|\bm{q}+\mathbf{G}|}&,\ \ \ |\bm{q}+\mathbf{G}|>2k_{\mathrm{F}}\ \end{aligned}\end{cases},

(48)

where
k_{\mathrm{F}}=\sqrt{\pi n}
is the Fermi wavevector,
n
being the carrier (electrons or holes) density.
In the limit
k_{\mathrm{F}}\to 0
, Eq. (
48
) tends to Eq. (
47
).

As in the previous case, by inserting Eqs. (
47
) and (
32
) into Eq. (
46
), the screened interaction can be evaluated analytically.

IV.2.3
W
of freestanding graphene

In this case, we want to calculate the irreducible polarizability obtained from the quasi-particle band structure in Eq. (
40
).
The Dirac cone approximation can no more be applied, due to the Fermi velocity renormalization with a logarithmic divergence at the neutrality point, as shown later in detail.

However, in Ref.
31
a proper low-energy model of the quasi-particle band structure is derived.
We suppose the band structure of the quasi-particle cone has the following functional form

\begin{split}\epsilon_{\pi^{*}/\pi}(k)&=\pm v_{\mathrm{F}}k\pm fv_{\mathrm{F}}\frac{k}{2}\left[\cosh^{-1}{\left(\frac{2k_{c}}{k}\right)}+\frac{1}{2}\right]\ ,\end{split}

(49)

where
f
and
k_{c}
are numerical parameters fitted with numerical data obtained by sampling the surroundings of the Dirac point.

Next, the k sum of the irreducible polarizability in Eq. (
45
) is evaluated numerically in elliptic coordinates to exploit the symmetries of the sum.
The resulting screened interaction is evaluated on a numerical logarithmic grid around
q=0
, then evaluated when needed by the code with a linear interpolation scheme.

IV.2.4
W
of doped graphene

The procedure we used to calculate the screened interaction is the same as Sec.
IV.2.3
, but the low-energy quasi-particle band structure is interpolated with Chebyshev polynomials instead of fitted with Eq. (
49
). In fact, to the best of our knowledge, an analytical low-energy model that can be exploit to speed up the calculation is not present in the literature.

IV.3
Bethe-Salpeter equation

The kernels in Eqs. (
30

57

We model screened interaction
W^{\mathrm{2D}}
as explained in Sec.
IV.2

Non interacting excitation energies

are evaluated or from Eq. (

IV.4
Doping and environmental effects

We model doping effects by changing the chemical potential
\mu
in the occupation numbers
f_{m\bm{k}}
and using a proper screened interaction as explained in Sec.
IV.2
.
In principle, as doping changes the Kohn-Sham potential, the DFT TB parametrization is doping dependent.
However, we neglect the TB fit dependence on doping, as this effect is negligible
[
53
]
.

Environmental effects are taken into account with a static uniform dielectric constant
\varepsilon_{r}\neq 1
in the Coulomb interaction
v^{\mathrm{2D}}
given by Eq. (
32
).
For what concerns the screened interaction, the environment reduces its intensity through a reduction of
v^{\mathrm{2D}}
in Eq. (
43

However, there is also a screening reduction, enchancing
W
, given by an increase of the inverse dielectric function, as can be seen from Eq. (
35

The net effect is a reduction of
W
with respect to the freestanding case, but with a lower value than
\varepsilon_{r}
due to the reduced screening caused by the environment.

VI
Results

In this section, we summarize the results obtained with the computational scheme proposed in the theoretical section.
Firstly, we discuss the quasi-particle band structure, and compare the Fermi velocity renormalization with experiments.
Then, we study the optical conductivity.
In both cases, we focus on the role of self-consistency in the quasi-particle correction.
Finally, we study the effects of doping and dielectric embedding on both the group velocity near the K point and the optical conductivity.
A validation between our TB and ab-initio calculations can be found in Appx.
C
. In Appx.
D
, we show a wider range of data sets for completeness, along with the density-density response function at
q=
K.

In all of the figures in the results section, we highlight two region intervals in the low-energy sector of the graphene spectrum.
The first region (
|\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}|\lesssim 0.1
eV) is typically relevant for low-doping electron-phonon mediated transport properties or for the hydrodynamic transport regime dominated by e-e interaction
[
32
,
69
]
. In such a region, the band structure is strongly affected by the many-body renormalization of Fermi velocities, with an expected large impact on the evaluation of transport properties.
The second region is determined by the phonon energy range (
|\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}|\lesssim 0.2
eV), where electron excitations are in resonance with optical phonons, originating non-adiabatic effects
[
70
]
.

VII
Conclusions

We proposed a TB plus many-body perturbation theory model to describe low- and high-energy many-body effects in graphene with a unified framework.
Within the static screening approximation, the renormalization of the Fermi velocity is in agreement with experiments, provided that the quasi-particle corrections are calculated self-consistently with the screened interaction, in accordance with Ref.
37
.
The frequency dependence of the screened interaction may be relevant for doped graphene, due to the presence of the low-energy Drude plasmon, as emphasized in Ref.
74
for the fundamental gap of doped transition-metal-dichalcogenide monolayers. However, the inclusion of finite frequency effects in the screened interaction is by itself a major topic on its own, and will be the addressed in a future work.
The optical conductivity is in accordance both with ab-initio calculations and experiments. In this case, the self-consistent solution of the quasi-particle corrections and the screened interaction does not play a critical role due to the partial cancellation between quasi-particle corrections and excitonic effects.
Also the density-density response function at
q=\mathrm{K}
(shown in Appx.
D
) and low energy is in accordance with ab-initio calculations.
Instead, at higher energies some deviations occur, probably due to the approximate description of local field effects in the TB model, that does not accurately catch the microscopic description of the electronic orbitals.

We further studied the effect of doping and environment on the quasi-particle band structure and electronic response.
We found that doping quenches the logarithmic divergence of the group velocity, while the environment reduces the group velocity due to the screening of electron-electron interaction, but without changing qualitatively the results.
In the optical conductivity, the environmental effects slightly red shift the
\pi
plasmon peak.
Instead, the presence of doping do not change the plasmon position.
This counter-intuitive behaviours, since in both cases the electron-electron interaction is more screened than in the freestanding case, are due to the partial cancellation between quasi-particle corrections and excitonic effects.
The same results are valid also for the density-density response function at
q=\mathrm{K}
.

In principle, this framework can be extended to take into account also the lattice response and electron-phonon coupling matrix elements. The proposed unified framework will allow also to study whether the many-body effects described in this paper may influence the lattice response, as we will do in a subsequent work.
