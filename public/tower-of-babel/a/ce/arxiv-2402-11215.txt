AdAdaGrad : Adaptive Batch Size Schemes for Adaptive Gradient Methods
Tim Tsz-Kit Lau, Han Liu, Mladen Kolar

Abstract
The choice of batch sizes in minibatch stochastic gradient optimizers is critical in large-scale model training for both optimization and generalization performance. Although large-batch training is arguably the dominant training paradigm for large-scale deep learning due to hardware advances, the generalization performance of the model deteriorates compared to small-batch training, leading to the so-called “generalization gap” phenomenon. To mitigate this, we investigate adaptive batch size strategies derived from adaptive sampling methods, originally developed only for stochastic gradient descent. Given the significant interplay between learning rates and batch sizes, and considering the prevalence of adaptive gradient methods in deep learning, we emphasize the need for adaptive batch size strategies in these contexts. We introduce AdAdaGrad and its scalar variant AdAdaGrad-Norm , which progressively increase batch sizes during training, while model updates are performed using AdaGrad and AdaGrad-Norm . We prove that AdAdaGrad-Norm converges with high probability at a rate of O ⁡ ( 1 / K ) {O}(1/K) to find a first-order stationary point of smooth nonconvex functions within K K iterations. AdAdaGrad also demonstrates similar convergence properties when integrated with a novel coordinate-wise variant of our adaptive batch size strategies. We corroborate our theoretical claims by performing image classification experiments, highlighting the merits of the proposed schemes in terms of both training efficiency and model generalization. Our work unveils the potential of adaptive batch size strategies for adaptive gradient optimizers in large-scale model training.

Introduction

Large-scale optimization algorithms
(
Bottou et al. 2018
)
form the foundation of the success of deep learning in the era of generative AI. Minibatch stochastic gradient descent (
SGD
)
(
Robbins and Monro 1951
)
and its many variants, along with batch sampling techniques, are the main workhorse for training deep neural networks. However, training deep neural networks, such as those in transformers, is notoriously challenging because of their high dimensionality and nonconvex landscape. This complexity necessitates extensive hyperparameter tuning and sophisticated training strategies for optimizers to circumvent premature divergence and training instabilities. Consequently, training deep learning models often appears as more an art than a science. The most critical hyperparameter is arguably the learning rate (or step size). Adaptive gradient methods with adaptive learning rates, such as
AdaDelta

(
Zeiler 2012
)
,
AdaGrad

(
Duchi et al. 2011
)
, and
Adam

(
Kingma and Ba 2015
)
, are now prevalent, as they reduce the need for meticulous tuning and complex learning rate schedules, typically required for
SGD
. Another important, yet frequently overlooked hyperparameter is the batch size. It moderates the trade-off between computational efficiency and model generalization by controlling the magnitude of noise in batch gradients. However, the selection of batch sizes in deep learning training remains largely heuristic, such as using a constant size for convolutional networks or a linear warm-up for large language models
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Rae et al. 2021
;
Hoffmann et al. 2022
)
, and is usually predetermined before training begins. Furthermore, from a hardware utilization perspective, the use of a large number of distributed computational resources (i.e., GPUs or TPUs) necessitates the large-batch training regime when parallel minibatch
SGD

(
Zinkevich et al. 2010
;
Dean et al. 2012
)
is used. In addition, the intricate relationship between learning rates and batch sizes deserves attention. Specifically,
Smith et al. 2018
demonstrated the equivalence of reducing step sizes and increasing batch sizes, but this principle applies mainly to
SGD
. The impact of varying batch sizes in adaptive gradient methods has not yet been fully explored.

In light of this, our work seeks to unravel the complexities surrounding the determination of optimal batch sizes for adaptive gradient methods. We aim to introduce automated schedules capable of dynamically deciding when to increase the batch size and by how much, based on the training needs. Our approach is theoretically grounded and relies on the statistics of batch gradients of iterates, thereby adapting to the training dynamics of the models. Our proposed schedules are able to reduce the generalization gap in large-batch training, but at the same time also maintain the utilization of large batches at the later stage of the overall training process. Our proposed strategies are based on adaptive sampling methods
(
Byrd et al. 2012
;
Bollapragada et al. 2018a
)
. In the context of deep learning,
De et al. 2016
;
De et al. 2017
have numerically shown the effectiveness of these methods with
AdaDelta
. However, the convergence properties of such adaptive sampling methods for adaptive gradient methods have not been thoroughly investigated, leaving a gap between theory and practice. Moreover, in existing adaptive sampling methods developed mainly for
SGD
, step sizes are often fixed or adjusted using backtracking line search procedures (mainly for convex problems). This becomes computationally impractical or inefficient for larger models, especially given the nonconvex nature of deep neural networks.

The development of adaptive batch size strategies for deep learning is not a novel concept, featuring methodologies such as Big Batch
SGD

(
De et al. 2016
;
De et al. 2017
)
, CABS
(
Balles et al. 2017
)
, AdaBatch
(
Devarakonda et al. 2017
)
, SimiGrad
(
Qin et al. 2021
)
, and AdaScale
SGD

(
Johnson et al. 2020
)
, which adapts learning rates for large batches rather than directly adjusting batch sizes. However, these methods often lack a principled basis with rigorous convergence guarantees or are limited to analyses of
SGD
under stringent conditions (e.g., convexity or the Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition), despite the prevalence of adaptive gradient methods in deep learning. Furthermore, these approaches still necessitate the choice of learning rate adjustments, such as line search routines or schedulers, leaving a gap in achieving full adaptivity. Furthermore, strategies for determining new batch sizes may rely on heuristic rules, such as geometric growth or decay rates
(
Qin et al. 2021
)
.

Contributions.

In this work, our objective is to bridge the theoretical and practice gaps of adaptive batch size schemes for adaptive gradient methods. We introduce two principal adaptive batch size schemes, grounded in the so-called
adaptive sampling methods

(
Byrd et al. 2012
;
Friedlander and Schmidt 2012
;
Bollapragada et al. 2018a
)
, tailored for adaptive gradient methods. Our focus is on
AdaGrad
and its norm variant
AdaGrad-Norm
, which are among the simplest and most extensively studied adaptive gradient methods. The development of adaptive batch size schemes for these methods has significant theoretical and practical relevance.

The technical contributions of this work are three-fold.
From a theoretical perspective, we establish a sublinear convergence rate (with high probability) for our proposed methods when applied with
AdaGrad-Norm
and
AdaGrad
to smooth nonconvex objectives. This significantly broadens the existing body of work which has mainly focused on
SGD
. Moreover, we relax the Lipschitz smoothness condition on the objective function by adopting the generalized smoothness concept introduced in
Zhang et al. 2020b
;
Zhang et al. 2020a
. This adaptation allows for more generalized and realistic applications in the current practice of deep learning. On the empirical front, we demonstrate the efficacy of our proposed methods through various numerical experiments on image classification tasks. These experiments highlight the benefits of the adaptivity of our schemes, driven by both adaptive batch sizes and step sizes. Finally, we provide an efficient implementation of the proposed approach in PyTorch, making use of the
torch.func
module for efficient parallelized computations of per-sample gradients using the so-called vectorizing map function
vmap
. An open source code repository will be provided upon acceptance. To the best of our knowledge, our proposed methods are the first adaptive batch size schemes based on adaptive sampling methods for adaptive gradient methods, which are convergent with guarantees, empirically performant and efficiently implemented in deep learning libraries.

Related Work

We give an overview of related work on batch sizes in model training and adaptive sampling methods. Further related work can be found in
Appendix

A
.

Large-batch training.

In stochastic gradient optimizers, batch sizes play a crucial role in controlling the variance of stochastic gradients as estimators of the full deterministic gradients. Although the noise from stochastic gradients offers benefits for nonconvex optimization, the trend towards using larger batches in large-scale model training has become standard, thanks to advancements in parallelized hardware such as GPUs, significantly reducing training time. The concept of large-batch training in deep learning was popularized in
Smith and Le 2018
;
Smith et al. 2018
and has been widely adopted in applications such as ImageNet classification
(
Goyal et al. 2017
)
and BERT training
(
You et al. 2020
)
. In the work of
LeCun et al. 2002
, it has been understood that model generalization deteriorates in large-batch training. Large-batch training tends to result in a loss landscape with many sharp minima, which are harder to escape during optimization and hence lead to worse generalization
(
Keskar et al. 2017
)
. The impact of batch sizes has been further examined in a more principled manner in subsequent studies. For example,
McCandlish et al. 2018
introduced an empirical model for large batch training without providing rigorous proof, postulating the existence of critical batch sizes through extensive numerical simulations on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), LSTMs, and VAEs. Meanwhile,
Kaplan et al. 2020
focused their experiments on transformers.
Zhang et al. 2019
explored how critical batch sizes vary with the nature of optimizers, including acceleration (momentum), preconditioning, and exponential moving average, through both large-scale experiments and a noisy quadratic model.
Granziol et al. 2022
applied random matrix theory to examine the batch Hessian, theoretically establishing learning rates as a function of batch size (i.e., the
scaling rule
) for both
SGD
(
linear
) and adaptive gradient methods (
square root
), with experimental validation. Although the “generalization gap” can be narrowed by using larger learning rates proportional to batch sizes to maintain the gradient noise scale
(
Hoffer et al. 2017
;
Smith and Le 2018
;
Smith et al. 2018
)
, it cannot be completely eliminated. However,
Shallue et al. 2019
investigated the impact of batch sizes in the context of data parallelism and empirically characterized the effects of large-batch training, finding no evidence of a degradation in generalization performance.

Adaptive sampling methods.

In the realm of stochastic optimization, a more theoretically grounded approach known as
adaptive sampling methods
has been developed for batch (or minibatch) algorithms.
Byrd et al. 2012
introduced a method termed the norm test, which adaptively increases the batch size throughout the optimization process. The rationale behind the norm test traces back to
Carter 1991
and represents a more generalized condition compared to the approach of
Friedlander and Schmidt 2012
. Both approaches demonstrate linear convergence when the batch size increases geometrically.
Bollapragada et al. 2018a
proposed the augmented inner product test, which allows for more gradual increases in batch sizes than the norm test. Furthermore,
Cartis and Scheinberg 2018
introduced a relaxation of the norm test that allows its condition to be violated with a probability of less than
0.5
. This suite of adaptive sampling methods is considered part of the variance-reduced optimization algorithms
(
Johnson and Zhang 2013
;
Reddi et al. 2016
)
that are widely used in machine learning
(
Gower et al. 2020
)
.

3
Problem Formulation

Notation.

We define
\llbracket n\rrbracket\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,n\}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
,
\mathbb{R}_{+}\coloneqq\left[0,\infty\right)
and
\mathbb{R}_{++}\coloneqq\left(0,\infty\right)
. We denote the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and its induced
L_{2}
norm by
\|\cdot\|
. For a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
[x]_{j}
denotes its
j
th coordinate (
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
). For a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
,
\partial_{j}f
denotes its partial derivative with respect to its
j
th coordinate for
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
. The ceiling function is denoted by
\lceil\cdot\rceil
.

Problem setting.

We consider the problem of minimizing the
expected risk

\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathsf{P}}[f(x;\xi)]
with respect to
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the random variable
\xi\in\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
is distributed according to the unknown true data distribution
\mathsf{P}
. We approximate
\mathsf{P}
with the empirical distribution
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\updelta_{\xi_{i}}
, where
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i\in\llbracket n\rrbracket}
is the sample of size
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, which leads to the
empirical risk minimization
problem:

\minimize_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ F(x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\widehat{\mathsf{P}}}[f(x;\xi)]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x;\xi_{i}).

When the sample size
n
is large, the gradient of
F
is approximated by its batch counterparts. Given a batch of samples
\mathcal{B}\subset\llbracket n\rrbracket
of size
b\coloneqq|\mathcal{B}|
, we define the batch loss associated with the batch
\mathcal{B}
by
F_{\mathcal{B}}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}f(x;\xi_{i})
. If
f(\cdot;\xi)
is continuously differentiable for any
\xi\in\mathcal{Z}
, the loss and batch loss gradients are given, respectively, by
\nabla F(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla f(x;\xi_{i})
and
\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}}(x)=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\nabla f(x;\xi_{i})
. The batch gradient is an unbiased estimator for the loss function gradient, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}}[\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}}(x)]=\nabla F(x)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In many applications, including deep learning, the objective function
F
is nonconvex. Thus, we consider the problem of finding a (first-order)
\varepsilon
-stationary point
x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies
\|\nabla F(x^{\star})\|^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon
, rather than a global minimum that is intractable.

4
Adaptive Sampling Methods

The adaptive batch schemes proposed in this paper are based on a family of
adaptive sampling methods
for stochastic optimization problems. Although such methods are extended to problems with different structures (see
Section

2
for details), here we focus on the simplest
unconstrained
setting.

4.1
Norm Test

Byrd et al. 2012
proposed an adaptive sampling method, called the
norm test
or the
norm condition
, that was derived according to the following fundamental fact in stochastic optimization. If
f(\cdot;\xi)
is a convex and continuously differentiable function for any
\xi\in\mathcal{Z}
, then
-\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
is a descent direction for
F
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
if there exists
\eta\in\left[0,1\right)
such that

\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)\coloneqq\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}}(x)-\nabla F(x)\|\leqslant\eta\|\nabla F(x)\|.

(1)

See
Appendix

C
for its proof. When the sample size
n
is large,
\nabla F
is not available, but
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
can be approximated by

\widehat{\delta}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)^{2}\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}}(\nabla f(x;\xi_{i}))}{b}\cdot\frac{n-b}{n-1},

where, for any vector-valued function
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the sample variance is defined by

\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}}(h(x;\xi_{i}))\coloneqq\frac{1}{b-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\left\lVert h(x;\xi_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{i\in\mathcal{B}}[h(x;\xi_{i})]\right\rVert^{2}.

(2)

Therefore, as the sample size
n\to\infty
, condition (
1
) can be approximated as
\frac{1}{b}\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}}(\nabla f(x;\xi_{i}))\leqslant\eta^{2}\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}}(x)\|^{2}
, which also corresponds to the case of
sampling with replacement
. In a practical implementation, the
dynamic sample gradient algorithm
with the norm test
(
Byrd et al. 2012
)
performs the following
SGD
update:
x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})
with step size
\alpha_{k}>0
, and then checks the following
(approximate) norm test
condition:

\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}))}{b_{k}}\leqslant\eta^{2}\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|^{2}.

(3)

This can also be viewed as an approximation of the
exact variance norm test
in the stochastic setting:

\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})-\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\right]\leqslant\eta^{2}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2},

(4)

meaning that the condition (
1
) holds in expectation. Here we abbreviate the conditional expectation on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
(that is, the sigma algebra
\mathcal{F}_{k}\coloneqq\sigma(\{x_{1},\mathcal{B}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{B}_{k-1}\})
) by
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k}]
. Should the condition (
3
) not be met, the subsequent batch size is determined by the formula:
b_{k+1}=\left\lceil\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}))/\left(\eta^{2}\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|^{2}\right)\right\rceil
, and the batch
\mathcal{B}_{k+1}
expands accordingly. The selection of the constant
\eta
influences the probability of obtaining a descent direction, affecting the probability of increasing the batch size.

To ensure that the enlarged batch
\mathcal{B}_{k+1}
satisfies the norm test condition, we employ a heuristic approach based on the assumption that the increase in batch size occurs gradually enough. Therefore, for any iterate
x_{k}
produced by
SGD
, the following approximations hold:
\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k+1}}(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}))\approx\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}))
and
\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k+1}}(x_{k})\|\approx\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|
, where
\mathcal{B}_{k}
is the original batch and
\mathcal{B}_{k+1}
is the new larger batch. In practice, these approximate equalities are not explicitly verified to maintain computational efficiency, and the batch for the next iteration,
\mathcal{B}_{k+1}
, is used directly without additional checks.

4.2
Inner Product Test

Bollapragada et al. 2018a
noted that the norm test often leads to a rapid increase in batch size, which could negate the benefits of adaptive sampling, and proposed the
inner product test
as an alternative. This test further controls the variance of the term
\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k}),\nabla F(x_{k})\rangle
to ensure that
-\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})
is a descent direction with
high probability
. The test selects a large enough batch size
b_{k}
to meet the
(exact variance) inner product test
condition, which is satisfied when there is a
\vartheta>0
ensuring that:

\frac{1}{b_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\left(\langle\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}),\nabla F(x_{k})\rangle-\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\right)^{2}\right]\leqslant\vartheta^{2}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{4}.

(5)

As before, since
\nabla F
is not available, the following
(approximate) inner product test
condition is usually used in practice:

\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}(\langle\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}),\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\rangle)}{b_{k}}\leqslant\vartheta^{2}\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|^{4},

(6)

where the variance on the left hand side can be evaluated by (
2
). To establish a convergence rate, it is crucial to eliminate the possibility that
\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})
and
\nabla F(x_{k})
are (near) orthogonal. Motivated by this need for theoretical guarantees,
Bollapragada et al. 2018a
also introduce the following condition of the
(exact variance) orthogonality test
: there exists
\nu>0
such that

\frac{1}{b_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\left\lVert\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i})-\frac{\langle\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}),\nabla F(x_{k})\rangle}{\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}}\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leqslant\nu^{2}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}.

(7)

The above exact test also takes an approximate form for implementation purposes, given by

\frac{1}{b_{k}}\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i})-\frac{\langle\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}),\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\rangle}{\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|^{2}}\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\right)\leqslant\nu^{2}\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|^{2},

(8)

where the variance on the left hand side can again be computed via (
2
). These two tests are collectively referred to as the
augmented inner product test
.

Note that both (
6
) and (
8
) have to be satisfied, which collectively leads to the
(approximate) augmented inner product test
. If any of the conditions are violated, the batch size will be updated using the formula

b_{k+1}=\left\lceil\max\left\{\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}(\langle\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}),\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\rangle)}{\vartheta^{2}\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|^{4}},\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i})-\frac{\langle\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}),\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\rangle}{\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|^{2}}\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\right)}{\nu^{2}\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|^{2}}\right\}\right\rceil,

and the batch
\mathcal{B}_{k+1}
is augmented with the above batch size. The constants
(\vartheta,\nu)\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{2}
have to be chosen in practice. In implementation, we might also use the inner product test alone, since the (near) orthogonality of
\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})
and
\nabla F(x_{k})
has not been observed in practice as mentioned in
Bollapragada et al. 2018a
, and the computation of the quantities in (
8
) would induce extra computational overhead for large-scale training. Then the batch size is updated using the formula
b_{k+1}=\left\lceil\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}(\langle\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}),\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\rangle)/\left(\vartheta^{2}\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|^{4}\right)\right\rceil
.

4.3
Adaptive Sampling Methods for Adaptive Gradient Methods

We focus on two simple adaptive gradient methods,
AdaGrad

(
Duchi et al. 2011
;
McMahan and Streeter 2010
)
and
AdaGrad-Norm

(
Streeter and McMahan 2010
)
, whose step sizes are computed adaptively based on the magnitudes of previous (batch) stochastic gradients.

AdaGrad
was proposed for online convex optimization, which takes the following update:

(\forall k\in\mathbb{N}^{*})\quad v_{k}=v_{k-1}+g_{k}^{2},\quad x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha g_{k}\odot v_{k}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},

(9)

where
g_{k}\coloneqq\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})
,
\alpha>0
is a constant step size (or learning rate),
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product, and the power operations are performed coordinate-wise. Since
(v_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}_{++}^{d}
is a sequence of positive vectors,
AdaGrad
has adaptive coordinate-wise step sizes.

AdaGrad-Norm
, also known as
SGD
with
AdaGrad
stepsizes, is the norm version of
AdaGrad
. It is given by

(\forall k\in\mathbb{N}^{*})\quad v_{k}=v_{k-1}+\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert^{2},\quad x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha g_{k}/\sqrt{v_{k}},

(10)

where
(v_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}_{++}
is a sequence of positive scalars. The scalar-valued adaptive step size
\alpha/\sqrt{v_{k}}
makes the convergence of
AdaGrad-Norm
easier to analyze
(
Ward et al. 2019
;
Ward et al. 2020
;
Faw et al. 2022
;
Attia and Koren 2023
)
.

Although the norm test and the (augmented) inner product test were originally developed for
SGD
, their potential applicability to adaptive gradient methods is intriguing, given the widespread use of such methods in deep learning. Moreover, these tests focus on the approximation of loss gradients through batch gradients, making them optimizer-agnostic. As a result, adapting these batch size schemes for
AdaGrad
and
AdaGrad-Norm
—resulting in the creation of
AdAdaGrad
and
AdAdaGrad-Norm
, respectively—simply involves substituting the
SGD
updates with those from
AdaGrad
and
AdaGrad-Norm
. The complete pseudocode for these algorithms is provided in
Algorithm

1
.

The coordinate-wise nature of adaptive step sizes in
AdaGrad
, along with the simplicity of theoretical analysis, necessitates a
coordinate-wise
variant of
AdAdaGrad
. This variant introduces a more stringent coordinate-wise condition for the exact variance norm test: for each
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
,

\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\left(\partial_{j}F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})-\partial_{j}F(x_{k})\right)^{2}\right]\leqslant\eta^{2}(\partial_{j}F(x_{k}))^{2},

(11)

with its approximate form being

\frac{1}{b_{k}(b_{k}-1)}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left(\partial_{j}f(x_{k};\xi_{i})-\partial_{j}F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\right)^{2}\leqslant\eta^{2}(\partial_{j}F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k}))^{2}
.
However, the existence of inner products in the inner product test and the orthogonality test precludes the derivation of their coordinate-specific versions.

5
Convergence Analysis

We provide two sets of convergence rate results for
AdAdaGrad
and
AdAdaGrad-Norm
. We first establish the convergence rate results of the norm test and the augmented inner product test for
AdaGrad-Norm
and
AdaGrad
for smooth nonconvex objectives. Then, we provide a convergence rate analysis of
AdAdaGrad-Norm
for non-uniformly smooth objectives. Proofs of this section can be found in
Appendix

C
.
We might invoke the following assumptions in our analysis.

Assumption 1

(
L
-Lipschitz smoothness)
.

The function
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is continuously differentiable, bounded below by
F^{\star}\coloneqq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(x)=F(x^{\star})\in\mathbb{R}
with
x^{\star}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(x)
, and
L
-Lipschitz smooth
, i.e., its gradient
\nabla F
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous with constant
L\geqslant 0
: for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\|\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(y)\|\leqslant L\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert
.

The uniform smoothness condition often proves to be excessively strict for practical applications (compare this with a uniform upper bound on the spectrum of the Hessian of
F
when
F
is twice differentiable, denoted
\nabla^{2}F\preccurlyeq LI_{d}
with
\preccurlyeq
representing the Loewner order). For example,
Zhang et al. 2020a
;
Zhang et al. 2020b
have shown through numerical evidence that transformer architectures in language models exhibit loss landscapes that either do not meet the Lipschitz smoothness assumption or have significantly large Lipschitz constants
L
. To address such issues,
Zhang et al. 2020b
propose the following more relaxed
generalized smoothness
condition:

Assumption 2

(
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness)
.

The function
F
is
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth
with constants
(L_{0},L_{1})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
, i.e., for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\left\lVert\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(y)\right\rVert\leqslant(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x)\right\rVert)\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert
.

Aside from the smoothness assumption, the conditions of the two adaptive sampling methods imply the following assumption on the second moment of the stochastic gradient of (non)convex functions. Using the nomenclature in
Khaled and Richtárik 2023
, we introduced the expected strong growth condition
(
Vaswani et al. 2019
)
below.

Definition 1

(Expected strong growth)
.

For any batch
\mathcal{B}\subset\llbracket n\rrbracket
, the
expected strong growth
(E-SG) condition is given by
(\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\;\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}}(x)\|^{2}]\leqslant\tau\|\nabla F(x)\|^{2}
,
for some constant
\tau>0
.
This condition can be relaxed in a sense that it only holds at each iteration, i.e.,

(\forall k\in\mathbb{N}^{*})\quad\mathbb{E}_{k}[\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|^{2}]\leqslant\tau\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2},

(12)

for some constant
\tau>0
and
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
are the iterates generated by some stochastic gradient methods.

Proposition 5.1

(Informal)
.

For every iteration
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, if the conditions of the exact variance norm test hold with constant
\eta\in\left(0,1\right)
and the conditions of the exact variance augmented inner product test hold with constants
(\vartheta,\nu)\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{2}
respectively, then the E-SG condition (
12
) holds with constant
\tau>0
, where
\tau=1+\eta^{2}
for the exact variance norm test and
\tau=1+\vartheta^{2}+\nu^{2}
for the exact variance inner product test respectively.

A more precise statement and its proof are given in
Appendix

C
. Although the expected strong growth condition holds in over-parameterized models
(
Vaswani et al. 2019
)
and is widely adopted in deep learning, the constant
\tau
is usually problem-specific and unknown. In contrast, the two adaptive sampling methods impose a much weaker assumption; they require that this condition holds only for the iterates
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
. In a practical implementation, the chosen constant
\tau
is pre-specified and fixed, which is also likely to be smaller than the value for the global expected strong growth condition. If the (approximate) condition does not hold, the batch size is increased so that it holds for the next iterate (see
Section

4
).

7
Concluding Remarks

In this work, we demonstrate the versatility of adaptive sampling methods as generic adaptive batch size schemes suitable for adaptive gradient optimizers, supported by convergence guarantees and numerical results. This revelation opens up a myriad of intriguing research directions for adaptive batch size schemes as a promising and flexible option for large-scale model training. On the theoretical front, delving deeper into the convergence guarantees of this class of methods when combined with other stochastic gradient optimizers, such as momentum-based methods, as well as proximal
SGD
methods for constrained problems with deterministic nonsmooth regularizers, would be fascinating. Practically, exploring the implementation of adaptive batch size schemes under various parallelism paradigms for large-scale distributed training—including data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism
(
Shoeybi et al. 2019
;
Rajbhandari et al. 2020
;
Smith et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2023
)
—is worthwhile. This exploration aims to ensure these schemes are viable for large-scale applications, such as the (pre-)training of autoregressive language and image models. Furthermore, examining the impact of adaptive batch size schemes for adaptive gradient methods, in contrast to those designed for
SGD
, particularly for language models based on transformers in addition to CNNs for vision tasks discussed in this paper, is crucial. This is because, unlike the marginal utility of adaptive methods for CNNs and RNNs
(
Wilson et al. 2017
)
, adaptive gradient methods such as
Adam
significantly outperform
SGD
in optimizing transformers
(
Zhang et al. 2020b
;
Zhang et al. 2020c
;
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Kunstner et al. 2023
;
Pan and Li 2023
;
Ahn et al. 2023
)
.

Appendix A
Further Related Work

A.1
Adaptive Sampling Methods

Adaptive sampling methods have also been extended to tackle problems outside the unconstrained optimization setting.
Xie et al. 2023
proposed a proximal extension of the norm test and the inner product test to minimize a convex composite objective, which comprises a stochastic function and a deterministic, potentially nonsmooth function.
Beiser et al. 2023
explored deterministic constrained problems, including those with nonconvex objectives. Moreover, adaptive sampling methods have been applied to various optimization problems and algorithms, such as
Bollapragada et al. 2018b
for L-BFGS,
Berahas et al. 2022
for sequential quadratic programming (SQP) in the context of equality-constrained problems,
Bollapragada et al. 2023
for augmented Lagrangian methods, and
Bollapragada and Wild 2023
for quasi-Newton methods.

A.2
Convergence of Stochastic Gradient Methods

We give a brief overview on the convergence results of
SGD
and adaptive gradient methods.
The convergence of
SGD
for smooth nonconvex functions was first analyzed in
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
, assuming a uniform bound on the variance of stochastic gradients.
Arjevani et al. 2023
gave a tight lower bound.
Bottou et al. 2018
extended the convergence result with the so-called
affine variance
noise model. Different assumptions on the second moment of stochastic gradients appearing in the literature were reviewed in
Khaled and Richtárik 2023
, with a view to developing a general convergence theory of
SGD
for smooth nonconvex functions.

Another line of work concerns the convergence guarantees in expectation of adaptive gradient methods.
Ward et al. 2019
;
Ward et al. 2020
;
Li and Orabona 2019
;
Faw et al. 2022
each gave a rate of convergence of
\widetilde{{O}}(1/\sqrt{K})
for
AdaGrad
under different assumptions on stochastic gradients.
Défossez et al. 2022
gave a simple proof on the convergence of both
AdaGrad
and a simplified version of
Adam
through a unified formulation.

Regarding high probability convergence bounds,
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
established convergence with high probability for
SGD
with properly tuned learning rate, given knowledge of the smooth and sub-Gaussian stochastic gradient noise bounds. Under the same assumptions,
Zhou et al. 2018
;
Li and Orabona 2020
also established similar results for (delayed)
AdaGrad
. Again, under different assumptions on stochastic gradients and proof techniques,
Kavis et al. 2022
;
Faw et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Attia and Koren 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
obtained high probability convergence rates for
AdaGrad
(-
Norm
).

Proof.

This is part of the result of

Since (

Then, applying
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k}),\nabla F(x_{k})\rangle]=\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\;\quad\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\left\lVert\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})-\frac{\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k}),\nabla F(x_{k})\rangle}{\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}}\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}\right]

which leads to

(C.3)

C.2.3

We give a technical lemma concerning upper bounds for finite sums of sequences of nonnegative real numbers.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a sequence of nonnegative real numbers with
a_{0}>0
. Then, the following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{a_{k}}{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\right)^{\negthickspace\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2}{\sqrt{a_{0}}},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{a_{k}}{\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\log\sum_{k=0}^{K}a_{k}-\log a_{0}.

Proof.

The first inequality is due to

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{a_{k}}{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\right)^{\negthickspace\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}\leqslant\int_{a_{0}}^{\sum_{k=0}^{K}a_{k}}\frac{1}{t^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}\,\mathrm{d}t,

whereas the second inequality is due to

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{a_{k}}{\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}}\leqslant\int_{a_{0}}^{\sum_{k=0}^{K}a_{k}}\frac{1}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

∎

b\log(2b)=b(\log 2+\log b)\leqslant 2b(\log(b+2)-\log 2)=2b\log\left(b/2+1\right)
. Consequently, we get
x-b\log x=x/2+x/2-b\log x\geqslant x/2+b-b\log(2b)\geqslant x/2+b-2b\log\left(b/2+1\right)
. Therefore, we have
x/2+b-2b\log\left(b/2+1\right)\leqslant a
, which implies the desired inequality.
∎

By
Lemma

C.2
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{v_{k}}]\leqslant 2c_{1}\tau+8c_{2}\tau\log(c_{2}\tau+1)\eqqcolon c_{3}
.
Since, by Hölder’s inequality,

c_{1}+2c_{2}\log\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{v_{k}}]\geqslant\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\right]\geqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{K}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}\right]\geqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}}\right]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{v_{K}}]},

we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}}\right]^{\negthinspace 2}\leqslant\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{v_{K}}]\cdot\left(c_{1}+2c_{2}\log\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{v_{K}}]\right)\leqslant c_{3}(c_{1}+2c_{2}\log c_{3}).

Then by Markov’s inequality, we obtained the desired result.
∎

Proof of

Proof.

Following similar arguments in the proof of

, using

and taking conditional expectation with respect to

, we have

+\alpha\left\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right)\odot g_{k}\right]\right\rangle+\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\odot g_{k}\right\rVert^{2}.

\left\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right)\odot g_{k}\right]\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\partial_{j}F(x_{k})\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1,j}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k,j}}}\right)g_{k,j}\right],

where
g_{k,j}\coloneqq[g_{k}]_{j}=\partial_{j}F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})

\displaystyle\quad\;\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\partial_{j}F(x_{k})\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1,j}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k,j}}}\right)g_{k,j}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(\partial_{j}F(x_{k}))^{2}}{2\sqrt{v_{k-1,j}}}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{v_{k,j}}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{g_{k,j}^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k,j}}+\sqrt{v_{k-1,j}}}\right]\right)^{\negthickspace 2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(\partial_{j}F(x_{k}))^{2}}{2\sqrt{v_{k-1,j}}}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{v_{k,j}}}\mathbb{E}_{k}[g_{k,j}^{2}]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{g_{k,j}^{2}}{(\sqrt{v_{k,j}}+\sqrt{v_{k-1,j}})^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(\partial_{j}F(x_{k}))^{2}}{2\sqrt{v_{k-1,j}}}+\frac{\tau}{2\sqrt{v_{k,j}}}(\partial_{j}F(x_{k}))^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{g_{k,j}^{2}}{(\sqrt{v_{k,j}}+\sqrt{v_{k-1,j}})^{2}}\right]

by

5.2
.

\widetilde{\varphi}_{k,j}\coloneqq(\partial_{j}F(x_{k}))^{2}/\sqrt{v_{k,j}}

5.1
, we get

\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\partial_{j}F(x_{k})\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1,j}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k,j}}}\right)g_{k,j}\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\rho}\right)\frac{(\partial_{j}F(x_{k}))^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1,j}}}+\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbb{E}_{k}[\widetilde{\varphi}_{k-1,j}-\widetilde{\varphi}_{k,j}]\\
+\frac{\tau L^{2}\alpha^{2}}{2}(1+\rho\tau)\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1,j}}}\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\odot g_{k-1}\right\rVert^{2}.

) and summing (

) over

\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\rho}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\frac{(\partial_{j}F(x_{k}))^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1,j}}}\leqslant F(x_{1})-\mathbb{E}[F(x_{K+1})]+\frac{\tau\alpha}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\varphi}_{0,j}-\widetilde{\varphi}_{K,j}]\\
+\left(\frac{\tau L^{2}\alpha^{3}(1+\rho\tau)}{\min_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\sqrt{v_{0,j}}}+\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\mathbb{E}\log v_{K,j}-\log v_{0,j}\right).

Now we define

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{2}{1-\rho^{-1}}\left(\frac{\tau L^{2}\alpha^{2}(1+\rho\tau)}{\min_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\sqrt{v_{0,j}}}+\frac{L\alpha}{2}\right),

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{2}{\alpha(1-\rho^{-1})}(F(x_{1})-F^{\star})+\frac{\tau}{1-\rho^{-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\varphi}_{0,j}-\widetilde{\varphi}_{K,j}]-\widetilde{c}_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log v_{0,j}.

Following the proof of

5.1

\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{v_{K,j}}\right]\leqslant\widetilde{c}_{1}+\widetilde{c}_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log v_{K,j}\right]\leqslant\widetilde{c}_{1}+2d\widetilde{c}_{2}\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{v_{K,j}}\right]\right),

by Jensen’s inequality. Then, by

C.2
, we have the following inequality

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{v_{K,j}}\right]\leqslant 2\widetilde{c}_{1}\tau+8d\widetilde{c}_{2}\tau\log(d\widetilde{c}_{2}\tau+1).

Proof of

Proof.

As will be seen later, under the choice of the parameters
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\omega)
, the constant step size satisfies
\alpha\leqslant 1/L_{1}
and
\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})/\sqrt{v_{k}}\right\rVert\leqslant 1
, we have

\left\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\rVert=\alpha\left\lVert\frac{\nabla F(x_{k})}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right\rVert\leqslant\frac{1}{L_{1}}.

By
Lemma

C.2
and taking conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{k}[F(x_{k+1})]\leqslant F(x_{k})-\alpha\left\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{g_{k}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right]\right\rangle+\alpha^{2}\frac{L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left\lVert\frac{g_{k}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right\rVert^{2}.

(C.11)

Let us recall from (
C.4
) that we have

\left\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{g_{k}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right]\right\rangle=\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}+\left\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\right)g_{k}\right]\right\rangle.

(C.12)

The second order term in (
C.11
) is equal to

\frac{L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left\lVert\frac{g_{k}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right\rVert^{2}=\frac{L_{0}\alpha^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left\lVert\frac{g_{k}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{L_{1}\alpha^{2}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert}{2}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left\lVert\frac{g_{k}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right\rVert^{2}.

Also, following the proof of
Theorem

5.1
, the second term in (
C.12
) can be upper bounded by

\left\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\right)g_{k}\right]\right\rangle\leqslant\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}+\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\right].

Since
v_{k}
is nondecreasing in

, we have

\frac{L_{1}\alpha^{2}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert}{2}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left\lVert\frac{g_{k}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant L_{1}\alpha^{2}\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}+\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\right].

Then, we have the upper bound

\displaystyle\quad\;-\alpha\left\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{g_{k}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right]\right\rangle+\frac{L_{1}\alpha^{2}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left\lVert\frac{g_{k}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant(\alpha+L_{1}\alpha^{2})\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}+\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\alpha\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}+\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\right],

(C.13)

since
\alpha\leqslant 1/L_{1}
.
By the A.M.-G.M. inequality (i.e.,
2ab\leqslant\frac{1}{\rho_{1}}a^{2}+\rho_{1}b^{2}
for any
\rho_{1}>0
), we have

\displaystyle\quad\;2\alpha\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}+\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\alpha}{\rho_{1}}\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}+\frac{\rho_{1}\alpha}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}+\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\right]\right)^{\negthickspace 2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\alpha}{\rho_{1}}\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}+\frac{\rho_{1}\alpha}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\mathbb{E}_{k}[\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert^{2}]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{(\sqrt{v_{k}}+\sqrt{v_{k-1}})^{2}}\right]

by Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\alpha}{\rho_{1}}\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}+\frac{\rho_{1}\alpha\tau}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{(\sqrt{v_{k}}+\sqrt{v_{k-1}})^{2}}\right].

Also recall from the proof of
Theorem

5.1
that

\displaystyle\quad\;\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{(\sqrt{v_{k}}+\sqrt{v_{k-1}})^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k-1})\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}-\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right]+\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k-1})\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}.

Thus, by the reverse triangle inequality, we have

\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert-\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k-1})\right\rVert\leqslant\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})-\nabla F(x_{k-1})\right\rVert\leqslant(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert)\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert.

On the other hand, by the triangle inequality and
2
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k-1})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k-1})-\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert+\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert)\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert.

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\quad\;\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k-1})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert-\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k-1})\right\rVert\right)\left(\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert+\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k-1})\right\rVert\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert)\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert((L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert)\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert+2\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert)

\displaystyle=(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert)^{2}\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert^{2}+2(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert)\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert,

implying that

+\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left[(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert)^{2}\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert^{2}+2(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert)\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert\right].

By the A.M.-G.M. inequality, we have
(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert)^{2}\leqslant 2(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2})
, which leads to

\displaystyle\quad\;\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left[2(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2})\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert^{2}+2(L_{0}+L_{1}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert)\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{k}[\varphi_{k-1}-\varphi_{k}]

\displaystyle=\frac{2L_{0}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert+\frac{2L_{1}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{2L_{0}^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{2L_{1}^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{k}[\varphi_{k-1}-\varphi_{k}].

Again, by the A.M.-G.M. inequality, with any
\rho_{2}>0

\rho_{1}\alpha\tau\cdot\frac{2L_{0}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert\leqslant\frac{\rho_{1}\alpha}{\rho_{2}\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}L_{0}^{2}\tau^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert^{2}.

\rho_{1}\alpha\tau\cdot\frac{2L_{1}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert\leqslant\frac{2L_{1}\rho_{1}\alpha^{2}\tau}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant\omega\alpha\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}}{\sqrt{v_{k-1}}}.

Also, the choice of
\alpha\leqslant\sqrt{\omega}/(L_{1}\sqrt{2\rho_{1}\tau})

D.3
Three-layer Convolutional Neural Network on CIFAR-10

We then consider a similar problem with a three-layer CNN with a slightly different structure on the harder CIFAR-10 dataset. We use a training budget of 5 million samples (100 epochs) and a maximum batch size of 10,000 samples. To reduce the computational overhead brought by the tests, we perform the test every 10 steps.
In
Figure

8
, we again observe that
AdaSGD
converges more slowly than
AdAdaGrad
and
AdAdaGrad-Norm
. This would be due to the lack of a well-crafted learning rate scaling with respect to batch sizes (cf.
scaling rule
) for
SGD
in the nonconvex case: the rapid increase in its batch sizes implies a very small effective learning rate, which is equal to the ratio of the learning rate and the batch size. Without proper rescaling of the learning rate, such a small effective learning rate would potentially decelerate convergence. We could empirically confirm this claim since
AdaSGD
using the inner product test with
\vartheta=0.1
has its batch size increasing slowly and eventually plateaus at somewhere below 4,000. It also achieves much faster convergence compared to its
SGD
counterparts, with a final validation accuracy of 57%, approaching the performance of adaptive methods. We however point out that this
AdaSGD
instance has a much smaller average batch size, hence taking much more gradient updates than the adaptive methods given an equal budget of training samples.
