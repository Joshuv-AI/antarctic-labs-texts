Analysis of Primal-Dual Langevin Algorithms
Martin Burger, Matthias J. Ehrhardt, Lorenz Kuger, Lukas Weigand

Abstract
We analyze a recently proposed class of algorithms for the problem of sampling from probability distributions μ ∗ \mu^{\ast} in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} with a Lebesgue density of the form μ ∗ ​ ( x ) ∝ exp ⁡ ( − f ⁡ ( K ​ x ) − g ⁡ ( x ) ) \mu^{\ast}(x)\propto\exp(-f(Kx)-g(x)) , where K K is a linear operator and f , g f,g convex and non-smooth. The method is a generalization of the primal-dual hybrid gradient optimization algorithm to a sampling scheme. We give the iteration’s continuous time limit, a stochastic differential equation in the joint primal-dual variable, and its mean field limit Fokker–Planck equation. Under mild conditions, the scheme converges to a unique stationary state in continuous and discrete time. Contrary to purely primal overdamped Langevin diffusion, the stationary state in continuous time does not have μ ∗ \mu^{\ast} as its primal marginal. Thus, further analysis is carried out to bound the bias induced by the partial dualization, and potentially correct for it in the diffusion. Time discretizations of the diffusion lead to implementable algorithms, but, as is typical in Langevin Monte Carlo methods, introduce further bias. We prove bounds for these discretization errors, which allow to give convergence results relating the produced samples to the target. We demonstrate our findings numerically first on small-scale examples in which we can exactly verify the theoretical results, and subsequently on typical examples of larger scale from Bayesian imaging inverse problems.

Introduction

We analyze a recently proposed class of algorithms for sampling from a probability distribution on
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with log-concave, non-smooth density.
We are interested in target distributions with Lebesgue densities of the form

\mu^{\ast}(x)=\frac{\exp(-f(Kx)-g(x))}{\int\exp(-f(Kw)-g(w))\,\mathrm{d}w},

(1.1)

where we assume that the integral in the denominator exists and is finite. The operator
K:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\eqqcolon\mathcal{Y}
is assumed to be linear and
f:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
,
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
will be called potential terms and are both assumed convex and lower semi-continuous but not necessarily differentiable. We will denote the full potential by
f\circ K+g\eqqcolon h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
.

A standard class of algorithms to draw samples in Bayesian inference are Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods.
Compared to other types of MCMC methods, sampling schemes based on Langevin diffusion processes have proved efficient in inverse problems and imaging applications due to the preferable scaling behaviour to high dimensions
[
40
,
18
,
20
]
.
Typically, MCMC sampling algorithms based on Langevin diffusion perform a time discretisation of the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\nabla\log\mu^{\ast}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(1.2)

where
W_{t}
is standard
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued Brownian motion. Assuming
\mu^{\ast}
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality, it is the unique invariant distribution of the SDE and the law of
X_{t}
will converge to
\mu^{\ast}
as
t
grows.
The standard Euler–Maruyama discretization of (
1.2
) takes the form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\xi^{n+1}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{d})\\
X^{n+1}=X^{n}+\tau\nabla\log\mu^{\ast}(X^{n})+\sqrt{2\tau}\xi^{n+1},\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

this iteration is also known as unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA). ULA and other time discretizations of (
1.2
) introduce a bias between the law of the samples and the target
\mu^{\ast}
, hence many works have been concerned with the characterization of the invariant distribution of the Markov chain
(X^{n})_{n}
and its bias
[
40
,
19
,
44
,
14
]
.
Other typical questions concern preconditioning and convergence speed of the Markov chain and the behaviour of Metropolis–Hastings correction steps to overcome the bias and draw unbiased samples from
\mu^{\ast}
.

In this work, we are concerned with a new type of Langevin sampling algorithm which has been proposed as
Unadjusted Langevin Primal-Dual Algorithm
(ULPDA) in
[
35
]
and has since been mentioned in
[
31
,
25
]
.
Unlike ULA or its variants, ULPDA can not be interpreted as a time discretization of (
1.2
).
It rather consists in steps of a primal-dual optimization method, where the same stochastic term
\sqrt{2\tau}\xi
as in ULA is added to the primal iterate. The iteration takes the form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\xi^{n+1}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{d})\\
Y^{n+1}=\prox_{\sigma f^{\ast}}\left(Y^{n}+\sigma KX_{\theta}^{n}\right)\\
X^{n+1}=\prox_{\tau g}(X^{n}-\tau K^{T}Y^{n+1})+\sqrt{2\tau}\xi^{n+1}\\
X_{\theta}^{n+1}=X^{n+1}+\theta(X^{n+1}-X^{n}),\end{array}\right.

(1.4)

where
\tau,\sigma>0
are step sizes,
\theta>0
is an overrelaxation parameter,
f^{\ast}
denotes the convex conjugate of
f
and the proximal mapping of a convex function
\varphi
is the unique value
\prox_{\gamma\varphi}(z)\coloneqq(id+\gamma\partial\varphi)^{-1}(z)
.

The motivation for this update comes from the optimization perspective: The ULA iteration (
1.3
) can be interpreted as gradient descent with an additive stochastic term. While ULA requires to know the gradients of the potentials, the advantage of primal-dual algorithms is that the iteration can be applied without the need to evaluate any gradients or even the proximal mapping of the composition
f\circ K
.
f
and
g
need only be convex, not necessarily differentiable. Hence, it appears desirable to obtain more general sampling algorithms by adding stochasticity to other first-order optimization methods like the primal-dual update.

This is, of course, not a rigorous argument for convergence, and until now, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, no convergence theory for ULPDA has been provided. Indeed, the situation seems to be more intricate: When we run ULPDA on a simple toy example, see
Figure

1
, the distribution of the samples seems to depend more critically on the choice of step size hyperparameters
\tau,\sigma
of the algorithm than it is known from ULA.

1.1
Related Works

There is a vast literature on unadjusted Langevin algorithms, where we use the term to refer not only to (
1.3
), but any time discretization of (
1.2
). The convergence analysis is based on the simple fact that the target distribution is the stationary distribution of the SDE (
1.2
) and that the dynamics converge to the target in continuous time
[
40
]
. As an underlying concept, this convergence can be explained by drawing the connection between the diffusion dynamics and, equivalently, the gradient flow of relative entropy with respect to the target in Wasserstein space
[
28
,
44
]
. Standard convergence results on ULA typically require a Lipschitz continuous gradient of the potential in order to bound the error introduced by time discretization
[
14
,
20
,
16
]
.

Since non-smooth densities do not satisfy this gradient Lipschitz condition, many works have treated the extension to the non-smooth case. Generally, the approaches can be divided into two classes. The first regularizes non-smooth terms, resulting in a modified, smooth target and applies typical ULA to this modified distribution. The challenge then typically consists in bounding the terms between the target and the modified distribution, and numerically in trading off the introduced bias and convergence speed via the smoothing parameter
[
38
,
18
,
17
,
32
,
9
]
. The second approach consists in finding different discretizations of (
1.2
), i.e. implicit or semi-implicit schemes, that are implementable and provably convergent. These schemes are usually referred to as proximal or implicit Langevin methods, see e.g.
[
38
,
4
,
41
,
29
,
21
]
. A work of particular relevance for our setting is
[
25
]
, since the authors assume the same type of splitting of the potential
h=f\circ K+g
as we do. Their method ‘Prox-Sub’ can be understood as a first-order forward-backward discretization of (
1.2
), with the forward step in
f\circ K
realized as a subgradient since
f
is assumed non-smooth. We will see that this method is intimately connected to the primal-dual sampling algorithm since it forms the limiting case in which the primal-dual stationary distribution can be related to the target. In
[
35
]
, the authors proposed the primal-dual scheme that we consider here, in order to generalize from the class of methods that employ purely primal discretizations of (
1.2
). A partial dualization of the potential is also considered in
[
9
]
, but the scheme differs from the one considered here in that it runs an inner iteration to solve the dual optimality condition. Further, the algorithm in
[
9
]
assumes smoothness of
g
since the updates require evaluation of
\nabla g
. We also refer to
[
31
]
for a recent comparison of current methods applicable in the non-smooth case.

Constraints on the state space can be viewed as a special case of non-smooth potentials, where the constraint is enforced by adding an indicator function to the potential. Several works have considered this special case usually by incorporating a proximal (which amounts to a projection in the case of an indicator) or a reflection step
[
6
,
7
,
34
]
. Mirroring the Langevin dynamics can be another approach to precondition the dynamics as well as to enforce geometrical constraints
[
24
,
1
,
33
,
32
]
.

We also mention that due to the dualization, the primal-dual Langevin sampling strategy can be interpreted as an alternating stochastic descent-ascent scheme. There is a more general interest in the analysis of Langevin descent-ascent schemes in the context of saddle point problems, e.g. for the recovery of Nash equilibria, see
[
43
]
. Our results give answers on the convergence in continuous and discretized time for the case where the saddle point functional is of a Lagrangian form obtained from rewriting a convex optimization problem as a saddle point problem.

Contributions

Our main contributions are the following (see also
Figure

2
for an overview).

•

For the algorithm’s continuous time limit, we prove a contraction result using a coupling argument. This guarantees the dynamics are asymptotically stable and converge to a stationary distribution
\pi_{\lambda}(x,y)
, where
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\eqqcolon\mathcal{Z}
in the joint space of primal and dual variable. Crucially, the distribution depends on
\lambda>0
, a constant coupling primal and dual time scales.

•

We show that the marginal of
\pi_{\lambda}
in the primal variable
x
is not the target
\mu^{\ast}
. This can be interpreted as being a consequence of
\pi_{\lambda}
not concentrating its mass on a lower-dimensional submanifold of
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, although the diffusion coefficient is degenerate with diffusion only acting directly on
x
. In the case of smooth
f^{\ast},g
, we prove this property called hypoellipticity.

•

We consider two potential frameworks for “correcting” the bias between the stationary distribution
\pi_{\lambda}
and
\pi^{\ast}
(a concentrated push-forward of the target
\mu^{\ast}
to
\mathcal{Z}
). Firstly, a proposed modification of the dynamics with non-homogeneous diffusion coefficient can be employed to force
\pi^{\ast}
to be the stationary solution. The target becomes the stationary distribution, but the discretization of the diffusion SDE is impractical. Secondly, we consider the unmodified scheme, but with the ratio
\lambda
of dual and primal step sizes in the algorithm growing to infinity. The limit recovers the purely primal overdamped Langevin diffusion. Under regularity assumptions on the dualized potential

, we show convergence of
\pi_{\lambda}
to
\pi^{\ast}
.

•

In discrete time, we show stability of the algorithm in the sense that Wasserstein distances between two different instances are bounded if the potentials are convex. Under stronger regularity assumptions on
f^{\ast},g
, there exists a stationary solution
\pi_{\lambda,\tau}
to which the distribution of the iterates converges. We then provide a bound of the bias, i.e. a bound on the Wasserstein distance between
\pi_{\lambda}
and
\pi_{\lambda,\tau}
. This allows distance bounds between the generated samples and
\mu^{\ast}

2
Primal-Dual Langevin Sampling Schemes

Although the algorithmic approach is not limited to this setting, in the context of Bayesian inference, the target distribution
\mu^{\ast}
is typically a posterior distribution. Such a posterior target is defined through the likelihood
p(z|x)
of observing
z
given the true parameter
x
, combined with a prior distribution
p(x)
. It is given by

p(x|z)=\frac{1}{Z}p(z|x)p(x),

where the normalization constant
Z=\int p(z|w)p(w)\,\mathrm{d}w
is typically intractable in high dimensions.
As an example class of problems that we consider, in Bayesian inference for imaging inverse problems the distributions are typically high-dimensional and involve non-smooth terms. This is because model-based priors on image distributions often assume sparse coefficients in some basis, the recovery of which corresponds to minimization of non-smooth functions
[
10
]
. Therefore, many relevant posteriors
p(x|z)
fall into the class of targets given in (
1.1
). We start by explaining the motivation for primal-dual sampling from the point of view of convex, non-smooth optimization.

2.1
Motivation from Primal-Dual Optimization

A common task in Bayesian inverse problems is the computation of the maximum a posteriori estimate (MAP), i.e. the mode of the distribution
\mu^{\ast}=p(\cdot|z)
. This task forms the connection between Bayesian formulations of inverse problems and variational regularization approaches, since it amounts to the problem of computing the solution to the following minimization problem

\argmin_{x}f(Kx)+g(x).

(2.1)

In order to solve (
2.1
), depending on the structure of
f
,
K
and
g
it can be helpful to employ techniques from convex duality on parts of the potential
h=f\circ K+g
. A popular approach consists in reformulating the variational problem (
2.1
) as the saddle point problem

\displaystyle\min_{x}h(x)

\displaystyle=\min_{x}\big(g(x)+\max_{y}(\langle Kx,y\rangle-f^{*}(y))\big)

\displaystyle=\min_{x}\max_{y}s(x,y)

(2.2)

with
s(x,y):=g(x)+\langle Kx,y\rangle-f^{*}(y)
. Here
f^{\ast}
denotes the convex conjugate of
f
defined by

f^{\ast}(y):=\sup_{w\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\{\langle w,y\rangle-f(w)\right\}.

Under mild assumptions on
g
and
f^{\ast}
, one can prove existence of a saddle point
(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})
(see section 19 in
[
3
]
for details), the primal part
x^{\ast}
of which is a solution to the original problem (
2.1
). Instead of minimizing
h
, one can then employ primal-dual type algorithms to solve the saddle point problem (
2.2
). For the context of imaging inverse problems, we refer to
[
13
,
8
]
for an overview.

The primal-dual optimization method (primal descent and dual ascent with overrelaxation in the primal variable) for (
2.2
) takes the form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}Y^{n+1}=\prox_{\sigma f^{\ast}}\left(Y^{n}+\sigma KX_{\theta}^{n}\right)\\
X^{n+1}=\prox_{\tau g}(X^{n}-\tau K^{T}Y^{n+1})\\
X_{\theta}^{n+1}=X^{n+1}+\theta(X^{n+1}-X^{n}),\end{array}\right.

(2.3)

with step sizes
\tau,\sigma
, overrelaxation parameter
\theta
and an initial guess
(X^{0},Y^{0})
. By convention, we set
X_{\theta}^{0}=X^{0}
. For convergence theory of the scheme (
2.3
) and possible accelerations we refer to
[
12
,
13
]
.

In order to draw the parallels between computing the MAP of
\mu^{\ast}
and drawing samples from
\mu^{\ast}
, note that ULA (
1.3
) can be viewed as a scheme alternating a gradient descent step in
h=-\log\mu^{\ast}
and a diffusion step. The diffusion is scaled to ensure the samples have the correct covariance up to a bias which is due to the discretization error and is controlled by the step size
\tau
. Similar to ULA, several first-order optimization algorithms have been transformed into sampling algorithms by inserting a scaled diffusion step at some point in the iteration
[
31
]
. The choice of optimization step is fairly flexible as long as the resulting method can be viewed as an alternating scheme combining a descent step (i.e. a discretized step along the gradient flow of a potential energy) with the diffusion (an exact step along the gradient flow of entropy)
[
44
]
.

It appears natural to take a similar perspective on primal-dual optimization methods and add a diffusion step at some point in the iteration. Such a scheme, in the form of (
1.4
), was proposed under the name unadjusted Langevin primal-dual algorithm (ULPDA) in
[
35
]
. In this variant of the algorithm, the diffusion step is added only in the primal variable. We will consider both (
1.4
) and the more general scheme

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\xi^{n+1}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{d+m})\\
Y^{n+1}=\prox_{\sigma f^{\ast}}\left(Y^{n}+\sigma KX_{\theta}^{n}\right)+\sqrt{\tau}B_{Y}\xi^{n+1}\\
X^{n+1}=\prox_{\tau g}(X^{n}-\tau K^{T}Y^{n+1})+\sqrt{\tau}B_{X}\xi^{n+1}\\
X_{\theta}^{n+1}=X^{n+1}+\theta(X^{n+1}-X^{n}),\end{array}\right.

(2.4)

with general constant coefficients
B_{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d+m)}
and
B_{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(d+m)}

for the stochastic component. Note the increased dimension of the stochastic term compared to (
1.4
), which is a special case of (
2.4
) with
B_{X}=(\sqrt{2}I_{d},0)
and
B_{Y}=0
.

As a further variant of the algorithm, we remark that in (
1.4
), we add the stochastic term
\sqrt{2\tau}\xi^{n+1}
after applying the proximal operator. Similar to other proximal Langevin algorithms, it may instead be added inside the argument of the proximal mapping, effectively making the primal update read

X^{n+1}=\prox_{\tau g}(X^{n}-\tau K^{T}Y^{n+1}+\sqrt{2\tau}\xi^{n+1}).

Since the continuous time limit of both versions is the same SDE, our results on asymptotic behaviour in continuous time in
Section

3
hold for either variant. In the discrete time analysis in
Section

4
, we will focus on the version (
1.4
) with diffusion as the outer step. The same results hold for the other variant; we explain in
Corollaries

4.3
and
4.10
how our arguments can be modified to prove them.

2.2
Continuous Time Perspective

In the analysis of ULA’s convergence behaviour, it is helpful to view the update step as an Euler–Maruyama time discretization of the overdamped Langevin diffusion (
1.2
). Convergence results for ULA are based on the fact that the target measure
\mu^{\ast}
is the unique invariant distribution of the Markov process
X_{t}
.

In order to analyze ULPDA, we want to define a similar time-continuous limit of (
1.4
). We assume that the step sizes
\tau,\sigma
are coupled via the constant
\lim_{\tau,\sigma\to 0}\frac{\sigma}{\tau}=\lambda
where
0<\lambda<\infty
. Taking both step sizes to zero at this ratio, the formal limiting Markov process
(X_{t},Y_{t})
satisfies the diffusion equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}\in-(\partial g(X_{t})+K^{T}Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\\
\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}\in-\lambda(\partial f^{*}(Y_{t})-KX_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t,\end{array}\right.

(2.5)

which we can write in a joint variable
Z_{t}=(X_{t},Y_{t})
as

\,\mathrm{d}Z_{t}\in-A_{\text{{PD}}}(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+B_{\text{{PD}}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(2.6)

where we introduced the primal-dual coefficients

A_{\text{{PD}}}(Z_{t})\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\partial g(X_{t})+K^{T}Y_{t}\\
\lambda(\partial f^{\ast}(Y_{t})-KX_{t})\end{pmatrix},\qquad B_{\text{{PD}}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2}I_{d}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(2.7)

Note in particular that the diffusion coefficient
\Sigma_{\text{{PD}}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}B_{\text{{PD}}}B_{\text{{PD}}}^{T}
is only positive semidefinite (or degenerate) with an
m
-dimensional null space, where
m
is the dimension of the dual variable
Y
. The standard existence and uniqueness theory for Fokker–Planck equations with positive definite diffusion coefficient therefore does not apply.

In the more general scheme (
2.4
), the diffusion equation takes the form

\,\mathrm{d}Z_{t}\in-A_{\text{{PD}}}(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+B_{\text{{gPD}}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad B_{\text{{gPD}}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}B_{X}\\
B_{Y}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.8)

If
B_{\text{{gPD}}}
(where ‘gPD’ denotes ‘general primal-dual’) is invertible, then
\Sigma_{\text{{gPD}}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}B_{\text{{gPD}}}B_{\text{{gPD}}}^{T}
becomes positive definite, and the corresponding Fokker–Planck equation has a unique stationary solution
[
37
]
. We will check whether the stationary solution can be related to the density of the target distribution
\mu^{\ast}
.

3
The Stationary Solution in Continuous Time

We start by proving the existence of a unique stationary solution of the primal-dual diffusion SDE (
2.6
). We further show that the corresponding primal-dual Fokker–Planck equation is contractive in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
, the space of distributions over
\mathcal{Z}
with finite second moment. This will imply that any solution converges in distribution to a stationary state. Convergence is shown in weighted 2-Wasserstein distance: Given any norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
on
\mathcal{X}
, a transport distance on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
is defined by

\mathcal{T}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}):=\inf_{\mathcal{M}\in\Gamma(\mu,\tilde{\mu})}\left(\mathbb{E}_{(x,\tilde{x})\sim\mathcal{M}}\left[\lVert x-\tilde{x}\rVert^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2},

(3.1)

where
\Gamma(\mu,\tilde{\mu})
denotes the set of all couplings with marginals
\mu
and
\tilde{\mu}
, i.e.

\Gamma(\mu,\tilde{\mu})=\left\{\mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})~:~\mu=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\tilde{x})\,d\tilde{x}~\textrm{and}~\tilde{\mu}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{M}(x,\cdot)\,dx\right\}.

\mathcal{T}
defines a metric on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
. If
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is the Euclidean norm, then
\mathcal{T}
is the 2-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{2}
.

3.1
Fokker–Planck Equation and Existence of a Stationary Solution

The contraction result follows an argument from the literature on overdamped Langevin diffusion, see
[
23
,
20
]
. Since this simpler case is instructive, we repeat it here and then generalize to the setting of an SDE with any strongly monotone operator as drift coefficient.

3.1.1
Contraction of Overdamped Langevin Diffusion via a Coupling Argument

Suppose for now that the potential
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable and
\omega_{h}
-strongly convex. We repeat here an instructive calculation that has been carried out, e.g. in
[
23
,
20
]
, to show stability of the overdamped Langevin diffusion process

dX_{t}=-\nabla h(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2}dW_{t}.

(3.2)

The distribution
\mu_{t}
of the process
X_{t}
in (
3.2
), denoted
\mu_{t}=\Law(X_{t})
, satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation (or forward Kolmogorov equation)
[
37
]
given by

\frac{\partial\mu_{t}}{\partial t}=\nabla_{x}\cdot(\mu_{t}\nabla h(x))+\Delta_{x}\mu_{t}.

The target distribution
\mu^{\ast}
is the unique stationary solution of the Fokker–Planck equation. Furthermore, the PDE is contractive in Wasserstein distance, which can be shown using the following coupling argument. We will call two processes coupled if both solve (
3.2
) with the same Brownian motion. Let
X_{t},\tilde{X}_{t}
be coupled with
X_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
and
\tilde{X}_{0}\sim\tilde{\mu}_{0}
. Then the distribution
\mathcal{M}_{t}
of the joint variable
(X_{t},\tilde{X}_{t})
satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation

\frac{\partial\mathcal{M}_{t}}{\partial t}=\nabla_{x}\cdot(\mathcal{M}_{t}\nabla h(x))+\nabla_{\tilde{x}}\cdot(\mathcal{M}_{t}\nabla h(\tilde{x}))+(\nabla_{x}+\nabla_{\tilde{x}})\cdot(\nabla_{x}+\nabla_{\tilde{x}})\mathcal{M}_{t}.

(3.3)

Note that we can rewrite this using
(\nabla_{x}+\nabla_{\tilde{x}})\cdot(\nabla_{x}+\nabla_{\tilde{x}})=\Delta_{x+\tilde{x}}
by a coordinate change
(x,\tilde{x})\mapsto(\frac{x+\tilde{x}}{2},x-\tilde{x})
. For any distance function of the form
d(x,\tilde{x})=\rho(\lVert x-\tilde{x}\rVert_{2})
, we directly see
\Delta_{x+\tilde{x}}d(x,\tilde{x})=0
. Using this and the property (
3.3
) of the joint distribution
\mathcal{M}_{t}
, a contraction in Wasserstein distance can hence be shown in the following way. We start by estimating

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}_{(x,\tilde{x})\sim\mathcal{M}_{t}}\left[\lVert x-\tilde{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int\lVert x-\tilde{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathcal{M}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\lVert x-\tilde{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}\left[\nabla_{x}\cdot(\mathcal{M}_{t}\nabla h(x))+\nabla_{\tilde{x}}\cdot(\mathcal{M}_{t}\nabla h(\tilde{x}))+\Delta_{x+\tilde{x}}\mathcal{M}_{t}\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle=-\int(x-\tilde{x})\cdot(\nabla h(x)-\nabla h(\tilde{x}))\mathcal{M}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}\tilde{x})

\displaystyle\leq-\omega_{h}\mathbb{E}_{(x,\tilde{x})\sim\mathcal{M}_{t}}\left[\lVert x-\tilde{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right],

where we used that due to strong convexity of
h
, we have
(x-\tilde{x})\cdot(\nabla h(x)-\nabla h(\tilde{x}))\geq\omega_{h}\lVert x-\tilde{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Applying now Gronwall’s lemma, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{(x,\tilde{x})\sim\mathcal{M}_{t}}\left[\lVert x-\tilde{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\leq e^{-2\omega_{h}t}\mathbb{E}_{(x,\tilde{x})\sim\mathcal{M}_{0}}\left[\lVert x-\tilde{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right].

The left side is bounded from below by
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t},\tilde{\mu}_{t})
by definition. By taking the infimum over all couplings
\mathcal{M}_{0}
(with marginals
\mu_{0}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{0}
) on the right hand side, we obtain

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{t},\tilde{\mu}_{t})\leq e^{-\omega_{h}t}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{0},\tilde{\mu}_{0}).

In particular, if we choose one of the processes to be initialized at the target distribution as
\tilde{\mu}_{0}=\mu^{\ast}
, then the distribution of
\tilde{X}_{t}
is stationary at
\tilde{\mu}_{t}=\mu^{\ast}
and by the above we obtain the convergence result

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{t},\mu^{\ast})\leq e^{-\omega_{h}t}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu^{\ast}).

3.1.2
Contraction and Stationary Solution of the Primal-Dual Diffusion Equation

We can generalize the above argument allowing an analysis of the time-continuous limit (
2.6
) of the primal-dual sampling scheme (
1.4
). Although the primal-dual dynamics are not the gradient flow of a corresponding relative entropy anymore, contraction can equivalently be shown for a coupling of two processes. For that, let
Z_{t}
satisfy a general stochastic differential inclusion with non-homogeneous drift coefficient

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}\in-A(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+B\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(3.4)

Here
W_{t}
is Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
for some
k>0
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+m)\times k}
is constant. We assume the drift
A
to be a set-valued monotone operator, meaning

\langle w-\tilde{w},z-\tilde{z}\rangle\geq 0,\qquad\forall w\in A(z),\tilde{w}\in A(\tilde{z}).

We say that
A
is
\omega
-strongly monotone if the following stronger condition is satisfied:

\langle w-\tilde{w},z-\tilde{z}\rangle\geq\omega\lVert z-\tilde{z}\rVert^{2},\qquad\forall w\in A(z),\tilde{w}\in A(\tilde{z}).

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

be a solution to (
3.4
) and denote by
\pi_{t}
the density w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure of the distribution of
Z_{t}
. Then
\pi_{t}
solves the Fokker–Planck equation

\frac{\partial\pi_{t}}{\partial t}=\nabla_{z}\cdot\left(w\pi_{t}+\Sigma\nabla_{z}\pi_{t}\right),\qquad w\in A(z),

(3.5)

with
\Sigma=\frac{1}{2}BB^{T}
.

We refer to
[
37
, ch. 4]
for general background on the Fokker–Planck equation of SDEs and
[
39
]
for existence and uniqueness results in the special case of inclusions with multivalued, maximally monotone drift term.

Since we are repeatedly going to make use of coupling arguments, note that by applying
Proposition

3.1
to a coupled diffusion

\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}Z_{t}\\
\mathrm{d}\tilde{Z}_{t}\end{pmatrix}\in-\begin{pmatrix}A(Z_{t})\\
A(\tilde{Z}_{t})\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}t+\begin{pmatrix}B\\
B\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(3.6)

we can also obtain a Fokker–Planck equation for the joint density of the coupled processes.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
Z_{t},\tilde{Z}_{t}
be two coupled processes that both solve (
3.4
) with the same Brownian motion, i.e.
(Z_{t},\tilde{Z}_{t})
solves (
3.6

of

satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation

\frac{\partial\Pi_{t}}{\partial t}=\nabla_{z}\cdot\left(w\Pi_{t}\right)+\nabla_{\tilde{z}}\cdot\left(\tilde{w}\Pi_{t}\right)+(\nabla_{z}+\nabla_{\tilde{z}})\cdot(\Sigma(\nabla_{z}+\nabla_{\tilde{z}})\Pi_{t}),\qquad w\in A(z),\tilde{w}\in A(\tilde{z}).

(3.7)

For showing the contractivity of overdamped Langevin dynamics in
Section

3.1.1

h
, implying that
\nabla h
is a strongly monotone operator. The computation can be directly generalized under a strong monotonicity assumption on

.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose

with induced norm

For the proof, note that the exemplary computation for overdamped Langevin diffusion in

A

Section

A.1
.

Consider now the general primal-dual type diffusion (
2.8
) and denote by
\pi_{t}
the law of
Z_{t}
. By

3.1
,

solves the Fokker–Planck equation

\frac{\partial\pi_{t}}{\partial t}=\nabla_{z}\cdot\left(w\pi_{t}+\Sigma_{\text{{gPD}}}\nabla_{z}\pi_{t}\right),\qquad w\in A_{\text{{PD}}}(z),

(3.9)

with

as in (

) and

given in (

). The drift coefficient

is a monotone operator with respect to the weighted inner product

introduced below.

Under a strong convexity assumption on

and

, the drift is even strongly monotone in a weighted norm, so that we can apply
Lemma

3.3
to the primal-dual diffusion equation. In the following, for some

of joint primal and dual variable with the weighted inner product
\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle_{a,b}\coloneqq\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle_{a}+\langle y_{1},y_{2}\rangle_{b}\coloneqq a\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle+b\langle y_{1},y_{2}\rangle
, where

here denotes the Euclidean inner product on
\mathcal{X}
and

(on

and

(on

).

The induced transport distance (

) on

\mathcal{T}_{a,b}(\pi,\tilde{\pi})\coloneqq\inf_{\Pi\in\Gamma(\pi,\tilde{\pi})}\left(\mathbb{E}_{(z,\tilde{z})\sim\Pi}\left[\lVert z-\tilde{z}\rVert_{a,b}^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

(3.10)

Note that the equivalence of norms on

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that
g,f^{\ast}
are
\omega_{g}
-strongly convex and
\omega_{f^{\ast}}

\mathcal{T}_{1,\lambda^{-1}}(\pi_{t},\tilde{\pi}_{t})\leq e^{-\omega t}\mathcal{T}_{1,\lambda^{-1}}(\pi_{0},\tilde{\pi}_{0}),

The proof applies

3.3
: We observe that the scale

.

In the following, we will also use the

3.2
Non-Concentration of the Stationary Solution

Having established the existence of
\pi_{\lambda}
and its marginal
\mu_{\lambda}
, we need to check how they relate to the target
\mu^{\ast}
from which we wish to draw samples. While overdamped Langevin dynamics is designed to have the target
\mu^{\ast}
as its stationary solution, this is unfortunately not the case in the primal-dual setting. In general,
\mu^{\ast}\neq\mu_{\lambda}
. We demonstrate this on a simple example of a Gaussian target, where we can solve the Fokker–Planck equation (
3.9
) in closed form.

Theorem 3.5
.

Consider a one-dimensional setting with the target
\mu^{\ast}(x)\propto\exp(-h(x))
given by

f(y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2c_{f}}y^{2},\ g(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2c_{g}}x^{2},\ h(x)\coloneqq f(kx)+g(x)=\frac{k^{2}c_{g}+c_{f}}{2c_{f}c_{g}}x^{2},\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R},

where
k\neq 0
and
c_{g},c_{f}>0
. It holds
f^{\ast}(y)=\frac{c_{f}}{2}y^{2}
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
. The primal-dual diffusion equation (
2.6
) for the joint variable
Z_{t}=(X_{t},Y_{t})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
reads

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=-A_{\text{{PD}}}Z_{t}\mathrm{d}t+B_{\text{{PD}}}\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad\text{with }A_{\text{{PD}}}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{g}^{-1}&k\\
-\lambda k&\lambda c_{f}\end{pmatrix},B_{\text{{PD}}}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2}\\
0\end{pmatrix}

where
W_{t}
is one-dimensional Brownian motion and the weight
\lambda>0
relates primal and dual time scales. The following statements hold:

(i)

For any (possibly singular) initialization
\pi_{0}
such that
Z_{0}\sim\pi_{0}
, the distribution
\pi_{t}\coloneqq\Law(Z_{t})
has a
C^{\infty}
-smooth Lebesgue density for
t>0
.

(ii)

If
\pi_{0}
is Gaussian (including degenerate Gaussians with singular covariance matrix), then

\pi_{t}
is (non-degenerate) Gaussian for all
t>0
. As
t\to\infty
, the solution
\pi_{t}
converges to the Gaussian stationary distribution
\pi_{\lambda}
with mean zero and covariance matrix

\frac{1}{(c_{f}+k^{2}c_{g})(1+\lambda c_{f}c_{g})}\begin{pmatrix}c_{g}(c_{f}+k^{2}c_{g})+\lambda c_{f}^{2}c_{g}^{2}&k\lambda c_{f}c_{g}^{2}\\
k\lambda c_{f}c_{g}^{2}&k^{2}\lambda c_{g}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

The
x
-marginal
\mu_{\lambda}
is not equal to the target
\mu^{\ast}
. However, the limiting joint distribution as
\lambda\to\infty
is the degenerate Gaussian
(\id,f^{\prime}(k\,\cdot))_{\#}\mu^{\ast}
whose
x
-marginal is the target
\mu^{\ast}
.

The proof solves the Fokker–Planck equation via its characteristic equations and derives its stationary solution exactly. The computations can be found in
Section

A.2
.

The negative result
Theorem

3.5
naturally raises the question whether the diffusion equation can be modified such that the target becomes its stationary distribution. While we will consider the more general case of non-homogeneous, problem-dependent diffusion coefficients in
Section

3.4
, the (algorithmically) most straightforward approach would be to add homogeneous diffusion not only in the primal variable
X^{n}
of the algorithm, but also in the dual
Y^{n}
, as in (
2.4
). Unfortunately, the next theorem shows that the time-continuous limit (
2.8
) always remains asymptotically inconsistent.

Theorem 3.6
.

Consider the same target
\mu^{\ast}
as in
Theorem

3.5
, but the more general diffusion (
2.8
)

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=-A_{\text{{PD}}}Z_{t}\mathrm{d}t+B_{\text{{gPD}}}\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad\text{with }A_{\text{{PD}}}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{g}^{-1}&k\\
-\lambda k&\lambda c_{f}\end{pmatrix},B_{\text{{gPD}}}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{11}&b_{12}\\
b_{21}&b_{22}\end{pmatrix}

(3.11)

where
W_{t}
is Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then there exists no choice for the entries of

independent of

and

such that the stationary solution
\pi_{\lambda}
of (

x
-marginal
\mu^{\ast}
.

The proof can be found in
Section

A.2
.

We want to elaborate more on the concentration properties of
\pi_{\lambda}
in the scheme (
2.6
) with primal diffusion.
Theorem

3.5
showed that in the limiting case
\lambda\to\infty
, the stationary distribution does not have a Lebesgue density anymore since the mass concentrates on the optimality condition
\partial f^{\ast}(y)=Kx
. In that case, the primal marginal actually converges to the target distribution, which is intuitively clear since inserting
Y_{t}\in\partial f(KX_{t})
into the equation for
X_{t}
reduces the system to overdamped Langevin diffusion. We will rigorously generalize these properties of the toy example of
Theorem

3.5
in two ways. Specifically, we show in
Section

3.3
under which conditions the stationary solution converges, as
\lambda\to\infty
, to a singular push-forward of the target
\mu^{\ast}
in the space of distributions over
\mathcal{Z}
. Also, we next show that the concentration at the optimal dual value generally does not occur for finite
\lambda
, even though the diffusion coefficient is degenerate.

In the case of smooth coefficients, the Fokker–Planck operator can be shown to be hypoelliptic, implying that any solution to the dynamics has a smooth density with respect to the Lebesgue measure for all times
t>0
, even in the case of a concentrated initialization.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a partial differential operator with smooth coefficients. If, for every open set
\Omega
and every distribution
u
on
\Omega
,
\mathcal{L}u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
implies
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, then
\mathcal{L}
is called hypoelliptic.

Every elliptic operator is hypoelliptic, and equally any operator of the form
-\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}
where
\mathcal{L}
is the generator of a diffusion process with uniformly positive definite diffusion coefficient. A special case is overdamped Langevin diffusion with smooth drift, which naturally has a smooth transition density.

The degenerate diffusion coefficient in (
2.6
) results in a constant but only positive semidefinite diffusion coefficient
\Sigma_{\text{{PD}}}
in the Fokker–Planck equation (
3.9
). In order to prove that the Fokker–Planck equation is hypoelliptic, we hence have to employ a weaker condition for hypoellipticity, usually termed ‘Hörmander’s condition’
[
27
]
. In order to state it, we need the definition of the commutator operation and Lie algebras spanned by smooth vector fields.

Definition 3.8
.

For two vector fields
A,B
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the Lie bracket or commutator
[A,B]
is the vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

[A,B](x)=DB(x)A(x)-DA(x)B(x),

with the derivative matrices
(DA)_{i,j}(x)=\partial_{j}A_{i}(x)
.

Lemma 3.9

(
[
27
]
)
.

Assume that the generator
\mathcal{L}
of a diffusion process with state space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
C^{\infty}
-smooth coefficients and can be written in the form

\mathcal{L}=A_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{j}^{2}

with first-order differential operators
A_{0},\dots,A_{k}
. Interpreting the differential operators as vector fields, define collections of vector fields
\mathcal{A}_{l}
by

\mathcal{A}_{0}\coloneqq\{A_{j}\,:\,j=1,\dots,k\},\quad\mathcal{A}_{l+1}=\mathcal{A}_{l}\cup\{[A,A_{j}]\,:\,A\in\mathcal{A}_{l},j=0,\dots,k\}.

If the vector spaces
\mathcal{A}_{l}(x)\coloneqq\spanop\left(\{A(x)\,:\,A\in\mathcal{A}_{l}\}\right)
satisfy
\bigcup_{l\geq 0}\mathcal{A}_{l}(x)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\mathcal{L}
is said to satisfy the parabolic Hörmander condition. In that case,
\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L}
and
\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
are both hypoelliptic, hence any process generated by
\cal L
has
C^{\infty}
-smooth transition probability. In particular, any solution to the Fokker–Planck equation
\partial_{t}p=\mathcal{L}^{\ast}p
is
C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
-smooth.

We refer to
[
37
, ch. 6]
for a discussion in the context of diffusion SDEs and an application to the Langevin diffusion SDE with friction.
For the diffusion equation arising in our primal-dual setting, the generator is given by

\mathcal{L}_{\text{{PD}}}u=-A_{\text{{PD}}}^{T}(z)\nabla_{z}u+\Delta_{x}u.

The following result gives a sufficient condition under which the primal-dual dynamics considered here satisfy Hörmander’s condition, so that the corresponding Fokker–Planck operator is hypoelliptic.

Theorem 3.10
.

Assume that
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})
and
f^{\ast}\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})
, so that the diffusion (
2.6
) has smooth coefficients. If
\dim(\mathcal{Y})=m\leq d=\dim(\mathcal{X})
and
K
has full rank
m
, then
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{PD}}}
satisfies Hörmander’s condition. As a consequence, any stationary solution of (
3.9
), in particular
\pi_{\lambda}
under the assumptions of
Theorem

3.4
, has a
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{Z})
-smooth Lebesgue density.

The proof consists in verifying the assumptions of
Lemma

3.9
; it can be found in
Section

A.2
.

While the smoothness assumption on
g,f^{\ast}
is necessary for technical reasons, our numerical test validated that the dispersion in the dual variable is a more general phenomenon. Concentration in the dual variable can generally not be expected in the primal-dual dynamics.

3.3
Convergence to Overdamped Langevin Diffusion

We now continue by showing that in the limiting case
\lambda\to\infty
and under some smoothness assumption on
f
, the stationary solution
\pi_{\lambda}
of (
2.6
) becomes singular. In the limit, the primal marginal distribution coincides with the target.

Consider a solution
(X_{t},Y_{t})
of (
2.6
) with distribution
(X_{t},Y_{t})\sim\pi_{t}
, denoting the
x
-marginal by
X_{t}\sim\mu_{t}
. We are interested in the behaviour of
\mu_{t}
in the limit
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. Intuitively, as
\lambda
becomes large, it is clear that
Y_{t}
is forced to concentrate, with the (formally) limiting SDE taking the form

\begin{split}\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}&\in-(\partial g(X_{t})+K^{T}Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\\
Y_{t}&\in\partial f(KX_{t}).\end{split}

(3.12)

This coincides with overdamped Langevin diffusion on the potential
g+f\circ K
, with the target
\mu^{\ast}
as invariant distribution.

This suggests we can use a coupling argument between (
3.12
) and the primal-dual dynamics (
2.6
) to bound the Wasserstein distance between the stationary state of (
2.6
) and the target.

Theorem 3.11
.

Assume that
f\in C^{3}
and
g\in C^{1}
, that
g,f^{\ast}
are
\omega_{g}
- and
\omega_{f^{\ast}}
-strongly convex, respectively, and that the target distribution
\mu^{\ast}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu^{\ast}}\left[\lVert\nabla(\nabla f\circ K)(X)\rVert_{F}^{2}\right]=:D_{1}<\infty,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu^{\ast}}\left[\lVert\nabla\cdot\nabla(\nabla f\circ K)(X)\rVert^{2}\right]=:D_{2}<\infty,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu^{\ast}}\left[\lVert\nabla(\nabla f\circ K)(X)\left(\nabla g(X)+K^{T}\nabla f(KX)\right)\rVert^{2}\right]=:D_{3}<\infty,

with constants
D_{i}
, where in the second line the Laplacian is understood as acting component-wise on the
\mathcal{Y}
-valued function
\nabla f\circ K
. For
\omega_{g}/\omega_{f^{\ast}}<\lambda
and a constant
C_{1}(\lambda)=(\lambda\omega_{g}/D_{1})^{-1/2}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-3/2})
as
\lambda\to\infty
, the following two statements hold:

1.

If the primal-dual dynamics (
2.6
) are initialized with
X_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
for some
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
and
Y_{0}
concentrated at the optimality condition
Y_{0}=\nabla f(KX_{0})
, then for every
t>0
it holds

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{t},\mu^{\ast})\leq(1+\lambda^{-1}\omega_{f^{\ast}}^{-2}\lVert K\rVert^{2})^{1/2}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu^{\ast})e^{-\omega_{g}t}+C_{1}(\lambda).

2.

The
x
-marginal
\mu_{\lambda}
of the stationary solution
\pi_{\lambda}
of (
2.6
) satisfies

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{\lambda},\mu^{\ast})\leq C_{1}(\lambda).

The idea of this result’s proof is a synchronous coupling, similar to the computations in
Section

3.1.1
and
Lemma

3.3
. This time, however, we couple two different processes (
3.12
) and (
2.6
). The difference results in an offset term in the Gronwall inequality, which remains bounded under the assumptions, and leads to the bias
C_{1}(\lambda)
. The second part follows from the first by simply taking the limit
t\to\infty
. The full proof can be found in
Section

A.3
.

Note that in the above result, the convergence rate is controlled by the constant
\omega_{g}
. This could lead one to believe that
\omega_{f^{*}}
can be arbitrarily small without having an effect on the convergence properties. Inspecting the proof more closely, this is because we shift energy terms corresponding to the dual objective
\nabla f\circ K
to the bias term
C_{1}(\lambda)
(to be precise, by the specific choice of
\delta
after (
A.6
)). In the formulation of
Theorem

3.11
, reducing
\omega_{f^{*}}
implicitly increases the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla f
, leading to higher variance of the dual objective and therefore increased constants
D_{i}
. The residual terms
D_{i}
all gather in the bias term
C_{1}(\lambda)
, which grows arbitrarily as
\omega_{f^{*}}
shrinks to zero.

As the constants
D_{i}
are generally not available in closed form, the result does not give a clear answer to how the convergence behaviour changes under reparametrizations of the potential. For example, if
K
is invertible, one could sample from the modified density
\exp(-f(u)-g(K^{-1}u))
and push the samples forward under
K^{-1}
. The effect of this on convergence behaviour of the SDE presents an interesting future direction of research.

3.4
A Bias-Corrected Modification of Primal-Dual Diffusion

In this subsection, we want to follow up on the negative result of
Theorem

3.6
, where we saw that there is no way to construct a diffusion SDE with the primal-dual type drift coefficient and homogeneous, problem-independent diffusion that has the correct stationary distribution. Naturally, we can also consider modifications of the diffusion SDE with problem-dependent and/or non-homogeneous diffusion coefficient, so that the samples become (in continuous time) unbiased.

We aim to correct the stochastic process (
2.5
) by adding terms such that
\pi^{\ast}=(\id,\nabla f\circ K)_{\#}\mu^{\ast}
becomes its stationary solution. Throughout this subsection, we will assume that
g\in C^{1}(\mathcal{X})
and
f\in C^{3}(\mathcal{Y})
, and that
f^{\ast}
is differentiable and strictly convex, so that
\nabla f^{\ast}=(\nabla f)^{-1}
.
We define
M:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
by
M(x)\coloneqq\nabla(\nabla f\circ K)^{T}(x)=K^{T}H_{f}(Kx)
, where
H_{f}
denotes the Hessian of
f
, and
r:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
componentwise by
r_{j}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}M_{ij}(x)=\nabla_{x}\cdot M_{\cdot j}(x)
, the divergence of the
j
-th column of
M
. Consider now the modified stochastic differential equation

\,\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=-A_{\text{{mod}}}(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+B_{\text{{mod}}}(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(3.13)

where the modified drift and diffusion coefficients are given by

\displaystyle A_{\text{{mod}}}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\nabla g(x)+K^{T}y\\
\lambda(\nabla f^{*}(y)-Kx)+M(x)^{T}(\nabla g(x)+K^{T}\nabla f(Kx))-r(x)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle B_{\text{{mod}}}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{2}\,\nabla(\id,\nabla f\circ K)(x)=\sqrt{2}\begin{pmatrix}I\\
M(x)^{T}\end{pmatrix}.

In contrast to (
2.5
),
B_{\text{{mod}}}
depends on
x
and is therefore not a special case of
B_{\text{{gPD}}}
. The drift
A_{\text{{mod}}}
contains the same terms as
A_{\text{{PD}}}
, and an additional term
-M(x)^{T}\nabla\log\mu^{\ast}(x)-r(x)
. The Fokker–Planck equation under non-constant diffusion coefficients like in (
3.13
) is

\frac{\partial\pi_{t}}{\partial t}=\nabla_{z}\cdot\left(A_{\text{{mod}}}(z)\pi_{t}+\nabla_{z}\cdot\left(\Sigma_{\text{{mod}}}(z)\pi_{t}\right)\right),

(3.14)

where
\Sigma_{\text{{mod}}}(z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}B_{\text{{mod}}}(z)B_{\text{{mod}}}^{T}(z)
, see
[
37
]
.

Remark 3.12
.

As an intuitive explanation why (
3.13
) has the desired stationary distribution, note that we want the stationary solution to concentrate its mass in
\mathcal{Z}=\mathbb{R}^{d+m}
on the
d
-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{U}
described by
y=\nabla f(Kx)
. The tangent space to
\mathcal{U}
at any point
(x,\nabla f(Kx))
, denoted in the following by
\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathcal{U}
, is spanned by the columns of
\nabla(\id,\nabla f\circ K)=(I,M(x))^{T}
. This coincides with the column space of
\Sigma_{\text{{mod}}}
, hence locally on
\mathcal{U}
, the diffusion is confined to the manifold. The term
\nabla f^{\ast}(y)-Kx
in the drift vanishes upon concentration of the mass on
\mathcal{U}
; of the remaining terms,
M(x)^{T}(\nabla g(x)+K^{T}\nabla f(Kx))
is just the gradient of the negative log-density
-\nabla\log\mu^{\ast}(x)=\nabla g(x)+K^{T}\nabla f(Kx)
in the primal variable
x
mapped to the tangent space of
\mathcal{U}
by the map
(I,M(x))^{T}
, i.e. tangential to
\mathcal{U}
, and by itself would leave
\mathcal{U}
invariant just as in overdamped Langevin diffusion. The remaining term
-r(x)
is not tangential: since
\Sigma_{\text{{mod}}}
varies along
\mathcal{U}
, the diffusion alone would let mass leak off the manifold to second order, at a rate governed by the curvature of
\mathcal{U}
, i.e. by the divergence
r(x)
of the columns of
M
. The term
-r(x)
exactly cancels this leakage. Locally on
\mathcal{U}
, (
3.13
) coincides with overdamped Langevin diffusion, up to this curvature correction.

In order to show formally that the partially singular distribution
\pi^{\ast}
is a stationary solution of (
3.13
), we have to consider the weak formulation of the corresponding Fokker–Planck equation, given by

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}=\int\Sigma_{\text{{mod}}}(z):\nabla_{z}^{2}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}-\int\nabla\varphi\cdot A_{\text{{mod}}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\quad\forall\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{Z}),

(3.15)

where
\Sigma:\Phi=\sum_{ij}\Sigma_{ij}\Phi_{ij}
denotes the matrix scalar product.

Theorem 3.13
.

The distribution
\pi^{\ast}\coloneqq(\id,\nabla f\circ K)_{\#}\mu^{*}
is a stationary solution of (
3.15
).

In principle, this theorem might suggest that a time discretization of the modified diffusion (
3.13
) may be a better idea than the primal-dual sampling algorithm (
1.4
). Note, however, that apart from the higher-order derivative terms in
f
(which counter the motivation to use a primal-dual scheme in the first place), the term
A_{\text{{mod}}}
contains just the gradient of the target log-density, i.e. we explicitly add overdamped Langevin diffusion in the dual variable. This suggests that, algorithmically, the dualization is somewhat redundant and any sampler based on (
3.13
) would be inferior to a standard unadjusted Langevin algorithm.

The proof of
Theorem

3.13
can be found in
Section

A.4
, along with an auxiliary vector calculus identity (
Lemma

A.1
) that is needed in the proof. It consists in plugging the terms into the weak Fokker–Planck equation (
3.15
) and verifying equality.

Remark 3.14
.

While the last result showed that the target is now an invariant solution, the asymptotic stability of the new diffusion process is unclear. In general, it is hard to generalize a contraction result like
Lemma

3.3
to the case of non-homogeneous diffusion coefficients. In section 2 of
[
23
]
, this question is addressed in more detail for the heat equation with non-homogeneous diffusion. The authors show that it becomes possible to prove stability in a Wasserstein distance with a modified underlying metric, where the metric depends on the diffusion coefficient via an elliptic PDE.

Since the modified equation (
3.13
) is of limited numerical interest, we only sketch some main ideas and omit a detailed proof. At every point
x\in\mathcal{X}
one can define an inner product
\langle u,v\rangle_{x}\coloneqq u^{T}V^{-1}(x)v
where
V(x)=W(x)W(x)^{T}
is symmetric positive definite. The first
d
columns of
W
form a basis of the tangent space
\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{U}
at
x
, and the remaining
m
columns span the normal space
(\mathrm{T}_{x}\mathcal{U})^{\perp}
. We then need to equip
\mathcal{Z}
with the induced Riemannian metric

d_{\text{{mod}}}(z,\tilde{z})=\inf_{\zeta(0)=z,\,\zeta(1)=\tilde{z}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\dot{\zeta}(t)^{T}V^{-1}(\zeta(t))\dot{\zeta}(t)\right)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The difficulty then usually consists in finding a more convenient form of
d_{\text{{mod}}}(z,\tilde{z})
, which allows one to integrate the Fokker–Planck equation against
d_{\text{{mod}}}^{2}(z,\tilde{z})
in order to show contraction. We leave this to future work.

Example 3.15
.

We can check that in the one-dimensional quadratic case that was considered as an illustrative counterexample in
Theorems

3.5
and
3.6
, the modified equation now has the desired invariant distribution
\pi^{\ast}
. Furthermore, if
\lambda>k^{2}c_{f}^{-2}
, every solution
\pi_{t}
of the Fokker–Planck equation converges to
\pi^{\ast}
as
t\to\infty
. This can be seen as follows: We carry out the same computations as in the proof of
Theorem

3.5
to obtain the Fourier transform
p_{t}
of the solution
\pi_{t}
, given by

\displaystyle p_{t}(\zeta)=p_{0}(\exp(-tA_{\text{{mod}}}^{T})\zeta)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(B_{\text{{mod}}}^{T}\exp(-sA_{\text{{mod}}}^{T})\zeta)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right),

\displaystyle A_{\text{{mod}}}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{g}^{-1}&k\\
-\lambda k+kc_{f}^{-1}c_{g}^{-1}+k^{3}c_{f}^{-2}&\lambda c_{f}\end{pmatrix},\quad B_{\text{{mod}}}=\sqrt{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
kc_{f}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

This time, the quadratic form under the integral is not positive definite in
\zeta
since
B_{\text{{mod}}}
is an eigenvector of
A_{\text{{mod}}}
with eigenvalue
v_{h}\coloneqq c_{g}^{-1}+k^{2}c_{f}^{-1}
. Instead, it holds

\int_{0}^{t}(B_{\text{{mod}}}^{T}\exp(-sA_{\text{{mod}}}^{T})\zeta)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s=(2v_{h})^{-1}\left(1-e^{-2tv_{h}}\right)(B_{\text{{mod}}}^{T}\zeta)^{2}.

Taking the inverse Fourier transform shows that for every (possibly degenerate) initialization
\pi_{0}
the solution
\pi_{t}
converges to the invariant distribution, which is a degenerate Gaussian with mean zero and covariance matrix
(2v_{h})^{-1}B_{\text{{mod}}}B_{\text{{mod}}}^{T}
. This is precisely the target distribution
\pi^{\ast}
.

4
Convergence of the Discrete Time Algorithm

We want to apply similar techniques as in
Section

3
to the time discretized setting of the algorithm (
1.4
). Indeed, couplings like the ones we employed in the continuous time setting above have been used before in the stability and convergence analysis of Langevin algorithms. We begin this section by recalling such a computation in the simple case of ULA as an instructive example, and then expand to the primal-dual algorithm.

For a convex functional
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, points
x,\tilde{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and subgradients
p\in\partial h(x),\tilde{p}\in\partial h(\tilde{x})
, we will use the symmetric Bregman distance
D_{h,\text{sym}}^{p,\tilde{p}}(x,\tilde{x})=\langle x-\tilde{x},p-\tilde{p}\rangle
. If
h
is differentiable with unique subgradients, we will drop the reference to
p,\tilde{p}
and just write
D_{h,\text{sym}}(x,\tilde{x})
. Recall that, if
h
is convex,
D_{h,\text{sym}}
is always non-negative.

Suppose now that the potential
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\omega_{h}
-strongly convex and differentiable, and
\nabla h
is
L_{\nabla h}
-Lipschitz continuous. We consider two coupled Markov chains
(X^{n})_{n},(\tilde{X}^{n})_{n}
defined by initial values
X^{0}\sim\mu^{0},\tilde{X}^{0}\sim\tilde{\mu}^{0}
and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\xi^{n+1}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{d})\\
X^{n+1}=X^{n}-\tau\nabla h(X^{n})+\sqrt{2\tau}\xi^{n+1}\\
\tilde{X}^{n+1}=\tilde{X}^{n}-\tau\nabla h(\tilde{X}^{n})+\sqrt{2\tau}\xi^{n+1}.\end{array}\right.

Then we can compute that (see
[
20
, Prop. 3]
, where this calculation has been carried out)

\displaystyle\lVert X^{n+1}-\tilde{X}^{n+1}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert X^{n}-\tau\nabla h(X^{n})-\tilde{X}^{n}+\tau\nabla h(\tilde{X}^{n})\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert X^{n}-\tilde{X}^{n}\rVert^{2}-2\tau D_{h,\textrm{sym}}(X^{n},\tilde{X}^{n})+\tau^{2}\lVert\nabla h(X^{n})-\nabla h(\tilde{X}^{n})\rVert^{2}.

We can use the following bound for strongly convex functions with Lipschitz continuous gradient
[
36
, Thm 2.1.12]

D_{h,\textrm{sym}}(X^{n},\tilde{X}^{n})\geq\frac{\omega_{h}L_{\nabla h}}{\omega_{h}+L_{\nabla h}}\lVert X^{n}-\tilde{X}^{n}\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{\omega_{h}+L_{\nabla h}}\lVert\nabla h(X^{n})-\nabla h(\tilde{X}^{n})\rVert^{2},

and we obtain

\lVert X^{n+1}-\tilde{X}^{n+1}\rVert^{2}\leq\left(1-\tau\frac{2\omega_{h}L_{\nabla h}}{\omega_{h}+L_{\nabla h}}\right)\lVert X^{n}-\tilde{X}^{n}\rVert^{2}+\tau\left(\tau-\frac{2}{\omega_{h}+L_{\nabla h}}\right)\lVert\nabla h(X^{n})-\nabla h(\tilde{X}^{n})\rVert^{2}.

If the step size is chosen to be
\tau=2/(\omega_{h}+L_{\nabla h})
, we obtain the convergence rate

\lVert X^{n}-\tilde{X}^{n}\rVert^{2}\leq\left(\frac{L_{\nabla h}-\omega_{h}}{L_{\nabla h}+\omega_{h}}\right)^{2n}\lVert X^{0}-\tilde{X}^{0}\rVert^{2}.

Denoting the distribution of
X^{n}
by
\mu^{n}
and that of
\tilde{X}^{n}
by
\tilde{\mu}^{n}
, we obtain by taking the infimum over all couplings of
\mu^{0}
and
\tilde{\mu}^{0}
on the right side that

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu^{n},\tilde{\mu}^{n})\leq\left(\frac{L_{\nabla h}-\omega_{h}}{L_{\nabla h}+\omega_{h}}\right)^{n}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu^{0},\tilde{\mu}^{0}).

Since
(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\mathcal{W}_{2})
is complete,
\mu^{n},\tilde{\mu}^{n}
converge to a unique stationary distribution
\mu_{\tau}
in
\mathcal{W}_{2}
distance. We concentrate on Wasserstein convergence in the present work, but convergence in e.g. total variation distance can be derived
[
20
]
. Similar computations can be carried out for other time-discretizations of Langevin diffusion, e.g. time-implicit ones, leading to proximal sampling schemes
[
41
,
16
]
. Since the stationary distribution
\mu_{\tau}
of the time-discrete scheme is not the target distribution
\mu^{\ast}
, the missing step to relate the Markov chain samples to the target is to prove an error bound using a coupling of the time-discrete chain and the time-continuous process. Typically, this is more challenging and the introduced bias (in Wasserstein distance) between the two scales with a power of the step size
\tau

[
15
,
14
,
16
,
41
,
31
]
.

4.1
Stability of Primal-Dual Langevin Sampling

We now turn to the primal-dual setting of (
1.4
). Consider the following coupling in discrete time with initial values
(X^{0},Y^{0})\sim\pi^{0}
and
(\tilde{X}^{0},\tilde{Y}^{0})\sim\tilde{\pi}^{0}
and identical stochastic input to both iterations:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\xi^{n+1}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{d})\\
Y^{n+1}=\prox_{\sigma f^{\ast}}(Y^{n}+\sigma KX_{\theta}^{n})\\
X^{n+1}=\prox_{\tau g}(X^{n}-\tau K^{T}Y^{n+1})+\sqrt{2\tau}\xi^{n+1}\\
X_{\theta}^{n+1}=X^{n+1}+\theta(X^{n+1}-X^{n})\\
\tilde{Y}^{n+1}=\prox_{\sigma f^{\ast}}(\tilde{Y}^{n}+\sigma K\tilde{X}_{\theta}^{n})\\
\tilde{X}^{n+1}=\prox_{\tau g}(\tilde{X}^{n}-\tau K^{T}\tilde{Y}^{n+1})+\sqrt{2\tau}\xi^{n+1}\\
\tilde{X}_{\theta}^{n+1}=\tilde{X}^{n+1}+\theta(\tilde{X}^{n+1}-\tilde{X}^{n}).\end{array}\right.

(4.1)

By convention, we set
X^{-1}=X^{0}
and
\tilde{X}^{-1}=\tilde{X}^{0}
. In the following, we denote the distribution of
(X^{n},Y^{n})
by
\pi^{n}
and the
X^{n}
-marginal by
\mu^{n}
, and analogously by
\tilde{\pi}^{n},\tilde{\mu}^{n}
those of
(\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{Y}^{n})
. Denote further
L\coloneqq\lVert K\rVert
where
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is the operator norm induced by the Euclidean norm on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
. We assume that also in the discrete setting, the step sizes are coupled via the relation
\lambda=\sigma/\tau
.

We start by giving a stability result for the case where
g
and
f^{\ast}
are merely convex (not necessarily strongly convex).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(X^{n},Y^{n})
and
(\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{Y}^{n})
be given by the coupling (
4.1
) with overrelaxation parameter
\theta=1
and
\tau\sigma L^{2}\leq 1
. Then for the primal variable it holds

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu^{N},\tilde{\mu}^{N})\leq\mathcal{T}_{1,\lambda^{-1}}(\pi^{0},\tilde{\pi}^{0}),

where
\mathcal{T}_{1,\lambda^{-1}}
is the transport distance over the joint primal-dual variable defined in (
3.10
). If
\tau\sigma L^{2}<1
, then the joint variable obeys

\mathcal{T}_{1,\lambda^{-1}}(\pi^{N},\tilde{\pi}^{N})\leq C\mathcal{T}_{1,\lambda^{-1}}(\pi^{0},\tilde{\pi}^{0}),

with the constant
C=(1-\tau\sigma L^{2})^{-1/2}
.

In the case of strongly convex
g
and
f^{\ast}
, we can prove the following contraction result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that
g
and
f^{\ast}
are
\omega_{g}
- and
\omega_{f^{\ast}}
-strongly convex respectively. Let
(X^{n},Y^{n})
and
(\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{Y}^{n})
be given by the coupling (
4.1
) with overrelaxation parameter
\theta
satisfying

\max\{(1+2\omega_{g}\tau)^{-1},(1+2\omega_{f^{\ast}}\sigma)^{-1}\}\leq\theta<1,

and further
\theta\sigma\tau L^{2}\leq 1
. Then with the constant
\tilde{\lambda}\coloneqq\frac{1+2\omega_{g}\tau}{1+2\omega_{f^{\ast}}\sigma}\,\lambda
, it holds

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu^{N},\tilde{\mu}^{N})\leq\theta^{N/2}\mathcal{T}_{1,\tilde{\lambda}^{-1}}(\pi^{0},\tilde{\pi}^{0})

and if
\theta\sigma\tau L^{2}<1
, then with
\tilde{C}\coloneqq(1-\theta\tau\sigma L^{2})^{-1/2}
we have for the joint variables

\mathcal{T}_{1,\tilde{\lambda}^{-1}}(\pi^{N},\tilde{\pi}^{N})\leq\tilde{C}\theta^{N/2}\mathcal{T}_{1,\tilde{\lambda}^{-1}}(\pi^{0},\tilde{\pi}^{0}).

It follows directly that in the latter case a stationary distribution
\pi_{\lambda,\tau}
exists and the distribution
\pi^{N}
of samples converges to
\pi_{\lambda,\tau}
as
N\to\infty
.

The proofs of
Theorems

4.1
and
4.2
can be found in
Section

B.1
. In order to prove both results, we derive an auxiliary inequality akin to a descent lemma. Both proofs then result from iterating the descent inequality over
N
steps and are in spirit very similar to existing convergence proofs of primal-dual optimization algorithms
[
12
,
11
]
.

Having proven the stability theorems for the iteration (
1.4
), we briefly comment on the version of the algorithm that adds the stochastic term
\xi
inside the argument of the proximal operator.

Corollary 4.3
.

Instead of (
1.4
), consider the iteration procedure

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\xi^{n+1}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{d})\\
Y^{n+1}=\prox_{\sigma f^{\ast}}(Y^{n}+\sigma KX_{\theta}^{n})\\
X^{n+1}=\prox_{\tau g}(X^{n}-\tau K^{T}Y^{n+1}+\sqrt{2\tau}\xi^{n+1})\\
X_{\theta}^{n+1}=X^{n+1}+\theta(X^{n+1}-X^{n})\end{array}\right.

(4.2)

with the stochastic term
\sqrt{2\tau}\xi
added inside the proximal mapping. For the iterates of this algorithm, under the same assumptions, the results of
Theorems

4.1
and
4.2
also hold. Under the assumptions of
Theorem

4.2
with
\theta\sigma\tau L^{2}<1
, the algorithm therefore has a stationary distribution
\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda,\tau}
.

The proof can be found in
Section

B.1
.

Note that this result does not state anything about the relation between the stationary distributions
\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda,\tau}
of (
4.2
) and
\pi_{\lambda,\tau}
of (
1.4
); they will generally be different distributions. To see this, consider an example where
g=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}+\frac{\omega_{g}}{2}\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2}
(and
f^{\ast}
strongly convex) has the proximal map
\prox_{\tau g}(x)_{j}=(1+\tau\omega_{g})^{-1}\sign(x_{j})\max(\left|x_{j}\right|-\tau,0)
. Then some sets of Lebesgue measure zero like the singleton
\{0\}
necessarily have positive mass under the law of
X^{n+1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
in (
4.2
). Plugging in the stationary distribution
\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda,\tau}
implies that such sets have positive mass under
\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda,\tau}
. Therefore
\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda,\tau}
does not have a Lebesgue density.

Under the iteration (
1.4
), the samples are always the result of a diffusion step applied to the output of the proximal mapping, so samples can not be concentrated on a single value. A direct bound of the distance of the two distributions can likely be proven, but is outside the scope of the present work.

4.2
Convergence to the Continuous Time Stationary Solution

Having shown that the algorithm is stable with a limiting stationary distribution
\pi_{\lambda,\tau}
in the strongly convex case, we need to find bounds on the distance between
\pi_{\lambda,\tau}
and the continuous time stationary solution
\pi_{\lambda}
, in order to relate the drawn samples to the target. We approach this by considering again a coupling, but this time between the algorithm (
1.4
) and the stationary state of its continuous time limiting SDE (
2.6
).

Assume that the time steps
\tau,\sigma
are constant with
\lambda=\sigma/\tau
. Let
(X^{0},Y^{0})\sim\pi^{0}
for some initial distribution
\pi^{0}
, and set
X_{\theta}^{0}=X^{0}
. Now consider a process
\tilde{Z}_{t}=(\tilde{X}_{t},\tilde{Y}_{t})
,
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
which is initialized at
\tilde{Z}_{0}\sim\pi_{\lambda}
. Let
W_{t}
be
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued Brownian motion and denote
\xi^{n+1}:=\tau^{-1/2}\int_{n\tau}^{(n+1)\tau}\mathrm{d}W_{t}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We now consider
Z^{n}=(X^{n},Y^{n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\tilde{Z}_{t}=(\tilde{X}_{t},\tilde{Y}_{t})
,
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
defined by the coupling

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}Y^{n+1}=\prox_{\sigma f^{\ast}}(Y^{n}+\sigma KX_{\theta}^{n})\\
X^{n+1}=\prox_{\tau g}(X^{n}-\tau K^{T}Y^{n+1})+\sqrt{2\tau}\xi^{n+1}\\
X_{\theta}^{n+1}=X^{n+1}+\theta(X^{n+1}-X^{n})\\
\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{X}_{t}=-(\nabla g(\tilde{X}_{t})+K^{T}\tilde{Y}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\\
\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{Y}_{t}=-\lambda(\nabla f^{\ast}(\tilde{Y}_{t})-K\tilde{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{array}\right.

(4.3)

It immediately follows that the marginal distribution of
\tilde{Z}_{t}
is stationary with
\tilde{Z}_{t}\sim\pi_{\lambda}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. For notational convenience, we also define an overrelaxation variable in continuous time by
\tilde{X}_{t,\theta}:=\tilde{X}_{t}+\theta(\tilde{X}_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t-\tau})
and set
\tilde{X}_{t-\tau}=\tilde{X}_{0}
when
t\in[0,\tau)
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume that
g\in C^{1}(\mathcal{X})
and
\nabla g
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{\nabla g}>0
, that
f
is
\omega_{f}
-strongly convex with
\omega_{f}>0
, and that
g,f^{\ast}
are
\omega_{g}
- and
\omega_{f^{\ast}}
-strongly convex, respectively. Then
\nabla f^{\ast}
is
\omega_{f}^{-1}
-Lipschitz continuous, so that
A_{\text{{PD}}}
grows at most linearly, and since
\pi_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
by
Theorem

3.4
, the constant
D_{4}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\pi_{\lambda}}\left[\lVert A_{\text{{PD}}}(Z)\rVert^{2}\right]
is finite. Let the step sizes
\sigma,\tau
and the overrelaxation parameter
\theta
be chosen such that
\theta\sigma\tau L^{2}\leq 1
and

\max\{(1+\omega_{g}\tau)^{-1},(1+\omega_{f^{\ast}}\sigma)^{-1}\}\eqqcolon\theta_{\min}\leq\theta<1.

Let the iterates
(X^{n},Y^{n})
be generated by (
1.4
). Denoting by
\mu^{n}
the marginal distribution of
X^{n}
, we have

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu^{N},\mu_{\lambda})\leq\theta^{N/2}\mathcal{T}_{1,\bar{\lambda}^{-1}}(\pi^{0},\pi_{\lambda})+C_{2}(\tau,\theta).

If further
\theta\sigma\tau L^{2}<1
, then the joint distribution
\pi^{n}
of
(X^{n},Y^{n})
satisfies

\mathcal{T}_{1,\bar{\lambda}^{-1}}(\pi^{N},\pi_{\lambda})\leq\frac{\theta^{N/2}}{(1-\theta\sigma\tau L^{2})^{1/2}}\mathcal{T}_{1,\bar{\lambda}^{-1}}(\pi^{0},\pi_{\lambda})+\frac{C_{2}(\tau,\theta)}{(1-\theta\sigma\tau L^{2})^{1/2}},

with the constants

\bar{\lambda}\coloneqq\frac{1+\omega_{g}\tau}{1+\omega_{f^{\ast}}\sigma}\lambda,\quad C_{2}(\tau,\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{(1-\theta)^{1/2}}\left((4dL_{\nabla g}^{2}\omega_{g}^{-1}+6dL^{2}(1+2\theta^{2})\omega_{f^{\ast}}^{-1})^{1/2}\tau+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})\right).

Note that
\theta_{\min}
depends on
\tau
. Assuming the minimal choice
\theta=\theta_{\min}
to achieve the optimal convergence rate, it holds
1-\theta=\min\{\omega_{g},\omega_{f^{*}}\lambda\}\tau+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})
as
\tau\to 0
. This implies
C_{2}(\tau)\coloneqq C_{2}(\tau,\theta_{\min})=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{1/2})
.

The proof of
Theorem

4.4
can be found in
Section

B.2
.
This result is “typical” for Langevin algorithms in the sense that it uses a standard condition on the drift and results in the same order of bias as conventional, primal Langevin algorithms:

Remark 4.5
.

The finiteness of
D_{4}
corresponds to a standard assumption on the drift in Langevin sampling algorithms. In the case of overdamped Langevin diffusion, it is satisfied if the drift coefficient, i.e.
\nabla h
in (
3.2
), is Lipschitz continuous, see Lemma 2 of
[
15
]
.

In the case of primal-dual diffusion, it puts growth requirements on
\nabla g
and
\nabla f^{\ast}
instead: if there are constants
\alpha,\beta
such that
\lVert\nabla g(x)\rVert\leq\alpha+\beta\lVert x\rVert
and
\lVert\nabla f^{\ast}(y)\rVert\leq\alpha+\beta\lVert y\rVert
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, then
D_{4}<\infty
. Under the assumptions of
Theorem

4.4
, this holds automatically.

Remark 4.6
.

The Wasserstein bias term of order
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{1/2})
between discretized scheme and continuous time limit is the typical order known from purely primal Langevin algorithms under strong log-concavity
[
14
,
16
,
41
]
.

Apart from the bounded drift condition, the strong convexity and Lipschitz assumptions in
Theorem

4.4
restrict the target distribution
\mu^{*}
to be supported everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This is in contrast to existing theory and practice of primal-dual methods in optimization: Due to their proximal nature, convergence guarantees for these optimization algorithms can be derived for suitable objectives on bounded domains
[
13
]
. Extending the theory for sampling to constrained domains
\mathcal{X}\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{d}
likely requires more work, as the underlying SDE would have to change. This is an interesting future direction of research, as recent works have shown that primal Langevin algorithms can be restricted to some convex, constrained domains either by approximating the target or by modifying the SDE to a boundary-reflected process
[
34
,
30
]
. Our numerical results in
Section

5.4
suggest that primal-dual sampling appears to be effective for a target on the constrained domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

4.4
with
\theta\sigma\tau L^{2}<1
,
Theorem

4.2
shows that the discrete time algorithm converges to its stationary distribution
\pi_{\lambda,\tau}
as
N\to\infty
. From the nonasymptotic bounds in
Theorem

4.4
, we obtain the following bound between the invariant measures in discrete and continuous time.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let the assumptions of
Theorem

4.4
be satisfied with
\theta\sigma\tau L^{2}<1
and
\theta=\theta_{\min}
. Then, denoting by
\mu_{\lambda,\tau}
the
x
-marginal of
\pi_{\lambda,\tau}
, the following bounds hold:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{\lambda,\tau},\mu_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}(\tau,\theta_{\min})=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{1/2}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1,\bar{\lambda}^{-1}}(\pi_{\lambda,\tau},\pi_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{2}(\tau,\theta_{\min})}{(1-\theta\sigma\tau L^{2})^{1/2}}=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{1/2}).

Theorem

4.4
provides a bound between the samples produced by the algorithm and its continuous time limit. While this result is typical in the Langevin algorithms literature, we here have to combine this with
Theorem

3.11
in order to get a relation between samples and target distribution. The following result closes this final gap.

Corollary 4.8
.

Let the assumptions of
Theorems

3.11
and
4.4
be satisfied with
\theta\sigma\tau L^{2}<1
. Let
\pi^{n}
be the distribution of samples
(X^{n},Y^{n})
generated by (
1.4
) with
\theta=\theta_{\min}
. Then the
x
-marginal
\mu^{n}
of
\pi^{n}
obeys

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu^{N},\mu^{\ast})\leq\theta^{N/2}\mathcal{T}_{1,\bar{\lambda}^{-1}}(\pi^{0},\pi_{\lambda})+C_{1}(\lambda)+C_{2}(\tau),

with the constants
C_{1},C_{2}
from
Theorems

3.11
and
4.4
, respectively, which decay asymptotically like

\displaystyle C_{1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=D_{5}\lambda^{-1/2}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-3/2}),\quad\textrm{as }\lambda\to\infty,

\displaystyle C_{2}(\tau)

\displaystyle=D_{6}\tau^{1/2}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{3/2}),\quad\textrm{as }\tau\to 0,

with constants gathered in
D_{5},D_{6}
. It immediately follows that
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{\lambda,\tau},\mu^{\ast})\leq C_{1}(\lambda)+C_{2}(\tau)
.

Before moving on, let us comment on the implication of this result for numerical practice.

Remark 4.9
.

The final form of the bias
C_{1}(\lambda)+C_{2}(\tau)
exhibits two practical trade-offs. One is typical for Langevin samplers: Ideally,
\tau
is large so that subsequent samples are less correlated, but this at the same time increases the discretization bias
C_{2}(\tau)
, worsening the target approximation. Secondly, and specific to the primal-dual scheme,
\lambda
should be large, in order to guarantee small dualization bias, but
\sigma=\lambda\tau
is bounded since we have to control
\sigma\tau L^{2}
in order to guarantee stability of the iterates.

The constants
D_{i}
controlling the bias in
Theorem

3.11
are generally unknown, so we use numerical heuristics for the choice of
\lambda
and ablate on this parameter in several of the experiments below. Given
\lambda
, when
L
is known, we set
\tau=1/(\sqrt{\lambda}L)
and
\sigma=\sqrt{\lambda}/L
as the biggest step sizes that achieve guaranteed stability. When
\omega_{g},\omega_{f^{*}}
are known, the limited budget of discarded burn-in iterations implies we should choose
\theta=\theta_{\min}
in order to achieve rapid convergence to the target. These are of course limited heuristics motivated by our convergence results, and further studies on optimal parameter choices are needed.

In the following, final result, we comment on how the previous statements transfer to the modified scheme (
4.2
), which adds the random Gaussian increment before the proximal step.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
(X^{n},Y^{n})
be generated by the iteration (
4.2
), where the stochastic term
\xi
is added in the primal variable before applying the proximal mapping. By
Corollary

4.3
, this modified algorithm has a stationary state
\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda,\tau}
under suitable assumptions. For this distribution and the samples generated by the algorithm, the results of
Theorem

4.4
and
Corollaries

4.7
and
4.8
hold unchanged.

The proof can be found in
Section

B.2
.

5.2
Total Variation Based Image Denoising from Gaussian Noise

For the potential
f\circ K
that is dualized in the algorithm, we now consider the total variation (TV) functional, a typical model-based prior in imaging inverse problems. The (isotropic) TV functional is defined by

\TV(x):=\sum_{i,j}\sqrt{(\nabla_{h}x)_{i,j}^{2}+(\nabla_{v}x)_{i,j}^{2}},

where
\nabla_{h},\nabla_{v}
denote horizontal and vertical finite differences in the pixelated image
x
. We consider a Gibbs prior on
x
with density
\exp(-\alpha\TV(x))
where
\alpha
is a fixed regularization parameter (Note that this only defines a probability distribution on a subspace orthogonal to constant functions since TV has a one-dimensional null space. With a log-likelihood that grows fast enough at infinity along this null space, the posterior is however well-defined.). The corresponding potential term can be written in the form
\alpha\TV(x)=\alpha\lVert Kx\rVert_{2,1}=f(Kx)
where
K=(\nabla_{h},\nabla_{v})
and
f=\alpha\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2,1}
denotes a constant multiple of the nested
l_{2}
-
l_{1}
-norm (inner
l_{2}
over vertical/horizontal component, outer
l_{1}
over all pixels). We further assume that we make a noisy observation
\tilde{x}=x+\varepsilon
of
x
, where the noise is distributed like
\varepsilon\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I)
for some observation variance
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
. The corresponding negative log-likelihood is

g(x):=l_{\tilde{x}}(x)=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\lVert x-\tilde{x}\rVert_{2}^{2},

which is
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-2}
-strongly convex. We now want to sample from the posterior
\mu^{\ast}
, which by Bayes law has density proportional to
\exp(-\alpha\lVert Kx\rVert_{2,1}-l_{\tilde{x}}(x))=\exp(-f(Kx)-g(x))
.

5.2.1
Toy Example in Two Dimensions

We start with a small-scale example in which we are able to compute accurate approximations of Wasserstein distances between the samples’ distribution and the true posterior. Consider the artificial case where the ‘image’ consists of
2\times 1

\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{2}

reduces to

5.3
Total Generalized Variation Based Image Denoising

In this experiment, we consider an example with an image prior based on second order total generalized variation (TGV).
Like the TV functional, total generalized variation allows for discontinuous edges in images, but it generalizes TV in that it also employs higher-order derivatives.
Acting as a penalty also on higher order image gradients, TGV-based MAP estimates avoid the typical staircasing effects of TV in smooth but non-constant regions of images. For optimization purposes, the second order TGV functional is usually defined as

\displaystyle\TGV^{2}_{\alpha}(u)=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{V}}J^{2}_{\alpha}(u,v),

(5.1)

\displaystyle J^{2}_{\alpha}(u,v)=\alpha_{1}\lVert\nabla u-v\rVert_{2,1}+\alpha_{0}\lVert Ev\rVert_{2,1}.

Here
u\in\mathcal{U}
is an image while
v
is in the corresponding space
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}
of (discretized) gradients of
u
.
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}>0
are regularization parameters.
\nabla=(\nabla_{h},\nabla_{v}):\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
is a (forward discretized) gradient operator as in the case of TV, and
E:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{W}
is a symmetrized (backward discretized) gradient operator that acts as second order derivative. We refer to Section 2.1 of
[
5
]
for details on the implementation of the gradient operators.
In TGV-based variational minimization, the state space is typically expanded from the image space
\mathcal{U}
to the joint space
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}
and the minimization over
u
(in the variational problem) and
v
(in (
5.1
)) is carried out simultaneously. By applying primal-dual optimization methods, the iteration can be carried out efficiently without the need to evaluate the infimum in (
5.1
) explicitly
[
5
]
.
In the same way, we want to avoid evaluating
\TGV^{2}_{\alpha}
or its gradients explicitly in a sampling procedure. In order to recover the image
u
, we therefore propose to sample from a posterior of the form

\mu^{\ast}(x)\propto p(\tilde{x}|x)\exp\left(-J^{2}_{\alpha}(u,v)\right),\qquad x=(u,v)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{X},

where
p(\tilde{x}|x)
is the likelihood, for denoising with normally distributed noise given as before by

-\log p(\tilde{x}|x)=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\lVert u-\tilde{u}\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}^{2},\qquad x=(u,v),\tilde{x}=(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\in\mathcal{X}

We note that this is a relaxed version of regularization with
\TGV_{\alpha}^{2}
, since we dropped the requirement that
v
solves (
5.1
). Practically, the prior
\exp(-J^{2}_{\alpha}(u,v))
seems to yield very similar smoothing behaviour to the effect of total generalized variation, we leave its detailed analysis to future work.
Note that the chosen target log-density is in the necessary form (
1.1
):
g(x)
is, as before, the negative log-likelihood
\lVert u-\tilde{u}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{U}}/(2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
for all
x=(u,v)
. We define the linear operator

K=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla&-I\\
0&E\end{pmatrix}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{W}\eqqcolon\mathcal{Y},

where
I
is the identity on
\mathcal{V}
. The functional
f:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by
f(y)=\alpha_{1}\lVert v\rVert_{2,1}+\alpha_{0}\lVert w\rVert_{2,1}
for all
y=(v,w)
. Due to the separable structure in
v
and
w
, the proximal mapping of the convex conjugate
f^{\ast}
is simply a componentwise projection in the dual
l_{2}
-
l_{\infty}
-norms in the respective spaces
\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{W}
.

We run the experiment with empirically set values of
\lambda
that make sure the variance is not strongly overdispersed while keeping the computational expense moderate – recall that as
\lambda
grows,
\tau
needs to be chosen smaller, which slows down the convergence.

10^{5}
samples are drawn in each run, we report the resulting sample mean and pixel-wise variance in
Figure

8
.

5.4
Comparison of Proximal Variants: Image Inpainting with Constraint

We want to stress that the different variants of primal-dual and proximal schemes can have an effect on the quality of samples. Depending on the chosen splitting functions
f,g
and the application, some versions could be preferred over the others. In the following, let us consider a constrained inpainting problem in which the true image has non-negative values, but a fraction of the image pixels are unobserved and the remaining observed pixels follow a Poisson distribution. Let
M\in\{0,1\}^{p\times d}
(with
p=d/2
) denote the operator that masks 50% randomly selected pixels. Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be the corresponding noisy observation
y\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(Mx^{\ast})
. The resulting negative log-likelihood is

l(x):=\sum_{i\in M}x_{i}-y_{i}\log(x_{i}),

(5.2)

where
i\in M
is shorthand for
i
being an observed pixel. We set
l(x)=\infty
if any
x_{i}\leq 0
.

This likelihood is relevant in various inverse problems connected to photonic imaging modalities, since photon observation models are typically based on a Poisson distribution, see
[
26
]
for an overview. In such modalities, the reconstruction space is often constrained to be physically plausible, for example, quantities like light intensities or absorption coefficients are usually non-negative. The prior therefore contains a non-negativity constraint which we formulate by the formal density factor
\exp(-\iota_{\geq 0}(x))

6
Conclusion and Open Problems

We have analyzed a recently proposed algorithm for sampling from log-concave, non-smooth potentials which makes use of alternating primal-dual steps as is well-known in convex non-smooth optimization. We showed that the sampling algorithm corresponds to a stochastic differential equation and formulated the mean field limit Fokker–Planck equation. Using a simple coupling argument, contraction of the continuous-time limit to a stationary solution was proved. Unlike standard overdamped Langevin diffusion, the primal-dual Fokker–Planck equation is not a gradient flow in the corresponding Wasserstein space and does not have the target distribution as its stationary solution. A proposed correction of the Fokker–Planck equation forcing the target to be its stationary solution is possible, but eventually not useful for numerical implementation. However, the target distribution can be related to the stationary solution of the uncorrected scheme in the limit of the ratio of primal and dual step sizes growing to infinity. For the unmodified scheme, we made use of coupling techniques to prove a bound on Wasserstein distance between the stationary distribution and the target for a finite step size ratio.

With similar coupling arguments in discrete time, estimates in the typical spirit of convergence analysis of the optimization algorithm can be transferred to the sampling case. This allowed us to show stability of the sampling method in Wasserstein distance, resulting in the existence of a stationary measure in discrete time under strong convexity assumptions. Under the additional assumption of Lipschitz-smoothness of one of the potentials, we can bound the discretization error using a coupling between discrete and continuous time. This final bound implies a convergence result that relates the generated samples with the target. An important open question is whether the regularity assumptions on the potentials
f,g
in the stability result and the discretization error bounds can be relaxed to not require differentiability.

Numerically, the results suggest that the additional bias due to the finite step size ratio makes the primal-dual samples consistently over-dispersed compared to primal discretizations of overdamped Langevin diffusion like the method of
[
25
]
. We currently see the primal-dual method’s use cases in two settings. If the subgradient of the dualized term is not available, but the proximal mapping of its convex conjugate is, the method is the first applicable algorithm able to draw samples from an approximation of the posterior. Secondly, if the additional bias due to a larger finite step size ratio does not matter (because a correction step is added), the method can be applied with larger step sizes than a purely primal method, allowing for faster mixing time. To find out whether this makes the primal-dual method more efficient in applications, further numerical studies are needed. The combination of a proposal step using the primal-dual algorithm and a Metropolis–Hastings correction step seems like a promising method and should be analyzed further in future work.

\displaystyle\leq 2D_{4}(t+\tau-s)^{2}+4d(s-t)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert Y_{s}-Y_{t+\tau}\rVert^{2}\right]

n=0,\dots,N-1
we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta^{N}\leq\theta^{N}\Delta^{0}+\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\theta^{n}(R_{N-n-1}^{(1)}+R_{N-n-1}^{(2)}).

Denote
T=N\tau
. Bounding the remaining inner product in
\Delta^{N}
from below as in the proof of
Theorem

4.2
, now using
\tau L^{2}=\frac{\tau\sigma L^{2}}{1+\omega_{f^{\ast}}\sigma}(\sigma^{-1}+\omega_{f^{\ast}})\leq\theta\tau\sigma L^{2}(\sigma^{-1}+\omega_{f^{\ast}})
, which holds since
\theta\geq\theta_{\min}\geq(1+\omega_{f^{\ast}}\sigma)^{-1}
, this implies

\displaystyle\lVert X^{N}-\tilde{X}_{T}\rVert_{\tau^{-1}+\omega_{g}}^{2}+(1-\theta\tau\sigma L^{2})\lVert Y^{N}-\tilde{Y}_{T}\rVert_{\sigma^{-1}+\omega_{f^{\ast}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\theta^{N}\left(\lVert X^{0}-\tilde{X}_{0}\rVert_{\tau^{-1}+\omega_{g}}^{2}+\lVert Y^{0}-\tilde{Y}_{0}\rVert_{\sigma^{-1}+\omega_{f^{\ast}}}^{2}\right)+\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\theta^{n}(R_{N-n-1}^{(1)}+R_{N-n-1}^{(2)}).

We now take expectations on both sides. The left side can be lower bounded by either
(\tau^{-1}+\omega_{g})\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu^{N},\mu_{\lambda})
or, in the joint variable, by
(\tau^{-1}+\omega_{g})(1-\theta\tau\sigma L^{2})\mathcal{T}^{2}_{1,\bar{\lambda}^{-1}}(\pi^{N},\pi_{\lambda})
. Since the initialization of the coupling was so far arbitrary (we only used that the marginal measures are
\pi^{0}
and
\pi_{\lambda}
), we can choose the one that realizes the transport distance
\mathcal{T}_{1,\bar{\lambda}^{-1}}
and obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert X^{0}-\tilde{X}_{0}\rVert_{\tau^{-1}+\omega_{g}}^{2}+\lVert Y^{0}-\tilde{Y}_{0}\rVert_{\sigma^{-1}+\omega_{f^{\ast}}}^{2}\right]=(\tau^{-1}+\omega_{g})\mathcal{T}^{2}_{1,\bar{\lambda}^{-1}}(\pi^{0},\pi_{\lambda}).

Furthermore, by
Lemma

B.3
we know that for all
n=0,\dots,N-1
it holds

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{N-n-1}^{(1)}+R_{N-n-1}^{(2)}\right]\leq\underbrace{(4L_{\nabla g}^{2}\omega_{g}^{-1}+6L^{2}(1+2\theta^{2})\omega_{f^{\ast}}^{-1})}_{\eqqcolon C_{R}}d\tau+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}).

This means we can use
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\theta^{n}\leq(1-\theta)^{-1}
and after dividing by
\tau^{-1}+\omega_{g}
we obtain the two estimates

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu^{N},\mu_{\lambda})\leq\theta^{N}\mathcal{T}^{2}_{1,\bar{\lambda}^{-1}}(\pi^{0},\pi_{\lambda})+\frac{1}{1-\theta}\left(C_{R}d\,\tau^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{3})\right)

and

\mathcal{T}_{1,\bar{\lambda}^{-1}}^{2}(\pi^{N},\pi_{\lambda})\leq\frac{\theta^{N}}{1-\theta\sigma\tau L^{2}}\mathcal{T}^{2}_{1,\bar{\lambda}^{-1}}(\pi^{0},\pi_{\lambda})+\frac{C_{R}d\,\tau^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{3})}{(1-\theta\sigma\tau L^{2})(1-\theta)},

where we used that
(\tau+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}))/(\tau^{-1}+\omega_{g})=\tau^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{3})
as
\tau\to 0
. The statement of the theorem hence follows by taking the square root and using
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

4.10
.

As in the proof of
Corollary

4.3
, we have to modify the proof of the auxiliary inequality
Lemma

B.2
. It holds
p^{n+1}\in\partial g(X^{n+1})
, and accordingly we now choose
p^{\prime}\in\partial g(\tilde{X}_{t+\tau})
. Then, we start at the symmetric Bregman distance

D_{g,\text{sym}}^{p^{n+1},p^{\prime}}(X^{n+1},\tilde{X}_{t+\tau})=\langle U^{n+1},p^{n+1}-p^{\prime}\rangle.

The subsequent computations in the proof of
Lemma

B.2
remain unchanged.

In order to bound the discretization error, the statement of
Lemma

B.3
has to be changed to consider
p^{\prime}\in\partial g(X_{t+\tau})
(instead of
p^{\prime}\in\partial g(X_{t+\tau}-\sqrt{2\tau}\xi)
). The error bound remains unchanged; the only difference in the proof appears in (
B.6
), where the term under the integral is now not
\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert X_{s}-X_{t+\tau}+\sqrt{2\tau}\xi\rVert^{2}\right]
, but
\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert X_{s}-X_{t+\tau}\rVert^{2}\right]
. Carrying out the subsequent computations in the same way, we arrive again at the upper bound
4L_{\nabla g}^{2}d\tau^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{4})
for the primal discretization error.
∎
