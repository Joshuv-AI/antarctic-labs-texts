Parameter estimation for quantum jump unraveling
Marco Radaelli, Joseph A. Smiga, Gabriel T. Landi, Felix C. Binder

Abstract
We consider the estimation of parameters encoded in the measurement record of a continuously monitored quantum system in the jump unraveling. This unraveling picture corresponds to a single-shot scenario, where information is continuously gathered. Here, it is generally difficult to assess the precision of the estimation procedure via the Fisher Information due to intricate temporal correlations and memory effects. In this paper we provide a full set of solutions to this problem. First, for multi-channel renewal processes we relate the Fisher Information to an underlying Markov chain and derive a easily computable expression for it. For non-renewal processes, we introduce a new algorithm that combines two methods: the monitoring operator method for metrology and the Gillespie algorithm which allows for efficient sampling of a stochastic form of the Fisher Information along individual quantum trajectories. We show that this stochastic Fisher Information satisfies useful properties related to estimation in the single-shot scenario. Finally, we consider the case where some information is lost in data compression/post-selection, and provide tools for computing the Fisher Information in this case. All scenarios are illustrated with instructive examples from quantum optics and condensed matter.



In many situations of physical interest, an experimenter may want to obtain the value of a physical parameter that is not accessible via direct measurement.
To do so, they must estimate the parameter based on a finite sample of outcomes. Estimation theory

Sometimes the system can be reset after each measurement, leading to a scenario that is akin to the i.i.d. case. However, this is not always the case. As a consequence, each data point is no longer independent, leading to a (possibly intricate) memory structure in the detection record.

In this work we provide a full characterization of quantum metrology for quantum jump unravelings

;
Wiseman and Milburn 2009

In between jumps the evolution is smooth and governed by a modified non-Hermitian Hamiltonian. Such quantum jump unravelings are an apt description of the common experimental case in which the measurement apparatus only records the type and time of emissions from the system, e.g. in atomic physics

The measurement record of such an experiment is generally of the form

\omega_{1:N}=\{(\tau_{1},k_{1}),(\tau_{2},k_{2}),\ldots,(\tau_{N},k_{N})\}\,,

(1)

where
k_{i}

\tau_{i}
labels the time between jumps
k_{i}
and

We assume that the system dynamics depend on some parameter
\theta

\theta

While elegant quantum bounds that maximize over all unravelings have been introduced in Refs.
Tsang et al. 2011
;
Gammelmark and Mølmer 2014
;
Godley and Guta 2023
, these are generally not saturable in the jump unraveling. In contrast, we here focus on estimators
\hat{\theta}(\omega_{1:N})

An interesting feature of the quantum jump scenario are the intricate memory patterns that different jumps retain of the past.
The qubit in Fig.
1
is an example of a renewal process, where after each jump we know with certainty which state the system jumped to.
Multi-channel renewal systems can be mapped to semi-Markov process

;
Brown et al. 2023
so that the memory is short-lived.
Many quantum systems, however, are not renewal.
A simple example is an optical cavity subject to direct photodetection: each jump perturbs the system, but does not fully reset the state.
Quite the contrary, the memory in these systems can be extremely long lived.
This makes it difficult to estimate the Fisher Information (FI).
Our goal in this paper is to provide a full set of tools for doing so.
For multi-channel renewal we show that a simple-to-use formula can be derived (Sec.
II
).
Conversely, for non-renewal processes we introduce a new algorithm based on the monitoring operator

and the quantum Gillespie method
Radaelli et al. 2023a
. This approach naturally introduces the concept of a stochastic FI which can be sampled along individual trajectories by our algorithm (Sec.
III

.

Furthermore, as a relevant specification of our work, we discuss the loss of Fisher information under data compression/post-selection (Sec.
IV

For example, what happens if one loses the information about the distinction among channels
k_{i}
in Eq. (
1

?
Our results are illustrated with several examples in Sec.

I.1
Estimation theory

Consider a string of random variables
X_{1:N}
(here and in the following, the notation for strings is left- and right-inclusive:
X_{1:N}=X_{1}X_{2}\ldots X_{N-1}X_{N}
), distributed according to a joint probability distribution
\prob_{\theta}(X_{1:N})
which depends on some unknown parameter
\theta\in\Theta
. We assume
\theta
to be a scalar; the generalization to multi-parameters is straightforward.
The goal of estimation theory is to construct an estimator
\hat{\theta}(X_{1:N})
that provides an estimate of
\theta
.
The variance of an unbiased estimator (
\mathbb{E}(\hat{\theta})=\theta
) is bounded from below by the Cramér-Rao bound
Fisher 1922
;
Cramér 1946

\var\left[\hat{\theta}(X_{1:N})\right]\geq\frac{1}{F_{1:N}(\theta)}\,,

(2)

where the Fisher Information (FI)

F_{1:N}(\theta)=\sum_{x_{1:N}}\prob_{\theta}(x_{1:N})\big[\partial_{\theta}\log\prob_{\theta}(x_{1:N})\big]^{2}\,,

(3)

represents the total information that
\prob_{\theta}(X_{1:N})
contains about
\theta
.
In the simplest scenario the
X_{i}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), causing the FI to simplify as
F_{1:N}(\theta)=NF_{1}(\theta)

Zegers 2015
.
In this case, the quantity
F_{1}(\theta)
represents the information rate — i.e., the FI acquired per symbol observed.
In the presence of correlations, in contrast, the calculation of Eq. (
3
) becomes significantly involved, owing to the high dimensionality of the sum.

I.2
The waiting time distribution

In this paper we wish to investigate the above ideas in the context of continuously measured systems in the quantum jump unraveling.
We consider a general Markovian evolution of a quantum state
\rho(t)
, as described by a quantum master equation of the form

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=\mathcal{L}\rho=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mathcal{D}[L_{k}]\rho\,,

(4)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the Liouvillian,
H
is the system Hamiltonian and
\mathcal{D}
the dissipator, defined by the relation
\mathcal{D}[L]\bullet=L\bullet L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,\bullet\}
.

\{L_{k}\}
represents a set of jump operators.
We will refer to each
k
as a
channel
.
In practical problems, each can represent a different physical entity.
For example, photons might be emitted in different frequencies or polarizations.
Conversely, in quantum dots one jump operator might represent the injection of electrons from a lead, while another represents extraction.

Equation (
4
) can be unraveled in terms of quantum trajectories described by abrupt jumps occurring at random times and in random channels (as in Eq. (
1
))
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
.
In between jumps the system evolves smoothly according to a (non-unitary) no-jump dynamics.
The jump channels
k
can thus be associated with “detectors” which record whenever a jump occurs in channel
k
.
In many cases not all channels can be monitored.
We therefore define the set of monitored channels
\mathbb{M}
.
We may also assume that each channel has a finite efficiency
\eta_{k}
of recording a detection, such that
\eta_{k}=1
represents perfect efficiency.
We define the superoperators

\mathcal{J}_{k}\rho=\eta_{k}L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}\,,

(5)

as well as the no-jump superoperator

\mathcal{L}_{0}=\mathcal{L}-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{M}}\mathcal{J}_{k}\,,

(6)

where the sum is only over the monitored set
\mathbb{M}
1
1

1

Note here that from a mathematical perspective, it is actually not necessary to use both efficiencies
\eta_{k}
and a subset
\mathbb{M}
of monitored channels.
For example, we can treat all channels as monitored and then set
\eta_{k}=0
for those channels which are actually not.
Likewise, if we prefer not to use efficiencies, we can decompose a Lindblad dissipator as
\mathcal{D}[L_{k}]=\mathcal{D}[\sqrt{\eta_{k}}L_{k}]+\mathcal{D}[\sqrt{1-\eta_{k}}L_{k}]
splitting each channel in two, with new jump operators
\sqrt{\eta_{k}}L_{k}
and
\sqrt{1-\eta_{k}}L_{k}
. Then we can include
\sqrt{\eta_{k}}L_{k}
in the subset
\mathbb{M}
and leave
\sqrt{1-\eta_{k}}L_{k}
out.
Keeping both concepts can be convenient, however, especially when making the connection with experiments.
.

The stochastic dynamics in the quantum jump unraveling can now be written as
Bouten et al. 2004
;
Landi 2023

\displaystyle d\rho=dt\mathcal{L}\rho+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{M}}

\displaystyle\Big(dN_{k}(t)-dt\tr(\mathcal{J}_{k}\rho)\Big)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\frac{\mathcal{J}_{k}\rho}{\tr(\mathcal{J}_{k}\rho)}-\rho\right)\,,

(7)

where
dN_{k}
is a Poisson increment — that is, a random variable that takes the value 1 if a jump occurs in channel
k
, and 0 otherwise. The probability of this happening is
\prob(dN_{k}(t)=1)=dt\tr[\mathcal{J}_{k}\rho]
.
Since these probabilities are infinitesimal, at each time step
dt
the probability of a jump taking place is vanishingly small.
In other words, as is intuitive, most of the time
dN_{k}=0
and the system will evolve with no jumps.

Because of this structure, it is simpler (and entirely equivalent) to consider a stochastic process where we specify the time between jumps, as well as the channel in which the jump occurred.
This is precisely the trajectory
\omega_{1:N}
in Eq. (
1
) and is, in fact, the stochastic process that an experimentalist would observe.
Thus, instead of dealing with the stochastic master equation (
7
), we can simply write down the joint probability of observing the trajectory
\omega_{1:N}
.
For concreteness, we assume that the system is initially in the state,
\rho_{0}
.
The probability of observing the measurement record Eq. (
1
) is then

\prob(\omega_{1:N})=\tr\big\{\mathcal{J}_{k_{N}}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau_{N}}\cdots\mathcal{J}_{k_{1}}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau_{1}}\rho_{0}\big\}\,.

(8)

The question at hand is then the following:
assuming that
H
and
L_{k}
can depend on an unknown parameter
\theta
, what is the precision with which we can estimate
\theta
based only on the stochastic process in Eq. (
1
)?

The answer is given by the FI, Eq. (
3
), with the stochastic string
X_{1:N}
replaced by
\omega_{1:N}
.
In general, Eq. (
8
) can exhibit complex long-range correlations: the effects of a given jump can persist, even after other jumps are measured.
As a consequence, it cannot in general be decomposed in terms of simpler probabilities.
This makes a direct evaluation of Eq. (
3
) extremely involved.
Addressing this challenge is the main contribution of this paper.
Before proceeding, however, some comments about Eq. (
8
) are in order:

•

Equation (
8
) refers only to the monitored jumps
k\in\mathbb{M}
: in between them, any number of non-monitored jumps can occur. This is automatically taken into account via the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
.

•

The probability given by Eq. (
8
) refers to a specific number of jumps
N
. This can be viewed as an ensemble where
N
is fixed, but the final time
t_{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i}
is allowed to fluctuate. We call this the
N
-ensemble. One could also consider an ensemble where
N
fluctuates and
t_{f}
is fixed. We will call this the
t_{f}
-ensemble. In the limit of large
N
(or large
t_{f}
) results for the two ensembles is expected to coincide
Garrahan and Lesanovsky 2010
.

•

Equation (
8
) is not necessarily normalized. Sub-normalization occurs in cases where there is a non-zero probability that a jump never happens. Here, we exclude this possibility by assumption. That is, we work with processes where jumps must always eventually occur (called
persistent
in the stochastic process literature
Marzen and Crutchfield 2015
). Normalization is then given by
\int d\omega_{1:N}\prob(\omega_{1:N})=1
with

\int d\omega_{1:N}=\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{N}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\tau_{1}\cdots\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\tau_{N}\,.

(9)

II
Multi-channel renewal processes

A special subclass of processes exhibiting a much-simplified memory structure are the so-called renewal processes, in which the state is reset after each jump.
This occurs whenever the jump operators satisfy the
renewal condition
,

\mathcal{J}_{k}\rho=\tr\left[\mathcal{J}_{k}\rho\right]\sigma_{k}\,,

(10)

where
\sigma_{k}
is a physical state that depends on the jump channel but not on
\rho
.
Equation (
10
) causes Eq. (
8
) to factorize as

\prob(\omega_{N})=W(\tau_{N},k_{N}|k_{N-1})\cdots W(\tau_{1},k_{1}|k_{0})\,,

(11)

where

W(\tau,k|q)=\tr\big\{\mathcal{J}_{k}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}\sigma_{q}\big\}\,,

(12)

is the waiting time distribution for the next jump to occur after time interval
\tau
and in channel
k
, given that the previous jump occurred in channel
q
.
Notice that this is normalized as
\sum_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}W(\tau,k|q)d\tau=1
for all
q
.
In terms of composite random variables

x_{i}=(\tau_{i},k_{i})
, it is clear that under the renewal condition
\omega_{1:N}
itself is a Markov chain (Markov order 1 stochastic process
James et al. 2014
).

For renewal processes in which each measurement corresponds to a single jump operator
L_{k}
acting on the state,
\mathcal{J}_{k}\rho=L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}
, satisfying the renewal condition [Eq. (
10
)], one may show that the jump operators themselves must always have the form
L_{k}=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\ket{\mu_{k}}\bra{\nu_{k}}
, where
\gamma_{k}
is a constant, and
\ket{\mu_{k}}
and
\ket{\nu_{k}}
are generic normalized quantum states which need not be orthogonal (c.f. Appendix
A
for a proof).
It then follows that the post-jump states in this renewal process are pure,
\sigma_{k}=\ket{\mu_{k}}\bra{\mu_{k}}
, while the jump probabilities are
\tr(\mathcal{J}_{k}\rho)=\gamma_{k}\bra{\nu_{k}}\rho\ket{\nu_{k}}
.
The waiting time distribution in Eq. (
12
) can therefore be simplified to

W(\tau,k|q)=\gamma_{k}\bra{\nu_{k}}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}\big(\ket{\mu_{q}}\bra{\mu_{q}}\big)\ket{\nu_{k}}\,.

(13)

If all channels are monitored, the no-jump superoperator can be written as
\mathcal{L}_{0}\rho=-i(H_{e}\rho-\rho H_{e}^{\dagger})
, with a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian

H_{e}=H-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}=H-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\ket{\nu_{k}}\bra{\nu_{k}}\,.

(14)

The WTD then simplifies further to

W(\tau,k|q)=|\Psi(\tau,k|q)|^{2}\,,

(15)

where
\Psi(\tau,k|q)
is a “waiting time amplitude,” given by

\Psi(\tau,k|q)=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\bra{\nu_{k}}e^{-iH_{e}\tau}\ket{\mu_{q}}\,.

(16)

II.1
Fisher information of multi-channel renewal processes

We now analyze how Eq. (
3
) simplifies under the factorization in Eq. (
11
).
As shown in Appendix
B
, except for a small boundary term that is negligible when
N
is large, the FI for renewal processes can be written as

F_{1:N}(\theta)=N\sum_{k,q\in\mathbb{M}}p_{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\frac{\big[\partial_{\theta}W(\tau,k|q)\big]^{2}}{W(\tau,k|q)}\,.

(17)

That is, the Fisher information of the entire measurement record reduces to the Fisher information of the WTD.
Here

p_{k}:=\frac{\tr\left[\mathcal{J}_{k}\rho_{\rm ss}\right]}{\sum_{j}\tr\left[\mathcal{J}_{j}\rho_{\rm ss}\right]}\,,

(18)

is the steady-state probability with which jumps occur in channel
k
.
Equation (
17
) is our first main result.
Notice that the scaling of the Fisher information is still linear in the number of jumps; this is expected for processes of finite Markov order
Radaelli et al. 2023b
and beyond
Riechers 2023
, and is in line with Ref.
Gammelmark and Mølmer 2014
, which studied the quantum Fisher information maximized over all possible unravelings of the master equation.

In the case of a single jump channel Eq. (
11
) will factor into a product of probabilities.
The waiting times
\tau_{i}
therefore become independent and identically distributed (the process becomes of Markov order 0). This is the scenario of renewal theory
Vacchini 2020
and Eq. (
17
) simplifies to

F_{1:N}(\theta)=N\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,\frac{\big[\partial_{\theta}W(\tau)\big]^{2}}{W(\tau)}\,,

(19)

where
W(\tau)=\tr\big\{\mathcal{J}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}\sigma\big\}
.
This agrees with a result derived in Ref.
Gammelmark and Mølmer 2014
.

The Fisher information in Eq. (
17
) encompass the information contained in both the jump channels and the jump times.
It is possible to separate the two individual contributions using the basic decomposition law
F(X,Y)=F(Y)+F(X|Y)
, that holds for the Fisher information of any joint probability distribution
Zegers 2015
;
Micadei et al. 2015
;
Radaelli et al. 2023b
. As a result, one finds

F_{1:N}(\theta)=F_{1:N}^{\rm ch}(\theta)+F_{1:N}^{\rm times|ch}(\theta)\,,

(20)

where

F_{1:N}^{\rm ch}(\theta)=N\sum_{k,q\in\mathbb{M}}p(k|q)p_{q}\big(\partial_{\theta}\ln p(k|q)\big)^{2}\,,

(21)

is the Fisher information of the sequence of jump channels only, with

p(k|q)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\penalty\ W(\tau,k|q)=-\tr\big\{\mathcal{J}_{k}\mathcal{L}_{0}^{-1}\sigma_{q}\big\}\,,

(22)

being the probability distribution that the system transitions from
q\to k
, irrespective of when it happens.
We mention in passing that the quantity
p_{k}
in Eq. (
18
) is also the solution of

\sum_{q}p(k|q)p_{q}=p_{k}\,,

(23)

as one may verify by explicit substitution.
The second term in Eq. (
20
), on the other hand, is the conditional Fisher information of the time tags, given one knows the sequence of channels:

\displaystyle F_{1:N}^{\rm times|ch}(\theta)=N\sum_{k,q\in\mathbb{M}}p(k|q)p_{q}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,\frac{\big[\partial_{\theta}W(\tau|k,q)\big]^{2}}{W(\tau|k,q)}\,,

(24)

where

W(\tau|k,q)=\frac{W(\tau,k|q)}{p(k|q)}\,,

(25)

is the probability that the jump takes a time
\tau
, given that the sequence of jumps was
q\to k
.
The quantity
F_{1:N}^{\rm times|ch}
is therefore the FI of the time tags, given that one knows the channels.
The FI of the time tags when one is ignorant about the channels will be discussed in Sec.
IV
.

The two terms in Eq. (
20
) are individually non-negative, and have clear physical interpretations. Therefore, they can be used to assess how much information is contained in each aspect of the stochastic process (channels and times).
The relative contribution of
F^{\rm ch}
to the total Fisher information is illustrated in Fig.
3
(b-c).

In passing, we mention that if
we insert Eq. (
25
) into the general probability string Eq. (
11
), we can write it as

\displaystyle\prob(\omega_{N})

\displaystyle=\Big[W(\tau_{N}|k_{N},k_{N-1})\cdots W(\tau_{1}|k_{1},k_{0})\Big]

(26)

\displaystyle\times\Big[p(k_{N}|k_{N-1})\cdots p(k_{1}|k_{0})\Big]\,.

Thus, we see that for renewal processes the path trajectory can be split into a probability for the sequence of channels
k_{0}\to k_{1}\to\cdots
times the conditional probability of the jump times
\tau_{i}
, given this sequence of channels.

The integral appearing in Eq. (
17
) can be difficult to compute analytically, depending on the shape of the WTD. The same is true for Eq. (
24
), although not for Eq. (
20
).
In Appendix
C
, we discuss the special case of a WTD that is a single decaying exponential, for which the integration can be analytically carried out; this case most frequently appears in incoherent systems described by classical master equations.
Here, we mention that if the mean and variance of the WTDs are calculable, the following lower bound applies (see Appendix
D
):

F_{1:N}(\theta)\geq F_{1:N}^{\rm ch}(\theta)+N\sum_{k,q}\frac{\left[\partial_{\theta}\mu_{kq}\right]^{2}}{\sigma_{kq}^{2}}p\left(k\middle|q\right)p_{q}\,,

(27)

where
\mu_{kq}=\mathbb{E}\left[\tau\middle|k,q\right]
and
\sigma_{kq}^{2}=\var\left[\tau\middle|k,q\right]
are the conditional mean and variance of
\tau
, calculated from Eq. (
25
).

III
Non-renewal processes

The calculation of the FI in Eq. (
3
) for a general measurement record
\omega_{1:N}
is difficult because the probability does not factorize in any simple way as it did for renewal processes in Eq. (
11
).
As a consequence, one must compute a large number of high-dimensional sums/integrals, rendering the problem generally intractable.
In this section we start by reviewing the monitoring operator formalism
Gammelmark and Mølmer 2013
, which allows one to compute the Fisher information by sampling over quantum trajectories.
We then adapt this formalism to our specific ensemble distribution given by Eq. (
8
) and show that it is possible to devise an efficient sampling method using the quantum Gillespie algorithm introduced in Ref.
Radaelli et al. 2023a
.

III.1
Monitoring operator for generic stochastic quantum mechanics

Consider a generic set of superoperators
\{\mathcal{M}_{x}\}
that describe the evolution of the system after outcome
x
. Namely, if the system is initially in the normalized state
\rho
, then

\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}=\mathcal{M}_{x}\rho

(28)

is the unnormalized state such that
\tr\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}
is the probability of measuring outcome
x
and
\rho^{\prime}=\frac{\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}}{\tr\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}}
is the corresponding normalized state. Stringing many measurements together, starting from an initial state
\rho_{0}
, one defines the unnormalized density operator

\tilde{\rho}_{N}=\mathcal{M}_{x_{N}}\cdots\mathcal{M}_{x_{1}}\rho_{0}\,.

(29)

The corresponding probability is

\prob(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\tr\{\tilde{\rho}_{N}\}.

(30)

For the case of quantum jumps,
\mathcal{M}_{x}=\mathcal{J}_{k}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}
, so the outcomes are given by the pair
x=(k,\tau)
.

The
monitoring operator
for an unknown parameter
\theta
is defined as as
Gammelmark and Mølmer 2013

\xi_{N}=\frac{\partial_{\theta}\tilde{\rho}_{N}}{\tr\{\tilde{\rho}_{N}\}}\,.

(31)

One may verify that

F_{1:N}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\big[\tr(\xi_{N})^{2}\big]\,,

(32)

is precisely the general FI in Eq. (
3
) (here the ensemble average is over Eq. (
30
)).
Hence, stochastically sampling the monitoring operator provides us a method for computing the Fisher information in the non-renewal case.
In addition, as we also discuss in Sec.
III.2
,
the monitoring operator can be used to perform maximum likelihood estimation tasks on quantum jump trajectories, solving many numerical instabilities issues with respect to a more traditional approach.

A major benefit of the monitoring operator formalism is that there is a simple, inductive process to calculate it. First, for an initial state
\rho_{0}
, the monitoring operator is initialized as

\xi_{0}=\partial_{\theta}\rho_{0}\,.

(33)

Starting from the state,
\rho_{j}
, the next observation
x_{j+1}
is sampled from

\prob(x_{j+1}|\rho_{j})=\tr\{\mathcal{M}_{x_{j+1}}\rho_{j}\}\,,

(34)

and the state evolves to
\rho_{j+1}=\frac{\mathcal{M}_{x_{j+1}}\rho_{j}}{\tr\{\mathcal{M}_{x_{j+1}}\rho_{j}\}}
. Meanwhile, the monitoring operator evolves to

\xi_{j+1}=\frac{(\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{M}_{x_{j+1}})\rho_{j}+\mathcal{M}_{x_{j+1}}\xi_{j}}{\tr\{\mathcal{M}_{x_{j+1}}\}}\,.

(35)

Note that this calculation only depends on the previous monitoring operator
\xi_{j}
, the superoperators
\{\mathcal{M}_{x}\}
, and the normalized density operator
\rho_{j}
.
This process does not depend on keeping track of the unnormalized state, which leads to better numeric stability
Albarelli et al. 2018
.

The inductive algorithm described above enables a computationally simple way to determine the evolution of the state
\rho_{j}
and monitoring operator
\xi_{j}
from their respective initial conditions. For calculating the Fisher information, this process is repeated many times to calculate
F_{1:N}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\big[\tr(\xi_{N})^{2}\big]
. In the next session, we show how to use the monitoring operator formalism to perform MLE estimation on general quantum stochastic processes.

III.2
MLE estimation

Consider a measurement record
\omega_{1:N}
, i.e. a string of outcomes
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
. The
likelihood
is the probability of obtaining
\omega_{1:N}
, as a function
\theta
, the parameter to be estimated:

\ell(\theta)=\prob\left[\omega_{1:N}|\theta\right]\,.

(36)

In MLE
Kay 1993
;
Van der Vaart 2000
the estimator for
\theta
on a specific measurement record is taken as the maximum of the
\ell(\theta)
function over all values of
\theta
:

\hat{\theta}=\argmax_{\theta}\ell(\theta)\,.

(37)

In other words, MLE assumes that the currently observed measurement record must come from the value of the parameter that makes it the most likely compared to other values of
\theta
. It is possible to prove that, under suitable regularity conditions, MLE is asymptotically unbiased and saturates the Cramér-Rao bound.

In order to be able to perform the maximization, one has to be able to compute the likelihood
\ell(\theta)
for many different values of
\theta
. When considering long evolutions (that is, most situations of practical relevance in quantum metrology, we are faced with the problem that
\ell(\theta)
decays exponentially with the length of the measurement record. This renders the estimation task numerically unfeasible. In the context of finitely correlated processes (i.e., processes with a finite Markov order), the issue can be successfully tackled by maximizing the logarithm of the likelihood function, and exploiting the factorization properties of logarithms
Radaelli et al. 2023b
. However, quantum processes in general exhibit an infinite Markov order. Here, we show a method, based on the monitoring operator formalism, that circumvents this issue.

Given the measurement record
\omega_{1:N}
, and a candidate value for the parameter
\tilde{\theta}
, we can define a corresponding monitoring operator
\xi_{\omega_{1:N}}^{\tilde{\theta}}
, that is, the monitoring operator evolved along the measurement record
\omega_{1:N}
, assuming that the value of the parameter is
\tilde{\theta}
. In an analogous way, we can evolve the sub-normalized quantum state
\tilde{\rho}_{\omega_{1:N}}^{\tilde{\theta}}
on
\omega_{1:N}
. The likelihood can then be written as

\ell\left(\tilde{\theta}\right)=\prob\left[\omega_{1:N}|\tilde{\theta}\right]=\tr\left[\tilde{\rho}_{\omega_{1:N}}^{\tilde{\theta}}\right]\,.

(38)

The maximization condition reads
\partial_{\theta}\ell(\theta)\overset{!}{=}0
, hence:

\partial_{\theta}\tr\left[\tilde{\rho}_{\omega_{1:N}}^{\tilde{\theta}}\right]\overset{!}{=}0\iff\tr\left[\xi_{\omega_{1:N}}^{\tilde{\theta}}\right]\overset{!}{=}0\,.

(39)

In this way, we mapped the problem of finding the
\tilde{\theta}
that maximizes the likelihood to the one of finding the
\tilde{\theta}
that makes the monitoring operator traceless. Remarkably, the latter is a numerically stable problem, because the evolution of the monitoring operator can be integrally computed from the normalized conditional state. For practical purposes, the problem can be further mapped onto finding the
\tilde{\theta}
that minimizes the squared trace of the monitoring operator. To summarize:

\hat{\theta}=\argmax_{\tilde{\theta}}\ell(\theta)=\argmin_{\tilde{\theta}}\left(\tr\left[\xi_{\omega{1:N}}^{\tilde{\theta}}\right]\right)^{2}\,.

(40)

We remark that the monitoring operator formalism for MLE estimation is fully general, regardless of the specific form of the
\mathcal{M}_{x}
superoperators. An application to the case of quantum jump trajectories is presented in Fig.
2
for the coupled qubit model, whose details are discussed in Sec.
V.3
. For a fixed final time
t_{f}
, MLE gives normally distributed estimates for the unknown parameter, and the variance of such distribution saturates the Cramér-Rao bound. Code for MLE estimation on quantum jump trajectories, using the monitoring operator formalism, is made available in Julia
imp 2024
.

III.3
The Fisher-Gillespie algorithm

To compute the Fisher information according to Eq. (
32
), one has to sample from the distribution of all the possible sequences of superoperators
\mathcal{M}_{x}
, weighted by the appropriate probability of the trajectory. In the case of quantum jumps,
\mathcal{M}_{x}=\mathcal{J}_{k}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}
, so the time evolutions add an additional level of complexity as the superoperators are parameterized by a continuous variable, time, that can be arbitrarily subdivided.

The method is made computationally tractable for the case of quantum jumps by employing methods of the Gillespie algorithm
Gillespie 1976
;
Gillespie 1977
. This builds off of recent developments
Radaelli et al. 2023a
by incorporating the monitoring operator. In contrast with simulating arbitrarily small time steps to determine the monitoring operator (e.g., as in Ref.
Albarelli et al. 2018
), using a Gillespie algorithm improves efficiency by leaping from one observation to the next.

For a quantum jump process, our goal is to sample Eq. (
8
).
Starting from state
\rho_{j-1}
, we sample a random waiting time
T_{j}
from the distribution

W(\tau|\rho_{j-1})=\tr\left[\mathcal{J}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}\rho_{j-1}\right]\,,

(41)

where
\mathcal{J}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{M}}\mathcal{J}_{k}
. This gives the WTD for the next jump to occur, irrespective of the monitored channel it takes place in.
We then update the state
\rho_{j-1}
to

\bar{\rho}_{j}=\frac{e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}T_{j}}\rho_{j-1}}{\tr(e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}T_{j}}\rho_{j-1})}\,.

(42)

Next we sample the jump channel
k_{j}
from the probabilities

p(k|\bar{\rho}_{j})=\frac{\tr(\mathcal{J}_{k}\bar{\rho}_{j})}{\sum_{q}\tr(\mathcal{J}_{q}\bar{\rho}_{j})}\,,

(43)

and use the outcome
k_{j}
to update the state to

\rho_{j}=\frac{\mathcal{J}_{k_{j}}\bar{\rho_{j}}}{\tr(\mathcal{J}_{k_{j}}\bar{\rho_{j}})}\,.

(44)

We now continue in this way. At each step, the time
T_{j}
is sampled from
W(\tau|\rho_{j-1})
and the next jump
k_{j}
is sampled from
p(k|\bar{\rho}_{j})
. Combining these steps, the state is updated to

\rho_{j}=\frac{\mathcal{J}_{k_{j}}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}T_{j}}\rho_{j-1}}{\tr(\mathcal{J}_{k_{j}}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}T_{j}}\rho_{j-1})}\,.

(45)

Similarly, the monitoring operator evolves as in Eq. (
35
) with
\mathcal{M}_{x_{j}}=\mathcal{J}_{k_{j}}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}T_{j}}
,

\xi_{j}=\frac{\partial_{\theta}\big(\mathcal{J}_{k_{j}}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}T_{j}}\big)\rho_{j-1}+\mathcal{J}_{k_{j}}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}T_{j}}\xi_{j-1}}{\tr(\mathcal{J}_{k_{j}}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}T_{j}}\rho_{j-1})}\,.

(46)

This equation can be read in direct comparison with the results of Ref.
Albarelli et al. 2018
. In our case, however, the evolution is written in the Gillespie formalism, rather than the time-discretized picture.

The FI is obtained by repeating this process over sufficiently many trajectories to get the average (
32
).
This task can be done efficiently if we pre-compute some relevant operators. In particular, we must be able to efficiently sample Eq. (
41
).
We therefore define a list
\{T_{\alpha}\}
of times which has to (i) be sufficiently fine-grained to be able to resolve all relevant features of
W(\tau|\rho)
and (ii) span times much larger than the average waiting times, in order to cover as faithfully as possible the structure of the WTD.
We then pre-compute the operators
e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}
for each
\tau
in
\{T_{\alpha}\}
.
An enormous advantage of this approach is that the time step in
\{T_{\alpha}\}
does not have to be infinitesimal, but only small enough to be able to capture the features of the WTD.
For each
\rho_{i}
, it is then extremely efficient to sample Eq. (
41
).
It is also efficient to apply the relevant multiplications in order to evolve Eqs. (
45
) and (
46
).
For the latter, in particular, we also need to know the superoperators
\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{k}
and
\partial_{\theta}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}
, which can be pre-computed.

If the initial state is pure, all jump operators are one-dimensional projectors, and all jump channels are monitored, then the conditional state remains a pure state for the entire duration of the dynamics. It is therefore possible to write its evolution in terms of the effective Hamiltonian
H_{e}=H-i/2\sum_{k}\mathcal{J}_{k}
, with significant savings in terms of memory and computational time.

A pseudocode description of the Gillespie-Fisher algorithm in this simpler case is given in Algorithm
1
; an implementation in Julia can be found in Ref.
imp 2024
. The latter allows both for pure state evolution and for mixed states, making also possible to consider the partial monitoring of specific channels.

At the beginning of the dynamics,
\xi_{0}=\partial_{\theta}\rho_{0}
[Eq. (
33
)]; it follows that the monitoring operator is initially traceless (hence
F_{0}(\theta)=0
), in accordance with the fact that no information is available about
\theta
at the beginning. However, the initial state may depend on
\theta
; e.g., one could be given the steady state of the system as the initial state, and the steady state ordinarily depends on the dynamical parameters. While this cannot play a role in the initial Fisher information, it may become relevant at later times, when the value of
\xi
is modified by the subsequent application of the jump and no-jump superoperators. In principle henceforth all that follows may depend upon the specific choice of the initial state
\rho_{0}
. However, in cases wherein the information about the initial state is quickly lost during the time evolution, these effects eventually become vanishingly small.

III.4
Fisher information rate

In this section, we discuss the Fisher information rate
dF/dt
of the measurement record of any quantum jump process. For this, we need to work in the
t_{f}
-ensemble representation: the measurement record
\omega_{1:N}
is equivalently represented by a sequence of discrete time steps of equal length
dt
. For each step
t
, a random variable
X_{t}
can take values in the set
\{0,1,\ldots,|\mathbb{M}|\}
, where the value 0 represents the absence of a jump in the step, and the other values index the possible jump channels.

Given the Fisher information in the measurement record up to time step
T
,
F_{\theta}(X_{1:T})
, one can update it to the next time step
T+1
via the chain rule for the Fisher information
Zegers 2015
;
Radaelli et al. 2023b

F_{\theta}(X_{1:T+1})=F_{\theta}(X_{1:T})+F_{\theta}(X_{T+1}|X_{1:T})\,,

(47)

where
F(X|Y)
represents the conditional Fisher information for
X
given
Y
. The second term gives the variation of the Fisher information
dF_{T}
. By definition of the conditional Fisher information:

\begin{split}dF_{T}=&F(X_{T+1}|X_{1:T})\\
=&\sum_{x_{1:T+1}}\frac{\prob(x_{1:T})\left(\partial_{\theta}\prob(x_{T+1}|x_{1:T})\right)^{2}}{\prob(x_{T+1|x_{1:T}})}\,.\end{split}

(48)

The update probability
\prob(x_{T+1}=j|x_{1:T})
, for
j=1,\ldots,|\mathbb{M}|
, is given by

\begin{split}\prob(x_{T+1}=j|x_{1:N})=I^{j}_{T}dt\,,\end{split}

(49)

where
I_{T}^{j}\equiv\tr\left[L_{j}\rho^{(c)}_{T}L_{j}^{\dagger}\right]
is the stochastic current in channel
j
with corresponding jump operator
L_{j}
and
\rho_{T}^{(c)}
the conditional normalized state at time
T
. Expanding the calculations (see Appendix
F
), we find the Fisher information rate to be

\frac{dF_{T}}{dt}=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathbb{M}|}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{I_{T}^{k}}\left(\partial_{\theta}I_{T}^{k}\right)^{2}\right]\,,

(50)

where all terms of order higher than
dt
have been neglected (in the limit
dt\to 0
), and the expectation value is taken with respect to all the trajectories up to time step
T
. Hence: the Fisher information rate is only a function of the individual dependence on
\theta
of the currents; the interplay among stochastic currents of different channels does not play a role at first order.

IV
Information loss and compression

The measurement record
\omega_{1:N}
in Eq. (
1
) represents all the information obtained from the quantum jump unraveling.
The Fisher information studied in the previous sections describes the best attainable precision, maximized over all estimator functions
\hat{\theta}(\omega_{1:N})
that exploit the full data record.
In practice, however, we may often not end up using the full data.
For example, we might not have access to the jump symbols
k_{i}
, but only the time tags
\tau_{i}
, or vice versa.
Or we might want to use only a summary statistic, like the average waiting time.
Discarding part of the record in this fashion constitutes a form of information compression.
Since we are throwing away information, the corresponding Fisher information must be smaller; i.e., the estimation precision will be reduced.
This can be formalized by the data-processing inequality for the Fisher information
Zegers 2015
;
Zamir 1998
;
Ferrie 2014
: given any non-parameter-dependent map
\Phi
acting on a trajectory
\omega_{1:N}
,

F_{\omega_{1:N}}(\theta)\geq F_{\Phi(\omega_{1:N})}(\theta)\,.

(51)

This naturally establishes a trade-off between estimation precision and memory compression.

In this section we explore the loss of FI for different choices of data compression.
It turns out that most expressions derived in Secs.
II
and
III
can be adapted to also encompass this possibility.
The simplest case is that of partial monitoring; i.e., when one does not have access to all jump channels, or when they operate with finite efficiency.
This case was already discussed around Eq. (
5
), and simply amounts to adding an efficiency
\eta_{k}
to each channel, or restricting the sum in Eq. (
6
) to a subset
\mathbb{M}
.
One can think of the channel
\Phi
in Eq. (
51
) as an operation that removes entries with
k_{i}\notin\mathbb{M}
from
\omega_{1:N}
.
Partial monitoring therefore modifies the main superoperators
\mathcal{J}_{k}
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}
. But the general structure of the trajectory distributions remain unchanged, and hence all formulas from Secs.
II
and
III
still apply.

Another example of data compression is when we have access only to channel symbols
k_{i}
, but not time-tags
\tau_{i}
, or vice-versa.
Starting from the general trajectory probability (
8
) and marginalizing over all time-tags yields the symbol probability

\prob(k_{1},\ldots,k_{N})=\tr\big\{M_{k_{N}}\cdots M_{k_{1}}\rho_{0}\big\},

(52)

where
M_{k}=-\mathcal{J}_{k}\mathcal{L}_{0}^{-1}
.
This statistics was recently studied in detail in Ref.
Landi 2023
.
Renewal processes continues to be renewal in this case.
Hence, the corresponding FI will be given exactly by Eq. (
21
).
For non-renewal processes, on the other hand, one can still apply the monitoring operator formalism in Eq. (
35
), which remains largely unchanged. All one has to do is replace
\mathcal{M}_{x}
with
M_{k}
.

If we instead marginalize (
8
) over the jump channels we obtain

\prob(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{N})=\tr\big\{\mathcal{J}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}\cdots\mathcal{J}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}\rho_{0}\big\},

(53)

where
\mathcal{J}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{M}}\mathcal{J}_{k}
.
Notice how this result still depends on the choice of monitored channels
\mathbb{M}
, which influences both
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}
.
Eq. (
53
) therefore assumes one does not know which specific channel clicked, but one knows it belongs to
\mathbb{M}
.
In general, Eq. (
53
) will no longer be renewal, even if the original process was.
The reason is because the renewal condition (
10
) is not, in general, satisfied for
\mathcal{J}
.
Notwithstanding, the monitoring operator formalism in Eq. (
35
) remains perfectly valid, provided one replaces
\mathcal{M}_{x}\to\mathcal{J}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}
.

The above compression schemes assume one has limit access to one aspect of the dataset or another.
Another popular compression is to consider instead only summary statistics.
Suppose the process has a single jump channel.
Instead of considering estimators
\hat{\theta}(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{N})
that are arbitrary functions of the entire dataset, one might consider estimators
\hat{\theta}(T)
that depend only on the sample mean

T=\frac{\tau_{1}+\cdots+\tau_{N}}{N}.

(54)

This is another form of data compression and therefore must necessarily lead to a smaller Fisher information.
To calculate this FI one must take into account the fact that the waiting times can be correlated with each other.
As shown in
Radaelli et al. 2023b
;
Smiga et al. 2023
the asymptotic FI, for large
N
, will be

F_{T}(\theta)\simeq N\left(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial\theta}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}C_{i}},

(55)

where
\mu=\mathbb{E}[\tau_{i}]
and
\sigma^{2}=\var[\tau_{i}]
are the mean and variance of the individual waiting times (assumed stationary), while
C_{i-j}={\rm cov}(\tau_{i},\tau_{j})
is the covariance between two waiting times.
For renewal processes the waiting times become i.i.d. and
C_{i}=0
.
In this case, the WTD will be given by Eq. (
12
):
W(\tau)=\tr\{\mathcal{J}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}\sigma\}
.
And the mean and variance in Eq. (
55
) can be computed from the Laplace transform

\tilde{W}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\penalty\ W(\tau)e^{-s\tau}=\tr\big\{\mathcal{J}(s-\mathcal{L}_{0})^{-1}\sigma\big\},

(56)

as
\mu=-\tilde{W}^{\prime}(0)
and
\sigma^{2}=\tilde{W}^{\prime\prime}(0)-\mu^{2}
.
Conversely, for non-renewal processes
C_{i}\neq 0
and the correlations will play a non-trivial role, since the second term in the denominator of Eq. (
55
) can be positive or negative, depending on the model.
This is explored in more details in Refs.
Radaelli et al. 2023b
;
Smiga et al. 2023
.

Coupled qubits

are detuning terms, and

parametrizes the coupling strength. A tensor product with the identity is implied where not otherwise specified. There is only one jump operator, given by the emission from qubit

V.4
Single-atom maser

Figure 6:
(a): Illustration of the single-atom maser model. A beam of two-level atoms passes through a cavity where each atom interacts with the electromagnetic field. The energy of the atoms is measured when it exits the cavity. The cavity itself is coupled with the environment modeled as a thermal bath.

In this example, we apply the previously discussed concepts to the study of a model of relevant physical significance, the single-atom maser or micromaser
Scully and Zubairy 1997
, depicted in Fig.
6
. While being developed in the last decades of the past century, the model is still the object of intensive study
Korkmaz and Türkpençe 2023
;
Feyisa and Jen 2023
;
Mikhalychev et al. 2022
, and remains paradigmatic in the context of collision models. Physically, it consists of an optical cavity of high quality factor, across which a beam of highly excited Rydberg atoms passes. The typical energy of the excited states of those atoms with respect to their ground state is of the order of tens of GHz, i.e., in the microwave regime.

A beam of two-level atoms, all in the same initial state
\ket{\psi}=\alpha\ket{e}+\beta\ket{g}
, fly through a cavity. The beam is sparse enough that, on average, there is at most one atom inside the cavity. The atom interacts with the electromagnetic field inside the cavity via the Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonian

H=\frac{g}{2}\left[a\sigma_{+}+a^{\dagger}\sigma_{-}\right]\,,

(66)

where
a
is the annihilation operator for the single-mode field. As above,
\sigma_{+}=\ket{e}\bra{g}
,
\sigma_{-}=\ket{g}\bra{e}
;
g
is the coupling constant. This Hamiltonian acts while the atom is inside the cavity, for time
\tau
. Upon exiting the cavity, the atom is measured in the energy basis (projective measurement with
\ket{e}\bra{e}
and
\ket{g}\bra{g}
).

The cavity itself is coupled to the environment, represented by a thermal bath; hence, photons leak from the cavity to the environment and enter the cavity from the environment.

In the analytical treatment of the micromaser model, one usually considers only the degrees of freedom of the field in the cavity, while the atomic ones are traced out. In this case, there are four possible incoherent jump channels, which we label as:

•

a_{e}
and
a_{g}
, corresponding to the atom exiting the cavity being detected respectively in the excited and the ground state;

•

p_{i}
and
p_{o}
, corresponding to a photon entering the cavity from the thermal bath or being emitted from the cavity to the thermal bath, respectively.

We can obtain the jump operators corresponding to the atomic measurements by tracing out the atomic d.o.f. from the unitary corresponding to the Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonian
Scully and Zubairy 1997
. Denoting
\mathbf{\tilde{s}}=\sqrt{a^{\dagger}a}
and
\mathbf{s}=\sqrt{a^{\dagger}a+1}
this yields:

\displaystyle L_{a_{e}}

\displaystyle=\alpha\cos\left(g\tau\mathbf{s}\right)-i\beta\sin\left(g\tau\mathbf{s}\right)\mathbf{s}^{-1}a\,,

(67a)

\displaystyle L_{a_{g}}

\displaystyle=\alpha a^{\dagger}\sin\left(g\tau\mathbf{s}\right)\mathbf{s}^{-1}+i\beta\cos\left(g\tau\mathbf{\tilde{s}}\right)\,.

(67b)

The jump operators corresponding to the absorption by and emission from the cavity are given by

\displaystyle L_{p_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma\bar{n}_{\rm th}}a^{\dagger}\,,

(68a)

\displaystyle L_{p_{o}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma(\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)}a\,,

(68b)

where
\bar{n}_{\rm th}
is the average number of photons given by the Bose-Einstein distribution for the specific temperature of the bath, and
\gamma
is the coupling constant.

This model is clearly non-renewal, as no jumps are able to reset the state to a fixed post-jump state. An example of a quantum trajectory for the model is shown in the right panel of Fig.
6
.

The increased calculation efficiency given by the Gillespie-Fisher algorithm makes studying the Fisher information of a physical system such as the single-atom maser much more accessible. In particular, we are interested in the effect of the initial quantum state of the flying atoms on the Fisher information rate for the estimation of the coupling constant
g
. Let us define
\theta=\arccos(|\alpha|)
, so that
\ket{\psi}=\cos\theta\ket{e}+\sin\theta\ket{g}
, where we set the relative phase to zero. The behavior of the Fisher information rate for
g
, as a function of
\theta
, is shown in Fig.
7
.

The Fisher information rate, as a function of
\theta
, for the single-atom maser system. For each

, the simulations were performed with

,
\tau=1

We observe that the highest Fisher information rate is obtained for
\theta\sim 0
and
\theta\sim\pi
, corresponding to the incoming atoms being almost completely in the energy eigenstate
\ket{e}
. In contrast, the Fisher information rate appears to vanish for
\theta\sim\pi/2
, corresponding to the incoming atoms being almost completely in state
\ket{g}
, meaning that, in this regime, negligible information about
g
is contained in the measurement record. The state of the field is such that there is on average much less than one photon inside the cavity:

\ket{f}\sim(1-\varepsilon)\ket{0}+\varepsilon\ket{1}\,,

(69)

with
\varepsilon\ll 1
. Applying the unitary evolution given by the Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonian in Eq. (
66
) at first order,
U=\mathbb{I}-iH\tau
, one obtains a post-interaction state

\ket{\Psi}=\left(\mathbb{I}-iH\tau\right)\ket{f}\otimes\ket{\psi}\,.

(70)

If
\ket{\psi}=\ket{g}
, then
\ket{\Psi}=(1-\varepsilon)\ket{0}\otimes\ket{g}+\varepsilon g/2\ket{0}\otimes\ket{e}
, and we observe that the term bearing the
g
-dependence (hence the Fisher information) is suppressed in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit. On the contrary, if
\ket{\psi}=\ket{e}
, then
\ket{\Psi}\sim\ket{0}\otimes\ket{e}-ig\tau/2\ket{1}\otimes\ket{g}
in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit: the
g
-dependence is not suppressed. In other words, since there are almost always zero photons in the cavity field, some meaningful interaction can be obtained only if there is a significant overlap between the state of the incoming atom and
\ket{e}
, otherwise no interaction takes place, and the atom flies out of the cavity in the very same state in which it flew in. Since the
g
-dependence is only picked up if the interaction happens, atoms starting in state
\ket{g}
will not allow for any information about
g
to be collected.

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we provided a complete framework for the estimation of parameters encoded in quantum jump processes, applicable in scenarios where an experimenter has access to jump times and multiple jump channels.
First, we showed the asymptotic behavior of the Fisher information for multi-channel renewal processes in terms of the waiting time distribution, generalizing the results presented in Ref.
Kiilerich and Mølmer 2014
, and discussing the partition of information between jump channels and jump times.
We then discussed the more intricate case of non-renewal processes. Our treatment is based on the monitoring operator formalism, first introduced in Ref.
Gammelmark and Mølmer 2013
, and computationally enhanced in Ref.
Albarelli et al. 2018
. Here, we introduced a new evolution method, the Gillespie-Fisher algorithm based on the quantum Gillespie method
Radaelli et al. 2023a
, which allows for an efficient calculation of the Fisher information on quantum trajectories in many cases of interest. As we showed, the the Fisher information rate can be decomposed in terms of the stochastic currents, elucidating how it evolves in time. We showed how the monitoring operator can be employed to perform maximum likelihood estimation on quantum jump trajectories in a numerically stable way.
To understand the the behavior of the Fisher information under classical post-processing of the measurement record we provided an analysis based on the the data-processing inequality.
Finally, we presented a number of physical examples, of different levels of physical complexity, to showcase our findings.

These results provide a comprehensive set of tools for describing metrology of quantum jumps in any platform.
We expect these tools to have have a meaningful impact for developing new experiments — such as developing novel quantum sensors — as well as theoretical work for understanding optimal methods for encoding information.

Appendix A
Form of the jump operators for renewal processes

In this appendix, we prove that, in a renewal master equation in which all the jumps are monitored, the jump operators have to have a specific form. In particular, let
L_{k}
be a jump operator satisfying the renewal condition

\frac{L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}}{\tr\left[L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}\right]}=\sigma_{k}\,,

(71)

where
\sigma_{k}
does not depend on
\rho
. Then, we prove that
L_{k}=c_{k}\ket{\mu_{k}}\bra{\nu_{k}}
, where
c_{k}=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}
.

Let us perform a singular value decomposition of the operator
L_{k}
:

L_{k}=\sum_{\alpha}c_{k,\alpha}\ket{\mu_{k,\alpha}}\bra{\nu_{k,\alpha}}\,,

(72)

where
\{c_{k,\alpha}\}
are the singular values of
L_{k}
, and
\{\ket{\mu_{k,\alpha}}\}
and
\{\ket{\nu_{k,\alpha}}\}
are, respectively, the orthonormal bases of the left-singular and right-singular vectors.

The ratio defining the renewal condition can be written as

\frac{L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}}{\tr[L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}]}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\frac{c_{k,\alpha}c_{k,\beta}^{*}\ket{\mu_{k,\alpha}}\bra{\nu_{k,\alpha}}\rho\ket{\nu_{k,\beta}}\bra{\mu_{k,\beta}}}{\sum_{a}|c_{k,a}|^{2}\bra{\nu_{k,\alpha}}\rho\ket{\nu_{k,\alpha}}}\overset{!}{=}\sigma_{k}\,.

(73)

We observe that, in order for
\sigma_{k}
not to depend on
\rho
, only one of the singular values
\{c_{k,\alpha}\}
can be non-zero. It follows that the jump operator
L_{k}
can be written in the simpler form

L_{k}=c_{k}\ket{\mu_{k}}\bra{\nu_{k}}\,.

(74)

By plugging this equation again in the definition of renewal jump, we obtain that
\sigma_{k}=\ket{\mu_{k}}\bra{\mu_{k}}
. This implies that, in a renewal process in which all jumps are monitored, the destination state of every jump has to be a pure state.

Appendix B
Fisher information for renewal processes

In this Appendix, we prove the expression for the Fisher information of renewal processes, Eq. (
17
). We first show an intermediate result on the dynamical activity of channels.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\rho
be the steady state of a GKSL master equation, such that
\mathcal{L}\rho=0
. Let
f_{k}=\tr[\mathcal{J}_{k}\rho]
be the dynamical activity of channel
k
and define

p_{k}=\frac{f_{k}}{\sum_{j}f_{j}}\,.

(75)

For renewal processes,
p_{k}
(or
f_{k}
) is the steady state of the Markov chain

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{M}}p(j|i)f_{i}=f_{j}\,,

(76)

where

p(j|i)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\tr\left[\mathcal{J}_{j}e^{\mathcal{L}_{0}\tau}\sigma_{i}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau W(\tau,j|i)

(77)

is the probability of having a jump in channel
j
after a jump in channel
i
, with no other jumps in between, regardless of how much time elapses.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the result for
f_{k}
.
We assume that
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is invertible; this condition is tantamount to the normalisability of the waiting time distribution, and means that a jump will always occur in the end with unitary probability. Then,
p(j|i)
can be rewritten as

p(j|i)=-\tr\left[\mathcal{J}_{j}\mathcal{L}_{0}^{-1}\sigma_{i}\right]\,.

(78)

By Eq. (
10
) one then has that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{M}}p(j|i)f_{i}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i\in\mathbb{M}}\tr\big\{\mathcal{J}_{j}\mathcal{L}_{0}^{-1}\sigma_{i}\big\}\tr\{\mathcal{J}_{i}\rho_{\rm ss}\}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i\in\mathbb{M}}\tr\big\{\mathcal{J}_{j}\mathcal{L}_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{J}_{i}\rho_{\rm ss}\big\}\,.

We now use the fact that
\sum_{i\in\mathbb{M}}\mathcal{J}_{i}=\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}_{0}
, and
\mathcal{L}\rho_{\rm ss}=0
. Hence, we arrive at

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{M}}p(j|i)f_{i}=\tr\{\mathcal{J}_{j}\rho_{\rm ss}\}=f_{j}
.
∎

As defined, the measurement record probability distribution
\prob(\omega_{1:N})
in Eq. (
11
) is not a stationary process.
This is because it is conditioned on an arbitrary initial channel
k_{0}
.
We can make it stationary if we modify it as

\prob(\omega_{N})=\sum_{k_{0}}W(\tau_{N},k_{N}|k_{n-1})\cdots W(\tau_{1},k_{1}|k_{0})p_{k_{0}}\,,

(79)

where
p_{k_{0}}
is given in Eq. (
75
).
To illustrate the stationarity, consider
\omega_{2}=(k_{1},\tau_{1},k_{2},\tau_{2})
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\tau_{1}P(k_{1},\tau_{1},k_{2},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\tau_{1}\sum_{k_{0}}W(\tau_{2},k_{2}|k_{1})W(\tau_{1},k_{1}|k_{0})p_{k_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{0},k_{1}}W(\tau_{2},k_{2}|k_{1})p(k_{1}|k_{0})p_{k_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1}}W(\tau_{2},k_{2}|k_{1})p_{k_{1}}=P(k_{1},\tau_{1})\,.

An important consequence of stationarity is the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
g(\tau_{i},k_{i-1},k_{i})
denote a function depending only on two consecutive jumps, and the time
\tau_{i}
between them. Then, any average over a
N
-steps trajectory simplifies to

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[g(\tau_{i},k_{i-1},k_{i})\right]&=\sum_{k_{0}\ldots k_{N}\in\mathbb{M}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau_{1}\cdots\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau_{N}\prob\left(k_{0},(\tau_{1},k_{1}),\ldots,(\tau_{N},k_{N})\right)g(\tau_{i},k_{i-1},k_{i})\\
&=\sum_{k_{i-1},k_{i}\in\mathbb{M}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau_{i}W(\tau_{i},k_{i}|k_{i-1})p_{k_{i-1}}g(\tau_{i},k_{i-1},k_{i})\,.\end{split}

(80)

We can now turn to the calculation of the Fisher information in a measurement record
\omega_{1:N}
for a renewal process. Combining Eq. (
3
) and Eq. (
79
) we get

F_{1:N}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=2}^{N}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\log W(\tau_{j},k_{j}|k_{j-1})+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\log\sum_{k_{0}}W(\tau_{1},k_{1}|k_{0})p_{k_{0}}\right]\,.

(81)

The last term stems from our modification of
\prob(\omega_{1:N})
in Eq. (
79
). Notice that this is independent of
N
, while the first term will be extensive in
N
.
The second term, therefore, leads to a negligible contribution.
As for the first term, we can use Eq. (
80
).
This will give rise to
N
identical terms, leading us exactly to the expression in Eq. (
17
).

Appendix C
Single exponential WTD and classical master equations

In general, carrying out the integral in Eq. (
17
) is difficult because the WTD is usually a sum of (possibly complex) exponentials in
\tau
.
A case that offers a significant simplification is when it is a single decaying exponential:

W(\tau,k|q)=p(k|q)b_{kq}e^{-b_{kq}\tau}\,,

(82)

for coefficients
p(k|q)>0
and
b_{kq}>0
that may both depend on
\theta
.
This, as we will discuss below, is the case of classical (Pauli) master equations describing incoherent dynamics.
The coefficient
p(k|q)
is defined to match
Eq. (
22
), so that normalization implies
\sum_{k}p(k|q)=1
for all
q
.
Carrying out the integral in Eq. (
17
) allows us to write
it in the form of Eq. (
20
), with

\displaystyle F_{1:N}^{\rm ch}(\theta)

\displaystyle=N\sum_{k,q\in\mathbb{M}}p(k|q)p_{q}\big(\partial_{\theta}\ln p(k|q)\big)^{2}\,,

(83a)

\displaystyle F_{1:N}^{\rm times|ch}(\theta)

\displaystyle=N\sum_{k,q\in\mathbb{M}}p(k|q)p_{q}\big(\partial_{\theta}\ln b_{kq}\big)^{2}\,.

(83b)

The Fisher information contained in the channels is therefore the same as in the general renewal case of Eq. (
21
), but the second contribution now simplifies considerably.

We now discuss physical scenarios in which the single-exponential assumption of Eq. (
82
) will appear.
Recall that for renewal processes
L_{k}=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\ket{\mu_{k}}\bra{\nu_{k}}
, where
\ket{\nu_{k}}
and
\ket{\mu_{k}}
are generic (normalized) kets; i.e., they in principle do not need to be elements of the same basis.
A WTD with a single exponential will occur (i)
\ket{\nu_{k}}
are orthogonal among each other and (ii) satisfy

[H,\ket{\nu_{k}}\bra{\nu_{k}}]=0
for all
k
.
The
\ket{mu_{k}}
can still be arbitrary.
This therefore means that the post-jump states
\sigma_{k}=\ket{\mu_{k}}\bra{\mu_{k}}
can have arbitrary coherences, but the pre-jump states must be diagonal in the eigenbasis of
H
.
Referring to Eq. (
16
), this implies that

\Psi(\tau,k|q)=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}e^{-iH_{kk}t}e^{-\gamma_{k}t/2}\braket{\nu_{k}|\mu_{k}}\,,

(84)

where
H_{kk}=\braket{\nu_{k}|H|\nu_{k}}
. It then follows that

W(\tau,k|q)=\gamma_{k}e^{-\gamma_{k}t}|\braket{\nu_{k}|\mu_{q}}|^{2}\,,

(85)

which has exactly the form in Eq. (
82
), with transition probability between jumps
p(k|q)=|\braket{\nu_{k}|\mu_{q}}|^{2}
and decay rate
b_{kq}=\gamma_{k}
.
Interestingly, in this case
b_{kq}
only depends on the post-jump state, so that Eq. (
25
) simplifies to

W(\tau|k,q)\equiv W(\tau|k)=\gamma_{k}e^{-\gamma_{k}t}\,.

(86)

That is, the WTD between jumps in channels
q\to k
depends only on the final jump state
k
.

In practice, the above scenario appears most often when dealing with incoherent systems described by classical (Pauli) master equations.
Let
H\ket{i}=E_{i}\ket{i}
. Pauli equations occur when the jump operators have the form

L_{ji}=\sqrt{R_{ji}}\ket{j}\bra{i}
, where
R_{ji}
is the transition rate to go from
\ket{i}\to\ket{j}
(with
i\neq j
).
Because the transitions occur between energy eigenstates, the long-time dynamics will be incoherent in the basis
\ket{i}
.
In fact, starting from the original master Eq. (
4
), one may show that the probabilities
p_{i}=\braket{i|\rho|i}
will satisfy the Pauli rate equation

\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}=\sum_{j}R_{ij}p_{j}-\Gamma_{i}p_{i},\qquad\Gamma_{i}=\sum_{j}R_{ji}\,.

(87)

In quantum master equations we label the jumps by the channels
k
appearing in the jump operators
L_{k}
.
For classical master equations the index
k
is a composite index
k\equiv i\to j
, representing the initial and final state of that jump operator,
L_{ji}=\sqrt{R_{ji}}\ket{j}\bra{i}
.
If all channels are monitored, a jump
q=n\to m
can be followed by a jump
k=i\to j
if and only if
m\equiv i
.
The corresponding WTD is readily found from Eq. (
15
) or [(
85
)], and reads

W(\tau,i\to j|n\to m)=\delta_{m,i}R_{ji}e^{-\Gamma_{i}\tau}\,.

(88)

This is clearly in the form of Eq. (
82
), so the results in Eq. (
83
) apply.
However, some bookkeeping is required to translate the “
q\to k
” notation to “
n\to m=i\to j
.”
The transition probability
p(k|q)
in Eq. (
22
) becomes

p(i\to j|n\to m)=\delta_{m,i}\frac{R_{ji}}{\Gamma_{i}}
, while
b_{kq}
is replaced with

b_{i\to j,n\to m}=\Gamma_{i}
.

We also need
p_{q}\equiv p_{n\to m}
, which is the solution of Eq. (
23
):

\sum_{n}\frac{R_{ji}}{\Gamma_{i}}p_{n\to i}=p_{i\to j}\,.

(89)

As one may verify, the solution reads

p_{n\to m}=\frac{R_{mn}p_{n}^{\rm ss}}{A}\,,

(90)

where
A=\sum_{i}\Gamma_{i}p_{i}^{\rm ss}
is the steady-state dynamical activity (number of jumps per unit time) and
p_{i}^{\rm ss}
is the steady-state of the master equation (
87
); i.e., the solution of
\sum_{j}R_{ij}p_{j}^{\rm ss}=\Gamma_{i}p_{i}^{\rm ss}
.
Physically,
p_{n\to m}
is the probability of observing pairs of states
(n,m)
in the jump sequence.
Plugging this in Eq. (
83
) then finally yields, after some simplifications (since
\Gamma_{i}=\sum_{j}R_{ji}
):

\displaystyle F_{1:N}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{A}\sum_{i,j}p_{i}^{\rm ss}\frac{(\partial_{\theta}R_{ji})^{2}}{R_{ji}}\,,

(91a)

\displaystyle F_{1:N}^{\rm ch}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{A}\sum_{i,j}R_{ji}p_{i}^{\rm ss}\big(\partial_{\theta}\ln R_{ji}/\Gamma_{i}\big)^{2}\,,

(91b)

\displaystyle F_{1:N}^{\rm times|ch}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{A}\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\rm ss}\frac{(\partial_{\theta}\Gamma_{i})^{2}}{\Gamma_{i}}\,,

(91c)

and
F_{1:N}^{\rm ch}(\theta)=F_{1:N}(\theta)-F_{1:N}^{\rm times|ch}(\theta)
.

Appendix D
Bound on Fisher information

Even when Fisher information is difficult to calculate, it can be easier to calculate some bounds on the Fisher information. For example, if the theory is described by a probability distribution of a real variable
\textrm{P}(x)dx
with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, then
F\geq\frac{(\partial_{\theta}\mu)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}
. It is often the case that the mean and variance are relatively easy to calculate. In this appendix, a similar bound is derived in the case of WTDs of renewal processes.

The bound arises from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\int d\tau\,f^{2}(\tau)p(\tau)\times\int d\tau\,g^{2}(\tau)p(\tau)\leq\left[\int d\tau\,f(\tau)g(\tau)p(\tau)\right]^{2}\,,

(92)

where
f
and
g
are any real functions, while
p
is a non-negative function. For our purposes, the integral is evaluated over
[0,\infty)
, representing the time intervals. Observe that this inequality follows from the functional inner product,
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle=\int d\tau\,f(\tau)g(\tau)p(\tau)
. Here,

\displaystyle f(\tau)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\theta}\log p(x)\,,

(93a)

\displaystyle p(\tau)

\displaystyle=W(\tau,k|q)\,.

(93b)

Evaluating the terms in the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, first,

\int d\tau\,f^{2}(\tau)p(\tau)=\int d\tau\,\frac{\left[\partial_{\theta}W(\tau,k|q)\right]^{2}}{W(\tau,k|q)}\,.

(94)

Then

\displaystyle\int d\tau\,f(\tau)g(\tau)p(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int d\tau\,g(\tau)\partial_{\theta}W(\tau,k|q)

\displaystyle=\int d\tau\partial_{\theta}\left\{g(\tau)W(\tau,k|q)\right\}-d\tau\,[\partial_{\theta}g(\tau)]W(\tau,k|q)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\theta}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]p\left(k\middle|q\right)\right\}-\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{\theta}g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]p\left(k\middle|q\right)\,.

The above expression can be simplified by demanding that the (conditional) expectation of
g(\tau)
vanish. For a function
\tilde{g}(\tau)
that does not satisfy this condition, one can shift the function:

\tilde{g}(\tau)\to g(\tau)=\tilde{g}(\tau)-\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{g}(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]\,.

(95)

So

\int d\tau\,f(\tau)g(\tau)p(\tau)=-\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{\theta}g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]p\left(k\middle|q\right)\,.

(96)

Finally,

\int d\tau\,g^{2}(\tau)p(\tau)=\var\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]p\left(k\middle|q\right)\,,

(97)

where
\mathbb{E}\left[g^{2}(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]=\var\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]
because
\mathbb{E}\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]=0
.

The resulting inequality Eq. (
94
) can be written as

\int d\tau\,\frac{\left[\partial_{\theta}W(\tau,k|q)\right]^{2}}{W(\tau,k|q)}\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{\theta}g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]^{2}}{\var\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]}p\left(k\middle|q\right)\,.

(98)

Performing a weighted sum of the above inequality over
k
and
q
with weights
p_{q}
, yields the lower bound on Fisher information Eq. (
17
),

F_{1:N}(\theta)/N\geq\sum_{k,q}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{\theta}g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]^{2}}{\var\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]}p\left(k\middle|q\right)p_{q}\,,

(99)

where
g(\tau)
is any function of
\tau
with
\mathbb{E}\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]=0
.

One may also consider the Fisher information conditioned on the knowledge of the jumps. The derivation of a lower bound follows as above in Eq. (
98
) by considering
k
to be trivial (so, e.g.,
p\left(k\middle|q\right)\to 1
) and
q\to(k,q)
,

\int d\tau\,\frac{\left[\partial_{\theta}W(\tau|k,q)\right]^{2}}{W(\tau|k,q)}\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{\theta}g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]^{2}}{\var\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]}\,.

Comparing to Eq. (
24
) and taking the weighted sum over
k
and
q
with weights
p\left(k\middle|q\right)p_{q}
yields a bound,

F_{1:N}^{\rm times|ch}(\theta)/N\geq\sum_{k,q}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{\theta}g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]^{2}}{\var\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]}p\left(k\middle|q\right)p_{q}\,.

(100)

This is precisely the same as Eq. (
99
), except that the bound is on the conditional part of the Fisher information, alone. Because the Fisher information can be decomposed as
F=F^{\rm ch}+F^{\rm times|ch}
, we can thus improve the bound in Eq. (
99
) by adding the information contained in the observed channels,

F_{1:N}(\theta)\geq F_{1:N}^{\rm ch}(\theta)+N\sum_{k,q}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{\theta}g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]^{2}}{\var\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]}p\left(k\middle|q\right)p_{q}\,,

(101)

The common form of Eq. (
100
) uses
g(\tau)=\tau-\mu_{kq}
with
\mu_{kq}=\mathbb{E}\left[\tau\middle|k,q\right]
. Similarly denoting the variance
\sigma_{kq}^{2}=\var\left[\tau\middle|k,q\right]
, a bound on Fisher information is obtained,

F_{1:N}^{\rm times|ch}(\theta)\geq N\sum_{k,q}\frac{\left[\partial_{\theta}\mu_{kq}\right]^{2}}{\sigma_{kq}^{2}}p\left(k\middle|q\right)p_{q}\,.

(102)

This bound roughly arises from considering the average delay between observations.

One may consider how the calculations change when rescaling
g(\tau)
:

\bar{g}(\tau)=h(\theta,k,q)g(\tau)\,.

(103)

It remains the case that
\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{g}(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]=h(\theta,k,q)\mathbb{E}\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]=0
.
The variance is

\var\left[\bar{g}(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]=h^{2}(\theta,k,q)\var\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]\,.

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{\theta}\bar{g}(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]

\displaystyle=h(\theta,k,q)\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{\theta}g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]+\left(\partial_{\theta}h(\theta,k,q)\right)\mathbb{E}\left[g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]

\displaystyle=h(\theta,k,q)\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{\theta}g(\tau)\middle|k,q\right]\,.

The factor of
h^{2}(\theta,k,q)
from this expectation value (after squaring) cancels the same factor from the variance. That is, rescaling
g(\tau)
does not affect the bound on Fisher information. Considering a bound based on a weighted average instead of the average, as in Eq. (
102
), will then result in the same lower bound.

Though rescaling has no effect, the bound can be improved by considering the function
g(\tau)
with higher order terms in
\tau
. For example, if one considers quadratic functions in
\tau
, a stronger bound is obtained in terms of the first four statistical moments
Bhattacharyya 1946
;
Jarrett 1984
;
Stein and Nossek 2017
.

Appendix E
Monitoring operator for multi-parameter estimation

In this appendix, we present a generalization of the monitoring operator formalism to multi-parameter estimation. The parameters to be estimated are represented by the vector
\vec{\theta}
. The Fisher information matrix
Zegers 2015
;
Ly et al. 2017
is defined as

\left[F(\vec{\theta})\right]_{ij}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\prob(X)^{2}}\partial_{\theta_{i}}\prob(X)\partial_{\theta_{j}}\prob(X)\right]\,.

(104)

This expression can be rewritten in terms of the monitoring operator, introduced in Eq. (
31
), by defining parameter-specific operators
\xi^{(i)}_{t}
,
\xi^{(j)}_{j}
, with the important property

\tr\left[\xi_{t}^{(i)}\right]=\frac{1}{\prob(X)}\partial_{\theta_{i}}\prob(X)\,.

(105)

The operators
\xi^{(i)}_{t}
evolve in the same way as the operator
\xi_{t}
, but all the derivatives have to be taken with respect to
\theta_{i}
, while the other parameters are assumed to be fixed. In this way, we finally get the expression for the Fisher information matrix

\left[F(\vec{\theta})\right]_{ij}=\mathbb{E}\left[\tr\left[\xi_{t}^{(i)}\right]\tr\left[\xi_{t}^{(j)}\right]\right]\,.

(106)

In order to obtain the Fisher information matrix, one should then separately evolve monitoring operators
\xi_{t}^{(i)}
for each component of the parameters vector, and combine them according to Eq. (
106
).

Appendix F
Fisher information rate: average

In this appendix, we show the derivation of Eq. (
50
), yielding the average Fisher information rate for a generic measurement record. The Fisher information variation is given by

dF=F(X_{T+1}|X_{1:T})=\mathbb{E}_{1:T}\left[\frac{1}{\prob(x_{T+1}|x_{1:T})}\left(\partial_{\theta}\prob(x_{T+1}|x_{1:T})\right)^{2}\right]\,,

(107)

where the expectation value is taken with respect to all the possible trajectories of
T
time steps, not including timestep
T+1
. We now introduce the stochastic currents
I_{T}^{k}
, such that
\prob(k|x_{1:t})=I_{t}^{k}dt
, for
k
labeling one of the
|\mathbb{M}|
monitored jump channels:

dF=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathbb{M}|}\frac{1}{I_{T}^{k}dt}\left(\partial_{\theta}I^{k}_{T}dt\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{1-\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathbb{M}|}I_{T}^{k}dt}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathbb{M}|}\partial_{\theta}I_{T}^{k}dt\right)^{2}\right]\,.

(108)

We note that the first term, which only contains single-current contributions, is of order
dt
in the
dt\to 0
limit. On the contrary, the second term, which contains also some non-diagonal contributions mixing currents together, is of order
dt^{2}
. To compute the rate, only terms of order
dt
should be taken into account; we obtain therefore

dF=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathbb{M}|}\frac{1}{I_{T}^{k}dt}\left(\partial_{\theta}I_{T}^{k}dt\right)^{2}\right]\,,

(109)

from which the Fisher information rate follows.
