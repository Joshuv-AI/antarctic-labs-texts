A new upper bound for the Heilbronn triangle problem
Alex Cohen, Cosmin Pohoata, Dmitrii Zakharov

Abstract
For sufficiently large n n , we show that in every configuration of n n points chosen inside the unit square there exists a triangle of area less than n − 8 / 7 − 1 / 2000 n^{-8/7-1/2000} . This improves upon a result of Komlós, Pintz and Szemerédi from 1982. Our approach establishes new connections between the Heilbronn triangle problem and various themes in incidence geometry and projection theory which are closely related to the discretized sum-product phenomenon.



1
Introduction

Given an integer
n\geqslant 3
, the Heilbronn triangle problem asks for the smallest number
\Delta=\Delta(n)
such that in every configuration of
n
points in the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
one can always find three among them which form a triangle of area at most
\Delta
. A trivial upper bound of the form
\Delta=O(1/n)
follows from the simple observation that one can always triangulate a set of
n
points in
[0,1]^{2}
and obtain at least
n-2
triangles whose interiors are disjoint and are all contained in the unit square. By the pigeonhole principle, at least one of these triangles will have area at most
\frac{1}{n-2}
. There is also a simple lower bound
\Delta=\Omega(1/n^{2})
, which follows from either a direct greedy construction, a probabilistic construction, or the following explicit algebraic construction due to Erdős (see for example
[
1
]
and
[
22
]
). For a prime
n\leqslant p\leqslant 2n
, define

X=\left\{\left(\frac{x}{p},\frac{y}{p}\right):x,y\in\left\{0,\ldots,p-1\right\},\ y=x^{2}\hskip-8.53581pt\mod p\right\}.

This is a set of size
p
inside
[0,1]^{2}
with no three collinear points and, since all the elements in the dilated set
pX
are lattice points, any triangle with vertices in
X
must have area at least
1/2p^{2}\geqslant 1/8n^{2}
.

The question is named after Hans Heilbronn, who in the late 1940’s originally conjectured that
\Delta=O\left(n^{-2}\right)
. Komlós, Pintz and Szemerédi
[
12
]
disproved this conjecture by showing the existence of a configuration of
n
points in the unit square with all triangles of area
\geqslant c(\log n)/n^{2}
, for some constant
c>0
. Nevertheless, the problem of finding strong asymptotic upper bounds for
\Delta
has remained a longstanding open problem over the years. The first nontrivial upper bound for Heilbronn’s triangle problem was obtained by Roth in 1951, who showed that
\Delta=o(1/n)
must always hold. Roth’s proof consisted of a remarkable density increment argument that in fact derived the following quantitative improvement over the trivial bound:
\Delta=O\left(n^{-1}(\log\log n)^{-1/2}\right)
. In many ways, this result can be also seen as a precursor of Roth’s celebrated theorem about the maximum size of a set in
\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
without nontrivial three-term arithmetic progressions
[
19
]
. The first quantitative improvement of Roth’s upper bound came around in 1971 from Schmidt
[
23
]
, who proved that
\Delta=O\left(n^{-1}(\log n)^{-1/2}\right)
using a much simpler argument. In the subsequent year already, however, Roth returned and introduced a novel analytic method which established the first polynomial improvement over the trivial bound, showing that
\Delta\lessapprox n^{-1-\mu}
must hold for an absolute constant
\mu>0
. In fact, Roth (see
[
20
]
, and somewhat later
[
21
]
) obtained the following explicit values:

\mu=1-\sqrt{\frac{4}{5}}\approx 0.10557\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \mu=\frac{1}{8}(9-\sqrt{65})\approx 0.11721.

Optimizing Roth’s method, in 1981 Komlós, Pintz and Szemerédi ultimately managed to improve this exponent to
\mu=1/7\approx 0.14285
. More precisely, they showed that

\Delta\leqslant\frac{\exp(c\sqrt{\log n})}{n^{8/7}}

(1.1)

must hold for some absolute constant
c>0
.

In this paper, we break this barrier for the Heilbronn triangle problem by establishing some new connections with various themes in incidence geometry and projection theory which are closely related to the discretized sum-product phenomenon. Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

For sufficiently large
n
, any set of
n
points in the unit square contains three points forming a triangle of area at most

\Delta\leqslant n^{-8/7-1/2000}.

A key ingredient in our argument is a recent theorem of Orponen, Shmerkin, and Wang from
[
16
]
, so the polynomial improvement from Theorem
1.1
can be regarded in some sense as a manifestation of the discretized sum-product theorem of Bourgain
[
4
]
.
We will start discussing our approach in §
2
, along with an account of the important early ideas of Roth and Schmidt that our new framework builds on. As an intermediate step towards Theorem
1.1
, we will also give an alternative proof of the Komlós, Pintz and Szemerédi result.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
shows that there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that
\Delta\leqslant n^{-8/7-c}
, for some explicit constant
c
arising from a system of inequalities.
We made no serious attempts to optimize the exponent
c
satisfying these inequalities, nor the system itself. It seems plausible that with a more careful analysis of the argument one can obtain a slightly better value for
c
than
1/2000
.

Our methods also allow us to give a simple proof of a further improvement in the case when
P
is a
homogeneous
set of points. We say
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
is homogeneous if there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that in every axis-parallel
n^{-1/2}\times n^{-1/2}
square
Q
contains at most
C
points from
P
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For sufficiently large
n
, any homogeneous set of
n
points in the unit square contains three points forming a triangle of area at most

\Delta\leqslant n^{-7/6+\varepsilon}

for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Notational conventions
.
We will use the notation
f\lesssim g
to denote that
f=O(g)
, that is, there exists some constant
C>0
such that
f<Cg
. We write
f\sim g
if both
f\lesssim g
and
g\lesssim f
hold. Furthermore, whenever
f
and
g
are functions of the same variable
n
we write
f\lessapprox g
when
f<e^{c\sqrt{\log n}}g
holds for some constant
c>0
which is independent of
n
, and
f\lll g
when
f
is asymptotically much smaller than
g
. We use
C
to denote a numerical constant which may change from line to line.

2
Incidence geometry setup

Let
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
be a set of
n
points and let
{P\choose 2}
denote the set of non-degenerate pairs of points in
P
. For every
w>0
and
\tau=\{x,y\}\in{P\choose 2}
, let
\mathbb{T}_{\tau}(w)
denote the strip of width
w
containing all the points at distance
<w/2
from the line
\ell_{\tau}
supporting the pair
\tau
. Whenever more convenient, we shall say that
\mathbb{T}_{\tau}(w)
is the (open) strip of width
w
generated by
\tau
. The Heilbronn triangle problem is implicitly a problem involving incidences between points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and strips generated by elements of
{P\choose 2}
. For
\Delta>0
, if
d(\tau)
denotes the length of the segment determined by
x
and
y
, the locus of points
z
such that
x,y,z
forms a triangle with area
<\Delta
is precisely the strip
\mathbb{T}_{\tau}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{d(\tau)}\right)
. In particular, if
\Delta
denotes the smallest area determined by a triangle with vertices in
P
, then the strip
\mathbb{T}_{\tau}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{d(\tau)}\right)
cannot contain any point
z
from
P\setminus\left\{x,y\right\}
, or equivalently

\mathbb{T}_{\tau}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{d(\tau)}\right)\cap P=\left\{x,y\right\}\ \ \text{holds for every}\ \ \tau\in{P\choose 2}.

(2.1)

Starting with Roth’s original approach
[
18
]
, this simple observation has been the driving force behind all the progress on quantitative bounds for Heilbronn’s triangle problem. The point is that if
\Delta
is large, then these strips can be rather large, and there is also an unexpectedly small number of points from
P
in each of them.

In
[
18
]
, Roth restricts to a subfamily
\mathcal{F}\subset{P\choose 2}
of pairs
\tau
where the corresponding segments have small lengths and the slopes of the supporting lines are similar, noticing that the strips
\left\{\mathbb{T}_{\tau}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{d(\tau)}\right):\ \tau\in\mathcal{F}\right\}
are essentially pairwise disjoint. Passing to the complement of the union of these strips, one can then find a region of the square where
P
has an increased density (relative to its area), and subsequently iterate.
Schmidt’s argument
[
23
]
, on the other hand, does not directly address this tension, but it is the first approach that implicitly considers an estimation of the number of edges in an incidence graph. Schmidt establishes the following relation between
\Delta
and the so-called Riesz
2
-energy of
P
(see for instance
[
9
]
):

\Delta\lesssim\left(\sum_{\tau\in{P\choose 2}}\frac{1}{d(\tau)^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}.

(2.2)

The idea is as follows. Let
\mathcal{S}=[0,1]^{2}
and consider again the full set of strips
L:=\left\{\mathbb{T}_{\tau}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{d(\tau)}\right):\tau\in{P\choose 2}\right\}
. For every
(x,\mathbb{T})\in\mathcal{S}\times L
where
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{T}_{\tau}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{d(\tau)}\right)
, define the weight of the corresponding edge of the incidence graph of
\mathcal{S}
and
L
by
w(\mathbb{T}):=4\Delta/d(\tau)
, the width of the strip
\mathbb{T}
. Schmidt then noticed that the (weighted) degree of
x
becomes bounded by a positive constant, which is independent of
n
. Equivalently, for every
x\in\mathcal{S}
we have

\sum_{\mathbb{T}\in L}w(\mathbb{T})1_{\mathbb{T}}(x)\lesssim 1,

(2.3)

where
1_{\mathbb{T}}
denotes the usual characteristic function of
\mathbb{T}
. This in turn implies that the total number of weighted incidences
I_{w}(\mathcal{S},L)
determined by
\mathcal{S}
and
L
satisfies

I_{w}(\mathcal{S},L)=\int_{x\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{\mathbb{T}\in L}w(\mathbb{T})1_{\mathbb{T}}(x)\mathrm{d}x\lesssim 1.

On the other hand,

\int_{x\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{\mathbb{T}\in L}w(\mathbb{T})1_{\mathbb{T}}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{x\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{\tau\in{P\choose 2}}\frac{4\Delta}{d(\tau)}1_{\mathbb{T}_{\tau}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{d(\tau)}\right)}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{\tau\in{P\choose 2}}\frac{4\Delta}{d(\tau)}\int_{x\in\mathcal{S}}1_{\mathbb{T}_{\tau}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{d(\tau)}\right)}(x)\mathrm{d}x

and

\int_{x\in\mathcal{S}}1_{\mathbb{T}_{\tau}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{d(\tau)}\right)}(x)\mathrm{d}x\gtrsim\frac{\Delta}{d(\tau)},

so (
2.2
) follows.

For any set
P
, there is a lower bound
\sum_{\tau\in{P\choose 2}}\frac{1}{d(\tau)^{2}}\gtrsim n^{2}\log n
.
There are
\sim\log n
many distance scales, and every distance scale contributes
\gtrsim n^{2}
to the Riesz energy.
It follows that

\Delta^{2}\cdot n^{2}\log n\lesssim\Delta^{2}\cdot\sum_{\tau\in{P\choose 2}}\frac{1}{d(\tau)^{2}}\lesssim 1,

and so
\Delta\lesssim n^{-1}(\log n)^{-1/2}
holds for every set of
n
points
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
. See
[
23
]
for more details. From this perspective, it becomes natural to wonder whether one can use incidence geometry in order to make progress on the Heilbronn triangle problem.

In 1972, Roth
[
20
]
(indirectly) managed to do so and provided the first polynomial improvement over the trivial upper bound by introducing an elegant analytic method to better exploit the sparsity of the strips from (
2.1
). In modern language, the idea was as follows. For every
u>n^{-1/2}
consider the pairs
\tau=\{x,y\}\in{P\choose 2}
such that
d(\tau)\leqslant u
. For each such
\tau
let
\ell_{\tau}
denote the line supporting
\tau
, let
L=\{\ell_{\tau}\,:\,d(\tau)\leqslant u\}
, and let
d(P,L)
denote the smallest nontrivial distance between a point of
P
and line of
L
. First, notice that

\Delta\leqslant u\,d(P,L)

holds for every choice of
u
.
The number of incidences between
P
and
L
at scale
w>0
is given by
\#\{(p,\ell)\in P\times L\,:\,p\in\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(w)\}
. Here
\ell=\ell_{\tau}
and
\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(w)
is the strip of width
w
generated by
\tau
.
It will be convenient to work with a smoothed out version of incidences.
In §
3.1
we will construct a certain bump function
\eta:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
satisfying

\eta|_{[-1/2+1/10,1/2-1/10]}=1\quad\text{ and }\quad\supp\eta\subset[-1/2-1/10,1/2+1/10].

We define smoothed incidences by

I(w;P,L)=\sum_{(p,\ell)\in P\times L}\eta(w^{-1}d(p,\ell)).

(2.4)

Now, if one can show that
I(w_{f};P,L)\gtrsim w_{f}|P||L|
holds for some final scale
w_{f}
, then
\Delta\lesssim uw_{f}
. For instance, one could choose
u=n^{-1/3}
,
w_{f}=n^{-3/4}
,
\Delta=n^{-13/12}
. With this in mind, Roth’s method has two main steps. Given a set of points
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
with no small area triangles:

(A)

Initial estimate.
Show that for some initial scale
w_{i}
,

I(w_{i};P,L)\gtrsim w_{i}|P||L|.

(2.5)

(B)

Inductive step.
Show that at some final scale
w_{f}\lll w_{i}
there are still lots of incidences,

\left|\frac{I(w_{i};P,L)}{w_{i}|P||L|}-\frac{I(w_{f};P,L)}{w_{f}|P||L|}\right|\lll 1.

(2.6)

Notice that for every
w>0
,
w|P||L|
is the expected number of incidences between
P
and the set of strips
\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(w)
where
\ell\in L
, if the points from
P
were distributed uniformly at random in
[0,1]^{2}
. So the first step pinpoints a scale
w_{i}
at which every set
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
that lacks small triangles exhibits (pseudo)random behavior. At the other extreme, the strips
\mathbb{T}_{\ell}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{u}\right)
each contain an unexpectedly small number of points: if
\ell=\ell_{\tau}
, then
\mathbb{T}_{\ell}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{u}\right)\subset\mathbb{T}_{\ell}\left(\frac{4\Delta}{d(\tau)}\right)
and recall that the latter strip satisfies (
2.1
).
As one varies the scale from
w_{i}
(rich strips) to
\frac{4\Delta}{u}
(empty strips) it is sensible to expect that a phase transition in behavior must happen. One can quantify this behavior analytically as follows.

Given any two strips
\mathbb{T}_{\ell_{1}}(w_{f})
and
\mathbb{T}_{\ell_{2}}(w_{i})
, where
w_{f}<w_{i}
, the area of the parallelogram of intersection (if finite) is proportional to each of
w_{f}
and
w_{i}
and that enables one to construct systems of orthogonal functions from (weighted versions of) these strips: if for every line
\ell
, we let

\phi_{\ell}(w_{i},w_{f};x)=\frac{1}{w_{i}}1_{\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(w_{i})}(x)-\frac{1}{w_{f}}1_{\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(w_{f})}(x),

then for any two lines
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in L
we have that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi_{\ell_{1}}(w_{i},w_{f};x)\phi_{\ell_{2}}(w_{i},w_{f};x)\mathrm{d}x=0

whenever it is finite, for any choice of
w_{i}
and
w_{f}
. If
\psi_{\ell}
denotes the function obtained from
\phi_{\ell}
by defining
\psi_{\ell}
to be
0
outside the square
[-2,2]^{2}
, namely
\psi_{\ell}(w_{i},w_{f};x)=1_{[-2,2]^{2}}\phi_{\ell}(w_{i},w_{f};x)
, then an appropriate application of Selberg’s inequality (a generalization of Bessel’s inequality) with respect to the system of quasi-orthogonal functions
\left\{\psi_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell\in L}
readily provides an inequality relating the number of incidences at any two different scales
w_{f}<w_{i}
. With this tool in hand, one can then start with
w_{i}
and pinpoint a much smaller scale
w_{f}
that satisfies (
2.6
)

In his original paper on this method
[
20
]
, Roth applied Selberg’s inequality only once to compare incidences at an initial scale and final scale. His later refinement from
[
21
]
exploits the fact that it is beneficial to apply the inequality several times at a sequence of intermediate scales.
. We refer to the excellent survey of Roth
[

]
more details on this perspective.

In this paper, we take a different (and morally equivalent) route, via the so-called
high-low method
introduced in 2017 by Guth, Solomon, and Wang
[
8
]
to prove upper bounds for incidences determined by balls and well-spaced tubes. This allows one to derive the following comparison between the number of (renormalized) incidence counts at two different scales:

\left|\frac{I(w_{i};P,L)}{w_{i}\,|P||L|}-\frac{I(w_{f};P,L)}{w_{f}\,|P||L|}\right|\lesssim\left(\frac{M_{P}(w_{f}\times w_{f})}{|P|}\frac{M_{L}(w_{i}\times 1)}{|L|}\,w_{f}^{-3}\right)^{1/2}.

Here
M_{P}(w_{f}\times w_{f})
denotes the maximum number of points in a
w_{f}\times w_{f}
square
Q
, and
M_{L}(w_{i}\times 1)
is the maximum number of lines whose intersection with
[0,1]^{2}
is fully contained in a
w_{i}\times 1
tube
T
. The inequality above thus roughly says: if the points and lines are not too concentrated, then the normalized number of incidences doesn’t change too much as the scale varies.

In §
3
we prove this estimate and outline how it is used for the inductive step.

In this paper we will also establish a new connection between Heilbronn’s triangle problem and projection theory, which is the main novelty in our approach. Projection theory is an area of geometric analysis that started with a fundamental paper of Marstrand
[
14
]
from 1954, who showed that if
X
is a Borel set in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then the projection of
A
onto almost every line through the origin has Hausdorff dimension
\min\left\{1,\operatorname{dim}_{H}(X)\right\}
. See for example the survey of Falconer
[
7
]
.

An elegant result that can be derived from
[
14
]
is the following estimate on the Hausdorff dimension of the set of directions determined by a set in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a nonempty Borel set with Hausdorff dimension
\dim_{H}X>1
. Then

\dim_{H}S(X)=1,

where
S(X)\subset S^{1}
denotes the set of directions spanned by
X
, i.e.
S(X):=\Bigl\{\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}\ :\ x,y\in X\Bigr\}
.

See
[
15
]
for a discussion and further references.

The first important twist in our story is the fact that a discretized version of this result can be used to prove an initial estimate of the form (
2.5
) which turns out to be similar in quality to Roth’s original estimate. We will discuss this in §§
4
-
5
. We can then use this estimate to give a new proof of the Komlós, Pintz, and Szemerédi bound
\Delta\lessapprox n^{-8/7}
.

Estimating the dimension of the direction set determined by a set of points is closely related to the problem of estimating dimensions of radial projections, an active topic recently. See for example
[
16
]
and the references therein. One of the main results of Orponen, Shmerkin, and Wang from
[
16
]
is the following remarkable refinement of Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a nonempty Borel set not contained in any line. Then

\vskip 8.53581pt\dim_{H}S(X)\geqslant\min\left\{1,\dim_{H}X\right\}.

Theorem
2.2
can also be regarded as a continuous analogue of Szőny’s theorem
[
25
]
that every set
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
of size
1<|A|\leqslant p
determines at least
\frac{|A|+3}{2}
distinct directions, provided that
A
is not contained in any affine line. This result extends a previous theorem of Rédei
[
17
]
which addressed the case when
|A|=p
, and which corresponds to Theorem
2.1
in this analogy.

Using a discretized version of Theorem
2.2
rather than of Theorem
2.1
, we will be able to access finer scales and thus manage to provide an initial estimate in the style of (
2.5
) that allows us to bypass the Komlós, Pintz, and Szemerédi threshold.

3
Roth’s analytic method: a modern take

Now we state the high-low inequality which drives the inductive step. Let
w>0
be a scale. For
T
a
w
-tube, let

L\cap T=\{\ell\in L\,:\,\ell\cap[0,1]^{2}\subset T\}

be the set of lines contained in
T
. We sometimes abbreviate
\ell\cap[0,1]^{2}\subset T
by just saying
\ell\subset T
. In general, when evaluating subset relations always intersect the sets involved with
[0,1]^{2}
first.
Define

\displaystyle M_{P}(w\times w)

\displaystyle=\max_{\text{$Q$ a $w\times w$ square}}|P\cap Q|,

\displaystyle M_{L}(w\times 1)

\displaystyle=\max_{\text{$T$ a $w$-tube}}|L\cap T|.

Furthermore, let

B(w;P,L)=\frac{I(w;P,L)}{w\,|P||L|}

denote the normalized incidence count so that if
P
and
L
are distributed uniformly then
B(w;P,L)\sim 1
. To simplify notation, we will sometimes write
B(w)
instead of
B(w;P,L)
, and similarly with other functions.

Theorem 3.1

(High-low bound, single scale)
.

We have

|B(w;P,L)-B(w/10;P,L)|\lesssim\left(\frac{M_{P}(w\times w)}{|P|}\frac{M_{L}(w\times 1)}{|L|}\,w^{-3}\right)^{1/2}.

(3.1)

We can write the error term as

\Err(w;P,L)=\left(\frac{M_{P}(w\times w)}{|P|}\frac{M_{L}(w\times 1)}{|L|}\,w^{-3}\right)^{1/2}.

(3.2)

The definition of smoothed incidences is specially designed to accommodate the factor of
10
in (
3.1
): for example, in order to replace
w/10
with
w/8
, you would have to change the smoothing function
\eta
in (
2.4
).
Now we state some variations on the bound (
3.1
). For any
K>1
, if
10^{j}\leqslant K<10^{j+1}
, then

B(w/10^{j+1})\lesssim B(w/K)\lesssim B(w/10^{j}).

Using the inequality
\Err(w/10^{j})\leqslant 10^{3j/2}\Err(w)
, we can geometrically sum to obtain

B(w)-K^{3/2}\Err(w)\lesssim B(w/K)\lesssim B(w)+K^{3/2}\Err(w).

(3.3)

To compare the number of incidences at two far apart scales
w_{f}<w_{i}
(think of
w_{i}\sim|P|^{-0.1}
and
w_{f}\sim|P|^{-2/3}
), we sum the errors at several intermediate scales to get the estimate

B(w_{f})-\mathrm{Err}\lesssim B(w_{f})\lesssim B(w_{i})+\mathrm{Err},\quad\mathrm{Err}=\log\Bigl(2+\frac{w_{i}}{w_{f}}\Bigr)\sup_{w_{f}<w<w_{i}}\Err(w).

(3.4)

In order to run the inductive step we need
\Err(w)\lll 1
for all
w_{f}<w<w_{i}
. The error is small if the point set and line set are not too concentrated. On one extreme, if the points and lines are as well spaced as possible, then
M_{P}(w\times w)\sim\max\left\{1,w^{2}|P|\right\}
and
M_{L}(w\times 1)\sim\max\left\{1,w^{2}|L|\right\}
. Usually such a bound is too much to ask for. The Frostman regularity condition is a natural way of controlling how much points and lines are concentrated, it says that for some
0<\alpha_{P},\alpha_{L}\leqslant 2
,

M_{P}(w\times w)\lesssim\max\left\{1,w^{\alpha_{P}}|P|\right\},\quad M_{L}(w\times 1)\lesssim\max\left\{1,w^{\alpha_{L}}|L|\right\}.

This condition is a discrete analogue of
P
being an
\alpha_{P}
-dimensional set and
L
being an
\alpha_{L}
-dimensional set.
In order to run the inductive step, we need
\alpha_{P}+\alpha_{L}>3
.

For small enough
w
, we expect
M_{P}(w\times w)\sim M_{L}(w\times 1)\sim 1
. In this case
\Err(w)=(|P||L|)^{-1/2}w^{-3/2}
, which is controlled if
w\gtrsim(|P||L|)^{-1/3}
. So the smallest length scale reachable by the high-low method is
w_{f}=(|P||L|)^{-1/3}
. There is an example behind this barrier.

Szemerédi-Trotter obstruction
. The sharp example for the high-low inequality is the same as the sharp example for the Szemerédi-Trotter theorem
[
24
]
. Let

\displaystyle P=\Bigl\{\Bigl(\frac{a}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{b}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)\,:\,0\leqslant a,b\leqslant n^{1/2}\Bigr\}\subset[0,1]^{2}

be the square grid. Let

\displaystyle L=\Bigr\{\ell_{p,\vec{q}}\,:\,p,q\in P,\ \|q\|_{\infty}\sim n^{-1/3}\},\quad\ell_{p,\vec{q}}\text{ is the line based at $p$ in direction $q$}.

Each line in
L
passes through
\sim n^{1/3}
points of
P
, and each point
p\in P
has
\sim n^{1/3}
lines through it, so
|P|\sim|L|=n
.
We have

\displaystyle B(w;P,L)\sim\max\left\{1,w^{-1}n^{-2/3}\right\}.

The two terms in the maximum agree when
w=n^{-2/3}
, which is the smallest distance between a line and a point off the line.
We have

M_{P}(w\times w)\sim\max\left\{1,w^{2}|P|\right\},\quad M_{L}(w\times 1)\sim\max\left\{1,w^{2}|L|\right\},\ \text{and}\ \Err(w)\sim\max\left\{w^{1/2},n^{-1}w^{-3/2}\right\}.

This means that
\Err(w)\lll 1
holds as long as
w\ggg n^{-2/3}
, and the high-low estimate (
3.1
) shows that

B(w;P,L)\sim 1\text{ for all $w\gtrsim n^{-2/3}$}

which is the sharp regime. This example agrees with our earlier claim that
(|P||L|)^{-1/3}=n^{-2/3}
is the smallest scale the high-low method can reach.

We can take the same example and perturb each line slightly so that it’s as far as possible from the point set
P
. Then each line will have distance
n^{-2/3}
from any point, and we will have

B(w;P,L)\sim\begin{cases}1&\quad w>n^{-2/3},\\
0&\quad w<n^{-2/3}.\end{cases}

Once again the high-low estimate gives a lower bound on incidences in the sharp range of scales.

Lower bounds and upper bounds for incidences
.
Roth developed his inductive step to prove lower bounds for incidences. Guth, Solomon, and Wang
[
8
]
introduced the high-low method to prove upper bounds for incidences. Theorem
3.1
unifies these two perspectives: the same inequality can be used to prove upper bounds or lower bounds. Lots of famous problems in combinatorics and analysis—for example, the unit distance problem and the Kakeya problem—have to do with upper bounds for incidences, and there is a huge amount of research on this topic. On the other hand, incidence lower bounds have not been studied much.
We hope to draw attention that these lower bounds are the key in Heilbronn’s problem, and to encourage more research on lower bounds for incidences in general.

3.1
Proof of the high-low bound

In this section we prove the high-low estimate, Theorem
3.1
.

Let
P=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
be a set of points in
[0,1]^{2}
,
L=\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}\}
a set of lines.

Let

\displaystyle f_{w}=\sum_{\ell\in L}\frac{1}{w}1_{\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(w)},\quad g=\sum_{p\in P}\delta_{p}.

Then

\langle f_{w},g\rangle=w^{-1}\,\#\{(p,\ell)\in P\times L\,:\,d(p,\ell)\leqslant w/2\}.

We are ready to give a smoothed definition of incidences.
Let
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
be a fixed radially symmetric bump function with
\int\chi=1
and
\supp\chi\subset B_{1/50}
. Let
\chi_{w}(x)=w^{-2}\chi(w^{-1}x)
. We define the smoothed version of incidences by convolving
f_{w}
with
\chi_{w}*\chi_{w/10}
,

I(w;P,L):=w\,\langle\chi_{w}*\chi_{w/10}*f_{w},g\rangle.

(3.5)

Let

\eta(t)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\chi*\chi_{1/10})(t+r,s)\,ds\,dr.

Notice that
\eta|_{[-1/2+1/10,1/2-1/10]}=1
and
\supp\eta\subset[-1/2-1/10,1/2+1/10]
.
Because
\chi
was chosen radially symmetric, we see that

I(w;P,L)=\sum_{(p,\ell)\in P\times L}\eta(w^{-1}d(p,\ell))

as we claimed in (
2.4
). In the analysis we will use (
3.5
) rather than (
2.4
).
We have

B(w)=\frac{I(w)}{w\,|P|\,|L|}=\frac{1}{|P|\,|L|}\langle\chi_{w}*\chi_{w/10}*f_{w},g\rangle,

(3.6)

hence

\displaystyle B(w)-B(w/10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|P|\,|L|}\,\langle\chi_{w}*\chi_{w/10}*f_{w}-\chi_{w/10}*\chi_{w/100}*f_{w/10},g\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|P|\,|L|}\,\langle\chi_{w/10}*(\chi_{w}*f_{w}-\chi_{w/100}*f_{w/10}),g\rangle.

We convolved
f_{w}
with
\chi_{w}*\chi_{w/10}
in (
3.5
) rather than just
\chi_{w}
in order to factor out
\chi_{w/10}
in this equation. Putting the convolution on the other side and using Cauchy-Schwarz,

|B(w)-B(w/10)|\leqslant\frac{1}{|P|\,|L|}\,\|\chi_{w}*f_{w}-\chi_{w/100}*f_{w/10}\|_{L^{2}([-2,2]^{2})}\,\|\chi_{w/10}*g\|_{L^{2}([-2,2]^{2})}.

(3.7)

We estimate the term involving
g
using the quantity
M_{P}(w\times w)
,

\displaystyle\|\chi_{w/10}*g\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\|g\|_{1}\|\chi_{w/10}*g\|_{\infty}\lesssim w^{-2}|P|\,M_{P}(w\times w).

We estimate the term involving
f
using orthogonality and the quantity
M_{L}(w\times 1)
.
Let

\displaystyle\Phi_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\chi_{w}*w^{-1}1_{\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(w)}-\chi_{w/100}*(w/10)^{-1}1_{\mathbb{T}_{\ell}(w/10)}

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in L}\Phi_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\chi_{w}*f_{w}-\chi_{w/100}*f_{w/10}.

We expand the
L^{2}
norm as a sum of truncated inner products. Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,1]
be a fixed smooth bump function with
\psi|_{[-2,2]^{2}}=1
and
\supp\psi\subset[-3,3]^{2}
. We have

\Bigl\|\sum_{\ell\in L}\Phi_{\ell}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}([-2,2]^{2})}^{2}\leqslant\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\left|\int\Phi_{\ell_{1}}(x)\Phi_{\ell_{2}}(x)\,\psi(x)dx\right|

If
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are not parallel, then
\langle\Phi_{\ell_{1}},\Phi_{\ell_{2}}\rangle=0
where the inner product is taken in all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Here is an approximate version of this orthogonality. Let
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
be two lines. Say
\ell_{1}\sim\ell_{2}
if

\mathbb{T}_{2w}(\ell_{1})\cap\mathbb{T}_{2w}(\ell_{2})\cap[-3,3]^{2}\neq\emptyset.

If
\ell_{1}\not\sim\ell_{2}
then the truncated inner product vanishes.
Let
\ell_{1}\sim\ell_{2}
make an angle
0<\theta\leqslant\pi/2
.
Here is an easy estimate for the truncated inner product:

\left|\int\Phi_{\ell_{1}}(x)\Phi_{\ell_{2}}(x)\,\psi(x)dx\right|\leqslant\|\Phi_{\ell_{1}}\|_{L^{2}([-3,3]^{2})}\|\Phi_{\ell_{2}}\|_{L^{2}([-3,3]^{2})}\lesssim w^{-1}.

This is good enough when
\theta\leqslant w
, but we need an improvement using orthogonality when
\theta
is large. Let
R=\mathbb{T}_{2w}(\ell_{1})\cap\mathbb{T}_{2w}(\ell_{2})
, and let
x_{R}=\ell_{1}\cap\ell_{2}
be the center of
R
. Notice that
R
is a parallelogram which is contained in a rectangle with width
w
and length
2w/\sin\theta
.
Using the fact that
\Phi_{\ell_{1}}
and
\Phi_{\ell_{2}}
are orthogonal and re-centering to
R
,

\int\Phi_{\ell_{1}}(x)\Phi_{\ell_{2}}(x)\,\psi(x)dx=\int(\Phi_{\ell_{1}}\Phi_{\ell_{2}})(x_{R}+y)\,(\psi(x_{R}+y)-\psi(x_{R}))\,dy.

Because
\psi
is a fixed smooth function, we have the linear approximation

\psi(x_{R}+y)-\psi(x_{R})=\nabla\psi(x_{R})\cdot y+O(|y|^{2}).

The integral against the linear part vanishes due to the symmetry

\Phi_{\ell_{1}}(x_{R}+y)=\Phi_{\ell_{1}}(x_{R}-y)\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{\ell_{2}}(x_{R}+y)=\Phi_{\ell_{2}}(x_{R}-y).

The
O(|y|^{2})
term is bounded by
C\cdot\mathrm{Diameter}(R)^{2}
, so

\left|\int\Phi_{\ell_{1}}(x)\Phi_{\ell_{2}}(x)\,\psi(x)dx\right|\lesssim\Area(R)\,\mathrm{Diameter}(R)^{2}\,w^{-2}\lesssim(w^{2}\theta^{-1})\,(w\theta^{-1})^{2}\,w^{-2}=w^{2}\theta^{-3}.

Next, we have the estimate

\#\{\ell_{1}\sim\ell_{2}\,:\,\theta(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\in[\theta_{0},\theta_{0}+w/100]\}\lesssim M_{L}(w\times 1)\,\max(1,\theta_{0}/w)

because any line
\ell_{2}
in the set on the left has to be contained in one of
\lesssim\max(1,\theta_{0}/w)
many
w
tubes. Combining these estimates, we see that for fixed
\ell_{1}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell_{2}\in L}\left|\int\Phi_{\ell_{1}}(x)\Phi_{\ell_{2}}(x)\,\psi(x)dx\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{200w^{-1}}w^{2}(jw)^{-3}\,\#\{\ell_{1}\sim\ell_{2}\,:\,\theta(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\in[(j-1)w/100,jw/100]\}

\displaystyle\lesssim w^{-1}M_{L}(w\times 1)\,\sum_{j=1}^{200w^{-1}}j^{-2}\lesssim w^{-1}M_{L}(w\times 1).

Putting everything together,

|B(w)-B(w/10)|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{M_{P}(w\times w)}{|P|}\frac{M_{L}(w\times 1)}{|L|}\,w^{-3}}.

4
Initial estimates via projection theory

In this section, we describe our new projection theory approach to initial estimates of the form (
2.5
). This approach is quite different from Roth’s original argument from
[
20
]
(and the subsequent papers that used it) and is the main novelty of our paper. For a fixed scale
u>n^{-1/2}
, consider the pairs
\tau=\{x,y\}\in{P\choose 2}
such that
d(\tau)\leqslant u
. For each such
\tau
, let
\ell_{\tau}
denote the line supporting
\tau
. Partition
[0,1]^{2}
into a grid of
u\times u
squares. Within each of these squares
Q
, consider the set of points
P\cap Q
, and define the set of lines

L_{Q}=\left\{\ell_{\tau}\,:\,\tau\in{P\cap Q\choose 2}\right\}.

Then we make the line set
L=\bigcup_{Q}L_{Q}
. In order to prove an initial estimate of the form (
2.5
) for
P
and
L
at the scale
w_{i}
, we must show that for most of the lines
\ell\in L
,

\#\{p\in\mathbb{T}_{w_{i}}(\ell)\}\gtrsim w_{i}|P|.

(4.1)

Let us work one square
Q
at a time. The initial scale
w_{i}
will be much larger than
u
, so the tubes
\mathbb{T}_{w_{i}}(\ell)
will contain the square
Q
. Note also that the tube
\mathbb{T}_{w_{i}}(\ell)
is essentially determined by
\theta(\ell)
up to an angular resolution of
\sim w_{i}
. Say that a direction
\theta\in\left\{w_{i}/100,2w_{i}/100,\ldots,1\right\}
is
good
if the
w_{i}\times 1
tube in direction
\theta
containing
Q
has
\gtrsim w_{i}|P|
points of
P
, otherwise say
\theta
is bad. Using a double counting argument, we can ensure that for most of the squares
Q
in the partition there are not-too-many bad directions. The main challenge is to show that the set of directions spanned by the lines in
L_{Q}
is not concentrated in the small number of bad directions.

Figure 1
:
A set of lines
L_{Q}
spanned by points inside a box
Q
. On the left, the directions
\theta(\ell)
are concentrated in a few tubes
\mathbb{T}_{i}
and we cannot prove an initial estimate. On the right, the directions are spread out and many lines avoid ‘bad’ tubes.

A crucial observation is that if
P\cap Q
satisfies an
s
-dimensional Frostman condition for some
s>1
then a discretized version of Theorem
2.1
implies that the direction set is well spread out.
Before we state the Theorem, here is an important definition.

Definition
. Let
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
be a set of points. We say
P
is
s
-regular at all scales with constant
C
if for all
w\times w
squares
Q
, the following inequality holds:

|P\cap Q|\leqslant C\max\left\{1,|P|w^{s}\right\}.

We also say
P
is
s
-regular above scale
\delta
with constant
C
if

|P\cap Q|\leqslant C|P|w^{s}\quad\text{ for all $w>\delta$}.

(4.2)

A variant of these definitions goes back to the work of Katz and Tao
[
10
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
s>1
,
\sigma<1
, and
C>0
. There exists
K=K(C,s,\sigma)
such that the following holds. Let
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
be
s
-regular above scale
\delta
with constant
C
. Then there is a set of lines

L\subset\{\ell_{x,y}\,:\,x,y\in P\}

formed from pairs in
P
such that
|L|\geqslant K^{-1}|P|^{2}
and

\#\{\ell\in L\,:\,\theta(\ell)\in I\}\leqslant K|L|\,|I|^{\sigma},\quad\text{for all intervals $I\subset S^{1}$ with $|I|\geqslant\delta$}.

In order to use Theorem
4.1
we need to choose the set of squares more carefully: instead of starting with a partition of
[0,1]^{2}
into
u\times u
squares we will greedily construct a collection
\{Q\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
of pairwise disjoint squares such that each
P\cap Q
is
s
-dimensional for some fixed
s>1
.
Each set of lines
L_{Q}
thus spans a well spread out set of directions, which ensures (
4.1
) holds for most
\ell\in L
. It follows that
B(w;P,L)\gtrapprox 1
for some initial scale
w
and a set of lines
L
spanned by pairs of points from
P
at distance at most
u
. Here the distance
u
is the most popular side length among the squares from
\mathcal{Q}
.
Because of the way we construct
\mathcal{Q}
, we do not get to choose a specific value of
u
but have to work with an arbitrary
u
in a certain range.

By combining the lower bound
B(w;P,L)\gtrapprox 1
with the high-low method we can recover the classical bound
\Delta(n)\lessapprox n^{-8/7}
of Komlós–Pintz–Szemerédi. We present full details of this argument in Section
6.3
as a warm-up before the proof of the improved bound.

To improve on the
8/7
exponent we use the following discretized version of Orponen, Shmerkin, and Wang’s Theorem
2.2
.

Theorem 4.2
.

For all
0<s<1
,
\sigma<s
, and
C,w>0
there exists
\tau=\tau(\sigma,s)>0
and
K=K(C,w,\varepsilon,s,\sigma)<0
such that the following holds.
Let
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
be
s
-regular above scale
\delta
with constant
C
. Suppose

|P\cap T|\leqslant\tau|P|

(4.3)

for all
w
-tubes
T
. There is a set of lines

L\subset\{\ell_{x,y}\,:\,x,y\in P\}

formed from pairs in
P
such that
|L|\geqslant K^{-1}|P|^{2}
and

\#\{\ell\in L\,:\,\theta(\ell)\in I\}\leqslant K|L|\,|I|^{\sigma},\quad\text{$I\subset S^{1}$ an interval, $|I|\geqslant\delta$}.

Both Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
follow from
[
16
, Corollary 2.18]
by relatively standard arguments. We sketch this deduction in Appendix
A
.

By using Theorem
4.2
in place of Theorem
4.1
, we get an initial estimate for the line set
L_{Q}
even if
P\cap Q
is less than one dimensional. In the end we use this edge to improve the overall bounds, but there are significant technicalities.

For one, the condition (
4.3
) adds a complication: it is possible that the intersection
P\cap Q
is essentially contained in a tube
T
of width
w<u
. In this case the direction of lines in
L_{Q}
is concentrated near
T
, so we get a weaker initial estimate. On the one hand, if
w
is fairly large, the initial estimate is still good enough to run our argument. On the other hand, if
w
is very small then we can find a triangle of a small area inside the set
P\cap Q
alone.
Unfortunately, the ‘fairly large’ and ‘very small’ regimes for
w
do not always overlap. It turns out that if the initial distance scale
u
is below a certain threshold
u_{1}
then the conditions on
w
do overlap and we get an improved bound.
To deal with this technicality we split into two cases, the
square case
and the
rectangle case
. We greedily pick a collection of squares
\mathcal{Q}
with sides
\geqslant u_{1}
and a collection of rectangles
\mathcal{R}
with larger side
\leqslant u_{1}
such that: for
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
the set
P\cap Q
is
s_{1}
-dimensional for some fixed
s_{1}>1
and for
R\in\mathcal{R}
the set
P\cap R
is
s_{2}
-dimensional for some fixed
0<s_{2}<1
.

Statement of results

Here is a basic lower bound on the number of incidences.

Remark 5.2
.

We emphasize an important difference between using the high-low method to prove upper bounds vs. lower bounds. An upper bound looks like:
B(w_{f})\lesssim B(w_{i})+\mathrm{\Err}

B(w_{f})\gtrsim B(w_{i})-\mathrm{\Err}

\mathrm{\Err}\lll B(w_{i})

.

The following lemma will be used to deduce the improved bound for Heilbronn’s triangle problem. Given a lower bound on the number of incidences between the set of points

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle\geqslant 2\beta+\frac{2}{s_{2}}(1-\alpha-\beta),

\displaystyle 5\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle\geqslant 5-3\alpha+4\beta+2\ell(1-\alpha-\beta),

\displaystyle 6\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle\geqslant 7-4\alpha+3\beta+3\ell(1-\alpha-\beta).

where we can choose any
\alpha,\beta
such that:

0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant\beta,~~\alpha+\beta\leqslant 1,~~4/9\leqslant\beta\leqslant\gamma_{1}/2

and
s_{2}
such that
0.5<s_{2}<1
and
s_{2}\leqslant\frac{3}{2+\gamma_{0}}
.

Let
\gamma_{0}=8/7
,
s_{2}=\frac{3}{2+\gamma_{0}}=\frac{21}{22}
(so that
\ell=\frac{2}{21}
). For some
0\leqslant a\leqslant b
, let
\alpha=\frac{3}{7}+a
and
\beta=\frac{4}{7}-b
(so that
1-\alpha-\beta=b-a
). Plugging these values into the system above gives:

\displaystyle 3\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle\geqslant 3\cdot\frac{8}{7}+a,

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{8}{7}-2a+\frac{2}{21}(b-a),

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{8}{7}-2b+\frac{44}{21}(b-a),

\displaystyle 5\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle\geqslant 6-3a-4b+\frac{4}{21}(b-a),

\displaystyle 6\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle\geqslant 7-4a-3b+\frac{2}{7}(b-a).

Observe that each of the first 3 inequalities above implies
\gamma_{1}\geqslant 8/7+c
if we take
a>0
and
b
significantly larger than
a
.
On the other hand, the last two inequalities both imply a bound exceeding
8/7
provided that both
a
and
b
are sufficiently small. So by choosing
1\ggg b\ggg a>0
we get a small improvement of the exponent.

Specifically, let us take
a=0.0016
,
b=0.047
. One can check that each of the inequalities above implies that
\gamma_{1}\geqslant\frac{8}{7}+0.0005+c
for some
c>0
. Taking
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime\prime}<c
and all other epsilons correspondingly then implies the bound
\Delta<n^{-8/7-1/2000}
.
This completes the proof.

\Box

6.5
A further improved bound for homogeneous sets

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
. We recall that a homogeneous set
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
is a well-spaced set, in the sense that there exists a constant
C>0
such that in every axis-parallel
n^{-1/2}\times n^{-1/2}
square
Q
contains at most
C
points from
P
. We would like to show that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist three points forming a triangle of area at most

\Delta\leqslant n^{-7/6+\varepsilon}.

The proof will follow our approach from the previous sections, but we will now be able to use a much simpler network of pairwise disjoint squares, with easier access to the well-separated lines needed for the application of Proposition
5.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
be as in the statement. Note that for
w\geqslant n^{-1/2}
, any
w\times w
square
Q\subset[0,1]^{2}
can be covered by
\leqslant 4nw^{2}
squares with side
n^{-1/2}
, so by assumption and a simple union bound we get that

|P\cap Q|\leqslant 4Cnw^{2}.

(6.25)

Let
u=n^{-1/2+2\varepsilon}
and split the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
into
u\times u
squares
Q_{j}
. Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the set of squares
Q_{j}
such that
|P\cap Q_{j}|\geqslant u^{2}n/2
. Note that the squares from
\mathcal{Q}
cover at least
n/2
points of
P
. In particular, by (
6.25
) we have

u^{-2}\gtrsim\frac{n}{nu^{2}/2}\geqslant|\mathcal{Q}|\geqslant\frac{n/2}{4Cnu^{2}}\gtrsim u^{-2},

i.e.
|\mathcal{Q}|\sim u^{-2}
.

For each
Q_{j}\in\mathcal{Q}
, let
P_{Q_{j}}=P\cap Q_{j}
. By (
6.25
), note that the set
P_{Q_{j}}
is
2
-regular relative to
Q_{j}
at all scales with constant
8C
. Indeed, for any
w\times w
square
Q
, we have that

|P_{Q_{j}}\cap Q|\leqslant 4Cnw^{2}\leqslant 8\cdot\frac{nu^{2}}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{w}{u}\right)^{2}\leqslant 8C|P\cap Q_{j}|\left(\frac{w}{u}\right)^{2}.

By Theorem
4.1
applied to
P_{Q_{j}}
with
s=2
,
\sigma=1-\varepsilon
and
C=8C
, there is a set of lines
L_{j}
spanned by pairs of points in
P_{Q_{j}}
such that
|L_{j}|\geqslant|P_{Q_{j}}|^{2}/K
and the set of directions
\theta(L_{j})\subset S^{1}
is
\sigma
-regular all scales above
\delta\sim|P_{Q_{j}}|^{-1/2}
with constant
K=K(2,\sigma,8C)
. In particular, every interval
I\subset S^{1}
of length
\delta
contains at most
K\delta^{\sigma}|L_{j}|
directions from
\theta(L_{j})
.

For each
Q_{j}\in\mathcal{Q}
let
p_{j}
be the middle point of the square
Q_{j}
and note that all lines in
L_{j}
are at distance at most
10u
to
p_{j}
.
Let
w=10u
, and define
\Pi=\bigcup_{j:Q_{j}\in\mathcal{Q}}\{p_{j}\}
. Also, let
L=\bigcup_{j:Q_{j}\in\mathcal{Q}}L_{j}
.

By design, note that every
w
-square contains at most
100
points of
\Pi
, so take
\mu_{\Pi}=100/|\Pi|\sim u^{2}
,
\eta=\delta
, and
\mu_{L}=K\delta^{\sigma}
. We have

\mu_{\Pi}\mu_{L}^{2}\eta^{-1}\sim u^{2}\delta^{2\sigma-1}\lesssim u^{2}\delta^{1/2}\leqslant w^{2}\delta^{1/2}.

Since
\delta\sim|P_{Q_{j}}|^{-1/2}
is upper bounded by a small negative power of
n
, this implies that Proposition
5.1
is applicable to the set of points
\Pi
, the set of lines
L
, and scale
w
. Thus, we get
B(w;\Pi,L)\gtrsim n^{-\varepsilon}\eta\mu_{L}^{-1}\gtrsim n^{-2\varepsilon}
. Since every square
Q_{j}\in\mathcal{Q}
contains
\geqslant u^{2}n/2\sim\frac{|P|}{|\Pi|}
points of
P
, we get

B(10w;P,L)\gtrsim B(w;\Pi,L)\gtrsim n^{-2\varepsilon}.

Apply Lemma
6.1
to the set
P
, the set of lines
L
and
w=10w=100u
,
\kappa=n^{-2\varepsilon}
. Then the smallest area
\Delta
of a triangle in
P
satisfies:

\Delta/|\log\Delta|\lesssim u|P|^{-1/3}|L|^{-1/3}\kappa^{-2/3}+u|P|^{-1}\kappa^{-1},

we have
|P|=n
,
u=n^{-1/2+2\varepsilon}
and
|L|\gtrsim n^{2}u^{2}\sim n^{1+4\varepsilon}
, so after simplifications we get
\Delta\lesssim n^{-7/6+5\varepsilon}
, as desired.

\Box

Note that
u\approx n^{-1/2}
is the smallest scale at which we can ensure pairs of points of distance
\leqslant u
. If
L
denotes the set of lines spanned by the pairs of points in

7
Final remarks

In this paper we showed that in every set of
n
points chosen inside a unit square there exists a triangle of area less than
n^{-8/7-1/2000}
, thereby improving upon the classical result of Komlós, Pintz and Szemerédi from
[
11
]
by a polynomial factor. We also gave a further improved bound of
n^{-7/6}
, in the case when
P
is homogeneous. Perhaps most importantly, our approach established new connections between the Heilbronn triangle and various themes in incidence geometry and projection theory that are closely related to the discretized sum-product theorem of Bourgain
[
4
]
.

Apart from the problem of further improving the bounds for the Heilbronn triangle problem, there are several other related open questions which unfortunately are a bit less known. We would like to use this section as an opportunity to draw attention towards a few of our favorites. We hope that the ideas introduced in this paper will also lead to progress on some of these.

The first one is a problem due to Motzkin and Schmidt (cf.
[
5
]
and
[
3
]
). For every set of points
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
, let
\Phi(P)
denote the smallest real number
w\geqslant 0
for which there is a strip of width
w
containing at least three points from
P
. Let

\Phi(n):=\max\Phi(P),

where the maximum is taken over all sets of
n
points
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
.

Conjecture 1
.

\Phi(n)=o(1/n).

A simple pigeonholing argument shows that
\Phi(n)\leqslant 3/n
. Using an analytic technique in the style of Roth’s method discussed in §
2
, Beck
[
3
]
proved this conjecture in the
special case when the set is uniformly distributed in the sense that it contains
precisely one point from each
n^{-1/2}\times n^{-1/2}
square (note that this is a stronger condition than being homogeneous). No nontrivial upper bound is known in general.

It is worth emphasizing that Conjecture
2
is directly related to the Heilbronn triangle problem. Clearly,

\Delta(n)\leqslant\Phi(n),

but the inequality goes only one way: a triangle of area
o(1/n)
is not necessarily be contained in a strip of width
o(1/n)
.

The next problem is a natural generalization of the Heilbronn triangle problem, which was also proposed by Schmidt in
[
23
]
. Let
\Delta_{k}(n)
be the smallest number
\Delta=\Delta(n)
such that in every configuration of
n
points in the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
one can always find
k
points among them whose convex hull has area at most
\Delta_{k}(n)
.

Conjecture 2
.

For every
k\geqslant 3
,

\Delta_{k}(n)=o(1/n).

Clearly,
\Delta(n)=\Delta_{3}(n)\leqslant\Delta_{4}(n)\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\Delta_{k}(n)
holds for al
k\geqslant 3
, so Conjecture
2
holds for
k=3
. However, rather surprisingly, the question remains open for every
k\geqslant 4
. The best known lower bound is due to Leffman
[
13
]
and comes from a generalization of the semi-random construction from
[
11
]
: for every
k\geqslant 3
,

\Delta_{k}(n)\gtrsim\frac{(\log n)^{\frac{1}{k-2}}}{n^{1+\frac{1}{k-2}}}.

(7.1)

In the same spirit, one can also talk about an analogous generalization of the Motzkin-Schmidt problem, namely the question for strips that contain at least
k
points from
P
, where
k\geqslant 3
is fixed integer. Like before, let
\Phi_{k}(P)
be the smallest
w\geqslant 0
for which there is a strip of width
w
containing at least
k
points from
P
, and define
\Phi_{k}(n):=\max\Phi_{k}(P)
, where the maximum is taken again over all configurations of
n
points
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
. Naturally, we have that
\Delta_{k}(n)\leqslant\Phi_{k}(n)
for all
k\geqslant 3
, which already due to (
7.1
) suggests a rather interesting behavior for
\Phi_{k}(n)
when
k
becomes large.

We would like to end with a question of Bourgain in the opposite direction.

Question 3
.

Is it possible to find a set of
n
points
P\subset[0,1]^{2}
and an absolute constant
C>0
such that any tube of width
1/n
contains at most
C
points from
P
?

As Varjú writes in [Remembering Jean Bourgain (1954-2018), AMS Notices June 2021, p. 957], the motivation for this problem comes from a possible construction of spherical harmonics as a combination of Gaussian beams, which would have
L^{\infty}
norm bounded by a constant independently of the degree. If one could show that no such constant existed, this would immediately imply that
\Phi_{k}(n)=o(1/n)
holds for every
k\geqslant 3
.
