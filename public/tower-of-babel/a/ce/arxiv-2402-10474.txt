One-Bit Quantization and Sparsification for Multiclass Linear Classification via Regularized Regression
Reza GhaneDepartment of Electrical EngineeringCalifornia Institute of TechnologyPasadena, CA 91125 rghanekh@caltech.edu, Danil AkhtiamovDepartment of Computing and Mathematical SciencesCalifornia Institute of TechnologyPasadena, CA 91125 dakhtiam@caltech.edu Babak HassibiDepartment of Electrical EngineeringCalifornia Institute of TechnologyPasadena, CA 91125 hassibi@caltech.edu

Abstract
We study the use of linear regression for multiclass classification in the over-parametrized regime where some of the training data is mislabeled. In such scenarios it is necessary to add an explicit regularization term, λ ​ f ​ ( w ) \lambda f(w) , for some convex function f ⁡ ( ⋅ ) f(\cdot) , to avoid overfitting the mislabeled data. In our analysis, we assume that the data is sampled from a Gaussian Mixture Model with equal class sizes, and that a proportion c c of the training labels is corrupted for each class. Under these assumptions, we prove that the best classification performance is achieved when f ( ⋅ ) = ∥ ⋅ ∥ 2 2 f(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|^{2}_{2} and λ → ∞ \lambda\to\infty . We then proceed to analyze the classification errors for f ( ⋅ ) = ∥ ⋅ ∥ 1 f(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{1} and f ( ⋅ ) = ∥ ⋅ ∥ ∞ f(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\infty} in the large λ \lambda regime and notice that it is often possible to find sparse and one-bit solutions, respectively, that perform almost as well as the one corresponding to f ( ⋅ ) = ∥ ⋅ ∥ 2 2 f(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2} .

Introduction

While large neural networks, such as Large Language Models (LLMs), have increasingly become an indispensable part of almost every inference task, their impressive performance comes at the price of enormous scale. To name a few, Llama 2 and GPT-4 are each comprised of 65 billion and 1.76 trillion parameters, respectively. Such models suffer from the common issues pertaining to their size: they require very large memory for storage, can consume inordinate amounts of power, and it can be very inefficient to communicate them over communication networks. This calls for the development of efficient methods for model quantization and pruning, where quantization refers to the us of a small number of bits to store each model parameter, and where pruning refers to setting some of the parameter values to zero. While multiple papers suggesting such methods have appeared recently (see, e.g..
Frantar and Alistarh, 2023a
;
Frantar and Alistarh, 2023b
;
Wang et al., 2023
;
Shang et al., 2023
and the references therein), we have very little understanding of the theoretical limitations of such methods. In other words, one would like to have an answer to the following question: for a given model and data set, how much does one have to sacrifice in performance if instead of looking at all potential solutions one looks only at, say, solutions of a given sparsity, or solutions where the weights are quantized to a certain level, such as one-bit? Of course, this question remains wide open for general supervised machine learning problems. As a first step in this direction, we will investigate it in the context of multiclass linear classification using regularized linear regression.

Multiclass classifiers play a crucial role in numerous machine learning applications. In fact, it can be argued that the power of deep learning manifests itself most prominently in the multiclass setting. Many image databases, for example
Deng et al., 2009
, contain tens of classes which any classifier will need to contend with. Multiclass classification further arises in problems with categorical outputs, such as natural language processing
Sutskever et al., 2014
, in reinforcement learning
Jang et al., 2016
, where the agent has to choose the correct action among a large set of possible ones, in recommendation systems
Covington et al., 2016
, and in many others.

Despite the widespread use and success of multiclass classification, we still have a rather poor understanding of the theory behind their performance. There are many pertinent questions for which we do not have answers. For example, what is the classification error? How does it depend on the number of classes? How does it depend on the loss function and the regularizer being used in the training? What are the best algorithms for finding good sparse or good compressed classifiers? Can we determine the best possible generalization performance among all solutions of given sparsity, or among all solutions where each parameters is quantized to a single bit? Can we efficiently learn these from data? What is the best one can do on a dataset with label corruption? Currently, we are quite far from satisfactorily answering any of these questions.

A common feature of modern successful multiclass classifiers is that they are highly over-parametrized. In fact, the number of parameters that needs to be trained is often orders of magnitude larger than the number of training samples. In this setting, models can easily interpolate the training data and classical learning theory would raise the concern of “over-fitting" and loss of generalization performance. In practice, however, the opposite has been often observed—increasing the parameters in the model improves generalization performance, a phenomenon referred to as “double descent"
Belkin et al., 2020
. It is now largely agreed upon that, the reason why over-fitting does not arise is that the (stochastic) gradient-based methods that are used to train over-parametrized models possess “implicit bias" and ostensibly find “good" interpolating solutions
Gunasekar et al., 2017
;
Azizan and Hassibi, 2019
;
Azizan et al., 2021
;
Gunasekar et al., 2018a
;
Gunasekar et al., 2018b
. Nonetheless, the precise relationship between implicit bias and classification error is not well understood. For example, it has been observed that changing the training algorithm can change the implicit bias, and thereby the classification error
Azizan et al., 2021
. However, the relationship between implicit bias and generalization is not known and is not clear what type of implicit bias will lead to good generalization performance. Furthermore, the theoretical analysis of double descent (see, e.g.,
Belkin et al., 2020
;
Deng et al., 2022
becomes less responsive when it comes to having a training dataset that contains mislabeled data. Given the described challenges, in this paper we will focus our study on a special case that is much more amenable to analysis, namely multiclass linear classifiers, hoping that the insights obtained will be informative for more general multiclass classifications settings.

In particular, we assume that the data is sampled from a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) with equal class sizes where a proportion
c
of the training labels is corrupted for each class. Since we consider the over-parametrized regime, to avoid overfitting the mislabeled data we propose regularized linear regression with a regularizer
\lambda f(w)
, for some convex function
f(\cdot)
. Even this setting is rather challenging to analyze and so we focus our analysis on the regime of strong regularization, i.e., large
\lambda
, where we can obtain precise expressions for the classification error of the solution corresponding to an arbitrary convex regularizer
f(\cdot)
and an arbitrary number of classes. This is perhaps the most interesting case, because (as we show rigorously in the sequel) for
f(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
it results in the best classification performance. Further, for
f(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{1}
we show that large
\lambda
results in highly sparse sparse solutions with good performance, and that for
f(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
large
\lambda
results in solutions that can be compressed all the way to a single bit per parameter with little loss of performance. We also present numerical results validating the theory. Put together, our results suggest that in the over-parametrized regime, one can often find sparse or one-bit solutions without having to compromise much on performance even though the underlying data model has no inherent sparsity structure. We believe this observation might have significant theoretical implications for general over-parametrized models due to the common intuition that large neural networks tend to behave similarly to their first-order linear approximations.

Related works

In the case of binary classification, there has been a recent surge of results that provide a sharp analysis for a variety of methods tailored to different models (see, e.g.,
Thrampoulidis et al., 2018a
;
Huang, 2017
;
Candès and Sur, 2018
;
Sur and Candès, 2019
;
Kammoun and Alouini, 2020
;
Salehi et al., 2019
;
Taheri et al., 2020
;
Deng et al., 2019
;
Montanari et al., 2019
;
Mignacco et al., 2020
;
Lolas, 2020
;
Akhtiamov et al., 2023
and the references therein). These works typically pose the over-parameterized binary classification problem as an optimization problem and employ the Convex Gaussian Min-Max Theorem (CGMT)
Thrampoulidis et al., 2015
;
Thrampoulidis et al., 2018a
;
Stojnic, 2013
;
Gordon, 1985
to obtain formulas for the classification error that involve solutions to a system of non-linear equations (in a small number of variables) that often do not admit closed-form expressions. These results follow a long line of work that deals with obtaining
sharp
high-dimensional asymptotics of convex optimization-based estimators.

While the above works have significantly advanced our understanding of binary classification in the over-parameterized regime, they do not shed much light on the multiclass setting. Four interesting recent papers that study the multiclass setting include
Wang et al., 2021
;
Subramanian et al., 2022
;
Loureiro et al., 2021
;
Thrampoulidis et al., 2020
.

Wang et al., 2021
establishes that in certain over-parameterized regimes the solution to a multiclass supprt vector machine (SVM) problem is identical to the one obtained by minimum-norm interpolation of one-hot encoded labels (equivalently, that gradient descent on squared loss leads to the same solution as gradient descent on cross-entropy loss, since the solution is determined by the implicit bias of these algorithms, not of the loss). In addition,
Wang et al., 2021
shows that the multiclass problem with finitely many classes reduces to a finite set of pairwise competitions, all of which must be won for multiclass classification to succeed.
Subramanian et al., 2022
establishes that multiclass classification qualitatively behaves like binary classification, as long as there are not too many classes. It further makes note that the key difference from the binary classification setting is that, as the number of classes increases, there are relatively fewer positive training examples of each class, making the multiclass problem “harder" than the binary one. We remark that the settings of
Wang et al., 2021
and
Subramanian et al., 2022
differ from ours: they consider a 1-sparse noiseless model of the labels, and a bi-level ensemble model for the different classes. We instead consider a Gaussian mixture model for the different classes.

Thrampoulidis et al., 2020
provided the first precise analysis of linear multi-class classification in the high-dimensional regime. Their work revealed that the classification accuracy depends on certain quantities, such as the correlation between the trained weights for each class, as well as the correlation between the trained weights and the means of the underlying distributions for each class. Computing these quantities is quite challenging and
Thrampoulidis et al., 2020
is only able to do so for pure least-squares classifiers and for least-squares classifiers with
f(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
regularization.
*
*

*

Thrampoulidis et al., 2020
uses CGMT and a full analysis of the multiclass classification problem requires a matrix generalization of the CGMT which currently does not exist.
Also, they did not consider any label corruption, which would further impede the analysis.

Loureiro et al., 2021
use the celebrated replica method as intuition in order to derive an AMP (approximate message passing) sequence capturing the performance of the multiclass classification problem for an arbitrary convex loss function and an arbitrary convex regularizer. This way,
Loureiro et al., 2021
expresses the classification error in terms of a few scalar quantities that can be determined from a low-dimensional system of saddle-point equations. Their analysis shows that, if the means of the GMM are sparse, then strong
\ell_{1}
regularization improves performance. However, we show that, even when the means of the underlying GMMS are not sparse, strong
\ell_{1}
regularization can lead to sparse solutions without sacrificing too much on classification performance. Furthermore,
Loureiro et al., 2021
does not study the use of regularization for model compression and also do not consider any corruption in the labels. Finally, the methods developed in our work use CGMT and are therefore entirely different, in addition to being quite explicit in the large
\lambda
regime. From this vantage point, they could be of independent interest in their own right.

3.1
The Gaussian mixture model with corruption

We will assume that we have
k
classes,
n
training samples, and that the training data is given by
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where each
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an input, and
y_{i}
is the corresponding (potentially corrupted) class label. We will further assume that the

are generated in an iid fashion from a Gaussian mixture model (GMM), the data points are drawn uniformly from each class and the labels are corrupted in a uniform manner at a fixed rate
c<\frac{1}{k-1}
. In other words, denoting the true label designating the class to which

belongs by
\tilde{y_{i}}
,

\displaystyle\mbox{Prob}(\tilde{y}_{i}=\ell)=\frac{1}{k}

\displaystyle\mbox{Prob}(y_{i}=\ell)=\begin{cases}1-c,&\ell=\tilde{y}_{i}\\
\frac{c}{k-1},&\ell\neq\tilde{y}_{i}\end{cases}~~~\mbox{and}~~~x_{i}\sim{\cal N}(\mu_{\tilde{y_{i}}},\sigma^{2}I),

(1)

i.e. the inputs for each class
\ell
are drawn from an isotropic multi-variate Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu_{\ell}
and variance
\sigma^{2}I
. We will find it useful to aggregate the mean vectors into the
k\times d
matrix

M^{T}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mu_{1}&\mu_{2}&\ldots&\mu_{k}\end{array}\right]

If we further define the
n\times d
and

matrices

\displaystyle X^{T}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}x_{1}&x_{2}&\ldots&x_{n}\end{array}\right],\quad\tilde{Y}^{T}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}e_{\tilde{y}_{1}}&e_{\tilde{y}_{2}}&\ldots&e_{\tilde{y}_{n}}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle Y^{T}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}e_{y_{1}}&e_{y_{2}}&\ldots&e_{y_{n}}\end{array}\right]

where
e_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

\ell

X=\tilde{Y}M+A,

(2)

where

As one may expect, the performance of multiclass classification for the GMM model highly depends on the relative positions of the means
\mu_{\ell}

\mu_{\ell}
, we will assume that the

are generated acoording to Assumpion A2 in Section

\mu_{\ell}^{T}\mu_{\ell^{\prime}}\approx r\|\mu_{\ell}\|\cdot\|\mu_{\ell^{\prime}}\|

3.2
Why considering GMMs is not too limiting: Gaussian Universality

While it might seem at first that the GMM assumption is very restrictive, due to Gaussian universality, this is not really the case. Put in our context, intuitively this says that for a large class of mixtures of
k
distributions modeling the classes, replacing those with the GMM having the same means and covariances for each class will lead to asymptotically equal classification error as
n
grows large. An interested reader can find a rigorous exposition and detailed related discussions in
Dandi et al., 2023
. To further illustrate this point, we conducted experiments exploring the performance of regularized linear models trained on MNIST and observed the performance to be very similar qualitatively to our predictions for GMMs (see Section
J
in the Appendix).

3.3
CGMT

The CGMT framework starts with a so-called primary optimization (PO) problem

\min_{u\in S_{u}}\max_{v\in S_{v}}u^{T}Gv+\psi(u,v),

(3)

where
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
S_{u}
and
S_{v}
are convex sets,
\psi(\cdot,\cdot)
is convex in its first argument and concave in its second one, and
G\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
has iid
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries. To analyze the (PO), the CGMT framework introduces the so-called auxilliary optimization (AO) problem

\min_{u\in S_{u}}\max_{v\in S_{v}}\|u\|_{2}g^{T}v+\|v\|_{2}h^{T}u+\psi(u,v),

(4)

where
g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
have iid
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries. Roughly speaking, the CGMT frameowrk states that, if, as
n\rightarrow\infty
while keeping
\frac{d}{n}
fixed, the optimum of (
4
) concentrates to some value
c
, say, then the optimum of (
3
) concentrates to the
same
value. More importantly, if any Lipschitz function of the solution
{\hat{u}}_{AO}
of (
4
), say its
\ell_{2}
-norm, concentrates to some value, then the same Lipschitz function of the corresponding solution
{\hat{u}}_{PO}
of (
3
) will concentrate to the
same
value. In a nutshell properties of the solution to the (PO) can be inferred from properties of the solution to the (AO). The advantage of this approach is that analyzing the (AO) is usually much simpler than the (PO) since it contains two random vectors, rather than an entire random matrix. Details and much more rigorous statements, can be found in
Thrampoulidis et al., 2015
;
Thrampoulidis et al., 2018a
.

3.4

We will be considering linear classifiers. Thus, let
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}

and the

For

has iid
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})

We remark that probabilities of the form (

P_{e}=P_{e|\ell}=Q_{k}\left(\frac{\mu_{\ell}^{T}(w_{\ell}-w_{\ell^{\prime}})}{\sigma\|w_{\ell}-w_{\ell^{\prime}}\|}\right),

(9)

for any

\displaystyle Q_{k}(a)=\mathbb{P}((I+\frac{\mathds{1}\mathds{1}^{T}}{1+\sqrt{k}})g\geq\mathds{1}\sqrt{2}a)

3.5
Overview of approach

The linear multi-class classifier just described needs a set of weight vectors for each class. These weights should be learned from the training data. In what follows, we will assume that the estimator of the weights is found from solving the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{W}\left[\|XW-Y\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}f(w_{\ell})\right]=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\min_{w_{\ell}}\left[\|Xw_{\ell}-Y_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda f(w_{\ell})\right]

4.3
The Sandwich Theorem for multiclass linear classification in the large

6
Conclusion

We have rigorously analyzed the performance of regularized linear regression for multi-class classification in the presence of labeling errors in the strong regularization regime. Theory and simulations suggest that the optimal classification error is found by ridge regression with large regularization parameter as well as that using
f(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
and
f(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{1}
and regularization one can obtain one-bit and sparse solutions, respectively, with negligible loss of performance. Studying the implications of these results, and whether they generalize, to nonlinear regression models, such as deep neural networks, is an interesting topic for future study.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{d}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{2})dx_{2}...\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\frac{x_{d}^{2}}{2})dx_{d}(f(-\frac{1}{a_{1}}(c-\sum_{i=2}^{d}a_{i}x_{i},x_{2},..,x_{d}))\exp(-\frac{(-\frac{1}{a_{1}}(c-\sum_{i=2}^{d}a_{i}x_{i})^{2}}{2})-0)+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{d}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{2})dx_{2}...\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\frac{x_{d}^{2}}{2})dx_{d}\int_{-\frac{1}{a_{1}}(c-\sum_{i=2}^{d}a_{i}x_{i})}^{\infty}\partial_{x_{1}}f(x_{1},...,x_{d})\exp(-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2})dx=\mathbb{E}\mathds{1}(a^{T}X\geq c)\partial_{X_{1}}f(X)

In the second case:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X_{1}f(X)\mathds{1}(a^{T}X\geq c)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{d}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{2})dx_{2}...\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\frac{x_{d}^{2}}{2})dx_{d}\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{1}{a_{1}}(c-\sum_{i=2}^{d}a_{i}x_{i})}x_{1}f(x_{1},...,x_{d})\exp(-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2})dx=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{d}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{2})dx_{2}...\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\frac{x_{d}^{2}}{2})dx_{d}(f(-\frac{1}{a_{1}}(c-\sum_{i=2}^{d}a_{i}x_{i},x_{2},..,x_{d}))\exp(-\frac{(-\frac{1}{a_{1}}(c-\sum_{i=2}^{d}a_{i}x_{i})^{2}}{2})-0)+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{d}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{2})dx_{2}...\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\frac{x_{d}^{2}}{2})dx_{d}\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{1}{a_{1}}(c-\sum_{i=2}^{d}a_{i}x_{i})}\partial_{x_{1}}f(x_{1},...,x_{d})\exp(-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2})dx=\mathbb{E}\mathds{1}(a^{T}X\geq c)\partial_{X_{1}}f(X)

In the third case we get the same as in the two previous ones if we assign
-\frac{1}{a_{1}}(c-\sum_{i=2}^{d}a_{i}x_{i})^{2}:=\infty

∎

Lemma F.2
.

Using the previous assumption on f, then for an invertible
\mathcal{R}
, and
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X_{1}f(X)\mathds{1}(a^{T}X\geq c)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}X_{1}X_{i}\mathbb{E}\mathds{1}(a^{T}X\geq c)\partial_{X_{i}}f(X)

Proof.

Let
Y:=\mathcal{R}^{-\frac{1}{2}}X
be the whitened version of
X
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X_{1}f(X)\mathds{1}(a^{T}X\geq c)=\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}})_{1}^{T}Yf(\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}Y)\mathds{1}((\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}a)^{T}Y\geq c)=(\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}})_{1}^{T}\mathbb{E}Yf(\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}Y)\mathds{1}((\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}a)^{T}Y\geq c)

Now note that if
X
is such that
a^{T}X=c
, then
(\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}a)^{T}Y=c
, thus we can apply the previous lemma to the
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, and have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}})_{1}^{T}\mathbb{E}Yf(\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}Y)\mathds{1}((\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}a)^{T}Y\geq c)=(\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}})_{1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla f(\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}Y)\mathds{1}((\mathcal{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}a)^{T}Y\geq c)=(\mathcal{R})_{1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\nabla f(X)\mathds{1}(a^{T}X\geq c)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}X_{1}X_{i}\mathbb{E}\mathds{1}(a^{T}X\geq c)\partial_{X_{i}}f(X)

∎

Now we are ready to prove the main theorem of this section. For the sake of completeness, we present Theorem
4.2
here:

Theorem F.3
.

(LASSO Regression)

Under the assumptions (A1)-(A3), for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\lambda_{0}
such that for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
, the classification error corresponding to
f(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{1}
can be described as follows as
n\to\infty
:

|P_{e,\lambda}-Q_{k}(\frac{n\sqrt{d}(1-r)(\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1})R(\Xi)}{k|\Delta|(\Xi)})|<\epsilon

Where

\displaystyle\Xi^{2}:=\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}}(k-1)\gamma_{1}^{2}(1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n}+(k-2)r)+\gamma_{2}^{2}(1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n}))+\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}}(2\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}(k-1)r+\frac{k}{n}\sigma^{2}(\gamma_{3}^{2}+\gamma_{4}^{2}))

\displaystyle R(\Xi):=2Q(\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}(\Xi):=\frac{\Xi^{2}}{\pi\sqrt{1-\Omega^{2}}}\int_{G>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi}}\int_{G^{\prime}>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi}}(G-G^{\prime})^{2}\exp(-\frac{G^{2}+G^{{}^{\prime}2}-2\Omega GG^{\prime}}{2(1-\Omega^{2})})dGdG^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\frac{2\Xi^{2}}{\pi\sqrt{1-\Omega^{2}}}\int_{G>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi}}\int_{|G^{\prime}|<\frac{\lambda}{\Xi}}(G-\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})^{2}\exp(-\frac{G^{2}+G^{{}^{\prime}2}-2\Omega GG^{\prime}}{2(1-\Omega^{2})})dGdG^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\frac{\Xi^{2}}{\pi\sqrt{1-\Omega^{2}}}\int_{G>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi}}\int_{G^{\prime}<-\frac{\lambda}{\Xi}}(G-G^{\prime}-2\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})^{2}\exp(-\frac{G^{2}+G^{{}^{\prime}2}-2\Omega GG^{\prime}}{2(1-\Omega^{2})})dGdG^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Omega:=\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}\Xi^{2}}\left[\gamma_{1}^{2}(k-2)(1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n}+(k-1)r)+(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{2})r+2\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}(1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n}+(k-2)r)+2\frac{k}{n}\gamma_{3}\gamma_{4}\sigma^{2}\right]

Where
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}
are the solutions to the following scalar optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}}-\frac{d(\Xi^{2}+\lambda^{2})R(\Xi)}{4\rho}+\frac{d\Xi\lambda}{2\rho\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\Xi^{2}})+\frac{n}{k}\left[-c\gamma_{1}-(k-1)\frac{\gamma_{1}^{2}}{4}-(1-c)\gamma_{2}-\frac{\gamma_{2}^{2}}{4}-s\gamma_{3}-\frac{\gamma_{3}^{2}}{4}-t\gamma_{4}-\frac{\gamma_{4}^{2}}{4}\right]

Moreover, only
\left\lceil dR(\Xi)\right\rceil=\left\lceil 2dQ(\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})\right\rceil
entries of each classifier are non-zero.

Proof.

We break the proof into several steps, described in the following sections.

F.1
Scalarization of
\bar{\phi}_{\lambda}

First, consider the following optimization for an arbitrary
\rho=n\sigma^{2}
, to obtain the final result, we plug-in
\rho=n\sigma^{2}
.

\displaystyle\min_{w_{\ell},w_{\ell^{\prime}}}\frac{n}{k}\left[\sum_{\mathscr{r}\neq\ell}(w_{\ell}^{T}\tilde{\mu}_{\mathscr{r}}-\frac{c}{k-1})^{2}+(w_{\ell}^{T}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}-(1-c))^{2}+(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}w_{\ell}^{T}a-s)^{2}+(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}w_{\ell}^{T}b-t)^{2}\right]+\rho\|w_{\ell}\|^{2}+\lambda f(w_{\ell})+

\displaystyle+\frac{n}{k}\left[\sum_{\mathscr{r}\neq\ell^{\prime}}(w_{\ell^{\prime}}^{T}\tilde{\mu}_{\mathscr{r}}-\frac{c}{k-1})^{2}+(w_{\ell^{\prime}}^{T}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell^{\prime}}-(1-c))^{2}+(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}w_{\ell^{\prime}}^{T}a-t)^{2}+(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}w_{\ell^{\prime}}^{T}b-s)^{2}\right]+\rho\|w_{\ell^{\prime}}\|^{2}+\lambda f(w_{\ell^{\prime}})

Taking the Fenchel dual of each term and making use of the symmetry of the model, we arrive at the following:

\displaystyle\min_{w_{\ell},w_{\ell^{\prime}}}\max_{\gamma}\frac{n}{k}\left[\sum_{\mathscr{r}\neq\ell}[\gamma_{\ell,1}(w_{\ell}^{T}\tilde{\mu}_{\mathscr{r}}-\frac{c}{k-1})-\frac{\gamma_{\ell,1}^{2}}{4}]+\gamma_{\ell,2}(w_{\ell}^{T}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}-(1-c))-\frac{\gamma_{\ell,2}^{2}}{4}+\gamma_{\ell,3}(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}w_{\ell}^{T}a-s)-\frac{\gamma_{\ell,3}^{2}}{4}\right]+

\displaystyle+\frac{n}{k}\left[\gamma_{\ell,4}(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}w_{\ell}^{T}b-t)-\frac{\gamma_{\ell,4}^{2}}{4}\right]+\frac{n}{k}\left[\sum_{\mathscr{r}\neq\ell^{\prime}}[\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},1}(w_{\ell^{\prime}}^{T}\tilde{\mu}_{\mathscr{r}}-\frac{c}{k-1})-\frac{\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},1}^{2}}{4}]+\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},2}(w_{\ell^{\prime}}^{T}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell^{\prime}}-(1-c))-\frac{\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},2}^{2}}{4}\right]+

\displaystyle+\frac{n}{k}\left[\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},3}(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}w_{\ell^{\prime}}^{T}a-t)-\frac{\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},3}^{2}}{4}+\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},4}(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}w_{\ell^{\prime}}^{T}b-s)-\frac{\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},4}^{2}}{4}\right]+\rho\|w_{\ell}\|^{2}+\lambda f(w_{\ell})+\rho\|w_{\ell^{\prime}}\|^{2}+\lambda f(w_{\ell^{\prime}})

Due to symmetry, we only consider the optimization over
w_{\ell}
:

\displaystyle\min_{w_{\ell}}\frac{n}{k}\left[\gamma_{\ell,1}\sum_{r\neq\ell}\tilde{\mu}_{r}+\gamma_{\ell,2}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}+\gamma_{\ell,3}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}a+\gamma_{\ell,4}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}b\right]^{T}w_{\ell}+\rho\|w_{\ell}\|^{2}+\lambda\|w_{\ell}\|_{1}

We first tackle an equivalent scalar optimization:

\displaystyle\min_{x}ax+\lambda|x|+\rho x^{2}

Taking derivative and solving for
x
yields

\displaystyle a+\lambda\partial|x|+2\rho x=0

\displaystyle x^{*}=\frac{1}{2\rho}ST(-a;\lambda):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\rho}(-a-\lambda)&-a-\lambda>0\\
\frac{1}{2\rho}(-a+\lambda)&-a+\lambda<0\\
0&o.w\end{cases}

Where
ST(x;c)
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle ST(x;c):=\begin{cases}x-c&x>c\\
x+c&x<-c\\
0&o.w\end{cases}

The objective value would be

\displaystyle\min_{x}ax+\lambda|x|+\rho x^{2}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\rho}((-a^{2}-a\lambda)+(-\lambda a-\lambda^{2})+\frac{1}{2}(a+\lambda)^{2})&-a-\lambda>0\\
\frac{1}{2\rho}((-a^{2}+a\lambda)+(\lambda a-\lambda^{2})+\frac{1}{2}(a-\lambda)^{2})&-a+\lambda<0\\
0&o.w\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{4\rho}(a+\lambda)^{2}&-a-\lambda>0\\
-\frac{1}{4\rho}(a-\lambda)^{2}&-a+\lambda<0\\
0&o.w\end{cases}=-\frac{1}{4\rho}ST^{2}(-a;\lambda)

Now we apply this result to our problem. First let us define the following vectors for both of the classes
\ell,\ell^{\prime}
.

\displaystyle\Psi_{\ell}:=\frac{n}{k}\left[\gamma_{\ell,1}\sum_{r\neq\ell}\tilde{\mu}_{r}+\gamma_{\ell,2}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}+\gamma_{\ell,3}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}a+\gamma_{\ell,4}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}b\right]

\displaystyle\Psi_{\ell^{\prime}}:=\frac{n}{k}\left[\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},1}\sum_{\mathscr{r}\neq\ell^{\prime}}\tilde{\mu}_{\mathscr{r}}+\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},2}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell^{\prime}}+\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},3}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}a+\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},4}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}b\right]

Leveraging the assumptions on
\mu
’s, we observe that
Var(\tilde{\mu}_{i,r})=1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n}
and
\mathbb{E}\tilde{\mu}_{i,\mathscr{r}}\tilde{\mu}_{i,\mathscr{r}^{\prime}}=r
. Therefore, taking
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{n}{k}\sqrt{(k-1)\gamma_{\ell,1}^{2}(1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n}+(k-2)r)+\gamma_{\ell,2}^{2}(1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n})+2\gamma_{\ell,1}\gamma_{\ell,2}(k-1)r+\frac{k}{n}\sigma^{2}(\gamma_{\ell,3}^{2}+\gamma_{\ell,4}^{2})}G:=\Xi G

Then plugging in

\displaystyle w^{*}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2\rho}ST(-\frac{n}{k}(\gamma_{\ell,1}\sum_{r\neq\ell}\tilde{\mu}_{r}+\gamma_{\ell,2}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}+\gamma_{\ell,3}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}a+\gamma_{\ell,4}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}b);\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\rho}ST(-\Psi_{\ell};\lambda)

Using this result, the optimization over w would achieve the following value:

\displaystyle\min_{w_{\ell}}\frac{n}{k}\left[\gamma_{\ell,1}\sum_{r\neq\ell}\tilde{\mu}_{r}+\gamma_{\ell,2}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}+\gamma_{\ell,3}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}a+\gamma_{\ell,4}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}b\right]^{T}w_{\ell}+\rho\|w_{\ell}\|^{2}+\lambda\|w_{\ell}\|_{1}=\frac{-1}{4\rho}\|ST(-\Psi_{\ell};\lambda)\|^{2}

Now using the concentration properties we calculate the expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|ST(-\Psi_{\ell};\lambda)\|^{2}=d\mathbb{E}ST^{2}(\Xi G;\lambda)=d\mathbb{E}(\Xi G-\lambda)^{2}\mathds{1}(\Xi G>\lambda)+d\mathbb{E}(\Xi G+\lambda)^{2}\mathds{1}(\Xi G<-\lambda)=

\displaystyle 2\Xi^{2}d\mathbb{E}G^{2}\mathds{1}(\Xi G>\lambda)+2d\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\mathds{1}(\Xi G>\lambda)-2d\Xi\lambda\mathbb{E}G\mathds{1}(\Xi G>\lambda)+2d\Xi\lambda\mathbb{E}G\mathds{1}(\Xi G<-\lambda)=

\displaystyle 2\Xi^{2}d\mathbb{E}G^{2}\mathds{1}(G>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})+2d\lambda^{2}Q(\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})-4d\Xi\lambda\mathbb{E}G\mathds{1}(G>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})=2\Xi^{2}d\mathbb{E}G^{2}\mathds{1}(G>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})+2d\lambda^{2}Q(\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})-\frac{4d\Xi\lambda}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\Xi^{2}})=

\displaystyle 2d\lambda^{2}Q(\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})-\frac{4d\Xi\lambda}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\Xi^{2}})+2\Xi^{2}d(Q(\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})+\frac{\lambda}{\Xi\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\Xi^{2}}))=

\displaystyle 2dQ(\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})(\Xi^{2}+\lambda^{2})-\frac{2d\Xi\lambda}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\Xi^{2}})

Plugging back in, we get the following optimization

\displaystyle\max_{\gamma_{\ell,1},\gamma_{\ell,2},\gamma_{\ell,3},\gamma_{\ell,4}}\frac{n}{k}\left[-c\gamma_{\ell,1}-(k-1)\frac{\gamma_{\ell,1}^{2}}{4}-(1-c)\gamma_{\ell,2}-\frac{\gamma_{\ell,2}^{2}}{4}-s\gamma_{\ell,3}-\frac{\gamma_{\ell,3}^{2}}{4}-t\gamma_{\ell,4}-\frac{\gamma_{\ell,4}^{2}}{4}\right]-

\displaystyle-\frac{d(\Xi^{2}+\lambda^{2})R(\Xi)}{4\rho}+\frac{d\Xi\lambda}{2\rho\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\Xi^{2}})

Where the quantity

\displaystyle R(\Xi)=\mathbb{P}(|\Psi_{\ell}|=\Xi G\geq\lambda)=2Q(\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})

is the the fraction of nonzero entries of the optimal
w
.

F.2
Derivation of the classification error

First note that by the derivation from previous section,
\|w_{\ell}-w_{\ell^{\prime}}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{4\rho^{2}}\|ST(-\Psi_{\ell};\lambda)-ST(-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime}};\lambda)\|^{2}
and
\mu_{\ell}^{T}(w_{\ell}-w_{\ell^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{2\rho}\mu_{\ell}^{T}(ST(-\Psi_{\ell};\lambda)-ST(-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime}};\lambda))
. Furthermore, It can be seen that
\gamma_{\ell,1}=\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},1}=\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{\ell,2}=\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},2}=\gamma_{2}
and
\gamma_{\ell,3}=\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},4}=\gamma_{3}
and
\gamma_{\ell,4}=\gamma_{\ell^{\prime},3}=\gamma_{4}
due to symmetry. In fact, one has

\displaystyle(\Psi_{\ell,i},\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{2}_{\ell}&r_{\ell}\\
r_{\ell}&\sigma^{2}_{\ell^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix})

Where by symmetry,
\sigma^{2}_{\ell}=\sigma^{2}_{\ell^{\prime}}=\Xi^{2}
, and for the correlation,

\displaystyle\Omega:=\frac{1}{\Xi^{2}d}\mathbb{E}\Psi_{\ell}^{T}\Psi_{\ell^{\prime}}=\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}\Xi^{2}}\left[\gamma_{1}^{2}(\sum_{r\neq\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\tilde{\mu}_{r})^{2}+(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{2})\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell^{\prime}}\right]+\frac{n}{k\Xi^{2}}\gamma_{3}\gamma_{4}\left[a^{2}+b^{2}\right]+

\displaystyle+\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}\Xi^{2}}\left[\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}(\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}^{2}+\tilde{\mu}_{\ell^{\prime}}^{2})+\gamma_{1}^{2}(\sum_{r\neq\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}\tilde{\mu}_{r}+\sum_{r\neq\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell^{\prime}}\tilde{\mu}_{r})+\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}(\sum_{r\neq\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}\tilde{\mu}_{r}+\sum_{r\neq\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell^{\prime}}\tilde{\mu}_{r})\right]=

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}\Xi^{2}}\left[\gamma_{1}^{2}(k-2)(1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n}+(k-3)r)+(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{2})r+2\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}(1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n})+2(k-2)r\gamma_{1}^{2}+2\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}(k-2)r+2\frac{k}{n}\gamma_{3}\gamma_{4}\sigma^{2}\right]=

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}\Xi^{2}}\left[\gamma_{1}^{2}(k-2)(1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n}+(k-3)r)+(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{2})r+2\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}(1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n}+(k-2)r)+2(k-2)r\gamma_{1}^{2}+2\frac{k}{n}\gamma_{3}\gamma_{4}\sigma^{2}\right]=

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}\Xi^{2}}\left[\gamma_{1}^{2}(k-2)(1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n}+(k-1)r)+(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{2})r+2\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}(1+\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n}+(k-2)r)+2\frac{k}{n}\gamma_{3}\gamma_{4}\sigma^{2}\right]

Thus

\displaystyle(\Psi_{\ell,i},\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\begin{pmatrix}\Xi^{2}&\Omega\Xi^{2}\\
\Omega\Xi^{2}&\Xi^{2}\end{pmatrix})

And we can take
(\Psi_{\ell,i},\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(G,G^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\begin{pmatrix}1&\Omega\\
\Omega&1\end{pmatrix})
. Now we focus on the calculation of the classification error which requires the characterization of
\frac{\mu_{\ell}^{T}(w^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}}-w^{*}_{\ell})}{\|w^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}}-w^{*}_{\ell}\|}
. Beginning with
\|w^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}}-w^{*}_{\ell}\|
, we have from concentration.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\|ST(-\Psi_{\ell};\lambda)-ST(-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime}};\lambda)\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[(\Psi_{\ell,i}-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i})^{2}\mathds{1}(-\Psi_{\ell,i}>\lambda,-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}>\lambda)\right]+

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[(\Psi_{\ell,i}-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i})^{2}\mathds{1}(-\Psi_{\ell,i}<-\lambda,-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}<-\lambda)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[(\Psi_{\ell,i}+\lambda)^{2}\mathds{1}(-\Psi_{\ell,i}>\lambda,|\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}|<\lambda)\right]+

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[(\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}+\lambda)^{2}\mathds{1}(|\Psi_{\ell,i}|<\lambda,-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}>\lambda)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[(\Psi_{\ell,i}-\lambda)^{2}\mathds{1}(-\Psi_{\ell,i}<-\lambda,|\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}|<\lambda)\right]+

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[(\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}-\lambda)^{2}\mathds{1}(|\Psi_{\ell,i}|<\lambda,-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}<-\lambda)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[(\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}-\Psi_{\ell,i}-2\lambda)^{2}\mathds{1}(-\Psi_{\ell,i}>\lambda,-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}<-\lambda)\right]+

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[(\Psi_{\ell,i}-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}+2\lambda)^{2}\mathds{1}(-\Psi_{\ell,i}<-\lambda,-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}>\lambda)\right]=

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left[(\Psi_{\ell,i}-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i})^{2}\mathds{1}(-\Psi_{\ell,i}>\lambda,-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}>\lambda)\right]+4\mathbb{E}\left[(\Psi_{\ell,i}-\lambda)^{2}\mathds{1}(-\Psi_{\ell,i}<-\lambda,|\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}|<\lambda)\right]+

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left[(\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}-\Psi_{\ell,i}-2\lambda)^{2}\mathds{1}(-\Psi_{\ell,i}>\lambda,-\Psi_{\ell^{\prime},i}<-\lambda)\right]=2\Xi^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[(G-G^{\prime})^{2}\mathds{1}(G<-\frac{\lambda}{\Xi},G^{\prime}<-\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})\right]+

\displaystyle+4\Xi^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[(G-\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})^{2}\mathds{1}(G>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi},|G^{\prime}|<\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})\right]+2\Xi^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[(G-G^{\prime}+2\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})^{2}\mathds{1}(G<-\frac{\lambda}{\Xi},G^{\prime}>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})\right]

We calculate each term separately. Let
(G,G^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{R})
with
\mathcal{R}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\Omega\\
\Omega&1\end{pmatrix}

and
\mathcal{R}^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-\Omega^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\Omega\\
-\Omega&1\end{pmatrix}
. Using this, one can write

\displaystyle 2\Xi^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[(G-G^{\prime})^{2}\mathds{1}(G<-\frac{\lambda}{\Xi},G^{\prime}<-\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})\right]=\frac{\Xi^{2}}{\pi\sqrt{1-\Omega^{2}}}\int_{G>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi}}\int_{G^{\prime}>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi}}(G-G^{\prime})^{2}\exp(-\frac{G^{2}+G^{{}^{\prime}2}-2\Omega GG^{\prime}}{2(1-\Omega^{2})})

\displaystyle 4\Xi^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[(G-\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})^{2}\mathds{1}(G>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi},|G^{\prime}|<\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})\right]=\frac{2\Xi^{2}}{\pi\sqrt{1-\Omega^{2}}}\int_{G>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi}}\int_{|G^{\prime}|<\frac{\lambda}{\Xi}}(G-\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})^{2}\exp(-\frac{G^{2}+G^{{}^{\prime}2}-2\Omega GG^{\prime}}{2(1-\Omega^{2})})

\displaystyle 2\Xi^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[(G-G^{\prime}+2\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})^{2}\mathds{1}(G<-\frac{\lambda}{\Xi},G^{\prime}>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})\right]=\frac{\Xi^{2}}{\pi\sqrt{1-\Omega^{2}}}\int_{G>\frac{\lambda}{\Xi}}\int_{G^{\prime}<-\frac{\lambda}{\Xi}}(G-G^{\prime}-2\frac{\lambda}{\Xi})^{2}\exp(-\frac{G^{2}+G^{{}^{\prime}2}-2\Omega GG^{\prime}}{2(1-\Omega^{2})})

Combining results yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{\ell}^{T}(w^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}}-w^{*}_{\ell})}{\|w^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}}-w^{*}_{\ell})\|}=\frac{n\sqrt{d}(1-r)(\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1})(1-R(\Xi))}{2k\rho\Delta(\Xi)}

\displaystyle\Delta(\Xi)^{2}:=\frac{\Xi^{2}}{8\rho^{2}\pi\sqrt{1-\Omega^{2}}}\int_{|G|<\frac{2\rho\delta}{\Xi\lambda}}\int_{|G^{\prime}|<\frac{2\rho\delta}{\Xi\lambda}}(G-G^{\prime})^{2}\exp(-\frac{G^{2}+G^{{}^{\prime}2}-2\Omega GG^{\prime}}{2(1-\Omega^{2})})dGdG^{\prime}+

\displaystyle\frac{4\delta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}\pi\sqrt{1-\Omega^{2}}}\int_{G<-\frac{2\rho\delta}{\Xi\lambda}}\int_{G^{\prime}>\frac{2\rho\delta}{\Xi\lambda}}\exp(-\frac{G^{2}+G^{{}^{\prime}2}-2\Omega GG^{\prime}}{2(1-\Omega^{2})})dGdG^{\prime}+

\displaystyle\frac{\Xi^{2}}{4\rho^{2}\pi\sqrt{1-\Omega^{2}}}\int_{|G|<\frac{2\rho\delta}{\Xi\lambda}}\int_{G^{\prime}<-\frac{2\rho\delta}{\Xi\lambda}}(G+\frac{2\rho\delta}{\lambda\Xi})^{2}\exp(-\frac{G^{2}+G^{{}^{\prime}2}-2\Omega GG^{\prime}}{2(1-\Omega^{2})})dGdG^{\prime}+

\displaystyle\frac{\Xi^{2}}{4\rho^{2}\pi\sqrt{1-\Omega^{2}}}\int_{|G|<\frac{2\rho\delta}{\Xi\lambda}}\int_{G^{\prime}>\frac{2\rho\delta}{\Xi\lambda}}(G-\frac{2\rho\delta}{\lambda\Xi})^{2}\exp(-\frac{G^{2}+G^{{}^{\prime}2}-2\Omega GG^{\prime}}{2(1-\Omega^{2})})dGdG^{\prime}

∎
