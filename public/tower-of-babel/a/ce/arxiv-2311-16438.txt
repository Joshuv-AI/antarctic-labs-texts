The structure of the wave operator in four dimensions in the presence of resonances
Angus Alexander, Adam Rennie

Abstract
We show that the wave operators for Schrödinger scattering in ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4} have a particular form which depends on the existence of resonances. As a consequence of this form, we determine the contribution of resonances to the index of the wave operator.



1
Introduction

In this paper we study scattering for Schrödinger operators in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. In particular we analyse the structure of the wave operator in terms of the scattering operator, the generator of dilations, and a novel contribution corresponding to the existence of resonances. The form of the wave operator allows us to deduce the contribution of resonances to Levinson’s theorem via the index of the wave operator.

Levinson’s theorem
[
33
]
gives in dimension
n=1
that the number of eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity) of the Schrödinger operator
H=H_{0}+V
(with
H_{0}=-\Delta
) for a suitably decaying potential
V
satisfies

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\delta(0)-\delta(\infty)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\nu,

(1.1)

where
\delta
is the scattering phase and
\nu\in\{0,1\}
depends on the existence of a resonance (a distributional solution to
(-\Delta+V)\psi=0
with
\psi\notin L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
).

In dimension
n=3
, it was shown in
[
34
, Section 5]
that for spherically symmetric potentials, Equation (
1.1
) holds for each angular momentum mode. This result was extended by
[
35
, Section 7]
for general potentials, and it was shown that resonances can occur and provide a half-integer contribution to a Levinson-type theorem.

One approach to resonances is the threshold behaviour of the spectrum of Schrödinger operators as the strength of the potential is scaled,
\lambda\to\lambda V
. A resonance occurs just as an eigenvalue emerges from the continuous spectrum. Klaus and Simon
[
31
, Table I]
determined in dimension
n=2,4
that the logarithmic behaviour of the singularity of the free resolvent
R_{0}(z)=(H_{0}-z)^{-1}
near
z=0
gives resonances with different properties to those found in odd dimensions. In particular, in the case of a spherically symmetric potential the ‘resonances’ in dimension
n=2
are either
s
-resonances or
p
-resonances (corresponding to angular momentum
\ell=0
or
\ell=1
respectively) whilst the resonances in dimension
n=4
are
s
-resonances. It was observed in
[
21
,
31
]
that no such phenomena can occur in dimension
n\geq 5
.

In
[
23
]
a precise low energy expansion of the resolvents
R_{0}(z)
and
R(z)=(H-z)^{-1}
was given in dimension
n=4
, providing the definition of a resonance as a solution
\psi
to the equation
H\psi=0
which is not square-integrable but lies in some weighted Sobolev space. Obtaining such an expansion in dimension
n=2
was a more difficult task. In
[
7
,
8
]
a low energy expansion of the resolvent
R(z)
was provided in dimension
n=2
to give a precise definition of resonances and determine their contribution to Levinson’s theorem. It was shown that
s
-resonances give no contribution to Levinson’s theorem and
p
-resonances give an integer contribution, both contrasting the behaviour of resonances in dimension
n=1,3
.

More recently in dimension
n=4
it was shown in
[
11
, Equation (26b)]
that in the case of a sufficiently decaying spherically symmetric potential, the
s
-resonances provide an integer contribution to Levinson’s theorem. In
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
this result was generalised to non-spherically symmetric potentials.

In
[
24
]
a new symmetrised technique was introduced for performing low-energy expansions of an operator related to
R(z)
in terms of powers of a single variable, avoiding the double Laurent expansion of
[
8
]
. The technique in
[
24
]
allows us to systematically isolate the behaviour at zero in the spectrum of
H
into the range of a decreasing sequence of finite rank projections. This technique is described in detail for four dimensional Schrödinger operators in
[
12
]
. See also
[
14
,
15
,
43
]
.

In
[
38
, Theorem 1.1]
,
[
40
, Theorem 1.3]
and
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
it was shown that for all
n\geq 2
and suitably decaying potentials
V
(with the additional assumption that there are no
p
-resonances in dimension
n=2
and no resonances in dimension
n=4
), the wave operator
W_{-}
is of the form

\displaystyle W_{-}

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}+\varphi(D_{n})(S-\textup{Id})+K,

(1.2)

where
D_{n}
is the generator of the dilation group on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is given by

\displaystyle\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh{(\pi x)}-i\cosh{(\pi x)}^{-1}\right)

(1.3)

and
K
is a compact operator.

In recent years much work has been done on developing formulae analogous to Equation (
1.2
) for various scattering systems, including Schrödinger scattering
[
4
,
29
,
30
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
, point interactions
[
28
]
, rank-one perturbations
[
37
]
, the Friedrichs-Faddeev model
[
20
]
, Aharanov-Bohm operators
[
27
]
, lattice scattering
[
6
]
, half-line scattering
[
19
]
, discrete scattering
[
17
]
, scattering for an inverse-square potential
[
10
,
18
]
and for 1D Dirac operators with zero-range interections
[
36
]
.

The exclusion of
p
-resonances in dimension
n=2
and resonances in dimension
n=4
to obtain Equation (
1.2
) is due to the presence of an additional logarithmic singularity near zero in the resolvent expansion provided by
[
12
, Proposition 5.3]
. Recent work
[
3
, Lemma 4.3]
shows, using the resolvent expansions of
[
24
]
, that in the presence of
p
-resonances in dimension
n=2
the wave operator satisfies

\displaystyle W_{-}

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}+\varphi(D_{n})(S-\textup{Id})+B_{res}+K,

(1.4)

where
\varphi
is given in Equation (
1.3
),
B_{res}
is a bounded operator depending on the existence of
p
-resonances and
K
is a compact operator.

The nature of the singularity in the resolvent at zero energy in the presence of resonances in dimension
n=4
is the same as that of
p
-resonances in dimension
n=2
. As such, we can use the techniques of
[
3
]
to show in Theorem
3.1
that Equation (
1.4
) holds in dimension
n=4
with
B_{res}
an operator depending on the existence of resonances.

After factorising the wave operator as the product of two Fredholm operators, we show in Corollary
4.5
that

\displaystyle\textup{Index}(W_{-})

\displaystyle=\textup{Index}(W_{S})+\dim(P_{s}),

(1.5)

where
\dim(P_{s})\in\{0,1\}
is the number of linearly independent
s
-resonances and
W_{S}=\textup{Id}+\varphi(D_{4})(S-\textup{Id})
is a Fredholm operator.

For a comparison, we recall that in
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
it is proved that for a sufficiently rapidly decaying potential the number of eigenvalues
N
of
H
(counted with multiplicity) is given by

\displaystyle-N

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\textup{Tr}\left(S(\lambda)^{*}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\right)-c_{1}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda-\beta_{2}+\dim(P_{s})

(1.6)

for some constants
c_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
depending on the potential. Even after taking into account the equality
\textup{Index}(W_{-})=-N
, Equation (
1.5
) cannot be immediately compared with Equation (
1.6
) other than to note that the existence of a resonance provides an integer contribution to both. To show that Equation (
1.6
) follows from Equation (
1.5
) requires a more subtle analysis of the high-energy behaviour of the scattering matrix in the trace norm, for which we defer to a future publication.

The layout of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the relevant concepts from scattering theory for four-dimensional Schrödinger operators and fix our notation. In particular, we discuss the low-energy resolvent expansion provided by
[
12
,
24
]
, the definition of a resonance and the properties of the scattering matrix. In Section
3
we prove, via a number of technical results and unitary transformations analogous to those in
[
3
]
, that the wave operator
W_{-}
in dimension
n=4
satisfies Equation (
1.4
). In Section
4
we decouple the resonant contribution
B_{res}
by providing a suitable factorisation of the wave operator and show that Equation (
1.5
) holds, giving a topological interpretation of Levinson’s theorem in which resonances provide an integer contribution.

2
Preliminaries on scattering theory

2.1
Standing assumptions and notation

Throughout this article we will consider the scattering theory on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
associated to the operators

H_{0}=-\sum_{j=1}^{4}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}=-\Delta\quad\mbox{and}\quad H=-\sum_{j=1}^{4}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}+V

where the (multiplication operator by the)
potential
V
is real-valued and satisfies

\displaystyle|V(x)|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|x|)^{-\rho}

(2.1)

for some
\rho>12
.
We denote the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
and its dual
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
and recall the weighted Sobolev spaces

H^{s,t}(\mathbb{R}^{4})=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{4}):\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{H^{s,t}}:=\left|\left|(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{t}{2}}(\textup{Id}-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}f\right|\right|<\infty\Big\}

with index
s\in\mathbb{R}
indicating derivatives and
t\in\mathbb{R}
associated to decay at infinity
[
5
, Section 4.1]
. With
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, we denote the Fourier transform by

\mathcal{F}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4}),\qquad[\mathcal{F}f](\xi)=(2\pi)^{-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}e^{-i\langle x,\xi\rangle}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Note that the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
is an isomorphism from
H^{s,t}
to
H^{t,s}
for any
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
. We denote by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
and
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
the bounded and compact operators from
\mathcal{H}_{1}
to
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.
For
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, we let

R_{0}(z)=(H_{0}-z)^{-1},\qquad R(z)=(H-z)^{-1}

and the boundary values of the resolvent are defined as

\displaystyle R_{0}(\lambda\pm i0)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{R_{0}(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad R(\lambda\pm i0)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{R(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)}.

(2.2)

The limiting absorption principle
[
1
, Theorem 4.2]
tells us that these boundary values exist in
\mathcal{B}(H^{0,t},H^{2,-t})
for any
t>\frac{1}{2}
and
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
. The operator
H_{0}
has purely absolutely continuous spectrum, and in particular no kernel. The operator
H
can have eigenvalues and for
V
satisfying Assumption (
2.1
) with
\rho>1
we have that these eigenvalues are negative, or zero
[
42
, Theorem 6.1.1]
(see also
[
26
, Section 1]
). We let
P_{0}
be the kernel projection of
H
, which may be zero.

The one-parameter unitary group of dilations on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
given on
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by

\displaystyle[U_{n}(t)f](x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{nt}{2}}f(\mathrm{e}^{t}x),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.3)

We denote the self-adjoint generator of
U_{n}
by
D_{n}
.
The generator of the group
(U_{+}(t))
of dilations on the half-line
\mathbb{R}^{+}
is denoted
D_{+}

(which is
D_{1}
restricted to the positive half-line). The generators of the dilation groups are given by

\displaystyle D_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{y}{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}+\frac{1}{2i}{\textup{Id}},\qquad D_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{x_{j}}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{n}{2i}{\textup{Id}}.

(2.4)

Since each of
D_{+},D_{n}
generate one-parameter groups, we can recognise functions of these operators. For
D_{+}
and
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
a bounded function whose Fourier transform has rapid decay, we have

\displaystyle[\varphi(D_{+})g](\rho)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{[\mathcal{F}^{*}\varphi](t)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{2}}g(\mathrm{e}^{t}\rho)\,\mathrm{d}t},

with a similar formula for
D_{n}
.

Several Hilbert spaces recur, and we adopt the notation (following
[
22
, Section 2]
which contains an excellent discussion on the relations between the spaces and operators we introduce here)

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4}),\quad\mathcal{P}=L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3}),\quad\mathcal{H}_{spec}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{P})\cong L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\otimes\mathcal{P}.

Here
\mathcal{H}_{spec}
provides the Hilbert space on which we can diagonalise the free Hamiltonian
H_{0}
.

Since
V
is bounded,
H=H_{0}+V
is self-adjoint with
\Dom(H)=\Dom(H_{0})
. The wave operators

W_{\pm}=\mathop{\textup{s-lim}}_{t\to\pm\infty}e^{itH}e^{-itH_{0}}

exist and are asymptotically complete if
\rho>1

[
42
, Theorem 1.6.2]
. We will use the stationary scattering theory, which coincides with the time-dependent approach
[
41
, Section 5.3]
given our assumptions.
For suitable
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
we can write
[
42
, Equation 0.6.9]

\displaystyle\langle W_{\pm}f,g\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\langle R_{0}(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)f,R(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)g\rangle}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}.

(2.5)

For our analysis of the wave operator, we describe the explicit unitaries which diagonalise our Hamiltonians.

For
\lambda>0
the trace operator
\gamma(\lambda):\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{4})\to\mathcal{P}
defined by

[\gamma(\lambda)f](\omega):=f(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega)
defines a bounded operator and for each
s>\frac{1}{2}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
extends to a bounded operator on
H^{s,t}
(see
[
32
, Theorem 2.4.3]
).

Definition 2.1
.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
t>\frac{1}{2}
we define the operator

\Gamma_{0}(\lambda):H^{s,t}\to\mathcal{P}\quad\mbox{by}\quad[\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)f](\omega)=2^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}[\mathcal{F}f](\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega)

and the operator which diagonalises the free Hamiltonian
H_{0}
as

F_{0}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}_{spec}\quad\mbox{by}\quad[F_{0}f](\lambda,\omega)=[\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)f](\omega).

Lemma 2.2

(
[
22
, p. 439]
)
.

The operator
F_{0}
is unitary. Moreover for
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
,
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{3}
and
f\in\mathcal{H}_{spec}
we have

[F_{0}H_{0}F_{0}^{*}f](\lambda,\omega)=\lambda f(\lambda,\omega)=:[Lf](\lambda,\omega).

Here we have defined the operator
L
of multiplication by the spectral variable.

2.2
Resolvent expansions, resonances and the scattering operator

Here we recall some known results regarding expansions related to the perturbed resolvent
R(z)
in the limit
z\to 0
. Only the terms in the expansion relevant to later computations will be shown, however we note that higher terms can be computed explicitly
[
12
,
23
,
43
]
. The low energy behaviour is sensitive to the presence of ‘zero-energy resonances’. These are essentially distributional solutions to
H\psi=0
which are not square-integrable but lie in some larger space. We will give the precise definition shortly. We define
v(x)=|V(x)|^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
u(x)=\textup{sign}(V(x))
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, so that
U,v
are self-adjoint,
U
is unitary and
V=vUv
.

Definition 2.3
.

Suppose that
V
satisfies the assumption (
2.1
) for some
\rho>12
. Then we say that there exists an
s
-resonance (or just simply a resonance) for
H=H_{0}+V
if there exists
\psi\in H^{0,-t}
for some
t>0
such that
H\psi=0
(in the sense of distributions) and
\psi\notin\mathcal{H}
.

Since
H
has no positive eigenvalues, our assumption on the decay of the potential and the limiting absorption principle
[
1
, Theorem 4.2]
guarantee that the norm limits

\displaystyle vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i0)v

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)v}\quad\textup{ and }\quad vR(\lambda\pm i0)v:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{vR(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)v}

exist in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and are continuous in
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
. For
\lambda,\varepsilon>0
we have the equality

\displaystyle U-UvR(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)vU

\displaystyle=\left(U+vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)v\right)^{-1},

which implies the existence and continuity of
(0,\infty)\ni\lambda\mapsto\left(u+vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i0)v\right)^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Furthermore, we have
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}{(U+vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i0)v)^{-1}}=U
, since
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}{vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i0)v}=0
by
[
42
, Proposition 7.1.2]
. On the other hand, the existence of the limits
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}{(U+vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i0)v)^{-1}}
depends on the existence of resonances and eigenvalues at zero. The problem has been described in detail in
[
12
]
using the method of
[
24
]
. We outline the main results below.

Let
k\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
with
\textup{Re}(k)\geq 0
and define
\eta=\frac{1}{\ln{(k)}}
(using the principal branch of the logarithm). Define the operator
M(k)=U+vR_{0}(-k^{2})v
. Then we have the following result
[
12
, Proposition 5.3]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

If
V
satisfies assumption (
2.1
) with
\rho>12
and
0<|k|<k_{0}
for sufficiently small
k_{0}
, the operator
M(k)^{-1}
has the expansion

\displaystyle M(k)^{-1}

\displaystyle=k^{-2}D_{2}+k^{-2}h(k)Q_{1}\tilde{T}_{1}Q_{1}+h(k)A_{1}+\eta^{-1}h(k)A_{2}+\eta^{-1}Q_{1}A_{3}Q_{1}+\tilde{R}(k),

where
h(k)=(c_{1}+\eta^{-1}c_{2})^{-1}
for some
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\tilde{R}(k)
is uniformly bounded for
0<|k|<k_{0}
,
D_{2},\tilde{T}_{1},A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
Q_{1}\geq Q_{2}
are orthogonal projections,
T_{1}=Q_{1}-Q_{2}
is (at most) a rank one projection and

\displaystyle\tilde{T}_{1}

\displaystyle=T_{1}-T_{1}C_{12}Q_{2}-Q_{2}C_{21}T_{1}+Q_{2}C_{22}Q_{2}

for some
C_{12},C_{21},C_{22}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

The projection
T_{1}
is related to the existence of resonances. For a non-zero resonance
\psi
, the projection
T_{1}
is given by
T_{1}=\langle\cdot,Uv\psi\rangle Uv\psi
. The value of the constant
c_{1}
is not important for our analysis, whilst we have

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(\langle v,Uv\psi\rangle\right)^{2}}{(8\pi^{2})},

(2.6)

see for example
[
2
, Lemma 3.2.36]
and
[
43
, Equation 2.2]
. To complete the analysis, we also need the small energy behaviour of
\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)v
, which we can obtain from
[
4
, Lemma 2.12]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose that
V
satisfies (
2.1
) for some
\rho>12
. Then in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{P})
we have the expansion

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)v

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma_{0}v+\lambda\gamma_{1}v+O(\lambda^{\frac{3}{2}})

as
\lambda\to 0
in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{P})
, where the operators
\gamma_{0}v,\gamma_{1}v\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{P})
are given for
f\in\mathcal{H}
by

\displaystyle[\gamma_{j}vf](\omega)

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{1}{2}}(2\pi)^{-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}{(-i\langle x,\omega\rangle)^{j}v(x)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}.

We can combine Theorem
2.4
and Lemma
2.5
to obtain an expansion of a related operator which will be useful in the next section. We use the convention that
\lambda>0
,
k=-i\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\eta=\left(\frac{\ln{(\lambda)}}{2}-i\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{-1}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose
V
satisfies the assumption (
2.1
) for some
\rho>12
. Then as
\lambda\to 0
we have the expansion

\displaystyle\left(U+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\right)^{-1}v\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}h(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})(T_{1}-Q_{2}C_{21}T_{1})v\gamma_{0}^{*}+D_{2}v\gamma_{1}^{*}+O(\eta)

in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

The identity
\gamma_{0}vQ_{2}=0=Q_{2}v\gamma_{0}^{*}

[
12
, Lemma 7.2]
yields
\tilde{T}_{1}v\gamma_{0}^{*}=(T_{1}-Q_{2}C_{21}T_{1})v\gamma_{0}^{*}
. Observing that as
\lambda\to 0
,
\eta\to 0
and
h(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})\to 0
, we multiply the expansions of Theorem
2.4
and Lemma
2.5
to obtain the statement.
∎

We summarise below some useful properties of the scattering matrix
[
42
, Proposition 1.8.1 and Proposition 8.1.9]
,
[
4
, Theorem 2.15 and Corollary 3.10]
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose that
V
satisfies the assumption (
2.1
) for some
\rho>1
. The scattering matrix
S(\lambda)
is given for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
by the equation

\displaystyle S(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}-2\pi i\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)v\left(U+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\right)^{-1}v\Gamma_{0}(\lambda)^{*}.

(2.7)

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the operator

is unitary in
\mathcal{P}=L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{3})
and depends continuously (in the sense of norm) on
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Furthermore, if

then
S(\lambda)-\textup{Id}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathcal{H})
, is differentiable in the norm of
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathcal{H})
and we have
S(0)=\textup{Id}
and
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}{S(\lambda)}=\textup{Id}
, where the limit is taken in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{P})
.

3
The form of the wave operator

In this section, we analyse the wave operator
W_{-}
in the space
\mathcal{H}_{spec}
(the spectral representation for
H_{0}
). Much of the analysis is identical to the two dimensional case in
[
3
]
, to which we refer for many details. The main result in this section is the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
V
satisfies the estimate (
2.1
) for some
\rho>12
. Then the wave operator
W_{-}
is given by

\displaystyle W_{-}

\displaystyle=\textup{Id}+\varphi(D_{4})(S-\textup{Id})+B_{res}+K,

(3.1)

where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is given by

\displaystyle\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh{(\pi x)}-i\cosh{(\pi x)}^{-1}\right),

B_{res}
is a bounded operator depending on the existence of resonances and
K
is a compact operator.

In Lemma
3.5
we will determine an explicit expression for the operator

\displaystyle\det(\Gamma_{2}(\ell))

\displaystyle=\det(S(\mathrm{e}^{2\ell})),\quad

\displaystyle\ell\in[0,\infty],

\displaystyle\det(\Gamma_{3}(\xi))

\displaystyle=1,\quad

\displaystyle\xi\in[\infty,0],

\displaystyle\det(\Gamma_{4}(s))

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2is-1}{2is+1}\right)^{\dim(P_{s})},\quad

\displaystyle=1,\quad

\displaystyle\xi\in[0,\infty],

\displaystyle\ell\in[\infty,0].

We now decouple the resonant contribution to the wave operator. We do this in order to isolate the numerical contribution of resonances to Levinson’s theorem.

Define the operators
W_{S},W_{R}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
by the equalities

\displaystyle F_{0}(W_{S}-\textup{Id})F_{0}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\varphi\left(-\frac{1}{2}D_{+}\right)(S(L)-\textup{Id}).

(4.1)

and

\displaystyle F_{0}(W_{R}-\textup{Id})

\displaystyle=-N\Xi B.

Then we have the following.

Lemma 4.3
.

The operators
W_{S}
and
W_{R}
are Fredholm and we have the equality

\displaystyle W_{-}

\displaystyle=W_{S}W_{R}+K

(4.2)

for a compact operator
K
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\textup{Index}(W_{-})

\displaystyle=\textup{Index}(W_{S})+\textup{Index}(W_{R}).

(4.3)

Proof.

To see that
W_{S}
defines a Fredholm operator, it is sufficient to note that
W_{S^{*}}
defines an inverse for
W_{S}
up to compacts (see
[
3
, Lemma 5.3]
). As in the proof of
[
3
, Proposition 5.5]
we have that
W_{R}^{*}
defines an inverse for
W_{R}
modulo compacts. We next observe that by Equation (
3.5
) we have the equality

\displaystyle W_{-}

\displaystyle=W_{S}+(W_{R}-\textup{Id})+K=W_{S}(\textup{Id}+W_{S}^{*}\left(W_{R}-\textup{Id}\right))+\tilde{K}

(4.4)

with
K,\tilde{K}
compact operators. Denote by
\Gamma_{S,j}
and
\Gamma_{R,j}
the components of
\mathcal{V}\mathcal{U}F_{0}W_{S}F_{0}^{*}\mathcal{U}^{*}\mathcal{V}^{*}
and
\mathcal{V}\mathcal{U}F_{0}W_{R}F_{0}^{*}\mathcal{U}^{*}\mathcal{V}^{*}
under the quotient map
q
. A proof similar to Proposition
4.1
shows that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S,1}(s)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}+\frac{1}{2}(S(1)-1)\begin{pmatrix}1&\phi(s)\\
\overline{\phi(s)}&1\end{pmatrix},\quad

\displaystyle s\in[-\infty,\infty],

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S,2}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}+\frac{1}{2}(S(\mathrm{e}^{2\ell})-1)\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad

\displaystyle\ell\in[0,\infty],

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S,3}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad

\displaystyle\xi\in[\infty,0],

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S,4}(s)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad

\displaystyle s\in[\infty,-\infty],

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S,5}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad

\displaystyle\xi\in[0,\infty],

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S,6}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

\displaystyle\Gamma_{R,4}(s)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

In particular we find

Lemma 4.4
.

We have the equality

\displaystyle\textup{Index}(W_{R})

\displaystyle=\dim(P_{s}).

Proof.

The operator
q(\mathcal{V}\mathcal{U}F_{0}W_{R}F_{0}^{*}\mathcal{U}^{*}\mathcal{V}^{*})
has components

\displaystyle\Gamma_{R,4}(s)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{2}{1+2is}P_{s}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad

\displaystyle s\in[\infty,-\infty],

\displaystyle\Gamma_{R,j}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad

\displaystyle j\in\{1,2,3,5,6\}.

The index of
W_{R}
can then be computed using Gohberg-Kreĭn theory
[
13
]
as the sum of winding numbers

\displaystyle\textup{Index}(W_{R})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{6}{\textup{Wind}(\Gamma_{R,j})}.

For
j\in\{1,2,3,5,6\}
we have
\textup{Wind}(\Gamma_{R,j})=0
. If
\dim(P_{s})=0
we have
\textup{Wind}(\Gamma_{R,4})=0
also. If
\dim(P_{s})=1
, then we find

\displaystyle\textup{Wind}(\Gamma_{R,4})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\det(\Gamma_{R,4}(s))}{\det(\Gamma_{R,4}(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\frac{4i}{4s^{2}+1}\,\mathrm{d}s}

\displaystyle=1,

from which the result follows.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

We have the equality

\displaystyle\textup{Index}(W_{-})

\displaystyle=\textup{Index}(W_{S})+\dim(P_{s}).

To determine an analytic formula for
\textup{Index}(W_{S})
requires a more subtle analysis of the high-energy behaviour of the scattering matrix (see for example
[
16
, Theorem III.1]
and
[
42
, Section 9.2]
) and we defer the proof to a future publication.
The analytic formula has previously been obtained by
[
25
]
, and reads

\displaystyle\textup{Index}(W_{-})=-N=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\textup{Tr}\left(S(\lambda)^{*}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\right)-c_{1}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda+\dim(P_{s})-\beta_{2}.

(4.5)

Here the constants are given by

c_{1}=-\frac{(2\pi i)\textup{Vol}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}{2(2\pi)^{4}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\mbox{and}\quad\beta_{2}=-\frac{\textup{Vol}(\mathbb{S}^{3})}{4(2\pi)^{4}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}V(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

The proof in
[
25
]
used high energy asymptotics of the spectral shift function, obtained using heat kernel methods. Our approach also uses high energy asymptotics for the spectral shift, but then utilises regularised determinants, the limiting absorption principle and Gohberg-Kreĭn theory.
