Quantitative unique continuation for Robin boundary value problems on C 1 , 1 C^{1,1} domains
Zongyuan Li, Weinan Wang

Abstract
. In this paper, we prove two unique continuation results for second order elliptic equations with Robin boundary conditions on C 1 , 1 C^{1,1} domains. The first one is a sharp vanishing order estimate of Robin problems with Lipschitz coefficients and differentiable, sign-changing potentials. This generalizes the result for the “Robin eigenfunctions” in [ 26 ] , which deals with the case with constant potentials. The second result is a unique continuation result from the boundary – any non-trivial solution cannot vanish at infinite order from the boundary or vanish on an open subset on the boundary. Such result generalizes the one in [ 1 ] for the Laplace equation on C 1 , 1 C^{1,1} domains with zero Neumann boundary conditions.

35J25, 35B99, 35J10

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we prove some unique continuation properties of the second-order divergence-form elliptic equation

(1.1)

\operatorname{div}(ADu)=Vu\,\,\text{in }\,\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad d\geq 3,

with Robin boundary condition

(1.2)

ADu\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\eta u\,\,\text{on }\,\partial\Omega.

We first study the strong unique continuation property (SUCP), which states that any nontrivial solution to an elliptic equation cannot vanish at an infinite order. Such property generalizes the commonly known fact for analytic (holomorphic) functions on the complex plane, and has been extensively studied. For instance, in
[
12
]
, a nearly optimal scaling invariant SUCP was proved for

\operatorname{div}(ADu+Bu)+W\cdot Du+Vu=0\quad\text{in}\,\,\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

with
A
being Lipschitz,
V\in L^{d/2}
, and
B,W
in spaces close to
L^{d}
. More precisely, if for some point
x_{0}\in\Omega
,

|u(x)|=O(|x-x_{0}|^{N})\quad\text{for any}\,\,N>0,

then
u\equiv 0
.
For the history of SUCP, one may refer to
[
12
]
and the references therein.

In the current paper, we prove the quantitative SUCP. We aim to find the sharp upper bound for the vanishing order
N
of nontrivial solutions to (
1.1
). Let us start from Laplace eigenfunctions, i.e., solutions to

\Delta u=-\lambda u.

From the spherical harmonics, one could simply see that solutions can vanish as fast as
|x-x_{0}|^{O(\sqrt{\lambda})}
, i.e., the upper bound is at least
O(\sqrt{\lambda})
. On the other hand, in
[
8
,
9
]
Donnelly and Fefferman showed that Laplace eigenfunctions can vanish at most of order
O(\sqrt{\lambda})
on compact smooth Riemannian manifolds (Dirichlet/Neumann boundary conditions are needed with the presence of the boundaries). Naturally, one expects the vanishing order bound
O(\sqrt{\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}})
for (
1.1
). However, later Meshkov in
[
19
]
discovered an example indicating that for complex potentials
V
(and hence, complex solutions), the vanishing order can be as large as
C\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2/3}
. Indeed, such order was proved to be a valid upper bound in
[
4
]
for the complex case. Since the method in
[
4
]
does not distinguish the real and complex cases, whether the order estimate
O(\sqrt{\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}})
holds for real
V
remains as an outstanding open question.

The study of the real case was initiated in
[
13
]
by Kukavica, who addressed the optimal vanishing rate of solutions to (
1.1
) with differentiable
V
. In this direction, the sharp upper bound
O(\|V\|_{W^{1,\infty}}^{1/2})
was proved in
[
2
]
and
[
25
]
using different methods, which recovers Donnelly-Fefferman’s result when
V=\lambda
. For merely bounded
V
, recently, remarkable progress was made in
[
17
]
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The authors proved the vanishing order estimate
O(\sqrt{\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}\log(\|V\|_{L^{\infty}})})
, which is sharp up to a log drift.

One could also study quantitative SUCP at a boundary point, if proper boundary conditions are given. Such problems with Dirichlet boundary conditions have been extensively studied in the literature, c.f.
[
1
,
6
,
15
,
16
,
24
]
. However, there are very few results on Neumann or Robin problems. In
[
1
]
, Adolfsson and Escauriaza proved that on
C^{1,1}
domain, harmonic functions with zero Neumann boundary condition cannot vanish at infinite order “from the bulk” or from the boundary, i.e., for any
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
and a non-trivial solution
u
, there exists a positive integer
N
, such that

(1.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x_{0})\cap\Omega}|u(x)|\geq Cr^{N},

(1.4)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x_{0})\cap\partial\Omega}|u(x)|\geq Cr^{N}.

In
[
23
]
, Tao and Zhang addressed the SUCP (
1.3
) for (
1.1
) with a Lipschitz coefficient matrix
A
, a potential
V
satisfying the so-called Kato-type condition, and the corresponding zero cornomal boundary condition. Moreover, in
[
23
]
, a restrictive (geometric) condition was assumed: for any
x\in\partial\Omega
close to
x_{0}
,

(1.5)

A(x)(x-x_{0})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}(x)=0,

where
\boldsymbol{n}
is the outward unit normal to
\partial\Omega
. Recently in
[
7
]
, (
1.3
)-(
1.4
) were proved for (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) with
\Omega
being a cone and
x_{0}
being its vertex. The leading coefficient matrix was assumed to be
A=a(x)I_{d}
, which means the geometric condition (
1.5
) also holds for their problem.

On the other hand, in the spirit of
[
8
,
9
]
, a quantitative SUCP was proved in
[
26
]
. More precisely, for the so-called Robin eigenfunctions on smooth domains, i.e., solutions to (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) with
A=Id_{d}
and
V=\lambda
,
\eta=\alpha
both being constants, we have (
1.3
) with
N=C(|\alpha|+\sqrt{|\lambda|})
.

In the current paper, we generalize the results in
[
1
]
and
[
26
]
to operators with Lipschitz coefficients
A
and Lipschitz potentials
V,\eta
. Our result also generalizes those in
[
23
]
(and to some extent,
[
7
]
), most importantly, by removing geometric condition (
1.5
) and proving a sharp vanishing order estimate.

Now we state our assumptions and main results. Consider the local problem

(1.6)

\begin{cases}\operatorname{div}(ADu)=Vu&\text{in }\,\Omega\cap B_{2},\\
ADu\cdot n=\eta u&\text{on }\,\partial\Omega\cap B_{2}.\end{cases}

We always assume
A=(a_{ij})
to be elliptic and symmetric: for some
\lambda\in(0,1]
,

(1.7)

\lambda I_{d}\leq A\leq\lambda^{-1}I_{d},\quad a_{ij}=a_{ji}.

Furthermore, we assume the potentials satisfy

(1.8)

M:=\|V\|_{W^{1,\infty}}<\infty\quad M_{\eta}:=\|\eta\|_{W^{1,\infty}}<\infty.

Assumption 1.1
.

We assume that
a_{ij}\in W^{1,1}_{loc}(\Omega)
, and that for some
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, there exists a positive function
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(r)
, such that for any
x\in\overline{\Omega}
with
|x-x_{0}|\leq 1
,

(1.9)

|Da_{ij}(x)|\leq\varepsilon(|x-x_{0}|)/|x-x_{0}|,\quad I_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{0}^{1}\varepsilon(r)/r\,dr<\infty.

Furthermore, we assume the conormal vector

(1.10)

A\boldsymbol{n}\in\operatorname{Lip}(\partial\Omega),

where
\boldsymbol{n}
is the outward unit normal to
\partial\Omega
.

Clearly, if
A
is Lipschitz and
\partial\Omega\in C^{1,1}
, then Assumption
1.1
is satisfied for any
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
.
Our first result is the sharp vanishing order estimate, “from the bulk”.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega\in C^{1,1}
and
0\in\partial\Omega
. Suppose that (
1.7
), (
1.8
), and Assumption
1.1
(with
x_{0}=0
) hold. Then there exist constants
R_{0},r_{0}\in(0,1)
depending on
(d,\lambda,I_{\varepsilon},\|\partial\Omega\|_{C^{1,1}},\|A\boldsymbol{n}\|_{C^{0,1}})
, such that the following assertions hold.

(A)

For any nontrivial
W^{1,2}
-weak solution
u
to (
1.6
) and any
r\leq R_{0}
,

\big(\fint_{\Omega\cap B_{r}}|u|^{2}\big)^{1/2}\geq Cr^{C(\sqrt{M}+M_{\eta}+\log(\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\cap B_{1})}/\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega\cap B_{r_{0}})})+1)}\big(\fint_{\Omega\cap B_{R_{0}}}|u|^{2}\big)^{1/2},

where
C
depends on
\lambda,d,\|\partial\Omega\|_{C^{1,1}},\|A\boldsymbol{n}\|_{C^{0,1}}
, and
I_{\varepsilon}
.

(B)

If we further assume that
\eta=-\eta_{0}<0
is a constant, then

\big(\fint_{\Omega\cap B_{r}}|u|^{2}\big)^{1/2}\geq Cr^{C(\sqrt{M}+\log(\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\cap B_{1})}/\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega\cap B_{r_{0}})})+1)}\big(\fint_{\Omega\cap B_{R_{0}}}|u|^{2}\big)^{1/2}.

For (A), our result is sharp even when
\eta
and
V
are constants, cf.,
[
26
]
. The significance of the result in (B) is that the vanishing order is independent of
\eta_{0}
: When
\eta_{0}\rightarrow-\infty
, this recovers the estimate for Dirichlet problems. Whether such independence still holds for non-constant, negative
\eta
, i.e.,

\eta=\eta(x)\leq\eta_{0}<0

remains open. Our next result is the unique continuation from the boundary.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega\in C^{1,1}
. Suppose that (
1.7
)-(
1.8
) hold and
a_{ij}
is Lipschitz. Then any nontrivial
W^{1,2}
-weak solution
u
to (
1.6
) cannot vanish at infinite order from the boundary. In other words, if for some
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega\cap B_{2}
and any
N>0
,

(1.11)

u(x)=O(|x-x_{0}|^{N}),\quad\text{as}\,\,x(\in\partial\Omega)\rightarrow x_{0},

then
u\equiv 0
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Under conditions of Theorem
1.3
, any nontrivial
W^{1,2}
-weak solution
u
to (
1.6
) cannot vanish on a subset of
\partial\Omega\cap B_{2}
with a positive surface measure.

Remark 1.5
.

While finishing this paper, the authors noticed a recent nice result in
[
5
]
by Burq and Zuily, regarding the quantitative unique continuation for conormal problems, i.e., (
1.6
) with
\eta=0
. We address the Robin boundary value problem, which is the main difference between
[
5
]
and the current paper. Essentially, after the flattening, they take the even extension for
u
and proper extensions for
a_{ij}
to the lower half space. Then, the original boundary becomes an interior surface, and the propogation of smallness result
[
18
]
by Logunov and Malinnikova applies. However, such extension is not available in the Robin setting.

Remark 1.6
.

After the completion of the current paper, the authors learned from G. Alessandrini about the papers by Sincich
[
21
,
22
]
in the context of inverse problems, where similar estimates were proved assuming the non-positivity of
\eta
.

The key step in the current paper is to prove a sharp doubling inequality:

\fint_{\Omega\cap B_{2r}}|u|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\cap B_{1})}}{\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega\cap B_{r_{0}})}}\right)^{C}e^{C(\sqrt{M}+M_{\eta}+1)}\fint_{\Omega\cap B_{r}}|u|^{2}.

See (
3.2
). For this, we prove the “almost monotonicity” of an Almgren-type frequency function. In a series of papers
[
10
,
11
]
, Garofalo and Lin pioneered the use of such frequency functions to study the SUCP of elliptic equations. Furthermore, we adopt an idea in
[
14
]
: the weight function
(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}
is allowed in such computation (essentially, in the radial deformation). Optimizing
\alpha
will lead us to sharp estimates. Similar ideas were also employed in
[
25
]
and
[
3
]
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we work on the half space. More precisely, we introduce the weighted frequency function on the half space, and prove the “almost monotonicity” in Proposition
2.1
, assuming the geometric condition (
1.5
). Then in Section
3.1
, we construct a change of variable which reduces the problem to the half-space case. From these, the doubling inequality in Proposition
3.2
is derived in Section
3.2
. Using the doubling inequality, we prove Theorem
1.2
(A) in Section
3.2
and Theorem
1.2
(B) in Section
4
. Eventually, Theorem
1.3
and Corollary
1.4
are proved in Section
5
.

2.
Frequency function and monotonicity on half space

In this section, we work on the half space
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}=\{(x^{\prime},x_{d}):x_{d}>0\}
. Let us denote

B_{r}^{+}=B_{r}(0)\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\quad\Gamma_{r}=B_{r}(0)\cap\partial\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}.

We consider the problem

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\operatorname{div}(ADu)=Vu&\text{in }\,B_{2}^{+},\\
ADu\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\eta u&\text{on }\,\Gamma_{2}.\end{cases}

Throughout this section, we assume (
1.7
)-(
1.8
), Assumption
1.1
with
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
and
x_{0}=0
, and the matrix
A
has a special shape

(2.2)

A=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{A}&0\\
0&a_{dd}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad A(0)=I_{d}.

One could simply check

\left<A(x)x,\boldsymbol{n}(x)\right>=0\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{2}.

As in
[
3
,
10
,
11
]
, we define the conformal factor

(2.3)

\mu(x)=\frac{\langle Ax,x\rangle}{|x|^{2}}.

For
\alpha\geq 1
and
r\in(0,2)
, we define the weighted height, energy, and frequency functions as

(2.4)

\begin{split}H(r)&:=\int_{B_{r}^{+}}|u(x)|^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}\mu(x)\,dx,\\
I(r)&:=2(\alpha+1)\int_{B_{r}^{+}}\left<A(x)Du(x),x\right>u(x)(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}\,dx,\\
N(r)&:=\frac{I(r)}{H(r)}.\end{split}

Now we state the main results in this section: the almost monotonicity of
N
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
r\in(0,1]
, we have

(2.5)

N^{\prime}(r)\geq-C\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}(r)}{r}N(r)-C\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}(r)}{r}(Mr+\alpha+M_{\eta}^{2}),

where
\tilde{\varepsilon}(r):=\varepsilon(r)+r
and
C=C(d,\lambda)
.

From Proposition
2.1
, we have the doubling property of
H
.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists a constant
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(d,\lambda,I_{\varepsilon})\in(0,1)
, such that the following assertion holds. For any
\kappa>1
,
R\in(0,1]
and
r\in(0,R/\kappa)
, there exists a constant
\delta\in(\delta_{0},1)
depending on
(d,\lambda,I_{\varepsilon},\kappa,r,R)
, such that

\log\left(\frac{H(\kappa r)}{H(r)}\right)\leq C\log\left(\frac{H(R)}{H(R/\kappa^{\delta})}\right)+C(\alpha+1)^{-1}(M+\alpha+\alpha^{2}+M_{\eta}^{2}).

Here
C=C(d,\lambda,I_{\varepsilon},\kappa)
.

The rest of this section will be devoted to the proofs of Proposition
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
. To compute
N^{\prime}
, we need
H^{\prime}
and
I^{\prime}
, which are related to the first and second variations of
H
. The computation of
H^{\prime}
is straightforward, which can be found in Section
2.2
. The computation of
I^{\prime}
is in Section
2.4
, which is based on the weighted inequalities obtained in Section
2.3
. Eventually, we prove Proposition
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
in Section
2.5
.

2.1.
Some preliminaries

Testing (
1.6
) by
u(x)(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha+1}
and doing the integration by parts, we can rewrite
I
in an equivalent form

(2.6)

\begin{split}I(r)&=\int_{B_{r}^{+}}\left<ADu,Du\right>(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha+1}+\int_{B_{r}^{+}}V|u|^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha+1}\\
&\quad-\int_{\Gamma_{r}}\eta|u|^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha+1}\\
&=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.\end{split}

Clearly
I_{1}>0
, while
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
might be sign-changing. We define the majorants

(2.7)

\widetilde{I}_{2}:=\int_{B_{r}^{+}}|V||u|^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha+1},\quad\widetilde{I}_{3}:=\int_{\Gamma_{r}}|\eta||u|^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha+1}.

Next, we discuss some properties of
\mu
defined in (
2.3
). First of all, from (
1.7
) and the fact that
A(0)=I_{d}
,

(2.8)

\lambda\leq\mu\leq\lambda^{-1},\quad\mu(0)=1.

From (
1.9
), we see

(2.9)

|\mu(x)-\mu(0)|\leq C\varepsilon(|x|).

Furthermore, we have

(2.10)

\begin{split}&|D_{i}\mu|=\left|D_{i}\left(\frac{(a_{jk}-\delta_{jk})x_{k}x_{j}}{|x|^{2}}\right)\right|\\
&=\left|\frac{D_{i}(a_{jk}-\delta_{jk})x_{k}x_{j}+(a_{jk}-\delta_{jk})D_{i}(x_{k}x_{j})}{|x|^{2}}-2\frac{(a_{jk}-\delta_{jk})x_{k}x_{j}x_{i}}{|x|^{4}}\right|\leq C(d)\frac{\varepsilon(|x|)}{|x|}.\end{split}

The following vector field is used in our computation:

\beta:=(\beta_{1}(x),\cdots,\beta_{d}(x))^{T}=Ax/\mu.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
\beta
defined above, the following hold

(2.11)

\displaystyle\beta(x)\cdot x=|x|^{2},

(2.12)

\displaystyle|x|\leq|\beta(x)|\leq\lambda^{-2}|x|,

(2.13)

\displaystyle|D_{i}\beta_{k}-\delta_{ik}|\leq C(d,\lambda)\varepsilon(|x|).

Proof.

By the definitions of
\beta
and
\mu
,

\beta\cdot x=\frac{Ax}{\mu}\cdot x=\frac{Ax\cdot x}{(Ax\cdot x)/|x|^{2}}=|x|^{2},

which is (
2.11
). From this, the lower bound in (
2.12
) follows simply by the Cauchy inequality. For the upper bound in (
2.12
), from (
1.7
), we have

|\beta|=\frac{|Ax|}{(Ax\cdot x)/|x|^{2}}\leq\frac{\lambda^{-1}|x|}{\lambda}=\lambda^{-2}|x|.

The computation for derivatives in (
2.13
) requires some more work:

(2.14)

\begin{split}D_{i}\beta_{k}=D_{i}\left(\frac{a_{kl}x_{l}}{\mu}\right)&=\frac{(D_{i}a_{kl})x_{l}}{\mu}+\frac{a_{kl}\delta_{il}}{\mu}-\frac{a_{kl}x_{l}D_{i}\mu}{\mu^{2}}\\
&=\frac{(D_{i}a_{kl})x_{l}}{\mu}+\frac{(a_{kl}-\delta_{kl})\delta_{il}}{\mu(0)}+\frac{\delta_{kl}\delta_{il}}{\mu(0)}+\delta_{kl}\delta_{il}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{1}{\mu(0)}\right)-\frac{a_{kl}x_{l}D_{i}\mu}{\mu^{2}}.\end{split}

From this, we obtain (
2.13
) by noting
\mu(0)=1
, (
1.9
), (
2.8
), (
2.9
), (
2.10
), and (
2.14
).
∎

2.2.
First variation of
H

Recall the definitions of
H,I
in (
2.4
). In this section, we compute
H^{\prime}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
u\in W^{1,2}(B_{2}^{+})
solves (
2.1
). Then for any
r\in(0,2)
,

H^{\prime}(r)=\frac{2\alpha+d+O(1)\varepsilon(r)}{r}H(r)+\frac{I(r)}{(\alpha+1)r}.

Here and throughout the section,
O(1)
represents a function which is bounded as
r\rightarrow 0
.

Proof.

Recall the definition of
H
in (
2.4
),

\displaystyle H^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2\alpha\int_{B_{r}^{+}}ru^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha-1}\mu=2\alpha\int_{B_{r}^{+}}\left(\frac{r^{2}-|x|^{2}}{r}+\frac{|x|^{2}}{r}\right)u^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha-1}\frac{\langle Ax,x\rangle}{|x|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\alpha}{r}H(r)+\frac{2\alpha}{r}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}\langle Ax,x\rangle u^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha-1}=\frac{2\alpha}{r}H(r)-\frac{2\alpha}{r}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}Axu^{2}\frac{D(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}}{2\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\alpha}{r}H(r)-\frac{1}{r}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}Axu^{2}D(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}.

For the second term, we do integration by parts. Noting that

\partial B_{r}^{+}=\Gamma_{r}\cup\{\partial B_{r}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\}

and

Ax\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\,\,\text{on}\,\,\Gamma_{r},\quad(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}=0\,\,\text{on}\,\,\partial B_{r},

we have

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{r}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}Axu^{2}D(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{r}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}}u^{2}(Ax\cdot n)(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{r}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}\text{div}(u^{2}Ax)(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}2u\langle ADu,x\rangle(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{r}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}u^{2}\text{div}(Ax)(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{I(r)}{(\alpha+1)r}+\frac{1}{r}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}u^{2}\partial_{i}(a_{ij}x_{j})(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{I(r)}{(\alpha+1)r}+\frac{1}{r}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}u^{2}\big(\operatorname{tr}(I_{d})+\operatorname{tr}(A-I_{d})+x_{j}\partial_{i}a_{ij}\big)(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{I(r)}{(\alpha+1)r}+\frac{d}{r}H(r)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{r}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}u^{2}\big(d(\mu(0)-\mu)+\operatorname{tr}(A-I_{d})+x_{j}\partial_{i}a_{ij}\big)(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}.

By (
1.9
), (
2.8
), and (
2.9
), we get

\displaystyle|d(\mu(0)-\mu)+\operatorname{tr}(A-I_{d})+x_{j}\partial_{i}a_{ij}|\leq C(d,\lambda)\varepsilon(r)\mu.

Combining all above yields the desired conclusion.
∎

2.3.
Some weighted inequalities

In this section, we prove some weighted inequalities which are needed in the computation of
I^{\prime}
. Throughout section
2.3
, we do not use the equation – the inequalities hold for any Sobolev functions
u
.

Recall the definition of
I_{1}
in (
2.6
).

Lemma 2.5
.

For any
u\in W^{1,2}(B_{r}^{+})
and
\alpha\geq 1
,

\alpha^{2}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}u^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha-1}|x|^{2}\leq C(\alpha H(r)+I_{1}(r)),

where
C=C(d,\lambda)
.

Proof.

By the divergence theorem, we see

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}u^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha-1}|x|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\alpha/2\int_{B_{r}^{+}}u^{2}x\cdot\nabla(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}=\alpha/2\int_{B_{r}^{+}}\text{div}(u^{2}x)(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=d\alpha/2\int_{B_{r}^{+}}u^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}+\alpha\int_{B_{r}^{+}}u(x\cdot\nabla u)(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\alpha\int_{B_{r}^{+}}u(x\cdot\nabla u)(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha}=\alpha\int_{B_{r}^{+}}u(x\cdot\nabla u)\left((r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha+1}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(2.15)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}u^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha-1}|x|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}|\nabla u|^{2}(r^{2}-|x|^{2})^{\alpha+1}

(2.16)

\mathbb{R}^{d}\cap B_{R_{0}}
for some

, with coefficients satisfying (

Since

, we know that there exists a global

. However, in application it is more convenient to work with distance with higher derivatives in the interior, which is the so-called “regularized distance”.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a distance function
\rho\in C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega})
such that

(a)

cd(x)\leq\rho(x)\leq Cd(x)
.

(b)

|D\rho|\geq C_{0}
on
\partial\Omega
.

(c)

|D^{k+2}\rho|\rho^{k}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, for any
k\geq 1
.

Such

can be constructed by simply mollifying

and thus we omit the proof. Now we construct our change of variable. Without loss of generality, suppose locally
D_{x_{d}}\rho\neq 0
.

Step (i)
: Flattening the boundary. We set

\begin{cases}z^{\prime}=x^{\prime},\\
z_{d}=\rho(x),\end{cases}

which locally takes
\Omega
to
\{z_{d}>0\}
. Clearly, such transformation is two-way
C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{1,1}(\overline{\Omega})
, with norms depending only on
\Omega
. Direct computation shows

\det\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)=\det\begin{pmatrix}I_{d-1}&0\\
(D_{x^{\prime}}\rho)^{T}&D_{x_{d}}\rho\end{pmatrix}=D_{x_{d}}\rho

and

d\sigma_{x}=\frac{|D_{x}\rho|}{D_{x}\rho\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{d}}d\sigma_{z}=\frac{|D_{x}\rho|}{D_{x_{d}}\rho}d\sigma_{z}\quad\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega=\{\rho=0\}=\{z_{d}=0\}.

In
z
-coordinates, the problem becomes

\begin{cases}\text{div}_{z}\left(A_{(z)}D_{z}u\right)=\frac{1}{D_{x_{d}}\rho}Vu\quad\text{in}\,\,B_{r}^{+}(0),\\
\big((\boldsymbol{n}_{A_{(z)}}\cdot D_{z})u\big)\cdot(-\boldsymbol{e}_{d})=\frac{|D_{x}\rho|}{D_{x_{d}}\rho}\eta u\quad\text{on}\,\,B_{r}(0)\cap\{z_{d}=0\}.\end{cases}

where

A_{(z)}=\frac{1}{D_{x_{d}}\rho}\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}A(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x})^{T}

and its conormal vector

\boldsymbol{n}_{A_{(z)}}=\frac{1}{D_{x_{d}}\rho}\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}A(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x})^{T}(-\boldsymbol{e}_{d}).

Step (ii)
: Mapping the conormal vector field to normal directions. More precisely, we aim to construct new coordinates
y=(y^{\prime},y_{d})
, such that we have the push-forward

(3.1)

\big(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\big)_{\#}\left(\frac{D_{x_{d}}\rho}{|D_{x}\rho|}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{d}}\right)=\boldsymbol{n}_{A_{(z)}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\quad\text{on}\,\,\{z_{d}=0\}\big(=\{y_{d}=0\}\big).

We will achieve this in two steps. First, we construct

with

\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}\right)_{\#}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{d}}\right)=\frac{1}{\mu_{d}}\boldsymbol{n}_{A_{(z)}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\quad\text{on}\,\,\{z_{d}=0\}\big(=\{y_{d}=0\}\big),

where by ellipticity

\mu_{d}:=\left<\boldsymbol{n}_{A_{(z)}},-\boldsymbol{e}_{d}\right>=\left<\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}A(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x})^{T}(-\boldsymbol{e}_{d}),(-\boldsymbol{e}_{d})\right>\geq C\lambda.

Thus, we can define
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\tau}(z^{\prime},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{(d-1)\times 1}
on
\{z_{d}=0\}
to be the first
(d-1)
components of
\boldsymbol{n}_{A_{(z)}}/\mu_{d}
, i.e.,

\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\tau}(z^{\prime},0)\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\boldsymbol{n}_{A_{(z)}}}{\mu_{d}}\bigg|_{z_{d}=0}.

From our construction,

\big(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\big)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{d}=D\rho.

Noting in
x
-coordinates the unit outward normal
\boldsymbol{n}=-D\rho/|D\rho|
and (
1.10
), we have

\boldsymbol{n}_{A_{(z)}}=\frac{1}{D_{x_{d}}\rho}\big(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\big)A\big(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\big)^{T}(-\boldsymbol{e}_{d})\in C^{0,1}.

Hence,
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in C^{0,1}
. Now, we can extend
\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\mu_{d}
to the upper half space, then mollify to obtain
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\widetilde{\mu}_{d}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}})\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
, with

z_{d}^{k-1}|D^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|\leq C(\|\tau\|_{C^{0,1}},\Omega)\,\,\text{and}\,\,z_{d}^{k-1}|D^{k}\widetilde{\mu}_{d}|\leq C(\|\mu\|_{C^{0,1}},\Omega)\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}.

Define

\begin{cases}w^{\prime}=z^{\prime}-z_{d}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\\
w_{d}=z_{d}.\end{cases}

Clearly such transformation takes
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
with

\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d-1}-z_{d}D_{z^{\prime}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}&-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-z_{d}D_{z_{d}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Restricted on the boundary, i.e. on
\{w_{d}=0\}=\{z_{d}=0\}

\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}=\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\right)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d-1}&\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore in
w
-coordinates, the boundary condition becomes

\frac{\partial u}{\partial w_{d}}=\frac{1}{\mu_{d}}\frac{|D_{x}\rho|}{D_{x_{d}}\rho}\eta u.

One could check that the transformation

is also two-way
C^{1,1}
. For example, the most singular term in

is
-z_{d}D^{2}_{w}\widetilde{\tau}
, which is bounded according to Lemma
3.1
.

Step (iii)
: We are left to do one more normalization by letting

\begin{cases}y^{\prime}=w^{\prime},\\
y_{d}=\frac{\widetilde{\mu}_{d}D_{x_{d}}\rho}{|D_{x}\rho|}w_{d}.\end{cases}

As before, using Lemma
3.1
and
w_{d}\approx z_{d}
, one may check that

is two-way
C^{1,1}
. Clearly, such

satisfies (
3.1

In the new coordinates

and
\eta

Let

Furthermore, replacing

From this and Lemma
2.2

\begin{split}\log\left(\frac{\tilde{h}(\kappa r/2)}{\tilde{h}(r)}\right)&\leq\log\left(\frac{H(\kappa r)}{H(r)}\right)-2\log(\lambda)-C\alpha\log(\kappa)\\

5.
Unique continuation from the boundary

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
. Using the change of variable in Section

, without loss of generality, we may assume
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
and
u
solves (
2.1
). We first prove a quantitative Cauchy uniqueness result.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any constant
\delta\in(0,1)
and
r\in(0,1)
,

(5.1)

\int_{B_{r}^{+}}|u|^{2}\leq Cr\int_{\Gamma_{2r}}|u|^{2}+\delta\int_{B_{2r}^{+}}|u|^{2}.

where

Before its proof, let us explain that although our Robin problem has a sign-changing

, the following Caccioppoli inequality and the local maximum principle still hold:

\fint_{B_{1/2}^{+}}|Du|^{2}\leq C\fint_{B_{1}^{+}}|u|^{2},\quad\|u\|_{L_{\infty}(B_{1/2}^{+})}\leq C\fint_{B_{1}^{+}}|u|^{2}.

The only difference is that the constant

here also depends on

. For the proof of Caccioppoli inequality, we need to use the interpolation-type trace theorem in the form of Lemma
2.6
(without the weight) and a standard iteration argument. From the Caccioppoli inequality, the local maximum principle simply follows.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

The proof is by contradiction, which is similar to that of
[
1
, Lemma 3.1]
. By rescaling, we only need to prove for
r=1
. Now suppose (
5.1
) fails, then there exist a sequence of function

, satisfying

\begin{cases}\operatorname{div}(A^{(n)}Du^{(n)})=V^{(n)}u^{(n)}&\text{in }\,B_{2}^{+},\\
A^{(n)}Du^{(n)}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\eta^{(n)}u^{(n)}&\text{on }\,\Gamma_{2},\end{cases}

where

with their elliptic constants being bounded by
\lambda
and Lipschitz constants being bounded by

,
\|V^{(n)}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\leq M
, and
\|\eta^{(n)}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\leq M_{\eta}
. After normalization, such

satisfies

(5.3)

\int_{B_{1}^{+}}|u^{(n)}|^{2}=1,

\int_{\Gamma_{2}}|u^{(n)}|^{2}\leq 1/n,\quad\text{and}\,\,\int_{B_{2}^{+}}|u^{(n)}|^{2}\leq 1/\delta.

From these, by the Caccioppoli inequality,

(5.5)

\int_{B_{3/2}^{+}}|Du^{(n)}|^{2}\leq C/\delta,

where

is independent of

. Denoting
v^{(n)}
and

From this, by passing to a subsequence, we have

(v^{(n)},f^{(n)})\rightarrow(v,f)\quad\text{weakly in}\,\,L^{2}\times(L^{2})^{d}.

Furthermore, from (

),

v^{(n)}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{strongly in}\,\,L^{2}(\Gamma_{2}).

Also, we take extensions of

, and
\eta^{(n)}
to the lower half space, with their Lipschitz norms being bounded. By the Arzela-Ascoli theorem, passing to a subsequence, we have the following uniform convergence on

Now we aim to prove

Since

Passing

Proof of Corollary

.
