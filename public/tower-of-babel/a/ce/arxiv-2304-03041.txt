Multi-Linear Kernel Regression and Imputation in Data Manifolds
Duc Thien Nguyen, Konstantinos Slavakis 1

Abstract
This paper introduces an efficient multi-linear nonparametric (kernel-based) approximation framework for data regression and imputation, and its application to dynamic magnetic-resonance imaging (dMRI). Data features are assumed to reside in or close to a smooth manifold embedded in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. Landmark points are identified to describe concisely the point cloud of features by linear approximating patches which mimic the concept of tangent spaces to smooth manifolds. The multi-linear model effects dimensionality reduction, enables efficient computations, and extracts data patterns and their geometry without any training data or additional information. Numerical tests on dMRI data under severe under-sampling demonstrate remarkable improvements in efficiency and accuracy of the proposed approach over its predecessors, popular data modeling methods, as well as recent tensor-based and deep-image-prior schemes.

III
Data Modeling

With
\varphi(\cdot)
denoting the feature mapping which maps vector
\mathbfit{l}_{k}
to vector

\varphi(\mathbfit{l}_{k})
in the feature space
\mathscr{H}
, the crux of the modeling approach
is that
\{\varphi(\mathbfit{l}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N_{\mathit{l}}}
lie into or close to an
unknown-to-the-user smooth manifold
\mathscr{M}

[
39
]
embedded in

\mathscr{H}
; see Fig.
2
. To provide structured solutions, it is
assumed that
\mathscr{H}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), equipped with a
reproducing kernel
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{C}^{\nu}\times\mathbb{C}^{\nu}\to\mathbb{C}
,
with well-documented merits in approximation theory
[
40
]
. To this end,
the feature mapping
\varphi(\cdot)
is defined as the mapping induced by the kernel
\kappa

of
\mathscr{H}
; cf.,
[
36
]
. A complex-valued
\mathscr{H}
is considered
here
[
41
,
36
]
.

Fig. 2:
A “collaborative-filtering” approach: Points

\{\varphi(\mathbfit{l}_{k_{j}})\}_{j=1}^{3}
, which lie into or close to the
unknown-to-the-user manifold
\mathscr{M}\subset\mathscr{H}
, collaborate affinely to
approximate
\varphi(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{t})
. All affine combinations of

\{\varphi(\mathbfit{l}_{k_{j}})\}_{j=1}^{3}
define the approximating “linear patch”
(gray-colored plane), which mimics the concept of a tangent space to
\mathscr{M}
.

The
(i,t)
th entry
y_{it}
of data
\mathbf{Y}
is approximated as

y_{it}\approx f_{i}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{t})
, where
f_{i}(\cdot):\mathbb{C}^{\nu}\to\mathbb{C}

is an unknown non-linear function that belongs to the functional space
\mathscr{H}
, and

\check{\bm{\mu}}_{t}
is an unknown complex-valued
\nu\times 1
vector. The well-known
reproducing property
[
40
]
of the RKHS
\mathscr{H}
suggests that

y_{it}\approx f_{i}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{t})=\langle f_{i}\mathop{}|\mathop{}\varphi(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{t})\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}
, where
\langle\cdot\mathop{}|\mathop{}\cdot\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}
stands for
the inner product of
\mathscr{H}
.

The concept of tangent spaces
[
39
]
(“linear patches” in simple words) to
manifolds will be used to model both
f_{i}
and
\varphi(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{t})
. Specifically,
it is assumed that

(i)

f_{i}
belongs to the linear span of

\{\varphi(\mathbfit{l}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N_{\mathit{l}}}
, i.e., there exists

\mathbf{d}_{i}\coloneqq[d_{i1},\ldots,d_{iN_{\mathit{l}}}]^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathit{l}}}
(
\intercal
denotes vector/matrix transposition) s.t.
f_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathit{l}}}d_{ik}\varphi(\mathbfit{l}_{k})=\bm{\Phi}(\mathbf{L})\mathbf{d}_{i}
, where

\bm{\Phi}(\mathbf{L})\coloneqq[\varphi(\mathbfit{l}_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(\mathbfit{l}_{N_{\mathit{l}}})]
; and

(ii)

\varphi(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{t})
lies into or close to
\mathscr{M}
and is approximated

affinely
by only a few members of

\{\varphi(\mathbfit{l}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N_{\mathit{l}}}
, i.e., there exists a sparse

N_{\mathit{l}}\times 1
vector
\mathbf{b}_{t}
s.t.
\varphi(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{t})=\bm{\Phi}(\mathbf{L})\mathbf{b}_{t}
, under the affine
constraint
\mathbf{1}_{N_{\mathit{l}}}^{{\mathsf{H}}}\mathbf{b}_{t}=1
, where

\mathbf{1}_{N_{\mathit{l}}}
is the
N_{\mathit{l}}\times 1
all-one vector and
\mathsf{H}

denotes complex conjugate vector/matrix transposition; cf., Fig.
2
.

In other words,

y_{it}\approx f_{i}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{t})=\langle f_{i}\mathop{}|\mathop{}\varphi(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{t})\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}=\langle\bm{\Phi}(\mathbf{L})\mathbf{d}_{i}\mathop{}|\mathop{}\bm{\Phi}(\mathbf{L})\mathbf{b}_{t}\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}=\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{b}_{t}
,
where
\mathbf{K}
is the complex-valued
N_{\mathit{l}}\times N_{\mathit{l}}
matrix whose

(k,k^{\prime})
th entry is equal to

\langle\varphi(\mathbfit{l}_{k})\mathop{}|\mathop{}\varphi(\mathbfit{l}_{k^{\prime}})\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}=\kappa(\mathbfit{l}_{k},\mathbfit{l}_{k^{\prime}})
. To offer compact notations, if

\mathbf{D}\coloneqq[\mathbf{d}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{N_{\text{k}}}]^{{\mathsf{H}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{k}}\times N_{\mathit{l}}}
and

\mathbf{B}\coloneqq[\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{N_{\text{fr}}}]\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathit{l}}\times N_{\text{fr}}}
, then data are modeled as

\mathbf{Y}\approx\mathbf{D}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{B}
.

Choosing
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)
to define
\mathbf{K}
entails the cumbersome tasks of cross
validation and fine tuning via extensive experimentation on data sets. A popular way to
surmount such cumbersome tasks, followed also in
[
36
]
, is via multiple
kernels:
\mathbf{Y}\approx\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{D}_{m}\mathbf{K}_{m}\mathbf{B}_{m}
, with a dictionary of
user-defined reproducing kernels
\{\kappa_{m}(\cdot,\cdot)\}_{m=1}^{M}
, and thus kernel
matrices
\{\mathbf{K}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, complex-valued
N_{\text{k}}\times N_{\mathit{l}}

matrices
\{\mathbf{D}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, and
N_{\mathit{l}}\times N_{\text{fr}}
sparse
matrices
\{\mathbf{B}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
satisfying

\mathbf{1}^{{\mathsf{H}}}_{N_{\mathit{l}}}\mathbf{B}_{m}=\mathbf{1}_{N_{\text{fr}}}^{{\mathsf{H}}}
.

To reduce the computational burden and effect low-rank constraints in the resultant inverse
problem, each
N_{\mathit{l}}\times N_{\mathit{l}}
matrix
\mathbf{K}_{m}
is substituted
in
[
36
]
by its low-dimensional
d\times N_{\mathit{l}}
rendition

\check{\mathbf{K}}_{m}
in

\mathbf{Y}\approx\sum_{m=1}^{M}\check{\mathbf{D}}_{m}\check{\mathbf{K}}_{m}\mathbf{B}_{m}
, with

d\ll N_{\mathit{l}}
and where
\{\check{\mathbf{D}}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
are low-rank

N_{\text{k}}\times d
matrices. To this end,
\check{\mathbf{K}}_{m}
was computed from

\mathbf{K}_{m}
via a dimensionality-reduction module (pre-step)
in
[
36
]
. However, such a dimensionality-reduction pre-step introduces the
following drawbacks:

(i)

when the numbers
M
of kernels and
N_{\mathit{l}}
of landmark points are large, the
dimensionality-reduction module inflicts heavy computations, while fine-tuning its
hyperparameters becomes a labor-intensive task; and

(ii)

the error from compressing
\mathbf{K}_{m}
into
\check{\mathbf{K}}_{m}
may propagate to the
next phase in the KRIM framework.

MultiL-KRIM avoids the previous drawbacks as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}\approx\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{D}_{m}^{(1)}\mathbf{D}_{m}^{(2)}\cdots\mathbf{D}_{m}^{(Q)}\mathbf{K}_{m}\mathbf{B}_{m}\,,

(1)

where
\mathbf{D}_{m}^{(q)}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{q-1}\times d_{q}}
, the inner matrix dimensions

\{d_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q-1}
are user-defined, with
d_{0}\coloneqq N_{\text{k}}
and

d_{Q}\coloneqq N_{\mathit{l}}
. Notice that for
Q=2
, the term
\mathbf{D}_{m}^{(2)}\mathbf{K}_{m}

may be considered as the dimensionality-reduced
\check{\mathbf{K}}_{m}

in
[
36
]
. Nonetheless,
\{\mathbf{D}_{m}^{(q)}\}_{(q,m)}
are identified
during a single-stage learning task, avoiding any pre-steps with their hyperparameter tuning
and errors. Note that
Q>1
in (
1
) may offer considerable savings in
computations with respect to the
Q=1
case. Indeed, the number of unknowns that need to be
identified in (
1
) for
Q>1
is

N_{Q>1}=M(\sum_{q=1}^{Q}d_{q-1}d_{q}+N_{\text{fr}}N_{\mathit{l}})
, as opposed to

N_{Q=1}=M(N_{\text{k}}N_{\mathit{l}}+N_{\text{fr}}N_{\mathit{l}})
in the
Q=1

case. If
N_{\mathit{l}}
is large, then
\{d_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q-1}
can be chosen so that

N_{Q>1}\ll N_{Q=1}
.

IV
Inverse Problem And its Iterative Solution

Letting

\mathbfcal{D}_{1}\coloneqq[\mathbf{D}_{1}^{(1)},\mathbf{D}_{2}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{D}_{M}^{(1)}]\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{k}}\times d_{1}M}
,

\mathbfcal{B}\coloneqq[\mathbf{B}_{1}^{\mathsf{H}},\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\mathsf{H}},\ldots,\mathbf{B}_{M}^{\mathsf{H}}]^{\mathsf{H}}\in\mathbb{C}^{MN_{\mathit{l}}\times N_{\text{fr}}}
, and
the block diagonal matrices

\mathbfcal{D}_{q}\coloneqq\bdiag{(\mathbf{D}_{1}^{(q)},\mathbf{D}_{2}^{(q)},\ldots,\mathbf{D}_{M}^{(q)})}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{q-1}M\times d_{q}M}
,
q\in\{2,\ldots,Q\}
,

\mathbfcal{K}\coloneqq\bdiag{(\mathbf{K}_{1},\mathbf{K}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{K}_{M})}\in\mathbb{C}^{MN_{\mathit{l}}\times MN_{\mathit{l}}}
, (
1
) takes the
form

\mathbf{Y}\approx\mathbfcal{D}_{1}\mathbfcal{D}_{2}\cdots\mathbfcal{D}_{Q}\mathbfcal{K}\mathbfcal{B}
.

In dMRI, it is often the case that inverse problems are formulated in the image domain, since,
after all, this is the domain where the end-product lies in. To this end, by using
\mathbf{X}

to denote the image-domain data and by defining

\mathbfcal{A}_{1}\coloneqq\mathscr{F}^{-1}(\mathbfcal{D}_{1})
(let also

\mathbfcal{A}_{q}\coloneqq\mathbfcal{D}_{q},\forall q\in\{2,\ldots,Q\}
, for uniform
notations),
the following inverse problem is postulated:

\displaystyle\min_{(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z},\{\mathbfcal{A}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q},\mathbfcal{B})}{}\

\displaystyle{}\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{X}-\mathbfcal{A}_{1}\mathbfcal{A}_{2}\cdots\mathbfcal{A}_{Q}\mathbfcal{K}\mathbfcal{B}\rVert^{2}_{\text{F}}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{1}\lVert\mathbfcal{B}\rVert_{1}+g(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z})+h(\{\mathbfcal{A}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q})

(2a)

s.to

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{Y})=\mathscr{S}\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{X})\,,

(2b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{N_{\mathit{l}}}^{{\mathsf{H}}}\mathbf{B}_{m}=\mathbf{1}_{N_{\text{fr}}}^{{\mathsf{H}}}\,,\forall m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}\,,

(2c)

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{A}_{q}\text{is block diagonal},\forall q\in\{2,\ldots,Q\}\,,

(2d)

where (
2b
) enforces consistency of the desired
\mathbf{X}
with the
data collected in the (k,t) domain,
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}
in (
2a
) is used
to impose sparsity on
\mathbfcal{B}
, and the convex regularizing functions
g(\cdot)
and

h(\cdot)
are used to incorporate prior knowledge. More specifically,
g(\cdot)
employs not
only variable
\mathbf{X}
but also the auxiliary
\mathbf{Z}
to facilitate computations. For
example, in the case where the dMRI data capture a periodic organ movement over a static
background, then

g(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z})\coloneqq(\lambda_{2}/2)\lVert\mathbf{Z}-\mathscr{F}_{\text{t}}(\mathbf{X})\rVert_{\text{F}}^{2}+\lambda_{3}\lVert\mathbf{Z}\rVert_{1}
, where

\mathscr{F}_{\text{t}}(\cdot)
stands for the temporal 1D DFT operator which acts on rows of
the matrix
\mathbf{X}
. Note that a row of
\mathbf{X}
corresponds to the time series, of
length
N_{\text{fr}}
, of a single pixel in the image domain. Additionally, the designer can
choose

h(\{\mathbfcal{A}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q})\coloneqq(\lambda_{4}/2)\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\lVert\mathbfcal{A}_{q}\rVert_{\text{F}}^{2}
to avoid unbounded solutions which may appear due to

\mathbfcal{A}_{1}\mathbfcal{A}_{2}\cdots\mathbfcal{A}_{Q}
in (
2
).

,

, and

2:

3:

Available are

and

.

4:

\gamma_{n+1}\coloneqq\gamma_{n}(1-\zeta\gamma_{n})
.

5:

Solve sub-tasks (
3
).

6:

\hat{\mathbfcal{S}}^{(n+1)}\coloneqq\gamma_{n+1}\hat{\mathbfcal{S}}^{(n+1/2)}+(1-\gamma_{n+1})\hat{\mathbfcal{S}}^{(n)}
.

7:

Set

and go to step

.

8:

end

while

Alg.
1
sketches a solution to (
2
), where

\hat{\mathbfcal{S}}^{(n+k/2)}\coloneqq(\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n+k/2)},\hat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(n+k/2)},\hat{\mathbfcal{A}}_{1}^{(n+k/2)},\ldots,\hat{\mathbfcal{A}}_{Q}^{(n+k/2)},\hat{\mathbfcal{B}}^{(n+k/2)})
,
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\forall k\in\{0,1\}
. Alg.

is based on the successive-convex-approximation framework
of
[

]
, which guarantees convergence to a stationary point of the
loss. The following convex sub-tasks need to be solved per iteration:

\forall q\in\{1,\ldots,Q\}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n+1/2)}

\displaystyle\in\arg\min_{{\mathbf{X}}}{}

\displaystyle{}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert{\mathbf{X}}-\hat{\mathbfcal{A}}_{1}^{(n)}\cdots\hat{\mathbfcal{A}}_{Q}^{(n)}\mathbfcal{K}\hat{\mathbfcal{B}}^{(n)}\rVert_{\text{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle{}\ +\tfrac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\lVert\hat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(n)}-\mathscr{F}_{\text{t}}({\mathbf{X}})\rVert_{\text{F}}^{2}+\tfrac{\tau_{X}}{2}\lVert\mathbf{X}-\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)}\rVert_{\text{F}}^{2}

s.to

\displaystyle{}\ \mathscr{S}(\mathbf{Y})=\mathscr{S}\mathscr{F}({\mathbf{X}})\,,

(3a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(n+1/2)}

\displaystyle\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{Z}}{}

\displaystyle{}\ \tfrac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\lVert\mathbf{Z}-\mathscr{F}_{\text{t}}(\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)})\rVert^{2}_{\text{F}}+\lambda_{3}\lVert\mathbf{Z}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle{}\ +\tfrac{\tau_{Z}}{2}\lVert\mathbf{Z}-\hat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(n)}\rVert_{\text{F}}^{2}\,,

(3b)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbfcal{A}}_{q}^{(n+1/2)}{}

VI
Conclusions

This paper extends the KRIM framework
[
36
]
into a faster kernelized matrix
factorization framework which avoids KRIM’s dimensionality reduction pre-step. Numerical tests
demonstrate that the proposed data-modeling approach matches the reconstruction performance of
KRIM under both Cartesian and radial data sampling, but with significant reduction in
computational time, and outperforms at the same time popular methods as well as
state-of-the-art tensor-based and deep-image-prior schemes.
