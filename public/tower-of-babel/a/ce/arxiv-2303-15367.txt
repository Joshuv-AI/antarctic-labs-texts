Abstract
. We analyse uniformly random proper k k -colourings of sparse graphs with maximum degree Δ \Delta in the regime Δ < k ​ ln ⁡ k \Delta<k\ln k . This regime corresponds to the lower side of the shattering threshold for random graph colouring, a paradigmatic example of the shattering threshold for random Constraint Satisfaction Problems. We prove a variety of results about the solution space geometry of colourings of fixed graphs, generalising work of Achlioptas, Coja-Oghlan [ ACO08 ] , and Molloy [ Mol12 ] on random graphs, and justifying the performance of stochastic local search algorithms in this regime. Our central proof relies only on elementary techniques, namely the first-moment method and a quantitative induction, yet it strengthens list-colouring results due to Vu [ Vu02 ] , and more recently Davies, Kang, P., and Sereni [ DKPS20 ] , and generalises state-of-the-art bounds from Ramsey theory in the context of sparse graphs. It further yields an approximately tight lower bound on the number of colourings, also known as the partition function of the Potts model, with implications for efficient approximate counting.



1.
Introduction

Theoretical Computer Science (TCS), Statistical Physics, and Combinatorics are nearest neighbours in the network of the sciences.
Their stated aims typically differ, but occasionally they converge, leading to a fruitful exchange of ideas.
A central example of this overlap is graph colouring; for TCS this is a constraint satisfaction problem (CSP), for Statistical Physics this is the anti-ferromagnetic Potts model, and for Combinatorics this is a
150
-year-old well of interesting problems.
In particular, colourings of sparse graphs and random graphs of average degree
\Delta
with
k\ln k=\Omega(\Delta)
colours have proved to be a rich source of phase transition type behaviour.
Our focus is on uniformly random
k
-colourings of graphs of maximum degree
\Delta
, satisfying some sparsity conditions (e.g. triangle-freeness) and under the assumption that
\Delta<k\ln k
, in other words right below the phase transition.

The bulk of the paper is centred around the TCS perspective. We build on the rich literature surrounding phase transitions in random CSPs and the so-called shattering threshold by proving that, above the shattering threshold, properties of the solution space geometry of random graphs — namely those investigated in
[
ACO08
,
ART06
,
Mol12
]
— in fact hold for any fixed sparse graph.
For Statistical Physics, we prove an approximately tight lower bound on the number of colourings of sparse graphs; this corresponds to a lower bound on the partition function of the
k
-state anti-ferromagnetic Potts model at temperature
0
.
These bounds in turn suggest interesting possibilities for limits of sequences of graphs with growing girth, as well as efficient approximate counting of colourings.
For Combinatorics, our result also extends to local list-colourings, where list sizes are defined according to local parameters (namely the degree of each vertex), and allow for a certain density of edges within the neighbourhoods.
Our results in this direction strengthen all previous results, and do so with a simpler proof.
The bounds that can be derived from our work are tight barring a breakthrough on a
50
-year-old Ramsey theory problem.

Here, we wish to prove concentration for the number of short lists that appear in the neighbourhood of a vertex in a uniformly random colouring.
We are unfortunately in a context where negative correlation does not hold and thus we introduce another property, namely Bernoulli-domination, that suffices to have high concentration inequalities in that context. This is obtained, in Theorem

This work constitutes, to our knowledge, the first rigorous analysis of the properties of a typical proper colouring of an arbitrary deterministic graph in this regime, and we suspect our methods may be adapted to answer many more questions about such typical colourings and CSP solutions.

2.1.
Solution Space Geometry

For over half a century we have known that many Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSPs) are NP-hard (in the worst case).
It was hoped that average case complexity would paint a brighter picture, and indeed early results suggested this might be so
[
Tur88
,
DF86
,
Kuč89
]
.
These results yielded polynomial-time algorithms for many random CSPs, including for
k
-colouring almost all graphs of chromatic number at most
k
.
However, a more challenging
1
1

1

More challenging and perhaps more natural, as the assumed condition is checkable in polynomial time.
test case soon emerged; finding a polynomial-time algorithm that, with high probability, finds a solution to a random CSP with a
fixed ratio of constraints to variables
.
When the CSP is graph colouring this test case becomes:
k
-colour the
Erdős-Rényi random graph
G_{n,dn/2}
.
That is, the graph chosen uniformly at random from all graphs with
n
vertices and
dn/2
edges, so of average degree
d
.
In
[
AM97
]
it was shown that w.h.p. the following algorithm suffices for
d\leq k\ln k
:

(1)

choose a vertex
v
uniformly at random from those with the least number of available colours;

(2)

colour
v
uniformly at random with one of its available colours.

Remarkably, while
G_{n,dn/2}
is known to be w.h.p.
k
-colourable for
d\sim 2k\ln k
, no algorithm has been proven to succeed for any
d>(1+o_{k}(1))k\ln k
.
This extremely naive algorithm is the state of the art.
As observed by Achlioptas and Coja-Oghlan in
[
ACO08
]
, this state of affairs appears to be universal in that it holds for
“nearly every random CSP of interest”

represents the ratio of constraints to variable).
That is, extremely naive algorithms (known for decades) work up to some threshold (

), which is well below the SAT-UNSAT threshold (

), but no algorithms are known to work for

The value of

is not arbitrary; it coincides with the conjectured shattering threshold

]
from the statistical physics literature (which is defined in terms of expected spatial correlation decay).
Drawing inspiration from these conjectures, and building on

]

-SAT and random

-uniform hypergraph

As stated in earlier work
[
ART06
]
a key idea underlying their approach is the following.

Hypothesis 1
.

The geometry of the solution space of a given CSP instance dictates the performance of algorithms on that instance
2
2

2

This has a very similar flavour to the Overlap Gap Property, see
[

They proved that w.h.p. two phase transitions, one local and one global, occur in the geometry of the solution space as

d_{Sh}\penalty\ k\ln k
for random graph colouring).

Globally
, the solution space starts as a large well-connected ball before shattering into exponentially many, exponentially small, well-separated clusters.

Locally
, typical solutions start as able to change the assignment of almost any variable by changing

“loose”
) and end up with this not being the case for almost all variables (almost all variables are
“rigid”

They hypothesised that these phase transitions are

That is, these properties of the geometry of the solution space explain both the existence of algorithms for

In
[
Mol12
]
, the local phase transition — also known as the freezing threshold — was further analysed in the case of random graph colouring, showing that an even stronger statement, Theorem

The local freezing threshold is of particular interest because, in
[
KMRT+07
,
Zde08
,
ZK07
]

Indeed there is evidence that supports this; while the thresholds are asymptotically equal (in
k

[
AM02
]
a random graph colouring algorithm is provided for

Thus while random CSPs have proved to be challenging problems, there was a consistent explanation of why algorithms succeed or not, based on the typical geometry of the solution space of a random problem instance.
However, a crack appeared in this picture when, in
[
Mol19
]
, Molloy used entropy compression (inspired by Moser-Tardos
[
MT10
]
) to prove that the following clever and simple algorithm yields a

-colouring of
any
triangle-free graph
G
of maximum degree
d\leq(1-o_{k}(1))k\ln k
(right up to the shattering threshold!):

(1)

start from an arbitrary partial proper
k
-colouring of
G
;

(2)

while there exists a
Bad
vertex
v
, resample a partial proper colouring of the neighbourhood of
v
uniformly at random

3

3

In fact in this step Molloy gave priority to the
Bad

;

(3)

extend the partial colouring to a complete proper colouring.

This is of course a stochastic local search algorithm
[
Ili19
]
. Molloy defined
Bad
vertices to be those with too few colours available, or too many uncoloured neighbours that might create a conflict on a given colour.
Via entropy compression, he demonstrated that after resampling neighbourhoods polynomially many times (in expectation), no vertices would be
Bad
.
Then, a direct application of the algorithmic Lovász Local Lemma
[
MT10
]
suffices to complete the colouring in polynomial-time.
In fact, Bernshteyn
[

]

[
AS16
]

The reason this constitutes a crack in our picture is that our barriers to the existence of efficient algorithms above the shattering threshold were hypothesised based on the analysis of random graphs.
But Molloy’s algorithm works for
all triangle-free graphs
, not just random graphs.
Thus, working off of Hypothesis
1
, we would expect that the geometry of the space of colourings of all triangle-free graphs of max degree

is similar to that of

as described by Achlioptas, Coja-Oghlan

]

If, for example, there existed a

The central contribution of this work, see Theorems
2.7
and
2.8
, is to show that no such graph exists in the former case, supporting the possibility that the local freezing threshold is a barrier to efficient algorithms.
Establishing positive results on the connectivity of the solution space is also particularly relevant to understanding the performance of stochastic local search algorithms, such as Molloy’s
[
Mol19
]
.

2.2.
Local List Colouring and Density

Given a graph
G
,
a
list-assignment
of
G
is a map
L\colon V(G)\rightarrow 2^{\mathbb{N}}
, and a
proper
L
-colouring

\sigma
of
G
is a map
c\colon V(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that
c(v)\in L(v)
for every vertex
v\in V(G)
, and
c(u)\neq c(v)
for every edge
uv\in E(G)
. Thus list-colouring is a CSP which includes colouring as a special case.
If there exists an
L
-colouring of
G
, we say that
G
is
L
-colourable
.
The
list chromatic number
of
G
, denoted
\chi_{\ell}(G)
, is the minimum
k
such that
G
is
L
-colourable for every list-assignment
L
with
|L(v)|\geq k
for every vertex
v\in V(G)
.
Note that the classical chromatic number
\chi(G)
is always at most
\chi_{\ell}(G)
.

In a seminal result, Johansson
[
Joh96
]
showed that the list chromatic number of triangle-free graphs of maximum degree
\Delta
never exceeds
O(\Delta/\ln\Delta)
as
\Delta\to\infty
. Two decades later, Molloy
[
Mol19
]
showed with the help of entropy compression that this bound can be tightened to
(1+o(1))\Delta/\ln\Delta
as
\Delta\to\infty
; Bernshteyn
[
Ber19
]
then showed that it was possible to replace the use of entropy compression with an application of the lopsided Lovász Local Lemma and obtain a similar result. Following that breakthrough, there has been a lot of interest for triangle-free graph colourings and extensions
[
AIS19
,
ABD21
,
BKNP18
,
DdJdVKP20
,
DKPS20
]
.

Although quite different, all the proofs in those works rely on a
2
-step colouring procedure.
This involves first finding a partial proper colouring with appropriate properties and then extending it in a second step.
This second step is usually called the Finishing Blow.
In contrast, our proof does not require the use of the Finishing Blow, and is the first of that nature to the best of our knowledge.
Our result also includes graphs where the density is bounded within each neighbourhood, a natural extension of triangle-free graphs. The first result in that vein is due to Alon, Krivelevich, and Sudakov
[
AKS99
]
.
They relied on the result of Johansson in order to prove that if a graph
G
of maximum degree
\Delta
is such that each neighbourhood spans at most
\Delta^{2}/f
edges, where
1\leq f\leq\Delta^{2}+1
, then its chromatic number is at most
O(\Delta/\ln f)
as
f\to\infty
. This result was then extended to the list chromatic number by Vu
[
Vu02
]
.
More recently, it has been proved by the second author together with Davies, Kang, and Sereni
[
DKPS20
, Corrolary 24]
, that this upper bound can be tightened to
(2+\varepsilon)\Delta/\ln f
, provided that
f\geq(\ln\Delta)^{2/\varepsilon}
, for every
\varepsilon>0
.
Our main theorem is a strengthening of these results which is close to being optimal.

We were inspired by a counting argument which was first used in the context of graph colouring by Rosenfeld
[
Ros20
]
(see
[
Woo20
, Section 3.5]
for an introduction to the method), and later devised more generally for hypergraph colouring by Wanless and Wood
[
WW20
]
.
We note however that our proof deviates from the standard arguments in order to exploit the relationship between uniformly random colourings of a graph
G
and uniformly random colourings of induced subgraphs
H\subset G
.

Given a list-assignment
L\colon V(G)\to 2^{\mathbb{N}}
of
G
, a proper
L
-colouring
\sigma
of a subgraph
H
of
G
and a vertex
v\in V(G)
, we denote
L_{\sigma}(v)\coloneqq L(v)\setminus\sigma(N_{H}(v))
the set of colours available at
v
given
\sigma
, and
\ell_{\sigma}(v)\coloneqq|L_{\sigma}(v)|
its size.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
G
be a graph of maximum degree
\Delta
such that every graph induced by a neighbourhood in
G
has average degree at most
d\leq\frac{\Delta}{6}-1
. Write
\rho\coloneqq\Delta/(d+1)
and let
\ell\geq(d+1)(\ln\rho)^{3}
. Then for every list-assignment
L\colon V(G)\to 2^{\mathbb{N}}
with

|L(v)|\geq\left(1+\frac{2}{\ln\rho}\right)\frac{\deg(v)}{W\left(\frac{\deg(v)}{\ell}\right)}

for every vertex
v\in V(G)
, the following holds.
For all
v\in V(G)
, the uniformly random proper
L
-colouring
{\bm{\sigma}}
of
G\setminus v
satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{\bm{\sigma}}(v)\right]\geq\ell
.

As before, the conclusion also holds for the uniformly random
k
-colouring of

.
In the statement of Theorem
2.10
, we use the

which is defined as the reciprocal of the function

. In the proof of Theorem

, we will use the well-known fact that

. Moreover, we note that

; hence, by fixing

, Theorem

implies Theorem

. Since we have already given a proof of Theorem

, whose statement suffices for most applications in this paper, we defer the proof of Theorem

to the Appendix.

In the context of Vu’s result
[
Vu02
]
, Theorem
2.10
implies the following; this is proved in the Appendix.

Corollary 2.11

Let

be a graph of maximum degree
\Delta
, such that every neighbourhood spans at most

It has been observed in

We note that the results of this section also hold in the context of DP-colouring, an interesting extension of list colouring introduced by Dvořák and Postle

We have decided to state those results in the context of list colouring in order to avoid the verbose formalism of DP-colouring, but the proof of the results in this section readily adapt to that context.

2.3.
Partition Functions and Approximation Schemes

Partition functions are important objects in statistical physics.
They count the weighted number of states of a system where said weight is proportional to the probability of seeing the system in said state.
Thus they also serve as the normalising constant when we write the probability of seeing a particular state.
It seems natural to expect that our lower bound on the expected number of colours available at each vertex would yield a lower bound on the number of colourings and thus said partition function.
By slightly adjusting the proof of Theorem

3.1.
Coupon-Collector-type results

We begin with a proof of the Coupon-Collector Lemma, of which we recall the statement hereafter.

Lemma 3.1

(Coupon-Collector Lemma)
.

Suppose we have random non-empty lists
\mathbf{L}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{L}_{d}
, each of which takes values in the finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
.
Fix some integer
t\geq 1
, and define the random variable
\mathbf{X}\coloneqq\#\{i\in[\Delta]:|\mathbf{L}_{i}|\leq t\}
.
Now choose an element
{\bm{\sigma}}(i)
of
\mathbf{L}_{i}
uniformly at random for each
i\in[d]
and define the random variable
\mathbf{L}\coloneqq[k]\setminus\{{\bm{\sigma}}(i):i\in[d]\}
.
Then

\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathbf{L}|\right]\geq k_{0}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)\frac{d}{k_{0}}},\quad\mbox{where }k_{0}=k-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{X}\right].

Proof.

Let us fix a realisation
L_{1},\ldots L_{d}
of the random lists.
We let
S=\{i:|L_{i}|\leq t\}
be the set of short lists, and
B=[d]\setminus S
be the set of big lists.
We observe that since
|L_{i}|\geq t+1
for every
i\in B
, we have
\frac{1}{|L_{i}|-1}\leq(1+\frac{1}{t})\frac{1}{|L_{i}|}
. Combining this observation with one due to Molloy
[
Mol19
]
, we obtain

(4)

\sum_{x\in[k]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in B\\
x\in L_{i}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|L_{i}|-1}\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)\sum_{x\in[k]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in B\\
x\in L_{i}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|L_{i}|}\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)\sum_{i\in B}\sum_{x\in L_{i}}\frac{1}{|L_{i}|}\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)d.

The observation of Molloy is that each list
L_{i}
appears in the first double sum exactly
|L_{i}|
times, and it does so with weight
1/|L_{i}|
, meaning its contribution is always
1
.

Let us fix
{\bm{\sigma}}(i)=x_{i}\in L_{i}
for every
i\in S
, and let
L_{0}\coloneqq[k]\setminus\{x_{i}:i\in S\}
. Note that
|L_{0}|\geq k-|S|
.
We may now pick
{\bm{\sigma}}(i)
uniformly at random from
L_{i}
for every
i\in B
, and let
\mathbf{L}_{0}\coloneqq L_{0}\setminus\{{\bm{\sigma}}(i):i\in B\}
. Note that
\mathbf{L}_{0}
is precisely
\mathbf{L}
under the condition that
{\bm{\sigma}}(S)=\{x_{i}:i\in S\}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathbf{L}_{0}|\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in L_{0}}\mathbb{P}\left[x\notin{\bm{\sigma}}(B)\right]=\sum_{x\in L_{0}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in B\\
x\in L_{i}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{|L_{i}|}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{x\in L_{0}}\exp\left(-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in B\\
x\in L_{i}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|L_{i}|-1}\right)

since
1-\frac{1}{z}>\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{z-1}}
for every
z>1
;

\displaystyle\geq|L_{0}|\exp\left(-\frac{1}{|L_{0}|}\sum_{x\in L_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in B\\
x\in L_{i}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|L_{i}|-1}\right)

by convexity of
\exp
;

\displaystyle\geq(k-|S|)\exp\left(-\frac{\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)d}{k-|S|}\right)

by
|L_{0}|>k-|S|
and (
4
).

It remains to average over all possible realisations of
\mathbf{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{L}_{d}
. We use Jensen’s inequality together with the convexity of the function
z\mapsto z\mathrm{e}^{-C/z}
for every
C>0
over the interval
(0,+\infty)
, and obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathbf{L}|\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[(k-\mathbf{X})\exp\left(-\frac{\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)d}{k-\mathbf{X}}\right)\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[(k-\mathbf{X})\right]\exp\left(-\frac{\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)d}{\mathbb{E}\left[k-\mathbf{X}\right]}\right)=k_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)\frac{d}{k_{0}}},

where
k_{0}=k-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{X}\right]
.

∎

A straightforward application of this result yields the following (deterministic) existence of a colouring that induces a big list at

3.2.
Bernoulli-domination

For the rest of the proofs, we will need high-concentration bounds, and to that end we introduce the concept of
Bernoulli-domination
. Let us first state the following useful Chernoff-Hoeffding type tail bound for binary random variables that are dominated by independent random variables.
This is a special case of
[
PS97
, Theorem 3.4]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{s}
are binary random variables and
\mathbf{Y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{s}
are independent binary random variables. Let
\mathbf{Y}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in[s]}\mathbf{Y}_{i}
and
\mathbf{X}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in[s]}\mathbf{X}_{i}
. Write
\mu\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{Y}\right]
. Then if

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in J}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in J}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\right],

for all
J\subset[s]
, it follows that for all
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathbf{X}\geq(1+\delta)\mu\right]\leq\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\delta}}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right]^{\mu}.

In particular, for every
\sigma\geq 6\mu
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathbf{X}\geq\sigma\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma}.

Motivated by the above theorem in the case where each
\mathbf{Y}_{i}
follows the law of Bernoulli
\text{Ber}(p)
, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 3.4
.

We say binary random variables
(\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{s})
are
\text{Ber}(p)
-dominated
if

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in J}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right]\leq p^{|J|},

for all
J\subseteq[s]
.

One can understand Theorem
3.3
as saying that the upper tail of the random variable is almost as small as that of a binomial random variable.
In fact, one can bootstrap Theorem
1.1
to make this analogy even stronger, by showing that large upward deviations on disjoint sets are also
\text{Ber}(p)
-dominated for some
p
.
The random variable
\mathbf{R}_{i}
in the next theorem is the indicator function of a large upward deviation on some set
Q_{i}\subset[s]
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose
(\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{s})
are
\text{Ber}(p)
-dominated.
Let disjoint subsets
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{m}\subseteq[s]
of equal order be given, fix
\delta>0
and define the binary random variables

\mathbf{R}_{i}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{j\in Q_{i}}\mathbf{X}_{j}>(1+\delta)p|Q_{i}|\right\},

for
i\in[m]
. Let
q
be the upper bound on
\mathbb{P}\left[\mathbf{R}_{i}=1\right]
given by Theorem
3.3
.
Then
(\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{m})
are
\text{Ber}(q)
-dominated.

Proof.

Consider
J\subseteq[m]
; we wish to show that

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in J}\mathbf{R}_{i}\right]\leq q^{|J|}.

To this end, let
I\coloneqq\cup_{j\in J}Q_{j}\subset[s]
and observe that, by assumption and Theorem
3.3
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{i\in I}\mathbf{X}_{i}>(1+\delta)p|I|\right]\leq\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\delta}}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right]^{p|I|}=\prod_{j\in J}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\delta}}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right]^{p|Q_{j}|}=q^{|J|}.

To conclude we observe that if
\mathbf{R}_{j}=1
for all
j\in J
then it follows that
\sum_{i\in I}\mathbf{X}_{i}>(1+\delta)p|I|
.
∎

We now show that the appearance of short lists at vertices in an independent set is
\text{Ber}(p)
-dominated for an appropriate

. This is crucial for both Theorems

and

Theorem 3.6
.

Let

and

be as in Theorem

and let

be an independent set of

be a uniformly random proper

Then the random variables
\mathbf{X}_{v}\coloneqq\{\ell_{{\bm{\sigma}}}(v)\leq p\ell\}
for

are
\text{Ber}(p)
-dominated.

Proof.

Let
J\subseteq I
, and label the vertices of

by

.
For every graph

So we have

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{v\in J}\mathbf{X}_{v}\right]=\frac{\#\{\sigma\in\mathscr{C}(G):\ell_{\sigma}(v_{1}),\dots,\ell_{\sigma}(v_{s})\leq p\ell\}}{|\mathscr{C}(G)|}\leq\frac{|\mathscr{C}(G\setminus J)|\cdot(p\ell)^{s}}{|\mathscr{C}(G\setminus J)|\cdot\ell^{s}}=p^{s}.

For every integers
