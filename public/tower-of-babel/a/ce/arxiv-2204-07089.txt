Semiclassical WKB Problem for the non-self-adjoint Dirac operator with an analytic rapidly oscillating potential
Setsuro Fujiié ∗, Nicholas Hatzizisis †, Spyridon Kamvissis ‡

Abstract
. In this paper we examine the semiclassical behavior of the scattering data of a non-self-adjoint Dirac operator with a rapidly oscillating potential that is complex analytic in some neighborhood of the real line. Some of our results are rigorous and quite general. On the other hand, complete and concrete understanding requires the investigation of the WKB geometry of specific examples. For such detailed computations we use a particular example that has been investigated numerically more than 20 years ago by Bronski and Miller and rely heavily on their numerical computations. Mostly employing the exact WKB method, we provide the complete rigorous uniform semiclassical analysis of the Bohr-Sommerfeld condition for the location of the eigenvalues across unions of analytic arcs as well as the associated norming constants. For the reflection coefficient as well as the eigenvalues near 0 in the spectral plane, we employ instead an older theory that has been developed in great detail by Olver. Our analysis is motivated by the need to understand the semiclassical behaviour of the focusing cubic NLS equation with initial data A ​ exp ⁡ { i ​ S / ϵ } A\exp\{iS/\epsilon\} , in view of the well-known fact discovered by Zakharov and Shabat that the spectral analysis of the Dirac operator enables the solution of the NLS equation via inverse scattering theory.

Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

Consider the
semiclassical limit
(
\epsilon\downarrow 0
) of the solution to the initial
value problem of the one-dimensional
nonlinear Schrödinger equation
with cubic
nonlinearity

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}i\epsilon\partial_{t}\psi+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\psi+|\psi|^{2}\psi=0,\quad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\\
\psi(x,0)=A(x)\exp\{iS(x)/\epsilon\},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}\end{array}\right.

where
A
and
S
are real valued integrable functions defined on the real line.

According to the seminal discovery in
[
28
]
, this initial value problem can be studied
via the so-called
inverse scattering method
. In order for one to do so, the first
ingredient is the
direct
spectral and scattering analysis of the associated
Dirac

(or Zakharov-Shabat) operator
\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}
given by

\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}&-iA(x)\exp\{iS(x)/\epsilon\}\\
-iA(x)\exp\{-iS(x)/\epsilon\}&\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}\end{bmatrix}.

Our main goal in this paper is to provide a rigorous investigation of the semiclassical
behavior of the
scattering data
(reflection coefficient, eigenvalues and their
associated norming constants) for the operator
\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}
and apply
our conclusions to the semiclassical investigation of the solutions to the focusing
NLS equation.

We are interested in
A(x)
and
S(x)
that are
real analytic
functions
which can be extended holomorphically to at least a region in the whole complex plane

1

1

For the
inverse scattering method
to be applicable we will also need that
S^{\prime}

is integrable for real
x
.
. Although a good deal of our analysis will be fairly general, we will eventually
focus on a very particular choice of

and
S(x)
so that we have a concrete configuration of the
geometry of turning points and Stokes lines. Our model will be the
A(x)=S(x)=\sech(2x)

since this has been the first case considered in detail: a very careful numerical analysis of
the eigenvalue problem was done by Bronski
[
1
]
in
1996
and a first investigation of
the formal WKB theory, involving numerical computations of turning points and Stokes lines,
was conducted by Miller
[

in

[

; there, the authors use
A(x)=30/(1+x^{4})

.

If the parameter
\epsilon
is not too small,
then of course the solution is described by a “nonlinear” superposition
of “breathers” (corresponding to purely imaginary eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}
),
traveling solitons (corresponding to all other eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}
)
and a “background radiation contribution” (corresponding to the continuous spectrum of

\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}

On the other hand, as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
, the solution attains a special form
which in particular regions attains a highly oscillatory behavior.
Often in such problems one expects the validity of the so-called
finite gap ansatz

for the semiclassical asymptotics.

Finite Gap Ansatz
.

Let
(x_{0},t_{0})
be a generic point with
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
t_{0}>0
.
The solution
\psi

\epsilon\downarrow 0
)
described (locally) as a slowly modulated
G

setting
x=x_{0}+\epsilon\hat{x}
and

(so that
x_{0},t_{0}

“slow” variables while
\hat{x},\hat{t}
are “fast” variables), there exist parameters
(all of which depend on the slow variables
x_{0}
,
t_{0}
but not on
\hat{x},\hat{t}
)

•

a,U_{0},k_{0},w_{0}
in
{\mathbb{C}}

•

{\bf U}=[U_{1},\dots,U_{G}]^{T}

\psi(x,t)\sim a(x_{0},t_{0})\exp\Big\{i\Big(\tfrac{U_{0}(x_{0},t_{0})}{\epsilon}+k_{0}(x_{0},t_{0})\hat{x}-w_{0}(x_{0},t_{0})\hat{t}\Big)\Big\}\\
\cdot\frac{\Theta\bigg({\bf Y}(x_{0},t_{0})+i\Big(\frac{{\bf U}(x_{0},t_{0})}{\epsilon}+{\bf k}(x_{0},t_{0})\hat{x}-{\bf w}(x_{0},t_{0})\hat{t}\Big)\bigg)}{\Theta\bigg({\bf Z}(x_{0},t_{0})+i\Big(\frac{{\bf U}(x_{0},t_{0})}{\epsilon}+{\bf k}(x_{0},t_{0})\hat{x}-{\bf w}(x_{0},t_{0})\hat{t}\Big)\bigg)}.

All parameters here are defined in terms of an underlying

In
[
19
]
we have been able to identify the first region in Figure

with the genus 0 region. The intermediate region is the genus 2 region and then comes the
genus 4 region. The separating curves are the “caustics”. In principle, there can be a very
large number of such caustics. But it is also worth pointing out that the
2\sech x

being a nonlinear superposition of many (of order

) breathers, is actually

. Of course, this is not seen in our

If we were to run our numerics in Figure

we would also observe many separated traveling solitons at different non-zero speeds,
since (as we shall see later) the initial data give rise to many eigenvalues with non-zero

The question of the semiclassical approximation of the scattering data has a
deeper significance in view of the instabilities of the problem which apper in
many levels. In fact even in the non-semiclassical regime, the
focusing
NLS
is the main model for the so-called “modulational instability” (see
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
),
although for positive fixed
\epsilon
the initial value problem is well-posed.
Semiclassically the instabilities become more pronounced. One way to see this,
is related to the underlying ellipticity of the formal semiclassical limit. To be more
specific, consider the well-known
Madelung transformation
(see
[
21
]
)

\begin{cases}\rho=|\psi|^{2}\\
\mu=\epsilon\Im(\psi^{*}\psi_{x})\end{cases}

where
\psi^{*}
denotes the complex conjugate of
\psi
.
Then the initial value problem (
1.1
) becomes

\begin{cases}\rho_{t}+\mu_{x}=0\\
\mu_{t}+\Big(\frac{\mu^{2}}{\rho}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\Big)_{x}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}\partial_{x}[\rho(\log\rho)_{xx}]\\
\rho(x,0)=A^{2}(x)\\
\mu(x,0)=A^{2}(x)S^{\prime}(x).\end{cases}

The formal limit, as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
, is

\begin{cases}\rho_{t}+\mu_{x}=0\\
\mu_{t}+\Big(\frac{\mu^{2}}{\rho}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\Big)_{x}=0\\
\rho(x,0)=A^{2}(x)\\
\mu(x,0)=A^{2}(x)S^{\prime}(x).\end{cases}

This is an initial value problem for an elliptic system of equations; and so one expects
that small perturbations of the initial data (independent of
\epsilon
) can lead to large
changes in the solution at any given time.

Instabilities also appear, independently, at the spectral analysis of the related
non-self-adjoint Dirac operator: indeed, small changes of the potential are expected
to result in relatively large changes in the discrete spectrum; this is not true when the
phase
S=0
but it is true otherwise. We refer to
[
2
]
for a numerical investigation
of this fact in the special case
A(x)=S(x)=sech(2x)
and to
[
8
]
for the discussion of the more general
phenomenon in the theory of pseudo-differential operators. Instabilities are evident also
at the related
equilibrium measure problem
(it is a “max-min” problem; see
[
20
]
),
the related
Whitham equations
(they are also elliptic) and even in the numerical
studies of the problem. What was initially called the modulational instability is just the tip of the iceberg!

One might even question whether the whole project is worthwhile studying in detail,
at least as a valid physical model, in view of all these instabilities. To this, a first response is
that the semiclassical analysis turns out in practice to be relevant even for not so small
values of the semi-classical parameter
\epsilon
(for example in applications to nonlinear
optics). From a mathematical point of view, it is
a fascinating instance of a difficult unstable
problem that can be approximated by solvable stable problems; this approximation is very
singular and non-trivial and its study involves important connections to different areas of
mathematics (namely
PDE theory
,
spectral theory of non-self-adjoint operators
,

WKB analysis
,
potential theory
(cf.
[
20
]
),
Riemann surface theory
,

hydrodynamic instability theory
).

Going back to the pictures presented above, in the first (genus 0) region there are no
fast oscillations and actually strong semiclassical limits exist for both
\rho
and
\mu
.
They actually satisfy the formally limiting PDE. In the other (higher genus) regions, violent
oscillations of frequency order
\epsilon^{-1}
appear and a strong pointwise limit does not
exist. But to the extent that we can perform a full asymptotic analysis, both at the direct
scatterring and the inverse scattering stage, we can show that there is at least a weak limit
and we are able to provide complete asymptotic formulae (as above).

The semiclassical analysis of the NLS solution
\psi
in
[
19
]
undertook the
asymptotic (
\epsilon\downarrow 0
) analysis of the inverse scattering problem, to
which Zakharov and Shabat have reduced the solution of the equation; in particular,
we worked on the formulation of that problem as a
Riemann-Hilbert factorisation
problem
and we applied and extended ideas and calculations going back to the seminal
work of
Deift, Venakides and Zhou
(
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
). But no careful
semiclassical analysis of the
direct
scattering problem had been undertaken until
recently. Instead, an ad hoc approximation of the eigenvalues by their

Bohr-Sommerfeld approximants
was used as a starting point (and the reflection
coefficient was set identically to 0, in the same formal spirit).

In
[
14
]
the rigorous semiclassical analysis of the scattering data of the
related Dirac (or Zakharov-Shabat) operator was completed in the case where
A

is real analytic, integrable, positive, symmetric, with only one local maximum (and where
for simplicity the second derivative of
A
is non-zero). We applied the so-called

exact WKB theory
, which will also be applied in the present work.

In
[
15
]
the analyticity assumption was replaced by a mild smoothness assumption
and a different method was employed, going back to
Langer
and
Olver

[
23
]
. In a sequel
[
16
]
, the general case with several local maxima and
minima was also completed, under the assumption that potentials are smooth and positive.
In all the above cases the initial phase
S(x)
is identically zero. Of course, even with an
initial phase zero, the Madelung system above shows that a non-trivial phase will appear
immediately for any small time
t
. But it is also reasonable to postulate a non-zero initial
phase especially in cases where the discrete spectrum is no more imaginary and the
instability due to the non-self-adjointness of the Dirac operator is instrumental.

The exact WKB method was first developed for the
Schrödinger operator
,
but here we apply it to the Dirac operator that is associated to the

focusing NLS equation
. The method goes back to works of
Ecalle

[
9
]
and
Voros

[
27
]
but here we argue along the lines of the papers of

Gérard-Grigis

[
13
]
and
Fujiié-Lasser-Nédélec

[
10
]
.
Rather than relying on the usual formal WKB method which relies on asymptotic
series that are in general divergent, we use a “resummation” of the series and in
fact construct “exact solutions” in terms of convergent series, thus resolving a
problem of “asymptotics beyond all orders”.

For the study of the eigenvalues with small imaginary part, the exact WKB method seems
to break down and Olver’s
[
23
]
is not directly applicable since we don’t know
a priori that the eigenvalues are purely imaginary (as in
[
15
]
). Fortunately the somewhat
forgotten paper
[
24
]
which generalizes his earlier work is proved to be useful
here.

2.
Eigenvalues Away From The Real Axis

2.1.
General strategy

This paragraph presents a general scheme (see
[
22
]
, and also
[
11
]

for the case of Schrödinger operators) for finding
the semiclassical EVs of the Dirac (or Zakharov-Shabat) operator

(2.1)

\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}&\omega(x,\epsilon)\\
-\omega^{*}(x,\epsilon)&\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}\end{bmatrix}

where
\omega
is the complex potential function

\omega(x,\epsilon)=-iA(x)\exp\Big\{i\frac{S(x)}{\epsilon}\Big\}

for some real-valued, analytic functions
A(x)
and
S(x)

defined on the real line
[
\omega^{*}(x,\epsilon)
represents the complex conjugate of

\omega(x,\epsilon)
] such that
A
,
S
and
S^{\prime}
are integrable. As previously
stated in the introduction, we shall be interested in the spectral (generalized eigenvalue)
problem

(2.2)

\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}\textbf{u}=\lambda\textbf{u}

where
\textbf{u}=[u_{1}\hskip 3.0ptu_{2}]^{T}
is a function from
{\mathbb{R}}
to
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}

and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
plays the role of the spectral parameter.

The operator

\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}
is non-self-adjoint and the eigenvalues are distributed
in the complex plane. But in the semiclassical limit, their location is restricted
close to the
numerical range

\mathcal{R}
of the semiclassical symbol.

We shall see [see the reasoning that follows, eventually leading to (
2.7
)]
that the eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}
coincide with those of the operator

(2.3)

\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}-\frac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x)&-iA(x)\\
-iA(x)&\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}-\frac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x)\end{bmatrix}.

We have the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

The
numerical range

\mathcal{R}
of the symbol

\begin{bmatrix}-\xi-\frac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x)&-iA(x)\\
-iA(x)&\xi-\frac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x)\end{bmatrix}

of the matrix/operator in (
2.3
) is defined to be the union of the
set of values of the eigenvalues
-\frac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x)\pm\sqrt{\xi-A(x)^{2}}
of this matrix
when
(x,\xi)
varies in the whole phase space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

We see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{R}}\cup{\mathcal{R}}_{0}\quad\text{where}\quad

(2.4)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Big[\inf_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}S^{\prime}(x),\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}S^{\prime}(x)\Big]\times\Big[-\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}|A(x)|,\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}|A(x)|\Big].

The following result concerning the semiclassical eigenvalues is well-known; see e.g.
[
8
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any compact set
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with
K\cap{\mathcal{R}}_{0}=\emptyset
,
there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that there is no eigenvalue in
K
for

0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
.

It is clear that (
2.2
) can be written equivalently as a first order
system of differential equations, namely

(2.5)

\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}\textbf{u}(x,\lambda,\epsilon)=K(x,\lambda,\epsilon)\textbf{u}(x,\lambda,\epsilon)

where

(2.6)

K(x,\lambda,\epsilon)=\begin{bmatrix}-\lambda&\omega(x,\epsilon)\\
\omega^{*}(x,\epsilon)&\lambda\end{bmatrix}.

Applying first the transformation

\textbf{u}(x,\lambda,\epsilon)=\begin{bmatrix}\exp\big\{i\frac{S(x)}{2\epsilon}\big\}&0\\
0&\exp\big\{-i\frac{S(x)}{2\epsilon}\big\}\end{bmatrix}\tilde{\textbf{v}}(x,\lambda,\epsilon)

where
\tilde{\textbf{v}}=[\tilde{v}_{1}\hskip 3.0pt\tilde{v}_{2}]^{T}
, takes the system in
(
2.5
) to a new form

(2.7)

\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}\tilde{\textbf{v}}(x,\lambda,\epsilon)=\begin{bmatrix}-\lambda-\frac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x)&-iA(x)\\
iA(x)&\lambda+\frac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x)\end{bmatrix}\tilde{\textbf{v}}(x,\lambda,\epsilon)

where prime denotes differentiation with respect to
x
. Next, we apply the mapping

\tilde{\textbf{v}}(x,\lambda,\epsilon)=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
-1&1\end{bmatrix}\textbf{v}(x,\lambda,\epsilon)

where

\textbf{v}=[v_{1}\hskip 3.0ptv_{2}]^{T}
, to finally express the initial system as

(2.8)

\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}\textbf{v}(x,\lambda,\epsilon)=M(x,\lambda)\textbf{v}(x,\lambda,\epsilon)

where

(2.9)

M(x,\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}0&g_{+}(x,\lambda)\\
-g_{-}(x,\lambda)&0\end{bmatrix}

(2.10)

\text{and}\quad g_{\pm}(x,\lambda)=\mp[\lambda+\frac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x)\pm iA(x)].

The important function to study is

\displaystyle V_{0}(x,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\det M(x,\lambda)

\displaystyle=g_{-}(x,\lambda)g_{+}(x,\lambda)

(2.11)

\displaystyle=-[\lambda+\tfrac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x)]^{2}-A^{2}(x).

The zeros of this function play an important role. So we state the following definition.

Definition 2.3
.

For a fixed value of
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, the zeros of
V_{0}(\cdot,\lambda)

in
\mathbb{C}
are called
turning points
of (
2.8
).

In our application, the potential function

V_{0}(\cdot,\lambda)
in (
2.1
), i.e.

V_{0}(x,\lambda)=-[\lambda+\tfrac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x)]^{2}-A^{2}(x)

is complex-valued for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
and the turning points are in
general complex. Assuming that the functions
A(x)
and
S(x)
extend analytically
to some fixed complex neighborhood
\Omega_{0}
of the real axis, we may consider the asymptotics
of solutions of (
2.5
) [eventually of (
2.8
)] on some contour
(in the complex
x
-plane), other than the real
x
-axis, connecting
-\infty
to
+\infty

and forcing that contour to pass through at least one pair of complex turning points.

Let us define

z(x,\lambda,\alpha)=i\int_{\gamma(\alpha\rightsquigarrow x)}\sqrt{-V_{0}(t,\lambda)}dt

for a fixed point
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
.

Definition 2.4
.

A path
\gamma
in
\Omega_{0}
along which
\Re z

is
strictly monotone will be called a
progressive path
.

Remark 2.5
.

This notion does not depend on the branch of the square root nor the base point
\alpha
.
This is equivalent to
\gamma
been transversal to the Stokes curves defined later in Definition
2.6
.
When the branch of the square root and the orientation of the path are specified, we also say
a path is
+
-progressive (resp.
-
-progressive) if
\Re z

is strictly increasing (resp. decreasing).

It is important to notice that, in our setting where
S^{\prime}
and
A
tend to 0 at infinity,

\sqrt{-V_{0}(t,\lambda)}
tends to
\pm\lambda
.
Hence if
\Im\lambda
is non-zero, then the real axis is progressive near infinity.

The level curves of
\Re[z(x,\lambda,\alpha)]
in the complex
x
-plane are very important
for what follows. Indeed,
(\pm)
-progressive curves are always transversal to those. For that,
they deserve a definition.

Definition 2.6
.

The level curves of
\Re[z(x,\lambda,\alpha)]
in the complex

x
-plane are called the
Stokes lines
(or
Stokes curves
) of
the system (
2.8
).

The geometric configuration of the Stokes curves is crucial in the investigation of
the domain of validity of the asymptotic expansion of the WKB solutions. Notice that
from a simple turning point [i.e. a simple zero of the function
V_{0}(\cdot,\lambda)

in (
2.1
)], exactly three Stokes lines emanate. At such a point, the angles
between two Stokes curves are all
2\pi/3
.

Fix
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
. With the above intuition in mind, we have the
following definition.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\{x_{-}(\lambda)
,
x_{+}(\lambda)\}
be a pair of simple complex roots of

V_{0}(x,\lambda)=0
. A contour
C=C^{-}\cup C^{0}\cup C^{+}
in
\Omega_{0}

shall be called
admissible
if the following four conditions hold true:

(i)

V_{0}
is holomorphic in
x
, in the region of the
complex
x
-plane enclosed by
C
and the real
x
-axis (this is needed to ensure
that the approximate eigenfunctions can be continued back to the real
x
-axis).

(ii)

The contour
C^{-}
is a progressive path from
x_{-}(\lambda)
to
-\infty
which
coincides with the real axis for
-\Re x\gg 1
.

(iii)

The contour
C^{+}
is a progressive path from
x_{+}(\lambda)
to
+\infty
which
coincides with the real axis for
\Re x\gg 1
.

(iv)

The contour
C^{0}
is a path from
x_{-}(\lambda)
to
x_{+}(\lambda)
along which

\Re z(x,\lambda,x_{-}(\lambda))
vanishes identically; in other words,
C^{0}
is a
Stokes line.

Remark 2.8
.

The term “admissible” also appears in
[
22
]
but here, we impose
a stronger requirement on
C^{-}
and
C^{+}
. This stronger definition is
directly applicable to the exact WKB method of the next section, and was
first used for the study of quantum resonances of the Schrödinger operator
in
[
11
]
.

Item (iv) in the list above can be reinterpreted as a differential equation for the path

C^{0}
in the complex
x
-plane. Indeed, if
x=u+iv
, where
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
, then
a field of curves is defined by the differential relation

(2.12)

\Re\bigg\{i\sqrt{-V_{0}(u+iv,\lambda)}(du+idv)\bigg\}=0.

The idea guiding the definition above is that the set of
\lambda
for which
such admissible contours exist is (hopefully) the locus of points in the complex plane that
attracts the actual eigenvalues as
\epsilon\to 0
. This cannot be true in general,
as we shall see soon, but an appropriately extented generalisation of this statement
probably is. See the last remark
2.25
of this section.

Suppose now that for a pair of complex turning points
\{x_{-}(\lambda),x_{+}(\lambda)\}

we have the condition

(2.13)

\Re\bigg\{i\int_{C^{0}(x_{-}(\lambda)\rightsquigarrow x_{+}(\lambda))}\sqrt{-V_{0}(t,\lambda)}dt\bigg\}=0

and let
\lambda
vary. Given a pair of turning points depending on
\lambda
that
are distinct throughout a domain in the complex
\lambda
-plane,
relation (
2.13
) itself determines a curve in the complex

\lambda
-plane. If
\lambda
is on one of these curves, the standard
WKB procedure can be expected to apply to determine whether
\lambda
is
in fact an
o(1)
distance away from an eigenvalue (of course, this is
subject to the curve avoiding any singularities
and the existence of appropriate progressive paths
C^{-}
,
C^{+}
).

Therefore, as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
, we expect that the discrete eigenvalues
of (
2.1
) will accumulate on the union of curves in the complex

\lambda
-plane described by formula (
2.13
),
with the union being taken over pairs of complex turning points.
Hence we are led to the following.

Definition 2.9
.

The curves in the complex
\lambda
-plane consisting of
\lambda
-points
that give rise to admissible contours
C
(on the
x
-plane) will be called

asymptotic spectral arcs
.

Definition 2.10
.

The set that is defined as the union of all the asymptotic spectral arcs in the complex

\lambda
-plane will be called the
asymptotic spectrum
of our Dirac operator.

Remark 2.11
.

It follows from the arguments above that this asymptotic spectrum is a union
of analytic arcs; if for each
\lambda
there is only a finite number of turning points,
then the asymptotic spectrum is a finite union of asymptotic spectral arcs.

Applying the exact WKB theory stated below in a neighborhood of the contour
C

(admissible contour), we shall rigorously obtain the eigenvalue approximation by

\lambda
’s satisfying

(2.14)

\frac{1}{\pi\epsilon}\Im\bigg\{i\int_{C^{0}(x_{-}(\lambda)\rightsquigarrow x_{+}(\lambda))}\sqrt{-V_{0}(t,\lambda)}dt\bigg\}-\frac{1}{2}\in\mathbb{Z}

which can be interpreted as a
Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization rule
.
Such
\lambda
’s are on the asymptotic spectral arc corresponding to the
condition (
2.13
) for the turning points
x_{-}(\lambda)
and

x_{+}(\lambda)
.

We remark that, in general, there may well be eigenvalues which are not associated
with such an asymptotic spectral arc as defined so far. This is illustrated by the
example below.

Example 2.12
.

Consider the simplest case where
S(x)
is linear, i.e.

S^{\prime}(x)=c\in{\mathbb{R}}
is constant. In this case, the rectangular region
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}

corresponds to the segment

{\mathcal{R}}_{0}=\Big\{-\frac{c}{2}\Big\}\times\Big[-\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}|A|,\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}|A|\Big].

Additionally, if
A(x)
is “bell-shaped”, i.e. if it decays at
\pm\infty
and

xA^{\prime}(x)<0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{\pm}
, then for any
\lambda
lying in either of the
segments
[-\frac{c}{2},-\frac{c}{2}+i\sup A]
or
[-\frac{c}{2},-\frac{c}{2}-i\sup A]
, there
exist exactly two real, simple turning points
x_{-}(\lambda)
,
x_{+}(\lambda)

connected by a Stokes line
C^{0}=[x_{-}(\lambda),x_{+}(\lambda)]\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, and

C_{-}=(-\infty,x_{-}(\lambda))
,
C_{+}=(x_{+}(\lambda),+\infty)
are progressive.
Hence
C={\mathbb{R}}
is an admissible contour and the vertical segments

[-\frac{c}{2},-\frac{c}{2}+i\sup A]
and
[-\frac{c}{2},-\frac{c}{2}-i\sup A]
are asymptotic
spectral arcs.

Now, if we keep the linearity of
S
but consider
A(x)
to be double humped,
say
A(x)=[(x^{2}-1)^{2}+1]^{-1}
, the complex intervals
[-\frac{c}{2},\frac{-c+i}{2}]
,

[-\frac{c}{2},\frac{-c-i}{2}]
are asymptotic spectral arcs just as above. In particular,
when
\lambda=\frac{-c\pm i}{2}
, there appears a double turning point at the origin

x=0
on the Stokes line
C^{0}=[-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}]
. However, for

\lambda\neq\frac{-c\pm i}{2}
in
[\frac{-c+i}{2},-\frac{c}{2}+i]
or
[\frac{-c-i}{2},-\frac{c}{2}-i]
,

C^{0}
splits into two Stokes lines, and such
\lambda
does not belong to the
asymptotic spectral arcs according to the above definition (although there do exist
eigenvalues near such
\lambda
, see
[
17
]
).

But there is no reason why we should not allow two or more Stokes lines apearing in an admissible
path. We thus propose the following general definition. We note
however that for the specific example we eventually focus on,
A(x)=S(x)=\sech(2x)
,
there is at most one Stokes line appearing.

Definition 2.13
.

Consider
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. In general, we call a contour
C

admissible
if the following
conditions hold.

•

V_{0}
is holomorphic in
x
, in the region of the
complex
x
-plane enclosed by
C
and the real
x
-axis.

•

C
coincides with
{\mathbb{R}}
outside a compact set.

•

C
contains a finite number of pairs of simple turning points

x_{-}^{i}(\lambda)
,
x_{+}^{i}(\lambda)
, where
i=1,...,N
, with

V_{0}(x^{i}_{\pm}(\lambda),\lambda)=0,

•

C
consists of a progressive curve
C^{-}
connecting
-\infty
and
x_{-}^{1}(\lambda)
,
a progressive curve
C^{+}
connecting
x_{+}^{N}(\lambda)
and
+\infty
, progressive
curves connecting
x^{i}_{+}(\lambda)
and
x^{i+1}_{-}(\lambda)
(
i=1,...,N-1
) and
Stokes lines connecting
x^{i}_{-}(\lambda)
and
x^{i}_{+}(\lambda)
,
i=1,...,N
.

Even this more general definition cannot be completely comprehensive as a
characterization of eigenvalues, since it does not take into account double
truning points, which can exist, at least non-generically, as we will eventually see.

2.2.
The exact WKB method

In this and next subsection
2.3
, we review the local
theory of the exact WKB method applied to our Zakharov-Shabat operator in a
fixed open set in
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Let us fix
\lambda
, let
\Omega
be a simply connected subdomain of the

x
-complex plane free from turning points, and take a fixed point

\alpha\in\Omega
. We define a
phase map

\displaystyle z(\cdot,\lambda,\alpha):\Omega

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\quad\text{with}

(2.15)

\displaystyle z(x,\lambda,\alpha)=i\int_{\gamma(\alpha\rightsquigarrow x)}

\displaystyle\sqrt{-V_{0}(t,\lambda)}dt

where of course the path of integration is irrelevant and we choose the
branch of
\sqrt{-V_{0}(x,\lambda)}
which makes it positive for large
x>0
.
Observe that
z
is uniquely defined in
\Omega
and it maps

\Omega
conformally to
z(\Omega,\lambda,\alpha)
.

We look for solutions of (
2.8
) having the form

(2.16)

\textbf{v}(x,\lambda,\epsilon,\alpha)=\exp\bigg\{\pm\frac{z(x,\lambda,\alpha)}{\epsilon}\bigg\}\tilde{\textbf{w}}^{\pm}[z(x,\lambda,\alpha)]

for

\tilde{\textbf{w}}^{\pm}=[\tilde{w}^{\pm}_{1}\hskip 3.0pt\tilde{w}^{\pm}_{2}]^{T}
.
Substituting this in (
2.8
), we get the system

(2.17)

\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dz}\tilde{\textbf{w}}^{\pm}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}\pm i&H(z)^{-2}\\
-H(z)^{2}&\pm i\end{bmatrix}\tilde{\textbf{w}}^{\pm}(z)

where [upon recalling (
2.10
)] the function
H
is given by

H(z)=\bigg[\frac{g_{-}(x,\lambda)}{g_{+}(x,\lambda)}\bigg]^{1/4}.

Finally, we set

P_{\pm}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}H(z)^{-1}&H(z)^{-1}\\
iH(z)&-iH(z)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}^{\frac{1\pm 1}{2}}

and apply the transformation

\tilde{\textbf{w}}^{\pm}(z)=P_{\pm}(z)\textbf{w}^{\pm}(z)

for
\textbf{w}^{\pm}=[w^{\pm}_{\rm even}\hskip 3.0ptw^{\pm}_{\rm odd}]^{T}
.
Then the system in (
2.17
) is written as

(2.18)

\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dz}\textbf{w}^{\pm}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathcal{H}(z)\\
\mathcal{H}(z)&\mp\frac{2}{\epsilon}\end{bmatrix}\textbf{w}^{\pm}(z)

in which we consider
\mathcal{H}
to be defined by

(2.19)

\mathcal{H}(z)=\frac{\dot{H}(z)}{H(z)}

and where the dot denotes differentiation with respect to
z
. Easily, one can
arrive at

\mathcal{H}(z)=\frac{d}{dz}\log[H(z)]=\frac{1}{4}\frac{A(x)S^{\prime\prime}(x)-2\lambda A^{\prime}(x)-A^{\prime}(x)S^{\prime}(x)}{\big\{[\lambda+\tfrac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x)]^{2}+A^{2}(x)\big\}^{3/2}}.

In the usual WKB theory, the vector valued
symbols

\textbf{w}^{\pm}
are
constructed as a power series in the parameter
\epsilon
, which is in general divergent.
Here we would like to introduce the reader to the so-called
exact
WKB method (along
the lines of Gérard-Grigis
[
13
]
and Fujiié-Lasser-Nédélec
[
10
]
). This method
consists in the resummation of this divergent series in the following way. By taking a point

x_{0}\in\Omega
, setting

z_{0}\equiv z_{0}(\lambda)=z(x_{0},\lambda,\alpha)

and postulating the
series ansatz

(2.20)

\textbf{w}^{\pm}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\textbf{w}^{\pm}_{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\begin{bmatrix}w^{\pm}_{2n}\\
w^{\pm}_{2n-1}\end{bmatrix}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}w^{\pm}_{\rm even}\\
w^{\pm}_{\rm odd}\end{bmatrix}

we see from (
2.18
) that the functions
w^{\pm}_{n},~-1\leq n\in\mathbb{Z},

can be defined inductively by

w_{-1}^{\pm}\equiv 0,\quad w_{0}^{\pm}\equiv 1

and for
1\leq n\in\mathbb{Z}

(2.21)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\frac{d}{dz}w_{2n}^{\pm}(z)&=\mathcal{H}(z)w_{2n-1}^{\pm}(z)\\
(\frac{d}{dz}\pm\frac{2}{\epsilon})w_{2n-1}^{\pm}(z)&=\mathcal{H}(z)w_{2n-2}^{\pm}(z)\\
w_{n}^{\pm}(z_{0})&=0.\end{array}\right.

These recurrence equations uniquely determine (at least in a neighborhood
of
x_{0}
) the sequence of scalar functions
\{w_{n}^{\pm}(z,\epsilon,z_{0})\}_{n=-1}^{\infty}

and hence the sequence of vector-valued functions

\{\textbf{w}_{n}^{\pm}(z,\epsilon,z_{0})\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
.
We can write the relations (
2.21
) in an integral form if we introduce
the following integral operators
\mathcal{I}_{\pm}
and
\mathcal{J}
taking
functions from
\mathscr{H}(z(\Omega,\lambda,\alpha))
to functions in

\mathscr{H}(z(\Omega,\lambda,\alpha))
by

(2.22)

\mathcal{I}_{\pm}[f](z)=\int_{\Gamma}e^{\pm 2(\zeta-z)/\epsilon}\mathcal{H}(\zeta)f(\zeta)d\zeta

(2.23)

\mathcal{J}[f](z)=\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{H}(\zeta)f(\zeta)d\zeta

where
\Gamma\equiv\Gamma(z_{0}\rightsquigarrow z)
is the image by
z

of a path
\gamma\equiv\gamma(x_{0}\rightsquigarrow x)
in
\Omega

connecting
x_{0}
,
x
; in other words
\Gamma=z(\gamma)
(for notation
cf. §
1.3
). Then (
2.21
) for
n\geq 1
becomes

(2.24)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}w_{2n}^{\pm}&=\mathcal{J}[w_{2n-1}^{\pm}]\\
w_{2n-1}^{\pm}&=\mathcal{I}_{\pm}[w_{2n-2}^{\pm}].\end{array}\right.

Going back to the initial system (
2.5
),
we have constructed (formal) WKB solutions of the form

(2.25)

\textbf{u}^{\pm}(x,\lambda,\epsilon,\alpha,x_{0})=\\
\exp\Big\{\pm\frac{z(x,\lambda,\alpha)}{\epsilon}\Big\}Q(x,\lambda,\epsilon)\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}^{\frac{1\pm 1}{2}}\textbf{w}^{\pm}[z(x,\lambda,\alpha),\epsilon,z_{0}]

where
Q(\cdot,\lambda,\epsilon)
is a matrix-valued function with value

Q(x,\lambda,\epsilon)=\begin{bmatrix}\exp\{-iS(x)/(2\epsilon)\}&0\\
0&\exp\big\{iS(x)/(2\epsilon)\}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
-1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}H(z)^{-1}&H(z)^{-1}\\
iH(z)&-iH(z)\end{bmatrix}.

Here, we added the superscript
\pm
to the solution

\textbf{u}=[u_{1}\hskip 3.0ptu_{2}]^{T}
-consequently arriving at the
notation
\textbf{u}^{\pm}=[u^{\pm}_{1}\hskip 3.0ptu^{\pm}_{2}]
-
to distinguish between the two different cases in the previous
discussion; also we keep in mind that the WKB solutions
in (
2.25
) depend on a base point
\alpha
for the phase
map (
2.15
) and a base point
x_{0}
for the symbols

\textbf{w}^{\pm}
.

Recall Definition
2.4
and write

\Omega_{\pm}=\{\,x\in\Omega\mid\exists\hskip 3.0pt(\pm)\text{-progressive path}\hskip 3.0pt\gamma(x_{0}\rightsquigarrow x)\subset\Omega\,\}.

We have the following basic theorem; for the proof, we refer to
[
10
]
.

Theorem 2.14
.

The exact WKB solutions in (
2.25
) satisfy the following properties.

(i)

The formal series in (
2.20
) is absolutely convergent in a
neighborhood of
x_{0}
.

(ii)

For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{\pm}-\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbf{w}^{\pm}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{N})\quad\text{as}\quad\epsilon\downarrow 0

\displaystyle w^{\pm}_{\rm even}-\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w_{2n}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{N})\quad\text{as}\quad\epsilon\downarrow 0

\displaystyle w^{\pm}_{\rm odd}-\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w_{2n-1}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{N})\quad\text{as}\quad\epsilon\downarrow 0

uniformly in any compact subset of
\Omega_{\pm}
. In particular,
there we have

\displaystyle w^{\pm}_{\rm even}

\displaystyle=1+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)\quad\text{as}\quad\epsilon\downarrow 0

\displaystyle\quad w^{\pm}_{\rm odd}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)\quad\text{as}\quad\epsilon\downarrow 0.

(iii)

Any two exact WKB solutions with different base points
for the symbol satisfy

(2.26)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}^{+}(x,\lambda,\epsilon,\alpha,x_{0}),{\bf u}^{-}(x,\lambda,\epsilon,\alpha,x_{1})]

\displaystyle=4iw_{\rm even}^{+}(z_{1},\epsilon,z_{0})

(2.27)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}^{+}(x,\lambda,\epsilon,\alpha,x_{0}),{\bf u}^{+}(x,\lambda,\epsilon,\alpha,x_{1})]

\displaystyle=-4ie^{2z_{1}/\epsilon}w_{\rm odd}^{+}(z_{1},\epsilon,z_{0})

where
x_{0}
,
x_{1}
are two different points in
\Omega
and

z_{j}=z(x_{j},\lambda,\alpha)
for
j=0,1
.

2.3.
Connection around a simple turning point

We continue in this section by presenting a local connection formula for exact WKB
solutions near a simple turning point. Let
\alpha
be a simple turning point, i.e.

\displaystyle\det M(\alpha)

\displaystyle=g_{+}(\alpha)g_{-}(\alpha)=0

\displaystyle(\det M)^{\prime}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=g_{+}(\alpha)g^{\prime}_{-}(\alpha)+g^{\prime}_{+}(\alpha)g_{-}(\alpha)\neq 0

which means that
\alpha
is a simple zero of one and only one of either
g_{+}(x)
or

g_{-}(x)
. Three Stokes lines
l_{0}
,
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
, numbered in the anti-clockwise
sense, emanate from
\alpha
and devide a neighborhood
\omega
of
\alpha
into
three regions
\omega_{0}
bounded by
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
,
\omega_{1}
bounded by
l_{2}

and
l_{0}
and
\omega_{2}
bounded by
l_{0}
and
l_{1}
. In each of these regions,
we are going to define exact WKB solutions
{\bf u}_{0}
,
{\bf u}_{1}
and
{\bf u}_{2}

respectively in such a way that its asymptotic behavior is known near
\alpha
by
Theorem
2.14
.

We have first to specify the branch of the multi-valued function

z(x,\lambda,\alpha)
defined by (
2.15
).
Let us take a base point
x_{j}
in
\omega_{j}

(j=0,1,2)
.
We put a branch cut on the Stokes line
l_{1}
, and take the branch of the function

\sqrt{\det M}
so that

\alpha

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{0}(x,\epsilon)={\bf u}^{+}(x,\lambda,\epsilon,\alpha,x_{0}),

(2.29)

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{1}(x,\epsilon)={\bf u}^{-}(x,\lambda,\epsilon,\alpha,x_{1}),

(2.30)

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{2}(x,\epsilon)={\bf u}^{+}(x,\lambda,\epsilon,\alpha,x_{2}).

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
{\bf u}_{0}
,
{\bf u}_{1}
,
{\bf u}_{2}
be three solutions to the differential equation
(
2.2
) in a region

(2.31)

{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2}]{\bf u}_{0}+{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{2},{\bf u}_{0}]{\bf u}_{1}+{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{0},{\bf u}_{1}]{\bf u}_{2}=0.

Proof.

If the three determinants
{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2}]
,
{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{2},{\bf u}_{0}]

and
{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{0},{\bf u}_{1}]
are all 0, the identity holds obviously.
Suppose that at least one of those does not vanish, say
{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2}]\neq 0
.
Then by taking the determinant of the LHS of (

{\bf u}_{1}
we find

{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2}]{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{0},{\bf u}_{1}]+{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{0},{\bf u}_{1}]{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{2},{\bf u}_{1}]

which is obviously zero. In the same way, we obtain that the determinant of the LHS of
(
2.31
) with

is zero. Since the pair of solutions

∎

Let us come back to our exact WKB solutions

,
{\bf u}_{1}
and
{\bf u}_{2}

defined above and compute the asymptotic behavior of the determinants appearing in

Proposition 2.16
.

As

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{0},{\bf u}_{1}]=2i(1+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon)),

(2.33)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2}]=-2i(1+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon)),

(2.34)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{2},{\bf u}_{0}]=\mp 2(1+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon)),

. In
particular, any two of the solutions

{\bf u}_{1}
and

Proof.

Using Theorem
2.14
(iii), we find

{\mathcal{W}}[{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2}]=-2iw_{\rm even}^{+}(z_{1},\epsilon,z_{2})

where

.
Since there exists a
(+)
-progressive path from
x_{2}
to

For the computation of the determinant

careful since there is a branch cut between

and
x_{0}
. Before applying the

on the Riemann sheet continued from

passing across the branch. Let

\displaystyle z(x,\lambda,\alpha)=-z(\hat{x},\lambda,\alpha),

\displaystyle H(x)=\mp iH(\hat{x}),\,\text{ if }\alpha\text{ is a zero of }g_{\pm}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle w^{\pm}(z,\epsilon,z_{2})=w^{\mp}(z,\epsilon,z_{2}).

Remark 2.24

We claim that no eigenvalue can appear away from the asymptotic spectral arcs.

Remark 2.25
.

The question arises whether there is a possibility of a general proof of the fact
that no eigenvalue can appear away from the asymptotic spectral arcs. A consequence
of this would be a general proof that the limiting spectrum (the minimal set such that
every actual eigenvalue lies
\epsilon
-close to this set) would be what we have called
the asymptotic spectrum (which we defined in terms of the existence of an admissible path).
In turn that would prove that the limiting spectrum is a union of analytic arcs.

A possible strategy is suggested by an analogous problem appearing in the semiclassical
analysis of the inverse scattering problem in
[
19

where a crucial ingredient of the
proof is the existence of appropriate “steepest descent paths”. The existence proof
in

In our own problem here, we can define a generalized admissible contour as a contour

path from
x=-\infty
to
x=+\infty

will show the existence of the desired contour which is either a progressive path from

\pm 1+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

Proof.

In this section we will consider the reflection coefficient for our Dirac

5.1.
Reflection away from zero.

We begin with the case where this

is
independent
of
\epsilon

(actually, by applying the transformation above, we get two independent equations;

(5.1)

\frac{d^{2}W}{dx^{2}}=[-\epsilon^{-2}f(x,\lambda)+g(x,\lambda)]y

(5.2)

\displaystyle g(x,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\big(\log\{A(x)-i[\lambda+\tfrac{A^{\prime}(x)}{2}]\}\big)^{\prime}\Big]^{2}-\frac{\Big(\{A(x)-i[\lambda+\tfrac{A^{\prime}(x)}{2}]\}^{1/2}\Big)^{\prime\prime}}{\{A(x)-i[\lambda+\tfrac{A^{\prime}(x)}{2}]\}^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4}\Bigg\{\frac{A^{\prime}(x)-i\frac{A^{\prime\prime}(x)}{2}}{A(x)-i[\lambda+\tfrac{A^{\prime}(x)}{2}]}\Bigg\}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{A^{\prime\prime}(x)-i\frac{A^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)}{2}}{A(x)-i[\lambda+\tfrac{A^{\prime}(x)}{2}]}

We have the following definitions.

Definition 5.1
.

We define an

for equation
(
5.1
), to be a primitive of

(5.4)

\frac{1}{f^{1/4}(x,\lambda)}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\Big[\frac{1}{f^{1/4}(x,\lambda)}\Big]-\frac{g(x,\lambda)}{f^{1/2}(x,\lambda)}.

Definition 5.2
.

We define the

of
H
in the interval

to be given by

(5.5)

\mathcal{V}_{x_{1},x_{2}}[H](\lambda)=\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\bigg|\frac{1}{f^{1/4}(t,\lambda)}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\Big[\frac{1}{f^{1/4}(t,\lambda)}\Big]-\frac{g(t,\lambda)}{f^{1/2}(t,\lambda)}\bigg|dt.

Observe that (
5.2
) implies
f>0
in
{\mathbb{R}}
. Consequently,
equation (
5.1
) has no turning points. Furthermore,
notice that

•

g
is complex-valued

•

f
is twice continuously differentiable with respect to
x
and

•

g
is continuous.

These properties allow one (cf. Theorem
2.2
of §
2.4

from chapter 6 of
[

]

of the same chapter) to arrive at the following theorem.

Theorem 5.3

Take an arbitrary interval

The remainders

|r_{\pm}(x,\epsilon)|\hskip 5.0pt,\hskip 5.0pt\epsilon f^{-1/2}(x,\epsilon)\Big|\frac{\partial r_{\pm}}{\partial x}(x,\epsilon)\Big|\leq\exp\{\epsilon\mathcal{V}_{\kappa,x}[H](\lambda)\}-1

where

\mathcal{V}_{\kappa,x}[H](\lambda)<+\infty

Remark 5.4
.

Since

is not real, we cannot expect the solutions

to be complex
conjugates.

Remark 5.5
.

It follows that

•

r_{\pm}(x,\epsilon)\rightarrow 0
as

•

\epsilon f^{-1/2}(x,\epsilon)\frac{\partial r_{\pm}}{\partial x}(x,\epsilon)\rightarrow 0
as

Using (
5.4
), notice that

is independent
of
\epsilon

\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
and fixed

\mathcal{V}_{x_{1},x_{2}}[H](\lambda)<+\infty

w_{\pm}(x,\epsilon)\sim f^{-1/4}(x,\lambda)\exp\Big\{\pm\frac{i}{\epsilon}\int f^{1/2}(t,\lambda)dt\Big\}\quad\text{as}\quad\epsilon\downarrow 0

uniformly in

.

Next we define the
Jost solutions
. The Jost solutions are defined as
the components of the bases

From scattering theory, we know that the

(5.7)

\mathcal{R}(\lambda,\epsilon)=\frac{W[J_{-}^{l},J_{-}^{r}]}{W[J_{+}^{r},J_{-}^{l}]}.

The next step is to construct the Jost solutions as

. For this, we define the following four

\displaystyle\bar{w}_{\pm}^{l}(x,\epsilon)=f^{-1/4}(x,\lambda)\exp\Big\{\pm\frac{i}{\epsilon}\Big(\lambda x+\int_{-\infty}^{x}[f^{1/2}(t,\lambda)-\lambda]dt\Big)\Big\}[1+\bar{r}_{\pm}^{l}(x,\epsilon)]

\displaystyle\bar{w}_{\pm}^{r}(x,\epsilon)=f^{-1/4}(x,\lambda)\exp\Big\{\pm\frac{i}{\epsilon}\Big(\lambda x+\int_{+\infty}^{x}[f^{1/2}(t,\lambda)-\lambda]dt\Big)\Big\}[1+\bar{r}_{\pm}^{r}(x,\epsilon)]

\displaystyle J_{\pm}^{l}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{1/2}\bar{w}_{\pm}^{l}

\displaystyle J_{\pm}^{r}

Let now

be four WKB solutions satisfying

\displaystyle w_{\pm}^{l}(x,\epsilon)=f^{-1/4}(x,\lambda)\exp\Big\{\pm\frac{i}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{x}f^{1/2}(t,\lambda)dt\Big\}[1+r_{\pm}^{l}(x,\epsilon)]

(5.9)

\displaystyle w_{\pm}^{r}(x,\epsilon)=f^{-1/4}(x,\lambda)\exp\Big\{\pm\frac{i}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{x}f^{1/2}(t,\lambda)dt\Big\}[1+r_{\pm}^{r}(x,\epsilon)].

\displaystyle J_{\pm}^{l}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{1/2}\exp\Big\{\pm\frac{i}{\epsilon}\int_{-\infty}^{0}[f^{1/2}(t,\lambda)-\lambda]dt\Big\}w_{\pm}^{l}\big(1+\text{o}(1)\big)

(5.11)

6.
Conclusion: Application to the semiclassical focusing NLS

In a sense what we have been able to prove in the previous sections is that the behavior of the solution of
(
1.1
) in the regime
\epsilon\downarrow 0
can be approximated by a
‘‘soliton ensemble”, i.e a purely soliton solution. This solution is uniquely defined
by the eigenvalues of the associated Dirac operator and their corresponding norming
constants: the eigenvalues are exactly the WKB approximations of the previous sections
while the norming constants alternate between -1 and 1. The role of the continuous part of
the spectrum is negligible when epsilon is small, since the reflection coefficient is
small away from zero

8
8

8

We refer to
[
16
]
for the actual details justifying the approximation.
.
The remaining question is the semiclassical behavior of the soliton ensemble itself.

This is a generalization of the problem considered in
[
19
]
and
[
20
]
, the difference
being that here the relevant WKB-eigenvalues lie in the
Y
-shaped set of Figure
10

(together with its reflection with respect to the real axis), while in
[
19
]
the spectrum
is purely imaginary (the four non-imaginary “branches” are not there). Even though the proofs
of
[
19
]
and
[
20
]
are long and complicated they can be readily transferred from one
case to another.

We simply recall that the starting point of the strategy in
[
19
]
consists of considering
a fairly arbitrary contour around the imaginary discrete spectrum and transforming the meromorphic
Riemann-Hilbert problem (with no jump contour) given by the inverse scattering theory to a
holomorphic Riemann-Hilbert problem with jump given on that fairly arbitrary contour.
Eventually

this contour has to be deformed to an optimal contour on which the asymptotic analysis of the
holomorphic Riemann-Hilbert problem will be feasible.

The limiting density of eigenvalues is a crucial quantity in the analysis.
In the special case
A(x)=S(x)=\sech(2x)
, for example,

(6.1)

\rho(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda}{\pi}\int_{x_{1}(\lambda)}^{x_{2}(\lambda)}\frac{dx}{(A(x)^{2}+(\lambda+\frac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x))^{2})^{1/2}}

for

(6.3)

\rho(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda}{\pi}\int_{x_{2}(\lambda)}^{x_{6}(\lambda)}\frac{dx}{(A(x)^{2}+(\lambda+\frac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(x))^{2})^{1/2}}

for
\lambda\in\tilde{\Lambda}_{26}

By symmetry, the last two integrals are equal and it is not hard to see that all three integrals
are analytic in the upper half-plane, the top integral one being equal to the sum of the two others

9

9

Just write every
integral as a contour integral on a closed curve wrapping around each
\tilde{\Lambda}_{ij}
and then deform to
a curve independent of
\lambda
to get local analyticity. Then, as
\lambda
varies,
start deforming those closed curves along the moving cuts (actually Stokes curves moving with

One can deform such that the number of turning points along each deformation remains two and cover the whole upper half-plane.

where a third turning point appears.

.
So
\rho
is not continuous at the bifurcation point

A.1.

Let us in this section of the appendix describe some properties of Modified Bessel

reader to
[

]
and §2 of
[

]
.

We start by investigating solutions of the following equation

\frac{d^{2}w(t)}{dt^{2}}=\frac{9}{4}tw(t).

When

is real we shall adopt the function

(A.2)

U(t)=\Big(\frac{2t}{\pi}\Big)^{1/2}K_{1/3}(t^{3/2})

being

to assume their principal values when

\mathrm{ph}t

A.2.
Secondary solutions

Let us fix some notation and define some
sectors
on the complex plane. So, for

we set

S_{j}=\{\,x\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid(2j-1)\tfrac{\pi}{3}\leq\mathrm{ph}x\leq(2j+1)\tfrac{\pi}{3}\,\}.

If there exists a number

We now introduce the secondary solutions for equation (

). Here, we
treat

It can be easily observed that

is a solution of equation (

)
that is decaying in the sector

, as

.

For

\displaystyle W[U_{j},U_{k}]

\displaystyle=3ie^{-i(j+k)\pi/3}\lambda_{j,k}\quad\text{where}

\displaystyle\lambda_{j,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin[(k-j)\pi/3]}{\sin\pi/3}

From this, we conclude that
U_{j}
,

are linearly independent when

with

.

Note
: throughout this section, we consider

with

First of all, it is well known that as

S_{j}\cup S_{k}
and in order to majorize these solutions, we shall introduce auxiliary
weight, modulus and phase functions.

We start by defining the function
E(t)
by the formula

E(t)=|\exp\{(-1)^{j}t^{3/2}\}|\quad\text{for}\quad t\in S_{j}

where the branch of
t^{3/2}
is
|t|^{3/2}\exp\{i3(\mathrm{ph}t)/2\}
.On the boundaries of

S_{j}
,
E(t)=1
and so
E
is continuous everywhere with
E(t)\geq 1
. As weight
functions in
S_{j}\cup S_{k}
we assign the functions
\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)
defined by

\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)=\begin{cases}1/E(t),\quad t\in S_{j}\\
E(t),\quad t\in S_{k}\end{cases}

It is a continuous function with
\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(0)=1
. Also it satisfies the following symmetry

\mathsf{E}_{k,j}(t)=1/\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)\quad\text{for}\quad t\in S_{j}\cup S_{k}.

For the modulus and phase functions in
S_{j}\cup S_{k}
we define

\displaystyle|U_{j}(t)|

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)\mathsf{M}_{j,k}(t)\cos\mathsf{\theta}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle|U_{j}^{\prime}(t)|

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)\mathsf{N}_{j,k}(t)\cos\mathsf{\omega}_{j,k}(t)

and

\displaystyle|U_{k}(t)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)}\mathsf{M}_{j,k}(t)\sin\mathsf{\theta}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle|U_{k}^{\prime}(t)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)}\mathsf{N}_{j,k}(t)\sin\mathsf{\omega}_{j,k}(t)

From these, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\bigg[\frac{|U_{j}(t)|^{2}}{\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)^{2}}+\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)^{2}|U_{k}(t)|^{2}\bigg]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\mathsf{N}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\bigg[\frac{|U_{j}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}}{\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)^{2}}+\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)^{2}|U_{k}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\bigg]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\mathsf{\theta}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\arctan\bigg[\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)^{2}\bigg|\frac{U_{k}(t)}{U_{j}(t)}\bigg|\bigg]

\displaystyle\mathsf{\omega}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\arctan\bigg[\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)^{2}\bigg|\frac{U_{k}^{\prime}(t)}{U_{j}^{\prime}(t)}\bigg|\bigg]

The above formulae show that each one of
\mathsf{M}_{j,k}(t),\mathsf{N}_{j,k}(t),\mathsf{\theta}_{j,k}(t)

and
\mathsf{\omega}_{j,k}(t)
is continuous in
S_{j}\cup S_{k}
. Also we have the following
symmetry relations

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{M}_{k,j}(t)

\displaystyle\mathsf{N}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{N}_{k,j}(t)

\displaystyle\mathsf{\theta}_{j,k}(t)+

\displaystyle\mathsf{\theta}_{k,j}(t)=\frac{\pi}{2}

\displaystyle\mathsf{\omega}_{j,k}(t)+

\displaystyle\mathsf{\omega}_{k,j}(t)=\frac{\pi}{2}

In internal parts of
S_{j}
,
S_{k}
, for large
|t|
the asymptotic behavior of the modulus
functions is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle\sim(1+\lambda_{j,k}^{2})^{1/2}|t|^{-1/4}

\displaystyle\mathsf{N}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{3}{2}(1+\lambda_{j,k}^{2})^{1/2}|t|^{1/4}.

But in the full domain
S_{j}\cup S_{k}
, the modulus and phase functions fluctuate
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. For
\mathsf{M}_{j,k}(t)
we have the following bound

\mathsf{M}_{j,k}(t)\leq C_{j,k}|t|^{-1/4}[1+\Theta(t^{3/2})]\quad\text{for}\quad t\in S_{j}\cup S_{k}

where
\Theta(t)
and
C_{j,k}
are given by

(A.3)

\displaystyle\Theta(t)

\displaystyle=\exp\{5\pi/(72|t|)\}-1

(A.4)

\displaystyle C_{j,k}

\displaystyle=\max\{[1+(|\lambda_{j,k}|+|\lambda_{j,k+1}|)^{2}]^{1/2},[1+(|\lambda_{j,k}|+|\lambda_{j,k-1}|)^{2}]^{1/2}\}.

Unfortunately, no such simple bound is available for
\mathsf{N}_{j,k}(t)
and for this we shall
introduce another pair of modulus and phase functions that will be convenient to work
with. This is defined by

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{d[t^{1/4}U_{j}(t)]}{dt}\bigg|

\displaystyle=|t|^{1/4}\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)\hat{\mathsf{N}}_{j,k}(t)\cos\hat{\mathsf{\omega}}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{d[t^{1/4}U_{k}(t)]}{dt}\bigg|

\displaystyle=|t|^{1/4}\frac{1}{\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)}\hat{\mathsf{N}}_{j,k}(t)\sin\hat{\mathsf{\omega}}_{j,k}(t)

Immediately, we find that

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{N}}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle=|t|^{-1/4}\bigg\{\frac{1}{\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)^{2}}\bigg|\frac{d[t^{1/4}U_{j}(t)]}{dt}\bigg|^{2}+\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)^{2}\bigg|\frac{d[t^{1/4}U_{k}(t)]}{dt}\bigg|^{2}\bigg\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{\omega}}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\arctan\bigg\{\mathsf{E}_{j,k}(t)^{2}\bigg|\frac{d[t^{1/4}U_{k}(t)]}{dt}\bigg|\Big/\bigg|\frac{d[t^{1/4}U_{j}(t)]}{dt}\bigg|\bigg\}.

Symmetry now tell us that

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{N}}_{j,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathsf{N}}_{k,j}(t)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{\omega}}_{j,k}(t)+

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{\omega}}_{k,j}(t)=\frac{\pi}{2}

while as
t\rightarrow\infty
in internal parts of
S_{j}
or
S_{k}
we have

\hat{\mathsf{N}}_{j,k}(t)\sim\frac{3}{2}(1+\lambda_{j,k}^{2})^{1/2}|t|^{1/4}.

Lastly,
\hat{\mathsf{N}}_{j,k}(t)
satisfies the following bound

\hat{\mathsf{N}}_{j,k}(t)\leq\frac{3}{2}C_{j,k}|t|^{1/4}[1+\Theta(t^{3/2})]\quad\text{for}\quad t\in S_{j}\cup S_{k}.

where
\Theta(t)
and
C_{j,k}
are given by (
A.3
) and (
A.4
)
respectively.
