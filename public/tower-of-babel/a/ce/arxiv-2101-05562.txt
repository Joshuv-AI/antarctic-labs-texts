Perturbation determinants and discrete spectra of semi-infinite non-self-adjoint Jacobi operators
L. Golinskii

Abstract
. We study the trace class perturbations of the half-line, discrete Laplacian and obtain a new bound for the perturbation determinant of the corresponding non-self-adjoint Jacobi operator. Based on this bound, we obtain the Lieb–Thirring inequalities for such operators. The spectral enclosure for the discrete spectrum and embedded eigenvalues are also discussed.

Introduction

In the last two decades there was a splash of activity around the spectral theory of non-self-adjoint perturbations
of some classical operators of mathematical physics, such as the Laplace and Dirac operators on the whole space,
their fractional powers, and others. Recently, there has been some interest in studying certain discrete models of the above
problem. In particular, the structure of the spectrum for compact, non-self-adjoint perturbations of the free Jacobi
and the discrete Dirac operators has attracted much attention lately. Actually the problem concerns the discrete component
of the spectrum and the rate of its accumulation to the essential spectrum. Such type of results under the various assumptions
on the perturbations are united under a common name
Lieb–Thirring inequalities
.
For a nice account of the existing results on the problem for non-self-adjoint, two-sided Jacobi
operators, the reader may consult two recent surveys
[
7
]
and
[
10
, Section 5.13]
and references therein.

The spectral theory of semi-infinite,
self-adjoint
Jacobi matrices is quite popular owing to their tight relation to the
theory of orthogonal polynomials on the real line
[
19
]
. In contrast, there are only a few papers where semi-infinite, non-self-adjoint
Jacobi matrices are examined
[
18
,
1
,
2
,
8
,
13
,
14
,
4
]
.

The main object under consideration is a semi-infinite Jacobi matrix

(0.1)

J(\{a_{j}\},\{b_{j}\},\{c_{j}\})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{1}&c_{1}&&&\\
a_{1}&b_{2}&c_{2}&&\\
&a_{2}&b_{3}&c_{3}&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{bmatrix},

with uniformly bounded complex entries, and
a_{j}c_{j}\not=0
,
j\in{\mathbb{N}}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
. The spectral theory of the underlying non-self-adjoint
Jacobi operator includes, among others, the structure of its spectrum. We denote by
J_{0}
the semi-infinite discrete Laplacian,
i.e.,
J_{0}=J(\{1\},\{0\},\{1\})
. If
J-J_{0}
is a compact operator, that is,

\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}=1,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}=0,

the geometric image of the spectrum is plainly evident

\sigma(J)=\sigma_{ess}(J_{0})\cup\sigma_{d}(J)=[-2,2]\cup\sigma_{d}(J),

the discrete component
\sigma_{d}(J)
, i.e., the set of isolated eigenvalues of finite algebraic multiplicity, is an at most countable
set of points in
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash[-2,2]
with the only possible limit points on
[-2,2]
.
To get some quantitative information on the rate of such accumulation one has to impose some further assumptions on the perturbation. Our case of
study in the paper is the
trace class
perturbations of the discrete Laplacian
J_{0}

(0.2)

J-J_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\ \Leftrightarrow\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(|1-a_{n}|+|b_{n}|+|1-c_{n}|)<\infty,

see, e.g.,
[
17
, Lemma 2.3]
.

Our strategy is similar to that in
[
8
]
and
[
12
]
. The key issue is the bound for the
perturbation determinant

(0.3)

L(\lambda,J):=\det(I+(J-J_{0})(J_{0}-\lambda)^{-1})

introduced by M.G. Krein
[
11
]
in the late 1950s. The main feature
of this analytic function on the resolvent set
\rho(J_{0})=\overline{{\mathbb{C}}}\backslash[-2,2]
is that the set of its zeros agrees with the discrete spectrum
of the perturbed operator
J
, and moreover, the multiplicity of each zero equals the algebraic multiplicity of the corresponding eigenvalue.
So the original problem of the spectral theory can be restated as a classical problem of the zero distributions of analytic functions, which goes back
to Jensen and Blaschke.

The argument is pursued in two steps. The first one results in the bound for the perturbation determinant, typical for the functions
of non-radial growth. The classes of such analytic (and subharmonic) functions in the unit disk were introduced and studied in

[
5
,
9
]
(for some advances see
[
6
]
). The Blaschke-type conditions for the zero sets (Riesz measures) were proved therein,
with an important amplification in
[
15
, Theorem 4]
, better adapted for applications.
The second step is just the latter result applied to the bound mentioned above.

Such two-step algorithm is applied, by and large, in
[
15
]
to the two-sided Jacobi operators.
In our approach to the problem the argument in the first step is totally different. The point is that for semi-infinite
Jacobi operators neither the Fourier transform machinery, nor a simple matrix representation for the resolvent of the free Jacobi operator are at
our disposal. Instead, we deal with the associated three-term recurrence relation

(0.4)

u_{k-1}+b_{k}u_{k}+a_{k}c_{k}u_{k+1}=\lambda(z)u_{k},\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}},\quad\lambda(z)=z+\frac{1}{z},

and its solutions. Here
\lambda(\cdot)
is the Zhukovsky function which maps the unit disk onto the resolvent set

\rho(J_{0})=\overline{{\mathbb{C}}}\backslash[-2,2]
.

The relation between solutions of (
0.4
) and eigenvectors of
J
is straightforward:
(v_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is an eigenvector of
J
if and
only if

(u_{k})_{k\geq 0}:\ \ u_{0}=0,\quad u_{1}=v_{1},\quad u_{k}=\frac{v_{k}}{a_{1}\ldots a_{k-1}}\,,\quad k\geq 2

is a solution of (
0.4
).

The solution
u^{+}=(u^{+}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of (
0.4
) is called the
Jost solution
if

(0.5)

\lim_{k\to\infty}z^{-k}u_{k}^{+}(z)=1,\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{0}:={\mathbb{D}}\backslash\{0\}.

Under certain assumptions on
J
the Jost solution exists and unique (see Theorem
1.2
below).

We study the Jost solutions by reducing the difference equation (
0.4
) to a Volterra-type discrete integral equation, the standard idea
in analysis, see, e.g.,
[
20
, Section 7.5]
,
[
8
,
12
]
. The bounds for the Jost solutions stem from the successive approximations method.
The crucial point in such an approach is that the perturbation determinant agrees with the first coordinate
u^{+}_{0}
of the Jost solution,
known as the
Jost function
, see the non-self-adjoint version of Killip and Simon
[
17
, Theorem 2.16]

(the calculation there has nothing to do with self-adjointness).

The Lieb–Thirring inequality for the discrete spectrum of semi-infinite Jacobi operators looks as follows.

Theorem 0.1
.

Let
J-J_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
. Then for each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there is a constant
C(\varepsilon)>0
so that

(0.6)

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{d}(J)}\frac{\dist(\lambda,[-2,2])}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}}}}\leq C(\varepsilon)\Delta,\quad\Delta:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(|b_{n}|+|1-a_{n}c_{n}|).

If
J
is the discrete Schrödinger operator, that is,
a_{n}=c_{n}\equiv 1
, then

(0.7)

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{d}(J)}\frac{\dist(\lambda,[-2,2])}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}}}}\leq C(\varepsilon)\|J-J_{0}\|_{1}.

Remark 0.2
.

The value
\Delta
in (
0.6
) in place of
\|J-J_{0}\|_{1}
looks quite natural, at least for small perturbations. Indeed, given a
Jacobi matrix
J
, consider a class
S(J)

of Jacobi matrices

\begin{split}S(J)&=\{\widehat{J}:=T^{-1}JT,\ T=\diag(t_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ {\rm is\ a\ diagonal\ isomorphism\ of}\ \ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})\},\\
\widehat{J}&=J\bigl(\{a_{j}r_{j}\},\{b_{j}\},\{c_{j}r_{j}^{-1}\}\bigr),\quad r_{n}=\frac{t_{n}}{t_{n+1}},\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.\end{split}

Clearly,
\sigma_{d}(\widehat{J})=\sigma_{d}(J)
since
\widehat{J}
is similar to
J
. Hence the left side of (
0.6
) is constant within the class

S(J)
, and so is
\Delta
, in contrast to
\|J-J_{0}\|_{1}
.
For the class
S(J_{0})
both sides of (
0.6
) vanish, whereas
\|J-J_{0}\|_{1}
,
J\in S(J_{0})
,
can be arbitrarily large.

Next,
|1-a_{n}c_{n}|\leq|1-a_{n}|+|1-c_{n}|+|1-a_{n}||1-c_{n}|
, and so

(0.8)

\begin{split}\Delta&\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(|b_{n}|+|1-a_{n}|+|1-c_{n}|\Bigr)+\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|1-a_{n}|\right)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|1-c_{n}|\right)\\
&\leq 3\|J-J_{0}\|_{1}+\|J-J_{0}\|_{1}^{2}.\end{split}

We see that for small perturbations the value
\Delta
has at least the same order as
\|J-J_{0}\|_{1}
.

The bounds for the Jost functions, obtained in the course of the proof of our main statement, provide some new spectral enclosure results.

Theorem 0.3
.

(i)
. Let
J-J_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
. The discrete spectrum
\sigma_{d}(J)
belongs to the following Cassini oval

(0.9)

\sigma_{d}(J)\subset\left\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash[-2,2]:\ |\lambda^{2}-4|\leq\left(\frac{2\Delta}{\log 2}\right)^{2}\right\}.

(ii)
. Assume that

(0.10)

\Delta_{1}:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\Bigl(|b_{n}|+|1-a_{n-1}c_{n-1}|\Bigr)<\infty.

Then the discrete spectrum is missing,
\sigma_{d}(J)=\emptyset
, as long as
\Delta_{1}<\log 2
.

Note that the latter effect is inherent to semi-infinite Jacobi matrices.

The Lieb–Thirring inequality for
two-sided
Jacobi operators is due to Hansmann and Katriel
[
15
, Theorem 1]
. It states that
for each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there is a constant
C(\varepsilon)>0
so that

(0.11)

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{d}(J)}\frac{\dist(\lambda,[-2,2])^{1+\varepsilon}}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}}}\leq C(\varepsilon)\|J-J_{0}\|_{1}.

It has been proved recently in
[
12
]
, that this bound can be refined. Actually, (
0.7
) holds for two-sided Jacobi operators
as well. Now the Wronskian of two Jost solutions plays a key role.

The result of Hansmann–Katriel (
0.11
) is known to be sharp in the sense that (
0.11
) is false for
\varepsilon=0
.
To prove that, the authors of
[
3
]
introduce a special class of two-sided Jacobi operators with rectangular (step) potentials.
In Section
3
we deal with an obvious counterpart of this class in semi-infinite setting.

Given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
h>0
, we study a semi-infinite discrete Schrödinger operator with a pure imaginary step potential

(0.12)

J_{n,h}:=\begin{bmatrix}b_{1}&1&&&\\
1&b_{2}&1&&\\
&1&b_{3}&1&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{bmatrix},\ b_{j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}ih,&\hbox{$j=1,\ldots,n;$}\\
0,&\hbox{$j\geq n+1$.}\end{array}\right.

It is clear that
J_{n,h}=J_{0}+ih{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
,
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
is the orthogonal projection onto the linear span of the first
n
basis vectors
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
.

We write
J_{n,h}=J_{n,\alpha}
for particular values of
h=h_{n}=n^{-\alpha}
,
0<\alpha<~1
.
For such operators some quantitative information about the discrete spectrum, which provides sharpness of Theorem
0.1
,
can be gathered.

Theorem 0.4
.

For the discrete Schrödinger operators
J_{n,\alpha}
the following lower bound holds for large enough
n

(0.13)

\frac{1}{\|J_{n,\alpha}-J_{0}\|_{1}}\,\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{d}(J_{n,\alpha})}\frac{\dist(\lambda,[-2,2])}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}\geq C\log n.

In particular,

(0.14)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\|J_{n,\alpha}-J_{0}\|_{1}}\,\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{d}(J_{n,\alpha})}\frac{\dist(\lambda,[-2,2])}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}=+\infty.

The argument in
[
3
]
relies heavily on a simple matrix representation for the resolvent of the whole-line free Jacobi matrix
and the theory of Kac–Murdock–Szegő matrices, neither of which is available in the semi-infinite case. Instead, we analyze directly the
recurrence relations for eigenvectors and apply Rouché’s Theorem to the roots of certain algebraic equations.

1.
Jost solutions and discrete Volterra equations

We derive the bounds for the Jost solution
u^{+}
by reducing the difference equation (
0.4
) to the
Volterra-type discrete integral equation. The unity of the first coefficient (
0.4
) appears to be crucial.

Define a (non-symmetric) Green kernel for
k,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
(as a two-sided Laurent matrix) by

(1.1)

G(k,m;z):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{z^{m-k}-z^{k-m}}{z-z^{-1}},&\hbox{$m\geq k,$}\\
0,&\hbox{$m\leq k,$}\end{array}\right.\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{0}.

The basic properties of this kernel can be verified directly

(1.2)

\begin{split}G(k,m-1;z)+G(k,m+1;z)-\Bigl(z+\frac{1}{z}\Bigr)\,G(k,m;z)&=\delta_{k,m},\\
G(k-1,m;z)+G(k+1,m;z)-\Bigl(z+\frac{1}{z}\Bigr)\,G(k,m;z)&=\delta_{k,m}.\end{split}

We make use of these properties for
k,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}:=\{0,1,\ldots\}
. The kernel

(1.3)

T(k,m;z):=-b_{m}G(k,m;z)+(1-a_{m-1}c_{m-1})G(k,m-1;z)

is a key player of the game. Note that the values of
b_{0}
,
a_{-1}
,
c_{-1}
are immaterial.

Theorem 1.1
.

The Jost solution
u^{+}=(u^{+}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of the difference equation
(
0.4
)
satisfies the discrete Volterra-type equation

(1.4)

u^{+}_{k}(z)=z^{k}+\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}T(k,m;z)u^{+}_{m}(z),\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{0}.

Conversely, each solution
u=(u_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of
(
1.4
)
solves
(
0.4
)
.

Proof.

We multiply the first relation (
1.2
) for
G
by
u^{+}_{m}
,
relation (
0.4
) for
u^{+}
by
G(k,m)
, and subtract the later from the former

\begin{split}\Bigl[G(k,m+1)u^{+}_{m}-G(k,m)u^{+}_{m-1}\Bigr]&+\Bigl[-b_{m}G(k,m)+G(k,m-1)\Bigr]u^{+}_{m}\\
&-a_{m}c_{m}G(k,m)u^{+}_{m+1}=\delta_{k,m}u^{+}_{m}.\end{split}

Next, we sum up over
m
from
k+1
to
N
, taking into account that

G(k,k+1)=1
,
G(k,k)=0

\begin{split}G(k,N+1)u^{+}_{N}&+\sum_{m=k+1}^{N}\Bigl[-b_{m}G(k,m)+G(k,m-1)\Bigr]u^{+}_{m}\\
&-\sum_{m=k}^{N}a_{m}c_{m}G(k,m)u^{+}_{m+1}=u^{+}_{k},\end{split}

or

u^{+}_{k}=G(k,N+1)u^{+}_{N}-a_{N}c_{N}G(k,N)u^{+}_{N+1}+\sum_{m=k+1}^{N}T(k,m)u^{+}_{m}.

The latter equality holds for arbitrary solutions of (
0.4
). If
u^{+}
is the Jost solution, then, by (
1.1
) and (
0.5
),

\lim_{N\to\infty}\Bigl[G(k,N+1)u^{+}_{N}-a_{N}c_{N}G(k,N)u^{+}_{N+1}\Bigr]=z^{k},

and (
1.4
) follows.

To prove the converse statement, let
u=(u_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be any solution of (
1.4
). Then

\begin{split}u_{k-1}+u_{k+1}&=\Bigl(z+\frac{1}{z}\Bigr)\,z^{k}+T(k-1,k)u_{k}+T(k-1,k+1)u_{k+1}\\
&+\sum_{m=k+2}^{\infty}\Bigl[T(k-1,m)+T(k+1,m)\Bigr]u_{m}.\end{split}

But

\begin{split}T(k-1,k)u_{k}&=-b_{k}u_{k},\\
T(k-1,k+1)u_{k+1}&=\Bigl[-b_{k+1}G(k-1,k+1)+(1-a_{k}c_{k})G(k-1,k)\Bigr]u_{k+1}\\
&=-\Bigl(z+\frac{1}{z}\Bigr)b_{k+1}u_{k+1}+(1-a_{k}c_{k})u_{k+1}\\
&=\Bigl(z+\frac{1}{z}\Bigr)T(k,k+1)u_{k+1}+(1-a_{k}c_{k})u_{k+1},\\
T(k-1,m)+T(k+1,m)&=\Bigl(z+\frac{1}{z}\Bigr)T(k,m).\end{split}

Finally,

u_{k-1}+u_{k+1}=-b_{k}u_{k}+(1-a_{k}c_{k})u_{k+1}+\Bigl(z+\frac{1}{z}\Bigr)\,\Bigl(z^{k}+\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}T(k,m)u_{m}\Bigr),

which is (
0.4
). The proof is complete.
∎

The further study of the Volterra equation (
1.4
) relies upon the modified kernel

(1.5)

\widetilde{T}(k,m;z):=T(k,m;z)\,z^{m-k}.

It is easy to verify that
\widetilde{T}(k,m;\cdot)
are polynomials of
z
. Indeed,

\begin{split}z^{m-k}G(k,m;z)&=z^{m-k}\,\frac{z^{m-k}-z^{k-m}}{z-z^{-1}}=z\,\frac{z^{2(m-k)}-1}{z^{2}-1}\,,\quad m\geq k;\\
z^{m-k}G(k,m-1;z)&=z^{m-k}\,\frac{z^{m-k-1}-z^{k-m+1}}{z-z^{-1}}=z^{2}\,\frac{z^{2(m-k-1)}-1}{z^{2}-1}\,,\quad m\geq k+1,\end{split}

as claimed. The bounds for the kernel
\widetilde{T}
follow from the latter relations

\begin{split}\bigl|z^{m-k}G(k,m;z)\bigr|&\leq|z|\cdot\min\Bigl\{(m-k)_{+},\ \frac{2}{|1-z^{2}|}\Bigr\},\quad(a)_{+}:=\max(a,0);\\
\bigl|z^{m-k}G(k,m-1;z)\bigr|&\leq|z|^{2}\cdot\min\Bigl\{(m-k-1)_{+},\ \frac{2}{|1-z^{2}|}\Bigr\},\end{split}

and so

(1.6)

\begin{split}\bigl|\widetilde{T}(k,m;z)\bigr|&\leq\delta_{m}|z|\min\Bigl\{(m-k)_{+},\ \frac{2}{|1-z^{2}|}\Bigr\},\quad z\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}},\\
\delta_{m}&:=|b_{m}|+|1-a_{m-1}c_{m-1}|,\qquad a_{0}=c_{0}=1.\end{split}

In particular,

(1.7)

\bigl|\widetilde{T}(k,m;z)\bigr|\leq|\omega(z)|\delta_{m},\quad\omega(z):=\frac{2z}{1-z^{2}},\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1}:=\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}\backslash\{\pm 1\}.

Theorem 1.2
.

(i)
. Assume that

(1.8)

\Delta=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta_{n}<\infty.

Then the equation (
1.4
) has a unique solution
u^{+}=(u^{+}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
so that
u^{+}_{k}
are analytic in
{\mathbb{D}}
, continuous in
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
, and
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1}

(1.9)

|u^{+}_{k}(z)-z^{k}|\leq|z|^{k}\left(e^{|\omega(z)|s_{0}(k)}-1\right),\quad s_{0}(k):=\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\delta_{n}.

(ii)
. Assume that
(
0.10
)
holds

\Delta_{1}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\delta_{n}<\infty.

Then (
1.4
) has a unique solution
u^{+}=(u^{+}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
so that
u^{+}_{k}
are analytic in
{\mathbb{D}}
, continuous in
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
, and
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
z\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}

(1.10)

|u^{+}_{k}(z)-z^{k}|\leq|z|^{k}\left(e^{|z|s_{1}(k)}-1\right),\quad s_{1}(k):=\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}n\delta_{n}.

Proof.

It is advisable to introduce new variables in (
1.4
)

f_{j}(z):=u^{+}_{j}(z)z^{-j}-1,\qquad j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},

so the equation (
1.4
) turns into

(1.11)

\begin{split}f_{k}(z)&=g_{k}(z)+\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}\widetilde{T}(k,m;z)f_{m}(z),\\
g_{k}(z)&:=\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}\widetilde{T}(k,m;z),\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1}.\end{split}

It is clear from (
1.7
) and the assumption (
1.8
) that the latter series converges absolutely and uniformly on compact
subsets of
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
and so represents an analytic function in
{\mathbb{D}}
. Moreover,

|g_{k}(z)|\leq|\omega(z)|s_{0}(k),\qquad k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1}.

We are going to solve (
1.11
) by using the successive approximations method. Let

f_{k,1}(z):=g_{k}(z),\quad f_{k,j+1}(z):=\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}\widetilde{T}(k,m;z)f_{m,j}(z),\quad j\in{\mathbb{N}}.

It is easy to see, by induction, that

(1.12)

|f_{k,p}(z)|\leq\frac{\bigl(|\omega(z)|s_{0}(k)\bigr)^{p}}{p!}\,,\qquad p\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Indeed, once the bound is true for
p=1
, we assume that it holds for
p=1,2,\ldots,j
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Then

|f_{k,j+1}(z)|\leq|\omega(z)|\,\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}\delta_{m}|f_{m,j}(z)|\leq\frac{|\omega(z)|^{j+1}}{j!}\,\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}\delta_{m}s_{0}^{j}(m).

The elementary inequality
(a+b)^{j+1}-a^{j+1}\geq(j+1)ba^{j}
,
a,b\geq 0
gives with
a=s_{0}(m)
,
b=\delta_{m}
,
a+b=s_{0}(m-1)

\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}\delta_{m}s_{0}^{j}(m)\leq\frac{1}{j+1}\,\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}\bigl(s_{0}^{j+1}(m-1)-s_{0}^{j+1}(m)\bigr)=\frac{s_{0}^{j+1}(k)}{j+1}\,,

and so

|f_{k,j+1}(z)|\leq\frac{|\omega(z)|^{j+1}s_{0}^{j+1}(k)}{j!(j+1)}=\frac{\bigl(|\omega(z)|s_{0}(k)\bigr)^{j+1}}{(j+1)!}\,,

as claimed.

Hence, the series

f_{k}:=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{k,j}(z)

converges absolutely and uniformly on compact subsets of
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
and represents an analytic in
{\mathbb{D}}
function, continuous in
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
.
It satisfies (
1.11
)

\begin{split}f_{k}(z)-g_{k}(z)&=f_{k}(z)-f_{k,1}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{k,j+1}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}\widetilde{T}(k,m;z)f_{m,j}(z)\\
&=\sum_{m=k+1}^{\infty}\widetilde{T}(k,m;z)f_{m}(z).\end{split}

This solution admits the bound, see (
1.12
),

|f_{k}(z)|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|f_{k,j}(z)|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\bigl(|\omega(z)|s_{0}(k)\bigr)^{j}}{j!}=e^{|\omega(z)|s_{0}(k)}-1,

which is (
1.9
).

As far as uniqueness goes, suppose that there are two solutions of (
1.4
)

u^{+}=(u^{+}_{k})_{k\geq 0},\qquad v^{+}=(v^{+}_{k})_{k\geq 0}.

Assume further that
z\not=0
. By (
1.7
), we have

(1.13)

|u^{+}_{k}(z)-v^{+}_{k}(z)|\leq|\omega(z)|\,\sum_{m\geq k+1}\delta_{m}|u^{+}_{m}(z)-v^{+}_{m}(z)|=:q_{k}(z).

Clearly,

As for the second one, the first equality in (
3.17
) and (
3.20
) imply

\rho_{k}^{2n+1}=\frac{n^{-\alpha}}{4\sin^{2}\theta_{k}}\,(1+O(n^{-c_{2}}))>\frac{n^{-\alpha}}{4}\,,

and in view of (
3.18
) and (
3.20
), the second term is

-4\sin(2n+1)\theta_{k}\sin\theta_{k}>c>0.

Hence,
1-|z_{k}|^{2}>0
for large enough
n
, as claimed.

Therefore, the numbers

\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k}(n):=\zeta_{k}+\zeta_{k}^{-1}+in^{-\alpha}=\rho_{k}e^{i\theta_{k}}+\rho_{k}^{-1}e^{-i\theta_{k}}+in^{-\alpha}

are
distinct
eigenvalues of
J_{n,\alpha}
for all large enough
n
and
k\in\Lambda
(
3.14
).
We have, as in (
3.12
) above,

(3.22)

\lambda_{k}=2\cos\theta_{k}+in^{-\alpha}+O\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr),

and so, by Proposition
3.1
,

(3.23)

{\rm dist}\,(\lambda_{k},[-2,2])=\text{\rm Im\,}\lambda_{n,k}=n^{-\alpha}(1+o(1)).

As we restrict ourselves with the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
,
k\in\Lambda
,

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{d}(J_{n,\alpha})}\frac{\dist(\lambda,[-2,2])}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}\geq\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\frac{\dist(\lambda_{k},[-2,2])}{|\lambda_{k}^{2}-4|^{1/2}}=:I_{n,a}.

Next, by the location of the discrete spectrum and (
3.22
), we see that

(3.24)

\begin{split}|\lambda_{k}+2|^{1/2}&\leq(4+n^{-\alpha})^{1/2}<\sqrt{5},\\
|\lambda_{k}-2|^{1/2}&\leq 2\sin\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}+n^{-\alpha/2}(1+o(1))\leq\theta_{k}+n^{-\alpha/2}(1+o(1)),\end{split}

and so (
3.23
) and (
3.24
) imply (see also (
3.19
))

\begin{split}I_{n,a}&\geq\frac{n^{-\alpha}(1+o(1))}{\sqrt{5}}\,\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\Bigl[\frac{\pi}{2(2n+1)}(4k+1)+n^{-\alpha/2}(1+o(1))\Bigr]^{-1}\\
&\geq\frac{n^{1-\alpha}(1+o(1))}{\pi\sqrt{5}}\,\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\frac{1}{k+u_{n}}\,,\quad u_{n}:=\frac{n^{1-\alpha/2}}{\pi}\,(1+o(1))+1.\end{split}

An elementary bound

\sum_{m_{1}}^{m_{2}}\frac{1}{k+u_{n}}>\int_{m_{1}}^{m_{2}}\frac{dx}{x+u_{n}}=\log\frac{m_{2}+u_{n}}{m_{1}+u_{n}}

with
m_{1}:=[a_{n}^{\prime}]+1
,
m_{2}:=[a_{n}^{\prime\prime}]
(see (
3.14
))
gives

(3.25)

I_{n,a}\geq\frac{n^{1-\alpha}(1+o(1))}{\pi\sqrt{5}}\,\log\frac{1-2a+4n^{-\alpha/2}(1+o(1))}{a+2n^{-\alpha/2}(1+o(1))}\,.

Up to this point we have tacitly assumed that the value
a\in(0,1/4)
from Proposition
3.3
is fixed. As a matter of fact,
a
can vary with
n
.
For example, if
a=n^{-\alpha/4}
, (
3.25
) yields

I_{n,a}\geq Cn^{1-\alpha}\,\log n.

The bound (
0.13
) now follows from

\|J_{n,\alpha}-J_{0}\|_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}(n)|=n^{1-\alpha}.

The proof is complete.

Remark 3.4
.

The Jost solution
u^{+}=(u_{j}^{+})_{j\geq 0}
for Jacobi operators with the step potential can be found explicitly. Indeed,
the recurrence relation

\begin{split}u_{k-1}^{+}(z)+ihu_{k}^{+}(z)+u_{k+1}^{+}(z)&=\Bigl(z+\frac{1}{z}\Bigr)\,u_{k}^{+}(z),\quad k=1,2,\ldots,n;\\
u_{k}^{+}(z)&=z^{k},\quad k=n,n+1\ldots\end{split}

can be resolved, and we come to the following expression for the Jost function
u_{0}^{+}
(which is the same as the perturbation determinant)

L(z,J_{n,h})=z^{n}U_{n}\Bigl(\frac{z+z^{-1}-ih}{2}\Bigr)-z^{n+1}U_{n-1}\Bigl(\frac{z+z^{-1}-ih}{2}\Bigr),

U_{k}
is the Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind. It might be a challenging problem analyzing the roots of the polynomial on the right side directly.
