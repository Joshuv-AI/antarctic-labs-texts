On Frobenius’ Theta Formula
Alessio FIORENTINO and Riccardo SALVATI MANNI

Abstract
Mumford’s well-known characterization of the hyperelliptic locus of the moduli space of ppavs in terms of vanishing and non-vanishing theta constants is based on Neumann’s dynamical system. Poor’s approach to the characterization uses the cross ratio. A key tool in both methods is Frobenius’ theta formula, which follows from Riemann’s theta formula. In a 2004 paper Grushevsky gives a different characterization in terms of cubic equations in second order theta functions. In this note we first show the connection between the methods by proving that Grushevsky’s cubic equations are strictly related to Frobenius’ theta formula and we then give a new proof of Mumford’s characterization via Gunning’s multisecant formula.

Introduction

We denote by
{\mathbb{H}}_{g}
the
Siegel upper half-space
– the
space of complex symmetric
g\times g
matrices with positive definite
imaginary part. An element
\tau\in{\mathbb{H}}_{g}
is called a
period
matrix
and defines the complex abelian variety
X_{\tau}:={\mathbb{C}}^{g}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{g}+\tau{\mathbb{Z}}^{g}
. It is a well-known fact that the moduli space of principally polarized abelian varieties (ppavs for short) can be identified with the quotient of
{\mathbb{H}}_{g}
by an action of the symplectic group
{\rm Sp}(2g,{\mathbb{Z}})
which is a generalization of the standard action of
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
on the complex upper-half plane, namely,

\gamma\cdot\tau:=(a\tau+b)\cdot(c\tau+d)^{-1},\qquad\forall\,\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm Sp}(2g,{\mathbb{Z}}),

where
a
,
b
,
c
,
d
are
g\times g
blocks. A ppav is called
irreducible
if it is not isomorphic to a product of two lower-dimensional ppavs.

For
\varepsilon,\delta\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{g}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{g}
we define the
first order theta function with characteristic
[\varepsilon,\delta]
to be

{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{\varepsilon}\\
{\delta}\end{matrix}\right]}(\tau,z):=\sum\limits_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{g}}\exp\pi{\rm i}\left[\left(m+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{t}\tau\left(m+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)+2\left(m+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{t}\left(z+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\right].

Characteristics can be defined for
\varepsilon
,
\delta
in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{g}
, but the
reduction formula

\displaystyle{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{\varepsilon+2\varepsilon^{\prime}}\\
{\delta+2\delta^{\prime}}\end{matrix}\right]}(\tau,z)=(-1)^{\langle\varepsilon,\delta^{\prime}\rangle}{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{\varepsilon}\\
{\delta}\end{matrix}\right]}(\tau,z)

(where the symbol
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
stands for the standard inner product) shows that these functions are uniquely determined up to a sign by considering
\varepsilon
and
\delta
as vectors of zeros and ones. Henceforward we shall work with reduced characteristics with the agreement that a theta function associated with a sum of characteristics is meant to be non-reduced.

Characteristics are defined
even
or
odd
depending on whether respectively
\langle\varepsilon,\delta\rangle=0,\,=1

{\rm mod}\,2
. A straightforward computation shows there are
2^{g-1}\big(2^{g}+1\big)
even characteristics and
2^{g-1}\big(2^{g}-1\big)
odd ones. It is easily seen that theta functions associated with even characteristics are even functions in the variable
z
, whereas those associated with odd characteristics are odd functions.
Triplets of characteristics
[\varepsilon,\delta]
,
[\varepsilon^{\prime},\delta^{\prime}]
,
[\varepsilon^{\prime\prime},\delta^{\prime\prime}]
are also called
azygetic
if
\langle\varepsilon,\delta\rangle+\langle\varepsilon^{\prime},\delta^{\prime}\rangle+\langle\varepsilon^{\prime\prime},\delta^{\prime\prime}\rangle+\langle\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime},\delta+\delta^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime\prime}\rangle=1
. More generally, a
k
-tuple of characteristics is called azygetic if its subtriplets are azygetic.

Theta functions satisfy an addition formula (cf.
[
12
]
for a general formulation and
[
9
]
or
[
10
]
for the specific version we use here)

\theta\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon\\
\delta\end{bmatrix}(2\tau,2z)\theta\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}\\
\delta\end{bmatrix}(2\tau,2w)=\frac{1}{2^{g}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{g}}(-1)^{\langle\varepsilon,\sigma\rangle}\theta\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{\prime}\\
\delta+\sigma\end{bmatrix}(\tau,z+w)\theta\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{\prime}\\
\sigma\end{bmatrix}(\tau,z-w).

For a given
\tau\in H_{g}
we denote by
X_{\tau}[2]
the group of points of order two on
X_{\tau}
and by
L_{\tau}
a symmetric line bundle on
X_{\tau}
defining the principal polarization. Note that

{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{\varepsilon}\\
{\delta}\end{matrix}\right]}(\tau,z)=\exp\pi{\rm i}\left[\frac{\varepsilon^{t}}{2}\tau\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(z+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\right]{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}\left(\tau,z+\tau\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)

and the function
z\to\theta\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\varepsilon\\
\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right](\tau,z)
defines the unique (up to scalar multiplication)
section of
t_{x}^{*}L_{\tau}
, i.e., the translate of the line bundle
L_{\tau}
by the point of order two
x=(\tau\varepsilon+\delta)/2
. Because of the duplication map, the functions
z\rightarrow\theta\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\varepsilon\\
\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right](\tau,2z)
are a basis of
H^{0}\big(X_{\tau},L_{\tau}^{\otimes 4}\big)
.

For
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{g}
we also
define the
second order theta function with characteristic
\varepsilon
to
be

\Theta[\varepsilon](\tau,z):={\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{\varepsilon}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}(2\tau,2z).

The functions
z\rightarrow\Theta[\varepsilon](\tau,z)
are a basis of
H^{0}\big(X_{\tau},L_{\tau}^{\otimes 2}\big)
. Since these functions are even, i.e.,
\Theta[\varepsilon](\tau,-z)=\Theta[\varepsilon](\tau,z)
, they induce a map

{\rm Th}_{2}\colon\ X_{\tau}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2^{g}-1}

that factorizes along the Kummer variety
K_{\tau}:=X_{\tau}/\pm 1
.

By evaluating first order theta functions at
z=0
we get the so-called
theta constants

\theta\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\varepsilon\\
\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right](\tau,0)
; because of the parity of theta functions, theta constants associated with odd characteristics are clearly trivial. Analogously, second order theta functions evaluated at
z=0
yield
second order theta constants

\Theta[\varepsilon](\tau,0)
. As functions of
\tau
these are not well defined on the moduli space of ppavs but, as a consequence of how they transform under the action of
{\rm Sp}(2g,{\mathbb{Z}})
, they induce maps on finite covers of the moduli space. More precisely, if we introduce the following family of subgroups of finite index in
{\rm Sp}(2g,{\mathbb{Z}})

\displaystyle\Gamma_{g}[n]:=\operatorname{Ker}({\rm Sp}(2g,{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\rm Sp}(2g,{\mathbb{Z}}_{n})),\qquad n\in{\mathbb{N}},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{g}[n,2n]:=\left\{\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{g}[n]\,|\,{\rm diag}\big(a^{t}b\big)\equiv{\rm diag}\big(c^{t}d\big)\equiv 0\ {\rm mod}\ 2n\right\},

first order theta constants are seen to transform as follows (cf.
[
12
]
and
[
18
]
for the general formula)

{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{\varepsilon}\\
{\delta}\end{matrix}\right]}(\gamma\cdot\tau,0)=\kappa(\gamma)\chi_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\gamma)\det{(c\tau+d)}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{\varepsilon}\\
{\delta}\end{matrix}\right]}(\tau,0)\qquad\forall\,\gamma\in\Gamma_{g}[2],

where
\kappa(\gamma)
is an eighth root of the unity for any
\gamma
and
\chi_{\varepsilon,\delta}
are characters of the group
\Gamma_{g}[2,4]/\Gamma_{g}[4,8]
; in particular, an action of
\Gamma_{g}[2,4]/\Gamma_{g}[4,8]
is naturally induced on theta constants and can be described in terms of these characters
\chi_{\varepsilon,\delta}
, which span the character group of
\Gamma_{g}[2,4]/\pm\Gamma_{g}[4,8]
(cf.
[
19
]
). Thanks to the transformation formula, first order theta constants induce a map of
{\mathbb{H}}_{g}/\pm\Gamma_{g}[4,8]
into the projective space; this map is known to be an immersion, cf.
[
12
]
. Second order theta constants also induce a map on a finite cover of the moduli space of ppavs

\mathcal{T}\colon\ {\mathbb{H}}_{g}/\Gamma_{g}[2,4]\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2^{g}-1},

which is generically injective for any
g
, cf.
[
19
]
. Once a basis
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{g}
for the cohomology of an algebraic curve of genus
g
is chosen together with a symplectic basis of cycles
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{g},\delta^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\delta^{\prime}_{g}
, the
g\times 2g
matrix
\big(\int_{\delta_{j}}\omega_{i},\int_{\delta^{\prime}_{j}}\omega_{i}\big)
defines a complex torus which is isomorphic to a ppav
X_{\tau}
; this complex torus is known as the
Jacobian variety
of the curve and the locus in the moduli space of ppavs defined by those ppavs that are isomorphic to Jacobians of curves is known as the
Jacobian locus
. Because of the existence of the map
\mathcal{T}
, one knows that the Jacobian locus and the
hyperelliptic locus
(i.e., the locus defined by those ppavs that are isomorphic to Jacobians of hyperelliptic curves) can be described in terms of equations involving theta constants by taking the preimages of these loci under the covering map
{\mathbb{H}}_{g}/\Gamma_{g}[2,4]\to{\mathbb{H}}_{g}/{\rm Sp}(2g,{\mathbb{Z}})
; the preimage of the hyperelliptic locus has several irreducible components in
{\mathbb{H}}_{g}/\Gamma_{g}[2,4]
.

As for the hyperelliptic locus, we know from the result in
[
15
]
that the irreducible components are defined in terms of vanishing and non-vanishing conditions for certain theta constants
\theta\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\varepsilon\\
\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right](\tau,0)
. Mumford’s result is based on Neumann’s dynamical system. In
[
17
]
Poor used the cross ratio to prove that the vanishing conditions alone are sufficient to characterize the hyperellipitic locus, once the irreducibility of the principally polarized abelian variety is assumed. In both cases a fundamental tool is the so-called Frobenius theta formula, which is a consequence of Riemann’s theta formula (cf.
[
8
]
and
[
15
]
). A few years ago in
[
9
]
, a characterization of the hyperelliptic abelian varieties was given in terms of cubic equations in the second order theta functions. These cubics were obtained by using the explicit coefficients for the addition formula from
[
4
]
.

The aim of this note is twofold: we first relate Grushevsky’s approach to the others’ by proving that his cubic equations are related to Frobenius’ theta formula; we then give a new proof of Mumford’s characterization by applying Gunning’s multisecant formula (cf.
[
11
]
), which is a remarkable geometrical characterization of the locus of Jacobians in terms of intersections of g-dimensional linear subspaces with the Kummer variety of an abelian variety.

In this way we have an explicit relation between various approaches; in particular, we get an immediate link between the geometrical characterization and the explicit equations for the hyperelliptic locus.

Incidentally, we also prove that Grushevsky’s cubics are induced by the quadratic relations related to the theta vanishing.

Theta functions

Throughout the rest of the paper we shall omit the subscript
\tau
whenever it is clear that
\tau
is fixed.
For the topics introduced here we shall follow Section 1 in
[
21
]
closely. Once we set
M:=L^{\otimes 2}
, as
t_{x}^{*}M\cong M
if and only if
x\in X[2]
, the following group is well defined

G(M):=\big\{(x,\phi)\,|\,x\in X[2],\,\phi\colon t_{x}^{*}M\xrightarrow{\cong}M\big\}

A theta structure is an isomorphism between

and the Heisenberg group, namely the set

H:={\mathbb{C}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{g}\times{\rm Hom}({\mathbb{Z}}_{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{g},

(t,x,x^{*})\cdot(s,y,y^{*}):=(tsy^{*}(x),x+y,x^{*}+y^{*}).

Now, let
B_{n}:=\Gamma(X,M^{n})

The action of

B_{n}^{+}
and
B_{n}^{-}
. One has
B_{1}=B_{1}^{+}
and
B_{1}
is an irreducible representation of

.
Moreover,

admits a basis
\{X_{\sigma}\}
with

such that

(t,x,x^{*})X_{\sigma}=tx^{*}(x+\sigma)X_{x+\sigma}

[
21
]

Proposition 2.1
.

(i)

A basis of eigenvectors for the action of H on

Maps between these spaces can be defined. Let

For

For

The maps

The decomposition of

\Phi_{4}\colon\ S^{2}\big(S^{2}B_{1}\big)\to S^{4}B_{1}.

3
Cubic equations

As soon as the genus is greater than 2, there are also cubic equations defining the Kummer variety. To focus on cubic relations, we first note that the spaces
S^{3}B_{1}
and
B_{3}^{+}
decompose under the action of the Heisenberg group into
2^{g}
irreducible representations that are all isomorphic to
B_{1}
(see
[
21
]
or
[
2
]
); we are interested in studying the kernel of the map
\Psi_{3}\colon S^{3}B_{1}\to B_{3}^{+}
. A simple way of constructing cubic relations is considering quadratic relations. Quadratic relations exist whenever we have the vanishing of some even theta constants i.e.,
\theta\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\varepsilon\\
\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right](\tau,0)=0
with
\langle\varepsilon,\delta\rangle=0

{\rm mod}\,2
. If none of the theta constants vanish, we can prove there always exist cubic relations that are not a product of a quadratic and a linear form; this is done by means of a dimensional argument, as we have

\dim_{{\mathbb{C}}}S^{3}B_{1}=2^{g}\big(2^{g}+1\big)\big(2^{g-1}+1\big)/3>2^{g-1}\big(3^{g}+1\big)=\dim_{{\mathbb{C}}}S^{3}B_{3}^{+}.

In the genus 3 case there are exactly 8 cubics: in the non-hyperelliptic case (no vanishing theta constants) these cubics can be obtained as derivatives of the Coble quartic, while in the hyperelliptic case (one vanishing theta constant) they are obtained as a product of a quadric
Q[\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}](\tau,z)
and a linear form in the
\Theta[\sigma](\tau,z)
.

When the genus is higher, we need to determine a set of theta constants
\theta\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\varepsilon\\
\varepsilon^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right](\tau,0)
that vanish at the point
\tau
in the hyperelliptic locus; to do this, we start with a hyperelliptic point to which we can associate a special fundamental system of characteristics, namely an azygetic collection of
2g+2
characteristics
m_{1},\dots,m_{g},m_{g+1},\dots,m_{2g+2}
such that the first
g
are odd and the last
g+2
are even. Once we denote by
\{e_{k}\}_{k=1,\dots,g}
the elements of the natural basis in
{{\mathbb{Z}}^{g}_{2}}
, and set
e_{g+1}=0
and
s_{k}=e_{1}+\dots+e_{k}
, we can choose the following as special fundamental system

\displaystyle m_{1}:=\begin{bmatrix}s_{1}\\
e_{1}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\dots,\qquad m_{k}:=\begin{bmatrix}s_{k}\\
e_{k}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\dots,\qquad m_{g}:=\begin{bmatrix}s_{g}\\
e_{g}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle m_{g+1}:=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
e_{1}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\dots,\qquad m_{2g}:=\begin{bmatrix}s_{g-1}\\
e_{g}\end{bmatrix},\qquad m_{2g+1}:=\begin{bmatrix}s_{g}\\
0\end{bmatrix},\qquad m_{\infty}:=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

(3.1)

To be consistent with Mumford’s notation in
[
15

\displaystyle B:=\{1,\dots,2g+1,\infty\},\qquad U:=\{g+1,\dots,2g+1\},

(3.2)

and for any subset

.
To an even subset
T\subset B
(modulo

T\to m_{T}=\sum_{j\in T}m_{j}.

This actually defines a bijection.

By setting
T\circ S:=(T\setminus S)\cup(S\setminus T)

(-1)^{\frac{\#(T\circ U)-g-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\theta_{m_{T}}(\tau,0)=0\qquad\text{if}\quad\#(T\circ U)\neq g+1,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\theta_{m_{T}}(\tau,0)\neq 0\qquad\text{if}\quad\#(T\circ U)=g+1.

The

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{k=0}^{g}\sum_{\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{g}}(-1)^{\langle\varepsilon+\sigma,e_{k+1}\rangle}\Theta[\varepsilon](\tau,z)\Theta[\varepsilon+s_{k}](\tau,z)\Theta[\sigma+s_{k}](\tau,z).

.

Let

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j\in B}\varepsilon_{U}(j)\prod_{i=1}^{4}\theta[m_{j}](\tau,z_{i})=0.

(3.8)

In

We remark that if we apply the addition formula for theta functions to Grushevsky’s cubic equations, we obtain similar equations that are also consequence of the generalized Frobenius theta formula; in fact, we have the following:

Proposition 3.4
.

If
\tau\in{\mathbb{H}}_{g}
satisfies equations (
3.6
),
then, for all

:

\displaystyle Q[0,0](\tau,z)Q[\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1}](\tau,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{g}Q[s_{k},e_{k+1}](\tau,z)Q[s_{k}+\varepsilon_{1},e_{k+1}+\delta_{1}](\tau,z).

(3.9)

Proof.

We only need to consider

\displaystyle Q[0,0](\tau,z)\sum_{\sigma}(-1)^{\langle\sigma,\delta_{1}\rangle}\Theta[\sigma](\tau,z)\Theta[\sigma+\varepsilon_{1}](\tau,z)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{k=0}^{g}Q[s_{k},e_{k+1}](\tau,z)\sum_{\sigma}(-1)^{\langle\sigma,\delta_{1}+e_{k+1}\rangle}\Theta[\sigma+s_{k}](\tau,z)\Theta[\sigma+\varepsilon_{1}](\tau,z).

Now, the l.h.s. yields
Q[0,0](\tau,z)Q[\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1}](\tau,z)
. Moreover, we have

Q[s_{k}+\varepsilon_{1},e_{k+1}+\delta_{1}](\tau,z)=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{g}}(-1)^{\langle\sigma,e_{k+1}+\delta_{1}\rangle}\Theta[\sigma+s_{k}+\varepsilon_{1}](\tau,z)\Theta[\sigma](\tau,z).

Hence, in the r.h.s. we obtain

\sum_{k=0}^{g}Q[s_{k},e_{k+1}](\tau,z)Q[s_{k}+\varepsilon_{1},e_{k+1}+\delta_{1}](\tau,z).

Remark 3.5
.

As a referee correctly pointed out, in the above proposition we are computing
M(\chi)R_{0}
.

As a particular yet fundamental case, we will consider

, i.e., the case obtained by setting
\varepsilon_{1}=\delta_{1}=0
; hence we get

Corollary 3.6
.

If
\tau\in{\mathbb{H}}_{g}
satisfies equations (

\displaystyle Q[0,0]^{2}(\tau,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{g}Q[s_{k},e_{k+1}]^{2}(\tau,z).

(3.10)

Remark 3.7
.

The important fact is that this formula is exactly the one given in

when

Formula (

.

If

\displaystyle{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}(\tau,0)^{2}{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{x}\\
{y}\end{matrix}\right]}(\tau,2z)^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{g}{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{s_{k}}\\
{e_{k+1}}\end{matrix}\right]}(\tau,0)^{2}{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{s_{k}+x}\\
{e_{k+1}+y}\end{matrix}\right]}(\tau,2z)^{2}.

(3.11)

Proof.

We know that equation (

But this is a trivial relation among the

∎

Another interesting consequence can be derived. As we recalled, Riemann’s relations of the form

(see Proposition

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle\varepsilon,\delta\rangle=0/\theta\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\varepsilon\\

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle\varepsilon,\delta\rangle=0/\theta\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\varepsilon\\

Corollary 3.10
.

Grushevsky’s cubics lie in the space spanned by linear forms and the above vanishing quadratic forms.

4
Characterization of the Hyperelliptic locus

Whenever
\tau
is the period matrix of a hyperelliptic curve, a classical result (cf.
[
20
]
) states that a system of characteristics as in (
3.1
) with the vanishing and non-vanishing properties (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) can be associated with
\tau
or with a conjugate of
\tau
via the action of
{\rm Sp}(2g,{\mathbb{Z}})
.

It was not until 1984 that Mumford proved the converse statement in
[
15
]
. We will now give a new proof of Mumford’s result with a different approach that has the merit of involving Gunning’s multisecant formula, as expressed in the following statement (cf.
[
11
]
):

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X
be an irreducible principally polarized abelian variety of dimension
g
, and let
A_{0},\dots,A_{g+1}
be distinct points of
X
. Suppose that
\forall\,z\in X
the
g+2
points
{\rm Th}_{2}(A_{i}+z)
are linearly dependent.
Assume moreover the following general position condition: that there
exist some
k
and
l
such that for
y=(-A_{k}+A_{l})/2
the linear span of the points
{\rm Th}_{2}(A_{i}+y)
for
i=0,\dots,g+1
has dimension precisely equal to
g+1
, and
not less. Then
X
is the Jacobian of some curve
C
, and all the points
A_{i}
are in the image of the Abel–Jacobi map.

We are now in a position to prove that the above conditions are satisfied when cases (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) hold; indeed, we have the following:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\tau
be a point whose vanishing and non-vanishing theta constants are those in (
3.3
) and (
3.4
); then
\tau
is the period
matrix of a hyperelliptic Jacobian.

Proof.

The period matrix turns out to be irreducible by counting the vanishing and non-vanishing theta constants or by checking the conditions of Theorem 4 in
[
19
]
, i.e., the characters associated with the non-vanishing theta constants span the character group of

\Gamma_{g}[2,4]/\pm\Gamma_{g}[4,8]
.

Moreover, the vanishing conditions imply that Frobenius’ formula (
3.10
)

Q[0,0]^{2}(\tau,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{g}Q[s_{k},e_{k+1}]^{2}(\tau,z)

holds, hence Grushevsky’s equations
(
3.6
) also hold with the coefficients being not all zero (see Proposition
3.9
).

We will prove that this implies the vectors
{\rm Th}_{2}(A_{i}+z)
are linearly dependent.
Here we take

A_{0}=0,\ A_{1}=(\tau s_{0}+e_{1})/2,\ \dots,\ A_{k}=(\tau s_{k-1}+e_{k})/2,\ \dots,\ A_{g+1}=(\tau s_{g}+e_{g+1})/2,

with the corresponding characteristics being

\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon\\
\delta\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}s_{0}\\
e_{1}\end{bmatrix},\dots\begin{bmatrix}s_{k}\\
e_{k+1}\end{bmatrix},\dots,\begin{bmatrix}s_{g}\\
e_{g+1}\end{bmatrix}.

The
\sigma
-th coefficient of the vector
{\rm Th}_{2}(A_{i}+z)
is

\Theta[\sigma](\tau,z+(\tau s_{i-1}+e_{i})/2)=(-1)^{\langle\sigma,e_{i}\rangle}\Theta[\sigma+s_{i-1}](\tau,z).

Thus, the
2^{g}
polynomials
R_{\sigma}
in (
3.6
) give one non-trivial linear relation between the
{\rm Th}_{2}(A_{i}+z)
for any
z
.

We are yet to prove the general position condition. We set

y=(A_{1}-A_{0})/2=(1/4,0,\dots,0)^{\rm t},

and collect the points
{\rm Th}_{2}(A_{i}+y)
in a
(g+2)\times 2^{g}
matrix

A=\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\begin{bmatrix}\Theta[0](A_{0}+y)&\dots&\dots&\Theta[\varepsilon](A_{0}+y)&\dots\\
\Theta[0](A_{1}+y)&\dots&\dots&\Theta[\varepsilon](A_{1}+y)&\dots\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\Theta[0](A_{g+1}+y)&\dots&\dots&\Theta[\varepsilon](A_{g+1}+y)&\dots\end{bmatrix}.\end{array}

Now
AA^{\rm t}=B=(b_{ij})
with

b_{ij}=\sum_{\varepsilon}\Theta[\varepsilon](A_{i}+y)\Theta[\varepsilon](A_{j}+y)={\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}(A_{i}-A_{j}){\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}(A_{i}+A_{j}+2y).

Up to constants we have

{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}(A_{0}-A_{j})={\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{s_{j}}\\
{e_{j+1}}\end{matrix}\right]}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}(A_{0}+A_{j}+2y)={\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{s_{j}}\\
{e_{j+1}+e_{1}}\end{matrix}\right]}

and for
1\leq i\leq j\leq g+1

{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}(A_{i}-A_{j})={\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{s_{i}+s_{j}}\\
{e_{i+1}+e_{j+1}}\end{matrix}\right]}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}(A_{i}+A_{j}+2y)={\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{s_{i}+s_{j}}\\
{e_{i+1}+e_{j+1}+e_{1}}\end{matrix}\right]}.

Thus, the matrix
B
turns into

B=\begin{bmatrix}{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{e_{1}}\end{matrix}\right]}&{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{e_{1}}\end{matrix}\right]}&0&0&\dots&0\vskip 2.84526pt\\
{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{e_{1}}\end{matrix}\right]}&{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{e_{1}}\end{matrix}\right]}&0&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{e_{1}}\end{matrix}\right]}&0&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&&{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{0}\end{matrix}\right]}{\theta\left[\begin{matrix}{0}\\
{e_{1}}\end{matrix}\right]}\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, the matrix
B
has rank
