Distributed Semi-Supervised Sparse Statistical Inference
Jiyuan Tu, Weidong Liu, Xiaojun Mao, Mingyue Xu

Abstract
The debiased estimator is a crucial tool in statistical inference for high-dimensional model parameters. However, constructing such an estimator involves estimating the high-dimensional inverse Hessian matrix, incurring significant computational costs. This challenge becomes particularly acute in distributed setups, where traditional methods necessitate computing a debiased estimator on every machine. This becomes unwieldy, especially with a large number of machines. In this paper, we delve into semi-supervised sparse statistical inference in a distributed setup. An efficient multi-round distributed debiased estimator, which integrates both labeled and unlabelled data, is developed. We will show that the additional unlabeled data helps to improve the statistical rate of each round of iteration. Our approach offers tailored debiasing methods for M M -estimation and generalized linear models according to the specific form of the loss function. Our method also applies to a non-smooth loss like absolute deviation loss. Furthermore, our algorithm is computationally efficient since it requires only one estimation of a high-dimensional inverse covariance matrix. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our method by presenting simulation studies and real data applications that highlight the benefits of incorporating unlabeled data.

-estimation; generalized linear model

Introduction

High-dimensional models are prevalent in various scientific fields such as genomic analysis, image processing, signal processing, finance, and so on. However, traditional methods, such as maximum likelihood, least square regression, and principle component analysis break down due to the high dimensionality. To address this issue, new statistical methods and theories have been developed in high-dimensional statistics, which have played a critical role in advancing these fields. A common approach is to impose a sparsity structure on the data and model parameters. Many pioneering methods, such as
\ell_{1}
regularization
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
, non-convex regularization
(
Fan and Li 2001
;
Zhang 2010b
;
Zhang 2010a
)
, iterative hard thresholding
(
Blumensath and Davies 2009
;
Jain et al. 2014
;
Yuan et al. 2018
)
and debiasing techniques
(
Javanmard and Montanari 2014
;
van de Geer et al. 2014
;
Zhang and Zhang 2014
;
Cai et al. 2021
)
have been proposed to overcome these problems.

Upon entering the era of big data, modern datasets exhibit more complex structures and larger scales, which pose additional challenges for data analysis. For example, in internet companies and hospitals, data are often stored across multiple agents independently. However, direct data sharing may be prohibited due to privacy concerns, and communication between agents can be costly. As a result, distributed learning has emerged as a natural solution and has garnered significant attention in recent years. On the other hand, in some datasets, such as electronic health records and genetics data, the covariates are much more easily attainable than the labels. The need to utilize unlabeled data to improve the overall performance of statistical models naturally calls for research on semi-supervised learning.

In this paper, we aim to estimate the parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
defined by the following population risk minimization problem

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}=\mathop{\rm{argmin}}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta},Y)\big],

(1)

in a distributed semi-supervised setup. This type of loss function encompasses a wide range of applications, such as Huber regression, logistic regression, quantile regression, and others. Specifically, we assume that the target parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
is sparse, meaning that the number of nonzero elements of
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
,
s
, is much less than the dimension
p
. While there has been extensive research on problem (
1
) in both low dimensional and high dimensional scenarios (see, e.g.,
Javanmard and Montanari 2014
;
van de Geer et al. 2014
;
Zhang and Zhang 2014
;
Huang and Huo 2019
;
Battey et al. 2018
;
Jordan et al. 2019
;
Fan et al. 2023
), the statistical analysis in a distributed semi-supervised setup poses unique challenges and is scarce in the existing literature.

In the field of distributed learning, there is a vast literature investigating the trade-off between communication efficiency and statistical accuracy. Among these methods, the divide-and-conquer (DC) approach stands out for its simplicity and practicality. This method involves executing the algorithm in parallel and combining the local estimators through averaging. The DC approach has been widely applied to various problems such as quantile regression
(
Volgushev et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2019
)
, kernel ridge regression
(
Zhang et al. 2015
;
Lin et al. 2017
)
and kernel density estimation
(
Li et al. 2013
)
. Although these one-shot methods offer high communication efficiency, it faces stringent restriction on the number of machines to obtain a minimax convergence rate. To alleviate these constraints, many iterative algorithms, such as distributed approximate Newton method
(
Shamir et al. 2014
;
Zhang and Lin 2015
;
Jordan et al. 2019
;
Fan et al. 2023
)
, distributed primal-dual framework
(
Smith et al. 2017
)
, local stochastic gradient descent
(
Stich 2019
)
, have been proposed. There are also some works studying the lower bound of communication complexity
(
Zhang et al. 2013
;
Arjevani and Shamir 2015
)
.

For high-dimensional distributed learning, naively averaging the penalized estimators would incur a large bias and would not improve over the local estimators. To address this issue, many works apply the divide-and-conquer method coupled with a well-known debiasing technique
(
Javanmard and Montanari 2014
;
van de Geer et al. 2014
;
Zhang and Zhang 2014
;
Lee et al. 2017
;
Zhao et al. 2020
;
Lian and Fan 2018
)
to achieve better performance. The debiasing technique is particularly useful when conducting hypothesis testing and constructing a confidence interval for high-dimensional parameters
(
Lee et al. 2017
;
Battey et al. 2018
;
Liu et al. 2021
)
. In the classical distributed debiased estimator, each machine constructs a local estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}
, and computes the local debiased estimator

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathrm{db}}_{j}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j})\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}}f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j},Y_{i})\boldsymbol{X}_{i},

(2)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j})
is an estimator of the inverse Hessian matrix
(\boldsymbol{H}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}))^{-1}=({\mathbb{E}}[f^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j},Y)\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}])^{-1}
, and
{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
denotes the index set of the data on the
j
-th machine (where
j=1,...,m
). Then it takes the average of these estimators
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathrm{db}}_{j}
as the final result. However, existing distributed debiased estimators are mostly one-shot, and unlike the single machine setup, a one-step debiasing approach may not be sufficient to eliminate the bias in a distributed setting, especially when the size of the local labeled sample is small, and the number of machines is large. Nevertheless, it is not straightforward to extend existing distributed debiased estimators to multi-round methods. Indeed, each debiasing step requires computing an estimator of the high-dimensional inverse Hessian matrix
(\boldsymbol{H}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}))^{-1}
, which is computationally prohibitive. Naively extending the algorithm to a multi-round procedure would incur a significant computational burden, rendering it impractical in practice.

To address the aforementioned issues, we improve the classical distributed debiased estimators from two aspects: reducing the overall computational cost of the debiasing step and accelerating the per-round statistical rate. In traditional debiasing methods, the Hessian matrix
\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
varies with respect to the model parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. In each round of iteration, we have to update the entire inverse Hessian matrix, which is computationally expensive. To reduce the overall computation burden of the multi-round debiasing approach, we propose debiasing methods for
M
-estimations and generalized linear models, respectively. Specifically, for the
M
-estimation problem, we propose to estimate the inverse Hessian matrix separately in two parts. For the generalized linear model, we weigh the gradients to eliminate the dependence of the Hessian matrix on the parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. In both approaches, we only need to estimate one inverse covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}=({\mathbb{E}}\{\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\})^{-1}
in the first round, which greatly facilitates computation. Our approach can also handle non-smooth loss functions like absolute deviation loss. Additionally, instead of constructing the debiased estimator on every local machine as in (
2
), our method aggregates gradients and constructs one debiased estimator on the first machine, which further saves computational resources.

On the other hand, to improve the convergence rate in each round and reduce the number of communication rounds, we propose a semi-supervised debiased estimator. Specifically, we leverage the unlabeled data to estimate the inverse Hessian matrix, which can significantly reduce bias when the unlabeled sample size is much larger than the locally labeled sample size. Semi-supervised learning has recently gained much interest in the statistical community, and several articles have provided semi-supervised inference frameworks that focus on estimating the mean of the response variable in both low dimensional
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
and high dimensional
(
Zhang and Bradic 2021
)
scenarios. Additionally, in
Chakrabortty and Cai 2018
;
Azriel et al. 2022
;
Deng et al. 2020
, the authors considered semi-supervised inference for the miss-specified linear model in both low-dimensions and high-dimensions, while some works have studies inferring explained variance in high-dimensional linear regression
(
Cai and Guo 2020
)
and evaluating the various prediction performance measures for the binary classifier
(
Gronsbell and Cai 2018
)
. For distributed SSL,
Chang et al. 2017
studied kernel ridge regression and showed that the semi-supervised DC method benefits significantly from unlabeled data and allows more machines than the ordinary DC. Following this line of research, a bunch of works such as
Lin and Zhou 2018
;
Guo et al. 2017
;
Guo et al. 2019
;
Hu and Zhou 2021
have been proposed. However, these works all focused on one-shot non-parametric learning. To the best of our knowledge, there is no work considering distributed semi-supervised learning in high dimensions.

S

Secondly, for the

We have numerically explored the impact of various hyperparameters on our model’s performance. Moreover, we have conducted a comparative analysis with existing methods, showcasing the advantages of our method both in terms of statistical accuracy and computational time.

2
Distributed Semi-Supervised Debiased Estimator for Sparse Linear Regression

In this section, we consider the distributed sparse linear regression with square loss as a toy example. We assume that the covariate
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{p})^{{\top}}
and the label
Y
are generated from the following linear model

Y=\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}+\epsilon,

(3)

Throughout this section, we take the loss function as
f(x)=x^{2}/2
. Then the empirical loss function on the
j
-th machine becomes

{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}-Y_{i})^{2}.

(4)

To conduct distributed learning, the classical way is to take the average of the debiased lasso estimator, which has been studied in
Lee et al. 2017
;
Battey et al. 2018
. More precisely, let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}
be the local Lasso estimator using the data on the
j
-th machine, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{j}
be the local estimator of the precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=({\mathbb{E}}\{\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\})^{-1}
, then the one-shot debiased estimator is constructed as

\,\hbox{\kern 0.09995pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.42082pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\boldsymbol{\beta}$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{j}\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j})\right\},

(5)

where
\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}Y_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}
. While this method has been proven to enjoy a better convergence rate, there is still much room for improvement in our setting. On the one hand, it requires each machine to estimate the inverse covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, which may waste much computational effort. On the other hand, we hope to incorporate the unlabeled data into the estimator.

To address these difficulties, we propose an alternative estimator as follows. We first construct an initial estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}
and send it to each machine, then each machine computes the local gradient
\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)})
and sends to the master machine. Next, we construct the following estimator

\,\hbox{\kern 0.09995pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.42082pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\boldsymbol{\beta}$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,^{(1)}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}),

(6)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}
denotes the consistent estimator of the precision matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
using all the covariates
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
’s on the master machine
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
. Compared with (
5
), our method only requires estimating one inverse covariance matrix, therefore saving much computational cost. Moreover, it makes use of all covariates information on
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
. As will be shown in Theorem
1
, the semi-supervised estimator constructed in (
6
) has a better convergence rate than its fully supervised counterpart.

For the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
in high dimension, there are many works such as
Meinshausen and Bühlmann 2006
;
Cai et al. 2011
;
Yuan 2010
;
Liu and Luo 2015
. In this paper, we utilize the
\mathrm{SCIO}
method proposed in
Liu and Luo 2015
, which suggests to forming the estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{1},...,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{p})
row by row. More specifically, the
l
-th row is given by minimizing the objective function

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{l}=\mathop{\rm{argmin}}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\omega}-\boldsymbol{e}_{l}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\omega}+\lambda_{l}|\boldsymbol{\omega}|_{1}\Big\},

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{l}
denotes the unit vector on the
l
-th coordinate, and
\lambda_{l}
is the corresponding regularization parameter. The problem (
7
) can be solved by many efficient algorithms like FISTA
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
and ADMM
(
Boyd et al. 2011
)
, which makes our method more effective.

Remark 1
.

Newton’s method has been widely employed in the domain of distributed learning, spanning applications in low dimensional learning
(
Shamir et al. 2014
;
Huang and Huo 2019
;
Jordan et al. 2019
;
Fan et al. 2018
)
, sparse learning
(
Wang et al. 2017
)
, and kernel learning
(
Zhou 2020
)
. Our proposed method resembles the distributed approximate Newton method and is tailored for the high-dimensional debiased estimator, which is unexplored in the literature. Consequently, our work makes a valuable contribution to distributed high-dimension statistical inference. Further, the existing body of work in this area has predominantly centered on the fully-supervised setup, with limited exploration of semi-supervised scenarios. In this paper, we advance this research by introducing a substantial quantity of unlabeled data to enhance the estimation of Hessian information. Our findings provide theoretical evidence that the incorporation of unlabeled data effectively accelerates the distributed training process. It is conceivable that the methodologies elucidated in the aforementioned works can be readily extended to the semi-supervised framework, thereby yielding similar acceleration benefits.

Remark 2
.

By leveraging the idea of
Lee et al. 2017
, our method can be easily extended to cases where more than one machine contains unlabeled data. Denote
{\mathcal{U}}
as the index of machines containing unlabeled data. Then we can let the
j
-th machine (where
j\in{\mathcal{U}}
) to estimate
\lfloor p/|{\mathcal{U}}|\rfloor
rows of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. The master machine sends the averaged gradient
m^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)})
to each
{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
, where
j\in{\mathcal{U}}
. Suppose the

-th machine estimates the

\,\hbox{\kern 0.10216pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.42082pt\hbox{\kern-1.02228pt$\beta$\kern-1.02228pt}}}\,^{(1)}_{l}=\widehat{\beta}_{l}^{(0)}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{l}^{{\top}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}),

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{l}
is given by (
7
) using the covariates on
{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
. This helps to further reduce the computational burden as the estimation of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{l}
’s is processed in parallel.

In this section, we have presented the fundamental concept of our approach, which distinguishes itself from the existing distributed debiased estimators in two aspects. Firstly, we aggregate the gradients from all local machines, instead of the local estimators, and only estimate one high-dimensional inverse Hessian matrix; Secondly, we employ more unlabeled covariates to estimate the Hessian matrix to improve per-round accuracy. It should be noted that the Hessian matrix of the square loss is uncomplicated since it is independent of both the parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and the label
Y
. However, in the subsequent sections, we discuss
M
-estimation and generalized linear model, which have more complex forms. To eliminate the dependence of the Hessian matrix on other parameters, we present two customized techniques for the specific forms of the loss functions, which will be elaborated on in the following sections.

3
Distributed Semi-Supervised Debiased Estimator for Sparse
M
-Estimation

In this section, we consider the distributed semi-supervised learning for a broader class of sparse
M
-estimation problems. In this model, the data are assumed to be generated from the linear model (
3
). Based on different assumptions on the noise
\epsilon
, we should choose different loss functions
f(x)
. In general, the empirical loss function on the
j
-th machine is

{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}f(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}-Y_{i}).

(8)

The situation becomes different from linear regression. To conduct the debiasing approach, a key step is to estimate the inverse of the population Hessian matrix, which is
\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\beta})={\mathbb{E}}[f^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}-Y)\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}]
. Notice that
\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
contains the label information
Y
, therefore we cannot directly use the unlabeled covariates in the debiasing step.

3.1
Separated Estimation of Inverse Hessian

To address this issue, we observe that when
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is close to the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
, the corresponding Hessian matrix satisfies

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[f^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}-Y)\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\big]

\displaystyle\approx{\mathbb{E}}\big[f^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}-Y)\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big[f^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)\big]{\mathbb{E}}\big[\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\big],

(9)

which is the multiplication of a scalar value and a matrix only involving the covariate
\boldsymbol{X}
. Therefore we can estimate the terms
{\mathbb{E}}\big[f^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)\big]
and
{\mathbb{E}}\big[\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\big]
separately. More specifically, given a consistent estimator
\widehat{H}^{(0)}
of
{\mathbb{E}}\big[f^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)\big]
, we can construct the following distributed semi-supervised debiased estimator

\,\hbox{\kern 0.09995pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.42082pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\boldsymbol{\beta}$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,^{(1)}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}-\frac{1}{\widehat{H}^{(0)}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}).

(10)

The construction of
\widehat{H}^{(0)}
will be presented in the next part. Since the debiased estimator
\,\hbox{\kern 0.09995pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.42082pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\boldsymbol{\beta}$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,^{(1)}
is dense, it does not have
\ell_{2}
-norm and
\ell_{1}
-norm consistency. To further encourage sparsity, we propose the thresholded estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}=(\widehat{\beta}^{(1)}_{1},...,\widehat{\beta}_{p}^{(1)})^{{\top}}
where each coordinate is defined by

\widehat{\beta}_{l}^{(1)}=\,\hbox{\kern 0.09995pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.42082pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\beta$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,_{l}^{(1)}\cdot{\mathbb{I}}(|\,\hbox{\kern 0.09995pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.42082pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\beta$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,_{l}^{(1)}|\geq\tau).

If we repeatedly take
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}
back to the above procedure, we can obtain an iterative algorithm, which is presented in Algorithm
1
. It is worth noting that, by (
10
) we know the estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}
is unchanged throughout iterations. Therefore, we only need to estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}
one time at the beginning of the procedure and keep it in the memory. At each iteration, it is enough to update the scalar
\widehat{H}^{(t-1)}
and the vector
\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t-1)})
, which makes our algorithm much efficient.

Construction of
\widehat{H}^{(t-1)}

In (
9
), we assume the loss function
f(\cdot)
is twice differentiable for the ease of presentation. Indeed, we allow
f(\cdot)
to be nonsmooth so that our theory embraces the case of median regression (see Example
2
). To achieve this goal, we first define the function
h(x)={\mathbb{E}}\{f^{\prime}(x+\epsilon)\}
. Then we know

\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big|_{x=0}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f^{\prime}(x+\epsilon)\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\right]=h^{\prime}(0){\mathbb{E}}[\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}].

Therefore, to estimate
h^{\prime}(0)
, we apply the same method as in Section 2.3 of
Tu et al. 2023
. More precisely, assume
f^{\prime}(\cdot)
has finitely many distinct discontinuous points
x_{1},\dots,x_{K}
, and it is differentiable outside of these points. We denote

\Delta_{k}=\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{k}^{+}}f^{\prime}(x)-\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{k}^{-}}f^{\prime}(x),

as the function gap at
x_{k}
, then
h^{\prime}(0)
can be explicitly written as

h^{\prime}(0)={\mathbb{E}}\{f^{\prime\prime}(Y-\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Delta_{k}\rho(x_{k}),

(12)

where
f^{\prime\prime}(x)
denotes the second-order derivative of
f(x)
, which can be defined almost everywhere on
{\mathbb{R}}
. Here
\rho(\cdot)
denotes the density function of the noise
\epsilon
. Given a kernel function
{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot)
, a bandwidth
h_{1}
and a consistent estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}
, on each local machine
{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
,
h^{\prime}(0)
can be locally estimated by

\displaystyle\widehat{H}_{j}^{(0)}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}}f^{\prime\prime}(Y_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\Delta_{k}}{nh_{1}}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}}{\mathcal{K}}\Big(\frac{Y_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}-x_{k}}{h_{1}}\Big).

(13)

Then the worker machine
{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
sends the local estimator
\widehat{H}_{j}^{(0)}
to the master machine
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, and we take average over these local estimators
\widehat{H}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widehat{H}_{j}^{(0)}
.
In the
t
-th iteration, we can estimate
\widehat{H}^{(t-1)}
in the same fashion.

Example 1
.

(Huber regression) Huber loss function is defined as

{\mathcal{L}}(x)=\begin{cases}x^{2}/2\quad&\text{for }|x|\leq\delta,\\
\delta|x|-\delta^{2}/2\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

where
\delta
is some pre-specified robustification parameter. In this case, the first-order derivative
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}(x)
is continuous, and we can compute that
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime\prime}(x)={\mathbb{I}}(|x|\leq\delta)
. Therefore
h^{\prime}(0)={\mathbb{P}}(|\epsilon|\leq\delta)
, and from (
13
) we know each local estimator
\widehat{H}_{j}^{(t-1)}
can be constructed by

\widehat{H}_{j}^{(t-1)}=n^{-1}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}}{\mathbb{I}}\big(|Y_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t-1)}|\leq\delta\big).

Example 2
.

(Median regression) In the median regression problem, the loss function is defined as
{\mathcal{L}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}|x|.
Therefore its derivative
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}(x)=1/2-{\mathbb{I}}(x\leq 0)
is discontinuous at point
x_{1}=0
. We can easily obtain that
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime\prime}(x)=0
(for the discontinuous point
x_{1}=0
, we can directly define
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
), and
\Delta_{1}=1
. Therefore from (
12
) we have
h^{\prime}(0)=\rho(0)
. By equation (
13
), we can estimate
h^{\prime}(0)
by

\displaystyle\widehat{H}_{j}^{(t-1)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{nh_{t}}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}}{\mathcal{K}}\Big(\frac{Y_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}_{i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t-1)}}{h_{t}}\Big),

on each local machine
{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
.

Remark 3
.

Debiased estimators for linear models have been extensively studied in the literature
(
Javanmard and Montanari 2014
;
Zhang and Zhang 2014
;
Cai et al. 2021
)
. However, for general loss functions, existing works typically recommend directly estimating the inverse Hessian matrix
(
van de Geer et al. 2014
;
Battey et al. 2018
;
Zhao et al. 2020
)
, which varies with the parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. This approach hurdles the development of multi-round debiasing estimators, which are necessary in a distributed setup. Separated Hessian estimation has been introduced in the context of quantile regression
(
Zhao et al. 2014
;
Chen et al. 2020
)
, and extended to general loss functions in
Tu et al. 2023
. However, these previous works have focused primarily on transforming the loss function to a square loss to overcome the computational bottlenecks arising from the non-smoothness of the loss function, which differs from our motivation.

In this section, we provide the theoretical results for the proposed methods. We first present several technical assumptions as follows. For ease of presentation, we denote
{\mathfrak{E}}(X_{0},\eta):={\mathbb{E}}\big\{X_{0}^{2}\exp(\eta|X_{0}|)\big\},

where

Condition A1

There exists some constants

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{p-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\left\{\exp(\kappa_{X}|\boldsymbol{v}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{X}|^{2})\right\}\leq C_{X},\quad\delta_{X}\leq\Lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\leq\Lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\leq\delta_{X}^{-1},

\displaystyle\|\Omega\|_{L_{1}}\leq C_{M},\quad\max_{1\leq l\leq p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\omega_{l,i}|^{q}\leq C_{q}s,

(14)

where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=(\omega_{i,l})_{i,l=1}^{p}
and

.

Condition A2
.

The loss function

satisfies
{\mathbb{E}}\{\boldsymbol{X}f^{\prime}(Y-\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\}=0
. Furthermore, there exist constants
\kappa_{f},C_{f}>0
such that, for every pair of
(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2})

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{E}}\Big\{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\beta}:|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}|_{2}\leq|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}|_{2}}\big|f^{\prime\prime}(Y-\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1})-f^{\prime\prime}(Y-\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta})\big|,\kappa_{f}\Big\}\leq C_{f}|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}|_{2},

Condition A3
.

There exists a constant
C_{d}>0
such that the noise
\epsilon
has probability density function
\rho(\cdot)
which satisfies

\big|\rho(x)\big|\leq C_{d},\quad\big|\rho(x)-\rho(y)\big|\leq C_{d}|x-y|,\quad\text{for any }x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Moreover, there exists a constant
C_{l}
such that
\min\{\rho(x_{1}),\dots\rho(x_{K})\}>C_{l}>0
.

Condition A4
.

The kernel function
{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot)
satisfies
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathcal{K}}(u)\mathrm{d}u=1
and
{\mathcal{K}}(u)=0
for
|u|\geq 1
. Moreover,
{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous satisfying
|{\mathcal{K}}(x)-{\mathcal{K}}(y)|\leq C_{k}|x-y|
holds for arbitrary
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Condition AS
.

(Smooth loss) There exists a constant
C_{s}>0
such that, for any two points
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, there is

\displaystyle|f^{\prime}(x_{1})-f^{\prime}(x_{2})|\leq C_{s}|x_{1}-x_{2}|.

Condition ANS
.

(Non-smooth loss) There exist constants
\kappa_{ns},C_{ns}>0
such that, for every pair of
(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2})
, there is

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{p-1}}{\mathfrak{E}}\Big\{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\beta}:|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}|_{2}\leq|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}|_{2}}\big|\boldsymbol{v}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{X}f^{\prime}(Y-\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1})-\boldsymbol{v}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{X}f^{\prime}(Y-\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta})\big|,\kappa_{ns}\Big\}\leq C_{ns}|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}|_{2},

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{p-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\big|\boldsymbol{v}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{X}f^{\prime}(Y-\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1})-\boldsymbol{v}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{X}f^{\prime}(Y-\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2})\big|\leq C_{ns}|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}|_{2}.

Condition
A1
is some regularity conditions on the covariate
\boldsymbol{X}
. In particular, we assume the inverse covariance matrix is
\ell_{q}
-sparse (where

;

assumes the continuity of the second-order derivative

4
Distributed Semi-Supervised Debiased Estimator for Sparse GLM

In this section, we consider distributed semi-supervised learning for generalized linear models (GLM). In a GLM, given a function
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, the observations
(\boldsymbol{X},Y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p+1}
are generated according to the following conditional probability function

{\mathbb{P}}(Y\mid\boldsymbol{X})=\widetilde{c}\exp\left\{\frac{Y\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}-\psi(\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})}{c(\sigma)}\right\},

(19)

where
\widetilde{c}
and
c(\sigma)
are some scale constants, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
is the true model parameter. The corresponding empirical loss function on the
j
-th machine is

{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}f(Y_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}\Big\{-Y_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\psi(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta})\Big\}.

(20)

We note that the population Hessian matrix at
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
,

\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})={\mathbb{E}}[\nabla^{2}|_{\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}}f(Y,\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta})]={\mathbb{E}}[\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}],

(21)

does not contain the label
Y
. Therefore, to construct a DSS debiased estimator, a direct way is to use all covariate information to estimate the inverse Hessian matrix (
21
). However, the Hessian matrix
\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is dependent on the model parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. When performing a multi-round debiasing approach, we need to update the parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and estimate the inverse Hessian matrix recursively, which is computationally expensive.

4.1
Accelerated Debiasing by Weighted Gradients

To alleviate the computational burden and accelerate the multi-round debiasing algorithm, we propose a gradient-weighting approach as follows. To motivate our construction, we first consider the generic form of the one-step debiased estimator. More precisely, for each coordinate
l\in\{1,\dots,p\}
, there is

\widetilde{\beta}_{l}^{(1)}=\widehat{\beta}_{l}^{(0)}-\boldsymbol{u}_{l}^{{\top}}\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}w_{i}\Big\{-Y_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}+\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)})\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\Big\},

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}
is an initial estimator,
w_{i}
’s are data-dependent gradient weight, and
\boldsymbol{u}_{l}
’s are the projection directions. Applying Taylor expansion for
\psi^{\prime}
at
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}
, we can decompose the error of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{l}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
as follows

\displaystyle\widetilde{\beta}_{l}^{(1)}-\beta_{l}^{*}=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{l}^{{\top}}\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}w_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\epsilon_{i}+\Big(\boldsymbol{e}_{l}^{{\top}}-\boldsymbol{u}_{l}^{{\top}}\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}w_{i}\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)})\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\Big)(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{{\top}}\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}w_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\Delta_{i},

(22)

where
\epsilon_{i}=\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})-Y_{i}
,
\Delta_{i}=\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\{\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\}^{2}
for some
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
lying between
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}
, and
\boldsymbol{e}_{l}
is the canonical basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
. From this decomposition, to conduct statistical inference for the debiased estimator, we require the second term dominated by the first term in (
22
). Therefore, the projection direction
\boldsymbol{u}_{l}
should approximate the
l
-th column of the inverse of the matrix
\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}=\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}w_{i}\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)})\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}
. However, to construct
\boldsymbol{u}_{l}
for every coordinate, we should solve
p
high-dimensional optimization problems successively, which incurs heavy computational overhead. To reduce the computational burden, we can take
w_{i}=1/\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)})
so that the matrix
\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}
is independent of the parameter
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}
.

In summary, given an initial parameter
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}
, we compute the following weighted gradient

\boldsymbol{g}_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)})}\Big\{-Y_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}+\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)})\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\Big\},

(23)

and construct the distributed semi-supervised debiased estimator

\,\hbox{\kern 0.09995pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.42082pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\boldsymbol{\beta}$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,^{(1)}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{g}_{j}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}).

(24)

Compared with directly estimate the inverse of
\boldsymbol{H}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)})
in (
21
), this approach only estimate the inverse covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, which is independent of the parameter
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}
. Therefore we only need to estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}
for one time, which saves much computational power when performing multi-round algorithm.

To obtain a sparse estimator, we threshold each coordinate and get
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}=(\widehat{\beta}^{(1)}_{1},...,\widehat{\beta}_{p}^{(1)})^{{\top}}
where

\widehat{\beta}_{l}^{(1)}=\,\hbox{\kern 0.09995pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.42082pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\beta$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,_{l}^{(1)}\cdot{\mathbb{I}}(|\,\hbox{\kern 0.09995pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.42082pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\beta$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,_{l}^{(1)}|\geq\tau).

The iterative algorithm is presented in Algorithm
2
.

Example 3
.

(Logistic regression) In the logistic regression model, we generate the labels from the following distribution

\begin{cases}{\mathbb{P}}(Y=1|\boldsymbol{X})=\frac{\exp(\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})}{1+\exp(\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})},\\

(25)

Then the function
\psi(\cdot)
is defined as

\psi(x)=\log(1+\exp(x)).

We can readily compute that

and
\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)=1/\{(1+\exp(x))(1+\exp(-x))\}
.

Remark 4
.

In this section, we have demonstrated the significance of the gradient-weighting technique in reducing the computational cost of implementing the multi-round debiasing approach.
The gradient-weighting technique has been applied in contemporary works for various purposes, such as simultaneous testing
(
Ma et al. 2021
)
, case probability inference
(
Guo et al. 2021
)
, genetic relatedness inference
(
Ma et al. 2022
)
, and confidence interval construction
(
T. Tony Cai and Ma 2023
)
for logistic regression models. Different weights were proposed for different applications. However, these studies primarily focus on a single-machine setup, and the rationale for weighting the gradient differs from ours. Furthermore, while all aforementioned works only consider logistic models, our method extends to a broader class of generalized linear models.

4.2
Theoretical Results of
\mathrm{DISSD}
for GLM

In this section, we provide the theories of
\mathrm{DISSD}
for GLM. We first list some technical conditions as follows.

Condition B1
.

There exists some constants
\kappa_{X},C_{X},C_{M},C_{q},\delta_{X}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{p-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\left\{\exp(\kappa_{X}|\boldsymbol{v}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{X}|^{2})\right\}\leq C_{X},\quad\delta_{X}\leq\Lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\leq\Lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\leq\delta_{X}^{-1},

\displaystyle\|\Omega\|_{L_{1}}\leq C_{M},\quad\max_{1\leq l\leq p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\omega_{l,i}|^{q}\leq C_{q}s,

(26)

where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=(\omega_{i,l})_{i,l=1}^{p}
and
0\leq q<1
.

Condition B2
.

There exists some constants
\kappa_{0},C_{0}
such that

\max\Big\{\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{p-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\left\{\exp(\kappa_{0}|\boldsymbol{v}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{X}|^{2})\right\},{\mathbb{E}}\big[\exp(\kappa_{0}|\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})-Y|^{2})\big]\Big\}\leq C_{0}.

Condition B3
.

The function
\psi(\cdot)
is three-time differentiable, and there exists a uniform constant
c_{\psi}>0
such that

\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}\max\{|\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)|,|\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)|\}\leq c_{\psi}.

Condition B4
.

With probability not less than
1-p^{-\gamma}
, there holds
\min\{\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\}\geq c_{l}
for
i\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}
(
1\leq j\leq m
) and some small constant
c_{l}>0
.

Condition
B1
is the same as Condition
A1
in Section
3.2
. Condition
B2
assumes subgaussianity of the gradient
\{-Y_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}+\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\}
. In Condition
B3
, we assume the function
\psi
to be smooth with bounded derivatives. Condition
B4
requires
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
to be bounded for all
1\leq i\leq N
with high probability, which is a common assumption in the literature. As will be seen in Appendix
7.4
, the logistic regression model in Example
3
satisfies these conditions given the covariate
\boldsymbol{X}
is uniformly bounded.

With the above conditions, we can prove the following convergence rate.

Theorem 4
.

Under Conditions
B1
to
B4
, assume the initial estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}
satisfies
|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}|_{2}=O(r_{n})
and
|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}|_{0}\leq C_{r}s
. There are rate constraints
s=o(\min\{(mn)/\log p,(n^{*}/\log p)^{(1-q)/3}\})
,
r_{n}=o(1/(s^{3/2}\log^{2}p))
. Moreover, take
\lambda_{l}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n^{*}}}
in (
7
), and the threshold level
\tau
satisfies
\tau\asymp C_{\tau}(\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{mn}}+r_{n}s\big(\frac{\log p}{n^{*}}\big)^{(1-q)/2}+s\log^{2}pr_{n}^{2})
for some
C_{\tau}>0
. Then the one-step distributed debiased estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}
satisfies

\displaystyle\big|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\big|_{\infty}=

\displaystyle O_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{mn}}+r_{n}s\Big(\frac{\log p}{n^{*}}\Big)^{(1-q)/2}+r_{n}\sqrt{\frac{s\log p}{mn}}+s\log^{2}pr_{n}^{2}\right),

(27)

\displaystyle\big|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\big|_{2}=

\displaystyle O_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{s\log p}{mn}}+r_{n}s^{3/2}\Big(\frac{\log p}{n^{*}}\Big)^{(1-q)/2}+r_{n}\sqrt{\frac{s^{2}\log p}{mn}}+s^{3/2}\log^{2}pr_{n}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\big|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\big|_{1}=

\displaystyle O_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left(s\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{mn}}+r_{n}s^{2}\Big(\frac{\log p}{n^{*}}\Big)^{(1-q)/2}+r_{n}s\sqrt{\frac{s\log p}{mn}}+s^{2}\log^{2}pr_{n}^{2}\right).

We can observe that the convergence rate of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}
for GLM is similar to that of M-estimator in Theorem
1
, except for the last term. More specifically, the last term of GLM has an additional factor
s\log^{2}p
, which is caused by the weighting procedure. Compared with Theorem
1
and
2
, the theory for GLM requires more stringent conditions on the initial rate
r_{n}
, which can be regarded as the price of weighting the gradients. Next, we present the convergence rate of the multi-round estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(T)}
.

Corollary 2
.

Under assumptions in Theorem
1
or Theorem
2
then we have that the
T
-th round
\mathrm{DISSD}
estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(T)}
satisfies

\displaystyle\big|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(T)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\big|_{\infty}=

\displaystyle O_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{mn}}+r_{n}s^{(3T-1)/2}\Big(\frac{\log p}{n^{*}}\Big)^{(1-q)T/2}+\frac{1}{s^{2}\log^{2}p}\big(s^{3/2}\log^{2}pr_{n})^{2^{T}}\right),

(28)

\displaystyle\big|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(T)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\big|_{2}=

\displaystyle O_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{s\log p}{mn}}+r_{n}s^{3T/2}\Big(\frac{\log p}{n^{*}}\Big)^{(1-q)T/2}+\frac{1}{s^{3/2}\log^{2}p}\big(s^{3/2}\log^{2}pr_{n})^{2^{T}}\right),

\displaystyle\big|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(T)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\big|_{1}=

\displaystyle O_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left(s\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{mn}}+r_{n}s^{(3T+1)/2}\Big(\frac{\log p}{n^{*}}\Big)^{(1-q)T/2}+\frac{1}{s\log^{2}p}\big(s^{3/2}\log^{2}pr_{n})^{2^{T}}\right).

Similarly as in Corollary
1
, achieving a near-optimal statistical rate entails the dominance of the first term over the second term in (
28
). Consequently, the number of iterations required, denoted as
T
, should align with the conditions established in (
18
). Next, we prove the asymptotic normality result for our
\mathrm{DISSD}
estimator for GLM.

Theorem 5
.

Under assumptions in Corollary
2
, and the iteration number
T
is large enough such that
|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(T)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}|_{2}=O_{{\mathbb{P}}}(\sqrt{\log p/(mn)})
. Further assume the rate constraints
s=o\big((mn)^{1/4}/\log^{3/2}p
,
(n^{*})^{(1-q)/3}/(\log^{(2-q)/3}p)\big)
, then we have that

\frac{\sqrt{mn}}{\sigma_{l}}(\,\hbox{\kern 0.10216pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.42082pt\hbox{\kern-1.02228pt$\beta$\kern-1.02228pt}}}\,^{(T)}_{l}-\beta^{*}_{l})\xrightarrow{d}{\mathcal{N}}(0,1),

where

\sigma_{l}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{l}^{{\top}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})}\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\top}}\Big]\boldsymbol{\omega}_{l}.

Compared with Theorem
3
, we have a more strict constraint on the sparsity level, namely,
s=o((mn)^{1/4}/\log^{3/2}p)
. This is also caused by the gradient-weighting technique.

Parameter Settings.

To illustrate the performance of our method, we mainly compare six methods

Local Lasso: Solve the Lasso problem using only the data on

•

6
Concluding Remarks and Future Study

In this paper, we investigate the problem of semi-supervised sparse learning in a distributed setup and demonstrate that incorporating unlabeled data can enhance the statistical accuracy in distributed learning. Throughout this paper, we construct the inverse covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
using SCIO
Liu and Luo 2015
, which requires
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
to be sparse in each column. However, there exist other debiasing approaches, such as
Javanmard and Montanari 2014
;
van de Geer et al. 2014
;
Zhang and Zhang 2014
;
Cai et al. 2021
, that can eliminate the sparsity constraint on
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. It would be interesting to explore the application of these approaches to our DISSD method in future work to see if similar results can be achieved. Furthermore, in addition to debiased estimators, other methods for sparse learning exist, such as adding
\ell_{1}
penalty
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
or non-convex penalties
(
Fan and Li 2001
;
Zhang 2010b
;
Zhang 2010a
)
, or using the iterative hard thresholding method
(
Blumensath and Davies 2009
;
Jain et al. 2014
;
Yuan et al. 2018
)
. Investigating the use of distributed semi-supervised methods for these techniques is of significant importance.

is the average of
mn
i.i.d. terms
\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})}\Big\{-Y_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}+\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\Big\}
. By Lemma

, we have that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{g}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\Big|_{\infty}\leq C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{mn}},\cap{\mathfrak{X}}_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{mn}\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})}\Big\{-Y_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}+\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\Big\}{\mathbb{I}}({\mathfrak{X}}_{i})\Big|_{\infty}\leq C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{mn}},\cap{\mathfrak{X}}_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-p^{-\gamma}-{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{mn}\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})}\Big\{-Y_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}+\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\Big\}{\mathbb{I}}({\mathfrak{X}}_{i})\Big|_{\infty}\geq C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{mn}}\Big).

For each
i
, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{p-1}}{\mathfrak{E}}\Big\{\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})}\boldsymbol{v}^{{\top}}\Big\{-Y_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}+\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\Big\}{\mathbb{I}}({\mathfrak{X}}_{i}),c_{l}\kappa_{0}/2\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{p-1}}{\mathfrak{E}}\Big\{\frac{1}{c_{l}}\boldsymbol{v}^{{\top}}\Big\{-Y_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}+\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\Big\},c_{l}\kappa_{0}/2\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4}{c_{l}^{2}\kappa_{0}^{2}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{p-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\exp\kappa_{0}\boldsymbol{v}^{{\top}}\Big\{-Y_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}+\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\Big\}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{c_{l}^{2}\kappa_{0}^{2}}\Big\{\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{p-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\exp\Big\{\kappa_{0}|\boldsymbol{v}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}|^{2}\Big\}\Big]+{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\exp\Big\{\kappa_{0}|\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})-Y_{i}|^{2}\Big\}\Big]\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4}{c_{l}^{2}\kappa_{0}^{2}}C_{0}.

Therefore, we can apply Lemma
1
and obtain that

{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{mn}\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})}\Big\{-Y_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}+\psi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{{\top}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\Big\}{\mathbb{I}}({\mathfrak{X}}_{i})\Big|_{\infty}\geq C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{mn}}\Big)=O(p^{-\gamma_{1}}),

which proves the lemma.
∎
