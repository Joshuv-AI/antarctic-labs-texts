On some possible combinations of mixing rates for strictly stationary, reversible Markov chains
Richard C. Bradley

Abstract
A class of examples is constructed to show that for strictly stationary Markov chains that are reversible, the simultaneous mixing rates for the ρ \rho -mixing and strong mixing ( α \alpha -mixing) conditions can be fairly arbitrary, within certain unavoidable tight restrictions. The examples constructed here have the added property that the mixing rate for the absolute regularity ( β \beta -mixing) condition is within a constant factor of that for strong mixing.

strictly stationary, reversible Markov chain;

strong mixing; absolute regularity;
\rho
-mixing

Theory developed in the papers by

[

[

It is well known that that “key result” can be formulated in the terminology of the dependence coefficients associated with the “absolute regularity” (“
\beta
-mixing”) condition and the “
\rho
-mixing” condition.
All that will be discussed in a little more detail after

In an effort to provide a relatively gentle exposition of

That expository effort in

\rho

This paper here is intended to give at least a partial answer to that question.
The construction given in this paper — a class of strictly stationary, reversible Markov chains — will illustrate, within the unavoidable narrow constraints alluded to above, a pretty broad spectrum of possible combinations of such mixing rates. As the examples will also involve a state space that is countable (rather than, say, the whole real number line), the arguments involved in checking the various properties of the examples here will be pretty elementary.

Part 5

). A given strictly stationary Markov chain

(D) As is well known, any strictly stationary

The explanation is simple.

\displaystyle P(\{X_{0}=0\}\cap\{X_{1}=1\})\

\displaystyle=\ P(X_{0}=0)-P(\{X_{0}=0\}\cap\{X_{1}=0\})

P((X_{0},X_{1})=(0,1))=P((X_{1},X_{0})=(0,1))

Remark 1.4

{\rm for\ all}\ x\in(e,\infty),\ \ h(x):=\log(\eta(x))=-qx^{a}+b(\log x)+c\log(\log x).

Notations 2.1

The construction (in Section

(Again recall Part 5(D) of Setting

Part 1.

Joint probability matrices
. First we shall spell out a class of
2\times 2
matrices that will be used for
joint

(not transition) probabilities.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]
and any
\theta\in(0,1)
, define the
2\times 2
matrix

\Lambda^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}:=(\lambda^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}_{ij})_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\lambda^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}_{00}

\displaystyle:=(1-\varepsilon)^{2}+(1-\varepsilon)\varepsilon\theta;

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lambda^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}_{01}=\lambda^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}_{10}

\displaystyle:=(1-\varepsilon)\varepsilon-(1-\varepsilon)\varepsilon\theta;\ \ {\rm and}

(2.2)

\displaystyle\lambda^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}_{11}

\displaystyle:=\varepsilon^{2}+(1-\varepsilon)\varepsilon\theta.

(2.3)

Note that (under the stipulated conditions on
\varepsilon
and
\theta
), these four entries are all positive, and their sum is 1.

We shall return to that class of matrices in Lemma
2.2
below.

Part 2.

Transition probability matrices
. The matrices below will play a key role in transition probabilities (of some strictly stationary, 2-state Markov chains).

The
2\times 2
identity matrix
(\delta_{ij})_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}
(where

\delta_{00}=\delta_{11}=1
and
\delta_{01}=\delta_{10}=0
)
will be denoted simply as
I_{2}
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]
, let
A^{(\varepsilon)}:=(a^{(\varepsilon)}_{ij})_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}
be the

2\times 2
matrix in which each of the two rows is
[1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]
and any
\theta\in(0,1)
, define the
2\times 2
matrix

\mathbb{P}^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}:=(p^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}_{ij})_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}
as follows:

\mathbb{P}^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}:=\theta I_{2}+(1-\theta)A^{(\varepsilon)}.

(2.4)

By simple arithmetic, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]
and any

\theta\in(0,1)
, the entries of the

2\times 2
matrix
\mathbb{P}^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}
are as follows:

\displaystyle p^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}_{00}

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\theta\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ p^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}_{01}=\varepsilon-\varepsilon\theta;

(2.5)

\displaystyle p^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}_{10}

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)-(1-\varepsilon)\theta\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ p^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}_{11}=\varepsilon+(1-\varepsilon)\theta.

(2.6)

Note that in each of the two rows of
\mathbb{P}^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}
, the two entries are positive and their sum is 1.

By trivial matrix multiplication,
(A^{(\varepsilon)})^{2}=A^{(\varepsilon)}
for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]
.
Hence by simple matrix multiplication, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]
and any pair of numbers

, one has that

\mathbb{P}^{(\varepsilon,\theta)}\mathbb{P}^{(\varepsilon,\tau)}=\mathbb{P}^{(\varepsilon,\theta\tau)}.

(2.7)

We shall return to those notations in Lemma
2.3
below.

Proof of Theorem 1.3

In this proof, in order to avoid some unnecessary clutter, the use of properties of the function

in Theorem 1.3 (such as the “log convexity” assumption) will sometimes be at “less than full strength”, resulting in some crude inequalities and leaving unsaid some natural “extra” or “stronger” observations that will not be needed in the argument.

In this proof, just in an attempt to “make the arithmetic a little nicer”, base-2 logarithms will be used, instead of natural logarithms.

The proof will be spelled out here in a series of (mostly)
small “steps”, identified by consecutive letters
(“Step A”, “Claim B”, “Step C”, and so on).

A large portion of this proof here will involve setting up some
elementary but tedious “scaffolding” involving lines in connection with the “log convexity” assumption in the theorem.
Essentially the same scaffolding, with just minor differences, was used by the author
[
1
]

to prove a quite different but somewhat related theorem (involving more mixing conditions, in a
non-Markovian, “non-reversible” context).
There the scaffolding was spelled out tersely.
In the presentation of that theorem and its proof (again)
in [
[
2
]
, v.3, Theorem 26.5],
that scaffolding was spelled out in detail, partly in the proof
itself and partly in the Appendix at the end of that book.
As compared to the context there, the context here will involve some small but nontrivial differences in some information that is developed and used.
For the reader’s convenience, we shall spell out the scaffolding here in detail, in a self-contained form that is designed to
cleanly fit our context here.

Step A.
As in the statement of Theorem 1.3, suppose

, and

Define the continuous, strictly decreasing (and negative) function

as follows:

{\rm For\ each}\ x\in[0,\infty),\ \ \ g(x)\ :=\ \log_{2}[\,r^{x}\,f(x)]\ =\ x(\log_{2}r)+\log_{2}f(x).

(3.1)

As a trivial consequence of hypothesis (iii) (which involved the
natural logarithm) in Theorem
1.3
, the function

is convex, and hence the function

in (

Claim B.

The following three statements hold: (1)
(\log_{2}r)x-g(x)\to\infty
as
x\to\infty
. (2) For every
t<\log_{2}r
, one has that

as

Proof.
To verify (1), note that by (

) and hypothesis (i) in Theorem

,

g(x)-(\log_{2}r)x=\log_{2}f(x)\to-\infty
as

Thus (1) holds.

To verity (2), suppose

is any number such that

t<\log_{2}r
.
Then

and hence
2^{t}/r<1
.
Hence by hypothesis (ii) in Theorem
1.3
,

(2^{t}/r)^{x}/f(x)\to 0
as
x\to\infty
.
That is,
2^{tx}/[r^{x}f(x)]\to 0
as
x\to\infty
.
Hence by the first equality in (

),

tx-g(x)\to-\infty
as
x\to\infty
.
Thus (2) holds.
That completes the proof.

Step C.
For convenient later reference, we shall just list here again, with

)
[and the related number

(0)

. (1)

Step D.
Suppose

\zeta_{v,y}\ :=\ {{g(y)-g(v)}\over{y-v}}.

(3.2)

By Step C(4),
\zeta_{v,y}<0
.

Define the (affine) function

as follows:

{\rm For\ each}\ x\in[v,y],\ \ \ C_{v,y}(x)\ :=\ g(v)+\zeta_{v,y}(x-v).

(3.3)

Then

and

.
This function

simply specifies the

from the point

(v,g(v))
to the point

By (

(3.4)

Define the nonnegative real number

by

M_{v,y}:=\sup_{x\in[v,y]}[C_{v,y}(x)-g(x)].

(3.5)

Claim E.

For any element

.

Proof.
Suppose
v\in[0,\infty)

\beta_{X}(m)\ \leq\ \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\beta_{W(j)}(m)\ \leq\ \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\,[2\cdot 2^{a(j)}\cdot(2^{s(j)})^{m}]\ \leq\ 12\cdot r^{m}f(m).

Thus (3) holds.
That completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
